Unmarked simple length spectral rigidity for covers
Tarik Aougab, Max Lahn, Marissa Loving, Nicholas Miller

Abstract
. We prove that every closed orientable surface S \displaystyle S of negative Euler characteristic admits a pair of finite-degree covers which are length isospectral over S \displaystyle S but generically not simple length isospectral over S \displaystyle S . To do this, we first characterize when two finite-degree covers of a connected, orientable surface of negative Euler characteristic are isomorphic in terms of which curves have simple elevations. We also construct hyperbolic surfaces X \displaystyle X and Y \displaystyle Y with the same full unmarked length spectrum but so that for each k \displaystyle k , the sets of lengths associated to curves with at most k \displaystyle k self-intersections differ.



1.
Introduction

Otal
[
Ota90
]
showed that any negatively curved metric on
\displaystyle S
is determined up to isotopy by its
marked length spectrum
, the function associating each closed curve to its infimal length. More generally, one can ask this question for different sets of closed curves and/or different families of metrics (see Fricke, Wolpert
[
Wol79
]

[
Ham03
]
work on hyperbolic metrics and Duchin–Leininger–Rafi
[
DLR10
]
and Loving’s
[
Lov20
]
work on flat metrics).

The roots of this paper lie in a family of interrelated questions distinguished from the above work by the absence of markings. Concretely, consider a
\displaystyle\mathrm{Mod}\left\lparen S\right\rparen
-invariant set
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}
of closed curves on
\displaystyle S
. For example, we will consider for each
\displaystyle k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\cup\left\{\infty\right\}
the set
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{k}
of curves whose self-intersection number is at most
\displaystyle k
. The
unmarked
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}
-length spectrum
of a hyperbolic metric
\displaystyle\mu
on
\displaystyle S
is the multi-set of infimal lengths of closed curves in
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}
in the metric
\displaystyle\mu
. Particularly relevant examples are the
unmarked length spectrum
and the
unmarked simple length spectrum
, which arise in the cases when
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{G}_{\infty}
is the set of all closed curves on
\displaystyle S
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{G}_{0}
is the set of simple curves on
\displaystyle S

Question 1
.

For which
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}
is a hyperbolic metric
\displaystyle\mu
on
\displaystyle S
determined up to isometry by its unmarked
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}
-length spectrum?

Unlike in the case of marked spectra, it is well-known that many classes of metrics on
\displaystyle S
are not unmarked length spectrally rigid. Indeed, examples of unmarked length isospectral but non-isometric complete hyperbolic surface metrics were first constructed by Vignéras
[
Vig80
]
in the arithmetic setting. Since then, a celebrated construction of Sunada
[
Sun85
]
yields a robust method for producing unmarked length isospectral but non-isometric surface metrics arising as pullbacks of metrics along covers of a fixed surface.

On the other hand, relatively little is known about the question of unmarked simple length spectral rigidity for hyperbolic metrics. Baik–Choi–Kim
[
BCK21
]
recently showed that, up to excluding the loci of some length identities, the family of hyperbolic metrics with the property that lengths in the simple length spectrum have multiplicity
\displaystyle 1
(that is, for each positive real number
\displaystyle L
, there is at most one simple closed geodesic of length
\displaystyle L
) is simple length spectrally rigid. This result motivates our examination of pullbacks of hyperbolic metrics along finite-degree covers, since high multiplicity is common in this regime.

Explicitly, fix for the remainder of this article two finite-degree covers
\displaystyle p\colon X\xrightarrow{}S
and
\displaystyle q\colon Y\xrightarrow{}S
of a closed, orientable surface
\displaystyle S

Question 2

If
\displaystyle X
and
\displaystyle Y
are generically unmarked simple length isospectral over
\displaystyle S

\displaystyle p
and
\displaystyle q

Here (and in what follows), we say that a property of hyperbolic metrics on
\displaystyle X

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle S

1.1.
Main results

Our first result implies that there are infinitely many pairs of hyperbolic surfaces (i.e. topological surfaces equipped with a choice of hyperbolic metric) which are unmarked length isospectral but not unmarked simple length isospectral.

Theorem 1
.

Every closed surface
\displaystyle S
of negative Euler characteristic admits a pair of finite-degree covers which are unmarked length isospectral over
\displaystyle S
but generically not unmarked simple length isospectral over
\displaystyle S
.

In fact, the pairs of isospectral hyperbolic surfaces we obtain in the proof of
1
have the stronger property that the respective multi-sets of lengths associated to curves with any bounded number of self intersections do not coincide.

Corollary 2
.

There exist hyperbolic surfaces
\displaystyle X
and
\displaystyle Y
which are unmarked length isospectral but so that for each
\displaystyle k\geq 0
,
\displaystyle X
and
\displaystyle Y
are not unmarked
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{k}
-length isospectral.

The statement of
1
combines work of Maunchang
[
Mau13
]
for a surface of genus
\displaystyle 2
with our work for surfaces of genus at least
\displaystyle 3
(see
Section 4.3
). Maungchang uses Sunada’s construction to build a pair of finite-degree covers
\displaystyle X\xrightarrow{}S
and
\displaystyle Y\xrightarrow{}S
of a closed surface
\displaystyle S
of genus
\displaystyle 2
so that a generic hyperbolic metric on
\displaystyle S
pulls back to metrics on
\displaystyle X
and
\displaystyle Y
which have the same length spectrum but not the same simple length spectrum. Our proof of
1
generalizes Maungchang’s technique and relies crucially on the following strengthening of
[
Aou+20
, Theorem 1.1]
, which uses entirely new proof techniques in order to remove the regularity hypothesis, thus resolving
[
Aou+20
, Conjecture 1.4]
. Given a finite cover
\displaystyle p\colon X\xrightarrow{}S
, a curve
\displaystyle\alpha
on
\displaystyle X
is an
elevation
of a curve
\displaystyle\gamma
on
\displaystyle S
along
\displaystyle p
if
\displaystyle p\left\lparen\alpha\right\rparen
is a nonzero iterate of
\displaystyle\gamma
.

Theorem 3
.

The covers
\displaystyle p\colon X\xrightarrow{}S
and
\displaystyle q\colon Y\xrightarrow{}S
are isomorphic if and only if for every curve
\displaystyle\gamma
on
\displaystyle S
,
\displaystyle\gamma
has a simple elevation along
\displaystyle p
to
\displaystyle X
if and only if it has a simple elevation along
\displaystyle q
to
\displaystyle Y

In
1

2

2

any

\displaystyle S

1.2.
Applications to simple length isospectrality of covers

Our applications of
3
aim towards answering
1
for the unmarked simple length spectra of hyperbolic metrics. More precisely,
3
also allows us to specify a finite-degree cover of
\displaystyle S
by the data of which curves have simple elevations. When one can find a curve that lifts simply to one cover but not the other (
3
guarantees that one can always do this) and which has no
length twins
(distinct curves on
\displaystyle S
whose lengths are commensurable in any hyperbolic metric, see
Section 2.7
), this lifting data is sufficient to distinguish the simple length spectra of the pullbacks of generic hyperbolic metrics on
\displaystyle S
to
\displaystyle X
and
\displaystyle Y
. In particular, the following proposition together with
3
yields a tool for verifying that such a pair of covers are not simple length isospectral.

Proposition 4
.

Suppose that there is a curve
\displaystyle\gamma
on
\displaystyle S
and a positive integer
\displaystyle m\geq 1
with the following properties:

(i)

\displaystyle\gamma
has no length twins on
\displaystyle S
; and

(ii)

\displaystyle\gamma
has different numbers of simple elevations of degree
\displaystyle m
along each of the covers
\displaystyle p\colon X\xrightarrow{}S
and
\displaystyle q\colon Y\xrightarrow{}S
.

Then
\displaystyle X
and
\displaystyle Y
are generically not unmarked simple length isospectral over
\displaystyle S
.

We use
4
to provide an affirmative answer to
2
when
\displaystyle p
and
\displaystyle q
are regular and
simply generated
, meaning that the fundamental groups of
\displaystyle X
and
\displaystyle Y
are generated by the set of all elevations of simple closed curves on
\displaystyle S
(see
Section 4.2
for a formal definition and discussion).

Theorem 5
.

If
\displaystyle p\colon X\xrightarrow{}S
and
\displaystyle q\colon Y\xrightarrow{}S
are regular and simply generated, then they are either isomorphic or generically not unmarked simple length isospectral over
\displaystyle S
.

It is known by work of Koberda–Santharoubane
[
KS16
]
and Malestein–Putman
[
MP19
]
that not all regular covers are simply generated, but their examples are necessarily of very high degree. We therefore think of
5
as addressing
2
in the setting of low-degree regular covers.

We also use
4
to exhibit a method for identifying when pairs of covers produced from Sunada’s construction are unmarked simple length non-isospectral. Sunada’s construction begins with a surjective homomorphism of a closed surface group onto a finite group, from which one finds finite covers by pulling back a pair of almost conjugate subgroups. We exhibit a general strategy for identifying when these covers arising from Sunada’s construction satisfy the hypotheses of
4
, and are therefore generically not simple length isospectral over the original surface. We use this method to produce three distinct infinite families of such pairs. This provides a vast generalization of the work of Maungchang
[
Mau13
]
, producing infinitely many new examples of pairs of isospectral surfaces with distinct simple length spectra, and proving
1
.

Identifying and controlling the presence of pairs of curves which have the same geodesic length in any hyperbolic metric presents a major challenge when attempting to distinguish covers by their simple length spectra. Such curves were shown to exist and studied extensively by Horowitz
[
Hor72
]
. Leininger
[
Lei03
]
proved that any such pair has the property that the modulus of their traces must coincide under any representation
\displaystyle\pi_{1}\left\lparen S\right\rparen\xrightarrow{}\mathrm{SL}_{2}\left\lparen\mathbb{C}\right\rparen
. Indeed, the existence of such curves is a phenomenon that arises from trace identities specific to
\displaystyle\mathrm{SL}_{2}\left\lparen\mathbb{C}\right\rparen
. The existence (or non-existence) of words in the free group
\displaystyle F_{2}
on two generators for which the traces coincide in any representation to some
\displaystyle\mathrm{SL}_{n}\left\lparen\mathbb{C}\right\rparen
with
\displaystyle n\geq 3
remains a significant open question
[
HT10
]
.

Organization of paper

In

2.1.
Curves and their elevations

By a
(closed) curve
on a surface
\displaystyle S
, we mean an equivalence class of continuous maps from the circle
\displaystyle\mathbb{S}^{1}
to
\displaystyle S
, where two such maps are considered equivalent if one is freely homotopic to a reparametrization of the other. Note that with this definition, curves are not oriented. A curve
\displaystyle\gamma
on a surface
\displaystyle S
is:

(i)

essential
if it has no freely nullhomotopic representative, and
inessential
otherwise;

(ii)

primitive
if none of its representatives factors through a non-trivial finite-degree covering map of the circle
\displaystyle\mathbb{S}^{1}
, and
non-primitive
otherwise; and

(iii)

simple
if one of its representatives is injective, and
non-simple
otherwise.

We will implicitly assume that all curves under consideration are essential. Under this assumption, all simple curves are primitive.

Now consider a finite-degree cover
\displaystyle p\colon X\xrightarrow{}S
. A curve
\displaystyle\alpha
on
\displaystyle X
is an
elevation
of a curve
\displaystyle\gamma
on
\displaystyle S
along
\displaystyle p
of degree
\displaystyle d\geq 1
if there are parametrized representatives such that
\displaystyle p\circ\alpha
traces
\displaystyle d
times along
\displaystyle\gamma
. More formally,
\displaystyle\alpha
is an elevation of
\displaystyle\gamma
along
\displaystyle p
of degree
\displaystyle d
if there are parametrized representatives so that the following square commutes:

\displaystyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{S}^{1}}
\displaystyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
\displaystyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{S}^{1}}
\displaystyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces S}
\displaystyle\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
\displaystyle\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha}
\displaystyle\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p}
\displaystyle\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\gamma}

where
\displaystyle f\colon\mathbb{S}^{1}\xrightarrow{}\mathbb{S}^{1}
is a covering map of degree
\displaystyle d
. Note that the degree of an elevation is bounded above by the degree of the cover
\displaystyle p\colon X\xrightarrow{}S
.

The
deck group
of a finite-degree cover
\displaystyle p\colon X\xrightarrow{}S
is the group of homeomorphisms of
\displaystyle X
which cover the identity homeomorphism of
\displaystyle S
; that is,

\mathrm{Deck}\left\lparen p\right\rparen\coloneqq\left\{\varphi\in\mathrm{Homeo}\left\lparen X\right\rparen:p\circ\varphi=p\right\}.

\displaystyle\mathrm{Deck}\left\lparen p\right\rparen

\displaystyle p
and so is a finite group with
\displaystyle\left\lvert\mathrm{Deck}\left\lparen p\right\rparen\right\rvert\leq\deg\left\lparen p\right\rparen
. When
\displaystyle\left\lvert\mathrm{Deck}\left\lparen p\right\rparen\right\rvert=\deg\left\lparen p\right\rparen
, then
\displaystyle\mathrm{Deck}\left\lparen p\right\rparen
also acts transitively on each fiber. In this case, the covering map
\displaystyle p\colon X\xrightarrow{}S
is the quotient associated to this action and is called
regular
(these covers are also sometimes called

or

\displaystyle X
induces an action on the set of degree
\displaystyle d

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle S
.

A choice of basepoint
\displaystyle s\in S
yields a bijection between the set of curves on
\displaystyle S
and the set of equivalence classes of non-identity elements of the fundamental group
\displaystyle\pi_{1}\left\lparen S,s\right\rparen

are considered equivalent if either
\displaystyle\alpha
and
\displaystyle\beta
are conjugate or
\displaystyle\alpha
and
\displaystyle\beta^{-1}
are conjugate. From this perspective, primitive curves correspond to equivalence classes of elements which cannot be written non-trivially as a power of another element.

Moreover, choices of basepoints
\displaystyle x\in X

\displaystyle s\in S
so that
\displaystyle p\left\lparen x\right\rparen=s
give an alternate characterization of elevations of degree
\displaystyle d

\displaystyle\alpha\in\pi_{1}\left\lparen X,x\right\rparen
and
\displaystyle\gamma\in\pi_{1}\left\lparen S,s\right\rparen
which cannot be written non-trivially as a power of another element, the primitive curve on
\displaystyle X

\displaystyle\alpha
is an elevation of the primitive curve on
\displaystyle S
represented by
\displaystyle\gamma
along
\displaystyle p
of degree
\displaystyle d
if and only if
\displaystyle p_{*}\left\lparen\alpha\right\rparen

\displaystyle\pi_{1}\left\lparen S,s\right\rparen

\displaystyle\gamma^{d}

\displaystyle\gamma^{-d}

\displaystyle p_{*}\colon\pi_{1}\left\lparen X,x\right\rparen\xhookrightarrow{}\pi_{1}\left\lparen S,s\right\rparen

2.3.
The Thurston compactification of Teichmüller space

Both the Teichmüller space
\displaystyle\mathscr{T}\left\lparen S\right\rparen
and the curve complex
\displaystyle\mathscr{C}\left\lparen S\right\rparen
(which will be introduced in
Section 2.4
) of a surface
\displaystyle S
admit compactifications whose boundary points may be described in the language of geodesic laminations. A
geodesic lamination
on a hyperbolic surface
\displaystyle S
is a compact subset of
\displaystyle S
which is the union of pairwise disjoint simple geodesics (some, all, or none of which may be closed) called
leaves
. We note that geodesic laminations may also be defined independently of a reference hyperbolic metric on
\displaystyle S
.

A
transverse measure
on a geodesic lamination
\displaystyle\lambda
is a positive real-valued function on the set of arcs transverse to
\displaystyle\lambda
which is invariant under leaf-preserving isotopy and absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure. A
measured lamination
on
\displaystyle S
is a pair
\displaystyle\left\lparen\lambda,\mu\right\rparen
, where
\displaystyle\lambda
is a geodesic lamination on
\displaystyle S
and
\displaystyle\mu
is a tranverse measure on
\displaystyle\lambda
. We will denote by
\displaystyle\mathcal{ML}\left\lparen S\right\rparen
the space of measured laminations on
\displaystyle S
topologized by the weak* topology.

Two measured laminations on
\displaystyle S
are called
projectively equivalent
if they have the same underlying geodesic lamination and their transverse measures differ by multiplication by a positive constant. A projective equivalence class of measured laminations is called a
projective measured lamination
. We will denote the space of such projective measured laminations by
\displaystyle\mathcal{PML}\left\lparen S\right\rparen
, which is homeomorphic to a sphere of dimension
\displaystyle 6g-7
. Thurston
[
Thu82
, Theorem 5.3]
introduced a compactification
\displaystyle\overline{\mathscr{T}\left\lparen S\right\rparen}
of Teichmüller space, now known as the
Thurston compactification
, in which
\displaystyle\overline{\mathscr{T}\left\lparen S\right\rparen}\setminus\mathscr{T}\left\lparen S\right\rparen=\mathcal{PML}\left\lparen S\right\rparen
. For this reason,
\displaystyle\mathcal{PML}\left\lparen S\right\rparen
is called the
Thurston boundary
of Teichmüller space.

Many of the relevant properties of the Teichmüller space extend naturally to the Thurston boundary and Thurston compactification. For example, the mapping class group
\displaystyle\mathrm{Mod}\left\lparen S\right\rparen
acts naturally on
\displaystyle\mathcal{PML}\left\lparen S\right\rparen

\displaystyle\mathrm{Mod}\left\lparen S\right\rparen

\displaystyle\mathscr{T}\left\lparen S\right\rparen

\displaystyle\mathrm{Mod}\left\lparen S\right\rparen

\displaystyle\overline{\mathscr{T}\left\lparen S\right\rparen}=\mathscr{T}\left\lparen S\right\rparen\sqcup\mathcal{PML}\left\lparen S\right\rparen

and
\displaystyle\beta
among all parametrized representatives. When
\displaystyle\alpha=\beta
, this number
\displaystyle i\left\lparen\alpha,\alpha\right\rparen
is called the
self-intersection number
. A curve
\displaystyle\alpha
on
\displaystyle S
is simple if and only if
\displaystyle i\left\lparen\alpha,\alpha\right\rparen=0
, and a pair of curves
\displaystyle\alpha
and
\displaystyle\beta
on
\displaystyle S
are called
disjoint
if
\displaystyle i\left\lparen\alpha,\beta\right\rparen=0
.

A choice of parametrized representatives for a set of curves on
\displaystyle S
is said to be in
minimal position
if every representative achieves its self-intersection number and every pair of representatives achieves its geometric intersection number. It is well-known that any set of closed geodesics on a hyperbolic surface are in minimal position. In particular, this implies that every set of curves on
\displaystyle S
has a set of parametrized representatives in minimal position.

The
curve complex

\displaystyle\mathscr{C}\left\lparen S\right\rparen
of a closed surface
\displaystyle S
of genus
\displaystyle g\geq 2
is a flag simplicial complex introduced by Harvey
[
Har81
]
. The simplices in
\displaystyle\mathscr{C}\left\lparen S\right\rparen
correspond to
multi-curves
on
\displaystyle S
, which are collections of pairwise disjoint simple curves on
\displaystyle S
; that is, the vertices of
\displaystyle\mathscr{C}\left\lparen S\right\rparen
are simple curves on
\displaystyle S
and a set of vertices span a simplex if they are pairwise disjoint. There is a natural simplicial action of the mapping class group
\displaystyle\mathrm{Mod}\left\lparen S\right\rparen
on
\displaystyle\mathscr{C}\left\lparen S\right\rparen
. Moreover,
\displaystyle\mathscr{C}\left\lparen S\right\rparen
may be equipped with a metric
\displaystyle d_{S}
so that every simplex is a regular Euclidean simplex of side length
\displaystyle 1
. Harvey
[
Har81
]
observed that the
\displaystyle 1
-skeleton of
\displaystyle\mathscr{C}\left\lparen S\right\rparen
is a connected metric graph.

Masur–Minsky
[
MM99
, Theorem 1.1]
proved that, considered with the metric
\displaystyle d_{S}
, the curve complex
\displaystyle\mathscr{C}\left\lparen S\right\rparen
is a Gromov hyperbolic geodesic space, and Klarreich
[
Kla18
]
constructed an explicit parametrization of its Gromov boundary by the space
\displaystyle\mathcal{EL}\left\lparen S\right\rparen
of ending laminations of
\displaystyle S
, which we define in
Section 2.5
.

As proven by Rafi–Schleimer
[
RS09
]
, finite-degree covers of surfaces give rise to quasi-isometric embeddings of their curve complexes. Specifically, given a finite-degree cover
\displaystyle p\colon X\xrightarrow{}S
, we define
\displaystyle\widetilde{p}\colon\mathscr{C}\left\lparen S\right\rparen\xrightarrow{}\mathscr{C}\left\lparen X\right\rparen
so that for each simple curve
\displaystyle\gamma\in\mathscr{C}\left\lparen S\right\rparen
,
\displaystyle\widetilde{p}\left\lparen\gamma\right\rparen
is a choice of elevation of
\displaystyle\gamma
along
\displaystyle p
to
\displaystyle X
. At first glance, our definition of
\displaystyle\widetilde{p}
appears to rely significantly on a choice of elevation for every simple curve on
\displaystyle S
and thus its properties will not be well behaved when defined as described. However, any two elevations of a simple curve
\displaystyle\gamma
along
\displaystyle p
are disjoint on
\displaystyle X
, since any intersection between distinct elevations on
\displaystyle X
would descend to a self-intersection of
\displaystyle\gamma
on
\displaystyle S
. Thus different choices of elevations for
\displaystyle\widetilde{p}
yield maps which have uniformly bounded distance at most
\displaystyle 1
, and
\displaystyle\widetilde{p}\colon\mathscr{C}\left\lparen S\right\rparen\xrightarrow{}\mathscr{C}\left\lparen X\right\rparen
is said to be
coarsely well-defined
.

Theorem 2.2

(
[
RS09
, Theorem 7.1]
)
.

If
\displaystyle p\colon X\xrightarrow{}S
is a finite-degree cover of a closed, orientable surface
\displaystyle S
of negative Euler characteristic, then
\displaystyle\widetilde{p}\colon\mathscr{C}\left\lparen S\right\rparen\xrightarrow{}\mathscr{C}\left\lparen X\right\rparen
is a quasi-isometric embedding.

Note that in
[
RS09
]
, the above theorem is formulated in terms of the many-to-one relation which associates a simple curve on the base surface to its full pre-image on the cover, which is a multi-curve. Since a multi-curve has diameter at most
\displaystyle 1
in the curve complex, this distinction will not matter for our purposes.

2.5.
The Gromov boundary of the curve complex

Recall that a geodesic lamination of a closed hyperbolic surface
\displaystyle S
is a closed subset of
\displaystyle S
foliated by simple geodesics, each of which is called a
leaf
. We will call a geodesic lamination on a surface
\displaystyle S
:

(i)

minimal
if each leaf is dense in
\displaystyle S
;

(ii)

filling
if every component of the complement of
\displaystyle\lambda
in
\displaystyle S
is simply connected; and

(iii)

ending
if
\displaystyle\lambda
is both minimal and filling.

Note that these properties are well-defined irrespective of the choice of hyperbolic metric and that, necessarily, a minimal geodesic lamination cannot contain a closed leaf.

The space
\displaystyle\mathcal{EL}\left\lparen S\right\rparen
of ending laminations on
\displaystyle S
is topologized as follows: Denote by
\displaystyle\mathcal{PMEL}\left\lparen S\right\rparen
the space of projective measured laminations whose underlying geodesic lamination is both minimal and filling. There is a natural surjective map
\displaystyle\left\lparen\>\cdot\>\right\rparen_{\mathrm{top}}\colon\mathcal{PMEL}\left\lparen S\right\rparen\xrightarrow{}\mathcal{EL}\left\lparen S\right\rparen
defined by the formula
\displaystyle\left[\left\lparen\lambda,\mu\right\rparen\right]_{\mathrm{top}}=\lambda
; that is, this map sends a projective measured ending lamination to its underlying geodesic lamination. We equip
\displaystyle\mathcal{EL}\left\lparen S\right\rparen
with the quotient topology associated to this map. An equivalent characterization of this topology is described by Hamenstädt
[
Ham06
]
, in which it is called the
coarse Hausdorff topology
.

As previously mentioned, Klarreich
[
Kla18
]
parametrized the Gromov boundary of the curve complex
\displaystyle\mathscr{C}\left\lparen S\right\rparen
of a closed surface
\displaystyle S
of negative Euler characteristic by the space
\displaystyle\mathcal{EL}\left\lparen S\right\rparen
of ending laminations.

Theorem 2.3

(
[
Kla18
, Theorem 1.3]
)
.

There is a natural homeomorphism
\displaystyle\Lambda_{S}\colon\mathscr{C}\left\lparen S\right\rparen\sqcup\partial\mathscr{C}\left\lparen S\right\rparen\xrightarrow{}\mathscr{C}\left\lparen S\right\rparen\sqcup\mathcal{EL}\left\lparen S\right\rparen

\displaystyle\mathscr{C}\left\lparen S\right\rparen

\displaystyle\partial\mathscr{C}\left\lparen S\right\rparen
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{EL}\left\lparen S\right\rparen
.

Recall that a finite-degree cover
\displaystyle p\colon X\xrightarrow{}S
induces a coarsely well-defined quasi-isometric embedding
\displaystyle\widetilde{p}\colon\mathscr{C}\left\lparen S\right\rparen\xrightarrow{}\mathscr{C}\left\lparen X\right\rparen
. This in turn induces a continuous embedding
\displaystyle\widetilde{p}\colon\partial\mathscr{C}\left\lparen S\right\rparen\xhookrightarrow{}\partial\mathscr{C}\left\lparen X\right\rparen

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle\lambda\in\mathcal{EL}\left\lparen S\right\rparen

2.6.
The simple length spectrum and Sunada’s construction

The
(unmarked) length spectrum
(resp.
simple length spectrum
) of a closed hyperbolic surface
\displaystyle X
is the multi-set of lengths of closed geodesics (resp. simple closed geodesics), counted with multiplicity. We will call two such surfaces
length isospectral
(resp.
simple length isospectral
) if they have the same length spectrum (resp. simple length spectrum).

Most known examples of length isospectral surfaces arise from a construction due to Sunada
[
Sun85
]
, which we now describe. Sunada’s construction relies on a surjective homomorphism
\displaystyle\rho\colon\pi_{1}\left\lparen S,s\right\rparen\twoheadrightarrow G
, called the
constructing representation
, of a closed hyperbolic surface group
\displaystyle\pi_{1}\left\lparen S,s\right\rparen
onto a finite group
\displaystyle G
and a pair of subgroups
\displaystyle A,B\leq G
, called the
constructing subgroups
. The pre-images
\displaystyle\rho^{-1}\left\lparen A\right\rparen
and
\displaystyle\rho^{-1}\left\lparen B\right\rparen
are finite-index subgroups of
\displaystyle\pi_{1}\left\lparen S,s\right\rparen
and so the Galois correspondence between subgroups of
\displaystyle\pi_{1}\left\lparen S,s\right\rparen
and based covers of
\displaystyle\left\lparen S,s\right\rparen
yields the commutative diagram of finite-degree based covers drawn in
Figure 1
.

\displaystyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p_{*}\left\lparen\pi_{1}\left\lparen X,x\right\rparen\right\rparen}
\displaystyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces q_{*}\left\lparen\pi_{1}\left\lparen Y,y\right\rparen\right\rparen}

. Note that since
\displaystyle\mathrm{ker}\left\lparen\rho\right\rparen

\displaystyle\pi_{1}\left\lparen S,s\right\rparen

\displaystyle\pi_{S}\colon Z\xrightarrow{}S

\displaystyle\pi_{X}
and
\displaystyle\pi_{Y}

\displaystyle\mathrm{Deck}\left\lparen\pi_{X}\right\rparen\cong A

.

The constructing subgroups
\displaystyle A,B\leq G
of the finite group
\displaystyle G
are called
almost conjugate
(or
Gassmann equivalent
) if they contain the same number of elements of every conjugacy class in
\displaystyle G
; that is,
\displaystyle A
and
\displaystyle B
are almost conjugate in
\displaystyle G
if
\displaystyle\left\lvert A\cap C\right\rvert=\left\lvert B\cap C\right\rvert
for every conjugacy class
\displaystyle C\subseteq G
. Note that two conjugate subgroups of
\displaystyle G
are almost conjugate, but almost conjugate subgroups need not be conjugate in
\displaystyle G
.

Theorem 2.4

(
[
Sun85
]
)
.

Let
\displaystyle p\colon X\xrightarrow{}S
and
\displaystyle q\colon Y\xrightarrow{}S
be the finite-degree covers of a closed surface
\displaystyle S
of negative Euler characteristic associated to a surjective homomorphism
\displaystyle\rho\colon\pi_{1}\left\lparen S,s\right\rparen\twoheadrightarrow G
and a pair of subgroups
\displaystyle A,B\leq G
as described above.

1.

If
\displaystyle A
and
\displaystyle B
are almost conjugate in
\displaystyle G
, then for every
\displaystyle\mu\in\mathscr{T}\left\lparen S\right\rparen
,
\displaystyle p^{*}\left\lparen\mu\right\rparen
and
\displaystyle q^{*}\left\lparen\mu\right\rparen
are length isospectral; and

2.

If
\displaystyle A
and
\displaystyle B
are almost conjugate but not conjugate in
\displaystyle G
, then for a generic
\displaystyle\mu\in\mathscr{T}\left\lparen S\right\rparen
,
\displaystyle p^{*}\left\lparen\mu\right\rparen
and
\displaystyle q^{*}\left\lparen\mu\right\rparen
are not isometric.

The first part of Sunada’s theorem can be stated in more general contexts involving actions of finite groups on complete and connected Riemannian manifolds by isometries with at most finitely many fixed points. A proof of this more general form (which implies the first part of
2.4
) can be found in
[
Bus10
, 291-294]
.

Fix two finite-degree covers
\displaystyle p\colon X\xrightarrow{}S
and
\displaystyle q\colon Y\xrightarrow{}S
of a closed, orientable surface
\displaystyle S
of negative Euler characteristic. For a given
\displaystyle\mu\in\mathscr{T}\left\lparen S\right\rparen
on
\displaystyle S
, we will say that
\displaystyle X
and
\displaystyle Y
are
simple length isospectral over
\displaystyle S
if
\displaystyle\left\lparen X,p^{*}\left\lparen\mu\right\rparen\right\rparen
and
\displaystyle\left\lparen Y,q^{*}\left\lparen\mu\right\rparen\right\rparen
are simple length isospectral. The manifold structure on
\displaystyle\mathscr{T}\left\lparen S\right\rparen
(or equivalently, the Weil–Petersson measure on
\displaystyle\mathscr{T}\left\lparen S\right\rparen
) gives a notion of genericness for these kinds of conditions; we say that
\displaystyle X
and
\displaystyle Y
generically have a certain property over
\displaystyle S
if the set of all
\displaystyle\mu\in\mathscr{T}\left\lparen S\right\rparen
for which
\displaystyle\left\lparen X,p^{*}\left\lparen\mu\right\rparen\right\rparen
and
\displaystyle\left\lparen Y,q^{*}\left\lparen\mu\right\rparen\right\rparen
do not have that property has null measure in
\displaystyle\mathscr{T}\left\lparen S\right\rparen
. In this language, we can rephrase
2.4
as saying that when the constructing subgroups are almost conjugate but not conjugate, the resulting covers are isospectral but generically not isometric.

2.7.
Trace-commensurability in free and surface groups

The
character
of an element
\displaystyle\alpha
in a group
\displaystyle\Gamma
is the complex-valued function
\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left\lparen\alpha\right\rparen
on the space
\displaystyle\mathrm{hom}\left\lparen\Gamma,\mathrm{SL}_{2}\left\lparen\mathbb{C}\right\rparen\right\rparen
of linear representations of
\displaystyle\Gamma
in
\displaystyle\mathrm{SL}_{2}\left\lparen\mathbb{C}\right\rparen
defined by the formula
\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left\lparen\alpha\right\rparen\left\lparen\rho\right\rparen=\mathrm{tr}\left\lparen\rho\left\lparen\alpha\right\rparen\right\rparen
. We will call two elements
\displaystyle\alpha,\beta\in\Gamma

trace-commensurable
if there are non-zero integers
\displaystyle m,n
so that
\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left\lparen\alpha^{m}\right\rparen^{2}=\mathrm{tr}\left\lparen\beta^{n}\right\rparen^{2}
as functions on
\displaystyle\mathrm{hom}\left\lparen\Gamma,\mathrm{SL}_{2}\left\lparen\mathbb{C}\right\rparen\right\rparen
. More explicitly,
\displaystyle\alpha
and
\displaystyle\beta
are trace-commensurable if
\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left\lparen\rho\left\lparen\alpha^{m}\right\rparen\right\rparen^{2}=\mathrm{tr}\left\lparen\rho\left\lparen\beta^{n}\right\rparen\right\rparen^{2}
for all linear representations
\displaystyle\rho\colon\Gamma\xrightarrow{}\mathrm{SL}_{2}\left\lparen\mathbb{C}\right\rparen
.

This equivalence relation on
\displaystyle\Gamma
is a natural generalization of the notion of
trace equivalence
defined in
[
Lei03
]
, in which
\displaystyle\Gamma
is a closed hyperbolic surface group and
\displaystyle m=n=1
.

Observation 2.5
.

Consider two elements
\displaystyle\alpha
and
\displaystyle\beta
of a group
\displaystyle\Gamma
.

(i)

If
\displaystyle\alpha
and
\displaystyle\beta
are trace-commensurable, then there are non-zero integers
\displaystyle m
and
\displaystyle n
such that
\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left\lparen\alpha^{m}\right\rparen^{2}=\mathrm{tr}\left\lparen\beta^{n}\right\rparen^{2}
. However, the trace relation

\mathrm{tr}\left\lparen\mathbf{A}\right\rparen\cdot\mathrm{tr}\left\lparen\mathbf{B}\right\rparen=\mathrm{tr}\left\lparen\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\right\rparen+\mathrm{tr}\left\lparen\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}^{-1}\right\rparen

for matrices
\displaystyle\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}\left\lparen\mathbb{C}\right\rparen
implies that

\mathrm{tr}\left\lparen\alpha^{2m}\right\rparen=\mathrm{tr}\left\lparen\alpha^{m}\right\rparen^{2}-2=\mathrm{tr}\left\lparen\beta^{n}\right\rparen^{2}-2=\mathrm{tr}\left\lparen\beta^{2n}\right\rparen.

(ii)

Suppose that
\displaystyle\alpha
and
\displaystyle\beta
are trace-commensurable, so that there are non-zero integers
\displaystyle m
and
\displaystyle n
such that
\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left\lparen\alpha^{m}\right\rparen^{2}=\mathrm{tr}\left\lparen\beta^{n}\right\rparen^{2}
. Let
\displaystyle\varphi\colon\Gamma\xrightarrow{}\Delta
be a group homomorphism and note that for any linear representation
\displaystyle\rho\colon\Delta\xrightarrow{}\mathrm{SL}_{2}\left\lparen\mathbb{C}\right\rparen
of
\displaystyle\Delta
, the composition
\displaystyle\rho\circ\varphi\colon\Gamma\xrightarrow{}\mathrm{SL}_{2}\left\lparen\mathbb{C}\right\rparen
is a linear representation of
\displaystyle\Gamma
, which implies that

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left\lparen\rho\left\lparen\varphi\left\lparen\alpha\right\rparen^{m}\right\rparen\right\rparen^{2}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}\left\lparen\rho\left\lparen\varphi\left\lparen\alpha^{m}\right\rparen\right\rparen\right\rparen^{2}=\mathrm{tr}\left\lparen\left\lparen\rho\circ\varphi\right\rparen\left\lparen\alpha^{m}\right\rparen\right\rparen^{2}=\mathrm{tr}\left\lparen\left\lparen\rho\circ\varphi\right\rparen\left\lparen\alpha^{m}\right\rparen^{2}\right\rparen

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}\left\lparen\left\lparen\rho\circ\varphi\right\rparen\left\lparen\beta^{n}\right\rparen^{2}\right\rparen=\mathrm{tr}\left\lparen\left\lparen\rho\circ\varphi\right\rparen\left\lparen\beta^{n}\right\rparen\right\rparen^{2}=\mathrm{tr}\left\lparen\rho\left\lparen\varphi\left\lparen\beta^{n}\right\rparen\right\rparen\right\rparen^{2}=\mathrm{tr}\left\lparen\rho\left\lparen\varphi\left\lparen\beta\right\rparen^{n}\right\rparen\right\rparen^{2}.

Thus
\displaystyle\varphi\left\lparen\alpha\right\rparen
and
\displaystyle\varphi\left\lparen\beta\right\rparen
are trace-commensurable as elements of
\displaystyle\Delta
, with the same commensurability constants.

We will call two curves
\displaystyle\alpha
and
\displaystyle\beta
on
\displaystyle S

(hyperbolic) length-commensurable
if there are positive integers
\displaystyle m,n\geq 1
such that

m\cdot\ell_{\mu}\left\lparen\alpha\right\rparen=n\cdot\ell_{\mu}\left\lparen\beta\right\rparen,

for all points
\displaystyle\mu\in\mathscr{T}\left\lparen S\right\rparen
. This equivalence relation on the space of currents is a natural generalization of the notion of
hyperbolic equivalence
of curves as in
[
Lei03
]
, in which
\displaystyle\alpha
and
\displaystyle\beta
are primitive curves and
\displaystyle m=n=1
. Leininger gave an equivalent characterization in terms of certain characters of representations of the fundamental group, which we now present.

Fix a basepoint
\displaystyle s\in S
on a closed, orientable surface
\displaystyle S
of negative Euler characteristic, and suppose that two elements
\displaystyle\alpha,\beta\in\pi_{1}\left\lparen S,s\right\rparen
are trace-commensurable, so that there are non-zero integers
\displaystyle m,n
such that
\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left\lparen\rho\left\lparen\alpha\right\rparen^{m}\right\rparen^{2}=\mathrm{tr}\left\lparen\rho\left\lparen\beta\right\rparen^{n}\right\rparen^{2}
for all linear representations
\displaystyle\rho\colon\pi_{1}\left\lparen S,s\right\rparen\xrightarrow{}\mathrm{SL}_{2}\left\lparen\mathbb{C}\right\rparen
. Note that any point
\displaystyle\mu\in\mathscr{T}\left\lparen S\right\rparen
is represented by a discrete and faithful representation
\displaystyle\pi_{1}\left\lparen S,s\right\rparen\xrightarrow{}\mathrm{PSL}_{2}\left\lparen\mathbb{R}\right\rparen
, which lifts to a linear representation
\displaystyle\pi_{1}\left\lparen S,s\right\rparen\xrightarrow{}\mathrm{SL}_{2}\left\lparen\mathbb{R}\right\rparen\subsetneq\mathrm{SL}_{2}\left\lparen\mathbb{C}\right\rparen
. Thus

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left\lvert m\right\rvert\cdot\ell_{\mu}\left\lparen\alpha\right\rparen

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\ell_{\mu}\left\lparen\alpha^{m}\right\rparen=2\mathrm{cosh}^{-1}\left\lparen\frac{\left\lvert\mathrm{tr}\left\lparen\rho\left\lparen\alpha^{m}\right\rparen\right\rparen\right\rvert}{2}\right\rparen

\displaystyle\displaystyle=2\mathrm{cosh}^{-1}\left\lparen\frac{\left\lvert\mathrm{tr}\left\lparen\rho\left\lparen\beta^{n}\right\rparen\right\rparen\right\rvert}{2}\right\rparen=\ell_{\mu}\left\lparen\beta^{n}\right\rparen=\left\lvert n\right\rvert\cdot\ell_{\mu}\left\lparen\beta\right\rparen.

In summary, the above argument shows that trace-commensurable elements of
\displaystyle\pi_{1}\left\lparen S,s\right\rparen
represent length-commensurable curves on
\displaystyle S
. The following theorem of Leininger provides a converse. Note that Leininger considers the slightly more restrictive relationships of trace equivalence and hyperbolic equivalence mentioned above.

Theorem 2.6

(
[
Lei03
, Theorem 1.4]
)
.

Fix a basepoint
\displaystyle s\in S
on a closed, orientable surface
\displaystyle S
of negative Euler characteristic. Two elements
\displaystyle\alpha,\beta\in\pi_{1}\left\lparen S,s\right\rparen
are trace-commensurable in
\displaystyle\pi_{1}\left\lparen S,s\right\rparen
if and only if the curves
\displaystyle\left[\alpha\right]
and
\displaystyle\left[\beta\right]
they represent are length-commensurable.

Moreover, for any two trace-commensurable elements
\displaystyle\alpha,\beta\in\pi_{1}\left\lparen S,s\right\rparen
representing primitive curves and any simple curve
\displaystyle\gamma
on
\displaystyle S
,
\displaystyle i\left\lparen\left[\alpha\right],\gamma\right\rparen=0
if and only if
\displaystyle i\left\lparen\left[\beta\right],\gamma\right\rparen=0
.

Consider elements
\displaystyle\alpha
and
\displaystyle\beta
of a group
\displaystyle\Gamma
. If some non-zero powers
\displaystyle\alpha^{m}
and
\displaystyle\beta^{n}
are conjugate in
\displaystyle\Gamma
, then the conjugation-invariance of the trace implies that
\displaystyle\alpha
and
\displaystyle\beta
are trace-commensurable. However, not all pairs of trace-commensurable elements need be of this form. We will call such a pair of elements, which are trace-commensurable but not for the aforementioned reason,
trace twins
.

It may be surprising to learn that examples of trace twins exist in many groups, including free groups and surface groups. For example, consider the free group
\displaystyle F_{2}=\left\langle a,b\mid\right\rangle
on two generators
\displaystyle a
and
\displaystyle b
. Horowitz computes
[
Hor72
, Example 5.2]
that

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left\lparen\rho\left\lparen a^{2}b^{-1}ab\right\rparen\right\rparen^{2}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}\left\lparen\rho\left\lparen a^{2}bab^{-1}\right\rparen\right\rparen^{2}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left\lparen\rho\left\lparen ab^{2}a^{2}b\right\rparen\right\rparen^{2}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}\left\lparen\rho\left\lparen aba^{2}b^{2}\right\rparen\right\rparen^{2},

under any representation
\displaystyle\rho\colon F_{2}\xrightarrow{}\mathrm{SL}_{2}\left\lparen\mathbb{C}\right\rparen
, but no powers of these elements are conjugate to each other. Thus
\displaystyle a^{2}b^{-1}ab
and
\displaystyle a^{2}bab^{-1}
are trace twins in
\displaystyle F_{2}
and so are
\displaystyle ab^{2}a^{2}b
and
\displaystyle aba^{2}b^{2}
.

By
2.6
above, trace twins in a surface group yield homotopically distinct curves on the surface whose lengths are commensurable in any hyperbolic metric. As described in greater detail in
Section 4
, we will call such a pair of curves
length twins
. We will find it very useful to find elements in a group which have no trace twins. Horowitz gives useful characterizations of such elements in free groups and uses them to build a wealth of examples.

Theorem 2.7

(
[
Hor72
, Corollary 7.2]
)
.

Let
\displaystyle a,b\in F
be primitive elements of a free group
\displaystyle F
. For any integers
\displaystyle j,k\in\mathbb{Z}
and element
\displaystyle u\in F
, if
\displaystyle a^{j}b^{k}
and
\displaystyle u
are trace equivalent in
\displaystyle F
, then
\displaystyle u
is conjugate in
\displaystyle F
to either
\displaystyle a^{j}b^{k}
or its inverse.

In
Section 4.1
, we extend Horowitz’s original argument to show that elements of the form
\displaystyle a^{j}b^{k}
have no trace twins in
\displaystyle F
(this involves dealing with commensurability constants other than
\displaystyle 1
), and then derive a consequence for the lengths of certain curves on surfaces represented by such elements.

3.1.
Uniquely ergodic ending laminations

A geodesic lamination is
uniquely ergodic
if it admits a single projective equivalence class of transverse measures. Masur’s criterion provides a sufficient condition for the endpoint of a Teichmüller geodesic to be uniquely ergodic based on recurrence to the thick part of Teichmüller space, which we now define: given a positive real number
\displaystyle\epsilon>0
, the
\displaystyle\epsilon
-thick part
of
\displaystyle\mathscr{T}\left\lparen X\right\rparen
is the subset corresponding to metrics with injectivity radius strictly greater than
\displaystyle\epsilon
. In other words, a point
\displaystyle\left[\mu\right]\in\mathscr{T}\left\lparen X\right\rparen
is in the
\displaystyle\epsilon
-thick part if
\displaystyle\left\lparen X,\mu\right\rparen
has no closed geodesic with length at most
\displaystyle\epsilon
.

Theorem 3.1

(
[
Mas92
]
)
.

Let
\displaystyle R
be a geodesic ray in
\displaystyle\mathscr{T}\left\lparen X\right\rparen
with endpoint
\displaystyle\lambda\in\mathcal{PML}\left\lparen X\right\rparen
. If there is a positive real number
\displaystyle\epsilon>0
such that
\displaystyle R
recurs infinitely often to the
\displaystyle\epsilon
-thick part of
\displaystyle\mathscr{T}\left\lparen X\right\rparen
, then the underlying lamination
\displaystyle\lambda_{\mathrm{top}}
is uniquely ergodic.

The following result of Lindenstrauss–Mirzakhani implies that the mapping class group orbit of any uniquely ergodic measured lamination is dense in the space of measured laminations.

Theorem 3.2

(
[
LM08
, Theorem 8.9]
)
.

A measured lamination
\displaystyle\lambda\in\mathcal{ML}\left\lparen X\right\rparen
on a closed, orientable surface
\displaystyle X
of negative Euler characteristic has dense
\displaystyle\mathrm{Mod}\left\lparen X\right\rparen
-orbit if and only if its support contains no simple closed curves.

We now use the above two results to show that the space
\displaystyle\mathcal{PMEL}_{\epsilon}\left\lparen X\right\rparen
of projective measured ending laminations whose underlying laminations are the endpoints of Teichmüller geodesic rays in
\displaystyle\mathscr{T}\left\lparen X\right\rparen
which recur infinitely often to the
\displaystyle\epsilon
-thick part is dense in
\displaystyle\mathcal{PML}\left\lparen X\right\rparen
. We will use this in the proof of
3
.

Lemma 3.3
.

For any closed, orientable surface
\displaystyle X
of negative Euler characteristic, there is a positive real number
\displaystyle\epsilon_{X}>0
such that
\displaystyle\mathcal{PMEL}_{\epsilon}\left\lparen X\right\rparen
is dense in
\displaystyle\mathcal{PML}\left\lparen X\right\rparen
for any
\displaystyle 0<\epsilon\leq\epsilon_{X}
.

Proof.

For any positive real number
\displaystyle\epsilon>0
, let
\displaystyle\mathcal{MEL}_{\epsilon}\left\lparen X\right\rparen\coloneqq F^{-1}\left\lparen\mathcal{PMEL}_{\epsilon}\left\lparen X\right\rparen\right\rparen
, where
\displaystyle F\colon\mathcal{ML}\left\lparen X\right\rparen\twoheadrightarrow\mathcal{PML}\left\lparen X\right\rparen
is the quotient map. We will first show that there exists
\displaystyle\epsilon_{X}>0
so that
\displaystyle\mathcal{MEL}_{\epsilon_{X}}\left\lparen X\right\rparen
is dense in
\displaystyle\mathcal{ML}\left\lparen X\right\rparen
.

Let
\displaystyle\phi\in\mathrm{Mod}\left\lparen X\right\rparen
be a pseudo-Anosov mapping class with stable lamination
\displaystyle\lambda_{\mathrm{top}}\in\mathcal{EL}\left\lparen X\right\rparen
. There is a positive real number
\displaystyle\epsilon_{X}>0
, which we fix for the remainder of this proof, so that the axis
\displaystyle R
of
\displaystyle\phi
in
\displaystyle\mathscr{T}\left\lparen X\right\rparen
is contained in the
\displaystyle\epsilon_{X}
-thick part of
\displaystyle\mathscr{T}\left\lparen X\right\rparen
. This implies that any measured lamination
\displaystyle\lambda
with underlying lamination
\displaystyle\lambda_{\mathrm{top}}
has the property that
\displaystyle F(\lambda)
lies in
\displaystyle\mathcal{PMEL}_{\epsilon_{X}}\left\lparen X\right\rparen
. Fix such a measured lamination
\displaystyle\lambda
.

Since
\displaystyle\mathrm{Mod}\left\lparen X\right\rparen
acts by isometries of the Teichmüller metric which preserve the
\displaystyle\epsilon_{X}
-thick part of
\displaystyle\mathscr{T}\left\lparen X\right\rparen
,
\displaystyle\mathcal{MEL}_{\epsilon_{X}}\left\lparen X\right\rparen
is invariant under this action. Indeed, for any mapping class
\displaystyle g\in\mathrm{Mod}\left\lparen X\right\rparen
,
\displaystyle F\left\lparen g\cdot\lambda\right\rparen=g\cdot F\left\lparen\lambda\right\rparen
is an endpoint of the Teichmüller geodesic
\displaystyle g\cdot R
, which is contained in the
\displaystyle\epsilon_{X}
-thick part of
\displaystyle\mathscr{T}\left\lparen X\right\rparen
. Thus,
\displaystyle\mathcal{MEL}_{\epsilon_{X}}\left\lparen X\right\rparen
contains the entire orbit
\displaystyle\mathrm{Mod}\left\lparen X\right\rparen\cdot\lambda
.

3.1
implies that the underlying lamination
\displaystyle\lambda_{\mathrm{top}}
of
\displaystyle\lambda
is uniquely ergodic, and so
3.2
implies that its orbit under the mapping class group
\displaystyle\mathrm{Mod}\left\lparen X\right\rparen
is dense in
\displaystyle\mathcal{ML}\left\lparen X\right\rparen
. Since
\displaystyle\mathcal{MEL}_{\epsilon_{X}}\left\lparen X\right\rparen
contains this orbit by the above argument, it is also dense in
\displaystyle\mathcal{ML}\left\lparen X\right\rparen
. Therefore, since
\displaystyle\mathcal{PMEL}_{\epsilon_{X}}\left\lparen X\right\rparen=F\left\lparen\mathcal{MEL}_{\epsilon_{X}}\left\lparen X\right\rparen\right\rparen
is the image of a dense subset under a continuous surjection, it is dense in
\displaystyle\mathcal{PML}\left\lparen X\right\rparen
. Finally, note that for any
\displaystyle 0<\epsilon\leq\epsilon_{X}
,
\displaystyle\mathcal{PMEL}_{\epsilon_{X}}\left\lparen X\right\rparen\subseteq\mathcal{PMEL}_{\epsilon}\left\lparen X\right\rparen
and so
\displaystyle\mathcal{PMEL}_{\epsilon}\left\lparen X\right\rparen
is also dense in
\displaystyle\mathcal{PML}\left\lparen X\right\rparen
, as desired. ∎

3.2.
The deck group of a finite-degree cover

The pullback along a finite-degree regular cover of a hyperbolic metric on the base is a hyperbolic metric on the total space for which all deck transformations are isometries. If the base is a closed surface of genus at least three, these deck transformations generically comprise the entire isometry group.

Lemma 3.4
.

For any finite-degree regular cover
\displaystyle\pi\colon Z\xrightarrow{}X
of a closed, orientable surface
\displaystyle X
of genus at least
\displaystyle 3
, there is a hyperbolic metric
\displaystyle\mu
on
\displaystyle X
such that
\displaystyle\mathrm{Isom}\left\lparen Z,\pi^{*}\mu\right\rparen=\mathrm{Deck}\left\lparen\pi\right\rparen
.

Proof.

For any hyperbolic metric
\displaystyle\mu
on
\displaystyle X
, it follows directly from the definitions of the deck group and the pullback metric that
\displaystyle\mathrm{Deck}\left\lparen\pi\right\rparen\leq\mathrm{Isom}\left\lparen Z,\pi^{*}\mu\right\rparen
. It therefore suffices to show there is a hyperbolic metric
\displaystyle\mu
on
\displaystyle X
so that the reverse containment holds.

Fix basepoints
\displaystyle x\in X
and
\displaystyle z\in Z
such that
\displaystyle\pi\left\lparen z\right\rparen=x
, and let
\displaystyle\rho_{\mu}\colon\pi_{1}\left\lparen X,x\right\rparen\xhookrightarrow{}\mathrm{PSL}_{2}\left\lparen\mathbb{R}\right\rparen
be an associated holonomy representation. Such a choice is well-defined up to conjugation by an element of
\displaystyle\mathrm{PGL}_{2}\left\lparen\mathbb{R}\right\rparen
. We will denote

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Gamma

\displaystyle\displaystyle\coloneqq\rho_{\mu}\left\lparen\pi_{1}\left\lparen X,x\right\rparen\right\rparen,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle\displaystyle\coloneqq\rho_{\pi^{*}\mu}\left\lparen\pi_{1}\left\lparen Z,z\right\rparen\right\rparen,

where
\displaystyle\rho_{\pi^{*}\mu}\coloneqq\rho_{\mu}\circ\pi_{*}\colon\pi_{1}\left\lparen Z,z\right\rparen\xhookrightarrow{}\mathrm{PSL}_{2}\left\lparen\mathbb{R}\right\rparen
is a holonomy representation of the cover
\displaystyle\left\lparen Z,\pi^{*}\mu\right\rparen
. Recall that the
commensurator
of
\displaystyle\Gamma
in
\displaystyle\mathrm{PSL}_{2}\left\lparen\mathbb{R}\right\rparen
is defined by

\mathrm{Comm}\left\lparen\Gamma\right\rparen\coloneqq\left\{A\in\mathrm{PSL}_{2}\left\lparen\mathbb{R}\right\rparen:\Gamma\cap A\Gamma A^{-1}\textrm{ has finite index in both }\Gamma\textrm{ and }A\Gamma A^{-1}\right\},

and that when
\displaystyle\Gamma
is non-arithmetic,
\displaystyle\mathrm{Comm}\left\lparen\Gamma\right\rparen
is a lattice, and the quotient
\displaystyle\mathbb{H}^{2}/\mathrm{Comm}\left\lparen\Gamma\right\rparen
is the unique minimal area representative in the commensurability class of
\displaystyle\Gamma
. Moreover, since
\displaystyle\Gamma\leq\mathrm{Comm}\left\lparen\Gamma\right\rparen
and
\displaystyle\mathrm{Comm}\left\lparen\Delta\right\rparen=\mathrm{Comm}\left\lparen\Gamma\right\rparen
, there are covering maps
\displaystyle\mathbb{H}^{2}/\Gamma\xrightarrow{}\mathbb{H}^{2}/\mathrm{Comm}\left\lparen\Gamma\right\rparen
and
\displaystyle\mathbb{H}^{2}/\Delta\xrightarrow{}\mathbb{H}^{2}/\mathrm{Comm}\left\lparen\Gamma\right\rparen
.

For the rest of the proof, we fix a metric
\displaystyle\mu
on
\displaystyle X
for which
\displaystyle\mathrm{Comm}\left\lparen\Gamma\right\rparen=\Gamma
. By
[
Gre63
, Theorems 2, 3A]
, the set of points in Teichmüller space represented by such metrics is a dense open subset of
\displaystyle\mathscr{T}\left\lparen X\right\rparen
when
\displaystyle X
has genus at least
\displaystyle 3
. Note that with this choice of
\displaystyle\mu
,
\displaystyle\Gamma
is necessarily non-arithmetic by the Margulis dichotomy
[
Mar75
]
, as otherwise
\displaystyle\mathrm{Comm}\left\lparen\Gamma\right\rparen
would be dense in
\displaystyle\mathrm{PSL}_{2}\left\lparen\mathbb{R}\right\rparen
.

Recall that
\displaystyle\mathrm{Isom}\left\lparen Z,\pi^{*}\mu\right\rparen
is canonically isomorphic to
\displaystyle N_{\mathrm{PSL}_{2}\left\lparen\mathbb{R}\right\rparen}\left\lparen\Delta\right\rparen/\Delta
, where

N_{\mathrm{PSL}_{2}\left\lparen\mathbb{R}\right\rparen}\left\lparen\Delta\right\rparen\coloneqq\left\{A\in\mathrm{PSL}_{2}\left\lparen\mathbb{R}\right\rparen:A\Delta A^{-1}=\Delta\right\},

is the normalizer of
\displaystyle\Delta
in
\displaystyle\mathrm{PSL}_{2}\left\lparen\mathbb{R}\right\rparen
. Note that

\Delta\leq N_{\mathrm{PSL}_{2}\left\lparen\mathbb{R}\right\rparen}\left\lparen\Delta\right\rparen\leq\mathrm{Comm}\left\lparen\Delta\right\rparen=\mathrm{Comm}\left\lparen\Gamma\right\rparen=\Gamma.

Since
\displaystyle\pi
is a regular cover,
\displaystyle\Delta
is normal in each of these groups, and so

\mathrm{Isom}\left\lparen Z,\pi^{*}\mu\right\rparen\cong\frac{N_{\mathrm{PSL}_{2}\left\lparen\mathbb{R}\right\rparen}\left\lparen\Delta\right\rparen}{\Delta}\leq\frac{\mathrm{Comm}\left\lparen\Gamma\right\rparen}{\Delta}=\frac{\Gamma}{\Delta}\cong\mathrm{Deck}\left\lparen\pi\right\rparen.

By the remark at the beginning of the proof, we see that for any such choice of
\displaystyle\mu
as above,
\displaystyle\mathrm{Isom}\left\lparen Z,\pi^{*}\mu\right\rparen=\mathrm{Deck}\left\lparen\pi\right\rparen
as required. ∎

We remark that the key reason for the hypothesis in
3.4
that
\displaystyle X
has genus at least
\displaystyle 3
is due to the hyper-elliptic involution of surfaces of genus
\displaystyle 2
. Specifically, due to the presence of this isometry, we cannot conclude that
\displaystyle\mathrm{Comm}\left\lparen\Gamma\right\rparen=\Gamma
for a generic metric; in this case, we can only show that
\displaystyle\left[{\mathrm{Comm}\left\lparen\Gamma\right\rparen}:{\Gamma}\right]\leq 2
and therefore that
\displaystyle\mathrm{Isom}\left\lparen Z,\pi^{*}\left\lparen\mu\right\rparen\right\rparen
is at worst a
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
extension of
\displaystyle\mathrm{Deck}\left\lparen\pi\right\rparen
for generic
\displaystyle\mu\in\mathscr{T}\left\lparen X\right\rparen
.

3.4
has the following consequences for the collection of mapping classes corresponding to deck transformations of a finite-degree cover.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
\displaystyle\pi\colon Z\xrightarrow{}X
be a finite-degree cover of a closed, orientable surface
\displaystyle X
of negative Euler characteristic and consider the quotient map
\displaystyle Q\colon\mathrm{Homeo}^{+}\left\lparen Z\right\rparen\twoheadrightarrow\mathrm{Mod}\left\lparen Z\right\rparen
. The restriction of
\displaystyle Q
to the deck group
\displaystyle\mathrm{Deck}\left\lparen\pi\right\rparen
is injective. Moreover, if
\displaystyle\pi
is regular and
\displaystyle X
has genus at least
\displaystyle 3
, then
\displaystyle Q
has image

Q\left\lparen\mathrm{Deck}\left\lparen\pi\right\rparen\right\rparen=\left\{\varphi\in\mathrm{Mod}\left\lparen Z\right\rparen:\varphi\cdot\pi^{*}\left\lparen\mu\right\rparen=\pi^{*}\left\lparen\mu\right\rparen\textrm{ for all }\mu\in\mathscr{T}\left\lparen X\right\rparen\right\}.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle\varphi\in\mathrm{Deck}\left\lparen\pi\right\rparen
, fix an orientation and a hyperbolic metric on
\displaystyle X
, which pullback along
\displaystyle\pi
to an orientation and a hyperbolic metric on
\displaystyle Z
. Then any deck transformation of
\displaystyle\pi
is an orientation-preserving isometry of
\displaystyle X
.

Fix a non-separating simple closed geodesic
\displaystyle\gamma
on
\displaystyle X
and a component
\displaystyle\alpha
of its full pre-image on
\displaystyle Z
, which is also a non-separating simple closed geodesic. Since
\displaystyle\alpha
is simple and non-separating, there is a simple closed geodesic
\displaystyle\beta
on
\displaystyle Z
which intersects it exactly once. Since
\displaystyle\varphi
is an isometry,
\displaystyle\varphi\left\lparen\alpha\right\rparen
and
\displaystyle\varphi\left\lparen\beta\right\rparen
are simple closed geodesics on
\displaystyle Z
which intersect exactly once.

Suppose that
\displaystyle Q\left\lparen\varphi\right\rparen
is trivial in
\displaystyle\mathrm{Mod}\left\lparen Z\right\rparen
. Then
\displaystyle Q\left\lparen\varphi\right\rparen
acts trivially on the homotopy classes of
\displaystyle\alpha
and
\displaystyle\beta
, and so
\displaystyle\varphi\left\lparen\alpha\right\rparen
and
\displaystyle\varphi\left\lparen\beta\right\rparen
are homotopic to
\displaystyle\alpha
and
\displaystyle\beta
, respectively. Since the metric on
\displaystyle Z
is negatively curved, closed geodesics are unique within their homotopy class, and hence
\displaystyle\varphi
preserves
\displaystyle\alpha
and
\displaystyle\beta
. This implies that
\displaystyle\varphi
fixes the unique intersection point between
\displaystyle\alpha
and
\displaystyle\beta
. Since any non-trivial element of the deck group
\displaystyle\mathrm{Deck}\left\lparen\pi\right\rparen
acts freely on the total space
\displaystyle Z
, this implies that
\displaystyle\varphi=\mathrm{id}_{Z}
is trivial in
\displaystyle\mathrm{Homeo}^{+}\left\lparen Z\right\rparen
.

Now suppose that
\displaystyle\pi
is regular and that
\displaystyle X
has genus at least
\displaystyle 3
. The containment

Q\left\lparen\mathrm{Deck}\left\lparen\pi\right\rparen\right\rparen\subseteq\left\{\varphi\in\mathrm{Mod}\left\lparen Z\right\rparen:\varphi\cdot\pi^{*}\left\lparen\mu\right\rparen=\pi^{*}\left\lparen\mu\right\rparen\textrm{ for all }\mu\in\mathscr{T}\left\lparen X\right\rparen\right\},

is clear, since pullbacks of metrics along
\displaystyle\pi
are invariant under the deck group
\displaystyle\mathrm{Deck}\left\lparen\pi\right\rparen
. To show the other containment, fix a hyperbolic metric
\displaystyle\mu
on
\displaystyle X
as in
3.4
; that is, we require
\displaystyle\mathrm{Isom}\left\lparen Z,\pi^{*}\mu\right\rparen=\mathrm{Deck}\left\lparen\pi\right\rparen
. Let
\displaystyle\varphi\in\mathrm{Homeo}^{+}\left\lparen Z\right\rparen
, and suppose that
\displaystyle Q\left\lparen\varphi\right\rparen\cdot\pi^{*}\left\lparen\left[\mu\right]\right\rparen=\pi^{*}\left\lparen\left[\mu\right]\right\rparen
. Then

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left[\left\lparen\pi\circ\varphi\right\rparen^{*}\mu\right]

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\left[\varphi^{*}\left\lparen\pi^{*}\mu\right\rparen\right]=Q\left\lparen\varphi^{-1}\right\rparen\cdot\left[\pi^{*}\mu\right]=Q\left\lparen\varphi^{-1}\right\rparen\cdot\left\lparen Q\left\lparen\varphi\right\rparen\cdot\left[\pi^{*}\mu\right]\right\rparen

\displaystyle\displaystyle=Q\left\lparen\varphi^{-1}\circ\varphi\right\rparen\cdot\left[\pi^{*}\mu\right]=Q\left\lparen\mathrm{id}_{Z}\right\rparen\cdot\left[\pi^{*}\mu\right]=\left[\pi^{*}\mu\right],

and so there is a homeomorphism
\displaystyle\psi\in\mathrm{Homeo}^{+}_{0}\left\lparen Z\right\rparen
such that

\left\lparen\varphi\circ\psi\right\rparen^{*}\left\lparen\pi^{*}\mu\right\rparen=\left\lparen\pi\circ\varphi\circ\psi\right\rparen^{*}\mu=\pi^{*}\mu.

Thus
\displaystyle\varphi\circ\psi\in\mathrm{Isom}\left\lparen Z,\pi^{*}\mu\right\rparen=\mathrm{Deck}\left\lparen\pi\right\rparen
is an isometry of
\displaystyle\pi^{*}\mu
and therefore a deck transformation of
\displaystyle\pi
. In particular, this implies that
\displaystyle Q\left\lparen\varphi\right\rparen=Q\left\lparen\varphi\circ\psi\right\rparen\in Q\left\lparen\mathrm{Deck}\left\lparen\pi\right\rparen\right\rparen
. ∎

Claim 4

\displaystyle\mathrm{Deck}\left\lparen\pi_{S}\right\rparen\cdot\pi_{X}^{*}\left\lparen\mathscr{T}\left\lparen X\right\rparen\right\rparen=\mathrm{Deck}\left\lparen\pi_{S}\right\rparen\cdot\pi_{Y}^{*}\left\lparen\mathscr{T}\left\lparen Y\right\rparen\right\rparen

, we will establish two preliminary lemmas, the first of which states that powers of simple curves have no length twins.

Lemma 4.3
.

Powers of simple curves have no length twins.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle\alpha
and
\displaystyle\beta
be a pair of length-commensurable curves on a closed surface
\displaystyle S
of negative Euler characteristic. Suppose that
\displaystyle\alpha
is a power of a simple curve
\displaystyle\gamma
,
\displaystyle\beta
is a power of a primitive curve
\displaystyle\delta
, and that
\displaystyle\gamma
and
\displaystyle\delta
are length-commensurable.

Since
\displaystyle\gamma
and
\displaystyle\delta
are primitive and
\displaystyle i\left\lparen\gamma,\gamma\right\rparen=0
,
2.6
implies that
\displaystyle i\left\lparen\gamma,\delta\right\rparen=0
. If
\displaystyle\gamma\neq\delta
, take a simple curve
\displaystyle\eta\neq\gamma
obtained from
\displaystyle\delta
via surgery. Note that
\displaystyle i\left\lparen\gamma,\eta\right\rparen=0
, and so Fenchel-Nielsen coordinates yield, for any two positive real numbers
\displaystyle c
and
\displaystyle e
, a hyperbolic metric
\displaystyle\mu
on
\displaystyle S
for which
\displaystyle\ell_{\mu}\left\lparen\gamma\right\rparen=c
and
\displaystyle\ell_{\mu}\left\lparen\eta\right\rparen=e
. Note that
\displaystyle\ell_{\mu}\left\lparen\delta\right\rparen\geq\ell_{\mu}\left\lparen\eta\right\rparen=e
. This contradicts our assumption that
\displaystyle\gamma
and
\displaystyle\delta
are length-commensurable, and thus
\displaystyle\delta=\gamma
. We have shown that any curve which is length-commensurable to
\displaystyle\alpha
is also a power of
\displaystyle\gamma
, and so
\displaystyle\alpha
has no length twins. ∎

Recall
2.6
, which states that length-commensurable curves on a surface are represented by trace-commensurable elements in the corresponding surface group. Finding curves without length twins is thus equivalent to finding elements with no trace twins. The following argument augments Horowitz’s proof of
2.7
in
[
Hor72
, Corollary 7.2]
to find examples of such elements in free groups.

Lemma 4.4
.

For any integers
\displaystyle j,k\in\mathbb{Z}
and primitive elements
\displaystyle a,b\in F
of a free group
\displaystyle F
, the element
\displaystyle w=a^{j}b^{k}
has no trace twins in
\displaystyle F
.

Proof.

Note that
[
Hor72
, Theorem 1.1]
implies that we may assume that
\displaystyle F
has finite rank. If
\displaystyle j=0
,
\displaystyle k=0
, or
\displaystyle a=b
, then the desired result follows immediately from
2.7
. Thus we may assume that
\displaystyle j
and
\displaystyle k
are both non-zero and that
\displaystyle a\neq b
. Suppose that
\displaystyle w=a^{j}b^{k}
and some element
\displaystyle x\in F
are trace-commensurable, so that

\mathrm{tr}\big\lparen\underbrace{a^{j}b^{k}\dotsm a^{j}b^{k}}_{m}\big\rparen^{2}=\mathrm{tr}\left\lparen\big\lparen a^{j}b^{k}\big\rparen^{m}\right\rparen^{2}=\mathrm{tr}\left\lparen w^{m}\right\rparen^{2}=\mathrm{tr}\left\lparen x^{n}\right\rparen^{2}

for some non-zero integers
\displaystyle m,n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\left\{0\right\}
. Extend
\displaystyle\left\{a,b\right\}
to a basis
\displaystyle B
for
\displaystyle F
, and let
\displaystyle y=x^{n}
.

[
Hor72
, Theorem 3.1]
gives a way to express
\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left\lparen w^{m}\right\rparen
as a polynomial in
\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left\lparen a\right\rparen
,
\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left\lparen b\right\rparen
, and
\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left\lparen ab\right\rparen
. Similarly, this theorem gives a way to express
\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left\lparen y\right\rparen
as a polynomial in products of the elements of
\displaystyle B
which appear in a freely reduced word representing
\displaystyle y
. Since these traces must be equal for all linear representations of
\displaystyle F
, this implies that
\displaystyle y\in\left\langle a,b\right\rangle
, so that
\displaystyle y
is conjugate to a word of the form

z=a^{e_{1}}b^{f_{1}}a^{e_{2}}b^{f_{2}}\dotsm a^{e_{p}}b^{f_{p}}

for some integers
\displaystyle e_{1},\dotsc,e_{p},f_{1},\dotsc,f_{p}\in\mathbb{Z}
, at most one of which is zero. We will show that
\displaystyle w
is either
\displaystyle z
or
\displaystyle z^{-1}
. Since the trace is invariant under conjugation,

\mathrm{tr}\big\lparen\underbrace{a^{j}b^{k}\dotsm a^{j}b^{k}}_{m}\big\rparen^{2}=\mathrm{tr}\left\lparen x^{n}\right\rparen^{2}=\mathrm{tr}\left\lparen y\right\rparen^{2}=\mathrm{tr}\left\lparen a^{e_{1}}b^{f_{1}}\dotsm a^{e_{p}}b^{f_{p}}\right\rparen^{2}.

[
Hor72
, Lemma 6.1]
implies that
\displaystyle m=p
,
\displaystyle\left\lvert e_{1}\right\rvert=\dotsb=\left\lvert e_{p}\right\rvert=\left\lvert j\right\rvert
, and
\displaystyle\left\lvert f_{1}\right\rvert=\dotsb=\left\lvert f_{p}\right\rvert=\left\lvert k\right\rvert
. Let
\displaystyle\varphi\colon F_{2}\xrightarrow{}\mathbb{Z}
be the homomorphism defined by the conditions
\displaystyle\varphi\left\lparen a\right\rparen=\mathrm{sgn}\left\lparen j\right\rparen
and
\displaystyle\varphi\left\lparen b\right\rparen=\mathrm{sgn}\left\lparen k\right\rparen
, where

\mathrm{sgn}\left\lparen t\right\rparen=\begin{cases}-1,&if $\displaystyle x<0$;\\
0,&if $\displaystyle x=0$; and\\
1,&otherwise if $\displaystyle x>0$.\end{cases}

Since
\displaystyle\varphi
is a homomorphism,
\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left\lparen\varphi\left\lparen w^{m}\right\rparen\right\rparen^{2}=\mathrm{tr}\left\lparen\varphi\left\lparen z\right\rparen\right\rparen^{2}
as functions on
\displaystyle\mathrm{hom}\left\lparen\mathbb{Z},\mathrm{SL}_{2}\left\lparen\mathbb{C}\right\rparen\right\rparen
, and so
\displaystyle\left\lvert\varphi\left\lparen w^{m}\right\rparen\right\rvert=\left\lvert\varphi\left\lparen z\right\rparen\right\rvert
by

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left\lvert m\right\rvert\cdot\big\lvert\left\lvert j\right\rvert+\left\lvert k\right\rvert\big\rvert

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\left\lvert m\right\rvert\cdot\left\lvert j\cdot\mathrm{sgn}\left\lparen j\right\rparen+k\cdot\mathrm{sgn}\left\lparen k\right\rparen\right\rvert=\big\lvert m\cdot\varphi\big\lparen a^{j}b^{k}\big\rparen\big\rvert=\left\lvert m\cdot\varphi\left\lparen w\right\rparen\right\rvert=\left\lvert\varphi\left\lparen w^{m}\right\rparen\right\rvert

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\left\lvert\varphi\left\lparen z\right\rparen\right\rvert=\left\lvert\varphi\left\lparen a^{e_{1}}b^{f_{1}}\dotsm a^{e_{m}}b^{f_{m}}\right\rparen\right\rvert=\left\lvert\mathrm{sgn}\left\lparen j\right\rparen\sum_{i=1}^{m}{e_{i}}+\mathrm{sgn}\left\lparen k\right\rparen\sum_{i=1}^{m}{f_{i}}\right\rvert.

Since
\displaystyle\left\lvert e_{1}\right\rvert=\dotsb=\left\lvert e_{m}\right\rvert=\left\lvert j\right\rvert
and
\displaystyle\left\lvert f_{1}\right\rvert=\dotsb=\left\lvert f_{m}\right\rvert=\left\lvert k\right\rvert
, the only way for this to happen is for there to be no cancellation in the sum appearing in the bottom right of the above equations. Thus there are two cases; either
\displaystyle e_{1}=\dotsb=e_{m}=j
and
\displaystyle f_{1}=\dotsb=f_{m}=k
, in which case
\displaystyle w=z
, or alternatively
\displaystyle e_{1}=\dotsb=e_{m}=-j
and
\displaystyle f_{1}=\dotsb=f_{m}=-k
, in which case
\displaystyle w
is conjugate to
\displaystyle z^{-1}
.

In summary, we have shown that for any element
\displaystyle x\in F

\displaystyle w
and
\displaystyle x
are trace-commensurable, then some non-zero powers of
\displaystyle w
and
\displaystyle x
are conjugate in
\displaystyle F
(and in fact conjugate in
\displaystyle\left\langle a,b\right\rangle
). Therefore,
\displaystyle w=a^{j}b^{k}
has no trace twins in
\displaystyle F
. ∎
