Jérémy Blanc, Pierre-Marie Poloni, Immanuel Van Santen

Abstract
. We study the complement problem in projective spaces ℙ n {\mathbb{P}}^{n} over any algebraically closed field: If H , H ′ ⊆ ℙ n H,H^{\prime}\subseteq{\mathbb{P}}^{n} are irreducible hypersurfaces of degree d d such that the complements ℙ n ∖ H {\mathbb{P}}^{n}\setminus H , ℙ n ∖ H ′ {\mathbb{P}}^{n}\setminus H^{\prime} are isomorphic, are the hypersurfaces H H , H ′ H^{\prime} isomorphic? For n = 2 n=2 , the answer is positive if d ≤ 7 d\leq 7 and there are counterexamples when d = 8 d=8 . In contrast we provide counterexamples for all n , d ≥ 3 n,d\geq 3 with ( n , d ) ≠ ( 3 , 3 ) (n,d)\neq(3,3) . Moreover, we show that the complement problem has an affirmative answer for d = 2 d=2 and give partial results in case ( n , d ) = ( 3 , 3 ) (n,d)=(3,3) . In the course of the exposition, we prove that rational normal projective surfaces admitting a desingularisation by trees of smooth rational curves are piecewise isomorphic if and only if they coincide in the Grothendieck ring, answering affirmatively a question posed by Larsen and Lunts for such surfaces.

1.
Introduction

Let
{\mathbf{k}}
be a field, let
n\geq 2
be an integer and let
f\in{\mathbf{k}}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]
be an irreducible homogeneous polynomial of degree
d\geq 1
. The variety

{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{f}=\{\,[x_{0}:\cdots:x_{n}]\in{\mathbb{P}}^{n}\ |\ f(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})\neq 0\,\}

is the complement in
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
of the hypersurface given by
f=0
. It is an affine open subset of
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
with Picard group
{\mathbb{Z}}/d{\mathbb{Z}}
. Moreover, any open subset of
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
isomorphic to it is of the form
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{g}
for some irreducible homogeneous polynomial
g\in{\mathbf{k}}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]
of degree
d
(see Lemma
2.6
).
One may then ask the following natural questions, for all
irreducible homogeneous polynomials
f,g\in{\mathbf{k}}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]
of the same degree
d\geq 1
.

(1)

Does any isomorphism
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{f}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\simeq}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{g}
extend to an element of
\mathrm{Aut}({\mathbb{P}}^{n})
?

(2)

If
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{f}
and
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{g}
are isomorphic, is there an element of
\mathrm{Aut}({\mathbb{P}}^{n})
that sends
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{f}
onto
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{g}
?

(3)

If
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{f}
and
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{g}
are isomorphic, are the zero loci
V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{n}}(f)
and
V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{n}}(g)
of
f
and
g
isomorphic?

Of course, any positive answer to one of the questions also gives a positive answer to the next one(s).

If
d=1
, the first question has a negative answer, as there are many automorphisms of
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{f}\simeq{\mathbb{A}}^{n}
that do not extend
to automorphisms of
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
. However, the two other questions have a positive answer.

For
d=2
, we can obtain similar results, in any dimension, when
{\mathbf{k}}
is algebraically closed. Indeed, we will answer the first question in the negative for any irreducible quadric, see Example
5.6
,
and provide positive answers to the two other questions, see Theorem
B
below.

The situation for
d=3
is already more complicated, at least in dimension
n=3
.
The first question has a negative answer for each singular cubic with Du Val singularities
over an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero
[
CDP18
, Theorem C and Theorem 4.3]
, and
more generally for each singular cubic
over any algebraically closed field (Proposition
5.22
),
but is wide open for any smooth cubic surface
[
CDP18
, Conjecture 1.1]
. The two other questions are open as well.

In dimension
n=2
, it was conjectured in
[
Yos84
]
that the answer to the second question should be always positive
for algebraically closed fields of characteristic zero. This conjecture was proved for all algebraically closed fields if the curve
C\subseteq{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
given by
f=0
is such that
C\setminus L\simeq{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
for some line (see
[
Yos84
, Theorem]
for a proof
in characteristic zero and
[
Hem19
, Theorem 1]
for a proof in any characteristic). The conjecture was however proven to be false in general, with a first example of degree
39
given in
[
Bla09
]
. A second family of counterexamples
of degree
9+4m
for each
m\geq 0
was given later in
[
Cos12
]
. Finally, an example of degree
8
and a proof that the conjecture is true for curves of degree
\leq 7
appeared in

[
Hem19
, Corollary 1.2, Theorem 3]
for algebraically closed fields of any characteristic. Moreover, the examples given in
[
Hem19
]
and
[
Bla09
]
also give a negative answer to the third question.

In dimension
n\geq 3
, there was no negative answer to Questions
(2)
and
(3)

until now. In this article, we show by constructing explicit examples,
that the answer of the third question, and thus of the two others, is negative for large degree and large dimension.
More precisely, in any dimension
n\geq 3
, the smallest degree for counterexamples is
3
, except maybe for
n=3
, where the smallest degree might be
4
. This contrasts the case of dimension
n=2
where counterexamples
for algebraically closed fields always have degree
\geq 8
.

Theorem A
.

Let
\mathbf{k}
be a field and let
d,n\geq 3
be integers such that
d
is not a multiple of

and such that
(d,n)\neq(3,3)

There are two non-isomorphic irreducible hypersurfaces

at the end of §

Theorem C

at the end of §

. For

,

]

). A similar result does not hold for all rational normal projective surfaces (Example

). Using the Grothendieck ring

)

).

.

.

Let

be algebraically closed and let

be rational

2.
Lift to affine hypersurfaces

Let
n\geq 1
. For each homogeneous polynomial
f\in\mathbf{k}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]
of degree
d\geq 1
and each
\mu\in{\mathbf{k}}^{*}
, we denote by
X_{f,\mu}\subseteq{\mathbb{A}}^{n+1}
the hypersurface given by

X_{f,\mu}=\mathrm{Spec}({\mathbf{k}}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]/(f-\mu))\subseteq{\mathbb{A}}^{n+1}\,,

and obtain a canonical finite morphism

\begin{array}[]{cccc}\pi_{f,\mu}\colon&X_{f,\mu}&\to&{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{f}\\
&(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})&\mapsto&[x_{0}:\cdots:x_{n}]\end{array}

of degree
d
, which is an étale covering if
\car(\mathbf{k})
does not divide
d
.

If
\mathbf{k}
is algebraically closed, all the varieties
X_{f,\mu}
are isomorphic to
X_{f,1}
by homotheties.

If
{\mathbf{k}}
is equal to the field of complex numbers
{\mathbb{C}}
, then
\pi_{f,\mu}

is the universal abelian covering, as the abelianisation of the
fundamental group of
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{f}
with respect to the Euclidean topology is equal to
{\mathbb{Z}}/d{\mathbb{Z}}

[
Lib05
, Proposition 2.3]
.

We now prove that for each field
\mathbf{k}
, the isomorphisms between complements of hypersurfaces lift to these coverings. This will be useful in the sequel, in order to study isomorphisms between varieties
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{f}
and
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{g}
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\mathbf{k}
be a field, let
n\geq 1
be an integer and let
f,g\in{\mathbf{k}}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]
be irreducible homogeneous polynomials
of degree
d\geq 1
. Let
\Phi_{0},\ldots,\Phi_{n}\in{\mathbf{k}}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]
be homogeneous polynomials of degree
\ell\geq 1
.
Then, the following statements are equivalent:

(1)

The rational map

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\Phi\colon{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{f}&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\simeq}}{{\longrightarrow}}&{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{g}\,,\\
{[x_{0}:\cdots:x_{n}]}&\mapsto&[\Phi_{0}(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}):\cdots:\Phi_{n}(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})]\,,\end{array}

is an isomorphism and the gcd of
\Phi_{0},\ldots,\Phi_{n}
is a power of
f
.

(2)

There exists
\mu\in{\mathbf{k}}^{*}
such that the following map is an isomorphism

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\varphi\colon X_{f,1}&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\simeq}}{{\longrightarrow}}&X_{g,\mu}\,,\\
(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})&\mapsto&(\Phi_{0}(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}),\ldots,\Phi_{n}(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}))\,.\end{array}

Moreover, if these statements hold, then
\ell
is invertible modulo
d
.

Proof.

“
(1)

\Rightarrow

(2)
”:
By assumption there exists
r\geq 0
and
\Phi_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,\Phi_{n}^{\prime}
in
\mathbf{k}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]

without a common factor such that

\Phi_{i}=f^{r}\Phi_{i}^{\prime}
for all
i
. The domain
\textrm{dom}(\Phi)
of the rational self-map
\Phi\colon{\mathbb{P}}^{n}\dashrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{n}

is thus equal to

{\mathbb{P}}^{n}\setminus V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{n}}(\Phi^{\prime}_{0},\ldots,\Phi^{\prime}_{n})
and contains
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{f}
.
As
g
is nowhere zero on
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{g}
, the polynomial
g(\Phi_{0},\ldots,\Phi_{n})=f^{dr}g(\Phi_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,\Phi_{n}^{\prime})
is nowhere zero on
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{f}
and is thus equal to
\mu f^{t}
for some
\mu\in{\mathbf{k}}^{*}
and some integer
t\geq 1
. We then obtain a morphism

\varphi\colon X_{f,1}\to X_{g,\mu}\,,\quad(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})\mapsto(\Phi_{0}(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}),\ldots,\Phi_{n}(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}))\,.

It remains to see that
\varphi
is an isomorphism.

The inverse of
\Phi
is given by

\Psi\colon{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{g}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\simeq}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{f}\,,\quad[x_{0}:\cdots:x_{n}]\mapsto[\Psi_{0}(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}):\cdots:\Psi_{n}(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})]\,,

where
\Psi_{0},\ldots,\Psi_{n}\in{\mathbf{k}}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]
are homogeneous polynomials of the same degree
\ell^{\prime}\geq 1
, without common factor.

As
\Psi\circ\Phi=\mathrm{id}_{{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{f}}
and
\Phi\circ\Psi=\mathrm{id}_{{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{g}}
, there exist homogeneous polynomials
A,B\in{\mathbf{k}}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]
of degree
\ell\ell^{\prime}-1
such that

\Psi_{i}(\Phi_{0},\ldots,\Phi_{n})=Ax_{i}\text{ and }\Phi_{i}(\Psi_{0},\ldots,\Psi_{n})=Bx_{i}\text{ for all }i\in\{0,\ldots,n\}.

Moreover, as the zero locus of
A
is sent by
\Phi

into the base-locus of
\Psi

and the zero locus of
B
is sent by
\Psi
into the base-locus of

\Phi
, we find that
A
is nowhere vanishing on
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{f}
and
B
is nowhere vanishing on
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{g}
. We get
A=\lambda f^{s}
and
B=\lambda^{\prime}g^{s}
where
\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}\in{\mathbf{k}}^{*}
and where
s\geq 1
is such that
ds=\ell\ell^{\prime}-1
. In particular,
\ell
is invertible modulo
d
.
We replace
\Psi_{i}
with
\Psi_{i}/\lambda
for each
i\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
and reduce to the case where
\lambda=1
.

Consider now the morphism

\psi\colon X_{g,\mu}\to{\mathbb{A}}^{n+1}\,,\quad(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})\mapsto(\Psi_{0}(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}),\ldots,\Psi_{n}(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}))\,.

Using that
\Psi_{i}(\Phi_{0},\ldots,\Phi_{n})=f^{s}x_{i}
for each
i\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
, we obtain that

\psi\circ\varphi\colon X_{f,1}\to{\mathbb{A}}^{n+1}
is the natural closed embedding.
Hence,
(\psi\circ\varphi)^{\ast}=\varphi^{\ast}\circ\psi^{\ast}\colon\mathbf{k}[{\mathbb{A}}^{n+1}]\to\mathbf{k}[X_{f,1}]
is surjective and
\varphi
is dominant, i.e.
\varphi^{\ast}\colon\mathbf{k}[X_{g,\mu}]\to\mathbf{k}[X_{f,1}]
is injective.
This implies that

\varphi^{\ast}
and thus
\varphi
is an isomorphism.

“
(2)

\Rightarrow

(1)
”: As
\Phi_{0},\ldots,\Phi_{n}
are homogeneous of degree
\ell
, we may define a rational self-map
\Phi\colon[x_{0}:\cdots:x_{n}]\mapsto[\Phi_{0}(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}):\cdots:\Phi_{n}(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})]
of
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
. We now prove that
\Phi
restricts to an isomorphism
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{f}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\simeq}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{g}
and that the gcd
of
\Phi_{0},\ldots,\Phi_{n}
is a power of
f
.

By assumption
g(\Phi_{0},\ldots,\Phi_{n})=(f-1)p+\mu
for some polynomial
p\in\mathbf{k}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]

of degree
r=d(\ell-1)\geq 0
.
Write
p=\sum_{i=0}^{r}p_{i}
where
p_{i}
is homogeneous of degree
i
.
Since
g(\Phi_{0},\ldots,\Phi_{n})
is homogeneous, it follows that
p_{0}=\mu
and
p_{di}=fp_{d(i-1)}
for all

1\leq i\leq\ell-1
. This implies that

g(\Phi_{0},\ldots,\Phi_{n})=\mu f^{\ell}\,.

Since
g
is homogeneous
and
f
is irreducible the gcd of
\Phi_{0},\ldots,\Phi_{n}
is a power of
f
.
Moreover,
\Phi
restricts to a morphism
\Phi\colon{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{f}\to{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{g}
.

Using that
\pi_{g,\mu}\circ\varphi=\Phi\circ\pi_{f,1}
, we get the following commutative diagram

\textstyle{\mathbf{k}(X_{g,\mu})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\varphi^{\ast}}
\textstyle{\mathbf{k}(X_{f,1})}
\textstyle{\mathbf{k}({\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{g})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\pi_{g,\mu}^{\ast}}
\scriptstyle{\Phi^{\ast}}
\textstyle{\mathbf{k}({\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{f})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\pi_{f,1}^{\ast}}

and that
\Phi\colon{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{f}\to{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{g}
is a quasi-finite surjection.
Note that
\pi_{f,1}^{\ast}
and
\pi_{g,\mu}^{\ast}
are field extensions of degree
d
.
Since
\varphi^{\ast}
is an isomorphism,
\Phi^{\ast}
is an isomorphism as well, i.e.
\Phi\colon{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{f}\to{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{g}

is birational. Zariski’s Main Theorem
[
Gro61
, Corollaire 4.4.9]

gives that
\Phi\colon{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{f}\to{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{g}
is an isomorphism.
∎

Remark 2.2
.

If there exists an isomorphism
\Phi\colon{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{f}\to{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{g}
, there are homogeneous polynomials

\Phi_{0},\ldots,\Phi_{n}\in{\mathbf{k}}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]
of degree
\ell\geq 1
without common factor
such that

\Phi([x_{0}:\cdots:x_{n}])=[\Phi_{0}(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}):\cdots:\Phi_{n}(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})]
.
Proposition
2.1
then gives the existence of
\mu\in{\mathbf{k}}^{*}
such that

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\varphi\colon X_{f,1}&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\simeq}}{{\longrightarrow}}&X_{g,\mu}\,,\\
(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})&\mapsto&(\Phi_{0}(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}),\ldots,\Phi_{n}(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}))\end{array}

is an isomorphism. If

is moreover algebraically closed, then
we may multiply each
\Phi_{i}
with a root of
\mu^{-1}
and assume that
\mu=1
. Hence,
X_{f,1}
and

are isomorphic.

Remark 2.3
.

Let
\mathbf{k}
be a field,
n\geq 1
be an integer and let
f,g\in{\mathbf{k}}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]
be irreducible homogeneous polynomials of degree

. Let

be an integer and let
\Phi_{0},\ldots,\Phi_{n}\in{\mathbf{k}}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]

. If

is an isomorphism, then we may multiply the monomials of
\Phi_{i}
with some powers of
f
and assume that
\Phi_{0},\ldots,\Phi_{n}
are homogeneous of the same degree

to obtain an isomorphism

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\mathbf{k}
be a field,
n\geq 1
be an integer,
f,g\in{\mathbf{k}}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]
be irreducible homogeneous polynomials of degree
d\geq 1
, and let
\Phi\colon{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{f}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\simeq}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{g}
be an isomorphism given by homogeneous polynomials of degree
\ell
such that their gcd is a power of
f
.
If
\ell
is congruent to
1
modulo
d
, then
{\mathbb{P}}^{m}_{f}
and
{\mathbb{P}}^{m}_{g}
are isomorphic for each
m\geq n
.

Proof.

We write
\Phi
as
[x_{0}:\cdots:x_{n}]\mapsto[\Phi_{0}(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}):\cdots:\Phi_{n}(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})]
, where
\Phi_{0},\ldots,\Phi_{n}\in{\mathbf{k}}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]
are homogeneous of degree
\ell
and the
\mathrm{gcd}
of
\Phi_{0},\ldots,\Phi_{n}
is a power of
f
. By Proposition
2.1
, the morphism

\varphi\colon X_{f,1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\simeq}}{{\longrightarrow}}X_{g,\mu}\,,\quad(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})\mapsto(\Phi_{0}(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}),\ldots,\Phi_{n}(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}))

is an isomorphism, where
X_{f,1},X_{g,\mu}\subseteq{\mathbb{A}}^{n+1}
are defined as before. If
\ell
is congruent to
1
modulo
d
, we can write
\ell=1+dr
for some integer
r\geq 0
. For each
m\geq n
,
we thus get an isomorphism

X_{f,1}\times{\mathbb{A}}^{m-n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\simeq}}{{\longrightarrow}}X_{g,\mu}\times{\mathbb{A}}^{m-n}
given by

(x_{0},\ldots,x_{m})\mapsto(\varphi(x_{0},\ldots,x_{m}),x_{n+1}f(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})^{r},\ldots,x_{m}f(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})^{r}))\,.

Applying again Proposition
2.1
, now in the converse direction, we get that the homogeneous polynomials
\Phi_{0},\ldots,\Phi_{n},x_{n+1}f^{r},\ldots,x_{m}f^{r}
of degree
\ell
also define an isomorphism
{\mathbb{P}}^{m}_{f}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\simeq}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\mathbb{P}}^{m}_{g}
.
∎

Example 2.5
.

Let
{\mathbf{k}}
be a field and let

{\mathbb{P}}^{2}\setminus\Gamma
that does not extend to
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
and has degree
8
. Explicitely, we define
\Phi_{0},\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2}\in{\mathbf{k}}[x,y,z]

\displaystyle\Phi_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-x^{4}z+2x^{3}y^{2}-2x^{2}yz^{2}+2xy^{3}z+y^{5}-y^{2}z^{3})\cdot f

\displaystyle\Phi_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(x^{2}+yz)\cdot f^{2}

\displaystyle\Phi_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x^{7}y-x^{6}z^{2}+6x^{5}y^{2}z-x^{4}y^{4}-3x^{4}yz^{3}+9x^{3}y^{3}z^{2}

\displaystyle+x^{2}y^{5}z-3x^{2}y^{2}z^{4}-xy^{7}+4xy^{4}z^{3}+2y^{6}z^{2}-y^{3}z^{5}

and calculate that these define an involution

{\mathbb{P}}^{2}_{f}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\simeq}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\mathbb{P}}^{2}_{f}\,,\quad[x:y:z]\mapsto[\Phi_{0}(x,y,z):\Phi_{1}(x,y,z):\Phi_{2}(x,y,z)]\,.

Here, the common degree is
8
, that is not congruent to
1
modulo
3
, but invertible in
{\mathbb{Z}}/3{\mathbb{Z}}
, as Proposition
2.1
says.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
\mathbf{k}
be a field,
n\geq 1
,
f\in\mathbf{k}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]
an irreducible homogeneous polynomial of degree
d\geq 1

and let
U\subseteq{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
be an open subvariety.
If there exists an isomorphism
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{f}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\simeq}}{{\longrightarrow}}U
, then there exists an irreducible homogeneous polynomial
g\in\mathbf{k}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]
of degree
d
with
U={\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{g}
.

Before we start the proof we recall the following fact: If
X
is an affine irreducible
normal variety over a field
\mathbf{k}
and if
U\subseteq X
is a big open subset, i.e. the codimension of
X\setminus U
in
X
is at least
2
,
then
\mathbf{k}[U]=\mathbf{k}[X]
(see
[
Mat86
, Theorem 11.5]
). In particular, if
U
is affine as well, then
U=X
.

Proof of Lemma
2.6
.

If
U
is big open in
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
, then for any open affine subvariety
V\subseteq{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
,
the affine subvariety
U\cap V
is big open in
V
and hence
U\cap V=V
. This would imply that
U={\mathbb{P}}^{n}

is affine, a contradiction since
n\geq 1
. Hence the union
Z
of the
(n-1)
-dimensional
irreducible components of
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}\setminus U
is non-empty. As
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}\setminus Z
is an affine
big open subset of
U
we get
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}\setminus Z=U
.
Moreover,
\mathbf{k}[{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{f}]^{\ast}={\mathbf{k}}^{\ast}
yields
\mathbf{k}[U]^{\ast}={\mathbf{k}}^{\ast}
, which implies that
Z
is irreducible. Hence, there exists an irreducible homogeneous polynomial
g\in\mathbf{k}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]
such that
Z

is the zero locus of
g
, i.e.
U={\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{g}
. Finally,
{\mathbb{Z}}/\deg(g){\mathbb{Z}}\simeq\Pic({\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{g})\simeq\Pic({\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{f})\simeq{\mathbb{Z}}/d{\mathbb{Z}}
implies that
\deg(g)=d
.
∎

3.
Non-isomorphic hypersurfaces having isomorphic complements

3.1.
Explicit isomorphisms between complements of projective cones

The following formulas are
inspired by isomorphisms between cylinders over Danielewski surfaces given in
[
MJP21
]
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\mathbf{k}
be a field, let
s,m,n\geq 0
be integers and let
S={\mathbf{k}}[x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{s}]
be a polynomial ring in
s+1
variables over
\mathbf{k}
.
Let
P,Q\in S[z]
and denote by
H_{P}
and
H_{Q}
the hypersurfaces of
{\mathbb{A}}^{s+3}_{{\mathbf{k}}}=\mathrm{Spec}(S[y,z])
defined by the equations

x_{0}^{n}y=P(x_{0},\ldots,x_{r},z)\quad\text{and}\quad x_{0}^{n}y=Q(x_{0},\ldots,x_{r},z)\,,

respectively. Suppose that there exist
A,B\in S[z]
such that

(1)

z-A(B(z))
and
z-B(A(z))
both belong to the ideal
x_{0}^{m}S[z]
,

(2)

Q\left(A(z)+x_{0}^{m}w\right)\in x_{0}^{n}S[z,w]+P(z)S[z,w]
and
P\left(B(z)+x_{0}^{m}w\right)\in x_{0}^{n}S[z,w]+Q(z)S[z,w]
, where
w
is transcendental over
S[y,z]
.

Then, the following are inverse isomorphisms.

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\Phi\colon H_{P}\times{\mathbb{A}}^{1}_{{\mathbf{k}}}&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\simeq}}{{\longrightarrow}}&H_{Q}\times{\mathbb{A}}^{1}_{{\mathbf{k}}}\\
(x_{0},\ldots,x_{s},y,z,w)&\mapsto&(x_{0},\ldots,x_{s},\frac{Q\left(A(z)+x_{0}^{m}w\right)}{x_{0}^{n}},\\
&&A(z)+x_{0}^{m}w,\frac{z-B\left(A(z)+x_{0}^{m}w\right)}{x_{0}^{m}})\,,\\
\Psi\colon H_{Q}\times{\mathbb{A}}^{1}_{{\mathbf{k}}}&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\simeq}}{{\longrightarrow}}&H_{P}\times{\mathbb{A}}^{1}_{{\mathbf{k}}}\\
(x_{0},\ldots,x_{s},y,z,w)&\mapsto&(x_{0},\ldots,x_{s},\frac{P\left(B(z)+x_{0}^{m}w\right)}{x_{0}^{n}},\\
&&B(z)+x_{0}^{m}w,\frac{z-A\left(B(z)+x_{0}^{m}w\right)}{x_{0}^{m}}).\end{array}

Proof.

The hypotheses on
A
and
B
imply that
\Phi
and
\Psi
are morphisms. Moreover, the compositions are given by

(\Psi\circ\Phi)(x_{0},\ldots,x_{s},y,z,w)=(x_{0},\ldots,x_{s},P(z)/x_{0}^{n},z,w),

(\Phi\circ\Psi)(x_{0},\ldots,x_{s},y,z,w)=(x_{0},\ldots,x_{s},Q(z)/x_{0}^{n},z,w).

∎

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\mathbf{k}
be a field,
d\geq 4
be integers and let
f,g\in{\mathbf{k}}[x,y,z]
be the homogeneous polynomials of degree
d
defined by

f=x^{d-1}y+z^{d}\text{ and }g=x^{d-1}y+z^{d}+dx^{d-2}z^{2}.

Then, for each
r\geq 1
, the
open subvarieties
{\mathbb{P}}^{r+2}_{f},{\mathbb{P}}^{r+2}_{g}\subseteq{\mathbb{P}}^{r+2}
are isomorphic.

Remark 3.3
.

The open subvarieties
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}_{f},{\mathbb{P}}^{2}_{g}\subseteq{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
are not isomorphic when
\mathbf{k}=\mathbb{C}
is the field of complex numbers. This follows from Proposition
2.1
and from the fact that, for every
\mu\in{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
, the (Danielewski) hypersurfaces
X_{f,1},X_{g,\mu}\subseteq{\mathbb{A}}^{3}_{\mathbb{C}}
defined by the equations
f=1
and
g=\mu
, respectively, are not isomorphic (see
[
Pol11
, Theorem 9]
).

Proof.

We define
s=0
,
x_{0}=x
,
S=\mathbf{k}[x]
,
m=d
,
n=d-1
,

\begin{array}[]{rclrcl}P(z)&=&1-z^{d},&Q(z)&=&1-z^{d}-dx^{d-2}z^{2}\,,\\
A(z)&=&z-x^{d-2}z^{3},&B(z)&=&z+x^{d-2}z^{3}\,,\end{array}

and prove that the hypotheses of Lemma
3.1
are satisfied for the above polynomials in
S[z]
.

Firstly, we have
A(z)\equiv z\equiv B(z)\pmod{x^{d-2}}
. Thus, since
d\leq 2(d-2)
, as
d\geq 4
, we get
x^{d-2}A(z)^{3}\equiv x^{d-2}z^{3}\equiv x^{d-2}B(z)^{3}\pmod{x^{d}}
and we obtain

\displaystyle z-A(B(z))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle z-B(z)+x^{d-2}B(z)^{3}\equiv 0\pmod{x^{d}}\,,

\displaystyle z-B(A(z))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle z-A(z)-x^{d-2}A(z)^{3}\equiv 0\pmod{x^{d}}\,.

We then check that
P\left(B(z)+x^{d}w\right)\in x^{d-1}S[z,w]+Q(z)S[z,w]
:

\displaystyle P(B(z)+x^{d}w)

\displaystyle\equiv P(B(z))

\displaystyle\equiv 1-(z+x^{d-2}z^{3})^{d}

\displaystyle\equiv 1-z^{d}-dx^{d-2}z^{d+2}

\displaystyle\equiv(1-z^{d}-dx^{d-2}z^{2})\cdot(1+dx^{d-2}z^{2})

\displaystyle\equiv Q\cdot(1+dx^{d-2}z^{2})\,\pmod{x^{d-1}}.

Similarly, we have

Q(A(z)+x^{d}w)\in x^{d-1}S[z,w]+P(z)S[z,w]
:

\displaystyle Q(A(z)+x^{d}w)

\displaystyle\equiv Q\left(A(z)\right)

\displaystyle\equiv 1-(z-x^{d-2}z^{3})^{d}-dx^{d-2}(z-x^{d-2}z^{3})^{2}

\displaystyle\equiv 1-z^{d}+dx^{d-2}z^{d+2}-dx^{d-2}z^{2}

\displaystyle\equiv(1-z^{d})\cdot(1-dx^{d-2}z^{2})

\displaystyle\equiv P\cdot(1-dx^{d-2}z^{2})\,\pmod{x^{d-1}}.

Noting that
f-1=x^{d-1}y-P(z)
and
g-1=x^{d-1}y-Q(z)
, we may now apply Lemma
3.1
to obtain an isomorphism

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\varphi\colon\mathrm{Spec}({\mathbf{k}}[x,y,z,w]/(f-1))&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\simeq}}{{\longrightarrow}}&\mathrm{Spec}({\mathbf{k}}[x,y,z,w]/(g-1))\\
(x,y,z,w)&\mapsto&(x,\frac{Q(A(z)+x^{d}w)}{x^{d-1}},A(z)+x^{d}w,\\
&&\frac{z-B(A(z)+x^{d}w)}{x^{d}}).\end{array}

Arguing as in Remark
2.3
, we may then use this isomorphism to construct an isomorphism
{\mathbb{P}}^{3}_{f}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\simeq}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\mathbb{P}}^{3}_{g}
. Indeed, the second component of
\varphi
can be replaced by a polynomial, since

Q\left(A(z)+x^{d}w\right)\equiv P\cdot(1-dx^{d-2}z^{2})\pmod{x^{d-1}}

and
P\equiv x^{d-1}y\pmod{f-1}
.
Moreover, the fourth component of
\varphi
is a polynomial, since
z-A(B(z))\equiv 0\pmod{x^{d}}
.
Doing this, we obtain an expression for
\varphi
given by four polynomials. It remains to check that each monomial appearing in that polynomial expression of
\varphi
has degree congruent to
1
modulo
d
. Working modulo
d
with the degrees, we observe that
A,B,P,Q
are homogeneous of degree
1,1,0,0
, respectively, hence that the polynomials
z-B(A(z)+x^{d}w)
,
Q(A(z)+x^{d}w)
and
P-x^{d-1}y
are homogeneous of degree
1
,
0
and
0
, respectively. Therefore, we can conclude that
{\mathbb{P}}^{3}_{f}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\simeq}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\mathbb{P}}^{3}_{g}
as in Remark
2.3
and furthermore that
{\mathbb{P}}^{r+2}_{f}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\simeq}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\mathbb{P}}^{r+2}_{g}
for all
r\geq 1
by Lemma
2.4
.
∎

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\mathbf{k}
be a field,
d\geq 3
be an integer and let
f,g\in{\mathbf{k}}[x_{0},x_{1},y,z]
be the homogeneous polynomials of degree
d
defined by

f=x_{0}^{d-1}y+z^{d}\quad\textrm{and}\quad g=x_{0}^{d-1}y+z^{d}+dx_{0}^{d-2}x_{1}^{2}\,.

Then, for each
r\geq 1
, the open subvarieties
{\mathbb{P}}^{r+3}_{f},{\mathbb{P}}^{r+3}_{g}\subseteq{\mathbb{P}}^{r+3}
are isomorphic.

Remark 3.5
.

We don’t know, whether the open subvarieties
{\mathbb{P}}^{3}_{f},{\mathbb{P}}^{3}_{g}\subseteq{\mathbb{P}}^{3}
are isomorphic,
or even if the hypersurfaces
X_{f,1},X_{g,1}\subseteq{\mathbb{A}}^{3}
given by
f=1
and
g=1
, respectively, are isomorphic or not.

Proof of Proposition
3.4
.

We define
s=1
,
S=\mathbf{k}[x_{0},x_{1}]
,
m=d
,
n=d-1
,
\Delta=x_{0}^{d-2}x_{1}^{2}
,

\begin{array}[]{rclrcl}P(z)&=&1-z^{d},&Q(z)&=&1-z^{d}-d\Delta,\\
A(z)&=&z(1-\Delta+\Delta^{2}),&B(z)&=&z(1+\Delta)\,,\end{array}

and prove that the hypotheses of Lemma
3.1
are satisfied for the above polynomials in
S[z]
.

Firstly, we calculate
A(B(z))=B(A(z))=z(1-\Delta+\Delta^{2})(1+\Delta)=z(1+\Delta^{3})
. As
d\geq 3
,
\Delta^{3}
is divisible by
x_{0}^{3d-6}
. Hence, we find that
A(B(z))=B(A(z))
is congruent to
z
modulo
x_{0}^{d}
.

We then check that
P(B(z)+x_{0}^{d}w)\in x_{0}^{d-1}S[z,w]+Q(z)S[z,w]
. As
d\geq 3
,
\Delta^{2}
is divisible by
x_{0}^{d-1}
. Hence:

\displaystyle P(B(z)+x_{0}^{d}w)

\displaystyle\equiv P(B(z))

\displaystyle\equiv 1-z^{d}(1+\Delta)^{d}

\displaystyle\equiv 1-z^{d}(1+d\Delta)

\displaystyle\equiv(1-z^{d}-d\Delta)\cdot(1+d\Delta)

\displaystyle\equiv Q\cdot(1+d\Delta)\,\pmod{x_{0}^{d-1}}.

Similarly, we have

Q(A(z)+x_{0}^{d}w)\in x_{0}^{d-1}S[z,w]+P(z)S[z,w]
:

\displaystyle Q(A(z)+x_{0}^{d}w)

\displaystyle\equiv Q(A(z))

\displaystyle\equiv 1-z^{d}(1-\Delta)^{d}-d\Delta

\displaystyle\equiv 1-z^{d}(1-d\Delta)-d\Delta

\displaystyle\equiv(1-z^{d})\cdot(1-d\Delta)

\displaystyle\equiv P\cdot(1-d\Delta)\,\pmod{x_{0}^{d-1}}.

Since
f-1=x_{0}^{d-1}y-P(z)
and
g-1=x_{0}^{d-1}y-Q(z)
, we may now apply Lemma
3.1
to obtain an isomorphism

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\varphi\colon\mathrm{Spec}({\mathbf{k}}[x_{0},x_{1},y,z,w]/(f-1))&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\simeq}}{{\longrightarrow}}&\mathrm{Spec}({\mathbf{k}}[x_{0},x_{1},y,z,w]/(g-1))\\
(x_{0},x_{1},y,z,w)&\mapsto&(x_{0},x_{1},\frac{Q(A(z)+x_{0}^{d}w)}{x_{0}^{d-1}},A(z)+x_{0}^{d}w,\\
&&\frac{z-B(A(z)+x_{0}^{d}w)}{x_{0}^{d}})\,.\end{array}

We again proceed as in Remark
2.3
.
The third component of
\varphi
can be expressed by a polynomial, since

Q(A(z)+x_{0}^{d}w)\equiv P\cdot(1-dx_{0}^{d-2}x_{1}^{2})\pmod{x_{0}^{d-1}}

and
P\equiv x_{0}^{d-1}y\pmod{f-1}
.
The last component of
\varphi
is already a polynomial, as its numerator is a multiple of its denominator. It remains to check that each monomial appearing in the expression of
\varphi
has degree congruent to
1
modulo
d
. For this, we simply work with the degrees modulo
d
and observe that
\Delta
is homogeneous of degree
0
, so that
A,B,P,Q
are homogeneous of degree
1,1,0,0
, respectively. Hence,
z-B(A(z)+x_{0}^{d}w)
,
Q(A(z)+x_{0}^{d}w)
and
P-x_{0}^{d-1}y
are homogeneous of degree
1
,
0
and
0
, respectively. Therefore, we can conclude by Remark
2.3
and Lemma
2.4
.
∎

3.2.
Non-isomorphic hypersurfaces

We now want to prove that the hypersurfaces of Propositions
3.2
and
3.4
are not isomorphic (except when the characteristic divides
d
, in which case both are in fact equal).

Recall that the multiplicity
\mathrm{mult}_{p}(X)
of a variety
X
at a point
p
is the multiplicity of the maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}_{X,p}
in the local ring
\mathcal{O}_{X,p}
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
n\geq 1
be an integer, let
H\subseteq{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
be a hypersurface of degree
d\geq 1
. The set
M=\{p\in H\mid\mathrm{mult}_{p}(H)=d\}
is either empty or a linear subspace of
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
.

Proof.

It suffices to take two different points
p,q\in M
and to prove that the line containing
p
and
q
is contained in
M
. Changing coordinates, we may assume that
p=[0:\cdots:0:1]
and
q=[0:\cdots:0:1:0]
. The equation of
H
is then given by a non-zero homogeneous polynomial
P\in{\mathbf{k}}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n-2}]
of degree
d\geq 1
(and thus we have
n\geq 2
). Hence the line
V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{n}}(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n-2})
(which passes through
p
and
q
) is contained in
M
.
∎

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\mathbf{k}
be a field, let
n\geq 1
and let
f,g\in\mathbf{k}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]
be homogeneous polynomials of the same degree
d\geq 1
. If
V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{n+1}}(f)
and
V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{n+1}}(g)
are isomorphic, then
V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{n}}(f)
and
V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{n}}(g)
are isomorphic.

Proof.

We write
H=V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{n+1}}(f)
,
H^{\prime}=V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{n+1}}(g)
.
Let
\varphi\colon H\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\simeq}}{{\longrightarrow}}H^{\prime}
be an isomorphism. It then sends
M
onto
M^{\prime}
, where
M=\{\,x\in H\ |\ \mathrm{mult}_{p}(H)=d\,\}
and
M^{\prime}=\{\,x\in H^{\prime}\ |\ \mathrm{mult}_{p}(H^{\prime})=d\,\}
. As
f,g\in{\mathbf{k}}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]
, the point
q=[0:\cdots:0:1]\in{\mathbb{P}}^{n+1}
belongs to both
M
and
M^{\prime}
. By Lemma
3.6
,
M
and
M^{\prime}
are linear subspaces of
{\mathbb{P}}^{n+1}
given by
r\geq 1

linearly independent linear polynomials in the variables
x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}
(and
r\leq n+1
). Applying a change of coordinates on
x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}
, we may assume that
M=M^{\prime}=V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{n+1}}(x_{0},\ldots,x_{r-1})
. The restriction of
\varphi
then becomes a linear automorphism of
M=M^{\prime}
, and
f,g\in{\mathbf{k}}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{r-1}]
. Replacing
\varphi
with
\varphi\circ\alpha
, where
\alpha\in\mathrm{Aut}({\mathbb{P}}^{n+1})
does nothing on
x_{0},\ldots,x_{r-1}
and is a linear automorphism on
x_{r},\ldots,x_{n+1}
, we may assume that
\varphi
fixes the point
q=[0:0:\cdots:0:1]
. It then induces an isomorphism
\hat{H}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\simeq}}{{\longrightarrow}}\hat{H}^{\prime}
, where
\hat{H}
,
\hat{H}^{\prime}
are the blow-ups of
H
and
H^{\prime}
at the point
q
. The exceptional divisors are isomorphic to
V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{n}}(f)
and
V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{n}}(g)
, respectively, so we find that these hypersurfaces are isomorphic.
∎

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
\mathbf{k}
be a field, let
d\geq 4
be an integer that is not a multiple of
\mathrm{char}(\mathbf{k})
and let

f=x^{d-1}y+z^{d}\text{ and }g=x^{d-1}y+z^{d}+\mu x^{d-2}z^{2},

where
\mu\in{\mathbf{k}}
. Suppose that
\alpha\colon V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{2}}(f)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\simeq}}{{\longrightarrow}}V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{2}}(g)
is an isomorphism. Then,
\mu=0
and
\alpha
is equal to
[x:y:z]\mapsto[a^{d}x:y:a^{d-1}z]
for some
a\in{\mathbf{k}}^{*}
.

Proof.

Expressing
y
in terms of
x,z
, we obtain two birational morphisms

\begin{array}[]{rrcl}\tau\colon&{\mathbb{P}}^{1}&\to&V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{2}}(f)\\
&{[u:v]}&\mapsto&[u^{d}:-v^{d}:u^{d-1}v]\end{array}

and

\begin{array}[]{rrcl}\tau^{\prime}\colon&{\mathbb{P}}^{1}&\to&V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{2}}(g)\\
&{[u:v]}&\mapsto&[u^{d}:-v^{d}-\mu u^{d-2}v^{2}:u^{d-1}v]\end{array}

whose inverses are given by
[x:y:z]\mapsto[x:z]
. They induce isomorphisms
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\setminus\{[0:1]\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\simeq}}{{\longrightarrow}}V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{2}}(f)\setminus\{[0:1:0]\}
and
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\setminus\{[0:1]\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\simeq}}{{\longrightarrow}}V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{2}}(g)\setminus\{[0:1:0]\}
.

Both
\tau
and
\tau^{\prime}
are bijective and they send
[0:1]
onto
[0:1:0]
, which is the unique singular point of
V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{2}}(f)
and
V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{2}}(g)
,
respectively. Since the isomorphism
\alpha\colon V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{2}}(f)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\simeq}}{{\longrightarrow}}V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{2}}(g)
must fix the point
[0:1:0]
, the birational map
\hat{\alpha}=(\tau^{\prime})^{-1}\alpha\tau
is an automorphism of
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
that fixes the point
[0:1]
. Thus,
\hat{\alpha}
is of the form
[u:v]\mapsto[u:av+bu]
for some
a\in{\mathbf{k}}^{*}
,
b\in{\mathbf{k}}
.

Note that
s_{a}\colon[x:y:z]\mapsto[a^{d}x:y:a^{d-1}z]
is an automorphism of
V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{2}}(f)
, that lifts to
\hat{s}_{a}=(\tau)^{-1}s_{a}\tau=[u:v]\mapsto[au:v]
. Replacing
\alpha
with
\alpha s_{a}
, we replace
\hat{\alpha}
with
\hat{\alpha}\hat{s}_{a}
,
and we may assume that
a=1
. It remains to see that
b=\mu=0
.
We calculate

\displaystyle\alpha([x:y:z])

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tau^{\prime}\hat{\alpha}\tau^{-1}([x:y:z])

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tau^{\prime}\hat{\alpha}([x:z])

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tau^{\prime}([x:z+bx])

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[x^{d}:-(z+bx)^{d}-\mu x^{d-2}(z+bx)^{2}:x^{d-1}(z+bx)]\,.

As
\alpha
fixes
[0:1:0]
, the pull-back by
\alpha
of
x/y
and
z/y
are rational functions

\frac{x^{d}}{-(z+bx)^{d}-\mu x^{d-2}(z+bx)^{2}}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{x^{d-1}}{-(z+bx)^{d-1}-\mu x^{d-2}(z+bx)}\,,

whose restrictions to
V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{2}}(f)
are regular at
[0:1:0]
. For each
j\in\{d-1,d\}
, there are thus two homogeneous polynomials
P_{j},Q_{j}\in{\mathbf{k}}[x,y,z]
, of the same degree, such that
Q_{j}(0,1,0)\neq 0
and such that

\frac{P_{j}(x,y,z)}{Q_{j}(x,y,z)}=\frac{x^{j}}{-(z+bx)^{j}-\mu x^{d-2}(z+bx)^{j+2-d}}

on the curve
V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{2}}(f)
. Using the morphism

{\mathbb{A}}^{1}\to V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{2}}(f)\,,\quad t\mapsto\tau([t:1])=[t^{d}:-1:t^{d-1}]=[t:-t^{1-d}:1]\,,

we find

\frac{P_{j}(t^{d},-1,t^{d-1})}{Q_{j}(t^{d},-1,t^{d-1})}=\frac{t^{j}}{B_{j}}\,,

where
B_{j}=-(1+bt)^{j}-\mu t^{d-2}(1+bt)^{j+2-d}\in\mathbf{k}[t]
.
As
Q_{j}(0,1,0)\neq 0
, the polynomial
Q_{j}(t^{d},-1,t^{d-1})\in{\mathbf{k}}[t]
is not divisible by
t
. As
B_{j}(0)\neq 0

(we use here
d\geq 4
),
t^{j}
divides
P_{j}(t^{d},-1,t^{d-1})
. There is thus
A_{j}\in{\mathbf{k}}[t]
such that

P_{j}(t^{d},-1,t^{d-1})=A_{j}\cdot t^{j}\quad\text{and}\quad Q_{j}(t^{d},-1,t^{d-1})=A_{j}\cdot B_{j}\,.

We consider now the case
j=d
and prove that
b=0
. We shall afterwards prove
\mu=0
by considering the case
j=d-1
.

As
d\geq 4
, the equality
P_{d}(t^{d},-1,t^{d-1})=A_{d}\cdot t^{d}
gives the existence of
\epsilon\in{\mathbf{k}}
such that
A_{d}\equiv\epsilon\pmod{t^{2}}
. We moreover have
B_{d}\equiv-1-bdt\pmod{t^{2}}
. This gives

Q_{d}(t^{d},-1,t^{d-1})=A_{d}\cdot B_{d}\equiv-\epsilon-\epsilon bdt\pmod{t^{2}}.

The polynomial
Q(t^{d},-1,t^{d-1})
is not divisible by
t
, so
\epsilon\neq 0
, and its coefficient of
t
is zero, as
d\geq 4
. Since
\mathrm{char}({\mathbf{k}})
does not divide
d
, this gives
b=0
.

We now use
j=d-1
. As
d\geq 4
, the equality
P_{d-1}(t^{d},-1,t^{d-1})=A_{d-1}\cdot t^{d-1}
gives the existence of
\xi,\xi^{\prime}\in{\mathbf{k}}
such that
A_{d-1}\equiv\xi+t\xi^{\prime}\pmod{t^{d-1}}
. As
b=0
, we find
B_{d-1}=B_{d}=-1-\mu t^{d-2}
. This gives

Q_{d-1}(t^{d},-1,t^{d-1})=A_{d-1}\cdot B_{d-1}\equiv-\xi-t\xi^{\prime}-\mu\xi t^{d-2}\pmod{t^{d-1}}.

As
t
does not divide this polynomial,
\xi\neq 0
. Moreover, we obtain
\xi^{\prime}=0
and
\mu=0
, as
d\geq 4
.
∎

Proposition 3.9
.

Let
\mathbf{k}
be a field, let
d\geq 4
be an integer that is not a multiple of
\mathrm{char}(\mathbf{k})
and let
f,g\in\mathbf{k}[x,y,z]
be defined by

f=x^{d-1}y+z^{d}\text{ and }g=x^{d-1}y+z^{d}+\mu x^{d-2}z^{2},

where
\mu\in{\mathbf{k}}^{*}
. Then, for each
r\geq 0
, the two hypersurfaces
V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{r+2}}(f)
,
V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{r+2}}(g)
in
{\mathbb{P}}^{r+2}
are not isomorphic.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.8
, the result is true when
r=0
. Using Lemma
3.7
, we can then argue by induction and obtain the result for every integer
r
.
∎

Proposition 3.10
.

Let
\mathbf{k}
be a field,
d\geq 3
be an integer and let
f,g\in\mathbf{k}[x_{0},x_{1},y,z]
be defined by

f=x_{0}^{d-1}y+z^{d}\,,\quad g=x_{0}^{d-1}y+z^{d}+\mu x_{0}^{d-2}x_{1}^{2}\,,

where
\mu\in{\mathbf{k}}^{*}
. Then, for each
r\geq 0
, the two hypersurfaces
V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{r+3}}(f)
,
V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{r+3}}(g)
in
{\mathbb{P}}^{r+3}
are not isomorphic.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.7
, it suffices to consider the case
r=0
and to prove that
V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{3}}(f)
and
V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{3}}(g)
are not isomorphic. Looking at the derivative with respect to
y
, the singular locus of both hypersurfaces is contained in the line
\ell\subseteq{\mathbb{P}}^{3}

given by
x_{0}=z=0
.

The surface
V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{3}}(f)
has multiplicity
d
at the point where
x_{0}=y=z=0
.

It remains to see that
V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{3}}(g)
has multiplicity
<d
at every point. For this, write
g=z^{d}+x_{0}^{d-2}(x_{0}y+\mu x_{1}^{2})
and observe that
V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{3}}(x_{0}y+\mu x_{1}^{2})
is smooth outside
x_{0}=x_{1}=y=0
and thus on
\ell
.
∎

We may now prove Theorem
A
, which is a direct consequence of Propositions
3.2
,
3.4
,
3.9
and
3.10
.

Proof of Theorem
A
.

As in the statement, we take a field
\mathbf{k}
, and integers
d,n\geq 3
such that
d
is not a multiple of
\mathrm{char}(\mathbf{k})
and such that
(d,n)\neq(3,3)
. We are looking for hypersurfaces
H=V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{n}}(f),H^{\prime}=V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{n}}(g)\subseteq{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{\mathbf{k}}
that are not isomorphic but have isomorphic complements.

If
d\geq 4
, we may choose
f=x^{d-1}y+z^{d}
and
g=x^{d-1}y+z^{d}+dx^{d-2}z^{2}
, that we see in
{\mathbf{k}}[x,y,z,w_{1},\ldots,w_{r}]
with
r=n-2\geq 1
. By Propositions
3.2
, the complements of
H
and
H^{\prime}
are isomorphic, and by Proposition
3.9
, the hypersurfaces
H
and
H^{\prime}
are not isomorphic.

If
n\geq 4
, we may choose
f=x_{0}^{d-1}y+z^{d}
and
g=x_{0}^{d-1}y+z^{d}+dx_{0}^{d-2}x_{1}^{2}
, that we see in
{\mathbf{k}}[x_{0},x_{1},y,z,w_{1},\ldots,w_{r}]
with

. By Propositions
3.4
, the complements of
H
and

are isomorphic, and by Proposition

, the hypersurfaces
H
and

are not isomorphic.
∎

4.
Topological Euler characteristic and piecewise isomorphisms

Throughout this section we assume that
\mathbf{k}
is algebraically closed.

In the sequel we recall the definition and some basic facts of the topological Euler characteristic.
In order to do this, we also recall some facts from étale
\ell
-adic cohomology with compact support.
As a reference we take
[
Mil13
]
and
[
Mil80
]
.

Let
\ell
be a prime number that is different from the characteristic of the ground field
\mathbf{k}
.
For a variety
X
, the group
H^{i}_{c}(X_{\textrm{\'{e}t}},{\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell})

denotes the
i
-th étale
\ell
-adic cohomology with compact support,
i.e.

H^{i}_{c}(X_{\textrm{\'{e}t}},{\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell})\coloneqq\left(\varprojlim H^{i}_{c}(X_{\textrm{\'{e}t}},{\mathbb{Z}}/\ell^{n}{\mathbb{Z}})\right)\otimes_{{\mathbb{Z}}_{\ell}}{\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell}\,,

where
{\mathbb{Z}}_{\ell}\coloneqq\varprojlim{\mathbb{Z}}/\ell^{n}{\mathbb{Z}}
and
{\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell}
is the quotient field of
{\mathbb{Z}}_{\ell}
,
see e.g.
[
Mil13
, §18, §19]
. We have:

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
X
be a variety. Then the
{\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell}
-vector space

H^{i}_{c}(X_{\textrm{\'{e}t}},{\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell})
has finite dimension and vanishes for
i>2\dim X
.

Proof.

Let
k\colon X\to\bar{X}
be a completion. By definition
(see e.g.
[
Mil13
, Definition 18.1]
) we have

H^{i}_{c}(X_{\textrm{\'{e}t}},{\mathbb{Z}}/\ell^{n}{\mathbb{Z}})=H^{i}(\bar{X}_{\textrm{\'{e}t}},k_{!}({\mathbb{Z}}/\ell^{n}{\mathbb{Z}}))\,.

By
[
Mil13
, Theorem 19.2]
the limit
\varprojlim H^{i}(\bar{X}_{\textrm{\'{e}t}},k_{!}({\mathbb{Z}}/\ell^{n}{\mathbb{Z}}))

is a finitely generated
{\mathbb{Z}}_{\ell}
-module; this implies the first statement.
By
[
Mil13
, Theorem 15.1]
, we have that
H^{i}(\bar{X}_{\textrm{\'{e}t}},k_{!}({\mathbb{Z}}/\ell^{n}{\mathbb{Z}}))

vanishes for all
i>2\dim X
and hence the second statement follows.
∎

The topological Euler-characteristic of a variety
X
is defined by

\chi(X)\coloneqq\sum_{i=0}^{2\dim X}(-1)^{i}\dim_{{\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell}}H^{i}_{c}(X_{\textrm{\'{e}t}},{\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell})\,.

The following properties are very useful in order to compute the topological Euler characteristic.
For lack of
a reference with proof, we give an argument here:

Lemma 4.2
.

We have:

(1)

Let
X
be a variety and let
Z\subseteq X
be a closed subvariety. Then

\chi(X)=\chi(X\setminus Z)+\chi(Z)
.

(2)

Let
X,Y
be varieties. Then
\chi(X\times Y)=\chi(X)\cdot\chi(Y)
.

Proof.

(1)
: Let
U\coloneqq X\setminus Z
.
By
[
AGV73
, Ch.XVII, §5.1.16]
we have a long exact sequence

\displaystyle\ldots\to H^{i}_{c}(U_{\textrm{\'{e}t}},{\mathbb{Z}}/\ell^{n}{\mathbb{Z}})\to H^{i}_{c}(X_{\textrm{\'{e}t}},{\mathbb{Z}}/\ell^{n}{\mathbb{Z}})\to H^{i}_{c}(Z_{\textrm{\'{e}t}},{\mathbb{Z}}/\ell^{n}{\mathbb{Z}})

\displaystyle\to H^{i+1}_{c}(U_{\textrm{\'{e}t}},{\mathbb{Z}}/\ell^{n}{\mathbb{Z}})\to\ldots\,.

Taking the limit over
n

and tensoring with
{\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell}
over
{\mathbb{Z}}_{\ell}

gives a long exact sequence

\ldots\to H^{i}_{c}(U_{\textrm{\'{e}t}},{\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell})\to H^{i}_{c}(X_{\textrm{\'{e}t}},{\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell})\to H^{i}_{c}(Z_{\textrm{\'{e}t}},{\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell})\to H^{i+1}_{c}(U_{\textrm{\'{e}t}},{\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell})\to\ldots\,.

The statement follows now by using Lemma
4.1
.

(2)
: By the Künneth-formula
(see
[
Mil80
, Ch. VI, Corollary 8.23]
)
we get for
0\leq k\leq\dim X+\dim Y

\dim_{{\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell}}H^{k}_{c}((X\times Y)_{\textrm{\'{e}t}},{\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell})=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\dim_{{\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell}}H^{i}_{c}(X_{\textrm{\'{e}t}},{\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell})\otimes_{{\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell}}H^{k-i}_{c}(Y_{\textrm{\'{e}t}},{\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell})\,,

which gives
\chi(X\times Y)=\chi(X)\cdot\chi(Y)
.
∎

Example 4.3
.

Let
C
be a smooth irreducible projective curve of genus
g
. Then

H^{0}(C,{\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell})=H^{2}(C,{\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell})={\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell}\quad\textrm{and}\quad H^{1}(C,{\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell})={\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell}^{2g}\,,

see
[
Mil13
, Proposition 14.2]
. Using that
H^{i}(C,{\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell})=0
for all
i>2

(see Lemma
4.1
), it follows that
\chi(C)=2-2g
.

Example 4.4
.

Let
m\geq 0
. Then

H^{i}({\mathbb{P}}^{m},{\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}{\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell}\,,&\textrm{$0\leq i\leq 2m$ and $i$ is even}\\
0\,,&\textrm{otherwise}\end{array}\right.\,,

see
[
Mil13
, Example 16.3]
. Hence,

\chi({\mathbb{P}}^{m})=m+1
. Using Lemma
4.2
and that

{\mathbb{A}}^{m}\simeq{\mathbb{P}}^{m}\setminus{\mathbb{P}}^{m-1}
for all
m\geq 0

(where
{\mathbb{P}}^{m-1}
is linearly embedded in
{\mathbb{P}}^{m}
and

{\mathbb{P}}^{-1}=\varnothing
), it follows that
\chi({\mathbb{A}}^{m})=1
for all
m\geq 0
.

Example 4.5
.

Let
n\geq 0
and let
\pi\colon X\to B
be a locally trivial
{\mathbb{A}}^{n}
-bundle with respect to the Zariski topology, where

B
is any variety.
Then
\chi(X)=\chi(B)
. Indeed, we proceed by induction on
\dim B
and note that the
case
\dim B=0
is clear. By assumption there exists a closed subvariety
B^{\prime}\subseteq B

such that
\pi
is trivial over
B\setminus B^{\prime}
and
\dim B^{\prime}<\dim B
. Hence,

\chi(X)=\chi({\mathbb{A}}^{n}\times(B\setminus B^{\prime}))+\chi(\pi^{-1}(B^{\prime}))=\chi({\mathbb{A}}^{n}\times B)=\chi({\mathbb{A}}^{n})\chi(B)=\chi(B)\,,

where for the second equality we used the induction hypothesis
\chi(\pi^{-1}(B^{\prime}))=\chi(B^{\prime})=\chi({\mathbb{A}}^{n}\times B^{\prime})
.

Let us denote by
\Var_{\mathbf{k}}
the set of isomorphism classes of varieties (over
\mathbf{k}
)
and denote by
{\mathbb{Z}}\Var_{\mathbf{k}}
the free abelian group over
\Var_{\mathbf{k}}
. Moreover, let

I\subseteq{\mathbb{Z}}\Var_{\mathbf{k}}
be the subgroup that is generated by

[X]-[Z]-[X\setminus Z]

for all varieties
X
and closed subvarieties
Z
(here
[W]
denotes the isomorphism class of a variety
W
).
By bilinear extension of the operation
[X]\cdot[Y]\coloneqq[X\times Y]
we get a ring structure on

{\mathbb{Z}}\Var_{\mathbf{k}}
and
I
is an ideal of it. The
Grothendieck ring
is then the quotient of
{\mathbb{Z}}\Var_{\mathbf{k}}
by
I
:

K_{0}(\Var_{\mathbf{k}})\coloneqq{\mathbb{Z}}\Var_{\mathbf{k}}/I\,,

see e.g.
[
LS10
, §2.2.1]
.
By abuse of notation we denote by
[X]
the class of a variety
X
in
K_{0}(\Var_{\mathbf{k}})
.
Using Lemma
4.2
, the topological Euler characteristic
gives a ring homomorphism

\chi\colon K_{0}(\Var_{\mathbf{k}})\to{\mathbb{Z}}\,,\quad\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}[X_{i}]\mapsto\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}\chi(X_{i}).

Definition 4.6
.

Let
X,Y
be varieties. Then
X,Y
are called
piecewise isomorphic
if
X
and
Y

can be decomposed as disjoint unions of
n\geq 1
locally closed subsets
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
and

Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}
, respectively, such that
X_{i}\simeq Y_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq n
.
Note that we take on
X_{i}
the reduced scheme structure.

Note that “piecewise isomorphic” defines an equivalence relation among the varieties.

Remark 4.7
.

In the above definition, if some
X_{i}
is not irreducible, we replace
X_{i}
in the decomposition by one of its irreducible components and its complement, and do the same for their images in
Y_{i}
. After finitely many such steps we may assume that all
X_{i}
are irreducible, and thus all
Y_{i}
are irreducible.

Remark 4.8
.

If
X,Y
are piecewise isomorphic, then
\dim X=\dim Y
.

Example 4.9
.

Let
\Gamma\subseteq{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
be an irreducible cuspidal cubic curve. Then
\Gamma
is piecewise isomorphic to
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
. Indeed, we choose
X_{1},X_{2}\subseteq\Gamma
as
X_{1}
to be the singular locus (one point) and
X_{2}=\Gamma\setminus X_{1}
. Then,
X_{1}
is isomorphic to
Y_{1}=[0:1]\in{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
and
X_{2}
is isomorphic to
Y_{2}={\mathbb{P}}^{2}\setminus Y_{1}\simeq{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
.

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
X,Y
be varieties and consider the following statements:

(1)

X
and
Y
are piecewise isomorphic;

(2)

There are open subsets
U\subseteq X
,
V\subseteq Y
such that

U\simeq V\,,\quad\dim X\setminus U=\dim Y\setminus V<\dim X=\dim Y

and
X\setminus U
,
Y\setminus V
are piecewise isomorphic.
Moreover, if
X^{\prime}\subseteq X
,
Y^{\prime}\subseteq Y
are locally closed subsets such that

\dim X\setminus X^{\prime},\dim Y\setminus Y^{\prime}<\dim X=\dim Y

we may assume that
U\subseteq X^{\prime}
and
V\subseteq Y^{\prime}
.

(3)

[X]=[Y]
inside
K_{0}(\Var_{\mathbf{k}})
;

(4)

\chi(X)=\chi(Y)
.

Then we have
\ref{lemm.Grothendieckring1}\Leftrightarrow\ref{lemm.Grothendieckring1.5}\Rightarrow\ref{lemm.Grothendieckring3}\Rightarrow\ref{lemm.Grothendieckring4}
.

Proof.

“
\ref{lemm.Grothendieckring1}\Rightarrow\ref{lemm.Grothendieckring1.5}
”:
Let
X_{1},\ldots X_{n}
and
Y_{1},\ldots Y_{n}
be disjoint locally closed subsets of
X
and
Y
, respectively,
such that their union is equal to
X
and
Y
, respectively, and such that there are isomorphisms

\psi_{i}\colon X_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\simeq}}{{\longrightarrow}}Y_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq n
.
By replacing
X_{i}
with
X_{i}\cap X^{\prime}
and
X_{i}\setminus X^{\prime}
and analogously for
Y_{i}
we may assume
that each
X_{i}
is either contained in
X^{\prime}
or
X\setminus X^{\prime}
and

Y_{i}
is either contained in
Y^{\prime}
or
Y\setminus Y^{\prime}
.
Using Remark
4.7
, we may moreover assume that all
X_{i}
and
Y_{i}
are irreducible.

Let
d\coloneqq\dim X=\dim Y
. After reordering, we may assume that the closures
\overline{X_{1}},\ldots,\overline{X_{m}}
in
X
are
the irreducible components of
X
of dimension
d
.
Then
\overline{Y_{1}},\ldots,\overline{Y_{m}}
are irreducible closed subsets of
Y
of dimension
d

and since
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{m}
are disjoint and locally closed in
Y
,

\overline{Y_{1}},\ldots,\overline{Y_{m}}
are mutually different. This
implies that
\overline{Y}_{1},\ldots,\overline{Y}_{m}
are the irreducible components of
Y
of dimension
d
.
For
1\leq i\leq m
, note that
X_{i}
and
Y_{i}
is contained in
X^{\prime}
and
Y^{\prime}
, respectively, since
X\setminus X^{\prime}

and
Y\setminus Y^{\prime}
have strictly lower dimension that
X
and
Y
.

For each
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
, the locally closed subset
X_{i}\subseteq X
is equal to
X_{i}=A\cap\overline{X_{i}}
, where
A\subseteq X_{i}
is open. Choosing
U_{i}^{\prime}\subseteq X
to be the open subset
A\setminus I
, where
I
is the union of all
irreducible components of
X
different from
\overline{X_{i}}
, we find a subset
U^{\prime}_{i}\subseteq X_{i}
that is open in
X
with
\overline{U^{\prime}_{i}}=\overline{X}_{i}
. By construction,
U_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,U^{\prime}_{m}
are disjoint.
Similarly, there exist open disjoint subsets
V^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,V^{\prime}_{m}
in
Y
such that
for all
1\leq i\leq m
we get
V^{\prime}_{i}\subseteq Y^{\prime}_{i}
and
\overline{V_{i}^{\prime}}=\overline{Y_{i}}
. For
1\leq i\leq m
, let

U_{i}\coloneqq U^{\prime}_{i}\cap\psi_{i}^{-1}(V^{\prime}_{i})\subseteq X\quad\textrm{and}\quad V_{i}\coloneqq\psi_{i}(U^{\prime}_{i})\cap V^{\prime}_{i}\subseteq Y\,.

Then the restriction
\psi_{i}|_{U_{i}}\colon U_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\simeq}}{{\longrightarrow}}V_{i}
is an isomorphism.
Let
U\coloneqq\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}U_{i}
and
V\coloneqq\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}V_{i}
.
By construction,
U\subseteq X^{\prime}
and
V\subseteq Y^{\prime}
.
Since

U_{1},\ldots,U_{m}
are disjoint open subsets of
X
and

V_{1},\ldots,V_{m}
are disjoint open subsets of
Y
, we have
U\simeq V
.
Moreover,
X\setminus U
and
Y\setminus V
are piecewise isomorphic
(take the locally closed subsets
X_{1}\setminus U_{1},\ldots,X_{m}\setminus U_{m},X_{m+1},\ldots,X_{n}

and
Y_{1}\setminus V_{1},\ldots,Y_{m}\setminus V_{m},Y_{m+1},\ldots,Y_{n}
of
X
and
Y
,
respectively). By construction,

\dim X\setminus U<\dim X
.

“
\ref{lemm.Grothendieckring1.5}\Rightarrow\ref{lemm.Grothendieckring1}
”: The decomposition of
X\setminus U
, together with
U
, gives the decomposition of
X
, and we do similarly for
Y
.

“
\ref{lemm.Grothendieckring1.5}\Rightarrow\ref{lemm.Grothendieckring3}
”:
By assumption we have
[U]=[V]
inside
K_{0}(\Var_{\mathbf{k}})
.
As
X\setminus U
,
Y\setminus V
are piecewise isomorphic and since

\dim X\setminus U=\dim Y\setminus V<\dim X=\dim Y
we may proceed by induction on
d

in order to get
[X\setminus U]=[Y\setminus V]
inside
K_{0}(\Var_{\mathbf{k}})
. This implies
(3)
.

“
\ref{lemm.Grothendieckring3}\Rightarrow\ref{lemm.Grothendieckring4}
”: Follows from Lemma
4.2
.
∎

Corollary 4.11
.

If
X
and
Y
are two varieties that are both equidimensional and piecewise isomorphic, then
X
and
Y
are birational to each other.

Proof.

Follows from the implication
(1)

\Rightarrow

(2)

in Lemma
4.10
.
∎

Example 4.12
.

Corollary
4.11
really needs both
X
and
Y
to be equidimensional. Indeed,
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}\amalg\{pt\}
and
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
are piecewise isomorphic, but not birational.

Lemma 4.13
.

For
i\in\{1,2\}
, let
\Gamma_{i}
be a variety of dimension
1
, and let
\Gamma_{i}^{\prime}\subseteq\Gamma_{i}
be the union of the irreducible components of
\Gamma_{i}
of dimension
1

(
we remove isolated points
)
. Then, the following are equivalent:

(1)

\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
are piecewise isomorphic;

(2)

\Gamma_{1}^{\prime}
and
\Gamma_{2}^{\prime}
are birational, and
\chi(\Gamma_{1})=\chi(\Gamma_{2})
.

Proof.

“
\ref{CurvepiecewiseBir1}\Rightarrow\ref{CurvepiecewiseBir2}
”: Suppose that
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
are piecewise isomorphic. The implication
(1)

\Rightarrow

(2)

in Lemma
4.10
implies that
\Gamma_{1}^{\prime}
and
\Gamma_{2}^{\prime}
are birational and the implication
(1)

\Rightarrow

(4)
yields
\chi(\Gamma_{1})=\chi(\Gamma_{2})
.

“
\ref{CurvepiecewiseBir2}\Rightarrow\ref{CurvepiecewiseBir1}
”: As
\Gamma_{1}^{\prime}
and
\Gamma_{2}^{\prime}
are birational, there are open dense subsets
U_{1}\subseteq\Gamma_{1}^{\prime}
and
U_{2}\subseteq\Gamma_{2}^{\prime}
such that
U_{1}\simeq U_{2}
. For each
i\in\{1,2\}
, the set
U_{i}
is open in
\Gamma_{i}
, and
F_{i}=\Gamma_{i}\setminus U_{i}
is finite. As
\chi(\Gamma_{1})=\chi(\Gamma_{2})
and
\chi(U_{1})=\chi(U_{2})
, Lemma
4.2
implies that
\chi(F_{1})=\chi(F_{2})
. The two finite sets
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
are thus isomorphic, which proves that

and

are piecewise isomorphic.
∎

Lemma 4.14
.

(1)

Let
X
be a disjoint union of trees of smooth projective rational curves, with
r
irreducible components and
s
connected components. Then,
X
is piecewise isomorphic to the disjoint union of
r
copies of
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
and
s
points.

(2)

Let
X
,
Y
be two disjoint unions of trees of smooth projective rational curves. Then,
X
and
Y
are piecewise isomorphic if and only if they have the same number of irreducible components and the same number of connected components.

Proof.

(1)
:
If
T
is a tree of smooth projective rational curves, with
r
irreducible components, it is piecewise isomorphic to the disjoint union of one point and
r
copies of
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
. Hence, if
X
has
r
irreducible components and
s
connected components, then
X
is piecewise isomorphic to the disjoint union of
r
copies of
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
and
s
points, and thus satisfies

(Lemma
4.2
and Example

)

(2)
: By Lemma

,

and

are piecewise isomorphic if and only if they are birational and

∎

Lemma 4.15
.

Let
U\subseteq{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
be a dense open subset.
For
i=1,2
, let

X_{i}\coloneqq U\amalg S_{i}\amalg R_{i}\,,

where
S_{i}
is a disjoint union of
s_{i}\geq 0
points and
R_{i}
is the disjoint union of
r_{i}\geq 0
irreducible curves.
If

and
X_{2}
are piecewise isomorphic, then:

(1)

R_{1}
and
R_{2}
are birational; in particular

.

(2)

If moreover all irreducible components of
R_{1}
,
R_{2}
are isomorphic, then

.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.10
there exist open dense subsets

that are isomorphic and such that

and

Let
({\mathbb{P}}^{2}\setminus U_{i})^{\prime}
and
(U\setminus U_{i})^{\prime}
be the union of one-dimensional irreducible
components of
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}\setminus U_{i}
and

, respectively. The isomorphism

gives a birational map

{\mathbb{P}}^{2}\dasharrow{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
that decomposes into
n
blow-ups and
n
blow-downs.
Hence,
({\mathbb{P}}^{2}\setminus U_{1})^{\prime}
and

are birational and thus

(U\setminus U_{1})^{\prime}
and

are birational as well.
By Lemma
4.13
, the unions of

and

are birational
and

it follows that

and

are birational. If the irreducible components of

and

are all isomorphic to

, we get

Proposition 4.17
.

Let
X
,
Y
be two varieties, that are piecewise isomorphic and let
E\subseteq X
,
F\subseteq Y
be locally closed subvarieties of dimension at most
1
. If
E
and
F
are piecewise isomorphic, then
X\setminus E
and
Y\setminus F
are piecewise isomorphic.

Proof.

By definition,
X
and
Y

can be decomposed as disjoint unions of
n\geq 1
locally closed subsets
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
and

Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}
, respectively, such that there is an isomorphism
\varphi_{i}\colon X_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\simeq}}{{\longrightarrow}}Y_{i}
for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
.

\mathbf{(A)}
We first consider the case where
E
is one point (and thus
F
is also one point). In this case, there is exactly one
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
and one
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
such that
E\in X_{i}
and
F\subseteq Y_{j}
. If
i=j
, the isomorphism
\varphi_{i}
induces an isomorphism from
X_{i}\setminus(E\cup\varphi_{i}^{-1}(F))
to
Y_{i}\setminus(F\cup\varphi_{i}(E))
, and we are done if
\varphi_{i}(E)=F
or otherwise use an isomorphism from the point
\varphi_{i}^{-1}(F)
to the point
\varphi_{i}(E)
.
If
i\neq j
, then
\varphi_{i}
gives an isomorphisms
X_{i}\setminus E\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\simeq}}{{\longrightarrow}}Y_{i}\setminus\varphi_{i}(E)
and
\varphi_{j}
gives an isomorphism
X_{j}\setminus\varphi_{j}^{-1}(F)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\simeq}}{{\longrightarrow}}Y_{j}\setminus F
. It then remains to use an isomorphism from the point
\varphi_{j}^{-1}(F)
to the point
\varphi_{i}(E)
.

\mathbf{(B)}
If
E
(and thus
F
) is finite, we proceed by induction on the number of points, applying
\mathbf{(A)}
, and obtain the result.

\mathbf{(C)}
In the general case, we proceed by induction on the number of irreducible components of
E
of dimension
1
. When no such component exists, we are done by
\mathbf{(B)}
. As
E
and
F
are piecewise isomorphic, there are open subsets
U^{\prime}\subseteq E
and
V^{\prime}\subseteq F
such that
E\setminus U^{\prime}
and
F\setminus V^{\prime}
are finite, with the same number of points, and there is an isomorphism
\psi\colon U^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\simeq}}{{\longrightarrow}}V^{\prime}
(Lemma
4.10
).
We take an open subset
U\subseteq U^{\prime}
, that is irreducible and infinite, take
V=\psi(U)\subseteq V^{\prime}
, which is also open, irreducible and infinite, and obtain that
E\setminus U
and
F\setminus V
are piecewise isomorphic. As
U,V
are irreducible curves, there is exactly one
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
and one
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
such that
U\cap X_{i}
and
V\cap Y_{j}
are infinite.
By removing finitely many points of
U
and
V
respectively, we may assume that
U\subseteq X_{i}
,
V\subseteq Y_{j}
and still assume that
U
,
V
are isomorphic (we remove points outside of
X_{i}
or
Y_{j}
and their images under
\psi
or
\psi^{-1}
), and that
E\setminus U
,
F\setminus V
are piecewise isomorphic.
We now prove that
X\setminus U
and
Y\setminus V
are piecewise isomorphic, which will achieve the proof, as
E\setminus U
and
F\setminus V
are piecewise isomorphic, with one irreducible component of dimension
1
less.

If
i=j
, the isomorphism
\varphi_{i}
restricts to an isomorphism

X_{i}\setminus(U\cup\varphi_{i}^{-1}(V))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\simeq}}{{\longrightarrow}}Y_{i}\setminus(V\cup\varphi_{i}(U))\,,

so we only need to see that
\varphi_{i}^{-1}(V)\setminus(U\cap\varphi_{i}^{-1}(V))
and
\varphi_{i}(U)\setminus(V\cap\varphi_{i}(U))
are piecewise isomorphic. Applying
\varphi_{i}
to the first set, we need to show that
V\setminus(\varphi_{i}(U)\cap V)
and
\varphi_{i}(U)\setminus(V\cap\varphi_{i}(U))
are piecewise isomorphic. If
\varphi_{i}(U)\cap V
is finite, this follows from
\mathbf{(B)}
. If
\varphi_{i}(U)\cap V
is infinite, then
V\setminus(\varphi_{i}(U)\cap V)
and
\varphi_{i}(U)\setminus(V\cap\varphi_{i}(U))
are both finite, with the same number of points
by using the topological Euler characteristic (Lemma
4.2
).

If
i\neq j
, then
\varphi_{i}
gives an isomorphisms
X_{i}\setminus U\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\simeq}}{{\longrightarrow}}Y_{i}\setminus\varphi_{i}(U)
and
\varphi_{j}
gives an isomorphism
X_{j}\setminus\varphi_{j}^{-1}(V)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\simeq}}{{\longrightarrow}}Y_{j}\setminus V
. It then remains to use an isomorphism from the curve
\varphi_{j}^{-1}(V)
to the point
\varphi_{i}(U)
.
∎

Lemma 4.18
.

Let
X
be a smooth projective rational surface with
Picard group
\Pic(X)\simeq{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
. Then, there is an open subset
U\subseteq X
isomorphic to
{\mathbb{A}}^{2}
, a point
p\in X
and locally closed curves
C_{1},\ldots,C_{n}\subseteq X
such that each
C_{i}
is isomorphic to
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
and
X
is the disjoint union

X=U\amalg C_{1}\amalg\cdots\amalg C_{n}\amalg\{p\}\,.

Moreover, for each finite set
\Delta\subseteq X
and each closed curve
E\subseteq X
, we may choose the above decomposition such that
\Delta\subseteq U
,
\{p\}\cap(\Delta\cup E)=\varnothing
and
C_{i}\not\subseteq E
for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
.

Proof.

If
X={\mathbb{P}}^{2}
, we choose a general line
\ell\subseteq{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
and a general point
p\in\ell
, and let
U={\mathbb{P}}^{2}\setminus\ell
,
C_{1}=\ell\setminus\{p\}
. This gives the statement in case
X={\mathbb{P}}^{2}
.

We may thus assume that
X
is not isomorphic to
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
, so there is a birational morphism
X\to\mathbb{F}_{d}
for some Hirzebruch surface
\mathbb{F}_{d}
where
d\geq 0
.
We proceed by induction on the number of blow-ups of
X\to{\mathbb{F}}_{d}
. If
X={\mathbb{F}}_{d}
, we choose a general section
\ell_{1}

of
{\mathbb{F}}_{d}\to{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
of self-intersection
d
and a general fibre
\ell_{2}
of
{\mathbb{F}}_{d}\to{\mathbb{P}}^{1}

and let
p\coloneqq\ell_{1}\cap\ell_{2}
,
U\coloneqq{\mathbb{F}}_{d}\setminus(\ell_{1}\cup\ell_{2})
.
Then,
C_{i}=\ell_{i}\setminus\{p\}\simeq{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
for each
i\in\{1,2\}
.

We then do successive blow-ups, where we may always assume that the points blown-up are in the subset
U\simeq{\mathbb{A}}^{2}
. Then, taking a general line of
{\mathbb{A}}^{2}
passing through the point, the strict transform of the line is a closed curve
C_{i}\simeq{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
of the blow-up of
{\mathbb{A}}^{2}
, with complement isomorphic to
{\mathbb{A}}^{2}
.
∎

Proposition 4.19
.

Let
Y
be a normal
projective rational surface,
that admits a desingularisation
X\to Y
with
\Pic(X)\simeq{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}

and exceptional divisor being a finite disjoint union of trees of smooth rational curves with
r\geq 0

irreducible components
(
this holds for instance when
Y
only has Du Val singularities
)
.
Then,
Y
is piecewise isomorphic to the disjoint union of
{\mathbb{A}}^{2}
, one point, and
n-r
copies of
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
,
with
n-r\geq 1
.

Proof.

If
Y
is smooth, then the result, with
r=0
, directly follows from Lemma
4.18
.
We then assume that
Y
is singular. Let
E\subseteq X
be the exceptional divisor of the desingularisation
X\to Y
,
let
r
be the number of irreducible components of
E
and
let
s
be the number of connected components of
E
.
Then
s
is equal to the number of points of the image of
E
under
X\to Y
,
since all fibres of
X\to Y
are connected by the normality of
Y
,
see Zariski’s main theorem,
[
Har77
, Ch. III, Corollary 11.4]
.
Moreover,
r,s\geq 1
, since otherwise every fibre of the proper birational morphism
X\to Y
onto the normal variety
Y

would be a single point, i.e.
X\to Y
would be an isomorphism, see

[
Gro61
, Corollaire 4.4.9]
.

We observe that
n-r\geq 1
. Indeed, the
r
irreducible components
E_{1},\ldots,E_{r}
of
E
, together with the pull-back
P
of an irreducible curve of
Y
not passing through the image of
E
under
Y\to X

are
r+1
effective divisors on
X
, that are linearly independent:
Assume
D\coloneqq aP+\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{i}E_{i}=0
in
\Pic(X)
for some

a,a_{1},\ldots,a_{r}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. Then
0=L\cdot D=aL^{2}
, which shows that
a=0
. Since the intersection matrix

(E_{i}\cdot E_{j})
is negative definite (see
[
Art62
, §2, (2.1)]
), we get
a_{1}=\ldots=a_{r}=0
.

By Lemma
4.14
(1)
,
E
is piecewise isomorphic to the disjoint union of
r
copies of
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
and
s
points.
By Lemma
4.18
,
X
is piecewise isomorphic to the disjoint union of
{\mathbb{A}}^{2}
with one point and
n
copies of
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
.

Hence, Proposition
4.17
shows that
X\setminus E
is piecewise isomorphic to the disjoint union of
{\mathbb{A}}^{2}\setminus\Delta
with
n-r
copies of
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
, where
\Delta
is a finite set of
s-1
points.

Let
S
be the image of
E
under
X\to Y
.
As
Y\setminus S
is isomorphic to
X\setminus E
and
S
contains
s
points, this proves that

Y
is piecewise isomorphic to the disjoint union of
{\mathbb{A}}^{2}
with one point and
n-r
copies of
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
.
∎

5.1.
Degree two

The goal of this subsection is to prove Theorem
B
, which gives an
affirmative answer to Question
(2)
(and thus
(3)
) for
irreducible quadric hypersurfaces in
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{\mathbf{k}}
over an algebraically closed field
\mathbf{k}
.
For doing this, we count the
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
-rational points, where
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
denotes the finite field with
q

elements.

We start with a lemma that is certainly well-known to the specialists. For lack of a reference, we insert a proof.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
{\mathbb{Z}}\subseteq A
,
{\mathbb{Z}}\subseteq B
be finitely generated ring extensions such that
A
,
B

are integral domains.
If there exists a field
K
and a
K
-isomorphism between
K\otimes_{{\mathbb{Z}}}A
and
K\otimes_{{\mathbb{Z}}}B
, then

{\mathbb{F}}_{q}\otimes_{{\mathbb{Z}}}A
and
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}\otimes_{{\mathbb{Z}}}B
are
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
-isomorphic
for some prime power
q
.

Proof.

As
{\mathbb{Z}}
is a principal ideal domain, it is a regular ring.
Using that
A
,
B
are integral domains, we get that
A
,
B
are flat
{\mathbb{Z}}
-modules; see
[
Har77
, Proposition 9.7, Ch. III]
. Hence, every ring extension
R_{1}\subseteq R_{2}

induces injections

R_{1}\otimes_{{\mathbb{Z}}}A\subseteq R_{2}\otimes_{{\mathbb{Z}}}A\quad\textrm{and}\quad R_{1}\otimes_{{\mathbb{Z}}}B\subseteq R_{2}\otimes_{{\mathbb{Z}}}B\,.

Let
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in A
with
A={\mathbb{Z}}[a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}]
and

b_{1},\ldots,b_{m}\in B
with
B={\mathbb{Z}}[b_{1},\ldots,b_{m}]
. Denote by

\varphi\colon K\otimes_{{\mathbb{Z}}}A\xlongrightarrow{\sim}K\otimes_{{\mathbb{Z}}}B
a
K
-isomorphism.
There exist
finitely many
c_{1},\ldots,c_{r}\in K
such that the
{\mathbb{Z}}
-algebra
R
spanned by

c_{1},\ldots,c_{r}
inside
K
satisfies
\varphi(a_{i})\in R\otimes_{{\mathbb{Z}}}B

for all
1\leq i\leq n
and
\varphi^{-1}(b_{j})\in R\otimes_{{\mathbb{Z}}}A
for all
1\leq j\leq m
.
In particular,
\varphi
restricts to an
R
-isomorphism
R\otimes_{{\mathbb{Z}}}A\simeq R\otimes_{{\mathbb{Z}}}B
.
We may assume that
R
is non-zero and thus contains a maximal ideal
{\mathfrak{m}}
. Then we get an
R/{\mathfrak{m}}
-isomorphism

(R/{\mathfrak{m}})\otimes_{{\mathbb{Z}}}A\xlongrightarrow{\sim}(R/{\mathfrak{m}})\otimes_{{\mathbb{Z}}}B\,.

Denote by
F
the prime field of
R/{\mathfrak{m}}
, i.e. the
quotient field of the image of the unique ring homomorphism
{\mathbb{Z}}\to R/{\mathfrak{m}}
.
As
R
is finitely generated as a
{\mathbb{Z}}
-algebra,

R/{\mathfrak{m}}
is finitely generated as an
F
-algebra
and by Noether’s normalization theorem, the field extension
F\subseteq R/{\mathfrak{m}}
is finite. By
the Artin-Tate lemma (see
[
AM69
, Proposition 7.8]
)
it follows that
F
is a finitely generated
{\mathbb{Z}}
-algebra. Hence,
F
cannot be isomorphic to
{\mathbb{Q}}

and thus
F={\mathbb{F}}_{p}
where
p=\car(R/{\mathfrak{m}})
. As the field extension
F\subseteq R/{\mathfrak{m}}
is finite,

R/{\mathfrak{m}}={\mathbb{F}}_{p^{r}}
for some
r\geq 1
.
∎

Corollary 5.2
.

Let
X,Y
be affine integral schemes of finite type over
{\mathbb{Z}}

such that
X\to\mathrm{Spec}({\mathbb{Z}})
,
Y\to\mathrm{Spec}({\mathbb{Z}})
are dominant.
If the pull-backs

-rational points of a scheme

In case

Lemma 5.3
.

For all integers
m,n
with
1\leq 2m-1\leq n
, let

be the hypersurface in
{\mathbb{A}}^{n+1}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}
given by

\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}x_{2i}x_{2i+1}=1.

Then for any prime power
q
we get

\#X_{m,n}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})=\#X_{m,n}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})_{\reg}=q^{n-m}(q^{m}-1)\,.

Proof.

As
X_{m,n}
is smooth over
\mathrm{Spec}({\mathbb{Z}})
,
X_{m,n}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})_{\reg}=X_{m,n}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})
.
We proceed now by induction on
m\geq 1
. For any
n\geq 1
, we get

\#X_{1,n}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})=q^{n-1}(q-1)
, as
X_{1,n}
and
({\mathbb{A}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{1}\setminus\{0\})\times{\mathbb{A}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{n-1}

are isomorphic over
{\mathbb{Z}}
. Moreover, for
m>1
:

X_{m,n}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})=V_{X_{m,n}}(x_{2m-1})({\mathbb{F}}_{q})\coprod(X_{m,n})_{x_{2m-1}}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})\,.

As
(X_{m,n})_{x_{2m-1}}
and
({\mathbb{A}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{1}\setminus\{0\})\times{\mathbb{A}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{n-1}
are isomorphic over
{\mathbb{Z}}
,
it follows that
\#(X_{m,n})_{x_{2m-1}}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})=q^{n-1}(q-1)
. As
V_{X_{m,n}}(x_{2m-1})
and

are isomorphic over
{\mathbb{Z}}
, we get by induction

\displaystyle\#X_{m,n}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\#X_{m-1,n-1}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})+q^{n-1}(q-1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle q^{n-m}(q^{m-1}-1)+q^{n-1}(q-1)=q^{n-m}(q^{m}-1)\,.\qed

Lemma 5.4
.

For all integers with
0\leq 2m\leq n
,
let
Y_{m,n}
be the hypersurface in
{\mathbb{A}}^{n+1}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}
that is given by

\left(\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}x_{2i}x_{2i+1}\right)+x_{2m}^{2}=1.

Then for every
odd
prime power
q
we get

\#Y_{m,n}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})=\#Y_{m,n}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})_{\reg}=q^{n-m}(q^{m}+1)\,,

and for every
even

q
, we have

\#(Y_{m,n})({\mathbb{F}}_{q})=q^{n}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\#(Y_{m,n})({\mathbb{F}}_{q})_{\reg}=q^{n-2m}(q^{2m}-1)\,.

Proof.

Suppose first that
q
is odd. Here,
Y_{m,n}
is smooth over
\mathrm{Spec}({\mathbb{Z}})\setminus\{(2)\}
,
so
Y_{m,n}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})_{\reg}=Y_{m,n}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})
.
We proceed by induction on
m\geq 0
. For any
n\geq 1
, we get
\#Y_{0,n}=2q^{n}
,
as
Y_{0,n}
is given by
x_{0}^{2}-1
in
{\mathbb{A}}^{n+1}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}
over
{\mathbb{Z}}
. Assume
m>0
.
As in Lemma
5.3
, we count the points where

and the points where

, and obtain:

\displaystyle\#Y_{m,n}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\#Y_{m-1,n-1}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})+q^{n-1}(q-1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle q^{n-m}(q^{m-1}+1)+q^{n-1}(q-1)=q^{n-m}(q^{m}+1)\,.

Suppose now that

is a power of

{\mathbb{F}}_{q}\to{\mathbb{F}}_{q},t\to t^{2}
is bijective, the projection

The set of singular

-rational points of

and hence

∎

Let

Lemma 5.8
.

An irreducible cubic hypersurface in
{\mathbb{P}}^{3}
is non-rational if and only if it is the cone
over a smooth cubic curve in
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
.

Proof.

Let
X\subseteq{\mathbb{P}}^{3}
be a non-rational irreducible cubic hypersurface.
Then
X
has a singularity (as otherwise it would be the blow-up of
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
at six points, see Remark
5.7
),
say at
P=[1:0:0:0]\in{\mathbb{P}}^{3}
and it
is given by a homogeneous polynomial of the form
f_{2}(x,y,z)w+f_{3}(x,y,z)
,
where
[w:x:y:z]
denote the homogeneous coordinates of
{\mathbb{P}}^{3}
and

f_{i}\in\mathbf{k}[x,y,z]
is homogeneous of degree
i
. As
X
is non-rational, we get

f_{2}=0
(otherwise the projection to
x,y,z
gives a birational map to
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
) and
X
is the cone in
{\mathbb{P}}^{3}
over a cubic curve
\Gamma\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
given by
f_{3}=0
. As
X
is irreducible, so is
\Gamma
. Then,
X
is birational to
\Gamma\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
, and thus
\Gamma
is non-rational as
X
is. This implies that
\Gamma
is smooth, and that
X
is the cone in
{\mathbb{P}}^{3}
over a smooth cubic curve in
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
.

Assume now that
X
is a cone over a smooth cubic curve
C
.
Blowing up the vertex yields a projective smooth ruled surface
S
over

C
. In particular
H^{1}(S,\mathcal{O}_{S})
doesn’t vanish
(see
[
Har77
, Ch. V, Corollary 2.5]
) and hence

is not rational
(see
[
KSC04
, Theorem 3.2]
).
∎

The next lemma is certainly known to the specialists. For lack of a proof in any characteristic we provide one:

Lemma 5.9
.

Let
X\subseteq{\mathbb{P}}^{3}
be a normal rational cubic hypersurface.
Then
X
has only Du Val singularities and is piecewise isomorphic to the disjoint union of
{\mathbb{A}}^{2}
, one point, and
n
copies of
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
, with
1\leq n\leq 7
. Moreover,
3\leq\chi(X)=2+n\leq 9

and
\chi(X)=9
if and only if

is smooth.

Proof.

If
X
is smooth, then
X
is the blow-up of
6
points of
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
(Remark
5.7
) and is thus piecewise isomorphic to the disjoint
union of
{\mathbb{A}}^{2}
, one point and seven copies of
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
by Lemma
4.18
.
In particular,
\chi(X)=9
.

Thus we may assume that
X
is singular and after some coordinate change we may assume that
X
is given by

f_{2}(x,y,z)w+f_{3}(x,y,z)

are homogeneous polynomials of degree

, respectively,
and
[w:x:y:z]
denote the homogeneous coordinates in
{\mathbb{P}}^{3}
. If
f_{2}=0
, then,

is a cone over

is a rational cubic,

is irreducible and

.

The projection

[w:x:y:z]\mapsto[x:y:z]

gives a birational morphism
whose inverse

By
[

, Proposition 1.5]
, there exists a smooth projective surface

which

(2)

[X_{1}]=[X_{2}]
inside
K_{0}(\Var_{\mathbf{k}})
;

(3)

\chi(X_{1})=\chi(X_{2})
.

Proof.

The implications “
(1)

\Rightarrow

(2)
” and “
(2)

\Rightarrow

(3)
” both follow
from Lemma
4.10
. Proposition
4.19
says
that
X_{i}
is piecewise isomorphic to the disjoint union of
{\mathbb{A}}^{2}
, one point and
s_{i}\geq 0
copies of
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
.
If
\chi(X_{1})=\chi(X_{2})
, then we get
s_{1}=s_{2}
, i.e.
X_{1}
,
X_{2}
are piecewise isomorphic.
This gives the implication “
(3)

\Rightarrow

(1)
”.
∎

Corollary 5.15
.

Let
f,g\in{\mathbf{k}}[x,y,z,w]
be irreducible homogeneous polynomials such that
{\mathbb{P}}^{3}_{f}\simeq{\mathbb{P}}^{3}_{g}
.
Assume moreover that the zero loci
X\coloneqq V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{3}}(f)
,
Y\coloneqq V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{3}}(g)

a)

are both normal, rational and
each admits a desingularisation by trees of smooth rational curves or

b)

are both of degree
3
and rational.

Then
X
,
Y
are piecewise isomorphic.

Proof.

Since
{\mathbb{P}}^{3}_{f}\simeq{\mathbb{P}}^{3}_{g}
we get
[X]=[Y]
inside
K_{0}(\Var_{\mathbf{k}})

and thus
\chi(X)=\chi(Y)
, see Lemma
4.10
. In case
a)
,
the statement follows from Proposition
5.14
.
So assume we are in case
b)
. Then the statement follows from
Proposition
4.19
in combination with Lemma
5.9

if
X
and
Y
are normal and from
Proposition
5.10
in combination with
Lemma
5.11
in the other case.
∎

We now focus on cones over smooth cubic curves, i.e. on non-rational cubic hypersurfaces of
{\mathbb{P}}^{3}
. This consists the last unproven part of Theorem
C
. To prove that for such hypersurfaces, Question
(2)
has an affirmative answer, we will need the following result:

Lemma 5.16
.

Let
f\in{\mathbf{k}}[x,y,z]
be an irreducible homogeneous polynomial of degree
3
such that
V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{2}}(f)
is a smooth cubic curve. Then, the surface

X=V_{{\mathbb{A}}^{3}}(f-1)

admits no non-trivial
{\mathbb{G}}_{a}
-action.

Proof.

Suppose for contradiction that
X
admits a non-trivial
{\mathbb{G}}_{a}
-action.
By
[
BFT23
, Proposition 2.5.1]
,
there exists a
{\mathbb{G}}_{a}
-invariant affine dense open subset
X^{\prime}\subseteq X
, that is a
{\mathbb{G}}_{a}
-cylinder, i.e.
X^{\prime}
is
{\mathbb{G}}_{a}
-isomorphic to
{\mathbb{G}}_{a}\times U
, where
U
is a smooth affine curve and where the
{\mathbb{G}}_{a}
-action
on
{\mathbb{G}}_{a}\times U
is given
s\cdot(t,u)\coloneqq(s+t,u)
for
s,t\in{\mathbb{G}}_{a}
,
u\in U
.

Using the canonical embedding
{\mathbb{A}}^{3}\hookrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{3},(x,y,z)\mapsto[1:x:y:z]
we
can view
X
as an open subset of the irreducible surface
Y=V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{3}}(f-w^{3})
,
where
[w:x:y:z]
denote the homogeneous coordinates on
{\mathbb{P}}^{3}
.
Here,
X=Y_{w}
is the complement in
Y
of a smooth curve
\Gamma

and
\Gamma\simeq V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{2}}(f)
.

Suppose first that
Y
is a cone over a smooth cubic curve
C
. Writing
p\in Y
the singular point, the projection away from
p
gives to
Y\setminus\{p\}
an
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
-bundle structure over
C
. Hence, every closed rational curve on
Y
is one of the lines through
p
. As
\Gamma
is a hyperplane section of
Y
and as
\Gamma
is not rational,
it intersects a general such line into at least one point outside of
p
. The open subset
X^{\prime}\subseteq Y\setminus\Gamma
being contained in the smooth locus of
Y
,
it contains only finitely many curves isomorphic to
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
. This contradicts the fact that
X^{\prime}
is a
{\mathbb{G}}_{a}
-cylinder.

In the remaining case,
Y
is a rational cubic surface (Lemma
5.8
). Hence,
U
is a smooth affine rational curve, and is thus isomorphic to
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}\setminus\Delta
for some finite set
\Delta
of
r\geq 0
points. Hence,
X^{\prime}
is isomorphic to the open subset

V^{\prime}={\mathbb{A}}^{1}\times({\mathbb{A}}^{1}\setminus\Delta)\subseteq{\mathbb{A}}^{2}
. By Corollary
5.12
,
Y
is piecewise isomorphic to the disjoint union of
{\mathbb{A}}^{2}
, one point, and
n
copies of
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
, with

0\leq n\leq 7
, and thus to the disjoint union of
X^{\prime}\simeq V^{\prime}

with one point and
n+r
copies of
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
. As the
one-dimensional variety
Y\setminus X^{\prime}
contains
\Gamma
, that is not rational,
we get a contradiction, by Lemma
4.15
.
∎

We are now able to prove part
(3)
of Theorem
C
, that is Proposition
5.17
. If
{\mathbf{k}}={\mathbb{C}}
, then Proposition
5.17
follows essentially from
[
LS12
]
.

Proposition 5.17
.

Let
f,g\in\mathbf{k}[x,y,z,w]
be irreducible homogeneous polynomials of degree three such that

V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{3}}(f)
,
V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{3}}(g)
are non-rational.
If
{\mathbb{P}}^{3}_{f}\simeq{\mathbb{P}}^{3}_{g}
, then there exists
\varphi\in\mathrm{Aut}({\mathbb{P}}^{3})
with

\varphi(V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{3}}(f))=V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{3}}(g)
.

Proof.

By Lemma
5.8
we may assume that

f,g\in\mathbf{k}[x,y,z]
and that the zero loci
V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{2}}(f)
,
V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{2}}(g)
are smooth cubic curves.

By Lemma
5.16
, every
{\mathbb{G}}_{a}
-action on the affine surfaces
X_{0}\coloneqq V_{{\mathbb{A}}^{3}}(f-1)
and
Y_{0}\coloneqq V_{{\mathbb{A}}^{3}}(g-1)
is trivial.

Let
\theta\colon{\mathbb{P}}_{f}^{3}\to{\mathbb{P}}^{3}_{g}
be an isomorphism. By Proposition
2.1

and Remark
2.2
,
we get an isomorphism

\varphi\colon{\mathbb{A}}^{1}\times X_{0}=V_{{\mathbb{A}}^{4}}(f-1)\to V_{{\mathbb{A}}^{4}}(g-1)={\mathbb{A}}^{1}\times Y_{0}

such that
\pi_{g,1}\circ\varphi=\theta\circ\pi_{f,1}
, where
the morphisms
\pi_{f,1}\colon V_{{\mathbb{A}}^{4}}(f-1)\to{\mathbb{P}}^{3}_{f}
and

\pi_{g,1}\colon V_{{\mathbb{A}}^{4}}(g-1)\to{\mathbb{P}}^{3}_{g}
denote the canonical projections.

Using that
X_{0}
admit no non-trivial
{\mathbb{G}}_{a}
-action, by
[
Cra04
, Proposition 4.7]
it follows that
the intersection of the invariant subalgebras
\mathbf{k}[{\mathbb{A}}^{1}\times X_{0}]^{{\mathbb{G}}_{a}}
inside
\mathbf{k}[{\mathbb{A}}^{1}\times X_{0}]
over all

{\mathbb{G}}_{a}
-actions on
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}\times X_{0}
is equal to
\mathbf{k}[X_{0}]
.
Using that a similar statement holds for
Y_{0}\times{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
, we get that
\varphi

maps the fibres of
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}\times X_{0}\to X_{0}
onto the fibres of
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}\times Y_{0}\to Y_{0}
.
Using the commutative diagrams

\textstyle{{\mathbb{A}}^{1}\times X_{0}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{(t,x_{0})\mapsto x_{0}}
\scriptstyle{\pi_{f,1}}
\textstyle{{\mathbb{P}}^{3}_{f}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\begin{subarray}{c}\textrm{projection}\\
\textrm{from $[1:0:0:0]$}\end{subarray}}
\textstyle{X_{0}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{{\mathbb{P}}^{2}_{f}}

\textstyle{{\mathbb{A}}^{1}\times Y_{0}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{(t,y_{0})\mapsto y_{0}}
\scriptstyle{\pi_{g,1}}
\textstyle{{\mathbb{P}}^{3}_{g}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\begin{subarray}{c}\textrm{projection}\\
\textrm{from $[1:0:0:0]$}\end{subarray}}
\textstyle{Y_{0}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{{\mathbb{P}}^{2}_{g}}

we get that
\theta\colon{\mathbb{P}}_{f}^{3}\to{\mathbb{P}}^{3}_{g}
maps the fibres of
the locally trivial
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
-bundle
{\mathbb{P}}^{3}_{f}\to{\mathbb{P}}^{2}_{f}
(with respect to the Zariski topology)
onto those of
{\mathbb{P}}^{3}_{g}\to{\mathbb{P}}^{2}_{g}
. Hence, we get an isomorphism
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}_{f}\simeq{\mathbb{P}}^{2}_{g}
.
As
V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{2}}(f)
and
V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{2}}(g)
are non-rational this isomorphism extends to an automorphism of
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
.
∎

Now we are able to give the proof of Theorem
C
:

Proof of Theorem
C
.

(1)
: This follows from Corollary
5.15
.

(3)
: If
H
is a non-rational cubic surface, then as before,

, Lemma
5.13

H^{\prime}
is non-rational.
Thus
(3)
follows from Proposition
5.17
.

(2)
: If
H
or

is a cubic surface, then both are cubics (by using the order of the Picard groups

H
or
H^{\prime}

implies that
H
and
H^{\prime}
are isomorphic, and thus also piecewise isomorphic. We may thus assume that both

and

are rational. By Corollary

it follows that
H
(respectively

) is piecewise isomorphic to a disjoint union of
{\mathbb{A}}^{2}

n
(respectively
n^{\prime}
) copies of
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
. As
{\mathbb{P}}^{3}\setminus H
and

are isomorphic, we get

and hence

,

are piecewise isomorphic.
If moreover

.

Question 5.18
.

Let
f,g\in{\mathbf{k}}[x,y,z,w]
be given by

f=x^{2}y+z^{3}\quad\textrm{and}\quad g=x^{2}y+z^{3}+xw^{2}\,.

Are the varieties
{\mathbb{P}}^{3}_{f}
and
{\mathbb{P}}^{3}_{g}
isomorphic?

An affirmative answer would give a negative answer to Question

in degree three and dimension three,

f
in

g
in
{\mathbb{P}}^{3}
is normal.
Moreover,
\chi(V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{3}}(f))=\chi(V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{3}}(g))
, since
{\mathbb{P}}^{4}_{f}\simeq{\mathbb{P}}^{4}_{g}
by Proposition

{\mathbb{P}}^{4}_{f}\to{\mathbb{P}}^{3}_{f}

5.3.
Automorphisms of complements of singular cubic surfaces

Throughout this subsection we always assume that
\mathbf{k}
is algebraically closed.

We finish this text by giving some examples, that give a negative answer to Question
(1)
, for singular irreducible cubic hypersurfaces of
{\mathbb{P}}^{3}
. As explained in the introduction, this question is wide open for smooth cubics and known to have a negative answer for singular cubic surfaces with Du Val singularities
in characteristic zero
[
CDP18
, Theorem C and Theorem 4.3]
. We now extend this to other singular cubics.
Each irreducible cubic hypersurface of
{\mathbb{P}}^{3}
that does not have Du Val singularities is either the cone
over a smooth cubic curve in
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
or is rational and non-normal (this follows from Lemmas
5.8
and
5.9
). In this latter case, it always contains a line with multiplicity
2
(Proposition
5.10
). These two cases are done in the next two simple lemmas, that work in any dimension.

Lemma 5.19
.

Let
d\geq 1
,
n\geq 3
and let
X\subseteq{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
be an irreducible hypersurface of degree
d
, having a point of multiplicity
d

(
i.e. being a cone
)
. Then, there exists an element
\varphi\in\mathrm{Aut}({\mathbb{P}}^{n}\setminus X)
that does not extend to an element of
\mathrm{Aut}({\mathbb{P}}^{n})
.

Proof.

Changing coordinates, we may assume that
[0:\cdots:0:1]
is a point of
X
of multiplicity
d
. Hence,
X=V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{n}}(f)
, where
f\in{\mathbf{k}}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n-1}]
is irreducible and homogeneous of degree
d
. We define
\varphi\in\mathrm{Aut}({\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{f})
to be the involution given by

[x_{0}:\cdots:x_{n}]\mapsto\left[x_{0}:\cdots:x_{n-1}:-x_{n}+\frac{x_{0}^{d+1}}{f(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n-1})}\right]\,.

∎

Lemma 5.20
.

Let
d\geq 1
,
n\geq 3
and let
X\subseteq{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
be an irreducible hypersurface of degree
d
, being of multiplicity
d-1
along a linear subspace
L\subseteq{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
of dimension
r\in\{1,\ldots,n-2\}
. Then, there exists an element
\varphi\in\mathrm{Aut}({\mathbb{P}}^{n}\setminus X)
that does not extend to an element of
\mathrm{Aut}({\mathbb{P}}^{n})
.

Proof.

Changing coordinates, we may assume that
L=V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{n}}(x_{r+1},\ldots,x_{n})
. Hence,
X=V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{n}}(f)
, where
f\in{\mathbf{k}}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]
is irreducible, homogeneous of degree
d
and of the form

f=x_{0}a_{0}+\cdots+x_{r}a_{r}+b\,,

where
a_{0},\ldots,a_{r},b\in{\mathbf{k}}[x_{r+1},\ldots,x_{n}]
. We define a
\mathbb{G}_{a}
-action on
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{f}
by

\begin{array}[]{ccc}\mathbb{G}_{a}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{f}&\to&{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{f}\\
(t,[x_{0}:\cdots:x_{n}])&\mapsto&\left[x_{0}+t\frac{a_{1}x_{n}^{2}}{f(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})}:x_{1}-t\frac{a_{0}x_{n}^{2}}{f(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})}:x_{2}:\cdots:x_{n}\right]\end{array}

and choose
\varphi\in\mathrm{Aut}({\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{f})
to be any non-trivial element of
\mathbb{G}_{a}\subseteq\mathrm{Aut}({\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{f})
.
∎

The following lemma follows from
[
CDP18
, Theorem C and Theorem 4.3]
in characteristic zero.
We insert a proof (in any characteristic), as the argument is simple.

Lemma 5.21
.

Let
X\subseteq{\mathbb{P}}^{3}
be a normal rational singular cubic hypersurface. Then

{\mathbb{P}}^{3}\setminus X
contains an open affine subset
U
that is an
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
-cylinder, i.e.
U\simeq{\mathbb{A}}^{1}\times U^{\prime}

for some affine variety
U^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Since
X
is singular, we may assume (after a coordinate change) that
X
is given by

f\coloneqq f_{2}(x,y,z)w+f_{3}(x,y,z)

where
f_{i}\in\mathbf{k}[x,y,z]
is homogeneous of degree
i
and
[w:x:y:z]
denote the homogeneous coordinates of
{\mathbb{P}}^{3}
. As
X
is normal and rational,
f_{2}\neq 0
, see Lemma
5.8
.
Let
C\coloneqq V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{2}}(f_{2})
. We may choose the coordinates
(x,y,z)

in such a way, that
V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{2}}(z)
is contained in
C
if
f_{2}
is reducible, and
that
V_{{\mathbb{P}}^{2}}(z)
is tangent to
C
if
f_{2}
is irreducible. Then

{\mathbb{P}}^{2}_{zf_{2}}\simeq{\mathbb{A}}^{2}_{f_{2}(x,y,1)}\simeq D\times{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
, where
D\in\{{\mathbb{A}}^{1},{\mathbb{A}}^{1}\setminus\{0\}\}
,
and we get an isomorphism

\begin{array}[]{cccc}{\mathbb{P}}^{3}_{zff_{2}}\simeq&{\mathbb{A}}^{3}_{f(w,x,y,1)f_{2}(x,y,1)}&\xrightarrow{\simeq}&({\mathbb{A}}^{1}\setminus\{0\})\times{\mathbb{A}}^{2}_{f_{2}(x,y,1)}\\
&(w,x,y)&\mapsto&(f_{2}(x,y,1)w+f_{3}(x,y,1),x,y)\\
&\left(\frac{u-f_{3}(x,y,1)}{f_{2}(x,y,1)},x,y\right)&\mapsfrom&(u,x,y)\,.\end{array}

∎

Proposition 5.22
.

For each singular irreducible cubic hypersurface
X\subseteq{\mathbb{P}}^{3}
, there exists an element
\varphi\in\mathrm{Aut}({\mathbb{P}}^{3}\setminus X)
that does not extend to an element of
\mathrm{Aut}({\mathbb{P}}^{3})
.

Proof.

If
X
is a cone, this follows from Lemma
5.19
. If
X
is not normal, it contains a line with multiplicity
2
(Proposition
5.10
), and the result follows from Lemma
5.20
. In the remaining case,
X
is rational (Lemma
5.8
) and normal.
As
\Pic({\mathbb{P}}^{3}\setminus X)\simeq{\mathbb{Z}}/3{\mathbb{Z}}
is finite,
Lemma
5.21

implies that
{\mathbb{P}}^{3}\setminus X
admits a non-trivial
{\mathbb{G}}_{a}
-action, see e.g.
[
DK15
, Propostion 2]
. This implies the result.
∎
