Uniform attachment with freezing
Étienne Bellin, Arthur Blanc-Renaudie, Emmanuel Kammerer, Igor Kortchemski

Abstract
In the classical model of random recursive trees, trees are recursively built by attaching new vertices to old ones. What happens if vertices are allowed to freeze, in the sense that new vertices cannot be attached to already frozen ones? We are interested in the impact of freezing on the height of such trees.

Introduction

Random graphs are instrumental in the study of real-world networks. Uniform recursive trees (sometimes also called uniform attachment trees) are one of such models. They are constructed recursively by starting with one single vertex, and successively attaching new vertices to a previous existing vertex, chosen uniformly at random. This model has been introduced in
[
33
]
in the context of system generation, and has received considerable interest since, starting with graph-theoretical properties such as the height, number of leaves, etc. (see e.g.
[
37
]
and references therein, as well as the survey
[
39
]
). Recursive trees have been proposed as models for the spread of epidemics
[
32
]
, the family trees of preserved copies of ancient or medieval texts
[
34
]
, pyramid schemes
[
21
]
, internet interface maps
[
25
]
and appear in the study of Hopf algebras
[
23
]
. Very interesting connections have been made with other probabilistic objects such as the Bolthausen-Sznitman coalescent
[
22
]
and elephant random walks
[
29
]
. Uniform recursive trees have also been extended in several directions, for instance by introducing deterministic weights
[
28
]
or random weights with a random environment
[
10
]
.

In the present work, we introduce and study a modification of this model by introducing
freezing
, in that existing vertices can freeze and new vertices cannot be attached to frozen vertices.

Our motivation is twofold. First, in the context of real-world networks such mechanisms are natural: for instance, on the social network Twitter a user can choose to set their account to “private” which prevents strangers from “following” them; also performing an infection-tracing of an SIR epidemics falls within this framework (see Section
6
). Second, it is natural to investigate from a mathematical point of view the impact of freezing in dynamically-built random graph models. In particular, in a companion paper
[
7
]
, we investigate the regime where the number of active vertices roughly evolves as the total number of vertices to the power
\alpha
, for fixed
\alpha\in(0,1]
, we describe a phase transition where the macroscopic geometry of our model drastically changes according to the value of
\alpha
. Let us also mention the work
[
5
]
, which considers a growth-fragmentation-isolation process on random recursive trees in the context of contact tracing, see also
[
8
]
.

Uniform attachment with freezing.

Let us first define our model. In order to incorporate freezing in the model of uniform attachment trees and to obtain results in a rather general setup, we shall fix beforehand the steps where an attachment takes place and the steps where freezing takes place. Specifically, our input is a deterministic sequence
\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}=(\mathrm{x}_{i})_{i\geq 1}
of elements of
\{-1,+1\}
. Starting from a sole active vertex, we recursively build random trees by reading the elements of the sequence one after the other, by applying a “freezing” step when reading
-1
(which amounts to freezing an active vertex chosen uniformly at random) and a “uniform attachment” step when reading
+1
(which amounts to attaching a new vertex to an active vertex chosen uniformly at random).

More precisely, given a sequence
\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}=(\mathrm{x}_{i})_{i\geq 1}\in\{-1,+1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
, we set
S_{0}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})\coloneqq 1
and for every
n\geq 1

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:defintro}S_{n}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})\coloneqq 1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{x}_{i}\qquad\text{then}\qquad\tau(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})\coloneqq\inf\{n\geq 1:S_{n}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})=0\}.

(1.1)

Observe that
\tau(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})
, if it is finite, is the first time when all the vertices are frozen, so that afterwards the tree does not evolve any more.
For every
0\leq n\leq\tau(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})
, let
\mathcal{T}_{n}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})
be the random tree recursively built in this fashion after reading the first
n
elements of
\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}
(see Algorithm
2.1
in Sec.
2.1
for a precise definition and Fig.
3
for an example).

Let us comment on our choice of parametrization. It would have been possible to define the model starting with a random sequence
\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}
, but the choice of a deterministic sequence defines a more general model, for which our results can then be applied.

Local limits.

When
\tau(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})=\infty
, the next result allows to give a meaning to
\mathcal{T}_{\infty}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})
as a local limit of finite trees (see Sec.
3
for background on the local topology).

Theorem 1
.

Let
(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}^{n})_{n\geq 0}
be a sequence of elements of
\{-1,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
. Suppose that there exists
\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}
such that
\tau(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})=\infty
and such that for all
i\geq 1
,
\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}^{n}_{i}=\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}_{i}
for all
n
large enough. Then the following assertions are equivalent:

(I)

The sequence of trees
(\mathcal{T}_{n}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}^{n}))_{n\geq 0}
converges locally, in distribution.

(II)

The sequence of trees
(\mathcal{T}_{n}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}))_{n\geq 0}
converges locally, almost surely, towards
a tree

\mathcal{T}_{\infty}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})
.

(III)

The sum
\sum_{i\geq 1}\frac{1}{S_{i}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathrm{x}_{i}=-1\}}
diverges.

In this case
\mathcal{T}_{\infty}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})
is the local limit of
(\mathcal{T}_{n}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}^{n}))_{n\geq 0}
.

Examples.

It is interesting to note that this model encompasses the two classical models of random recursive trees and random uniform plane trees:

–

when
\mathrm{x}_{i}=1
for every
i\geq 1
, then
\mathcal{T}_{n}{(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})}
is a random recursive tree on
n
vertices built by uniform attachment;

–

when
\mathrm{\mathbf{X}}=(\mathrm{X}_{i})_{i\geq 1}
is a sequence of non constant i.i.d. uniform random variables on
\{-1,+1\}
, then for every
n\geq 1
, conditionally given
\tau(\mathrm{\mathbf{X}})=n
,
\mathcal{T}_{n}{(\mathrm{\mathbf{X}})}
is a uniform plane tree when the plane order among the vertices of
\mathcal{T}_{n}({\mathrm{\mathbf{X}}})
is chosen uniformly at random.

More generally we have the following result, in which we consider the case of random sequences. In this case we will write
\mathrm{\mathbf{X}}
in upper case rather than
\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}
to emphasize the fact that the sequence is random. And, the law of
(\mathcal{T}_{n}(\mathrm{\mathbf{X}}))_{n\geq 0}
given the random sequence
\mathrm{\mathbf{X}}
follows the description above where
\mathrm{\mathbf{X}}
is considered to be fixed. In other words, we first choose
\mathrm{\mathbf{X}}
randomly, then, conditionally on
\mathrm{\mathbf{X}}
, we construct
\mathcal{T}_{n}(\mathrm{\mathbf{X}})
following Algorithm
2.1
where we consider the sequence
\mathrm{\mathbf{X}}
to be deterministic.

Theorem 2
.

Let
p\in[0,1)
and let
\mathrm{\mathbf{X}}=(\mathrm{X}_{i})_{i\geq 1}
be a sequence of i.i.d random variables such that
{{\mathbb{P}}(X_{1}=+1)=p}
and
{\mathbb{P}}(\mathrm{X}_{1}=-1)=1-p
. Then
\mathcal{T}_{\infty}(\mathrm{\mathbf{X}})
has the law of a Bienaymé tree with offspring distribution
\mu
given by
\mu(k)=(1-p)p^{k}
for
k\geq 0
.

Roughly speaking, this comes from the fact that the subtrees grafted on the initial vertex evolve in an i.i.d. fashion.

Height of uniform attachment trees with freezing.

Since we consider large trees, let us consider for
n\geq 1
, a sequence

\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}^{n}\in\{-1,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
such that
\tau(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}^{n})>n
, and let
\mathcal{T}_{n}\coloneqq\mathcal{T}_{n}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}^{n})
. Also, to simplify notation, let
S_{k}^{n}\coloneqq S_{k}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}^{n})
for
0\leq k\leq n
.

Our next main theorem is that the height of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
is of order

\mathsf{h}^{+}_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{S^{n}_{i}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathrm{x}^{n}_{i}=1\}}.

Theorem 3
.

The following results hold.

(I)

Let
U^{n}
be an active vertex of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
chosen uniformly at random, and denote by
\mathsf{H}(U^{n})
its height. Then for all
p\geq 1
:

\frac{\mathsf{H}(U^{n})}{\mathsf{h}^{+}_{n}}\enskip\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{\mathbb{L}^{p}}_{n\to\infty}\enskip 1.

(II)

For all
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
:

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\varepsilon\leq\frac{\mathsf{Height}(\mathcal{T}_{n})}{\mathsf{h}^{+}_{n}}\leq{e+\varepsilon}}}\right)\quad\mathop{\longrightarrow}_{n\rightarrow\infty}\quad 1.

(III)

Assume that
{\ln(n)}/{\mathsf{h}^{+}_{n}}\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Then for all
p\geq 1
:

\frac{\mathsf{Height}(\mathcal{T}_{n})}{\mathsf{h}^{+}_{n}}\enskip\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{\mathbb{L}^{p}}_{n\to\infty}\enskip 1.

The proof of Theorem
3
is based on an alternative construction of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
, based on time-reversal, through a growth-coalescent process of rooted forests. This construction can be roughly described as follows (see Algorithm
2.2
for a precise definition): start with a forest made of
S_{n}^{n}
rooted one-vertex trees. Read successively
\mathrm{x}^{n}_{n},\mathrm{x}^{n}_{n-1},\ldots,\mathrm{x}^{n}_{1}
; when reading
+1
add a new one-vertex tree to the forest; when reading
-1
, choose successively two different forests uniformly at random, connect their roots by an edge, and root this new tree at the root of the first forest. It turns out that, in the end, one gets a tree having the same distribution as
\mathcal{T}^{n}
(see Theorem
8
for a precise statement). This is a generalization of the connection between random recursive trees and Kingman’s coalescent which first appeared in
[
19
]
(see
[
17
, Sec. 6]
,
[
37
, Sec. 3]
,
[
1
, Sec. 2.2]
,
[
3
]
for applications), in the context of union-find data structures (which are data structures that store a collection of disjoint sets where merging sets and finding a representative member of a set), see e.g.
[
27
]
. The main difference is that a growth feature must be added when freezing is introduced.

We are next interested in the specific regime where roughly speaking the number of active vertices increases linearly compared to the size of the tree.

Theorem 4
.

Let
c\in(0,1]
. Let
(A_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of positive numbers such that
A_{n}\rightarrow\infty
and
A_{n}=o(\log n)
as
n\to\infty
. Assume that

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:assumptions}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\max_{A_{n}\leq i\leq\varepsilon n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{S^{n}_{i}}{i}-c}}\right|=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\forall\varepsilon{\in(0,1)},\enskip\liminf_{n\to\infty}\min_{\varepsilon n\leq i\leq n}\frac{S^{n}_{i}}{n}>0.

(1.2)

The following assertions hold.

(I)

We have
\displaystyle\frac{\mathsf{h}^{+}_{n}}{\ln n}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\frac{c+1}{2c}
.

(II)

For all
n\geq 1
, conditionally given
\mathcal{T}_{n}
, let
V_{1}^{n}
and
V_{2}^{n}
be independent uniform vertices of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
. Then for every
p\geq 1

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:fvprobahn}\frac{\mathsf{H}(V_{1}^{n})}{\ln n}\enskip\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{\mathbb{L}^{p}}_{n\to\infty}\enskip\frac{c+1}{2c}\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\frac{d^{n}(V^{n}_{1},V^{n}_{2})}{\ln n}\enskip\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{\mathbb{L}^{p}}_{n\to\infty}\enskip\frac{c+1}{c},

(1.3)

where
\mathsf{H}(V_{1}^{n})
is the height of
V_{1}^{n}
in
\mathcal{T}_{n}
and
d^{n}
denotes the graph distance in
\mathcal{T}_{n}
.

(III)

We have

\frac{\mathsf{Height}(\mathcal{T}_{n})}{\ln(n)}\enskip\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{{\mathbb{P}}}_{n\to\infty}\enskip\frac{{c+1}}{2c}f(c),

where
f(c)
is the unique solution with
f(c)>1
to
f(c)(\ln f(c)-1)=(c-1)/(c+1)
.

Let us make some comments on these results. Roughly speaking,
\mathcal{T}_{n}
looks like a “tentacular bush” in the sense that two typical vertices are always at the same distance of order
2\mathsf{h}_{n}^{+}\sim\tfrac{c+1}{c}\cdot\ln(n)
, but the total height is of order
f(c)\cdot\mathsf{h}_{n}^{+}
. A typical example where the assumptions (
1.2
) are satisfied is e.g. when
\max_{A_{n}\leq i\leq n}|{S^{n}_{i}}/{i}-c|\rightarrow 0
. This is for instance the case when
p\in(1/2,1)
,
\mathrm{\mathbf{X}}=(\mathrm{X}_{i})_{i\geq 1}
is a sequence of i.i.d random variables such that
{{\mathbb{P}}(X_{1}=+1)=p}
and
{\mathbb{P}}(\mathrm{X}_{1}=-1)=1-p
, setting
S_{i}^{n}=S_{i}(\mathrm{\mathbf{X}})
, conditionally given
\tau(\mathrm{\mathbf{X}})>n
, Theorem
4
applies almost surely with
c=2p-1
thanks to the laws of large numbers. The reason we rather use (
1.2
) is for applications to contact-tracing in the SIR model (see Sec.
6
).

Figure 2:
Plot of the function
(\frac{{c+1}}{2c}f(c):0<c\leq 1)
.

Theorem
4
(III) gives a more precise result than Theorem
3
(II) under an additional assumption. It generalizes the well known result that when
\mathcal{T}_{n}
is a random recursive tree (which is obtained by taking
\mathrm{x}_{n}=1
for every
n\geq 1
), we have
[
17
, Theorem 10]
:
\mathsf{Height}(\mathcal{T}_{n})/\ln(n)\rightarrow e
in probability (see also
[
37
, Theorem 1]
for a different approach based on continuous-time branching processes).

The proof of Theorem
4
(III) is rather delicate: the main difficulty is that the presence of freezing impedes the direct use of branching process techniques. The alternative growth-coalescent process construction previously mentioned lies at the heart of our proof, which is different from how corresponding results would be proved for the random recursive tree.

Let us mention that it is possible to check that
(\mathcal{T}_{n}/\ln(n))_{n\geq 1}
is not tight for the so-called Gromov–Hausdorff–Prokhorov topology. However, for the topology considered in
[
20
,
26
]
,
\mathcal{T}_{n}/\ln(n)
converges to the so-called long dendron
\Upsilon_{\nu}
(we use the notation of
[
26
, Example 3.12]
) with
\nu
being the Dirac mass
\nu=\delta_{(c+1)/2c}
.

Application to contact-tracing in a stochastic SIR dynamics.

Our results may be applied to analyze the geometry of the so-called “infection tree” of a stochastic SIR dynamics, in which the vertices are individuals and where edges connect two individuals if one has infected the other. To keep this introduction at reasonable length, we refer to Section
6
for details and to Theorem
24
for a precise statement.

Plan of the paper.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows: In Section
2

and

about local limits. In Sections

and

to a stochastic SIR dynamics. Finally, in Section

Uniform attachment with freezing: growth-coalescent construction

As before, let
\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}=(\mathrm{x}_{n})_{n\geq 1}\in\{-1,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}

2.3
Laws of the birth and coalescence times

Let
\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}=(\mathrm{x}_{n})_{n\geq 1}\in\{-1,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
. First, define

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:labels}\mathbb{F}_{n}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})=\{i\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket:\mathrm{x}_{i}=-1\},\qquad\mathbb{A}_{n}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{S_{n}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})}\},\qquad\mathbb{V}_{n}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})=\mathbb{F}_{n}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})\cup\mathbb{A}_{n}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}).

(2.1)

It is crucial to observe that while
\mathcal{T}^{n}
is a random tree, the labels of its vertices are deterministic quantities that only depend on
\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}
:
\mathbb{A}_{n}
are the labels of the active vertices of
\mathcal{T}^{n}
and
\mathbb{F}_{n}
are the labels of the frozen vertices of
\mathcal{T}^{n}
. In particular, the elements of
\mathbb{V}_{n}
will be called vertices of
\mathcal{T}^{n}
.

Next, for every
u\in\mathbb{V}_{n}
, let
\mathsf{b}_{n}(u)
be the largest
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
such that
u
belongs to the forest
\mathcal{F}^{n}_{i}
. Explicitly, if

is an active vertex then
\mathsf{b}_{n}({u})=n
, and if
u\in\mathbb{F}_{n}
(note that

is then an integer) then
\mathsf{b}_{n}(u)=u-1
(see Fig.
4
for an example). We say that
\mathsf{b}_{n}(u)
is the

of
u
, since it encodes the first time when vertex
u
appears in Algorithm
2.2
. For
0\leq i\leq\mathsf{b}_{n}(u)
, let
\mathsf{H}^{n}_{i}(u)
be the height of vertex
u
in
\mathcal{F}^{n}_{i}
(that is, the graph distance between
u
and the root of whichever tree of
\mathcal{F}^{n}_{i}
contains
u
).

Finally, for
u,v\in\mathbb{V}_{n}
, let
\mathsf{c}_{n}(u,v)
be the largest
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
such that
u
and

belong to the same tree in the forest
\mathcal{F}^{n}_{i}
obtained when building
\mathcal{T}^{n}
in Algorithm
2.2
. We say that

between

and

and

(observe that while

We now state several simple consequences of Algorithm
2.2
, which will be useful to study the geometry of

.

Lemma 5
.

Fix
1\leq n\leq\tau
and
{v}\in\mathbb{V}_{n}
. For every
1\leq i\leq\mathsf{b}_{n}(v)
:

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathsf{H}^{n}_{i-1}(v)-\mathsf{H}^{n}_{i}(v)=1|\mathcal{F}^{n}_{n},\ldots,\mathcal{F}^{n}_{i}}}\right)=\frac{1}{S_{i}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathrm{x}_{i}=1\}}.

This is clear from the definition of Algorithm
2.2
. It follows from Lemma
5
that if
(Y_{i})_{1\leq i\leq n}
are independent Bernoulli random variables of respective parameters
(1/S_{i})_{1\leq i\leq n}

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:H0}\mathsf{H}^{n}_{0}(u)\quad\mathop{=}^{(d)}\quad\sum_{i=1}^{\mathsf{b}_{n}(u)}Y_{i}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathrm{x}_{i}=1\}}.

(2.2)

The next result identifies the law of the birth time of a vertex of
\mathcal{T}^{n}
chosen uniformly at random.

Lemma 6
.

Fix
1\leq n\leq\tau
. Let
V
be an element of
\mathbb{V}_{n}
chosen uniformly at random. For every
1\leq m\leq n
,

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathsf{b}_{n}(V)<m}}\right)=\frac{m+1-S_{m}}{n+1+S_{n}}.

Proof.

We noted that
\mathsf{b}_{n}(u)=n
if
u\in\mathbb{A}_{n}
and
\mathsf{b}_{n}(u)=u-1
if
u\in\mathbb{F}_{n}
. Also, conditionally given the fact that

,
\mathsf{b}_{n}(V)
is uniform on
\mathbb{F}_{n}
. Since
\#\mathbb{A}_{n}=S_{n}
and
\#\mathbb{F}_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathrm{x}_{i}=-1\}}
, it follows by the definition of

that

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathsf{b}_{n}(V)<m}}\right)=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathrm{x}_{i}=-1\}}}{S_{n}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathrm{x}_{i}=-1\}}}=\frac{m+1-S_{m}}{n+1+S_{n}},

where for the last equality we have used the fact that

for every

.
∎

The last useful result identifies the law of the coalescence times between two vertices.

Lemma 7
.

Fix
1\leq n\leq\tau
and consider

. Then for every

with

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathsf{c}_{n}(u,v)=c}}\right)=\frac{1}{\binom{S_{c+1}}{2}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=c+2\\
\text{s.t. }\mathrm{x}_{i}=1\end{subarray}}^{\mathsf{b}_{n}(u)\wedge\mathsf{b}_{n}(v)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{1}{\binom{S_{i}}{2}}}}\right).

Proof.

When running Algorithm

, the factors in the product correspond to the probability that the two vertices born at times

and

do not coalesce until time

and the factor

corresponds to the conditional probability that the two vertices coalesce at time

∎

3.2
A connection with Geometric Bienaymé trees: Proof of Theorem
2

For the proof of Theorem
2
, we need the Ulam–Harris formalism of plane trees. Let
\mathcal{U}
be the set of labels defined by

\mathcal{U}=\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{N}^{n},

where, by convention,
(\mathbb{N})^{0}=\{\varnothing\}
. In other words, an element of
\mathcal{U}
is a (possibly empty) sequence
u=u_{1}\cdots u_{j}
of positive integers. When
u=u_{1}\cdots u_{j}
and
v=v_{1}\cdots v_{k}
are elements of
\mathcal{U}
, we let
uv=u_{1}\cdots u_{j}v_{1}\cdots v_{k}
be the concatenation of
u
and
v
. In particular,
u\varnothing=\varnothing u=u
. Finally, a (Ulam–Harris)
plane tree
is a subset of
\mathcal{U}
satisfying the following three conditions:

(i)

\varnothing\in\tau
,

(ii)

if
v\in\tau
and
v=uj
for some
j\in\mathbb{N}
, then
u\in\tau
,

(iii)

for every
u\in\tau
, there exists an integer
k_{u}(\tau)\geq 0
such that for every
j\in\mathbb{N}
,
uj\in\tau
if and only if
1\leq j\leq k_{u}(\tau)
.

If
\tau
is a plane tree and
u\in\tau
let
\theta_{u}\tau=\{v\in\mathcal{U}:uv\in\tau\}
be the subtree of descendants of
u
. We finally let
\mathbb{T}
be the set of all plane trees (observe that such trees may be infinite, but are always locally finite).

Given a probability distribution
\mu
on
\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, the law
{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu}
of a Bienaymé tree with offspring distribution
\mu
(sometimes known as a Galton–Watson tree, but we prefer to use the terminology suggested in
[
2
]
) is a probability measure on
\mathbb{T}
that can be characterized by the following two properties (see e.g.
[
36
]
for more general statements):

(i)

{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu}(k_{\varnothing}=j)=\mu(j)
for
j\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,

(ii)

for every
j\geq 1
with
\mu(j)>0
, the shifted trees
\theta_{1}\tau,\ldots,\theta_{j}\tau
are independent
under the conditional probability
{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu}({\,\cdot\,}|k_{\varnothing}=j)
and their conditional
distribution is
{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu}
.

The following simple result gives a characterization of Bienaymé trees with geometric offspring distribution in terms of an invariance property involving subtrees (since we have not managed to find a reference in the literature we include the short proof for completeness).

Lemma 12
.

Let
p\in[0,1)
. Let
\mathcal{T}
be a random plane tree such that
\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k_{\varnothing}(\mathcal{T})=0}}\right)=1-p
and under the conditional probability
{\mathbb{P}}(\,\cdot\,|k_{\varnothing}(\mathcal{T})>0)
the two trees
\theta_{1}\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{T}\backslash\theta_{1}\mathcal{T}
are independent with conditional distribution equal to the law of
\mathcal{T}
. Then
\mathcal{T}
is a Bienaymé tree with offspring distribution
\mu
given by
\mu(k)=(1-p)p^{k}
for
k\geq 0
.

Proof.

First, for
j\geq 1
we have
\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k_{\varnothing}(\mathcal{T})\geq j}}\right)=\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k_{\varnothing}(\mathcal{T})>0}}\right)\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k_{\varnothing}(\mathcal{T}\backslash\theta_{1}\mathcal{T})\geq j-1|k_{\varnothing}(\mathcal{T})>0}}\right)
, which implies
\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k_{\varnothing}(\mathcal{T})\geq j}}\right)=p\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k_{\varnothing}(\mathcal{T})\geq j-1}}\right)
. Thus
\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k_{\varnothing}(\mathcal{T})=k}}\right)=(1-p)p^{k}
for
k\geq 0
.

Second, we argue by induction. Take
n\geq 1
and assume that the shifted trees
\theta_{1}\mathcal{T},\ldots,\theta_{n}\mathcal{T}
are independent under the conditional probability
{\mathbb{P}}(\,\cdot\,|k_{\varnothing}(\mathcal{T})=n)
with conditional distribution equal to the law of
\mathcal{T}
. Then for
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n+1}\geq 0
measurable

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[f_{1}(\theta_{1}\mathcal{T})\cdots f_{n+1}(\theta_{n+1}\mathcal{T})|k_{\varnothing}(\mathcal{T})=n+1}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[f_{1}(\theta_{1}\mathcal{T})g_{n}(\mathcal{T}\backslash\theta_{1}\mathcal{T})|k_{\varnothing}(\mathcal{T})>0}}\right]

with
g_{n}(\tau)=\mathbbm{1}_{k_{\varnothing}(\tau)=n}f_{2}(\theta_{1}\tau)\cdots f_{n+1}(\theta_{n}\tau)\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k_{\varnothing}(\mathcal{T})>0}}\right)/\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k_{\varnothing}(\mathcal{T})=n+1}}\right)
. Thus, by assumption, we have

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[f_{1}(\theta_{1}\mathcal{T})\cdots f_{n+1}(\theta_{n+1}\mathcal{T})|k_{\varnothing}(\mathcal{T})=n+1}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[f_{1}(\mathcal{T})}}\right]\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[g_{n}(\mathcal{T})}}\right]
. But

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[g_{n}(\mathcal{T})}}\right]=\frac{\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k_{\varnothing}(\mathcal{T})=n}}\right)\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k_{\varnothing}(\mathcal{T})>0}}\right)}{\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k_{\varnothing}(\mathcal{T})=n+1}}\right)}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[f_{2}(\theta_{1}\mathcal{T})\cdots f_{n+1}(\theta_{n}\mathcal{T})|k_{\varnothing}(\mathcal{T})=n}}\right],

which is equal to
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[f_{2}(\theta_{1}\mathcal{T})\cdots f_{n+1}(\theta_{n}\mathcal{T})|k_{\varnothing}(\mathcal{T})=n}}\right]
thanks to the first step. The desired result follows from the induction hypothesis.
∎

First, we claim that almost surely the sequence

and
{\mathbb{P}}\coloneqq{\mathbb{P}}_{(x,y,1)}
. Suppose that
y_{s}\notin A
, then
m_{s+1}>m_{s}
and

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{x},\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{y},V_{m_{s+1}}=y_{s+1}}}\right)={\mathbb{P}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{x},\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{y}}}\right){\mathbb{P}}_{(x_{r},y_{s},1)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\exists n\geq 1,\,I_{1}=\cdots=I_{n-1}=1,\,I_{n}=2,\,V_{n}=y_{s+1}}}\right).

By induction hypothesis,
{\mathbb{P}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{x},\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{y}}}\right)={\mathbb{P}}_{x}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}}}\right){\mathbb{P}}_{y}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{y}}}\right)
. Moreover

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}_{(x_{r},y_{s},1)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\exists n\geq 1,\,I_{1}=\cdots=I_{n-1}=1,\,I_{n}=2,\,V_{n}=y_{s+1}}}\right)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}_{(x_{r},y_{s},1)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\exists n\geq 1,I_{n}=2}}\right)\alpha(y_{s},y_{s+1})

\displaystyle=\alpha(y_{s},y_{s+1}).

This shows the formula for
(r,s+1)
in the case
y_{s}\notin A
. Now, if
y_{s}\in A
then

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{x},\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{y},V_{m_{s+1}}=y_{s+1}}}\right)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{x},\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{y}}}\right){\mathbb{P}}_{(x_{r},y_{s},1)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\forall m\geq 1,I_{m}=1}}\right)\mathbbm{1}_{y_{s}=y_{s+1}}

\displaystyle\qquad+{\mathbb{P}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{x},\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{y}}}\right){\mathbb{P}}_{(x_{r},y_{s},1)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\exists m\geq 1,I_{m}=2}}\right)\alpha(y_{s},y_{s+1})

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}_{x}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}}}\right){\mathbb{P}}_{y}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{y},Y_{s+1}=y_{s+1}}}\right).

This completes the proof.
∎

4
The height of uniform attachment trees with freezing

We shall now study the geometry of uniform attachment trees with freezing.
It will be convenient to work with
\mathcal{T}^{n}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})
as built using Algorithm
2.2
. Recall from Theorem
8
that: the only difference between
\mathcal{T}_{n}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})
and
\mathcal{T}^{n}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})
is that all the active vertices of
\mathcal{T}^{n}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})
are labeled
a_{1},\ldots,a_{S_{n}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})}
, while all the active vertices of
\mathcal{T}_{n}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})
are labelled
a
. In particular the graph structure of both trees is the same in law, so it is equivalent to establish our main results with
\mathcal{T}_{n}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})
replaced with
\mathcal{T}^{n}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})
.

Also recall from (
2.1
) the definition of
\mathbb{V}_{n}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})
, which is a deterministic set representing the labels of the vertices of
\mathcal{T}^{n}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})
, and that by a slight abuse of notation we view elements of
\mathbb{V}_{n}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})
as vertices of
\mathcal{T}^{n}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})
.

An important ingredient in our proofs is Bennett’s concentration inequality. Since it will be used multiple times, we state it here, tailored for our purpose:

Proposition 14

(Bennett’s inequality)
.

Let
(Y_{i})_{1\leq i\leq n}
be independent Bernoulli random variables of respective parameters
(p_{i})_{1\leq i\leq n}
. Set
m_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}
and assume that
m_{n}>0
. Then for every
t>0
:

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}>m_{n}+t}}\right)\leq\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-m_{n}g\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{t}{m_{n}}}}\right)}}\right)\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}<m_{n}-t}}\right)\leq\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-m_{n}g\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{t}{m_{n}}}}\right)}}\right),

where
g(u)=(1+u)\ln(1+u)-u
for
u>0
.

4.1
Uniform bounds on the height

We keep the notation introduced before (see in particular Table
1
). In particular,
\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}=(\mathrm{x}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is a fixed sequence of elements of
\{-1,1\}
,
S_{0}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})\coloneqq 1
and for every
n\geq 1

S_{n}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})\coloneqq 1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{x}_{i};\qquad\tau(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})\coloneqq\inf\{n\geq 1:S_{n}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})=0\}.

(4.1)

To lighten notation we will drop again the
\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}
in parenthesis (e.g. we will write
S_{n}
instead of
S_{n}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})
,
\tau
instead of
\tau(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})
,
\mathcal{T}^{n}
instead of
\mathcal{T}^{n}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})
,
\mathbb{V}_{n}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})
instead of
\mathbb{V}_{n}
, etc.)
but we keep in mind that all the quantities depend on the sequence
\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}
.
We first start with an estimate concerning the height of
\mathcal{T}^{n}
(recall its construction using Algorithm
2.2
in Section
2.2
).

Proposition 15
.

Fix
n\geq 1
and assume that
\tau>n
. Let
\mathsf{Height}(\mathcal{T}^{n})
denote the height of
\mathcal{T}^{n}
. Then, for every
t>0
,

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathsf{Height}(\mathcal{T}^{n})-h^{+}_{n}>t}}\right)\leq\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-h^{+}_{n}\,g\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{t}{h^{+}_{n}}}}\right)+\ln(n)}}\right)

and

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathsf{Height}(\mathcal{T}^{n})-h^{+}_{n}<-t}}\right)\leq\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-h^{+}_{n}\,g\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{t}{h^{+}_{n}}}}\right)}}\right)

where
g(u)=(1+u)\ln(1+u)-u
for
u>0
.

Proof.

Fix
u\in\mathbb{V}_{n}
. From (
2.2
), if
(Y_{i})_{1\leq i\leq n}
are independent Bernoulli random variables of respective parameters
(1/S_{i})_{1\leq i\leq n}
, we have

\mathsf{H}^{n}_{0}(u)\quad\mathop{=}^{(d)}\quad\sum_{i=1}^{\mathsf{b}_{n}(u)}Y_{i}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathrm{x}_{i}=1\}}.

Thus, by a union bound over all vertices of
\mathcal{T}^{n}
we get

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathsf{Height}(\mathcal{T}^{n})>h^{+}_{n}+t}}\right)\leq n\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Y_{i}-\frac{1}{S_{i}}}}\right)\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathrm{x}_{i}=1\}}>t}}\right).

In addition, when
u\in\mathbb{V}_{n}
is an active vertex (so that
\mathsf{b}_{n}(u)=n
), using the fact that
\mathsf{H}^{n}_{0}(u)\leq\mathsf{Height}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
, we get

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathsf{Height}(\mathcal{T}^{n})<h^{+}_{n}-t}}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Y_{i}-\frac{1}{S_{i}}}}\right)\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathrm{x}_{i}=1\}}<-t}}\right).

The desired bounds then both follow from Bennett’s inequality.
∎

4.2
A simple lower bound for
\mathsf{h}^{+}_{n}

In view of Theorem
3
, which tells us that
\mathsf{h}^{+}_{n}
is the order of magnitude of
\mathsf{Height}(\mathcal{T}^{n})
, it is useful to have estimates on
\mathsf{h}_{n}^{+}
. We give here a simple lower bound on
\mathsf{h}^{+}_{n}
.

Lemma 16
.

Let
n\geq 1
. If
\tau>n
then
\mathsf{h}^{+}_{n}\geq\ln(n/4)
.

Proof.

Set
p_{n}\coloneqq\#\{1\leq i\leq n:\mathrm{x}_{i}=+1\}
and
m_{n}\coloneqq\#\{1\leq i\leq n:\mathrm{x}_{i}=-1\}
. If there exists
1\leq i\leq n-1
such that
\mathrm{x}_{i}=-1
and
\mathrm{x}_{i+1}=+1
, set
\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}=(\mathrm{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathrm{x}_{i-1},+1,-1,\mathrm{x}_{i+2},\mathrm{x}_{i+3},\ldots)
. Then it is a simple matter to check that
\mathsf{h}^{+}_{n}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})\geq\mathsf{h}^{+}_{n}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime})
. By iteration, it readily follows that
\mathsf{h}^{+}_{n}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})\geq\mathsf{h}^{+}_{n}((+1,\ldots,+1,-1,\ldots,-1,\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}_{n+1},\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}_{n+2},\ldots))
where
+1
appears
p_{n}
times and
-1
appears
m_{n}
times. Since
\tau(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})>n
entails
p_{n}\geq n/2
, it follows that
\mathsf{h}^{+}_{n}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})\geq\sum_{i=1}^{p_{n}}1/(i+1)\geq\ln((p_{n}+1)/2)\geq\ln(n/4)
.
∎

4.3
Limit theorems for the height

We are now in position to establish Theorem
3
.

Proof of Theorem
3
.

We start with (I). Recall that for an active vertex
u\in\mathbb{A}_{n}
we always have
\mathsf{b}_{n}(u)=n
. Therefore, by (
2.2
)

\mathsf{H}(U^{n})\quad\mathop{=}^{(d)}\quad\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y^{n}_{i}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathrm{x}_{i}=1\}}.

Using Bennett’s inequality (Proposition
14
), for all
\varepsilon>0

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{\mathsf{H}(U^{n})}{\mathsf{h}^{+}_{n}}-1}}\right|>\varepsilon}}\right)\leq 2\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\mathsf{h}_{n}^{+}\,g(\varepsilon)}}\right).

This upper bound goes to
0
as
n
tends to infinity since
\mathsf{h}_{n}^{+}
goes to infinity by Lemma
16
. We deduce that the sequence
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\mathsf{H}(U^{n})}/{\mathsf{h}^{+}_{n}}-1}}\right)_{n}
converges to
0
in probability. To conclude it suffices to show that it is bounded in
\mathbb{L}^{p}
for all
p\geq 1
. Let
p\geq 1
, we have

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{\mathsf{H}(U^{n})}{\mathsf{h}^{+}_{n}}-1}}\right|^{p}}}\right]=p\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{\mathsf{H}(U^{n})}{\mathsf{h}^{+}_{n}}-1}}\right|\geq u}}\right)u^{p-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u.

Using Bennett’s inequality again and by splitting the integral in half at
u=7
, the above quantity is bounded by

7^{p}+2p\int_{7}^{\infty}\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\mathsf{h}^{+}_{n}\,g(u)}}\right)u^{p-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u.

Notice that for
u\geq 7\geq e^{2}-1
, we have
g(u)\geq u
thus

\int_{7}^{\infty}\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\mathsf{h}^{+}_{n}\,g(u)}}\right)u^{p-1}du\leq\int_{7}^{\infty}\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\mathsf{h}^{+}_{n}\,u}}\right)u^{p-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u=\frac{1}{(\mathsf{h}^{+}_{n})^{p}}\int_{7\mathsf{h}^{+}_{n}}^{\infty}\exp(-u)u^{p-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u.

By an iterated integration by parts, the last quantity is
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\exp(-7\mathsf{h}^{+}_{n})}/{\mathsf{h}^{+}_{n}}}}\right)
. Therefore the boundedness in
\mathbb{L}^{p}
follows.

We now establish (II) and (III). Fix
C>1
. By Proposition
15
, for all
n\geq 1
:

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\mathsf{Height}(\mathcal{T}^{n})}{\mathsf{h}^{+}_{n}}>C}}\right)\leq\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\mathsf{h}^{+}_{n}\,g(C-1)+\ln(n)}}\right).

This upper bound goes to
0
when
\ln(n)=o(\mathsf{h}^{+}_{n})
since
g(C-1)>0
. It also goes to
0
when
C=e+\varepsilon
since
g(e-1+\varepsilon)>1
and by Lemma
16
,
\mathsf{h}^{+}_{n}\geq\ln(n/4)
. Similarly,

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\mathsf{Height}(\mathcal{T}^{n})}{\mathsf{h}^{+}_{n}}<1-\varepsilon}}\right)\leq\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\mathsf{h}^{+}_{n}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})g\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon}}\right)}}\right)\quad\mathop{\longrightarrow}_{n\rightarrow\infty}\quad 0.

This completes the proof of (II). It also shows that the convergence

\frac{\mathsf{Height}(\mathcal{T}^{n})}{\mathsf{h}^{+}_{n}}\enskip\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{{\mathbb{P}}}_{n\to\infty}\enskip 1

holds in probability when
\ln(n)=o(\mathsf{h}^{+}_{n})
. To fully show (III) we just need to prove that the sequence
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\mathsf{Height}(\mathcal{T}^{n})}/{\mathsf{h}^{+}_{n}}-1}}\right)_{n}
is bounded in
\mathbb{L}^{p}
for all
p\geq 1
when
\ln(n)=o(\mathsf{h}^{+}_{n})
. It is done using the same method as in the proof of (I)
by using the fact that for
n
sufficiently large we have

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\mathsf{Height}(\mathcal{T}^{n})}{\mathsf{h}^{+}_{n}}>C}}\right)\leq\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\mathsf{h}^{+}_{n}\,g(C-1)/2}}\right)

for every
C\geq 2
.
∎

5
Regime with a linear amount of active vertices

We shall now investigate the regime where the number of active vertices grows roughly linearly in the size of the trees: the main goal of this section is to establish Theorem
4
. Here we assume that (
1.2
) is in force, namely that there exist
c\in(0,1]
and a sequence
(A_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of positive numbers such that
A_{n}=o(\log n)
as
n\to\infty
and such that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\max_{A_{n}\leqslant i\leqslant\varepsilon n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{S^{n}_{i}}{i}-c}}\right|=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\forall\varepsilon>0,\enskip\liminf_{n\to\infty}\min_{\varepsilon n\leqslant i\leqslant n}\frac{S^{n}_{i}}{n}>0.

5.1
An asymptotic equivalent for
\mathsf{h}_{n}^{+}

Here we show the convergence of Theorem
4
(I), namely:

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline vhncaslineaire}\displaystyle\frac{\mathsf{h}^{+}_{n}}{\ln n}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\frac{c+1}{2c}.

(5.1)

To simplify notation, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and for all
\varepsilon>0
, set

\eta_{n}(\varepsilon)\coloneqq\max_{A_{n}\leqslant i\leqslant\varepsilon n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\frac{S^{n}_{i}}{i}-c}}\right\rvert.

By (
1.2
), we know that
\limsup\eta_{n}(\varepsilon)\to 0
when
\varepsilon\to 0
.

Lemma 17
.

Suppose that the assumptions (
1.2
) hold with
A_{n}\to\infty
when
n\to\infty
. Then, for all
C>1

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:estimeeuniformesurh_{n}+}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}A_{n}\leqslant a<b\leqslant\varepsilon n\\
aC<b\end{subarray}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{1}{\ln(b/a)}\sum_{i=a+1}^{b}\frac{1}{S_{i}^{n}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathrm{x}_{i}^{n}=1\}}-\frac{{c+1}}{2c}}}\right|=0.

(5.2)

Proof of Lemma
17
.

We prove (
5.2
) with a double bound. First, let us start with the upper bound. Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
\beta>1
. For all
A_{n}\leq a<b\leq\varepsilon n
:

\sum_{i=a+1}^{b}\frac{1}{S_{i}^{n}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathrm{x}_{i}^{n}=1\}}={\sum_{a<i\leqslant\beta^{\ell}}\frac{1}{S_{i}^{n}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathrm{x}_{i}^{n}=1\}}}+\sum_{k=1+\ell}^{m}\sum_{\beta^{k-1}<i\leqslant\beta^{k}}\frac{1}{S_{i}^{n}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathrm{x}_{i}^{n}=1\}}+\sum_{\beta^{m}<i\leqslant b}\frac{1}{S_{i}^{n}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathrm{x}_{i}^{n}=1\}}

where
\ell\coloneqq\lceil\ln a/\ln\beta\rceil
,
m\coloneqq\lfloor\ln b/\ln\beta\rfloor
and where, in all the sums over
i
, it is implicit that
i
is an integer. Let
P_{n}\coloneqq\{1\leq i\leq n:\mathrm{x}_{i}=1\}
and
M_{n}\coloneqq\{1\leq i\leq n:\mathrm{x}_{i}=-1\}
.
Note that for all
1\leq i<j\leq n
, we have

\#(P_{n}\cap[i+1,j])-\#(M_{n}\cap[i+1,j])=S_{j}^{n}-S_{i}^{n},\quad\#(P_{n}\cap[i+1,j])+\#(M_{n}\cap[i+1,j])=j-i.

Moreover, when
A_{n}\leq i<j\leq\varepsilon n
, we have
(j-i)c-2j\eta_{n}(\varepsilon)\leq S_{j}^{n}-S_{i}^{n}\leq(j-i)c+2j\eta_{n}(\varepsilon)
.
As a result,

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline qProp9}\frac{c+1}{2}(j-i)-j\eta_{n}(\varepsilon)\leq\#(P_{n}\cap[i+1,j])=\frac{S_{j}^{n}-S_{i}^{n}+j-i}{2}\leq\frac{c+1}{2}(j-i)+j\eta_{n}(\varepsilon).

(5.3)

Therefore, for every
k\in\llbracket 1+\ell,m\rrbracket
,

\displaystyle\sum_{\beta^{k-1}<i\leqslant\beta^{k}}\frac{1}{S_{i}^{n}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathrm{x}_{i}^{n}=1\}}\leq\sum_{\beta^{k-1}<i\leq\beta^{k}}\frac{1}{i(c-\eta_{n}(\varepsilon))}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathrm{x}_{i}^{n}=1\}}

\displaystyle\leq\#\{\beta^{k-1}<i\leq\beta^{k}:\mathrm{x}_{i}^{n}=1\}\frac{1}{\beta^{k-1}(c-\eta_{n}(\varepsilon))}

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{c+1}{2}(\beta^{k}-\beta^{k-1}{+1})+\beta^{k}\eta_{n}(\varepsilon)}}\right)\frac{1}{\beta^{k-1}(c-\eta_{n}(\varepsilon))}

\displaystyle{\leq}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{c+1}{2}(\beta-1+{1/A_{n}})+\beta\eta_{n}(\varepsilon)}}\right)\frac{1}{(c-\eta_{n}(\varepsilon))}\eqcolon U_{n}(\varepsilon)

where the third inequality comes from (
5.3
) and where for the fourth inequality we used that
\beta^{k-1}\geq\beta^{\ell}\geq a\geq A_{n}
.
Similarly, since
\beta^{\ell-1}\leq a
,

\sum_{a<i\leqslant\beta^{\ell}}\frac{1}{S_{i}^{n}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathrm{x}_{i}^{n}=1\}}\leq\#\{a<i\leq\beta^{\ell}:\mathrm{x}_{i}^{n}=1\}\frac{1}{\beta^{\ell-1}(c-\eta_{n}(\varepsilon))}\leq\frac{\beta^{\ell}-\beta^{\ell-1}}{\beta^{\ell-1}(c-\eta_{n}(\varepsilon))},

and since
\beta^{m+1}\geq b
,

\sum_{\beta^{m}<i\leqslant b}\frac{1}{S_{i}^{n}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathrm{x}_{i}^{n}=1\}}\leq\#\{\beta^{m}<i\leq b:\mathrm{x}_{i}^{n}=1\}\frac{1}{\beta^{\ell-1}(c-\eta_{n}(\varepsilon))}\leq\frac{\beta^{m+1}-\beta^{m}}{\beta^{m}(c-\eta_{n}(\varepsilon))}.

Thus

\sum_{i=a+1}^{b}\frac{1}{S_{i}^{n}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathrm{x}_{i}^{n}=1\}}\leq(m-\ell)U_{n}(\varepsilon)+\frac{\beta^{\ell}-\beta^{\ell-1}}{\beta^{\ell-1}(c-\eta_{n}(\varepsilon))}+\frac{\beta^{m+1}-\beta^{m}}{\beta^{m}(c-\eta_{n}(\varepsilon))}\leq\frac{\ln(b/a)}{\ln\beta}U_{n}(\varepsilon)+\frac{2(\beta-1)}{c-\eta_{n}(\varepsilon)}.

For the lower bound, the same ideas apply.
Indeed, for every integer
k\in\llbracket 1+\ell,m\rrbracket
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\beta^{k-1}<i\leqslant\beta^{k}}\frac{1}{S_{i}^{n}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathrm{x}_{i}^{n}=1\}}

\displaystyle\geq\#\{\beta^{k-1}<i\leq\beta^{k}:\mathrm{x}_{i}^{n}=1\}\frac{1}{\beta^{k}(c+\eta_{n}(\varepsilon))}

\displaystyle\geq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{c+1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\beta^{k}-\beta^{k-1}-2}}\right)-\beta^{k}\eta_{n}(\varepsilon)}}\right)\frac{1}{\beta^{k}(c+\eta_{n}(\varepsilon))}

\displaystyle\geq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{c+1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{1}{\beta}-\frac{2}{\beta A_{n}}}}\right)-\eta_{n}(\varepsilon)}}\right)\frac{1}{(c+\eta_{n}(\varepsilon))}\eqcolon L_{n}(\varepsilon)

where the second inequality comes from (
5.3
) and the third one comes from from the bound
\beta^{k}\geq\beta^{\ell+1}\geq\beta a\geq\beta A_{n}
. Thus

\sum_{i=a+1}^{b}\frac{1}{S_{i}^{n}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathrm{x}_{i}^{n}=1\}}\geq(m-\ell)L_{n}(\varepsilon)\geq\frac{\ln(b/a)}{\ln\beta}L_{n}(\varepsilon)-2L_{n}(\varepsilon)\geq\frac{\ln(b/a)}{\ln\beta}L_{n}(\varepsilon)-\frac{c+1}{c}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{1}{\beta}-\frac{2}{\beta A_{n}}}}\right).

Using the assumption that
aC<b
, we end up with the following inequalities

\frac{L_{n}(\varepsilon)}{\ln\beta}-\frac{c+1}{c\ln C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{1}{\beta}-\frac{2}{\beta A_{n}}}}\right)\leq\frac{1}{\ln(b/a)}\sum_{i=a+1}^{b}\frac{1}{S_{i}^{n}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathrm{x}_{i}^{n}=1\}}\leq\frac{U_{n}(\varepsilon)}{\ln\beta}+\frac{2(\beta-1)}{{(c}-\eta_{n}(\varepsilon))\ln C}.

Using the fact that
A_{n}\rightarrow\infty
, notice that
\limsup_{{n\rightarrow\infty}}U_{n}(\varepsilon)\to(\beta-1)(c+1)/(2c)
and
\liminf_{{n\rightarrow\infty}}L_{n}(\varepsilon)\to(1-1/\beta)(c+1)/(2c)
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Therefore

\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}A_{n}\leqslant a<b\leqslant\varepsilon n\\
aC<b\end{subarray}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{1}{\ln(b/a)}\sum_{i=a+1}^{b}\frac{1}{S_{i}^{n}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathrm{x}_{i}^{n}=1\}}-\frac{{c+1}}{2c}}}\right|\leq\frac{{c+1}}{2c}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{\beta-1}{\ln\beta}-1}}\right|+\frac{c+1}{c\ln C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{1}{\beta}}}\right)+\frac{2(\beta-1)}{c\ln C}.

This bound tends to
0
as
\beta\rightarrow 1
; since
\beta>1
was arbitrary, the result follows.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4
(I).

Let
(A^{\prime}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of positive integers such that
A_{n}\leq A^{\prime}_{n}
,
A^{\prime}_{n}=o(\ln n)
and
A^{\prime}_{n}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
. Applying Lemma

we see that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{1}{\ln n}\sum_{A^{\prime}_{n}<i\leqslant\varepsilon n}\frac{1}{S_{i}^{n}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathrm{x}_{i}^{n}=1\}}-\frac{{c+1}}{2c}}}\right|=0

Since
S_{i}^{n}\geq 1

, we have

\sum_{i=1}^{A^{\prime}_{n}}\frac{1}{S_{i}^{n}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathrm{x}_{i}^{n}=1\}}=o(\ln n).

Finally, for all

, by the second assumption of (

),

\sum_{\varepsilon n\leqslant i\leqslant n}\frac{1}{S_{i}^{n}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathrm{x}_{i}^{n}=1\}}=\mathcal{O}(1).

Combining everything gives the desired convergence (

5.2
Distances between uniform vertices

Here we prove Theorem
4
(II). We keep the notation introduced in Section
2.3
: for a vertex
v\in\mathbb{V}_{n}
, let
\mathsf{b}_{n}(v)
be the largest
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
such that

6
Application: contact-tracing in a stochastic SIR dynamics

Our results can be applied to study the so-called “infection tree” of a stochastic SIR dynamics, which is a classical model for the evolution of epidemics (for background on stochastic epidemic models, see
[
4
,
12
]
). We assume that initially there is
1
infectious individual (that has just become infected) and
n
susceptible individuals. The infectious periods of different infectives are i.i.d distributed according to an exponential random variable of parameter
1
. During its infectious period an infective makes contacts with a given individual at the time points of a time homogeneous Poisson process with intensity
\lambda_{n}
. If a contacted individual is still susceptible, then it becomes infectious and is immediately able to infect other individuals. An individual is considered “removed” once its infectious period has terminated, and is then immune to new infections, playing no further part in the epidemic spread. The epidemic ceases as soon as there are no more infectious individual present in the population. All Poisson processes are assumed to be independent of each other; they are also independent of the infectious periods.

We call a “step” an event where either a susceptible individuals becomes infective, or where an individual’s infectious period terminates. Denote by
\tau_{n}
the number of steps made when the epidemic ceases. For
0\leq k\leq\tau_{n}
, let
{\mathscr{T}^{n}_{k}}
be the infection tree after
k
steps, in which the vertices are individuals and where an edge is present between two individuals if one has infected the other. We are interested in the evolution of the associated “infection tree”
({\mathscr{T}_{k}^{n}})_{0\leq k\leq\tau_{n}}
, as well as in the shape of the full infection tree
{\mathscr{T}^{n}_{\tau_{n}}}
when the epidemic ceases.

Let
(U^{n}_{k},I^{n}_{k})_{k\geq 0}
be a Markov chain with initial state
(U^{n}_{0},I^{n}_{0})=(n,1)
and transition probabilities given by

(U^{n}_{k+1},I^{n}_{k+1})=\begin{cases}(U^{n}_{k}-1,I^{n}_{k}+1)&\textrm{with probability }\frac{\lambda_{n}U^{n}_{k}}{1+\lambda_{n}U^{n}_{k}}\\
(U^{n}_{k},I^{n}_{k}-1)&\textrm{with probability }\frac{1}{1+\lambda_{n}U^{n}_{k}}\end{cases}

with
\{(k,0):0\leq k\leq n\}
as absorbing states (we use “
U
” for “uninfected”). Observe that evolution of the number of susceptible individuals and the number of infectious individuals in the infection tree evolves according to this Markov chain. Then define the random sequence
\mathrm{\mathbf{X}}^{n}=(\mathrm{X}^{n}_{i})_{1\leq i\leq\tau^{\prime}_{n}}
of
\pm 1
as follows: let
\tau^{\prime}_{n}
be the absorption time of the Markov chain, and for
1\leq i\leq\tau^{\prime}_{n}
set
\mathrm{X}^{n}_{i}=I^{n}_{i}-I^{n}_{i-1}
.

Then by construction, it is clear that:

({\mathscr{T}^{n}_{k}})_{0\leq k\leq\tau_{n}}\quad\mathop{=}^{(d)}\quad(\mathcal{T}_{k}(\mathrm{\mathbf{X}}^{n}))_{0\leq k\leq\tau^{\prime}_{n}}.

To simplify notation, let
{\mathscr{T}^{n}}={\mathscr{T}^{n}_{\tau_{n}}}
be the full infection tree when the epidemic ceases. We also identify the random variables in the above equality in distribution. For all
p\in[0,1)
, we denote by
\mathcal{G}(1-p)
the geometric distribution
\mu
given by
\mu(k)=(1-p)p^{k}
for every integer
k\geq 0
.

.

The following assertions hold.

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{I^{n}_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}}{n}}}\right)_{1\leq t\leq 2}\enskip\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{{\mathbb{P}}}_{n\to\infty}\enskip(2-t)_{1\leq t\leq 2},

which concludes the proof of (
6.3
).

We finally turn to (II) (b). As in the proof of (I) (a), the first two convergences of (
6.4
) are proved by applying Theorem
4
to the tree
\mathcal{T}_{\lfloor(2-\varepsilon)n\rfloor}(\mathrm{\mathbf{X}}^{n})
for some
\varepsilon>0
small enough, with
c=1
and
A_{n}=\log\log n
. Indeed, by (
6.13
),

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline qlgninfectioncasrecursif}\sup_{\log\log n\leq k\leq n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{I^{n}_{k}}{k}-1}}\right|\enskip\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{{\mathbb{P}}}_{n\to\infty}\enskip 0.

(6.14)

After applying Skorokhod’s representation theorem, (
6.14
) implies the left hand side of (
1.2
) and the right hand side follows from (
6.3
).

The last convergence of (II) (b) is more delicate. The idea is to apply Theorem
4
to the tree
\mathcal{T}_{n}(\mathrm{\mathbf{X}}^{n})
and then to show that the vertices added between times
n
and
\tau^{\prime}_{n}
, in a first approximation, do not affect the height.

More precisely, one can first see that by Theorem
4
the height of
\mathcal{T}_{n}(\mathrm{\mathbf{X}}^{n})
divided by
\ln n
converges in probability towards
e
.
Indeed, by (
6.14
), after applying Skorokhod’s representation theorem, entails that (
1.2
) holds almost surely for
\mathcal{T}_{n}(\mathrm{\mathbf{X}}^{n})
with
c=1
and
A_{n}=\log\log n
.

Next, to understand how the height of
{\mathscr{T}^{n}}
behaves compared to
\mathcal{T}_{n}(\mathrm{\mathbf{X}}^{n})
we need to estimate the quantity

\mathsf{h}^{n}\coloneqq\sum_{i=n}^{\tau^{\prime}_{n}-1}\frac{1}{I_{i}^{n}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathrm{X}^{n}_{i}=1\}}.

Indeed, as we have already seen multiple times, roughly speaking this quantity represents “the height” added between times
n
and
\tau^{\prime}_{n}
.

Let us prove that

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline qconvergencedestransitionscaspresquerecursif}\sup_{3n/2\leq k\leq 2n}\frac{\lambda_{n}U^{n}_{k}}{1+\lambda_{n}U^{n}_{k}}\leq\frac{\lambda_{n}U^{n}_{\lfloor 3n/2\rfloor}}{1+\lambda_{n}U^{n}_{\lfloor 3n/2\rfloor}}\enskip\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{{\mathbb{P}}}_{n\to\infty}\enskip 0.

(6.15)

The inequality holds since
(U^{n}_{k})_{k\geq 0}
is non-increasing. For the above convergence, assume by contradiction that there exists
\varepsilon>0
and an increasing sequence of integers
(n_{j})_{j\geq 1}
such that for all
j\geq 1
,

{\mathbb{P}}\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\lambda_{n_{j}}U^{n_{j}}_{\lfloor 3n_{j}/2\rfloor}}{1+\lambda_{n_{j}}U^{n_{j}}_{\lfloor 3n_{j}/2\rfloor}}\geq\varepsilon}}\right)\geq\varepsilon.

On this event, by monotonicity for every
k\in\llbracket n_{j},\lfloor 3n_{j}/2\rfloor\rrbracket
we have

\frac{\lambda_{n_{j}}U^{n_{j}}_{k}}{1+\lambda_{n_{j}}U^{n_{j}}_{k}}\geq\varepsilon.

Then, using a coupling between
(I^{n_{j}}_{k})_{k\geq n}
and a simple random walk
(\widetilde{Y}^{\varepsilon}_{k})_{k\geq n_{j}}
starting at
I^{n_{j}}_{n_{j}}
at time
n_{j}
whose step is
+1
with probability
\varepsilon
and
-1
with probability
1-\varepsilon
, by the law of large numbers and since
I^{n}_{n}/n\to 1
in probability as
n\to\infty
by (
6.3
), one obtains that on the same event,

\frac{I^{n_{j}}_{\lfloor 3n_{j}/2\rfloor}}{n_{j}}\geq\frac{\widetilde{Y}^{\varepsilon}_{\lfloor 3n_{j}/2\rfloor}}{n_{j}}\enskip\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{{\mathbb{P}}}_{n\to\infty}\enskip 1+\frac{2\varepsilon-1}{2},

which is absurd in view of (
6.3
). This proves (
6.15
).

Next, let us prove that

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline qhntendverszero}\frac{\mathsf{h}^{n}}{\log n}\enskip\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{{\mathbb{P}}}_{n\to\infty}\enskip 0.

(6.16)

Define
\hat{\mathrm{X}}_{i}^{n}=-\mathrm{X}_{\tau^{\prime}_{n}-i+1}^{n}
for all
i\in\llbracket 1,\tau^{\prime}_{n}\rrbracket
and
\hat{S}_{i}^{n}=I_{\tau^{\prime}_{n}-i}^{n}=\hat{\mathrm{X}}_{1}^{n}+\cdots+\hat{\mathrm{X}}_{i}^{n}
for all
i\in\llbracket 0,\tau^{\prime}_{n}\rrbracket
. Then

\mathsf{h}^{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{\tau^{\prime}_{n}-n}\frac{1}{\hat{S}_{i}^{n}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\hat{\mathrm{X}}^{n}_{i}=-1\}}.

By (
6.3
), to prove (
6.16
), it suffices to prove that

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline qsommejusquensur2tendverszero}\frac{1}{\log n}\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor\wedge(\tau^{\prime}_{n}-n)}\frac{1}{\hat{S}_{i}^{n}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\hat{\mathrm{X}}^{n}_{i}=-1\}}\enskip\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{{\mathbb{P}}}_{n\to\infty}\enskip 0.

(6.17)

For all
\varepsilon>0
, for all
n\geq 1
, define the event

\mathcal{A}^{n}_{\varepsilon}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\sup_{3n/2\leq k\leq 2n}\frac{\lambda_{n}U^{n}_{k}}{1+\lambda_{n}U^{n}_{k}}\leq\varepsilon}}\right\}.

By (
6.15
), we know that

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline qlimiteprobaAnepsilontendvers1}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\liminf_{n\to\infty}{\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{A}^{n}_{\varepsilon})=1.

(6.18)

Besides, on the event
\mathcal{A}^{n}_{\varepsilon}
, one can couple the
(\hat{\mathrm{X}}^{n}_{i})_{1\leq i\leq n/2\wedge(\tau_{n}-n)}
’s with i.i.d. random variables
(\xi^{\varepsilon}_{i})_{i\geq 1}
such that
{\mathbb{P}}(\xi^{\varepsilon}_{i}=-1)=\varepsilon
and
{\mathbb{P}}(\xi^{\varepsilon}_{i}=1)=1-\varepsilon
so that for all
i\leq n/2\wedge(\tau_{n}-n)
, we have
\xi^{\varepsilon}_{i}\leq\hat{\mathrm{X}}^{n}_{i}\leq 1
. For all
i\geq 0
, let
\hat{Y}^{\varepsilon}_{i}=\xi^{\varepsilon}_{1}+\ldots+\xi^{\varepsilon}_{i}
. Then, for all
i\leq n/2\wedge(\tau_{n}-n)
, we have
\hat{Y}^{\varepsilon}_{i}\leq\hat{S}^{n}_{i}
. In particular, on the event
\mathcal{A}^{n}_{\varepsilon}\cap\{\forall i\in\llbracket 1,n/2\rrbracket,\ \hat{Y}^{\varepsilon}_{i}>0\}
,

\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor\wedge(\tau^{\prime}_{n}-n)}\frac{1}{\hat{S}_{i}^{n}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\hat{\mathrm{X}}^{n}_{i}=-1\}}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\frac{1}{\hat{Y}_{i}^{\varepsilon}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\xi^{\varepsilon}_{i}=-1\}}.

But, by the strong law of large numbers, we have

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline qlimiteprobaYepsilontoujoursplusgrandqueisur2}{\mathbb{P}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\forall i\geq 0,\ \hat{Y}^{\varepsilon}_{i}\geq i/2}}\right)\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0}1.

(6.19)

For all
\varepsilon>0
, on the event
\mathcal{A}^{n}_{\varepsilon}\cap\{\forall i\geq 0,\hat{Y}^{\varepsilon}_{i}\geq i/2\}
, we obtain that

\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor\wedge(\tau^{\prime}_{n}-n)}\frac{1}{\hat{S}_{i}^{n}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\hat{\mathrm{X}}^{n}_{i}=-1\}}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\frac{2}{i}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\xi^{\varepsilon}_{i}=-1\}}.

Besides,

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\frac{2}{i}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\xi^{\varepsilon}_{i}=-1\}}}}\right]=2\varepsilon\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\frac{1}{i}.

Therefore, we obtain that

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{A}^{n}_{\varepsilon}\cap\{\forall i\geq 0,\ \hat{Y}^{\varepsilon}_{i}\geq i/2\}}\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor\wedge(\tau^{\prime}_{n}-n)}\frac{1}{\hat{S}_{i}^{n}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\hat{\mathrm{X}}^{n}_{i}=-1\}}}}\right]\leq 2\varepsilon\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\frac{1}{i}.

After letting
\varepsilon\to 0
, using (
6.18
) and (
6.19
), we deduce (
6.17
).

It remains to show how (
6.16
) implies that
\mathsf{Height}({\mathscr{T}^{n}})=\mathsf{Height}(\mathcal{T}_{n}(\mathrm{\mathbf{X}}^{n}))+o(\ln n)
. To do so we will use, once again, the coalescence construction and Bennett’s inequality. By Algorithm
2.2
, conditionally on
\tau^{\prime}_{n}
and
(I_{i}^{n})_{0\leqslant i\leqslant\tau^{\prime}_{n}}
, the height of any new vertex added between time
n
and
\tau^{\prime}_{n}
is stochastically dominated by
\mathsf{Height}(\mathcal{T}_{n}(\mathrm{\mathbf{X}}^{n}))+\sum_{i=n}^{\tau^{\prime}_{n}-1}Y_{i}
where
(Y_{i}^{n})_{n\leqslant i\leqslant\tau^{\prime}_{n}-1}
are i.i.d Bernoulli random variables of respective parameters
(1/I_{i}^{n}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathrm{X}^{n}_{i}=1\}})_{n\leqslant i\leqslant\tau^{\prime}_{n}-1}
. Using Bennett’s inequality and a union bound, as in Sec.
4.3
, for all
\varepsilon>0
we obtain that

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathsf{Height}({\mathscr{T}^{n}})-\mathsf{Height}(\mathcal{T}_{n}(\mathrm{\mathbf{X}}^{n}))\geq\mathsf{h}^{n}+\varepsilon\ln n\,\middle|\,{\tau^{\prime}_{n},(I_{i}^{n})_{0\leqslant i\leqslant\tau^{\prime}_{n}}}}}\right)\leq n\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\mathsf{h}^{n}g\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\varepsilon\ln n}{\mathsf{h}^{n}}}}\right)}}\right)

where
g(u)=(u+1)\ln(u+1)-u\sim u\ln u
as
u\to+\infty
. Since
\mathsf{h}^{n}=o(\ln n)
in probability, the above upper bound goes to
0
when
n
goes to
\infty
and thus
\mathsf{Height}({\mathscr{T}^{n}})
is of order
e\ln n
.
∎

7
Perspectives and extensions

We mention here some perspectives for future research in the direction of studying the impact of freezing in random graph models.

(1)

What is the number of ends of
\mathcal{T}_{\infty}({\mathbf{x}})
?

(2)

It would be interesting to study the evolution of the sizes of the trees in the forests obtained by Algorithm
2.2
(in the particular case where
\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}_{n}=1
for every
n\geq 1
this corresponds to Kingman’s coalescent), as well as the evolution of degree vertices (in the particular case where
\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}_{n}=1
for every
n\geq 1
there are nice connections with Pólya urns).

(3)

How small can be the typical height of
\mathcal{T}_{n}(\mathrm{x})
when
\tau(\mathrm{x})\geq n
? We conjecture that for any sequence
(\mathrm{x}^{n})_{n\geq 1}
such that
\tau(\mathrm{x}^{n})\geq n
for every
n\geq 1
, for every
\varepsilon>0
we have

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathsf{Height}(\mathcal{T}_{n}(\mathrm{x}))\geq(e-\varepsilon)\ln(n)}}\right)\quad\mathop{\longrightarrow}_{n\rightarrow\infty}\quad 1.

(4)

A natural question is to obtain a limit theorem for the height of the epidemic tree
{\mathscr{T}}^{n}
in the setting of Theorem
24
(I) (b) (see Remark
25
).

(5)

It would be very interesting to extend our results to other attachment mechanisms, such as preferential attachment, where new vertices attach to existing vertices proportional to their degree.

(6)

Similarly one may wonder what happens when the frozen vertices are not chosen uniformly, notably when vertices with high degrees are more or less likely to freeze.

(7)

An important question arising in the context of growing real-world networks or in the context of infection tracing is how to find the root, or patient zero
[
38
]
. This is a very active area of research, sometimes called
network archeology

[
35
]
. Such questions have been for instance considered for uniform attachment and preferential attachment trees
[
24
,
13
,
31
]
, as well as Bienaymé trees
[
11
]
.
