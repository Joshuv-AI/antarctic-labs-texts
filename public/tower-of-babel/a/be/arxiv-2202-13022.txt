Arbitrary Order Energy and Enstrophy Conserving Finite Element Methods for 2D Incompressible Fluid Dynamics and Drift-Reduced Magnetohydrodynamics Journal: Journal of Computational Physics
Milan Holec, Ben Zhu, Ilon Joseph, Christopher J. Vogl, Ben S. Southworth, Alejandro Campos, Andris M. Dimits, Will E. Pazner

Abstract
Maintaining conservation laws in the fully discrete setting is critical for accurate long-time behavior of numerical simulations and requires accounting for discrete conservation properties in both space and time. This paper derives arbitrary order finite element exterior calculus spatial discretizations for the two-dimensional (2D) Navier-Stokes and drift-reduced magnetohydrodynamic equations that conserve both energy and enstrophy to machine precision when coupled with generally symplectic time-integration methods. Both continuous and discontinuous-Galerkin (DG) weak formulations can ensure conservation, but only generally symplectic time integration methods, such as the implicit midpoint method, permit exact conservation in time. Moreover, the symplectic implicit midpoint method yields an order of magnitude speedup over explicit schemes. The methods are implemented using the MFEM library and the solutions are verified for an extensive suite of 2D neutral fluid turbulence test problems. Numerical solutions are verified via comparison to a semi-analytic linear eigensolver as well as to the finite difference Global Drift Ballooning (GDB) code. However, it is found that turbulent simulations that conserve both energy and enstrophy tend to have too much power at high wavenumber and that this part of the spectrum should be controlled by reintroducing artificial dissipation. The DG formulation allows upwinding of the advection operator which dissipates enstrophy while still maintaining conservation of energy. Coupling upwinded DG with implicit symplectic integration appears to offer the best compromise of allowing mid-range wavenumbers to reach the appropriate amplitude while still controlling the high-wavenumber part of the spectrum.



1
Introduction

In the absence of dissipation, the equations of fluid dynamics and plasma physics possess conserved quantities, such as energy and enstrophy (in 2D) or helicity (in 3D), that are invariant under evolution in time.
Many numerical methods, including finite difference, finite element, finite volume, and pseudo-spectral methods, have been explored for the purpose of developing numerical approximations for solutions of the underlying partial differential equations, such as the Navier-Stokes equations, the magnetohydrodynamics (MHD) equations, and the Braginskii equations.
It is important for any numerical approximation scheme to approximately conserve these invariants in order to ensure the long-term accuracy (and often the stability) of the approximate solution.
The introduction of finite element exterior calculus (FEEC)
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
has reinvigorated interest in finite element approaches to fluid and plasma physics and, as will be shown, provides a natural framework for enforcing these conservation laws.

For the equations of fluid and plasma physics, the numerical discretization of the advection operator is key to ensuring conservation.
In 2D, the finite difference community benefits from the ability to write the advection operator using the numerical Arakawa bracket
[
4
]
to conserve the two quadratic invariants: energy and enstrophy.
In contrast, existing finite element approaches tend to introduce more dissipation than necessary, leading to an artificial reduction of energy at small spatial scales.
One approach to enforcing conservation focuses on using an antisymmetric form for the advection operator
[
5
,

,

,

]
.
Another conservative approach
[
9

, developed for a specific discontinuous Galerkin (DG) finite element method (FEM), expresses the advection operator in the form of a Poisson bracket that enforces both antisymmetry as well as a discrete divergence theorem for products of functions.
A review of structure-preserving integration techniques used in computational engineering was recently given in
[
10
]
.

Recently, low-order energy and enstrophy conserving schemes were developed within the FEEC framework for the 2D Navier-Stokes equations
[

]
,
[
12
]
, as well as for the rotating shallow water equations and the shallow water equations on the sphere
[

,
14
,

In this work, an FEEC approach to the 2D incompressible Navier-Stokes
[
16
]
and the 2D drift-reduced magnetohydrodynamics (MHD) equations
[
17
,
18
,
19
]
is derived that conserves both energy and enstrophy for arbitrary polynomial order, in the absence of dissipation.
Because the theorems of vector calculus are satisfied at the discrete level within the FEEC framework, the spatial discretization automatically conserves both energy and enstrophy as long as the proper finite element spaces are chosen for the primary variables.

A conservative scheme also requires an appropriate choice of numerical time integration technique,
an important fact that has not been discussed by previous authors.
This is particularly relevant here, as both energy and enstrophy correspond to quadratic functions, and significant care must be taken to conserve quadratic invariants (in contrast to other physical problems where energy is a linear first integral invariant that is trivial to conserve using standard time integration schemes).
In fact, Ref.
[
20
]
proves that for the majority of integrators used in practice only generally symplectic time integration techniques are able to conserve quadratic invariants exactly and that any method that conserves an approximate invariant exactly must be conjugate to a symplectic method.
Our results verify the theory and also indicate that certain explicit methods that approximate symplectic methods also have relatively good conservation properties, perhaps because they require small times steps for stability.

However, an extensive set of numerical tests demonstrate that the schemes that conserve both energy and enstrophy exactly lead to turbulent simulations with unphysically high amplitudes at short spatial wavelengths.
Reintroducing dissipation in a number of different ways, e.g. by adding numerical viscosity or by using an asymmetric implicit time integration method, like backward Euler, tends to tame the short wavelength part of the spectrum.
Unfortunately, controlling the spectrum through a non-symplectic time-integrator spoils conservation in manner similar to adding an artificially large viscosity.
For discontinuous Galerkin methods there is another choice: upwinding the advection operator within the FEEC framework leads to a numerical scheme that dissipates enstrophy but conserves energy. This combination appears to offer the best compromise of capturing the correct mesoscopic spatial mode structure by allowing mid-range scales to grow to the proper amplitude, while still controlling the spectrum of the short wavelength spatial modes.
The upwinded DG can be seen as the stream function/vorticity
formulation of the Lie derivative method
[
12

and also provides
a simpler alternative to the SUPG formulation of stream function/vorticity introduced in
[
11
]

\sim 10\times
speed-up. This means that the CFL condition is too restrictive in order to capture the relevant physics in mesoscopic spatial mode range.

In order to verify and explore the theoretical results, a new FEEC fluid solver based on the MFEM finite element library

The solver is written in C++ and has been run within a high-performance computing environment on thousands of cores.
Both linear and nonlinear solvers for the equations have been developed, including preconditioning strategies for the linear solves.

A series of numerical test problems that display both decaying and forced 2D turbulence, recently reviewed in

In order to do so, we reproduce the direct and inverse cascades predicted by Batchelor and Kraichnan

The numerical model is verified by comparing the linear growth rates with predictions of a semi-analytical eigensolver for various initial and boundary conditions.
The nonlinear results are benchmarked by comparing the Fourier spectrum to the results of the Global Drift Ballooning (GDB) finite difference code

Model equations

Conservation of mass is expressed as the advection of the mass density
\rho
by the fluid velocity
\vec{v}

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\vec{\nabla}\cdot\rho\vec{v}

\displaystyle=0.

(1)

Because the time-rate of change of the mass density along the fluid trajectory is given by

\displaystyle d\rho/dt:=\partial_{t}\rho+\vec{v}\cdot\vec{\nabla}\rho

\displaystyle=-\rho\vec{\nabla}\cdot\vec{v},

the condition that the fluid flow be incompressible requires the velocity to be divergence free
\vec{\nabla}\cdot\vec{v}=0
.
Momentum conservation is expressed via the incompressible Navier-Stokes equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\rho\vec{v})+\vec{\nabla}\cdot(\rho\vec{v}\vec{v})+\vec{\nabla}p

\displaystyle=\vec{\nabla}\cdot\overleftrightarrow{\mu}\cdot\vec{\nabla}\vec{v}-\alpha\rho\vec{v}+\vec{F}_{v}

(2)

where
p
is the scalar pressure,
\overleftrightarrow{\mu}
is the viscosity tensor,

\alpha
is a frictional damping rate, and
\vec{F}_{v}
is a “stirring force".
Enforcing the constraint of incompressible flow requires the pressure to satisfy the relation

\displaystyle\vec{\nabla}\cdot\rho^{-1}\vec{\nabla}p

\displaystyle=\vec{\nabla}\cdot\rho^{-1}\left(\vec{\nabla}\cdot\overleftrightarrow{\mu}\cdot\vec{\nabla}\vec{v}-\alpha\rho\vec{v}+\vec{F}_{v}-\rho\vec{v}\cdot\vec{\nabla}\vec{v}\right).

(3)

In 2D, one can ensure that the velocity is exactly divergence free by assuming that it is defined via

\displaystyle\vec{v}=\hat{z}\times\vec{\nabla}\Phi

(4)

where
\Phi
is the stream function
and solving for the vorticity

\displaystyle w=\vec{\nabla}\cdot(\hat{z}\times\vec{v})=-\vec{\nabla}\cdot(\overleftrightarrow{1}-\hat{z}\otimes\hat{z})\cdot\vec{\nabla}\Phi.

(5)

For constant density,
\rho
, and constant transport coefficients,
\alpha
and
\mu
, the potential formulation allows one to rewrite the equations as

\displaystyle{\partial_{t}w}+\vec{\nabla}\cdot(w\vec{v})

\displaystyle=\vec{\nabla}\cdot\mu\vec{\nabla}w-\alpha w+S_{w}

(6)

where
S_{w}:=-\hat{z}\cdot\vec{\nabla}\times\vec{F}_{v}/\rho
.

The drift-reduced MHD model is derived using an adiabatic approximation to the drift velocity that assumes that the spatial and temporal variations of the magnetic field,
\vec{B}
, are much slower than the dynamics of interest.
For the models studied here
[
17
,
18
]
, the electric field
\vec{E}
, will be assumed to be approximately electrostatic, i.e. determined via
\vec{E}=-\vec{\nabla}\phi
where
\phi
is the electric potential.
Generalizations of this model
[
29
,
30
,
19
,
31
,
32
,
33
]
can also be used to describe the physics of shear Alfvén waves and magnetic reconnection.
The dominant momentum balance is ensured by requiring the Lorentz force, proportional to
\vec{E}+\vec{v}\times\vec{B}
, to vanish to lowest order.
This implies that the drift velocity is approximately given by the
\vec{E}\times\vec{B}
velocity

\displaystyle\vec{v}=\vec{E}\times\frac{{\hat{b}}}{B}=\frac{{\hat{b}}}{B}\times\vec{\nabla}\phi.

(7)

where
{\hat{b}}=\vec{B}/B
is the direction of the magnetic field and
B=\left|\vec{B}\right|
is its magnitude.
Although the drift-reduced MHD velocity is not exactly divergence free for spatially varying
B
, it is approximately divergence-free in the sense that the scale length for variations is much longer than that of turbulent fluctuations.
The model is exactly divergence-free when
\vec{B}
is constant in space, e.g.
\vec{B}=B_{0}\hat{z}
for constant
B_{0}
, in which case it reduces to the same form as for the incompressible neutral fluid.

Assuming that the plasma dynamics is quasineutral requires the electric current,
\vec{J}
, to be divergence free:
\vec{\nabla}\cdot\vec{J}=0
.
Solving for the velocity to one higher order yields a polarization current proportional to the rate of change of the drift velocity

\displaystyle\vec{v}=\vec{E}\times\frac{{\hat{b}}}{B}+\frac{{\hat{b}}}{B}\times\left[\partial_{t}+\vec{\nabla}\cdot\vec{v}\right]\left(\rho\vec{E}\times\frac{{\hat{b}}}{B}\right)+\dots.

(8)

Using the quasineutrality constraint
\vec{\nabla}\cdot\vec{J}_{\|}=\vec{\nabla}\cdot({\hat{b}}\sigma_{\|}E_{\|})=-\vec{\nabla}\cdot\vec{J}_{\perp}
yields the dynamics of the charge density
w=\vec{\nabla}\cdot\frac{{\hat{b}}}{B}\times\rho\vec{v}
.
Truncating the expansion at the first nontrivial order yields a closed set of equations

\displaystyle{\partial_{t}w}+\vec{\nabla}\cdot w\vec{v}

\displaystyle=\vec{\nabla}\cdot\overleftrightarrow{\mu}\cdot\vec{\nabla}w-\alpha w+\vec{\nabla}_{\|}\cdot\sigma_{\|}\vec{\nabla}_{\|}\phi+S_{w},

(9a)

\displaystyle\vec{\nabla}\cdot\frac{\rho}{B^{2}}\vec{\nabla}_{\perp}\varphi

\displaystyle=-w,

(9b)

\displaystyle\vec{v}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\hat{b}}}{B}\times\vec{\nabla}_{\perp}\varphi

(9c)

Here
\rho
is the mass density,
\overleftrightarrow{\mu}
is the viscosity tensor,

\alpha
represents a frictional damping coefficient, e.g. friction with a neutral gas assumed to be at rest, and
\sigma_{\|}
represents electrical conductivity parallel to magnetic field lines.
Here, directionality with respect to the magnetic field

\vec{B}
is expressed using the conventions

\displaystyle\vec{\nabla}_{||}

\displaystyle:={\hat{b}}\otimes{\hat{b}}\cdot\vec{\nabla}

\displaystyle\vec{\nabla}_{\perp}

\displaystyle:=\vec{\nabla}-\vec{\nabla}_{||}=\left(\overleftrightarrow{1}-{\hat{b}}\otimes{\hat{b}}\right)\cdot\vec{\nabla}

where
\overleftrightarrow{1}
is the identity matrix.
Clearly, these equations must be supplemented with an appropriate set of initial conditions and boundary conditions in order for the solution to be unique.

For the incompressible model, kinetic energy,
{\mathcal{E}}
, which we simply refer to as energy, is defined as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}:=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\rho\vec{v}^{2}~dV=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\rho}{B^{2}}\vec{\nabla}_{\perp}\varphi\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{\perp}\varphi~dV

(10)

where
dV
is the 2D volume element, i.e. the area, for the region of interest
\Omega
.
Similarly, enstrophy,
{\mathcal{S}}
, is defined as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}:=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}w^{2}~dV.

(11)

Both energy and enstrophy are conserved in the absence of dissipation and external forces when an appropriate set of boundary conditions is chosen that eliminates the net fluxes over the boundary of the region of interest,
\partial\Omega
.

For both of the vorticity equations (
6
) and (
9a
), aside from the forcing terms, the right-hand side represents a set of terms that dissipate both energy and enstrophy.
This is easily proven by noting that, for an appropriate set of boundary conditions, the dissipative terms on the right hand side are negative semi-definite operators.
Hence, the conservative part of the equation is simply the left hand side, the advection of the vorticity by the flow

\displaystyle{\partial_{t}w}+\vec{\nabla}\cdot w\vec{v}

\displaystyle={\partial_{t}w}+\vec{v}\cdot\vec{\nabla}w=0.

(12)

It is precisely the conservative discretization of the advection operator that will be investigated theoretically in Sec.
3.4
.

3
Conservative Methods for Arbitrary Polynomial Order

3.1
Overview of Results

Conservation of energy and enstrophy can each be posed as the preservation of a quadratic invariant in time, also known as a quadratic first integral.
Thus, it is critical to consider conservation of quantities of interest in both the discrete space and discrete time setting.
In this work, we explore numerical approaches that use the method of lines, i.e. the tensor product of a spatial finite element discretization and a temporal discretization based on a numerical time integration method.
This allows one to treat the spatially discretized system as a large set of ordinary differential equations (ODEs) and to choose the approaches to spatial and temporal discretization independently.

In the next section, we treat the temporal discretization by recalling the theorems of Chartier, Faou, and Murua (2006)
[
20
]
that prove that, for the majority of numerical integration methods used in practice, in order to exactly preserve a quadratic invariant, the method must either be a generally symplectic method or must be conjugate to a generally symplectic method (see
[
34
]
for a comprehensive overview).
The general symplecticity conditions
[
35
]
require a symplectic method to be fully implicit Runge-Kutta Gauss method.
The simplest methods that preserve quadratic invariants are the second order implicit midpoint method, which is symplectic, and the implicit trapezoidal method, which is conjugate to implicit midpoint.
We also discuss the ability of certain explicit and multistep schemes to approximately conserve invariants (
Section

3.3
), essentially because they
approximate symplectic methods such as implicit midpoint.

Then, in
Section

3.4
we treat the spatial discretization by proving that, in the continuous time setting, spatial
discretization with the appropriate choice of finite-element
spaces leads to an energy and enstrophy conserving method.
This leads to the result that using a symplectic time integration method for the resulting set of discretized ODEs yields the desired conservation laws.
Finally, for reference, Sec.
3.7
explicitly expresses these results in terms of the Poisson bracket, a form for the advection operator that is commonly used in the fluid dynamics and plasma physics literature.

3.2
Exactly Conservative Time Integrators: Fully Implicit Symplectic Methods

For time-dependent simulations, it is critical to consider conservation of
quantities of interest in both the discrete space and time setting.
In Sec.
3.4
, we prove that the finite element spatial discretization leads to a discrete system of ODEs that possess the quadratic invariants of energy and enstrophy.
Consider the general system of ODEs

\frac{d\boldsymbol{u}}{dt}=\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{u}).

A conserved quantity,
C(\boldsymbol{u},t)
, is constant in time, i.e. invariant under translations in time, if it satisfies
dC/dt=0
.
A quadratic form is defined by a
symmetric matrix
\mathbf{Q}
via

C=\tfrac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{u}^{T}\mathbf{Q}\boldsymbol{u}

and it defines a quadratic invariant if it satisfies

\frac{dC}{dt}=\boldsymbol{u}^{T}\mathbf{Q}\frac{d\boldsymbol{u}}{dt}=\boldsymbol{u}^{T}\mathbf{Q}\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{u})=0,

(13)

for all solutions
\boldsymbol{u}
in the domain of
\boldsymbol{f}
(see, e.g.,

[
36
]
); i.e. for all solutions
\boldsymbol{u}(t)
to the set of ODEs.
We will show that with careful construction of the FEM spaces and discretization,
energy and enstrophy conservation can both be expressed in the form
of (
13
).

The monograph of Hairer, Lubich, and Wanner
[
34
]
provides an excellent review of numerical integrators that conserve nonlinear invariants as well as of numerical integrators in general.
In fact, the construction of time integrators that conserve nonlinear invariants is one of the primary motivations for the development of geometric numerical integration.
In the following we highlight a few of the main points that are important for our conclusions.

The pioneering work of Butcher
[
37
]
and Hairer and Wanner
[
38
]
enabled the analysis
of many widely used time integration techniques, including Runge-Kutta (multi-stage) methods and multi-step methods, and led to the introduction of general linear methods.
Like the exact solution, the result of these methods can be expressed as a “B-series” defined as a formal power series over the set of elementary differentials.
Because the elementary differentials are indexed by the graphs of “rooted trees,” graph-theoretical methods can be brought to bear on the analysis
[
34
]
.
The results can also be extended to partitioned-series or “P-series” integration methods
[
34
]
that partition the system into two sets of variables,
\boldsymbol{u}=\left[\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{p}\right]^{T}
, where each part uses a different numerical integrator.
In this case, the elementary differentials can be indexed by trees with roots of two different colors.
Moreover, B-series and P-series form a group under composition, so the results can be extended to any composition of methods.
Thus, compositions of B-series and P-series methods contain a large portion of the numerical time integration techniques used in practice.

For all B-series time integrators, quadratic invariants are only
conserved if the integration method is a generally symplectic method
[
20
, Theorem 1]
:

Theorem 3.1

(Only symplectic integrators conserve quadratic invariants)
.

Any B-series time integrator that exactly conserves a quadratic invariant must be a generally symplectic integrator.

The general symplecticity conditions for B-series integrators
[
35
]
require the method to be a symplectic Runge-Kutta method (originally called “orbitally stable” Runge-Kutta methods by
[
36
]
).
Symplectic Runge-Kutta methods must be of the Gauss-Legendre (Gauss) type
[
39
,
40
,
41
]
.
The Gauss-Radau and Gauss-Lobatto Runge-Kutta methods fail to be symplectic due to their lack of symmetry in time.
Thus, the simplest symplectic method is the second-order implicit midpoint method.
Note, however, that no Runge-Kutta method can conserve all polynomial invariants for polynomial order
>2

[
34
]
.

It is clear that a spatial discretization will only preserve the conservation of a quadratic invariant under certain conditions, e.g. to be derived in
Section

3.4
. This immediately implies

Corollary 3.0.1
.

For B-series time integrators, only generally symplectic methods coupled with a
conservative spatial discretization are guaranteed to conserve
enstrophy and energy exactly.

Integration methods that are conjugate to symplectic method have the form
\Phi_{h}=\chi_{h}^{-1}\circ\Psi_{h}\circ\chi_{h}
, where
\Psi_{h}(\boldsymbol{u})
is a symplectic transformation for time step
h
, and
\chi_{h}(\boldsymbol{u})
is a near identity transformation of order
{\mathcal{O}}(h)
in a neighborhood of the initial condition. This form ensures that that application of multiple steps still leads to a result that is
{\mathcal{O}}(h)
close to the symplectic result, i.e.
\Phi_{nh}=(\Phi_{h}\circ)^{n}=\chi_{h}^{-1}\circ\Psi_{nh}\circ\chi_{h}
. Clearly, if an invariant is exactly conserved by the symplectic integrator,
\Psi_{h}\circ C=C
, then there is an approximate invariant,
C_{h}=\chi_{h}^{-1}\circ C
, that is exactly conserved,
\Phi_{h}\circ C_{h}=C_{h}
.
In fact, conjugate symplectic methods are the only methods have this property
[
20
, Theorem 4]
:

Theorem 3.2

(Only conjugate symplectic methods conserve approximate quadratic invariants)
.

Any B-series time integrator that exactly conserves an approximate quadratic invariant must be conjugate to a generally symplectic integrator.

The simplest and most practical conjugate symplectic method is the second order implicit trapezoidal integrator, a Gauss-Lobatto method that is conjugate to implicit midpoint.

The same line of reasoning regarding spatial discretization leads to:

Corollary 3.0.2
.

For B-series time integrators, only generally symplectic methods coupled with
a conservative spatial discretization are guaranteed to exactly conserve
invariants that represent enstrophy and energy.

Chartier, Faou, and Murua
[
20
]
also extend these two theorems to show that conservative P-series methods must either be symplectic or conjugate-symplectic.
However, proper P-series methods can only conserve quadratic forms that, like angular momentum, are linear in each of the partitioned variables, i.e. the invariant must be of the form of,
\boldsymbol{q}\mathbf{E}\boldsymbol{p}
with constant matrix
\mathbf{E}
.
For example, Gauss-Lobatto IIIA-IIIB pairs conserve all quadratic invariants of this form
[
42
]
.
However, any P-series method that conserves general quadratic invariants such as energy and enstrophy must actually be a B-series method
[
20
]
, and, hence, must be a Gauss-Legendre Runge-Kutta method.

Note that these methods must be fully implicit as opposed to diagonally implicit Runge-Kutta (DIRK) methods.
In order for an implicit method to provide exact conservation,
an exact solve of each of the stage equations is required.
Inexact solution methods such as iterative solvers can potentially degrade perfect conservation.
For example, one must take care to require sufficiently accurate
linear solves within each nonlinear stage to maintain good conservation properties.

Moreover, although Gauss and other fully implicit Runge-Kutta methods
can offer high accuracy in addition to good conservation properties,
historically, high-order methods have rarely been used in practice
for numerical PDEs due to the difficulty of solving the fully coupled
stage equations. However, recent theoretical and algorithmic
developments have made such integration tractable and even faster
than diagonally implicit Runge-Kutta (DIRK) methods at times
[
43
,
44
]
.

Finally, we note that, since the finite element method presents the equations in variational form, it should be of interest to explore variational integrators.
Variational integrators are typically partitioned partially implicit methods, but
in special cases, e.g. when the Hamiltonian is separable in kinetic and potential energy, variational methods can even be explicit.
Since the 2D Euler equations are a Lie-Poisson Hamiltonian system, with a degenerate Poisson bracket, specialized variational integrators must be used
[
45
,
46
,
47
]
.

3.3
Approximately Conservative Time Integrators: Explicit & Multistep Methods

As discussed above, Runge-Kutta schemes must be fully implicit in order
to conserve quadratic invariants.
Our numerical results in
Section

4
demonstrate
that, with effective solvers, implicit methods can notably outperform explicit
methods in terms of wall-clock time as well. However, in some instances, such as
problems with less restrictive time step constraints or software packages that
want to avoid the machinery of implicit integration, explicit integration may
still be desirable.

Numerical results show that explicit midpoint and other methods that approximate the
implicit midpoint method can still have relatively good approximate
conservation properties for quadratic invariants.
This may be the case because explicit integration tends to require very small time
steps for stable integration, and so these approximations may have relatively small error for each time step.
Due to the fact that the implicit trapezoidal method is conjugate to implicit midpoint, one would expect similar behavior for explicit methods that approximate implicit trapezoidal.
As yet another example, the GDB code
[
28
]
uses the explicit “trapezoidal leapfrog method”
[
48
,
49
]
, a second order accurate general linear method that stabilizes the leapfrog method by using an explicit trapezoidal predictor step to improve the estimate for the value at the midpoint.
In this case, the explicit trapezoidal predictor step approximates implicit trapezoidal, while the final result approximates implicit midpoint.
Our numerical results in
Section

4
demonstrate that both the explicit midpoint method and the trapezoidal leapfrog method have relatively good conservation properties.

A number of additional time-reversible multistep methods were identified by Ref.
[
50
]
that have good conservation properties, even though no multistep method can be generally symplectic
[
51
]
.
The two simplest methods are (i) the implicit trapezoidal method, which is conjugate to implicit midpoint, and (ii) the leap-frog method (which they referred to as explicit midpoint).
For the system of ODEs
d\boldsymbol{u}/dt=\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{u})
, the leapfrog multistep method
[
52
]
is simply

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{u}_{n-1}+\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n})2\delta t.

If the system of ODEs preserves the quadratic invariant
C=\tfrac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{u}^{T}(t)\mathbf{Q}\boldsymbol{u}(t)
, then the leapfrog method preserves the invariant

C=\tfrac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{u}_{n}^{T}\mathbf{Q}\boldsymbol{u}_{n+1}.

This is easy to show by using the symmetry of
\mathbf{Q}
to find
[
52
]

\boldsymbol{u}_{n}^{T}\mathbf{Q}\boldsymbol{u}_{n+1}-\boldsymbol{u}_{n}^{T}\mathbf{Q}\boldsymbol{u}_{n-1}=\boldsymbol{u}_{n}^{T}\mathbf{Q}\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n})\delta t=0.

The good conservation properties result from the fact that the underlying one-step method is symmetric in time, and, hence, by Theorem 1.1 of Ref.
[
53
]
, this one-step method is conjugate to a symplectic method.

It should be pointed out that the leapfrog method is known
to have nonlinear instabilities, as analyzed in
[
52
,
54
]

(observed as early as 1965
[
55
]
).
In fact, explicit general linear methods all have similar nonlinear
instabilities, analogous to the leapfrog method
[
56
]
.
They also generically suffer from linear instabilities, known as parasitic modes, unless, like leapfrog, the methods satisfy the “zero-growth” conditions
[
50
]
.
In fact, Ref.
[
50
]
identified all of the time-reversible multi-step time-integration of sixth order and below that satisfy the “zero-growth” conditions for parasitic modes.
For problems or software packages where explicit integration is desirable and nonlinear instabilities can be avoided, these zero-growth methods may have the
highest order accuracy and the best conservation properties.
Again, the simplest of these is the second-order leapfrog method and one could also consider adaptive or modified versions
of leapfrog as discussed in
[
57
]
.

3.4
Spatial Discretization: Arbitrary Order Finite Element Methods

In this section, we derive conservative finite element discretizations for Eqs.
(
9
).
In the next two subsections, we prove that the spatially discretized equations are conservative if the potential and vorticity are chosen from the appropriate finite element spaces.
This leads to the result that the combination of correctly chosen spatial and temporal discretization is conservative.

Recall that a finite element discretization presents a set of partial differential equations in integral form.
First let us establish some notation: let
\Omega
be the computational domain,
\partial\Omega
be the boundary of the domain,
and
\vec{n}_{\partial\Omega}
be the outward normal to the boundary.
Let
\tau_{h}
be a mesh covering
\Omega
, specified by finite element cells
K
, and their boundaries
\partial K
, as well as nodal interpolation points within each element.
Let
\Gamma
denote the union of the internal faces between elements of the mesh.
The mesh
\tau_{h}
also defines a uniquely oriented unit vector,
\vec{n}_{\Gamma}
, normal to every face of
\Gamma
.
A weak formulation allows the solutions and their partial derivatives to manifest discontinuities across
\Gamma
.
For the weak integral formulation of the partial differential equations, the effects of the discontinuities are carefully handled using various numerical strategies.

Definition 3.1

(Jump and average operators)
.

The jump and average operators are defined on the element interfaces
\Gamma
via

\displaystyle\left\llbracket\eta\right\rrbracket

\displaystyle=\eta^{+}-\eta^{-}

\displaystyle\left\{\eta\right\}

\displaystyle=(\eta^{+}+\eta^{-})/2

\displaystyle\mathrm{on}~\Gamma

(14)

where
\eta^{+}
and
\eta^{-}
are upwind and downwind values of

\eta\in L^{2}(\tau_{h})
with respect to
\vec{n}_{\Gamma}
.

Finite element exterior calculus crucially relies on the
the jump operator defined in Eq. (
14
), which
measures the lack of
continuity
, the essential property defining the spaces of discrete differential forms.
The space of discrete differential zero-forms,
H^{1}(\tau_{h})
, corresponds to the finite element space
of continuous piecewise polynomial scalar functions, defined by
\left\llbracket\psi\right\rrbracket=0
for
any function
\psi\in H^{1}(\tau_{h})
.
The notation
H^{1}_{p}(\tau_{h})
denotes continuous piecewise polynomial functions of order
\leq p
.
In contrast, the space of discrete differential volume-forms (three-forms),
L^{2}(\tau_{h})
, corresponds to the finite element space
of piecewise polynomial functions that are potentially discontinuous
\left\llbracket\eta\right\rrbracket\neq 0
across
\Gamma
, for
any function
\eta\in L^{2}(\tau_{h})
.
The notation
L^{2}_{p}(\tau_{h})
denotes piecewise polynomial functions of order
\leq p
that are potentially discontinuous across
\Gamma
.

The space of piecewise polynomial vector fields that are potentially discontinuous across
\Gamma
is denoted
\left[L^{2}(\tau_{h})\right]^{d}
for dimension
d=3
.
On the surface
\Gamma
, a vector field,

\vec{v}=\vec{v}_{n}+\vec{v}_{t}
, can be decomposed into components
normal,
\vec{v}_{n}=(\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n}_{\Gamma})\vec{n}_{\Gamma}
, and
tangential,
\vec{v}_{t}=\vec{v}-\vec{v}_{n}
, to
\Gamma
.
For a Cartesian mesh, the space of discrete differential one-forms corresponds to the finite element space,

H^{\rm Curl}(\tau_{h})
, of piecewise polynomial vector functions with a continuous tangential component,

\left\llbracket\vec{v}_{t}\right\rrbracket=0
, but a potentially discontinuous normal component across
\Gamma
.
Similarly, the space of discrete differential two-forms corresponds to the finite element space,

H^{\rm Div}(\tau_{h})
, of piecewise polynomial vector functions with a continuous normal component,

\left\llbracket\vec{v}_{n}\right\rrbracket=0
, but a potentially discontinuous tangential component across
\Gamma
.
In
[
58
,
59
,
60
]
it
was shown how arbitrary order differential forms can be constructed on general
non-Cartesian meshes. In this case,
the conditions on continuity require that the integral of the jump over each
face
e
of
\Gamma
must vanish, i.e.

\int_{e}\left\llbracket\vec{v}\right\rrbracket\cdot\vec{n}_{\Gamma}qdS=0
for any two-form

\vec{v}\in H^{\rm Div}(\tau_{h})
and any zero-form
q\in H^{1}(\tau_{h})
, and

\int_{e}\left\llbracket\vec{v}\right\rrbracket\cdot(\vec{n}_{\Gamma}\times\vec{q})dS=0

for any two one-forms
\vec{v},\vec{q}\in H^{\rm Curl}(\tau_{h})
. Note that
q
and

\vec{n}_{\Gamma}\times\vec{q}
are single valued on
e
.
Clearly, the space of discontinuous vector fields is the union of vector spaces
\left[L^{2}(\tau_{h})\right]^{d}~=~H^{\rm Curl}(\tau_{h})~\cup~H^{\rm Div}(\tau_{h})
.
This is the discrete Hodge decomposition theorem (also known as the Helmholtz decomposition in 3D).

For the problems of interest, where the flows are nearly incompressible, it will be assumed that
\vec{v}\in H^{\rm Div}(\tau_{h})
, so that the normal component
\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n}_{\Gamma}
across
\Gamma
must be continuous.

Lemma 3.1

(Divergence of the drift velocity)
.

If
\varphi\in H^{1}(\tau_{h})
and
{\hat{b}}/B\in H^{\rm Curl}(\tau_{h})
,
then the discrete approximation of the drift velocity (
9c
) implies that
\vec{v}\in H^{\rm Div}(\tau_{h})
.
Furthermore, when
\vec{\nabla}\times\frac{{\hat{b}}}{B}=\vec{0}
,

\vec{v}
is divergence-free at the discrete level:
\vec{\nabla}\cdot\vec{v}=0
.

Proof.

The FEEC relations imply that the usual theorems of vector calculus hold at the discrete level.
The assumption
\varphi\in H^{1}(\tau_{h})
implies
\vec{\nabla}\varphi\in H^{\rm Curl}(\tau_{h})
.
If
\vec{a},\vec{c}\in H^{\rm Curl}(\tau_{h})
, then
\vec{a}\times\vec{c}\in H^{\rm Div}(\tau_{h})
.
Hence, the assumptions imply that
\frac{{\hat{b}}}{B}\times\vec{\nabla}\varphi\in H^{\rm Div}(\tau_{h})
.
If
\vec{c}\in H^{\rm Curl}(\tau_{h})
, then
\vec{\nabla}\cdot\vec{\nabla}\times\vec{c}=0
.
Thus, the assumptions
\frac{{\hat{b}}}{B}\in H^{\rm Curl}(\tau_{h})
and
\varphi\in H^{1}(\tau_{h})
, imply that

\frac{{\hat{b}}}{B}\varphi\in H^{\rm Curl}(\tau_{h})
.
To prove the final relation, note that if
\vec{\nabla}\times\frac{{\hat{b}}}{B}=\vec{0}
,
then
\vec{v}=\vec{\nabla}\times\left(\frac{{\hat{b}}}{B}\varphi\right)
and, thus,

\vec{\nabla}\cdot\vec{v}=0
.
∎

Consistency with the condition

\vec{v}\in H^{\rm Div}(\tau_{h})
requires
\vec{\nabla}\phi\in H^{\rm Curl}(\tau_{h})
, hence,
the potential to be continuous
\varphi\in H^{1}(\tau_{h})
.
The weak formulation of the potential equation (
9b
)
in the finite element space of piecewise continuous polynomial functions of

\varphi\in H^{1}_{p}(\tau_{h})

\int_{\Omega}\frac{\rho}{B^{2}}\vec{\nabla}_{\perp}\psi\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{\perp}\varphi~dV=\int_{\Omega}\psi w~dV+\int_{\partial\Omega}\psi\frac{\rho}{B^{2}}\vec{\nabla}_{\perp}\varphi\cdot\vec{n}_{\partial\Omega}~dS,\hfill~\forall\psi\in H^{1}_{p}(\tau_{h}).

(15)

Similarly, the weak continuous formulation of polynomial
order
\tilde{p}
for the vorticity (
9a
),
w\in H^{1}_{\tilde{p}}(\tau_{h})

\int_{\Omega}\psi\left(\partial_{t}w+\vec{v}\cdot\vec{\nabla}w\right)~dV=\\
\int_{\Omega}\left(\psi S_{w}-\psi\alpha w-\vec{\nabla}\psi\cdot\overleftrightarrow{\mu}\cdot\vec{\nabla}w-\vec{\nabla}\psi\cdot\sigma_{\|}\vec{\nabla}_{\|}\varphi\right)~dV\\
+\int_{\partial\Omega}\psi\left(\overleftrightarrow{\mu}\cdot\vec{\nabla}w+\sigma_{\|}\vec{\nabla}_{\|}\varphi\right)\cdot\vec{n}_{\partial\Omega}~dS,\hskip 18.06749pt~\forall\psi\in H^{1}_{\tilde{p}}(\tau_{h}).

(16)

Next, we derive the weak discontinuous Galerkin (DG) finite element formulation of the vorticity.
In order to do so, let us remind the reader of a few important facts.
First, the jump of a product of functions

is

\displaystyle\left\llbracket ab\right\rrbracket=\left\{a\right\}\left\llbracket b\right\rrbracket+\left\llbracket a\right\rrbracket\left\{b\right\}.

(17)

This result follows upon substitution of the definitions (
14

Second, the divergence theorem for

a\in L^{2}(\tau_{h})
and
\vec{v}\in H^{\rm Div}(\tau_{h})
yields

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\vec{\nabla}\cdot(a\vec{v})~dV=\int_{\partial\Omega}a\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n}_{\partial\Omega}~dS+\int_{\Gamma}\left\llbracket a\right\rrbracket\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n}_{\Gamma}~dS.

(18)

This can be shown by using the divergence theorem on the finite element mesh

,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\vec{\nabla}\cdot(a\vec{v})~dV=\sum_{K\in\tau_{h}}\int_{\partial K}\vec{\nabla}\cdot(a\vec{v})~dV=\sum_{K\in\tau_{h}}\int_{\partial K}a\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n}_{\partial K}~dS.

When

\left\llbracket\vec{v}\right\rrbracket\cdot\vec{n}_{\Gamma}=0

\left\llbracket a\vec{v}\right\rrbracket\cdot\vec{n}_{\Gamma}=\left\llbracket a\right\rrbracket\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n}_{\Gamma}

and the sum of discontinuities can be expressed using the jump operator

\displaystyle\sum_{K\in\tau_{h}}\int_{\partial K}a\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n}_{\partial K}~dS=\int_{\Gamma}\left\llbracket a\right\rrbracket\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n}_{\Gamma}~dS+\int_{\partial\Omega}a\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n}_{\partial\Omega}~dS.

Combining the two equations above yields (

Finally, combining Eq. (

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\vec{\nabla}\cdot(ab\vec{v})~dV=\int_{\partial\Omega}ab\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n}_{\partial\Omega}~dS+\int_{\Gamma}\left(\left\llbracket a\right\rrbracket\left\{b\right\}+\left\{a\right\}\left\llbracket b\right\rrbracket\right)\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n}_{\Gamma}~dS.

The weak discontinuous formulation for the vorticity,

\Gamma

Thus, the weak DG formulation of polynomial order

\int_{\Omega}\eta\left(\partial_{t}w+\vec{v}\cdot\vec{\nabla}w\right)~dV-\int_{\Gamma}\left[\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n}_{\Gamma}\left\{\eta\right\}\left\llbracket w\right\rrbracket-\delta\left|\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n}_{\Gamma}\right|\left\llbracket\eta\right\rrbracket\left\llbracket w\right\rrbracket\right]~dS=\\
\int_{\Omega}\left(\eta S_{w}-\eta\alpha w-\vec{\nabla}\eta\cdot\overleftrightarrow{\mu}\cdot\vec{\nabla}w-\vec{\nabla}\eta\cdot\sigma_{\|}\vec{\nabla}_{\|}\varphi\right)~dV\\
+\int_{\partial\Omega}\eta\left(\overleftrightarrow{\mu}\cdot\vec{\nabla}w+\sigma_{\|}\vec{\nabla}_{\|}\varphi\right)\cdot\vec{n}_{\partial\Omega}~dS\\
+\int_{\Gamma}\left[\left\llbracket\eta\right\rrbracket\vec{n}_{\Gamma}\cdot\overleftrightarrow{\mu}\cdot\left\{\vec{\nabla}w\right\}+\left\{\vec{\nabla}\eta\right\}\cdot\overleftrightarrow{\mu}\cdot\vec{n}_{\Gamma}\left\llbracket w\right\rrbracket-\frac{C}{h}\left\llbracket\eta\right\rrbracket\cdot\overleftrightarrow{\mu}\cdot\left\llbracket w\right\rrbracket\right]~dS\\
+\int_{\Gamma}\left\llbracket\eta\right\rrbracket\vec{n}_{\Gamma}\cdot\sigma_{\|}\left\{\vec{\nabla}_{\|}\varphi\right\}~dS,\hskip 18.06749pt\forall\eta\in L^{2}_{\tilde{p}}(\tau_{h}).

Here, we use the standard DG formulation of advection

3.5
Enstrophy Conservation

Solving either Eq. (
16
) or Eq. (
20
) with a symplectic integrator will conserve enstrophy for an appropriate set of assumptions.

Lemma 3.2

(Enstrophy as a discrete quadratic form)
.

Let the vorticity
w
be approximated within a finite-dimensional function space
\mathcal{V}
, e.g. either
L^{2}_{\tilde{p}}(\tau_{h})
or
H^{1}_{\tilde{p}}(\tau_{h})
, spanned by a set of basis functions
\boldsymbol{\psi}
, so that one can express the vorticity field as
w=\boldsymbol{\psi}^{T}\boldsymbol{w}
, where
\boldsymbol{w}
represent the coefficients of the finite element representation.
Then, the enstrophy is a quadratic form

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}w^{2}~dV=\tfrac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{w}^{T}\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{w}

(21)

where the mass matrix
\mathbf{M}
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\psi}\boldsymbol{\psi}^{T}~dV.

(22)

Proof.

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}w^{2}~dV=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\psi}^{T}\boldsymbol{w}\boldsymbol{\psi}^{T}\boldsymbol{w}~dV=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{w}^{T}\boldsymbol{\psi}\boldsymbol{\psi}^{T}\boldsymbol{w}=\boldsymbol{w}^{T}\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{w}.

∎

Theorem 3.3

(Semi-discrete enstrophy conservation for continuous time)
.

Let the velocity
\vec{v}\in H^{\rm Div}(\tau_{h})
and assume
(i) either
w\in H^{1}_{\tilde{p}}(\tau_{h})
in (
16
) or

w\in L^{2}_{\tilde{p}}(\tau_{h})
in (
20
) without upwinding
(
\delta=0
),
(ii)
\vec{\nabla}\cdot\vec{v}=0
in
\Omega
, and
(iii) the weak form satisfies
\int_{\partial\Omega}~w^{2}~\vec{v}\cdot~\vec{n}_{\partial\Omega}~dS~=~0
.
Then, the spatially discretized vorticity ODE, which are continuous in time, conserve enstrophy

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}w^{2}~dV=0.

Note that the condition
\int_{\partial\Omega}w^{2}\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n}_{\partial\Omega}~dS=0
is met by periodic or homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions (or a mixture thereof) on
w
and
\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n}_{\partial\Omega}
.

Proof.

Because the expression for
w
in (
20
) with
\delta=0
holds for all
\eta\in L^{2}_{\tilde{p}}(\tau_{h})
and
w\in L^{2}_{\tilde{p}}(\tau_{h})
, the expression holds for
\eta=w
:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}w\partial_{t}w~dV=-\int_{\Omega}w\vec{v}\cdot\vec{\nabla}w~dV+\int_{\Gamma}\left\{w\right\}\left\llbracket w\right\rrbracket\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n}_{\Gamma}~dS.

Noting that
\left\{w\right\}\left\llbracket w\right\rrbracket=\frac{1}{2}\left\llbracket w^{2}\right\rrbracket
from (
17
)
and letting
a=w^{2}
in (
18
), the expression above
yields

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}w^{2}~dV=\int_{\Omega}w^{2}\vec{\nabla}\cdot\vec{v}~dV-\int_{\partial\Omega}w^{2}\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n}_{\partial\Omega}~dS.

The assumptions
\vec{\nabla}\cdot\vec{v}=0
in
\Omega
and
\int_{\partial\Omega}w^{2}\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n}_{\partial\Omega}~dS=0
then yield

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}w^{2}~dV=\int_{\Omega}w\partial_{t}w~dV=0,

which completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 3.4

(Fully discrete enstrophy conservation)
.

Let the velocity
\vec{v}\in H^{\rm Div}(\tau_{h})
and assume
(i) either
w\in H^{1}_{\tilde{p}}(\tau_{h})
in (
16
) or

w\in L^{2}_{\tilde{p}}(\tau_{h})
in (

) without upwinding (

),
(ii)
\vec{\nabla}\cdot\vec{v}=0
in
\Omega
, and
(iii) periodic or homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions (or a mixture thereof). Then, if

Proof.

Because
w\in L^{2}_{\tilde{p}}(\tau_{h})
,
Lemma

3.2
implies that
the enstrophy is equal to a quadratic form on

.
Using (i) the weak formulations (

) or
(

), in conjunction with conditions (ii)-(iii),

implies that the spatially discretized form
of the enstrophy is conserved in continuous time.
If a symplectic integrator is used to integrate the resulting vorticity ODE, then

implies that the enstrophy will be conserved in discrete time.
∎

3.6
Energy Conservation

Integrating the discretized equations (
15
) with a symplectic integrator also conserves total kinetic energy for an appropriate set of assumptions. In particular, the polynomial order of the finite element spaces for potential and vorticity must be consistent with one another.

Lemma 3.3

(Energy as a discrete quadratic form for the potential)
.

Let the potential
\varphi
be approximated as an element of a finite-dimensional function space
\mathcal{V}
, e.g. either
H^{1}_{\tilde{p}}(\tau_{h})
or
L^{2}_{\tilde{p}}(\tau_{h})
, and let
\boldsymbol{\psi}
be a vector of basis functions for this function space, so that the potential can be expressed as
\varphi=\boldsymbol{\psi}^{T}\boldsymbol{\varphi}
.
Then, the energy is equivalent to a quadratic form:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{\rho}{B^{2}}\vec{\nabla}_{\perp}\varphi\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{\perp}\varphi~dV=\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{T}\mathbf{L}\boldsymbol{\varphi},

where
\mathbf{L}
is the symmetric positive semi-definite “diffusion” matrix defined via

\displaystyle L_{ij}=\int_{\Omega}\frac{\rho}{B^{2}}\vec{\nabla}_{\perp}\psi_{i}\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{\perp}\psi_{j}~dV.

(23)

Proof.

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{\rho}{B^{2}}\vec{\nabla}_{\perp}\varphi\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{\perp}\varphi~dV

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\frac{\rho}{B^{2}}\vec{\nabla}_{\perp}\left(\sum_{i}\varphi_{i}\psi_{i}\right)\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{\perp}\left(\sum_{j}\varphi_{j}\psi_{j}\right)~dV

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\sum_{j}\varphi_{i}\varphi_{j}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\rho}{B^{2}}\vec{\nabla}_{\perp}\psi_{i}\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{\perp}\psi_{j}~dV=\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{T}\mathbf{L}\boldsymbol{\varphi}

∎

Corollary 3.3.1

(Energy as a discrete quadratic form for the vorticity)
.

Suppose the potential approximation
\varphi=\boldsymbol{\psi}^{T}\boldsymbol{\varphi}
and vorticity approximation
w=\boldsymbol{\eta}^{T}\boldsymbol{w}
satisfy the weak formulation (
15
) with a set of boundary conditions that make
\varphi
unique, or equivalently that

\displaystyle\mathbf{L}\boldsymbol{\varphi}=\mathbf{N}\boldsymbol{w}

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{N}=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\psi}\boldsymbol{\eta}^{T}~dV

and
\mathbf{L}
the symmetric positive definite matrix in Eq. (
23
) defined by the the choice of
\mathcal{V}
.
Then energy can be expressed as a quadratic form for the vorticity:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{T}\mathbf{L}\boldsymbol{\varphi}=\tfrac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{w}^{T}\mathbf{N}^{T}\mathbf{L}^{-1}\mathbf{N}\boldsymbol{w}.

Theorem 3.5

(Semi-discrete energy conservation for continuous time)
.

Let the potential
\varphi\in H^{1}_{p}(\tau_{h})
determined by (
15
)
and velocity
\vec{v}\in H^{\rm Div}(\tau_{h})

determined by (
9c
), and assume
(i) either
w\in H^{1}_{\tilde{p}}(\tau_{h})
in (
16
) or

w\in L^{2}_{\tilde{p}}(\tau_{h})
in (
20
),
where
\tilde{p}\geq p
,
(ii)
\vec{\nabla}\cdot\vec{v}=0
in
\Omega
,
(iii)
\int_{\partial\Omega}w\varphi\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n}_{\partial\Omega}~dS=0
,
(iv)
\int_{\partial\Omega}\varphi\vec{\nabla}_{\perp}\varphi\cdot\vec{n}_{\partial\Omega}~dS=0
, and
(v)
\int_{\partial\Omega}\partial_{t}\varphi\vec{\nabla}_{\perp}\varphi\cdot\vec{n}_{\partial\Omega}~dS=0
.
Then, the spatially discretized ODEs, which are continuous in time,
conserve energy,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\rho}{B^{2}}\vec{\nabla}_{\perp}\varphi\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{\perp}\varphi~dV=0.

Note that the conditions
\int_{\partial\Omega}w\varphi\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n}_{\partial\Omega}~dS=0
,
\int_{\partial\Omega}\varphi\vec{\nabla}_{\perp}\varphi\cdot\vec{n}_{\partial\Omega}~dS=0
, and
\int_{\partial\Omega}\partial_{t}\varphi\vec{\nabla}_{\perp}\varphi\cdot\vec{n}_{\partial\Omega}~dS=0
are met by periodic or homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions (or a mixture thereof) on
w
and
\varphi
.

Proof.

Using (
15
) with the test function
\psi=\varphi\in H^{1}_{p}(\tau_{h})
along with (iv)
\int_{\partial\Omega}\varphi\vec{\nabla}_{\perp}\varphi\cdot\vec{n}_{\partial\Omega}~dS=0
implies

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{\rho}{B^{2}}\vec{\nabla}_{\perp}\varphi\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{\perp}\varphi~dV=\int_{\Omega}w\varphi~dV.

The time derivative of this expression is

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\rho}{B^{2}}\vec{\nabla}_{\perp}\varphi\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{\perp}\varphi~dV=\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}w\varphi~dV=\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}\left(\varphi w\right)~dV.

Using (
15
) with the test function
\psi=\partial_{t}\varphi\in H^{1}_{p}(\tau_{h})
along with (v)
\int_{\partial\Omega}\partial_{t}\varphi\vec{\nabla}_{\perp}\varphi\cdot\vec{n}_{\partial\Omega}~dS=0

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\rho}{B^{2}}\vec{\nabla}_{\perp}\varphi\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{\perp}\varphi~dV=\int_{\Omega}w\partial_{t}\varphi~dV,

because
\int_{\partial\Omega}\partial_{t}\varphi\vec{\nabla}_{\perp}\varphi\cdot\vec{n}_{\partial\Omega}~dS=0
by assumption.
Subtracting the two results yields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\rho}{B^{2}}\vec{\nabla}_{\perp}\varphi\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{\perp}\varphi~dV=\int_{\Omega}\varphi\partial_{t}w~dV.

The DG formulation for vorticity (
20
) holds for
any

and
\varphi\in H^{1}_{p}(\tau_{h})
. Crucially, since

, as long as
\tilde{p}\geq p
, then the following

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\varphi\partial_{t}w~dV=-\int_{\Omega}\varphi\vec{v}\cdot\vec{\nabla}w~dV+\int_{\Gamma}\left\{\varphi\right\}\left\llbracket w\right\rrbracket\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n}_{\Gamma}~dS-\delta\int_{\Gamma}|\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n}_{\Gamma}|\left\llbracket w\right\rrbracket\left\llbracket\varphi\right\rrbracket~dS.

Note that because of the continuity of
\varphi\in H^{1}_{p}(\tau_{h})
,
one has
\left\llbracket\varphi\right\rrbracket=0
, and the upwinding term vanishes for any

\delta
. Choosing

a=\varphi w
in (
18
) and
a=\varphi,b=w
in

17
, and without the jump terms involving

\left\llbracket\varphi\right\rrbracket
, the above expression simplifies to

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\varphi\partial_{t}w~dV=\int_{\Omega}w\vec{v}\cdot\vec{\nabla}\varphi~dV+\int_{\Omega}\varphi w\vec{\nabla}\cdot\vec{v}~dV-\int_{\partial\Omega}\varphi w\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n}_{\partial\Omega}~dS.

The continuous formulation in (
16

, leads to the same result, since the jumps,
\left\llbracket w\right\rrbracket

H^{1}_{p}(\tau_{h})\subset H^{1}_{\tilde{p}}(\tau_{h})
, which simply requires
\tilde{p}\geq p
.

Using assumptions (ii)
\vec{\nabla}\cdot\vec{v}=0
and (iii)

\int_{\partial\Omega}w\varphi\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n}_{\partial\Omega}~dS=0
, and noting that (
9c
) implies that
\vec{v}\cdot\vec{\nabla}\phi=0
completes the proof:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\rho}{B^{2}}\vec{\nabla}_{\perp}\varphi\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{\perp}\varphi~dV=\int_{\Omega}\varphi\partial_{t}w~dV=0.

∎

(Fully discrete energy conservation)
.

Let the potential

\varphi

3.7
Poisson Bracket Formulation

The results of the previous sections can also be naturally stated in a manner that expresses the advection operator as a Poisson bracket.
This is useful for connecting with the extensive theoretical and numerical literature that utilizes the Poisson bracket to express the advection operator.
This subsection shows that these theorems explicitly hold true for the Poisson bracket formulation.

The particular case of constant
\vec{B}=B_{0}\hat{z}
for constant
B_{0}

(25)

In order to give the Poisson bracket form of the conservation laws, simply use
(

) with appropriate choices of
test functions
\eta
to find:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}w~dV

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}\left\{\left\{\varphi,w\right\}\right\}~dV/B_{0}+\int_{\Gamma}\left\llbracket w\right\rrbracket\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n}_{\Gamma}~dS,

(26)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\varphi\partial_{t}w~dV

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}\left\{\left\{\tfrac{1}{2}\varphi^{2},w\right\}\right\}~dV/B_{0}+\int_{\Gamma}\left\llbracket\varphi w\right\rrbracket\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n}_{\Gamma}~dS,

(27)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}w^{2}~dV

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}\left\{\left\{\varphi,w^{2}\right\}\right\}~dV/B_{0}+\int_{\Gamma}\left\llbracket w^{2}\right\rrbracket\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n}_{\Gamma}~dS-2\delta\int_{\Gamma}\left\llbracket w\right\rrbracket^{2}\left|\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n}_{\Gamma}\right|~dS.

(28)

4.1
Kelvin-Helmholtz Instability

The Kelvin-Helmholtz (KH) instability is a well-known fluid instability that occurs when nontrivial shear is present in the velocity field. This instability begins with the linear growth of unstable modes, then transitions to a non-linear stage of turbulence, and concludes with turbulence decay. To simulate the KH instability, Eq. (
6
) with
\alpha=S_{w}=0
is solved using the FEEC formulation detailed in
Section

3
. A conjugate gradient linear solver with the BoomerAMG preconditioner is used for (
15
),
a GMRES linear solver with AIR preconditioner
[
63
,
64
]

is used for (
16
), and a GMRES linear solver with either ILU
preconditioner is used for (
20
).
Piccard iteration is used as nonlinear solver. Results from the linear and
nonlinear phases are presented in the two subsections below.

4.1.1
Geometry and initial conditions

The physical domain used for the KH instability is a 2D rectangular slab with lengths
L_{x}
and
L_{y}
in the
x
and
y
directions, respectively. The fluid flows in the
y
direction with a sinusoidal profile given by the following

\displaystyle\varphi_{0}

\displaystyle{=}\sin(mk_{x0}x)/m^{2},

\displaystyle\vec{v}_{\perp,0}

\displaystyle{=}V_{0}\cos(mk_{x0}x)\hat{y},

(29)

\displaystyle w_{0}

\displaystyle{=}-k_{x0}^{2}\sin(mk_{x0}x),

where
k_{x0}=2\pi/L_{x}
and
V_{0}=k_{x0}/m
. To induce the instability, a perturbation is added to the initial conditions. For example, the full initial vorticity is given by

w(t=0)=w_{0}+\tilde{w}_{0}
, where
\tilde{w}_{0}
is a perturbation defined by

\displaystyle\tilde{w}_{0}=\epsilon~w_{0}\sum_{n=1}^{n=N}e^{-(nk_{y0}/N)^{2}}\sin{(nk_{y0}y)}.

(30)

In the above,
\epsilon\in[10^{-8},10^{-2}]
and
k_{y0}=2\pi/L_{y}

k_{y0}{\leq}mk_{x0}

L_{y}>mL_{x}

4.1.2
Verification of the linear growth rate

Verification of the numerical implementation of the governing equations is performed by comparing the computed linear growth rates against quasi-analytical predictions from linear stability analysis. That is, assume
\varphi=\varphi_{0}+\varphi_{1}
and
w=w_{0}+w_{1}
, where
\varphi_{0}
and
w_{0}
are the equilibrium solutions and
\varphi_{1}
and
w_{1}
are the perturbed components given by

\varphi_{1}=\varphi_{1}(x)e^{\gamma t+ik_{y}y},

(31)

w_{1}=(\varphi_{1}^{\prime\prime}-k_{y}^{2}\varphi_{1})e^{\gamma t+ik_{y}y}.

(32)

The vorticity equation can be linearized by neglecting high-order nonlinear terms, which results in the 1D eigenvalue equation

\gamma(\varphi_{1}^{\prime\prime}-k_{y}^{2}\varphi_{1})=k_{y}[\varphi_{0}^{\prime}(\varphi_{1}^{\prime\prime}-k_{y}^{2}\varphi_{1})-\varphi_{0}^{\prime\prime\prime}\varphi_{1}],

(33)

where
\varphi^{\prime}=d\varphi/dx
. After specifying the boundary conditions for the
x
direction, one obtains a 1D eigenvalue problem that can be solved numerically.
The numerically simulated linear growth rates are calculated from the finite element simulation by first performing a spatial Fourier transform to determine
\varphi_{k}

\gamma_{\rm sim}=\frac{1}{2\Delta t}\ln\left(\frac{|\varphi_{k}|_{t+\Delta t}}{|\varphi_{k}|_{t-\Delta t}}\right)

for each Fourier mode.

For this test case we choose
m=1,L_{x}=1
, and
L_{y}=16

32\times 512

The high-

,

\Delta m=5
and:

varying resolution for

and

;

2.

varying polynomial order

but keep roughly the same DOFs on
fpot
with
\mu=10^{-8}
;

3.

applying upwinding level
\delta
on a
512\times 512
grid and
\mu=0
,
p=3
.

Figures
11
,
12
and
13
plot the corresponding test results, and the averaged
\left<{\mathcal{E}}\right>
and
\left<{\mathcal{S}}\right>

.

As shown in Figure

, increasing resolution from
256\times 256
to

has little influence on
\left<{\mathcal{E}}\right>
(decreasing less than

\left<{\mathcal{S}}\right>

As a result, not only has the high-

The polynomial order

can also impact the high-

p=2
to
p=4

Finally, upwinding is extremely effective at preventing the high-
k
pile-up.
For a simulation that is poorly resolved, it would likely be prudent to use the maximum upwinding parameter
\delta=1/2
, as is customary in the finite element community.
For these simulations, which are moderately under-resolved, it is interesting to explore the effect of the choice of
\delta
on the results.
In the inviscid
\mu=0

\delta=0

\left<{\mathcal{S}}\right>

183.2

For the forced turbulence model, upwinding mostly affects the high

\delta

As high-

\left<{\mathcal{E}}\right>

\delta

1/2

Notably, for this model, there is little change in the spectrum as
\delta

Another interesting feature of upwinding is that the computational cost reduces as the upwinding factor
\delta
increases. This is because smoother solutions can be reached with fewer iterations, i.e,. easier to converge.

5
Conclusion

In conclusion, a finite element exterior calculus (FEEC) approach to the two-dimensional (2D) incompressible Navier-Stokes equations and to drift-reduced magnetohydrdynamics is introduced that conserves the two well-known quadratic invariants, energy and enstrophy, for arbitrary polynomial order.
Because the theorems of vector calculus are satisfied at the discrete level within the FEEC framework, the spatial discretization automatically conserves both energy and enstrophy as long as the proper finite element spaces are chosen for the primary variables.
A conservative scheme also requires an appropriate choice of numerical time integrator, and, for B-series time integrators, only generally symplectic time integrators, such as the fully implicit midpoint method are conservative.

Various combinations of numerical methods were tested for an extensive suite of 2D neutral fluid turbulence test problems.
Linear results were verified against a semi-analytical numerical eigensolver, and nonlinear results were verified against theory as well as to the Global Drift Ballooning (GDB) code
[
28
]
.
In addition to implicit midpoint, it was found that certain explicit methods, such as the explicit midpoint method and the explicit trapezoidal leapfrog method, which approximate implicit midpoint, still do relatively well at approximately conserving energy and enstrophy.
These observations may be due to the relatively small time step sizes that must be taken for the explicit methods to remain stable.
However, it was also found that turbulent simulations that conserve both energy and enstrophy tend to result in too much power at high wavenumber.

The high wavenumber part of the spectrum can be controlled by reintroducing artificial dissipation, e.g. through numerical viscosity or through an asymmetric implicit time integrator, such as backward Euler.
Perhaps the most interesting option is to use an upwinded DG formulation of the advection operator which dissipates enstrophy while still conserving energy exactly.
This formulation makes the best compromise of allowing mid-range wavenumbers to reach the appropriate amplitude, while still controlling the high-wavenumber part of the spectrum.
Thus, our conclusion is that using the upwinded DG formulation in conjunction with the symplectic implicit midpoint method appears to offer the greatest advantages in terms of accuracy and efficiency.
