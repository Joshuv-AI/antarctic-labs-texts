Can Generative Models Improve Self-Supervised Representation Learning?
Arash Afkanpour, Vahid Reza Khazaie, Sana Ayromlou, Fereshteh Forghani

Abstract
The rapid advancement in self-supervised learning (SSL) has highlighted its potential to leverage unlabeled data for learning powerful visual representations. However, existing SSL approaches, particularly those employing different views of the same image, often rely on a limited set of predefined data augmentations. This constrains the diversity and quality of transformations, which leads to sub-optimal representations. In this paper, we introduce a novel framework that enriches the SSL paradigm by utilizing generative models to produce semantically consistent image augmentations. By directly conditioning generative models on a source image representation, our method enables the generation of diverse augmentations while maintaining the semantics of the source image, thus offering a richer set of data for self-supervised learning. Our experimental results demonstrate that our framework significantly enhances the quality of learned visual representations. This research demonstrates that incorporating generative models into the SSL workflow opens new avenues for exploring the potential of unlabeled visual data. This development paves the way for more robust and versatile representation learning techniques.

Introduction

Self-supervised learning (SSL) is a machine learning approach where methods create labels from the input data, facilitating learning without explicit human-annotated labels. In the absence of any prior knowledge, most SSL methods assume that each data point is semantically different from other examples in a dataset. While this assumption can help devise a self-supervised representation learning algorithm, it is not sufficient to guarantee the generalization of representations to new tasks. As a result,
joint embedding
SSL methods rely on augmentation techniques (e.g.,
[
13
,
25
,
23
]
). These augmentations typically consist of a series of transformations, such as random crop, color jitter, or Gaussian blur, as shown in Fig.

(a), which are used to define
positive labels
for the SSL task. In a nutshell, these augmentations determine pixel-space changes that should result in similar embedding-space representations. Since these transformations specify what representations learn, a natural question is whether additional transformations improve the generalization and robustness of representations.

While previous works have studied the impact of augmentations in representation learning
[
13
,
10
]
until recently, this area has remained largely under-explored. More recently, new image transformations have been introduced in the context of self-supervised learning. For example,
[
37
]
introduced random field transformations by applying local affine and color transformations whose parameters are specified by Gaussian random fields
[
1
]
. The authors of
[
43
]
consider style transfer as a form of transformation for SSL. While these works show that new transformations can improve representation learning, their transformations remain limited to certain predefined forms.

In this paper, we propose a generalized transformation, providing a new image that (1) is semantically similar to the original image, and (2) follows the same underlying distribution of the original data rather than modifying the contents of the image in pixel space. Thus, two conceptually similar images could be considered as each other’s transformation for SSL. The question arises: given a source image, how can one generate augmentations that fit these criteria? For this task, we leverage instance-conditioned generative models. In particular, we study conditional generative adversarial networks (GAN)
[
12
]
and latent diffusion models
[
44
]
that generate an output that is semantically similar to a source image. Empirical results in in-distribution and out-of-distribution cases demonstrate the effectiveness of our method for representation learning.

The key contributions of our work are as follows:

•

We introduce a novel framework that leverages instance-conditioned generative models for generating semantically similar augmentations in self-supervised learning, advancing beyond traditional augmentation techniques.

•

Our empirical results demonstrate the effectiveness of generative models to enhance the generalization and robustness of representations.

•

By relying on instance-conditioned image generation, we eliminate the need for text-based image generation, as suggested in previous work. This enables the application of our method to datasets without a textual description, resulting in a more versatile and effective strategy for SSL training.

Related Work

2.1
Self-Supervised Learning

Self-supervised learning (SSL) leverages unlabeled data to create rich and generalizable representations. This approach offers numerous advantages due to its ability to train on extensive unlabeled data
[
4
]
. Moreover, SSL methods can learn representations that are more resilient to adversarial examples, label corruption, and input perturbations, as well as more equitable compared to their supervised counterparts
[
26
,
22
]
. In SSL for computer vision, a line of study known as
joint embedding
methods encourages two views of the same image, formed by augmentations such as crop or color jitter, to be mapped to similar representations. This approach enables models to learn to differentiate the representation of each image, leading to decent linear separability. These methods, based on their objective functions or architectures, can be categorized as follows
[
4
]
:

Deep metric learning methods
originate from the concept of contrastive loss in an unsupervised manner, which pulls representations of different transformed versions of an image together while repelling representations of different images apart. For instance, MoCo
[
25
]
learns an encoder by matching an encoded query to a dictionary of encoded keys using contrastive loss. SimCLR
[
9
]
is another method that uses the examples in each batch to calculate the contrastive loss.

Self-distillation methods
rely on a straightforward mechanism: feeding two different views to two encoders and mapping one to the other using a predictor. Various techniques are employed to prevent representation collapse, which is when an encoder predicts a constant for any input. A common approach to prevent collapse is to update one of the two encoder weights with a moving average of the other encoder’s weights. BYOL
[
10
]
first introduced self-distillation as a means to avoid collapse. SimSiam
[
14
]
replaces the BYOL moving average encoder with a stop-gradient mechanism. DINO
[
11
]
is another method that extends BYOL and SimSiam to discrete representations, still relying on a momentum encoder. DINOv2
[
40
]
and iBot
[
56
]
build upon DINO by combining its objective with a latent space masked-image modeling one.

Canonical correlation analysis methods
originate from the Canonical Correlation Framework (CCA)
[
29
]
. The high-level goal of CCA is to infer the relationship between two variables by analyzing the covariance matrix. VICReg
[
6
]
, the most recent among these methods, combines variance, invariance, and covariance loss terms to learn representations from unlabeled data. Barlow Twins
[
53
]
is another method that uses cross-correlation of the embedding features to achieve self-supervised learning goals. SwAV
[
51
]
uses a combination of clustering and contrastive loss to learn a similar representation for different views of an image.

Another category of self-supervised learning methods, known as
masked image modeling methods
, learns representations by predicting masked patches of an image or representation, motivated by masked language modeling in NLP
[
17
]
. Such works include masked autoencoders (MAE)
[
24
]
, SimMIM
[
52
]
, and MSN
[
2
]
, which aim to directly reconstruct masked image patches rather than discrete image tokens extracted from an encoder.

2.2
Generative methods

Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)

[
21
]
have significantly advanced the field of image generation. They consist of a generator and a discriminator competing against each other. The generator aims to create images that are indistinguishable from real images, while the discriminator strives to differentiate between the generator’s output and real images. There have been many variations in the field since its proposal
[
30
,
39
]
. Recently, there have been some works on conditioning GANs over a feature vector
[
36
,
12
]
. In this work, we chose instance-conditioned GANs (ICGAN)
[
12
]
, which innovatively extends this framework by conditioning the generation and discrimination processes on specific samples within the dataset. ICGAN aims to model the data distribution as a mixture of local densities around each instance, utilizing representations of these instances as additional input to both the generator and discriminator. This conditioning allows the model to generate images that are semantically similar to a given instance. Such an approach addresses the optimization difficulties and mode collapse issues prevalent in traditional GANs by ensuring a more focused generation process. This method can be leveraged to create semantically consistent augmentations for SSL.

Diffusion models
, a class of likelihood-based generative models, have gained attention for their ability to generate high-quality images
[
18
,
27
,
38
]
. They work by gradually reducing noise from a signal to generate samples, with their training objective expressed as a re-weighted variational lower-bound
[
27
]
. Despite their ability to generate high-quality images, diffusion models have traditionally faced challenges of long training and inference times. While inference challenges can be mitigated with advanced sampling strategies
[
46
,
48
]
and hierarchical approaches
[
28
]
, training on high-resolution image data remains computationally expensive due to the need to calculate gradients. To address this, stable latent diffusion models
[
44
]
have been introduced. These models operate on a latent space with lower dimensionality than the input space, making training computationally cheaper, and speeding up inference with almost no reduction in synthesis quality. They also introduced cross-attention layers into the model architecture, where various conditioning inputs such as text, images, or representations can be applied to get conditional output. Given their ability to generate high-quality diverse samples, diffusion models stand out as a compelling option to increase the efficacy of SSL methods.

With the advance of such generative models, the use of synthetic data has become a tool for training better models in various domains. In computer vision, synthetic data has been utilized to enhance model performance in tasks such as object detection
[
34
]
, semantic segmentation
[
15
,
45
]
, and classification
[
3
,
47
]
. In representation learning, synthetic data has been leveraged for multi-task learning
[
42
]
, and manipulating latent data representation
[
35
,
5
,
31
]
. The authors of
[
31
]
investigate learning visual representations from synthetic data generated by black-box models, emphasizing the ability of latent space transformations to facilitate contrastive learning and improve performance through careful sampling and training methods. It highlights generative models as a compact alternative to traditional datasets, predicting a shift towards reliance on model-generated data. The works of
[
19
,
20
]
introduce Bidirectional Generative Adversarial Networks (BiGAN) and BigBiGAN, which incorporate an inverse mapping mechanism within the GAN framework to enable both forward and inverse mappings between latent space and data. This allows for capturing semantic variations more effectively and making the representations valuable for auxiliary tasks.
More recently, StableRep
[
49
]
showed how visual representations learned from synthetic text-to-image data can be more effective than representations learned by using only real images in various downstream tasks.

3
Generative Self-Supervised Learning

Joint embedding SSL methods create two or more views by applying a series of transformations to an image. These transformations specify what pixel-space changes should keep the semantics of the views fairly unchanged.

More formally, given a batch of images,
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\}
, joint embedding SSL methods obtain the views:

\displaystyle v_{ij}=A_{j}(x_{i}),\hskip 8.53581ptj\in\{1,2\},\hskip 8.53581pti\in\{1,\ldots,N\},

where

\displaystyle A_{j}=T_{1}^{\theta_{1}}\circ\ldots\circ T_{K}^{\theta_{K}},

is a series of
K
transformations where
T_{i}^{\theta_{i}}
is parameterized by
\theta_{i}
. For example, a crop transformation could be parameterized by crop area, aspect ratio, etc.
While the parameterization of transformations increases the versatility of the views, transformation types are often limited to only a few. For example, previous SSL methods apply transformations such as random crop, color jitter, horizontal flip, grayscale (color removal), and Gaussian blur. Adding more transformations will create more diversity in the output of the augmentation pipeline, which could potentially improve the generalization of learned representations.

Figure 2
:

instance guidance
component is pretrained and does not change during SSL training.

Another shortcoming of these transformations is that they may not fully capture the inherent diversity present in real-world data, and could potentially distort the semantics of the input as shown in Fig.
1
. For instance, the color jitter transformation could result in color modifications that make an image look unrealistic.

To address these shortcomings, we generalize SSL transformations by introducing a new transformation that need not belong to a parametric form and is capable of producing diverse, realistic images that follow the distribution of real-world data. The only constraint is that to learn useful representations, a transformed image must remain semantically close to its source image.
As demonstrated in Fig.
2
, our new transformation is based on conditional generative models. These models, such as conditional Variational Autoencoders (cVAEs)
[
54
]
, instance-conditioned GANs
[
12
]
, and instance-conditioned diffusion models
[
7
,
44
]
, offer the capability to generate images conditioned on input data.
By conditioning the generation process on the input, the model ensures that the semantics of the original image are preserved to a large extent.
Moreover, the use of conditional generative models allows for a more nuanced augmentation process, where the generated samples closely resemble the input data while exhibiting variations that enhance the diversity of the training data for self-supervised learning. Our new transformation is denoted by:

\displaystyle T^{*}(x)=G(\varepsilon;f(x)),

where
G
denotes a conditional generative model,
\varepsilon
is a noise vector, and
f
is a pretrained encoder (such as a CLIP image encoder
[
41
]
) that provides the condition vector for the generative model.

Note that, unlike StableRep
[
49
]
, we do not replace a real dataset with a synthetic one. Instead, we leverage conditional generative models to enrich augmentations for self-supervised learning. In addition, our method does not require text prompts and directly uses images as input to the generative model.

5
Conclusion

Image augmentation is a crucial component of joint embedding self-supervised learning methods.
Although most works in visual self-supervised learning rely on a limited set of transformations, recently, a few studies have proposed new parametric pixel-space transformations to enhance augmentations. In this work, we introduce a new augmentation method that leverages instance-conditioned generative models to transform an image while preserving its semantics. In addition, this transformation is capable of producing realistic images, which results in more diversity in training data. Our empirical study with ICGAN and Stable Diffusion models demonstrates the effectiveness of the generative transformations for self-supervised representation learning.

Several directions exist to expand this work. For example, the condition vector of generative models is obtained from a pretrained encoder. We should compare the representations of these pretrained encoders against the new representations learned by applying generative transformations. Moreover, in our study, we apply traditional SSL augmentations to the output of the generative transformation. A possible ablation study involves omitting the traditional augmentations from the pipeline.

Another interesting direction is to co-train the instance-conditioned generative model and the SSL encoder simultaneously. This could potentially lead to the enhancement of the generative model for the SSL task.
