2.
Introduction: Siegel Zeroes

There are, however, other countervailing conjectures that assume that primes do
not
behave nicely in certain circumstances. One of the most famous is the assumption of Landau-Siegel zeroes, which is explained as follows.

For a character
\chi_{q}
modulo
q
, we define as usual

L(s,\chi_{q})=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\chi_{q}(n)}{n^{s}}.

In 1918, Landau
[
La
]
proved the following:

Theorem
.

(Landau 1918)
There exists a constant
c
such that for any
q
and any real-valued character
\chi_{q}
mod
q
, there exists at most one
s=\sigma+it
for which
L(s,\chi_{q})=0
and

Re(s)>1-\frac{c}{\log q(|t|+2)}.

.

Such a zero, if it is to exist, is known as a
Siegel zero
, a
Landau-Siegel zero
, or an
exceptional zero
, and the associated character is known as an
exceptional character
. It is known that if such a zero exists, it must be real (i.e.
t=0
) and the character must be real-valued as well.

We note that in our definition above (and indeed in the literature about the topic), the definition of a Siegel zero is a bit ill-defined in that it depends on the choice of a constant
c
in the equation above. Generally, at the beginning of a paper assuming the existence of Siegel zeroes, the authors must declare which specific definition of Siegel zero they will require for their theorem; we will do so here shortly.

Before we do that, however, we recall the two theorems that give us the best known upper bounds for Siegel zeroes. The first is the best effective bound; the second is the best ineffective bound:

Theorem
.

(Landau 1918)
There exists an effectively computable constant
C
such that for any
q
and any real-valued character
\chi_{q}
mod
q
, a Siegel zero
\beta
must satisfy

\beta<1-\frac{C}{q^{\frac{1}{2}}\log^{2}q}.

.

Theorem
.

(Siegel 1935)
For any
\epsilon
there exists a constant
C(\epsilon)
such that

\beta<1-C(\epsilon)q^{-\epsilon}.

Of course, mathematicians do not expect such a zero to exist for any
q
or
\chi_{q}
; in fact, the generalized Riemann hypothesis predicts all non-trivial zeroes will have
Re(s)=\frac{1}{2}
. Nevertheless, we cannot yet rule out the existence of Siegel zeroes. In fact, the world of Siegel zeros has been described as ‘‘an ‘illusory’ or ‘ghostly’ parallel universe in analytic number theory that should not actually exist, but is surprisingly self-consistent and to date proven to be impossible to banish from the realm of possibility
1
1

.”

We reiterate that if such an
s

\chi_{q}

\chi_{q}
being a primitive character mod
q

\chi_{q}
must also be a real-valued character mod

M|q

\chi_{q}(n)
is derived from the Kronecker symbol

Surprisingly, the existence of a Siegel zero often yields unusually nice results, including results related to prime gaps. Perhaps the nicest example of such a result is the following, which appears in

(Heath-Brown 1983) Let
\chi_{q}

\beta>1-\frac{1}{3\log q}.

If there are infinitely many

for which such a

In particular, Heath-Brown found that for any such

, the number of twin primes
(p,p+2)
with
q^{250}\leq p\leq q^{500}
is as conjectured in the Hardy-Littlewood conjectures

These bounds on

Unfortunately, Heath-Brown’s methods do not appear to generalize to tuples beyond pairs. However, with the new results on prime tuples, it seems natural to ask whether Siegel zeroes might be of help in the more general prime tuple case. This is the motivating question of our current paper.

Main Result

Then for any

For smaller values of

Main Theorem 2
.

With the same assumptions about Siegel zeroes as given in the previous theorem, we have

\displaystyle H_{2}\leq 264,

\displaystyle H_{3}\leq 49,192,

\displaystyle H_{4}\leq 439,812,

\displaystyle H_{5}\leq 3,775,860.

We note that all of these are better results than the assumption of Elliott-Halberstam, although our

5.
Remarks

Obviously, no one has made conjectures as to what the largest possible value for

[

]

[
FI03

The second bound, however, comes from the famous Weil bound for Kloosterman sums; as such, any improvement over 2/3 would likely require either a method of estimating the main term that avoids using this bound or else an improvement on the Weil bound itself.

On the other hand, conjectures about primes in arithmetic progressions (such as Montgomery’s conjecture on arithmetic progressions

6.
Outline

The proof, for the most part, will follow the framework of Maynard and Tao. The differences are as follows:

1.) Obviously, instead of invoking Bombieri-Vinogradov or Elliott-Halberstam, we invoke the result of Friedlander and Iwaniec. Since we have an explicit error term mod
q
for each
q
, we are not required to do any fancy Cauchy-Scwhartz machinations; we can simply plug in an estimate for
\pi(x,q,a)
and then sum this term up over all allowable choices of
q
.

2.) If we wish to ensure that the number of primes is double what Dirichlet’s theorem would predict, we must shift the prime tuple so as to put all of the terms in classes
a
mod
q
for which
\chi_{D}(a)=-1
and
D|q
. Since
D
is small relative to
x
, this causes little issue in our analysis. This shifting is covered in section
7
.

From here, the proof mostly follows the ideas described in
[
Ma
]
, albeit with
W
a bit bigger than in the original paper. We note that
[
Ka
]
and
[
BFM
]
outline the process for dealing with a larger
W
, and the effect on the final calculations is minimal.

7.
Shifting the Primes

To begin, let us choose an admissible tuple

T=\{n+h_{1},\cdots,n+h_{k}\}.

In order to use the Friedlander-Iwaniec result, we must first shift our prime tuple so that all of the terms are in a good class for the character modulo
D
.

We know that an exceptional zero indicates that the character must be real-valued and hence must arise from a Kronecker character. As such, the character can only take the values of
-1
, 1, and 0. We will deal only with the case where
\chi_{D}
is a primitive character; in other words,

\chi_{D}(n)=\left(\frac{\chi_{D}(-1)D}{n}\right).

We do not need to consider the case when
\chi_{D}
is imprimitive, for if it is, it must arise from a primitive character
\chi_{C}
for some
C|D
. If that were true,
L(s,\chi_{C})
would have the same Siegel zero with

Re(s)>1-\frac{1}{(\log D)^{r^{r}+A}}>1-\frac{1}{(\log C)^{r^{r}+A}}.

So we may simply take
C
to be our initial
D
and proceed as normal.

First, let
g
be the largest prime divisor of
D
, and let

(2)

\displaystyle D=gD^{\prime}.

Since
\chi_{D}
is primitive, we know that
D
must be square-free except possibly for a power of 2 that is at most
2^{3}
. In particular, this means that
g\gg\log D.

Now, our character must split up multiplicatively, so

\chi_{D}(n)=\chi_{g}(n)\chi_{D^{\prime}}(n),

where
\chi_{g}(n)
and
\chi_{D^{\prime}}(n)
must both take values in
\{-1,1,0\}
.

In this section, our goal will be to show the following:

Lemma 7.1
.

For a fixed
k
, let
D
be such that
\log\log\log(D)\gg k
. Then there exists an
l\leq D
such that for all
i
,

\chi_{D}\left(l+h_{i}\right)=-1.

Proof.

First, we know that since our tuple is admissible, there exists an
n^{\prime}
such that
(n^{\prime}+h_{i},D)=1
for each
i
.

Now, consider the expression

\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left(1-\chi_{D}(x+n^{\prime}+h_{i})\right).

Clearly, this expression is always non-negative, but it will only equal a non-zero value when all of the
\chi_{D}(x+n^{\prime}+h_{i})
are either -1 or 0.

So for
D^{\prime}
and
g
as defined above in (
2
), let
x=D^{\prime}y
and consider the sum

H=\sum_{y=1}^{g}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left(1-\chi_{D}(D^{\prime}y+n^{\prime}+h_{i})\right),

Note that since
g
is prime and
(n^{\prime}+h_{i},gD^{\prime})=1
for all
i
,
\chi_{D}(D^{\prime}y+n^{\prime}+h_{i})
can only equal 0 if
g|D^{\prime}y+n^{\prime}+h_{i}
; for each choice of
i
, this will occur exactly once as
y
ranges from 1 to
g
. So there are exactly
k
choices of
y
where any of the
\chi_{D}(D^{\prime}y+n^{\prime}+h_{i})=0
. For each of these choices of
y
,

0\leq\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left(1-\chi_{D}(D^{\prime}y+n^{\prime}+h_{i})\right)\leq 2^{k-1}.

So if
H>k2^{k-1}
then there must exist a tuple where all of the
\chi_{D}(D^{\prime}y+n^{\prime}+h_{i})=-1
, which is as required.

Now, we can rewrite
H
as

\displaystyle H=

\displaystyle\sum_{y=1}^{g}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left(1-\chi_{D^{\prime}}(D^{\prime}y+n^{\prime}+h_{i})\chi_{g}(D^{\prime}y+n^{\prime}+h_{i})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{y=1}^{g}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left(1-\chi_{D^{\prime}}(n^{\prime}+h_{i})\chi_{g}(D^{\prime}y+n^{\prime}+h_{i})\right)

For each
i
, let us write
\epsilon_{i}=\chi_{D^{\prime}}(n^{\prime}+h_{i})
. Again, these
\epsilon_{i}
are all
\pm 1
since
(D^{\prime},n^{\prime}+h_{i})=1
. So

\displaystyle H=

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{y=1}^{g}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left(\epsilon_{i}-\chi_{g}(D^{\prime}y+n^{\prime}+h_{i})\right)\right|

Let us multiply out the product. So

\displaystyle H=

\displaystyle\left|\left(\sum_{y=1}^{g}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\epsilon_{i}\right)+\left(\sum_{y=1}^{g}\sum_{j=1}^{2^{k}-1}\left(\prod_{i\in S_{j}}\epsilon_{i}\right)\chi_{g}(Q_{j}(y))\right)\right|

where the
Q_{j}(y)
are polynomials of degree
\leq k
that are square-free when viewed over
\mathbb{F}_{g}
, and the
S_{j}
are distinct subsets of
\{1,2,\cdots,k\}
. By triangle inequality,

\displaystyle H\geq

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{y=1}^{g}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\epsilon_{i}\right|-\left|\sum_{j=1}^{2^{k}-1}\left(\prod_{i\in S_{j}}\epsilon_{i}\right)\sum_{y=1}^{g}\chi_{g}(Q_{j}(y))\right|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{y=1}^{g}1\right|-\sum_{j=1}^{2^{k}-1}\left|\sum_{y=1}^{g}\chi_{g}(Q_{j}(y))\right|

The first absolute value is clearly equal to
g
; for the second, we can use the Weil bound for character sums over
\mathbb{F}_{g}
, finding that

\left|\sum_{y=1}^{g}\chi_{g}(Q_{j}(y))\right|\leq(k-1)\sqrt{g}.

So

H\geq g-k2^{k-1}\sqrt{g}.

This is clearly larger than
k2^{k-1}
for large values of
g
. So there must exist a
y^{\prime}\leq g
such that

\chi_{D}(D^{\prime}y^{\prime}+n^{\prime}+h_{i})=-1
for all
i
. We then set

l\equiv D^{\prime}y^{\prime}+n^{\prime}\pmod{D}

to complete the theorem.
∎

8.
The Maynard-Tao Sieve

Now, we proceed as in the work of Maynard-Tao. First, in
[

]

Fix a large

Because our tuple is admissible, we know that there exists a congruence class

\displaystyle v_{0}\equiv v\pmod{V}

\displaystyle v_{0}\equiv l\pmod{D}

As is standard, for a well-chosen function

X\leq n\leq 2X\end{subarray}}\left(\sum_{d_{i}|n+h_{i}}\lambda_{d_{1},\cdots,d_{k}}\right)^{2},

X\leq n\leq 2X\end{subarray}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\textbf{1}_{\mathbb{P}}(n+h_{i})\left(\sum_{d_{i}|n+h_{i}}\lambda_{d_{1},\cdots,d_{k}}\right)^{2}.

This is essentially the sum that was considered in

Obviously, it will be helpful if we have a definition for
\lambda

To this end, let
S_{k}
be the set of all piecewise differentiable functions
F:[0,\infty)^{k}\to\mathbb{R}
where the support of

lies on the simplex

\mathcal{R}_{k}=\{(t_{1},...,t_{k}):\sum_{i=1}^{k}t_{i}\leq 1\}

For our purposes, we will choose functions
F\in\mathcal{S}_{k}
and then let

\lambda_{d_{1},\cdots,d_{k}}=\mu(d_{1})\cdots\mu(d_{k})F\left(\frac{\log d_{1}}{\log R},\cdots,\frac{\log d_{k}}{\log R}\right).

We require a lemma about the behavior of such functions; this lemma is the crux of Maynard and Tao’s work, since this lemma allows one to turn the search for prime gaps into a search for the best possible
F

\displaystyle I_{k}(F)=\int_{0}^{\infty}...\int_{0}^{\infty}F(t_{1},...,t_{k})^{2}dt_{1}...dt_{k},

(4)

\displaystyle J_{k}^{(m)}(F)=\int_{0}^{\infty}...\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}F(t_{1},...,t_{k})^{2}dt_{m}\right)dt_{1}...dt_{m-1}dt_{m+1}dt_{k}.

Lemma 8.1
.

Let

,
\lambda
,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1},\cdots d_{k},e_{1},\cdots,e_{k}\\
[d_{1},e_{1}],\cdots,[d_{k},e_{k}],W\mbox{ }coprime\end{subarray}}\frac{\lambda_{d_{1},\cdots,d_{k}}\lambda_{e_{1},\cdots,e_{k}}}{[d_{j},e_{j}]}=(1+o(1))B^{-k}I_{k}(F)

and

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1},\cdots d_{k-1},e_{1},\cdots,e_{k-1}\\
[d_{1},e_{1}],\cdots,[d_{k},e_{k}],W\mbox{ }coprime\end{subarray}}\frac{\lambda_{d_{1},\cdots,d_{k-1},1}\lambda_{e_{1},\cdots,e_{k-1},1}}{\phi([d_{j},e_{j}])}=(1+o(1))B^{1-k}J_{k}(F).

Proof.

This is essentially the evaluation of (5.2) and (5.18) of
[

. That paper only deals with very small

10.

First, we evaluate

The usual trick is to expand out the square and reverse the order of summation, giving

S_{1}=\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1},\cdots,d_{k}\\
e_{1},\cdots,e_{k}\end{subarray}}\lambda_{d_{1},\cdots,d_{k}}\lambda_{e_{1},\cdots,e_{k}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\equiv v_{0}\pmod{W},X\leq n\leq 2X\\

where the prime indicates that the sum requires the

to be pairwise coprime. The

and

(by choice of

); moreover,

. We can then replace the inside sum with

\displaystyle\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1},\cdots,d_{k}\\
e_{1},\cdots,e_{k}\end{subarray}}\lambda_{d_{1},\cdots,d_{k}}\lambda_{e_{1},\cdots,e_{k}}\left(\frac{X}{W\prod[d_{i},e_{i}]}+O(1)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1},\cdots,d_{k}\\
e_{1},\cdots,e_{k}\end{subarray}}\lambda_{d_{1},\cdots,d_{k}}\lambda_{e_{1},\cdots,e_{k}}\frac{X}{W\prod[d_{i},e_{i}]}\right)+O(R).

Hence the error term is negligible.

For the main term, we pull out the

\frac{X}{W}\left(\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1},\cdots,d_{k}\\

We then invoke Lemma

From Theorem

The work of Friedlander and Iwaniec allows any
