Low-energy moiré phonons in twisted bilayer van der Waals heterostructures Preprint: APS/123-QED
Jonathan Z. Lu, Ziyan Zhu, Mattia Angeli, Daniel T. Larson, Efthimios Kaxiras

Abstract
We develop a low-energy continuum model for phonons in twisted moiré bilayers, based on a configuration-space approach. In this approach, interatomic force constants are obtained from density functional theory (DFT) calculations of untwisted bilayers with various in-plane shifts. This allows for efficient computation of phonon properties for any small twist angle, while maintaining DFT-level accuracy. Based on this framework, we show how the low-energy phonon modes, including interlayer shearing and layer-breathing modes, vary with the twist angle. As the twist angle decreases, the frequencies of the low-energy modes are reordered and the atomic displacement fields corresponding to phonon eigenmodes break translational symmetry, developing periodicity on the moiré length scale. We demonstrate the capabilities of our model by calculating the phonon properties of three specific structures: bilayer graphene, bilayer molybdenum disulfide (MoS 2 ), and molybdenum diselenide-tungsten diselenide (MoSe 2 -WSe 2 ).

Introduction

Two-dimensional (2D) van der Waals (vdW) multi-layer structures can be constructed by stacking 2D materials in a layer-by-layer fashion. If the stacking involves a twist of one layer by an angle
\theta
with respect to the next layer, the relative
misorientation between atomic lattices produces a moiré interference pattern with a length scale larger than the lattice constants of the individual layers.
Such moiré bilayers can give rise to localized electronic states and tunable electronic properties
Carr et al. 2017
;
Yankowitz et al. 2018
;
Liu et al. 2020
;
Song et al. 2019
;
Burg et al. 2019
;
Cao et al. 2020
;
Moriyama et al. 2019
. In twisted bilayer graphene, superconductivity and other unconventional correlated states
have been observed when the twist angle
\theta
takes values near the “magic angle”
Cao et al. 2018a
;
Cao et al. 2018b
;
Lu et al. 2019
;
Xie and MacDonald 2020
. Similar behavior has been shown in twisted bilayers composed of transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDCs), albeit often with decreased sensitivity to the twist angle
Wang et al. 2019
;
Li et al. 2019
;
Wu et al. 2018
;
Liu et al. 2014
;
Wu et al. 2019
;
Zhang et al. 2021
;
Tran et al. 2019
;
Jin et al. 2019
;
Alexeev et al. 2019
;
Seyler et al. 2019
;
Rivera et al. 2015
;
van der Zande et al. 2014
;
Angeli and MacDonald 2021
.
Determination of the role of phonons is a crucial step towards an understanding of these strongly correlated electronic states, particularly superconductivity
Wu et al. 2018
;
Lian et al. 2019
;
Cea and Guinea 2021
;
Angeli et al. 2019
.
The collective vibration of a twisted bilayer can be strongly affected by the moiré pattern, forming the so-called moiré phonons
Koshino and Son 2019
;
Angeli et al. 2019
. Moiré phonons are also of general experimental interest, and have recently been observed in graphene
Gadelha et al. 2021
.
The variation of phonon properties with the twist angle allow Raman spectroscopy to be used to help characterize moiré bilayers

;
Wu et al. 2014
;
Huang et al. 2016
;
Lin et al. 2018
;
Quan et al. 2021
.

The interplay of two distinct length scales and the large number of atoms within a moiré cell make calculations of the phonon properties extremely challenging.
Due to the rapid scaling in computational cost with respect to the moiré length, direct calculations of phonons using density functional theory (DFT) are currently not feasible for twist angles less than a few degrees. Moreover, DFT calculations using periodic supercells can only be carried out for a specific set of commensurate angles. Instead, a continuum approximation has been successfully used to model the electronic structure and atomic relaxation of twisted bilayers and trilayers
Dai et al. 2016
;
Nam and Koshino 2017
;
Español et al. 2017
;
Español et al. 2018
;
Leconte et al. 2019
;
Carr et al. 2018
;
Zhu et al. 2020
. Continuum models have also been considered for moiré phonon analysis. The most efficient of these are empirical continuum models, but they leave out important details such as the out-of-plane degree of freedom and phonon modes that require coupling, for instance the shearing and layer-breathing modes
Koshino and Son 2019
.

We propose a first-principles-based continuum model of moiré phonons, similar to the one developed by
Quan et al. 2021
to help interpret the Raman spectra of twisted MoS
2
bilayers, but we have extended it to momenta beyond the
\Gamma
-point and have applied the model to several different bilayer systems. To bypass the need to compute the interatomic forces for all atoms in a large moiré supercell, we adopt a configuration space formalism to describe the local atomic environment. This approach has been used as the basis for a continuum model of atomic relaxations in twisted vdW heterostructures
Massatt et al. 2016
;
Cazeaux et al. 2016
;
Cancès et al. 2017
;
Cazeaux et al. 2019
;
Carr et al. 2018
.
Using this model we obtain the low-energy phonon properties of bilayer graphene, bilayer MoS
2
, and a MoSe
2
-WSe
2
heterostructure, and show how a relative twist between the layers modifies the phonon frequencies and eigenmodes.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section

In Section

II
Configuration Space Continuum Model

The workflow of our configuration space continuum (CSC) model is summarized in Fig.
1
. We first construct the moiré dynamical matrix by computation of the dynamical matrices of many rigidly shifted bilayers—one for each configuration of atoms in layer 1 with respect to layer 2—and the individual monolayers. From the monolayer we obtain the optimized lattice constant and the elastic constants that are used for relaxation. We relax the ionic positions of each shifted bilayer as well, keeping the horizontal positions of one atom per layer fixed to maintain the registry between the layers. We then compute atomic forces using the frozen phonon approach, which under the CSC formalism produces the unrelaxed (rigid) moiré dynamical matrix. Finally, we include twist-dependent relaxation which yields the relaxed moiré form of the dynamical matrix. We provide a derivation of the model in this section, and details of the calculations can be found in Appendices
A
and
B
. Throughout the derivation, we use tildes to indicate a quantity in the moiré supercell, and when referring to the dynamical matrix we use a bar to denote the matrix in real space (the absence of the bar means the matrix is expressed in Fourier space).

Figure 1:
Schematic representation of the numerical implementation of the CSC model. A collection of monolayer (blue) and bilayer (pink) calculations are combined into a rigid (unrelaxed) moiré dynamical matrix and used to determine the bilayer relaxation pattern (both purple). All of these elements are combined to form the relaxed moiré dynamical matrix. The square labeled “relaxed moiré dynamical matrix” formally represents the dynamical matrix construction: the diagonal blocks are from the monolayer calculations whereas the off-diagonal blocks are constructed from the stacking dependent force field calculations.

II.1
Moiré Dynamical Matrix

The phonon spectrum of a crystal can be obtained by diagonalizing the dynamical matrix with the basis expanded in momentum space.
The moiré dynamical matrix at a point
\widetilde{\bm{k}}

in the moiré Brillouin zone, which we call the center site, is represented generically as a layer-block matrix encoding intralayer (diagonal) and interlayer (off-diagonal) couplings:

\displaystyle D_{\text{moir\'{e}}}(\bm{\widetilde{k}})=\begin{bmatrix}D^{1}(\bm{\widetilde{k}})&D^{12}\\
D^{12*}&D^{2}(\bm{\widetilde{k}}).\end{bmatrix},

(1)

Each block of the moiré dynamical matrix depends on two reciprocal-space degrees of freedom,
\bm{k}^{(\ell)}
and
\bm{k}^{(\ell^{\prime})}
, which are momenta from the monolayer Brillouin zones of layers
\ell
and
\ell^{\prime}
.
This is because in a twisted system, the interatomic forces depend not only on the pair-wise distance of two atoms, but also the position of the atom due to the change of the local environment.

II.2
Moiré Scattering Selection Rule

We first derive how the degrees of freedom in the basis of Eq. (
1
) are connected to each other through a selection rule.
We begin with a monolayer, which is the intralayer term that has no stacking dependence. Considering only one layer
\ell
, the monolayer phonon equation of motion can be expressed as

\displaystyle\sum_{j\nu\beta}\overline{D}_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}^{\ell}(\bm{R}_{i}-\bm{R}_{j})\delta\widehat{u}_{j\ell\nu\beta}=\omega^{2}\delta\widehat{u}_{i\ell\mu\alpha},

(2)

where
\overline{D}_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}^{\ell}(\bm{R}_{i}-\bm{R}_{j})
is the dynamical matrix element,
\bm{R}_{i}
is a monolayer lattice vector,
\mu,\nu
are Cartesian degrees of freedom,
\alpha,\beta
are sublattice degrees of freedom, and
\delta\widehat{u}_{i\ell\mu\alpha}
is the phonon displacement component that corresponds to atom
\alpha
in direction
\mu
. We can define a phonon displacement in the Bloch basis as follows,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \delta\widehat{u}_{i\ell\mu\alpha}(\bm{k}^{(\ell)})&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\Gamma^{*}|}}e^{i\bm{k}^{(\ell)}\cdot\bm{R}_{i}}\delta u_{\ell\mu\alpha}(\bm{k}^{(\ell)}),\end{aligned}

(3)

where
\bm{k}^{(\ell)}

is a momentum degree of freedom in
\Gamma^{(\ell)*}
, the monolayer Brillouin zone of layer
\ell
with area
|\Gamma^{*}|
.
We first note the orthogonality relation

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \sum_{\mu\alpha}\delta\widehat{u}_{i\ell\mu\alpha}^{*}\delta\widehat{u}_{j\ell\mu\alpha}=\delta_{ij}.\end{aligned}

(4)

To expand the equation of motion, Eq. (
2
), in the Fourier basis, we multiply both sides by
\delta\widehat{u}_{k\ell\mu\alpha}^{*}
, then sum over
k
,
\mu
, and
\alpha
.

\displaystyle\sum_{jk\mu\nu\alpha\beta}\overline{D}_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}^{\ell}(\bm{R}_{i}-\bm{R}_{j})\delta\widehat{u}_{k\ell\mu\alpha}^{*}\delta\widehat{u}_{j\ell\nu\beta}

\displaystyle=\omega^{2}\sum_{k\mu\alpha}\delta\widehat{u}_{k\ell\mu\alpha}^{*}\delta\widehat{u}_{i\ell\mu\alpha}.

(5)

Using Eq. (
4
):

\displaystyle\sum_{ij\mu\nu\alpha\beta}\overline{D}_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}^{\ell}(\bm{R}_{i}-\bm{R}_{j})\delta\widehat{u}_{i\ell\mu\alpha}^{*}\delta\widehat{u}_{j\ell\nu\beta}

\displaystyle=\omega^{2}.

(6)

Relabeling
\bm{R}=\bm{R}_{i},\Delta\bm{R}=\bm{R}_{i}-\bm{R}_{j}
and
changing to the Bloch basis using
Eq. (
3
) yields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\Gamma^{*}|}\sum_{\bm{R}}\left[e^{-i(\bm{k}^{(\ell)}-\bm{k}^{\prime(\ell)})\cdot\bm{R}}\sum_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}\sum_{\Delta\bm{R}}\overline{D}_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}(\Delta\bm{R})e^{-i\bm{k}^{\prime(\ell)}\cdot\Delta\bm{R}}\delta u_{\ell\mu\alpha}^{*}(\bm{k}^{(\ell)})\delta u_{\ell\nu\beta}(\bm{k}^{\prime(\ell)})\right]

\displaystyle=\omega^{2}.

(7)

Note the Poisson resummation rule:

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{R}}e^{-i(\bm{k}^{(\ell)}-\bm{k}^{\prime(\ell)})\cdot\bm{R}}=|\Gamma^{*}|\sum_{\bm{G}^{(\ell)}}\delta(\bm{k}^{(\ell)}-\bm{k}^{\prime(\ell)}-\bm{G}^{(\ell)}),

(8)

where
\bm{G}^{(\ell)}
is a reciprocal lattice vector in layer
\ell
.
Defining
D_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}(\bm{k}^{\prime(\ell)})
as:

\displaystyle D_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}(\bm{k}^{\prime(\ell)})=\sum_{\Delta\bm{R}}\overline{D}_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}(\Delta\bm{R})e^{-i\bm{k}^{\prime(\ell)}\cdot\Delta\bm{R}},

(9)

the phonon equation of motion becomes

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \sum_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}D_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}(\bm{k}^{(\ell)})\delta u_{\ell\mu\alpha}^{*}(\bm{k}^{(\ell)})\delta u_{\ell\nu\beta}(\bm{k}^{(\ell)})=\omega^{2},\end{aligned}

(10)

for each
\bm{G}^{(\ell)}
, since the phonon displacement
\delta u_{i\ell\mu\alpha}
is the same at
\bm{k}^{(\ell)}
and
\bm{k}^{(\ell)}+\bm{G}^{(\ell)}
.
Equation (
10
) is equivalent to:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} D(\bm{k}^{(\ell)})\cdot\delta\bm{u}(\bm{k}^{(\ell)})=\omega^{2}\delta\bm{u}(\bm{k}^{(\ell)}),\end{aligned}

(11)

which is the eigenvalue equation that describes the intralayer phonon at
\bm{k}^{(\ell)}
, and the dynamical matrix
D(\bm{k}^{(\ell)})
can be computed using either DFPT or frozen phonon approaches.

In a moiré cell the dynamical matrix gains an additional degree of freedom because the forces depend not only on the difference in lattice vectors, but also the real space position itself due to the twist.
The general phonon equation of motion is given as the following:

\displaystyle\sum_{j\nu\beta\ell^{\prime}}\overline{D}_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}(\bm{R}_{i},\bm{R}_{j})\delta\widehat{u}_{j\ell^{\prime}\nu\beta}=\omega^{2}\delta\widehat{u}_{i\ell\mu\alpha},

(12)

where
\bm{R}_{i}
and
\bm{R}_{j}
correspond to the atomic positions on layers
\ell
and
\ell^{\prime}
respectively.
Similar to the intralayer term, we multiply both sides of the equation by
\delta\widehat{u}^{*}_{i\ell\mu\alpha}
and sum over
\mu
,
\alpha
, and
\ell
. Plugging in the definition in Eq. (
3
):

\displaystyle\sum_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta\ell\ell^{\prime}}D_{\widetilde{\bm{k}}\mu\nu\alpha\beta}(\bm{k}^{(\ell)},\bm{k}^{(\ell^{\prime})})\delta u^{*}_{\ell\mu\alpha}(\bm{K}^{(\ell)})\delta u_{\ell^{\prime}\nu\beta}(\bm{K}^{(\ell^{\prime})})=\omega^{2},

(13)

where

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} D_{\widetilde{\bm{k}}\mu\nu\alpha\beta}(\bm{k}^{(\ell)},\bm{k}^{(\ell^{\prime})})=\frac{1}{|\Gamma^{*}|}\sum_{ij}\overline{D}_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}(\bm{R}_{i},\bm{R}_{j})e^{-i\bm{K}^{(\ell)}\cdot\bm{R}_{i}}e^{i\bm{K}^{(\ell^{\prime})}\cdot\bm{R}_{j}}.\end{aligned}

(14)

and
\bm{K}^{(\ell)}=\widetilde{\bm{k}}+\bm{k}^{(\ell)}
is an expansion for small momentum
\bm{k}^{(\ell)}
about a given point
\widetilde{\bm{k}}
in the moiré Brillouin zone.
Making a two-center approximation such that
\overline{D}_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}(\bm{R}_{i},\bm{R}_{j})=\overline{D}_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}(\bm{R}_{i}-\bm{R}_{j})
, we can then perform a Fourier expansion on
\overline{D}_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}(\bm{R}_{i}-\bm{R}_{j})
:

\displaystyle\overline{D}_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}(\bm{R}_{i}-\bm{R}_{j})=\int\frac{\differential^{2}{\bm{p}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,e^{i\bm{p}\cdot(\bm{R}_{i}-\bm{R}_{j})}D_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}(\bm{p}),

(15)

Note that despite the two-center approximation, the momentum-space dynamical matrix
D_{\tilde{\bm{k}}\mu\nu\alpha\beta}
still depends on two different momenta from the opposite layers
\ell
and
\ell^{\prime}
.
Using Eq. (
15
), as well as Eq. (
8
) in Eq. (
14
) yields

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} D_{\widetilde{\bm{k}}\mu\nu\alpha\beta}(\bm{k}^{(\ell)},\bm{k}^{(\ell^{\prime})})&=\frac{1}{|\Gamma^{*}|}\int\frac{\differential^{2}{\bm{p}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\;D_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}(\bm{p})\sum_{ij}e^{-i(\bm{K}^{(\ell)}+\bm{p})\cdot\bm{R}_{i}}e^{i(\bm{K}^{(\ell^{\prime})}+\bm{p})\cdot\bm{R}_{j}}\\
&=\frac{|\Gamma^{*}|}{(2\pi)^{2}}\sum_{\bm{G}^{(\ell)}\bm{G}^{(\ell^{\prime})}}\int\differential^{2}{\bm{p}}\,D_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}(\bm{p})\,\delta(\bm{K}^{(\ell)}+\bm{p}-\bm{G}^{(\ell)})\,\delta(\bm{K}^{(\ell^{\prime})}+\bm{p}-\bm{G}^{(\ell^{\prime})})\\
&=\frac{1}{|\Gamma|}\sum_{\bm{G}^{(\ell)}\bm{G}^{(\ell^{\prime})}}D_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}(\widetilde{\bm{k}}+\bm{k}^{(\ell)}-\bm{G}^{(\ell)})\,\delta_{\bm{k}^{(\ell^{\prime})}-\bm{k}^{(\ell)},\bm{G}^{(\ell^{\prime})}-\bm{G}^{(\ell)}},\\
\end{aligned}

(16)

where
|\Gamma|
is the area of the monolayer unit cell and we note that
|\Gamma|=(2\pi)^{2}/|\Gamma^{*}|
. The
\delta
-function imposes a selection rule that constrains the allowed values of
\bm{k}^{(\ell)}
and
\bm{k}^{(\ell^{\prime})}
.
For concreteness, we define each reciprocal lattice vector as

\displaystyle\bm{G}^{(\ell)}_{mn}=m\bm{g}^{(\ell)}_{1}+n\bm{g}_{2}^{(\ell)},

(17)

where
\bm{g}_{1}^{(\ell)}
and
\bm{g}_{2}^{(\ell)}
are a reciprocal lattice basis for layer
\ell
. Neglecting higher-order coupling, we assume that only terms with equal
m,n
in
\bm{G}^{(\ell)}
and
\bm{G}^{(\ell^{\prime})}
are nonzero, so the above simplifies to

\displaystyle D_{\widetilde{\bm{k}}\mu\nu\alpha\beta}(\bm{k}^{(\ell)},\bm{k}^{(\ell^{\prime})})=\frac{1}{|\Gamma|}\sum_{mn}

\displaystyle D_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}(\widetilde{\bm{k}}+\bm{k}^{(\ell)}-\bm{G}^{(\ell)}_{mn})\,\delta_{\bm{k}^{(\ell)}-\bm{k}^{(\ell^{\prime})},\widetilde{\bm{G}}_{mn}}.

(18)

The difference
\bm{G}^{(\ell)}_{mn}-\bm{G}^{(\ell^{\prime})}_{mn}
between corresponding reciprocal lattice vectors from each layer is a moiré reciprocal lattice vector
\widetilde{\bm{G}}_{mn}
.
Without loss of generality, taking
\ell=1
and
\ell^{\prime}=2
, Eq. (
18
) imposes a constraint on the momenta given by

\displaystyle\bm{k}^{(1)}-\bm{k}^{(2)}=\bm{G}^{(2)}_{mn}-\bm{G}^{(1)}_{mn}=-\widetilde{\bm{G}}_{mn}.

(19)

We henceforth drop the subscripts on
\widetilde{\bm{G}}
for clarity. Equation (
18
) is the fundamental equation governing the moiré dynamical matrix. The stacking-dependent part of the moiré phonon equation of motion becomes

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{1}{|\Gamma|}\sum_{\nu\beta\ell^{\prime}}\sum_{\widetilde{\bm{G}}}D_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}(\widetilde{\bm{k}}+\bm{k}^{(\ell)}-\bm{G}^{(\ell)})\delta u_{\ell^{\prime}\nu\beta}(\bm{K}^{(\ell^{\prime})}-\widetilde{\bm{G}})&=\omega^{2}\delta u_{\ell\mu\alpha}(\bm{K}^{(\ell)}),\end{aligned}

(20)

where we note that
\widetilde{\bm{G}}
and
\bm{G}^{(\ell)}
are in one-to-one correspondence via Eq. (
19
). To simplify the above we choose a gauge
\bm{k}^{(\ell)}=\bm{G}^{(\ell^{\prime})}
(though other gauges will work, and we will choose a different one to justify the construction of the moiré dynamical matrix) with
\ell\neq\ell^{\prime}
. Without loss of generality, we let
\ell=1
:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} D_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}(\widetilde{\bm{k}}+\bm{k}^{(1)}-\bm{G}^{(1)})&=D_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}(\bm{k}+\bm{G}^{(2)}-\bm{G}^{(1)})\\
&=D_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}(\widetilde{\bm{k}}-\widetilde{\bm{G}}).\end{aligned}

(21)

Note that reciprocal space has sufficient symmetry that any choice of a pair of signs for
\bm{k}
and
\widetilde{\bm{G}}
produce the same term above.

Combining the monolayer term (Eq. (
11
)) and the twist-dependent term (Eq. (
20
)), we obtain the moiré dynamical matrix.
We verified numerically that the magnitude of the dynamical matrix decays rapidly with distance from the
\Gamma
-point, so it suffices to expand up to the first shell of the moiré reciprocal lattice, which contains six vectors
\widetilde{\bm{G}}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{\bm{G}}_{6}
. For the off-diagonal terms, we ignore
\bm{\widetilde{k}}
-dependence. The interlayer term is explicitly given as follows:

\displaystyle D^{12}=\begin{bmatrix}D_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}^{12}(\widetilde{\bm{G}}_{0})&D_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}^{12}(\widetilde{\bm{G}}_{1})&\cdots&D_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}^{12}(\widetilde{\bm{G}}_{6})\\
D_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}^{12}(-\widetilde{\bm{G}}_{1})&D_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}^{12}(\widetilde{\bm{G}}_{0})&&\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\\
D_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}^{12}(-\widetilde{\bm{G}}_{6})&&&D_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}^{12}(\widetilde{\bm{G}}_{0})\end{bmatrix},

(22)

where
D^{ij}_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}(\widetilde{\bm{G}})
is a submatrix of the dynamical matrix at
\widetilde{\bm{G}}
with rows (columns) corresponding to degrees of freedom due to layer
i
(
j
). The
(i,j)
block thus represents phonon hoppings between reciprocal lattice sites
\bm{\widetilde{G}}_{i}
and
\bm{\widetilde{G}}_{j}
. We have disregarded a small number of first-shell terms above that do not live along the first row, first column, or the diagonal, after having verified that their inclusion negligibly affects the numerical results. That is, we ignore hoppings that do not include
\bm{\widetilde{G}}_{0}
. Similarly for the intralayer term,

\displaystyle D^{i}(\bm{\widetilde{k}})=\begin{bmatrix}D_{P}^{i}(\bm{\widetilde{k}}+\widetilde{\bm{G}}_{0})&D_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}^{ii}(\widetilde{\bm{G}}_{1})&\cdots&D_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}^{ii}(\widetilde{\bm{G}}_{6})\\
D_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}^{ii}(-\widetilde{\bm{G}}_{1})&D_{P}^{i}(\bm{\widetilde{k}}+\widetilde{\bm{G}}_{1})&&\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\\
D_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}^{ii}(-\widetilde{\bm{G}}_{6})&&&D_{P}^{i}(\bm{\widetilde{k}}+\widetilde{\bm{G}}_{6})\end{bmatrix},

(23)

where the diagonal terms are the dynamical matrix of the pristine monolayer
D^{i}_{P}(\bm{\widetilde{k}}+\bm{\widetilde{G}})
and the off-diagonal terms being the twist-dependent intralayer term constructed from Eq. (
20
) when
\ell=\ell^{\prime}
.

II.3
Dynamical Matrix in Configuration Space

We are particularly interested in structures with a small twist angle, which means the moiré cell contains a large number of primitive cells and consequently many atoms.
In order to make the model computationally feasible, we express
D_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}
in terms of a collection of local atomic environments, known as configuration space
Carr et al. 2018
.
Within the continuum approximation we replace the primitive cell lattice vectors
\mathbf{R}
with a continuous variable
\bm{r}
defined throughout the moiré cell, and allow quantities of interest to be defined at all
\bm{r}
. At each location
\bm{r}
the local environment can be approximated by an untwisted bilayer with a horizontal shift of one layer relative to the other. We specify the interlayer shift by
\bm{b}(\bm{r})
, and the space formed by all
\bm{b}
is configuration space. It is in one-to-one correspondence with real space by a linear transformation

\displaystyle\bm{b}(\bm{r})=(\mathbb{1}-A_{2}A_{1}^{-1})\bm{r},

(24)

where
A_{i}
is a matrix whose columns are the primitive lattice vectors of layer
i
and
\mathbb{1}
is the
2\times 2
identity matrix.
This correspondence also gives a useful relationship between the the reciprocal lattice vectors of the moiré cell,
\bm{\widetilde{G}}
, and those of the primitive unit cell,
\bm{G}
:

\displaystyle\bm{\widetilde{G}}\cdot\bm{r}=\bm{G}\cdot\bm{b}(\bm{r}).

(25)

Using these concepts, we can express the momentum-space dynamical matrix as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} D^{\text{rig}}_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}(\widetilde{\bm{k}}+\widetilde{\bm{G}})=&\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\Gamma^{*}|}}\int d^{2}\bm{r}\,\overline{D}_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}(\bm{r})\,e^{i(\tilde{\bm{k}}+\tilde{\bm{G}})\cdot\bm{r}}\\
=&\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\Gamma^{*}|}}\int d^{2}\bm{b}\,\overline{D}_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}(\bm{b})\,e^{i(\bm{k}+\bm{G})\cdot\bm{b}}\\
\approx&\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\Gamma^{*}|}}\frac{|\Gamma|}{N^{2}}\sum_{\bm{b}\in\mathcal{S}(N)}\overline{D}_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}^{(\bm{b})}\,e^{i(\bm{k}+\bm{G})\cdot\bm{b}},\end{aligned}

(26)

The factor of
|\Gamma|
, arising from discretization, cancels out the normalization from Eq. (
18
).
Configuration space allows us to replace
\overline{D}(\bm{r})
, which we cannot easily determine, with
\overline{D}(\bm{b})
, which we can readily compute with DFT for a primitive bilayer cell with any specified shift
\bm{b}
.
In the small
\theta
limit the local environment varies slowly and smoothly with
\bm{b}
, so we can approximate the integral over
\bm{b}
with the sum
over a
N\times N
uniform mesh
\mathcal{S}(N)
of
\bm{b}
values.
Since
\mathcal{S}(N)
is uniform,
D^{\text{rig}}_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}(\widetilde{\bm{k}}+\widetilde{\bm{G}})
describes a moiré bilayer with a uniform distribution of local configurations. In other words, it represents a rigid moiré structure with no twist-induced continuum relaxation, which is a good approximation for
\theta
sufficiently large.
We describe the procedure for including relaxation effects in the next subsection.

II.4
Continuum Relaxation

We emphasize that in all structures considered here atomic relaxation to the lowest-energy (equilibrium) configuration is included. However, its effect varies as a function of twist angle: for
moderate to small twist angles, twist-induced atomic relaxation
is much more important than for large twist angles. The range
of angles over which the atomic relaxation is important
varies by material, but relaxation typically begins to have a significant impact
for twist angles smaller than
about
2^{\circ}
. In real space, atomic relaxation results in enlarged areas of the lowest energy stacking configurations (AB and BA in graphene) at the expense of high energy stackings (AA), with domain walls forming in between.
In configuration space relaxation can be viewed as a
higher density of configurations in the lowest-energy stackings
Carr et al. 2018
;
Zhu et al. 2020
.
This can be accounted for by shifting each
\bm{b}
in the uniform mesh
\mathcal{S}(N)
by an angle-dependent relaxation displacement vector
\bm{u}_{\theta}(\bm{b})
, yielding
a non-uniform mesh
\bm{B}=\bm{b}+\bm{u}_{\theta}(\bm{b})
denoted
\mathcal{S}_{\theta}(N)
.
We use linear elasticity theory to describe the intralayer energy, combined with the generalized stacking fault energy (GSFE) functional for the interlayer energy. (The GSFE parameters are given in Appendix
A
.) By minimizing the total energy we can compute
\bm{u}_{\theta}(\bm{b})
in configuration space.
We refer to
Carr et al. 2018
and
Zhu et al. 2020
for a detailed overview of continuum relaxation.
The dynamical matrix can then be updated by Fourier interpolation, using an inverse transform

\displaystyle\overline{D}_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}^{(\bm{B})}(\bm{\widetilde{k}})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\Gamma^{*}|}}\sum_{\bm{\widetilde{G}}}D^{\text{rig}}_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}(\widetilde{\bm{k}}+\widetilde{\bm{G}})e^{-i(\bm{k}+\bm{G})\cdot\bm{B}},

(27)

followed by a second forward transform, which arrives at the following relaxed moiré dynamical matrix:

\displaystyle D_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}(\widetilde{\bm{k}}+\widetilde{\bm{G}})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\Gamma^{*}|}}\frac{|\Gamma|}{N^{2}}\sum_{\bm{b}\in\mathcal{S}(N)}\overline{D}_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}^{(\bm{B})}(\widetilde{\bm{k}})e^{i(\bm{k}+\bm{G})\cdot\bm{b}}.

(28)

To obtain the real space phonon vibration pattern, we perform an inverse Fourier transform by summing over components of the eigenvectors as follows,

\displaystyle\delta u_{n\ell\mu\alpha\widetilde{\bm{k}}}(\bm{r})=\sum_{\bm{\widetilde{G}}}\delta u_{n\ell\mu\alpha\widetilde{\bm{G}}}(\widetilde{\bm{k}})e^{i(\bm{\widetilde{G}}+\bm{\widetilde{k}})\cdot\bm{r}},

(29)

where
n
is the band index and
\delta u_{n\ell\mu\alpha\widetilde{\bm{G}}}
is the eigenvector component that corresponds to
\widetilde{\bm{G}}
.

II.5
Acoustic Sum Rules

Even though the total forces are fixed to be zero in the force field calculations of individual stackings, the combination of all configurations in the moiré dynamical matrix construction modifies the total force of the combined system.
Therefore, an acoustic sum of the moiré dynamical matrix is required to ensure the total force stays zero in addition to the sum rules on the unrotated bilayers and monolayers.
We use a correction matrix (chosen without loss of generality to be diagonal in the latter pair of indices)

\displaystyle C_{\mu\nu\alpha\alpha}=\sum_{\beta}\sqrt{\frac{M_{\beta}}{M_{\alpha}}}D_{\text{moir\'{e}},\mu\nu\beta\alpha}^{*}(\Gamma),

(30)

where
D_{\text{moir\'{e}},\mu\nu\beta\alpha}^{*}(\Gamma)
is the moiré dynamical matrix by replacing the pristine monolayer blocks
D_{P}(\bm{k}+\bm{\widetilde{G}})
with
D_{P}(\Gamma)
, as we enforce translational invariance only at the
\Gamma
point and enforce continuity everywhere else. We then adjust
D_{\text{moir\'{e}}}(\bm{\widetilde{k}})\leftarrow D_{\text{moir\'{e}}}(\bm{\widetilde{k}})-C
, observing numerically that the above approximation satisfies adequately the translational invariance constraint. The sum rule is adapted from
Quan et al. 2021
.

III
Model Validation

To validate our CSC model, we compare results obtained from it with two first-principles models, namely direct DFT on the moiré supercell for a small system where such calculations are feasible, and with molecular dynamics (MD) simulations that employ an interatomic potential
Angeli et al. 2019
;
Angeli et al. 2018
. We also compare our CSC results to another empirical continuum (EC) model adapted from
Koshino and Son 2019
.

III.1
Comparison with Molecular Dynamics and DFT

Direct DFT phonon calculations on the moiré cell are the most accurate but are restricted by the size of the cell. MD simulations allow for larger supercell sizes, but have inherent limitations on accuracy due to the choice of interatomic potential. Both direct DFT and MD are computationally expensive and require a commensurate supercell. Since the CSC model uses DFT inputs in a well-defined approximation, it should reflect the direct DFT and MD calculations reasonably well and does not suffer constraints based on moiré supercell size or commensurability.
We choose a twist angle,
\theta=7.34^{\circ}
, that gives a commensurate pattern in bilayer graphene for these comparisons.

IV
Moiré Phonon Properties

Using our CSC model, we calculate the phonon properties of three different moiré bilayers: graphene, MoS
2
, and a MoSe
2
-WSe
2
heterostructure.
Near the
\Gamma

IV.1
Low-energy mode frequencies

The phonon dispersion relations are qualitatively similar for twisted bilayer graphene and TMDCs. Here, we focus on graphene.
Figure
5
shows the graphene phonon bands at different twist angles
\theta
.
As
\theta
decreases, the frequency where folded clusters of phonon bands appear also decreases, due to the shrinking of the moiré Brillouin zone area.
Atomic relaxation due to interlayer interactions becomes more significant as the twist angle decrease, leading to three qualitatively different regimes characterized by the value of
\theta
:
a “decoupled” regime (large
\theta
), a “soft” regime (moderate
\theta
) and a “relaxed” regime (small
\theta
).
The exact values of
\theta
separating these regimes are specific to the material.
For graphene they are approximately
4.2^{\circ}
and
0.5^{\circ}
, respectively; for MoS
2
they are
7.6^{\circ}
and
1^{\circ}
; and for MoSe
2
-WSe
2
they are
5.5^{\circ}
and
1.0^{\circ}
.
In the decoupled regime (Fig.
5
(a)) the relatively large twist angle and small moiré scale means there is little atomic relaxation and the net interlayer coupling is very weak. Here, the S mode is slightly positive at
\Gamma
with twofold degeneracy (when the distinction between the two S modes becomes important, we shall refer to the lower-energy curve as S
1
and the higher-energy curve as S
2
), while the LB mode is essentially dispersionless. The zero-frequency modes at
\Gamma
are the three translational modes. In the soft regime (Fig.
5
(b)), where atomic relaxation starts to become more noticeable, the S mode begins to exhibit imaginary frequencies at the
\Gamma
point (plotted as negative values for continuity in the phonon bands). In the relaxed regime (Fig.
5
(d)) the low-energy stacking regions grow in size at the expense of the high-energy stacking regions. Thus, most of the atoms are in local energy minima and are therefore resistant to shearing. As a result, the shearing
mode frequency becomes positive again.

Such ultrasoft S modes have been discussed in previous work
Maity et al. 2020
. In the limit of small angles (large moiré cells) that are truly incommensurate, they are zero-modes at
\Gamma
that correspond to the invariance of the moiré pattern to a horizontal shift of one layer relative to the other
Bistritzer and MacDonald 2011
;
Ochoa 2019
. Given the complicated structure of the moiré dynamical matrix, the delicate numerical cancellations necessary to reproduce a (near) zero-mode are unlikely to occur in practice. For comparison, in standard phonon calculations the zero-modes (acoustic phonons, particularly the translational mode with
z
invariance in 2D materials) are corrected by imposing an acoustic sum rule that explicitly enforces translational invariance. As discussed previously, we impose such a rule in the CSC model as well, which is why there are no negative-frequency acoustic phonon bands. In the same spirit, to deal with the ultrasoft shearing modes one could impose a “shearing sum rule” to enforce this additional symmetry of moiré structures. Such a rule, however, is more subtle and involved than the acoustic sum rule, and we leave its imposition to future work. The imaginary frequencies are not a simple consequence of numerical sampling error or relaxation effects, as we demonstrate in Appendix
D
.

In the soft regime, the LB mode becomes dispersive, as seen in Fig.
5
(b)-(c), but progressively flattens out again as
\theta
approaches the relaxed regime, Fig.
5
(d). At
1.1^{\circ}

as the Brillouin zone shrinks (
5
(c)), and similarly for
\theta=0.4^{\circ}

IV.2
Real Space Atomic Displacements at the
\Gamma
Point

By performing the inverse Fourier transform of eigenmodes in reciprocal space (Eq. (
29
)), we obtain the atomic displacement field of each phonon mode in real space.
The eigenmodes in reciprocal space are unit vectors, but application of the Fourier transform gives the magnitude of the displacement vectors a physical interpretation.
Specifically, the overall magnitudes across various twist angles serve as an indicator for the magnitude of each eigenmode component
\delta\bm{u}(\widetilde{\bm{G}})
in reciprocal space. For if most of the magnitude in reciprocal space is concentrated in the
\widetilde{\bm{G}}_{0}
component, the real space magnitude will be large; but if most of the magnitude is concentrated in the first shell
\widetilde{\bm{G}}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{\bm{G}}_{6}
, the phases in the inverse Fourier transform reduce the real space magnitude to a much smaller quantity. In the absence of the moiré interlayer couplings, the magnitude in Fourier space should be concentrated in the
\widetilde{\bm{G}}_{0}
component, but at smaller values of
\theta
, emergence of periodicity of the moiré length scale imply that the magnitude becomes instead concentrated in higher shells. Thus, we should expect the magnitude in real space to fall substantially near the angle at which the magnitudes of the
\widetilde{\bm{G}}_{0}
component and the
\widetilde{\bm{G}}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{\bm{G}}_{6}
components intersect.

IV.3
Higher energy LB (LB
2
and LB
3

In addition to the lowest-energy LB mode analyzed above (which we now call LB
1
), we observe two LB modes of higher frequency.
The key characteristics of moiré LB modes are the oppositely oriented swirling in-plane motion and the opposite out-of-plane motion between each layer at every point
\bm{r}
; these are properties that all three LB modes exhibit.
However, in LB
1
, as shown in Fig.
9

This does not hold for the LB
2
and LB
3
modes for all
\theta

To quantify the uniformity in out-of-plane displacement direction, we define the following quantity:

\mathcal{U}_{\text{LB}}^{12}=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N^{2}}\delta_{\operatorname{sgn}[\delta\bm{u}_{z}^{(1)}(\bm{r}_{i})],1}\delta_{\operatorname{sgn}[\delta\bm{u}_{z}^{(2)}(\bm{r}_{i})],-1},

(31)

where
N
is the sampling grid size in the moiré supercell and
\delta\bm{u}_{z}^{(\ell)}(\bm{r}_{i})
is the
z
component of the phonon at point
\bm{r}_{i}
in layer
\ell
.
Equation (
31
) essentially estimates the fraction of area where
\delta u_{z}^{(1)}
points in the positive direction while
\delta u_{z}^{(2)}
points in the negative direction.
Since it does not matter which layer breathes in which out-of-plane direction, we define the breathing uniformity value
\mathcal{U}_{\text{LB}}=\max(\mathcal{U}_{\text{LB}}^{12},\mathcal{U}_{\text{LB}}^{21})
, which estimates the fraction of the moiré cell that breathes in the same out-of-plane direction. A translational mode with
z
invariance has
\mathcal{U}_{\text{LB}}=0
,
and a perfectly uniform breathing motion (such as that of LB
1
) has
\mathcal{U}_{\text{LB}}=1
. A maximally nonuniform breathing motion has
\mathcal{U}_{\text{LB}}=0.5
, which means that at each point, the field is equally likely to point in either direction.

V
Summary

We developed an accurate and efficient model for calculating phonon properties in bilayer moiré vdW heterostructures, which is based on the configuration space continuum (CSC) formalism. Within this formalism, we showed that phonons
related to moiré patterns in twisted bilayers can be calculated directly from ensembles of phonons related to rigidly shifted bilayers at various arrangementss, with first-principles accuracy.

By analyzing the low-frequency shearing (S) and layer breathing (LB) modes in three representative materials (twisted bilayer graphene, bilayer MoS
2
, and a MoSe
2
-WSe
2
heterostructure),
we showed that the physics of moiré-pattern phonons depend on the twist angle
\theta
.
Phonon frequencies vary slowly and smoothly with
\theta
, while the atomic displacement magnitude is more sensitive to small changes in twist angle near certain
\theta
values.
Our CSC model can be applied more generally to classes of bilayers beyond the three materials on which we focus here, such as bilayers that are oppositely oriented (the pristine bilayer has one layer rotated by
180^{\circ}
relative to the other) or Janus materials.

Despite its efficiency and accuracy, the CSC model is limited in its present form to lowest-frequency modes and negative shearing frequencies due to the approximations inherent in the model.
In the work presented here, we only expand the moiré dynamical matrix to the first shell of the moiré Brillouin zone. Increasing the cutoff radius would allow for the extension to higher frequency modes, although a new derivation of the acoustic sum rule is required.
Future work to broaden the scope of the CSC model may include introducing
\widetilde{\bm{k}}
dependence on the off-diagonal components of the moiré dynamical matrix in order to remove the low-frequency approximation.
Moreover, the CSC model may be applied to further investigate relatively low-energy phonon modes of interest, such as chiral phonons
Zhang and Niu 2015
;
Suri et al. 2021
;
Maity et al. 2022
.

Building upon previous experimental studies with Raman spectroscopy
Campos-Delgado et al. 2013
;
Wu et al. 2014
;
Huang et al. 2016
;
Lin et al. 2018
;
Quan et al. 2021
, the phonon features derived by our CSC model may be used to guide further experimental comparison for other materials.
The most direct application of the CSC model is as a phonon framework for a model of electron-phonon coupling in moiré materials.
Ultimately, such studies will provide a theoretical foundation for the origin of strongly correlated states in moiré systems, and the extent to which phonons play a role in such phenomena.

The GSFE functional measures the energy as a function of stacking configuration.
We evaluate the GSFE landscape

according to a first shell expansion from
Carr et al. 2018
. Since the Fourier expansion is written in coefficients of
e^{i\bm{G}\cdot\bm{b}}

\bm{G}
a reciprocal lattice vector of the bilayer at a configuration
\bm{b}
, denote for a Bravais lattice basis
\bm{a}_{1}=a_{0}(1,0),\bm{a}_{2}=a_{0}(1/2,\sqrt{3}/2)
:

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\mu\\
\nu\end{pmatrix}=\frac{2\pi}{a}\begin{bmatrix}1&-1/\sqrt{3}\\
0&2/\sqrt{3}\end{bmatrix}\bm{b}.

(32)

Using
120^{\circ}
rotational and

Appendix C
Empirical Continuum Model

One approach to obtaining moiré phonons is through an empirical continuum model based on the relaxation model introduced in
Carr et al. 2018
. The approach is introduced by
Koshino and Son 2019
; we briefly review and adapt it here.
To obtain the in-plane phonon modes, we perturb around the equilibrium positions after relaxation.
Here, we assume a homobilayer, for which the relaxation in layers 1 and 2 have symmetry
\bm{u}^{(1)}(\bm{r})=-\bm{u}^{(2)}(\bm{b})\equiv\bm{u}.

The equilibrium position is calculated by minimizing the total energy, which is the sum of the interlayer energy that is the integral of the GSFE in configuration space (Eq. (
33
)),

\displaystyle E^{\mathrm{inter}}(\bm{u})=\int V^{\mathrm{GSFE}}(\bm{u}+\bm{b})\,\differential^{2}\bm{b},

(34)

and the intralayer elastic energy:

\displaystyle E^{\mathrm{intra}}(\bm{u})

\displaystyle=2\int\Phi^{\mathrm{intra}}(\bm{u})\,\differential^{2}\bm{b},

(35)

with

\displaystyle\Phi^{\mathrm{intra}}(\bm{u})=\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{\bm{r}}\bm{u}(\bm{b}):C:\nabla_{\bm{r}}\bm{u}(\bm{b}),

(36)

where
\nabla_{\bm{r}}
is the real space gradient and
C
is the linear elasticity tensor.
For in-plane deformations,
C
is a rank-4 tensor with its components defined as follows:

\displaystyle C_{11ij}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{K}+\mathcal{G}&0\\
0&\mathcal{G}\end{pmatrix}\quad C_{12ij}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\mathcal{K}-\mathcal{G}\\
\mathcal{G}&0\end{pmatrix}\quad

(37)

\displaystyle C_{21ij}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\mathcal{G}\\
\mathcal{K}-\mathcal{G}&0\end{pmatrix}\quad C_{22ij}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{G}&0\\
0&\mathcal{K}+\mathcal{G}\end{pmatrix}.

A lattice vibration perturbs the equilibrium positions, leading to an additional kinetic energy contribution:

T=\sum_{\ell=1}^{2}\int\frac{\rho}{2}\left[\left(\dot{U}_{x}^{(\ell)}\right)^{2}+\left(\dot{U}_{y}^{(\ell)}\right)^{2}\right]\differential^{2}\bm{r},

(38)

where
\rho
is the area density of a single layer (
\rho=7.61\times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{kg/m^{2}}
for graphene),
\ell
labels the layer, and
\dot{U}^{(\ell)}_{\alpha}
is the time derivative of the cartesian component
\alpha=x,y
of
U
in layer
\ell
.

The Lagrangian of the system is given by
\mathcal{L}=T-(E^{\mathrm{intra}}+E^{\mathrm{inter}})
as a function of
\bm{U}^{(\ell)}(\bm{r})
. We define
\bm{U}^{\pm}=\bm{U}^{(2)}\pm\bm{U}^{(1)}
and rewrite
\mathcal{L}
as a function of
\bm{U}^{\pm}
.
Let us rewrite the GSFE from Eq. (
33
) in a more compact form:

\displaystyle V^{\mathrm{GSFE}}(\bm{b})=c_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{3}\sum_{n=1}^{3}c_{j}\cos(\vec{G}_j^n \cdot\vec{b}),

(39)

where
\bm{G}_{j}^{n}
are the monolayer reciprocal lattice vectors defined in Fig.
14
. Here, the subscript
j
labels the shell (vectors that are the same distance from the origin) and the superscript
n
labels the element within the shell.
For simplicity we assume symmetry between AB and BA stacking and only consider the cosine components of the GSFE here (
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}
), but the following results can be easily generalized to include the sine components (
c_{4},c_{5},c_{6}
).

The equations of motion for
\bm{U}^{-}
are given as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{1}{2}\rho\ddot{U}_{x}^{-}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathcal{K}+\frac{\mathcal{G}}{3}\right)\left(\frac{\partial^{2}U_{x}^{-}}{\partial x^{2}}+\frac{\partial^{2}{U_{y}^{-}}}{\partial{x}\partial{y}}\right)+\frac{\mathcal{G}}{2}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}{U_{x}^{-}}}{\partial{x}^{2}}+\frac{\partial^{2}{U_{x}^{-}}}{\partial{y}^{2}}\right)+\sum_{j=1}^{3}\sum_{n=1}^{3}G_{j,x}^{n}c_{j}\sin(\vec{G}_j^n \cdot(\vec{b} + \vec{u^{-}}))\\
\frac{1}{2}\rho\ddot{U}_{y}^{-}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathcal{K}+\frac{\mathcal{G}}{3}\right)\left(\frac{\partial^{2}U_{y}^{-}}{\partial y^{2}}+\frac{\partial^{2}{U_{x}^{-}}}{\partial{x}\partial{y}}\right)+\frac{\mathcal{G}}{2}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}{U_{y}^{-}}}{\partial{x}^{2}}+\frac{\partial^{2}{U_{y}^{-}}}{\partial{y}^{2}}\right)+\sum_{j=1}^{3}\sum_{n=1}^{3}G_{j,y}^{n}c_{j}\sin(\vec{G}_j^n \cdot(\vec{b} + \vec{u^{-}})),\end{aligned}

(40)

where
G_{j,\alpha}^{n}
is the
\alpha=x,y
component of
\bm{G}_{j}^{n}
.
Note that
\bm{U}(\bm{r})
is the real space displacement and
\bm{u}(\bm{b})
is the configuration space displacement and they are related by the linear transformation in Eq. (
24
).
The terms on the 2nd lines of Eq. (
40
) that are proportional to
c_{j}
come from the GSFE, which depends only on
\bm{U}^{-}
, the difference between relaxation in layer 1 and layer 2, and not on
\bm{U}^{+}
.
Thus the equations of motion for
\bm{U}^{+}
are given by replacing
\bm{U}^{-}
with
\bm{U}^{+}
and dropping the terms proportional to
c_{j}
.

39
). where the subscript labels different shells (different colors) and the superscript labels different components within the same shell (same color). The gray scatter points are reciprocal lattice vectors.

Because
\bm{U}^{+}
represents equal motion in both layers, its fluctuations yield the acoustic phonons of a monolayer.

To determine the moiré phonons we study
\bm{U}^{-}
and consider a small vibration around the static equilibrium relaxation:

\displaystyle\bm{U}^{-}(\bm{r},t)=\bm{U}_{0}^{-}(\bm{r})+\delta\bm{U}^{-}(\bm{r},t),

(41)

where
\bm{U}_{0}^{-}
is the static relaxation that minimizes the total energy,
E^{\mathrm{intra}}+E^{\mathrm{inter}}
, and
\delta\bm{U}^{-}(\bm{r},t)
is the perturbation around
\bm{U}_{0}^{-}
, which constitutes the phonon modes.
We define the following Fourier coefficients to express the displacement and equations of motion in Fourier space:

\displaystyle\bm{U}_{0}^{-}(\bm{r})=\sum_{\bm{G}}\bm{U}_{0,\widetilde{\bm{G}}}e^{i\widetilde{\bm{G}}\cdot\bm{r}},

\displaystyle\delta\bm{U}^{-}(\bm{r},t)=e^{-i\omega t}\sum_{\bm{q}}\delta\bm{U}^{-}_{\bm{q}}e^{i\bm{q}\cdot\bm{r}},

\displaystyle\sin(\vec{G}_j^n \cdot(\vec{b} + \vec{u^{-}}))=\sum_{\widetilde{\bm{G}}}f_{j,\bm{G}}^{n}e^{i\widetilde{\bm{G}}\cdot\bm{r}},

\displaystyle\cos(\vec{G}_j^n \cdot(\vec{b} + \vec{u^{-}}))=\sum_{\widetilde{\bm{G}}}h_{j,\bm{G}}^{n}e^{i\widetilde{\bm{G}}\cdot\bm{r}},

(42)

where
\widetilde{\bm{G}}
is the moiré reciprocal lattice vector. Note that
\widetilde{\bm{G}}\cdot\bm{r}=\bm{G}\cdot\bm{b}
, where
\bm{G}
is the monolayer reciprocal lattice vector. We expand Eq. (
40
) around the static solution
\bm{U}_{0}
to the first order in
\delta\bm{U}
and perform a Fourier expansion to obtain the equation of motion for the perturbation part:

\rho\omega^{2}\delta\bm{U}^{-}_{\widetilde{\bm{G}}+\bm{q}}=\widehat{K}_{\widetilde{\bm{G}}+\bm{q}}\delta\bm{U}^{-}_{\widetilde{\bm{G}}+\bm{q}}-4\sum_{\widetilde{\bm{G}^{\prime}}}\widehat{V}_{\widetilde{\bm{G}}-\widetilde{\bm{G}}^{\prime}}\delta\bm{U}^{-}_{\widetilde{\bm{G}^{\prime}}+\bm{q}},

(43)

where

\widehat{K}_{\widetilde{\bm{G}}}=\begin{pmatrix}(\mathcal{K}+\frac{4}{3}\mathcal{G})G_{x}^{2}+\mathcal{G}G_{y}^{2}&(\mathcal{K}+\frac{\mathcal{G}}{3})G_{x}G_{y}\\
(\mathcal{K}+\frac{\mathcal{G}}{3})G_{x}G_{y}&(\mathcal{K}+\frac{4}{3}\mathcal{G})G_{y}^{2}+\mathcal{G}G_{x}^{2}\end{pmatrix}

(44)

and

\widehat{V}_{\widetilde{\bm{G}}}=\sum_{j=1}^{3}c_{j}\sum_{n=1}^{3}h_{j,\widetilde{\bm{G}}}^{n}\begin{pmatrix}G_{j,x}^{n}G_{j,x}^{n}&G_{j,x}^{n}G_{j,y}^{n}\\
G_{j,x}^{n}G_{j,y}^{n}&G_{j,y}^{n}G_{j,y}^{n}\end{pmatrix}.

(45)

Here, the
\widehat{V}_{\widetilde{\bm{G}}-\widetilde{\bm{G}}^{\prime}}
is essentially an interlayer scattering selection rule that couples
\widetilde{\bm{G}}
and
\widetilde{\bm{G}}^{\prime}

After obtaining the equilibrium relaxation displacement vectors
\bm{U}_{0}^{-}
self-consistently
Carr et al. 2018
, we solve the eigenvalue problem in Eq. (
43
) to obtain the eigenmodes
\omega^{2}
.

Appendix D
Further Discussion of the Soft Regime

A natural question from the analysis of the CSC model is whether the imaginary shearing frequencies in the soft regime are simply due to sampling issues. In this section we provide evidence that this is not the case.

Figure 15:
Eigenvalues
\omega^{2}
of the dynamical matrix of MoS
2
at
2.0^{\circ}
for two configuration space sampling densities,
9\times 9
and
12\times 12
.

Our calculations in the continuum approach have used a
9\times 9
discretization of configuration space. Thus, one might imagine the possibility that this discretization was not sufficiently dense to cover enough configurations, and caused numerical error that led to soft modes. However, as Fig.
15
illustrates for bilayer MoS
2
with a representative twist angle of
\theta=2.0^{\circ}
, an improved sampling grid of
12\times 12
does not significantly increase the negative eigenvalues; in fact, if we were to plot the values of
\omega
, that is, the square root of the results shown in Fig.
15
, the difference is visually indistinguishable.

Figure 16:
Updated configurations of MoS
2
at
\theta=2.0^{\circ}
after continuum relaxation.

Moreover, as mentioned earlier, relaxation cannot alone fix the softness of the shearing modes. For a twist angle of
2.0^{\circ}
the relaxation produces only very small changes to the configuration vectors
\bm{b}
, as shown in Fig.
16
. Hence, the problem of ultrasoft shearing modes in the soft regime is a problem more subtle than that of sampling or relaxation in configuration space.
