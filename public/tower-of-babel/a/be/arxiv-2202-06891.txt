Counterfactual inference in sequential experiments
Raaz Dwivedi, Katherine Tian, Sabina Tomkins, Predrag Klasnja, Susan Murphy, Devavrat Shah

Abstract
We consider after-study statistical inference for sequentially designed experiments wherein multiple units are assigned treatments for multiple time points using treatment policies that adapt over time. Our goal is to provide inference guarantees for the counterfactual mean at the smallest possible scale—mean outcome under different treatments for each unit and each time —with minimal assumptions on the adaptive treatment policy. Without any structural assumptions on the counterfactual means, this challenging task is infeasible due to more unknowns than observed data points. To make progress, we introduce a latent factor model over the counterfactual means that serves as a non-parametric generalization of the non-linear mixed effects model and the bilinear latent factor model considered in prior works. For estimation, we use a non-parametric method, namely a variant of nearest neighbors, and establish a non-asymptotic high probability error bound for the counterfactual mean for each unit and each time. Under regularity conditions, this bound leads to asymptotically valid confidence intervals for the counterfactual mean as the number of units and time points grows to ∞ \infty together at suitable rates. We illustrate our theory via several simulations and a case study involving data from a mobile health clinical trial HeartSteps.

This work is motivated by the scientific desires of performing multiple primary and secondary analyses after-study

2.1
Sequential policy

First, we describe how the treatments are assigned in the sequential experiment. Let
\uppi_{t}
denote the treatment policy at time
t
that generates the treatments
\{A_{i,t}\}_{i=1}^{N}
. We use

\mathcal{F}_{t\!-\!1}\triangleq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{Y_{i,t^{\prime}},A_{i,t^{\prime}},i\in[N],t^{\prime}\in[t\!-\!1]}}\right\}

\{\uppi_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
are sequentially adaptive, and in fact the dependence of the policy can be arbitrary on the past data:

Assumption 1

(Sequential policy)
.

The treatment policy
\uppi_{t}
at time
t
is measurable with respect to the sigma-algebra,
\mathcal{F}_{t\!-\!1}
, where
\uppi_{t,i}(a)=\mathbb{P}(A_{i,t}=a|\mathcal{F}_{t\!-\!1})
for unit
i\in[N]
and treatment
a\in\mathcal{A}
. Conditioned on
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
, the variables
\{A_{i,t}\}_{i=1}^{N}
are drawn independently.
Moreover, for
a\in\mathcal{A}
, let
\{\underline{\uppi}_{a,t}\}_{t=1}^{\infty}
denote a sequence of non-increasing scalars (that can decay to
0
) such that

\displaystyle\inf_{t^{\prime}\leq t,i\in[N]}\!\uppi_{t^{\prime},i}(a)\geq\underline{\uppi}_{a,t}\quad\text{almost surely for $t\in[T]$.}\quad

(3)

1
states that the treatments at time
t
are assigned based on a policy that depends on the data observed till time
t\!-\!1
; and conditional on the history, the treatments are independently assigned at time
t
. Notably
1
puts no restrictions on how the policy is updated, e.g., it could have pooled the data across units, it could be based on bandit algorithms like Thompson sampling,
\epsilon
-greedy, multiplicative weights, softmax etc, or it can come from a non-Markovian algorithm that might use past data in a non-trivial way. The term
\underline{\uppi}_{a,t}
captures the rate of decay in the minimum exploration of the underlying sampling policy. E.g., an
\epsilon
-greedy or pooled
\epsilon
-greedy based treatment policy, with

as
\epsilon_{t}

\underline{\uppi}_{a,t}=\frac{\epsilon_{t}}{|\mathcal{A}|}

, for the treatment being sending a notification (or not)—whether or not user

Bounded noise

Assumption 2

(i.i.d zero mean noise)

The noise

\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{i,t}^{(a)}]=0

2.2
Non-parametric factor model on counterfactuals

We impose a non-linear factor model on the collection of mean counterfactual parameters
\Theta
. Our model involves two sets of
d
-dimensional latent factors—
\mathcal{U}\triangleq\{u_{i}^{(a)},a\in\mathcal{A},i\in[N]\}
, and
\mathcal{V}\triangleq\{v_{t}^{(a)},a\in\mathcal{A},t\in[T]\}
. In particular, we assume that in the study, given a treatment
a\in\mathcal{A}
, for each unit we can associate a latent unit factor
u_{i}^{(a)}
, and with each time we can associate a latent time factor
v_{t}^{(a)}
, such that conditioned on these latent factors

\displaystyle\theta_{i,t}^{(a)}=f^{(a)}(u_{i}^{(a)},v_{t}^{(a)}),

(4)

for some
fixed but unknown
function
f^{(a)}
. For the sequential experiments motivating our work, the unit latent factors can account for the unobserved unit-specific traits, while the latent time factors encode time-specific causes that might correspond, e.g., to unobserved societal changes. Our model allows the non-linearity, the unit and time factors to vary treatment
a
.
Putting this together with
Eq.

2
, we conclude that our counterfactual model is given by

\displaystyle Y_{i,t}^{(a)}=f^{(a)}(u_{i}^{(a)},v_{t}^{(a)})+\varepsilon_{i,t}^{(a)}.

(5)

In particular,
u_{i}^{(a)}
, and
v_{t}^{(a)}
capture the heterogeneity of counterfactuals for a given treatment
a
across units and time respectively, while
f^{(a)}
allows for a different non-linear function across treatments.
We highlight that
Eq.

5
is a flexible non-parametric model as it does not assume the knowledge of the non-linear function
f^{(a)}
and puts no parametric assumption on the distributions of latent factors and the noise variables. This functional form generalizes the classical (i) non-linear mixed effects model, and (ii) bilinear latent factor model in causal panel data, which we now discuss.

2.2.1
Generalization of prior models

We first discuss how
Eq.

5
generalizes a class of non-linear mixed effects models, followed by the bilinear factor model in causal panel data.

Relation with non-linear mixed effects model

Our non-linear latent factor model
Eq.

5
is a (statistical) model-free generalization of non-linear mixed effect model with no observed covariates. For such a model, we note that the corresponding equation for the counterfactuals is given by
[
50
, Eqn. 2.1]
,
[
55
, Eqn. 3.1]
):

\displaystyle Y_{i,t}^{(a)}=g(\beta,\widetilde{u_{i}}^{(a)},t,a)+\varepsilon_{i,t}^{(a)}.

(6)

Here the function
g
takes a
known
non-linear functional form and is parameterized by the unknown parameter
\beta
, also called the
fixed effect
;
\widetilde{u_{i}}^{(a)}
is a zero mean random variable specific to unit
i
and treatment
a
, and is referred to as unit-specific
random effect
assumed be drawn i.i.d from a known family of distributions (typically Gaussian distribution with unknown variance); and
\varepsilon_{i,t}^{(a)}
denotes i.i.d mean zero noise, that is also typically assumed to be Gaussian with unknown variance. The noise and random effects are assumed to be drawn independent of each other. Some common examples of
Eq.

6
include the univariate linear mixed effects model:

\displaystyle g(\beta,\widetilde{u_{i}}^{(a)},t,a)=\beta_{a}+\widetilde{u_{i}}^{(a)}+\widetilde{v_{t}}^{(a)},

(7)

where the scalars
\widetilde{v_{t}}^{(a)}
capture the variation of
g
with time
t
and
a
. A multivariate linear mixed effects model is given by

\displaystyle g(\beta,\widetilde{u_{i}}^{(a)},t,a)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u_{i}^{(a)},v_{t}^{(a)}}}\right\rangle,

(8)

where
u_{i}^{(a)}
denotes the unit-specific random effect, and
v_{t}^{(a)}
denotes the time-specific random effect.
We highlight that
Eq.

7
can be obtained as a special case of
Eq.

8
by choosing
u_{i}^{(a)}\leftarrow(\beta_{a},\widetilde{u_{i}}^{(a)},1)^{\top}
, and
v_{t}^{(a)}\leftarrow(1,1,\widetilde{v_{t}}^{(a)})^{\top}
. For these models, the quantities of interest include the fixed effect (parameter)
\beta
, and the unit-specific random effect (random variable)
\widetilde{u_{i}}^{(a)}
for each unit
i
. Typical estimation strategies include Expectation-Maximization for maximum likelihood for estimating
\beta
, followed by empirical Bayes for estimating
\widetilde{u_{i}}^{(a)}
when the random effects and noise are Gaussian; or Markov chain Monte Carlo for random effects and noise from a general parametric family
[
32
,
55
]
(see the book
[
24
]
for a thorough treatment for random effects models).

Tradeoff between assumptions and estimands

Given the discussion above, we conclude that our model
Eq.

5
is a generalization of the non-linear mixed effects model
Eq.

6
since we assume
no knowledge
of (i) the non-linear function
f^{(a)}
, (ii) the distribution of
u_{i}^{(a)}
, and (iii) the distribution of noise
\varepsilon_{i,t}^{(a)}
. We tradeoff this increase in the flexibility of the model with a restriction on the quantities of interest from the unknown parameter and random effects to the conditional mean of counterfactuals. E.g., in the setting
Eq.

7
instead of estimating each of the (fixed and random) effects
\beta,\widetilde{u_{i}}^{(a)},\widetilde{v_{t}}^{(t)}
, we directly estimate the unit-time level conditional mean
\beta_{a}+\widetilde{u_{i}}^{(a)}+\widetilde{v_{t}}^{(a)}
. Note that this change of estimand also implies that our latent factors are not necessarily mean zero. This tradeoff in the favor of a weaker assumptions with a weaker estimand is motivated by our main goal in this paper, namely estimating the unit
\times
time-level treatment effect. This choice is well-suited for numerous applications in healthcare, behavioral science and social science, where the functional form of treatment effect is not known due to the lack of well-established mechanistic models between the unobserved random effects and the observed outcomes. (In the mobile health study considered later in this work, the functional form of relationship between the unobserved user traits and the step-counts over time is not well-understood.)

Relation with causal panel data

We note that special cases of model
Eq.

5
have appeared in prior work in panel data settings, synthetic control and synthetic interventions with a bilinear
f^{(a)}
and shared unit latent factors across treatments. In particular, the synthetic control literature
[
1
,
53
,
5
]
and the causal panel data literature
[
6
,
10
]
imposes a bilinear factor model as the counterfactual means under control (treatment
0
) as
\theta_{i,t}^{(0)}=\langle u_{i},v_{t}\rangle
as their primary estimands are the control counterfactuals
\theta_{i,t}^{(0)}
. On the other hand, in synthetic interventions
[
3
]
, all counterfactuals are modeled via bilinear factor model with shared unit latent factors across treatments, that is,
\theta_{i,t}^{(a)}=\langle u_{i},v_{t}^{(a)}\rangle
. Thus our model
Eq.

5
is a non-linear generalization of the counterfactual mean model assumed in these works.

We finally state our non-parametric model as a formal assumption for ease of reference throughout the paper. For simplifying the presentation of results for the most of the paper, we state most of our guarantees primarily for a bilinear latent factor model. We highlight that this assumption is
not
necessary for our analysis and we present a more general result for the non-linear factor model with Lipschitz
f^{(a)}
in
Section

4.1
.

Assumption 3

(Latent factorization of counterfactuals)
.

Conditioned on the latent factors
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{V}
, the mean counterfactuals satisfy
\theta_{i,t}^{(a)}\!\triangleq\mathbb{E}[Y_{i,t}^{(a)}|\mathcal{U},\mathcal{V}]=\!\langle u_{i}^{(a)},v_{t}^{(a)}\rangle
for
a\in\mathcal{A}
.

We make a final remark before describing the overall data generating mechanism.

Remark 1

(Dimensionality reduction)
.

The factorization assumption
Eq.

5
implicitly serves the purpose of dimensionality reduction as it reduces the effective degrees of freedom for our counterfactual inference task with
N_{T}
observed outcomes of estimating
|\mathcal{A}|NT

\Theta
to estimating
|\mathcal{A}|(N+T)d
unknowns in
(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{V})
. Throughout this work, we treat the dimension

get large.

2.2.2
Distributional assumptions on latent factors, and data generating mechanism

Next, we state assumptions on how the latent factors are generated—put simply, we assume that all the latent factors are exogenous and drawn i.i.d. For simplicity, we also assume that the latent factors are bounded, and discuss how our results can be easily extended to the setting with sub-Gaussian latent factors in
Section

G.1
.

Assumption 4

(i.i.d unit latent factors)
.

The unit latent factors
\mathcal{U}\triangleq\{u_{i}^{(a)},a\in\mathcal{A}\}_{i=1}^{N}
are exogenous, and drawn i.i.d from a distribution with bounded support and mean not necessarily zero, and
\|{u_{i}^{(a)}}\|_{2}\leq c_{u}
a.s.

Assumption 5

(i.i.d latent time factors)
.

The time latent factors
\mathcal{V}\triangleq\{v_{t}^{(a)},a\in\mathcal{A}\}_{t=1}^{T}
are exogenous, and for each
a\in\mathcal{A}
, they are drawn i.i.d from a distribution with bounded support and mean not necessarily zero,
\|v_{t}^{(a)}\|_{2}\leq c_{v}
a.s., and
\Sigma_{v}^{(a)}\triangleq\mathbb{E}[v_{t}^{(a)}(v_{t}^{(a)})^{\top}]\succeq\lambda_{a}\mathbf{I}_{d}
for some constants
c_{v},\lambda_{a}>0
.

Before discussing
4
and
5
, we note that putting together
1
,
2
,
5
,
4
, and
3
imply the following data generating mechanism:

1.

Generate
N|\mathcal{A}|
i.i.d latent factors
\mathcal{U}
,
T|\mathcal{A}|
i.i.d time latent factors
\mathcal{V}
and
NT
i.i.d noise variables
\{\varepsilon_{i,t}^{(a)}\}
, independently of each other. (These random variables determine the
NT|\mathcal{A}|
counterfactual mean parameters
\Theta
, and the counterfactual outcomes in
Eq.

5
.)

2.

Initialize the treatment policy at time
t=1
to some vector
\uppi_{1}\in[0,1]^{N}
.

3.

For
t=1,2,\ldots,T
:

(i)

Generate treatments
A_{i,t}\sim\mathrm{Multinomial}(\uppi_{t,i})
, independently across all units
i\in[N]
.

(ii)

Observe noisy outcomes
\{Y_{i,t}=\theta^{(A_{i,t})}_{i,t}+\varepsilon_{i,t}^{(a)}\}_{i=1}^{N}
are observed for all units
i\in[N]
.

(iii)

Update the treatment policy to
\uppi_{t+1}\in[0,1]^{N}
using all the observed data so far.

This mechanism serves as a simplified but representative setting for several sequential experiments, especially the ones on digital platforms (like mobile health, online education).

Now we discuss
4
and
5
. The i.i.d assumption on the unit latent factors (
4
) is a reasonable assumption when the units in the experiment are drawn in an i.i.d manner from some superpopulation. This assumption is standard in the literature on experiments (as well as its analog that the unit-specific random effects are drawn i.i.d in the literature on mixed-effects models). Such a distributional assumption on the unit factors allows us to easily characterize the neighborhood (number of neighbors, smoothness in how that number varies) around a fixed unit factor as a function of the distance from that unit factor. These distributional assumptions can be relaxed and instead regularity conditions that provide a tight control on the number of neighbors as a function of radius.

The i.i.d assumption on the time latent factors however can be a bit restrictive, as the exogenous time-varying causes might be correlated. We discuss possible extensions to such settings in
Section

G.3

2.2.3
Estimands and terminology

Our main goal is estimating the unit
\times
time-level counterfactual mean
\theta_{i,t}^{(a)}
(conditioned on
u_{i}^{(a)},v_{t}^{(a)}
) for an arbitrary tuple
(i,t,a)
, and we are interested in quantifying non-asymptotic performance of our estimate as well as constructing asymptotically valid prediction intervals for the estimand.
Our secondary goal is to estimate the population-level counterfactual mean—which due to
3
can be rewritten as
\bar{\theta}^{(a)}\triangleq\mathbb{E}[u_{i}^{\top}v_{t}^{(a)}]
for
a\in\mathcal{A}
—and establish asymptotic consistency of the estimate and asymptotic validity of the constructed confidence intervals. Note that while
\theta_{i,t}^{(a)}
is a random variable akin to the unit
\times
time-level random effect in
Eq.

7
,
\bar{\theta}^{(a)}
is a parameter akin to the fixed effect in
Eq.

7
. Consequently, we use the terms
prediction interval
and
confidence interval
for the intervals constructed to quantify the uncertainty in the estimation of
\theta_{i,t}^{(a)}
, and
\bar{\theta}^{(a)}
respectively. Moreover, it is conventional to refer estimating the random variable
\theta_{i,t}^{(a)}
as a prediction problem, and the corresponding estimate as a predictor. However, for consistency in the language, we continue to use the terms
estimation
and
inference
for our task of estimating both these quantities, and quantifying the associated uncertainty.

2.3
Algorithm

We solve the task at hand via a non-parameteric method, namely a variant of nearest neighbors. To estimate
\theta_{i,t}^{(a)}
for a given tuple
(i,t,a)
, our nearest neighbors estimate is constructed in two simple steps: (i) use all the available data under treatment
a
to estimate a set of nearest neighbors for
i
, and (ii) compute an average of the observations across these neighbors that also have
A_{i,t}=a
. To perform step (i), we make use of a hyperparameter
\eta
that needs to tuned (that we discuss in
Section

F
). We now state the algorithm for construction our estimates and uncertainty intervals.

2.3.1
Estimates and uncertainty quantification

We start with the unit level estimates, and then describe the population-level estimates.

Estimate and prediction interval for
\theta_{i,t}^{(a)}

For any
i,j\in[N]
and
a\in\mathcal{A}
define

\displaystyle\rho_{i,j,a}\triangleq\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{s=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}(A_{i,s}=a)\mathbf{1}(A_{j,s}=a)(Y_{i,s}\!-\!Y_{j,s})^{2}}{\sum_{s=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}(A_{i,s}=a)\mathbf{1}(A_{j,s}=a)}.

(9)

In words,
\rho_{i,j,a}
denotes the mean squared distance of observed outcomes over time points when both unit
i
and
j
were assigned treatment
a
. Next, the set of available nearest neighbors for the tuple
(i,t,a,\eta)
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathbf{N}_{i,t,a,\eta}\triangleq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{j\in[N]\backslash\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{i}}\right\}\ \big|\ \rho_{i,j,a}\leq\eta,A_{j,t}=a}}\right\}.

(10)

Denote
N_{i,t,a,\eta}=|\mathbf{N}_{i,t,a,\eta}|
. Our estimate for
\theta_{i,t}^{(a)}
is given by a simple outcome average:

\displaystyle\ \text{if}\ N_{i,t,a,\eta}>0\quad\widehat{\theta}_{i,t,\eta}^{(a)}\triangleq\displaystyle\frac{1}{N_{i,t,a,\eta}}\sum_{j\in\mathbf{N}_{i,t,a,\eta}}Y_{j,t}.

(11)

When the number of available neighbors
N_{i,t,a,\eta}=0
, the estimate is as follows:

\displaystyle\ \text{if}\ A_{i,t}=a\quad\widehat{\theta}_{i,t,\eta}^{(a)}=Y_{i,t},\quad\text{else}\quad\widehat{\theta}_{i,t,\eta}^{(a)}=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}(A_{i,t}=a)Y_{j,t}}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}(A_{i,t}=a)}.

(12)

That is, when
N_{i,t,a,\eta}=0
, we return the observed outcome if
A_{i,t}=a
, else we simply return the averaged over all observed outcomes corresponding to treatment
a
at time
t
. Notably, our guarantees are established for
Eq.

11
(and not
Eq.

12
), and we establish regularity conditions such that
N_{i,t,a,\eta}=0
happens with small probability.
2
2

2

While theoretically unit
i
is allowed to be its own neighbor, in practice we do not include it since the test/validation error in real settings is measured with respect to held out observations and is severely impacted if we allow the unit to be its own neighbor.
Notably, the estimates above do not require any knowledge of the sequential policy
\uppi_{t}
although when the policy is known, one can instead consider an inverse-propensity-weighted version (see
Section

G.3
).

Next, we construct the prediction interval for
\theta_{i,t}^{(a)}
conditioned on
(u_{i}^{(a)},v_{t}^{(a)})
. For a given level
\alpha\!>\!0
, our estimate for the
(1\!-\!\alpha)
confidence interval for
\theta_{i,t}^{(a)}
is provided only if

and is given by

\displaystyle\bigg(\widehat{\theta}_{i,t,\eta}^{(a)}\!\!-\!\!\frac{z_{\alpha/2}\,\widehat{\sigma}_{a}}{\sqrt{N_{i,t,a,\eta}}},\,\,\widehat{\theta}_{i,t,\eta}^{(a)}\!\!+\!\!\frac{z_{\alpha/2}\,\widehat{\sigma}_{a}}{\sqrt{N_{i,t,a,\eta}}}\bigg),

(13)

where
z_{\frac{\alpha}{2}}
denotes the
1\!-\!\frac{\alpha}{2}
quantile of standard normal random variable, and
\widehat{\sigma}_{a}^{2}
is the estimated noise variance (see
Section

F
).

Estimate and confidence interval for
\bar{\theta}^{(a)}

Our estimate for the counterfactual mean averaged over all units and time is

\displaystyle\hat{\bar{\theta}}^{(a)}_{\eta}\!\triangleq\!\frac{1}{NT}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big(\mathbf{1}(A_{i,t}=a)Y_{i,t}\!+\!\mathbf{1}(A_{i,t}\neq a)\widehat{\theta}_{i,t,\eta}^{(a)}\big).

(14)

Finally, to construct the
(1-\alpha)
confidence interval for
\bar{\theta}^{(a)}
, we randomly sub-sample
K
indices (denoted by
\mathbf{K}
) from the set
[N]\times[T]
and construct the interval

\displaystyle\bigg(\hat{\bar{\theta}}^{(a)}_{\eta,K}\!-\!\frac{z_{\alpha/2}\,\widehat{\sigma}_{a,\mathbf{K}}}{\sqrt{K}},\,\,\hat{\bar{\theta}}^{(a)}_{\eta,K}\!+\!\frac{z_{\alpha/2}\,\widehat{\sigma}_{a,\mathbf{K}}}{\sqrt{K}}\bigg),\quad\text{where}\quad

(15)

\displaystyle\hat{\bar{\theta}}^{(a)}_{\eta,K}\!\triangleq\!\frac{1}{K}\!\!\sum_{(i,t)\in\mathbf{K}}\!\!\widehat{\theta}_{i,t,\eta}^{(a)}\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{a,\mathbf{K}}\!\triangleq\!\frac{1}{K\!-\!1}\!\!\sum_{(i,t)\in\mathbf{K}}\!\!(\widehat{\theta}_{i,t,\eta}^{(a)}\!-\!\hat{\bar{\theta}}^{(a)}_{\eta,K})^{2}.

(16)

3.2.1
Robustness of (vanilla) nearest neighbors

Recall that our nearest neighbors algorithm is both model and policy agnostic, i.e., it requires no knowledge of the latent factor model (and dimension
d
) and the treatment assignment policies. Yet our guarantees apply to a wide range of factor models and sequential policies.
Overall
Theorems

3.1
and
1
(and the results and experiments in the sequel), perhaps surprisingly, immediately reveal two remarkable robustness properties of nearest neighbors.

First, as long as the policies have some sufficient exploration (which can decay over time) and the neighborhood around the unit in consideration is smooth, (vanilla) nearest neighbors already provides non-trivial estimates. Such a property is revealed by two technical innovations in our proof: (i) a martingale-based analysis to show the concentration of distance and (ii) a careful sandwich argument to show that the neighbors estimated at time
t
are not too biased despite using observations from time points
>t
.
Section

3.2.2
elaborates the bias-variance tradeoff as well as the additional tradeoff introduced by the bias arising due to adaptivity as a function of
\eta
. Also, see
Section

3.5
for a sketch of the technical argument that allows us to establish a non-trivial error for the nearest neighbors.

Second, note that while the guarantees in
Corollary

1
depend on the latent dimension
d
of the factors, running the NN algorithm requires no knowledge about
d
. Put simply,
Theorems

3.1
and
1
demonstrate that the error guarantee of NN is adaptive to the underlying smoothness structures in the latent factors. In practice, while cross-validation is an easy way to gauge the performance of NN, one may additionally do a spectral value decomposition (see
Sections

5.2
and
3
) of the outcome matrix and check if the eigenvalues decay fast enough to provide a sanity check on whether or not

can be assumed to be small.

3.2.2
The bias-variance and adaptivity tradeoff in
Theorem

3.1

The general error bound
Eq.

19
comprises of four terms: (i) The first term of order
\eta-2\sigma_{a}^{2}
denotes the squared bias arising due to the threshold hyper-parameter
\eta
and it decays to

as
\eta
decreases to the noise variance
2\sigma_{a}^{2}
. (ii) The second term scales as
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(1/(\underline{\uppi}_{a,T}\sqrt{T}))
and denotes the second source of bias arising due to imperfect estimation of distance
\rho_{i,j,a}
between two units using
common treatment points that are lower bounded with high probability by
\underline{\uppi}_{a,T}^{2}T
.
3
3

3

The bound for this term is tight (up to log-factors) without further assumptions, as can be verified with a non-adaptive sampling policy that satisfies
\uppi_{t,i}(a)\equiv\underline{\uppi}_{a,T}
.

. (iii) The third term corresponds to the effective noise variance due to the averaging over “good set” of neighbors that can be lower bounded by
\underline{\uppi}_{a,T}\Phi_{i,a}(\eta^{\prime})(N\!-\!1)
with high probability.
(iv) The last term denotes the worst-case inflation in noise variance—due to the sequentially adaptive policy, since the neighbors
\mathbf{N}_{i,t,a,\eta}
are estimated using entire data, the noise
\{\varepsilon_{i,t}^{(a)}\}
at time
t
does not remain independent of the estimated set of neighbors.
4
4

4

Indeed, this term would be zero if the policy
\uppi_{t}
was non-adaptive.

Overall, the first term of order
\eta-2\sigma_{a}^{2}
in
\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}

Eq.

19
and the first term of order
1/(\underline{\uppi}_{a,T}\Phi_{i,a}(\eta^{\prime})(N\!-\!1))
in
\widetilde{\mathbb{V}}

21
characterize the natural bias-variance tradeoff for our estimator as a function of
\eta
—as
\eta
gets large, the bias increases since the distance of neighboring units for unit

increases, but the variance decreases since the number of neighboring units increases. The second term appearing in the definition of
\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}
does not depend on
\eta
, has a very mild logarithmic dependence on
N
, and decreases polynomially with
T
for sampling policies that have a suitable decay rate in their exploration; this bias gets worse as the minimum exploration rate of the policy decays faster.

The second term appearing in

21
introduces another tradeoff for our estimator—and this term arises purely in the presence of adaptive/sequential policies (see
Remark

2
). This term is small if the neighbor sampling probability function
\Phi_{u,a}(r)
varies smoothly with
r
around

; and is thereby small whenever
\eta
is large. This term captures the worst-case inflation in error caused due to the fact that while constructing our estimates at time
t
, we used the data from future time points (
>t
) which is correlated with the observed data at time
t
due to sequentially adaptive policies. Put simply, despite sequential adaptivity (and despite any adaptive/importance sampling-based reweighting), we control this error by sandwiching the estimated set of neighbors in an annulus induced by two close radii. So if the number of neighbors does not change too abruptly around the considered value of
\eta
, this worst-case inflation in error due to adaptivity can be well controlled.

.

3.5
Proof sketch

We now provide a sketch of the proof of
Theorem

3.1
.
A key highlight of our proof is a novel sandwich argument with nearest neighbors that may be of independent interest for dealing with adaptively collected data. In particular, this sandwich argument is the key technique that allows us to establish the robustness of nearest neighbors for sequential policies without requiring any knowledge of the policy (as discussed in
Section

3.2.1
).

Overall, our proof proceeds in several steps. First, we decompose the error in terms of a bias (
\mathbb{B}
) and variance term (
\mathbb{V}
) using basic inequalities
Eq.

S3
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}\triangleq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\sum_{j\in\mathbf{N}_{i,t,a,\eta}}|\theta_{i,t}^{(a)}-\theta_{j,t}^{(a)}|}{N_{i,t,a,\eta}}}}\right)^{2},\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{V}\triangleq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\sum_{j\in\mathbf{N}_{i,t,a,\eta}}\varepsilon_{j,t}^{(a)}}{N_{i,t,a,\eta}}}}\right)^{2}.

(39)

Next, we discuss our strategy to bound the bias
\mathbb{B}
in
Section

3.5.1
using martingales, followed by the strategy to bound the variance
\mathbb{V}
in
Section

3.5.2
using a sandwich argument.

3.5.1
Controlling the bias via martingales

We show that the bias
\mathbb{B}
bounded by the worst-case distance between unit factor
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{u_{i}-u_{j}}}}\right\|_{2}
over
j
in the nearest neighbor set
\mathbf{N}_{i,t,a,\eta}

Eq.

S5
. To show that this distance is small, we use suitably constructed martingales and establish a tight control on the estimated distance
\rho_{i,j,a}
leveraging the exogenous nature of latent factors and noise variables despite the sequential nature of policy. In particular, we show that
\rho_{i,j,a}
concentrates around its suitable expectation denoted by
\rho^{\star}_{i,j,a}
(see
Lemma

A.1
),
which in turn helps us to control the distance between unit factors in terms of
\eta,\sigma_{a}^{2}
and
\lambda_{a}

using the fact that
\rho_{i,j,a}\leq\eta
. To provide this tight control on the separation between
\rho_{i,j,a}
and
\rho^{\star}_{i,j,a}
,
we use the condition
Eq.

3
and a standard Binomial concentration result and establish a lower bound of
\Omega(\underline{\uppi}_{a,T}^{2}T)
on the denominator in
Eq.

9
. Overall, we prove that the first two terms on the RHS of the display
Eq.

19
upper bound the bias
\mathbb{B}

Eq.

39
.

3.5.2
Controlling the variance via sandwiching the estimated neighbors

Establishing a bound on
\mathbb{V}
is more involved due to the sequential nature of policy in
1
which induces correlations between the neighbor set
\mathbf{N}_{i,t,a,\eta}
and the noise terms
\{\varepsilon_{j,t}^{(a)}\}
. For this part, we use the novel sandwich argument with the estimated set of neighbors. Without loss of generality we set
a=1
, so that we can use the simplified notation
\mathbf{1}(A_{i,t}=a)=A_{i,t}
. The complete argument is provided in
Lemma

A.1
.

We first show that there exists two sets of units denoted by
\mathbf{N}^{\star}_{i,a,\eta}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{N}}^{\star}_{i,a,\eta}
that are contained in the sigma-algebra of the unit factors
\mathcal{U}
, and there exists a high probability event conditioned on
\mathcal{U}
(corresponding to the tight control on
\rho_{i,j,a}
discussed above), such that the estimated set of neighbors
{\mathbf{N}}_{i,a,\eta}\triangleq\{j\in[N]\ \big|\ \rho_{i,j,a}\leq\eta\}
is sandwiched as

\displaystyle\mathbf{N}^{\star}_{i,a,\eta}\subseteq{\mathbf{N}}_{i,a,\eta}\subseteq\widetilde{\mathbf{N}}^{\star}_{i,a,\eta}.

(40)

This sandwich relation helps us to provide a tight control on the variance
\mathbb{V}
despite the arbitrary dependence between the observed noise at time
t
with the neighbors
{\mathbf{N}}_{i,a,\eta}
due to (i) the sequential nature of the treatment policy, and (ii) the fact that entire data is used to estimate the neighbors
{\mathbf{N}}_{i,a,\eta}
. In particular,
Eq.

40
allows to decompose the variance
\mathbb{V}
in two terms, each of which are easy to control as we now elaborate.

Note that by definition
Eq.

10
, we have
\mathbf{N}_{i,t,a,\eta}=\{j\in{\mathbf{N}}_{i,a,\eta}\ \big|\ A_{j,t}=1\}
, which when combined with the sandwich condition
Eq.

40
implies that the variance
\mathbb{V}
can be decomposed further in two terms:

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{1}\!\triangleq\!\bigg(\frac{\sum_{j\in\mathbf{N}^{\star}_{i,a,\eta}}A_{j,t}\varepsilon_{j,t}^{(a)}}{\sum_{j\in{\mathbf{N}}_{i,a,\eta}}A_{j,t}}\bigg)^{2}\ \text{and}\ \mathbb{V}_{2}\!\triangleq\!\bigg(\frac{\sum_{j\in{\mathbf{N}}_{i,a,\eta}\backslash\mathbf{N}^{\star}_{i,a,\eta}}A_{j,t}\varepsilon_{j,t}^{(a)}}{\sum_{j\in{\mathbf{N}}_{i,a,\eta}}A_{j,t}}\bigg)^{2}.

(41)

In simple words,
\mathbb{V}_{1}
corresponds to the variance of averaged noise for the units in
\mathbf{N}^{\star}_{i,a,\eta}
that are assigned treatment
a
at time
t
, and
\mathbb{V}_{2}
corresponds to the averaged noise in
{\mathbf{N}}_{i,a,\eta}\backslash\mathbf{N}^{\star}_{i,a,\eta}
that are assigned treatment
a
at time
t
.

Bounding
\mathbb{V}_{1}
and
\mathbb{V}_{2}

We argue that the variance
\mathbb{V}_{1}
behaves (roughly) like the variance of an average of i.i.d noise variables due to
1
,
4
, and
2
, so that this averaged noise is of the order
1/(\underline{\uppi}_{a,t}|\mathbf{N}^{\star}_{i,a,\eta}|)
and hence decays with the number of neighbors in
\mathbf{N}^{\star}_{i,a,\eta}
increases.

To bound
\mathbb{V}_{2}
, first we note that since the policy is sequential, the noise terms at time
t
affect the observed data in the future, and thereby the noise terms in
{\mathbf{N}}_{i,a,\eta}\backslash\mathbf{N}^{\star}_{i,a,\eta}
are correlated as their realized values affect the future policy, and thereby the observed data and subsequently which units are included in the set
{\mathbf{N}}_{i,a,\eta}
. Analyzing this term without further assumptions remains non-trivial, and we overcome this challenge by using a worst-case bound on this term in terms of the bound on noise, and the fact that
|{\mathbf{N}}_{i,a,\eta}\backslash\mathbf{N}^{\star}_{i,a,\eta}|\leq|\widetilde{\mathbf{N}}^{\star}_{i,a,\eta}\backslash\mathbf{N}^{\star}_{i,a,\eta}|
due to
Eq.

40
. Overall, we prove that
\mathbb{V}_{2}
is of the order of
|\widetilde{\mathbf{N}}^{\star}_{i,a,\eta}\backslash\mathbf{N}^{\star}_{i,a,\eta}|^{2}/(\underline{\uppi}_{a,t}^{2}|\mathbf{N}^{\star}_{i,a,\eta}|^{2})
, and thereby would decay if the relative difference between the two neighbor sets
\widetilde{\mathbf{N}}^{\star}_{i,a,\eta}
and
\mathbf{N}^{\star}_{i,a,\eta}
decays. See
Section

A
for the complete argument.

Bounding the size of sets
\mathbf{N}^{\star}_{i,a,\eta}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{N}}^{\star}_{i,a,\eta}\backslash\mathbf{N}^{\star}_{i,a,\eta}

Finally, we prove a lower bound on
|\mathbf{N}^{\star}_{i,a,\eta}|
, and an upper bound on
|\widetilde{\mathbf{N}}^{\star}_{i,a,\eta}\backslash\mathbf{N}^{\star}_{i,a,\eta}|
using i.i.d sampling of units (
4
), the definition of the neighbor sampling probability
Eq.

17
, and a standard Binomial concentration.

Putting the pieces together, we conclude that that the third term and fourth term on the RHS of
Eq.

19
respectively upper bound
\mathbb{V}_{1}
, and
\mathbb{V}_{2}
defined in
Eq.

41
, which when combined with
Eq.

39
yields the bound

19
established in
Theorem

3.1
.

Assumption 6

(Non-linear factorization of counterfactuals)
.

Conditioned on the latent factors

(43)

Assumption 7

(Lower bounded density of time factors)

The time latent factors

We note that the Lipschitzness with respect to the first argument is not required explicitly for the main result below, but useful to argue that any given unit will have a non-trivial number of neighbors.

4.2
Inference with spillover effects

So far, we assumed that there is no spillover effect since the observed outcome for unit
i
at time
t
depends only on the treatment assigned to that unit at that time
A_{i,t}
, i.e.,
Y_{i,t}=Y_{i,t}^{(A_{i,t})}
in
Eq.

2
.
Next, we show how nearest neighbor algorithm and the results can be generalized to several settings with spillover effects.

Bounded spillover effects

Consider a setting, where the potential outcomes at time
t
depend on a bounded sequence of treatments. In particular, suppose there exists an integer
K
, such that for any
(i,t)
and any treatment sequence
\overline{a}\in\{0,1\}^{K}
, we have the following potential outcome and observation model:

\displaystyle Y_{i,t}^{(\overline{a})}=\theta_{i,t}^{(\overline{a})}+\varepsilon_{i,t}^{(\overline{a})}\ \text{and}\ Y_{i,t}=Y_{i,t}^{(A_{i,t-K+1:t})},\ \text{where}\ A_{i,s_{1}:s_{2}}\triangleq(A_{i,s_{2}},A_{i,s_{2}-1},A_{i,s_{1}}),s_{2}\geq s_{1},

(47)

where
\theta_{i,t}^{(\overline{a})}
denotes the mean counterfactual for unit
i
at time
t
for the sequence of treatments
\overline{a}
, and
\varepsilon_{i,t}^{(\overline{a})}
denotes exogenous zero-mean noise. This model allows
bounded spillover/lagged effects
in time since (i) the counterfactual mean parameters are parameterized by
K
treatments and (ii) the outcome observed at time
t
is the potential outcome corresponding to the sequence of treatments assigned for the last
K
time points.
The presence of lagged effects necessitates us to define the treatment effects for unit
i
at time
t
as a function of past
K
treatments. For this model,
Section

G.4
shows how a direct extension of our NN algorithm would yield consistent estimates for
\theta_{i,t}^{(\overline{a})}
for any
\overline{a}\in\{0,1\}^{K}
when
\uppi_{i,t}\in[p,1-p]
and
\min(p^{K}T,p^{K}N)\to\infty
.

In
Section

G.4
, we also consider a setting with unbounded
K
, i.e., where the lagged effects at time
t
can depend on the entire sequence of treatments till time
t
, but are moderated by the lagged outcome
Y_{i,t-1}
.

The two extensions above, while generalizing the factor model set-up to spillover effects, is also complimentary to the growing literature in reinforcement learning for off-policy evaluation
[
30
,
49
,
15
]
, which are naturally equipped to handle spillover effects. Recently,
[
15
]
leverage a factor model to account for the unobserved confounding in the treatment assignment and outcome models and provides entry-wise guarantees while allowing for spillover effects. However, to our knowledge all these works assume that after conditioning on latent factors and past unit-specific data, the treatments are assigned independently across units—a setting violated for the pooled sequential policies (
1
) motivating this work.

5.2
HeartSteps case study

We illustrate our approach using data from HeartSteps, a mobile health clinical trial aimed at improving physical activity in users with stage I hypertension
[

. In this trial, users were equipped with a Fitbit tracker and a mobile app that used a contextual bandit variant of the Thompson Sampling (TS) algorithm

We focus on data from 45 participants over a 90-day period from September 14, 2019, to December 5, 2019, where a treatment decision was made 5 times per day. Of the 45 users, 35 were assigned treatments via an individualized TS, a reinforcement learning algorithm based on a Bayesian Gaussian linear model, updated independently for each user using their own data. These 35 are referred to as non-pooled users.
The remaining 10 users participated in a feasibility study and were assigned treatments using a pooled variant of TS called IntelligentPooling (IP-TS)
[
48

. IP-TS learns both person-specific and population-level parameters to balance the need for individualization in a heterogeneous population with the need for quick learning from limited data. These users are referred to as pooled users. The IP-TS algorithm was initialized with data from 10 non-pooled users prior to September 14, 2019, and updated nightly using data from all pooled users and the 10 non-pooled users.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we introduced a non-parametric latent factor model for counterfactual inference at unit
\times
time-level in experiments with sequentially adaptive treatment policies. Using a variant of nearest neighbors algorithm, we estimate each of the counterfactual means and provide a non-asymptotic error bound, and asymptotic normality results for these means. We also use these estimates to construct confidence interval for the average treatment effect. We illustrate our theory via several examples, and then illustrate its benefits via simulations, and two case studies.

Our work naturally opens door to several future directions. While this paper focused on counterfactual inference in sequential experimental design, our unit
\times
time-level results also can be easily extended to provide guarantees for a broad class of panel data settings (like synthetic control
[
53
]
, synthetic interventions
[
3
]
, including those with staggered adoption
[
6
]
. While our model generalizes the non-linear mixed effects model, and the bilinear factor model considered in prior work, it does not cover the settings where time-varying features for units are available at each decision time (e.g., in contextual bandits). Our model also does not allow spill-over effects of treatment which arises in several real-world settings.

Notably, our algorithm can be applied to tackle two settings easily: (i) categorical context variables by treating each possible covariate category separately and (ii) context variables that themselves follow a factor model structure, by simply treating the variables as latent. In either case, not leveraging the additional known information would necessarily lead to loss of statistical power, and thus developing a better strategy that allows us to learn from contexts is desirable. It would also be interesting to characterize the downstream gains of our unit
\times
time-level estimates and the associated guarantees with adaptively collected data, e.g., in off-policy evaluation, or optimization, when the data is collected using a learning algorithm (like in
1
).

Finally, we note that our work can also be useful for data imputation strategies. Our guarantees can help provide theoretical justification (with a generic non-parameteric factor model) for the success of nearest neighbor observed empirically in various missing data settings and multiple imputation strategies
[
12
,
13
,
14
,
43
]
— when the data is missing at random in a sequential manner.

Proof of part

C.3
Proof of
Lemma

C.1

We make use of a martingale concentration argument similar to that in the proof of
Lemma

A.1
(a) in
Section

A.1
.
Without loss of generality we can assume
a=1
, so that we can use the simplified notation
\mathbf{1}(A_{i,t}=a)=A_{i,t}
henceforth.

Step (i): Constructing the martingale

For any fixed
i\in[N]
, define the sequence
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{t_{(\ell)}(i)}}\right\}_{\ell=0}^{\infty}
as follows: Set
t_{(0)}(i)\triangleq 0
, and for
\ell=1,2,\ldots
, define

\displaystyle t_{(\ell)}(i)=\begin{cases}\min\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{t:t_{(\ell-1)}(i)<t<T,A_{i,t}=1}}\right\}\ \text{if this problem is feasible,}\ \\
T\quad\text{otherwise}\quad\end{cases}

(S143)

That is,
t_{(\ell)}(i)
denotes the time point such that the unit
i
received the treatment
1
for the
\ell
-th time. Clearly,
t_{(\ell)}(i)\leq T
for all
\ell
deterministically, since
T
is the total number of time points. Moreover,
t_{(\ell)}(i)
is a stopping time with respect to the filtration
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathcal{F}_{t}}}\right\}_{t=1}^{T}
, i.e.,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{t_{(\ell)}(i)\leq t}}\right\}\in\mathcal{F}_{t}
. Let
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}
denote the sigma-field generated by the stopping time
t_{(\ell)}(i)
. Define the sequence

\displaystyle W_{\ell}\triangleq\varepsilon_{i,t_{(\ell)}(i)}^{(a)},\qquad\ell=1,2,\ldots,T.

(S144)

We clearly have
|W_{\ell}|\leq c_{\varepsilon}
for all
\ell
, almost surely, as well as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[W_{\ell}|\mathcal{H}_{\ell}]\!=\!\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\varepsilon_{i,t_{(\ell)}(i)}^{(a)}|\mathcal{H}_{\ell}}}\right]

\displaystyle\!=\!\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\varepsilon_{i,t_{(\ell)}(i)}^{(a)}|\mathcal{H}_{\ell}}}\right]\!\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(i)}}{{=}}\!0,

(S145)

so that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{W_{\ell}}}\right\}_{\ell=0}^{T}
is a bounded Martingale difference sequence with respect to the filtration
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathcal{H}_{\ell}}}\right\}_{\ell=0}^{T}
; here step (i) follows from the fact that conditioned on
t_{(\ell)}(i)=t
, the distribution of
\varepsilon_{i,t}^{(a)}
is independent of the treatment
A_{i,t}
at that time since the policy is sequential (
1
), and the latent time factors at time
t
, and noise variables at time
t
are exogenous and drawn independently of the policy at time
t
(
2
), so that the noise at time
t
is independent of the event
t_{(\ell)}(i)=t
(and thereby the sigma-algebra
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}
).

Step (ii): Relating the martingale to
\sum_{t=1}^{T}A_{i,t}\varepsilon_{i,t}^{(a)}

Define
T_{i}\triangleq\sum_{t=1}^{T}A_{i,t}
. Then we have

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}A_{i,t}\varepsilon_{i,t}^{(a)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{eq:tstop_i}}}{{=}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{T_{i}}\varepsilon_{i,t_{(\ell)}(i)}^{(a)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{eq:wl_eps}}}{{=}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{T_{i}}W_{\ell}.

(S146)

Step (iii): Applying martingale concentration

Fix
\delta>0
. Now, applying Azuma Hoeffding concentration bound
Eq.

S18
, we find that for any fixed
K>0
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}W_{\ell}}}\right|\leq c_{\varepsilon}\sqrt{K\log(2/\delta)}}}\right]\geq 1-\delta.

(S147)

Note that if
K=0
, the bound insider the parentheses above applies deterministically.
Applying a union bound over
K\in[T]
, and noting that the event
T_{i}>0
implies
T_{i}\in[T]
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\sum_{\ell=1}^{T_{i}}W_{\ell}}}\right|\leq c_{\varepsilon}\sqrt{T_{i}\log(2/\delta)}}}\right]\geq 1-T\delta.

(S148)

Putting the pieces together, and taking a union bound over
i\in[N]
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\sum_{\ell=1}^{T_{i}}W_{\ell}}}\right|\leq c_{\varepsilon}\sqrt{T_{i}\log(2/\delta)}\ \text{for all}\ i\in[N]}}\right]\geq 1-NT\delta,

(S149)

Finally, noting that
|\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T}A_{i,t}\varepsilon_{i,t}^{(a)}|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\sum_{t=1}^{T}A_{i,t}\varepsilon_{i,t}^{(a)}|
along with
Eq.

S146
we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[|\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T}A_{i,t}\varepsilon_{i,t}^{(a)}|\leq c_{\varepsilon}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sqrt{\log(NT/\delta)\sum_{t=1}^{T}A_{i,t}}}}\right]\geq 1-\delta,

(S150)

where we have also substituted
\delta
by
\delta/(NT)
in the bounds above. The claim now follows.

E.1
Tuning
\eta
and estimating
\sigma

We do a data-split to tune
\eta
. Consider a two-way split of time points
[T]
—denoted by
\mathcal{T}_{1}
and
\mathcal{T}_{2}
. First, we compute the distances
\rho_{\mathrm{vec},a}\triangleq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\rho_{i,j,a}}}\right\}_{i\neq j}

Eq.

9
for all pairs of units and for each treatment
a
using only the data in time
\mathcal{T}_{1}
. Next, given a threshold
\eta
, let
\widehat{\theta}_{i,t,\eta}^{(a)}
denote the estimate for
\theta_{i,t}^{(a)}
using neighbors using the distances in
\rho_{\mathrm{vec},a}
. Now, we compute a grid of
k
suitably chosen percentiles from the s the distance vector
\rho_{\mathrm{vec},a}
, and call this the candidate set
\eta_{\mathrm{set},a}\triangleq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{k}}}\right\}
. Then, for each treatment
a
, we tune
\eta
as

\displaystyle\eta_{\textrm{tuned},a}=\arg\min_{\eta\in\eta_{\mathrm{set},a}}\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{a,\eta}\quad\text{where}\quad\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{a,\eta}\triangleq\frac{\sum_{i\in N}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{2}}\mathbf{1}(A_{i,t}=a)(Y_{i,t}-\widehat{\theta}_{i,t,\eta}^{(a)})^{2}}{\sum_{i\in N}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{2}}\mathbf{1}(A_{i,t}=a)}.

(S181)

Note that
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{a,\eta}
is effectively the mean squared error on the validation set.

E.2
Consistent estimate for the noise variance

Our next result establishes the consistency of the noise variance estimate obtained using the procedure described above.

Theorem E.1
(Consistency of variance estimate).

Consider the set-up from
Theorem

3.3
such that the sequence
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\eta_{T}^{\prime},N_{T},\underline{\uppi}_{a,T}}}\right\}_{T=1}^{\infty}
satisfies
Eq.

32
after substituting
\underline{\uppi}_{a,T}^{-1/2},\underline{\uppi}_{a,T}^{-1}
in place of

\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{\eta_{T}}

Eq.

S181
using
\mathcal{T}_{1}=[T/2]
, and

satisfies
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{\eta_{T}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\longrightarrow}}\sigma_{a}^{2}

.

Interpretation

Theorem

E.1
establishes the consistency of our variance estimate
\widehat{\sigma}
assuming that a good choice of
\eta_{T}
is already known. However, a good choice of
\eta_{T}
is close to
2\sigma_{a}^{2}
. This requirement is similar in spirit to the analysis of iterative algorithms that require a good initialization. However, this requirement also appears circular as the knowledge of a good

\sigma

\eta

using

to

converges. In full generality, our result do not immediately establish that this procedure contracts towards

due to unknown constants that arise in the analysis. However, if the multiplicative constant in front of
\eta-2\sigma_{a}^{2}
in display
Eq.

19

satisfies
\frac{2c_{v}^{2}}{\lambda_{a}}\leq C

).

For binary treatments, the sampling policy for the
\epsilon

\epsilon

\displaystyle\uppi_{t,i}(1)=\begin{cases}\frac{1+\epsilon}{2}\quad\text{if}\quad\frac{\sum_{t^{\prime}<t}Y_{i,t^{\prime}}A_{i,t^{\prime}}}{\sum_{t^{\prime}<t}A_{i,t^{\prime}}}>\frac{\sum_{t^{\prime}<t}Y_{i,t^{\prime}}(1-A_{i,t^{\prime}})}{\sum_{t^{\prime}<t}(1-A_{i,t^{\prime}})}\\

(S192)

\epsilon
-greedy used by us, the policy
\uppi_{t,i}
is common across all users is given by:

\displaystyle\uppi_{t,i}(1)=\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{1+\epsilon}{2}\quad\text{if}\quad\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{t^{\prime}<t}Y_{j,t^{\prime}}A_{j,t^{\prime}}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t^{\prime}<t}A_{i,t}}>\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{t^{\prime}<t}Y_{j,t^{\prime}}(1-A_{j,t^{\prime}})}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{t^{\prime}<t}(1-A_{j,t^{\prime}})}\\

Other extensions

We now discuss how to extend our theoretical results with possible relaxations of assumptions
2
,
5
, and
4

G.1
Sub-Gaussian latent factors, and noise variables

We now describe how to extend
Theorem

3.1
to the setting with sub-Gaussian latent factors and noise variables. If
u_{i}
, and
v_{t}
are i.i.d sub-Gaussian random vectors with parameter
\sigma_{u}
, and
\sigma_{v}
respectively, and the noise is sub-Gaussian with parameter
\sigma_{\varepsilon}
, then standard sub-Gaussian concentration results yield that

\displaystyle\max_{i\in[N]}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{u_{i}}}}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sigma_{u}\sqrt{d}(2+\sqrt{\log(3N/\delta)}),

(S195)

\displaystyle\max_{t\in[T]}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{v_{t}}}}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sigma_{v}\sqrt{d}(2+\sqrt{\log(3T/\delta)}),

(S196)

\displaystyle\quad\text{and}\quad\max_{i\in[N],t\in[T]}|\varepsilon_{i,t}^{(a)}|

\displaystyle\leq\sigma_{\varepsilon}\sqrt{2\log(6NT/\delta)}),

(S197)

with at least
1-\delta
. Thus, we can relax the boundedness assumption on the latent factors, and noise variables in
4
,
5
, and
2
to sub-Gaussian tails for them, and obtain a modified version of
Theorem

3.1
, wherein the constants
c_{u},c_{v}
and
c_{\varepsilon}
in the bound
Eq.

19
are replaced with the appropriate terms appearing in the display above, and this new bound holds with probability at least
1\!-\!2\delta\!-\!2\!\exp(-\frac{\underline{\uppi}_{a,t}\Phi_{u_{i}}(\eta^{\prime})(N\!{-}\!1)}{16})
. Overall, we find that the error bound
Eq.

19
is inflated only by logarithmic in
(N,T)
factors, so that the subsequent results (e.g.,
Theorems

3.2
and
3.3
) would also hold with logarithmic factor adjustments.

G.2
Non-positive definite covariance
\Sigma_{v}^{(a)}

In
5
, we assert that the minimum eigenvalue of
\Sigma_{v}^{(a)}
—the non-centered covariance of latent time factors under treatment
a
—is non-zero. We now argue that this assumption is not restrictive. Consider the following eigendecomposition of
\Sigma_{v}^{(a)}
in terms of the sorted eigenvalues
\lambda^{(1)}\geq\lambda^{(2)}\ldots\geq\lambda^{(d)}
, and the corresponding orthonormal eigenvectors
\{w_{\ell}\}
:

\displaystyle\Sigma_{v}^{(a)}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}\lambda^{(\ell)}w_{\ell}w_{\ell}^{\top}.

(S198)

In terms of this notation, the RHS of
Eq.

19
scales with
\lambda^{(d)}
. Suppose that
\lambda^{(d)}=0
and
\lambda^{(d-1)}>0
, then we claim that
Eq.

19
holds with
\lambda_{a}\leftarrow\lambda^{(d-1)}
. To prove the claim, first we note that

\displaystyle 0=\lambda_{d}=w_{d}^{\top}\Sigma_{v}^{(a)}w_{d}=w_{d}^{\top}\mathbb{E}[v_{t}^{(a)}(v_{t}^{(a)})^{\top}]w_{d}=\mathbb{E}[((v_{t}^{(a)})^{\top}w_{d})^{2}],

(S199)

which implies that
w_{d}^{\top}v_{t}^{(a)}=0
almost surely.
Thus in this setting, we can repeat the proof of
Theorem

3.1
with the latent unit factors projected on the subspace spanned by the vectors
\{w_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{d-1}
. In other words, the effective dimensionality of the problem is
d-1
for both the latent time factors and latent unit factors (after projecting on to a suitable subspace). Our claim follows.

G.3
Non-i.i.d latent time factors

As noted earlier, the i.i.d sampling assumption on the latent time factors in
5
is a bit restrictive, especially for the applications motivating this work. E.g., the latent time factors capture unobserved societal changes over time, and these causes might be correlated, or drawn from a mixing process. We present one illustrative experiment in
Section

5.1
(see
Eqs.

S194
and
7
) that shows that the performance of nearest neighbors does not deteriorate when the i.i.d assumption is violated.

For our analysis, the i.i.d assumption on the latent time factors can be relaxed if we (i) restrict the class of policy (in
1
) to be unit-wise sequential (i.e., the policy is not pooling data across units) and (ii) use the treatment assignment probabilities to adjust our distance computation. Let
\mathcal{F}_{i,t\!-\!1}\triangleq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{Y_{i,t^{\prime}},A_{i,t^{\prime}},t^{\prime}\in[t\!-\!1]}}\right\}
denote the sigma-algebra of the data observed for unit
i
till time
t\!-\!1
. Suppose the treatment policies are sequentially adaptive independently across unit, i.e., at time
t
,
A_{i,t}
is sampled using policy
\uppi_{t,i}
, where
\uppi_{t,i}(a)=\mathbb{P}(A_{i,t}=a|\mathcal{F}_{i,t\!-\!1})
, and that
Eq.

3
holds for some sequence of non-increasing scalars
\{\underline{\uppi}_{a,t}\}_{t=1}^{\infty}
. Note that under this assumption, treatment assignment to two different units are independent of each other. Next, assuming known
\uppi_{t}
, we replace the distance
Eq.

9
by

\displaystyle\rho_{i,j,a}^{\pi}\triangleq\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\mathbf{1}(A_{i,t}=a)\mathbf{1}(A_{j,t}=a)}{\uppi_{t,i}(a)\uppi_{t,j}(a)}(Y_{i,t}\!-\!Y_{j,t})^{2},

(S200)

with neighbors
Eq.

10
and the estimate
Eq.

11
defined using this modified distance
Eq.

S200
. Then a straightforward adaptation of our proof would yield a guarantee analogous to
Theorem

3.1
, now conditional on all time latent factors
\mathcal{V}
, the covariance matrix
\Sigma_{v}^{(a)}
replaced by
\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}v_{t}^{(a)}(v_{t}^{(a)})^{\top}
, and
e_{\delta}
replaced by
e_{\delta}/\underline{\uppi}_{a,t}
. (The main change needed in the analysis is to establish an appropriate version of the bound
Eq.

S7
with
e_{\delta}
replaced by
e_{\delta}/\underline{\uppi}_{a,t}
, which in fact follows directly by using Azuma-Hoeffiding concentration.

More generally, the key challenge is to identify whether there exists a deterministic matrix
B
and a function
g
(that decreasing with respect to its second argument
T
) such that

\displaystyle\sup_{i\neq j}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}(A_{i,t}=a,A_{j,t}=a)v_{t}^{(a)}(v_{t}^{(a)})^{\top}}{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}(A_{i,t}=a,A_{j,t}=a)}-B}}}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq g(N,T,\delta)

(S201)

with probability at least
1-\delta
. Under this condition, we can establish a modified version of
Theorem

3.1
that holds conditional on the latent time factors, with
\Sigma_{v}^{(a)}
and
e_{\delta}
replaced by
B
and
g(N,T,\delta)
in all relevant terms and definitions. However, it is non-trivial to identify simple conditions on a sequential policy that pools across users, that would imply
Eq.

S201
, and we leave a further exploration in this direction for future work.

G.4
Spillover effects

We continue our discussion from
Section

4.2
on how our algorithm can be generalized for inference with spillover effects.

Bounded spillover effects

First we extend the nearest neighbor algorithm to estimate
\theta_{i,t}^{(\overline{a})}
for any
\overline{a}\in\{0,1\}^{K}
for the model with bounded spillover effects. In essence, effectively the same algorithm from
Section

2.3.1
works once we redefine the treatments at time
t
to be the sequence of assignments
A_{i,t-K+1:t}
and extend the treatment space to
\{0,1\}^{K}
. Put simply, the nearest neighbor estimate for
\theta_{i,t}^{(\overline{a})}
for the bounded spillover set-up is given by

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{i,t,\eta}^{(\overline{a})}

\displaystyle\triangleq\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathbf{N}_{i,t,\overline{a},\eta}|}\sum_{j\in\mathbf{N}_{i,t,\overline{a},\eta}}Y_{j,t}\ \text{where}\ \mathbf{N}_{i,t,\overline{a},\eta}\triangleq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{j\neq i\ \big|\ \rho_{i,j,\overline{a}}\leq\eta,A_{j,t-K+1:t}=\overline{a}}}\right\}

(S202)

\displaystyle\quad\text{and}\quad\rho_{i,j,\overline{a}}

\displaystyle\triangleq\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{s=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}(A_{i,s-K+1:s}=\overline{a})\mathbf{1}(A_{j,s-K+1:s}=\overline{a})(Y_{i,s}\!-\!Y_{j,s})^{2}}{\sum_{s=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}(A_{i,s-K+1:s}=\overline{a})\mathbf{1}(A_{j,s-K+1:s}=\overline{a})}.

(S203)

Under suitable generalization of the assumptions, e.g., a factor model on the counterfactual parameters for
\overline{a}
and
\uppi_{i,t}\in[p,1-p]
, an analogous error guarantee to
Theorem

3.1
for
(\widehat{\theta}_{i,t,\eta}^{(\overline{a})}-\theta_{i,t,\eta}^{(\overline{a})})^{2}
can be derived for any
\overline{a}\in\{0,1\}^{K}
. However, as expected, this squared error would scale with
p^{-K}
and thus such a strategy would work only when
\min(p^{K}T,p^{K}N)\to\infty
, i.e.,
K
grows very slowly with
\min(T,N)
.

Spillover effects dependent on entire history of treatments

Next we consider a setting where the spillover effects at time
t
are moderated by the lagged outcome at time
t-1
. In particular, consider a potential outcome and observation model given by,

\displaystyle Y_{i,t}^{(a|y)}=\alpha^{(a)}y+\theta_{i,t}^{(a)}+\varepsilon_{i,t}^{(a)}\quad\text{and}\quad Y_{i,t}=Y_{i,t}^{(A_{i,t}|Y_{i,t-1})}.

(S204)

In this case,
Y_{i,t}^{(a|y)}
is the potential outcome for unit
i
at time
t
given treatment
a\in\{0,1\}
and lagged outcome taking value
y
at time
t-1
. This model allows for lagged treatment effects, where the lagged effect is carried over via the auto-regressive term,
\alpha^{(a)}Y_{i,t-1}
, with
\alpha^{(a)}\in(-1,1)
being the auto-regressive coefficient for treatment
a\in\{0,1\}
. Note that the dependence of
Y_{i,t}
on
Y_{i,t-1}
makes
Y_{i,t}
a function of the entire sequence of treatments till time time
t
.

In this case, an important quantity of interest is the following treatment effect for unit
i
and time
t
:

\displaystyle\textrm{Exc-Eff}_{i,t}\triangleq\mathbb{E}[Y_{i,t}^{(1|Y_{i,t-1})}-Y_{i,t}^{(0|Y_{i,t-1})}|Y_{i,t-1}]=(\alpha^{(1)}-\alpha^{(0)})Y_{i,t-1}+\theta_{i,t}^{(1)}-\theta_{i,t}^{(0)}.

(S205)

Such effects are analogous to one-step excursion effects
[
41
,
18
]
for this outcome model. Suppose the outcomes are bounded in magnitude by
C_{y}
and we are equipped with an estimate
\alpha^{(a)}
with an error
\max_{a}|\widehat{\alpha}^{(a)}-{\alpha}^{(a)}|\leq\Delta(\alpha)
from historical data (e.g., one can using algorithms from
[
9
, Sec. 5]
to estimate the auto-regressive coefficients). Then we can estimate the excursion treatment effect via the formula

\displaystyle\hat{\textrm{Exc-Eff}}_{i,t}\triangleq(\widehat{\alpha}^{(1)}-\widehat{\alpha}^{(0)})Y_{i,t-1}+\widehat{\theta}_{i,t,\eta_{1}}^{(1)}-\widehat{\theta}_{i,t,\eta_{0}}^{(0)}

(S206)

where
\widehat{\theta}_{i,t,\eta_{a}}^{(a)}
denote the nearest neighbor estimates obtained from using the residual matrix
\{\widetilde{Y_{i,t}}=Y_{i,t}-\widehat{\alpha}^{(A_{i,t})}Y_{i,t-1}\}_{i\in[N],t\in[T]}
as the outcome matrix and the originally observed treatment matrix in constructing the estimates. We remark that our analysis can be generalized to account for deterministic/misspecification errors, so that the overall squared error in estimating one-step excursion effects would scale as the sum of errors obtained from
Theorem

3.1
for
a=0
and
1
, plus
4\Delta^{2}(\alpha)C^{2}_{y}
.

H
Proof of Thm. 4.1: Non-asymptotic error bound for
\widehat{\theta}_{i,t,\eta}^{(a)}
under a non-linear factor model

The proof of
Theorem

4.1
mimics most of the steps from the proof of

from

, except with one major change: calculating the bias term requires new results.

We start with the decomposition

from the proof of

.

(S207)

Let us replace the definition

\displaystyle\rho^{\star}_{i,j,a}\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{v^{(a)}}(f^{(a)}(u_{i},v^{(a)})-f^{(a)}(u_{j},v^{(a)}))^{2}+2\sigma_{a}^{2}.

(S208)

and redefine the event from

Asymptotic results for non-linear factor model

In a manner similar to the generalization of

to

, the primary difference between the bilinear and non-linear model results lies in the scaling of the bias term, where in the bias goes to

Nevertheless, one can see that given

25
