Oscillations of laminar flow velocity in a channel induced by harmonic perturbation of mass injection velocity through the permeable wall
Andrei Kulikovsky

Abstract
Transient 2D Navier–Stokes equations for the laminar flow of incompressible fluid in a channel with permeable wall are reduced to a single transient one–dimensional equation for the transversal profile of longitudinal flow velocity. Small–amplitude harmonic perturbation of injection velocity induces oscillations of longitudinal velocity with the peak at the walls. The peak amplitude increases with the distance along the channel. The effect of increasing amplitude is explained by formation of linearly growing along the channel perturbation of pressure gradient. With the frequency growth, the peaks move toward to the walls.



I

Introduction

Laminar flow of incompressible fluid in channels with permeable wall(s) is of interest
for ultrafiltration applications
(
Chao et al. 2018
)
and fuel cells
(
Eikerling and Kulikovsky 2014
)
.
In 1953,
Berman 1953
reduced 2D problem for the flow between parallel
permeable walls with constant velocity of injection to
a single ODE for the transversal shape of longitudinal velocity and provided
an elegant asymptotic solution. Later, Berman’s approach has been used
to solve the problem of a flow in pipes and ducts
with permeable walls and variable along the pipe/duct velocity
of suction/injection (
Terrill and Thomas 1969
;
Terrill 1983
;
Kosinski et al. 1970
;
Granger et al. 1989
;
Karode 2001
).
So far, however, the transient effects due to time–dependent
injection velocity have not been considered.

In 1929,
Richardson and Tyler 1929
reported measurements
of oscillating flow in a pipe with impermeable wall
induced by harmonic longitudinal pressure gradient.
They demonstrated formation of a peak of velocity oscillations
amplitude close to the pipe wall. A year later,
Sexl 1930

developed a model for oscillating flow in a circular pipe and derived a
simple solution for the radial shape of velocity amplitude.
He showed that the oscillations amplitude is distributed along
the radius according to the Bessel function with a peak (shoulder)
positioned at the distance

l_{*}=\sqrt{\dfrac{\nu}{\omega}}

(1)

from the wall.
Here

\nu
is the air kinematic viscosity and

\omega
is the angular frequency of perturbation.

Harris et al. 1969
provided accurate measurements
confirming the result of Sexl. In all these works the velocity oscillations
were induced by harmonic variation of longitudinal pressure gradient.

Below, unsteady laminar flow of incompressible fluid
between parallel walls with a variable and time–dependent rate of mass
injection through one of the walls is considered.
Following the Berman’s approach, the system of transient Navier–Stokes equations
is reduced to a single equation for the transversal shape
of longitudinal flow velocity. The equation is used to study the
flow response to a small–amplitude harmonic perturbation of the injection
velocity. It is shown that such a perturbation induces
perturbations of the longitudinal flow velocity.
At high frequencies, close to the walls a peak of oscillations amplitude forms
which increases dramatically with the distance along the channel.
The effect is explained by linearly growing along the channel perturbation
amplitude of pressure gradient. The peaks are located at the distance

l_{*}
from the nearest wall.

Model

II.1

Basic equations

Consider laminar flow of incompressible fluid between parallel walls
separated by the distance
2h
and let the upper wall be permeable
to mass injection (Figure
1
).

Schematic of the channel.

Navier–Stokes equations for longitudinal
u
and transversal
v

flow velocity components are

\dfrac{\partial{u}}{\partial{t}}+u\dfrac{\partial{u}}{\partial{x}}+v\dfrac{\partial{u}}{\partial{y}}=-\dfrac{1}{\rho}\dfrac{\partial{p}}{\partial{x}}+\nu\left(\dfrac{\partial^{2}{u}}{\partial{x}^{2}}+\dfrac{\partial^{2}{u}}{\partial{y}^{2}}\right)

(2)

\dfrac{\partial{v}}{\partial{t}}+u\dfrac{\partial{v}}{\partial{x}}+v\dfrac{\partial{v}}{\partial{y}}=-\dfrac{1}{\rho}\dfrac{\partial{p}}{\partial{y}}+\nu\left(\dfrac{\partial^{2}{v}}{\partial{x}^{2}}+\dfrac{\partial^{2}{v}}{\partial{y}^{2}}\right)

(3)

Here
p
is the pressure and
\rho
is the flow density.
As
\rho
is constant, the continuity equation is

\dfrac{\partial{u}}{\partial{x}}+\dfrac{\partial{v}}{\partial{y}}=0

(4)

Introducing dimensionless variables

\tilde{t}=\dfrac{tu_{0}}{h},\quad\tilde{x}=\dfrac{x}{h},\quad\tilde{y}=\dfrac{y}{h},\quad\tilde{u}=\dfrac{u}{u_{0}},\quad\tilde{v}=\dfrac{v}{u_{0}},\\
\tilde{p}=\dfrac{p}{\rho u_{0}^{2}},\quad\tilde{\omega}=\dfrac{\omega h}{u_{0}},

(5)

Eqs.(
2
) – (
4
) transform to

\dfrac{\partial{\tilde{u}}}{\partial{\tilde{t}}}+\tilde{u}\dfrac{\partial{\tilde{u}}}{\partial{\tilde{x}}}+\tilde{v}\dfrac{\partial{\tilde{u}}}{\partial{\tilde{y}}}=-\dfrac{\partial{\tilde{p}}}{\partial{\tilde{x}}}+\dfrac{1}{{\rm Re}}\left(\dfrac{\partial^{2}{\tilde{u}}}{\partial{\tilde{x}}^{2}}+\dfrac{\partial^{2}{\tilde{u}}}{\partial{\tilde{y}}^{2}}\right)

(6)

\dfrac{\partial{\tilde{v}}}{\partial{\tilde{t}}}+\tilde{u}\dfrac{\partial{\tilde{v}}}{\partial{\tilde{x}}}+\tilde{v}\dfrac{\partial{\tilde{v}}}{\partial{\tilde{y}}}=-\dfrac{\partial{\tilde{p}}}{\partial{\tilde{y}}}+\dfrac{1}{{\rm Re}}\left(\dfrac{\partial^{2}{\tilde{v}}}{\partial{\tilde{x}}^{2}}+\dfrac{\partial^{2}{\tilde{v}}}{\partial{\tilde{y}}^{2}}\right)

(7)

\dfrac{\partial{\tilde{u}}}{\partial{\tilde{x}}}+\dfrac{\partial{\tilde{v}}}{\partial{\tilde{y}}}=0,

(8)

where

{\rm Re}=\dfrac{u_{0}h}{\nu}

(9)

is the inlet Reynolds number,

u_{0}
is the mean over the
y
–axis
x
–component of inlet flow velocity,
and
\omega
is the angular frequency of perturbations (see below).

Following the idea of
Berman 1953
we introduce a stream function

\psi=\left(1+\int_{0}^{\tilde{x}}\tilde{w}\left(\tilde{t},\xi\right)\,d\xi\right)f\left(\tilde{t},\tilde{y}\right)

(10)

where
\tilde{w}
is the dimensionless velocity of injection. Setting

\tilde{u}=\dfrac{\partial{\psi}}{\partial{\tilde{y}}},\quad\tilde{v}=-\dfrac{\partial{\psi}}{\partial{\tilde{x}}},

(11)

Eq.(
8
) is satisfied. For
\tilde{u}
and
\tilde{v}
we thus have

\tilde{u}=(1+R)f^{\prime},\quad\tilde{v}=-\tilde{w}f

(12)

where

R\equiv\int_{0}^{\tilde{x}}\tilde{w}\left(\tilde{t},\xi\right)\,d\xi,

(13)

the prime sign indicates partial derivative over
\tilde{y}
or
\tilde{x}
,
depending upon the variable

f^{\prime}\equiv\dfrac{\partial{f}}{\partial{\tilde{y}}},\quad\tilde{w}^{\prime}\equiv\dfrac{\partial{\tilde{w}}}{\partial{\tilde{x}}},

(14)

and similar for higher derivatives.
Substituting Eqs.(
12
) into Eqs.(
6
), (
7
) we come to

\dfrac{\partial{(1+R)f^{\prime}}}{\partial{\tilde{t}}}+(1+R)\tilde{w}\left(f^{\prime}f^{\prime}-ff^{\prime\prime}\right)\\
=-\dfrac{\partial{\tilde{p}}}{\partial{\tilde{x}}}+\dfrac{1}{{\rm Re}}\left(\tilde{w}^{\prime}f^{\prime}+(1+R)f^{\prime\prime\prime}\right)

(15)

-\dfrac{\partial{(\tilde{w}f)}}{\partial{\tilde{t}}}+\left(\tilde{w}^{2}-(1+R)\tilde{w}^{\prime}\right)ff^{\prime}\\
=-\dfrac{\partial{\tilde{p}}}{\partial{\tilde{y}}}-\dfrac{1}{{\rm Re}}\left(\tilde{w}^{\prime\prime}f+\tilde{w}f^{\prime\prime}\right)

(16)

Differentiating Eq.(
15
) over
\tilde{y}
and Eq.(
16
) over
\tilde{x}
we get

\dfrac{\partial{(1+R)f^{\prime\prime}}}{\partial{\tilde{t}}}+(1+R)\tilde{w}\left(f^{\prime}f^{\prime\prime}-ff^{\prime\prime\prime}\right)\\
=-\dfrac{\partial{}}{\partial{\tilde{y}}}\left(\dfrac{\partial{\tilde{p}}}{\partial{\tilde{x}}}\right)+\dfrac{1}{{\rm Re}}\bigl(\tilde{w}^{\prime}f^{\prime\prime}+(1+R)f^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}\bigr)

(17)

-\dfrac{\partial{(\tilde{w}^{\prime}f)}}{\partial{\tilde{t}}}+\bigl(\tilde{w}\tilde{w}^{\prime}-(1+R)\tilde{w}^{\prime\prime}\bigr)ff^{\prime}\\
=-\dfrac{\partial{}}{\partial{\tilde{x}}}\left(\dfrac{\partial{\tilde{p}}}{\partial{\tilde{y}}}\right)-\dfrac{1}{{\rm Re}}\left(\tilde{w}^{\prime\prime\prime}f+\tilde{w}^{\prime}f^{\prime\prime}\right)

(18)

Subtracting Eq.(
18
) from Eq.(
17
) we come to

\dfrac{\partial{}}{\partial{\tilde{t}}}\biggl((1+R)f^{\prime\prime}+\tilde{w}^{\prime}f\biggr)+(1+R)\tilde{w}\left(f^{\prime}f^{\prime\prime}-ff^{\prime\prime\prime}\right)\\
-\bigl(\tilde{w}\tilde{w}^{\prime}-(1+R)\tilde{w}^{\prime\prime}\bigr)ff^{\prime}\\
=\dfrac{1}{{\rm Re}}\bigl(2\tilde{w}^{\prime}f^{\prime\prime}+(1+R)f^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}+\tilde{w}^{\prime\prime\prime}f\bigr)

Eq.(
19
) is the general equation for the
flow in channel with a non–uniform along
\tilde{x}

and time–dependent velocity of mass injection.
The boundary conditions for this equation follow from Eqs.(
12
):

f(1)=1,\quad f^{\prime}(1)=0,\quad f(-1)=f^{\prime}(-1)=0

(20)

II.2

Small oscillations of uniform injection velocity

It is advisable
to consider the case of uniform along
\tilde{x}
injection velocity
\tilde{w}

Setting in Eq.(

)

\tilde{w}^{\prime}

Eq.(
21

\tilde{y}

\left(\dfrac{\tilde{x}}{1+\tilde{x}\tilde{w}}\right)\dfrac{\partial{\tilde{w}}}{\partial{\tilde{t}}}f^{\prime}+\dfrac{\partial{f^{\prime}}}{\partial{\tilde{t}}}+\tilde{w}\bigl(f^{\prime}f^{\prime}-ff^{\prime\prime}\bigr)-\dfrac{f^{\prime\prime\prime}}{{\rm Re}}=k

where

)

Substituting

(23)

into Eq.(
22

and subtracting the static equation Eq.(
25
),
we come to a linear problem for the complex perturbation amplitude

(24)

where
f_{0}(\tilde{y})
is a solution to the static Berman’s equation

\tilde{w}_{0}\left(f_{0}^{\prime}f_{0}^{\prime}-f_{0}f_{0}^{\prime\prime}\right)-\dfrac{f_{0}^{\prime\prime\prime}}{{\rm Re}}=k_{0}

Here, the subscripts 0 and 1 mark the static variables and the small
perturbation amplitudes in the
\omega

The boundary condition for

at

follows from equation

\tilde{v}_{0}+\tilde{v}_{1}=-(\tilde{w}_{0}+\tilde{w}_{1})(f_{0}+f_{1})

Thus, the boundary conditions for Eq.(

f_{1}(1)=f_{1}^{\prime}(1)=f_{1}(-1)=f_{1}^{\prime}(-1)=0.

(26)

IV

Conclusions

A transient model of laminar incompressible flow between
parallel walls, one of which is
permeable to mass injection is developed. Two–dimensional
transient Navier–Stokes equations and continuity equation are reduced
to a single one–dimensional transient equation for the longitudinal velocity.
Linearization and Fourier–transform lead
to equation for the small perturbation amplitude of this velocity.

The results show that a small harmonic perturbation of injection
velocity at the permeable wall is converted to oscillations
of longitudinal flow velocity. At high frequencies,
the transversal profile of oscillations amplitude
exhibits two peaks which dramatically increase along the channel.
The effect is caused by linearly growing along the channel
perturbation amplitude of pressure gradient.
The peaks are located at the distance
\sqrt{\nu/\omega}
from the walls, i.e.,
with the frequency growth each peak moves toward the nearest wall.
