On the shape derivative of polygonal inclusions in the conductivity problem
Martin Hanke

Abstract
We consider the conductivity problem for a homogeneous body with an inclusion of a different, but known, conductivity. Our interest concerns the associated shape derivative, i.e., the derivative of the corresponding electrostatic potential with respect to the shape of the inclusion. For a smooth inclusion it is known that the shape derivative is the solution of a specific inhomogeneous transmission problem. We show that this characterization of the shape derivative is also valid when the inclusion is a polygonal domain, but due to singularities at the vertices of the polygon, the shape derivative fails to belong to H 1 H^{1} in this case.

Introduction

The inverse conductivity problem aims at information
about the spatial electric conductivity distribution within a body,
which is only accessible to electrostatic or quasi-electrostatic
measurements at its boundary.
For example, in electric impedance tomography a series of probing currents
is applied through electrodes attached to the surface of the body,
and the resulting boundary potentials are measured (or the other way round).
As shown by Astala and Päivärinta
[
2
]

the full set of these current/voltage pairs completely determines an
isotropic conductivity distribution in two space dimensions.
See the monographs by Mueller and Siltanen
[
26
]
and
Kirsch
[
20
]
for an exposition of electric impedance tomography.
Except for the D-bar
[
21
]

and the layer stripping methods
[
30
]
most of the pertinent
inversion algorithms are iterative in nature:
The spatial conductivity in question is represented by an element
from the nonnegative cone of a reasonable infinite dimensional vector space
of functions, and in each iteration the gradient of some loss function
is used to update the current approximation of the conductivity distribution;
cf., e.g.,
[
7
,
10
,
27
]
for some early references.
This is the method of choice when the material is very inhomogeneous.

When the object is homogeneous, except for an inclusion of some other known
material say, then one can hope that only one or a few pairs of Cauchy data
may suffice to determine the location and the shape of the inclusion.
Sophisticated noniterative reconstruction methods
like pole fitting methods
[
3
,
19
,
13
]

or the computation of the convex source support
[
15
]
have been
developed for this setting, but iterative methods can also be an option,
provided they
exploit gradient information with respect to the shape of
the inclusion (or its parameterization).

Such derivatives have been termed
shape derivative
or

domain derivative
, and have originally been developed
in the optimization community, cf., e.g.,
[
29
,
16
]
.
The idea is to analyze the impact
of a pointwise perturbation of the boundary of the inclusion in the direction
of a vector field that is attached to it. Then a gradient descent or Newton
type method can be used to shift and deform an approximate inclusion so as
to match the given data as good as possible.

Shape derivatives for inverse conductivity problems have been determined
in the late 90’s of the previous century.
However, up to recently their rigorous foundation
has been limited to inclusions with a certain smoothness,
e.g.,
C^{1,1}
-domains. For this smooth case it has been shown
by Hettlich and Rundell
[
17
]
that the shape derivative of an
inclusion with prescribed conductivity can be characterized via the solution
of a transmission problem with distributed sources sitting on the boundary of
the inclusion: The solution of this differential equation provides
the perturbation of the electric potential at each individual point
within and on the boundary of the body under consideration.
More details and references follow in the subsequent section.

In the optimization community shape derivatives for less regular domains,
e.g., Lipschitz domains,
have been studied, e.g., by Delfour and Zolésio
[
9
]
,
Lamboley, Novruzi, and Pierre
[
24
]
, and Laurain
[
23
]
;
these works focus on the derivative of certain integrated shape functionals.
In the application that we are interested in, such a functional can be,
for example, the inner product of the given data with a certain test function
on the boundary of the two-dimensional body. For this particular
case Beretta, Francini, and Vessella
[
5
]
have determined
in a
tour de force

the impact of a perturbation of a conducting polygonal inclusion.
In a subsequent paper,
Beretta, Micheletti, Perotto, and Santacesaria
[
6
]

gave a somewhat more elegant derivation of the same result
using the established shape derivative theory.
Still, this functional only provides the shape derivative of the given data
in a weak (i.e., variational) form, which lacks
the interpretation of
[
17
]
via an underlying transmission problem.
Further, the impact of the perturbation of the polygon on the electric
potential in the interior of the body (i.e., the state variable)
remained unresolved.

In this paper we show that the same transmission problem as in the smooth
case describes the sensitivity of the electric potential at each point
within the body; its trace on the outer boundary provides an explicit
definition of the shape derivative of the electrostatic measurements.
Due to singularities at the vertices of the polygon, the solution of this
transmission problem is less regular than in the smooth case.
This presents some difficulties in proving the existence of a solution,
which can be overcome by using a technique
which goes back to Kondratiev
[
22
]
(see also Grisvard
[
12
]
),
and which has also been employed in
[
23
]
.

Our results are relevant for investigating the stability of
numerical algorithms for solving the inverse problem. As we will show in a
companion paper
[
14
]
, the outcome of the present work implies
that the determination of a polygon from
two pairs of current/voltage boundary measurements
– which is possible according to Seo
[
28
]
– is actually well-posed,
i.e., the inverse operator is locally Lipschitz continuous.

The outline of this paper is as follows. In Section
2

we set up the problem under consideration and review known results about
the associated shape derivative.
After that we focus on polygonal inclusions only.
In Section
3
we recapitulate the properties of the
electric potential for such inclusions.
Then, in Section
4
we derive some estimates for the shape
derivative of the electrostatic boundary measurements determined in

[
5
,
6
]
, and provide preliminary results about the
shape derivative of the electric potential within the body.
In Section
5
we turn to the transmission problem
formulated in
[
17
]
, and show that it has a unique solution
when the inclusion is a polygon, and we determine its regularity.
That this solution coincides with the shape derivative of the electric
potential is the topic of Section
6
. In the final
section we briefly discuss the case of an
insulating or a perfectly conducting polygonal inclusion; all our results
essentially extend to these two degenerate cases.

2
The conductivity problem and associated shape derivatives

Let
\mathchar 266\relax\subset{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}^{2}
be a simply connected Lipschitz domain,

{\mathscr{D}}
be another Lipschitz domain with simply connected closure

\overline{{\mathscr{D}}}\subset\mathchar 266\relax

The outer normal vector on

\partial\mathchar 266\relax

We call

\sigma(x)\,=\,\begin{cases}k\,,&\text{for $x\in{\mathscr{D}}$}\,,\\

Given a fixed (nontrivial) driving boundary current

\int_{\mathchar 266\relax}\sigma\,\nabla u\cdot\nabla w\,\mathrm{d}x\,=\,\int_{\partial\mathchar 266\relax}fw\,\mathrm{d}s\qquad\text{for all $w\in H^{1}(\mathchar 266\relax)$}\,.

(4)

Assuming that the (normalized) background conductivity and its
anomalous value
k>0
in the inclusion are fixed (and known),
we are interested in the sensitivity of the boundary data

u|_{\partial\mathchar 266\relax}\,=:\,\mathchar 259\relax_{f}({\mathscr{D}})\,\in\,L^{2}_{\diamond}(\partial\mathchar 266\relax)

of the electrostatic potential with respect to perturbations of

.
To be specific, assume that a vector field

is prescribed
on the Lipschitz boundary of
{\mathscr{D}}

\mathchar 256\relax_{h}\,=\,\bigl\{x+h(x)\,:\,x\in\partial{\mathscr{D}}\}

in the direction of

provided this limit exists. Likewise,

Under the assumption that the boundary of

Theorem 2

\int_{\partial{\cal B}_{\delta}}\sigma w_{i}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu}v_{g}\,\mathrm{d}s\,=\,O(\delta^{2\gamma_{i1}-1})\,=\,o(1)\,,\qquad\delta\to 0\,,

(51c)

by virtue of (
14
).

Finally, since
\delta
is so small that
\chi_{i}=1
in
{\cal B}_{\delta}(x_{i})

the Neumann boundary derivative of
w_{i}
satisfies

\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu}w_{i}\,=\,\begin{cases}\beta_{i1}(\gamma_{i1}-1){\widetilde{y}}_{i}(\theta)\,\delta^{\gamma_{i1}-2}\,,&\text{on $\partial{\cal B}_{\delta}(x_{i})$}\,,\\[4.30554pt]
0\,,&\text{on $\partial{\cal B}_{\delta}(x_{j})$, $j\neq i$}\,,\end{cases}

while

v_{g}\,=\,v_{g}(x_{i})\,+\,O(\delta^{\gamma_{i1}})\qquad\text{on $\partial{\cal B}_{\delta}(x_{i})$}\,,

and hence,

\displaystyle\int_{\partial{\cal B}_{\delta}}\!\!\sigma v_{g}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu}w_{i}\,\mathrm{d}s\,=\,\beta_{i1}(\gamma_{i1}-1)v_{g}(x_{i})\left(\int_{0}^{\alpha_{i}}k\,{\widetilde{y}}_{i}(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta+\int_{\alpha_{i}}^{2\pi}{\widetilde{y}}_{i}(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta\right)\delta^{\gamma_{i1}-1}

(51d)

\displaystyle+\,O(\delta^{2\gamma_{i1}-1})\,.

Assembling the pieces of (
51
) and inserting them into
(
50
) we thus obtain

\displaystyle\langle\,w,g\,\rangle_{L^{2}(\partial\mathchar 266\relax)}\,=\,(1-k)\int_{\partial{\mathscr{D}}}(h\cdot\nu)\,\nabla u^{-}\cdot(M\nabla v_{g}^{-})\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle-\,(k-1)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta_{i1}\gamma_{i1}v_{g}(x_{i})\bigl(h_{i}^{-}(A_{i1}^{-}c_{i1}+B_{i1}^{-}s_{i1})\,+\,h_{i}^{+}A_{i1}^{-}\bigr)\delta^{\gamma_{i1}-1}

\displaystyle-\,\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta_{i1}(\gamma_{i1}-1)v_{g}(x_{i})\left(\int_{0}^{\alpha_{i}}k\,{\widetilde{y}}_{i}(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,+\,\int_{\alpha_{i}}^{2\pi}{\widetilde{y}}_{i}(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta\right)\delta^{\gamma_{i1}-1}\,+\,o(1)\,.

The two integrals over
{\widetilde{y}}_{i}
in the bottom line have already been
evaluated in the course of the proof of Theorem
6
,
compare (
47
). Their value is such that all terms of
order
\delta^{\gamma_{i1}-1}
cancel, proving that

\langle\,w,g\,\rangle_{L^{2}(\partial\mathchar 266\relax)}\,=\,(1-k)\int_{\partial{\mathscr{D}}}(h\cdot\nu)\,\nabla u^{-}\cdot(M\nabla v_{g}^{-})\,\mathrm{d}s\,+\,o(1)\,,\qquad\delta\to 0\,.

Since the left-hand side of this equation is independent of
\delta
, the
desired identity (
49
) must hold true.

As shown in Theorem
5
the shape derivative
u_{h}^{\prime}
of
u

is harmonic in
{\mathscr{D}}
and in
\mathchar 266\relax\setminus\overline{{\mathscr{D}}}
and has homogeneous
Neumann boundary values. Accordingly,
u_{h}^{\prime}
and
w

share the same Cauchy data on
\partial\mathchar 266\relax
. Since they
are both harmonic in
\mathchar 266\relax\setminus\overline{{\mathscr{D}}}
they coincide up to the
boundary of
{\mathscr{D}}
by virtue of Holmgren’s theorem. In particular, their
restrictions to
\mathchar 266\relax\setminus\overline{{\mathscr{D}}}
have
the same trace on
\partial{\mathscr{D}}
. Since the material derivative

{\dot{u}}_{h}
belongs to
H^{1}(\mathchar 266\relax)
and
the traces of
u^{\pm}
(and their tangential derivatives) coincide on

\partial{\mathscr{D}}
it follows from (
36
) and (
3
) that

\bigl[u_{h}^{\prime}\bigr]_{\partial{\mathscr{D}}}\,=\,-h\cdot\bigl[\nabla u\bigr]_{\partial{\mathscr{D}}}\,=\,-(h\cdot\nu)\Bigl(\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu}u^{+}\,-\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu}u^{-}\Bigr)\,=\,(1-k)(h\cdot\nu)\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu}u^{-}\,.

According to (
37
) this shows that
w
and
u_{h}^{\prime}
experience the
same jump across
\partial{\mathscr{D}}
, and therefore the traces on
\partial{\mathscr{D}}
of

w|_{\mathscr{D}}
and
u_{h}^{\prime}|_{\mathscr{D}}
also coincide. Both being harmonic functions
we thus conclude that
w=u_{h}^{\prime}
in
{\mathscr{D}}
, and hence, in all of
\mathchar 266\relax
.
∎

Note that it follows from Theorem
7
that the shape
derivative
\partial u({\mathscr{D}})h

.

7
The case of an insulating or a perfectly conducting polygonal
inclusion

So far we have assumed that the conductivity of the inclusion is a known
positive value
k>0
. Now we turn to the limiting cases which are formally
described by
k=0
(the insulating case) and
k=+\infty
(the perfectly
conducting case).

7.1
The insulating case

The appropriate formulation of the
conductivity equation (
2
) when
{\mathscr{D}}
is an insulating
inclusion, is in terms of the boundary value problem

\begin{array}[]{c}\Delta u\,=\,0\quad\text{in $\mathchar 266\relax\setminus\overline{{\mathscr{D}}}$}\,,\qquad{\displaystyle\int_{\partial\mathchar 266\relax}u\,\mathrm{d}s\,=\,0}\,,\\[8.61108pt]
\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\nu}u\,=\,0\quad\text{on $\partial{\mathscr{D}}$}\,,\qquad\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\nu}u\,=\,f\quad\text{on $\partial\mathchar 266\relax$}\,.\end{array}

(52)

When
{\mathscr{D}}
is smooth the existence of an associated shape derivative of

u=u({\mathscr{D}})
is implicit in the result of
[
29
, Section 3.2]
:
this shape derivative in direction
h\in{W_{c}^{1,\infty}(\mathchar 266\relax)}
is given by the
solution
u_{h}^{\prime}\in H^{1}(\mathchar 266\relax\setminus\overline{{\mathscr{D}}})
of the
Neumann boundary value problem

\begin{array}[]{c}\Delta u_{h}^{\prime}\,=\,0\quad\text{in $\mathchar 266\relax\setminus\overline{{\mathscr{D}}}$}\,,\qquad{\displaystyle\int_{\partial\mathchar 266\relax}u_{h}^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}s\,=\,0}\,,\\[8.61108pt]
\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\nu}u_{h}^{\prime}\,=\,\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\tau}\Bigl((h\cdot\nu)\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\tau}u\Bigr)\quad\text{on $\partial{\mathscr{D}}$}\,,\qquad\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\nu}u_{h}^{\prime}\,=\,0\quad\text{on $\partial\mathchar 266\relax$}\,.\end{array}

(53)

To the best of our knowledge the existence of a corresponding shape derivative
for a polygonal inclusion
{\mathscr{D}}
has not yet been investigated.
However, the analysis in
[
6
]
can be adapted in a
straightforward manner to derive the respective material derivative

{\dot{u}}_{h}\in H^{1}(\mathchar 266\relax\setminus\overline{{\mathscr{D}}})
in the direction of
h

for the insulating case and the corresponding variational definition
of
\partial\mathchar 259\relax_{f}({\mathscr{D}})h|_{\partial{\mathscr{D}}}={\dot{u}}_{h}|_{\partial\mathchar 266\relax}
, i.e.,

\langle\,\partial\mathchar 259\relax_{f}({\mathscr{D}})h|_{\partial{\mathscr{D}}},g\,\rangle_{L^{2}(\partial\mathchar 266\relax)}\,=\,\int_{\partial{\mathscr{D}}}(h\cdot\nu)\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}u\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}v_{g}\,\mathrm{d}s

for every
g\in L^{2}_{\diamond}(\partial\mathchar 266\relax)
, where
v_{g}
is the corresponding
solution of (
52
) with
f
replaced by
g
.
This is the natural analog of (
8
) for
k=0
.

For the proof of the corresponding version of Theorem
6

analogous properties of the
solution of the forward problem (
52
), as recollected in
Section
3
for the case
k>0
, are needed.
The corresponding results can be found, e.g., in
[
12
]
:
In the case
k=0
the eigenvalues
\gamma_{ij}^{2}
and eigenfunctions
y_{ij}

which enter into the expansion of
u
near any of the vertices,
are known explicitly, cf.
[
12
, p. 50]
, namely

\gamma_{ij}\,=\,j\pi/(2\pi-\alpha_{i})\,,\qquad j\in{\mathord{\mathbb{N}}}_{0}\,,

(54)

and, for
j\in{\mathord{\mathbb{N}}}
,

y_{ij}(\theta)\,=\,\Bigl(\frac{2}{2\pi-\alpha_{i}}\Bigr)^{1/2}\cos\gamma_{ij}(\theta-\alpha_{i})\,,\qquad\alpha_{i}<\theta<2\pi\,.

Take note that the nonnegative roots
\gamma_{ij}

of these eigenvalues still satisfy (
14
) when
0<\alpha_{i}<\pi
,
whereas they are all greater than one, if
\pi<\alpha_{i}<2\pi
.
Using these properties of the solution of the forward problem
the proof of Theorem
6
carries over to the insulating case
with straightforward modifications.
Since at least one of the interior angles of
{\mathscr{D}}
is smaller than
\pi
,
the corresponding singular
function
w_{i}
only belongs to
H^{\gamma}(\mathchar 266\relax\setminus\overline{{\mathscr{D}}})
for some

\gamma\in(1/2,1)
according to
[
12
, Theorem 1.2.18]
,
and hence the overall smoothness of the solution
u_{h}^{\prime}
of
(
53
) is of similar type than in the case
of a polygonal inclusion with positive conductivity.

If the inhomogeneous Neumann boundary data on
\partial{\mathscr{D}}

in (
53
) are defined by some vector field
h\in{W_{c}^{1,\infty}(\mathchar 266\relax)}
,
a little extra care is necessary to show that the corresponding solution
of (
53
) is the shape derivative of
u
in direction
h
,
because the function
(u_{th}-u)/t
is not defined in all of

\mathchar 266\relax
; in fact, the two functions
u_{th}
and
u
live on different domains.
To overcome this problem one can consider an arbitrary domain

\mathchar 266\relax^{\prime}\subset\mathchar 266\relax\setminus\overline{{\mathscr{D}}}

which satisfies
\partial\mathchar 266\relax^{\prime}\cap\partial{\mathscr{D}}=\emptyset
, in which case

\frac{u_{th}-u}{t}\,:\,\mathchar 266\relax^{\prime}\to{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}

is well-defined for

sufficiently close to zero.
The analog of Theorems
5
,
6
, and

7
then reads as follows.

Theorem 8
.

Let
{\mathscr{D}}
be a polygon with simply connected closure
\overline{{\mathscr{D}}}\subset\mathchar 266\relax

and
h\in{W_{c}^{1,\infty}(\mathchar 266\relax)}
.
Then the boundary value problem
(
53
)
has a unique solution

u^{\prime}\in H^{\gamma}(\mathchar 266\relax\setminus\overline{{\mathscr{D}}})
for some
\gamma\in(1/2,1)
.
This solution is the shape derivative of

,

\frac{u_{th}-u}{t}\,\to\,u_{h}^{\prime}\,=\,{\dot{u}}_{h}\,-\,h\cdot\nabla u\,,\qquad t\to 0\,,

This shape derivative also belongs to

\mathchar 259\relax_{f}({\mathscr{D}})

The other results of Section
4
apply
verbatim to the insulating case;
the corresponding modifications of the proofs are straightforward.

7.2
The perfectly conducting case

When
k
becomes infinitely large, on the other hand, the
transmission condition (
3
) forces
u^{-}
to approximate a
constant but unknown value; this is in agreement with our intuition that
in a perfect conductor potential differences equilibrate immediately.
Up to an additive constant the corresponding potential

u\in H^{1}(\mathchar 266\relax\setminus\overline{{\mathscr{D}}})
is therefore given by the
solution of the boundary value problem

\Delta u\,=\,0\quad\text{in $\mathchar 266\relax\setminus\overline{{\mathscr{D}}}$}\,,\qquad u\,=\,0\quad\text{on $\partial{\mathscr{D}}$}\,,\qquad\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu}u\,=\,f\quad\text{on $\partial\mathchar 266\relax$}\,.

(55)

Note that
u
can be extended by zero to a function in
H^{1}(\mathchar 266\relax)
.
Ito, Kunisch, and Li
[
18
]
pointed out that
the solution of the boundary value problem

\begin{array}[]{c}\Delta u_{h}^{\prime}\,=\,0\quad\text{in $\mathchar 266\relax\setminus\overline{{\mathscr{D}}}$}\,,\\[8.61108pt]
u_{h}^{\prime}\,=\,-(h\cdot\nu)\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\nu}u\quad\text{on $\partial{\mathscr{D}}$}\,,\qquad\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\nu}u_{h}^{\prime}\,=\,0\quad\text{on $\partial\mathchar 266\relax$}\,.\end{array}

(56)

is the shape derivative of
u=u({\mathscr{D}})
of (
55
)
in the direction of
h\in{W_{c}^{1,\infty}(\mathchar 266\relax)}
, provided that
{\mathscr{D}}
has a smooth boundary.

When
{\mathscr{D}}
is a polygon the solution of the
forward problem (
55
) admits a similar expansion
near the vertices of
{\mathscr{D}}
as in the insulating case;
again, see
[
12
]
for details.
The eigenvalues
\gamma_{ij}^{2}
which are relevant for this expansion
are the same as in the insulating case, cf. (
54
),
this time the eigenfunctions being the corresponding sine functions

y_{ij}(\theta)\,=\,\Bigl(\frac{2}{2\pi-\alpha_{i}}\Bigr)^{1/2}\sin\gamma_{ij}(\theta-\alpha_{i})\,,\qquad\alpha_{i}<\theta<2\pi\,,

where the index
j
runs through the natural numbers only; all eigenvalues
are strictly positive.

The analysis in
[
6
]
also extends to the perfectly conducting
case, showing that the shape derivative
\partial\mathchar 259\relax_{f}({\mathscr{D}})
of
a polygonal perfect conductor
{\mathscr{D}}
exists, and that it satisfies

\langle\,\partial\mathchar 259\relax_{f}({\mathscr{D}})h|_{\partial{\mathscr{D}}},g\,\rangle_{L^{2}(\partial\mathchar 266\relax)}\,=\,-\int_{\partial{\mathscr{D}}}(h\cdot\nu)\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu}u\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu}v_{g}\,\mathrm{d}s

for every
g\in L^{2}_{\diamond}(\partial\mathchar 266\relax)
, where
v_{g}
solves the boundary
value problem (
55
) with
f
replaced by
g
.
Note that the projection of
\partial\mathchar 259\relax_{f}({\mathscr{D}})h
onto

L^{2}_{\diamond}(\partial\mathchar 266\relax)
is independent of the chosen grounding
in the definition (
55
) of
u
and
v_{g}
,
respectively.

Further, the analysis from Sections
4
to

6
can be modified in a straightforward way to achieve
the following result.

Theorem 9
.

Let
{\mathscr{D}}
be a polygon with simply connected closure

\overline{{\mathscr{D}}}\subset\mathchar 266\relax
and
h\in{W_{c}^{1,\infty}(\mathchar 266\relax)}
.
Then the shape derivative of the solution

of

(

(

Like in the insulating case the other results of Section
