Positive Hennessy-Milner Logic for Branching Bisimulation
Herman Geuvers, Anton Golov

Abstract
Labelled transitions systems can be studied in terms of modal logic and in terms of bisimulation. These two notions are connected by Hennessy-Milner theorems, that show that two states are bisimilar precisely when they satisfy the same modal logic formulas. Recently, apartness has been studied as a dual to bisimulation, which also gives rise to a dual version of the Hennessy-Milner theorem: two states are apart precisely when there is a modal formula that distinguishes them. In this paper, we introduce “directed” versions of Hennessy-Milner theorems that characterize when the theory of one state is included in the other. For this we introduce “positive modal logics” that only allow a limited use of negation. Furthermore, we introduce directed notions of bisimulation and apartness, and then show that, for this positive modal logic, the theory of s s is included in the theory of t t precisely when s s is directed bisimilar to t t . Or, in terms of apartness, we show that s s is directed apart from t t precisely when the theory of s s is not included in the theory of t t . From the directed version of the Hennessy-Milner theorem, the original result follows. In particular, we study the case of branching bisimulation and Hennessy-Milner Logic with Until (HMLU) as a modal logic. We introduce “directed branching bisimulation” (and directed branching apartness) and “Positive Hennessy-Milner Logic with Until” (PHMLU) and we show the directed version of the Hennessy-Milner theorems. In the process, we show that every HMLU formula is equivalent to a Boolean combination of Positive HMLU formulas, which is a very non-trivial result. This gives rise to a sublogic of HMLU that is equally expressive but easier to reason about.



Concurrent systems are usually modeled as labelled transition
systems (LTS), where a labelled transition step takes one from one
state to the other in a non-deterministic way. Two states in such a
system are considered equivalent in case the same labelled
transitions can be taken to equivalent states. This is captured via
the notion of bisimulation, which is a type of observable equality:
two states are bisimilar if we can make the same observations on
them, where the observations are the labelled transitions to
equivalent states.

Often it is the case that some transitions cannot be observed and

\tau

the coarsest equivalence in the well-known “van Glabbeek spectrum”

bisimulation does not make this assumption that

is
reflexive, but both branching bisimulation and the satisfaction
relation for HMLU introduced by van Glabbeek
[

are invariant under reflexively closing the silent step relation
(cf. Theorem

believe this to be the proper setting to look at these constructions.
Moreover, questions that are difficult in the general case are
simplified when we require reflexivity of silent steps: for example,
the composition of two branching bisimulation relations is again a
branching bisimulation under this assumption, which
Basten
[
1
]
has shown is not the case in general.
The relations we define can be considered for an arbitrary labelled transition with
silent steps by adapting the rules to make every silent step optional.
We do this explicitly in Appendix

This may be desirable when working with notions like divergence-preserving
branching bisimulation
[
8

18

where silent steps play an additional role.

3
Hennessy-Milner Logic with Until and Branching Bisimulation

The Hennessy-Milner theorem for branching bisimulation and modal logic
was found by De Nicola and Vaandrager in

The modal logic in question is Hennessy-Milner Logic with Until,
where a diamond formula can impose a requirement not only on the state

We now briefly recap this logic, using the semantics introduced by

Definition 12

Formulas of Hennessy-Milner Logic with Until (HMLU) are given by the following inductive definition

\varphi:=\top\mid\neg\varphi\mid\varphi_{1}\wedge\varphi_{2}\mid\varphi_{1}\langle\alpha\rangle\varphi_{2}.

\neg(\neg\varphi_{1}\wedge\neg\varphi_{2})

\top\langle\alpha\rangle\varphi

The modality binds tightly on the left and loosely on the right,
so

and
\delta\langle\alpha\rangle\varphi\langle\beta\rangle\psi

\delta\langle\alpha\rangle(\varphi\langle\beta\rangle\psi)

The semantics of interpreting a formula

3.1
Positive and Negative Formulas

As was the case with Hennessy-Milner Logic, Hennessy-Milner Logic with Until
needs to be adapted to a positive variant to allow for non-trivial inclusions
of theories.
Unlike HML, however, simply requiring that all negations be under a modality
is insufficient for our purposes.
Instead, we require that all negations be on the right-hand side of a modality.
The motivation for this is as follows.

A positive formula expresses what eventual
\alpha

If

\text{$s\to_{\tau}t$ and $t\vDash\varphi$}\quad\Rightarrow\quad s\vDash\varphi.

(3)

3.2
Positive Hennessy-Milner Logic with Until

We could use positive Hennessy-Milner Logic with Until formulas as our definition of a
positive theory and obtain a directed Hennessy-Milner theorem that way.
However, we have found that we can simplify our proofs even further by defining a new
logic which we call Positive Hennessy-Milner Logic with Until,
where all formulas are both positive and left-positive.

Definition 17

Formulas of Positive Hennessy-Milner Logic with Until (PHMLU) are given by the following inductive definition

\varphi:=\top\mid\bot\mid\varphi_{1}\wedge\varphi_{2}\mid\varphi_{1}\vee\varphi_{2}\mid\varphi_{1}\langle\alpha\rangle(\varphi_{2}\wedge\neg\varphi_{3}).

We again abbreviate
\top\langle\alpha\rangle\varphi
to
\langle\alpha\rangle\varphi

and define
{\textrm{Th}_{\textrm{P}}}(s):=\left\{\varphi\in{\textrm{PHMLU}}\mid s\vDash\varphi\right\}
.
We regard formulas of PHMLU as also being formulas of HMLU, allowing us to use
the same semantics.
By induction, we see that all PHMLU formulas are positive and left-positive;
in particular, this means that the transfer property of Lemma
2

and the semantics of Lemma
3
hold for PHMLU formulas.

Like with PHML, there are two syntactic classes related to PHMLU that will be useful for our proofs,
namely
\pm{\textrm{PHMLU}}
, the set of PHML formulas and their negations,
and
\bigwedge\pm{\textrm{PHMLU}}
, the set of conjunctions of
\pm{\textrm{PHML}}

Given a formula
\varphi\in\bigwedge\pm{\textrm{PHMLU}}

\varphi^{+}
and
\varphi^{-}

to denote the PHMLU formulas such that
\varphi
is equivalent to
\varphi^{+}\wedge\neg\varphi^{-}
.
So
\varphi^{+}
is the conjunction of all ‘positive’ conjuncts of
\varphi

and
\varphi^{-}
is the disjunction of
\psi

\varphi
.
Concretely, for
\varphi=\varphi_{1}\wedge\varphi_{2}\wedge\neg\varphi_{3}\wedge\neg\varphi_{4}

\varphi^{+}=\varphi_{1}\wedge\varphi_{2}
and
\varphi^{-}=\varphi_{3}\vee\varphi_{4}
, and so
\varphi
is equivalent to
\varphi^{+}\wedge\neg\varphi^{-}
.
Given

\delta\langle\alpha\rangle\varphi
as a shorthand for the PHMLU
formula
\delta\langle\alpha\rangle\varphi^{+}\wedge\neg\varphi^{-}
.

At this point, it may be concerning that we have both ‘positive HMLU formulas’ and
‘PHMLU formulas’, where the former is a superset of the latter; for example

\langle a\rangle(\neg(\langle b\rangle\top)\vee\langle a\rangle\top)

The following theorem shows that when reasoning up to equivalence, this is not a problem,
as every positive HMLU formula is equivalent to some PHMLU formula.

In the previous two sections, we have introduced Positive Hennessy-Milner Logic with Until

6
Related Work

Our approach for defining formulas is somewhat similar to that taken by
Beohar et al in
[
2
]
.
There too, the ‘formulas’ (elements of
\mathbb{L}
in their terminology)
are only closed under negation under the modality.
This corresponds to our presentation of PHML in this paper.
It would be interesting to see whether our results can be formulated in their setting.

A notion similar to directed bisimulation is the one introduced by Bergstra and Klop in
[
3
]
,
who are working in the context of process algebras and have defined an addition operation.
In our terms, the state
s+t
should be seen as the state that can do anything
s
or
t

can do; in other words,
s+t\to_{a}t
precisely when
s\to_{a}t
or
t\to_{a}t
.
Bergstra and Klop define
s\sqsubseteq t
as follows:
s=t
or there exists a
t
such that
s+t=t
.
In our context, it makes most sense to interpret this equality as bisimulation.

In the strong context, we in fact have that
s\mathrel{\mathrel{\underline{\rightarrow}}_{ds}}t
precisely when
s\sqsubseteq t
.
However, in the branching case this does not hold: if
s
is the state with no
outgoing transitions except the reflexive
\tau
-step
s\to_{\tau}s
,
then
s\sqsubseteq t
for every
t
, but
s\mathrel{\mathrel{\underline{\rightarrow}}_{ds}}t
does not in general hold;
for example
s\vDash\langle\tau\rangle\neg\langle a\rangle\top
, while there exist states
where this formula fails.

Moreover, even for strong bisimulation we feel that our presentation gives something that
is not covered by the process algebra view.
Namely, directed bisimulation is built up coinductively, and has an inductive dual.
This means that the structure of a proof of
s\mathrel{\mathrel{\underline{\rightarrow}}_{ds}}t
or
s\mathrel{\mathrel{\underline{\#\!\!\!{>}}}_{ds}}t
can be useful
when reasoning about a labelled transition system.
For the same reason, we do not use the characterization in Theorem
5.2

as our definition of directed branching bisimulation.

For Kripke structures, a notion of “directed modal simulation” has
been studied by Kurtonina and de Rijke in
[
17
]
,
where it is also related to theories of positive formulas in modal
logic. Bisimulation for Kripke structures is comparable to strong
bisimulation for LTSs, so silent steps are not treated. One of the goals of

[
17
]
is to capture the class of modal formulas
that are preserved by a directed modal simulation, so their notion
of directed bisimulation is weaker then ours:
a difference is that
directed modal simulation in each direction does not imply
bisimulation, while a directed bisimulation (in our sense) in each
direction does imply bisimilarity.

Notions of bisimilarity exist for a large variety of systems and
calculi (process algebras,
\pi
-calculus, …), for example see

[
20
]
. Desharnais et al
[
5
]
have
studied bisimulation relations on Markov processes, and in their
work, bisimulation is equivalent to two-way simulation. In
particular, we would call their Proposition 2.11 a directed
Hennessy-Milner theorem.

7
Conclusion

We have formulated and proved a directed Hennessy-Milner
theorem, by adapting known Hennessy-Milner logic to a positive
variant and by adapting bisimulation to directed bisimulation. From
this directed version, the usual Hennessy-Milner theorem follows as
a corollary. We also show that the dual of bisimulation,
apartness (and its directed variant),
enables a direct proof of the Hennessy-Milner theorem by induction.

We have illustrated this using LTSs (labelled transition systems) with strong
bisimulation and using LTSs with silent steps with branching bisimulation.
The situation for LTSs with silent steps and weak bisimulation is very similar to strong bisimulation.
Applications of our approach to other logics and notions of bisimilarity would be of interest.
Variations like divergence-preserving branching bisimulation
[
8
,
18
]

would be interesting to consider from this directed perspective.

In the branching setting, our “directed approach” brings the logic and bisimulation
sides of the Hennessy Milner theorem closer together.
There is a remarkable
similarity between proving that a positive formula holds in a state
and proving that a state is on the right of a directed bisimilarity.
Namely, to show that
t\vDash\delta\langle\alpha\rangle(\psi^{+}\wedge\neg\psi^{-})
we show

\exists t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime}.\,t\twoheadrightarrow_{\tau}t^{\prime}\to_{\alpha}t^{\prime\prime}\wedge t^{\prime}\vDash\delta\wedge t^{\prime\prime}\vDash\psi^{+}\wedge t^{\prime\prime}\not\vDash\psi^{-},

while to show that
s\mathrel{\mathrel{\underline{\rightarrow}}_{ds}}t
, we show that for every step
s\to_{\alpha}s^{\prime}
,

\exists t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime}.\,t\twoheadrightarrow_{\tau}t^{\prime}\to_{\alpha}t^{\prime\prime}\wedge s\mathrel{\mathrel{\underline{\rightarrow}}_{ds}}t^{\prime}\wedge s^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathrel{\underline{\rightarrow}}_{ds}}t^{\prime\prime}\wedge t^{\prime\prime}\mathrel{\mathrel{\underline{\rightarrow}}_{ds}}s^{\prime}.

Similarly, showing that a formula fails to hold in a state
is akin to showing that the state is on the right of a directed apartness.

This level of similarity suggests that there should be some way of constructing
a modal logic from a coinductive notion of bisimilarity, or vice-versa,
but this has so far proved elusive.
For bisimulation relations, this question is raised in a considerably more general setting
by Kupke and Rot
[
16
]
.
The work of Beohar et al
[
2
]
may be relevant to this point.

Let us begin by recalling some conventions.
The modality binds tightly on the left and loosely on the right;

\delta\wedge\gamma\langle\alpha\rangle\varphi\wedge\psi

\delta\wedge(\gamma\langle\alpha\rangle(\varphi\wedge\psi))

The set
\pm{\textrm{PHMLU}}
is the set of PHMLU formulas and their negations

\bigwedge\pm{\textrm{PHMLU}}

\pm{\textrm{PHMLU}}
formulas.

Given a

\varphi^{+}
and
\varphi^{-}

to denote the decomposition of
\varphi

\varphi^{+}\wedge\neg\varphi^{-}

Concretely,
\varphi^{+}
is the conjunction of the conjuncts of
\varphi

while
\varphi^{-}
is the disjunction of those

\neg\psi
is a conjunct of
\varphi

Given formulas
\delta\in{\textrm{PHMLU}}
and
\varphi\in\bigwedge\pm{\textrm{PHMLU}}

to denote the PHMLU formula

To aid in our proof, we define two functions

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\alpha}

\displaystyle:{\textrm{HMLU}}^{+}\times{\textrm{HMLU}}\to{\textrm{HMLU}}

\displaystyle\Gamma^{P}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle:\left(\bigwedge\pm{\textrm{PHMLU}}\right)^{+}\times\bigwedge\pm{\textrm{PHMLU}}\to\bigwedge\pm{\textrm{PHMLU}}

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\alpha}(\delta;\psi)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\alpha}(\delta_{1},\delta_{2},\ldots,\delta_{n};\psi)

\displaystyle:=\neg\delta^{-}\wedge\delta^{+}\langle\alpha\rangle\psi

\displaystyle\Gamma^{P}_{\alpha}(\delta_{1},\delta_{2},\ldots,\delta_{n};\psi)

\displaystyle:=\neg\delta_{1}^{-}\wedge\delta_{1}^{+}\langle\tau\rangle\Gamma^{P}_{\alpha}(\delta_{2},\ldots,\delta_{n};\psi).

(Restatement of Theorem

)

For every HMLU formula

Proof

The proof proceeds by induction, with all cases except the modality being straightforward.

Suppose
\varphi=\delta\langle\alpha\rangle\psi
.
By induction,
\delta
and
\psi
are equivalent to
\check{\Delta}=\bigvee_{i}\Delta_{i}
and
\bigvee_{j}\Psi_{j}

respectively.
We define a family
\Phi

as follows:

\Phi_{\vec{i},j}:=\Gamma^{P}_{\alpha}(\Delta_{i_{1}},\ldots,\Delta_{i_{n}};\Psi_{j}).

It remains to show that

is equivalent to

The chain of reasoning for this is as follows:

\displaystyle\check{\Delta}\langle\alpha\rangle\bigvee_{j}\Psi_{j}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\bigvee_{j}\bigvee_{\vec{i}\in\NRS(I)}\Gamma^{P}_{\alpha}(\Delta_{i_{1}},\ldots,\Delta_{i_{n}};\Psi_{j})

As in the case of Theorem

the disjunction distributes over the modality when on the right.

\varphi

Appendix 0.D
Coq

Throughout the project, we have formalized some of our constructions in
Coq.
3
3

3

See
https://github.com/jesyspa/directed-branching-bisimulation
for the code.

We have focused on formalizing the parts pertaining to apartness and modal logic,
as these are inductive notions that are best-suited for formalization.
The corresponding theorems for bisimulation can be obtained by duality.
We have also specifically formalized the branching case;
a formalization of the strong case would be similar, but simpler.

The highlights of this effort are formalized proofs of the apartness counterpart
of Theorem
4.1

and one direction of Theorem
5.1
.
These required us to formalize both the notions of apartness and the various
forms of Hennessy-Milner Logic with Until that we have used throughout the paper.
We have not formalized any of the notions of bisimulation: since these are coinductive
notions, they are harder to work with in Coq than the inductive notions of apartness.

We have postulated the Law of Excluded Middle as an axiom in our formalization.
This is necessary to show that for any LTS
X
, any
s\in X
and formula
\varphi
,

s\vDash\varphi
or
s\not\vDash\varphi
holds.
We believe this could be avoided by imposing image-finiteness constraints on
X
.
We also do not distinguish between different non-silent transitions; in practice,
this never comes up in our proofs so it would be of little benefit to do so.

Our setup is split into a number of files containing general definitions about LTSs
and the syntax of formulas.
The definitions of apartness are split over the
reflexive
and
nonreflexive

directories, corresponding to definitions that do and do not assume that the LTS has
reflexive silent steps.
Many notions are defined in both; we then show that these notions are equivalent.
The key definitions are
b_apart
(branching apartness),
db_dapart

(directed branching apartness), and
db_apart
(the symmetric closure of
directed branching apartness).

In place of working with branching apartness, we often work with semi-branching apartness,
which we define as
sb_apart
.
Semi-branching apartness is an equivalent formulation of the branching apartness relation that is
easier to work with in an LTS without reflexive silent steps.
The two are known to be equivalent, see for example Geuvers and Jacobs
[
6
]
.

Our key results are formalization of the dual of
Theorem
4.1
(
db_apart_iff_sb_apart
)
and a variation of one direction of
Theorem
5.1
(
good_distinguish_gives_apartness
, together with
_pos

_neg

The latter shows that a positive distinguishing formula gives a directed branching apartness,
but the claim is strengthened as follows:
instead of showing that any PHMLU formula gives a directed branching apartness,
we show that any left-positive formula gives a branching apartness,
and that this apartness is directed in one direction or the other if the
formula is additionally positive or negative respectively.

We have not formalized the proof of the other direction of Theorem
5.1
,
which shows that a directed branching apartness gives rise to a positive distinguishing formula,
nor of Theorem
3.1
.
Showing this would require reasoning about finiteness in Coq, which is possible but
would be more complicated than the arguments we have made so far.

We found using Coq to be very useful for gaining confidence in our results as we developed them,
and experienced little trouble formalizing notions of apartness in it.
