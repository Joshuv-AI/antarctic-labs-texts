A diffusion approach to Stein’s method on Riemannian manifolds
Huiling Le, Alexander Lewis, Karthik Bharath, Christopher Fallaize

Abstract
. We detail an approach to developing Stein’s method for bounding integral metrics on probability measures defined on a Riemannian manifold 𝑴 \mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits . Our approach exploits the relationship between the generator of a diffusion on 𝑴 \mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits having a target invariant measure and its characterising Stein operator. We consider a pair of such diffusions with different starting points, and through analysis of the distance process between the pair, derive Stein factors, which bound the solution to the Stein equation and its derivatives. The Stein factors contain curvature-dependent terms and reduce to those currently available for ℝ m \mathbb{R}^{m} , and moreover imply that the bounds for ℝ m \mathbb{R}^{m} remain valid when 𝑴 \mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits is a flat manifold.



1.
Introduction

The eponymous method to estimate integral metrics and semi-metrics on spaces of probability measures proposed by Charles Stein
(
Stein 1972
)
has led to tremendous improvements in distributional approximation techniques. See, for example, the surveys by
Barbour and Chen 2014
and
Ross 2011
. The method has mainly been developed for probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
or
\mathbb{N}^{m}
for
m\geq 1
. The focus of this paper is on developing a version of the method that can be employed to approximate probability measures on an
m
-dimensional Riemannian manifold.

Abstracting Stein’s method to a general space
\mathcal{X}
in a heuristic manner is useful for elucidating its key ingredients and the ensuing challenges in developing a corresponding version on manifolds. The goal is to bound an integral (semi-)metric

d_{\mathcal{H}}(\mu,\nu):=\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\left|\int h\text{d}\mu-\int h\text{d}\nu\right|,

between a probability measure
\nu
and a target probability measure
\mu
on
\mathcal{X}
with respect to a class
\mathcal{H}
of real-valued test functions on
\mathcal{X}
. Stein’s method is centred around the construction and study of an operator
L
that maps functions
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
in a certain class
\mathcal{F}
into mean-zero functions under
\mu
: if
X\sim\mu
, then
\operatorname{E}\left[Lf(X)\right]=0
for every
f\in\mathcal{F}
. The operator
L
thus encodes information about
\mu
and, when
\mathcal{F}
is sufficiently large, one may determine a function
f_{h}\in\mathcal{F}
associated with every
h\in\mathcal{H}
that solves the
Stein equation
(or the Poisson equation in PDE literature)

h(x)-\operatorname{E}\left[h(X)\right]=Lf_{h}(x).

As a consequence, bounding
d_{\mathcal{H}}(\mu,\nu)
reduces to bounding the term
\sup_{f_{h}\in\mathcal{F}}\operatorname{E}\left[Lf_{h}(Z)\right]
, where
Z\sim\nu
, achieved in application-specific ways. An important implication, profitably used in some applications, is that the need to compute an expectation with respect to
\mu
in
d_{\mathcal{H}}
is circumvented; an example is when
\nu
is the empirical measure based on points
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
on
\mathcal{X}
and
\mu
represents a conjectured limit probability measure. Upper bounds on
d_{\mathcal{H}}
then depend explicitly on the smoothness of the functions in
\mathcal{F}
. Hence, integral to the success of Stein’s method in upper bounding
d_{\mathcal{H}}
are the following requirements: (a) construction of the operator
L
and identifying its domain
\mathcal{F}
; and, (b) determination of the solution
f_{h}
and its regularity properties.

An introductory account on choices of
L
satisfying requirement (a) for various probability measures
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}
(or some subset thereof) is available in
Ross 2011
. When
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{m},m>1
, focus has mainly been restricted to the case when
\mu
is a Gaussian measure (see
Chatterjee and Meckes 2008
;
Barbour 1988
;
Meckes 2009
) although more recently results on extensions to non-Gaussian measures have appeared in
Mackey and Gorham 2016
;
Mijoule et al. 2019
;
Fang et al. 2019
;
Chen et al. 2019
.

An important observation by
Barbour 1988
relates the operator
L
to the infinitesimal generator of a diffusion process on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
that solves an SDE with invariant measure
\mu
. This observation enables identification, and examination, of the solution to the Stein equation with the transition semigroup associated with
L
. The diffusion approach hence opens up the possibility of defining
L
for
\mu
on a manifold
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
by considering an SDE on
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
whose solution is a diffusion with invariant measure
\mu
.

Broadly, this is the approach we adopt in this paper. On a complete Riemannian manifold
(\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits,g)
without boundary, we consider approximating probability measures of the form
\mu_{\phi}
with density, up to a normalisation constant,
e^{-\phi}
with respect to the volume measure
\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits\mathop{\rm vol}\nolimits
for a smooth
\phi
. Under some conditions on
\phi
and the geometry of
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
, the diffusion with infinitesimal generator

L_{\phi}:=\frac{1}{2}\left\{\Delta-\langle\nabla\phi,\,\nabla\rangle\right\}

has
\mu_{\phi}
as its invariant measure, where
\nabla
and
\Delta
are the (Riemannian) gradient and Laplace-Bertrami operators, respectively. The operator
L_{\phi}
generates mean-zero functions under
\mu_{\phi}
.

We address requirement (b) for generalising Stein’s method to
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
by adapting the approach in
Mackey and Gorham 2016
for log-concave measures
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
to the manifold setting. In their paper, bounds on lower-order derivatives of the solution
f_{h}
, known as
Stein factors
(see
Röllin 2012
), were derived by studying the distance between a pair of coupled diffusions
X_{t}
and
Y_{t}
with same invariant measure
\mu
starting at distinct points. Analogously, we construct a pair of diffusions
X_{t,x}
and
Y_{t,y}
on
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
starting at
x
and
y
with identical generator
L_{\phi}
, and study the distance process
\rho(X_{t,x},Y_{t,y})
around neighbourhoods of non-empty cut loci. In particular, when there is no first conjugate point contained in the cut locus to any given point in
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
we establish exponential
pathwise
contraction for trajectories of the two diffusions towards their initial points; on the other hand when first conjugate points are present, we establish a similar contraction property that holds on
average
.

The study of the distance process enables the determination of Stein factors which bound the Lipschitz constants of the solution
f_{h}
, and its first and second derivatives, where the geometry of
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
manifests itself through curvature-dependent terms in the factors. The derived bounds on
f_{h}
, as well as on its first and second derivatives, reduce to the ones of
Mackey and Gorham 2016
for
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, which we show remain valid for complete, connected flat manifolds. The Stein factors are then used to construct upper bounds on integral (semi-)metrics between
\mu_{\phi}
and another probability measure on
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
for specific choices of the class of test functions
\mathcal{H}
. In particular, using the first order bound on
f_{h}
, we derive an upper bound on the Wasserstein distance between
\mu_{\phi}
and
\mu_{\psi}
. A related generalisation of Stein’s method to manifolds, based on the approach of
Fang et al. 2019
, can be found in
Thompson 2020
.

The paper is organised as follows. In Section
2.1
we define relevant quantities and introduce notation, and in Section
2.2
we describe assumptions on probability measures and diffusions under consideration, and the key condition (
3
) and assumption (A1) for the derivation of our results; the conditions are explicated with some examples. In Section
3
we describe the coupling of a pair of diffusions on
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
and analyse their distance process, when conjugate points are absent (Section
3.1
) and present (Section
3.2
). In Section
4
we consider the Stein equation and its solution and derive Stein factor bounds that depend on the curvature of
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
. In Section
5
, using the Stein factors, we derive bounds for integral (semi-)metrics: in Section
5.1
we derive an upper bound on the Wasserstein distance between
\mu_{\phi}
and a probability measure of similar type, and in Section
5.2
we do the same for an integral semi-metric between
\mu_{\phi}
and an arbitrary probability measure on
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Notation and definitions

We assume throughout that
(\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits,g)
is a complete and connected Riemannian manifold without boundary of dimension
m
and with covariant derivative
D
; by
D^{i},i>1
we then denote higher orders of
D
. We shall denote by
\rho(x,y)
the Riemannian distance between any two points
x
and
y
in
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
, and by
\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits\mathop{\rm vol}\nolimits
the Riemannian volume measure of
(\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits,g)
. We denote by
T_{x}(\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits)
the tangent space to
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
at
x\in\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
and by
\mathop{\boldsymbol{T\!M}}\nolimits
the tangent bundle of
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
. For
k\geq 1
,
\mathcal{C}^{k}(\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits)
denotes the class of
k
-times continuously differentiable real-valued functions on
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
,
\mathcal{C}(\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits)
denotes the set of continuous functions, and
\mathcal{C}_{0}(\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits)
denotes continuous functions vanishing at infinity. The Lipschitz constant
C_{0}(h)
of a Lipschitz function
h\in\mathcal{C}(\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits)
is defined as

C_{0}(h):=\sup_{x\not=y\in\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits}\frac{|h(x)-h(y)|}{\rho(x,y)}\thinspace.

Higher-order Lipschitz constants of a function depend on bounding tensor fields. Accordingly, for each
x\in\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
define the operator norm at
x
for a tensor field
T
on
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
, based on
n
-fold tangent vectors at
x
, as

\|T\|_{op}:=\sup_{v_{1},\cdots,v_{n}\in T_{x}(\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits),\,|v_{i}|\not=0}\frac{|T(v_{1},\cdots,v_{n})|}{\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}|v_{i}|}.

Then, if
h\in\mathcal{C}^{k}(\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits)
, for
k\geq 1
, we may define

(1)

C_{i}(h):=\sup_{\gamma_{x,y},x\not=y\in\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits}\frac{\|D^{i}h(x)-\Pi_{\gamma_{x,y}}(D^{i}h(y))\|_{op}}{\rho(x,y)},\qquad i=1,\ldots,k,

and call them the Lipschitz constants of
D^{i}h
, where
\gamma_{x,y}
denotes any possible minimal geodesic from
y
to
x
and
\Pi_{\gamma_{x,y}}
denotes the parallel transport from
T_{y}(\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits)
to
T_{x}(\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits)
along
\gamma_{x,y}
. Note that
Dh=dh
and that
\hbox{Hess}^{h}=D^{2}h
, where
\hbox{Hess}^{h}
is the Hessian of
h
. Note also that
\sup_{x\in\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits}\|D^{i+1}h(x)\|_{op}=C_{i}(h)
for
i=0,\cdots,k-1
. Finally, if
X
and
Z
are two random variables on
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
with
X\sim\mu
and
Z\sim\nu
, abusing notation, we interchangeably use
d_{\mathcal{H}}(\nu,\mu)
and
d_{\mathcal{H}}(Z,X)
to denote the integral (semi-)metric between the two probability measures, where

d_{\mathcal{H}}(Z,X):=\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}|\operatorname{E}\left[h(Z)\right]-\operatorname{E}\left[h(X)\right]|

with respect to a set of real-valued test functions
\mathcal{H}
.

2.2.
Key assumptions

On
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
, we consider probability measures of the form

\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits\mu_{\phi}=\frac{1}{c(\phi)}e^{-\phi}\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits\mathop{\rm vol}\nolimits,

with
c(\phi)=\int_{\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits}e^{-\phi}\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits\mathop{\rm vol}\nolimits

<\infty
and with support on the entire space
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
. We assume that
\phi\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits)
is such that
\nabla\phi
is Lipschitz; specifically, we assume that
D\phi
has finite Lipschitz constant
C_{1}(\phi)
. Throughout,
X
denotes a random variable with
X\sim\mu_{\phi}
.

The uniformly elliptic operator
L_{\phi}=1/2\left\{\Delta-\langle\nabla\phi,\,\nabla\rangle\right\}
then is the infinitesimal generator of a Feller diffusion process that solves the Itô stochastic differential equation

(2)

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits X_{t}=\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits B^{\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits}_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\phi(X_{t})\,\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits t,

where
B^{\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits}_{t}
is a Brownian motion on
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
.
If there is a constant
\kappa>0
such that

(3)

\displaystyle\hbox{Ric}(x)+\hbox{Hess}^{\phi}(x)\geqslant-\kappa\,g(x),\quad\forall x\in\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits,

where Ric is the Ricci curvature tensor, then the corresponding semigroup
P_{t}=e^{tL_{\phi}}
is conservative (see
Bakry 1986
), i.e.,
P_{t}1\equiv 1
for all
t>0
or, equivalently,
X_{t}
will, with probability one, not leave
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
in finite time. For a successful development of the Stein’s method on
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
, we need the
Bakry-Emery curvature criterion
: there is a constant
\kappa>0
such that,

(\text{A1}):\qquad\hbox{{\rm Ric}}(x)+\hbox{{\rm Hess}}^{\phi}(x)\geqslant 2\kappa\,g(x)\quad\forall x\in\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits.

Evidently, assumption (A1) implies the condition in (
3
).

Remark 1
.

When
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits=\mathbb{R}^{m}
, (A1) simplifies to
v^{\top}\hbox{Hess}^{\phi}v\geqslant 2\kappa
for any unit (column) vector
v
in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
where, as usual,
\hbox{Hess}^{\phi}
is treated as an
m\times m
matrix. Hence, (A1) reduces to the requirement in
Mackey and Gorham 2016
that
-\phi
is
2\kappa
-strongly concave, noting that in their notation,
\phi
here is
-\log p
, up to a constant. This is also true if the Ricci curvature of
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
is always non-positive. In general, (A1) is weaker than the requirement that
-\phi
is
c
-strongly concave for some
c>0
.

Example 1
.

In order to elucidate condition (
3
) and assumption (A1) we look at a few example manifolds and probability measures
\mu_{\phi}
.

(i)

\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
is the standard sphere
S^{m}
of dimension
m
. The function
\phi(x)
corresponding to the von Mises-Fisher distribution
M_{m}(x_{0},c)
takes the form
\phi(x)=-c\cos(r(x))
, with
r(x)=\rho(x_{0},x)
for
c>0
and a fixed point
x_{0}\in\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
. Since
D^{2}f(r)=f^{\prime\prime}(r)\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits r\times\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits r+f^{\prime}(r)D^{2}r
on general manifolds and since
D^{2}r=\cot(r)\{g-\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits r\times\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits r\}
on
S^{m}
(see
Greene and Wu 1979
), it follows that

\hbox{Hess}^{\phi}(x)=-c\,D^{2}\cos(r(x))=c\,\cos(r(x))\,g(x);

this ensures that

\hbox{Ric}(x)+\hbox{Hess}^{\phi}(x)\geqslant\{(m-1)-c\}g(x),

and condition (
3
) holds for the von Mises-Fisher distribution with
\kappa>\max\{-(m-1)+c,0\}
. However, if there is a
\kappa>0
such that assumption (A1) holds, then we must have
0<c<m-1
. This requires in particular
m>1
, and thus any von Mises-Fisher distribution on the circle fails to satisfy (A1).

(ii)

\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
is hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{m}
with sectional curvature
-1
. For
\phi(x)=c\rho(o,x)^{2}
where
c>0
and
o
is a fixed point in
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
, we have
\int_{\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits}e^{-\phi}\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits\hbox{vol}<\infty
as, in terms of normal coordinates at
o
,
\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits\hbox{vol}=\sinh(\rho)^{m-1}\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits\rho\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits\theta
. On the other hand, Hess
{}^{\phi}(x)\geqslant 2cg(x)
by the Hessian Comparison Theorem and Ric
(x)=-(m-1)g(x)
. Hence condition (
3
) holds with
\kappa>\max\{(m-1)-2c,0\}
for such a
\phi
. Moreover, if
c>(m-1)/2
, then there is a
\kappa>0
such that assumption (A1) holds.

(iii)

\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
is the complex projective space
\mathbb{CP}^{m}
equipped with the Fubini-Study metric. This is also the Kendall shape space of configurations in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
m+1
labelled landmarks.
Let
A
be an
(m+1)\times(m+1)
Hermitian matrix, i.e.
A=A^{*}
and
\phi(z)=-z^{*}Az
, for
z=x+iy\in\mathbb{C}^{m+1}
(column vectors) and
|z|=1
, where
A^{*}
denotes the complex conjugate transpose of
A
. Without loss of generality, we may assume that the smallest eigenvalue of
A
is zero. The corresponding
\mu_{\phi}
is the complex Bingham distribution on
\mathbb{C}S^{m}=S^{2m+1}
. Since
\phi(z)=\phi(e^{i\theta}z)
,
\mu_{\phi}
can be regarded as a distribution on
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
(see
Kent 1994
). It can be shown that Hess
{}^{\phi}(w,w)=2\{\phi(z)-\phi(w)\}\geqslant-2\lambda_{\max}
for a horizontal (with respect to the projection from
S^{2m+1}
to
\mathbb{CP}^{m}
) unit vector
w\in T_{z}(S^{2m+1})
, where
\lambda_{\max}>0
is the largest eigenvalue of
A
.

The complex projective space
\mathbb{CP}^{m}
equipped with the Fubini-Study metric is an Einstein manifold with its Ricci curvature tensor equal to
2(m+1)
times the metric tensor. Thus,

\text{Ric}+\text{Hess}^{\phi}\geqslant 2\left\{m+1-\lambda_{\max}\right\}g,

and so, for the complex Bingham distribution on
\mathbb{CP}^{m}
, condition (
3
) holds with
\kappa>2\max\{\lambda_{\max}-(m+1),0\}
and assumption (A1) holds if
\lambda_{\max}<m+1
.

(iv)

\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
is the rotation group
SO(m)
with the bi-invariant metric determined by
g(E_{1},E_{2})

:=-\frac{1}{2}\text{tr}(E_{1}E_{2})
for skew-symmetric
E_{1},E_{2}
, where
m>2
. Assume that, for
S\in\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
,
\phi(S)=-c\,\text{tr}(S_{0}S)
with
S_{0}\in SO(m)
and a constant
c>0
. Then, the corresponding
\mu_{\phi}
is a von Mises-Fisher distribution on
SO(m)
. It can be shown that Hess
{}^{\phi}\geqslant-c\,g
.

Recall that the Killing form of
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
is
B(E_{1},E_{2})=(m-2)\text{tr}(E_{1}E_{2})
and the Ricci curvature Ric
(E_{1},E_{2})=-\frac{1}{4}B(E_{1},E_{2})=\frac{m-2}{2}g(E_{1},E_{2})
. Thus, in this case,

\text{Ric}+\text{Hess}^{\phi}\geqslant\left\{\frac{m-2}{2}-c\right\}g,

and so, for the von Mises-Fisher distribution on
SO(m)
, condition (
3
) holds with
\kappa>\max\{c-(m-2)/2,0\}
and assumption (A1) holds if
c<(m-2)/2
.

3.
The distance between coupled diffusions

Our approach to define the Stein equation on
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
and analyse properties of its solution rests on the construction of a pair of diffusions
(X_{t},Y_{t})
, and handling of the distance process
\rho(X_{t},Y_{t})
between the pair. In particular, we prove exponential contraction of
\rho(X_{t},Y_{t})
towards the initial points, and thus extend the approach used by
Mackey and Gorham 2016
on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
to the manifold setting. In contrast to the Euclidean setting, since the distance function
(x,y)\mapsto\rho(x,y)
is not in
\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits\times\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits)
if the cut locus of a point in
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
is not empty, analysis of the distance process
\rho(X_{t},Y_{t})
requires additional care.

3.1.
When no conjugate points are present in cut loci

We first consider the relatively simple situation where there is no conjugate point in the cut locus of any given point in
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
. In this setting, by modifying the arguments in
Kendall 1986a
and
Kendall 1986b
, we are able to establish exponential pathwise contraction of distance between the diffusions, aided by a key result given in Lemma 3 in Appendix A of Supplementary Material, which expresses the distance function in terms of finitely many smooth functions in neighbourhoods of cut point, despite it not belonging to
\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits\times\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits)
.

Note first that, in terms of a Brownian motion
B_{t}
on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
starting from the origin, the Itô differential equation (
2
) with initial condition
X_{0}=x_{0}
is equivalent to

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathop{\rm d}\nolimits_{s}\!X_{t}&=&\Xi_{t}\,\mathop{\rm d}\nolimits_{s}\!B_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\phi(X_{t})\,\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits t,\qquad X_{0}=x_{0};\\
\mathop{\rm d}\nolimits_{s}\!\Xi_{t}&=&H_{\Xi}\mathop{\rm d}\nolimits_{s}\!X_{t},\qquad\Xi(X_{0})=\xi_{0},\end{array}

where
\mathop{\rm d}\nolimits_{s}
denotes the Stratonovich differential,
H
the horizontal lift from
T\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
to the tangent bundle of the orthonormal frame bundle
\mathcal{O}(\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits)
, where
\xi_{0}
sits above
x_{0}
. For an introduction to horizontal lifts and orthonormal frame bundles, see for example,
Kobayashi and Nomizu 1963
.

Theorem 1
.

Assume that
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
has the property that there is no conjugate point to any given point in
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
, and that the Bakry-Emery curvature criterion (A1) holds for a constant
\kappa>0
. Then, for any
x_{0},y_{0}\in\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
, there is a pair of coupled diffusions
(X_{t},Y_{t})
starting from
(x_{0},y_{0})
such that both
X_{t}
and
Y_{t}
satisfy (
2
) and, for any
\ell\geqslant 1
,

(7)

\displaystyle\rho(X_{t},Y_{t})^{\ell}\leqslant\rho(x_{0},y_{0})^{\ell}e^{-\ell\kappa t},\qquad t\geqslant 0.

Proof.

Consider the map

\hbox{Exp}:\,\,T\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits\rightarrow\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits\times\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits;\quad(x,v)\mapsto(x,\exp_{x}(v)).

For any
(x,v)\in T\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
, this map provides an intervening geodesic
s\mapsto\exp_{x}(sv)
,
0\leqslant s\leqslant 1
, connecting
x
and
\exp_{x}(v)
. The length of this geodesic is at least the distance between
x
and
\exp_{x}(v)
. If the interior of this geodesic does not intersect the cut locus of
x
, then it is also a minimal geodesic between its two end points. Denote by
\tilde{\Pi}_{(x,v)}
the parallel transport along this
intervening
geodesic from
x
to
\exp_{x}(v)
where, for our purpose,
\tilde{\Pi}_{(x,v)}
is taken to be the identity map on
T_{x}(\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits)
if
x=\exp_{x}(v)
even though this may imply a discontinuity.

For any given
(x_{0},y_{0})\in\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits\times\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
, we take
v_{0}\in T_{x_{0}}(\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits)
such that

(8)

\displaystyle\exp_{x_{0}}(v_{0})=y_{0}\qquad\hbox{ and }\qquad|v_{0}|=\rho(x_{0},y_{0}).

Under the given assumptions,
y_{0}
is not conjugate to
x_{0}
. Then, if
y_{0}
is a cut point of
x_{0}
, a consequence of the proof of Lemma 3 in Appendix A of Supplementary Material is that there is a neighbourhood
\mathcal{N}
of
(x_{0},y_{0})
such that
\text{Exp}^{-1}(\mathcal{N})
is a disjoint union of a finite number of open sets on
\mathop{\boldsymbol{T\!M}}\nolimits
and, restricted to each such set, Exp is a diffeomorphism from that set onto
\mathcal{N}
. If
y_{0}
is not a cut point of
x_{0}
, then
v_{0}
is uniquely determined by
v_{0}=\exp^{-1}_{x_{0}}(y_{0})
and a similar result holds with just one component in
\text{Exp}^{-1}(\mathcal{N})
. Hence, in particular,
\mathop{\boldsymbol{T\!M}}\nolimits
is locally a covering space of
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits\times\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
. Within such a neighbourhood
\mathcal{N}
of a given
(x_{0},y_{0})
, we can determine a continuous process
(X_{t},V_{t})\in\mathop{\boldsymbol{T\!M}}\nolimits
starting from
(x_{0},v_{0})
associated with (
2
), by solving the following coupled diffusions
X_{t}
and
Y_{t}=\exp_{X_{t}}(V_{t})
:

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{crl}\mathop{\rm d}\nolimits_{s}\!X_{t}&=&\Xi_{t}\,\mathop{\rm d}\nolimits_{s}\!B_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\phi(X_{t})\,\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits t;\qquad X_{0}=x_{0};\\
\mathop{\rm d}\nolimits_{s}\!Y_{t}&=&\Upsilon_{t}\,\mathop{\rm d}\nolimits_{s}\!B^{\prime}_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\phi(Y_{t})\,\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits t,\qquad Y_{0}=y_{0};\\
\mathop{\rm d}\nolimits_{s}\!\Xi_{t}&=&H_{\Xi}\mathop{\rm d}\nolimits_{s}\!X_{t},\qquad\Xi(X_{0})=\xi_{0}\vskip 2.84544pt;\\
\mathop{\rm d}\nolimits_{s}\!\Upsilon_{t}&=&H_{\Upsilon}\mathop{\rm d}\nolimits_{s}\!Y_{t},\qquad\Upsilon(Y_{0})=\eta_{0}\vskip 2.84544pt;\\
\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits B^{\prime}_{t}&=&(\Upsilon^{-1}_{t}\tilde{\Pi}_{X_{t},V_{t}}\Xi_{t})\,\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits B_{t},\end{array}

where, similarly to
\Xi
and
\xi_{0}
for
X
,
\Upsilon
and
\eta_{0}
are respectively a lift of
Y
to the orthonormal frame bundle
\mathcal{O}(\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits)
and
\eta_{0}
sits above
y_{0}
. Since
B_{t}^{\prime}
is also a Brownian motion on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, both
X_{t}
and
Y_{t}
are diffusions satisfying (
2
) before they leave
\mathcal{N}
.

When
(X_{t},Y_{t})
hits the boundary of
\mathcal{N}
, we can find a neighbourhood
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
of
(X_{t},Y_{t})
satisfying the above properties of
\mathcal{N}
. Then, allowing
V_{t}
to move discontinuously without altering
(X_{t},Y_{t})
such that, after the jump, it satisfies (
8
), we can continue to run
(X_{t},Y_{t})
within
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
so defined. Note that, if
X_{t_{0}}=Y_{t_{0}}
for some
t_{0}\geqslant 0
, then
X_{t}=Y_{t}
for
t\geqslant t_{0}
.

For
(X_{t},Y_{t})
constructed as above, denote by
\tilde{\rho}(X_{t},Y_{t})
the length of the intervening geodesic
\exp_{X_{t}}(sV_{t})
between
X_{t}
and
Y_{t}=\exp_{X_{t}}(V_{t})
; and write
\gamma_{t}
for the unit speed intervening geodesic from
X_{t}
to
Y_{t}
, that is,
\gamma_{t}(s)=\exp_{X_{t}}(sV_{t}/|V_{t}|)
. Note that
\tilde{\rho}(X_{t},Y_{t})
depends implicitly on the choice of
v_{0}
, which is not unique when
y_{0}
is a cut point of
x_{0}
. On the other hand, for any given
v_{0}
which satisfies (
8
),
\tilde{\rho}
is a smooth function of
(x,y)
within the neighbourhood
\mathcal{N}
chosen as above. However, the change of neighbourhood from
\mathcal{N}
to
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
usually results in a discontinuity for the process
\tilde{\rho}(X_{t},Y_{t})
. Nevertheless,
\rho(X_{t},Y_{t})
is always continuous and

\rho(X_{t},Y_{t})\leqslant\tilde{\rho}(X_{t},Y_{t}),\qquad t\geqslant 0,

where the latter becomes an equality immediately after the jump. Hence, to find an upper bound for
\rho(X_{t},Y_{t})
, it is sufficient to find an upper bound for
\tilde{\rho}(X_{t},Y_{t})
.

To bound
\tilde{\rho}(X_{t},Y_{t})
we may assume, without loss of generality, that
(X_{t},Y_{t})
lies in
\mathcal{N}
for all
t\geqslant 0
. Write
u_{0},u_{1},\cdots,u_{m-1}
for an orthonormal base in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that
\Xi_{t}u_{0}=\dot{\gamma}_{t}(0)
, and, for
i=0,1,\cdots,m-1
, let
v_{i}=(\Upsilon^{-1}_{t}\tilde{\Pi}_{(X_{t},V_{t})}\Xi_{t})u_{i}
. Then, the Itô formula for
\tilde{\rho}(X_{t},Y_{t})
is given by

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits\tilde{\rho}(X_{t},Y_{t})&=&\left(\Xi_{t}u_{0}\right)\tilde{\rho}(X_{t},Y_{t})\,\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits\,\langle u_{0},B_{t}\rangle+\left(\Upsilon_{t}v_{0}\right)\tilde{\rho}(X_{t},Y_{t})\,\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits\,\langle v_{0},B^{\prime}_{t}\rangle\\
&&+\,\,\dfrac{1}{2}\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\left(\Xi_{t}u_{i}+\Upsilon_{t}v_{i}\right)^{2}\tilde{\rho}(X_{t},Y_{t})\,\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits t\\
&&+\,\,\dfrac{1}{2}\left\{\langle\nabla\phi(X_{t}),\dot{\gamma}_{t}(0)\rangle-\langle\nabla\phi(Y_{t}),\dot{\gamma}_{t}(\tilde{\rho}(X_{t},Y_{t}))\rangle\right\}\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits t.\end{array}

Since
\langle u_{0},B_{t}\rangle=\langle v_{0},B^{\prime}_{t}\rangle
, since
\left(\Xi_{t}u_{0}\right)\tilde{\rho}(X_{t},Y_{t})=-\left(\Upsilon_{t}v_{0}\right)\tilde{\rho}(X_{t},Y_{t})
and since

\left(\Xi_{t}u_{0}\right)^{2}\tilde{\rho}(X_{t},Y_{t})=\left(\Upsilon_{t}v_{0}\right)^{2}\tilde{\rho}(X_{t},Y_{t})=\left(\Xi_{t}u_{0}\right)\left(\Upsilon_{t}v_{0}\right)\tilde{\rho}(X_{t},Y_{t})=0,

(
3.1
) simplifies to

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{rcl}2\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits\tilde{\rho}(X_{t},Y_{t})&=&\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\left(\Xi_{t}u_{i}+\Upsilon_{t}v_{i}\right)^{2}\tilde{\rho}(X_{t},Y_{t})\,\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits t\\
&&+\,\,\left\{\langle\nabla\phi(X_{t}),\dot{\gamma}_{t}(0)\rangle-\langle\nabla\phi(Y_{t}),\dot{\gamma}_{t}(\tilde{\rho}(X_{t},Y_{t}))\rangle\right\}\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits t.\end{array}

Denote by
J^{i}_{t}
the Jacobi vector field along
\gamma_{t}
with
J^{i}_{t}(0)=\Xi_{t}u_{i}
and
J^{i}_{t}(1)=\Upsilon_{t}v_{i}
. Then, since
\tilde{\rho}
is smooth under the assumption that
(X_{t},Y_{t})
lies in a given neighbourhood of
(x_{0},y_{0})
, using the second-variation formula (see
Cheeger and Ebin 1975
), a modification of the argument by
Kendall 1986a
shows that the right hand side of (
3.1
) is given by

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{rcl}&&\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\tilde{\rho}(X_{t},Y_{t})}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\!\left\{|D_{\dot{\gamma}_{t}(s)}(J^{i}_{t}(s))|^{2}\!\!-\!\langle R(J^{i}_{t}(s),\dot{\gamma}_{t}(s))\dot{\gamma}_{t}(s),J^{i}_{t}(s)\rangle\right\}\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits s\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits t\\
&&\quad+\,\,\left\{\langle\nabla\phi(X_{t}),\dot{\gamma}_{t}(0)\rangle-\langle\nabla\phi(Y_{t}),\dot{\gamma}_{t}(\tilde{\rho}(X_{t},Y_{t}))\rangle\,\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits t\right\},\end{array}

where the integral is along
\gamma_{t}
and
R
denotes the curvature tensor of
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
.

To analyse the first term of (
3.1
), we use a modified form of the argument in
Cheeger and Ebin 1975
, the proof of Lemma 1.21. It shows that, for each
i=1,\ldots,m-1
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\tilde{\rho}(X_{t},Y_{t})}\left\{|D_{\dot{\gamma}_{t}(s)}(J^{i}_{t}(s))|^{2}-\langle R(J^{i}_{t}(s),\dot{\gamma}_{t}(s))\dot{\gamma}_{t}(s),J^{i}_{t}(s)\rangle\right\}\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits s

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\tilde{\rho}(X_{t},Y_{t})}\left\{|D_{\dot{\gamma}_{t}(s)}(V^{i}_{t}(s))|^{2}-\langle R(V^{i}_{t}(s),\dot{\gamma}_{t}(s))\dot{\gamma}_{t}(s),V^{i}_{t}(s)\rangle\right\}\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits s,

where
V^{i}_{t}(s):=(\tilde{\Pi}_{(X_{t},sV_{t}/|V_{t}|)}\Xi_{t})u_{i}
. Now, since
V^{i}_{t}
is parallel along
\gamma_{t}
, it follows that
D_{\dot{\gamma}_{t}(s)}(V^{i}_{t}(s))=0
. As a consequence, since
\{\dot{\gamma}_{t}(s),V^{1}_{t}(s),\ldots,V^{m-1}_{t}(s)\}
forms an orthonormal base of
T_{\gamma_{t}(s)}(\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits)
and
\langle R(\dot{\gamma}_{t}(s),\dot{\gamma}_{t}(s))\dot{\gamma}_{t}(s),\dot{\gamma}_{t}(s)\rangle\equiv 0
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{rcl}&&\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\tilde{\rho}(X_{t},Y_{t})}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\left\{|D_{\dot{\gamma}_{t}(s)}(J^{i}_{t}(s))|^{2}-\langle R(J^{i}_{t}(s),\dot{\gamma}_{t}(s))\dot{\gamma}_{t}(s),J^{i}_{t}(s)\rangle\right\}\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits s\\
&\leqslant&-\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\tilde{\rho}(X_{t},Y_{t})}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\langle R(V^{i}_{t}(s),\dot{\gamma}_{t}(s))\dot{\gamma}_{t}(s),V^{i}_{t}(s)\rangle\,\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits s\\
&=&-\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\tilde{\rho}(X_{t},Y_{t})}\hbox{Ric}(\gamma_{t}(s))(\dot{\gamma}_{t}(s),\dot{\gamma}_{t}(s))\,\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits s.\end{array}

For the remaining two terms of (
3.1
), we note that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits}{\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits s}\langle\nabla\phi(\gamma_{t}(s)),\dot{\gamma}_{t}(s)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle D_{\dot{\gamma}_{t}(s)}(\nabla\phi(\gamma_{t}(s))),\dot{\gamma}_{t}(s)\rangle+\langle\nabla\phi(\gamma_{t}(s)),D_{\dot{\gamma}_{t}(s)}\dot{\gamma}_{t}(s)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle D_{\dot{\gamma}_{t}(s)}(\nabla\phi(\gamma_{t}(s))),\dot{\gamma}_{t}(s)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hbox{Hess}^{\phi}(\dot{\gamma}_{t}(s),\dot{\gamma}_{t}(s)),

as
\gamma_{t}
is a geodesic. From this, we deduce that

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{rcl}\langle\nabla\phi(Y_{t}),\dot{\gamma}_{t}(\tilde{\rho}(X_{t},Y_{t}))\rangle-\langle\nabla\phi(X_{t}),\dot{\gamma}_{t}(0)\rangle&=&\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\tilde{\rho}(X_{t},Y_{t})}\hbox{Hess}^{\phi}(\dot{\gamma}_{t}(s),\dot{\gamma}_{t}(s))\,\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits s.\end{array}

Thus, under the Bakry-Emery curvature criterion (A1) condition, (
3.1
), (
3.1
) and (
3.1
) together give that

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{rcl}2\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits\tilde{\rho}(X_{t},Y_{t})&\leqslant&-\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\tilde{\rho}(X_{t},Y_{t})}\!\!\left\{\hbox{Ric}(\dot{\gamma}_{t}(s),\dot{\gamma}_{t}(s))\!+\!\hbox{Hess}^{\phi}(\dot{\gamma}_{t}(s),\dot{\gamma}_{t}(s))\right\}\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits s\,\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits t\\
&\leqslant&-\,\,2\kappa\tilde{\rho}(X_{t},V_{t})\,\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits t.\end{array}

Now, for any
\ell\geqslant 1
, it follows from (
3.1
) that

\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits\left(\tilde{\rho}(X_{t},Y_{t})^{\ell}\right)\leqslant-\ell\,\kappa\,\tilde{\rho}(X_{t},Y_{t})^{\ell}\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits t,

so that

\displaystyle e^{\ell\kappa t}\tilde{\rho}(X_{t},Y_{t})^{\ell}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}(X_{0},Y_{0})^{\ell}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{\ell\kappa s}\left\{\ell\kappa\,\tilde{\rho}(X_{s},Y_{s})^{\ell}\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits s+\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits\left(\tilde{\rho}(X_{s},Y_{s})^{\ell}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}(X_{0},Y_{0})^{\ell}.

Finally, by recalling that
\tilde{\rho}(X_{0},Y_{0})=\rho(X_{0},Y_{0})
, we have

\rho(X_{t},Y_{t})^{\ell}\leqslant\tilde{\rho}(X_{t},Y_{t})^{\ell}\leqslant\rho(X_{0},Y_{0})^{\ell}e^{-\ell\kappa t}

as required.
∎

3.2.
When conjugate points are present in cut loci

When conjugate points are present in cut loci in
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
, the construction of a pair of diffusions in the proof of Theorem
1
fails at such points. More precisely, if
y_{0}
is a (first) conjugate point of
x_{0}
along the geodesic
\exp_{x_{0}}(sv)
, which also lies in the cut locus of
x_{0}
, then
D\exp_{x_{0}}(v)
is singular. This means that it would be impossible to find a neighbourhood
\mathcal{N}
of
(x_{0},y_{0})
that has the properties described above following (
8
). In particular, it would be impossible to find a subset of
\mathop{\boldsymbol{T\!M}}\nolimits
, as specified there, such that Exp is a diffeomorphism from that subset onto
\mathcal{N}
. It is evident from the proof of Theorem
1
that the existence of such a diffeomorphism offers a way to couple
(X_{x,t},Y_{y,t})
at, and beyond, cut points.

Nevertheless, we now show that it is still possible to construct a pair of diffusions on
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
with properties that (
\mathrm{i}
) they both satisfy (
2
) and (
\mathrm{ii}
) the
expected
distance between them contracts at least exponentially. This relies on a generalisation of the technique used in Theorem 5 of
Kendall 1986b
to deal with the presence of conjugate points. In the non-conjugate part of the cut locus of
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
analysis proceeds as with Theorem
1
. To warn us of when the diffusions get close to the first conjugate locus, we use the operator
L_{\phi}
, and monitor the value of its action on the distance function
\rho
; this value decays towards
-\infty
when the points approach the first conjugate locus. Effectively, we determine a neighbourhood
N_{2\delta}\subset\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits\times\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
of the first conjugate locus in
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits\times\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
for a constant
\delta
that depends on
\kappa
and the injectivity radius of
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
. Once the coupled diffusions enter
\bar{N}_{2\delta}
, the closure of
N_{2\delta}
, we decouple them, run independent diffusions until they hit
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits\setminus N_{\delta}
, where
N_{\delta}\supset N_{2\delta}
, and then return to coupling again.

We first need two preliminary results before stating and proving the main result in this section. Observe that the set

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{E}}:=\{(x,v)\in\mathop{\boldsymbol{T\!M}}\nolimits\,\mid

\displaystyle\,\hbox{the geodesic }\exp_{x}(sv),\,0\leqslant s\leqslant 1,

\displaystyle\,\hbox{contains no conjugate point of }x\}

is an open set in
\mathop{\boldsymbol{T\!M}}\nolimits
. The map Exp
:(x,v)\rightarrow(x,\exp_{x}(v))
maps
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}
surjectively to its image

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{rl}\mathcal{E}:=\{(x,y)\in\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits\times\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits\,\mid&\hbox{there is a geodesic from $x$ to $y$}\\
&\hbox{containing no conjugate point}\}.\end{array}

Then, the construction (
3.1
) of
(X_{t},Y_{t})
can be applied to the case when the starting point
(x_{0},y_{0})
is in
\mathcal{E}
and it remains valid until the first exit of
(X_{t},V_{t})
from
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}
. We now modify the construction by
Kendall 1986b
: combine the coupled diffusions
(X_{t},Y_{t})
defined by (
3.1
), while the corresponding
(X_{t},V_{t})
is not too close to the boundary of
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}
, with
X_{t}
,
Y_{t}
evolving independently.

For this, we first need a result on the distance function of two independent diffusions on
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
specified by (
2
). Lemma 3 in Appendix A of the Supplementary Material ensures the following property of
\rho(x,y)
on neighbourhoods of the cut locus

\mathcal{C}:=\{(x,y)\in\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits\times\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits\mid y\hbox{ lies in the cut locus of }x\}

of
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits\times\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
: there is a set
\mathcal{C}_{0}\subset\mathcal{C}
such that

(i)

\mathcal{C}_{0}
contains the (first)-conjugate part of
\mathcal{C}
;

(ii)

for any
(x,y)\in\mathcal{C}\setminus\mathcal{C}_{0}
, there is a neighbourhood
\mathcal{N}
of
(x,y)
in
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits\times\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
and two smooth functions
\varrho_{1}
and
\varrho_{2}
on
\mathcal{N}
such that

\rho(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=\min\{\varrho_{1}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),\varrho_{2}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\},\qquad\forall(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in\mathcal{N}.

Since the (first)-conjugate part of
\mathcal{C}
has co-dimension 2 in
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits\times\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
(see
Barden and Le 1997
), the result of that Lemma also implies that
\mathcal{C}_{0}
can be chosen to have co-dimension 2. Also, similarly to the argument at the beginning of the proof of Theorem 1,
\mathcal{N}
in
(ii)
above can be chosen such that
\text{Exp}^{-1}(\mathcal{N})
is a disjoint union of two open sets
\mathcal{V}_{1}
,
\mathcal{V}_{2}
in
\mathop{\boldsymbol{T\!M}}\nolimits
and, restricted to each
\mathcal{V}_{i}
, Exp is a diffeomorphism from that set to
\mathcal{N}
. Then, the smooth function
\varrho_{i}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
constructed in the proof of Lemma 3 in Appendix A of the Supplementary Material is in fact the length of the geodesic from
x^{\prime}
and
y^{\prime}
, the initial tangent vector
v_{i}
to which lies in
\mathcal{V}_{i}
. That is, using our notation for the length of intervening geodesics, we have
\varrho_{i}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=\tilde{\rho}(x^{\prime},\exp_{x^{\prime}}(v_{i}))
. This leads to the following generalisation of Theorem 5 of
Kendall 1986b
and of Theorem 3 of
Barden and Le 1997
. The proof of this generalisation is a slight modification of the proof for Theorem 3 of
Barden and Le 1997
(see also
Le and Barden 1995
for more detailed derivations), and we hence omit it here.

Lemma 1
.

Suppose that
X_{t}
and
Y_{t}
are independent diffusions on
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
, both satisfying (
2
). Then, the distance
\rho(X_{t},Y_{t})
is a semimartingale and, before the first time that
X_{t}=Y_{t}
,

\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits\rho(X_{t},Y_{t})=\sqrt{2}\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits B_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\left\{\mathcal{L}_{\phi,1}\rho(X_{t},Y_{t})+\mathcal{L}_{\phi,2}\rho(X_{t},Y_{t})\right\}\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits t-\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits L_{t},

where
B_{t}
is a Brownian motion on
\mathbb{R}
;
L
is a non-decreasing process that is locally constant outside
\mathcal{C}
; and, for fixed
x_{0}
and
x\not=x_{0}
,

\mathcal{L}_{\phi,1}\rho(x,x_{0})\!:=\!\!\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\hbox{if }(x,x_{0})\in\mathcal{C}_{0};\\
\!\!\!\dfrac{1}{2}\{L_{\phi}\tilde{\rho}(\exp_{x_{0}}\!(v_{1}),x_{0})\!+\!L_{\phi}\tilde{\rho}(\exp_{x_{0}}\!(v_{2}),x_{0})\}&\hbox{if }(x,x_{0})\in\mathcal{C}\!\setminus\!\mathcal{C}_{0};\\
L_{\phi}\rho(x,x_{0})&\hbox{otherwise},\end{array}\right.

and
\mathcal{L}_{\phi,2}\rho
is similarly defined with respect to the second argument of
\rho
, and where the operator
L_{\phi}
is defined by
L_{\phi}=\frac{1}{2}\{\Delta-\langle\nabla\phi,\,\nabla\rangle\}
.

To detect that the coupled
(X_{t},Y_{t})
, constructed by (
3.1
), is close to the boundary of
\mathcal{E}
and to control the independent diffusions
X_{t}
and
Y_{t}
, we need the following generalisation of a geometric description (see
Kendall 1986b
), wherein we replace the Laplacian operator considered there with
\mathcal{L}_{\phi}
, and replace the lower bound constant

determining the set
\mathcal{O}_{c}
(which was denoted by

by
Kendall 1986b
) by
c\rho(x,y)
. Since
\phi
is in
\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits)
, the proof for our result is analogous to that for the lemma in
Kendall 1986b
, and we omit it here.

Lemma 2
.

For any
c>0
,

\mathcal{O}_{c}\subset\bar{\mathcal{O}}_{c}\subset\tilde{\mathcal{E}},

where

\mathcal{O}_{c}:=\{(x,v)\in\tilde{\mathcal{E}}\mid\mathcal{L}_{\phi,1}\tilde{\rho}(x,\exp_{x}(v))+\mathcal{L}_{\phi,2}\tilde{\rho}(x,\exp_{x}(v))>-2c\rho(x,\exp_{x}(v))\}

and, as before,
\tilde{\rho}(x,exp_{x}(v))
denotes the length of the intervening geodesic
\gamma(t)=\exp_{x}(tv)
,
0\leqslant t\leqslant 1
.

We are now ready to prove the following result for Riemannian manifolds
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
with non-empty conjugate locus (e.g., spheres), which is weaker than Theorem
1
in that the exponential contraction between the diffusions towards their initial points is in expection and not pathwise.

Theorem 2
.

Assume that the Bakry-Emery curvature criterion (A1) holds for a constant
\kappa>0
. Then, for any
\ell\geqslant 1
and for any
x_{0},y_{0}\in\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
, there is a pair of diffusions
(X_{t},Y_{t})
starting from
(x_{0},y_{0})
such that both
X_{t}
and
Y_{t}
satisfy (
2
) and

(37)

\displaystyle\operatorname{E}\left[\rho(X_{t},Y_{t})^{\ell}\right]\leqslant\rho(x_{0},y_{0})^{\ell}e^{-\ell\kappa t},\qquad t\geqslant 0.

Note that, unlike the result of Theorem
1
, the
(X_{t},Y_{t})
constructed here will depend on
\ell
.

Proof.

Let
\kappa>0
be the constant in Bakry-Emery curvature criterion (A1) For given
\ell\in[1,n]
, fix
\delta_{n}>0
sufficiently large such that

(i)

\delta_{n}>\kappa+4(n-1)/r_{0}^{2}
, where
r_{0}>0
is the minimum of the injectivity radius and a fixed positive constant
r_{0}^{\prime}
say;

(ii)

O_{\delta_{n}}\supset\{(x,y)\in\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits\times\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits\mid\rho(x,y)<r_{0}/2\}
, where
O_{\delta}=\text{Exp}\left(\mathcal{O}_{\delta}\right)
and where
\mathcal{O}_{c}
is the subset of
\mathop{\boldsymbol{T\!M}}\nolimits
as defined in Lemma
2
above.

We now construct diffusions
X_{t}
and
Y_{t}
, both satisfying (
2
), as follows. For given
(x_{0},y_{0})\in\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits\times\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
, if there is a minimal geodesic between them which contains no conjugate point, we construct diffusions
X_{t}
and
Y_{t}
by solving (
3.1
) beginning at
(x_{0},y_{0})
. By allowing the corresponding
(X_{t},V_{t})
to jump if necessary, as commented following the construction (
3.1
), we continue such a construction for
(X_{t},Y_{t})
until the first time that
(X_{t},V_{t})
leaves
\mathcal{O}_{2\delta_{n}}
. Suppose that
(X_{t},V_{t})
leaves
\mathcal{O}_{2\delta_{n}}
at time
\tau
. We then consider all minimal geodesics between
X_{\tau}
and
Y_{\tau}
containing no conjugate point and, if possible, choose one for which the corresponding
(X_{\tau},V_{\tau})
lies in
\bar{\mathcal{O}}_{\delta_{n}}
. We then repeat the construction as before with the chosen new starting point. This iterated construction continues until the choice of such
(X_{\tau},V_{\tau})
in
\bar{\mathcal{O}}_{\delta_{n}}
is no longer possible.

If it is not possible initially to choose a minimal geodesic containing no conjugate point, or if at some stage a choice of the above
(X_{\tau},V_{\tau})
in
\bar{\mathcal{O}}_{\delta_{n}}
is impossible, then we continue the construction of
X_{t}
and
Y_{t}
by evolving them independently until
(X_{t},Y_{t})
hits
\bar{O}_{\delta_{n}}
.

To show that the required result holds for
(X_{t},Y_{t})
constructed in such a way, it is sufficient by Theorem 1 to restrict to the case when
X_{t}
and
Y_{t}
evolve independently. Then,
(X_{t},Y_{t})
is not in
\bar{O}_{\delta_{n}}
. Recalling that a co-dimension 2 set in
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits\times\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
is a polar set of a non-degenerate diffusion on
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits\times\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
it follows from Lemmas
1
and
2
and from the choice of
\delta_{n}
that

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits\left(e^{\ell\kappa t}\rho(X_{t},Y_{t})^{\ell}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits M_{t}+\ell\kappa e^{\ell\kappa t}\rho(X_{t},Y_{t})^{\ell}\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits t

\displaystyle+\,\,\frac{1}{2}\ell e^{\ell\kappa t}\rho(X_{t},Y_{t})^{\ell-1}\left\{\mathcal{L}_{\phi,1}\rho(X_{t},Y_{t})+\mathcal{L}_{\phi,2}\rho(X_{t},Y_{t})\right\}\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits t

\displaystyle+\,\,\ell(\ell-1)\,e^{\ell\kappa t}\rho(X_{t},Y_{t})^{\ell-2}\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits t

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits M_{t}+\ell e^{\ell\kappa t}\rho(X_{t},Y_{t})^{\ell}\left\{\kappa-\delta_{n}+(\ell-1)\rho(X_{t},Y_{t})^{-2}\right\}\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits t

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits M_{t}+\ell e^{\ell\kappa t}\rho(X_{t},Y_{t})^{\ell}\left\{\kappa-\delta_{n}+4(n-1)/r_{0}^{2}\right\}\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits t

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits M_{t},

where
M_{t}
is a martingale. Hence, we have
\operatorname{E}\left[\rho(X_{t},Y_{t})^{\ell}\right]\leqslant\rho(x_{0},y_{0})^{\ell}e^{-\ell\kappa t}
as required.
∎

Remark 2
.

In the literature, there are several ways to construct couplings for proving the existence of contractivity. For example, in the curvature setting, the framework of weighted Riemannian manifolds is now part of a broader one for CD-spaces (see e.g.,
Sturm 2006a
;
Sturm 2006b
). In this context, the existence of contractive couplings was treated by
Kuwada 2010
;
von Renesse and Sturm 2005
. In particular, the Kuwada duality theorem (see
Kuwada 2010
, Theorem 2.2), in conjunction with the implication of contractivity of the heat flow under Curvature-Dimension condition, implies the existence of a contractive coupling such as in the proof of Corollary 1 in
von Renesse and Sturm 2005
. The coupling we construct here, in addition to proving the required contractivity, will also be employed in the Supplementary Material to study certain stochastic vector fields along the paths
X_{x,t}
and
Y_{y,t}
, which play important roles in obtaining the Stein factors.

4.
Solution to the Stein equation and Stein factors

We are now ready to turn our attention to the
Stein equation

(38)

h(x)-\operatorname{E}\left[h(X)\right]=L_{\phi}f_{h}(x),

where
h
belongs to a suitable class of real-valued test functions on
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
. Using the distance process
\rho(X_{x,t},Y_{y,t})
for a pair of diffusions
(X_{x,t},Y_{y,t})
constructed above, in this Section we determine the solution
f_{h}
to the Stein equation (
38
) and examine its properties.

4.1.
The solution
f_{h}

Let

(39)

\mathcal{H}_{0}:=\{h\in\mathcal{C}_{0}(\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits)\mid h\hbox{ is Lipschitz with }C_{0}(h)<\infty\}.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
be a complete and connected Riemannian manifold. Assume that the Bakry-Emery curvature criterion (A1) holds for a constant
\kappa>0
and that
X
is a random variable on
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
with distribution
\mu_{\phi}
such that
\operatorname{E}\left[\rho(X,x)\right]<\infty
for some
x\in\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
.
For every
h\in\mathcal{H}_{0}
the function

(40)

f_{h}(x):=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left\{\operatorname{E}\left[h(X)\right]-\operatorname{E}\left[h(X_{x,t})\right]\right\}\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits t

is (i) well-defined; (ii) Lipschitz with constant
C_{0}(f_{h})\leqslant C_{0}(h)/\kappa
.

Remark 3
.

If
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits=\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
\text{Ric}(u,u^{\prime})+\hbox{Hess}^{\phi}(u,u^{\prime})=\hbox{Hess}^{\phi}(u,u^{\prime})
. Thus, Proposition
1
(ii) recovers the corresponding result in
Mackey and Gorham 2016
, as the constant
2\kappa
here corresponds to constant
k
there. Moreover, the result of Proposition
1
(ii) is equivalent to that of Proposition 6.1 in
Thompson 2020
.

Proof.

Let
(X_{x,t},Y_{y,t})
be the pair of diffusions in Theorem
2
with
\ell=1
, starting from
(x,y)
. Then, both
X_{x,t}
and
Y_{y,t}
satisfy (
2
). Since
\mu_{\phi}
is the invariant measure for
Y_{t}
, using the Lipschitz property of
h
and Theorem
2
,

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{\infty}\left\{\operatorname{E}\left[h(X)\right]-\operatorname{E}\left[h(X_{x,t})\right]\right\}\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits t\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits}\left\{\operatorname{E}\left[h(Y_{y,t})\right]-\operatorname{E}\left[h(X_{x,t})\right]\right\}\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits\mu_{\phi}(y)\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits t\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle C_{0}(h)\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits}\operatorname{E}\left[\rho(X_{x,t},Y_{y,t})\right]\,\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits\mu_{\phi}(y)\,\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits t

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle C_{0}(h)\operatorname{E}\left[\rho(X,x)\right]\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\kappa t}\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits t<\infty.

This proves that
f_{h}
is well-defined. Now, for any
x,y\in\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
,

\displaystyle|f_{h}(y)-f_{h}(x)|

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\left|\operatorname{E}\left[h(Y_{y,t})\right]-\operatorname{E}\left[h(X_{x,t})\right]\right|\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits t

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle C_{0}(h)\int_{0}^{\infty}\operatorname{E}\left[\rho(X_{x,t},Y_{y,t})\right]\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits t

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle C_{0}(h)\rho(x,y)\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\kappa t}\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits t=\frac{1}{\kappa}C_{0}(h)\rho(x,y).

∎

The next result shows that the function
f_{h}
defined by (
40
) solves the Stein equation for the probability measure
\mu_{\phi}
.

Theorem 3
.

Assume that
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
is a complete and connected Riemannian manifold and that Bakry-Emery curvature criterion (A1) holds for a constant
\kappa>0
. Let
X
be a random variable on
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
with distribution
\mu_{\phi}
such that
\operatorname{E}\left[\rho(X,x)\right]<\infty
for some
x\in\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
. For
h\in\mathcal{H}_{0}
, the function
f_{h}
in (
40
) solves the Stein equation (
38
).

Remark 4
.

When
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits=\mathbb{R}^{m}
this result recovers the result by
Mackey and Gorham 2016
; in particular,
\operatorname{E}\left[L_{\phi}f_{h}(X)\right]=0
. On the other hand, the Bakry-Emery curvature criterion (A1) implies certain restrictions on the probability measures to which we can apply Theorem
3
. For example, as noted in Example
1
(i), one cannot apply it to von Mises-Fisher distributions on the circle.
In this case, using direct integration by parts, for probability measures
\mu_{\phi}
with
X\sim\mu_{\phi}
on
S^{1}
, the function

g_{h}(x)=c(\phi)e^{\phi(x)}\left\{a+\int_{-\pi}^{x}(h(y)-\operatorname{E}\left[h(X)\right])\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits\mu_{\phi}(y)\right\},

for a constant
a
,
solves the Stein equation
h(x)-\operatorname{E}\left[h(X)\right]=g^{\prime}_{h}(x)-\phi^{\prime}(x)\,g_{h}(x)
associated with first-order Stein operator
A_{\phi}g-\phi g=g^{\prime}-\phi^{\prime}\,g
(see
Lewis 2021
).

Proof.

Let
X_{x,t}
be a diffusion starting from
x
and satisfying (
2
). Since the corresponding semigroup
\{P_{t}\mid t\geqslant 0\}
is strongly continuous on
\mathcal{C}_{0}(\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits)
and
L_{\phi}
is the infinitesimal generator of
X_{x,t}
, we have

(P_{t}h)(x)-h(x)=L_{\phi}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\operatorname{E}\left[h(X_{x,s})\right]\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits s\right)

for
h\in\mathcal{C}_{0}(\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits)

(
Ethier and Kurtz 1986
, Prop. 1.5)
. However, for
\tilde{h}(x)=h(x)+a
where
a\in\mathbb{R}
,
\tilde{h}(x)-\operatorname{E}\left[\tilde{h}(X)\right]=h(x)-\operatorname{E}\left[h(X)\right]
. Then, by taking
a=\operatorname{E}\left[h(X)\right]
and noting
L_{\phi}(a)=0
, we can also write the above as

(41)

\displaystyle(P_{t}h)(x)-h(x)=-L_{\phi}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\left\{\operatorname{E}\left[h(X)\right]-\operatorname{E}\left[h(X_{x,s})\right]\right\}\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits s\right).

Now, take
(X_{x,t},Y_{y,t})
to be the pair of diffusions, starting from
(x,y)
, as Theorem
2
with
\ell=1
. Since
Y_{t}
satisfies (
2
), the fact that
\mu_{\phi}
is the invariant measure of
Y_{t}
gives that

\displaystyle\big|\operatorname{E}\left[h(X)\right]-(P_{t}h)(x)\big|=\left|\int_{\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits}\left\{\operatorname{E}\left[h(Y_{y,t})\right]-\operatorname{E}\left[h(X_{x,t})\right]\right\}\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits\mu_{\phi}(y)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle C_{0}(h)\int_{\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits}\operatorname{E}\left[\rho(Y_{y,t},X_{x,t})\right]\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits\mu_{\phi}(y)\leqslant C_{0}(h)\operatorname{E}\left[\rho(X,x)\right]e^{-\kappa t},

where the last inequality follows from Theorem
2
and where
C_{0}(h)
is the Lipschitz constant for
h
. Thus,

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}(P_{t}h)(x)=\operatorname{E}\left[h(X)\right].

On the other hand, the result of Theorem
2
implies that we may apply the Dominated Convergence Theorem to obtain that, as
t\rightarrow\infty
, the right hand side of (
41
) tends to
-L_{\phi}f_{h}(x)
, so that

h(x)-\operatorname{E}\left[h(X)\right]=L_{\phi}f_{h}(x)

as required.
∎

4.2.
Stein factors

In the literature, Stein factors refer to bounds on solutions
f_{h}
of the Stein equation (
38
).
A direct consequence of Proposition
1
and Theorem
3
is that
f_{h}
defined by (
40
) is differentiable and
Df_{h}
is bounded.

Proposition 2
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
3
,
Df_{h}
exists and

\sup_{x\in\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits}\|Df_{h}(x)\|_{op}\leqslant C_{0}(h)/\kappa

where
f_{h}
is defined by (
40
).

We will see later in Section
5.1
that the bound on
Df_{h}
given above suffices to bound the Wasserstein distance between the probability measure
\mu_{\phi}
and another
\mu_{\psi}\propto e^{-\psi}
. However, for bounding more general integral (semi-)metrics, bounds on first- and second-order derivatives of
f_{h}
, known as Stein factors, are needed.

Accordingly, denote by
\text{Ric}^{\sharp}_{\phi}
the tensor equivalent to
\text{Ric}+\hbox{Hess}^{\phi}
in the sense that, for any
x\in\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
, and for any
u,u^{\prime}\in T_{x}(\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits)

(42)

\displaystyle\langle\text{Ric}^{\sharp}_{\phi}(u),\,u^{\prime}\rangle=\text{Ric}(u,u^{\prime})+\hbox{Hess}^{\phi}(u,u^{\prime}).

Recall that (see
O’Neill 1983
)

(43)

\displaystyle\hbox{Hess}^{\phi}(u,u^{\prime})=\langle D_{u}(\nabla\phi),\,u^{\prime}\rangle\thickspace,

and that, in terms of a (local) frame field
e_{1},\cdots,e_{m}
,

\text{Ric}(u,u^{\prime})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\langle R(u,e_{i})e_{i},\,u^{\prime}\rangle,

where
R
denotes the Riemannian curvature tensor. Thus, it is possible to express
\text{Ric}^{\sharp}_{\phi}
explicitly in terms of the frame field as

(44)

\displaystyle\text{Ric}^{\sharp}_{\phi}(u)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}R(u,e_{i})e_{i}+D_{u}(\nabla\phi).

We can define the Lipschitz constant for
\text{Ric}^{\sharp}_{\phi}
in a similar way to the definition of the Lipschitz constant given in (
1
). Let

(45)

\mathcal{H}_{1}:=\{h\in\mathcal{C}_{0}(\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits)\cap\mathcal{C}^{1}(\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits)\mid C_{0}(h)<\infty,\thickspace C_{1}(h)<\infty\}.

Proposition 3
.

Assume that the conditions of Theorem
3
hold. Assume further that
{\rm Ric}^{\sharp}_{\phi}
is Lipschitz with finite Lipschitz constant
L({\rm Ric}^{\sharp}_{\phi})
. For every
h\in\mathcal{H}_{1}
with
f_{h}
defined in (
40
),
Df_{h}
is Lipschitz with constant

C_{1}(f_{h})\leqslant C_{1}(h)\frac{1}{2\kappa}+C_{0}(h)\frac{L({\rm Ric}^{\sharp}_{\phi})}{2\kappa^{2}}.

Remark 5
.

As noted in Remark
3
, if
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits=\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
\langle\text{Ric}^{\sharp}_{\phi}(u),\,u^{\prime}\rangle=\hbox{Hess}^{\phi}(u,u^{\prime})
. Then, since
\hbox{Hess}^{\phi}=D^{2}\phi
,
L(\text{Ric}^{\sharp}_{\phi})=C_{2}(\phi)
. Thus, Proposition
3
recovers the corresponding result in
Mackey and Gorham 2016
.
On the other hand, the result of Proposition
3
differs from the corresponding Proposition 6.2 in
Thompson 2020
: in theirs, the relationship between the constant
c_{1}
obtained and those given in the assumptions is not specified; using our notation, the upper bound for
C_{1}(f_{h})
there would depend only on
C_{0}(h)
while ours depends on both
C_{0}(h)
and
C_{1}(h)
.

Proof.

The proof uses Lemmas 4 and 5 given in Appendix B of Supplementary Material. For any
x\in\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
and
v\in T_{x}(\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits)
, consider the vector field
v^{x}_{t}
along the path
X_{x,t}
which solves the differential equation

(46)

\displaystyle\frac{Dv^{x}_{t}}{\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits t}=-\frac{1}{2}\text{Ric}^{\sharp}_{\phi}(v^{x}_{t})

with
v^{x}_{0}=v
, where
X_{x,t}
is the solution to (
2
). It is known that, for any fixed
t>0
and under the given condition for
h
,
N_{s}=D\operatorname{E}\left[h(X_{X_{x,s},t-s})\right](v^{x}_{s})
is a local martingale for
0\leqslant s\leqslant t
(see
Thalmaier 1997
). Since

|N_{s}|\leqslant\|D\operatorname{E}\left[h(X_{X_{x,s},t-s})\right]\|_{op}|v^{x}_{s}|\leqslant C_{0}(h)\,|v^{x}_{s}|,

using Lemma 4 (Appendix B of Supplementary Material) with
q=1
, we see that
\operatorname{E}\left[|N_{s}|\right]<\infty
. Hence,
N_{s}
is in fact a martingale on
[0,t]
, and so
\operatorname{E}\left[N_{0}\right]=\operatorname{E}\left[N_{t}\right]
, which in turn gives

D\operatorname{E}\left[h(X_{x,t})\right](v)=\operatorname{E}\left[Dh(X_{x,t})(v^{x}_{t})\right].

(See also
Thompson 2020
, where the
Z
there corresponds to
-2\nabla\phi
here.) Thus, from the definition of
f_{h}
, the Dominated Convergence Theorem and Theorem
2
, it follows that, for any
v\in T_{x}(\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits)
,

(47)

\displaystyle Df_{h}(x)(v)=\int_{0}^{\infty}D\operatorname{E}\left[h(X_{x,t})\right](v)\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits t=\int_{0}^{\infty}\operatorname{E}\left[Dh(X_{x,t})(v^{x}_{t})\right]\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits t.

Now, consider the pair of diffusions
(X_{x,t},Y_{y,t})
, starting from
(x,y)
, in Theorem
2
with
\ell=2
. First, by applying the Hölder inequality, Theorem
2
and Lemma 4 (Appendix B of Supplementary Material), we have that

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{rcl}&&\operatorname{E}\left[\left|\left(Dh(X_{x,t})-\Pi_{\gamma^{\vphantom{A}}_{X_{x,t},Y_{y,t}}}Dh(Y_{y,t})\right)(v^{x}_{t})\right|\right]\\
&\leqslant&C_{1}(h)\operatorname{E}\left[\rho(X_{x,t},Y_{y,t})\,|v^{x}_{t}|\right]\vskip 2.84544pt\leqslant C_{1}(h)\rho(x,y)\,|v|\,e^{-2\kappa t}.\end{array}

Moreover, writing
v^{y}_{t}
for the solution of (
61
) with the underlying path
X_{x,t}
replaced by
Y_{y,t}
and with the initial condition
v^{y}_{0}=\Pi_{\gamma_{x,y}}(v)
, and denoting
\Pi_{\gamma^{\vphantom{A}}_{X_{x,t},Y_{y,t}}}(v^{x}_{t})
by
\tilde{v}^{x}_{t}
, we also have

(51)

\displaystyle\operatorname{E}\left[\left|\left(Dh(Y_{y,t})(\tilde{v}^{x}_{t}-v^{y}_{t})\right)\right|\right]\leqslant C_{0}(h)\operatorname{E}\left[\left|\tilde{v}^{x}_{t}-v^{y}_{t}\right|\right]\leqslant C_{0}(h)\dfrac{L(\text{Ric}^{\sharp}_{\phi})}{2\kappa}\rho(x,y)\,|v|\,e^{-\kappa t},

where the second inequality follows from Lemma 5 (Appendix B of Supplementary Material) with
q=1
.

Finally, noting that
\Pi_{\gamma_{x,y}}(Df_{h}(y))(v)=Df_{h}(y)(\Pi_{\gamma_{x,y}}(v))
, together with (
47
), (
4.2
) and (
51
), implies that

\displaystyle|(Df_{h}(x)-\Pi_{\gamma_{x,y}}Df_{h}(y))(v)|\vskip 2.84544pt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|Df_{h}(x)(v)-Df_{h}(y)(\Pi_{\gamma_{x,y}}(v))|

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\left|\operatorname{E}\left[Dh(X_{x,t})(v^{x}_{t})-Dh(Y_{y,t})(v^{y}_{t})\right]\right|\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits t

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\operatorname{E}\left[\left|\left(Dh(X_{x,t})-\Pi_{\gamma^{\vphantom{A}}_{X_{x,t},Y_{y,t}}}Dh(Y_{y,t})\right)(v^{x}_{t})\right|\right]\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits t

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{\infty}\operatorname{E}\left[\left|Dh(Y_{y,t})(\tilde{v}^{x}_{t}-v^{y}_{t})\right|\right]\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits t

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\left\{C_{1}(h)\frac{1}{2\kappa}+C_{0}(h)\frac{L(\text{Ric}^{\sharp}_{\phi})}{2\kappa^{2}}\right\}\rho(x,y)|v|,

i.e.
Df_{h}
is Lipschitz with the required constant.
∎

The argument in Remark
5
regarding the case when
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits=\mathbb{R}^{m}
can be extended to the case when
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
has constant Ricci curvature, which implies that the bounds in
Mackey and Gorham 2016
continue to hold for such
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
. This gives the following corollary.

Corollary 1
.

Assume that the conditions of Theorem
3
hold. Assume further that
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
is
Ric
flat and
\phi
has finite Lipschitz constant
C_{2}(\phi)
. Then, for every
h\in\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
f_{h}
as defined in (

),

is Lipschitz with constant

C_{1}(f_{h})\leqslant C_{1}(h)\frac{1}{2\kappa}+C_{0}(h)\frac{C_{2}(\phi)}{2\kappa^{2}}.

The curvature of the manifold plays a more explicit role in the Lipschitz constant for
D^{2}f_{h}
. To see this, define the tensor
\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits^{\star}R
by

\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits^{\star}R(u,v)=-\hbox{tr}D_{.}R(\cdot,u)v.

Then
\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits^{\star}R
satisfies

\langle\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits^{\star}R(v_{1},v_{2}),\,v_{3}\rangle=\langle(D_{v_{3}}\hbox{Ric}^{\sharp})(v_{1}),\,v_{2}\rangle-\langle(D_{v_{2}}\hbox{Ric}^{\sharp})(v_{3}),\,v_{1}\rangle.

Noting that
R(\nabla\phi)(u,v)=R(\nabla\phi,u)v
, to simplify notation, we also define

(52)

\displaystyle R^{\sharp}_{\phi}=\mathop{\rm d\!}\nolimits^{\star}\!R+D{\rm Ric}^{\sharp}_{\phi}+R(\nabla\phi).

The bound on
Df_{h}
requires restriction to the smaller and smoother class
\mathcal{H}_{1}
; the same is required when bounding

. Let

(53)

\mathcal{H}_{2}:=\{h\in\mathcal{C}_{0}(\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits)\cap\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits)\mid C_{0}(h)<\infty,\thickspace C_{1}(h)<\infty,\thickspace C_{2}(h)<\infty\}.

Proposition 4
.

Assume that the conditions of Theorem
3
hold and that

\chi^{\phantom{A}}_{1}=\sup_{x\in\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits}\|R^{\sharp}_{\phi}\|_{op}(x)\quad\hbox{and}\quad\chi^{\phantom{A}}_{2}=m\sup_{x\in\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits}\|R\|^{2}_{op}(x)

are both finite, where

is defined by (
63
). Further, assume that
{\rm Ric}^{\sharp}_{\phi}
,
R^{\sharp}_{\phi}
and
R
are all Lipschitz with finite Lipschitz constants
L({\rm Ric}^{\sharp}_{\phi})
,
L(R^{\sharp}_{\phi})
and
L(R)
respectively. For every
h\in\mathcal{H}_{2}
with
f_{h}
defined in (
40
):

(i)

If
\chi^{\phantom{A}}_{2}=0
,
D^{2}f_{h}
exists and is Lipschitz with constant

C_{2}(f_{h})\leqslant\frac{1}{3\kappa}C_{2}(h)+\frac{3}{4\kappa^{2}}C_{1}(h)\,C_{2}(\phi)+C_{0}(h)\left(\frac{1}{4\kappa^{2}}C_{3}(\phi)+\frac{3}{4\kappa^{3}}C_{2}(\phi)^{2}\right).

(ii)

If
\chi^{\phantom{A}}_{2}>0
and
\kappa>1/2
, then
D^{2}f_{h}
exists and is Lipschitz with constant

\displaystyle C_{2}(f_{h})

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle C_{2}(h)\frac{1}{3\kappa}+C_{1}(h)\left\{\frac{L({\rm Ric}^{\sharp}_{\phi})}{2\kappa^{2}}+\frac{4}{8\kappa-1}\left(\frac{\chi_{1}^{2}+2\chi^{\phantom{A}}_{2}}{4\kappa+1}\right)^{1/2}\right\}

\displaystyle+\,\,C_{0}(h)\frac{2\tilde{\beta}}{2\kappa-1}

where

\tilde{\beta}^{2}=\frac{\beta_{1}}{4\kappa+1}+\frac{\beta_{2}}{3\kappa+1}+\frac{\beta_{3}}{2\kappa+1},

with

\displaystyle\beta_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2mL(R)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}L(R^{\sharp}_{\phi})^{2}+\frac{\chi_{1}^{2}+6\chi^{\phantom{A}}_{2}}{4\kappa+1}L({\rm Ric}^{\sharp}_{\phi})^{2},

\displaystyle\beta_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\chi^{\phantom{A}}_{1}}{\kappa}L({\rm Ric}^{\sharp}_{\phi})\,L(R^{\sharp}_{\phi}),

\displaystyle\beta_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\kappa^{2}}\left(\frac{\chi_{1}^{2}}{2}+2\chi^{\phantom{A}}_{2}\right)L({\rm Ric}^{\sharp}_{\phi})^{2}.

Remark 6
.

Note that,
\chi^{\phantom{A}}_{2}=0
corresponds to
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
being a flat manifold, such as a Euclidean space, a cylinder or a flat torus. Consequently,
\chi^{\phantom{A}}_{1}=L({\rm Ric}^{\sharp}_{\phi})=C_{2}(\phi)
and
L(R^{\sharp}_{\phi})=C_{3}(\phi)
. Our result thus recovers the corresponding bound given in
Mackey and Gorham 2016
for
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, where
L_{i}
,

and

in
Mackey and Gorham 2016
correspond respectively to
C_{i-1}(\phi)
,
C_{i-1}(h)
and

here. Our result establishes that their upper bound also holds for general complete and connected flat manifolds.

On the other hand, if
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits

5.
Application to bounding integral (semi-)metrics

A key application of Stein’s method is in obtaining upper bounds on an integral (semi-)metric
d_{\mathcal{H}}(X,Z)
, with respect to some function class
\mathcal{H}
, for an arbitrary random variable
Z\sim\nu
. Exploiting the characterising property of the operator
L_{\phi}
,

\operatorname{E}\left[h(Z)\right]-\operatorname{E}\left[h(X)\right]=\operatorname{E}\left[L_{\phi}f_{h}(Z)\right],\qquad\forall h\in\mathcal{H},

the task then reduces to obtaining a uniform upper bound on
\operatorname{E}\left[L_{\phi}f_{h}(Z)\right]
over functions
f_{h}
using the Stein factors. The quantity
d_{\mathcal{H}}
is clearly a semi-metric and is a metric only if
\mathcal{H}
separates points in the set of signed measures on
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
.

5.1.
Wasserstein distance between
\mu_{\phi}
and
\mu_{\psi}

The result of Theorem
3
in conjunction with the first-order bound in Proposition
2
can be used to obtain an upper bound on the 1-Wasserstein distance between certain types of random variables. For this we consider the function class

\mathcal{H}^{1}_{\leq 1}:=\{h\in\mathcal{C}(\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits)\mid h\text{ is Lipschitz with }C_{0}(h)\leqslant 1\},

under which
d_{\mathcal{H}}
is a bonafide metric.
The 1-Wasserstein distance between two random variables
Z_{1}
and
Z_{2}
on
\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
is then defined as

d_{\mathcal{W}}(Z_{1},Z_{2}):=\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}^{1}_{\leq 1}}|\operatorname{E}\left[h(Z_{1})\right]-\operatorname{E}\left[h(Z_{2})\right]|.

Theorem 4
.

Assume that the conditions of Theorem
3
hold. Let
Z\sim\mu_{\psi}
such that
\operatorname{E}\left[\rho(Z,x)\right]<\infty
for some
x\in\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits
, where
\psi
satisfies (
3
) with some constant
\kappa^{\prime}>0
. Then

d_{\mathcal{W}}(Z,X)\leqslant\frac{1}{2\kappa}\operatorname{E}\left[\left|\nabla(\psi-\phi)(Z)\right|\right].

Proof.

The proof pursues a similar argument to that of Proposition 4.1 of
Mijoule et al. 2019
. Note first that

\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}^{1}_{\leq 1}}|\operatorname{E}\left[h(Z)\right]-\operatorname{E}\left[h(X)\right]|=\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}^{1}_{\leq 1}\cap\mathcal{C}_{0}(\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits)}|\operatorname{E}\left[h(Z)\right]-\operatorname{E}\left[h(X)\right]|.

For
h\in\mathcal{H}^{1}_{\leq 1}\cap\mathcal{C}_{0}(\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits)
, we have by Theorem
3
that

\operatorname{E}\left[h(Z)\right]-\operatorname{E}\left[h(X)\right]=\operatorname{E}\left[L_{\phi}f_{h}(Z)\right].

On the other hand, the given assumption that
Z\sim\mu_{\psi}
, where
\psi
satisfies (
3
), also implies that
\operatorname{E}\left[L_{\psi}f_{h}(Z)\right]=0
for
h\in\mathcal{H}^{1}_{\leq 1}\cap\mathcal{C}_{0}(\mathop{\boldsymbol{M}}\nolimits)
. Noting that

L_{\phi}f_{h}(x)=L_{\psi}f_{h}(x)+\frac{1}{2}\langle\nabla\psi(x)-\nabla\phi(x),f_{h}(x)\rangle,

we obtain

\operatorname{E}\left[h(Z)\right]-\operatorname{E}\left[h(X)\right]=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{E}\left[\langle\nabla\psi(Z)-\nabla\phi(Z),\nabla f_{h}(Z)\rangle\right],

so that the result follows from Proposition
2
.
∎
