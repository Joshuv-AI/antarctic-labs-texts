Painlevé IV Critical Asymptotics for Orthogonal Polynomials in the Complex Plane This paper is a contribution to the Special Issue on Painlevé Equations and Applications in Memory of Andrei Kapaev. The full collection is available at https://www.emis.de/journals/SIGMA/Kapaev.html
Marco BERTOLA †‡, José Gustavo ELIAS REBELO † and Tamara GRAVA †§

Abstract
We study the asymptotic behaviour of orthogonal polynomials in the complex plane that are associated to a certain normal matrix model. The model depends on a parameter and the asymptotic distribution of the eigenvalues undergoes a transition for a special value of the parameter, where it develops a corner-type singularity. In the double scaling limit near the transition we determine the asymptotic behaviour of the orthogonal polynomials in terms of a solution of the Painlevé IV equation. We determine the Fredholm determinant associated to such solution and we compute it numerically on the real line, showing also that the corresponding Painlevé transcendent is pole-free on a semiaxis.

Introduction

In this work we consider the critical asymptotic behaviour for the orthogonal polynomials that are associated to the matrix model described here. Over the set of normal
n\times n
matrices
\mathcal{N}_{n}:=\{M\colon[M,M^{\star}]=0\}\subset\operatorname{Mat}_{n\times n}(\mathbb{C})
we consider the measure of the form

\displaystyle M\to\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{n,N}}{\rm e}^{-N\operatorname{Tr}(W(M))}\mathrm{d}M,\qquad\mathcal{Z}_{n,N}=\int_{\mathcal{N}_{n}}{\rm e}^{-N\operatorname{Tr}(W(M))}\mathrm{d}M,

(1.1)

where
{\rm d}M
stands for the induced volume form on
\mathcal{N}_{n}
, invariant under conjugation by unitary matrices.

Since normal matrices are diagonalizable by unitary transformations, the probability density (
1.1
) can be reduced to the form
[
35
]

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{Z_{n,N}}\prod_{i<j}|\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}|^{2}{\rm e}^{-N\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}{W(\lambda_{i})}}\mathrm{d}A(\lambda_{1})\cdots\mathrm{d}A(\lambda_{n}),

(1.2)

where
\lambda_{j}
are the complex eigenvalues of the normal matrix
M

[
11
]
and
{\rm d}A(\lambda)={\rm d}\operatorname{Re}(\lambda){\rm d}\operatorname{Im}(\lambda)
is the area element in the complex plane.

Associated to the above data we consider the sequence of (monic)
orthogonal polynomials

p_{n}(z)=z^{n}+\cdots
,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{C}}p_{n}(\lambda)\overline{p_{m}(\lambda)}{\rm e}^{-NW(\lambda)}\mathrm{d}A(\lambda)=h_{n,N}\delta_{n,m},\qquad h_{n,N}>0,\qquad n,m=0,1,\dots,

(1.3)

where
W\colon\mathbb{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the external potential.

Likewise in the standard GUE, the orthogonal polynomial
p_{n}(z)
is also the expectation value of the characteristic polynomial of the random normal matrix
M
:
p_{n}(z)=\langle\det(z{\mathbf{1}}-M)\rangle
, where the expectation is with respect to the measure (
1.1
) (see, e.g.,
[
18
]
). The statistical quantities related to the eigenvalues of matrix models can be expressed in terms of the associated orthogonal polynomials,
p_{n}(\lambda)
. In particular, the probability measure (
1.2
) can be written as a determinantal point field (sometimes also called “process”) with Christoffel–Darboux kernel

\displaystyle K(\lambda,\eta)={\rm e}^{-N(W(\lambda)+W(\eta))/2}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\frac{1}{h_{j,N}}p_{j}(\lambda)\overline{p_{j}(\eta)}.

The average density of eigenvalues is

\displaystyle\rho_{n,N}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{n}K(\lambda,\lambda)

and in the limit

\displaystyle n\to\infty,\qquad N\to\infty,\qquad\frac{N}{n}\to\frac{1}{T},

it converges to the unique probability measure,
\mu^{*}
, in the plane which minimizes the functional
[
19
,
26
]

\displaystyle I(\mu)=\iint\log|\lambda-\eta|^{-1}\mathrm{d}\mu(\lambda)\mathrm{d}\mu(\eta)+\frac{1}{T}\int W(\lambda)\mathrm{d}\mu(\lambda).

The functional
I(\mu)
is the Coulomb energy functional in two dimensions and the existence of a unique minimizer is a well-established fact under mild assumptions on the potential
W(\lambda)

[
37
]
. If
W(\lambda)
is twice continuously differentiable then the equilibrium measure is given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\mu^{*}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{4\pi T}\Delta W(\lambda)\chi_{D}(\lambda)\mathrm{d}A(\lambda),

where
\chi_{D}
is the characteristic function of the compact support set
D=\operatorname{supp}(\mu^{*})
and
\Delta=4\partial_{\lambda}\bar{\partial}_{\lambda}
. For example, if
W(\lambda)=|\lambda|^{2}
, one has the Ginibre ensemble
[
23
]
and the measure
\mathrm{d}\mu^{*}(\lambda)
is the uniform measure on the disk of radius
\sqrt{T}
. Following
[
4
]
, we consider the external potential to be of the form

\displaystyle W(\lambda)=|\lambda|^{2d}-t\lambda^{d}-\bar{t}\bar{\lambda}^{d},\qquad\lambda\in\mathbb{C},\qquad d\in{\mathbb{N}},\qquad t\in{\mathbb{C}}^{*}.

(1.4)

The case
d=1
is reducible to Hermite polynomials and we will consider only
d\geq 2
. Up to a rotation
\lambda\mapsto{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\theta}\lambda
we can assume without loss of generality that
t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, leading to the external potential and orthogonality measure

\displaystyle W(\lambda)=|\lambda|^{2d}-t\big(\lambda^{d}+\bar{\lambda}^{d}\big),\qquad\lambda\in\mathbb{C},\qquad{\rm e}^{-N(|\lambda|^{2d}-t(\lambda^{d}+\bar{\lambda}^{d}))}\mathrm{d}A(\lambda).

(1.5)

There remains a residual discrete rotational
\mathbb{Z}_{d}
-symmetry which allows us to further reduce the problem as observed in
[
5
]
and
[
20
]
. The potential
W
in (

) can be written as
W(\lambda)=\frac{1}{d}Q\big(\lambda^{d}\big)
, and hence the equilibrium measure for
W
can be obtained from the equilibrium measure associated to

by an unfolding procedure. Considering the particular case of (
1.4
), the potential

can be rewritten as

\displaystyle Q(u)=d|u|^{2}-dt(u+\bar{u})=d|u-t|^{2}-t^{2}d,

which corresponds to the Ginibre ensemble whose equilibrium measure is the normalized area measure of the disk centered at
u=t
and of radius
t_{c}

\displaystyle|u-t|\leq t_{c},\qquad t_{c}=\sqrt{\frac{T}{d}}.

Pulling back to the
\lambda
-plane, we obtain the equilibrium measure of

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\mu_{W}=\frac{d}{\pi t^{2}_{c}}|\lambda|^{2(d-1)}\chi_{D}\mathrm{d}A(\lambda),

where
\mathrm{d}A
is the area measure and

is the characteristic function of the domain

whose boundary is a

\displaystyle D:=\big\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C},\,\big|\lambda^{d}-t\big|\leq t_{c}\big\}.

(1.6)

Painlevé IV and Fredholm determinant

The purpose of this section is to introduce the Painlevé IV equation and study various aspects related to it that are connected with the work that we will be doing in the following sections.

This will correspond to a special Riemann–Hilbert problem whose properties will be outlined. The reason and motivation for introducing this solution is the fact that this Riemann–Hilbert problem will be seen to be useful in the following section.

2.1
The general Painlevé IV

We recall the Lax pair formulation of the Painlevé IV equation as in the original work of Miwa, Jimbo and Ueno
[
30
,
31
]
, with notation adapted to our purposes.

Consider the matrix–valued function
\Psi
, associated with Painlevé IV, as the fundamental joint solution of the system of linear differential equations, the Lax pair, given by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}\Psi=A(\lambda;s)\Psi,\qquad\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\Psi=B(\lambda;s)\Psi,

(2.1)

where

\displaystyle A(\lambda;s)=-\frac{1}{2}\left(\lambda+s\right)\sigma_{3}+\begin{pmatrix}0&\dfrac{Z}{U}\vskip 2.84526pt\\
-U&0\end{pmatrix}+\frac{1}{\lambda}\begin{pmatrix}\Theta_{\infty}-Z&\dfrac{(\Theta_{\infty}-Z)^{2}-\Theta^{2}_{0}}{YU}\vskip 2.84526pt\\
-UY&Z-\Theta_{\infty}\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle B(\lambda;s)=-\frac{1}{2}\lambda\sigma_{3}+\begin{pmatrix}0&\dfrac{Z}{U}\vskip 2.84526pt\\
-U&0\end{pmatrix},

where
\sigma_{3}
is the third Pauli matrix,
Y
,
Z
and
U
are functions of
s
whose dependence we now derive, while
\Theta_{0}
and
\Theta_{\infty}
are arbitrarily chosen constants. The compatibility of (
2.1
) yields the zero-curvature equation

\displaystyle[\partial_{\lambda}-A,\partial_{s}-B]\equiv 0,

which, in turn, yields the following system of nonlinear ODEs

\displaystyle U^{\prime}=U(Y-s),

\displaystyle Z^{\prime}=ZY+\dfrac{-(Z-\Theta_{\infty})^{2}+\Theta^{2}_{0}}{Y},

\displaystyle Y^{\prime}=-Y^{2}+sY-2Z+2\Theta_{\infty}.

(2.2)

In the equations above the prime means differentiation with respect to
s
. By eliminating
U
,
Z
from the above equations we obtain the classical form of the fourth Painlevé equation

\displaystyle Y^{\prime\prime}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{(Y^{\prime})^{2}}{Y}+\frac{3}{2}Y^{3}-2sY^{2}+\left(1+\frac{s^{2}}{2}-2\Theta_{\infty}\right)Y-\frac{2\Theta^{2}_{0}}{Y}.

(2.3)

2.1.1
Stokes’ phenomenon and the Monodromy problem

The review material of this section is mostly based on
[
21
]

[
30
,
31
]
. The solution of the first equation in (

\displaystyle\Psi_{1}(s)=\begin{bmatrix}H(s)&\dfrac{Z(s)}{U(s)}\vskip 2.84526pt\\
U(s)&-H(s)\end{bmatrix},

(2.4)

\displaystyle\Psi_{2}(s)=\left[\begin{matrix}\dfrac{1}{2}\big({H(s)^{2}+Z(s)-sH(s)}\big)&{\dfrac{Z(s)(Z(s)-\gamma-Y(s)s-H(s)Y(s))}{U(s)Y(s)}}\vskip 2.84526pt\\

(2.5)

\displaystyle\theta(\lambda)=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4}+\frac{s}{2}\lambda,

(2.6)

where

\displaystyle H=\left(s+\frac{2\Theta_{\infty}}{Y}-Y\right)Z-\frac{\Theta_{\infty}^{2}-\Theta_{0}^{2}}{Y}-\frac{Z^{2}}{Y}.

(2.7)

In each of the sectors

More specifically, the type of quadrature formulæ that we will use are the so-called “Gauss–Hermite” quadratures, which are defined in the following way

Definition 2.6
.

Considering an integral of the form

\displaystyle\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}f(x){\rm e}^{-\Lambda x^{2}}\mathrm{d}x,

the
Gauss–Hermite quadrature
is an approximation of the value of this integral and is defined as

\displaystyle\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}f(x){\rm e}^{-\Lambda x^{2}}\mathrm{d}x\simeq\sum^{m}_{i=1}f\big(x^{(m)}_{i}\big)w^{(m)}_{i},

(2.18)

where
m
is the number of points used in this approximation: the points
\big\{x_{j}^{(m)}\big\}_{j=1}^{m}
are called the
nodes
and the coefficients
\big\{w_{j}^{(m)}\big\}_{j=1}^{m}
are called the
weights
of the quadrature rule. The nodes
x^{(m)}_{j}
are the roots of the
m
-th Hermite polynomial
H_{m}\big(\sqrt{\Lambda}x\big)
and the weights
w^{(m)}_{i}
are given by

\displaystyle w^{(m)}_{i}=\frac{2^{m-1}m!\sqrt{\pi}}{\sqrt{\Lambda}m^{2}\big(H_{m-1}\big(\sqrt{\Lambda}x^{(m)}_{i}\big)\big)^{2}}.

We will have to adapt this definition so that it is suitable to be applied to our case.

In order to use this technique for the computation of
F(x,y)
in (
2.15
) we rewrite it as

\displaystyle F(x,y)={\rm e}^{\frac{-x^{2}-y^{2}}{4}}H(x,y),

\displaystyle H(x,y):=\frac{\sin{(\pi\gamma)}}{2\pi^{2}}|x|^{\frac{\gamma}{2}}|y|^{\frac{\gamma}{2}}{\rm e}^{-\frac{s}{2}(x+y)}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}t\frac{({\rm i}t+\varepsilon)^{-\gamma}{\rm e}^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}}{\rm e}^{{\rm i}t(s+\varepsilon)}}{(x-\varepsilon-{\rm i}t)(y-\varepsilon-{\rm i}t)}{\rm e}^{\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}+s\varepsilon}.

Then Nystrom’s method states that the Fredholm determinant is approximated by

\displaystyle\det{}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{-})}\big[\operatorname{Id}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{-})}-\hat{\cal{F}}\big]\simeq\det{}_{n\times n}\left[\operatorname{Id}_{n}-\begin{bmatrix}H\big(x^{(2n)}_{i},x^{(2n)}_{j}\big)\sqrt{w^{(2n)}_{i}w^{(2n)}_{j}}\end{bmatrix}^{n}_{i,j=1}\right],

where the nodes
x^{(2n)}_{i}
and the weights
w^{(2n)}_{i}
are chosen as in (

) with
\Lambda=\frac{1}{2}
and where we are selecting only half of the nodes, namely
n
out of
2n
that lie on the negative real axis. Therefore, the matrix
\mathbb{H}=[H_{jk}]_{j,k=1}^{n}
that we are interested in computing is given by

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}H_{jk}\end{bmatrix}^{n}_{j,k=1}:=\begin{bmatrix}H\big(x^{(2n)}_{j},x^{(2n)}_{k}\big)\sqrt{w^{(2n)}_{j}w^{(2n)}_{k}}\end{bmatrix}^{n}_{j,k=1},

where

\displaystyle H_{jk}=\frac{{\rm e}^{\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}+s\varepsilon}\sin{(\pi\gamma)}}{2\pi^{2}}\big|x^{(2n)}_{j}x^{(2n)}_{k}\big|^{\frac{\gamma}{2}}{\rm e}^{-\frac{s}{2}\big(x^{(2n)}_{j}+x^{(2n)}_{k}\big)}

\displaystyle\hphantom{H_{j k} =}{}\times\sqrt{w^{(2n)}_{j}w^{(2n)}_{k}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}t\frac{{\rm e}^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}}({\rm i}t+\varepsilon)^{-\gamma}{\rm e}^{{\rm i}t(s+\varepsilon)}}{\big(x^{(2n)}_{j}-\varepsilon-{\rm i}t\big)\big(x^{(2n)}_{k}-\varepsilon-{\rm i}t\big)}.

In order to compute the above integral, we once again apply the Gauss–Hermite quadrature

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}t\frac{{\rm e}^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}}({\rm i}t+\varepsilon)^{-\gamma}{\rm e}^{{\rm i}t(s+\varepsilon)}}{\big(x^{(2n)}_{j}-\varepsilon-{\rm i}t\big)\big(x^{(2n)}_{k}-\varepsilon-{\rm i}t\big)}=\sum^{2n}_{\ell=1}\frac{w^{(2n)}_{\ell}\big({\rm i}x^{(2n)}_{\ell}+\varepsilon\big)^{-\gamma}{\rm e}^{{\rm i}x^{(2n)}_{\ell}(s+\varepsilon)}}{\big(x^{(2n)}_{j}-\varepsilon-{\rm i}x^{(2n)}_{\ell}\big)\big(x^{(2n)}_{k}-\varepsilon-{\rm i}x^{(2n)}_{\ell}\big)}.

and this will be accomplished by evaluating perturbatively the error matrix

4.3.1

The exterior parametrix,

(4.10)

4.3.3
The double scaling limit

Let
\phi(z;z_{0})
be defined by

\displaystyle\phi(z;z_{0})=\left(\frac{z-1}{z_{0}}-\log{z}\right).

We note that
\phi(z;z_{0})
is the analytic continuation of
\varphi(z;z_{0})
(
4.3
) (setting
r=1
) from the exterior of
\Gamma=\Gamma_{1}
. For
z_{0}\sim 1
we see that it has a single critical point in the neighbourhood of
z=1
; then

Proposition 4.5
.

There exists a jointly analytic function
\zeta(z;z_{0})
which is univalent in a fixed neighbourhood of
z=1
, with
z_{0}
in a neighbourhood of
z_{0}=1
, and an analytic function
A(z_{0})
near
z_{0}=1
such that

\displaystyle\phi(z;z_{0})=\frac{1}{2}\zeta^{2}(z;z_{0})+A(z_{0})\zeta(z;z_{0}),\qquad\zeta(1;z_{0})\equiv 0,\qquad A(1)=0.

(4.11)

Proof.

The function
\phi(z;z_{0})
has a critical point at
z=z_{0}
with critical value
\phi_{\rm cr}=\frac{z_{0}-1}{z_{0}}-\ln z_{0}
. Since we are interested in the values
z_{0}\simeq 1
we observe that
\phi_{\rm cr}=-\frac{1}{2}(z_{0}-1)^{2}(1+\mathcal{O}(z_{0}-1))
. Hence we can write

\displaystyle\phi_{\rm cr}=-\frac{A(z_{0})^{2}}{2}

with
A(z_{0})
an analytic series near
z_{0}=1
. Moreover

\displaystyle\phi(z;z_{0})-\phi_{\rm cr}=\frac{1}{2z_{0}^{2}}(z-z_{0})^{2}(1+\mathcal{O}(z-z_{0})).

Define

\displaystyle\frac{\zeta(z;z_{0})}{\sqrt{2}}:=\sqrt{\phi(z;z_{0})+\frac{A^{2}(z_{0})}{2}}-\frac{A(z_{0})}{\sqrt{2}}.

This is a conformal map in a neighbourhood of
z=z_{0}
of uniform radius of convergence for
z_{0}\simeq 1
; moreover, from
\phi(1;z_{0})=0
follows that
\zeta(1;z_{0})\equiv 0
. The equation (
4.11
) is trivially satisfied.
∎

Remark 4.6
.

A simple computation yields the following first few terms for the expansion (
\delta=z_{0}-1
)

\displaystyle\zeta=\left(1+\frac{\delta}{3}+\frac{\delta^{2}}{4}\right)(z-1)-\frac{(z-1)^{2}}{3}\left(1-\frac{\delta}{4}+\frac{11\delta^{2}}{60}\right)+\mathcal{O}\big((z-1)^{3},\delta^{3}\big),

\displaystyle A(\delta)=-\delta+\frac{2}{3}\delta^{3}+\mathcal{O}\big(\delta^{3}\big).

From this it follows

\displaystyle\sqrt{k}\zeta(z;z_{0})=\left(z-1-\frac{(z-1)^{2}}{3}\right)\sqrt{k}+\mathcal{O}(1),

which manifests the fact that
\sqrt{k}\zeta
homothetically expands the image of the disk
\mathbb{D}
.

Definition 4.7

(double-scaling limit)
.

The double-scaling limit is defined by taking
k\rightarrow\infty
and
A\rightarrow 0
(or equivalently
z_{0}\rightarrow 1
) so that

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty,\,z_{0}\to 1}\sqrt{k}A=\mathcal{S}

with
\mathcal{S}
in compact subsets of the complex plane. Equivalently, it can be defined as

\displaystyle A\sim\frac{\mathcal{S}}{\sqrt{k}}\qquad\hbox{or}\qquad z_{0}\sim{1-\frac{\mathcal{S}}{\sqrt{k}}}.

(4.12)

4.3.4

We will now recall the Riemann–Hilbert Problem

for the function

associated to the Painlevé IV equation. For convenience, we replace the contour

with

Given that

is the characteristic function that is one on the left of the contour

and is zero otherwise. It is now straightforward to check that the Riemann–Hilbert problem for

is given by

.

The matrix
\widehat{\Psi}(\lambda)\textrm{is analytic in }\mathbb{C}\setminus\Sigma_{\widehat{\Psi}}

1.

Jumps on

\displaystyle\widehat{\Psi}_{+}(\lambda)=\widehat{\Psi}_{-}(\lambda)v_{\widehat{\Psi}}(\lambda),\qquad\zeta\in\Sigma_{\widehat{\Psi}},

where

\displaystyle v_{\widehat{\Psi}}(\lambda)=\begin{cases}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
{\rm e}^{-2\theta(\lambda)}&1\end{pmatrix},&z\in\hat{\Gamma}_{i},\\
\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix},&z\in\hat{\Gamma}_{r=1},\\
\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
{\rm e}^{2\theta(\lambda)}&1\end{pmatrix},&z\in\hat{\Gamma}_{e},\\
{\rm e}^{-\gamma\pi{\rm i}\sigma_{3}},&z\in\mathbb{R}^{-}.\end{cases}

(4.14)

2.

Behaviour for
\lambda\to\infty

\displaystyle\widehat{\Psi}(\lambda)=\left(\mathbf{1}+\dfrac{\Psi_{1}}{\lambda}+\dfrac{\Psi_{2}}{\lambda^{2}}+\mathcal{O}\big(\lambda^{-3}\big)\right)\lambda^{\frac{\gamma}{2}\sigma_{3}}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}^{\chi_{L}}.

(4.15)

Comparing the jump matrices for
P(z)
and

, we are now ready to obtain the local parametrix

defined by the Riemann–Hilbert Problem

\displaystyle P(z)={N}(z)\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}^{\chi_{L}}\big(\sqrt{k}\zeta(z)\big)^{-\frac{\gamma}{2}\sigma_{3}}\widehat{\Psi}\big(\sqrt{k}\zeta(z);\sqrt{k}A\big),

(4.16)

where

as defined in (

4.10

in the limit

and

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\sqrt{k}A=\mathcal{S}.

In the analysis above and below, we assume that

belongs to compact sets where the solution of the Painlevé IV equation does not have poles. Using (
4.15
), (
2.4
) and (
2.5
), we obtain in the limit

\displaystyle P(z)={N}(z)\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}^{\chi_{L}}\big(\sqrt{k}\zeta(z)\big)^{-\frac{\gamma}{2}\sigma_{3}}

\displaystyle\hphantom{P (z)=}{}\times\left(\mathbf{1}+\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{k}\zeta}\begin{bmatrix}H&\dfrac{Z}{U}\vskip 2.84526pt\\
U&-H\end{bmatrix}+\mathcal{O}\big(k^{-1}\big)\right)\big(\sqrt{k}\zeta\big)^{\frac{\gamma}{2}\sigma_{3}}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}^{\chi_{L}}

\displaystyle\hphantom{P (z)}{}={N}(z)\!\left(\mathbf{1}+\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}^{\chi_{L}}\!\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{k}\zeta}\!\begin{bmatrix}H&\big(\sqrt{k}\zeta\big)^{-\gamma}\dfrac{Z}{U}\vskip 2.84526pt\\
\big(\sqrt{k}\zeta\big)^{\gamma}U&-H\end{bmatrix}\!\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\

From the above expansion we can see that the subleading terms are not uniformly small with respect to

4.3.5
Improved parametrix

To construct an improved parametrix we define

\displaystyle\widetilde{{N}}(z)=\left(\mathbf{1}+\frac{B\sigma_{-}}{z-1}\right){N}(z),\qquad\sigma_{-}:=\left[\begin{matrix}0&0\\
1&0\end{matrix}\right],\qquad\sigma_{+}:=\left[\begin{matrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{matrix}\right],

(4.18)

where
B
is to be determined. In the same way we define

\displaystyle\widetilde{P}(z)=\widetilde{{N}}(z)\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}^{\chi_{L}}\big(\sqrt{k}\zeta(z)\big)^{-\frac{\gamma}{2}\sigma_{3}}\left(\mathbf{1}-\dfrac{U\sigma_{-}}{\sqrt{k}\zeta(z)}\right)\widehat{\Psi}\big(\sqrt{k}\zeta(z);\sqrt{k}A\big),

(4.19)

where
U
is the
(2,1)
entry of the subleading term of the expansion of
\widehat{\Psi}(\lambda)
for
\lambda\to\infty
. Now we have in the limit
k\to\infty

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\widetilde{P}(z)=\widetilde{{N}}(z)\left(\mathbf{1}+\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}^{\chi_{L}}\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{k}\zeta}\begin{bmatrix}H&(\sqrt{k}\zeta)^{-\gamma}\dfrac{Z}{U}\vskip 2.84526pt\\
0&-H\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{bmatrix}^{\chi_{L}}+\mathcal{O}\big(k^{-1+\gamma/2}\big)\right)\\
&\hphantom{\widetilde{P}(z)}{}=\widetilde{{N}}(z)\big(\mathbf{1}+\mathcal{O}\big(k^{-\frac{1}{2}}\big)\big).\end{split}

The improved parametrics
\widetilde{{N}}(z)
and
\widetilde{P}(z)
have the same jump discontinuities as before, but
\widetilde{P}(z)
might have poles at

. The constant

in (

is bounded at

. This gives the constant

\displaystyle B=Uk^{\frac{\gamma-1}{2}}.

We are now ready to compute the error matrix

4.3.6
Error matrix

The error matrix
E(z)
is defined as

\displaystyle E(z)=\begin{cases}T(z)\widetilde{{N}}(z)^{-1},&z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{D},\\
T(z)\widetilde{P}(z)^{-1},&z\in\mathbb{D},\end{cases}

(4.20)

where the boundary of
\mathbb{D}
is oriented clockwise. The matrix has also jumps on
\widehat{\Gamma}_{i}\cup\widehat{\Gamma}_{e}\setminus\mathbb{D}
which are exponentially close to the identity matrix and can be ignored for the purposes of the analysis. Then, the matrix
E(z)
satisfies the Riemann–Hilbert problem

\displaystyle E_{+}(z)=E_{-}(z)v_{E}(z),\qquad z\in\partial\mathbb{D},\qquad v_{E}(z)=\widetilde{{N}}(z)\widetilde{P}^{-1}(z).

In the double scaling limit
k\to\infty
, after using (
4.14
), (
4.15
), (
2.4
), (
2.5
) and the last equation in (
4.10
), the jump matrix

takes the form

\displaystyle v_{E}(z)=\left(\mathbf{1}+\dfrac{Uk^{\frac{\gamma-1}{2}}}{z-1}\sigma_{-}\right)\left(\dfrac{z-1}{\sqrt{k}z\zeta}\right)^{\frac{\gamma}{2}\sigma_{3}}

\displaystyle\hphantom{v_{E} (z) =}{}\times\left(\mathbf{1}-\dfrac{\begin{pmatrix}H&\dfrac{Z}{U}\vskip 2.84526pt\\
0&-H\end{pmatrix}}{\sqrt{k}\zeta}+\dfrac{\begin{bmatrix}\dfrac{H^{2}+sH-Z}{2}&\dfrac{HZY+HY+ZY^{2}}{UY}\vskip 2.84526pt\\
sU-UY&\dfrac{H^{2}-sH+Z}{2}\end{bmatrix}}{k\zeta^{2}}+\mathcal{O}\big(k^{-\frac{3}{2}}\big)\right)

\displaystyle\hphantom{v_{E} (z) =}{}\times\left(\dfrac{z-1}{\sqrt{k}z\zeta}\right)^{-\frac{\gamma}{2}\sigma_{3}}\left(\mathbf{1}-\dfrac{Uk^{\frac{\gamma-1}{2}}}{z-1}\sigma_{-}\right)=\mathbf{1}+\dfrac{v_{E}^{(1)}}{\sqrt{k}}+\dfrac{v_{E}^{(2)}}{k^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\gamma}{2}}}+\dfrac{v_{E}^{(3)}}{k^{1-\frac{\gamma}{2}}}+\mathcal{O}\big(k^{-1}\big),

where the expansion is for
z\in\partial\mathbb{D}
(while
v_{E}
is exponentially close to the identity on
\Gamma_{E}\setminus\mathbb{D}
), and

\displaystyle v_{E}^{(1)}=-\dfrac{H}{\zeta}\sigma_{3},\qquad v_{E}^{(2)}=-\left(\dfrac{z-1}{z\zeta}\right)^{\gamma}\left(\dfrac{Z}{U\zeta}\right)\sigma_{+},

\displaystyle v_{E}^{(3)}=\left(\left(\dfrac{z-1}{z\zeta}\right)^{-\gamma}\dfrac{(s-Y)U}{\zeta^{2}}-2\dfrac{HU}{(z-1)\zeta}\right)\sigma_{-},

where
\sigma_{+}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
. By the standard theory of small norm Riemann–Hilbert problems (see for example
[
17
, Chapter 7]
), one has a similar expansion for

, namely

\displaystyle E(z)=\mathbf{1}+\dfrac{E^{(1)}}{\sqrt{k}}+\dfrac{E^{(2)}}{k^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\gamma}{2}}}+\dfrac{E^{(3)}}{k^{1-\frac{\gamma}{2}}}+\mathcal{O}\big(k^{-1}\big),

so that

\displaystyle E^{(j)}_{+}(z)=E^{(j)}_{-}(z)+v_{E}^{(j)}(z),\qquad z\in\partial\mathbb{D},\qquad j=1,2,3.

By solving the corresponding Riemann–Hilbert problem, we obtain, using the Plemelj–Sokhtski formula

\displaystyle E^{(j)}(z)=\dfrac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}\dfrac{v_{E}^{(i)}(\xi)}{\xi-z}\mathrm{d}\xi,\qquad j=1,2,3,

which gives

\displaystyle E^{(1)}(z)=-\dfrac{\mbox{Res}_{\xi=1}v_{E}^{(1)}(\xi)}{z-1}=\frac{H\sigma_{3}}{z-1},\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{D},

\displaystyle E^{(2)}(z)=-\dfrac{\mbox{Res}_{\xi=1}v_{E}^{(2)}(\xi)}{z-1}=\frac{(Z/U)\sigma_{+}}{z-1},\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{D},

\displaystyle E^{(3)}(z)=-\dfrac{\mbox{Res}_{\xi=1}v_{E}^{(3)}(\xi)}{(z-1)}-\dfrac{\mbox{Res}_{\xi=1}(\xi-1)v_{E}^{(2)}(\xi)}{(z-1)^{2}}

\displaystyle\hphantom{E^{(3)} (z)}{}=\left(\dfrac{2}{3}\dfrac{\gamma(2H+\gamma-s)\gamma+H+\gamma-s}{z-1}+\dfrac{U(2H+Y-s)}{z-1}\right)\sigma_{-},\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{D},

(4.21)

and

\displaystyle E^{(1)}(z)=v_{E}^{(1)}-\dfrac{\mbox{Res}_{\xi=1}v_{E}^{(1)}(\xi)}{z-1}=v_{E}^{(1)}+\frac{H\sigma_{3}}{z-1},\qquad z\in\mathbb{D},

\displaystyle E^{(2)}(z)=v_{E}^{(2)}-\dfrac{\mbox{Res}_{\xi=1}v_{E}^{(2)}(\xi)}{z-1}=v_{E}^{(2)}+\frac{(Z/U)\sigma_{+}}{z-1},\qquad z\in\mathbb{D},

Proof.

Observing the asymptotic expansion (

in

From the relations (
