Higher-order boundary layers and regularity for Stokes systems over rough boundaries
Mitsuo Higaki, Jinping Zhuge

Abstract




Abstract.
In this paper, we study the large-scale boundary regularity for the Stokes system in periodically oscillating John domains. Our main contribution is the construction of boundary layer correctors of arbitrary order. This is a significant generalization of the known results restricted to the first and second orders. As an application, we prove the large-scale regularity estimate, as well as a Liouville theorem, of arbitrary order for the Stokes system. Our results are also related to higher-order boundary layer tails and wall laws in viscous fluids over rough boundaries.

Keywords.
Boundary layer, Large-scale regularity, Stokes system, Rough boundary, Wall laws.

76M50, 76D10, 76D07, 35B27.

Introduction

1.1
Motivations

The effect of rough boundaries is an important and challenging topic in the field of hydrodynamics. It has been extensively studied experimentally, numerically and theoretically with a vast literature during the past half century. A remarkable progress has been made in deriving the wall law for viscous fluids, which is a method to replace rough boundaries artificially by flat ones and to impose the effective conditions on them. The reader is referred to the preceding works
[
24
,
25
]
and references therein for more detailed backgrounds, including approximation of flows via the wall laws and the investigation of turbulence.

In this paper, we focus on the local behavior of the stationary Stokes flows over periodically oscillating boundaries. There are two critical parameters for the geometry
of a rough boundary — thickness and structure. In applications, the thickness of rough boundaries is relatively small, compared to the characteristic scales of fluid flows. The periodicity is a mathematical simplification of realistic boundaries having self-similar structures, such as rusted or sanded metal plates, natural riverbeds, fish skins, etc. In our situation, since the Stokes system is linear, the thickness and period of rough boundaries can be enlarged and rescaled respectively to 1 and
2\pi
by choosing the period suitably (e.g., a multiple of the smallest period). Precisely, we assume that the domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
as well as its boundary
\Gamma:=\partial\Omega
satisfies the following two basic assumptions:

•

Thickness:

\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}:=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}~|~y>0\}\subset\Omega\subset\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}~|~y>-1\};

(1)

•

Periodicity:

\Omega+(2\pi z,0)=\Omega\quad\text{for any }z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d-1},

(2)

where
\Omega+(\tau,0):=\{(x+\tau,y)~|~(x,y)\in\Omega\}
for fixed
\tau\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
.

Throughout this paper, the point in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
will always be written as
(x,y)
with
x=(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,

x_{d-1})\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
and
y\in\mathbb{R}
.
Calligraphy letters
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y},\cdots
will also be used only when it makes the description more concise.
The condition (
1
) says that the boundary thickness of
\Gamma
is at most 1. The condition (
2
) says that the domain
\Omega
is
(2\pi)
-periodic in all
(d-1)
-horizontal directions. Another essential technical geometric assumption for
\Omega
will be given later.

Let
R>1
and let
Q_{R}(0)\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote the cube centered at the origin with side length
2R
, i.e.,
Q_{R}(0)=(-R,R)^{d}
. Set
B_{R,+}=Q_{R}(0)\cap\Omega
and
\Gamma_{R}=Q_{R}(0)\cap\Gamma
. Then we consider the following Stokes system subjected to the no-slip boundary condition:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta u+\nabla p=0&\mbox{in}\ B_{R,+}\\
\nabla\cdot u=0&\mbox{in}\ B_{R,+}\\
u=0&\mbox{on}\ \Gamma_{R},\end{array}\right.

(S)

where
u=(u_{1},\cdots,u_{d})
denotes the velocity field and
p
denotes the pressure field. Our interest is the mesoscopic structures or large-scale behaviors of an arbitrary weak solution
(u,p)
in
B_{r,+}
for
1<r<R
, typically when
R
is large. If the boundary is flat, i.e., if
\Omega
and
\Gamma
are respectively
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}
and
\partial\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}:=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}~|~y=0\}
, the solution
(u,p)
of (
S
) is smooth up to the boundary
\Gamma_{R/2}
. Particularly, we have the Taylor expansion for
u

u(x,y)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\alpha|+l\leq m\\
l\geq 1\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\alpha!l!}\partial_{x}^{\alpha}\partial_{y}^{l}u(0,0)x^{\alpha}y^{l}+O(|(x,y)|^{m+1}).

(3)

In the above summation,
l
is not equal to zero by the boundary condition. Moreover, it can be verified that the polynomial on the right-hand side of (
3
) solves the Stokes system in
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
. In other words, if
\Gamma
is flat, for any
m\geq 1
, the weak solution
u
is close to a polynomial solution of degree
m
in
B_{r,+}
with a higher-order remainder. A similar consideration can also be made for the pressure
p
. These properties demonstrate the local structures and
C^{m,1}
regularity of the solutions to (
S
) when the boundaries are flat. Now, it is natural to ask if there is an analog of (
3
) if
\Omega
has a periodically oscillating boundary.

We point out that similar questions have been well-understood in elliptic homogenization with oscillating coefficients when there is no boundary. Roughly it says that the local solutions can be approximated by heterogeneous polynomial solutions involving higher-order correctors; see e.g.,
[
9
,
4
,
7
]
or
[
5
]
. Turning to our problem, however, the oscillation is imposed on the boundary instead of coefficients, which leads to a completely different situation at least at a technical level. Recently, the local large-scale regularity (lower-order) to PDEs with oscillating boundaries has been investigated in several works. The uniform Lipschitz estimate was first obtained in
[
29
,
30
]
for elliptic equations by the compactness method in the case when the boundary is a Lipschitz graph; see also
[
21
]
for related work. The Lipschitz graph assumption then was removed in
[
37
]
so that the boundaries can be arbitrary at small scales. Similar results, up to
C^{2,\mu}
estimates, have been extended to stationary Navier-Stokes system in
[
24
,
25
]
, which will be discussed below.

The key to deriving the higher-order analog of (
S
) is to introduce the so-called boundary layers, which play similar roles as the correctors in elliptic homogenization with oscillating coefficients; see e.g.,
[
9
,
4
,
31
,
22
,
11
,
7
,
6
]
. The first-order boundary layers are classical, given by the bounded solutions of the following cell problem: for
i\in\{1,2,\cdots,d\}
,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta v_{(i)}+\nabla q_{(i)}=0&\mbox{in}\ \Omega\\
\nabla\cdot v_{(i)}=0&\mbox{in}\ \Omega\\
v_{(i)}(x,y)+y\mathbf{e}_{i}=0&\mbox{on}\ \Gamma.\end{array}\right.

(4)

Historically, the first-order boundary layer was studied to derive the Navier wall law rigorously, which had been known to be effective empirically. The mathematical analysis was pioneered by
[
1
,
2
,
26
,
27
]
. The extensions have been given to the case of more general boundaries
[
10
,
33
,
17
,
18
,
14
,
13
]
and to the nonstationary case
[
34
,
23
]
. On the other hand, the connection to the regularity theory for the stationary Navier-Stokes system was investigated by
[
24
]
. Indeed, the local large-scale
C^{1,\mu}
regularity has been obtained in
[
24
]
and
[
25
]
, namely that for a Navier-Stokes flow
u
in
B_{r,+}
, there exists a constant
c_{i}
such that

\Big\|\nabla u-\nabla\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}c_{i}(y\mathbf{e}_{i}+v_{(i)})\Big\|_{\underline{L}^{2}(B_{r,+})}\leq Cr^{\mu},\quad r\in(1,R).

(5)

The above estimate is called a large-scale
C^{1,\mu}
estimate since the radius is restricted to
r>1
. It in general does not hold for
r\leq 1
if the boundary has no structure such as smoothness at scales less than
1
. This is a natural phenomenon in homogenization theory.

More recently in
[
25
]
,
C^{2,\mu}
regularity was firstly proved for the stationary Navier-Stokes system with the help of the second-order boundary layers. The proof relies on the periodicity assumption on the boundary. We emphasize that, in contrast, the first-order boundary layers can be constructed without periodicity. A typical second-order boundary layer is a weak solution to the Stokes system in
\Omega
subjected to the boundary condition

v(x,y)+x_{j}y\mathbf{e}_{i}=0\quad\text{on }\Gamma,

where
j\in\{1,2,\cdots,d-1\}
. The difficulty in solving this cell problem is that the boundary value has linear growth when
x_{j}\to\infty
. As a result, unlike the first-order case, one cannot rely on the Dirichlet-Neumman map
[
18
,
16
,
15
,
30
,
24
]
or the Green function
[
25
]
, which are useful for the solutions with finite Dirichlet energy. The key observation in
[
25
]
is that

v(x,y)-x_{j}v_{(i)}(x,y)=0\quad\text{on }\Gamma

holds, where
v_{(i)}
is the first-order boundary layer given by (
4
). Hence if we introduce a new unknown function
V:=v-x_{j}v_{(i)}
, then it satisfies the no-slip condition on
\Gamma
and a Stokes system with periodic “force terms” involving
v_{(i)}
. This transformation allows one to find a solution
V
on a periodic cell, which is an infinite cylinder in our setting. When solving the problem, we need to take advantage of a good quantitative convergence of
v_{(i)}
when
y\to\infty
, which essentially requires a periodic structure of the boundary.

As indicated, the boundary layers are correctors that adjust to the oscillating boundaries and are crucial to understanding the local behavior of the solutions to (
S
). In this paper, we will construct the boundary layers of arbitrary order by recognizing their algebraic structures and recurrence relations. As far as the authors know, this is a new result even in smooth domains. The boundary layers will be used to derive a higher-order version of (
5
) analogous to the classical estimate (
3
), which directly leads to the Liouville theorem for the homogeneous Stokes system on
\Omega
. We also introduce a notion of the effective Stoke polynomials and provide the higher-order wall laws in a local sense. This new class of boundary conditions contains the Navier wall law in
[
26
,
27
,
17
,
18
]
as the first-order case.

1.2
Assumptions and statements of results

In the analysis of (
S
), other than the assumptions (
1
) and (
2
), we still need to assume a technical geometric condition on
\Omega
as in
[

. This is recalled in Definition

Definition 1.1

(
[
28
,
32
]
)

Let
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an open bounded set and
\tilde{\mathcal{X}}\in D
. We say that

is a (bounded)

with respect to
\tilde{\mathcal{X}}
and with constant

if for any
\mathcal{Y}\in D
, there exists a Lipschitz mapping
\rho:[0,|\mathcal{Y}-\tilde{\mathcal{X}}|]\to D
with Lipschitz constant
L\in(0,\infty)
, such that
\rho(0)=\mathcal{Y},\rho(|\mathcal{Y}-\tilde{\mathcal{X}}|)=\tilde{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\rm dist}\,(\rho(t),\partial D)\geq t/L
for all

.

The boundary of a John domains allows for fractals and inward cusps. Lipschitz domains, NTA domains, domains with inward cusps or certain fractals such as Koch’s snowflake are John domains. Domains with outward cusps are not.

Definition 1.2

(
[
25
]
)

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an open set satisfying (
1
).

(i)

We say that
\Omega
is a

with constants
(L,K)
if for any
\mathcal{X}\in\{(x,0)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
and any

\Omega_{R}(\mathcal{X})
with respect to
\mathcal{X}_{R}=\mathcal{X}+R\mathbf{e}_{d}
and with constant

according to Definition
1.1
such that

B_{R,+}(\mathcal{X})\subset\Omega_{R}(\mathcal{X})\subset B_{2R,+}(\mathcal{X}).

(6)

(ii)

We say that
\Omega
is a

with constants
(L,K)
if
\Omega
is a bumpy John domain with constants
(L,K)
and satisfies the condition (
2
).

It is easily seen that the constants of bumpy John domains are rescaling- and translation-invariant. We will say that a constant

depends on
\Omega
if it depends on the constant

in Definition

as well as the dimension

. A typical example of the periodic bumpy John domains is one with a porous boundary (see Figure

The John domain assumption can be thought of as the minimum boundary requirement when dealing with the stationary Stokes system. Indeed, it is proved in
[
3
]
that, under suitable conditions, the (bounded) domain in which a right-inverses of the divergence operator exists must be a John domain. The right-inverse of the divergence, also known as the Bogovskii operator, plays an important role for estimating the pressure. Let us mention that Definition
1.2
was first introduced in
[
25
]
, where the first-order boundary layers were constructed over non-periodic boundaries. The proof uses the Green function and its properties following from the a priori large-scale Lipschitz regularity for (
S
). These enable one to establish a local energy estimate of the pressure in bumpy John domains. As is discussed in
[
25
]
, it is unclear whether the method in
[

,
16
,
15
,
30
,
24
]
applies in this case. A major source of difficulty is that the boundary is not necessarily represented by a graph.

Our main result of this paper is the existence and the corresponding estimates of the boundary layers of arbitrary order. Let
\mathcal{P}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be the set of all real-coefficient polynomials of at most degree
m
and set

\mathcal{P}_{m,0}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+})=\{P\in\mathcal{P}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})~|~P(x,0)=0\}\subset\mathcal{P}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

Theorem 1.3

Let
\Omega
be a periodic bumpy John domain. For all
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, there exists a linear map
\mathscr{S}[\cdot]:\mathcal{P}_{m,0}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+})^{d}\rightarrow H^{1}_{\rm loc}(\overline{\Omega})^{d}\times L^{2}_{\rm loc}(\overline{\Omega})
which maps
P\in\mathcal{P}_{m,0}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+})^{d}
to the pair
\mathscr{S}[P]=(v,q)
which is a weak solution to

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta v+\nabla q=0&\mbox{in}\ \Omega\\
\nabla\cdot v=0&\mbox{in}\ \Omega\\
v+P=0&\mbox{on}\ \Gamma.\end{array}\right.

(7)

Moreover, the growth rate of
v
at spatial infinity is strictly less than that of
P
.

The above theorem is a straightforward corollary of Proposition
3.1
which also contains several basic estimates (details are omitted here for simplicity). Theorem
1.3
recovers the classical first-order boundary layers if
m=1
and the second-order boundary layers in
[
25
]
if
m=2
. The structure of
\mathscr{S}[P]
, nontrivially mixing polynomials and periodic functions, can be seen in Subsection
1.3
; see also Proposition
3.6
for an intrinsic formula for
\mathscr{S}[P]
.

As an important application, using the boundary layer correctors in Theorem
1.3
, one can show the higher-order regularity for the weak solutions of (
S
). To state the result, we introduce some function spaces in a convenient manner. The precise definitions and their properties are given in Section
2
and Subsection
3.3
. Let
\mathcal{S}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+})
be the space of Stokes polynomials
(P,Q)
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}
, that is the set of polynomial solutions of degree at most
m
to the Stokes equations imposed on
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
. Moreover, let
\mathcal{S}_{m}(\Omega)
be the space of heterogeneous Stoke polynomials in
\Omega
:

\mathcal{S}_{m}(\Omega)=\{(w,\pi)=(P,Q)+\mathscr{S}[P]~|~(P,Q)\in\mathcal{S}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+})\}.

(8)

Notice that the boundary layer
\mathscr{S}[P]
corrects the trace of the polynomial
P
on
\Gamma
. Hence
(w,\pi)
is a solution of the Stokes system in
\Omega
having the same growth rate as
(P,Q)
.

Now we can state the optimal higher-order regularity for (
S
) at large scales.

Theorem 1.4

Let
\Omega
be a periodic bumpy John domain. For all
R>1
and
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, if
(u,p)\in H^{1}(B_{R,+})^{d}\times L^{2}(B_{R,+})
is a weak solution of (
S
), then there exist
(w,\pi)\in\mathcal{S}_{m}(\Omega)
and a constant
c_{p}
such that, for all
r\in(1,R/2)
,

\displaystyle\|\nabla u-\nabla w\|_{\underline{L}^{2}(B_{r,+})}+\|p-\pi-c_{p}\|_{\underline{L}^{2}(B_{r,+})}\leq C\Big(\frac{r}{R}\Big)^{m}\|\nabla u\|_{\underline{L}^{2}(B_{R,+})}.

(9)

The constant
C
is independent of
R
and
r
, while depends on
\Omega
and
m
.

Remark 1.5

One can obtain the estimate of
u
itself if the Caccioppoli inequality holds for (
S
) in
B_{R,+}
. If it does, from (
9
) combined with the Poincaré inequality, we find

\displaystyle\|u-w\|_{\underline{L}^{2}(B_{r,+})}\leq C\Big(\frac{r}{R}\Big)^{m+1}\|u\|_{\underline{L}^{2}(B_{R,+})},\quad r\in(1,R/4).

However, the validity of the usual Caccioppoli inequality in bumpy John domains seems to be a delicate question of independent interest. We refer to the Appendix A of
[
25
]
.

Remark 1.6

With the estimates of boundary layers in Proposition
3.1
, the proof for Theorem
1.4
is more or less standard via a quantitative Campanato iteration originating from
[

]
(also see
[

,

]
or recent monographs
[

,

]
). The application of this method to Stokes systems or linear elasticity involving the pressure can be found in
[

,
25
]
.

Theorem
1.4
is essentially a large-scale version of (
3
) with the Stokes polynomial replaced by the heterogeneous Stokes polynomial from
\mathcal{S}_{m}(\Omega)
. This result can be compared to the large-scale
C^{m,1}
regularity in elliptic homogenization with oscillating coefficients (e.g.,
[
4
,
7
]
).
Theorem
1.4
also implies the Liouville theorem of arbitrary order for the homogeneous Stokes system imposed on the whole domain
\Omega
. This basically says that
\mathcal{S}_{m}(\Omega)
contains all the solutions in
\Omega
whose velocity gradients grow at most like
o(|(x,y)|^{m})
and has the same dimension as
\mathcal{S}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+})
; see Subsections
3.3
and
4.4
for details.

Note that Theorem
1.4
captures the structure of the solutions near the boundary. Then it is natural to ask what happens to a local solution away from the boundary. In fact, the singularity only appears near the boundary and homogenization should be expected to take place far away from the boundary. Actually, it is well-known that the first-order boundary layers converge exponentially to constant values as
y\to\infty
. These limits at
y=\infty
are called the boundary layer tails. This motivates us to define the boundary layer tails for higher-order boundary layers, and therefore to define the effective Stokes polynomials in this paper. Indeed, we can see from Proposition
3.1
that the boundary layer
\mathscr{S}[P]
given in Theorem
1.3
converges exponentially to a Stokes polynomial as
y\to\infty
. Consequently, the element in
\mathcal{S}_{m}(\Omega)
, which is a special solution, also converges exponentially to a Stokes polynomial. Thus, we may define the space of effective Stokes polynomials as

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{m}^{\rm eff}(\Omega)=

\displaystyle\big\{(w_{\rm poly},\pi_{\rm poly})\in\mathcal{P}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{m-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})~|~

\displaystyle\lim_{y\to+\infty}((w,\pi)-(w_{\rm poly},\pi_{\rm poly}))=0,\ \ (w,\pi)\in\mathcal{S}_{m}(\Omega)\big\}.

The following theorem shows that the behavior of the local solutions, slightly away from the boundary, may be pointwise captured by the effective Stokes polynomials in
\mathcal{S}_{m}^{\rm eff}(\Omega)
, instead of the heterogeneous Stokes polynomials in
\mathcal{S}_{m}(\Omega)
.

Theorem 1.7

Under the same assumptions as in Theorem
1.4
, if
R
is sufficiently large depending on
m
, then there exist
(w_{\rm poly},\pi_{\rm poly})\in\mathcal{S}_{m}^{\rm eff}(\Omega)
and a constant
c_{p}
such that for all
(x,y)\in B_{R/2,+}
with
y\geq 4
,

\displaystyle|\nabla u(x,y)-\nabla w_{\rm poly}(x,y)|+|p(x,y)-\pi_{\rm poly}(x,y)-c_{p}|

(10)

\displaystyle\leq C\Big\{\Big(\frac{r}{R}\Big)^{m}+(1+|x|)^{m-1}e^{-y/2}\Big\}\|\nabla u\|_{\underline{L}^{2}(B_{R,+})},

and

|u(x,y)-w_{\rm poly}(x,y)|\leq C\Big\{y\Big(\frac{r}{R}\Big)^{m}+(1+|x|)^{m-1}e^{-y/2}\Big\}\|\nabla u\|_{\underline{L}^{2}(B_{R,+})},

(11)

where
r=|(x,y)|
and
C
depends only on
m
and
\Omega
.

The estimate (
10
) shows that if
y\geq 2m\ln(R)
, then the solution of (
S
) can be approximated equally well by the effective Stokes polynomials as the approximation in (
9
) by the heterogeneous Stokes polynomials. Moreover, from the proof, which is a combination of Theorem
1.4
and Proposition

, one can see that the effective Stokes polynomial
(w_{\rm poly},\pi_{\rm poly})
in Theorem

in Theorem

.

do not satisfy the no-slip condition either on

. However, in view of Theorem

. We would like to understand it as the wall law for

in a local setting. This interpretation is indeed compatible with the literature, in the sense that the condition satisfied by the elements from the first-order space

is an intrinsic constant (

,

,

,

,

,

. In Subsection

.

1.3
Key ideas of the proof of Theorem
1.3

Let us explain the key ideas of the proof of Theorem
1.3
, which is the core of this paper. The goal is to build a weak solution
(v^{\alpha},q^{\alpha})=(v^{\alpha,l}_{(i)},q^{\alpha,l}_{(i)})
to the Stokes system

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta v^{\alpha}+\nabla q^{\alpha}=0&\text{in }\Omega\\
\nabla\cdot v^{\alpha}=0&\text{in }\Omega\\
v^{\alpha}(x,y)+x^{\alpha}y^{l}{\bf e}_{i}=0&\text{on }\Gamma\end{array}\right.

(BL
α
)

for a given triplet
(\alpha,l,i)\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{Z}_{+}\times\{1,\cdots,d\}
. Theorem
1.3
then follows by linearity of the equations. Notice that (
BLα
) coincides with (
4
) if
(\alpha,l)=(0,1)
and is a system for the second-order boundary layers if
|\alpha|+l=2
. Again, the difficulty in solving the problem (
BLα
) is that the boundary value has growth when
|x|\to\infty
. The method in
[
25
]
is no longer applicable since it is restricted to cases when
|\alpha|+l=2
.

The new idea is to introduce an ansatz

\displaystyle v^{\alpha}(x,y)=\sum_{\beta\leq\alpha}\dbinom{\alpha}{\beta}x^{\alpha-\beta}V^{\beta}(x,y),\qquad q^{\alpha}(x,y)=\sum_{\beta\leq\alpha}\dbinom{\alpha}{\beta}x^{\alpha-\beta}Q^{\beta}(x,y),

(12)

where the family of new unknown functions
\{(V^{\beta},Q^{\beta})\}
is assumed to be periodic in
x
. This ansatz is adopted in view of the Taylor expansion of
(v^{\alpha},q^{\alpha})
at the origin but with variable coefficients. It systematically generalizes the heuristic method in
[
25
]
. In order to recover the boundary condition on
v^{\alpha}
, one needs compatibility conditions on
\Gamma
for
\{(V^{\beta},Q^{\beta})\}
. Here we assume that
(V^{0},Q^{0})
is the solution bounded at infinity of (
BLα
) with
\alpha=0
and that
V^{\beta}=0
on
\Gamma
for all
\beta\neq 0
. The unique existence of
(V^{0},Q^{0})
can be proved easily thanks to the small vertical extent of
\Omega\setminus\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}
; see Lemma
3.4
.

Inserting (
12
) to (
BLα
), we see that
(V^{\beta},Q^{\beta})
must solve the recursive system

\displaystyle-\Delta V^{\beta}+\nabla Q^{\beta}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}\Big(2\dbinom{\beta_{i}}{2}V^{\beta-2{\bf e}_{i}}+2\dbinom{\beta_{i}}{1}\partial_{i}V^{\beta-{\bf e}_{i}}-\dbinom{\beta_{i}}{1}Q^{\beta-{\bf e}_{i}}{\bf e}_{i}\Big),

(13)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot V^{\beta}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}\dbinom{\beta_{i}}{1}V^{\beta-{\bf e}_{i}}_{i}.

Here all the notations with multi-indices belonging to
\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{d}
are understood to be zero. Despite its linearity, the analysis of the system (
13
) is not straightforward. In fact, the solutions of (
13
) can grow at spatial infinity, which prevents us to apply the Lax-Milgram lemma. To illustrate it, let us put
\beta={\bf e}_{1}
in (
13
). Then the second line reads

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot V^{{\bf e}_{1}}

\displaystyle=-V^{0}_{1}.

It can be shown that the right-hand side converges to a negative constant if
(l,i)=(1,1)
and
\Omega\neq\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}
, namely if
V^{0}
is a nontrivial first-order boundary layer. We refer to
[
24
, Proposition 3]
for the proof when
d=3
. Consequently, the solutions of (
13
) periodic in
x
may grow when
y\to\infty
, otherwise the equation can be inconsistent. In addition, we need to consider the solutions with polynomial growth in
y
as the recurrence proceeds.

The insight for this obstacle is to split
(V^{\beta},Q^{\beta})
into

V^{\beta}(x,y)=V^{\beta}_{\rm poly}(y)+V^{\beta}_{\rm per}(x,y),\qquad Q^{\beta}(x,y)=Q^{\beta}_{\rm poly}(y)+Q^{\beta}_{\rm per}(x,y),

and study the pairs
(V^{\beta}_{\rm poly},Q^{\beta}_{\rm poly})
and
(V^{\beta}_{\rm per},Q^{\beta}_{\rm per})
in the two different manners:

•

V^{\beta}_{\rm poly}
and
Q^{\beta}_{\rm poly}
are polynomials in
y
of degree at most
|\beta|
. Roughly speaking, both are obtained by the integration of
V^{\beta-{\bf e}_{i}}_{\rm poly},V^{\beta-2{\bf e}_{i}}_{\rm poly}
and
Q^{\beta-{\bf e}_{i}}_{\rm poly}
. However, a simple computation does not work due to the vectorial structure of the problem (
13
).

•

V^{\beta}_{\rm per}
and
Q^{\beta}_{\rm per}
are periodic functions in
x
decaying exponentially when
y\to\infty
. Although they are represented explicitly on flat domains
\{y=L\}
for
L>1
, we need to be careful in deriving the quantitative bounds when
|\beta|
is large.

The procedure outlined above is carried out in Section
3
. We rely on induction on the length
|\beta|
of
\beta\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{d-1}
in the proof, but the details are complex in nature, as at each level we need to estimate the decomposed
(V^{\beta},Q^{\beta})
. Eventually, the solutions of (
BLα
) are constructed based on the ansatz (
12
), as stated in Proposition
3.1
.

1.4
Notations and definitions

The following is a summary of the notations and definitions used in this paper.

Domains.
For
R>1
and
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we define
Q_{R}=Q_{R}(0)=(-R,R)^{d}
and

Q_{R}(x,y)=Q_{R}(0)+(x,y),\qquad B_{R,+}(x,0)=Q_{R}(x,0)\cap\Omega.

For simplicity, we write
B_{R,+}=B_{R,+}(0,0)
.

Weak solutions.

A pair
(u,p)\in H^{1}(B_{R,+})^{d}\times L^{2}(B_{R,+})
is said to be a weak solution of (
S
) if it satisfies: (i)
\nabla\cdot u=0
in the sense of distributions, (ii)
\psi u\in H^{1}_{0}(Q_{R}(0))^{d}
for any cut-off function
\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(Q_{R}(0))
, and (iii) the weak formulation

\displaystyle\int_{B_{R,+}}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\varphi-\int_{B_{R,+}}p(\nabla\cdot\varphi)=0\quad\text{for any }\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(B_{R,+})^{d}.

We emphasize that
u
has natural zero-extension
\tilde{u}
to
Q_{R}
so that
\tilde{u}\in H^{1}(Q_{R})^{d}
. We do not distinguish between
u
and
\tilde{u}
. This fact will be used without mentioning in Section
4
.

Moreover, a pair
(v,q)\in H^{1}_{{\rm loc}}(\overline{\Omega})^{d}\times L^{2}_{{\rm loc}}(\overline{\Omega})
is said to be a weak solution of (
7
) if it satisfies: (i)
\nabla\cdot v=0
in the sense of distributions, (ii)
\chi(v+P)\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)^{d}
for any
\chi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and (iii) the weak formulation:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla v\cdot\nabla\phi-\int_{\Omega}q(\nabla\cdot\phi)=0,\quad\text{for any }\phi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega)^{d}.

In addition, the weak solution of the system (
13
) is defined in the similar manner as above.

Derivatives.
The derivative with respect
x_{i}
and
y
will be denoted respectively by
\partial_{i}=\partial/\partial x_{i}
and
\partial_{y}=\partial/\partial y
. Moreover, we use the following derivative operators:

\displaystyle\nabla^{\prime}=(\partial_{1},\cdots,\partial_{d-1}),

\displaystyle\nabla=(\nabla^{\prime},\partial_{y}),

\displaystyle\Delta^{\prime}=\nabla^{\prime}\cdot\nabla^{\prime},

\displaystyle\Delta=\nabla\cdot\nabla.

Multi-index.
An element of
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{d-1}
is called a multi-index. For
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{d-1})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{d-1}
, we define the length of
\alpha
by
|\alpha|=\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{d-1}
. We also define
\alpha!=\alpha_{1}!\cdots\alpha_{d-1}!
.
For
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{d-1}
, we say that
\beta\leq\alpha
if
\alpha_{i}\leq\beta_{i}
holds for all
i=1,\cdots d-1
. Moreover, using
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{d-1}
, we define the monomial and the higher-order derivative respectively by

x^{\alpha}=x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots x_{d-1}^{\alpha_{d-1}},\qquad\partial_{x}^{\alpha}=\partial_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\partial_{d-1}^{\alpha_{d-1}}.

Binomial and multinomial coefficients.
For
0\leq k\leq n
, the binomial coefficients is

\binom{n}{k}=\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}.

For
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{d-1}
with
\beta\leq\alpha
, the (generalized) binomial coefficient is defined by

\binom{\alpha}{\beta}=\frac{\alpha!}{\beta!(\alpha-\beta)!}=\binom{\alpha_{1}}{\beta_{1}}\cdots\binom{\alpha_{d-1}}{\beta_{d-1}}.

Moreover, for
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{d-1}
with
|\alpha|=m
, the multinomial binomial coefficient is defined by

\binom{m}{\alpha}=\frac{m!}{\alpha!}.

Vector and tensor calculus.
The standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is denoted by
\{{\bf e}_{1},\cdots,{\bf e}_{d}\}
.
For
\mathcal{X}=(\mathcal{X}_{1},\cdots,\mathcal{X}_{d}),\mathcal{Y}=(\mathcal{Y}_{1},\cdots,\mathcal{Y}_{d})\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
, we set
\mathcal{X}\cdot\mathcal{Y}=\mathcal{X}_{1}\mathcal{Y}_{1}+\cdots+\mathcal{X}_{d}\mathcal{Y}_{d}
. This coincides with the standard inner product if
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Moreover, depending on the context, we will write
(x,y)=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{d-1},y)\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
as the column vector
\begin{bmatrix}x\\
y\\
\end{bmatrix}
, which is distinguished from the binomial coefficient above.
For
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and
\mathcal{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the symmetric tensor
\mathcal{X}^{\otimes m}=\{(\mathcal{X}^{\otimes m})_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha}
is defined by

(\mathcal{X}^{\otimes m})_{\alpha}=\mathcal{X}^{\alpha},\quad\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},\quad|\alpha|=m.

For symmetric tensors
S=\{S_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha},T=\{T_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha}
of order
m
, we define the contraction by

S:T=\sum_{|\alpha|=m}\dbinom{m}{\alpha}S_{\alpha}T_{\alpha},\quad\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}.

These notations are useful in briefly describing Taylor expansions; see Subsection
4.1
.

Average integral.
For a bounded open set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a measurable function
f
on
E
, we set

\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{E}f=\frac{1}{|E|}\int_{E}f,\qquad\|f\|_{\underline{L}^{2}(E)}=\bigg(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{E}|f|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}.

2

Stokes polynomials

In this section, we consider the Stokes equations imposed on the half-space:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta u+\nabla p=0&\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}\\
\nabla\cdot u=0&\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}\\
u=0&\text{on }\partial\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}.\end{array}\right.

(SH)

Our aim is to study the structure of the vector space
\mathcal{S}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+})
of all the polynomial solutions of (
SH
) up to degree
m
. We are aware that the main theorems can be proved independently of this section. However, it could serve as a useful reference when the results of this paper are applied in more practical settings.

Let
\dot{\mathcal{P}}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be the space of homogeneous polynomials of degree
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. Define the space of homogeneous Stokes polynomials by

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{S}}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+})=\{(u,p)\in\dot{\mathcal{P}}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}\times\dot{\mathcal{P}}_{m-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})~|~(u,p)\ \,\text{is a solution of}\ \,\eqref{eq.stokes.halfspace}\}.

When
m=1
, it is easily seen that

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{S}}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+})

\displaystyle=\Big\{(\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}A_{i}y{\bf e}_{i},L)~|~A_{i},L\in\mathbb{R}\Big\}

\displaystyle=\spn\{(y{\bf e}_{1},0),\cdots,(y{\bf e}_{d-1},0),(0,1)\}.

Thus,

\dim\dot{\mathcal{S}}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+})=d.

(14)

To investigate the structure of
\dot{\mathcal{S}}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+})
when
m\geq 2
, we focus on the pressure term. The solenoidal condition implies that
p=p(x,y)
solves the following Neumann problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Delta p=0&\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}\\
\partial_{y}p=\Delta u_{d}&\text{on }\partial\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}.\end{array}\right.

We recall that
u_{d}
denotes the
d
-th component of
u=(u_{1},\cdots,u_{d})
. Introducing

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{A}}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

\displaystyle:=\{p\in\dot{\mathcal{P}}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})~|~\Delta p=0\},

we see that the definition of
\dot{\mathcal{S}}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+})
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{S}}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+})

\displaystyle=\{(u,p)\in\dot{\mathcal{P}}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}\times\dot{\mathcal{A}}_{m-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})~|~(u,p)\ \,\text{is a solution of}\ \,\eqref{eq.stokes.halfspace}\}.

Thus we begin by considering harmonic polynomials in half-spaces. The following lemma provides a relation between an element of
\dot{\mathcal{A}}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and its trace on
\partial\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}
. We omit the proof as it is a simple computation.

Lemma 2.1

Let
m\geq 1
and
q\in\dot{\mathcal{A}}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then there exists
q_{1}(x)\in\dot{\mathcal{P}}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d-1})
and
q_{2}(x)\in\dot{\mathcal{P}}_{m-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d-1})
such that

q(x,y)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{(2j)!}((\Delta^{\prime})^{j}q_{1}(x))y^{2j}+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{(2j+1)!}((\Delta^{\prime})^{j}q_{2}(x))y^{2j+1}.

In particular, if
q(x,0)=0
, then
q_{1}=0
.

Next let us define an operator
\Delta_{\rm D}^{-1}
on the set
\{x^{\alpha}y^{l}~|~|\alpha|+l=m\}
by

\displaystyle\Delta_{\rm D}^{-1}(x^{\alpha}y^{l})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{j}l!}{(l+2j+2)!}((\Delta^{\prime})^{j}x^{\alpha})y^{l+2j+2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(l+2)(l+1)}x^{\alpha}y^{l+2}-\frac{1}{(l+4)(l+3)(l+2)(l+1)}(\Delta^{\prime}x^{\alpha})y^{l+4}+\cdots.

Then
\Delta_{\rm D}^{-1}
extends to an
\mathbb{R}
-linear map
\Delta_{\rm D}^{-1}:\dot{\mathcal{P}}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\dot{\mathcal{P}}_{m+2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Lemma 2.2

Let
m\geq 1
. Then the following statements hold.

(i)

Let
f\in\dot{\mathcal{P}}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then
u:=\Delta_{\rm D}^{-1}f
satisfies

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Delta u=f&\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}\\
u=0&\text{on }\partial\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}.\end{array}\right.

(15)

(ii)

Let
f\in\dot{\mathcal{P}}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. For
i=1,2,\cdots,d-1
, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{i}\Delta_{\rm D}^{-1}f=\Delta_{\rm D}^{-1}\partial_{i}f,

(16)

and

\displaystyle\partial_{y}\Delta_{\rm D}^{-1}f=\Delta_{\rm D}^{-1}\partial_{y}f+\partial_{y}\Delta_{D}^{-1}(f(x,0)),

(17)

In particular,
\partial_{y}
does not commute with
\Delta_{\rm D}^{-1}
on the space spanned by
\{x^{\alpha}\}_{|\alpha|=m}
.

Proof.

Note that (
15
) and (
16
) are obvious by the definition of
\Delta_{D}^{-1}
. To see (
17
), first observe that
\partial_{y}\Delta_{D}^{-1}(x^{\alpha}y^{l})=\Delta_{D}^{-1}\partial_{y}(x^{\alpha}y^{l})
, if
l\geq 1
. By linearity, this implies

\partial_{y}\Delta_{\rm D}^{-1}(f(x,y)-f(x,0))=\Delta_{\rm D}^{-1}\partial_{y}(f(x,y)-f(x,0))=\Delta_{\rm D}^{-1}\partial_{y}f,

which gives the desired result.
∎

Now we give a solution formula for (
SH
), which regards the pressure as a source term.

Proposition 2.3

Let
m\geq 2
. The following statements hold.

(i)

Let
p\in\dot{\mathcal{A}}_{m-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Set

U=\Delta_{\rm D}^{-1}\nabla p\in\dot{\mathcal{P}}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}.

Then the pair
(U,p)
satisfies

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta U+\nabla p=0&\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}\\
\nabla\cdot U=\partial_{y}\Delta_{\rm D}^{-1}(\partial_{y}p(x,0))&\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}\\
U=0&\text{on }\partial\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}.\end{array}\right.

(18)

(ii)

Let
p\in\dot{\mathcal{A}}_{m-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Set

V=(V_{1},0,\cdots,0)\in\dot{\mathcal{P}}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}

with
V_{1}
defined by

\displaystyle V_{1}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{(2j+1)!}((\Delta^{\prime})^{j}c_{m-1}(x))y^{2j+1}

\displaystyle=c_{m-1}(x)y-\frac{1}{3\cdot 2}(\Delta^{\prime}c_{m-1}(x))y^{3}+\cdots,

\displaystyle c_{m-1}(x)

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{x_{1}}\partial_{y}p(z,x_{2},\cdots,x_{d-1},0)\,{\rm d}z.

Then
V
satisfies

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta V=0&\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}\\
\nabla\cdot V=-\partial_{y}\Delta_{\rm D}^{-1}(\partial_{y}p(x,0))&\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}\\
V=0&\text{on }\partial\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}.\end{array}\right.

(19)

(iii)

Define the injective linear map
S:\dot{\mathcal{A}}_{m-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\dot{\mathcal{P}}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+})^{d}
by

S[p]=U+V.

Then the pair
(S[p],p)
is a solution of (
SH
), namely,
(S[p],p)\in\dot{\mathcal{S}}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+})
.

Proof.

(i) Lemma
2.2
(i) implies that the first and third lines of (
18
) are satisfied by
(U,p)
. It follows from (
16
) and (
17
) that

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot U

\displaystyle=\nabla^{\prime}\cdot\Delta_{D}^{-1}\nabla^{\prime}p+\partial_{y}\Delta_{D}^{-1}\partial_{y}p

(20)

\displaystyle=\Delta_{D}^{-1}\Delta^{\prime}p+\Delta_{D}^{-1}\partial_{y}^{2}p+\partial_{y}\Delta_{D}^{-1}(\partial_{y}p(x,0))

\displaystyle=\Delta_{D}^{-1}\Delta p+\partial_{y}\Delta_{D}^{-1}(\partial_{y}p(x,0))

\displaystyle=\partial_{y}\Delta_{D}^{-1}(\partial_{y}p(x,0)),

where the last line follows from the fact
p\in\dot{\mathcal{A}}_{m-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Hence
(U,p)
satisfies the second line of (
18
) as well.

(ii) It is easy to check that
V
satisfies the first and third lines of (
19
). A direct computation shows

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot V(x,y)

\displaystyle=\partial_{1}V_{1}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{(2j+1)!}((\Delta^{\prime})^{j}\partial_{1}c_{m-1}(x))y^{2j+1}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{(2j+1)!}((\Delta^{\prime})^{j}\partial_{y}p(x,0))y^{2j+1}

\displaystyle=-\partial_{y}\Delta_{\rm D}^{-1}(\partial_{y}p(x,0)),

where the definition of
\Delta_{D}^{-1}
has been used in the last equation.
This gives the second line of (
19
).

(iii) It follows from (i) and (ii) that
(S[p],p)
is a solution of (
SH
). To show the injectivity, let us assume that
S[p]=0
. Then one has
\nabla p=0
from the equations, which implies
p=0
because
p\in\dot{\mathcal{A}}_{m-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
m\geq 2
. This completes the proof.
∎

Now we provide a structure property of
\dot{\mathcal{S}}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+})
.

Proposition 2.4

Let
m\geq 2
and let
S
be the operator in Proposition
2.3
(iii). Then we have

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{S}}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+})=V_{m}^{(1)}\oplus V_{m}^{(2)},

where

\displaystyle V_{m}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\{(u,p)\in\dot{\mathcal{S}}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+})~|~p\in\dot{\mathcal{A}}_{m-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),u=S[p]\},

\displaystyle V_{m}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\{(u,p)\in\dot{\mathcal{S}}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+})~|~p=0\}.

Remark 2.5

By this proposition and the injectivity of
S
, for given pair
(u,p)\in\dot{\mathcal{S}}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+})
,
one can find a unique pressure
p\in\dot{\mathcal{A}}_{m-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
from the velocity
u\in\dot{\mathcal{P}}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}
.

Proof.

It suffices to show that
\dot{\mathcal{S}}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+})\subset V_{N}^{(1)}\oplus V_{N}^{(2)}
. Let
(u,p)\in\dot{\mathcal{S}}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+})
. If
p=0
, then
(u,p)\in V_{N}^{(2)}
. If
p\in\dot{\mathcal{A}}_{m-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\setminus\{0\}
, then
(S[p],p)\in V_{N}^{(1)}
and
(v,q):=(u,p)-(S[p],p)\in V_{N}^{(2)}
. Hence we have
(u,p)=(S[p],p)+(v,q)\in V_{N}^{(1)}\oplus V_{N}^{(2)}
, which concludes the proof.
∎

One can characterize the element of
V_{m}^{(2)}
as follows.

Lemma 2.6

Let
w=(w_{1},\cdots,w_{d-1},w_{d})\in V_{m}^{(2)}
. Then we have
w_{d}=0
and

\displaystyle w_{i}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{(2j+1)!}((\Delta^{\prime})^{j}a^{(i)}_{m-1}(x))y^{2j+1},\qquad i=1,\cdots,d-1

(21)

for some
a^{(1)}_{m-1},\cdots,a^{(d-1)}_{m-1}\in\dot{\mathcal{P}}_{m-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d-1})
with constraint

\displaystyle\partial_{1}a^{(1)}_{m-1}(x)+\cdots+\partial_{d-1}a^{(d-1)}_{m-1}(x)=0.

(22)

Conversely, any
w\in\dot{\mathcal{P}}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}
satisfying
w_{d}=0
, (
21
) and (
22
) belongs to
V_{m}^{(2)}
.

Proof.

Let
w\in V_{m}^{(2)}
. Then
w
satisfies

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Delta w=0&\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}\\
\nabla\cdot w=0&\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}\\
w=0&\text{on }\partial\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}.\end{array}\right.

From the first and third lines, and Lemma
2.1
, there are
a^{(1)}_{m-1},\cdots,a^{(d)}_{m-1}\in\dot{\mathcal{P}}_{m-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d-1})
such that (
21
) holds.
On the other hand,
\nabla\cdot w=0
leads to

\displaystyle 0=a^{(d)}_{m-1}(x)+(\partial_{1}a^{(1)}_{m-1}(x)+\cdots+\partial_{d-1}a^{(d-1)}_{m-1}(x))y+\cdots.

This implies both that
w_{d}=0
and that
\partial_{1}a^{(1)}_{m-1}(x)+\cdots+\partial_{d-1}a^{(d-1)}_{m-1}(x)=0
. It is easy to see that the converse holds true by the argument so far. The proof is complete.
∎

Proposition
2.4
and Lemma
2.6
imply the following corollary.

Corollary 2.7

Let
d\geq 2
and
m\geq 1
. Then we have

\dim\dot{\mathcal{S}}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+})=d\dbinom{m+d-3}{d-2}.

(23)

Proof.

By Proposition
2.4
, one has

\displaystyle\dim\dot{\mathcal{S}}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+})=\dim V_{m}^{(1)}+\dim V_{m}^{(2)}.

(24)

The injectivity of
S:\dot{\mathcal{A}}_{m-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\dot{\mathcal{P}}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+})^{d}
implies that

\displaystyle\dim V_{m}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\dim\dot{\mathcal{A}}_{m-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

(25)

By Lemma
2.6
, there is an isomorphism

\displaystyle V_{m}^{(2)}

\displaystyle\cong\{a=(a^{(1)}_{m-1}(x),\cdots,a^{(d-1)}_{m-1}(x))\in\mathcal{P}_{m-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d-1}_{+})^{d-1}~|~\nabla^{\prime}\cdot a=0\}

\displaystyle=\ker\nabla^{\prime}\cdot.

Here
\nabla^{\prime}\cdot
is the linear map
\nabla^{\prime}\cdot:\dot{\mathcal{P}}_{m-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d-1})^{d-1}\to\dot{\mathcal{P}}_{m-2}(\mathbb{R}^{d-1})
. Since it is obviously surjective, the homomorphism theorem yields that

Proposition 4.3

For any
r\in(1,R)
and
\mu\in(0,1)
,

H_{m}(u;r)\leq C_{m,\mu}\Big(\frac{r}{R}\Big)^{m-\mu}H_{m}(u;R),

where
C_{m,\mu}
depends on
\Omega,m
and
\mu
.

Proof.

First of all, for any
\mu\in(0,1)
, we can find sufficiently small
\theta\in(0,1/4)
such that
C_{m}\theta^{m}\leq(1/2)^{m+1-\mu}\theta^{m-\mu}
. Fix such
\theta
. Set

\rho_{0}=\Big(C_{m,\theta}\Big(\frac{1}{2}\Big)^{-m-1+\mu}\theta^{-m+\mu}\Big)^{2}.

Then, for any

, we have
C_{m,\theta}r^{-1/2}\leq(1/2)^{m+1-\mu}\theta^{m-\mu}
. Consequently,
it follows from (
120
) that

H_{m}(u;\theta r)\leq(\theta/2)^{m-\mu}H_{m}(u;2r).

(122)

Now let
\theta_{1}=\theta/2
. By an iteration, we have

H(u;\theta_{1}^{k}R)\leq\theta_{1}^{k(m-\mu)}H(u;R),

(123)

provided
\theta_{1}^{k}R\geq\rho_{0}
. This implies (
121
) for any
r\geq\rho_{0}
. Finally, the case

follows from the case

by enlarging the constant

. The proof is complete.
∎

4.2

Improvements

Let
(W_{r}^{*},\Pi_{r}^{*})
denote the minimizer for

H_{m}(u;r)=\bigg(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{B_{r,+}}|\nabla u-\nabla W_{r}^{*}|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}.

Clearly, the minimizer

To this end, we have to take advantage of the polynomial-like properties of the heterogeneous Stokes polynomials in

The next lemma describes a polynomial-like growth property for the heterogeneous polynomials.

As a application of the large-scale estimates, we have the Liouville theorem for

\|\nabla u-\nabla w\|_{\underline{L}^{2}(B_{r,+})}+\|p-\pi-c_{p}\|_{\underline{L}^{2}(B_{r,+})}\leq C\Big(\frac{r}{R}\Big)^{m}\|\nabla u\|_{\underline{L}^{2}(B_{R,+})}.

(146)

Let
(x,y)\in B_{R/2,+}
and
y\geq 4
. Put
r:=|(x,y)|
. Without loss of generality, assume
1<r<R/5
. Then the interior and boundary Lipschitz estimates (see, e.g., (
105
)) for the solution
(u-w,p-\pi-c_{p})
implies

\displaystyle|\nabla u(x,y)-\nabla w(x,y)|

\displaystyle\leq C\bigg(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{Q_{y/2}(x,y)}|\nabla(u-w)|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}

(147)

\displaystyle\leq C\bigg(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{B_{2y,+}(x,0)}|\nabla(u-w)|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq C\bigg(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{B_{2r,+}(x,0)}|\nabla(u-w)|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq C\bigg(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{B_{3r,+}}|\nabla(u-w)|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{m}\Big(\frac{r}{R}\Big)^{m}\|\nabla u\|_{\underline{L}^{2}(B_{R,+})}.

To estimate the pressure, since
p-\pi-c_{p}
is harmonic in
\Omega
, the interior estimate (such as the mean value property) implies that

\displaystyle|p(x,y)-\pi(x,y)-c_{p}|

\displaystyle\leq C\bigg(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{Q_{y/2}(x,y)}|p-\pi-c_{p}|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq C\bigg(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{B_{2y,+}(x,0)}|p-\pi-c_{p}|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq C\bigg(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{B_{2y,+}(x,0)}|p-\pi-c_{p}-\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{B_{2r,+}(x,0)}(p-\pi-c_{p})|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}

\displaystyle+C\bigg|\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{B_{2r,+}(x,0)}(p-\pi-c_{p})\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq C\bigg(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{B_{4r,+}(x,0)}|\nabla(u-w)|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}+C\|p-\pi-c_{p}\|_{\underline{L}^{2}(B_{4r,+})}

\displaystyle\leq C\Big(\frac{r}{R}\Big)^{m}\|\nabla u\|_{\underline{L}^{2}(B_{R,+})},

where we have used (
105
) and (
146
) in the last two inequalities.

On the other hand, let
(w_{\rm poly},\pi_{\rm poly})
be the effective Stokes polynomial of
(w,\pi)
. Then Proposition
5.1
implies

\displaystyle|\nabla w(x,y)-\nabla w_{\rm poly}(x,y)|+|\pi(x,y)-\pi_{\rm poly}(x,y)|

\displaystyle\leq C_{m}\|\nabla w\|_{\underline{L}^{2}(B_{r_{0},+})}(1+|x|)^{m-1}e^{-y/2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{m}\|\nabla w\|_{\underline{L}^{2}(B_{R/2,+})}(1+|x|)^{m-1}e^{-y/2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{m}\|\nabla u\|_{\underline{L}^{2}(B_{R,+})}(1+|x|)^{m-1}e^{-y/2},

where we have used (
146
) in the last inequality. Combining the above three estimates, we obtain the desired estimate (
10
) by the triangle inequality.

Finally, using the interior and boundary Lipschitz estimates, as well as the Poincaré inequality, we have

\displaystyle|u(x,y)-w(x,y)|

\displaystyle\leq C\bigg(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{B_{y/2}(x,y)}|u-w|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq C\bigg(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{B_{2y,+}(x,0)}|u-w|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq Cy\bigg(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{B_{2y,+}(x,0)}|\nabla(u-w)|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{m}y\Big(\frac{r}{R}\Big)^{m}\|\nabla u\|_{\underline{L}^{2}(B_{R,+})},

where we have used the similar argument as (
147
) in the last inequality. This, combined with (
144
) again, leads to (
11
). Thus, the proof is complete.

\Box

5.2

Higher-order wall laws

Our aim here is to identify the (local) wall laws described in the introduction. As seen from Proposition
5.2
below, these depend only on the structure of
\partial\Omega
, not on the specific elements in
\mathcal{S}_{m}^{\rm eff}(\Omega)
. This indicates that the wall laws are intrinsic to the domain
\Omega
.

Firstly we describe how the Navier wall law will be derived in our context. Let us take
(v^{\alpha},q^{\alpha})=(v^{\alpha,l}_{(i)},q^{\alpha,l}_{(i)})
in Proposition
3.1
. The general element in
\mathcal{S}_{1}^{\rm eff}(\Omega)
takes a form of

w_{\rm poly}(x,y)=\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}c_{i}(y\mathbf{e}_{i}+T_{(i)}),\qquad T_{(i)}:=\lim_{y\to 0}v^{0,1}_{(i)}(x,y),

where
T_{(i)}
is the constant vector known as the first-order boundary layer tail. As a side note, we mention that
T_{(i)}\cdot{\bf e}_{d}=0
by the divergence-free condition. Now if we set

\Phi^{0,1}=(T_{(1)},T_{(2)},\cdots,T_{(d-1)},0)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},

(148)

then we recover the classical Navier wall law, or the Navier slip condition, as

w_{\rm poly}(x,0)=\Phi^{0,1}\partial_{y}w_{\rm poly}(x,0)\quad\text{for any }(w_{\rm poly},\pi_{\rm poly})\in\mathcal{S}_{1}^{\rm eff}(\Omega).

(149)

Next let
W^{\alpha,l}_{(i)}
denote the effective polynomial of
x^{\alpha}y^{l}\mathbf{e}_{i}+v^{\alpha,l}_{(i)}
in the meaning of Subsection
5.1
. Then we define the matrix-valued polynomial
W^{\alpha,l}=W^{\alpha,l}(x,y)
by

W^{\alpha,l}=(W^{\alpha,l}_{(1)},W^{\alpha,l}_{(2)},\cdots,W^{\alpha,l}_{(d)}).

We also define the matrix-valued polynomial
\Phi^{\alpha,l}=\Phi^{\alpha,l}(x)
recursively. Starting from
\Phi^{0,1}
in (
148
), for
(\alpha,l)
with
|\alpha|+l=m
, we define

\Phi^{\alpha,l}(x)=\frac{1}{\alpha!l!}\bigg(W^{\alpha,l}(x,0)-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\beta|+k\leq m-1\\
\beta\geq 0,k\geq 1\end{subarray}}\Phi^{\beta,k}(x)\partial_{x}^{\beta}\partial_{y}^{k}W^{\alpha,l}(x,0)\bigg).

(150)

The higher-order wall laws are described by
\{\Phi^{\alpha,l}\}_{\alpha\geq 0,l\geq 1}
. Indeed, we have

Proposition 5.2

Let
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. Then, for any
(w_{\rm poly},\pi_{\rm poly})\in\mathcal{S}_{m}^{\rm eff}(\Omega)
,

\displaystyle w_{\rm poly}(x,0)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\alpha|+l\leq m\\
\alpha\geq 0,l\geq 1\end{subarray}}\Phi^{\alpha,l}(x)\partial_{x}^{\alpha}\partial_{y}^{l}w_{\rm poly}(x,0).

(151)

Proof.

The proof uses an induction on the order
m
. We already proved the case when
m=1
in (
148
) and (
149
). Let us assume that (
151
) holds for
m-1
. Let
(w_{\rm poly},\pi_{\rm poly})\in\mathcal{S}_{m}^{\rm eff}(\Omega)
. Since the highest degree of
w_{\rm poly}
is
m
, we can easily find its leading term as

\frac{1}{\alpha!l!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\alpha|+l=m\\
\alpha\geq 0,l\geq 1\end{subarray}}W^{\alpha,l}(x,y)\partial_{x}^{\alpha}\partial_{y}^{l}w_{\rm poly}(x,0).

Define

\widetilde{w}_{\rm poly}(x,y)=w_{\rm poly}(x,y)-\frac{1}{\alpha!l!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\alpha|+l=m\\
\alpha\geq 0,l\geq 1\end{subarray}}W^{\alpha,l}(x,y)\partial_{x}^{\alpha}\partial_{y}^{l}w_{\rm poly}(x,0).

(152)

Similarly, we can define the corresponding pressure part
\widetilde{\pi}_{\rm poly}(x,y)
. Since

(\widetilde{w}_{\rm poly}(x,y),\widetilde{\pi}_{\rm poly}(x,y))\in\mathcal{S}_{m-1}^{\rm eff}(\Omega),

by the inductive assumption, one has

\displaystyle\widetilde{w}_{\rm poly}(x,0)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\beta|+k\leq m-1\\
\beta\geq 0,k\geq 1\end{subarray}}\Phi^{\beta,k}(x)\partial_{x}^{\beta}\partial_{y}^{k}\widetilde{w}_{\rm poly}(x,0).

Combined with (
152
), we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{w}_{\rm poly}(x,0)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\beta|+k\leq m-1\\
\beta\geq 0,k\geq 1\end{subarray}}\Phi^{\beta,k}(x)\partial_{x}^{\beta}\partial_{y}^{k}w_{\rm poly}(x,0)

(153)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\alpha!l!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\alpha|+l=m\\
\alpha\geq 0,l\geq 1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\beta|+k\leq m-1\\
\beta\geq 0,k\geq 1\end{subarray}}\Phi^{\beta,k}(x)\partial_{x}^{\beta}\partial_{y}^{k}W^{\alpha,l}(x,0)\partial_{x}^{\alpha}\partial_{y}^{l}w_{\rm poly}(x,0).

Again by (
152
), (
153
), and the definition of
\Phi^{\alpha,l}
with
|\alpha|+l=m
in (
150
), we obtain

\displaystyle w_{\rm poly}(x,0)

(154)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{w}_{\rm poly}(x,0)+\frac{1}{\alpha!l!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\alpha|+l=m\\
\alpha\geq 0,l\geq 1\end{subarray}}W^{\alpha,l}(x,0)\partial_{x}^{\alpha}\partial_{y}^{l}w_{\rm poly}(x,0)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\beta|+k\leq m-1\\
\beta\geq 0,k\geq 1\end{subarray}}\Phi^{\beta,k}(x)\partial_{x}^{\beta}\partial_{y}^{k}w_{\rm poly}(x,0)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\alpha!l!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\alpha|+l=m\\
\alpha\geq 0,l\geq 1\end{subarray}}\bigg(W^{\alpha,l}(x,0)-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\beta|+k\leq m-1\\
\beta\geq 0,k\geq 1\end{subarray}}\Phi^{\beta,k}(x)\partial_{x}^{\beta}\partial_{y}^{k}W^{\alpha,l}(x,0)\bigg)\partial_{x}^{\alpha}\partial_{y}^{l}w_{\rm poly}(x,0)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\alpha|+l\leq m\\
\alpha\geq 0,l\geq 1\end{subarray}}\Phi^{\alpha,l}(x)\partial_{x}^{\alpha}\partial_{y}^{l}w_{\rm poly}(x,0).

This is the desired relation (
151
). The proof is complete.
∎

5.3

An example for
d=m=2

To provide a concrete example, we describe the second-order wall law for two-dimensional flows in
\Omega
. A basis of the space
\mathcal{S}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
can be found as

\displaystyle(0,1),\quad(y{\bf e}_{1},0),\quad(y^{2}{\bf e}_{1},2x),\quad(-2xy{\bf e_{1}}+y^{2}{\bf e_{2}},2y).

We would like to make explicit the elements in
\mathcal{S}_{2}^{\rm eff}(\Omega)
, which is nothing more than determining the effective polynomial corresponding to the following velocity field

\displaystyle u(x,y):=c_{1}y{\bf e}_{1}+c_{2}y^{2}{\bf e}_{1}+c_{3}(-2xy{\bf e_{1}}+y^{2}{\bf e_{2}}),\quad c_{i}\in\mathbb{R}.

(155)

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
,
l\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and
i\in\{1,2\}
. By Proposition
3.1
, the polynomial part of the boundary layer
v^{\alpha,l}_{(i)}
correcting
x^{\alpha}y^{l}{\bf e}_{i}
is given by

\displaystyle(v^{\alpha,l}_{(i)})_{\rm poly}(x,y)=\sum_{\beta=0}^{\alpha}\dbinom{\alpha}{\beta}x^{\alpha-\beta}(V^{\beta,l}_{(i)})_{\rm poly}(y)

with
(V^{\beta,l}_{(i)})_{\rm poly}(y)=V^{\beta}_{\rm poly}(y)
determined recursively by

V^{0}_{\rm poly}(y)=V^{0}_{\rm poly}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(V^{0}_{\rm const})_{1}{\bf e}_{1}&\text{if }\ i=1,\\
V^{0}_{\rm const}&\text{if }\ i=2,\end{array}\right.\quad\text{and}\quad Q^{0}_{\rm poly}(y)=0,

(156)

and from the proof of Proposition
3.5
,

\displaystyle V^{\beta}_{\rm poly}(y)

\displaystyle=V^{\beta}_{\rm const}-\bigg(\int_{0}^{y}\dbinom{\beta}{1}(V^{\beta-1}_{\rm poly})_{1}(t)\,{\rm d}t\bigg){\bf e}_{2}

(157)

\displaystyle+\bigg(\int_{0}^{y}\int_{0}^{t}\bigg(-2\dbinom{\beta}{2}(V^{\beta-2}_{\rm poly})_{1}(s)+\dbinom{\beta}{1}Q^{\beta-1}_{\rm poly}(s)\bigg)\,{\rm d}s\,{\rm d}t\bigg){\bf e}_{1},

\displaystyle Q^{\beta}_{\rm poly}(y)

\displaystyle=\bigg(\int_{0}^{y}2\dbinom{\beta}{2}(V^{\beta-2}_{\rm poly})_{2}(t)\,{\rm d}t\bigg)-\dbinom{\beta}{1}(V^{\beta-1}_{\rm poly})_{1}(y),

where all the constants are gathered together and denoted by
V^{\beta}_{\rm const}
in the first line of (
157
). In (
156
), the second component of
V^{0}_{\rm poly}
is zero when
i=1
, because of the divergence-free condition and the boundary condition. The proof for the 3D case can be found in
[
24
, Proposition 3]
. The argument for the case of general dimension is the same.

The effective polynomials corresponding to
y{\bf e}_{1}
,
y^{2}{\bf e}_{1}
,
y^{2}{\bf e_{2}}
in (
155
) are, respectively,

\displaystyle\big(y+((V^{0,1}_{(1)})_{\rm const})_{1}\big){\bf e}_{1},\qquad\big(y^{2}+((V^{0,2}_{(1)})_{\rm const})_{1}\big){\bf e}_{1},\qquad y^{2}{\bf e_{2}}+(V^{0,2}_{(2)})_{\rm const}.

Thus we focus on the effective polynomial corresponding to
-2xy{\bf e_{1}}
in (
155
), namely
-2(xy{\bf e_{1}}+(v^{1,1}_{(1)})_{\rm poly})
. By a direct computation, one has

\displaystyle(v^{1,1}_{(1)})_{\rm poly}(x,y)

\displaystyle=x(V^{0,1}_{(1)})_{\rm poly}(y)+(V^{1,1}_{(1)})_{\rm poly}(y)

\displaystyle=x((V^{0,1}_{(1)})_{\rm const})_{1}{\bf e}_{1}+(V^{1,1}_{(1)})_{\rm const}-y((V^{0,1}_{(1)})_{\rm const})_{1}{\bf e}_{2}.

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle-2\big(xy{\bf e_{1}}+(v^{1,1}_{(1)})_{\rm poly}(x,y)\big)

\displaystyle=-2\big(xy+x((V^{0,1}_{(1)})_{\rm const})_{1}\big){\bf e_{1}}+2y((V^{0,1}_{(1)})_{\rm const})_{1}{\bf e}_{2}-2(V^{1,1}_{(1)})_{\rm const}.

Combining the above, we see that the general element in
\mathcal{S}_{2}^{\rm eff}(\Omega)
is written as

\displaystyle w(x,y)

\displaystyle=c_{1}\big(y+((V^{0,1}_{(1)})_{\rm const})_{1}\big){\bf e}_{1}+c_{2}\big(y^{2}+((V^{0,2}_{(1)})_{\rm const})_{1}\big){\bf e}_{1}

\displaystyle-2c_{3}\big(xy+x((V^{0,1}_{(1)})_{\rm const})_{1}\big){\bf e_{1}}+2c_{3}y((V^{0,1}_{(1)})_{\rm const})_{1}{\bf e}_{2}+c_{3}y^{2}{\bf e_{2}}

\displaystyle-2c_{3}(V^{1,1}_{(1)})_{\rm const}+c_{3}(V^{0,2}_{(2)})_{\rm const},\quad c_{i}\in\mathbb{R}.

Next we derive the second-order wall law described by
w
. Taking the trace of
w(x,y)
on
\{y=0\}
, we find

\displaystyle w(x,0)

\displaystyle=c_{1}((V^{0,1}_{(1)})_{\rm const})_{1}{\bf e_{1}}+c_{2}((V^{0,2}_{(1)})_{\rm const})_{1}{\bf e_{1}}

(158)

\displaystyle+c_{3}\big(-2x((V^{0,1}_{(1)})_{\rm const})_{1}{\bf e_{1}}-2(V^{1,1}_{(1)})_{\rm const}+(V^{0,2}_{(2)})_{\rm const}\big).

Moreover, computing the derivatives,

\displaystyle\partial_{y}\partial_{x}w(x,0)

\displaystyle=-2c_{3}{\bf e_{1}},

\displaystyle\partial_{y}^{2}w(x,0)

\displaystyle=2c_{2}{\bf e_{1}}+2c_{3}{\bf e_{2}},

\displaystyle\partial_{y}w(x,0)

\displaystyle=(c_{1}-2c_{3}x){\bf e_{1}}+2c_{3}((V^{0,1}_{(1)})_{\rm const})_{1}{\bf e_{2}}.

The latter system can be solved for
(c_{1},c_{2},c_{3})
:

\displaystyle c_{1}

\displaystyle=\partial_{y}w_{1}(x,0)+2c_{3}x=\partial_{y}w_{1}(x,0)-x\partial_{y}\partial_{x}w_{1}(x,0),

\displaystyle c_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{y}^{2}w_{1}(x,0),

\displaystyle c_{3}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{y}\partial_{x}w_{1}(x,0).

Inserting this into
(
158
), we obtain the following second-order wall law:

\displaystyle w(x,0)

\displaystyle=\big(\partial_{y}w_{1}(x,0)-x\partial_{y}\partial_{x}w_{1}(x,0)\big)((V^{0,1}_{(1)})_{\rm const})_{1}{\bf e_{1}}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{y}^{2}w_{1}(x,0)((V^{0,2}_{(1)})_{\rm const})_{1}{\bf e_{1}}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{y}\partial_{x}w_{1}(x,0)\big(-2x((V^{0,1}_{(1)})_{\rm const})_{1}{\bf e_{1}}-2(V^{1,1}_{(1)})_{\rm const}+(V^{0,2}_{(2)})_{\rm const}\big)

\displaystyle=\Big(((V^{0,1}_{(1)})_{\rm const})_{1}\partial_{y}w_{1}(x,0)+\frac{1}{2}((V^{0,2}_{(1)})_{\rm const})_{1}\partial_{y}^{2}w_{1}(x,0)\Big){\bf e_{1}}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\big(-2(V^{1,1}_{(1)})_{\rm const}+(V^{0,2}_{(2)})_{\rm const}\big)\partial_{y}\partial_{x}w_{1}(x,0).

In applications, the roughness of the boundary, i.e., amplitude and wavelength, are assumed to be small. Denoting it by
\varepsilon
, after rescaling, the second-order wall law reads as

\displaystyle w(x,0)

\displaystyle=\Big(\varepsilon((V^{0,1}_{(1)})_{\rm const})_{1}\partial_{y}w_{1}(x,0)+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}((V^{0,2}_{(1)})_{\rm const})_{1}\partial_{y}^{2}w_{1}(x,0)\Big){\bf e_{1}}

(159)

\displaystyle-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\big(-2(V^{1,1}_{(1)})_{\rm const}+(V^{0,2}_{(2)})_{\rm const}\big)\partial_{y}\partial_{x}w_{1}(x,0).

If we set
\lambda=((V^{0,1}_{(1)})_{\rm const})_{1}
and neglect the terms of order
\varepsilon^{2}
, then (
159
) becomes

\displaystyle w_{1}(x,0)=\varepsilon\lambda\partial_{y}w_{1}(x,0),\qquad w_{2}(x,0)=0.

It implies that the principal part of the the second-order wall law is the Navier wall. It is worth noting that
\lambda
is known to be positive. This is called the slip length and plays an important role especially in the energy computation; see
[
13
,
23
]
for details.

In contrast, the sign of
((V^{0,2}_{(1)})_{\rm const})_{1}
does not seem to be known, which is important when one studies the second-order wall law in the global stationary/nonstationary settings. We refer to the numerical analysis in
[
12
, Section 6]
for the case of the Laplace equation. The results suggest that the sign of the corresponding constant is negative.

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d-1}\setminus\{0\}}\bigg\{\mathcal{F}_{k}(y-L)-\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{i}k\\
-|k|\\
\end{bmatrix}{\mathcal{Q}}_{k}(y-L)-\begin{bmatrix}0\\
(\partial_{z}\mathcal{Q}_{k})(y-L)\\
\end{bmatrix}\bigg\}e^{-|k|(y-L)}e^{\mathrm{i}k\cdot x}.

This, combined with (
161
) and (
35
), leads to

-\Delta V+\nabla Q=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d-1}\setminus\{0\}}\mathcal{F}_{k}(y-L)e^{-|k|(y-L)}e^{\mathrm{i}k\cdot x}=F.

(169)

This proves the first equation in (
45
).

Now, we show
\nabla\cdot V=0
. To this end, we first calculate

\Delta Q=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d-1}\setminus\{0\}}\big\{(\partial_{z}^{2}\mathcal{Q}_{k})(y-L)-2|k|(\partial_{z}\mathcal{Q}_{k})(y-L)\big\}e^{-|k|(y-L)}e^{\mathrm{i}k\cdot x}.

(170)

Moreover,

\partial_{z}\mathcal{Q}_{k}(z)=-\int_{z}^{\infty}\bigg\{\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{i}k\\
-|k|\\
\end{bmatrix}\cdot\mathcal{F}_{k}(w)+(\partial_{z}\mathcal{F}_{k})_{d}(w)\bigg\}e^{2|k|(z-w)}\,{\rm d}w,

(171)

\partial_{z}^{2}\mathcal{Q}_{k}(z)=\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{i}k\\
-|k|\\
\end{bmatrix}\cdot\mathcal{F}_{k}(z)+(\partial_{z}\mathcal{F}_{k})_{d}(z)+2|k|(\partial_{z}\mathcal{Q}_{k})(z).

(172)

Combining (
170
) and (
172
), we have

\displaystyle\Delta Q

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d-1}\setminus\{0\}}\bigg\{\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{i}k\\
-|k|\\
\end{bmatrix}\cdot\mathcal{F}_{k}(y-L)+(\partial_{z}\mathcal{F}_{k})_{d}(y-L)\bigg\}e^{-|k|(y-L)}e^{\mathrm{i}k\cdot x}

(173)

\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot F.

Hence, (
169
) and (
173
) together yield that
\nabla\cdot V
is harmonic, namely,

\Delta(\nabla\cdot V)=0.

(174)

To show
\nabla\cdot V=0
, it suffices to show
\nabla\cdot V(\cdot,L)=0
and
\lim_{y\to\infty}\nabla\cdot V(\cdot,y)=0
. In order to see this, we compute

\nabla\cdot V=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d-1}\setminus\{0\}}\bigg\{\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{i}k\\
-|k|\\
\end{bmatrix}\cdot\mathcal{V}_{k}(y-L)+\begin{bmatrix}0\\
1\\
\end{bmatrix}\cdot(\partial_{z}\mathcal{V}_{k})(y-L)\bigg\}e^{-|k|(y-L)}e^{\mathrm{i}k\cdot x}.

(175)

Using (
49
) and (
47
), we have

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{i}k\\
-|k|\\
\end{bmatrix}\cdot\mathcal{V}_{k}(z)+\begin{bmatrix}0\\
1\\
\end{bmatrix}\cdot(\partial_{z}\mathcal{V}_{k})(z)

(176)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{i}k\\
-|k|\\
\end{bmatrix}\cdot\hat{V}_{k}-c_{k}+\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{i}k\\
-|k|\\
\end{bmatrix}\cdot(\underline{\mathcal{V}}_{k}(z)-\underline{\mathcal{V}}_{k}(0))+\begin{bmatrix}0\\
1\\
\end{bmatrix}\cdot\partial_{z}\underline{\mathcal{V}}_{k}(z)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{i}k\\
-|k|\\
\end{bmatrix}\cdot(\underline{\mathcal{V}}_{k}(z)-\underline{\mathcal{V}}_{k}(0))+\begin{bmatrix}0\\
1\\
\end{bmatrix}\cdot(\partial_{z}\underline{\mathcal{V}}_{k}(z)-\partial_{z}\underline{\mathcal{V}}_{k}(0)).

Clearly, (
175
) and (
176
) imply
\nabla\cdot V(\cdot,L)=0
. On the other hand, notice that
\underline{\mathcal{V}}_{k}(z)
is a polynomial of
z
, which means that (
176
) has at most polynomial growth as
z\to\infty
. This implies that
\nabla\cdot V(\cdot,y)\to 0
as
y\to\infty
, due to the exponential decay factor
e^{-|k|(y-L)}
in (
175
). As a result, we derive
\nabla\cdot V=0
from (
174
).

Finally, it is easy to see that
V(\cdot,L)=b
. Therefore, we have verified that
(V,Q)
satisfies all the equations in (
45
).
