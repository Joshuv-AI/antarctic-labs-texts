On the theory of high- T c T_{\text{c}} superconductivity of doped cuprates
Y.G. Pogorelov ? {}^{\textsf{\footnotesize{\mbox{?}}}}, V.M. Loktev ? , ?

Abstract
A theoretical analysis is presented on possible effects of disorder by dopants in high-temperature superconducting cuprate perovskites, to define their basic spectra of spin and electronic excitations, and the subsequent observable properties, especially doping dependence of superconducting order parameter. The central point in the proposed physical picture is formation of specific impurity subband within the insulating bandgap of initial undoped material, serving as a source for the system metallization and further transition into superconducting state with anisotropic order parameter. Key words: dopant concentration, cuprate magnetic and electronic spectra, superconductivity, superconducting gap and transition temperature PACS: 71.30.+h, 71.35.-i, 74.0.-z, 74.20.Rp, 74.62.Dh, 74.72.-h

to reach a maximum and then decaying to vanish at some upper limit

[
8

transition from insulating to metallic state, beginning from the Anderson resonating valence bond model
[

,
10
,
11

,
Zhang-Rice singlet model
[

]
, Emery-Kivelson phase fluctuation model
[

and many others. For instance, a recent study by this

cuprates under the doping induced disorder
[

some issues in this field still remain out of scope of the existing approaches, for instance recent experimental findings of anomalously long

[

It should be noted here that all the above mentioned theoretical pictures of SC state, either canonical or strong-correlational, consider many-body
processes in high-
T_{\text{c}}
materials, even in presence of randomly located dopants, as in uniform crystalline systems with full translational invariance,
implicitly disregarding the effects of static disorder. On the other hand, such effects can qualitatively modify spectral and related observable properties
of various crystalline systems with disorder as was shown by many researchers, beginning from the seminal studies by Lifshitz on emergence of local
modes and impurity bands
[
17
,
18
]
and by Mott on mobility thresholds between localized and band-like states
[
19
,
20
]
. In
particular, restructuring of excitation spectra in high-
T_{\text{c}}
materials under perturbations from both dopants and isovalent impurities was discussed in
detail by the authors
[
21
,
22

, though leaving aside the role of these perturbations for the very formation of the SC order. The present work is
just aimed at extension of our earlier theoretical approaches to this latter aspect of the high-
T_{\text{c}}
problem, in order to clarify the decisive importance
of disorder effects for understanding the physical difference between BCS and high-
T_{\text{c}}
types of superconductivity. This task is being done here
with parallel analysis of two different kinds of elementary excitations in the perovskite compound: fermion quasiparticles by electron hopping between
neighbor Cu sites and boson-like spin excitations at strong enough indirect antiferromagnetic (AFM) superexchange between these sites via intermediate
O sites. Their interplay is found to define the insulating state of the undoped material and then, introducing perturbations from dopant atoms on both
excitation modes, the possibility for its transition to a specific metallic state with strong disorder effects and anomalously narrow conduction band is
established. The latter specifics is further analyzed for its influence, together with the fermion-boson interplay, on the formation and properties of the
SC state, in particular, the non-monotonous dependence of the SC order parameter on the doping level.

The following consideration starts in section
2
from definition of the basic Green functions, specific for the analysis of different types of elementary
excitations in a disordered crystalline system. Section
3
explains the choice of effective Hamiltonians for spin and charge degrees of freedom,
including their perturbations due to dopants. Then, we find the excitation spectra in this system, with a special emphasis on the effects of spin dynamics
on charge dynamics, either in the initial state or under finite doping. In the latter case, the important doping effects for decay of long range spin order
and for metallization of charge carrier spectrum are analyzed in section
4

described by their dispersion laws, possible localized modes near the dopant atoms, all resumed in the corresponding density of states (DOS) function.
In particular, this function permits to define the position of Fermi level under doping, one of central issues for the onset of SC order. Then, the characteristics
of the resulting SC state as functions of doping and its related disorder are obtained in section
5
, permitting to better understand the experimentally
measured anomalies in this state, compared to the conventional BCS theory predictions. Finally, some discussion and conclusions on the developed
SC model are presented in section
6

2
Green functions and observable values

The spin and charge dynamics in this system are analyzed below through the standard techniques of two-time Green functions (GF’s)
[
25
,
26
]
:

{\langle\langle}A|B{\rangle\rangle}_{\pm,\varepsilon}=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\piup}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\varepsilon+\mathrm{i}0)t}\langle[A(t),B(0)]_{\pm}\rangle\mathrm{d}t,

(1)

where
\langle\ldots\rangle=(\Tr\mathrm{e}^{-\beta H})^{-1}(\Tr\mathrm{e}^{-\beta H}\ldots)
is the quantum statistical average of any
operator in the system with full Hamiltonian
H
at inverse temperature
\beta=1/(k_{\text{B}}T)
, the commutator
[A,B]_{-}
or anticommutator
[A,B]_{+}
is
chosen respectively for the operators
A
,
B
of Bose or Fermi type (implying respective types of thermal averaging for them), while positive infinitesimal
imaginary part
\text{i}0
in the energy argument of Fourier transform corresponds to the retarded GF type. These functions obey the equation of motion:

\varepsilon{\langle\langle}A|B{\rangle\rangle}_{\pm,\varepsilon}=\langle[A,B]_{\pm}\rangle+{\langle\langle}[A,H]_{-}|B{\rangle\rangle}_{\pm,\varepsilon}\,,

(2)

permitting their consequent calculation with given
H
(in what follows, the explicit symmetry and energy indices of the GFs are typically omitted). The
basic GF advantage is to provide directly the system observable values from the spectral theorem:

\langle AB\rangle=\frac{1}{\piup}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathop{Im}\nolimits{\langle\langle}B|A{\rangle\rangle}_{\varepsilon}\mathrm{d}\varepsilon.

(3)

It should be noted that the two-time GF’s defined by equations (
1
)–(
3
), though being most suitable to analyze the effects by doped charges and
associated disorder from dopant atoms (of central importance in the present work), but are only justified in the zero temperature limit and they are mostly
employed in this limit below. Otherwise, the case of finite temperatures should be treated, strictly speaking, with more specialized Matsubara GF’s
[
27
,
28
]
, though producing some technical problems for their use in disordered systems. Nevertheless, some qualitative estimates of critical temperatures
for both AFM and SC ordering under doping are possible even from the simpler two-time GF formalism, so we choose to stay with it for all the following study.
To distinguish between the two different types of excitation spectra in the present system, we shall denote the GF energy argument
\omega
for Bose-type spin
states and
\varepsilon
(referred to the chemical potential
\mu
) for Fermi-type charge carrier states.

3.1
Spin dynamics

For treating the spin order and spin excitations in the initial undoped structure, we use the simplest model Hamiltonian involving the strong AFM
superexchange
J
between nearest neighbor Cu spins (to impose their antiparallel orientation) as well as the weak terms of tetragonal anisotropy
\Delta J_{\textrm{t}}
and of
Dzyaloshinskii-Moriya antisymmetric exchange
D
(to provide stability of the AFM order):

H_{s}=\sum_{\mathbf{n},\boldsymbol{\delta}}\left[J\mathbf{s}_{\mathbf{n}}\cdot\mathbf{s}_{\mathbf{n}+\boldsymbol{\delta}}-\Delta J_{\textrm{t}}s_{\mathbf{n}}^{y}s_{\mathbf{n}+\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{y}-D\left(s_{\mathbf{n}}^{y}s_{\mathbf{n}+\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{z}-s_{\mathbf{n}}^{z}s_{\mathbf{n}+\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{y}\right)\right],

(4)

with the vectors
\boldsymbol{\delta}
of separation between nearest neighbor Cu sites.

Under the strongest
J
effect only, the spin ground state would have two enclosed square sublattices with strictly opposite spin orientations but unstable because
of their free absolute orientation. Adding the weak
\Delta J_{\textrm{t}}
effect, restricts this freedom to the
xz
-plane and, finally, the weak
D
effect fixes the sublattice quantization
axes almost antiparallel along the
z
-axis and with slight symmetric deviations from
z
- towards
y
-axis by an angle
\theta
(see inset in figure
1
). This
implies the following expressions of spin components on
j
-th sublattice sites in
\mathbf{n}
-th unit cell through the local spin-flip operators
b_{\mathbf{n}_{j}}
:

\displaystyle s_{\mathbf{n}_{1}}^{z}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{2}-b_{\mathbf{n}_{1}}^{\dagger}b_{\mathbf{n}_{1}}\right)\cos\theta+\frac{b_{\mathbf{n}_{1}}-b_{\mathbf{n}_{1}}^{\dagger}}{2\mathrm{i}}\sin\theta,\qquad s_{\mathbf{n}_{2}}^{z}=\left(b_{\mathbf{n}_{2}}^{\dagger}b_{\mathbf{n}_{2}}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\cos\theta+\frac{b_{\mathbf{n}_{2}}-b_{\mathbf{n}_{2}}^{\dagger}}{2\mathrm{i}}\sin\theta,

\displaystyle s_{\mathbf{n}_{1}}^{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(b_{\mathbf{n}_{1}}^{\dagger}b_{\mathbf{n}_{1}}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\sin\theta+\frac{b_{\mathbf{n}_{1}}-b_{\mathbf{n}_{1}}^{\dagger}}{2\mathrm{i}}\cos\theta,\quad\quad s_{\mathbf{n}_{2}}^{y}=\left(b_{\mathbf{n}_{2}}^{\dagger}b_{\mathbf{n}_{2}}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\sin\theta-\frac{b_{\mathbf{n}_{2}}-b_{\mathbf{n}_{2}}^{\dagger}}{2\mathrm{i}}\cos\theta,

\displaystyle s_{\mathbf{n}_{1}}^{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{b_{\mathbf{n}_{1}}+b_{\mathbf{n}_{1}}^{\dagger}}{2}\,,\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\,\,\,\,s_{\mathbf{n}_{2}}^{x}=\frac{b_{\mathbf{n}_{2}}+b_{\mathbf{n}_{2}}^{\dagger}}{2}\,.

(5)

Being inserted in equation (
4
), they generate the terms up to fourth order in
b
-operators but the ground state stability needs the linear terms to vanish (together with
the third order terms) and this requires the deviation angle to be:

\theta=\frac{1}{2}\arctan\frac{2D}{2J-\Delta J_{\textrm{t}}}\ll 1.

(6)

Next, the quartic terms can be neglected for small excitation numbers,
\langle b_{\mathbf{n}_{j}}^{\dagger}b_{\mathbf{n}_{j}}\rangle\ll 1
, when
b_{\mathbf{n}_{j}}
can be seen as Bose
operators (the Holstein-Primakoff ones) and the spin Hamiltonian turns bilinear in their Fourier-transforms
b_{j,\mathbf{k}}=N^{-1/2}\sum_{\mathbf{n}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{k}\mathbf{n}_{j}}b_{\mathbf{n}_{j}}
(summed over
N
unit cells):

\displaystyle H_{s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\left[A\left(b_{1,\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}b_{1,\mathbf{k}}+b_{2,\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}b_{2,\mathbf{k}}\right)+B\gamma_{\mathbf{k}}\left(b_{1,\mathbf{k}}b_{2,-\mathbf{k}}+b_{1,\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}b_{2,-\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\right)+C\gamma_{\mathbf{k}}\left(b_{1,\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}b_{2,\mathbf{k}}+b_{2,\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}b_{1,\mathbf{k}}\right)\right],

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2J\cos 2\theta+2\Delta J_{\textrm{t}}\sin^{2}\theta+2D\sin 2\theta,\quad B=J(1+\cos 2\theta)-\Delta J_{\textrm{t}}\cos^{2}\theta+D\sin 2\theta,

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle J(1-\cos 2\theta)+\Delta J_{\textrm{t}}\cos^{2}\theta-D\sin 2\theta,\quad\gamma_{\mathbf{k}}=\cos\frac{k_{x}}{2}\cos\frac{k_{z}}{2}

(7)

(with wave-vectors
\mathbf{k}
measured in units of inverse lattice parameter
a^{-1}
). We notice that the difference between the big terms
A\approx 2J+D^{2}/J
and

B\approx 2J-\Delta J_{\textrm{t}}+D^{2}/2J
is of the order of the small term
C\approx\Delta J_{\textrm{t}}-D^{2}/2J
.

The Hamiltonian, equation (
7

b_{j,\mathbf{k}}
operators into the
\beta_{\pm,\mathbf{k}}
operators of
eigen-modes (symmetric or antisymmetric in the sublattice indices). This can be compactly expressed in terms of 4-spinors transformed by a 4

b_{\mathbf{k}}=\hat{U}_{\mathbf{k}}\beta_{\mathbf{k}}
, or in their components:

b_{\mathbf{k}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}b_{1,\mathbf{k}}\\
b_{1,-\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\\
b_{2,\mathbf{k}}\\
b_{2,-\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\end{array}\right),\quad\hat{U}_{\mathbf{k}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}\cosh\theta_{+,\mathbf{k}}&\sinh\eta_{+,\mathbf{k}}&\cosh\eta_{-,\mathbf{k}}&-\sinh\eta_{-,\mathbf{k}}\\
\sinh\eta_{+,\mathbf{k}}&\cosh\eta_{+,\mathbf{k}}&-\sinh\eta_{-,\mathbf{k}}&\cosh\eta_{-,\mathbf{k}}\\
\cosh\eta_{+,\mathbf{k}}&\sinh\eta_{+,\mathbf{k}}&-\cosh\eta_{-,\mathbf{k}}&\sinh\eta_{-,\mathbf{k}}\\
\sinh\eta_{+,\mathbf{k}}&\cosh\eta_{+,\mathbf{k}}&\sinh\eta_{-,\mathbf{k}}&-\cosh\eta_{-,\mathbf{k}}\end{array}\right),\quad\beta_{\mathbf{k}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\beta_{+,\mathbf{k}}\\
\beta_{+,-\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\\
\beta_{-,\mathbf{k}}\\
\beta_{-,-\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\end{array}\right).

(8)

Here, the 4

4 matrices can be expressed as direct products of two kinds of Pauli matrices,
\hat{\sigma}_{j}
in spin indices (e.g., between
b_{j,\mathbf{k}}
and

b_{j,\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}
) and
\hat{\tau}_{j}
in sublattice/mode indices (e.g., between

and

\hat{U}_{\mathbf{k}}=\left[\mathrm{e}^{\eta_{+,\mathbf{k}}\hat{\sigma}_{1}}\otimes\left(\hat{\tau}_{1}-\mathrm{i}\hat{\tau}_{2}+\hat{\tau}_{3}+1\right)+\mathrm{e}^{-\eta_{-,\mathbf{k}}\hat{\sigma}_{1}}\otimes\left(\hat{\tau}_{1}+\mathrm{i}\hat{\tau}_{2}+\hat{\tau}_{3}-1\right)\right]/\sqrt{8}\,.

This permits to reduce multiplication of 4
\times
4 matrices to that of 2
\times
2 ones by the rule:
[f(\hat{\sigma}_{j})\otimes g(\hat{\tau}_{k})][f^{\prime}(\hat{\sigma}_{l})\otimes g^{\prime}(\hat{\tau}_{m})]=[f(\hat{\sigma}_{j})f^{\prime}(\hat{\sigma}_{l})]\otimes[g(\hat{\tau}_{k})g^{\prime}(\hat{\tau}_{m})]
, for any functions

,

,

, and

.

Then, with the choice of hyperbolic rotation arguments as:

\eta_{\pm,\mathbf{k}}=\frac{1}{2}\arctanh\frac{B\gamma_{\mathbf{k}}}{A\pm C\gamma_{\mathbf{k}}}\,,

(9)

the diagonal

H_{s}=\sum_{j=\pm}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\omega_{j,\mathbf{k}}\beta_{j,\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\beta_{j,\mathbf{k}}\,,\quad\textrm{with}\quad\omega_{\pm,\mathbf{k}}=\sqrt{\left(A\pm C\gamma_{\mathbf{k}}\right)^{2}-B^{2}\gamma_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}}\,.

(10)

In the most important low-energy range, where
\gamma_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}\approx 1-k^{2}/4
, the dispersion laws are approximated as:

\omega_{j,\mathbf{k}}\approx\sqrt{\omega_{j}^{2}+J^{2}k^{2}}\,,

(11)

with the spectrum gaps
\omega_{-}=2D
and
\omega_{+}=2\sqrt{2J\Delta J_{\textrm{t}}}
. These values just testify that the rigidity of the antisymmetric mode (in-plane rotations generated
mainly by the spin

-components) comes from the in-plane

y
-components) does
from the out-of-plane
\Delta J_{\textrm{t}}
anisotropy. The spin dynamics of this spin system is contained in the GF matrix
\hat{G}_{\mathbf{k}}={\langle\langle}\beta_{\mathbf{k}}|\beta_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}{\rangle\rangle}
. In
particular, DOS of spin excitations is defined as:

\rho(\omega)=\frac{1}{\piup N}\mathop{Im}\nolimits\Tr\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\hat{G}_{\mathbf{k}}\,,

(12)

where the sum over 2D Brillouin zone, as usually, can be substituted by integration according to the rule:

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}f(\mathbf{k})=\frac{1}{4\piup^{2}}\int_{-\piup}^{\piup}\mathrm{d}k_{x}\int_{-\piup}^{\piup}\mathrm{d}k_{z}f(\mathbf{k}).

(13)

For the unperturbed Hamiltonian, equation (
10
), the GF matrix takes the simplest diagonal form,
\hat{G}_{\mathbf{k}}\Rightarrow\hat{g}_{\mathbf{k}}
, with its non-zero elements:

{\langle\langle}\beta_{j,\mathbf{k}}|\beta_{j,\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}{\rangle\rangle}=\frac{1}{\omega-\omega_{j,\mathbf{k}}}\,,\qquad{\langle\langle}\beta_{j,\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}|\beta_{j,\mathbf{k}}{\rangle\rangle}=-\frac{1}{\omega+\omega_{j,\mathbf{k}}}.

Then, using the equation (
11

g_{j}(\omega)=N^{-1}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\frac{1}{\omega-\omega_{j,\mathbf{k}}}\approx-\frac{1}{J}\left(1+\frac{\omega}{2J}\ln\frac{2J}{\omega_{j}-\omega}\right),

(14)

and hence the low-energy approximation for DOS:

\rho(\omega)\approx\frac{\omega}{4J^{2}}\left[\theta(\omega-\omega_{-})+\theta(\omega-\omega_{+})\right].

(15)

\omega_{\pm}
, limiting thermal occupation of excited states over the fully ordered ground state. Eventually,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\langle b_{1,\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}b_{1,\mathbf{k}}\rangle

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{j,\mathbf{k}}\left(\cosh^{2}\eta_{j,\mathbf{k}}\langle\beta_{j,\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\beta_{j,\mathbf{k}}\rangle+\sinh^{2}\eta_{j,\mathbf{k}}\langle\beta_{j,\mathbf{k}}\beta_{j,\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\rangle\right)

\displaystyle\frac{J}{N}\sum_{j,\mathbf{k}}\frac{2n_{\text{B}}\left(\omega_{j,\mathbf{k}}\right)+1}{\omega_{j,\mathbf{k}}}-\frac{1}{2}\,,

involving the Bose occupation function:

The temperature independent part in equation (

and, for the choice of parameters:

3.2
Charge dynamics

Another kind of dynamics results from much faster charge carrier hopping over Cu sites. Aiming to define the Fermi level position from the resulting
spectrum, we start with the model hopping Hamiltonian referred to the atomic Cu
d
-term:

H_{\text{hop}}=-\sum_{\mathbf{k},\sigma}\left[t_{\mathbf{k}}(a_{1,\sigma,\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}a_{2,\sigma,\mathbf{k}}+a_{2,\sigma,\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}a_{1,\sigma,\mathbf{k}})+t^{\prime}_{\mathbf{k}}(a_{1,\sigma,\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}a_{1,\sigma,\mathbf{k}}+a_{2,\sigma,\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}a_{2,\sigma,\mathbf{k}})\right].

(18)

Here,
a_{j,\sigma,\mathbf{k}}=N^{-1/2}\sum_{\mathbf{n}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{k}\mathbf{n}_{j}}a_{\mathbf{n}_{j},\sigma}
are the Fourier transforms of the Fermi operators
a_{\mathbf{n},\sigma}
for an electron with the given spin
value
\sigma
on
\mathbf{n}
-th site. The hopping factors
t_{\mathbf{k}}=4t\cos\tfrac{k_{x}}{2}\cos\tfrac{k_{z}}{2}
(for nearest neighbors) and
t^{\prime}_{\mathbf{k}}=2t^{\prime}(\cos k_{x}+\cos k_{z})
(next nearest
neighbors) involve the hopping parameters
t
and
t^{\prime}
(both taken with negative sign for their indirect realization through the excited O levels
[
29
]
). This model
can be seen as a simplified version of the much more detailed LDA Hamiltonian
[
34
]
.

Actually, the spin operators (in neglect of small deviations
\theta
from
z
-axis, inessential for the charge dynamics) can be also presented in terms of the Fermi
operators as:

s_{\mathbf{n}}^{z}=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(a_{\mathbf{n},\uparrow}^{\dagger}a_{\mathbf{n},\uparrow}-a_{\mathbf{n},\downarrow}^{\dagger}a_{\mathbf{n},\downarrow}\right),\qquad s_{\mathbf{n}}^{+}=a_{\mathbf{n},\uparrow}^{\dagger}a_{\mathbf{n},\downarrow}\,,\qquad s_{\mathbf{n}}^{-}=a_{\mathbf{n},\downarrow}^{\dagger}a_{\mathbf{n},\uparrow}\,.

(19)

Then, the dominant purely exchange part of the spin Hamiltonian, equation (
4
), obtains in these terms a four-fermion structure:

\displaystyle H_{\text{ex}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle J\sum_{\mathbf{n},\boldsymbol{\delta}}\left[\tfrac{1}{4}\left(a_{\mathbf{n}_{1},\uparrow}^{\dagger}a_{\mathbf{n}_{1},\uparrow}-a_{\mathbf{n}_{1},\downarrow}^{\dagger}a_{\mathbf{n}_{1},\downarrow}\right)\left(a_{\mathbf{n}_{1}+\boldsymbol{\delta},\uparrow}^{\dagger}a_{\mathbf{n}_{1}+\boldsymbol{\delta},\uparrow}-a_{\mathbf{n}_{1}+\boldsymbol{\delta},\downarrow}^{\dagger}a_{\mathbf{n}_{1}+\boldsymbol{\delta},\downarrow}\right)\right.

(20)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left.\tfrac{1}{2}\left(a_{\mathbf{n}_{1},\uparrow}^{\dagger}a_{\mathbf{n}_{1},\downarrow}a_{\mathbf{n}_{1}+\boldsymbol{\delta},\downarrow}^{\dagger}a_{\mathbf{n}_{1}+\boldsymbol{\delta},\uparrow}+a_{\mathbf{n}_{1},\downarrow}^{\dagger}a_{\mathbf{n}_{1},\uparrow}a_{\mathbf{n}_{1}+\boldsymbol{\delta},\uparrow}^{\dagger}a_{\mathbf{n}_{1}+\boldsymbol{\delta},\downarrow}\right)\right],

or, passing to the band operators:

\displaystyle H_{\text{ex}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{J}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime},\mathbf{q}}\left[\left(a_{1,\uparrow,\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}a_{1,\uparrow,\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}-a_{1,\downarrow,\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}a_{1,\downarrow,\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\right)\left(a_{2,\uparrow,\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}^{\dagger}a_{2,\uparrow,\mathbf{k}^{\prime}-\mathbf{q}}-a_{2,\downarrow,\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}^{\dagger}a_{2,\downarrow,\mathbf{k}^{\prime}-\mathbf{q}}\right)\right.

(21)

\displaystyle+

This can be also reduced to a bilinear form, like that in equation (

products (and neglecting as usually small fluctuations beside the averages). We note that this known procedure involves here either “normal”,
\langle a^{\dagger}a\rangle

\langle aa\rangle

\langle a^{\dagger}a^{\dagger}\rangle

Particularly, the “normal” averages relate to the local spin values and they are selected from the first, longitudinal part under the sum in equation (
20

\langle a_{\mathbf{n}_{1},\uparrow}^{\dagger}a_{\mathbf{n}_{1},\uparrow}-a_{\mathbf{n}_{1},\downarrow}^{\dagger}a_{\mathbf{n}_{1},\downarrow}\rangle=-\langle a_{\mathbf{n}_{2},\uparrow}^{\dagger}a_{\mathbf{n}_{2},\uparrow}-a_{\mathbf{n}_{2},\downarrow}^{\dagger}a_{\mathbf{n}_{2},\downarrow}\rangle=2s,

(22)

where the

are due to delocalized pairs of electrons with opposite spins and momenta and they follow from both parts in equation (

Now, we compose the effective two-fermion Hamiltonian for normal electrons:
H_{\text{n}}=H_{\text{hop}}+H_{\text{n}}^{(2)}
, involving the “normally” averaged spin exchange part:

H_{\text{n}}^{(2)}=J\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\left(a_{1,\downarrow,\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}a_{1,\downarrow,\mathbf{k}}+a_{2,\uparrow,\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}a_{2,\uparrow,\mathbf{k}}-a_{1,\uparrow,\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}a_{1,\uparrow,\mathbf{k}}-a_{2,\downarrow,\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}a_{2,\downarrow,\mathbf{k}}\right),

(23)

which can be seen as a spin-Hubbard repulsion, favouring an electron with the given spin to occupy the sites from its proper sublattice (1 for
\uparrow
and 2 for
\downarrow

Similarly to the above boson case, we construct fermion 4-spinors in sublattice/spin indices:
a_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}=(a_{1,\uparrow,\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger},a_{2,\uparrow,\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger},a_{2,\downarrow,\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger},a_{1,\downarrow,\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger})

\alpha_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}=(\alpha_{+,\uparrow,\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger},\alpha_{-,\uparrow,\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger},\alpha_{+,\downarrow,\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger},\alpha_{-,\downarrow,\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger})
by a unitary rotation:

a_{\mathbf{k}}=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\lambda_{\mathbf{k}}\hat{\tau}_{2}}\otimes\hat{1}\alpha_{\mathbf{k}}\,,

(24)

(with the direct product of
\hat{\tau}

4
Doping effects

Now, we pass to the effects of doping on both kinds of excitation spectra. First of all, there are two principal perturbation mechanisms for a non-isovalent
dopant atom (located out of the relevant CuO
2
plane) on the insulating host with the AFM spin order:

1) the dopant Coulomb potential, acting on in-plane Cu sites within a certain screening radius
r_{\text{scr}}
, and

2) appearance of a charge carrier (a hole), localized by this potential within a certain localization radius
r_{\text{loc}}
.

Generally, these mechanisms can influence all the parameters in the original Hamiltonians, equations (
4
) and (
18
), producing quasiparticle scattering
of rather complicated structure and considerable technical problems for its analysis.

Nevertheless, a qualitatively reasonable physical picture for such a disordered system is expected to follow already from the simplest one-parametric
Lifshitz perturbation models for each diagonalized Hamiltonian, equations (
10
) or (
25
). Of course, strictly speaking, the Lifshitz model with a single
momentum-independent scattering amplitude should correspond to a purely point-like perturbation potential in the real space, or, in more general, to a
perturbation that affects a single local component of a plane-wave excitation state (that is, its inverse Fourier transform). However, in the present system, the

\alpha_{\mathbf{k}}
and
\beta_{\mathbf{k}}
eigen-modes are composed of the original
a_{\mathbf{k}}
and
b_{\mathbf{k}}
modes, according to equations (
24
) and (
8
), and their
inverse Fourier transforms:

\alpha_{\mathbf{n}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\mathbf{k}\mathbf{n}}\alpha_{\mathbf{k}}=\sum_{\mathbf{n}^{\prime}}\hat{f}^{(a)}(\mathbf{n}-\mathbf{n}^{\prime})a_{\mathbf{n}^{\prime}}\,,\qquad\beta_{\mathbf{n}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\mathbf{k}\mathbf{n}}\beta_{\mathbf{k}}=\sum_{\mathbf{n}^{\prime}}\hat{f}^{(b)}(\mathbf{n}-\mathbf{n}^{\prime})b_{\mathbf{n}^{\prime}}\,,

(33)

are the distributions of the original point-like
a_{\mathbf{n}}
and
b_{\mathbf{n}}
with some kernel matrices
\hat{f}^{(a,b)}(\mathbf{r})
. The latter matrix elements are made of
the inverse Fourier transforms of
\cos\lambda_{\mathbf{k}}
,
\sin\lambda_{\mathbf{k}}
(for
\alpha
-modes) or
\cosh\eta_{\pm,\mathbf{k}}
,
\sinh\eta_{\pm,\mathbf{k}}
(for
\beta
-modes) and they have as
long decay range as
r^{(b)}\sim J/\omega_{j}\gg 1
for boson
\beta
-states but only
r^{(a)}\sim t/J\sim 1
for fermion
\alpha
-states. Therefore, the above mentioned
perturbations of
\sim r_{\text{loc}}
or
\sim r_{\text{scr}}
range can be felt by the broadly distributed boson modes as effectively point-like and that may justify the Lifshitz
model approximation for them. As to the sharper resolved fermion modes, where a more detailed perturbation model should be applied, fortunately it does not
make essential technical problems to obtain an effective Lifshitz approximation also for this case (to be shown later).

Now, beginning from the spin excitations, we supplement equation (
10
) with the Lifshitz perturbation Hamiltonian of the form:

H^{\prime}_{s}=-\frac{V_{s}}{N}\sum_{j,\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime},\mathbf{p}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{k}^{\prime})\mathbf{p}}\beta_{j,\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\beta_{j,\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\,,

(34)

where
\mathbf{p}
are the on-plane projections of random dopant positions (see figure
1
) and the negative sign of the single perturbation parameter
V_{s}

indicates a general effective suppression of spin exchange and anisotropies due to the dopant potentials on Cu and O sites and to the reduction of spin density
because of hole carriers. Then, in the standard general expression for GF matrix
\hat{G}_{\mathbf{k}}=(\hat{g}_{\mathbf{k}}^{-1}-\hat{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{k}})^{-1}
, the simplest approximation
for the self-energy matrix is:
\hat{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{k}}\approx c\hat{T}_{s}
, where the momentum-independent (but energy-dependent) diagonal T-matrix for spin states:

\hat{T}_{s}(\omega)=\left[\frac{1+\hat{\tau}_{3}}{2}T_{+}(\omega)+\frac{1-\hat{\tau}_{3}}{2}T_{-}(\omega)\right]\otimes\hat{1},

(35)

and the subband terms
T_{\pm}(\omega)=-V_{s}/\left[1+V_{s}g_{\pm}(\omega)\right]
involve the locator functions by equation (
14
).

It is well known for disordered systems that zeroes of T-matrix denominators beyond the band spectrum define localized impurity levels in the diluted
limit of doping and those can produce new impurity subbands at higher doping levels. Since each
g_{j}(\omega)
is negative and logarithmically diverging
near the respective subband edge
\omega_{j}
, such levels
\omega_{j,\text{loc}}
are formally produced by both terms in equation (
35
), but of extremely shallow depth:

\omega_{j}-\omega_{j,\text{loc}}\approx 2J\exp\left[-\frac{2J(J-V_{s})}{\omega_{j}V_{s}}\right],

(36)

due to the huge
\sim J/\omega_{j}\gg 1
factor in the negative exponent (except for an improbable closeness of
V_{s}
to
J
within to
\sim\omega_{j}
), so they are
practically stuck to those edges. Moreover, the fast decay of logarithms in
g_{\pm}(\omega)
by equation (
14
) beyond an exponentially narrow vicinity of the edge,

\omega_{j}-\omega\sim J(\omega_{j}-\omega_{j,\text{loc}})/\omega_{j}\ll\omega_{j}
, makes them negligible besides the resting constant and then the only doping effect in the spin spectrum remains
in equal downshifts of the subband edges as:

\omega_{j}(c)=\omega_{j}-\frac{cV_{s}J}{J-V_{s}}.

(37)

This immediately defines the concentrational decay of the Néel temperature,
T_{\text{N}}(c)
, calculated again from equation (
17
) but with the change of
\omega_{j}
for

\omega_{j}(c)

\omega_{\text{min}}(c)=\min_{j}\omega_{j}(c)
reaches zero [and so
T_{\text{N}}(c)
, with no more long-range
AFM order in the system] defines the critical doping value

(38)

At

\omega_{+}\omega_{-}
[e.g., fitted to the known

5
Superconductivity

As it was already referred, the unusual features of SC state in doped perovskite compounds are mainly treated in literature within different scenarios of strongly
correlated electrons in a uniform medium, also incorporating the doping effects into this framework
[
40
]
. Here, we instead consider the narrow
J
-induced gap
and explicit localization by dopant potential as effective factors of such strong correlations. A similar approach was recently used for a description of destructive effects
by magnetic impurities on high-
T_{\text{c}}
SC order
[
41
]
,
[
22
]
but now we focus on its relevance for establishing of such an order. Namely, the possibility for SC
coupling between charge carriers in the considered system is brought by the “anomalously” averaged part of its four-fermion Hamiltonian, equation (
21
). Then, using
the unitary transformation, equation (
24
), the eigen-modes from the relevant majority subband (for brevity, this subband index is omitted in what follows) are extracted
from both longitudinal and transversal terms in equation (
21
), enabling the common formation of Cooper pairs. Thus, we get the anomalous part of the SC Hamiltonian
as:

H_{\text{an}}^{(\text{sc})}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\left(\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}\alpha_{\uparrow,\mathbf{k}}\alpha_{\downarrow,-\mathbf{k}}+\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}^{\ast}\alpha_{\downarrow,\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\alpha_{\uparrow,-\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\right),

(49)

with the gap function:

\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}V_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{(\text{sc})}\langle\alpha_{\uparrow,\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\alpha_{\downarrow,-\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\rangle

(50)

and the SC coupling function:

\quad V_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{(\text{sc})}=J\gamma_{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\left(1+\cos^{2}\lambda_{\mathbf{k}}+\cos^{2}\lambda_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}-2\cos^{2}\lambda_{\mathbf{k}}\cos^{2}\lambda_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\right).

(51)

Then, taking the normal part of this Hamiltonian from equation (
25
) as:

H_{\text{n}}^{(\text{sc})}=\sum_{\sigma,\mathbf{k}}\xi_{\mathbf{k}}\alpha_{\sigma,\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\alpha_{\sigma,\mathbf{k}},

(52)

with the dispersion law
\xi_{\mathbf{k}}=\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}-\varepsilon_{\text{F}}
, the usual diagonal form of the SC Hamiltonian:

H^{(\text{sc})}=H_{\text{n}}^{(\text{sc})}+H_{\text{an}}^{(\text{sc})}=\sum_{\sigma,\mathbf{k}}E_{\mathbf{k}}\tilde{\alpha}_{\sigma,\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\tilde{\alpha}_{\sigma,\mathbf{k}},

(53)

contains the BSC spectrum
E_{\mathbf{k}}=\sqrt{\xi_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}+|\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}|^{2}}
and the Bogolyubov quasiparticle operators:

\tilde{\alpha}_{\sigma,\mathbf{k}}=\cos\phi_{\mathbf{k}}\alpha_{\sigma,\mathbf{k}}+\sin\phi_{\mathbf{k}}\alpha_{-\sigma,-\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger},

with
\phi_{\mathbf{k}}=\arctan E_{\mathbf{k}}/|\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}|
.

As known, the BCS standard routine for common metals expresses the anomalous average in the gap function, equation (
50
), through the spectral theorem, equation (
3
),
where the imaginary part of anomalous GF is:

\frac{1}{\piup}\mathop{Im}\nolimits{\langle\langle}\alpha_{\downarrow,-\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}|\alpha_{\uparrow,\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}{\rangle\rangle}=\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}\delta\left(\varepsilon^{2}-E_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}\right)\tanh\frac{\beta\varepsilon}{2}.

(54)

Then, its integration in
\varepsilon
results in the general gap equation:

\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}=\frac{1}{2N}{\sum_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}}^{\prime}\frac{V_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{(\text{sc})}\Delta_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}}{E_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}}\tanh\frac{\beta E_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}}{2},

(55)

where the summation range in
\sum^{\prime}_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}
is delimited by isoenergetic surfaces of equal energy distance
\omega_{\text{D}}
(the Debye energy) up and down from

\varepsilon_{\text{F}}
(the BCS shell), and the SC coupling function is usually taken in the factorized form,
V_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{(\text{sc})}=V^{(\text{sc})}f_{\mathbf{k}}f_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}
, with the SC coupling
parameter
V^{(\text{sc})}
and the SC symmetry factor
f_{\mathbf{k}}=f(\varphi)\theta(\omega_{\text{D}}^{2}-\xi^{2})
restricted to the BCS shell and admitting some angular dependence.
Then, the gap function is expressed as
\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}=\Delta f_{\mathbf{k}}
where the gap parameter
\Delta
is found from the BCS gap equation:

\frac{1}{\lambda}=\frac{1}{2\piup}\int_{0}^{2\piup}\mathrm{d}\varphi f^{2}(\varphi)\int_{0}^{\omega}\frac{\mathrm{d}\xi}{\sqrt{\xi^{2}+\Delta^{2}f^{2}(\varphi)}}\tanh\frac{\beta\sqrt{\xi^{2}+\Delta^{2}f^{2}(\varphi)}}{2}

(56)

with the dimensionless SC coupling constant
\lambda=V\rho_{\text{F}}
, and this should define all the SC state properties.

However, the considered case of doped and disordered metal differs from the above classical scenario in some essential conditions:

I) the band-like states possess a finite linewidth
\Gamma(\varepsilon)
whose characteristic value near the Fermi energy
\Gamma_{\text{F}}=\Gamma(\varepsilon_{\text{F}})\sim\delta_{c}
[by equation (
47
) at
the
\sim
relation] can be comparable to or even greater than the expected SC spectrum gap
\Delta
,

II) the inner limit for
{\sum}^{\prime}
around the M-point is defined by the mobility edge
\varepsilon_{c}
, since there are no mobile carriers for
\varepsilon_{\sigma,\mathbf{k}}>\varepsilon_{c}
, so the BCS shell
upper width (above
\varepsilon_{\text{F}}
),
w_{u}(c)=\delta_{\text{F}}-\delta_{c}
, only makes sense at
c\geqslant c_{\text{met}}
and then it grows with
c
as shown in figure
7
,

III) the outer
{\sum}^{\prime}
limit is defined by the decay of SC coupling function and it is deduced from equation (
51
) to be near the isoenergetic line of
\sim J
, which is
doping independent, but the BCS shell lower width (below
\varepsilon_{\text{F}}
):
w_{l}(c)=J-\delta_{\text{F}}
, decreases with
c
from a high initial value and vanishes at certain
c_{\text{cr}}
.

The condition I) modifies equation (
54
) by changing there the delta-function for a Lorentzian:

\frac{2\varepsilon\Gamma_{\text{F}}}{\left(\varepsilon^{2}-E_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}-G_{\text{F}}^{2}\right)^{2}+4\varepsilon^{2}\Gamma_{\text{F}}^{2}}\,,

and, after the spectral theorem integration in
\varepsilon
(at
T=0

55

1-\frac{1}{\piup}\arctan\frac{2E_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\Gamma_{\text{F}}}{E_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{2}-\Gamma_{\text{F}}^{2}},

besides
E_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{-1}
, which notably differs from unity at
E_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\sim\Gamma_{\text{F}}

6
Discussion and conclusions

The above model consideration does not pretend to readily give a complete description of all the details in the multifaceted process of SC pairing in real doped cuprate
perovskites (which are known as “bad metals”
[
8
]
) and its transformations with the external parameters such as temperature, magnetic field, current density, etc. Nevertheless,
this first model approximation demonstrates that some basic features of such a process can be really determined by the specific mechanisms introduced here:

- the spin-exchange splitting of the initial spin-independent band structure into the subbands of spin-majority and spin-minority carriers, as valence and conductance bands
for a narrow gap insulating state of the undoped system;

- the fundamental role of the localized impurity level produced within this bandgap by the dopant potential to give rise to a continuous impurity subband with a growing doping;

- a strong hybridization between the spin-majority and impurity subbands that produces a significant quasiparticle weight transfer from impurity to valence subband and hence
a transfer of the Fermi level to within the spin-majority subband, near its top;

- the essential disorder effect on the states near this Fermi level, defining the necessary condition for metallization of the initially insulating system and an anomalous closeness
of this level to the Mott mobility edge delimiting the quasiparticle weight of conductive states;

- the strong dependence of the size and shape of the Fermi surface in the Brillouin zone on the doping level above the metallization threshold, with a tendency to be transformed
from separate Fermi arcs to a closed Fermi line;

- the specific limitation of the ranges for SC pairing on carrier states above and below the Fermi level (an analog to the classical BCS shell), whose width is determined by the
mobility edge in the normal spectrum combined with the common decay range of the SC coupling function;

- the
d
-wave symmetry of the resulting SC order parameter directly related to the local symmetry of two AFM sublattices, its non-monotonous dependence on the doping level,
and an anomalously low superfluid density of the SC condensate.

Other physical properties of the proposed model, including its behavior at finite temperatures, will constitute the topic of further studies.
