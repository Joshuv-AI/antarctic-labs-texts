Border rank of powers of ternary quadratic forms
Cosimo Flavi

Abstract
. We determine the border rank of each power of any quadratic form in three variables. Since the problem for rank 1 1 and rank 2 2 quadratic forms can be reduced to determining the rank of powers of binary forms, we primarily focus on non-degenerate quadratic forms. We begin by considering the quadratic form q n = x 1 2 + ⋯ + x n 2 q_{n}=x_{1}^{2}+\dots+x_{n}^{2} in an arbitrary number n n of variables. We determine the apolar ideal of any power q n s q_{n}^{s} , proving that it corresponds to the homogeneous ideal generated by the harmonic polynomials of degree s + 1 s+1 . Using this result, we select a specific ideal contained in the apolar ideal for each power of a quadratic form in three variables, which, without loss of generality, we assume to be the form q 3 q_{3} . After verifying certain properties, we utilize the recent technique of border apolarity to establish that the border rank of any power q 3 s q_{3}^{s} is equal to the rank of its middle catalecticant matrix, namely ( s + 1 ) ​ ( s + 2 ) / 2 (s+1)(s+2)/2 .

Introduction

The problem of determining the minimum integer
r
such that a homogeneous polynomial of degree
d\in\mathbb{N}
can be expressed as a sum of
r
different
d
-th powers of linear forms, also known as
Waring rank
, is a classical problem that remains relevant in many applications today. Formally, a homogeneous polynomial can be seen as a symmetric tensor, and the Waring rank corresponds to its symmetric rank. This fact clearly represents a way to approach the classical problem of determining Waring rank of polynomials, referred to as the
Big Waring Problem
. For an overview of tensor rank, border rank, tensor decomposition, and its applications, we refer to
[
1
]
and
[
17
]
. Instead, to have a survey on some algorithms or methods that can be used to determine the symmetric rank of a symmetric tensor, one can consult
[
2
]
or
[
16
]
.

In this paper, our focus is on the powers of the quadratic form

q_{n}=x_{1}^{2}+\dots+x_{n}^{2}.

Determining the rank of this form is also a classical problem that is related to the Laplace operator. Decompositions of this form as sums of powers of linear polynomials have appeared in various classical papers and books, even dating back to the 19
th
century (see, for example,
[
14
]
Questions 38-39, p. 129). Many considerations and examples are presented in
[
19
]
by B. Reznick, who specifically focuses on real decompositions, also known as
representations
. The problem of determining the rank for the case of three variables is still open (with the exception of some specific cases), but in this paper we concentrate on its border rank. To do this, we require an understanding of classical apolarity theory and a relatively recent concept introduced by W. Buczyńska and J. Buczyński in
[
3
]
, called
border apolarity
. Their results provide a method to study the border rank of a tensor in a similar manner to the classic
apolarity lemma
(see, for example,
[
15
]
*Lemma 1.15).

We begin by recalling some essential elements of apolarity theory in
section 1
, including apolarity action, catalecticant map and apolar ideal. For detailed information, we refer the readers to
[
15
]
. Then,
section 2
is focused on the space of harmonic polynomials, which has a special role for the decompositions we are dealing with. In fact, we will prove in
section 3

(refer to
section 3
) that the apolar ideal of the form
q_{n}^{s}
corresponds exactly to the ideal generated by harmonic polynomials of degree
s+1
.
Understanding the apolar ideal is essential to establish the minimality of the border rank of the form
q_{3}^{s}
. It is a well-known fact that the rank and border rank of a symmetric tensor are always greater or equal to the rank of its most-squared catalecticant matrix. B. Reznick has already demonstrated in
[
19
]
that the rank of each catalecticant matrix of
q_{n}^{s}
is maximum, leading to the following inequalities:

(0.1)

\rk\bigl(q_{n}^{s}\bigr)\geq\brk\bigl(q_{n}^{s}\bigr)\geq\binom{s+n-1}{n-1}.

Using border apolarity, we prove that the second inequality in formula (
0.1
) is an equality for the case of three variables. Specifically, we determine the border rank of every power of any ternary quadratic form, including degenerate forms. In particular, given a degenerate quadratic form
g\in S^{2}\mathbb{C}^{3}
, if
\rk g=1
, then
\rk\bigl(g^{s}\bigr)=1
as well. On the other hand, if
\rk g=2
, we employ binary forms to establish that

\rk\bigl(g^{s}\bigr)=\brk\bigl(g^{s}\bigr)=s+1.

We summarize it in the following theorem.

Theorem
.

Let
g\in S^{2}\mathbb{C}^{3}
be an arbitrary ternary quadratic form. For every
s\in\mathbb{N}
, if
g
is degenerate, then

\brk\bigl(g^{s}\bigr)=\rk\bigl(g^{s}\bigr)=\binom{s+\rk g-1}{\rk g-1}.

Otherwise,

\brk\bigl(g^{s}\bigr)=\binom{s+2}{2}=\frac{(s+1)(s+2)}{2}.

The strategy we employ to prove this theorem, detailed in

]
).

To continue our analysis of the form

We observe that the kernel of a homogeneous differential operator is related to the contraction pairing, as it can be seen as a specific orthogonal complement of the space

Proposition 2.0

For any

g\circ(\phi\psi)=(\phi\psi)\circ g=0,

which implies

\psi\circ(\phi\circ g)=0.

This means that

\Der_{\phi}(g)=\phi\circ g=0,

and hence

The apolar ideal of

Now we will prove that the apolar ideal of

Now, considering the action of

Proposition 3.0

Let

\displaystyle g\circ q_{n}^{s}

\displaystyle=q_{n}^{s-m+t}\Bigg(h_{t}+q_{n}p_{t}+\sum_{j=t+1}^{\left\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\right\rfloor}C_{j}q_{n}^{j-t}h_{j}+q_{n}^{j-k+1}p_{j}\Bigg)

The stabilizer of the form
q_{n}^{s}
has a quite simple structure and, in particular, it contains the special orthogonal group
\mathrm{O}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
.

Lemma 3.0
.

For every
n,s\in\mathbb{N}
the stabilizer of the form
q_{n}^{s}
with respect to the action of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
is the group

G_{q_{n}^{s}}=\mathbb{Z}_{s}\times\mathrm{O}_{n}(\mathbb{C}).

Proof.

Given any
A\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, we have

A\cdot q_{n}^{s}=A\cdot\bigl(x_{1}^{2}+\cdots+x_{n}^{2}\bigr)^{s}=\bigl((A\cdot x_{1})^{2}+\dots+(A\cdot x_{n})^{2}\bigr)^{s}=(A\cdot q_{n})^{s}

and hence

A\cdot q_{n}^{s}=(A\cdot q_{n})^{s}=q_{n}^{s},

that is,

\bigl((A\cdot x_{1})^{2}+\dots+(A\cdot x_{n})^{2}\bigr)^{s}=\bigl(x_{1}^{2}+\cdots+x_{n}^{2}\bigr)^{s}.

This means, considering the
(2s)
-th roots of unity, that

A\cdot q_{n}=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{2(j-1)\uppi\mathrm{i}}{s}}q_{n}

for some
j\in\mathbb{N}
such that
1\leq j\leq s
and this implies that

\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{2(j-1)\uppi\mathrm{i}}{s}}A\cdot q_{n}=q_{n}.

In particular, since the stabilizer of the form
q_{n}
corresponds to the orthogonal group
\mathrm{O}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, we have

A=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{2(j-1)\uppi\mathrm{i}}{s}}B

for some
B\in\mathrm{O}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, which proves the statement.
∎

Before proving that the set of
(s+1)
-harmonic polynomials generates the apolar ideal, we need to prove another lemma. Let us define the linear forms
u,v\in R_{1}
as

u=y_{1}+\mathrm{i}y_{2},\qquad v=y_{1}-\mathrm{i}y_{2}.

Lemma 3.0
.

For every
d\in\mathbb{N}
, the polynomials
u^{d},v^{d}\in\mathcal{D}_{d}
are harmonic. Moreover, if
d\geq s+1
, then
u^{d},v^{d}\in\big(q_{n}^{s}\big)^{\perp}
.

Proof.

We prove the statement only for the linear polynomial
u
, since the case of
v
is analogous. Let us suppose
d\geq 2
. Then we have

\Delta u^{d}=\frac{\mathnormal{\partial}^{\mskip 1.0mu2}}{\mathnormal{\partial}y_{1}^{2}}(y_{1}+\mathrm{i}y_{2})^{d}+\frac{\mathnormal{\partial}^{\mskip 1.0mu2}}{\mathnormal{\partial}y_{2}^{2}}(y_{1}+\mathrm{i}y_{2})^{d}=d(d-1)(y_{1}+\mathrm{i}y_{2})^{d-2}\big(1+\mathrm{i}^{2}\big)=0.

We now observe that

(y_{1}+\mathrm{i}y_{2})\circ(x_{1}+\mathrm{i}x_{2})=1+\mathrm{i}^{2}=0,

and

u\circ q_{n}^{s}=(y_{1}+\mathrm{i}y_{2})\circ\big(x_{1}^{2}+\dots+x_{n}^{2}\big)^{s}=2s(x_{1}+\mathrm{i}x_{2})\big(x_{1}^{2}+\dots+x_{n}^{2}\big)^{s-1}.

Then, if
d\geq s+1
, we obtain by Leibniz’s rule

u^{d}\circ q_{n}^{s}=(y_{1}+\mathrm{i}y_{2})^{s+1}\circ\big(x_{1}^{2}+\dots+x_{n}^{2}\big)^{s}=2^{s}s!(y_{1}+\mathrm{i}y_{2})^{d-s}\circ(x_{1}+\mathrm{i}x_{2})^{s}=0,

and hence
u^{d}\in\big(q_{n}^{s}\big)^{\perp}
.
∎

Remark 3.0
.

It is clear that the product of two powers of the polynomials
u
and
v
cannot be harmonic. Indeed, given any
l,m\in\mathbb{N}
such that
l,m\geq 1
, we have

u^{l}v^{m}=\big(y_{1}^{2}+y_{2}^{2}\big)(y_{1}+\mathrm{i}y_{2})^{l-1}(y_{1}-\mathrm{i}y_{2})^{m-1},

which means that
\big(y_{1}^{2}+y_{2}^{2}\big)\mathrel{\bigm|}u^{l}v^{m}
.

We can finally analyze each component of the catalecticant map to determine its kernel and reveal the structure of the apolar ideal of
q_{n}^{s}
.

Proposition 3.0
.

For every
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
1\leq k\leq s
, let

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Cat_{q_{n}^{s}}^{s+k}\colon\mathcal{D}_{s+k}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{R}_{s-k}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\hphantom{\Cat_{q_{n}^{s}}^{s+k}\colon{}}}\phi}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phi\circ q_{n}^{s}}
\leftarrow
\rightarrow
\mapsfrom
\rightarrow

be the component of degree
s+k
of the catalecticant map of
q_{n}^{s}
. Then,

(3.1)

\big(q_{n}^{s}\big)_{s+k}^{\perp}=\Ker\left(\Cat_{q_{n}^{s}}^{s+k}\right)=\bigoplus_{j=0}^{k-1}q_{n}^{j}\mathcal{H}_{n}^{s+k-2j}.

Proof.

If
1\leq k\leq s
, then by
section 2
we have

\mathcal{D}_{s+k}=\bigg(\bigoplus_{j=0}^{k-1}q_{n}^{j}\mathcal{H}_{n}^{s+k-2j}\bigg)\oplus q_{n}^{k}\mathcal{H}_{n}^{s-k}.

To prove the statement it is sufficient to verify that

q_{n}^{k}\mathcal{D}_{s-k}\cap\Ker\Bigl(\Cat_{q_{n}^{s}}^{s+k}\Bigr)=\{0\}

and

\bigoplus_{j=0}^{k-1}q_{n}^{j}\mathcal{H}_{n}^{s+k-2j}\subseteq\Ker\left(\Cat_{q_{n}^{s}}^{s+k}\right).

For the first assertion, let us consider a polynomial
h\in\mathcal{D}_{s-k}
. Then, as we saw in the proof of
section 3
, we have

\big(q_{n}^{k}h\big)\circ q_{n}^{s}=h\circ\big(\Delta^{k}q_{n}^{s}\big)=C_{k}h\circ q_{n}^{s-k},

where

C_{k}=2^{k}\frac{s!}{(s-k)!}\biggl(\prod_{l=1}^{k}\bigl(n+2(s-l)\bigr)\biggr).

In particular, by
section 3
, we have
h\circ q_{n}^{s-k}=0
if and only if

h\in\big(q_{n}^{s-k}\big)_{s-k}^{\perp},

which implies
h=0
.

Before proving the second assertion, we recall that

\mathcal{H}_{n}^{d}
is an irreducible
\mathrm{SO}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
-module for every
d\in\mathbb{N}
. Moreover, for every
j\in\mathbb{N}
such that
j\leq k-1
, since by
section 3
we have

\mathrm{SO}_{n}(\mathbb{C})\subseteq G_{q_{n}^{j}},

also the space

q_{n}^{j}\mathcal{H}_{n}^{s+k-2j}

is an irreducible
\mathrm{SO}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
-module.
Now, by
section 3
, the catalecticant map

\Cat_{q_{n}^{s}}^{s+k}\colon\mathcal{D}_{s+k}\to\mathcal{R}_{s-k}

is
\mathrm{SO}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
-equivariant. Hence, by Schur’s Lemma (see e.g.
[
9
]
*Lemma 1.7), it follows that the restriction

\Cat^{s+k}_{q_{n}^{s}}\big|_{q_{n}^{j}\mathcal{H}_{n}^{s+k-2j}}\colon q_{n}^{j}\mathcal{H}_{n}^{s+k-2j}\to\mathcal{R}_{s-k}

must be the zero function or an isomorphism on the image. So, if we take the form

u=y_{1}+\mathrm{i}y_{2}\in\mathcal{D}_{1},

then, using
section 3
, as

q_{n}^{j}u^{s+k-2j}\in q_{n}^{j}\mathcal{H}_{n}^{s+k-2j},

we obtain the equality

\bigl(q_{n}^{j}u^{s+k-2j}\bigr)\circ q_{n}^{s}=C_{j}u^{s+k-2j}\circ q_{n}^{s-j}=u^{k-j+1}\circ\bigl(u^{s-j+1}\circ q_{n}^{s-j}\bigr)=0,

which implies that

\Cat^{s+k}_{q_{n}^{s}}\big|_{q_{n}^{j}\mathcal{H}_{n}^{s+k-2j}}\equiv 0.

Therefore, the inclusion is proved.
∎

We finally have all the necessary elements to describe the apolar ideal
\big(q_{n}^{s}\big)^{\perp}
, which is generated by harmonic polynomials of degree
s+1
.

Theorem 3.1
.

The apolar ideal of the form
q_{n}^{s}
is given by

\big(q_{n}^{s}\big)^{\perp}=\bigl(\mathcal{H}_{n}^{s+1}\bigr).

Proof.

By
section 3
, we have observed that
\big(q_{n}^{s}\big)^{\perp}_{k}=0
for every
1\leq k\leq s
. Therefore, to prove the statement, we simply need to show that

\big(q_{n}^{s}\big)_{d}^{\perp}=\mathcal{H}_{n}^{s+1}\mathcal{D}_{d-s-1}

for every
d\geq s+1
. Now, let
d=s+k
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, by
section 3
, if
1\leq k\leq s+1
, this is equivalent to proving that

(3.2)

\bigoplus_{j=0}^{k-1}q_{n}^{j}\mathcal{H}_{n}^{s+k-2j}=\mathcal{H}_{n}^{s+1}\mathcal{D}_{k-1}.

Gain by
section 3
, we also have

\big(q_{n}^{s}\big)_{s+1}^{\perp}=\mathcal{H}_{n}^{s+1},

and hence we immediately obtain the inclusion

(3.3)

\bigoplus_{j=0}^{k-1}q_{n}^{j}\mathcal{H}_{n}^{s+k-2j}\supseteq\mathcal{H}_{n}^{s+1}\mathcal{D}_{k-1}.

The reverse inclusion can be obtained by induction on
k
, separately for odd and even values. If
k=1
, the equality is obvious and we have already seen it. If
k=2
, we have to show that

\mathcal{H}_{n}^{s+2}\oplus q_{n}\mathcal{H}_{n}^{s}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{n}^{s+1}\mathcal{D}_{1}.

We consider the polynomials

u=y_{1}+\mathrm{i}y_{2},\qquad v=y_{1}-\mathrm{i}y_{2},

and, as shown in
section 3
, we can write the polynomial
u^{s+2}\in\mathcal{H}_{n}^{s+2}
as

u^{s+1}u\in\mathcal{H}_{n}^{s+1}\mathcal{D}_{1},

thus proving, since
\mathcal{H}_{n}^{s+2}
is an irreducible
\mathrm{SO}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
-module, that

\mathcal{H}_{n}^{s+2}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{n}^{s+1}\mathcal{D}_{1}.

Now, by
section 3
, we also have that
u^{s+1}v\notin\mathcal{H}_{n}^{s+2}
, and in particular, using inclusion (
3.3
), we have

u^{s+1}v\in\mathcal{H}_{n}^{s+1}\mathcal{D}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{n}^{s+2}\oplus q_{n}\mathcal{H}_{n}^{s}.

This means that there exist unique forms
h_{1}\in\mathcal{H}_{n}^{s+2}
and
h_{2}\in\mathcal{H}_{n}^{s}
, with
h_{2}\neq 0
such that

u^{s+1}v=h_{1}+q_{n}h_{2}\in\mathcal{H}_{n}^{s+2}\oplus q_{n}\mathcal{H}_{n}^{s}

and in particular,

q_{n}h_{2}=u^{s+1}v-h_{1}\in\mathcal{H}_{n}^{s+1}\mathcal{D}_{1}.

Hence, since
q_{n}\mathcal{H}_{n}^{s}
is an irreducible
\mathrm{SO}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
-module, we have

q_{n}\mathcal{H}_{n}^{s}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{n}^{s+1}\mathcal{D}_{1},

providing the required inclusion. Now, for
3\leq k\leq s
, assuming the equality is true for
k-2
, we need to show that

\bigoplus_{j=0}^{k-1}q_{n}^{j}\mathcal{H}_{n}^{s+k-2j}=\mathcal{H}_{n}^{s+k}\oplus q_{n}\left(\bigoplus_{j=0}^{k-2}q_{n}^{j-1}\mathcal{H}_{n}^{s+k-2-2j}\right)\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{n}^{s+1}\mathcal{D}_{k-1}.

As previously mentioned, we can consider the polynomial

u^{s+k}\in\mathcal{H}_{s+k}
, which can be written as

u^{s+1}u^{k-1}\in\mathcal{H}_{n}^{s+1}\mathcal{D}_{k-1},

and conclude, once again by irreducibility, that

\mathcal{H}_{n}^{s+k}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{n}^{s+1}\mathcal{D}_{k-1}.

Therefore, since by inductive hypothesis

\bigoplus_{j=0}^{k-2}q_{n}^{j-1}\mathcal{H}_{n}^{s+k-2-2j}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{n}^{s+1}\mathcal{D}_{k-3},

we have, as
q_{n}\in\mathcal{D}_{2}
,

q_{n}\bigg(\bigoplus_{j=0}^{k-2}q_{n}^{j-1}\mathcal{H}_{n}^{s+k-2-2j}\bigg)\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{n}^{s+1}\mathcal{D}_{k-1},

which implies the inclusion

\bigoplus_{j=0}^{k-1}q_{n}^{j}\mathcal{H}_{n}^{s+k-2j}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{n}^{s+1}\mathcal{D}_{k-1},

thus proving equality (
3.2
).
It remains to show that for every
d\geq 2(s+1)
,

(3.4)

\big(q_{n}^{s}\big)^{\perp}_{d}=\mathcal{H}_{n}^{s+1}\mathcal{D}_{d-s-1}

and, since by definition we have

\big(q_{n}^{s}\big)^{\perp}_{d}=\mathcal{D}_{d}

for every
d\geq 2(s+1)
, we simply have to prove that

\mathcal{D}_{2s+m+1}=\mathcal{H}_{n}^{s+1}\mathcal{D}_{s+m}

for every
m\geq 1
. We have just seen, by formulas (
3.1
) and (
3.2
), that

\big(q_{n}^{s}\big)^{\perp}_{2s+1}=\mathcal{H}_{n}^{s+1}\mathcal{D}_{s}=\bigoplus_{j=0}^{s}q_{n}^{j}\mathcal{H}_{n}^{2s-2j+1}.

This implies, by decomposition of
section 2
, that

\mathcal{H}_{n}^{s+1}\mathcal{D}_{s}=\mathcal{D}_{2s+1},

from which we easily get

\mathcal{D}_{2s+m+1}=\mathcal{D}_{2s+1}\mathcal{D}_{m}=\mathcal{H}_{n}^{s+1}\mathcal{D}_{s}\mathcal{D}_{m}=\mathcal{H}_{n}^{s+1}\mathcal{D}_{s+m}

for every
m\geq 1
, that corresponds to equality (
3.4
).
∎

By
section 3
, it is quite easy to obtain a lower bound for the rank of the form
q_{n}^{s}
. Indeed, as a direct consequence of
section 1
, we have the following corollary.

Corollary 3.4
.

For every
n
,
s\in\mathbb{N}

\rk\left(q_{n}^{s}\right)\geq\brk\left(q_{n}^{s}\right)\geq\binom{s+n-1}{n-1}.

Border rank in three variables

The irreducibility of the space of harmonic polynomials as
\mathrm{SO}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
-modules allows us to determine a canonical basis for the case of three variables in each component. Considering the Lie algebra of the Lie group
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
, given by the set

\mathfrak{sl}_{2}\mathbb{C}=\Set{A\in\mathrm{Mat}_{2}(\mathbb{C})}{\tr A=0},

we know that its only irreducible representations correspond to the spaces of even symmetric powers of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
(see
[
9
]
*Section 11.1 for details). That is,

S^{2d}\mathbb{C}^{2}\simeq\mathbb{C}[x,y]_{2d}

for each
d\in\mathbb{N}
.
In particular, we can consider a natural basis
\mathcal{A}_{2}=\{H,E,F\}
of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}\mathbb{C}
, where

H=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix},\quad E=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad F=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},

satisfying the relations

(4.1)

[H,E]=2E,\quad[H,F]=-2F,\quad[E,F]=H.

This basis allows the set of monomials

\mathcal{B}_{d}={\big\{x^{2d-j}y^{j}\big\}}_{j=0,\dots,2d}

to represent a basis of eigenvectors of the action of
H
on
S^{2d}\mathbb{C}^{2}
, which is therefore diagonalizable.
Moreover, we have that

E\bigl(x^{j}y^{2d-j}\bigr)\in\bigl\langle x^{j+1}y^{2d-j-1}\bigr\rangle,\qquad F\bigl(x^{2d-j}y^{j}\bigr)\in\bigl\langle x^{2d-j-1}y^{j+1}\bigr\rangle,

for every
j=0,\dots,2d-1
.

Now, the reason why we have introduced the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}\mathbb{C}
is connected to a well-known consideration regarding representations of
\mathrm{SO}_{3}(\mathbb{C})
(see
[
9
]
*Section 18.2 for further details). Let us consider the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{so}_{3}\mathbb{C}
of the special orthogonal group
\mathrm{SO}_{3}(\mathbb{C})
, defined as

\mathfrak{so}_{3}\mathbb{C}=\Set{A\in\mathrm{Mat}_{3}(\mathbb{C})}{A=-\mathchoice{\hphantom{{}^{{{\mathrm{t}}}}_{{\mathchoice{\makebox[3.12502pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[3.12502pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[1.90555pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.36111pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}}}}A^{{\kern-8.03058pt{\mathrm{t}}\kern 6.01392pt}}_{{\kern-12.2228pt\mathchoice{\makebox[3.12502pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[3.12502pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[1.90555pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.36111pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}\kern 6.01392pt}}}{\hphantom{{}^{{{\mathrm{t}}}}_{{\mathchoice{\makebox[3.12502pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[3.12502pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[1.90555pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.36111pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}}}}A^{{\kern-8.03058pt{\mathrm{t}}\kern 6.01392pt}}_{{\kern-12.2228pt\mathchoice{\makebox[3.12502pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[3.12502pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[1.90555pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.36111pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}\kern 6.01392pt}}}{\hphantom{{}^{{{\mathrm{t}}}}_{{\mathchoice{\makebox[3.12502pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[3.12502pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[1.90555pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.36111pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}}}}A^{{\kern-5.14723pt{\mathrm{t}}\kern 3.675pt}}_{{\kern-8.14168pt\mathchoice{\makebox[3.12502pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[3.12502pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[1.90555pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.36111pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}\kern 3.675pt}}}{\hphantom{{}^{{{\mathrm{t}}}}_{{\mathchoice{\makebox[3.12502pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[3.12502pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[1.90555pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.36111pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}}}}A^{{\kern-4.09723pt{\mathrm{t}}\kern 2.625pt}}_{{\kern-7.09167pt\mathchoice{\makebox[3.12502pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[3.12502pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[1.90555pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.36111pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}\kern 2.625pt}}}}.

Since
\mathcal{H}_{3}^{d}
is an irreducible
\mathrm{SO}_{3}(\mathbb{C})
-module, then it is also an irreducible representation of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{so}_{3}\mathbb{C}
(see e.g.
[
9
]
*Exercise 8.17).
So, if we consider the basis
\mathcal{A}_{3}=\{H,E,F\}
of
\mathfrak{so}_{3}\mathbb{C}
, where

H=\begin{pmatrix}0&-2\mathrm{i}&0\\
2\mathrm{i}&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad E=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&-1\\
0&0&-\mathrm{i}\\
1&\mathrm{i}&0\end{pmatrix},\quad F=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\\
0&0&-\mathrm{i}\\
-1&\mathrm{i}&0\end{pmatrix},

we observe that relations (
4.1
) are satisfied. Thus, it means not only that

\mathfrak{so}_{3}\mathbb{C}\simeq\mathfrak{sl}_{2}\mathbb{C},

but also, by the uniqueness of irreducible representations of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}\mathbb{C}
, that

\mathcal{H}_{3}^{d}\simeq S^{2d}\mathbb{C}^{2}.

Furthermore, by using the linear change of variables in the space
\mathcal{D}=\mathbb{C}[y_{1},y_{2},y_{3}]
, defined by

u=\frac{y_{1}+\mathrm{i}y_{2}}{2},\quad v=\frac{y_{1}-\mathrm{i}y_{2}}{2},\quad z=y_{3},

we can express the elements of
\mathcal{A}_{3}
as

H=\begin{pmatrix}2&0&0\\
0&-2&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad E=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&-2\\
0&0&0\\
0&1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad F=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\
0&0&2\\
-1&0&0\end{pmatrix}.

Thus, we can naturally choose a basis that corresponds to the set of monomials
\mathcal{B}_{d}
, identifying it
as a set of harmonic polynomials, given by

\mathcal{B}_{d}=\left\{p_{d,j}\right\}_{-d\leq j\leq d},

where, for every
k\geq 0
,

p_{d,k}=\sum_{j=0}^{\left\lfloor\frac{d-k}{2}\right\rfloor}(-1)^{j}u^{[k+j]}z^{[d-k-2j]}v^{[j]}

and

p_{d,-k}=\sum_{j=0}^{\left\lfloor\frac{d-k}{2}\right\rfloor}(-1)^{j}u^{[j]}z^{[d-k-2j]}v^{[k+j]},

using the notation of the
divided powers
, where we define

u^{[m]}=\frac{1}{m!}u^{m},\quad v^{[m]}=\frac{1}{m!}v^{m},\quad z^{[m]}=\frac{1}{m!}z^{m},

for every
m\in\mathbb{N}
.

[

]
*Theorem 9.5)
.

The minimal real decompositions of
q_{2}^{s}
are given by

q_{2}^{s}=\sum_{j=1}^{s+1}(a_{j}x_{1}+b_{j}x_{2})^{2s},

where the
2s+2
points in the set

\bigl\{\pm(a_{j},b_{j})\bigr\}_{j=1,\dots,s+1}

are the vertices of a regular
(2s+2)
-gon inscribed in a circle of radius
2r(s)
, where

r(s)=(s+1)^{-\frac{1}{2s}}\binom{2s}{s}^{-\frac{1}{2s}}.

section 4
shows that the minimal real decompositions of
q_{2}^{s}
are essentially unique up to an orthogonal transformation. In particular, we can write

q_{2}^{s}=\sum_{j=1}^{s+1}\big(2r(s)\cos(\tau_{j})x_{1}+2r(s)\sin(\tau_{j})x_{2}\big)^{2s}=\sum_{j=1}^{s+1}\big(r(s)e^{\mathrm{i}\tau_{j}}u+r(s)e^{-\mathrm{i}\tau_{j}}v\big)^{2s},

where

\tau_{j}=\frac{(j-1)\uppi}{s+1}

for every
j=1,\dots,s+1
.
By apolarity, we can easily extend this result and obtain the uniqueness of decompositions even in the complex case. Indeed, by
section 3
and
section 2
, we know that

\bigl(q_{2}^{s}\bigr)^{\perp}=\bigl(u^{s+1},v^{s+1}\bigr).

Hence, by the classical apolarity lemma (
[
15
]
*Lemma 1.15), every decomposition of
q_{2}^{s}
corresponds to a polynomial with distinct roots in its apolar ideal. Therefore, each linear combination of
u^{s+1}
and
v^{s+1}
, up to scalars, can be written as

f_{s+1}=u^{s+1}-e^{i(\theta+\mathrm{i}k)}v^{s+1},

for some
\theta\in[0,2\uppi)
and
k\in\mathbb{R}
(with the only exception of
u^{s+1}
and
v^{s+1}
). The roots of these polynomials are given by the projective points

[u_{j}:v_{j}]=\bigl[e^{\mathrm{i}w_{k,\theta,j}}:e^{-\mathrm{i}w_{k,\theta,j}}\bigr],\quad j=1,\dots,s+1

where

w_{k,\theta,j}=\frac{2(j-1)\uppi+\theta+\mathrm{i}k}{2(s+1)}

for every
j=1,\dots,s+1
. By solving a linear system, we obtain

q_{2}^{s}=\sum_{j=1}^{s+1}\big(2r(s)\cos(w_{k,\theta,j})x_{1}+2r(s)\sin(w_{k,\theta,j})x_{2}\big)^{2s}=\sum_{j=1}^{s+1}\big(r(s)e^{\mathrm{i}w_{k,\theta,j}}u+r(s)e^{-\mathrm{i}w_{k,\theta,j}}v\big)^{2s}.

Thus, we have established the following theorem.

Theorem 4.8
.

The minimal decompositions of

r(s)=(s+1)^{-\frac{1}{2s}}\binom{2s}{s}^{-\frac{1}{2s}}.

and

\tau_{j}=\frac{(j-1)\uppi}{s+1}

for every
j=1,\dots,s+1

We now have all the tools to determine the border rank of powers of every quadratic form in three variables. We aim to prove the following theorem.

Theorem 4.8
.

Let
g\in S^{2}\mathbb{C}^{3}
be an arbitrary ternary quadratic form. For every
s\in\mathbb{N}
, if
g
is degenerate, then

\brk\bigl(g^{s}\bigr)=\rk\bigl(g^{s}\bigr)=\binom{s+\rk g-1}{\rk g-1}.

Otherwise,

\brk\bigl(g^{s}\bigr)=\binom{s+2}{2}=\frac{(s+1)(s+2)}{2}.

In the case where a quadratic form is degenerate, it means that, according to Sylvester’s law, it can be expressed, after a linear change of coordinates, as a quadratic form in two variables. Therefore, in this case, being degenerate is equivalent to not being concise.
As mentioned earlier, in order to prove
section 4
, we employ border apolarity. However, to establish this, we first need to determine an ideal that belongs to
\mathrm{Slip_{r,n}}
.

Next, we recall the classical notion of the
Hilbert scheme
parametrizing
r
points of the projective space
\mathbb{P}^{n}
. This concept was initially developed by A. Grothendieck (see
[
12
]
). Essentially, it is a scheme that parametrizes all the closed subschemes of
\mathbb{P}^{n}
of length
r
.

It is important to note the distinction from the multigraded Hilbert scheme
\mathrm{Hilb}^{\scriptscriptstyle{h_{r,n}}}_{{\scriptscriptstyle S[\mathbb{P}^{n}]}}
, defined in
section 1
, which parametrizes all homogeneous ideals of
S[\mathbb{P}^{n}]
, with a fixed Hilbert function
h_{r,n}
. In particular,
\mathrm{Hilb}^{\scriptscriptstyle{h_{r,n}}}_{{\scriptscriptstyle S[\mathbb{P}^{n}]}}
may contain both saturated and non-saturated ideals (for further details about Hilbert schemes, refer to
[
6
]
*Section VI.2.2).

Nevertheless, we need to consider classical Hilbert schemes due to a specific observation made by T. Mańdziuk in
[
18
]
. Let
\bm{\mathcal{H}ilb}_{r}\bigl(\mathbb{P}^{n}\bigr)
denote the Hilbert scheme of the set of
r
-tuples of points in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
. The result of T. Mańdziuk is applicable to the case of the projective plane
\mathbb{P}^{2}
, whose Hilbert scheme
\bm{\mathcal{H}ilb}_{r}\bigl(\mathbb{P}^{2}\bigr)
is smooth and irreducible (see
[
8
]
*Theorem 2.4).

Proposition 4.8

(
[
18
]
*Remark 4.7)
.

If
\bm{\mathcal{H}ilb}_{r}\bigl(\mathbb{P}^{n}\bigr)
is irreducible,
I
is a saturated ideal and

I\in\mathrm{Hilb}^{\scriptscriptstyle{h_{r,n}}}_{{\scriptscriptstyle S[\mathbb{P}^{n}]}},

then
I\in\mathrm{Slip}_{r,n}
.

Therefore, to demonstrate that
I_{d}\in\mathrm{Slip}_{r,n}
, it suffices to prove that
I_{d}
is a saturated ideal,where

I_{d}=(p_{d,d},p_{d,d-1},\dots,p_{d,0}).

To establish this, we start by demonstrating that its leading ideal
\mathrm{LT}(I_{d})
is saturated. For this purpose, we require the following lemma, which represents a well-known fact, concerning the ideal of a fat point (refer to
[
11
]
for further details on fat points).

Lemma 4.8
.

For every
s\in\mathbb{N}
, the monomial ideal

J_{d}=\bigl(u^{d},u^{d-1}z,\dots,uz^{d-1},z^{d}\bigr)\subseteq\mathbb{K}[u,z,v],

corresponding to the ideal generated by all the monomials of degree
d
in the variables
u
and
z
, is saturated.

Next, we need to prove that the ideal
J_{d}
of
section 4
is indeed the leading ideal of
I_{d}
. To demonstrate this, we simply need to show that the chosen generators form a Gröbner basis of
I_{d}
. The classical method for proving this is to use Buchberger’s criterion, which is presented in its standard version in
[
7
]
*Theorem 15.8. However, we will employ a specific version of this criterion, described accurately by W. Decker and F.-O. Schreyer in
[
4
]
*Theorem 2.3.9, which significantly simplifies the verification process.

Theorem 4.8

(Buchberger’s criterion)
.

Let
f_{1},\dots,f_{r}\in\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
and let
I=(f_{1},\dots,f_{r})
. For every
j=2,\dots,r
, let
M_{j}
denote the ideal

M_{j}=\bigl(\mathrm{LT}(f_{1}),\dots,\mathrm{LT}(f_{j-1})\bigr):\bigl(\mathrm{LT}(f_{j})\bigr).

Then
\{f_{1},\dots,f_{r}\}
is a Gröbner basis for
I
if and only if for every
j=2,\dots,r
and each minimal generator
x^{\alpha}
of
M_{j}
, the remainder of the division of
x^{\alpha}f_{j}
by
f_{1},\dots,f_{r}
is equal to zero.

We can utilize this criterion to establish that the ideal
J_{d}
of
section 4
is indeed the leading ideal of
I_{d}
.

Lemma 4.8
.

For every
s\in\mathbb{N}
, the set

\mathcal{B}_{d}=\left\{p_{d,0},\dots,p_{d,d}\right\}

is a Gröbner basis of the ideal
I_{d}
with respect to the lexicographic monomial order defined by the relation

z>_{\text{lex}}u>_{\text{lex}}v.

Proof.

By Buchberger’s criterion, it suffices to prove that, for every
k=0,\dots,s
, considering the colon ideal

M_{k}=\bigl(z^{d},\dots,z^{d-k+1}u^{k-1}\bigr):\bigl(z^{d-k}u^{k}\bigr)=(z),

the remainder of
zp_{d,k}
divided by
p_{d,0},\dots,p_{d,d}
is equal to zero. If
k=s
, then we have

zp_{d,d}=zu^{d}=up_{d,d-1}

and the statement holds. So, given
k\leq d-1
, since

zp_{d,k}=z\sum_{j=0}^{\left\lfloor\frac{d-k}{2}\right\rfloor}(-1)^{j}u^{[k+j]}z^{[d-k-2j]}v^{[j]}=\sum_{j=0}^{\left\lfloor\frac{d-k}{2}\right\rfloor}(-1)^{j}(d-k-2j+1)u^{[k+j]}z^{[d-k-2j+1]}v^{[j]},

we can proceed with Buchberger’s algorithm by first considering
\mathrm{LT}(h_{d,k-1})=z^{[d-k+1]}u^{[k-1]}
. Indeed we obtain

zp_{d,k}=\frac{\mathrm{LT}(zp_{d,k})}{\mathrm{LT}(p_{d,k-1})}p_{d,k-1}+r_{1}=\frac{d-k+1}{k}up_{d,k-1}+r_{1},

where
r_{1}
is the first remainder. In particular, we have

\displaystyle r_{1}

\displaystyle=zp_{d,k}-\frac{d-k+1}{k}up_{d,k-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\left\lfloor\frac{d-k}{2}\right\rfloor}(-1)^{j}(d-k-2j+1)u^{[k+j]}z^{[d-k-2j+1]}v^{[j]}+

\displaystyle\hphantom{{}={}}{-}\frac{d-k+1}{k}u\sum_{j=0}^{\left\lfloor\frac{d-k+1}{2}\right\rfloor}(-1)^{j}u^{[k+j-1]}z^{[d-k-2j+1]}v^{[j]}.

Now, if
d-k
is even, then

\left\lfloor\frac{d-k}{2}\right\rfloor=\left\lfloor\frac{d-k+1}{2}\right\rfloor,

while if it is odd, we observe that the coefficient
d-k-2j-1
vanishes for

j=\left\lfloor\frac{d-k+1}{2}\right\rfloor

and hence we can add a term in the first sum. This allows us to use a formula with only one sum, given by

\displaystyle r_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\left\lfloor\frac{d-k+1}{2}\right\rfloor}(-1)^{j}\bigg((d-k-2j+1)-\frac{(d-k+1)(k+j)}{k}\bigg)u^{[k+j]}z^{[d-k-2j+1]}v^{[j]}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\left\lfloor\frac{d-k+1}{2}\right\rfloor}(-1)^{j}\frac{(d-k-2j+1)k-(d-k+1)(k+j)}{k}u^{[k+j]}z^{[d-k-2j+1]}v^{[j]}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\left\lfloor\frac{d-k+1}{2}\right\rfloor}(-1)^{j-1}\frac{(d+k+1)j}{k}u^{[k+j]}z^{[d-k-2j+1]}v^{[j]}

\displaystyle=\frac{d+k+1}{k}v\sum_{j=1}^{\left\lfloor\frac{d-k+1}{2}\right\rfloor}(-1)^{j-1}u^{[k+j]}z^{[d-k-2j+1]}v^{[j-1]}

\displaystyle=\frac{d+k+1}{k}v\sum_{j=0}^{\left\lfloor\frac{d-k-1}{2}\right\rfloor}(-1)^{j}u^{[k+j+1]}z^{[d-k-2j-1]}v^{[j]}

\displaystyle=\frac{d+k+1}{k}vp_{d,k+1},

which guarantees that the last remainder is equal to zero.
∎

Lemma 4.8

Let

and let

\mathrm{LT}_{<}\big(\overline{I}\big)\subseteq\overline{\mathrm{LT}_{<}(I)}.

\mathrm{LT}_{<}(I)

Proof of
section 4
.

If
g
is a degenerate form such that
\rk g=2
, then the form
g^{s}
is equivalent, under the action of
\mathrm{SL}_{3}(\mathbb{C})
, to the form

q_{2}^{s}=\bigl(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}\bigr)^{s}.

Thus, by
section 4
, we conclude that

\brk\bigl(g^{s}\bigr)=\rk\bigl(g^{s}\bigr)=s+1.

If instead
g
is a non-degenerate form, then
g^{s}
is equivalent to the form

q_{3}^{s}=\bigl(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}\bigr)^{s}.

Now,
section 4
and

I_{s+1}=(p_{s+1,s+1},p_{s+1,s},\dots,p_{s+1,0}),

is a saturated ideal for every

.
If we now consider the Hilbert function of the ideal

given by

we first observe that for every

Moreover, since

\HF_{I_{s+1}}(s+1)=\dim\bigl(\mathcal{D}_{s+1}\big/(I_{s+1})_{s+1}\bigr)=\binom{s+3}{2}-(s+1)=\frac{(s+3)(s+2)-2(s+2)}{2},

which can be simplified to

\HF_{I_{s+1}}(s+1)=\frac{s^{2}+3s+2}{2}=\binom{s+2}{2}=\HF_{I_{s+1}}(s),
