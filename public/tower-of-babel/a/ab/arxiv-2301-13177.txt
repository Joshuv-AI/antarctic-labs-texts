Infinite-Variate L 2 L^{2} -Approximation with Nested Subspace Sampling
Kumar Harsha, Michael Gnewuch, and Marcin Wnuk

Abstract
We consider L 2 L^{2} -approximation on weighted reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces of functions depending on infinitely many variables. We focus on unrestricted linear information, admitting evaluations of arbitrary continuous linear functionals. We distinguish between ANOVA and non-ANOVA spaces, where, by ANOVA spaces, we refer to function spaces whose norms are induced by an underlying ANOVA function decomposition. In ANOVA spaces, we provide an optimal algorithm to solve the approximation problem using linear information. We determine the upper and lower error bounds on the polynomial convergence rate of n n -th minimal worst-case errors, which match if the weights decay regularly. For non-ANOVA spaces, we also establish upper and lower error bounds. Our analysis reveals that for weights with a regular and moderate decay behavior, the convergence rate of n n -th minimal errors is strictly higher in ANOVA than in non-ANOVA spaces.



1
Introduction

We want to investigate
L^{2}
-approximation on weighted reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHSs) of functions depending on infinitely many variables. For each of
those RKHSs, there is a family of weights and a function decomposition, which determine the norm on the space. With the help of the function decomposition, it is possible to represent an infinite-variate function as an infinite sum of functions depending only on finitely many variables.

In this paper, we focus on linear algorithms that may obtain information about an input function
f
by evaluating finitely many continuous linear functionals in
f
. The information cost of such an algorithm is the sum of the cost of all functional evaluations, which, in turn, depends on the underlying function decomposition of the weighted RKHS we are working on.
Such a situation has already been studied in
Was12
;
WW11a
, where the considered cost model is a generalization of the one introduced in
KSWW10a
for information consisting only of function evaluation (so-called standard information). In
WW11a
, a rather specific approximation problem, called the
\mathcal{G}
-approximation problem, is analyzed; there, the approximation error is measured in some very special Hilbert space
\mathcal{G}
. In
Was12
,
L^{2}
-approximation is investigated in full generality, apart from the case that can be reduced to the
\mathcal{G}
-approximation problem.
To describe the results from
Was12
;
WW11a
and from this paper in a more common language, we distinguish two cases of weighted RKHSs: the ones whose underlying function decomposition is an ANOVA decomposition are called ANOVA spaces, whereas the remaining RKHSs are referred to as non-ANOVA spaces (for a rigorous definition, see Section
2.1
).

The case of ANOVA spaces was covered by Wasilkowski and Woźniakowski, who in
WW11a
exactly determined the polynomial convergence rates
r_{\infty}
of
n
-th minimal errors (for a definition, see Section
2.2
). For the non-ANOVA spaces, Wasilkowski derived in
Was12
a lower bound for
r_{\infty}
; with the help of auxiliary weights, he re-used the constructive results from
WW11a
. He also proved a matching upper bound. Interestingly, it turns out that for slowly decaying weights, the convergence rate in the ANOVA case is superior to the one in the non-ANOVA case, while it stayed the same if the weights decrease quickly enough.

The cost model used in
Was12
;
WW11a
is unrestricted subspace sampling (see Section
2.2
). In this paper, we instead consider the nested subspace sampling cost model, which is less generous than the unrestricted one. For the nested subspace sampling cost model, we have derived lower and upper bounds on the polynomial convergence rate
r_{\infty}
of
n
-th minimal errors in the ANOVA case, see Theorem
3.2
, where the bounds match if the weights exhibit a regular decay behavior. In the non-ANOVA case, we are able to provide upper and constructive lower bounds on
r_{\infty}
, see Propositions
1
and
2
. Although these bounds do not match in general, they show that in some cases the convergence rate in the ANOVA case is higher than the one in the non-ANOVA case, cf. Corollary
2
. The reason for that is mainly that the cost of an algorithm may depend on what kind of function decomposition underlies the function space it is defined on. This dependence, unfortunately, prevents us from simply using the embedding machinery developed in
HR13
;
GHHR17
;
GHHRW19
to transfer results for ANOVA spaces directly to spaces with a different underlying function decomposition.

Algorithms, Cost Models, and Errors

Admissible algorithms and cost

We know that non-adaptive algorithms are as good as adaptive algorithms in the class of all deterministic algorithms for linear problems
S:F\to G
where
F
is a pre-Hilbert space, see
(
NW08
, Theorem 4.5)
; for a precise definition of deterministic algorithms, see
TWW88
. Moreover, we know that linear algorithms are optimal for the aforementioned linear problems, see
(
NW08
, Theorem 4.8)
. Therefore, we may confine ourselves to linear algorithms using arbitrary linear information of the form

\mathcal{A}(f)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}L_{{\bm{u}}_{j}}(f)\cdot g_{j}

(7)

for some

, index sets

, and linear functionals

, see
TWW88
. Owing to the Riesz representation theorem, we can define the linear functional

as inner product with a representer as

L_{{\bm{u}}_{j}}(f):=\left\langle f,\eta_{{\bm{u}}_{j}}\right\rangle_{H_{{\bm{\gamma}}}},\quad\text{where }\eta_{{\bm{u}}_{j}}\in\bigoplus_{{\bm{v}}\subseteq{\bm{u}}_{j}}H_{{\bm{v}}}.

(8)

The worst-case error of the algorithm

for

-approximation with respect to

is given as

e(\mathcal{A},H_{{\bm{\gamma}}}):=e(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{S},H_{{\bm{\gamma}}}):=\sup_{\|f\|_{H_{{\bm{\gamma}}}}\leq 1}\|\mathcal{S}(f)-\mathcal{A}(f)\|_{L^{2}({\mathfrak{X}},\rho^{\mathbb{N}})}.

\$:{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\to[1,\infty)
which is non-decreasing. Consider a linear functional

Minimal errors and complexity

For the results in this paper, we work with the NSS cost model unless specified otherwise. On that basis, we define the
n
-th minimal error
for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
as

e(n,H_{{\bm{\gamma}}}):=\inf\{e(\mathcal{A},H_{{\bm{\gamma}}})\ :\ \mathcal{A}\text{ as in \eqref{eq:lin_algo_all}}\text{ and }\textup{cost}(\mathcal{A})\leq n\}.

The approximation problem is said to be
strongly tractable
if and only if there exist non-negative constants
c,\alpha>0
such that for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, it holds that

e(n,H_{{\bm{\gamma}}})\leq c\cdot n^{-\alpha}.

In order to quantify convergence rates, we introduce the notion of decay rates. The
lower polynomial decay rate
of a null sequence of positive reals
{\bm{x}}:=\{x_{j}\}_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is given by

\textup{d}^{\textup{low}}_{{\bm{x}}}:=\decay({\bm{x}})=\sup\left\{\alpha\geq 0:\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}x_{j}^{1/\alpha}<\infty\right\}.

(9)

This leads to the definition of the
polynomial convergence rate of
n
-th minimal errors
, c.f.
(
GHHR17
, Sec 4)
,

r_{\infty}:=r_{\infty}(H_{{\bm{\gamma}}})=\decay(\{e(n,H_{{\bm{\gamma}}})\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}),

(10)

which is the quantity of interest in later sections. Since
e(n,H_{{\bm{\gamma}}})
forms a non-increasing sequence, the definition (
10
) is equivalent to

r_{\infty}(H_{{\bm{\gamma}}})=\sup\left\{\alpha\geq 0:\lim_{n\to\infty}e(n,H_{{\bm{\gamma}}})n^{\alpha}=0\right\},

as mentioned without a proof in
FHW12
; for the reader’s convenience, we present a proof in Lemma
7
. We define the
upper polynomial decay rate
of a null sequence of positive reals
\{x_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
as

\textup{d}^{\textup{up}}_{{\bm{x}}}:=\inf\left\{\alpha\geq 0:\inf_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}x_{n}n^{\alpha}>0\right\}.

(11)

We show that
\textup{d}^{\textup{low}}_{{\bm{x}}}\leq\textup{d}^{\textup{up}}_{{\bm{x}}}
in general, see Lemma
8
. Although one can construct sequences such that
\textup{d}^{\textup{low}}_{{\bm{x}}}<\textup{d}^{\textup{up}}_{{\bm{x}}}
, equality holds for most practical choices of sequences; confer Remark
3
. In any case, we highlight the difference in the lower and upper decay rates in all results, except Corollary
2
.

We now introduce complexity concepts which also appear in tractability studies as another perspective on the minimal errors. Let
\varepsilon>0
be the error demand. The worst case
\varepsilon
-information complexity
, also referred to as
\varepsilon
-complexity
of function approximation is defined as, c.f.
WW11a
,

\textup{comp}(\varepsilon,H_{{\bm{\gamma}}}):=\inf\left\{\textup{cost}(\mathcal{A})\ :\ \mathcal{A}\text{ as in \eqref{eq:lin_algo_all}}\text{ and }e(\mathcal{A},H_{{\bm{\gamma}}})\leq\varepsilon\right\}.

The approximation problem is strongly tractable if and only if there exist non-negative constants
c,\alpha>0
such that

\textup{comp}(\varepsilon,H_{{\bm{\gamma}}})\leq c\cdot\varepsilon^{-\alpha}.

The
exponent of tractability
is defined as

p_{\infty}:=p_{\infty}(H_{{\bm{\gamma}}})=\inf\left\{\alpha\geq 0:\exists C_{\alpha}>0\forall\varepsilon\in(0,1)\ \textup{comp}(\varepsilon,H_{{\bm{\gamma}}})\leq C_{\alpha}\varepsilon^{-\alpha}\right\}.

(12)

We can relate the exponent of tractability to the convergence rate of the
n
-th minimal error as
r_{\infty}=1/p_{\infty}
.

Univariate and Multivariate
L^{2}
-Approximation

Consider the univariate solution operator
S:H(1+k)\to L^{2}(D,\rho),f\mapsto f
. It follows from (
4
) that
S
is compact. This means that the self-adjoint operator
W:=S^{\ast}\circ S
is compact and positive definite. Consequently,

has a null sequence of positive eigenvalues

(13)

We assume that the eigenvalues are ordered, i.e.
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\dots
.

Remark 2

We provide three examples that exhibit a sharp decay rate for the eigenvalues

. For the approximation problem defined on a Korobov space of smoothness

, it can be checked that the eigenvalues behave as
\lambda_{j}\asymp j^{-2\alpha}
; see
(
NW08
, p 341)
. On the classical Sobolev space, the eigenvalues follow

; see
(
WW11a
, p 410)
. In both of these cases,
\textup{d}^{\textup{up}}_{{\bm{\lambda}}}=\textup{d}^{\textup{low}}_{{\bm{\lambda}}}

Another example, which recently captured a lot of interest, is Hermite spaces with the reproducing kernel

, where the Fourier weights

are essentially of the form

(PG), or
\alpha_{\nu}:=2^{r\nu^{b}}
for some

(EG). Furthermore,

denotes the

-normalized Hermite polynomial of degree

. The first paper considering spaces of this type was

; for more recent results, see

and the literature mentioned therein. Here, the eigenvalues are
\lambda_{j}=\alpha_{j+1}^{-1}
for

; see, e.g.
(

, Section 4.1.2)
. Hence, we have
\textup{d}^{\textup{up}}_{\bm{\lambda}}=r=\textup{d}^{\textup{low}}_{\bm{\lambda}}
in the case (PG) and

in the case (EG).

Let
\textup{d}^{\textup{low}}_{\bm{\lambda}}
denote the lower decay rate of the eigenvalues
{\bm{\lambda}}
of

. We assume that

To show the other part, we adapt the proof strategy from

This shows that

Corollary 2

Assume that
\textup{d}^{\textup{low}}_{\bm{\gamma}}=\textup{d}^{\textup{up}}_{\bm{\gamma}}=:\textup{d}_{\bm{\gamma}}
. If
1<\textup{d}_{\bm{\gamma}}<1+s
, then the convergence rate of
L^{2}
-approximation in ANOVA spaces is strictly larger than the one in non-ANOVA spaces.

Proof

Note that due to
1<\textup{d}^{\textup{low}}_{\bm{\lambda}}
, the condition
\textup{d}_{\bm{\gamma}}<1+s
implies
\textup{d}^{\textup{low}}_{\bm{\lambda}}>(\textup{d}_{\bm{\gamma}}-1)/s
.
Hence, under the given condition, the exact convergence rate of
n
-th minimal errors in the ANOVA case from Theorem
3.2
is greater than the largest possible convergence rate in the non-ANOVA case from Proposition
1
, i.e.

\min\left\{\frac{\textup{d}^{\textup{low}}_{\bm{\lambda}}}{2},\frac{\textup{d}_{\bm{\gamma}}-1}{2s}\right\}=\frac{\textup{d}_{\bm{\gamma}}-1}{2s}<\min\left\{\frac{\textup{d}^{\textup{low}}_{\bm{\lambda}}}{2},\frac{\textup{d}_{\bm{\gamma}}}{2(1+s)}\right\}=\frac{\textup{d}_{\bm{\gamma}}}{2(1+s)}.

The statement is hence proved.

As we see from the analysis, there is a gap between the convergence rates in the ANOVA and the non-ANOVA setting. Note that we can use the function
f^{\ast}
from Proposition
1
to obtain the same upper bound on the convergence rate for the integration problem in the non-ANOVA setting,
I:H_{{\bm{\gamma}}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
. More specifically, we have
I(f^{\ast})=c_{1}\sqrt{\sum_{j>L}\gamma_{j}}\gtrsim N^{(1-\textup{d}^{\textup{up}}_{\bm{\gamma}}-\delta)/2s}
, which implies that the convergence rate is at most
(\textup{d}^{\textup{up}}_{\bm{\gamma}}-1)/2s
. On the other hand, the integration problem is trivial in the ANOVA setting. This provides some intuition for the gap since we know that the function approximation problem is, in general, not easier than the integration problem.

Lemma 7

If
{\bm{x}}
is a non-increasing null sequence of positive reals, then we have the following identity

\textup{d}^{\textup{low}}_{1}=\textup{d}^{\textup{low}}_{2}=\textup{d}^{\textup{low}}_{3}.

Proof

We first claim
\textup{d}^{\textup{low}}_{1}\leq\textup{d}^{\textup{low}}_{3}
. Let
\alpha<\textup{d}^{\textup{low}}_{1}
. Using the monotonicity of
{\bm{x}}
, we get for all
N\in{\mathbb{N}}

Nx_{N}^{1/\alpha}\leq\sum_{n=1}^{N}x_{n}^{1/\alpha}\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}x_{n}^{1/\alpha}=:c_{1}<\infty.

Therefore, for all
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we have
x_{N}N^{\alpha}\leq c_{1}^{\alpha}
, implying
\alpha\leq\textup{d}^{\textup{low}}_{3}
. As
\alpha\to\textup{d}^{\textup{low}}_{1}
, we have the claim.

Next, we show
\textup{d}^{\textup{low}}_{3}\leq\textup{d}^{\textup{low}}_{2}
. Let
\alpha<\textup{d}^{\textup{low}}_{3}
. Then we know that
\{x_{n}n^{\alpha}\}
is bounded. Consequently, for every
\varepsilon>0
, the sequence
\{x_{n}n^{\alpha-\varepsilon}\}
converges to zero, and
\alpha-\varepsilon\leq\textup{d}^{\textup{low}}_{2}
. In the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
and
\alpha\to\textup{d}^{\textup{low}}_{3}
, we have the result.

Finally, we prove
\textup{d}^{\textup{low}}_{2}\leq\textup{d}^{\textup{low}}_{1}
. Let
\alpha<\textup{d}^{\textup{low}}_{2}
and
c_{2}>0
. From (
34
), we know that there exists
N_{2}\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that for all
n\geq N_{2}
, we have
x_{n}\leq c_{2}n^{-\alpha}
. Then for all
\varepsilon>0

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}x_{n}^{1/(\alpha-\varepsilon)}=\sum_{n=1}^{N_{2}-1}x_{n}^{1/(\alpha-\varepsilon)}+\sum_{n={N_{2}}}^{\infty}x_{n}^{1/(\alpha-\varepsilon)}\leq\sum_{n=1}^{N_{2}-1}x_{n}^{1/(\alpha-\varepsilon)}+c_{2}^{\frac{1}{\alpha-\varepsilon}}\sum_{n={N_{2}}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-\varepsilon}}}<\infty,

which means
\alpha-\varepsilon\leq\textup{d}^{\textup{low}}_{1}
. The result follows as
\varepsilon\to 0
and
\alpha\to\textup{d}^{\textup{low}}_{2}
.

The identity is hence proved.

Let

Remark 3

We provide two examples of sequences to show that the lower and upper decay rates are equal in most relevant cases but can also differ significantly. As first example, let
\{x_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
behave as follows

c_{1}n^{-\alpha}\ln(n+1)^{\beta_{1}}\leq x_{n}\leq c_{2}n^{-\alpha}\ln(n+1)^{\beta_{2}},

for
n
large enough, where
\alpha>0,c_{1},c_{2}>0,\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
\beta_{1}\leq\beta_{2}
. Then, we show
\textup{d}^{\textup{low}}_{{\bm{x}}}=\textup{d}^{\textup{up}}_{{\bm{x}}}=\alpha
. For all
\varepsilon>0
, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}x_{n}n^{\alpha-\varepsilon}\leq c_{2}\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\ln(n+1)^{\beta_{2}}}{n^{\varepsilon}}=0.

Since we consider only positive values for
x_{n}
, we actually get
\lim_{n\to\infty}x_{n}n^{\alpha-\varepsilon}=0
. Hence, using (
34
), we get
\textup{d}^{\textup{low}}_{{\bm{x}}}\geq\alpha
. Moreover, for all
\varepsilon>0
, we also get

\inf_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}x_{n}n^{\alpha+\varepsilon}\geq c_{1}\inf_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\ln(n+1)^{\beta_{1}}n^{\varepsilon}>0.

Therefore, using (
11
), we have
\textup{d}^{\textup{up}}_{\bm{x}}\leq\alpha
. From Lemma
8
follows
\textup{d}^{\textup{low}}_{{\bm{x}}}=\alpha=\textup{d}^{\textup{up}}_{{\bm{x}}}
.

In contrast, define recursively
n_{1}=0
and
n_{k+1}=2^{n_{k}}+1
and consider the sequence
y_{n}=2^{-n_{k}}
for
n\in[n_{k},n_{k+1})
. In this case, we show
\textup{d}^{\textup{low}}_{\bm{y}}=1
and
\textup{d}^{\textup{up}}_{\bm{y}}=\infty
. Let
0<\alpha<1
. Then

\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}y_{n}^{1/\alpha}=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\sum_{n=n_{k}}^{n_{k+1}-1}2^{-n_{k}/\alpha}=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\frac{2^{n_{k}}-n_{k}}{2^{n_{k}/\alpha}}\leq\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}2^{-n_{k}(\frac{1}{\alpha}-1)}<\infty,

which implies
\alpha\leq\textup{d}^{\textup{low}}_{\bm{y}}
and hence
\textup{d}^{\textup{low}}_{\bm{y}}\geq 1
. For
\alpha=1
, the series above diverges.
Therefore, we have
\textup{d}^{\textup{low}}_{\bm{y}}\leq 1
, and altogether
\textup{d}^{\textup{low}}_{\bm{y}}=1
. On the other hand, we have for all
\alpha>0

\inf_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}y_{n}n^{\alpha}=\inf_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\min_{n\in[n_{k},n_{k+1})}y_{n}n^{\alpha}=\inf_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\min_{n\in[n_{k},n_{k+1})}\frac{n^{\alpha}}{2^{n_{k}}}=\inf_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\frac{n^{\alpha}}{2^{n}}=0.

Consequently,
\textup{d}^{\textup{up}}_{\bm{y}}=\infty
.
