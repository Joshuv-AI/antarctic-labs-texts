Abstract
Optimal k k -thresholding algorithms are a class of k k -sparse signal recovery algorithms that overcome the shortcomings of traditional hard thresholding algorithms caused by the oscillation of the residual function. In this paper, a novel convergence analysis for optimal k k -thresholding algorithms is established, which reveals the data-time tradeoffs of these algorithms. Both the analysis and numerical results demonstrate that when the number of measurements is small, the algorithms cannot converge; when the number of measurements is suitably large, the number of iterations required for successful recovery has a negative correlation with the number of measurements, and the algorithms can achieve linear convergence. Furthermore, the main theorems indicate that the number of measurements required for successful recovery is of the order of k ​ log ⁡ ( n / k ) k\log({n}/{k}) , where n n is the dimension of the target signal.



I
Introduction

Compressed sensing (CS) together with its sparse optimization models has attracted plenty of attention in signal and image processing over the past few decades
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
.
CS concerns the problem of recovering a sparse signal from a relatively small number of noisy measurements

\bm{y}=\bm{A}\bm{x}^{\star}+\bm{n},

(1)

where
\bm{x}^{\star}\in\mathbbm{R}^{n}
is the target signal,
\bm{A}\in\mathbbm{R}^{m\times n}\,(m\leq n)
is the sensing matrix, and
\bm{n}\in\mathbbm{R}^{m}
is the noise. Finding the target signal from the above underdetermined system can be formulated as the following optimization problem with a sparsity constraint

\min_{\bm{x}}\left\{\|\bm{y}-\bm{A}\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2}:\left\lVert\bm{x}\right\rVert_{0}\leq k\right\},

(2)

where
\left\lVert\bm{x}\right\rVert_{0}
denotes the number of nonzero entries of the vector
\bm{x}
,
k\ll n
is an integer number. Although the problem (
2
) is NP-hard
[
4
]
, it can be solved by some efficient algorithms with high probability.

The plausible algorithms to solve this problem have been classified into three categories: convex optimization
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
, heuristic methods
[
8
,
9
]
, and thresholding methods
[
10
,
11
,
12
]
. Here we focus on the thresholding methods. Generally speaking, thresholding methods can be classified into soft thresholding
[
10
]
, hard thresholding
[
11
]
, and optimal
k
-thresholding
[
12
]
. The soft thresholding algorithms are established by considering the optimality condition of certain convex problems. To describe hard thresholding methods, it is convenient to introduce the hard thresholding operator
\mathcal{H}_{k}(\bm{z})
, which retains the
k
largest absolute entries of
\bm{z}
and zeroes out the remaining entries. By connecting
\mathcal{H}_{k}(\bm{z})
and the gradient method for minimizing the residual function
\|\bm{y}-\bm{A}\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2}
, Blumensath and Davies
[
11
]
proposed the so-called iterative hard thresholding (IHT) to solve the problem (
2
), which takes the scheme

\bm{x}^{p+1}=\mathcal{H}_{k}(\bm{x}^{p}+\bm{A}^{T}(\bm{y}-\bm{A}\bm{x}^{p})).

(3)

By combining IHT and the pursuit step such as minimizing the residual subject to the support determined by IHT, Foucart
[
13
]
put forward an accelerated version called hard thresholding pursuit (HTP). However, in many numerical situations, it demonstrates that the residual reduction is irrelevant
to
the thresholding step, causing the divergence of IHT,
and
even HTP. It is worth mentioning that Zhao
[
12
]
proposed an iterative scheme to seek optimal
k
locations of
\bm{u}^{p}
that minimize the residual reduction before the thresholding step

\bm{w}(\bm{u}^{p})=\mathrm{min}\big\{\|\bm{y}-\bm{A}(\bm{u}^{p}\odot\bm{w})\|^{2}_{2},~\textrm{{e}}^{T}\bm{w}=k,~\bm{w}\in\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}\big\},

(4)

and update the next iteration with
\bm{x}^{p+1}=\bm{u}^{p}\odot\bm{w}(\bm{u}^{p})
, where
\bm{u}^{p}=\bm{x}^{p}+\bm{A}^{T}(\bm{y}-\bm{A}\bm{x}^{p})
,
\textrm{{e}}\in\mathbbm{R}^{n}
denotes all-one vector and the symbol
\odot
denotes Hadamard product. This algorithm is called optimal
k
-thresholding (OT) algorithm and its variant with pursuit step over the support of
\bm{w}(\bm{u}^{p})
is called optimal
k
-thresholding pursuit (OTP) algorithm. To make the algorithms tractable, Zhao studied their convex relaxation versions by replacing
\bm{w}\in\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}
with
0\leq\bm{w}\leq\textrm{{e}}
, i.e., ROT and ROTP algorithms (see Algorithm
1
and Algorithm
2
for more details). Numerical experiments have shown that ROP or ROTP algorithms are more robust and stable during the course of iterations, and have better performance on signal recovery than traditional hard thresholding methods
[
12
]
.

Under the assumption that the sensing matrix satisfies the restricted isometry property (RIP), Zhao
[
12
]
has shown that the restricted isometry constant
\delta_{3k}<1/5
is sufficient for the convergence of both ROT and ROTP. However, the main drawback of using RIP in convergence analysis for the algorithms is that
RIP-based convergence analysis cannot characterize the relationship among reconstruction accuracy, the number of measurements and the number of iterations, which is of great importance in the field related with data acquisition from underdetermined systems
[
14
,
15
]
. In particular, the theoretical analysis of algorithms is required to consider and understand the precise tradeoffs between the number of obtainable measurements and the convergence speed, which we called the data-time tradeoffs for algorithms. The researches in
[
16
,
17
]
showed that more efficient algorithms can be obtained by the use of hierarchies of convex relaxation when the number of measured data is relatively large. The sharp data-time tradeoffs for projected gradient methods to solve linear inverse problems were analyzed by Oyamk, Recht, and Soltanolkotabi
[
14
]
. Chen and Liu
[
15
]
characterized the data-time tradeoffs for projected gradient descent algorithms in corrupted sensing under sub-Gaussian measurements.

To overcome the drawback, this paper establishes a novel convergence analysis for the relaxed optimal
k
-thresholding algorithms ROT and ROTP, and provides the data-time tradeoffs for these algorithms under sub-Gaussian measurements. Theoretical analysis presents that when the number of measurements is relatively large, with the increase of the number of measurements, the number of iterations needed for successful reconstruction decreases accordingly. Moreover, if the number of measurements is of the order of
k\log({n}/{k})
with a relatively large constant, we show that the algorithms can achieve linear convergence rate.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
II
serves as an introduction to some necessary definitions and facts. Section
III
presents the theoretical analysis (considering the data-time tradeoffs) for ROT and ROTP algorithms. The numerical results are provided in Section
IV
. Finally, the conclusion and future work are given in Section
V
.

II-A

Useful definitions

A random variable
x
is called
sub-Gaussian
if it has a finite sub-Gaussian norm

\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}=\inf\left\{t>0:\textbf{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{x^{2}}{t^{2}}\right)\right]\leq 2\right\}.

(5)

For example, Gaussian, Bernoulli, and bounded random variables are typical sub-Gaussian variables. A random vector
\bm{x}\in\mathbbm{R}^{n}
is called
sub-Gaussian
if for any
\bm{y}\in\mathbbm{R}^{n}
,
\left\langle\bm{x},\bm{y}\right\rangle
is sub-Gaussian. The corresponding norm is defined as

\left\lVert\bm{x}\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}=\sup_{\bm{y}\in\mathbbm{S}^{n-1}}\left\lVert\left\langle\bm{x},\bm{y}\right\rangle\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}},

(6)

where
\mathbbm{S}^{n-1}
denotes the
n
-dimensional unit sphere.

A random vector
\bm{x}
is
isotropic
if it satisfies

\textbf{E}[\bm{x}\bm{x}^{T}]=\bm{I}_{n},

(7)

where
\bm{I}_{n}
denotes the
n
-dimensional identity matrix.

The
Gaussian complexity
of a bounded subset
\mathbb{T}
is defined as

\gamma(\mathbb{T})=\textbf{E}\Big[\sup_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{T}}|\left\langle\bm{g},\bm{x}\right\rangle|\Big],

and the
Gaussian width
of a bounded subset
\mathbb{T}
is defined as

w(\mathbb{T})=\textbf{E}\Big[\sup_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{T}}\left\langle\bm{g},\bm{x}\right\rangle\Big],

where
\bm{g}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I}_{n})
.
The above two quantities are of the same order of magnitude
[
19
]
due to the fact

\frac{1}{3}(w(\mathbb{T})+\|\bm{y}\|_{2})\leq\gamma(\mathbb{T})\leq 2\left(w(\mathbb{T})+\|\bm{y}\|_{2}\right),\forall\,\bm{y}\in\mathbb{T}.

(8)

II-B

Useful facts

Fact 1
(Matrix deviation inequality,
[
20
]
)

Let
\bm{A}
be an
{m\times n}
random matrix whose rows
\{\bm{A}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
are independent, centered, isotropic and sub-Gaussian vectors. For any bounded subset
\mathbb{D}\subseteq\mathbbm{R}^{n}
and
t\geq 0
, the inequality

\sup\limits_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{D}}\left|\left\lVert\bm{A}\bm{x}\right\rVert_{2}-\sqrt{m}\left\lVert\bm{x}\right\rVert_{2}\right|\leq CK^{2}[\gamma(\mathbb{D})+t\cdot\mbox{rad}(\mathbb{D})]

holds with probability at least
1-2\exp(-t^{2})
. Here,
\mbox{rad}(\mathbb{D})=\sup_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{D}}\left\lVert\bm{x}\right\rVert_{2}
,
K=\max_{i}\left\lVert\bm{A}_{i}\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}
and
C>0
is an absolute constant.

Fact 2
(Gaussian width of
\ell_{0}
-balls,
[
21
]
)

Let
\mathbb{D}_{k}=\{\bm{z}\in\mathbbm{R}^{n}\big|\left\lVert\bm{z}\right\rVert_{0}\leq k,\left\lVert\bm{z}\right\rVert_{2}=1\}
. Then its Gaussian width satisfies

\displaystyle w(\mathbb{D}_{k})\leq c\sqrt{k\log\frac{en}{k}}.

(9)

For the convenience of the convergence analysis, we first give some useful lemmas, which will be frequently used in our later analysis (Theorems
1
and

By assigning
\mathbb{D}\in\mathbbm{S}^{n-1}
and
t=\gamma(\mathbb{D})
from Fact
1
, we have the following lemma.

Lemma 1

Let
\bm{A}
be an
{m\times n}
random matrix as defined in Fact
1
. Let
\mathbb{D}\in\mathbbm{S}^{n-1}
and
t=\gamma(\mathbb{D})
, then for any
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{D}
, the inequality

\sqrt{m}-2CK^{2}\gamma(\mathbb{D})\leq\left\lVert\bm{A}\bm{x}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\sqrt{m}+2CK^{2}\gamma(\mathbb{D})

holds with probability at least
1-2\exp(-\gamma^{2}(\mathbb{D}))
.

Lemma 2

Let
\bm{A}
be an
{m\times n}
random matrix as defined in Fact
1
. Let
\mathbb{D}_{k}=\{\bm{z}\in\mathbbm{R}^{n}\big|\|\bm{z}\|_{0}\leq k,\|\bm{z}\|_{2}=1\}
. Let
\mathbb{L}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}
be an index set with cardinality
l
. Define
\left[\bm{x}\right]_{\mathbb{L}}
as the vector obtained by retaining the components of
\bm{x}
with the support
\mathbb{L}
and zeroing out the remaining components. Let
\eta
be a step size such that
\eta m\leq 1
. Then we have

a)
For any
k
-sparse vector
\bm{v}\in\mathbbm{R}^{n}
, with probability at least
1-4\exp(-\gamma^{2}(\mathbb{D}_{k}+\mathbb{D}_{l}))
, we have
\left\lVert[(\bm{I}-\eta\bm{A}^{T}\bm{A})\bm{v}]_{\mathbb{L}}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\max\left\{s_{1},s_{2}\right\}\cdot\left\lVert\bm{v}\right\rVert_{2}
, where

\displaystyle s_{1}

\displaystyle=1-\eta m\cdot\frac{\sqrt{m}-6CK^{2}\gamma(\mathbb{D}_{k}+\mathbb{D}_{l})}{\sqrt{m}},

\displaystyle s_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{3CK^{2}\gamma(\mathbb{D}_{k}+\mathbb{D}_{l})}{\sqrt{m}}\left(\frac{3CK^{2}\gamma(\mathbb{D}_{k}+\mathbb{D}_{l})}{2\sqrt{m}}+1\right).

b)
For any
\bm{x}\in\mathbbm{R}^{m}
, with probability at least
1-2\exp(-\gamma^{2}(\mathbb{D}_{l}))
, we have
\left\lVert[\bm{A}^{T}\bm{x}]_{\mathbb{L}}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\left\lVert\bm{x}\right\rVert_{2}(\sqrt{m}+2CK^{2}\gamma(\mathbb{D}_{l}))
.

Sketch of Proof
.

a)
Reformulate
\left\lVert[(\bm{I}-\eta\bm{A}^{T}\bm{A})\bm{v}]_{\mathbb{L}}\right\rVert_{2}
as

\displaystyle\left\lVert[(\bm{I}-\eta\bm{A}^{T}\bm{A})\bm{v}]_{\mathbb{L}}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\langle[(\bm{I}-\eta\bm{A}^{T}\bm{A})\bm{v}]_{\mathbb{L}},\bm{\mu}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\bm{v}\right\rVert_{2}\left\langle(\bm{I}-\eta\bm{A}^{T}\bm{A})\bm{\nu},\bm{\mu}\right\rangle,

(10)

where

\bm{\mu}=\frac{[(\bm{I}-\eta\bm{A}^{T}\bm{A})\bm{v}]_{\mathbb{L}}}{\left\lVert[(\bm{I}-\eta\bm{A}^{T}\bm{A})\bm{v}]_{\mathbb{L}}\right\rVert_{2}}\in\mathbb{D}_{l},~\bm{\nu}=\frac{\bm{v}}{\left\lVert\bm{v}\right\rVert_{2}}\in\mathbb{D}_{k}.

As for
\left\langle(\bm{I}-\eta\bm{A}^{T}\bm{A})\bm{\nu},\bm{\mu}\right\rangle
, we have

\displaystyle\left\langle(\bm{I}-\eta\bm{A}^{T}\bm{A})\bm{\nu},\bm{\mu}\right\rangle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\Bigg(\left\lVert\bm{\nu}+\bm{\mu}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}-\eta\left\lVert\bm{A}(\bm{\nu}+\bm{\mu})\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle-\left\lVert\bm{\nu}-\bm{\mu}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\eta\left\lVert\bm{A}(\bm{\nu}-\bm{\mu})\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\Bigg).

(11)

Using Lemma
1
yields the bounds

\left\lVert\bm{A}(\bm{\nu}+\bm{\mu})\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\\
\geq\left(\max\{\sqrt{m}\left\lVert\bm{\nu}+\bm{\mu}\right\rVert_{2}-3CK^{2}\gamma(\mathbb{D}_{k}+\mathbb{D}_{l}),0\}\right)^{2}

(12)

and

\left\lVert\bm{A}(\bm{\nu}-\bm{\mu})\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\left[\sqrt{m}\left\lVert\bm{\nu}-\bm{\mu}\right\rVert_{2}+3CK^{2}\gamma(\mathbb{D}_{k}+\mathbb{D}_{l})\right]^{2}

(13)

with probability at least
1-2\exp(-\gamma^{2}(\mathbb{D}_{k}+\mathbb{D}_{l}))
respectively.

Then we give the upper bound of
\left\langle(\bm{I}-\eta\bm{A}^{T}\bm{A})\bm{\nu},\bm{\mu}\right\rangle
in two cases:
\sqrt{m}\left\lVert\bm{\nu}+\bm{\mu}\right\rVert_{2}-3CK^{2}\gamma(\mathbb{D}_{k}+\mathbb{D}_{l})\geq 0
and
\sqrt{m}\left\lVert\bm{\nu}+\bm{\mu}\right\rVert_{2}-3CK^{2}\gamma(\mathbb{D}_{k}+\mathbb{D}_{l})<0
. After that, we complete the proof.

b)
Reformulate
\left\lVert[\bm{A}^{T}\bm{x}]_{\mathbb{L}}\right\rVert_{2}
as

\displaystyle\left\lVert[\bm{A}^{T}\bm{x}]_{\mathbb{L}}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\langle[\bm{A}^{T}\bm{x}]_{\mathbb{L}},\bm{z}\right\rangle=\left\langle\bm{A}^{T}\bm{x},\bm{z}\right\rangle=\left\langle\bm{x},\bm{A}\bm{z}\right\rangle,

(14)

where

\bm{z}=\frac{[\bm{A}^{T}\bm{x}]_{\mathbb{L}}}{\left\lVert[\bm{A}^{T}\bm{x}]_{\mathbb{L}}\right\rVert_{2}}\in\mathbb{D}_{l}.

Using Hölder’s inequality and Lemma
1
completes the proof

\left\lVert[\bm{A}^{T}\bm{x}]_{\mathbb{L}}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\left\lVert\bm{x}\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert\bm{A}\bm{z}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\left\lVert\bm{x}\right\rVert_{2}(\sqrt{m}+2CK^{2}\gamma(\mathbb{D}_{l})).

\square

Lemma 3

Let
\mathbb{D}_{k}=\{\bm{z}\in\mathbbm{R}^{n}\big|\|\bm{z}\|_{0}\leq k,\|\bm{z}\|_{2}=1\}
. Then we have

a)

\gamma(\mathbb{D}_{2k})\leq 2\gamma(\mathbb{D}_{k}),

b)

\gamma(\mathbb{D}_{k}+\mathbb{D}_{k})\leq 2\gamma(\mathbb{D}_{k}),

c)

\mathbb{D}_{k}\subseteq\mathbb{D}_{l}
and
\gamma(\mathbb{D}_{k})\leq\gamma(\mathbb{D}_{l})
for any
k\leq l
.

Theorem 1

Let
\bm{A}
be an
{m\times n}
random matrix as defined in Fact
1
. Let
\bm{x}^{\star}\in\mathbbm{R}^{n}
be a
k
-sparse vector such that
\bm{y}=\bm{A}\bm{x}^{\star}+\bm{n}
. Let
\eta
be the step size satisfying
\eta m\leq 1
.
Then for any sequence
\left\{\bm{x}^{p}\right\}
generated by ROT, one has

\left\lVert\bm{x}^{\star}-\bm{x}^{p}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\rho_{1}\left\lVert\bm{x}^{\star}-\bm{x}^{p-1}\right\rVert_{2}+c_{2}\left\lVert\bm{n}\right\rVert_{2}

(15)

with probability
1-c\exp(-\gamma^{2}(\mathbb{D}_{2k}))
, where
c>0
is a constant and

\displaystyle\rho_{1}=

\displaystyle c_{1}\max\left\{r_{1},r_{2}\right\},~~c_{2}=c_{21}+c_{22},

\displaystyle r_{1}=

\displaystyle 1-\eta m\cdot\frac{\sqrt{m}-12CK^{2}\gamma(\mathbb{D}_{2k})}{\sqrt{m}},

\displaystyle r_{2}=

\displaystyle\frac{6CK^{2}\gamma(\mathbb{D}_{2k})}{\sqrt{m}}\left(\frac{3CK^{2}\gamma(\mathbb{D}_{2k})}{\sqrt{m}}+1\right),

\displaystyle c_{1}=

\displaystyle\frac{4\sqrt{m}+4CK^{2}\gamma(\mathbb{D}_{k})}{\sqrt{m}-2CK^{2}\gamma(\mathbb{D}_{2k})},

\displaystyle c_{21}=

\displaystyle\frac{3\eta(\sqrt{m}+2CK^{2}\gamma(\mathbb{D}_{k}))^{2}+2}{\sqrt{m}-2CK^{2}\gamma(\mathbb{D}_{2k})},

\displaystyle c_{22}=

\displaystyle\eta(\sqrt{m}+2CK^{2}\gamma(\mathbb{D}_{k})).

Let
m
be suitably large satisfying
\rho_{1}<1
. Then
\left\{\bm{x}^{p}\right\}
can approximate
\bm{x}^{\star}
with the error

\|\bm{x}^{\star}-\bm{x}^{p}\|_{2}\leq\rho_{1}^{p}\|\bm{x}^{\star}-\bm{x}^{0}\|_{2}+\frac{c_{2}}{1-\rho_{1}}\left\lVert\bm{n}\right\rVert_{2}

(16)

with probability
1-c^{\prime}\exp(-\gamma^{2}(\mathbb{D}_{2k}))
, where
c^{\prime}>0
is a constant. In particular, when
\bm{n}=\bm{0}
, the sequence
\left\{\bm{x}^{p}\right\}
converges to
\bm{x}^{\star}
.

Sketch of Proof
.

The main step is to bound
\|\bm{x}^{\star}-\bm{x}^{p}\|_{2}
by using the inequality in
[
12
]
:

\displaystyle\|\bm{x}^{\star}-\bm{x}^{p}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\left[\bm{u}^{p-1}\odot\bm{w}(\bm{u}^{p-1})-\bm{x}^{\star}\right]_{\mathbb{S}^{p}\setminus\mathbb{S}}\right\rVert_{2},

\displaystyle\qquad+\left\lVert\left[\bm{u}^{p-1}\odot\bm{w}(\bm{u}^{p-1})-\bm{x}^{\star}\right]_{\mathbb{S}^{p}\cup\mathbb{S}}\right\rVert_{2},

(17)

where
\mathbb{S}^{p}
and
\mathbb{S}
are the support of
\bm{x}^{p}
and
\bm{x}^{\star}
, respectively.

First, we give the upper bound of
\|[\bm{u}^{p-1}\odot\bm{w}(\bm{u}^{p-1})-\bm{x}^{\star}]_{\mathbb{S}^{p}\setminus\mathbb{S}}\|_{2}
. By using
\bm{u}^{p-1}=\bm{x}^{p-1}+\eta\bm{A}^{T}(\bm{y}-\bm{A}\bm{x}^{p-1})
and
\bm{y}=\bm{A}\bm{x}^{\star}+\bm{n}
, we obtain

\bm{x}^{\star}-\bm{u}^{p-1}=(\bm{I}-\eta\bm{A}^{T}\bm{A})(\bm{x}^{\star}-\bm{x}^{p-1})-\eta\bm{A}^{T}\bm{n}.

(18)

Due to the fact that
\bm{x}^{\star}_{\mathbb{S}^{p}\setminus\mathbb{S}}=0
, we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert\left[\bm{u}^{p-1}\odot\bm{w}(\bm{u}^{p-1})-\bm{x}^{\star}\right]_{\mathbb{S}^{p}\setminus\mathbb{S}}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\lVert\left[(\bm{u}^{p-1}-\bm{x}^{\star})\odot\bm{w}(\bm{u}^{p-1})\right]_{\mathbb{S}^{p}\setminus\mathbb{S}}\right\rVert_{2}.

(19)

Using (
18
), the triangle inequality, and
(\bm{w}(\bm{u}^{p-1}))_{i}\in[0,1],i=1,\ldots,n
yields that

\left\lVert\left[\bm{u}^{p-1}\odot\bm{w}(\bm{u}^{p-1})-\bm{x}^{\star}\right]_{\mathbb{S}^{p}\setminus\mathbb{S}}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\eta\left\lVert\left[\bm{A}^{T}\bm{n}\right]_{\mathbb{S}^{p}\setminus\mathbb{S}}\right\rVert_{2}\\
+\left\lVert\left[(\bm{I}-\eta\bm{A}^{T}\bm{A})(\bm{x}^{\star}-\bm{x}^{p-1})\right]_{\mathbb{S}^{p}\setminus\mathbb{S}}\right\rVert_{2},

(20)

By using Lemmas
2
and
3
, we get

\left\lVert\left[\bm{u}^{p-1}\odot\bm{w}(\bm{u}^{p-1})-\bm{x}^{\star}\right]_{\mathbb{S}^{p}\setminus\mathbb{S}}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\Big[\max\left\{r_{1},r_{2}\right\}\\
\cdot\left\lVert\bm{x}^{\star}-\bm{x}^{p-1}\right\rVert_{2}\Big]+\eta\left\lVert\bm{n}\right\rVert_{2}(\sqrt{m}+2CK^{2}\gamma(\mathbb{D}_{k})).

(21)

Next, we provide the upper bound of
\|[\bm{u}^{p-1}\odot\bm{w}(\bm{u}^{p-1})-\bm{x}^{\star}]_{\mathbb{S}^{p}\cup\mathbb{S}}\|_{2}
. Note that

Theorem 2

Let the signal model be defined as in Theorem

. Let

, where

\displaystyle\rho_{2}=

\displaystyle\rho_{1}/\sqrt{1-\left(\max\{r_{1},r_{2}\}\right)^{2}},

\displaystyle c_{3}=

\displaystyle\frac{c_{2}}{\sqrt{1-\left(\max\{r_{1},r_{2}\}\right)^{2}}}+\frac{\eta(\sqrt{m}+2CK^{2}\gamma(\mathbb{D}_{k}))}{\left[1-\left(\max\{r_{1},r_{2}\}\right)\right]}.

can approximate

(26)

with probability

. In particular, if
\bm{n}=\bm{0}

.

(27)

Next, we will relate

\displaystyle\left\lVert\bm{x}^{\star}-\bm{x}^{p}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\left(\bm{x}^{\star}-\bm{x}^{p}\right)_{\mathbb{S}^{p}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\left\lVert\left(\bm{x}^{\star}-\bm{x}^{p}\right)_{\overline{\mathbb{S}^{p}}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

The above theorems show the tradeoffs in ROT or ROTP between the number of measurements and the number of iterations. Note that if
m
is larger than some value, the convergence rate
\rho_{1}
or
\rho_{2}
is less than
1
; if not, the algorithm might diverge. Besides, notice that the value
\rho_{1}
or
\rho_{2}
decreases with the increase of
m
, leading to a fast convergence rate. Therefore, we can conclude that when the number of measurements is large enough, the larger the number of measurements, the faster the convergence rate and hence the smaller number of iterations for fixed accuracy. In particular, let the step size
\eta
be equal to
{1}/{m}
. In this case, the convergence rate satisfying
\rho_{1}<1
for ROT algorithm is ensured by
\sqrt{m}>C^{\prime}CK^{2}\gamma(\mathbb{D}_{2k})
, where
C^{\prime}\approx 50.943
is the positive root of the equation
t^{2}-50t-48=0
. Similarly, in the ROTP algorithm, the convergence rate satisfying
\rho_{2}<1
is guaranteed by
\sqrt{m}>C^{\prime\prime}CK^{2}\gamma(\mathbb{D}_{2k})
, where
C^{\prime\prime}\approx 52.2614
is the positive real root of the equation
t^{4}-4t^{3}-2444t^{2}-4032t-2880=0
.

Note that in the noise-free situation, i.e.,
\bm{n}=\bm{0}
, if the conditions
\sqrt{m}>50.943CK^{2}\gamma(\mathbb{D}_{2k})
and
\eta m\leq 1
hold, the convergence rate in Theorem
1
is strictly less than 1. It means that a linear convergence rate can be achieved by the algorithm ROT. Similarly, the algorithm ROTP also achieves a linear convergence rate when a certain condition holds.

It should be pointed out that the Gaussian complexity
\gamma(\mathbb{D}_{k})
and the Gaussian width
w(\mathbb{D}_{k})
have the same order due to Eq. (
8
). This, combined with Fact
2
, tells us that
\gamma(\mathbb{D}_{k})
is of the order of
\sqrt{k\log({n}/{k})}
, where
n
and
k
are the dimension and sparsity of the target signal, respectively. Based on the main results in Theorems
1
,
2
and the above remarks, it is not hard to see that the number of measurements required for successful recovery is of the order of
k\log({n}/{k})
.

IV
Simulations

In this section, we make simulations to validate the theory. To show the tradeoffs between the number of measurements
m
and the number of iterations

, we draw the phase transitions for ROT and ROTP algorithms. The target signal
\bm{x}^{\star}

-sparse

\bm{x}^{0}
is set to be
\bm{0}

, the sparsity is set to be

{\left\lVert\bm{x}^{\star}-\bm{x}^{p}\right\rVert_{2}}/{\left\lVert\bm{x}^{\star}\right\rVert_{2}}\leq\varepsilon
. Then we calculate the successful probability for each pair and get the phase transition results.

. Note the white area for ROTP algorithm is larger than that for ROT algorithm, which means ROTP algorithm has a better recovery performance than ROT algorithm. III
. The curves present that when the number of measurements is smaller than a certain number, the algorithms cannot recover the target signal. .

V
Conclusion and future work

In this paper, we have provided the analysis for the data-time tradeoffs for optimal
k
-thresholding algorithms in compressed sensing. The theory has shown that the number of measurements should be suitably large to guarantee the convergence of the optimal
k
-thresholding algorithms. It also presented that the number of measurements required for successful reconstruction has a negative correlation with the number of iterations. Simulations have been provided to validate the correctness of our theory.

For future work, it would be interesting to extend the data-time tradeoffs analysis for optimal
k
-thresholding algorithms from compressed sensing to other important linear inverse problems such as quantized compressed sensing, corrupted sensing and so on.

Proof of part a)
: For any
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{D}_{2k}
, there exist two vectors
\bm{y},\bm{z}\in\mathbb{D}_{k}
such that
\bm{x}=(\bm{y}+\bm{z})/\left\lVert\bm{y}+\bm{z}\right\rVert_{2}
and
y_{i}z_{i}=0,i=1,\ldots,n
, which means the locations of non-zeros for
\bm{y}
and
\bm{z}
are different. Then we have

\displaystyle\gamma(\mathbb{D}_{2k})

\displaystyle=\textbf{E}\sup_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{D}_{2k}}|\left\langle\bm{g},\bm{x}\right\rangle|

\displaystyle=\textbf{E}\sup_{(\bm{y}+\bm{z})/\left\lVert\bm{y}+\bm{z}\right\rVert_{2}\in\mathbb{D}_{2k}}\left|\left\langle\bm{g},\frac{\bm{y}+\bm{z}}{\left\lVert\bm{y}+\bm{z}\right\rVert_{2}}\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle=\textbf{E}\sup_{\bm{y}\in\mathbb{D}_{k},\bm{z}\in\mathbb{D}_{k}}\left|\left\langle\bm{g},\frac{\bm{y}+\bm{z}}{\left\lVert\bm{y}+\bm{z}\right\rVert_{2}}\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq\textbf{E}\sup_{\bm{y}\in\mathbb{D}_{k}}\left|\left\langle\bm{g},\frac{\bm{y}}{\left\lVert\bm{y}+\bm{z}\right\rVert_{2}}\right\rangle\right|+\textbf{E}\sup_{\bm{z}\in\mathbb{D}_{k}}\left|\left\langle\bm{g},\frac{\bm{z}}{\left\lVert\bm{y}+\bm{z}\right\rVert_{2}}\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq\textbf{E}\sup_{\bm{y}\in\mathbb{D}_{k}}\left|\left\langle\bm{g},\frac{\bm{y}}{\left\lVert\bm{y}\right\rVert_{2}}\right\rangle\right|+\textbf{E}\sup_{\bm{z}\in\mathbb{D}_{k}}\left|\left\langle\bm{g},\frac{\bm{z}}{\left\lVert\bm{y}\right\rVert_{2}}\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle=\textbf{E}\sup_{\bm{y}\in\mathbb{D}_{k}}\left|\left\langle\bm{g},\bm{y}\right\rangle\right|+\textbf{E}\sup_{\bm{z}\in\mathbb{D}_{k}}\left|\left\langle\bm{g},\bm{z}\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle=2\gamma(\mathbb{D}_{k}),

(A.41)

where the first inequality uses the triangle inequality and the second inequality applies the fact that the locations of non-zeros for
\bm{y}
and
\bm{z}
are totally different.

Proof of part b)
: For any
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{D}_{k}+\mathbb{D}_{k}
, there exists
\bm{y},\bm{z}\in\mathbb{D}_{k}
such that
\bm{x}=\bm{y}+\bm{z}
. So we have

\displaystyle\gamma(\mathbb{D}_{k}+\mathbb{D}_{k})

\displaystyle=\textbf{E}\sup_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{D}_{k}+\mathbb{D}_{k}}|\left\langle\bm{g},\bm{x}\right\rangle|

\displaystyle=\textbf{E}\sup_{\bm{y}\in\mathbb{D}_{k},\bm{z}\in\mathbb{D}_{k}}\left|\left\langle\bm{g},{\bm{y}+\bm{z}}\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq\textbf{E}\sup_{\bm{y}\in\mathbb{D}_{k}}\left|\left\langle\bm{g},{\bm{y}}\right\rangle\right|+\textbf{E}\sup_{\bm{z}\in\mathbb{D}_{k}}\left|\left\langle\bm{g},\bm{z}\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle=2\gamma(\mathbb{D}_{k}).

(A.42)
