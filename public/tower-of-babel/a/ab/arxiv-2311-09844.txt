Observability of the linear Zakharov–Kuznetsov equation
Roberto de A. Capistrano–Filho, Vilmos Komornik, Ademir F. Pazoto

Abstract
We study the linear Zakharov–Kuznetsov equation with periodic boundary conditions. Employing some tools from the nonharmonic Fourier series we obtain several internal observability theorems. Then we prove various exact controllability and rapid uniform stabilization results by applying a duality principle and a general feedback construction. The method presented here introduces a new insight into the control of dispersive equations in two-dimensional cases and may be adapted to more general equations.

Introduction

The Zakharov–Kuznetsov equation

u_{t}+u_{x}+u_{xxx}+u_{xyy}+uu_{x}=0

was originally derived by Zakharov and Kuznetsov
ZK1974
in three dimensions to describe weakly magnetized ion-acoustic waves in a strongly magnetized plasma. The two-dimensional equation still has some physical meaning, modeling the behavior of weakly nonlinear ion-acoustic waves in a plasma comprising cold ions and hot isothermal electrons in the presence of a uniform magnetic field (see
MoPa-99
;
MoPa-00
for the details and
LaLiSa-13
for rigorous mathematical treatment.)

In this paper, we consider the linearized Zakharov–Kuznetsov (ZK) equation

u_{t}+u_{x}+u_{xxx}+u_{xyy}=0

(1.1)

on the two-dimensional torus
\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{T}
, where
u(x,y,t)
denotes a real-valued function of three real variables
x
,
y
and
t
, and
\mathbb{T}
denotes the unit circle. As a higher-dimensional analog of the Korteweg–de Vries equation it has also been extensively studied and the body of literature is huge (see below). In the following, we deliver an overview of the well-posedness and the controllability theory in two dimensions.

1.1
Historical background

The Zakharov–Kuznetsov equation and its linearized version are special cases of a broad class of dispersive equations for which the well-posedness theory associated with the pure initial-value problem posed on the whole plane has been intensively investigated. In this respect, we can mention the works
Faminskii-95
;
Kinoshita-21
;
LinPas-09
;
MolPil-15
and the references therein. As for the periodic case, the authors of the pioneering work
LinPanRo-19
proved that the initial value problem for the Zakharov–Kuznetsov equation is locally well-posed in the Sobolev spaces
H^{s}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{T})
for
s>5/3
. Their main tool was a variant of Strichartz’s estimate. By using a nonlinear Loomis–Whitney type inequality, the assumption
s>5/3
was weakened to
s>1
in
KinSch-21
. More recently, it was shown by Osawa
Osawa-22
that the two-dimensional Zakharov–Kuznetsov equation is also locally well-posed in
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{T})
, for
9/10<s<1
.

By contrast, the mathematical theory pertaining to the study of controllability properties is considerably less advanced, especially in what concerns the two-dimensional linear dispersive-type equations on a periodic setting. To our knowledge, the first result on the subject was obtained by Rivas and Sun in
RiSu-20
, where the authors studied the internal controllability of the Kadomtsev–Petviashvili II equation. More precisely, by applying the Hilbert Uniqueness Method due to Lions
Lions-1988
and the techniques of semiclassical and microlocal analysis, they proved the controllability in
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{T})
from a vertical strip. Additionally, a negative result for the controllability in
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{T})
from a horizontal strip is also shown.

Recently, Pastor and Vielma Leal
LePa-22
applied the moment method
Russell-78
to establish the controllability of several two-dimensional linear dispersive equations posed on the two-dimensional torus in the framework of Sobolev spaces
H^{s}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{T})
with
s\geq 0
. To obtain the results, the author assumes that the control has a specific form and acts on the union of a narrow vertical and a narrow horizontal strip. As an application of the controllability results, they built an appropriate linear bounded operator to show that the resulting closed-loop system is exponentially stable with an arbitrary exponential decay rate. We emphasize that this is a direct consequence of the Hilbert Uniqueness Method mentioned above and the classical principle: Exact controllability implies exponential stabilizability for conservative control systems
Liu-1997
;
Slemrod-1974
. Their analysis also covers the linearized equation (
1.1
). In this context, it is worth mentioning the work
CaGoMu
where Capistrano-Filho et al investigated the nonlinear Kawahara–Kadomtsev–Petviashvili II equation with a damping mechanism
a(x,y)u
, acting as a feedback-damping mechanism, and an anti-damping mechanism, preventing energy decrease. In addition, Rosier and Mo Chen addressed in
MoRo-20
the exact controllability of the linear equation (
1.1
) on a rectangle with a left Dirichlet boundary control by using the flatness approach.

Well-posedness and eigenfunction expansions

We establish the well-posedness of the homogeneous system (
1.2
). We have the following result:

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
s\in\mathbb{R}
.
Given any initial datum
u_{0}\in H^{s}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{T})
, the problem (
1.2
) has a unique solution

u\in C(\mathbb{R};H^{s}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{T}))\cap C^{1}(\mathbb{R};H^{s-3}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{T})).

Furthermore, it is represented by the series

u(x,y,t)=\sum_{m,n\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{m,n}e^{i(mx+ny+\omega_{m,n}t)},\quad\omega_{m,n}:=m^{3}+mn^{2}-m

(2.1)

with suitable complex coefficients
c_{m,n}
, depending on
u_{0}
.

Moreover, the map
u_{S}\mapsto u_{0}
is an isometric automorphism of
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{T})
for every
S\in\mathbb{R}
, and

\left\lVert u(S)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{T})}^{2}=4\pi^{2}\sum_{m,n\in\mathbb{Z}}\left\lvert c_{m,n}\right\rvert^{2}.

Proof.

The proof follows a standard application of linear semigroup theory. The present problem is even simpler because there is an orthonormal base formed of eigenfunctions of the underlying operator

\mathcal{A}u:=-(u_{x}+u_{xxx}+u_{xyy}),\quad u\in H^{s+3}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{T}).

The first part of the theorem readily follows for example by applying
(
KomLor-2005
, Theorem 3.1)
for this operator in the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}=H^{s}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{T})
.

The isometry property is a consequence of Parseval’s formula:

\displaystyle\left\lVert u(S)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{T})}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{T}}\left\lvert\sum_{m,n\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{m,n}e^{i(mx+ny+\omega_{m,n}S)}\right\rvert^{2}\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle=4\pi^{2}\sum_{m,n\in\mathbb{Z}}\left\lvert c_{m,n}e^{i\omega_{m,n}S}\right\rvert^{2}=4\pi^{2}\sum_{m,n\in\mathbb{Z}}\left\lvert c_{m,n}\right\rvert^{2}

for every
S\in\mathbb{R}
.

Finally, each map
u_{S}\mapsto u_{0}
is onto because if
(c_{m,n})_{(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}
runs over
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
, then
(c_{m,n}e^{i\omega_{m,n}S})_{(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}
also runs over
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
.

A short review of Ingham type theorems

First, we recall a theorem of Ingham
Ingham-1936
and its strengthened form given by Beurling
Beurling
and Kahane
Kahane-1962
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
(\lambda_{k})_{k\in K}
be a uniformly separated family of real numbers, i.e., satisfying the following
gap condition
:

\gamma:=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}m,n\in K\\
k\neq n\end{subarray}}\left\lvert\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{n}\right\rvert>0.

Then for each bounded interval
I
of length
\left\lvert I\right\rvert>\frac{2\pi}{\gamma}
there exist two constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
such that every square summable family
(c_{k})_{k\in K}
of complex numbers satisfies the following inequalities:

C_{1}\sum_{k\in K}\left\lvert x_{k}\right\rvert^{2}\leq\int_{I}\left\lvert\sum_{k\in K}c_{k}e^{i\lambda_{k}t}\right\rvert^{2}\,dt\leq C_{2}\sum_{k\in K}\left\lvert x_{k}\right\rvert^{2}.

(3.1)

Moreover, the conclusion holds (with different constants) under the weaker condition

\left\lvert I\right\rvert>\frac{2\pi}{{\gamma(K\setminus L)}}\quad\text{with}\quad{\gamma(K\setminus L)}:=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}m,n\in K\setminus L\\
k\neq n\end{subarray}}\left\lvert\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{n}\right\rvert,

where
L
is any finite subset of
K
.

Example 1
.

The family
(\lambda_{k}:=k^{3})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
satisfies the assumptions of the theorem with
\gamma=1
.
Furthermore, if
L_{m}=\left\{k\in\mathbb{Z}\ :\ |k|<m\right\}
for some integer
m\geq 1
, then

{\gamma(K\setminus L_{m})=3m(m-1)+1}

by a simple verification.
Since
\gamma(K\setminus L_{m})\to+\infty
as
m\to+\infty
, the inequalities (
3.1
) (with
K=\mathbb{Z}
) hold for bounded intervals of arbitrary positive length.

Theorem
3.1
was extended to higher dimensions by Kahane
Kahane-1962
.
We only state here a special case of his results that will be sufficient for our applications. To do that, let us introduce some notations.

Definition
.

Consider a set
\Lambda=\left\{\lambda_{k}\ :\ k\in K\right\}
in a normed space.

\Lambda
is
uniformly separated
if

\gamma(\Lambda):=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}m,n\in K\\
k\neq n\end{subarray}}\left\lVert\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{n}\right\rVert>0.

\Lambda
is
sparse
if for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists a finite cover
\Lambda=\Lambda_{1}\cup\cdots\cup\Lambda_{m}
of
K
such that

\frac{1}{\gamma(\Lambda_{1})}+\cdots+\frac{1}{\gamma(\Lambda_{m})}<\varepsilon.

Remarks 1
.

(i)

It follows from the definition that both properties are invariant for similarities, and they are preserved by taking subsets.

(ii)

The empty set is sparse.
The singletons
\left\{\lambda\right\}
are sparse because
\gamma(\left\{\lambda\right\})=+\infty
by definition.

(iii)

The union of finitely many sparse sets
\Lambda_{1}
, …,
\Lambda_{m}
is still sparse.
Indeed, given
\varepsilon>0
, it suffices to apply the definition to each
\Lambda_{j}
with
\varepsilon/m
instead of
\varepsilon
and then to take the union of all these covers.

(iv)

Every finite set is sparse by (ii) and (iii).

(v)

A sparse set remains sparse in every other equivalent norm.

Let us give a typical nontrivial example:

Example 2
.

The set
\Lambda=\left\{k^{3}\ :\ k\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}
is sparse in
\mathbb{R}
.
Indeed, for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
choose an integer
m>1/3\varepsilon
, and consider the finite cover
\Lambda_{1},\ldots,\Lambda_{2m+1}
of
\Lambda
by the sets

\left\{k^{3}\ :\ k\geq m\right\},\quad\left\{k^{3}\ :\ k\leq-m\right\}

and the singletons
\left\{j^{3}\right\}
for
j=-m+1,\ldots,m-1
.
The first set has a uniform gap
>3m^{2}
because if
k_{1}>k_{2}\geq m
, then

k_{1}^{3}-k_{2}^{3}\geq(k_{2}+1)^{3}-k_{2}^{3}=3k_{2}^{2}+3k_{2}+1>{3m^{2}.}

The second set is a reflection of
\Lambda_{1}
, hence it has the same uniform gap.
Since the singletons have infinite uniform gaps, we conclude that

\sum_{j=1}^{2m+1}\frac{1}{\gamma(\Lambda_{j})}<\frac{1}{{3m^{2}}}+\frac{1}{{3m^{2}}}+\frac{1}{+\infty}+\cdots+\frac{1}{+\infty}={\frac{2}{3m^{2}}}<\varepsilon.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
(\lambda_{k})_{k\in K}
be a uniformly separated and sparse family of vectors in

C_{1},C_{2}>0
such that

C_{1}\sum_{k\in K}\left\lvert x_{k}\right\rvert^{2}\leq\int_{G}\left\lvert\sum_{k\in K}c_{k}e^{i\lambda_{k}\cdot z}\right\rvert^{2}\,dz\leq C_{2}\sum_{k\in K}\left\lvert x_{k}\right\rvert^{2},

for every square summable family
(x_{k})_{k\in K}
of complex numbers.

4
Observability on segments

This section is dedicated to giving the proof of Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
. Let us start with the proof of Theorem
1.1
. We will apply Ingham’s inequality infinitely many times, by ensuring the existence of the same constants for each application. This kind of proof was first applied in
Haraux-1989
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Fix an integer
N\geq 1
such that
\frac{2\pi}{N^{2}}<|I|
.
For each fixed
n
with
\left\lvert n\right\rvert\geq N
the sequence

(\omega_{m,n})_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}=(m^{3}+mn^{2}-m)_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}

has a uniform gap
\geq N^{2}
because

(m+1)^{3}+(m+1)n^{2}-(m+1)-(m^{3}+mn^{2}-m)=3m(m+1)+n^{2}\geq n^{2}\geq N^{2},

for every
m\in\mathbb{Z}
.

It follows that we may apply Ingham’s theorem for each of these sequences with common uniform constants
C_{1}^{\prime},C_{2}^{\prime}>0
, depending only on
|I|
:

C_{1}^{\prime}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\left\lvert c_{m,n}\right\rvert^{2}\leq\int_{I}\left\lvert\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{m,n}e^{i(mx_{0}+\omega_{m,n}t)}\right\rvert^{2}\,dt\leq C_{2}^{\prime}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\left\lvert c_{m,n}\right\rvert^{2},

(4.1)

for each fixed
n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\left\{-N+1,\ldots,N-1\right\}
, in the previous inequality we have used the following trivial equality
\left\lvert c_{m,n}e^{imx_{0}}\right\rvert=\left\lvert c_{m,n}\right\rvert
.

For each of the remaining
2N-1
values of
n
, the truncated sequence

(m^{3}+mn^{2}-m)_{\left\lvert m\right\rvert\geq N+1}

also has a uniform gap
\geq N^{2}
because

3m(m+1)+n^{2}=3|m|\cdot|m+1|+n^{2}\geq N^{2},

whenever
\left\lvert m\right\rvert\geq N+1
and thus
\left\lvert m+1\right\rvert\geq N
.
Applying the last part of Theorem
3.1
, there exist suitable constants
C_{1,n}^{\prime},C_{2,n}^{\prime}>0
, depending only on
|I|
, such that

C_{1,n}^{\prime}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\left\lvert c_{m,n}\right\rvert^{2}\leq\int_{I}\left\lvert\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{m,n}e^{i(mx_{0}+\omega_{m,n}t)}\right\rvert^{2}\,dt\leq C_{2,n}^{\prime}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\left\lvert c_{m,n}\right\rvert^{2},

(4.2)

for each fixed
n\in\left\{-N+1,\ldots,N-1\right\}
.

Setting

C_{1}^{\prime\prime}:=\min\left\{C_{1}^{\prime},C_{1,1-N}^{\prime},\ldots,C_{1,N-1}^{\prime}\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad C_{2}^{\prime\prime}:=\max\left\{C_{2}^{\prime},C_{2,1-N}^{\prime},\ldots,C_{2,N-1}^{\prime}\right\},

and, adding the equations (
4.1
) and (
4.2
), we obtain that

C_{1}^{\prime\prime}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\left\lvert c_{m,n}\right\rvert^{2}\leq\int_{I}\left\lvert\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{m,n}e^{i(mx_{0}+\omega_{m,n}t)}\right\rvert^{2}\,dt\leq C_{2}^{\prime\prime}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\left\lvert c_{m,n}\right\rvert^{2},

for each
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Summing them we obtain the following estimate:

C_{1}^{\prime\prime}\sum_{m,n\in\mathbb{Z}}\left\lvert c_{m,n}\right\rvert^{2}\leq\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{I}\left\lvert\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{m,n}e^{i(mx_{0}+\omega_{m,n}t)}\right\rvert^{2}\,dt\leq C_{2}^{\prime\prime}\sum_{m,n\in\mathbb{Z}}\left\lvert c_{m,n}\right\rvert^{2}.

(4.3)

Since

\displaystyle\int_{I}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left\lvert u(x_{0},y,t)\right\rvert^{2}\ dy\ dt

\displaystyle=\int_{I}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left\lvert\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{iny}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{m,n}e^{i(mx_{0}+\omega_{m,n}t)}\right\rvert^{2}\ dy\ dt

\displaystyle=2\pi\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{I}\left\lvert\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{m,n}e^{i(mx_{0}+\omega_{m,n}t)}\right\rvert^{2}\ dt

by Parseval’s formula, and

\left\lVert u_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{T})}^{2}=4\pi^{2}\sum_{m,n\in\mathbb{Z}}\left\lvert c_{m,n}\right\rvert^{2}

by Proposition
2.1
, the estimates (
1.4
) of Theorem
1.1
follow from (
4.3
) with
C_{1}=C_{1}^{\prime\prime}/2\pi
and
C_{2}=C_{2}^{\prime\prime}/2\pi
.
∎

Now we turn to the proof of Theorem
1.2
.
Since we consider only initial data belonging to
L^{2}_{x}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{T})
, all coefficients
c_{0,n}
vanish in the series representation (
2.1
) of the solutions.
Since
\omega_{m,n}=\omega_{m,-n}
, we have for any fixed
y_{0}\in\mathbb{T}
and for every non-degenerate bounded open interval
I
the following identity by a similar computation as in the preceding proof:

\displaystyle\int_{I}\int_{\mathbb{T}}

\displaystyle\left\lvert u(x,y_{0},t)\right\rvert^{2}\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{I}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left\lvert\sum_{{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}}}e^{imx}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{m,n}e^{i(ny_{0}+\omega_{m,n}t)}\right\rvert^{2}\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle=2\pi\sum_{{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}}}\int_{I}\left\lvert\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{m,n}e^{i(ny_{0}+\omega_{m,n}t)}\right\rvert^{2}\,dt

\displaystyle=2\pi\sum_{{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}}}\int_{I}\left\lvert c_{m,0}{e^{i\omega_{m,0}t}}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(c_{m,n}e^{iny_{0}}+c_{m,-n}e^{-iny_{0}}\right)e^{i\omega_{m,n}t}\right\rvert^{2}\,dt.

Applying Ingham’s theorem to the last expression as in the proof of Theorem
1.1
, we obtain the equivalence

\int_{I}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left\lvert u(x,y_{0},t)\right\rvert^{2}\,dx\,dt\asymp\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}}\left(\left\lvert c_{m,0}\right\rvert^{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left\lvert c_{m,n}e^{iny_{0}}+c_{m,-n}e^{-iny_{0}}\right\rvert^{2}\right).

This shows that the observation on one horizontal segment does not allow us to determine separately
c_{m,n}
and
c_{m,-n}
, but only a linear combination of them.

If we observe the solution on two horizontal segments on identical or different time intervals
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
, then we obtain that

\int_{I_{1}}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left\lvert u(x,y_{1},t)\right\rvert^{2}\,dx\,dt+\int_{I_{2}}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left\lvert u(x,y_{2},t)\right\rvert^{2}\,dx\,dt

(4.4)

is equivalent to

\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}}\left(\left\lvert c_{m,0}\right\rvert^{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\left\lvert c_{m,n}e^{iny_{1}}+c_{m,-n}e^{-iny_{1}}\right\rvert^{2}+\left\lvert c_{m,n}e^{iny_{2}}+c_{m,-n}e^{-iny_{2}}\right\rvert^{2}\right)\right),

or to

\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}}\left(\left\lvert c_{m,0}\right\rvert^{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\left\lvert c_{m,n}e^{2iny_{1}}+c_{m,-n}\right\rvert^{2}+\left\lvert c_{m,n}e^{2iny_{2}}+c_{m,-n}\right\rvert^{2}\right)\right).

(4.5)

With this in hand, we are now in a position to prove the second main result of this article.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

(i) In view of the equivalence between (
4.4
) and (
4.5
) it suffices to show the implications

c_{m,n}e^{2iny_{1}}+c_{m,-n}=c_{m,n}e^{2iny_{2}}+c_{m,-n}=0\Longrightarrow c_{m,n}=c_{m,-n}=0.

This holds if and only if
e^{2iny_{1}}\neq e^{2iny_{2}}
, that is, if
(y_{1}-y_{2})/\pi
is irrational, showing item (i).

(ii) Let us first assume that
N=1
, i.e., we observe the solution on two segments.
Then we have to prove the following estimate with some constant
\gamma>0
:

\int_{I_{1}}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left\lvert u(x,y_{1},t)\right\rvert^{2}\,dx\,dt+\int_{I_{2}}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left\lvert u(x,y_{2},t)\right\rvert^{2}\,dx\,dt\geq\gamma\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{\left\lvert c_{m,n}\right\rvert^{2}}{n^{2}}.

(4.6)

Since
(y_{1}-y_{2})/\pi
is irrational,
e^{2iny_{1}}\neq e^{2iny_{2}}
for every positive integer
n
.
Therefore we may express
c_{m,n}
and
c_{m,-n}
by

a_{m,n}:=c_{m,n}e^{2iny_{1}}+c_{m,-n}\quad\text{and}\quad b_{m,n}:=c_{m,n}e^{2iny_{2}}+c_{m,-n}.

We obtain the equalities

c_{m,n}=\frac{a_{m,n}-b_{m,n}}{e^{2iny_{1}}-e^{2iny_{2}}}\quad\text{and}\quad c_{m,-n}=\frac{b_{m,n}e^{2iny_{1}}-a_{m,n}e^{2iny_{2}}}{e^{2iny_{1}}-e^{2iny_{2}}},

and they imply the following estimates:

\left\lvert c_{m,n}\right\rvert^{2}+\left\lvert c_{m,-n}\right\rvert^{2}\leq 4\frac{\left\lvert a_{m,n}\right\rvert^{2}+\left\lvert b_{m,-n}\right\rvert^{2}}{\left\lvert e^{2iny_{1}}-e^{2iny_{2}}\right\rvert^{2}}.

We may write it in the following equivalent form:

\left\lvert c_{m,n}e^{2iny_{1}}+c_{m,-n}\right\rvert^{2}+\left\lvert c_{m,n}e^{2iny_{2}}+c_{m,-n}\right\rvert^{2}\geq\frac{\left\lvert e^{2in(y_{1}-y_{2})}-1\right\rvert^{2}}{4}\left(\left\lvert c_{m,n}\right\rvert^{2}+\left\lvert c_{m,-n}\right\rvert^{2}\right).

Thanks to the fact that

\left\lvert e^{2\pi i\theta}-1\right\rvert\geq 2\gamma_{1}\dist(\theta,\mathbb{Z}),

for all real numbers
\theta
with some constant
\gamma_{1}>0
, this yields

\left\lvert c_{m,n}e^{2iny_{1}}+c_{m,-n}\right\rvert^{2}+\left\lvert c_{m,n}e^{2iny_{2}}+c_{m,-n}\right\rvert^{2}\\
\geq\gamma_{1}^{2}\left(\dist\left(\frac{n(y_{1}-y_{2})}{\pi},\mathbb{Z}\right)\right)^{2}\left(\left\lvert c_{m,n}\right\rvert^{2}+\left\lvert c_{m,-n}\right\rvert^{2}\right).

Now we recall, e.g., from
(
Cassels-1957
, Case
n=1
of Theorem III on p. 79)
that if
\theta
is a quadratic irrational algebraic number, then there exists a constant
\gamma_{2}>0
such that

\dist(n\theta,\mathbb{Z})\geq\frac{\gamma_{2}}{n},

for every integer
n\geq 1
.
Applying this with
\theta=(y_{1}-y_{2})/\pi
we obtain the following estimate:

\left\lvert c_{m,n}e^{2iny_{1}}+c_{m,-n}\right\rvert^{2}+\left\lvert c_{m,n}e^{2iny_{2}}+c_{m,-n}\right\rvert^{2}\geq\frac{\gamma_{1}^{2}\gamma_{2}^{2}}{n^{2}}\left(\left\lvert c_{m,n}\right\rvert^{2}+\left\lvert c_{m,-n}\right\rvert^{2}\right).

Using this inequality the required estimate (
4.6
) follows from the equivalence of the expressions (
4.4
) and (
4.5
).

Under the weaker assumption that
(y_{1}-y_{2})/\pi
is an irrational algebraic number, we may repeat the preceding proof, by using at the end Roth’s theorem (see. e.g.,
(
Cassels-1957
, Theorem I on p. 104)
):

\dist(n\theta,\mathbb{Z})\geq\frac{\gamma_{s}}{n^{s}},

for every integer
n\geq 1
.
This yields the theorem mentioned in Remark
1
.

Now let us turn to the case
N\geq 2
.
Applying the first part of the proof for
N=1
with
y_{1}-y_{j}
for
j=2,\ldots,N+1
we obtain the inequality

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N+1}\int_{I_{j}}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left\lVert u(x,y_{j},t)\right\rVert^{2}\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle\geq\gamma_{1}^{2}\sum_{j=2}^{N+1}\left(\dist(n(y_{1}-y_{j})/\pi,\mathbb{Z})\right)^{2}\left(\left\lvert c_{m,n}\right\rvert^{2}+\left\lvert c_{m,-n}\right\rvert^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\gamma_{1}^{2}\max_{j=2}^{N+1}\left\{\dist(n(y_{1}-y_{j})/\pi,\mathbb{Z})\right\}^{2}\left(\left\lvert c_{m,n}\right\rvert^{2}+\left\lvert c_{m,-n}\right\rvert^{2}\right).

Thanks to our assumptions on
y_{1},\ldots,y_{N+1}
we may apply
(
Cassels-1957
, Theorem III on p. 79)
to obtain the inequality

\max_{j=2}^{N+1}\left\{\dist(n(y_{1}-y_{j})/\pi,\mathbb{Z})\right\}\geq\gamma_{2}n^{-1/N},

for all
n
.
Therefore we have

\sum_{j=1}^{N+1}\int_{I_{j}}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left\lVert u(x,y_{j},t)\right\rVert^{2}\,dx\,dt\geq\gamma_{1}^{2}\gamma_{2}^{2}n^{-2/N},

for all integers
n\geq 1
, so the proof of item (ii) is achieved, and Theorem
1.2
is proved.
∎

5
Observability on small balls

This section deals with the presentation of the proof of Theorem
1.3
. In this theorem, we consider only initial data belonging to
L^{2}_{x}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{T}):=H^{0,0}_{x}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{T})
.
Then the terms with
m=0
are missing in formula (
2.1
), so that the solution of (
1.2
) is given by

u(x,y,t)=\sum_{(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}\times\mathbb{Z}}c_{m,n}e^{i(mx+ny+\omega_{m,n}t)},\quad\omega_{m,n}:=m^{3}+mn^{2}-m,

with square summable complex coefficients
c_{m,n}
.

In view of Theorem
3.2
, Theorem
1.3
will be proved if we establish the following result:

Proposition 5.1
.

The set

\Lambda=\left\{\lambda_{m,n}:=(m,n,m^{3}+mn^{2}-m)\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}\ :\ (m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}\times\mathbb{Z}\right\}

is uniformly separated and sparse.

For simplicity, we will consider the usual Euclidean norm of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
; this is not essential because all other norms are equivalent to this one.
We recall from Remark 1 (v) that a sparse set remains sparse in every other equivalent norm.
Then
\Lambda
is obviously uniformly separated with
\gamma(\Lambda)\geq 1
. We now prove that
\Lambda
is sparse. To do that, we need four lemmas.

Lemma 5.2
.

A set
\Lambda
in a normed space is sparse if it has the following property: for every
R>0
there exists a sparse set
\Lambda^{\prime}
such that
\gamma(\Lambda\setminus\Lambda^{\prime})\geq R
.

Proof.

For any fixed
\varepsilon>0
choose
R>2/\varepsilon
, and let
\Lambda_{1}
, …,
\Lambda_{m}
be a finite cover of the corresponding sparse set
\Lambda^{\prime}
such that

\frac{1}{\gamma(\Lambda_{1})}+\cdots+\frac{1}{\gamma(\Lambda_{m})}<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

Then the finitely many sets
\Lambda_{1},\ldots,\Lambda_{m},\Lambda^{\prime}
cover of
\Lambda
, and

\frac{1}{\gamma(\Lambda_{1})}+\cdots+\frac{1}{\gamma(\Lambda_{m})}+\frac{1}{\gamma(\Lambda\setminus\Lambda^{\prime})}<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\frac{1}{R}\leq\varepsilon.\qed

The following is a generalization of Example
2
:

Lemma 5.3
.

Consider a set of the form

\Lambda=\left\{(g(m),f(m))\ :\ m\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N},

where
g:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}^{N-1}
is an arbitrary function, and
f:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}
is a function satisfying the limit relation

\lim_{|m|\to-\infty}\left(f(m+1)-f(m)\right)=+\infty.

(5.1)

Then
\Lambda
is sparse. The same conclusion holds if the limit is
-\infty
.

Proof.

As mentioned before, we consider the Euclidean norm of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, so that

\left\lVert(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})\right\rVert\geq\max\left\{\left\lvert x_{1}\right\rvert,\ldots,\left\lvert x_{N}\right\rvert\right\}.

It follows from our assumptions that

\lim_{m\to-\infty}f(m)=-\infty\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{m\to+\infty}f(m)=+\infty.

Therefore, for any fixed
R>0
we may choose an integer
n\geq 1
such that

\displaystyle f(n)-f(-n)\geq R,

\displaystyle f(m+1)-f(m)\geq R\quad\text{for all}\quad m\leq-n,\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle f(m+1)-f(m)\geq R\quad\text{for all}\quad m\geq n.

In particular,
f
is increasing in the intervals
(-\infty,-n]
and
[n,+\infty)
.
We complete the proof of the first part by showing that the set

\Lambda^{\prime}:=\left\{(g(m),f(m))\ :\ |m|<n\right\}

satisfies the hypotheses of Lemma
5.2
.

Indeed, first of all,
\Lambda\setminus\Lambda^{\prime}
is a finite set, hence it is sparse.
It remains to show that if
|m_{1}|\geq n
,
|m_{2}|\geq n
and
m_{1}\neq m_{2}
, then

\left\lVert(g(m_{1}),f(m_{1}))-(g(m_{2}),f(m_{2}))\right\rVert\geq R.

We may assume by symmetry that
m_{1}>m_{2}
, so that
m_{1}\geq m_{2}+1
.

We are going to prove the stronger inequality
f(m_{1})-f(m_{2})\geq R
.
It is satisfied if
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
have different signs, because then

f(m_{1})-f(m_{2})\geq f(n)-f(-n)\geq R.

If
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
have equal signs, then

f(m_{1})-f(m_{2})\geq f(m_{2}+1)-f(m_{2})\geq R.

If the limit is
-\infty
is the assumption of the lemma, then
-\Lambda
is sparse by the first part, and then its reflection
\Lambda
is also sparse.
∎

Based on Lemmas
5.2
,
5.3
and on the fact that a finite union of sparse sets is still sparse, our strategy for the proof of Proposition
5.1
is the following: for any given
R>0
we will construct a finite number of sparse sets
\Lambda_{k,\ell,R}
of the type studied in Lemma
5.3
such that their union
\Lambda^{\prime}
satisfies the uniform gap condition
\gamma(\Lambda\setminus\Lambda^{\prime})\geq R
.

The construction of the sets
\Lambda_{k,\ell,R}
is inspired by a method developed by Jaffard
Jaffard-1990
for the proof of the interior observability of rectangular plates.

To do that, we will need some preliminary technical results.
If

\displaystyle\lambda_{m,n}=(m,n,m^{3}+mn^{2}-m)

and

\displaystyle\lambda_{m+k,n+\ell}=\left({m+k},n+\ell,(m+k)^{3}+(m+k)(n+\ell)^{2}-(m+k)\right),

are two distinct elements of

\Lambda=\left\{\lambda_{m,n}:=(m,n,m^{3}+mn^{2}-m)\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}\ :\ (m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}\times\mathbb{Z}\right\},

then
(k,\ell)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\setminus\left\{(0,0)\right\}
, and

\lambda_{m+k,n+\ell}-\lambda_{m,n}=(k,\ell,Q(k,\ell,m,n))

(5.2)

with

Q(k,\ell,m,n):=k(3m^{2}+n^{2})+2\ell mn+(3k^{2}+\ell^{2})m+{k(2\ell n+k^{2}+\ell^{2}-1).}

Lemma 5.4
.

The polynomial
Q(k,\ell,m,n)
satisfies the following relation:

Q(k,\ell,m,n)=k(3m^{2}+n^{2})+2\ell mn+o\left(m^{2}+n^{2}\right)\quad\text{as}\quad\left\lVert(m,n)\right\rVert\to+\infty.

(5.3)

Furthermore, we have

Q(k,\ell,m,n)=m\ell(2n+\ell)\quad\text{if}\quad k=0,

(5.4)

and

\begin{split}3kQ(k,\ell,m,n)=&\left(3k\left(m+\frac{k}{2}\right)+\ell\left(n+\frac{\ell}{2}\right)\right)^{2}\\
&-(\ell^{2}-3k^{2})\left(n+\frac{\ell}{2}\right)^{2}+\frac{3k^{2}}{4}\left(k^{2}+\ell^{2}-4\right)\end{split}

(5.5)

in the general case.

Proof.

The relations (
5.3
) and (
5.4
) can be obtained according to the classical procedure to obtain the canonical form a quadratic form. Note that we may write
3kQ(k,\ell,m,n)
in the form

(3k+\ell n+c_{1})^{2}+c_{2}(n+c_{3})^{2}+c_{4},

with suitable constants
c_{1},\ldots,c_{4}
. An elementary computation allows us to determine these constants, leading to the identity (
5.5
).
∎

In order to estimate the norms
\lambda_{m+k,n+\ell}-\lambda_{m,n}
by (
5.2
), first we investigate the sets

B_{k,\ell,R}:=\left\{(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}\times\mathbb{Z}\ :\ \left\lvert Q(k,\ell,m,n)\right\rvert<R\right\},

for
(k,\ell)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\setminus\left\{(0,0)\right\}
and
R>0
.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
(k,\ell)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\setminus\left\{(0,0)\right\}
and
R>0
.

(i)

If
3k^{2}>\ell^{2}
, then the set
B_{k,\ell,R}
is finite.

(ii)

If
k=0
and
\ell
is even, then
B_{k,\ell,R}
is covered by a finite set and the sequence
\left(m,-\frac{\ell}{2}\right)
, with
m\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}
.

(iii)

If
k=0
and
\ell
is odd, then
B_{k,\ell,R}
is finite.

(iv)

If
0<3k^{2}<\ell^{2}
, then
B_{k,\ell,R}
is covered by a finite set and two sequences
(m_{1}(n),n)_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
and
(m_{2}(n),n)_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
satisfying the relations

m_{j}(n)=\alpha_{j}n+\beta_{j}+o(1),\quad|n|\to+\infty,\quad j=1,2

(5.6)

with suitable constants
\alpha_{j}\neq 0
and
\beta_{j}
(see Figure
1
for an example of this case).

It is clear that the four cases of Lemma
5.5
correspond to all the possible situations.

Proof.

(i) If
3k^{2}>\ell^{2}

of

Then we infer from (

) the relation

\left\lvert Q(k,\ell,m,n)\right\rvert\to+\infty\quad\text{as}\quad\left\lVert(m,n)\right\rVert\to+\infty,

so that

is a finite set.

(ii) If
k=0
, then
\ell\neq 0
and

Q(k,\ell,m,n)=m\ell(2n+\ell)

by (

).
This expression vanishes if
\ell
is even and

, so that
\mathbb{Z}^{*}\times\left\{-\ell/2\right\}\subset B_{k,\ell,R}
in this case.

Since

by our assumption, if

, then

\left\lvert Q(k,\ell,m,n)\right\rvert\geq\min\left\{|m|,|\ell(2n+\ell)|\right\},

and the last expression becomes larger than
R
when
\left\lVert(m,n)\right\rVert\to+\infty
is sufficiently large.
Therefore
B_{k,\ell,R}
is covered by
\mathbb{Z}^{*}\times\left\{-\ell/2\right\}
and a finite set.

(iii) We may repeat the proof of (ii).
Since now
\ell
is odd,
\mathbb{Z}^{*}\times\left\{-\ell/2\right\}
is disjoint from
B_{k,\ell,R}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, and hence
\mathbb{Z}^{*}\times\left\{-\ell/2\right\}
is not necessary for the covering of
B_{k,\ell,R}
.

(iv) Setting

y=3k\left(m+\frac{k}{2}\right)+\ell\left(n+\frac{\ell}{2}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad x=\sqrt{\ell^{2}-3k^{2}}\left(n+\frac{\ell}{2}\right)

for brevity, (
5.5
) may be written in the form

3kQ(k,\ell,m,n)=y^{2}-x^{2}+\frac{3k^{2}}{4}\left(k^{2}+\ell^{2}-4\right).

Using this equality, the condition
\left\lvert Q(k,\ell,m,n)\right\rvert<R
in the definition of
B_{k,\ell,R}
implies the relation

\left\lvert y^{2}-x^{2}\right\rvert<3|k|R+\frac{3k^{2}}{4}\left\lvert k^{2}+\ell^{2}-4\right\rvert=:C,

or equivalently

-C<y^{2}-x^{2}<C.

Observe that
|x|\to+\infty\Longleftrightarrow|y|\to+\infty
in this region (see Figure
2
).

Figure 2:
The region
-9<y^{2}-x^{2}<9

Hence either

\displaystyle|x|<\sqrt{C}\quad\text{and}\quad|y|<\sqrt{2C},

or

\displaystyle|x|\geq\sqrt{C}\quad\text{and}\quad\sqrt{x^{2}-C}<|y|<\sqrt{x^{2}+C}.

Observe that the set of these vectors
(x,y)
shrinks to asymptotes
y=\pm x
of the corresponding hyperbola because, as
|x|\to+\infty
,

\Bigl\lvert|y|-|x|\Bigr\rvert<\sqrt{x^{2}+C}-\sqrt{x^{2}-C}=\frac{2C}{\sqrt{x^{2}+C}+\sqrt{x^{2}-C}}\to 0.

We may also write it in the following form:

\min\left\{\left\lvert y-x\right\rvert,\left\lvert y+x\right\rvert\right\}=o(1)\quad\text{as}\quad|x|\to+\infty.

Returning to the variables
m,n
, dividing by
3k
, and introducing the sequences

\widetilde{m_{1}}(n):=\frac{\sqrt{\ell^{2}-3k^{2}}-\ell}{3k}\left(n+\frac{\ell}{2}\right)-\frac{k}{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\widetilde{m_{2}}(n):=\frac{-\sqrt{\ell^{2}-3k^{2}}-\ell}{3k}\left(n+\frac{\ell}{2}\right)-\frac{k}{2},

the last relation becomes

\min\left\{\left\lvert m-\widetilde{m_{1}}(n)\right\rvert,\left\lvert m-\widetilde{m_{2}}(n)\right\rvert\right\}=o(1)\quad\text{as}\quad|n|\to+\infty;

(5.7)

because the relation
|n|\to+\infty
implies
|x|\to+\infty
.
If
|n|
is sufficiently large, say
|n|>N
, then

\min\left\{\left\lvert m-\widetilde{m_{1}}(n)\right\rvert,\left\lvert m-\widetilde{m_{2}}(n)\right\rvert\right\}<\frac{1}{2}.

Observe that at most two integers
m
may satisfy this inequality: the closest integers to
\widetilde{m_{1}}(n)
and
\widetilde{m_{2}}(n)
.
Denoting these closest integers by
m_{1}(n)
and
m_{2}(n)
if
|n|>N
, and defining
m_{j}(n):=\widetilde{m_{j}}(n)
otherwise, the set

\left\{(m,n)\in B_{k,\ell,R}\ :\ |n|>N\right\}

is covered by the two sequences
(m_{1}(n),n)_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
and
(m_{2}(n),n)_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
.
Furthermore, in view of (
5.7
) and the definition of the sequences
(\widetilde{m_{j}}(n))
the relations (
5.6
) are satisfied with

\alpha_{j}=\frac{\pm\sqrt{\ell^{2}-3k^{2}}-\ell}{3k}\quad\text{and}\quad\beta_{j}=\frac{\alpha_{j}\ell-k}{2},\quad j=1,2.

Note that
\alpha_{j}\neq 0
.

To complete the proof it remains to show that the set

\left\{(m,n)\in B_{k,\ell,R}\ :\ |n|\leq N\right\}

is finite.
This follows by observing that for any fixed
n
, by (
5.5
) at most finitely many integers
m
may satisfy the inequality
\left\lvert Q(k,\ell,m,n)\right\rvert<R
, showing the item (iv).
∎

As a consequence of the previous lemma, we have the following:

Corollary 5.6
.

The sets

\Lambda_{k,\ell,R}:=\left\{\lambda_{m,n}\ :\ (m,n)\in B_{k,\ell,R}\right\}

are sparse for all
(k,\ell)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\setminus\left\{(0,0)\right\}
and
R>0
.

Proof.

We distinguish the four cases of Lemma
5.5
.

(i) If
3k^{2}>\ell^{2}
, then the set
B_{k,\ell,R}
is finite, so that
\Lambda_{k,\ell,R}
is finite, too, and hence it is sparse.

(ii) If
k=0
, then
B_{k,\ell,R}
is covered by a finite set and the sequence

\left(m,-\frac{\ell}{2}\right),\quad m\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}.

Hence it is sufficient to prove that the set

\left\{\left(m,-\frac{\ell}{2},m^{3}+\frac{m\ell^{2}}{4}-1\right)\ :\ m\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}

is sparse.
This follows from Lemma
5.3
with
N=3
and
f(m)=m^{3}+\frac{m\ell^{2}}{4}-1
.
The assumption (
5.1
) of the lemma is satisfied because

f(m+1)-f(m)=3m(m+1)+1+\frac{\ell^{2}}{4}\to+\infty\quad\text{as}\quad|m|\to+\infty.

(iii) As in case (i),
B_{k,\ell,R}
is finite, so that
\Lambda_{k,\ell,R}
is finite, and hence sparse.

(iv) Similarly to the case (ii), it suffices to show that the two sets

S_{j}:=\left\{(m_{j}(n),n,m_{j}(n)^{3}+m_{j}(n)n^{2}-m_{j}(n))\ :\ n\in\mathbb{Z}\right\},\quad j=1,2

are sparse.
We are going to apply Lemma
5.3
, so let us rewrite these sets in the form

S_{j}=\left\{(g_{j}(n),f_{j}(n))\ :\ n\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}

with

g_{j}(n)=(m_{j}(n),n)\quad\text{and}\quad f_{j}(n)=m_{j}(n)^{3}+m_{j}(n)n^{2}-m_{j}(n).

Since the following treatment is identical for both values of
j
, we omit the subscript
j
for simplicity.
By Lemma
5.5
(iv) there exist two constants
\alpha\neq 0
and
\beta
such that

m(n)=\alpha n+\beta+o(1)\quad\text{as}\quad|n|\to+\infty.

Using this we obtain the relation (henceforth all relations are understood for
|n|\to+\infty
)

f(n)=m(n)^{3}+m(n)n^{2}-m(n)=(\alpha^{3}+\alpha)n^{3}+(3\alpha^{2}\beta+\beta)n^{2}+o(n^{2}).

It follows that

f(n+1)=(\alpha^{3}+\alpha)(n^{3}+3n^{2})+(3\alpha^{2}\beta+\beta)n^{2}+o(n^{2}),

and therefore

f(n+1)-f(n)=3(\alpha^{3}+\alpha)n^{2}+o(n^{2}).

Since
3(\alpha^{3}+\alpha)\neq 0
by our condition
\alpha\neq 0
, the assumption (
5.1
) of Lemma
5.3
is satisfied, and we conclude that
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are sparse sets.
∎

Now we are ready to prove the main proposition of this section which ensures that Theorem
1.3
holds.

Proof of Proposition
5.1
.

Fix a real number
R>0
, and consider the sets

\Lambda_{k,\ell,R}
, where
(k,\ell)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\setminus\left\{(0,0)\right\}
,
|k|<R
and
|\ell|<R
.
They are sparse by Corollary
5.6
, therefore their union
\Lambda^{\prime}
is also sparse.
By Lemma
5.2
it remains to show that
\gamma(\Lambda\setminus\Lambda^{\prime})\geq R
.

If
\lambda_{m+k,n+\ell}
and
\lambda_{m,n}
are two distinct elements of
\Lambda\setminus\Lambda^{\prime}
, then (see (
5.2
))

\left\lVert\lambda_{m+k,n+\ell}-\lambda_{m,n}\right\rVert=\left\lVert(k,\ell,Q(k,\ell,m,n))\right\rVert\geq\max\left\{|k|,|\ell|,|Q(k,\ell,m,n)|\right\}.

This obviously yields
\left\lVert\lambda_{m+k,n+\ell}-\lambda_{m,n}\right\rVert\geq R
if
|k|\geq R
or
|\ell|\geq R
.
Otherwise, we infer from the definition of
\Lambda^{\prime}
that

\left\lVert\lambda_{m+k,n+\ell}-\lambda_{m,n}\right\rVert\geq|Q(k,\ell,m,n)|\geq R.

This proves the relation
\gamma(\Lambda\setminus\Lambda^{\prime})\geq R
.
∎

6
A review on observation, control and stabilization

Consider a linear evolutionary problem

z^{\prime}=\mathcal{A}z+\mathcal{B}v,\quad z(0)=z_{0},

(6.1)

where
\mathcal{A}
is a densely defined, closed linear operator in some Hilbert space
H
, and
\mathcal{B}
is a bounded linear operator of another Hilbert space
G
into
D(\mathcal{A}^{*})^{\prime}
.
Let us also consider the dual problem

u^{\prime}=-\mathcal{A}^{*}u,\quad u(0)=u_{0},\quad\psi=\mathcal{B}^{*}u.

(6.2)

Here
\mathcal{A}^{*},\mathcal{B}^{*}
denote the adjoints of
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
.

In control-theoretical terminology,
\mathcal{B}
is a control operator,
u
is a control, and
\mathcal{B}^{*}
is an observability operator.
Assume that the following three hypotheses are satisfied (we denote by
G^{\prime},H^{\prime}
the (anti)dual spaces of
G
and
H
, respectively):

(H1)

The operator
\mathcal{A}^{*}
generates a group
e^{s\mathcal{A}^{*}}
in
H^{\prime}
;

(H2)

We have
D(\mathcal{A}^{*})=D(\mathcal{B}^{*})
, and there exist two numbers
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\left\lVert\mathcal{B}^{*}u\right\rVert\leq c\left\lVert(\mathcal{A}+\lambda I)^{*}u\right\rVert\quad\text{for all}\quad u_{0}\in D(\mathcal{A}^{*}).

(H3)

There exist two numbers
T^{\prime},c^{\prime}>0
such that

\left\lVert\mathcal{B}^{*}u\right\rVert_{L^{2}(0,T^{\prime};G^{\prime})}\leq c^{\prime}\left\lVert u_{0}\right\rVert_{H^{\prime}}\quad\text{for all}\quad\varphi_{0}\in D(\mathcal{A}^{*}).

It follows from (H1) that for every
u_{0}\in H^{\prime}
the equation in (
6.2
) has a unique solution
u\in C(\mathbb{R},H^{\prime})
, and that

u\in C(\mathbb{R},D(\mathcal{A}^{*}))\cap C^{1}(\mathbb{R},H^{\prime})
if
u_{0}\in D(\mathcal{A}^{*})
. Moreover, note that hypothesis (H2) is a weakening of the boundedness of the operator
\mathcal{B}
; it is satisfied in many problems where the observation is made on the boundary or some non-open set of the underlying domain like in the case of Theorem
1.1
.

It follows from hypotheses (H1) and (H2) that hypothesis (H3) is meaningful. It is a (hidden) regularity property, allowing us to extend the observation to
\psi\in L^{2}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R},G^{\prime})
for every
u_{0}\in H^{\prime}
. Moreover, (H1)–(H3) imply the following strengthened version of (H3): for every
T>0
there exists a constant
c_{T}
such that the solutions of (
6.2
) satisfy the estimates

\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert_{L^{2}(-T,T;G^{\prime})}\leq c_{T}\left\lVert u_{0}\right\rVert_{H^{\prime}}\quad\text{for all}\quad u_{0}\in H^{\prime}.

(6.3)

This result also shows that the value of
T^{\prime}
in (H3) has no importance: if it is satisfied for some
T^{\prime}>0
, then, in fact, it is satisfied for
every

T^{\prime}>0
.

Next, we define the solutions of (
6.1
) by transposition. Fix
z_{0}\in H
and
v\in L^{2}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R},G)
arbitrarily.
Multiply the equation in (
6.1
) by the solution
u
of the equation in (
6.2
). Integrating by parts formally between
0
and
T\in\mathbb{R}
, we easily obtain the identity

\left\langle z(T),u(T)\right\rangle_{H,H^{\prime}}=\left\langle z_{0},u_{0}\right\rangle_{H,H^{\prime}}+\int_{0}^{T}\left\langle z(S),\psi(S)\right\rangle_{G,G^{\prime}}\,ds.

(6.4)

Definition
.

We define a solution of (
6.1
) as a function
z:\mathbb{R}\to H
satisfying the identity (
6.4
) for all
u_{0}\in H^{\prime}
and
T\in\mathbb{R}
.

The previous definition is justified by the following proposition:

Proposition 6.1
.

Given
z_{0}\in H
and
v\in L^{2}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R},G)
arbitrarily, the problem (
6.1
) has a unique solution.
Moreover,
z:\mathbb{R}\to H
is continuous, and satisfies for every
T>0
the following estimate:

\left\lVert z\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(-T,T;H)}\leq c_{T}\left(\left\lVert z_{0}\right\rVert_{H}+\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{L^{2}(-T,T;G)}\right).

Definitions
.

Now we introduce two definitions:

•

The system (
6.1
) is
exactly controllable
in time
T>0
if for every pair of data
z_{0},z_{1}\in H
there exists a function
v\in L^{2}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R},G)
such that

\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{L^{2}(0,T;G)}\leq c^{\prime}\left(\left\lVert z_{0}\right\rVert_{H}+\left\lVert z_{1}\right\rVert_{H}\right),

and that the solution of (
6.1
) satisfies the final condition
z(T)=z_{1}
.

•

The system (
6.2
) is
observable
in time
T>0
if its solutions satisfy the estimates

\left\lVert u_{0}\right\rVert_{H^{\prime}}\leq c^{\prime\prime}\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert_{L^{2}(0,T;G^{\prime})}\quad\text{for all}\quad u_{0}\in H^{\prime}.

We note that, similarly to (
6.3
), by a density argument the observability is equivalent to the following, apparently weaker condition:

\left\lVert u_{0}\right\rVert_{H^{\prime}}\leq c^{\prime\prime}\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert_{L^{2}(0,T;G^{\prime})}\quad\text{for all}\quad u_{0}\in D(A^{*}).

(H4)

The following important duality result extends a classical finite-dimensional theorem; it was essentially proved in
DolRus-1977
:

Theorem 6.2
.

Assume (H1)–(H3).
For any given
T>0
, the system (
6.1
) is exactly controllable if and only if the system (
6.2
) is observable.

Next, we state from
Kom-1997
a rapid stabilization theorem that extended a former result of Slemrod
Slemrod-1974
on bounded control operators:

Theorem 6.3
.

Assume (H1)–(H4) for some
T>0
, and fix
\omega>0
arbitrarily.
There exists a linear map
F_{\omega}:H\to G
and a constant
C>0
such that
the closed-loop problem

z^{\prime}=\mathcal{A}z+\mathcal{B}F_{\omega}z,\quad z(0)=z_{0},

is well-posed in
H
, and its solutions satisfy the estimate

\left\lVert z(t)\right\rVert_{H}\leq C\left\lVert z_{0}\right\rVert_{H}e^{-\omega t},

for all
z_{0}\in H
and
t\geq 0
.

7
Controllability and stabilization results

With the presentation of the previous review, we are in a position to prove Theorems
1.4
–
1.7
by applying Theorems
6.2
and
6.3
.

Proof of Theorems
1.6
and
1.7
.

These theorems will follow with the following choices:

\displaystyle H^{\prime}:=L^{2}_{x}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{T}),\quad G^{\prime}:=L^{2}(\Omega);

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{*}u:=u_{x}+u_{xxx}+u_{xyy},\quad\mathcal{B}^{*}u:=u|_{\Omega};

and

\displaystyle D(\mathcal{A}^{*})=D(\mathcal{B}^{*})=\left\{u\in H^{\prime}\ :\ u_{x}+u_{xxx}+u_{xyy}\in H^{\prime}\right\}.

Using the Fourier expansion

u(x,y)=\sum_{(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}\times\mathbb{Z}}c_{m,n}e^{i(mx+ny)}

(7.1)

of the elements of
H^{\prime}
and Parseval’s formula

\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{\prime}}^{2}=4\pi^{2}\sum_{(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}\times\mathbb{Z}}\left\lvert c_{m,n}\right\rvert^{2},

we may show that an element
u\in H^{\prime}
belongs to
D(\mathcal{A}^{*})=D(\mathcal{B}^{*})
if and only if

\sum_{(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}\times\mathbb{Z}}\left\lvert m(m^{2}+n^{2}-m)\right\rvert^{2}\left\lvert c_{m,n}\right\rvert^{2}<+\infty.

(7.2)

Indeed, using formula (
7.1
) we have

(u_{x}+u_{xxx}+u_{xyy})(x,y)=\sum_{(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}\times\mathbb{Z}}m(m^{2}+n^{2}-m)c_{m,n}e^{i(mx+ny)},

(7.3)

whence
u_{x}+u_{xxx}+u_{xyy}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{T})
if and only if the condition (
7.2
) is satisfied.
Furthermore, since the index
m=0
is missing in (
7.3
) by our assumption
u\in H^{\prime}
,
u_{x}+u_{xxx}+u_{xyy}
also belongs to
H^{\prime}
if and only if it belongs to
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{T})
.

Hypothesis (H1) follows at once from the representation (
7.1
).
The following computation shows that hypothesis (H2) is satisfied with
\lambda=1
:

\displaystyle\left\lVert(I+\mathcal{A}^{*})u\right\rVert_{H^{\prime}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{T}}\left\lvert u+u_{x}+u_{xxx}+u_{xyy}\right\rvert^{2}\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{T}}\left\lvert\sum_{(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}\times\mathbb{Z}}\left(1+im(m^{2}+n^{2}-1)\right)c_{m,n}e^{i(mx+ny)}\right\rvert^{2}\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle=4\pi^{2}\sum_{(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}\times\mathbb{Z}}\left\lvert 1+im(m^{2}+n^{2}-1)\right\rvert^{2}\left\lvert c_{m,n}\right\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle\geq 4\pi^{2}\sum_{(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}\times\mathbb{Z}}\left\lvert c_{m,n}\right\rvert^{2}=\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{\prime}}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{T}}\left\lvert u\right\rvert^{2}\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\Omega}\left\lvert u\right\rvert^{2}\,dx\,dy=\left\lVert\mathcal{B}^{*}u\right\rVert_{G^{\prime}}^{2}.

Hypotheses (H3) and (H4) are equivalent to the two-sided estimates of Theorem
1.4
with
U:=\Omega\times(0,T)
.
We may thus applying Theorems
6.2
and
6.3
to obtain Theorems
1.6
and
1.7
, respectively.
∎

Proof of Theorems
1.4
and
1.5
.

Now, we make the following choices:

\displaystyle H^{\prime}:=L^{2}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{T}),\quad G^{\prime}:=L^{2}(\left\{x_{0}\right\}\times\mathbb{T});

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{*}u:=u_{x}+u_{xxx}+u_{xyy},\quad\mathcal{B}^{*}u:=u|_{\left\{x_{0}\right\}\times\mathbb{T}};

and

\displaystyle D(\mathcal{A}^{*})=D(\mathcal{B}^{*})=\left\{u\in H^{\prime}\ :\ u_{x}+u_{xxx}+u_{xyy}\in H^{\prime}\right\}.

Note that
L^{2}(\left\{x_{0}\right\}\times\mathbb{T})
is isometrically isomorphic to (and hence may be identified with)
L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
.

Hypothesis (H1) now follows from the representation

u(x,y)=\sum_{(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}c_{m,n}e^{i(mx+ny)}

of the elements of
H^{\prime}
and Parseval’s formula

\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{\prime}}^{2}=4\pi^{2}\sum_{(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\left\lvert c_{m,n}\right\rvert^{2}.

For the verification of hypothesis (H2) first, we observe that

\left\lVert(I+\mathcal{A}^{*})u\right\rVert_{H^{\prime}}^{2}=4\pi^{2}\sum_{(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\left\lvert 1+im(m^{2}+n^{2}-1)\right\rvert^{2}\left\lvert c_{m,n}\right\rvert^{2}

(7.4)

because this part of the preceding similar computation remains valid without the assumption
m\neq 0
.

Next, we apply the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality to obtain the following relations, where we write

\mu_{m,n}:=\left\lvert 1+im(m^{2}+n^{2}-1)\right\rvert

for brevity:

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathcal{B}^{*}u\right\rVert_{G^{\prime}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left\lvert\sum_{(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}c_{m,n}e^{i(mx_{0}+ny)}\right\rvert^{2}\,dy=2\pi\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\left\lvert\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{m,n}e^{imx_{0}}\right\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\pi\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\left(\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\left\lvert c_{m,n}e^{imx_{0}}\mu_{m,n}\right\rvert^{2}\right)\cdot\left(\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{1}{\mu_{m,n}^{2}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=2\pi\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\left(\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\left\lvert c_{m,n}\mu_{m,n}\right\rvert^{2}\right)\cdot\left(\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{1}{\mu_{m,n}^{2}}\right)\right).

Since
\mu_{0,n}=1
, and
\mu_{m,n}\geq\left\lvert m\right\rvert
if
m\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}
, we have

\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{1}{\mu_{m,n}^{2}}\leq 1+2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k^{2}}<5,

for every
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, and therefore we infer from the preceding estimate that

\left\lVert\mathcal{B}^{*}u\right\rVert_{G^{\prime}}^{2}\leq 10\pi\sum_{(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\left\lvert 1+im(m^{2}+n^{2}-1)\right\rvert^{2}\left\lvert c_{m,n}\right\rvert^{2}.

Combining with (
7.4
) and noticing that
10\pi<4\pi^{2}
, we obtain that

\left\lVert\mathcal{B}^{*}u\right\rVert_{G^{\prime}}\leq\left\lVert(I+\mathcal{A}^{*})u\right\rVert_{H^{\prime}},

i.e., hypothesis (H2) is satisfied.

Finally, hypotheses (H3) and (H4) are equivalent to the two-sided estimates of Theorem
1.3
.
Applying Theorems
6.2
and
6.3
, Theorems
1.6
and
1.7
follow.
∎

8
Further comments

In this paper, we considered only internal observability and controllability properties of the system (
1.1
), however boundary observability and controllability results may also be obtained by adapting our approach; see e.g.,
KomMia-2014
and
KomLor-2014
for analogous results on membrane and plate models.

Concerning Theorems
1.1
,
1.2
and
1.3
it is natural to ask whether they remain valid in more general situations, for example, if the segments are oblique or they are moving in time (moving observation). For linear plate models, these questions were studied in
JamKom-2020
, and they probably may be adapted to the Zakharov–Kuznetsov equation (
1.1
), however, these issues are still open to study.

Note that the method developed in this work was based on the notion of sparse sets, it may be adapted to more general equations, containing further higher-order terms, in the two-dimensional cases.
