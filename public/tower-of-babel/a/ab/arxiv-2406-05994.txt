Perron solutions and boundary regularity for nonlocal nonlinear Dirichlet problems
Anders Björn

Abstract




Abstract
.

For nonlinear operators of fractional
p\mspace{1mu}
-Laplace type, we consider two types of solutions
to the nonlocal Dirichlet problem: Sobolev solutions based on fractional Sobolev spaces
and Perron solutions based on superharmonic functions.
These solutions give rise to two different concepts of regularity for boundary points,
namely Sobolev and Perron regularity.
We show that these two notions are equivalent
and we also provide several characterizations of regular boundary points.
Along the way, we give a new definition of Perron solutions,
which is applicable to arbitrary exterior Dirichlet
data
g:\Omega^{c}\to[-\infty,\infty]
.
We obtain resolutivity
results for these Perron solutions,
and show that the Sobolev and Perron solutions coincide
for a large class of exterior Dirichlet data.
This also implies invariance of the Perron solutions under perturbations
on sets of zero fractional capacity.
A uniqueness result for the Dirichlet problem is also obtained for
the class of bounded solutions
taking prescribed continuous
exterior data quasieverywhere on the boundary.

Key words and phrases
:
Barrier,
boundary regularity,
capacity,
fractional
p\mspace{1mu}
-Laplacian,
nonlocal nonlinear Dirichlet problem,
Perron solution,
resolutivity,
Sobolev solution.

Introduction

The aim of this paper is
to study the Perron method and boundary regularity for
the nonlocal nonlinear equation
\mathcal{L}u=0
.
The operator
\mathcal{L}
is of the form

\mathcal{L}u(x)=2\,\mathrm{p.v.}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}|u(x)-u(y)|^{p-2}(u(x)-u(y))k(x,y)\,dy,

where
1<p<\infty
,
n\geq 1
and
k:\mathbf{R}^{n}\times\mathbf{R}^{n}\to[0,\infty]
is a symmetric measurable kernel
that satisfies the ellipticity condition

\frac{\Lambda^{-1}}{|x-y|^{n+sp}}\leq k(x,y)\leq\frac{\Lambda}{|x-y|^{n+sp}},

(1.1)

with
0<s<1
and
\Lambda\geq 1
.
Note that
\mathcal{L}
is the fractional
p\mspace{1mu}
-Laplacian
(-\Delta_{p})^{s}
when

k(x,y)=|x-y|^{-n-sp}
.

Nonlocal and fractional problems have been studied for a long time,
see e.g.
[
1
,
9
]
, but in recent years, starting perhaps
with the pioneering paper by Caffarelli–Silvestre
[
13
]
,
they have received
a lot more attention (see e.g. our list of references).
Fractional operators also have significant applications in various contexts,
such as
the obstacle problem
[
12
,
48
]
,
materials science
[
3
,
4
]
,
phase transitions
[
11
,
18
,
49
]
,
soft thin films
[
37
]
, quasi-geostrophic dynamics
[
14
]

and image processing
[
21
]
.

We assume in the rest of the introduction
that
\Omega\subset{\mathbf{R}^{n}}
is a nonempty bounded open set.
In the
Dirichlet problem
one seeks
a solution of
\mathcal{L}u=0

in
\Omega
which takes on some
prescribed exterior Dirichlet data

g:{\Omega^{c}}\to{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}:=[-\infty,\infty]
.
Due to the nonlocal nature of the problem the Dirichlet data
have to be prescribed on the whole complement
{\Omega^{c}}
of
\Omega
.
For this reason, the Dirichlet problem is
also called the exterior value problem or the complement value problem,
but we will call it the Dirichlet problem for short.

As in the classical setting of harmonic functions one can in general
not solve the Dirichlet problem so that the prescribed boundary values
are taken as limits on
\partial\Omega
,
even if
g
is bounded and continuous.
Hence, the Dirichlet problem has to be understood in a relaxed sense.
The two main ways of doing so are the
Sobolev solutions (taking the boundary values in the Sobolev sense)
and the Perron solutions.

A natural question is then which boundary points are
regular
,
i.e. whether all
continuous Dirichlet data are attained as limits of the
corresponding solutions.
The two notions of solutions lead to two different types of boundary regularity:
Sobolev regularity and Perron regularity.
One of our main results is that
these two types of regularity are equivalent.
To show this, we first need to
substantially develop the Perron method.
For classical harmonic functions it
goes back to
Perron
[
44
]
and independently Remak
[
46
]
, see
the end of the introduction for further historical remarks.
Our results on boundary regularity are summarized in Theorem
1.7
.

To the best of our knowledge, the solvability of the nonlocal Dirichlet problem in
the Sobolev sense was first established by Hoh–Jacob
[
27
]
when
p=2
.
It was later extended to the nonlocal Dirichlet problems associated with
(not necessarily symmetric)
kernels
k
by Felsinger–Kassmann–Voigt
[
19
]
.
For general
p
and operators
\mathcal{L}
as above,
it was solved using the direct method of calculus of variations
by Di Castro–Kuusi–Palatucci
[
16
]
.
These results together with the interior regularity results prove the existence and
uniqueness of an
\mathcal{L}
-harmonic function (a weak solution that is continuous in
\Omega
)
solving the nonlocal Dirichlet problem with Sobolev data
g\in V^{s,p}(\Omega)
,
see Section
3
.
We denote this solution by
Hg
.

Perron solutions of nonlocal equations were studied by Bliedtner–Hansen
[
9
]

for the fractional linear
Laplacian
(-\Delta)^{s}
, Lindgren–Lindqvist
[
39
]
for
the fractional nonlinear
p\mspace{1mu}
-Laplacian
(-\Delta_{p})^{s}

and by Korvenpää–Kuusi–Palatucci
[
36
]
for general
\mathcal{L}
as here.
However, the definitions of Perron solutions in these works are all different
even when restricted to
(-\Delta)^{s}
,
and the one in
[
36
]
seems unnaturally restrictive.
Before discussing the differences between these definitions,
we provide our definition of Perron solution.
For
\mathcal{L}
-superharmonic functions we follow the definition
in
[
36
]
, see Definition
8.1
.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
g:{\Omega^{c}}\to{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}
.
The
upper class

\mathcal{U}_{g}=\mathcal{U}_{g}(\Omega)

of

consists of all functions

u:\mathbf{R}^{n}\to(-\infty,\infty]
such that

1.

u
is
\mathcal{L}
-superharmonic in
\Omega
,

2.

is bounded from below in
\Omega
,

3.

\liminf_{\Omega\ni y\to x}u(y)\geq g(x)
for all
x\in\partial\Omega
,

4.

in
{\Omega^{c}}
.

The
upper Perron solution
of

is defined by

{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\displaystyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\textstyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptstyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptscriptstyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}}g(x)={\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\displaystyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\textstyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptstyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptscriptstyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}}_{\Omega}g(x)=\inf_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{g}}u(x),\quad x\in\mathbf{R}^{n},

and the
lower Perron solution
of
g
by

{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{$\underline{\phantom{\displaystyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\underline{\phantom{\textstyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\underline{\phantom{\scriptstyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\underline{\phantom{\scriptscriptstyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}}g=-{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\displaystyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\textstyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptstyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptscriptstyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}}(-g)
.
(As usual,
\inf\varnothing=\infty
.)

We usually omit
\Omega
from the notation.
When
{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{$\underline{\phantom{\displaystyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\underline{\phantom{\textstyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\underline{\phantom{\scriptstyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\underline{\phantom{\scriptscriptstyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}}g={\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\displaystyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\textstyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptstyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptscriptstyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}}g
we denote the common solution by

and say that

is
resolutive
.

Our definition is essentially the same
as the one given in Lindgren–Lindqvist
[
39
, Section 4]
.
(In
[
39
]
they only considered bounded continuous functions

On the other hand, it differs from the definition
in Korvenpää–Kuusi–Palatucci

in
conditions
3
and

In particular, we have an inequality in

which makes it possible to define Perron solutions

More importantly, it immediately gives the comparisons

{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{$\underline{\phantom{\displaystyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\underline{\phantom{\textstyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\underline{\phantom{\scriptstyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\underline{\phantom{\scriptscriptstyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}}g_{1}\leq{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{$\underline{\phantom{\displaystyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\underline{\phantom{\textstyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\underline{\phantom{\scriptstyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\underline{\phantom{\scriptscriptstyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}}g_{2}\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\displaystyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\textstyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptstyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptscriptstyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}}g_{1}\leq{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\displaystyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\textstyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptstyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptscriptstyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}}g_{2}\quad\text{in }\mathbf{R}^{n}\qquad\text{whenever }g_{1}\leq g_{2}\text{ in }\Omega^{c},

which do not appear in

Nevertheless, we are able to show that the Perron solutions

In particular they do satisfy (

The analogue of

.

.

Let

1.

Then
{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\displaystyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\textstyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptstyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptscriptstyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}}g

(which follows from Theorems

differs in two aspects from the corresponding

Second, the upper and the lower Perron solutions

The next main results concern resolutivity,
which is one of the central questions for the Perron method.
It also relates Perron solutions to the Sobolev solutions

,
which is a key step to showing
that Sobolev and Perron regularity are equivalent.
Here,

is a suitable fractional Sobolev space associated with the

\mathcal{L}
, see (

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
g\in C(\mathbf{R}^{n})\cap V^{s,p}(\Omega)

and
h:{\mathbf{R}^{n}}\to{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}
be such that

h=0
in

, where the Sobolev capacity
{C_{s,p}}(E)=0
.
Then both
g
and
g+h
are resolutive and

Moreover, both
Pg
and
P(g+h)
are
\mathcal{L}
-harmonic in
\Omega
.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
g:{\Omega^{c}}\to\mathbf{R}
be a bounded measurable function that is continuous
at every
x\in\partial\Omega
.
Assume that
h:{\Omega^{c}}\to{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}
is such that

h=0
in
{\Omega^{c}}\setminus E
, where the Sobolev capacity
{C_{s,p}}(E)=0
.
Then both
g
and
g+h
are resolutive and

P(g+h)=Pg+h\chi_{{\Omega^{c}}}.

Moreover, both
Pg
and
P(g+h)
are
\mathcal{L}
-harmonic in
\Omega
.

Lindgren–Lindqvist
[
39
]

proved the resolutivity (but not invariance
with
h\not\equiv 0
)
of bounded continuous Dirichlet data

(with a limit at infinity)
for
\mathcal{L}=(-\Delta_{p})^{s}
(without kernel but with bounded right-hand sides).
We only require continuity at all

(and boundedness in
{\Omega^{c}}
).

In the linear nonlocal case, Bliedtner–Hansen
[
9
]

(using a different type of Perron solutions) obtained
more extensive resolutivity results corresponding
to Brelot’s classical resolutivity result for harmonic functions,
but they substantially used linearity
(which is not at our disposal here).
For example, the invariance under negligible perturbations of the
Dirichlet data is an easy
consequence of the linearity.
In neither paper, there are any results connecting Sobolev and Perron solutions.

In order to prove Theorem

we show the Kellogg property, which is of independent interest.

Theorem 1.5
.

(Kellogg property)

Let
I_{\Omega}\subset\partial\Omega
be the set of Sobolev irregular boundary points for

in
\Omega

Then the Sobolev capacity

The nonlocal equation and Sobolev spaces

Throughout the paper, we assume that

1<p<\infty
,
0<s<1
,
n\geq 1
, that
k

is a symmetric measurable kernel satisfying (
1.1
),
and that
\Omega\subset{\mathbf{R}^{n}}

is a nonempty open set.
From Section
3
onwards we also
always assume that
\Omega
is bounded.

In order to consider weak solutions of the equation

\mathcal{L}u=0\quad\text{in}~\Omega,

(2.1)

we need to define some function spaces.

For a measurable function
u:\Omega\to{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}:=[-\infty,\infty]
(which is
finite a.e.) we define the fractional seminorm

[u]_{W^{s,p}(\Omega)}=\biggl(\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{p}}{|x-y|^{n+sp}}\,dy\,dx\biggr)^{1/p}.

The fractional Sobolev space
W^{s,p}(\Omega)
consists of the functions
u
such that
the norm

\|u\|_{W^{s,p}(\Omega)}^{p}:=\|u\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{p}+[u]_{W^{s,p}(\Omega)}^{p}<\infty.

The above spaces and (semi)norms go under various names,
such as fractional Sobolev,
Gagliardo–Nirenberg, Sobolev–Slobodetskiĭ and Besov.

By
W^{s,p}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)
we denote the space of functions that belong to
W^{s,p}(G)

for every open
G\Subset\Omega
.
As usual, by
E\Subset\Omega
we mean that
{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\displaystyle E}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{E}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\textstyle E}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{E}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptstyle E}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{E}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptscriptstyle E}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{E}}}

is a compact subset of
\Omega
.
We refer the reader to Di Nezza–Palatucci–Valdinoci
[
17
]
for properties of these spaces.

As (
2.1
) is a nonlocal equation, we also need a function space that captures integrability of
functions in the whole of
{\mathbf{R}^{n}}
.
The
tail space

L^{p-1}_{sp}(\mathbf{R}^{n})
is given by

L^{p-1}_{sp}(\mathbf{R}^{n})=\biggl\{u\text{ measurable}:\int_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}\frac{|u(y)|^{p-1}}{(1+|y|)^{n+sp}}\,dy<\infty\biggr\},

(2.2)

see Kassmann
[
28
]
(for
p=2
) and Di Castro–Kuusi–Palatucci
[
16
]
.
For measurable functions
u,v:\mathbf{R}^{n}\to{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}

we define the quantity

\mathcal{E}(u,v)=\int_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}|u(x)-u(y)|^{p-2}(u(x)-u(y))(v(x)-v(y))k(x,y)\,dy\,dx,

provided that it is finite. Note that
\mathcal{E}(u,v)
is well defined for

u\in W^{s,p}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)\cap L^{p-1}_{sp}(\mathbf{R}^{n})
and
v\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
,
and that it is linear in the second argument.
Here and later,
C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
denotes
the space of
C^{\infty}
functions with compact support in
\Omega
.
Similarly,
{\Lip_{c}}(\Omega)
denotes the space of Lipschitz
functions with compact support in
\Omega
.
The
support
of a function

is

\supp u=\overline{\{x:u(x)\neq 0\}}
.

(resp.

\mathcal{E}(u,\varphi)\geq 0

If

\mathcal{L}
-harmonic

For simplicity we will just say that

If

As usual, we let

Lemma 2.5

Assume that

and
u_{j}\to u
in
{V^{s,p}(\Omega)}
as

Let

\bar{u}=\min\{u,g\}\quad\text{and}\quad\bar{u}_{j}=\min\{u_{j},g\}.

Then
\bar{u}_{j}\to\bar{u}
in

as

This probably belongs to folklore but since we have not been able to find
a reference, we provide a proof.
See however Costea
[
15

,
which is similar but deals with a
different nonlocal norm.
To indicate a certain subtlety in the argument, we point out that
even though

|{\min\{a,m\}-\min\{b,m\}}|\leq|a-b|

always holds, here we need to estimate

|(\min\{a,m\}-\min\{b,m\})-(\min\{c,t\}-\min\{d,t\})|,

which can in general be larger than
|(a-b)-(c-d)|
, e.g. when

a=d=m=t=0
and
b=-c\neq 0

So the proof is not straightforward even if

(or

).
We will need this result with nonconstant

Lemma

Let

\displaystyle v_{j}(x,y)

\displaystyle=u_{j}(x)-u_{j}(y),

\displaystyle\quad v(x,y)

\displaystyle=u(x)-u(y),

\displaystyle\bar{v}_{j}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\bar{u}_{j}(x)-\bar{u}_{j}(y),

\displaystyle\quad\bar{v}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\bar{u}(x)-\bar{u}(y),

and define the product measure
\mu
on
\Omega\times\mathbf{R}^{n}

by

d\mu(x,y)=\frac{dy\,dx}{|x-y|^{n+sp}}.

Since
u_{j}\to u
in
{V^{s,p}(\Omega)}
, we see that

\|\bar{u}_{j}-\bar{u}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq\|u_{j}-u\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\to 0\quad\text{and}\quad\|v_{j}-v\|_{L^{p}(\Omega\times\mathbf{R}^{n},\mu)}\to 0,

as
j\to\infty
.
It is therefore enough to show that
\|\bar{v}_{j}-\bar{v}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega\times\mathbf{R}^{n},\mu)}\to 0

Assume for a contradiction that
there is
\varepsilon>0
and a subsequence (also denoted by

)
such that

\|\bar{v}_{j}-\bar{v}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega\times\mathbf{R}^{n},\mu)}\geq\varepsilon\quad\text{for }j=1,2,\dots.

(2.4)

By passing to another subsequence if necessary we can also require that

\|v_{j}-v\|_{L^{p}(\Omega\times\mathbf{R}^{n},\mu)}<2^{-j}\qquad\text{and}\qquad v_{j}\to v\quad\text{$\mu$-a.e.\ in }\Omega\times\mathbf{R}^{n}\text{ as $j\to\infty$}.

Clearly, also

\bar{v}_{j}\to\bar{v}\quad\text{$\mu$-a.e.\ in $\Omega\times\mathbf{R}^{n}$, as $j\to\infty$.}

(2.5)

To use dominated convergence, we define

v_{0}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}|v_{j}-v|\in L^{p}(\Omega\times\mathbf{R}^{n},\mu).

(2.6)

The triangle inequality and a simple comparison show that

\displaystyle|\bar{v}(x,y)|

\displaystyle\leq|{\min\{u(x),g(x)\}-\min\{u(y),g(x)\}}|

\displaystyle\quad+|{\min\{u(y),g(x)\}-\min\{u(y),g(y)\}}|

\displaystyle\leq|u(x)-u(y)|+|g(x)-g(y)|

\displaystyle=:|v(x,y)|+g_{0}(x,y)

and similarly

|\bar{v}_{j}(x,y)|\leq|v_{j}(x,y)|+g_{0}(x,y)
.
Using (
2.6
) and

u,g\in V^{s,p}(\Omega)
, we then get that

\displaystyle|\bar{v}_{j}-\bar{v}|

\displaystyle\leq|\bar{v}_{j}|+|\bar{v}|\leq|v_{j}|+|v|+2g_{0}

\displaystyle\leq|v_{j}-v|+2|v|+2g_{0}\leq v_{0}+2|v|+2g_{0}\in L^{p}(\Omega\times\mathbf{R}^{n},\mu).

Therefore dominated convergence and (
2.5
) give

\|\bar{v}_{j}-\bar{v}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega\times\mathbf{R}^{n},\mu)}\to 0,\quad\text{as }j\to\infty,

contradicting (
2.4
).
∎

Suppose that
u
is a supersolution in
\Omega
and let
0\leq\varphi\in V^{s,p}_{0}(\Omega)
.
By definition, there exist
\varphi_{j}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)

such that
\varphi_{j}\to\varphi
in
V^{s,p}(\Omega)
as
j\to\infty
.
Hence

(\varphi_{j})_{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle+$}}}}\in\Lip_{c}(\Omega)\subset V^{s,p}(\Omega)
.
It now follows from Hölder’s inequality and Lemma
2.5
that

|\mathcal{E}(u,(\varphi_{j})_{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle+$}}}})-\mathcal{E}(u,\varphi)|\leq 2\Lambda[u]_{V^{s,p}(\Omega)}^{p-1}[(\varphi_{j})_{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle+$}}}}-\varphi]_{V^{s,p}(\Omega)}\to 0,\quad\text{as }j\to\infty,

where
\Lambda\geq 1
is as in (
1.1
).
Since
\supp{(\varphi_{j})_{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle+$}}}}}\Subset\Omega
,
Proposition
2.2
shows that

\mathcal{E}(u,\varphi)=\lim_{j\to\infty}\mathcal{E}(u,(\varphi_{j})_{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle+$}}}})\geq 0.

The proof for solutions is similar or follows from the above
and Corollary
2.3
.
∎

3
The Dirichlet and obstacle problems

From now on, we always assume that
\Omega\subset{\mathbf{R}^{n}}

is a nonempty bounded open set.

In this section, we study the obstacle problem, which plays an important role
in the development of potential theory.
The Dirichlet problem (in the Sobolev sense)
is a special case of the obstacle problem, namely with
the obstacle
\psi\equiv-\infty
, i.e. without an obstacle.
Recall from Section
2
that all functions are defined
pointwise everywhere
and that
v-g\in V^{s,p}_{0}(\Omega)
implies
that
v\equiv g
in
{\Omega^{c}}
.

Definition 3.1
.

For
g\in V^{s,p}(\Omega)
and
\psi:\Omega\to{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}
, let

\mathcal{K}_{\psi,g}(\Omega)=\{v\in V^{s,p}(\Omega):v-g\in V^{s,p}_{0}(\Omega)\text{ and }v\geq\psi\text{ a.e.\ in }\Omega\}.

A function
u\in\mathcal{K}_{\psi,g}(\Omega)

is a
solution of the
\mathcal{K}_{\psi,g}(\Omega)
-obstacle problem
if

\mathcal{E}(u,v-u)\geq 0\quad\text{for all $v\in\mathcal{K}_{\psi,g}(\Omega)$.}

It follows directly from the definition that a solution of the

\mathcal{K}_{\psi,g}(\Omega)
-obstacle
problem is a supersolution of
\mathcal{L}u=0
in
\Omega
,
and moreover that it is a solution of
\mathcal{L}u=0
if
\psi\equiv-\infty
,
i.e. for the Dirichlet problem.

The obstacle problem was studied in detail by
Korvenpää–Kuusi–Palatucci
[
35
]
.
However, here we follow Kim–Lee
[
33
]
and use a somewhat
different definition of the obstacle problem that is more suitable for
our purposes.

In this paper it will also be important to consider
lsc-regularizations which we define in the following way.
For any measurable function
u:\mathbf{R}^{n}\to{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}
we define its

lsc-regularization
in
\Omega
as

\hat{u}(x):=\begin{cases}u(x),&\text{if }x\in{\Omega^{c}},\\
\displaystyle\essliminf_{y\to x}u(y),&\text{if }x\in\Omega.\end{cases}

(3.1)

We also say that
u
is
lsc-regularized
(in
\Omega
) if
u\equiv\hat{u}
.

Theorem 3.2
.

(Kim–Lee
[
33
, Theorem 4.9]
and
Korvenpää–Kuusi–Palatucci
[
36
, Theorem 9]
)

Let
g\in V^{s,p}(\Omega)
and
\psi:\Omega\to{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}
.
If
\mathcal{K}_{\psi,g}(\Omega)\neq\varnothing
,
then the
\mathcal{K}_{\psi,g}(\Omega)
-obstacle problem is solvable,
and the solution
u
is unique up to measure zero.

Moreover,
u
is a supersolution,
\hat{u}=u
a.e., and

\hat{u}
is the unique lsc-regularized solution of the

\mathcal{K}_{\psi,g}(\Omega)
-obstacle problem.

Note that the lsc-regularization is only within
\Omega
.

The existence and a.e.-uniqueness, and that
u
is a supersolution was
shown by Kim–Lee
[
33
, Theorem 4.9]
.
Since
u
is a supersolution, it follows from
[
36
, Theorem 9]

that
\hat{u}=u
a.e.
Thus
\hat{u}
is also a solution
of the
\mathcal{K}_{\psi,g}(\Omega)
-obstacle problem and its
pointwise uniqueness follows immediately from the
a.e.-uniqueness of
u
.
∎

For functions
g\in V^{s,p}(\Omega)
we can solve
the Dirichlet problem in the Sobolev sense.
To make it precise we define
Hg
as follows.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let

g\in V^{s,p}(\Omega)
.
Then there is a unique function
Hg=H_{\Omega}g:\mathbf{R}^{n}\to{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}
with the following properties
:

1.

Hg
is
\mathcal{L}
-harmonic in
\Omega
,

2.

Hg-g\in V^{s,p}_{0}(\Omega)
, and in particular

Hg\equiv g
outside
\Omega
.

This essentially follows from Theorem
3.2
:
There is a solution
u
of the
\mathcal{K}_{-\infty,g}(\Omega)
-obstacle problem which is unique
up to measure zero.
At the same time,

u
is a solution of
\mathcal{L}u=0
in
\Omega
and thus, after redefinition
on a set of measure zero within
\Omega
, can be required to be
\mathcal{L}
-harmonic in
\Omega
.
∎

We will need several comparison principles in this paper.
The following result is for obstacle problems.

Theorem 3.4
.

(Comparison principle for obstacle problems)

Let
u_{i}
be a solution of the
\mathcal{K}_{\psi_{i},g_{i}}(\Omega)
-obstacle problem for
i=1,2
.
If
\psi_{1}\leq\psi_{2}
a.e. in
\Omega
and

(g_{1}-g_{2})_{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle+$}}}}\in V^{s,p}_{0}(\Omega)
,
then
u_{1}\leq u_{2}
a.e. in
\Omega
.

Theorem
3.4
is a direct consequence of the following lemma
and the fact that solutions of the obstacle problem are
supersolutions of
\mathcal{L}u=0
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
u
be a solution of the
\mathcal{K}_{\psi,g}(\Omega)
-obstacle problem.
If
v\in V^{s,p}(\Omega)
is a supersolution in
\Omega
such that

\min\{u,v\}\in\mathcal{K}_{\psi,g}(\Omega)
, then
v\geq u
a.e. in
\Omega
.

Since
v
is a supersolution and

w:=u-\min\{u,v\}\in V^{s,p}_{0}(\Omega)
is nonnegative, it follows from
Proposition
2.4
that
\mathcal{E}(v,w)\geq 0
.
Moreover,
u-w=\min\{u,v\}\in\mathcal{K}_{\psi,g}(\Omega)

and thus
-\mathcal{E}(u,w)=\mathcal{E}(u,(u-w)-u)\geq 0
, by
the definition
of the
\mathcal{K}_{\psi,g}(\Omega)
-obstacle problem.
Hence

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{E}(v,w)-\mathcal{E}(u,w)

\displaystyle=\int_{\{u>v\}}\int_{\{u>v\}}(\Phi(v(x)-v(y))-\Phi(u(x)-u(y)))(w(x)-w(y))k(x,y)\,dy\,dx

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\{u>v\}}\int_{\{u\leq v\}}(\Phi(v(x)-v(y))-\Phi(u(x)-u(y)))w(x)k(x,y)\,dy\,dx

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\{u\leq v\}}\int_{\{u>v\}}(\Phi(v(x)-v(y))-\Phi(u(x)-u(y)))(-w(y))k(x,y)\,dy\,dx

\displaystyle=:I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3},

where
\Phi(t):=|t|^{p-2}t
is a strictly increasing function.
If
u(x)>v(x)
and
u(y)\leq v(y)
, then
v(x)-v(y)<u(x)-u(y)

and hence

\Phi(v(x)-v(y))<\Phi(u(x)-u(y)).

(3.2)

This leads to
I_{2}\leq 0
, and similarly we have
I_{3}\leq 0
.
On the other hand, if
u(x)>v(x)
and
u(y)>v(y)
, then

(\Phi(v(x)-v(y))-\Phi(u(x)-u(y)))(w(x)-w(y))\leq 0

since
w(x)-w(y)=(u(x)-u(y))-(v(x)-v(y))
and

(\Phi(a)-\Phi(b))(b-a)=(b-a)\int_{b}^{a}(p-1)|t|^{p-2}\,dt\leq 0

whenever
a,b\in\mathbf{R}
.
Thus, we obtain
I_{1}\leq 0
, which leads us to
I_{1}=I_{2}=I_{3}=0
.
In particular, since the integrand in
I_{2}
is strictly negative
(by (
3.2
)), we conclude that
either
|\{x:u(x)>v(x)\}|=0
(which gives the claim) or

0=|\{y:u(y)\leq v(y)\}|=|\{y:w(y)=0\}|\geq|\Omega^{c}|>0,

which is impossible.
(Here
|\cdot|
denotes the Lebesgue measure.)
∎

Corollary 3.6
.

(Comparison principle for
H
-solutions)

Let
g_{1},g_{2}\in V^{s,p}(\Omega)
be such that

(g_{1}-g_{2})_{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle+$}}}}\in V^{s,p}_{0}(\Omega)
.
Then
Hg_{1}\leq Hg_{2}
in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
.

By Theorem
3.4
(with
\psi_{1}=\psi_{2}\equiv-\infty
),

Hg_{1}\leq Hg_{2}
a.e. in
\Omega
.
Since
Hg_{1}
and
Hg_{2}
are continuous in
\Omega
,
the inequality holds everywhere in
\Omega
.
Moreover,
Hg_{1}=g_{1}\leq g_{2}=Hg_{2}
in
{\Omega^{c}}
.
∎

4
Sobolev regularity

We follow Kim–Lee–Lee
[
31
, Theorem 1.1]
in defining
Sobolev regularity as follows.
(They called it regularity, but we want to distinguish it from Perron regularity,
which a priori could be a different property even though it will be shown to be equivalent
in Theorem
10.3
.)

Definition 4.1
.

A boundary point
x_{0}\in\partial\Omega
is
Sobolev regular
(with respect to
\mathcal{L}

and
\Omega
)
if

\lim_{\Omega\ni x\to x_{0}}Hg(x)=g(x_{0})\quad\text{for every }g\in V^{s,p}(\Omega)\cap C(\mathbf{R}^{n}).

Otherwise we say that
x_{0}
is
Sobolev irregular
.

The main result in Kim–Lee–Lee
[
31
]
is the Wiener
criterion in the following form.
(The case
sp>n

was treated incorrectly in
[
31
]
.
A correction was made later in Kim–Lee–Lee
[
32
]
, see the last paragraph of Remark 1.5 in
[
32
]
.)

Theorem 4.2
.

(Wiener criterion
[
31
, Theorem 1.1]
)

A boundary point
x_{0}\in\partial\Omega
is Sobolev regular if and only if

\int_{0}^{1}\biggl(\frac{{\capp_{s,p}}({\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\displaystyle B(x_{0},\rho)}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{B(x_{0},\rho)}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\textstyle B(x_{0},\rho)}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{B(x_{0},\rho)}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptstyle B(x_{0},\rho)}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{B(x_{0},\rho)}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptscriptstyle B(x_{0},\rho)}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{B(x_{0},\rho)}}}\setminus\Omega,B(x_{0},2\rho))}{\rho^{n-sp}}\biggr)^{1/(p-1)}\,\frac{d\rho}{\rho}=\infty,

(4.1)

where the condenser capacity
{\capp_{s,p}}
is defined in Definition
5.1
below.

Note that since (
4.1
) is independent of the kernel
k
, so
is the Sobolev regularity of
x_{0}
.
It is easy to see that if
\Omega
satisfies the exterior cone condition or
the exterior corkscrew condition at
x_{0}
, then the
Wiener integral (
4.1
) diverges, and hence

x_{0}
is Sobolev regular.
Also the following important consequences follow immediately.

Corollary 4.3
.

Let
G
be a bounded open set and let
x_{0}\in\partial G\cap\partial\Omega
.

1.

(Sobolev regularity is a local property)

If
B\cap G=B\cap\Omega
for some ball
B\ni x_{0}
,
then
x_{0}
is Sobolev regular for
G
if and only it is Sobolev regular for
\Omega
.

2.

If
G\subset\Omega

and
x_{0}
is Sobolev regular for
\Omega
, then it is also Sobolev regular for
G
.

Before characterizing Sobolev regularity, we need to define the
\mathcal{L}
-potential.

Definition 4.4
.

Let
K\Subset\Omega

be compact and

\psi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
be such that
\psi=1
on
K
.
Then the function
H_{\Omega\setminus K}\psi
is called the
\mathcal{L}
-potential

of
K
in
\Omega
and is denoted by
\mathfrak{R}(K,\Omega)
.

The
\mathcal{L}
-potential is independent of the choice of
\psi

by Corollary
3.6
,
and it
is a supersolution in
\Omega
by Lemma 2.16(iv) in Kim–Lee–Lee
[
31
]
.
It therefore follows from
Theorem 9 in
Korvenpää–Kuusi–Palatucci
[
36
]

that its lsc-regularization

\widehat{\mathfrak{R}}(K,\Omega)
within
\Omega
satisfies

\widehat{\mathfrak{R}}(K,\Omega)=\mathfrak{R}(K,\Omega)
a.e.,
and is thus also a supersolution in
\Omega
.
(Moreover,
\widehat{\mathfrak{R}}(K,\Omega)
is
\mathcal{L}
-superharmonic in
\Omega
,
by Theorem
8.3
, but we will not use this fact.)

We are now ready to give our first characterizations of Sobolev regularity.
For a ball
B=B(x_{0},r):=\{x\in{\mathbf{R}^{n}}:|x-x_{0}|<r\}
, we let

\lambda B=B(x_{0},\lambda r)
.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
x_{0}\in\partial\Omega
.
Then the following are equivalent
:

1.

x_{0}
is Sobolev regular.

2.

\lim_{\Omega\ni x\to x_{0}}Hg(x)=g(x_{0})\quad\text{for every bounded }g\in V^{s,p}(\Omega)\cap C({\mathbf{R}^{n}}).

3.

\lim_{\Omega\ni x\to x_{0}}Hg(x)=g(x_{0})

for every bounded
g\in V^{s,p}(\Omega)

that is continuous at
x_{0}
.

4.

\lim_{\Omega\ni x\to x_{0}}Hd_{x_{0}}(x)=0,\quad\text{where }d_{x_{0}}(x):=\min\{1,|x-x_{0}|\}.

5.

\widehat{\mathfrak{R}}({\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\displaystyle B}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{B}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\textstyle B}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{B}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptstyle B}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{B}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptscriptstyle B}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{B}}}\setminus\Omega,2B)(x_{0})=1\quad\text{for every open ball }B\ni x_{0}.

6.

There is a decreasing sequence of radii
r_{j}\searrow 0
such that

In order to prove the Kellogg property (Theorem

Two types of capacities are used in this paper:

In contrast to Kim–Lee–Lee

We therefore make the following definitions.

Definition 5.1
.

The

of a compact set

{\capp_{s,p}}(K,\Omega)=\inf_{u}{[u]_{W^{s,p}(\mathbf{R}^{n})}^{p}},

Definition 5.2
.

The

of a compact set

{C_{s,p}}(K)=\inf_{u}{\|u\|_{W^{s,p}({\mathbf{R}^{n}})}^{p}},

where the infimum is taken over all

such that

Both capacities are then extended first to open and then to arbitrary sets
in the usual way as follows:
For open sets

\displaystyle{\capp_{s,p}}(G,\Omega)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}K\text{ compact}\\
K\Subset G\end{subarray}}{\capp_{s,p}}(K,\Omega),

\displaystyle\text{if $G\subset\Omega$},

(5.2)

\displaystyle{C_{s,p}}(G)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}K\text{ compact}\\
K\Subset G\end{subarray}}{C_{s,p}}(K),

\displaystyle\text{if $G\subset{\mathbf{R}^{n}}$}.

Similarly, for arbitrary sets

,

\displaystyle{\capp_{s,p}}(E,\Omega)

\displaystyle=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}G\text{ open}\\
E\subset G\subset\Omega\end{subarray}}{\capp_{s,p}}(G,\Omega),

\displaystyle\text{if $E\subset\Omega$},

(5.3)

\displaystyle{C_{s,p}}(E)

\displaystyle=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}G\text{ open}\\

{C_{s,p}}(K_{j})\leq\|\bar{u}_{j}\|^{p}_{W^{s,p}({\mathbf{R}^{n}})}\leq\|u\|^{p}_{W^{s,p}({\mathbf{R}^{n}})}+1/j.

Letting

then gives
{C_{s,p}}(G)\leq\|u\|^{p}_{W^{s,p}({\mathbf{R}^{n}})}
,
which shows the
\leq
inequality in (
5.7
).
In particular (
5.7
) holds when
{C_{s,p}}(G)=\infty
.

Assume next that
{C_{s,p}}(G)<\infty
.
Then we can find
u_{j}\in C_{c}^{\infty}({\mathbf{R}^{n}})
so that
u_{j}\geq 1
on
K_{j}

and
\|u_{j}\|^{p}_{W^{s,p}({\mathbf{R}^{n}})}\leq{C_{s,p}}(G)+1/j
.
Let
v_{j}=\min\{(u_{j})_{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle+$}}}},1\}
.
Since truncations decrease the norm, we see that

\|v_{j}\|^{p}_{W^{s,p}({\mathbf{R}^{n}})}\leq\|u_{j}\|^{p}_{W^{s,p}({\mathbf{R}^{n}})}\leq{C_{s,p}}(G)+1/j<\infty.

By the reflexivity of
W^{s,p}({\mathbf{R}^{n}})
, there is a
subsequence of
\{v_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}

which converges weakly in
W^{s,p}({\mathbf{R}^{n}})

to a function
\psi

with
\|\psi\|^{p}_{W^{s,p}({\mathbf{R}^{n}})}\leq{C_{s,p}}(G)
.
Using that

is a bounded linear functional
on
W^{s,p}(\mathbf{R}^{n})
for every bounded measurable set

,
we obtain that
0\leq\psi\leq 1
a.e. and
\psi=1
a.e. in
G
.
After redefinition on a set of measure zero we get

0\leq\psi\leq 1
everywhere on
{\mathbf{R}^{n}}
and

\psi=1
everywhere in
G
.
Thus the
\geq
inequality in (
5.7
) also holds and
the infimum is attained.
∎

The Kellogg property (Theorem

)

We are now ready to prove the Kellogg property, that the

Theorem

shows that

Proposition 6.2

Let

Then

{\capp_{s,p}}(E,\Omega)=\inf_{u\in\mathcal{A}}{[u]_{W^{s,p}({\mathbf{R}^{n}})}^{p}},

In the proof we will use the following lemma,

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
g\in V^{s,p}(\Omega)
and
\psi:\Omega\to{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}
be such that
\mathcal{K}_{\psi,g}(\Omega)\neq\varnothing

Then
u
is a solution of the
\mathcal{K}_{\psi,g}(\Omega)
-obstacle problem
if and only if
u
minimizes the energy
\mathcal{E}(u,u)
among all functions in

\mathcal{K}_{\psi,g}(\Omega)
.

Assume first that
u
is a solution of the
\mathcal{K}_{\psi,g}(\Omega)
-obstacle problem.
Let
v\in\mathcal{K}_{\psi,g}(\Omega)
.
Then,
\mathcal{E}(u,v)-\mathcal{E}(u,u)=\mathcal{E}(u,v-u)\geq 0
.
Hölder’s inequality, then gives

\mathcal{E}(u,u)\leq\mathcal{E}(u,v)\leq\mathcal{E}(u,u)^{1-1/p}\mathcal{E}(v,v)^{1/p}.

(6.1)

Since
\mathcal{E}(u,u)<\infty
, we conclude that
\mathcal{E}(u,u)\leq\mathcal{E}(v,v)

and thus
u
is an energy minimizer.

To show the converse implication, assume that

u\in\mathcal{K}_{\psi,g}(\Omega)
is an energy minimizer within
\mathcal{K}_{\psi,g}(\Omega)
.
To show that
u
is a solution of the
\mathcal{K}_{\psi,g}(\Omega)
-obstacle problem,
let
v
be such a solution
provided by Theorem
3.2
.
By the above,
v
is also a minimizer and so
we must have equality throughout (
6.1
),
in particular in the Hölder inequality.
Therein, equality holds if and only if the functions

(x,y)\mapsto u(x)-u(y)
and
(x,y)\mapsto v(x)-v(y)

are linearly dependent.
Hence, for some

a,b\in\mathbf{R}
,
(a,b)\neq(0,0)
,

a(u(x)-u(y))=b(v(x)-v(y))\quad\text{for a.e.\ }(x,y)\in\mathbf{R}^{2n}

and thus
|a|^{p}\mathcal{E}(u,u)=|b|^{p}\mathcal{E}(v,v)
.
Since
\mathcal{E}(u,u)=\mathcal{E}(v,v)
we must have

,
and using that

for a.e.

If
u=v
a.e. in
\Omega

is a solution
of the
\mathcal{K}_{\psi,g}(\Omega)
-obstacle problem.
On the other hand, if

and
so
\mathcal{E}(u,u)\leq\mathcal{E}(0,0)=0

also a solution of the
\mathcal{K}_{\psi,g}(\Omega)
-obstacle problem.

7
A convergence result for the obstacle problem

Next, we discuss the pointwise convergence of sequences of solutions of the obstacle problem and the Dirichlet problem.

Theorem 7.1
.

Assume that
g_{j}\in V^{s,p}(\Omega)
,
j=0,1,\mathinner{.}\nonscript\!.\nonscript\!\mathinner{.}
, and that
g_{j}\to g_{0}
in
V^{s,p}(\Omega)

and
g_{j}\searrow g_{0}
pointwise in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
, as
j\to\infty
.
Let
u_{j}
be a solution of the

\mathcal{K}_{g_{j},g_{j}}(\Omega)
-obstacle problem,
j=0,1,\mathinner{.}\nonscript\!.\nonscript\!\mathinner{.}
.
Then
u_{j}\searrow u_{0}
a.e. in
\Omega
.

Moreover,
Hg_{j}\searrow Hg_{0}
everywhere in
\Omega
.

For the Dirichlet problem in the last part
of Theorem
7.1

we get convergence everywhere.
On the contrary, the following example shows that
for the obstacle problem the pointwise convergence

u_{j}(x)\to u_{0}(x)
can fail at some points, even if all
u_{j}
,
j=0,1,\mathinner{.}\nonscript\!.\nonscript\!\mathinner{.}
,
are continuous.

Example 7.2
.

Assume that
0<sp\leq n
.
Let
\Omega=B(0,2)
,
K_{j}={\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\displaystyle B(0,1/j)}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{B(0,1/j)}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\textstyle B(0,1/j)}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{B(0,1/j)}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptstyle B(0,1/j)}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{B(0,1/j)}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptscriptstyle B(0,1/j)}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{B(0,1/j)}}}
and

g_{j}=\mathfrak{R}(K_{j},\Omega)
be the corresponding
\mathcal{L}
-potentials,
j=1,2,\mathinner{.}\nonscript\!.\nonscript\!\mathinner{.}
,
as in Definition
4.4
.
Then each boundary point of
\Omega\setminus K_{j}
satisfies
the exterior cone condition and is thus Sobolev regular
by the Wiener criterion (Theorem

).
Hence
g_{j}
is continuous in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
.
Moreover,

\{g_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
is a pointwise decreasing sequence,
by the comparison principle (Corollary

).
Let
g_{0}=\lim_{j\to\infty}g_{j}
.

Next, for every function
u\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
, which is admissible
for
{\capp_{s,p}}(K_{j},\Omega)
,
Lemma
2.7
shows that the function
\tilde{u}:=\min\{u,1\}
satisfies

\tilde{u}-g_{j}\in V^{s,p}_{0}(\Omega\setminus K_{j}),

and so
\tilde{u}\in\mathcal{K}_{-\infty,g_{j}}(\Omega\setminus K_{j})
.
It thus follows from Lemma
6.3
,
with
\Lambda\geq 1
as in (

), that

\Lambda^{-1}[g_{j}]^{p}_{W^{s,p}(\mathbf{R}^{n})}\leq\mathcal{E}(g_{j},g_{j})\leq\mathcal{E}(\tilde{u},\tilde{u})\leq\Lambda[\tilde{u}]^{p}_{W^{s,p}(\mathbf{R}^{n})}\leq\Lambda[u]^{p}_{W^{s,p}(\mathbf{R}^{n})}.

Taking infimum over all such
u
shows that

0\leq\limsup_{j\to\infty}{[g_{j}]_{W^{s,p}(\mathbf{R}^{n})}^{p}}\leq\Lambda^{2}\lim_{j\to\infty}{\capp_{s,p}}(K_{j},\Omega)=\Lambda^{2}{\capp_{s,p}}(\{0\},\Omega)=0,

by Lemma
5.5
. (Here we use that
sp\leq n
.)
It then follows from the Poincaré inequality (
5.5
) that also

\lim_{j\to\infty}\|g_{j}\|_{L^{p}(\mathbf{R}^{n})}=0
, and thus
g_{0}=0
a.e.

Clearly
u_{j}:=g_{j}
is the continuous solution of the

\mathcal{K}_{g_{j},g_{j}}(\Omega)
-obstacle problem,
j=1,2,\mathinner{.}\nonscript\!.\nonscript\!\mathinner{.}
.
On the other hand,
u_{0}\equiv 0
is the continuous solution of the

\mathcal{K}_{g_{0},g_{0}}(\Omega)
-obstacle problem.
Thus
u_{j}(0)\searrow g_{0}(0)=1>0=u_{0}(0)
,
i.e. the convergence is not everywhere.
In fact, it follows from Harnack’s convergence theorem
(Theorem
7.3
below)
that
g_{0}=\chi_{\{0\}}
.

The sequence
\{u_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
is decreasing a.e. in
\Omega
by Theorem
3.4
.
Write
g=g_{0}
and define

u=\liminf_{j\to\infty}u_{j}.

In particular,
u=\lim_{j\to\infty}g_{j}=g
in
{\Omega^{c}}
.
It is enough to show that
u
is a solution of
the
\mathcal{K}_{g,g}(\Omega)
-obstacle problem
because the uniqueness of solutions to the obstacle problem
(see Theorem
3.2
)
then yields
u=u_{0}
a.e. in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

We first claim that
u\in\mathcal{K}_{g,g}(\Omega)
.
Indeed, from
Hg\leq u\leq u_{j}\leq u_{1}
a.e. we see that

u\in L^{p}(\Omega)
.
In order to prove that
u\in V^{s,p}(\Omega)
, we define

d\mu(x,y)=k(x,y)\,dy\,dx
and introduce

w_{j}(x,y)=|u_{j}(x)-u_{j}(y)|^{p-2}(u_{j}(x)-u_{j}(y)),\quad j=1,2,\dots.

Note that

\Lambda^{-1}[u_{j}]_{V^{s,p}(\Omega)}^{p}\leq\|w_{j}\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}((\Omega^{c}\times\Omega^{c})^{c},\mu)}^{p^{\prime}}\leq 2\Lambda[u_{j}]_{V^{s,p}(\Omega)}^{p},

(7.1)

where
p^{\prime}=p/(p-1)
and

\Lambda\geq 1
is as in (
1.1
).
Since
u_{j}
is a solution of the
\mathcal{K}_{g_{j},g_{j}}(\Omega)
-obstacle problem
and
g_{j}\in\mathcal{K}_{g_{j},g_{j}}(\Omega)
,
it follows from Lemma
6.3

that

\|w_{j}\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}((\Omega^{c}\times\Omega^{c})^{c},\mu)}=\mathcal{E}(u_{j},u_{j})^{1/p^{\prime}}\leq\mathcal{E}(g_{j},g_{j})^{1/p^{\prime}}\leq(2\Lambda)^{1-1/p}[g_{j}]_{V^{s,p}(\Omega)}^{p-1}.

(7.2)

In particular, (
7.1
) and (
7.2
) show that

[u]_{V^{s,p}(\Omega)}\leq\liminf_{j\to\infty}[u_{j}]_{V^{s,p}(\Omega)}<\infty
,
which gives
u\in V^{s,p}(\Omega)
.
Next,
0\leq(u-g_{j})_{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle+$}}}}\leq(u_{j}-g_{j})_{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle+$}}}}
a.e. and

(u_{j}-g_{j})_{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle+$}}}}\in V^{s,p}_{0}(\Omega)

by Lemma
2.6
.
Thus Corollary
2.9
shows that

(u-g_{j})_{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle+$}}}}\in V^{s,p}_{0}(\Omega)
for all
j=1,2,\mathinner{.}\nonscript\!.\nonscript\!\mathinner{.}
.
Lemma
2.5
,
together with the fact that
u-g_{j}\to u-g

in
V^{s,p}(\Omega)
as
j\to\infty
, yields

V^{s,p}_{0}(\Omega)\ni(u-g_{j})_{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle+$}}}}\to(u-g)_{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle+$}}}}\quad\text{in }V^{s,p}(\Omega),

and hence
(u-g)_{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle+$}}}}\in V^{s,p}_{0}(\Omega)

(because
V^{s,p}_{0}(\Omega)
is complete by definition).
Since clearly
u\geq g
a.e. in
\Omega
and
u=g
in
\Omega^{c}
,
this implies that

u-g\in V^{s,p}_{0}(\Omega)
and so

u\in\mathcal{K}_{g,g}(\Omega)
.

Next we prove that
u
is a solution of the
\mathcal{K}_{g,g}(\Omega)
-obstacle
problem.
For every
v\in\mathcal{K}_{g,g}(\Omega)
, we have
v+g_{j}-g\in\mathcal{K}_{g_{j},g_{j}}(\Omega)

and hence
\mathcal{E}(u_{j},v+g_{j}-g-u_{j})\geq 0
, which yields

\displaystyle\iint_{(\Omega^{c}\times\Omega^{c})^{c}}|u_{j}(x)-u_{j}(y)|^{p}\,d\mu

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\iint_{(\Omega^{c}\times\Omega^{c})^{c}}w_{j}(x,y)(v(x)-v(y))\,d\mu

(7.3)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+\iint_{(\Omega^{c}\times\Omega^{c})^{c}}w_{j}(x,y)((g_{j}-g)(x)-(g_{j}-g)(y))\,d\mu.

It follows from (
7.2
) that
\{w_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
is a bounded sequence
in
L^{p^{\prime}}((\Omega^{c}\times\Omega^{c})^{c},\mu)
.
We can therefore find a subsequence, also denoted by
w_{j}
, weakly converging to
w

in
L^{p^{\prime}}((\Omega^{c}\times\Omega^{c})^{c},\mu)
, such that

w_{j}(x,y)\to w(x,y):=|u(x)-u(y)|^{p-2}(u(x)-u(y))\quad\text{$\mu$-a.e.\ as }j\to\infty.

The weak convergence of
w_{j}
in
L^{p^{\prime}}((\Omega^{c}\times\Omega^{c})^{c},\mu)
implies that

\iint_{(\Omega^{c}\times\Omega^{c})^{c}}w_{j}(x,y)(v(x)-v(y))\,d\mu\to\iint_{(\Omega^{c}\times\Omega^{c})^{c}}w(x,y)(v(x)-v(y))\,d\mu.

At the same time, by Hölder’s inequality,

\iint_{(\Omega^{c}\times\Omega^{c})^{c}}w_{j}(x,y)((g_{j}-g)(x)-(g_{j}-g)(y))\,d\mu\leq 2\Lambda[u_{j}]_{V^{s,p}(\Omega)}^{p-1}[g_{j}-g]_{V^{s,p}(\Omega)}\to 0,

since
\{u_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
is a bounded sequence in
V^{s,p}(\Omega)

and
g_{j}\to g
in
V^{s,p}(\Omega)
.
Inserting the last two estimates into (
7.3
),
we therefore obtain that

\displaystyle\iint_{(\Omega^{c}\times\Omega^{c})^{c}}|u(x)-u(y)|^{p}\,d\mu

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{j\to\infty}\iint_{(\Omega^{c}\times\Omega^{c})^{c}}|u_{j}(x)-u_{j}(y)|^{p}\,d\mu

\displaystyle\leq\iint_{(\Omega^{c}\times\Omega^{c})^{c}}w(x,y)(v(x)-v(y))\,d\mu,

from which we conclude that
\mathcal{E}(u,v-u)\geq 0
.
Thus
u
is a solution of the
\mathcal{K}_{g,g}(\Omega)
-obstacle problem.

The convergence
Hg_{j}\to Hg
a.e. is proved in the same way, just replacing

\mathcal{K}_{g,g}(\Omega)
and
\mathcal{K}_{g_{j},g_{j}}(\Omega)
by
\mathcal{K}_{-\infty,g}(\Omega)
and

\mathcal{K}_{-\infty,g_{j}}(\Omega)
.
As
Hg_{j}
and
Hg
are continuous, we also know
that
\{Hg_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
is a decreasing sequence of

\mathcal{L}
-harmonic functions in
\Omega
.
It then follows from
Harnack’s convergence theorem below
(applied to
\{-Hg_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
),
that there is an
\mathcal{L}
-harmonic function
u
in
\Omega

such that
Hg_{j}\searrow u
everywhere.
In particular,
u\equiv g
in
{\Omega^{c}}
and
u=Hg
a.e. in
\Omega
.
Since both
u
and
Hg
are continuous in
\Omega
, they must be identical.
∎

It remains to state the Harnack’s convergence theorem, which we
will also need later on.

Theorem 7.3
.

(Harnack’s convergence theorem,
Korvenpää–Kuusi–Palatucci
[
36
, Theorem 15]
)

Let
\{u_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
be an increasing sequence of functions
on
\mathbf{R}^{n}
such that

is
\mathcal{L}
-harmonic in
\Omega
,

.
Then either

is
\mathcal{L}
-harmonic in
\Omega

or

in
\Omega
.

This also enables us to show that the existence of a barrier characterizes regularity.
Along the way we
show that regularity for obstacle problems is the same as
for the Dirichlet problem, see

Here is the definition of barriers.

Definition 10.1
.

A function

(relative to
\Omega

1.

\mathcal{L}

\lim_{\Omega\ni x\to x_{0}}u(x)=0

3.

\inf_{B(x_{0},r)^{c}}u>0

\mathcal{L}
-superharmonic in
\Omega

\mathcal{L}

By Theorem

Remark 10.5
.

Depending on the value of
sp
we have three different cases.

Case 1.

sp>n
.
In this case every point has positive capacity, by Lemma
5.5
.
It thus follows from the Kellogg property (Theorem
1.5
)
that every nonempty bounded open set is regular (i.e. all its boundary points are regular).
For Sobolev regularity this also follows directly from
the Wiener criterion (Theorem
4.2
)
together with Lemma 2.17
in Kim–Lee–Lee
[
31
]
,
but for Perron regularity this does not seem to be so straightforward without
appealing to Theorem
1.7
.

Case 2.

1<sp\leq n
.
It follows from Corollary 5.1.14 in Adams–Hedberg
[
1
]

that
{C_{s,p}}(\partial\Omega)>0
, and hence
\Omega
has some regular boundary
points by the Kellogg property (Theorem
1.5
).
In fact the set of regular boundary points must be dense
in
\partial\overline{\Omega}
.
(Note that by Triebel
[
50
, Theorem p. 172 and Remark 4 pp. 189–190]

and
[
1
, Corollary 2.6.8 and Proposition 4.4.4]

the Bessel potential capacity (denoted
by
C_{s,p}
in
[
1
]
) is comparable to our Sobolev capacity
{C_{s,p}}
.)

Case 3.

sp\leq 1
.
In this case it can happen that
{C_{s,p}}(\partial\Omega)=0
,
by Theorem 5.1.9 in
[
1
]
,
in which case the Kellogg property does not yield anything.
Nevertheless, the set of regular boundary points is dense
in
\partial\overline{\Omega}
also in this case.
Indeed, let
x_{0}\in\partial\overline{\Omega}
and
\varepsilon>0
.
Then there is
y\in{\mathbf{R}^{n}}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
with
|y-x_{0}|<\varepsilon
.
Now pick a closest point
x\in\partial\overline{\Omega}
to
y
, i.e.
|x-y|=\min_{z\in\partial\overline{\Omega}}|z-y|
. (This point does not have to be unique.)
Then
x
satisfies the exterior cone condition and is thus regular
by the Wiener criterion (Theorem
4.2
).
Moreover,

|x-x_{0}|\leq|x-y|+|y-x_{0}|\leq|x_{0}-y|+|y-x_{0}|<2\varepsilon.
