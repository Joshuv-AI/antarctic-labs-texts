Bifurcation domains from any high eigenvalue for an overdetermined elliptic problem Thanks: Research was supported by the National Natural Science Foundation of China (Grants No. 12371110 and No. 12301133).
Guowei Dai, Yong Zhang

Abstract
In this paper, we consider the following overdetermined eigenvalue problem on an unbounded domain Ω ⊂ ℝ N + 1 \Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N+1} with N ≥ 1 N\geq 1 { − Δ ​ u = λ ​ u in ​ Ω , u = 0 on ​ ∂ Ω , ∂ ν u = const on ​ ∂ Ω . \left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta u=\lambda u&\text{in}\,\,\Omega,\\ u=0&\text{on}\,\,\partial\Omega,\\ \partial_{\nu}u=\text{const}&\text{on}\,\,\partial\Omega.\end{array}\right. Let λ k \lambda_{k} be the k k -th eigenvalue of the zero-Dirichlet Laplacian on the unit ball for any k ∈ ℕ + k\in\mathbb{N^{+}} with k ≥ 3 k\geq 3 . We can construct k k smooth families of nontrivial unbounded domains Ω \Omega , bifurcating from the straight cylinder, which admit a nonsymmetric solution to the overdetermined problem with changing the sign by k − 1 k-1 times. While the existence of such domains for k = 1 , 2 k=1,2 has been well-known, to the best of our knowledge this is the first construction for any positive integer k ≥ 3 k\geq 3 . Due to the complexity of studying high eigenvalue problem, our proof involves some novel analytic ingredients. These results can be regarded as counterexamples to the Berenstein conjecture on unbounded domain.



Keywords:
Berenstein conjecture; Overdetermined problem; Bifurcation; Eigenvalue; Unbounded domain

Introduction

Overdetermined elliptic problem has important application background in plasma physics
[
27
]
,
nuclear reactors
[
3
]
and tomography
[
26
]
.
A celebrated theorem of Serrin
[
24
]
asserts that one overdetermined condition on the boundary is enough to obtain the radial symmetry solution of the following overdetermined torsion problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Delta u+1=0&\text{in}\,\,\Omega,\\
u>0&\text{in}\,\,\Omega,\\
u=0&\text{on}\,\,\partial\Omega,\\
\partial_{\nu}u=\text{const}&\text{on}\,\,\partial\Omega\end{array}\right.

(1.1)

with
\Omega
being a bounded domain of class
C^{2}
and
\nu
being the unit outward normal vector about
\partial\Omega
. That is to say, if there is a solution to problem (
1.1
) on bounded domain, then
\Omega
must be a ball.
Subsequently, for the more general overdetermined elliptic problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Delta u+f(u)=0&\text{in}\,\,\Omega,\\
u>0&\text{in}\,\,\Omega,\\
u=0&\text{on}\,\,\partial\Omega,\\
\partial_{\nu}u=\text{const}&\text{on}\,\,\partial\Omega,\end{array}\right.

(1.2)

where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a given regular domain and
f
is a Lipschitz function, Berestycki, Caffarelli and Nirenberg
[
6
]
proposed the following conjecture.

BCN Conjecture.
If
\Omega
is a smooth domain and
\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
is connected such that there exists a bounded solution to problem (
1.2
), then
\Omega
is either a ball, a half-space, a generalized cylinder
B^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{N-k}
where
B^{k}
is a ball in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, or the complement of one of them.

It is well known that many positive answers to the BCN conjecture have been given in bounded domain by using the so-called moving plane method. If
f
is a Lipschitz function and
\Omega
is bounded, Pucci and Serrin
[
18
]
proved that BCN Conjecture is holding. Under some suitable assumptions, Berestycki, Caffarelli and Nirenberg
[
6
]
proved that if problem (
1.2
) admits a smooth bounded solution, then
\Omega
is a half-space.
If
\Omega
is a globally
Lipschitz smooth epigraph of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
with
N=2
,
3
, Farina and Valdinoci
[
11
]
proved that
\Omega
admits a positive solution
u
to problem (
1.2
) with
C^{1}
bistable nonlinearity must be a half-space and
u
must be a function of only one variable, which can be seen as a confirm answer to BCN or Berenstein conjecture on unbounded domain.
When
N=2
, with some more weaker conditions on
f
and
u
, Ros, Ruiz and Sicbaldi
[
20
]
proved that the BCN Conjecture is valid on
C^{1,\alpha}
domain whose boundary is unbounded and connected. With some suitable conditions, Serrin’s result is also valid on some exterior domains
[
1
,
19
]
.

In two surprising papers however, Sicbaldi
[
25
]
constructed a counter-example to BCN Conjecture and proved that the cylinder
B_{1}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}
with
N\geq 2
can be perturbed to an unbounded domain whose boundary is a periodic
hypersurface of revolution with respect to the
\mathbb{R}
-axis, such that the problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Delta u+\lambda u=0&\text{in}\,\,\Omega,\\
u=0&\text{on}\,\,\partial\Omega,\\
\partial_{\nu}u=\text{const}&\text{on}\,\,\partial\Omega\end{array}\right.

has a bounded positive
solution, where
B_{1}^{N}
is the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
centered at the origin.
Subsequently, Schlenk and Sicbaldi
[
23
]
further proved that the above conclusion is valid for
N=1
and these new extremal domains belong to a smooth bifurcation family of domains.
It is worth noting that these nontrivial solutions constructed in
[
25
]
can be regarded as the perturbation of the eigenfunction
\phi_{1}
corresponding to the first eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
of the Laplacian on the unit ball, which are positive. Recently, we
[
9
]
prove the existence of two smooth families of unbounded domains in
\mathbb{R}^{N+1}

such that the above overdetermined eigenvalue problem has a sign-changing solution, which can be seen the perturbation of the eigenfunction corresponding to
\lambda_{2}
.

The natural question is whether can we obtain sign-changing solutions by perturbing eigenfunction function corresponding to any high eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}
?
In this paper, we managed to show the existence of nontrivial sign-changing solutions bifurcating from the eigenfunction
\phi_{k}
corresponding to any high eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}
of the Laplacian on the unit ball for
k\geq 3
. In fact, these results also can be seen as counter-examples to the following Conjecture on unbounded domains due to Berenstein
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
.

Berenstein conjecture.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded
C^{2,\alpha}
domain in
\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
with
\alpha\in(0,1)
. If there exists a nontrivial
solution

of the overdetermined eigenvalue problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Delta u+\lambda u=0&\text{in}\,\,\Omega,\\
u=0&\text{on}\,\,\partial\Omega,\\
\partial_{\nu}u=\text{const}&\text{on}\,\,\partial\Omega,\end{array}\right.

(1.3)

then
\Omega
is a ball, where
\nu
is the unit outer normal on
\partial\Omega
.

Our main results are stated as follows.

Let

with

k-1

T_{s}
-periodic functions

Different from

Theorem 1.2.

Under the assumptions of Theorem 1.1, there exist

positive numbers
T_{2,*}
,

,

with

Moreover, there exist

T_{s}
-periodic functions

r_{j}(t):\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow\left(0,1+s\left(\beta\cos\left(\frac{2\pi}{T_{s}}t\right)+\sum_{n=1}^{m}\gamma_{n}\cos\left(\frac{2l_{j_{n}}\pi}{T_{s}}t\right)\right)+sw_{s}\left(\frac{2\pi}{T_{s}}t\right)\right)

Preliminaries

2.1
The formulation of the bifurcation problem

Consider the following eigenvalue problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Delta u+\lambda u=0&\text{in}\,\,B_{1},\\
u=0&\text{on}\,\,\partial B_{1}.\end{array}\right.

(2.1)

It is well known (see
[
7
,
13
]
or

[
28
, p. 269]
) that problem (
2.1
) has a sequence eigenvalues
0<\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}<\cdots<\lambda_{k}\nearrow+\infty
.
Let
\overline{\phi}_{k}
be the corresponding eigenfunction
to
\lambda_{k}
with
\int_{B_{1}}\overline{\phi}_{k}^{2}\,\text{d}x=1/\left(2\pi\right)
and
\overline{\phi}_{k}(0)>0
.
From now on, we use
C
or
C_{k}
to denote some positive constant whose exact value may change from line to line.

In particular, for
N=1
, we have that

\lambda_{k}=\frac{(2k-1)^{2}\pi^{2}}{4}\,\,\,\text{and}\,\,\,\overline{\phi}_{k}(r)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\cos\left(\frac{(2k-1)\pi}{2}r\right).

(2.2)

For
N=3
, it follows from
[
9
, Lemma 2.1]
that

\lambda_{k}=k^{2}\pi^{2}\,\,\,\text{and}\,\,\,C_{k}\overline{\phi}_{k}(r)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{\sin(k\pi r)}{r}&\text{if}\,\,r>0,\\
k\pi&\text{if}\,\,r=0,\end{array}\right.

(2.3)

where
C_{k}
is chosen such that
\int_{B_{1}}\overline{\phi}_{k}^{2}\,\text{d}x=1/\left(2\pi\right)
.
Clearly, we see that
\left(\lambda_{k},{\phi}_{k}(x,t)\right)
with
{\phi}_{k}(x,t)=\overline{\phi}_{k}(x)
is also a solution pair of

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Delta_{\mathring{g}}{\phi}+\lambda{\phi}=0&\text{in}\,\,C_{1}^{T},\\
{\phi}=0&\text{on}\,\,\partial C_{1}^{T},\end{array}\right.

(2.4)

where
\mathring{g}
is the Euclidean metric and

C_{1}^{T}=\left\{(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}/T\mathbb{Z}:|x|<1\right\}.

It is obvious that

\int_{C_{1}^{2\pi}}{\phi}_{k}^{2}\,\text{d}\text{vol}_{\mathring{g}}=1.

As
\phi_{k}
does not depend on
t
and it is radial, we can denote
\phi_{k}(x,t)
by
\phi_{k}(r)
with
r=|x|
.

We now transform the aim problem (
1.3
) into an abstract operator equation.
For any
v\in\mathcal{C}^{2,\alpha}_{\text{even},0}\left(\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}\right)
with
v>-1
, define

C_{1+v}^{T}=\left\{(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}/T\mathbb{Z}:|x|<1+v\left(\frac{2\pi t}{T}\right)\right\}

for all
T>0
.
Then we consider the following eigenvalue problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Delta_{\mathring{g}}{\phi}+\lambda{\phi}=0&\text{in}\,\,C_{1+v}^{T},\\
{\phi}=0&\text{on}\,\,\partial C_{1+v}^{T}.\end{array}\right.

(2.5)

It follows from
[
2
, Theorem 1.13]
that the problem (
2.5
) has a sequence of eigenvalues
0<\lambda_{1,v}<\lambda_{2,v}\leq\cdots
,
\lambda_{k,v}\nearrow+\infty
.

Let
{\phi}_{k,v}
be an eigenfunction corresponding to
\lambda_{k,v}
such that

\int_{C_{1+v}^{2\pi}}{\phi}_{k,v}^{2}\left(x,\frac{T}{2\pi}t\right)\,\text{dvol}_{\mathring{g}}=1,

where

\partial C_{1+v}^{T}=\left\{(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}/T\mathbb{Z}:|x|=1+v\left(\frac{2\pi t}{T}\right)\right\}.

By applying
[
12
, Theorem 11.4]
, we can see that
\phi_{k,v}\in\mathcal{C}^{2,\alpha}\left(\overline{C_{1+v}^{T}}\right)
.
Obviously,
{\phi}_{k,v}
and
\lambda_{k,v}
depend smoothly on
v
(the smoothness can also be obtained by the Implicit Function Theorem as that of
[
22
, Proposition 4.1]
), and
{\phi}_{k,0}=\pm{\phi}_{k}
,
\lambda_{k,0}=\lambda_{k}
.
For simplicity, we assume
{\phi}_{k,0}={\phi}_{k}
.
For any fixed
t
, we see that
{\phi}_{k,v}
is radially symmetric with respect to the first variable.
Hence, for any fixed
t
,
{\phi}_{k,v}(r,t)
has exactly
k-1
simple zeros in
(0,1+v(t))
which are denoted by
r_{1}(t),\ldots,r_{k-1}(t)
.
It follows from Implicit Function Theorem that
r_{j}(t)
with
j\in\{1,\ldots,k-1\}
is
C^{2,\alpha}
in local. By the arbitrariness of
t
,
{\phi}_{k,v}(x,t)
has exactly
k-1
nodal domains.
Thus,
r_{j}(t)
with
j\in\{1,\ldots,k-1\}
is the zero line of
{\phi}_{k,v}(r,t)
.

Define

\mathcal{N}(v,T)=\mathring{g}\left(\nabla{\phi}_{v},\vartheta\right)\Big|_{\partial C_{1+v}^{T}}-\frac{1}{\text{Vol}_{\mathring{g}}\left(\partial C_{1+v}^{T}\right)}\int_{\partial C_{1+v}^{T}}\mathring{g}\left(\nabla{\phi}_{v},\vartheta\right)\,\text{dvol}_{\mathring{g}},

where
\vartheta
denotes the unit normal vector field to
\partial C_{1+v}^{T}
.
We further define

F(v,T)=\mathcal{N}(v,T)(t)\left(\frac{T}{2\pi}t\right).

Since
\partial_{r}{\phi}_{k}(1)
is a constant, one has
F(0,T)=0
for any
T>0
.

To use the Crandall-Rabinowitz bifurcation theorem to obtain the nontrivial solutions of
F(v,T)=0
, we would find the degenerate point of the linearization operator
F_{v}(0,T)
and verify the transversality condition. Thus let us first consider the following equation

\left(\partial_{r}^{2}+\frac{N-1}{r}\partial_{r}+\lambda_{k}\right)c-\left(\frac{2m\pi}{T}\right)^{2}c=0

(2.6)

with
m\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
,
c(1)=-\partial_{r}\phi_{k}(1)
and
c^{\prime}(0)=0
. We will find that the problem (
2.6
) is well-posed far from some special ”sigularity” points. This process is achieved by following the similar discussion as in
[
9
, Proposition 2.1]
.

Proposition 2.1.

There is no solution to problem (
2.6
) when
T=T_{i}:=2m\pi/\sqrt{\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{i}}
for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,k-1\}
, however the problem (
2.6
) has a unique solution
c_{m}
if
T\neq 2m\pi/\sqrt{\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{i}}
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,k-1\}
. Moreover,
c_{m}^{\prime}
is analytic for
T\in(0,+\infty)\setminus\left\{T_{1},\ldots,T_{k-1}\right\}
.

Proof.
For
T=T_{i}
with
i\in\{1,\ldots,k-1\}
, we have that

\left(\partial_{r}^{2}+\frac{N-1}{r}\partial_{r}+\lambda_{i}\right)c=0.

Set
\vartheta(r):=c(r)+\partial_{r}\phi_{k}(1)
. Then, we can see that

\left(\partial_{r}^{2}+\frac{N-1}{r}\partial_{r}+\lambda_{i}\right)\vartheta=\lambda_{i}\phi_{k}^{\prime}(1)

(2.7)

with
\vartheta^{\prime}(0)=0
and
\vartheta(1)=0
.
Since
\phi_{i}
satisfies

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\left(r^{N-1}\phi_{i}^{\prime}\right)^{\prime}+\lambda_{i}r^{N-1}\phi_{i}=0&\text{in}\,\,(0,1),\\
\phi_{i}^{\prime}(0)=\phi_{i}(1)=0,\end{array}\right.

by integration we obtain that

\lambda_{i}\phi_{k}^{\prime}(1)\int_{0}^{1}r^{N-1}\phi_{i}\,dr=-\phi_{k}^{\prime}(1)\phi_{i}^{\prime}(1)\neq 0.

It follows that
\lambda_{i}\phi_{k}^{\prime}(1)
is not orthogonal to
\phi_{i}
.
By the Fredholm alternative theorem
[
10
, Theorem 6.2.4]
, problem (
2.7
) has no solution.

If
T\neq 2m\pi/\sqrt{\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{i}}
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,k-1\}
,
we have that

\left(\partial_{r}^{2}+\frac{N-1}{r}\partial_{r}+\left(\lambda_{k}-\left(\frac{2m\pi}{T}\right)^{2}\right)\right)\vartheta=\left(\lambda_{k}-\left(\frac{2m\pi}{T}\right)^{2}\right)\phi_{k}^{\prime}(1).

We find that

\lambda_{k}-\left(\frac{2m\pi}{T}\right)^{2}<\lambda_{k}\,\,\,\text{and}\,\,\,\lambda_{k}-\left(\frac{2m\pi}{T}\right)^{2}\neq\lambda_{i}.

Using the Fredholm alternative theorem
[
10
, Theorem 6.2.5]
again we see that problem (
2.7
) has a unique solution.
In conclusion, problem (
2.6
) has a unique solution if and only if
T\neq 2m\pi/\sqrt{\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{i}}
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,k-1\}
.
We use
c_{m}
to denote the unique solution.

We finally prove the analyticity of
c_{m}^{\prime}
. It is enough to show that
c_{m}
is analytic with respect to
T
.
We use the following fact to show the analyticity of
c_{m}
:
if
F
is an invertible operator, by the equality

(I-sF)^{-1}=\sum_{j\geq 0}s^{j}F^{j}

for each
s\in\mathbb{R}
, the solution of

\left(F-\frac{\rho}{T^{2}}I\right)u=h

is analytic in
T
, where
I
is the identity,
h
is any continuous function and
\rho
is a constant.

Consider

F=\partial_{r}^{2}+\frac{N-1}{r}\partial_{r}+\lambda_{k}-\left(\frac{2m\pi}{T}\right)^{2}

acting on
X:=\left\{c\in C[0,1]\cap C^{2}[0,1):c(1)=-\partial_{r}\phi_{k}(1),c^{\prime}(0)=0\right\}
.
Then, for any
f\in C[0,1]
, we consider

\left(\partial_{r}^{2}+\frac{N-1}{r}\partial_{r}+\lambda_{k}\right)c-\left(\frac{2m\pi}{T}\right)^{2}c=f(x)

on
X

2.2
Some properties of Bessel functions

In this subsection, we state some important properties of Bessel functions which would be used in the proof of our main theorem. Although the similar content has been given in
[
9
,
23
]
, we now show them again for convenience.
For
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
, the Bessel function of the first kind is defined by

J_{\tau}(s)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{m}\left(\frac{s}{2}\right)^{2m+\tau}}{m!\Gamma(\tau+m+1)},

which is a solution of the Bessel equation

s^{2}f^{\prime\prime}(s)+sf^{\prime}(s)+\left(s^{2}-\tau^{2}\right)f(s)=0.

If
\tau
is an integer, then
J_{-\tau}(s)=(-1)^{\tau}J_{\tau}(s)
. Moreover, one has that
J_{0}(0)=1
and
J_{\tau}(0)=0
for all
\tau>0
.

We have known that the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of the Dirichlet Laplacian on the unit ball have the following relations

\lambda_{k}=j_{\nu,k}^{2}\,\,\,\text{and}\,\,\,|x|^{\nu}\overline{\phi}_{k}=C_{k}J_{\nu}\left(j_{\nu,k}|x|\right),

where
j_{\nu,k}
is the
k
-th positive zero of
J_{\nu}
for
\nu=(N-2)/2
with
N\geq 2
. Denote

\mu=:\frac{2\pi}{j_{\nu,k}}=\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}}.

(2.10)

Here we assume that
\overline{\phi}_{1}
is positive. It follows that
J_{\nu}
is positive in
\left(0,\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}\right)
.

In
[
9
]
, we also verified that the above relation is also valid for
N=1
or
N=3
.
In particular,

J_{-\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{(2k-1)\pi}{2}r\right)=\left(\frac{(2k-1)\pi}{2}r\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\cos\left(\frac{(2k-1)\pi}{2}r\right)

and

J_{\frac{1}{2}}\left(k\pi r\right)=\left(k\pi r\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\sin(k\pi r)}{r},

which implies that
\lim_{z\rightarrow 0^{+}}J_{-\frac{1}{2}}(z)=+\infty
, the first positive zero of
J_{\frac{1}{2}}
is
\pi
and the second positive zero of
J_{\frac{1}{2}}
is
2\pi
.

If
\tau
is real, then
J_{\tau}(s)
has an infinite number of positive real zeros which are all simple. The
m
-th positive zero of
J_{\tau}(s)
is denoted by
j_{\tau,m}
.
When
\tau\geq 0
, the zeros have the following interlace property

j_{\tau,1}<j_{\tau+1,1}<j_{\tau,2}<j_{\tau+1,2}<j_{\tau,3}<\cdots.

(2.11)

When
\tau\geq-1
, the zeros of
J_{\tau}(s)
are all real.

For
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
, the modified Bessel function of the first kind is defined by

I_{\tau}(s)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{\left(\frac{s}{2}\right)^{2m+\tau}}{m!\Gamma(\tau+m+1)},

(2.12)

which is the solution of the modified Bessel equation

s^{2}f^{\prime\prime}(s)+sf^{\prime}(s)-\left(s^{2}+\tau^{2}\right)f(s)=0.

Proof of Proposition 6.1.
We can find that

\text{dim}\text{Ker}\left(D_{u}\widetilde{F}(\mu,0)\right)=1.

Since
D_{u}\widetilde{F}\left(\lambda,0\right)
is a Fredholm operator with index zero, there exists a one-dimensional
closed subspace
\widetilde{Z}
such that

\widetilde{Y}=\widetilde{Z}\oplus\text{Im}\left(D_{u}\widetilde{F}(\mu,0)\right).

For any fixed
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
, define

by

G(s,z,\lambda)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{1}{s}\widetilde{F}\left(\lambda,s\left(w_{i}+z\right)\right)&s\neq 0,\\
D_{u}\widetilde{F}\left(\lambda,0\right)\left(w_{i}+z\right)&s=0.\end{array}\right.

Clearly,
G\left(0,0,\mu\right)=0
.
Since
\widetilde{F}
is
C^{1}
and
\widetilde{F}_{\lambda u}
is continuous, it follows that
G_{z}
and
G_{\lambda}
are continuous
and

G_{z}\left(0,0,\mu\right)=D_{u}\widetilde{F}(\mu,0)\,\,\,\text{and}\,\,\,G_{\lambda}\left(0,0,\lambda_{0}\right)=\widetilde{F}_{\lambda u}\left(\mu,0\right)w_{i}.

Then we have that

G_{(z,\lambda)}\left(0,0,\mu\right)\left(z_{*},\lambda_{*}\right)=D_{u}\widetilde{F}(\mu,0)z_{*}+\lambda_{*}\widetilde{F}_{\lambda u}\left(\mu,0\right)w_{i}:X_{0}\times\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow\widetilde{Y}.

We next prove that
G_{(z,\lambda)}\left(0,0,\mu\right)
is an isomorphism on to
\widetilde{Y}
.

We first assume that

G_{(z,\lambda)}\left(0,0,\mu\right)\left(z_{*},\lambda_{*}\right)=D_{u}\widetilde{F}(\mu,0)z_{*}+\lambda_{*}\widetilde{F}_{\lambda u}\left(\mu,0\right)w_{i}=0.

If
\lambda_{*}
does not vanish, then we have that

\widetilde{F}_{\lambda u}\left(\mu,0\right)w_{i}=-\lambda_{*}^{-1}D_{u}\widetilde{F}(\mu,0)z_{*}\in\text{Im}\left(D_{u}\widetilde{F}(\mu,0)\right),

which is contradicted with the transversality condition.
Thus we have that

\lambda_{*}=0,

which leads further to that
D_{u}\widetilde{F}(\mu,0)z_{*}=0
. Since
D_{u}\widetilde{F}(\mu,0):X_{0}\rightarrow\text{Im}\left(D_{u}\widetilde{F}(\mu,0)\right)
is an isomorphism, we obtain that

.
Hence we have shown that
G_{(z,\lambda)}\left(0,0,\mu\right)
is an injection.

We now show that
G_{(z,\lambda)}\left(0,0,\mu\right)
is also a surjection.
For any

such that

G_{(z,\lambda)}\left(0,0,\mu\right)\left(z_{*},\lambda_{*}\right)=D_{u}\widetilde{F}(\mu,0)z_{*}+\lambda_{*}\widetilde{F}_{\lambda u}\left(\mu,0\right)w_{i}=y.

From the above argument we know that
\widetilde{F}_{\lambda u}\left(\mu,0\right)w_{i}\in\widetilde{Y}
.
By the Hahn-Banach theorem,
there exists a linear functional

with
\widetilde{Y}^{*}
being the dual space of
\widetilde{Y}
such that

\widetilde{Y}_{0}=\{v\in\widetilde{Y}:l(v)=0\}=\text{Im}\left(D_{u}\widetilde{F}(\mu,0)\right).

Applying
l
on the both sides of
G_{(z,\lambda)}\left(0,0,\mu\right)\left(z_{*},\lambda_{*}\right)=y
, we obtain that

\lambda_{*}=l(y)/l\left(\widetilde{F}_{\lambda u}\left(\mu,0\right)w_{i}\right).

Furthermore, we obtain that

D_{u}\widetilde{F}(\mu,0)z_{*}=y-\frac{l(y)}{l\left(\widetilde{F}_{\lambda u}\left(\mu,0\right)w_{i}\right)}\widetilde{F}_{\lambda u}\left(\mu,0\right)w_{i}.

Since
D_{u}\widetilde{F}(\mu,0):X_{0}\rightarrow\text{Im}\left(D_{u}\widetilde{F}(\mu,0)\right)
is an isomorphism, it is reversible. Then we have that

z_{*}=\left(D_{u}\widetilde{F}(\mu,0)\right)^{-1}\left(y-\frac{l(y)}{l\left(\widetilde{F}_{\lambda u}\left(\mu,0\right)w_{i}\right)}\widetilde{F}_{\lambda u}\left(\mu,0\right)w_{i}\right).

Therefore, we prove that

is a surjection.

Based on the argument above, it is obvious that the inverse mapping of

is continuous.
Since

Thus, the desired conclusions can be obtained by applying the
