Existence and polynomial time construction of biregular, bipartite Ramanujan graphs of all degrees
Aurelien Gribinski, Adam W. Marcus

Abstract
We prove that there exist bipartite, biregular Ramanujan graphs of every degree and every number of vertices provided that the cardinalities of the two sets of the bipartition divide each other. This generalizes the main result of Marcus, Spielman, and Srivastava [ 14 ] and, similar to theirs, the proof is based on the analysis of expected polynomials. The primary difference is the use of some new machinery involving rectangular convolutions, developed in a companion paper [ 5 ] . We also prove the constructibility of such graphs in polynomial time in the number of vertices, extending a result of Cohen [ 8 ] to this biregular case.

Introduction

Ramanujan graphs are expander graphs with optimal asymptotic spectral
properties.
This paper proves two new results concerning Ramanujan graphs.
Our first result extends the methods of
[
14
]
to show the existence of

One notable aspect of the result in

Our second result is an extension of this construction to the biregular

1.1
Motivation

(c,d)
-biregular bipartite graphs generalize
d
-regular bipartite graphs in
an obvious way
— they allow for graphs which have partitions of different sizes.
This makes them better suited for applications such as deep neural networks and
LDPC codes.
Similar to the
d
-regular case, however, specific graphs can be better or
worse suited for a particular applications.
In many situations, the “suitability” can be expressed as a function of
the expansion properties of the graph.
While most expansion properties are NP-complete to compute, there is a
well-know relation between many of these expansions properties and the
so-called
spectral gap
via Cheeger’s inequality.

To be specific, note that the adjacency matrix of a
(c,d)
-biregular
bipartite graph has the form

B=\begin{pmatrix}0&{A}\\
{A}^{T}&0\end{pmatrix}

where
A
is an
m\times n
rectangular matrix with
d

1

row and
c

1
’s in each column.
Such matrices necessarily have eigenvalues
\pm\sqrt{cd}
(often called the
“trivial” eigenvalues); the spectral gap is then the distance from these
trivial eigenvalues to the next largest eigenvalue (in absolute value).
Since the eigenvalues of
B
are symmetric about
0
, maximizing the spectral
gap can be simplified to minimizing the size of the second eigenvalue.

There is a well-known lower bound on the size of the second eigenvalue of
biregular, bipartite graphs (at least asymptotically) that is an extension of
the more well-known Alon–Boppana bound
[
4
]
:

Theorem 1.1

(Feng–Li)
.

Let

be a sequence of
(c,d)
biregular, bipartite graphs and
suppose there exists a real number
\theta
for which the second eigenvalue of

is at most
\theta
for all

.
Then

\theta\geq\sqrt{c-1}+\sqrt{d-1}.

Following
[
11
]
, we call a sequence of graphs for which

Ramanujan
.
The goal, then, is to find such a sequence.

Remark 1
.

Note that in the special case

, a
(c,d)
-biregular graph is simply a

d
-regular bipartite graph, and so (
1
) reduces to the well-known

The general idea of the algorithm is to use the interlacing family tree
constructed in the existence proof.
If we consider the root node to correspond to the empty graph, then the

Every

k

The leaves (at depth

Now we can assign polynomials to each node — the leaves will have the

\chi\left[\sum_{i=1}^{d}(P_{i}\oplus S_{i})M(P_{i}\oplus S_{i})^{T}\right]

The fact that this forms an interlacing family implies two things:

•

Every polynomial associated to a node has only real roots

•

The algorithm goes down the tree

At each step, it computes all of the characteristic polynomials associated to

After

There are two things that need to be checked:

•

At each level the number of characteristic polynomials we need to check

•

The first item is straightforward, and the second is straightforward when we

Assume we are at level for which

Let

directly to the
characteristic polynomials of our adjacency matrices, we will apply them to the
polynomials that we get after projecting them orthogonally to the all
1

vector.
Fortunately (due to the biregularity), the all
1

eigenvector of all of the other matrices we will need to consider, and so this
projection will simply pull off a single (trivial) root without affecting the
remaining ones.
All of this is stated in the following theorem (recall that we are using

\vec{1}_{n}
to denote the normalized version of the all
1

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
A
and
B
be
m\times n
matrices for which

A\vec{1}_{n}=a\vec{1}_{m}\and A^{T}\vec{1}_{m}=a\vec{1}_{n}\and B\vec{1}_{n}=b\vec{1}_{m}\and B^{T}\vec{1}_{m}=b\vec{1}_{n}

so that

\chi\left[A^{T}A\right]=(x-a^{2})p(x)\and\chi\left[B^{T}B\right]=(x-b^{2})q(x).

Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\in\mathcal{P}_{m}\\
S\in\mathcal{P}_{n}\end{subarray}}\left\{\mathbb{S}\chi\left[\begin{pmatrix}0&{A}\\
{A}^{T}&0\end{pmatrix}+(P\oplus S)\begin{pmatrix}0&{B}\\
{B}^{T}&0\end{pmatrix}(P\oplus S)^{T}\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}\left\{\left[x-(a+b)^{2}\right]\left[p\boxplus_{m-1,n-1}q\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(x^{2}-(a+b)^{2}\right)\mathbb{S}(p\boxplus_{m-1,n-1}q)

Proof.

We begin by applying change of basis to
A
and
B
related to their singular
value decompositions.
More precisely, let
U\in\mathcal{O}_{m},V\in\mathcal{O}_{n}
, such that the last

U

is
\vec{1}_{m}
and the last vector of

is
\vec{1}_{n}
.
As for any
P\in\mathcal{P}_{m},S\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
:
P\vec{1}_{m}=\vec{1}_{m},S\vec{1}_{n}=\vec{1}_{n}
, we
get

U^{T}AV=\hat{A}\oplus a\qquad U^{T}BV=\hat{B}\oplus b\qquad U^{T}PU=\hat{P}\oplus 1\qquad V^{T}SV=\hat{S}\oplus 1

where the matrices
\hat{P}
and
\hat{S}
are random elements of

\mathcal{A}_{m-1}
and
\mathcal{A}_{n-1}
respectively.
Conjugating by
U\oplus V

\displaystyle\chi\left[\begin{pmatrix}0&{A}\\
{A}^{T}&0\end{pmatrix}+(P\oplus S)\begin{pmatrix}0&{B}\\
{B}^{T}&0\end{pmatrix}(P\oplus S)^{T}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\chi\left[\begin{pmatrix}0&{(\hat{A}\oplus a)}\\
{(\hat{A}\oplus a)}^{T}&0\end{pmatrix}+(\hat{P}\oplus 1)\oplus(\hat{S}\oplus 1)\begin{pmatrix}0&{(\hat{B}\oplus b)}\\
{(\hat{B}\oplus b)}^{T}&0\end{pmatrix}(\hat{P}\oplus 1)\oplus(\hat{S}\oplus 1)^{T}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\chi\left[\begin{pmatrix}0&{\big((\hat{A}+\hat{P}\hat{B}\hat{S}^{T})\oplus(a+b)\big)}\\
{\big((\hat{A}+\hat{P}\hat{B}\hat{S}^{T})\oplus(a+b)\big)}^{T}&0\end{pmatrix}\right]

So averaging gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\in\mathcal{P}_{m}\\
S\in\mathcal{P}_{n}\end{subarray}}\left\{\mathbb{S}\chi\left[\left((\hat{A}+\hat{P}\hat{B}\hat{S}^{T})\oplus(a+b)\right)\big((\hat{A}+\hat{P}\hat{B}\hat{S}^{T})\oplus(a+b)\big)^{T}\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(x^{2}-(a+b)^{2}\right)\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\in\mathcal{P}_{m}\\
S\in\mathcal{P}_{n}\end{subarray}}\left\{\mathbb{S}\chi\left[\left(\hat{A}+\hat{P}\hat{B}\hat{S}^{T})\right)\left(\hat{A}+\hat{P}\hat{B}\hat{S}^{T}\right)^{T}\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(x^{2}-(a+b)^{2}\right)\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\in\mathcal{P}_{m}\\
S\in\mathcal{P}_{n}\end{subarray}}\left\{\chi\left[\begin{pmatrix}0&{\hat{A}}\\
{\hat{A}}^{T}&0\end{pmatrix}+(\hat{P}\oplus\hat{S})\begin{pmatrix}0&{\hat{B}}\\
{\hat{B}}^{T}&0\end{pmatrix}(\hat{P}\oplus\hat{S})^{T}\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(x^{2}-(a+b)^{2}\right)\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\hat{P}\in\mathcal{A}_{m-1}\\
\hat{S}\in\mathcal{A}_{n-1}\end{subarray}}\left\{\chi\left[\begin{pmatrix}0&{\hat{A}}\\
{\hat{A}}^{T}&0\end{pmatrix}+(\hat{P}\oplus\hat{S})\begin{pmatrix}0&{\hat{B}}\\
{\hat{B}}^{T}&0\end{pmatrix}(\hat{P}\oplus\hat{S})^{T}\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(x^{2}-(a+b)^{2}\right)(p\boxplus_{m-1,n-1}q)

where the last equality comes from the fact that
p(x)=\chi\left[\hat{A}^{T}\hat{A}\right]
and
q(x)=\chi\left[\hat{B}^{T}\hat{B}\right]
and that

\mathcal{A}_{m}
and
\mathcal{A}_{n}
are minor-orthogonal and Theorem
2.1
.
∎

Applying Theorem

Corollary 2.7
.

Let

be

A_{i}\vec{1}_{n}=a_{i}\vec{1}_{m}\and{A_{i}}^{T}\vec{1}_{m}=a_{i}\vec{1}_{n}\and\chi\left[A_{i}^{T}A_{i}\right]=(x-a_{i}^{2})p_{i}(x)

Then

\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}P_{1},\dots,P_{k}\\
S_{1},\dots,S_{k}\end{subarray}}\left\{\chi\left[\sum_{i=1}^{k}(P_{i}\oplus S_{i})\begin{pmatrix}0&{A_{i}}\\
{A_{i}}^{T}&0\end{pmatrix}(P_{i}\oplus S_{i})^{T}\right]\right\}=\left(x^{2}-\left(\sum_{i=1}^{l}a_{i}\right)^{2}\right)\mathbb{S}(p_{1}\boxplus\dots\boxplus p_{k})

where

.

3
Ramanujan Bound

In this section we use the results of the previous section to get the Ramanujan
bound (proving Theorem
1.2
).
We recall that the
adjacency matrix of any
(n,k,d)
-graph has the form:

B=\sum_{i=1}^{d}(P_{i}\oplus S_{i})\begin{pmatrix}0&{I_{n}^{[k]}}\\
{I_{n}^{[k]}}^{T}&0\end{pmatrix}(P_{i}\oplus S_{i})^{T}\qquad\text{where}\qquad I_{n}^{[k]}=\begin{pmatrix}I_{n}\\
I_{n}\\
\vdots\\
I_{n}\end{pmatrix}

for some permutation matrices
P_{i}
and
S_{i}
.
We first note that
B
satisfies the eigenvector properties needed to use
Corollary
2.7
.
In particular, we have for
A=I_{n}^{[k]}
and
m=kn
,

A\vec{1}_{n}=\sqrt{\frac{m}{n}}\vec{1}_{m}=\sqrt{k}\vec{1}_{m}\and A^{T}\vec{1}_{m}=k\sqrt{\frac{n}{m}}\vec{1}_{n}=\sqrt{k}\vec{1}_{n}

and

\chi\left[A^{T}A\right]=(x-k)^{n}=(x-\sqrt{k}^{2})(x-k)^{n-1}

Hence by Corollary
2.7
we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\{\chi\left[B\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=\left(x^{2}-\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sqrt{k}\right)^{2}\right)\mathbb{S}(p\boxplus_{m-1,n-1}\dots\boxplus_{m-1,n-1}p)\quad\text{
($d$ times)}

\displaystyle=(x-d\sqrt{k})(x+d\sqrt{k})\mathbb{S}(p\boxplus_{m-1,n-1}\dots\boxplus_{m-1,n-1}p)

for
p(x):=(x-k)^{n-1}
.
As the first two factors are the “trivial” eigenvalues, we are then
interested in the largest root of the polynomial

\mathbb{S}(p\boxplus_{m-1,n-1}\dots\boxplus_{m-1,n-1}p).

(3)

To this end, we consider the polynomial

q_{d}^{[\theta,m,n]}:=\mathbb{S}\underbrace{\left((x-\theta)^{n}\boxplus_{m,n}(x-\theta)^{n}\boxplus_{m,n}\dots\boxplus_{m,n}(x-\theta)^{n}\right)}_{d\text{ times}},

(4)

For the sake of generality, we prove the following proposition for general
values of
m\geq n
here (not
m=kn
) as in the rest of this
section.

Proposition 3.1
.

For all
u,\theta\geq 0
and all integers
m,n,d
with
m\geq n

we have

\lambda_{1}\left(q_{d}^{[\theta,m,n]}\right)\leq\Big(d\sqrt{\theta+m^{2}u^{2}}-dmu+(m+n)u\Big)^{2}-(m-n)^{2}u^{2}=:R^{[\theta,m,n]}_{d}(u)

Proof.

By the first part of Theorem
2.4
, we can directly upper-bound for all

u\geq 0
:

\lambda_{1}\left(q_{d}^{[\theta,m,n]}\right)\leq\mathcal{Q}^{m,n}_{q_{d}}(u^{2}):={t_{d}^{[\theta,m,n]}}.

Furthermore, by the second part of Theorem
2.4
, we have

\displaystyle\sqrt{{t_{d}^{[\theta,m,n]}}^{2}+(m-n)^{2}u^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{{t_{d-1}^{[\theta,m,n]}}^{2}+(m-n)^{2}u^{2}}+\sqrt{{t_{1}^{[\theta,m,n]}}^{2}+(m-n)^{2}u^{2}}-(m+n)u

\displaystyle\leq d\sqrt{{t_{1}^{[\theta,m,n]}}^{2}+(m-n)^{2}u^{2}}-(d-1)(m+n)u.

(5)

We claim that

\sqrt{({t_{1}^{[\theta,m,n]}}^{2}+(m-n)^{2}u^{2}}=nu+\sqrt{\theta+m^{2}u^{2}}

(6)

which, if it holds, can be plugged into (
5
) to imply that

\sqrt{{t_{d}^{[\theta,m,n]}}^{2}+(m-n)^{2}u^{2}}\leq d\sqrt{\theta+m^{2}u^{2}}-dmu+(m+n)u

for any value of
u\geq 0
(hence proving the proposition).
To see that (
6
) holds, we note that (by definition)

{t_{1}^{[\theta,m,n]}}^{2}=\mathrm{maxroot}\left\{(x^{2}-\theta)^{2}-4mnu^{2}(x^{2}-\theta)-4n^{2}\theta u^{2}\right\}

which, by the quadratic equation gives

{t_{1}^{[\theta,m,n]}}^{2}-\theta=\mathrm{maxroot}\left\{x^{2}-4mnu^{2}x-4n^{2}\theta u^{2}\right\}=2nmu^{2}+2nu\sqrt{\theta+m^{2}u^{2}}.

Hence we have

\displaystyle{t_{1}^{[\theta,m,n]}}^{2}+(m-n)^{2}u^{2}

\displaystyle=\theta+2nmu^{2}+2nu\sqrt{\theta+m^{2}u^{2}}+(m-n)^{2}u^{2}

\displaystyle=\theta+m^{2}u^{2}+n^{2}u^{2}+2nu\sqrt{\theta+m^{2}u^{2}}

\displaystyle=\left(nu+\sqrt{\theta+m^{2}u^{2}}\right)^{2}

which in turn proves (
6
).
∎

Since Proposition
3.1
holds for any
u\geq 0
, we are free to
choose the best one (which will obviously depend on the value of
\theta
).
For
\theta\geq 2
, simple calculus provides us with the value

u_{0}=\frac{\sqrt{\theta}(v-1)}{2m\sqrt{v}}.

where (for the sake of clarity) we have used the substitution
v=\sqrt{d-1}\sqrt{dm/n-1}
.
Plugging this into Proposition
3.1
, then gives the following:

Corollary 3.2
.

For all
\theta\geq 2
and all positive integers
d,n
and
m\geq n
, we have

\lambda_{1}\left(q_{d}^{[\theta,m,n]}\right)\leq\sqrt{\frac{\theta n}{m}}\left(\sqrt{d-1}+\sqrt{(dm/n-1}\right).

At first glance, Corollary
3.2
seems to be precisely the bound we want
(recalling that we wish to set
m=kn
).
However, one will notice that our definition of
q_{d}^{[\theta,m,n]}
in
(
4
) is not exactly the quantity in (
3
) that we want to
find (it made the math a bit simpler to write).
For the quantity we are actually interested in, Corollary
3.2
implies
the bound

\lambda_{1}\left(q_{d}^{[k,m-1,n-1]}\right)\leq\sqrt{\frac{k(n-1)}{m-1}}\left(\sqrt{d-1}+\sqrt{\frac{m-1}{n-1}d-1}\right).

Fortunately for us (and curiously), the bound we get because of this shift is
actually
better
than Ramanujan.

Lemma 3.3
.

For all
k>0
,
d\geq 2
,
n\geq 1
and
m=kn
, we have

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{k(n-1)}{m-1}}\left(\sqrt{d-1}+\sqrt{\frac{m-1}{n-1}d-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{(d-1)}\sqrt{\left(1-\frac{m-n}{n(m-1)}\right)}+\sqrt{dk-1+\frac{m-n}{n(m-1)}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sqrt{d-1}+\sqrt{dk-1}\right)

Proof.

Consider the function

f(x)=a\sqrt{1-x}+\sqrt{b+x}

for positive numbers
a,b
.
Then

f^{\prime}(x)=\frac{\sqrt{1-x}-a\sqrt{b+x}}{2\sqrt{1-x}\sqrt{b+x}}

and so
f^{\prime}(x)\leq 0
for
x\in[0,1]
and
a,b\geq 1
.
Hence

a\leftarrow\sqrt{d-1}\geq 1\and b\leftarrow dk-1\geq 1\and x\leftarrow\frac{m-n}{n(m-1)}<1

implies the inequality we want (which in particular is true for
d\geq 2
).
∎

4
Polynomial time algorithm

4.1
Description of the algorithm

The algorithm follows the proof of existence, starting with the expected
characteristic polynomial and using an interlacing family to find a particular
polynomial with
see
[
8
]
and
[
13
]
for more details).
We will consider a tree with many nodes, where each node
w
corresponds to
a
graph
G_{w}
.
The roots node corresponds to the empty graph on
kn+n
vertices, and the
graphs corresponding to child nodes will consist of the graph corresponding to
the parent node with a single edge added.
This will be done in a particular way so that after every
k
edges we have
added another
k
-claw and after every
nk
edges, we have added another

k
-claw matching.
Intuitively, one can think of the graphs
G_{w}
as “partial assignment”
graphs; that is, we construct the Ramanujan graph one edge at a time and

G_{w}

is the graph consisting of edges we have picked so far.

To each node
n
we also associate a polynomial
p_{w}
, however these will
be
built starting at the leaf nodes and working up the tree.
The polynomials
p_{w}
associated to a leaf node
w
will simply be the
characteristic polynomial of the adjacency
matrix of
G_{w}
(its associated graph).
All other nodes will receive the polynomial formed by averaging over all of the
leaf node polynomials below it in the tree.
Given the interpretation of
G_{w}
as a partial assignment graph, the
resulting
polynomials can be thought of as the expected characteristic polynomial of the
final graph conditioned on the fact that it must contain
G_{w}
as a
subgraph.
We will perform a greedy search of the graph, moving
k
levels at a time (so
corresponding to adding a new
k
-claw matching each turn), starting with the
root and descending all the way to one of the leaves (which will be the
resulting Ramanujan graph).

Of particular note is that the root (which, as we noted above, corresponds to
the empty graph) will have the expected characteristic polynomial (taken over
all of the leaves) associated to it, which (by Theorem
1.2
) has its
second largest root smaller than the Ramanujan bound.
Furthermore, this polynomial can be written in the form

\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}P_{1},\dots,P_{d}\in\mathcal{P}_{nk}\\
S_{1},\dots,S_{d}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}\end{subarray}}\left\{\chi\left[\sum_{i=1}^{d}(P_{i}\oplus S_{i})\begin{pmatrix}0&{I_{n}^{[k]}}\\
{I_{n}^{[k]}}^{T}&0\end{pmatrix}(P_{i}\oplus S_{i})^{T}\right]\right\}.

Note that each pair of permutations
(P_{i},S_{i})
corresponds to a
k
-claw
matching, with the sum being the union of the graphs.
The leaves of our tree will include all possible biregular bipartite graphs
that can be formed this way (with repetitions) and their associated polynomials
will be

\chi\left[\sum_{i=1}^{d}(P_{i}\oplus S_{i})\begin{pmatrix}0&{I_{n}^{[k]}}\\
{I_{n}^{[k]}}^{T}&0\end{pmatrix}(P_{i}\oplus S_{i})^{T}\right]

for each possible sequence of permutation matrices
P_{1},\dots,P_{d}\in\mathcal{P}_{nk}
and
S_{1},\dots S_{d}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
.

By construction, every node will differ by exactly one edge (the same edge)
from each of its siblings.
The utility of this is that the characteristic polynomials of the adjacency
matrices of such graphs will have a common interlacer.
This makes the tree itself an
interlacing family
(as first defined in

[
13
]
) and therefore gives it the property that every parent node has at
least one child whose associated polynomial has a smaller
k
th root than the
one associated to the parent.
Hence any bound on the second eigenvalue of a node can be propagated down the
tree until one hits a leaf.
By Theorem
1.2
and Lemma
3.3
, we know that the root
satisfies the Ramanujan bound, and so there exists a path down the tree which
maintains this bound.
Every node will have at most
kn
children, and so the number of nodes that are

k
levels lower will be at most
(kn)^{k}
(which, for fixed
k
, is polynomial
in
n
).
Hence we can examine all of them and find the one whose associated polynomial
has the smallest second eigenvalue, if we can compute the associated
polynomials efficiently.
This is precisely what was shown in
[
8
]
for the special case
k=1
.

The first observation one can make when trying to construct the associated
polynomials is that the polynomials associated to nodes that are on levels
which are multiples of
kn
are easy to compute.
This is because the corresponding graph
G_{n}
contains a union of complete

k
-claw matchings, and so the expectation is over the remaining
k
-claw
matchings that need to be placed.
In particular, this causes the polynomial to be an expectation over entire
permutation groups and this provides enough symmetry to allow us to compute the
associated polynomial for this node from the characteristic polynomial of its
associated graph using polynomial convolutions (see, for example,
[
6
]
).
Hence we can restrict our attention to graphs where
r-1

k
-claw matchings
have been chosen and the
r
th
k
-claw matching has been partial assigned.
Since we are moving down
k
rows at a time, we will always be in a situation
where
t
of the
k
-claws have been chosen and therefore
\ell
more

k
-claws remain to finish the
r
th
k
-claw matching (so
t+\ell=n
).
If we let

\begin{pmatrix}0&{A}\\
{A}^{T}&0\end{pmatrix}

be the adjacency matrix of the graph at this node (where
A
is a
kn\times n
rectangular matrix), we can then represent the

\ell
remaining
k
-claws by the block matrix

C_{\ell}=\begin{pmatrix}I_{\ell}^{[k]}&0_{k\ell\times t}\\
0_{kt\times\ell}&0_{kt\times t}\end{pmatrix}

(where
I_{\ell}^{[k]}
consists of
k
vertical copies of the identity matrix
of size

l
).
Hence the entire algorithm comes down to our ability to compute (in polynomial
time) the following quantity:

\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\in\mathcal{P}_{k\ell}\\
S\in\mathcal{P}_{l}\end{subarray}}\left\{\chi\left[\begin{pmatrix}0&{A}\\
{A}^{T}&0\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}{P\oplus I_{kt}}&0\\
0&{S\oplus I_{t}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&{C_{\ell}}\\
{C_{\ell}}^{T}&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}{P\oplus I_{kt}}&0\\
0&{S\oplus I_{t}}\end{pmatrix}^{T}\right]\right\}

(7)

4.2
Elimination of the second permutation

The first issue that one finds when trying to compute (

the appearance of two permutation matrices

However we will see that computing all possible permutations of

To add complication to the issue, all of this must be done after we have

Remark 4
.

The primary reason for this redundancy (at least in linear algebraic terms) is

The clearest way to see this (and the approach we will take),

The case when

To begin, let

Since

\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\in\mathcal{P}_{k\ell}\\

which is a singular

Let

In particular,

\hat{C}_{\ell}=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{k}I_{\ell-1}\oplus 0&0_{\ell\times t}\\

and

and

•

where

\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\hat{P}\in\mathcal{A}_{k\ell-1}\\

{\hat{C}_{\ell}}^{T}&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}{\hat{P}\oplus I_{kt+1}}&0\\

The main results of

Lemma 4.1
.

If

\mathbb{E}_{\hat{P}\in\mathcal{A}_{r}}\left\{\chi\left[C+(\hat{P}\oplus I)D(\hat{P}\oplus I)^{T}\right]\right\}=\mathbb{E}_{Q\in\mathcal{O}_{r}}\left\{\chi\left[C+(Q\oplus I)D(Q\oplus I)^{T}\right]\right\}.

.

Decomposing the matrix

\begin{pmatrix}{\hat{P}\oplus I_{kt+1}}&0\\
0&{\hat{S}\oplus I_{t+1}}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}{\hat{P}\oplus I_{kt+1}}&0\\
0&{I_{n}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}{I_{kn}}&0\\
0&{\hat{S}\oplus I_{t+1}}\end{pmatrix}

we can apply Lemma
4.1
twice to (
8
) to get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\hat{P}\in\mathcal{A}_{k\ell-1}\\
\hat{S}\in\mathcal{A}_{\ell-1}\end{subarray}}\left\{\chi\left[\begin{pmatrix}0&{\hat{A}}\\
{\hat{A}}^{T}&0\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}{\hat{P}\oplus I_{kt+1}}&0\\
0&{\hat{S}\oplus I_{t+1}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&{\hat{C}_{\ell}}\\
{\hat{C}_{\ell}}^{T}&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}{\hat{P}\oplus I_{kt+1}}&0\\
0&{\hat{S}\oplus I_{t+1}}\end{pmatrix}^{T}\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q\in\mathcal{O}_{k\ell-1}\\
R\in\mathcal{O}_{\ell-1}\end{subarray}}\left\{\chi\left[\begin{pmatrix}0&{\hat{A}}\\
{\hat{A}}^{T}&0\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}{Q\oplus I_{kt+1}}&0\\
0&{R\oplus I_{t+1}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&{\hat{C}_{\ell}}\\
{\hat{C}_{\ell}}^{T}&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}{Q\oplus I_{kt+1}}&0\\
0&{R\oplus I_{t+1}}\end{pmatrix}^{T}\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q\in\mathcal{O}_{k\ell-1}\\
R\in\mathcal{O}_{\ell-1}\end{subarray}}\left\{\chi\left[\begin{pmatrix}0&{(\hat{A}+(Q\oplus I)\hat{C}_{\ell}(R\oplus I)^{T})}\\
{(\hat{A}+(Q\oplus I)\hat{C}_{\ell}(R\oplus I)^{T})}^{T}&0\end{pmatrix}\right]\right\}

(9)

Now, notice that

(Q\oplus I)\hat{C}_{\ell}=\sqrt{k}\begin{pmatrix}Q_{(\ell-1)}&0_{(\ell-1)\times(t+1)}\\
0_{(kt+1)\times(\ell-1)}&0_{(kt+1)\times(t+1)}\end{pmatrix}.

where
Q_{(\ell-1)}
corresponds to the rectangular matrix of size
(k\ell-1)\times(\ell-1)
made up by keeping only the first
\ell-1
columns of

Q
.

Remark 5
.

The matrix
Q_{(\ell-1)}
is often referred to as an
(\ell-1)
-frame of
Q
.
More generally for
s>r
,
the set of orthonormal
r
-frames of dimension
s
consist of all rectangular

s\times r
matrices such that the columns form an orthonormal family of
vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{s}
.
Such families are well studied (see, for example,
[
10
]
).
In particular, it is well known that the set of

r
-frames of dimension
s
forms a
compact manifold called the
Stiefel manifold
, which we denote

V_{r}(\mathbb{R}^{s})
.
These manifolds can be equipped with a Haar uniform measure that
is invariant under left action by
\mathcal{O}_{s}
and right action by

\mathcal{O}_{r}
.
Hence it follows that for any
R\in\mathcal{O}_{\ell-1}
we have

\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{Q}\in V_{\ell-1}(\mathbb{R}^{k\ell-1})}\left\{f(\tilde{Q}R)\right\}=\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{Q}\in V_{\ell-1}(\mathbb{R}^{k\ell-1})}\left\{f(\tilde{Q})\right\}

(10)

for any continuous function
f
, where the expectation is taken over the
uniform/Haar measure (and similarly for multiplication on the left).

Using these properties, we can simplify (
9
) even further:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q\in\mathcal{O}_{k\ell-1}\\
R\in\mathcal{O}_{\ell-1}\end{subarray}}\left\{\chi\left[\begin{pmatrix}0&{(\hat{A}+(\sqrt{k}Q_{\ell-1}R^{T}\oplus 0))}\\
{(\hat{A}+(\sqrt{k}Q_{\ell-1}R^{T}\oplus 0))}^{T}&0\end{pmatrix}\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{R\in\mathcal{O}_{\ell-1}}\left\{\mathbb{E}_{Q\in\mathcal{O}_{k\ell-1}}\left\{\chi\left[\begin{pmatrix}0&{(\hat{A}+(\sqrt{k}Q_{\ell-1}R^{T}\oplus 0))}\\
{(\hat{A}+(\sqrt{k}Q_{\ell-1}R^{T}\oplus 0))}^{T}&0\end{pmatrix}\right]\right\}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{R\in\mathcal{O}_{\ell-1}}\left\{\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{Q}\in V_{\ell-1}(\mathbb{R}^{k\ell-1})}\left\{\chi\left[\begin{pmatrix}0&{(\hat{A}+(\sqrt{k}\tilde{Q}\oplus 0))}\\
{(\hat{A}+(\sqrt{k}\tilde{Q}\oplus 0))}^{T}&0\end{pmatrix}\right]\right\}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{Q}\in V_{\ell-1}(\mathbb{R}^{k\ell-1})}\left\{\chi\left[\begin{pmatrix}0&{(\hat{A}+(\sqrt{k}\tilde{Q}\oplus 0))}\\
{(\hat{A}+(\sqrt{k}\tilde{Q}\oplus 0))}^{T}&0\end{pmatrix}\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{Q}\in V_{\ell-1}(\mathbb{R}^{k\ell-1})}\left\{\mathbb{S}\chi\left[(\hat{A}+(\sqrt{k}\tilde{Q}\oplus 0))(\hat{A}+(\sqrt{k}\tilde{Q}\oplus 0))^{T}\right].\right\}

(11)

4.3
Characteristic polynomials in polynomial time

The goal is to express (
11
) in a computable way, which we
do by computing an explicit formula for the coefficients in terms of
A
.
To this end, we will fix a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
with
m\geq n
and we will let
Q
be a matrix sampled uniformly from
V_{r}(\mathbb{R}^{s})

(where
m\geq s\geq r
and
n\geq r
)
and then consider the expansion of the polynomial

\mathbb{E}_{Q}\left\{\det\left[xI+\left(A+\sqrt{k}\begin{pmatrix}{{Q}}&0\\
0&{0}\end{pmatrix}\right)\left(A+\sqrt{k}\begin{pmatrix}{{Q}}&0\\
0&{0}\end{pmatrix}\right)^{T}\right]\right\}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}x^{n-i}c_{i}(A).

(12)

We remind the reader that we use the notation

\left[C\right]_{S,T}=\det\left[\{C_{ij}\}_{i\in S,j\in T}\right]

to denote the
(S,T)
-minor of
C
and note that the main property of
Q
that
we will use is that it is
minor-orthogonal
as defined in

[
12
]
.
That is, for any sets
S,T,U,V
with
|S|=|T|=k
and
|U|=|V|=\ell
, we
have

\mathbb{E}_{Q}\left\{\left[Q\right]_{S,T}\left[Q\right]_{U,V}\right\}=\frac{1}{\binom{s}{k}}\delta_{\left\{S=U\right\}}\delta_{\left\{T=V\right\}}

(13)

The proof of this fact is given in Lemma
5.4
in the Appendix.

Proposition 4.2
.

For fixed
m,n,s,r
, the coefficients
c_{i}(A)
from (
12
)
have the explicit formula

c_{i}(A)=\sum_{j,p,q}\frac{\binom{s-p}{i-j}\binom{r-q}{i-j}}{\binom{s}{i-j}}k^{i-j}A_{p,q}^{j}\quad\text{where}\quad A_{p,q}^{j}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}X\in\binom{[m]}{j},Y\in\binom{[n]}{j}\\
|X\cap[s]|=p,|Y\cap[r]|=q\end{subarray}}\left[A\right]_{X,Y}^{2}

Proof.

Using Corollary
5.3
and then Lemma
5.2
we can start by writing

c_{i}(A)=\sum_{S\in\binom{[m]}{i},T\in\binom{[n]}{i}}\left[A+\sqrt{k}\begin{pmatrix}{{Q}}&0\\
0&{0}\end{pmatrix}\right]_{S,T}^{2}.

By Lemma
5.1
, we can then expand

\displaystyle\left[A+\begin{pmatrix}{{\sqrt{k}Q}}&0\\
0&{0}\end{pmatrix}\right]_{S,T}

\displaystyle=\sum_{|U|=|V|}(-1)^{\|U+V\|_{1}}k^{|U|/2}\left[\begin{pmatrix}{{Q}}&0\\
0&{0}\end{pmatrix}\right]_{U,V}\left[A\right]_{S\setminus U,T\setminus V}\delta_{\left\{U\subseteq S\right\}}\delta_{\left\{V\subseteq T\right\}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{|U|=|V|}(-1)^{\|U+V\|_{1}}k^{|U|/2}\left[Q\right]_{U,V}\left[A\right]_{S\setminus U,T\setminus V}\delta_{\left\{U\subseteq S\cap[s]\right\}}\delta_{\left\{V\subseteq T\cap[r]\right\}}.

By minor orthogonality, we have

\mathbb{E}_{Q}\left\{\left[Q\right]_{U,V}\left[Q\right]_{U^{\prime},V^{\prime}}\right\}=\frac{1}{\binom{s}{|U|}}\delta_{\left\{U=U^{\prime}\right\}}\delta_{\left\{V=V^{\prime}\right\}}

and so conditioning on
|U|=|V|=j
, we get

\mathbb{E}_{Q}\left\{\left[A+\begin{pmatrix}{{\sqrt{k}Q}}&0\\
0&{0}\end{pmatrix}\right]_{S,T}^{2}\right\}=\sum_{j}\frac{k^{j}}{\binom{s}{j}}\sum_{|U|=|V|=j}\left[A\right]_{S\setminus U,T\setminus V}^{2}\delta_{\left\{U\subseteq S\right\}}\delta_{\left\{V\subseteq T\right\}}\delta_{\left\{U\subseteq[s]\right\}}\delta_{\left\{V\subseteq[r]\right\}}.

Summing this over all
S
and
T
of size
i
, we then get

\displaystyle c_{i}(A)

\displaystyle=\sum_{|S|=|T|=i}\sum_{j}\frac{k^{j}}{\binom{s}{j}}\sum_{|U|=|V|=j}\left[A\right]_{S\setminus U,T\setminus V}^{2}\delta_{\left\{U\subseteq S\right\}}\delta_{\left\{V\subseteq T\right\}}\delta_{\left\{U\subseteq[s]\right\}}\delta_{\left\{V\subseteq[r]\right\}}.

Now we simply need to change our reference sets: given that
U\subseteq S

with

|U|=j
and
|S|=i
, we must have
|S\setminus U|=i-j
(and similarly with
T
and
V
).
So if we substitute
X=S\setminus U
and
Y=T\setminus V
, we can rewrite
this sum as

\displaystyle c_{i}(A)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\frac{k^{j}}{\binom{s}{j}}\sum_{|U|=|V|=j}\sum_{|X|=|Y|=i-j}\left[A\right]_{X,Y}^{2}\delta_{\left\{X\cap U=\varnothing\right\}}\delta_{\left\{Y\cap V=\varnothing\right\}}\delta_{\left\{U\subseteq[s]\right\}}\delta_{\left\{V\subseteq[r]\right\}}.

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\frac{k^{j}}{\binom{s}{j}}\sum_{|X|=|Y|=i-j}\left[A\right]_{X,Y}^{2}\sum_{|U|=|V|=j}\delta_{\left\{X\cap U=\varnothing\right\}}\delta_{\left\{Y\cap V=\varnothing\right\}}\delta_{\left\{U\subseteq[s]\right\}}\delta_{\left\{V\subseteq[r]\right\}}.

Given a fixed
X
with
|X\cap[s]|=p
, it is easy to see that

\sum_{|U|=j}\delta_{\left\{X\cap U=\varnothing\right\}}\delta_{\left\{U\subseteq[s]\right\}}=\binom{s-p}{j}

and similarly for fixed
Y
with
|Y\cap[r]|=q
.
Hence we have

c_{i}(A)=\sum_{j,p,q}\frac{k^{j}}{\binom{s}{j}}\binom{s-p}{j}\binom{r-q}{j}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|X|=|Y|=i-j\\
|X\cap[s]|=p,|Y\cap[r]|=q\end{subarray}}\left[A\right]_{X,Y}^{2}

and so the proposition follows from the change of variable
j\leftarrow i-j
.
∎

The key observation in
[
8
]
is that, while each term
A_{p,q}^{j}
is
itself a sum with an exponential number of terms, the total number of the

A_{p,q}^{j}
is polynomial in
n
.
Hence if there was a way to compute these terms more efficiently (than the
brute-force sum used in their definition), then the full polynomial could then
be computed efficiently as well.
To accomplish this,
[
8
]
introduces a multivariate
characteristic polynomial which will be used as a generating function for these
terms.

Definition 3
.

For
m,n,s,r
as in Proposition
4.2
and a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, we define the matrix
\tilde{A}(y,z)
whose
entries are polynomials in
y
and
z
:

\tilde{A}(y,z)=\begin{pmatrix}{yI_{s}}&0\\
0&{I_{m-s}}\end{pmatrix}A\begin{pmatrix}{zI_{r}}&0\\
0&{I_{n-r}}\end{pmatrix}

and let the polynomial

\theta_{A}(x,y,z)=\det\left[xI+\tilde{A}(y,z)^{T}\tilde{A}(y,z)\right]

(14)

The idea will be for
\theta_{A}(x,y,z)
to act as a generating function, with
the
y
and
z
variables keeping track of terms involving the upper left
corner of
A
while simultaneously allowing us to compute the polynomial as a
simple determinant (which can be computed efficiently, despite being
expressable as a sum with an exponential number of terms).
More precisely we have the following result:

Lemma 4.3
.

\theta_{A}(x,y,z)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\sum_{p=0}^{s}\sum_{q=0}^{r}x^{n-j}y^{2p}z^{2q}A_{p,q}^{j}

where
A_{p,q}^{j}
is the same as in Proposition
4.2
.

Proof.

By Corollary
5.3
, we have

\displaystyle\det\left[xI+\tilde{A}(y,z)^{T}\tilde{A}(y,z)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n}x^{n-i}\left(\sum_{V\in\binom{[n]}{i},U\in\binom{[m]}{i}}\left[\tilde{A}(y,z)\right]_{U,V}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{d}x^{n-i}\sum_{p=0}^{s}\sum_{q=0}^{r}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|U|=|V|=i\\
|U\cap[s]|=p,|V\cap[r]|=q\end{subarray}}\left[\tilde{A}(y,z)\right]_{U,V}^{2}\right).

where the last line is simply conditioning on the sizes of
|U\cap[s]|
and

|V\cap[t]|
(and summing over all possibilities).
Since each element of
\tilde{A}
contains a factor of
y
if and only if it is in
the first
s
rows, any
U
with
|U\cap[s]|=p
will result in a factor of

y^{p}
.
Similarly each element of
\tilde{A}
contains a factor of
z
if and only if it is
in the first
r
columns, so any set
V
with
|V\cap[t]|=q
will result in
a factor of
z^{q}
.
Hence we have

\theta_{A}(x,y,z)=\sum_{k=0}^{d}x^{n-i}\sum_{p=0}^{s}\sum_{q=0}^{r}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|U|=|V|=i\\
|U\cap[s]|=p,|V\cap[r]|=q\end{subarray}}y^{2p}z^{2q}\left[A\right]_{U,V}^{2}\right)

and so the result follows by changing the order of summations.
∎

Hence the
A^{j}_{p,q}
terms can be computed in polynomial time using

14
as a generating function, thus giving a polynomial time algorithm
for computing (
12
) as needed.

Remark 6
.

Recall from Section
4.1
that in the special case
s=kn
and

r=n
(corresponding to a graph that is a union of
k
-claw matchings), we
noted that it was possible to compute the expected characteristic polynomial
efficiently from the (classic) characteristic polynomial of the graph.
In this case, it is easy to calculate
\tilde{A}(y,z)=yzA
so (
14
)
reduces to

\theta_{A}(x,y,z)=(yz)^{s}\chi\left[A^{T}A\right](x/{yz})

which is exactly the information we were using before.
Hence the ability to use
\theta_{A}(x,y,z)
to compute
(
7
) seems to be the appropriate generalization of this
observation.

5
Appendix: Tools from linear algebra

We will need two formulas that give an expansion of the determinant as a
function of the minors of matrices.
The first, which can be found in
[
15
]
, is for the sum of matrices:

Lemma 5.1
.

For all matrices
A,B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, one has

\det\left[A+B\right]=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S,T\subseteq[n]\\
|S|=|T|\end{subarray}}(-1)^{\|S+T\|_{1}}\left[A\right]_{S,T}\left[B\right]_{[n]\setminus S,[n]\setminus T}

where
S+T
denotes the symmetric difference (XOR) of
S
and
T
, and we use
the notation

\|X\|_{1}=\sum_{x\in X}x
.

The second, which is a well-known result of Cauchy and (independently) Binet,
regards the product of matrices (see
[
7
]
):

Lemma 5.2
.

For all matrices
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
and for
all sets
|S|=|T|=k
with
k\leq\min\{m,n,p\}
, we have

\left[AB\right]_{S,T}=\sum_{U\in\binom{[n]}{k}}\left[A\right]_{S,U}\left[B\right]_{U,T}

We also need a well-known expansion of the characteristic polynomial in terms
of the principal minors (see
[
7
]
, but this can also be derived directly
from Lemma
5.1
and Lemma
5.2
):

Corollary 5.3
.

For a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, one has

\det\left[xI+A\right]=\sum_{k=0}^{n}x^{n-k}\sum_{S\in\binom{[n]}{k}}\left[A\right]_{S,S}

Finally, we use the fact (which we prove here) that random elements of the
Stiefel manifold
V_{r}(\mathbb{R}^{s})

under the invariant (Haar/uniform) measure is
minor-orthogonal
.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
Q
be a random matrix drawn uniformly from
V_{r}(\mathbb{R}^{s})
.
Then for any sets
S,T,U,V
with
|S|=|T|=i
and
|U|=|V|=\ell
, we
have

\mathbb{E}_{Q}\left\{\left[Q\right]_{S,T}\left[Q\right]_{U,V}\right\}=\frac{1}{\binom{s}{i}}\delta_{\left\{S=U\right\}}\delta_{\left\{T=V\right\}}

Proof.

Define the matrix
E_{t}
to be the diagonal matrix with diagonal entries
1

except for
E_{t}(t,t)
which is
-1
.
Now assume that
S\neq U
and let
h\in S\setminus U
.
Then

\left[E_{t}Q\right]_{S,T}\left[E_{t}Q\right]_{U,V,=}-\left[Q\right]_{S,T}\left[Q\right]_{U,V}

and so

\mathbb{E}_{Q}\left\{\left[E_{t}Q\right]_{S,T}\left[E_{t}Q\right]_{U,V}\right\}=-\mathbb{E}_{Q}\left\{\left[Q\right]_{S,T}\left[Q\right]_{U,V}\right\}

however the function

f(X)=\left[X\right]_{S,T}\left[X\right]_{U,V}

is continuous and so by the left invariance of
V_{r}(\mathbb{R}^{s})
(see
Remark
5
), we have

\mathbb{E}_{Q}\left\{\left[E_{t}Q\right]_{S,T}\left[E_{t}Q\right]_{U,V}\right\}=\mathbb{E}_{Q}\left\{\left[Q\right]_{S,T}\left[Q\right]_{U,V}\right\}.

Hence we must have

\mathbb{E}_{Q}\left\{\left[Q\right]_{S,T}\left[Q\right]_{U,V}\right\}=0

and similarly when
T\neq V
.

In the case that
S=U
and
T=V
, we must compute

\mathbb{E}_{Q}\left\{\left[Q\right]_{S,T}^{2}\right\}.

Again, by the invariance properties of
V_{r}(\mathbb{R}^{s})
(this time under
multiplication by permutation matrices on each side), this quantity is
independent of the actual
S
and
T
and instead depends only on
|S|=|T|=j
.
On the other hand, by Lemma
5.2
, we have for any
S
with
|S|=j

1=\left[Q^{T}Q\right]_{S,S}=\sum_{U\in\binom{[s]}{j}}\left[Q\right]_{S,U}^{2}

since
Q^{T}Q=I_{r}
(the
r\times r
identity matrix).
Since there are
\binom{s}{j}
terms in this sum (and all are equal in
expectation), we conclude that

\mathbb{E}_{Q}\left\{\left[Q\right]_{S,T}^{2}\right\}=\frac{1}{\binom{s}{j}}.

∎
