A parametric version of the Hilbert Nullstellensatz
Rida Ait El Manssour, Nikhil Balaji, Klara Nosan, Mahsa Shirmohammadi, James Worrell

Abstract
. Hilbert’s Nullstellensatz is a fundamental result in algebraic geometry that gives a necessary and sufficient condition for a finite collection of multivariate polynomials to have a common zero in an algebraically closed field. Associated with this result, there is the computational problem HN \mathrm{HN} of determining whether a system of polynomials with coefficients in the field of rational numbers has a common zero over the field of algebraic numbers. In an influential paper, Koiran showed that HN \mathrm{HN} can be determined in the polynomial hierarchy assuming the Generalised Riemann Hypothesis (GRH). More precisely, he showed that HN \mathrm{HN} lies in the complexity class 𝐀𝐌 {\bf{AM}} under GRH. In a later work he generalised this result by showing that the problem DIM \mathrm{DIM} , which asks to determine the dimension of the set of solutions of a given polynomial system, also lies in 𝐀𝐌 {\bf{AM}} subject to GRH. In this paper we study the solvability of polynomial equations over arbitrary algebraically closed fields of characteristic zero. Up to isomorphism, every such field is the algebraic closure of a field of rational functions. We thus formulate a parametric version of HN \mathrm{HN} , called HNP \mathrm{HNP} , in which the input is a system of polynomials with coefficients in a function field ℚ ⁡ ( 𝒙 ) \mathbb{Q}({\bm{x}}) and the task is to determine whether the polynomials have a common zero in the algebraic closure ℚ ⁡ ( 𝒙 ) ¯ \overline{\mathbb{Q}({\bm{x}})} . We observe that Koiran’s proof that DIM \mathrm{DIM} lies in AM can be interpreted as a randomised polynomial-time reduction of DIM \mathrm{DIM} to HNP \mathrm{HNP} , followed by an argument that HNP \mathrm{HNP} lies in 𝐀𝐌 {\bf{AM}} . Our main contribution is a self-contained proof that HNP \mathrm{HNP} lies in 𝐀𝐌 {\bf AM} that follows the same basic idea as Koiran’s argument – namely random instantiation of the parameters – but whose justification is purely algebraic, relying on a parametric version of Hilbert’s Nullstellensatz, and avoiding recourse to semi-algebraic geometry.



1.
Introduction

Hilbert’s Nullstellensatz is a fundamental result in algebraic geometry.
Given a system of polynomial equations

(1)

f_{1}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})=0,\;\ldots,\;f_{\ell}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})=0

where
f_{i}\in k[y_{1},\dots,y_{n}]
for
k
a field with algebraic closure
K
, the Nullstellensatz says that the system is unsatisfiable over
K
if and only if there exist polynomials
g_{1},\dots,g_{\ell}\in k[y_{1},\dots,y_{n}]
such that

(2)

\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}f_{i}g_{i}=1

A naturally associated computational problem asks to
determine whether a given family of polynomials
f_{1},\dots,f_{\ell}
has a common zero.
More specifically, we have the problem

\mathrm{HN}
in which the input is system of polynomial equations with rational coefficients and the task is to determine
whether there exists a solution in the field of algebraic numbers
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
.
1
1

1

By the Nullstellensatz this is equivalent to asking whether a system of polynomial equations with rational coefficients admits a common solution over
\mathbb{C}
.

The characterisation given in Equation (
2
) essentially reduces
\mathrm{HN}
to an
ideal membership problem
, namely whether the constant 1 lies in the ideal generated by the
f_{i}
. By a result of Mayr and Meyer
[
23
]
, this places
\mathrm{HN}
in the complexity class
EXPSPACE
. On the other hand, it is easy to reduce the Boolean satisfiability problem to
\mathrm{HN}
, making it at least
NP
-hard.

Over the years, various
Effective Nullstellensätze

[
18
,
19
,
13
]
have yielded single-exponential degree bounds on
g_{i}
, reduces the ideal membership problem arising from the Nullstellensatz to that of solving a single-exponential-sized system of linear equations. This yields a
PSPACE
upper bound for
\mathrm{HN}
. In an influential paper
[
15
,
16
]
, Koiran proved that
\mathrm{HN}
lies in the complexity class
AM
(and thereby in the second level of the polynomial hierarchy) if one assumes GRH. He achieves this bound by exhibiting a
gap theorem
: For any system
\mathcal{S}
of polynomial equations with integer coefficients, if
\mathcal{S}
is satisfiable over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
then there are at least
B
primes
p
such that
\mathcal{S}
is satisfiable over
the finite field

\mathbb{F}_{p}
, whereas if
\mathcal{S}

is not satisfiable over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
then there are
at most
A
primes for which
\mathcal{S}
is satisfiable over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
. Here
A<B
are both numbers whose magnitude is a single-exponential function of the parameters (namely, number of variables, degree, bit size of coefficients, number of polynomial equations) of the system
\mathcal{S}
. Therefore to decide
\mathrm{HN}
it suffices to count the number of primes for which
\mathcal{S}
is satisfiable over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
. Since
satisfiability over
\mathbb{F}_{p}

has an easy
NP
procedure, one obtains a
\mbox{{\#\bf P}}^{\bf{NP}}
algorithm for
\mathrm{HN}
. By further observing that there is a sufficiently large gap between
A
and
B
, Koiran proves that it suffices to approximately count the number of primes, which yields the claimed upper bound for the problem.

In this paper we study a version of
\mathrm{HN}
over arbitrary algebraically closed fields
of characteristic zero. In general, such fields have the form
\overline{\mathbb{Q}({\bm{x}})}
for

{\bm{x}}
a set of indeterminates. Specifically we consider the problem
\mathrm{HNP}
, which asks to determine
whether a system of multivariate polynomials with coefficients in
\mathbb{Q}({\bm{x}})
has a solution
in
\overline{\mathbb{Q}({\bm{x}})}
, where
{\bm{x}}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})
.
Evidently
\mathrm{HNP}
generalises
\mathrm{HN}
. We remark also that
systems of polynomial equations with coefficients in
\mathbb{Q}({\bm{x}})
are
central objects of study in algebraic combinatorics and in the theory of formal languages, where they are used to specify generating functions of combinatorial objects (see, e.g., the overviews in
[
2
,
20
]
).

We present a generalisation of the technique introduced in
[
15
]
, allowing the use of algebraic arguments to establish a randomised polynomial-time reduction from
\mathrm{HNP}

to
\mathrm{HN}
. In particular, given a system of polynomials with coefficients in
\mathbb{Q}({\bm{x}})
, we compute a bound
D
such that if we specialise the variables
x_{i}
uniformly at randomly from
\{1,\ldots,D\}
and use Koiran’s algorithm to determine whether the specialised system is satisfiable over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
, with high enough probability, the algorithm will give a correct answer to the problem over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}({\bm{x}})}
. We thus obtain our main result:

Theorem 1
.

\mathrm{HNP}
is in
\bf{AM}
assuming GRH.

To further undertand the context of Theorem
1
, it is useful to take a geometric view.
The set of common zeros of a collection of polynomials
f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}\in\mathbb{Q}[y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}]
is an

algebraic variety

V\subseteq\overline{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}
. The
\mathrm{HN}
problem asks whether this variety is non-empty. More generally, we have the

\mathrm{DIM}
problem which, given an integer
d
, asks whether
V
has dimension at least
d
. Note that
\mathrm{HN}
is precisely the problem
\mathrm{DIM}
specialised to
d=0
. A randomised polynomial-time reduction of
\mathrm{DIM}
to
\mathrm{HN}
is established in
[
17
]
, thus placing
\mathrm{DIM}
in
AM
assuming GRH.
The reduction works by first applying a random linear transformation
A
to the variety
V
such that if
V
has dimension at least
d
then with high probability the projection onto the coordinates

y_{1},\ldots,y_{d}

of the image
AV
is Zariski-dense in
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}^{d}
. The second step of the reduction involves randomly choosing integers
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{d}
, adding equations

y_{1}=\alpha_{1},\ldots,y_{d}=\alpha_{d}

to the system defining
AV
, and verifying the satisfiability of the new system over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
via the
\mathrm{HN}
algorithm.

The
\mathrm{HNP}
problem has a
geometric interpretation closed related to the above discussion of
\mathrm{DIM}
.
Given polynomials
f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}\in\mathbb{Q}[{\bm{x}}][y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}]
where
{\bm{x}}:=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})
, let
V
be the subvariety of
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}^{m+n}
defined by the system when treating the parameters
x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}
as variables. Then the satisfiability of the system
over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}({\bm{x}})}
is equivalent to the projection of
V

onto its first
m
coordinates being Zariski-dense in
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}^{m}
. Indeed, by the weak Hilbert Nullstellensatz for
\overline{\mathbb{Q}({\bm{x}})}
,
the system if satisfiable over

\overline{\mathbb{Q}({\bm{x}})}

iff

1\not\in I
for

I\subseteq\mathbb{Q}({\bm{x}})[{\bm{y}}]
the ideal
generated by
f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}
.
Since the constants in this setting belong to
\mathbb{Q}({\bm{x}})
, the condition corresponds to
I\cap\mathbb{Q}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}]
being zero. By
[
6
, Section 9.5, Corollary 4 and Proposition 5]
this is equivalent to the projection of

V
on
x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}
being dense in
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}^{m}
.

The above discussion shows that we can
see
\mathrm{HNP}
as a midpoint in the reduction
of
\mathrm{DIM}
to
\mathrm{HN}
—the first step
being a randomised polynomial-time
reduction of
\mathrm{DIM}
to
\mathrm{HNP}
,
and the second step being a randomised polynomial-time reduction of
\mathrm{HNP}
to
\mathrm{HN}
.
However the approach to the
\mathrm{HNP}
problem taken in the present paper
differs from
[
17
]
in its correctness proof and error analysis.
Our probability bounds use the Polynomial Identity Lemma (also called the DeMillo-Lipton-Schwartz-Zippel Lemma), in support of which we use a parametric version of the Nullstellensatz and basic facts about ring and field extensions. By contrast, the approach of
[
17
]
relies on real algebraic geometry via an identification of sub-varieties of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
with definable sets in
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
. In particular, it uses a technique of
[
14
]
for counting integer points in semi-algebraic sets in terms of their volume and number of connected components.

Finally, we mention that HNP can be formulated using the so-called
generic quantifier

\forall^{*}
considered by Koiran
[
17
]
, where
\forall^{*}\bm{x}\,\varphi(\bm{x})
expresses that the that the predicate
\varphi(\bm{x})
holds on a Zariski dense set. Then HNP for polynomials

f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}\in\mathbb{Q}[{\bm{x}}][y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}]
can be written as

\forall^{*}\bm{x}\,\exists\bm{y}\,\bigwedge_{i=1}^{k}f_{i}(\bm{x},\bm{y})=0
.

1.1.
Related work

The
\mathrm{HN}
problem is known to be polynomial-time equivalent to the
tensor rank
problem; see, e.g.
[
27
, Theorem 3]
.
A more general problem related to
\mathrm{HN}
is the
radical membership
problem. Garg and Saxena
[
12
]
study the complexity of radical membership parameterised by the transcendence degree of the polynomials in the system.

We study the
\mathrm{HN}
problem over algebraically closed fields.
The problem of determining whether a family of polynomials has real zero is known to lie in
PSPACE

[
4
]
and has given rise to the complexity class
\exists\mathbb{R}

[
25
]
.

We work with a formulation of
\mathrm{HN}
in the Turing model of computation. The problem is also naturally expressed in the Blum-Shub-Smale model, where it is the canonical complete problem for the analogue of
NP
in that setting
[
3
]
.

Overview

notation along the way.
We recall that
AM
is the class of languages with an interactive
proof that consist of the verifier sending a random string, the prover responding with a string,
both of polynomial length,
and where the decision to accept is obtained by applying a deterministic polynomial-time function
to the transcript.
[
1
, Section 8.4]

All formal statements of results and corresponding proofs can be respectively found in
Section

3
and

]
.

2.1.
The
\mathrm{HNP}
problem.

Let
{\bm{x}}:=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})
, and recall that
\overline{\mathbb{Q}({\bm{x}})}
denotes the algebraic closure of the field of rational functions in variables
{\bm{x}}
over
\mathbb{Q}
.
We investigate the complexity of determining whether the system of polynomial equations

(3)

f_{1}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})=0,\;\ldots,\;f_{k}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})=0

with
f_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}({\bm{x}})[y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}]
has a solution in
\overline{\mathbb{Q}({\bm{x}})}^{n}
.
We call this the Parametric Hilbert Nullstellensatz problem, or
\mathrm{HNP}
for short. Henceforth we assume that the polynomials
f_{i}
have coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}[{\bm{x}}]
.
This is without loss of generality since any system with
coefficients in
\mathbb{Q}({\bm{x}})
can be transformed by scaling to one with coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}[{\bm{x}}]
that has the same set of solution over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}({\bm{x}})}
.
The conventions for representing
such polynomials will be detailed in Section
3.1
.

2.2.
Reduction from
\mathrm{HNP}
to
\mathrm{HN}

Let
{\bm{x}}:=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})
and
{\bm{y}}:=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
.
Henceforth we fix an instance of
\mathrm{HNP}
, comprising a collection
\mathcal{S}=\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}\}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}[{\bm{x}}][{\bm{y}}]
of polynomials for which we seek a common zero in
\overline{\mathbb{Q}({\bm{x}})}
.
We study the satisfiability of the system
\mathcal{S}
when specialised at a given tuple of values
{\bm{\alpha}}:=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m})
in
\mathbb{Z}^{m}
. That is, we consider whether the set of polynomials
\mathcal{S}_{{\bm{\alpha}}}:=\{f_{1}({\bm{\alpha}},{\bm{y}}),\ldots,f_{k}({\bm{\alpha}},{\bm{y}})\}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}[{\bm{y}}]
has a common zero in
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
.
Our aim is to show that with
high probability over a random choice of
{\bm{\alpha}}
in a suitable range,
the systems
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{S}_{{\bm{\alpha}}}
are equisatisfiable; thereby we obtain a randomised polynomial-time reduction of
\mathrm{HNP}
to
\mathrm{HN}
.

Unsatisfiable systems.

Assume that
\mathcal{S}
is not satisfiable in
\overline{\mathbb{Q}({\bm{x}})}
. The weak version of Hilbert’s Nullstellensatz implies that the ideal generated by
f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}
contains the constant
1
. In particular, there exists a non-zero polynomial
a\in\mathbb{Z}[{\bm{x}}]
and polynomials
g_{1},\ldots,g_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}[{\bm{x}}][{\bm{y}}]
such that

a=g_{1}f_{1}+\cdots+g_{k}f_{k}\,.

Observe that if the above equation holds then the specialised system
\mathcal{S}_{{\bm{\alpha}}}
can only have a solution if
a({\bm{\alpha}})=0
.
An effective parametric version of Hilbert’s Nullstellensatz (see Theorem
2
) gives a degree bound on
a({\bm{x}})
. We may thus apply the Polynomial Identity Lemma (see Lemma
3
) to deduce a bound on the probability that
a({\bm{\alpha}})=0
, and hence an upper-bound on the probability that
\mathcal{S}_{{\bm{\alpha}}}
admits a solution in
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
, where
{\bm{\alpha}}
is chosen uniformly at random in a fixed range; see
Proposition

4
.

Satisfiable systems.

Assume, conversely, that the system
\mathcal{S}
is satisfiable.
We first show that there exists a “small” solution
{\bm{\beta}}=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}({\bm{x}})}^{n}
of
\mathcal{S}
. In particular, in
Proposition

5
, we
use results on quantifier elimination for algebraically closed fields to
obtain a bound on the degree (in
{\bm{x}}
and
{\bm{y}}
) of the defining polynomials of the
\beta_{i}
’s.

Let
\mathbb{K}:=\mathbb{Q}({\bm{x}})(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})
be the finite extension of
\mathbb{Q}({\bm{x}})
obtained by adjoining the solution
{\bm{\beta}}
to
\mathbb{Q}({\bm{x}})
. Since the extension
\mathbb{K}
is separable over
\mathbb{Q}({\bm{x}})
, by the Primitive Element Theorem there exists
\theta\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}({\bm{x}})}
such that
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{Q}({\bm{x}})(\theta)
, i.e.,
every element in
\mathbb{K}
can be written as a
\mathbb{Q}({\bm{x}})
-linear combination of the powers
1,\theta,\ldots,\theta^{N-1}
, where
N
is the degree of
\mathbb{K}
over
\mathbb{Q}({\bm{x}})
.
In particular, for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
we have

(4)

\beta_{i}=\frac{P_{i}(\theta)}{b}\,,

where
P_{i}(y)\in\mathbb{Q}[{\bm{x}}][y]
and
b\in\mathbb{Q}[{\bm{x}}]
.

Denote by

m_{\theta}({\bm{x}},y)\in\mathbb{Q}[{\bm{x}}][y]
the minimal polynomial of
\theta
over
\mathbb{Q}({\bm{x}})
.
In
Proposition

6
we exhibit an upper bound on the degree of
m_{\theta}({\bm{x}},y)
in both
y
and
{\bm{x}}
. We use this bound to obtain, in turn, a degree bound on the polynomial

b
in (
4
).
Specifically, in
Proposition

7
, we show that
b\in\mathbb{Q}[{\bm{x}}]
has total degree singly exponential in the representation of the system
\mathcal{S}
.

Given
{\bm{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
, we claim
that the system
\mathcal{S}_{{\bm{\alpha}}}
remains satisfiable over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
if

b({\bm{\alpha}})\neq 0
.
Indeed, if

b({\bm{\alpha}})\neq 0
,
we have a ring homomorphism

\varphi_{{\bm{\alpha}}}\colon\mathbb{Q}[{\bm{x}},b({\bm{x}})^{-1}][\theta]\to\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
defined by

\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}q_{j}({\bm{x}})\theta^{j}\mapsto\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}q_{j}({\bm{\alpha}})\omega^{j}

where
q_{j}({\bm{x}})\in\mathbb{Q}[{\bm{x}},b({\bm{x}})^{-1}]

and
\omega
is a root of
m_{\theta}({\bm{\alpha}},y)\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[y]
.
Note that for
p\in\mathbb{Z}[{\bm{x}}]
we have

\varphi_{{\bm{\alpha}}}(p)=p({\bm{\alpha}})
, i.e., the restriction of
\varphi_{\alpha}
to
\mathbb{Z}[{\bm{x}}]
is just evaluation at
{\bm{\alpha}}
. It follows that the image of
{\bm{\beta}}

under
\varphi_{{\bm{\alpha}}}

is a solution of the specialised system

\mathcal{S}_{{\bm{\alpha}}}
.
We can use the Polynomial Identity Lemma to upper bound the probability that
b({\bm{\alpha}})=0
(and hence that
\mathcal{S}_{{\bm{\alpha}}}
is not satisfiable over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
) for
{\bm{\alpha}}
chosen uniformly at random from a fixed range.

Reduction to
\mathrm{HN}
.

Finally, we combine the results on unsatisfiable and satisfiable systems to compute a bound
D
such that if we choose
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m}
in
\{1,2,\ldots,D\}
independently and uniformly at random, with high probability

\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{S}_{{\bm{\alpha}}}
are equisatisfiable.
We thus obtain a randomised polynomial-time reduction from
\mathrm{HNP}
to
\mathrm{HN}
, placing
\mathrm{HNP}
in
AM
assuming GRH.

3.
Detailed proofs

3.1.
Conventions

Let
{\bm{x}}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})

and
{\bm{y}}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
be tuples of variables.
In this section
\mathcal{S}=\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}\}
denotes a finite collection of polynomials in

\mathbb{Z}[{\bm{x}}][{\bm{y}}]
given in sparse representation, i.e., as lists of monomials with all integer constants in binary.
We define the
size
of
\mathcal{S}

to be the maximum of the number
m
of
parameters
, number
n
of
variables
, number
k
of polynomials, and the
bit length of the integers appearing as coefficients or exponents in the description of the polynomials.
We further assume that the total degree of each polynomial
f_{i}
in
{\bm{x}}
and
{\bm{y}}
is at most
2
and that the integer coefficients are either
0
or
1
.
This assumption is without loss of generality since
there is a straightforward polynomial-time transformation of an arbitrary system into an equisatisfiable system that satisfies our conventions (which proceeds by introducing new variables for subexpressions and using iterated squaring to handle exponentiation);
see
[
15
,
16
, Section 1.1]
.
In what follows, the size of the systems we consider is thus equal to
\max(m,n,k)
.

Recall from Section
2
that for the given system
\mathcal{S}
, its specialisation at
{\bm{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
is denoted
\mathcal{S}_{{\bm{\alpha}}}
.
We proceed to compute explicit bounds on the probability (over the choice of
{\bm{\alpha}}
in a suitable range) that
\mathcal{S}
and

\mathcal{S}_{{\bm{\alpha}}}
are equisatisfiable, based on which we give a randomised
polynomial-time reduction of
\mathrm{HNP}
to
\mathrm{HN}
.

3.2.
Unsatisfiable systems

The following is an effective version of the weak Hilbert Nullstellensatz for
\overline{\mathbb{Q}({\bm{x}})}
given in
[
8
, Theorem 5 and Corollary 4.20]
.

Theorem 2

(Effective Parametric Hilbert’s Nullstellensatz)
.

Let
f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}[{\bm{x}}][y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}]
be a family of
k
polynomials of degree at most
2
in
{\bm{x}}
and
{\bm{y}}
that have no common zero in
\overline{\mathbb{Q}({\bm{x}})}
.
Then there exists
a\in\mathbb{Z}[{\bm{x}}]\setminus\{0\}
and
g_{1},\ldots,g_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}[{\bm{x}}][y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}]
such that

(5)

a=g_{1}f_{1}+\cdots+g_{k}f_{k},

with

•

\deg_{{\bm{y}}}(g_{i}f_{i})\leq 2^{k}
,

•

\deg_{{\bm{x}}}(a),\deg_{{\bm{x}}}(g_{i}f_{i})\leq k2^{k}
.

We use Theorem
2
to estimate the probability that an unsatisfiable system

\mathcal{S}
remains unsatisfiable when specialised at
{\bm{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
.
To this end we will rely on the Polynomial Identity Lemma
[
7
,
26
,
28
]
:

Lemma 3

(Polynomial Identity Lemma)
.

Let
f\in K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
be a non-zero polynomial of total degree
d\geq 0
over an integral domain
K
. Let
S
be a finite subset of
K
and let
r_{1},\ldots,r_{n}
be selected independently and uniformly at random from
S
. Then

\Pr\left(f(r_{1},\ldots,r_{n})=0\right)\leq\frac{d}{|S|}.

Recall that we use
s
to denote the size of our fixed system
\mathcal{S}
. Then we have:

Proposition 4
.

Let
D\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
D\geq s2^{s}
.
If
\mathcal{S}
has no solution in
\overline{\mathbb{Q}({\bm{x}})}
, then for
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m}
chosen independently and uniformly at random from
\{1,2,\ldots,D\}

\Pr\left(\mathcal{S}_{{\bm{\alpha}}}\text{ is satisfiable in }\overline{\mathbb{Q}}\right)\leq\frac{s2^{s}}{D}.

Proof.

If
\mathcal{S}
has no solution in
\overline{\mathbb{Q}({\bm{x}})}^{n}
then by the weak Hilbert Nullstellensatz, the ideal generated by the polynomials in
\mathcal{S}
contains the constant
1
. Furthermore,
Theorem

2
asserts that there exists
a\in\mathbb{Q}[{\bm{x}}]
of degree at most
s2^{s}
that can be computed as a
\mathbb{Z}[{\bm{x}}]
-linear combination of the polynomials in
\mathcal{S}
, i.e., Equation (
5
) holds. For all
{\bm{\alpha}}
such that
a({\bm{\alpha}})\neq 0
, the specialised system
\mathcal{S}_{{\bm{\alpha}}}
cannot be satisfiable in
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
.
That is, by the union bound

\Pr\left(\mathcal{S}_{{\bm{\alpha}}}\text{ is satisfiable in }\overline{\mathbb{Q}}\right)\leq\Pr\left(a(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{r})=0\right).

But
a
has degree at most
s2^{s}
, and hence by
Lemma

3
the bound follows.
∎

3.3.
Satisfiable systems

3.3.1.
Degree bound on solutions

In
[
16
, Theorem 7]
, Koiran showed that a system of polynomials with integer coefficients
that has a common zero has one such whose
degree is bounded by a certain explicit
function of the size of the system.
His proof relies on quantifier-elimination bounds for
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
. We follow similar reasoning to obtain an upper bound on the degree of a solution to a satisfiable polynomial systems with coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}[{\bm{x}}]
. For the full proof, which is a close adaptation of
[
16
, Theorem 7]
; see
Appendix

A
.

Proposition 5
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a system of
k
polynomial equations in
n
variables
{\bm{y}}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
with coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}[{\bm{x}}]
of degree at most
2
in
{\bm{x}}
and
{\bm{y}}
.
There exists an effective constant
c\in\mathbb{N}
such that if
\mathcal{P}
has a solution over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}({\bm{x}})}
, then there exists a solution
{\bm{\beta}}=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})
such that each component
\beta_{i}
is a root of a polynomial of degree at most
2^{(n\log k)^{c}}
with coefficients that are polynomials in
\mathbb{Z}[{\bm{x}}]
of degree at most
2^{((n+m)\log k)^{c}}
in
{\bm{x}}
.

3.3.2.
Primitive element

Assume that the system
\mathcal{S}
is satisfiable. Let
{\bm{\beta}}=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})
be a solution of
\mathcal{S}
in
\overline{\mathbb{Q}({\bm{x}})}^{n}
satisfying the bounds stated in Proposition
5

and write
\mathbb{K}:=\mathbb{Q}({\bm{x}})(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})
.
Denote by
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}}
the subring of
\mathbb{K}
comprising those elements that are
integral
over
\mathbb{Q}[{\bm{x}}]
, that is, whose minimal polynomial over
\mathbb{Q}({\bm{x}})
is monic with coefficients in
\mathbb{Q}[{\bm{x}}]
.

Recall that we write
s
to denote the size of the system
\mathcal{S}
.
In the following proposition
we compute a polynomial
d\in\mathbb{Q}[{\bm{x}}]
such that
\tilde{\beta}_{i}:=d\beta_{i}\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}}
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
and then follow
a standard construction of a primitive element
\theta\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}}
as a
\mathbb{Z}
-linear combination of the
\tilde{\beta}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{\beta}_{n}
.
(See
Appendix

B
for details.)

Proposition 6
.

There exists a primitive element
\theta
of
\mathbb{K}
with monic minimal polynomial
m_{\theta}({\bm{x}},y)\in\mathbb{Q}[{\bm{x}}][y]
of degree at most
2^{(s\log s)^{c}}
in
y
, and with coefficients of degree at most
2^{(s\log s)^{c}}
in
{\bm{x}}
, where
c
is an effective constant.

We next show how to recover representations of
the components
\beta_{i}
of the solution of
\mathcal{S}
in terms of the primitive element
\theta
.

Proposition 7
.

There exists
b\in\mathbb{Z}[{\bm{x}}]
of degree at most
2^{(s\log s)^{c}}
in
{\bm{x}}
and
P_{1},\ldots,P_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}[{\bm{x}}][y]

such that for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
,
\beta_{i}=\frac{P_{i}(\theta)}{b}
, where
c
is an effective constant.

Proof.

By
Proposition

12
in
Appendix

C
we have

\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}}\subseteq\frac{1}{\mathrm{disc}(m_{\theta})}\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\mathbb{Q}[{\bm{x}}]\theta^{i}
, where

\mathrm{disc}(m_{\theta})\in\mathbb{Q}[{\bm{x}}]
is the discriminant of the minimal polynomial
m_{\theta}
of
\theta
. In general,
the discriminant of a polynomial of degree
N
is a polynomial in its coefficients of total degree
2N-1
.
Thus, by
Proposition
5
,
\mathrm{disc}(m_{\theta})
is a polynomial
in
\mathbb{Q}[{\bm{x}}]
of total degree at most
(2N-1)\cdot 2^{(s\log s)^{c}}\leq 2^{(s\log s)^{c^{\prime}}}
, where
c^{\prime}>c
is again an effective
absolute constant.

Recall that

\tilde{\beta}_{i}=d\beta_{i}\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}}
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
where
d\in\mathbb{Q}[{\bm{x}}]
of total degree at most
2^{(s\log s)^{c}}
(see
Appendix

B
). We can thus write
\beta_{i}=\frac{P_{i}(\theta)}{b}
, where
b:=d\cdot\mathrm{disc}(m_{\theta})
is of total degree at
most
2^{(s\log s)^{c^{\prime\prime}}}
for an effective absolute constant
c^{\prime\prime}
.
∎

3.3.3.
Specialisation

We now show that if the
denominator
b

from
Proposition

7
does not vanish at
{\bm{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
then the system
\mathcal{S}_{\bm{\alpha}}
is satisfiable in
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
. The degree bound
on
b

in combination with the Polynomial Identity Lemma allows us to compute a bound on the probability of
\mathcal{S}_{{\bm{\alpha}}}
admitting a solution over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
when
{\bm{\alpha}}
is chosen uniformly at random in a fixed range.

Proposition 8
.

There exists an effectively computable constant
c
such that for all
D\in\mathbb{N}
with
D\geq 2^{(s\log s)^{c}}
, if
\mathcal{S}
has a solution in
\overline{\mathbb{Q}({\bm{x}})}
, then for
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m}
chosen independently and uniformly at random from
\{1,2,\ldots,D\}

\Pr\left(\mathcal{S}_{{\bm{\alpha}}}\text{ is satisfiable in }\overline{\mathbb{Q}}\right)\geq 1-\frac{2^{(s\log s)^{c}}}{D}.

Proof.

Suppose
\mathcal{S}
has a solution
{\bm{\beta}}=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})
in
\overline{\mathbb{Q}({\bm{x}})}^{n}
.
Recall we set
\theta
to be a primitive element for
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{Q}({\bm{x}})(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})
that is integral over
\mathbb{Q}[{\bm{x}}]
and denote by
m({\bm{x}},y)\in\mathbb{Q}[{\bm{x}}][y]
its minimal polynomial.
By
Proposition

7
, there exist polynomials
P_{1},\ldots,P_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}[{\bm{x}}][y]
and
b\in\mathbb{Z}[{\bm{x}}]
such that for all
\beta_{i}=\frac{P_{i}(\theta)}{b}
. Furthermore, the degree of
b
is at most
2^{(s\log s)^{c}}
where
c
is an effective constant.

We first show that given
{\bm{\alpha}}\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}^{m}
, the system
\mathcal{S}_{{\bm{\alpha}}}
is satisfiable over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}

if
b
does not
vanish on
{\bm{\alpha}}
.
For all
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
, let

(6)

g_{i}(y)=b^{2}f_{i}\left(\frac{P_{1}(y)}{b},\ldots,\frac{P_{n}(y)}{b}\right).

As discussed in the beginning of
Section

2
, we assume the degrees of the
f_{i}
’s in
y
are at most
2
, thus it must be that
g_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}[{\bm{x}}]
. Furthermore, the polynomials
g_{i}
must be multiples of
m
since
m
is irreducible and
g_{i}(\theta)=0
.

If
b
does not
vanish on
{\bm{\alpha}}
, (
6
) must also hold in
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
when specialised at
{\bm{x}}={\bm{\alpha}}
. This implies that if
\omega
is a solution of
m({\bm{\alpha}},y)
in
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
, then

\left(\frac{P_{1}({\bm{\alpha}},\omega)}{b({\bm{\alpha}})},\ldots,\frac{P_{n}({\bm{\alpha}},\omega)}{b({\bm{\alpha}})}\right)

is a solution of
\mathcal{S}_{{\bm{\alpha}}}
in
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
. Note that such a solution
\omega
of
m({\bm{\alpha}},y)\in\mathbb{Q}[y]
exists for all
{\bm{\alpha}}\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}^{m}
as the polynomial
m
is monic in
y
, hence
m({\bm{\alpha}},y)
is not constant.

Thus

\Pr\left(\mathcal{S}_{{\bm{\alpha}}}\text{ is satisfiable in }\overline{\mathbb{Q}}\right)\geq\Pr\left(b({\bm{\alpha}})\neq 0\right)=1-\Pr\left(b({\bm{\alpha}})=0\right),

which by the Polynomial Identity Lemma is at least
1-\frac{2^{(s\log s)^{c}}}{D}
.
∎

3.4.
Reduction to
\mathrm{HN}
.

We have just given estimates on the probability that the system
\mathcal{S}
specialised at an integer point
{\bm{\alpha}}
randomly chosen in a fixed range, is satisfiable over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
. We will now use them to exhibit a randomised polynomial-time reduction of
\mathrm{HNP}
to
\mathrm{HN}
. This allows us to use Koiran’s
AM
protocol for
\mathrm{HN}
to decide
\mathrm{HNP}
in
AM
assuming GRH as well.

Recall that without loss of generality we may assume
AM
protocols satisfy perfect completeness
[
11
, Section 3]
.
That is,
given a system of polynomial equations in
\mathbb{Z}[y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}]
that is satisfiable in
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
, we suppose the
AM
protocol for
\mathrm{HN}
outputs “satisfiable” with probability
1
, whereas given a system that is not satisfiable over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
, the probability of outputting “satisfiable” is at most
1/2
. We note that by standard amplification techniques, the error bound for false positives can be improved to
1/2^{\ell}
for a fixed
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
.

See
1

Proof.

Let
D:=3\cdot 2^{(s\log s)^{c}}
where
c
is the effective constant from
Proposition

8
.
We reduce our fixed instance of
\mathrm{HNP}
to an instance of
\mathrm{HN}
by choosing the values
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m}
independently and uniformly at random from the set
\{1,2,\ldots,D\}
, and running Koiran’s algorithm from
[
16
]
on
\mathcal{S}_{\bm{\alpha}}
.
Let us now analyse the probabilistic correctness of our reduction.

Denote by
\mathcal{A}
the event that Koiran’s algorithm outputs “satisfiable”, and by
\mathcal{A}^{c}
its complement, i.e., the event that the algorithm outputs “unsatisfiable”. Let
\mathcal{B}
be the event that for
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m}
chosen uniformly at random from
\{1,2,\ldots,D\}
, the system
\mathcal{S}_{{\bm{\alpha}}}
is satisfiable in
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
, and let
\mathcal{B}^{c}
be the complement of
\mathcal{B}
, i.e., the event that
\mathcal{S}_{{\bm{\alpha}}}
is not satisfiable in
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
.

Suppose that the system
\mathcal{S}
is not satisfiable over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}({\bm{x}})}
.
By
Proposition

4
, if we choose
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m}
uniformly at random in
\{1,2,\ldots,D\}
the probability that
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
is satisfiable over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
(the event
\mathcal{B}
) is at most
\frac{s2^{s}}{D}
. That is, since
D\geq 4s2^{s}
, we have
\Pr\left(\mathcal{B}\right)\leq\frac{1}{4}
.

We recall that Koiran’s algorithm admits perfect completeness, that is,
\Pr\left(\mathcal{A}^{c}\mid\mathcal{B}\right)=0
. By amplifying the error probability for unsatisfiable instances and repeating his algorithm
\ell=4
times, we further have
\Pr\left(\mathcal{A}\mid\mathcal{B}^{c}\right)\leq\frac{1}{16}
.

We can now bound from below the probability of the algorithm outputting “unsatisfiable” as follows.

\displaystyle\Pr\left(\mathcal{A}^{c}\right)

\displaystyle=\Pr\left(\mathcal{B}\right)\cdot\Pr\left(\mathcal{A}^{c}\mid\mathcal{B}\right)+\Pr\left(\mathcal{B}^{c}\right)\cdot\Pr\left(\mathcal{A}^{c}\mid\mathcal{B}^{c}\right)

\displaystyle=\Pr\left(\mathcal{B}^{c}\right)\cdot\Pr\left(\mathcal{A}^{c}\mid\mathcal{B}^{c}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\Big(1-\frac{1}{4}\Big)\cdot\Big(1-\frac{1}{16}\Big)=\frac{45}{64}\geq\frac{2}{3}\;,

which is the desired probability of correctness for unsatisfiable instances.

Let us now suppose the system
\mathcal{S}
is satisfiable over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}({\bm{x}})}
.
By
Proposition

8
, the probability that
\mathcal{S}_{{\bm{\alpha}}}
is satisfiable over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
when
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m}
are chosen uniformly at random in
\{1,2,\ldots,D\}
(the event
\mathcal{B}
) is at least
\frac{2^{(s\log s)^{c}}}{D}
.
In particular, our choice of
D=3\cdot 2^{(s\log s)^{c}}
, implies that
\Pr\left(\mathcal{B}\right)\geq\frac{2}{3}
.

Recall that Koiran’s algorithm admits perfect completeness, hence
\Pr\left(\mathcal{A}\mid\mathcal{B}\right)=1
.

We can bound the probability of the algorithm outputting “satisfiable” as follows.

\displaystyle\Pr\left(\mathcal{A}\right)

\displaystyle=\Pr\left(\mathcal{C}\right)\cdot\Pr\left(\mathcal{B}\mid\mathcal{C}\right)+\Pr\left(\mathcal{C}^{c}\right)\cdot\Pr\left(\mathcal{B}\mid\mathcal{C}^{c}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\Pr\left(\mathcal{B}\right)\cdot\Pr\left(\mathcal{A}\mid\mathcal{B}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2}{3}\cdot 1=\frac{2}{3}

We have again obtained the required probability of correctness for satisfiable instances, which completes our randomised polynomial-time reduction.
Since
AM
is closed under probabilistic reductions
[
1
, Section 8.4.2]
,
this proves the theorem.

∎
