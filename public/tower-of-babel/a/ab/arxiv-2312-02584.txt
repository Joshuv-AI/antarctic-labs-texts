On convexity and the Iwasawa decomposition of split real and complex Kac-Moody groups
Paul Zellhofer, Ralf Köhl

Abstract
We prove an analogue of Kostant’s convexity theorem for split real and complex Kac-Moody groups associated to free and cofree root data. The result can be seen as a first step towards describing the multiplication map in a Kac-Moody group in terms of Iwasawa coordinates. Our method involves a detailed analysis of the geometry of Weyl group orbits in the Cartan subalgebra of a real Kac-Moody algebra. It provides an alternative proof of Kostant convexity for semisimple Lie groups and also generalizes a linear analogue of Kostant’s theorem for Kac-Moody algebras that has been established by Kac and Peterson in 1984.



Introduction

Kostant convexity
is a fundamental property of semisimple Lie groups that has been established by B. Kostant in
[
Kos73
]
. The origins of Kostant’s theorem are related to the following problem. Let
G
be a connected semisimple real Lie group with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
and
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{k}\oplus\mathfrak{p}
a Cartan decomposition of
\mathfrak{g}
, where
\mathfrak{k}
is the fixed-point set of a Cartan involution
\theta:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}
and
\mathfrak{p}
is the
(-1)
-eigenspace of
\theta
. If
\mathfrak{a}
is a maximal abelian subspace of
\mathfrak{p}
, then the adjoint action of
\mathfrak{a}
on
\mathfrak{g}
is diagonalizable and the set
\Phi\subset\mathfrak{a}^{\ast}
of non-zero eigenvalues are the roots of
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{a})
. It is an abstract root system in
\mathfrak{a}^{\ast}
whose Weyl group
W
is finite. Denote by
\mathfrak{u}_{+}
the subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}
generated by all positive root spaces, then
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{k}\oplus\mathfrak{a}\oplus\mathfrak{u}_{+}
as vector spaces. This is known as the
Iwasawa decomposition
of
\mathfrak{g}
. It is a fundamental result in the theory of Lie groups that the Cartan involution
\theta
lifts to an involutive automorphism
\Theta:G\to G
with differential
\theta
. Its fixed-point set
K
is a maximal compact subgroup of
G
(if the center of
G
is finite) with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{k}
which contains all topological information about
G
in the sense that the quotient space
G/K
is contractible. In fact,
G/K
is a simply connected Riemannian symmetric space of non-compact type. The exponential map
\exp:\mathfrak{g}\to G
is injective on
\mathfrak{a}
and
\mathfrak{u}_{+}
and their exponential images
A
and
U_{+}
are contractible subgroups of
G
. Here,
A
is abelian and
U_{+}
is unipotent in the sense that its Lie algebra
\mathfrak{u}_{+}
is nilpotent. The multiplication map
K\times A\times U_{+}\to G
is a diffeomorphism which is called the
Iwasawa decomposition
of
G
.

However, this map is not a homomorphism of groups which raises the question whether it is possible to describe the multiplication in
G
in Iwasawa coordinates. More precisely, given two elements
g_{1}=k_{1}a_{1}u_{1}
and
g_{2}=k_{2}a_{2}u_{2}
of
G
, is it possible to compute the Iwasawa coordinates of the product
g_{1}g_{2}
from the ones of
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
? The major difficulty in this problem is the fact that
K
does not normalize
A
and
U_{+}
. A simpler question is to focus on one of the components and restrict
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
to certain subgroups of
G
. In applications, one is often interested in the
A
-component and we denote by
\Pi:G\to A,\,g=kau\mapsto a
the corresponding projection. It is clear that
\Pi(kg)=\Pi(g)
holds for all
k\in K
and
g\in G
, but there is no obvious way how to compute
\Pi(gk)
. Using the Cartan decomposition
G=KAK
, this problem can be reduced to the case
g=a\in A
.
Kostant’s convexity theorem
asserts that if
a\in A
and
k\in K
are arbitrary, then
\Pi(ak)
is contained in the (multiplicative) convex hull of the Weyl group orbit of
a
and, as
k
runs through all of
K
, every point of this convex hull is attained in this way. More precisely,
[
Kos73
, Theorem 4.1]
states that

\{\Pi(ak)\mid k\in K\}=\exp(\operatorname{conv}(W\cdot\log(a))).

(0.1)

Kac-Moody groups
are a natural generalization of semisimple Lie groups and inherit many of their properties, often by construction. It has thus been conjectured that a suitable analogue of Kostant’s theorem might be true in the Kac-Moody setting as well. This question is also related to the problem of determining whether the causal pre-order on Kac-Moody symmetric spaces is a partial order or trivial (cf.
[
FHHK20
, Proposition 7.46]
). In general, however, Kac-Moody groups carry no differentiable structure and an exponential map between a Kac-Moody algebra and group cannot be globally defined, so it is far more difficult to “linearize” problems about Kac-Moody groups.

In this article we prove an analogue of Kostant’s convexity theorem for certain classes of split real and complex Kac-Moody groups. The result is formulated as follows. Let
\mathbb{A}
be a symmetrizable generalized Cartan matrix with Weyl group
W
and let
\mathcal{D}
be a free and cofree Kac-Moody root datum associated to
\mathbb{A}
. If
\mathbb{K}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}\}
, the split minimal Kac-Moody group
G
over
\mathbb{K}
of type
\mathcal{D}
has an Iwasawa decomposition
G=KAU_{+}
generalizing the classical decomposition of a semisimple Lie group. Here,
K
is the fixed-point set of an involutive automorphism of
G
,
A
is the exponential image of the Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{a}
of the real Kac-Moody algebra of type
\mathcal{D}
and
U_{+}
is the subgroup of
G
generated by all positive root groups. The Weyl group
W
acts on
\mathfrak{a}
and
A
and the exponential map
\exp:\mathfrak{a}\to A
is a
W
-equivariant group isomorphism. The subset of
\mathfrak{a}
where all simple roots are non-negative is called the fundamental chamber and the union
X
of all its
W
-translates, called Weyl chambers, is the Tits cone of
\mathfrak{a}
. In Theorem
4.1
we prove the following result.

Main Theorem
.

Let
G
be a free and cofree, split, minimal, real or complex Kac-Moody group,
G=KAU_{+}
an Iwasawa decomposition of
G
and
\Pi:G\to A
the associated projection. Let
W
denote the Weyl group of
\mathbb{A}
. If
a\in A
is contained in the exponential image of an open chamber of the Tits cone
X\subset\mathfrak{a}
, then

\{\Pi(ak)\mid k\in K\}=\exp(\operatorname{conv}(W\cdot\log(a))).

If
\mathbb{A}
is the Cartan matrix of a finite-dimensional complex semisimple Lie algebra, then
G
is a reductive Lie group and the result is very similar to Kostant’s original theorem. In this case, the Weyl group
W
is finite and
X=\mathfrak{a}
. In the general case, however,
W
is typically infinite and
X
is a proper subset of
\mathfrak{a}
. Thus, it is a priori not clear whether the set
\operatorname{conv}(W\cdot\log(a))\subset\mathfrak{a}
is well-behaved in any sense. A major part of the proof of Theorem
4.1
consists of understanding the structure of this set. Our approach is inspired by the methods of
[
Kos73
]
. To obtain equality between the sets in (
0.1
), Kostant uses tools from the representation theory of
G
and an inductive argument. We proceed similarly, but our proof avoids tools that are particular to Lie theory and instead lays more emphasis on combinatorial properties of the Weyl group
W
, which is a (generally infinite) Coxeter group with respect to a finite generating set
S
. In particular, we relate the facial structure of
\operatorname{conv}(W\cdot\log(a))
to the Coxeter complex of
(W,S)
and present an inductive argument that allows to compute the Iwasawa coordinates of an element of the form
ak_{1}k_{2}
with
k_{1},k_{2}\in K
by iteration. In the Lie group case, our methods can even be extended in a suitable way to provide a new proof of Kostant’s original result. However, Kac-Moody groups usually do not admit a Cartan decomposition
G=KAK
, so our result does not yield a description of
\{\Pi(gk)\mid k\in K\}
for an arbitrary
g\in G
. Another consequence of Kostant’s result is that a semisimple Lie group satisfies
G=KU_{+}K
. As an application of Theorem
4.1
, we establish in Corollary
4.4
that this decomposition is not satisfied if
\mathbb{A}
is not of finite type.

Furthermore, if
\mathbb{A}
is of affine type, then
G
is an example of a group with an affine
BN
-pair. Such groups are always endowed with a different variant of the Iwasawa decomposition and act on an affine building which can be viewed as a discrete analogue of a symmetric space. Kostant’s convexity theorem has been transferred to the setting of affine buildings by P. Schwer
1
1

1

née Hitzelberger
in
[
Hit10
]
and generalized in
[
Hit11
]
. Hence, real and complex Kac-Moody groups of affine type can be endowed with two different Iwasawa decompositions that both satisfy an analogue of Kostant’s convexity theorem. The result of Schwer extends far beyond this setting as it is formulated entirely in the language of buildings and does not require any group action to begin with. In addition, it also applies to groups with an affine
BN
-pair outside the Kac-Moody realm such as semisimple algebraic groups over non-Archimedean local fields.

[

]
. We close our discussion in Section

Kac-Moody algebras

We begin by collecting the most important properties of Kac-Moody algebras and fix the setting in which we are going to work in the following chapters. We assume that the reader is familiar with the basic theory of these algebras and standard terminology in the subject. A
generalized Cartan matrix
(“GCM”) is an integer matrix
\mathbb{A}=(a_{ij})_{i,j\in I}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times n}
which satisfies the following three conditions for all

a_{ii}=2
, (ii)
a_{ij}\leq 0
for

, (iii)

if and only if
a_{ji}=0
. It is said to be

if there exist a diagonal matrix

and a symmetric matrix

of size

such that

. It is called

if
I
is the disjoint union

of two non-empty proper subsets

such that

for all
i\in I_{1}
and

. In this case, the submatrices
\mathbb{A}_{1}
and
\mathbb{A}_{2}
of

indexed by
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
are GCM’s as well and we write
\mathbb{A}=\mathbb{A}_{1}\oplus\mathbb{A}_{2}
. Otherwise,
\mathbb{A}
is said to be
indecomposable
. There is a complete classification of indecomposable GCM’s that is formulated as follows (cf.
[
Kac90
, Theorem 4.3]
): For a vector
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we write
\lambda>0
if
\lambda_{i}>0
for all
i\in I
and, likewise, we define
\lambda<0
,
\lambda\geq 0
and
\lambda\leq 0
. If
\mathbb{A}
is indecomposable, then exactly one of the following conditions holds. According to these three cases, we say that
\mathbb{A}
is of

,

.

(Fin)

\det(\mathbb{A})\neq 0

\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

(Aff)

\operatorname{rank}(\mathbb{A})=n-1
, there exists
\lambda>0
such that
\mathbb{A}\lambda=0
;
\mathbb{A}\lambda\geq 0
implies
\mathbb{A}\lambda=0
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

(Ind)

There exists

\mathbb{A}\lambda\geq 0

\displaystyle[h,e_{i}]

\displaystyle=\langle c_{i},h\rangle e_{i}\text{ and }[h,f_{i}]=-\langle c_{i},h\rangle f_{i}\text{ for $h\in\mathfrak{h}$ and $i\in I$}

Remark 1.1
.

It is immediate from the property
\langle c_{j},h_{i}\rangle=a_{ij}
for all

\mathcal{D}
is not cofree (resp. free), then the rows (resp. columns) of the GCM

are linearly dependent over

that is not cofree (resp. free). Hence, a GCM is invertible if and only if all associated root data are free and cofree. In the non-invertible case, there always exist Kac-Moody root data of rank

which are therefore neither free nor cofree.

If

such that every reduced decomposition of

contains precisely the reflections in

. We call
\operatorname{supp}(w):=\{i\in I:s_{i}\in S(w)\}

of

. For any subset

of

generated by

is called a

of

. A left coset

with

is called a

. If

for some

and

and
W_{I\setminus\{i\}}
. The

of

is the partially-ordered set (“poset”) of all standard cosets of

ordered by reversed inclusion and denoted by

\Sigma(W,S)=\{wW_{J}\mid w\in W,\,J\subset I\}.

We also write

for the poset with the same underlying set but ordered by non-reversed inclusion. Every standard coset
wW_{J}\subset W
has a unique element
w^{\prime}
of minimal length which is characterized by
l(w^{\prime}w_{J})=l(w^{\prime})+l(w_{J})
for all
w_{J}\in W_{J}
.

Next, we introduce some notions from the representation theory of
\mathfrak{g}
(cf.
[
Mar18
, Section 4.1]
or
[
Kac90
, Chapter 3 and 9]
). A representation
\rho
of
\mathfrak{g}
on a
\mathbb{K}
-vector space

is said to be
integrable
if
\mathfrak{h}
acts diagonally on
V
and for every
i\in I
and
v\in V
there exists some
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\rho(e_{i})^{N}v=\rho(f_{i})^{N}v=0
. A functional
\lambda\in\mathfrak{h}^{\ast}
is called a
dominant integral weight
if it satisfies
\langle\lambda,h_{i}\rangle\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
for all
i\in I
. For every such weight, there exists a unique irreducible integrable
\mathbb{K}
-linear representation
\rho_{\lambda}:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{gl}(L(\lambda))
with highest weight
\lambda
. This means that there exists a non-zero vector
v_{\lambda}\in L(\lambda)
, which is unique up to scalars, such that
\rho_{\lambda}(h)v_{\lambda}=\langle\lambda,h\rangle v_{\lambda}
for all
h\in\mathfrak{h}
, the subalgebra
\mathfrak{u}_{+}:=\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Phi_{+}}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}\subset\mathfrak{g}
annihilates
v_{\lambda}
and
L(\lambda)
is generated by
v_{\lambda}
as a representation of the universal enveloping algebra of
\mathfrak{g}
. The Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{h}\subset\mathfrak{g}
acts diagonally on
L(\lambda)
, its joint eigenvalues
\operatorname{wt}(L(\lambda))\subset\mathfrak{h}^{\ast}
are the
weights
of
L(\lambda)
and the joint eigenspace decomposition

L(\lambda)=\bigoplus_{\mu\in\operatorname{wt}(L(\lambda))}L(\lambda)_{\mu}

is the
weight space decomposition
of
L(\lambda)
. Every weight is of the form
\lambda-c(\alpha)
for some
\alpha\in Q_{+}
and the set of weights is
W
-invariant. If
\mu
is any weight and
x\in\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
for some
\alpha\in\Phi
, then it is immediate from the defining relations (
1.1
) of
\mathfrak{g}
that

\rho_{\lambda}(x)(L(\lambda)_{\mu})\subset L(\lambda)_{\mu+c(\alpha)}.

(1.4)

Depending on the Kac-Moody root datum
\mathcal{D}
, there does not necessarily exist a non-zero dominant integral weight. If
\mathcal{D}
is cofree, however, then
\{h_{i}\mid i\in I\}
can be extended to a
\mathbb{K}
-basis of
\mathfrak{h}
and the corresponding dual basis consists of dominant integral weights. If
\langle\lambda,h_{i}\rangle=0
for all

, then the defining relations of
\mathfrak{g}
imply that we may define a representation
\rho_{\lambda}
of
\mathfrak{g}
on the one-dimensional vector space
\mathbb{K}
by setting
\rho_{\lambda}(e_{i})=\rho_{\lambda}(f_{i}):=0
for all

and
\rho_{\lambda}(h)1:=\langle\lambda,h\rangle
for all

. This clearly defines an irreducible representation of
\mathfrak{g}
with highest weight
\lambda
. Since

is unique up to isomorphism, it follows that

is one-dimensional and isomorphic to
\mathbb{K}
under the map

.

Assumption 1.2
.

From now on, we will always make the following assumptions which will play a central role in the proof of our main theorem:

(i)

The GCM
\mathbb{A}
is symmetrizable.

(ii)

The Kac-Moody root datum
\mathcal{D}
is free and cofree.

(iii)

The field
\mathbb{K}
is either
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
.

The first assumption occurs frequently in the theory of Kac-Moody algebras and will be used explicitly in the proof of Theorem
4.1
. The necessity of the third will already be apparent in the formulation of that result as it involves an “exponential function” on the Cartan subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}
whose definition is based on the usual real or complex exponential map. In our discussion, it will usually be possible to treat real and complex Kac-Moody algebras at the same time, so we do not want to distinguish them in notation. Hence, for the rest of this article, we denote by
\mathfrak{g}
either the real or the complex Kac-Moody algebra of type
\mathcal{D}
. If we need to emphasize the field, we write
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}
or
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{C}}
. It will, however, be very important to distinguish the real Cartan subalgebra from the complex one, so we will write
\mathfrak{a}:=\Lambda^{\vee}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{R}
and
\mathfrak{h}:=\Lambda^{\vee}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{C}
. We explicitly do not denote these algebras by
\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{R}}
and
\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{C}}
since we often want to view
\mathfrak{a}
as a subset of
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{C}}
and a notation like
\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{R}}\subset\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{C}}
might be misleading. We denote by
\theta:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}
the involutive automorphism of
\mathfrak{g}
obtained from pre-composing the Chevalley involution of
\mathfrak{g}
with complex conjugation. More precisely, if
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}
, then
\theta
coincides with the Chevalley involution
\omega_{\mathbb{R}}
of
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}
, and if
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{C}
, then
\theta
is the unique extension of
\omega_{\mathbb{R}}
to a
\mathbb{C}
-antilinear automorphism of
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{C}}
. In both cases, we call
\theta
the
compact involution
of
\mathfrak{g}
and denote its fixed-point set by
\mathfrak{k}
.

Assumption (ii) is a simplification that requires some justification. It allows us to identify
Q
and
Q^{\vee}
with subsets of
\Lambda
and
\Lambda^{\vee}
. For every
i\in I
, we will therefore write
\alpha_{i}
for
c_{i}
and
\alpha_{i}^{\vee}
for
h_{i}
and suppress the inclusions

and
h
from the notation. With this convention, we may extend
\Phi_{0}^{\vee}\subset\Lambda^{\vee}
to an
\mathbb{R}
-basis
\mathcal{B}:=\{\alpha_{1}^{\vee},\ldots,\alpha_{n}^{\vee},h_{n+1},\ldots,h_{d}\}
of
\mathfrak{a}
, which also yields a
\mathbb{C}
-basis of
\mathfrak{h}
, and we denote by
\mathcal{B}^{\ast}:=\{\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{n},\phi_{n+1},\ldots,\phi_{d}\}\subset\mathfrak{a}^{\ast}
the corresponding dual basis.
Recall that the assumption that
\mathcal{D}
is free and cofree implies
d\geq 2n-\operatorname{rank}(\mathbb{A})\geq n
. The complex Kac-Moody algebra
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{C}}
is then simply a direct product of the Kac-Moody algebra
\mathfrak{g}(\mathbb{A})
studied in
[
Mar18
]
, which coincides with the definition in
[
Kac90
]
by assumption (i) and the Gabber-Kac theorem. More precisely, we have
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{C}}\cong\mathfrak{g}(\mathbb{A})\oplus\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}
as a Lie algebra, where
\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}
is a subspace of
\mathfrak{h}
of dimension
d-2n+\operatorname{rank}(\mathbb{A})
on which all roots vanish. The obvious analogue of this assertion holds in the real case. This allows us to use most results of
[
Kac90
]
and
[
Mar18
]
in our setting. Simply put, we study Kac-Moody algebras that behave exactly like the classical ones, except that we allow the Cartan subalgebra to have dimension larger than
2n-\operatorname{rank}(\mathbb{A})
. As discussed in Remark
1.1
, condition (ii) is always satisfied if
\mathbb{A}
is invertible.

Moreover, the assumption that
\mathcal{D}
is free guarantees that the
\mathbb{R}
-linear representation
W\to\operatorname{GL}(\mathfrak{a})
is faithful and the set

C:=\{h\in\mathfrak{a}:\langle\alpha_{i},h\rangle>0\,\,\forall i\in I\}

is not empty. It is called the
open fundamental chamber
and its closure (w.r.t. the standard Euclidean topology on
\mathfrak{a}
)

\overline{C}:=\{h\in\mathfrak{a}:\langle\alpha_{i},h\rangle\geq 0\,\,\forall i\in I\}

is the
closed fundamental chamber
. It can equivalently be described as

\overline{C}=\bigg\{h\in\mathfrak{a}:\forall w\in W:h-w(h)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\alpha_{i}^{\vee}\text{ for some $c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$}\bigg\}.

(1.5)

The
W
-translates of
\overline{C}
are called
Weyl chambers
. Every such set
w(\overline{C})\subset\mathfrak{a}
with
w\in W
is a simplicial cone which is bounded by the
walls

\{w(V_{\alpha_{i}})\mid i\in I\}
, where
V_{\alpha_{i}}:=\operatorname{ker}(\alpha_{i})
. The union of all Weyl chambers

X:=\bigcup_{w\in W}w(\overline{C})\subset\mathfrak{a}

is the
Tits cone
of
\mathfrak{a}
. The closed fundamental chamber is a strict fundamental domain for the
W
-action on
X\subset\mathfrak{a}
, i.e. every point of
X
lies in the
W
-orbit of a unique point in
\overline{C}
. If
h\in\overline{C}
and
J:=\{i\in I:\langle\alpha_{i},h\rangle=0\}
, then the stabilizer of

is the standard subgroup
W_{J}\subset W
generated by the reflections
S_{J}=\{s_{j}\mid j\in J\}
.

Finally, Assumption
1.2
also has one important consequence for the representation theory of
\mathfrak{g}
. If
\lambda\in\mathfrak{a}^{\ast}\subset\mathfrak{h}^{\ast}
is a dominant integral weight, then by
[
Kac90
, Proposition 9.4 and Lemma 11.5]
there exists a non-degenerate Hermitian form
H:L(\lambda)_{\mathbb{C}}\times L(\lambda)_{\mathbb{C}}\to\mathbb{C}
which is
contravariant
in the sense that

H(x\cdot v,w)=-H(v,\theta(x)\cdot w)\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for all $x\in\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{C}}$ and $v,w\in L(\lambda)_{\mathbb{C}}$.}

(1.6)

It is unique up to scalars and different weight spaces are
H
-orthogonal. Since
\lambda|_{\mathfrak{a}}
is real valued, it restricts to a non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form on
L(\lambda)_{\mathbb{R}}
. If the GCM
\mathbb{A}
is symmetrizable, then
H
is positive definite by
[
Kac90
, Theorem 11.7]
. In particular, if
\mathcal{D}
is free and cofree, the dual basis
\mathcal{B}^{\ast}\subset\mathfrak{a}^{\ast}
consists of dominant integral weights. For ease of reference, we summarize the most important consequences of Assumption
1.2
in the following proposition.

Proposition 1.3
.

Suppose that Assumption
1.2
is in effect, then
d\geq 2n-\operatorname{rank}(\mathbb{A})
and the following three properties hold:

(i)

The dual basis
\mathcal{B}^{\ast}=\{\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{n},\phi_{n+1},\ldots,\phi_{d}\}\subset\mathfrak{a}^{\ast}
consists of dominant integral weights. Each of the real or complex highest-weight modules
L(\omega_{1}),\ldots,L(\omega_{n}),L(\phi_{n+1}),\ldots,L(\phi_{d})
can be endowed with a positive definite, contravariant, symmetric bilinear or Hermitian form
H
such that weight spaces belonging to different weights are
H
-orthogonal.

(ii)

For every
i\in\{n+1,\ldots,d\}
, the highest-weight module
L(\phi_{i})
is one-dimensional. The subalgebras
\mathfrak{u}_{+}
and
\mathfrak{u}_{-}
of
\mathfrak{g}
act trivially on
L(\phi_{i})
and an element
h\in\mathfrak{h}
acts on
L(\phi_{i})
by multiplication with
\langle\phi_{i},h\rangle
.

(iii)

The open fundamental chamber
C\subset\mathfrak{a}
is not empty.

2
Convex hulls of Weyl group orbits

The goal of this chapter is to study the geometry of
W
-orbits in
\mathfrak{a}
and relate it to the combinatorial structure of the Coxeter system
(W,S)
. More precisely, we will be interested in the convex hull of the
W
-orbit of an element
h\in\mathfrak{a}
:

\Xi_{h}:=\operatorname{conv}(W\cdot h)

The structure of this set depends heavily on the location of
h
with respect to the double Tits cone
X\cup-X\subset\mathfrak{a}
. One can distinguish the following three possibilities. We say that
h
is

•

regular
if
h
or
-h
is contained in an open chamber of
X
.

•

singular
if
h
or
-h
is contained in a wall of
X
.

•

external
if neither
h
nor
-h
is contained in
X
.

In the present article, we will restrict our attention to regular elements, which exist since
\mathcal{D}
is assumed to be free. In this case, we shall see that
\Xi_{h}
has a simple combinatorial description in terms of the Coxeter complex
\Sigma(W,S)
. Some of our results do indeed generalize to the singular case and we briefly discuss this in Section
4.3
. We emphasize that Assumption
1.2
and the notational conventions following it are in effect for this entire chapter.

Remark 2.1
.

If the Weyl group
W
is finite, then
\Xi_{h}
is a convex polytope (see Section
2.1
for the definition) and most results of Section
2.2
can be deduced from the general theory of these sets. However, this is the case if and only if
\mathbb{A}
is the Cartan matrix of a finite-dimensional semisimple Lie algebra which is a very small subclass of Kac-Moody algebras. If
W
is infinite, it is not even a priori clear whether
\Xi_{h}
is a closed subset of
\mathfrak{a}
.

Convention:
From this point onward, the set of imaginary roots
\Phi^{\operatorname{im}}
will not appear in our discussion. Thus, we simplify the notation and denote the set of real roots by
\Phi
instead of
\Phi^{\operatorname{re}}
.

2.1
Some results from convex geometry

To prepare for the study of
\Xi_{h}
, we begin by collecting some basic properties of convex sets in finite-dimensional real vector spaces. We follow the terminology in
[
Roc70
]
. More details and proofs of the assertions about general convex sets can be found in
[
Roc70
, §1, 6, 11, 17, 18]
or
[
Gru07
, Chapter 3-5]
. A thorough discussion of convex polytopes that goes way beyond what is needed for this article is provided by
[
Gru07
, Chapter 14-20]
.

Let
V
be a real vector space of dimension
d<\infty
equipped with its standard Euclidean topology. For two points
x,y\in V
, we denote by
[x,y]
the closed line segment connecting
x
to
y
and by
(x,y)
,
[x,y)
and
(x,y]
the corresponding open and half-open line segments. Let
P\subset V
be a convex subset, not necessarily closed. A
face
of
P
is a non-empty convex subset
F\subset P
such that whenever
p_{1},p_{2}\in P
satisfy
(p_{1},p_{2})\cap F\neq\emptyset
, then we already have
[p_{1},p_{2}]\subset F
. The
dimension
of a face is the dimension of its affine span. We write
\mathcal{F}(P)
for the partially-ordered set of faces of
P
ordered by inclusion. Observe that
P
is trivially a face of itself, but we have excluded the empty set from being a face. This is merely a convention that will simplify the terminology in Section
2.2
. Hence,
\mathcal{F}(P)
has a maximal element, but not necessarily a minimal one. The intersection of any two faces of
P
is either empty or again a face of
P
and for any face
F
of
P
, the faces of
F
are precisely the faces of
P
contained in
F
. If
A\subset P
is a convex subset and
F
a face of
P
which contains a point from the relative interior of
A
, then it follows immediately from the definition of a face that
A\subset F
. In particular, every proper face
F\subsetneq P
is contained in the topological boundary of
P
and the relative interiors of different faces are disjoint. For two points
p_{1},p_{2}\in P
, we therefore have
[p_{1},p_{2}]\subset\partial P
if and only if
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
are contained in a common proper face. Note that non-trivial faces do not exist if
P\cap\partial P=\emptyset
.

We will mostly be interested in a less general notion. Suppose that
E\subset V
is an affine hyperplane
2
2

2

By an
affine hyperplane
in
V
, we always mean an affine subspace of codimension
1
.
such that
P
lies in one of the two closed half-spaces defined by
E
and
F:=P\cap E\neq\emptyset
. Then
F
is a face of
P
and faces of this type are said to be
exposed
. The plane
E
is called a
supporting hyperplane
of
P
and the half-space containing
P
is a
supporting half-space
. Exposed faces of dimension
0
and
1
are called
vertices
and
edges
, those of dimension
\dim(P)-1
are called
facets
and their supporting hyperplanes are referred to as
defining hyperplanes
. If
\dim(P)=d
, then defining hyperplanes are uniquely determined and, more generally, two defining hyperplanes give rise to the same face if and only if their intersections with the affine span of
P
coincide. The trivial face
P
is clearly exposed if
\dim(P)<d
since every affine hyperplane
E
containing
P
is supporting, but it is trivial in the sense that
P
is contained in both closed half-spaces defined by
E
. We also consider
P
to be exposed if
\dim(P)=d
. But
P
may have non-exposed proper faces which are then necessarily contained in
\partial P
. However, it follows from a separation argument (cf.
[
Roc70
, Theorem 11.6]
) that every point of
P\cap\partial P
is contained in a non-trivial supporting hyperplane of
P
. Consequently, every proper face of
P
is contained in a proper exposed face. In particular, if
P
is closed, then every point of
P
is either contained in the relative interior of
P
or lies on a non-trivial supporting hyperplane, which implies that
P
can be reconstructed as the intersection of all supporting half-spaces containing it. In algebraic terms, this means that a point is contained in
P
if and only if it satisfies a family of linear (in-)equalities. If
\dim(P)=d
, then every supporting hyperplane is non-trivial and none of these inequalities are equalities. By replacing
V
with the affine span of
P
, it is usually no restriction to assume that
\dim(P)=d
, but for our purposes, it will be more convenient to avoid this assumption.

As mentioned above, the faces of a face
F
of
P
are exactly the faces of
P
contained in
F
. In particular, this holds for exposed faces. More generally, suppose that
E\subset V
is an arbitrary hyperplane, not necessarily supporting. If
P^{\prime}:=P\cap E
is non-empty, then for every face
F
of
P
, the intersection
F\cap E
is either empty or a face of
P^{\prime}
. Conversely, it is not difficult to show that for every face
F^{\prime}
of
P^{\prime}
there exists a face
F
of
P
such that
F^{\prime}=F\cap E
. The intersection of all such faces of
P
also has this property, so if
F
is assumed to be minimal, it is uniquely determined and its dimension is given by

\operatorname{dim}(F)=\begin{cases}\operatorname{dim}(F^{\prime})&\text{if $F^{\prime}$ is a face of $P$ contained in $E$}\\
\operatorname{dim}(F^{\prime})+1&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(2.1)

For a set
Y\subset V
, the
convex hull
of
Y
is the smallest convex subset of
V
containing
Y
. It consists precisely of all finite convex combinations
\sum_{y\in Y}\lambda_{y}y
, where
\lambda_{y}\in[0,1]
for all
y\in Y
,
\lambda_{y}>0
only for finitely many
y\in Y
and
\sum_{y\in Y}\lambda_{y}=1
. It is denoted by
\operatorname{conv}(Y)
. If
Y
is finite, then
P:=\operatorname{conv}(Y)
is compact and called a
convex polytope
. A convex polytope
P
has only finitely many faces which are all exposed. Every face
F
of
P
is of the form
F=\operatorname{conv}(Y^{\prime})
for some subset
Y^{\prime}\subset Y
and thus again a convex polytope. For every
0\leq k\leq\dim(P)
, there exists a
k
-dimensional face of
P
and all maximal chains in
\mathcal{F}(P)
have the same length. In particular, every non-trivial face of
P
is contained in a facet, i.e. a maximal proper face.

If
Y
is infinite, however, it is not necessarily true that
P:=\operatorname{conv}(Y)
is closed, not even if
Y
is uniformly discrete. It might even be open in which case it cannot have any non-trivial faces. Hence, the theory of convex polytopes cannot be applied in this situation and it is not clear whether
\mathcal{F}(P)
has any interesting properties. However, it is still true that if
F\subset P
is an exposed face, then there exists a subset
Y^{\prime}\subset Y
such that
F=\operatorname{conv}(Y^{\prime})
.

2.2
Orbits of regular elements

We now turn to the study of
\Xi_{h}=\operatorname{conv}(W\cdot h)
where
h\in\mathfrak{a}
is regular. After possibly replacing
h
by
-h
, it is no restriction to assume that
h
is contained in an open chamber of
X
. In this case, the
W
-translates
\{w(h)\mid w\in W\}
are all distinct. Since
\Xi_{h}
is
W
-invariant, we may w.l.o.g. assume that
h\in C
, i.e.
\langle\alpha_{i},h\rangle>0
for all
i\in I
. Our goal is to show that
\Xi_{h}
is a closed convex set whose face poset
\mathcal{F}(\Xi_{h})
is isomorphic to
\Sigma^{\ast}(W,S)
, i.e. the poset of all standard cosets of
(W,S)
ordered by non-reversed inclusion. Our approach will consist of two steps: First, we explicitly construct exposed faces of
\Xi_{h}
. Afterwards, we show that
\Xi_{h}
is closed and that every face is exposed.

First of all, note that the assumption that
\mathcal{D}
is cofree implies
\dim(\Xi_{h})=n
since it is contained in the
n
-dimensional affine subspace
E_{h}:=h+\mathfrak{a}^{\prime}
, where
\mathfrak{a}^{\prime}:=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{R}\alpha_{i}^{\vee}
, and the
n+1
points
\{h,s_{1}(h),\ldots,s_{n}(h)\}\subset\Xi_{h}
are affinely independent. Next,
W
-invariance of
\Xi_{h}
also implies that the set of faces of
\Xi_{h}
is
W
-invariant. Hence, every face of
\Xi_{h}
is
W
-equivalent to a face that meets
\overline{C}
. The same is also true for the subset of exposed faces. Every exposed face
F
of
\Xi_{h}
is of the form
F=\operatorname{conv}(W^{\prime}\cdot h)
where
W^{\prime}
is a subset of
W
, so by
W
-invariance, it is also no restriction to assume that
1\in W^{\prime}
and hence
h\in F
. Thus, it is sufficient to construct exposed faces containing
h
. To this end, we define for every
i\in I
the hyperplane

H^{(i)}:=\{h^{\prime}\in E_{h}:\langle\omega_{i},h^{\prime}\rangle=\langle\omega_{i},h\rangle\}\subset E_{h}

In order to show that these hyperplanes give rise to faces of
\Xi_{h}
, we need the following lemma from the general theory of Coxeter groups, which is a refinement of (
1.5
).

Lemma 2.2
.

If
h\in C
, then
h-w(h)=\sum_{i\in I}c_{i}\alpha_{i}^{\vee}
with
c_{i}>0
for all
i\in\operatorname{supp}(w)
and
c_{i}=0
for all
i\notin\operatorname{supp}(w)
.

Proof.

By (
1.5
) and the fact that
c_{i}=0
if
i\notin\operatorname{supp}(w)
, it only remains to show that
c_{i}>0
for all
i\in\operatorname{supp}(w)
. Suppose that this was not the case, then we would have
\langle\omega_{i},w(h)\rangle=\langle\omega_{i},h\rangle
for some
i\in\operatorname{supp}(w)
. But then every element in the left coset
W^{(i)}w
satisfies the same equation, so we may assume that
w
is the unique element of minimal length in that coset. In this case, it has the form
w=s_{i}w^{\prime}
for some
w^{\prime}\in W
with
l(w^{\prime})=l(w)-1
and we have

\langle\omega_{i},h\rangle=\langle\omega_{i},w(h)\rangle=\langle s_{i}(\omega_{i}),w^{\prime}(h)\rangle=\langle\omega_{i},w^{\prime}(h)\rangle-\langle\alpha_{i},w^{\prime}(h)\rangle

with
\langle\omega_{i},w^{\prime}(h)\rangle\leq\langle\omega_{i},h\rangle
by (
1.5
) and
\langle\alpha_{i},w^{\prime}(h)\rangle=\langle w^{\prime-1}(\alpha_{i}),h\rangle>0
by (
1.3
) because
l(w^{\prime-1})=l(w^{\prime})<l(w)=l(w^{-1})=l(w^{\prime-1}s_{i})
. Hence, we arrive at a contradiction.
∎

Proposition 2.3
.

For every
i\in I
, the plane
H^{(i)}
is a defining hyperplane of
\Xi_{h}
and the corresponding facet of
\Xi_{h}
is given by

F^{(i)}:=\operatorname{conv}(W^{(i)}\cdot h),

where
W^{(i)}
is the subgroup of
W
generated by
S^{(i)}=S\setminus\{s_{i}\}
.

Proof.

If
h^{\prime}\in\Xi_{h}
, we can write it as
h^{\prime}=\sum_{w\in W}\lambda_{w}w(h)
with
\lambda_{w}\in[0,1]
for all
w\in W
and
\sum_{w\in W}\lambda_{w}=1
, where
\lambda_{w}>0
only for finitely many
w\in W
. Moreover, it follows from (
1.5
) that
\langle\omega_{i},h-w(h)\rangle\geq 0
for every
w\in W
, which implies that

\langle\omega_{i},h^{\prime}\rangle=\sum_{w\in W}\lambda_{w}\langle\omega_{i},w(h)\rangle\leq\sum_{w\in W}\lambda_{w}\langle\omega_{i},h\rangle=\langle\omega_{i},h\rangle.

Hence,
\Xi_{h}
lies on one side of
H^{(i)}
and
\Xi_{h}\cap H^{(i)}
is an exposed face. Moreover,
H^{(i)}
contains
h
and
s_{j}(h)
whenever
j\neq i
since

\langle\omega_{i},s_{j}(h)\rangle=\langle\omega_{i},h\rangle-\langle\alpha_{j},h\rangle\langle\omega_{i},\alpha_{j}^{\vee}\rangle=\langle\omega_{i},h\rangle.

As these
n
points are affinely independent, it follows that
\Xi_{h}\cap H^{(i)}
has dimension
n-1
and is therefore a facet of
\Xi_{h}
. Iterating the computation above shows that
H^{(i)}
contains
w(h)
for every
w\in W^{(i)}
and hence
F^{(i)}\subset\Xi_{h}\cap H^{(i)}
. Conversely, if
w\notin W^{(i)}
, then every reduced decomposition of
w
contains
s_{i}
, i.e.
i\in\operatorname{supp}(w)
. By Lemma
2.2
, this implies that
h-w(h)
is a linear combination of simple coroots in which
\alpha_{i}^{\vee}
occurs with a positive coefficient, so that
\langle\omega_{i},w(h)\rangle<\langle\omega_{i},h\rangle
. Let now
h^{\prime}\in\Xi_{h}\cap H^{(i)}
, then
h^{\prime}
has to be a convex combination of the points in
W\cdot h\cap H^{(i)}
which we have just shown to coincide with
W^{(i)}\cdot h
. Thus, it follows that
\Xi_{h}\cap H^{(i)}\subset F^{(i)}
and the proposition is proven.
∎

Observe that we consider the hyperplanes
H^{(1)},\ldots,H^{(n)}
as subsets of
E_{h}
, which is the affine span of
\Xi_{h}
, rather than the full vector space
\mathfrak{a}
. If
d>n
, then for every
i\in I
there are many hyperplanes in
\mathfrak{a}
that intersect
E_{h}
in
H^{(i)}
, but this extension process is irrelevant to our discussion since we are only interested in intersections with
\Xi_{h}\subset E_{h}
. As a subset of
E_{h}
,
H^{(i)}
is uniquely determined by
F^{(i)}
. Since the set of exposed faces is
W
-invariant and closed under intersections, the following corollary follows immediately from the previous proposition.

Corollary 2.4
.

For every subset
J\subset I
, the set
F_{J}:=\operatorname{conv}(W_{J}\cdot h)
is an exposed face of
\Xi_{h}
of dimension
|J|
. More generally, for every
w\in W
, the set
w(F_{J})
is an exposed face of dimension
|J|
and coincides with
\operatorname{conv}(wW_{J}\cdot h)
. In particular, every
W
-translate of
h
is a vertex of
\Xi_{h}
.

If
J,J^{\prime}\subset I
are two distinct subsets of
I
, then the affine spans of the Weyl group orbits
W_{J}\cdot h
and
W_{J^{\prime}}\cdot h
do not coincide, which implies that the faces
F_{J}
and
F_{J^{\prime}}
are also distinct. Hence,
h
is contained in at least
2^{n}
faces of
\Xi_{h}
. More generally, we have
w(F_{J})=w^{\prime}(F_{J^{\prime}})
if and only if
J=J^{\prime}
and
w^{-1}w^{\prime}\in W_{J}
, so the map
wW_{J}\mapsto w(F_{J})
defines an order-preserving injection
\Sigma^{\ast}(W,S)\to\mathcal{F}(\Xi_{h})
. In order to show that this map is also surjective, we need more information on the topology of
\Xi_{h}
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
h\in C
and
h^{\prime}\in\Xi_{h}
be such that
v:=h-h^{\prime}=\sum_{i\in I}c_{i}\alpha_{i}^{\vee}
satisfies
c_{i}>0
for all
i\in I
. Then the open line segment
(h,h^{\prime})
is contained in the relative interior of
\Xi_{h}\subset E_{h}
.

Proof.

Choose any
\lambda\in(0,1)
and set
x:=h-\lambda v\in\Xi_{h}
. First, we show that for every
i\in I
there exists some
\mu_{i}>0
such that
x-\mu_{i}\alpha_{i}^{\vee}\in\Xi_{h}
by writing this point as a convex combination of
\{h,h^{\prime},s_{i}(h)\}
. To this end, choose
\mu_{i}>0
such that
c:=\frac{\mu_{i}}{\langle\alpha_{i},h\rangle}<1-\lambda
, set
b:=\lambda
and
a:=1-b-c
. Then we have
a,b,c\in(0,1)
and
a+b+c=1
. Moreover, the following computation shows that the corresponding convex combination is the desired point:

\displaystyle ah+bh^{\prime}+cs_{i}(h)

\displaystyle=ah+bh+b(h^{\prime}-h)+c(h-\langle\alpha_{i},h\rangle\alpha_{i}^{\vee})

\displaystyle=(a+b+c)h-bv-c\langle\alpha_{i},h\rangle\alpha_{i}^{\vee}

\displaystyle=h-\lambda v-\mu_{i}\alpha_{i}^{\vee}=x-\mu_{i}\alpha_{i}^{\vee}

On the other hand,
\Xi_{h}
also contains
x+\mu v
for all
0\leq\mu\leq\lambda
. Since
\lambda>0
and
c_{i}>0
for all
i\in I
, it follows that for every
i\in I
,
\Xi_{h}
also contains
x+\mu_{i}^{\prime}\alpha_{i}^{\vee}
for sufficiently small
\mu_{i}^{\prime}>0
. Altogether, we obtain that
\Xi_{h}
contains the cube

x+\prod_{i\in I}[-\mu_{i},\mu_{i}^{\prime}]\alpha_{i}^{\vee}\subset E_{h}.

Since the simple roots form a basis of
\mathfrak{a}^{\prime}
, this set has non-empty relative interior in
E_{h}
, so
x
lies in the relative interior of
\Xi_{h}\subset E_{h}
.
∎

Proposition 2.6
.

If
h\in C
, then
\Xi_{h}
is closed.

Proof.

If
W
is finite, then
\Xi_{h}
is a convex polytope and hence compact. Thus, for the rest of the proof, we may assume that
|W|=\infty
. For every
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we set

U_{k}:=\{w\in W:l(w)\leq k\}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003ptX_{k}:=\operatorname{conv}(U_{k}\cdot h).

Since
U_{k}
is finite,
X_{k}
is a convex polytope and hence compact. Moreover, these polytopes yield a filtration of
\Xi_{h}
in the sense that
X_{k}\subset X_{k+1}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\Xi_{h}=\bigcup_{k=0}^{\infty}X_{k}
. Let
(y_{m})_{m=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence in
\Xi_{h}
converging to some
y\in E_{h}
. We are going to show that there exists some
k^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that
y_{m}\in X_{k^{\prime}}
for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Once this is established, we may conclude that the sequence
(y_{m})_{m=1}^{\infty}
is contained in the compact set
X_{k^{\prime}}
, so that
y\in X_{k^{\prime}}\subset\Xi_{h}
. The proof of this assertion will be reduced to a sequence of claims. Before we formulate them, we need to introduce some notation. Throughout this proof, we will identify
E_{h}
with
\mathbb{R}^{n}
by moving
h
to
0
and mapping
\Phi_{0}^{\vee}
to the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
in the natural order. For every
i\in I
,
\omega_{i}-\langle\omega_{i},h\rangle
corresponds to the
i
-th coordinate functional under this identification. Thus, if we set
\omega:=\sum_{i\in I}\omega_{i}
, then

|\langle\omega,p\rangle-\langle\omega,q\rangle|=\bigg|\sum_{i=1}^{n}(p_{i}-q_{i})\bigg|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}|p_{i}-q_{i}|=||p-q||_{1},\hskip 20.00003ptp,q\in E_{h}\cong\mathbb{R}^{n},

(2.2)

where
||\cdot||_{1}
denotes the
\ell^{1}
-norm on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Since all norms on a finite-dimensional vector space are equivalent, the
\ell^{1}
-topology on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
coincides with the standard Euclidean topology. Next, set
U_{-1}:=\emptyset
and define for every
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}

\displaystyle V_{k}

\displaystyle:=U_{k}\setminus U_{k-1}=\{w\in W:l(w)=k\},

\displaystyle m_{+}(k)

\displaystyle:=\max_{w\in V_{k}}\langle\omega,w(h)\rangle,

\displaystyle m_{-}(k)

\displaystyle:=\min_{w\in V_{k}}\langle\omega,w(h)\rangle,

\displaystyle d(k)

\displaystyle:=m_{+}(k)-m_{-}(k).

Since we assume that
W
is infinite, we have
V_{k}\neq\emptyset
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. We set
c:=\min_{i\in I}\langle\alpha_{i},h\rangle>0
.

Claim 1:
For every
w\in W
and
i\in I
with
l(w)<l(s_{i}w)
, we have
\langle\omega,s_{i}w(h)\rangle\leq\langle\omega,w(h)\rangle-c
.

Proof of Claim 1:
By (
1.3
), we have
\langle\alpha_{i},w(h)\rangle=\langle w^{-1}(\alpha_{i}),h\rangle\geq c
if
l(w)<l(s_{i}w)
, so that

\langle\omega,s_{i}w(h)\rangle=\langle\omega,w(h)\rangle-\langle\alpha_{i},w(h)\rangle\langle\omega,\alpha_{i}^{\vee}\rangle=\langle\omega,w(h)\rangle-\langle\alpha_{i},w(h)\rangle\leq\langle\omega,w(h)\rangle-c.

Claim 2:
For every
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
there exists some
k^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
m_{+}(k^{\prime})<m_{-}(k)
.

Proof of Claim 2:
Let
k^{\prime}\geq k
and
w\in V_{k^{\prime}}
be arbitrary and write
w=w_{1}w_{2}
with
l(w_{1})=k^{\prime}-k
and
l(w_{2})=k
. Iterating the computation in Claim 1 shows that

\langle\omega,w(h)\rangle\leq\langle\omega,w_{2}(h)\rangle-(k^{\prime}-k)c\leq m_{+}(k)-(k^{\prime}-k)c=m_{-}(k)+d(k)-(k^{\prime}-k)c.

Since the right-hand side does not depend on
w
, we conclude that

m_{+}(k^{\prime})\leq m_{-}(k)+d(k)-(k^{\prime}-k)c.

If we choose
k^{\prime}
so large that
(k^{\prime}-k)c>d(k)
, the claim is satisfied.

Claim 3:
For every
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, every proper face
F
of
X_{k}
is either contained in a proper face of
\Xi_{h}
or it is of the form
F=\operatorname{conv}(U\cdot h)
for a subset
U\subset V_{k}
.

Proof of Claim 3:
The statement is trivial if
k=0
since
X_{0}=\{h\}
is a vertex of
\Xi_{h}
. Thus, we may assume that
k\geq 1
. Since
X_{k}=\operatorname{conv}(U_{k}\cdot h)
is a convex polytope, every face is exposed. Hence,
F
is of the form
F=\operatorname{conv}(U\cdot h)
for some proper subset
U\subsetneq U_{k}
. If
U
is contained in a non-trivial standard coset of
W
, i.e.
U\subset wW_{J}
for some
w\in W
and
J\subsetneq I
, then
F
is contained in the proper face
w(F_{J})=\operatorname{conv}(wW_{J}\cdot h)
of
\Xi_{h}
. Otherwise, let
u\in U
be arbitrary, then for every
i\in I
, there exists some
u_{i}\in U
such that
u_{i}\notin uW^{(i)}
. Equivalently,
u^{-1}u_{i}\notin W^{(i)}
and hence
s_{i}\in S(u^{-1}u_{i})
. Now Lemma
2.2
implies that
h-u^{-1}u_{i}(h)
is a sum of simple coroots in which all coefficients are non-negative and the coefficient of
\alpha_{i}^{\vee}
is non-zero. Now consider the point

h^{\prime}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{n}u^{-1}u_{i}(h)=u^{-1}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{n}u_{i}(h)\bigg)\in u^{-1}(F).

Then we have

h-h^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{n}(h-u^{-1}u_{i}(h)),

so that
h-h^{\prime}
is a sum of simple coroots in which all coefficients are positive. By Lemma
2.5
, the open line segment
(h,h^{\prime})
is contained in the relative interior of
\Xi_{h}
. More precisely, if
l(u)<k
, the proof of Lemma
2.5
actually shows that this line runs through the relative interior of
u^{-1}(X_{k})
. Indeed, observe that in that proof we have only used
h
,
h^{\prime}
and the simple reflections to create open neighbourhoods and
s_{i}(h)=u^{-1}us_{i}(h)\in u^{-1}(X_{k})
for every
i\in I
since
l(us_{i})\leq l(u)+1\leq k
. But then the line segment
(u(h),u(h^{\prime}))\subset F
is contained in the relative interior of
X_{k}
, contradicting the assumption that
F
is a proper face of
X_{k}
. Since
u\in U
was arbitrary,
F
can only be a face if
l(u)=k
for all
u\in U
, i.e.
U\subset V_{k}
.

Claim 4:
If
q\in\Xi_{h}\setminus X_{k}
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, then
\langle\omega,q\rangle<m_{+}(k)
.

Proof of Claim 4:
The claim is clear if
k=0
since
X_{0}=\{h\}
and every point
q\in\Xi_{h}\setminus\{h\}
satisfies
\langle\omega,q\rangle<\langle\omega,h\rangle=m_{+}(0)
, so we may assume that
k\geq 1
. Consider the line segment
L:=[h,q]\subset\Xi_{h}
connecting
h
and
q
. Since
h\in X_{0}\subset X_{k}
and
q\notin X_{k}
, there exists a supporting hyperplane
E
for
X_{k}
such that
q
and
h
are not contained in the same closed half-space defined by
E
and
L
intersects the corresponding face
F:=X_{k}\cap E
. If
F
was contained in a face of
\Xi_{h}
, then we could choose
E
to be a supporting hyperplane for
\Xi_{h}
, but then
q\in\Xi_{h}
would imply that
q
and
h
were both contained in the same closed half-space. Thus,
F
is of the form
F=\operatorname{conv}(U\cdot h)
for some
U\subset V_{k}
by Claim 3. As
k\geq 1
and
q\notin F
, this face neither contains
h
nor
q
, so
h
and
q
lie in different open half-spaces defined by
E
and the line
L
intersects
F
in an interior point
x\in(h,q)
. Since
\langle\omega,u(h)\rangle\leq m_{+}(k)
for all
u\in U
and
x
is a convex combination of these vertices, we have
\langle\omega,x\rangle\leq m_{+}(k)
. In addition, since
k\geq 1
, we also have
m_{+}(k)<\langle\omega,h\rangle
. Hence,
\omega
is strictly monotonically decreasing along
L
. As
x
lies strictly between
h
and
q
, this yields that
\langle\omega,q\rangle<\langle\omega,x\rangle\leq m_{+}(k)
.

Claim 5:
Let
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and choose
k^{\prime}>k
such that
\delta:=m_{-}(k)-m_{+}(k^{\prime})>0
. Then
||p-q||_{1}>\delta
for all
p\in X_{k}
and
q\in\Xi_{h}\setminus X_{k^{\prime}}
.

Proof of Claim 5:
On the one hand,
p\in X_{k}
is a convex combination of
W
-translates of
h
of length at most
k
. The value of
\omega
on each of these vertices is at least
m_{-}(k)
since
m_{-}
is monotonically decreasing by Claim 1. This implies
\langle\omega,p\rangle\geq m_{-}(k)
. On the other hand, we have
\langle\omega,q\rangle<m_{+}(k^{\prime})
by Claim 4, so (
2.2
) implies that

||p-q||_{1}\geq|\langle\omega,p\rangle-\langle\omega,q\rangle|>m_{-}(k)-m_{+}(k^{\prime})=\delta.

Conclusion of the proof:
Since a convergent sequence is bounded, we find
R>0
such that
(y_{m})_{m=1}^{\infty}
is contained in a closed ball of radius
R
(w.r.t. the
\ell^{1}
-norm) around
y_{1}
. In addition, since for every
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
y_{m}
is a finite convex combination of
W
-translates of
h
, there exists some
k(m)\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that
y_{m}\in X_{k(m)}
. Set
k:=k(1)
and choose
k^{\prime}>k
such that
\delta:=m_{-}(k)-m_{+}(k^{\prime})\geq R
. Since
||y_{1}-y_{m}||_{1}\leq R
for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
, Claim 5 implies that the sequence
(y_{m})_{m=1}^{\infty}
must be contained in
X_{k^{\prime}}
, as required.
∎

With these topological results at hand, we are now in a position to show that the exposed faces of
\Xi_{h}
that we constructed so far actually make up the entire face poset.

Proposition 2.7
.

If
h\in C
and
F
is a face of
\Xi_{h}
, then
F=w(F_{J})
for some
w\in W
and a unique subset
J\subset I
. In particular, every face of
\Xi_{h}
is exposed.

Proof.

We have already discussed uniqueness of
J
after Corollary
2.4
, so it remains to show existence. If
F=\Xi_{h}
, the claim is satisfied if and only if
J=I
and
w\in W
is arbitrary. Thus, we may assume that
F
is a proper face of
\Xi_{h}
. We will argue by induction on
n=|I|
. If
n=1
, then
\Xi_{h}
is the line segment connecting the two distinct points
h
and
s_{1}(h)
. The only proper faces of
\Xi_{h}
are those two points which have the required description for
J=\emptyset
.

Let now
n>1
be arbitrary and let
x
be a point in the relative interior of
F
. As
F
is a proper face, the point
x
lies on the boundary of
\Xi_{h}
and since
\Xi_{h}
is closed, it is contained in a non-trivial supporting hyperplane
E
of
\Xi_{h}
. Let
G:=\Xi_{h}\cap E
be the corresponding exposed face, then
G
is also proper and
F\subset G
because
G
contains a point from the relative interior of
F
. This exposed face is of the form
G=\operatorname{conv}(W^{\prime}\cdot h)
for some proper subset
W^{\prime}\subset W
and upon replacing
F
by
w^{-1}(F)
and
G
by
w^{-1}(G)
for a suitable
w\in W
, we may assume that
1\in W^{\prime}
and
h\in G
. We will show that there exists some
i\in I
such that
W^{\prime}\subset W^{(i)}
.

Assume for a contradiction that for every
i\in I
, there exists some
w_{i}\in W^{\prime}
with
i\in\operatorname{supp}(w_{i})
. Then it follows from Lemma
2.2
that
h-w_{i}(h)
is a linear combination of simple coroots in which
\alpha_{i}^{\vee}
occurs with a positive coefficient. Since all other coefficients are non-negative, we may form a suitable convex combination of these points to deduce that there exists a point
y\in G
such that
h-y=\sum_{i\in I}c_{i}\alpha_{i}^{\vee}
with
c_{i}>0
for all
i\in I
. Now Lemma
2.5
implies that the line connecting
h
and
y
, which is contained in
G
, runs through the interior of
\Xi_{h}
, a contradiction.

Hence, we must have
W^{\prime}\subset W^{(i)}
for some
i\in I
, which implies that
G
is contained in the facet
F^{(i)}
. The same is then true for
F
, so
F
is a face of
F^{(i)}
. The induction hypothesis now implies that there exists a unique (not necessarily proper) subset
J\subset I\setminus\{i\}
and some
w\in W^{(i)}
such that
F=w(F_{J})=\operatorname{conv}(wW_{J}\cdot h)
. Since we have already shown that every face of this form is exposed, the proof is finished.
∎

The following theorem is now an immediate consequence of Corollary
2.4
and Proposition
2.7
and provides a completely combinatorial description of the face poset of
\Xi_{h}
.

Theorem 2.8
.

If
h\in C
, then the faces of
\Xi_{h}
correspond bijectively to the standard cosets of
(W,S)
. More precisely, for every standard coset
wW_{J}\subset W
with
w\in W
and
J\subset I
, the set
w(F_{J})=\operatorname{conv}(wW_{J}\cdot h)
is a face of
\Xi_{h}
of dimension
|J|
and, conversely, every face is of this form for a unique standard coset of
(W,S)
. In particular, the poset
\mathcal{F}(\Xi_{h})
is isomorphic to
\Sigma^{*}(W,S)
.

Remark 2.9
.

This theorem shows that every vertex of
\Xi_{h}
, i.e. every
W
-translate of
h
, is contained in precisely
2^{n}
faces of
\Xi_{h}
. In particular, every face is only contained in finitely many other faces. As every face is an intersection of sufficiently many facets, this means that every point of
\Xi_{h}
is only contained in finitely many defining hyperplanes, so the hyperplane arrangement

\{w(H^{(i)})\mid w\in W,\,i\in I\},

whose intersection pattern induces the facial structure on
\Xi_{h}
, is
locally finite
. Note that these hyperplanes are not linear. In the language of Coxeter groups, this is reflected in the fact that for every standard coset
wW_{J}
, there exist only finitely many standard cosets of
(W,S)
containing it. In contrast, a face may well contain infinitely many other faces. In fact, for every
J\subset I
, the face poset
\mathcal{F}(F_{J})
is isomorphic to
\Sigma^{\ast}(W_{J},S_{J})
, where
S_{J}=\{s_{j}\mid j\in J\}
, which is finite if and only if the standard subgroup
W_{J}\subset W
is finite. Since the generating set
S
is finite, this is the case if and only if the length function of
W
is bounded on
W_{J}
. By inspecting the proof of Proposition
2.6
, this implies that the face
F_{J}
is bounded (and hence, compact) if and only if
W_{J}
is finite, so the bounded faces of
\Xi_{h}
correspond precisely to the finite parabolic subgroups of
W
. Trivially, vertices and edges contain only finitely many faces, so unbounded faces must be at least two-dimensional. In combinatorial terms, this reflects the fact that there is only one Coxeter group of rank
1
, namely the finite group
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
.

The group
W
is said to be
k-spherical
for some
2\leq k\leq n
if every standard subgroup
W_{J}
with
|J|\leq k
is finite. In this case, every face of dimension at most
k
has only finitely many faces or, equivalently, is bounded. The only infinite Coxeter group of rank
2
is the infinite dihedral group

D_{\infty}=\langle s,t\mid s^{2}=t^{2}=1\rangle,

so
W
is 2-spherical if and only if the order of
s_{i}s_{j}\in W
is finite for all
i\neq j\in I
. By (
1.2
), this is the case if and only if the GCM
\mathbb{A}
satisfies
a_{ij}a_{ji}\leq 3
for all
i\neq j\in I
. If
\mathbb{A}
is of affine type, then
W
is infinite but every proper standard subgroup of
W
is finite. In this case,
W
is
(n-1)
-spherical, so every proper face of
\Xi_{h}
is bounded, but
\Xi_{h}
itself is not.

This behaviour is in a sense “dual” to the cellular structure of the Tits cone. A
cell
of
X
is any set of the form
w(D_{J})\subset X
for some
w\in W
and
J\subset I
, where

D_{J}:=\{h^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{a}:\langle\alpha_{i},h^{\prime}\rangle=0\,\,\forall i\in J,\,\,\langle\alpha_{i},h^{\prime}\rangle>0\,\,\forall i\in I\setminus J\}.

The sets
\{D_{J}\mid J\subset I\}
are precisely the faces of the closed fundamental chamber
\overline{C}
and for
h^{\prime}\in w(D_{J})
, the stabilizer of
h^{\prime}
is the parabolic subgroup
wW_{J}w^{-1}\subset W
. In particular, it depends only on the cell
w(D_{J})
, so the cell complex of
X
is isomorphic to the “usual” Coxeter complex
\Sigma(W,S)
where standard cosets are ordered by reversed inclusion. The Tits cone thus provides a geometric realization of
\Sigma(W,S)
. Every cell of
X
contains only finitely many cells, but may well be contained in infinitely many different ones. Observe that the cellular structure on
X
is induced by the intersection pattern of the hyperplane arrangement

\{w(\operatorname{ker}(\alpha_{i}))\mid w\in W,\,i\in I\}.

In contrast to the situation of
\Xi_{h}
, all these hyperplanes are linear, which implies that this hyperplane arrangement is locally finite if and only if it is finite or, equivalently,
W
is finite.

Another useful result, which is a direct consequence of Theorem
2.8
, is the following. We use standard terminology of Coxeter complexes as described in
[
AB08
, Chapter 3]
.

Corollary 2.10
.

If
h\in C
, the underlying graph of
\Xi_{h}
, i.e. the set of all edges and vertices of
\Xi_{h}
, is isomorphic to the chamber system of the Coxeter complex
\Sigma(W,S)
. In other words, two vertices
w_{1}(h)
and
w_{2}(h)
of
\Xi_{h}
are joined by an edge if and only if the chambers
\{w_{1}\}
and
\{w_{2}\}
of
\Sigma(W,S)
are adjacent, i.e.
w_{2}=w_{1}s_{i}
for some
i\in I
. Under this isomorphism, the graph distance in
\Xi_{h}
corresponds to the chamber distance in
\Sigma(W,S)
and minimal paths between vertices of
\Xi_{h}
are in bijective correspondence with minimal galleries between the corresponding chambers. Equivalently, the underlying graph of
\Xi_{h}
is also isomorphic to the Cayley graph of
(W,S)
.

Finally, recall that a closed convex set can be written as the intersection of all supporting half-spaces. As every proper face of
\Xi_{h}
is contained in a facet,
\Xi_{h}
can also be reconstructed as the intersection of all defining half-spaces. This observation yields the next corollary which will play a crucial role in the proof of Theorem
4.1
.

Corollary 2.11
.

If
h\in C
, then a point
h^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{a}
is contained in
\Xi_{h}
if and only if it satisfies the following (in-)equalities:

\begin{cases}\langle\omega_{i},w(h^{\prime})\rangle\leq\langle\omega_{i},h\rangle&\text{for all $w\in W$ and $i\in I$}\\
\langle\phi_{i},h^{\prime}\rangle=\langle\phi_{i},h\rangle&\text{for all $i\in\{n+1,\ldots,d\}$}\end{cases}

Proof.

Since the set of defining hyperplanes of
\Xi_{h}
is
\{w^{-1}(H^{(i)})\mid w\in W,\,i\in I\}
and
\Xi_{h}
is the intersection of all defining half-spaces, which we view as subsets of
E_{h}
,
h^{\prime}
is contained in
\Xi_{h}
if and only if it lies in
E_{h}
and satisfies the corresponding inequalities
\langle\omega_{i},w(h^{\prime})\rangle\leq\langle\omega_{i},h\rangle
for all
w\in W
and
i\in I
. Moreover, the condition
h^{\prime}\in E_{h}
is equivalent to
h-h^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{a}^{\prime}
and hence to
\langle\phi_{i},h\rangle=\langle\phi_{i},h^{\prime}\rangle
for all
i\in\{n+1,\ldots,d\}
.
∎

It is also not difficult to show that the inequalities in Corollary

directly imply that

lies in the Tits cone

. Once this is established, it turns out that most inequalities are in a certain sense redundant. In fact, if

are implied by those with

. If

is contained in

if and only if its

and satisfies

.

We have now fully determined the face poset of
\Xi_{h}
and found necessary and sufficient conditions that decide whether a point in
\mathfrak{a}
is contained in
\Xi_{h}
or not. Next, we want to study the intersections of
\Xi_{h}
with parallel translates of the defining hyperplanes. As we shall see, these intersections are invariant under subgroups of

of rank

For the rest of this section, we fix an arbitrary
i\in I
and consider parallel translates of the defining hyperplane
H^{(i)}
. Given any point
y\in E_{h}
, we set
y_{i}:=\langle\omega_{i},y\rangle
. For every

, we define

H^{(i)}(t):=\{y\in E_{h}:y_{i}=t\},

so that
H^{(i)}(t)
is parallel to
H^{(i)}=H^{(i)}(h_{i})
. We have already seen that every point

satisfies

with equality if and only if
y\in F^{(i)}
. Thus, the intersection

P^{(i)}(t):=\Xi_{h}\cap H^{(i)}(t)

can only be non-empty if

. The existence of a lower bound depends on whether the index of

in

is finite. This is obviously the case if

is finite. If

is infinite, however, this is only possible in a rather trivial situation.

Theorem 2.12
.

Let
(W,S)
be a Coxeter system, let

be the connected components of the Coxeter diagram of

and set

I_{\infty}:=\bigcup\{I_{k}\mid\text{$1\leq k\leq m,\,W_{I_{k}}$ is infinite}\}.

Then

is the smallest finite-index standard subgroup of
W
. If

is a finite-index standard subgroup of

, then

and
W_{J}\cong W_{I_{\infty}}\times W_{J\setminus I_{\infty}}
. In particular, if

is infinite and irreducible, then

does not contain a non-trivial standard subgroup of finite index.

Proof.

[
Hos02
, Corollary 3.3 and 3.4]

∎

In the formulation of the following lemma, we use the fact that in every Coxeter system
(W,S)
with
|W|<\infty
, there exists a unique element
w_{+}\in W
such that
l(w_{+})
is maximal.

3

3

In the literature on Coxeter groups, it is customary to denote this element by
w_{0}
rather than
w_{+}
. We do not use that notation on purpose since it will sometimes be convenient to be able to list elements of

as

, where the first element should not be confused with

if

is finite.
It is of order two and conjugation by

preserves the generating set

. Hence, it induces an automorphism of the Coxeter diagram of

of

Lemma 2.13
.

(i)

If
[W:W^{(i)}]
is finite, let
w_{+}
be the longest word of
W_{I\setminus I_{\infty}}
and let
j\in I
be the unique index such that

. Then

is non-empty if and only if

.

(ii)

If
[W:W^{(i)}]
is infinite, then
P^{(i)}(t)
is non-empty for all
t\in(-\infty,h_{i}]
.

Proof.

(i)

Since
W=W_{I_{\infty}}\times W_{I\setminus I_{\infty}}
is the direct product of an infinite and a finite Coxeter group, conjugation by the longest word of
W_{I\setminus I_{\infty}}
defines an automorphism
\sigma
of the Coxeter diagram of
(W,S)
which fixes all vertices in
I_{\infty}
. Hence, we have
w_{+}s_{k}w_{+}=s_{\sigma(k)}
for all
k\in I
, where
\sigma(k)=k
for all
k\in I_{\infty}
. This implies that
w_{+}(\alpha_{k}^{\vee})=\pm\alpha_{\sigma(k)}^{\vee}
for all
k\in I
, where the sign is positive if and only if
k\in I_{\infty}
by (
1.3
) because
l(w_{+})<l(w_{+}s_{k})
if and only if

. Thus, the action of
w_{+}
on
\mathfrak{a}^{\ast}
fixes
\omega_{k}
for every
k\in I_{\infty}
and for
k\in I\setminus I_{\infty}
, it maps
\omega_{k}
to
-\omega_{\sigma(k)}
. If
[W:W^{(i)}]
is finite, then
i\notin I_{\infty}
and
w_{+}(\omega_{i})=-\omega_{j}
for
j=\sigma(i)\in I\setminus I_{\infty}
. Since
i\in\operatorname{supp}(w_{+})
, we obtain from Lemma
2.2
that

0<\langle\omega_{i},h-w_{+}(h)\rangle=h_{i}-\langle w_{+}(\omega_{i}),h\rangle=h_{i}+h_{j},

so the interval

is well-defined. If

, we also have

. Proposition

for all

and hence

h_{i}^{\prime}=\langle\omega_{i},h^{\prime}\rangle=\langle w_{+}(\omega_{i}),w_{+}(h^{\prime})\rangle=\langle-\omega_{j},w_{+}(h^{\prime})\rangle\geq-h_{j}.

Thus, in this case
H^{(i)}(t)
can only intersect
\Xi_{h}
if

. On the other hand, we know that
P^{(i)}(h_{i})=F^{(i)}
is a face of
\Xi_{h}
and, using the same reasoning, one verifies that

P^{(i)}(-h_{j})=\operatorname{conv}(W^{(i)}w_{+}\cdot h)=\operatorname{conv}(w_{+}W^{(j)}\cdot h)=w_{+}(F^{(j)})

is also a face of
\Xi_{h}
and thus non-empty. Since

is convex, it follows that
P^{(i)}(t)
is non-empty for all
t\in[-h_{j},h_{i}]
.

(ii)

Consider the orbit of
\omega_{i}
under the

. Since

, it follows easily by induction on the word length (cf. the proof of

. Since

is infinite. In addition, it is contained in

which implies that the orbit contains elements of the form

with

and

unbounded. For every

, we therefore find

such that
t_{0}:=\langle\omega_{i},w(h)\rangle\leq t
. Since

contains

, it is non-empty. By convexity, it follows that

is non-empty for all

Using the notation of the preceding lemma, we define an interval

by

I_{h}:=\begin{cases}[-h_{j},h_{i}]&\text{if $[W:W^{(i)}]<\infty$,}\\

\Xi_{h}=\bigcup_{t\in I_{h}}P^{(i)}(t).

(2.3)

Our next goal is to determine the face poset of
P^{(i)}(t)
for

. By the discussion in Section

Lemma 2.14
.

Every face of
P^{(i)}(t)
is the convex hull of its vertices.

If

is a convex polytope, this is of course a well-known property. For arbitrary closed convex sets, however, this is not necessarily true without further requirements on the set. In our situation, the assertion can be easily reduced to the compact case by a cut-off argument.

Proof.

Let
F
be any face of
P^{(i)}(t)
and choose
F^{\prime}
as the smallest face of
\Xi_{h}
such that
F=F^{\prime}\cap H^{(i)}(t)
. By the preceding discussion, the set
Y
of vertices of

is discrete and not empty and
\operatorname{conv}(Y)\subset F
holds by convexity. To show the converse, we claim that for every
x\in F
, there exists a finite subset
Y^{\prime}\subset Y
such that
x\in\operatorname{conv}(Y^{\prime})
. Set
\omega:=\sum_{i\in I}\omega_{i}\in\mathfrak{a}^{\ast}
, let
x\in F
be arbitrary and consider the closed half-space

E_{+}:=\{y\in E_{h}:\langle\omega,x\rangle\leq\langle\omega,y\rangle\}.

Then
F_{+}:=F\cap E_{+}
is compact since

is closed and each of the linearly independent functionals
\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{n}\in\mathfrak{a}^{\ast}
is bounded on
F_{+}
. Every face of
F_{+}
is exposed, so by
[
Roc70

,

is the convex hull of its vertices. Now every vertex of
F_{+}
is either a vertex of
F
contained in
E_{+}
, or it lies in the interior of a unique edge of
F
. Let
Y_{1}^{\prime}\subset Y
be the set of vertices of
F
contained in
E_{+}
and let
Y_{2}^{\prime}\subset Y
be the set of all endpoints of edges of
F
which start in
E_{+}
and end outside of it. Since
Y
is discrete and
F_{+}
is compact, the set
Y^{\prime}:=Y_{1}^{\prime}\cup Y_{2}^{\prime}
is finite and every vertex of
F_{+}
is either contained in
Y^{\prime}
or it is a convex combination of two points in
Y^{\prime}
. Hence, it follows that
x\in F_{+}\subset\operatorname{conv}(Y^{\prime})\subset\operatorname{conv}(Y)
, as claimed. As

was arbitrary, we conclude that

.
∎

Moreover,
P^{(i)}(t)
is invariant under the action of the rank-

which is the Weyl group of the submatrix

of
\mathbb{A}
with the

-equivalent to a vertex in that set. More precisely, the next proposition asserts that there are only finitely many such vertices and all of them are already contained in the open fundamental chamber

Proposition 2.15
.

For every

, the set

is the convex hull of a finite union of
W^{(i)}
-orbits. More precisely, there exist finitely many vertices

Applying Lemma

to the trivial face

yields that
P^{(i)}(t)
is the convex hull of its vertices. Every such vertex is
W^{(i)}
-equivalent to a vertex in
\overline{C^{(i)}}
, so

is the convex hull of the union of all
W^{(i)}
-orbits of its vertices in
\overline{C^{(i)}}
. We first show that there are only finitely many such vertices. Recall that every vertex is of one of the following two types: It is either a vertex of

contained in

, i.e. equal to

for some

, or it lies in the interior of a unique edge of
\Xi_{h}
.

First, there can only be finitely many vertices of the first type. If
t\neq\langle\omega_{i},w(h)\rangle
for all
w\in W
, there cannot be any such vertices at all, so assume that
t=\langle\omega_{i},w(h)\rangle
for some
w\in W
and let

be such that
w^{-1}(\omega_{i})=\omega_{i}-\alpha
. For any other

, we have

. If

, so that

and

is

-equivalent to

. Otherwise, since

.

. The endpoints of that edge are

-inequivalent edges of
\Xi_{h}
that cross

.

that lie in

. We now show that they are actually contained in

. To this end, it suffices to show that none of these vertices is contained in a wall of

. If
x_{r}
is of the first type, then

for some

. By Corollary

, this edge crosses precisely one wall, so

. But then

and

is constant on

, it follows that

. However,

W^{(i)}
-orbit and the arguments of this section prove that its face poset is isomorphic to
\Sigma^{\ast}(W^{(i)},S^{(i)})
. If

, this is no longer true. Since

acts simply transitively on
W^{(i)}
-chambers, a face in the essential part cannot be stabilized by any non-trivial element of
W^{(i)}
. Conversely, the following result shows that if a face of
P^{(i)}(t)
is not contained in the essential part, it must have a certain degree of symmetry.

Proposition 2.16
.

Every face of
P^{(i)}(t)
is invariant under a parabolic subgroup of
W^{(i)}
. More precisely, for every face
F
of
P^{(i)}(t)
, there exist
1\leq t\leq m
,
r_{1},\ldots,r_{t}\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
,
w\in W^{(i)}
and a standard subgroup
W_{J}\subset W^{(i)}
such that

F=\operatorname{conv}\bigg(\bigcup_{s=1}^{t}wW_{J}\cdot x_{r_{s}}\bigg).

The second statement is indeed a refinement of the first because if
F
has the asserted shape, then
F
is invariant under the parabolic subgroup
wW_{J}w^{-1}\subset W^{(i)}
.

Proof.

Since
F
is the convex hull of its vertices, it suffices to show that the vertices of
F
form a union of orbits of a standard coset of
(W^{(i)},S^{(i)})
. After replacing
F
by
w^{-1}(F)
for a suitable
w\in W^{(i)}
, we may assume that at least one vertex of
F
is contained in
C^{(i)}
. We are going to show that there exists a unique maximal subset
J\subset I\setminus\{i\}
such that
F
is invariant under the standard subgroup
W_{J}\subset W^{(i)}
, so that
w(F)
will be invariant under the parabolic subgroup
wW_{J}w^{-1}
. If all vertices of
F
are already contained in the set
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}\}
, the claim is satisfied only by
J=\emptyset
. Otherwise, there exists an edge
L\subset F
which connects a vertex
x_{r}\in C^{(i)}
to a vertex
y\in w(C^{(i)})
for some
w\in W^{(i)}\setminus\{1\}
. We claim that
w=s_{k}
for some
k\neq i
and
y=s_{k}(x_{r})
.

Let
w=s_{i_{1}}\ldots s_{i_{l}}
be a reduced decomposition of
w
and set
k:=i_{1}\in I\setminus\{i\}
. Then we have
\langle\alpha_{k},x_{r}\rangle>0
as
x_{r}\in C^{(i)}
and
\langle\alpha_{k},y\rangle=\langle w^{-1}(\alpha_{k}),w^{-1}(y)\rangle<0
because
w^{-1}(\alpha_{k})\in\Phi^{(i)}_{-}
by (
1.3
) and
w^{-1}(y)\in C^{(i)}
. Hence,
x_{r}
and
y
lie in open
W^{(i)}
-chambers on different sides of the hyperplane
V_{\alpha_{k}}:=\operatorname{ker}(\alpha_{k})
, so the edge
L
crosses this plane at an interior point. Since the faces of
P^{(i)}(t)
are
W^{(i)}
-invariant,
L^{\prime}:=s_{k}(L)
is also an edge of
P^{(i)}(t)
. As
s_{k}
fixes an interior point of
L
, the two edges
L^{\prime}
and
L
intersect at a point that lies in the relative interior of both of them. This is only possible if
L^{\prime}=L
, which forces
w=s_{k}
and
y=s_{k}(x_{r})
. In particular, it shows that
L
is invariant under the rank-one standard subgroup of
W^{(i)}
generated by
s_{k}
.

Next, we show that the entire face
F
is
s_{k}
-invariant. To this end, let
E\subset\mathfrak{a}
be a supporting hyperplane for
F
and denote by
E^{\prime}:=E-x_{r}\subset\mathfrak{a}
the corresponding parallel translate containing the origin, i.e. the linear subspace corresponding to
E
. Observe that
E
contains the infinite line
L_{0}
through the two distinct points
x_{r}
and
y=s_{k}(x_{r})
which crosses the wall
V_{\alpha_{k}}
, so
E^{\prime}
contains the one-dimensional
s_{k}
-invariant subspace
L_{0}^{\prime}:=L_{0}-x_{r}
which is not contained in
V_{\alpha_{k}}
. Therefore,
E^{\prime}
and
V_{\alpha_{k}}
intersect in a
(d-2)
-dimensional subspace and every point
v\in E^{\prime}
can be uniquely written as
v=v_{1}+v_{2}
with
v_{1}\in L_{0}^{\prime}
and
v_{2}\in E^{\prime}\cap V_{\alpha_{k}}
. This yields that
s_{k}(v)=s_{k}(v_{1})+s_{k}(v_{2})=s_{k}(v_{1})+v_{2}\in E^{\prime}
, so
E^{\prime}
, and hence
F
, is
s_{k}
-invariant. Continuing in this way for all
W^{(i)}
-chambers adjacent to
C^{(i)}
that
F
meets, we conclude that
F
is invariant under a unique maximal standard subgroup
W_{J}
of
W^{(i)}
.

Hence, if
\{x_{r_{1}},\ldots,x_{r_{t}}\}
are the vertices of
F
contained in
C^{(i)}
, then the set
W_{J}\cdot\{x_{r_{1}},\ldots,x_{r_{t}}\}
also consists of vertices of
F
. We claim that these are all the vertices of
F
. Assume that this is not the case, then there exists a vertex
y_{1}
in a chamber
w_{1}(C^{(i)})
for some
w_{1}\in W^{(i)}\setminus W_{J}
. Since the underlying graph of
P^{(i)}(t)
is connected, we may choose
y_{1}
in such a way that it is connected by an edge
L
to a vertex of the form
y_{2}=w_{2}(x_{r_{s}})
, where
s\in\{1,\ldots,t\}
and
w_{2}\in W_{J}
. As
F
is
W_{J}
-invariant,
w_{2}^{-1}(L)
is an edge of
F
connecting
x_{r_{s}}
to a vertex
y^{\prime}:=w_{2}^{-1}(y_{1})\in w_{2}^{-1}w_{1}(C^{(i)})
. Exactly as in the first part of the proof, we conclude that
w_{2}^{-1}w_{1}=s_{l}
for some

, but
s_{l}\notin W_{J}
. This contradicts the maximality of
W_{J}
and shows that the set of vertices of
F
is the union of the
W_{J}
-orbits of its vertices in

. The proof is therefore complete.
∎

In order to construct the set
P^{(i)}(t)
from its vertices, one first has to form the union of a finite number of
W^{(i)}
-orbits and then take the convex hull of the resulting set. However, with the previous proposition at hand, we can now show that the essential part allows us to exchange these operations and instead express

as the union of convex hulls of

-orbits. Observe that if

Proposition 2.17
.

Every point in
P^{(i)}(t)
lies in the convex hull of the
W^{(i)}
-orbit of a point in
P^{(i)}_{\operatorname{ess}}(t)
, i.e.

P^{(i)}(t)=\bigcup_{y\in P^{(i)}_{\operatorname{ess}}(t)}\operatorname{conv}(W^{(i)}\cdot y).

Proof.

Let
x\in P^{(i)}(t)
be arbitrary and let
F
be the smallest face of
P^{(i)}(t)
containing
x
. We prove the claim by induction on the dimension of
F
. Since
P^{(i)}(t)
is
W^{(i)}
-invariant, we may w.l.o.g. assume that
x\in\overline{C^{(i)}}
, which implies that one of the vertices of
F
is contained in
C^{(i)}
. Let
x_{r}
for some
r\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
be one such vertex.

If
\operatorname{dim}(F)=0
, then
x=x_{r}\in P^{(i)}_{\operatorname{ess}}(t)
and there is nothing to show. Assume now that
\operatorname{dim}(F)>0
. If all vertices of
F
are in
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}\}
, then
F\subset P^{(i)}_{\operatorname{ess}}(t)
and we are done. Otherwise, Proposition
2.16
shows that

is invariant under a non-trivial standard subgroup
W_{J}\subset W^{(i)}
. Assume first that
x
is not a global fixed-point of

and choose some

such that
s_{k}(x)\neq x
. Then
\omega_{k}
is not bounded from above on the infinite line

spanned by

and
s_{k}(x)
, so

intersects the boundary of
F
in at least one point
x_{+}
which is contained in a proper face of
F
. By the induction hypothesis, there exists a point
y\in P^{(i)}_{\operatorname{ess}}(t)
such that
x_{+}\in\operatorname{conv}(W^{(i)}\cdot y)
. Since
x
and
s_{k}(x)
are contained in the relative interior of
F
and
x_{+}
in the boundary,
x_{+}
cannot lie between

and
s_{k}(x)
on

. This implies that
x
is a convex combination of
x_{+}
and
s_{k}(x_{+})
and we conclude that
x\in\operatorname{conv}(W^{(i)}\cdot y)
.

If
s_{k}(x)=x
for all
k\in J
, then the line

is not well-defined, but this issue is easily dealt with. By minimality of

,
x
has to lie in the relative interior of
F
. Let
E
be a supporting hyperplane for
F
and consider its parallel translate
E^{\prime}:=E-x
containing the origin. Let
k\in J
be arbitrary, then
s_{k}\in W_{J}
acts on
\mathfrak{a}
as a non-trivial involution, so one of its eigenvalues is
-1
. Moreover, it preserves
E^{\prime}
and acts non-trivially on it. Hence, there exists a non-zero vector

such that
s_{k}(v)=-v
. Choosing

sufficiently small, we have

and

can be written as the non-trivial convex combination

x=\frac{1}{2}(x+v+x-v)=\frac{1}{2}(x^{\prime}+s_{k}(x^{\prime})).

The same argument as above applied to the line spanned by
x^{\prime}
and

yields that
x^{\prime}
, and hence

, lies in the convex hull of

for some

.
∎

Kac-Moody groups

Having only dealt with Kac-Moody algebras and their Weyl groups so far, we now introduce the class of groups that will be relevant to our discussion. Kac-Moody groups can be defined in many different ways and varying levels of generality. A common approach that was introduced by J. Tits, following the scheme-theoretic construction of the classical Chevalley groups, is to define a Kac-Moody group over a field
\mathbb{K}
as the group
\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{K})
of
\mathbb{K}
-points of a group functor
\mathcal{G}:\text{$\mathbb{Z}$-alg}\to\text{Grp}
satisfying a list of axioms (cf.
[
Tit87
, Section 2]
). However, Tits also shows that over fields, this functor is uniquely determined (up to a certain non-degeneracy assumption) and the group
\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{K})
can be given an explicit presentation by generators and relations (cf.
[
Tit87
, Section 3.6 and 3.9, Theorem 1]
). For our purposes, it will suffice to consider Kac-Moody groups defined via this presentation which is called the
constructive Tits functor
in
[
Rém02
, Section 8.3]
. The set of imaginary roots does not occur in this definition of Kac-Moody groups, so we continue to denote the set of real roots by
\Phi
instead of
\Phi^{\operatorname{re}}
. In the first part of this chapter, however, we do not require Assumption
1.2
. For any field
\mathbb{K}
, we write
\mathbb{K}^{\ast}:=\mathbb{K}\setminus\{0\}
for its group of invertible elements.

Let
\mathcal{D}=(I,\mathbb{A},\Lambda,(c_{i})_{i\in I},(h_{i})_{i\in I})
be an arbitrary Kac-Moody root datum of rank
d
, not necessarily free or cofree here. For any field
\mathbb{K}
, we set

is a free abelian group of rank

and

\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
. Let
\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{d}\}
be a
\mathbb{Z}
-basis of
\Lambda^{\vee}
, denote by
\mathfrak{a}
the real Cartan subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}
and by
\mathfrak{h}
the complex Cartan subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{C}}
. There exists a well-defined surjective

\exp:\mathfrak{h}\to T(\mathbb{C}),\hskip 20.00003pt\sum_{i=1}^{d}r_{i}v_{i}\mapsto\prod_{i=1}^{d}(e^{r_{i}})^{v_{i}},\hskip 20.00003ptr_{1},\ldots,r_{d}\in\mathbb{C},

which corresponds to the coordinate-wise exponential map
\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}^{\ast}
under the isomorphisms
\mathfrak{h}\cong\mathbb{C}^{d}
and
T(\mathbb{C})\cong(\mathbb{C}^{\ast})^{d}
. It restricts to an injective map
\exp:\mathfrak{a}\to T(\mathbb{R})
whose image is isomorphic to
(\mathbb{R}_{>0})^{d}
and will be denoted by
A
. It can be viewed as a subgroup of
G(\mathbb{R})
and of
G(\mathbb{C})
. The polar decomposition in
\mathbb{C}
and its trivial restriction to
\mathbb{R}
imply that
T(\mathbb{K})\cong M\times A
, where
M
is isomorphic to
(S^{1})^{d}
if
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{C}
and to
(\mathbb{Z}_{2})^{d}
if
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}
. The extended Weyl group
\tilde{W}\subset G
normalizes
A
and
\exp:\mathfrak{a}\to A
is
W
-equivariant.

Moreover, the compact involution
\theta:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}
possesses an analogue on the group level. More precisely, there exists a unique involutive automorphism
\Theta:G\to G
which satisfies
\Theta(x_{\alpha}(r))=x_{-\alpha}(\overline{r})
for all
\alpha\in\Phi
and
r\in\mathbb{K}
and
\Theta(r^{v_{i}})=\overline{r}^{-v_{i}}
for all

and

. Here, the bars denote complex conjugation which can be omitted if
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}
. It is called the
compact involution
of
G
. Its fixed-point set is denoted by
K
and called the
compact form
of
G
. By definition of the extended Weyl group,
\tilde{W}
is contained in
K
.

Using the fact that all panels in the twin building associated to
G
(cf. Remark
3.1
) are isomorphic to the projective line over
\mathbb{K}
, it is not difficult to show that the action of
K
on this building is chamber-transitive and hence
G=KB_{+}
. Moreover, we have
B_{+}=T\ltimes U_{+}
and one easily verifies that
K\cap U_{+}=\{1\}
and
K\cap T=M
. Since
T\cong M\times A
, this shows that
G
has an
Iwasawa decomposition

G=KAU_{+}
in the sense that

the multiplication map
K\times A\times U_{+}\to G
is bijective.

(3.4)

Such a decomposition for certain types of Kac-Moody groups has been established, for example, in
[
KP85
, Proposition 5.1]
or
[
FHHK20
, Theorem 3.31]
. The argument that we have indicated is inspired by
[
DMGH09
, Section 3]
. Note that the group
A
is the same in the real and complex case, only
K
and
U_{+}
depend on the field. The corresponding projection onto the
A
-component will be denoted by

\Pi:G\to A,\hskip 20.00003ptg=kau\mapsto a.

We study a particular property of this map in our main theorem. A key ingredient of the proof will be the following facts about highest-weight representations of
G
. As discussed at the end of Chapter
1
, if
\lambda\in\mathfrak{a}^{\ast}\subset\mathfrak{h}^{\ast}
is a dominant integral weight, there exists a contravariant symmetric bilinear or Hermitian form
H
on the irreducible real or complex highest-weight module
L(\lambda)
. Using that
G
is generated by the torus
T
and all root groups
U_{\alpha}
with
\alpha\in\Phi
, it is straightforward to deduce from (
3.2
) and (
1.6
) that the corresponding representation of
G
on
L(\lambda)
satisfies

H(g\cdot v,w)=H(v,\Theta(g^{-1})\cdot w)\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for all $g\in G$ and $v,w\in L(\lambda)$.}

(3.5)

Therefore,
H
is
K
-invariant. If the GCM
\mathbb{A}
is symmetrizable, then
H
is positive definite, so that
K
acts on
L(\lambda)
by unitary operators. Moreover,
A
acts diagonally on
L(\lambda)
with positive eigenvalues. In fact, if
a=\exp(h)
with
h=\sum_{i=1}^{d}r_{i}v_{i}\in\mathfrak{a}
, then a simple computation using (
3.2
) shows that
a(\mu)=e^{\langle\mu,h\rangle}
for every weight
\mu
of
L(\lambda)
, which is positive since
\langle\mu,h\rangle\in\mathbb{R}
. The action of
U_{+}
is determined by (
3.3
). In particular, if Assumption
1.2
is satisfied, this discussion applies to the functionals in the dual basis
\mathcal{B}^{\ast}=\{\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{n},\phi_{n+1},\ldots,\phi_{d}\}\subset\mathfrak{a}^{\ast}
of Proposition
1.3
.

4
The convexity theorem

We now come to our main theorem. Throughout this chapter, we suppose that Assumption
1.2
is in effect. Let
G
be the split Kac-Moody group over
\mathbb{K}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}\}
associated to a free and cofree Kac-Moody root datum
\mathcal{D}
of rank
d\geq 2n-\operatorname{rank}(\mathbb{A})
and denote by
\mathfrak{a}
the Cartan subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}
with basis
\mathcal{B}=\{\alpha_{1}^{\vee},\ldots,\alpha_{n}^{\vee},h_{n+1},\ldots,h_{d}\}
. Let
G=KAU_{+}
be an Iwasawa decomposition of
G
and define

\Pi:G\to A,\hskip 20.00003ptg=kau\mapsto a

as the corresponding projection onto the
A
-component. It is clear that
\Pi(kg)=\Pi(g)
for all
k\in K
and
g\in G
, but there is no obvious way how to compute
\Pi(gk)
. The following theorem will answer this question in the special case that
g=a\in A
is contained in the exponential of an open chamber of the Tits cone
X\subset\mathfrak{a}
. Recall that the exponential map restricts to a
W
-equivariant isomorphism
\mathfrak{a}\to A
whose inverse will be denoted by
\log:A\to\mathfrak{a}
.

Theorem 4.1
.

If
a\in A
is contained in the exponential image of an open chamber of the Tits cone
X\subset\mathfrak{a}
, then

\{\Pi(ak)\mid k\in K\}=\exp(\operatorname{conv}(W\cdot\log(a))).

In order to prove Theorem
4.1
, let us fix some notation. Assume that
a\in A
is fixed and set
h:=\log(a)\in\mathfrak{a}
. By assumption,
h
is contained in an open chamber of
X
. We write
\Xi_{h}:=\operatorname{conv}(W\cdot h)
as in Chapter
2
and define

\Pi_{a}:=\{\Pi(ak)\mid k\in K\}=\Pi(aK).

Thus, we need to show that
\Pi_{a}=\exp(\Xi_{h})
. The proof will consist of two steps: First, we show that
\Pi_{a}\subset\exp(\Xi_{h})
by using tools from the representation theory of
G
. Afterwards, we prove that
\exp(\Xi_{h})\subset\Pi_{a}
by induction on the size
n
of the GCM
\mathbb{A}
. Note that both sets do not depend on the
W
-translate of
a
(resp.
h
). This is obvious for
\Xi_{h}
and for
\Pi_{a}
, we may argue as follows: If
w\in W
is arbitrary, then we may choose a representative
\tilde{w}\in\tilde{W}\subset K
such that conjugation by
\tilde{w}
induces the action of
w
on
A
and we have

\Pi_{w(a)}=\Pi(w(a)K)=\Pi(\tilde{w}a\tilde{w}^{-1}K)=\Pi(a\tilde{w}^{-1}K)=\Pi(aK)=\Pi_{a}.

Thus, we may w.l.o.g. assume that
h\in C
and
a\in\exp(C)
.

4.1
Proof of the main theorem

4.1.1
Step 1: Highest-weight representations

We begin by establishing that
\Pi_{a}\subset\exp(\Xi_{h})
. Let
k\in K
be arbitrary and write
ak\in G
according to its Iwasawa decomposition as

ak=k^{\prime}a^{\prime}u^{\prime}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{with $k^{\prime}\in K,\,a^{\prime}\in A,\,u^{\prime}\in U_{+}$.}

In order to prove that
h^{\prime}:=\log(a^{\prime})
is contained in
\Xi_{h}
, we show that
h^{\prime}
satisfies the inequalities

\begin{cases}\langle\omega_{i},w(h^{\prime})\rangle\leq\langle\omega_{i},h\rangle&\text{for all $w\in W$ and $i\in I$}\\
\langle\phi_{i},h^{\prime}\rangle=\langle\phi_{i},h\rangle&\text{for all $i\in\{n+1,\ldots,d\}$}\end{cases}

of Corollary
2.11
by studying the action of

1.3
and the discussion in Chapter

, the dual basis

of

consists of dominant integral weights and the group

acts on the corresponding real or complex highest-weight modules

. Each of these modules carries a positive definite, contravariant, symmetric bilinear or Hermitian form
H
such that weight spaces belonging to different weights are orthogonal and by (

), the action of the subgroup
K\subset G
is unitary. The subgroup
A\subset G
acts diagonally on these modules with positive real eigenvalues and the action of
U_{+}
is determined by (

is trivial. We write

and

and denote the associated representations of the Kac-Moody algebra

by

.

First, we fix an arbitrary

and show that
\langle\phi_{i},h\rangle=\langle\phi_{i},h^{\prime}\rangle
. Since

is one-dimensional and the

-action is unitary, we have

and thus

\hat{\rho}_{i}(ak)v=\pm\hat{\rho}_{i}(a)v=\pm a(\phi_{i})v

for any vector

. Moreover,

acts trivially on

which implies that

\hat{\rho}_{i}(k^{\prime}a^{\prime}u^{\prime})v=\hat{\rho}_{i}(k^{\prime}a^{\prime})v=a^{\prime}(\phi_{i})\hat{\rho}_{i}(k^{\prime})v=\pm a^{\prime}(\phi_{i})v.

Since

and

are both positive real numbers, so if we choose

, then equality is only possible if

or, equivalently,

. As the exponents are real numbers, this is equivalent to
\langle\phi_{i},h\rangle=\langle\phi_{i},h^{\prime}\rangle
.

Thus, it only remains to show that if
w\in W
and
i\in I
are arbitrary, then
\langle\omega_{i},w(h^{\prime})\rangle\leq\langle\omega_{i},h\rangle
. Since the set of weights of

is
W
-invariant,
\mu:=w^{-1}(\omega_{i})
is a weight of
L(\omega_{i})
and we may choose a vector

with
||v||:=H(v,v)=1
. As we assume
\mathcal{D}
to be free, every weight of

is of the form
\omega_{i}-\alpha
for some
\alpha\in Q_{+}
. The eigenvalues of the diagonal map
\hat{\rho}_{i}(a)
are precisely the exponentials of the eigenvalues of
\rho_{i}(h)
. Since

, we have
\langle\alpha,h\rangle>0
for all
\alpha\in Q_{+}
, so the largest eigenvalue is
a(\omega_{i})=e^{\langle\omega_{i},h\rangle}
. Moreover,

is a product of elements in positive root groups, so (
3.3
) implies that for every
\nu\in\operatorname{wt}(L(\omega_{i}))
with
\mu<\nu\leq\omega_{i}
, there exists a suitable weight-
\nu

such that

\hat{\rho}_{i}(u^{\prime})v=v+\sum_{\mu<\nu\leq\omega_{i}}v_{\nu}.

On the one hand, this allows us to make the following computation:

\displaystyle||\hat{\rho}_{i}(ak)v||

\displaystyle=||\hat{\rho}_{i}(k^{\prime}a^{\prime}u^{\prime})v||=\bigg|\bigg|\hat{\rho}_{i}(k^{\prime}a^{\prime})\bigg(v+\sum_{\mu<\nu\leq\omega_{i}}v_{\nu}\bigg)\bigg|\bigg|

(4.1)

\displaystyle=\bigg|\bigg|\hat{\rho}_{i}(k^{\prime})\bigg(a^{\prime}(\mu)v+\sum_{\mu<\nu\leq\omega_{i}}a^{\prime}(\nu)v_{\nu}\bigg)\bigg|\bigg|

\displaystyle=\bigg|\bigg|a^{\prime}(\mu)v+\sum_{\mu<\nu\leq\omega_{i}}a^{\prime}(\nu)v_{\nu}\bigg|\bigg|

\displaystyle\geq||a^{\prime}(\mu)v||=a^{\prime}(\mu)=a^{\prime}(w^{-1}(\omega_{i}))=w(a^{\prime})(\omega_{i})

To obtain the inequality (

), we have used that the

and that

. On the other hand, the vector

is contained in the image of the unit sphere under the diagonal map

. More precisely, since

has a basis consisting of weight vectors and

is a finite linear combination of basis vectors, there exists some

such that

\hat{\rho}_{i}(k)v\in\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in Q_{+},\\
\operatorname{ht}(\alpha)\leq m\end{subarray}}L(\omega_{i})_{\omega_{i}-\alpha}=:V_{0},

which is a finite-dimensional vector space, and

is contained in the unit sphere of

. Since
\hat{\rho}_{i}(a)
acts diagonally on

, it preserves

and the restriction of

is represented by a finite diagonal matrix whose diagonal entries are the values

, where

runs over all elements of

such that

and
\omega_{i}-\alpha\in\operatorname{wt}(L(\omega_{i}))
. Hence,
\hat{\rho}_{i}(ak)v
is contained in the image of the unit sphere of
V_{0}
under the diagonal map
\hat{\rho}_{i}(a)|_{V_{0}}
. This set is an ellipsoid whose half-axes have lengths given by the absolute values of the eigenvalues of
\hat{\rho}_{i}(a)|_{V_{0}}
. Since these are all positive, the absolute values can be omitted. In particular, the longest half-axis is the maximal eigenvalue

and we conclude that

w(a^{\prime})(\omega_{i})\leq||\hat{\rho}_{i}(ak)v||\leq a(\omega_{i}).

This is equivalent to

and hence to

, then
G\cong G_{1}\rtimes T_{2}
, where the conjugation action of
T_{2}
is trivial on
T_{1}
and satisfies
tx_{\pm\alpha_{1}}(r)t^{-1}=x_{\pm\alpha_{1}}(t(\pm\alpha_{1})r)
for all
t\in T_{2}
and
r\in\mathbb{K}
. In particular, if
t(\alpha_{1})=1
, then
t
commutes with
G_{1}
and thus lies in the center of
G
. The compact involution of
G
preserves
G_{1}
and under the isomorphism
\varphi
, it corresponds to the usual compact involution
g\mapsto(\overline{g}^{-1})^{T}
of
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{K})
. Hence,
G_{1}
inherits an Iwasawa decomposition from
G
by intersection, i.e.
G_{1}=K_{1}A_{1}U_{1}
, where
K_{1}=K\cap G_{1}
is isomorphic to
\operatorname{SO}(2)
if
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}
and to
\operatorname{SU}(2)
if
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{C}
,
A_{1}=A\cap G_{1}=\{r^{\alpha_{1}^{\vee}}\mid r\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}\}
and
U_{1}=U_{+}\cap G_{1}=U_{\alpha_{1}}
, and these three groups are simply the images under
\varphi
of the corresponding subgroups in the usual Iwasawa decomposition of
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{K})
described above.

In order to deduce the statement
\exp(\Xi_{h})\subset\Pi_{a}
for
a\in A\subset G
from that of
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{K})
, we claim that there exist
a_{1},a_{2}\in A
such that
a=a_{1}a_{2}
with
a_{1}\in A_{1}
and
a_{2}
in the center of
G
. Indeed, the Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{a}
is a direct sum
\mathfrak{a}=\mathfrak{a}_{1}\oplus\mathfrak{a}_{2}
, where
\mathfrak{a}_{1}=\mathbb{R}\alpha_{1}^{\vee}
and
\mathfrak{a}_{2}
is an arbitrary complement, so
h=h_{1}^{\prime}+h_{2}^{\prime}
with
h_{1}^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{a}_{1}
and
h_{2}^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{a}_{2}
. Set
r:=\alpha_{1}(h_{2}^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}
and rewrite
h
as
h=h_{1}+h_{2}
, where
h_{1}:=h_{1}^{\prime}+\frac{r}{2}\alpha_{1}^{\vee}\in\mathfrak{a}_{1}
and
h_{2}:=h_{2}^{\prime}-\frac{r}{2}\alpha_{1}^{\vee}
, so that
\alpha_{1}(h_{2})=0
. The exponential
a_{2}:=\exp(h_{2})\in A
then satisfies
a_{2}(\alpha_{1})=1
, which implies that
a_{2}
is central in
G
. In addition, we have
a_{1}:=\exp(h_{1})\in A_{1}
and
a=\exp(h)=\exp(h_{1}+h_{2})=a_{1}a_{2}
, as claimed. If we choose such a decomposition for our fixed element
a\in A
, then we have
\Pi(ak)=\Pi(a_{1}k)a_{2}
for every
k\in K
. Since we have already shown the result for
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{K})
and
\varphi
is compatible with the Iwasawa decompositions in
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{K})
and
G_{1}
, it follows that

\displaystyle\Pi_{a}\supset\Pi(aK_{1})

\displaystyle=\Pi(a_{1}K_{1})a_{2}\supset\exp(\operatorname{conv}(W\cdot h_{1}))a_{2}=\exp(\operatorname{conv}(W\cdot h_{1})+h_{2})

\displaystyle=\exp(\operatorname{conv}(W\cdot(h_{1}+h_{2})))=\exp(\operatorname{conv}(W\cdot h))=\exp(\Xi_{h}).

Here, the first equality in the second line is justified by the fact that
W\cong\mathbb{Z}_{2}
fixes
h_{2}
since
\alpha_{1}(h_{2})=0
. Thus, the proof of the case
n=1
is finished.

Assume from now on that
n>1
is arbitrary. To prepare for the induction step, we need to address a technical issue first. Recall from (
3.1
) and the discussion following it that for any subset
J\subset I
, the parabolic subgroup
P_{J}\subset G
has a Levi decomposition
P_{J}\cong G_{J}\ltimes U_{+}^{\perp}(J)
, where
G_{J}
is a Kac-Moody group associated to a free and cofree root datum
\mathcal{D}(J)
of rank
d
with GCM
\mathbb{A}_{J}
. Hence,
G_{J}
has an Iwasawa decomposition
G_{J}=K_{J}AU_{+}(J)
and a corresponding projection
\Pi_{J}:G_{J}\to A
, where
K_{J}
is the fixed-point set of the compact involution
\Theta_{J}:G_{J}\to G_{J}
and
U_{+}(J)
is the group generated by all
U_{\alpha}
with
\alpha\in\Phi_{+}(J)
. Observe that the group
A
has not changed since the root data
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{D}(J)
have the same free
\mathbb{Z}
-module
\Lambda
. By definition,
\Theta_{J}
is simply the restriction of the compact involution
\Theta:G\to G
to
G_{J}
, so that
K_{J}\subset K
and we also have
U_{+}(J)\subset U_{+}
. Hence, if we write an element
g_{0}\in G_{J}
according to its Iwasawa decomposition in
G_{J}
as
g_{0}=k_{0}a_{0}u_{0}
, then
k_{0}\in K
,
a_{0}\in A
and
u_{0}\in U_{+}
, so uniqueness implies that this is also the Iwasawa decomposition of
g_{0}
in the ambient group
G
and
\Pi_{J}=\Pi|_{G_{J}}
. Conversely, it follows that
G_{J}
contains the Iwasawa components of all its elements. The Weyl group of
\mathbb{A}_{J}
is the standard subgroup
W_{J}\subset W
and we may define an open fundamental chamber
C_{J}
and a Tits cone
X_{J}
for
W_{J}
in
\mathfrak{a}
by

C_{J}:=\{h^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{a}:\langle\alpha_{j},h^{\prime}\rangle>0\,\,\forall j\in J\}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003ptX_{J}:=\bigcup_{w\in W_{J}}w(\overline{C_{J}}).

If
J\subsetneq I
, the induction hypothesis applied to
G_{J}
yields that for every
a_{0}=\exp(h_{0})\in A
, where
h_{0}
is contained in an open chamber of
X_{J}\subset\mathfrak{a}
, we have

\Pi(a_{0}K_{J})=\Pi_{J}(a_{0}K_{J})\supset\exp(\operatorname{conv}(W_{J}\cdot h_{0})).

(4.3)

In case that
J=I\setminus\{i\}
for some
i\in I
, we set
\mathbb{A}^{(i)}:=\mathbb{A}_{I\setminus\{i\}}
,
C^{(i)}:=C_{I\setminus\{i\}}
and
X^{(i)}:=X_{I\setminus\{i\}}
and write the Iwasawa decomposition of
G^{(i)}:=G_{I\setminus\{i\}}
as
G^{(i)}=K^{(i)}AU_{+}^{(i)}
.

More generally, let
w\in W
be arbitrary and choose
\tilde{w}\in\tilde{W}\subset K
such that conjugation by
\tilde{w}
induces the action of
w
on
A
and
\Phi
. The subgroup
G_{w(J)}:=\tilde{w}G_{J}\tilde{w}^{-1}\subset G
is isomorphic to
G_{J}
and inherits an Iwasawa decomposition
G_{w(J)}=K_{w(J)}AU_{+}(w(J))
, where
K_{w(J)}=\tilde{w}K_{J}\tilde{w}^{-1}
and
U_{+}(w(J))=\tilde{w}U_{+}(J)\tilde{w}^{-1}
. Here, we have
K_{w(J)}\subset K
and
U_{+}(w(J))
is the group generated by all
U_{w(\alpha)}
with
\alpha\in\Phi_{+}(J)
, but it is not necessarily true that
U_{+}(w(J))\subset U_{+}
since some root
w(\alpha)
might be negative. However, the definition of
G_{w(J)}
is independent of the left coset
wW_{J}\subset W
, so we may assume that
w
is the unique element of minimal length in
wW_{J}
. Then
l(w)<l(ws_{j})
for all
j\in J
, which implies that
w(\alpha_{j})\in\Phi_{+}
for all
j\in J
by (
1.3
) and hence
w(\alpha)\in\Phi_{+}
for all
\alpha\in\Phi_{+}(J)
. With this choice, we have
U_{+}(w(J))\subset U_{+}
, so that the Iwasawa decomposition of
G_{w(J)}
is simply the restriction of that of
G
. In particular,
G_{w(J)}
contains the Iwasawa components of all its elements and the projection
\Pi_{w(J)}:G_{w(J)}\to A
satisfies
\Pi_{w(J)}=\Pi|_{G_{w(J)}}
and
\Pi_{w(J)}(\tilde{w}g_{0}\tilde{w}^{-1})=\tilde{w}\Pi_{J}(g_{0})\tilde{w}^{-1}
for all
g_{0}\in G_{J}
. If
J\subsetneq I
, we conclude from (
4.3
) that for every
a_{0}=\exp(h_{0})\in A
such that
h_{0}
lies in an open chamber of
X_{J}
, we have

\displaystyle\Pi(a_{0}K_{w(J)})

\displaystyle=\Pi_{w(J)}(a_{0}K_{w(J)})=\Pi_{w(J)}(a_{0}\tilde{w}K_{J}\tilde{w}^{-1})=\Pi_{w(J)}(\tilde{w}w^{-1}(a_{0})K_{J}\tilde{w}^{-1})

(4.4)

\displaystyle=\tilde{w}\Pi_{J}(w^{-1}(a_{0})K_{J})\tilde{w}^{-1}\supset\tilde{w}\exp(\operatorname{conv}(W_{J}\cdot w^{-1}(h_{0})))\tilde{w}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\exp(w(\operatorname{conv}(W_{J}\cdot w^{-1}(h_{0}))))=\exp(\operatorname{conv}(wW_{J}w^{-1}\cdot h_{0})).

We now continue with the proof of
\exp(\Xi_{h})\subset\Pi_{a}
. Our strategy is to “sweep out”
\Xi_{h}
by parallel translates of hyperplanes to which we may apply the induction hypothesis. We proceed as in Section
2.2
. Let
i\in I
be arbitrary and consider the hyperplanes
H^{(i)}(t):=\{y\in E_{h}:\langle\omega_{i},y\rangle=t\}
for
t\in\mathbb{R}
. We have seen in (
2.3
) that
\Xi_{h}=\bigcup_{t\in I_{h}}P^{(i)}(t)
, where
P^{(i)}(t)=\Xi_{h}\cap H^{(i)}(t)
and
I_{h}\subset\mathbb{R}
is the interval for which this intersection is not empty. Thus, it suffices to show that
\exp(P^{(i)}(t))\subset\Pi_{a}
for every
t\in I_{h}
.

Fix an arbitrary
t\in I_{h}
. First, we are going to show that for every face
F^{\prime}\subset P^{(i)}_{\operatorname{ess}}(t)
, we have
\exp(F^{\prime})\subset\Pi_{a}
. Observe that if
F^{\prime}
is contained in the essential part, it has to be a proper face of
P^{(i)}(t)
, which implies
\operatorname{dim}(F^{\prime})<\operatorname{dim}(P^{(i)}(t))=n-1
. By (
2.1
), there exists a unique face
F
of
\Xi_{h}
such that
F^{\prime}=F\cap H^{(i)}(t)
and it satisfies
\operatorname{dim}(F)\leq\operatorname{dim}(F^{\prime})+1<n
, so
F
is a proper face of
\Xi_{h}
. By Theorem
2.8
, there is a unique proper subset
J\subsetneq I
and some
w\in W
such that

F=w(F_{J})=\operatorname{conv}(wW_{J}\cdot h)=\operatorname{conv}(wW_{J}w^{-1}\cdot w(h)).

Applying (
4.4
) to the subgroup
G_{w(J)}\subset G
and the point
a_{0}=w(a)
yields that

\exp(F^{\prime})\subset\exp(F)=\exp(\operatorname{conv}(wW_{J}w^{-1}\cdot w(h)))\subset\Pi(w(a)K_{w(J)})=\Pi(aK_{J}\tilde{w}^{-1})\subset\Pi_{a},

where
\tilde{w}\in\tilde{W}\subset K
is a representative of
w
in the extended Weyl group. Since the face
F^{\prime}
was arbitrary, we have shown that
\exp(P^{(i)}_{\operatorname{ess}}(t))\subset\Pi_{a}
. For the next step, however, the more precise statement
\exp(F^{\prime})\subset\Pi(w(a)K_{w(J)})
will be important.

Let now
a^{\prime\prime}\in\exp(P^{(i)}(t))
be arbitrary and set
h^{\prime\prime}:=\log(a^{\prime\prime})\in P^{(i)}(t)
. By Proposition
2.17
, there exists a point
h^{\prime}\in P^{(i)}_{\operatorname{ess}}(t)
such that
h^{\prime\prime}\in\operatorname{conv}(W^{(i)}\cdot h^{\prime})
. Let
F^{\prime}\subset P^{(i)}_{\operatorname{ess}}(t)
be a face of the essential part containing
h^{\prime}
and set
a^{\prime}:=\exp(h^{\prime})
. By what we have just discussed, there exist
w\in W
and a proper subset
J\subsetneq I
such that
F^{\prime}\subset F=w(F_{J})
and
a^{\prime}\in\Pi(w(a)K_{w(J)})
. Hence, we find
k_{1},k^{\prime}\in K_{w(J)}
and
u^{\prime}\in U_{+}(w(J))
such that

w(a)k_{1}=k^{\prime}a^{\prime}u^{\prime}.

(4.5)

Now let
P^{(i)}:=P_{I\setminus\{i\}}
be the maximal parabolic subgroup of type
I\setminus\{i\}
with Levi decomposition
P^{(i)}\cong G^{(i)}\ltimes U_{+}^{\perp}(I\setminus\{i\}),
where the Levi factor
G^{(i)}
is generated by
T
and all root groups
U_{\alpha}
with
\alpha\in\Phi^{(i)}:=\Phi(I\setminus\{i\})
and the unipotent radical
U_{+}^{\perp}(I\setminus\{i\})
is generated by all
U_{\alpha}
with
\alpha\in\Phi_{+}\setminus\Phi^{(i)}
. As
h^{\prime}\in P^{(i)}_{\operatorname{ess}}(t)\subset C^{(i)}
is contained in an open chamber of
X^{(i)}
, we may apply (
4.4
) to the subgroup
G^{(i)}\subset G
and the point
a^{\prime}\in A
to conclude that
\exp(\operatorname{conv}(W^{(i)}\cdot h^{\prime}))\subset\Pi(a^{\prime}K^{(i)})
. Thus, we find
k_{2},k^{\prime\prime}\in K^{(i)}
and
u^{\prime\prime}\in U_{+}^{(i)}
such that

a^{\prime}k_{2}=k^{\prime\prime}a^{\prime\prime}u^{\prime\prime}.

(4.6)

Combining (
4.5
) and (
4.6
) leads to

w(a)k_{1}k_{2}=k^{\prime}a^{\prime}u^{\prime}k_{2}=k^{\prime}a^{\prime}k_{2}(k_{2}^{-1}u^{\prime}k_{2})=k^{\prime}k^{\prime\prime}a^{\prime\prime}u^{\prime\prime}(k_{2}^{-1}u^{\prime}k_{2}).

(4.7)

We claim that
k_{2}^{-1}u^{\prime}k_{2}\in U_{+}
. In general, it is rarely the case that a
K
-conjugate of some element in
U_{+}
also lies in
U_{+}
. In our situation, this is a consequence of our construction.

Since
u^{\prime}\in U_{+}(w(J))
, we can write it as a product
u^{\prime}=u_{1}\ldots u_{s}
such that for every
r\in\{1,\ldots,s\}
,
u_{r}
is an element of a root group
U_{\beta_{r}}
, where
\beta_{r}\in\Phi_{+}
and
\gamma_{r}:=w^{-1}(\beta_{r})\in\Phi(J)
. We claim that
\beta_{r}\in\Phi_{+}\setminus\Phi^{(i)}
for every
r\in\{1,\ldots,s\}
. Assume for a contradiction that
\beta_{r}\in\Phi^{(i)}
for some
r\in\{1,\ldots,s\}
, then the reflection
s_{\beta_{r}}=ws_{\gamma_{r}}w^{-1}
would be an element of
W^{(i)}
. In addition,
s_{\gamma_{r}}\in W_{J}
stabilizes
F_{J}=\operatorname{conv}(W_{J}\cdot h)
, so
s_{\beta_{r}}
stabilizes
w(F_{J})=\operatorname{conv}(wW_{J}w^{-1}\cdot w(h))=F
. Hence,
s_{\beta_{r}}
stabilizes
F\cap H^{(i)}(t)=F^{\prime}
. This is a contradiction because
F^{\prime}
is contained in the essential part of
P^{(i)}(t)
, which is a subset of the open fundamental chamber
C^{(i)}
of
X^{(i)}
, and is therefore not invariant under any non-trivial element of
W^{(i)}
. It follows that
\beta_{r}\in\Phi_{+}\setminus\Phi^{(i)}
, as claimed. This implies that for every
r\in\{1,\ldots,s\}
,
u_{r}
is contained in the unipotent radical of the maximal parabolic subgroup
P^{(i)}\subset G
. Hence,
u^{\prime}
is also contained in the unipotent radical, while
k_{2}\in K^{(i)}\subset G^{(i)}
is contained in the Levi factor of
P^{(i)}
. Thus,
k_{2}^{-1}u^{\prime}k_{2}
is also contained in the unipotent radical of
P^{(i)}
and hence in
U_{+}
. This shows that the expression on the right-hand side of (
4.7
) is indeed the Iwasawa decomposition of
w(a)k_{1}k_{2}
in
G
and we obtain that
a^{\prime\prime}=\Pi(w(a)k_{1}k_{2})\subset\Pi(w(a)K)=\Pi_{a}
. Since
a^{\prime\prime}\in\exp(P^{(i)}(t))
was arbitrary, we conclude that
\exp(P^{(i)}(t))\subset\Pi_{a}
for all
t\in I_{h}
, which finishes the proof of Theorem
4.1
.

4.2
A linear analogue

Theorem
4.1
is “non-linear” in the sense that it deals with the Iwasawa decomposition on the group level, but using very similar methods, it is possible to prove a ”linear” analogue on the level of Kac-Moody algebras. A variant of this result has already been established by V.G. Kac and D.H. Peterson in
[
KP84
, Theorem 2 b)]
. Their proof relies on transferring concepts from symplectic geometry, in particular the
moment map
, to highest-weight modules of Kac-Moody algebras. Our approach allows to show the following result.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a free and cofree Kac-Moody root datum associated to a symmetrizable GCM
\mathbb{A}
and let
\mathfrak{g}
be the real or complex Kac-Moody algebra of type
\mathcal{D}
. The same arguments as in the well-known finite-dimensional case show that
\mathfrak{g}
has an Iwasawa decomposition
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{k}\oplus\mathfrak{a}\oplus\mathfrak{u}_{+}
, where
\mathfrak{k}
is the fixed-point set of the compact involution of
\mathfrak{g}
. We denote by
\pi:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{a}
the corresponding linear projection. The group
G
acts on
\mathfrak{g}
via the adjoint representation
\operatorname{Ad}:G\to\operatorname{GL}(\mathfrak{g})
and the following “linear” convexity theorem holds.

Theorem 4.3
.

If
h\in\mathfrak{a}
is contained in an open chamber of the Tits cone
X\subset\mathfrak{a}
, then

\{\pi(\operatorname{Ad}(k)h)\mid k\in K\}=\operatorname{conv}(W\cdot h).

The result in
[
KP84
, Theorem 2 b)]
is less general in the sense that it only considers the “classical” Kac-Moody algebra
\mathfrak{g}(\mathbb{A})
and is therefore restricted to one specific root datum, but in contrast to Theorem
4.3
, it only requires
h
to be contained in the Tits cone
X\subset\mathfrak{a}
rather than an open chamber. Although Theorem
4.1
may appear to be an “exponentiated version” of Theorem
4.3
, the authors of the present article are not aware whether one of the results can be deduced from the other. One major obstruction to a naive approach is that for
k\in K
one typically has

\Pi\circ\operatorname{conj}_{k}\circ\exp|_{\mathfrak{a}}\neq\exp\circ\pi\circ\operatorname{Ad}(k)|_{\mathfrak{a}},

(4.8)

where
\operatorname{conj}_{k}
denotes conjugation by
k
in
G
. A counterexample is already easy to find in the most basic example
G=\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Only when an element
h
in an open chamber of
X
is fixed and
k
runs through the entire group
K
do the images of the resulting two maps coincide. A direct proof of this assertion would be a very interesting achievement that might lead to various generalizations of Theorem
4.1
. In the case where
G
is a connected semisimple Lie group, such a “linear” convexity theorem has already been established by Kostant in
[
Kos73
, Theorem 8.2]
and a direct relation between the linear and the non-linear result was found by J.J. Duistermaat in
[
Dui84
, Theorem 1.1]
by constructing diffeomorphisms of
K
that resolve the inequality in (
4.8
). It relies heavily on analytic properties of Lie groups and therefore does not generalize to the Kac-Moody setting in a straightforward way.

Although a direct connection between the linear and non-linear convexity theorem for Kac-Moody groups does not seem to be readily available, it is possible to prove Theorem
4.3
with almost the same methods as Theorem
4.1
and we briefly describe how this can be achieved. Using the extended Weyl group
\tilde{W}\subset K
, it is easy to show that the set
\pi_{h}
on the left-hand side in Theorem
4.3
does not depend on the
W
-translate of
h
, so we may again w.l.o.g. assume that
h\in C
. Set
\Xi_{h}:=\operatorname{conv}(W\cdot h)
, let
k\in K
be arbitrary and write

\operatorname{Ad}(k)h=X^{\prime}+h^{\prime}+Y^{\prime}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{with $X^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{k}$, $h^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{a}$ and $Y^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{u}_{+}$.}

(4.9)

In order to show that
h^{\prime}=\pi(\operatorname{Ad}(k)h)\in\pi_{h}
is contained in
\Xi_{h}
, it suffices again to verify the inequalities in Corollary
2.11
, i.e.
\langle\omega_{i},w(h^{\prime})\rangle\leq\langle\omega_{i},h\rangle
for all
w\in W
and
i\in I
and
\langle\phi_{i},h^{\prime}\rangle=\langle\phi_{i},h\rangle
for all
i\in\{n+1,\ldots,d\}
. These assertions follow as before by comparing the actions of
h
and
h^{\prime}
in the highest-weight modules
L(\omega_{i})
and
L(\phi_{i})
endowed with a positive definite, contravariant, symmetric bilinear or Hermitian form
H
. A crucial ingredient is the formula

\rho_{i}(\operatorname{Ad}(k)h)=\hat{\rho}_{i}(k)\rho_{i}(h)\hat{\rho}_{i}(k)^{-1},

(4.10)

which holds more generally for integrable modules of Kac-Moody algebras. One then compares the two different ways of computing the action of
\operatorname{Ad}(k)h
under
\rho_{i}
provided by (
4.9
) and (
4.10
). The equation
\langle\phi_{i},h^{\prime}\rangle=\langle\phi_{i},h\rangle
follows from the fact that
L(\phi_{i})
is one-dimensional, so (
4.10
) reduces to
\rho_{i}(h)
, and the observation that
\rho_{i}(X^{\prime})=\rho_{i}(Y^{\prime})=0
. To prove the inequalities
\langle\omega_{i},w(h^{\prime})\rangle\leq\langle\omega_{i},h\rangle
for
w\in W
and
i\in I
, one studies the action of
\operatorname{Ad}(k)h
on a normalized weight-
\mu
vector
v\in L(\omega_{i})_{\mu}
, where
\mu:=w^{-1}(\omega_{i})
. On the one hand, the same geometric argument as in the non-linear case can be applied to the action given by (
4.10
) to obtain an upper bound for the norm of
\rho_{i}(\operatorname{Ad}(k)h)v
, but one has to take care of the possibility that some eigenvalues of
\rho_{i}(h)
might be negative. This issue is easily dealt with by adding a suitable scalar multiple of the identity to
\rho_{i}(h)
, so that all eigenvalues become non-negative. On the other hand, it is not difficult to show that
H(v,\rho_{i}(X^{\prime})v)=H(v,\rho_{i}(Y^{\prime})v)=0
which allows to compare the value of
H(\rho_{i}(\operatorname{Ad}(k)h)v,v)
obtained from the two equations. These ideas prove that
\pi_{h}\subset\Xi_{h}
.

The converse inclusion follows again by induction on
n
, reducing the case
n=1
to
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{K})
and its usual Iwasawa decomposition by a similar argument, where the assertion is a straightforward computation. One then chooses an arbitrary
i\in I
and studies the adjoint actions of the same subgroups
G_{w(J)}
and
G^{(i)}
on
h
. The geometric setup of the proof is the same as in the non-linear case, i.e. for fixed
t\in I_{h}
, one first covers faces of the essential part
P^{(i)}_{\operatorname{ess}}(t)
using the adjoint action of some subgroup
K_{w(J)}\subset G_{w(J)}
and then uses the action of
K^{(i)}\subset G^{(i)}
to cover the remaining faces of
P^{(i)}(t)
. Again, the crucial step is to ensure that the repeated applications of these subgroups respect the Iwasawa decompositions. More precisely, one needs to show that if
k_{1}\in K_{w(J)}
and
Y
denotes the
\mathfrak{u}_{+}
-component of
\operatorname{Ad}(k_{1})h
, then
\operatorname{Ad}(k_{2})Y\in\mathfrak{u}_{+}
for every
k_{2}\in K^{(i)}
. Here, the proof does not require a geometric argument, the assertion is a consequence of (
3.3
) which holds in a very similar form for the adjoint representation. These arguments establish that
\Xi_{h}\subset\pi_{h}
and conclude the proof of Theorem
4.3
.

4.3
Concluding remarks

We close our discussion by relating Theorem
4.1
to Kostant’s original convexity theorem
[
Kos73
, Theorem 4.1]
and describing an application of our result to decompositions of Kac-Moody groups as well as some generalizations and open problems.

If every indecomposable submatrix of
\mathbb{A}
is of finite type, then
\mathbb{A}
is the Cartan matrix of a finite-dimensional complex semisimple Lie algebra and the Kac-Moody groups associated to
\mathbb{A}
essentially coincide with the corresponding Chevalley groups. In that case,
\mathbb{A}
is invertible, so that
\mathcal{D}^{\mathbb{A}}_{\text{Kac}}=\mathcal{D}^{\mathbb{A}}_{\text{sc}}
, and symmetrizable. If
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{D}^{\mathbb{A}}_{\text{Kac}}
, then
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{C}}
is the complex semisimple Lie algebra with Cartan matrix
\mathbb{A}
and
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}
is its split real form. Likewise, for
\mathbb{K}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}\}
,
G=G(\mathbb{K})
is the group of
\mathbb{K}
-points of the corresponding simply connected Chevalley-Demazure group scheme associated to
\mathbb{A}
. For an arbitrary free and cofree root datum of rank
d\geq 2n-\operatorname{rank}(\mathbb{A})=n
, one obtains semidirect extensions of these groups and algebras. We can therefore view
G
as the group of
\mathbb{K}
-points of a connected reductive
\mathbb{R}
-split algebraic
\mathbb{R}
-group and hence as a split real or complex Lie group with a reductive Lie algebra and finitely-many connected components in its Lie group topology. The existence of an Iwasawa decomposition for connected semisimple Lie groups is a classical result in Lie theory and has been generalized to groups of
\mathbb{R}
-points of connected reductive algebraic
\mathbb{R}
-groups in
[
PR94
, Proposition 3.13]
. We also have
X=\mathfrak{a}
, so every element is either regular or singular. Theorem
4.1
differs from
[
Kos73
, Theorem 4.1]
in the following two aspects:

(i)

On the one hand, Kostant’s result is a statement about arbitrary connected semisimple Lie groups. Not every such group arises as the group of
\mathbb{K}
-points of an algebraic
\mathbb{R}
-group, e.g. the universal cover of
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Moreover, by our choice of definition, the real Kac-Moody groups associated to GCM’s of finite type are
\mathbb{R}
-split, which need not be the case for general semisimple Lie groups. On the other hand, our result does not require
G
to be connected and allows the Lie algebra of
G
to be reductive instead of semisimple.

(ii)

In contrast to Kostant’s theorem, our result currently requires the assumption that
h
is a regular element of
\mathfrak{a}
.

The differences in (i) are entirely due to the setup that we work in and not necessary for our proof which only involves algebraic and representation-theoretic arguments. Our method does not require a matrix realization and applies equally well to semisimple real Lie groups whose Lie algebra is not necessarily a split real form of its complexification. Such a group
G
has similar structural and representation-theoretic properties, where the role of
T
is played by a maximal
\mathbb{R}
-split torus
T^{\prime}\subset G
. Then
G
is generated by
T^{\prime}
and appropriate root groups, which need not be one-dimensional in general, indexed by the roots of the (possibly non-reduced) root system arising from the adjoint action of
T^{\prime}
on the Lie algebra of
G
. The second restriction can also be lifted since the Weyl group
W
of a semisimple Lie group is finite and the convex hulls of
W
-orbits of points in
\mathfrak{a}
are compact. This has the consequence that if
h\in\mathfrak{a}
is an arbitrary (possibly singular) point in
\mathfrak{a}
and
(h_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of regular elements converging to
h
, which always exists in the Lie group case, then
(\Xi_{h_{k}})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to
\Xi_{h}
with respect to the Hausdorff distance in
\mathfrak{a}
by finiteness of
W
. Likewise,
(a_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
with
a_{k}:=\exp(h_{k})
converges to
a:=\exp(h)
by continuity of the exponential map and
(\Pi_{a_{k}})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to
\Pi_{a}
with respect to the Hausdorff distance in
A
by continuity of the Iwasawa projection
\Pi:G\to A
. Thus, Theorem
4.1
extends to singular elements if
W
is finite by approximation. Alternatively, it is also possible to repeat our proof of Theorem
4.1
for singular elements. The crucial step is to generalize Corollary
2.11
and Proposition
2.17
to singular points. In fact, that
\Xi_{h}
is defined by the same inequalities as in the regular case has already been shown by Kostant in
[
Kos73
, Lemma 3.3]
and also follows from our discussion since
\Xi_{h}
is automatically closed if
W
is finite. The validity of Proposition
2.17
in the singular case can be obtained by approximating
h
by regular elements as above. Thus, our proof of Theorem
4.1
does indeed provide an alternative proof of Kostant convexity in connected semisimple Lie groups as well. However, we strongly emphasize that such an approximation technique does not work in the general Kac-Moody setting because if
W
is infinite, the existence of unbounded faces causes the convex hulls of
W
-orbits of different points to have unbounded Hausdorff distance, so there is no hope of convergence in this case. In addition, contrary to Lie groups, Kac-Moody groups usually do not admit a Cartan decomposition
G=KAK
(cf.
[
Hor17
, Proposition 6.3 (iii)]
), so an analogue of
[
Kos73
, Corollary 4.2]
that describes the set
\{\Pi(gk)\mid k\in K\}
for an arbitrary
g\in G
does not automatically follow in our situation.

The non-existence of this result gives rise to another interesting question concerning the algebraic structure of Kac-Moody groups. Using
[
Kos73
, Corollary 4.2]
, Kostant concludes that every semisimple Lie group
G
has a decomposition
G=KU_{+}K
(cf.
[
Kos73
, Theorem 5.1]
). For the corresponding symmetric space
G/K
, this result implies that any two points of
G/K
are contained in a common horosphere. In the general Kac-Moody setting, however, Kostant’s arguments do not apply and the question whether a similar decomposition result holds for all Kac-Moody groups appears to have been open (cf.
[
Hor17
, Question 6.8]
). Our main result yields a negative answer to this question and also provides a slightly more precise result in the Lie group case.

Corollary 4.4
.

Let
\mathbb{A}
be a symmetrizable GCM of size
n
,
\mathcal{D}
a free and cofree Kac-Moody root datum of rank
d\geq 2n-\operatorname{rank}(\mathbb{A})
associated to
\mathbb{A}
,
G
the real or complex split Kac-Moody group of type
\mathcal{D}
and
G=KAU_{+}
an Iwasawa decomposition of
G
. Suppose that one of the following two conditions is satisfied:

(i)

d>n
.

(ii)

d=n
and not every indecomposable submatrix of
\mathbb{A}
is of finite type.

Then
G\neq KU_{+}K
.

Proof.

If an element
g\in G
is contained in
KU_{+}K
, then there exists some
k\in K
such that
gk\in KU_{+}
and hence
\Pi(gk)=1
. We are going to show that in either of the above cases, we can choose
g=a\in\exp(C)\subset A
such that this equality is impossible. By Theorem
4.1
, it suffices to show that there exists some
h\in C
such that
0\notin\operatorname{conv}(W\cdot h)=\Xi_{h}
.

Suppose first that
d>n
, which is automatically assumed to be the case if
\mathbb{A}
is not invertible, and take an arbitrary
h_{1}\in C
. Since
d>n
, the subspace
\mathfrak{c}:=\bigcap_{i\in I}\operatorname{ker}(\alpha_{i})\subset\mathfrak{a}
is at least one-dimensional. Hence, there exists
h_{2}\in\mathfrak{c}
such that
h:=h_{1}+h_{2}
satisfies
\langle\omega_{j},h\rangle<0
for at least one
j\in I
. In addition, we still have
\langle\alpha_{i},h\rangle=\langle\alpha_{i},h_{1}\rangle>0
for all
i\in I
and thus
h\in C
. Corollary
2.11
therefore implies that
0\notin\Xi_{h}
.

Now assume that
d=n
and let
\mathbb{A}=\mathbb{A}_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus\mathbb{A}_{m}
be the decomposition of
\mathbb{A}
into indecomposable submatrices. By assumption, there exists some
k\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
such that
\mathbb{A}_{k}
is not of finite type. The classification of GCM’s (cf. Section
1
) therefore implies that
\mathbb{A}_{k}
is of affine or indefinite type, where the former is impossible since
d=n
forces
\mathbb{A}
to be invertible. Denote the subset of
I
indexing
\mathbb{A}_{k}
by
I_{k}
. For a vector
h=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}\alpha_{j}^{\vee}\in\mathfrak{a}^{\prime}=\mathfrak{a}
, the condition
\langle\alpha_{i},h\rangle>0
for all
i\in I
is equivalent to
\mathbb{A}^{T}\lambda>0
, where
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and the obvious analogue of that assertion is true if one passes to subsets of
I
indexing the corresponding indecomposable submatrices of
\mathbb{A}
. Let
\mu
be the vector obtained from
\lambda
by removing all coordinates in
I\setminus I_{k}
. Since
\mathbb{A}_{k}
is of indefinite type, the same is true for
\mathbb{A}_{k}^{T}
and the condition
\mathbb{A}_{k}^{T}\mu\geq 0
with
\mu\geq 0
can only be satisfied if
\mu=0
, so if
\mathbb{A}_{k}^{T}\mu>0
, then at least one coordinate of
\mu
must be negative. Thus, if
h\in C
, then some coordinate of the subvector
\mu
must be negative, i.e.
\lambda_{j}<0
for at least one
j\in I_{k}
or, equivalently,
\langle\omega_{j},h\rangle<0
. As in the first case, it now follows that
0\notin\Xi_{h}
.
∎

Returning to the discussion at the beginning of this section, the next natural step would be to generalize Theorem
4.1
to singular points also in the “proper” Kac-Moody setting where
W
is infinite. This might also lead to a generalization of our result to Kac-Moody groups whose root datum is not necessarily free and cofree, because such groups also possess an associated Tits cone, but its open fundamental chamber might be empty. As mentioned, simple approximation techniques as in the finite case are not directly applicable. We believe that it is a more promising approach to try to generalize all the results from Chapter
2
to the singular case. The main difficulty is to understand how faces of
\Xi_{h}
degenerate as
h
moves towards certain walls of the fundamental chamber, which is closely connected to studying how the stabilizer of
h
decomposes the Coxeter diagram of
(W,S)
. Contrary to the regular case, however, it does not seem easy to decide whether
\Xi_{h}
is closed if
h
is singular. Another interesting open problem would be to investigate whether an analogue of Theorem
4.1
is also true for external elements. This is certainly a necessary step in order to decide whether the set
\{\Pi(gk)\mid k\in K\}
for arbitrary
g\in G
has an explicit description as in the case of Lie groups.
