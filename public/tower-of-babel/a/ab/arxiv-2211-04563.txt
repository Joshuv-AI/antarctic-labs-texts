A measure-valued stochastic model for vector-borne viruses
Mario Ayala, Jerome Coville, Raphael Forien

Abstract
. In this work we propose a measure-valued stochastic process representing the dynamics of a virus population, structured by phenotypic traits and geographical space, and where viruses are transported between spatial locations by mechanical vectors. As a first example of the use of this model, we show how to use this model to infer results on the probability of extinction of the virus population. Later, by combining various scalings on population sizes, speed of diffusion of vectors, and other relevant model parameters, we show the emergence of two systems of integro-differential equations as Macroscopic descriptions of the system. Under the existence of densities at time zero, we also show the propagation of this property for later times, and derive the strong formulation of the limiting systems of IDEs. These strong formulations, in a sense, correspond to spatial Lotka-Volterra competition models with mutation and vector-borne dispersal.



1.
Introduction

The selective pressure imposed on pathogens during the infection of a new type of host is one of the well known mechanisms that drive their evolution.
For plant pathogens, plant genes conferring major or partial resistance to these pathogens are then valuable natural resources which in a context of a plant of agricultural interest should be used in a way that maximizes the preservation of their efficiency in conjunction with the gain of crop productivity they can ensure.
To gain some insights on the possible optimized deployment strategies, the study of adaptation of pathogens to their hosts during repeated epidemic events is then of great importance. The understanding of such evolution process is at the heart of evolutionary epidemiology research, see for example the review
[
26
]
.
In the evolutionary epidemiology literature, most of the models deal with either the adaptation process with oversimplified epidemic process, as described in
[
4
]
, or conversely focus on the epidemic process forgetting a proper description of the evolution processes at stake. One of the main reasons essentially comes from the modelling tools used to describe these two phenomena. Indeed, selection mutation population models are often used to describe evolution phenomena ignoring the possible spatial structure of this epidemic event, whereas epidemics tend to be represented by SEIR type models using the host as proxy for describing the epidemic forgetting the description of the evolving pathogen population.

Modelling approaches trying to reconcile these two points of view have been recently introduced
[
6
,
5
,
25
,
16
,
8
,
12
]
where the epidemic approach considering the host as a proxy has been adapted in order to take into account a partial description of the population that evolves and its possible adaptation by mutation. However, whereas this approach seems well suited for pathogens that behave like spores, it does not seems flexible enough to describe viral populations that disseminate in a field through vectors. As well, they do not take into account stochastic effects due to low population densities at the beginning of the epidemic events.

In the spirit of
[
6
,
5
,
25
,
16
,
8
,
12
]
, we propose and analyse here new modelling tools that integrate on the same time scale the epidemic events and the adaptation processes. However, unlike the approach proposed in
[
25
,
16
,
8
]
that uses the hosts as a proxy, we fully describe the pathogen population with all its possible interactions making the assumption that the hosts is an environmental variable for the pathogen population. Our aim here is then to construct a stochastic realistic representation of the main processes of adaptation involved during an epidemic event, and to obtain various large population asymptotic limits depending on the choice of the scaling parameter considered. The stochastic nature of our model gives room to incorporate demographic stochasticity, which is an important feature to consider, since early stages potentially affect later stages of the epidemic. Moreover, our model permits to study both quantitative and qualitative trait scenarios.

Some of the results we present here are in the spirit of the so-called hydrodynamic limits from the theory of interacting particle systems, for physics see for example
[
7
,
20
,
28
]
, and for biology
[
13
,
2
,
1
,
23
]
. In particular, we use the methodology introduced in
[
13
]
, and in subsequent works (
[
2
,
1
,
23
]
), to build a measure-valued stochastic model representing the dynamics of a virus population structured by time, space, and phenotypic traits, and where viruses are subject to vector-borne dispersal, and can only grow in plants.

In the derivation of the scaling limits, our particular choice of re-scaling is important since it allows us to consider different scenarios that depend on the relation of the order of magnitude between the total number of viruses and vectors at time zero. In particular, when we have fewer vectors than viruses, in order to obtain a sensible limit, it becomes necessary to accelerate the diffusion of vectors. As the reader will see in Theorem
1.6
below, this acceleration has the consequence that for each
t>0
the population of vectors is given by the solution of an elliptic system which depends only on the current population of viruses at time
t
. We can interpret this as saying that the population of vectors is at an equilibrium that only changes through the time evolution of the virus population.

The Viral Epidemic model

Let us consider three populations, denoted by
{\nu_{v}}(t),{\nu_{c}}(t)
and
{\nu_{u}}(t)
, representing the viral population, the population of vectors that are charged with a virus, and the free vectors population, respectively. As in
[
13
,
2
,
1
,
23
]
, let us first introduce, for a Polish space
X
, the set
\mathcal{M}_{p}(X)
denoting the space of finite point measures on
X
. We define these populations by means of point measures on the adequate Polish spaces as follows.
First, we consider the viral population
{\nu_{v}}(t)
. We represent this population by means of a point measure over the space
E\times\mathcal{X}
, where
E
is a finite set of positions in a bounded smooth, at least
C^{3}
, domain
{\mathcal{D}}\subset{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
and
\mathcal{X}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is a compact set. The set
E
corresponds to the locations of the various host plants, and
\mathcal{X}
corresponds to the phenotypic trait space of viruses.
Thus, for
t\geq 0
,

(1.1)

{\nu_{v}}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{v}(t)}\delta_{x_{i}(t),z_{i}(t)},

where
N_{v}(t):=\langle{\nu_{v}}(t),1\rangle
is the total size of the virus population and

\displaystyle(x_{1}(t),\ldots,x_{N_{v}(t)}(t))\in E^{N_{v}(t)},

\displaystyle(z_{1}(t),\ldots,z_{N_{v}(t)}(t))\in\mathcal{X}^{N_{v}(t)}

correspond respectively to an arbitrary ordering of the positions and traits of the viruses.

Similarly, the uncharged vectors population is represented by a finite point measure on
{\mathcal{D}}
,
{\nu_{u}}(t)
, taking the form

\displaystyle{\nu_{u}}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{u}(t)}\delta_{Y_{i}^{u}(t)},

where
N_{u}(t)
is the number of uncharged vectors and
(Y^{u}_{1}(t),\ldots,Y^{u}_{N_{u}(t)}(t))
are their positions at time
t\geq 0
.
Finally, the population of vectors carrying viruses is represented by a measure
{\nu_{c}}(t)\in\mathcal{M}_{p}({\mathcal{D}}\times\mathcal{X})
, of the form

\displaystyle{\nu_{c}}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{c}(t)}\delta_{Y_{i}^{c}(t),z_{i}(t)},

where, for
i\in\{1,\ldots,N_{c}(t)\}
,
Y_{i}^{c}(t)
is the position of the vector and
z_{i}(t)
is the trait of the unique virus it is carrying.

In addition to the definitions of the point measures
{\nu_{v}}(t),{\nu_{c}}(t)
and
{\nu_{u}}(t)
, let us now define a specific set of biological rules that mechanistically determine the way that these measures evolve over time. To reflect the adaptation process during an epidemic event, we will assume that viruses are submitted to growth, death, mutation and transportation by vectors and that vectors move in
{\mathcal{D}}
according to a reflected Itô-diffusion and that they can load and unload viruses on plants. This translates into considering the following processes :

Reproduction:

viruses on plants reproduce asexually at rate
b(x,z)
, where
x\in E
denotes the position of the hosting plant and
z\in{\mathcal{X}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
the phenotypic trait of the virus. Given a reproductive event, with probability
1-\mu
the new virus inherits its parent trait, and with probability
\mu
undergoes mutation. In the latter case, the trait
z^{\prime}\mkern-1.2mu
of the new individual is drawn from a probability distribution with
density
m(z,z^{\prime}\mkern-1.2mu)
with respect to some measure
\bar{m}(dz^{\prime}\mkern-1.2mu)
on
{\mathcal{X}}
.

Death:

viruses on plants die naturally at rate
d(z)>0
, and due to competition at rate
cN_{x}(t)
, where
N_{x}(t)
denotes the number of viruses in a plant at position
x\in E
, and
c
is a positive constant. Likewise, viruses being carried by a vector die at rate
\gamma(z)
.

Diffusion:

vectors, free or charged with virus, diffuse in the spatial domain
{\mathcal{D}}\subset{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
according to a
non-degenerate (see
[
14
]
) Itô diffusion with normal reflection at the boundary of the domain
\partial{\mathcal{D}}
, i.e. for
\alpha\in\{u,c\}
, the diffusion has infinitesimal generator

\displaystyle{{\mathcal{L}}^{u}}{\phi_{u}}(y)

\displaystyle=a^{u}(y)\cdot\nabla{\phi_{u}}(y)+\frac{\sigma^{u}(y)^{2}}{2}\Delta{\phi_{u}}(y),

\displaystyle{{\mathcal{L}}^{c}}{\phi_{c}}(w,e)

\displaystyle=a^{c}(w)\cdot\nabla^{(w)}{\phi_{c}}(w,e)+\frac{\sigma^{c}(w)^{2}}{2}\Delta^{(w)}{\phi_{c}}(w,e),

where
{\phi_{u}}:\bar{{\mathcal{D}}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
and
{\phi_{c}}:\bar{{\mathcal{D}}}\times{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
are elements of their corresponding domains:

\displaystyle D({{\mathcal{L}}^{u}})

\displaystyle=\left\{\phi\in{\mathcal{C}}^{2}({\mathcal{D}}):\nabla\phi(y)\cdot\vec{n}(y)=0,\quad\forall y\in\partial D\right\},

\displaystyle D({{\mathcal{L}}^{c}})

\displaystyle=\left\{\phi:{\mathcal{D}}\times{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}:\forall e\in{\mathcal{X}};\phi(\cdot,e)\in{\mathcal{C}}^{2}({\mathcal{D}})\text{ and }\nabla\phi(y)\cdot\vec{n}(y)=0,\quad\forall w\in\partial D\right\}.

where
\vec{n}(y)
denotes the inward normal at
y\in\partial D
.

Charging of vectors:

vectors
successfully
bite plants and get charged with a virus at rate
\beta(t,y,x,N_{x}(t),z)
, where
y\in{\mathcal{D}}
denotes the current position of the vector,
x\in E
the plant’s location,
N_{x}(t)
is the total number of viruses at
x
, and
z\in{\mathcal{X}}
is the phenotype of the virus being taken. Trying to be consistent with biological studies (see
[
24
,
15
]
) which suggest that a vector carries and effectively transmits a small number of viruses, we assume that a vector can carry at most one virus at the same time.

Un-loading of vectors:

vectors
successfully
discharge viruses at rate
\eta(t,y,x,z)
, where
y\in{\mathcal{D}}
denotes the current position of the vector,
x\in E
the plant’s location, and
z\in{\mathcal{X}}
the phenotype of the virus being unloaded.

Furthermore, we also make the following, biologically consistent, additional assumptions:

Assumption 1
.

Let us assume that the coefficients
\sigma,a
are Lipschitz continuous,
\sigma>0
, that the mutation density is a probability density, that the competition parameter
c
is strictly positive, and
that there exist
(\bar{b},\bar{d},\bar{\gamma},\bar{\beta},\bar{\eta})\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{5}
, such that for all
t\geq 0
we have:

\displaystyle\sup_{(x,z)\in E\times{\mathcal{X}}}b(x,z)=\bar{b}<\infty,

\displaystyle\quad\sup_{z\in{\mathcal{X}}}d(z)=\bar{d}<\infty,\quad\sup_{u\in{\mathcal{X}}}\gamma(u)=\bar{\gamma}<\infty

\displaystyle\sup_{r\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\sup_{(y,z)\in{\mathcal{D}}\times{\mathcal{X}}}\int_{E}\beta(t,y,x,r,z)\,dx

\displaystyle=\bar{\beta}<\infty,\quad\sup_{(y,z)\in{\mathcal{D}}\times{\mathcal{X}}}\int_{E}\eta(t,y,x,z)\,dx=\bar{\eta}<\infty.

Moreover, for all
(t,y,x,z)\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathcal{D}}\times E\times{\mathcal{X}}
, we assume
\beta(t,y,x,\cdot,z):{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
to be Lipschitz continuous.

Our framework allows to incorporate the spatial extent of the plant. We can assume for example the existence of some function
\beta_{e}:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathcal{D}}\times E\times{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, and
r_{p}>0
such that:

\beta(t,y,x,N_{x}(t),z)=\begin{cases}\beta_{e}(t,|x-y|,N_{x}(t),z)&\text{ if }|x-y|\leq r_{p},\\
0&\text{ otherwise},\end{cases}

for all
t\geq 0
. Analogous assumptions can be imposed over the model parameter
\eta
.

Infinitesimal generator

We are interested in the time-evolution of the process
\{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{t}:t\geq 0\}=\{({\nu_{v}}(t),{\nu_{u}}(t),{\nu_{c}}(t)):t\geq 0\}
taking values in the space of measures
{\mathcal{M}}_{p}:={\mathcal{M}}_{p}(E\times{\mathcal{X}})\times{\mathcal{M}}_{p}({\mathcal{D}})\times{\mathcal{M}}_{p}({\mathcal{D}}\times{\mathcal{X}})
. Let us introduce the set of cylindrical functions that generates the set of bounded and measurable functions from
{\mathcal{M}}_{p}
to
{\mathbb{R}}

Definition 1.1
.

We call an admissible triplet, and denote it by
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}

\{{\phi_{v}},{\phi_{u}},{\phi_{c}}\}
such that:
{\phi_{u}}:{\mathcal{D}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is in the domain of the generator
{{\mathcal{L}}^{u}}
,
{\phi_{c}}:{{\mathcal{V}}_{c}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is in the domain of the generator
{{\mathcal{L}}^{c}}

Remark 1

For

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}^{u}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}=\{0,{\mathcal{L}}^{u}_{y}{\phi_{u}},0\},\text{ and }{\mathcal{L}}^{c}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}=\{0,0,{\mathcal{L}}^{c}_{w}{\phi_{c}}\}.

We now define the relevant set of cylindrical functions.

Definition 1.2

The class of cylindrical functions

Existence and uniqueness

We have just described the time-evolution of our measure-valued process in terms of the infinitesimal generator
{\mathcal{L}}
given by (
1.3
).
Our first result is to rigorously prove that under Assumption
1
the process is well defined.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{0}=({\nu_{v}}(0),{\nu_{u}}(0),{\nu_{c}}(0))
be such that we have:

(1.4)

\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\mathbf{1},{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{0}\rangle^{2}\right]<\infty,

where

\langle\mathbf{1},{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{0}\rangle:=\int_{E\times{\mathcal{X}}}1\,{\nu_{v}}(0)(dx,dz)+\int_{{\mathcal{D}}}1\,{\nu_{u}}(0)(dy)+\int_{{\mathcal{D}}\times{\mathcal{X}}}1\,{\nu_{c}}(0)(dy,dz).

Then, under Assumption
1
, part a), the process
\left({\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}=\left(\nu_{v}(t),{\nu_{u}}(t),{\nu_{c}}(t):t\geq 0\right)
satisfies:

(1.5)

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\langle\mathbf{1},{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{t}\rangle^{2}\right]<\infty.

In particular, we also have that the process
\nu_{t}
is well-defined.

For a proof we refer to the Appendix, where we proved (
1.5
) for higher-order moments, and where we also showed that the dynamics of the process
({\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
indeed corresponds to the one described by the infinitesimal generator
{\mathcal{L}}
.

Population-level descriptions as observables

The framework of this paper allows us to directly recover some quantities of interest as observables of the system. For example, the evolution of the process representing the total virus population can be recovered by integrating the constant function 1 with respect to the measures
{\nu_{v}}
and
{\nu_{c}}
:

(1.6)

P_{v}(t):=\langle 1,{\nu_{v}}(t)\rangle+\langle 1,{\nu_{c}}(t)\rangle.

In a similar way, we can also recover the total number of vectors charged with a virus and those free from viruses:

\displaystyle N_{u}(t):=\langle 1,{\nu_{u}}(t)\rangle,\text{ and }N_{c}(t):=\langle 1,{\nu_{c}}(t)\rangle.

Moreover, we show how this description can be used to extend known results about extinction to the case of vector-borne dispersal.

Theorem 1.4
.

Suppose that the initial set of measures
({\nu_{v}}(0),{\nu_{u}}(0),{\nu_{c}}(0))
is such that

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\langle\nu_{v}(0),1\rangle\right]<\infty.

Under Assumption
1
, we have that the total-virus population process
P_{v}(t)
given by (
1.6
) goes extinct almost surely.

We refer to Section
2
for details on the proof of this theorem.

Deterministc limits

Our main results concern the derivation of large population-level deterministic descriptions of our system. By introducing a rescaling parameter
K
, we derive these descriptions in the spirit of a law of large numbers for our processes. In this setting, the rescaling parameter
K
has the biological interpretation of imposing a carrying capacity to the system. We incorporate this idea by letting the competition parameter
c
depends on
K
as follows:

c_{K}=\frac{c}{K}.

Moreover, we consider two scenarios: one in which the population of viruses and vectors are of the same order, and one in which the population of vectors is of smaller order than that of viruses. We model these two scenarios by introducing a new parameter
\lambda\in(0,1]
, which rescales the population of viruses and vectors, at time zero, as follows:

\displaystyle{\nu_{v}}^{(K)}(0)=\frac{1}{K}{\nu_{v}}(0,K),\quad{\nu_{u}}^{(K)}(0)=\frac{1}{K^{\lambda}}{\nu_{u}}(0,K),\text{ and }{\nu_{c}}^{(K)}(0)=\frac{1}{K^{\lambda}}{\nu_{c}}(0,K).

where for each
\alpha\in\{v,u,c\}
and
K\in{\mathbb{N}}
, the random measure
\nu_{\alpha}(0,K)
is an element of
{\mathcal{M}}_{p}({\mathcal{V}}_{\alpha})
. An important ingredient needed for our results is the assumption that at time zero the sequences
\{\nu_{\alpha}^{(K)}(0)\}_{K\in{\mathbb{N}}}
converge for
\alpha\in\{v,u,c\}
. The convergence of these sequences implies that for the case
\lambda\in(0,1)
, the total number of vectors is of smaller order than the total population of viruses. This
lack of vectors
suggests the need to let the rest of the parameters depend on
K
as well. In particular, grossly speaking, we compensate the lack of vectors by letting the processes
{\nu_{u}}(t,K)
and
{\nu_{c}}(t,K)
(i.e. the time evolution of the processes with initial condition
{\nu_{u}}(0,K)
and
{\nu_{c}}(0,K)
) evolve with diffusive generator given by:

(1.7)

{\mathcal{L}}_{K}^{\alpha,\text{accel}}=K^{1-\lambda}{\mathcal{L}}^{\alpha},

where for
\alpha\in\{u,c\}
,
{\mathcal{L}}^{\alpha}
denotes the infinitesimal generator of the Itô diffusion driving the movement of the
\alpha
-class of vectors. Furthermore, the explicit form on which the rescaled parameters
\eta_{K}
,
\beta_{K}
and
\gamma_{K}
will depend on
K
comes from the idea of making the distance travelled by a vector between loading and unloading of order one. Additionally, we wish the number of virus deaths (on vectors) to be of the same order. This means:

K^{1+\lambda}\beta_{K}=K^{\lambda}\eta_{K}=K^{\lambda}\gamma_{K}=O(K)

or equivalenty

\eta_{K}=O(K^{1-\lambda}),\quad\gamma_{K}=O(K^{1-\lambda}),\text{ and }\beta_{K}=O(K^{-\lambda}).

We formalize these ideas with the following assumption:

Assumption 2
.

There exists a Lipschitz continuous function
\beta:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times\mathcal{D}\times E\times{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times\mathcal{X}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, continuous functions
\eta:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times\mathcal{D}\times E\times\mathcal{X}\to{\mathbb{R}}
and
\gamma:\mathcal{X}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, and a positive constant
c
such that:

\beta_{K}(t,y,x,N_{x}(t),z)=K^{-\lambda}\beta(t,y,x,N_{x}(t),z),\quad c_{K}=\frac{c}{K},\quad\eta_{K}(t,y,x,z)=K^{1-\lambda}\eta(t,y,x,z),\quad\gamma_{K}(z)=K^{1-\lambda}\gamma(z),

for all
t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
. Moreover, we assume the rest of the parameters to be independent of the scaling parameter
K
, and all parameters together satisfy Assumption
1
.

Under the assumptions above, we normalize our processes as follows:

(1.8)

\displaystyle{\nu_{v}}^{(K)}(t)=\frac{1}{K}{\nu_{v}}(t,K),\quad{\nu_{u}}^{(K)}(t)=\frac{1}{K^{\lambda}}{\nu_{u}}(t,K),\text{ and }{\nu_{c}}^{(K)}(t)=\frac{1}{K^{\lambda}}{\nu_{c}}(t,K).

where for
\alpha\in\{u,c\}
, the process
\nu_{\alpha}(t,K)
has dynamics with diffusive generator given by
{\mathcal{L}}_{K}^{\alpha,\text{accel}}
as in (
1.7
).

In the two regimes described above ( i.e. if
\lambda=1
or
\lambda\in(0,1)
) we show that, as the parameter
K
tends to infinity, the normalized triplet of measure-valued processes
({\nu_{v}}^{(K)}(t),{\nu_{u}}^{(K)}(t),{\nu_{c}}^{(K)}(t))
converges in path-space to a deterministic limiting triplet
(\xi_{v}(t),\xi_{u}(t),\xi_{c}(t))
characterized as being the solution of a system of non-local integro-differential equations. We refer the reader to Theorem
3.1
and Theorem
3.3
in Section
3
, which make rigorous this convergence in a general setting and describe the precise assumptions needed at time zero. In the following paragraphs, we present two simpler versions of Theorem
3.1
and Theorem
3.3
where in particular the deaths of viruses on vectors are neglected.

First regime

For simplicity let us assume that at time zero the sequences of finite point measures
\{{\nu_{v}}^{(K)}(0)\}_{K\in{\mathbb{N}}}
,
\{{\nu_{u}}^{(K)}(0)\}_{K\in{\mathbb{N}}}
and
\{{\nu_{c}}^{(K)}(0)\}_{K\in{\mathbb{N}}}
converge to deterministic limiting measures
\xi_{v}(0)
,
\xi_{u}(0)
and
\xi_{c}(0)
, respectively. Moreover, we assume that these limiting measures are absolutely continuous with respect to the relevant Haar measures in their respective state spaces (i.e., the counting measure
\zeta_{E}(dx)
on
E
, the Lebesgue measure on
{\mathcal{D}}
, etc…). Let us denote the densities of the limiting measures
\xi_{v}(0)
,
\xi_{u}(0)
and
\xi_{c}(0)
, by
g_{v}(x,z),g_{u}(y)
, and
g_{c}(y,z)
respectively. In Theorem
3.2
, we show that for reversible diffusions we have the propagation of absolutely continuity for later times. With an abuse of notation let us also denote by
g_{v}(t,x,z),g_{u}(t,y)
, and
g_{c}(t,y,z)
respectively, these densities for later times
t>0
.

For the sake of clarity let us assume that vectors diffuse in space according to reflected Brownian motion. Then a consequence of Theorem
3.1
is the following result which resembles the so-called Lotka-Volterra competition system in the presence of diffusion by vectors.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
\lambda=1
, and let
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{(K)}_{0}=({\nu_{v}}^{(K)}(0),{\nu_{u}}^{(K)}(0),{\nu_{c}}^{(K)}(0))
be such that:

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(\langle 1,{\nu_{v}}^{(k)}(0)\rangle+\langle 1,{\nu_{u}}^{(k)}(0)\rangle+\langle 1,{\nu_{c}}^{(k)}(0)\rangle\right)^{3}\right]<\infty

Then, the process
\{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{(K)}(t):t\geq 0\}
converges, as
K\to\infty
, to a deterministic process
\xi_{t}=(\xi_{v}(t),\xi_{u}(t),\xi_{c}(t))
with densities
(g_{v},g_{u},g_{c})
being the unique solution of the following system:

\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial t}g_{v}(t,x,z)&=(1-\mu)b(x,z)\,g_{v}(t,x,z)+\mu\int_{\mathcal{X}}b(x,z^{\prime}\mkern-1.2mu)m(z^{\prime},z)\,g_{v}(t,x,z^{\prime}\mkern-1.2mu)\,dz^{\prime}\\
&-\left[d(z)+c\,\left(\int_{\mathcal{X}}g_{v}(t,x,z^{\prime})dz^{\prime}\mkern-1.2mu\right)\right]g_{v}(t,x,z)\\
&-\left[\int_{\mathcal{D}}\beta\left(y,x,\int_{\mathcal{X}}g_{v}(t,x,z^{\prime})dz^{\prime},z\right)g_{u}(t,y)\,dy\right]\,g_{v}(t,x,z)+\left(\int_{{\mathcal{D}}}\eta(y,x,z)\,g_{c}(t,y,z)\,dy\right),\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}g_{u}(t,y)&=\Delta g_{u}(t,y)-\left[\int_{E\times{\mathcal{X}}}\beta\left(y,x,\int_{\mathcal{X}}g_{v}(t,x,z^{\prime})dz^{\prime},z\right)\,g_{v}(t,x,z)\,\zeta_{E}(dx)\,dz\right]\,g_{u}(t,y)\\
&+\int_{E\times{\mathcal{X}}}\eta(y,x,z)\,g_{c}(t,y,z)\,\zeta_{E}(dx)\,dz,\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}g_{c}(t,y,z)&=\Delta_{y}g_{c}(t,y,z)+\left[\int_{E}\beta\left(y,x,\int_{\mathcal{X}}g_{v}(t,x,z^{\prime})dz^{\prime},z\right)\,g_{v}(t,x,z)\,\zeta_{E}(dx)\right]\,g_{u}(t,y)\\
&-\left(\int_{E}\eta(y,x,z)\,\zeta_{E}(dx)\right)\,g_{c}(t,y,z),\end{aligned}\right.

with initial data and boundary conditions given by:

\displaystyle g_{v}(0,x,z)=g_{v}(x,z),\quad g_{u}(0,y)=g_{u}(y),\text{ and }\quad g_{c}(0,y,z)=g_{c}(y,z)

\displaystyle\nabla g_{u}(t,y)\cdot\vec{n}(y)=0\quad\text{ and }\quad\nabla g_{u}(t,y,z)\cdot\vec{n}(y)=0

for all
y\in\partial{\mathcal{D}}
.

Remark 4
.

Notice that this system extends the L-V competition model with mutation by incorporating two non-local additional terms representing vector-borne dispersal. Moreover, the system also keeps track of the uncharged and charged vector populations simultaneously with that of the viruses. Finally, this representation allows us to consider a continuous trait space
{\mathcal{X}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. This structure can be used for example to study the the adaptation of viruses to resistant genes of plants
[
10
,
11
]
.

Second regime

For the second regime, under the same assumptions on the sequence of initial measures, the following result is a consequence of Theorem
3.3
. It shows how the speeding up of the diffusion of vectors has the effect of changing the limiting evolution of the two populations of vectors to an equilibrium state.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{(K)}_{0}=({\nu_{v}}^{(K)}(0),{\nu_{u}}^{(K)}(0),{\nu_{c}}^{(K)}(0))
be such that:

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(\langle 1,{\nu_{v}}^{(k)}(0)\rangle+\langle 1,{\nu_{u}}^{(k)}(0)\rangle+\langle 1,{\nu_{c}}^{(k)}(0)\rangle\right)^{3}\right]<\infty

Then, the process
\{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{(K)}(t):t\geq 0\}
converges, as
K\to\infty
, to a deterministic process
\xi_{t}=(\xi_{v}(t),\xi_{u}(t),\xi_{c}(t))
with densities
(g_{v},g_{u},g_{c})
being solution of the following system:

\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial t}g_{v}(t,x,z)&=(1-\mu)b(x,z)\,g_{v}(t,x,z)+\mu\int_{\mathcal{X}}b(x,z^{\prime}\mkern-1.2mu)m(z^{\prime},z)\,g_{v}(t,x,z^{\prime}\mkern-1.2mu)\,dz^{\prime}\\
&-\left[d(z)+c\,\left(\int_{\mathcal{X}}g_{v}(t,x,z^{\prime})dz^{\prime}\mkern-1.2mu\right)\right]g_{v}(t,x,z)\\
&-\left[\int_{\mathcal{D}}\beta\left(y,x,\int_{\mathcal{X}}g_{v}(t,x,z^{\prime})dz^{\prime},z\right)g_{u}(t,y)\,dy\right]\,g_{v}(t,x,z)+\left(\int_{{\mathcal{D}}}\eta(y,x,z)\,g_{c}(t,y,z)\,dy\right),\\
\Delta g_{u}(t,y)&-\left[\int_{E\times{\mathcal{X}}}\beta\left(y,x,\int_{\mathcal{X}}g_{v}(t,x,z^{\prime})dz^{\prime},z\right)\,g_{v}(t,x,z)\,\zeta_{E}(dx)\,dz\right]\,g_{u}(t,y)+\int_{E\times{\mathcal{X}}}\eta(y,x,z)\,g_{c}(t,y,z)\,\zeta_{E}(dx)\,dz=0,\\
\Delta_{y}g_{c}(t,y,z)&+\left[\int_{E}\beta\left(y,x,\int_{\mathcal{X}}g_{v}(t,x,z^{\prime})dz^{\prime},z\right)\,g_{v}(t,x,z)\,\zeta_{E}(dx)\right]\,g_{u}(t,y)-\left(\int_{E}\eta(y,x,z)\,\zeta_{E}(dx)\right)\,g_{c}(t,y,z)=0,\end{aligned}\right.

with the same boundary conditions as in Theorem
1.5
.

Perspectives on extension to a continuous set of plants

As a final remark, we want to mention that in Theorem
1.5
and Theorem
1.6
we have deliberately used the notation:

\int_{E}f(x)\,\zeta_{E}(dx),

instead of the perhaps more natural:

\sum_{x_{i}\in E}f(x_{i}).

Our intention with this choice was to suggest in particular the possible extension of our results to the case in which the set of plants becomes increasingly large together with the rescaling parameter

. To be more precise, when the locations of plants form and increasing sequence of lattices

approximating the whole plantation space
{\mathcal{D}}
(and the measures
\zeta_{E_{K}}
approximating the Lebesgue measure
dx
) as

. We suspect that Theorems
3.1

and
3.3
can be extended to that setting.

Local persistence for the first regime

2.
Extinction probabilities

We are interested in the probability of extinction of the virus population, i.e., the probability

{\mathbb{P}}_{\nu(0)}(\exists s>0,P_{v}(s)=0),

where the process is initially started from the measure
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}(0)=({\nu_{v}}(0),{\nu_{u}}(0),{\nu_{c}}(0))
.

We first make the following remarks:

Remark 5
.

Notice that the random variable
P_{v}(t)
does not make distinction about the different phenotypes. Moreover, it also takes into account the sub-population of viruses residing on vectors. This is needed to properly study extinction since it avoids the possibility of re-emergence of the virus population.

Remark 6
.

The random process
\{P_{v}(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
is integer valued, in fact it is
({\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\})
-valued. Moreover, zero is an absorbing state, i.e, if for some
s\geq 0
,
P_{v}(s)=0
, then
P_{v}(t)=0
for all
t\geq s
.

Remark 7
.

Notice that by Remark
3
we have:

N_{u}(t)+N_{c}(t)=:V_{0}

for all
t\geq 0
.

Before proving Theorem
1.4
, we need the following lemma:

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\nu_{v}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{p}({{\mathcal{V}}_{p}})
, then we have:

(2.1)

\int_{{{\mathcal{V}}_{p}}}\left[\int_{{\mathcal{X}}}1\cdot{\nu_{v}}^{x}(t)(dz^{\prime}\mkern-1.2mu)\right]{\nu_{v}}(t)(dx,dz)\geq\frac{1}{\lvert E\rvert}N_{v}(t)^{2},

where
\lvert E\rvert
denotes the cardinality of the finite set
E
.

Proof.

For
{\nu_{v}}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{p}({{\mathcal{V}}_{p}})
, let us denote by
\bar{\nu}_{v}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{p}(E)
the following measure:

\bar{\nu}_{v}(dx)=\int_{X}1\,\nu_{v}(dx,dz).

Notice then that we can rewrite the LHS of (
2.1
) as follows:

\int_{E\times{\mathcal{X}}}\left[\int_{{\mathcal{X}}}1\cdot\nu_{v}^{x}(t)(dz^{\prime}\mkern-1.2mu)\right]\nu_{v}(t)(dx,dz)=\int_{E}\int_{E}\mathds{1}_{\left\{x\right\}}(x^{\prime}\mkern-1.2mu)\cdot\bar{\nu}_{v}^{x}(t)(dx^{\prime}\mkern-1.2mu)\,\bar{\nu}_{v}^{x}(t)(dx)

We conclude the proof of the lemma by using the fact that the set
E
is finite, and the fundamental inequality:

n\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{2}\geq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\right)^{2}

for
a_{i}\geq 0
.
∎

We now can proceed to the proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Proof.

Let us first claim:

(2.2)

\sup_{t\geq 0}\,{\mathbb{E}}\left[P_{v}(t)\right]<\infty.

To see that this is the case, let us define
f(t)
as follows:

f(t):={\mathbb{E}}\left[P_{v}(t)\right]={\mathbb{E}}\left(\langle\nu_{v}(t),1\rangle+\langle{\nu_{c}}(t),1\rangle\right).

By Proposition
A.7
we have

\displaystyle f(t)

\displaystyle=f(0)+\int_{0}^{t}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{{{\mathcal{V}}_{p}}}b(x,z)\nu_{v}(s)(dx,dz)-\int_{{{\mathcal{V}}_{p}}}\left(d(z)+c\langle\nu_{v}^{x},1\rangle\right)\nu_{v}(s)(dx,dz)\right]ds,

and as a consequence we obtain the differentiability of
f
. Moreover, we also have:

f^{\prime}\mkern-1.2mu(t)\leq(\bar{b}-\hat{d})f(t)-c\cdot{\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{{{\mathcal{V}}_{p}}}\langle\nu_{v}^{x}(t),1\rangle\nu_{v}(t)(dx,dz)\right].

where
\bar{b}
is given in Assumption
1
, and
\hat{d}:=\inf_{z\in{\mathcal{X}}}d(z)
.

By Jensen’s inequality we have:

\displaystyle f(t)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}\left[N_{v}(t)^{2}+2N_{c}(t)N_{v}(t)+N_{c}(t)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}\left[N_{v}(t)^{2}+2V_{0}\right(N_{v}(t)+N_{c}(t)\left)\right]

which can be rewritten as:

{\mathbb{E}}\left[N_{v}(t)^{2}\right]\geq f(t)^{2}-2V_{0}f(t),

This, together with Lemma
2.1
, implies

f^{\prime}\mkern-1.2mu(t)\leq\left((\bar{b}-\hat{d})+\frac{2cV_{0}}{\lvert E\rvert}\right)f(t)-\frac{c}{\lvert E\rvert}f(t)^{2}.

From the observation that the function:

y(x)=\left((\bar{b}-\hat{d})+\frac{2cV_{0}}{\lvert E\rvert}\right)x-\frac{c}{\lvert E\rvert}x^{2}

is negative for any
x
such that

x\geq x_{0}:=\lvert E\rvert\frac{(\bar{b}-\hat{d})}{c}+2V_{0},

we deduce that

f(t)\leq f(0)\vee x_{0}

for all
t\geq 0
. This implies (
2.2
).

Now we claim that:

\lim_{t\to\infty}\,P_{v}(t)\in\{0,\infty\}.

Using the fact that
P_{v}(t)
is
{\mathbb{N}}
-valued, it is enough to check that for any
M\in{\mathbb{N}}
we have

{\mathbb{P}}\left[\liminf_{t\to\infty}\,P_{v}(t)=M\right]=0.

Assume that it is not the case, and that
\liminf_{t\to\infty}\,P_{v}(t)=M
. By definition of
\liminf
, this implies that
P_{v}(t)
reaches the value
M
infinitely often, but the value
M-1
only a finite number of times. However, this is almost surely impossible since every time that the process
P_{v}
is at state
M
, the probability of going to state
(M-1)
is bounded from below by:

\frac{\hat{d}M}{\bar{b}M+\bar{d}M+cM^{2}\bar{\gamma}V_{0}}>0,

where we recall that
\hat{d}>0
. We only have to use the fact that
\{0\}
is an absorbing state to deduce that the limit exists and

\lim_{t\to\infty}P_{v}(t)\in\{0,\infty\}.

To conclude that a.s.

\lim_{t\to\infty}\,P_{v}(t)=0,

it is enough to show that

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\lim_{t\to\infty}P_{v}(t)\right]<\infty.

However, this is a consequence of Fatou’s lemma, expression (
2.2
), and the following reasoning:

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\lim_{t\to\infty}P_{v}(t)\right]={\mathbb{E}}\left[\liminf_{t\to\infty}P_{v}(t)\right]\leq\liminf_{t\to\infty}\,{\mathbb{E}}\left[P_{v}(t)\right]\leq\sup_{t\geq 0}\,{\mathbb{E}}\left[P_{v}(t)\right]<\infty.

∎

.

Rescaled processes

Let us denote by
\Lambda_{K}(t)
the measure-valued process of the form:

(3.1)

\Lambda_{K}(t)=({\nu_{v}}^{(K)}(t),{\nu_{u}}^{(K)}(t),{\nu_{c}}^{(K)}(t)),

{\nu_{v}}^{(K)}(t),{\nu_{u}}^{(K)}(t)

Under Assumption

, the process
\Lambda_{K}(t)

{\mathcal{L}}^{(K)}
, with jump part
{\mathcal{L}}_{1}^{(K)}
given by:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}_{1}^{(K)}F_{{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}({\boldsymbol{\nu}})

\displaystyle=K(1-\mu)\int_{{{\mathcal{V}}_{p}}}b(x,z)\left[F_{{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}(({\nu_{v}}+\tfrac{1}{K}\delta_{x,z},{\nu_{u}},{\nu_{c}}))-F_{{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}({\boldsymbol{\nu}})\right]{\nu_{v}}(dx,dz)

\displaystyle+K\int_{{{\mathcal{V}}_{c}}}\int_{E}\eta(t,w,x,e)\,\left[F_{{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}(({\nu_{v}}+\tfrac{1}{K}\delta_{x,e},{\nu_{u}}+\tfrac{1}{K^{\lambda}}\delta_{w},{\nu_{c}}-\tfrac{1}{K^{\lambda}}\delta_{w,e}))-F_{{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}({\boldsymbol{\nu}})\right]{\nu_{c}}(dw,de)\,dx

\displaystyle+K\int_{{{\mathcal{V}}_{c}}}\gamma(e)\,\left[F_{{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}(({\nu_{v}},{\nu_{u}}+\tfrac{1}{K^{\lambda}}\delta_{w},{\nu_{c}}-\tfrac{1}{K^{\lambda}}\delta_{w,e}))-F_{{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}({\boldsymbol{\nu}})\right]{\nu_{c}}(dw,de),

and a diffusive part:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}_{2}^{(K)}F_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}({\boldsymbol{\nu}})

\displaystyle:=K^{1-\lambda}\left(\int_{{\mathcal{D}}}{\mathcal{L}}_{y}^{u}{\phi_{u}}(y){\nu_{u}}(dy)+\int_{{{\mathcal{V}}_{c}}}{{\mathcal{L}}^{c}}{\phi_{c}}(w,e){\nu_{c}}(dw,de)\right)\,F_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{\prime}\mkern-1.2mu({\boldsymbol{\nu}})

\displaystyle+K^{1-2\lambda}\left(\int_{{\mathcal{D}}}\frac{(\sigma^{u}(y))^{2}}{2}|\nabla_{y}{\phi_{u}}(y)|^{2}{\nu_{u}}(dy)+\int_{{{\mathcal{V}}_{c}}}\frac{(\sigma^{c}(w))^{2}}{2}|\nabla_{w}{\phi_{c}}(w,e)|^{2}{\nu_{c}}(dw,de)\right)\,F_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{\prime\prime}\mkern-1.2mu({\boldsymbol{\nu}}).

Population at time zero

At time zero we consider a sequence of initial measures
\left\{\Lambda^{K}(0)\right\}_{\{k\geq 1\}}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{F}
of the form:

\Lambda^{(K)}(0)=({\nu_{v}}^{(K)}(0),{\nu_{u}}^{(K)}(0),{\nu_{c}}^{(K)}(0)).

We assume that this measure satisfies an estimate like the one needed in Proposition

, and that a law of large numbers is satisfied by the sequence
\left\{\Lambda^{K}(0)\right\}_{\{k\geq 1\}}
. More precisely we make the following assumption:

Assumption 3
.

The sequence of measures
\left\{\Lambda^{K}(0)\right\}_{\{K\geq 1\}}
converges in law and for the weak topology of
{\mathcal{M}}_{F}
to a deterministic finite measure
\xi_{0}=(\xi_{v}(0),\xi_{S}(0),\xi_{I}(0))\in{\mathcal{M}}_{F}.
Moreover we have the following estimate:

\sup_{K\in{\mathbb{N}}}\left({\mathbb{E}}\left[\langle\nu_{\alpha}^{K}(0),1\rangle^{3}\right]\right)<\infty,

for all
\alpha\in\{v,u,c\}
.

The IDE formulation, given by Theorem
3.1
below, can be thought of as a law of large numbers, for the limit of the sequence of measures
\left\{\Lambda_{K}(t):t\geq 0\right\}_{K\geq 1}
, seen as taking values in the path space
\mathbb{D}([0,T],{\mathcal{M}}_{F})
, for all
T>0
. Obtaining such a description requires to work with martingales associated to a Markov process and to control their quadratic variation. We will split the analysis of the martingales the two cases already described in Section
1
.

Remark 8
.

It is possible to find combinations of rescaling of parameters, different from those of Assumption
2
, such that the speeding of diffusion by vectors becomes unnecessary to derive a limiting description for the case
\lambda<1
. However, for such a combination, the limiting evolution equation for the population of viruses decouples from that of the vectors (i.e. viruses do not see the effect of vectors). Such a description becomes biologically irrelevant in the context of vector-borne dynamics.

3.1.
IDE formulation: first case

We have the following theorem:

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\lambda=1
. Consider the sequence of measure-valued processes
\left\{\Lambda_{K}\right\}_{K\geq 1}
given by (
3.1
)-(
1.8
). Suppose Assumptions
2
-
3
are satisfied. Then, for all
T>0
, the sequence of processes
\left\{\Lambda_{K}\right\}_{K\geq 1}
converges in law in
\mathbb{D}([0,T],{\mathcal{M}}_{F})
to a deterministic continuous function
\xi(t)=\left({\xi_{v}}(t),{\xi_{u}}(t),{\xi_{c}}(t)\right)
belonging to the path space
\mathbb{C}([0,T],{\mathcal{M}}_{F})
, and solving the following integro-differential equations:

\displaystyle\langle{\xi_{v}}(t),{\phi_{v}}\rangle

\left({\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}=(\nu_{v}(t),{\nu_{u}}(t),{\nu_{c}}(t);t\geq 0)
be a solution of (
A.3
) such that for all
T>0
we have

{\mathbb{E}}\left(\sup_{t\leq T}<\mathbf{1},{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{t}>^{2}\right)<\infty,

where
\mathbf{1}\in\Phi({\mathcal{M}}_{p})
denotes the admissible triplet with all functions equal to the constant 1. Then, under Assumption
1
, the process
\left({\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
is Markov with infinitesimal generator
{\mathcal{L}}
given by (
1.3
).

Proof.

The fact that it is Markov is standard. We need to verify that the generator is the one we claimed. We will use Itô’s lemma (see for example Theorem 5.1 in
[
17
]
) applied to (
A.3
) to find an expression for
F_{{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}({\boldsymbol{\nu}}(t))
. Let us split this in two, one part coming from jump events, and the other from diffusion, as follows:

F_{{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}({\boldsymbol{\nu}}(t))-F_{{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}({\boldsymbol{\nu}}(0))=(F_{{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}({\boldsymbol{\nu}}(t))-F_{{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}({\boldsymbol{\nu}}(0)))_{\text{Jump}}+(F_{{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}({\boldsymbol{\nu}}(t))-F_{{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}({\boldsymbol{\nu}}(0)))_{\text{Diff}}

The way to verify that this corresponds to the generator
{\mathcal{L}}
is to take expectations and later differentiate with respect to time. We refer to
[
23
]
, Proposition 6.3 in particular, for the same procedure in the absence of diffusion. Let us do this for the diffusive part of the generator. Notice that by Itô’s lemma, the diffusive part is given by:

\displaystyle(F_{{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}({\boldsymbol{\nu}}(t))

\displaystyle-F_{{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}({\boldsymbol{\nu}}(0)))_{\text{Diff}}=\int_{0}^{t}F_{{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}^{\prime}\mkern-1.2mu({\boldsymbol{\nu}}(s))\langle\mathcal{L}^{u}{\boldsymbol{\phi}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}}(s)\rangle\,ds+\int_{0}^{t}F_{{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}^{\prime}\mkern-1.2mu({\boldsymbol{\nu}}(s))\sum_{i=1}^{N_{u}(s^{-})}\sigma^{u}(Y^{u}_{i}(s^{-}))\nabla\phi_{u}(Y^{u}_{i}(s^{-}))\cdot dW^{i,u}_{s}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}F_{{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}^{\prime\prime}\mkern-1.2mu({\boldsymbol{\nu}}(s))\sum_{i=1}^{N_{u}(s^{-})}\sigma^{u}(Y^{u}_{i}(s^{-}))^{2}\mid\nabla\phi_{u}(Y^{u}_{i}(s^{-}))\mid^{2}\,ds+\int_{0}^{t}F_{{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}^{\prime}\mkern-1.2mu({\boldsymbol{\nu}}(s))\langle\mathcal{L}^{c}{\boldsymbol{\phi}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}}(s)\rangle\,ds

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}F_{{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}^{\prime}\mkern-1.2mu({\boldsymbol{\nu}}(s))\sum_{i=1}^{N_{c}(s^{-})}\sigma^{c}(Y^{c}_{i}(s^{-}))\nabla_{y}\,{\phi_{c}}(Y^{c}_{i}(s^{-}),z_{i}(s^{-}))\cdot dW^{i,c}_{s}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}F_{{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}^{\prime\prime}\mkern-1.2mu({\boldsymbol{\nu}}(s))\sum_{i=1}^{N_{c}(s^{-})}\sigma^{c}(Y^{c}_{i}(s^{-}))^{2}\mid\nabla_{y}\,{\phi_{c}}(Y^{c}_{i}(s^{-}),z_{i}(s^{-}))\mid^{2}\,ds.

In order to verify that we get the second part of the generator
{\mathcal{L}}
, we have to proceed as before. Notice however, that by taking expectations the Itô integrals vanish. Differentiating what is left at
t=0
leads to the RHS of (
1
).
∎

Now we show the well-definedness of the process
({\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
. That is, Theorem
1.3
. Moreover we show that a control of the
p
-th moment at time zero can be extended to later times. More precisely, we show the following proposition:

Proposition A.5
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{0}=({\nu_{v}}(0),{\nu_{u}}(0),{\nu_{c}}(0))
be such that for some
p\geq 1
, we have:

(A.3)

\mathbb{E}\left(\langle\mathbf{1},{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{0}\rangle^{p}\right)<\infty

Then, under Assumption
1
, the process
\left({\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}=\left(\nu_{v}(t),{\nu_{u}}(t),{\nu_{c}}(t):t\geq 0\right)
satisfies:

(A.4)

\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\langle\mathbf{1},{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{t}\rangle^{p}\right)<\infty

In particular, if (
A.3
) holds for
p=1
, we also have that the process
\nu_{t}
is well defined.

Proof.

To show (
A.4
), because the number of vectors is invariant under the dynamics, it is enough to show:

\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\langle 1,{\nu_{v}}(t)\rangle^{p}\right)<\infty.

In order to do so, we use a stopping time argument. Let us define
\tau_{n}
as follows:

\tau_{n}=\inf\left\{t\geq 0:\langle 1,{\nu_{v}}(t)\rangle\geq n\right\}

Let us also consider
F_{p}:{\mathcal{M}}_{p}^{\alpha}\to{\mathbb{R}}
given by the choice:

F_{p}({\boldsymbol{\nu}})=\left(\langle 1,{\nu_{v}}\rangle\right)^{p}+\left(\langle 1,{\nu_{c}}\rangle\right)^{p}.

Hence, by (
A.3
) we have:

\displaystyle\sup_{s\in[0,t\wedge\tau_{n}]}

\displaystyle\langle 1,{\nu_{v}}(s)\rangle^{p}\leq\langle 1,{\nu_{v}}(0)\rangle^{p}

\displaystyle\hskip-22.76228pt+\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{N}}^{*}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\left[(\langle 1,{\nu_{v}}(s-)\rangle+1)^{p}-\langle 1,{\nu_{v}}(s-)\rangle^{p}\right]\,\mathds{1}_{\left\{i\leq N_{v}(s^{-})\right\}}\mathds{1}_{\left\{\theta\leq(1-\mu)b(x_{i}(s^{-}),z_{i}(s^{-}))\right\}}Q_{cb}(ds\,di\,d\theta)

\displaystyle\hskip-22.76228pt+\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{N}}^{*}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times E}\left[(\langle 1,{\nu_{v}}(s-)\rangle+1)^{p}-\langle 1,{\nu_{v}}(s-)\rangle^{p}\right]\,\mathds{1}_{\left\{j\leq N_{c}(s^{-})\right\}}\mathds{1}_{\left\{\theta\leq\eta(s^{-},Y_{j}(s^{-}),x,z_{j}(s^{-}))\right\}}Q_{dis}(ds\,dx\,dj\,d\theta)

\displaystyle\hskip-22.76228pt+\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{N}}^{*}\times\mathcal{X}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\left[(\langle 1,{\nu_{v}}(s-)\rangle+1)^{p}-\langle 1,{\nu_{v}}(s-)\rangle^{p}\right]\mathds{1}_{\left\{i\leq N_{v}(s^{-})\right\}}\mathds{1}_{\left\{\theta\leq\mu b(x_{i}(s^{-}),z_{i}(s^{-}))m(z_{i}(s^{-}),z)\right\}}Q_{bm}(ds\,di\,dz\,d\theta).

where the diffusion terms vanished due to the presences of derivatives of the constant function
1
, and we have dropped the integral terms with a negative contribution (death terms, and loading terms).

Taking expectations and using the bounds given by Assumption
1
we obtain:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sup_{s\in[0,t\wedge\tau_{n}]}\langle 1,{\nu_{v}}(s)\rangle^{p}\right]

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}\left[\langle 1,{\nu_{v}}(0)\rangle^{p}\right]+C{\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{n}}N_{v}(s)\left[(\langle 1,{\nu_{v}}(s)\rangle+1)^{p}-\langle 1,{\nu_{v}}(s)\rangle^{p}\right]\,\right]\,ds

\displaystyle+C{\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{n}}\left[(\langle 1,{\nu_{v}}(s)\rangle+1)^{p}-\langle 1,{\nu_{v}}(s)\rangle^{p}\right]\right]\,ds.

\displaystyle\leq C\left(1+{\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{n}}N_{v}(s)\left[(\langle 1,{\nu_{v}}(s)\rangle+1)^{p}-\langle 1,{\nu_{v}}(s)\rangle^{p}\right]\,ds\right]\right)

where the constant
C
changed its value incorporating the constant given by (
A.3
).

Using the fact that
N_{v}(s)\leq\langle 1,{\nu_{v}}(s)\rangle
, and the simple inequality
(x+1)^{p}-x^{p}\leq C_{p}(1+x^{p-1})
, we obtain:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sup_{s\in[0,t\wedge\tau_{n}]}\langle 1,{\nu_{v}}(s)\rangle^{p}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C_{p}\left(1+{\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\left[\langle 1,{\nu_{v}}(s\wedge\tau_{n})\rangle+\langle 1,{\nu_{v}}(s\wedge\tau_{n})\rangle^{p}\right]\,ds\right]\right)

\displaystyle\leq C_{p}\left(1+{\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\langle 1,{\nu_{v}}(s\wedge\tau_{n})\rangle^{p}\,ds\right]\right)

where again the constant
C_{p}
changed its value incorporating new constants.

By Gronwalls inequality we then have:

(A.5)

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sup_{s\in[0,t\wedge\tau_{n}]}\langle 1,{\nu_{v}}(s)\rangle^{p}\right]\leq C_{p}e^{C_{p}t}

for some constant
C_{p}
independent of
n
.

From (
A.5
) we can deduce that
\tau_{n}
goes to infinity as
n\to\infty
a.s. We then apply Fatou’s lemma to conclude:

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\langle 1,{\nu_{v}}(s)\rangle^{p}\right]\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sup_{s\in[0,t\wedge\tau_{n}]}\langle 1,{\nu_{v}}(s)\rangle^{p}\right]\leq C_{p}e^{C_{p}t}.

To conclude the well-definedness of the process
\nu_{t}
one has to construct the process step by step, where the time steps are given by a sequence of jump instants
T_{n}
exponentially distributed with law:

R(\nu_{n-1})e^{-R(\nu_{n-1})t}

and where the total jump rate
R(\nu)
is given by (
A
).

It is then enough to check that the sequence
T_{n}
goes to infinity almost surely. This follows from

{\mathbb{E}}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\langle 1,{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{t}\rangle\right)<\infty

which is a consequence of (
A.4
) when
p=1
.
∎
