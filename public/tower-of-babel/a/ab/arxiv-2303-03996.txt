Optical polaron formation in quantum systems with permanent dipoles
Adam Burgess, Marian Florescu, Dominic M. Rouse

Abstract
Many optically active systems possess spatially asymmetric electron orbitals. These generate permanent dipole moments, which can be stronger than the corresponding transition dipole moments, significantly affecting the system dynamics and creating polarised Fock states of light. We derive a master equation for these systems by employing an optical polaron transformation that captures the photon mode polarisation induced by the permanent dipoles. This provides an intuitive framework to explore their influence on the system dynamics and emission spectrum. We find that permanent dipoles introduce multiple-photon processes and a photon sideband which causes substantial modifications to single-photon transition dipole processes. In the presence of an external drive, permanent dipoles lead to an additional process that we show can be exploited to optimise the decoherence and transition rates. We derive the emission spectrum of the system, highlighting experimentally detectable signatures of optical polarons, and measurements that can identify the parameters in the system Hamiltonian, the magnitude of the differences in the permanent dipoles, and the steady-state populations of the system.

Furthermore, most studies neglect an additional identity interaction also induced by the permanent dipoles, which, as we show here, modifies the initial state of the environment and can have a significant impact on the system dynamics. Consequently, such treatments do not capture the role of strong permanent dipoles in asymmetric systems under illumination by multimode fields, such as common thermal fields.

In this paper, we utilise a polaron transformation to derive a master equation for systems with strong permanent dipoles interacting with a thermal field. Importantly, we make no assumptions about the dipole matrix beyond perturbative
transition
dipoles. The polaron transformation is a unitary, state-dependent displacement transformation widely used when dealing with strong, pure dephasing interactions. In the polaron frame, pure dephasing interactions are absorbed into the definition of the basis and treated to all orders in the coupling strength. The basis describes an optical polaron quasiparticle, which is a hybridisation of the matter excitations and the displaced harmonic oscillator states of the multi-mode photonic field. These photonic states, called polarised Fock states, correspond to a non-zero (polarised) vector potential field and were first introduced to explain how permanent dipoles can generate multiphoton conversion in a single-mode cavity
Mandal et al. 2020a
and are useful in polaritonic chemistry

;
Mandal et al. 2023

The optical polaron formalism provides us with an essential intuition for the very complex phenomena introduced by the permanent dipoles, allowing us to unpick the role of permanent dipoles in open quantum system dynamics. We find that the formation of optical polarons results in unique physical phenomena such as modifications to single-photon transition dipole processes, entirely new multiple photon processes, the appearance of photonic sidebands, and a novel interplay with external driving that allows control over the dynamics. We also derive the emission spectrum for the system, highlighting experimentally detectable signatures of the optical polarons.

The model and polaron frame

III
Effects of strong permanent dipoles

In this section we derive the secularised PFME, provide a brief analytical review of it, and analyse the population transfer rates, decoherence rate, and Lamb shift appearing in the master equation, which are given by Fourier transforms of the environment correlation functions (ECFs). This will allow us to analytically explore the role of permanent dipoles.

(32b)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{V,1}(\omega)

\displaystyle\propto\Omega_{\mu\Delta}\left|V\right|\cos\left(\vartheta_{\mu V}\right)\cos(\theta_{\mu\Delta}),

(32c)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{V,0}(\omega)

\displaystyle\propto\left|V\right|^{2}.

(32d)

From Eqs. (
32
) we see that when the system is driven,
\Gamma(\omega)
is not an even function of
\theta_{\mu\Delta}
or
\vartheta_{\mu V}
. We will later show that this can be utilised to control the system, for example to minimise decoherence. Moreover, for perpendicular dipoles
\cos(\theta_{\mu\Delta})=0
, so
\Gamma(\omega)
becomes equivalent to the analogous function in the driven spin boson model where the transition dipole and permanent dipole interactions are with independent baths. Interactions proportional to
\cos(\theta_{\mu\Delta})
are therefore arise because the interactions are non-commutative.

The
\Gamma_{V,1}(\omega)
contribution in Eq. (
47
) varies cosinusoidally with the relative complex phase
\vartheta_{\mu V}
. This means that through controlling the phase of
V
one can determine the phase
\vartheta_{\mu}
of
\mathbf{d}_{\mu}
, for example by minimising the spectral linewidth, allowing an estimation of the relative phase between the excited and ground state. This will work provided that
V
is not generated via a coupling to the system’s transition dipole moments, in which case
\vartheta_{V}=\vartheta_{\mu}+\vartheta_{\text{external}}
and so
\vartheta_{\mu V}
would only depend on the phase of the external drive.

For additional context, when
|V|\ll\epsilon
– the parameter regime in which polaron theory is valid – the eigenbasis angle is
\varphi\approx 0
and so
g_{z}\approx 0
and
g_{+}\approx\mathcal{C}
. In this limit, the secular approximation used in the master equation in Eqs. (
26
) becomes exact, and
\gamma_{\uparrow}\approx 2\Re[\Gamma(-\eta)]
with
\Gamma(\omega)
in Eq. (
31
) and
\gamma_{\downarrow}\approx\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{d}s\ \mathrm{e}^{i\eta s}\langle\mathcal{C}(s)\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}(0)\rangle
which, as we show in Appendix
D
, evaluates to
\gamma_{\downarrow}\approx 2\Re[\bar{\Gamma}(\eta)]
where
\bar{\Gamma}(\omega)
is equal to Eq. (
31
) but with
\Gamma_{V,1}(\omega)\to-\Gamma_{V,1}(\omega)
. (Note that this asymmetry cannot lead to population inversion due to minus signs within
\Gamma_{V,1}(\omega)
.)

We will now discuss the four contributions to Eq. (
31
) in turn. Each are derived in Appendix
D
.

III.1
One photon processes,
\Gamma_{1}(\omega)

The function describing driving-independent one photon processes is

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1}(\omega)=\pi\Omega_{\mu\mu}\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{d}\nu\ \big[

\displaystyle J_{A}(\nu)\mathcal{K}(\omega+\nu)

\displaystyle\qquad+J_{E}(\nu)\mathcal{K}(\omega-\nu)\big],

(33)

where

\mathcal{K}(\varepsilon)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{d}s\ \mathrm{e}^{i\varepsilon s}\mathrm{e}^{\phi(s)-\phi(0)},

(34)

contains the influence of the permanent dipoles within this term, and we have introduced the absorption and emission spectral densities,

\displaystyle J_{A}(\nu)

\displaystyle=J(\nu)N(\nu),

(35)

\displaystyle J_{E}(\nu)

\displaystyle=J(\nu)\tilde{N}(\nu),

(36)

where
N(\nu)=1/[\exp(\beta\nu)-1]
is the Bose-Einstein distribution and
\tilde{N}(\nu)=N(\nu)+1
will be a useful notation throughout this paper.

To understand the physical processes associated with this term, it is convenient to temporarily ignore the factor of
\exp[\phi(s)-\phi(0)]
in Eq. (
34
). The resulting term is the typical function appearing in the standard optical master equation (SOME)
Breuer et al. 2002
- our model in the absence of permanent dipoles and driving - which is

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\text{SOME}}(\omega)=\Omega_{\mu\mu}\bigg[\pi\left(J_{A}(-\omega)+J_{E}(\omega)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+i\mathcal{P}\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{d}\nu\ \left(\frac{J_{A}(\nu)}{\omega+\nu}+\frac{J_{E}(\nu)}{\omega-\nu}\right)\bigg],

(37)

where
\mathcal{P}
denotes the principal value.
Clearly, when
\omega=-\eta
, twice the real part of
\Gamma_{\text{SOME}}(\omega)
describes excitation-by-absorption at a rate
2\pi\Omega_{\mu\mu}J_{A}(\eta)
, and when
\omega=\eta
it describes decay-by-emission at a rate
2\pi\Omega_{\mu\mu}J_{E}(\eta)
. The imaginary part of
\Gamma_{\text{SOME}}(\omega)
will determine the Lamb shift.

We now return to Eq. (
III.1
). In Ref.
Rouse et al. 2022
an analytic solution to Eq. (
34
) was found by exploiting the fact that
\mathcal{K}(\varepsilon)
only depends on
J(\nu)
through its moments
\mu_{m}=4\Omega_{\Delta\Delta}\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{d}\nu\ [J(\nu)/\nu^{2}]\nu^{m}
for
m=1,2,\ldots,\infty
, and that moments of lower order contribute relatively more. We can then evaluate
\mathcal{K}(\varepsilon)
by replacing
J(\nu)
with a truncated spectral density
J^{\prime}(\nu)=\sum_{\mathbf{k}=1}^{N_{*}}|f^{\prime}_{\mathbf{k}}|^{2}\delta(\nu-\nu_{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime})
, as long as we choose the coupling strengths
\{f_{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}\}
and energies
\{\nu_{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}\}
of the modes such that
J^{\prime}(\nu)/\nu^{2}
has the same lowest moments as
\mu_{m}
for
m=1,2,\ldots,2N_{*}

Rouse et al. 2022
. The solution converges rapidly for increasing
N_{*}
, and a single-mode truncation is often very accurate, with truncation mode parameters:
\nu^{\prime}_{1}\equiv\nu_{s}=\mu_{2}/\mu_{1}
and
f^{\prime}_{1}\equiv f_{s}=\mu_{2}^{1/2}\propto\Omega_{\Delta\Delta}^{1/2}

Rouse et al. 2022
. A single-mode truncation captures all of the essential physics described by
\mathcal{K}(\omega)
. Thus, for the analytical analysis in this work, we use a single-mode truncation to evaluate Eq. (
34
); however, in all of the simulations in this paper, we increase
N_{*}
until convergence.

From Ref.
Rouse et al. 2022
, the single mode solution of Eq. (
34
) is

\mathcal{K}(\varepsilon)=\sum_{\ell=-\infty}^{\infty}A_{\ell}\left[\delta(\varepsilon-\ell\nu_{s})+\frac{i}{\pi}\frac{\mathcal{P}}{\varepsilon-\ell\nu_{s}}\right],

(38)

where

A_{\ell}=\sum_{n=|\ell|}^{\infty^{\prime}}\sum_{m=\frac{n-\ell}{2}}^{n}\binom{n}{m}\binom{m}{m-\frac{n-\ell}{2}}W_{n}V_{m},

(39)

and the prime on the first summation indicates that only every other term is included, i.e.
n=|\ell|,|\ell|+2,\ldots,
and
W_{n}=S^{n}_{s}\exp[-S_{s}]/n!
is the Franck-Condon factor of the mode in the truncation,
S_{s}=|f_{s}|^{2}/\nu_{s}^{2}=\mu_{1}^{2}/\mu_{2}
is its Huang-Rhys factor and
V_{m}=N(\nu_{s})^{m}\exp[-2S_{s}N(\nu_{s})]

Rouse et al. 2022
.
A_{\ell}
has the normalisation property
\sum_{\ell=-\infty}^{\infty}A_{\ell}=1
, is maximised for
\ell=\text{Round}(S^{\prime})
, becomes
A_{\ell}=\delta_{\ell 0}
if
|\tilde{\mathbf{d}}_{\Delta}|=0
, and the
\ell<0
terms are only non-zero at finite temperature (see Ref.
Rouse et al. 2022
for a full discussion on the mode truncation solution).

Substituting Eq. (
38
) into Eq. (
III.1
) yields

\Gamma_{1}(\omega)=\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{1}(\omega)+iS_{1}(\omega),

(40)

where

\gamma_{1}(\omega)=2\pi\Omega_{\mu\mu}\sum_{\ell=-\infty}^{\infty}A_{\ell}\Big[J_{A}(\ell\nu_{s}-\omega)+J_{E}(\omega-\ell\nu_{s})\Big],

(41)

and

\displaystyle S_{1}(\omega)=i\Omega_{\mu\mu}

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=-\infty}^{\infty}A_{\ell}\mathcal{P}\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{d}\nu\

\displaystyle\times\bigg(\frac{J_{A}(\nu)}{\omega+\nu-\ell\nu_{s}}+\frac{J_{E}(\nu)}{\omega-\nu-\ell\nu_{s}}\bigg).

(42)

Eqs. (
41
) and (
III.1
) show that the effect of the permanent dipoles within
\Gamma_{1}(\omega)
is to introduce manifolds of harmonic levels (in the single mode truncation of
\mathcal{K}(\varepsilon)
there is only one manifold) to/from which transitions can occur, and which influence the Lamb shift value. This is commonly observed when vibrational displacement interactions occur simultaneously with transition dipole interactions and manifest in spectra as vibrational side bands and Stokes’s shift. However, in our model, this is a purely photonic effect and it results in a photon sideband in the spectrum of the system.
\Gamma_{\text{SOME}}(\omega)
in Eq. (
III.1
) is recovered from
\Gamma_{1}(\omega)
in the limit of no permanent dipoles because
A_{\ell}\to\delta_{\ell 0}
.

\ell=\ell_{*}

\text{Round}(\eta/\nu_{s})<\ell_{*}<\text{Round}(\eta/\nu_{s})+1

III.2
Two photon processes,
\Gamma_{2}(\omega)

We now consider the two photon processes in Eq. (
31
) that occur independent of the driving,
\Gamma_{2}(\omega)
. These processes require that the
\sigma_{z}
and
\sigma_{\pm}
do not commute, and so are unique to permanent dipole interactions. As shown in Appendix
D
, the rate function is

\displaystyle\Gamma_{2}(\omega)=

\displaystyle 4\kappa^{2}\Omega_{\mu\Delta}^{2}\cos^{2}\left(\theta_{\mu\Delta}\right)

\displaystyle\times\sum_{n,m\in\{-1,1\}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{d}s\ \mathrm{e}^{i\omega s+\phi(s)}\tilde{\chi}_{n,m}(s),

(43)

where

\tilde{\chi}_{n,m}(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{d}\nu\ \int_{0}^{\infty}\text{d}\nu^{\prime}\ \chi_{n,m}(\nu,\nu^{\prime})\mathrm{e}^{in\nu s}\mathrm{e}^{im\nu^{\prime}s},

(44)

is the inverse Fourier transform of
\chi_{n,m}(\nu,\nu^{\prime})
which we write in matrix notation as

\displaystyle\chi(\nu,\nu^{\prime})=\bar{J}(\nu)\bar{J}(\nu^{\prime})\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{N}(\nu)\tilde{N}(\nu^{\prime})&-\tilde{N}(\nu)N(\nu^{\prime})\\
-N(\nu)\tilde{N}(\nu^{\prime})&N(\nu)N(\nu^{\prime})\end{pmatrix},

(45)

where, for example,
\chi_{1,1}(\nu,\nu^{\prime})=4\Omega_{\mu\Delta}^{2}\bar{J}(\nu)\bar{J}(\nu^{\prime})N(\nu)N(\nu^{\prime})
. We have introduced the polarised spectral density,

\bar{J}(\nu)=\frac{J(\nu)}{\nu},

(46)

which will be relevant to all transitions resulting from permanent dipole interactions.

The two-frequency Fourier transform in Eq. (
44
) indicates that
\Gamma_{2}(\omega)
describes two simultaneous processes. From the phase factors in Eq. (
44
), when
n
or
m
indices are equal to
-1
we deal with emission processes, and when they are equal to
+1
we deal with absorption, into the frequency channel
\nu
and
\nu^{\prime}
for
n
and
m
, respectively. This interpretation is reinforced by the positions of the factors of
N(\nu)
,
N(\nu^{\prime})
,
\tilde{N}(\nu)
and
\tilde{N}(\nu^{\prime})
in the matrix in Eq. (
45
). Similar to the case of the one photon processes, the factor of
\exp[\phi(s)-\phi(0)]
in Eq. (
III.2
) will introduce a photon sideband to the overall process, again enabling processes with more photons.

III.3
Driving induced one photon processes,
\Gamma_{V,1}(\omega)

We now move onto the processes in Eq. (
31
) induced by the driving. The function for such one photon processes is

\displaystyle\Gamma_{V,1}

\displaystyle(\omega)=4\pi\Omega_{\mu\Delta}|V|\cos\left(\vartheta_{\mu V}\right)\cos\left(\theta_{\mu\Delta}\right)

(47)

\displaystyle\times\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{d}\nu\ \bigg(-\bar{J}_{A}(\nu)\mathcal{K}(\omega+\nu)+\bar{J}_{E}(\nu)\mathcal{K}(\omega-\nu)\bigg),

where
\mathcal{K}(\varepsilon)
is given in Eq. (
34
) and, analogously to Eqs. (
35
), we have introduced the polarised absorption and emission spectral densities,

\displaystyle\bar{J}_{A}(\nu)

\displaystyle=\bar{J}(\nu)N(\nu),

(48)

\displaystyle\bar{J}_{E}(\nu)

\displaystyle=\bar{J}(\nu)\tilde{N}(\nu),

(49)

where
\bar{J}(\nu)
is in Eq. (
46

\Gamma_{2}(\omega)

\Gamma_{V,1}(\omega)

\Gamma_{V,1}(\omega)=\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{V,1}(\omega)+iS_{V,1}(\omega),

\Gamma_{1}(\omega)

\cos(\vartheta_{\mu V})\cos(\theta_{\mu\Delta})

This term vanishes when

V
Emission spectrum

Due to the many new physical processes generated by the presence of permanent dipoles discussed in Sec.
III
we expect alterations to the emission spectrum. In the absence of permanent dipoles, the spectrum consists of a Mollow triplet with peaks at frequencies
-\eta,0,\eta
. When
V=0
, the triplet becomes a single peak at frequency
\epsilon
with a width determined by the decoherence rate in the standard optical master equation:
\pi J(\epsilon)[1+2N(\epsilon)]
. In the presence of strong permanent dipoles, we expect that there will be a photon sideband extending to negative frequencies. If
|\tilde{\mathbf{d}}_{\Delta}|
is large enough, the positions of the Mollow triplet peaks will shift noticeably from
\pm\eta
to
\pm\bar{\eta}
given in Eq. (
29
), and the widths of the peaks will be determined by
\gamma_{d}
in Eq. (
27b
).

As we prove in Appendix
F
, the emission spectrum is given by

I(\omega)=\alpha_{\text{prop}}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{R},\omega)I_{0}(\omega),

(52)

where
\alpha_{\text{prop}}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{R},\omega)=|\mathbf{d}_{\mu}\cdot\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{R},\omega)|^{2}
and
\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{R},\omega)
is the Green’s function of the medium
Roy-Choudhury and Hughes 2015
;
Iles-Smith et al. 2017
;
Hughes and Yao 2009
;
Bundgaard-Nielsen et al. 2021
;
Walls and Milburn 1994
.
\alpha_{\text{prop}}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{R},\omega)
accounts for propagation and filtering of the light from the dipole to the detector at positions
\mathbf{r}
and
\mathbf{R}
, respectively
Roy-Choudhury and Hughes 2015
. The polarisation spectrum is

I_{0}(\omega)=\left\langle\sigma^{+}(\omega)\sigma^{-}(\omega)\right\rangle,

(53)

or, equivalently,

I_{0}(\omega)=\lim_{t\to\infty}\Re\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{d}\tau\ \left\langle\sigma_{+}(t+\tau)\sigma_{-}(t)\right\rangle\mathrm{e}^{-i\omega\tau}\right].

(54)

In the following, we focus on evaluating the polarisation spectrum, which captures entirely the effects associated with the permanent dipoles. The specifics of the experimental set-up, for example, whether the dipole is coupled to a cavity or a waveguide, are described by the
\alpha_{\text{prop}}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{R},\omega)
term, which can be calculated separately.

The expectation value in Eq. (
54
) is taken with respect to the lab frame density operator
\rho_{l}
. Using
\rho_{l}=U^{\dagger}\rho_{p}U
where
U
is the polaron transformation in Eq. (
11
) and
\rho_{p}
is the polaron frame density operator, we have

\displaystyle\left\langle\sigma_{+}(t+\tau)\sigma_{-}(t)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\kappa^{2}\mathrm{e}^{\phi(\tau)}\text{Tr}\left[\sigma_{+}(t+\tau)\sigma_{-}(t)\rho_{p}(0)\right]

\displaystyle\equiv\kappa^{2}\mathrm{e}^{\phi(\tau)}\left\langle\sigma_{+}(t+\tau)\sigma_{-}(t)\right\rangle_{p},

(55)

where
\kappa
is defined above Eq. (
II
). Therefore, the final expression for the polaron frame polarisation spectrum is

\displaystyle I_{p,0}(\omega)=\kappa^{2}\lim_{t\to\infty}\Re\Big[

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{d}\tau\ \mathrm{e}^{\phi(\tau)}

\displaystyle\times\left\langle\sigma_{+}(t+\tau)\sigma_{-}(t)\right\rangle_{p}\mathrm{e}^{-i\omega\tau}\Big].

(56)

The two-time correlation function in Eq. (

) can be calculated using the quantum regression theorem (QRT)

;
McCutcheon 2016
. The QRT utilises the cyclicity of the trace to rewrite the two-time expectation value as

\lim_{t\to\infty}\left\langle\sigma_{+}(t+\tau)\sigma_{-}(t)\right\rangle_{p}=\text{Tr}\left[\sigma^{+}\Lambda_{p}(\tau)\right],

(57)

where

is a modified density operator with the initial state

\Lambda_{p}(0)=\sigma^{-}U_{0}(\infty)\rho_{p}(0)U_{0}^{\dagger}(\infty),

(58)

and, because

is the time evolution operator defined by the polaron frame Hamiltonian (see Eq. (
12

\Lambda_{p}(\tau)
operator but with the initial condition defined by Eq. (
58

Due to the polaron transformation, Eq. (

McCutcheon 2016
as well as any approximations used in the derivation of the master equation.

VI
Importance of initial conditions

It is typical in theoretical quantum optics to consider the system and environment initially in an uncorrelated state,

\rho(0)=\rho_{S}(0)\otimes\rho_{E},

(62)

where the environment is assumed to be in a thermal Gibbs state
\rho_{E}=\exp(-\beta H_{E})/\mathcal{Z}_{E}
with
H_{E}=\sum_{k}\nu_{\mathbf{k}}a_{k}^{\dagger}a_{k}
. This assumption is valid in weakly coupled systems where the system and the environment are only weakly correlated even after thermalisation. However, for strongly coupled systems, this assumption is invalidated, and the total Gibbs state
\rho_{\beta}=\exp(-\beta H)/\mathcal{Z}
should be used as the initial state where
H
now refers to the total Hamiltonian. Due to the interaction term, this will no longer be a separable state and so is difficult to model.

A benefit of the polaron transformation is that the separable initial state
\rho_{S}(0)\otimes\rho_{E}
in the polaron frame, models an initial state in the lab frame that is more similar to
\rho_{\beta}
. In our calculations,
\rho_{S}(0)=\left|g\right>\left<g\right|
, and so
\rho_{S}(0)\otimes\rho_{E}
in the polaron frame becomes
\rho_{l}(0)=\left|g\right>\left<g\right|\otimes B([D+\Delta]/\nu)\rho_{E}B(-[D+\Delta]/\nu)
in the lab frame, which is equal to
\rho_{\beta}
in the limit of negligible transition dipoles and projected onto the ground state. This is the state of the system after decaying to the ground state from a thermalised state, and so is a good initial state in which to model excitation.

Therefore, to compare the PFME to TEMPO (which operates in the lab frame), we must use the Hamiltonian in Eq. (
II
) with the initial state
\rho_{l}(0)
. However, most numerical techniques, including TEMPO, assume that the initial state is
\rho_{S}(0)\otimes\rho_{E}
. In Appendix
G
, we show that this discrepancy can be overcome by deriving an effective Hamiltonian to use in TEMPO such that the environment appears to be in the correct state. For
\rho_{S}(0)=\left|g\right>\left<g\right|
in the polaron frame, the required Hamiltonian is
\bar{H}=\bar{H}_{0}+\bar{H}_{I}
,
where

\displaystyle\bar{H}_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tilde{\epsilon}}{2}\sigma_{z}+\tilde{V}\sigma_{+}+\tilde{V}^{*}\sigma_{-}+\sum_{k}\nu_{\mathbf{k}}a_{k}^{\dagger}a_{k},

(63a)

\displaystyle\bar{H}_{I}

\displaystyle=A_{\Delta\Delta}\left(\mathcal{I}+\sigma^{z}\right)+\pi_{\mu\bar{\mu}}\sigma_{+}+\pi_{\bar{\mu}\mu}\sigma_{-},

(63b)

and
\pi_{pq}
is given in Eq. (
6
),
\tilde{\epsilon}=\epsilon+2G_{\Delta\Delta}
,
\tilde{V}=V+G_{\mu\bar{\mu}}
, where
G_{pq}=\sum_{k}(p_{k}\Delta_{k}^{*}+q_{k}^{*}\Delta_{k})/\nu_{\mathbf{k}}
. We note that because the displacement direction of the polaron transformation is state dependent, the necessary effective Hamiltonian depends on
\rho_{S}(0)
.

By comparing the Hamiltonians in Eqs. (
63
) and Eq. (
II
), we see stark differences if both are assumed to have the initial state
\rho_{S}(0)\otimes\rho_{E}
. This includes a renormalisation of the transition energy
\epsilon\to\tilde{\epsilon}
and the driving term
V\to\tilde{V}
. We can understand these differences by noting that the environment is far from equilibrium for a strongly coupled system, and initially, no optical polarons exist in our system. Dynamically created optical polarons effectively introduce a strong restoring force in the system that scales with the strength of the permanent dipoles. The ensuing dynamics are thus considerably different, exacerbating the need for careful consideration of the initial conditions of the physical models we deploy.

Identity type interactions, such as
\pi_{DD}\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
in Eq. (
II
), have a similar effect. As we showed in Eq. (
II
), this interaction can be removed through a displacement transformation and so, ultimately, the role of an identity interaction is to change the initial environment state from a thermal Gibbs state to a displaced one.

VII
Conclusion

We have studied a driven quantum optical system with strong permanent dipole moments associated with molecular orbital asymmetry. The optical polaron transformation, which captures the polarisation of photonic modes caused by the permanent dipoles, allows the construction of an optical master equation perturbative only in the transition dipole moment and driving strength, and provides an intuitive formalism to understand the effects associated with the presence of the permanent dipoles on the system dynamics and emission spectrum.

We have shown three key results. (1) In Sec.
III
, we showed that transition and decoherence rates can be engineered for practical application by exploiting permanent dipoles, for example, to reduce decoherence. By using the novel physical processes explicit in the polaron rate equations we derive, one can design systems to exploit these effects. The novel physical processes arising from the permanent dipole are: a photon sideband with a relative contribution to the emission spectrum scaling as
\kappa^{2}
, two-photon processes scaling as
\Omega_{\mu\Delta}^{2}
, and a term linear in the driving amplitude. The linear driving term is particularly useful for engineering enhanced or suppressed rates by varying the driving phase. (2) In Sec.
IV
, we proved that the optical polaron description provides a much more accurate master equation by comparing it to the DFME and TEMPO. (3) In Sec.
V
, we indicated distinguishable features of permanent dipoles in emission spectra and described possible measurements to obtain
\rho_{ee}(\infty)
,
\kappa
,
\bar{\eta}
and by extension the bare energy splitting
\epsilon
and the permanent dipole magnitude
|\tilde{\mathbf{d}}_{\Delta}|
. The distinguishable features are the photonic sideband and the altered width and position of peaks.

Furthermore, two of the processes we identified, namely the
\Gamma_{2}(\omega)
and
\Gamma_{V,1}(\omega)
processes, originate from the non-commutativity of the bosonic operators on the transition and permanent dipole interactions. These vanish if
\mathbf{d}_{\mu}
and
\mathbf{d}_{\Delta}
are perpendicular in which case our model becomes the driven spin boson model. We have also shown that in the absence of driving, this model maps onto the independent boson model in the limit of large cut off frequency (
\nu_{c}\to\infty
) or small Huang-Rhys parameter (
S\to 0
) where two photon processes are suppressed by the prefactor of the spectral density in Eq. (
23
).

There are many interesting features of optical polarons that warrant future exploration. For example, how the transition rates are affected by embedding the dipole in a structured
Burgess and Florescu 2022
, or anisotropic
Messinger et al. 2020
, dielectric medium, common to many biological systems. Moreover, many asymmetric systems couple strongly to vibrational baths and the interplay between photonic and vibrational physics leads to non-additive and non-equilibrium phenomena such as population inversion
Gribben et al. 2022a
;
Maguire et al. 2019
. How permanent dipoles affect these phenomena is still an open question. Additionally, it has been shown in Refs.
Guarnieri et al. 2018
;
Román-Ancheyta et al. 2021
;
Purkayastha et al. 2020
that the interplay between the pure dephasing and dissipative interactions leads to non-zero coherences in the steady state. It would be of great interest to explore the nature of the steady state coherences in the optical polaron formalism.

\pi_{pq}=\sum_{k}\left(p_{k}a_{k}^{\dagger}+q_{k}^{*}a_{k}\right).

(115)

The Pauli matrices in the eigenbasis are
\tau^{d}_{\pm}=\ket{\pm^{d}}\bra{\mp^{d}}
and
\tau^{d}_{z}=\ket{+^{d}}\bra{+^{d}}-\ket{-^{d}}\bra{-^{d}}
where

\begin{pmatrix}\ket{e}\\
\ket{g}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\left(\frac{\varphi^{d}}{2}\right)&-\sin\left(\frac{\varphi^{d}}{2}\right)\\
\sin\left(\frac{\varphi^{d}}{2}\right)&\cos\left(\frac{\varphi^{d}}{2}\right)\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\ket{+^{d}}\\
\ket{-^{d}}\end{pmatrix},

(116)

with
\cos(\varphi^{d})=\epsilon/\eta^{d}
and
\sin(\varphi^{d})=2|V|/\eta^{d}
.

Since Eq. (
113
) has the same structure as Eq. (
12
), the master equation in the displaced frame has the same algebraic form as in the polaron frame, i.e. given within the secular approximation by Eqs. (
78
) and in full in Appendix
C
, but the environment correlation functions (ECFs) are different. The displaced frame ECFs depend on linear combinations of Fourier transforms of the form

\Gamma^{d}_{pq,rs}(\omega)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{d}s\ \mathrm{e}^{i\omega s}\left\langle\pi_{pq}^{\dagger}(s)\pi_{rs}(0)\right\rangle\equiv\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{pq,rs}^{d}(\omega)+iS_{pq,rs}^{d}(\omega).

(117)

Substituting Eq. (
115
) into the ECF and using that
\langle a_{k}^{\dagger}a_{k^{\prime}}\rangle=\delta_{kk^{\prime}}N(\nu_{\mathbf{k}})
,
\langle a_{k}a_{k^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\rangle=\delta_{kk^{\prime}}\tilde{N}(\nu_{\mathbf{k}})
where
\tilde{N}(\nu)=1+N(\nu)
and that other combinations equal zero, leads to

\displaystyle\gamma^{d}_{pq,rs}(\omega)

\displaystyle=2\pi\left[\cos\left(\theta_{pr}\right)\Omega_{\bar{p}r}J(\omega)\tilde{N}(\omega)+\cos\left(\theta_{qs}\right)\Omega_{q\bar{s}}J(-\omega)N(-\omega)\right],

(118a)

\displaystyle S^{d}_{pq,rs}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{d}\nu\ J(\nu)\left[\cos\left(\theta_{pr}\right)\Omega_{\bar{p}r}\frac{\tilde{N}(\nu)}{\omega-\nu}+\cos\left(\theta_{qs}\right)\Omega_{q\bar{s}}\frac{N(\nu)}{\omega+\nu}\right].

(118b)

To exemplify how these functions relate to the rates in Eqs. (
78
) we will derive
\gamma_{--}^{d}(\eta^{d})
explicitly. This function is given by

\gamma_{--}^{d}(\eta^{d})=2\Re\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{d}s\ \mathrm{e}^{i\eta^{d}s}\left\langle g_{-}^{d\dagger}(s)g_{-}^{d}(0)\right\rangle.

(119)

Substituting in
g^{d}_{-}
and using Eqs. (
118
) we can read off that [ignoring arguments
(\eta^{d})
on the right-hand-side and temporarily denoting
\cos(\varphi^{d}/2)=c
and
\sin(\varphi^{d}/2)=s
],

\displaystyle\gamma_{--}^{d}(\eta^{d})=

\displaystyle\ 4c^{2}s^{2}\gamma^{d}_{\Delta\Delta,\Delta\Delta}+2cs^{3}\mathrm{e}^{i\theta}\gamma^{d}_{\Delta\Delta,\mu\bar{\mu}}-2c^{3}s\mathrm{e}^{-i\theta}\gamma^{d}_{\Delta\Delta,\bar{\mu}\mu}

\displaystyle+2cs^{3}\mathrm{e}^{-i\theta}\gamma^{d}_{\mu\bar{\mu},\Delta\Delta}+s^{4}\gamma^{d}_{\mu\bar{\mu},\mu\bar{\mu}}-s^{2}c^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2i\theta}\gamma^{d}_{\mu\bar{\mu},\bar{\mu}\mu}

\displaystyle-2c^{3}s\mathrm{e}^{i\theta}\gamma^{d}_{\bar{\mu}\mu,\Delta\Delta}-c^{2}s^{2}\mathrm{e}^{2i\theta}\gamma_{\bar{\mu}\mu,\mu\bar{\mu}}^{d}+c^{4}\gamma^{d}_{\bar{\mu}\mu,\bar{\mu}\mu}.

(120)

Using the symmetry that
\Omega_{ab}=\Omega_{ba}
and that
\mathbf{d}_{\Delta}\in\Re
, we can read off from Eq. (
118a
) that

\displaystyle\gamma^{d}_{\Delta\Delta,\Delta\Delta}(\eta^{d})

\displaystyle=\Omega_{\Delta\Delta}\gamma_{0}(\eta^{d}),

(121)

\displaystyle\gamma^{d}_{\bar{\mu}\mu,\mu\bar{\mu}}(\eta^{d})

\displaystyle=\Omega_{\mu\mu}\gamma_{0}(\eta^{d}),

(122)

\displaystyle\gamma^{d}_{\mu\bar{\mu},\bar{\mu}\mu}(\eta^{d})

\displaystyle=\Omega_{\bar{\mu}\bar{\mu}}\gamma_{0}(\eta^{d}),

(123)

\displaystyle\gamma^{d}_{\bar{\mu}\mu,\Delta\Delta}(\eta^{d})

\displaystyle=\gamma^{d}_{\Delta\Delta,\mu\bar{\mu}}(\eta^{d})=\cos\left(\theta_{\mu\Delta}\right)\Omega_{\mu\Delta}\gamma_{0}(\eta^{d}),

(124)

\displaystyle\gamma^{d}_{\mu\bar{\mu},\Delta\Delta}(\eta^{d})

\displaystyle=\gamma^{d}_{\Delta\Delta,\bar{\mu}\mu}(\eta^{d})=\cos\left(\theta_{\mu\Delta}\right)\Omega_{\bar{\mu}\Delta}\gamma_{0}(\eta^{d}),

(125)

\displaystyle\gamma^{d}_{\mu\bar{\mu},\mu\bar{\mu}}(\eta^{d})

\displaystyle=\gamma^{d}_{\bar{\mu}\mu,\bar{\mu}\mu}(\eta^{d})=\cos\left(\theta_{\mu\Delta}\right)\Omega_{\mu\bar{\mu}}\gamma_{0}(\eta^{d}),

(126)

where
\gamma_{0}(\omega)=2\pi[J(\omega)\tilde{N}(\omega)+J(-\omega)N(-\omega)
]. Using these we find that

\gamma_{--}^{d}(\eta^{d})=k^{0}_{--}\gamma_{0}(\eta^{d}),

(127)

where the rate coefficient is

k^{0}_{--}=\left[c^{4}+s^{4}\right]\Omega_{\mu\bar{\mu}}+4c^{2}s^{2}\Omega_{\Delta\Delta}-s^{2}c^{2}\left[\Omega_{\bar{\mu}\bar{\mu}}+\Omega_{\mu\mu}\right]-2sc\left[c^{2}-s^{2}\right]\left[\Omega_{\bar{\mu}\Delta}+\Omega_{\mu\Delta}\right]\cos\left(\theta_{\mu\Delta}\right).

(128)

This is rather complicated but only because we have complex transition dipole moments. If we assume real transition dipoles (
\mathbf{d}_{\bar{\mu}}\to\mathbf{d}_{\mu}
) we find
k^{0}_{--}=\Omega_{--}=(8\pi/3)(\mathbf{d}_{-}\cdot\mathbf{d}_{-})
where we have defined a new dipole vector

\mathbf{d}_{-}=\left[c^{2}-s^{2}\right]\Re[\mathbf{d}_{\mu}]+2cs\mathbf{d}_{\Delta}.

(129)

Likewise, if we choose purely imaginary transition dipoles (
\mathbf{d}_{\mu}\to i\Im[\mathbf{d}_{\mu}]
) we find that
k^{0}_{--}=\Omega_{--}
but with the dipole vector now defined as

\mathbf{d}_{-}=\left[c^{2}-s^{2}\right]\Im[\mathbf{d}_{\mu}]+2cs\mathbf{d}_{\Delta}.

(130)

A final limit worth checking is when the eigenstates fully localise,
\ket{+}\to\ket{e}
and
\ket{-}\to\ket{g}
, i.e.
c\to 1
and
s\to 0
. In this case,
k_{--}^{0}\to\Omega_{\mu\bar{\mu}}
, and so the decay rate is
\gamma_{--}^{d}(\eta^{d})=\gamma_{--}^{d}(\epsilon)=\Omega_{\mu\bar{\mu}}\gamma_{0}(\epsilon)
, which is the decay rate in the standard optical master equation.

Appendix F
Derivation of the emission spectrum

In this appendix, we derive Eq. (
52
), which gives the emission spectrum for the single emitter system. Our derivation closely follows those provided for single emitter systems without permanent dipoles in Refs.
Ficek and Swain 2005
;
Roy-Choudhury and Hughes 2015
;
Iles-Smith et al. 2017
;
Hughes and Yao 2009
;
Walls and Milburn 1994
. Due to the permanent dipoles, there are additional light-matter interaction terms in the Hamiltonian in Eq. (
II
), and so the derivation is slightly more cumbersome. However, as we show here, these additional terms do not affect the expression for the emission spectrum of the emitter within the standard approximations.

The emission spectrum is given exactly by

I(\omega)=\lim_{t\to\infty}\Re\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{d}\tau\ \mathrm{e}^{i\omega\tau}\left\langle\mathbf{E}_{-}\left(\mathbf{R},t\right)\cdot\mathbf{E}_{+}\left(\mathbf{R},t+\tau\right)\right\rangle,

(131)

where
\mathbf{R}
is the position of the detector and the positive frequency component of the electric field is

\mathbf{E}_{+}\left(\mathbf{R},t\right)=i\sum_{k}\mathbf{e}_{k}f_{\mathbf{k}}a_{k}\mathrm{e}^{-i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{R}},

(132)

and
\mathbf{E}_{-}(\mathbf{R},t)=\mathbf{E}_{+}(\mathbf{R},t)^{\dagger}
. Our aim is to express the expectation value
\langle a_{k}^{\dagger}a_{q}\rangle
in terms of dipole operators
\sigma_{\pm}
, which is achieved through the Heisenberg equation of motion

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}a_{k}(t)=-i\left[H,a_{k}(t)\right],

(133)

where
H
is the lab frame Hamiltonian in Eq. (
1
) and equivalently in Eq. (
II
). In doing so we will have arrived at the form of the emission spectrum in Eq. (
52
).

Using
[a_{k},a_{q}^{\dagger}]=\delta_{kq}
, one can show that

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}a_{k}(t)=-i\nu_{\mathbf{k}}a_{k}(t)-\Delta_{k}\sigma_{z}(t)-\mu_{k}\sigma_{+}(t)-\mu_{k}^{*}\sigma_{-}(t),

(134)

where
\sigma_{\alpha}(t)=U(t)^{\dagger}\sigma_{\alpha}U(t)
and
U(t)=\exp(-iHt)
, which can be solved to yield

a_{k}(t)=a_{k}(0)\mathrm{e}^{-i\nu_{\mathbf{k}}t}-\int_{0}^{t}\text{d}\tau\ \mathrm{e}^{i\nu_{\mathbf{k}}\left(\tau-t\right)}\left[\Delta_{k}\sigma_{z}(\tau)+\mu_{k}\sigma_{+}(\tau)-\mu_{k}^{*}\sigma_{-}(\tau)\right].

(135)

The first term in Eq. (
135
) is the free field term which does not contribute to the spectrum of the emitter and is henceforth ignored. After substituting the second term of Eq. (
135
) into Eq. (
132
) and taking the continuum limit we obtain

\mathbf{E}_{+}\left(\mathbf{R},t\right)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{d}\nu\ \sqrt{J(\nu)}\int_{0}^{t}\text{d}\tau\ \mathrm{e}^{i\nu_{\mathbf{k}}\left(\tau-t\right)}\left[\mathbf{O}_{z}\sigma_{z}(\tau)+\mathbf{O}_{\mu}\sigma_{+}(\tau)+\mathbf{O}_{\bar{\mu}}\sigma_{-}(\tau)\right],

(136)

where

\mathbf{O}_{p}=\int_{d\Omega_{\mathbf{k}}}\ \text{d}\Omega_{\mathbf{k}}\sum_{\lambda}\mathbf{e}_{k}\left(\mathbf{d}_{p}\cdot\mathbf{e}_{k}\right)\mathrm{e}^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\left(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{R}\right)}.

(137)

To progress analytically, we need to know how the emitter operators
\sigma_{\alpha}(t)
evolve in time. However, this is very complicated. Instead, we make the so-called harmonic decomposition (see Chapter 2.2 of Ref.
Ficek and Swain 2005
) in which we assume that the timescale over which the emitter evolves unitarily is much faster than the timescale over which spontaneous emission occurs. Within this approximation, we write that

\sigma_{+}(\tau)\approx\sigma_{+}(t)\mathrm{e}^{i\epsilon\left(\tau-t\right)},\quad\ \sigma_{-}(\tau)\approx\sigma_{-}(t)\mathrm{e}^{-i\epsilon\left(\tau-t\right)},\quad\text{ and}\quad\ \sigma_{z}(\tau)\approx\sigma_{z}(t).

(138)

Making the harmonic decomposition in Eq. (
136
) yields

\mathbf{E}_{+}(\mathbf{R},t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{d}\nu\ \sqrt{J(\nu)}\left[\mathbf{O}_{z}\sigma_{z}(t)j_{0}(\nu,t)+\mathbf{O}_{\mu}\sigma_{+}(t)j_{+}(\nu,t)+\mathbf{O}_{\bar{\mu}}\sigma_{-}(t)j_{-}(\nu,t)\right],

(139)

where

j_{\alpha}(\nu,t)=\int_{0}^{t}\text{d}\tau\ \mathrm{e}^{i\left(\tau-t\right)\left(\nu+\alpha\epsilon\right)}=-i\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-it\left(\nu+\alpha\epsilon\right)}}{\nu+\alpha\epsilon}.

(140)

The function
j_{\alpha}(\nu,t)
is dominated by the contribution near to
\nu=-\alpha\epsilon
and so we approximate it as a delta function,
j_{\alpha}(\nu,t)\approx 2\pi\delta(\nu+\alpha\epsilon)

Ficek and Swain 2005
. After performing this approximation, one finds that the delta function corresponding to
j_{+}(\nu,t)
lies outwith the integration domain
\nu\in[0,\infty]
and so the term going as
\sigma_{+}(t)
in Eq. (
139
) vanishes. Moreover, the term proportional to
j_{0}(\nu,t)
provides a delta function at zero frequency, leading to the term going as
\sigma_{z}(t)
in Eq. (
139
) to be proportional to
\sqrt{J(0)}
which is equal to zero for any well-defined spectral density. Therefore, within the standard approximations outlined in this derivation, the presence of permanent dipoles does not change the expression of the emission spectrum from Eq. (
52
). Thus, the only surviving term in Eq. (
139
) is proportional to
\sigma_{-}(t)
, and so we obtain

\mathbf{E}_{+}\left(\mathbf{R},t\right)\approx 2\pi\sqrt{J(\epsilon)}\mathbf{O}_{\bar{\mu}}\left(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{R}\right)\sigma_{-}(t),

(141)

where we have made the dependence of
\mathbf{O}_{\bar{\mu}}
on
\mathbf{r}
and
\mathbf{R}
explicit. After substituting Eq. (
141
) and its Hermitian conjugate into Eq. (
131
), one obtains Eq. (
52
), and an explicit expression for the Green’s function
\alpha_{\text{prop}}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{R},\omega)
.

Appendix G
Effective Hamiltonian

In many numerical schemes to solve the open quantum dynamics of systems coupled to thermal environments, it is assumed that the environment is in a free Gibbs state at the associated temperature
T=1/\beta
. In the polaron framework, the environment is not in such a convenient form. As such, we derive here an effective Hamiltonian that encapsulates the polaron thermalised state but has the effective environment in a free Gibbs state.

We start with the lab frame Hamiltonian,

H=\frac{\epsilon}{2}\sigma_{z}+V\sigma_{+}+V^{*}\sigma_{-}+E_{\text{dip}}+\sum_{k}\nu_{\mathbf{k}}a_{k}^{\dagger}a_{k}+\pi_{DD}\mathcal{I}+\pi_{\Delta\Delta}\sigma_{z}+\pi_{\mu\bar{\mu}}\sigma_{+}+\pi_{\bar{\mu}\mu}\sigma_{-},

(142)

where

\displaystyle\pi_{pq}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\left(p_{k}a_{k}^{\dagger}+q_{k}^{*}a_{k}\right),

(143)

\displaystyle p_{k}

\displaystyle=i\left(\mathbf{d}_{p}\cdot\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{k}}\right),

(144)

with
p,q\in\{\mu,\bar{\mu},D,\Delta\}
. In our calculations, the polaron frame initial state is

\rho_{p}^{0}=\left|g\right>\left<g\right|\otimes\rho_{E},

(145)

where
\rho_{E}=\exp[-\beta\sum_{k}\nu_{\mathbf{k}}a_{k}^{\dagger}a_{k}]/\mathcal{Z}_{E}
is a thermal state. Performing the inversion of the unitary transformations to go from the displaced polaron frame to the displaced frame, the initial state is
\rho_{d}^{0}=U^{\dagger}\rho_{p}^{0}U
where the polaron transformation is
U=B(\delta)\left|e\right>\left<e\right|+B(-\delta)\left|g\right>\left<g\right|
and
\delta_{k}=\Delta_{k}/\nu_{\mathbf{k}}
and
B(\alpha)=\exp[\sum_{k}(\alpha_{k}a_{k}^{\dagger}-\alpha_{k}^{*}a_{k})]
. Therefore,

\rho_{d}^{0}=\left|g\right>\left<g\right|\otimes\eta(\delta),

(146)

where we have defined a displaced thermal state as
\eta(\alpha)=B(\alpha)\rho_{E}B(-\alpha)
. We can then obtain the lab frame initial state via
\rho^{0}_{l}=B(-d)\rho_{d}^{0}B(d)
where
d_{k}=D_{k}/\nu_{\mathbf{k}}
, leading to

\rho^{0}_{l}=\left|g\right>\left<g\right|\otimes\eta(\delta-d).

(147)

The expression for
\eta(\alpha)
can be rewritten by making use of the identity,

\exp\left(\mathrm{e}^{S}X\mathrm{e}^{-S}\right)=\mathrm{e}^{S}\mathrm{e}^{X}\mathrm{e}^{-S},

(148)

which holds if
\mathrm{e}^{S}\mathrm{e}^{-S}=\mathcal{I}
. Using this identity yields,

\eta(\alpha)=\exp\left[-\beta\sum_{k}\nu_{\mathbf{k}}\left(a_{k}^{\dagger}-\alpha_{k}^{*}\right)\left(a_{k}-\alpha_{k}\right)\right].

(149)

We now rewrite the lab frame Hamiltonian using new ladder operators
b_{k}
, which we will relate to the
a_{k}
to ensure we model our desired initial state,
\eta(\delta-d)
. The initial environment state in the effective lab frame will be
\bar{\rho}_{E}=\exp[-\beta\sum_{k}\nu_{\mathbf{k}}b_{k}^{\dagger}b_{k}]/\mathcal{Z}_{E}
and so by comparison with Eq. (
149
), we know that

b_{k}=a_{k}-(\delta_{k}-d_{k}).

(150)

Substituting this into our actual lab frame Hamiltonian in Eq. (
142
) and ignoring any terms that are identity operators in both Hilbert spaces, we arrive at the effective Hamiltonian:

\bar{H}=\bar{H}_{0}+\bar{H}_{I},

(151)

where

\displaystyle\bar{H}_{0}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\epsilon}{2}+G_{\Delta\Delta}\right)\sigma_{z}+V\sigma_{+}+V^{*}\sigma_{-}+G_{\mu\bar{\mu}}\sigma_{+}+G_{\bar{\mu}\mu}\sigma_{-}+\sum_{k}\nu_{\mathbf{k}}b_{k}^{\dagger}b_{k},

(152)

\displaystyle\bar{H}_{I}

\displaystyle=\pi^{\prime}_{\Delta\Delta}\left(\mathcal{I}+\sigma^{z}\right)+\pi^{\prime}_{\mu\bar{\mu}}\sigma_{+}+\pi^{\prime}_{\bar{\mu}\mu}\sigma_{-},

(153)

and

\displaystyle\pi^{\prime}_{pq}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\left(p_{k}b_{k}^{\dagger}+q_{k}^{*}b_{k}\right),

(154)

\displaystyle G_{pq}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\left(p_{k}\delta_{k}^{*}+q_{k}^{*}\delta_{k}\right).

(155)

In order to get numerical agreement between TEMPO and the PFME, both of which assume separable initial states but crucially in different frames, we, therefore, must use
\bar{H}
in Eq. (
151
) for TEMPO calculations.
