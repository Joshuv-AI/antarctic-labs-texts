Angular Bloch Oscillations and their applications
Bernd Konrad, Maxim Efremov

Abstract
To advance precise inertial navigation, we present a compact quantum sensor which is based on novel quantum phenomenon of the angular Bloch oscillations and measures solely the angular acceleration of slow external rotation. We investigate the dynamics of ultra-cold atoms confined in a toroidal trap with a ring-lattice along the azimuth angle, realized with the superposition of two copropagating Laguerre-Gaussian beams. In the presence of external rotation of small angular acceleration, or prescribed linear chirp between the two beams, the measured angular momentum of trapped atoms displays a specific periodic behaviour in time, which we name as the angular Bloch oscillations. This discovered quantum phenomenon is shown to be a key element of fruitful applications for (i) an efficient transfer of quantized angular momentum from light field to atoms by controlling the chirp, and (ii) the direct determination of the angular acceleration of external rotation by measuring the Bloch period.



I
Introduction

Bloch oscillations (BOs) describe the periodic motion of a particle in a periodic potential responding to a constant force.
Initially proposed for electrons in the periodic crystal structure of metals by Bloch in 1929
Bloch 1929
, this quantum phenomenon remained non-observable for many decades, because the Bloch period, being inversely proportional to the lattice period, exceeds the decoherence time in the system by many orders.
However, in atomic physics the lattice period could be substantially extended and BOs have been observed with utracold atomic gas trapped in optical lattice
Ben Dahan et al. 1996
;
Peik et al. 1997
.
The fundamental fact that the atom picks up the linear momentum from the light during BOs has improved substantially the sensitivity and precision of atom interferometers.
Nowadays they are utilized in many metrological applications, including precise measurements of fine structure constant
Bouchendira et al. 2011
, gravitational acceleration
Charrière et al. 2012
;
Geiger et al. 2020
;
Abend et al. 2016
, gravity gradient
McGuirk et al. 2002
;
Asenbaum et al. 2017
, the Newtonian gravitational constant
Lamporesi et al. 2008
;
Rosi et al. 2014
, and the Coriolis non-inertial force
Lenef et al. 1997
;
Gustavson et al. 1997
;
Gauguet et al. 2009
;
Stockton et al. 2011
;
Berg et al. 2015
;
Dutta et al. 2016
;
Durfee et al. 2006
;
Wu et al. 2007

The latter is of great importance for realizing large enclosed space-time areas
Lan et al. 2012
and observing interference of massive objects
Fein et al. 2020
.

In addition, the field of inertial navigation
Nusbaum et al. 2019
and seismic records
Mat-Isa and Usher 1992
require sensors for precise measurement of the time-dependent angular velocity. At present, there have already been developed indirect
Schreiber et al. 2023
;
Ovaska and Valiviita 1998
and direct
Nusbaum et al. 2019
schemes to determine the angular acceleration.
In particular, when the angular velocity changes faster than the repetition rate of measurement, the angular acceleration can be estimated with an array of classical inertial sensors
Nilsson and Skog 2016
.

In this paper, we make a prediction of new quantum phenomenon of the angular Bloch oscillations (ABOs) and present valuable applications of ABOs to (i) transfer efficiently the
quantized
portion of angular momenta to the particle and (ii) realize
compact quantum
sensor for measuring exclusively the angular acceleration of external rotation.
To observe ABOs in a lab, we propose and consider in detailed the experimental scheme, which is based on ultra-cold atomic gas tightly confined in a toroidal trap with an additional periodic potential along the angular variable.
Such a ring lattice can be realized via superposition of two copropagating Laguerre-Gaussian (LG) beams with the angular indices
l
and
-l

Franke-Arnold et al. 2007
. We show that in the presence of external rotation of constant angular acceleration, or prescribed linear chirp between the LG beams, the measured angular momentum of trapped atoms displays the characteristic periodic behavior in time, that is ABOs.
As the first application, we proof that ABOs are mostly prominent in the regime of a shallow ring lattice and thus offer a very efficient way to transfer the well-defined amount of angular momenta, namely multiple of
2l\hbar
, from the field to the trapped atoms.
This technique significantly advances guided-wave Sagnac atom interferometers
Navez et al. 2016
and persistent current in a toroidal Bose-Einstein condensate
Ryu et al. 2007
;
Ramanathan et al. 2011
.
As the second application, we demonstrate that our setup based on ABOs measures in fact only the non-inertial Euler force, being entirely determined by the angular acceleration, rather than the Coriolis or centrifugal ones.

Our paper is organized as follows.
In Section
II
we introduce our setup for realizing a ring lattice suitable for observation of ABOs. Here we also show how the strong confinement of ultra-cold atoms allows us to describe dynamics appearing in the proposed scheme by the effective one-dimensional model.
We proof in Section
III
that the resulting one-dimensional system indeed displays ABOs and then present the detailed timeline of observation procedure.
Section
IV
presents fruitful applications of ABOs, namely to transfer the quantized angular momentum from the ring lattice to the atoms, calibrate experimentally our effective one-dimensional model and design quantum sensor for determining the angular acceleration of external rotation.
We briefly summarize in Section
V
our results and provide an outlook.
In order to keep our article self-contained but focused on the central ideas, many lengthy calculations are included in four appendices.
In Appendix
A
we investigate the adiabatic time evolution of our system.
The loading of atoms into the ground-state of a ring lattice is quantitatively studied in Appendix
B
.
We then consider in Appendix
C
the non-adiabatic transitions induced by fast rotation.
Finally, Appendix
D
is devoted to derivation of the analytical formulas for the first two energy bands in the case of a shallow lattice. These results help us to evaluate analytically the main parameters of ABOs.

II.2
From 3D to effective 1D

When we neglect atom-atom interaction, the dynamics of atoms in the potential
U(\mathbf{r},t)
is governed by the 3D Schrödinger equation

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Psi=\left[-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\mathbf{r}^{2}}+U(\mathbf{r},t)\right]\Psi

(5)

for the wave function
\Psi(\mathbf{r},t)
.

As is well known, the amplitude of the linear BOs reaches its maximum in the regime of the shallow lattice.
Consequently, to study the Bloch oscillations, now on the finite domain of the ring (effectively 1D system), our experimental setup has to be designed in a way that the atoms are
strongly
confined on the ring,
r=r_{0}
and
z=0
, by the toroidal trap, Eq. (
2
), and experience only
weak
periodic potential along the angle
\varphi
provided by the ring-lattice trap, Eq. (
3
).
To achieve this, we first require that the characteristic length of confinement
\sqrt{\hbar/(m\omega_{\mkern 3.0mu\vphantom{\perp}\vrule depth=0.0pt\mkern 2.0mu\vrule depth=0.0pt\mkern 3.0mu})}
along
z
is much smaller than the Rayleigh length
z_{\mathrm{R}}
,
\sqrt{\hbar/(m\omega_{\mkern 3.0mu\vphantom{\perp}\vrule depth=0.0pt\mkern 2.0mu\vrule depth=0.0pt\mkern 3.0mu})}\ll z_{\mathrm{R}}
.
This effectively allows us to consider the ring-lattice potential
U_{\mathrm{RL}}
, Eq. (
3
), entirely at
z=0
.
Moreover, since the function
f_{l}(\xi)
has a single maximum at
\xi=\sqrt{l}
,
f_{l}(\sqrt{2}r/w_{0})
in
U_{\mathrm{RL}}(\mathbf{r},t)
has a maximum at
r=\sqrt{l/2}w_{0}
.
This radius needs to be matched with the minimum of the toroidal trap
U_{\mathrm{T}}
, Eq. (
2
), at
r=r_{0}
, resulting in the condition
r_{0}=\sqrt{l/2}w_{0}
.
We also demand that the toroidal trap creates a well-defined ring structure, that is the ring width
\sqrt{\hbar/(m\omega_{\perp})}
is small compared to its radius
r_{0}
,
\sqrt{\hbar/(m\omega_{\perp})}\ll r_{0}
.

As a result, when the frequencies
\omega_{\perp}
and
\omega_{\mkern 3.0mu\vphantom{\perp}\vrule depth=0.0pt\mkern 2.0mu\vrule depth=0.0pt\mkern 3.0mu}
in the radial and axial directions are sufficiently large in comparison with the characteristic frequency
\omega_{\varphi}\sim\hbar/(mr_{0}^{2})
in the angular direction, the entire dynamics takes place only in
\varphi
direction.
In this way, the wave function
\Psi(\mathbf{r},t)
can then be approximated by

\displaystyle\Psi(r,\varphi,z,t)=Z_{0}(z)\frac{R_{0}(r)}{\sqrt{r}}\Phi(\varphi,t)\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\hbar}\varepsilon_{0}t},

(6)

where
Z_{0}(z)
and
R_{0}(r)
represent the normalized wave-functions of the ground state only in the toroidal trap
U_{\mathrm{T}}(\mathbf{r})
, Eq. (
2
), in the
r
and
z
directions, accordingly,
\varepsilon_{0}
is the energy phase factor.
The wave-function
\Phi(\varphi,t)
describes the dynamics in the
\varphi
direction and obeys the effective 1D Schrödinger equation

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Phi=\left\{-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2I}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\varphi^{2}}+V\cos^{2}\left[l\varphi-a(t)\right]\right\}\Phi

(7)

with the effective moment of inertia

\displaystyle I=

\displaystyle\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}r\frac{\left|R_{0}(r)\right|^{2}}{mr^{2}}\,\right]^{-1}

(8)

and the effective potential depth

\displaystyle V=U\!\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}r\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}z\left|Z_{0}(z)\right|^{2}\left|R_{0}(r)\right|^{2}f_{l}\left[\frac{\sqrt{2}r}{w(z)}\right].

(9)

The energy phase factor
\varepsilon_{0}
in Eq. (
6
) then reads

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{0}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}r\frac{\left|R_{0}(r)\right|^{2}}{4r^{2}}\,+\frac{\hbar}{2}\left(\omega_{\mkern 3.0mu\vphantom{\perp}\vrule depth=0.0pt\mkern 2.0mu\vrule depth=0.0pt\mkern 3.0mu}+\omega_{\perp}\right)-V.

(10)

For sufficiently strong toroidal trap, the parameters
I
, Eq. (
8
), and
V
, Eq. (
9
), of our 1D effective model, described by Eq. (
7
), can be easily estimated,
I\approx{mr_{0}^{2}}
and
V\approx Uf_{l}(\sqrt{2}r_{0}/w_{0})=Uf_{l}(\sqrt{l})=U(l^{l}/l!)\mathrm{e}^{-l}
.
However, the exact values of
I
and
V
are unknown and depend on specific details of the actual experimental setup. In Section
IV.2
we show how the ABOs can be applied for the determination or calibration of these parameters experimentally.

In fact, there are also other possibilities to create a similar system.
Any periodic modulation along the ring (the
\varphi
direction) leads to the same results we are presenting in this paper. In particular, one can use the blue-detuned LG beams instead, which builds up a dark lattice, where the atoms are trapped in the vicinity of the small intensity. This gives rise to longer coherence times due to smaller scattering rates

III
Angular Bloch Oscillations

The previous section has presented the experimental scheme to realize a ring lattice suitable for observation of ABOs.
In this section, we first show that the resulting 1D system indeed displays ABOs and then discuss in detail an observation procedure for this phenomenon.

III.1
Particle in a ring lattice

If there is additionally an external rotation with the time-dependent angular velocity
\mathbf{\Omega}(t)=\Omega(t)\mathbf{e}_{z}
around the symmetry axis of the ring, Fig.
1
, the effective 1D dynamics of our system is described by the Schrödinger equation

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Phi=\left\{-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2I}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\varphi^{2}}+V\cos^{2}\left[l\varphi-a(t)\right]+\Omega(t)\mathrm{i}\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial\varphi}\right\}\Phi

(11)

for the wave function
\Phi(\varphi,t)
. This equation should be solved on the finite domain
-\pi<\varphi\leq\pi
with periodic boundary condition for
\Phi(\varphi,t)
, namely
\Phi(-\pi,t)=\Phi(\pi,t)
and
[\mathrm{d}\Phi/\mathrm{d}\varphi](-\pi,t)=[\mathrm{d}\Phi/\mathrm{d}\varphi](\pi,t)
.

In general, the dynamics of our system, Eq. (
11
), is controlled by three parameters:

(\mathrm{i})
the magnitude of the periodic potential
V(t)
,

(\mathrm{ii})
the function
a(t)
, and
(\mathrm{iii})
the angular velocity
\Omega(t)
of external rotation.
Now we show that the effect of the chirp can be treated as effective rotation.
When looking at any point of the periodic potential,
e.g.
a maximum or a minimum, its angular position moves with the angular velocity
\dot{a}(t)/l
, where we denote the time derivative of
a(t)
as
\dot{a}(t)=\mathrm{d}a/\mathrm{d}t
.
Therefore, by considering our system in the co-rotation frame, which moves with the angular velocity
\dot{a}(t)/l
with respect to the lab frame, the transformed wave function

\displaystyle\Theta(\varphi,t)=\exp{\left[\frac{a(t)}{l}\frac{\partial}{\partial\varphi}\right]}\Phi(\varphi,t)

(12)

obeys the new form of the Schrödinger equation

\mathrm{i}\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Theta=\left[-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2I}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\varphi^{2}}+V(t)\cos^{2}(l\varphi)+\Omega_{\rm eff}(t)\mathrm{i}\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial\varphi}\right]\Theta,

(13)

which should be solved on the domain
\varphi\in(-\pi,\pi]
with the periodic boundary conditions.
Here the total time-dependent angular velocity

\Omega_{\rm eff}(t)\equiv\Omega(t)+\frac{\dot{a}(t)}{l}=\Omega(t)+\frac{\Delta\omega(t)}{2l}

(14)

combines together two sources of rotation and defines

\displaystyle-\hbar\eta(t)\equiv I\Omega_{\rm eff}(t)

(15)

as the total angular momentum associated with this rotation of the atoms on the ring.
As a result, the dynamics in the co-rotating frame is entirely determined only by the two parameters:
V(t)
and
\Omega_{\rm eff}(t)
.

V(t)=V

\hbar\eta(t)=\hbar\eta_{0}

III.3
ABOs in an accelerating ring lattice

Now we investigate the dynamics appearing in a ring lattice rotating with the constant angular acceleration
\dot{\Omega}_{\mathrm{eff}}=\mathrm{d}\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}/\mathrm{d}t
, corresponding to the linear change of the total angular momentum
\eta(t)=st
in time, with
s=-I\dot{\Omega}_{\mathrm{eff}}/\hbar
, Eq. (
15
). If the system is initialized in its ground state with the wave-function
u_{0,0}(\varphi)
and the energy
E_{0,0}^{\Theta}
, as depicted by the purple dot in Fig.
4
, for small enough

, the system is staying in its instantaneous eigenstate and moves along the blue solid line, following the energy dispersion relation, as shown in Fig.

According to Appendix

\displaystyle\Theta(\varphi,t)=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\phi(t)}u_{0,\eta(t)}(\varphi)

\displaystyle\phi(t)=\phi_{\mathrm{d}}(t)+\phi_{\mathrm{g}}(t)

\displaystyle\phi_{\mathrm{d}}(t)=-\frac{1}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{t}\!{E}_{0,0}^{\Theta}(st^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}

\displaystyle\phi_{\mathrm{g}}(t)=\mathrm{i}\int_{0}^{t}\!\Bra{u_{0,\eta(t^{\prime})}}\frac{\partial}{\partial t^{\prime}}\Ket{u_{0,\eta(t^{\prime})}}\,\mathrm{d}t^{\prime},

\displaystyle t_{\mathrm{B}}=\frac{2l}{s}.

\displaystyle\Theta(\varphi,t+t_{\mathrm{B}})=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\delta\phi(t)}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}2l\varphi}\Theta(\varphi,t),

Thus, our system indeed displays periodic dynamics in the state and we call this phenomenon the angular Bloch oscillations. Now, the important question arises: which measurable quantity does display this periodic behaviour?
For the conventional Bloch oscillations in a linear periodic potential

In analogy, we analyze the mean value
\braket{\hat{L}_{z}}\!(t)=\Bra{\Phi(t)}\hat{L}_{z}\Ket{\Phi(t)}

\hat{L}_{z}

\Phi(\varphi,t)=\exp[-\mathrm{i}a\hat{L}_{z}/(\hbar l)]\Theta(\varphi,t)

With
\Theta(\varphi,t)
given by Eq. (
21

\displaystyle\braket{\hat{L}_{z}}\!(t)=-\hbar\eta(t)+\frac{I}{\hbar}\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}{E}_{0}(q)}{\mathrm{d}q}\right|_{q=\eta(t)}.

(27)

During the adiabatic evolution,
\braket{\hat{L}_{z}}\!(t)
has two contributions:
(\mathrm{i})
the total angular momentum
\hbar\eta(t)

(\mathrm{ii})
the derivative of the energy band
{E}_{0}(q)

\hbar\eta(t)

Scheme for observation of ABOs

\mathrm{(iv)}

First of all

IV
Applications

We have discovered the new phenomenon of the ABOs and presented in detail the scheme to observe them in a lab. Now we focus on three applications of this phenomenon. Here, we first discuss the method to transfer the large
quantized
angular momentum from the ring lattice to the atoms. We further utilize ABOs for the direct experimental
calibration
of our effective 1D model and then for the
sensing
of angular acceleration.

IV.1
Large angular momentum transfer

With our setup, ABOs can be used as a tool for transferring a large angular momentum to the center-of-mass degree of freedom of the atom. This process is similar to the one of the conventional Bloch oscillations on the line, where the atom picks up the large and well-defined linear momentum
Abend et al. 2019
.

First of all, ABOs occur only when the ramp parameter
s\equiv(\mathrm{d}\eta/\mathrm{d}t)
of the effective angular acceleration of the ring lattice is quite small,
s\ll s_{\mathrm{c}}
, where

\displaystyle s_{\mathrm{c}}=\frac{\pi l}{\hbar}\frac{V^{2}}{32E_{\mathrm{r}}}.

(28)

As shown in Appendix
C
, under this condition, there occur no the Landau-Zener transitions and the atoms follow adiabatically the slowly rotating ring lattice, with the atomic wave-function in co-rotation frame given by Eq. (
21
). Hence, according to Eq. (
26
), for a given rotation time
t
, the average angular momentum
\braket{\hat{L}_{z}}\!(t)
changes by

\braket{\hat{L}_{z}}\!(t+t_{\mathrm{B}})-\braket{\hat{L}_{z}}\!(t)=-2l\hbar

(29)

after each Bloch period
t_{\rm B}
.

The quantized transfer of
\braket{\hat{L}_{z}}\!(t)
becomes even more prominent in the case of shallow lattices, as clearly seen from the comparison of Fig.
5
, for
V=3E_{\mathrm{r}}
, and Fig.
7
, for
V=0.5E_{\mathrm{r}}
.
For weaker lattices, the step-like change of
\braket{\hat{L}_{z}}\!(t)
takes place only at the edges of the Brillouin zones,
t=(n+1/2)t_{\mathrm{B}}
with
n=0,1,2\ldots
, while
\braket{\hat{L}_{z}}\!(t)
is almost constant at other times. However, the weaker lattice reduces
s_{\mathrm{c}}
, Eq. (
28
), and thereby increases the experimental time needed for keeping adiabatic time evolution.

Still, we have to keep in mind that these results are obtained within the effective 1D model of our proposed setup, Eq. (
11
), where there occurs only the dynamics in the
\varphi
direction. However, for quite large transferred angular momentum, the centrifugal energy becomes comparable to the excitation energy
\hbar\omega_{\perp}
, leading to the energy transition between the
\phi
to
r
directions. This process limits the maximally allowed transfer of the angular momentum from the ring lattice to the atoms.

IV.2
Parameters of the effective 1D model

Now we take a look back at the effective 1D dynamics governed by Eq. (

Despite the external rotation and the chirp, we have two parameters characterizing the system, namely the effective moment of inertia

and the magnitude

In order to calibrate

In the setup proposed in Section

IV.3
Measurement of external angular acceleration

Due to the equivalence of external rotation and the chirp between the LG beams in our setup, we can employ ABOs for sensing the external rotation.
Indeed, according to Eqs. (
13
) and (
14
), in the co-rotating frame the atoms are trapped in the non-rotating periodic potential
V\cos^{2}(l\varphi)
and experience total rotation with the effective angular velocity
\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}(t)
.

For external rotation with the constant acceleration
\dot{\Omega}
, we again prescribe the linear chirp
\Delta\omega(t)
, Eq. (
30
), with a given constant
{\mathcal{B}}
. In this way, the inverse Bloch period

\displaystyle\frac{1}{t_{\mathrm{B}}}=\frac{\hbar}{8E_{\mathrm{r}}}\left({\mathcal{B}}-2l\dot{\Omega}\right)

(36)

of the induced ABOs depends linearly on
{\mathcal{B}}
with the offset
2l\dot{\Omega}
. This dependence is presented in Fig.
9
. We note that when the chirp constant
{\mathcal{B}}
approaches the offset, that is when the angular velocity of external rotation is compensated by the chirp, and the resulting Bloch period becomes infinite.

Fig. 9:

Dependence of the inverse Bloch time
t_{\mathrm{B}}^{-1}
, Eq. (
36
), on the prescribed chirp constant
{\mathcal{B}}
.

Thus, to measure the angular acceleration
\dot{\Omega}
, we perform two experiments with two sufficiently different values
{\mathcal{B}}_{1,2}
of the chirp constant and measure the corresponding Bloch periods
t_{\mathrm{B}}^{(1,2)}
. According to Eq. (
36
) and Fig.
9
, these two measurements completely determine the linear dependence of
t_{\rm B}^{-1}
on
\mathcal{B}
and the unknown angular acceleration is given by

\displaystyle\dot{\Omega}=\frac{1}{2l}\frac{{\mathcal{B}}_{2}t_{\mathrm{B}}^{(2)}-{\mathcal{B}}_{1}t_{\mathrm{B}}^{(1)}}{t_{\mathrm{B}}^{(2)}-t_{\mathrm{B}}^{(1)}}

(37)

in terms of prescribed
{\mathcal{B}}_{1,2}
and measured
t_{\rm B}^{(1,2)}
.

In conclusion, it is important to understand to which non-inertial force the proposed setup is in fact sensitive to. Rotation with a time-dependent angular velocity is described by the term
-\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}(t)\hat{L}_{z}
in Eq. (
11
) and corresponds to three non-inertial forces appearing in co-rotating frame, i.e. the Coriolis, centrifugal and Euler one.
For atoms moving on the ring, both the Coriolis and the centrifugal forces act along the radial direction and are therefore compensated by the strong confinement. As a result, our scheme actually measures only the Euler force determined entirely by the angular acceleration
\dot{\Omega}
.

V
Conclusion
\&
Outlook

We have predicted the new quantum phenomenon of the angular Bloch oscillations, which is analog of the ordinary Bloch oscillations in one dimension. For their observation we have proposed and analyzed in detail the experimental scheme based on state-of-the-art technologies used for ultra-cold quantum gases. In addition, we have presented valuable applications of ABOs to (i) transfer efficiently the angular momenta to atoms and (ii) measure precisely the angular acceleration of external rotation.

To realize the ABOs in a lab, we tightly confine atoms in a toroidal trap and add a ring lattice along the azimuth angle, made of two copropagating LG beams with the opposite angular indices
l
and
-l
. We have demonstrated that in the presence of external rotation of small angular acceleration, or prescribed linear chirp between these LG beams, the measured angular momentum of trapped atoms displays the characteristic periodic behavior in time, i.e. ABOs.

In the regime of a shallow ring lattice, ABOs can be utilized for the transfer of the well-defined
quantized
portion of angular momenta from the field to the trapped atoms. Moreover, to design a compact and reliable
quantum
sensor for the Euler non-inertial force, we have proposed an experimental scheme based on ABOs for measuring small angular acceleration of external rotation. It is important to mention that a similar setup, based on a pair of deep state-dependent counter-rotating ring lattices, has recently been proposed as Sagnac atom interferometer for measuring the constant angular velocity
Dash et al. 2023
.

As outlook, our theoretical model of the proposed setup might be improved further by solving the 3D Schrödinger equation (
5
) numerically. This allows us to quantify the effect of coupling between radial and angular components, since this coupling puts limitations on the maximal angular momentum that can efficiently be transferred to the atoms. Moreover, it is also important to understand the effect of atom-atom interaction on the dynamics of ABOs and their applications.

Furthermore, here we have analyzed the optical trapping using a bright lattice formed by two LG beams with the angular indices
l
and
-l
. However, two LG beams with certain indices can create a dark lattice
Franke-Arnold et al. 2007
;
Dash et al. 2023
, resulting in reduction of photon scattering and hence longer coherence times. It might also be beneficial to consider other setups realizing a ring confinement with a additional periodic modulation along the angular variable,
e.g.
by combining magnetic and optical traps
Franke-Arnold et al. 2007
;
Amico et al. 2021
;
Amico et al. 2022
.

Appendix A
Adiabatic time evolution

Here, we investigate the adiabatic time evolution of our system, prepared initially in its ground state.
To do it, we first expand the solution

\displaystyle\Ket{\Theta(t)}=\sum_{n,m}c_{n,m}(t)\Ket{\Theta_{n,m}^{t}}

(38)

of the Schrödinger equation

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Ket{\Theta(t)}=\hat{H}(t)\Ket{\Theta(t)}

(39)

with the time-dependent Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\hat{H}(t)=-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2I}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\varphi^{2}}+V(t)\cos^{2}(l\varphi)+\Omega_{\rm eff}(t)\mathrm{i}\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial\varphi}

(40)

in terms of the Bloch states as instantaneous eigenstates

\displaystyle\Braket{\varphi|\Theta_{n,m}^{t}}=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}m\varphi}u_{n,m+\eta(t)}^{V(t)}(\varphi)

(41)

with the integer
m\in(-l,l]
and
\eta(t)=-I\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}(t)/\hbar
.

The functions
u_{n,q}^{v}(\varphi)
are the solutions of the differential equation

\displaystyle{E}_{n}^{v}(q)u_{n,q}^{v}=\left[\frac{1}{2I}\left(-\mathrm{i}\hbar\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\varphi}+\hbar q\right)^{2}+v\cos^{2}(l\varphi)\right]u_{n,q}^{v}

(42)

with
n=0,1,2\ldots
.
This equation should be solved for
-\pi/2l<\varphi\leq\pi/2l
and with the periodic boundary conditions for
u_{n,q}^{v}(\varphi)
.

By inserting Eq. (
38
) into Eq. (
39
) and using the eigenvalues
{E}^{\Theta}_{n,m}={E}_{n}^{V}(m+\eta)-\hbar^{2}\eta^{2}/(2I)
of
\hat{H}(t)
, we arrive at the system of coupled ordinary differential equations

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \left[\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\hbar}E_{n,m}^{\Theta}(t)+\Braket{\Theta_{n,m}^{t}|\frac{\partial}{\partial t}|\Theta_{n,m}^{t}}\right]c_{n,m}(t)\\
=\sumsum_{(n^{\prime},m^{\prime})\neq(n,m)}\frac{\Braket{\Theta_{n,m}^{t}|\frac{\partial\hat{H}}{\partial t}|\Theta_{n^{\prime},m^{\prime}}^{t}}}{E_{n,m}^{\Theta}(t)-E_{n^{\prime},m^{\prime}}^{\Theta}(t)}c_{n^{\prime},m^{\prime}}(t)\end{aligned}

(43)

for the expansion coefficients
c_{n,m}(t)
.

When the right-hand side of Eq. (
43
) is negligible, that is if

\displaystyle\hbar\left|\Braket{\Theta_{n,m}^{t}|\frac{\partial\hat{H}}{\partial t}|\Theta_{n^{\prime},m^{\prime}}^{t}}\right|\ll\Big|E_{n,m}^{\Theta}(t)-E_{n^{\prime},m^{\prime}}^{\Theta}(t))\Big|^{2}

(44)

with
(n^{\prime},m^{\prime})\neq(n,m)
, the system stays in the state, where it has been initially prepared.
This is the adiabatic limit.

As a result of adiabatic evolution, for our system prepared initially in its ground state, that is
c_{0,0}(0)=1
and
c_{n,m}(0)=0
for
n>0
or
m>0
, we obtain

\displaystyle\Theta(\varphi,t)=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\phi(t)}\Theta_{0,0}^{t}(\varphi)=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\phi(t)}u_{0,\eta(t)}^{V(t)},

(45)

where the imprinted time-dependent phase

\displaystyle\phi(t)=\phi_{\mathrm{d}}(t)+\phi_{\mathrm{g}}(t)

(46)

consists of the dynamical phase

\displaystyle\phi_{\mathrm{d}}(t)=-\frac{1}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{t}\!{E}_{0,0}^{\Theta}(st^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}

(47)

and the geometrical phase

\displaystyle\phi_{\mathrm{g}}(t)=\mathrm{i}\int_{0}^{t}\!\Bra{u_{0,\eta(t^{\prime})}^{V(t^{\prime})}}\frac{\partial}{\partial t^{\prime}}\Ket{u_{0,\eta(t^{\prime})}^{V(t^{\prime})}}\,\mathrm{d}t^{\prime},

(48)

originating from the change of the instantaneous eigenstates
\Ket{u_{0,\eta(t)}^{V(t)}}
with time
Schleich 2001
.

Appendix C
Angular acceleration and Landau-Zener transitions

Here we investigate the loss of atoms from the lowest energy band
E_{0}(q)
to the higher energy band
E_{1}(q)
, depicted in Fig.
2
, during the process of ABOs derived for a shallow lattice.
The dynamics of the atoms is governed by Eq. (
39
) with the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}(t)
given by Eq. (
40
), where
V
is kept constant and
\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}(t)
is the prescribed function of time.
Moreover, our system is assumed to be initially prepared in its ground state
\Ket{\Theta_{0,0}^{t=0}}

When we try to use Eq. (

(n^{\prime},m^{\prime})

n^{\prime}\neq n
) with
m^{\prime}=m
.
Since our system is initially prepared in the
(0,0)
-state of the shallow lattice, the difference of the relevant time-dependent energies
{E}^{\Theta}_{0,0}(t)-{E}^{\Theta}_{n^{\prime},0}(t)=E_{0}^{V}[\eta(t)]-E_{n^{\prime}}^{V}[\eta(t)]
in the denominator becomes very small as
n^{\prime}=1
and
\eta(t)=l
, that is when the quasi angular momentum
\eta(t)
reaches the edge of the first Brillouin zone
\eta(t)=l
, as depicted in Fig.
2
.

To examine quantitatively the non-adiabatic transitions in the shallow lattice, we reconsider Eq. (
42
) in the limit
V\rightarrow 0
. At
V=0
, the solutions of this equation are given by
\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}2lk\varphi}
, with
k=0,\pm 1,\pm 2,\ldots
, for any value of
q
and correspond to the energies
\mathcal{E}_{k}(q)=(q-2lk)^{2}E_{\mathrm{r}}/l^{2}
.
To define the bands, these solutions should be sorted according to their energies. Namely, for
q\in(0,l)
the state with
k=0
corresponds to the ground state band (
n=0
) and the state with
k=1
to the first excited band (
n=1
), while in the range
q\in(l,2l)
these roles are interchanged. Most importantly, at
q=l
the energies of these two states are equal, that is the energy bands cross.

For small
V>0
, this degeneracy is lifted. Following the standard methods of quantum mechanics, we can describe the dynamics of our system by writing the wave function

\displaystyle\Theta(\varphi,t)=d_{0}+d_{1}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}2l\varphi}

(53)

in the form of the linear superposition of these two degenerate bands, or the non-perturbed torus states. By inserting Eq. (
53
) into Eq. (
39
), we arrive at the equations

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}\hbar\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}{d}_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{V}{2}d_{0}+\frac{V}{4}d_{1}

(54)

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}\hbar\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}{d}_{1}

\displaystyle=\left[4E_{\mathrm{r}}\left(1-\frac{\eta(t)}{l}\right)+\frac{V}{2}\right]d_{1}+\frac{V}{4}d_{0}

(55)

for the coefficients
d_{0}
and
d_{1}
, where
\eta(t)=-I\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}(t)/\hbar
.

For any time dependent
\eta(t)
we can shift the common terms into the phase

\displaystyle\phi_{0}=\frac{1}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{t}\!\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\left\{2E_{\mathrm{r}}\left[1-\frac{\eta(t^{\prime})}{l}\right]+\frac{V}{2}\right\}

(56)

and bring Eqs. (
54
) and (
55
) into symmetric form

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}\hbar\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}D_{0}

\displaystyle=-

\displaystyle 2E_{\mathrm{r}}\left[1-\frac{\eta(t)}{l}\right]D_{0}

\displaystyle+\frac{V}{4}D_{1}

(57)

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}\hbar\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}D_{1}

\displaystyle=+

\displaystyle 2E_{\mathrm{r}}\left[1-\frac{\eta(t)}{l}\right]D_{1}

\displaystyle+\frac{V}{4}D_{0},

(58)

where
D_{0}=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\phi_{0}}d_{0}
and
D_{1}=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\phi_{0}}d_{1}
.

For a linear behaviour
\eta(t)=st
, these two equations perfectly coincide with the well-known time evolution for the Landau-Zener process
Zener 1932
. As a result, the transition probability, or the loss of atoms,

\displaystyle T_{\mathrm{LZ}}=\exp{\left(-2\pi\gamma\right)}

(59)

from the lowest band to the first excited band during one passage through the Brillouin zone edge is determined by the Landau-Zener parameter
Zener 1932

\displaystyle\gamma=\frac{1}{\hbar}\frac{(V/4)^{2}}{(4E_{\mathrm{r}}s/l)}.

(60)

This approximation is valid when the effective interaction region, corresponding to a small energy difference, is small compared to the size of the Brillouin zone. This is in fact the case for shallow lattices.

In addition to the geometrical and the dynamical phase, the state that remains in the lowest band experiences the phase shift
Zener 1932

\displaystyle\phi_{\mathrm{LZ}}=\frac{\pi}{4}+\arg\left[\Gamma\left(1-\mathrm{i}\gamma\right)\right]+\gamma\left[\ln(\gamma)-1\right]

(61)

due to the interaction with the higher band, where
\Gamma(\xi)
denotes the Gamma function.

For the given potential depth and small enough scanning speed
s
, the parameter
\gamma
is large and the non-adiabatic losses are exponentially small, as shown by Eq. (
59
). Therefore, for
\gamma\gg 1
, we can use the identity
\Gamma\left(1-\mathrm{i}\gamma\right)=-\mathrm{i}\gamma\Gamma\left(-\mathrm{i}\gamma\right)
and the Stirling’s formula

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \ln\left[\Gamma\left(-\mathrm{i}\gamma\right)\right]=&-\mathrm{i}\gamma\ln\left(-\mathrm{i}\gamma\right)+\mathrm{i}\gamma\\
&+\frac{1}{2}\ln\left(\mathrm{i}\frac{2\pi}{\gamma}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\gamma}\right)\end{aligned}

(62)

for the Gamma function, to obtain

\displaystyle\arg\left[\Gamma\left(1-\mathrm{i}\gamma\right)\right]=-\frac{\pi}{4}+\gamma\left[1-\ln\left(\gamma\right)\right]+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\gamma}\right)

(63)

and then

\displaystyle\phi_{\mathrm{LZ}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\gamma}\right)

(64)

for the additional phase shift
\phi_{\mathrm{LZ}}
, Eq. (
61

This limit reproduces perfectly the predictions of the adiabatic theorem: no transitions and no additional phase shift. Qualitatively, it takes place when
2\pi\gamma\ll 1

s\ll s_{\mathrm{c}}
with the critical value

\displaystyle s_{\mathrm{c}}\equiv\frac{\pi l}{\hbar}\frac{V^{2}}{32E_{\mathrm{r}}}.

(65)
