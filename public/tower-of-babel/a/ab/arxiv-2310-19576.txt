High Order Expansion Method for Kuiper’s V n V_{n} Statistic in Goodness-of-fit Test
Hong-Yan Zhang 1,2 , Zhi-Qiang Feng 1 , Haoting Liu 2,1 , Rui-Jia Lin 3 and Yu Zhou 1 1 School of Information Science and Technology, Hainan Normal University, Haikou 571158, China 2 School of Automation, University of Science and Technology Beijing, Beijing 100083, China 3 Information Network and Data Center, Hainan Normal University, Haikou 571158, China

Abstract
Kuiper’s V n V_{n} statistic, a measure for comparing the difference of ideal distribution and empirical distribution, is of great significance in the goodness-of-fit test. However, Kuiper’s formulae for computing the cumulative distribution function, false positive probability and the upper tail quantile of V n V_{n} can not be applied to the case of small sample capacity n n since the approximation error is 𝒪 ⁡ ( n − 1 ) \mathcal{O}\left(n^{-1}\right) . In this work, our contributions lie in three perspectives: firstly the approximation error is reduced to 𝒪 ( n − ( k + 1 ) / 2 ) \mathcal{O}\left(n^{-(k+1)/2}\right) where k k is the expansion order with the high order expansion for the exponent of differential operator; secondly, a novel high order formula with approximation error 𝒪 ⁡ ( n − 3 ) \mathcal{O}\left(n^{-3}\right) is obtained by massive calculations; thirdly, the fixed-point algorithms are designed for solving the Kuiper pair of critical values and upper tail quantiles based on the novel formula. The high order expansion method for Kuiper’s V n V_{n} -statistic is applicable for various applications where there are more than five samples of data. The principles, algorithms and code for the high order expansion method are attractive for the goodness-of-fit test. Keywords : Goodness-of-fit Methods, Kuiper’s statistic, Quantile estimation, Algorithm design, High order expansion (HOE)

Introduction

In statistics, for two cumulative distribution functions (CDF)
F_{1}(x)
and
F_{2}(x)
, Kuiper’s test statistic
[
1

V=D^{+}+D^{-}=\sup_{x}[F_{1}(x)-F_{2}(x)]+\sup_{x}[F_{2}(x)-F_{1}(x)]

D^{+}=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}[F_{1}(x)-F_{2}(x)],\quad D^{-}=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}[F_{2}(x)-F_{1}(x)]=-\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}}[F_{1}(x)-F_{2}(x)]

represent the maximum deviation above and below the two CDFs being
compared, respectively. The trick with Kuiper’s test is to use the quantity
D^{+}+D^{-}

as the test statistic instead of
\displaystyle\max(D^{+},D^{-})=\sup_{x}\left|F_{1}(x)-F_{2}(x)\right|
in Kolmogrov-Smirnov test.
This small change makes Kuiper’s test as sensitive in the tails as at the median and
also makes it invariant under cyclic transformations of the independent random variables.
The Kuiper’s test is useful for the goodness-of-fit test, also named by distribution fitting test

Suppose

statistic of critical value for the

-test. For more details of how to compute the

with data collection and the null hypothesis in Kuiper’s test, please see

\displaystyle F_{V_{n}}(v)

\displaystyle=\Pr\left\{V_{n}\leq v\right\}=\Pr\left\{V_{n}\cdot\sqrt{n}\leq v\cdot\sqrt{n}\right\}

\displaystyle=\Pr\left\{K_{n}\leq c\right\}=\mathscr{A}(c)+\frac{\mathscr{B}(c)}{\sqrt{n}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &K_{n}=\sqrt{n}\cdot V_{n}\\
&c=v\sqrt{n}\end{aligned}\right.

(5)

and

\mathscr{A}(c)=1-\sum^{\infty}_{j=1}2(4j^{2}c^{2}-1)\mathrm{e}^{-2j^{2}c^{2}},\quad\mathscr{B}(c)=\frac{8}{3}c\sum^{\infty}_{j=1}j^{2}(4j^{2}c^{2}-3)\mathrm{e}^{-2j^{2}c^{2}}

(6)

are functions of
c\in\mathbb{R}^{+}

Let

(7)

(8)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \alpha&=\Pr\left\{K_{n}>c^{\alpha}_{n}\right\}=-\mathscr{A}(c^{\alpha}_{n})-\frac{\mathscr{B}(c^{\alpha}_{n})}{\sqrt{n}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\\
v^{\alpha}_{n}&=c^{\alpha}_{n}/\sqrt{n}\end{aligned}\right.

Given the UTP

\alpha=\Pr\left\{K_{n}>c\right\}\approx\left[-2+8c^{2}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\cdot\frac{8}{3}(4c^{3}-3c)\right]\mathrm{e}^{-2c^{2}},\quad c>\frac{6}{5}.

High Order Expansion Formula for Kuiper’s
V_{n}
-Statistic

2.1
High Order Generalization of Kuiper’s Probability Formula

For the purpose of presenting the formula (
4
), Kuiper
[
15
,
1
]
introduced the auxiliary function

\Phi(a,b)=\sum^{\infty}_{j=-\infty}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-2j^{2}(a+b)^{2}}-\mathrm{e}^{-2(ja+(j-1)b)^{2}}\right]

(16)

and the generating function

\Phi_{n}(a,b)=\Phi(a,b)+\frac{1}{6\sqrt{n}}\left(\frac{\partial{}}{\partial{a}}+\frac{\partial{}}{\partial{b}}\right)\Phi(a,b)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{n}\right),\quad a,b\in\mathbb{R}

(17)

for the CDF of
K_{n}=\sqrt{n}\cdot V_{n}
.
Let

\Dif_{n}=\frac{1}{6\sqrt{n}}\left(\frac{\partial{}}{\partial{a}}+\frac{\partial{}}{\partial{b}}\right),\quad\Dif_{n}^{i}=\frac{1}{6^{i}n^{i/2}}\left(\frac{\partial{}}{\partial{a}}+\frac{\partial{}}{\partial{b}}\right)^{i},

(18)

be the partial derivative operator and its powers respectively, we immediately have

\Phi_{n}(a,b)=\Phi(a,b)+\Dif_{n}\Phi(a,b)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{n}\right).

(19)

Obviously, this is a first expansion for the
\Phi_{n}(a,b)
in the sense of the power of
\Dif_{n}
, or a
1/2
-order expansion for the

\Phi_{n}(a,b)
in the sense of sample capacity of
n
since the factor
1/\sqrt{n}
appears in expression of
\Dif_{n}
. For the applications with small sample capacity, what we need is a better expression with smaller approximation error, say
\mathcal{O}\left(n^{-(k+1)/2}\right)
for
k=2,3,\cdots
where
k
is a parameter of order for the approximation error. Thus it is necessary for us to expand the
\Phi_{n}(a,b)
with more terms which reduces the approximation error as well as makes sure the expansion is convergent without any extra condition when
k\to+\infty
. It is well known in functional analysis that the norm of the operator
\Dif_{n}
is infinite. For the candidate generalization of (
19
) in the form of series, the domain of convergence should also be infinite since the operator
\Dif_{n}
is involved. In consequence, it is a natural strategy for us to replace (
19
) by the following expression for the purpose of generalization

\displaystyle\Phi_{n}(a,b)

\displaystyle=\exp(\Dif_{n})\Phi(a,b)=\left[1+\frac{\Dif_{n}}{1!}+\frac{\Dif_{n}^{2}}{2!}+\cdots+\frac{\Dif_{n}^{k}}{k!}\right]\Phi(a,b)+\mathcal{O}\left(n^{-(k+1)/2}\right).

(20)

For the
i
-th term in (
20
) , we can set

\Phi^{(i)}_{n}(a,b)=\frac{1}{i!}\Dif_{n}^{i}\Phi(a,b)=\frac{1}{n^{i/2}}\cdot\frac{1}{i!6^{i}}\left[\frac{\partial{}}{\partial{a}}+\frac{\partial{}}{\partial{b}}\right]^{i}\Phi(a,b)

(21)

as the
i
-th component. Hence
\Phi_{n}(a,b)
is the superposition of the various components, namely

\Phi_{n}(a,b)=\sum^{k}_{i=0}\Phi^{(i)}_{n}(a,b)+\mathcal{O}\left(n^{-(k+1)/2}\right)

(22)

It is easy to find that the computation of
\left\{\Phi^{(i)}_{n}(a,b):i=0,1,2,3,\cdots\right\}
can be done iteratively since we have

\Phi^{(r+1)}_{n}(a,b)=\frac{1}{(r+1)!}\Dif_{n}^{r+1}\Phi(a,b)=\frac{1}{r+1}\Dif_{n}\Phi^{(r)}_{n}(a,b)

(23)

for
r=0,1,2,\cdots
.

Equation (
21
) shows that the continuous variables
a,b
and the discrete variable
n
can be separated to distinguish the impacts of the sample capacity
n
and the label
i
for the component
\Phi^{(i)}_{n}(a,b)
. Let

Q_{i}(a,b)=\frac{1}{i!6^{i}}\left[\frac{\partial{}}{\partial{a}}+\frac{\partial{}}{\partial{b}}\right]^{i}\Phi(a,b),

(24)

then we immediately have

\Phi_{n}(a,b)=\sum^{k}_{i=0}\frac{Q_{i}(a,b)}{n^{i/2}}+\mathcal{O}\left(n^{-{(k+1)}/2}\right).

(25)

We now follow Kuiper’s derivation
[
1
]
as follows:

\displaystyle\Pr\left\{\sqrt{n}\cdot V_{n}\leq c\right\}

\displaystyle=\int^{c}_{b=0}\left\{\int^{c-b}_{a=0}\frac{\partial^{2}{\Phi_{n}}}{\partial{a}\partial{b}}\dif a\right\}\dif b=\int^{c}_{b=0}\left[\frac{\partial{\Phi_{n}}}{\partial{b}}\right]\dif b

(26)

\displaystyle=\sum^{k}_{i=0}\frac{1}{n^{i}/2}\int^{c}_{b=0}\left[\frac{\partial{Q_{i}(a,b)}}{\partial{b}}\right]_{a=c-b}\dif b+\mathcal{O}\left(n^{-(k+1)/2}\right)

Let

B_{i}(c)=\int^{c}_{b=0}\left[\frac{\partial{Q_{i}}}{\partial{b}}\right]_{a=c-b}\dif b,\quad i=0,1,2,\cdots,k

(27)

then we can obtain the following
k
-th order expansion

\Pr\left\{K_{n}\leq c\right\}=\Pr\left\{\sqrt{n}\cdot V_{n}\leq c\right\}=\sum^{k}_{i=0}\frac{B_{i}(c)}{n^{i/2}}+\mathcal{O}\left(n^{-(k+1)/2}\right),

(28)

for the CDF
F_{K_{n}}(c)
about the sample capacity
n
with the approximation error
\mathcal{O}\left(n^{-(k+1)/2}\right)
. Consequently, the relation of
v=c/\sqrt{n}
implies that the CDF of
V_{n}
can be expressed by

\Pr\left\{V_{n}\leq v\right\}=\sum^{k}_{i=0}\frac{B_{i}(v\sqrt{n})}{n^{i/2}}+\mathcal{O}\left(n^{-(k+1)/2}\right)

(29)

By comparing (
29
) with (
4
) and (
14
), it is easy to find that

•

the HOE formula (
29
) is a direct generalization of (
4
), where more terms about the powers of sample capacity
n
are involved;

•

the approximation error is
\mathcal{O}\left(n^{-(k+1)/2}\right)
for (
29
), but the counterpart for (
14
) is missing;

•

the computation of (
14
) involves the factorial
n!
and
(n-1)!
, which may lead to the overflow problem caused by large
n
;

•

the impact of the sample capacity
n
is separated from the function
B_{i}(v\sqrt{n})
in (
29
), which reflects the difference between small capacity and large capacity.

2.2
Expressions for
B_{i}(c)

The principle for computing the functions
B_{i}(c)
is not complex but the practical calculation is somewhat tedious and awesome although only the elementary knowledge of calculus is required. For
k=5
, we have

\Pr\left\{K_{n}\leq c\right\}=\sum^{5}_{i=0}\frac{B_{i}(c)}{n^{i/2}}+\mathcal{O}\left(n^{-3}\right)

(30)

where

\left\{\begin{aligned} B_{0}(c)&=1-2\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}(4j^{2}c^{2}-1)\mathrm{e}^{-2j^{2}c^{2}}\\
B_{1}(c)&=\dfrac{8}{3}\sum^{\infty}_{j=1}cj^{2}(4c^{2}j^{2}-3)\mathrm{e}^{-2c^{2}j^{2}}\\
B_{2}(c)&=-\frac{1}{18}+\frac{1}{9}\sum^{\infty}_{j=1}\left\{\mathrm{e}^{-2j^{2}c^{2}}\left[4c^{2}j^{2}(-16c^{2}j^{4}+24j^{2}+1)-12j^{2}-1\right]\right\}\\
B_{3}(c)&=\frac{16}{81}\sum^{\infty}_{j=1}cj^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2j^{2}c^{2}}\left\{16c^{4}j^{6}-40c^{2}j^{4}-4c^{2}j^{2}+15j^{2}+3\right\}\\
B_{4}(c)&=\frac{1}{648}+\frac{1}{972}\sum^{\infty}_{j=1}\left\{\mathrm{e}^{-2j^{2}c^{2}}[16c^{4}j^{4}(-64c^{2}j^{6}+240j^{4}+40j^{2}+1)\right.\\
&\hskip 71.13188pt\left.-24c^{2}j^{2}(120j^{4}+40j^{2}+1)+120j^{2}(2j^{2}+1)+3]\right\}\\
B_{5}(c)&=\frac{2}{3645}\sum^{\infty}_{j=1}\left\{16\mathrm{e}^{-2c^{2}j^{2}}[16c^{5}j^{6}(32c^{2}j^{6}-168j^{4}-40j^{2}-3)\right.\\
&\hskip 71.13188pt\left.+40c^{3}j^{4}(84j^{4}+40j^{2}+3)-15cj^{2}(56j^{4}+40j^{2}+3)]\right\}\end{aligned}\right.

(31)

It is prosaic that the
B_{i}(c)
is a generalization of Kuiper’s
\mathscr{A}(c)
and
\mathscr{B}(c)
since
B_{0}(c)=\mathscr{A}(c)
and
B_{1}(c)=\mathscr{B}(c)
. Particularly, for
k=1
, (
28
) is the same with (
4
). For more details about the computation of
B_{i}(c)
, please go to the GitHub site

https://github.com/GrAbsRD/KuiperVnHOE
for the supplementary material.

Equation (
30
) is a third order formula for computing the CDF, which is much more accurate than the first order version obtained by Kuiper. For
n=6
, the approximation error is about
6^{-3}\approx 4.63\times 10^{-3}
, thus the probability calculated with (
30
) must be accurate enough for solving practical problems in science, technology, engineering and mathematics. In other words, (
30
) can be applied to the scenario when the sample capacity
n
is relative small. Although we can get more accurate result for
k>5
according to (
28
) theoretically, it is not necessary to do so due to the satisfactory accuracy for
k=5
and boring complexity of calculating the
B_{i}(c)
for large
i
.

3.2
Fixed-point Equation for the Upper Tail Quantile

For the FFP
\alpha=\Pr\left\{\sqrt{n}\cdot V_{n}>c\right\}=1-\Pr\left\{\sqrt{n}\cdot V_{n}\leq c\right\}
we have

\displaystyle 1-\alpha

\displaystyle=\sum^{k}_{i=0}\frac{B_{i}(c)}{n^{i/2}}+\mathcal{O}\left(n^{-(k+1)/2}\right)

(34)

\displaystyle\approx\sum^{k}_{i=0}\sum^{2}_{j=0}\frac{B^{j}_{i}(c)}{n^{i/2}}\mathrm{e}^{-2j^{2}c^{2}}+\mathcal{O}\left(n^{-(k+1)/2}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum^{2}_{j=0}\left[\sum^{k}_{i=0}\frac{B^{j}_{i}(c)}{n^{i/2}}\right]\mathrm{e}^{-2j^{2}c^{2}}+\mathcal{O}\left(n^{-(k+1)/2}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum^{k}_{i=0}\frac{B^{0}_{i}(c)}{n^{i}/2}+\mathrm{e}^{-2c^{2}}\sum^{k}_{i=0}\frac{B^{1}_{i}(c)}{n^{i/2}}+\mathrm{e}^{-8c^{2}}\sum^{k}_{i=0}\frac{B^{2}_{i}(c)}{n^{i/2}}+\mathcal{O}\left(n^{-(k+1)/2}\right).

Hence, we approximately have

\alpha\approx\left[1-\sum^{k}_{i=0}\frac{B^{0}_{i}(c)}{n^{i/2}}\right]-\mathrm{e}^{-2c^{2}}\sum^{k}_{i=0}\frac{B^{1}_{i}(c)}{n^{i/2}}-\mathrm{e}^{-8c^{2}}\sum^{k}_{i=0}\frac{B^{2}_{i}(c)}{n^{i/2}}

(35)

with the error bound
\mathcal{O}\left(n^{-(k+1)/2}\right)
.
Let

\displaystyle A_{j}(c,n,k)

\displaystyle=-\sum^{k}_{i=0}\frac{B^{j}_{i}(c)}{n^{i/2}}

(36)

\displaystyle=-\left[B^{j}_{0}(c)+\frac{B^{j}_{1}(c)}{n^{1/2}}+\frac{B^{j}_{2}(c)}{n}+\frac{B^{1}_{3}(c)}{n^{3/2}}+\cdots+\frac{B^{1}_{k}(c)}{n^{k/2}}\right],

then the following iterative formulae hold for computing
A^{(k)}_{i}
, i.e.,

A_{j}(c,n,r+1)=A_{j}(c,n,r)-\frac{B_{r+1}^{j}(c)}{n^{(r+1)/2}},\quad j=0,1,2

(37)

for
r=0,1,2,\cdots,k-1
. It should be noted that each
B^{0}_{i}(c)
is an expression about
n
and does not depend on
c
, thus we can omit the variable
c
and denote it as
B^{0}_{i}
. Therefore, for
j\in\left\{0,1,2\right\}
we have

\left\{\begin{aligned} A_{0}(n,k)&=-\left[B^{0}_{0}+\frac{B^{0}_{1}}{n^{1/2}}+\frac{B^{0}_{2}}{n}+\frac{B^{0}_{3}}{n^{3/2}}+\cdots+\frac{B^{0}_{k}}{n^{k/2}}\right]\\
A_{1}(c,n,k)&=-\left[B^{1}_{0}(c)+\frac{B^{1}_{1}(c)}{n^{1/2}}+\frac{B^{1}_{2}(c)}{n}+\frac{B^{1}_{3}(c)}{n^{3/2}}+\cdots+\frac{B^{1}_{k}(c)}{n^{k/2}}\right]\\
A_{2}(c,n,k)&=-\left[B^{2}_{0}(c)+\frac{B^{2}_{1}(c)}{n^{1/2}}+\frac{B^{2}_{2}(c)}{n}+\frac{B^{2}_{3}(c)}{n^{3/2}}+\cdots+\frac{B^{2}_{k}(c)}{n^{k/2}}\right]\end{aligned}\right.

(38)

By substituting (
38
) into (
35
), we can obtain

\alpha=\left[1+A_{0}(n,k)\right]+A_{1}(c,n,k)\mathrm{e}^{-2c^{2}}+A_{2}(c,n,k)\mathrm{e}^{-8c^{2}}.

(39)

Multiplying the factor
\mathrm{e}^{2c^{2}}
on the two sides of (

\mathrm{e}^{2c^{2}}\left\{\alpha-\left[1+A_{0}(n,k)\right]\right\}=A_{1}(c,n,k)+A_{2}(c,n,k)\mathrm{e}^{-6c^{2}}.

(40)

Equivalently, we have the fixed-point equation

\boxed{2c^{2}+\ln\left[\alpha-1-A_{0}(n,k)\right]=\ln\left[A_{1}(c,n,k)+A_{2}(c,n,k)\mathrm{e}^{-6c^{2}}\right]}

(41)

for the UTQ

for
K_{n}

for

3.3
Fixed-Point Method for Solving Upper Tail Quantile

We can obtain the critical value
c^{\alpha}_{n}(k)
and the upper tail quantile
v^{\alpha}_{n}(k)=c^{\alpha}_{n}(k)/\sqrt{n}
by solving the fixed-point of (
41
) with iterative methods.

3.3.1
Direct Iterative Method

Let

{f}_{\mathrm{ctm}}(c,\alpha,n,k)=\sqrt{\frac{\ln\left[A_{1}(c,n,k)+A_{2}(c,n,k)\mathrm{e}^{-6c^{2}}\right]-\ln\left[\alpha-1-A_{0}(n,k)\right]}{2}},

(45)

then
c
is the fixed-point of

c=\mathcal{A}^{\alpha,k}_{n}({f}_{\mathrm{ctm}},c)={f}_{\mathrm{ctm}}(c,\alpha,n,k)

(46)

We can solve the fixed-point of (
41
) with the following iterative formula

c_{i+1}=\mathcal{A}^{\alpha,k}_{n}({f}_{\mathrm{ctm}},c_{i})={f}_{\mathrm{ctm}}(c_{i},\alpha,n,k),\quad i=0,1,2,3,\cdots

(47)

with an appropriate initial value
c_{0}
.

3.3.2
Newton Iterative Method

Let

{f}_{\mathrm{nlm}}(c,\alpha,n,k)=2c^{2}+\ln\left[\alpha-1-A_{0}(n,k)\right]-\ln\left[A_{1}(c,n,k)+A_{2}(c,n,k)\mathrm{e}^{-6c^{2}}\right],

(48)

then the Newton’s iterative formula for the fixed-point can be written by

c_{i+1}=\mathcal{B}^{\alpha,k}_{n}({f}_{\mathrm{nlm}},c_{i})

(49)

where

\mathcal{B}^{\alpha,k}_{n}({f}_{\mathrm{nlm}},c)=c-\frac{{f}_{\mathrm{nlm}}(c,\alpha,n,k)}{{f}_{\mathrm{nlm}}^{\prime}(c,\alpha,n,k)}

(50)

is the Newton’s updating function.
The mapping
\mathcal{B}^{\alpha,k}_{n}({f}_{\mathrm{nlm}},c)
is a contractive mapping for appropriate domain of
c
.

5.2
Probability of Type I Errors

The order of HOE for Kuiper’s
V_{n}
-statistic has significant impacts on the
probability of Type I errors
, which is denoted by
p^{\text{I}}_{\text{err}}
. In order to demonstrate this impact, we take the standard normal population and sample data to validate the performance of interest.

Let
X_{1},X_{2},\cdots,X_{{n}_{\mathrm{rep}}}\overset{\textrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. We can perform
{n}_{\mathrm{rep}}
independent Monte Carlo experiments to calculate the value of
p^{\text{I}}_{\text{err}}
. For the
i
-th Kuiper’s goodness-of-fit test with significance level
\alpha
, we can set the following hypothesis:

•

null hypothesis
\mathcal{H}_{0}
, namely the data set
\left\{x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{{n}_{\mathrm{rep}}}\right\}
generated form the
X_{1},X_{2},\cdots,X_{{n}_{\mathrm{rep}}}
satisfy the distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
;

•

alternative hypothesis
\mathcal{H}_{1}
, namely the data set
\left\{x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{{n}_{\mathrm{rep}}}\right\}
does not satisfy the distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.

Let
\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{I}}_{i}(k)
be the indicator variable for the Type I error with
k
-th order method such that

\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{I}}_{i}(k)=\begin{cases}1,&\mathcal{H}_{0}\text{\penalty\ is rejected --- Type I error occurs}\\
0,&\mathcal{H}_{0}\text{\penalty\ is accepted --- no error}\\
\end{cases}

(52)

for
1\leq i\leq{n}_{\mathrm{rep}}
, then the
p^{\text{I}}_{\text{err}}
for the
k
-th order method can be calculated by

p^{\text{I}}_{\text{err}}(k)=\dfrac{1}{{n}_{\mathrm{rep}}}\sum_{i=1}^{{n}_{\mathrm{rep}}}\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{I}}_{i}(k)

according to the large number’s law if
{n}_{\mathrm{rep}}
is large enough. In our experiment, we set
{n}_{\mathrm{rep}}=1000
. The simulation result is shown in
Table

8
.
It is trivial to obtain some observations for the same sample capacity
n
:

•

For
1\leq k\leq 5
and
n\geq 6
, we always have
p^{\text{I}}_{\text{err}}(k)\leq\alpha=0.05
. However, for the Kolmogrov-Smirnov test we have

p^{\text{I}}_{\text{err}}>\alpha
and for
n\in\left\{7,9,10,30,50,100\right\}
we can find that
p^{\text{I}}_{\text{err}}
is near
\alpha
although
p^{\text{I}}_{\text{err}}<\alpha
. Moreover, for
n\in\left\{6,7\right\}
the
p^{\text{I}}_{\text{err}}
for the Stephens’s test is larger than
\alpha
.

•

For Kuiper’s test, we have
p^{\text{I}}_{\text{err}}(2)=p^{\text{I}}_{\text{err}}(3)=p^{\text{I}}_{\text{err}}(4)=p^{\text{I}}_{\text{err}}(5)
due to the Kuiper pairs
\left\langle{c^{0.05}_{n}(k)},{v^{0.05}_{n}(k)}\right\rangle
are approximately the same for
2\leq k\leq 5
according to
Table

. In other words,
Table

and
Table

imply that
the precision of the the Kuiper pair

beyond the third decimal place is irrelevant to the calculation of the probability of Type I errors.

•

For
k\geq 2
, the

is much less than the
p^{\text{I}}_{\text{err}}(1)
, which means HOE method is better than the first order method.

•

The

Consequently, for small sample capacity

5.3
Discussion

It should be remarked that the configurations of
\alpha
and
n
are correlated in applications. Our suggestions based on the simulation for the parameters
\alpha
and
n
are as follows:

•

for
\alpha=10^{-2}
, we should set
n\geq 6
;

•

for
\alpha=10^{-3}
, we should set
n\geq 56
;

•

for
\alpha=10^{-4}
, we should set
n\geq 560
.

Generally, the smaller the
\alpha
is, the larger the sample capacity
n
should be.
The reason for these phenomenons is obvious: the
\alpha
means the upper tail significance level or the probability of Type I errors in hypothesis test, hence it is unwise to set tiny
\alpha
when the sample capacity
n
is small in the sense of science and statistics. In other words, only large sample capacity
n
permits small
\alpha
for the probability of Type I errors.

6
Conclusion

Kuiper’s formula for computing the CDF, FFP and UTQ is limited for small sample capacity
n
since its accuracy is controlled by
\mathcal{O}\left(n^{-1}\right)
.
With the help of high order expansion of
\Phi_{n}(a,b)=\exp(\Dif_{n})\Phi(a,b)
, the CDF of
V_{n}
can be generalized in order to obtain a small upper bound
\mathcal{O}\left(n^{-(k+1)/2}\right)
where
k\in\mathbb{N}
is the expansion order. For
k=5
, the novel formula (
30
) and its equivalent forms are obtained through massive calculations to compute the CDF, FFP and UTQ with the approximation error
\mathcal{O}\left(n^{-3}\right)
, which can be applied directly for the scenarios with small capacity
n
.

Unlike the Stephens’s work
[
11
]
on the similar objective of improving the accuracy of computing CDF and FFP, our HOE method is more natural and simple. Stephens’s modified Kuiper test
T_{n}
is not compatible with the large sample capacity due to the asymptotic behavior
T_{n}\sim K_{n}
for sufficiently large
n
. By comparison, we always have
\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\Pr\left\{K_{n}\leq c\right\}=B_{0}(c)
,
which leads to the formulae for the large sample capacity naturally.

With the help of the numerical results for the Kuiper pairs
\left\langle{c^{\alpha}_{n}(k)},{v^{\alpha}_{n}(k)}\right\rangle
, the potential users can set the order
k
appropriately according to his/her practical problem. Our recommendation for the choice of the expansion order is
k=5
for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
.

For the convenience of potential users such as researchers, engineers, statisticians and college students, the fixed-point algorithms are also designed and explained briefly. We believe that the pseudo-code for the algorithms and C code for the implementation of the algorithms based on the HOE method are valuable for the readers and users of interest.

Appendix A
Goodness-of-fit Test with Kuiper’s
V_{n}
statistic

A.1
Problem and Assumption

For the finite
n
i.i.d samples
X_{1},X_{2},\cdots,X_{n}
, a fundamental problem is: whether the samples
\left\{X_{t}:1\leq t\leq n\right\}
satisfy the given continuous distribution of a population
X
with the distribution function
F_{X}(x)
? Here we assume that the samples are identically independent variables.

A.2
Hypothesis

For the continuous population
X
with the CDF
F_{X}(x)
and sample set
\left\{X_{t}:1\leq t\leq n\right\}
, the null hypothesis
\mathcal{H}_{0}
is: the samples
\left\{X_{t}:1\leq t\leq n\right\}
satisfy the distribution
F_{X}(x)
.

A.3
Calculation of
V_{n}
with Sample Data

The Kuiper’s
V_{n}
statistic is the sum of
D^{+}_{n}
and
D^{-}_{n}
, which can be computed with the sample set
\left\{X_{t}:1\leq t\leq n\right\}
directly. For the sample set
\left\{X_{t}:1\leq t\leq n\right\}
of population
X
, the
empirical distribution function
(EDF) will be

\hat{F}_{X}(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum^{n}_{t=1}\mathbb{1}[X_{t}\leq x]

(53)

where

\mathbb{1}[X_{t}\leq x]=\begin{cases}1,&X_{t}\leq x\\
0,&X_{t}>x\end{cases}

(54)

is the indicator function.
Suppose the
X_{(t)}
is the order statistics such that

X_{(1)}<X_{(2)}<\cdots<X_{(k)}<\cdots<X_{(n-1)}<X_{(n)},

then the EDF can be calculated easily for the sorted data, that is,

\displaystyle\hat{q}_{t}=\hat{F}_{X}\left(X_{(t)}\right)=\hat{q}^{(0)}_{t}=\frac{1}{n}\sum^{n}_{i=1}\mathbb{1}[X_{i}\leq X_{(t)}]=\frac{t}{n}.

(55)

Let

q_{t}=F_{X}\left(X_{(t)}\right)

(56)

then

\left\{\begin{aligned} D^{+}_{n}&=\max_{1\leq t\leq n}\left[\hat{F}_{X}\left(X_{(t)}\right)-F_{X}\left(X_{(t)}\right)\right]=\max_{1\leq t\leq n}\left(\hat{q}_{t}-q_{t}\right)\\
D^{-}_{n}&=\max_{1\leq t\leq n}\left[F_{X}\left(X_{(t)}\right)-\hat{F}_{X}\left(X_{(t)}\right)\right]=\max_{1\leq t\leq n}\left(q_{t}-\hat{q}_{t}\right)\end{aligned}\right.

(57)

Thus we can obtain the value of Kuiper’s
V_{n}
-test statistic

V_{n}=D^{+}_{n}+D^{-}_{n}=\max_{1\leq t\leq n}\left(\hat{q}_{t}-q_{t}\right)+\max_{1\leq t\leq n}\left(q_{t}-\hat{q}_{t}\right)

with the date samples
\left\{X_{t}:1\leq t\leq n\right\}
.

Please note that we can sort the sample data
\left\{X_{t}:1\leq t\leq n\right\}
by a sorting algorithm (such as the quick sorting with computational complexity
\mathcal{O}\left(n\log_{2}n\right)
) in an increasing order, thus the
t
-th sorted value is the
X_{(t)}
of interest. We also remark that the disadvantage for the
\hat{q}_{t}=t/n
is that
\hat{q}_{n}\equiv 1
when
t=n
.
There are several choices for computing the empirical probability

\hat{q}_{t}
listed in
Table

9
for
1\leq t\leq n
and different expressions for
\hat{q}_{t}
will lead to different performances for the Kuiper’s goodness-of-fit test.

Appendix B
Methods for Solving Fixed-point

B.1
Principle and Unified theoretic Framework

For a nonlinear equation

f(x)=0,\quad x\in[a,b],

(59)

usually we can convert it into an equivalent fixed-point equation with the form

x=T(f,x),\quad x\in[a,b]

(60)

where
f(\cdot)
is a mapping and
T(\cdot,\cdot)
is contractive operator. There are two typical categories of fixed-point equation:

(i)

Direct iterative scheme

x_{i+1}=T({f}_{\mathrm{ctm}}(x_{i}),x_{i})={f}_{\mathrm{ctm}}(x_{i})

(61)

which depends on the function
{f}_{\mathrm{ctm}}
.

(ii)

Newton’s iterative scheme

x_{i+1}=T({f}_{\mathrm{nlm}}(x_{i}),x_{i})=x_{i}-\frac{{f}_{\mathrm{nlm}}(x_{i})}{{f}_{\mathrm{nlm}}^{\prime}(x_{i})},

(62)

which depends not only on the equivalent nonlinear mapping

{f}_{\mathrm{nlm}}(x)
from

\phi(x)=0\Longleftrightarrow{f}_{\mathrm{nlm}}(x)=0

(63)

but also on its derivative
{f}_{\mathrm{nlm}}^{\prime}(x)
.

We remark that the strings “ctm” and “nlm” in the subscripts means
c
on
t
ractive
m
apping and
n
on
l
inear
m
apping respectively.

The stopping condition for the iterative method is satisfied by the Cauchy’s criteria for the convergent sequence
\left\{x_{i}:i=0,1,2,3,\cdots\right\}

d(x_{i+1},x_{i})<\epsilon

(64)

where
\epsilon
denotes the precision and
d(\cdot,\cdot)
is a metric for the difference of the current value of
x
, say
{x}_{\mathrm{guess}}
and the its updated version, say
{x}_{\mathrm{improve}}
. The simplest choice of
d(\cdot,\cdot)
is the absolute value function for
x\in[a,b]\in\mathbb{R}
, i.e.,

d(x_{i},x_{i+1})=d(x_{i+1},x_{i})=\left|x_{i+1}-x_{i}\right|

(65)

For practical problems arising in various fields, there may be some extra parameters in the function
\phi
, say
\phi(x,\alpha,n)
where
\alpha
is a real number,
n
is an integer and the order of
x,\alpha,n
is not important
1
1

1

In other words, both
\phi(x,\alpha,n)
and
\phi(x,n,\alpha)
are feasible and correct for computer programming.
. In general, we can use the notations
\phi(x,\mu_{1},\cdots,\mu_{r}),f(x,\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{r})
and
T(f,x,\mu_{1},\cdots,\mu_{r})
to represent the scenario when there are some extra parameters. We remark that
the derivative of
f(x,\mu_{1},\cdots,m_{r})
with respect to the variable
x
should be calculated by

f^{\prime}(x,\mu_{1},\cdots\mu_{r})=\lim_{\Delta x\to 0}\frac{f(x+\Delta x,\mu_{1},\cdots,\mu_{r})-f(x,\mu_{1},\cdots,\mu_{r})}{\Delta x}

(66)

for the Newton’s iterative method.
