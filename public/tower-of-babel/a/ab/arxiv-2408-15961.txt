Supertranslations from Scattering Amplitudes
Asaad Elkhidir, Donal O’Connell, Radu Roiban

Abstract
On-shell methods have found a new application to local observables such as asymptotic radiation fields and gravitational waveforms. While these observables are invariant under small gauge transformations, they are known to depend on a choice of asymptotic gauge; in gravity on asymptotically Minkowski spacetimes, this is a choice of BMS frame. In this letter, we provide a method for capturing these supertranslations, to all orders in perturbation theory, using the on-shell framework of scattering amplitudes.

IV
Supertranslations

Consider the expectation value of the metric operator in the future asymptotic state
|\psi\rangle_{\text{out}}={S}\exp(iQ_{s})|\psi\rangle
:

\displaystyle h_{\mu\nu}(x)

\displaystyle\equiv\braket{\psi|e^{-iQ_{s}}{S}^{\dagger}\mathbb{h}_{\mu\nu}(x){S}e^{iQ_{s}}|\psi}

(24)

\displaystyle\quad=\braket{\psi|e^{-i(Q_{s}+Q_{h})}{S}^{\dagger}\mathbb{h}_{\mu\nu}(x){S}e^{i(Q_{s}+Q_{h})}|\psi}\,.

We introduced the hard charge above, noting that
Q_{h}\ket{\psi}=0
.
More generally, if the initial state includes a gravitational background, then inclusion of
Q_{h}
in this way introduces a phase which cancels in the expectation value because it is a function only of incoming kinematic invariants; matter hard charges also cancel in the expectation value for a similar reason.
We have explicitly verified that
Q_{h,M}
gives vanishing contributions to
\langle\mathbb{h}_{\mu\nu}\rangle
in processes with two incoming and two outgoing matter particles.
Since
[{S},Q_{s}+Q_{h}]=0
and
[Q_{s},Q_{h}]=0
, we may equivalently write,
using also Eq. (
8
),

\displaystyle h_{\mu\nu}(x)

\displaystyle=\braket{\psi|{S}^{\dagger}e^{-iQ_{h}}e^{-iQ_{s}}\mathbb{h}_{\mu\nu}(x)e^{iQ_{s}}e^{iQ_{h}}{S}|\psi}

(25)

\displaystyle=\braket{\psi|{S}^{\dagger}e^{-iQ_{h}}\mathbb{h}_{\mu\nu}(x)e^{iQ_{h}}{S}|\psi}+h_{\mu\nu}^{\textrm{Schw}}(x)\,,

Thus, to all orders, the effect of the soft charge is to introduce the linearised Schwarzschild term in the metric.

The effect of the hard charge can also be made explicit in Eq. (
25
) to all orders. Indeed, using Eq. (
22
), it is not difficult to check that

\displaystyle e^{-iQ_{h,0}}a_{\eta}(k)e^{iQ_{h,0}}=e^{i\omega T(n)}a_{\eta}(k)\,,

(26)

where for each matter particle we have defined
T(n)
as

\displaystyle T(n)\equiv 2G(p\cdot n)\left(\log(v\cdot n)+Y(\beta,\epsilon)\right)\,.

(27)

Here, we set
\omega=k^{0}
and
n=k/\omega
and made use of the relation between the momentum
p
and the velocity
v
.
Notice that the ratio
K^{\mu}/K^{0}
, which appeared in the definition, Eq. (
22
), of the hard charge, has been replaced by

due to the action of the number operator on
a_{\eta}(k)
.

In view of the
u
dependence of Eq. (
1
), the net result is a shift for each matter particle,

\displaystyle u\rightarrow u+2Gm\,v\cdot n\log v\cdot n\,,

(28)

where we absorbed the regulator-dependent

by an appropriate choice of the origin of retarded time.
As

after use of Eq. (
1
), the shift in Eq. (
28
) is an angle-dependent translation of the retarded time, i.e. a BMS supertranslation, which is in fact precisely that of Veneziano and Vilkovisky.

Classically, the Veneziano-Vilkovisky supertranslation
[
27
]
relates the “intrinsic” and “canonical” BMS frames.
In the former
[
27
]
, the metric strain at
{\cal O}(G)
is that of linearised Schwarzschild while in the latter the metric strain is zero
[
28
]
.
Our result demonstrates that the hard charge generates a large change of coordinates. Thus, while rooted in perturbative (quantum) field theory, our approach makes direct contact with the classical physics of large gauge transformations.

Eq. (
28
) also shows that the Veneziano-Vilkovisky transformation connects the intrinsic and canonical frames to all orders in the coupling. This situation is reminiscent of the Kerr-Schild coordinates, in which all higher-order corrections to the linearised metric are absorbed by a judicious change of coordinates.
Although the phase (
26

[

and further confirmed in

,

]

[

and Multipolar post-Minkowskian
[

results for the one-loop waveform.

As another perspective, notice that the supertranslation of Eq. (
28

This
echoes the suggestion of Ref.
[

that large gauge effects can be connected to a modified LSZ prescription.

\displaystyle 2G\sum_{X,h}\int\mathrm{d}\Phi(k,\tilde{p}_{1},\tilde{p}_{2},X)\bra{\psi}{S}^{\dagger}a_{\eta}^{\dagger}(k)\ket{\tilde{p}_{1},\tilde{p}_{2},X}

\displaystyle\times\bra{\tilde{p}_{1},\tilde{p}_{2},X}a_{\eta}(k){S}\ket{\psi}k_{[\alpha}p_{\beta]}\log(v\cdot n),

(36)

where we have inserted a complete set of intermediate states. Noting that each of the matrix elements above contributes at
\mathcal{O}(\kappa^{3})
, we see that the leading contribution of
L^{\text{hard}}_{\mu\nu}
enters at
\mathcal{O}(G^{4})
. Indeed, this contribution is known to arise from static fields
[
18
]
and is associated with the nonlinear memory effect
[
48
,
49
,
50
]
.

VI
Connection to Classical Treatment

The classical charges are expectation values of the quantum operators.
To make the connection with the classical BMS transformation more transparent, we will reorganize the frequency integrals in Eqs. (
5
) and (
22
) as integrals over the retarded time, and also expose the parameter
T(n)
of the transformation.

Focusing on the most familiar case of gravity and using the amplitude (
6
), the soft charge (
5
) operator is

\displaystyle Q_{s}=\sum_{\eta}\frac{1}{4}\int\hat{\mathrm{d}}\omega\,\hat{\mathrm{d}}^{2}\Omega\,\omega\,\hat{\delta}_{+}(\omega)\frac{\kappa\;e_{\eta}^{\mu\nu}p_{\mu}p_{\nu}}{n\cdot p}a_{\eta}(\omega n)+\textrm{h.c.}

(37)

where, as before,
n=k/\omega=(1,\bm{n}(\theta,\phi))
is a general null vector. We denote to the polar angles
\theta
and
\phi
as
n^{A}
.

It is useful to choose a basis of polarisation vectors
2
2

2

We work in a mostly-minus signature.

\displaystyle\epsilon_{A}^{\mu}=\partial_{A}n^{\mu}=D_{A}n^{\mu}\quad A=1,2\,,

(38)

where
D_{A}
is the covariant derivative on the (unit) sphere.
These vectors have the property that

\displaystyle\epsilon_{A}\cdot\epsilon_{B}=-\Omega_{AB}\,,

(39)

where
\Omega_{AB}
is the unit metric on the two-sphere.
We may use this basis to write the graviton polarisation tensor as

\displaystyle e^{\eta}_{\mu\nu}=e^{\eta}_{AB}\epsilon^{A}_{\mu}\epsilon^{B}_{\nu}\,.

(40)

(The
A,B
indices are raised and lowered using
\Omega
.)
Tracelessness of
e^{\eta}_{\mu\nu}
implies that
e^{\eta}_{AB}\Omega^{AB}=0
.
The soft charge then becomes

\displaystyle Q_{s}=\frac{1}{4}

\displaystyle\int\hat{\mathrm{d}}^{2}\Omega\,\kappa\,\frac{D_{A}(n\cdot p)D_{B}(n\cdot p)}{n\cdot p}

(41)

\displaystyle\times\sum_{\eta}\int\hat{\mathrm{d}}\omega\;\omega\;\hat{\delta}_{+}(\omega)a_{\eta}(\omega n)e_{\eta}^{AB}+\textrm{h.c.}\,.

We proceed by invoking the equality

\displaystyle e_{\eta}^{AB}D_{A}D_{B}T(n)=2Ge_{\eta}^{AB}\frac{D_{A}(n\cdot p)D_{B}(n\cdot p)}{n\cdot p}\,,

(42)

which relies on

D_{A}D_{B}\bm{n}=-\Omega_{AB}\bm{n}

and tracelessness of
e_{\eta}^{AB}
.
This allows us to expose the parameter
T(n)
in the first integral in Eq. (
41
).
For the
\omega
integral, we define a shear operator (of dimension length) by

\displaystyle\mathbb{f}_{AB}(u,\bm{n})=|\bm{x}|\kappa\,\mathbb{h}_{\mu\nu}(x)\epsilon^{\mu}_{A}\epsilon^{\nu}_{B}\,,

(43)

with
\mathbb{h}_{\mu\nu}
given by the stationary phase approximation in the second Eq. (
1
). Then the soft charge becomes

\displaystyle Q_{s}=\frac{-1}{8\pi G}\int\mathrm{d}u\;\mathrm{d}^{2}\Omega\;\dot{\mathbb{f}}^{AB}\,D_{A}D_{B}T(n)\,.

(44)

As we will see below, this form of the soft charge operator makes
the connection with the classical BMS charges more transparent.
Before doing so, we rewrite the hard charge in a similar manner.
We note that

\displaystyle\sum_{\eta}\int_{0}^{\infty}\hat{\mathrm{d}}\omega\,\omega^{2}a^{\dagger}_{\eta}(\omega n)\,a_{\eta}(\omega n)=\frac{\pi}{4G}\int\mathrm{d}u\;:\dot{\mathbb{f}}_{AB}\dot{\mathbb{f}}^{AB}:\,,

(45)

using the normal-ordering symbol, and discarding terms involving
\int\mathrm{d}\omega\,\theta(\omega)\theta(-\omega)
.
The hard charge is

\displaystyle Q_{h,0}=-\frac{1}{32\pi G}\int\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}^{2}\Omega\;:\dot{\mathbb{f}}_{AB}\dot{\mathbb{f}}^{AB}:\,T(n)\ .

(46)

The total charge at null infinity,
Q_{0}=Q_{s}+Q_{h,0}
, is then

\displaystyle Q_{0}=\frac{-1}{8\pi G}\int\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}^{2}\Omega\,T(n)\left(D_{A}D_{B}\dot{\mathbb{f}}^{AB}+\frac{1}{4}:\dot{\mathbb{f}}_{AB}\dot{\mathbb{f}}^{AB}:\right)\,.

(47)

The classical expectation value of this operator in the out-state
S\ket{\psi}
,
Q_{\textrm{out}}\equiv\braket{\psi|S^{\dagger}Q_{0}S|\psi}
is

\displaystyle Q_{\textrm{out}}=\frac{-1}{8\pi G}\int\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}^{2}\Omega\,T(n)\left(D_{A}D_{B}\dot{{f}}^{AB}+\frac{1}{4}\dot{{f}}_{AB}\dot{{f}}^{AB}\right)\,,

(48)

where the classical shear (in the future) is

\displaystyle f_{AB}\equiv\bra{\psi}S^{\dagger}{\mathbb{f}}_{AB}S\ket{\psi}

(49)

and we assumed that
S\ket{\psi}
is a classical state, so that

\displaystyle\bra{\psi}S^{\dagger}:\dot{\mathbb{f}}_{AB}\dot{\mathbb{f}}^{AB}:S\ket{\psi}=\bra{\psi}S^{\dagger}\dot{\mathbb{f}}_{AB}S\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}S^{\dagger}\dot{\mathbb{f}}^{AB}S\ket{\psi}

(50)

(see Ref.
[
51
]
for a discussion of these relations in terms of amplitudes). Notice that the forward scattering (
S=S^{\dagger}=1
) term in
f_{AB}
does not vanish but instead contains the static
\mathcal{O}(G)
contribution to the shear. However, the charge
Q_{\textrm{out}}
only depends on
\dot{f}_{AB}
so that it does not receive contributions from the forward scattering term. This allows us to identify

\displaystyle Q_{\textrm{out}}=\Delta Q_{0}\equiv\braket{\psi|S^{\dagger}Q_{0}S|\psi}-\braket{\psi|Q_{0}|\psi},

(51)

where we have subtracted the vanishing term
\braket{\psi|Q_{0}|\psi}
.

To connect this to the classical BMS charges, consider the total variation of the classical supertranslation charge at null infinity

\displaystyle\Delta Q_{\textrm{cl}}\equiv\int\mathrm{d}u\,\dot{Q}_{\textrm{cl}}=\int\mathrm{d}u\,\hat{\mathrm{d}}^{2}\Omega\,\dot{m}\,T(n)\ .

(52)

Using the Bondi mass-loss formula which, in our conventions, is

\displaystyle\dot{m}=-\frac{1}{8\pi G}\left(D_{A}D_{B}\dot{f}^{AB}+\frac{1}{4}\dot{f}_{AB}\dot{f}^{AB}\right)\,,

(53)

it is easy to see that
\Delta Q_{\textrm{cl}}
coincides with Eq.(
48
). We emphasise that this equality holds provided that there are no gravitons in the initial state
\ket{\psi}
.

Note that the classical charge in Eq. (
52
) is defined by a surface integral at null infinity.
In particular, it does not include contributions
Q_{h,M}
from massive particles located at future/past timelike infinity
i^{+}
and
i^{-}
respectively.
Such contributions are accounted for using the boundary-to-bulk propagators introduced in
[
52
,
53
,
54
]
.
In general BMS conservation laws require that the variation of the total BMS charge vanishes, i.e.

\displaystyle\Delta Q_{0}+\Delta Q_{h,M}=0,

(54)

where
\Delta Q_{h,M}
is the difference of the hard charges of the incoming and outgoing matter particles.

VII
Discussion

The choice of BMS frame has observable consequences, as demonstrated by differences in physical predictions in the intrinsic and canonical frames.
Starting with the observation that low frequency mediators break assumptions built into the large-distance approximation for asymptotic observables, we defined new asymptotic states that resum their contribution. They turn out to be coherent states dressing the usual single-particle asymptotic states.
We showed that the argument
Q_{s}
of the dressing phase operator, which is linear in the mediator’s creation and annihilation operators, can be completed to an operator
Q=Q_{s}+Q_{h}
that commutes with the scattering operator to all orders in the coupling, at least in the classical (large mass) limit.

Using this conservation we found the exact contribution of the coherent state of low frequency mediators to the asymptotic radiation field, observing the close relation between the linearized Schwarzschild background and
a particular BMS supertranslation in the far future.
We strengthened this connection by explicitly showing that the final-state expectation value of the conserved operator
Q
equals the (variation of the) classical BMS charge. This also makes contact with discussions of BMS symmetry of the
S
matrix from a general relativity perspective, e.g.
[
55
,
56
,
57
,
58
,
59
,
60
]
, and its connection to the Faddeev-Kulish formalism
[
61
,
62
]
while effectively solving the supertranslation Ward identity for final-state observables.

It would be interesting to understand how to include in the on-shell formalism more general BMS supertranslations than the Veneziano-Vilkovisky one, connecting the intrinsic and canonical BMS frames, perhaps by extending the expression for the soft charge to a more general function of angles (or of
p\cdot n
) and constructing the corresponding hard charge.
Similarly, it remains an interesting problem for the future to understand whether analogous results hold for superrotations
[
63
,
64
,
65
]
. The corresponding Ward identities have been discussed in
[
66
,
67
]
and their connection to classical soft theorems in
[
68
,
69
,
70
,
71
,
72
]
.

We have also evaluated the contribution of the low-frequency mediators to the orbital angular momentum radiated in a scattering process and recovered
known results and extended them to all orders in the coupling.

While our main focus was on gravitationally-interacting
massive particles, our analysis carries over, with minimal modifications, to other types of mediators. It would be interesting to explore the scalar charge model
[
73
,
74
]
, in which one of the massive scalars interacts with gravity through a massless scalar.
The asymptotic symmetries have been discussed in
[
75
,
76
]
.

One aim of our work was to include low frequency mediators which spoil the large
\omega|\bm{x}|
assumption of the large-distance approximation. As mentioned, an alternative approach to this problem is to have the asymptotic states created and destroyed at finite distance as well as a finite IR cutoff for the mediator’s frequency.
At finite, but not large distance (when
1/|\bm{x}|^{2}
terms in the metric are relevant) BMS transformations are no longer expected to be symmetries of the metric
[
28
]
, and thus our analysis does not apply.
Thus, exploring the relation between this and our approach should shed light on the interpretation of BMS transformations away from null infinity.
