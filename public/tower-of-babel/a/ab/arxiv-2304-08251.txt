Nouar Chorfi, Salem Abdelmalek, Samir Bendoukha

Abstract
In this article, we consider an HIV/AIDS epidemic model with four classes of individuals. We have discussed about basic properties of the system and found the basic reproduction number R 0 R_{0} of the system. The stability analysis of the model shows that the system is locally as well as globally asymptotically stable at disease-free equilibrium E 0 E_{0} when R 0 < 1 R_{0}<1 . When R 0 > 1 R_{0}>1 endemic equilibrium E 1 E_{1} exists and the system becomes locally asymptotically stable at E 1 E_{1} . An optimal controller is presented that considers the use of three different measures to combat the spread of HIV/AIDS, namely: the use of condoms, screening of unaware infective individuals, and treatment of the HIV infected population. The objective of the optimal controller is to minimize the size of the susceptible and infected populations. Our investigation of the controlled system starts with establishing the existence of the optimal control, followed by identifying the necessary conditions of optimality.

2
Mathematical model

In this section, we consider the fourth-order model proposed in
[
10
]
. The model assumes a total population of size
N(t)
at time
{\large t}
divided into four sub-populations: susceptible individuals
S(t)
,
infective individuals who are not aware of their infection
I_{1}(t)
, HIV
positive individuals who are aware of their infection
I_{2}(t)
, and
individuals with AIDS
A(t)
. Hence, by design,

N(t)=S(t)+I_{1}(t)+I_{2}(t)+A(t).

(2.1)

The model dynamics are described by the ODE system:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\dfrac{dS(t)}{dt}\text{ }{\large=}\text{ }Q_{0}-\beta_{m}S(t){\large-\mu}S(t),\\
\dfrac{dI_{1}(t)}{dt}\text{ }{\large=}\text{ }\beta_{m}S(t)-\left(\theta+\mu+\delta\right)I_{1}(t),\\
\dfrac{dI_{2}(t)}{dt}\text{ }{\large=}\text{ }\theta I_{1}(t)-\left(\delta+\mu+\pi\right)I_{2}(t),\\
\dfrac{dA(t)}{dt}\text{ }{\large=}\text{ }\delta I_{1}(t)+\delta I_{2}(t)+\pi I_{2}(t)-\left(\alpha+\mu\right)A(t),\end{array}\right.

(2.2)

where

\beta_{m}=\dfrac{\mathbf{(}1-u_{1}\mathbf{)(}\beta_{1}c_{1}I_{1}\mathbf{+}\beta_{2}c_{2}I_{2}\mathbf{+}\beta_{3}c_{3}A\mathbf{)}}{N}.

(2.3)

The system is equipped with initial conditions

S(0)=S_{0},\ I_{1}(0)=I_{0}{}_{1},\ I_{2}(0)=I_{0}{}_{2},\ A(0)=A_{0}.

(2.4)

The terms
c_{i}

(i=1,2,3)

denote the number of
sexual partners of susceptible individuals with unaware infectives, aware
infectives, and AIDS individuals, respectively, in each time period. Also,
\beta_{i}

(i=1,2,3)
represent the interaction probabilities
for susceptible individuals with unaware infectives, aware infectives, and
AIDS individuals, respectively.

Note that
\beta_{m}
involves a control parameter
u_{1}\in[0,1]
,
which represents the successful use of condoms by susceptible individuals as
a protection measure. The term
\theta
measures the rate at which unaware
infectives are detected by a screening method to become aware infectives.
The term
\pi
measures the progression rate at which the already-aware
infective individuals on treatment move to the
A
class in each time
period. Here,
\delta
is the rate by which both types of infectives not on
treatment develop AIDS
.
The parameter
\mu

denotes the natural mortality rate unrelated to HIV/AIDS, whereas
\alpha

denotes the AIDS related death rate. It is assumed that the rate
of contact of susceptibles with AIDS individuals is much less than aware
infectives which in turn is much less than that with unaware infectives
\left(\beta_{3}\ll\beta_{2}\ll\beta_{1}\right)
. This is so because,
on becoming aware of their infection, the infected persons may choose to use
preventive measures and change their behavior and thus may contribute little
in spreading the infection. We assume also that the
A
class is less
sexually active. Now, we describe that all solutions of the system with
non-negative initial data will remain non-negative for all time.

3
Positivity and boundedness of solutions

For the HIV/AIDS transmission system (
2.2
) to be
epidemiologically meaningful, it is important to prove that all solutions
with non-negative initial data will remain non-negative for all time.

3.1
Positivity of solutions

Theorem 1

If
S(0)\geq 0,\ I_{1}(0)\geq 0,\ I_{2}(0)\geq 0
and
A(0)\geq 0
, the
solutions
S(t)
,
I_{1}(t)
,
I_{2}(t)
and
A(t)
of system (
2.1
)
are positive for all
t\geq 0
.

Proof 1

It follows from the first equation of system (
2.2
) that

\frac{dS(t)}{dt}+\left(\beta_{m}+\mu\right)S(t)\geq 0.

(3.1)

Multiplying inequality (
3.1
) by
\exp\left(\mu t+\int_{0}^{t}\beta_{m}(s)ds\right)
yields

\frac{dS(t)}{dt}\exp\left(\mu t+\int_{0}^{t}\beta_{m}(s)ds\right)+\exp\left(\mu t+\int_{0}^{t}\beta_{m}(s)ds\right)\left(\beta_{m}+\mu\right)S(t)\geq 0.

(3.2)

Hence,

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left[S(t)\exp\left(\mu t+\int_{0}^{t}\beta_{m}(s)ds\right)\right]\geq 0.

(3.3)

Integrating (
3.4
) leads to

S(t)\geq S(0)\exp\left(-\mu t-\int_{0}^{t}\beta_{m}(s)ds\right).

(3.4)

Therefore, solution
S(t)
is positive.

Following the same steps for the remaining equations of system (
2.2
)
yields

I_{1}(t)\geq I_{1}(0)\exp(-(\theta+\delta+\mu)t)\geq 0,

(3.5)

I_{2}(t)\geq I_{2}(0)\exp(-(\pi+\delta+\mu)t)\geq 0,

(3.6)

and

A(t)\geq A(0)\exp(-(\alpha+\mu)t)\geq 0.

(3.7)

We can see that all solutions are positive for all
t\geq 0
. This completes
the proof.

3.2
Invariant region

Theorem 2

The feasible region
\Upsilon
defined by

\Upsilon=\left\{\left(S,I_{1},I_{2},\text{ }A\right)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{4}:N=S+I_{1}+I_{2}+A\leq\frac{Q_{0}}{\mu},\right\}

with non-negative initial conditions
S(0),I_{1}(0),I_{2}(0)
and
A(0)
is
positively invariant.

Proof 2

Let
\left(S\left(t\right),I_{1}\left(t\right),I_{2}\left(t\right),A\left(t\right)\right)
be any solution of (
2.2
) with positive
initial conditions. Since

N(t)=S(t)+I_{1}(t)+I_{2}(t)+A(t),

the time derivative of
N(t)
along the solution of (
2.2
) is

\left.\dfrac{dN(t)}{dt}=\dfrac{d{\large S(t)}}{dt}+\dfrac{d{\large I}_{1}{\large(t)}}{dt}+\dfrac{d{\large I}_{2}{\large(t)}}{dt}+\dfrac{d{\large A(t)}}{dt}.\right.

This implies that

\dfrac{dN(t)}{dt}\leq Q_{0}-\mu N(t).

(3.8)

The solution
N(t)
of the differential equation (
3.8
) has the
following property,

{\large N(t)\leq N}(0){\large e}^{-\mu t}+\dfrac{Q_{0}}{\mu}\left(1-e^{-\mu t}\right),

where
{\large N}\left(0\right)
represents the sum of the initial values
of the variables. As
t\rightarrow\infty
, we have

\overline{\underset{t\rightarrow\infty}{\lim}}{\large N(t)}\underset{t\rightarrow\infty}{=\lim\sup}{\large N(t)\leq}\frac{Q_{0}}{\mu}.

(3.9)

It has been proven that all the solutions of (
2.2
) which initiate in
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{4}
are confined to the region

\Upsilon{\large=}\left\{\left(S,I_{1},I_{2},A\right)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{4}:S+I_{1}+I_{2}+A\leq\frac{Q_{0}}{\mu}\right\}.

(3.10)

Hence, the solutions are bounded in the interval
\left[0,\infty\right).

4
Existence of equilibria and the basic reproduction number
R_{0}

In this section, we will show that system (
2.2
) has equilibrium
solutions and calculate the basic reproduction number
R_{0}
.

Theorem 3

Considering system (
2.2
) and the basic reproduction number
R_{0}
:

(i) If
R_{0}\leq 1
, the system admits the single disease free equilibrium
E_{0}=\left(\dfrac{Q_{0}}{\mu},0,0,0\right)
.

(ii) If
R_{0}

>1
, the system admits two distinct equilibria:
E_{0}
and
the positive endemic equilibrium
E_{1}=(S^{\ast},I_{1}^{\ast},I_{2}^{\ast},A^{\ast})
.

Proof 3

By setting
I_{1}=I_{2}=A=0
, we may simply find the disease free
equilibrium to be
E_{0}=\left(\dfrac{Q_{0}}{\mu},0,0,0\right)
.

Next, we study the existence a positive equilibrium of (
2.2
). If one
exists, it is called an endemic equilibrium and is denoted by
E_{1}=(S^{\ast},I_{1}^{\ast},I_{2}^{\ast},A^{\ast})
. Substituting this
into (
2.2
) yields:

\displaystyle Q_{0}-\beta_{m}^{\ast}S^{\ast}-\mu S^{\ast}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

(4.1)

\displaystyle\beta_{m}^{\ast}S^{\ast}-\left(\theta+\mu+\delta\right)I_{1}^{\ast}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

(4.2)

\displaystyle\theta I_{1}^{\ast}-\left(\delta+\mu+\pi\right)I_{2}^{\ast}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

(4.3)

\displaystyle\delta I_{1}^{\ast}+\delta I_{2}^{\ast}+\pi I_{2}^{\ast}-\left(\alpha+\mu\right)A^{\ast}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

(4.4)

where

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}S^{\ast}=\dfrac{Q_{0}}{\beta_{m}^{\ast}+\mu},\\
I_{1}^{\ast}=\dfrac{Q_{0}\beta_{m}^{\ast}}{(\theta+\delta+\mu)(\beta_{m}^{\ast}+\mu)},\\
I_{2}^{\ast}=\dfrac{Q_{0}\beta_{m}^{\ast}\theta}{(\theta+\delta+\mu)(\delta+\mu+\pi)(\beta_{m}^{\ast}+\mu)},\\
A^{\ast}=\dfrac{Q_{0}\beta_{m}^{\ast}((\pi+\delta)(\delta+\theta)+\delta\mu)}{(\alpha+\mu)(\theta+\delta+\mu)(\delta+\mu+\pi)(\beta_{m}^{\ast}+\mu)},\\
N^{\ast}=\dfrac{Q_{0}-\alpha A^{\ast}}{\mu}.\end{array}\right.

(4.5)

By solving system (
2.2
) at the equilibrium, we obtain

B_{1}\beta_{m}^{\ast}+B_{0}=0,

(4.6)

which implies that

\beta_{m}^{\ast}=-\dfrac{B_{0}}{B_{1}},

(4.7)

where

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}B_{1}=\left(\delta+\alpha+\mu\right)\left(\theta+\mu+\delta\right)+\pi\left(\alpha+\delta+\mu+\theta\right)>0,\\
B_{0}=\left(\alpha+\mu\right)\left(\pi+\delta+\mu\right)\left(\delta+\theta+\mu\right)\left(1-R_{0}\right).\end{array}\right.\

(4.8)

Note that
R_{0}
is the basic reproductive number of system (
2.2
),
which represents the expected number of new infections produced by a typical
infective individual on a completely susceptible population. If
R_{0}\leq 1
, we obtain
\beta_{m}^{\ast}\leq 0.
Therefore, the system (
2.2
)
admits a disease free equilibrium
E_{0}
. On the other hand, the case
R_{0}>1
leads to
\beta_{m}^{\ast}>0
, in which case system (
2.2
)
admits two distinct equilibria:
E_{0}
and the unique positive endemic
equilibrium
E_{1}(S^{\ast},I_{1}^{\ast},I_{2}^{\ast},A^{\ast})
.

Now that we have identified the effect of
R_{0}
on the system’s
equilibria, we must determine a formula for the number itself. We follow the
method explained in Driessche and Watmough’s study
[
24
]
.
We write

R_{0}=\rho\left(FV^{-1}\right),

where the special matrices
F
, for new infection terms, and
V
, for the
remaining transmission terms, related to the system (
2.2
) are given as

F\left(E_{0}\right)=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\left(1-u_{1}\right)\beta_{1}c_{1}&\left(1-u_{1}\right)\beta_{2}c_{2}&\left(1-u_{1}\right)\beta_{3}c_{3}\\
0&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{array}\right],

(4.9)

and

V\left(E_{0}\right)=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\left(\theta+\delta+\mu\right)&0&0\\
-\theta&\left(\delta+\mu+\pi\right)&0\\
-\delta&-\left(\delta+\pi\right)&\left(\alpha+\mu\right)\end{array}\right].

(4.10)

Since
\det(V)=\left(\theta+\delta+\mu\right)\left(\delta+\mu+\pi\right)\left(\alpha+\mu\right)\neq 0
, matrix
V
has the inverse

V^{-1}=\frac{1}{\det(V)}\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\left(\delta+\mu+\pi\right)\left(\alpha+\mu\right)&0&0\\
\theta\left(\alpha+\mu\right)&\left(\theta+\delta+\mu\right)\left(\alpha+\mu\right)&0\\
\theta\left(\delta+\pi\right)+\delta\left(\theta+\mu+\pi\right)&\left(\delta+\pi\right)\left(\delta+\mu+\pi\right)&\left(\theta+\delta+\mu\right)\left(\delta+\mu+\pi\right)\end{array}\right].

(4.11)

Therefore, the basic reproduction number
R_{0}
is given by

R_{0}=\zeta_{1}+\zeta_{2}+\zeta_{3}+\zeta_{4},

(4.12)

where

\displaystyle\zeta_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(1-u_{1})\beta_{1}c_{1}}{\left(\theta+\delta+\mu\right)},\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt

\displaystyle\zeta_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(1-u_{1})\beta_{2}c_{2}\theta}{\left(\theta+\delta+\mu\right)\left(\delta+\mu+\pi\right)},\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt

\displaystyle\zeta_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(1-u_{1})\beta_{3}c_{3}\theta\left(\delta+\pi\right)}{\left(\theta+\delta+\mu\right)\left(\delta+\mu+\pi\right)\left(\alpha+\mu\right)},\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt

\displaystyle\zeta_{4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(1-u_{1})\beta_{3}c_{3}\delta}{\left(\theta+\delta+\mu\right)\left(\alpha+\mu\right)}.\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt

5
Stability analysis

5.1
Local asymptotic stability when
R_{0}<1

Assuming
R_{0}<1
, system (
2.2
) admits a single disease free
equilibrium
E_{0}
. We aim to investigate its local asymptotic stability as
described in the following theorem.

Theorem 4

For system (
2.2
), if
R_{0}<1
, the disease-free
equilibrium solution
E_{0}
is locally asymptotically stable.

Proof 4

The Jacobian matrix corresponding to system (
2.2
) evaluated at
E_{0}

is given by

J(E_{0})=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}-\mu&-\Lambda_{1}&-\Lambda_{2}&-\Lambda_{3}\\
0&\Lambda_{1}-\left(\theta+\delta+\mu\right)&\Lambda_{2}&\Lambda_{3}\\

where
\Lambda_{1}=(1-u_{1})\beta_{1}c_{1},\ \Lambda_{2}=(1-u_{1})\beta_{2}c_{2},\ \text{and}\ \Lambda_{3}=(1-u_{1})\beta_{3}c_{3}
.

It is clear that
-\mu
is one of the eigenvalues. Hence, by removing the
first column and the first row, the Jacobian matrix reduces to

J(E_{0})=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\Lambda_{1}-\left(\theta+\delta+\mu\right)&\Lambda_{2}&\Lambda_{3}\\
\theta&-\left(\delta+\mu\mathbf{+}\pi\right)&0\\
\delta&\delta+\pi&-\left(\alpha\mathbf{+}\mu\right)\end{array}\right].

(5.2)

It suffices to calculate the eigenvalues of the reduced matrix. Setting

\det(J(E_{0})-\lambda)=0,

leads to the characteristic polynomial

\lambda^{3}+a_{1}\lambda^{2}+a_{2}\lambda+a_{3}=0,

(5.3)

where

\displaystyle a_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\alpha+\mu\right)+\left(\delta+\mu\mathbf{+}\pi\right)+\left(\theta+\delta+\mu\right)(1-\zeta_{1}),\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt

\displaystyle a_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\alpha+\mu\right)\left(\delta+\mu\mathbf{+}\pi\right)

\displaystyle+\left(\delta+\mu\mathbf{+}\pi\right)\left(\theta+\delta+\mu\right)\left(1-\zeta_{1}-\zeta_{2}\right)

\displaystyle+\left(\alpha+\mu\right)\left(\theta+\delta+\mu\right)\left(1-\zeta_{1}-\zeta_{4}\right)\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt

\displaystyle a_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\theta+\delta+\mu\right)\left(\delta+\mu\mathbf{+}\pi\right)\left(\alpha+\mu\right)\left(1-R_{0}\right).\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt

The condition

\displaystyle a_{1}a_{2}-a_{3}

\displaystyle=

5.2
Local asymptotic stability when
R_{0}>1

As described earlier in the paper, for
R_{0}>1
, system (
2.2
) admits
two equilibria: the DFE
E_{0}
and the endemic equilibrium
E_{1}(S^{\ast},I_{1}^{\ast},I_{2}^{\ast},A^{\ast})
. In order to establish their local
asymptotic stability, let us first state a necessary lemma taken from

Lemma 1

[
26
]

(Descartes’ Rule of Signs)
Consider a polynomial with real coefficients of the form

p\left(\lambda\right)=a_{0}\lambda^{n}+a_{1}\lambda^{n-1}+...+a_{n-1}\lambda+a_{n}=0,a_{0}\neq 0.

(5.5)

The sequence of coefficients of (
5.5
) is given by

a_{n},a_{n-1},...,a_{1},a_{0}.

Let
k
be the total number of sign changes from one coefficient to the next
in the sequence. Then, the number of positive real roots of the polynomial
is either equal to
k
, or
k
minus a positive even integer. (Note: if
\ k=1
, then there exists exactly one positive real root).

With this lemma in mind, we move to study the local asymptotic stability of
the two equilibria as described in the following theorem.

Theorem 5

For system (
2.2
), if
R_{0}>1
,
E_{0}
is unstable and

E_{1}(S^{\ast},I_{1}^{\ast},I_{2}^{\ast},A^{\ast})
is locally
asymptotically stable.

Proof 5

If
R_{0}>1
, from what we saw in the proof of Theorem
4
,
E_{0}

is clearly unstable. The second equilibrium
E_{1}(S^{\ast},I_{1}^{\ast},I_{2}^{\ast},A^{\ast})
requires evaluation. The Jacobian matrix
corresponding to system (
2.2
) evaluated at
E_{1}
is given by

J(E_{1})=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}-G-\mu&-F_{1}+J_{1}&-F_{2}+J_{1}&-F_{3}+J_{1}\\

where

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}G=\dfrac{(N^{\ast}-S^{\ast})(1-u_{1})(\beta_{1}C_{1}I_{1}^{\ast}+\beta_{2}C_{2}I_{2}^{\ast}+\beta_{3}C_{3}A^{\ast})}{N^{\ast^{2}}},\\
F_{1}=\dfrac{(1-u_{1})\beta_{1}C_{1}}{N^{\ast}}S^{\ast},\\

10
Conclusion

In this paper, we studied the HIV/AIDS model proposed in
[
10
]
.
The stability of the model’s equilibria was investigated using common
stability theory. The model was modified by including three control
measures: the use of condoms and the screening and treatment of infective
individuals. The aim of the control strategy was to achieve the optimal
effect for these measures in two respects: minimizing the sizes of the HIV
and AIDS populations, and realizing it at the lowest possible cost. We
established the existence of an optimal control by means of standard theory
and identified the characteristics of the control parameters through state
and adjoint functions. Simulation results were presented to confirm the
analytical findings. Results confirmed the importance of condom use as a
limiting measure for the spread of the disease.
