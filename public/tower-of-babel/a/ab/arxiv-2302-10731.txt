Real roots of real cubics and optimization
Heinz H. Bauschke, Manish Krishan Lal, Xianfu Wang

Abstract
The solution of the cubic equation has a century-long history; however, the usual presentation is geared towards applications in algebra and is somewhat inconvenient to use in optimization where frequently the main interest lies in real roots. In this note, we present the roots of the cubic in a form that makes them convenient to use and we also focus on information on the location of the real roots. Armed with this, we provide several applications in optimization where we compute Fenchel conjugates, proximal mappings and projections.

1
Introduction

The history of solving cubic equations is rich and centuries old; see, e.g.,
Confalonieri’s recent book
[
7
]
on Cardano’s work. Cubics do also appear in convex and nonconvex optimization.
However, treatises on solving the cubic often focus on the general complex case making the results less useful to optimizers.
The purpose of this note is two-fold. We present a largely self-contained derivation of the solution of the cubic with an emphasis on usefulness to practitioners. We do not claim novelty of these results; however, the presentation appears to be particularly convenient. We then turn to novel results. We show how the formulas can be used to compute Fenchel conjugates and proximal mappings of some convex functions. We also discuss projections on convex and nonconvex sets.

Fact 1.1
.

Let
f(x)
be a nonconstant complex polynomial and let

such that

Then the multiplicity of

is is the smallest integer

such that
the

is nonzero:
f^{(k-1)}(r)=0
and

When

then we say that

is a simple, double, or triple root, respectively.

Fact 1.2
.

(Vieta)

Suppose
f(x)=ax^{3}+bx^{2}+cx+d
is a cubic polynomial (i.e.,
a\neq 0
) with complex coefficients.
If
r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}
denote the (possibly repeated and complex) roots of
f
, then

\displaystyle r_{1}+r_{2}+r_{3}

\displaystyle=-\frac{b}{a}

(1a)

\displaystyle r_{1}r_{2}+r_{1}r_{3}+r_{2}r_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{c}{a}

(1b)

\displaystyle r_{1}r_{2}r_{3}

\displaystyle=-\frac{d}{a}.

(1c)

Conversely, if
r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}
in
\mathbb{C}
satisfy
Eq.

1
,
then they are the (possibly repeated) roots of
f
.

Fact 1.3
.

Suppose
f(x)=ax^{3}+bx^{2}+cx+d
is a cubic polynomial (i.e.,
a\neq 0
) with real coefficients. Then
f
has three (possibly complex) roots (counting multiplicity).
More precisely, exactly one of the following holds:

(i)

f
has exactly one real root which either is simple (and the two remaining roots are nonreal simple roots and conjugate to each other) or is a triple root.

(ii)

f
has exactly two distinct real roots: one is simple and the other double.

(iii)

f
has exactly three distinct simple real roots.

Remark 1.4
.

We mention that the roots of a polynomial of a
fixed
degree depend continuously on the coefficients — see
[
10
, Theorem 1.3.1]

for a precise statement and also the other results in
[
10
, Section 1.3]
.

4
Convex Analysis of the general quartic

In this section, we study the function

h(x):=\alpha x^{4}+\beta x^{3}+\gamma x^{2}+\delta x+\varepsilon,\quad\text{where $\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta,\varepsilon$ are in $\mathbb{R}$ with $\alpha\neq 0$.}

(41)

We start by characterizing convexity.

Proposition 4.1
.

(convexity)

The general quartic
Eq.

41
is convex
if and only if

\alpha>0\;\;\text{and}\;\;8\alpha\gamma\geq 3\beta^{2}.

(42)

Proof
. Note that

h^{\prime}(x)=4\alpha x^{3}+3\beta x^{2}+2\gamma x+\delta
and, also completing the square,

\displaystyle h^{\prime\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=12\alpha x^{2}+6\beta x+2\gamma=\frac{3}{4}\alpha\Big(4x+\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\Big)^{2}+2\gamma-\frac{3\beta^{2}}{4\alpha}.

(43)

Hence
h^{\prime\prime}\geq 0

\Leftrightarrow

[
\alpha>0
and
2\gamma\geq 3\beta^{2}/(4\alpha)
]

\Leftrightarrow

Eq.

42
.
(For further information on deciding nonnegativity of polynomials,
see
[
6

.)

\hfill\quad\blacksquare

Having characterization convexity, we shall assume this condition for the remainder of this section:

\text{$h$ is convex, i.e.,\;\;}\alpha>0\;\text{and}\;8\alpha\gamma\geq 3\beta^{2}.

(44)

(Fenchel conjugate)

6
Projection onto epigraph of a parabola

In this section, we study projection onto the epigraph of the function

h\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\colon\mathbf{x}\mapsto\alpha\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2},\quad\text{where $\alpha>0$.}

(64)

Theorem 6.1
.

Set
E:=\operatorname{epi}h\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
.
Let
(\mathbf{y},\eta)\in(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R})
.
If
(\mathbf{y},\eta)\in E
, then
P_{E}(\mathbf{y},\eta)=(\mathbf{y},\eta)
.
So we assume that

(\mathbf{y},\eta)\in(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R})\smallsetminus E
, i.e.,

\alpha\|\mathbf{y}\|^{2}>\eta
. Set
\nu:=\|\mathbf{y}\|\geq 0
,

p:=-\frac{(2\alpha\eta-1)^{2}}{12\alpha^{2}},\quad q:=\frac{(2\alpha\eta-1)^{3}-27\alpha^{2}\nu^{2}}{108\alpha^{3}},

(65)

\Delta:=(p/3)^{3}+(q/2)^{2}=(27\alpha^{2}{\nu^{2}}-2(2\alpha\eta-1)^{3})\nu^{2}/(1728\alpha^{4})
, and

x:=\begin{cases}-\dfrac{\alpha\eta+1}{3\alpha}+\sqrt[3]{-q/2+\sqrt{\Delta}}+\sqrt[3]{-q/2-\sqrt{\Delta}},&\text{if $\Delta\geq 0$;}\\[14.22636pt]
-\dfrac{\alpha\eta+1}{3\alpha}+\dfrac{|2\alpha\eta-1|}{3\alpha}\cos\Big(\dfrac{1}{3}\arccos\dfrac{-q/2}{(-p/3)^{3/2}}\Big),&\text{if $\Delta<0$.}\end{cases}

(66)

Then

P_{E}(\mathbf{y},\eta)=\Big(\frac{\mathbf{y}}{1+2\alpha x},\eta+x\Big).

(67)

See
Fig.

3
for an illustration for the case
\alpha=1/2
.

Proof
. For
x\geq 0
, we have
xh=x\alpha\|\cdot\|^{2}
,

x\nabla h=2\alpha x\operatorname{Id}
,

\operatorname{Id}+x\nabla h=(1+2\alpha x)\operatorname{Id}
and therefore

\operatorname{Prox}_{xh}=(1+2\alpha x)^{-1}\operatorname{Id}
.
In view of
[
5
, Theorem 6.36]
, we must first find a positive root
x
of

\varphi(x):=h(\operatorname{Prox}_{xh}(\mathbf{y}))-x-\eta=\alpha\|\mathbf{y}\|^{2}/(1+2\alpha x)^{2}-x-\eta=0
.
Note that
\varphi(0)>0
, that
\varphi
is strictly decreasing on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, and that

\varphi(x)\to-\infty
as
x\to+\infty
.
Hence
\varphi
has
exactly one
positive root.
Multiplying by
(1+2\alpha x)^{2}>0
, where
x>0
, results in the cubic

\alpha\nu^{2}-(x+\eta)(1+2\alpha x)^{2}=0
,
which must have
exactly one
positive root.
Re-arranging leads us to

f(x):=4\alpha^{2}x^{3}+4\alpha(\alpha\eta+1)x^{2}+(4\alpha\eta+1)x+\eta-\alpha\nu^{2}=0,

\lambda=\begin{cases}\sqrt[3]{\dfrac{\|y\|}{\gamma}+\sqrt{\Delta}}+\sqrt[3]{\dfrac{\|y\|}{\gamma}-\sqrt{\Delta}},&\text{if $\Delta\geq 0$;}\\[19.91692pt]
2(-p/3)^{1/2}\cos\Big(\dfrac{1}{3}\arccos\dfrac{\|y\|/\gamma}{(-p/3)^{3/2}}\Big),&\text{if $\Delta<0$.}\end{cases}

(110)

Proof
. The first cases where already provided in
[
3
, Example 24.57]
.
Now assume

\|y\|^{2}+2\gamma\eta>0
.
It was also observed in
[
3
, Example 24.57]
that if
y=0
, then
\operatorname{Prox}_{\gamma h}(y,\eta)=(0,\eta)
.

So assume also that
y\neq 0
.
It follows from the discussion that in

[
3
, Example 24.57]
that

\lambda
is the unique positive solution of
the already depressed cubic

\lambda^{3}+\frac{2(\eta+\gamma)}{\gamma}\lambda-\frac{2\|y\|}{\gamma}=0,

(111)

which is where the discussion in
[
3
]
halted. Continuing here, we set

p:=\frac{2(\eta+\gamma)}{\gamma},\quad q:=-\frac{2\|y\|}{\gamma}<0,

(112)

and
\Delta:=(p/3)^{3}+(q/2)^{2}
.
Using
Corollary

2.2
, we see that if
\Delta<0
, then

\lambda=2(-p/3)^{1/2}\cos\Big(\frac{1}{3}\arccos\frac{-q/2}{(-p/3)^{3/2}}\Big)

(113)

while if
\Delta\geq 0
, then

\lambda=\sqrt[3]{\frac{-q}{2}+\sqrt{\Delta}}+\sqrt[3]{\frac{-q}{2}-\sqrt{\Delta}}

(114)

which slightly simplifies to the expression provided in
Eq.

110
.

Finally, notice that if
y=0
, then
the assumption that
\|y\|^{2}+2\gamma\eta>0
yields

\eta>0
; thus,
p>0
,
q=0
, and hence
\Delta>0
. Formally, our
\lambda
then simplifies to
0
which conveniently allows us to combine this case with the case
y\neq 0
.

\hfill\quad\blacksquare
