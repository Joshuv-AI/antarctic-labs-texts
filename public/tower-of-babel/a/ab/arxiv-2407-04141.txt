Spectral densities from Euclidean lattice correlators via the Mellin transform
Mattia Bruno, Leonardo Giusti, Matteo Saccardi

Abstract
Spectral densities connect correlation functions computed in quantum field theory to observables measured in experiments. For strongly-interacting theories, their non-perturbative determinations from lattice simulations are therefore of primary importance. They entail the inverse Laplace transform of correlation functions calculated in Euclidean time. By making use of the Mellin transform, we derive explicit analytic formulae to define spectral densities from the time dependence of correlation functions, both in the continuum and on the lattice. The generalization to smeared spectral densities turns out to be straightforward. The formulae obtained here within the context of lattice field theory can be easily applied or extended to other areas of research.

Preliminaries on the Laplace transform
— Based on known results from the mathematical literature
[
35
,
36
,
37
]
, in this section we introduce the generic solution to the ILT in the continuum.
We consider a vector space of real functions with support
over
\mathbb{R}^{+}
equipped with the inner
product
\langle f|g\rangle=\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,f(t)\,g(t)
.
Denoting by
\mathbb{I}
the identity operator, we introduce the orthonormal

time basis

\{|t\rangle\}
, with
t\in[0,\infty)
,
such that
\langle t|t^{\prime}\rangle=\delta(t-t^{\prime})
and

\mathbb{I}=\int_{t}|t\rangle\langle t|\,,\quad\text{with }\int_{t}\equiv\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,.

(3)

Next, we consider a second set of (non-orthogonal) vectors
\{|\omega\rangle\}
, with
\omega\in[0,\infty)
, whose coordinates in the time basis are

\langle t|\omega\rangle=\langle\omega|t\rangle=e^{-t\omega}\,.

(4)

Note that in our notation both
t
and
\omega
are dimensionless variables, and when they carry a physical dimension, e.g. time and energy, we assume to take a common reference scale to cancel it.
A consequence of Eq. (
4
) is that the metric

\langle\omega|\omega^{\prime}\rangle=\frac{1}{\omega+\omega^{\prime}}\equiv\mathcal{H}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})

(5)

is non-diagonal and corresponds to the so-called Carleman operator
[
47
]
.
With the aim of diagonalizing
\mathcal{H}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})
, we introduce the “Mellin” basis
\{|s\rangle\}
with
s\in\mathbb{R}
, defined as
[
35
,
36
,
37
]

u_{s}(t)=\langle t|s\rangle\equiv\frac{e^{is\log(t)}}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}\,.

(6)

As shown in Ref.
[
48
]
, the vectors
\{|s\rangle\}
form an orthonormal basis, and obey the relation

\langle\omega|s\rangle=\int_{t}\,e^{-tw}\,u_{s}(t)=\lambda_{s}\,u_{-s}(w)=\lambda_{s}\,u_{s}^{\ast}(w)\,,

(7)

where
\lambda_{s}\equiv\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}+is\right)
and
\Gamma(z)
is the Euler Gamma function.
In this basis the metric
\mathcal{H}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})
is diagonal

\mathcal{H}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})=\int_{s}\langle\omega|s\rangle\langle s|\omega^{\prime}\rangle=\int_{s}u_{s}^{\ast}(\omega)\,|\lambda_{s}|^{2}\,u_{s}(\omega^{\prime})\,,

(8)

with eigenvalues

|\lambda_{s}|^{2}=\frac{\pi}{\cosh(\pi s)}\in\left[0,\pi\right]
and
\int_{s}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}ds
.
From Eq. (
7
) it follows that the Laplace transform is bounded and self-adjoint with purely absolutely
continuous spectrum,
\pm|\lambda_{s}|\in[-\sqrt{\pi},+\sqrt{\pi}]
, of multiplicity one.
Similarly, we observe that
\mathcal{H}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})
is bounded, with spectrum of multiplicity two, acting on functions in
L^{2}(0,\infty,d\omega)
.

Analogously to the Fourier-transform, despite the representation in terms of the complex functions
u_{s}(\omega)
, the integral in Eq. (
8
) is real.
In fact, an equivalent diagonal representation with real basis functions exists
[
48
]
.

Inverse Laplace transform
— Let us consider functions
C(t)
in
L^{2}(0,\infty,dt)
, satisfying the representation in Eq. (
1
), namely

|C\rangle=\int_{\omega}\rho(\omega)\,|\omega\rangle\,.

(9)

When only
|C\rangle
is known, the spectral density is obtained from the solution of the Fredholm integral equation of first kind

\langle\omega|C\rangle=\int_{\omega^{\prime}}\mathcal{H}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})\,\rho(\omega^{\prime})\,.

(10)

According to Hadamard, the problem is mathematically ill-posed (for additional details see for example Ref.
[
49
]
), due to the difficulties in inverting
\mathcal{H}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})
stemming from the rapid exponential decay of its eigenvalues, which tend to zero in the limit
s\to\pm\infty
.
This does not imply the absence of a solution, since its inverse may nevertheless be defined by regulating the smallest
|\lambda_{s}|^{2}
with the introduction of the Tikhonov-regulated operator
[
50
,
51
,
49
]

\mathcal{H}^{-1}_{\alpha}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})=\int_{s}u_{s}^{\ast}(\omega)\frac{1}{|\lambda_{s}|^{2}+\alpha}u_{s}(\omega^{\prime})\,,\quad\alpha>0\,.

(11)

Its convolution with
\mathcal{H}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})
forms the smeared Dirac
\delta
function

\delta_{\alpha}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})=\langle\omega|S_{\alpha}|\omega^{\prime}\rangle=\int_{s}u_{s}^{\ast}(\omega)\frac{|\lambda_{s}|^{2}}{|\lambda_{s}|^{2}+\alpha}u_{s}(\omega^{\prime})\,,

(12)

obeying
\lim_{\alpha\to 0}\delta_{\alpha}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})=\delta(\omega-\omega^{\prime})
in the space
L^{2}(0,\infty,d\omega)

[
48
]
, with the operator
S_{\alpha}
defined as

S_{\alpha}\equiv\int_{s}|s\rangle\frac{1}{|\lambda_{s}|^{2}+\alpha}\langle s|\,.

(13)

Defining the convolution of the spectral density with
\delta_{\alpha}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})
as
\rho_{\alpha}(\omega)
, the ILT is obtained from the limit

\rho(\omega)=\lim_{\alpha\to 0}\rho_{\alpha}(\omega)\,,

(14)

with
\rho_{\alpha}(\omega)
being

\rho_{\alpha}(\omega)=\langle\omega|S_{\alpha}|C\rangle=\int_{t}g_{\alpha}(t|\omega)\,C(t)\,.

(15)

Since in practice we know
|C\rangle
only in the time basis, in the equation above we inserted the completeness relation arriving at the coefficients

g_{\alpha}(t|\omega)=\langle\omega|S_{\alpha}|t\rangle=\int_{s}\frac{u_{s}^{\ast}(\omega)\lambda_{s}u_{s}^{\ast}(t)}{|\lambda_{s}|^{2}+\alpha}\,.

(16)

The

are real (
s\in\mathbb{R}
) and, for finite
\alpha

By expressing the smeared

\delta_{\alpha}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})=\int_{t}\langle\omega|S_{\alpha}|t\rangle\langle t|\omega\rangle

\int_{\omega}[\delta(\omega-\omega^{\prime})-\delta_{\alpha}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})]^{2}+\alpha\langle\omega^{\prime}|S_{\alpha}^{2}|\omega^{\prime}\rangle

Similarly our definition in Eq. (

Regulated spectral densities
— Let us consider a correlator
C(t)
obeying Eq. (
1

\rho(\omega)=\omega^{k}\rho_{\mathsf{s}}(\omega)

Inspired by dispersive relations, where the asymptotic growth is regulated by inverse powers of the energy with the addition of subtraction constants, we first generalize Eq. (

\int_{t}e^{-\omega t}\,t^{p}\,u_{s}(t)=\frac{\lambda_{s,p}}{\omega^{p}}u_{s}^{\ast}(\omega)\,,

g_{\alpha}(t|\omega,p,q)\equiv\int_{s}\frac{u_{s}^{\ast}(\omega)\,\lambda_{s,p}\,u_{s}^{\ast}(t)}{\lambda_{s,p}\lambda_{s,q}^{\ast}+\alpha}\,t^{q}\,,

Spectral densities from lattice correlation functions
— In lattice field theories,

—
By excluding the first time slice

Finite temporal domain
— Now we turn to the case where the correlator
C(t)
is known on a finite temporal range
t\in[0,t_{\mathrm{max}}]
, e.g. the typical time subdomain of a simulated lattice where the non-zero temperature effects are negligible.
With this restriction the metric becomes

\frac{1-e^{-t_{\mathrm{max}}(\omega+\omega^{\prime})}}{\omega+\omega^{\prime}}\,.

(30)

Here the relevant observation is that, in field theories with a mass gap, the large-time behavior of correlation functions is bounded by a scale
M>0
such that
0\leq|C(t)|\leq|C(t_{\mathrm{max}})|e^{-M(t-t_{\mathrm{max}})}
for
t>t_{\mathrm{max}}
.
As a consequence, the systematic difference between
\rho_{\alpha}(\omega)
and the spectral density calculated by restricting the time interval in Eq. (
15

[0,t_{\mathrm{max}}]
vanishes exponentially in

[

]
.
Notice that this bound is not sufficient for the thermal theory. Following the strategy of this letter, one should instead diagonalize the appropriate finite-temperature metric, similar to the one in Eq. (

Taming statistical errors
—
In presence of statistical fluctuations, the ILT regulator may have a beneficial effect on the variance. Therefore it may be used to tame the statistical error on the spectral density, while keeping the corresponding systematic effect significantly below it. For instance, if one adopts Tikhonov’s scheme, e.g. Eq. (
11
), by taking
\rho(\omega)\in L^{2}(0,\infty,d\omega)
indeed one can show that

\begin{split}|\rho_{\kappa}-\rho_{\kappa,\alpha}|^{2}&\leq\,\alpha^{2}\bigg[\int_{\omega}\rho(\omega)^{2}\bigg]\\
&\times\int_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}\kappa(\omega)\kappa(\omega^{\prime})\int_{s}\frac{u_{s}^{\ast}(\omega)u_{s}(\omega^{\prime})}{(|\lambda_{s}|^{2}+\alpha)^{2}}\,.\end{split}

(31)

This relation can be used to roughly estimate a good value of
\alpha
, but a test of the systematic effect due to
\alpha\neq 0
has to be performed directly on the data, by varying the value of
\alpha
in a suitable range.
This is not needed when the theoretical prediction is compared with the corresponding experimental data smeared with
\int_{\omega}\kappa(\omega)\delta_{\alpha}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})

[
30
,
32
]
.

Conclusions
— The level of precision reached by modern numerical lattice calculations may enable the direct computation of spectral densities. Motivated by this progress, we have derived the explicit analytic formula to relate them to Euclidean-time correlators in the continuum limit.
An optimal adaptation to the discrete case would be achieved by replacing the integral over time with a sum spanning geometrically distributed (i.e.
a^{\mathbb{N}}
) data
[
59
]
, which however is evidently not usable in lattice field theories.
To overcome this problem, we have generalized the formula for the continuum theory to the case of a regular discrete sampling in time, so that discretization errors induced by the ILT on the spectral density are minimized.

These findings open the door to computations with controlled and improvable systematic errors for a wide variety of phenomenologically relevant non-perturbative quantities. More specifically, finite-size and discretization effects on the estimated spectral density may be directly inferred from those of the correlators.
In other fields where data is available under similar constraints, without the hassle of intrinsic discretization effects in
C(t)
, the solution in Eq. (
26
) is exact for
\alpha\to 0
.

Properties of the “Mellin” basis
— The orthogonality of the basis vectors
\{|s\rangle\}
, defined in Eq. (
6
), may be proven by considering the change of variable
\eta=\log(t)
so that

\hskip-3.55658pt\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,u_{s}^{\ast}(t)\,u_{s^{\prime}}(t)\!=\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\eta}{2\pi}\,e^{i\eta(s-s^{\prime})}\!=\!\delta(s-s^{\prime})\,,

while the completeness of the basis is guaranteed by

\int_{s}\,u_{s}^{\ast}(t)\,u_{s}(t^{\prime})=\frac{\delta(\log(t/t^{\prime}))}{\sqrt{tt^{\prime}}}=\delta(t-t^{\prime})\,.

(33)

Analogously to the Fourier transform, also in this case it is possible to work with a basis of real eigenfunctions

\displaystyle u_{s}^{+}(t)=\frac{u_{s}(t)+u_{s}^{\ast}(t)}{\sqrt{2}}=\frac{\cos(s\log(t))}{\sqrt{\pi t}}\,,

(34)

\displaystyle u_{s}^{-}(t)=\frac{u_{s}(t)-u_{s}^{\ast}(t)}{\sqrt{2}i}=\frac{\sin(s\log(t))}{\sqrt{\pi t}}\,,

(35)

with
s\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
.
In this basis Carleman’s operator becomes

\begin{split}\mathcal{H}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})=\int_{s\in\mathbb{R}^{+}}\,|\lambda_{s}|^{2}\big[&u_{s}^{+}(\omega)u_{s}^{+}(\omega^{\prime})\\
&+u_{s}^{-}(\omega)u_{s}^{-}(\omega^{\prime})\big]\,,\end{split}

(36)

making more explicit the (known) double degeneracy of its spectrum.

Least-square solution
— As mentioned in the main text, the coefficients
g_{\alpha}(t|\omega)
are equivalently defined from the minimization of a least-square problem.
Let us introduce new unknown coefficients
\widetilde{g}_{\alpha}(t|\omega)
and the (regularized) norm

\int_{\omega^{\prime}}\left[\delta(\omega-\omega^{\prime})-\int_{t}e^{-\omega^{\prime}t}\widetilde{g}_{\alpha}(t|\omega)\right]^{2}+\alpha\int_{t}\widetilde{g}_{\alpha}(t|\omega)^{2}\,.

(37)

By setting the first derivative w.r.t.
\widetilde{g}_{\alpha}(t|\omega)
to zero one finds

\widetilde{g}_{\alpha}(t|\omega)=\int_{t^{\prime}}\,A_{\alpha}^{-1}(t,t^{\prime})\,e^{-\omega t^{\prime}}\,,

(38)

where
A_{\alpha}^{-1}(t,t^{\prime})
is the inverse of the regularized operator

A_{\alpha}(t,t^{\prime})=\frac{1}{t+t^{\prime}}+\alpha\,\delta(t-t^{\prime})=\langle t|S_{\alpha}|t^{\prime}\rangle

(39)

which proves the equivalence with our definition in Eq. (
16
), namely
g_{\alpha}(t|\omega)=\widetilde{g}_{\alpha}(t|\omega)
.

Similarly
\rho_{\alpha}(\omega)
minimizes the distance with
\rho(\omega)
. In analogy to Eq. (
37
), starting from a new set of unknown coefficients
\widetilde{r}_{\alpha}(t)
, with little additional algebra one may prove that at the minimum of the following norm

\int_{\omega}\Big[\rho(\omega)-\int_{t}e^{-\omega t}\widetilde{r}_{\alpha}(t)\Big]^{2}+\alpha\int_{t}\widetilde{r}_{\alpha}(t)^{2}\,,

(40)

\rho_{\alpha}(\omega)=\int_{t}e^{-\omega t}\widetilde{r}_{\alpha}(t)=\int_{t}g_{\alpha}(t|\omega)C(t)
.

Finite temporal extent
— At fixed
\alpha>0
, the systematic error due to the restriction of the time integral to
t\in[0,t_{\mathrm{max}}]
of the continuous solution in Eq. (
15
) is

\rho_{\alpha}(\omega)-\rho_{\alpha,t_{\mathrm{max}}}(\omega)=\int_{s}\frac{u_{s}^{\ast}(\omega)\lambda_{s}}{|\lambda_{s}|^{2}+\alpha}\int_{t_{\mathrm{max}}}^{\infty}\!\!\!\!dt\,u_{s}^{\ast}(t)C(t)\,.

(41)

From
|C(t)|\leq|C(t_{\mathrm{max}})|e^{-M(t-t_{\mathrm{max}})}
for
t\geq t_{\mathrm{max}}
, we find

\left|\int_{t_{\mathrm{max}}}^{\infty}dt\,C(t)u_{s}^{\ast}(t)\right|\leq\frac{|C(t_{\mathrm{max}})|}{\sqrt{2\pi M}\sqrt{Mt_{\mathrm{max}}}}\,,

(42)

which leads, with little additional algebra, to

\begin{split}|\rho_{\alpha}(\omega)-\rho_{\alpha,t_{\mathrm{max}}}(\omega)|\leq&\,\frac{|C(t_{\mathrm{max}})|}{2\pi\sqrt{\omega M}\sqrt{Mt_{\mathrm{max}}}}\\
\times&\int_{s}\frac{|\lambda_{s}|}{|\lambda_{s}|^{2}+\alpha}\,.\end{split}

(43)

This bound is finite as long as
\alpha>0
and exponentially suppressed by
|C(t_{\mathrm{max}})|
. Instead, from the concrete assumption
C(t)=c\,e^{-Mt}
at
t\geq t_{\mathrm{max}}
, we obtain

\int_{t_{\mathrm{max}}}^{\infty}dt\,u_{s}^{\ast}(t)C(t)=c\,u_{s}(M)\lambda_{s}^{\ast}(Mt_{\mathrm{max}})\,,

(44)

where
\lambda_{s}^{\ast}(x)\equiv\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}-is,x\right)
is the upper incomplete
\Gamma
-function.
The resulting systematic error

\begin{split}\rho_{\alpha}(\omega)-\rho_{\alpha,t_{\mathrm{max}}}(\omega)=c\int_{s}\frac{u_{s}^{\ast}(\omega)\lambda_{s}u_{s}(M)}{|\lambda_{s}|^{2}+\alpha}\lambda_{s}^{\ast}(x)\,,\end{split}

(45)

is bounded and exponentially suppressed in
x=Mt_{\mathrm{max}}
, and it can be calculated explicitly provided that
M
and
c
are known.

Properties of the
v_{s}(\omega,a)
functions
— In this section we examine known properties of the so-called Hilbert matrix to derive the explicit functional form of the
v_{s}(\omega,a)
functions in Eq. (
23
).
We begin from the diagonalization of the (infinite) Hilbert matrix
[
55
]
regarded as an operator acting on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{+})

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n+m+2}x^{\mu}_{n}=\frac{\pi}{\sin(\pi\mu)}x^{\mu}_{m}

(46)

with
\mu=\frac{1}{2}+is
, by the eigenfunctions

x^{\mu}_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}(-1)^{k}\frac{\Gamma(k+\mu)\Gamma(k+1-\mu)}{\Gamma(k+2)\Gamma(k+1)}\,.

(47)

By using a known integral representation of Euler’s
\beta
function and its relation with the
\Gamma
function

\beta(x,y)=\int_{0}^{1}dr\,r^{x-1}(1-r)^{y-1}=\frac{\Gamma(x)\Gamma(y)}{\Gamma(x+y)}\,,

(48)

and the property

\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}x^{k}=(1+x)^{n}\,,

(49)

with little algebra we derive the following representation in terms of the hypergeometric function
{}_{3}F_{2}

[
56
]

x^{\mu}_{n}=\frac{\pi}{\sin(\pi\mu)}\ {}_{3}F_{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{c|}-n,\mu,1-\mu\\
1,2\end{array}\,1\right)\,,

(50)

much more suitable for numerical evaluation than Eq. (
47
).
For
\mu=\frac{1}{2}+is
the eigenvalues in Eq. (
46
) coincide with those in the continuum
|\lambda_{s}|^{2}
. Instead the eigenfunctions
x_{n}^{\frac{1}{2}+is}
are related to the so-called continuous dual Hahn polynomials, defined according to
[
60
,
61
,
62
]

\begin{split}S_{n}(x^{2};a,b,c)=&\,(a+b)^{(n)}(a+c)^{(n)}\\
&\times{}_{3}F_{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{c|}-n,a+ix,a-ix\\
a+b,a+c\end{array}\,1\right)\,,\end{split}

(51)

with the rising factorial being

(x)^{(n)}=\frac{\Gamma(x+n)}{\Gamma(x)}\,,\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{Z}_{0}^{-}\,.

(52)

They satisfy the orthogonality relation (see e.g. Ref.
[
60
]
)

\begin{gathered}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}ds\,|W_{s}(a,b,c)|^{2}\,S_{m}(s^{2};a,b,c)\,S_{n}(s^{2};a,b,c)\\
=\Gamma(n+a+b)\Gamma(n+a+c)\Gamma(n+b+c)n!\,\delta_{nm}\,,\end{gathered}

(53)

with

W_{s}(a,b,c)=\frac{\Gamma(a+is)\Gamma(b+is)\Gamma(c+is)}{\Gamma(2is)}\,.

(54)

By setting
a=b=1/2
and
c=3/2
, we obtain a completeness relation for the eigenfunctions of the Hilbert matrix acting on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{+})

\int_{0}^{\infty}ds\,\frac{|\lambda_{s}|^{2}}{|N_{s}|^{2}}x_{n}^{\frac{1}{2}+is}\,x_{m}^{\frac{1}{2}+is}=\delta_{nm}

(55)

with
N_{s}
defined in Eq. (
24
).
Since the Hilbert matrix is typically expressed in terms of integer numbers, we denote its corresponding dimensionful counterpart as

\overline{A}_{a}
, with matrix elements

\overline{A}_{a}(an,am)=\frac{1}{a(n+m+2)}\,.

(56)

It may be interpreted as the projection of Eq. (
39
) (at
\alpha=0
) to discrete time coordinates and its normalized eigenfunctions are given by

\overline{v}_{s}(an,a)\equiv\frac{|\lambda_{s}|}{\sqrt{a}|N_{s}|}x_{n}^{\frac{1}{2}+is}\,.

(57)

Starting from the relation between
\overline{A}_{a}(t,t^{\prime})
and
\overline{\mathcal{H}}_{a}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})

\begin{split}a\sum_{n=0}e^{-a\omega(n+1)}&\overline{A}_{a}(an,am)=\\
&\,\int_{\omega^{\prime}}\,\overline{\mathcal{H}}_{a}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})e^{-a\omega^{\prime}(m+1)}\,,\end{split}

(58)

one easily finds that they share the same spectrum

\int_{\omega^{\prime}}\overline{\mathcal{H}}_{a}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})v_{s}(\omega^{\prime},a)=|\lambda_{s}|^{2}\,v_{s}(\omega,a)\,,

(59)

while their eigenfunctions are related as follows

|\lambda_{s}|\,v_{s}(\omega,a)=a\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}e^{-a\omega(n+1)}\overline{v}_{s}(an,a)\,.

(60)

From the exclusion of the first time slice
t=0
, it follows that
an=t-a
as in Eq. (
27
).
Their orthogonality stems from the spectral theorem, while their completeness follows from Eq. (
55
).
Hence, they define a complete orthonormal basis in
L^{2}(0,\infty,d\omega)
where
\overline{\mathcal{H}}_{a}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})
is diagonal

\overline{\mathcal{H}}_{a}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})=\int_{0}^{\infty}ds\,v_{s}(\omega,a)|\lambda_{s}|^{2}v_{s}(\omega^{\prime},a)\,.

(61)

Now, we briefly study the leading discretization effects of the
v_{s}(\omega,a)
functions, while a complete derivation will be reported in a longer publication
[
48
]
.
By starting from Eq. (
60
), after some algebra, we arrive at the integral representation (
z=e^{-a\omega}[1-e^{-a\omega}]^{-1}
)

v_{s}(\omega,a)=\sqrt{a}z\frac{|\lambda_{s}|^{2}}{|N_{s}|}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\begin{array}[]{c|}\frac{1}{2}+is,\frac{1}{2}-is\\
2\end{array}\,-z\right)\,.

(62)

Using known relations among the hypergeometric function
{}_{2}F_{1}
with different arguments
[
56
]
,
we obtain the equivalent form used in Eq. (
23
).
From the expansion of Eq. (
23
) around
a=0
, we prove that discretization errors linear in the lattice spacing vanish, and in the continuum limit the functions

v_{s}(\omega,a)
tend to the specific linear combination of
u_{s}(\omega)
and
u_{s}^{\ast}(\omega)
given in Eq. (
29
).
In the alternative basis of real functions in the continuum,
v_{s}(\omega,a)
would tend to a combination of
u_{s}^{+}(\omega)
and
u^{-}_{s}(\omega)
.
Finally, we note that by squaring Eq. (
29
) we obtain four terms:
a[u_{s}(a\omega)u_{s}^{\ast}(a\omega^{\prime})+u_{s}^{\ast}(a\omega)u_{s}(a\omega^{\prime})]
lead to
\delta(\omega-\omega^{\prime})
upon integration over
s
, while the remaining additional terms, proportional to
u_{s}(a\omega)u_{s}(a\omega^{\prime})
and
u_{s}^{\ast}(a\omega)u_{s}^{\ast}(a\omega^{\prime})
, produce a vanishing contribution for
a\to 0
, after integrating in
s
.
