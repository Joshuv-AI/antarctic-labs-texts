Large deviation principles induced by the Stiefel manifold, and random multi-dimensional projections


Abstract
For fixed positive integers k < n k<n , given an n n -dimensional random vector X ( n ) X^{(n)} , consider its k k -dimensional projection 𝐚 n , k ​ X ( n ) \mathbf{a}_{n,k}X^{(n)} , where 𝐚 n , k \mathbf{a}_{n,k} is an n × k n\times k -dimensional matrix belonging to the Stiefel manifold 𝕍 n , k \mathbb{V}_{n,k} of orthonormal k k -frames in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} . For a class of sequences { X ( n ) } n ∈ ℕ \{X^{(n)}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}} that includes the uniform distributions on suitably scaled ℓ p n \ell_{p}^{n} balls, p ∈ ( 1 , ∞ ] p\in(1,\infty] , and product measures with sufficiently light tails, it is shown that the sequence of projected vectors { 𝐚 n , k ⊺ ​ X ( n ) } n ∈ ℕ \{\mathbf{a}_{n,k}^{\intercal}X^{(n)}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}} satisfies a large deviation principle whenever the empirical measures of the rows of n ​ 𝐚 n , k \sqrt{n}\mathbf{a}_{n,k} converge, as n → ∞ n\rightarrow\infty , to a probability measure on ℝ k \mathbb{R}^{k} . In particular, when 𝐀 n , k \mathbf{A}_{n,k} is a random matrix drawn from the Haar measure on 𝕍 n , k \mathbb{V}_{n,k} , this is shown to imply a large deviation principle for the sequence of random projections { 𝐀 n , k ⊺ ​ X ( n ) } n ∈ ℕ \{\mathbf{A}_{n,k}^{\intercal}X^{(n)}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}} in the quenched sense (that is, conditioned on almost sure realizations of { 𝐀 n , k } n ∈ ℕ \{\mathbf{A}_{n,k}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}} ). Moreover, a variational formula is obtained for the rate function of the large deviation principle for the annealed projections { 𝐀 n , k ⊺ ​ X ( n ) } n ∈ ℕ \{\mathbf{A}_{n,k}^{\intercal}X^{(n)}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}} , which is expressed in terms of a family of quenched rate functions and a modified entropy term. A key step in this analysis is a large deviation principle for the sequence of empirical measures of rows of n ​ 𝐀 n , k \sqrt{n}\mathbf{A}_{n,k} , n ≥ k n\geq k , which may be of independent interest. The study of multi-dimensional random projections of high-dimensional measures is of interest in asymptotic functional analysis, convex geometry and statistics. Prior results on quenched large deviations for random projections of ℓ p n \ell_{p}^{n} balls have been essentially restricted to the one-dimensional setting.

Introduction

1.1
Background

The study of high-dimensional measures and their lower-dimensional projections is a central theme in high-dimensional probability, asymptotic functional analysis and
convex geometry, where in the latter case the measures of interest are distributions on
convex bodies, which are compact, convex sets with non-empty interior (see, e.g.,
[
16
,
22
]
).
Multidimensional projections of high-dimensional random vectors are also relevant in
statistics, data analysis and computer science
[
7
,
13
]
.
Recent work has shown that large deviation principles (LDPs) that capture the tail behavior
of lower-dimensional random projections can provide more interesting information about the original high-dimensonal measures
than central limit theorem type results that capture universal phenomena of fluctuations. For example, in the case of

\ell_{p}^{n}
balls,
p\in[1,\infty)
, which are fundamental objects in convex geometry,
this was first illustrated by
LDPs for one-dimensional projections obtained in
[
10
,
15
]
, and subsequently by LDPs
for norms of samples from
\ell_{p}^{n}
balls and their multi-dimensional projections in

[
1
,
18
,
17
]
, as well as corresponding
refined large deviation estimates
obtained in
[
21
,
14
]
.
LDPs of random projections of high-dimensional measures
are broadly of two types, the terminology arising from statistical physics: so-called “quenched” LDPs, where one conditions on the choice of the (sequence of) sub-spaces, bases or
directions onto which one projects; or “annealed” LDPs, which average over the randomness arising in the choice of the projection.
While most of the work described above on
\ell_{p}^{n}
balls focused on one-dimensional LDPs (either for one-dimensional projections or norms of higher-dimensional projections),
in
[
17
]
, annealed LDPs were also established for multi-dimensional projections
of high-dimensional measures that satisfy a general condition called the
asymptotic thin shell condition. This condition was shown to be satisfied in

[
17
]
by several classes of measures, including
product measures with polynomial tail decay,

\ell_{p}^{n}
balls,
p\in[1,\infty]
, and classes of Orlicz balls and Gibbs measures.

In this article, we establish
quenched LDPs for multidimensional random projections of a class of high-dimensional measures
as the dimension
n
goes to infinity.
Quenched LDPs can often provide more geometric information
than annealed LDPs, but their analysis is typically more difficult because one can no longer exploit
symmetry properties of the random projection measure.
To state our results more precisely, for
k\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let
I_{k}
denote the
k\times k

identity matrix, and for
n>k
, let

\mathbb{V}_{n,k}:=\{A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}:A^{\intercal}A=I_{k}\}

(1.1)

denote the Stiefel manifold of
k
-frames in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Observe that the set
\mathbb{V}_{n,n}
can be
identified with the set
{\mathcal{O}}(n)
of
n\times n
orthogonal matrices with columns of
norm
1
. Also, note that
for
k,n\in\mathbb{N}
,
k<n
, any
a_{n,k}\in\mathbb{V}_{n,k}
defines a linear
projection from
n
to
k
dimensions.
Fixing a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
, we consider sequences of random vectors
\{X^{(n)}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
defined on this space
that satisfy a certain set of conditions (see Assumption
2.1
),
which include, for example,
X^{(n)}
uniformly distributed on an
\ell_{p}^{n}
ball of radius
n^{1/p}
,

p\geq 2
,
or
X^{(n)}
distributed according to a product measure with sufficiently light tails.
For any fixed
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mathbb{N}_{k}:=\{n\in\mathbb{N}:n>k\}
, and
consider the sequence of
k
-dimensional projections

\{n^{-1/2}\mathbf{a}_{n,k}^{\intercal}X^{(n)}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{k}},

(1.2)

where
\mathbf{a}:=\{\mathbf{a}_{n,k}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{k}}
, with
\mathbf{a}_{n,k}\subset\mathbb{V}_{n,k}
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}_{k}
.
Also, let

\mathsf{L}_{n,k}^{\mathbf{a}}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{\sqrt{n}\mathbf{a}_{n,k}(i,\cdot\,)},\quad n\in\mathbb{N}_{k},

(1.3)

be the associated sequence of empirical measures of the rows of
\sqrt{n}\mathbf{a}_{n,k}
.
Our first result, Theorem
2.4
, shows that whenever
\{\mathsf{L}_{n,k}^{\mathbf{a}}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{k}}

converges in the
q_{\star}
-Wasserstein topology (see Definition

) to a measure
\nu
, then the sequence of
random projections
\{n^{-1/2}\mathbf{a}_{n,k}^{\intercal}X^{(n)}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{k}},
satisfies an LDP
with a rate function that we denote by
{\mathcal{J}}^{\sf{qu}}_{\nu}
.
In particular, this implies a quenched LDP for the
sequence
\{\mathbf{A}_{n,k}^{\intercal}X^{(n)}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{k}},
where the random matrix

\mathbf{A}_{n,k}=[\mathbf{A}_{n,k}(i,j)]_{i=1,\dots,n;\,j=1,\dots,k}

is sampled, independently of

, from
\sigma_{n,k}
, the Haar measure on
\mathbb{V}_{n,k}

(i.e., the unique probability measure on
\mathbb{V}_{n,k}
that is invariant under the group

of orthogonal transformations).
In
[
17
]
, it was shown that

\{\mathbf{A}_{n,k}^{\intercal}X^{(n)}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{k}}

Our second result, Theorem

.
Along the way, for any

,
in Theorem
2.8
, we also
establish an LDP for the random empirical measure sequence
\{\mathsf{L}^{A}_{n,k}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{k}}
in
the
q
-Wasserestein topology,
which may be of independent interest.

.

1.2
Notation and Terminology

We first recall the definition of an LDP, referring to
[
8
]
for further background on large deviations theory.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a topological space with Borel sigma-algebra
\mathcal{B}
. A sequence of probability measures
\{P_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
is said to satisfy a
large deviation principle (LDP)
in
\mathcal{X}
with rate function
I:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow[0,\infty]
if for all
B\in\mathcal{B}
,

-\inf_{x\in B^{\circ}}I(x)\leq\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\tfrac{1}{n}\log P_{n}(B)\leq\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\tfrac{1}{n}\log P_{n}(B)\leq-\inf_{x\in\bar{B}}I(x),

where
B^{\circ}
and
\bar{B}
denote the interior and closure of
B
, respectively. We say
I
is a
good rate function (GRF)
if it has compact level sets. Analogously, a sequence of
\mathcal{X}
-valued random variables
\{\mathsf{x}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is said to satisfy an LDP with GRF
I
if the sequence of their laws
\{\mathbb{P}\circ\mathsf{x}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
does.

We recall some definitions that will prove useful in our discussion of rate functions. Given a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow[-\infty,\infty]

for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
we let
f^{*}
denote its
Legendre transform
:

f^{*}(t):=\sup_{s\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\left[\langle s,t\rangle-f(s)\right],\qquad t\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.

Since we will frequently
invoke the contraction principle, Cramér’s theorem and Sanov’s theorem, we refer the reader to
Theorem 4.2.1, Corollary 6.1.6, and Section 6.2, respectively, of
[
8
]
, for precise statements.

Next, for
p\in[1,\infty]
, let
\|\cdot\|_{p}
denote the
\ell_{p}^{k}
norm on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
. When
p=2
, and where the context makes it clear, we will omit the subscript and simply write
\|\cdot\|
for the Euclidean norm. Let
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
denote the space of probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, by default equipped with the topology of weak convergence. We will sometimes consider the following restricted subsets of probability measures: for
q>0
, let

\mathcal{P}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{k}):=\left\{\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{k}):\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\|x\|^{q}\nu(dx)<\infty\right\}.

Definition 1.2
.

For
q>0
, we say a sequence of probability measures
\{\nu_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{P}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

converges to a limit
\nu
with respect to the
q
-Wasserstein topology
if we have both weak convergence, denoted

\nu_{n}\Rightarrow\nu
, as well as convergence of
q
-th moments
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|x\|^{q}\nu_{n}(dx)\rightarrow\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|x\|^{q}\nu(dx)
. As noted in Section 6 of
[
30
]
, the
q
-Wasserstein topology is metrizable through the
q
-Wasserstein metric
which we denote by
\mathcal{W}_{q}
.

2
Main results

We now provide a precise statement of our results. We start by defining the class of random vectors that we consider.
As in
[
8
, Definition 2.3.5]
, we define the domain of an (extended real-valued) function
f
defined on a Euclidean space
S
, denoted
D_{f}
, as the subset of points in
S
for which
f
is finite; furthermore, the function
f
is said to be
essentially smooth
if:
D_{f}\neq\emptyset
;
f
is differentiable in the interior
D_{f}^{\circ}
of
D_{f}
; and
f
is “steep” (i.e., if
D_{f}
has a boundary
\partial D_{f}
, then
\lim_{t\rightarrow\partial D_{f}}|\nabla f(t)|=\infty
).

Assumption 2.1

(quenched)
.

We impose the following conditions on the sequence of random vectors
\{X^{(n)}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.

(i)

Representation:
there exists a sequence of i.i.d. real-valued random variables
\{\xi_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
, a Borel measurable function
\mathbf{r}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
, and a continuous function
\rho:\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that

X^{(n)}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{(d)}}\xi^{(n)}\cdot\rho\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{r}(\xi_{i})\right),\quad n\in\mathbb{N},

where
\xi^{(n)}:=(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})
. Let

\Lambda
denote the log moment-generating function (mgf) of
(\xi_{1},\mathbf{r}(\xi_{1}))
:

\Lambda(s_{1},s_{2}):=\log\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(s_{1}\xi_{1}+s_{2}\mathbf{r}(\xi_{1})\right)\right],\quad s_{1}\in\mathbb{R},s_{2}\in\mathbb{R}.

(2.1)

(ii)

Log Moment-Generating Function (MGF):

There exists
q_{\star}>0
such that for every
(s_{1},s_{2})\in D_{\Lambda},

there exists a finite constant
C_{s_{2}}
(depending only on
s_{2}
and not
s_{1}
) such that

\Lambda(s_{1},s_{2})\leq C_{s_{2}}(1+|s_{1}|^{q_{\star}}).

(2.2)

Furthermore, there exists
T\leq\infty
such that
D_{\Lambda}=\mathbb{R}\times(-\infty,T)
.

(iii)

Integrated Log Mgf:
For any
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
, the function
\Psi_{\nu}:\mathbb{R}^{k+1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
obtained
as an integrated form of the log mgf,

\Psi_{\nu}(t_{1},t_{2}):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\Lambda\left(\langle t_{1},x\rangle,t_{2}\right)\nu(dx),\quad t_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{k},t_{2}\in\mathbb{R},

(2.3)

contains 0 in the interior of its domain, is lower semicontinuous, and is essentially smooth.

(iv)

Log MGF of Square:
The log mgf
\bar{\Lambda}
of
(\xi_{1}^{2},\mathbf{r}(\xi_{1}))
, given by

\bar{\Lambda}(s_{1},s_{2}):=\log\mathbb{E}[\exp(s_{1}\xi_{1}^{2}+s_{2}\mathbf{r}(\xi_{1}))],\qquad s_{1}\in\mathbb{R},\,s_{2}\in\mathbb{R},

(2.4)

is finite in a non-empty neighborhood of the origin
(0,0)
.

(v)

Tail Bound:
The exponent
q_{\star}
of part (ii) is bounded above,
q_{\star}<2
.

Remark 2.2
.

The inequality (
2.2
) in Assumption
2.1
(ii) implies that
for
t_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
and
t_{2}\in(-\infty,T)
, the map
\nu\mapsto\Psi_{\nu}(t_{1},t_{2})

is continuous with respect to the
q_{\star}
-Wasserstein topology. Further, for
t_{2}>T
,
we have
\Lambda(y,t_{2})=\infty
for all
y\in\mathbb{R}
, and hence,
(
2.3
) shows that
\Psi(t_{1},t_{2})=\infty
for all
t_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
.

Remark 2.3
.

A wide class of product measures satisfy Assumption
2.1
with
\rho\equiv\mathbf{r}\equiv 1
; namely those that have sufficiently light tails, in the sense of parts (iv) and (v). Examples of sequences of non-product measures
satisfying Assumption

More precisely, fix

, and for

\eta_{n,p}(S):=\frac{{\rm vol}_{n}(\{cx:x\in S,c\in[0,n^{1/p}]\})}{{\rm vol}_{n}(\mathbb{D}_{n,p})},

denoting Lebesgue measure on

, and let

.
Then:

(i)

for

, this condition follows from
results in
[

,

, with

(Quenched)

Theorem 2.6

(Annealed)
.

Proof.

It follows from Theorem 2.7 of

that

\{n^{-1/2}\mathbf{A}_{n,k}^{\intercal}X^{(n)}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{k}}
satisfies an

as defined above

To state the variational representation for the rate function

\mathbb{R}^{k}

\mathcal{C}(\nu):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}[x\otimes x]\,\nu(dx),\quad\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{k}).

(2.9)

\mathbb{H}_{k}(\nu):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}H(\nu|\gamma^{\otimes k})+\frac{1}{2}\textnormal{tr}(I_{k}-\mathcal{C}(\nu))&\textnormal{ if }\mathcal{C}(\nu)\preceq I_{k}\\
+\infty&\textnormal{ else}\end{array}\right.,\quad\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{k}).

(2.10)

Theorem 2.7
.

Fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
, suppose that the sequence
\{X^{(n)}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies Assumption

and

Then, we have the following variational formula:

\mathcal{J}^{\sf{an}}(x)=\inf_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{k})}\left\{\mathcal{J}_{\nu}^{\sf{qu}}(x)+\mathbb{H}_{k}(\nu)\right\},\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{k}.

Note that

when

, as would be expected from Jensen’s inequality given

More generally, the optimization problem (

can be interpreted as saying that at the large deviation level,
the annealed probability of a rare event is the infimum, over all random “environments” (in this case “projections”), of the probability
of the rare event conditioned on that environment plus the cost of the choice of the environment.
While such a relation is intuitive, rigorous proofs of such informal statements are typically non-trivial.
For example, such variational representations have been rigorously established only in a few specific cases, such as LDPs for random
walks in random environments on
\mathbb{Z}
in

and on supercritical Galton-Watson trees in
[
2
]
.
The one-dimensional case (

[

The proof of the

\mathsf{L}_{n,k}:=\mathsf{L}_{n,k}^{\mathbf{A}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{\sqrt{n}\mathbf{A}_{n,k}(i,\cdot)},\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

(2.12)

.

Fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Then for all

, the sequence
\{\mathsf{L}_{n,k}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{k}}

[

Remark 2.9
.

The first term in the definition (
2.10
) of
\mathbb{H}_{k}
is the relative entropy with respect to the
k
-dimensional
standard Gaussian measure, which is (due to Sanov’s theorem) the large deviation rate function for the sequence of empirical
measures of the rows of an
n\times k
matrix of i.i.d. standard Gaussian elements. Hence, Theorem

offers a way of characterizing the distinction between Haar-distributed matrices on the Stiefel manifold and Gaussian matrices. Outside of the large deviations literature, a different comparison between such Stiefel and Gaussian matrices can be found in
[
29
]
, which analyzes expectations of sublinear convex functions of random matrices.

Remark 2.10
.

The second term in the definition (
2.10
) of
\mathbb{H}_{k}
arises from the orthogonality and normalization constraint defining the Stiefel manifold.
Note that because
\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{A}_{n,k}^{\intercal}\mathbf{A}_{n,k}=I_{k})=1
, we have,
\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.}
,

\displaystyle 0=\text{tr}\left(I_{k}-\mathbf{A}_{n,k}^{\intercal}\mathbf{A}_{n,k}\right)=\text{tr}(I_{k}-\mathcal{C}(\mathsf{L}_{n,k})),\quad n\in\mathbb{N}_{k}.

(2.13)

Nonetheless, the definition of the rate function
\mathbb{H}_{k}
includes the trace term
\text{tr}(I_{k}-\mathcal{C}(\nu))
, and
\mathbb{H}_{k}(\nu)
is finite even for
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
such that
\text{tr}(I_{k}-\mathcal{C}(\nu))\neq 0
, due to the fact that the statement of
an LDP (Definition
1.1
) involves infimization of the rate function
\mathbb{H}_{k}
not over a set like
\mathcal{V}_{k}:=\{\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{k}):I_{k}=\mathcal{C}(\nu)\}
, but rather over its interior and closure (in the space of probability measures). In particular, the example set
\mathcal{V}_{k}
is neither open nor closed with respect to the weak topology. In fact, it is possible to show from
[
31
]
that
\mathcal{V}_{k}
is neither open nor closed with respect to
any
topology for which the sequence
of empirical measures
\{\mathsf{L}_{n,k}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{k}}
satisfies an LDP.

An immediate consequence of Theorem
2.8
is the following:

Corollary 2.11
.

Fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Then for all
q\in(0,2)
, the sequence
\{\mathsf{L}_{n,k}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{k}}
satisfies the strong law of large numbers in
\mathcal{P}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
. That is, almost surely, as

, we have
\mathcal{W}_{q}(\mathsf{L}_{n,k},\gamma_{2}^{\otimes k})\to 0
.

Proof.

By Theorem
2.8
, the rate function
\mathbb{H}_{k}
in (
2.10
) is strictly convex. Since
\mathbb{H}_{k}(\gamma^{\otimes k})=0
,
\mathbb{H}_{k}
attains its unique minimum over
\mathcal{P}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
at
\gamma_{2}^{\otimes k}
. For
\epsilon>0
, due to the LDP for
\{\mathsf{L}_{n,k}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{k}}
and the uniqueness of the minimum of
\mathbb{H}_{k}
, there exists
\delta>0
and

such that for

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{W}_{q}(\mathsf{L}_{n,k},\gamma_{2}^{\otimes k})>\epsilon)\leq e^{-n\delta}
, which combined with the Borel-Cantelli Lemma yields the almost sure convergence of
\mathsf{L}_{n,k}
.
∎

3
The Bartlett decomposition and its consequences

We recall the well known result of Bartlett on the
QR
decomposition of a matrix with independent standard Gaussian entries, and derive
certain consequences which will be used in the proofs of the main theorem.
Throughout, let
\mathbb{U}_{k}
denote the space of
k\times k
upper triangular matrices, and recall that
\mathbb{V}_{n,k}
denotes the Stiefel manifold of
k
-frames in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Definition 3.1
.

Fix
k\leq n\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
\mathbf{Z}_{n,k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
be an
n\times k
matrix with i.i.d. standard Gaussian elements. Let
\mathbf{Z}_{n,k}=\mathbf{Q}_{n,k}\mathbf{R}_{n,k}
be the
QR
decomposition of
\mathbf{Z}_{n,k}
as the product of the semi-orthogonal matrix
\mathbf{Q}_{n,k}\in\mathbb{V}_{n,k}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
and the upper triangular matrix
\mathbf{R}_{n,k}\in\mathbb{U}_{k}\subset\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
.

Proposition 3.2

(Bartlett decomposition
[
4
]
)
.

The law of
\mathbf{Q}_{n,k}
is
\sigma_{n,k}
, the Haar measure on
\mathbb{V}_{n,k}
. Moreover, the diagonal entries of
\mathbf{R}_{n,k}
satisfy
\mathbf{R}_{n,k}(i,i)\sim\chi_{n-i+1}
, the chi distribution with
n-i+1
degrees of freedom, for
i=1,\cdots,k
.

Remark 3.3
.

In fact, the matrices
\mathbf{Q}_{n,k}
and
\mathbf{R}_{n,k}
of the Bartlett decomposition are independent, and moreover, the marginal law of the off-diagonal entries of
\mathbf{R}_{n,k}
are also explicitly known; however, we will not need these facts for our analysis. Also, note that when
k=1
, the Bartlett decomposition corresponds to the classical polar decomposition of the
n
-dimensional Gaussian measure.

Let
\mathsf{L}^{\mathbf{Z}}_{n,k}
denote the empirical measure of the
rows
of
\mathbf{Z}_{n,k}
,

\mathsf{L}^{\mathbf{Z}}_{n,k}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{\mathbf{Z}_{n,k}(i,\cdot)}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{k}).

Then, due to Proposition
3.2
, for
\mathbf{A}_{n,k}
distributed according to the Haar measure
\sigma_{n,k}
on
\mathbb{V}_{n,k}
, we have

\mathbf{A}_{n,k}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{(d)}}\mathbf{Q}_{n,k}=\mathbf{Z}_{n,k}\mathbf{R}_{n,k}^{-1}.

(3.1)

In the second equality, we use the fact that
\mathbf{R}_{n,k}
is (almost surely) invertible, since it is an upper triangular matrix with diagonal entries that are all (almost surely) positive. Recalling the definition of
\mathsf{L}_{n,k}
from (
2.12
), and using
the representation (
3.1
), we have for any Borel set
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}
,

\displaystyle\mathsf{L}_{n,k}(B)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{(d)}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{r=1}^{n}\delta_{\sqrt{n}\mathbf{Z}_{n,k}(r,\cdot)\mathbf{R}_{n,k}^{-1}}(B)=\mathsf{L}^{\mathbf{Z}}_{n,k}\left(B\tfrac{\mathbf{R}_{n,k}}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

(3.2)

Fortuitously, each element of the matrix
\mathbf{R}_{n,k}
can be computed as a function of the
rows
of the matrix
\mathbf{Z}_{n,k}
, and can be written as the image of a linear functional of the measure
\mathsf{L}^{\mathbf{Z}}_{n,k}
. To be more precise, we recall the Gram-Schmidt process. For a matrix
Z\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
with columns
z_{1},\dots,z_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let

\displaystyle y_{1}

\displaystyle:=z_{1}

\displaystyle q_{1}:=\frac{y_{1}}{\|y_{1}\|_{2}};\hskip 64.60918pt

\displaystyle y_{i}

\displaystyle:=z_{i}-\sum_{h=1}^{i-1}\langle q_{h},z_{i}\rangle q_{h}

\displaystyle q_{i}:=\frac{y_{i}}{\|y_{i}\|_{2}},\quad i=2,\dots,k.

Then, we have the decomposition
Z=QR
where
Q=(q_{1},\dots,q_{k})
and

R=\begin{pmatrix}\langle q_{1},z_{1}\rangle&\langle q_{1},z_{2}\rangle&\langle q_{1},z_{3}\rangle&\cdots&\langle q_{1},z_{k}\rangle\\
0&\langle q_{2},z_{2}\rangle&\langle q_{2},z_{3}\rangle&\cdots&\langle q_{2},z_{k}\rangle\\
0&0&\langle q_{3},z_{3}\rangle&\cdots&\langle q_{3},z_{k}\rangle\\
\vdots&&&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&&&&\langle q_{k},z_{k}\rangle\end{pmatrix}.

(3.3)

Note that we also have the following relation among the elements of
R
: for
1\leq i\leq j\leq k
,

\langle q_{i},z_{j}\rangle=\frac{\langle y_{i},z_{j}\rangle}{\|y_{i}\|_{2}}=\frac{\langle z_{i},z_{j}\rangle-\sum_{h=1}^{i-1}\langle q_{h},z_{i}\rangle\langle q_{h},z_{j}\rangle}{\left(\|z_{i}\|^{2}-\sum_{h=1}^{i-1}\langle q_{h},z_{i}\rangle^{2}\right)^{1/2}}.

(3.4)

This expression allows us to clarify the relationship between
\mathbf{R}_{n,k}
and
\mathsf{L}^{\mathbf{Z}}_{n,k}
.

Definition 3.4
.

Let
\text{Sym}_{k}
be the space of real symmetric
k\times k
matrices. For
L,M\in\text{Sym}_{k}
, we write
L\succeq M
(resp.,
L\succ M
) if
L-M
is positive semi-definite (resp., positive definite).

We equip
\text{Sym}_{k}\subset\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
with the induced
Borel
\sigma
-algebra when viewing it as a measurable space, and
the Frobenius norm when viewed as a Banach space.

Definition 3.5
.

Define the map
\Gamma:\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}\rightarrow\mathbb{U}_{k}
according to the following iterative procedure: for
M\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
,
j=1,\dots,k
, and
i=1,\dots,j
,

\Gamma(M)_{ij}:=\frac{M_{ij}-\sum_{h=1}^{i-1}\Gamma(M)_{hi}\,\Gamma(M)_{hj}}{\left(M_{ii}-\sum_{h=1}^{i-1}\Gamma(M)_{hi}^{2}\right)^{1/2}}\,.

(3.5)

In the case
i=j=1
, we abide by the convention that empty summations are set to zero, so
\Gamma(M)_{11}:=M_{11}^{1/2}
.

Lemma 3.6
.

We have
\mathbb{P}
-a.s.,
n^{-1/2}\mathbf{R}_{n,k}=\Gamma(\mathcal{C}(\mathsf{L}^{\mathbf{Z}}_{n,k}))
, where
\mathcal{C}
is the covariance map of (
2.9
).

Proof.

The result follows from (
3.3
) and (
3.4
) upon noticing that for
1\leq i\leq j\leq k
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{ij}(\mathsf{L}^{\mathbf{Z}}_{n,k})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{r=1}^{n}\mathbf{Z}_{n,k}(r,i)\mathbf{Z}_{n,k}(r,j)=\frac{1}{n}\langle\,\mathbf{Z}_{n,k}(\cdot,i),\mathbf{Z}_{n,k}(\cdot,j)\,\rangle.

∎

Example 3.7
.

For example, when
k=1
, we have

\frac{\mathbf{R}_{n,1}}{\sqrt{n}}=\mathcal{C}_{11}(\mathsf{L}_{n,1})^{1/2}=\frac{\|\mathbf{Z}_{n,1}\|_{2}}{\sqrt{n}}
,
and for
k=2
, we have

\frac{\mathbf{R}_{n,2}}{\sqrt{n}}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{C}_{11}(\mathsf{L}_{n,2})&\frac{\mathcal{C}_{12}(\mathsf{L}_{n,2})}{\mathcal{C}_{11}(\mathsf{L}_{n,2})^{1/2}}\\
\ 0&\left(\mathcal{C}_{22}(\mathsf{L}_{n,2})-\frac{\mathcal{C}_{21}(\mathsf{L}_{n,2})^{2}}{\mathcal{C}_{11}(\mathsf{L}_{n,2})}\right)^{1/2}\end{pmatrix}.

Remark 3.8
.

Note that if
M
is symmetric positive semi-definite, then
\Gamma(M)
computes the
Cholesky decomposition
of
M
, so that
\Gamma(M)^{\intercal}\Gamma(M)=M
.

4
Proof of the empirical measure large deviations

The representation (
3.2
) and Lemma
3.6
suggest our plan of attack for the proof of Theorem
2.8
: first prove a joint LDP for
\{\mathsf{L}^{\mathbf{Z}}_{n,k},\mathcal{C}(\mathsf{L}^{\mathbf{Z}}_{n,k})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{k}}
; then, establish an LDP for
\{\mathsf{L}_{n,k}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{k}}
. Note that we do not attempt to directly establish an LDP for
\{\mathsf{L}_{n,k}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{k}}
from that for
\{\mathsf{L}^{\mathbf{Z}}_{n,k}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{k}}
, because the map
\mathsf{L}^{\mathbf{Z}}_{n,k}\mapsto\mathsf{L}_{n,k}
is not continuous with respect to the weak topology. Nor do we attempt to directly establish an LDP for
\{\mathsf{L}^{\mathbf{Z}}_{n,k},\Gamma(\mathcal{C}(\mathsf{L}^{\mathbf{Z}}_{n,k}))\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{k}}
, because the map
\Gamma\circ\mathcal{C}
is a nonlinear functional on the space of measures on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
. In contrast, our proposed first step is tractable precisely because
\mathcal{C}
is a linear functional.
We will make use of the following result, which is stated in
[
17
, Corollary A.2]
as a corollary of
[
3
, Proposition 6.4]
.

Lemma 4.1

(approximate contraction principle)
.

Let
\Sigma
be a Polish space and
{\mathcal{X}}
be a separable Banach space with topological dual
\mathcal{X}^{*}
. Let
\{\mathscr{L}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of
\mathcal{P}(\Sigma)
-valued random variables such that for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathscr{L}_{n}
is the empirical measure of
n
i.i.d.
\Sigma
-valued random variables
\mathsf{s}_{1},\dots,\mathsf{s}_{n}
with common distribution
\mu
(that does not depend on
n
). For any continuous
W:\Sigma\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, define

\widehat{\Lambda}(W):=\log\mathbb{E}[e^{W(\mathsf{s}_{1})}].

(4.1)

Also, let
\mathbf{c}:\Sigma\mapsto{\mathcal{X}}
be a continuous map such
that
0
lies in the interior
\mathscr{D}^{\circ}
of the set

\mathscr{D}:=\left\{\alpha\in\mathcal{X}^{*}:\widehat{\Lambda}(\langle\alpha,\mathbf{c}(\cdot)\rangle)<\infty\right\},

(4.2)

and let
\mathscr{C}_{n}:=\int_{\Sigma}\mathbf{c}(x)\mathscr{L}_{n}(dx)
. Lastly, define
F:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as

F(x):=\sup_{\alpha\in\mathscr{D}_{\circ}}\langle\alpha,x\rangle,\quad x\in\mathcal{X}.

(4.3)

Then,
\{\mathscr{L}_{n},\mathscr{C}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies an LDP with the GRF
\mathbb{I}:\mathcal{P}(\Sigma)\times\mathcal{X}\rightarrow[0,\infty]
defined by

\mathbb{I}(\nu,x):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}H(\nu|\mu)+F\left(x-\int_{\Sigma}\mathbf{c}\,d\nu\right)&\textnormal{ if }H(\nu|\mu)<\infty,\\
+\infty&\textnormal{ else,}\end{array}\right.\quad\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\Sigma),x\in\mathcal{X}.

(4.4)

Lemma 4.2
.

For any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the sequence
\{\mathsf{L}^{\mathbf{Z}}_{n,k},\mathcal{C}(\mathsf{L}^{\mathbf{Z}}_{n,k})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{k}}
satisfies an LDP in
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{k})\times\textnormal{Sym}_{k}
with GRF
\mathbb{J}_{k}:\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{k})\times\textnormal{Sym}_{k}\rightarrow[0,\infty]
, defined, for
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
and
M\in\textnormal{Sym}_{k}
, to be

\mathbb{J}_{k}(\nu,M):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}H(\nu|\gamma^{\otimes k})+\tfrac{1}{2}\textnormal{tr}\left(M-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}[z\otimes z]\,\nu(dz)\right)&\textnormal{ if }\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}[z\otimes z]\,\nu(dz)\preceq M,\\
+\infty&\textnormal{ else.}\end{array}\right.

(4.5)

Proof.

We invoke the approximate contraction principle of Lemma
4.1
with the following parameters:

\Sigma=\mathbb{R}^{k}
;
\mathcal{X}=\text{Sym}_{k}
and
\mathcal{X}^{*}=\text{Sym}_{k}
;

\mathbf{c}(z):=[z\otimes z]
for
z\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
;

\mathscr{L}_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{\mathsf{s}_{j}}\mbox{ for }\mathsf{s}_{1},\mathsf{s}_{2},\dots
i.i.d. random vectors with common distribution
\gamma^{\otimes k};

and
\mathscr{C}_{n}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\mathbf{c}\,d\mathscr{L}_{n}
.
Note that

(\mathsf{L}^{\mathbf{Z}}_{n,k},\mathcal{C}(\mathsf{L}^{\mathbf{Z}}_{n,k}))\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{(d)}}(\mathscr{L}_{n},\mathscr{C}_{n}),\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}_{k}.

(4.6)

With
\widehat{\Lambda}
as defined in (
4.1
),
the domain
\mathscr{D}
specified in (
4.2
) takes the form

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}

\displaystyle=\left\{\zeta\in\text{Sym}_{k}:\log\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\exp\left(\langle\zeta,\mathbf{c}(z)\rangle\right)\gamma^{\otimes k}(dz)<\infty\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{\zeta\in\text{Sym}_{k}:\log\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{k/2}}\exp\left(-z^{\intercal}(\tfrac{1}{2}I_{k}-\zeta)z\right)dz<\infty\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{\zeta\in\text{Sym}_{k}:\tfrac{1}{2}I_{k}-\zeta\succ 0\right\}.

This last expression indicates that
\mathscr{D}
is a shifted reflection of the positive definite cone, hence open, implying that
\mathscr{D}^{\circ}=\mathscr{D}
. This expression for the form of
\mathscr{D}
also makes it clear that
0\in\mathscr{D}^{\circ}
. Lastly the value of the supremum in the definition of
F
takes an explicit form due to the linearity of the trace functional and the fact that the constraint set
\mathscr{D}
is a cone (due to the positive definiteness constraint): for
\eta\in\text{Sym}_{k}
,

F(\eta)=\sup_{\zeta\in\text{Sym}_{k}}\left\{\text{Tr}(\zeta\eta):\zeta\prec\tfrac{1}{2}I_{k}\right\}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\frac{1}{2}\text{Tr}(\eta)&\text{ if }\eta\succeq 0,\\
+\infty&\text{ else.}\end{array}\right.

Therefore, (
4.6
) and Lemma
4.1
together imply that

\{(\mathsf{L}^{\mathbf{Z}}_{n,k},\mathcal{C}(\mathsf{L}^{\mathbf{Z}}_{n,k}))\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{k}}
satisfies the stated LDP.
∎

We now establish a relation between the rate function
\mathbb{J}_{k}
and the rate function
\mathbb{H}_{k}
of Theorem
2.8
.

Lemma 4.3
.

For any
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
, given
\mathbb{H}_{k}
of (
2.10
),
\mathbb{J}_{k}
of (
4.5
), and
\Gamma
of(
3.5
), we have

\mathbb{H}_{k}(\nu)=\widetilde{\mathbb{H}}_{k}(\nu):=\inf_{M\in\textnormal{Sym}_{k}}\mathbb{J}_{k}(\nu(\,\cdot\times\Gamma(M)^{-1}),M).

Proof.

As noted in Remark
3.8
, for
M\in\text{Sym}_{k}
and
\Gamma
as in (
3.5
), we have
M=\Gamma(M)^{\intercal}\Gamma(M)
. Given this equality, the constraint

M\succeq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}[x\otimes x]\,\nu(dx\times\Gamma(M)^{-1})

can be rewritten, using the notation
\mathcal{C}
from (
2.9
), as

I_{k}\succeq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}[x\otimes x]\nu(dx)=\mathcal{C}(\nu).

If the preceding constraint is satisfied, then using the form of
\mathbb{J}_{k}
in (
4.5
) in the first equality below, the chain rule for relative entropy in the third equality, and then the form of the Gaussian distribution
\gamma^{\otimes k}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{J}_{k}

\displaystyle(\nu(\,\cdot\times\Gamma(M)^{-1}),M)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(M_{ii}-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}x_{i}^{2}\,\nu(dx\times\Gamma(M)^{-1})\right)+H(\nu(\,\cdot\times\Gamma(M)^{-1})|\gamma^{\otimes k})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}M_{ii}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}y^{\intercal}\,\Gamma(M)\Gamma(M)^{\intercal}y\,\nu(dy)+H(\nu|\gamma^{\otimes k}(\,\cdot\times\Gamma(M)))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}M_{ii}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}y^{\intercal}\,\Gamma(M)\Gamma(M)^{\intercal}y\,\nu(dy)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\log(\tfrac{d\gamma^{\otimes k}(\,\cdot\times\Gamma(M))}{d\gamma^{\otimes k}})\,d\nu+H(\nu|\gamma^{\otimes k})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}M_{ii}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}y^{\intercal}\,\Gamma(M)\Gamma(M)^{\intercal}y\,\nu(dy)+\frac{1}{2}\log\det(\Gamma(M)^{-\intercal}\Gamma(M)^{-1})

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}y^{\intercal}\,(\Gamma(M)\Gamma(M)^{\intercal}-I_{k})y\,\nu(dy)+H(\nu|\gamma^{\otimes k})

Due to the upper triangular structure of
\Gamma(M)
, we have

\frac{1}{2}\log\det(\Gamma(M)^{-\intercal}\Gamma(M)^{-1})=-\log\det(\Gamma(M))=-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\log\Gamma(M)_{ii}.

Also, note that
\text{Tr}(I_{k}-\mathcal{C}(\nu))=k-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}y^{\intercal}y\,\nu(dy)
.
Hence, invoking the definition of
\mathbb{H}_{k}
in (
2.10
),
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{J}_{k}(\nu(\,\cdot\times\Gamma(M)^{-1}),M)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}(M_{ii}-1)-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\log\Gamma(M)_{ii}+\mathbb{H}_{k}(\nu).

Taking the infimum of the expression above over
M\in\textnormal{Sym}_{k}
, we see that

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbb{H}}_{k}(\nu)

\displaystyle=\inf_{M\in\textnormal{Sym}_{k}}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(\frac{M_{ii}-1}{2}-\log\Gamma(M)_{ii}\right)\right\}+\mathbb{H}_{k}(\nu).

(4.7)

Note that by the definition of
\Gamma
in (
3.5
),

\Gamma(M)_{ii}=\left(M_{ii}-\sum_{h=1}^{i-1}\Gamma(M)_{hi}^{2}\right)^{1/2},

and by the definition of the Gram-Schmidt process, we have
M_{ii}\geq\sum_{h=1}^{i-1}\Gamma(M)_{hi}^{2}\geq 0
. Thus, for all
i=1,\dots,k
, for any fixed
M_{ii}
, the maximum value of
\Gamma(M)_{ii}
is attained when
\Gamma(M)_{hi}=0
for
h=1,\dots,i-1
. Therefore,
once again using
M=\Gamma(M)^{\intercal}\Gamma(M)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\inf_{M\in\textnormal{Sym}_{k}}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(\frac{M_{ii}-1}{2}-\log\Gamma(M)_{ii}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=\inf_{M_{ii}\geq 0,i=1,\dots,k}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(M_{ii}-1-\log M_{ii}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\inf_{M_{ii}\geq 0}\left\{M_{ii}-1-\log M_{ii}\right\},

which is clearly equal to zero.
Together with (
4.7
), this shows that
\mathbb{H}_{k}=\widetilde{\mathbb{H}}_{k}
.
∎

Fix

For any
q\in(0,2)

-Wasserstein topology. In addition,

Proof.

The proof is an elementary modification of the proof of the

Proof of Theorem
2.8
.

Let
\Gamma
be as defined in (
3.5
). Due to (

) and Lemma
3.6
, we have

\mathsf{L}_{n,k}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{(d)}}\mathsf{L}^{\mathbf{Z}}_{n,k}(\,\cdot\times\tfrac{\mathbf{R}_{n,k}}{\sqrt{n}})=\mathsf{L}^{\mathbf{Z}}_{n,k}\left(\,\cdot\times\Gamma\left(\mathcal{C}(\mathsf{L}^{\mathbf{Z}}_{n,k})\right)\right).

The image of

is also continuous. An application of the contraction principle

in

(i.e., with respect to the weak topology), with GRF

\displaystyle\inf_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{k}),M\in\textnormal{Sym}_{k}}\{\mathbb{J}_{k}(\mu,M):\nu=\mu(\,\cdot\times\Gamma(M))\}=\inf_{M\in\textnormal{Sym}_{k}}\mathbb{J}_{k}(\nu(\,\cdot\times\Gamma(M)^{-1}),M)=\mathbb{H}_{k}(\nu),

Fix

. In order to establish the LDP for

in
\mathcal{P}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
(i.e., with respect to the stronger
q
-Wasserstein topology), by Corollary 4.2.6 of
[
8
]
,
it suffices to show exponential tightness of

in the
q
-Wasserstein topology. Let

be the set defined in (

), which is compact (with respect to the

-Wasserstein topology) due to Lemma

, and

.

Lastly, the strict convexity of

5
Proof of the quenched LDP

In this section, we state the proof of Theorem
2.4
. As a precursor, we state two lemmas that will assist with part (iii) of the theorem.

Lemma 5.1
.

Fix
m\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
\mathscr{F}
be a set of functions from
\mathbb{R}^{m}
to
\mathbb{R}
such that every
f\in\mathscr{F}
is symmetric about 0 and convex. Then, defining
g:\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as

g(x):=\inf_{f\in\mathscr{F}}f(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{m},

the function
g
is monotone with respect to scaling in the sense that for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, the mapping

\mathbb{R}_{+}\ni c\mapsto g(cx)\in\mathbb{R}

(5.1)

is non-decreasing.

Proof.

Fix
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
c_{1}<c_{2}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. For any
f\in\mathscr{F}
, the symmetry about 0 and convexity of
f
implies that
f
has a global minimum at 0, hence

\displaystyle f(c_{1}x)

\displaystyle=f(\tfrac{c_{1}}{c_{2}}\times c_{2}x+\tfrac{c_{2}-c_{1}}{c_{2}}\times 0)

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{c_{1}}{c_{2}}f(c_{2}x)+\tfrac{c_{2}-c_{1}}{c_{2}}f(0)

\displaystyle=f(c_{2}x)+\tfrac{c_{2}-c_{1}}{c_{2}}(f(0)-f(c_{2}x))

\displaystyle\leq f(c_{2}x),

where the first inequality follows from convexity, and the second inequality is due to the global minimum at 0. Taking the infimum over all
f\in\mathscr{F}
on both sides, we find that
g(c_{1}x)\leq g(c_{2}x)
, completing the proof.
∎

Lemma 5.2
.

Fix
m\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
\mathsf{x}=\{\mathsf{x}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
denote a sequence of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
-valued random variables that satisfies an LDP with GRF
I_{\mathsf{x}}
. Let
U
be a uniformly distributed random variable on
[0,1]
independent of
\{\mathsf{x}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. If for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, the mapping
\mathbb{R}_{+}\ni c\mapsto I_{\mathsf{x}}(cx)\in[0,\infty]
is non-decreasing, then the scaled sequence
\{U^{1/n}\mathsf{x}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies an LDP with GRF
I_{\mathsf{x}}
.

Proof.

Due to
[
10
, Lemma 3.3]
, the sequence
\{U^{1/n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies an LDP with the good rate function

I_{U}(u):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\log u&u\in(0,1];\\
+\infty&\textnormal{ else. }\end{array}\right.

By independence, the sequence
\{U^{1/n},\,\,\mathsf{x}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies a joint LDP with the GRF
I_{U,\mathsf{x}}:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
defined as
I_{U,\mathsf{x}}(u,w):=I_{U}(u)+I_{\mathsf{x}}(x)
. By the contraction principle, the scaled sequence
\{U^{1/n}\mathsf{x}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies an LDP with the rate function
I
, where for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,

\displaystyle I(x)

\displaystyle:=\inf_{u\in\mathbb{R},\,y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\{I_{U}(u)+I_{\mathsf{x}}(y):uy=x\}=\inf_{u\in(0,1]}\{-\log u+I_{\mathsf{x}}(\tfrac{x}{u})\}.

The mapping
u\mapsto 1/u
is monotonically decreasing, which when combined with the assumption on
I_{\mathsf{x}}
implies that
u\mapsto I_{\mathsf{x}}(x/u)
is monotonically decreasing. Since
u\mapsto-\log u
is also monotonically decreasing, the infimum above is attained at
u=1
, hence
I(x)=I_{\mathsf{x}}(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.4
.

Suppose Assumption
2.1
holds for some
\{\xi_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
,
\mathbf{r}
,
\rho
,
q_{\star}>0
, and
T\leq\infty
, all as defined in the statement of the assumption. We first prove an LDP for the following
\mathbb{R}^{k+1}
-valued sequence:

R_{\mathbf{a}}^{(n)}:=\left(n^{-1/2}\,\mathbf{a}_{n,k}^{\intercal}\xi^{(n)},\,\,\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{r}(\xi_{i})\right),\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}.

(5.2)

In terms of the log mgf
\Lambda
of
(\xi_{1},\mathbf{r}(\xi_{1}))
, the scaled log mgf of
R_{\mathbf{a}}^{(n)}
takes the form:
for
t_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
and
t_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(n\,\langle t,R_{\mathbf{a}}^{(n)}\rangle)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\sqrt{n}\,\xi_{i}\langle t_{1},\mathbf{a}_{n,k}(i,\cdot)\rangle+t_{2}\,\mathbf{r}(\xi_{i})\right)\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\log\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\sqrt{n}\,\xi_{i}\langle t_{1},\mathbf{a}_{n,k}(i,\cdot)\rangle+t_{2}\,\mathbf{r}(\xi_{i})\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\Psi_{\mathsf{L}_{n,k}^{\mathbf{a}}}(t_{1},t_{2}),

Fix
t_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}

t_{2}\geq T

+\infty
due to Remark
2.2
. For

-Wasserstein continuity pointed out in Remark
2.2

-Wasserstein convergence of (

), we take the limit as

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(n\,\langle t,R_{\mathbf{a}}^{(n)}\rangle)\right]

\displaystyle=\Psi_{\nu}(t_{1},t_{2}).

Due to the lower semicontinuity and essential smoothness of
\Psi_{\nu}
, which follow from Assumption
2.1
(iii), the Gärtner-Ellis theorem (see, e.g.,
[
8
, Theorem 2.3.6]
) yields the LDP for the sequence
\{R_{\mathbf{a}}^{(n)}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathbb{R}^{k+1}
with the GRF
\Psi_{\nu}^{*}
from (
2.5
).

Due to the representation of
X^{(n)}
given by Assumption
2.1
(i), we have

n^{-1/2}\mathbf{A}_{n,k}^{\intercal}X^{(n)}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{(d)}}\rho\left((R_{\mathbf{a}}^{(n)})_{2}\right)(R_{\mathbf{a}}^{(n)})_{1},

where

is continuous. The LDP for
\{R_{\mathbf{a}}^{(n)}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

\mathbb{R}^{k+1}\ni(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})\mapsto\rho(\tau_{2})\tau_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}

\{n^{-1/2}\mathbf{a}_{n,k}^{\intercal}X^{(n)}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{k}}

\mathbb{R}^{k}

2.7
) follows from using the constraint

In turn, the LDP from part (i) implies part (ii) of the theorem since by Corollary
2.11
, almost surely,

{\mathcal{W}}_{q_{\star}}(\mathsf{L}_{n,k}^{\mathbf{A}},\gamma^{\otimes k})={\mathcal{W}}_{q_{\star}}(\mathsf{L}_{n,k},\gamma^{\otimes k})\rightarrow 0
as

.

5.1
with dimension

, the set of symmetric convex functions

5.2
with

∎

Lemma 6.2
.

,

, independently of
\{\xi_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
. For

\Phi_{n}(t_{1},t_{2}):=\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\sqrt{n}\,\xi^{(n)}\mathbf{A}_{n,k}t_{1}+t_{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{r}(\xi_{i})\right)\right],\quad t_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{k},\,t_{2}\in\mathbb{R},

(6.3)

where

.
Then, for

and

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Phi_{n}(t_{1},t_{2})=\Phi(t_{1},t_{2}),

\Phi(t_{1},t_{2}):=\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{k})}\left\{\Psi_{\nu}(t_{1},t_{2})-\mathbb{H}_{k}(\nu)\right\}=\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{K}}\left\{\Psi_{\nu}(t_{1},t_{2})-\mathbb{H}_{k}(\nu)\right\}.

(6.4)

\displaystyle\Phi_{n}(t_{1},t_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\sqrt{n}\,\xi_{i}\left(\mathbf{A}_{n,k}t_{1}\right)_{i}+t_{2}\mathbf{r}(\xi_{i})\right)\big|\mathbf{A}_{n,k}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Lambda(\sqrt{n}\,\langle\mathbf{A}_{n,k}(i,\cdot),t_{1}\rangle,\,t_{2}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(n\,\Psi_{\mathsf{L}_{n,k}}(t_{1},t_{2})\right)\right],

for
t_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
and
t_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
,
where
\mathsf{L}_{n,k}
is as in (
2.12
). Now, let
T\leq\infty
and
q_{\star}\in(0,2)
be as specified in Assumption
2.1
(ii).
For
t_{2}\geq T
, by Remark
2.2
, both

\Phi_{n}(\cdot,t_{2})
and
\Phi(\cdot,t_{2})
are identically equal to infinity, and so the limit holds trivially.
Now, suppose
t_{2}<T
. Recall from Theorem
2.8
that the sequence
\{\mathsf{L}_{n,k}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{k}}
satisfies an LDP in
\mathcal{P}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
for all
q\in(0,2)
, with the GRF
\mathbb{H}_{k}
. Due to the bound in Assumption
2.1
(ii), the map
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})\ni\nu\mapsto\Psi_{\nu}(t_{1},t_{2})\in\mathbb{R}
is continuous with respect to the
q_{\star}
-Wasserstein topology, and we know
q_{\star}\in(0,2)
due to Assumption
2.1
(v). By Varadhan’s lemma
[
8
, Theorem 4.3.1]
which is applicable due to Lemma
6.1
, it follows that the limit of
\Phi_{n}(t_{1},t_{2})
is given by
\Phi(t_{1},t_{2})
defined in (
6.4
).

To complete the proof of the lemma, it only remains to
establish the last equality in (
6.4
), but this is an immediate consequence of the fact that
\mathbb{H}_{k}(\nu)=\infty

for
\nu\notin\mathcal{K}
.

∎

Lemma 6.3
.

Suppose Assumption
2.1
holds, and for each
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
,
let
\Psi_{\nu}
be as defined in (
2.3
), let
\Psi_{\nu}^{*}
denote its Legendre transform, as specified in
(
2.5
), and let
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
be the set defined in (
4.8
).
Then the Legendre transform
\Phi^{*}
of the function
\Phi
defined in (
6.4
) satisfies,
for
\tau_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, and
\tau_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
,

{\Phi}^{*}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})=\inf_{\nu\in\mathcal{K}}\left\{\Psi_{\nu}^{*}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})+\mathbb{H}_{k}(\nu)\right\}=\inf_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{k})}\left\{\Psi_{\nu}^{*}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})+\mathbb{H}_{k}(\nu)\right\}.

(6.5)

Proof.

First, note that the second equality in (
6.5
) holds because
\mathbb{H}_{k}(\nu)=\infty
for
\nu\not\in\mathcal{K}
.
Next, fix the following:

•

let
\Lambda
,
T
be as in Assumption
2.1
(ii), and define
\mathcal{D}_{T}:=\mathbb{R}^{k}\times(-\infty,T)
;

•

let
q_{\star}\in(0,2)
be as in Assumption
2.1
(ii), and let
\mathcal{M}_{q_{\star}}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
denote the space of finite signed measures (not necessarily probability measures) on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, equipped with the
q_{\star}
-Wasserstein topology.

Fix
\tau=(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}
. Then by the definition (
2.5
) of
\Psi_{\nu}^{*}
,

\displaystyle\Psi_{\nu}^{*}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})

\displaystyle=\sup_{(t_{1},t_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}}\{\langle\tau_{1},t_{1}\rangle+\tau_{2}t_{2}-\Psi_{\nu}(t_{1},t_{2})\}

\displaystyle=\sup_{(t_{1},t_{2})\in\mathcal{D}_{T}}\{\langle\tau_{1},t_{1}\rangle+\tau_{2}t_{2}-\Psi_{\nu}(t_{1},t_{2})\},

(6.6)

where the second equality holds because, by Remark
2.2
,

\Psi_{\nu}(t_{1},t_{2})=\infty
if
t_{2}\geq T
.
Thus, the right-hand side of (
6.5
) is equal to

\inf_{\nu\in\mathcal{K}}\sup_{t=(t_{1},t_{2})\in\mathcal{D}_{T}}\inf_{\nu\in\mathcal{K}}F_{\tau}(\nu,t),

where

F_{\tau}(\nu,t):=\langle\tau_{1},t_{1}\rangle+\tau_{2}t_{2}-\Psi_{\nu}(t_{1},t_{2})+\mathbb{H}_{k}(\nu),\quad\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{k}),\,t=(t_{1},t_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}.

On the other hand, by the definition of
\Phi^{*}
and the
representation (
6.4
) for
\Phi
,

\displaystyle\Phi^{*}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{(t_{1},t_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{k+1}}\{\langle\tau_{1},t_{1}\rangle+\tau_{2}t_{2}-\Phi(t_{1},t_{2})\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{t=(t_{1},t_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{k+1}}\inf_{\nu\in\mathcal{K}}F_{\tau}(\nu,t),

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{t=(t_{1},t_{2})\in\mathcal{D}_{T}}\inf_{\nu\in\mathcal{K}}F_{\tau}(\nu,t),

where the last equality uses the fact that
for
t_{2}>T
,
\Psi_{\nu}(t_{1},t_{2})=\infty
and hence,
F_{\tau}(\nu,t)=-\infty
(see Remark
2.2
).
Thus, to prove the
first equality in (
6.5
), it suffices to show that for all

(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}
,

\inf_{\nu\in\mathcal{K}}\sup_{(t_{1},t_{2})\in\mathcal{D}_{T}}F_{\tau}(\nu,(t_{1},t_{2}))=\sup_{(t_{1},t_{2})\in\mathcal{D}_{T}}\inf_{\nu\in\mathcal{K}}F_{\tau}(\nu,(t_{1},t_{2})).

(6.7)

To justify the exchange of infimum and supremum in (
6.7
), we verify the conditions of the minimax theorem
[
27
, Corollary 3.3]
. That is, for
(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}
, we note that

•

the set
\mathcal{D}_{T}=\mathbb{R}^{k}\times(-\infty,T)
is a convex subset of the topological vector space
\mathbb{R}^{k+1}
;

•

due to Lemma
4.4
,
\mathcal{K}
is a convex compact subset of the topological vector space
\mathcal{M}_{q_{\star}}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
;

•

for
t=(t_{1},t_{2})\in\mathcal{D}_{T}
: the lower semicontinuity of
F_{\tau}(\cdot,t)
follows from the lower semicontinuity of
\nu\to\Psi_{\nu}(t)
due to Assumption
2.1
(iii) and of
\mathbb{H}_{k}
(as it is a GRF); the convexity of
F_{\tau}(\cdot,t)
follows from the linearity of
\nu\mapsto\Psi_{\nu}(t)
and the convexity of
\mathbb{H}_{k}
, which was established in Theorem
2.8
;

•

for
\nu\in\mathcal{K}
: the lower semicontinuity of
t\to\Psi_{\nu}(t)
on
\mathcal{D}_{T}
follows from Assumption
2.1
(iii); the convexity of
\Psi_{\nu}
on
\mathcal{D}_{T}
follows from linearity of expectation, the definition (
2.3
), and the fact that
\Lambda
is convex since it is a log mgf;

•

since
t\mapsto\langle\tau_{1},t_{1}\rangle+\tau_{2}t_{2}
is continuous and linear, it follows that
F_{\tau}(\nu,\cdot)
is upper semicontinuous and concave on
\mathcal{D}_{T}
.

Due to the conditions verified above, the minimax theorem can be applied to conclude that (
6.7
), and hence, the desired first equality in (
6.5
), holds. This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.7
.

Let
\{\xi_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\mathbf{r}
be as in Assumption
2.1
, let
\xi^{(n)}:=(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n})
and
define the sequence
\{R^{(n)}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathbb{R}^{k+1}
by

R^{(n)}:=\left(n^{-1/2}\,\mathbf{A}_{n,k}^{\intercal}\xi^{(n)},\,\,\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{r}(\xi_{i})\right),\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}.

(6.8)

First note that the law of
\mathbf{A}_{n,k}
is invariant to orthogonal transformation and independent of
\xi^{(n)}
, hence
\mathbf{A}_{n,k}^{\intercal}\tfrac{\xi^{(n)}}{\|\xi^{(n)}\|_{2}}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{(d)}}\mathbf{A}_{n,k}(1,\cdot)

and
\mathbf{A}_{n,k}^{\intercal}\tfrac{\xi^{(n)}}{\|\xi^{(n)}\|_{2}}
is independent of
\xi^{(n)}
; we refer to
[
10
, Lemma 6.3]
for the proof of the simpler case when
k=1
. As a consequence,

R^{(n)}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{(d)}}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sqrt{n}\mathbf{A}_{n,k}(1,\cdot)\|\xi^{(n)}\|_{2},\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{r}(\xi_{i})\right).

Define the
\mathbb{R}^{k+2}
-valued sequence of random variables,

S^{(n)}:=\left(\mathbf{A}_{n,k}(1,\cdot),\frac{1}{n}\|\xi^{(n)}\|_{2}^{2},\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{r}(\xi_{i})\right),\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

Since by part (iv) of Assumption
2.1
, the domain of
\bar{\Lambda}
, the log mgf of
(\xi_{1}^{2},\mathbf{r}(\xi_{1}))
,
contains a neighborhood of the origin,
by Cramér’s theorem

\{\left(\|\xi^{(n)}\|_{2}^{2},\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{r}(\xi_{i})\right)\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies an LDP
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with the convex GRF
\bar{\Lambda}^{*}
, equal to the
Legendre transform of
\bar{\Lambda}
.

The independence of
\mathbf{A}_{n,k}
from
\{\xi_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
, along with
Theorem 3.4 of
[
5
]
(applied to the case of
p=2
therein, with their canonically projected
X^{(k)}
equivalent to our
\mathbf{A}_{n,k}(1,\cdot)
) then implies that the sequence
\{S^{(n)}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

satisfies an LDP with the convex GRF
J:\mathbb{R}^{k+2}\rightarrow[0,\infty]
defined by

J(a,b,c):=-\tfrac{1}{2}\log(1-\|a\|_{2}^{2})+\widehat{J}(b,c),

for
a\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
such that
\|a\|_{2}^{2}<1
and
b,c\in\mathbb{R}
. Then, by the contraction principle,
\{R^{(n)}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies an LDP with the GRF
J_{R}:\mathbb{R}^{k+1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined as follows:

J_{R}(x,z):=\inf_{y\in\mathbb{R}:y>\|x\|^{2}}J(xy^{-1/2},y,z),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{k},z\geq 0.

Note that
J_{R}
is convex due to
[
10
, Lemma 6.2]
and Theorem 5.3 of
[
25
]
.

Let
\Phi
be as in (
6.4
) and
T
be as in Assumption
2.1
(ii).
Fix
t_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
,
t_{2}<T
, and let
t=(t_{1},t_{2})
. For
0<\delta<\infty
,

\mathbb{E}[e^{\delta n\langle t,R^{(n)}\rangle}]=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\delta n\Psi_{\mathsf{L}_{n,k}}(t_{1},t_{2})}\right].

(6.9)

Since
t_{2}<T
, due to Lemma
6.1
and the observation (
6.9
), we see that
\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{E}[e^{\delta n\langle t,R^{(n)}\rangle}]
is finite. By Varadhan’s lemma
[
8
, Theorem 4.3.1]
and the finiteness condition (4.3.3) therein applied to any
\delta>1
, we find that

\Phi(t_{1},t_{2})=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{E}[e^{n\langle t,R^{(n)}\rangle}]=\sup_{\tau_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{k},\tau_{2}\in\mathbb{R}}\{\langle t_{1},\tau_{1}\rangle+t_{2}\tau_{2}-J_{R}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})\}.

(6.10)

where the first equality above holds by Lemma
6.2
on observing that
\Phi_{n}
in (
6.3
) is the scaled log mgf of
R_{n}
.

Now fix
t_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
and
t_{2}\geq T
.
We claim that (
6.10
) continues to hold, but now with both sides equal to infinity.
The fact that
\Phi(t_{1},t_{2})=\infty
follows from the definition (
6.4
) of
\Phi
and the observation that

\Phi_{\nu}(t_{1},t_{2})=\infty
when
t_{2}\geq T
(by Remark
2.2
).
To show that the term on the right-hand side of (
6.10
) is also equal to infinity, for
s_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
, define
\widetilde{\Lambda}(s_{2}):=\Lambda(0,s_{2})
. Note that
\widetilde{\Lambda}
is the log mgf of
\mathbf{r}(\xi_{1})
. Due to Assumption
2.1
(iv), the domain of
\widetilde{\Lambda}
contains a non-empty neighborhood around 0, hence by Cramér’s theorem
the sequence
\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{r}(\xi_{i})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies an LDP in
\mathbb{R}
with GRF
\widetilde{\Lambda}^{*}
. However, due to the contraction principle and the continuity of the projection map, we also know that
\widetilde{\Lambda}^{*}(\tau_{2})=\inf_{\tau_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}J_{R}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})
for all
\tau_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
. Note that this infimum is attained at some
\tau_{1}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
because, as a good rate function,
J_{R}
is lower semicontinuous with compact level sets. Therefore, on the right-hand side of (
6.10
), if
t_{2}\geq T
, then

\displaystyle\sup_{\tau_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{k},\tau_{2}\in\mathbb{R}}\{\langle t_{1},\tau_{1}\rangle+t_{2}\tau_{2}-J_{R}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})\}

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{\tau_{2}\in\mathbb{R}}\{\langle t_{1},\tau_{1}^{*}\rangle+t_{2}\tau_{2}-J_{R}(\tau_{1}^{*},\tau_{2})\}=\langle t_{1},\tau_{1}^{*}\rangle+\widetilde{\Lambda}(t_{2})=\Lambda(0,t_{2})=\infty,

where the first equality used the identity
(\widetilde{\Lambda}^{*})^{*}=\widetilde{\Lambda}.
Hence, (
6.10
) holds for
all

t_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
and
t_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
.

Note that (
6.10
) shows that
\Phi=J_{R}^{*}
.
Due to the convexity of
J_{R}
and Legendre duality (see, e.g.,
[
8
, Lemma 4.5.8]
), we have that
J_{R}=\Phi^{*}
. Hence, applying the contraction principle to the LDP for
\{R^{(n)}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, we find that the annealed rate function
\mathcal{J}^{\sf{an}}
of Theorem
2.6
can be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{\sf{an}}(x)

\displaystyle=\inf_{\tau_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{k},\tau_{2}\in\mathbb{R}:\tau_{1}\rho(\tau_{2})=x}\Phi^{*}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})

\displaystyle=\inf_{\tau_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{k},\tau_{2}\in\mathbb{R}:\tau_{1}\rho(\tau_{2})=x}\inf_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{k})}\{\Psi_{\nu}^{*}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})+\mathbb{H}_{k}(\nu)\}

\displaystyle=\inf_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{k})}\inf_{\tau_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{k},\tau_{2}\in\mathbb{R}:\tau_{1}\rho(\tau_{2})=x}\{\Psi_{\nu}^{*}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})+\mathbb{H}_{k}(\nu)\}

\displaystyle=\inf_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{k})}\{\mathcal{J}_{\nu}^{\sf{qu}}(x)+\mathbb{H}_{k}(\nu)\},

where the second equality invokes (
6.5
) of Lemma
6.3
, and the last equality relies on (
2.7
).
This completes the proof of Theorem
2.7
.
∎
