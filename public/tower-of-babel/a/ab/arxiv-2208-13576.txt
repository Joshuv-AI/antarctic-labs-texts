A note on the Jacobian problem of Coifman, Lions, Meyer and Semmes Thanks: The author was supported by the ERC Advanced Grant 834728.
Sauli Lindberg

Abstract
. Coifman, Lions, Meyer and Semmes asked in 1993 whether the Jacobian operator and other compensated compactness quantities map their natural domain of definition onto the real-variable Hardy space ℋ 1 ​ ( ℝ n ) \mathcal{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}) . We present an axiomatic, Banach space geometric approach to the problem in the case of quadratic operators. We also make progress on the main open case, the Jacobian equation in the plane.



1.
Introduction

The real-variable Hardy space
\mathscr{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
often acts as a good substitute for
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, mirroring many of the ways in which
\textup{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
substitutes
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

[
15
,
47
]
. In order to define
\mathscr{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
we fix
\chi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\chi(x)\,dx\neq 0
, denote
\chi_{t}(x)\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}t^{-n}\chi(x/t)
for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
t>0
and set

\mathcal{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}\left\{f\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\colon\sup_{t>0}\left\lvert f*\chi_{t}(\cdot)\right\rvert\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\right\}.

We endow
\mathscr{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with the norm
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\mathscr{H}^{1}}\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}\left\lVert\sup_{t>0}\left\lvert f*\chi_{t}(\cdot)\right\rvert\right\rVert_{L^{1}}
.

Connections between
\mathscr{H}^{1}
integrability, commutators and weak sequential continuity were explored by Coifman & al. in the highly influential work
[
3
]
. Coifman & al. showed that when
n\geq 2
, the Jacobian determinants of mappings in
\dot{W}^{1,n}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n})
and several other compensated compactness quantities belong to
\mathscr{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. The result was motivated by Müller’s higher integrability result on Jacobians: if
u\in W^{1,n}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
J_{u}\geq 0
a.e. in an open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then
Ju\log(2+Ju)\in L^{1}_{loc}(\Omega)

[
43
]
, in direct analogy to Stein’s classical
L\textup{log}L
result on
\mathscr{H}^{1}
functions. For later developments of the
\mathcal{H}^{1}
theory of compensated compactness quantities see e.g.
[
4
,
14
,
16
,
19
,
20
,
29
,
35
,
36
,
39
,
41
,
45
,
48
,
51
]
.

Coifman & al. proceeded to ask whether these nonlinear quantities are surjections onto
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

[
3
, p. 258]
. The most famous open case is the following:

(1.1)

\textup{Is }J\colon\dot{W}^{1,n}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n})\to\mathscr{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\textup{ surjective?}

As a partial result, Coifman & al. showed that
\mathscr{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is the smallest Banach space that contains the range of the Jacobian. More precisely,

(1.2)

\mathscr{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}Ju_{j}\colon\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left\lVert Du_{j}\right\rVert_{L^{n}}^{n}<\infty\right\}

[
3
, Theorem III.2]
. Further partial results were presented in
[
16
,
18
,
38
]
. When the domain of definition of
J
is the
inhomogeneous
Sobolev space
W^{1,n}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n})
, the author proved non-surjectivity in
[
39
]
.

In bounded domains, the Dirichlet problem
Ju=f
in
\Omega
,
u=\textup{id}
on
\partial\Omega
has a classical theory starting from the seminal works of Moser and Dacorogna in
[
8
,
42
]
and reviewed in
[
7
]
. In a setting close to ours, when
u\in W^{1,n}_{\textup{id}}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{n})
has Jacobian
Ju\geq 0
a.e., Müller’s higher integrability result implies that
Ju\in L\log L(\Omega)
. As an analogue of (
1.1
), Hogan & al. asked whether every non-negative
f\in L\log L(\Omega)
with mean 1 has a solution
u\in W^{1,n}_{\textup{id}}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{n})

[
23
, p. 206]
. Counter-examples were recently given by Guerra & al. in
[
17
]
. For all
L^{q/n}
data,
1<q<n
, ”pointwise” (as opposed to distributional) solutions in
W^{1,q}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{n})
with arbitrary boundary values in
W^{1-1/q,q}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{n})
were constructed in
[
33
]
. At any rate, compared to the Dirichlet problem, different ideas are needed in the case of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
due to the unboundedness of the domain and the absence of boundary conditions.

In
[
28
]
, Iwaniec conjectured that for every
n\geq 2
and
p\in[1,\infty)
the Jacobian operator
J\colon\dot{W}^{1,np}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n})\to\mathcal{H}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
not only is surjective but also has a continuous right inverse
G\colon\mathscr{H}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to\dot{W}^{1,np}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n})
; recall that
\mathscr{H}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
whenever
1<p<\infty
. Hytönen proved in
[
27
]
the natural analogue of (
1.2
), so that
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is, again, the minimal Banach space containing
J(\dot{W}^{1,np}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n}))
. We discuss Hytönen’s contribution in §
7
.

We next briefly summarise some of the ideas presented in
[
28
]
. Whenever
f\in\mathcal{H}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and the equation
Jv=f
has a solution, it has a
minimum norm solution

u\in\dot{W}^{1,np}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n})
, that is,
Ju=f
and
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\lvert Du\right\rvert^{np}=\min_{Jv=f}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\lvert Dv\right\rvert^{np}
. Furthermore, the range of
J
is dense in
\mathcal{H}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Iwaniec suggested a possible way of finding a continuous right inverse
G
:

Strategy 1.1
.

When
n\geq 2
and
1\leq p<\infty
, the following claims would yield a continuous right inverse of
J\colon\dot{W}^{1,np}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n})\to\mathscr{H}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
:

(1)

Every energy-minimal solution
u
satisfies
\left\lVert Du\right\rVert_{L^{np}}^{n}\lesssim\left\lVert Ju\right\rVert_{\mathscr{H}^{p}}
.

(2)

For every
f\in\mathscr{H}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
there is a unique energy-minimal solution
u_{f}
, modulo rotations.

(3)

There exist rotations
R_{f}\in SO(n)
such that
f\mapsto R_{f}u_{f}
is a continuous right inverse of
J
.

Claim (
1
) would easily imply the surjectivity of
J\colon\dot{W}^{1,np}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n})\to\mathscr{H}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
(see
[
38
, p. 36]
). In
[
16
]
, (
1
) was shown to be, in fact,
equivalent
to the surjectivity of
J\colon\dot{W}^{1,np}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n})\to\mathscr{H}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
; this amounts to an open mapping theorem for the Jacobian. In
[
18
]
, in turn, (
2
) was shown to be false whenever
n=2
and
1\leq p<\infty
. Nevertheless, in
[
18
]
, Guerra & al. found an explicit class of data whose energy-minimal solution is, indeed, unique up to rotations. Another large class of such data is constructed in Theorem
1.10
below. Despite the falsity of (
2
), claim (
1
) and Iwaniec’s conjecture itself remain open. In the case
n=2
,
p=1
, a novel Banach space geometric approach was presented in
[
38
]
to attack claim (
1
).

In this work, we present a natural abstract framework for the ideas of
[
38
]
and study the surjectivity question for rather general quadratic compensated compactness quantities, streamlining the exposition of
[
38
]
considerably. We also make further progress on the main special case
J\colon\dot{W}^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{R}^{2})\to\mathcal{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. In §
7
, we discuss how to adapt the methods to the case
n=2
,
1<p<\infty
.

1.1.
Connection to commutators

Question (
1.1
) can be seen as a variant of the classical
factorisation problem
from complex analysis
[
46
, §4.2]
. Indeed, an equivalent formulation of (
1.1
) in terms of differential forms is whether
\mathscr{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=*\wedge_{j=1}^{n}\textup{d}\dot{W}^{1,n}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
(since
Ju=*\textup{d}u_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\textup{d}u_{n}
). The decomposition (
1.2
) is called a
weak factorisation
of
\mathscr{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. In the plane, one can also deduce (
1.2
) from the two-sided commutator estimate

(1.3)

c\left\lVert b\right\rVert_{\operatorname{BMO}}\leq\left\lVert[b,T]\right\rVert_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}\leq C\left\lVert b\right\rVert_{\operatorname{BMO}}

for the Beurling transform
T={\mathcal{S}}\colon L^{2}(\mathbb{C},\mathbb{C})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{C},\mathbb{C})
and
b\in\textup{BMO}(\mathbb{C})

[
38
]
.

Estimates of the form (
1.3
) go back to the seminal work
[
5
]
of Coifman & al., with Nehari’s theorem on Hankel operators as a precursor. When
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
and
T=R_{j}
is a Riesz transform, the
upper bound estimate

\left\lVert[b,R_{j}]\right\rVert_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}\leq C\left\lVert b\right\rVert_{\text{BMO}}

[
5
]
, the formula

(1.4)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}b(\omega R_{j}\gamma+\gamma R_{j}\omega)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\omega[b,R_{j}]\gamma

and
\mathcal{H}^{1}-\operatorname{BMO}
duality imply that
\omega R_{j}\gamma+\gamma R_{j}\omega\in\mathscr{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for all
\omega,\gamma\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. The
lower bound estimate

\left\lVert[b,R_{j}]\right\rVert_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}\geq c\left\lVert b\right\rVert_{\text{BMO}}

[
5
,
30
,
49
]
gives the weak factorisation
\mathscr{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\{\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(\omega_{i}R_{j}\gamma_{i}+\gamma_{i}R_{j}\omega_{i})\colon\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(\left\lVert\omega_{i}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\left\lVert\gamma_{i}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2})<\infty\}
by simple functional analysis. By a result of Uchiyama, the commutator
[b,R_{j}]
is compact if and only if
b\in\textup{CMO}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Note that (
1.4
) and the compactness of
[b,R_{j}]
immediately lead to the weak-to-weak
∗
sequential continuity of the quadratic operator
(\omega,\gamma)\mapsto\omega R_{j}\gamma+\gamma R_{j}\omega\colon L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{2}\to\mathscr{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. These results have been extended in numerous ways, as reviewed in
[
50
]
. In §
3.1
we briefly discuss the case of commutators with Calderón-Zygmund operators.

In Assumptions
1.2
–
1.3
below, we axiomatise the above-mentioned properties of the Riesz transform
R_{j}
and the quantity
\omega R_{j}\gamma+\gamma R_{j}\omega
. We study the factorisation problem for quadratic weakly continuous quantities in real-variable function spaces such as
\mathscr{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, using isometric Banach space geometry as the main tool. Since our choice of methodology is rather unconventional in the study of nonlinear PDE’s, we motivate it at length in §
1.3
. First, however, we specify the mathematical setting of this paper.

1.2.
The mathematical setting

We fix a real Banach space
X
with a separable dual
X^{*}
and a real or complex Hilbert space
H
, and we denote the coefficient field of
H
by
\mathbb{K}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}\}
. The canonical examples are
X=\operatorname{CMO}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
X^{*}=\mathcal{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
X^{**}=\operatorname{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
H=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{m})
or
H=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{C})
, where
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
.

Assumption 1.2
.

A bilinear mapping

(b,\omega)\mapsto T_{b}\omega\colon X^{**}\times H\to H

satisfies the following conditions:

(i)

c\left\lVert b\right\rVert_{X^{**}}\leq\left\lVert T_{b}\right\rVert_{H\to H}\leq C\|b\|_{X^{**}}
for every
b\in X^{**}
,

(ii)

T_{b}
is compact for every
b\in X
.

Assumption 1.3
.

The bilinear mapping
(b,\omega)\mapsto T_{b}\omega
satisfies the following conditions for every
b\in X^{**}
:

(i)

T_{b}
is self-adjoint.

(ii)

\left\lVert T_{b}\right\rVert_{H\to H}=\sup_{\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{H}=1}\langle T_{b}f,f\rangle
.

Definition 1.4
.

Given
X
,
H
and
(b,\omega)\mapsto T_{b}f
we define a norm-to-norm and weak-to-weak
∗
sequentially continuous mapping
Q\colon H\to X^{*}
by

\langle b,Q\omega\rangle_{X-X^{*}}\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}\langle T_{b}\omega,\omega\rangle_{H}.

Henceforth, Assumptions
1.2
–
1.3
will remain in place for the rest of the introduction. Assumption
1.3
is made mainly to make the quadratic operator
Q
real-valued and ensure that
X^{*}=\{\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}Q\omega_{j}\colon\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left\lVert\omega_{j}\right\rVert_{H}^{2}<\infty\}
. Whenever the map
(b,\omega)\mapsto T_{b}\omega
satisfies Assumption
1.2
, the modified operator
(b,(\omega,\gamma))\mapsto\tilde{T}_{b}(\omega,\gamma)\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}(T_{b}^{*}\gamma,T_{b}\omega)\colon X^{**}\times(H\times H)\to H\times H
satisfies Assumptions
1.2
–
1.3
(see Proposition
2.10
). Examples
3.5
–
3.6
illustrate the role of Assumption
1.3
further.

The planar Jacobian arises as follows (see Example
3.5
): defining
T_{b}\colon L^{2}(\mathbb{C},\mathbb{C})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{C},\mathbb{C})
by
T_{b}\omega\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}\overline{({\mathcal{S}}b-b{\mathcal{S}})\overline{{\mathcal{S}}\omega}}
, where
{\mathcal{S}}
is the Beurling transform, we can write
Q\omega=\left\lvert{\mathcal{S}}\omega\right\rvert^{2}-\left\lvert\omega\right\rvert^{2}=\left\lvert u_{z}\right\rvert^{2}-\left\lvert u_{\bar{z}}\right\rvert^{2}=Ju
, where
u\in\dot{W}^{1,2}(\mathbb{C},\mathbb{C})
is the Cauchy transform of
\omega\in L^{2}(\mathbb{C},\mathbb{C})
.

Assumptions
1.2
–
1.3
are not enough to determine whether
Q(H)=X^{*}
. Indeed,
Q\colon L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathscr{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
,
Q(\omega)=\omega^{2}-(H\omega)^{2}
is non-surjective but (its Gâteaux derivative)
\tilde{Q}\colon L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\times L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathscr{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
,
\tilde{Q}(\omega,\gamma)=\omega\gamma-H\omega H\gamma
is surjective (see Example
3.6
). We address the following question:

Question 1.5
.

Under which extra assumptions is
Q(H)=X^{*}
?

Below we mostly study all the operators given by Definition
1.4
in a unified manner, as precise information on
Q(H)
is valuable whether one intends to prove surjectivity or non-surjectivity. However, in Assumption
1.8
we specify a useful criterion which
holds
for the operators
\omega\mapsto\omega^{2}-(H\omega)^{2}\colon L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathscr{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and
\omega\mapsto\left\lvert{\mathcal{S}}\omega\right\rvert^{2}-\left\lvert\omega\right\rvert^{2}\colon L^{2}(\mathbb{C},\mathbb{C})\to\mathscr{H}^{1}(\mathbb{C})
but
fails
for their Gâteaux derivatives.

1.3.
Overall strategy and aim

We next describe the motivation behind the approach adopted in
[
38
]
and this paper. Elementary proofs of various statements are given in §
4
.

\bullet
Whenever
f\in X^{*}
and the equation
Q\gamma=f
has a solution, it has a
minimum norm solution

\omega\in H
, that is,
Q\omega=f
and
\left\lVert\omega\right\rVert_{H}=\min_{Q\gamma=f}\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{H}
.

\bullet
We define an
energy functional

\mathcal{E}\colon X^{*}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
by

\mathcal{E}(f)\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}\begin{cases}\min\{\left\lVert\omega\right\rVert_{H}^{2}\colon Q\omega=f\},&f\in Q(H),\\
\infty,&f\notin Q(H).\end{cases}

Almost by definition,
\mathcal{E}\geq\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{X^{*}}
. Claim (
1
) in Strategy
1.1
can be stated equivalently as the estimate
\mathcal{E}\lesssim\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{X^{*}}
.

\bullet
By a nonlinear version of the Banach-Schauder open mapping theorem, recently proved by Guerra & al. in
[
16
]
, for translation-invariant operators (such as the Jacobian) we have

\textup{either }\mathcal{E}\lesssim\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{X^{*}}\textup{ in }X^{*}\textup{ or }\mathcal{E}=\infty\textup{ outside a meagre set.}

In particular, the surjectivity of
Q\colon H\to X^{*}
is
equivalent
to the statement that
Q(H)
is dense in
X^{*}
and every minimum norm solution satisfies
\left\lVert\omega\right\rVert_{H}^{2}\lesssim\left\lVert Q\omega\right\rVert_{X^{*}}
. This gives a metamatematical justification for taking claim (
1
) as a goal.

\bullet
Given a minimum norm solution
\omega\in H
of
Q\omega=f
, we may use calculus of variations to study
\omega
via perturbed solutions
\omega_{\epsilon}\in H
of
Q\omega_{\epsilon}=f
,
\left\lVert\omega_{\epsilon}-\omega\right\rVert_{H}\to 0
. However, it tends to be very hard to construct variations that satisfy the nonlinear constraint
Q\omega_{\epsilon}=f
. In particular, the first variation
\omega_{\epsilon}=\omega+\epsilon\varphi
,
\varphi\in H
, is in general unavailable.

\bullet
The constraint
Q\omega_{\epsilon}=f
could be relaxed if we found a
Lagrange multiplier
, that is,
b\in X^{**}
satisfying

(1.5)

\left.\frac{d}{d\epsilon}(\langle b,Q(f+\epsilon\varphi)\rangle_{X^{**}-X^{*}}-\left\lVert f+\epsilon\varphi\right\rVert_{H}^{2})\right|_{\epsilon=0}=0\qquad\text{for every }\varphi\in H.

If, furthermore,
\|b\|_{X^{**}}\leq C
uniformly in
f
, then setting
\varphi=f
in (
1.5
) would give the sought inequality
\mathcal{E}\lesssim\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{X^{*}}
. However, given an arbitrary minimum norm solution, the construction of a Lagrange multiplier
b\in X^{**}
is a formidable task–in particular, the standard Liusternik-Shnirelman method is not applicable.

\bullet
Nevertheless, many minimum norm solutions do possess a Lagrange multiplier. Indeed, (
1.5
) says that
\omega
is a critical point of the functional
I_{b}\colon H\to\mathbb{R}
,

I_{b}(\theta)\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}\langle b,Q\theta\rangle_{X^{**}-X^{*}}-\left\lVert\theta\right\rVert_{H}^{2}=\langle T_{b}\theta,\theta\rangle_{H}-\left\lVert\theta\right\rVert_{H}^{2}.

Now, if
b\in X
and
\left\lVert T_{b}\right\rVert_{H\to H}=1
, then
\sup_{\left\lVert\theta\right\rVert_{H}=1}I_{b}(\theta)=0
is attained at some
\omega\in\mathbb{S}_{H}
, so that
b
is a Lagrange multiplier of
\omega
and
\left\lVert\omega\right\rVert_{H}^{2}\lesssim\left\lVert Q\omega\right\rVert_{X^{*}}
.

\bullet
As every
b\in X
with
\left\lVert T_{b}\right\rVert_{H\to H}=1
is a Lagrange multiplier, we use the norm of
X
given by

\left\lVert b\right\rVert_{X_{Q}}\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}\sup_{\left\lVert\omega\right\rVert_{H}=1}\langle b,Q\omega\rangle_{X-X^{*}}=\left\lVert T_{b}\right\rVert_{H\to H},

endow
X^{*}
with the dual norm

\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{X^{*}_{Q}}\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}\sup_{\left\lVert b\right\rVert_{X_{Q}}=1}\langle f,b\rangle_{X^{*}-X}

and denote the resulting Banach spaces by
X_{Q}
and
X^{*}_{Q}
. Thus every
b\in\mathbb{S}_{X_{Q}}
is a Lagrange multiplier of some
\omega\in\mathbb{S}_{H}
with
Q\omega\in\mathbb{S}_{X_{Q}^{*}}
.

\bullet

As a consequence, the set

\mathscr{A}\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}\{\omega\in\mathbb{S}_{H}\colon Q\omega\in\mathbb{S}_{X^{*}_{Q}}\}\subset\mathbb{S}_{H}

and its image
Q(\mathscr{A})\subset\mathbb{S}_{X^{*}_{Q}}
are rather large. The motivation above leads to the following more precise variant of Question
1.5
, presented for the planar Jacobian in
[
38
]
:

Question 1.6
.

Is
Q(\mathscr{A})=\mathbb{S}_{X^{*}_{Q}}
? Equivalently, is
\mathcal{E}=\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{X^{*}}
?

As already noted, there exist some natural cases where the answer to Question
1.6
is negative. A positive answer holds for the simple operator
(b,(\omega,\gamma))\mapsto(b\omega,-b\gamma)\colon\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
. It is hoped that suitable further properties of
T
, beyond Assumptions
1.2
–
1.3
, will yield an answer to Question
1.6
for natural classes of operators. In this paper we present several partial results.

1.4.
Main results

As a first largeness criterion on
Q(\mathscr{A})
we mention that

(1.6)

Q(\mathscr{A})\supset\operatorname{ext}(\mathbb{B}_{X^{*}_{Q}}),

where
\operatorname{ext}(\mathbb{B}_{X^{*}_{Q}})
is the set of extreme points of
\mathbb{B}_{X_{Q}^{*}}
. Since
X^{*}_{Q}
is a separable dual space, the Bessaga-Pelczynski Theorem (Theorem
2.7
) implies that
\overline{\operatorname{co}}(\operatorname{ext}(\mathbb{B}_{X^{*}_{Q}}))=\mathbb{B}_{X^{*}_{Q}}
. The inclusion (
1.6
) is contained in Theorem
5.1
. We will find more refined information on
Q(\mathscr{A})
by using the set-valued duality mapping.

Definition 1.7
.

The
duality mapping

D\colon\mathbb{S}_{X_{Q}}\to 2^{\mathbb{S}_{X^{*}_{Q}}}
is defined by

D(b)\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}\{h\in\mathbb{S}_{X^{*}_{Q}}\colon\langle b,h\rangle_{X_{Q}-X_{Q}^{*}}=1\}.

We denote the set of
norm-attaining points
by
\textit{NA}_{\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{X_{Q}}}\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}\cup_{b\in\mathbb{S}_{X_{Q}}}D(b)
.

By the Bishop-Phelps Theorem
[
12
, Theorem 3.54]
,
NA_{\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{X_{Q}}}
is dense in
\mathbb{S}_{X^{*}_{Q}}
. Since
Q(\mathscr{A})
is closed, Question
1.6
reduces to the question whether
D(b)\cap Q\mathscr{A}=D(b)
for every
b\in\mathbb{S}_{X_{Q}}
. Before presenting partial results we formulate a useful extra assumption which is satisfied by the planar Jacobian. Its aim is to quantify the symmetries of the class
\mathscr{A}
(see Remark
5.6
).

Assumption 1.8
.

If
\omega,\gamma\in\mathscr{A}
satisfy
Q\omega=Q\gamma
, then
Q^{\prime}_{\omega}(c\gamma)\neq 0
for some

.

The following result collects partial results on Question

; for relevant definitions see §

. The parts (iv)–(vi) are new also in the case of the Jacobian.

Theorem 1.9
.

Under Assumptions
1.2
–
1.3
, the following statements hold:

(i)

For every
b\in\mathbb{S}_{X_{Q}}
, the convex set
D(b)
has finite affine dimension.

(ii)

For every

(iii)

Under the further Assumption

(iv)

For every

for some

and

(v)

(vi)

The norm and relative weak

The proof is presented in §

Theorem 1.10
.

Suppose Assumptions
1.2
–
1.3
and

In view of (

.

In the case of

Preliminaries

The main tools of this work come from isometric Banach space geometry. We collect some notions and results and refer to
[
11
]
and
[

]
for most of the proofs.

2.1.
Smoothness properties of norms and duality mappings

In this subsection,
X
is a real Banach space. Suppose
U\subset Z
is open and
g\colon U\to Z
is convex, where
Z
is a real Banach space. We say that
g
is
Gâteaux differentiable
at
x\in U
if there exists
L\in\mathscr{B}(X,Z)
such that

(2.1)

\lim_{t\to 0}\left\lVert\frac{g(x+th)-g(x)}{t}-Lh\right\rVert=0\quad\textup{for every }h\in X.

The operator
L
is then denoted by
g^{\prime}_{x}
and called the
Gâteaux derivative
of
g
at
x
. If the limit in (
2.1
) is uniform in
h\in\mathbb{S}_{X}
, then
g
is said to be
Fréchet differentiable
at
x
.

Note that the convex function
g(x)=\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{X}
is Gâteaux (Fréchet) differentiable at
x\in\mathbb{S}_{X}
if and only if it is Gâteaux (Fréchet) differentiable at
\lambda x
for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
. We will use the following theorem of Asplund and Lindenstrauss (see
[
12
, Theorem 8.21]
) on the norm of
X
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Suppose
X^{*}
is separable and
g\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
is a continuous convex function. Then
g
is Fréchet differentiable in a dense
G_{\delta}
subset of
X
.

Recall that the duality mapping
D\colon\mathbb{S}_{X}\to 2^{\mathbb{S}_{X^{*}}}
is defined by

D(x)\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}\{x^{*}\in\mathbb{S}_{X^{*}}\colon\langle x^{*},x\rangle_{X^{*}-X}=1\}.

If
g(x)=\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{X}
is Gâteaux differentiable at
x\in\mathbb{S}_{X}
, then
D(x)=\{g^{\prime}_{x}\}
.

Definition 2.2
.

Suppose
Y
and
Z
are Hausdorff topological spaces. A mapping
F\colon Y\to 2^{Z}\setminus\{\emptyset\}
is said to be
upper semicontinuous
at
y\in Y
if for any open set
V\supset F(y)
, there exists a neighbourhood
U
of
y
in
Y
such that
F(U)\subset V
.

We recall characterisations of the norm-to-norm upper semicontinuity of the duality mapping
[
13
,
24
]
.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
x\in\mathbb{S}_{X}
. The following statements are equivalent.

(i)

D
is norm-to-norm upper semicontinuous at
x
and
D(x)
is norm compact.

(ii)

For every sequence of points
x_{j}^{*}
in
\mathbb{S}_{X^{*}}
such that
\langle x_{j}^{*},x\rangle\to 1
, there exists a subsequence convergent to some
x^{*}\in D(x)
.

(iii)

The weak
∗
and norm topologies agree on
\mathbb{S}_{X^{*}}
at points of
D(x)
.

The norm-to-norm upper semicontinuity of
D
can also be used to characterise Fréchet differentiability of the norm (see
[
11
, Corollary 7.16]
).

Theorem 2.4
.

The norm
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{X}
is Fréchet differentiable at
x\in\mathbb{S}_{X}
if and only if
D(x)
is a singleton and
D
is norm-to-norm upper semicontinuous at
x
.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
Y
and
Z
be Hausdorff topological spaces. A set-valued map
F\colon Y\to 2^{Z}\setminus\{\emptyset\}
is said to be
cusco
(convex upper semicontinuous nonempty compact-valued) if it is norm-to-norm upper semicontinuous and
F(y)
is compact for every
y\in Y
.

2.2.
Extreme points of the unit ball of a separable dual

When
C
is a convex subset of a real Banach space
Y
, a point
y\in C
is an
extreme point
of
C
if there exists no proper line segment that contains
y
and lies in
C
.

Definition 2.6
.

A Banach space is said to have the
Krein-Milman property
if every closed, bounded, convex set is the closed convex hull of its extreme points.

We recall a result of C. Bessaga and A. Pełczynski (see e.g.
[
9
, p. 198]
).

Theorem 2.7
.

Every separable dual space has the Krein-Milman property.

Recall that in Assumptions
1.2
–
1.3
, the dual space
X^{*}
is separable. The real-variable Hardy space
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=(\operatorname{CMO}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))^{*}
satisfies this criterion (see
[
38
, Proposition 2.15]
). We also recall Milman’s theorem on extreme points which is formulated as follows (see
[
11
, Theorem 3.66]
).

Theorem 2.8
.

Let
K
be a non-empty subset of a Hausdorff locally convex space such that
\overline{\operatorname{co}}(K)
is compact. Then every extreme point of
\overline{\operatorname{co}}(K)
lies in
\overline{K}
.

2.3.
Self-adjoint variants of non-self-adjoint operators

When Assumption
1.2
holds but Assumption
1.3
does not, we use a natural self-adjoint modification of
T_{b}
. Its existence and uniqueness are guaranteed by the following simple lemma.

Lemma 2.9
.

Suppose
A\colon H\to H
is
\mathbb{K}
-linear. Then there exists a unique self-adjoint
\mathbb{K}
-linear operator
B\colon H\times H\to H\times H
such that

\langle B(x,y),(x,y)\rangle_{H\times H}=2\mathbf{Re}\langle Ax,y\rangle_{H}\quad\text{for all }x,y\in H.

The operator
B
is of the form
B(x,y)=(A^{*}y,Ax)
and satisfies
\left\lVert B\right\rVert_{H\times H\to H\times H}=\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{H\to H}
.

Proposition 2.10
.

If a bilinear mapping
T\colon X^{**}\times H\to H
satisfies Assumption
1.2
, then the modified operator

(b,f,g)\mapsto\widetilde{T_{b}}(f,g)\colon X^{**}\times(H\times H)\to H\times H,\qquad\widetilde{T_{b}}(f,g)\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}(T_{b}^{*}g,T_{b}f)

satisfies Assumptions
1.2
–
1.3
.

If
T
satisfies Assumptions
1.2
–
1.3
, then
\tilde{T}
is related to the Gâteaux derivative of
Q
by

(2.2)

\langle\tilde{T}_{b}(f,g),(f,g)\rangle_{H\times H}=\langle b,Q^{\prime}_{f}g\rangle_{X^{**}-X^{*}}\qquad\text{for all }f,g\in H,\,b\in X^{**}.

We illustrate Proposition
2.10
in Examples
3.5
–
3.6
.

3.
Operators satisfying Assumptions
1.2
–
1.3

In this section we discuss two classes of operators which satisfy assumption
1.2
: commutators of Calderón-Zygmund operators with
\operatorname{BMO}
functions and paracommutators. Specific examples are then given in §
3.3
.

3.1.
Commutators of Calderón–Zygmund operators and
\operatorname{BMO}
functions

Recall that

\textup{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}\left\{b\in L^{1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\colon\left\lVert b\right\rVert_{\operatorname{BMO}}\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}\sup_{Q}-\penalty\hskip-10.8pt\penalty\int_{Q}\left\lvert b(x)-b_{Q}\right\rvert\,dx<\infty\right\},

where the supremum is taken over cubes
Q\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
b_{Q}\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}-\penalty\hskip-8.9pt\penalty\int_{Q}b(y)\,dy
. The closure of
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
in
\textup{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is called
\operatorname{CMO}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. By classical results by Fefferman and by Coifman and Weiss, respectively, we have the dualities
[\mathcal{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})]^{*}=\textup{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
[\operatorname{CMO}(\mathbb{R}^{n})]^{*}=\mathscr{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Coifman & al. showed in
[
5
]
that when
T
is a Calderón-Zygmund operator with a suitable smooth kernel
\Omega
, we have
\left\lVert[b,T]\right\rVert_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}\lesssim\left\lVert b\right\rVert_{\operatorname{BMO}}
for all
b\in\textup{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Specifically,
\Omega
was assumed to be homogeneous of degree zero with vanishing mean over
S^{n-1}
and to satisfy
\left\lvert\Omega(x)-\Omega(y)\right\rvert<\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert
for all
x,y\in S^{n-1}
. They also showed that if
[b,R_{j}]\colon L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is bounded for every
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, then
b\in\operatorname{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\left\lVert b\right\rVert_{\operatorname{BMO}}\lesssim\max_{1\leq j\leq n}\left\lVert[b,R_{j}]\right\rVert_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}
. Uchiyama
[
49
]
and Janson
[
30
]
showed independently that in order to obtain
b\in\textup{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, it suffices to show boundedness of
[b,T]
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for only one of the kernels
\Omega\not\equiv 0
in the result of
[
5
]
. Uchiyama also showed that
[b,T]
is compact if and only is
b\in\operatorname{CMO}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Thus
T_{b}f\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}[b,T]f
satisfies Assumption
1.2
.

The two-sided estimate (
1.3
) has been extended in numerous ways (to multi-parameter and weighted spaces etc.); see e.g.
[
22
,
27
,
50
]
and the references contained therein. In the case of commutators with Calderón-Zygmund operators, Hytönen recently proved the estimate
\left\lVert[b,T]\right\rVert_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}\simeq\left\lVert b\right\rVert_{\operatorname{BMO}}
under the very weak assumption that the kernel is ”non-degenerate”. We recall relevant definitions from
[
27
]
.

Definition 3.1
.

A measurable function
K\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is called an
\omega
-Calderón-Zygmund kernel
if

K(x,y)\leq c_{K}\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert^{-n}
whenever
x\neq y
and

\left\lvert K(x,y)-K(x^{\prime},y)\right\rvert+\left\lvert K(y,x)-K(y,x^{\prime})\right\rvert\leq\frac{1}{\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert^{n}}\omega\left(\frac{\left\lvert x-x^{\prime}\right\rvert}{\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert}\right)

whenever
\left\lvert x-x^{\prime}\right\rvert<\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert/2
, where
\omega\colon[0,1)\to[0,\infty)
is increasing.

Definition 3.2
.

A measurable function
K\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is called a
non-degenerate Calderón-Zygmund kernel
if
K
satisfies at least one of the following two conditions:

(1)

K
is an
\omega
-Calderón-Zygmund kernel with
\omega(t)\to 0
as
t\to 0
and for every
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
r>0
, there exists
x\in\complement B(y,r)
such that
\left\lvert K(x,y)\right\rvert\leq cr^{-d}
.

(2)

K
is an homogeneous Calderón-Zygmund kernel with
\Omega\in L^{1}(S^{n-1})\setminus\{0\}
.

For compactness of commutators of
CMO
functions and Calderón-Zygmund operators on (possibly weighted)
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
we refer to
[
1
,
2
,
21
,
26
,
34
,
49
]
. In particular, when an homogeneous kernel
\Omega\in L^{1}(S^{n-1})\setminus\{0\}
has vanishing mean and satisfies

\sup_{\zeta\in S^{n-1}}\int_{S^{n-1}}\left\lvert\Omega(\eta)\right\rvert\left(\textup{ln}\frac{1}{\left\lvert\eta\cdot\zeta\right\rvert}\right)^{\theta}\textup{d}\eta<\infty

for some
\theta>2
, the corresponding commutator satisfies Assumption
1.2

[
1
,
27
]
. Assumption
1.2
is verified for commutators with the Cauchy integral operator in
[
37
]
.

In many natural instances, an operator
T_{b}
satisfying Assumption
1.2
is not precisely a commutator but, for instance, the composition of a commutator with a Calderón-Zygmund operator. A natural general framework for such operators is provided by
paracommutators
.

3.2.
Paracommutators

We briefly recall basic definitions and results from the theory of paracommutators and refer to
[
31
]
. Paracommutators are operators
T_{b}(A)
of the form

(3.1)

\widehat{T_{b}(A)\omega}(\xi)\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}(2\pi)^{-n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\hat{b}(\xi-\eta)A(\xi,\eta)\hat{\omega}(\eta)\,d\eta,\quad\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

where
A\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\omega\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
are functions and
b\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is called the
symbol of
T_{b}(A)
.

As the most basic example,
A(\xi,\eta)\equiv 1
yields (under suitable integrability assumptions) the multiplication operator
T_{b}\omega=b\omega
. The commutators
T_{b}(A)=[b,R_{j}]
arise via
A(\xi,\eta)=\xi_{j}/\left\lvert\xi\right\rvert-\eta_{j}/\left\lvert\eta\right\rvert
. More generally, if
T
is a Calderón-Zygmund singular integral operator with Fourier symbol
m
and
A(\xi,\eta)=m(\xi)-m(\eta)
, then
T_{b}(A)=[b,T]
. Several further examples are presented in
[
31
, pp. 469-473]
and
[
45
, pp. 513-519]
.

In
[
31
]
, Janson and Peetre gave conditions on the function
A
under which the boundedness of
T_{b}(A)\colon L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is equivalent to the condition
b\in\operatorname{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. In order to state the result we need some definitions. We do not motivate them here but refer to
[
31
]
for more information.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
U,V\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The space
M(U\times V)
consists of functions
\varphi\in L^{\infty}(U\times V)
that admit a representation

(3.2)

\varphi(\xi,\eta)=\int_{X}\alpha(\xi,x)\beta(\eta,x)\,d\mu(x)

for some
\sigma
-finite measure space
(X,\mu)
and measurable functions
\alpha\colon U\times X\to\mathbb{R}
and
\beta\colon V\times X\to\mathbb{R}
which satisfy

(3.3)

\int_{X}\left\lVert\alpha(\cdot,x)\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(U)}\left\lVert\beta(\cdot,x)\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(V)}\,d\mu(x)<\infty.

Furthermore,
M(U\times V)
is a Banach algebra in the norm given by minimising the left hand side of (
3.3
) over all representations (
3.2
).

For every
j\in\mathbb{Z}
we denote
\Delta_{j}\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\colon 2^{j}\leq\left\lvert\xi\right\rvert<2^{j+1}\}
. We list some of the assumptions of
[
31
]
and retain their numbering.

(A0) There exists
r>1
such that
A(r\xi,r\eta)=A(\xi,\eta)
for all
\xi,\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

(A1)
\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{M(\Delta_{j}\times\Delta_{k})}\leq C
for all
j,k\in\mathbb{Z}
.

(A2) There exist
A_{1},A_{2}\in M(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\delta>0
such that

\displaystyle A(\xi,\eta)=A_{1}(\xi,\eta)\quad\text{when }\left\lvert\eta\right\rvert<\delta\left\lvert\xi\right\rvert,

\displaystyle A(\xi,\eta)=A_{2}(\xi,\eta)\quad\text{when }\left\lvert\xi\right\rvert<\delta\left\lvert\eta\right\rvert.

(A3(
\alpha
)): There exist
\alpha,\delta>0
such that whenever
r<\delta\left\lvert\xi_{0}\right\rvert
, we have

\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{M(B(\xi_{0},r),B(\xi_{0},r))}\leq C\left(\frac{r}{\left\lvert\xi_{0}\right\rvert}\right)^{\alpha}.

(A5) For every
\xi_{0}\neq 0
there exist
\delta>0
and
\eta_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
1/A(\xi,\eta)\in M(U\times V)
, where
U=\{\xi\colon\left\lvert\xi/\left\lvert\xi\right\rvert-\xi_{0}/\left\lvert\xi_{0}\right\rvert\right\rvert<\delta\text{ and }\left\lvert\xi\right\rvert>\left\lvert\xi_{0}\right\rvert\}
and
V=B(\eta_{0},\delta\left\lvert\xi_{0}\right\rvert))
.

We collect the results of
[
31
]
that are most relevant to this paper.

Theorem 3.4
.

Suppose
A\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies (A0), (A1), (A2), (A3(
\alpha
)) and A(5), where
\alpha>0
. Then
T_{b}(A)
satisfies Assumption
1.2
.

By a result of Peng, under the assumptions (A0), (A1), (A3(
\alpha
)) and (A5), compactness of
T_{b}\colon L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
conversely implies
b\in\textup{CMO}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

[
44
]
. We also mention that under (A0)–(A5), Assumption
1.3
is not satisfied in general.
Indeed, denoting
\tilde{b}(x)\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}b(-x)
and abusing notation,
\langle T_{b}(A(\xi,\eta))\omega,\gamma\rangle=\langle\omega,T_{\tilde{b}}(A(\eta,\xi))\gamma\rangle
for all
\omega,\gamma\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

If the assumptions of Theorem
3.4
and Assumption
1.3
hold, we may use Definition
1.4
to define a quadratic operator
Q\colon L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to\mathscr{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Its form is

(3.4)

Q_{\tilde{A}}(\omega,\gamma)(x)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\hat{\omega}(\xi)\hat{\gamma}(\eta)\tilde{A}(\xi,\eta)e^{-i(\xi+\eta)\cdot x}\,d\xi\,d\eta,

where
\tilde{A}(\eta,-\xi)=A(\xi,\eta)
.
Conversely,
Q_{\tilde{A}}
, defined via (
3.4
), gives rise to a paracommutator
T_{b}(A)
defined by (
3.1
). These and many other relations between
T_{b}(A)
and
Q_{\tilde{A}}
are explored in
[
45
]
.

3.3.
Specific operators

We list some concrete quadratic operators which arise via Assumption
1.2
; numerous further examples are presented in
[
31
,
45
]
.

Example 3.5
.

Let
u\in\dot{W}^{1,2}(\mathbb{C},\mathbb{C})
. When
u_{z}\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}2^{-1}(\partial_{x}-i\partial_{y})(u_{1}+iu_{2})
and
u_{\bar{z}}\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}2^{-1}(\partial_{x}+i\partial_{y})(u_{1}+iu_{2})
are the Wirtinger derivatives of
u
and
\mathcal{S}\colon L^{2}(\mathbb{C},\mathbb{C})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{C},\mathbb{C})
is the Beurling transform, we have
\mathcal{S}u_{\bar{z}}=u_{z}
. Therefore, the Jacobian
Ju=|u_{z}|^{2}-|u_{\bar{z}}|^{2}
corresponds to
Q\omega\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}|{\mathcal{S}}\omega|^{2}-|\omega|^{2}
via the isometric isomorphism
u\mapsto u_{\bar{z}}\mathrel{=\mkern-0.25mu:}\omega\colon\dot{W}^{1,2}(\mathbb{C},\mathbb{C})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{C},\mathbb{C})
whose inverse is the Cauchy transform
\mathcal{C}
. (We identify elements of
\dot{W}^{1,2}(\mathbb{C},\mathbb{C})
that differ by a constant and set
\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{\dot{W}^{1,2}}\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}\left\lVert u_{\bar{z}}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}=\left\lVert Du\right\rVert_{L^{2}}/2
.) The quadratic operator
Q
arises via the formula
\langle b,Q\omega\rangle_{\operatorname{BMO}-\mathcal{H}^{1}}=\langle T_{b}\omega,\omega\rangle_{L^{2}}
, where
T_{b}\omega\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}\overline{(Sb-bS)\overline{S\omega}}
satisfies Assumption
1.2
for
H=L^{2}(\mathbb{C},\mathbb{C})
and
X=\textup{CMO}(\mathbb{C})
(see
[
38
]
).

It is instructive to also consider the planar Jacobian in real variable notation. Define

(3.5)

T_{b}\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}R_{1}[b,R_{2}]-R_{2}[b,R_{1}]\colon L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}).

Now
T_{b}^{*}=-T_{b}\neq 0
and thus
T_{b}
is not self-adjoint. However, the operator
\widetilde{T_{b}}\colon L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{R}^{2})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{R}^{2})
, defined in Proposition
2.10

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\widetilde{T_{b}}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})\cdot(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})

Example 3.6
.

On the real line, two quadratic
\mathcal{H}^{1}
-integrable quantities arise as follows. Isomorphically,
\mathcal{H}^{p}(\mathbb{R})\cong\mathcal{H}^{p}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C})\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}\{g+iHg\colon g,Hg\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R})\}

As is well-known,

(Q_{1})_{\omega}^{\prime}\gamma=\omega\gamma-H\omega H\gamma

(3.6)

\{\alpha^{2}\colon\alpha\in\mathcal{H}^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C})\}\subsetneq\{\alpha\beta\colon\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{H}^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C})\}=\mathcal{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}).

We give a proof of (

) in Appendix
A
for the reader’s convenience.

The operator
Q_{1}
arises via the bounded linear operator
T_{b}\colon L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
,
T_{b}\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}[H,b]H
by the formula

–
1.3
.

Example 3.7
.

In

\Omega=\omega+\sum_{j=1}^{n}R_{j}\omega\mathbf{e}_{j}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{C}_{(n)})

Each component of

\mathcal{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\{\omega\gamma-\sum_{j=1}^{n}R_{j}\omega R_{j}\gamma\colon\omega,\gamma\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\}

[

Wu also studied more general combinations of Riesz transforms that arise via

The planar Monge-Ampère equation arises as follows. Define

4.1.
Minimum norm solutions

We begin by noting that if the equation
Q\omega=f
has a solution, then the direct method gives a minimum norm solution.

Proposition 4.1
.

Suppose
f\in X^{*}_{Q}
,
\gamma\in H
and
Q\gamma=f
. Then there exists
\omega\in H
with
Q\omega=f
and
\left\lVert\omega\right\rVert_{H}=\min_{Q\gamma=f}\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{H}
.

Proof.

Choose a minimising sequence so that
Q\omega_{j}=f
for every
j\in\mathbb{N}
and
\lim_{j\to\infty}\left\lVert\omega_{j}\right\rVert_{H}=\inf_{Q\gamma=f}\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{H}
. Since
\mathbb{B}_{H}
is sequentially weakly compact and
Q
is weak-to-weak
∗
continuous, we have
\omega_{j}\rightharpoonup\omega
and
Q\omega_{j}\overset{*}{\rightharpoonup}Q\omega
for a subsequence, and
\omega
is the sought minimum norm solution.
∎

We then show that surjectivity of
Q\colon H\to X^{*}_{Q}
would follow from the weak
∗
density of the range
Q(H)
in
X^{*}_{Q}
combined with a suitable a priori estimate.

Proposition 4.2
.

Suppose that
\overline{Q(H)}^{\operatorname{w}-*}=X^{*}_{Q}
and that
\left\lVert\omega\right\rVert_{H}^{2}\lesssim\left\lVert Q\omega\right\rVert_{X^{*}_{Q}}
for every minimum norm solution. Then
Q(H)=X^{*}_{Q}
.

Proof.

Let
f\in X^{*}
and choose minimum norm solutions
\omega_{j}\in H
with
Q\omega_{j}\overset{*}{\rightharpoonup}\omega
. For large enough
j\in\mathbb{N}
we have
\left\lVert\omega_{j}\right\rVert_{H}^{2}\lesssim\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{X^{*}}+1
. After passing to a subsequence, a weak limit
\omega
of
(\omega_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}
satisfies
Q\omega=f
.
∎

We also mention a version of the Banach-Schauder open mapping theorem for multilinear (and more general) operators from
[
16
]
. We say that
Q
enjoys
generalised translation invariance
if there exist isometric isomorphisms
\sigma_{j}\in H^{H}
and
\sigma_{j}\in(X_{Q}^{*})^{X_{Q}^{*}}
,
j\in\mathbb{N}
, such that
Q\circ\sigma_{j}=\sigma_{j}\circ Q
for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
and
\sigma_{j}f\overset{*}{\rightharpoonup}0
as
j\to\infty
for all
f\in X_{Q}^{*}
. The example one should have in mind is
\sigma_{j}\omega(x)=\omega(x-je)
and
\sigma_{j}f(x)\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}f(x-je)
for a non-zero vector
e
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Suppose
Q
enjoys generalised translation invariance. Then one of the following claims holds:

(1)

Q(H)=X_{Q}^{*}
and every minimum norm solution satisfies
\left\lVert\omega\right\rVert_{H}^{2}\lesssim\left\lVert Q\omega\right\rVert_{X_{Q}^{*}}
.

(2)

Q(H)
is meagre in
X_{Q}^{*}
.

Theorem
4.3
is primarily a practical tool for showing
non
-surjectivity of
Q\colon H\to X^{*}_{Q}
; disproving the
a priori
estimate
\left\lVert\omega\right\rVert_{H}^{2}\lesssim\left\lVert Q\omega\right\rVert_{X_{Q}^{*}}
is a markedly less daunting task than disproving surjectivity
[
16
]
.

4.2.
Definition and existence of Lagrange multipliers

When
\omega\in H
is a minimum norm solution, it is natural to look for a Lagrange multiplier of
\omega
, as discussed in §
1.3
. The Lagrange multiplier condition (
1.5
) can be written more concisely as follows:
b\in X^{**}_{Q}
is a Lagrange multiplier of
\omega\in H
if

(4.1)

2\mathbf{Re}\langle\omega,\varphi\rangle_{H}=\langle b,Q^{\prime}_{\omega}\varphi\rangle_{X_{Q}^{**}-X_{Q}^{*}}\quad\text{ for every }\varphi\in H.

Yet more consicely,
(Q_{\omega}^{\prime})^{*}b=2\omega
. The existence of
b
then means that
\omega\in\operatorname{ran}((Q_{f}^{\prime})^{*})=\ker(Q_{f}^{\prime})^{\perp}

The standard tool for showing the existence of a Lagrange multiplier in a Banach space is the Liusternik-Schnirelman theorem which, in this setting, says that if
Q_{\omega}^{\prime}
maps
H
onto
X_{Q}^{*}
, then
\omega
possesses a Lagrange multiplier. However, in the cases of interest to us, the Liusternik-Schnirelman theorem is not available, as shown belov.

Proposition 4.4
.

Suppose
X^{*}_{Q}
is not isomorphic to a Hilbert space. Then we have
\{Q^{\prime}_{\omega}\gamma\colon\gamma\in H\}\subsetneq X^{*}_{Q}
for every
\omega\in H
.

Proof.

Seeking a contradiction, suppose
Q^{\prime}_{\omega}H=X^{*}
. Thus
Q^{\prime}_{\omega}\colon\operatorname{ker}(Q^{\prime})^{\perp}\to X^{*}
is an isomorphism from a Hilbert space onto
X^{*}
.
∎

Proposition 4.5
.

Every
b\in\mathbb{S}_{X_{Q}}
is a Lagrange multiplier of some
\omega\in\mathcal{A}
.

Proof.

Choose a maximizing sequence:
\langle T_{b}\omega_{j},\omega_{j}\rangle_{H}\to 1
. For a subsequence,
\omega_{j}\rightharpoonup\omega
in
H
, and so
T_{b}\omega_{j}\to T_{b}\omega
, giving
1=\langle T_{b}\omega,\omega\rangle_{H}=\langle b,Q\omega\rangle_{X_{Q}-X_{Q}^{*}}\leq\left\lVert Qf\right\rVert_{X_{Q}^{*}}\leq\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{H}^{2}\leq 1
, so that
\omega\in\mathscr{A}
.
∎

Definition 4.6
.

When
Y
is a real Banach space, a set
A\subset\mathbb{S}_{Y^{*}}
is call a
James boundary of
Y
if for every
y\in\mathbb{S}_{Y}
there exists
y^{*}\in A
such that
\langle y^{*},y\rangle_{Y^{*}-Y}=1
.

Proposition
4.5
then says that
Q(\mathscr{A})
is a James boundary of
X_{Q}
. By Godefroy’s Theorem (see
[
12
, Theorem 3.46]
), if
A\subset\mathbb{S}_{Y^{*}}
is a separable James boundary of
Y
, then
\overline{\operatorname{co}}(A)=\mathbb{B}_{Y^{*}}
.

Corollary 4.7
.

\overline{\operatorname{co}}(Q(\mathscr{A}))=\mathbb{B}_{X^{*}_{Q}}
.

Corollary 4.8
.

\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{X_{Q}^{*}}=\inf\{\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left\lVert\omega_{j}\right\rVert_{H}^{2}\colon f=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}Q\omega_{j}\}
for all
f\in X_{Q}^{*}
.

4.3.
Further properties of Lagrange multipliers

The Lagrange multiplier condition (
4.1
) has several useful equivalent characterisations, and we collect two of them in the following proposition.

Proposition 4.9
.

Whenever
b\in\mathbb{S}_{X_{Q}^{**}}
and
\omega\in\mathbb{S}_{H}
, the following conditions are equivalent:

(i)

b
is a Lagrange multiplier of
\omega
, i.e., (
4.1
) holds.

(ii)

\langle b,Q\omega\rangle_{X_{Q}^{**}-X_{Q}^{*}}=1
.

(iii)

\omega\in\ker(I-T_{b})
.

If (i)–(iii) hold, then
\omega
is a minimum norm solution and belongs to
\mathscr{A}
.

Proof.

We first prove (i)
\Leftrightarrow
(ii). Suppose (ii) holds and fix
\varphi\in H
and small
\delta>0
. The function
I\colon[-\delta,\delta]\to\mathbb{R}
,
I(\epsilon)\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}\langle b,Q(\omega+\epsilon\varphi)\rangle_{X_{Q}^{**}-X_{Q}^{*}}/\left\lVert\omega+\epsilon\varphi\right\rVert_{H}^{2}
is maximised at
\epsilon=0
, and therefore
I^{\prime}(0)=\langle b,Q^{\prime}_{\omega}\varphi\rangle_{X_{Q}^{**}-X_{k}^{*}}-2\mathbf{Re}\langle\omega,\varphi\rangle_{H}=0
, giving (i). The direction (i)
\Rightarrow
(ii) is proved by setting
\varphi=\omega
. We then prove (ii)
\Leftrightarrow
(iii). First, (ii) gives
\langle\omega,T_{b}\omega\rangle_{H}=1
. Since
\left\lVert T_{b}\right\rVert_{H\to H}=1
and
H
is strictly convex, we conclude that (iii) holds. On the other hand, if (iii) holds, then
\langle b,Q\omega\rangle_{X_{Q}^{**}-X_{Q}^{*}}=\langle\omega,T_{b}\omega\rangle_{H}=\langle\omega,\omega\rangle_{H}=1
.
∎

In many cases, conditions (i)–(iii) can be supplemented by Euler-Lagrange equations for a suitable potential. In the case of the planar Jacobian, denoting
u_{\bar{z}}=\omega
as before, (i)–(iii) are equivalent to
u_{z\bar{z}}=(bu_{\bar{z}})_{z}-(bu_{z})_{\bar{z}}

[
38
, Proposition 4.8]
.

Lemma 4.10
.

(X^{**},\left\lVert T_{\cdot}\right\rVert_{H\to H})=(X_{Q})^{**}
isometrically.

Proof.

Let
b\in X^{**}
. Since
Q(\mathbb{B}_{H})\subset\mathbb{B}_{X_{Q}^{*}}=\overline{\operatorname{co}}(Q\mathscr{A})
, we get

\left\lVert T_{b}\right\rVert_{H\to H}=\sup_{\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{H}=1}\langle b,Qf\rangle_{X^{**}-X^{*}}=\sup_{h\in\operatorname{co}(Q\mathscr{A})}\langle b,h\rangle_{X^{**}-X^{*}}=\left\lVert b\right\rVert_{(X^{*},\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{X_{Q}^{*}})^{*}}.

∎

Proposition 4.11
.

Suppose
b\in\mathbb{S}_{X_{Q}^{**}}
. The following conditions are equivalent.

(i)

b
is a Lagrange multiplier of some
\omega\in\mathscr{A}
.

(ii)

b
is norm-attaining.

Proof.

If
b
is a Lagrange multiplier of
\omega\in\mathscr{A}
, then
T_{b}\omega=\omega
and therefore
\langle b,Q\omega\rangle_{X_{Q}^{**}-X_{Q}^{*}}=\langle T_{b}\omega,\omega\rangle_{H}=1
.

Conversely, if
b
is norm-attaining, denote
D^{-1}(b)\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}\{f\in\mathbb{S}_{X_{Q}^{*}}\colon\langle b,f\rangle_{X_{Q}^{**}-X_{Q}^{*}}=1\}
. Since

is closed, bounded and convex, Theorem
2.7
implies that

contains an extreme point
f
. The definition of

implies that

is also an extreme point of

, and Lemma
5.1
then gives

.
∎

We also characterise elements of
\mathbb{S}_{H}
that possess a Lagrange multiplier.

Proposition 4.12
.

Let
\omega\in\mathbb{S}_{H}
. The following conditions are equivalent.

(i)

\omega
has a Lagrange multiplier
b\in\mathbb{S}_{X_{Q}^{**}}
.

(ii)

\omega\in\mathscr{A}
.

Proof.

If
\omega\in\mathbb{S}_{H}
has a Lagrange multiplier

We then show that every extreme point of

We recall the statement of Theorem

Claim 5.2
.

Suppose Assumptions

–

Proof.

Seeking a contradiction, suppose

, where
\omega,\gamma\in\mathscr{A}
are not equal up to a rotation. Thus we may write

with
\left\lvert s\right\rvert^{2}+\left\lvert t\right\rvert^{2}=1
,

and
\omega^{\perp}\in\mathbb{S}_{H}\cap\{\omega\}^{\perp}
. We intend to show that

(5.1)

Q(\omega^{\perp})=Q\omega.

Once (

such that

\psi^{\pm}\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}(\omega\pm c\omega^{\perp})/\sqrt{2}\in\mathbb{S}_{H}

Formula (

\tilde{\omega}^{\perp}\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}(\left\lvert st\right\rvert/s\bar{t})\omega^{\perp}

(5.2)

Since

Whenever

(5.3)

By choosing any

(5.4)

7.
The Jacobian equation with
L^{p}
data

Many of the ideas of this paper can be adapted to study the range of the operator
J\colon\dot{W}^{1,2p}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{R}^{2})\to L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
when
1<p<\infty
. In this section we outline such an approach and list those results whose proof extends to this new setting in a straightforward manner.

We can again use basic properties of the Beurling transform to write

\langle b,Q\omega\rangle_{L^{p^{\prime}}-L^{p}}=\langle T_{b}\omega,\omega\rangle_{L^{(2p)^{\prime}}-L^{2p}},\quad T_{b}\omega\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}\overline{({\mathcal{S}}b-b{\mathcal{S}})\overline{{\mathcal{S}}\omega}},\quad Q\omega\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}\left\lvert{\mathcal{S}}\omega\right\rvert^{2}-\left\lvert\omega\right\rvert^{2}.

Note that
\left\lVert T_{b}\right\rVert_{L^{2p}\to L^{(2p)^{\prime}}}\lesssim_{p}\left\lVert b\right\rVert_{L^{p^{\prime}}}
for all
b\in L^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{C})
by a simple application of Hölder’s inequality and the boundedness of
{\mathcal{S}}\colon L^{p}(\mathbb{C},\mathbb{C})\to L^{p}(\mathbb{C},\mathbb{C})
. The lower bound estimate

(7.1)

\left\lVert T_{b}\right\rVert_{L^{2p}\to L^{(2p)^{\prime}}}\gtrsim_{p}\left\lVert b\right\rVert_{L^{p^{\prime}}},

however, is far from trivial and cannot be proved by simply following the proof of estimates (
1.3
).

Hytönen proved (
7.1
) rather recently in
[
27
]
. More generally, on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, Hytönen showed that the commutator of a degenerate Calderón-Zygmund operator with
b\in L^{1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
defines a bounded operator from
L^{q_{1}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
into
L^{q_{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
q_{1}>q_{2}>1
, if and only if
b=a+c
with
a\in L^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
1/r=1/{q_{2}}-1/{q_{1}}
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
. In notable contrast to the case of
[b,T]\colon L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, the possible cancellations of
b
do not play an important role; recall, for instance, that there exist
b\notin\textup{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that
\left\lvert b\right\rvert\in\textup{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

As a corollary of (
7.1
), Hytönen obtained an analogue of (
1.2
): for every
f\in L^{p}(\mathbb{C})
there exist
u_{j}\in\dot{W}^{1,2p}(\mathbb{C},\mathbb{C})
such that
f=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}Ju_{j}
and
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left\lVert Du_{j}\right\rVert_{L^{2p}}^{2}\lesssim\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{p}}

[
27
]
. Hytönen also showed an analogous result in higher dimensions by different methods.

In order to adapt the methods of this paper to the
L^{p}
case, another crucial ingredient is the compactness of
[b,T]\colon L^{2p}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{R}^{2})\to L^{(2p)^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{R}^{2})
for all
b\in L^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. Hytönen & al. recently proved this property for a large class of degenerate Calderón-Zygmund kernels in
[
25
]
. We formulate their two main results.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
1<q_{2}<q_{1}<\infty
and
1/r=1/{q_{2}}-1/{q_{1}}
. Suppose

is a non-degenerate Calderón-Zygmund operator which satisfies one of the following two:

(1)

3.2
with the Dini condition

(2)

is compact from

to
L^{q_{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

Remark 7.6

Assumption

are self-adjoint and therefore the operator norm and numerical radius of

coincide,
\left\lVert T_{b}\right\rVert_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}=\sup_{\left\lVert\omega\right\rVert_{L^{2}}=1}\left\lvert\langle T_{b}f,f\rangle\right\rvert

satisfies the parallelogram law
\left\lVert\omega+\gamma\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\left\lVert\omega-\gamma\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}=2(\left\lVert\omega\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2})

\left\lVert T_{b}\right\rVert_{L^{2p}\to L^{(2p)^{\prime}}}=\sup_{\left\lVert\omega\right\rVert_{L^{2p}}=1}\left\lvert\langle T_{b}\omega,\omega\rangle_{L^{(2p)^{\prime}}-L^{2p}}\right\rvert

\left\lVert\omega+\gamma\right\rVert_{L^{2p}}^{2}+\left\lVert\omega+\gamma\right\rVert_{L^{(2p)^{\prime}}}^{2}\leq 2(\left\lVert\omega\right\rVert_{L^{2p}}^{2}+\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{L^{2p}}^{2})
fails (see

Appendix A
A proof of (
3.6
)

While formula (
3.6
) is well-known and classical, the author has been unable to find a proof in the literature, and therefore one is sketched in this appendix. Note that (
3.6
) is equivalent to the following proposition.

Proposition A.1
.

Every
f\in\mathcal{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
can be written as

f=\omega\gamma-H\omega H\gamma

for some
\omega,\gamma\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. However, there exists
f\in\mathcal{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
that cannot be written as
f=\omega^{2}-(H\omega)^{2}
for any
\omega\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.

The relevant background can be found e.g. in
[
10
]
or
[
40
]
. Whenever
0<p<\infty
, we denote

\left\lVert U\right\rVert_{p}\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}\sup_{0<y<\infty}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left\lvert U(x+iy)\right\rvert^{p}dx\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

The analytic Hardy space
\mathcal{H}^{p}(\mathbb{C}_{+})
consists of analytic functions
U\colon\mathbb{C}_{+}\to\mathbb{C}
with
\left\lVert U\right\rVert_{p}<\infty
.

Proof of Proposition
A.1
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
; then the Hilbert transform
Hf\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. We extend
f+iHf
analytically into
\mathbb{C}_{+}
by using the Poisson kernel
P_{y}
and the conjugate Poisson kernel
Q_{y}
,

P_{y}(x)\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{y}{x^{2}+y^{2}},\quad Q_{y}(x)\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{x}{x^{2}+y^{2}}.

We denote

U_{1}(x+iy)+iU_{2}(x+iy)\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}f*P_{y}(x)+i\,f*Q_{y}(x);

then
U=U_{1}+iU_{2}
belongs to
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\mathbb{C}_{+})
and, furthermore,
\lim_{y\searrow 0}\left\lVert U_{1}(\cdot,y)-f\right\rVert_{L^{1}}=0
and
\lim_{y\searrow 0}\left\lVert U_{2}(\cdot,y)-Hf\right\rVert_{L^{1}}=0
.

We form the Blaschke product
B
of
U
and write
U=B\Phi
, where
\Phi\in\mathcal{H}^{1}(\mathbb{C}_{+})
is zero-free. Since
\mathbb{C}_{+}
is simply connected,
\Phi
has an analytic square root
\Phi^{1/2}
. Set

V\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}B\Phi^{1/2}\in\mathcal{H}^{2}(\mathbb{C}_{+})\quad\text{and}\quad W\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}\Phi^{1/2}\in\mathcal{H}^{2}(\mathbb{C}_{+}).

Then there exist
\omega,\gamma\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
such that

V_{1}(\cdot+iy)+iV_{2}(\cdot+iy)\to\omega+iH\omega,

W_{1}(\cdot+iy)+iW_{2}(\cdot+iy)\to\gamma+iH\gamma

in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
as
y\searrow 0
. As a consequence,

\mathbf{Re}[VW]=V_{1}W_{1}-V_{2}W_{2}\to\omega\gamma-H\omega H\gamma

in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
as
y\searrow 0
. On the other hand,

\mathbf{Re}[VW]=\mathbf{Re}\,U\to f

in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
as
y\searrow 0
. Thus
f=w\gamma-H\omega H\gamma
.

We then find
f\in\mathcal{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
that cannot be written as
f=\omega^{2}-(H\omega)^{2}
for any
\omega\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Choose
U\in\mathcal{H}^{1}(\mathbb{C}_{+})
that has at least one zero of odd order. (Given
W\in\mathcal{H}^{1}(\mathbb{C}_{+})
one may set
U(z)=[(z-z_{0})/(z-\bar{z_{0}})]W(z)
, where
z_{0}\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
and
W(z_{0})\neq 0
.) Then
U
is not the square of any analytic function. We write
f\mathrel{:\mkern-0.25mu=}\lim_{y\searrow 0}U_{1}\in\mathcal{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
where the limit holds in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
.

Seeking a contradiction, assume that
f=\omega^{2}-(H\omega)^{2}
for some
\omega\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Then there exists
V=V_{1}+iV_{2}\in\mathcal{H}^{2}(\mathbb{C}_{+})
such that
\lim_{y\searrow 0}\left\lVert V_{1}(\cdot+iy)-\omega\right\rVert_{L^{2}}=0
. Now
V^{2}\in\mathcal{H}^{1}(\mathbb{C}_{+})
and

\mathbf{Re}[V^{2}]=V_{1}^{2}-V_{2}^{2}\to\omega^{2}-(H\omega)^{2}=f

in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
as
y\searrow 0
. Thus
U-V^{2}\in\mathcal{H}^{1}(\mathbb{C}_{+})
has vanishing boundary values at
y=0
, and so
U=V^{2}
, which yields a contradiction.
∎
