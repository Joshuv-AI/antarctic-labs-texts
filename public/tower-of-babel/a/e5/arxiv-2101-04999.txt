1

In the sense that the two families of graphs are

In the dictionary between group-theoretical properties of

\diam(\cay(G/H_{k},S))\geq C|G/H_{k}|^{\alpha}.

Note that property

(1)

This paper considers the

with presentation

BS(n,m)=\langle a,t|ta^{n}t^{-1}=t^{m}\rangle.

They all map onto

, by

. On the other hand they are known to be residually finite
if and only if

[

BS(1,m)
may be viewed as a semi-direct product

BS(1,m)=\mathbb{Z}[1/m]\rtimes\mathbb{Z}

where the factor
\mathbb{Z}
corresponds to the subgroup
\langle t\rangle
acting by powers of
m
. We may identify this semi-direct product with the following subgroup
G_{m}
of
upper triangular matrices in
GL_{2}(\mathbb{Z}[1/m])

G_{m}=\left\{\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}m^{k}&r\\

The isomorphism is obtained by mapping

and

to

. The associated embedding of

In

we may define congruence subgroups. Let

of the reduction modulo

M_{N}=\ker\left[GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z}[1/m])\rightarrow GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})\right].

Definition 1.2.

If

is any subgroup of

, and

, then the

For a sequence of integers such
that each one divides the next one, one obtains a sequence of nested congruence subgroups, and thus a box space of
BS(1,m)
. Such box spaces deserve to be called

. We will study property

for the arithmetic box spaces of
BS(1,m)
through the
standard embedding. From Theorem

with property

, but what about arithmetic box spaces?
We will prove that box spaces with

, for

, can be distinguished from arithmetic box spaces by coarse geometry. More precisely:

Theorem 1.3.

For any

, the following statements are true :

(1)

\square_{(G_{m}(N_{k}))_{k}}G_{m}

has property

without property

If

Proposition 1.4.

Fix

.

Of course the choice of the standard embedding raises a natural question: how do the congruence subgroups in

BS(1,m)
depend on the choice of the embedding
\rho
? Since congruence subgroups have finite index, this is
related to a form of the
congruence subgroup property
(CSP), which we now recall.

Definition 1.6.

Let
G
be a subgroup of
GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z}[1/m])
. We say that
G
has the CSP if every finite index subgroup in
G
contains
G(N)
for some
N>0
.

Let
T_{n}(\mathbb{Z}[1/m])
denote the group of upper triangular matrices in
GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z}[1/m])
. We shall
prove:

Theorem 1.7.

For every embedding
\rho:BS(1,m)\rightarrow T_{n}(\mathbb{Z}[1/m])
, the group
\rho(BS(1,m))

has the CSP.

This result can be reformulated as follows. Taking the congruence subgroups
\rho(G_{m})(N)

as a neighbourhood basis for the identity gives a topology on
\rho(G_{m})
(namely the congruence
topology relative to
\rho
) and the completion
\overline{\rho(G_{m})}
with respect to this topology is a profinite group called the congruence completion of
\rho(G_{m})
. As
G_{m}
is residually finite, it also embeds in its profinite completion
\widehat{G_{m}}

which maps onto
\overline{\rho(G_{m})}
. The CSP for
\rho(G_{m})
says that the map
\widehat{G_{m}}\rightarrow\overline{\rho(G_{m})}
is an isomorphism.

Another immediate consequence of CSP is that, if
\rho:G_{m}\rightarrow T_{n}(\mathbb{Z}[1/m])
is an embedding, then any box space of
G_{m}\simeq\rho(G_{m})
is covered by some arithmetic box space of
\rho(G_{m})
.

When
m=p^{\ell}
is a prime power, we also propose another approach to CSP for
BS(1,m)
: we view
BS(1,p^{\ell})
as a subgroup of the affine group
\mathbb{G}(\mathbb{Q})
, and this subgroup is commensurable to the group
\mathbb{G}(\mathcal{O}_{S})
of

. We prove:

Proposition 1.8.

Let
\mathbb{H}
be a
\mathbb{Q}
-algebraic subgroup of
GL_{n}
, and let
\rho:\mathbb{G}\rightarrow\mathbb{H}
be a
\mathbb{Q}
-isomorphism. Then
\rho(BS(1,p^{\ell}))
has CSP.

Lemma 2.2.

Let

(2)

\lcm(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})=\frac{a_{1}\cdots a_{n}}{\gcd(a_{1}\cdots a_{n-1},a_{1}\cdots a_{n-2}a_{n},\ldots,a_{2}\cdots a_{n})}.

we obtain by a direct computation that

\displaystyle\lcm(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n+1})

\displaystyle=\lcm(\lcm(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}),a_{n+1})

\displaystyle=\frac{\lcm(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\cdot a_{n+1}}{\gcd\left(\lcm(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}),a_{n+1}\right)}

(\mathbb{Z}[1/m]/(a_{1}^{k}-a_{2}^{k})\mathbb{Z}[1/m])^{\times}
, hence also in
(\mathbb{Z}[1/m]/N_{k}\mathbb{Z}[1/m])^{\times}
. It is therefore enough to prove that the map
k\mapsto N_{k}
takes infinitely many values on odd integers. This will follow immediately from the following.

Claim:
There is an infinite family of odd integers
k
such that
q_{k}\leq(a_{1}^{2r}-a_{2}^{2r})^{r}
.

To prove the claim, take
k
odd with
k>2r
. By the pigeonhole principle there are
i,j\in\{0,1\dots,r\}
with
i<j
such that the dominant primes in
q_{k-2i}
and
q_{k-2j}
are the same. We have that

\displaystyle(s^{k-2i}-1)-(s^{k-2j}-1)

\displaystyle=s^{k-2j}(s^{2(j-i)}-1)

\displaystyle=\frac{a_{1}^{k-2j}}{a_{2}^{k-2i}}(a_{1}^{2(j-i)}-a_{2}^{2(j-i)}).

Write
p_{i}^{\beta_{i}}
for the dominating factor of
q_{k-2i}
. Set
\beta=\min\{\beta_{i},\beta_{j}\}
and
p=p_{i}
; say that
\beta=\beta_{i}
(otherwise replace
i
by
j
). We see that
p^{\beta}
divides the numerator on the left, hence it divides the numerator on the right. We note that
p
does not divide
a_{1}
, nor
a_{2}
, hence it must divide
a_{1}^{2(j-i)}-a_{2}^{2(j-i)}
, which is bounded by
(a_{1}^{2r}-a_{2}^{2r})
. So we get
p^{\beta}\leq(a_{1}^{2r}-a_{2}^{2r})
and it follows that
q_{k-2i}\leq(a_{1}^{2r}-a_{2}^{2r})^{r}
. ∎

We study the diameter of arithmetic box spaces of

There exist constants

Let

the

As mentioned before,

Proposition 3.2.

Let

be such that

(7)

G_{m}/G_{m}(N)\simeq\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}\rtimes_{m}\mathbb{Z}/\ord_{m}(N)\mathbb{Z},\quad\text{ and }\quad\left|G_{m}/G_{m}(N)\right|=N\cdot\ord_{m}(N),

Consider reduction modulo

\begin{array}[]{cccl}\varphi\colon&G_{m}&\to&GL_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})\\
&w=\begin{pmatrix}m^{k}&x\\

So

and the result follows from the first isomorphism theorem.

Lemma 3.4.

Let

. Then

(8)

\diam(\cay(G_{m}/G_{m}(N)))=\Theta(\ord_{m}(N)).

More precisely, there
exists a constant
C_{m}>0
such that

(9)

\frac{1}{3}\cdot\ord_{m}(N)\leq\diam(\cay(G_{m}/G_{m}(N)))\leq C_{m}\cdot\ord_{m}(N).

Proof.

Let
m\geq 2
and consider
BS(1,m)\simeq G_{m}\subset\mathsf{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}[1/m])
.
Recall that
G_{m}/G_{m}(N)\simeq\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}\rtimes_{m}\mathbb{Z}/\ord_{m}(N)\mathbb{Z}
so that

\diam(G_{m}/G_{m}(N))=\diam(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}\rtimes_{m}\mathbb{Z}/\ord_{m}(N)\mathbb{Z})\geq\diam(\mathbb{Z}/\ord_{m}(N)\mathbb{Z})

Since the Cayley graph of

is a
cycle, we can roughly estimate the diameter to obtain

(10)

\diam(G_{m}/G_{m}(N))\geq\frac{1}{3}\ord_{m}(N).

For the second inequality, let
([x],[k])\in\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}\rtimes_{m}\mathbb{Z}/\ord_{m}(N)\mathbb{Z}
be an element realising the diameter. We rewrite

([x],[k])
as
\big(\begin{smallmatrix}m^{k}&x\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\big)G_{m}(N)
. The induced

\left\|\begin{pmatrix}m^{k}&x\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}G_{m}(N)\right\|_{G_{m}/G_{m}(N)}\leq\left\|\begin{pmatrix}m^{k}&x\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\right\|_{G_{m}}.

Recall that any word

\omega\in BS(1,m)
can be written in normal form as
\omega=t^{-i}a^{\ell}t^{j}
. In the quotient

G_{m}/G_{m}(N)
, the situation is even simpler, as by the semi-direct product stucture every word can be written as

with

0\leq\ell<N
and
0\leq j<\ord_{m}(N)
.
With
\ell=x
and

, we identify
\big(\begin{smallmatrix}m^{k}&x\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\big)
with the element

in normal form in
G_{m}
.
If

we get

and

\|T^{k}\|_{G_{m}}=k<\ord_{m}(N).

Assume that

. From Proposition

, we obtain

(11)

\left\|\begin{pmatrix}m^{k}&x\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\right\|_{G_{m}}=\|A^{x}T^{k}\|_{G_{m}}\leq 2m(k+\log x+1).

Note that since

(equivalently

) and moreover

, Eq. (

(12)

\left\|\begin{pmatrix}m^{k}&x\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\right\|_{G_{m}}\leq 2m(2+\log(m))\ord_{m}(N).

Setting

, we obtain

(13)

\diam(\cay(G_{m}/G_{m}(N)))\leq C_{m}\cdot\ord_{m}(N).

Proposition 3.5.

An arithmetic box space
\square_{(G_{m}(N_{k}))_{k}}G_{m}

Using Lemma

\square_{(G_{m}(N_{k}))_{k}}G_{m}\,\mbox{has}\,D_{\alpha}\iff\diam(G_{m}/G_{m}(N_{k}))=\Omega(|G_{m}/G_{m}(N_{k})|^{\alpha})

\iff\ord_{m}(N_{k})=\Omega(N_{k}^{\alpha}\cdot\ord_{m}(N_{k})^{\alpha})\iff\ord_{m}(N_{k})=\Omega(N_{k}^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}).

∎

We present here the main structure theorem for the arithmetic box spaces of

.

Theorem 3.6.

For any

, the following statements hold :

(1)

(2)

(3)

for any

(4)

Fix

(1)

If
\square_{(G_{m}(N_{k}))_{k}}G_{m}

and Proposition

, which forces

.

(2)

Let

be the sequence defined by

. Clearly,

for every

. We apply Lemma

and

We thus obtain:

\ord_{m}(N_{k})=2\cdot(m^{2}-1)^{k-1},\forall k\geq 1.

. By Proposition

.

(3)

We consider the sequence

defined by

and prove that the arithmetic box space

\square_{(G_{m}(N_{k}))_{k}}G_{m}
does not have property
D_{\alpha}
for any
\alpha\in]0,1/2]
. It is
straightforward that

for every

\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\ord_{m}(N_{k})}{N_{k}^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}}=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{2^{k}}{(m^{2^{k}}-1)^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}}=0,

.

. Pick an integer

\frac{D}{D+1}\geq\alpha

be any arithmetic box space of

. Define

and

3.2.
Density results

A natural question after encountering the constructions of Theorem

have

following paragraphs, we give a partial answer to this question.

Let
(N_{k})_{k}\subset\mathbb{N}
be such that
N_{k}\mid N_{k+1}
for every
k>0
, and denote by
P_{k}
the set of
prime factors of
N_{k}
. Moreover, we define the set of prime factors of the sequence
(N_{k})_{k}
by

(15)

P:=\bigcup_{k=1}^{+\infty}P_{k}.

Before stating our main result from this section, we need to introduce some definitions about the density of
prime numbers. We follow Powell
[
Powell 1980
]
for the terminology.

Definition 3.7.

Let
P\subset\mathcal{P}
be a subset of the prime numbers. The
natural primitive density
of
P
is
(if the limit exists)

d^{\prime}(P):=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\left|\{p\leq N\mid p\in P\}\right|}{\left|\{p\leq N\mid p\in\mathcal{P}\}\right|}.

The
analytic primitive density
of
P
is (if the limit exists)

D^{\prime}(P)=\lim_{s\to 1^{+}}\frac{\sum_{p\in P}\frac{1}{p^{s}}}{\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\frac{1}{p^{s}}}.

If
P
is finite then
d^{\prime}(P)=D^{\prime}(P)=0
. Suppose now that
D^{\prime}(P)>0
. In this case, we see that
\sum_{p\in P}\frac{1}{p}=+\infty
, otherwise
D^{\prime}(P)
would be equal to
0
. Observe that

\prod_{p\in P}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)=0\iff\sum_{p\in P}\ln\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)=-\infty.

But using that
\ln(1+x)\leq x
for
x>-1

\sum_{p\in P}\ln\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)\leq-\sum_{p\in P}\frac{1}{p}=-\infty.

Therefore, we obtain

(16)

\prod_{p\in P}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)=0

if
D^{\prime}(P)>0
.

Theorem 3.8.

Let
\square_{(G_{m}(N_{k}))_{k}}G_{m}
be an arithmetic box space, and let
P
be the set of prime factors of
the sequence
(N_{k})_{k}
.

(1)

If
|P|<+\infty
, then
\square_{(G_{m}(N_{k}))_{k}}G_{m}
has
D_{1/2}
;

(2)

If
D^{\prime}(P)>0
, then
\square_{(G_{m}(N_{k}))_{k}}G_{m}
does not have
D_{\alpha}
for every
\alpha>0
.

Proof.

In view of Proposition
3.5
, we must study the asymptotics of the quotient

\frac{\ord_{m}(N_{k})}{N_{k}}
.

(1)

By Lemma
2.8
, there exists a constant
C(m,P)
such that
\frac{\ord_{m}(N_{k})}{N_{k}}\geq C(m,P)
, i.e.
\ord_{m}(N_{k})=\Omega(N_{k})
. Proposition

applies to show that
\square_{(G_{m}(N_{k}))_{k}}G_{m}

(2)

Assume now

4.

For a subring
A
of
\mathbb{Q}
, we define three subgroups of
GL_{n}(A)
:

•

T_{n}(A)
, the subgroup of upper triangular matrices;

•

U_{n}(A)
, the unipotent subgroup, i.e. the subgroup of
T_{n}(A)
consisting of matrices with 1’s down the diagonal;

•

D_{n}(A)
, the subgroup of diagonal matrices.

The map
\Delta:T_{n}(A)\rightarrow D_{n}(A)
taking a matrix to its diagonal, is a surjective group homomorphism with kernel
U_{n}(A)
.

We will also need the set
N_{n}(A)
of upper triangular nilpotent matrices, i.e. upper triangular matrices with 0’s down the diagonal. Note that
T_{n}(A)={\bf 1}_{n}+N_{n}(A)
.

4.1.
Representations of
A
into
U_{n}(A)

We recall Chernikov’s theorem (see Theorem 4.10 in
[
Warfield 1976
]
): if
N
is a torsion-free nilpotent group,
for every
k\geq 1
the map
N\rightarrow N:x\mapsto x^{k}
is injective.

The first lemma discusses one-parameter subgroups in
U_{n}(A)
.

Lemma 4.1.

For every
g\in U_{n}(A)
, there exists a unique homomorphism
\alpha:A\rightarrow U_{n}(A)
such that
\alpha(1)=g
.

Proof.

The existence follows from Cor. 10.25 in
[
Warfield 1976
]
: if
g={\bf 1}_{n}+X
, with
X\in N_{n}(A)
, then for
r\in A
:

\alpha(r)=({\bf 1}_{n}+X)^{r}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\binom{r}{k}X^{k}={\bf 1}_{n}+rX+\frac{r(r-1)}{2}X^{2}+...

(note that the sum is finite as
X^{n}=0
). For the uniqueness, let
\beta
be another homomorphism with

\beta(1)=g
; for
r\in A
, write
r=\frac{a}{b}
with
a,b\in\mathbb{Z},b>0
and
a,b
coprime. Then

\alpha(r)^{b}=\alpha(a)=g^{a}=\beta(a)=\beta(r)^{b},

so
\alpha(r)=\beta(r)
by Chernikov’s theorem.
∎

Definition 4.2.

For a subgroup
H
of
U_{n}(A)
, the
isolator
of
H
is:

I(H)=\{g\in U_{n}(A):g^{k}\in H\,\mbox{for some}\,k\geq 1\}.

By Theorem 3.25 in
[
Warfield 1976
]
,
I(H)
is a subgroup of
U_{n}(A)
. Clearly
H\subset I(H)
.

Lemma 4.3.

For
m\geq 2
, let
\alpha:\mathbb{Z}[1/m]\rightarrow U_{n}(\mathbb{Z}[1/m])
be an injective homomorphism. Then
I(\alpha(\mathbb{Z}[1/m]))
is abelian and the exponent of the group
I(\alpha(\mathbb{Z}[1/m]))/\alpha(\mathbb{Z}[1/m])
is finite.

Proof.

The proof is in three steps.

(1)

By Lemma
4.1
, there exists a unique homomorphism
\tilde{\alpha}:\mathbb{Q}\rightarrow U_{n}(\mathbb{Q})

that extends
\alpha
. We show that
I(\alpha(\mathbb{Z}[1/m])\subset\tilde{\alpha}(\mathbb{Q})
, from which the first
statement will follow. For
g\in I(\alpha(\mathbb{Z}[1/m]))
, there exists
k\geq 1
and
r\in\mathbb{Z}[1/m]
such that

g^{k}=\alpha(r)
. Then

\tilde{\alpha}\left(\frac{r}{k}\right)^{k}=\tilde{\alpha}(r)=\alpha(r)=g^{k},

hence
g=\tilde{\alpha}(\frac{r}{k})
by Chernikov’s theorem.

(2)

Let us show that there exists some non-zero
x_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}[1/m]
such that, for

g\in I(\alpha(\mathbb{Z}[1/m])),k\geq 1,r\in\mathbb{Z}[1/m]
:

g^{k}=\alpha(r)\Longrightarrow\frac{r}{k}x_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}[1/m].

Write
\alpha(1)={\bf 1}_{n}+X
, with
X\in N_{n}(\mathbb{Z}[1/m])
, as in Lemma
4.1
. Note that
X\neq 0

as
\alpha
is injective. Then

g=\tilde{\alpha}\left(\frac{r}{k}\right)=({\bf 1}_{n}+X)^{\frac{r}{k}}={\bf 1}_{n}+\frac{r}{k}X+...

For
1\leq i<n
and an upper triangular matrix
Y
of size
n\times n
, we denote by
Y_{(i)}
the
i
-th
parallel to the diagonal (moving upwards from the diagonal). Let
i
be the smallest index such that

X_{(i)}\neq 0
. Since
(X^{k})_{(i)}=0
for
k\geq 2
, we have
g_{(i)}=\frac{r}{k}X_{(i)}
. Let
x_{0}
be any
non-zero coefficient of
X_{(i)}
. Since
g\in U_{n}(\mathbb{Z}[1/m])
, we have
\frac{r}{k}x_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}[1/m]
as desired.

(3)

Let
\pi(m)
be the set of primes dividing
m
. An integer is a
\pi(m)
-number
if all its
prime divisors are in
\pi(m)
. Write
x_{0}=\frac{b}{t}
, with
t
a
\pi(m)
-number, and
b\in\mathbb{Z}
is coprime
to
t
. For
g\in I(\alpha(\mathbb{Z}[1/m]))
, with
g^{k}=\alpha(r)
as above, write
\frac{r}{k}=\frac{a}{s\ell}
,
where
a,s,\ell
are pairwise coprime,
s
is a
\pi(m)
-number and
\ell
is coprime with
m
. By the
previous step
\frac{r}{k}x_{0}=\frac{ab}{st\ell}\in\mathbb{Z}[1/m]
. Since
a
and
\ell
are coprime, this may
happen only if
\ell
divides
b
. Finally

g^{b}=\tilde{\alpha}\left(\frac{r}{k}\right)^{b}=\tilde{\alpha}\left(\frac{br}{k}\right)=\tilde{\alpha}\left(\frac{ab}{s\ell}\right).

But
\frac{ab}{s\ell}\in\mathbb{Z}[1/m]
as
\ell
divides

. This implies that

, hence the exponent of

divides

.

∎

4.2.
Special representations of
BS(1,m)

For
m\geq 2
, set
G_{m}=BS(1,m)=\mathbb{Z}[1/m]\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
. We will write
A_{m}
for
\mathbb{Z}[1/m]
when viewed as a normal subgroup of
G_{m}
.

Definition 4.4.

A
special representation
of
G_{m}
is an injective homomorphism
\rho:G_{m}\rightarrow T_{n}(\mathbb{Z}[1/m])

such that
\rho(A_{m})\subset U_{n}(\mathbb{Z}[1/m])
.

We note that the standard embedding
G_{m}\rightarrow T_{2}(\mathbb{Z}[1/m])
, is a special representation.

Lemma 4.5.

(1)

If
\rho
is a special representation, then
\rho^{-1}(U_{n}(\mathbb{Z}[1/m]))=A_{m}
.

(2)

If
m
is even, then any injective homomorphism
\rho:G_{m}\rightarrow T_{n}(\mathbb{Z}[1/m])
is a special
representation.

Proof.

We work with the presentation
G_{m}=\langle a,t|tat^{-1}=a^{m}\rangle
, observing that the normal subgroup

A_{m}
coincides with the normal subgroup generated by
a
.

(1)

Suppose by contradiction that
A_{m}
is strictly contained in
\rho^{-1}(U_{n}(\mathbb{Z}[1/m]))
. Then there
exists
k>0
such that
\rho(t^{k})\in U_{n}(\mathbb{Z}[1/m])
. Consider the subgroup
H
of
G_{m}
generated by

A_{m}\cup\{t^{k}\}
, so that
\rho(H)\subset U_{n}(\mathbb{Z}[1/m])
. Since
\rho
is injective, we see that
H
is
nilpotent. As
H
also has finite index in
G_{m}
, we deduce that
G_{m}
is virtually nilpotent, which is a
contradiction.

(2)

Suppose that
m
is even and
\rho:G_{m}\rightarrow T_{n}(\mathbb{Z}[1/m])
is an injective homomorphism. It is
enough to see that
\rho(a)
belongs to
U_{n}(\mathbb{Z}[1/m])=\ker(\Delta)
. But we have

\rho(a^{m-1})=\rho([a,t])\in[T_{n}(\mathbb{Z}[1/m]),T_{n}(\mathbb{Z}[1/m])]\subset\ker(\Delta).

Now the image of
\Delta
, namely
D_{n}(\mathbb{Z}[1/m])\simeq(\mathbb{Z}[1/m]^{\times})^{n}
, contains only 2-torsion; since

\Delta(\rho(a))^{m-1}={\bf 1}_{n}
and
m-1
is odd, we have
\Delta(\rho(a))={\bf 1}_{n}
as desired.

∎

We now head towards CSP for special representations of

. The next lemma is proved exactly as Lemma 4
in Formanek
[

Lemma 4.6.

Let

. There exists a function

such that, if

is an

\square

[

4.3.
From special representations to all injective representations in
T_{n}(\mathbb{Z}[1/m])

In this section we show that for any injective representation
\rho
of
G_{m}
into
T_{n}(\mathbb{Z}[1/m])
, the group

\rho(G_{m})
has CSP. The main idea is to pass to subrepresentations which are special and extract information
about
\rho
.

Lemma 4.8.

Let
G
be a subgroup of
GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z}[1/m])
.

(1)

Let
H
be a finite index subgroup of
G
, where
H
has CSP. If
H
contains a congruence subgroup of

G
, then
G
also has CSP.

(2)

For
1\leq i\leq k
, let
H_{i}
be a finite index subgroup of
G
. Suppose
G=\cup_{i=1}^{k}H_{i}
, then
if all
H_{i}
have CSP,
G
also has CSP.

Proof.

(1)

Let
N
be a finite index subgroup of
G
, then
N\cap H
is a finite index subgroup of
H
and thus
contains some congruence subgroup of the form
H(M)
. By assumption there is some
\tilde{M}
such that

G(\tilde{M})\subset H
, hence we have that

G(M\tilde{M})\subset G(M)\cap G(\tilde{M})\subset H(M)\subset N\cap H\subset N.

(2)

Suppose by contradiction that
G
does not have CSP, then there is some finite index subgroup
N

which does not contain any congruence subgroup. However each
N\cap H_{i}
contains a congruence subgroup
of the form
H(M_{i})
. Set
\overline{M}=\Pi_{i}M_{i}
, then there is some

g\in G(\overline{M})\backslash N
. Note that this
g
is necessarily in one of the
H_{i}
’s. So it is
necessarily in
H(M_{i})
since
H(\overline{M})\subset H(M_{i})
. However it is not in
N\cap H_{i}
: a
contradiction.

∎

Lemma 4.9.

Let
\rho:G_{m}\rightarrow T_{n}(\mathbb{Z}[1/m])
be a monomorphism. Suppose
\Delta(\rho(a))\neq{\bf 1}_{n}
and the
diagonal of
\rho(t)
is strictly positive, then
\rho(G_{m})
has CSP.

Proof.

Note that the subgroup
B=\langle a^{2},t\rangle
is also a Baumslag-Solitar group isomorphic to
BS(1,m)
. The
restriction
\rho|_{B}
is a special representation of
BS(1,m)
; indeed by the proof of Lemma

4.5
, we have
\Delta(\rho(a))^{m-1}={\bf 1}_{n}
, so
\Delta(\rho(a))
has finite order in

D_{n}(\mathbb{Z}[\frac{1}{m}])
; but the torsion subgroup of
D_{m}(\mathbb{Z}[\frac{1}{m}])
is
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
, so

\Delta(\rho(a^{2}))={\bf 1}_{n}
. By Proposition
4.7
, the group
\rho(B)
has the CSP.

We show there is some congruence subgroup of
\rho(G_{m})
that is fully contained inside

\rho(B)
.
Since
\Delta(\rho(a))\neq{\bf 1}_{n}
, there is some
-1
on the diagonal, suppose it appears at the

i^{\text{th}}
position. Let
s
be the element of
(\mathbb{Z}[1/m])^{\times}
in the
i^{\text{th}}
position in

t
. By Lemma
2.10
, we find
M>1
, coprime with
m
, such that
s
has odd order in

(\mathbb{Z}[1/m]/M\mathbb{Z}[1/m])^{\times}
.

We note that if
\omega
is a word with letters in
\{a,t\}
representing an element of
G_{m}\setminus B
,
then it contains an odd number of
a
’s. Hence, the element on the
i^{\text{th}}
position on the diagonal
of
\rho(\omega)
is of the form
-s^{r}
for some
r\in\mathbb{Z}
. If we suppose that

\rho(\omega)\in\rho(G_{m})(M)
, then
s^{r}=-1\mod M
. This is impossible since
s
has odd order in

(\mathbb{Z}[\frac{1}{m}]/M\mathbb{Z}[1/m])^{\times}
and thus cannot have a power equal to
-1
. So
\rho(\omega)
cannot be
in the corresponding congruence subgroup, i.e.
\rho(G_{m})(M)\subset\rho(B)
. By Lemma

4.8
, the result follows.
∎

We are ready to complement Proposition
4.7
.

Proposition 4.10.

For any injective homomorphism
\rho:G_{m}\rightarrow T_{n}(\mathbb{Z}[1/m])
, the group
\rho(G_{m})
has CSP.

Proof.

Since we already know the result for
m
even (by Proposition
4.7
and Lemma
4.5
),
we may and will assume that
m
is odd.

The group
G_{m}
has three subgroups
B_{1}=\langle a^{2},t\rangle
,
B_{2}=\langle a^{2},at\rangle
and

B_{3}=\langle a,t^{2}\rangle
, which are all isomorphic to
BS(1,m)
or
BS(1,m^{2})
. We show that they cover

G_{m}
. Any element
\omega\in G_{m}
can be described by a word of the form
t^{-k}a^{r}t^{\ell}
, with

k,\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and
r\in\mathbb{Z}
. Replacing
\omega
by
\omega^{-1}
if necessary, we may assume

k\leq\ell
.

•

If
r
is even, then
\omega\in B_{1}
;

•

If
k
and
l
are both even or odd, then
\omega\in B_{3}
; indeed

\omega=t^{-2k}(t^{k}a^{r}t^{-k})t^{k+\ell}=t^{-2k}a^{rm^{k}}t^{k+\ell}\in B_{3}
as
k+\ell
is even.

•

If
r
is odd and
k,\ell
do not have the same parity, then
\omega\in B_{2}
. Indeed

t^{-k}a^{r}t^{\ell}=(t^{-1}a^{-1})^{k}a^{\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}m^{i}}a^{r}a^{-\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1}m^{i}}(at)^{l}=(at)^{-k}a^{r-\sum_{i=k}^{\ell-1}m^{i}}(at)^{\ell}.

Since
r,\ell-k
and
m
are odd, the exponent of
a
in the latter expression is even, so
\omega\in B_{2}
.

Note that if
\Delta(\rho(a))={\bf 1}_{n}
, then it is a special representation and Proposition

4.7
applies. If it is not then the restrictions of
\rho
to
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
are special
representations, and the restriction of
\rho
to
B_{3}
satisfies the assumptions of Lemma

4.9
. Hence the
\rho(B_{i})
’s all have CSP. Now simply applying Lemma

4.8
finishes the argument.
∎

4.4.
An alternative approach to CSP for
BS(1,p^{\ell})
(
p
prime)

We use the language of
\mathbb{Q}
-algebraic groups. Denoting by
\mathbb{G}_{a}
(resp.
\mathbb{G}_{m}
) the additive
group (resp. the multiplicative group), we set
\mathbb{G}=\mathbb{G}_{a}\rtimes\mathbb{G}_{m}
, the affine group
viewed as a subgroup of
GL_{2}
via the standard embedding.

Let
p_{1},...,p_{r}
be distinct primes, and let
S=\{p_{1},...,p_{r},\infty\}
be viewed as a set of places of

\mathbb{Q}
. Then the ring
\mathcal{O}_{S}
of
S

\mathbb{Q}
is precisely

\mathcal{O}_{S}=\mathbb{Z}[1/p_{1},...,1/p_{r}],

so that
\mathcal{O}_{S}^{\times}=\{\pm p_{1}^{k_{1}}...p_{r}^{k_{r}}:k_{1},...,k_{r}\in\mathbb{Z}\}
and

\mathbb{G}(\mathcal{O}_{S})=\left\{\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\pm p_{1}^{k_{1}}...p_{r}^{k_{r}}&a\\
0&1\end{array}\right):k_{1},...k_{r}\in\mathbb{Z},\;a\in\mathbb{Z}[1/p_{1},...,1/p_{r}]\right\}.

It is known that
\mathbb{G}(\mathcal{O}_{S})
satisfies CSP: see formula
(\ast\ast)
on page 108 of

[
Raghunathan 1976
]
.

Note that taking
r=1
, i.e
S=\{p.\infty\}
and
\mathcal{O}_{S}=\mathbb{Z}[\frac{1}{p}]
, makes
BS(1,p^{\ell})
appear
as a finite index subgroup in
\mathbb{G}(\mathcal{O}_{S})
, namely

[\mathbb{G}(\mathcal{O}_{S}):BS(1,p^{\ell})]=2\ell
.

Proposition 4.11.

Let
\mathbb{H}\subset GL_{n}
be a
\mathbb{Q}
-subgroup, and let
\rho:\mathbb{G}\rightarrow\mathbb{H}
be a

\mathbb{Q}
-isomorphism. Then
\rho(BS(1,p^{\ell}))
satisfies the CSP.

Proof.

Let
K
be a finite index subgroup in
\rho(BS(1,p^{\ell}))
. Since
\mathbb{G}(\mathcal{O}_{S})
satisfies the
CSP, we find
N>0
such that
\mathbb{G}(\mathcal{O}_{S})(N)\subset\rho^{-1}(K)
. By Lemma 3.1.1.(ii) in

[
Margulis 1991
]
,
the subgroup
\rho(\mathbb{G}(\mathcal{O}_{S})(N))
is an
S
-congruence subgroup in

\mathbb{H}(\mathcal{O}_{S})
, i.e. it contains
\mathbb{H}(\mathcal{O}_{S})(M)
for some

. Then

(\rho(BS(1,p^{\ell})))(M)\subset\mathbb{H}(\mathcal{O}_{S})(M)\subset\rho(\mathbb{G}(\mathcal{O}_{S})(N))\subset K
.
∎
