Balanced Allocations with Heterogeneous Bins: The Power of Memory Full version of a paper appearing in SODA 2023 [ 23 ]
Dimitrios Los, Thomas Sauerwald, John Sylvester

Abstract
We consider the allocation of m m balls (jobs) into n n bins (servers). In the standard Two-Choice process, at each step t = 1 , 2 , … , m t=1,2,\ldots,m we first sample two bins uniformly at random and place a ball in the least loaded bin. It is well-known that for any m ⩾ n m\geqslant n , this results in a gap (difference between the maximum and average load) of log 2 ⁡ log ⁡ n + Θ ⁡ ( 1 ) \log_{2}\log n+\Theta(1) (with high probability). In this work, we consider the Memory process [ 30 ] where instead of two choices, we only sample one bin per step but we have access to a cache which can store the location of one bin. Mitzenmacher, Prabhakar and Shah [ 26 ] showed that in the lightly loaded case ( m = n m=n ), the Memory process achieves a gap of 𝒪 ⁡ ( log ⁡ log ⁡ n ) \mathcal{O}(\log\log n) . Extending the setting of Mitzenmacher et al. in two ways, we first allow the number of balls m m to be arbitrary, which includes the challenging heavily loaded case where m ⩾ n m\geqslant n . Secondly, we follow the heterogeneous bins model of Wieder [ 33 ] , where the sampling distribution of bins can be biased up to some arbitrary multiplicative constant. Somewhat surprisingly, we prove that even in this setting, the Memory process still achieves an 𝒪 ⁡ ( log ⁡ log ⁡ n ) \mathcal{O}(\log\log n) gap bound. This is in stark contrast with the Two-Choice (or any d d -Choice with d = 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 ) d=\mathcal{O}(1) ) process, where it is known that the gap diverges as m → ∞ m\rightarrow\infty [ 33 ] . Further, we show that for any sampling distribution independent of m m (but possibly dependent on n n ) the Memory process has a gap that can be bounded independently of m m . Finally, we prove a tight gap bound of 𝒪 ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) \mathcal{O}(\log n) for Memory in another relaxed setting with heterogeneous (weighted) balls and a cache which can only be maintained for two steps.

1.2
Challenges and Techniques

One of the main challenges of analyzing the
Memory
process are the strong long-term dependencies which are introduced through the cache. This makes
Memory
quite different from
Two-Choice
, despite their apparent similarity in terms of the gap bound. In fact, the ability of
Memory
to store and fill up a light bin is crucial to achieve the
\mathcal{O}(\log\log n)
gap under biased sampling distributions. In contrast, the decisions between different steps of
Two-Choice
are essentially independent (if one disregards the change of the load vector). Formalizing this,
Two-Choice
can be expressed by a time-invariant distribution vector
p
, which specifies the probability
p_{i}
to allocate a ball to the
i
-th heaviest bin at each step. This type of analysis, paired with a two-sided exponential (a.k.a. hyperbolic cosine) potential, was pioneered in
[
28
]
(and used thereafter in, e.g.,
[
2
,
31
,
22
]
). This technique suffices to prove an
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
gap bound, which is independent of
m
, and provides a useful starting point for tighter gap bounds, as demonstrated in
[
31
,
20
]
.

For
Memory
, establishing this starting point, i.e.,
base case
, is already quite challenging.
To reduce dependencies, we reduce
Memory
to
d
-Weak-Memory
, a version of
Memory
, which resets the cache every
d=\mathcal{O}(1)
steps and uses outdated load information. This reduction is formalized by a coupling of
Memory
to
d
-Weak-Memory
which bounds the hyperbolic potential of the former by the latter.

Then, we prove that the hyperbolic potential in
d
-Weak-Memory
is
\mathcal{O}(n)
in expectation at any step. While this on its own suffices for an
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
gap bound, it does not establish an exponentially concentrated load distribution with high probability.
To get this stronger conclusion, we study the interplay between two versions of the hyperbolic potential with two smoothing parameters.
Apart from the analysis of
Memory
, this result for
d
-Weak-Memory
may be of independent interest as it gives some insight into the behavior of a version of
Memory
where the cache is reset periodically. Finally, we remark that this concentration result is also useful in establishing a lower bound of
\Omega(\log\log n)
for
Memory
, which holds in all steps
m\geqslant n
with high probability.

Once the base case is established, we begin in earnest to work on the actual
Memory
process with all its long-term correlations. We employ a judicious partition of steps separated by rounds (see Figure
1
), where we define
rounds
inductively through a “folding” procedure; a new round can only start if we did not sample a light bin, either at the beginning of the round, or for a sufficiently large number of consecutive steps. In particular, at the beginning of a round in the folded process we start with a fresh cache, so we are allocating using
One-Choice
. To prove an
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
gap, it is sufficient to reset the cache every two steps (a process we call
2
-
Reset-Memory
). However, to prove an
o(\log n)
gap, we need to look at intervals of length
\omega(1)
to counteract this potentially bad allocation. Essentially, we prove that the time between two rounds is large enough with sufficiently high probability, meaning that there is a strong drift away from the heavy bins so that their contribution to the potential is reduced. In the spirit of layered induction, we analyze increasingly steeper versions of the hyperbolic potentials that grow super-exponentially, and prove that each of them is linear with high probability. After completing
\mathcal{O}(\log\log n)
steps of the induction, we finally obtain the desired gap bound of
\mathcal{O}(\log\log n)
.

The main difference compared to the analyses in
[
28
,
22
,
19
,
21
]
is that we need to consider the change of the potential over increasingly longer intervals of a non-constant number of steps, which also requires a slightly different form of super-exponential potentials. Another additional challenge is that the handling of
(a,b)
-biased sampling distributions requires a lot of caution in the base case, in particular, when computing the allocation probabilities in the coupling of
d
-Weak-Memory
with
Memory
.

Basic Definitions

We consider the allocation of

is the number of balls allocated into bin

x_{i}^{0}=0
for every

. The

at step

\Gap(t)=\max_{i\in[n]}x_{i}^{t}-\frac{t}{n}.

It will be also convenient to keep the load vector

is a permutation of
x^{t}-(\frac{t}{n},\dots,\frac{t}{n})

y_{1}^{t}\geqslant y_{2}^{t}\geqslant\cdots\geqslant y_{n}^{t}
.
Note that
\sum_{i\in[n]}y_{i}^{t}=0
and

. Further, we say that a vector

majorizes

if for all

, the prefix sums satisfy:
\sum_{i=1}^{k}v_{i}\geqslant\sum_{i=1}^{k}u_{i}
.

Following
[
28
]
, many allocation processes can be described by a time-invariant
probability allocation vector

p=(p_{i})_{i\in[n]}
, such that at each step

,

-th most heavily loaded bin.

By

we denote the filtration of the process until step

. For random variables

we say that

(or equivalently,

2.2
Processes

In this paper we consider balanced allocation processes where the sampling distribution (the distribution of the bin index supplied to the process when it requests a “random” bin) will not necessarily be uniform. We let
\mathcal{S}
be a sampling distribution over the
n
bins, this is a vector
(s_{1},\dots,s_{n})
and use this to define our processes.
We emphasize that, even though in the analysis we usually work with the load vector sorted decreasingly at each step by relabeling the bins, the sampling distribution assigns a fixed probability to each bin and is not affected by a relabeling. In particular,
(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n})
should not be confused with
(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n})
.

We first give a formal description of the
d
-Choice

d
-Choice
​​(
\mathcal{S}
) Process:

Iteration:
For each step
t\geqslant 0

\mathcal{S}
. Let

x_{i}^{t+1}=x_{i}^{t}+1.

It is immediate that for the uniform sampling distribution the probability allocation vector of

p_{i}=\frac{2i-1}{n^{2}},\qquad\mbox{ for all $i\in[n]$.}

​​(

Iteration:

3
Outline of the Proof of Theorem
1.1

In this section we outline from a high level the proof of
1.1
, as this is by far the most substantial result in the paper and takes up the lion’s share of the analysis.

The rough idea is that, through a layered induction, we prove a series of bounds for ever steeper potential functions. The
j
-th such potential function, where
j\leqslant j_{\max}
, roughly has the form
\sum_{i\in[n]}e^{f_{j}\cdot(y_{i}^{m}-g_{j})}
for functions
f_{j}:=f_{j}(n)
and
g_{j}:=g_{j}(n)
. As
j
increases the functions
f_{j}(n)
and
g_{j}(n)
tend to
\infty
faster with
n
. For the last “layer”
j=j_{\max}
we have
f_{j}(n)=\Theta(\log n)
and
g_{j}(n)=\Theta(\log\log n)
and so a bound of
\mathcal{O}(n)
on the potential at level
j_{\max}
implies that
\Gap(m)=\max_{i\in[n]}y_{i}^{m}=\mathcal{O}(\log\log n)
. The reason for the layered induction is at each layer
j
a bound on the potential at layer
j
gives tail bounds on the number of bins with at least a given load; it is this bound which is used to prove a bound on the potential at layer
j+1
.

To start this induction we use a bound on the hyperbolic cosine potential function which in itself is strong enough to prove an
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
gap bound on the process (recall that ultimately our target is
\mathcal{O}(\log\log n)
).
There are many challenges to making this rigorous. We first outline the base case in more detail, followed by the layered induction.

3.1
Base Case

For the normalized load vector
(y_{i}^{t})_{i\in[n]}
in step
t
, the
hyperbolic cosine potential

[
28
]
with smoothing parameter
\alpha>0
, is defined as

\Gamma^{t}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{\alpha y_{i}^{t}}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{-\alpha y_{i}^{t}}.

This potential penalizes over/underloaded bins with an exponential cost in their normalized load. For the base case of the layered induction, we will use that for some sufficiently small constant
\alpha=\Theta(1)
, w.h.p.
\Gamma^{t}=\mathcal{O}(n)
at an arbitrary step. This will allow us to show that the number of bins with normalized load at least

is at most
\mathcal{O}(n\cdot e^{-\alpha v})
, which is essential for the layered induction (

).

Theorem
4.3

(Restated)
.

Consider the

(a,b)
-biased sampling distribution, for constants

To prove this result, we first need to establish that the expectation of the potential has a drift downwards after a certain number of sufficiently many steps.

Theorem
4.1

(Restated)
.

Consider the
Memory

(a,b)
-biased sampling distribution, for constants
a,b\geqslant 1
. Then, there exist constants
\alpha^{\prime}:=\alpha^{\prime}(a,b)>0

Having established the expectation bound and drift inequalities in

Recovery.

Through an interplay between different potentials, some defined for rounds and some defined for steps, we obtain the desired drop inequalities and conclude that, conditioning on the

Layered Induction.

For ease of reading and to keep the proof self-contained, several definitions and explanations from

In

The analysis will be done using

.

p

be the probability of

4.2.1
Couplings Between Processes

The
Memory
process is quite tricky to work with due to long range dependencies between allocations introduced by the cache. The
d
-Weak-Memory
process “forgets” the cache every
d
steps also and makes its decision for steps
t+1,\dots,t+d
using the load vector
x^{t}
.
For a positive integer
d
, we can then relate the results for the weaker
d
-Weak-Memory
processes back to the
Memory
processes via the following coupling over one run of
d
allocations.

Lemma 4.5
.

Consider any fixed
d\geqslant 1
and the potentials
\Phi:=\Phi(\alpha)
and
\Psi:=\Psi(\alpha)
for any
0<\alpha<1/d
. Fix a load vector
x^{t}
at step
t
and order the bins decreasingly by their loads. Let
\mu
be any
(a,b)
-biased sampling vector, and fix
p_{i,j}:=p_{i,j}(\mu,d)
and
\widehat{p}_{i,j}:=\widehat{p}_{i,j}(\mu,d)
. Then,

\displaystyle\alpha\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Phi_{i}^{t}\cdot\sum_{j=0}^{d}p_{i,j}\cdot j

\displaystyle\leqslant\alpha\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Phi_{i}^{t}\cdot\sum_{j=0}^{d}\widehat{p}_{i,j}\cdot j+\Phi^{t}\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\cdot(2d^{3}b),

and

\displaystyle-\alpha\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Psi_{i}^{t}\cdot\sum_{j=0}^{d}p_{i,j}\cdot j

\displaystyle\leqslant-\alpha\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Psi_{i}^{t}\cdot\sum_{j=0}^{d}\widehat{p}_{i,j}\cdot j+\Psi^{t}\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\cdot(2d^{3}b).

Proof.

For the first statement, our goal is to upper bound

\alpha\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Phi_{i}^{t}\cdot\sum_{j=0}^{d}p_{i,j}\cdot j=\alpha\cdot\mathbf{E}\left[\,\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Phi_{i}^{t}\cdot\eta_{i}\;\Bigg|\;\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right],

where
\eta_{i}:=\eta_{i}(\mu,t,d,\sigma)
and
\widehat{\eta}_{i}:=\widehat{\eta}_{i}(\mu,t,d,\sigma)
for each
i\in[n]
and any ordering
\sigma
of the bins by load. We consider a coupling between the two processes
Memory
and
d
-Weak-Memory
by sampling the same bins in steps
t+1,\ldots,t+d
, starting with the normalized load vector
y^{t}
. Let
c
be the cache of the
Memory
process at step
t
and let
\mathcal{D}
be the event that in steps
t+1,\ldots,t+d
, none of the bins is sampled twice and neither bin
c
is sampled.

Case 1 [
\mathcal{D}
holds]:

In this case, all sampled bins (and initial cache
c
) are different. Hence,
Memory
always stores a lesser loaded bin than the cache of
d
-Weak-Memory
, and so it allocates to a lesser loaded bin than
d
-Weak-Memory
. Hence,

\alpha\cdot\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta_{i}\cdot\Phi_{i}^{t}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{\eta}_{i}\cdot\Phi_{i}^{t}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t},\,\mathcal{D}\,\right]\leqslant 0.

(4.3)

Case 2 [
\mathcal{D}
does not hold]:

On the other hand, if there is a step
s\in[t+1,t+d]
such that processes do sample a bin for a second time or sample bin
c
, then for any subsequent step
r\in[s,t+d]
,
Memory
could allocate to a bin
i
and
d
-Weak-Memory
to a bin
i^{\prime}
such that
y_{i}^{t}\leqslant y_{i^{\prime}}^{t}+d
, since in
d
allocations the load of a bin can change by at most
d
, and so the cache of
Memory
can be at most
d
balls larger than that of
d
-Weak-Memory
.

In order to upper bound the probability that the event
\neg\mathcal{D}
occurs, we define
\neg\mathcal{D}^{r}
for
1\leqslant r\leqslant d
, the event that in step
t+r
, we sampled a bin for the second time or the cache

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\neg\mathcal{D}\mid\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\neg\mathcal{D}^{1}\mid\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]+\ldots+\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\neg\mathcal{D}^{d}\mid\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{b}{n}+2\cdot\frac{b}{n}+3\cdot\frac{b}{n}+\ldots+d\cdot\frac{b}{n}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{d^{2}b}{n}.

(4.4)

Since
y_{i}^{t}\leqslant y_{i^{\prime}}^{t}+d
, the term
\Phi_{i}^{t}
can be upper bounded by the term
\Phi_{i^{\prime}}^{t}
as follows

\Phi_{i}^{t}=e^{\alpha y_{i}^{t}}\leqslant e^{\alpha\cdot(y_{i^{\prime}}^{t}+d)}\leqslant\Phi_{i^{\prime}}^{t}\cdot e^{\alpha d}\leqslant\Phi_{i^{\prime}}^{t}\cdot(1+2\alpha d)=\Phi_{i^{\prime}}^{t}+\Phi_{i^{\prime}}^{t}\cdot 2\alpha d.

using that
\alpha\leqslant\frac{1}{d}
and that
e^{z}\leqslant 1+2z
for any
|z|\leqslant 1
. Since there are at most
d
different bins
j_{1},\ldots,j_{d}
allocated in steps
t+1,\ldots,t+d
, we have that

\alpha\cdot\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta_{i}\cdot\Phi_{i}^{t}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{\eta}_{i}\cdot\Phi_{i}^{t}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t},\,\neg\mathcal{D}\,\right]\leqslant\sum_{k=1}^{d}\Phi_{j_{k}}^{t}\cdot 2\alpha d\leqslant\Phi^{t}\cdot 2\alpha d.

(4.5)

Hence, combining
Eq.

4.3
and
Eq.

4.5
we can bound the expectation

\displaystyle\alpha\cdot\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta_{i}\cdot\Phi_{i}^{t}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{\eta}_{i}\cdot\Phi_{i}^{t}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\alpha\cdot\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta_{i}\cdot\Phi_{i}^{t}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{\eta}_{i}\cdot\Phi_{i}^{t}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t},\mathcal{D}\,\right]\cdot\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathcal{D}\mid\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\alpha\cdot\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta_{i}\cdot\Phi_{i}^{t}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{\eta}_{i}\cdot\Phi_{i}^{t}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t},\,\neg\mathcal{D}\,\right]\cdot\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\neg\mathcal{D}\mid\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant 0+\Phi^{t}\cdot 2\alpha^{2}d\cdot\frac{d^{2}b}{n}=\Phi^{t}\cdot\frac{2\alpha^{2}d^{3}b}{n}.

Now we proceed similarly for
\Psi
, by upper-bounding the term
-\Psi_{i}^{t}
by
-\Psi_{i^{\prime}}^{t}

-\Psi_{i}^{t}=-e^{-\alpha y_{i}^{t}}\leqslant-e^{-\alpha\cdot(y_{i^{\prime}}^{t}+d)}\leqslant-\Psi_{i^{\prime}}^{t}\cdot e^{-\alpha d}\leqslant-\Psi_{i^{\prime}}^{t}+\Psi_{i^{\prime}}^{t}\cdot 2\alpha d,

using that
\alpha\leqslant\frac{1}{d}
and that
e^{z}\leqslant 1+2z
for any
|z|\leqslant 1
. Hence,

-\alpha\cdot\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta_{i}\cdot\Psi_{i}^{t}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{\eta}_{i}\cdot\Psi_{i}^{t}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]\leqslant\Psi^{t}\cdot\frac{2\alpha^{2}d^{3}b}{n}.\qed

4.2.2
Properties of the Allocation Probabilities in
d
-
Weak-Memory

Our result bounding the expected potential drop for the
Memory
process (
4.1
) follows from a bound on the expected potential drop for the
d
-Weak-Memory
process. This is proved by applying
4.4
, which requires showing that the probability allocation vector of the process (or a proxy for it) induces a bias away from heavily loaded bins.

In this sub-section we gather several results on the proxy-allocation vector of
d
-Weak-Memory
that will allow us to apply
4.4
to prove a drop in the expected potential over one run. The first two results allow us to analyze the proxy-allocation vector of
d
-Weak-Memory
on a
(a,b)
-step distribution rather than an arbitrary
(a,b)
-biased distribution.

Recall that we say that a vector
v=(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n})

majorizes

u=(u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{n})
if for all
1\leqslant k\leqslant n
, the prefix sums satisfy:
\sum_{i=1}^{k}v_{i}\geqslant\sum_{i=1}^{k}u_{i}
.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
d\geqslant 1
,
t_{0}\geqslant 0
, and
a,b>1
be such that
M=n\cdot\frac{a-1}{ab-1}
is an integer. Let
\nu
be the
(a,b)
-step distribution and
\mu
be an any
(a,b)
-biased distribution. Then,
\sum_{k=1}^{i}\widehat{\eta}_{k}(\mu)\preceq\sum_{k=1}^{i}\widehat{\eta}_{k}(\nu)
holds for any
i\in[n]
. Thus,
\left(\mathbf{E}\left[\,\widehat{\eta}_{j}(\nu)\mid\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}}\,\right]\right)_{j\in[n]}
majorizes
\left(\mathbf{E}\left[\,\widehat{\eta}_{j}(\mu)\mid\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}}\,\right]\right)_{j\in[n]}
.

Proof.

We couple
\mathcal{N}
, an instance of
d
-Weak-Memory
on a
(a,b)
-step distribution
\nu=(\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{n})
, to
\mathcal{M}
, an instance of
d
-Weak-Memory
on the
(a,b)
-biased distribution
\mu=(\mu_{1},\cdots,\mu_{n})
. Both processes will start from the same initial load configuration
x^{t_{0}}
and ordering
\sigma
at time
t_{0}
.

We give a coupling
(s_{\nu},s_{\mu})
of the sampling distributions
s_{\nu}\sim\nu
and
s_{\mu}\sim\mu
as follows:

For each
k\in[n]
set
q_{k}=\min\{\nu_{k},\mu_{k}\}
and
q=\sum_{k=1}^{n}q_{k}
. Let
X\sim\operatorname{Ber}(q)
.

•

If
X=\mathsf{heads}
sample a bin
k\in[n]
with probability
q_{k}/q
and set
(s_{\nu},s_{\mu})=(k,k)
.

•

If
X=\mathsf{tails}
sample
U
uniformly from
[0,1]
. Set
s_{\nu}\in[n]
and
s_{\mu}\in[n]
to be the smallest integers satisfying
\sum_{k=1}^{s_{\nu}}(\nu_{k}-q_{k})/(1-q)\geqslant U
and
\sum_{k=1}^{s_{\mu}}(\mu_{k}-q_{k})/(1-q)\geqslant U
respectively.

To see that this is a valid coupling of
\nu
and
\mu
observe that for any
j\in[n]
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,s_{\nu}=j\,\right]=\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,X=\mathsf{heads}\,\right]\cdot\frac{q_{j}}{q}+\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,X=\mathsf{tails}\,\right]\cdot\frac{\nu_{j}-q_{j}}{1-q}=\nu_{j},

and similarly
\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,s_{\mu}=j\,\right]=\mu_{j}
. We then couple
\mathcal{N}
and
\mathcal{M}
ball by ball by, in each step, sampling
(s_{\nu},s_{\mu})
according to the coupling and giving bin sample
s_{\nu}
to
\mathcal{N}
and
s_{\mu}
to
\mathcal{M}
. We claim that, under the coupling above, at each step
t\leqslant d
of the run the following invariant holds:

(\mathcal{J}):\text{ $\mathcal{N}$ samples a bin that is higher in the ordering than the bin sampled by $\mathcal{M}$, i.e., $s_{\nu}\leqslant s_{\mu}$.}

It is clear that invariant
\mathcal{J}
holds at every step where
X=\mathsf{heads}
since then
s_{\nu}=s_{\mu}
, i.e., the same bin sample is given to both processes under the coupling.

Otherwise, if
X=\mathsf{tails}
, then for any
j\leqslant M
,

\sum_{k=1}^{j}\frac{\nu_{k}-q_{k}}{1-q}=\sum_{k=1}^{j}\frac{b/n-q_{k}}{1-q}\geqslant\sum_{k=1}^{j}\frac{\mu_{k}-q_{k}}{1-q}.

(4.6)

Likewise for any
j>M
, as in this case
q_{j}=\min\{\nu_{j},\mu_{j}\}=1/(an)
, we have

\sum_{k=j}^{n}\frac{\nu_{k}-q_{k}}{1-q}=\sum_{k=j}^{n}\frac{1/(an)-q_{k}}{1-q}=0\leqslant\sum_{k=j}^{n}\frac{\mu_{k}-q_{k}}{1-q},

and so since
\sum_{k=j}^{n}\frac{\mu_{k}-q_{k}}{1-q}=1
and
\sum_{k=j}^{n}\frac{\mu_{k}-q_{k}}{1-q}=1
, for any
j>M
we obtain

\sum_{k=1}^{j}\frac{\nu_{k}-q_{k}}{1-q}=1-\sum_{k=j+1}^{n}\frac{\nu_{k}-q_{k}}{1-q}\geqslant 1-\sum_{k=j+1}^{n}\frac{\mu_{k}-q_{k}}{1-q}=\sum_{k=1}^{j}\frac{\mu_{k}-q_{k}}{1-q},

(4.7)

where we take empty sums to be zero. Thus, by (
4.6
) and (
4.7
), if
X=\mathsf{tails}
then
s_{\nu}\leqslant s_{\mu}
.

Observe that, for any
i\in[n]
, if either process is given a bin sample
j\geqslant i
at some point then no further balls are allocated to bins
k<i
as there will be a bin in the cache that was lighter in the initial ordering. Thus
\sum_{k=1}^{i}\widehat{\eta}_{k}(\mu)\preceq\sum_{k=1}^{i}\widehat{\eta}_{k}(\nu)
holds for any
i\in[n]
, as
\mathcal{J}
holds at every step of the run. The second claim in the statement then follows from linearity of expectation and the definition of majorization.
∎

We now show that the proxy-allocation vector for
d
-Weak-Memory
on a step distribution is piece-wise non-decreasing.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
d\geqslant 1
,
t_{0}\geqslant 0
, and
a,b>1
be such that
M=n\cdot\frac{a-1}{ab-1}
is an integer. Let
\nu
be the
(a,b)
-step distribution and
\widehat{\eta}_{i}^{\,t}:=\widehat{\eta}_{i}^{\,t}(\nu)
, where
t\in[d]
. Then, for any
i,i^{\prime}\in[n]
satisfying either
i<i^{\prime}\leqslant M
or
M<i<i^{\prime}
we have
\widehat{\eta}_{i}^{\,t}\preceq\widehat{\eta}_{i^{\prime}}^{\,t}
for any
t\in[d]
.

Proof.

We shall couple two instances
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
of
d
-Weak-Memory
with the same
(a,b)
-step sampling distribution, initial load configuration, and ordering
\sigma
, for one run as follows:

Given a sequence
(b_{j})_{j\in[d]}
of bins, let
(b_{j}^{\prime})_{j\in[d]}
be such that if
b_{j}=i
then
b_{j}^{\prime}=i^{\prime}
, if
b_{j}=i^{\prime}
then
b_{j}^{\prime}=i
, and otherwise
b_{j}=b_{j}^{\prime}
. Then if
\mathcal{P}
is given
(b_{j})_{j\in[d]}
, we couple by giving the sequence
(b_{j}^{\prime})_{j\in[d]}
as input to
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
.

Observe that since either
i<i^{\prime}\leqslant M
or
M<i<i^{\prime}
, both bin
i
and
i^{\prime}
are sampled with the same probability. Thus the function taking
(b_{j})_{j\in[d]}
to
(b_{j}^{\prime})_{j\in[d]}
is a measure preserving bijection from the set of inputs to a single run in
d
-Weak-Memory
to itself, giving a coupling of
\mathcal{P}
to
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
.

Recall that
d
-Weak-Memory
does not update the loads within one run, so the ordering
\sigma
is fixed. Observe that if the cache contains the
\ell
-th bin, then no ball can be allocated to any bin
b<\ell
in any subsequent step in the same run. Consider the first time
\tau
at which
b_{\tau}\in\{i,i^{\prime}\}
. If
b_{t}>i^{\prime}
for any
t<\tau
then no balls are allocated to
i
or
i^{\prime}
during the whole run so the claim in the statement holds. If
i<\min_{t<\tau}b_{t}<i^{\prime}
then a ball is added to bin
i^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
at time
\tau
but not to bin
i
in
\mathcal{P}
(as it has a lower bin in the cache) so
\widehat{\eta}_{i}^{\,\tau}<\widehat{\eta}_{i^{\prime}}^{\,\tau}
and the claim in the statement holds as no further balls can be added to
i
. Otherwise, a single ball is added to bin
i
in
\mathcal{P}
and
i^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
at time
\tau
, thus
\widehat{\eta}_{i}^{\,\tau}=\widehat{\eta}_{i^{\prime}}^{\,\tau}
, and these bins occupy their respective caches. Going forward these bins are displaced from the cache if and only if a bin
b\notin\{i,i^{\prime}\}
strictly higher in the ordering is selected. Since
i^{\prime}>i
it follows that any time a ball is allocated to
i
it is also allocated to
i^{\prime}
, proving the result. ∎

The final result in this section determines the allocation probabilities of
d
-Weak-Memory
on a step distribution exactly.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
t_{0}\geqslant 0
,
d\geqslant 1
and
a,b>1
be such that
M=n\cdot\frac{a-1}{ab-1}
is an integer. Let
\nu
be the
(a,b)
-step distribution,
\sigma
be any ordering of the bins by load at time
t_{0}
, and
\widehat{p}_{i,j}:=\widehat{p}_{i,j}(\nu,t_{0},d,\sigma)
. Then, for any
1\leqslant j\leqslant d
, we have

\widehat{p}_{i,j}=\begin{cases}\frac{b}{n}\cdot\left(\frac{ib}{n}\right)^{j-1}\cdot\left[1-\frac{b}{n-b(i-1)}\cdot\left(1-\left(\frac{b(i-1)}{n}\right)^{d-j}\right)\right]&\text{ if }i\leqslant M,\\
\frac{a}{n}\cdot\left(1-\frac{a(n-i)}{n}\right)^{j-1}\cdot\left[1-\frac{a}{a(n-i+1)}\cdot\left(1-\left(1-\frac{a(n-i+1)}{n}\right)^{d-j}\right)\right]&\text{ if }M<i\leqslant n.\end{cases}

Proof.

Recall that under
\sigma
the bins are ordered by load so that a bin with heaviest load is at position
1
and lightest load is at position
n
. The
d
-Weak-Memory
process can only compare the loads at time
t
, in particular it makes all comparisons based on an ordering
\sigma
that is fixed throughout the run. Thus, all references to ‘most loaded’ etc in what follows are w.r.t.
\sigma
.

Observe that if the
i^{\prime}
-th most loaded bin, where
i^{\prime}>i
, is sampled then we can never place another ball in the
i
-th most loaded bin within the current run. Also note that if the
i
-th most loaded bin is in the cache then it will remain in the cache until a bin which is higher in the ordering is sampled. Let
e_{i}
,
f_{i}
,
g_{i}
and
h_{i}=1-g_{i}
be the probabilities that an element equal to, strictly lower than, lower than or equal to, or strictly greater than
i
, respectively, in the ordering is chosen. Then, we claim that for
1\leqslant j\leqslant d
we have

\widehat{p}_{i,j}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{d-j-1}f_{i}^{\ell}\cdot e_{i}\cdot g_{i}^{j-1}\cdot h_{i}+f_{i}^{d-j}\cdot e_{i}\cdot g_{i}^{j-1}.

(4.8)

To see this holds we first consider the second term; this is the case where only the last
j
balls are allocated to the
i
-th most loaded bin and none before this. Thus, the first
d-j
balls must be allocated to the
i-1
most loaded bins, which happens with probability
f_{i}^{d-j}
. Then we allocate to
i
followed by
j-1
samples of bins that are at least as high as
i
in the ordering, this occurs with probability
e_{i}\cdot g_{i}^{j-1}
. Turning to the sum; each summand corresponds the case where the block of
j
balls allocated to the
i
-th bin starts
\ell
places into the run. The first three terms in these probabilities are similar to before and then the
h_{i}
term is the probability we sample a bin higher than
i
in the ordering in the
(\ell+j+1)
-th step.

We can simplify (
4.8
) using a geometric series,

\widehat{p}_{i,j}=e_{i}\cdot g_{i}^{j-1}\cdot\left[h_{i}\cdot\sum_{\ell=0}^{d-j-1}f_{i}^{\ell}+f_{i}^{d-j}\right]=e_{i}\cdot g_{i}^{j-1}\cdot\left[h_{i}\cdot\frac{1-f_{i}^{d-j}}{1-f_{i}}+f_{i}^{d-j}\right].

Now, observing that
h_{i}=1-g_{i}=1-f_{i}-e_{i}
, we have

\widehat{p}_{i,j}=e_{i}\cdot g_{i}^{j-1}\cdot\left[(1-f_{i}-e_{i})\cdot\frac{1-f_{i}^{d-j}}{1-f_{i}}+f_{i}^{d-j}\right]=e_{i}\cdot g_{i}^{j-1}\cdot\left[1-\frac{e_{i}}{1-f_{i}}\cdot(1-f_{i}^{d-j})\right].

(4.9)

Recall
M:=\frac{n(a-1)}{ab-1}
from the definition of the
(a,b)
-step vector and observe that

\begin{array}[]{|l||c|c|c|}\hline\cr i&e_{i}&f_{i}&g_{i}\\
\hline\cr\hline\cr i\leqslant M&b/n&b(i-1)/n&bi/n\\
\hline\cr M<i<n&a/n&1-a(n-i+1)/n&1-a(n-i)/n\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

(4.10)

For
i\leqslant M
, by (
4.10
) and (
4.9
), we have

Lemma 4.10

Lemma 4.11
.

Consider any process
\mathcal{P}
satisfying the preconditions of
4.2
. For any step
t\geqslant 0
where
\Gamma_{1}^{t}\leqslant cn^{2\kappa+1}
, we have that

\displaystyle(i)

\displaystyle\qquad\Gamma_{2}^{t}\leqslant n^{4/3},

\displaystyle(ii)

\displaystyle\qquad|\Gamma_{2}^{t+1}-\Gamma_{2}^{t}|\leqslant n^{1/3}.

Proof.

Consider an arbitrary step
t
where
\Gamma_{1}^{t}\leqslant cn^{2\kappa+1}
. We start by proving the following bound on the normalised load
y_{i}^{t}
for any bin
i\in[n]

\Gamma_{1}^{t}\leqslant cn^{2\kappa+1}\Rightarrow e^{\alpha_{1}\cdot y_{i}^{t}}+e^{-\alpha_{1}\cdot y_{i}^{t}}\leqslant cn^{2\kappa+1}\Rightarrow y_{i}^{t}\leqslant\frac{3\kappa}{\alpha_{1}}\cdot\log n\,\wedge\,-y_{i}^{t}\leqslant\frac{3\kappa}{\alpha_{1}}\cdot\log n,

where in the second implication we used
\log c+\frac{2\kappa+1}{\alpha_{1}}\log n\leqslant\frac{3\kappa}{\alpha_{1}}\log n
, for sufficiently large
n
as

is a constant and
\kappa\geqslant 6\geqslant 1
.

First Statement.
Recall that
\alpha_{2}\leqslant\alpha_{1}/12\kappa
. By the definition of
\Gamma_{2}^{t}
and the bound on each normalised bin load, we get that

\Gamma_{2}^{t}\leqslant 2\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{n}\exp\Big(\alpha_{2}\cdot\frac{3\kappa}{\alpha_{1}}\cdot\log n\Big)=2n\cdot n^{1/4}\leqslant n^{4/3}.

Second Statement.
Consider
\Gamma_{2}^{t+1}
as a sum over
2n
exponentials, which is obtained from
\Gamma_{2}^{t}
by slightly changing the values of the
2n
exponents. The total
\ell_{1}
-change in the exponents is upper bounded by
4d

d
entries in the load vector
x^{t}
(and each of these entries appear twice), and we will also increment the average load by
\frac{d}{n}
in all
2n
exponents. Since
\exp(\cdot)
is convex, the largest change is upper bounded by the (hypothetical) scenario in which the largest exponent increases by

and all others remain the same,

\displaystyle\left|\Gamma_{2}^{t+d}-\Gamma_{2}^{t}\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\exp\Big(\alpha_{2}\cdot\big(4d+\max_{i\in[n]}|y_{i}^{t}|\big)\Big)\leqslant e^{4d\alpha_{2}}\cdot\exp\Big(\alpha_{2}\cdot\frac{3\kappa}{\alpha_{1}}\cdot\log n\Big)=e^{4d\alpha_{2}}\cdot n^{1/4}\leqslant n^{1/3},

\alpha_{2}\leqslant\alpha_{1}\leqslant 1/(2d)

Using the second and third statements in

Auxiliary process.

Let
\mathcal{P}

•

\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}

•

Let

(iii)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcap_{s\in[t_{0},m]}\left\{y_{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}^{s}=y^{s}\right\}\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcap_{s\in[t_{0},m]}\left\{\Gamma_{1}^{s}\leqslant cn^{2\kappa+1}\right\}\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-2\kappa}\cdot T_{r}.

(4.24)

The process
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{t_{0}}

•

(

) The

and
\Gamma_{2,\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}
(first and second preconditions) for any step

\mathcal{P}

).

Further, we define the event that the potential
\Gamma_{1}
is small at step

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}^{t_{0}}:=\left\{\Gamma_{1,\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}^{t_{0}}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}cn^{2\kappa+1}\right\},

(4.25)

where

is the constant defined in

.
When the event

holds, then the process
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{t_{0}}
also satisfies the following property (which “implements” the conditioning that

•

(
Property 2
) For any step

\Gamma_{1,\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}^{t_{0}}\leqslant cn^{2\kappa+1},

At any step

\sigma

and in every
n

n
bins with load equal to the minimum load (at step

\Gamma_{1,\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}^{t_{0}}\leqslant e^{\alpha_{1}}\cdot\Gamma_{1,\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}^{\sigma}\leqslant e^{\alpha_{1}}\cdot\frac{1}{2}cn^{2\kappa+1}\leqslant cn^{2\kappa+1}.

Lemma 4.12

(Recovery)
.

If

Since at any step
s

\Gamma_{2}^{s}\geqslant 2n
, we conclude that when

Lemma 4.13

(Stabilization)
.

Consider any step
t\geqslant 0
and the auxiliary process
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{t-T_{r}}:=\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{t-T_{r}}(\mathcal{P})
for any
\mathcal{P}
satisfying the preconditions of
4.2
and with
\mathcal{Z}^{t-T_{r}}
being the event defined in
Eq.

4.25

as defined in

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left.\bigcap_{s\in[r,t]}\left\{\Gamma_{2}^{s}\leqslant 3cn\right\}\cup\bigcup_{s\in(r,t]}\left(\bigcap_{u\in[r,s]}\left\{\Gamma_{2}^{u}\leqslant 3cn\right\}\cap\left\{\Gamma_{2}^{s}\leqslant cn\right\}\right)\,\,\right|\,\,\mathcal{Z}^{t-T_{r}},\mathfrak{F}^{r},\Gamma_{2}^{r}\in[cn,2cn]\,\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\geqslant 1-n^{-\frac{4}{3}\kappa}.

r\in[t-T_{r},t]
such that
\Gamma_{2}^{r}\in[cn,2cn]
. We define the stopping time

and for any step

The idea behind this definition is that

forms a super-martingale. To see this, note that by

(ii)
, for any step

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.X_{r}^{s+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{s},s<\tau\,\right]\leqslant\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\Gamma_{2}^{s+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{s},\Gamma_{2}^{s}\geqslant cn\,\right]\leqslant\Gamma_{2}^{s},

and for any step

\mathcal{Z}^{t-T_{r}}
holds, then by Property 2 (see
Section

.

•

and

. So the following event holds for

Hence, this concludes the claim.∎

The proof will be concerned with steps
\in[t-T_{r},t]
. First, by applying

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left.\bigcup_{r_{0}\in[t-T_{r},t]}\left\{\Gamma_{2}^{r_{0}}\leqslant cn\right\}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t-T_{r}},\mathcal{Z}^{t-T_{r}}\,\right]\geqslant 1-2n^{-2\kappa-1}.

Differences to Previous Applications:

These potentials are similar in form to the ones used in
[
20

for
\Theta(\log\log n)

[
21

for

z_{j}+1
, the potential
\Phi_{j}
will increase in expectation over one step. Considering rounds consisting of several balls, introduces several challenges:

•

Issue 1:
In each round we could allocate as many as
k_{j}
balls, which could be
\Omega(n^{\varepsilon})
. This would mean, that starting from a round
r_{0}
with
\Phi_{j}^{r_{0}}=\mathcal{O}(e^{\frac{1}{2}\log^{3}n})
and having a drop inequality similar to that in
[
20
,
21
]
, e.g.,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\overline{\Phi}_{j}^{r+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{r},\overline{\Phi}_{j-1}^{r}\leqslant Cn\,\right]\leqslant\overline{\Phi}_{j}^{r}\cdot\Big(1-\frac{1}{n}\Big)+2,

we may need
\Omega(n\log^{3}n)
rounds to prove that the potential becomes
\mathcal{O}(n)
in expectation. In these rounds, we could allocate
\Omega(n^{1+\varepsilon}\cdot\log^{3}n)
balls (
k_{j}\cdot\frac{v}{\alpha}
in each round) and so the length of the interval of the entire analysis would need to be
\omega(n\cdot\operatorname{polylog}(n))
. However, it would not be possible to tolerate a
\operatorname{poly}(n)
probability decrease in each layer of the layered induction (
5.13
), as we have
j_{\max}=\Theta(\log\log n)
layers.

Solution:
Define the potential function
\ddot{\Phi}_{j}
over just the bins with normalized load at least
z_{j}
. For this potential function, we can show that:

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\ddot{\Phi}_{j}^{r+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{r},\overline{\Phi}_{j-1}^{r}\leqslant 2Cn\,\right]\leqslant\ddot{\Phi}_{j}^{r}\cdot\Big(1-\frac{e^{v^{j+1}}}{n}\Big)+e^{-v^{j}}.

This means that starting from a round
r_{0}
with
\Phi_{j}^{r_{0}}=\mathcal{O}(e^{\frac{1}{2}\log^{3}n})
, we need to wait only for
n\cdot e^{-v^{j+1}}\cdot\log^{3}n
rounds, so at most
n\cdot\log^{6}n
steps (which is at most
\mathcal{O}(n\cdot\operatorname{polylog}(n))
), for the potential to become
\mathcal{O}(n)
.

•

Issue 2:
Unfortunately, for stabilization, i.e., showing that w.h.p.
\Phi_{j}^{s}=\mathcal{O}(n)
for
n\cdot\operatorname{polylog}(n)
steps, we cannot use just the partial potential function
\ddot{\Phi}_{j}
. The reason is that in a single round, the potential could change by
\Omega(n)
, so the bounded difference inequality cannot be applied.

Consider the case where there are
n\cdot e^{-v^{j}}
bins (for
j=1
), whose load is
z_{j}+\frac{k_{j}}{n}
. Then in a single round, we could allocate
k_{j}
balls only in light bins, so that the potential becomes
0
. Hence, the potential decreases by
n\cdot e^{-v^{j}}\cdot e^{\alpha_{2}k_{j}/n}=\Omega(n)
, for
j=1
. This means that we can no longer apply the concentration inequality, as the bounded difference condition is not strong enough.

Solution:
For this part of the analysis, we use the full potential
\overline{\Phi}_{j}
and a stopping time to guarantee that the number of balls allocated at every application of the concentration inequality is at most
n/\log^{2}n
. This allows us to apply the smoothness argument (
5.9
) to argue that the potential is
\mathcal{O}(n)
in every step of the interval.

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\ddot{\Psi}_{j}^{r+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{r},\mathcal{K}_{j-1}^{r}\,\right]\leqslant\ddot{\Psi}_{j}^{r}\cdot\Big(1-\frac{e^{v^{j+1}}}{n}\Big)+e^{-v^{j}/2}.

Proof.

Using
5.2
, we have that the number of bins
\ell\in[n]
with load
y_{\ell}^{r,s}\geqslant z_{j-1}+\frac{2v}{\alpha_{2}}
for all substeps
s
of round
r
is at most
\frac{1}{b}n\cdot e^{-v^{j}}
.

We will now analyze the expected contribution of a bin
i\in[n]
to the potential
\ddot{\Phi}_{j}^{r+1}
.

Case 1 [
y_{i}^{r,0}>z_{j}
]:
When we sample a
heavy
bin
\ell\in[n]
with
y_{\ell}^{r,s}\geqslant z_{j-1}+\frac{2v}{\alpha_{2}}
(happens with probability at most
\frac{b}{n}\cdot\frac{n}{b}\cdot e^{-v^{j}}=e^{-v^{j}}
) then the average increases by
1/n
. Otherwise, we sample a
light
bin, and a new phase begins. So, since bin
i
is heavy we have that

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\ddot{\Phi}_{j,i}^{r+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{r},\mathcal{K}_{j-1}^{r}\,\right]

\displaystyle\quad\leqslant\ddot{\Phi}_{j,i}^{r}\cdot\Big(\underbrace{\frac{b}{n}\cdot e^{\alpha_{2}v^{j}\cdot(1-1/n)}}_{\text{Bin $i$ is chosen}}+\underbrace{\Big(e^{-v^{j}}-\frac{b}{n}\Big)\cdot e^{-\alpha_{2}v^{j}/n}}_{\text{Another heavy bin is chosen}}+\underbrace{(1-e^{-v^{j}})\cdot\sum_{k=1}^{k_{j}}A_{k}}_{\text{A light bin is chosen}}\Big),

(5.2)

where
A_{k}
is the expected contribution to the potential if exactly
k
phases are executed. Each subsequent phase starts with probability at least
1-(e^{-v^{j}})^{v/\alpha_{2}}
. Hence, we have for
p=e^{-v^{j+1}/\alpha_{2}}\leqslant e^{-v^{j+1}}
(since
\alpha_{2}\leqslant 1
), by domination

\sum_{k=1}^{k_{j}}A_{k}\leqslant\sum_{k=1}^{k_{j}}\underbrace{\frac{(1-p)^{k-1}\cdot p}{1-(1-p)^{k_{j}}}}_{\text{Probability of exactly $k$ phases}}\cdot\underbrace{e^{-kv^{j+1}/n}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Change of $\ddot{\Phi}_{j,i}^{r}$ due}\\
\text{to change in average}\end{subarray}},

using that after
k
phases the average load changes by
\frac{kv}{n\alpha_{2}}
and that during a phase we don’t allocate to heavy bins, i.e., we won’t allocate to bin
i
(as it remains heavy). Plugging in
p=e^{-v^{j+1}}
, we obtain

\sum_{k=1}^{k_{j}}A_{k}\leqslant e^{-v^{j+1}/n}\cdot e^{-v^{j+1}}\cdot\frac{1}{1-(1-e^{-v^{j+1}})^{k_{j}}}\cdot\sum_{k=0}^{k_{j}-1}\Big((1-e^{-v^{j+1}})\cdot e^{-v^{j+1}/n}\Big)^{k}.

(5.3)

We first bound the sum on the right hand side of (
5.3
), as

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{k_{j}-1}\Big((1-e^{-v^{j+1}})\cdot e^{-v^{j+1}/n}\Big)^{k}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{1-(1-e^{-v^{j+1}})\cdot e^{-v^{j+1}/n}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leqslant}}\frac{1}{1-(1-e^{-v^{j+1}})\cdot(1-\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{v^{j+1}}{n})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{e^{-v^{j+1}}}\cdot\frac{1}{1-\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{v^{j+1}}{n}+\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{v^{j+1}}{n}\cdot e^{v^{j+1}}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\leqslant}}\frac{1}{e^{-v^{j+1}}}\cdot\frac{1}{1+\frac{1}{4}\cdot\frac{v^{j+1}}{n}\cdot e^{v^{j+1}}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(c)}}{{\leqslant}}\frac{1}{e^{-v^{j+1}}}\cdot\Big(1-\frac{1}{8}\cdot\frac{v^{j+1}}{n}\cdot e^{v^{j+1}}\Big),

(5.4)

using in
(a)
that
e^{z}\geqslant 1+\frac{1}{2}z
for
|z|<1
and that
v^{j+1}\leqslant v^{j_{\max}}\leqslant\log n=o(n)
(since
j+1\leqslant j_{\max}=\log_{v}(\frac{\alpha_{2}}{2v}\log n)
), in
(b)
that
e^{v^{j+1}}\geqslant 2
since
v^{j+1}\geqslant v\geqslant 36
, in
(c)
that
\frac{1}{1+z}\leqslant 1-\frac{z}{2}
for
0<z\leqslant 1
(
A.3
) and
\frac{1}{4}\cdot\frac{v^{j+1}}{n}\cdot e^{v^{j+1}}\leqslant\frac{1}{4}\cdot\log n\cdot n^{-1/2}
.

Applying the bound from (
5.2.1
) to (
5.3
) gives

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{k_{j}}A_{k}

\displaystyle\leqslant e^{-v^{j+1}/n}\cdot\frac{1}{1-(1-e^{-v^{j+1}})^{k_{j}}}\cdot\Big(1-\frac{1}{8}\cdot\frac{v^{j+1}}{n}\cdot e^{v^{j+1}}\Big)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leqslant}}e^{-\alpha_{2}v^{j}/n}\cdot\frac{1}{1-(1-e^{-v^{j+1}})^{k_{j}}}\cdot\Big(1-\frac{1}{8}\cdot\frac{v^{j+1}}{n}\cdot e^{v^{j+1}}\Big)

\displaystyle\leqslant e^{-\alpha_{2}v^{j}/n}\cdot\frac{1}{1-\exp\left(-e^{-v^{j+1}}\cdot k_{j}\right)}\cdot\Big(1-\frac{1}{8}\cdot\frac{v^{j+1}}{n}\cdot e^{v^{j+1}}\Big)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\leqslant}}e^{-\alpha_{2}v^{j}/n}\cdot\left(1+2\exp\left(-e^{-v^{j+1}}\cdot k_{j}\right)\right)\cdot\Big(1-\frac{1}{8}\cdot\frac{v^{j+1}}{n}\cdot e^{v^{j+1}}\Big)

\displaystyle=e^{-\alpha_{2}v^{j}/n}\cdot\left(1+2\exp\left(-\log^{3}n\right)\right)\cdot\Big(1-\frac{1}{8}\cdot\frac{v^{j+1}}{n}\cdot e^{v^{j+1}}\Big)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(c)}}{{\leqslant}}e^{-\alpha_{2}v^{j}/n}\cdot\Big(1-\frac{1}{9}\cdot\frac{v^{j+1}}{n}\cdot e^{v^{j+1}}\Big),

(5.5)

using in
(a)
that
\alpha_{2}\leqslant 1
and
v\geqslant 1
, in
(b)
that
\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}\leqslant 1+2\varepsilon
for
0<\varepsilon\leqslant 1/2
by
A.4
and in
(c)
that
2\exp\left(-\log^{3}n\right)=o(n^{-2})
, while
\frac{v^{j+1}}{n}\cdot e^{v^{j+1}}\geqslant\frac{1}{n}
.

Returning to
Section

5.2.1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\ddot{\Phi}_{j,i}^{r+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{r}\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\ddot{\Phi}_{j,i}^{r}\cdot e^{-\alpha_{2}v^{j}/n}\cdot\Big(\frac{b}{n}\cdot e^{\alpha_{2}v^{j}}+\Big(e^{-v^{j}}-\frac{b}{n}\Big)+(1-e^{-v^{j}})\cdot e^{\alpha_{2}v^{j}/n}\cdot\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}A_{k}\Big)

\displaystyle=\ddot{\Phi}_{j,i}^{r}\cdot e^{-\alpha_{2}v^{j}/n}\cdot\Big(1+\frac{b}{n}\cdot(e^{\alpha_{2}v^{j}}-1)+(1-e^{-v^{j}})\cdot\Big(e^{\alpha_{2}v^{j}/n}\cdot\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}A_{k}-1\Big)\Big)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:bddOnA_k}}}{{\leqslant}}\ddot{\Phi}_{j,i}^{r}\cdot e^{-\alpha_{2}v^{j}/n}\cdot\Big(1+\frac{b}{n}\cdot e^{\alpha_{2}v^{j}}-(1-e^{-v^{j}})\cdot\Big(\frac{1}{9}\cdot\frac{v^{j+1}}{n}\cdot e^{v^{j+1}}\Big)\Big)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leqslant}}\ddot{\Phi}_{j,i}^{r}\cdot e^{-\alpha_{2}v^{j}/n}\cdot\Big(1+\frac{b}{n}\cdot e^{\alpha_{2}v^{j}}-\frac{2b}{n}\cdot e^{v^{j+1}}\Big)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\leqslant}}\ddot{\Phi}_{j,i}^{r}\cdot\Big(1-\frac{e^{v^{j+1}}}{n}\Big),

using in
(a)
that
1-e^{-v^{j}}\geqslant 1-e^{-v}\geqslant 1/2
and
v^{j}\geqslant v\geqslant 36b
, and in
(b)
that
\alpha_{2}\leqslant 1
and
b\geqslant 1
.

Case 2 [
y_{i}^{r,0}\in(z_{j}-1+\frac{1}{n},z_{j}]
]:
For this case, we will argue about the aggregate contribution of all such bins
i
. In round
r
, we can allocate at most one ball to
i
, since
y_{i}^{r,0}>z_{j-1}+\frac{2v}{\alpha_{2}}
(which means that we can only allocate to this bin in the first substep). In this case the potential
\ddot{\Phi}_{j,i}^{r}
of said bin raises from
0
to at most
e^{\alpha_{2}v^{j}}
. This can only occur if we pick a heavy bin
\ell\in[n]
with load
y_{\ell}^{r}\geqslant z_{j-1}+\frac{2v}{\alpha_{2}}
, thus with probability at most
e^{-v^{j}}
. So, on aggregate we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell:y_{\ell}^{r,0}\in(z_{j}-1+\frac{1}{n},z_{j}]}\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\ddot{\Phi}_{j,\ell}^{r+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{r}\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant e^{\alpha_{2}v^{j}}\cdot e^{-v^{j}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leqslant}}e^{-v^{j}/2}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{=}}\sum_{\ell:y_{\ell}^{r,0}\in(z_{j}-1+\frac{1}{n},z_{j}]}\ddot{\Phi}_{j,\ell}^{r}\cdot\Big(1-\frac{e^{v^{j+1}}}{n}\Big)+e^{-v^{j}/2},

where inequality
(a)
holds since
\alpha_{2}\leqslant 1/2
and
(b)
as
\ddot{\Phi}_{j,\ell}^{r}=0
for each
\ell
with
y_{\ell}^{r,0}\in(z_{j}-1+\frac{1}{n},z_{j}]
.

Case 3(a) [
y_{i}^{r,0}\leqslant z_{j-1}+\frac{2v}{\alpha_{2}}
]:
In round
r
, we could allocate multiple balls to bin
i
, however its load will always remain at most
z_{j-1}+\frac{4v}{\alpha_{2}}<z_{j}
and so
\ddot{\Phi}_{j,i}^{r+1}=\ddot{\Phi}_{j,i}^{r}=0
.

Case 3(b) [
y_{i}^{r,0}\in(z_{j-1}+\frac{2v}{\alpha_{2}},z_{j}-1+\frac{1}{n}]
]:
In round
t
, we can allocate at most one ball to
i
, since
y_{i}^{r,0}>z_{j-1}+\frac{2v}{\alpha_{2}}
(which means that we can only allocate to this bin in the first step); so as in Case 3(a),
\ddot{\Phi}_{j,i}^{r+1}=\ddot{\Phi}_{j,i}^{r}=0
.

Hence, by aggregating over the three cases, we get the conclusion for
\ddot{\Phi}_{j}
. The same analysis also works for
\ddot{\Psi}_{j}
, as we only used that
\alpha_{2}\leqslant 1/2
(which also holds for
\alpha_{1}
) and
\alpha_{1}\geqslant\alpha_{2}
.
∎

This implies that when the potentials are large, we get a multiplicative expected drop.

Corollary 5.4
.

For any
1\leqslant j\leqslant j_{\max}-1
, and any round
r\geqslant 0
, we have that

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\ddot{\Psi}_{j}^{r+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{r},\ddot{\Psi}_{j}^{r}\geqslant 2n\,\right]\leqslant\ddot{\Psi}_{j}^{r}\cdot\Big(1-\frac{e^{v^{j+1}}}{2n}\Big),

and

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\ddot{\Phi}_{j}^{r+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{r},\ddot{\Phi}_{j}^{r}\geqslant 2n\,\right]\leqslant\ddot{\Phi}_{j}^{r}\cdot\Big(1-\frac{e^{v^{j+1}}}{2n}\Big).

Proof.

Using
5.3
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\ddot{\Psi}_{j}^{r+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{r},\ddot{\Psi}_{j}^{r}\geqslant 2n\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\ddot{\Psi}_{j}^{r}\cdot\Big(1-\frac{e^{v^{j+1}}}{n}\Big)+e^{-v^{j}/2}

\displaystyle=\ddot{\Psi}_{j}^{r}\cdot\Big(1-\frac{e^{v^{j+1}}}{2n}\Big)-\ddot{\Psi}_{j}^{r}\cdot\frac{e^{v^{j+1}}}{2n}+e^{-v^{j}/2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\ddot{\Psi}_{j}^{r}\cdot\Big(1-\frac{e^{v^{j+1}}}{2n}\Big)-e^{v^{j+1}}+e^{-v^{j}/2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\ddot{\Psi}_{j}^{r}\cdot\Big(1-\frac{e^{v^{j+1}}}{2n}\Big).

Working in exactly the same way for
\ddot{\Phi}_{j}
, we obtain the second claim.
∎

For the stabilization phase in the layered induction step (
5.13
), we make use of the following drop inequalities for the full
\overline{\Phi}_{j}
potential:

Lemma 5.5
.

For any
1\leqslant j\leqslant j_{\max}-1
, and any round

, we have that

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\overline{\Phi}_{j}^{r+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{r},\mathcal{K}_{j-1}^{r}\,\right]\leqslant\overline{\Phi}_{j}^{r}\cdot\Big(1-\frac{1}{n}\Big)+2,

and

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\overline{\Phi}_{j}^{r+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{r},\mathcal{K}_{j-1}^{r},\overline{\Phi}_{j}^{r}\geqslant 4n\,\right]\leqslant\overline{\Phi}_{j}^{r}\cdot\Big(1-\frac{1}{2n}\Big).

Proof.

For the first statement, the analysis proceeds similarly to
5.3
, with Case 1 being unchanged:

Case 2 [
y_{i}^{r,0}\in(z_{j}-1+\frac{1}{n},z_{j}]
]:
For this case, we will argue again about the aggregate contribution of all such bins
i
. In round
r
, we can allocate at most one ball to
i
, since
y_{i}^{r,0}>z_{j-1}+\frac{2v}{\alpha_{2}}
(which means that we can only allocate to this bin in the first step). In this case the potential
\overline{\Phi}_{j,i}^{r}
of said bin raises from
0
to at most
e^{\alpha_{2}v^{j}}
. This can only occur if we pick a bin
\ell\in[n]
with load

, thus with probability at most

. Thus we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i:y_{i}^{r,0}\in(z_{j}-1+\frac{1}{n},z_{j}]}\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\overline{\Phi}_{j,i}^{r+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{r}\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i:y_{i}^{r,0}\in(z_{j}-1+\frac{1}{n},z_{j}]}\overline{\Phi}_{j,i}^{r}+e^{\alpha_{2}v^{j}}\cdot e^{-v^{j}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leqslant}}\sum_{i:y_{i}^{r,0}\in(z_{j}-1+\frac{1}{n},z_{j}]}\overline{\Phi}_{j,i}^{r}+1

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{=}}\sum_{i:y_{i}^{r,0}\in(z_{j}-1+\frac{1}{n},z_{j}]}\left(\overline{\Phi}_{j,i}^{r}\cdot\Big(1-\frac{1}{n}\Big)+\frac{1}{n}\right)+1,

where inequality

holds since

and
(b)
as
\overline{\Phi}_{j,i}^{r}=1
for each
i
with
y_{i}^{r,0}\in(z_{j}-1+\frac{1}{n},z_{j}]
.

Case 3(a) [
y_{i}^{r,0}\leqslant z_{j-1}+\frac{2v}{\alpha_{2}}
]:
In round

, we could allocate multiple balls to bin

, however its load will always remain at most

5.2.2
Deterministic Relations between the Potential Functions

We collect several basic facts about the potential functions
\Phi_{j}^{s}
and
\Psi_{j}^{s}
related to the
Memory
process on an
(a,b)
-biased sampling distribution with parameters fixed by (
5.1
).

Claim 5.6
.

For any
1\leqslant j\leqslant j_{\max}-1
and for any step
s\geqslant 0
,

\Phi_{j-1}^{s}\leqslant 2Cn~~\Rightarrow~~\Psi_{j}^{s}\leqslant\exp\Big(\frac{1}{2}\cdot\log^{3}n\Big).

Proof.

Assuming
\Phi_{j-1}^{s}\leqslant 2Cn
implies that for any bin
i\in[n]
,

\displaystyle\Phi_{j-1,i}^{s}=\exp\Big(\alpha_{2}\cdot v^{j-1}\cdot\Big(y_{i}^{s}-\frac{5v}{\alpha_{2}}\cdot(j-1)\Big)^{+}\Big)\leqslant 2Cn.

By re-arranging we get

\displaystyle y_{i}^{s}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\log(2Cn)}{\alpha_{2}}\cdot v^{-(j-1)}+\frac{5v}{\alpha_{2}}\cdot(j-1)\leqslant\log^{1.5}n,

for sufficiently large
n
, since
j\leqslant j_{\max}=\mathcal{O}(\log\log n)
.

Hence, we can upper bound
\Psi_{j}^{s}
by

\displaystyle\Psi_{j}^{s}

\displaystyle\leqslant n\cdot\exp\Big(\alpha_{1}\cdot v^{j}\cdot\log^{1.5}n\Big)\leqslant\exp\Big(\frac{1}{2}\cdot\log^{3}n\Big),

for sufficiently large
n
, using that
v^{j}\leqslant v^{j_{\max}}\leqslant\log n
.
∎

The next claim bounds
\Phi_{j}
given that
\Psi_{j}=\operatorname{poly}(n)
.

Claim 5.7
.

For any
1\leqslant j\leqslant j_{\max}-1
and for any step
s\geqslant 0
, if
\Psi_{j}^{s}\leqslant Cn^{12}
, then for any bin
i\in[n]
, we have that
\Phi_{j,i}^{s}\leqslant n^{1/6}
and so by aggregating over all bins
\Phi_{j}^{s}\leqslant n^{7/6}
.

Proof.

We will begin by showing that when
\Psi_{j}^{s}\leqslant Cn^{12}
we have that for any bin
i\in[n]
,

y_{i}^{s}\leqslant z_{j}+\frac{14}{\alpha_{1}}\cdot(\log n)\cdot v^{-j}.

Assuming the contrary, i.e., that for some bin
i
,
y_{i}^{s}>z_{j}+\frac{14}{\alpha_{1}}\cdot(\log n)\cdot v^{-j}
, then we get
\Psi_{j}^{s}>\exp(\alpha_{1}\cdot\frac{14}{\alpha_{1}}\cdot\log n)=n^{14}
, which is a contradiction.

Next, we turn to upper bounding the contribution of any bin
i\in[n]
,

\displaystyle\Phi_{j,i}^{s}

\displaystyle=\exp\bigl(\alpha_{2}\cdot v^{j}\cdot\bigl(y_{i}^{s}-z_{j}\bigr)^{+}\bigr)

\displaystyle\leqslant\exp\Big(\alpha_{2}\cdot v^{j}\cdot\frac{14}{\alpha_{1}}\cdot(\log n)\cdot v^{-j}\Big)

\displaystyle=\exp\Big(\frac{14\cdot\alpha_{2}}{\alpha_{1}}\cdot\log n\Big)=n^{1/6},

(5.6)

since
\alpha_{1}=6\cdot 14\cdot\alpha_{2}
. Hence, by aggregating over all bins,

\Phi_{j}^{s}\leqslant n\cdot n^{1/6}=n^{7/6}.\qed

The next claim is crucial for applying the concentration inequality in
5.13
, since the third statement bounds the maximum additive change of
\overline{\Phi}^{r}
(assuming
\overline{\Psi}^{r}=\operatorname{poly}(n)
):

Claim 5.8
.

For any
1\leqslant j\leqslant j_{\max}-1
and for any round
r\geqslant 0
, if
\overline{\Psi}_{j}^{r}\leqslant Cn^{12}
, then
|\overline{\Phi}_{j}^{r+1}-\overline{\Phi}_{j}^{r}|\leqslant n^{1/3}
.

Proof.

We will start by obtaining lower and upper bounds for
\overline{\Phi}_{j}^{r+1}
. By
5.7
, in any round
r
with
\Psi_{j}^{r}\leqslant Cn^{12}
, we have that
\Phi_{j,i}^{r}\leqslant n^{1/6}
for each bin. Now, for the upper bound, note that in any round
r+1
we can allocate to at most one heavy bin. Let
i\in[n]
be that heavy bin, then

\overline{\Phi}_{j}^{r+1}\leqslant\overline{\Phi}_{j}^{r}+\overline{\Phi}_{j,i}^{r}\cdot\exp\bigl(\alpha_{2}\cdot v^{j}\bigr)\leqslant\overline{\Phi}_{j}^{r}+n^{1/6}\cdot n^{1/6}=\overline{\Phi}_{j}^{r}+n^{1/3},

using that
v^{j}\leqslant v^{j_{\max}}\leqslant\log n
,
\alpha_{2}\leqslant 1/6
by (
5.1
) and applying
Eq.

5.6
. For the lower bound, we pessimistically assume that all bin loads decrease by
1/n
in each step of the round
r+1
. So, since there are at most
k_{j}\cdot\frac{v}{\alpha_{2}}
steps

\overline{\Phi}_{j}^{r+1}\geqslant\overline{\Phi}_{j}^{r}\cdot\exp\Big(-\frac{v^{j+1}}{n}\cdot k_{j}\Big)\geqslant\overline{\Phi}_{j}^{r}\cdot\Big(1-\frac{v^{j+1}}{n}\cdot k_{j}\Big)\geqslant\Phi_{j}^{r}-\frac{n\cdot n^{1/6}}{n}\cdot(\log n)\cdot n^{1/7}\geqslant\overline{\Phi}_{j}^{r}-n^{1/3},

using that
e^{x}\geqslant 1+x
(for any
x
),
v^{j+1}\leqslant v^{j_{\max}}\leqslant\log n
,
\overline{\Phi}_{j}^{r}\leqslant n\cdot n^{1/6}
by
5.7
for
s=T(r)
and
k_{j}\leqslant n^{1/7}
. Combining the two bounds we get the statement.
∎

The next claim is a simple “smoothness” argument showing that the potential cannot decrease quickly within
n/\log^{2}n
steps. The derivation is elementary and relies on the fact that the average load does not change by more than
1/\log^{2}n
within these steps.

Claim 5.9
.

For any
1\leqslant j\leqslant j_{\max}-1
, any step
s\geqslant 0
and any step
u\in[s,s+n/\log^{2}n]
, we have
\Phi_{j}^{u}\geqslant 0.99\cdot\Phi_{j}^{s}
.

Proof.

The normalized load after
u-s
steps can decrease by at most
\frac{u-s}{n}\leqslant\frac{1}{\log^{2}n}
. Hence, for any bin
i\in[n]
,

\displaystyle\Phi_{j,i}^{u}

\displaystyle=e^{\alpha_{2}\cdot v^{j}\cdot(y_{i}^{u}-z_{j})^{+}}\geqslant e^{\alpha_{2}\cdot v^{j}\cdot(y_{i}^{s}-\frac{u-s}{n}-z_{j})^{+}}\geqslant e^{\alpha_{2}\cdot v^{j}\cdot(y_{i}^{s}-z_{j})^{+}-\alpha_{2}\cdot v^{j}\cdot\frac{1}{\log^{2}n}}=\Phi_{j,i}^{s}\cdot e^{-\frac{\alpha_{2}\cdot v^{j}}{\log^{2}n}}

\displaystyle\geqslant\Phi_{j,i}^{s}\cdot e^{-o(1)}\geqslant 0.99\cdot\Phi_{j,i}^{s},

for sufficiently large
n
, using that
v^{j}\leqslant v^{j_{\max}}\leqslant\log n
and
\alpha_{2}\leqslant 1
. By aggregating over all bins, we get the claim.
∎

The next claim relates the full and partial potentials defined at the start of Section
5
.

Claim 5.10
.

For any round
r\geqslant 0
with one or more phases we have that for any substep
s\geqslant 0
of round
r
,
(i)

\Phi_{j}^{r,s}\leqslant\overline{\Phi}_{j}^{r}
,
(ii)

\Psi_{j}^{r,s}\leqslant\overline{\Psi}_{j}^{r}

(iii)

\dot{\Phi}_{j}^{T(r)+s}\leqslant\ddot{\Phi}_{j}^{r}

\dot{\Psi}_{j}^{T(r)+s}\leqslant\ddot{\Psi}_{j}^{r}

Proof.

For all substeps of a round, we allocate to a bin that does not contribute to the potential. Hence, the potential can only decrease (because of the change of the average load).
∎

The first lemma proves that

.

Proof.

By

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\ddot{\Psi}_{j}^{r+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{r},\mathcal{K}_{j-1}^{r}\,\right]\leqslant\ddot{\Psi}_{j}^{r}\cdot\Big(1-\frac{e^{v^{j+1}}}{n}\Big)+e^{-v^{j}/2}.

(5.7)

Now we define the potential function for any round

-Weak-Memory

To upper bound

When

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\widetilde{\Psi}_{j}^{T_{\beta_{j-1}}(r)}\,\right]

5.3
Completing the Proof of Key Lemma (
5.13
)

The proof of
5.13
shares some of the ideas from the proof of
4.2
. However, there we could more generously take a union bound over the entire time-interval (consisting of
n\cdot\operatorname{polylog}(n)
steps) to ensure that the potential is indeed small everywhere with high probability. Here we cannot afford to lose a polynomial factor in the error probability, as the induction step has to be applied
j_{\max}=\Theta(\log\log n)
times. To overcome this, we will partition the time-interval into consecutive intervals of length
n/\log^{2}n
. Then, we will prove that at the end of each such interval the potential is small w.h.p., and finally use a simple smoothness argument to argue that the potential is small w.h.p. in
all
steps.

Lemma 5.13

(
Induction Step
)
.

Consider the
Memory

(a,b)
-biased sampling distribution, for constants
a,b\geqslant 1
. Then, for any step

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left.\bigcap_{s\in[t_{0},t+n\log^{8}n]}\bigcup_{u\in[s,s+n/\log^{2}n]}\left\{\Phi_{j}^{u}\leqslant(C+1)n\right\}~\right|~\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\Phi_{j}^{t_{0}}\leqslant Cn\,\right]\geqslant 1-\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{(\log n)^{11j}}{n^{4}}.

Applying the smoothness argument of
5.9
for each step
s
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left.\bigcap_{s\in[t_{0},t+n\log^{8}n]}\left\{\Phi_{j}^{s}\leqslant\frac{C+1}{0.99}\cdot n\right\}~\right|~\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\Phi_{j}^{t_{0}}\leqslant Cn\,\right]\geqslant 1-\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{(\log n)^{11j}}{n^{4}}.

Since
2C\geqslant\frac{C+1}{0.99}
(as
C\geqslant 6
) and
t_{0}\leqslant\beta_{j}
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left.\bigcap_{s\in[\beta_{j},t+n\log^{8}n]}\left\{\Phi_{j}^{s}\leqslant 2Cn\right\}~\right|~\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\Phi_{j}^{t_{0}}\leqslant Cn\,\right]\geqslant 1-\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{(\log n)^{11j}}{n^{4}}.

(5.15)

Now, the next step is to obtain a bound without the conditioning. To this end, we define the stopping time
\tau:=\inf\{t_{0}\geqslant\beta_{j-1}+n\log^{5}n\colon\Phi_{j}^{t_{0}}\leqslant Cn\}
. Then by a union bound,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\tau\leqslant\beta_{j}\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left(\bigcup_{s\in[\beta_{j-1}+n\log^{5}n,\beta_{j}]}\left\{\widetilde{\Phi}_{j}^{s}\leqslant Cn\right\}\right)\cap\bigcap_{s\in[\beta_{j-1}+n\log^{5}n,\beta_{j}]}\left\{\Phi_{j-1}^{s}\leqslant 2Cn\right\}\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcup_{s\in[\beta_{j-1}+n\log^{5}n,\beta_{j}]}\left\{\widetilde{\Phi}_{j}^{s}\leqslant Cn\right\}\,\right]-\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\neg\bigcap_{s\in[\beta_{j-1}+n\log^{5}n,\beta_{j}]}\left\{\Phi_{j-1}^{s}\leqslant 2Cn\right\}\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant 1-n^{-5}-\frac{(\log n)^{11(j-1)}}{n^{4}}\geqslant 1-\frac{2(\log n)^{11(j-1)}}{n^{4}},

where in the third inequality we used
5.12
and
Eq.

5.13
(since
\beta_{j}\leqslant t+n\log^{5}n
).

Finally, we get the conclusion by combining this with
Eq.

5.15
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcap_{s\in[\beta_{j},t+n\log^{8}n]}\left\{\Phi_{j}^{s}\leqslant 2Cn\right\}\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\sum_{t_{0}=\beta_{j-1}+n\log^{5}n}^{\beta_{j}}\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left.\bigcap_{s\in[\beta_{j},t+n\log^{8}n]}\left\{\Phi_{j}^{s}\leqslant 2Cn\right\}~\right|~\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\Phi_{j}^{t_{0}}\leqslant Cn\,\right]\cdot\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\tau=t_{0}\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\left(1-\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{(\log n)^{11j}}{n^{4}}\right)\cdot\left(1-\frac{2(\log n)^{11(j-1)}}{n^{4}}\right)

\displaystyle\geqslant 1-\frac{(\log n)^{11j}}{n^{4}}.\qed

5.4
Proof of Main Theorem (
1.1
) using
5.13

Theorem
1.1

(Restated)
.

Consider the
Memory
process with an
(a,b)
-biased sampling distribution, for any constants
a,b\geqslant 1
. Then there exists a constant
\kappa:=\kappa(a,b)>0
such that for every step
m\geqslant 1
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Gap(m)\leqslant\kappa\cdot\log\log n\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-3}.

Proof.

Consider first the case where
m\geqslant n\log^{8}n
and let
t=m-n\log^{8}n
. We will proceed by induction on the potential functions
\Phi_{j}
for
j=1,\ldots,j_{\max}-1
. The base case follows by
4.2
, since
\Phi_{0}^{s}\leqslant\Gamma^{s}
for all steps
s\in[t,t+n\log^{8}n]
. Hence,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcap_{s\in[t,m]}\left\{\Phi_{0}^{s}\leqslant 2Cn\right\}\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-4}.

For the induction step, we use
5.13
. After
j_{\max}=\Theta(\log\log n)
applications, we get

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcap_{s\in[t+\beta_{j_{\max}-1},m]}\left\{\Phi_{j_{\max}-1}^{s}\leqslant 2Cn\right\}\,\right]\geqslant 1-\frac{(\log n)^{11\cdot(j_{\max}-1)}}{n^{4}}\geqslant 1-n^{-3}.

When this event occurs, the gap at round
m
cannot be more than
z_{j_{\max}-1}+\frac{4v^{2}}{\alpha_{2}^{2}}
for some constant
\kappa:=\kappa(\alpha_{2},v)>0
, since otherwise

\displaystyle 2Cn\geqslant\Phi_{j_{\max}-1}^{m}

\displaystyle\geqslant\exp\Big(\alpha_{2}\cdot v^{j_{\max}-1}\cdot\frac{4v^{2}}{\alpha_{2}^{2}}\Big)

\displaystyle=\exp\Big(4\cdot v^{j_{\max}}\cdot\frac{v}{\alpha_{2}}\Big)=\exp\Big(4\cdot\frac{\alpha_{2}}{2v}\log n\cdot\frac{v}{\alpha_{2}}\Big)=\exp(2\cdot\log n)=n^{2},

which leads to a contradiction. Hence,
\Gap(m)\leqslant z_{j_{\max}-1}+\frac{2v^{2}}{\alpha_{2}^{2}}=\frac{5v}{\alpha_{2}}\cdot(\log_{v}\left(\frac{\alpha_{2}}{2v}\log n\right)-1)+\frac{2v^{2}}{\alpha_{2}^{2}}\leqslant\kappa\cdot\log\log n
, for some constant
\kappa:=\kappa(\alpha_{2},v),\kappa(a,b)>0
, so

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Gap(m)\leqslant\kappa\cdot\log\log n\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-3}.

The other case is
m<n\log^{8}n
, when some of the
\beta_{j}
’s of the analysis above will be negative. To fix this, consider a modified process. The modified process starts at round
n\log^{8}n-m
with an empty load vector. For any time
t\in[n\log^{8}n,-m]
, it allocates a ball of fractional weight
\frac{1}{n}
to each of the
n
bins. For
t\geqslant 1
, it works exactly as the original
Memory
process. Since the load vector is perfectly balanced at each step
t<0
, it follows that
\dot{\Psi}_{j}^{t}=\dot{\Phi}_{j}^{t}=0
(and
\Phi_{j}^{t}=\Psi_{j}^{t}=n
) deterministically. Since our proof relies only on upper bounds on the potential functions, these are trivially satisfied and hence the above analysis applies for the modified process. Further, as the relative loads of the modified process and the original process behave identically for
t\geqslant 1
, the statement follows.
∎

6.1
General Biased Sampling

Recall that

proves that, for any constants

, the

-biased distribution has an
\mathcal{O}(\log\log n)
gap w.h.p.. The following result shows that the gap remains bounded independently of

w.h.p. for
any

We start with the following simple lemma that allows us to apply
4.4
as we did in the proof of
4.1
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Consider any probability vector
p
satisfying condition
\mathcal{C}_{1}
for
(\delta,\varepsilon)
for some
\delta\leqslant 1/2
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. Then,
p
also satisfies
\mathcal{C}_{1}
for
(1/2,\varepsilon^{\prime})
with
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\varepsilon/n
.

Proof.

We will prove that
p
satisfies the
\mathcal{C}_{1}
condition for
(1/2,\varepsilon^{\prime})
with
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\varepsilon/n
. We consider the following cases for the index
k\in[n]
:

•

Case A [
1\leqslant k\leqslant\delta n
]:

\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}\leqslant(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\frac{k}{n}\leqslant\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{n}\right)\cdot\frac{k}{n}.

•

Case B [
\delta n<k\leqslant n/2
]:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}

\displaystyle\leqslant 1-\left(1+\varepsilon\cdot\frac{\delta}{1-\delta}\right)\cdot\left(1-\frac{k}{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{k}{n}+\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{1-\delta}\cdot\left(\frac{k}{n}-1\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leqslant}}\frac{k}{n}-\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{1-\delta}\cdot\frac{k}{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{k}{n}\cdot\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{1-\delta}\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\leqslant}}\frac{k}{n}\cdot\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{n}\right),

using in
(a)
that
k\leqslant n/2
and in
(b)
that
\delta/(1-\delta)\geqslant 1/n
since
\delta\geqslant 1/n
.

•

Case C [
n/2<k
]:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=k}^{n}p_{i}

\displaystyle\geqslant\left(1+\varepsilon\cdot\frac{\delta}{1-\delta}\right)\cdot\frac{n-k+1}{n}\geqslant\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{n}\right)\cdot\frac{n-k+1}{n}.\qed

Theorem
1.3

(Restated)
.

Consider the
Memory
process with any sampling distribution
s=(s_{i})_{i\in[n]}
satisfying
s_{\min}:=\min_{i\in[n]}s_{i}>0
. Then for every step
m\geqslant 1
we have

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Gap(m)\leqslant\frac{n^{8}}{s_{\min}^{10}}\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-1}.

Proof.

To begin, we observe the every distribution
s
satisfying
s_{\min}>0
is an
(a,b)
-biased distribution for
a=1/(s_{\min}n)
and
b\leqslant n(1-(n-1)s_{\min})
. Also observe that since every
(a,b)
-biased distribution is an
(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
-biased distribution for any
a^{\prime}\geqslant a
and
b^{\prime}\geqslant b
. Thus is suffices to consider
(a,b)
-biased distributions where
a=1/(s_{\min}n)
and
b\leqslant n(1-(n-1)s_{\min})
and we can assume both
a
and
b
are sufficiently large.

We proceed the same way as in the proof of
4.1
. In
4.9
, we showed that
(\widehat{p}_{i})_{i\in[n]}
satisfies condition
\mathcal{C}_{1}
for
\delta=M/n=\frac{a-1}{ab-1}
,
\varepsilon=1/2
when we pick
d=\left\lceil\frac{2b(ab-1)^{2}}{(b-1)^{2}(1-\varepsilon)}\right\rceil
. Since
(a,b)
are not-necessarily constants, we use
6.1
to obtain that
(\widehat{p}_{i})_{i\in[n]}
satisfies
\mathcal{C}_{1}
for (constant)
\delta^{\prime}=1/2
and
\varepsilon^{\prime}=1/n
. This will allow us to apply
4.4
which requires the quantile
\delta
to be constant. Observe that by (
4.20
) we can also take
K=2bd^{3}
.

As in the proof of
4.1
, applying
4.4
, we get that there exists a constant
c>0
such that the hyperbolic cosine potential
\Gamma
with smoothing parameter
\alpha\leqslant\min\{\frac{1}{d},\frac{\varepsilon^{\prime}\delta}{d\cdot 8K}\}
, for any step
s
such that
d
divides
s
, we have that

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\Gamma^{s}\,\right]\leqslant cn.

By Markov’s inequality, we have that

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Gamma^{s}\leqslant cn^{3}\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-2}.

Hence, since the gap can increase by at most
d
in an interval of

,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Gap(t)\leqslant d+\frac{\log(cn^{3})}{\alpha}\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-2}.

Recall that we can assume that

by the opening paragraph. Finally as

and
\delta=\frac{a-1}{ab-1}\geqslant 1/n
, we can choose

. Thus, as

, we have

d+\frac{\log(cn^{3})}{\alpha}=\mathcal{O}\left(bd^{4}\cdot n^{2}\log n\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{b^{5}(ab-1)^{10}}{(b-1)^{10}}\cdot n^{3}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(a^{10}b^{5}\cdot n^{3}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{n^{8}}{s_{\min}^{10}}\right),

which concludes the claim.
∎

6.2
Weighted 2-

We consider the weighted setting for
2
-
Weak-Memory
. Following
[
28
]
, the weight of each ball will be drawn independently from a fixed distribution
W
over
[0,\infty)
, satisfying:

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[\,W\,]=1.

(6.1)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[\,e^{\lambda W}\,]<\infty\text{ for some constant }\lambda>0.

(6.2)

It is clear that when
\mathbf{E}[\,W\,]=\Theta(1)
, by scaling

\mathbf{E}[\,W\,]=1
.
Specific examples of distributions satisfying above conditions (after scaling) are the geometric, exponential, binomial and Poisson distributions.

We will make use of the following lemma:

Lemma 6.2

(Lemma 2.4 in
[
19
]
)
.

For any random variable
W
satisfying (
6.1
) and (
6.2
), there exists
S:=S(\lambda)\geqslant\max(1,1/\lambda)
, such that for any
\alpha\in(0,\min(\lambda/2,1))
and any

,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,e^{\alpha\cdot\kappa\cdot W}\,\right]\leqslant 1+\alpha\cdot\kappa+S\alpha^{2}\cdot\kappa^{2}.

We can now state and prove the result of this subsection.

Theorem
1.5

(Restated)
.

Consider the
2
-
Weak-Memory
process with a uniform sampling distribution. Further, assume the weight of each ball is drawn independently from a distribution
W
satisfying
\mathbf{E}[\,W\,]=1
and
\mathbf{E}[\,e^{\lambda W}\,]<\infty
for a constant
\lambda>0
.
Then there exists a constant
\kappa:=\kappa(W)>0
such that for every step
m\geqslant 1
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Gap(m)\leqslant\kappa\cdot\log n\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-2}.

Proof.

Consider any even step
2t\geqslant 0
for the
2
-
Weak-Memory
process and let
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
be the weights of the
(2t+1)
-th and
(2t+2)
-th balls. Then, with probability:

•

q_{11}=\frac{1}{n}\cdot\frac{i}{n}
: we allocate both balls to the
i
-th most loaded bin.

•

q_{01}=\frac{i-1}{n}\cdot\frac{1}{n}
: we allocate the second ball to the
i
-th most loaded bin.

•

q_{10}=\frac{1}{n}\cdot\left(1-\frac{i}{n}\right)
: we allocate the first ball to the
i
-th most loaded bin.

•

q_{00}=1-q_{11}-q_{01}-q_{10}
: we allocate none of the two balls to the
i
-th most loaded bin.

We now consider the expected change of the
\Phi
potential over two steps

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\Phi^{2t+2}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{2t}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\Phi^{2t}\cdot\Big(q_{00}\cdot\mathbf{E}\left[\,e^{-\alpha(W_{1}+W_{2})/n}\,\right]+q_{10}\cdot\mathbf{E}\left[\,e^{\alpha W_{1}(1-1/n)}\,\right]\cdot\mathbf{E}\left[\,e^{-\alpha W_{2}/n}\,\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+q_{01}\cdot\mathbf{E}\left[\,e^{-\alpha W_{1}/n}\,\right]\cdot\mathbf{E}\left[\,e^{\alpha W_{2}(1-1/n)}\,\right]+q_{11}\cdot\mathbf{E}\left[\,e^{\alpha(W_{1}+W_{2})(1-1/n)}\,\right]\Big)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leqslant}}\Phi^{2t}\cdot\Big(1+\alpha\cdot\Big(-q_{00}\cdot\frac{2}{n}+q_{10}\cdot\Big(1-\frac{2}{n}\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+q_{01}\cdot\Big(1-\frac{2}{n}\Big)+q_{11}\cdot\Big(2-\frac{2}{n}\Big)\Big)+6S\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\Big)

\displaystyle=\Phi^{2t}\cdot\Big(1+\alpha\cdot\Big(\frac{i-1+n-i+2i}{n^{2}}-\frac{2}{n}\Big)+6S\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\Big)

\displaystyle=\Phi^{2t}\cdot\Big(1+\alpha\cdot\Big(\frac{2i-1}{n^{2}}-\frac{1}{n}\Big)+6S\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\Big),

using
6.2
in
(a)
, for some constant
S:=S(\lambda)\geqslant\max(1,1/\lambda)
.

Similarly, we have

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\Psi^{2t+2}\right|\mathfrak{F}^{2t}\,\right]\leqslant\Psi^{2t}\cdot\Big(1+\alpha\cdot\Big(\frac{2i-1}{n^{2}}-\frac{1}{n}\Big)+6S\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\Big).

By noticing that
p_{i}=\frac{2i-1}{n^{2}}
is the
Two-Choice
probability allocation vector, by
[
19
, Proposition 2.3]
it satisfies condition
\mathcal{C}_{1}
with
\delta=\frac{1}{4}
and
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{2}
. So, applying
4.4
at even indices, there exists a constant
c>0
such that for
\alpha=\frac{1}{384S}

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\Gamma^{2t+2}\,\right]\leqslant\Gamma^{2t}\cdot\Big(1-\frac{\alpha}{384n}\Big)+c\cdot\alpha,

implying that for any step
2t\geqslant 0
,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\Gamma^{2t}\,\right]\leqslant 384c\cdot n.

By using Markov’s inequality,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Gap(2t)\leqslant\frac{1}{\alpha}\cdot\left(3\log n+\log(384c)\right)\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-2}.

Let
t:=\lceil m/2\rceil
. If
m=2t
, then we are done, otherwise the
(2t)
-th ball is w.h.p.
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
, because the MGF is constant. Hence, in that last step the gap cannot change by more than
\mathcal{O}(\log n/n)
and hence, we deduce the claim for
\kappa:=4/\alpha
.
∎

7
Proofs of the Lower Bounds

Our first result gives a lower bound on the gap of the
Memory

Theorem
1.2

(Restated)
.

Consider the
Memory
process with a uniform sampling distribution. Then there is a constant
\kappa>0
such that for every step

,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Gap(m)\geqslant\kappa\cdot\log\log n\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-1}.

On a high level, the proof of this theorem follows the layered induction argument used by
[
4
]
to lower bound the gap of
Two-Choice
in the lightly loaded case. However, for the
Memory
process in the heavily loaded case, we require some additional arguments to bootstrap the induction and also deal with correlations introduced by the cache.

7.1
Proof of Theorem
1.2

We first provide some general definitions and notations, used in the proof. Recall the definition of the hyperbolic cosine potential from
Section

4.1
:

\Gamma^{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{\alpha y_{i}^{t}}+e^{-\alpha y_{i}^{t}}.

The starting point of the proof is round
t_{0}:=m-n
.
For convenience we may assume that
m
is a multiple of
n
. This holds since if
m
is not a multiple of
n
, we may simply apply the theorem to the largest
\widetilde{m}\leqslant m
which is a multiple of
n
, and deduce the same gap bound as the (normalized) load of any bin can only decrease by at most
1
within
n
steps.

We will now divide the interval
[t_{0},m]
into consecutive phases, labeled
1
to
j_{\max}:=\varepsilon\cdot\log\log n
for a sufficiently small constant
\varepsilon:=1/(2\log C)
, where
C:=100
. Each phase lasts for
\ell:=n/j_{\max}
steps. Further, associated to each phase
j\geqslant 1
, we define the set of bins

B_{j}:=\left\{i\in[n]\colon x_{i}^{t_{0}+j\cdot\ell}\geqslant\frac{t_{0}}{n}+L+j\right\},

for some constant
L
to be defined below and we define the event

\mathcal{E}_{j}:=\left\{\left|B_{j}\right|\geqslant\frac{n}{(\log n)^{C^{j}}}\right\}.

We also define the events
\mathcal{F}:=\{\Gamma^{t_{0}}\leqslant 6cn\}
and
\mathcal{L}=\{y_{\min}^{t_{0}}\geqslant-\widetilde{c}\cdot\log n\}
for some constants
c,\widetilde{c}>0
. The key step in the proof of Theorem
1.2
is to establish the following two claims about the events
\mathcal{E}_{j}
, where
0\leqslant j\leqslant j_{\max}
.

Claim 7.1
.

We have that
\mathcal{L}\cap\mathcal{E}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
and
\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathcal{F}\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-2}
.

Claim 7.2
.

For any
j\in[1,j_{\max}]
we have
\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathcal{E}_{j}\,\left|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}+\ell\cdot(j-1)},\;\cap_{k=0}^{j-1}\mathcal{E}_{k},\;\mathcal{F}\right.\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-\omega(1)}
.

Before establishing these two claims, we complete the proof of
1.2
.

Proof of
1.2
(assuming
7.1
and
7.2
hold).

First observe that for any
1\leqslant j\leqslant j_{\max}
, if the event
\mathcal{E}_{j}
holds then

\displaystyle|B_{j}|\geqslant\frac{n}{(\log n)^{C^{j}}}\geqslant\frac{n}{(\log n)^{C^{j_{\max}}}}\geqslant\frac{n}{(\log n)^{\sqrt{\log n}}}\geqslant\sqrt{n}.

(7.1)

Thus if
\mathcal{E}_{j_{\max}}
holds, there is a bin

with

, hence

y_{i}^{m}=x_{i}^{m}-\frac{m}{n}\geqslant\frac{t_{0}}{n}+L+j_{\max}-\frac{m}{n}=L+j_{\max}-1=\Omega(\log\log n),

as
|L|=\mathcal{O}(1)
. It remains to lower bound
\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathcal{E}_{j_{\max}}\,\right]
. Using
7.2
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathcal{E}_{j_{\max}}\,\mid\,\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\mathcal{F}\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left.\bigcap_{k=0}^{j_{\max}}\mathcal{E}_{k}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\mathcal{F}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathcal{E}_{j_{\max}}\,\left|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\mathcal{F},\bigcap_{k=0}^{j_{\max}-1}\mathcal{E}_{k}\right.\,\right]\cdot\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left.\bigcap_{k=0}^{j_{\max}-1}\mathcal{E}_{k}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\mathcal{F}\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant(1-n^{-\omega(1)})^{j_{\max}}

\displaystyle=1-n^{-\omega(1)}.

Further, as
\mathcal{F}
is measurable with respect to
\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}}
and
\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathcal{F}\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-2}
by

,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathcal{E}_{j_{\max}}\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathcal{E}_{j_{\max}}\cap\mathcal{F}\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathcal{F}\,\right]\cdot\mathbf{E}\left[\,\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathcal{E}_{j_{\max}}\,\mid\,\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\;\mathcal{F}\,\right]\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant(1-n^{-2})\cdot\left(1-n^{-\omega(1)}\right)

\displaystyle\geqslant 1-n^{-1},

which completes the proof.
∎

It remains to prove
7.1
and
7.2
. First we establish
7.1
, which concerns the event
\mathcal{E}_{0}
.

Proof of
7.1
.

By
4.3
, there exist some constants
c,\alpha>0
such that for
\Gamma:=\Gamma(\alpha)
and
\mathcal{F}:=\left\{\Gamma^{t_{0}}\leqslant 6cn\right\}
we have

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathcal{F}\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-2}.

Furthermore, for convenience, in the following we can assume that
c
in the definition of
\mathcal{F}
satisfies
c\geqslant 1
.
By the definition of
\Gamma
, the event
\mathcal{F}
implies that
y_{\min}^{t_{0}}\geqslant-\widetilde{c}\cdot\log n
for some constant
\widetilde{c}>0
, that is
\mathcal{L}\supseteq\mathcal{F}
. By a first moment argument,
\mathcal{F}
also implies that there exist constants
c_{1}>0
and
c_{2}>0
such that at time

, there are at least
c_{1}\cdot n
bins with a load in
[-c_{2},+c_{2}]
. Hence by the pigeonhole principle, there is a load threshold
L\in[-c_{2},c_{2}]
such that the number of bins
i
with
y_{i}^{t_{0}}=L
(equivalently,
x_{i}^{t_{0}}=\frac{t_{0}}{n}+L
) is at least
c_{1}/(2c_{2}+1)\cdot n
. Note that
L
may be positive or negative (or zero), all we need is that it is in the interval
[-c_{2},c_{2}]
. Let us define
B_{0}:=\left\{i\in[n]\colon x_{i}^{t_{0}}=\frac{t_{0}}{n}+L\right\}
; so
|B_{0}|\geqslant c_{3}\cdot n\geqslant\frac{n}{(\log n)^{C}}
for the constant
c_{3}:=c_{1}/(2c_{2}+1)
.
∎

Next we prove the more involved induction step from
j-1
to
j
:

Proof of
7.2
.

In order to establish this key inequality, we will start the analysis from step
t_{0}+\ell\cdot(j-1)
onwards (the first step of phase
j
), and assume for this step an arbitrary load (and cache) configuration such that
\mathcal{L}\cap\bigcap_{k=0}^{j-1}\mathcal{E}_{k}
holds.

Consider any step
s
in phase
j
. Since
\mathcal{E}_{j-1}
holds, we have

\left|\left\{i\in[n]\colon x_{i}^{s}\geqslant\frac{t_{0}}{n}+L+(j-1)\right\}\right|\geqslant\frac{n}{(\log n)^{C^{j-1}}}.

Further, we may assume that

\displaystyle\left|\left\{i\in[n]\colon x_{i}^{s}\geqslant\frac{t_{0}}{n}+L+j\right\}\right|<\frac{n}{(\log n)^{C^{j}}},

(7.2)

since otherwise
\mathcal{E}_{j}
holds, and we are done. Combining the last two inequalities yields,

\displaystyle\left|\left\{i\in[n]\colon x_{i}^{s}=\frac{t_{0}}{n}+L+(j-1)\right\}\right|

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{n}{(\log n)^{C^{j-1}}}-\frac{n}{(\log n)^{C^{j}}}\geqslant\frac{n}{2(\log n)^{C^{j-1}}}.

(7.3)

Regarding the bin
b^{s}
in the cache at step
s
, we can deduce that
x_{b^{s}}^{s}\geqslant x_{\min}^{s}\geqslant x_{\min}^{t_{0}}\geqslant\frac{t_{0}}{n}-\widetilde{c}\cdot\log n
, since we are assuming that the event
\mathcal{L}
holds.

Recall that whenever in a round
r
the load of the sampled bin
i^{r}
is strictly greater than the load of the cached bin
b^{r}
, we allocate the ball to the cached bin (and do not update the cache). Further, if the sampled load is at least the load of the cache, then we necessarily increment one bin with that load.
With this in mind, define the stopping time

\rho:=\min\left\{r\geqslant s\;\colon\;x_{b^{s}}^{r}\geqslant\varphi(j)\right\},

where
\varphi(j):=\frac{t_{0}}{n}+L+(j-1)
. Note that

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\rho\leqslant\varphi(j)-x_{b^{s}}^{s}\,\left|\,\mathfrak{F}^{s},\;\cap_{k=0}^{j-1}\mathcal{E}_{k},\;\mathcal{F}\right.\,\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\geqslant\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcap_{r=s}^{\varphi(j)-x_{b^{s}}^{s}}\left\{x_{i^{r}}^{r}\geqslant x_{b^{s}}^{s}+(r-s)\right\}~\bigg|~\mathfrak{F}^{s},\;\cap_{k=0}^{j-1}\mathcal{E}_{k},\;\mathcal{F}\,\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\geqslant\prod_{r=s}^{\varphi(j)-x_{b^{s}}^{s}}\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,x_{i^{r}}^{r}\geqslant x_{b^{s}}^{s}+(r-s)~\bigg|~\mathfrak{F}^{r-1},\;\cap_{k=0}^{j-1}\mathcal{E}_{k},\;\mathcal{F}\,\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\geqslant\prod_{\lambda=x_{b_{s}}^{s}-\frac{t_{0}}{n}}^{L+j-1}p(\lambda).

(7.4)

where

p(\lambda):=\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,x_{i^{\frac{t_{0}}{n}+\lambda-x_{b_{s}}^{s}}}^{\frac{t_{0}}{n}+\lambda-x_{b_{s}}^{s}}\geqslant\frac{t_{0}}{n}+\lambda~\bigg|~\mathfrak{F}^{r-1},\;\cap_{k=0}^{j-1}\mathcal{E}_{k},\;\mathcal{F}\,\right];

the important thing to remember is lower bounding
p(\lambda)
means that we are lower bounding the probability of sampling a bin whose load is at least
\frac{t_{0}}{n}+\lambda
(conditioned on the previous
j-1
layers of “good” events).
Recall that since we assume that
\mathcal{F}:=\left\{\Gamma^{t_{0}}\leqslant 6cn\right\}
holds, we infer that for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,

\left|\left\{i\in[n]\colon y_{i}^{t_{0}}<\lambda\right\}\right|\leqslant 6cn\cdot e^{\alpha\lambda},

and this implies that for any future step
r\geqslant t_{0}
,

\left|\left\{i\in[n]\colon x_{i}^{r}<\frac{t_{0}}{n}+\lambda\right\}\right|\leqslant\left|\left\{i\in[n]\colon x_{i}^{t_{0}}<\frac{t_{0}}{n}+\lambda\right\}\right|\leqslant 6cn\cdot e^{\alpha\lambda}.

Hence for any
r\geqslant s
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left.x_{i^{r}}^{r}\geqslant\frac{t_{0}}{n}+\lambda\,\,\right|\,\,\mathfrak{F}^{r-1},\;\cap_{k=0}^{j-1}\mathcal{E}_{k},\mathcal{F}\,\right]\geqslant 1-6c\cdot e^{\alpha\lambda}.

(7.5)

Secondly, we have an alternative estimate based on the fact that
\mathcal{F}
implies
|B_{0}|\geqslant c_{3}\cdot n
. For any
\lambda\leqslant L
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left.x_{i^{r}}^{r}\geqslant\frac{t_{0}}{n}+\lambda\,\,\right|\,\,\mathfrak{F}^{r-1},\;\cap_{k=0}^{j-1}\mathcal{E}_{k},\;\mathcal{F}\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,i^{r}\in B_{0}\,\left|\,\mathfrak{F}^{r-1},\;\cap_{k=0}^{j-1}\mathcal{E}_{k},\;\mathcal{F}\right.\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{|B_{0}|}{n}\geqslant c_{3}>0.

(7.6)

Finally, for any
1\leqslant\lambda<j
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left.x_{i^{r}}^{r}\geqslant\frac{t_{0}}{n}+L+\lambda\,\,\right|\,\,\mathfrak{F}^{r-1},\cap_{k=0}^{j-1}\mathcal{E}_{k},\mathcal{F}\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{|B_{\lambda}|}{n}\geqslant(\log n)^{-C^{\lambda}}.

(7.7)

We will now apply
Eq.

7.5
,
Eq.

7.6
and
Eq.

7.7
in order to lower bound
Eq.

7.4
. For simplicity, let us assume that
L
is positive (the case where
L
is negative is similar, and we obtain an even stronger lower bound on the probability). Returning to the product in
Eq.

7.4
where the load threshold ranges from
x_{b_{s}}^{s}
to
L+j
, we group the load values into three parts:
(i)
from
x_{b_{s}}^{s}
to
-c_{4}
;
(ii)
from
-c_{4}+1
to
L
and
(iii)
from
L+1
to
L+j
, where the constant
c_{4}>0
as the smallest integer such that:

6c\cdot\sum_{z=c_{4}}^{\infty}e^{-\alpha(z-1)}\leqslant 1/2.

Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\rho\leqslant\varphi(j)-x_{b^{s}}^{s}\,\left|\,\mathfrak{F}^{s},\;\cap_{k=0}^{j-1}\mathcal{E}_{k},\;\mathcal{F}\right.\,\right]

\displaystyle=\prod_{\lambda=x_{b_{s}}^{r}-\frac{t_{0}}{n}}^{-c_{4}}p(\lambda)\cdot\prod_{\lambda=-c_{4}+1}^{L}p(\lambda)\cdot\prod_{\lambda=L+1}^{L+j-1}p(\lambda)

\displaystyle\geqslant\prod_{\lambda=x_{b_{s}}^{r}-\frac{t_{0}}{n}}^{-c_{4}}\left(1-6c\cdot e^{\alpha\lambda}\right)\cdot c_{3}^{L+c_{4}}\cdot\prod_{\lambda=1}^{j-1}(\log n)^{-C^{\lambda}},

where we have lower bounded the first product by
Eq.

7.5
, the second by
Eq.

7.6
and the third by
Eq.

7.7
. Further estimating this lower bound yields,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\rho\leqslant\varphi(j)-x_{b^{s}}^{s}\,\left|\,\mathfrak{F}^{s},\;\cap_{k=0}^{j-1}\mathcal{E}_{k},\;\mathcal{F}\right.\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\left(1-6c\cdot\sum_{z=c_{4}}^{\infty}e^{-\alpha(z-1)}\right)\cdot c_{3}^{L+c_{4}}\cdot(\log n)^{-\sum_{\lambda=1}^{j-1}C^{\lambda}},

\displaystyle\geqslant\left(1-6c\cdot\sum_{z=c_{4}}^{\infty}e^{-\alpha(z-1)}\right)\cdot c_{3}^{L+c_{4}}\cdot(\log n)^{-C^{j+1/3}},

\displaystyle\geqslant 4\cdot(\log n)^{-C^{j+2/3}},

where we have used the fact that
C=100
and the first and second factors in the penultimate line are both constants that are strictly greater than
0
.

Note that
\varphi(j)-x_{b_{s}}^{s}\leqslant c\log n+L+j-1\leqslant 2c\log n
, and therefore,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\rho\leqslant 2c\log n\,\left|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t},\mathcal{E}_{0}\right.\,\right]\geqslant 4\cdot(\log n)^{-C^{j+2/3}}.

(7.8)

Hence from any step
s
in phase
j
, with probability at least
4\cdot(\log n)^{-C^{j+2/3}}
after at most
2c\log n
additional steps, we reach the situation where the bin in the cache has load at least
x_{b_{r}}^{r}\geqslant\varphi(j)=\frac{t_{0}}{n}+L+j-1
.

Recall that by
Eq.

7.2
we assumed that we have at least
\frac{n}{2(\log n)^{C^{j}}}
bins with load
\frac{t_{0}}{n}+L+(j-1)
(see
Eq.

7.3
), this means that with probability at least
\frac{1}{2(\log n)^{C^{j}}}
the next sampled bin has load
\frac{t_{0}}{n}+L+(j-1)
, and thus one bin load reaches

\frac{t_{0}}{n}+L+j
.

Let
X_{1},X,\ldots,X_{z}
with
z:=n/(j_{\max}\cdot(2c\log n+1))
be independent Bernoulli random variables with success probability

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\rho\leqslant 2c\log n\,\left|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t},\mathcal{E}_{0}\right.\,\right]\cdot\frac{1}{2(\log n)^{C^{j}}}\geqslant 4\cdot(\log n)^{-C^{j+2/3}}\cdot\frac{1}{2(\log n)^{C^{j}}}\geqslant 2\cdot(\log n)^{-C^{j+3/4}},

where the first inequality holds by (
7.8
) and the last holds for large
n
since
C=100
. Let
X=\sum_{i=1}^{z}X_{i}
. Then, again since we fixed
C=100
and can assume that

is large, we have

\mathbf{E}\left[\,X\,\right]:=\frac{n}{j_{\max}(2c\log n)}\cdot\frac{2}{(\log n)^{C^{j+3/4}}}\geqslant 2\cdot\frac{n}{(\log n)^{C^{j+1}}}.

Since
j\leqslant j_{\max}
, and
j_{\max}\leqslant\varepsilon\cdot\log\log n
,
\varepsilon=1/(2\log C)
, we have
\mathbf{E}\left[\,X\,\right]=\Omega(\operatorname{poly}(n))
(due to

7.1
), and thus by a Chernoff bound,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,X\geqslant\frac{n}{(\log n)^{C^{j+1}}}\,\right]\geqslant\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,X\geqslant 1/2\cdot\mathbf{E}\left[\,X\,\right]\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-\omega(1)}.

This means, we will have at least
\frac{n}{(\log n)^{C^{j+1}}}
bins with load at least
\frac{t_{0}}{n}+L+j
during one step in phase
j
, which concludes the induction step. Thus we have established the claim.
∎

7.2
Lower Bound for
d
-Weak-Memory

We also give a simple argument for a lower bound on the gap of
d
-Weak-Memory
with runs of a constant length
d
.

Lemma 7.3
.

For the
d
-Weak-Memory
process with constant
d>0
and
m=\frac{1}{400d}n\log n
, we have that

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Gap(m)\geqslant\frac{1}{400d}\cdot\log n\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-2}.

Proof.

In
d
-Weak-Memory
, every
d
steps the cache is reset and so the ball is allocated using
One-Choice
. Hence, in
m
steps, there are
m/d
balls allocated using
One-Choice
. By e.g.
[
28
, Section 4]
(see also
[
21
, Lemma A.9]
), when
cn\log n
balls are allocated using
One-Choice
, for any constant
c>0
, then with probability at least
1-n^{-2}
, the max load is at least
(c+\sqrt{c}/10)\log n
. Hence, we get

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\max_{i\in[n]}y_{i}^{m}\geqslant\left(\frac{1}{400d^{2}}+\frac{1}{200d}\right)\cdot\log n\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-2}.

Therefore, since at step
m
the average is
(\log n)/(400d)
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Gap(m)\geqslant\frac{1}{400d}\cdot\log n\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-2}.\qed

8
Conclusions

In this work, we presented an asymptotically tight analysis of
Memory
in the heavily loaded case
m\geqslant n
. We proved that the gap of
Memory
is
\Theta(\log\log n)
, matching the performance of
Two-Choice
up to constants. In contrast to
Two-Choice
, we showed
Memory
still works well in a heterogeneous setting where the sampling distribution may be distorted by some arbitrarily large constant factor.
We also analyzed other relaxed settings, including one where balls are weighted. In these settings, the cache is reset every constant number of steps (
d
-Weak-Memory
). In those cases, we proved that the gap is still
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
and remains independent of
m
.

There are several interesting directions. One of them is to consider even more skewed sampling distributions, e.g., heavy-tailed distributions such as Power-Law (similar to
[
9
]
, where
m=n
was studied). One might suspect that
Memory
is still able to outperform
d
-Choice
. In particular, we have established that Memory gives a bounded gap on even the most unruly sampling distributions (
1.3
). It would be interesting to get more precise gap bounds for
Memory
on specific sampling distributions of interest such as Power-law distributions.

A second direction is to determine the leading constant in the gap bound. The results by
[
26
]
for
m=n
suggest
Memory
might be slightly superior to
Two-Choice
also in the heavily loaded case.

Another avenue is to study the impact of
d
in
d
-Weak-Memory
(or
d
-
Reset-Memory
), when
d
may be a function of
n
. For instance, what is the smallest value of
d
that still achieves a gap of
\mathcal{O}(\log\log n)
? So far, we only know that
d
cannot be a constant, but we do not know whether
d
needs to be, say logarithmic or polynomial.

Finally, to the best of our knowledge, all implementations of
Memory
(including ours) make use of the greedy-rule, i.e., always allocate the ball in the least loaded option among the cache and the sampled bin, and update the cache in the same vein. Note that in the presence of a larger cache size and weighted balls, this may not be optimal, as there are more sophisticated strategies that preemptively try to maintain at least one significantly underloaded bin in the cache.

Definition A.1

(cf.

x

A.2
Inequalities

We proceed with two simple inequalities, which we include for the sake of completeness.

Lemma A.3
.

For any
0<z\leqslant 1
, we have

\frac{1}{1+z}\leqslant 1-\frac{z}{2}.

Proof.

For
z\in(0,1]
, the following chain of implications holds

\frac{1}{1+z}\leqslant 1-\frac{z}{2}\Leftrightarrow 1\leqslant 1+z-\frac{z}{2}-\frac{z^{2}}{2}\Leftrightarrow z\leqslant 1.\qed

Lemma A.4
.

For any
0<z\leqslant 1/2
, we have

\frac{1}{1-z}\leqslant 1+2z.

Proof.

For
z\in(0,1]
, the following chain of implications holds

\frac{1}{1-z}\leqslant 1+2z\Leftrightarrow 1\leqslant 1+2z-z-2z^{2}\Leftrightarrow z\leqslant\frac{1}{2}.\qed

Lemma A.5
.

For any
r<1
we have
\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}(i+1)\cdot r^{i}=\frac{1}{1-r}+\frac{r}{(1-r)^{2}}
.

Proof.

For the first sum observe that for any
d
,

(1-r)\sum_{i=0}^{d}(i+1)\cdot r^{i}=\sum_{i=0}^{d}(i+1)\cdot r^{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}i\cdot r^{i}=1+\sum_{i=1}^{d}r^{i}+(d+1)r^{d+1}.

Thus letting
d\rightarrow\infty
and using the sum for the geometric series
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}r^{i}=\frac{r}{1-r}
gives

(1-r)\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}(i+1)\cdot r^{i}=1+\frac{r}{1-r},

as claimed.
∎

A.3
Probabilistic Inequalities

For convenience, we state and prove the following well-known result.

Lemma A.6
.

Consider a sequence of random variables
(Z_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
such that there are
0<a<1
and
b>0
such that every
i\geqslant 1
,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,Z_{i}\mid Z_{i-1}\,\right]\leqslant Z_{i-1}\cdot a+b.

Then for every
i\geqslant 1
,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,Z_{i}\mid Z_{0}\,\right]\leqslant Z_{0}\cdot a^{i}+\frac{b}{1-a}.

Proof.

We will prove by induction that for every
i\in\mathbb{N}
,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,Z_{i}\mid Z_{0}\,\right]\leqslant Z_{0}\cdot a^{i}+b\cdot\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}a^{j}.

For
i=0
,
\mathbf{E}\left[\,Z_{0}\mid Z_{0}\,\right]\leqslant Z_{0}
. Assuming the induction hypothesis holds for some
i\geqslant 0
, then since
a>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,Z_{i+1}\mid Z_{0}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}\left[\,\mathbf{E}\left[\,Z_{i+1}\mid Z_{i}\,\right]\mid Z_{0}\,\right]\leqslant\mathbf{E}\left[\,Z_{i}\mid Z_{0}\,\right]\cdot a+b

\displaystyle\leqslant\Big(Z_{0}\cdot a^{i}+b\cdot\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}a^{j}\Big)\cdot a+b

\displaystyle=Z_{0}\cdot a^{i+1}+b\cdot\sum_{j=0}^{i}a^{j}.

The claims follows using that for
a\in(0,1)
,
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}a^{j}=\frac{1}{1-a}
.
∎

Lemma A.7
.

Consider any sequence of random variables
(Z_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
such that there exists
0<\alpha<1
and

\mathbf{E}\left[\,Z_{i}\mid Z_{i-1}\,\right]\leqslant Z_{i-1}\cdot(1-\alpha)+b.

Then, assuming that

, then for any

,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,Z_{i}\,\right]\leqslant\frac{b}{\alpha}.

Proof.

We will prove the claim by induction. The base case follows by the assumption

. Then, assuming that it holds for

, then for

we have,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,Z_{i+1}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}\left[\,\mathbf{E}\left[\,Z_{i+1}\mid Z_{i}\,\right]\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbf{E}\left[\,Z_{i}\cdot(1-\alpha)+b\,\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}\left[\,Z_{i}\,\right]-\alpha\cdot\mathbf{E}\left[\,Z_{i}\,\right]+b

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbf{E}\left[\,Z_{i}\,\right]-\alpha\cdot\frac{b}{\alpha}+b=\mathbf{E}\left[\,Z_{i}\,\right].\qed

Let

A.4
Concentration Inequality with a Bad Event

A central tool in our analysis will be the use of a concentration inequality by Chung and Lu
[
10
]
for super-martingales which will be conditional on a bad event not occurring. In this case, the bad event will be
\Phi_{1}^{s}=\operatorname{poly}(n)
for all
s\in[t-T_{r},t]
.

We start with the following definitions from
[
10
]
. Consider any r.v.
X
(in our case it will be the potential function
\Gamma_{2}
) that can be evaluated by a sequence of decisions
Y_{1},Y_{2},\ldots,Y_{N}
of finitely many outputs (the chosen bins of the allocated balls). We can describe the process by a
decision tree

T
, a complete rooted tree with depth
N
with vertex set
V(T)
. Each edge
(u,v)
of
T
is associated with a probability
p_{u,v}
depending on the decision made from
u
to
v
.

We say
f:V(T)\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies an
admissible condition

P
if
P=\{P_{v}\}
holds for every vertex
v\in V(T)
. For an admissible condition
P
, the associated bad set
B^{i}
over the
X^{i}
is defined to be

B^{i}=\{v\mid\text{the depth of $v$ is $i$, and $P_{u}$ does not hold for some ancestor $u$ of $v$}\}.

Theorem A.9

(Theorem 8.5 in
[
10
]
)
.

For a filter
\mathfrak{F}
,
\{\emptyset,\Omega\}=\mathfrak{F}^{0}\subset\mathfrak{F}^{1}\subset\ldots\subset\mathfrak{F}^{N}=\mathfrak{F}
,
suppose that a random variable
X^{s}
is
\mathfrak{F}^{s}
-measurable, for
0\leqslant s\leqslant N
. Let
B=B^{N}
be the
bad set associated with the following admissible conditions:

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,X^{s}\mid\mathfrak{F}^{s-1}\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant X^{s-1},

\displaystyle\mathbf{Var}\left[\,X^{s}\mid\mathfrak{F}^{s-1}\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\sigma_{s}^{2},

\displaystyle X^{s}-\mathbf{E}\left[\,X^{s}\mid\mathfrak{F}^{s-1}\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant a_{s}+M,

for some
\sigma_{s}>0
and
a_{s}>0
. Then, we have for any
\lambda>0
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,X^{N}\geqslant X^{0}+\lambda\,\right]\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2(\sum_{s=1}^{N}(\sigma_{s}^{2}+a_{s}^{2})+M\lambda/3)}\right)+\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,B\,\right].

In particular, we always make use of the following simplified version,

Theorem A.10

(Corollary of
A.9
)
.

For a filter
\mathfrak{F}
,
\{\emptyset,\Omega\}=\mathfrak{F}^{0}\subset\mathfrak{F}^{1}\subset\ldots\subset\mathfrak{F}^{N}=\mathfrak{F}
,
suppose that a random variable
X^{s}
is
\mathfrak{F}^{s}
-measurable, for
0\leqslant s\leqslant N
. Let
B=B^{N}
be the bad set associated with the following admissible conditions:

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,X^{s}\mid\mathfrak{F}^{s-1}\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant X^{s-1},

\displaystyle|X^{s}-X^{s-1}|

\displaystyle\leqslant D,

for some
D>0
. Then, we have for any
\lambda>0
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,X^{N}\geqslant X^{0}+\lambda\,\right]\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{10ND^{2}}\right)+\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,B\,\right].

Proof.

We will show that the bounded difference condition
|X^{s}-X^{s-1}|\leqslant D
, implies the second and third conditions in
A.10
.

For the second condition, we will use Popovicius’ inequality for variances (
A.11
), which states that for any random variable
Y
such that
a\leqslant Y\leqslant b
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Var}\left[\,Y\,\right]\leqslant\frac{1}{4}\cdot(b-a)^{2}.

The bounded difference condition implies that

X^{s-1}-D\leqslant X^{s}\leqslant X^{s-1}+D.

Combining the two, we get that

\displaystyle\mathbf{Var}[\,X^{s}\mid\mathfrak{F}^{s-1}\,]\leqslant\frac{1}{4}\cdot\left(\left.X^{s-1}+D-\bigl(X^{s-1}-D\bigr)\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{s-1}\right)^{2}=D^{2}.

For the third condition,

X^{s}-\mathbf{E}\left[\,X^{s}\mid\mathfrak{F}^{s-1}\,\right]\leqslant X^{s-1}+D-(X^{s-1}-D)\leqslant 2D.

Finally, we get the conclusion by
A.10
with

,
\sigma_{s}=D
, and
M=0
.
