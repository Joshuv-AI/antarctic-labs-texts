Abstract
Limitation of the cost of coordination and contention among a large number of nodes calls for grant-free approaches, exploiting physical layer techniques to solve collisions. Successive Interference Cancellation is becoming a key building block of multiple access channel receiver, in an effort to support massive Internet of Things. In this paper, we explore the large-scale performance of Successive Interference Cancellation in a theoretical framework. A general model of a Successive Interference Cancellation receiver is stated for a shared channel with n n transmitters. The asymptotic sum-rate performance is characterized as n → ∞ n\rightarrow\infty , for a suitably scaled target Signal to Noise plus Interference Ratio. The probability distribution of the number of correctly decoded packets is shown to tend to a deterministic distribution asymptotically for large values of n n . The asymptotic analysis is carried out for any probability distribution of the wireless channel gain, assuming that the average received power level is same for all nodes, through power control.

Introduction

Internet of Things is transforming and bringing a deep impact in every aspect of human life such as healthcare, education, Industry, agrifood chain, power grid, autonomous driving, logistic services, and smart cities

The evolution of Internet of Things toward these applications opens up a paradigm of massive access that aims at realizing efficient and reliable communication for a massive number of Internet of Things devices
[
5

.
The main characteristics of massive access include low power, massive connectivity, short packets, and low signaling overhead, which call for more robust and flexible wireless communication networks

Also, these requirements envision the 6G ecosystem as the playground of a data-driven society, which involves instant and unlimited connectivity to anything, ranging from tiny static sensors to autonomous objects, from anywhere, and anytime.
This paradigm calls for the design of innovative multiple access techniques to provide massive connectivity, and low latency to future massive Internet of Things systems.

A generic scenario of massive multiple access in Internet of Things involves a huge number of connected devices that give rise typically to sporadic and random traffic.
Hence, the actual number of backlogged devices, contending for access to the medium, is largely variable.
A fundamental issue toward such a massive Internet of Things scenario involves reducing the cost of coordination of a varying and possibly large number of devices in an efficient and timely manner.
One promising solution toward this paradigm is the grant-free random multiple access approach, in which each active device directly transmits its data to the Base Station without waiting for any permission from the access point.
Traditional grant-based access requires multiple signaling exchanges between the device and the network to allocate resources, which can be inefficient for small and infrequent data transmissions, especially in Massive machine-type Communication.
Grant-free access minimizes this overhead
[
9
]
.

The key features of grant-free random multiple access include no prior scheduling which reduces the latency and improves the system response, scalability to support a huge number of connections, energy efficiency, eliminating the need for devices to wait for a grant, and collision management.

As for the last point, since multiple devices may attempt to transmit simultaneously, leading to collisions, grant-free random access protocols often incorporate mechanisms to handle these collisions, such as coding, advanced signal processing techniques, or Non-Orthogonal Multiple Access
[
10
]
.
Non-Orthogonal Multiple Access enables multiple users to share the same time-frequency resources non-orthogonally
[
11

[

.

Several Non-Orthogonal Multiple Access schemes have been proposed over the past few years, which can be divided into two main categories code-domain Non-Orthogonal Multiple Access and power-domain Non-Orthogonal Multiple Access
[
14
]
.
A few variants of code-domain Non-Orthogonal Multiple Access includes Pattern Division Multiple Access
[
15
]
, and Multi User Shared Access
[
16
]
.
In this paper, we mainly focus on the power-domain Non-Orthogonal Multiple Access, as recently implemented in 3GPP LTE
[
17
]
.
It involves the superposition of signals from different users, then exploiting Successive Interference Cancellation to decode them
[
11
]
.

Recent researches highlight a significant interest in leveraging advanced signal processing and machine learning techniques to address the challenges of Grant Free Multiple Access in modern wireless networks
[

19

For instance,

A reinforcement learning-based approach is introduced in

An optimal power from a pool of powers is chosen, with the help multi-agent framework, to optimize spectral efficiency and user fairness while mitigating interference.
A stochastic optimization-based adaptive grant-free scheme is proposed in

It is quite evident that all these performance enhancements towards adaptive grant-free protocols come from advances carried out at the physical layer, i.e. Successive Interference Cancellation

Hence, it becomes a crucial aspect to understand the behavior of Multi-Packet Reception-capable receivers, like those based on Successive Interference Cancellation, under a massive number of users.

Several works have been done toward the design, dimensioning, and optimization of scalable Multi-Packet Reception systems, with respect to the number of transmitting nodes in the range of thousands.
The authors in
[
25

proposed a theoretical framework to investigate the effect of several parameters, such as the capture threshold and the multi-packet reception capability, with the help of Successive Interference Cancellation, with a limited number of iterations involved in Successive Interference Cancellation decoding process.
Enabling the Successive Interference Cancellation mechanism brings significant performance gains in terms of throughput, especially for low capture thresholds, however, the normalized throughput increment rapidly diminishes with each iteration of Successive Interference Cancellation

Successive Interference Cancellation is utilized under the framework of ALOHA in

There still exists a significant gap in understanding the performance of Successive Interference Cancellation in large-scale systems.
The role of Successive Interference Cancellation toward massive access needs great attention, especially in view of the standardization of Non-Orthogonal Multiple Access in 3GPP network

We characterize the asymptotic behavior of the probability distribution of the number of correctly decoded packets utilizing Successive Interference Cancellation in large-scale systems.

This analysis is valid for any probability distribution of the channel random gain, given that the average received power is the same for all nodes.

System Model

We consider
n

The focus is on uplink, i.e., packets are sent by nodes and addressed to the Base Station.

The communication link is modeled as an Additive White Gaussian Noise channel with block Rayleigh fading.
Let
P_{j}

Let also

The coefficient

It is normalized so that
\mathrm{E}[|h_{j}|^{2}]=1

The received symbol string
\mathbf{R}=[R(1),\dots,R(\ell)]
at the Base Station is modeled as

\mathbf{R}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\sqrt{P_{j}}h_{j}\mathbf{S}_{j}}+\mathbf{Z}

(1)

where
\mathbf{Z}=[Z(1),\dots,Z(\ell)]

Z(i)\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,P_{N})

Assimilating interference to additive Gaussian noise, the Signal to Noise plus Interference Ratio of node

\Gamma_{j}=\frac{P_{j}|h_{j}|^{2}}{P_{N}+I_{j}}

(2)

Node

The target Signal to Noise plus Interference Ratio

\eta

, consistently with the Additive White Gaussian Noise channel assumption.

The time axis is divided in slots.
Nodes make transmission attempts in each slot.
The slot size just fits a packet transmission time.
Let
L
be the length of transmitted packets for all nodes and
W
the channel bandwidth.
The time required to transmit a packet for a given target Signal to Noise plus Interference Ratio

T=T(\gamma)=\frac{L}{W\log_{2}(1+\gamma)}

(3)

In the following, we drop the subscript
j

We assume that node

Then, node

P_{0}

P_{0}

\epsilon

Decoding of a packet sent by a single transmitting node is successful, if the Signal to Noise Ratio exceeds

P_{\text{rx}}/P_{N}\geq\gamma

Since the average received power level is

S_{0}=\frac{P_{0}}{P_{N}}=\frac{\gamma}{-\log(1-\epsilon)}=\frac{\gamma}{c}

III
Scaling of multiple access parameters with the number of nodes

Let us consider a multiple access channel with slotted time axis and a configurable target Signal to Noise plus Interference Ratio
\gamma
, as described in the previous Section.
Nodes that are backlogged at the beginning of a slot transmit in that slot with probability
p
, or defer a new attempt in next slot with probability
1-p
.
In this Section we motivate why the target Signal to Noise plus Interference Ratio
\gamma
and the transmission probability
p
must scale with the number of backlogged nodes
n
for stability.

To measure the efficiency of the communication channel we focus on the sum-rate metric.
The sum-rate gives the achieved spectral efficiency of the multiple access channel in bit/s/Hz
[
24
]
.
Let us assume that a backlogged node transmits in a slot with probability
p
and sets the target Signal to Noise plus Interference Ratio to
\gamma
when transmitting.
Assuming an AWGN channel over a bandwidth
W
and a time slot size
T
, the number of bits delivered with a single successful transmission is
T\,W\,\log_{2}(1+\gamma)

Let also
M_{n}(p,\gamma)
denote the mean number of successfully decoded packets, when
n
nodes are backlogged, transmit with probability
p
, use a target Signal to Noise plus Interference Ratio
\gamma
.
Then, the achieved sum-rate with
n
backlogged ndoes is

U=U_{n}(p,\gamma)=\log_{2}(1+\gamma)M_{n}(p,\gamma)

(10)

We have

M_{n}(p,\gamma)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}{m_{k}(\gamma)\binom{n}{k}p^{k}(1-p)^{n-k}}

(11)

where we let
m_{k}(\gamma)
denote the mean number of correctly decoded packets in a slot with target Signal to Noise plus Interference Ratio equal to
\gamma
, given that
k
nodes transmit in that slot.
In Appendix
A
it is proved that the following bounds hold on
m_{k}(\gamma)
:

\frac{k(1-\epsilon)}{(1+\gamma)^{k-1}}\leq m_{k}(\gamma)\leq\frac{k^{2}(1-\epsilon)}{(1+\gamma)^{k-1}}

(12)

for all
k\geq 0
and all
\gamma>0
.

Exploiting those bounds, in Appendix
B
it is shown that
U_{n}(p,\gamma)\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
, for any fixed values of
p
and
\gamma
.
We must therefore consider adaptive values of
p_{n}
and
\gamma_{n}
.

In Appendix
C
it is shown that the sum-rate vanishes as
n
grow, if we let
p
and
\gamma
scale with
n
in such a way that
p=p_{n}\sim O(1/n^{a})
and
\gamma=\gamma_{n}\sim O(1/n^{b})
with
a
and
b
non-negative numbers such that
a+b\neq 1
.
Putting this result together with that of Appendix
B
, we conclude that the only way to get a non vanishing limit of the sum-rate as the number of backlogged nodes grows can possibly be adapting
p_{n}
and
\gamma_{n}
as described above, with
a+b=1
.

Scaling the transmission probability with the number of backlogged nodes is a well known approach to the stabilitzation of Slotted ALOHA
[
3
]
and Carrier-Sense Multiple Access
[
2
]
.
We therefore focus on the less studied scaling, where
\gamma
is set inversely proportional to
n
, while leaving all backlogged nodes transmit in a slot, i.e., we consider the scaling
\gamma_{n}=1/(\alpha n)
and
p_{n}=1
.

.

Let
\gamma_{n}=1/(\alpha n)

\alpha

Let also

where

is defined as
4

The meaning of this result is that it is possible to successfully decode a substantial (i.e., proportional to

The achieved sum-rate is defined as the spectral efficiency of the channel.
The sum-rate gives the achieved spectral efficiency of the multiple access channel in bit/s/Hz

]

Given that

\displaystyle U_{n}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{E}[\text{ \# delivered bits per unit time }|\,n,\gamma]}{\text{ Channel bandwidth }}

\displaystyle=\frac{L\,\mathrm{E}[\text{ \# delivered packets per slot }|\,n,\gamma]/T(\gamma)}{W}

\displaystyle=\log_{2}(1+\gamma)\,\mathrm{E}[\text{ \# delivered packets per slot }|\,n,\gamma]

\displaystyle=\log_{2}(1+\gamma)m_{n}(\gamma)

Setting

Finally,

4
gives an explicit form of the mean number of successfully decoded packets in case there is no Successive Interference Cancellation (see

).
This result is not new (e.g., see [CITATION]).
We report it here for ease of the reader, along with the asymptotic regime.

Theorem 4
.

Let us consider
n
concurrent transmissions towards a single receiver that can only exploit capture (no Successive Interference Cancellation) with a target Signal to Noise plus Interference Ratio
\gamma
.
The mean number of successfully decoded packets
m_{n}(\gamma)
is given by

m_{n}(\gamma)=e^{-\gamma P_{N}/P_{0}}\frac{n}{(1+\gamma)^{n-1}}=\frac{(1-\epsilon)n}{(1+\gamma)^{n-1}}

(27)

in view of the setting
P_{0}/P_{N}=-\gamma/\log(1-\epsilon)
.
With
\gamma=1/(\alpha n)
, it is

U_{\infty}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{U_{n}}=\frac{(1-\epsilon)}{\log 2}\frac{1}{\alpha}e^{-1/\alpha}

(28)

Setting
\gamma=1/(\alpha n)
, it is easy to derive the asymptotic expansion
m_{n}(1/(\alpha n))\sim n(1-\epsilon)\exp(-1/\alpha)
.
The asymptotic sum-rate in this case is maximized for
\alpha=1
.
The maximum asymptotic sum-rate is
U_{\infty}^{*}=(1-\epsilon)/(e\log 2)
.

The model with no Successive Interference Cancellation applies to those multiple access schemes that mitigate interference, yet they do not cancel it actively, e.g., Code Division Multiple Access [REF] or Golden Modulation based on Zadoff-Chu sequences [REF].
As a matter of example, in the former case
\gamma=\gamma^{\prime}/PG
, where
\gamma^{\prime}
is the target narrowband Signal to Noise Ratio of a node (it is typically
\gamma^{\prime}\gg 1
) and
PG
is the processing gain (typically
PG\gg 1
).

V
Generalization

Let us now generalize the model of the random variable
S_{i}
, that represents the Signal to Noise Ratio of node
i
.
Let us write
S_{i}=S_{0}Y_{i}
, where
S_{0}
is the Signal to Noise Ratio level required to guarantee successful reception of a single transmitter with probability
1-\epsilon
.
In the analysis developed in previous Sections it has been assumed that
Y_{i}
be a negative exponential random variable with mean 1, representing the Rayleigh fading gain.
We now generalize the analysis, by allowing
Y_{i}
to be a general non-negative random variable, with continuous Probability Density Function, still constrained to have mean equal to 1.
Sources of randomness in the description of
Y_{i}
come from node path loss statistics and from the selected transmission power level.

Given
n
nodes transmitting, we assume
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
are independent and identically distributed random variables with Cumulative Distribution Function
F(t)=\mathcal{P}(Y\leq t)
, Complementary Cumulative Distribution Function
G(t)=1-F(t)
, and Probability Density Function
f(t)
.

The target average received Signal to Noise Ratio
S_{0}
is set so that
\mathcal{P}(S_{0}Y\geq\gamma)\geq 1-\epsilon
.
Since
\mathcal{P}(Y>t)=G(t)
, we have
G(\gamma/S_{0})=1-\epsilon
, i.e.,
S_{0}=\gamma/G^{-1}(1-\epsilon)
.
To keep notation uniform with the one used in previous section, we let
c=G^{-1}(1-\epsilon)
, so that we still wrtie
S_{0}=\gamma/c
.
In case of Rayleigh fading it is
G(t)=e^{-t}
.
hence,
G^{-1}(y)=-\log y
, and we recover
c=-\log(1-\epsilon)
(see
Equation

5
).

The order statistics associated with
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
is denoted with
Y_{(1)},\dots,Y_{(n)}
, where labeling is assigned so that
Y_{(1)}\geq\dots\geq Y_{(n)}
, i.e., in descending order.
It is known that
[
30
]

f_{Y_{(h)}}(u)=\frac{n!}{(n-h)!(h-1)!}\left[G(u)\right]^{h-1}\left[1-G(u)\right]^{n-h}f(u)

(29)

for
u\geq 0
and
h=1,\dots,n
.
The mean value of
Y_{(h)}
is

\displaystyle\mu_{h}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}[Y_{(h)}]=\int_{0}^{\infty}{uf_{Y_{(h)}}(u)\,du}

\displaystyle=n\int_{0}^{\infty}{u\binom{n-1}{h-1}\left[G(u)\right]^{h-1}\left[1-G(u)\right]^{n-h}f(u)\,du}

\displaystyle=n\int_{0}^{1}{G^{-1}(u)\binom{n-1}{h-1}u^{h-1}(1-u)^{n-h}\,du}

(30)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}{G^{-1}(u)\beta_{h,n-h+1}(u)\,du}

(31)

where
\beta_{a,b}(u)
is the Beta Probability Density Function with parameters
a
and
b
, for
u\in[0,1]
and
a,b>0
.

Packet from node
h
is decoded correctly if all packets sent by nodes whose signal was stronger (nodes from 1 to
h-1
) have been decoded correctly and if the Signal to Noise plus Interference Ratio of node
h
exceeds the threshold
\gamma
, i.e.,

\frac{S_{0}Y_{(h)}}{1+\sum_{i=h+1}^{n}{S_{0}Y_{(i)}}}\geq\gamma

(32)

This inequality can be re-arranged as follows:

Y_{(h)}-\gamma\sum_{i=h+1}^{n}{Y_{(i)}}\geq c

(33)

where we have used the equality
S_{0}=\gamma/c
.

For large
n
, we expect that the random variable on the left-hand side collapses to a deterministic distribution, hence it is equal to its mean with probability 1.
Let us therefore turn to the study of the inquality obtained by replacing the random variable on the left-hand side with its
mean
.

\mu_{h}-\gamma\sum_{i=h+1}^{n}{\mu_{i}}\geq c

(34)

We consider the asymptotic regime where
h\sim nx
, for a fixed
x\in(0,1)
, and
\gamma=1/(\alpha n)
, with
\alpha>0
.
Then, we have

\displaystyle\mu_{n}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}{G^{-1}(u)\beta_{nx,n(1-x)+1}(u)\,du}

\displaystyle\sim\int_{0}^{1}{G^{-1}(u)\beta_{nx,n(1-x)}(u)\,du}

(35)

where we account for the fact that
n(1-x)+1\sim n(1-x)
for large
n
and fixed
x
.

We use the following asymptotic property of the beta probability distribution:

\beta_{na,nb}(u)\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\frac{a}{a+b},\frac{1}{n}\,\frac{ab}{(a+b)^{2}}\right)

(36)

Therefore, in our case we get

\beta_{nx,n(1-x)}(u)\sim\mathcal{N}\left(x,\frac{x(1-x)}{n}\right)

(37)

This shows that as
n\rightarrow\infty
, the scaled Beta distribution tends to a Dirca impulse centered in
x
.
As a consequence, we find as
n
tends to infinity:

\mu_{n}\rightarrow\int_{0}^{1}{G^{-1}(u)\delta(u-x)\,du}=G^{-1}(x)

(38)

As for the sum appearing on the right-hand side of
Equation

34
, we have, for
\gamma=1/(\alpha n)
,

\gamma\sum_{i=h+1}^{n}{\mu_{i}}=\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{0}^{1}{G^{-1}(u)\sum_{i=h}^{n-1}{B_{u,n-1}(i)}\,du}

(39)

where

B_{u,n-1}(i)=\binom{n-1}{i}u^{i}(1-u)^{n-1-i}\,,\quad i=0,1,\dots,n-1,

(40)

is a Binomial probability distribution with parameters
u
and
n-1
.
Let
Z
denote the binomial random variable with probability distribution in
Equation

40
.
Then, we have

\sum_{i=h}^{n-1}{B_{u,n-1}(i)}=\mathcal{P}(Z\geq h)

(41)

In the considered asymptotic regime, it is
h\sim nx
.
Moreover, we use the following asymptotic property of the Binomial probability distribution:

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathcal{P}\left(\frac{Z-nu}{\sqrt{nu(1-u)}}\leq t\right)}=\Phi(t)

(42)

where
\Phi(t)
is the Cumulative Distribution Function of the standard Gaussian random variable:

\Phi(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{t}{e^{-u^{2}/2}\,du}

(43)

This stems from the Central Limit Thoerem and from the fact that

Z=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}{B_{k}}

(44)

where
B_{k}
is a Bernoulli random variable that equals 1 with probability
u
and it is equal to 0 with probability
1-u
.

From
Equation

42
, we derive that

\mathcal{P}(Z\leq nx)\sim\Phi\left(\frac{nx-nu}{\sqrt{nu(1-u)}}\right)=\Phi\left(\sqrt{n}\frac{x-u}{\sqrt{u(1-u)}}\right)

(45)

As
n
gets large, it is seen that
\Phi\left(\sqrt{n}\frac{x-u}{\sqrt{u(1-u)}}\right)\rightarrow 0
, if
x<u
, whereas
\Phi\left(\sqrt{n}\frac{x-u}{\sqrt{u(1-u)}}\right)\rightarrow 1
, if
x>u
.
Therefore, the probability of the event
Y\leq h=nx
tends to a step function with jump located at
x
as
n
grows.

Summing up, we have

\sum_{i=nx}^{n-1}{B_{u,n-1}(i)}=\mathcal{P}(Y\geq nx)\rightarrow\begin{cases}1&u>x\text{ },\\
0&u<x.\end{cases}

(46)

Inserting this asymptotic result in the integral in
Equation

39
, we have as
n
tends to infinity:

\gamma\sum_{i=nx+1}^{n}{\mu_{i}}\rightarrow\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{x}^{1}{G^{-1}(u)\,du}

(47)

In the end, the inequality on the mean values in
Equation

38
approaches the following limiting inequality in the asymptotic regime

G^{-1}(x)-\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{x}^{1}{G^{-1}(u)\,du}\geq c

(48)

for a given
x\in(0,1)
.
The quantity
x
represents the fraction of correctly decoded packets.
The asymptotic sum rate is
U_{\infty}=\frac{x^{*}(\alpha)}{\alpha\log 2}
, where
x^{*}(\alpha)
is the smallest value of
x\in(0,1)
such that the inequality in
Equation

48
is satisfied.

In the special case where
Y
is a negative exponential random variable with mean 1, it is
G(t)=e^{-t}
, hence
G^{-1}(u)=-\log u
.
It can be verified that
Equation

48
reduces to
Equation

16
.

Equation

48
can be transformed as follows:

y-\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{0}^{y}{uf(u)\,du}\geq c=G^{-1}(1-\epsilon)

(49)

where
y=G^{-1}(x)
, hence it is
x=G(y)
.
The fraction of transmission that can be correctly decoded is
x^{*}=G(y^{*})
, with
y^{*}
equal to the largest value of
y
such that the left-hand side of
Equation

49
is less than
c
.
Formally,

y^{*}=\inf\left\{y:z\geq c+\int_{0}^{z}{uf(u)du},\forall z\in(y,\infty)\right\}

(50)

Since
f(u)\geq 0
and the mean of the Probability Density Function
f(u)
is constrained to be 1, it is easy to verify that it must be
c\leq y^{*}\leq c+1/\alpha
.
Since
x^{*}=G(y^{*})
and
G(\cdot)
is monotonously decreasing, we have
G\left(G^{-1}(1-\epsilon)+1/\alpha\right)\leq x^{*}\leq G\left(G^{-1}(1-\epsilon)\right)=1-\epsilon
.

To provide a numerical example, we assume
Y
has a Gamma Probability Density Function, one of the simplest probability distributions that provides two degrees of freedom.
We set the mean of
Y
to 1 and let
\eta
denote the reciprocal of the Squared Coefficient Of Variation of
Y
, i.e.,
\eta
is the ratio of the mean of
Y
squared to the variance of
Y
.
As
\eta
gets smaller, the random variable
Y
exhibits more variability, which is deemed to be favorable to the success of Successive Interference Cancellation decoding.

Formally, for any
\eta>0
, we have

f(t)=\frac{\eta^{\eta}t^{\eta-1}}{\Gamma(\eta)}e^{-\eta t}\,,\quad t>0.

(51)

where
\Gamma(a)
is the Euler Gamma function, defined by
\Gamma(a)=\int_{0}^{\infty}{u^{a-1}e^{-u}\,du}
for
a>0
.
With this Probability Density Function,
Equation

49
specializes into

y-\frac{1}{\alpha}\frac{\Gamma(\eta+1,\eta y)}{\Gamma(\eta+1)}\geq c=\frac{\Gamma^{-1}\left(\eta,\epsilon\Gamma(\eta)\right)}{\eta}

(52)

where
\Gamma(a,x)
is the incomplete Euler Gamma Function, given by
\Gamma(a,x)=\int_{0}^{x}{u^{a-1}e^{-u}\,du}
for
a,x>0
, and
\Gamma^{-1}(a,\cdot)
is its inverse with respect to the second variable,
x
.
Note
\Gamma(a)=\Gamma(a,\infty)
and
\Gamma(a,x)
is a monotonously increasing function of
x
for any positive
a
.
We have also used the equality
\Gamma(\eta+1)=\eta\Gamma(\eta)
, holding for any
\eta>0
.

Exploiting the equation
U_{\infty}=x^{*}/(\alpha\log(2))
, it is possible to find numerical values of the asymptotic sum-rate
U_{\infty}
as a function of

, for several values of the Squared Coefficient Of Variation of
Y
(see

).

In General, we can write
Y=XZ
, where
X
represents the random component of the block fading channel and
Z
stems from a randomization of the transmission power level.
We remind that it must be
\mathrm{E}[Y]=1
.
If we assume Rayleigh fading, so that
\mathcal{P}(X>u)=e^{-u}
, then it must be
\mathrm{E}[Z]=1
.
With a two level choice for transmission power, we have
Z=1/b
with probability
b/(1+b)
and
Z=b
with probability
1/(1+b)
, for any
b\geq 1
.
It can be verified that
\mathrm{E}[Y^{2}]=2\mathrm{E}[Z^{2}]=2\left(b+1/b-1\right)
.
Hence, the Squared Coefficient Of Variation of
Y
is
2\left(b+1/b-1\right)-1
, which can be made as large as desired with growing values of
b
.
To ease transmission power setting, the nodes could be instructed to opt for the largest transmission power level in case their average channel gain is above a suitable threshold, for the lower value otherwise.
Setting the threshold properly assigns probability of using either level as desired (i.e., so that the average of
Z
be 1).

VI
Conclusions

We have characterized the asymptotic behavior of the mean number of correctly decoded packets and the ensuing sum-rate of a Successive Interference Cancellation receiver with
n
transmitting nodes and a target Signal to Noise plus Interference Ratio that scales in inverse proportion to
n

The considered Successive Interference Cancellation is quite general, encompassing any kind of random fading.
The average received power level is assumed to be same for all nodes.
It is found that the random variable representing the number of correctly decoded packets converges to a deterministic quantity as
n\rightarrow\infty
.
The asymptotic regime is fully characterized.

Two major points for further work stem out of the result of this paper.
First, the generalization to systems where nodes play with transmission power levels, so as to obtain different average received power levels, are of interest.
In fact, Successive Interference Cancellation performance are expected to improve, if superposed signals have well separated reception power levels.
Secondly, practical techniques for realizing Successive Interference Cancellation should be evaluated.
Referring to specific Successive Interference Cancellation algorithms allows the identification of a refined reception model.

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mu_{j}(n)|_{j/n=x}}=f_{\alpha,\xi}(x)

Putting together the limits of variance and mean of

Applying Chebichev inequality, we can write

Taking the limit for

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathcal{P}\left(\left|\frac{V_{j}}{\mu_{j}(n)}-1\right|>\epsilon\right)}=0

which proves that
