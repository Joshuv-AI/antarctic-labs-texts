Sampling Colorings and Independent Sets of Random Regular Bipartite Graphs in the Non-Uniqueness Region
Zongchen ChenAndreas GalanisDaniel Štefankovič, Eric Vigoda

Abstract
For spin systems, such as the q q -colorings and independent-set models, approximating the partition function in the so-called non-uniqueness region, where the model exhibits long-range correlations, is typically computationally hard for bounded-degree graphs. We present new algorithmic results for approximating the partition function and sampling from the Gibbs distribution for spin systems in the non-uniqueness region on random regular bipartite graphs. We give an 𝖥𝖯𝖱𝖠𝖲 \mathsf{FPRAS} for counting q q -colorings for even q = O ⁡ ( Δ log ⁡ Δ ) q=O\big(\tfrac{\Delta}{\log{\Delta}}\big) on almost every Δ \Delta -regular bipartite graph. This is within a factor O ⁡ ( log ⁡ Δ ) O(\log{\Delta}) of the sampling algorithm for general graphs in the uniqueness region and improves significantly upon the previous best bound of q = O ⁡ ( Δ ( log ⁡ Δ ) 2 ) q=O\big(\tfrac{\sqrt{\Delta}}{(\log\Delta)^{2}}\big) by Jenssen, Keevash, and Perkins (SODA’19). Analogously, for the hard-core model on independent sets weighted by λ > 0 \lambda>0 , we present an 𝖥𝖯𝖱𝖠𝖲 \mathsf{FPRAS} for estimating the partition function when λ = Ω ⁡ ( log ⁡ Δ Δ ) \lambda=\Omega\big(\tfrac{\log{\Delta}}{\Delta}\big) , which improves upon previous results by an Ω ⁡ ( log ⁡ Δ ) \Omega(\log\Delta) factor. Our results for the colorings and hard-core models follow from a general result that applies to arbitrary spin systems. Our main contribution is to show how to elevate probabilistic/analytic bounds on the marginal probabilities for the typical structure of phases on random bipartite regular graphs into efficient algorithms, using the polymer method. We further show evidence that our result for colorings is within a constant factor of best possible using current polymer-method approaches.

Polymer models have recently been used to obtain algorithms for spin systems in regimes where standard algorithmic tools (such as correlation-decay algorithms or Gibbs sampling/Glauber dynamics) are inefficient. The prototypical class of graphs where polymer models have been applied to are classes of expander and random regular graphs

Random bipartite regular graphs are particularly tantalizing

[

This paper will focus on finding the algorithmic limits of the polymer method for the two canonical models of interest, the
q
-colorings and the hard-core model (weighted independent sets), though our results apply much more generally as we will detail later. One of the main contributions of this work is to elevate the rough guarantees obtained by analytic/probabilistic methods into efficient approximate sampling/counting algorithms.

We begin with the colorings problem: given an integer

In contrast when

Current bounds are far from resolving this conjecture but have made considerable progress. On the algorithmic side, recent results establish

The above results address the problem on worst-case graphs; in this paper we address the behavior on

Proof Outline

2.1
Preliminaries: general spin systems and bicliques

Let
q\geq 2
be an integer. A general
q
-spin system
(\mathbf{B},\boldsymbol{\lambda})
consists of a symmetric interaction matrix
\mathbf{B}=\{B_{ij}\}_{i,j\in[q]}
, whose entries are between 0 and 1, and an activity vector
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i\in[q]}
with strictly positive entries which are
\leq 1
. Note, that up to normalising, we may assume that
\mathbf{B}
and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
have each at least one entry equal to 1.

For a graph
G=(V,E)
, an assignment
\sigma:V\rightarrow[q]
has weight
w_{G}(\sigma)=\prod_{(u,v)\in E}B_{\sigma(u),\sigma(v)}
. The Gibbs distribution is given by
\mu_{G}(\sigma)=w_{G}(\sigma)/Z_{G}
, where
Z_{G}=\sum_{\sigma:V\rightarrow[q]}w_{G}(\sigma)
is the partition function. We let
\Sigma_{G}
be the set of all spin assignments.
For a spin system on a bipartite graph
G
, the following notion of
bicliques
is relevant
[
14
,
21
,
13
,
17
]
.

Definition 4

(Biclique)
.

For a
q
-spin system with interaction matrix
\mathbf{B}
, we say that a pair
(S,T)
where
S,T\subseteq[q]
is a
biclique
if
B_{ij}=1
for all
i\in S,j\in T
. A biclique
(S,T)
is
maximal
if there is
no
other biclique
(S^{\prime},T^{\prime})\neq(S,T)
satisfying
S\subseteq S^{\prime}\subseteq[q]
and
T\subseteq T^{\prime}\subseteq[q]
.

Note that bicliques are defined using only the interaction matrix
\mathbf{B}
and do not depend on
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
.

Example 5
.

For the
q
-colorings model, we have that
\mathbf{B}
is the
q\times q
matrix with all ones except on the diagonal where the entries are zero (and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
is the all-ones vector). The bicliques
(S,T)
are given by pairs of disjoint sets
S,T\subseteq[q]
, whereas maximal bicliques by pairs of
S,T\subseteq[1]
that form a partition of
[q]
. For the hard-core model, we have
\mathbf{B}=\big[\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\
1&0\end{smallmatrix}\big]
and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=\big[\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
\lambda\end{smallmatrix}\big]
. Indexing the rows/columns of
\mathbf{B}
with
\{0,1\}
(instead of
\{1,2\}
), the bicliques are
\{(0,0),(0,1),(1,0),(0,01),(01,0)\}
and the maximal bicliques are
\{(0,01),(01,0)\}
.

2.2
Our approach: phase vectors and phase maximality

Let
(\mathbf{B},\boldsymbol{\lambda})
be an arbitrary spin system and
G
be a
\Delta
-regular bipartite graph, whose vertex set
V
is partitioned as
(L,R)
with
|L|=|R|=n
.
Our approach to obtain approximation algorithms is to consider the likely frequencies of the spins on each side of the graph in the Gibbs distribution of
G
. Adapting methods from
[
14
,
21
]
, we show that we can obtain efficient approximation schemes for those spin systems where the “likely” frequency vectors are captured by maximal bicliques
(S,T)
, see the upcoming Definition
7
. The main new ingredient in our work is to give a tight method to study when this condition is satisfied for general spin systems, which ultimately yields Theorems
1
and
2
as special cases.

To formalise the above, for
q
-dimensional probability vectors
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i\in[q]},\boldsymbol{\beta}=\{\beta_{i}\}_{i\in[q]}
, we let

\Sigma^{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{G}=\{\sigma:V\rightarrow[q]\,\big|\,|\sigma^{-1}(i)\cap L|=n\alpha_{i},|\sigma^{-1}(i)\cap R|=n\beta_{i}\}

(1)

be the set of spin assignments where exactly
n\alpha_{i},n\beta_{i}
vertices are assigned the spin
i\in[q]
on
L,R
, respectively. Denote by
Z_{G}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}}
the contribution to the partition function from configurations in
\Sigma^{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{G}
, i.e.,
Z_{G}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}}=\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma^{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{G}}w_{G}(\sigma)
. We will be interested in those pairs
(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
that contribute significantly to the partition function, as detailed below.

Definition 6

(Phase vectors)
.

Let
\eta>0
. For a
q
-spin system on an
n
-vertex regular bipartite graph
G
, we say that a pair
(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
of
q
-dimensional probability vectors is an
\eta
-phase vector of
G
if
Z_{G}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}}/Z_{G}\geq\mathrm{e}^{-\eta n}
.

Understanding the phase vectors is in general a hard task. For random bipartite regular graphs, these have been identified to lie among the set of fixpoints
(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{c})
of the following so-called tree recursions on the
\Delta
-regular tree
[
15
]
:

r_{i}\propto\lambda_{i}\left(\mbox{$\sum_{j\in[q]}$}B_{ij}c_{j}\right)^{\Delta-1}\mbox{ for $i\in[q]$};\qquad c_{j}\propto\lambda_{j}\left(\mbox{$\sum_{i\in[q]}$}B_{ij}r_{i}\right)^{\Delta-1}\mbox{ for $j\in[q]$}.

(2)

The underpinning principle here leading to this correspondence is that the neighbourhood structure of a random
\Delta
-regular bipartite graph is similar to a
\Delta
-regular tree. Nevertheless, identifying the actual phase vectors, even among the finite-set of fixpoints in (
2
), has turned out to be rather challenging. Even in the canonical case of
q
-colorings, the current best known analysis works for even
q
and is a result of technically intense arguments.

Before looking into this in more detail, we first explain how to convert the information about phase vectors into algorithms. Adapting methods from
[
14
,
21
]
, we show that this is feasible when the phase vectors correspond to maximal bicliques. More precisely, for a non-empty set
S\subseteq[q]
, define the
q
-dimensional probability vector
\mathbf{g}_{S}
whose
i
-th entry is given by
\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\sum_{j\in S}\lambda_{j}}
for
i\in S
, and 0 otherwise. The following notion of “phase maximality” will be important in what follows.

Definition 7

(Phase Maximality)
.

Let
(\mathbf{B},\boldsymbol{\lambda})
be a
q
-spin system and
\Delta\geq 3
. For
\rho>0
and a set of maximal bicliques
\mathcal{B}_{\Delta}
, we say that the spin system is
\rho
-maximal with respect to
\mathcal{B}_{\Delta}
if there is
\eta>0
such that, for almost all
\Delta
-regular bipartite graphs, every
\eta
-phase vector
(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
satisfies
\left\lVert(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})-(\mathbf{g}_{S},\mathbf{g}_{T})\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\rho
for some maximal biclique
(S,T)\in\mathcal{B}_{\Delta}
.

The key new ingredient to prove Theorems
1
and
2
is to establish maximality for the colorings and hard-core models in the corresponding parameter regimes, as detailed in the following theorems.

Lemma 8
.

For even
q\geq 4
and
\Delta\geq 8q\log\Delta
, the
q
-colorings model is
\tfrac{1}{12\Delta q}
-maximal with respect to the set of bicliques
\mathcal{B}_{\Delta}=\{(S,[q]\backslash S)\,\big|\,|S|=\frac{q}{2}\}
.

Lemma 9
.

For
\Delta\geq 50
and
\lambda\geq\tfrac{50}{\Delta}
, the hard-core model with fugacity
\lambda
is
\tfrac{1}{24\Delta}
-maximal with respect to the set of bicliques
\mathcal{B}_{\Delta}=\{(0,01),(01,0)\}
.

Previous approaches in
[
21
,
14
,
22
]
to establish the analogues of Lemmas
8
and
9
used expansion properties of random
\Delta
-regular bipartite graphs which do not however give tight results in terms of the range of the parameters that they apply. Instead, we follow a more direct analytical approach, using the tree-recursions view mentioned in (
2
), further details are given in Section
3
with the final technical bounds obtained in Section
5
. These more precise bounds allow us to push significantly further the applicability of the polymer method, see also the beginning of Section
4
for further explanation.

Indeed, we show that
\rho
-maximality yields approximation algorithms on random
\Delta
-regular bipartite graphs, provided that
\rho
is sufficiently small and that the weight of configurations corresponding to maximal bicliques is sufficiently big relatively to other type of configurations. To capture the latter condition, recall that the entries of
\mathbf{B},\boldsymbol{\lambda}
are between 0 and 1, and each of them includes at least one entry equal to 1. We say that
\mathbf{B}
is a
\delta
-matrix for some
\delta\in[0,1)
if the second largest entry of
\mathbf{B}
is
\leq\delta
, and we denote by
\min(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
the minimum entry in
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
(note that this is strictly bigger than 0). By applying the polymer method appropriately (inspired by
[
14
]
), we show the following in Section
4
.

Lemma 10
.

Let
(\mathbf{B},\boldsymbol{\lambda})
be a
q
-spin system,
\Delta\geq 3
be an integer, and
\rho=\tfrac{1}{12\Delta q}
. Suppose further that
\mathbf{B}
is a
\delta
-matrix for some
\delta\in[0,1)
and that
\Delta(1-\delta)\min(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\geq 7q\big(5+\log\tfrac{(q-1)\Delta^{3}}{\min(\boldsymbol{\lambda})}\big)
.

If the spin system is
\rho
-maximal, then there is an
\mathsf{FPRAS}
for the partition function for almost all
\Delta
-regular bipartite graphs. In fact, for almost all
\Delta
-regular bipartite graphs, for
\epsilon=\exp(-\Omega(n))
, the algorithm produces an
\epsilon
-estimate for the partition function and an
\epsilon
-sample from the Gibbs distribution in time
O\big((\tfrac{n}{\epsilon})^{2}(\log\tfrac{n}{\epsilon})^{3}\big)
.

Using the above ingredients, we can prove our main Theorems
1
and
2
.

Proof of Theorems
1
and
2
.

We first prove the result for colorings, Theorem
1
. We just need to combine Lemmas
8
and
10
. In the setting of Lemma

and Example
5
, we have that the interaction matrix for colorings is a
\delta
-matrix for
\delta=0
and
\min(\boldsymbol{\lambda})=1
. Hence, for
\Delta\geq 100q\log q
, we have that
\Delta(1-\delta)\min(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\geq 7q\big(5+\log\tfrac{(q-1)\Delta^{3}}{\min(\boldsymbol{\lambda})}\big)
as needed. Moreover, Lemma
8
establishes the required
\rho
-maximality that is further needed. Therefore, the conclusion of Lemma

applies and we obtain the Theorem
1
.

The proof for independent sets, Theorem
2
, is analogous, by now combining Lemmas
9
and
10
. We may assume that
\lambda<1
, otherwise the result follows from the FPRAS for
\Delta\geq 53
in
[

, Theorem 1]
. In the setting of Example

, we have that
q=2
,
\delta=0
and
\min(\boldsymbol{\lambda})=\lambda
. Then, for
\lambda>100\tfrac{\log\Delta}{\Delta}

\Delta(1-\delta)\min(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\geq 7q\big(5+\log\tfrac{(q-1)\Delta^{3}}{\min(\boldsymbol{\lambda})}\big)

∎

Finally, as mentioned in the introduction, we give evidence that the bounds on
q
in Theorem

Lemma 11
.

For all even
q\geq 4
and
\Delta=O(q\log q)
, for the
q
-colorings model,
O(\tfrac{1}{\Delta q})
-maximality fails with respect to any set of bicliques on almost all

We note that Lemma

in the non-uniqueness region, polymers can be of size roughly

, which is in contrast to what happens when the polymer method applies (where the size of polymers turns out to be logarithmic in

).

3
Phase vectors on random bipartite regular graphs

Let
(\mathbf{B},\boldsymbol{\lambda})
be a
q
-spin system. In this section, we use results from
[
15
]
to pinpoint the phase vectors on random
\Delta
-regular bipartite graphs, and give a sufficient condition to conclude maximality (Corollary
14
). We will invoke this in Section
5
to prove Lemmas
8
and
9
.

For
q
-dimensional probability vectors
\mathbf{r},\mathbf{c}
, we will consider the function

\Phi_{\mathbf{B},\boldsymbol{\lambda},\Delta}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{c})=\frac{\mathbf{r}^{\intercal}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{c}}{\|\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{-1}\mathbf{r}\|_{p}\|\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{-1}\mathbf{c}\|_{p}},

where
p=\tfrac{\Delta}{\Delta-1}
and
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
is the
q\times q
diagonal matrix whose
i
-th diagonal entry is equal to
\lambda_{i}^{1/\Delta}
. We will be interested in the maximizers
(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{c})
of
\Phi
.

Lemma 12
.

Suppose that the interaction matrix
\mathbf{B}
is ergodic, i.e., irreducible and aperiodic. Then, the maximizers of
\Phi_{\mathbf{B},\boldsymbol{\lambda},\Delta}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{c})
are fixpoints of the tree recursions (
2
).

Proof.

The proof follows by a relatively standard Lagrange multiplier argument. The assumption that
\mathbf{B}
is ergodic is needed to exlcude maximizers at the boundary, i.e., that some entry of
\mathbf{r},\mathbf{c}
is equal to zero. A closely related argument in the case
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=\mathbf{1}
can be found in
[
15
, Lemma 4.11]
.
∎

Let
\mathbf{L}
denote the matrix
\big\{\tfrac{B_{ij}r_{i}c_{j}}{\sqrt{r_{i}^{\prime}c_{j}^{\prime}}}\big\}_{i,j\in[q]}
, where
r_{i}^{\prime}:=r_{i}(\sum_{j\in[q]}B_{ij}c_{j})
for
i\in[q]
and
c_{j}^{\prime}:=c_{j}(\sum_{i\in[q]}B_{ij}r_{i})
for
j\in[q]
. A maximiser

Let

Following

(5)

where

F_{u}=\sum_{i\in S}\lambda_{i}\prod_{v\in V_{\gamma}\cap\partial u}B_{i,\sigma_{\gamma}(v)}\mbox{ if }u\in\partial V_{\gamma}\cap L,\quad F_{u}=\sum_{j\in T}\lambda_{j}\prod_{v\in V_{\gamma}\cap\partial u}B_{j,\sigma_{\gamma}(v)}\mbox{ if }u\in\partial V_{\gamma}\cap R.

(6)

Let
\mathcal{P}^{S,T}_{G}
be the set of all polymers

Lemma 16
.

Every configuration
\Gamma
satisfies
|V_{\Gamma}|\leq 12n/\Delta
.

Proof.

Suppose that there exists a configuration
\Gamma
with
|V_{\Gamma}|>12n/\Delta
. Then, we can extract greedily disjoint configurations
\Gamma_{1},\ldots,\Gamma_{36}\subseteq\Gamma
(which are a collection of polymers belonging to
\Gamma
) such that
\tfrac{n}{6\Delta}<|\Gamma_{i}|\leq\tfrac{n}{3\Delta}
. By Lemma

, we have that
|V_{\Gamma_{i}}^{+}|\geq\tfrac{\Delta-1}{2}|\Gamma_{i}|>\frac{n}{6\Delta}\tfrac{\Delta-1}{2}
and therefore

G
has
2n

V_{\Gamma_{1}}^{+},\ldots,V_{\Gamma_{36}}^{+}
cannot be pairwise disjoint, contradicting the fact that the configuration
\Gamma
consists of pairwise compatible polymers.
∎

The weight
w^{S,T}_{G}(\Gamma)
of a configuration

\Gamma
consists of. We define the partition function of the polymer model as

Z^{S,T}_{G}=\mbox{$\sum_{\Gamma\in\Omega^{S,T}_{G}}$}\,w^{S,T}_{G}(\Gamma),\mbox{ and its Gibbs distribution by }\mu^{S,T}_{G}(\Gamma)=w^{S,T}_{G}(\Gamma)/Z^{S,T}_{G}\mbox{ for $\Gamma\in\Omega^{S,T}_{G}$}.

Finally, we let
Z^{\mathrm{pmer}}_{G}=\sum_{(S,T)\in\mathcal{B}_{\Delta}}\,\big(\mbox{$\sum_{i\in S}$}\,\lambda_{i}\big)^{n}\big(\mbox{$\sum_{j\in T}$}\,\lambda_{j}\big)^{n}Z^{S,T}_{G}
.

Lemma 17
.

Let
\Delta\geq 3
be an integer, and
(\mathbf{B},\boldsymbol{\lambda})
be a
q
-spin system which is
\tfrac{1}{12\Delta q}
-maximal with respect to a set of maximal bicliques
\mathcal{B}_{\Delta}
. Suppose further that
\Delta\min(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\geq 15q
. Then, there is
\epsilon=\mathrm{e}^{-\Omega(n)}
such that, for almost all
\Delta
-regular bipartite graphs
G
with
n
vertices on each part, it holds that
(1-\epsilon)Z_{G}\leq Z^{\mathrm{pmer}}_{G}\leq(1+\epsilon)Z_{G}
.

Proof.

By the
\tfrac{1}{12\Delta q}
-maximality of the spin system with respect to
\mathcal{B}_{\Delta}
(cf. Definition
7
), there is an
\eta>0
such that for almost all
\Delta
-regular graphs
G
, every
\eta
-phase vector
(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
of
G
belongs to

\mathcal{F}_{\Delta}:=\Big\{(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})\,\Big|\,\|(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})-(\mathbf{g}_{S},\mathbf{g}_{T})\|_{\infty}\leq\tfrac{1}{12\Delta q}\mbox{ for some maximal biclique }(S,T)\in\mathcal{B}_{\Delta}\Big\}.

Let
\Sigma_{G}^{\max}=\{\sigma\mid\sigma\in\Sigma^{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{G}\mbox{ for some }(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})\in\mathcal{F}_{\Delta}\}
where, recall from (
1
), that
\Sigma^{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{G}
is the set of spin assignments where exactly
n\alpha_{i},n\beta_{i}
vertices are assigned the spin

on

, respectively.

We first show the lower bound on
Z^{\mathrm{pmer}}_{G}
. Consider the polymer model corresponding to a maximal biclique
(S,T)\in\mathcal{B}_{\Delta}
. Every configuration
\Gamma\in\Omega^{S,T}_{G}
maps to a set of spin assignments

\Sigma^{S,T}_{G}(\Gamma)=\{\sigma:V\rightarrow[q]\mid\sigma(V_{\Gamma})=\sigma_{\Gamma},\,\sigma(L\backslash V_{\Gamma})\subseteq S,\,\sigma(R\backslash V_{\Gamma})\subseteq T\},

where recall that
\sigma_{\Gamma}
is a spin assignment on
V_{\Gamma}
that satisfies
\sigma_{\Gamma}(V_{\Gamma}\cap L)\subseteq[q]\backslash S
and
\sigma_{\Gamma}(V_{\Gamma}\cap R)\subseteq[q]\backslash T
. Therefore, for distinct
\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime}\in\Omega^{S,T}_{G}

\Sigma^{S,T}_{G}(\Gamma)
and
\Sigma^{S,T}_{G}(\Gamma^{\prime})
are disjoint. Let
\Sigma^{S,T}_{G}=\bigcup_{\Gamma\in\Omega^{S,T}_{G}}\Sigma^{S,T}_{G}(\Gamma)
. Using that configurations
\Gamma
consist of disjoint

-connected sets, we obtain that the aggregate weight
\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma^{S,T}_{G}(\Gamma)}w_{G}(\sigma)
equals
\big(\mbox{$\sum_{i\in S}$}\,\lambda_{i}\big)^{n}\big(\mbox{$\sum_{j\in T}$}\,\lambda_{j}\big)^{n}w^{S,T}_{G}(\Gamma)
(see for example
[
14

), and therefore

Z^{S,T}_{G}=\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma^{S,T}_{G}}w_{G}(\sigma).

Moreover, note that for
(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
with
\|(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})-(\mathbf{g}_{S},\mathbf{g}_{T})\|_{\infty}\leq\tfrac{1}{12\Delta q}
, the number of vertices in
L

is at most
\tfrac{n}{12\Delta}
, and similarly for vertices in
R

. Observe now that every

\eta
-phase vector and therefore

. There are at most

n\boldsymbol{\alpha},n\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{Z}^{q}

Z_{G}-Z^{\mathrm{pmer}}_{G}\leq\sum_{(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})\notin\mathcal{F}_{\Delta}}Z^{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{G}\leq n^{2q}\mathrm{e}^{-\eta n}Z_{G}\leq\mathrm{e}^{-\Omega(n)}Z_{G},

showing that
Z^{\mathrm{pmer}}_{G}\geq(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\Omega(n)})Z_{G}
.

We next show the upper bound on

. Consider
\Sigma^{\mathrm{overlap}}_{G}=\bigcup_{(S,T)\neq(S^{\prime},T^{\prime})\in\mathcal{B}_{\Delta}}(\Sigma^{S,T}_{G}\cap\Sigma^{S^{\prime},T^{\prime}}_{G})

\eta

. Therefore we obtain that the aggregate weight of spin assignments in

.

It remains to prove that
\Sigma^{\mathrm{overlap}}_{G}\subseteq\Sigma_{G}\backslash\Sigma_{G}^{\max}

\sigma

(S,T),(S^{\prime},T^{\prime})\in\mathcal{B}_{\Delta}

such that
\sigma\in\Sigma^{S,T}_{G}\cap\Sigma^{S^{\prime},T^{\prime}}_{G}
and
\sigma\in\Sigma^{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{G}

(S,T)
and
(S^{\prime},T^{\prime})

and

(S^{*},T^{*})

and

be the vertices in

that have the spin

\sigma

\sigma\in\Sigma^{S,T}_{G}(\Gamma)
for some

are
\leq 1

\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\sum_{i^{\prime}\in S^{*}}\lambda_{i^{\prime}}}\geq\min(\boldsymbol{\lambda})/q\geq 15/\Delta

|\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\sum_{i^{\prime}\in S^{*}}\lambda_{i^{\prime}}}-\tfrac{n_{i}}{n}|\geq\tfrac{3}{\Delta}>\tfrac{1}{12\Delta q}

[
8
]
, as employed for general spin systems in
[

]
.

Lemma
10
.

Let
(\mathbf{B},\boldsymbol{\lambda})
be a
q
-spin system,
\Delta\geq 3
be an integer, and
\rho=\tfrac{1}{12\Delta q}
. Suppose further that
\mathbf{B}
is a
\delta
-matrix for some
\delta\in[0,1)
and that
\Delta(1-\delta)\min(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\geq 7q\big(5+\log\tfrac{(q-1)\Delta^{3}}{\min(\boldsymbol{\lambda})}\big)
.

If the spin system is
\rho
-maximal, then there is an
\mathsf{FPRAS}
for the partition function for almost all
\Delta
-regular bipartite graphs. In fact, for almost all
\Delta
-regular bipartite graphs, for
\epsilon=\exp(-\Omega(n))
, the algorithm produces an
\epsilon
-estimate for the partition function and an
\epsilon
-sample from the Gibbs distribution in time
O\big((\tfrac{n}{\epsilon})^{2}(\log\tfrac{n}{\epsilon})^{3}\big)
.

Proof.

The main ingredient that we need to check is the so-called polymer sampling condition
[
8
, Definition 4]
for each polymer model defined by bicliques
(S,T)\in\mathcal{B}_{\Delta}
; this gives an
\epsilon
-counting algorithm for
Z^{S,T}_{G}
and an
\epsilon
-sampling algorithm for
\mu^{S,T}_{G}
with the desired guarantees. The estimates in Lemma
17
then yield that these algorithms can be extended to algorithms for
Z_{G}
, by the argument in
[
14
, Proof of Theorem 3]
.

The polymer sampling condition captures that the weight of the polymers as a function of their size decays exponentially relatively to the growth rate of the number of polymers (containing a vertex); in this case, since we are working with
G^{3}
whose degree is bounded by
\Delta^{3}
, the condition we need to check, cf.
[
8
, Definition 4]
, is that
w^{S,T}_{G}\leq\mathrm{e}^{-\tau|\gamma|}
for some constant
\tau\geq 5+3\log((q-1)\Delta^{3})
.

Let
\gamma=(V_{\gamma},\sigma_{\gamma})\in\mathcal{P}^{S,T}_{G}
. Since the entries of
\mathbf{B},\boldsymbol{\lambda}
are
\leq 1
, we have from (
5
) that

w^{S,T}_{G}(\gamma)\leq\frac{\prod_{u\in\partial V_{\gamma}}F_{u}}{\big(\sum_{i\in S}\lambda_{i})^{|V_{\gamma}^{+}\cap L|}\big(\sum_{j\in T}\lambda_{j}\big)^{|V_{\gamma}^{+}\cap R|}},

Now for
u\in\partial V_{\gamma}\cap L
, recall that
F_{u}=\sum_{i\in S}\lambda_{i}\prod_{v\in V_{\gamma}\cap\partial u}B_{i,\sigma_{\gamma}(v)}
. We have that there exist
i\in S
and
v\in V_{\gamma}\cap\partial u
such that
B_{i,\sigma_{\gamma}(v)}\leq\delta
; otherwise, since
\mathbf{B}
is a
\delta
-matrix, we would have that
B_{i,\sigma_{\gamma}(v)}=1
for all
i\in S
, and therefore
(S,T\cup\{\sigma_{\gamma}(v)\})
would be a biclique, contradicting the maximality of
(S,T)
since
\sigma_{\gamma}(v)\notin T
(by the definition of
\mathcal{P}^{S,T}_{G}
). We therefore obtain that
F_{u}\leq\sum_{i\in S}\lambda_{i}-(1-\delta)\min(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
. Similarly, for
u\in\partial V_{\gamma}\cap R
, we have that
F_{u}\leq\sum_{j\in T}\lambda_{j}-(1-\delta)\min(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
. Using the crude bounds
\min(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\leq\sum_{i\in S}\lambda_{i},\sum_{j\in T}\lambda_{j}\leq q
, we obtain that

w^{S,T}_{G}(\gamma)\leq(\min(\boldsymbol{\lambda}))^{-|V_{\gamma}|}\Big(1-\frac{(1-\delta)\min(\boldsymbol{\lambda})}{q}\Big)^{-|\partial V_{\gamma}|}.

By the
\rho
-extremality assumption (or, more precisely, by the definition of the set of polymers
\mathcal{P}^{S,T}_{G}
), we have that
|V_{\gamma}|\leq 2q\rho n\leq\tfrac{n}{6\Delta}
, and therefore, by Lemma
15
,
|\partial V_{\gamma}|\geq\tfrac{\Delta}{7}V_{\gamma}
. We therefore have that

w^{S,T}_{G}(\gamma)\leq\mathrm{e}^{-\big(\log(\min{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})+\tfrac{\Delta(1-\delta)\min(\boldsymbol{\lambda})}{7q}\big)|V_{\gamma}|}

Thus the polymer sampling condition is satisfied as long as
\tfrac{\Delta(1-\delta)\min(\boldsymbol{\lambda})}{7q}+\log(\min(\boldsymbol{\lambda}))\geq 5+3\log((q-1)\Delta^{3})
, which gives the desired conclusion.
∎

In this section we establish phase maximality for colorings and hard-core model.
In particular, we prove Lemmas
8
and
9
from Section
2.2
. Recall that the tree-recursion on the
\Delta
-regular tree for a general

-spin system with interaction matrix
\mathbf{B}

r_{i}\propto\lambda_{i}\left(\mbox{$\sum_{j\in[q]}$}B_{ij}c_{j}\right)^{\Delta-1}\mbox{ for $i\in[q]$};\qquad c_{j}\propto\lambda_{j}\left(\mbox{$\sum_{i\in[q]}$}B_{ij}r_{i}\right)^{\Delta-1},\mbox{ for $j\in[q]$}.

(
2
)

For the colorings and hard-core models, Lemma
12
shows that the fixpoints of (
2
) include all maximizers of the function

\Phi_{\mathbf{B},\boldsymbol{\lambda},\Delta}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{c})=\frac{\mathbf{r}^{\intercal}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{c}}{\|\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{-1}\mathbf{r}\|_{p}\|\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{-1}\mathbf{c}\|_{p}}

where

and
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
is the diagonal matrix whose

\lambda_{i}^{1/\Delta}
.
Finally, Corollary
14
implies that, to show
\tfrac{1}{12\Delta q}
-maximality for a set of maximal bicliques
\mathcal{B}_{\Delta}
, it is enough to show that
all maximizers
(\mathbf{r}^{*},\mathbf{c}^{*})
of
\Phi_{\mathbf{B},\boldsymbol{\lambda},\Delta}
are Hessian dominant and satisfy that
\|(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*})-(\mathbf{g}_{S},\mathbf{g}_{T})\|_{\infty}\leq\tfrac{1}{15\Delta q}
for some
(S,T)\in\mathcal{B}_{\Delta}
, where
(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*})=(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{r}^{*}),\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{c}^{*}))
is given by

\alpha^{*}_{i}=\frac{(\lambda_{i}^{-1/\Delta}r^{*}_{i})^{p}}{\|\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{-1}\mathbf{r}^{*}\|_{p}^{p}}\quad\text{and}\quad\beta^{*}_{i}=\frac{(\lambda_{i}^{-1/\Delta}c^{*}_{i})^{p}}{\|\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{-1}\mathbf{c}^{*}\|_{p}^{p}}\quad\text{for~}i\in[q].

5.1
Phase maximality for colorings

In this subsection we prove Lemma
8
by showing phase maximality for colorings.

Let
q,\Delta\geq 3
be integers and let
d=\Delta-1
.
For
q
-colorings, using the correspondence in Example
5
, the tree-recursion can be written as:

r_{i}=\frac{(1-c_{i})^{\Delta-1}}{\sum_{j\in[q]}(1-c_{j})^{\Delta-1}},\quad c_{i}=\frac{(1-r_{i})^{\Delta-1}}{\sum_{j\in[q]}(1-r_{j})^{\Delta-1}}\quad\mbox{for~}i\in[q].

(7)

Note that
(\mathbf{g}_{[q]},\mathbf{g}_{[q]})
is a trivial solution to (
7
).
The following lemma summarizes results from
[
15
, Section 7]
and describes the nontrivial fixpoints of the tree recursion (
7
) when
q\geq 4
is an even integer in the non-uniqueness region
q<\Delta
.

Lemma 18

(
[
15
]
)
.

Suppose that
q\geq 4
is even and
\Delta>q
.
Then there is a one-to-one correspondence between all maximizers of
\Phi_{\mathbf{B},\mathbf{1},\Delta}
and all bicliques in
\mathcal{B}_{\Delta}=\{(S,[q]\backslash S)\,\big|\,|S|=\frac{q}{2}\}
:
there exists
a=a(\Delta,q)
,
b=b(\Delta,q)
satisfying
0<b<a<\frac{2}{q}
and
a+b=\frac{2}{q}
, such that
every biclique
(S,[q]\setminus S)\in\mathcal{B}_{\Delta}
corresponds to a maximizer
(\mathbf{r}^{*},\mathbf{c}^{*})
of the form

r^{*}_{i}=\left\{\begin{aligned} a,\quad&i\in S;\\
b,\quad&i\in[q]\setminus S,\end{aligned}\right.\quad\text{and}\quad c^{*}_{i}=\left\{\begin{aligned} b,\quad&i\in S;\\
a,\quad&i\in[q]\setminus S.\end{aligned}\right.

Furthermore, all maximizers of
\Phi_{\mathbf{B},\mathbf{1},\Delta}
are Hessian dominant.

We now prove Lemma
8
, which we restate here for convenience.

Lemma
8
.

For even
q\geq 4
and
\Delta\geq 8q\log\Delta
, the
q
-colorings model is
\tfrac{1}{12\Delta q}
-maximal with respect to the set of bicliques
\mathcal{B}_{\Delta}=\{(S,[q]\backslash S)\,\big|\,|S|=\frac{q}{2}\}
.

Proof.

Let
k=\frac{q}{2}
and
d=\Delta-1
for convenience.
By Lemmas
12
and
18
, for a given biclique
(S,[q]\setminus S)\in\mathcal{B}_{\Delta}
, the corresponding maximizer
(\mathbf{r}^{*},\mathbf{c}^{*})
of
\Phi_{\mathbf{B},\mathbf{1},\Delta}
satisfies the tree-recursion (
7
) as follows:

\left\{\begin{aligned} a&=\frac{\left(ka+(k-1)b\right)^{d}}{k\left[\left(ka+(k-1)b\right)^{d}+\left((k-1)a+kb\right)^{d}\right]};\\
b&=\frac{\left((k-1)a+kb\right)^{d}}{k\left[\left(ka+(k-1)b\right)^{d}+\left((k-1)a+kb\right)^{d}\right]},\end{aligned}\right.

where
a,b
are the constants given in Lemma
18
.
We are going to show that, for sufficiently large
\Delta
, the constant
a
is close to
\frac{2}{q}
and the constant
b
is close to
0
.
Taking the ratio of
a
and
b
, we get

\frac{a}{b}=\left(\frac{ka+(k-1)b}{(k-1)a+kb}\right)^{d},\mbox{ and therefore }h=\left(\frac{h+t}{th+1}\right)^{d}.

where
h=\frac{a}{b}>1
and
t=\frac{k-1}{k}=1-\frac{2}{q}
. Consider the function
f(x)=\left(\frac{x+t}{tx+1}\right)^{d}
.
Then
h
is a fixpoint of
f
(i.e.,
f(h)=h
).
In fact, the function
f
has three fixpoints:
x=h>1
,
x=1
, and
x=\frac{1}{h}<1
.
Let
h_{0}=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{d}{2q}}>3
when
d\geq 3q
.
We show next that
h>h_{0}
.
By considering the monotone intervals of
f(x)-x
, it suffices to show that
f(h_{0})>h_{0}
.
We then compute that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}\log f(h_{0})

\displaystyle=\log\left(\frac{h_{0}+t}{th_{0}+1}\right)=\log\left(1+\frac{(1-t)(h_{0}-1)}{th_{0}+1}\right)

\displaystyle>\frac{(1-t)(h_{0}-1)}{2(th_{0}+1)}>\frac{1-t}{4}=\frac{1}{2q},

where the first inequality follows from
\log(1+\varepsilon)>\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
for
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
and the second inequality is due to
h_{0}>3
and
t<1
.
Therefore,
f(h_{0})>\mathrm{e}^{\frac{d}{2q}}=h_{0}
and thus
h>h_{0}
.

Finally, notice that
(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*})=(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{r}^{*}),\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{c}^{*}))
is also in the form of

\alpha^{*}_{i}=\left\{\begin{aligned} a^{\prime},\quad&i\in S;\\
b^{\prime},\quad&i\in[q]\setminus S,\end{aligned}\right.\quad\text{and}\quad\beta^{*}_{i}=\left\{\begin{aligned} b^{\prime},\quad&i\in S;\\
a^{\prime},\quad&i\in[q]\setminus S,\end{aligned}\right.

where
0<b^{\prime}<a^{\prime}<\frac{2}{q}
,
a^{\prime}+b^{\prime}=\frac{2}{q}
, and
\frac{a^{\prime}}{b^{\prime}}=\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^{p}>\frac{a}{b}=h>h_{0}=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{d}{2q}}
.
It follows that

\left\lVert(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*})-(\mathbf{g}_{S},\mathbf{g}_{[q]\setminus S})\right\rVert_{\infty}=b^{\prime}=\frac{2}{q(\frac{a^{\prime}}{b^{\prime}}+1)}<\frac{2\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{d}{2q}}}{q}\leq\frac{1}{15\Delta q},

where the last inequality holds for
\Delta\geq 8q\log\Delta
.
The lemma then follows from Corollary
14
, using the fact from Lemma
18
that all maximizers are Hessian dominant.
∎

Our proof of Lemma
8
can also be modified to show that
O(\frac{1}{\Delta q})
-maximality fails when
q=\Omega(\frac{\Delta}{\log\Delta})
.
This allows us to prove Lemma
11
from Section
2.2
.

Proof of Lemma
11
.

We use the same notation and approach as in the proof of Lemma
8
.
In particular, we show that
h<h_{1}
for
h_{1}=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{4d}{q}}
, which can be deduced from
f(h_{1})<h_{1}
.
We have that

\frac{1}{d}\log f(h_{1})=\log\left(\frac{h_{1}+t}{th_{1}+1}\right)=\log\left(1+\frac{(1-t)(h_{1}-1)}{th_{1}+1}\right)\\
<\frac{(1-t)(h_{1}-1)}{th_{1}+1}\leq\frac{1-t}{t}\leq\frac{4}{q},

where the last inequality is because
t=1-\frac{2}{q}\geq\frac{1}{2}
.
Therefore, we get
f(h_{1})<\mathrm{e}^{\frac{4d}{q}}=h_{1}
and consequently
h<h_{1}
.
It follows that
\frac{a^{\prime}}{b^{\prime}}=h^{p}<\mathrm{e}^{\frac{4dp}{q}}=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{4\Delta}{q}}
,
and thus for
q\geq\frac{4\Delta}{\log\Delta}
one has

\left\lVert(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*})-(\mathbf{g}_{S},\mathbf{g}_{[q]\setminus S})\right\rVert_{\infty}=b^{\prime}=\frac{2}{q(\frac{a^{\prime}}{b^{\prime}}+1)}>\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{4\Delta}{q}}}{q}\geq\frac{1}{\Delta q}.

Combining with Lemma
13
, this gives that
\frac{1}{2\Delta q}
-maximality fails when
q\geq\frac{4\Delta}{\log\Delta}
.
∎

5.2
Phase maximality for hard-core model

In this subsection we consider the hard-core model and establishes phase maximality. The goal is to prove Lemma
9
from Section
2.2
.

Let
\Delta\geq 3
be an integer and
\lambda>0
be a real.
Recall from Example
5
that the interaction matrix
\mathbf{B}=\{B_{ij}\}_{i,j\in\{0,1\}}
for the hard-core model is given by
B_{00}=B_{01}=B_{10}=1
and
B_{11}=0
, and the activity vector with fugacity
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i\in\{0,1\}}
is given by
\lambda_{0}=1
and
\lambda_{1}=\lambda
.
Hence, the tree-recursion (
2
) becomes:

r_{1}=\frac{\lambda c_{0}^{\Delta-1}}{\lambda c_{0}^{\Delta-1}+1},\quad r_{0}=\frac{1}{\lambda c_{0}^{\Delta-1}+1},\quad c_{1}=\frac{\lambda r_{0}^{\Delta-1}}{\lambda r_{0}^{\Delta-1}+1},\quad c_{0}=\frac{1}{\lambda r_{0}^{\Delta-1}+1}.

As is standard, it would be easier to work with the ratios
x=\frac{r_{1}}{r_{0}}
and
y=\frac{c_{1}}{c_{0}}
, so that the tree-recursion can be equivalently written as

x=\frac{\lambda}{(1+y)^{\Delta-1}},\quad y=\frac{\lambda}{(1+x)^{\Delta-1}}.

(8)

Note that the function
f(x)=\frac{\lambda}{(1+x)^{\Delta-1}}
has a unique fixpoint
x_{0}
, and we are interested in the nontrivial solutions to (
8
) (i.e.,
(x,y)\neq(x_{0},x_{0})
). We restate Lemma
9
here for convenience.

Lemma
9
.

For
\Delta\geq 50
and
\lambda\geq\tfrac{50}{\Delta}
, the hard-core model with fugacity
\lambda
is
\tfrac{1}{24\Delta}
-maximal with respect to the set of bicliques
\mathcal{B}_{\Delta}=\{(0,01),(01,0)\}
.

Proof.

Take an arbitrary maximizer
(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{c})
of
\Phi_{\mathbf{B},\boldsymbol{\lambda},\Delta}
and let
x=r^{*}_{1}/r^{*}_{0}
,
y=c^{*}_{1}/c^{*}_{0}
.
It is known that
x\neq x_{0}
,
y\neq x_{0}
, and
(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{c})
is Hessian dominant when
\lambda>\lambda_{c}(\Delta)
is in the non-uniqueness region; see, e.g.,
[
16
,
15
]
. Suppose that
x<y
without loss of generality.

We first show that
x\leq\frac{1}{30\lambda\Delta^{2}}
when
\Delta\geq 50
and
\lambda\geq\frac{50}{\Delta}
.
By (
8
) we have

\lambda=x(1+y)^{\Delta-1}=y(1+x)^{\Delta-1}.

Define
f(t)=\frac{(1+t)^{\Delta-1}}{t}
, and note that
f(x)=f(y)
. The function
f(t)
is monotone decreasing when
t<\frac{1}{\Delta-2}
and monotone increasing when
t>\frac{1}{\Delta-2}
.
This implies
x<\frac{1}{\Delta-2}<y
.
We then deduce from (
8
) that for
\Delta\geq 50
and
\lambda\geq\frac{50}{\Delta}
,

y=\frac{\lambda}{(1+x)^{\Delta-1}}\geq\frac{\lambda}{(1+\frac{1}{\Delta-2})^{\Delta-1}}\geq\frac{\lambda}{3}>\frac{1}{\Delta-2}.

Hence,

f(x)=f(y)\geq f\left(\frac{\lambda}{3}\right)=\frac{3}{\lambda}\left(1+\frac{\lambda}{3}\right)^{\Delta-1}.

Meanwhile, we have

f\left(\frac{1}{30\lambda\Delta^{2}}\right)=30\lambda\Delta^{2}\left(1+\frac{1}{30\lambda\Delta^{2}}\right)^{\Delta-1}\leq 30\lambda\Delta^{2}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{1}{30\lambda\Delta}}\leq 33\lambda\Delta^{2}.

We claim that

11(\lambda\Delta)^{2}\leq\left(1+\frac{\lambda}{3}\right)^{\Delta-1}

(9)

when
\Delta\geq 50
and
\lambda\geq\frac{50}{\Delta}
.
Given (
9
), we get

f\left(\frac{1}{30\lambda\Delta^{2}}\right)\leq 33\lambda\Delta^{2}\leq\frac{3}{\lambda}\left(1+\frac{\lambda}{3}\right)^{\Delta-1}\leq f(x)

and thus
x\leq\frac{1}{30\lambda\Delta^{2}}
as wanted.
It remains to prove (
9
).
We consider two cases.
If
\lambda\leq 1
, then we have

\frac{4}{3}\left(1+\frac{\lambda}{3}\right)^{\Delta-1}\geq\left(1+\frac{\lambda}{3}\right)^{\Delta}\geq\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\lambda\Delta}{3+\lambda}}\geq\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\lambda\Delta}{4}}\geq 15(\lambda\Delta)^{2},

where the second inequality follows from
1+\varepsilon\geq\exp(\frac{\varepsilon}{1+\varepsilon})
for
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
, and the last inequality holds when
\lambda\Delta\geq 50
.
Meanwhile, if
\lambda>1
then we have

\frac{9}{\lambda^{2}}\left(1+\frac{\lambda}{3}\right)^{\Delta-1}\geq\left(1+\frac{\lambda}{3}\right)^{\Delta-3}\geq\left(\frac{4}{3}\right)^{\Delta-3}\geq 100\Delta^{2},

where the last inequality holds when
\Delta\geq 50
.
Therefore, (
9
) holds when
\Delta\geq 50
and
\lambda\geq\frac{50}{\Delta}
, and we conclude with
x\leq\frac{1}{30\lambda\Delta^{2}}
in this parameter regime.

Now, for a fixed
\Delta
, both the fixpoint
(\mathbf{r}^{*},\mathbf{c}^{*})
of the tree recursion with
r^{*}_{1}/r^{*}_{0}<c^{*}_{1}/c^{*}_{0}
and the ground state
(\mathbf{g}_{0},\mathbf{g}_{01})
converge to
(1,0,0,1)
as
\lambda
tends to infinity.
Consequently,
(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*})
converges to the same point as well.
Hence, for
3\leq\Delta<50
, there exists a universal constant
C>0
such that
\left\lVert(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*})-(\mathbf{g}_{0},\mathbf{g}_{01})\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{30\Delta}
whenever
\lambda\geq\frac{C}{\Delta}
.
It remains to deal with the case that
\Delta\geq 50
and
\lambda\geq\frac{50}{\Delta}
.
Observe that

\left\lVert(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*})-(\mathbf{g}_{0},\mathbf{g}_{01})\right\rVert_{\infty}=\max\left\{\alpha^{*}_{1},\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}-\beta^{*}_{1}\right\}.

We will upper bound the two terms using our bound on
x
.
Recall that
p=\frac{\Delta}{\Delta-1}
.
First, we have

\alpha^{*}_{1}\leq\frac{\alpha^{*}_{1}}{\alpha^{*}_{0}}=\left(\lambda^{-\frac{1}{\Delta}}\frac{r^{*}_{1}}{r^{*}_{0}}\right)^{p}=\lambda^{-\frac{1}{\Delta-1}}x^{p}\leq 2x\leq\frac{1}{15\lambda\Delta^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{30\Delta},

where
\lambda^{-\frac{1}{\Delta-1}}\leq 2
in our parameter regime.
Next, notice that

\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}-\beta^{*}_{1}=\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}-\frac{\beta^{*}_{1}/\beta^{*}_{0}}{1+\beta^{*}_{1}/\beta^{*}_{0}}=\frac{\lambda-\beta^{*}_{1}/\beta^{*}_{0}}{(1+\lambda)(1+\beta^{*}_{1}/\beta^{*}_{0})}\leq\lambda-\frac{\beta^{*}_{1}}{\beta^{*}_{0}}.

Since we have

\frac{1}{\lambda}\frac{\beta^{*}_{1}}{\beta^{*}_{0}}=\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(\lambda^{-\frac{1}{\Delta}}\frac{c^{*}_{1}}{c^{*}_{0}}\right)^{p}=\left(\frac{y}{\lambda}\right)^{p}=\frac{1}{(1+x)^{\Delta}},

it follows that

\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}-\beta^{*}_{1}\leq\lambda\left(1-\frac{1}{(1+x)^{\Delta}}\right)\leq\lambda\left(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\Delta x}\right)\leq\lambda\Delta x\leq\frac{1}{30\Delta}.

This yields
\left\lVert(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*})-(\mathbf{g}_{0},\mathbf{g}_{01})\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{30\Delta}
.
The lemma then follows from Corollary
14
.
∎
