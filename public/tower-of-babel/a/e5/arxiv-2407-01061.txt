Abstract
. We provide a sharp C 1 , α \displaystyle C^{1,\alpha} estimate up to the boundary for a viscosity solution of a degenerate fully nonlinear elliptic equation with the oblique boundary condition on a C 1 \displaystyle C^{1} domain. To this end, we first obtain a uniform boundary Hölder estimate with the oblique boundary condition in an “almost C 1 \displaystyle C^{1} -flat” domain for the equations which is uniformly elliptic only where the gradient is far from some point, and then we establish a desired C 1 , α \displaystyle C^{1,\alpha} regularity based on perturbation and compactness arguments.

1.
Introduction

This paper is concerned with the boundary
\displaystyle C^{1,\alpha}
regularity
for a viscosity solution of the degenerate fully nonlinear elliptic
equation with oblique boundary condition

(1.1)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{cases}|Du|^{\gamma}F(D^{2}u)=f\ &\text{ in }\Omega\\
\beta\cdot Du=g&\text{ on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

where
\displaystyle\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a bounded
\displaystyle C^{1}
domain with
boundary
\displaystyle\partial\Omega
,
\displaystyle F=F(M)
is uniformly elliptic with

\displaystyle F(0)=0
,
\displaystyle\gamma\geq 0
,
\displaystyle f\in C(\Omega)
,
\displaystyle g\in C^{\alpha}(\partial\Omega)
and
\displaystyle\beta\in C^{\alpha}(\partial\Omega)

is a vector-valued function satisfying the oblique condition below
in (
2.1
).

This type of a singular/degenerate fully nonlinear equation was
studied first by Birindelli and Demengel in the pioneering works

[
11
,
12
]
for the singular case that

\displaystyle-1<\gamma<0
. For the degenerate case that
\displaystyle\gamma>0
, the
groundbreaking work was made by Imbert and Silvestre in

[
23
]
where they showed an interior
\displaystyle C^{1,\alpha}

estimate for a solution of a degenerate fully nonlinear equation. In

[
2
]
Araújo, Ricarte and Teixeira proved an optimal interior
\displaystyle C^{1,\alpha}
estimate when
\displaystyle F
is convex. For the related interior regularity
results, we refer to
[
4
,
1
,
28
,
15
,
22
,
21
]
and the references therein.

For the Dirichlet boundary condition there has been much progress in
the regularity of solutions of degenerate equations up to the
boundary. Birindelli and Demengel
[
13
]
proved a global

\displaystyle C^{1,\alpha}
regularity under the regular boundary datum.
Araújo and Sirakov
[
3
]
proved a sharp boundary

\displaystyle C^{1,\alpha}
regularity under the
\displaystyle C^{1,\alpha}
boundary datum
and a
\displaystyle C^{2}
domain, and they also obtained an optimal global

\displaystyle C^{1,\alpha}
regularity when
\displaystyle F
is convex. D. Li and X. Li

[
29
]
proved
\displaystyle C^{1,\alpha}
regularity on a
\displaystyle C^{1,\alpha}

domain without using the flattening argument. For a further
discussion on the regularity for the Dirichlet boundary condition,
see
[
5
,
27
]
and the references therein.

For the Neumann boundary condition Milakis and Silvestre

[
31
]
obtained regularity results for the uniformly elliptic case that
\displaystyle\gamma=0

including
\displaystyle C^{1,\alpha}
estimates on a flat domain. For the oblique
boundary condition D. Li and K. Zhang
[
30
]
obtained
regularity estimates results such as
\displaystyle C^{1,\alpha}
on a
\displaystyle C^{1}

domain for the uniformly elliptic case. Further notable regularity results regarding
the Neumann and oblique boundary condition are to be
found in for uniformly elliptic equation, see
[
9
,
8
,
10
,
16
,
17
]
, etc.

For the singular/degenerate case Patrizi
[
32
]
proved a
global
\displaystyle C^{1,\alpha}
regularity for the singular case under the
homogeneous Neumann condition and on a
\displaystyle C^{2}
domain. Birindelli,
Demengel and Leoni
[
14
]
proved the existence and
uniqueness, and then a global Hölder regularity for the
singular/degenerate case under the mixed boundary condition.
Banerjee and Verma
[
6
]
established a global

\displaystyle C^{1,\alpha}
regularity for the degenerate case under the
nonhomogeneous Neumann condition and on a
\displaystyle C^{2}
domain. Ricarte

[
33
]
proved an optimal
\displaystyle C^{1,\alpha}
regularity under a

\displaystyle C^{2}
domain when
\displaystyle F
is convex.

Our purpose in this paper is to obtain an optimal
\displaystyle C^{1,\alpha}

regularity for the degenerate problem (
1.1
). Comparing with
the earlier papers mentioned above, there are two main points. One
is to consider the oblique boundary condition which is a natural
extension of the Neumann boundary condition. The other is to relax a
regularity requirement on the boundary of the domain, namely from

\displaystyle C^{2}
to
\displaystyle C^{1}
. To this end, we are going to show that a solution

\displaystyle u
under consideration can be approximated by an affine function
with an error of order
\displaystyle r^{1+\alpha}
in any ball of the radius
\displaystyle r
.
Our proof is based on the iterative argument to find a sequence of
affine function
\displaystyle l_{k}
with
\displaystyle u-l_{k}
getting smaller in the smaller
ball. This can be made by the scaling and compactness argument
applied to
\displaystyle v=u-l
with
\displaystyle l(x)=a-q\cdot x
being an affine
function. However
\displaystyle u-l
is not a solution of (
1.1
), instead
it is a solution of

(1.2)

\displaystyle\displaystyle|Dv-q|^{\gamma}F(D^{2}v)=f.

Therefore we need to obtain an equicontinuous estimate for a
solution of (
1.2
) independent of
\displaystyle q
in order to use the
compactness argument.

In
[
23
]
an interior Hölder estimate, independent of

\displaystyle q
, was proved depending on whether
\displaystyle|q|
is large or
\displaystyle|q|
is
small. When
\displaystyle|q|
is small, (
1.2
) is uniformly elliptic where
the gradient
\displaystyle Dv
is large, and so the Hölder estimate can be
shown by the method of sliding cusps in
[
24
]
. On the
other hand when
\displaystyle|q|
is large, the Lipschitz estimate can be proved
by adapting Ishii-Lion’s method in
[
20
]
. For the Neumann
boundary case in
[
6
]
, a boundary Hölder estimate
on the flat domain was derived in the spirit of
[
23
]
.
When
\displaystyle|q|
is small, a boundary Hölder estimate on the flat domain, for the uniformly elliptic equation that holds only where
the gradient is large, was derived as in
[
24
]
by adapting the method of sliding cusps. When
\displaystyle|q|
is large, however in general
it seems difficult to use the Ishii-Lion’s method for the Neumann
and oblique case. Indeed, there are notable results for the Neumann
and oblique boundary case by using the Ishii-Lion’s method as in

[
32
,
14
]
where the homogeneous Neumann boundary
condition is treated, and, as in
[
7
,
25
]
where
additional regularity requirements both on the oblique vector
function
\displaystyle\beta
and on the boundary of on the domain are added,
respectively. In this regards the authors in
[
6
]

instead adapted the approach used by Colombo and Figalli in

[
19
]
, which was motivated by Savin in the paper

[
34
]
, to use the method of sliding paraboloids for the
establishing of a boundary Hölder estimate on the flat domain
for the uniformly elliptic equation that holds only where the
gradient is small.

Our analytic tools in the present paper are based on those in the
previous papers
[
24
]
, and,
[
6
]
where the
standard flattening argument is used as the boundary of the
underlying domain is regular enough, say
\displaystyle C^{2}
. On the other hand,
since the domain under consideration is allowed to be
\displaystyle C^{1}
, we
thereby can not employ this flattening argument. More precisely,
unlike
[
6
]
in which the Neumann boundary condition on
the flat domain is assigned, we here in the present paper are
dealing with the oblique boundary condition on the
\displaystyle C^{1}
domain in
order to prove a required boundary Hölder estimate, independent
of
\displaystyle q
, of a solution of the problem (
1.2
). Therefore we
consider a concept of the so called “almost Neumann” boundary and
“almost
\displaystyle C^{1}
-flat” domain. “Almost Neumann” means that

\displaystyle\sup\left|\frac{\beta^{\prime}}{\beta_{n}}\right|
is small enough so that

\displaystyle\beta\approx e_{n}
, and, “almost
\displaystyle C^{1}
-flat” domain means that the

\displaystyle C^{1}
-norm of the local graph of the boundary of the domain is small
enough so that
\displaystyle\Omega\cap B_{1}\approx B^{+}_{1}
. This kind of a
perturbation is enough to prove a similar result in
[
24
]
by the sliding cusp method. Moreover the proof of the
\displaystyle L^{\epsilon}
estimate
in
[
6
]
was based on the Calder’on-Zygmund cube
decomposition which can be used for only on the flat domain, whence
we instead use the so called growing ink-spot lemma for a corkscrew
domain (Lemma
2.1
). An advantage of this measure covering
lemma is that we only need to consider a ball contained in the
domain so that an interior estimate can be adapted in the ball.
Therefore, we can use the interior measure estimate inside the ball
without considering the boundary, and, by choosing an appropriate
barrier function we can prove a doubling type lemma with the oblique
boundary condition.

Unlike the method in
[
24
]
by Imbert and Silvestre, it seems
difficult to use the method in
[
34
]
by Savin for the
oblique boundary condition on a
\displaystyle C^{1}
domain. The equation, which is
uniformly elliptic only where the gradient is large, is not scaling
invariant under the scaled function,
\displaystyle v(x)=u(rx)/M
with
\displaystyle r<1
and

\displaystyle M>1
while the equation, which is uniformly elliptic only where the
gradient is small, is scaling invariant. In order to deal with this
difficulty, we observe that the equation (
1.2
) is uniformly
elliptic where
\displaystyle|Du-q|>1
and that for the scaling function
\displaystyle v(x)=u(rx)/M
, it is uniformly elliptic
except where
\displaystyle\left|Dv-\frac{r}{M}q\right|<\frac{r}{M}
.
Therefore, the set where
\displaystyle\left|Dv-\frac{r}{M}q\right|<\frac{r}{M}
is disjoint with either the set
\displaystyle|Dv|<A
or the set

\displaystyle|Dv|>A+2
wherever
\displaystyle\frac{r}{M}q
is, and, it is uniformly elliptic
where
\displaystyle|Dv|<A
or it is uniformly elliptic where
\displaystyle|Dv|>A+2
.
Consequently, we shall employ the sliding paraboloid method when it
is uniformly elliptic where
\displaystyle|Dv|<A
while the sliding cusp method
can be used when it is uniformly elliptic where
\displaystyle|Dv|>A+2
. In
summary, if we find a suitable estimate both for a solution
of the uniformly elliptic equation that only holds where
\displaystyle|Du|<A
,
and for a solution of the uniformly elliptic equation that only
holds where
\displaystyle|Du|>A+2
, then the scaled function also satisfies the
same estimate whatever
\displaystyle q
is. This enables us to prove a Hölder
estimate for the equation which is uniformly elliptic where
\displaystyle|Du-q|>1
independent of
\displaystyle q
.

The paper is organized as follows. In section
2
we
introduce basic notations, the main results and preliminaries which
will be used later for the proof of the main theorem. In section

3
we establish a boundary Hölder estimate with the
oblique boundary condition on an “almost
\displaystyle C^{1}
-flat” domain on
which the associated problem is uniformly elliptic where
\displaystyle|Du-q|>1
,
independent of
\displaystyle q
. Section
4
is devoted to proving an
improvement of flatness lemma by the compactness method. In section

5
we finally prove the main theorem, Theorem
2.1
.

2.
Preliminaries and main results

For
\displaystyle r>0
we write
\displaystyle B_{r}=\{|x|<r\}
the ball with center 0 and
radius
\displaystyle r
,
\displaystyle B^{+}_{r}=B_{r}\cap\{x_{n}>0\}
the half-ball, and
\displaystyle T_{r}=B_{r}\cap\{x_{n}=0\}
the flat boundary of
\displaystyle B^{+}_{r}
. For
\displaystyle x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
we write
\displaystyle B_{r}(x_{0})=B_{r}+x_{0}
the ball with center

\displaystyle x_{0}
. For a domain
\displaystyle\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\displaystyle x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
we write
\displaystyle\Omega_{r}=\Omega\cap B_{r}
,
\displaystyle\partial\Omega_{r}=\partial\Omega\cap B_{r}
and
\displaystyle\Omega_{r}(x_{0})=\Omega\cap B_{r}(x_{0})
,
\displaystyle\partial\Omega_{r}(x_{0})=\partial\Omega\cap B_{r}(x_{0})
.
We denote by
\displaystyle S(n)
the space of symmetric
\displaystyle n\times n
real matrices, and
\displaystyle I
the identity matrix.

We now list our basic structural assumptions. We always assume that

\displaystyle\beta\in C^{\alpha}(\partial\Omega)
is oblique, i.e., there
exists a positive constant
\displaystyle\delta_{0}>0
such that

(2.1)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\beta\cdot\mathbf{n}\geq\delta_{0}\text{ and }\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\partial\Omega)}\leq 1,

where
\displaystyle\mathbf{n}
is the inner normal of
\displaystyle\partial\Omega
. We
always assume that
\displaystyle F(0)=0
and
\displaystyle F
is uniformly elliptic, i.e.,
there exist constants
\displaystyle 0<\lambda\leq\Lambda
such that

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lambda\left\lVert N\right\rVert\leq F(M+N)-F(M)\leq\Lambda\left\lVert N\right\rVert

for any
\displaystyle M,N\in S(n)
with
\displaystyle N\geq 0
, where
\displaystyle\left\lVert N\right\rVert
is the
spectral radius of
\displaystyle N
.

Let
\displaystyle\alpha_{0}=\alpha_{0}(n,\lambda,\Lambda,\delta_{0})\in(0,1]
be
the optimal exponent of regularity theory for a homogeneous equation
with constant oblique boundary condition, i.e., any viscosity
solution
\displaystyle h
of

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{cases}F(D^{2}h)=0\ &\text{ in }B^{+}_{1}\\
\beta_{0}\cdot Dh=0&\text{ on }T_{1},\end{cases}

where
\displaystyle\beta_{0}
is a constant oblique vector satisfying
(
2.1
), is locally of the class
\displaystyle C^{1,\alpha_{0}}(B^{+}_{1})
with
the estimate

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left\lVert h\right\rVert_{C^{1,\alpha_{0}}(B^{+}_{1/2})}\leq C_{e}\left\lVert h\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(B^{+}_{1})}.

for some constant
\displaystyle C_{e}=C_{e}(n,\lambda,\Lambda,\delta_{0})>1
.

D. Li and K. Zhang
[
30
]
proved that there exists a universal
constant
\displaystyle 0<\alpha_{0}<1
for any uniformly elliptic
\displaystyle F
and that

\displaystyle\alpha_{0}=1
for any convex
\displaystyle F
.

We now state our main theorem.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\displaystyle\Omega\in C^{1}
,
\displaystyle 0\in\partial\Omega_{1}
,

\displaystyle\gamma\geq 0
and
\displaystyle u
be a viscosity solution of

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{cases}|Du|^{\gamma}F(D^{2}u)=f\ &\text{ in }\Omega_{1}\\
\beta\cdot Du=g&\text{ on }\partial\Omega_{1},\end{cases}

where
\displaystyle\alpha\in\left(0,\alpha_{0}\right)\cap\left(0,\frac{1}{1+\gamma}\right]
,
\displaystyle f\in C(\overline{\Omega})
, and
\displaystyle g,\beta\in C^{\alpha}(\partial\Omega)
with
\displaystyle\beta
satisfying (
2.1
). Then
\displaystyle u\in C^{1,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega_{1/2}})
and

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{C^{1,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega_{1/2}})}\leq C\left(\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega_{1}})}+\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega_{1}})}^{\frac{1}{1+\gamma}}+\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\partial\Omega_{1})}\right),

where
\displaystyle C
depends only on
\displaystyle n,\lambda,\Lambda,\alpha,\gamma,\delta_{0},[\beta]_{C^{\alpha}(\partial\Omega_{1})}
and
\displaystyle C^{1}
modulus
of
\displaystyle\partial\Omega_{1}
.

From Theorem
2.1
, we have the following corollary.

Corollary 2.1
.

Let
\displaystyle\Omega\in C^{1}
,
\displaystyle 0\in\partial\Omega_{1}
,
\displaystyle\gamma\geq 0
and

\displaystyle u
be a viscosity solution of

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{cases}|Du|^{\gamma}F(D^{2}u)=f\ &\text{ in }\Omega_{1}\\
\beta\cdot Du+hu=g&\text{ on }\partial\Omega_{1},\end{cases}

where
\displaystyle\alpha\in\left(0,\alpha_{0}\right)\cap\left(0,\frac{1}{1+\gamma}\right]
,
\displaystyle f\in C(\overline{\Omega})
, and
\displaystyle g,\beta,h\in C^{\alpha}(\partial\Omega)
with
\displaystyle\beta
satisfying (

). Then
\displaystyle u\in C^{1,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega_{1/2}})
and

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{C^{1,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega_{1/2}})}\leq C\left(\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega_{1}})}+\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega_{1}})}^{\frac{1}{1+\gamma}}+\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\partial\Omega_{1})}\right),

where
\displaystyle C
depends only on
\displaystyle n,\lambda,\Lambda,\alpha,\gamma,\delta_{0},[\beta]_{C^{\alpha}(\partial\Omega_{1})}
,

We now recall the definition of viscosity solution. (See

[
20
,
18
]
)

Definition 2.1
.

We say that
\displaystyle u
is a viscosity subsolution (resp. supersolution) of (
1.1
) if for any
\displaystyle x_{0}\in\Omega\cup\partial\Omega
and test function
\displaystyle\phi\in C^{2}(\Omega\cup\partial\Omega)
such that
\displaystyle u-\phi
has a local minimum (resp. maximum) at
\displaystyle x_{0}
, then

\displaystyle\displaystyle|D\phi(x_{0})|^{\gamma}F(D^{2}\phi(x_{0}))\geq(\text{resp.}\leq)\ f(x_{0})\quad\text{ if }x_{0}\in\Omega,

and

\displaystyle\displaystyle D\phi(x_{0})\cdot\beta(x_{0})\geq(\text{resp.}\leq)\ g(x_{0})\quad\text{ if }x_{0}\in\partial\Omega.

If
\displaystyle u
is both subsolution and supersolution, then we call
\displaystyle u
a viscosity solution.

Since
\displaystyle\Omega\in C^{1}
, we see that
\displaystyle\Omega
satisfies the corkscrew
condition. The following is the definition of the corkscrew
condition
[
26
]
.

Definition 2.2
.

For
\displaystyle\rho\in(0,1)
, we say that
\displaystyle\Omega
satisfies
\displaystyle\rho
-corkscrew condition if for any
\displaystyle x\in\Omega
and
\displaystyle 0<r<\diam(\Omega)/3
, there exists
\displaystyle y\in\Omega
satisfying
\displaystyle B_{\rho r}(y)\subset B_{r}(x)\cap\Omega
.

Remark 2.1
.

The standard definition of the (interior) corkscrew condition as in

[
26
]
is defined only for the boundary point
\displaystyle x\in\partial\Omega
unlike the above definition defined for
\displaystyle x\in\Omega
, but it is easy to show that they are equivalent.
In fact, assuming
\displaystyle\Omega
has the corkscrew condition for the boundary, for any
\displaystyle x\in\Omega
and
\displaystyle 0<r<\diam(\Omega)/3
, choose
\displaystyle x_{0}\in\partial\Omega
such that
\displaystyle\dist(x,\partial\Omega)=\dist(x,x_{0})
and if
\displaystyle r/2<\dist(x,x_{0})
, then
\displaystyle B_{r/2}(x)\subset B_{r}(x)\cap\Omega
.
If
\displaystyle r/2\geq\dist(x,x_{0})
, then by the definition of the corkscrew condition for the boundary, there exists a ball
\displaystyle B_{\rho r/2}(y)\subset B_{r/2}(x_{0})\cap\Omega\subset B_{r}(x)\cap\Omega
, which implies
\displaystyle\Omega
satisfies the above definition of the corkscrew condition.

For the corkscrew domain we can obtain a simple measure covering
lemma as in
[
34
]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\displaystyle\Omega
satisfy
\displaystyle\rho
-corkscrew condition and
assume that there exist two open sets
\displaystyle E,F\subset\Omega
and some
constant
\displaystyle\epsilon\in(0,1)
such that:

(1)

\displaystyle E\subset F\subset\Omega
and
\displaystyle F\neq\Omega
.

(2)

For any ball
\displaystyle B\subset\Omega
satisfies
\displaystyle|B\cap E|>(1-\epsilon)|B|
, then
\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}B\cap\Omega\subset F
where
\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}=\frac{4}{\rho}
.

Then
\displaystyle|E|\leq(1-3^{-n}\rho^{n}\epsilon)|F|
.

Proof.

We write
\displaystyle E^{c}=\Omega\setminus E
and
\displaystyle F^{c}=\Omega\setminus F\neq\emptyset
. Given
\displaystyle x\in F
, let
\displaystyle r=\dist(x,F^{c})<\diam(\Omega)
.
We claim that

\displaystyle\displaystyle|B_{r/3}(x)\cap\Omega\cap E^{c}|\geq 3^{-n}\rho^{n}\epsilon|B_{r}(x)|.

Applying
\displaystyle\rho
-corkscrew condition to
\displaystyle B_{r/3}(x)
, we know that there exists
\displaystyle y\in\Omega
such that
\displaystyle B_{\rho r/3}(y)\subset\Omega\cap B_{r/3}(x)
.
Thus,
\displaystyle\dist(y,F^{c})\leq\dist(y,x)+\dist(x,F^{c})\leq\frac{4-\rho}{3}r<\frac{4}{3}r
, which implies
\displaystyle\frac{4}{\rho}B_{\rho r/3}(y)\cap\Omega\not\subset F
.
Therefore, we have
\displaystyle|B_{\rho r/3}(y)\cap E|\leq(1-\epsilon)|B_{\rho r/3}|
. Thus,

\displaystyle\displaystyle|B_{r/3}(x)\cap\Omega\cap E^{c}|

\displaystyle\displaystyle\geq|B_{\rho r/3}(y)\cap E^{c}|

\displaystyle\displaystyle=|B_{\rho r/3}(y)|-|B_{\rho r/3}(y)\cap E|

\displaystyle\displaystyle\geq\epsilon|B_{\rho r/3}|=3^{-n}\rho^{n}\epsilon|B_{r}(x)|.

Now for every
\displaystyle x\in F
let
\displaystyle r=\dist(x,F^{c})
. Then
\displaystyle\{B_{r}(x)\}
is
a covering of
\displaystyle F
. By the Vitali covering lemma, there exists a
subcover
\displaystyle\{B_{r_{i}}(x_{i})\}
of
\displaystyle F
such that
\displaystyle\{B_{r_{i}/3}(x_{i})\}

are disjoint. Considering
\displaystyle B_{r_{i}/3}(x_{i})\cap\Omega\subset F
, we
have

\displaystyle\displaystyle|F|

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i}|B_{r_{i}}(x_{i})|

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq 3^{n}\rho^{-n}\epsilon^{-1}\sum_{i}|B_{r_{i}/3}(x_{i})\cap\Omega\cap E^{c}|

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq 3^{n}\rho^{-n}\epsilon^{-1}|F\cap E^{c}|.

Therefore, we conclude that

\displaystyle\displaystyle|E|

\displaystyle\displaystyle=|F|-|F\cap E^{c}|

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq(1-3^{-n}\rho^{n}\epsilon)|F|.

∎

In the next section we will consider an “almost
\displaystyle C^{1}
-flat” domain

\displaystyle\Omega_{1}
. We always assume
\displaystyle 0\in\partial\Omega_{1}
. Then the
boundary
\displaystyle\partial\Omega_{1}
can be represented by a graph of
function
\displaystyle\varphi=\varphi_{\Omega}:T_{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

and
\displaystyle\varphi\in C^{1}
. This means that we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\{(x^{\prime},x_{n})\in B_{1}|x_{n}=\varphi(x^{\prime})\}=\partial\Omega_{1},\ \{(x^{\prime},x_{n})\in B_{1}|x_{n}>\varphi(x^{\prime})\}\subset\Omega_{1}.

We also write
\displaystyle\beta\in C^{\alpha}(T_{1})
by
\displaystyle\beta(x^{\prime})=\beta(x^{\prime},\varphi(x^{\prime}))
.

Note that the inner normal vector of
\displaystyle\partial\Omega
is
\displaystyle\mathbf{n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|D\varphi|^{2}+1}}(D\varphi,1)
.
Thus if
\displaystyle[\varphi]_{C^{1}}\leq\delta_{0}/2
, we have
\displaystyle\beta_{n}\geq\delta_{0}/2
using
\displaystyle\beta\cdot\mathbf{n}\geq\delta_{0}
.
Instead of (
2.1
), we always assume that
\displaystyle\beta=(\beta^{\prime},\beta_{n})
and
\displaystyle\varphi
satisfy

(2.2)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\varphi(0)=0,\quad[\varphi]_{C^{1}}\leq\delta_{0}/2,\quad\beta_{n}\geq\delta_{0}/2,\quad\left\lVert\beta\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leq 1.

Then we can define
\displaystyle\tau\in C(T_{1})
as
\displaystyle\tau(x):=\frac{\beta^{\prime}(x)}{\beta_{n}(x)}
, which represents how close
\displaystyle\beta
is
to
\displaystyle e_{n}
. Note that
\displaystyle\sup|\tau|
is bounded by
\displaystyle\sup|\tau|\leq\sqrt{\frac{4}{\delta_{0}^{2}}-1}
.

We next show that this “almost
\displaystyle C^{1}
-flat” boundary of
\displaystyle\Omega_{1}
with
a small
\displaystyle C^{1}
-norm satisfies the corkscrew condition.

Proposition 2.1
.

Suppose
\displaystyle 0\in\partial\Omega_{1}
and
\displaystyle[\varphi]_{C^{1}}\leq\mu<1/3
. Then
\displaystyle\Omega_{1}
satisfies
\displaystyle(1-3\mu)/4
-corkscrew
condition.

Proof.

For any
\displaystyle x_{0}\in\Omega_{1}
and
\displaystyle r<2/3
, let us define

\displaystyle\displaystyle x_{1}=\begin{cases}x_{0}-\frac{r}{2}\frac{x_{0}}{|x_{0}|}\quad\text{if}\ |x_{0}|>\frac{r}{2}\\
0\quad\text{if}\ |x_{0}|\leq\frac{r}{2}.\end{cases}

Then we have
\displaystyle B_{r/2}(x_{1})\subset B_{r}(x_{0})\cap B_{1}
.
We divide the proof into the 3 cases.

Case 1 :
\displaystyle x_{1}=0
.
Then since
\displaystyle|\varphi(x^{\prime})|\leq\frac{r}{2}\mu
in
\displaystyle x^{\prime}\in T_{r/2}(0)
, for
\displaystyle x_{2}=\left(\frac{r}{2}\mu+\frac{r}{8}(1-\mu)\right)e_{n}
, we have
\displaystyle B_{r(1-\mu)/4}(x_{2})\subset B_{r/2}(x_{1})\cap\Omega_{1}\subset B_{r}(x_{0})\cap\Omega_{1}
.

Case 2 :
\displaystyle x_{1}\neq 0
and
\displaystyle e_{n}\cdot\frac{x_{1}}{|x_{1}|}\geq\cos\left(\arctan{\frac{1}{\mu}}\right)
. Note that the cone

\displaystyle\left\{x\in B_{1}:e_{n}\cdot\frac{x}{|x|}\geq\cos\left(\arctan{\frac{1}{\mu}}\right)\right\}
is in
\displaystyle\Omega_{1}
.
For
\displaystyle x_{2}=x_{1}+\left(\frac{r}{2}\mu+\frac{r}{8}(1-\mu)\right)e_{n}
, we have
\displaystyle B_{r(1-\mu)/4}(x_{2})\subset B_{r/2}(x_{1})\cap\Omega_{1}\subset B_{r}(x_{0})\cap\Omega_{1}
.

Case 3 :
\displaystyle x_{1}\neq 0
and
\displaystyle e_{n}\cdot\frac{x_{1}}{|x_{1}|}<\cos\left(\arctan{\frac{1}{\mu}}\right)
. Since
\displaystyle|x^{\prime}_{0}-x^{\prime}_{1}|<r/2
,
we have
\displaystyle|(x_{0})_{n}-(x_{1})_{n}|<\mu r/2
and
\displaystyle|\varphi(x^{\prime}_{0})-\varphi(x^{\prime})|<\mu r
for any
\displaystyle x^{\prime}\in T_{r/2}(x^{\prime}_{1})
. Moreover, since

\displaystyle(x_{0})_{n}\geq\varphi(x^{\prime}_{0})
, we have
\displaystyle\varphi(x^{\prime})-(x_{1})_{n}\leq\frac{3}{2}r\mu
. Therefore, for
\displaystyle x_{2}=x_{1}+\left(\frac{3}{2}r\mu+\frac{r}{8}(1-3\mu)\right)e_{n}
, we get
\displaystyle B_{r(1-3\mu)/4}(x_{2})\subset B_{r/2}(x_{1})\cap\Omega_{1}\subset B_{r}(x_{0})\cap\Omega_{1}
.

Thus we conclude that
\displaystyle\Omega_{1}
satisfies
\displaystyle(1-3\mu)/4
-corkscrew
condition.
∎

3.
Hölder estimates up to the boundary for uniformly equations that hold only where
\displaystyle|Du-q|>1

We need a uniform Hölder estimate for a solution of

\displaystyle|Du-q|^{\gamma}F(D^{2}u)=f
. Note that this equation is uniform
elliptic where
\displaystyle|Du-q|>1
, and we need to find an uniform Hölder
estimate independent of
\displaystyle q
.

For a given
\displaystyle 0<\lambda<\Lambda
, let

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{+}_{\Lambda,\lambda}(D^{2}u,Du)=\Lambda\tr D^{2}u^{+}-\lambda\tr D^{2}u^{-}+\Lambda|Du|,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{-}_{\Lambda,\lambda}(D^{2}u,Du)=\lambda\tr D^{2}u^{+}-\Lambda\tr D^{2}u^{-}-\Lambda|Du|.

We want to prove the following theorem:

Theorem 3.1
.

Assume
\displaystyle\Omega\in C^{1}
. Then there exists a small

\displaystyle\mu=\mu(\delta_{0})\leq\delta_{0}/2
such that if

\displaystyle[\varphi_{\Omega}]_{C^{1}}\leq\mu
, for any
\displaystyle u\in C(\overline{\Omega\cap B_{1}})
satisfying

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{cases}\mathcal{P}^{-}(D^{2}u,Du)\leq C_{0}&\text{ in }\{|Du-q|>\theta\}\cap\Omega_{1},\\
\mathcal{P}^{+}(D^{2}u,Du)\geq-C_{0}&\text{ in }\{|Du-q|>\theta\}\cap\Omega_{1},\\
\beta\cdot Du=g&\text{ on }\partial\Omega_{1},\\
\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega_{1}})}\leq 1,\\
\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(T_{1})}\leq C_{0},\end{cases}

for some
\displaystyle q\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\displaystyle 0<\theta\leq 1
, then we have

\displaystyle u\in C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega_{1/2}})
for some

\displaystyle\alpha(n,\lambda,\Lambda,\delta_{0})>0
. We also have the estimates
independent of
\displaystyle q
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega_{1/2}})}\leq C(n,\lambda,\Lambda,\delta_{0},C_{0}).

Remark 3.1
.

Note that by scaling, we can always make
\displaystyle\sup|\tau|
arbitrary small so that
\displaystyle\beta
is “almost Neumann” with
\displaystyle\Lambda/\lambda
changing in a universal way.
Indeed, for any
\displaystyle R>1
and
\displaystyle u\in C(\overline{\Omega_{1}})
satisfying the assumption of Theorem
3.1
, let

\displaystyle\displaystyle\tilde{u}(x^{\prime},x_{n})=u\left(x^{\prime},\frac{1}{R}x_{n}\right)

then
\displaystyle\tilde{u}
satisfies the following:

\displaystyle\displaystyle D^{2}u=A\cdot D^{2}\tilde{u}\cdot A,\quad Du=A\cdot D\tilde{u}.

where
\displaystyle A\in S(n)
is a diagonal matrix
\displaystyle A=\text{diag}(1,...,1,R)
. Note that
\displaystyle|Du|\leq R|D\tilde{u}|
and

\displaystyle\{|D\tilde{u}-\tilde{q}|>\theta\}
is contained in
\displaystyle\{|Du-q|>\theta\}
where
\displaystyle\tilde{q}=\left(q^{\prime},\frac{1}{R}q_{n}\right)
.
Moreover, since
\displaystyle I\leq A\leq RI
, for any symmetric matrices

\displaystyle\lambda I\leq B\leq\Lambda I
, we get
\displaystyle\lambda I\leq ABA\leq R^{2}\Lambda I
, which implies

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{-}_{\lambda,\Lambda}(D^{2}{u},D{u})\geq\mathcal{P}^{-}_{\lambda,R^{2}\Lambda}(D^{2}\tilde{u},D\tilde{u})
.
Therefore,
\displaystyle\tilde{u}\in C(\overline{\tilde{\Omega}_{1}})
satisfies
the following equations:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{cases}\mathcal{P}^{-}_{\lambda,R^{2}\Lambda}(D^{2}\tilde{u},D\tilde{u})\leq C_{0}&\text{ in }\{|D\tilde{u}-\tilde{q}|>\theta\}\cap\tilde{\Omega}_{1},\\
\mathcal{P}^{+}_{\lambda,R^{2}\Lambda}(D^{2}\tilde{u},D\tilde{u})\geq-C_{0}&\text{ in }\{|D\tilde{u}-\tilde{q}|>\theta\}\cap\tilde{\Omega}_{1},\\
\tilde{\beta}\cdot D\tilde{u}=g/R&\text{ in }\partial\tilde{\Omega}_{1},\\
\end{cases}

where
\displaystyle\tilde{\beta}=\left(\frac{1}{R}\beta^{\prime},\beta_{n}\right)
,
\displaystyle\tilde{\Omega}=\{(x^{\prime},Rx_{n}):(x^{\prime},x_{n})\in\Omega\}
and
\displaystyle\varphi_{\tilde{\Omega}}=R\varphi_{\Omega}
.

Then we have
\displaystyle|\tilde{\beta}|\leq 1
, and
\displaystyle\tilde{\beta}_{n}\geq\delta_{0}/2
. Moreover, since
\displaystyle\tilde{\tau}=\frac{\beta^{\prime}}{R\beta_{n}}
, we have
\displaystyle\sup|\tilde{\tau}|\leq\frac{\sup|\tau|}{R}
and we can make
\displaystyle\sup|\tilde{\tau}|
smaller
than some universal constant by choosing a large
\displaystyle R=R(\delta_{0})
.
Since
\displaystyle[\varphi_{\tilde{\Omega}}]_{C^{1}}\leq R[\varphi_{\Omega}]_{C^{1}}\leq R\mu
, we may choose a small
\displaystyle\mu=\mu(\delta_{0})
so that
\displaystyle R\mu<\delta_{0}/2
. Then
\displaystyle\varphi_{\tilde{\Omega}}
and
\displaystyle\tilde{\beta}

also satisfy the assumption
(
2.2
)
. Note that

\displaystyle[u]_{C^{\alpha}(\Omega_{\frac{1}{2R}})}\leq R^{\alpha}[\tilde{u}]_{C^{\alpha}(\tilde{\Omega}_{1/2})}
. Therefore,
if we prove Theorem
3.1
for small
\displaystyle\sup|\tau|
, then we
get
\displaystyle\tilde{u}\in C^{\alpha}(\tilde{\Omega}_{1/2})
, and by using
standard covering and interior estimates, we can also prove
\displaystyle u\in C^{\alpha}(\Omega_{1/2})
.

Remark 3.2
.

For some
\displaystyle A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let
\displaystyle u
satisfy

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{-}(D^{2}u,Du)\leq 1\ \text{ in }\{Du\in A\}\cap B_{1},

then for the scaled function
\displaystyle v(x)=\frac{u(rx)}{M}
for
\displaystyle M>1
and

\displaystyle r<1
, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{-}(D^{2}v,Dv)\leq\frac{r^{2}}{M}\ \text{ in }\left\{Dv\in\frac{r}{M}A\right\}\cap B_{1/r}.

If
\displaystyle\frac{r}{M}A\supset A
, then we have
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{-}(D^{2}v,Dv)\leq 1
in
\displaystyle\{Dv\in A\}\cap B_{1}
, which is the same assumption on

\displaystyle u
. Thus, if we prove some result with the above assumption on
\displaystyle u
,
then we can prove the same result on the scaled function
\displaystyle v
and
find more information about
\displaystyle u
.

For example, if
\displaystyle A=\{|x|\geq\theta\}
, which means the
inequality holds where the gradient is large, then
\displaystyle\frac{r}{M}A=\left\{|x|\geq\frac{r}{M}\theta\right\}\supset A
and we can use the
scaled function
\displaystyle v
. However, if
\displaystyle A=\{|x|\leq\theta\}
, which
means the inequality holds where the gradient is small, then

\displaystyle\frac{r}{M}A=\left\{|x|\leq\frac{r}{M}\theta\right\}\not\supset A
.

In particular, if
\displaystyle A=\{|x-q|\geq\theta\}
for some
\displaystyle q\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, which means the inequality holds where
\displaystyle\{|Du-q|\geq\theta\}
, then
\displaystyle\frac{r}{M}A=\left\{\left|x-\frac{r}{M}q\right|\geq\frac{r}{M}\theta\right\}\not\supset A
, which implies the scaled
function does not satisfy the same assumption of
\displaystyle u
. But we observe
that the center
\displaystyle q
moved to
\displaystyle\frac{r}{M}q
and the radius
\displaystyle\theta

became
\displaystyle\frac{r}{M}\theta
, smaller than
\displaystyle\theta
. From this
observation, we prove that
\displaystyle v
satisfies the assumption below.

Proposition 3.1
.

For any
\displaystyle q\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\displaystyle 0<\theta\leq 1
and
\displaystyle u\in C(B_{1})
satisfying

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{-}(D^{2}u,Du)\leq 1\ \text{ in }\{|Du-q|>\theta\}\cap B_{1},

the scaled function
\displaystyle v(x)=u(rx)/M
where
\displaystyle 0<r\leq 1
and
\displaystyle M\geq 1
satisfies either

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{-}(D^{2}v,Dv)\leq 1\ \text{ in }\{|Dv|\leq A_{0}\}\cap B_{1/r},\ \text{ or }\ \mathcal{P}^{-}(D^{2}v,Dv)\leq 1\ \text{ in }\{|Dv|\geq A_{0}+2\}\cap B_{1/r},

for any
\displaystyle A_{0}>0
.

Proof.

Note that
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{-}(D^{2}v,Dv)\leq\frac{r^{2}}{M}\leq 1\ \text{ in }\left\{\left|Dv-\frac{r}{M}q\right|\geq\frac{r}{M}\theta\right\}\cap B_{1/r}
.

If
\displaystyle\left|\frac{r}{M}q\right|\geq A_{0}+1
, then
\displaystyle\{|x|\leq A_{0}\}\subset\left\{\left|x-\frac{r}{M}q\right|\geq\frac{r}{M}\theta\right\}
.
This is because if
\displaystyle|x|\leq A_{0}
, then
\displaystyle\left|x-\frac{r}{M}q\right|\geq\left|\frac{r}{M}q\right|-|x|\geq 1\geq\frac{r}{M}\theta
.
Therefore, we have
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{-}(D^{2}v,Dv)\leq 1\ \text{ in }\{|Dv|\leq A_{0}\}\cap B_{1/r}
.

If
\displaystyle\left|\frac{r}{M}q\right|\leq A_{0}+1
, then also
\displaystyle\{|x|\geq A_{0}+2\}\subset\left\{\left|x-\frac{r}{M}q\right|\geq\frac{r}{M}\right\}
.
This is because if
\displaystyle|x|\geq A_{0}+2
, then
\displaystyle\left|x-\frac{r}{M}q\right|\geq|x|-\left|\frac{r}{M}q\right|\geq 1\geq\frac{r}{M}\theta
.
Therefore, we have
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{-}(D^{2}v,Dv)\leq 1\ \text{ in }\{|Dv|\geq A_{0}+2\}\cap B_{1/r}
.
∎

Therefore, we instead find some result for both of inequalities, one holds where
\displaystyle\{|Du|\leq A_{0}\}
and the other holds where
\displaystyle\{|Du|\geq A_{0}+2\}
. Then since the scaled function
\displaystyle v
satisfies either of the inequalities, we can prove the same result on
\displaystyle v
whatever
\displaystyle q
is.

Now, we prove the interior measure estimate lemma following the idea of
[
24
]
, using a paraboloid instead of a cusp since we first consider the equation which is uniformly elliptic where the gradient is small.

Lemma 3.1
.

There exist small
\displaystyle\epsilon_{0}>0
and large
\displaystyle K>0
,
\displaystyle A_{0}>0
such that for any
\displaystyle u\in C(\overline{B_{1}})
satisfying

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{cases}u\geq 0\text{ in }B_{1},\\
\mathcal{P}^{-}(D^{2}u,Du)\leq 1\text{ in }\{|Du|\leq A_{0}\}\cap B_{1},\\
|\{u>K\}\cap B_{1}|\geq(1-\epsilon_{0})|B_{1}|,\end{cases}

we have
\displaystyle u>1
in
\displaystyle B_{1/4}
.

Proof.

First, assume that
\displaystyle u\in C^{2}(B_{1})\cap C(\overline{B_{1}})
. We prove this lemma by
contradiction. We assume the contrary that for all
\displaystyle\epsilon_{0}
,
\displaystyle K

and
\displaystyle A_{0}
, we can find
\displaystyle u
such that the above conditions hold but

\displaystyle u(x_{0})\leq 1
for some
\displaystyle x_{0}\in B_{1/4}
. Consider
\displaystyle G=\{u>K\}\cap B_{1/4}
. Then
\displaystyle|G|\geq|B_{1/4}|-\epsilon_{0}|B_{1}|=(c-\epsilon_{0})|B_{1}|>0
for small
\displaystyle\epsilon_{0}<c=c(n)
.

We slide a paraboloid
\displaystyle\phi(z)=-10|z-x|^{2}
with vertex
\displaystyle x\in G
from below, until it touches the graph of
\displaystyle u
for the first time at a point
\displaystyle y\in\overline{B_{1}}
. Then we have

(3.1)

\displaystyle\displaystyle u(y)+10|y-x|^{2}=\inf_{z\in\overline{B_{1}}}\{u(z)+10|z-x|^{2}\}.

We claim that
\displaystyle y\in B_{1}
.
If
\displaystyle y\in\partial B_{1}
, then since
\displaystyle u\geq 0
and
\displaystyle x\in B_{1/4}
, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle u(y)+10|y-x|^{2}>10\left|1-\frac{1}{4}\right|^{2}.

However, since
\displaystyle x_{0}\in B_{1/4}
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle u(x_{0})+10|x_{0}-x|^{2}\leq 1+10\left|\frac{1}{2}\right|^{2}<10\left|1-\frac{1}{4}\right|^{2}.

which contradicts with (
3.1
).

Let
\displaystyle K=1+10\left|\frac{1}{2}\right|^{2}
, then
\displaystyle u(y)<K
. Note that we
have

\displaystyle\displaystyle Du(y)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=D\phi(y)=20(x-y),

\displaystyle\displaystyle D^{2}u(y)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\geq D^{2}\phi(y)=-20I.

Choosing
\displaystyle A_{0}>40\geq\sup_{B_{1}}|D\phi(y)|
, we have
\displaystyle|Du|\leq A_{0}

at a touching point
\displaystyle y
. Therefore, we get
\displaystyle|D^{2}u(y)|\leq C(\lambda,\Lambda)
since
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{-}(D^{2}u,Du)\leq 1
. Observe
that
\displaystyle x=y+\frac{1}{20}Du(y)
, so let
\displaystyle U\subset B_{1}
be the set of
touching points
\displaystyle y
and define a map
\displaystyle m:U\rightarrow G
by
\displaystyle m(y):=x
. Then we have
\displaystyle U\subset\{u<K\}\cap B_{1}
,
\displaystyle m(U)=G
and

\displaystyle|Dm(y)|=\left|I+\frac{1}{20}D^{2}u(y)\right|\leq C
. Therefore,
using the area formula for
\displaystyle m:U\rightarrow G
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle(c-\epsilon_{0})|B_{1}|\leq|G|=\int_{U}|\det Dm(y)|dy\leq C|U|\leq C\epsilon_{0}|B_{1}|,

which is a contradiction if we choose
\displaystyle\epsilon_{0}
small enough.

If
\displaystyle u
is semiconcave, we can repeat the argument as in the proof of Proposition 3.5 in
[
24
]
.
∎

Note that if
\displaystyle[\varphi_{\Omega}]_{C^{1}}\leq\mu\leq 1/6
,
\displaystyle\Omega_{1}
satisfies
\displaystyle\rho
-corkscrew condition with
\displaystyle\rho=1/8
by Proposition

.
Since the corkscrew condition is scaling invariant, we can say that
\displaystyle\Omega_{\tilde{\rho}+1}=\Omega_{33}

\displaystyle 1/8
-corkscrew condition when

Now we consider the barrier function
\displaystyle b(x)=|x-\sigma e_{n}|^{-p}
for some
\displaystyle\sigma\geq 1
.
Then for

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{-}(D^{2}b,Db)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\lambda p(p+1)|x-\sigma e_{n}|^{-p-2}-\Lambda(n-1)p|x-\sigma e_{n}|^{-p-2}-\Lambda p|x-\sigma e_{n}|^{-p-1}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\geq p|x-\sigma e_{n}|^{-p-2}\geq p(\tilde{\rho}+1)^{-p-2},

if

is large enough.
Moreover, for
\displaystyle x\in\partial\Omega_{\tilde{\rho}+1}(\sigma e_{n})
, we have
\displaystyle|x_{n}|\leq(\tilde{\rho}+1)[\varphi_{\Omega}]_{C^{1}}\leq(\tilde{\rho}+1)\mu
and thus

\displaystyle\displaystyle\beta\cdot Db(x)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=-p|x-\sigma e_{n}|^{-p-2}(x-\sigma e_{n})\cdot\beta

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\beta_{n}p|x-\sigma e_{n}|^{-p-2}(\sigma-x_{n}-\tau\cdot x^{\prime})

\displaystyle\displaystyle\geq\frac{\delta_{0}}{2}p|x-\sigma e_{n}|^{-p-2}(1-(\tilde{\rho}+1)\mu-(\tilde{\rho}+1)\tau_{0})

\displaystyle\displaystyle>\delta_{0}p(\tilde{\rho}+1)^{-p-3}\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)=:\eta_{0}>0,

if

Using the above barrier function, we prove the doubling type lemma for the “almost Neumann” condition on “almost
\displaystyle C^{1}

Lemma 3.2
.

There exist small
\displaystyle\tau_{0}>0
,
\displaystyle\mu>0
,
\displaystyle\eta>0
, and large
\displaystyle K>1
,
\displaystyle A_{0}>1
such that if
\displaystyle\sigma\geq 1
,
\displaystyle\sup|\tau|<\tau_{0}
and
\displaystyle[\varphi_{\Omega}]_{C^{1}}<\mu
, for any
\displaystyle u\in C(\Omega_{\tilde{\rho}+1}(\sigma e_{n}))
satisfying

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{cases}u\geq 0\text{ in }\Omega_{\tilde{\rho}+1}(\sigma e_{n}),\\
\mathcal{P}^{-}(D^{2}u,Du)\leq 1\text{ in }\{|Du|\leq A_{0}\}\cap\Omega_{\tilde{\rho}+1}(\sigma e_{n}),\\

u>K\text{ in }B_{1/4}(\sigma e_{n}),\end{cases}

then
\displaystyle u>1
in
\displaystyle\Omega_{\tilde{\rho}}(\sigma e_{n})
.

This lemma deals with both interior and boundary cases.
If
\displaystyle\sigma
is large enough so that
\displaystyle B_{\tilde{\rho}+1}(\sigma e_{n})\subset\Omega

\displaystyle\partial\Omega_{\tilde{\rho}+1}(\sigma e_{n})=\emptyset

Proof.

Note that
\displaystyle\overline{B_{1/4}(\sigma e_{n})}\subset\Omega

\displaystyle\mu>0

We compare
\displaystyle u

\displaystyle\displaystyle B(x)=\frac{K}{2\cdot 4^{p}}(|x-\sigma e_{n}|^{-p}-(\tilde{\rho}+1)^{-p}).

Then for
\displaystyle\tau_{0},\mu<1/(2\tilde{\rho}+2)
and
\displaystyle\eta<\eta_{0}

(1)

\displaystyle B(x)\leq 0
in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{\tilde{\rho}+1}(\sigma e_{n})

(2)

\displaystyle B(x)<K

Moreover, choosing a large

and

in
\displaystyle\{|DB|\leq A_{0}\}\cap B_{\tilde{\rho}+1}(\sigma e_{n})\setminus B_{1/4}(\sigma e_{n})

\displaystyle B(x)>1
in
\displaystyle B_{\tilde{\rho}}(\sigma e_{n})
.

Finally we claim that
\displaystyle u\geq B
in
\displaystyle\Omega_{\tilde{\rho}+1}(\sigma e_{n})\setminus B_{1/4}(\sigma e_{n})
. We
assume the contrary that
\displaystyle u-B
has a negative minimum at
\displaystyle x_{0}\in\overline{\Omega_{\tilde{\rho}+1}(\sigma e_{n})\setminus B_{1/4}(\sigma e_{n})}
.
Then from (1) and (2),
\displaystyle x_{0}
cannot be on

\displaystyle\partial B_{1/4}(\sigma e_{n})

\displaystyle x_{0}\in\partial\Omega\cap B_{\tilde{\rho}+1}(\sigma e_{n})

, which also contradicts

Therefore we get
\displaystyle u>1
in
\displaystyle\Omega_{\tilde{\rho}}(\sigma e_{n})

\displaystyle C^{1,\alpha_{0}}
estimates for
\displaystyle h
, letting
\displaystyle\tilde{l}(x)=h(0)+Dh(0)\cdot x
, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle|h(x)-\tilde{l}(x)|\leq C_{e}|x|^{1+\alpha_{0}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\beta_{0}\cdot Dh(0)=0,\ |Dh(0)|\leq C_{e}.

Now we choose small
\displaystyle r<\frac{1}{2}
such that
\displaystyle C_{e}r^{1+\alpha_{0}}\leq\frac{1}{3}r^{1+\alpha}
and choose
\displaystyle\delta=\frac{1}{3}r^{1+\alpha}
.
Letting
\displaystyle l(x)=u(0)+Dh(0)\cdot x
, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left\lVert u-\tilde{l}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{r})}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert u-h\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{r})}+\left\lVert h-\tilde{l}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{r})}+|u(0)-h(0)|

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq 2\delta+C_{e}r^{1+\alpha_{0}}\leq r^{1+\alpha}.

∎
