Norm rigidity for arithmetic and profinite groups
Leonid Polterovich, Yehuda Shalom, Zvi Shem-Tov

Abstract
Let A A be a commutative ring, and assume every non-trivial ideal of A A has finite-index. We show that if SL n ​ ( A ) {\rm{SL}}_{n}(A) has bounded elementary generation then every conjugation-invariant norm on it is either discrete or precompact. If G G is any group satisfying this dichotomy we say that G G has the dichotomy property . We relate the dichotomy property, as well as some natural variants of it, to other rigidity results in the theory of arithmetic and profinite groups such as the celebrated normal subgroup theorem of Margulis and the seminal work of Nikolov and Segal. As a consequence we derive constraints to the possible approximations of certain non residually finite central extensions of arithmetic groups, which we hope might have further applications in the study of sofic groups. In the last section we provide several open problems for further research.

Introduction

A classical theorem of Ostrowski says that every absolute value on the field of rational numbers
{\mathbb{Q}}
,
or equivalently on the ring of integers
{\mathbb{Z}}
, is equivalent to either the standard (real) absolute value, or
a
p
-adic absolute value for which the closure of
{\mathbb{Z}}
is compact.
One of the main purposes of this paper is a non-abelian analogue of this result for
{\rm{SL}}_{n\geq 3}({\mathbb{Z}})
and
related groups of arithmetic type. In a different direction, our results can also be viewed as part of the Margulis–Zimmer
rigidity theory: they extend the celebrated Margulis’ normal subgroup theorem for some arithmetic groups, and
prove for them rigidity theorems for homomorphisms into certain non-locally compact groups– those equipped with a
bi-invariant metric. Somewhat surprisingly, this turns out to be strongly related also to the deep work of Nikolov–Segal
on profinite groups, and to constraints on sofic approximations of certain central extensions of arithmetic groups.
To make all this precise, we shall need the notion of a conjugation-invariant norm on a group.

The main results – dual compact setting.

We next move to present a duality between the discrete and compact framework.

Given a topological metrizable group

is separable norm complete.

We prove the theorem in Section
3
.
We point out a relation to the work
[
18
]
of Nikolov and Segal on quotients of finitely generated
profinite groups.
Recall that a group
G
is said to be FAb if every virtually-abelian quotient of
G
is finite. When
G

is a topological group, this refers to continuous quotients.
Nikolov and Segal showed that for a finitely generated profinite group

An example of a finitely-presented meager group with possible application to the theory of approximable groups.

Recall that a group is called
meager
if any conjugation-invariant norm on it is bounded and discrete.
As an application of Theorem
1.8
, we construct an example of a finitely-presented meager group.
In
[
14
]
, Moore constructed by number theoretic methods (following Weil’s work on the
metaplectic kernel) non-split
2\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}1
central extensions
\pi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\tilde{G}\to G={\rm{SL}}_{n}({\mathbb{Q}}_{p})
.
As an application of Theorem
1.8
we have the following result.

Theorem 1.17
.

Let
\tilde{\Gamma}=\pi^{-1}({\rm{SL}}_{n}({\mathbb{Z}}[\frac{1}{p}]))
. Then every conjugation-invariant norm on
\tilde{\Gamma}
is discrete
and bounded.

We prove the theorem at the end of Section
2
. It seems that all previously known meager groups were
essentially boundedly simple (from any non trivial element one can get to any other by uniformly bounded multiplication of conjugates).
This property implies uniform discreteness of all conjugation invariant norms. In the above example the abundance of (finite index)
normal subgroups makes such uniformity impossible.
We hope Theorem
1.17
might be of interest in the theory of approximable groups. One of the equivalent
definitions of a group
G
being approximable with respect to a class of groups endowed with a length function
is that
G
can be embedded in a certain large, ultra product group, endowed naturally with a bi-invariant metric.
From Theorem
1.17
we see that
any embedding of
\tilde{\Gamma}
in such a group must be discrete
.
This yields some inherent uniformity in all possible approximations of
\tilde{\Gamma}
, which might be useful
in trying to contradict their existence.

Norm rigidity for abstract groups

We start with proving the first part of the Theorem

.
As pointed out in the introduction, proving the second part is more involved.
In particular, we will see that in general the dichotomy property
must not be preserved under passage to a finite-index subgroup (Proposition

2.1
Proof of Theorem
1.8
(
i
)

To prove the first part of the theorem, we analyze the restrictions of our conjugation-invariant norm to certain
abelian subgroups. These subgroups are described in the following lemma.

Lemma 2.1

(

[
22
, Lemma 2.4]
).

Fix an integer
n\geq 3
. Every elementary matrix
I+te_{ij}\in{\rm{SL}}_{n}(A)
belongs to some copy of a subgroup isomorphic to
A^{2}
, contained naturally in the semi-direct product
{\rm{SL}}_{2}(A)\rtimes A^{2}
, itself embedded in
{\rm{SL}}_{n}(A)
.
In fact, the asserted copy
{\rm{SL}}_{2}(A)
can always be found along the
main diagonal (i.e. occupying the entries
(i,j)
with
i,j=k,k+1
, for some

1\leq k\leq n-1
).

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
A
be an integral domain with unit, and
{\mathfrak{q}}
an infinite ideal in
A
.
Let
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}
be a non-discrete norm on
{\mathfrak{q}}\oplus{\mathfrak{q}}
. Assume
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}
is invariant
under the standard linear action of
{\rm{E}}(2,{\mathfrak{q}},A)
on
{\mathfrak{q}}\oplus{\mathfrak{q}}
. Then for every
\epsilon>0
there
exists a non-zero ideal
{\mathfrak{q}}^{\prime}
, contained in
{\mathfrak{q}}
, such that
{\mathfrak{q}}^{\prime}\oplus{\mathfrak{q}}^{\prime}
is contained in
D_{\epsilon}(0)

(the
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}
-ball of radius

\epsilon
, centered at
0
).

Proof.

Let
(x,y)\in{\mathfrak{q}}\oplus{\mathfrak{q}}
. Then for each
z\in{\mathfrak{q}}
we have

\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}1&-z\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0&z\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}zy\\
0\end{pmatrix},

and so

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert(zy,0)\right\rVert}\leq 2\mathinner{\!\left\lVert(x,y)\right\rVert}.

(2)

Similarly,

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert(0,zx)\right\rVert}\leq 2\mathinner{\!\left\lVert(x,y)\right\rVert}.

(3)

Applying (
2
) again we get

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert(z^{\prime}zx,0)\right\rVert}\leq 4\mathinner{\!\left\lVert(x,y)\right\rVert},

for each
z,z^{\prime}\in{\mathfrak{q}}
.
Combining this with (
3
) we find that

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert(z^{\prime}z^{\prime\prime}x,zx)\right\rVert}\leq 6\mathinner{\!\left\lVert(x,y)\right\rVert},

for any
z,z^{\prime},z^{\prime\prime}\in{\mathfrak{q}}
.
In particular, for any
a,b\in A

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert(ax^{3},bx^{3})\right\rVert}\leq 6\mathinner{\!\left\lVert(x,y)\right\rVert}.

Thus if
(x,y)\in{\mathfrak{q}}\oplus{\mathfrak{q}}
satisfies
6\mathinner{\!\left\lVert(x,y)\right\rVert}\leq\epsilon
, then the ideal
{\mathfrak{q}}^{\prime}=Ax^{3}

satisfies the required property. The result now follows from the fact that
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}
is non-discrete.
∎

Notice that for this proof, we only used the fact there exists
M>0
such that
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert gv\right\rVert}\leq M\mathinner{\!\left\lVert v\right\rVert}

for each
g\in{\rm{E}}(2,{\mathfrak{q}},A)
and
v\in{\mathfrak{q}}\oplus{\mathfrak{q}}
.

Lemma 2.3
.

With the notation of Lemma
2.2
, let
\overline{{\mathfrak{q}}\oplus{\mathfrak{q}}}
be the metric completion of
{\mathfrak{q}}\oplus{\mathfrak{q}}

with respect to
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}
. Assume that
A
has the property that every non-zero ideal in
A
has
finite index. Then
\overline{{\mathfrak{q}}\oplus{\mathfrak{q}}}
is profinite.

Proof.

Consider all subgroups of
{\mathfrak{q}}\oplus{\mathfrak{q}}
of the form
{\mathfrak{q}}^{\prime}\oplus{\mathfrak{q}}^{\prime}
, where
{\mathfrak{q}}^{\prime}
is a non-trivial ideal of
{\mathfrak{q}}
.
Viewing them as subgroups of
\overline{{\mathfrak{q}}\oplus{\mathfrak{q}}}
, we may take the closure. In this way we get for each
non-trivial ideal
{\mathfrak{q}}^{\prime}
of
{\mathfrak{q}}
a closed subgroup of
\overline{{\mathfrak{q}}\oplus{\mathfrak{q}}}
. Denote this subgroup
by
U_{{\mathfrak{q}}^{\prime}}
. Since
{\mathfrak{q}}^{\prime}
is non-trivial it has finite index in
{\mathfrak{q}}
, hence
U_{{\mathfrak{q}}^{\prime}}
has finite index in

\overline{{\mathfrak{q}}\oplus{\mathfrak{q}}}
. Thus
U_{{\mathfrak{q}}^{\prime}}
is open.
On the other hand, by Lemma
2.2
for each
\epsilon>0
there exists a non-trivial ideal
{\mathfrak{q}}^{\prime}
such that

U_{{\mathfrak{q}}^{\prime}}
is contained in the ball of radius
\epsilon
around
0
. By taking translates we
obtain a finite cover of
\overline{{\mathfrak{q}}\oplus{\mathfrak{q}}}
by open-closed cosets,
each of them is contained in a ball of radius
\epsilon
.
We conclude that
\overline{{\mathfrak{q}}\oplus{\mathfrak{q}}}

is profinite.
∎

Now we are ready to complete the proof.
Let
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}
be a non-discrete conjugation-invariant norm on
{\rm{E}}_{n}(A)
, and
denote the metric completion of
{\rm{E}}_{n}(A)
with respect to
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}
by
G
.
By Theorem
A.2
we have that the elementary subgroups are non-discrete. Thus by Lemma

2.1
and Lemma
2.3
, the completion of each elementary subgroup is profinite.
Denote these completions by
U_{1},\dots,U_{n(n-1)}
. Then each
U_{i}
can be seen as a subgroup
of
G
, and from the fact that
{\rm{E}}_{n}(A)
is boundedly generated
by its elementary groups we get that
G
is boundedly generated by the
U_{i}
.
This means that
G=U_{i_{1}}\cdots U_{i_{m}}
for some finite sequence
(i_{1},\dots,i_{m})
(
1\leq i_{j}\leq n(n-1)
).
Hence to finish the proof
it suffices to show that for any topological group
G
, if
G
is a finite (set-theoretic) product

G=G_{1}\cdots G_{m}
of a finite collection of subgroups
G_{i}<G
, and each
G_{i}
is profinite in the relative
topology, then
G
is profinite itself. To see this, notice first for each
i
, any finite-dimensional representation

\pi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G_{i}\to{\rm{GL}}_{d}({\mathbb{C}})
has finite image. This follows easily from the fact that
G_{i}
is profinite and
that
{\rm{GL}}_{d}({\mathbb{C}})
has no small subgroups, meaning
that there exists an identity neighborhood
U
such that the only subgroup of
{\rm{GL}}_{d}({\mathbb{C}})
contained in
U
is the
trivial group. Hence the image of any finite-dimensional representation
\pi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\to{\rm{GL}}_{d}({\mathbb{C}})
is finite.
Since
G
is compact, being a continuous image of the compact group
G_{1}\times\dots\times G_{m}
,
it follows that the kernel of any such representation must be open. On the other hand,
as a consequence of the Peter-Weyl theorem, each non-trivial element
g\in G

has an open neighborhood
V_{g}
, lying outside of the kernel of some finite dimensional representation

\rho_{g}
of
G
. Given any open identity neighborhood
V\subset G
,
we can cover its complement by finitely many of the
V_{g}
(using again the fact that
G
is compact),
so that the kernel of
the direct sum of the corresponding
\rho_{g}
’s is contained in
V
. Thus for any open identity neighborhood

V
there exists a finite-dimensional representation
\pi
as above,
with
\ker{\pi}\subset V
.
It follows that
G
admits a basis
of the identity consisting of open and closed subgroups, hence
G
is profinite.
∎

Preparations for the proof of Theorem
1.8
(
ii
) –
Behavior under passage to finite-index subgroups, and the weak dichotomy property

Before we turn to the proof of the second part of the theorem, we introduce a slightly weaker version
of the dichotomy property called the
weak dichotomy property
.
The following lemma states that any group having a finite-index subgroup with the (strong) dichotomy
property satisfies the (strong) dichotomy property itself.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
G
be a group, and
H
a finite-index subgroup of
G
. Assume
H
has the (strong) dichotomy property.
Then
G
has the (strong) dichotomy property as well.

Proof.

Let
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}
a non-discrete conjugation-invariant norm on
G
. We need to show that
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}

is precompact.
To prove this, we claim that the restriction of
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}
to
H
is non-discrete as well.
Indeed, suppose
otherwise, then
H
is discrete (with respect to the metric on
G
induced by
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}
).
In particular,
H
is closed in
G
. Thus all of the cosets of
H
are closed and discrete in
G
.
Since
H
has finite index in
G
it follows that
G
is discrete, which is a contradiction.
Thus the restriction of
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}
to
H
is non-discrete, as claimed.
Since
H
has the dichotomy property, this restriction is precompact. Since
H
has finite index in
G
it follows that

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}
is precompact as well. If the metric completion of
H
is further profinite then clearly
the same is true for
G
.
∎

It turn out that the converse statement, that the dichotomy property is preserved under passage to
a finite-index subgroup is false (Proposition
2.7
), and this fact is our motivation
for defining the weak dichotomy property.
We start with the following simple but useful lemma.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
G
be a group and
N
a normal subgroup of
G
. Let
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}
be a non-discrete conjugation-invariant
norm on
G
. Assume that the restriction of
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}
to
N
is discrete. Then
C_{G}(N)
(the centralizer
of
N
in
G
)
contains an open neighborhood of
1
in
G
(with respect to the topology induced by
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}
).

Proof.

Since the restriction of
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}
to
N
is discrete, there exists
\epsilon>0
such that
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert n\right\rVert}>\epsilon

for every
1\neq n\in N
. Let
g\in G
, and suppose that
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert g\right\rVert}<\frac{\epsilon}{2}
. Then for
every
n\in N
we have

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert[g,n]\right\rVert}\leq 2\mathinner{\!\left\lVert g\right\rVert}<\epsilon.

(4)

On the other hand
[g,n]
belongs to
N
, since
N
is normal in
G
. Thus

[g,n]=1
, and so
g\in C_{G}(N)
. Thus the ball of radius
\frac{\epsilon}{2}
is contained in
C_{G}(N)
,
finishing the proof of the lemma.
∎

Corollary 2.6
.

Let
G
be a group, and
A,B
subgroups of
G
. Suppose
G=AB
, a direct product. Suppose
A
has trivial center.
Let
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}
be a non-discrete norm on
G
. Then at least one of the restrictions of
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}

to
A
and
B
is non-discrete.

Proof.

Suppose the restriction to
A
is discrete. Then by Lemma
2.5
we
have that
B
contains a neighborhood of
1
in
G
.
Since
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}
is non-discrete, it follows that its restriction to
B
is non-discrete.
∎

Note that a direct product
\Gamma_{1}\times\Gamma_{2}
of two infinite, residually finite groups cannot satisfy
the dichotomy property, due to Proposition
1.5
.
Using this observation, we show that the dichotomy property must not be preserved
under passage to finite-index subgroups.

Proposition 2.7

(Example)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a group having the dichotomy property. Assume
\Gamma
has trivial center. Let

G
be the semidirect product
G=(\Gamma\times\Gamma)\rtimes({\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}})
,
where the action of the non-trivial element in
{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}
on
\Gamma\times\Gamma

is given by switching the coordinates.
Then
G
has the dichotomy property (while its finite-index subgroup
\Gamma\times\Gamma

does not, as long as
\Gamma
admits some non-discrete conjugation-invariant norm).

Proof.

Denote by
\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2}
the copies of
\Gamma
in
G
in the first and second coordinate respectively.
Let
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}

be a non-discrete, conjugation-invariant norm on
G
. We need to show that
G
is precompact with respect
to that norm.
To prove this, note first that
\Gamma_{1}\Gamma_{2}
is non-discrete.
Thus either
\Gamma_{1}
or
\Gamma_{2}
are non-discrete,
due to Corollary
2.6
. Assume without loss of generality that
\Gamma_{1}
is non-discrete. Then
applying the non-trivial element of
{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}
on
\Gamma_{1}
we see that
\Gamma_{2}
is non-discrete as well. Since
\Gamma

has the dichotomy property it follows that both
\Gamma_{1}

and
\Gamma_{2}
are precompact. Thus
\Gamma_{1}\Gamma_{2}
is precompact. Since
\Gamma_{1}\Gamma_{2}

has finite index in
G
, we conclude that
G
is precompact as well.
∎

Our example in Proposition
2.7
uses significantly the fact that the dichotomy property is not preserved
under taking direct products. If
G=A\times B
then our basic example of a conjugation-invariant
norm on
G
which is neither discrete or precompact, is the singular extension of a
non-discrete norm on one of the factors
A
or
B
of
G
(Construction
1.2
).
Denote these extension norms by
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}_{1}
and
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}_{2}
respectively. Then clearly the sum
norm
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}_{1}+\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}_{2}

is discrete.
As we shall see, the existence of such norms is essentially the reason that the dichotomy property
is not preserved under taking direct products. This leads us to make the following two definitions.

Definition 2.8
.

Let
G
be a group. A conjugation-invariant norm
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}
on
G
is
called
strongly non-discrete
(

) if for any non-discrete norm
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}_{1}

on
G
, the sum norm
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}+\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}_{1}
is non-discrete.

Definition 2.9
.

Let
G
be a group. We say that
G
has the
weak dichotomy property
if every SND norm on
G

is precompact.

Theorem 2.10
.

Let
G
be a group and let
N
be a finite-index, normal subgroup of
G
. Assume that
G
has the weak dichotomy property.
Then
N
has the weak dichotomy property as well.
Similarly, if any SND norm on
G
has a profinite completion then the same is true for
N
.
If every SND norm on
G
is compact, then
N
has this property as well.

Proof.

Let
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}
be an SND norm on
N
. Replacing
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}
by

n\mapsto\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert n\right\rVert}}{1+\mathinner{\!\left\lVert n\right\rVert}}
, we may assume that

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}
is bounded by
1
. For each element
g\in G
consider the function on
N
given by

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}_{g}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathinner{\!\left\lVert gng^{-1}\right\rVert}
. It is easy to see that
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}_{g}
is again an SND norm on
N
.
Finally, set
\tilde{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert n\right\rVert}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{1}{[G\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}N]}\sum_{s\in G/N}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert n\right\rVert}_{s}
. It is easy to see that

is again an SND norm on
N
, and is bounded by
1
. Clearly we have

\tilde{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert gng^{-1}\right\rVert}}=\tilde{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert n\right\rVert}}
for each element

in
G
.
Thus we can consider the singular extension of
\tilde{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}}
to
G
(Construction

). We continue to denote
this extension by
\tilde{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}}
. Since the restriction of
\tilde{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}}
to

is an SND norm on
N
, we have
that
\tilde{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}}
is an SND norm on
G
. Since

has the weak dichotomy property,
\tilde{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}}
is

is precompact. Thus
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}
is precompact. This proves the first part of the theorem.
The second and third parts follow by repeating the argument above.
∎

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
G
be a direct product

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}_{M}

.

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}_{N}

. For each element

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert g\right\rVert}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathinner{\!\left\lVert n\right\rVert}_{N}+\mathinner{\!\left\lVert m\right\rVert}_{M}.

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}

.

Clearly
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}
is a non-discrete, conjugation-invariant norm on

. To prove that

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}_{1}

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}_{N}

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}_{1}
to

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}_{1}
to

. Combining this with the fact that

is precompact, we get that the restriction of
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}+\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}_{1}
to

is non-discrete. In particular
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}+\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}_{1}
is non-discrete, which is a contradiction. This contradiction

∎

Lemma 2.12
.

Let
G
be a direct product
G=N\times M
of groups, and assume

has trivial center.
Let
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}
be any SND norm on

.
Then
the restriction
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}|_{N}
, of
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}
to

, is an SND norm on

.

Proof.

Let
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}_{N}
be
a non-discrete conjugation-invariant norm on
N
. We have to show that

The following lemma plays a key role in our proof

Lemma 2.15

Let

∎

2.3

We turn to the proof of the second part of Theorem

2.4
Proof of Theorem

We need the following lemma.

Lemma 2.16

Let

be a group and

Proof.

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}_{G}

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}
is discrete. We claim that if the metric completion

\overline{G}
(with respect to
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}_{G}

\overline{E}

of

(with respect to
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}
). To see this, notice that
\overline{G}

is isometric to

is profinite, due to the fact that being profinite is closed under

∎

To prove Theorem
1.17
, let
\pi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\tilde{G}\to{\rm{SL}}_{n}({\mathbb{Q}}_{p})
be any non-split finite central extension of topological groups.
By

the lifted group

Notice that by the triangle inequality and finiteness of

Finally, we remark that while one can show that any finite index subgroup of

3
Norm rigidity for compact groups

3.1
Generalities

Recall that we called compact metrizable group
G

norm-complete
if any non-discrete conjugation-invariant norm on
G

generates the standard topology of
G
(Definition
1.10
). We say that
G
is
separable
norm complete if
any separable conjugation invariant norm on it induces the standard topology (Definition
1.15
).
Throughout we shall make use of two basic properties of separable metric spaces: The first one is that
a subspace of a separable metric space is always separable as well. The second one is that compact metric spaces
are always separable.
The following proposition shows that the property of being norm complete is equivalent to the seemingly weaker property that every
non-discrete conjugation-invariant norm is compact, making the notion of norm complete
analogous to the dichotomy property (Definition
1.3
).

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
G
be a compact metrizble group.
Assume that every non-discrete conjugation-invariant norm
on
G
is compact. Then any such norm generates the standard topology on
G
.

Proof.

Let
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}_{0}
be a non-discrete, conjugation-invariant norm on
G
. Then

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}_{0}
is compact and we have to show that
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}_{0}

induces the standard topology on
G
.
Fix any conjugation-invariant norm
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}
on
G
inducing the standard
topology, and consider the sum norm
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}_{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}+\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}_{0}
.
Let
G_{0}=G
with the topology induced by
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}_{0}
and let
G_{1}
be the
abstract group
G
with the topology induced by
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}_{1}
.
The identity map
\varphi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}x\to x
is a continuous bijection between
G_{1}
and
G_{0}
.
Since
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}
and
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}_{0}
are compact,
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}_{1}
is non-discrete, hence
compact as well. Thus
G_{1}
is compact.
Thus
\varphi
is a homeomorphism. Thus

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}_{1}
is equivalent to
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}_{0}
. A symmetric argument
shows that
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}_{1}
is equivalent
to
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}
. Thus
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}_{0}
is equivalent to
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}
which finishes the proof of the
proposition.
∎

We remark that although the proposition is stated for norm complete groups, the argument above proves the analogous statement for separable norm complete groups. Recall that norm-complete groups are just-infinite, meaning that every infinite normal subgroup
of a norm-complete group must be of finite index (Proposition
1.5
). The following observation, which is a consequence of the seminal work
of Gleason-Montomery-Zippin towards Hilbert’s fifth problem, gives a rough classification of norm-complete groups.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
G
be a compact metrizable just-infinite group. Then
G
is either a profinite group, or
a semisimple Lie group.

Since we could not find a suitable reference we give a proof here.

Proof.

Let
G_{0}
denote the identity connected component of
G
.
If
G_{0}
is finite then it must be trivial, and in this case
G
is profinite.
Hence, since
G
is just infinite, we may assume
G_{0}
has finite-index in
G
.
By
[
13
]
, we have that
G
is an inverse limit of Lie groups,
G=\lim_{\alpha}{G_{\alpha}}
.
Then for each
\alpha
, we have a surjection
\phi_{\alpha}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\to G_{\alpha}
, and the kernel of
\phi_{\alpha}

is either finite or of finite index in
G
. In the latter case,
G_{\alpha}
is finite. Since
G
has finitely many connected
components we must have at least one
\alpha
for which the kernel of
\phi_{\alpha}
is finite. But then

G
is a finite extension of
G_{\alpha}
hence
G
is a Lie group itself. Thus
G_{0}
is a compact connected Lie group.
Let
Z_{0}
denote the center of
G_{0}
. Since
Z_{0}
is a characteristic subgroup of
G_{0}
, it is normal in
G
, and so
Z_{0}

is either finite or of finite index. We claim that the latter possibility, that
Z_{0}
has finite index, cannot occur. Indeed,
otherwise
Z_{0}=G_{0}
is a compact torus (a product of circles). Then the torsion subgroup of
Z_{0}
forms an
infinite characteristic subgroup of infinite index, hence an infinite normal subgroup of
G
of infinite index,
and we get a contradiction. Thus
Z_{0}
is finite, and so
G_{0}
, and hence
G
, is semisimple, completing the proof of the lemma.
∎

As indicated in the introduction, Proposition

For this, let

be the valuation ring of

{\rm{GL}}(R_{p})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\cup_{n}{\rm{GL}}_{n}(R_{p})

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert g\right\rVert}_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathinner{\!\left\lVert I_{n}-g\right\rVert}_{op}

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}_{op}

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert X\right\rVert}_{op}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sup_{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert v\right\rVert}_{\infty}=1}(\mathinner{\!\left\lVert Xv\right\rVert}_{\infty}).

Here
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\sum_{i}v_{i}e_{i}\right\rVert}_{\infty}=\sup_{i}|v_{i}|_{p}

is the standard basis of

and

To define a norm on

Then

can be described as follows: As an abstract group

4
Questions

We close the paper by posing several questions that naturally arise from our work.

1. The dichotomy property for higher-rank lattices.

Let
\Gamma
be a so-called “higher-rank” arithmetic lattice, or, more generally
S
-arithmetic lattice of a simply connected simple algebraic group
{\mathbb{G}}
defined over a global field
K
.
In our paper we showed that when

{\mathbb{G}}={\rm{SL}}_{n\geq 3}
,
\Gamma
has the dichotomy property,
but it is very natural to conjecture that, as with most elements of the rigidity theory of arithmetic groups,
this should hold for all
{\mathbb{G}}
and
K
(and
S
). The most non-trivial ingredient in our approach is the use of bounded generation, hence we certainly expect (although it would still be nice to establish) that as first step these results can be extended to other Chevalley groups like
{\rm{Sp}}_{2n}
.

Two especially challenging cases seem to stand out: The first is that when
{\mathbb{G}}
is anisotropic over
K
, i.e.
\Gamma
has no unipotent elements
(and is a co-compact lattice in its “natural envelope”; see
[
30
, Section 6.8]
for more detalis, as well as
a construction of such groups by means of maximal orders in division algebras).
Recall from the introduction that the dichotomy property implies being just infinite,
and in these cases, even that of
{\mathbb{G}}({\mathbb{R}})={\rm{SL}}_{3}({\mathbb{R}})
, there is no known algebraic proof of the latter (in fact no other proof besides that of Margulis, invoking
property (T) and amenability).

In the original version submitted for publication we discussed the question
of
{\mathbb{G}}={\rm{SL}}_{2}
with
K={\mathbb{Q}}
but
S
containing some finite prime, i.e.,
\Gamma={\rm{SL}}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}}[1/p])
.
Here there is an algebraic proof for the normal subgroup theorem, but a main ingredient in our approach breaks completely:
Unlike in the proof of Theorem
1.8
, there does exist a norm on the unipotent subgroup

{\mathbb{Z}}[1/p]
invariant under its normalizer in
\Gamma
(acting on it via multiplication by powers of
p
), which is neither discrete nor precompact.
A construction of such a norm was given to us by Dor Elboim, to whom we are thankful. We refer the reader to the previous version of the paper, appearing on arXiv, for a sketch
of Elbom’s construction. Fortunately, following the distribution of that version, A. Trost
[
25
]
found a way around this issue, by adapting Lemma
A.8
.

2. Norm-completeness for finitely-generated FAb profinite groups.

In their deep work on finitely generated profinite groups (
[
17
,
18
]
),
Nikolov and Segal have shown that such groups
G
which are so-called FAb
have the property that their normal subgroups of countable index must in
fact be of finite index (and open)
[
18
, Corollary 5.24]
. If the group
G
is furthermore just infinite in the topological sense, then they show that it must be so in an abstract sense as well – any infinite normal subgroup must have finite index
[
18
, Corollary 1.5]
.
Recall that in our paper we established these two same properties: The first for
G
which is separable norm-complete, and the second for
G
which is norm-complete, and it seems very natural to wonder if those two norm rigidity properties are the “reason” underlying these deep results of Nikolov–Segal, i.e.,
Is it true that a finitely generated profinite FAb group must be separable norm complete, and one which is furthermore just-infinite must be norm complete?

3. Norms on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
invariant under thin subgroups of
{\rm{SL}}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
.

Let
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}
be a non-discrete norm on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
(viewed as an additive
group
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}=({\mathbb{Z}}^{2},+)
), and assume
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}
is invariant under the usual linear action of
some subgroup
\Gamma<{\rm{SL}}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
.
If
\Gamma=\left<\begin{pmatrix}1&m\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
m&1\end{pmatrix}\right>
for some
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
then
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}
must be precompact, due to Lemma
2.3
.
It is well known that if
m\geq 3
then
\Gamma
has infinite index
in
{\rm{SL}}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
, making it a
thin
subgroup, i.e. an infinite co-volume, discrete, Zariski dense subgroup of
{\rm{SL}}_{2}({\mathbb{R}})
.
An interesting question is whether any thin subgroup has this property. That is,

Must any non-discrete norm on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
invariant under the linear action of a thin subgroup
\Gamma<{\rm{SL}}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})

be pre-compact?

Note that there do exist non-discrete, unbounded norms on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
which are invariant under the action of a cyclic
subgroup of
{\rm{SL}}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
. For instance, let
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}
be a norm on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
defined by

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}(x,y)\to\max\{\frac{|x|_{p}}{2},|y|\},

where
|\cdot|_{p}
is the
p
-adic absolute value and
|\cdot|
is the usual absolute value on
{\mathbb{Z}}
.
It is easy to check that
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}
is a non-discrete, unbounded norm on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
, invariant under the action
of the matrix
\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}
. It seems likely possible to construct a non-discrete, unbounded norm
invariant under the action of a hyperbolic matrix as well.

A possible approach to attack the aforementioned question is as follows: For a subgroup
\Gamma<{\rm{SL}}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})

and a positive number
M>0
, let
S_{M}(\Gamma)
denote the set of sums:

S_{M}(\Gamma)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{\sum_{i=1}^{l}\gamma_{i}\mid\gamma_{i}\in\Gamma,l\leq M\}.

Suppose that
S_{M}(\Gamma)
contains some congruence subgroup of
SL_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
(and in particular a subgroup of the form

H=\left<\begin{pmatrix}1&m\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
m&1\end{pmatrix}\right>
for some
m
).
If
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}
is any non-discrete norm on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
invariant under the action of
\Gamma
, then for
v\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
and
h\in H

we have

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert hv\right\rVert}\leq M\mathinner{\!\left\lVert v\right\rVert}.

It follows from the remark after the proof of Lemma
2.2
and Lemma
2.3
that
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}

is pre-compact (in fact, its metric completion is profinite). This raises the following question, which
seems of interest in its own right, being related to various ”sum-product” phenomena in the deep theory of so-called ”super-strong approximation”:

Let
\Gamma<{\rm{SL}}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
be a thin subgroup. Does there exist a positive number
M>0

such that the set
S_{M}(\Gamma)
defined above contains some congruence subgroup of
SL_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
?

(in fact,
S_{M}(\Gamma)
should contain the group of all scalar congruence matrices, which is invariant under addition.)

The above discussion shows that any thin subgroup
\Gamma<SL_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
for which the answer is positive has the property that
any non-discrete norm it preserves on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
must be pre-compact.

To see a non-trivial example where one has positive answer to this question note that for any positive integer
m
, any element of the full congruence subgroup
{\rm{SL}}(2,{\mathbb{Z}},m)=\ker({\rm{SL}}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})\to{\rm{SL}}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}}/m^{2}{\mathbb{Z}}))
can be written as a sum of a bounded number (depending on
m
) of elements in
the thin subgroup
\Gamma=\left<\begin{pmatrix}1&m\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
m&1\end{pmatrix}\right>
, as the following computation shows:

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}1+m^{2}a&mb\\
mc&1+m^{2}d\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=(2m^{2}-3)\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}1&m(b-2)\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle+\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
m(c-a-d+4)&1\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}1+m^{2}(a-2)&m\\
m(a-2)&1\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle+\begin{pmatrix}1&m\\
m(d-2)&1+m^{2}(d-2)\end{pmatrix}.

Of course, one can naturally extend the problem above for all
n\geq 2
, and more general rings
A
in place of
{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Appendix A
Production of elementary matrices

The results of this appendix are related to
conjugacy width
in
{\rm{SL}}_{n}
.
We feel that such results should be standard, but as we were unable to find suitable versions
in the literature we give proofs. Our proof of Theorem
A.2

relies on computations from
[
1
]
.
Let
A
be an integral domain with unit, and fix an integer
n\geq 2
.
We fix notation for some subgroups of
{\rm{SL}}_{n}(A)
.
For each pair of integers
1\leq i\neq j\leq n
we denote by
e_{ij}
the
n\times n
matrix
with
1
in the
(i,j)
-entry and
0
elsewhere. We denote by
I
(or
I_{n}
if we want to emphasize
the dimention) the identity matrix in
{\rm{SL}}_{n}(A)
.
We denote by
{\rm{E}}_{ij}(A)
the group of matrices of the form
I+ae_{ij}
, where
a\in A
.
A matrix of this form is called an
elementary matrix
.
We denote by
{\rm{E}}_{n}(A)
the subgroup of
{\rm{SL}}_{n}(A)
generated by all elementary matrices.
For any non-zero ideal
{\mathfrak{q}}
in
A
,
we denote by
{\rm{E}}(n,A,{\mathfrak{q}})
the subgroup of
{\rm{E}}_{n}(A)

generated by the elementary matrices with off-diagonal entries belonging to
{\mathfrak{q}}
.
We denote
{\rm{SL}}(n,A,{\mathfrak{q}})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\text{ker}({\rm{SL}}_{n}(A)\to{\rm{SL}}_{n}(A/{\mathfrak{q}}))
(the map being the natural projection).
If it is clear from the context what
A
and
n
are, we denote also
{\rm{SL}}(n,A,{\mathfrak{q}})=\Gamma({\mathfrak{q}})
.

Let
G
be any group and
g
an element
g\in G
. Let
S
be any subset of
G
.
We define

g^{S}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{sg^{i}s^{-1}\mid s\in S,i=\pm 1\}.

(5)

Consider the group
\left<g^{S}\right>
generated by
g^{S}
.
For any subgroup
H
of
G
we can ask what is the minimal length of a non-trivial element
in
\left<g^{S}\right>\cap H
, with respect to the set of generators
g^{S}
.
The main result of this section is obtaining a uniform bound on this quantity, over all non-central
elements in
G
, where

G={\rm{SL}}(n,A,{\mathfrak{q}})
,
S={\rm{E}}(n,A,{\mathfrak{q}})
, and
H
an elementary group
H={\rm{E}}_{ij}({\mathfrak{q}})
.
Here
n
is any integer
n\geq 3
,
A
is an integral domain satisfying
{\rm{SR}}_{n-1}

(see Definition
A.1
below), and
{\mathfrak{q}}
is
any non-zero ideal in
A
. In fact, the bound we obtain is
absolute
(
2^{9}
), although
we do not make any use of this fact.

We start by recalling the definition of the
stable range
of a ring.

Definition A.1
.

Let
A
be a commutative ring with unit.
For
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
, an element
v=\sum_{i=1}^{r}v_{i}e_{i}\in A^{r}
is called
unimodular
if

\sum v_{i}A=A
. Here
e_{i}
(
1\leq i\leq r
) is the standard basis of
A^{r}
(
1
is the
i
th coordinate
and
0
elsewhere).

A
satisfies
{\rm{SR}}_{m}
if for any unimodular element
v=\sum_{i=1}^{m+1}v_{i}e_{i}\in A^{m+1}

there exist elements
t_{1},\dots,t_{m}\in A
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{m}(v_{i}+t_{i}v_{m+1})e_{i}\in A^{m}
is
unimodular.
The smallest integer
m
such that
A
satisfies
{\rm{SR}}_{m}
is called the
stable range of
A
.

Theorem A.2
.

Let
n\geq 3
. Let
A
be an integral domain with unit, satisfying
{\rm{SR}}_{n-1}
. Let
{\mathfrak{q}}
be a non-zero ideal in
A
.
Let
\sigma
be any non-central element in
{\rm{SL}}(n,A,{\mathfrak{q}})
. Let
1\leq i\neq j\leq n
. Then
there exists a non-trivial element in
\left<\sigma^{{\rm{E}}(n,A,{\mathfrak{q}})}\right>\cap{\rm{E}}_{ij}({\mathfrak{q}})
of length at most
2^{9}
with
respect to the set of generators
\sigma^{{\rm{E}}(n,A,{\mathfrak{q}})}
.

For the rest of the section, we fix
n\geq 3
,
A
a ring satisfying
{\rm{SR}}_{n-1}
,
G={\rm{SL}}(n,A,{\mathfrak{q}})
,
and
S={\rm{E}}(n,A,{\mathfrak{q}})
.
We call a transformation
T\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\to G
a
{\mathfrak{q}}
-operation
if
T
is either of the form

g\mapsto sgs^{-1}
,
g\mapsto[g,s]
, or
g\mapsto[s,g]
, where
s\in S
.
To prove Theorem
A.2
, we will show that any non-central element
\sigma\in G

can be reduced to a non-trivial element of
{\rm{E}}_{ij}({\mathfrak{q}})
, using at most
9

{\mathfrak{q}}
-operations.
We shall make use of the following facts, which are easily verified:

(i)

Let
g,h
be any two matrices in
{\rm{GL}}_{n}(A)
. Let
1\leq i\neq j\leq n
.
Let
\alpha
be the
i
-th column of
g
and
\beta
the
j
-th column of
h
.
Then

ge_{ij}h=\alpha\beta.

(6)

(ii)

Let
x,y\in{\rm{GL}}_{n-1}(A)
,
v\in A^{n-1}
, and
u\in A^{\times}
.
Then

\left[\begin{pmatrix}u&v\\
0&x\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&y\end{pmatrix}\right]=\begin{pmatrix}1&(vy-v)(yx)^{-1}\\
0&[x,y]\end{pmatrix}.

(7)

Here we view
v
as a row vector.

(iii)

In the notation of (
ii
),

\left[\begin{pmatrix}x&v\\
0&u\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}y&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\right]=\begin{pmatrix}\left[x,y\right]&u^{-1}(v-xyx^{-1}v)\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}.

(8)

Here we view
v
as a column vector.

We will also make use of the following simple lemma concerning unimodular elements.

Lemma A.3
.

If
(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\in A^{n}
is unimodular, then so is
(a_{1},\dots,a_{n-1},a^{2}_{n})
.

Proof.

Let
(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})
so that

t_{1}a_{1}+\dots+t_{n}a_{n}=1.

(9)

Multiplying (
9
) by
a_{n}
we get

a_{n}t_{1}a_{1}+\dots+a_{n}t_{n-1}a_{n-1}+t_{n}a_{n}^{2}=a_{n}.

Hence
a_{n}
belongs to the ideal generated by
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}^{2}
and we get

a_{1}A+\dots+a_{n-1}A+a_{n}^{2}A=a_{1}A+\dots+a_{n}A=A.

∎

We turn to the proof of Theorem
A.2
. We divide the proof in several steps.
The first step is reducing
\sigma

to a copy of the semi-direct product
{\rm{Aff}}_{n-1}(A)={\rm{SL}}_{n-1}(A)\ltimes A^{n-1}
. We consider two copies of

{\rm{Aff}}_{n-1}(A)
inside
{\rm{SL}}_{n}(A)
; the images of the embeddings

(\gamma,v)\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}\gamma&v\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}
, and

(\gamma,v)\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}1&v^{T}\\
0&\gamma\end{pmatrix}
.
We denote these copies by
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
respectively.

Lemma A.4
.

Any non-central element
\sigma\in G
can be reduced to a non-central element
of either
G_{1}
or
G_{2}
, using at most
4

{\mathfrak{q}}
-operations.

Proof.

Let
\alpha=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}\\
\vdots\\
a_{n}\end{pmatrix}

be the first column of
\sigma
, and
\beta=\begin{pmatrix}b_{1},\dots,b_{n}\end{pmatrix}

be the second row of
\sigma^{-1}
. Let

\tau=I_{n}+pe_{12}
,
where
p
is some arbitrary non-zero element in
{\mathfrak{q}}
.

Case 1.
First, we consider the case where
\tau
and
\sigma
commute. Then we have

\displaystyle I_{n}

\displaystyle=[\sigma,\tau]

\displaystyle=\sigma(I_{n}+pe_{12})\sigma^{-1}(I_{n}-pe_{12})

\displaystyle=(I_{n}+p\sigma e_{12}\sigma^{-1})(I_{n}-pe_{12})

\displaystyle=(I_{n}+p\alpha\beta)(I_{n}-pe_{12}),

and so
\alpha\beta=e_{12}
. Hence

\alpha=ue_{1}
for some unit
u\in A
.
Thus in this case we have

\sigma=\begin{pmatrix}u&v\\
0&\sigma^{\prime}\end{pmatrix},

where
v\in{\mathfrak{q}}^{n-1}
and
\sigma^{\prime}\in{\rm{GL}}(n-1,A,{\mathfrak{q}})
. Suppose that
\sigma^{\prime}

does not belong to the centralizer of
{\rm{E}}(n-1,A,{\mathfrak{q}})
. Then there exists

\sigma^{\prime\prime}\in{\rm{E}}(n-1,{\mathfrak{q}},A)
so that
[\sigma^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime\prime}]\neq I_{n-1}
. By commutating

\sigma
with
\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&\sigma^{\prime\prime}\end{pmatrix}
we then get

\left[\sigma,\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&\sigma^{\prime\prime}\end{pmatrix}\right]=\begin{pmatrix}1&*\\
0&\left[\sigma^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime\prime}\right]\end{pmatrix},

which is non-central, due to the fact that
[\sigma^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime\prime}]\neq I_{n-1}
, and we are done.
If
\sigma^{\prime}
belongs to the centralizer of
{\rm{E}}(n-1,A,{\mathfrak{q}})
, then

\sigma^{\prime}=tI_{n-1}
for some unit
t\in A
, and we have

\begin{pmatrix}u&pte_{1}+v\\
0&tI_{n-1}\end{pmatrix}=\tau\sigma=\sigma\tau=\begin{pmatrix}u&pue_{1}+v\\
0&tI_{n-1}\end{pmatrix},

and so
u=t
.
Thus in this case
\sigma=\begin{pmatrix}u&v\\
0&uI_{n-1}\end{pmatrix}
.
Since
\sigma
is non-central we must have
v\neq 0
.
Thus there exists an element
\sigma^{\prime\prime}\in{\rm{E}}(n-1,A,{\mathfrak{q}})
so that
v\sigma^{\prime\prime-1}\neq v
.
Commutating
\sigma
with
\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&\sigma^{\prime\prime}\end{pmatrix}
we get,
using (
ii
),

\left[\sigma,\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&\sigma^{\prime\prime}\end{pmatrix}\right]=\begin{pmatrix}1&u^{-1}(v-v\sigma^{\prime\prime-1})\\
0&I_{n-1}\end{pmatrix},

which is non-central because
v\sigma^{\prime\prime-1}\neq v
.
Thus we see that if
\sigma
and
\tau
commute then
\sigma

can be reduced to an element belonging to one of
the copies of the semi-direct product by at most two

{\mathfrak{q}}
-operations.

Case 2.
Assume now that
\sigma
and
\tau
do not commute, i.e.

[\sigma,\tau]\neq I_{n}.

(10)

We first reduce to the case where
(a_{1},\dots,a_{n-1})\in A^{n-1}
is unimodular.
It is clear that
\alpha
is unimodular, and so

(a_{1},\dots,a_{n-1},a^{2}_{n})
is unimodular, due to Lemma
A.3
.
Thus there exists
t=(t_{1},\dots,t_{n-1})\in A^{n-1}
so that

(a_{1}+t_{1}a^{2}_{n},\dots,a_{n-1}+t_{n-1}a^{2}_{n})
is unimodular.
Conjugating
\sigma
by
\begin{pmatrix}I_{n-1}&a_{n}t\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}
(viewing

as a column vector) we get

\begin{pmatrix}I_{n-1}&a_{n}t\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\sigma\begin{pmatrix}I_{n-1}&-a_{n}t\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}+t_{1}a^{2}_{n}&*&\dots&*\\
a_{2}+t_{2}a^{2}_{n}&*&\dots&*\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
a_{n-1}+t_{n-1}a^{2}_{n}&*&\dots&*\\
*&*&\dots&*\end{pmatrix}.

Thus after one conjugation we may assume that
(a_{1},\dots,a_{n-1})
is unimodular.
Assuming this, let
d=(d_{1},\dots,d_{n-1})\in A^{n-1}
such that
\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}d_{k}a_{k}-1=0
,
and let

\lambda=\begin{pmatrix}I_{n-1}&0\\
a_{n}d&1\end{pmatrix}
.
Conjugating
[\sigma,\tau]
by

we get

\begin{split}\lambda^{-1}[\sigma,\tau]\lambda&=\lambda^{-1}(\sigma\tau\sigma^{-1}\tau^{-1})\lambda\\
&=\lambda^{-1}(\sigma(I_{n}+pe_{12})\sigma^{-1}\tau^{-1})\lambda\\
&=\lambda^{-1}(I_{n}+p\alpha\beta)\tau^{-1})\lambda\\
&=\lambda^{-1}(\tau^{-1}+p\alpha\beta\tau^{-1})\lambda\\
&=\lambda^{-1}\tau^{-1}\lambda+p\lambda^{-1}\alpha\beta\tau^{-1}\lambda.\end{split}

(11)

Note that by construction,
\lambda^{-1}\alpha

\lambda^{-1}\alpha\beta\tau^{-1}\lambda

is
0
.
Thus using (

is equal to the last row of

\lambda^{-1}\tau\lambda
,
which is
(0,-pa_{n}d_{1},0,\dots,0,1)
.
Denote
\rho=\lambda^{-1}[\sigma,\tau]\lambda
.
We claim that
\rho
is non-central.
Indeed, otherwise
\rho
would be a scalar matrix, and since (at least) one entry of

is

. But then
[\sigma,\tau]=I_{n}
, which contradicts (

Now let
\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}
be the first column and second row of

\rho^{-1}

As before, if

(12)

As in (

[\rho,\tau]=\tau^{-1}+p\alpha^{\prime}\beta^{\prime}\tau^{-1}.

(13)

As before, since

\alpha^{\prime}\beta^{\prime}\tau^{-1}

[\rho,\tau]

.
As before,
[\rho,\tau]
is non-central since one of its diagonal entries equals

.
Thus we see that
\sigma

{\mathfrak{q}}
-operations.
∎

Lemma A.5
.

Any non-central matrix in
G_{1}
may be reduced, using at most
1

{\mathfrak{q}}
-operation
to a non-central matrix of the form
\begin{pmatrix}I_{n-1}&*\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}
.
Any non-central matrix in
G_{2}
may be reduced, using at most
1

{\mathfrak{q}}
-operation
to a non-central matrix of the form
\begin{pmatrix}I_{n-1}&0\\
*&1\end{pmatrix}
.

Proof.

Let
\sigma=\begin{pmatrix}\gamma&v\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}
,
where
\gamma\in{\rm{SL}}_{n-1}(A)
and

. Assume

Lemma A.6
.

Any non-central matrix of the form
\begin{pmatrix}I_{n-1}&*\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}

may be reduced to a non-trivial elementary matrix
using at most
1

{\mathfrak{q}}
-operation.
The same is true for non-central elements of the form

\begin{pmatrix}I_{n-1}&0\\

.

Proof.

Let
\sigma=\begin{pmatrix}I_{n-1}&v\\

, where

\vdots\\
v_{n-1}\end{pmatrix}
.
Assume that
\sigma
is non-central, so that

be any non-zero element of

. We have

Lemma A.7
.

Let
1\leq i\neq j\leq n
. Then any non-trivial elementary matrix may be reduced to a non-trivial element
in

by using at most
3

{\mathfrak{q}}
-operations.

For the proof we use the relations

[I_{n}+ae_{\alpha\beta},I_{n}+be_{\beta\gamma}]=I_{n}+abe_{\alpha\gamma},

are pairwise distinct.
Suppose we are given an element
I_{n}+re_{kl}\in{\rm{E}}_{kl}
where
(i,j)\neq(k,l)
.
We consider the following two cases:

Case 1

i=k
or

.

Case 2

i\neq k
,
j\neq l
.

Suppose first we are in Case 1. If
j=l
, then

are pairwise distinct, and thus
for any

[I_{n}+qe_{ik},I_{n}+re_{kl}]=I_{n}+qre_{il}=I_{n}+qre_{ij},

Suppose we are in Case 2.
If

are pairwise distinct, then for any

and we are in Case 1 again. If

are not pairwise distinct then
necessarily

.

we can find some

Lemma A.8
.

Let
A
be a commutative ring with unit and
{\mathfrak{q}}
be an ideal in

.
Let
\sigma=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\Gamma({\mathfrak{q}})

Suppose there exists a unit

in

, such that
u\equiv 1\mod c^{2}A
.
Then there exists an non-trivial

which can be written as a product of at most

\sigma
and
\sigma^{-1}
by elements of
\Gamma({\mathfrak{q}})
. The same is true if we replace
{\rm{E}}_{12}({\mathfrak{q}})

{\rm{E}}_{21}({\mathfrak{q}})
and require the existence of a unit

.

Proof.

Let
x\in A
such that

and let

.
Let

0&1\end{pmatrix}\sigma\begin{pmatrix}1&-t\\

, and

0&u^{-2}\end{pmatrix}\sigma\begin{pmatrix}u^{-2}&0\\
