Estimates for the Constant Mean Curvature Dirichlet Problem on catenoids
Stephen J. Kleene

Abstract
. In this article, we solve the constant mean curvature dirichlet problem on catenoidal necks with small scale in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} . The solutions are found in exponentially weighted Hölder spaces with non-integer weight and are a-priori bounded by a uniform constant times r 1 + γ r^{1+\gamma} , where r r denotes the distance to the axis of the neck and where γ \gamma belongs to the interval ( 0 , 1 ) (0,1) . By comparing the solutions with their limits on the disk, we improve the estimate to γ = 1 \gamma=1 . As a corollary, we prove differentiability of solutions in τ \tau down to τ = 0 \tau=0 . The surfaces we construct have applications to gluing constructions.

Introduction

A
catenoidal neck
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is the intersection of a catenoid with a tube about its axis of rotation. Up to scalings and rigid motions, catenoidal necks comprise a one parameter family
\mathcal{N}_{\tau}=\tau\mathcal{C}\cap\{r\leq 1\}

\mathcal{N}_{\tau}

\mathcal{N}_{\tau}^{\pm}

\mathcal{N}_{\tau}

\{\pm z\geq 0\}

\mathcal{N}^{\pm}_{\tau}
converges in

\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}^{+}_{\tau}=\mathcal{N}_{\tau}^{+}-\tau\text{arccosh}(1/\tau)e_{z}

In this article, we study the constant mean curvature Dirichlet problem on catenoidal necks of small scale. For
\tau
sufficiently small, the surfaces

are non-degenerate, and thus the CMC dirichlet problem is solvable for small boundary data and small mean curvature. Since the family
\mathcal{N}_{\tau}
is divergent in the moduli space, there is a-priori no uniform estimate on the smallness required for solvability across the family, and in fact there are obstructions arising from lower modes–functions in the span of

\cos(x)

\sin(x)

Since the boundary values of our solutions are not completely determined, in order to uniquely characterise our solutions we demand that the solutions satisfy a set of orthogonality conditions along the waist of the necks, and a function satisfying these conditions is said to be
normalized

Theorem 1.1

There is

\mathcal{N}_{\tau}

1.1.
General comments about notation, conventions, and the proof

1.1.1.
Notation, terminology and other convections

We do not define within this article widely used notation and terminology that is considered standard, such as, for example, Hölder spaces
C^{k,\alpha}(D)
or standard objects associated with euclidean spaces. We use the standard practice that
C
and
\epsilon
denote any number of constants that must be chosen large or small subject to various consideration. Thus, for example the reader should regard an ‘
\epsilon
’, when it is encountered, as the minimum of the previous encountered ‘
\epsilon
’ along with any additional constraints required. Since we are not interested in quantifying the dependence of our solutions on any of these constants, we do not distinguish them. We use the non-standard notation
f\leq^{C}g
to mean
f\leq Cg
, which is convenient in the case that the right hand side contains many terms and would otherwise need to be enclosed in parentheses.

Frequently, the surfaces we consider in this article come with a specified parametrization. When this is case, we do not carefully distinguish between objects defined on the surface and the pullbacks under the parametrization. Whether we are considering a function as defined on a surface or on the parametrization domain will be clear from context.

1.1.2.
General comments of the proof

The central objects we consider are geometric operators such as the mean curvature and unit normal operators
F\mapsto H_{F}
and
F\mapsto N_{F}
defined on immersions
F
, and the resulting remainder terms that arise when perturbing immersions by graphs. Rather than write these terms explicitly, we appeal abstractly to the fact that these operators are smooth–in the sense of Frechet differentiability–as mappings on spaces of class
C^{2,\alpha}
Hölder immersions, and are thus locally bounded in
C^{k}
in their domains. Standard integral formulae then apply for taylor remainders and most of the required estimates follow easily. A few of the more delicate estimates require deeper analysis, and we pause here to observe them so they are not a surprise to the reader later. Firstly in places–such as the proof of Lemma
9.5
–we will need the fact that the hessian of the graph mean curvature operator vanishes at affine maps. This allows for improved decay estimates for various terms since the standard conformal parametrization of the catenoid is exponentially flat.

1.1.3.
Arguments requiring mode preservation

The most delicate estimates in the paper arise in the proof of Propositions
8.1
and
9.5
. They require a separation of the error term into the lower and higher parts, with the higher part satisfying improved bounds relative to the whole terms. In both these cases, the rotational symmetry of the catenoid is exploited in the following way: Let
F
be an immersion of a domain
D=\mathbb{S}^{1}\times I
with coordinates
(x,s)
into
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
u
a function on
D
. Then the mean curvature
H_{F}(u)
of the normal graph over
F
by
u
can be computed as:

H_{F}(u)=\Phi(\mathcal{J}_{u}(x,s),x,s)

where
\mathcal{J}_{u}=\left(u,\nabla u,\nabla^{2}u\right)
is the vector of partial derivatives of
u
of up to order
2
, and where
\Phi
is a smooth function defined on an open subset of a euclidean space about the origin. Suppose now that
F
is an immersion of rotation, so in cylindrical coordinates, we have:

F(x,s)=r(s)e_{r}(x)+h(z)e_{z},

where above we have set
e_{r}(x)=\cos(x)e_{x}+\sin(x)e_{x}
. Then the symmetry of the parametrization
F
implies that the function
\Phi
does not depend on
x
, so

\Phi(\mathcal{J},x,s)=\Phi(\mathcal{J},s).

We then have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}e^{-ikx}\left.DH_{F}\right|_{0}(u)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}e^{ikx}\left.D\Phi\right|_{\left(0,s\right)}\left(\mathcal{J}_{u},s\right)

\displaystyle\left.D\Phi\right|_{\left(0,s\right)}\left(\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}e^{ikx}\mathcal{J}_{u},s\right)

This allows for bounds to be estimated for modes separately. This observation generalizes the following way, which is needed in the proof of Proposition
9.5
. Assume now that
\xi=\xi(s)
is a rotationally symmetric function on
D
. Then,
\mathcal{J}_{\xi}
is a function only of
s
and we have

\displaystyle\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{ikx}\left.D^{2}H\right|_{u}\left(\xi,u\right)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{ikx}\left.D^{2}\Phi\right|_{(0,s)}\left(\mathcal{J}_{\xi},e^{ikx}\mathcal{J}_{u}\right)dx

\displaystyle=\left.D^{2}\Phi\right|_{(0,s)}\left(\mathcal{J}_{\xi},\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{ikx}\mathcal{J}_{u}dx\right)

We refer this property of the operators
\left.DH_{F}\right|_{0}(0)
and
\left.D^{2}H_{F}\right|_{0}(\xi,-)
as
mode preserving
. The mode preserving property is critical for our required estimates.

1.1.4.
Other comments

In order to keep this article as self-contained as possible, we have included here the proofs of several results that are by this point fairly standard in singular perturbation literature. In particular, our treatment of the mapping properties of the jacobi operator on the catenoid in exponentially weighted Hölder spaces closely follows similar proofs found in
[
12
]
. The only external machinery we rely upon is what is frequently referred to as ‘standard theory’, specifically, the existence and regularity theory for second order elliptic operators on bounded domains (c.f.
[
5
]
), the Implicit Function Theorem, and some basic properties of differentiability in Banach spaces. Specifically, our argument relies on the fact a mapping which is continuously Gateaux differentiable on a domain is Frechet differentiable (see, for example
[
1
]
Proposition A.3).

Finally, we remark that we are considering Dirichlet-Poission problems on the catenoid in which no symmetry is presumed. In presence of enough symmetries–for example, if we require invariance under reflections through the
x
and
y
axis, then the challenging estimates required in our proof become trivial and go through automatically. This is because such symmetry requirements kill the problematic lower modes, and the remaining ones necessarily satisfy improved decay rates.

2.
Preliminaries and Background

2.1.
Geometric objects as smooth mappings

The basic objects we consider in this article are spaces of class
C^{k,\alpha}
immersions into
\mathbb{R}^{3}
as well as geometric quantities associated to oriented immersions such as the unit normal and mean curvature. These geometric quantities can be regarded as smooth mappings in the sense of Frechet differentiability on space of class
C^{k,\alpha}
immersions as follows: Given a smooth compact domain
D
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, let
R^{k,\alpha}(D)\subset C^{k,\alpha}(D,\mathbb{R}^{3})
denote the space of class
C^{k,\alpha}
immersions of
D
into
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. We require immersions to extend as immersions to an open set containing
D
, so that
R^{k,\alpha}(D)
is an open subset of
C^{k,\alpha}(D,\mathbb{R}^{3})
. The mean curvature mapping
H:F\mapsto H_{F}
, taking an immersion
F
to its mean curvature
H_{F}
is then smooth as a map from the space
R^{k+2,\alpha}(D,\mathbb{R}^{3})
into
C^{k,\alpha}(D)
. Similarly, the unit normal mapping
N:F\mapsto N_{F}
taking an immersion to its unit normal is smooth as a mapping from
R^{k+1,\alpha}(D)
into
C^{k,\alpha}(D,\mathbb{R}^{3})
. We have omitted the proof of these facts, but they are easy to establish by, for example, computing the mean curvature as a smooth function of components of the derivative of an immersion, so

H_{F}=\overline{H}(\nabla F,\nabla^{2}F)

where
\overline{H}=\overline{H}(q_{i},r_{i,j})
is defined and smooth on the open subset of the euclidean space
\left(\mathbb{R}^{3}\right)^{\times 2}\times\left(\mathbb{R}^{3}\right)^{\times 4}
with
\det(q_{i}\cdot q_{j})\neq 0
.
Smoothness in this sense then works as it does in the standard euclidean setting. In particular, the usual integral expressions for Taylor remainders holds on locally convex domains in
R^{k,\alpha}(D)
.

We let
H_{F}
and
N_{F}
denote the mean curvature of a
C^{2}
immersion of
F:D\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
, which we regard as functions on
D
. Given a function
u:D\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, the normal graph over
F
by
u
is the mapping

F_{u}:=F+uN_{F}

Provided
F_{u}
is a
C^{2}
immersion again, we let
H_{F}(u)
denote its mean curvature. When
D
is compact,
F_{u}
is an immersion whenever
u
is sufficiently small in
C^{2}
and we refer to the mapping
u\mapsto H_{F}(u)
as the mean curvature operator at
F
. The variation of the mean curvature of
F
induced by a vector field
V
is given by

(2.1)

\displaystyle\left.DH\right|_{F}(V):=\nabla_{V^{\top}}H+L_{F}V^{\perp}

wheret
V^{\perp}=V\cdot N_{F}
denotes the normal part of
V
and
V^{\top}=V-V^{\perp}N_{F}
the tangential part, and where
L_{F}
is the stability, or jacobi, operator of
F
and is given by

L_{F}=\Delta_{F}+\left|A_{F}\right|^{2}.

where above
\Delta_{F}
denotes the Laplace operator of the immersion
F
and
\left|A_{F}\right|^{2}
the square length of the second fundamental form of
F
.

2.2.
The Poisson-Dirichlet Problem for the mean curvature operator

If
\mathcal{Q}
is a second order elliptic operator, a solution to the
(E,f)
-Poisson-Dirichlet problem for and operator
\mathcal{Q}
over the domain
D
, where
E
is a function on
D
and
f
is a function on
\partial D
, is a function
u
on
D
satisfying

\mathcal{Q}_{F}(u)=E,\quad\mathcal{tr}(u)=f

where above
\mathcal{tr}(u)
denotes the trace of
u
. We refer to
E
as the Poission data and to
f
as the Dirichlet data of the problem. We assume that
E
is class
C^{0,\alpha}
and
f
is class
C^{2,\alpha}
. Standard elliptic regularity theory then implies that any solution
u
is class
C^{2,\alpha}
. The solvability of this equation for small
E
and
f
is determined studying the Poisson Dirichlet problem for the linearization
\dot{\mathcal{Q}}
of
\mathcal{Q}
at
u=0
:

\dot{\mathcal{Q}}_{F}(u)=E,\quad\mathcal{tr}(u)=f

We take
\mathcal{Q}=H_{F}
for some fixed immersion

, so that
\dot{\mathcal{Q}}=L_{F}
.
L_{F}
is a self-adjoint elliptic operator and the linearized Possion-Dirichlet problem for

is solvable if and only if the immersion

is non-degenerate, meaning that the kernel of

is trivial.

2.3.
Conformal parametrization of the catenoid

The standard catenoid
\mathcal{C}
is conformally parametrized by the mapping
\mathcal{F}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
, where
\Omega:=\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{R}
is the cylinder and where

(2.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(x,s):=\cosh(s)e_{r}(x)+se_{z},\quad e_{r}(x)=\cos(x)e_{x}+\sin(x)e_{y}.

The first and second fundamental forms of
\mathcal{F}
are given by

\mathcal{g}_{\mathcal{F}}:=\begin{pmatrix}\cosh^{2}(s)&0\\
\\
0&\cosh^{2}(s)\end{pmatrix},\quad A_{\mathcal{F}}:=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}.

The unit normal is

(2.3)

\displaystyle N_{\mathcal{F}}=\cosh^{-1}(s)e_{r}(x)-\tanh(s)e_{z}.

and the stability operator is

L_{\mathcal{F}}=\cosh^{-2}(s)\Delta_{\Omega}+2\cosh^{-4}(s).

2.4.
Catenoidal necks

For each
\tau>0
, let
\mathcal{G}_{\tau}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
denote the mapping given by

\mathcal{G}_{\tau}:=\tau\mathcal{F}.

The neck
\mathcal{N}_{\tau}
is parametrized by the restriction of
\mathcal{G}_{\tau}
to the domain

\mathcal{N}_{\tau}^{*}:=\Omega_{l_{\tau}}:=\mathbb{S}^{1}\times[-l_{\tau},l_{\tau}],\quad l_{\tau}=\text{Arccosh}\left(\frac{1}{\tau}\right).

Given constants
\tau_{0}
and
\tau
, we define a family of diffeomorphisms
\phi_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}:\mathcal{N}_{\tau_{0}}\rightarrow\mathcal{N}_{\tau}
as follows: First, observe that there is a scalar
\lambda
such that
\lambda\mathcal{N}_{\tau}
is a normal graph over
\mathcal{N}_{\tau_{0}}
. The scalar
\lambda
can be determined as follows: The boundary of the surface
\mathcal{N}_{\tau}
in cylindrical coordinates is given by

(r,z)=\left(1,\tau l\right)

and outward conormal is
\left(\sqrt{1-\tau^{2}},\tau\right)
. We choose the scalar
\lambda
so that the difference vector between the boundaries is orthogonal to the outward conormal, which by rotational symmetry implies that it is a multiple of the unit normal. This means that

\left\{\lambda(1,\tau l)-(1,\tau_{0}l_{0})\right\}\cdot\left(\sqrt{1-\tau^{2}},\tau\right)=0.

Solving for
\lambda
gives

\lambda=\lambda_{\tau}=\frac{\tau^{2}_{0}l_{0}+\sqrt{1-\tau_{0}^{2}}}{\sqrt{1-\tau_{0}^{2}}+\tau_{0}\tau l}

Observe that
\lambda=1
when
\tau=\tau_{0}
and

\dot{\lambda}:=\left.\frac{d}{d\tau}\right|_{\tau=\tau_{0}}\lambda\rightarrow 0

as
\tau_{0}\rightarrow 0
. The diffeomorphisms
\phi_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}:\mathcal{N}_{\tau_{0}}\rightarrow\mathcal{N}_{\tau}
induce diffeomorphims on the domains
\phi_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}:\mathcal{N}^{*}_{\tau_{0}}\rightarrow\mathcal{N}^{*}_{\tau}
, which we still denote by
\phi_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}
, which satisfy

\lambda\mathcal{G}_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}-\mathcal{G}_{\tau_{0}}=fN_{\mathcal{G}_{\tau}}

for a function
f
, where above we have set
\mathcal{G}_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}=\mathcal{G}_{\tau}\circ\phi_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}
. Differentiating in
\tau
at
\tau=\tau_{0}
and using that
\phi_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}
is a function of
s
only gives:

(2.4)

\displaystyle\left(\dot{\lambda}\tau+1\right)\mathcal{F}-\frac{\partial\mathcal{G}_{\tau}}{\partial s}\dot{\phi}=\dot{f}N_{\mathcal{G}_{\tau}}.

Thus, the normal variation field generated by the family of immersion
\mathcal{G}_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}
is

\left(\dot{\mathcal{G}}_{\tau_{0}}\right)^{\perp}=\left(\tau\dot{\lambda}+1\right)\mathcal{F}^{\perp}=\left(\tau\dot{\lambda}+1\right)\xi,

where above
\xi=s\tanh(s)-1
. Here, we have used that
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{G}_{\tau}}=\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{F}}
and (
2.2
) and (
2.3
). Taking the dot product of (
2.4
) with
\frac{\partial\mathcal{G}_{\tau_{0}}}{\partial s}
gives

(2.5)

\displaystyle\dot{\phi}_{\tau_{0}}=\left(\tau\dot{\lambda}+1\right)\frac{\mathcal{F}\cdot\frac{\partial\mathcal{G}_{\tau_{0}}}{\partial s}}{\left|\frac{\partial\mathcal{G}_{\tau_{0}}}{\partial s}\right|^{2}}=\left(\tau\dot{\lambda}+1\right)\frac{\tanh(s)+s/\cosh^{2}(s)}{\tau_{0}}.

The total variation field is

(2.6)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{G}}_{\tau_{0}}=\left(\tau\dot{\lambda}+1\right)\xi N_{\mathcal{F}}-\dot{\lambda}\tau\mathcal{F}.

2.5.
The modified Dirichlet-Poisson Problem on catenoidal necks

As mentioned above, there are obstructions to solving the Poission-Dirichlet problem for
H
on catenoidal necks with uniform estimates for small
\tau
. We instead impose the additional condition that the solutions are
normalized
–defined below–and in exchange we give up control on the lower modes of the boundary values. A lower mode on the circle is a function in the span of
1
,
\cos(\theta)
and
\sin(\theta)
. If
f
is a function on the circle
\mathbb{S}^{1}
, we let
\overline{f}
denote the projection of
f
onto the space of lower modes and
\mathring{f}
denote the projection of
f
into the orthogonal compliment. We refer to
\overline{f}
as the lower part of
f
and to
\overline{f}
as the higher part. More generally, if
E
is a function on a cylindrical domain
\Omega_{a,b}
for some
a,b
, we define the lower part of
E
to be the function
\overline{E}(x,s)
, where for each
s

\overline{E}(x,s)
is the lower part of the function
x\mapsto E(x,s)
. The higher part of
E
is then

\mathring{E}:=E-\overline{E}

and we refer to
\mathring{E}
as the
higher part
of
E
.

A solution to the
modified

(E,f)
-Poisson Dirichlet problem on
\Omega_{l}
for
H_{F}
is then a normalized function

satisfying

H_{F}(u)=E,\quad\mathring{\mathcal{tr}}(u)=\mathring{f}.

2.6.

The space of lower modes on

\overline{\left(\left.\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial u}\right|_{\left(\left.\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}\right|_{s_{0}},\left.\widetilde{\overline{\mathcal{G}}}_{\tau}\right|_{s_{0}},0\right)}\right)\left(\mathcal{h}^{*}\right)}=\left(\left.\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial u}\right|_{\left(\left.\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}\right|_{s_{0}},\left.\widetilde{\overline{\mathcal{G}}}_{\tau}\right|_{s_{0}},0\right)}\right)\left(\overline{\mathcal{h}^{*}}\right).

This then gives:

\left\|\overline{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}\right\|^{0,\alpha}\leq^{C}\left(\cosh^{-1}(s_{0})\right)\left(\|\overline{\mathcal{h}^{*}}\|^{2,\alpha}\right)\leq^{C}\tau\omega.

Thus we can split
\mathcal{E}
into the sum of the higher and lower parts
\mathcal{E}=\overline{\mathcal{E}}+\mathring{\mathcal{E}}
where

\left\|\mathring{\mathcal{E}}\right\|^{0,\alpha,0}_{1}\leq^{C}\tau,\quad\left\|\overline{\mathcal{E}}\right\|^{0,\alpha,1}_{\tau}\leq^{C}\tau

Observe that

\displaystyle H^{\prime}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{h}^{\prime})-H^{\prime}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{h}^{*})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\left.L^{\prime}_{\mathcal{F}}\right|_{\mathcal{h}(t)}\left(\mathcal{h}^{\prime}-\mathcal{h}^{*}\right)dt

\displaystyle=:\mathcal{L}\left(\mathcal{h}^{\prime}-\mathcal{h}^{*}\right)

where above we have set
\mathcal{h}(t)=(1-t)\mathcal{h}^{\prime}+t\mathcal{h}^{*}
and where the operator
\mathcal{L}
is defined implicitly above. Combining with (
8.7
) we have

\mathcal{L}\left(\mathcal{h}^{\prime}-\mathcal{h}^{*}\right)=\mathcal{E}.

By Proposition
5.4
, there is a unique function
u
such that

L_{F}^{\prime}(u)=\mathring{\mathcal{E}},\quad\mathcal{tr}(u)=0

and satisfying the estimate
\left\|u\right\|^{2,\alpha,0}_{1}\leq^{C}\epsilon\tau
.
Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\left(\mathcal{h}^{\prime}-\mathcal{h}^{*}-u\right)

\displaystyle=\overline{\mathcal{E}}+\left(\mathcal{L}-L^{\prime}_{\mathcal{F}}\right)(u).

We then have

\displaystyle\left\|\left.\left(\mathcal{L}-L^{\prime}_{\mathcal{F}}\right)(u)\right|_{s_{0}}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq^{C}\sup\left\|\left.\mathcal{h}(t)\right|_{s_{0}}\right\|\left\|\left.u\right|_{s_{0}}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq^{C}\epsilon\tau\omega^{\gamma}.

and thus

\left\|\mathcal{L}\left(\mathcal{h}^{\prime}-\mathcal{h}^{*}-u\right)\right\|^{0,\alpha,\gamma}_{\tau}\leq^{C}\epsilon\tau.

The operator
\mathcal{L}
is a easily seen to be perturbation of
L_{F}^{\prime}
in the weighted spaces
W^{k,\alpha,\gamma}_{\tau}
, and thus by Proposition
5.3
, there is a unique

such that

and such that
\left\|v\right\|^{2,\alpha,\gamma}_{\tau}\leq^{C}\tau
.
In particular, this gives the local estimate

(8.9)

\displaystyle\left\|\left.\left(\mathcal{h}^{\prime}-\mathcal{h}^{*}\right)\right|_{s_{0}}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq^{C\epsilon}\tau+\tau\omega_{0}^{\gamma}

\displaystyle\leq^{C\epsilon}\tau

\displaystyle\left\|\left.\mathcal{h}^{\prime}\right|_{s_{0}}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\left.\mathcal{h}^{*}\right|_{s_{0}}\right\|+\left\|\left.\mathcal{h}^{*}\right|_{s_{0}}-\left.\mathcal{h}^{\prime}\right|_{s_{0}}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq^{C\epsilon}\omega_{0}+\tau

\displaystyle\leq^{C\epsilon}\omega_{0}.

Taking the supremum over

gives

\|\mathcal{h}^{{}^{\prime}}\|^{2,\alpha,\gamma}_{1}\leq^{C}\epsilon.

Finally, the second estimate follow from the fact that
\overline{u}=0
and thus

(8.10)

\displaystyle\left\|\overline{\mathcal{h}^{\prime}}-\overline{\mathcal{h}^{*}}\right\|^{2,\alpha,\gamma}_{\tau}=\left\|v\right\|^{2,\alpha,\gamma}_{\tau}\leq^{C}\tau.

9.
C^{1}
-extension of the Dirichlet Solutions to scale zero

In this section, we prove that the
\tau
-derivative of the solutions
\mathcal{h}_{\tau,\delta,f}
extend continuously to
\tau=0
. Recall that
\mathcal{h}_{\tau,\delta,f}
solves the modified
(\delta,f)
-Poisson Dirichlet problem for
\mathcal{G}_{\tau}
on the domain
\mathcal{N}_{\tau}^{*}=\Omega_{l_{\tau}}
, so:

H_{\mathcal{G}_{\tau}}\left(\mathcal{h}_{\tau,\delta,f}\right)=\delta,\quad\mathring{\mathcal{tr}}(\mathcal{h}_{\tau,\delta,f})=f.

Proposition 9.1
.

Let
\dot{\mathcal{h}}_{\tau_{0},\delta,f}^{\pm}
denote the restrictions of
\dot{\mathcal{h}}_{\tau_{0},\delta,f}
to
\mathcal{N}^{\pm}_{\tau_{0}}
.
Then the functions
\dot{\mathcal{h}}^{\pm}=\dot{\mathcal{h}}^{\pm}_{\tau_{0}}
converge in
C^{2,\alpha}
away from the origin to
\dot{\mathcal{h}}^{+}_{\delta,f}

The proof of Proposition
9.1
is long and we thus split it apart into the following steps. First in Lemma
9.2
, we show that the Gateaux derivative
\dot{\mathcal{h}^{\pm}_{\delta,f}}
at
\tau=0
is well-defined in
C^{2,\alpha}
and solves a modified Poission-Dirichlet problem on the disk with vanishing Dirichlet data. Next, in Lemma
9.4
, we show that for positive
\tau
, the derivatives
\dot{\mathcal{h}}_{\tau,\delta,f}
solves a modified Poission-Dirichlet problem on
\mathcal{N}_{\tau}
with vanishing Dirichlet data. The Poisson data of problem splits into two parts
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}
, both bounded in different weighted holder spaces. The first,
\mathcal{E}_{1}
, is large but decaying, and we can apply the invertibility result Proposition
5.4
to obtain a normalized solution to the Poisson problem for the higher part
\mathring{\mathcal{E}}_{1}
of
\mathcal{E}_{1}
. The term
\mathcal{E}_{2}
is small but growing and can be solve for using Proposition
5.3
. Solving for the higher part
\overline{\mathcal{E}}_{1}
of
\mathcal{E}_{1}
can then be done using separation of variables. This then allows us to show, in Lemma
9.6
, that the functions
\dot{\mathcal{h}}_{\tau,\delta,f}
are uniformly bounded in
C^{2,\alpha}
away from the origin and thus the restrictions
\dot{\mathcal{h}}^{\pm}_{\tau,\delta,f}
converge to a limit on the disk. The limit then satisfies the same Poisson problem as
\dot{\mathcal{h}}^{\pm}_{\delta,f}
, with boundary values agreeing up to lower modes. In order to show that the two functions agree, we need to know that that the limit is normalized. To do this, in Lemma
9.5
we show that higher part
\overline{\mathcal{E}}_{1}
satisfies an improved bound relative to the whole term.

Lemma 9.2
.

The function
\dot{\mathcal{h}}^{\pm}_{\delta,f}
is well-defined away from the origin on
\mathcal{D}
and satisfies

\left.DH_{\mathcal{D}}\right|_{\mathcal{h}^{+}_{\delta,f}}\left(\dot{\mathcal{h}}^{\pm}_{\delta,f}\right)=\pm\left.DH\right|_{\mathcal{N}^{+}_{\delta,f}}\left(\log(r)e_{z}-\mathcal{h}_{\delta,f}^{\pm}\frac{e_{r}}{r}\right),\quad\mathring{\mathcal{tr}}\left(\dot{\mathcal{h}}^{\pm}_{\delta,f}\right)=0

and the estimate

\sup\left|\dot{\mathcal{h}}^{\pm}_{\delta,f}(r,\theta)\right|\leq^{C\epsilon}r^{1+\gamma}

Remark 9.3
.

It is not immediately obvious that the terms on the right hand side of the equation satisfied by
\dot{\mathcal{h}}^{\pm}_{\delta,f}
are uniformly bounded over the whole disk. It is possible to compute directly that it is, bounded, using the uniform bound
|\mathcal{h}|\leq^{c}r^{2}
.

Proof.

Fix
\delta>0
. Then for
\tau<\delta
,
\mathcal{N}^{+}_{\tau}\cap K
is a normal graph over the domain
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}:=\mathcal{D}\setminus B_{\delta}(0)
. and thus the mapping
\phi_{0}^{\tau,+}:\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathcal{N}^{+}_{\tau}
is defined. Recalling the notation used in the proof of Proposition
8.1
we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{h}^{\prime}

\displaystyle:=\frac{\mathcal{h}_{0,\delta,f}^{\tau}\circ\overline{\mathcal{G}}_{\tau}}{\omega}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{h}_{\tau,\delta,f}\circ\mathcal{G}_{\tau}}{\omega}

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{h}^{*}:=\frac{\mathcal{h}_{\delta,f}\circ\overline{\mathcal{G}}_{\tau}}{\omega}.

Recalling the estimate (
8.9
) we have

\left\|\left(\left.\mathcal{h}^{\prime}-\mathcal{h}^{*}\right)\right|_{s_{0}}\right\|^{2,\alpha}\leq^{C\epsilon}\tau

Thus, as functions on
\mathcal{D}
we have the uniform bound

\left|\left.\left(\mathcal{h}^{\tau,+}_{0,\delta,f}-\mathcal{h}^{+}_{\delta,f}\right)\right|_{(r,\theta)}\right|\leq^{C\epsilon}\tau

as well as local uniform bounds in
C^{2,\alpha}
. Thus, the difference quotients
\frac{\mathcal{h}^{\tau,+}_{0,\delta,f}-\mathcal{h}_{\delta,f+}}{\tau}
and converge locally in
C^{2,\alpha/2}
as
\tau\rightarrow 0
to a limiting function
\dot{\mathcal{h}}^{+}_{\delta,f}
satisfying the estimate:

\left\|\dot{\mathcal{h}}^{+}_{\delta,f}\right\|\leq^{C\epsilon}r^{1+\gamma}

Moreover since
\mathcal{N}_{\tau}^{+}
is a smooth family with variation field
\dot{\mathcal{N}}^{+}
at
\tau=0
given by
-\log(r)e_{z}
, the variation in the unit normal at
\tau=0
is
\dot{N}_{\mathcal{N}}=\frac{1}{r}e_{r}
. We then have

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{H(\mathcal{N}^{+}_{\tau,\delta,f})-H(\mathcal{D}^{+}_{\delta,f})}{\tau}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\left.DH\right|_{\mathcal{N}(t)}\left(\frac{\mathcal{N}^{+}_{\tau,\delta,f}-\mathcal{D}^{+}_{\delta,f}}{\tau}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\left.DH\right|_{\mathcal{N}(t)}\left(\frac{\mathcal{N}_{\tau}-\mathcal{D}}{\tau}+\left(\frac{\mathcal{h}^{+}_{\tau,\delta,f}-\mathcal{h}^{+}_{\delta,f}}{\tau}\right)e_{z}+\mathcal{h}^{+}_{\tau,\delta,f}\frac{N_{\mathcal{N}_{\tau}}-e_{z}}{\tau}\right)

where we have set
\mathcal{N}(t)=(1-t)\mathcal{D}_{\delta,f}+t\mathcal{N}_{\tau,\delta,f}
.
As
\tau=0
we have
\frac{\mathcal{N}_{\tau}-\mathcal{D}}{\tau}\rightarrow-\log(r)e_{z}
,
\left(\frac{\mathcal{h}^{+}_{\tau,\delta,f}-\mathcal{h}^{+}_{\delta,f}}{\tau}\right)\rightarrow\dot{\mathcal{h}}^{+}_{\delta,f}
and
\frac{N_{\mathcal{N}_{\tau}}-e_{z}}{\tau}\rightarrow\frac{1}{r}e_{r}
, and the operator
\int_{0}^{1}\left.DH\right|_{\mathcal{N}}(t)dt
converges to
DH_{\mathcal{D}}(-)
. Thus, in the limit as
\tau=0

\left.DH_{\mathcal{D}}\right|_{\mathcal{h}^{+}_{\delta,f}}(\dot{\mathcal{h}}^{+}_{\delta,f})+\left.DH_{\mathcal{D}}\right|_{\mathcal{h}^{+}_{\delta,f}}\left(-\log(r)e_{z}+\mathcal{h}^{+}_{\delta,f}\frac{1}{r}e_{r}\right)=0,\quad\mathring{\mathcal{tr}}(\dot{\mathcal{h}}^{+}_{\delta,f})=0.

Finally, we observe that by (
8.10

\dot{\mathcal{h}}^{+}_{\delta,f}
satisfies the improved bound

\left|\overline{\dot{\mathcal{h}}^{+}_{\delta,f}}\right|\leq^{C\epsilon}r^{1+\gamma}

The function
\dot{\mathcal{h}}^{+}_{\delta,f}
then extends to the disk in

and the higher part satisfies
\mathring{\mathcal{h}}(0)=\nabla\mathring{\mathcal{h}}(0)=0
and thus

\left|\mathring{\mathcal{h}}(r,\theta)\right|\leq^{C\epsilon}r^{2}.

It then follows that:

\left|\dot{\mathcal{h}}^{+}_{\delta,f}\right|\leq^{C\epsilon}r^{1+\gamma}.

This completes the proof.
∎
