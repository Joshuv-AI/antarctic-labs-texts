Performance Analysis of Quantum CSS Error-Correcting Codes via MacWilliams Identities
Diego Forlivesi, Lorenzo Valentini, Marco Chiani

Abstract
Quantum error correcting codes are of primary interest for the evolution towards quantum computing and quantum Internet. We analyze the performance of stabilizer codes, one of the most important classes for practical implementations, on both symmetric and asymmetric quantum channels. To this aim, we first derive the WE ( WE ) for the undetectable errors based on the quantum MacWilliams identities. The WE is then used to evaluate tight upper bounds on the error rate of CSS quantum codes with MW decoding. For surface codes we also derive a simple closed form expression of the bounds over the depolarizing channel. Finally, we introduce a novel approach that combines the knowledge of WE with a logical operator analysis. This method allows the derivation of the exact asymptotic performance for short codes. For example, on a depolarizing channel with physical error rate ρ → 0 \rho\to 0 it is found that the logical error rate ρ L \rho_{\mathrm{L}} is asymptotically ρ L ≈ 16 ​ ρ 2 \rho_{\mathrm{L}}\approx 16\rho^{2} for the [ [ 9 , 1 , 3 ] ] [[9,1,3]] Shor code, ρ L ≈ 16.3 ​ ρ 2 \rho_{\mathrm{L}}\approx 16.3\rho^{2} for the [ [ 7 , 1 , 3 ] ] [[7,1,3]] Steane code, ρ L ≈ 18.7 ​ ρ 2 \rho_{\mathrm{L}}\approx 18.7\rho^{2} for the [ [ 13 , 1 , 3 ] ] [[13,1,3]] surface code, and ρ L ≈ 149.3 ​ ρ 3 \rho_{\mathrm{L}}\approx 149.3\rho^{3} for the [ [ 41 , 1 , 5 ] ] [[41,1,5]] surface code. For larger codes our bound provides ρ L ≈ 1215 ​ ρ 4 \rho_{\mathrm{L}}\approx 1215\rho^{4} and ρ L ≈ 663 ​ ρ 5 \rho_{\mathrm{L}}\approx 663\rho^{5} for the [ [ 85 , 1 , 7 ] ] [[85,1,7]] and the [ [ 181 , 1 , 10 ] ] [[181,1,10]] surface codes, respectively.

Preliminaries and Background

2.1
Quantum Stabilizer Codes

A qubit is an element of the two-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}^{2}
, with basis
\ket{0}
and
\ket{1}

[
12
]
.
The Pauli operators
\bm{I},\bm{X},\bm{Z}
, and
\bm{Y}
, are defined by
\bm{I}\ket{a}=\ket{a}
,
\bm{X}\ket{a}=\ket{a\oplus 1}
,
\bm{Z}\ket{a}=(-1)^{a}\ket{a}
, and
\bm{Y}\ket{a}=i(-1)^{a}\ket{a\oplus 1}
for
a\in\{0,1\}
. These operators either commute (e.g.
\bm{I}\bm{X}=\bm{X}\bm{I}
) or anticommute (e.g.
\bm{X}\bm{Z}=-\bm{Z}\bm{X}
) with each other. Also, apart from an overall factor
\pm 1,\pm i
, the composition of two Pauli produces another Pauli (e.g.
\bm{X}\bm{Y}=i\bm{Z}
). Thus, all the Pauli operators, together with multiplicative factors
\pm 1,\pm i
constitute a group, indicated as
\mathcal{G}_{1}
. Similarly, all Pauli operators on
n
qubits together with multiplicative factors
\pm 1,\pm i
form the
\mathcal{G}_{n}
Pauli group
[
15
,
12
]
.
We indicate with
[[n,k,d]]
a
QECC
with minimum distance
d
, that encodes
k
information qubits
\ket{\varphi}
(called logical qubits) into a codeword of
n
qubits
\ket{\psi}
(called data or physical qubits), allowing the decoder to correct all patterns up to
t=\lfloor(d-1)/2\rfloor
errors (and some patterns of more errors). To simplify our analysis, we consistently adopt the assumption that
d
is an odd number. The codewords will be assumed equiprobable in the following.
Using the stabilizer formalism, we start by choosing
n-k
independent and commuting operators
\bm{G}_{i}\in\mathcal{G}_{n}
, called stabilizer generators (or simply generators).
The subgroup of
\mathcal{G}_{n}
generated by all combinations of the
\bm{G}_{i}\in\mathcal{G}_{n}
is a stabilizer, indicated as
\mathcal{S}
. The code
\mathcal{C}
is the set of quantum states
\ket{\psi}
stabilized by
\mathcal{S}
, i.e., satisfying

\bm{S}\ket{\psi}=\ket{\psi}\,\forall\bm{S}\in\mathcal{S}
, or, equivalently,

\bm{G}_{i}\ket{\psi}=\ket{\psi},\,i=1,2,\ldots,n-k
.
For a subgroup
\mathcal{H}
of a group
\mathcal{G}
we indicate with

\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{H})
the normalizer, and with
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{H})
the centralizer.
The centralizer and the normalizer of the stabilizer
\mathcal{S}
are coincident,
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{S})=C(\mathcal{S})
.

Assume a codeword
\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{C}
is affected by a channel error.
Measuring the received state according to the generators
\bm{G}_{i}
with the aid of ancilla qubits, the error collapses on a discrete set of possibilities represented by the Pauli operators
\bm{E}\in\mathcal{G}_{n}

[
15
]
. We call this
\bm{E}
a Pauli error. The weight of an error
\bm{E}\in\mathcal{G}_{n}
is the number of single qubits Pauli operators which are not equal to the identity.
For example, the error
\bm{E}=\bm{X}_{{2}}\bm{Y}_{{3}}
has weight two, with
\bm{X}
occurred on the second qubit and
\bm{Y}
occurred on the third qubit (we implicitly mean that the others qubits see the Pauli identity operator).
The measurement procedure over the ancilla qubits results in a quantum error syndrome
\bm{s}(\bm{E})=(s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{n-k})
, with each
s_{i}=0
or
1
depending on
\bm{E}
commuting or anticommuting with
\bm{G}_{i}
, respectively
[
15
]
. In the following, we will refer to ancillas measuring
s_{i}=1
as
defects
.
Note that an error
\bm{E}\in\mathcal{S}
has no effect on a codeword since in this case
\bm{E}\ket{\psi}=\ket{\psi}
. A minimum weight decoder will infer the most probable error
\bm{\hat{E}}\in\mathcal{G}_{n}
compatible with the measured syndrome.
Within the category of stabilizer codes, among the earliest introduced quantum codes were the
[[9,1,3]]
Shor code
[
7
]
, with generators

\begin{array}[]{lllllll}\bm{G}_{1}=\mathbf{Z}_{1}\mathbf{Z}_{2}&\bm{G}_{2}=\mathbf{Z}_{2}\mathbf{Z}_{3}&\\
\bm{G}_{3}=\mathbf{Z}_{4}\mathbf{Z}_{5}&\bm{G}_{4}=\mathbf{Z}_{5}\mathbf{Z}_{6}&\\
\bm{G}_{5}=\mathbf{Z}_{7}\mathbf{Z}_{8}&\bm{G}_{6}=\mathbf{Z}_{8}\mathbf{Z}_{9}&\\
\bm{G}_{7}=\mathbf{X}_{1}\mathbf{X}_{2}\mathbf{X}_{3}\mathbf{X}_{4}\mathbf{X}_{5}\mathbf{X}_{6}&\\
\bm{G}_{8}=\mathbf{X}_{4}\mathbf{X}_{5}\mathbf{X}_{6}\mathbf{X}_{7}\mathbf{X}_{8}\mathbf{X}_{9},&\end{array}

the
[[7,1,3]]
Steane code
[
52
]
, with generators

\begin{array}[]{lllllll}\bm{G}_{1}=\mathbf{X}_{1}\mathbf{X}_{3}\mathbf{X}_{5}\mathbf{X}_{7}&\bm{G}_{2}=\mathbf{X}_{2}\mathbf{X}_{3}\mathbf{X}_{6}\mathbf{X}_{7}&\\
\bm{G}_{3}=\mathbf{X}_{4}\mathbf{X}_{5}\mathbf{X}_{6}\mathbf{X}_{7}&\bm{G}_{4}=\mathbf{Z}_{1}\mathbf{Z}_{3}\mathbf{Z}_{5}\mathbf{Z}_{7}&\\
\bm{G}_{5}=\mathbf{Z}_{2}\mathbf{Z}_{3}\mathbf{Z}_{6}\mathbf{Z}_{7}&\bm{G}_{6}=\mathbf{Z}_{4}\mathbf{Z}_{5}\mathbf{Z}_{6}\mathbf{Z}_{7},&\\
\end{array}

and the
[[5,1,3]]
perfect code
[
53
]
with generators

\begin{array}[]{lllllll}\bm{G}_{1}=\mathbf{X}_{1}\mathbf{Z}_{2}\mathbf{Z}_{3}\mathbf{X}_{4}&\quad\bm{G}_{2}=\mathbf{X}_{2}\mathbf{Z}_{3}\mathbf{Z}_{4}\mathbf{X}_{5}&\\
\bm{G}_{3}=\mathbf{X}_{1}\mathbf{X}_{3}\mathbf{Z}_{4}\mathbf{Z}_{5}&\quad\bm{G}_{4}=\mathbf{Z}_{1}\mathbf{X}_{2}\mathbf{X}_{4}\mathbf{Z}_{5}.&\end{array}

A simple channel model is one characterized by errors occurring independently and with the same statistic on the individual qubits of each codeword. In this model, the error on each physical qubit can be
\bm{X}
,
\bm{Z}
or
\bm{Y}
with probabilities
p_{\mathrm{X}}
,
p_{\mathrm{Z}}
, and
p_{\mathrm{Y}}
, respectively. The probability of a generic error on a physical qubit is
\rho=p_{\mathrm{X}}+p_{\mathrm{Z}}+p_{\mathrm{Y}}
.
Two important models are the
depolarizing channel
where
p_{\mathrm{X}}=p_{\mathrm{Z}}=p_{\mathrm{Y}}=\rho/3
, and the
phase flip channel
where
\rho=p_{\mathrm{Z}}
,
p_{\mathrm{X}}=p_{\mathrm{Y}}=0
.
We will also consider more general asymmetric channels with the constraint
p_{\mathrm{X}}=p_{\mathrm{Y}}
, therefore completely characterized by the bias parameter
A=2p_{\mathrm{Z}}/(\rho-p_{\mathrm{Z}})
. Note that for
A=1
we have the depolarizing channel, and for
A\to\infty
we have the phase flip channel.

In the following, we will also adopt the notation
[[n,k,d_{\mathrm{X}}/d_{\mathrm{Z}}]]
for asymmetric codes able to correct all patterns up to
t_{\mathrm{X}}=\lfloor(d_{\mathrm{X}}-1)/2\rfloor
Pauli
\bm{X}
errors and
t_{\mathrm{Z}}=\lfloor(d_{\mathrm{Z}}-1)/2\rfloor
Pauli
\bm{Z}
errors.

2.2
Quantum Topological Codes

One of the most important families of stabilizer
QECC
is that of
topological
codes. The general design principle behind these codes is that they are built by patching together repeated elements. Using this kind of approach, they can be easily scaled in size in order to increase the distance of the code, still guaranteeing commutativity of the generators. As regards the actual implementation, these codes have a great intrinsic advantage. In fact, they require only nearest-neighbor interactions
[
18
]
.
The most important codes within this category are the
surface
codes, in which all the check operators are local and the qubits are arranged on planar sheets. Each stabilizer is associated either with one of the sites or one of the cells that are called “plaquettes”
[
54
]
.
The stabilizer’s generators in the interior are four-qubits plaquette or site operators, while the ones at the boundaries are three-qubits operators. Along a plaquette or “rough”  edge, each generator is a three-qubits operator
\bm{Z}^{\otimes 3}
, while, along a site edge or “smooth”  edge, each generator is a three-qubits operator
\bm{X}^{\otimes 3}
.
The entire lattice is able to encode one logical qubit (
k=1
).
It can be shown that in a code with distance
d
, the lattice has
d^{2}+(d-1)^{2}
physical qubits
[
49
]
. For example, two equivalent graphical representations of the resulting lattice for the
[[13,1,3]]
surface code are shown in Fig.
1
. For this surface code, the generators are

\begin{array}[]{lllllll}\bm{G}_{1}=\mathbf{X}_{1}\mathbf{X}_{2}\mathbf{X}_{4}&\bm{G}_{2}=\mathbf{X}_{2}\mathbf{X}_{3}\mathbf{X}_{5}&\\
\bm{G}_{3}=\mathbf{Z}_{1}\mathbf{Z}_{4}\mathbf{Z}_{6}&\bm{G}_{4}=\mathbf{Z}_{2}\mathbf{Z}_{4}\mathbf{Z}_{5}\mathbf{Z}_{7}&\\
\bm{G}_{5}=\mathbf{Z}_{3}\mathbf{Z}_{5}\mathbf{Z}_{8}\\
\bm{G}_{6}=\mathbf{X}_{4}\mathbf{X}_{6}\mathbf{X}_{7}\mathbf{X}_{9}&\bm{G}_{7}=\mathbf{X}_{5}\mathbf{X}_{7}\mathbf{X}_{8}\mathbf{X}_{10}&\\
\bm{G}_{8}=\mathbf{Z}_{6}\mathbf{Z}_{9}\mathbf{Z}_{11}&\bm{G}_{9}=\mathbf{Z}_{7}\mathbf{Z}_{9}\mathbf{Z}_{10}\mathbf{Z}_{12}&\\
\bm{G}_{10}=\mathbf{Z}_{8}\mathbf{Z}_{10}\mathbf{Z}_{13}\\
\bm{G}_{11}=\mathbf{X}_{9}\mathbf{X}_{11}\mathbf{X}_{12}&\bm{G}_{12}=\mathbf{X}_{10}\mathbf{X}_{12}\mathbf{X}_{13}\,.&\end{array}

The logical logical
\bm{Z}_{L}
operator can be chosen as a tensor product of
\bm{Z}
’s acting on a chain of qubits running from a rough edge to the one at the opposite side of the lattice. Similarly, the
\bm{X}_{L}
logical operator will be the tensor product of
\bm{X}
’s acting on a chain running from a smooth link to the correspondent one at the other side of the dual lattice.

Figure 1:
[[13,1,3]]
Surface code. (a) Actual lattice with data qubits
D
(circles) and ancillae
A
(squares). (b) Simplified representation with
\bm{X}
generators corresponding to sites and
\bm{Z}
generators to plaquettes. A smooth edge and a rough edge are depicted in green and blue, respectively. Examples of logical operators are drawn on the lattice.

Another important feature is that a simple complete decoder exists for surface codes: the
MWPM

[
50
]
. This decoder connects pairs of defects in the shortest way.
Moreover, any set of
\bm{Z}
(
\bm{X}
) operators which form a closed loop on the edges of the lattice is contained in the stabilizer. Hence, if the correction operator applied by the decoder together with the channel error closes a loop, the error is correctly recovered. As a consequence, surface codes can also easily correct a large number of errors with a weight larger than
t=\lfloor(d-1)/2\rfloor
.

In general, a decoding error occurs every time that the concatenation of actual channel error and correction operator realizes a logical operator, so the whole chain operator crosses the lattice from boundary to boundary, realizing a logical operator. For instance, consider a two qubits error
\bm{Z}_{2}\bm{Z}_{3}
for the code in Fig.
1
. In this scenario, the ancilla qubit
A_{1}
is the only one that changes its state, hence the decoder assumes (wrongly) an error
\bm{Z}
on data qubit
D_{1}
. It can be easily noticed that the whole operator applied to the lattice represents the logical
\bm{Z}_{L}
.

Rectangular surface codes, with differing horizontal and vertical dimensions, can be effectively employed in asymmetric channels. For instance, in hardware implementations,
\bm{Z}
errors tend to occur more frequently
[
27
,
30
,
55
]
. In such scenarios, the
[[23,1,3/5]]
surface code is a viable option, where the horizontal direction is two qubits longer than the vertical one. This configuration results in the logical
\bm{Z}_{L}
operator being a chain of 5 qubits, in contrast to the
[[13,1,3]]
code, where it spans only 3 qubits. Despite requiring a lattice of 23 qubits, this code has a distance of
d_{Z}=5
, enabling it to correct weight two
\bm{Z}
errors.

2.3
Quantum Codes with Bounded Distance Decoding

The codeword error probability,
\rho_{\mathrm{L}}
, also called logical error probability, is defined as the probability that the decoder does not correct the errors introduced by the quantum channel.

Let us first assume an
[[n,k,d]]

QECC
together with a decoder which corrects up to
t=\lfloor(d-1)/2\rfloor
generic errors (i.e.,
\bm{X}
,
\bm{Z}
, or
\bm{Y}
) per codeword, and no others. For this
BD
(
BD
) decoder, the logical error probability is simply

\rho_{\mathrm{L}}=1-\sum_{j=0}^{t}\binom{n}{j}\rho^{j}\,(1-\rho)^{n-j}

(1)

that, for
\rho\ll 1
, can be approximated as

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{L}}

\displaystyle\simeq\binom{n}{t+1}\rho^{t+1}\,.

(2)

We can see that the slope in the log-log plot of the logical error probability
\rho_{\mathrm{L}}
vs. the physical error probability
\rho
is
t+1
.

The error probability analysis has been recently generalized to asymmetric
QECC
assuming a decoder able to correct up to
e_{\mathrm{g}}
generic errors plus up to
e_{\mathrm{Z}}
Pauli
\bm{Z}
errors per codeword, and no others. In this case, weighting each pattern with the corresponding probability of occurrence, the bounded distance decoding error rate (
1
) over an asymmetric quantum channel with arbitrary
p_{\mathrm{X}}
,
p_{\mathrm{Y}}
, and
p_{\mathrm{Z}}
becomes
[
30
]

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{L}}

\displaystyle=1-\sum_{j=0}^{e_{\mathrm{g}}+e_{\mathrm{Z}}}\binom{n}{j}(1-\rho)^{n-j}\times

\displaystyle\sum_{i=(j-e_{\mathrm{g}})^{+}}^{j}\binom{j}{i}\,p_{\mathrm{Z}}^{i}\,\left(\rho-p_{\mathrm{Z}}\right)^{j-i}

(3)

where
(x)^{+}=\max\{x,0\}
. For a channel with asymmetry parameter
A=2p_{\mathrm{Z}}/(\rho-p_{\mathrm{Z}})
, noting that
(j-e_{\mathrm{g}})^{+}=0
if
j\leq e_{\mathrm{g}}
and using the binomial theorem, the expression in (
2.3
) can be rewritten

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{L}}=

\displaystyle\>1-\sum_{j=0}^{e_{\mathrm{g}}+e_{\mathrm{Z}}}\alpha_{j}\binom{n}{j}\rho^{j}(1-\rho)^{n-j}

(4)

where

\displaystyle\alpha_{j}=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}1&\text{if}\penalty\ j\leq e_{\mathrm{g}}\\
\left(\frac{2}{A+2}\right)^{j}\sum_{i=j-e_{\mathrm{g}}}^{j}\binom{j}{i}\left(\frac{A}{2}\right)^{i}&\text{otherwise}.\\
\end{cases}

(5)

Note that, when the code is symmetric
e_{\mathrm{Z}}=0
, and (
4
) reduces to (
1
).

Similarly to the approximation done in (
2
) derived from (
1
) when
\rho\ll 1
, considering the most significant terms in (
4
), the asymptotic slope analysis can be extended to asymmetric codes with
e_{\mathrm{Z}}\geqslant 1
as

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{L}}

\displaystyle\simeq\begin{cases}\binom{n}{e_{\mathrm{g}}+1}\,\left(\frac{2\rho}{A+2}\right)^{e_{\mathrm{g}}+1}&1\leq A<\infty\\
\binom{n}{e_{\mathrm{g}}+e_{\mathrm{Z}}+1}\,\rho^{e_{\mathrm{g}}+e_{\mathrm{Z}}+1}&A\to\infty\,.\\
\end{cases}

(6)

We observe that in this case, the asymptotic slope is
e_{\mathrm{g}}+1
for all finite
A
. On the other hand, considering a phase flip channel (
A\to\infty
) the slope becomes
e_{\mathrm{g}}+e_{\mathrm{Z}}+1
.

3
Quantum Codes with Miminum Weight Decoding

Let us now assume to have a decoder that always outputs a codeword (complete decoder). This could allow correcting also some (but not all) error patterns which are not correctable with bounded distance decoding. Specifically, we analyse the
MW
decoder, which can be implemented as the
MWPM
decoder for surface codes.

Definition 1
.

We indicate with
\beta_{j}
the fraction of errors of weight
j
that can be corrected by a complete decoder.

Note that
\beta_{j}
depends in general on the code structure, on the decoder, and on the channel asymmetry parameter
A
.

Definition 2

(Error class)
.

We state that two error patterns are in the same class if they have the same Pauli weight, i.e. the same amount of Pauli
\bm{X}
,
\bm{Y}
, and
\bm{Z}
errors.

In general, the logical error probability of an error-correcting code of length
n
is

\displaystyle\rho_{L}=\sum_{\bm{E}\in\mathcal{C}_{n}}\mathbb{P}\hskip-1.07639pt\left\{\text{error}|\bm{E}\right\}\mathbb{P}\hskip-1.07639pt\left\{\bm{E}\right\}

(7)

where
\mathcal{C}_{n}
is the set of all possible error classes over
n
qubits,
\mathbb{P}\hskip-1.07639pt\left\{\text{error}|\bm{E}\right\}
is the probability to have an error given the particular error class
\bm{E}
, and
\mathbb{P}\hskip-1.07639pt\left\{\bm{E}\right\}
is the occurrence probability of
\bm{E}
.
Since the probability
\mathbb{P}\hskip-1.07639pt\left\{\text{error}|\bm{E}\right\}
depends only on how many
\bm{X}
,
\bm{Y}
, and
\bm{Z}
the error
\bm{E}
contains, we define
f_{j}(i,\ell)
as the fraction of errors of weight
j
with
i
Pauli
\bm{Z}
and
\ell
Pauli
\bm{X}
errors that are not corrected (thereby leading to a logical error).
Then, we can write

\displaystyle\rho_{L}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\binom{n}{j}(1-\rho)^{n-j}\rho^{j}(1-\beta_{j})

(8)

where

\displaystyle 1-\beta_{j}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\rho^{j}}\sum_{i=0}^{j}\binom{j}{i}\,p_{\mathrm{Z}}^{i}\,\sum_{\ell=0}^{j-i}\binom{j-i}{\ell}\,p_{\mathrm{X}}^{\ell}\,p_{\mathrm{Y}}^{j-i-\ell}f_{j}(i,\ell)\,.

(9)

Considering a symmetric error correcting code with bounded distance decoding which corrects up to
e_{\mathrm{g}}
errors, we have that
f_{j}(i,\ell)=0
for
j\leq e_{\mathrm{g}}
and
f_{j}(i,\ell)=1
otherwise. In the case of an asymmetric code able to correct
e_{\mathrm{g}}
generic errors plus
e_{\mathrm{Z}}
Pauli
\bm{Z}
errors (see Section
2.3
),
f_{j}(i,\ell)=0

for
j\leq e_{\mathrm{g}}
,
f_{j}(i,\ell)=0
for
e_{\mathrm{g}}<j\leq e_{\mathrm{g}}+e_{\mathrm{z}}
and
i\geqslant e_{\mathrm{z}}
, and
f_{j}(i,\ell)=1
otherwise.
For channels with
p_{\mathrm{X}}=p_{\mathrm{Y}}
(e.g., depolarizing and asymmetric) we have that

\displaystyle 1-\beta_{j}=\frac{1}{(A+2)^{j}}\sum_{i=0}^{j}\,A^{i}\,\sum_{\ell=0}^{j-i}\binom{j}{i}\binom{j-i}{\ell}f_{j}(i,\ell)\,

(10)

and
\beta_{j}=\beta_{j}(A)
. The dependence on
A
will be indicated only when necessary. In the following we will assume

.

For a symmetric code, starting from (

) we obtain the upper bound

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{L}}\leqslant

\displaystyle\left(1-\beta_{t+1}\right)\binom{n}{t+1}\rho^{t+1}\left(1-\rho\right)^{n-t-1}

\displaystyle+\,\sum_{j=t+2}^{n}\binom{n}{j}(1-\rho)^{n-j}\rho^{j}\,.

(11)

Also, we can approximate the logical error rate

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{L}}

\displaystyle\approx\left(1-\beta_{t+1}\right)\binom{n}{t+1}\rho^{t+1}\,.

(12)

Note that equation (

are not correctable. The asymptotic slope in log-log domain remains

, but with an offset depending on
(1-\beta_{t+1})
, compared to (

In a similar way, we can find the asymptotic error correction capability of an asymmetric code. In particular, for
\rho\ll 1
the performance of the code becomes

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{L}}

\displaystyle\approx(1-\beta_{e_{\mathrm{g}}+e_{\mathrm{Z}}+1})\binom{n}{e_{\mathrm{g}}+e_{\mathrm{Z}}+1}\rho^{e_{\mathrm{g}}+e_{\mathrm{Z}}+1}

\displaystyle+(1-\beta_{e_{\mathrm{g}}+1})\binom{n}{e_{\mathrm{g}}+1}\rho^{e_{\mathrm{g}}+1}\,.

As indicated by (

\beta_{j}
.

4
Undetectable errors weight enumerator from Quantum MacWilliams identities

4.1
Undetectable Error
WE

Definition 3

(Logical operators)
.

The logical operators of a
[[n,k,d]]

QECC
are the elements of the set
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{S})\setminus S
, namely the operators that commute with the stabilizer but are not contained in it.

Definition 4

(Undetectable errors)
.

The undetectable errors operators are those coincident with the logical operators. They transform a codeword into another codeword and are therefore undetectable.

Thus, the set of logical operators coincides with the set of undetectable errors. In the following, we will use the two terms interchangeably.

The
undetectable errors
WE
for a
[[n,k,d]]
quantum code is

\displaystyle L(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{w=0}^{n}L_{w}z^{w}

(14)

where
L_{w}
is the number of undetectable errors (logical operators) of weight
w
.

Then, we will show that
L(z)
can be written as

\displaystyle L(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{k}}B(z)-\frac{1}{4^{k}}A(z)

(15)

where

A(z)=\sum_{w=0}^{n}A_{w}z^{w}
,
B(z)=\sum_{w=0}^{n}B_{w}z^{w}
,

A_{w}=4^{k}\sum_{\bm{E}_{w}}\left|\bm{E}_{w}\cap\mathcal{S}\right|

(16)

\displaystyle B_{w}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}\;\sum_{s=0}^{w}\;\;\binom{\ell}{s}\binom{n-\ell}{w-s}(-1)^{s}3^{w-s}A_{\ell}

(17)

and where the sum in (
16
) is over all operators
\bm{E}_{w}\in\mathcal{G}_{n}
of weight
w
.

We prove now that (
15
), together with (
16
) and (
17
), gives indeed the undetectable
WE
. To this aim, let us consider the operators
\bm{E}_{w}\in\mathcal{G}_{n}
with weight
w
, i.e., containing exactly
d
Pauli operators different from the identity. For any two hermitian operators
\bm{O}_{1}
and
\bm{O}_{2}
we can introduce two
WE

A_{w}
and
B_{w}

[
56
,
57
]

\displaystyle A_{w}(\bm{O}_{1},\bm{O}_{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{E}_{w}}\tr\left(\bm{E}_{w}\bm{O}_{1}\right)\tr\left(\bm{E}_{w}\bm{O}_{2}\right)

(18)

\displaystyle B_{w}(\bm{O}_{1},\bm{O}_{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{E}_{w}}\tr\left(\bm{E}_{w}\bm{O}_{1}\bm{E}_{w}\bm{O}_{2}\right)

(19)

where the sum is over all the
\bm{E}_{w}
, and
w=0,\ldots,n
. We will often drop the operators
\bm{O}_{1},\bm{O}_{2}
when the dependence is clear in the context.
In the case in which
\bm{O}_{1}=\bm{O}_{2}=\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}
, the projector onto a
[[n,k,d]]
binary stabilizer code,
A(z)
and
B(z)
carry some important properties of the code. Indeed, let
\bm{G}_{i}\in\mathcal{S}
, with
i=1,...,n-k
, generators of the stabilizer group of a code, then
[
58
]

\displaystyle\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n-k}}\prod_{i=1}^{n-k}\left(\bm{I}+\bm{G}_{i}\right)=\frac{1}{2^{n-k}}\sum_{\bm{S}\in\mathcal{S}}\bm{S}\,.

(20)

It can be shown
[
58
]
that the
WE

A(z)
defined in (
18
) is proportional to the stabilizer
WE

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4^{k}}A(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{w=0}^{n}\sum_{\bm{E}_{w}}\left|\bm{E}_{w}\cap\mathcal{S}\right|z^{w}.

(21)

Moreover,
B(z)
is proportional to the normalizer
WE

[
58
]

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{k}}B(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{w=0}^{n}\sum_{\bm{E}_{w}}\left|\bm{E}_{w}\cap\mathcal{N(S)}\right|z^{w}.

(22)

In order to find the relation between
A_{w}
and
B_{w}
we write the associated
WE
in the form

\displaystyle A(v,z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{w=0}^{n}A_{w}v^{n-w}z^{w}

\displaystyle\quad B(v,z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{w=0}^{n}B_{w}v^{n-w}z^{w}\,.

(23)

These two polynomials are related through the quantum MacWilliam identities
[
59
,
57
,
58
]

\displaystyle B(v,z)

\displaystyle=A\left(\frac{v+3z}{2},\frac{v-z}{2}\right).

(24)

Using (
24
) in (
4.1
) we get (
17
).
Finally, the number of undetectable errors of weight
w
is given by the number of operators of weight
w
which commute with
\mathcal{S}
, given in (
22
), minus the number of stabilizers of weight
w
, given by (
21
), which leads to (
15
).

The evaluation of the undetectable errors weight enumerator (
15
) requires therefore only computing the
A_{w}
in (
16
), which can be carried out by direct inspection of
\mathcal{S}
for small code sizes, or more in general by using the tools for the computation of the weight distribution of classical codes as discussed in Section
4.4
.

For example, let’s consider the [[3,1]] repetition code, able to correct one bit flip error. In this case, we have

\begin{array}[]{lllllll}\bm{G}_{1}=\mathbf{Z}_{1}\mathbf{Z}_{2},&\bm{G}_{2}=\mathbf{Z}_{2}\mathbf{Z}_{3}.&\end{array}

Considering
\bm{O}_{1}=\penalty\ \bm{O}_{2}=\penalty\ \Pi_{\mathcal{C}}
, we have from (

) that the stabilizer is
\mathcal{S}=\{\mathbf{I}_{1}\mathbf{I}_{2}\mathbf{I}_{3},\mathbf{Z}_{1}\mathbf{Z}_{2},\mathbf{Z}_{2}\mathbf{Z}_{3},\mathbf{Z}_{1}\mathbf{Z}_{3}\}
. If we combine (
20
) and (

), we obtain
\frac{1}{4}A(z)=1+3z^{2}
. Then we use (
17
) to compute
\frac{1}{2}B(z)=1+3z+3z^{2}+9z^{3}
. Finally, the undetectable errors
WE
is:
L(z)=3z+9z^{3}
.

More in general, a trivial way to obtain
A(z)
for the
[[n,k,d]]
code that we want to study is to compute all linear combinations among the set of generators. Alternatively, it is possible to consider the connection between stabilizer codes and codes over the Galois field

by identifying the operators
\bm{I}

\bm{X}

\bm{Z}
and
\bm{Y}
with the four elements of the field
[

,
15
]
. Hence, the evaluation of

can be seen as the computation of the weight distribution of classical codes over
GF(4)
.
Although this problem may be classified as NP-hard
[
61
]
, a variety of advanced and optimized algorithms have been developed. These algorithms surpass traditional brute force methods in efficiently calculating key metrics such as the weight distribution and the minimum weight.
Some of them are the Brouwer–Zimmermann algorithm and its various modifications for cyclic codes, quasi-cyclic codes, and divisible codes
[
62
,
63
,
64
,
65
,
66
,
67
,
68
]
. Such algorithms are implemented in software tools related to coding theory, such as MAGMA
[
69
]
. For all codes analyzed in this paper, the required time for the computation on a laptop of the weight distribution was less then one second.
Recently, new techniques have been developed for computing the quantum weight enumerator polynomial
A(z)
, which, for some degenerate stabilizer codes, provide up to an exponential speed up compared to the previous methods
[
70
]
.

4.2
Bounds on
\beta_{t+1}
via Undetectable Errors Weight Enumerator

The performance of an
[[n,k,d]]

QECC

3

\beta_{t+1}

A first bound, valid in general for stabilizer codes, is obtained assuming that each logical operator of weight

(25)

In this equation,
L_{w}
is the number of logical operators of weight
w
,

is the number of permutations of the different Pauli operators of weight
t+1
that cause a logical operator, and
\binom{w}{t+1}
refers to the number of patterns of errors (made of fixed Pauli operators) with weight
t+1
that can realize the corresponding logical operator.
For instance, if we consider
t+1=2
, we have
3^{2}=9
different Pauli errors:
\bm{Z}
\bm{Z}
,
\bm{X}
\bm{Z}
,
\bm{Z}
\bm{X}

\bm{X}
\bm{X}

\bm{Z}

\bm{Y}
\bm{Z}

\bm{Y}

\bm{Y}

Moreover, a logical operator of weight

Note that, the summation in (

The previous bound can be made more tight if we deal with

are formed by one Pauli type only. Consequently, a logical operator may be caused by only two types of Pauli errors (instead of the previously considered three). For instance, let us consider the logical operator

Remark on degeneracy:
If we want to have a more precise estimation of the error performance we should get tighter bounds on
\beta_{t+1}

To explain the role of degeneracy, assume we have a code with logical operators that share the same Pauli operators on
t+1

Therefore, by having knowledge of the structure of the logical operators within a stabilizer code, we could improve the previous bounds.
For example, the estimation

Note that this parameter is contingent on both the weight and the distinct Pauli operators that constitute the logical operators.
For instance, if the code is asymmetrically degenerate with respect to the different Pauli operators, different logical operators of the same weight could share a different number of Pauli operators. We remark that the bounds (

4.3

As evident from (

In the following, we present an expression for determining these values without the necessity of computing the MacWilliams identities.
Specifically, given a surface code with minimum distance

4.4
QECC
Performance via Logical Operators Analysis

Now we show that it is possible to obtain the exact
\beta_{j}
parameters with no need of simulations. This requires an analysis of the structure of the code logical operators, which we do explicitly for some small-size codes. The same approach may become too complicated for large codes, where the use of the closed-form bounds (
4.2
), and (
4.2
) is preferable. Since we are interested in the value of
\beta_{j}
, we have to find the fraction of errors of weight
j
that can cause the decoder to miscorrect and induce a logical error. In order to compute it, we will consider not only
L(z)
but also the structure of the stabilizer.

In the case of a
CSS
stabilizer code, taking into account that the total number of different error patterns of weight
j
is
\binom{n}{j}
, we can express
\beta_{j}
as

\displaystyle\beta_{j}=

\displaystyle 1-\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{j}\,A^{i}\sum_{\ell=0}^{j-i}\,\sum_{w}L_{w}(i,\ell)\,\mu^{(w)}_{j}(i,\ell)\,\gamma_{w}(i,\ell)}{(A+2)^{j}{\binom{n}{j}}}

(35)

where:
L_{w}(i,\ell)
stands for the number of logical operators of weight
w
that can be caused by the combined action of channel errors of weight
j
composed by
i

\bm{Z}
and
\ell

\bm{X}
Pauli operators and
w-j
correction operators applied by a complete decoder;
\mu^{(w)}_{j}(i,\ell)
refers to the number of different patterns of errors with weight
j
that can induce the corresponding logical operator of weight
w
; and
\gamma_{w}(i,\ell)\in\left[{1}/{\binom{w}{j}},1\right]
is the average fraction of potential Pauli error patterns of weight
j
that are not shared between two or more logical operators.
Note that (
4.4
) and (
10
) are equivalent, since

\displaystyle\sum_{w}L_{w}(i,\ell)\,\mu^{(w)}_{j}(i,\ell)\,\gamma_{w}(i,\ell)=

\displaystyle\quad\binom{n}{j}\binom{j}{i}\binom{j-i}{\ell}f_{j}(i,\ell).

(36)

In particular, on both sides of (
4.4
) we find the total number of logical errors induced by the weight
j
error class identified by
i
operators of type
\bm{Z}
and
\ell
operators of type
\bm{X}
.

In the following, we provide some examples over a depolarizing channel (
A=1
), in order to clarify the reasoning behind the evaluation of
\beta_{j}
.

First, let us consider the
[[5,1,3]]
perfect code
[

]
. Note that this is not a
CSS
code. Using (

) we obtain

is

\displaystyle\beta_{2}=1-\frac{30\cdot 3}{\binom{5}{2}3^{2}}=0

(37)

where we have considered that the total number of pairs of Pauli errors is
\binom{5}{2}3^{2}
, and that each logical operator of weight

can be caused exactly by three different combinations of errors of weight

A more interesting case is that of the

\displaystyle=1-\frac{1}{\binom{7}{2}3^{2}}\bigg[7\mu^{(3)}_{2}(2,0)+7\mu^{(3)}_{2}(1,0)+

\displaystyle=1-\frac{7\,(3+6)+7\,(3+6)+7\cdot 3}{\binom{7}{2}3^{2}}=\frac{2}{9}\simeq 0.22.

6
Conclusions

We have derived the
WE
for undetectable errors in stabilizer codes, starting from the quantum MacWilliams identities. Based on this result, we have calculated theoretical upper bounds for the error correction capability of
CSS
stabilizer codes with
MW
decoding.
Furthermore, we illustrate how to achieve even more stringent bounds by analyzing the structure of the logical operators for a specific stabilizer code. As an example of application of the method, we examined the performance of Steane and Shor codes with
MW
decoding, as well as surface codes with
MWPM
decoding, comparing the theoretical results with simulations on both symmetric and asymmetric quantum channels.
For example we write the asymptotic error rate in the form
\eta\rho^{t+1}
, with
\eta
depending on the fraction of channel errors with weight
t+1
that the decoder can correct.
We also derive a tight closed form approximation for the performance of surface codes over the depolarizing channel.

Appendix

Here we provide the detailed derivation of (
4.2
). Specifically, for a general
CSS
code, a logical operator made of
\bm{Y}
Pauli operators can be caused only by channel errors of weight
t+1
composed exclusively by
\bm{Y}
operators. This is a consequence to the fact that half of the generators consists of
\bm{X}
Pauli operators while the other half of
\bm{Z}
operators. For instance, considering a
CSS
code of distance
d=3
, we have that one channel error of weight
t+1=2
that is not exclusively composed by
\bm{Y}
Pauli operators, such as
\bm{Z}\bm{Y}
, will never cause a
\bm{Y}\bm{Y}\bm{Y}
logical operator. In fact, the
\bm{X}
error is always corrected by a code of distance
d=3
, while the remaining
\bm{Z}\bm{Z}
error will cause a logical operator composed by
\bm{Z}\bm{Z}\bm{Z}
. Furthermore, assuming no knowledge about the fraction of
\bm{Y}
logical operators, we conclude that a generic logical operator of weight
w=2t+1
or
w=2t+2
can be caused by
2^{t+1}
different Pauli errors of weight
t+1
. Moreover, a
CSS
dual-containing quantum code is characterized by a set of generators made of all
\bm{X}
or
\bm{Z}
Pauli operators applied to the same qubits. As a result, logical operators of minimum weight are made of only one kind of Pauli, i.e., there is an equal number of logical operator composed only of
\bm{X}
, of
\bm{Z}
, or of
\bm{Y}
. Therefore, for the
CSS
dual-containing, two third of logical operators made either of
\bm{X}
or
\bm{Z}
Pauli matrices are caused by
2^{t+1}-1
different Pauli errors, where the
-1
is to exclude the error composed by
t+1

\bm{Y}
operators. Note that this kind of error is the only one that can cause a logical operator composed of only
\bm{Y}
Pauli operators.
