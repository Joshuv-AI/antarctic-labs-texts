Energy storage and coherence in closed and open quantum batteries
Francesco Caravelli, Bin Yan, Luis Pedro García-Pintos, Alioscia Hamma

Abstract
We study the role of coherence in closed and open quantum batteries. We obtain upper bounds to the work performed or energy exchanged by both closed and open quantum batteries in terms of coherence. Specifically, we show that the energy storage can be bounded by the Hilbert-Schmidt coherence of the density matrix in the spectral basis of the unitary operator that encodes the evolution of the battery. We also show that an analogous bound can be obtained in terms of the battery’s Hamiltonian coherence in the basis of the unitary operator by evaluating their commutator. We apply these bounds to a 4-state quantum system and the anisotropic XY Ising model in the closed system case, and the Spin-Boson model in the open case.

2
Coherence and work in closed quantum batteries

2.1
Energy storage and coherence bounds

We describe a closed quantum battery by a quantum system on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}\simeq\mathbb{C}^{n}
with time dependent Hamiltonian
H(t)=H_{0}+V(t)
. Time evolution generated by the Hamiltonian is unitary and reads
\dot{\rho}_{t}=i[H,\rho_{t}]
, where we use units in which
\hbar=1
.
The energy of the battery is measured as the expectation value of the bare Hamiltonian
H_{0}
so that the work extracted from the battery is
[
31
]

{W(t)=\text{Tr}\Big((\rho_{0}-\rho_{t})H_{0}\Big)},

(1)

where we used the fact that no entropy change occurs for unitary dynamics.
This kind of quantum battery is described by a closed time-dependent quantum system.
The battery is externally driven by
V(t)
in order to modify its population levels and change its energy.
We derive upper bounds to the energy, and thus also find maximum bounds on the
ergotropy
, which is the maximum energy change when maximizing over unitary operations
[
31
]
, and in particular of the
isospectral twirling
of the work, e.g. the spectral-preserving unitary average over the time evolution
[
33
,
70
,
81
]
, or entangling power
[
52
]
.

We are interested in obtaining bounds in terms of (generalizations of) quantum coherence.
There are different ways of defining quantum coherence in a quantum system.
First of all, there is the coherence of the state in a given basis
\mathcal{B}=\{|i\rangle\}
. The norm of coherence for the state
\rho
can be defined as the weight of the off diagonal elements in the basis
\mathcal{B}
, namely
\mathbb{C}(\rho):=\sum_{i\neq j}|\rho_{ij}|^{2}

[
17
,
103
,
101
,
102
,
94
]
. If we define the dephasing super-operator
\mathcal{D}(\cdot)=\sum_{i}\Pi_{i}\cdot\Pi_{i}
, with
\Pi_{i}=|i\rangle\langle i|
are rank one projectors, then in terms of the dephasing superoperator, we have that

\mathbb{C}(\rho)=\|\rho-\mathcal{D}(\rho)\|_{F}^{2},

(2)

where
\|A\|_{F}=\sqrt{{\mbox{Tr\,}}{AA^{\dagger}}}
is the Frobenius norm. A simple calculation shows that
\mathbb{C}(\rho)={\mbox{Tr\,}}\rho^{2}-{\mbox{Tr\,}}(\mathcal{D}\rho)^{2}
. As quantum coherence in a state is a basis dependent notion, it is important to determine what is the relevant basis. In a typical quantum experiment, quantum coherence is relevant in the basis of observables that will display interference.

Here, we consider the instantaneous eigenbasis
of the unitary evolution operator
U_{t}
:

U_{t}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}e^{i\theta_{j}(t)}\Pi_{j}(t),

(3)

where
\rho_{t}=U_{t}\rho_{0}U_{t}^{\dagger}
.
For instance, if one deals with a time-independent Hamiltonian
H=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\Pi_{i}
, then
U_{t}=\sum_{i}e^{-i\lambda_{i}t}\Pi_{i}
.

One can easily see that the work, expressed as the energy storage, can be written as
[
33
]

\displaystyle W\ =W_{A}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underbrace{\text{Tr}(U_{t}^{\dagger}H_{0}[\rho_{0},U_{t}])}_{A},

(4)

\displaystyle W\ =W_{B}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underbrace{\text{Tr}(U_{t}[U_{t}^{\dagger}H_{0},\rho_{0}])}_{B},

(5)

\displaystyle W\ =W_{C}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underbrace{\text{Tr}(\rho_{0}[U_{t},U_{t}^{\dagger}H_{0}])}_{C}.

(6)

Lower and upper bounds to work can be found by applying the Von Neumann’s trace inequality to the energy storage, resulting in bounds in terms of the singular values of the Hamiltonian. While these bounds are given for completeness in App.
A
, the present paper focuses on bounds in terms of the norm of the dephased operator.

The identities above show that the amount of work one can extract from a quantum battery is related to how much the initial state
\rho_{0}
anticommutes with the evolution operator
U_{t}
(A), with the operator
U_{t}^{\dagger}H_{0}
(B), or how much these last two operators anticommute with each other (C).
The amount of non-commutativity between two operators is related to how much off diagonal weight one operator has in the eigenbasis of the other. For a state, this is the definition of quantum coherence. More generally, we can define an operator-coherence in the basis
\mathcal{B}
as

\mathbb{C}_{{\mathcal{B}}}(\hat{X}):=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j}\|[\hat{X},\Pi_{j}]\|^{2}_{F}.

(7)

We refer to the above super operator
\mathbb{C}(\cdot)
as
generalized coherence operator
and the quantity
\mathbb{C}_{{\mathcal{B}}}(\hat{X})
as the generalized coherence of an arbitrary operator
\hat{X}
in
\mathcal{B}
.
In the following, all the definitions of norms are provided in App.
B
.
The proof of the identity relationship between Eq. (
7
) and Eq. (
2
) is provided for completeness in App.
C
.
For quantum states, this reduces to the usual definition of Frobenius-norm coherence.
Given the notation above, let us now introduce some useful bounds that we will use in the following, and proved in App.
D
. We will denote with
\|\cdot\|
the operator norm
and with
\|\cdot\|_{F}
the Frobenius norm.

In general, one has the following Frobenius inequality:

\displaystyle|\text{tr}(A^{\dagger}B)|\leq\|A\|_{F}\|B\|_{F}.

(8)

Also, following from Hölder inequality, one has (see App.
B
for details):

\displaystyle|\text{tr}(A^{\dagger}B)|\leq\|A\|\sqrt{\text{r}(B)}\|B\|_{F},

(9)

where
\|A\|
is the operator norm, that is, the maximum eigenvalue of the operator
A
; and
\text{r}(B)
is the rank of the operator
B
, i.e., the number of its non-zero eigenvalues.
Moreover, we will use the following two inequalities:

Lemma 1 - Single Normal Coherence Inequality.

Let
A
be a normal operator and let
B
be an Hermitian operator.
Then,

\displaystyle\|[A,B]\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 4\|A\|^{2}\mathbb{C}_{A}(B).

(10)

Proof.

Let
U=\sum_{i}\eta_{i}\Pi_{i}
, with
\Pi_{i}\Pi_{j}=\delta_{ij}\Pi_{i}
. Then,
we have

\displaystyle\|[U,A]\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{ij}\eta_{i}\eta_{j}^{*}\text{tr}([\Pi_{j},A]^{\dagger}[\Pi_{i},A])

(11)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sum_{ij}\eta_{i}\eta_{j}^{*}\text{tr}\Big(A^{2}\Pi_{i}\delta_{ij}-A\Pi_{i}A\Pi_{j}\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sum_{i}|\eta_{i}|^{2}\text{tr}\Big(A^{2}\Pi_{i}-A\Pi_{i}A\Pi_{i}\Big)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle 2\sum_{i\neq j}\eta_{i}\eta_{j}^{*}\text{tr}\Big(A\Pi_{i}A\Pi_{j}\Big)

We note that
2\sum_{i}|\eta_{i}|^{2}\text{tr}\Big(A^{2}\Pi_{i}-A\Pi_{i}A\Pi_{i}\Big)\leq 2\text{sup}_{i}|\eta_{i}^{2}\mathbb{C}_{U}(A)
and that

\displaystyle-2\sum_{i\neq j}\eta_{i}\eta_{j}^{*}\text{tr}\Big(A\Pi_{i}A\Pi_{j}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\sum_{ij}|\eta_{i}\eta_{j}^{*}|\ |a_{ij}|^{2}

(12)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2|\eta_{i}^{2}\mathbb{C}_{U}(A).

It follows that

\|[U,A]\|_{F}^{2}\leq 4\|A\|^{2}\mathbb{C}_{U}(A),

as claimed.
\square

A similar lemma applies to the following commutator,
whose proof is shown in App.
D
.

Lemma 2 - Double Normal Coherence Inequality.
Assume A to be a normal operator and let B be Hermitian. Then, the following bound applies:

\|[A^{\dagger},BA]\|_{F}^{2}\leq 4\|A\|^{4}\mathbb{C}_{A}(B).

(13)

The bound above applies in the case of open systems as we will see soon.
Also, according to Proposition 2 in App.
D
,
one has
\|[A^{\dagger},BA]\|_{F}^{2}=\|[A^{\dagger},AB]\|_{F}^{2}
. In the following we will use also the subadditivity property of the rank, e.g. given two operators
A:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
B:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have
\text{r}(A+B)\leq\text{r}(A)+\text{r}(B)
. Clearly, however,
\text{r}(A+B)\leq\text{min}\Big(\text{r}(A)+\text{r}(B),n\Big)
, and if
U
is a unitary operator,
\text{r}(U^{\dagger}AU)=\text{r}(A)
. Also, as a side remark, we note that by using a different procedure, also bounds in terms of
l_{1}
coherence can be obtained (see for instance Lemma 2(b) in App.
D
).

2.2
Bounds in terms of the generalized coherence

Given the definitions above,
we prove the following three upper bounds:

\displaystyle|W(t)|

\displaystyle\underbrace{\leq}_{A}

\displaystyle 2\|H_{0}\|\sqrt{\text{min}\Big(2\text{r}(\rho_{0}),n\Big)\mathbb{C}_{U_{t}}(\rho_{0})}

(14)

\displaystyle\underbrace{\leq}_{B}

\displaystyle 2\|\rho_{0}\|\sqrt{\mathbb{C}_{\rho_{0}}(U_{t}^{\dagger}H_{0})}

\displaystyle\underbrace{\leq}_{C}

\displaystyle 2\|\rho_{0}\|_{F}\sqrt{\mathbb{C}_{U_{t}}(H_{0})}

with

the dimension of the Hilbert space. We also assumed that
\rho_{0}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}

The expressions (A) and (C) bound the energy storage in terms of the quantum coherence of
\rho_{0}
and
H_{0}
in the basis of

. One problem with the above bounds is that both the rank of

or the

We study specific models in Sec.
4
. The bound (B) relates work to the purity of the initial state, as
\|\rho\|_{F}^{2}={\mbox{Tr\,}}(\rho^{2})
. For a pure initial state work is upper bounded by the operator norm.
The bound is saturated when the system is prepared in the highest excited state. One can obtain a further bound by substituting the operator norm of
\rho
with its purity.
As a side comment, it is interesting to note that since the bounds (
14
) can be also obtained in the interaction representation, this is also true if we use
\rho_{I}
in the interacting representation
\rho_{I}=U_{0}^{\dagger}\rho U_{0}
,
U_{0}=e^{-iH_{0}t}
and
\Pi_{j}
’s are in the interaction representation as well.

Inequality A
.— We start with the inequality
|\text{Tr}(AB)|\leq\sqrt{\text{r}(B)}\|A\|\|B\|_{F},
where
\text{r}(X)
is the matrix rank (see App.
B
for a proof).
We now pick
A=H_{0}
and
B=U_{t}^{\dagger}[\rho_{0},U_{t}]
. We have
\|U_{t}^{\dagger}[\rho_{0},U_{t}]\|_{F}\leq\|[\rho_{0},U_{t}]\|_{F}
. Now note that
\text{r}(U_{t}^{\dagger}[\rho_{0},U_{t}])\leq\text{min}(2\text{r}(\rho_{0}),n)
because of the subadditivity of the rank.

If
\rho_{0}
has a smaller rank, e.g.,
\text{r}(\rho_{0})<n/2
, then using Eq. (
10
), we obtain

\displaystyle|W_{A}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|H_{0}\|\sqrt{2\text{r}(\rho_{0})\|[\rho_{0},U_{t}]\|_{F}^{2}}

(15)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|H_{0}\|\sqrt{2\text{r}(\rho_{0})4\mathbb{C}_{U_{t}}(\rho_{0})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sqrt{2}\|H_{0}\|\sqrt{\text{r}(\rho_{0})\mathbb{C}_{U_{t}}(\rho_{0})},

which is the upper bound we reported above.

Inequality B
.— If we instead start with the general bound
[
24
,
97
]

\|[A,B]\|_{F}^{2}\leq 2\|A\|_{F}\|B\|_{F}-2({\mbox{Tr\,}}{A^{\dagger}B})^{2},

(16)

the bound would be loose, as
\|H_{0}\|_{F}
is generally very large. We can then apply the trace inequality of Eq. (
8
), and obtain the following upper bound:

|W_{B}||\text{Tr}(U_{t}[U_{t}^{\dagger}H_{0},\rho_{0}])|\leq\|U_{t}\|_{F}\sqrt{\|[U_{t}^{\dagger}H_{0},\rho_{0}])\|_{F}^{2}}.

(17)

We can now use
\rho_{0}
as the operator in which we perform the spectral basis expansion. Then, using Lemma 1, Eq. (
10
), we have

|\text{Tr}(U_{t}[U_{t}^{\dagger}H_{0},\rho_{0}])|\leq 2\|\rho_{0}\|\sqrt{\mathbb{C}_{\rho_{0}}(U_{t}^{\dagger}H_{0})}

(18)

as claimed.

Inequality C
.— We start with the trace inequality of Eq. (
8
), with
|\text{Tr}(AB)|\leq\|A\|_{F}\|B\|_{F}
. Using the fact that
\sqrt{\text{tr}(\rho^{2})}=\|\rho\|_{F}=\sqrt{\text{purity}(\rho)}\leq 1
, we obtain

|W_{C}|=\text{Tr}(\rho_{0}[U_{t},U_{t}^{\dagger}H_{0}])\leq\|\rho_{0}\|_{F}\|[U_{t},U_{t}^{\dagger}H_{0}]\|_{F}.

(19)

Let us now focus on
\|[U_{t},U_{t}^{\dagger}H_{0}]\|_{F}^{2}
. Using the spectral decomposition for the unitary operator
U_{t}
, we have

(20)

\displaystyle|[U^{\dagger},H_{0}A]\|_{F}^{2}\leq 4\|A\|^{4}\mathbb{C}_{A}(B).

(21)

We see that the expression above is not in the form which can be directly expressed in terms of coherence.
Given the inequality above, then we have the following inequality

\displaystyle|W_{C}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\|\rho_{0}\|_{F}\sqrt{\mathbb{C}_{U}(H_{0})},

(22)

which is the inequality we reported above. This bound has an interesting application in terms of designing better quantum batteries, as it states that better energy transfer can be achieved by a) starting from a pure state and b) using a driving system that is maximally not commuting with the Hamiltonian

defining the energy.

2.3
Power and OTOC for closed systems

Similar bounds to those we have obtained for the energy storage in the case of the closed system can be obtained for the power, that is, for the rate of change of the energy as a function of time. The energy can be written as

\displaystyle W(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle W(0)+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{d}{dt^{\prime}}W(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}

(23)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle W(0)+\int_{0}^{t}P(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle W(t)\ t\ \text{sup}_{t^{\prime}\in[0,t]}|P(t^{\prime})|.

This inequality will be useful in later sections in particular in the case of open systems.

Bounds for the power can then be useful for obtaining bounds on the energy. In particular, as we will see below, the bounds on the power can be still expressed in terms of the coherence operator
\mathbb{C}_{Q}(\cdot)
, but where as we will see in a moment
Q
is different from the unitary operator.

Let us then consider the power in a closed system. The power is given by

\displaystyle P_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\frac{d}{dt}W|=|\text{Tr}\Big(H_{0}\dot{\rho}_{t}\Big)|

(24)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\text{Tr}\Big(H_{0}\Big[H_{0}+V,\rho_{t}\Big]\Big)|.

Following the three formulations of the work formula as in the previous sections, we have

\displaystyle P_{t}=\frac{d}{dt}W

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{Tr}\Big(H_{0}\dot{\rho}_{t}\Big)=\text{Tr}\Big(H_{0}\Big[H_{0}+V,\rho_{t}\Big]\Big)

\displaystyle\underbrace{=}_{A}

\displaystyle\text{Tr}\Big(H_{0}\Big[V,\rho_{t}\Big]\Big)

\displaystyle\underbrace{=}_{B}

\displaystyle\text{Tr}\Big(V\Big[\rho_{t},H_{0}\Big]\Big)

\displaystyle\underbrace{=}_{C}

\displaystyle\text{Tr}\Big(\rho_{t}\Big[H_{0},V\Big]\Big),

where in the last equality we introduced the interaction picture operators
V_{I}=U_{0}^{\dagger}VU_{0}
and
\rho_{t}^{I}=U_{0}^{\dagger}\rho_{t}U_{0}
.

The power obeys the following upper bounds:

\displaystyle\underbrace{\leq}_{A}

\displaystyle 2\|H_{0}\|\cdot\|V\|\sqrt{\text{r}([V,\rho_{t}])\cdot\mathbb{C}_{V}(\rho_{t})},

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle\underbrace{\leq}_{B}

\displaystyle 2\|H_{0}\|\cdot\|V\|\sqrt{\text{r}([\rho_{t},H_{0}])\cdot\mathbb{C}_{H_{0}}(\rho_{t})},

take the form of Out-of-Time-Order-Correlators (OTOC)

,
22
,
99
,
98
]
at infinite temperature,
which grow fast in time for typical

and

Moreover, note that inequalities A and B can be combined to obtain,

\displaystyle\left(\frac{P}{\|H_{0}\|\cdot\|V\|}\right)^{2}\leq\text{min}\big(\mathcal{Q}(\rho_{t}),\mathcal{T}(\rho_{t})\big),

(34)

where we defined
\mathcal{Q}=\text{r}([\rho_{t},H_{0}])\|[\rho_{t},H_{0}]\|_{F}^{2}
and

.
Here, we see observe that if

Finally, inequality C shows that the power is zero for

3
Coherence and energy in open quantum batteries

In the previous sections we discussed the case of closed quantum batteries undergoing unitary evolution. More generally, we can consider the case of non-unitary quantum evolutions that describe, e.g., open quantum systems.

3.1
Bounds in terms of Kraus operators

A generic quantum operation can be described by a quantum channel, that is, a completely positive, trace preserving map

\mathcal{E}:\rho\mapsto\rho^{\prime}=\mathcal{E}(\rho)

(35)

In the Kraus operator sum representation such a map can be described as

\mathcal{E}(\rho)=\sum_{k}A_{k}\rho A_{k}^{\dagger}

(36)

where the Kraus operators
A_{k}
are linear operators satisfying
\sum_{k}A_{k}^{\dagger}A_{k}=I
. This description can be used to model the evolution of a subsystem interacting with other quantum systems. Indeed, for a composite system
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{S}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
evolving unitarily with a Hamiltonian
H\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
by
U=\exp(-iHt)
, the evolution of the reduced system is given by a completely positive map
\rho_{\mathcal{S}}={\mbox{Tr\,}}_{B}(U\rho U^{\dagger})\equiv\sum_{k}A_{k}\rho_{A}A_{k}^{\dagger}
.
For open quantum systems, or more generally for non unitary evolution, work cannot be identified with the difference of energy but one has to consider also variations of entropy. Here we focus thus just on the energy exchanged
\Delta E(t)
.

Now we move on to generalize the bounds to energy exchange in terms of coherence for the evolution map
\mathcal{E}_{t}

Let us define
\rho_{a}(t)=A_{a}\rho(0)A_{a}^{\dagger}
. Also, we define
\mathcal{E}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(X)=\sum_{a}A_{a}XA_{a}^{\dagger},\ \ \ \ \mathcal{E}_{*}(X)=\sum_{a}A_{a}^{\dagger}XA_{a}.

(37)

First, we note that because of the Hilbert-Schmidt duality, we have

\displaystyle\text{Tr}(\mathcal{E}(\rho)H_{0})=\text{Tr}(\rho\mathcal{E}_{*}(H_{0})).

(38)

Then, we can rewrite

\displaystyle\Delta E

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{a}\text{Tr}\Big(\big(\rho_{\mathcal{S}}(0)-\rho_{a}(t)\big)H_{0}\Big)

(39)

\displaystyle\underbrace{=}_{A}

\displaystyle\sum_{a}\text{Tr}\Big(A_{a}H_{0}[A_{a}^{\dagger},\rho_{\mathcal{S}}(0)]\Big)

\displaystyle\underbrace{=}_{B}

\displaystyle\sum_{a}\text{Tr}\Big(A_{a}^{\dagger}[\rho_{\mathcal{S}}(0),A_{a}H_{0}]\Big)

\displaystyle\underbrace{=}_{C}

\displaystyle\sum_{a}\text{Tr}\Big(\rho_{\mathcal{S}}(0)[A_{a}H_{0},A_{a}^{\dagger}]\Big),

We can upper bound
|\Delta E|
as in the case of the closed quantum system, with the difference that now instead of a unique evolution operator, we have a Kraus representation, which albeit satisfying
\|\sum_{a}A_{a}^{\dagger}A_{a}\|=\|I\|=1
, are not unitary operators.
We can also write

\|A_{j}^{\dagger}+\sum_{a\neq j}A_{a}^{\dagger}A_{a}A_{j}^{\dagger}\|=\|A_{j}^{\dagger}\|
.

Proposition

The following inequalities apply for Hermitian
A_{a}

\displaystyle|\Delta E|

\displaystyle\underbrace{\leq}_{A}

\displaystyle 2\|H_{0}\|\sum_{a}\|A_{a}\|^{2}\sqrt{R_{a}^{1}\mathbb{C}_{a}(\rho_{\mathcal{S}}(0))},

\displaystyle|\Delta E|

\displaystyle\underbrace{\leq}_{B}

\displaystyle 2\|\rho_{S}(0)\|\|H_{0}\|

\displaystyle\cdot\sum_{a}\|A_{a}\|\sqrt{R_{a}^{2}\ \mathbb{C}_{\rho_{\mathcal{S}}(0)}(A_{a}H_{0})},

\displaystyle|\Delta E|

\displaystyle\underbrace{\leq}_{C}

\displaystyle 2\|\rho_{\mathcal{S}}(0)\|_{F}\sum_{a}\|A_{a}\|^{2}\sqrt{{\mathbb{C}}_{a}(H_{0})},

(40)

where
R_{a}^{1}=\text{r}([A_{a}^{\dagger},\rho_{\mathcal{S}}])
and
R_{a}^{2}=\text{r}([\rho_{\mathcal{S}}(0),A_{a}H_{0}])
;
\mathbb{C}_{a}(\cdot)
is the coherence operator
in the spectral resolution of
A_{a}
, while
\mathbb{C}_{\rho_{\mathcal{S}}(0)}(\cdot)
is the generalized coherence operator in the spectral basis of the reduced density matrix.

Proof.
As an example, let us consider first the latter equality in Eqs. (
39
).
We can write, for

,

\displaystyle\Delta E

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{a}\text{Tr}\Big(\rho_{\mathcal{S}}(0)[A_{a}H_{0},A_{a}^{\dagger}]\Big).

We can thus write, using the trace inequality of Eq. (

\displaystyle|\Delta E|\leq\|\rho_{\mathcal{S}}(0)\|_{F}\sum_{a}\cdot\sqrt{\|[A_{a}H_{0},A_{a}^{\dagger}]\|_{F}^{2}}.

(41)

We now make some assumptions about the Kraus operators, i.e., that these are Hermitian operators, in order to obtain an upper bound for
\|[A_{a}H_{0},A_{a}]\|_{F}
in terms of the coherence of the Hamiltonian in the
A_{a}
basis. While this assumption is unphysical, it is a loss of generality and extension of these bounds will be considered in the future.

Using Lemma 2 in Eq. (

where

is the coherence operator in the spectral basis of

The final upper bound above is given in terms of the sum of the coherences of

with respect to the Kraus operators

. While these bounds might be useful in some cases, typically it is easier to work in terms of Lindblad operators, as we we do in the next section.

If instead we start from

and

3.2
Bounds in terms of Lindblad operators

Consider the case of a quantum system
\mathcal{H}_{S}
interacting with a bath
\mathcal{H}_{B}
.
Under standard assumptions such as Markovian evolution and small couplings to the environment, the evolution of the density matrix
\rho_{S}
of the subsystem can be described by the Liouvillian operator
\mathcal{L}
through the master equation:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\rho_{\mathcal{S}}(t)=\mathcal{L}(\rho_{\mathcal{S}}(t)).

We can then write

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathcal{S}}(t)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}(1+\frac{\mathcal{L}t}{n})^{n}=e^{\mathcal{L}t}\rho_{\mathcal{S}}(0).

Then if we assume that
\rho_{\mathcal{S}}(t)
has a Kraus representation, at the order
dt
we must have

\displaystyle\hat{A}_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle I+dt(-i\hat{H}+\sum_{i=1}\hat{L}_{i}\hat{L}_{i}^{\dagger}),

\displaystyle\hat{A}_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{dt}\ \hat{L}_{i},i\geq 1,

see, e.g.,
[
56
]
.

The Kraus operators in fact contain both the battery Hamiltonian and the interaction with the bath. These enter in a non-trivial way into the density matrix evolution for the battery in terms of Kraus operators. One way to overcome this problem is via the fundamental theorem of calculus, which gives

\displaystyle E(t)-E(0)=\int^{t}_{0}\frac{dE}{dt}dt=\int^{t}_{0}P_{t^{\prime}}dt^{\prime}.

(44)

In order to bound the energy difference for
t>0
, we can use the integral inequality, e.g. that for arbitrary scalar functions
A(\tau)
we have
\int_{0}^{t}A(\tau)d\tau\leq\int_{0}^{t}|A(\tau)|d\tau\leq t\ \text{sup}_{\tau\in[0,t]}|A(\tau)|
. This bound works in particular well for short times
t
.
In an open system we have that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}E(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{tr}\left(\frac{d}{dt}\rho_{\mathcal{S}}(t)H_{0}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{tr}\bigg(\Big(\frac{i}{\hbar}[\rho_{\mathcal{S}},H_{0}+V]+\mathbb{L}(\rho_{t})\Big)H_{0}\bigg),

where

\displaystyle\mathbb{L}(\rho_{\mathcal{S}})=\sum_{n}\gamma_{n}\Big(L_{n}\rho_{\mathcal{S}}(t)L_{n}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{L_{n}^{\dagger}L_{n},\rho_{\mathcal{S}}(t)\}\Big)

with
\{\cdot,\cdot\}
the anticommutators, and where the Lindblad operators
L_{n}
satisfy
\text{tr}[L_{p}L_{t}^{\dagger}]=\delta_{pt}
.
We assume below that
L_{n}
is Hermitian that, while not being the most general case, does include many physically relevant models.

Following the definitions above, then, we prove the following

Proposition
(Upper bound on stored energy).
Consider an open quantum system with Hamiltonian
H_{0}
evolving under Hermitian Lindlad operators.
Then, the stored energy, as defined in eqn. (
44
), satisfies the following upper bound:

\displaystyle\frac{\hbar|E(t)-E_{0}|}{\|H_{0}\|}\leq t(W_{A}+W_{B}),

(45)

where the quantity
W_{A}
can be expressed in the following forms:

\displaystyle W_{A}=2\ \text{min}_{\tau\in[0,t]}\begin{cases}\|V\|\sqrt{\text{r}([\rho_{\mathcal{S}}(\tau),V])\mathbb{C}_{V}(\rho_{\mathcal{S}}(\tau))}\\
\|\rho_{\mathcal{S}}(\tau)\|_{F}\sqrt{\mathbb{C}_{H_{0}}(V)}\\
\|V\|\sqrt{\text{r}([\rho_{\mathcal{S}},H_{0}])\mathbb{C}_{H_{0}}(\rho_{\mathcal{S}}(\tau))},\end{cases}

(46)

while

\displaystyle W_{B}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 3\sum_{n}\gamma_{n}\text{sup}_{\tau\in[0,t]}\sqrt{r_{n}\mathbb{C}_{L_{n}}\big(\rho_{\mathcal{S}}(\tau)\big)},

(47)

where
C_{L_{n}}(\rho_{\mathcal{S}})
is the coherence in the spectral basis of
L_{n}
of
\rho_{\mathcal{S}}(\tau)
. Let us call
\Pi_{i}^{n}
the spectral basis of
L_{n}
. Then,

\displaystyle C_{L_{n}}(\rho_{\mathcal{S}})=\sum_{i}\|[\Pi_{i}^{n},\rho_{\mathcal{S}}]\|_{F}^{2}.

(48)

The constant
r_{n}
is given by
r_{n}=\text{r}(\rho_{\mathcal{S}})+\text{r}(L_{n})
.

Proof.

Following from the integral inequality, we begin with,

\displaystyle\hbar|\Delta E(t)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}|\text{tr}([\rho_{\mathcal{S}}(\tau),V]H_{0})|d\tau

(49)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}|\text{tr}(\mathbb{L}\big(\rho_{\mathcal{S}}(\tau)\big)H_{0})|d\tau.

The first term can be bounded as we did in the case of the closed system, thus we have the identities

\displaystyle\underbrace{|\text{tr}([\rho_{\mathcal{S}}(\tau),V]H_{0})|}_{W_{A}^{1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underbrace{|\text{tr}([V,H_{0}]\rho_{\mathcal{S}}(\tau))|}_{W_{A}^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underbrace{|\text{tr}([\rho_{\mathcal{S}},H_{0}]V)|}_{W_{A}^{3}}.

It follows that can use, following the bounds of Sec.
2.2
, we immediately write the three possible inequalities

\displaystyle W_{A}^{1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\|H_{0}\|\|V\|\sqrt{\text{r}([\rho_{\mathcal{S}}(\tau),V])\mathbb{C}_{V}(\rho_{0})},

\displaystyle W_{A}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\|\rho_{\mathcal{S}}\|_{F}\|H_{0}\|\sqrt{\mathbb{C}_{H_{0}}(V)},

\displaystyle W_{A}^{3}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\|V\|\|H_{0}\|\sqrt{\text{r}([\rho_{\mathcal{S}},H_{0}])\mathbb{C}_{H_{0}}(\rho_{0})}.

(50)

In the expressions above we defined
\mathbb{C}_{V}(\cdot)=\sum_{k}\|[\cdot,\Pi_{k}^{V}]\|_{F}^{2}
,, and we wrote
\Pi_{k}^{V}
is the spectral basis of the interaction operator
V
, and of course similarly
\mathbb{C}_{H_{0}}(\cdot)=\sum_{k}\|[\cdot,\Pi_{k}^{H_{0}}]\|_{F}^{2}
, with
\Pi_{k}^{H_{0}}
the spectral basis of the battery Hamiltonian.
Let us now focus on the Lindbladian. First, note that we have, because
\{A,B\}=[A,B]+2BA

\displaystyle\mathbb{L}\big(\rho_{\mathcal{S}}(\tau)\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\gamma_{n}\big(L_{n}\rho_{\mathcal{S}}L_{n}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{L_{n}^{\dagger}L_{n},\rho_{\mathcal{S}}\}\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{n}\gamma_{n}\Big([\rho_{\mathcal{S}}L_{n}^{\dagger},L_{n}]+\frac{1}{2}[L_{n}^{\dagger}L_{n},\rho_{\mathcal{S}}(\tau)]\Big).

We thus have

\displaystyle\hbar|\Delta E(t)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle W_{A}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\frac{\gamma_{n}}{2}\int_{0}^{t}|\text{tr}\Big([L_{n}^{\dagger}L_{n},\rho_{\mathcal{S}}(\tau)]H_{0}\Big)|d\tau

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\gamma_{n}\int_{0}^{t}|\text{tr}\Big([\rho_{\mathcal{S}}(\tau)L_{n}^{\dagger},L_{n}]H_{0}\Big)|d\tau.

Let us now make the assumption the Lindblads are Hermitian operators, e.g.
L_{n}^{\dagger}=L_{n}
. This bound is the one we use in the following for the case of the Spin-Boson model, for which such condition holds true.

For the term of Eq. (
3.2
), we use the trace inequality of Eq. (
9
), and the fact that
L_{n}^{\dagger}L_{n}
is an Hermitian operator, it has a spectral decomposition of the form

\displaystyle\tilde{L}_{n}^{2}=L_{n}^{\dagger}L_{n}=\sum_{k}|\eta^{n}_{k}|^{2}\Pi_{k}^{n}.

(53)

We can now use the bounds we used in terms of coherence. We have, using Lemma 1 and Eq. (
10

\displaystyle\|[L_{n}^{\dagger}L_{n},\rho_{\mathcal{S}}(\tau)]\|_{F}^{2}\leq 4\|L_{n}\|^{4}\mathbb{C}_{L_{n}}\big(\rho_{\mathcal{S}}(\tau)\big),

(54)

where

. Since
\text{tr}(L_{n}^{\dagger}L_{n})=1

and
\|L_{n}\|\leq 1

If we call

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}

\displaystyle|\text{tr}\Big([L_{n}^{\dagger}L_{n},\rho_{\mathcal{S}}(\tau)]H_{0}\Big)|d\tau

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\ t\ \|H_{0}\|\cdot\text{sup}_{\tau\in[0,t]}\sqrt{R_{n}^{\prime}\mathbb{C}_{L_{n}}\big(\rho_{\mathcal{S}}(\tau)\big)}

For the second term, in Eq. (

If

is an Hermitian operator, then it does have a spectral decomposition of the form

\displaystyle|\text{tr}\Big([\rho_{\mathcal{S}}(\tau)L_{n}^{\dagger},L_{n}]H_{0}\Big)|

3.3
Ensemble of batteries

A setup of interest for practical applications is the case of an array of batteries interacting through a potential
V
, and we want to show how the coherence enters in this case. Here we assume that the system is closed, but the formalism is analogous to the case of the open system as we discuss below. We consider
M
copies of the same system, a battery, coupled only via an interaction potential. Formally, such situation can be represented as follows. The
M
copies of the same system are represented by a Hamiltonian of the form
H=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{M}H_{i}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{M}(H_{0}^{i}+V^{i})
, e.g. an ensemble of batteries If we assume that the
M
copies are not interacting, since
\rho(t)=\otimes_{i=1}^{M}\rho_{i}(t)
, we have

\displaystyle W(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{M}\text{Tr}_{i}\Big(\big(\rho^{i}(t)-\rho^{i}(0)\big)H_{0}^{i}\Big)-E_{0}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle MW_{max},

where
W_{max}
is maximum work obtained for the single system
H_{0}^{i}+V^{i}
. In this case we can apply the formalism of the closed system for each subsystem and thus it is not so interesting.
If however we only have that
H_{0}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{M}H_{0}^{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}H_{0}^{i}\otimes I_{\mathcal{S}\setminus i}
, while
V
does have support on multiple batteries single battery, then we have that the density matrix is in general not factorizable, and the energy storage can be written in the form

\displaystyle W(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{Tr}\big(\rho(t)(\sum_{i=1}^{M}H_{0}^{i}\otimes I_{\mathcal{S}\setminus_{i}})\big)-E_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{M}\text{Tr}_{i}\Big(\text{Tr}_{\mathcal{S}\setminus i}\big(\rho(t)\big)H_{0}^{i}\Big)-E_{0},

where we denote with
\mathcal{S}\setminus i
as the system
\mathcal{S}
without the
battery
subsystem
i
, and
where
\rho_{i}(t)=\text{Tr}_{\mathcal{S}\setminus i}\big(\rho(t)\big)
is the local density matrix relative to subsystem
i
.
In this case, the time evolution of the reduced density matrix can be written in terms of a positive map
[
84
,
89
]
. We can write
\rho_{i}(t)=\sum_{a}A_{a}^{i}(t)\rho_{i}(0)A_{a}^{i}(t)
, with
\sum_{a}A_{a}^{i}(t)(A_{a}^{i}(t))^{\dagger}=I
where
A_{a}^{i}
are Kraus operators. It follows that we can write

\displaystyle W(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{M}\sum_{a}\text{Tr}_{i}\Big(\big(A_{a}^{i}(t)\rho_{i}(0)A_{a}^{i}(t)^{\dagger}-\rho_{i}(0)\big)H_{0}^{i}\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{M}\sum_{a}\text{Tr}_{i}\Big(\rho_{i}(0)[A_{a}^{i},H_{0}^{i}(A_{a}^{i})^{\dagger}]\Big),

where in the second line we have used the Hilbert-Schmidt duality. Here, we called
A_{a}^{i}
the Kraus operators to avoid confusion with the open system case, but the meaning is similar.

If the Kraus operators
(A^{i})^{\dagger}_{a}=A^{i}_{a}
are Hermitian, similarly to what we have done before, they can be expanded via a spectral decomposition of the form
A_{a}^{i}=\sum_{k}(\eta_{i}^{a})_{k}(\Pi_{i}^{a})_{k}
, and we can apply Lemma 2 of Eq. (
13
), proven in App.
D
. Using the spectral basis decomposition, then we have

\displaystyle\|[A_{a}^{i},H_{0}^{i}A_{a}^{i}]\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 4\|A_{a}^{i}\|^{4}\mathbb{C}_{ai}(H_{0}^{i}),

(57)

where
\mathbb{C}_{ai}(H_{0}^{i})=1/2\sum_{k}\|[(\Pi_{i}^{a})_{k},H_{0}^{i}]\|_{F}^{2}
.
We now use the trace inequality of Eq. (
8
), obtaining

\displaystyle W(t)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{M}\sum_{a}\|\rho_{i}(0)\|_{F}\|[A_{a}^{i},H_{0}^{i}(A_{a}^{i})^{\dagger}]\|_{F}.

(58)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\sum_{i=1}^{M}\sum_{a}\|\rho_{i}(0)\|_{F}\|A_{a}^{i}\|^{2}\sqrt{\mathbb{C}_{ai}(H_{0}^{i})},

from which it follows that the energy storage of the system can be reduced to coherence bounds on the single subsystem.

Models study

In this section we study the work and charging power for some models. We consider two closed quantum systems. \|H_{0}\|\cdot\|V\|\sqrt{\text{r}([V,\rho_{t}])\cdot\mathbb{C}_{V}(\rho_{t})}

4.2
The anisotropic XY model

With increasing experimental control over larger unitary quantum systems, it becomes more pressing to study the thermodynamics of many-body quantum systems.
Theoretical studies have shown advantages by exploiting collective effects in quantum batteries
[
23
,
32
,
41
,
36
,
83
,
69
]
.
Here, we apply the bounds on work extraction to a simple many-body spin system described by the XY model
[
73
,
39
,
18
]
, which can be investigated experimentally
[
59
,
104
]
.

The (anisotropic) transverse-field XY model in one dimension is a well-known spin model in Statistical Mechanics. One of its advantages is that properties of the ground
and excited states are known exactly. The transverse XY Ising model has an interesting phase diagram in which also quantum phase transitions are present.
In recent years the transverse field XY model has been studied in relation to quantities
of interest in quantum information theory, such as entanglement and quantum discord. Also, it has been shown that fidelity measures present signatures of QPTs.

The Hamiltonian of the anisotropic XY spin chain reads

H=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\frac{1+\eta}{2}\sigma_{i}^{x}\sigma_{i+1}^{x}+\frac{1-\eta}{2}\sigma_{i}^{y}\sigma_{i+1}^{y}+h\sigma_{i}^{z}\right),

(71)

where
h
is the external magnetic field, and
\eta
is the anisotropy parameter. We also assume perodic boundary condition.

For any value of
\eta,h
, the XY model can be diagonalized using the Jordan-Wigner transformation:

\sigma_{i}^{z}=1-2c^{\dagger}_{i}c_{i},\ \sigma_{i}^{-}=c_{i}^{\dagger}e^{i\pi\sum_{i=1}^{i-1}c^{\dagger}_{j}c_{j}}.

(72)

The Hamiltonian is then diagonalzed in the form

H=\sum_{k>0}\Lambda_{k}\left(\gamma^{\dagger}_{k}\gamma_{k}+\gamma^{\dagger}_{-k}\gamma_{-k}-1\right).

(73)

Here, the fermionic operator
\gamma_{k}
is defined by the Bogoliubov transformation of the Fourier transformed operators,
c_{k}
i.e.

c_{k}=\cos{\theta_{k}}\gamma_{k}+i\sin{\theta_{k}}\gamma^{\dagger}_{-k},

(74)

with the dispersion ralation given by
\Lambda_{k}=\sqrt{\epsilon_{k}^{2}+\eta^{2}\sin^{2}{k}}
,
\epsilon_{k}=h-\cos{k}
, and the angle
\theta_{k}=\tan^{-1}[(\eta\sin{k})/(\epsilon_{k}+\Lambda_{k})]
.

4.2.1
Work and power

We take the XY model Hamiltonain without external field
h
as the internal Hamiltonian of the battery, and the battery is initially prepared in the ground state of its internal Hamiltonian.
The charging process is achieved by turning on a constant field, i.e., via a standard quantum quench. In the following, we use superscript
(1)
to label the operators with initial parameters, and
(2)
(or no superscript for simplicity) to label the operators with quenched parameters.

\displaystyle H^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k>0}\Lambda_{k}^{(1)}[\cos^{2}{\chi_{k}}(\gamma_{k}^{\dagger}\gamma_{k}+\gamma_{-k}^{\dagger}\gamma_{-k})+

Now, the time-dependent work and power can be computed explicitly, i.e.,

W(t)=2\sum_{k>0}\Lambda_{k}^{(1)}\sin^{2}{2\chi_{k}}\sin^{2}{\Lambda_{k}^{(2)}t},

P(t)=2\sum_{k>0}\Lambda_{k}^{(1)}\Lambda_{k}^{(2)}\sin^{2}{2\chi_{k}}\sin{2\Lambda_{k}^{(2)}t}.

For non-zero anisotropic paramaters

Therefore, an optimal protocol could use an initial high field to fast charge the battery, and then switch to a critical field to maintain the maximal work.

4.2.2
Upper bounds

We now apply the some of the upper bounds derived in the previous sections to the work and power of the anisotropic XY model.

The first example we studied is the bound (C) for work, which involves the coherence of the internal Hamiltonian
H_{0}=H^{(1)}
in the basis of the evolution operator. The coherence of
H_{0}
can be computed as well, i.e.,

\displaystyle C_{U_{t}}(H_{0})=

(81)

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha}\left[\sum_{k>0}\delta_{\alpha_{k},\alpha_{-k}}(-1)^{\beta_{k}(\alpha)}\Lambda_{k}^{(1)}(1+\cos{(2\chi_{k})})\right.

\displaystyle\left.\times\sum_{k>0}\delta_{\alpha_{k},\alpha_{-k}}(-1)^{\beta_{k}(\alpha)}\Lambda_{k}^{(1)}(1-\cos{(2\chi_{k})})\right],

where the outer summation ranges over all possible configurations of the strings
\alpha=\{\alpha_{k},\alpha_{-k}\}_{k>0}
and
\alpha_{\pm k}
is either
0
or
1
.
\delta
is the Kronecker delta. Also,

\beta_{k}(\alpha)=\left\{\begin{matrix}0,\ \text{if}\ \alpha_{k}=\alpha_{-k}=1,\\
1,\ \text{if}\ \alpha_{k}=\alpha_{-k}=0.\end{matrix}\right.

(82)

This result gives a quantitative evaluation of the energy extracted in a quantum many-body quench since the work extracted is basically upper bounded by twice the square root of the coherence of
H_{0}
(See Eq. (
14
)). As confirmed by our numerical evaluation, this upper bound increases exponentially fast in the number of spins. Consequently, for the quench parameters in Fig.
2
, this bound is larger than the exact work for a few orders of magnitude. The exponential scaling is more apparent for the upper bounds of the power. Hence, these upper bounds can be very loose for many-body systems.

The more interesting case is the upper bound (A) of the work in terms of the coherence of the initial state in the basis of the time evolution operators. The the quench Hamiltonian is time-independent, hence the eigen-basis of the unitary evolution operator
U
is the basis of the Hamiltonian itself, i.e.,

\Pi_{\alpha}=\prod_{k>0}(\gamma_{k}^{\dagger})^{\alpha_{k}}(\gamma_{-k}^{\dagger})^{\alpha_{-k}}|0\rangle\langle 0|\gamma_{k}^{\alpha_{k}}\gamma_{-k}^{\alpha_{-k}}.

(83)

Here,
\alpha_{k}
is either
0
or
1
, and the string
\alpha=\{\alpha_{k},\alpha_{-k}\}_{k>0}
labels posibble configurations of all
\alpha_{k}
’s. There are
2^{N}
possible configurations corresponding to the
2^{N}
projectors.

For each given projector, its average over the initial state
\rho_{0}=|\psi(0)\rangle\langle\psi(0)|
is

\displaystyle\langle\psi(0)|\Pi_{\alpha}|\psi(0)\rangle

(84)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{k>0}\left(\cos^{2}{\chi_{k}}\delta_{\alpha_{k},0}\delta_{\alpha_{-k},0}+\sin^{2}{\chi_{k}}\delta_{\alpha_{k},1}\delta_{\alpha_{-k},1}\right).

The coherence is then

\displaystyle C_{U_{t}}(\rho_{0})=

\displaystyle\text{Tr}\rho_{0}^{2}-\sum_{\alpha}|\langle\psi(0)|\Pi_{\alpha}|\psi(0)\rangle|^{2}

(85)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\prod_{k>0}\left(\cos^{4}{\chi_{k}}+\sin^{4}{\chi_{k}}\right).

The maximum eigenvalue of initial Hamiltonian is

E^{(1)}_{max}=\sum_{k>0}\Lambda_{k}^{(1)}.

(86)

Together with the coherence of the initial state, we get the upper bound (A) of the work,

\displaystyle W(t)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\sqrt{2}|\sum_{k>0}\Lambda_{k}^{(1)}|\sqrt{1-\prod_{l>0}\left(\cos^{4}{\chi_{l}}+\sin^{4}{\chi_{l}}\right)}

Since
\chi_{k}\equiv\theta_{k}^{(2)}-\theta_{k}^{(1)}
is cannot be zero for all
k^{\prime}s
, unless the quenched Hamiltonian is the same as the initial Hamiltonian, the product term in the above bound vanishes exponentially fast with the system size. Hence, this upper bound of the work is only determined by the largest eigenvalue of the internal Hamiltonian, i.e., its operator norm, which scales linearly in the number of spins, and again can be very loose for many-body systems. However, they could be very useful for small systems which are more sensitive to the coherence of the initial state. In this case the product term in the above equation can have non-trivial contributions. Fig.
3
shows the upper bound (A) and the maximum work extracted from the exact solution (
79
) at various number of spins. The bound becomes tighter for smaller particle numbers.

4.3
Spin-Boson model

As an application of the bounds in the open system, we consider the simplest exactly solved model, the spin-boson model
[
42
,
30
,
82
]
. The spin-model model describes the precession of the two-level system of a spin in an open environment. The open environment is described by a reservoir of Harmonic oscillators. The model is well known because it serves the purpose of describing decoherence in a simple exactly solvable setting
[
89
]
.

The total Hamiltonian of the system is given by

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}(\omega_{0}\sigma_{z}-\Delta_{0}\sigma_{x})}_{H_{0}}+\sum_{k}\omega_{k}b_{k}^{\dagger}b_{k}

(88)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k}\sigma_{z}(g_{k}b_{k}^{\dagger}+g_{k}^{*}b_{k}).

The constants
\omega_{0}
and
\omega_{k}
are the level spacing of the two-level system and the frequencies of the bosonic degrees of freedom respectively, with
[b_{k},b_{k^{\prime}}^{\dagger}]=\delta_{k,k^{\prime}}
. The parameter
\Delta_{0}
is associated to the tunneling between the two states.

Here, we consider
\mathcal{S}
to be the two-level system. Under the condition of markovianity, the master equation for the spin-boson system can be written in the form
[
89
]

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\rho_{\mathcal{S}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{i}{\hbar}[\rho_{\mathcal{S}},H_{0}]+\gamma\sigma_{z}\rho_{\mathcal{S}}\sigma_{z}^{\dagger}-\frac{\gamma}{2}\sigma_{z}^{\dagger}\sigma_{z}\rho_{\mathcal{S}}-\frac{\gamma}{2}\sigma_{z}^{\dagger}\sigma_{z}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{i\omega_{0}}{2\hbar}[\rho_{\mathcal{S}},\sigma_{z}]-\frac{i\Delta_{0}}{2\hbar}[\rho_{\mathcal{S}},\sigma_{z}]+\gamma\sigma_{z}\rho_{\mathcal{S}}\sigma_{z}-\gamma\rho_{\mathcal{S}},

where we used
\sigma_{z}^{2}=I
.
where the single Lindblad operator are given by
L_{1}=L_{1}^{\dagger}=\sigma_{z}
, and thus is Hermitian. In the derivation of the reduced equation above the assumption of a thermal reservoir is made, with the constant
\gamma>0
containing the spectrum of the bath.

For the reduced system, it is easy to see that the energy storage is given by

\displaystyle E(t)=\frac{\omega_{0}}{2}\text{tr}(\rho_{\mathcal{S}}\sigma_{z})-\frac{\Delta_{0}}{2}\text{tr}(\rho_{\mathcal{S}}\sigma_{x}).

(90)

If we define

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathcal{S}}=\begin{pmatrix}\rho_{11}&\rho_{12}\\
\rho_{21}&\rho_{22}\end{pmatrix}\rightarrow E(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\omega_{0}}{2}(\rho_{11}-\rho_{22})

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta_{0}}{2}(\rho_{12}+\rho_{21}).

The evolution of the system is given by the following general dynamical equations:

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}_{11}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}i\Delta_{0}\left(\rho_{12}-\rho_{21}\right),

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}_{22}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}i\Delta_{0}\left(\rho_{21}-\rho_{12}\right),

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}_{12}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\gamma\rho_{12}-\frac{1}{2}i\Delta_{0}\left(\rho_{11}-\rho_{22}\right)-\frac{1}{2}i\rho_{12}\omega_{0},

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}_{21}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\gamma\rho_{21}-\frac{1}{2}i\Delta_{0}\left(\rho_{22}-\rho_{11}\right)+\frac{1}{2}i\rho_{21}\omega_{0},

from which it is immediate to see that the trace of the density matrix is preserved, but the off-diagonal terms change due to decoherence arising from the interaction with the bath.
Substituting the equations above into
\frac{dE}{dt}
we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{dE}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\Delta_{0}}{2}\Big(\rho_{12}\left(-\gamma-\frac{i\omega_{0}}{2}\right)+\rho_{21}\left(-\gamma+\frac{i\omega_{0}}{2}\right)\Big)

(93)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Delta_{0}\text{Re}\Big(\rho_{12}\left(\gamma+\frac{i\omega_{0}}{2}\right)\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Delta_{0}\gamma\text{Re}(\rho_{12})-\frac{\Delta_{0}\omega_{0}}{2}\text{Im}(\rho_{12}),

from which we see instead that a change in energy is associated to the tunneling, and is zero otherwise. It is also easy to see that the energy storage changes when coherence is present, due to the proportionality of its derivative with
\rho_{12}
.

We now wish to apply the bounds of Sec.
3.2
, in particular Eq. (
45
), in which only term
W_{B}
contributes:

\displaystyle\frac{|\Delta E(t)|}{\|H_{0}\|}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 3\gamma t\text{sup}_{\tau\in[0,t]}\sqrt{r_{n}\mathbb{C}_{L_{n}}\big(\rho_{\mathcal{S}}(\tau)\big)}.

To this end, we first calculate various quantities that are related to the bound.
The eigenvalues of
\Lambda(H_{0})=\{\pm\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\Delta_{0}^{2}+\omega_{0}^{2}}\}
. Thus we have
\|H_{0}\|=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\Delta_{0}^{2}+\omega_{0}^{2}}\approx\Delta_{0}/2
, while
\|L_{1}^{2}\|=1
and
l_{1}=1
. Also, both
R_{n}\leq 4
and
r_{n}\leq 2
. The spectral decomposition of
\sigma_{z}
is given by

\displaystyle\Pi_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\\
\end{pmatrix},\Pi_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&1\\
\end{pmatrix},

(94)

from which, after an immediate calculation, we get

\displaystyle\sqrt{\mathbb{C}_{L_{1}}(\rho_{\mathcal{S}})}=\sqrt{2(|\rho_{12}|^{2}+|\rho_{21}|^{2})}.

(95)

Using the fact that
\rho_{21}=\rho_{12}^{*}
,
assuming
\hbar=1
, and applying the upper bound of Eq. (
45
), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{|E(t)-E_{0}|}{t}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Delta_{0}3\sqrt{2}\gamma sup_{\tau\in[0,t]}|\rho_{12}(\tau)|

(96)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 4.24(2)\Delta_{0}\gamma|\rho_{12}^{0}|.

Let us now make some plots in the regime of strong decoherence and tunneling, in which we have
\gamma\gg\Delta_{0}\gg\omega_{0}
.
In this case we have
\rho_{12}(t)=\rho_{21}^{*}(t)\approx\rho_{12}^{0}e^{-(i\frac{\omega_{0}}{2}+\gamma)t}
, and thus,

\displaystyle E(t)-E_{0}\approx|\rho_{12}^{0}|\Delta_{0}\frac{2e^{-\gamma t}\left(-\cos\left(\frac{t\omega_{0}}{2}\right)+e^{\gamma t}\right)}{2}.

At the first order in
t
, we have instead the exact result

\displaystyle\frac{|E(t)-E_{0}|}{t}\approx 0.25\Delta_{0}\gamma|\rho_{12}^{0}|+O(t).

We thus see that for short times the bound has the same functional form as the actual first order approximation of the energy, and the two constants are both of order one.

5
Conclusions

In this paper, we studied and have made an explicit connection between work and coherence in a quantum battery.
In particular, we have provided a quantitative framework for evaluating upper bounds to energy storage in quantum batteries, based on the notion of Hilbert-Schmidt density matrix coherence, and its generalization to other operators like the Hamiltonian used to measure the energy of the system, and the internal Hamiltonian
H_{0}
.
We derived two key results. The first one is that the charging (or discharging) of a quantum battery is governed by the amount of non commutativity of both the density matrix of the system and the internal Hamiltonian in the basis given by the spectral decomposition of the interaction. We provided bounds which clarify the intricate interplay between coherence of the density matrix, of the Hamiltonian and the interaction in order for work to be performed by a quantum battery. The amount of non commutativity is quantified by using the Frobenius norm, namely by summing the modulus squares of all off-diagonal elements of an operator in the desired basis. For a state, this is a measure of coherence, and is bounded by one. For a generic operator, e.g.
H_{0}
, this form of coherence can be very large and
scale with the size of the Hilbert space in certain cases, rather than the number of particles in the system. As such, some of our bounds, if taken as they are, can be very loose for large systems.
Nonetheless, this also implies that some of our bounds can capture the behaviour of small systems well. We have tested these bounds on two exactly solvable closed quantum models, i.e. a 4-level system and the anisotropic XY model. In the former, our bounds are fairly tight, while in the latter we show under which conditions the operator coherence is small enough in order to obtain a tight bound.

The second result is the extension to the case of generic quantum channels, e.g., open quantum systems, both in terms of Kraus and Lindblad operators.
In order to see how these bounds apply quantitively, we have studied ensembles of quantum batteries and the Spin Boson model, showing the role of coherence in charging such battery model.
In the case of open systems we have focused on Hermitian Kraus and Linblad operators, but some of these bounds can be extended to non-Hermitian ones, with some technical challenges. This will be the subject of future investigations. In perspective, we find that the results of this paper open a certain number of interesting questions. Since in an open quantum system coherence is typically exponentially suppressed, we are interested in showing how decoherence free subspaces
[
100
,
62
,
72
]
can be used to obtain more efficient quantum batteries. In the spirit of the typicality arguments used both in
[
33
,
102
,
81
]
we can ask how typical quantum maps can be used to exchange energy. Finally, an important generalization would be to take in consideration the entropy change in open quantum systems and extend these results to the free energy available to a quantum battery.

In the case of many-body quantum batteries, it is natural to connect coherence to entanglement within the constituents of the battery.
Connections between the charging of quantum batteries and their entanglement have been studied in
[
6
,
68
,
32
,
23
,
61
]
.
In this paper we have focused on an Frobenius measure of coherence. While it does not satisfy all the coherence monotones axioms defined in
[
17
]
,
such coherence measure is connected to entanglement, in the sense that the more a state is entangled the less coherence can be stored in certain local parts of a system, which can be seen analyzing the Frobenius measure of coherence, via a Schmidt decomposition
[
55
]
. Thus, the higher the entanglement in a certain system the more coherent the state can be. In addition, some of our bounds could possibly be extended to
l_{1}
measures of coherence, which will be to focus of future investigations. If this is case, it is known that from the point of view of resource theory that entanglement and coherence are quantitatively equivalent
[
92
]
. Thus, while our bounds focused on the relation between energy storage and coherence, a complementary picture can be obtained in terms of entanglement.

Moreover, in
[
33
,
70
]
it has been shown that, in the context of random quantum batteries, there is a quantum advantage with respect to classical devices due to the behavior of the spectral gaps in the eigenvalues of the evolution operator
U
. These gaps are relevant when the initial state populates both eigenstates of
U
, and in turn this contributes to the coherence of
U
in the basis of the initial state. It would be interesting to see whether one can bound the quantum advantage of a battery in terms of coherence, which would result in a guide to designing superior devices at the microscopic level.

Appendix A
Work and von Neumann’s trace inequality

Let us now consider another set of bound for the work, based on von Neumann’s trace inequalities. Consider two operators
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
. Let
\alpha_{i}
and
\beta_{i}
be the ordered set of singular values of
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
, e.g.
\alpha_{i-1}\geq\alpha_{i}
, and
\beta_{i-1}\geq\beta_{i}
.
Then we have

\displaystyle|{\mbox{Tr\,}}{\mathcal{A}\mathcal{B}}|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\beta_{i},

(98)

and if
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
are Hermitian, then

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\beta_{n-i+1}\leq{\mbox{Tr\,}}{\mathcal{A}\mathcal{B}}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\beta_{i}.

(99)

It follows that, if we use the equalities from the previous section:

W=\underbrace{\text{Tr}(U_{t}^{\dagger}H_{0}[\rho_{0},U_{t}])}_{A}=\underbrace{\text{Tr}(\rho[U_{t},U_{t}^{\dagger}H_{0})]}_{B}=\underbrace{\text{Tr}(U_{t}[U_{t}^{\dagger}H_{0},\rho_{0}])}_{C},

(100)

we have three pairs of operators:
\mathcal{A}_{A}=U_{t}^{\dagger}H_{0}
,
\mathcal{B}_{A}=[\rho,U_{t}]
;
\mathcal{A}_{B}=\rho
,
\mathcal{B}_{B}=[U_{t},U_{t}^{\dagger}H_{0}]
and
\mathcal{A}_{C}=U_{t}
,
\mathcal{B}_{C}=[U_{t}^{\dagger}H_{0},\rho]
.

Let us thus consider the singular values of the three pairs. Given an operator
Q
, the singular values square are the (square roots of the) eigenvalues of the operator
Q_{2}=Q^{\dagger}Q
.
Von Neumann’s trace inequality can be applied
to equality
C
as both
\rho
and
[U_{t},U_{t}^{\dagger}H_{0}]=H_{0}-U_{t}^{\dagger}H_{0}U_{t}
are Hermitian operators.
The singular values of
\rho
are the eigenvalues of
\rho^{2}
. Let
d_{i}
be the singular values of
\rho
, and
\sigma_{i}(t)
be the singular values of
H_{0}-U_{t}^{\dagger}H_{0}U_{t}
.
Then, it follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{n-i+1}\sigma_{i}(t)\leq|W(t)|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}\sigma_{i}(t)

(101)

We can perform another upper bound:

W(t)<\sum_{i}d_{i}\sigma_{i}(A+B)
where A is
H_{0}
and
B=-U^{\dagger}H_{0}U

now, we can use Weyl’s inequality for the singular values of the sum of Hermitian matrices. This is

W(t)<\text{max}\ d\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}(A+B)<\text{max}\ d\sum_{i}(\sigma_{i}(A)+\sigma_{i}(U^{\dagger}AU))<2\text{max}\ d\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}(A)<2nd\ \text{max}_{i}\ \sigma_{i}(H_{0})

One interesting comment is that both
\rho
and
[U_{t},U_{t}^{\dagger}H_{0}]
are Hermitian operators. Thus, following von Neumann’s trace inequality, we can both upper and lower bound the work with

\displaystyle\underline{W}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{n-i+1}\sigma_{i}(t)\leq|W(t)|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}\sigma_{i}(t)=\overline{W}

(102)

where
\sigma_{i}(t)
are the singular values of
\hat{\epsilon}=H_{0}-U_{t}H_{0}U_{t}^{\dagger}
, while
d_{i}
are the singular values of
\rho
. It can be immediately seen that the inequality of Eq. (
102
) is tighter.

These bounds can be further simplified as follows. Consider the upper
bound
\overline{W}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}\sigma_{i}(t)
. We can further upper bound it via

\displaystyle\bar{W}\leq\bar{d}\sum_{j}\sigma_{j}(t),

(103)

where
\bar{d}=\text{max}_{i}d_{i}
. We note that numerically the upper bound above is hard to calculate because of necessity to diagonalize 3 matrices.
Now, for the singular values of a sum of Hermitian matrices we have
\sum_{j}\sigma_{j}(A+B)\leq\sum_{j}(\sigma_{j}(A)+\sigma_{j}(B))

[
57
]
. Now note that
B=-U^{\dagger}AU
where
U
is a unitary transformation, and thus
\sigma_{i}(U^{\dagger}AU)=\sigma_{i}(A)
. It follows that

\displaystyle\bar{W}\leq 2\ n\ \bar{d}\ \bar{\sigma}(H_{0})

(104)

where
n
is the dimension of the Hilbert space and
\bar{\sigma}(H_{0})=\text{max}_{i}\sigma_{i}(H_{0})
.
We thus obtain the upper bound

\displaystyle|W(t)|\leq 2\ n\ \bar{d}\ \bar{\sigma}(H_{0}).

(105)

which only requires to solve two maximum eigenvalues problems.

Appendix B
Norms definitions and upper bounds

In the paper we use various norms, so for the sake of clarity we define the following quantities.
Given a matrix
A
, we define the
2-norm
as
\|A\|_{2}=\sup_{i}\sigma_{i}(A)
, where
\sigma_{i}(A)
is the i-th singular value, while
\|A\|
. For a square matrix
A
of size
n
, We then call the Frobenius norm (or Hilbert-Schmidt) norm the following:

\displaystyle\|A\|_{F}=\sqrt{\sum_{ij}|a_{ij}|^{2}}=\sqrt{{\mbox{Tr\,}}{A^{\dagger}A}}=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}^{2}(A)}.

(106)

A bound for singular value of two matrices will be the following. Consider two matrices
A
and
B
. First, let us prove a property of the Frobenius norm that will turn useful in the following. If
\text{tr}(A^{\dagger}B)=\text{tr}(B^{\dagger}A)=0
, then
\|A+B\|_{F}^{2}=\|A\|_{F}^{2}+\|B\|_{F}^{2}
. In order to see this, notice that
\|A+B\|_{F}^{2}=\text{tr}(A+B)^{\dagger}(A+B)=\|A\|_{F}^{2}+\|B\|_{F}^{2}+\text{tr}(A^{\dagger}B+B^{\dagger}A)
from which the statement follows.

In general, we have the inequalities

\|A\|_{2}\leq\|A\|_{F}\leq\sqrt{n}\|A\|_{2}.

(107)

Let us now prove the following statement. In general, one has that
|\text{tr}(AB)|\leq\sqrt{\|A\|_{F}^{2}\|B\|_{F}^{2}}
. In this paper we do use the notation
\|A\|_{F}
for the Frobenius norm to avoid confusion with the spectral norm
\|A\|
. A tighter series of inequalities can be however:

\displaystyle|\text{tr}(A^{\dagger}B)|\leq\sigma_{1}(A)\sum_{j}\sigma_{j}(B)\leq\sigma_{1}(A)\sum_{j}\sigma_{j}(B)\leq\sqrt{n}\sigma_{1}(A)\sqrt{\sum_{j}\sigma_{j}^{2}(B)}.

(108)

The inequality follows from the following two statements, which can be proved using the singular value decomposition. For any matrix
M=A^{\dagger}B
, we have
|\text{tr}(M)|\leq\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}(M)
. Also,
\sigma_{i}(A^{\dagger}B)\leq\sigma_{1}(A)\sigma_{i}(B)
, which can be proved using the Fischer min-max theorem.

Another inequality in terms of the Frobenius norm for the trace can be derived as follows.
Consider again
|\text{tr}(A^{\dagger}B)|
. We can write the following Hölder inequality

\displaystyle|\text{tr}(A^{\dagger}B)|\leq|\text{tr}(|A^{\dagger}|^{p})|^{\frac{1}{p}}|\text{tr}(|B|^{q})|^{\frac{1}{q}}

(109)

with
1/p+1/q=1
. Then, if we write
p\rightarrow\infty
(or q), we can write

\displaystyle|\text{tr}(A^{\dagger}B)|\leq\|A\|\|B\|_{1}

(110)

where
\|A\|
is the operator norm, and
\|B\|_{1}
the 1-norm. We also make use of the inequality

\displaystyle\|B\|_{F}\leq\|B\|_{1}\leq\sqrt{\text{r}(B)}\|B\|_{F}

(111)

where
\text{r}(B)
is the rank of
B
.
The right inequality holds when the singular values of
B
are all the same, while the left inequality when only one singular value is nonzero.
We can say something more if at least one of the two matrices can be diagonalized, which is our case in the paper.

If
B=U_{t}^{\dagger}[\rho_{0},U_{t}]=\rho_{t}-\rho_{0}
, then because of the subadditivity of the rank and the fact that a unitary transformation does not change the rank, we have
\text{r}(\rho_{t}-\rho_{0})\leq 2\text{r}(\rho_{0})
. If the state
\rho_{0}
is thus pure, we have that
\text{r}(B)=2
, which is of order
1
.

A bound we will also use is the one for the square root. In fact we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\sqrt{\lambda_{i}a_{i}}\leq\sqrt{\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}}\sqrt{\sum_{i}a_{i}},

(112)

which is due to the concavity of the square root.

Appendix C
Relation between
\mathbb{C}(\cdot)
and
\mathcal{D}(\cdot)

Let us define the overlap in a certain basis defined by projector operators
\Pi_{k}
as

\mathbb{C}(X)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\|[X,\Pi_{j}]\|_{F}^{2}.

(113)

If the operator
X
is the density matrix,
\mathbb{C}(\rho)
is exactly the coherence of
\rho
in the basis given by
\Pi
’s.

First, we show that the function
\mathbb{C}(X)
is the coherence. We define the super operator
\mathcal{D}(\cdot)=\sum_{i}\Pi_{i}\cdot\Pi_{i}
, and the coherence as

\displaystyle\|X-\mathcal{D}(X)\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|X-\sum_{i}\Pi_{i}X\Pi_{i}\|_{F}^{2}

(114)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{Tr}\Big((X^{\dagger}-\sum_{j}\Pi_{j}X^{\dagger}\Pi_{j})(X-\sum_{i}\Pi_{i}X\Pi_{i})\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{Tr}\Big(X^{\dagger}X-\sum_{j}\Pi_{j}X^{\dagger}\Pi_{j}X-X\sum_{i}\Pi_{i}X\Pi_{i}+\sum_{ij}\Pi_{j}X^{\dagger}\Pi_{j}\Pi_{i}X\Pi_{i}\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{Tr}\Big(X^{\dagger}X-\sum_{i}X^{\dagger}\Pi_{i}X\Pi_{i}\Big).

Now we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}(X)\equiv\mathbb{C}_{\Pi}(X)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\|[X,\Pi_{i}]\|_{F}^{2}={\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{i}\text{Tr}\Big((X\Pi_{i}-\Pi_{i}X)^{\dagger}(X\Pi_{i}-\Pi_{i}X)\Big)

(115)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\text{Tr}\Big(\Pi_{i}X^{\dagger}X\Pi_{i}-\Pi_{i}X^{\dagger}\Pi_{i}X-X^{\dagger}\Pi_{i}X\Pi_{i}+X^{\dagger}\Pi_{i}\Pi_{i}X\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\frac{2}{2}}\text{Tr}\Big(X^{\dagger}X-\sum_{i}X^{\dagger}\Pi_{i}X\Pi_{i}\Big)=\|X-\mathcal{D}(X)\|_{F}^{2}.

It follows that if
X=\rho
,
\mathbb{C}(\rho)
is the coherence operator in the basis of
\Pi
’s. Also, we note that, notationally if we write
X_{ij}
in the basis of
\Pi_{i}
’s, in general
\sum_{i\neq j}|x_{ij}|^{2}=\mathbb{C}_{\Pi}(X)
. If a certain operator
A
has a spectral decomposition in terms of the projectors
\Pi
’s, we will write with an abuse of notation
\mathbb{C}_{\Pi}(\cdot)=\mathbb{C}_{A}(\cdot)
.

Note that
\|[X,\Pi_{i}]\|_{F}^{2}={\cancel{2}(\sum_{k}|x_{ik}|^{2}-|x_{ii}|^{2})}
. Also, note that

\displaystyle\text{Tr}\Big(X^{\dagger}X-\sum_{i}X^{\dagger}\Pi_{i}X\Pi_{i}\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{Tr}\Big(X^{\dagger}X\sum_{i}\Pi_{i}-\sum_{i}X^{\dagger}\Pi_{i}X\Pi_{i}\Big)

(116)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\text{Tr}\Big(X^{\dagger}X\Pi_{i}-X^{\dagger}\Pi_{i}X\Pi_{i}\Big)=\sum_{i}\text{Tr}(X^{\dagger}[X\Pi_{i},\Pi_{i}])

which is an expression we will use later.

Appendix D
Coherence bounds

In this section we prove various auxiliary propositions which enter into the coherence bounds lemmas proved below and reported in the main text.

First, we prove the following:

Proposition 1
.
Assuming that
\Pi_{i}\Pi_{j}=\delta_{ij}\Pi_{i}
,
we have

\text{tr}([\Pi_{i},A\Pi_{i}]^{\dagger}[\Pi_{j},A\Pi_{j}])=\delta_{ij}\|[\Pi_{j},A\Pi_{j}]\|_{F}^{2}

.

Proof.
Let us now prove some auxiliary properties related to the spectral decomposition. Note that
\Pi_{i}^{2}=I
, and
\Pi_{i}\Pi_{j}=0
if
i\neq j
. Then we have

\displaystyle\text{tr}([\Pi_{i},A\Pi_{i}]^{\dagger}[\Pi_{j},A\Pi_{j}])=0

(117)

for any operator
A
. In fact

\displaystyle\text{tr}([\Pi_{i},A\Pi_{i}]^{\dagger}[\Pi_{j},A\Pi_{j}])

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{tr}\Big((\Pi_{i}A\Pi_{i}-A\Pi_{i})^{\dagger}(\Pi_{j}A\Pi_{j}-A\Pi_{j})\Big)

(118)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\text{tr}\Big((\Pi_{i}A\Pi_{i}-\Pi_{i}A)(\Pi_{j}A\Pi_{j}-A\Pi_{j})\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\text{tr}\Big(\Pi_{i}A\Pi_{i}\Pi_{j}A\Pi_{j}-\Pi_{i}A\Pi_{i}A\Pi_{j}-\Pi_{i}A\Pi_{j}A\Pi_{j}+\Pi_{i}A\Pi_{i}\Pi_{j}A\Pi_{j}\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\text{tr}\Big(\Pi_{i}A\Pi_{i}\Pi_{j}A\Pi_{j}-\Pi_{j}\Pi_{i}A\Pi_{i}A-\Pi_{j}\Pi_{i}A\Pi_{j}A+\Pi_{j}\Pi_{i}A\Pi_{i}\Pi_{j}A\Big).

Since in every term there is a product of the form
\Pi_{i}\Pi_{j}
, the trace is given by

\displaystyle\text{tr}([\Pi_{i},A\Pi_{i}]^{\dagger}[\Pi_{j},A\Pi_{j}])=\delta_{ij}\|[\Pi_{j},A\Pi_{j}]\|_{F}^{2},

(119)

which completes the proof.
\square

Proposition 2

If
\Pi_{i}\Pi_{j}=\Pi_{i}\delta_{ij}
, then

\displaystyle\|[\Pi_{i},B\Pi_{j}]\|_{F}^{2}=\|[\Pi_{i},\Pi_{j}B]\|_{F}^{2}

(120)

Proof
.
Let us evaluate both sides of the equality. On the right hand side we have

\displaystyle\text{tr}([\Pi_{i},\Pi_{j}B]^{\dagger}[\Pi_{i},\Pi_{j}B])

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{tr}\Big((\Pi_{i}\Pi_{j}B-\Pi_{j}B\Pi_{i})^{\dagger}(\Pi_{i}\Pi_{j}B-\Pi_{j}B\Pi_{i})\Big)

(121)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{tr}\Big((B\Pi_{i}\Pi_{j}-\Pi_{i}B\Pi_{j})(\Pi_{i}\Pi_{j}B-\Pi_{j}B\Pi_{i})\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{tr}(B^{2}\Pi_{i}\Pi_{j}\Pi_{i}\Pi_{j}-B\Pi_{i}\Pi_{j}\Pi_{j}B\Pi_{i}-\Pi_{i}B\Pi_{j}\Pi_{i}\Pi_{j}B+\Pi_{i}B\Pi_{j}\Pi_{j}B\Pi_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{tr}\Big(\delta_{ij}B^{2}\Pi_{i}-2\delta_{ij}B\Pi_{i}B\Pi_{i}+\Pi_{i}B\Pi_{j}B\Big).

On the left hand side instead we have

\displaystyle\text{tr}([\Pi_{i},B\Pi_{j}]^{\dagger}[\Pi_{i},B\Pi_{j}])

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{tr}\Big((\Pi_{i}B\Pi_{j}-B\Pi_{j}\Pi_{i})^{\dagger}(\Pi_{i}B\Pi_{j}-B\Pi_{j}\Pi_{i})\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{tr}\Big((\Pi_{j}B\Pi_{i}-\Pi_{i}\Pi_{j}B)(\Pi_{i}B\Pi_{j}-B\Pi_{j}\Pi_{i})\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{tr}\Big(\Pi_{j}B\Pi_{i}\Pi_{i}B\Pi_{j}-\Pi_{j}B\Pi_{i}B\Pi_{j}\Pi_{i}-\Pi_{i}\Pi_{j}B\Pi_{i}B\Pi_{j}+\Pi_{i}\Pi_{j}B^{2}\Pi_{j}\Pi_{i}\Big)

Lemma
1^{\prime}
- Single Unitary coherence inequality

Let
U
be a unitary operator and
A
an Hermitian operator.
Then

\displaystyle\|[U,A]\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\sqrt{2}\|A\|\Big(2^{-\frac{1}{4}}\sqrt{n\|A\|}\mathbb{C}^{2}_{U}(A)+\mathbb{C}^{1}_{U}(A)\Big)

(136)

Proof.

Let
U=\sum_{i}\eta_{i}\Pi_{i}
, with
\Pi_{i}\Pi_{j}=\delta_{ij}\Pi_{i}
. Then,
we have

\displaystyle\|[U,A]\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{ij}\eta_{i}\eta_{j}^{*}\text{tr}([\Pi_{j},A]^{\dagger}[\Pi_{i},A])

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{ij}\eta_{i}\eta_{j}^{*}\text{tr}\Big((\Pi_{j}A-A\Pi_{j})(\Pi_{i}A-A\Pi_{i})\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\sum_{ij}\eta_{i}\eta_{j}^{*}\text{tr}(\Pi_{j}A\Pi_{i}A-\delta_{ij}A^{2}\Pi_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sum_{ij}\eta_{i}\eta_{j}^{*}\text{tr}(A[A\Pi_{j},\Pi_{i}])

We can now upper bound the quantity above with the absolute value, and using the identity
|\text{tr}(A[A\Pi_{j},\Pi_{i}])|=|\text{tr}(A[\Pi_{j}A,\Pi_{i}])|
, we have

\displaystyle\|[U,A]\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\sum_{ij}|\text{tr}(A[\Pi_{j}A,\Pi_{i}])|

(138)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sum_{ij}|\text{tr}(A[\Pi_{j}A,\Pi_{i}])|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\|A\|\sum_{ij}\sqrt{\text{r}([\Pi_{j}A,\Pi_{i}])\|[\Pi_{j}A,\Pi_{i}]\|_{F}^{2}}

We note now that
\text{r}([\Pi_{j}A,\Pi_{i}])\leq 2
. Then

\displaystyle\|[U,A]\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\sqrt{2}\|A\|\sum_{ij}\sqrt{\|[\Pi_{j}A,\Pi_{i}]\|_{F}^{2}}

(139)

We now use the fact that, from Corollary 1,

\displaystyle\|[U,A]\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\sqrt{2}\|A\|\sum_{ij}\sqrt{\delta_{ij}\text{tr}\Big(A[A\Pi_{i},\Pi_{i}]\Big)+(1-\delta_{ij})\text{tr}(A\Pi_{j}A\Pi_{i})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sqrt{2}\|A\|\Big(\sum_{i}\sqrt{\text{tr}\Big(A[A\Pi_{i},\Pi_{i}]\Big)}+\sum_{i\neq j}\sqrt{\text{tr}(A\Pi_{j}A\Pi_{i})}\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sqrt{2}\|A\|\Big(\sum_{i}\sqrt{\text{tr}\Big(A[A\Pi_{i},\Pi_{i}]\Big)}+\sum_{i\neq j}|a_{ij}|\Big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\sqrt{2}\|A\|\Big(\sqrt{\sqrt{2}\|A\|}\sum_{i}\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\|[\Pi_{i},A]\|_{F}^{2}}+\sum_{i\neq j}|a_{ij}|\Big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\sqrt{2}\|A\|\Big(2^{-\frac{1}{4}}\sqrt{\|A\|}\sum_{i}\sqrt{\ \|[\Pi_{i},A]\|_{F}^{2}}+\sum_{i\neq j}|a_{ij}|\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sqrt{2}\|A\|\Big(2^{-\frac{1}{4}}\sqrt{\|A\|}\sum_{i}(\sum_{k\neq i}|a_{ik}|^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}+\sum_{i\neq j}|a_{ij}|\Big)

The bound above is the first step of the bound.
We now use the fact that the square root is concave, and thus
\sum_{ij}\sqrt{\lambda_{ij}a_{ij}}\leq\sqrt{\sum_{ij}\lambda_{ij}}\sqrt{\sum_{ij}a_{ij}}
. Then

\displaystyle\|[U,A]\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\sqrt{2}\|A\|\Big(2^{-\frac{1}{4}}\sqrt{\|A\|}\sqrt{n}\sqrt{\sum_{i\neq j}|a_{ij}|^{2}}+\sum_{i\neq j}|a_{ij}|\Big)

(141)

It follows that

\displaystyle\|[U,A]\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\sqrt{2}\|A\|\Big(2^{-\frac{1}{4}}\sqrt{n\|A\|}\mathbb{C}^{2}_{U}(A)+\mathbb{C}^{1}_{U}(A)\Big)

(142)

which concludes the proof.
\square

Lemma 2 - Double Normal Coherence Inequality
Assume A be a normal operator and let B be Hermitian. Then,

\|[A^{\dagger},BA]\|_{F}^{2}\leq 4\|A\|^{4}\mathbb{C}_{A}(B)

Proof
.
For simplicity, we assume that
A
is a normal operator, e.g. we have
A=\sum_{i}\Pi_{i}\eta_{i}
, where
\eta_{i}
are the eigenvalues of
A
. If the operator is normal, then
A
and
A^{\dagger}
have an identical spectral decomposition and the proof goes along similar steps, which is Lemma 3.

Then

\displaystyle\|[A^{\dagger},BA]\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\sum_{ij}\eta_{i}^{*}\eta_{j}[\Pi_{i},B\Pi_{j}]\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{ij}\sum_{ab}\eta_{i}\eta_{j}^{*}\eta_{a}^{*}\eta_{b}\text{tr}([\Pi_{i},B\Pi_{j}]^{\dagger}[\Pi_{a},B\Pi_{b}]).

Now using Proposition 4, we have

\displaystyle\|[A,BA]\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\sum_{ij}\eta_{i}\eta_{j}[\Pi_{i},B\Pi_{j}]\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{ij}\sum_{ab}\eta_{i}\eta_{j}^{*}\eta_{a}^{*}\eta_{b}\text{tr}([\Pi_{i},B\Pi_{j}]^{\dagger}[\Pi_{a},B\Pi_{b}])

(144)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{ij}\sum_{ab}\eta_{i}\eta_{j}^{*}\eta_{a}^{*}\eta_{b}\Big(\delta_{ja}\delta_{ab}\text{tr}\Big(B[B\Pi_{i},\Pi_{a}]\Big)+(\delta_{ia}\delta_{jb}-\delta_{ji}\delta_{ib})\text{tr}(B\Pi_{j}B\Pi_{a})\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{ij}\eta_{i}\eta_{j}^{*}|\eta_{j}|^{2}\text{tr}\Big(B[B\Pi_{i},\Pi_{j}]\Big)+\sum_{i\neq j}(|\eta_{i}|^{2}|\eta_{j}|^{2}-|\eta_{i}|^{2}\eta_{i}\eta_{j}^{*})||b_{ij}|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{ij}\eta_{i}\eta_{j}^{*}|\eta_{j}|^{2}\delta_{ij}\text{tr}\Big(B^{2}\Pi_{i}-B\Pi_{i}B\Pi_{i}\Big)-\sum_{i\neq j}\eta_{i}\eta_{j}^{*}|\eta_{j}|^{2}\text{tr}\Big(B\Pi_{i}B\Pi_{j}\Big)+\sum_{i\neq j}\eta_{i}\eta_{j}^{*}(\eta_{i}^{*}\eta_{j}-|\eta_{i}|^{2})||b_{ij}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\text{sup}_{k}|\eta_{k}|^{4}\sum_{i}\text{tr}\Big(B^{2}\Pi_{i}-B\Pi_{i}B\Pi_{i}\Big)+\sum_{i\neq j}|\eta_{i}\eta_{j}^{*}(\eta_{i}^{*}\eta_{j}-|\eta_{i}|^{2}-|\eta_{j}|^{2})|||b_{ij}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\text{sup}_{k}|\eta_{k}|^{4}\sum_{i\neq j}||b_{ij}|^{2}+\sum_{i\neq j}|\eta_{i}\eta_{j}(\eta_{i}^{*}\eta_{j}-|\eta_{i}|^{2}-|\eta_{j}|^{2})|||b_{ij}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 4\ \text{sup}_{k}|\eta_{k}|^{4}\sum_{i\neq j}||b_{ij}|^{2}=4\|A\|^{4}\mathbb{C}_{A}(B)

which is what we stated we would prove.
\square

l_{1}

Lemma 2(b) - Double Hermitian Coherence Inequality
Assume
A
and
B
are Hermitian operators. Then,

\|[A,BA]\|_{F}^{2}\leq\|A^{2}\|^{2}\Big(\|B\|\big(2^{-\frac{1}{4}}\sqrt{\|B\|}\sqrt{n}\sqrt{\sum_{k\neq i}|b_{ik}|^{2}}+\sum_{i\neq j}|b_{ij}|\big)+2\mathbb{C}_{A}(B)\Big).

where
b_{ij}
are the elements of the matrix
B
in the basis of
A
.

Proof
.
For simplicity, we assume that
A
is Hermitian, we have
A=\sum_{i}\Pi_{i}\eta_{i}
, where
\eta_{i}
are the eigenvalues of
A
and are real. If the operator is normal, then
A
and
A^{\dagger}
have an identical spectral decomposition and the proof goes along similar steps, which is Lemma 3.

Then

\displaystyle\|[A,BA]\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\sum_{ij}\eta_{i}\eta_{j}[\Pi_{i},B\Pi_{j}]\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{ij}\sum_{ab}\eta_{i}\eta_{j}\eta_{a}\eta_{b}\text{tr}([\Pi_{i},B\Pi_{j}]^{\dagger}[\Pi_{a},B\Pi_{b}]).

Now using Proposition 4, we have

\displaystyle\|[A,BA]\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\sum_{ij}\eta_{i}\eta_{j}[\Pi_{i},B\Pi_{j}]\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{ij}\sum_{ab}\eta_{i}\eta_{j}\eta_{a}\eta_{b}\text{tr}([\Pi_{i},B\Pi_{j}]^{\dagger}[\Pi_{a},B\Pi_{b}])

(146)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{ij}\sum_{ab}\eta_{i}\eta_{j}\eta_{a}\eta_{b}\Big(\delta_{ja}\delta_{ab}\text{tr}\Big(B[B\Pi_{i},\Pi_{a}]\Big)+(\delta_{ia}\delta_{jb}-\delta_{ji}\delta_{ib})\text{tr}(B\Pi_{j}B\Pi_{a})\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{ij}\eta_{i}\eta_{j}|\eta_{j}|^{2}\text{tr}\Big(B[B\Pi_{i},\Pi_{j}]\Big)+\sum_{ij}(\eta_{i}^{2}\eta_{j}^{2}-\eta_{i}^{3}\eta_{j})||b_{ij}|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{ij}\eta_{i}\eta_{j}|\eta_{j}|^{2}\text{tr}\Big(B[B\Pi_{i},\Pi_{j}]\Big)+\sum_{i\neq j}\eta_{i}\eta_{j}(\eta_{i}\eta_{j}-\eta_{i}^{2})||b_{ij}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{ij}|\eta_{i}\eta_{j}|\ |\eta_{j}|^{2}\ |\text{tr}\Big(B[B\Pi_{i},\Pi_{j}]\Big)|+\sum_{i\neq j}|\eta_{i}\eta_{j}(\eta_{i}\eta_{j}-\eta_{i}^{2})|\ ||b_{ij}|^{2}

Here we wrote
b_{ij}
as the elements
ij
of the operator
B
in the basis of
\Pi
’s.
Let us now look at the two expressions separately. The exact expression for first term is given by Frobenius norm is thus bounded by another Frobenius norm as follows:
First, we note that
|\text{tr}([B[B\Pi_{i},\Pi_{j}])|=|\text{tr}([B[\Pi_{i}B,\Pi_{j}])|
. Then, using
|\text{tr}(AB)|\leq\|A\|\sqrt{\text{r}(B)}\|B\|_{F}

\displaystyle\sum_{ij}|\eta_{i}\eta_{j}|\ |\eta_{j}|^{2}\ |\text{tr}\Big(B[B\Pi_{i},\Pi_{j}]\Big)|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{ij}|\eta_{i}\eta_{j}|\eta_{i}^{2}\text{tr}([B[\Pi_{i}B,\Pi_{j}])|\leq\|B\|\sum_{ij}|\eta_{i}\eta_{j}|\eta_{i}^{2}\sqrt{\text{r}([\Pi_{i}B,\Pi_{j}])\|[\Pi_{i}B,\Pi_{j}]]\|_{F}^{2}}

Now note that
\text{r}([\Pi_{i}B,\Pi_{j})=\text{r}(\Pi_{i}B\Pi_{j}-\Pi_{j}\Pi_{i}B)\leq 2

\displaystyle\|[\Pi_{i}B,\Pi_{j}]]\|_{F}^{2}=\delta_{ij}\text{tr}\Big(B[B\Pi_{i},\Pi_{i}]\Big)+(1-\delta_{ij})\text{tr}(B\Pi_{j}B\Pi_{i}).

\displaystyle\sum_{ij}|\eta_{i}\eta_{j}|\ |\eta_{j}|^{2}\ |\text{tr}\Big(B[B\Pi_{i},\Pi_{j}]\Big)|

\displaystyle\|B\|\ \|A^{2}\|^{2}\Big(2^{-\frac{1}{4}}\sqrt{\|B\|}\sqrt{n}\sqrt{\sum_{k\neq i}|b_{ik}|^{2}}+\sum_{i\neq j}|b_{ij}|\Big)

For the second term, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i\neq j}|\eta_{i}\eta_{j}(\eta_{i}\eta_{j}-\eta_{i}^{2})|\ |b_{ij}|^{2}\leq\text{sup}_{ij}|\eta_{i}\eta_{j}(\eta_{i}\eta_{j}-\eta_{i}^{2})|\sum_{i\neq j}|b_{ij}|^{2}.

\displaystyle\|[A,BA]\|_{F}^{2}\leq\|A^{2}\|^{2}\Big(\|B\|\big(2^{-\frac{1}{4}}\sqrt{\|B\|}\sqrt{n}\sqrt{\sum_{k\neq i}|b_{ik}|^{2}}+\sum_{i\neq j}|b_{ij}|\big)+2\mathbb{C}_{A}(B)\Big)

(153)

which is what we stated we would prove.
