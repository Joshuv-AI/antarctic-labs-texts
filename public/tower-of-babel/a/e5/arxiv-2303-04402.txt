Goodness-of-fit tests for multivariate skewed distributions based on the characteristic function
Maicon J. Karling

Abstract




Abstract.

We employ a general Monte Carlo method to test composite hypotheses of goodness-of-fit for several popular multivariate models that can accommodate both asymmetry and heavy tails. Specifically, we consider weighted L2-type tests based on a discrepancy measure involving the distance between empirical characteristic functions and thus avoid the need for employing corresponding population quantities which may be unknown or complicated to work with. The only requirements of our tests are that we should be able to draw samples from the distribution under test and possess a reasonable method of estimation of the unknown distributional parameters. Monte Carlo studies are conducted to investigate the performance of the test criteria in finite samples for several families of skewed distributions. Real-data examples are also included to illustrate our method.

Keywords:

Empirical characteristic function, Goodness-of-fit tests, Heavy tails, Skewed distributions, Skew-normal distribution, Tukey g-and-h distribution.

AMS 2020 classification numbers:

62F03, 62H12, 62H15.

1
Introduction

Since the late 1980s, L2-type tests for goodness-of-fit based on the characteristic function (CF) have witnessed increasing popularity. The main reason is that the CF uniquely determines the underlying distribution and that it may be consistently estimated by the empirical CF. For multivariate distributions, there is the extra advantage that multivariate CFs and empirical CFs are well-defined and smooth, unlike the cumulative distribution function and its empirical counterpart, and thus it is easier to work with, even when the population distribution function is known.

Not surprisingly, testing for multivariate normality occupies a prominent place in this setting (see, e.g., Chen and Genton
[
14
]
, Ebner et al.
[
15
]
, Henze
[
23
]
, Henze et al.
[
24
]
, Henze and Wagner
[
25
]
, and Pudełko
[
43
]
) as a wide range of procedures is available, including CF-based tests. Outside the multivariate Gaussian context, however, the range of CF-based goodness-of-fit procedures is limited to only a handful of distributions, most of them belonging to the elliptical class (see, e.g., Fragiadakis and Meintanis
[
19
]
, Meintanis et al.
[
36
]
, and Székely and Rizzo
[
48
]
). One of the main reasons for this lack of available procedures is that CFs and empirical CFs, despite being smooth, are often required to be numerically integrated in the L2 setting, a task that may be problematic in higher dimensions, let alone the fact that the analytic form of the population CFs may be altogether unknown for most multivariate distributions under test.

Recently Chen et al.
[
13
]
proposed a procedure that is based on a Monte Carlo approximation of the CF under test, thereby avoiding the use of corresponding population quantities. However, the elliptical families considered by Chen et al.
[
13
]
, as important as they may be, render a range of shapes that limit their potential application, given the fact that asymmetry, in addition to excess kurtosis, is typically expected in real data analysis from Economics, Finance, and most other disciplines.

In this paper, we follow the approach suggested by Chen et al.
[
13
]
, but, at the same time, abandon the context of ellipticity adopted therein towards more general shapes. Specifically, we consider goodness-of-fit tests for certain popular families of multivariate skewed distributions. In this connection, an extra element that needs to be addressed in implementing the tests compared to Chen et al.
[
13
]
is that, unlike the parameters-free tests proposed in that paper, in the current setting the presence of shape parameters necessitates an additional re-sampling cycle to replicate the empirical distribution of the test statistic for a given parameter configuration. In doing so we take advantage of the canonical form of the distributional family under test, whenever available. In Section
2
, we revisit some of the main ideas of the work by Chen et al.
[
13
]
and the background for our tests shall be provided.

The remainder of this work unfolds as follows. Section
3
specifies the actual implementation of the new test procedure using bootstrap re-sampling. In Section
4
we introduce and provide a short review of the collection of families that shall be used for the simulations and goodness-of-fit tests and study their respective canonical forms. An extensive Monte Carlo study is presented to illustrate the finite-sample properties of the tests in Section
5
. The paper concludes with several real-data applications in Section
6
and a discussion of the overall results in Section
7
. An online Supplement contains some extra Monte Carlo results.

2
Characteristic function-based tests

Let
{{\boldsymbol{X}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
(
p\geq 1
) be a random vector with an absolutely continuous distribution function
F_{{\boldsymbol{X}}}
. We are interested in the composite goodness-of-fit testing problem represented by the null hypothesis

(2.1)

\displaystyle{\cal{H}}_{0}:\mbox{the law of}\ {{\boldsymbol{X}}}\in{\cal{F}}_{{{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}}},\quad\mbox{for some}\ {{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}}\in\Theta,

where
{\cal{F}}_{{{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}}}=\{F_{{{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}}},\,{{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}}\in\Theta\}
denotes a specific parametric family of distributions admitting a parameterization in terms of the parameter vector
{{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}}
. The corresponding parameter space
\Theta
will be taken as an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{q}
(
q\geq 1
).
Given the uniqueness of CFs, we may equivalently state the null hypothesis in (
2.1
) as

(2.2)

\displaystyle\varphi_{{\boldsymbol{X}}}({{\boldsymbol{t}}})=\varphi_{{{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}}}({{\boldsymbol{t}}}),\quad\forall\,{{\boldsymbol{t}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p},\ \mbox{for some}\ {{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}}\in\Theta,

where
\varphi_{{\boldsymbol{X}}}({{\boldsymbol{t}}})=\mathbb{E}(e^{{\rm{i}}{{\boldsymbol{t}}}^{\top}{{\boldsymbol{X}}}})
denotes the CF of
{{\boldsymbol{X}}}
and
\varphi_{{{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}}}({{\boldsymbol{t}}})
corresponds to the CF of some random vector in the family
{\cal{F}}_{{{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}}}
. Here
{\rm{i}}=\sqrt{-1}
and
\top
means transposition of vectors and matrices.

A CF-based statistic for goodness-of-fit is typically formulated in terms of
\|\varphi_{n}-\varphi_{\widehat{{{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}}}_{n}}\|_{w}^{2}
, where

(2.3)

\|f-g\|^{2}_{w}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{p}}|f({{\boldsymbol{t}}})-g({{\boldsymbol{t}}})|^{2}\,w({{\boldsymbol{t}}})\,{\rm{d}}{{\boldsymbol{t}}}

is an L2-type weighted distance between the pair of complex-valued functions
(f,g)
,

(2.4)

\displaystyle\varphi_{n}({{\boldsymbol{t}}})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}e^{{\rm{i}}{{\boldsymbol{t}}}^{\top}{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{j}}

is the empirical CF computed from a collection
({{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{1},\ldots,{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{n})
of independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) copies of
{{\boldsymbol{X}}}
, and
\varphi_{\widehat{{{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}}}_{n}}
is the CF corresponding to the null hypothesis
{\cal{H}}_{0}
with the parameter
{{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}}
replaced by an estimator
\widehat{{{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}}}_{n}:=\widehat{{{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}}}_{n}({{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{1},\ldots,{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{n})
. The weight function
w>0
will be further specified below.

There exist cases though of distributions, some of which will be considered herein, for which the null CF
\varphi_{{{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}}}(\cdot)
is either completely unknown or too complicated to work with. In such cases we suggest formulating a test statistic analogously but without direct reference to the CF of the distribution under test. Specifically, and in line with Chen et al.
[
13
]
, we suggest the test statistic

(2.5)

\displaystyle T^{(w)}_{n,m}=\|\varphi_{n}-\widehat{\varphi}_{0,m}\|_{w}^{2},

where
\varphi_{n}(\cdot)
is as in (
2.4
), while

(2.6)

\displaystyle\widehat{\varphi}_{0,m}({{\boldsymbol{t}}})=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}e^{{\rm{i}}{{\boldsymbol{t}}}^{\top}{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{0,j}}

is an empirical CF computed from a sample
({{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{0,1},\ldots,{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{0,m})
which is drawn from
F_{\widehat{{{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}}}_{n}}
, i.e., from a sample of size
m
(
m\geq n
) taken from the distribution under test with parameter estimated by a consistent estimator
\widehat{{{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}}}_{n}:=\widehat{{{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}}}_{n}({{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{1},\ldots,{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{n})
. In other words,
\widehat{\varphi}_{0,m}
is a Monte Carlo approximation of the null CF
\varphi_{{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}}(\cdot)
. Rejection is for large values of
T^{(w)}_{n,m}
.

A clear advantage of using the test statistic
T^{(w)}_{n,m}
is its computational simplicity. To see this, write
|\cdot|
for the modulus of a complex number and, thereafter, by using standard algebra, we obtain

\displaystyle|\varphi_{n}({{\boldsymbol{t}}})-\widehat{\varphi}_{0,m}({{\boldsymbol{t}}})|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}\cos{{\boldsymbol{t}}}^{\top}({{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{j}-{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{k})+\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{j,k=1}^{m}\cos{{\boldsymbol{t}}}^{\top}({{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{0,j}-{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{0,k})

\displaystyle-\frac{2}{nm}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\cos{{\boldsymbol{t}}}^{\top}({{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{j}-{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{0,k}).

Now suppose that the weight function
w(\cdot)
, figuring in (
2.3
) and (
2.5
), is chosen as the density of a random vector
{{\boldsymbol{W}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}

following a certain spherical distribution. Then it is well known that the CF of
{{\boldsymbol{W}}}
simplifies to
\varphi_{{{\boldsymbol{W}}}}({{\boldsymbol{t}}})=\mathbb{E}(\cos{{\boldsymbol{t}}}^{\top}{{\boldsymbol{W}}})
and it is eventually given by
\Psi(\|{{\boldsymbol{t}}}\|^{2})
, where
\Psi(\cdot)
is called the “kernel” associated with
{{\boldsymbol{W}}}
and
\|\cdot\|
stands for the standard Euclidean norm in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
(see Fang et al.
[
16
]
). By using the last equation and such a weight function
w(\cdot)
in (
2.5
), we end up with the test statistic

(2.7)

\displaystyle T^{(\Psi)}_{n,m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}\Psi(\|{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{j}-{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{k}\|^{2})+\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{j,k=1}^{m}\Psi(\|{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{0,j}-{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{0,k}\|^{2})

\displaystyle-\frac{2}{nm}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\Psi(\|{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{j}-{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{0,k}\|^{2}),

where we have made the dependence of the test statistic on the kernel
\Psi
explicit. Provided that the kernel
\Psi(\cdot)
is simple enough, (
2.7
) can be readily computed. Some prominent examples of simple kernels at our disposal are:

•

the standard normal kernel
\Psi(\xi)=e^{-\xi/2}
;

•

the kernel
\Psi(\xi)=e^{-\xi^{b/2}}
,
b\in(0,2)
, that originates from the stable distributions (see Nolan
[
40
]
);

•

and the generalized Laplace kernel
\Psi(\xi)=(1+\xi)^{-b},\ b>0
(see Kozubowski et al.
[
32
]
).

In the present work, we shall restrict our tests by making use only of the standard normal kernel. For a more in-depth discussion on kernels, we refer to Micchelli et al.
[
37
]
.

3
Test implementation by re-sampling

When some of the component parameters occurring in
{{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}}
can be standardized out, the asymptotic null distribution of the proposed test statistic
\widehat{T}^{(\Psi)}_{n,m}
in (
2.5
) does not depend on them. Such parameters are typically location and scatter parameters, while others, labeled as shape parameters, such as skewness and kurtosis, cannot usually be standardized out and, therefore, will ultimately affect the asymptotic null distribution of the test statistic (see Meintanis and Swanepoel
[
34
]
). In such cases, we can decompose the parameter vector as
{{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}}=({{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}})
, where
{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
denotes the non-shape parameters and
{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}
denotes the part of
{{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}}
that contains only shape parameters. In the presence of a canonical form of the distribution under test (see Section
4
), the asymptotic null distribution of the test statistic may be simulated by setting
{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}={{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{0}
, where
{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{0}
is some standard value of
{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
, and
{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}
is set equal to its value
\widetilde{{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}}:=\widetilde{{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}}({{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}},{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})
in the canonical form.

In the following, we outline the re-sampling procedure used within this work to approximate the test statistic’s asymptotic distribution under the null hypothesis and indicate how the test can be carried out in practice. For definiteness, and for fixed
(n,m,\Psi)
, we write the test statistic in (
2.7
) as
T({\cal{X}}_{n};{\cal{X}}_{0,m})
, where
{\cal{X}}_{n}=({{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{1},\ldots,{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{n})
denotes the observed data and
{\cal{X}}_{0,m}=({{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{0,1},\ldots,{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{0,m})
denotes the data generated from
{\cal{F}}_{\widehat{{{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}}}_{n}}
, i.e., from the null distribution with estimated parameters. We consider two cases of null hypotheses, one “composite” with all parameters being estimated, while the other will be labeled “simple”, although in this second case too, some, but not all, parameters are estimated.

3.1
Simple null hypothesis

Here we are interested in the goodness-of-fit testing problem associated with the simple null hypothesis

(3.1)

\displaystyle{\cal{H}}^{\rm s}_{0}:\mbox{the law of}\ {{\boldsymbol{X}}}\in{\cal{F}}_{{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}},\ \mbox{for a fixed}\ {{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}={{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}_{0},\mbox{ and for some}\ {{\boldsymbol{\theta}}};

and alternative hypothesis

(3.2)

\displaystyle{\cal{H}}^{\rm s}_{1}:\mbox{the law of}\ {{\boldsymbol{X}}}\not\in{\cal{F}}_{{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}},\ \mbox{for a fixed}\ {{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}={{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}_{0},\mbox{ and any}\ {{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}.

Although we labeled (
3.1
) as a simple null hypothesis, it should be pointed out that the parameter
{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
is left unspecified in
{\cal{H}}^{\rm s}_{0}
, and that our test procedure incorporates an estimation step for this parameter. For this case, the computation of critical points is based on simple Monte Carlo sampling from the distribution figuring in the null hypothesis. The steps of this Monte Carlo run are as follows:

Step 1 -

Generate a random sample
{\cal{X}}_{n}=\{{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{1},\ldots,{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{n}\}
from
{\cal{F}}_{{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{0},{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}_{0}}
, compute the estimate
\widehat{{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}_{n}
, and obtain the standardized sample
\widehat{\cal{X}}_{n}=(\widehat{{{\boldsymbol{X}}}}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{{{\boldsymbol{X}}}}_{n})
, where
\widehat{{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{j}}=\widehat{{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{j}}({{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{j}},\widehat{{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}_{n}),\ j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
.

Step 2 -

Generate a random sample
{\cal X}_{0,m}=\{{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{0,1},\ldots,{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{0,m}\}
from
{\cal{F}}_{{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{0},{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}_{0}}
.

Step 3 -

Compute the test statistic
T:=T^{\Psi}(\widehat{\cal{X}}_{n},{\cal X}_{0,m})
, according to (
2.7
).

Step 4 -

Repeat Steps 1-3 several times, say
M
, and obtain the set of test statistics
\{T_{1},\ldots,T_{M}
}. Then the critical point, say
\widehat{c}_{\delta}
, is defined as the
(1-\delta)\%
quantile of
(T_{m},\ m=1,\ldots,M)
.

Having obtained the empirical critical point,
\widehat{c}_{\delta}
is used to compute the test’s empirical powers. In this connection, we generate a random sample
{\cal X}_{n}=\{{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{1},\ldots,{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{n}\}
from any distribution belonging to the set of alternatives in the alternative hypothesis
{\cal{H}}^{\rm s}_{1}
, and perform Steps 1-3 above, thereby computing the test statistic
T
. We reject the null hypothesis
{\cal{H}}^{\rm s}_{0}
if
T>\widehat{c}_{\delta}
. We repeat this procedure several times, say
L
, and obtain the empirical power rate as
L^{-1}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\mathds{1}_{T_{\ell}>\widehat{c}_{\delta}}
, where
T_{\ell}
denotes the test statistic corresponding to the
\ell^{\rm th}
sample, for
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,L\}
.

3.2
Composite null hypothesis

Here we are interested in the (fully) composite goodness-of-fit testing problem whereby all distributional parameters are estimated from the observed data. For reasons of explicitness, we state the null hypothesis as

(3.3)

\displaystyle{\cal{H}}^{\rm c}_{0}:\mbox{the law of}\ {{\boldsymbol{X}}}\in{\cal{F}}_{{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}},\quad\mbox{for some}\ ({{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}});

as well as the alternative

(3.4)

\displaystyle{\cal{H}}^{\rm c}_{1}:\mbox{the law of}\ {{\boldsymbol{X}}}\not\in{\cal{F}}_{{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}},\quad\mbox{for any}\ ({{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}).

For this case, the re-sampling scheme is as follows. On the basis of
{\cal{X}}_{n}
, compute the estimator
\widehat{{{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}}}_{n}=(\widehat{{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}_{n},\widehat{{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}}_{n})
of
{{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}}
and standardize the observations as
\widehat{{{\boldsymbol{X}}}}_{j}=\widehat{{{\boldsymbol{X}}}}_{j}({{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{j},\widehat{{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}_{n})
,
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Now generate a random sample
\widehat{\cal{X}}_{0,m}:=(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{0,1},\ldots,\widehat{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{0,m})
under the null hypothesis with
({{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}})
set equal to
({{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{0},\widehat{\widetilde{{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}}}_{n})
, where
\widehat{\widetilde{{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}}}_{n}=\widehat{\widetilde{{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}}}_{n}(\widehat{{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}_{n},\widehat{{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}}_{n})
is the parameter estimate of
{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}
induced by the parametrization. Then the value of the original test statistic is computed according to (
2.7
) as
T=T(\widehat{\cal{X}}_{n};\widehat{\cal{X}}_{0,m})
, where
\widehat{\cal{X}}_{n}:=(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{n})
. In turn, the critical point against which the value of
T
will be compared is computed using a
parametric bootstrap
procedure, the steps of which are outlined below:

Step 1:

Generate a random sample
{\cal{X}}^{*}_{n}:=({{\boldsymbol{X}}}^{*}_{1},\ldots,{{\boldsymbol{X}}}^{*}_{n})
under the null hypothesis with
({{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}})
set equal to
({{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{0},\widehat{\widetilde{{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}}}_{n})
.

Step 2:

On the basis of
{\cal{X}}^{*}_{n}
, compute the estimator
\widehat{{{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}}}^{*}_{n}=(\widehat{{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}^{*}_{n},\widehat{{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}}^{*}_{n})
.

Step 3:

Standardize the components of
{\cal{X}}^{*}_{n}
as
\widehat{{{\boldsymbol{X}}}}^{*}_{j}=\widehat{{{\boldsymbol{X}}}}^{*}_{j}({{\boldsymbol{X}}}^{*}_{j},\widehat{{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}^{*}_{n})
,
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
.

Step 4:

Generate a random sample
\widehat{\cal{X}}^{*}_{0,m}:=(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{X}}}^{*}_{0,1},\ldots,\widehat{{\boldsymbol{X}}}^{*}_{0,m})
under the null hypothesis with
({{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}})
set equal to
({{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{0},\widehat{\widetilde{{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}}}^{*}_{n})
, where
\widehat{\widetilde{{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}}}^{*}_{n}=\widehat{\widetilde{{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}}}^{*}(\widehat{{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}^{*}_{n},\widehat{{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}}^{*}_{n})
is the bootstrap parameter estimate of
\widehat{\widetilde{{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}}}_{n}
.

Step 5:

Compute the value of the bootstrap test statistic by (
2.7
) as
T^{*}=T(\widehat{\cal{X}}^{*}_{n};\widehat{\cal{X}}^{*}_{0,m})
, where
\widehat{\cal{X}}^{*}_{n}:=(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{X}}}^{*}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{{\boldsymbol{X}}}^{*}_{n})
.

Step 6:

Steps 1-5 are repeated several times, say
B
, and thereby we compute the
(1-\delta)\%
quantile
c_{\delta}
, with
\delta\in(0,1)
, of the empirical distribution of
(T^{*}_{b},b=1,\ldots,B)
as the size-
\delta
critical value of the test statistic.

Step 7:

Repeat Steps 1-6 several times, say
M
, and thereby obtain pairs of test statistics and corresponding bootstrap critical points
(T_{m},c_{\delta,m})
,
m\in\{1,\ldots,M\}
.

Step 8:

Compute the empirical rejection rate as
M^{-1}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathds{1}_{T_{m}>c_{\delta,m}}
.

Because the above parametric bootstrap procedure is time-consuming, we adopt the
warp-speed bootstrap
method of Giacomini et al.
[
20
]
. Thus, rather than computing a critical value
c_{\delta,m}
for each of the
M
Monte Carlo samples, we produce a single critical value that is used for all Monte Carlo samples. To do so, we generate only one single bootstrap sample, i.e., with
B=1
on Step 6, for each of the
M
Monte Carlo samples and compute the corresponding bootstrap test statistic, say
T_{m}^{*}
, from this single bootstrap sample. Then the warp-speed critical value, say
\widetilde{c}_{\delta}
, is computed from
(T_{m}^{*},\ m\in\{1,\ldots,M\})
analogously as in Step 6 above, and the empirical rejection rate is given by
M^{-1}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathds{1}_{T_{m}>\widetilde{c}_{\delta}}
.

4
Families of skewed distributions

In this section, we consider a collection of five families of skewed distributions and exemplify how our method, described in Sections
2
and
3
, may be applied to perform goodness-of-fit tests with them. In the following Sections
5
and
6
, we shall use these five families of distributions, respectively, in simulation studies and applications to real data sets.

4.1
Multivariate skew-normal distribution

The multivariate skew-normal (SN) distribution may be conveniently defined by the CF (see Azzalini and Dalla Valle
[
6
]
)

(4.1)

\displaystyle\varphi_{{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}}({{\boldsymbol{t}}})=2e^{{\rm{i}}{{\boldsymbol{t}}}^{\top}{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}-\frac{1}{2}{{\boldsymbol{t}}}^{\top}{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}{{\boldsymbol{t}}}}\>\Phi\left({\rm{i}}\frac{{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}^{\top}{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}{{\boldsymbol{t}}}}{\sqrt{1+{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}^{\top}{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}}}\right),

where
\Phi(\cdot)
is the standard normal cumulative distribution function,
{{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}}:=({{\boldsymbol{\xi}}},{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}},{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}})
is the associated parameters vector, with
({{\boldsymbol{\xi}}},{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}})\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}
being, respectively, the location and skewness parameters, and where
{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
is a symmetric positive definite matrix. We shall write SN
{}_{p}({{\boldsymbol{\xi}}},{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}},{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}})
to denote this distribution, with
{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}={\boldsymbol{0}}
rendering the
p
-variate normal distribution with mean
{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}
and covariance matrix equal to
{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}
. To apply our test, a consistent estimator of the parameters in
{{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}}
is required. There exist a variety of methods for estimating them, including maximum-likelihood (MLE) and moments-based estimation methods (see, e.g., Azzalini and Capitanio
[
4
]
, Azzalini et al.
[
8
]
, and Flecher et al.
[
18
]
), as well as packages available for the same purpose (see Azzalini
[
3
]
). There also exist a few goodness-of-fit tests in this case, namely, the tests of Balakrishnan et al.
[
9
]
, González-Estrada et al.
[
21
]
, Jiménez-Gamero and Kim
[
27
]
, and Meintanis and Hlávka
[
35
]
which will be discussed further down in the paper (see Subsection
5.4
).

It may be shown that if
{{\boldsymbol{X}}}\sim{\rm{SN}}_{p}({{\boldsymbol{\xi}}},{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}},{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}})
, then there exists a matrix
{{\boldsymbol{\rm{H}}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
such that
{{\boldsymbol{\rm{H}}}}^{\top}({{\boldsymbol{X}}}-{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}})\sim{\rm{SN}}_{p}({\boldsymbol{0}},{{\boldsymbol{\rm{I}}}},{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}^{*})
, with
{\boldsymbol{0}}
and
{{\boldsymbol{\rm{I}}}}
denoting, respectively, the zero vector and identity matrix in the indicated dimension, and
{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}^{*}=(\alpha^{*},0,\ldots,0)^{\top}
, with
\alpha^{*}=({{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}^{\top}\bar{{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}}{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}})^{1/2}
and
{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}={\boldsymbol{\omega}}\bar{{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}}{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
, where
{\boldsymbol{\omega}}=\mbox{diag}(\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{p})
is a positive-definite scale matrix (see eq. (5.2) in Azzalini and Capitanio
[
5
]
). In the literature (see, e.g., Azzalini and Capitanio
[
5
]
and Capitanio
[
12
]
), the
{\rm{SN}}_{p}({\boldsymbol{0}},{{\boldsymbol{\rm{I}}}},{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}^{*})
distribution is also called the canonical form. Moreover, it was proved by Capitanio
[
12
]
that the choice of

(4.2)

\displaystyle{{\boldsymbol{\rm{H}}}}={{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}^{-1/2}{{\boldsymbol{\rm{Q}}}},

where
{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}^{-1/2}
is the unique inverse matrix of the positive definite symmetric square root matrix of
{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}
, and
{{\boldsymbol{\rm{Q}}}}
is obtained through the spectral decomposition
{{\boldsymbol{\rm{Q}}}}{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}{{\boldsymbol{\rm{Q}}}}^{\top}={{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}^{-1/2}{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}^{-1/2}
, with
{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}
being the covariance matrix of
{{\boldsymbol{X}}}
, leads to the conclusion that
{{\boldsymbol{\rm{H}}}}^{\top}({{\boldsymbol{X}}}-{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}})
follows a canonical skew-normal distribution. In this connection, write
\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}}_{n}=(\widehat{{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}}_{n},\widehat{{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}}_{n},\widehat{{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}}_{n})
for an estimator of
{{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}}
, and consider the standardized observations
\widehat{{{\boldsymbol{X}}}}_{j}=\widehat{{{\boldsymbol{\rm{H}}}}}_{n}^{\top}({{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{j}-\widehat{{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}}_{n}),j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, where
\widehat{{{\boldsymbol{\rm{H}}}}}_{n}=\widehat{{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}}_{n}^{-1/2}\widehat{{{\boldsymbol{\rm{Q}}}}}_{n}
. Then the test figuring in (
2.7
) is readily applied by replacing
{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{j}
by
\widehat{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{j}
, and where the
{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{0,j}
are drawn from a SN distribution with parameters
({{\boldsymbol{\xi}}},{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}})=({\boldsymbol{0}},{{\boldsymbol{\rm{I}}}})
and
{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}^{*}
, the latter being replaced by
\widehat{{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}}_{n}^{*}=(\widehat{\alpha}_{n}^{*},0,\ldots,0)^{\top}
, where
\widehat{\alpha}_{n}^{*}=(\widehat{{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}}_{n}^{\top}\widehat{\bar{{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}}}_{n}\widehat{{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}}_{n})^{1/2}
. For obtaining the estimates of
\widehat{{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}}_{n}
,
\widehat{{{\boldsymbol{\rm{H}}}}}_{n}
, and

4.2
Multivariate skew-t distribution

The multivariate skew-t (ST) distribution is related to the multivariate skew-normal distribution through the stochastic equation
{{\boldsymbol{Y}}}={{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}+\sqrt{\eta}{{\boldsymbol{X}}}
, where
{{\boldsymbol{X}}}
has a multivariate skew-normal distribution,
{{\boldsymbol{X}}}\sim{\rm{SN}}_{p}({\boldsymbol{0}},{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}},{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}})
, and
\eta
has an inverse-Gamma distribution with shape and scale parameters both equal to
\nu/2
, i.e.,
\eta\sim{\rm IG}(\nu/2,\nu/2)
. It was shown by Kim and Genton
[
30
]
, theorem 7, that the CF of
{{\boldsymbol{Y}}}
is given by

(4.3)

\displaystyle\varphi_{{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}}({{\boldsymbol{t}}})=\exp({\rm i}{{{\boldsymbol{t}}}}^{\top}{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}})[\psi_{T_{p}}({{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}^{1/2}{{\boldsymbol{t}}})+{\rm i}\tau^{+}({{\boldsymbol{\rho}}},\omega{{\boldsymbol{t}}})],

where

\displaystyle{{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}

\displaystyle={{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}/(1+{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}^{\top}{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}})^{1/2},

\displaystyle\psi_{T_{p}}({{\boldsymbol{t}}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\|\sqrt{\nu}{{\boldsymbol{t}}}\|^{\nu/2}}{\Gamma(\nu/2)\,2^{\nu/2-1}}\ K_{\nu/2}(\|\sqrt{\nu}{{\boldsymbol{t}}}\|),\quad\mbox{for }{{\boldsymbol{t}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p},\ \nu>0,

\displaystyle\tau^{+}({{\boldsymbol{\rho}}},\omega{{\boldsymbol{t}}})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp(-x{{\boldsymbol{t}}}^{\top}{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}{{\boldsymbol{t}}}/2)\,\tau(\sqrt{x}{{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}^{\top}\omega{{\boldsymbol{t}}})\,\mbox{d}H(x),\quad\mbox{for }{{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}^{\top}\omega{{\boldsymbol{t}}}>0,

with
\tau^{+}({{\boldsymbol{\rho}}},-\omega{{\boldsymbol{t}}})=-\tau^{+}({{\boldsymbol{\rho}}},\omega{{\boldsymbol{t}}})
,
\tau(x)=\int_{0}^{x}\sqrt{2/\pi}\exp(u^{2}/2)\,{{\rm d}}u
, for
x>0
, with
\tau(-x)=-\tau(x)
,
H(x)=\Gamma(\nu/2,\nu/(2x))/\Gamma(\nu/2)
, for
x>0
, denoting the cumulative distribution function of
\eta
, with
\Gamma(a)=\Gamma(a,0)
and where
\Gamma(a,b)=\int_{b}^{\infty}t^{a-1}e^{-t}\mbox{d}t
, for
b\geq 0
, represents the upper incomplete Gamma function, and
K_{\lambda}(\cdot)
is the integral representation of the modified Bessel function of the third kind, defined as
K_{\lambda}(w)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{\lambda-1}\exp\left\{-\frac{w}{2}\left(x+\frac{1}{x}\right)\right\}\mbox{d}x
, for
w>0
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. Here
{{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}}=({{\boldsymbol{\xi}}},{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}},{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}},\nu)
and we write
{{\boldsymbol{Y}}}\sim{\rm ST}_{p}({{\boldsymbol{\xi}}},{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}},{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}},\nu).

Since the multivariate skew-t distribution can be expressed as a scale mixture of a skew-normal distribution, proposition 2 in Capitanio
[
12
]
guarantees that by taking once again the matrix
{{\boldsymbol{\rm{H}}}}
as defined in (
4.2
), any random vector
{{\boldsymbol{Y}}}\sim
ST
{}_{p}({{\boldsymbol{\xi}}},{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}},{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}},\nu)
can be transformed into the canonical skew-t distribution
{{\boldsymbol{\rm{H}}}}^{\top}{{\boldsymbol{Y}}}\sim
ST
{}_{p}({\boldsymbol{0}},{{\boldsymbol{\rm{I}}}},{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}_{{\boldsymbol{Y}}}^{*},\nu)
. In particular,
{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}
and
{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}
, like in the skew-normal case, are nuisance parameters so that they can be dismissed for hypothesis testing after the standardization is performed. Additionally, for the simulation studies in Section
5
, it will suffice to implement the tests for different choices of
p
,
\nu
, and
\alpha^{*}
(the unique non-null component of
{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}_{{\boldsymbol{Y}}}^{*}=(\alpha^{*},0,\ldots,0)^{\top}
), substantially reducing the cases that need a proper investigation. Here the
sn

[
3
]
package in
R

[

]
can also be used to retrieve the desired multivariate skew-t’s parameters estimates as it was also designed for this purpose. The hypothesis test is then carried out similarly to the skew-normal case discussed in the preceding subsection.

4.3
Multivariate skew-Laplace distribution

The multivariate skew-Laplace (SL) distribution may be conveniently defined by the CF (see Arslan
[
2
]
)

(4.4)

\displaystyle\varphi_{{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}}({{\boldsymbol{t}}})=\frac{e^{{{\rm i}}{{\boldsymbol{t}}}^{\top}{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}}}{(1+{{\boldsymbol{t}}}^{\top}{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}\,{{\boldsymbol{t}}}-2{{\rm i}}{{\boldsymbol{t}}}^{\top}{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}})^{(p+1)/2}},

with
{{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}}=({{\boldsymbol{\xi}}},{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}},{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}})
, where
({{\boldsymbol{\xi}}},{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}})\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}
are, respectively, location and skewness parameters, and
{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}
is a symmetric positive definite matrix. We will use the notation SL
{}_{p}({{\boldsymbol{\xi}}},{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}},{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}})
for this distribution. Although the multivariate skew-Laplace distribution proposed by Arslan
[
2
]
has very similar properties to the distinct version introduced by Kotz et al.
[
31
]
, Arslan’s alternative has a simpler probability density function, allowing for uncomplicated estimation methods of its parameters in the multivariate setting. Also, Arslan
[
2
]
proposed an efficient EM algorithm that can be used for the estimation of
{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}},{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}},
and
{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}
.

Analogously to the skew-normal and skew-t distributions, provided that
{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\neq{\boldsymbol{0}}
, it may be shown that
{{\boldsymbol{X}}}\sim{{\rm SL}}_{p}({{\boldsymbol{\xi}}},{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}},{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}})
can be reduced to a canonical form. This novel result brings down the burden of testing for nuisance parameters, as well as reducing the skewness to a singular one-dimensional component.

Proposition 4.1

(Canonical form - SL
p
distribution)
.

Let
{{\boldsymbol{X}}}\sim\mbox{SL}_{p}({{\boldsymbol{\xi}}},{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}},{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}})
with
{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\neq{\boldsymbol{0}}
and consider the affine non-singular transform

(4.5)

{{\boldsymbol{X}}}^{*}={{\boldsymbol{\rm{H}}}}^{\top}({{\boldsymbol{X}}}-{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}),

with
{{\boldsymbol{\rm{H}}}}={{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}^{-1/2}{{\boldsymbol{\rm{Q}}}}
, where
{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}^{-1/2}
denotes the inverse of the unique positive definite symmetric square root matrix of
{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}
, and
{{\boldsymbol{\rm{Q}}}}=[{{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{1}\ldots{{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{p}]
is the orthogonal matrix with
{{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{1}={{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}^{-1/2}{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}/\|{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}^{-1/2}{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\|
as its first column vector and the remaining columns
{{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{2},\ldots,{{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{p}
belong to the orthogonal complement of
{{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{1}
. Then
{{\boldsymbol{X}}}^{*}\sim\mbox{SL}_{p}({\boldsymbol{0}},{{\boldsymbol{\rm{I}}}},{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}_{{\boldsymbol{X}}}^{*})
with
{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}_{{{\boldsymbol{X}}}}^{*}=(\alpha^{*},0,\ldots,0)^{\top}
and
\alpha^{*}={\|{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}^{-1/2}{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\|}
.

Proof.

From proposition 3 in Arslan
[
2
]
, if
{{\boldsymbol{\rm{A}}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
is any full rank matrix, it follows that
{{\boldsymbol{\rm{A}}}}({{\boldsymbol{X}}}-{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}})\sim\mbox{SL}_{p}({\boldsymbol{0}},{{\boldsymbol{\rm{A}}}}{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}{{\boldsymbol{\rm{A}}}}^{\top},{{\boldsymbol{\rm{A}}}}{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}})
. Since
{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{p})^{\top}\neq{\boldsymbol{0}}
and
{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}^{-1/2}
is non-singular, we have
{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}^{-1/2}{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\neq{\boldsymbol{0}}
. Hence, there is at least one component
u_{k}
in
{{\boldsymbol{u}}}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{p})^{\top}={{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}^{-1/2}{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}
that is non-null. Moreover, if
{\boldsymbol{e}}_{j}
represents the
j
th canonical vector in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
, for
j\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
, then
\{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\}\cup\{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:j\neq k\}
is a basis of
\mathbb{R}^{p}
. For ease of reading, let us rename the vectors
{{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{1}={{\boldsymbol{u}}}
,
{{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{j+1}={\boldsymbol{e}}_{j}
, for
1\leq j<k
, and
{{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{j}={\boldsymbol{e}}_{j}
, for
k<j\leq p
. Then we can apply the Gram-Schmidt process to find an orthonormal basis
\{{{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{1},\ldots,{{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{p}\}
of
\mathbb{R}^{p}
. For this, take
\tilde{{{\boldsymbol{v}}}}_{1}={{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{1}={{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}^{-1/2}{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}
and
\tilde{{{\boldsymbol{v}}}}_{j}={{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{j}-\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}\frac{\tilde{{{\boldsymbol{v}}}}_{i}^{\top}{{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{j}}{\tilde{{{\boldsymbol{v}}}}_{i}^{\top}\tilde{{{\boldsymbol{v}}}}_{i}}\tilde{{{\boldsymbol{v}}}}_{i}
, for
2\leq j\leq p
. The desired basis is obtained from the normalizations
{{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{j}=\tilde{{{\boldsymbol{v}}}}_{j}/\|\tilde{{{\boldsymbol{v}}}}_{j}\|
, for
j\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
. Therefore, taking
{{\boldsymbol{\rm{Q}}}}=[{{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{1}\ldots{{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{p}]
and
{{\boldsymbol{\rm{H}}}}={{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}^{-1/2}{{\boldsymbol{\rm{Q}}}}
, it follows that
{{\boldsymbol{\rm{H}}}}^{\top}({{\boldsymbol{X}}}-{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}})\sim\mbox{SL}_{p}({\boldsymbol{0}},{{\boldsymbol{\rm{H}}}}^{\top}{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}{{\boldsymbol{\rm{H}}}},{{\boldsymbol{\rm{H}}}}^{\top}{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}})
, with
{{\boldsymbol{\rm{H}}}}^{\top}{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}{{\boldsymbol{\rm{H}}}}={{\boldsymbol{\rm{Q}}}}^{\top}{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}^{-1/2}{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}\,{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}^{-1/2}{{\boldsymbol{\rm{Q}}}}={{\boldsymbol{\rm{I}}}}
and
{{\boldsymbol{\rm{H}}}}^{\top}{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}={{\boldsymbol{\rm{Q}}}}^{\top}{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}^{-1/2}{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}=(\|{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}^{-1/2}{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\|,0,\ldots,0)^{\top}
.
∎

Analogously to the SN and ST cases, the fact that a canonical form is available for the skew-Laplace distribution is useful in the implementation of the goodness-of-fit test due to a reduced number of parameters to be considered. Moreover, the proof of Proposition
4.1
gives at the same time an algorithm to find the canonical form of a multivariate SL
p
distribution. Taking this in consideration, let
\widehat{{{\boldsymbol{X}}}}_{j}=\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\rm{H}}}}_{n}^{\top}({{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{j}-\widehat{{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}}_{n})
,
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, be the standardized observations. Then the test figuring in (
2.7
) is applied by replacing
{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{j}
by
\widehat{{{\boldsymbol{X}}}}_{j}
, where
{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{0,j}
are drawn from a SL distribution with parameters
({{\boldsymbol{\xi}}},{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}})=({\boldsymbol{0}},{{\boldsymbol{\rm{I}}}})
and
{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}
is set equal to
(\|\widehat{{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}}_{n}^{-1/2}\widehat{{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}}_{n}\|,0,\ldots,0)^{\top}
. To obtain the estimates of
\widehat{{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}}_{n}
,
\widehat{{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}}_{n}
, and

, we suggest the EM algorithm proposed by Arslan
[
2
]
.

4.4
Multivariate Tukey
{{\boldsymbol{g}}}
-and-
{{\boldsymbol{h}}}
distribution

The multivariate Tukey
g
-and-
h
distribution (GH) was first introduced by Field and Genton
[
17
]
as a generalization of its univariate counterpart presented by Tukey in 1977. It has been gaining popularity due to its flexible marginal distributions, allowing for the fitting of skewed and heavy-tailed data sets from climate and environmental problems (see, e.g., Jeong et al.
[
26
]
, Yan and Genton
[
51
]
, and Yan et al.
[
52
]
). Given two parameter vectors
{{\boldsymbol{g}}}=(g_{1},\ldots,g_{p})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
{{\boldsymbol{h}}}=(h_{1},\ldots,h_{p})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}_{+}
, the random vector
{{\boldsymbol{Y}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is said to have a standard multivariate Tukey
g
-and-
h
distribution if it can be represented as

(4.6)

{{\boldsymbol{Y}}}={{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}}_{{{\boldsymbol{g}}},{{\boldsymbol{h}}}}({{\boldsymbol{Z}}}):=(\tau_{g_{1},h_{1}}(Z_{1}),\ldots,\tau_{g_{p},h_{p}}(Z_{p}))^{\top},

where
{{\boldsymbol{Z}}}=(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{p})^{\top}\sim{N}_{p}({\boldsymbol{0}},{{\boldsymbol{\rm{I}}}})
has a standard multivariate normal distribution and, for two given
g\in\mathbb{R}
and
h\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, the univariate function
\tau_{g,h}
is defined as

(4.7)

\tau_{g,h}(z)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\left(\frac{\exp(gz)-1}{g}\right)\exp\left(\frac{hz^{2}}{2}\right),&g\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\},\\[8.53581pt]
\displaystyle z\exp\left(\frac{hz^{2}}{2}\right),&g=0,\end{cases}

for any
z\in\mathbb{R}
. The general multivariate Tukey
g
-and-
h
distribution is then defined as

(4.8)

{{\boldsymbol{Y}}}={{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}\,{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}_{{{\boldsymbol{g}}},{{\boldsymbol{h}}}}({{\boldsymbol{Z}}})+{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}},

where
{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
and
{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
are, respectively, a positive definite matrix and a location vector. Here
{{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}}=({{\boldsymbol{\xi}}},{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}},{{\boldsymbol{g}}},{{\boldsymbol{h}}})
and the nuisance parameters are
{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}
and
{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}
as the can be standardised out. We shall use the notation GH
{}_{p}({{\boldsymbol{\xi}}},{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}},{{\boldsymbol{g}}},{{\boldsymbol{h}}})
.

In contrast with the SN, ST, and SL distributions, the Tukey
g
-and-
h
distribution does not have a known transformation that allows one to represent it in a reducible canonical form. Also, since the inverse of
\tau_{g,h}(\cdot)
does not have a closed expression, classical estimation methods, such as the MLE method, rely on numerical approximations. Likewise, the CF, probability density function, and cumulative distribution function can only be computed numerically. Several different techniques have been proposed for the estimation and fitting of the univariate Tukey
g
-and-
h
distribution (see the review paper by Möstel et al.
[
39
]
). However, for the multivariate case, only a few methods are available. For instance, Field and Genton
[
17
]
used multivariate quantiles for data fitting. He and Raghunathan
[
22
]
assumed that
{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}
is a diagonal matrix and, for this reason, they proposed an algorithm that uses quantiles from the univariate Tukey
g
-and-
h
.

In this work, we opted for the MLE method. For this purpose, we need to find the parameters
{{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}}
that maximize the log-likelihood function

(4.9)

\ell({{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}}|{{\boldsymbol{Y}}}_{1},\ldots,{{\boldsymbol{Y}}}_{n})=-n\ln|{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}|+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\left[\ln\left\{\phi(u_{i}^{(j)})\right\}-\ln\left\{\tau^{\prime}_{g_{j},h_{j}}(u_{i}^{(j)})\right\}\right],

where
u_{i}^{(j)}=\tau^{-1}_{g_{j},h_{j}}\left(\{{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}^{-1}({{\boldsymbol{Y}}}_{i}-{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}})\}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{j}\right)
, for
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
and
j\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
,
{\boldsymbol{e}}_{j}
denotes the
j
th canonical vector in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
,
\phi(\cdot)
is the pdf of a standard univariate normal distribution, and
\tau^{\prime}_{g_{j},h_{j}}(z)
denotes the derivative of
\tau_{g_{j},h_{j}}(z)
concerning its argument
z
. To give an approximated value of the quantities
u_{i}^{(j)}
, we use the
uniroot
function available in the
stats

[
45
]
package in
R

[
44
]
. For the maximization procedure, we use the
optim
function available in the
MASS

[
46
]
package in
R

[
44
]
, together with the “Nelder-Mead” method.

4.5
Multivariate
{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}
-stable distributions

The
\alpha
-stable distributions (AS), similar to the multivariate skew-t and Tukey
g
-and-
h
distributions, are another possible extension of the multivariate Gaussian distribution that comports skewness. However, while the multivariate skew-t family has finite second moments, the
\alpha
-stable distributions are regulated by a parameter
\alpha\in(0,2]
, called the tail index, and it has only finite second moments if
\alpha=2
, which reduces itself to the multivariate Gaussian case. Particularly, if
{{\boldsymbol{X}}}
has a multivariate
\alpha
-stable distribution, then
{\mathbb{E}}(\|{{\boldsymbol{X}}}\|^{s})<\infty
, if
0<s<\alpha
, and
{\mathbb{E}}(\|{{\boldsymbol{X}}}\|^{s})=\infty
, if
s\geq\alpha
. Several parameterizations for the CF have been proposed in the literature. Here, for numerical reasons, we adopt the S
0
parameterization introduced in Abdul-Hamid and Nolan
[
1
]
, for which the CF is given by

(4.10)

\varphi_{{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}}({{\boldsymbol{t}}})=\exp\left(-\int_{\mathcal{S}_{p}}\psi_{\alpha}({{\boldsymbol{t}}}^{\top}{{\boldsymbol{s}}})\,\Gamma(d{{\boldsymbol{s}}})+{\rm i}{{\boldsymbol{t}}}^{\top}{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}\right),\quad{{\boldsymbol{t}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p},

where

(4.11)

\psi_{\alpha}(u)=\begin{cases}|u|^{\alpha}\Big(1+{\rm i}\,\mbox{sign}(u)\tan\left(\frac{\pi\alpha}{2}\right)\left(|u|^{1-\alpha}-1\right)\Big),&\alpha\neq 1,\\
|u|\penalty\ \Big(1+{\rm i}\frac{2}{\pi}\mbox{sign}(u)\ln|u|\Big),&\alpha=1.\end{cases}

In this case, we denote
{{\boldsymbol{X}}}\sim{\rm S}_{\alpha}^{0}(\Gamma,{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}})
to indicate that the random vector
{{\boldsymbol{X}}}
has an
\alpha
-stable distribution with finite spectral measure
\Gamma(\cdot)
, defined on the unitary sphere
\mathcal{S}_{p}:=\{{{\boldsymbol{s}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\|{{\boldsymbol{s}}}\|=1\}
, and shift vector
{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
. We note from (
4.10
) that the multivariate
\alpha
-stable distributions are a semi-parametric family, being completely defined by the triplet
(\alpha,\Gamma,{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}})
and belonging to the more general class of infinitely divisible distributions. Furthermore,
{{\boldsymbol{X}}}-{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}\sim{\rm S}_{\alpha}^{0}(\Gamma,{\boldsymbol{0}})
, so that
{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}
is a nuisance parameter vector and it coincides with the mean vector when
1<\alpha\leq 2
.

Byczkowski et al.
[
11
]
proved that an approximation can be given for the spectral measure
\Gamma
, being useful for numerical computations and simulations. This approximation is described as follows: consider a finite partition
P=\{A_{1},\ldots,A_{m}\}
of
\mathcal{S}_{p}
and a set of points
S=\{{{\boldsymbol{s}}}_{1},\ldots,{{\boldsymbol{s}}}_{m}\}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{p}
. Then, by setting
\gamma_{i}=\Gamma(A_{i})
, the discrete spectral measure associated to
(P,S,\Gamma)
is defined as

(4.12)

\Gamma^{*}(\cdot)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Gamma(A_{i})\,\mathds{1}_{{{\boldsymbol{s}}}_{i}}(\cdot)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\gamma_{i}\,\mathds{1}_{{{\boldsymbol{s}}}_{i}}(\cdot),

for any Borelian in
\mathcal{S}_{p}
. Here
\Gamma^{*}
is implicitly defined by concentrating the mass of
\Gamma(A_{i})
at each point
{{\boldsymbol{s}}}_{i}
, the only requirement being that
\sup_{{{\boldsymbol{s}}}\in A_{i}}|{{\boldsymbol{s}}}-{{\boldsymbol{s}}}_{i}|
is sufficiently small, for each
i=1,\ldots,m
. Hence, the discrete spectral measure
\Gamma^{*}
can be used in practice instead of its continuous counterpart
\Gamma
. For simulating multivariate
\alpha
-stable random vectors, we shall use the result from Modarres and Nolan
[
38
]
which states that, if
{{\boldsymbol{X}}}\sim{\rm S}_{\alpha}^{0}(\Gamma^{*},{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}})
, with
\Gamma^{*}(\cdot)
defined in (
4.12
), then

(4.13)

{{\boldsymbol{X}}}=\begin{cases}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\gamma_{i}^{1/\alpha}Z_{i}{{\boldsymbol{s}}}_{i}+\tilde{{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}},&\alpha\neq 1,\\
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\gamma_{i}(Z_{i}+\frac{2}{\pi}\ln(\gamma_{i})){{\boldsymbol{s}}}_{i}+\tilde{{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}},&\alpha=1,\end{cases}

where
\tilde{{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}}={{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}-\tan\left(\frac{\pi\alpha}{2}\right)\sum_{i=1}^{m}\gamma_{i}{{\boldsymbol{s}}}_{i}
and
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{m}
are i.i.d. one-dimensional
\alpha
-stable random variables with
Z_{i}\sim{\rm S}_{\alpha}(1,1,0)
(i.e., scale = skewness = 1 and location = 0). As for the estimation part, we use the projection method proposed by Nolan et al.
[
41
]
, which relies on the projections of the multivariate samples into a specifically chosen grid of values from the unitary sphere.

As it is well known,
{{\boldsymbol{X}}}\sim{\rm S}_{\alpha}^{0}(\Gamma,{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}})
is symmetric if and only if
\Gamma(\cdot)
is symmetric on
\mathcal{S}_{p}
. So far, no tests available in the literature have been designed specifically for the general asymmetric multivariate
\alpha
-stable distributions, except for the one presented by Meintanis et al. (2015) which covers only the symmetric case. Here we propose a test that can be used for both symmetric and asymmetric cases. Although it is not usual in the literature, we use the notation
{{\boldsymbol{X}}}\sim{\rm AS}_{p}({{\boldsymbol{\xi}}},\Gamma,\alpha)
to indicate that
{{\boldsymbol{X}}}
has an asymmetric
\alpha
-stable distribution. For a more substantial review of these and further technical details concerning multivariate
\alpha
-stable distributions, we suggest reading Karling et al.
[
29
]
and Samorodnitsky and Taqqu
[
47
]
.

5.4
Comparison over competitor tests

Although the major tests proposed in the literature are restricted to the SN family, we dedicate this subsection to discussing and comparing our test to these alternatives. In a retrospective overview, Meintanis and Hlávka
[
35
]
introduced one of the first goodness-of-fit tests for the family of multivariate skew-normal distributions which utilizes the empirical moment-generating function. However, the computational formulas for this test are practically restricted to the two-dimensional case since the case
p>2
requires a solution of a differential equation that is difficult to be numerically evaluated. Later on, Balakrishnan et al.
[
9
]
proposed a test that is based on the skew-normal’s canonical form. Its main advantage relies on the argument that no re-sampling step is needed, saving computational time. However, it is only valid for the skew-normal distribution as the test is based on the fact that the ratios
X_{i,j}/|X_{i,1}|
, for
j\in\{2,\ldots,p\}
, of the observations
{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{i}=(X_{i,1},\ldots,X_{i,p})^{\top}
, for
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, are distributed as Cauchy random variables if the
{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{i}
’s follow a canonical skew-normal distribution. More recently, González-Estrada et al.
[
21
]
introduced two randomized tests that, similarly to Balakrishnan et al.
[
9
]
’s test, are based on the estimated canonical form of the SN distribution. The first test (W) applies the principle of a generalization of the Shapiro-Wilk test after the sample is transformed into approximately multivariate standard normal observations, whereas the second test (S) relies on a closure property of the sum of univariate independent skew-normal and normal random variables. Jiménez-Gamero and Kim
[
27
]
proposed a pair of re-sampling schemes, one of which is the parametric bootstrap (PB in the paper) which is what we also use in our tests.

In the next simulation, we replicate one of the original simulation studies from Balakrishnan et al.
[
9
]
and compare the estimated powers of our test with the ones reported by these authors and the ones given in González-Estrada et al.
[
21
]
.

Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed a goodness-of-fit test for several types of multivariate skewed distributions. On the one hand, the major advantage of the technique addressed in our work resides in the fact that it is flexible and can be applied to any multivariate parametric family of distributions, provided that a reasonable method of estimation of its parameters is available and that the generation of new replicates is feasible. On the other hand, in terms of computational cost, the implementation is highly demanding since the parametric bootstrap step requires an extra cycle of re-sampling within each Monte Carlo run.

While the need for such nested re-sampling is shared by most goodness-of-fit tests available in the literature, this drawback can be easily circumvented with the use of a parallel algorithm, since the parametric bootstrap does not require any sequential procedures, and with the use of the warp-speed bootstrap method of Giacomini et al.
[
20
]
. An important fact to be mentioned is that all tests were run with the help of an Intel Xeon Gold 6230R CPU, of which 100 out of its 104 threads have been intensively used to accelerate even more the completion of the simulations. We demonstrated its effectiveness through five families of multivariate distributions, namely, the multivariate skew-normal, skew-t, asymmetric skew-Laplace, skew
\alpha
-stable, and Tukey
g
-and-
h
(for most of which there are no available tests), by utilizing the corresponding canonical forms whenever possible.

As the simulations in Subsection
5.1
show (see Table
1
), the estimated sizes of the test are reasonable and consistent for all five families. Similarly, the simulations presented in Subsections
5.2
and
5.3
show that the tests have enough power to detect and reject alternative hypotheses. Compared to the alternative options of tests introduced in the literature, as presented in Subsection
5.4
, for the particular case when testing under the composite null hypothesis of an SN distribution, our test has also shown to be better in terms of power when testing against the alternative ST distribution.

The effectiveness of our tests has also been illustrated with real data examples in Section
6
, showing its applicability and usefulness when applied to biological and natural events observed, respectively, in our daily lives and our environment. In closing we wish to remind the reader that our test allows a certain flexibility concerning the actual kernel
\Psi
used; refer to the last paragraph of Section
2
. In this connection, it would be interesting to investigate the effect that this choice has on the finite-sample properties of our test. More work is needed in this direction.
