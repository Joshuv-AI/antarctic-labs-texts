Asymptotic Analysis of Deep Residual Networks Alain Rossier’s research was supported through EPSRC Centre for Doctoral Training in Mathematics of Random Systems: Analysis, Modelling and Simulation (EP/S023925/1).
Rama Cont, Alain Rossier, Renyuan Xu

Abstract
We investigate the asymptotic properties of deep Residual networks (ResNets) as the number of layers increases. We first show the existence of scaling regimes for trained weights markedly different from those implicitly assumed in the neural ODE literature. We study the convergence of the hidden state dynamics in these scaling regimes, showing that one may obtain an ODE, a stochastic differential equation (SDE) or neither of these. In particular, our findings point to the existence of a diffusive regime in which the deep network limit is described by a class of stochastic differential equations (SDEs). Finally, we derive the corresponding scaling limits for the backpropagation dynamics.



1
Introduction

Residual networks, or ResNets, are multilayer neural network architectures in which a
skip connection
is introduced at every layer (
[
13
]
). This allows very deep networks to be trained by circumventing vanishing and exploding gradients, mentioned in
[
3
]
. The increased depth in ResNets has lead to commensurate performance gains in applications ranging from speech recognition
[
14
,
33
]
to computer vision
[
13
,
16
]
.

A residual network with
L
layers may be represented as

h^{(L)}_{k+1}=h^{(L)}_{k}+\delta^{(L)}_{k}\sigma_{d}\left(A^{(L)}_{k}h^{(L)}_{k}+b^{(L)}_{k}\right),

(1.1)

where
h^{(L)}_{k}
is the hidden state at layer
k=0,\ldots,L
,
h^{(L)}_{0}=x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
the input,
h^{(L)}_{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
the output,
\sigma\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
a nonlinear activation function,
\sigma_{d}(x)=(\sigma(x_{1}),\ldots,\sigma(x_{d}))^{\top}
its component-wise extension to
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
A_{k}^{(L)}
,
b_{k}^{(L)}
, and
\delta_{k}^{(L)}
trainable network weights for
k=0,\ldots,L-1
.

ResNets have been the focus of several theoretical studies due to a perceived link with a class of differential equations. The idea, put forth in
[
12
]
and
[
4
]
, is to view (
1.1
) as a discretization of a system of ordinary differential equations

\frac{\mathrm{d}H_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\sigma_{d}\left(\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muA\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu_{t}H_{t}+\overline{b}_{t}\right),

(1.2)

where
\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muA\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and
\overline{b}\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
are appropriate smooth functions and
H(0)=x
.
This may be justified (
[
31
]
) by assuming that

\delta^{(L)}\sim 1/L,\quad A_{k}^{(L)}\sim\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muA\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu_{k/L},\quad b_{k}^{(L)}\sim\overline{b}_{k/L}

(1.3)

as
L
increases. Such models, named neural ordinary differential equations or neural ODEs
[
4
,
7
]
, have motivated the use of optimal control methods to train ResNets
[
8
]
.

However, the precise link between deep ResNets and the neural ODE (
1.2
) is unclear: in practice, the weights
A^{(L)}
and
b^{(L)}
result from training, yet the validity of the scaling assumptions (
1.3
) for trained weights is far from obvious.
As a matter of fact, there is empirical evidence showing that using a scaling factor
\delta^{(L)}\sim 1/L
can deteriorate the network accuracy
[
2
]
. Also, there is no guarantee that weights obtained through training have a non-zero limit which depends smoothly on the layer, as (
1.3
) would require. In fact, for ResNet architectures used in practice, empirical evidence points to the contrary
[
5
]
. These observations motivate an in-depth examination of the actual scaling behavior of weights with network depth in ResNets and of its impact on the asymptotic behavior of those networks.

Contributions.

We systematically investigate the scaling behavior of trained networks weights and examine in detail the consequence of this scaling for the asymptotic properties of ResNets as the number of layers increases.
We first show, through detailed numerical experiments,
the existence of scaling regimes for trained weights markedly different from those implicitly assumed in the neural ODE literature. We study the convergence of the hidden state dynamics in these scaling regimes, showing that one may obtain an ODE, a stochastic differential equation (SDE) or neither of these. More precisely, we show strong convergence of the hidden state dynamics to a limiting ODE or SDE, by viewing the discrete hidden state dynamics as a “nonlinear Euler scheme” of the limiting equation.
At a mathematical level, we extend the convergence analysis of Higham et al.
[
15
]
for discretization schemes of time-homogeneous (Markov) diffusions to a class of nonlinear approximations for Itô processes with bounded coefficients.

In particular, our findings point to the existence of a “diffusive regime” in which the deep network limit is described by a class of stochastic differential equations (SDEs). These novel findings on the relation between ResNets and neural ODEs complement previous work
[
31
,
9
,
11
,
25
,
29
]
.
Finally, we derive the corresponding scaling limit for the backpropagation dynamics.
The results we obtain are different from previous ones on asymptotics of ResNets
[
4
,
12
,
23
]
, and correspond to a different scaling regime which is relevant for trained weights in practical settings.

In particular, in the diffusive regime we find a limit different from the “Neural SDE” literature
[
21
]
. Indeed, we observe that the Jacobian of the output with respect to the hidden states depends on hidden states across all levels, so may
not
be directly expressed as the solution of a forward or backward stochastic differential equation, as proposed in
[
21
]
.
However, in Section
5
we obtain a representation for the asymptotics of the backpropagation dynamics in terms of an auxiliary forward SDE.

Outline.

Section
2
describes the various scaling regimes for trained weights evidenced in
[
5
]
and the methodology for studying this scaling behaviour in the deep network limit.
In Section
3
, we report detailed numerical experiments on the scaling of trained network weights across a range of ResNet architectures and datasets, showing the existence of at least three different scaling regimes, none of which correspond to (
1.3
). In Section
4
, we show that under these scaling regimes, the dynamics of the the hidden state may be described in terms of a class of ordinary or stochastic differential equations, different from the neural ODEs studied in
[
4
,
12
,
23
]
. In Section
5
, we derive the large depth limit of the backpropagation dynamics under each scaling regime.

2
Scaling regimes

We start by providing a framework for describing the scaling regimes for trained network weights, as identified in numerical experiments on deep ResNets
[
5
]
.

2.1
Scaling regimes for trained network weights

As described in Section
1
, the neural ODE limit assumes

\delta^{(L)}\sim\frac{1}{L}\quad{\rm and}\quad A^{(L)}_{\left\lfloor Lt\right\rfloor}\overset{L\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muA\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu_{t},\quad b^{(L)}_{\left\lfloor Lt\right\rfloor}\overset{L\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\overline{b}_{t}

(2.1)

for
t\in[0,1]
, where
\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muA\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and
\overline{b}\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
are smooth functions
[
31
]
. Our numerical experiments, detailed in Section
3
, show that the norm of the weights generally shrinks as
L
increases (see for example Figures
2
and
4
), so one cannot expect the above assumption to hold, unless weights are renormalized in some way.
We consider here a more general assumption which includes (
2.1
) but allows for shrinking weights.

Scaling regime 1
.

There exist
\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muA\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu\in\mathcal{C}^{0}\left([0,1],\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\right)
and
\beta\in\left[0,1\right]
such that

\forall s\in[0,1],\qquad\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muA\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu_{s}=\lim_{L\to\infty}L^{\beta}\,A^{(L)}_{\left\lfloor Ls\right\rfloor}.

(2.2)

Figure 1:
Trained weights as a function of
k/L
for
k=0,\ldots,L
and
L=9100
. Left: rescaled weights
L^{\beta}A^{(L)}_{k,(0,0)}
for a
\tanh
network with
\beta=0.2
. Right: cumulative sum
\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}A^{(L)}_{j,(0,0)}
for a
\mathrm{ReLU}
network. Note that each
A^{(L)}_{k,(0,0)}\in\mathbb{R}
.

These renormalized weights do converge to a continuous function of the layer in some cases, as shown in Figure
1
(top) which displays a ResNet (
1.1
) with fully connected layers and
\tanh
activation function, without explicit regularization (see Section
3.2
).

Yet, it is not the case that network weights always converge to a smooth function of the layer, even after rescaling. Indeed, network weights
A_{k}^{(L)}
are usually initialized to random, independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) values, whose scaling limit would then correspond to a
white noise
, which cannot be represented as a function of the layer. In this case, the
cumulative sum

\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}A^{(L)}_{j}
of the weights behaves like a random walk, which does have a well-defined scaling limit
W\in\mathcal{C}^{0}\left(\left[0,1\right],\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\right)
.
Figure
1
(bottom) shows that, for a
\mathrm{ReLU}
ResNet with fully-connected layers, this cumulative sum of trained weights converges to an
irregular
, that is, non-smooth function of the layer.

This observation motivates the consideration of a different scaling regime where the weights
A^{(L)}_{k}
are represented as the
increments
of a continuous function
W^{A}
, i.e. the
cumulative sum
of the weights may converges to a limit but not the weight themselves. We also allow for a
trend
term as in Scaling regime
1
.

Scaling regime 2
.

There exist
\beta\in\left[0,1\right)
,
\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muA\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu\in\mathcal{C}^{0}\left([0,1],\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\right)
, and
W^{A}\in\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,1],\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})
non-zero such that
W^{A}_{0}=0
and

A_{k}^{(L)}=L^{-\beta}\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muA\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu_{k/L}+W^{A}_{(k+1)/L}-W^{A}_{k/L}.

(2.3)

The above decomposition is unique. Indeed, for
s\in[0,1]
,

\displaystyle L^{\beta-1}\sum_{k=0}^{\left\lfloor Ls\right\rfloor-1}A_{k}^{(L)}

\displaystyle=L^{-1}\sum_{k=0}^{\left\lfloor Ls\right\rfloor-1}\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muA\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu_{k/L}+L^{\beta-1}W^{A}_{\left\lfloor Ls\right\rfloor/L}\mathop{\to}^{L\to\infty}\int_{0}^{s}\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muA\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu_{r}\mathrm{d}r.

(2.4)

The integral of
\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muA\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu
is thus uniquely determined by the weights
A_{k}^{(L)}
, so
\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muA\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu
can be obtained by discretization and
W^{A}
by fitting the residual error in (
2.4
). In addition, Scaling Regimes
1
and
2
are mutually exclusive since Scaling regime
2
requires
W^{A}
to be non-zero.

Remark 1
.

In the case of independent Gaussian weights

A_{k,mn}^{(L)}\overset{{\tiny\text{i.i.d}}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}\left(0,L^{-1}d^{-2}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad b_{k,n}^{(L)}\overset{{\tiny\text{i.i.d}}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}\left(0,L^{-1}d^{-1}\right),

where
A_{k,mn}^{(L)}
is the
(m,n)
-th entry of
A_{k}^{(L)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and
b_{k,n}^{(L)}
is the
n
-th entry of
b_{k}^{(L)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
we can represent the weights
\{A^{(L)},b^{(L)}\}
as the increments of a matrix-valued Brownian motion

A_{k}^{(L)}=d^{-1}\left(W^{A}_{(k+1)/L}-W^{A}_{k/L}\right),

which is a special case of Scaling regime 2.

This remark shows that Scaling regime 2 corresponds to a ’diffusive’ regime.

3
Scaling behavior of trained weights: numerical experiments

We now report on detailed numerical experiments to investigate the scaling properties and asymptotic behavior of trained weights for residual networks as the number of layers increases. We focus on two types of architectures: fully-connected and convolutional networks.

3.1
Methodology

We underline that Scaling Regimes
1
and
2
are mutually exclusive since Scaling regime
2
requires
W^{A}
to be non-zero. In order to examine whether one of these scaling regimes, or neither, holds for the trained weights
A^{(L)}
and
b^{(L)}
, we proceed as follows.

Step 1:
First, to obtain the scaling exponent
\beta\in[0,1)
, note that under Scaling regime
2
,

L^{\beta-1}\sum_{k=1}^{L}A_{k}^{(L)}=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{k=1}^{L}\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muA\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu_{k/L}+L^{\beta-1}W^{A}_{1}\qquad\mathop{\to}^{L\to\infty}\int_{0}^{1}\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muA\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu_{s}\mathrm{d}s.

Hence, we perform a logarithmic regression of the cumulative sum norm of
A^{(L)}
with respect to
L
, and the rate of increase of
\sum_{k=1}^{L}A_{k}^{(L)}
as
L\to\infty
is
1-\beta
.

Step 2:
After identifying the correct scale
L^{-\beta}
for the weights, we compute the
\beta
-scaled norm of increments of
A^{(L)}
to check whether they satisfy Scaling regime
1
and measure the smoothness of the trained weights. On one hand, if the
\beta
-scaled norm of increments of
A^{(L)}
does not vanish as
L\to\infty
, it means that the rescaled weights cannot be represented as a continuous function of the layer, as in Scaling regime
1
. On the other hand, if the
\beta
-scaled norm of increments of
A^{(L)}
vanishes (say, as
L^{-\nu}
) when
L
increases, it supports Scaling regime
1
with a Hölder-continuous limit function
\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muA\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu\in\mathcal{C}^{\nu}([0,1],\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})
.

Step 3:
To discriminate between Scaling regimes
1
and
2
, we decompose the cumulative sum
\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}A_{j}^{(L)}
of the trained weights into a
trend
component
\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muA\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu
and a
noise
component
W^{A}
, as shown in (
2.4
). The presence of non-negligible noise term
W^{A}
favors Scaling regime
2
.

Step 4:
Finally, we estimate the regularity of the term
W^{A}
under Scaling regime
2
. If
W^{A}
has
diffusive
behavior, as in the example of i.i.d. random weights, then its quadratic variation tensor defined by

\displaystyle\left[W^{A}\right]_{s}

\displaystyle=\lim_{L\to\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{\left\lfloor Ls\right\rfloor-1}\left(W^{A}_{\frac{k+1}{L}}-W^{A}_{\frac{k}{L}}\right)\otimes\left(W^{A}_{\frac{k+1}{L}}-W^{A}_{\frac{k}{L}}\right)^{\top}

has a finite limit as
L\to\infty
. Hence, using (
2.3
) and Cauchy-Schwarz, we obtain

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\left[W^{A}\right]_{s}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}\leq 2\cdot\lim_{L\to\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{\left\lfloor Ls\right\rfloor-1}\left\lVert A^{(L)}_{k}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}+L^{1-2\beta}\left\lVert\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muA\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}

(3.1)

where
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\cdot\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}
is the Hilbert-Schmidt norm. As
\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muA\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu
is continuous on a compact domain, its
L^{2}
norm is finite. Hence, if we have
\beta\geq\frac{1}{2}
, the fact that the root sum of squares of
A^{(L)}
is upper bounded as
L\to\infty
implies that the quadratic variation of
W^{A}
is finite.

We follow all of the above steps for
b^{(L)}
as well. Note that the scaling exponent
\beta
may not be the same for
A^{(L)}
and
b^{(L)}
.

Remark 2
.

Note that
\sigma=\mathrm{ReLU}
is homogeneous of degree
1
, so we can write

\delta\cdot\sigma_{d}\left(Ah+b\right)=\mathrm{sign}(\delta)\cdot\sigma_{d}\left(\left|\delta\right|Ah+\left|\delta\right|b\right).

Hence, when analyzing the scaling of trained weights in the case of a
\mathrm{ReLU}
activation with fully-connected layers, we look at the quantities
\left|\delta^{(L)}\right|A^{(L)}
and
\left|\delta^{(L)}\right|b^{(L)}
, as they represent the total scaling of the residual connection.

3.2
Results for fully-connected layers

We first consider the case where the network layers are fully-connected. We consider the network architecture (
1.1
) for two different setups:

(i)

\sigma=\tanh
,
\delta^{(L)}_{k}=\delta^{(L)}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
trainable,

(ii)

\sigma=\mathrm{ReLU}
,
\delta^{(L)}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
trainable.

We choose to present these two cases for the following reasons. First, both
\tanh
and
\mathrm{ReLU}
are widely used in practice. Further, having
\delta^{(L)}
scalar makes the derivation of the limiting behavior simpler. Also, since
\tanh
is an odd function, the sign of
\delta^{(L)}
can be absorbed into the activation. Therefore, we can assume that
\delta^{(L)}
is non-negative for
\tanh
. Regarding
\mathrm{ReLU}
, having a shared
\delta^{(L)}
would hinder the expressiveness of the network. Indeed, if for instance
\delta^{(L)}>0
, we would get
h^{(L)}_{k+1}\geq h^{(L)}_{k}
element-wise since
\mathrm{ReLU}
is non-negative. This would imply that
h^{(L)}_{L}\geq x
, which is not desirable. The same argument applies to the case
\delta^{(L)}<0
. Thus, we let
\delta^{(L)}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
depend on the layer number for
\mathrm{ReLU}
networks.

We consider two data sets. The first one is synthetic: fix
d=10
and generate
N
i.i.d samples
x_{i}
coming from the
d-
dimensional uniform distribution in
[-1,1]^{d}
. Let
K=100
and simulate the following dynamical system:

\begin{cases}z_{0}^{x_{i}}&=x_{i}\\
z_{k}^{x_{i}}&=z_{k-1}^{x_{i}}+K^{-1/2}\tanh_{d}\left(g_{d}\left(z_{k-1}^{x_{i}},k,K\right)\right),\,\,\,k=1,\ldots,K,\end{cases}

where
g_{d}(z,k,K)\coloneqq\sin(5k\pi/K)z+\cos(5k\pi/K)\mathbbm{1}_{d}
. The targets
y_{i}
are defined as
y_{i}=z_{K}^{x_{i}}/\left\lVert z_{K}^{x_{i}}\right\rVert
. The motivation behind this low-dimensional dataset is to be able to train very deep residual networks and to be sure that there exists at least a (sparse) optimal solution.

The second dataset is a low-dimensional embedding of the MNIST handwritten digits dataset
[
20
]
. Let
\left(\widetilde{x},c\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{28\times 28}\times\left\{0,\ldots,9\right\}
be an input image and its corresponding class. We transform
\widetilde{x}
into a lower dimensional embedding
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
using an untrained convolutional projection, where
d=25
. More precisely, we stack two convolutional layers initialized randomly, we apply them to the input and we flatten the downsized image into a
d-
dimensional vector. Doing so reduces the dimensionality of the problem while allowing very deep networks to reach at least
99\%
training accuracy. The target
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the one-hot encoding of the corresponding class.

The weights are updated by stochastic gradient descent (SGD) using batches of size
B
on the mean-square loss and a constant learning rate
\eta
, until the loss falls below
\epsilon
, or when the maximum number of updates
T_{\max}
is reached. We repeat the experiments for depths
L
varying from
L_{\min}
to
L_{\max}
. Details are given in Appendix
A
.

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle 2^{p_{0}-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq s\leq 1}\left\lVert\left(\int_{0}^{s}\sum_{k,l=1}^{d}\left(q_{t}^{A}\right)_{ijkl}\left(\mathrm{d}B^{A}_{t}\right)_{kl}\right)_{i,j}\right\rVert^{p_{0}}\right]

(i)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq s\leq 1}\left\lVert\left(\int_{0}^{s}\sum_{k,l=1}^{d}\left(q_{t}^{A}\right)_{ijkl}\left(\mathrm{d}B^{A}_{t}\right)_{kl}\right)_{i,j}\right\rVert^{p_{0}}\right]\leq C_{p_{0}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{1}\Sigma_{u}^{A}\mathrm{d}u\right)^{p_{0}/2}\right]\leq C_{p_{0}}\,C_{1}^{p_{0}/2}.

Combining the two inequalities above, we get

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq s\leq 1}\left\lVert W^{A}_{s}\right\rVert^{p_{0}}\right]<\infty
. Similarly
\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq s\leq 1}\left\lVert W^{b}_{s}\right\rVert^{p_{0}}\right]<\infty
holds.
∎

Write
Q:[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where each component
Q_{i}
is defined, for
i=1,\ldots,d
, as

Q_{i}(t,x)\coloneqq\sum_{j,k=1}^{d}x_{j}x_{k}\left(\Sigma^{A}_{t}\right)_{ijik}+\Sigma^{b}_{t,ii}.

(4.13)

Let
\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muH\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern-1.0mu^{(L)}:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a continuous-time extension of the hidden states
h^{(L)}_{k}
:

\displaystyle\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muH\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern-1.0mu^{(L)}_{t}\coloneqq h_{k}^{x,(L)}\mathbbm{1}_{\frac{k}{L}\leq t<\frac{k+1}{L}},\quad k=0,1,\ldots,L.

(4.14)

Assumption 3

(Uniform integrability)
.

There exist
p_{1}>4
and a constant
C_{0}
such that for all
L
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\left\lVert\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muH\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern-1.0mu^{(L)}_{t}\right\rVert^{p_{1}}\right]\leq C_{0}.

(4.15)

Assumption
3
is standard in the convergence of approximation schemes for SDEs
[
15
]
.
In practice, condition (
4.15
) is guaranteed throughout the training as both the inputs and the outputs of the network are bounded.

Let us now describe the intuition behind the deep network limit when
\beta>0
. Denote
t_{k}=k/L
and define for
s\in[t_{k},t_{k+1})
:

\displaystyle\widetilde{M}^{(L)}_{k,s}\coloneqq\left(W^{A}_{s}-W^{A}_{t_{k}}\right)h^{(L)}_{k}+\left(W^{b}_{s}-W^{b}_{t_{k}}\right)+L^{1-\beta}\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muA\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu_{t_{k}}h^{(L)}_{k}(s-t_{k})+L^{1-\beta}\overline{b}_{t_{k}}(s-t_{k}).

Using Itô’s formula
[
18
]
to
\sigma\big(\widetilde{M}^{(L)}_{k,s}\big)
for
s\in[t_{k},t_{k+1})
, we obtain the following approximation

h^{(L)}_{k+1}-h^{(L)}_{k}=\delta^{(L)}\sigma\hskip-1.0pt\left(\widetilde{M}_{k,t_{k+1}}^{(L)}\right)\simeq D_{1}+D_{2}+D_{3},

(4.16)

where

\displaystyle D_{1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq L^{-\alpha}\left(\left(W^{A}_{t_{k+1}}-W^{A}_{t_{k}}\right)h_{k}^{(L)}+\left(W^{b}_{t_{k+1}}-W^{b}_{t_{k}}\right)\right),

\displaystyle D_{2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}L^{-\alpha}\sigma^{\prime\prime}(0)\,Q\hskip-1.0pt\left(t_{k},{h^{(L)}_{k}}\right)({t_{k+1}-t_{k}}),

\displaystyle D_{3}

\displaystyle\coloneqq L^{1-\beta-\alpha}\left(\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muA\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu_{t_{k}}h_{k}^{(L)}({t_{k+1}-t_{k}})+\overline{b}_{t_{k}}({t_{k+1}-t_{k}})\right).

We observe from
D_{1}
that (
4.16
) admits a diffusive limit only when
\alpha=0
. In this case, we see that
D_{2}
and
D_{3}
do not explode only when
\beta\geq 1
, corresponding to a stochastic differential equation (SDE) limit that is diffusive. Another case where we obtain a non-trivial limit is when
\alpha>0
and
\alpha+\beta=1
, which leads to an ODE limit.

We now provide a precise mathematical description of the different scaling limits of
\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muH\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern-1.0mu^{(L)}
for various values of
\alpha
and
\beta
, using the concept of uniform convergence in
L^{2}
, also known as strong convergence. For a general exponent
p\geq 1
, we have the following definition.

Definition 1

(Uniform convergence in
L^{p}
)
.

Let
p\geq 1
and
\mathcal{M}
be the class of random functions
X:[0,1]\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathop{\sup}_{t\in[0,1]}\left\lVert X(t)\right\rVert^{p}\right]<\infty.

We say that a sequence
(X^{(L)})_{L\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{M}

converges uniformly in
L^{p}
to
X^{*}\in\mathcal{M}
if

\lim_{L\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\left\lVert X^{(L)}_{t}-{X}^{*}_{t}\right\rVert^{p}\right]=0.

(4.17)

We now show that Scaling regime
2
together with a smooth activation function lead to an ODE limit (which is different from the neural ODE) or a stochastic differential equation (SDE) depending on the values of
\alpha
and
\beta
.

Theorem 2

(ODE limit under Scaling regime
2
)
.

Under Assumptions
1
,
2
, and
3
,
if
\alpha>0
,
\beta>0
and
\alpha+\beta=1
, then the interpolated hidden state dynamics (
4.14
) converge uniformly in
L^{2}
to the solution to the ODE

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}H_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muA\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu_{t}H_{t}+\overline{b}_{t},\qquad H_{0}=x.

(4.18)

In particular, this implies the convergence of the hidden state process
for any typical initialization (i.e almost surely with respect to the initialization). Note that in Theorem
2
, the limit (
4.18
) defines a linear input-output map behaving like a linear network
[
1
]
. This is different from the neural ODE (
1.2
), where the activation function
\sigma
appears in the limit.

Theorem 3

(SDE limit under Scaling regime
2
)
.

Let Assumptions
1
,
2
and
3
hold and let
\alpha=0
and
\beta\geq 1
. Denote
H
as the solution to the SDE

\mathrm{d}H_{t}=\mathrm{d}W^{A}_{t}H_{t}+\mathrm{d}W^{b}_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{\prime\prime}(0)Q(t,H_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\mathbbm{1}_{\beta=1}(\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muA\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu_{t}H_{t}+\overline{b}_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t,

(4.19)

with initial condition
H_{0}=x
.
If there exist
p_{2}>2
such that
\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\|H_{t}\|^{p_{2}}\right]<\infty
, then the interpolated hidden state dynamics (
4.14
) converge uniformly in
L^{2}
to the solution of (
4.19
).

The proofs of Theorem
3
is given in Section
4.4
. And the proof of Theorem
2
follows similar ideas. In particular,
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
vanish in the limit when
\alpha>0
in (
4.16
).

Interestingly, when the activation function
\sigma
is smooth, all limits in both Theorems
2
and
3
depend on the activation only through
\sigma^{\prime}(0)
(assumed to be
1
for simplicity) and
\sigma^{\prime\prime}(0)
. In contrast to the behavior of the neural ODE limit (
1.2
), the characteristics of
\sigma
away from
0
are not relevant to the limit. In addition, our proofs rely on the smoothness of
\sigma
at
0
. If the activation function is not differentiable at
0
, then a different limit should be expected.

The case
\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muA\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu\equiv 0
,
\overline{b}\equiv 0
,
\alpha=0
, and
\beta=1
in Theorem
3
is considered in
[
26
]
, under the additional assumption that
W^{A}
and
W^{b}
are Brownian motions with constant drift. We consider a more general setup, where we introduce nonzero terms
\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muA\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu
and
\overline{b}
and we allow
W^{A}
and
W^{b}
to be arbitrary Itô processes. Moreover,
[
26
]
prove weak convergence, which corresponds to convergence of quantities averaged across many trained networks with random independent initializations, whereas in practice, the training is done only once. Thus, the strong convergence, shown in Theorems
1
and
3
, is a more relevant notion for studying the asymptotic behavior of deep neural networks.

Although the ResNet dynamics (
4.5
) is
not
expressed as an Euler scheme of a (ordinary or stochastic) differential equation, we nevertheless show strong convergence to a limitng ODE (in the case of Theorem
2
) or SDE (in the case of Theorem
3
), using techniques inspired by
[
15
]
. The challenge is to bound the difference between the ResNet dynamics and the Euler scheme of the limiting SDE. It is worth mentioning that the results in
[
15
]
hold for a class of time-homogeneous (Markov) diffusion processes whereas our result holds for Itô processes with bounded coefficients. This distinction is important for training neural networks since the “diffusion” assumption involves the distribution of the hidden state dynamics which can never be tested in practice. We can only verify the smoothness of the hidden state dynamics as detailed in Section
3
. In addition, we also relax one technical condition assumed in
[
15
]
, which is difficult to verify in practice. See Remark
3
.

Note that we assume that the Ito processes
W^{A}
and
W^{b}
are driven by
uncorrelated
Brownian motions
B^{A}
and
B^{b}
. This assumption might look strong, but we pose it for ease of exposition: assuming a generic correlation structure between
B^{A}
and
B^{b}
would only a
cross-term
in the definition of
Q
.

Indeed, denote

\displaystyle\left\lVert h_{k+1}^{(L)}-h_{k}^{(L)}\right\rVert\leq\frac{C_{\sigma}}{L}\left\lVert\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muA\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu_{t_{k}}h_{k}^{(L)}+\overline{b}_{t_{k}}\right\rVert\leq\frac{C_{\sigma}}{L}\left(A_{\max}+b_{\max}\right)\left(\left\lVert h_{k}^{(L)}\right\rVert+1\right),

where
A_{\max}:=\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\left\lVert\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muA\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu_{t}\right\rVert<\infty

\displaystyle\left\lVert h_{k+1}^{(L)}\right\rVert\leq\left(\frac{C_{\sigma}C_{\max}}{L}+1\right)\left\lVert h_{k}^{(L)}\right\rVert+\frac{C_{\sigma}C_{\max}}{L}.

By induction:

\displaystyle\left\lVert h_{j}^{(L)}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\lVert x\right\rVert\left(\frac{C_{\sigma}C_{\max}}{L}+1\right)^{j}+\frac{C_{\sigma}C_{\max}}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{j}\left(\frac{C_{\sigma}C_{\max}}{L}+1\right)^{i-1}

\displaystyle\left(\left\lVert x\right\rVert+C_{\sigma}C_{\max}\right)\left(\frac{C_{\sigma}C_{\max}}{L}+1\right)^{L}

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle\left(\left\lVert x\right\rVert+C_{\sigma}C_{\max}\right)\exp\left(C_{\sigma}C_{\max}\right)\quad{\rm as}\,\,L\rightarrow\infty.

Hence (
4.20
) holds.

Denote

Denote as well
\Delta h_{k,i}^{(L)}
and
M_{k,i}^{(L)}
the
i

\Delta h_{k}^{(L)}
and

, respectively.
Applying a third-order Taylor expansion of
\sigma
around

\displaystyle\Delta h_{k,i}^{(L)}=L^{-\alpha}\sigma_{d}\left(L^{-\beta}M_{k,i}^{(L)}\left(h_{k}^{(L)}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=L^{-\beta-\alpha}M_{k,i}^{(L)}\left(h_{k}^{(L)}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{\prime\prime}(0)L^{-2\beta-\alpha}\left(M_{k,i}^{(L)}\left(h_{k}^{(L)}\right)\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{6}\sigma^{\prime\prime\prime}\left(\xi^{(L)}_{k,i}\right)L^{-3\beta-\alpha}\left(M_{k,i}^{(L)}\left(h_{k}^{(L)}\right)\right)^{3}

\displaystyle=L^{-1}M_{k,i}^{(L)}\left(h_{k}^{(L)}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{\prime\prime}(0)L^{-\beta-1}\left(M_{k,i}^{(L)}(h_{k}^{(L)})\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{6}\sigma^{\prime\prime\prime}\left(\xi^{(L)}_{k,i}\right)L^{-2\beta-1}\left(M_{k,i}^{(L)}\left(h_{k}^{(L)}\right)\right)^{3}

with

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{6}\sigma^{\prime\prime\prime}\left(\xi^{(L)}_{k,i}\right)L^{-2\beta-1}\left(M_{k,i}^{(L)}\left(h_{k}^{(L)}\right)\right)^{3}.

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{1-A_{\max}L^{-1}}\right)^{k+1}e^{(L),2}_{0}+L^{-(1+\gamma)}G\frac{\left(\frac{1}{1-A_{\max}L^{-1}}\right)^{k+2}-1}{\left(\frac{1}{1-A_{\max}L^{-1}}\right)-1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\exp\left(2A_{\max}\frac{k+1}{L}\right)\frac{1}{L-A_{\max}}+L^{-\gamma}\frac{G}{A_{\max}}\exp\left(2A_{\max}\frac{k+2}{L}\right).

(
4.26
) holds since
e_{0}^{(L),2}\leq\frac{1}{L-A_{\max}}
and
\frac{1}{1-A_{\max}L^{-1}}<1+2A_{\max}L^{-1}\leq\exp(2A_{\max}L^{-1})
when
L>2A_{\max}
. Therefore, we conclude

\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{0\leq k<L}e^{(L),2}_{k}=0.

4.4.2
Proof of Theorem
2

We provide a complete proof of Theorem
2
for the case
\alpha=0
and
\beta=1
. Other cases follow similarly. When
\alpha=0
and
\beta=1
, we define the targeted SDE limit for the discrete scheme (
4.5
) as follows:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}H_{t}=\mu(t,H_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}V_{t}^{A}\,H_{t}+\mathrm{d}V_{t}^{b}\,\,\,\,\,\mbox{for}\,\,t\in\left[0,1\right],\,\,\,\,H_{0}=x,

(4.27)

in which

\displaystyle\mu\left(t,h\right)

\displaystyle\coloneqq U_{t}^{A}\,h+\,U_{t}^{b}+\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muA\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu_{t}h+\overline{b}_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{\prime\prime}(0)Q(t,h),

(4.28)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}V_{t}^{A}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{k,l=1}^{d}\left(q_{t}^{A}\right)_{ijkl}\left(\mathrm{d}B_{t}^{A}\right)_{kl},\quad\mathrm{d}V_{t}^{b}\coloneqq q_{t}^{b}\,\mathrm{d}B_{t}^{b},

with
V_{0}^{A}=0
and
V_{0}^{b}=0
. Here the quadratic variation process
\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{\prime\prime}(0)Q(t,h)
is the
Itô correction
term for the drift. On the one hand this correction term introduces non-linearity into the drift and makes the proof challenging. On the other hand, this term is the key for the convergence analysis. See (
4.55
) and (
4.56
).

Euler-Maruyama scheme of the limiting SDE.
Denote
\Delta_{L}=1/L
as the sub-interval length and
t_{k}=k/L
,
k=0,1,\ldots,L
as the uniform partition of the interval
[0,1]
. Further denote
\Delta V^{A}_{k}=V^{A}_{t_{k+1}}-V^{A}_{t_{k}}
and
\Delta V^{b}_{k}=V^{b}_{t_{k+1}}-V^{b}_{t_{k}}
as the increment of the stochastic processes. Define the Euler-Maruyama discretization scheme of the SDE (
4.27
) as:

\displaystyle\widehat{h}_{k+1}^{(L)}-\widehat{h}_{k}^{(L)}\coloneqq\mu\hskip-1.0pt\left(t_{k},\widehat{h}_{k}^{(L)}\right)\Delta_{L}+\Delta V_{k}^{A}\,\widehat{h}_{k}^{(L)}+\Delta V_{k}^{b},

(4.29)

and the one-step forward increment follows:

\displaystyle f^{(L)}(k,h)\coloneqq\mu\left(t_{k},h\right)\Delta_{L}+\Delta V_{k}^{A}\,h+\Delta V_{k}^{b}.

(4.30)

Therefore (
4.29
) can be rewritten as
\widehat{h}_{k+1}^{(L)}=\widehat{h}_{k}^{(L)}+f^{(L)}\hskip-2.0pt\left(k,\widehat{h}_{k}^{(L)}\right)
.

Continuous-time extension.

Recall that we extend the scheme
\big\{h^{(L)}_{k}:k=0,\ldots,L\big\}
to a continuous-time process
\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muH\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern-1.0mu^{(L)}_{t}
on
t\in[0,1]
by a piecewise constant and right-continuous interpolation of
\{h^{(L)}_{k}:k=0,\ldots,L-1\}
:

\displaystyle\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muH\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern-1.0mu^{(L)}_{t}\coloneqq\sum_{k=0}^{L}h_{k}^{(L)}{\bf 1}_{t_{k}\leq t<t_{k+1}}.

(4.31)

We call
\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muH\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern-1.0mu^{(L)}_{t}
the
continuous-time extension
(CTE) of
\{h^{(L)}_{k}:k=0,\ldots,L-1\}
.

Continuous-time approximation.

Denote

\displaystyle M^{(L)}_{k}(h)

\displaystyle\coloneqq

\displaystyle\left(\mu\left(t_{k},h\right)-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{\prime\prime}(0)Q(t_{k},h)\right)\Delta_{L}+\Delta V_{k}^{A}\,h+\Delta V_{k}^{b}

(4.32)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(U^{A}_{t_{k}}\,h+\,U^{b}_{t_{k}}+\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muA\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu_{t_{k}}h+\overline{b}_{t_{k}}\right)\Delta_{L}+\Delta V_{k}^{A}\,h+\Delta V_{k}^{b}

\displaystyle=:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mu}\left(t_{k},h\right)\Delta_{L}+\Delta V_{k}^{A}\,h+\Delta V_{k}^{b},

and from (
4.16
) we thus have

\Delta h_{k}^{(L)}:=h_{k+1}^{(L)}-h_{k}^{(L)}=\sigma\hskip-1.0pt\left(M_{k}^{(L)}\hskip-2.0pt\left(h_{k}^{(L)}\right)\right).

Denote
\Delta h_{k,i}^{(L)}
and
M_{k,i}^{(L)}
the
i
-th element of
\Delta h_{k}^{(L)}
and
M_{k}^{(L)}
, respectively.
Applying a third-order Taylor expansion of
\sigma
around
0
with the help of Assumption
1
, for
i=1,2,\ldots,d
, we get

\displaystyle\Delta h_{k,i}^{(L)}=\sigma\hskip-1.0pt\left(M^{(L)}_{k,i}\hskip-2.0pt\left(h_{k}^{(L)}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=M^{(L)}_{k,i}\hskip-2.0pt\left(h_{k}^{(L)}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{\prime\prime}(0)M^{(L)}_{k,i}\hskip-2.0pt\left(h_{k}^{(L)}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{6}\sigma^{\prime\prime\prime}\hskip-2.0pt\left(\xi^{(L)}_{k,i}\right)M^{(L)}_{k,i}\hskip-2.0pt\left(h_{k}^{(L)}\right)^{3}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{{\mu}_{i}\hskip-1.0pt\left(t_{k},h_{k}^{(L)}\right)\Delta_{L}+\left(\Delta V_{k}^{A}\,h_{k}^{(L)}\right)_{i}+\left(\Delta V_{k}^{b}\right)_{i}}_{f_{i}^{(L)}\left(k,h_{k}^{(L)}\right)}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{\prime\prime}(0)\left(M_{k,i}^{(L)}\hskip-2.0pt\left(h_{k}^{(L)}\right)^{2}-Q_{i}\hskip-1.0pt\left(t_{k},h_{k}^{(L)}\right)\right)}_{N_{k,i}^{(L)}\left(h_{k}^{(L)}\right)}

\displaystyle\quad\,+\frac{1}{6}\sigma^{\prime\prime\prime}\hskip-2.0pt\left(\xi^{(L)}_{k,i}\right)M^{(L)}_{k,i}\hskip-2.0pt\left(h_{k}^{(L)}\right)^{3}

\displaystyle=f^{(L)}_{i}\hskip-2.0pt\left(k,h_{k}^{(L)}\right)+N_{k,i}^{(L)}\hskip-2.0pt\left(h_{k}^{(L)}\right)+\frac{1}{6}\sigma^{\prime\prime\prime}\hskip-2.0pt\left(\xi^{(L)}_{k,i}\right)M^{(L)}_{k,i}\hskip-2.0pt\left(h_{k}^{(L)}\right)^{3},

with
\left|\xi^{(L)}_{k,i}\right|<\left|M^{(L)}_{k,i}\hskip-2.0pt\left(h_{k}^{(L)}\right)\right|
. The increment of the ResNet
\Delta h_{k,i}^{(L)}
has two parts: the increment of the Euler-Maruyama scheme
f^{(L)}_{i}\hskip-2.0pt\left(k,h_{k}^{(L)}\right)
and the residual

D_{k,i}^{(L)}\hskip-2.0pt\left(h_{k}^{(L)}\right)\coloneqq\frac{1}{6}\sigma^{\prime\prime\prime}\hskip-2.0pt\left(\xi^{(L)}_{k,i}\right)M^{(L)}_{k,i}\hskip-2.0pt\left(h_{k}^{(L)}\right)^{3}+N_{k,i}^{(L)}\hskip-2.0pt\left(h_{k}^{(L)}\right).

(4.33)

It is clear from here that the Euler-Maruyama scheme of the limiting SDE is different from the ResNet dynamics. Hence classical results on the convergence of discrete SDE schemes cannot be applied directly.

In our analysis it will be more natural to work with the following
continuous-time approximation
(CTA), defined as

\displaystyle\widetilde{H}^{(L)}_{t}\coloneqq h_{0}^{(L)}+\int_{0}^{t}\mu\hskip-1.0pt\left({{t_{k_{s}}}},\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muH\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern-1.0mu^{(L)}_{s}\right)\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\left(\mathrm{d}V^{A}_{s}\,\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muH\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern-1.0mu^{(L)}_{s}+\mathrm{d}V^{b}_{s}\right)+\sum_{k<Lt}D_{k}^{(L)}\hskip-2.0pt\left(h_{k}^{(L)}\right),

(4.34)

where
D_{k}^{(L)}(h)=\left(D_{k,1}^{(L)}(h),\ldots,D_{k,d}^{(L)}(h)\right)^{\top}
and
k_{s}
is the integer for which
s\in[t_{k_{s}},t_{k_{s}+1})
for a given
s\in[0,1)
.

Here
\widetilde{H}^{(L)}_{t}
approximates the CTE (
4.31
) with a continuous version, with interpolations both in time and in space, of the
f^{(L)}(k,h)
part while the residual term
D_{k}^{(L)}(h)
remains the same.
By design we have
\widetilde{H}^{(L)}_{t_{k}}=\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muH\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern-1.0mu^{L}_{t_{k}}=h^{(L)}_{k}
, that is,
\widetilde{H}^{(L)}_{t}
and
\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muH\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern-1.0mu^{(L)}_{t}
coincide with the discrete solution at grid points
t_{k}
,
k=0,1,\ldots,L-1
. This relationship is instrumental in order to control the error.

We will first study the difference between
\widetilde{H}
and
h^{(L)}
, and then the difference between
\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muH\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern-1.0mu
and
h^{(L)}
, in the supremum norm. The sum of the two will give a bound for the error of the discrete approximation.

4.4.4
Proof of Theorem
3

We are now ready to show the proof of Theorem

.

Proof.

Let us define two stopping times to utilize the local Lipschitz property of
\mu
:

\displaystyle\tau_{R}:=\inf\left\{t\geq 0:\left\lVert\widetilde{H}^{(L)}_{t}\right\rVert\geq R\right\},\quad\rho_{R}:=\inf\left\{t\geq 0:\left\lVert H_{t}\right\rVert\geq R\right\},\quad\theta_{R}:=\tau_{R}\land\rho_{R},

and define the approximation errors

\displaystyle e_{1}(t):=\widetilde{H}^{(L)}_{t}-H_{t},\,\,\text{ and }\,\,e_{2}(t):=\widetilde{H}^{(L)}_{t}-\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muH\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern-1.0mu^{(L)}_{t}.

(4.41)

The proof contains two steps. The first step is to show
\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\left\lVert e_{1}(t)\right\rVert^{2}\right]=0
and the second step is to show
\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\left\lVert e_{2}(t)\right\rVert^{2}\right]=0

Following the idea in
[
15
]
, we first show that for any
\delta>0
(to be determined later):

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\left\lVert e_{1}(t)\right\rVert^{2}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\left\lVert\widetilde{H}^{(L)}_{t\wedge\theta_{R}}-H_{t\wedge\theta_{R}}\right\rVert^{2}\right]+\frac{2^{p+1}\delta C}{p}+\frac{2(p-2)C}{p\delta^{2/(p-2)}R^{p}},

(4.42)

where
C
and
p
are defined in (
4.36
).
To see this, recall that by Young’s inequality, for
r^{-1}+q^{-1}=1
, we have

ab=\delta^{1/r}a\cdot\delta^{1/q-1}b\leq\frac{\delta}{r}a^{r}+\frac{1}{q\delta^{q/r}}b^{q},\quad\forall a,b,\delta>0.

(4.43)

First decompose the left-hand side of (
4.42
) to obtain, for all
\delta>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\|e_{1}(t)\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\|e_{1}(t)\|^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\tau_{R}>1,\rho_{R}>1\}}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\|e_{1}(t)\|^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\tau_{R}\leq 1\text{ or }\rho_{R}\leq 1\}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\|e_{1}(t\wedge\theta_{R})\|^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\theta_{R}>1\}}\right]+\frac{2\delta}{p}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\|e_{1}(t)\|^{p}\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\,\frac{1-2/p}{\delta^{2/(p-2)}}\mathbb{P}\left(\tau_{R}\leq 1\text{ or }\rho_{R}\leq 1\right).

where we apply (
4.43
) with
r=p/2
to the second term. Now

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\tau_{R}\leq 1)=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\{\tau_{R}\leq 1\}}\frac{\|\widetilde{H}^{(L)}_{\tau_{R}}\|^{p}}{R^{p}}\right]\leq\frac{1}{R^{p}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\left\lVert\widetilde{H}^{(L)}_{t}\right\rVert^{p}\right]\leq\frac{C}{R^{p}}.

(4.45)

A similar result can be derived for
\rho_{R}
, so that we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\tau_{R}\leq 1\text{ or }\rho_{R}\leq 1)\leq\mathbb{P}(\tau_{R}\leq 1)+\mathbb{P}(\rho_{R}\leq 1)\leq\frac{2C}{R^{p}}.

(4.46)

Using the inequalities in (
4.45
)–(
4.46
), along with

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\left\lVert e_{1}(t)\right\rVert^{p}\right]\leq 2^{p-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\left(\left\lVert\widetilde{H}^{(L)}_{t}\right\rVert^{p}+\left\lVert H_{t}\right\rVert^{p}\right)\right]\leq 2^{p}\,C

(4.47)

in (
4.44
), we show the desired result (
4.42
).

To obtain a uniform bound on
\widetilde{H}-H
, we bound the first term on the right-hand side of (
4.42
). Using the definition of the targeted SDE limit in (

5
Asymptotic analysis of the backpropagation dynamics

The most widely used method to train neural networks is the pairing of

•

the backpropagation algorithm to find the exact gradient (or a stochastic approximation) of the loss function with respect to the network weights, and

•

a variant of the gradient descent algorithm to iteratively update the network weights.

We are interested to study the behaviour of the former in residual networks, under our Scaling regimes
1
and
2
. To do so, we will first formalize the objective function and the discrete backward equation linking the gradient of the loss function across layers.

5.1

Suppose we want to learn the mapping
f_{\mathrm{true}}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d})

\mathcal{D}\coloneqq\left\{(x_{i},y_{i}):i=1,\ldots,N\right\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}

(5.1)

Here,

\ell(\widehat{y},y)=\left\lVert y-\widehat{y}\right\rVert^{2}

\mathcal{D}
. Fix
L\in\mathbb{N}
and define

\theta^{(L)}\coloneqq\left(A^{(L)}_{k},b^{(L)}_{k}\right)_{k=1}^{L}\in\left(\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)^{L}.

For an input

, recall the following forward dynamics for the residual network

\displaystyle h_{0}^{(L),x}

\displaystyle=x,

(5.2)

\displaystyle h^{(L),x}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=h^{(L),x}_{k}+L^{-\alpha}\,\sigma_{d}\hskip-1.0pt\left(A^{(L)}_{k}h^{(L),x}_{k}+b^{(L)}_{k}\right).

We define
\phi_{\theta^{(L)}}(x)\coloneqq h_{L}^{(L),x}

\nabla_{\theta^{(L)}}J\hskip-1.0pt\left(\theta^{(L)}\right)
. Observe from the definition (

\nabla_{\theta_{k}^{(L)}}J\hskip-1.0pt\left(\theta^{(L)}\right)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\nabla_{\theta_{k}^{(L)}}h^{(L),\,x_{i}}_{k+1}\left(\frac{\partial h_{L}^{(L),\,x_{i}}}{\partial h^{(L),\,x_{i}}_{k+1}}\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\widehat{y}}\left(h_{L}^{(L),\,x_{i}},y_{i}\right)\right).

The terms

\displaystyle=g_{k+1}^{(L),x}\left(I_{d}+L^{-\alpha}\,\mathrm{diag}\left(\sigma^{\prime}_{d}\hskip-1.0pt\left(A_{k}^{(L)}h_{k}^{(L),x}+b_{k}^{(L)}\right)\right)A^{(L)}_{k}\right),

In a recent work,

show that, when the hidden state

satisfies a continuous-time ’neural SDE’ dynamics,

where

is the time-reversed Brownian motion defined by

is the solution of the backward flow of diffeomorphisms generated by the forward SDE (

It is clear that the limit

5.13

which depends on weights across all layers i.e. the entire path of

[

]

\displaystyle G_{t}=J_{1}J_{t}^{-1}

\displaystyle=J_{1}\left(J_{1}^{-1}+\int_{t}^{1}J_{s}^{-1}\left(-\nu(s,H_{s})\mathrm{d}s-\mathrm{d}W_{s}^{A}+\mathrm{d}\left[W^{A}\right]_{s}\right)\right)

\widetilde{G}^{(L)}_{t}\coloneqq g_{k+1}^{(L)}\left(I_{d}+(t_{k+1}-t)\,\mathrm{diag}\left(\sigma^{\prime}_{d}\hskip-1.0pt\left(\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muA\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu_{t_{k}}H_{t_{k}}+\overline{b}_{t_{k}}\right)\right)\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muA\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu_{t_{k}}\right),

where
\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muA\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu
and
\overline{b}
are specified in Theorem
1
.
Hence, we can directly deduce that

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\left[0,1\right]}\left\lVert\widetilde{G}^{(L)}_{t}-\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muG\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu^{(L)}_{t}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq L^{-1}\sup_{0\leq k<L}\sup_{t\in\left(t_{k},t_{k+1}\right]}\left\lVert g_{k+1}^{(L)}\mathrm{diag}\left(\sigma^{\prime}_{d}\hskip-1.0pt\left(\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muA\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu_{t_{k}}H_{t_{k}}+\overline{b}_{t_{k}}\right)\right)\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muA\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu_{t_{k}}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq L^{-1}\sup_{0\leq k<L}\left\lVert g_{k+1}^{(L)}\right\rVert\,\,\sup_{t\in\left[0,1\right]}\left\lVert\mathrm{diag}\left(\sigma^{\prime}_{d}\hskip-1.0pt\left(\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muA\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu_{t}H_{t}+\overline{b}_{t}\right)\right)\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muA\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu_{t}\right\rVert

By continuity of
\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muA\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu
,
\overline{b}
and
H
, the first supremum is finite and by a similar argument as in the proof of Theorem
1
, the second supremum is also finite. Thus, there exists a constant
G_{\infty}>0
such that
\sup_{t\in\left[0,1\right]}\left\lVert\widetilde{G}^{(L)}_{t}-\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muG\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu^{(L)}_{t}\right\rVert\leq G_{\infty}L^{-1}
. Now, we also have, for
t\in(t_{k},t_{k+1}]
,

\displaystyle\widetilde{G}^{(L)}_{t}-G_{t}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{G}^{(L)}_{t_{k+1}}-G_{t_{k+1}}+(t_{k+1}-t)\,g_{k+1}^{(L)}\,\mathrm{diag}\left(\sigma^{\prime}_{d}\hskip-1.0pt\left(\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muA\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu_{t_{k}}H_{t_{k}}+\overline{b}_{t_{k}}\right)\right)\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muA\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu_{t_{k}}

\displaystyle-\int_{t}^{t_{k+1}}G_{s}\mathrm{diag}\left(\sigma^{\prime}_{d}\hskip-1.0pt\left(\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muA\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu_{s}H_{s}+\overline{b}_{s}\right)\right)\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muA\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu_{s}\mathrm{d}s.

Hence, for
e^{(L)}_{k}\coloneqq\sup_{t_{k}<t\leq t_{k+1}}\left\lVert\widetilde{G}^{(L)}_{t}-G_{t}\right\rVert
, we can estimate

\displaystyle e^{(L)}_{k}

\displaystyle\leq e^{(L)}_{k+1}+\int_{t}^{t_{k+1}}\left\lVert G_{s}J_{s}-g_{k+1}^{(L)}J_{t_{k}}\right\rVert\mathrm{d}s.

\displaystyle\leq e^{(L)}_{k+1}+\int_{t}^{t_{k+1}}\left(\left\lVert G_{s}-g_{k+1}^{(L)}\right\rVert\left\lVert J_{s}\right\rVert+\left\lVert g_{k+1}^{(L)}\right\rVert\left\lVert J_{s}-J_{t_{k}}\right\rVert\right)\mathrm{d}s.

\displaystyle\leq e^{(L)}_{k+1}+J_{\infty}L^{-1}\left(e_{k}^{(L)}+G_{\infty}L^{-1}\right)+\int_{t}^{t_{k+1}}\left\lVert g_{k+1}^{(L)}\right\rVert\left\lVert J_{s}-J_{t_{k}}\right\rVert\mathrm{d}s,

where
J_{s}\coloneqq\mathrm{diag}\left(\sigma^{\prime}_{d}\hskip-1.0pt\left(\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muA\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu_{s}H_{s}+\overline{b}_{s}\right)\right)\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muA\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu_{s}
and
J_{\infty}\coloneqq\sup_{s\in\left[0,1\right]}\left\lVert J_{s}\right\rVert<\infty
. Now, recall that
\left\lVert g_{k+1}^{(L)}\right\rVert
is uniformly bounded in
k,L
, and we have
\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muA\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu,\overline{b}\in\mathcal{H}^{1}
and
H\in\mathcal{C}^{1}
, so there exists a constant
J^{\prime}_{\infty}<\infty
such that
\|g_{k+1}^{(L)}\|\int_{t}^{t_{k+1}}\left\lVert J_{s}-J_{t_{k}}\right\rVert\mathrm{d}s<J^{\prime}_{\infty}L^{-2}
. Thus,

\left(1-J_{\infty}L^{-1}\right)e^{(L)}_{k}\leq e^{(L)}_{k+1}+\left(J_{\infty}G_{\infty}+J^{\prime}_{\infty}\right)L^{-2}.

By Gronwall’s lemma and the fact that
e_{L}^{(L)}=\mathcal{O}(L^{-1})
, we deduce that
\max_{k}e_{k}^{(L)}=\mathcal{O}(L^{-1})
and conclude

\lim_{L\to\infty}\sup_{t\in\left[0,1\right]}\left\lVert\widetilde{G}^{(L)}_{t}-G_{t}\right\rVert\leq\lim_{L\to\infty}\left(\sup_{t\in\left[0,1\right]}\left\lVert\widetilde{G}^{(L)}_{t}-\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muG\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu^{(L)}_{t}\right\rVert+\max_{k}e_{k}^{(L)}\right)=0.

5.4.2
Proof of Theorem
5

The ideas of the proof follow closely those of Theorem
3
, and we will provide here the main arguments for the case
\alpha=0
and
\beta=1
. Other cases follow similarly. For the ease of notation exposition, we consider
U=0
and we use
C
to denote some generic constant (independent from
L
and other parameters, such as
\varepsilon
,
\delta
, and
R
, to be defined later) that may vary from step to step.

The proof consists of 11 steps that can be summarized as follows. Step 1 decomposes the discrete gradient
g_{k}^{(L)}
into two terms: the Jacobian of the output with respect to the input, and the Jacobian of the hidden state
h_{k}^{(L)}
with respect to the input, which we denote by
J_{k}^{(L)}
. We then write a forward equation for
J_{k}^{(L)}
. Step 2 defines a continuous-time approximation
\widetilde{J}_{k}^{(L)}
and a continuous-time interpolation
\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muJ\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu_{k}^{(L)}
. Step 3 establishes a uniform bound
\mathcal{O}(L^{-1})
between
\widetilde{J}_{k}^{(L)}
and
\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muJ\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu_{k}^{(L)}
. Step 4 defines high-probability events under which the hidden states
h^{(L)}_{k}
and the continuous-time limit
J_{t}
are uniformly bounded. Step 5 decomposes the difference between
\widetilde{J}^{(L)}
and
J
with a drift term and an error term
D^{(L)}
, which can be further decomposed into a variance term
N^{(L)}
and a Taylor remainder term
R^{(L)}
. Step 6 proves that
R^{(L)}
uniformly vanishes as
\mathcal{O}(L^{-1})
. Step 7 decomposes
N^{(L)}
into three terms. Step 8 and 9 prove that these terms uniformly vanishes as
\mathcal{O}(L^{-\min(1,\kappa)})
. Step 10 wraps everything together to show a uniform
L^{2}
bound between
\widetilde{J}^{(L)}
and
J^{(L)}
. Step 11 uses it to prove a uniform
L^{1}
bound between the discrete gradients
g^{(L)}_{k}
and their limit
G_{t}=J_{1}J_{t}^{-1}
.

Step 0: Well-posedness of the statement.
The matrix-valued linear stochastic differential equation (
5.10
) has a continuous and adapted solution, and this solution is unique in the sense that almost all sample processes of any two solutions coincide, see for example
[
10
]
. Furthermore,
\mathbb{P}-
a.s.,
J_{t}
is invertible for all
t\in\left[0,1\right]
, see Corollary 2.1 in
[
6
]
. Also, Theorem 3.1 in
[
32
]
states that
K_{t}\coloneqq J_{t}^{-1}
solves the following SDE.

\mathrm{d}K_{t}=K_{t}\left(-\nu(t,H_{t})\mathrm{d}t-\mathrm{d}W_{t}^{A}+\mathrm{d}\left[W^{A}\right]_{t}\right),\quad K_{0}=I_{d}.

Recall from Assumption
2
that the quadratic variation of
W^{A}
is uniformly continuous with resepect to the Lesbegue measure. Therefore, by Lemma
3
and Assumption
4
, we conclude that the fourth moments of the supremum of
J
and
J^{-1}
are finite.

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in\left[0,T\right]}\max\left(\left\lVert J_{t}^{-1}\right\rVert_{F},\left\lVert J_{t}\right\rVert_{F}\right)^{4}\right]\leq C\,\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(8\int_{0}^{T}\left|\mathrm{tr}\left(\nu(s,H_{s})\right)\right|\mathrm{d}s\right)\right]^{1/2}<\infty.

(5.15)

Step 1: Rewrite the discrete backpropagation equation.
First, observe that multiplying (
5.3
) together gives, for
k=0,\ldots,L
,

g_{0}^{(L),x}=g_{k}^{(L),x}\left[\prod_{k^{\prime}=k-1}^{0}\left(I_{d}+\mathrm{diag}\left(\sigma^{\prime}_{d}\hskip-1.0pt\left(A_{k^{\prime}}^{(L)}h_{k^{\prime}}^{(L),x}+b_{k^{\prime}}^{(L)}\right)\right)A^{(L)}_{k^{\prime}}\right)\right].

(5.16)

Define
J_{0}^{(L),x}\coloneqq I_{d}
and for

:

J_{k+1}^{(L),x}\coloneqq\left(I_{d}+\mathrm{diag}\left(\sigma^{\prime}_{d}\hskip-1.0pt\left(A_{k}^{(L)}h_{k}^{(L),x}+b_{k}^{(L)}\right)\right)A^{(L)}_{k}\right)J_{k}^{(L),x}.

(5.17)

Note that by the chain rule, we directly have
J_{k}^{(L),x}=\nabla_{x}h_{k}^{(L),x}
and
g_{0}^{(L),x}=g_{k}^{(L),x}J_{k}^{(L),x}
. In the following, we omit the explicit dependence on the initial data
x
when the context is clear. Recall now the definition
M_{k}^{(L)}(h)\coloneqq A_{k}^{(L)}h+b_{k}^{(L)}
from (
4.32
). By Taylor’s theorem on
\sigma^{\prime}
, as

is continuous, for each

\left|\xi_{k,i}^{(L)}\right|<\left|M_{k}^{(L)}\Big(h_{k}^{(L)}\Big)_{i}\right|

\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}\left(M_{k}^{(L)}\Big(h_{k}^{(L)}\Big)_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\sigma^{\prime}(0)+\sigma^{\prime\prime}(0)M_{k}^{(L)}\Big(h_{k}^{(L)}\Big)_{i}+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{\prime\prime\prime}\hskip-2.0pt\left(\xi_{k,i}^{(L)}\right)M_{k}^{(L)}\Big(h_{k}^{(L)}\Big)_{i}^{2}.

Hence, using
\sigma^{\prime}(0)=1
,

, and (

), we get

\displaystyle J_{k+1}^{(L)}

\displaystyle=\left(I_{d}+\mathrm{diag}\left(\sigma^{\prime}_{d}\hskip-1.0pt\left(M_{k}^{(L)}\Big(h_{k}^{(L)}\Big)\right)\right)A^{(L)}_{k}\right)J_{k}^{(L)}

\displaystyle=\left(I_{d}+A^{(L)}_{k}+\sigma^{\prime\prime}(0)\mathrm{diag}\left(M_{k}^{(L)}\Big(h_{k}^{(L)}\Big)\right)A^{(L)}_{k}\right)J_{k}^{(L)}

\displaystyle\quad+\mathrm{diag}\left(\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{\prime\prime\prime}\hskip-2.0pt\left(\xi_{k}^{(L)}\right)\odot M_{k}^{(L)}\Big(h_{k}^{(L)}\Big)^{\odot,\,2}\right)A_{k}^{(L)}J_{k}^{(L)}

\displaystyle=\Big(I_{d}+\Big(\underbrace{\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muA\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu_{t_{k}}+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{\prime\prime}(0)\nabla_{h}Q\Big(t_{k},h_{k}^{(L)}\Big)}_{\eqqcolon\,\nu\big(t_{k},h_{k}^{(L)}\big)}\Big)\Delta_{L}+\Delta W^{A}_{k}\Big)J_{k}^{(L)}

\displaystyle\quad+\Big(\sigma^{\prime\prime}(0)\Big(\underbrace{\mathrm{diag}\left(M_{k}^{(L)}\Big(h_{k}^{(L)}\Big)\right)A^{(L)}_{k}-\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{h}Q\Big(t_{k},h_{k}^{(L)}\Big)\Delta_{L}}_{\eqqcolon\,\,N^{(L)}_{k}\big(J_{k}^{(L)},\,h_{k}^{(L)}\big)}\Big)\Big)J_{k}^{(L)}

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{\left(\mathrm{diag}\left(\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{\prime\prime\prime}\hskip-2.0pt\left(\xi_{k}^{(L)}\right)\odot M_{k}^{(L)}\Big(h_{k}^{(L)}\Big)^{\odot,\,2}\right)\right)A_{k}^{(L)}J_{k}^{(L)}}_{\eqqcolon\,\,R^{(L)}_{k}\big(J_{k}^{(L)},\,h_{k}^{(L)}\big)}

\displaystyle+3\left\lVert\sum_{k<Lt}D_{k}^{(L)}\hskip-2.0pt\left(J_{k}^{(L)},h_{k}^{(L)}\right)\right\rVert^{2}.

The goal is to find an upper bound of the first two terms, consisting of the sum of the

distance between

\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muJ\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu^{(L)}

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nu(t_{2},h_{2})J_{2}-\nu(t_{1},h_{1})J_{1}\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 3\left\lVert\nu(t_{2},h_{2})\right\rVert^{2}\left\lVert J_{2}-J_{1}\right\rVert^{2}+3\left\lVert\nu(t_{2},h_{2})-\nu(t_{2},h_{1})\right\rVert^{2}\left\lVert J_{1}\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(1+\left\lVert h_{2}\right\rVert^{2}\right)\left\lVert J_{2}-J_{1}\right\rVert^{2}+C\left\lVert h_{2}-h_{1}\right\rVert^{2}\left\lVert J_{1}\right\rVert^{2}

We used the fact that

Hölder continuous. We directly deduce that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{E^{(L)}_{R}}\int_{0}^{T}\left\lVert\nu\left(t_{k_{s}},\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muH\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu_{s}^{(L)}\right)\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muJ\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu_{s}^{(L)}-\nu\left(s,H_{s}\right)J_{s}\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right]

\displaystyle\leq C(1+R^{2})\,\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{E^{(L)}_{R}}\int_{0}^{T}\left\lVert\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muJ\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu_{s}^{(L)}-J_{s}\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right]+CR^{2}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\left\lVert\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muH\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu_{s}^{(L)}-H_{s}\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right]

(5.26)

\displaystyle\quad+CR^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\left\lVert H_{s}\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right]L^{-\kappa}

\displaystyle\leq CR^{2}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{E^{(L)}_{R}}\int_{0}^{T}\left\lVert\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muJ\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu_{s}^{(L)}-J_{s}\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right]+CR^{2}L^{-\min(1/2,\,\kappa)}.

(5.27)

The last inequality holds by Theorem
3
. Hence, we obtain from (
5.25
)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\left\lVert J_{t}-\widetilde{J}^{(L)}_{t}\right\rVert^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{E^{(L)}_{R}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq CR^{2}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{E^{(L)}_{R}}\int_{0}^{T}\left\lVert\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muJ\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu_{s}^{(L)}-J_{s}\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right]+CR^{2}L^{-\min(1/2,\,\kappa)}

\displaystyle+3\,\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\left\lVert\sum_{k<Lt}D_{k}^{(L)}\hskip-2.0pt\left(J_{k}^{(L)},h_{k}^{(L)}\right)\right\rVert^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{E^{(L)}_{R}}\right].

(5.28)

We applied Doob’s martingale inequality
[
28
]
on the second term of (
5.25
), as
\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muJ\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu
,
J
, and
E_{R}^{(L)}
are adapted to the filtration generated by
W^{A}
. We now estimate the error term
D^{(L)}
in (
5.28
). Recall that it decomposes into a variance term
N^{(L)}
and a Taylor remainder term
R^{(L)}
.

Step 6: Prove that the remainder
R^{(L)}_{k}
uniformly vanishes.
We proceed to show that

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\left\lVert\sum_{k<Lt}R_{k}^{(L)}\hskip-2.0pt\left(J_{k}^{(L)},h_{k}^{(L)}\right)\right\rVert^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{E^{(L)}_{R}}\right]\leq CR^{6}L^{-1},

(5.29)

which is straightforward since:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\left\lVert\sum_{k<Lt}R_{k}^{(L)}\hskip-2.0pt\left(J_{k}^{(L)},h_{k}^{(L)}\right)\right\rVert^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{E^{(L)}_{R}}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{k=0}^{L-1}\left\lVert R_{k}^{(L)}\hskip-2.0pt\left(J_{k}^{(L)},h_{k}^{(L)}\right)\right\rVert\right)^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{E^{(L)}_{R}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq CR^{2}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{k=0}^{L-1}\left(\left\lVert A_{k}^{(L)}\right\rVert R+\left\lVert b_{k}^{(L)}\right\rVert\right)^{2}\left\lVert A_{k}^{(L)}\right\rVert\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq CR^{2}L\,\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{L-1}\left(\left\lVert A_{k}^{(L)}\right\rVert R+\left\lVert b_{k}^{(L)}\right\rVert\right)^{4}\left\lVert A_{k}^{(L)}\right\rVert^{2}\right]\leq CR^{6}L^{-1}.

The last inequality holds for the same reasons as (
4.57
).

Step 7: Prove that the remainder
N^{(L)}_{k}
uniformly vanishes.
First note that we can write
N^{(L)}_{k,ij}=N^{(L),0}_{k,ij}+\sum_{m=1}^{d}N^{(L),1}_{k,ij,m}
, where

\displaystyle N^{(L),0}_{k,ij}

\displaystyle\coloneqq b^{(L)}_{k,i}A^{(L)}_{k,ij}

\displaystyle N^{(L),1}_{k,ij,m}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left(A^{(L)}_{k,im}A^{(L)}_{k,ij}-\left(\Sigma^{A}_{t_{k}}\right)_{imij}\Delta_{L}\right)h^{(L)}_{k,m}

(5.30)

We assumed that the Ito processes
W^{A}
and
W^{b}
are driven by uncorrelated Brownian motions, hence
N^{(L),0}_{k,ij}
uniformly vanishes in
L^{2}
at rate
\Delta_{L}
.
Thus, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\left|\sum_{k<Lt}N_{k,ij}^{(L)}\right|^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{E^{(L)}_{R}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq CL^{-1}+d\sum_{m=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\left|\sum_{k<Lt}N_{k,ij,m}^{(L),1}\right|^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{E^{(L)}_{R}}\right].

(5.31)

Using the discrete (forward) filtration
\big\{\mathcal{G}_{k}:k=-1,0,\ldots,L-1\big\}
defined in (
4.51
), we now expand (
5.30
) using the definition of Scaling regime
2
.

\displaystyle N^{(L),1}_{k,ij,m}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\left(\left(\Delta W^{A}_{k}\right)_{im}\left(\Delta W^{A}_{k}\right)_{ij}-\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\Sigma^{A}_{s}\right)_{imij}\nonscript\;\middle\nonscript\;\mathcal{G}_{k-1}\right]\mathrm{d}s\right)h^{(L)}_{k,m}}_{\raisebox{0.5pt}{\textcircled{\fontsize{8.5pt}{0}\fontfamily{and}\selectfont 1}}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{h^{(L)}_{k,m}\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\Sigma^{A}_{s}-\Sigma^{A}_{t_{k}}\right)_{imij}\nonscript\;\middle\nonscript\;\mathcal{G}_{k-1}\right]\mathrm{d}s}_{\raisebox{0.5pt}{\textcircled{\fontsize{8.5pt}{0}\fontfamily{and}\selectfont 2}}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\left[\left(\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muA\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu_{t_{k}}\right)_{im}\left(\Delta W^{A}_{k}\right)_{ij}+\left(\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muA\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu_{t_{k}}\right)_{ij}\left(\Delta W^{A}_{k}\right)_{im}\right]h^{(L)}_{k,m}\Delta_{L}+\left(\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muA\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu_{t_{k}}\right)_{im}\left(\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muA\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu_{t_{k}}\right)_{ij}h^{(L)}_{k,m}(\Delta_{L})^{2}}_{\raisebox{0.5pt}{\textcircled{\fontsize{8.5pt}{0}\fontfamily{and}\selectfont 3}}}.

Step 8: Prove that the term
\fontsize{8.5pt}{0}\fontfamily{and}\selectfont1⃝
uniformly vanishes.
Define

X^{(L)}_{k,ij,m}\coloneqq\left(\Delta W^{A}_{k}\right)_{im}\left(\Delta W^{A}_{k}\right)_{ij}-\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\Sigma^{A}_{s}\right)_{imij}\nonscript\;\middle\nonscript\;\mathcal{G}_{k-1}\right]\mathrm{d}s,

and
S^{(L)}_{k,ij,m}\coloneqq\sum_{k^{\prime}=0}^{k}X^{(L)}_{k^{\prime},ij,m}
. Observe that
\big\{S^{(L)}_{k,ij,m}\colon k=0,\ldots,L\big\}
is a
(\mathcal{G}_{k})-
martingale, where the filtration
\mathcal{G}_{k}
is defined in (
4.51
). Hence, by Doob’s martingale inequality, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\left|\sum_{k<Lt}X_{k,ij,m}^{(L)}\right|^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\left|S_{\left\lfloor Lt\right\rfloor,ij,m}^{(L)}\right|^{2}\right]\leq 4\,\mathbb{E}\left[\left|S_{\left\lfloor LT\right\rfloor,ij,m}^{(L)}\right|^{2}\right].

(5.33)

Fix
k=0,\ldots,L-1
and compute the following conditional expectation.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(S^{(L)}_{k,ij,m}\right)^{2}\nonscript\;\middle\nonscript\;\mathcal{G}_{k-1}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(S^{(L)}_{k-1,ij,m}\right)^{2}+2X^{(L)}_{k,ij,m}S^{(L)}_{k-1,ij,m}+\left(X^{(L)}_{k,ij,m}\right)^{2}\nonscript\;\middle\nonscript\;\mathcal{G}_{k-1}\right]

\displaystyle=\left(S^{(L)}_{k-1,ij,m}\right)^{2}+\mathbb{E}\left[\left(X^{(L)}_{k,ij,m}\right)^{2}\nonscript\;\middle\nonscript\;\mathcal{G}_{k-1}\right].

(5.34)

The cross-term disappear as
\mathbb{E}\left[X^{(L)}_{k,ij,m}\nonscript\;\middle\nonscript\;\mathcal{G}_{k-1}\right]=0
. Furthermore, conditionally on
\mathcal{G}_{k-1}
, observe that
\big(X^{(L)}_{k,ij,m}\big)^{2}
is the variance of a product of two normal random variable with
O(L^{-1})
variance, uniformly in
k
by (
4.9
), so

\sup_{0\leq k<L}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(X^{(L)}_{k,ij,m}\right)^{2}\nonscript\;\middle\nonscript\;\mathcal{G}_{k-1}\right]\leq CL^{-2}.

Hence, plugging it back into (
5.33
), we obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\left|\sum_{k<Lt}\raisebox{0.5pt}{\textcircled{\fontsize{8.5pt}{0}\fontfamily{and}\selectfont 1}}\,\right|^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{E^{(L)}_{R}}\right]\leq R^{2}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\left|\sum_{k<Lt}X_{k,ij,m}^{(L)}\right|^{2}\right]\leq CR^{2}L^{-1}.

(5.35)

Step 9: Prove that the terms
\raisebox{0.5pt}{\textcircled{\fontsize{8.5pt}{0}\fontfamily{and}\selectfont 2}}-\raisebox{0.5pt}{\textcircled{\fontsize{8.5pt}{0}\fontfamily{and}\selectfont 3}}
uniformly vanishes.
The term
\fontsize{8.5pt}{0}\fontfamily{and}\selectfont2⃝
can be estimated directly using Cauchy-Schwarz, Tonelli, and (
4.10
):

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\left|\sum_{k<Lt}\raisebox{0.5pt}{\textcircled{\fontsize{8.5pt}{0}\fontfamily{and}\selectfont 2}}\,\right|^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{E^{(L)}_{R}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq R^{2}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{k=0}^{L}\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\left(\Sigma^{A}_{s}-\Sigma^{A}_{t_{k}}\right)_{imij}\right\nonscript\;\middle\nonscript\;\mathcal{G}_{k-1}\right]\mathrm{d}s\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq CR^{2}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{L}\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}\left|s-t_{k_{s}}\right|^{\kappa/2}\mathrm{d}s\right)^{2}\leq CR^{2}L^{-\kappa}.

(5.36)

The estimation for term
\fontsize{8.5pt}{0}\fontfamily{and}\selectfont3⃝
is straightforward and similar to (
4.57
):

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\left|\sum_{k<Lt}\raisebox{0.5pt}{\textcircled{\fontsize{8.5pt}{0}\fontfamily{and}\selectfont 3}}\,\right|^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{E^{(L)}_{R}}\right]\leq CR^{2}L^{-1}.

(5.37)

Step 10: Uniform bound between
J
and
\widetilde{J}^{(L)}
.
From equations (
5.29
) (
5.31
), (
5.35
), (
5.36
), and (
5.37
), we deduce that

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\left\lVert\sum_{k<Lt}D_{k}^{(L)}\hskip-2.0pt\left(J_{k}^{(L)},h_{k}^{(L)}\right)\right\rVert^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{E^{(L)}_{R}}\right]\leq CR^{6}L^{-\min(1,\kappa)}.

(5.38)

We then plug (
5.38
) into (
5.28
), together with Tonelli’s theorem, to get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\left\lVert J_{t}-\widetilde{J}^{(L)}_{t}\right\rVert^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{E^{(L)}_{R}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq CR^{2}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{E^{(L)}_{R}}\int_{0}^{T}\left\lVert\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muJ\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu_{s}^{(L)}-J_{s}\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right]+CR^{6}L^{-\min(1/2,\,\kappa)}

\displaystyle\leq CR^{2}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{E^{(L)}_{R}}\int_{0}^{T}\left\lVert\widetilde{J}_{s}^{(L)}-J_{s}\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right]+CR^{6}L^{-\min(1/2,\,\kappa)}.

We use (
5.21
) for the last inequality. Hence, by Gronwall lemma, we deduce:

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\left\lVert J_{t}-\widetilde{J}^{(L)}_{t}\right\rVert^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{E^{(L)}_{R}}\right]\leq CR^{6}L^{-\min(1/2,\,\kappa)}\exp\big(CR^{2}\big).

(5.39)

Step 11: Difference between
G
and
g
.

We first estimate the
L^{1}
distance between the discrete gradients
g_{k}^{(L)}
and the continuous-time limit

. For each

, we have the identity

\displaystyle\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muG\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu_{t}^{(L)}-G_{t}

\displaystyle=\left(J_{L}^{(L)}-J_{1}\right)J_{t}^{-1}+g_{k_{t}}^{(L)}-J_{L}^{(L)}J_{t}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\left(J_{L}^{(L)}-J_{1}\right)J_{t}^{-1}+g_{k_{t}}^{(L)}\left(J_{t}-J_{k_{t}}^{(L)}\right)J_{t}^{-1}.

Hence, by Assumption
4

5.15
), (
5.21
), and (
5.39

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\left\lVert G_{t}-\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muG\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu^{(L)}_{t}\right\rVert\mathbbm{1}_{E^{(L)}_{R}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muJ\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu^{(L)}_{1}-J_{1}\right\rVert^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{E^{(L)}_{R}}\right]^{1/2}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\left\lVert(J_{t})^{-1}\right\rVert^{2}\right]^{1/2}

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\left\lVert\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muG\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu_{t}\right\rVert^{4}\right]^{1/4}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\left\lVert J_{t}-\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muJ\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu^{(L)}_{t}\right\rVert^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{E^{(L)}_{R}}\right]^{1/2}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\left\lVert(J_{t})^{-1}\right\rVert^{4}\right]^{1/4}.

\displaystyle\leq CR^{3}L^{-\min(1/4,\,\kappa/2)}\exp\big(CR^{2}\big).

We plug it in (
5.24
) to obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\left\lVert G_{t}-\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muG\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 0.0mu^{(L)}_{t}\right\rVert\right]\leq C_{1}R^{3}L^{-\min(1/4,\,\kappa/2)}\exp\big(C_{2}R^{2}\big)+C_{3}\delta+\frac{C_{4}}{\delta R^{4}}.

(5.40)

To conclude, given any

Appendix B
Auxiliary lemma

Lemma 3
.

Let
\left(Y_{t}\right)_{t\in\left[0,T\right]}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be a continuous semimartingale that can be decomposed as
\mathrm{d}Y_{t}=A_{t}\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}M_{t}
, where
A
is a square-integrable adapted process,
M
is a continuous square-integrable martingale with quadratic variation
\mathrm{d}\big[M,M^{\top}\big]_{t}=Q_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t
, and
\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\left\lVert Q_{t}\right\rVert_{F}<Q_{\infty}<\infty
, where
Q_{\infty}
is a deterministic constant. Let
\left(X_{t}\right)_{t\in\left[0,T\right]}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be the unique solution to the linear matrix-valued SDE
\mathrm{d}X_{t}=X_{t}\mathrm{d}Y_{t}
, with
X_{0}
being a deterministic non-zero matrix. Then, for each
p>1
, there exists a constant
C\equiv C(p,d,Q_{\infty},X_{0},T)
such that

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in\left[0,T\right]}\left\lVert X_{t}\right\rVert_{F}^{p}\right]\leq C\,\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(2p\int_{0}^{T}\left|\mathrm{tr}\left(A_{s}\right)\right|\mathrm{d}s\right)\right]^{1/2}.

Proof.

We apply the multidimensional Ito formula and linearity of the trace operator to first get

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\left\lVert X_{t}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}=\mathrm{d}\,\mathrm{tr}(X_{t}^{\top}X_{t})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}\left(\mathrm{d}X_{t}^{\top}X_{t}+X_{t}^{\top}\mathrm{d}X_{t}+\mathrm{d}\left[X^{\top},X\right]_{t}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}\left(X_{t}^{\top}X_{t}\left(\mathrm{d}Y_{t}+\mathrm{d}Y_{t}^{\top}\right)+\mathrm{d}\left[X^{\top},X\right]_{t}\right)

Now, by cyclic permutation invariance of the trace, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left(\mathrm{d}\left[X^{\top},X\right]_{t}\right)=\mathrm{tr}\left(\mathrm{d}\left[X,X^{\top}\right]_{t}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}\left(X_{t}\,\mathrm{d}\left[Y,Y^{\top}\right]_{t}X_{t}^{\top}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}\left(X_{t}^{\top}X_{t}\,\mathrm{d}\left[Y,Y^{\top}\right]_{t}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}\left(X_{t}^{\top}X_{t}Q_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t\right).

Therefore,

\mathrm{d}\left\lVert X_{t}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}=\mathrm{tr}\left(X_{t}^{\top}X_{t}\left(A_{t}+A_{t}^{\top}+Q_{t}\right)\right)\mathrm{d}t+\left\lVert X_{t}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\mathrm{d}N_{t},

(B.1)

where

N_{t}\coloneqq\mathrm{tr}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\frac{X_{s}^{\top}X_{s}}{\left\lVert X_{s}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}}\left(\mathrm{d}M_{s}+\mathrm{d}M_{s}^{\top}\right)\right)

(B.2)

is a martingale with quadratic variation given by

\left[N\right]_{t}=\sum_{i_{1},j_{1},i_{2},j_{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\left\lVert X_{s}\right\rVert_{F}^{-4}\left(X^{\top}_{s}X_{s}\right)_{i_{1}j_{1}}\left(X^{\top}_{s}X_{s}\right)_{i_{2}j_{2}}\mathrm{d}\left[(M+M^{\top})_{i_{1}j_{1}},(M+M^{\top})_{i_{2}j_{2}}\right]_{s}\\

By the Kunita-Watanabe inequality,

\displaystyle\left[N\right]_{t}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i_{1},j_{1},i_{2},j_{2}}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\left\lVert X_{s}\right\rVert_{F}^{-4}\left(X^{\top}_{s}X_{s}\right)^{2}_{i_{1}j_{1}}\mathrm{d}\left[(M+M^{\top})_{i_{1}j_{1}}\right]_{s}\right)^{1/2}\cdot

\displaystyle\qquad\left(\int_{0}^{t}\left\lVert X_{s}\right\rVert_{F}^{-4}\left(X^{\top}_{s}X_{s}\right)^{2}_{i_{2}j_{2}}\mathrm{d}\left[(M+M^{\top})_{i_{2}j_{2}}\right]_{s}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{i,j}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\left\lVert X_{s}\right\rVert_{F}^{-4}\left(X^{\top}_{s}X_{s}\right)^{2}_{ij}\mathrm{d}\left[(M+M^{\top})_{ij}\right]_{s}\right)^{1/2}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq d^{2}\sum_{i,j}\int_{0}^{t}\left\lVert X_{s}\right\rVert_{F}^{-4}\left(X^{\top}_{s}X_{s}\right)^{2}_{ij}\mathrm{d}\left[(M+M^{\top})_{ij}\right]_{s}

\displaystyle\leq 4d^{2}Q_{\infty}\int_{0}^{t}\left\lVert X_{s}\right\rVert_{F}^{-4}\left\lVert X_{s}^{\top}X_{s}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\mathrm{d}s\leq 4d^{2}Q_{\infty}t.

(B.3)

The second inequality follows from Cauchy-Schwarz. Now, by conditioning on
\left\lVert X_{t}\right\rVert_{F}>0
if necessary, we have by the Ito’s formula and (
B.1
)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\log\left\lVert X_{t}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert X_{t}\right\rVert_{F}^{-2}\mathrm{d}\left\lVert X_{t}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert X_{t}\right\rVert_{F}^{-4}\mathrm{d}\left[\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\right]_{t}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert X_{t}\right\rVert_{F}^{-2}\mathrm{tr}\left(X_{t}^{\top}X_{t}\left(A_{t}+A_{t}^{\top}+Q_{t}\right)\right)\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}N_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{d}\left[N\right]_{t}.

Hence, by integrating and taking the exponential, we get

\displaystyle\left\lVert X_{t}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert X_{0}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}\left\lVert X_{s}\right\rVert_{F}^{-2}\mathrm{tr}\left(X_{s}^{\top}X_{s}\left(A_{s}+A_{s}^{\top}+Q_{s}\right)\right)\mathrm{d}s\right)\exp\left(N_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\left[N\right]_{t}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert X_{0}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}\left|\mathrm{tr}\left(2A_{s}+Q_{s}\right)\right|\mathrm{d}s\right)\mathcal{E}(N)_{t}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert X_{0}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\exp\left(dQ_{\infty}T\right)\exp\left(2\int_{0}^{t}\left|\mathrm{tr}\left(A_{s}\right)\right|\mathrm{d}s\right)\mathcal{E}(N)_{t},

where
\mathcal{E}(N)_{t}\coloneqq\exp\left(N_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\left[N\right]_{t}\right)
denotes the stochastic exponential of
N
. The first inequality follows from
\mathrm{tr}\left(AB\right)\leq\left|\mathrm{tr}(A)\right|\left|\mathrm{tr}(B)\right|
. Therefore, for
p>1
, by Cauchy-Schwarz,

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in\left[0,T\right]}\left\lVert X_{t}\right\rVert_{F}^{p}\right]\leq\left\lVert X_{0}\right\rVert_{F}^{p}\exp\left(\frac{p}{2}dQ_{\infty}T\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(2p\int_{0}^{T}\left|\mathrm{tr}\left(A_{s}\right)\right|\mathrm{d}s\right)\right]^{1/2}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in\left[0,T\right]}\left|\mathcal{E}(N)_{t}\right|^{p}\right]^{1/2}.

(B.4)

Now, as
\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(1/2\left[N\right]_{T}\right)\right]\leq\exp\left(2d^{2}Q_{\infty}T\right)<\infty
, Novikov condition implies that
\left(\mathcal{E}(N)_{t}\right)_{t\in\left[0,T\right]}
is a (continuous) martingale. Therefore, by Doob’s inequality, we get

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in\left[0,T\right]}\left|\mathcal{E}(N)_{t}\right|^{p}\right]\leq\left(\frac{p}{p-1}\right)^{p}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\mathcal{E}(N)_{T}\right|^{p}\right]

Finally, we use the definition of the stochastic exponential and Cauchy-Schwarz to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\mathcal{E}(N)_{T}\right|^{p}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(pN_{T}-\frac{p}{2}\left[N\right]_{T}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(pN_{T}-p^{2}\left[N\right]_{T}\right)\exp\left(\frac{2p^{2}-p}{2}\left[N\right]_{T}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{E}(2pN)_{T}\right]^{1/2}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left((2p^{2}-p)\left[N\right]_{T}\right)\right]^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(2(2p^{2}-p)d^{2}Q_{\infty}T\right).

We plug this last inequality into (
B.4
) to conclude the proof, with

C(p,d,Q_{\infty},X_{0},T)\coloneqq\left(\frac{p\left\lVert X_{0}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}}{p-1}\right)^{p/2}\exp\left(2p^{2}d^{2}Q_{\infty}T\right).

∎
