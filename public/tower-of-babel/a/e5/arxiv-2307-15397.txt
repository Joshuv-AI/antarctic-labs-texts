Abstract
. We generalise a result of D. J. Collins on intersections of conjugates of Magnus subgroups of one-relator groups to the context of one-relator products of locally indicable groups.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 20F65, 57K20. Secondary 20E06, 20F06, 57M07

1.
Introduction

Recall that a
Magnus subgroup
of a one-relator group
G=\langle X\mid R=1\rangle
is the subgroup generated by a subset of the generating set
X
omitting at least one generator that occurs in
R
.
In two articles
[
4
,
5
]
, Collins gave a complete analysis of the possible intersections of Magnus subgroups and their conjugates in a one-relator group.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
4
]
)
.

If
M(Y),M(Z)
are the Magnus subgroups of
G
generated by
Y,Z\subset X
respectively, then the intersection of
M(Y)
and
M(Z)
in
G
is either the Magnus subgroup
M(Y\cap Z)
or
M(Y\cap Z)*C
for some cyclic group
C
.

Theorem 1.2

(
[
5
]
)
.

If
g\in G
and the intersection of
M(Y)
and
g^{-1}M(Z)g
in
G
is not cyclic, then there are elements
h\in M(Z)
and
k\in M(Y)
such that
g=hk
in
G
.

Note that, in the conclusion of Theorem
1.2
we have
h^{-1}M(Z)h=M(Z)
and
k^{-1}M(Y)k=M(Y)
, so
M(Y)\cap g^{-1}M(Z)g=k^{-1}(M(Y)\cap M(Z))k
and Theorem
1.1
applies.

In the case of a one-relator group with torsion, the above results can be strengthened
[
4
]
: The second case
M(Y\cap Z)*C
in Theorem
1.1
cannot occur, and in Theorem
1.2
one can replace “cyclic” by “trivial”.

In
[
10
]
, the first-named author generalised Theorem
1.1
– and also the stronger version in the torsion case – to the situation of one-relator products of locally indicable groups.
The purpose of the present paper is to provide analogous generalisations of both versions of Theorem
1.2
. Specifically, we prove the following two results.

Theorem A
.

Let
\{G_{\lambda};\lambda\in\Lambda\}
be a collection of locally indicable groups, let
R\in\ast_{\lambda\in\Lambda}G_{\lambda}
be a cyclically reduced word of free product length at least
2
, and let
G:=(\ast_{\lambda\in\Lambda}G_{\lambda})/\langle\langle R\rangle\rangle
, where
\langle\langle R\rangle\rangle
denotes the normal closure of
R
. Let
I,J
be subsets of
\Lambda
and
g\in\ast_{\lambda\in\Lambda}G_{\lambda}
. If the intersection of
\ast_{i\in I}G_{i}
and

g^{-1}\cdot\left(\ast_{j\in J}G_{j}\right)\cdot g

in
G
is not cyclic, then there are elements
h\in\ast_{j\in J}G_{j}
and
k\in\ast_{i\in I}G_{i}
such that
g=hk
in
G
.

Theorem B
.

Let
G
be as in Theorem
A
with
R=Z^{m}
in
\ast_{\lambda\in\Lambda}G_{\lambda}
for some
Z\in\ast_{\lambda\in\Lambda}G_{\lambda}
and some
m>1
. If the intersection of
\ast_{i\in I}G_{i}
and

g^{-1}\cdot\left(\ast_{j\in J}G_{j}\right)\cdot g

in
G
is not trivial, then there are elements
h\in\ast_{j\in J}G_{j}
and
k\in\ast_{i\in I}G_{i}
such that
g=hk
in
G
.

Our proofs will make extensive use of the following special case of Theorem
A
due to Brodsky
[
2
, Theorem 6]
(see also
[
11
, Theorem F]
).

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
G
be as in Theorem
A
. If
g\in G
and
\lambda,\mu\in\Lambda
are such that the intersection in
G
of
G_{\lambda}
and
g^{-1}G_{\mu}g
is not cyclic, then
\lambda=\mu
and
g\in G_{\lambda}
.

We also require the following stronger version when the relator is a proper power. See
[
11
, Corollary 3.7]
.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
G
be as in Theorem
B
. If
g\in G
and
\lambda,\mu\in\Lambda
are such that the intersection in
G
of
G_{\lambda}
and
g^{-1}G_{\mu}g
is not trivial, then
\lambda=\mu
and
g\in G_{\lambda}
.

Other results on locally indicable groups from the literature that we will use include the following:

Theorem 1.5

(Freiheitssatz,
[
1
]
,
[
7
]
,
[
13
]
)
.

Let
A,B
be locally indicable groups and
R\in A*B

a cyclically reduced word of length at least two.

The natural map
A\to\frac{A*B}{\langle\langle R\rangle\rangle}
is injective.

Theorem 1.6

(Weinbaum’s Theorem,
[
8
]
)
.

Let
A,B
be locally indicable groups and
R\in A*B

a cyclically reduced word of length at least two.

No proper subword of
R
represents the identity element of
\frac{A*B}{\langle\langle R\rangle\rangle}
.

Theorem 1.7

(
[
8
]
)
.

Let
A,B
be locally indicable groups and
R\in A*B

a cyclically reduced word of length at least two which is not a proper power in
A*B
.
Then
\frac{A*B}{\langle\langle R\rangle\rangle}
is locally indicable.

A
right ordering
on a group
G
is a total order
<
such that
(\forall~x,y,z\in G)

y<z\Rightarrow yx<zx
.
A group is
right orderable
if it has a right ordering.

Theorem 1.8

(
[
3
]
)
.

Every locally indicable group is right orderable.

In §
2

we reformulate the main theorems in terms of 2–complexes with
two distinguished sets of edges.
We explain
why the proof of Theorem
A
reduces to consideration of a free subgroup
K<[G,G]
of rank
2
in the conjugacy intersection.
Also
we explain how pictures are to be used,
and the essential use of a right order to select maximal and minimal edges
on each disc in the pictures is described.

In §3 we use the methods of §
2
to prove the torsion version of the main theorem, Theorem
B
.

We begin §4 with the plan for the proof in the torsion-free case, Theorem
A
, which is broken into
several stages. We then introduce a notion of complexity that forms the basis for an inductive proof.

We make some technical adjustments to our
2
-complex, and prove the initial step of the induction.
The inductive step involves lifting our pictures to a suitable cover; and then splits into two cases. The first of these is when the lifted annular pictures contain a relabelled disc meeting all the edges
lying in the generators of
K
:
this is handled in §
5
. The other, more complicated, case is dealt with in the final three sections. In §
6
we reduce to consideration of one particular conjugating element; in §
7
we show that annular pictures for conjugacies with that particular conjugating element have a special form; and finally in §
8
we use that special form to complete the proof.

Reduction 1
It suffices to prove Theorems

and

If

The result then follows from the conjugacy properties of free products as above.

So from now on we shall assume that

2.2.
Pictures

We shall make use of the duals of van Kampen diagrams,
known as
pictures
.
Originally a van Kampen diagram over a presentation was
a finite decomposition of a surface (usually a disc or an annulus)
as a 2-complex mapping cellularly to the standard 2-complex for the presentation.
Pictures were introduced by Rourke in
[
12
]
and studied in the relative context by the second author
[
13
]
.
We generalise slightly the original definition.
A picture arises from a continuous map
\Sigma\to Y
, where
\Sigma
is a compact orientable surface,
here a disc or an annulus and
Y
a
2
-complex,
using transversality. It consists of a finite collection of
discs
or
(fat) vertices
,
whose interiors each map homeomorphically onto the interior of a
2
-cell of
Y
,
and a properly embedded
1
-submanifold of the complement of the interiors of the discs,
each component of which is called an
arc
, carries a transverse orientation and is labelled by a
1
-cell of
X
.
A small regular neighbourhood of each arc is mapped to the corresponding
1
-cell in the direction of the transverse orientation.
The generalisation of the original pictures is that we do not insist that the 1–cells in
Y
be
loops. For more details on pictures, and examples of their usefulness in group theory we refer the reader to
[
6
]
,
[
9
]
.

For the proofs of our main theorems,
we are interested here in pictures over

Y=X\cup\mathcal{S}\cup\mathcal{T}\cup\alpha
, where
X
is a 2–complex
(not necessarily connected),
\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T}
are sets of edges, and
\alpha
is a 2-cell, such that
Y
is connected.
We get a
relative picture
over
Y
(relative to
\mathcal{S}\cup\mathcal{T}\cup\alpha
)
by removing from the picture all discs that do not
map to
\alpha
, and all arcs other than those labelled in
\mathcal{S}\cup\mathcal{T}
.
Segments of the boundaries of the
\alpha
discs and of
\Sigma

between
\mathcal{S}\cup\mathcal{T}
arcs are labelled by words describing paths in
X
.
In particular we shall be interested in loops in
Y

of the form
U=Q_{1}VQ_{2}^{-1}
where
U
is a loop in
X\cup\mathcal{S}
based at
v_{0}
,

V
is a loop in
X\cup\mathcal{T}
based at
v_{1}
,
and
Q_{1},Q_{2}
paths in
Y^{(1)}
from
v_{0}
to
v_{1}
.
There is an obvious way to draw a corresponding picture
as a rectangle with top side labelled
U
, bottom side labelled
V
and vertical sides labelled
Q_{1},Q_{2}
.

Figure 1.
The top side is labelled by
U\in X\cup\mathcal{S}
, the bottom by
V\in X\cup\mathcal{T}

When

, the vertical sides can be identified to give a picture on

We refer to these as rectangular and annular relative pictures.
Given two such rectangular pictures
\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime}
for

and for

the right hand side of one can be identified with the left hand side of the other to

2.3.
Syllables and syllable length

In analogy with a commonly used terminology for words in a free product with amalgamation, we will use the term
syllable
to denote a subpath of a path in
X\cup\mathcal{S}\cup\mathcal{T}
which contains
\mathcal{S}
-edges or
\mathcal{T}
-edges but not both, and is maximal with respect to that property – except possibly for an initial and/or a terminal sub-path that is contained in
X
.
We will speak of
\mathcal{S}
-syllables and
\mathcal{T}
-syllables with the obvious meaning. The
syllable length

SL(P)
of a path

is defined to be the number of syllables into which it can be subdivided.

We will also use another measure of length: the
\mathcal{S}\cup\mathcal{T}

of a path in
Y^{(1)}
is the number of occurrences of edges from
\mathcal{S}^{\pm 1}\cup\mathcal{T}^{\pm 1}
.

2.4.
Strong reduction

An edge-path in
X^{(1)}\cup\mathcal{S}\cup\mathcal{T}
is said to be
strongly reduced
if it is reduced and has no subpath of form
u^{\varepsilon}\cdot\gamma\cdot u^{-\varepsilon}
with
u\in\mathcal{S}\cup\mathcal{T}
,
\varepsilon=\pm 1
, and
\gamma
a closed path in
X^{(1)}
that is nullhomotopic in
X
. Every path in
X^{(1)}\cup\mathcal{S}\cup\mathcal{T}
is homotopic rel end-points in
X\cup\mathcal{S}\cup\mathcal{T}
to a strongly reduced path. A closed path in
X^{(1)}\cup\mathcal{S}\cup\mathcal{T}
is
strongly cyclically reduced
if each of its cyclic subpaths is strongly reduced. Every closed path in
X^{(1)}\cup\mathcal{S}\cup\mathcal{T}
is freely homotopic in
X\cup\mathcal{S}\cup\mathcal{T}
to a strongly cyclically reduced path.
For example, the closed path
\partial\alpha
in
X^{(1)}\cup\mathcal{S}\cup\mathcal{T}
is strongly cyclically reduced.

We introduce a move on rectangular and annular relative pictures called
boundary surgery
. This consists of cutting an
\mathcal{S}
-arc (resp
\mathcal{T}
-arc) close to the top (resp. bottom) boundary and moving the cut ends onto the top (resp. bottom) boundary. The inverse move identifies a pair of adjacent arcs on the top or bottom boundary if they have the same label, opposite orientations, and are separated on the boundary by a path labelled by a nullhomotopic closed path in
X
.

We shall suppose that our annular pictures have
strongly reduced boundaries.
For an annular picture it may be that the top or bottom label is not strongly cyclically reduced,
even if it was formed from a
rectangular picture with strongly reduced labels on each side.
We may again perform boundary surgeries on such a picture.
If the original picture represented a conjugacy equation
U=QVQ^{-1}

with
U
based at
v_{0}
and

V
based at
v_{1}

then the surgered version will represent a slightly different conjugacy equation
U^{\prime}=Q^{\prime}V^{\prime}{Q^{\prime}}^{-1}
.
Here
U^{\prime}
is a conjugate of
U
in the edge labels in
X\cup\mathcal{S}
– say
