On characteristic invariants of matrix pencils and linear relations
H. GernandtF. Martínez PeríaF. Philipp, C. Trunk

Abstract
The relationship between linear relations and matrix pencils is investigated. Given a linear relation, we introduce its Weyr characteristic. If the linear relation is the range (or the kernel) representation of a given matrix pencil, we show that there is a correspondence between this characteristic and the Kronecker canonical form of the pencil. This relationship is exploited to obtain estimations on the invariant characteristics of matrix pencils under rank one perturbations.



1
Introduction

Canonical forms for square matrices like the Jordan normal form are a fundamental tool in matrix analysis and uniquely describe the matrix up to similarity transformations: Two matrices are similar if their Jordan normal forms coincide. This is the case if and only if
they share the same eigenvalues and in
these eigenvalues the Segre or the Weyr characteristic coincide.
These two characteristics count the Jordan chains at an eigenvalue in two different
ways.

The Weyr characteristic (for a given eigenvalue
\lambda
) is a finite, non-increasing sequence
w
of natural numbers, the first entry of
w
corresponds to the dimension of the kernel
N(A-\lambda)
of
A-\lambda
, the second to the dimension of the quotient space
N(A-\lambda)^{2}/N(A-\lambda)
, etc. The
Segre characteristic is the conjugate partition of the
Weyr characteristic; it counts
the dimensions of the Jordan blocks corresponding to the eigenvalue
\lambda
or, what is the same,
it counts the length of the corresponding Jordan chains

[
30
,
31
,
34
,
35
]
.

Weyr and Segre characteristics exist also for matrix pencils.
Recall that matrix pencils allow a normal form: the
Kronecker canonical form, see Appendix
A
. Instead of one Segre characteristic as above,
four different types of indices appear: the finite and the infinite
elementary divisors, the row minimal indices and the column minimal indices.
These indices measure the sizes of the different blocks in the
Kronecker canonical form.
Moreover, two matrix pencils are strictly equivalent if and only if all the four indices coincide, see
[
22
]
, or e.g.
[
12
,
13
]
. Thus they can be considered as Segre-type
indices for matrix pencils.
As in the case of matrices, where the Segre and the Weyr characteristics are conjugate partitions, we define here the Weyr characteristic of a matrix pencil as the conjugate partition of the
four Segre-type indices, see Section
4
.
We cite

[
17
]
and
[
26
]
where the terms Segre and
Weyr characteristics were used for (regular) matrix pencils.

Parallel to the Weyr characteristic of matrix pencils, we introduce the Weyr characteristic of subspaces in the cartesian product

\mathbb{C}^{m}\times\mathbb{C}^{m}
in Section
3
. These subspaces are usually called
linear relations in
\mathbb{C}^{m}

[
1
,
16
]
. Different from the matrix
situation, it is well-known that linear relations contain not
only Jordan chains but also
chains at the eigenvalue
\infty
, singular chains,
and
multi-shifts
, see
[
9
,
10
,
14
,
32
,
33
]
.
A recent result states that every linear relation
in a finite dimensional space allows a direct sum
decomposition into four parts which are related to the
above chains
[
10
]
. We use this decomposition and define in Section

3
the Weyr characteristic of a linear relation.
As in the case of matrices and matrix pencils, it turns out
that the so defined Weyr characteristic
is a set of invariants of a linear relation in the
following sense (cf. Section
5
): Given two linear relations in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
, their Weyr
characteristics coincide if and only
if the linear relations are strictly equivalent.
Concerning this notion, we refer to Section
2
.

The main goal of this paper is to reveal
the connection between the Weyr characteristic
of matrix pencils and the
Weyr characteristic of linear relations. The connection is established
by the kernel and range representations of a matrix pencil, see also
[
14
,
29
]
.
In order to introduce those representations, we consider a matrix pencil of
the form

sE-F,\quad\mbox{with}\quad E,F\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times m}

If
E
is non-singular, multiplication of the pencil from the left and the right with the inverse of
E
leads to the pencils

sI-E^{-1}F\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad sI-FE^{-1},

which shows that the spectrum of the original pencil coincides with those of the matrices
E^{-1}F
and
FE^{-1}
.
However, in general the matrix
E
is singular. But its graph is a linear relation, and
the inverse of a linear relation always exists. Hence, we consider the linear relations

E^{-1}F=\left\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{C}^{m}\times\mathbb{C}^{m}:Fx=Ey\right\}\quad\mbox{and}\quad FE^{-1}=\left\{(Ex,Fx):x\in\mathbb{C}^{m}\right\},

which were already studied in
[
7
,
8
]
.
We
call
E^{-1}F
the
kernel
and
FE^{-1}
the
range
representation
(see also
[
14
]
)
as we have

E^{-1}F=N[F,-E]\qquad\mbox{ and }\qquad FE^{-1}=R\begin{bmatrix}E\\
F\end{bmatrix}.

It is natural to compare the Weyr characteristic
of a matrix pencil and the Weyr characteristic of the corresponding
kernel and range representations. For a given matrix pencil
sE-F
with
nontrivial row minimal indices and/or column minimal indices
a certain peculiarity happens: depending on the representation (either
E^{-1}F
or
FE^{-1}
) only the
row minimal indices and/or column minimal indices
which are large enough are visible in the linear relation.
We provide two examples for this in Section
4
.
Nevertheless, we show
how to calculate the Weyr characteristic of the corresponding
kernel and range representations from the Kronecker canonical form of a given matrix pencil.

Also, considering the sizes of the matrices
E
and
F
, it is possible to recover
the Weyr characteristic of the matrix pencil
sE-F
from the
Weyr characteristic of its kernel or its range representation,
see Sections
5
and
6
. Hence,
one can associate
a linear object (the kernel or the range representation) to a
matrix pencil. This is extremely helpful, for instance, in perturbation
theory or stability, see e.g.
[
11
,
23
]
.
As a consequence, new perturbation results for the Kronecker form of matrix pencils under rank one perturbations can be derived from perturbation results for the Weyr characteristic of linear relations given in
[
29
]
. These results can then be used to describe the controllability indices of the associated descriptor systems, see
[
15
]
.
In Section
7
below we give an application to the rank one perturbation theory for matrix pencils where both (kernel and range) representations are used.

2
Linear relations in
\mathbb{C}^{m}

Throughout this paper elements (pairs) from
\mathbb{C}^{m}\times\mathbb{C}^{m}
will be denoted by
(x,y)
, where
x,y\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
. A
linear relation
in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
is a linear subspace of
\mathbb{C}^{m}\times\mathbb{C}^{m}
. A matrix
A\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
can be identified with a linear relation in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
via its
graph:

\displaystyle\Gamma(A):=\left\{(x,Ax):\ x\in\mathbb{C}^{m}\right\}.

For the basic notions and properties of linear relations we refer to
[
1
,
20
]
, and for the more advanced notions see
[
9
,
32
,
33
]
. Here, we denote the domain and the range of a linear relation
S
in
X
by
{\rm dom\,}S
and
R(S)
, respectively,

\displaystyle{\rm dom\,}S

\displaystyle=\left\{x\in\mathbb{C}^{m}\ :\ (x,y)\in S\ \text{for some $y\in\mathbb{C}^{m}$}\right\}\quad\mbox{and}

\displaystyle R(S)

\displaystyle=\left\{y\in\mathbb{C}^{m}\ :\ (x,y)\in S\ \text{for some $x\in\mathbb{C}^{m}$}\right\}.

Furthermore,
N(S)
and
\mul(S)
denote the
kernel
and the

multivalued
part of
S
,

\displaystyle N(S)=\left\{x\in\mathbb{C}^{m}\;:\;(x,0)\in S\right\}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mul(S)=\left\{y\in\mathbb{C}^{m}\;:\;(0,y)\in S\right\}.

Obviously, a linear relation
S
is the graph of a matrix if and only if
\mul(S)=\{0\}
.

Given a linear relation
S
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
, one defines
\lambda S=\left\{(x,\lambda y)\ :\ (x,y)\in S\right\}
.
For relations
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
the
operator-like sum

S_{1}+S_{2}
is the relation defined by

\displaystyle S_{1}+S_{2}=\left\{(x,y+z)\ :\ (x,y)\in S_{1},\ (x,z)\in S_{2}\right\}.

If
S_{1}\cap S_{2}=\{0\}
then the
direct sum
is given by

\displaystyle S_{1}\oplus S_{2}:=\{(x_{1}+x_{2},y_{1}+y_{2})\ :\ (x_{i},y_{i})\in S_{i},\;i=1,2\}.

The product of the linear relations
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
is the linear relation in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
defined by

S_{1}S_{2}=\left\{(x,z)\ :\ (y,z)\in S_{1}\mbox{ and }(x,y)\in S_{2}\;\mbox{ for some }y\in\mathbb{C}^{m}\right\}

As for matrices, the sum and product of linear relations are associative operations.

The inverse
S^{-1}
of a linear relation
S
in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
always exists, and it is the linear relation in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
given by

S^{-1}=\left\{(y,x)\ :\ (x,y)\in S\right\}.

In the following, if
M
is a matrix in
\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
and
S
is a linear relation in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
, we denote by
\left[\begin{smallmatrix}M&0\\
0&M\end{smallmatrix}\right]\cdot S
the action of
M
onto
S
:

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}M&0\\
0&M\end{bmatrix}\cdot S:=\{(Mx,My)\in\mathbb{C}^{m}\times\mathbb{C}^{m}:(x,y)\in S\}.

Given two linear relations
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
we say that they are
strictly equivalent
if there exists an invertible matrix
T\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
such that

\displaystyle S_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}T&0\\
0&T\end{bmatrix}\cdot S_{1}.

(1)

If one identifies the matrices
T
and
T^{-1}
with their graphs, (
1
)
can be written in the sense of linear relations in the following form

\displaystyle S_{2}=TS_{1}T^{-1}.

The notions of eigenvalue, root manifolds and point spectrum also apply to linear relations. Given
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
,
S-\lambda
stands for the linear relation
S-\lambda I
:

\displaystyle S-\lambda=\left\{(x,y-\lambda x)\ :\ (x,y)\in S\right\}.

Then,
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
is an
eigenvalue of
S
if
N(S-\lambda)\neq\{0\}
. On the other hand, we say that
S
has an
eigenvalue at

\infty
if
\mul(S)\neq\{0\}
. The point spectrum of
S
is the set
\sigma_{p}(S)
consisting of the eigenvalues
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}
of
S
.

We denote
S^{0}:=I
, where
I
denotes the identity relation (i.e. the graph of the identity matrix in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
). For
k=1,2,\ldots
the
k
-th power of
S
is defined recursively by

S^{k}:=S\cdot S^{k-1}.

Thus, we have
(x_{k},x_{0})\in S^{k}
if and only if there exist
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k-1}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
such that

(x_{k},x_{k-1}),(x_{k-1},x_{k-2}),\ldots,(x_{1},x_{0})\in S.

(2)

In what follows we show that any linear relation
S
in a finite-dimensional space admits a decomposition into four different parts. To do so, we have
to introduce some subspaces of
\mathbb{C}^{m}
and subrelations of
S
.
We start with the following notions:
A sequence of pairs in
S
such as in (
2
) is called a
chain
in
S
. The chain is called
singular
, if
x_{k}=x_{0}=0
.
The root manifolds
\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(S)
and
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}(S)
are given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(S):=\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}N(S-\lambda)^{k}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{R}_{\infty}(S):=\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mul S^{k},

respectively.
Note that
\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(S)=\mathcal{R}_{0}(S-\lambda)
and
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}(S)=\mathcal{R}_{\infty}(S-\lambda)
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
.
If
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
and
x\in N(S-\lambda)^{l}
, then there exist vectors
x_{1},\ldots,x_{l-1}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
with

(x,x_{l-1}),(x_{l-1},x_{l-2}),\ldots,(x_{1},0)\in S-\lambda.

(3)

A chain in
S-\lambda
as in (
3
) is called a
Jordan chain of
S
at
\lambda
. A chain of the form

(0,x_{1}),(x_{1},x_{2}),\ldots,(x_{l-1},x_{l})\in S

is called a
Jordan chain of
S
at
\infty
.
Note that a singular chain is always a Jordan chain at both zero and
\infty
. The notion of singular chains
will be used for a finer analysis of the root manifolds. For this,
the
singular chains subspace
associated to
S
is defined by

\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S):=\mathcal{R}_{0}(S)\cap\mathcal{R}_{\infty}(S).

The following lemma is from
[
9
]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
S
be a linear relation in a vector space
X
.
Consider a singular chain of the form

(0,x_{s}),(x_{s},x_{s-1}),\dots,(x_{2},x_{1}),(x_{1},0)\in S.

Then, for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
, the vectors defined by

z_{j}:=\sum_{i=1}^{j}\begin{pmatrix}s-i\\
j-i\end{pmatrix}\lambda^{j-i}x_{i},\quad j=1,\ldots,s,

(4)

satisfy

(0,z_{s}),(z_{s},z_{s-1}),\ldots,(z_{2},z_{1}),(z_{1},0)\in S-\lambda.

This implies that, for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}
,

\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S)\subset\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(S).

Moreover, for
\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}

with
\lambda\neq\mu
, in
[
9
]
it is shown that

\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S)=\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(S)\cap\mathcal{R}_{\mu}(S).

(5)

Obviously, if
\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S)\neq\{0\}
then
\sigma_{p}(S)=\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}
.
Otherwise, if
\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S)=\{0\}
, then the number of different eigenvalues in
\sigma_{p}(S)
is bounded by the space dimension
m
, cf.
[
32
, Proposition 3.2 and Theorem 4.6]
. Hence, it is natural
to call an eigenvalue
\lambda\in\sigma_{p}(S)

degenerate
if
\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(S)\subset\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S)
and
proper
otherwise.
A linear relation
S
is called
completely singular
if
S\subseteq\mathcal{R}_{c}(S)\times\mathcal{R}_{c}(S)
. If
S
is not completely singular,

\displaystyle S_{\rm sing}:=S\cap(\mathcal{R}_{c}(S)\times\mathcal{R}_{c}(S)),

denotes the
completely singular part of
S
.

Finally, there may exist a part of
S
which has no Jordan chains
at all, e.g.

S:=\text{span}\{(x_{1},x_{2}),(x_{2},x_{3}),\ldots,(x_{l-1},x_{l})\},

where
x_{1},\ldots,x_{l}
are linear independent vectors in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
.
Then this linear relation has empty spectrum. A linear relation
with empty spectrum is called a
multi-shift
.

In the following we present a decomposition result for
linear relations in finite dimensional spaces, see
[
9
]
.
To do so, we need the notion of a reducing sum decomposition, cf.
[
10
]
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
S
be a linear relation in a vector space
X
. Assume that

X=D_{1}\oplus D_{2}\oplus\cdots\oplus D_{n}

and

S=S_{1}\oplus S_{2}\oplus\cdots\oplus S_{n},

(6)

where
S_{j}
is a linear relation in
D_{j}
for
j=1,\ldots,n
.
Then the decomposition (
6
) is called a
reducing sum decomposition of
S
with
respect to
(D_{1},\ldots,D_{n})
.

The following result is taken from
[
33
, Lemma 8.1]
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
S
be a linear relation in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
and assume that

S
has a reducing sum decomposition
with respect to
(D_{1},\ldots,D_{n})
as in Definition
2.2
.
Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
, then

S^{k}=S_{1}^{k}\oplus S_{2}^{k}\oplus\cdots\oplus S_{n}^{k},\quad

is a reducing sum decomposition of
S^{k}

with respect to
(D_{1},\ldots,D_{n})
.

Let
S
be a linear relation in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
and assume that

S
has a reducing sum decomposition
S=S_{1}\oplus S_{2}

with respect to
\mathbb{C}^{m}=D_{1}\oplus D_{2}
. First of all, let us show that

N(S)=N(S_{1})\oplus N(S_{2}).

(7)

Obviously,
N(S_{1})+N(S_{2})\subset N(S)
. On the other hand, if
x\in N(S_{1})\cap N(S_{2})
then

(x,0)\in S_{1}
and
(x,0)\in S_{2}
.
As
S=S_{1}\oplus S_{2}
is a direct sum,
x=0
follows and
the sum of the nullspaces is a direct sum.

Also, if
x\in N(S)
then there exist
y_{1}\in D_{1}
and

y_{2}\in D_{2}
such that
(x,0)=(x_{1},y_{1})+(x_{2},y_{2})

with
(x_{1},y_{1})\in S_{1}
and
(x_{2},y_{2})\in S_{2}
. As
S_{1}\oplus S_{2}
is a reduced sum decomposition,
y_{1}=y_{2}=0
and the proof is completed.

By Lemma
2.3
it is immediate that

N(S^{k})=N(S_{1}^{k})\oplus N(S_{2}^{k})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{R}_{0}(S)=\mathcal{R}_{0}(S_{1})\oplus\mathcal{R}_{0}(S_{2}).

(8)

Given
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
, replacing the linear relations
S,S_{1}
, and
S_{2}
by
S-\lambda,S_{1}-\lambda
, and
S_{2}-\lambda
, respectively, we conclude

N((S-\lambda)^{k})=N((S_{1}-\lambda)^{k})\oplus N((S_{2}-\lambda)^{k})\mbox{ and }\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(S)=\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(S_{1})\oplus\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(S_{2}).

(9)

Moreover, if
\mu\in\mathbb{C}
,

\mu\neq\lambda
, by (
5
) we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(S)\cap\mathcal{R}_{\mu}(S)=\left(\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(S_{1})\oplus\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(S_{2})\right)\cap\left(\mathcal{R}_{\mu}(S_{1})\oplus\mathcal{R}_{\mu}(S_{2})\right)

(10)

\displaystyle=\left(\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(S_{1})\cap\mathcal{R}_{\mu}(S_{1})\right)\oplus\left(\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(S_{2})\cap\mathcal{R}_{\mu}(S_{2})\right)=\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S_{1})\oplus\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S_{2}).

In a similar way, replacing
S,S_{1}
, and
S_{2}
by their inverses we obtain

\mul(S^{k})=\mul(S_{1}^{k})\oplus\mul(S_{2}^{k}).

(11)

Finally, notice that it is possible to show that

R(S^{k})=R(S_{1}^{k})\oplus R(S_{2}^{k}).

(12)

If
S
has a reducing sum decomposition as in (
6
) then decompositions similar to those presented in (
7
)-(
12
) follow by induction.

Given a proper eigenvalue
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
of
S
, consider the subrelation

S\cap\left(\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(S)\times\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(S)\right).

Since
S_{\rm sing}
is contained in it and
\mathcal{R}_{c}(S)\subset\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(S)
, it is possible to find a subspace
D_{\lambda}
such that
\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(S)=\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S)\oplus D_{\lambda}
and a linear operator
J_{\lambda}:D_{\lambda}\rightarrow D_{\lambda}
with
\sigma_{p}(J_{\lambda})=\{\lambda\}
such that

S\cap\left(\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(S)\times\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(S)\right)=S_{\rm sing}\oplus J_{\lambda},

(13)

is a reducing sum decomposition with respect to
(\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S),D_{\lambda})
, see
[
10
]
. If
\infty
is a proper eigenvalue of
S
then
J_{\infty}
stands for a linear relation with
\sigma_{p}(J_{\infty})=\{\infty\}
, which is the inverse (of the graph) of a linear operator on a subspace
D_{\infty}
such that
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}(S)=\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S)\oplus D_{\infty}
and

S\cap\left(\mathcal{R}_{\infty}(S)\times\mathcal{R}_{\infty}(S)\right)=S_{\rm sing}\oplus J_{\infty}

(14)

is a reducing sum decomposition with respect to
(\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S),D_{\infty})
.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
S
be a linear relation in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
with proper point spectrum
\{\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{l}\}\subset\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}
. Then,
there exist subspaces
D_{\lambda_{1}},\ldots,D_{\lambda_{l}},D_{M}
such that
\mathbb{C}^{m}=\mathcal{R}_{c}(S)\oplus D_{\lambda_{1}}\oplus\ldots\oplus D_{\lambda_{l}}\oplus D_{M}
and
linear relations
S_{\rm sing},J_{\lambda_{1}},\ldots,J_{\lambda_{l}},S_{M}
as in (
13
) and (
14
) such that

S=S_{\rm sing}\oplus J_{\lambda_{1}}\oplus\cdots\oplus J_{\lambda_{l}}\oplus S_{M}

(15)

is a reduced sum decomposition of
S
with respect to

\left(\mathcal{R}_{c}(S),D_{\lambda_{1}},\ldots,D_{\lambda_{l}},D_{M}\right).

The linear relation
S_{\rm sing}=S\cap\left(\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S)\times\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S)\right)
is completely singular and
S_{M}
is a multishift. For
1\leq j\leq l
the linear relation

J_{\lambda_{j}}
satisfies
\sigma_{p}(J_{j})=\lambda_{j}
and

S\cap\left(\mathcal{R}_{\lambda_{j}}(S)\times\mathcal{R}_{\lambda_{j}}(S)\right)=S_{\rm sing}\oplus J_{\lambda_{j}}

(16)

is a reducing sum decomposition with respect to
(\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S),D_{\lambda_{j}})
.

3
Weyr characteristic of a linear relation in
\mathbb{C}^{m}

As it was mentioned in the introduction, the Weyr characteristic of a linear operator
S
acting on
\mathbb{C}^{m}
is a complete set of invariants which determines the Jordan normal form of
S
. The aim of this section is to introduce a complete set of invariants for a linear relation and to show that this is an analogue of the Weyr characteristic of a linear operator.

Let
S
be a linear relation in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
and assume that it is decomposed as in (
15
).
We introduce the Weyr characteristic for a linear relation according to four different parts of it.
We distinguish between (a) the singular part of
S
, (b) each finite proper eigenvalue, and (c) the possible proper eigenvalue
\infty
. The fourth kind corresponds to the part of
S
which has no eigenvalues.

Definition 3.1
.

Given a linear relation
S
in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
, let
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{l}
be its different proper eigenvalues and assume that (
15
) holds.

1.

The
Weyr characteristic corresponding to a proper eigenvalue

\lambda_{j}\in\mathbb{C}
of
S
is given by the sequence
W(\lambda_{j}):=(W_{k}(\lambda_{j}))_{k\geq 1}
, where

\displaystyle W_{k}(\lambda_{j}):=\dim\frac{N((S-\lambda_{j})^{k})+\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S)}{N((S-\lambda_{j})^{k-1})+\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S)}.

2.

If
\infty
is a proper eigenvalue of
S
then the
Weyr characteristic corresponding to

\infty
is given by the sequence
A:=(A_{k})_{k\geq 1}
, where

\displaystyle A_{k}:=\dim\frac{\mul(S^{k})+\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S)}{\mul(S^{k-1})+\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S)}.

3.

If
S_{\rm sing}\neq\{0\}
then the
Weyr characteristic corresponding to the singular chains subspace
is defined by the sequence
B:=(B_{k})_{k\geq 1}
, where

\displaystyle B_{k}:=\dim\frac{N(S^{k})\cap\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S)}{N(S^{k-1})\cap\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S)}.

4.

If
S_{M}\neq\{0\}

then the
Weyr characteristic corresponding to the multi-shifts
is given by the sequence
C:=(C_{k})_{k\geq 1}
, where

\displaystyle C_{1}:=\dim\frac{R(S)+{\rm dom\,}S}{R(S)+\mathcal{R}_{0}(S)}\quad\text{and}\quad C_{k}:=\dim\frac{R(S^{k-1})+\mathcal{R}_{0}(S)}{R(S^{k})+\mathcal{R}_{0}(S)},\ k\geq 2.

Each of the sequences
W(\lambda_{j})
,
A
,
B
, and
C
is
non-increasing
[
10
]
. Since the underlying space is finite-dimensional each of the Weyr characteristic sequences contains only a finite number of non-zero terms.
In what follows, we shall treat them as finite sequences and omit the zero entries.
Also, we collect the Weyr characteristics corresponding to the different finite proper eigenvalues in a single sequence: if these are
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{l}
, we set

W:=(W(\lambda_{1}),W(\lambda_{2}),\ldots,W(\lambda_{l})).

Definition 3.2
.

The collection of finite sequences

\big(W,A,B,C\big)

(17)

is called the
Weyr characteristic
of the linear relation
S
.
If it necessary to refer to the underlying linear relation we also
write
\big(W(S),A(S),B(S),C(S)\big)
. Moreover, in the same manner, we
write then
W(S)=(W_{S}(\lambda_{1}),W_{S}(\lambda_{2}),\ldots,W_{S}(\lambda_{l}))
and

W_{S}(\lambda_{j})=(W_{k}(S,\lambda_{j}))_{k\geq 1}
,
A(S)=(A_{k}(S))_{k\geq 1}
,
B(S)=(B_{k}(S))_{k\geq 1}
, and

C(S)=(C_{k}(S))_{k\geq 1}
.

Next, we explain the quantities measured by
W(\lambda_{j})
,
A
,
B
, and
C
. Let us start with
B
.
Since
S
is a linear relation in a finite dimensional space, there exists
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathcal{R}_{0}(S)=N(S^{k_{0}})
. If
[x]\neq 0
belongs to
\frac{N(S^{k_{0}})\cap\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S)}{N(S^{k_{0}-1})\cap\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S)}
, then there exist
y_{1},\ldots,y_{k_{0}-1}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
such that

\displaystyle(x,y_{1}),(y_{1},y_{2}),\ldots,(y_{k_{0}-1},0)\in S.

We have
x\in\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S)
and the chain
(x,y_{1}),(y_{1},y_{2}),\ldots,(y_{k_{0}-1},0)
is a Jordan chain (at 0) of length
k_{0}
. Moreover, the vectors
x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{k_{0}-1}
are linearly independent. Hence,
B_{k_{0}}
is the number of linearly independent Jordan chains (at 0) of length
k_{0}
in
S
that can be extended to a singular chain. Following this reasoning, it is easy to see that for each
k
,
1\leq k\leq k_{0}
,

B_{k}
represents the number of linearly independent Jordan chains (at 0) of length at least
k
that can be extended to a singular chain.

From the definition of
S_{\rm sing}
we have that
(S^{-1})_{\rm sing}=(S_{\rm sing})^{-1}
, which implies the following lemma.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
S
be a linear relation in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
. Then, for each
k\geq 1
,

B_{k}(S)=B_{k}(S^{-1}).

To continue with the meaning of
W(\lambda_{j})
for a complex eigenvalue
\lambda_{j}
of
S
we show that, for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the sum
W_{k}(\lambda_{j})+B_{k}
equals the dimension of
\frac{N((S-\lambda_{j})^{k})}{N((S-\lambda_{j})^{k-1})}
. Actually, this is true not only for
\lambda_{j}
but for any complex number
\lambda
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
S
be a linear relation in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
. For
k\geq 1
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
,

W_{k}(S,\lambda)+B_{k}(S)=\dim\frac{N((S-\lambda)^{k})}{N((S-\lambda)^{k-1})}.

Proof.

First, assume that
x\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
is such that
[x]\neq 0
in
\frac{N(S^{k})\cap\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S)}{N(S^{k-1})\cap\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S)}
. Then there exist
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k-1}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
such that

\displaystyle(x,x_{k-1}),(x_{k_{1}},x_{k-2}),\ldots,(x_{1},0)\in S.

Also, since
x\in\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S)
, there exist
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
x_{k+1},\ldots,x_{k+n}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
such that

\displaystyle(0,x_{k+n}),(x_{k+n},x_{k+n-1}),\ldots,(x_{k+1},x)\in S.

Applying Lemma
2.1
to the chain
(0,x_{k+n})
,
(x_{k+n},x_{k+n-1})
, …,
(x_{1},0)
in
S
, we obtain a chain
(0,z_{k+n})
,
(z_{k+n},z_{k+n-1})
, …,
(z_{1},0)
in
S-\lambda
, where each
z_{j}
is given by (
4
). In particular,
z_{k}
is a linear combination of
x
and the vectors
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k-1}
. Hence,
[z_{k}]\neq 0
in
\frac{N((S-\lambda)^{k})\cap\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S)}{N((S-\lambda)^{k-1})\cap\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S)}
.

A similar argument shows that if
[x^{1}],\ldots,[x^{l}]
are linearly independent in
\frac{N(S^{k})\cap\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S)}{N(S^{k-1})\cap\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S)}
, then there exist linearly independent classes
[z_{k}^{1}],\ldots,[z_{k}^{l}]
in
\frac{N((S-\lambda)^{k})\cap\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S)}{N((S-\lambda)^{k-1})\cap\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S)}
. Thus, Lemma
2.1
implies that

\dim\frac{N(S^{k})\cap\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S)}{N(S^{k-1})\cap\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S)}=\dim\frac{N((S-\lambda)^{k})\cap\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S)}{N((S-\lambda)^{k-1})\cap\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S)}.

Therefore, the statement in the Lemma
3.4
is of the form

\dim\frac{X+Z}{Y+Z}+\dim\frac{X\cap Z}{Y\cap Z}=\dim\frac{X}{Y},

(18)

where
X:=N((S-\lambda)^{k})
,
Y:=N((S-\lambda)^{k-1})
, and
Z:=\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S)
. But for arbitrary finite-dimensional subspaces
X
,
Y
,
Z
such that
Y\subseteq X
it holds that

\displaystyle\dim\frac{X+Z}{Y+Z}

\displaystyle=\dim(X+Z)-\dim(Y+Z)

\displaystyle=\dim X-\dim(X\cap Z)-\dim Y+\dim(Y\cap Z),

\displaystyle\dim\frac{X\cap Z}{Y\cap Z}

\displaystyle=\dim(X\cap Z)-\dim(Y\cap Z),

and summing up the above equations (
18
) follows.

As a consequence of Lemma
3.4
, for each
k\geq 1
,
W_{k}(\lambda_{j})
represents the number of linearly independent Jordan chains of
S
at
\lambda_{j}
of length at least
k
that
cannot
be extended into a singular chain.

Since
\infty
is an eigenvalue of
S
if and only if
0
is an eigenvalue of the inverse relation
S^{-1}
, and
\mul(S)=N(S^{-1})
, the above arguments also show that, for each
k\geq 1
,
A_{k}
represents the number of linearly independent Jordan chains of
S
at
\infty
of length at least
k
that cannot be extended into a singular chain. To make this precise, we formulate another lemma.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
S
be a linear relation in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
. Then, for each
k\geq 1
,

A_{k}(S)+B_{k}(S)=\dim\frac{\mul(S^{k})}{\mul(S^{k-1})}.

Proof.

This is an immediate consequence of Lemmas
3.3
and
3.4
. Indeed, for every
k\geq 1
, since
A_{k}(S)=W_{k}(S^{-1},0)
and
B_{k}(S)=B_{k}(S^{-1})
we obtain that

\displaystyle A_{k}(S)+B_{k}(S)

\displaystyle=W_{k}(S^{-1},0)+B_{k}(S^{-1})=\dim\frac{N((S^{-1})^{k})}{N((S^{-1})^{k-1})}

\displaystyle=\dim\frac{\mul(S^{k})}{\mul(S^{k-1})}.

The meaning of
C
is clearly explained in
[
10
]
. Given
k\geq 1
,
C_{k}
stands for the number of linearly independent chains of the form

(x_{1},x_{2}),(x_{2},x_{3}),\ldots,(x_{k},x_{k+1})

that are contained in
S_{M}
and are composed by linearly independent vectors
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k+1}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
. We say that such a chain is a
multi-shift chain
(
of length
k
) in
S_{M}
.
Therefore,
C_{k}
represents the number of linearly independent multi-shift chains of length
k
in
S
that are not part of a Jordan chain of
S
at
\lambda=0
.

The above considerations are made somehow more precise in Proposition
3.7
below.
Before, we need to
prove a somehow technical but very helpful lemma. Given two multi-indices
a=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
and
b=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{l})\in\mathbb{N}^{l}
with
k\geq l
, we define
a+b
as the multi-index in
\mathbb{N}^{k}
given by

a+b:=(a_{1}+b_{1},\ldots,a_{l}+b_{l},b_{l+1},\ldots,b_{k}).

Note that if
a
and
b
are non-increasing multi-indices, then so is
a+b
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
S
be a linear relation in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
and assume that it has a reduced sum decomposition
S=S_{1}\oplus S_{2}
with respect to
\mathbb{C}^{m}=D_{1}\oplus D_{2}
. Then,

A(S)=A(S_{1})+A(S_{2}),\quad B(S)=B(S_{1})+B(S_{2}),\quad\text{and\;\;}C(S)=C(S_{1})+C(S_{2}).

If
\lambda
is a proper eigenvalue of
S_{1}
or
S_{2}
, we have

W_{S}(\lambda)=W_{S_{1}}(\lambda)+W_{S_{2}}(\lambda).

(19)

Proof.

Let
\lambda
be a proper eigenvalue of
S_{1}
or
S_{2}
.
Then, using (
9
) and (
10
) we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{N((S-\lambda_{j})^{k})+\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S)}{N((S-\lambda_{j})^{k-1})+\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(N((S_{1}-\lambda_{j})^{k})+\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S_{1})\right)\oplus\left(N((S_{2}-\lambda_{j})^{k})+\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S_{2})\right)}{\left(N((S_{1}-\lambda_{j})^{k-1})+\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S_{1})\right)\oplus\left(N((S_{2}-\lambda_{j})^{k-1})+\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S_{2})\right)}

which shows (
19
). The statement on
A(S)
follows in the same way, using (
9
) and (
11
). The same applies to
C(S)
, where one uses
(
12
) and (
8
).

It remains to show the statement on
B(S)
. Using the same argument as above, we have

\displaystyle\dim\frac{N((S-\lambda)^{k})}{N((S-\lambda)^{k-1})}=\dim\frac{N((S_{1}-\lambda)^{k})}{N((S_{1}-\lambda)^{k-1})}+\dim\frac{N((S_{2}-\lambda)^{k})}{N((S_{2}-\lambda)^{k-1})}.

(20)

Then, applying Lemma
3.4
repeatedly, together with (
20
), we have

\displaystyle W_{k}(S,\lambda)+B_{k}(S)

\displaystyle=\dim\frac{N((S-\lambda)^{k})}{N((S-\lambda)^{k-1})}

\displaystyle=\left(W_{k}(S_{1},\lambda)+B_{k}(S_{1})\right)+\left(W_{k}(S_{2},\lambda)+B_{k}(S_{2})\right)

\displaystyle=W_{k}(S,\lambda)+B_{k}(S_{1})+B_{k}(S_{2}).

Therefore,
B_{k}(S)=B_{k}(S_{1})+B_{k}(S_{2})
for each
k\geq 1
.

Proposition 3.7
.

Given a linear relation
S
in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
assume that the reduced sum decomposition (
15
) holds.
Then, the Weyr characteristic of
S
satisfies:

\displaystyle W_{S}(\lambda_{j})=W_{J_{\lambda_{j}}}(\lambda_{j}),\qquad\text{for}\ 1\leq j\leq l,

\displaystyle A(S)

\displaystyle=A(J_{\infty}),\quad B(S)=B(S_{\rm sing}),\quad\text{and}\quad C(S)=C(S_{M}).

Proof.

Fixed
1\leq i\leq l
, applying Lemma
3.6
to the reducing sum decomposition (
15
) we get

\displaystyle W_{S}(\lambda_{i})

\displaystyle=W_{S_{\rm sing}}(\lambda_{i})+\sum_{j=1}^{l}W_{J_{\lambda_{j}}}(\lambda_{i})+W_{S_{M}}(\lambda_{i}),

and similar descriptions hold for
A
,
B
and
C
.

Note that
W_{S_{\rm sing}}(\lambda_{i})
and
W_{S_{M}}(\lambda_{i})
are trivial sequences because
S_{\rm sing}\subset\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S)\times\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S)
and
\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S_{M})=N((S_{M}-\lambda)^{k})=\{0\}
for every
k\geq 1
and every
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\cup\{0\}
, respectively. Therefore,
W_{S}(\lambda_{i})=\sum_{j=1}^{l}W_{J_{\lambda_{j}}}(\lambda_{i})
. Moreover, since
\sigma_{p}(J_{\lambda_{j}})=\{\lambda_{j}\}
it follows that

\displaystyle W_{S}(\lambda_{i})=\sum_{j=1}^{l}W_{J_{\lambda_{j}}}(\lambda_{i})=W_{\lambda_{i}}(\lambda_{i}).

Let us analyze the case of the sequence
A
. Once again,
A(S_{\rm sing})
and
A(S_{M})
are trivial because
S_{\rm sing}\subset\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S)\times\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S)
and
\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S_{M})=\mul(S_{M}^{k})=\{0\}
for every
k\geq 0
, respectively. Also, if
\lambda_{j}\in\mathbb{C}
then
A(J_{\lambda_{j}})
is trivial because
\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S_{J_{\lambda_{j}}})=\mul(J_{\lambda_{j}}^{k})=\{0\}
for every
k\geq 1
and every
1\leq j\leq l
. Hence,
A(S)=A(J_{\infty})
.

According to (
16
),
\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(J_{\lambda_{j}})\subset\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S)\cap D_{\lambda_{j}}=\{0\}
for every
1\leq j\leq l
. Then,
B(J_{\lambda_{j}})
is trivial for every
1\leq j\leq l
. Also,
B(S_{M})
is trivial because
N(S_{M}^{k})=\{0\}
for every
k\geq 1
. Thus,
B(S)=B(S_{\rm sing})
.

To complete the proof let us show that
C(S)=C(S_{M})
. We have that
C(S_{\rm sing})
is trivial because
R(S_{\rm sing}^{k})\subseteq\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S_{\rm sing})\subseteq\mathcal{R}_{0}(S_{\rm sing})
for every
k\geq 1
.

It remains to prove that
C(J_{\lambda_{j}})
is trivial for each
j
,
1\leq j\leq l
.
Firstly, assume that
\lambda_{j}=0
for some
j
. In this case,
J_{0}
is a nilpotent operator i.e. there exist
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
J_{0}^{k_{0}}=0
. Then,
\mathcal{R}_{0}(J_{0})=N(J_{0}^{k_{0}})=D_{0}={\rm dom\,}J_{0}
and we have that
R(J_{0}^{k})\subseteq D_{0}=\mathcal{R}_{0}(J_{0})
for each
k\geq 1
, so
C(J_{0})
is trivial.

Secondly, assume that
\lambda_{j}=\infty
for some
j
. Since
J_{\infty}
is the inverse of the graph of a linear operator
T_{0}
defined on
D_{\infty}
, it is immediate that
{\rm dom\,}J_{\infty}=R(T_{0})\subseteq D_{\infty}
,
\mathcal{R}_{0}(J_{\infty})=\{0\}
and
R(J_{\infty}^{k})={\rm dom\,}(T_{0}^{k})=D_{\infty}
for each
k\geq 1
. Hence,
C(J_{\infty})
is trivial.
Finally, given
1\leq j\leq l
, assume that
\lambda_{j}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
. Since
0\notin\sigma_{p}(J_{\lambda_{j}})
we have that
J_{\lambda_{j}}
is invertible. In particular,
\mathcal{R}_{0}(J_{\lambda_{j}})=\{0\}
and
R(J_{\lambda_{j}}^{k})=D_{\lambda_{j}}={\rm dom\,}J_{\lambda_{j}}
for each
k\geq 1
, which shows that
C(J_{\lambda_{j}})
is the trivial sequence.
Therefore,
C(S)=C(S_{M})
.

We close this section with showing that the Weyr characteristic is invariant under strict equivalence.

Lemma 3.8
.

The Weyr characteristics of two strictly equivalent linear relations in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
coincide.

Proof.

Let
S
and
\tilde{S}
be two strictly equivalent linear relations in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
. By definition, there
is an invertible matrix
T
such that
(x,y)\in S
if and only if
(Tx,Ty)\in\tilde{S}
. Therefore, for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
and any
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
,

\displaystyle\mul(\tilde{S}^{k})=T(\mul(S^{k})),\quad N((\tilde{S}-\lambda)^{k})

\displaystyle=T(N((S-\lambda)^{k})),\quad\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(\tilde{S})=T(\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S)),

\displaystyle R(\tilde{S}^{k})=T(R(S^{k})),\quad

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad\mathcal{R}_{r}(\tilde{S})=T(\mathcal{R}_{r}(S)).

Hence,

\displaystyle\frac{N(\tilde{S}-\lambda)^{k})+\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(\tilde{S})}{N((\tilde{S}-\lambda)^{k-1})+\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(\tilde{S})}=\frac{T\big(N((S-\lambda)^{k})+\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S)\big)}{T\big(N((S-\lambda)^{k-1})+\mathcal{R}_{\rm c}(S)\big)}.

The invertibility of
T
thus implies that
W(S)=W(\tilde{S})
. The remaining equalities
A(S)=A(\tilde{S})
,
B(S)=B(\tilde{S})
, and
C(S)=C(\tilde{S})
are proved similarly.

4
Kernel and range representation for matrix pencils

Kronecker proved that any pair of matrices
E,F\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times m}
, or what is the same
any matrix pencil of the form
sE-F
,
can be transformed into a canonical form, cf. (
21
) below. Nowadays, this form is referred to as the
Kronecker canonical form
,
see e.g.
[
12
,
13
,
22
]
.

According to Kronecker
[
27
]
, there exist invertible matrices
U\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
and
V\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
such that
V(sE-F)U
has the following block quasi-diagonal form

\displaystyle V(sE-F)U=\begin{bmatrix}sI_{n_{0}}-J_{0}&0&0&0\\
0&sN_{\alpha}-I_{|\alpha|}&0&0\\
0&0&sK_{\beta}-L_{\beta}&0\\
0&0&0&sK_{\gamma}^{\top}-L_{\gamma}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}

(21)

where
J_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{0}\times n_{0}}
is in Jordan normal form, and it is unique up to a permutation of its Jordan blocks, and
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n_{\alpha}}
,
\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{n_{\beta}}
,
\gamma\in\mathbb{N}^{n_{\gamma}}
are multi-indices which are unique up to a permutation of their entries and the absolute value of
\alpha
is given by
|\alpha|=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\alpha}}\alpha_{i}
.
For further details on the remaining blocks appearing in (
21
) we refer to Appendix
A
, which is mainly based on
[
22
, Chapter XII]
.

For our purposes it is important to assume that the entries of the multi-indices
\alpha
,
\beta
and
\gamma
as well as the sizes of the different Jordan blocks in
J_{0}
corresponding to a given eigenvalue
\lambda
are arranged in non-increasing order.

Recall that the Weyr characteristic of a matrix arises from its Segre characteristic by computing the conjugate partition. Similar to the Segre characteristic of the matrix
J_{0}
, also the multi-indices
\alpha,\beta
, and
\gamma
reflect the various block sizes in the Kronecker canonical form. Hence, it is natural to define the Weyr characteristic of a matrix pencil by conjugate partitions of the Kronecker invariants.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
P(s)=sE-F
be a matrix pencil with matrices

E
and
F
in
\mathbb{C}^{n\times m}
.
If
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{l}
are the different eigenvalues of the matrix
J_{0}
in (
21
),
denote by
w_{\lambda_{1}},\ldots,w_{\lambda_{l}}
the corresponding Weyr characteristics. Assuming that
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{l}
are given in a certain order, we define the Weyr characteristic of
J_{0}
by
w=(w_{\lambda_{1}},\ldots,w_{\lambda_{l}})
.
Also, consider the conjugate partitions of the multi-indices
\alpha
,
\beta
, and

\gamma
and denote them by
a=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n_{a}})
,

b=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n_{b}})
, and

c=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n_{c}})
, respectively.
Then, the collection of multi-indices

w(P):=(w,a,b,c)

(22)

is called the
Weyr characteristic of the matrix pencil

P(s)=sE-F
.

As it was explained in the introduction, a given matrix pencil
P(s)=sE-F
gives rise to two associated linear relations, namely

\displaystyle E^{-1}F=N[F,-E]\qquad\text{and}\qquad FE^{-1}=R\begin{bmatrix}E\\
F\end{bmatrix},

which are the kernel and the range representation of the pencil, respectively, see
[
14
]
. Assuming that the Kronecker canonical form of
P(s)=sE-F
is given by (
21
), in
[
14
, Example 4.4]
the authors showed that the eigenvalues of
P(s)
are exactly the same as the proper eigenvalues of
FE^{-1}
. The same correspondence holds for the kernel representation
E^{-1}F
. The proof is omitted because it is similar to the arguments used for the range representation.

Proposition 4.2
.

Given
E,F\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times m}
, consider the pencil
P(s)=sE-F
. If
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}
, the following conditions are equivalent:

(a)

\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
P(s)
;

(b)

\lambda
is a proper eigenvalue of the linear relation
E^{-1}F
;

(c)

\lambda
is a proper eigenvalue of the linear relation
FE^{-1}
.

In Sections
5
and
6
below we describe the connection between the Weyr characteristic of the matrix pencil and of its kernel and range representation, respectively.
It turns out that their Weyr characteristics coincide in the first two entries in (
17
) and in (
22
), whereas there is a certain
shift in the last two entries. Before proving this in Sections
5
and
6
, we present two simple examples.

Example 4.3
.

Given
E=K_{(2,1,1)}=[1,0,0,0]
and
F=L_{(2,1,1)}=[0,1,0,0]
in
\mathbb{C}^{1\times 4}
, consider the pencil

\displaystyle P(s)=sE-F=[s,-1,0,0].

It is given in Kronecker canonical form, and only the third block in the Kronecker canonical form is present.

According to Definition
4.1
, the only non-empty entry in the Weyr characteristic of
P(s)
is the entry
b=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n_{b}})
, which in this case is the conjugate of the multi-index
\beta=(2,1,1)
. Hence,

b=(3,1).

(23)

On the one hand, the kernel representation of the pencil
P(s)
is

E^{-1}F=\left\{\big((x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}),(x_{2},y_{1},y_{2},y_{3})\big):\ x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4},y_{1},y_{2},y_{3}\in\mathbb{C}\right\},

and also
(E^{-1}F)^{2}=\mathbb{C}^{4}\times\mathbb{C}^{4}
. This implies that
\mathcal{R}_{c}(E^{-1}F)=\mathbb{C}^{4}
. Moreover, since
\dim N(E^{-1}F)=3
and
\dim N(E^{-1}F)^{2}=4
, we obtain

B(E^{-1}F)=(3,1),

which coincides with (
23
).

On the other hand, the range representation of the matrix pencil
P(s)
is

FE^{-1}=R\begin{bmatrix}E\\
F\end{bmatrix}=\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}.

Again, this implies that
\mathcal{R}_{c}(FE^{-1})=\mathbb{C}
and, since
\dim N(FE^{-1})=1
,

5
Weyr characteristics of a pencil and its kernel representation

In this section we present one of the main results of this paper. Theorem
5.1
and Proposition
5.2
below show that there is a correspondence between the Weyr characteristic of a matrix pencil and that of its kernel representation. Moreover, it is shown that strict equivalence of pencils and linear relations is essentially the same as having the same Weyr characteristics.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
P(s)=sE-F
be a matrix pencil with matrices

E
and
F
in
\mathbb{C}^{n\times m}

and Weyr characteristic
w(P)=(w,a,b,c)
. If
(W,A,B,C)
is the Weyr characteristic of the kernel representation
E^{-1}F
then

\displaystyle W=w,\quad A=a,\quad B=b.

Moreover, if
c=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n_{c}})
, then
C=(c_{3},\ldots,c_{n_{c}})
.

Proof.

Since
w(P)
is invariant under strict equivalence, we assume that
P(s)=sE-F
is in Kronecker canonical form, i.e. (
21
) holds with

and

. Then,

\displaystyle E^{-1}F=S_{n_{0}}\oplus S_{\alpha}\oplus S_{\beta}\oplus S_{\gamma},

(25)

is a reducing sum decomposition in the sense of Definition
2.2
, where
S_{n_{0}}=N[J_{0},-I]
,
S_{\alpha}=N[I_{\alpha},-N_{\alpha}]
,
S_{\beta}=N[L_{\beta},-K_{\beta}]
and
S_{\gamma}=N[L_{\gamma}^{\top},-K_{\gamma}^{\top}]
.

Moreover,
S_{n_{0}}
admits a reducing
sum decomposition where each subrelation is determined by one and only one Jordan block of
J_{0}
. Analogously,
S_{\alpha}

admits a reducing sum decomposition where each subrelation is given by one (and only one) of the blocks along the diagonal of
N_{\alpha}
.
Hence, according to Lemma
3.6
, there is no restriction
in assuming that the matrix
J_{0}
contains only a single Jordan block
J_{n_{0}}(0)

of size
n_{0}
associated to the eigenvalue
\lambda=0
and that
the multi-index
\alpha
consists of one entry only. In this case, the Segre
characteristic of
sI-J_{0}
is just the number
n_{0}
, whereas its Weyr characteristic is the finite sequence
(1,1,\ldots,1)\in\mathbb{N}^{n_{0}}
. Similarly, the Weyr characteristic of
sN_{\alpha}-I_{\alpha}
is

.

In general, the multi-indices
\beta
and
\gamma
are of the form

\displaystyle\beta=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{l_{\beta}},1,\ldots,1),\quad\gamma=(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{l_{\gamma}},1,\ldots,1),

where
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{l_{\beta}}
and
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{l_{\gamma}}
are integers larger than one and
the number of entries equal to one in
\beta
and
\gamma
are given by
m_{\beta}
and
m_{\gamma}
, respectively.
As with the other relations in (
25
),
S_{\beta}
and

S_{\gamma}
admit a reducing
sum decomposition with respect to the blocks corresponding to

\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{l_{\beta}-1}
,

(\beta_{l_{\beta}}1,\ldots,1)
,
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{l_{\gamma}-1}

and
(\gamma_{l_{\gamma}},1,\ldots,1)
, respectively.
Hence, in view of Lemma
3.6
, there is no restriction in assuming that
\beta
is either
\beta=(\beta_{1})
,
\beta=(\beta_{1},1,\ldots,1)
or
\beta=(1,\ldots,1)
for some
m_{\beta}>1
. The same applies to
\gamma
. However, the computation of the Weyr characteristic
(W,A,B,C)
of

depends on the form of
\beta
and
\gamma
. In the following we analyze five different cases.

Case 1.
If
\beta=(\beta_{1},1,\ldots,1)\in\mathbb{N}^{m_{\beta}+1}
for

, and

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ll}w=(1,1,\ldots,1)\in\mathbb{N}^{n_{0}},&a=(1,1,\ldots,1)\in\mathbb{N}^{\alpha},\\

(28)

The third linear relation is of the form

(32)

Theorem
5.1

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
S
be a linear relation in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
with Weyr characteristic
(W,A,B,C)
. If there exist matrices

such that the kernel representation

of the matrix pencil

\displaystyle w=W,\quad a=A,\quad b=B,

Proof.

Assume that

is the kernel representation of the matrix pencil

. Then, there exist invertible matrices

Note that

This implies that

\displaystyle(UEV)^{-1}(UFV)=\begin{bmatrix}V^{-1}&0\\

is already in Kronecker canonical from.

Now, let

be the Weyr characteristic of the pencil
P(s)
.
It is possible to express the numbers

and
n
of the matrix pencil
P(s)
in (

) in terms of the Weyr characteristic. In fact,

and

are the number of rows (and columns) of

determined by the blocks associated to the finite and the infinity eigenvalues, respectively. On the other hand,

are the number of rectangular blocks corresponding to the pencils

|b|
, respectively. Analogously, the number of rows and columns of

By Theorem

, we have that

Also, we know the indices in

\displaystyle|c|=|C|+c_{1}+c_{2}.

Replacing the above formula in (

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle=|W|+|A|+(|B|-B_{1})+|C|+c_{1}+c_{2},

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle=|W|+|A|+|B|+|C|+c_{2}.

Thus,

\displaystyle c_{2}

\displaystyle=m-|W|-|A|-|B|-|C|,\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle c_{1}

\displaystyle=n-|W|-|A|-(|B|-B_{1})-|C|-c_{2}

\displaystyle=n-|W|-|A|-(|B|-B_{1})-|C|-(m-|W|-|A|-|B|-|C|)

\displaystyle 2m-|W|-|A|-|B|-B_{1}-|C|

\displaystyle n=2m-|W|-|A|-|B|-B_{1}-|C|.

In the following we are going to prove the converse of Lemma
3.8

S_{1}
and
S_{2}
in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
have the same Weyr characteristic, then they are strictly equivalent, see Theorem

Proposition 5.4
.

Given
E_{1},E_{2},F_{1},F_{2}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times m}
, consider the matrix pencils
P_{1}(s)=sE_{1}-F_{1}
and
P_{2}(s)=sE_{2}-F_{2}
. Let
S_{1}=E_{1}^{-1}F_{1}
and
S_{2}=E_{2}^{-1}F_{2}
be their corresponding kernel representations. If
P_{1}(s)
is strictly equivalent to
P_{2}(s)
,
then
S_{1}
is strictly equivalent to
S_{2}
. In this case, the Weyr
characteristics of
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
coincide.

Proof.

Assume that
P_{1}(s)
is strictly equivalent to
P_{2}(s)
, i.e.
there exist invertible matrices
U\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
and
V\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
such that

\displaystyle E_{2}=UE_{1}V\quad\text{and}\quad F_{2}=UF_{1}V,

(cf. Appendix
A
). Then,
S_{2}=E_{2}^{-1}F_{2}=(UE_{1}V)^{-1}(UF_{1}V)
, or equivalently,

\displaystyle S_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\{(x,y):\ F_{1}Vx=E_{1}Vy\right\}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}V^{-1}&0\\
0&V^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\cdot\left\{(Vx,Vy):\ F_{1}Vx=E_{1}Vy\right\}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}T&0\\
0&T\end{pmatrix}\cdot S_{1},

where
T:=V^{-1}
. Therefore,
S_{1}
is strictly equivalent to
S_{2}
. The last statement about the Weyr characteristics
follows from Lemma
3.8
.

Theorem 5.5
.

Let
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
be linear relations in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
. Then their Weyr
characteristics coincide if and only
if the linear relations
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are strictly equivalent.

Proof.

On the one hand, in Lemma
3.8
we showed that if
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are strictly equivalent then their Weyr
characteristics coincide.

On the other hand, if
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
have the same Weyr characteristic then, defining
n
as the minimal value given in Corollary
5.3
, there exist matrices
E_{1},F_{1},E_{2},F_{2}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times m}
such that

S_{1}=E_{1}^{-1}F_{1}\quad\text{and}\quad S_{2}=E_{2}^{-1}F_{2}.

Moreover, the Weyr characteristics of the associated matrix pencils
P_{1}(s)=sE_{1}-F_{1}
and
P_{2}(s)=sE_{2}-F_{2}
coincide. Therefore,
P_{1}
is strictly equivalent to
P_{2}
and, by Proposition
5.4
, the kernel representations
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are also strictly equivalent.

7
Rank one perturbations of matrix pencils via linear relations

There is a lot of recent work on low-rank perturbations of matrix pencils, see
e.g.
[
6
,
17
,
18
,
19
]
for generic perturbation results and

[
3
,
4
,
5
,
21
,
24
]
for non-generic
perturbation results.
The above mentioned non-generic results are
mainly based on the use
of invariant factors and the concept of majorization of finite sequences.

Here we follow a different approach.
Our goal is to obtain perturbation results for matrix pencils via the Weyr characteristic of their kernel
or range representations.
Due to the results developed in the Sections
5
and
6
these Weyr characteristics can be deduced
from the Weyr characteristic of the underlying, unperturbed pencil.
Recent results for perturbations of linear
relations
[
29
]
describe
the change of the Weyr characteristic under perturbations, and, again due to
Sections
5
and
6
,
allow to give bounds for the change of the Weyr characteristic of the perturbed matrix pencil.
The choice whether to use kernel or range
representation depends on the appearance of the rank-one pencil.

Along this section we consider two matrix pencils,

\displaystyle P(s):=sE-F\quad\text{and}\quad Q(s):=sG-H,

where
E,F,G,H\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times m}
, and assume that they are a rank one perturbation of each other, i.e.

\displaystyle\rank(P-Q)=1.

Every rank one pencil in
\mathbb{C}^{n\times m}
can be written in one of the following ways:

\displaystyle(su-v)w^{*}

or

(40)

\displaystyle w(su^{*}-v^{*}),

(41)

where
(u,v)\in\mathbb{C}^{m}\times\mathbb{C}^{m}
,
(u,v)\neq(0,0)
and
w\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
,
w\neq 0
, see
[
24
]
.

The following definition, taken from
[
2
]
, specifies the idea of a rank-one perturbation for linear relations.

Definition 7.1
.

Let
S
and
T
be linear relations in a vector space
X
. Then
S
is called a
rank one perturbation
of
T
(and vice versa) if

\max\left\{\dim\frac{S}{S\cap T},\,\dim\frac{T}{S\cap T}\right\}=1.

Before returning to matrix pencils, we prove a simple result on rank one perturbations of linear relations.

Lemma 7.2
.

Let
S
and
T
be linear relations in an
m
-dimensional vector space
X
. Then, the following conditions are equivalent:

(a)

S
is a rank one perturbation of
T
;

(b)

S^{-1}
is a rank one perturbation of
T^{-1}
;

(c)

S^{\bot}
is a rank one perturbation of
T^{\bot}
.

Proof.

In order to prove the equivalences, it suffices to show that

\displaystyle\dim\left(\frac{S}{S\cap T}\right)

\displaystyle=\dim\left(\frac{S^{-1}}{S^{-1}\cap T^{-1}}\right)=\dim\left(\frac{T^{\bot}}{S^{\bot}\cap T^{\bot}}\right).

for two linear relations
S
and
T
in
X
. It is easy to see that
(S\cap\ T)^{-1}=S^{-1}\cap T^{-1}
and also
\dim S^{-1}=\dim S
. Hence,

\displaystyle\dim\left(\frac{S^{-1}}{S^{-1}\cap T^{-1}}\right)

\displaystyle=\dim S^{-1}-\dim\big(S^{-1}\cap T^{-1}\big)=\dim S-\dim\big((S\cap T)^{-1}\big)

\displaystyle=\dim S-\dim(S\cap T)=\dim\left(\frac{S}{S\cap T}\right).

Since
X
is finite dimensional, we have that

S^{\bot}\cap T^{\bot}=\left\{(x_{1}+x_{2},y_{1}+y_{2})\,:\,(x_{1},y_{1})\in S,(x_{2},y_{2})\in T\right\}^{\bot},

and
\dim S^{\bot}\cap T^{\bot}=2m-\dim S-\dim T+\dim S\cap T
. Hence,

\displaystyle\dim\left(\frac{T^{\bot}}{S^{\bot}\cap T^{\bot}}\right)

\displaystyle=\dim T^{\bot}-\dim S^{\bot}\cap T^{\bot}

\displaystyle=\dim T^{\bot}-(2m-\dim S-\dim T+\dim S\cap T)

\displaystyle=\dim S-\dim S\cap T=\dim\left(\frac{S}{S\cap T}\right).

In the following lemma we show that rank one perturbations of matrix pencils can always be reformulated as rank one perturbations of their kernel or of their range representation.

Lemma 7.3
.

Let
P(s)=sE-F
and
Q(s)=sG-H
be matrix pencils in
\mathbb{C}^{n\times m}
.

(a)

If
(Q-P)(s)
is a rank one matrix pencil as in (
41
), then

FE^{-1}\quad\mbox{and}\quad HG^{-1},

either coincide or they are rank one perturbations of each other.

(b)

If
(Q-P)(s)
is a rank one matrix pencil as in (
40
), then

E^{-1}F\quad\mbox{and}\quad G^{-1}H,

either coincide or they are rank one perturbations of each other.

Proof.

We first prove item (a). If
(Q-P)(s)
is a rank one matrix pencil as in (
41
), then
G=E+uw^{*}
and
H=F+vw^{*}
. Hence,

\displaystyle HG^{-1}=R\begin{bmatrix}G\\
H\end{bmatrix}=R\begin{bmatrix}E+uw^{*}\\
F+vw^{*}\end{bmatrix},

i.e.
(x,y)\in HG^{-1}
if and only if there exists
z\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
such that
x=(E+uw^{*})z
and
y=(F+vw^{*})z
.

If
w^{*}z=0
then
(x,y)\in(HG^{-1})\cap(FE^{-1})
. Therefore,

\displaystyle HG^{-1}=(HG^{-1})\cap(FE^{-1})+{\rm span\,}\{(u_{0},v_{0})\},

where
u_{0}=Ew+(w^{*}w)u
and
v_{0}=Fw+(w^{*}w)v
.
Thus, the range representations are rank one perturbations of each other, cf. Definition
7.1
.

Now, if
(Q-P)(s)
is a rank one matrix pencil as in (
40
), then

\displaystyle G^{-1}H=\left(E+wu^{*}\right)^{-1}(F+wv^{*})=N[F+wv^{*},-E-wu^{*}].

Thus, the orthogonal complements of
E^{-1}F
and
G^{-1}H
are of the form

\displaystyle(E^{-1}F)^{\bot}=R\begin{bmatrix}F^{*}\\
-E^{*}\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad(G^{-1}H)^{\bot}=R\begin{bmatrix}F^{*}+vw^{*}\\
-E^{*}-uw^{*}\end{bmatrix},

respectively, i.e. they are the range representations of the pencils
\widetilde{P}(s)=sE^{*}+F^{*}
and
\widetilde{Q}(s)=s(F^{*}+vw^{*})+(E^{*}+uw^{*})
, which differ in a rank one perturbation of the form (
41

7.2
we have that
E^{-1}F
and
G^{-1}H
are rank one perturbations of each other.

Appendix A
Invariant characteristics of matrix pencils

In the following, we recall some basic definitions and results for matrix pencils and use the opportunity to fix some notation. Given a pair of matrices
E,F\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times m}
, the matrix pencil

P(s):=sE-F,\quad s\in\mathbb{C},

is called
singular
if one of the following conditions holds:
n\neq m
or
n=m
and
\det(P(s))=0
for all
s\in\mathbb{C}
. Otherwise the pencil is called
regular
.

The
rank
(or
normal rank
)
of the pencil
P
is the largest possible size of a regular submatrix pencil of
P(s)
. We denote it by
\rank(P)
.

Definition A.1
(
[
36
]
).

A complex number
\lambda
is a
finite eigenvalue
of
P(s)=sE-F
, if the rank of the matrix
P(\lambda)
is less than
\rank(P)
. In addition,

\infty
is an eigenvalue of
P
if zero is an eigenvalue of the dual pencil
sF-E
.

Assume that
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
are two matrix pencils. They are called
strictly equivalent
if there are invertible matrices
U\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
and
V\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
such that
P_{2}(s)=UP_{1}(s)V
for all
s\in\mathbb{C}
.

Recall that every pencil
P(s)=sE-F
is strictly equivalent to its
Kronecker canonical form
, see e.g.
[
12
,
13
,
22
]
. To introduce this form the following notation is used: for

k\in\mathbb{N}
consider the square matrices

\displaystyle N_{k}:=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}0&&&\\
1&0&&\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&&1&0\end{array}\right]\in\mathbb{C}^{k\times k}.

For a multi-index
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{l})\in\mathbb{N}^{l}
with absolute value
|\alpha|:=\sum_{i=1}^{l}\alpha_{i}
, define the block diagonal matrix

\displaystyle N_{\alpha}:={\rm diag\,}(N_{\alpha_{1}},\ldots,N_{\alpha_{l}})\in\mathbb{C}^{|\alpha|\times|\alpha|}.

(42)

On the other hand, for
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
k>1
, consider the rectangular matrices

\displaystyle K_{k}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}&&&\\
1&0&&\\
&\ddots&\ddots&&\\
&&1&0\\
\end{smallmatrix}\right],\ L_{k}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}&&&\\
0&1&&\\
&\ddots&\ddots&&\\
&&0&1\\
\end{smallmatrix}\right]\in\mathbb{C}^{(k-1)\times k}.

For some multi-index
{\bf\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{l})\in\mathbb{N}^{l}

\alpha_{j}>1
for all
j=1,\ldots,l

\displaystyle K_{\alpha}:={\rm diag\,}(K_{\alpha_{1}},\ldots,K_{\alpha_{l}}),\ L_{\alpha}:={\rm diag\,}(L_{\alpha_{1}},\ldots,L_{\alpha_{l}})\in\mathbb{C}^{(|\alpha|-l)\times|\alpha|}.

0&1&0&0&0&0&0\\

0_{l\times n_{0}}\end{bmatrix}.
