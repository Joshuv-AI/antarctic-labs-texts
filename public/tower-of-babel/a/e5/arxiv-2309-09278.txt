First double mode of the Poisson distribution of order k k Journal: (internal report CC23-5)
S. R. Mane

Abstract
The Poisson distribution of order k k is a special case of a compound Poisson distribution. For k = 1 k=1 it is the standard Poisson distribution. Our focus in this note is for k ≥ 2 k\geq 2 . For sufficiently small values of the rate parameter λ \lambda , both the median and mode equal zero. The median is zero if and only if λ ≤ ( ln ⁡ 2 ) / k \lambda\leq(\ln 2)/k . The supremum value of λ \lambda for the mode to be zero is known only for small values of k k . This note presents results for the “first double mode” by which is meant the first occasion (smallest value of λ \lambda ) the distribution is bimodal, with modes at 0 0 and m > 0 m>0 . Next, an almost complete answer is supplied to the question “which positive integers cannot be modes of the Poisson distribution of order k k ?” The term “almost complete” signifies that some parts of the answer are conjectures based on numerical searches. However, if the conjectures are proved to be correct, the solution presented in this note is complete: all parameter values are covered.

Definition 1.1

The Poisson distribution of order
k

f_{k}(n;\lambda)=e^{-k\lambda}\sum_{n_{1}+2n_{2}+\dots+kn_{k}=n}\frac{\lambda^{n_{1}+\dots+n_{k}}}{n_{1}!\dots n_{k}!}\,,\qquad n=0,1,2\dots

(1.1)

The Poisson distribution of order

[
3

Although exact expressions for the mean and variance of the Poisson distribution of order
k
are known
[
4
]
,

For sufficiently small values of the rate parameter
\lambda
, both the median and mode equal zero.
It was derived in
[
1

\lambda\leq(\ln 2)/k

The supremum value of
\lambda
for the mode to be zero is known only for small values of
k

[
5
,
6
]

This note presents results for the “first double mode”

\lambda

Basic notation and definitions

For later reference we define the parameter
\kappa=k(k+1)/2
.
We denote the mode
m
(with pertinent subscripts, etc. to denote the dependence on
k
and
\lambda
, see below).
The mode is defined as the location(s) of the
global maximum
of the probability mass function.
It is known that the mode may not be unique.
For the standard Poisson distribution with parameter
\lambda
, the mode equals
\lfloor\lambda\rfloor
if
\lambda\not\in\mathbb{N}
,
but both
\lambda-1
and
\lambda
are modes if
\lambda\in\mathbb{N}
.
Kwon and Philippou
[
6
]
published a table of values of
\lambda
where the Poisson distribution of order
k
has a double mode,
for
2\leq k\leq 4
and
0<\lambda\leq 2
.

We study the problem of the “first double mode”
by which is meant the first occasion (smallest value of
\lambda
) the distribution is bimodal, with modes at
0
and
m>0
.
Philippou
[
5
]
showed that for
k=2
, the answer is
\lambda=\sqrt{3}-1
.
Kwon and Philippou
[
6
]
published the answers for
k=3
and
4
(roots of cubic and quartic polynomials, respectively).
This note studies the general case
k\geq 2
.
We follow the notation in
[
6
]
and
define
h_{k}(n;\lambda)=e^{k\lambda}f_{k}(n;\lambda)
, with initial value
h_{k}(0;\lambda)=1
(eq. (6) in
[
6
]
).
Observe from eq. (
1.1
) that
h_{k}(n;\lambda)
is a polynomial in
\lambda
.
Note the following:

1.

The polynomial
h_{k}(n;\lambda)
has degree
n
because the highest power of
\lambda
in
the sum in eq. (
1.1
) is given by the tuple
(n_{1},\dots,n_{k})=(n,0,\dots,0)
.
The resulting term in the sum is
\lambda^{n}/n!
.

2.

Next,
h_{k}(0;\lambda)=1
and
h_{k}(n;\lambda)
has no constant term for
n>0
.

3.

All the coefficients in
h_{k}(n;\lambda)
are positive.

4.

Hence for
n>0
,
h_{k}(n;0)=0
and
h_{k}(n;\lambda)
is strictly increasing in
\lambda
for
\lambda\geq 0
.

Note also that for fixed
\lambda
and
n\geq 0
, the polynomials
h_{k}(n;\lambda)
are identical for all
k\geq n
.

Proposition 2.1
.

For fixed
\lambda
and
n\geq 0
,
h_{k}(n;\lambda)=h_{n}(n;\lambda)
for all
k>n
.

Proof.

If
n=0
all the polynomials are identically
1
. Hence assume
n>0
.
Observe from eq. (
1.1
) that if
k>n
then
x_{j}=0
for
j>n
.
Hence the sums for
h_{k}(n;\lambda)
and
h_{n}(n;\lambda)
contain the same tuples.
∎

Note also for fixed
\lambda>0
and
n\geq 2
, the polynomials
h_{k}(n;\lambda)
form an increasing sequence for
k=1,\dots,n
.

Proposition 2.2
.

For fixed
\lambda>0
and
n\geq 2
, we have the following sequence for
k=1,\dots,n
:

\frac{\lambda^{n}}{n!}=h_{1}(n;\lambda)<\dots<h_{n}(n;\lambda)\,.

(2.1)

Proof.

The cases
n=0
and
n=1
are vacuous (no sequence), hence we must have
n\geq 2
.
Fix a value
k\leq n
.
Then the terms in the sum in eq. (
1.1
) contain exactly
k
summands.
By conditioning on the value of
n_{k}
, we obtain the following sum

h_{k}(n;\lambda)=\sum_{n_{k}=0}^{\lfloor(n/k)\rfloor}\frac{\lambda^{n_{k}}}{n_{k}!}\,h_{k-1}(n-kn_{k};\lambda)\,.

(2.2)

Split off the term
n_{k}=0
to obtain

h_{k}(n;\lambda)=h_{k-1}(n;\lambda)+\sum_{n_{k}=1}^{\lfloor(n/k)\rfloor}\frac{\lambda^{n_{k}}}{n_{k}!}\,h_{k-1}(n-kn_{k};\lambda)\,.

(2.3)

The sum is nonempty because
k\leq n
and its value is positive for
\lambda>0
,
hence
h_{k}(n;\lambda)>h_{k-1}(n;\lambda)
.
The starting value of the sequence is
h_{1}(n;\lambda)=\lambda^{n}/n!
.
∎

Remark 2.3
.

Observe that eq. (
2.2
) furnishes an alternative recurrence for
h_{k}(n;\lambda)
,
as a sum of lower degree polynomials
h_{k-1}(\cdot)
.

3
Useful lemmas by Kwon and Philippou

For ease of reference, we state Lemma 1 by Kwon and Philippou
[
6
]
, because it will be useful below.

Lemma 3.1
.

(restatement of Lemma 1 in
[
6
]
)
For
2\leq n\leq k
and a fixed
\lambda>0
,

\lambda\leq h_{k}(n-1;\lambda)<h_{k}(n;\lambda)\,.

(3.1)

We can express eq. (
3.1
) as the following increasing sequence, which is in fact more useful

\lambda=h_{k}(1;\lambda)<h_{k}(2;\lambda)<\dots<h_{k}(k;\lambda)\,.

(3.2)

Kwon and Philippou
[
6
]
defined
r_{k}
as the positive root of the equation
h_{k}(k,\lambda)=1
.
They proved that
r_{k}
is unique and
r_{k}\in(0,1)
.
We supply a proof which uses only the Intermediate Value Theorem and no calculus.

Lemma 3.2
.

(Lemma 2 in
[
6
]
)
For
k\geq 2
and
0<\lambda<1
, the equation
h_{k}(k;\lambda)=h_{k}(0;\lambda)
has exactly one root
\lambda=r_{k}\;(0<r_{k}<1)
such that

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}h_{k}(0;\lambda)>h_{k}(k;\lambda)&&\mathit{if}\;0<\lambda<r_{k}\\
h_{k}(0;\lambda)<h_{k}(k;\lambda)&&\mathit{if}\;r_{k}<\lambda<1\,.\end{array}\right.

(3.3)

Proof.

Since
h_{k}(0;\lambda)=1
, the equation to solve is
h_{k}(n;\lambda)=1
.
Also (because
k>0
)
h_{k}(k;\lambda)
has no constant term hence
h_{k}(k;0)=0
.
Next note that the sum
n_{1}+2n_{2}+\dots+kn_{k}=k
contains a tuple
(n_{1},\dots,n_{k})=(0,\dots,0,1)
, i.e.
n_{k}=1
and the other
n_{i}
are zero.
The corresponding term in
h_{k}(k;\lambda)
is simply
\lambda^{1}/1!
, i.e.
\lambda
(and it is the
only
term containing
n_{k}
).
Hence
h_{k}(k;\lambda)=\lambda+
(positive terms) for
\lambda>0
.
Set
\lambda=1
to deduce
h_{k}(k;1)>1
.
Hence by the Intermediate Value Theorem, the equation
h_{k}(k;\lambda)=1
has a root
r_{k}
in the interval
r_{k}\in(0,1)
.
Lastly (also because
k>0
)
h_{k}(k;\lambda)
is strictly increasing in
\lambda
for
\lambda\geq 0
,
hence the root
r_{k}
is unique and the inequalities in eq. (
3.3
) also follow.
∎

Also for ease of reference, we state Lemma 3 by Kwon and Philippou
[
6
]
, because it will be useful below.

Lemma 3.3
.

(restatement of Lemma 3 in
[
6
]
)
For
k\geq 2
and
0<\lambda\leq r_{k}<1
,

h_{k}(k;\lambda)>h_{k}(k+1;\lambda)\,.

(3.4)

Remark 3.4
.

We can improve the lower bound on
r_{k}
as follows:
r_{k}\in[1/\kappa,1)
.

Proof.

Philippou
[
5
]
proved that the Poisson distribution of order

has a unique mode of zero if
\lambda<1/\kappa
.
But if
\lambda=r_{k}
then
h_{k}(k;\lambda)=1
and zero is not a unique mode.
Hence
r_{k}\geq 1/\kappa
.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

Combining eqs. (
3.2
) and (
3.4
) reveals that if
0<\lambda\leq r_{k}
,
then the value of
h_{k}(k;\lambda)
is a local maximum.
This does not imply the value

is a mode, which requires a global maximum.

A histogram plot of

is displayed in Fig.

Observe that

.
This is the increasing sequence of Lemma 1 by Kwon and Philippou
[

]
(see eq. (
3.2
)).
Here

, which is small enough to satisfy the requirement of Lemma 3 in
[

.
The histogram indeed dips at

Observe that

For values

, the structure of the histogram is complicated.
There is another local maximum at

The above value of

The (joint) global maximum is at

4
First double mode

4.1
Notation for first double mode

To avoid confusion below about the use of
m
when discussing the mode,
let
k\geq 2
and denote the value of the first double mode by
\hat{m}_{k}

and the corresponding value of
\lambda
by
\hat{\lambda}_{k}
.
Then by definition
\hat{m}_{k}>0
and

h_{k}(\hat{m}_{k},\hat{\lambda}_{k})=1\,.

(4.1)

We wish to prove that if
k_{2}>k_{1}
then (i)
\hat{m}_{k_{2}}>\hat{m}_{k_{1}}
and (ii)
\hat{\lambda}_{k_{2}}<\hat{\lambda}_{k_{1}}
.
For
k=1
it is known that
\hat{m}_{1}=1
and
\hat{\lambda}_{1}=1
.

4.2
Sharp lower bound for the location of the first double mode

Proposition 4.1
.

For any
k\geq 2
, if the first double modes are located at
0
and
m
, then
m\geq k
(which is a sharp lower bound).

Proof.

We argue by contradiction.
Fix
k\geq 2
and suppose the first double modes are located
0
and
\tilde{m}
, where
\tilde{m}<k
, and that
\lambda=\tilde{\lambda}
.
By definition
h_{k}(\tilde{m};\tilde{\lambda})=1
.
But Lemma 1 by Kwon and Philippou
[
6
]
then tells us that
h_{k}(\tilde{m}+1;\tilde{\lambda})>1
.

Hence there is a smaller value
\hat{\lambda}<\tilde{\lambda}
such that
h_{k}(\tilde{m}+1;\hat{\lambda})=1
.

Hence
\tilde{\lambda}
is
not
the smallest value of
\lambda
to attain a double mode, and
\tilde{m}
is
not
the location of the first double mode.
Furthermore, we know from Philippou
[
5
]
that for
k=2
,
the locations of the first double modes are at
0
and
2
, i.e.
m=k
for
k=2
(and
\lambda=\sqrt{3}-1
).
Hence the lower bound
m=k
is attained, i.e. it is a sharp lower bound.
∎

4.3
Bounds on
\hat{\lambda}_{k}

Proposition 4.2
.

For all
k\geq 2
,
\hat{\lambda}_{k}\in[1/\kappa,r_{k}]
.

Proof.

Upper bound:

By definition
h_{k}(k;r_{k})=1
, which means that if
\lambda=r_{k}
then the Poisson distribution of order
k
does not have a unique mode of zero.
Also by definition
\hat{\lambda}_{k}
is the smallest value of
\lambda
such that a double mode is attained.
Hence
\hat{\lambda}_{k}\leq r_{k}
.

Lower bound:

Philippou
[
5
]
proved that the Poisson distribution of order
k
has a unique mode of zero if
\lambda<1/\kappa
.
But if the distribution has a double mode then zero is not a unique mode.
Hence
\hat{\lambda}\geq 1/\kappa
.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

Recall also that
\hat{m}_{k}\geq k
.
Hence we can further note if
\hat{m}_{k}=k
, then
\hat{\lambda}_{k}=r_{k}
.
Philippou
[
5
]
proved that for
k=2
the double modes are at
0
and
2
.
Hence the upper bound
\hat{\lambda}_{k}\leq r_{k}
is sharp.
We can write the following sequence of inequalities

1/\kappa\leq\hat{\lambda}_{k}\leq r_{k}<1\,.

(4.2)

4.4
Case
\hat{m}_{k}=k

Fix a value
k\geq 2
and by hypothesis
\hat{m}_{k}=k
.
We know such a case exists because Philippou
[
5
]
proved that
\hat{m}_{2}=2
for
k=2
.
Then because
\hat{m}_{k+1}\geq k+1
, it is immediate that
\hat{m}_{k+1}>\hat{m}_{k}
.
Next set
n=\hat{m}_{k}(=k)
and invoke Prop.
2.1
to deduce

h_{k+1}(k;\lambda)=h_{k}(k;\lambda)
, for all
\lambda>0
.
Next set
\lambda=\hat{\lambda}_{k}
.
Then
h_{k}(k;\hat{\lambda}_{k})=1
hence

h_{k+1}(k;\hat{\lambda}_{k})=1
.
However, Lemma 1 by Kwon and Philippou
[
6
]
then tells us that

h_{k+1}(k+1;\hat{\lambda}_{k})>h_{k+1}(k;\hat{\lambda}_{k})
, hence

h_{k+1}(k+1;\hat{\lambda}_{k})>1
.
Hence the value
\hat{\lambda}_{k+1}
which yields the first double mode for
h_{k+1}
is a strictly smaller number:
\hat{\lambda}_{k+1}<\hat{\lambda}_{k}
.
This establishes that if
\hat{m}_{k}=k
, then (i)
\hat{m}_{k+1}>\hat{m}_{k}
and (ii)
\hat{\lambda}_{k+1}<\hat{\lambda}_{k}
.

4.5
Case
\hat{m}_{k}>k

Fix a value
k\geq 2
and by hypothesis
\hat{m}_{k}>k
.
Such a case is known to exist, e.g.
k=15

[
1
]
.
Set
x=\hat{m}_{k}
in eq. (
2.1
) in Prop.
2.2
to deduce

h_{k+1}(\hat{m}_{k};\lambda)>h_{k}(\hat{m}_{k};\lambda)
.
Next set
\lambda=\hat{\lambda}_{k}
, so
h_{k}(\hat{m}_{k};\hat{\lambda}_{k})=1
.
Then we deduce
h_{k+1}(\hat{m}_{k};\hat{\lambda}_{k})>1
.
Next comes a key step:
to attain a double mode for
h_{k+1}(\cdot)
, we must have
h_{k+1}(n;\lambda)\leq 1
for
all
values
n>0
.
The value
\hat{\lambda}_{k}
is too large to be the smallest value of
\lambda
to attain a double mode for
h_{k+1}(\cdot)
.
The value
\hat{\lambda}_{k+1}
which yields the first double mode for
h_{k+1}(\cdot)
is a strictly smaller number, i.e.
\hat{\lambda}_{k+1}<\hat{\lambda}_{k}
.

To prove
\hat{m}_{k+1}>\hat{m}_{k}
is more complicated and will be left for future work.
We sketch some of the complications.
Recall the histogram plot of
h_{k}(n;\lambda)
for the Poisson distribution of order
50
in Fig.
1
.
It exhibits the first double mode at
0
and
\hat{m}_{k}=113
(and
\hat{\lambda}_{k}\simeq 0.10194
).
As already noted, the value of
h_{k}(n;\lambda)
is increasing for
1\leq n\leq k(=50)

which is the increasing sequence of Lemma 1 by Kwon and Philippou
[
6
]
(see eq. (
3.2
)).
However for
n>k
, which is what we need here, the structure of the histogram is complicated.
There is a local maximum at
n=98
but its height is
0.9835\dots
and is not a global maximum.
The (joint) global maximum is at
0
and
113
.
It is difficult to establish an “increasing sequence”
\{h_{k}(n;\lambda),h_{k}(n+1;\lambda),\dots\}
as in Lemma 1 by Kwon and Philippou
[
6
]
.
This makes it hard(er) to prove
\hat{m}_{k+1}>\hat{m}_{k}
when
\hat{m}_{k}>k
.

5
Integers which cannot be values of the mode

In the last line of their paper, Kwon and Philippou
[
6
]
posed the question:
“What positive integers cannot be modes of the Poisson distribution of order
k
?”

First we dispose of the case
k=1
, the standard Poisson distribution.
It is well-known that all nonnegative integers
n\geq 0
can be modes of the Poisson distribution.
The question is nontrivial only for
k\geq 2
.
Next, since we proved above that the value of the first double mode must be at least
k
(recall
\hat{m}_{k}\geq k
),
a partial answer is that for
k\geq 2
, integers in the interval
[1,k-1]
cannot be modes of the Poisson distribution of order
k
.
In particular, the value
1
is never a mode for any
k\geq 2
.

In this section, we give an “almost complete” answer to the above question.
We say “almost complete” because the answer relies partly on numerical conjectures.
If the conjectures are proved, then the answer below is complete.
One numerical conjecture is this: for
k\geq 2
and
n\geq 2\kappa
and
\lambda=n/\kappa
,
the mode
m_{k}(\lambda)
of the Poisson distribution of order
k
is given by (eq. (4.1) in
[
1
]
)

m_{k}(n/\kappa)=n-\biggl\lfloor\frac{3k+5}{8}\biggr\rfloor\,.

(5.1)

The above conjecture implies that any integer

i\geq k(k+1)-\lfloor(3k+5)/8\rfloor
can be a mode of the Poisson distribution of order
k
.
Hence, if the above conjecture is correct, then for any given
k\geq 2

it is only necessary to test integers in the interval
[k,k(k+1)-\lfloor(3k+5)/8\rfloor)
.

We employ the term “excluded values” for positive integers which cannot be modes of the Poisson distribution of order
k
.
The value of the mode was calculated numerically for
k\geq 2
and the following results were obtained.

1.

For all
k\geq 2
, integers in the interval
[1,\hat{m}_{k}-1]
are excluded.

2.

Because
\hat{\lambda}_{k}\leq r_{k}
(see Prop.
4.2
and eq. (
4.2
)),
Lemma 3 by Kwon and Philippou
[
6
]
tells us that
k+1
can never be a mode, for any
k\geq 2
.
Numerical calculations confirm this.

3.

For
2\leq k\leq 14
, the set of excluded values is listed in Table

.
Note that
\hat{m}_{k}=k
for
k\in[2,14]
.

4.

For
15\leq k\leq 28
, the set of excluded values is listed in Table
2
.
Note that
\hat{m}_{k}=2k-5
for
k\in[15,28]
.

5.

For
29\leq k\leq 37
, the set of excluded values is listed in Table
3
.
Note that
\hat{m}_{k}=2k-4
for
k\in[29,37]
.

6.

For
38\leq k\leq 41
, the set of excluded values is listed in Table
4
.
Note that
\hat{m}_{k}=2k-3
for
k\in[38,41]
.

7.

For
k\geq 42
, the interval
[1,\hat{m}_{k}-1]
is the
only
interval of excluded values.
The number
0
and all integers
\geq\hat{m}_{k}
can be modes of the Poisson distribution of order
k
.

Note that the last statement “
k\geq 42
” is itself a numerical conjecture.

All values
42\leq k\leq 100
were tested, and Monte Carlo sampling for values
101\leq k\leq 500
. No counterexamples were found.
The computations were too slow to go up to
k=1000
.
Hence if the numerical conjectures are proved, the above is a complete tabulation of the integers which cannot be modes of the Poisson distribution of order
k
.

Conclusion

One of the two foci of this note was the “first double mode” by which is meant the first occasion (smallest value of
\lambda
)
the Poisson distribution of order
k\geq 2
is bimodal, with modes at
0
and
m>0
.
Denoting the corresponding value of
\lambda
by
\hat{\lambda}_{k}
, it was shown that
\hat{\lambda}_{k}\in[1/\kappa,r_{k}]

where
\kappa=k(k+1)/2
and
r_{k}
is the unique positive root of the equation
h_{k}(k;r_{k})=1
.
It was also shown that the sequence
\{\hat{\lambda}_{k},\,k=2,3,\dots\}
is strictly decreasing in
k
.
(We can trivially include the mode value
m=1
with corresponding value
\lambda=1
for the case
k=1
.)
The corresponding value of
m
for a double mode is denoted by
\hat{m}_{k}
.
It was shown that
\hat{m}_{k}\geq k
.
It was found in
[
1
]
that
\hat{m}_{k}=k
for
2\leq k\leq 14
, but
\hat{m}_{k}>k
for
k\geq 15
.
Numerical calculations show that asymptotically
\hat{m}_{k}\simeq 2.34\,\kappa^{5/8}
(see Fig.
3
).

Next, Sec.
5
presented an “almost complete” answer to the question (posed in
[
6
]
)
“which positive integers cannot be modes of the Poisson distribution of order
k
?”
The answer is “almost complete” because some parts rely on numerical conjectures.
If the numerical conjectures are proved, the answer is complete.

In closing, the calculations in this note do not rule out the possibility of a triple mode.

It is possible that there exist integers
0<m_{1}<m_{2}
such that
0
,
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
are joint modes.
In such a case,
\hat{\lambda}_{k}
would have to be a simultaneous positive real root of the equations
h_{k}(m_{1};\hat{\lambda}_{k})=1
and
h_{k}(m_{2};\hat{\lambda}_{k})=1
.
Such a possibility was not investigated in this note.
More generally, it is an open question if the Poisson distribution of order
k
has triple (or quadruple, etc.) modes,
not necessarily including zero as one of the mode values.

Corrigendum 9/19/2023

After submission of the first version of this post, it was realized that Fig.
3
contains an inconsistency.
It was shown in
[
1
]
that the value of
\kappa\lambda
, i.e. the mean, at the location of the first double mode
scales as
k^{9/8}
(see Fig. 4 in
[
1
]
).
According to the fit in Fig.
3
, the location of the first double mode
\hat{m}_{k}
scales as
\kappa^{5/8}
.
Since
\kappa=k(k+1)/2
, this implies that the location of the first double mode
\hat{m}_{k}
scales as
k^{10/8}
.

However, this is impossible because it was proved in Theorem 2.1 in
[
2
]
that the value of the mode never exceeds the mean.

Note also that Fig. 4 in
[
1
]
is a logarithmic plot (which is better suited to estimate exponents of growth rates),
whereas Fig.
3
is not.

The error of this inconsistency in the analysis is regretted.

Fig.
4
displays a logarithmic plot of the mean (solid curve) and first double mode (dotdash curve) together.
It is seen that the mode is always less than the mean and that asymptotically, they have equal (power law) growth rates.
The dashed line is a fit (
\textrm{constant}\times k^{9/8}
), as in Fig. 4 in
[
1
]
.

It is more informative to study the
difference
between the mean
\mu
and the first double mode
\hat{m}_{k}
.
Fig.
5
displays a plot of the difference
\mu-\hat{m}_{k}
for
2\leq k\leq 10^{4}
(dashed line)
for the Poisson distribution of order
k
.
The dotted line is a fit
3.0+0.38\,k
and is visually indistinguishable from the data.
The above reults indicate the following.

1.

The value of the mode is less than the mean (correct).

2.

The difference between the mean and first double mode is linear in
k
.

3.

The values of both the mean and the first double mode scale asymptotically as
k^{9/8}
.

For fixed
k\geq 1
and
\lambda>0
, and denoting the mode by
m_{k,\lambda}
, Theorem 2.1 in
[
2
]
states that

\lfloor\lambda k(k+1)/2\rfloor-\frac{k(k+1)}{2}+1-\delta_{k,1}\leq m_{k,\lambda}\leq\lfloor\lambda k(k+1)/2\rfloor\,.

(7.2)

Given the results in this updated note (and treating
k\geq 2
only to avoid the Kronecker delta),
the lower bound can be sharpened to subtract
O(k)
.

Conjecture 7.1
.

For fixed
k\geq 2
and
\lambda>0
, an improved lower bound for the mode
m_{k,\lambda}
is

\max\{0,\,\lfloor\lambda k(k+1)/2\rfloor-(c_{0}+c_{1}\,k)\}\leq m_{k,\lambda}\,.

(7.3)

Here
c_{0}
and
c_{1}
are constants to be determined.
