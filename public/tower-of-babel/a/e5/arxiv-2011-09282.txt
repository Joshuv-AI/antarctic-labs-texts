Hyperkähler metrics near Lagrangian submanifolds and symplectic groupoids
Maxence Mayrand

Abstract
. The first part of this paper is a generalization of the Feix–Kaledin theorem on the existence of a hyperkähler metric on a neighbourhood of the zero section of the cotangent bundle of a Kähler manifold. We show that the problem of constructing a hyperkähler structure on a neighbourhood of a complex Lagrangian submanifold in a holomorphic symplectic manifold reduces to the existence of certain deformations of holomorphic symplectic structures. The Feix–Kaledin structure is recovered from the twisted cotangent bundle. We then show that every holomorphic symplectic groupoid over a compact holomorphic Poisson surface of Kähler type has a hyperkähler structure on a neighbourhood of its identity section. More generally, we reduce the existence of a hyperkähler structure on a symplectic realization of a holomorphic Poisson manifold of any dimension to the existence of certain deformations of holomorphic Poisson structures adapted from Hitchin’s unobstructedness theorem.



1.
Introduction

A hyperkähler manifold, as first defined by Calabi

[
12
]
, is a Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
with three complex structures
I,J,K
that are Kähler with respect to
g
and satisfy the quaternionic identities
I^{2}=J^{2}=K^{2}=IJK=-1
.
One of the main features of this geometry is its deep connection with symplectic geometry.
In particular, every hyperkähler manifold has an underlying holomorphic symplectic structure, namely
(I,\omega_{J}+i\omega_{K})
, where
\omega_{J}
and
\omega_{K}
are the Kähler forms of
J
and
K
, respectively.
Conversely, Yau’s solution to the Calabi conjecture
[
50
]
implies that every
compact
holomorphic symplectic manifold of Kähler type is endowed with a hyperkähler structure, as first observed by Beauville
[
4
, Proposition 4]
.

In the non-compact case, there is no such general existence result, and the problem of constructing hyperkähler metrics on non-compact holomorphic symplectic manifolds has generated a lot of research since Calabi’s first examples
[
12
]
on
T^{*}\mathbb{CP}^{n}
.
Prominent examples include coadjoint orbits of complex semisimple Lie algebras
[
35
,
36
,
6
,
32
]
, cotangent bundles of hermitian symmetric spaces
[
7
,
8
]
, the ALE spaces on resolutions of Kleinian singularities
[
34
]
later generalized to Nakajima quiver varieties
[
42
]
, and various moduli spaces in gauge theory, such as moduli spaces of instantons
[
39
]
, monopoles
[
2
]
, Higgs bundles
[
24
]
, and Nahm’s equations
[
35
,
36
,
6
,
32
,
33
,
5
]
.
Perhaps the most general existence result is that of Feix
[
19
]
and Kaledin
[
27
,
28
]
, who independently showed that the cotangent bundle of any Kähler manifold has a hyperkähler structure on a neighbourhood of its zero section.

The first result of this paper is a generalization of the Feix–Kaledin theorem: we reduce the problem of constructing a hyperkähler metric on a neighbourhood of a complex Lagrangian submanifold in a holomorphic symplectic manifold to the existence of certain deformations of holomorphic symplectic structures (Theorem
A
).
The Feix–Kaledin theorem is recovered from the twisted cotangent bundle (Example
1.4
).
The proof uses twistor theory, as in Feix’s proof
[
19
]
.
We then use this result to associate a hyperkähler manifold to every compact holomorphic Poisson surface endowed with a Kähler metric, as we now explain.

The notion of symplectic groupoids, introduced by Weinstein
[
48
]
, Karasev
[
30
,
29
]
, and Zakrzewski
[
51
,
52
]
, has played a central rôle in Poisson geometry, especially for the problem of quantization.
Every smooth Poisson manifold
(X,\sigma)
has an associated Lie algebroid
(T^{*}X)_{\sigma}
on its cotangent bundle, so one can ask for a Lie groupoid
\mathcal{G}\rightrightarrows X
integrating it.
If it exists,
\mathcal{G}
is a
symplectic groupoid
, i.e. it is endowed with a canonical symplectic form compatible with multiplication
[
40
, §5]
.
Conversely, every symplectic groupoid
\mathcal{G}\rightrightarrows X
induces a Poisson structure
\sigma
on
X
such that
\Lie(\mathcal{G})=(T^{*}X)_{\sigma}
.
Poisson manifolds are then viewed as infinitesimal versions of symplectic groupoids, and we say that
\mathcal{G}
an
integration

X
, in analogy with the integration of a Lie algebra to a Lie group.
Not all Poisson manifolds are integrable, but they are all integrable by a symplectic
local
groupoid (the axiomatization of a neighbourhood of the identity section of a symplectic groupoid), as first shown by Karasev
[
29
]
and Weinstein
[
48
,
15
]
. This was also reproved later by Cattaneo–Felder
[
13
]
using a Poisson sigma model, which led to a complete criterion for global integrability by Crainic–Fernandes
[
16
,
17
]
.

A symplectic local groupoid is, in particular, a
symplectic realization

[
47
,
15
]
, i.e. a symplectic manifold
M
together with a surjective Poisson submersion
M\to X
and a Lagrangian section
X\hookrightarrow M
.
Conversely, all symplectic realizations are symplectic local groupoid, and this structure is essentially unique after restricting to a neighbourhood of the identity section
[
15
, III §1]
.
The symplectic realization of a Poisson manifold
X
can then be thought of as a canonical desingularization of
X
, where the foliation by symplectic leaves on
X
has been resolved to a single leaf
M
.

There is an analogous theory of Poisson manifolds in the holomorphic category, which has gained considerable interest in recent years, in particular through its relationship with generalized geometry, deformation theory, and classification problems.
In particular, the question of which holomorphic Poisson manifold admits an integration by a holomorphic symplectic groupoid has been solved
[
37
,
38
]
, and one of the consequences is an analogue of the Karasev–Weinstein theorem: every holomorphic Poisson manifold is integrable by a holomorphic symplectic local groupoid (see also
[
11
]
).
As examples of holomorphic symplectic manifolds (typically non-compact), it is natural to ask if they admit hyperkähler metrics.

The archetypal example is the dual
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
of a Lie algebra with its Kirillov–Kostant–Souriau Poisson structure, which is integrated by
T^{*}G
, where
G
is any Lie group integrating
\mathfrak{g}
.
If
\mathfrak{g}
is complex semisimple,
T^{*}G
admits a hyperkähler structure discovered by Kronheimer
[
33
]
as a moduli space of solutions to the one-dimensional reduction of the anti-self-dual Yang–Mills equations (Nahm’s equations).
Similarly, if
X
is endowed with the zero Poisson structure

\sigma=0
, then
T^{*}X
is an integration, and we have the
Feix–Kaledin metric
[
19
,
27
,
28
]
on a neighbourhood of the zero section, which is the identity section when viewed as a groupoid.
At the other extreme, if
\sigma
is non-degenerate and
X
is compact, then
X
itself is hyperkähler, and hence so is its groupoid
X\times X
.
From these examples, it is natural to ask:

Question 1.1
.

Does every holomorphic symplectic groupoid over a Kähler manifold have a hyperkähler structure on a neighbourhood of its identity section?

We will show that the answer is ‘yes’ when the base
X
is of complex dimension two and is compact.
This can be viewed as another instance where real dimension four plays a special rôle.

To prove this existence result, we first formulate the problem in any dimension to a problem of finding special deformations of Poisson structures on
X
adapted from Hitchin’s unobstructedness theorem
[
25
]
.
If they exist, these deformations can be lifted to the holomorphic symplectic groupoid to construct the twistor space (Theorem
B
).
We then show existence in the two dimensional case (Theorem
C
).

1.1.
Statement of results

Recall that the complex structure of a holomorphic symplectic manifold
(M,\Omega_{0})
is uniquely determined by the symplectic form
\Omega_{0}
, since
T^{0,1}M=\ker(\Omega_{0})
.
Hence, we define a
deformation of holomorphic symplectic structures
to be a family of complex 2-forms
\Omega_{\zeta}
on
M
, depending holomorphically on
\zeta
in a neighbourhood of
0
in
\mathbb{C}
, such that each
\Omega_{\zeta}
is holomorphic symplectic with respect to some (unique) complex structure.
We say that the family is
real-analytic
if it is given in holomorphic coordinates of
(M,\Omega_{0})
by real-analytic functions.
Also, since a holomorphic symplectic form determines its complex structure, a hyperkähler structure
(g,I,J,K)
can be identified with its triple
(\omega_{I},\omega_{J},\omega_{K})
of Kähler forms.

Our first result is a characterization of hyperkähler metrics near Lagrangian submanifolds in terms of deformations of holomorphic symplectic structures:

Theorem A
.

Let
(M,\Omega_{0})
be a holomorphic symplectic manifold and
X
a complex Lagrangian submanifold.
Suppose that there is a real-analytic deformation of holomorphic symplectic structures
\Omega_{\zeta}
of
(M,\Omega_{0})
such that
\iota^{*}\Omega_{\zeta}=2i\zeta\omega
for some Kähler form
\omega
on
X
, where
\iota:X\hookrightarrow M
is the inclusion map.
Then, there is a hyperkähler structure
(\omega_{I},\omega_{J},\omega_{K})
on a neighbourhood of
X
in
M
such that
\Omega_{0}=\omega_{J}+i\omega_{K}
and
\iota^{*}\omega_{I}=\omega
.

The hyperkähler structure is obtained by constructing the twistor space by gluing two halves together, as in
[
19
]
.
We review this method in §
2
and prove Theorem
A
in §
3
.

Remark 1.2
.

Conversely, every hyperkähler structure
(\omega_{I},\omega_{J},\omega_{K})
can be obtained in this way using the twistor family (see §
2
) of holomorphic symplectic forms

\Omega_{\zeta}\coloneqq(\omega_{J}+i\omega_{K})+2i\zeta\omega_{I}+\zeta^{2}(\omega_{J}-i\omega_{K}).

(1.1)

Remark 1.3
.

More generally, if
\omega
is a pseudo-Kähler form on
X
of signature
(p,q)
, we get a pseudo-hyperkähler structure of signature
(2p,2q)
(Lemma
3.6
).

Example 1.4
.

If
X
is a complex manifold with a real-analytic Kähler
form
\omega
, we recover the Feix–Kaledin hyperkähler
metric
[
19
,
27
,
28
]
on a
neighbourhood of the zero section of
M=T^{*}X
by taking
\Omega_{\zeta}\coloneqq\Omega_{0}+2i\zeta\pi^{*}\omega
, where
\pi:T^{*}X\to X
is the bundle map and
\Omega_{0}
is the canonical holomorphic symplectic form (see §
4.2
).
This is known as the
twisted cotangent bundle
, and appears, for example, in Donaldson’s reformulation of Kähler geometry
[
18
, §2]
, as also explained in
[
9
, §2]
.
The complex structure induced by
\Omega_{\zeta}
is not the original one, but is biholomorphic to the affine bundle modeled on
T^{*}X
defined by the Kähler class
[\omega]\in H^{1}(X,T^{*}X)
(see
[
1
, Appendix]
and
[
21
]
).

Remark 1.5

(Deformations of hyperkähler structures)
.

Theorem
A
implies that any complex Lagrangian submanifold in a hyperkähler manifold defines a deformation of hyperkähler structures.
Indeed, (
1.1
) satisfies the condition of the theorem, but so does
\Omega_{t\zeta}
for any
t>0
.
Hence, there is a family of Kähler forms
\omega_{I}(t)
on neighbourhoods of
X
, such that
(\omega_{I}(t),\omega_{J},\omega_{K})
is hyperkähler and
\iota^{*}\omega_{I}(t)=t\iota^{*}\omega_{I}
for all
t>0
.
For example, by applying this idea to Kronheimer’s hyperkähler structure on the cotangent bundle of a complex reductive group
[
33
]
, the zero section induces the one-parameter family of hyperkähler structures obtained by varying the length of the interval on which Nahm’s equations are defined.

We now turn our attention to holomorphic Poisson geometry.
Recall that a
holomorphic Poisson manifold
is a complex manifold
X
together with a holomorphic bivector field
\sigma
such that the bilinear operator
\{f,g\}\coloneqq\sigma(df,dg)
acting on holomorphic functions
f,g
satisfies the Jacobi identity.
A
holomorphic symplectic groupoid
(see e.g.
[
38
, §3]
) is a holomorphic Lie groupoid
\mathcal{G}\rightrightarrows X
together with a holomorphic symplectic form
\Omega
such that the graph of the multiplication map is Lagrangian in
\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{G}^{-}
, where
\mathcal{G}^{-}
is
\mathcal{G}
with the opposite symplectic structure
-\Omega
.
It follows that
X
is endowed with a canonical holomorphic Poisson structure such that the source map
s:\mathcal{G}\to X
is a holomorphic Poisson submersion.
The identity section is always a complex Lagrangian submanifold
[
15
, II Proposition 1.1]
, and we wish to use it with Theorem
A
.
Since we are only considering a neighbourhood of the identity section, we can in fact generalize the discussion to symplectic realizations in the following sense.

Definition 1.6

(Weinstein
[
47
, §7]

[
15
, III §1]
)
.

A
symplectic realization
of a holomorphic Poisson manifold
X
is a holomorphic symplectic manifold
M
containing
X
as a complex Lagrangian submanifold together with a surjective holomorphic Poisson submersion
s:M\to X
such that
s\circ\iota=\mathrm{id}_{X}
, where
\iota:X\hookrightarrow M
is the inclusion map.

Remark 1.7
.

In
[
15
, III Definition 1.1]
, this is called a
strict
symplectic realization.

For example, a neighbourhood of the identity section of a holomorphic symplectic groupoid together with its source map is a symplectic realization.
Conversely, any symplectic realization has the structure of a symplectic local groupoid, possibly after restricting to a neighbourhood of
X
in
M

[
15
, III Theorem 1.2]
.
Also, every holomorphic Poisson manifold has a symplectic realization in this sense
[
11
]
.

The next result shows that a hyperkähler structure on a symplectic realization can be constructed if we can solve certain differential equations on the Poisson manifold.

Theorem B
.

Let
(X,\sigma)
be a (not-necessarily compact) holomorphic Poisson manifold.
Suppose that there is a real-analytic family
\omega(\zeta)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\omega_{n}\zeta^{n}
of
(1,1)
-forms on
X
solving the equation

d\omega+\tfrac{1}{2}\partial i_{\sigma}(\omega\wedge\omega)=0,

(1.2)

for
\zeta
in a neighbourhood
U
of
0
in
\mathbb{C}
, such that

(a)

\omega_{1}
is a Kähler form,

(b)

1-\phi_{\zeta}\bar{\phi}_{\zeta}
is invertible for all
\zeta\in U
, where
\phi_{\zeta}\coloneqq-\sigma\circ\omega(\zeta):T_{\mathbb{C}}X\to T_{\mathbb{C}}X
,

(c)

i_{\sigma}(\omega_{2n}\wedge\omega_{1})=0
and
\omega_{2n+1}=0
for all
n\geq 1
.

Then, any symplectic realization
(M,\Omega_{0})
of
(X,\sigma)
has a hyperkähler structure
(\omega_{I},\omega_{J},\omega_{K})
on a neighbourhood of
X
such that
\Omega_{0}=\omega_{J}+i\omega_{K}
and
\iota^{*}\omega_{I}=\omega_{1}
.

Equation (
1.2
) comes from Hitchin’s method
[
25
]
for constructing deformations of holomorphic Poisson structures, where
\phi_{\zeta}
is shown to solve the Maurer–Cartan equation.
This will be explained in details in §
4
.
The conditions on the coefficients
\omega_{n}
ensure that this deformation can be lifted to a deformation of holomorphic symplectic structures
\Omega_{\zeta}
on
M
in such a way that
\iota^{*}\Omega_{\zeta}=2i\zeta\omega_{1}
, as required for Theorem
A
.
More precisely, we define

\Omega_{\zeta}\coloneqq\Omega_{0}+s^{*}\beta(i\zeta)-t^{*}\beta(-i\zeta),

where
\beta(\zeta)=\omega(\zeta)+\tfrac{1}{2}i_{\sigma}(\omega(\zeta)\wedge\omega(\zeta))
(which can be viewed as a
B
-field) and
t
is a target map.
If
X
is compact Kähler, there always exists
\omega(\zeta)
satisfying (a) and (b), but (c) imposes additional constraints which we can only obtain in special cases.

Example 1.8
.

We recover the Feix–Kaledin hyperkähler structure even more easily from Theorem
B
with
\sigma=0
and
\omega(\zeta)\coloneqq\zeta\omega_{1}
for any real-analytic Kähler form
\omega_{1}
.
We will show that our hyperkähler structure coincides with the Feix–Kaledin one in §
4.2
.

Example 1.9
.

Let
X
be a hyperkähler manifold with Kähler forms
(\omega_{I},\omega_{J},\omega_{K})
and let
\sigma
be the inverse of the holomorphic symplectic structure
\Omega=\omega_{J}+i\omega_{K}
.
Then,
\omega(\zeta)=\zeta\omega_{I}
satisfies the conditions of Theorem
B
.
Indeed,
\tfrac{1}{2}i_{\sigma}(\omega_{I}\wedge\omega_{I})=\omega_{I}\sigma\omega_{I}=\tfrac{1}{4}\omega_{I}(\omega_{J}^{-1}-i\omega_{K}^{-1})\omega_{I}=\tfrac{1}{4}(-\omega_{J}+i\omega_{K})=-\frac{1}{4}\bar{\Omega}
, using that
\omega_{J}^{-1}\omega_{I}=K
so
\omega_{J}^{-1}\omega_{I}=-\omega_{I}^{-1}\omega_{J}
and similarly
\omega_{K}^{-1}\omega_{I}=-\omega_{I}^{-1}\omega_{K}
(cf.
[
22
, Example 5.7]
).
Hence, both terms of
d\omega+\tfrac{1}{2}\partial i_{\sigma}(\omega\wedge\omega)
are zero.
Now, the pair groupoid
X\times X
is a symplectic realization of
X
, with symplectic form
(\Omega,-\Omega)
, source map
s(x,y)=x
, and identity section
\iota(x)=(x,x)
.
Then, Theorem
B
recovers the hyperkähler structure on
X\times X
with complex structures
(I,I)
,
(J,-J)
,
(K,-K)
.

Finally, we show that the equations in Theorem
B
can be solved in the two-dimensional case.

Theorem C
.

Let
(X,\sigma)
be a compact holomorphic Poisson surface together with a real-analytic Kähler form
\omega
and let
s:(M,\Omega)\to(X,\sigma)
be a symplectic realization.
Then, there is a hyperkähler structure
(\omega_{I},\omega_{J},\omega_{K})
on a neighbourhood of
X
in
M
such that
\Omega=\omega_{J}+i\omega_{K}
and
\iota^{*}\omega_{I}=\omega
.

Remark 1.10
.

Real-analyticity of the Kähler form is a necessary condition since the twistor correspondence
[
26
, §3(F)]
implies that all hyperkähler structures are real-analytic.
On the other hand, every Kähler class has a real-analytic representative
[
21
, Proposition 1.7]
.

Example 1.11
.

For any two-dimensional compact holomorphic symplectic manifold
X
with a real-analytic Kähler form
\omega
, Theorem
C
shows that there is a hyperkäler structure
(\omega_{I},\omega_{J},\omega_{K})
on a neighbourhood of the diagonal in
X\times X
such
\iota^{*}\omega_{I}=\omega
.
There is, of course, already a hyperkähler structure on
X
by Yau’s theorem
[
50
]
and hence on
X\times X
, but the restriction of the first Kähler form to the diagonal is not equal to
\omega
in general, so the two hyperkähler structures are different.

1.2.
Future work

The next natural step is, of course, the study of examples.
There is a complete classification of compact holomorphic Poisson surfaces up to birational equivalences derived from the Enriques–Kodaira classification, so there is a wealth of explicit examples to study (see e.g. Pym’s overview
[
45
]
).
In particular, there is a large family of Poisson structures on
\mathbb{CP}^{2}
obtained by choosing a cubic curve to specify the vanishing locus of the Poisson bivector field.
Using the Fubini–Study metric, our result then implies the existence of a canonical hyperkähler manifold associated to any elliptic curve, and which deforms the complete hyperkähler metric on
T^{*}\mathbb{CP}^{2}
found by Calabi
[
12
]
(which can be viewed as coming from the zero Poisson structure).
An interesting question for future work is whether these new metrics extend to the whole groupoid and are complete.
Since hyperkähler structures are real-analytic, the extension is unique.

2.
Twistor spaces by gluing

We now recall and clarify a general construction of hyperkähler metrics by gluing two halves of a twistor space.
This is the idea used by Feix
[
19
]
for the cotangent bundle of a Kähler manifold.
It also appears in many works, such as Deligne’s construction of the twistor space of the Hitchin moduli space, as explained by Simpson
[
46
, §4]
, and Atiyah–Hitchin’s description of the hyperkähler metric of the monopole moduli space
[
2
, Ch. 5]
, for instance.

2.1.
The hyperkähler twistor correspondence

In this subsection, we will first recall Hitchin–Karlhede–Lindström–Roček’s adaptation of Penrose’s non-linear twistor theory
[
44
]
for the construction of hyperkähler metrics
[
26
, §3(F)]
.
The idea is encode a hyperkähler structure into purely holomorphic data on an auxiliary space.

We work with pseudo-hyperkähler manifolds first; the positive definiteness of the metric is a separate condition.
Recall that a
pseudo-hyperkähler manifold
is a smooth manifold
M
with a pseudo-Riemannian metric
g
and complex structures
I,J,K
which are pseudo-Kähler with respect to
g
and satisfy
IJK=-1
.
The corresponding pseudo-Kähler forms will be denoted
\omega_{I},\omega_{J},\omega_{K}
and, since they determine
I,J,K
and
g
, a pseudo-hyperkähler structure will be identified with its triple
(\omega_{I},\omega_{J},\omega_{K})
of symplectic forms.

Let
S^{2}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{3}
be the unit two-sphere endowed with its standard complex structure
\mathsf{I}_{x}y=x\times y
for
x\in S^{2}
and
y\in T_{x}S^{2}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
For
x\in S^{2}
, let
I_{x}\coloneqq x_{1}I+x_{2}J+x_{3}K
and
\omega_{x}\coloneqq x_{1}\omega_{I}+x_{2}\omega_{J}+x_{3}\omega_{K}
.
Then,
(g,I_{x},\omega_{x})
is a pseudo-Kähler structure for all
x\in S^{2}
.
Moreover, the space
Z\coloneqq M\times S^{2}
becomes a complex manifold by endowing it with the almost complex structure
(I_{x},\mathsf{I})
at
(p,x)\in M\times S^{2}
, and the projection
\pi:Z\to S^{2}
is a surjective holomorphic submersion.

Let
x\in S^{2}
and choose
y,z\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
so that
(x,y,z)
is a positively oriented orthonormal basis.
Then, the complex two-form
\omega_{y}+i\omega_{z}
is a holomorphic symplectic form with respect to
I_{x}
.
Moreover, by viewing
y
as a tangent vector to
x
, the tensor product
(\omega_{y}+i\omega_{z})\otimes y\in\mathchoice{{\textstyle\bigwedge}}{{\bigwedge}}{{\textstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}^{2}(\ker d\pi)^{*}\otimes T_{x}S^{2}
is independent of the choice of
(y,z)
, and defines a global
holomorphic
section
\Omega
of
\mathchoice{{\textstyle\bigwedge}}{{\bigwedge}}{{\textstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}^{2}(\ker d\pi)^{*}\otimes\pi^{*}TS^{2}
.
Also, the map
\tau:Z\to Z:(p,x)\mapsto(p,-x)
is a real structure (i.e. an anti-holomorphic involution) covering the antipodal map on
S^{2}
and such that
\tau^{*}\bar{\Omega}=-\Omega
.
Finally, each point
p
of
M
induces a holomorphic section
x\mapsto(p,x)
of
\pi
, which is
\tau
invariant and has normal bundle isomorphic to
\mathcal{O}(1)^{\oplus 2n}
.

Now, identify
S^{2}
with
\mathbb{CP}^{1}
via the biholomorphism

\mathbb{CP}^{1}\longrightarrow S^{2},\quad\zeta\longmapsto\left(\frac{1-|\zeta|^{2}}{1+|\zeta|^{2}},i\frac{\bar{\zeta}-\zeta}{1+|\zeta|^{2}},-\frac{\zeta+\bar{\zeta}}{1+|\zeta|^{2}}\right).

Then, the holomorphic section
\Omega
defined above is of the form

\Omega=\left((\omega_{J}+i\omega_{K})+2i\zeta\omega_{I}+\zeta^{2}(\omega_{J}-i\omega_{K})\right)\otimes\tfrac{i}{2}\tfrac{\partial}{\partial\zeta}.

Upon identifying
T\mathbb{CP}^{1}
with
\mathcal{O}(2)
, we arrive at:

Definition 2.1
.

A
hyperkähler twistor space
is a complex manifold
Z
of dimension
2n+1
, together with

(a)

a surjective holomorphic submersion
\pi:Z\to\mathbb{CP}^{1}
,

(b)

a global holomorphic section
\Omega
of
\mathchoice{{\textstyle\bigwedge}}{{\bigwedge}}{{\textstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}^{2}(\ker d\pi)^{*}\otimes\pi^{*}\mathcal{O}(2)
which restricts to a holomorphic symplectic form on each fibre
Z_{\zeta}\coloneqq\pi^{-1}(\zeta)
, and

(c)

a real structure
\tau:Z\to Z
covering the antipodal map
\rho:\mathbb{CP}^{1}\to\mathbb{CP}^{1}
and such that
\tau^{*}\bar{\Omega}=-\Omega
.

A
twistor line
is a holomorphic section of
\pi
whose normal bundle is isomorphic to
\mathcal{O}(1)^{\oplus 2n}
.
A twistor line
s
is
real
if
\tau\circ s=s\circ\rho
.

The twistor method says that, conversely, this data is enough to construct a hyperkähler manifold.

Theorem 2.2

(Hitchin–Karlhede–Lindström–Roček
[
26
, §3(F)]
)
.

Let
(Z,\pi,\Omega,\tau)
be a hyperkähler twistor space.
Then, the set
\mathcal{M}
of real twistor lines is a smooth manifold endowed with a pseudo-hyperkähler structure
(\omega_{I},\omega_{J},\omega_{K})
.
Moreover, for each
\zeta\in\mathbb{CP}^{1}\setminus\{\infty\}
, the evaluation map
\mathrm{ev}_{\zeta}:\mathcal{M}\to Z_{\zeta}
is a local biholomorphism with respect to
I_{\zeta}
on
\mathcal{M}
and a complex symplectomorphism, i.e.

\mathrm{ev}_{\zeta}^{*}\Omega_{\zeta}=(\omega_{J}+i\omega_{K})+2i\zeta\omega_{I}+\zeta^{2}(\omega_{J}-i\omega_{K}),

where
\Omega_{\zeta}
is the
2
-form on
Z_{\zeta}
defined by
\Omega=\Omega_{\zeta}\otimes\tfrac{i}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta}
. ∎

In particular, if we find a single real twistor line
s:\mathbb{CP}^{1}\to Z
passing through a point
x\in Z_{0}
, the theorem says that
s
lives in a
4n
-dimensional family of real twistor lines and that there is a hyperkähler structure on a neighbourhood of
x
in
Z_{0}
.
If, more generally, we have a submanifold
X\subseteq Z_{0}
and a family real twistor lines
s_{x}\in\mathcal{M}
parametrized by
X
such that
s_{x}(0)=x
for all
x\in X
, then there is a hyperkähler structure on a neighbourhood of
X
in
Z_{0}
compatible with its holomorphic symplectic structure.
This is the idea behind Feix’s proof
[
19
]
, which summarize as follows:

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
(Z,\pi,\Omega,\tau)
be a hyperkähler twistor space and let
\mathcal{M}
be the space of real twistor lines.
Suppose that there is a smooth submanifold
X\subseteq Z_{0}
and a smooth map
X\to\mathcal{M}:x\mapsto s_{x}
such that
s_{x}(0)=x
for all
x\in X
.
Then, there is a unique pseudo-hyperkähler structure
(\omega_{I},\omega_{J},\omega_{K})
on a neighbourhood of
X
in
Z_{0}

such that

\mathrm{ev}_{\zeta}^{*}\Omega_{\zeta}=\mathrm{ev}_{0}^{*}((\omega_{J}+i\omega_{K})+2i\zeta\omega_{I}+\zeta^{2}(\omega_{J}-i\omega_{K}))

for all
\zeta\neq\infty
, where
\Omega_{\zeta}
is defined by
\Omega=\Omega_{\zeta}\otimes\frac{i}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta}
.

Proof.

Since
\mathrm{ev}_{0}:\mathcal{M}\to Z_{0}
is a local diffeomorphism and
s:X\to\mathcal{M}:x\mapsto s_{x}
is a section of
\mathrm{ev}_{0}
,
s
is an embedding.
Hence, by restricting to a sufficiently small neighbourhood
U
of
s(X)
in
\mathcal{M}
,
\mathrm{ev}_{0}:U\to Z_{0}
is a diffeomorphism onto its image, so we can pushforward the pseudo-hyperkähler structure.
∎

We now make a few observations which will be useful to find twistor lines.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
(Z,\pi,\Omega,\tau)
be a
(2n+1)
-dimensional hyperkähler twistor space.
Then, the normal bundle of any holomorphic section of
\pi:Z\to\mathbb{CP}^{1}
has degree
2n
.

Proof.

The normal bundle
N
of a holomorphic section
s
can be identified with
s^{*}\ker d\pi
, so
\Omega
restricts to a section of
\mathchoice{{\textstyle\bigwedge}}{{\bigwedge}}{{\textstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}^{2}N^{*}\otimes\mathcal{O}(2)
and hence
\Omega^{n}
gives a non-vanishing section of
\mathchoice{{\textstyle\bigwedge}}{{\bigwedge}}{{\textstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}^{2n}N^{*}\otimes\mathcal{O}(2n)
, so
\det(N)\cong\mathcal{O}(2n)
.
∎

To get a twistor line, we need moreover that
N\cong\mathcal{O}(1)^{\oplus 2n}
, and this will be obtained from the following lemma.

Lemma 2.5

(cf.
[
19
, p. 41]
)
.

Let
E\to\mathbb{CP}^{1}
be a holomorphic vector bundle of rank
m
.
Then,
E\cong\mathcal{O}(1)^{\oplus m}
if and only if
\deg(E)=m
and there exist global holomorphic sections
s_{1},\ldots,s_{m}
of
E
which are linearly independent in at least one of the fibres and each
s_{i}
has a zero.

Proof.

The forward direction is clear.
Conversely, by Grothendieck’s theorem, we have
E=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{m}\mathcal{O}(k_{i})
for some
k_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{m}k_{i}=m
, so it suffices to show that
k_{i}>0
for all
i
.
Suppose that
k_{i}\leq 0
for some
i
. If
k_{i}=0
, then for all
j
, the projection of
s_{j}
to
\mathcal{O}(k_{i})=\mathcal{O}
is zero since
s_{j}
has a zero.
This is also true if
k_{i}<0
since then
\mathcal{O}(k_{i})
has no non-zero section.
In particular, for all
\zeta\in\mathbb{CP}^{1}
,
s_{1}(\zeta),\ldots,s_{m}(\zeta)
are contained in an
(m-1)
-dimensional subspace of
E_{\zeta}
, contradicting that they linear independent in one of the fibres.
∎

2.2.
The gluing construction

If
(Z,\pi,\Omega,\tau)
is a hyperkähler twistor space, the real structure
\tau
identifies
Z|_{\mathbb{CP}^{1}\setminus\{\infty\}}
and
Z|_{\mathbb{CP}^{1}\setminus\{0\}}
.
Hence,
Z
can be recovered from its restriction to
\mathbb{C}\subseteq\mathbb{CP}^{1}
and the gluing map
\tau:Z|_{\mathbb{C}^{*}}\to Z|_{\mathbb{C}^{*}}
.
In the context of deformation theory, we obtain deformation spaces
Z\to\mathbb{C}
over arbitrarily small neighbourhoods of
0
in
\mathbb{C}
, so it is useful to formulate this gluing principle slightly more generally.

For
\varepsilon,\delta>0
, define the discs, circle, and annulus

\displaystyle D_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{\zeta\in\mathbb{C}:|\zeta|<\varepsilon\}

\displaystyle\bar{D}_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{\zeta\in\mathbb{C}:|\zeta|\leq\varepsilon\}

\displaystyle S^{1}_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{\zeta\in\mathbb{C}:|\zeta|=\varepsilon\}

\displaystyle A_{\varepsilon,\delta}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{\zeta\in\mathbb{C}:\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\varepsilon+\delta}<|\zeta|<\varepsilon+\delta\}.

Then, the antipodal map
\zeta\mapsto-\zeta
on
S^{1}_{\varepsilon}
extends uniquely to a real structure

\rho_{\varepsilon}:\mathbb{C}^{*}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{*},\quad\rho_{\varepsilon}(\zeta)=-\varepsilon^{2}/\bar{\zeta},

(2.1)

which restricts to
A_{\varepsilon,\delta}\to A_{\varepsilon,\delta}
for all
\delta>0
.
Moreover,
\mathbb{CP}^{1}
can be constructed by gluing two copies of
D_{\varepsilon+\delta}
by
\rho_{\varepsilon}:A_{\varepsilon,\delta}\to A_{\varepsilon,\delta}
, where the second copy of
D_{\varepsilon+\delta}
has the opposite complex structure.
Twistor spaces can be built similarly by deformation spaces over small discs in
\mathbb{C}
:

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
Z
be a complex manifold together with

(a)

a holomorphic submersion
\pi:Z\to\mathbb{C}
such that
\bar{D}_{\varepsilon}\subseteq\pi(Z)
for some
\varepsilon>0
,

(b)

a global holomorphic section
\Omega
of
\mathchoice{{\textstyle\bigwedge}}{{\bigwedge}}{{\textstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}^{2}(\ker d\pi)^{*}\to Z
which restricts to a holomorphic symplectic form on each fibre of
\pi
,

(c)

a real structure
\tau:U\to U
covering
\rho_{\varepsilon}
and satisfying
\tau^{*}\bar{\Omega}=\varepsilon^{2}\Omega/\pi^{2}
, where
U\subseteq Z
is an open set such that
S^{1}_{\varepsilon}\subseteq\pi(U)
and such that
\tau
extends continuously to
\bar{U}\setminus Z_{0}
.

Then, this data defines a hyperkähler twistor space
\mathcal{Z}
extending
(Z,\pi,\Omega,\tau)
, where
\mathcal{Z}
is defined by gluing two copies of
V\coloneqq U\cup\pi^{-1}(D_{\varepsilon})
via
\tau:U\to U
,

\mathcal{Z}\coloneqq(V\sqcup V)/\{z\sim\tau(z)\}.

A section
s
of
\mathcal{Z}
is equivalent to a pair of sections
s_{\pm}:D_{\varepsilon+\delta}\to U\cup\pi^{-1}(D_{\varepsilon})
of
\pi
, for some
\delta>0
, such that
\tau(s_{+}(\zeta))=s_{-}(\rho_{\varepsilon}(\zeta))
for all
\zeta\in s_{+}^{-1}(U)
.
The section is real if and only if
s_{+}=s_{-}
.
The normal bundle of
s
in
\mathcal{Z}
is isomorphic to the gluing of the bundles
s_{+}^{*}(\ker d\pi)
and
s_{-}^{*}(\ker d\pi)
over
D_{\varepsilon+\delta}
via
d\tau
.

Lemma 2.7
.

The space
\mathcal{Z}
is Hausdorff.

Proof.

It suffices to show that for all
x,y\in\bar{U}\cap V\setminus U
there are neighbourhoods
V_{x}
and
V_{y}
of
x
and
y
in
V
such that
\tau(V_{x}\cap U)\cap V_{y}=\emptyset
.
If
x\in Z_{0}
, we can take
0<\delta<\varepsilon
small enough so that
|\pi(y)|+\delta<\frac{1}{\delta}
and let
V_{x}=\pi^{-1}(D_{\delta})
and
V_{y}=\pi^{-1}(A_{|\pi(y)|,\delta})\cap V
.
Then, if
z\in\tau(V_{x}\cap U)\cap V_{y}
, we have
\frac{1}{\delta}<|\pi(z)|<|\pi(y)|+\delta
, contradicting the choice of
\delta
.
Hence, we may assume that
x\in\bar{U}\setminus Z_{0}
so that
\tau(x)
is well-defined.
We claim that
\tau(x)\notin V
.
First, note that
|\pi(\tau(x))|>\varepsilon
since
\pi(\tau(x))=\rho_{\varepsilon}(\pi(x))
and
|\pi(x)|<\varepsilon
.
Hence, if
\tau(x)\in V
, then
\tau(x)\in U
so
x=\tau(\tau(x))\in U
, a contradiction.
So
\tau(x)\notin V
and hence
\tau(x)\neq y
.
Since
Z
is Hausdorff, there are neighbourhoods
W_{x}
and
W_{y}
of
\tau(x)
and
y
in
Z
such that
W_{x}\cap W_{y}=\emptyset
so we can take
V_{x}=V\cap\tau^{-1}(W_{x})
and
V_{y}=V\cap W_{y}
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
2.6
.

Since
\tau
is anti-holomorphic, it follows that
\mathcal{Z}
is a complex manifold by endowing the second copy of
V
with the opposite complex structure.
Moreover, by identifying
\mathbb{CP}^{1}
with the gluing of two copies of
D_{\varepsilon+\delta}
by
\rho_{\varepsilon}
, we get a surjective holomorphic submersion
\pi:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{CP}^{1}
.
The real structure
\mathcal{T}:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathcal{Z}
is defined to be the anti-holomorphic involution which descends from the map
V\sqcup V\to V\sqcup V
exchanging the two copies of
V
.

Now, consider the local section
\Psi\coloneqq\Omega\otimes\frac{i}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta}
of
\mathchoice{{\textstyle\bigwedge}}{{\bigwedge}}{{\textstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}^{2}(\ker d\pi)^{*}\otimes T\mathbb{CP}^{1}
.
Let
\tau_{\zeta}:U\cap Z_{\zeta}\to U\cap Z_{\rho_{\varepsilon}(\zeta)}
be the restriction of
\tau
on the fibres.
Then, the condition
\tau^{*}\bar{\Omega}=\varepsilon^{2}\Omega/\pi^{2}
is equivalent to
\tau_{\zeta}^{*}\bar{\Omega}_{\rho_{\varepsilon}(\zeta)}=\varepsilon^{2}\Omega_{\zeta}/\zeta^{2}
, so we have

\tau^{*}\bar{\Psi}=\tau_{\zeta}^{*}\bar{\Omega}_{\rho_{\varepsilon}(\zeta)}\otimes d\rho_{\varepsilon}(-\tfrac{i}{2}\tfrac{\partial}{\partial\overline{\zeta}}\big|_{\rho_{\varepsilon}(\zeta)})=(\tfrac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\zeta^{2}}\Omega_{\zeta})\otimes(-\tfrac{i}{2}\tfrac{\zeta^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\tfrac{\partial}{\partial\zeta})=-\Psi.

Hence,
\Psi
glues to a global holomorphic section of
\mathchoice{{\textstyle\bigwedge}}{{\bigwedge}}{{\textstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}^{2}(\ker d\pi)^{*}\otimes T\mathbb{CP}^{1}\to\mathcal{Z}
such that
\mathcal{T}^{*}\bar{\Psi}=-\Psi
.

The final statements about holomorphic sections of
\mathcal{Z}
follow directly from the definition of
\mathcal{Z}
.
∎

One way to think of the twistor correspondence is that we start with a deformation of holomorphic symplectic structures
\Omega_{\zeta}
on a holomorphic symplectic manifold
(M,\Omega_{0})
, which is not necessarily quadratic in
\zeta
.
If the deformation space
Z=M\times\mathbb{C}
has a real structure
\tau
enabling us to glue it to a twistor space as in Proposition
2.6
, then there is a family of diffeomorphisms
f_{\zeta}\coloneqq\mathrm{ev}_{\zeta}\circ\mathrm{ev}_{0}^{-1}:M\to M
with the very special property that
f_{\zeta}^{*}\Omega_{\zeta}=\Omega_{0}+2i\zeta\omega_{I}+\zeta^{2}\bar{\Omega}_{0}
for some Kähler form
\omega_{I}
whose metric is hyperkähler.
In some situations, such as Hitchin’s construction of the Gibbons–Hawking gravitational multi-instantons
[
23
]
, this family of diffeomorphisms
f_{\zeta}
can be computed explicitly by solving algebraic equations (i.e. finding all real twistor lines), which then produces an explicit description of the hyperkähler metric.

3.
Hyperkähler metrics near Lagrangian submanifolds

The goal of this section is prove Theorem
A
.

Let
(M,\Omega_{0})
be a holomorphic symplectic manifold,
X\subseteq M
a complex Lagrangian submanifold, and
\iota:X\hookrightarrow M
the inclusion map.
Let
\Omega_{\zeta}
be a real-analytic family of holomorphic symplectic forms on
M
depending holomorphically on
\zeta
in a disc
D_{r}=\{\zeta\in\mathbb{C}:|\zeta|<r\}
for some
r>0
, where the complex structure determined by
\Omega_{\zeta}
may also be varying.
More precisely, in holomorphic coordinates
(z_{k}=x_{2k-1}+ix_{2k})_{k=1}^{m}
on
M
(in the complex structure determined by
\Omega_{0}
), we have
\Omega_{\zeta}=\Omega_{ij}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{2m},\zeta)dx^{i}\wedge dx^{j}
, where
\Omega_{ij}
are real-analytic complex-valued functions which are holomorphic in
\zeta
. Suppose also that there is a pseudo-Kähler form
\omega
on
X
such that
\iota^{*}\Omega_{\zeta}=2i\zeta\omega
for all
\zeta\in D_{r}
.

We will construct a pseudo-hyperkähler structure
(\omega_{I},\omega_{J},\omega_{K})
on a neighbourhood of
X
in
M
such that
\Omega_{0}=\omega_{J}+i\omega_{K}
and
\iota^{*}\omega_{I}=\omega
, and which is positive definite if
\omega
is.
It will be obtained by constructing the twistor space as in Proposition
2.6
.

Let
Z=M\times D_{r}
with the almost complex structure
(I_{\zeta},i)
at
(x,\zeta)\in M\times D_{r}
, where
I_{\zeta}
is the complex structure corresponding to
\Omega_{\zeta}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

The almost complex structure on
Z=M\times D_{r}
is integrable.

Proof.

We show that
T^{0,1}Z
is involutive, which implies the result by the Newlander–Nirenberg theorem
[
43
]
.
By viewing
\Omega
as a
2
-form on
Z
, sections of
T^{0,1}Z
are spanned by sections of the form
X:Z\to T_{\mathbb{C}}M
such that
i_{X}\Omega=0
and by
\partial_{\bar{\zeta}}\coloneqq\tfrac{\partial}{\partial\overline{\zeta}}
.
Note that, as a form on
Z
,
\Omega
is not closed, but

d\Omega=\tfrac{\partial\Omega_{ij}}{\partial\zeta}dx^{i}\wedge dx^{j}\wedge d\zeta=(\mathcal{L}_{\partial_{\zeta}}\Omega)\wedge d\zeta,

(3.1)

where we used that
\Omega
depends holomorphically on
\zeta
.
In particular, if
X,Y:Z\to T_{\mathbb{C}}M
are such that
i_{X}\Omega=i_{Y}\Omega=0
, then
i_{X}d\Omega=(i_{X}\mathcal{L}_{\partial_{\zeta}}\Omega)\wedge d\zeta=(i_{[X,\partial_{\zeta}]}\Omega)\wedge d\zeta
so
i_{X}i_{Y}d\Omega=(i_{[X,\partial_{\zeta}]}i_{Y}\Omega)\wedge d\zeta=0
.
Hence,
i_{[X,Y]}\Omega=(\mathcal{L}_{X}i_{Y}-i_{Y}\mathcal{L}_{X})\Omega=i_{X}i_{Y}d\Omega=0
, so
[X,Y]
is a section of
T^{0,1}Z
.
Moreover,
i_{[X,\partial_{\bar{\zeta}}]}\Omega=(\mathcal{L}_{X}i_{\partial_{\bar{\zeta}}}-i_{\partial_{\bar{\zeta}}}\mathcal{L}_{X})\Omega=i_{X}i_{\partial_{\bar{\zeta}}}d\Omega=0
by (
3.1
), so
T^{0,1}Z
is involutive.
∎

We then have a complex manifold
Z
together with a surjective holomorphic submersion
\pi:Z\to D_{r}
.
Moreover, the family of holomorphic symplectic forms
\Omega_{\zeta}
can be viewed as a holomorphic section of
\mathchoice{{\textstyle\bigwedge}}{{\bigwedge}}{{\textstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}^{2}(\ker d\pi)^{*}
.
It remains to find the real structure
\tau
as in Proposition
2.6
(c).

Recall that a
totally real submanifold
of a complex manifold
(N,I)
is a smooth submanifold
Y\subseteq N
such that
TY\cap ITY=0
.
If
Y
is further real-analytic and
\dim_{\mathbb{R}}Y=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}N
, then the inclusion
Y\subseteq N
is locally isomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{n}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n}
, so real-analytic functions on
Y
can be analytically continued to holomorphic functions on a neighbourhood of
Y
.
In particular, a real-analytic diffeomorphism
Y\to Y
can be extended to a biholomorphism on a neighbourhood of
Y
; see e.g.
[
14
, Lemma 5.40]
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
(N,I,\Omega)
be a holomorphic-symplectic manifold
and let
\iota:Y\hookrightarrow N
be a smooth submanifold
such that
\iota^{*}\Omega
is non-degenerate (as a section of
\mathchoice{{\textstyle\bigwedge}}{{\bigwedge}}{{\textstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}^{2}T^{*}Y\otimes\mathbb{C}
). Then,
Y
is
totally real in
N
.

Proof.

Write
\Omega=\omega+i\eta
, where
\omega
and
\eta
are the real and imaginary parts of
\Omega
, so that
I=\eta^{-1}\omega
.
Let
u\in TY
be such that
v\coloneqq Iu\in TY
.
Then,
\omega(u)=\eta(v)
.
Also,
Iv=-u
so
\omega(v)=-\eta(u)
.
Hence,
\Omega(u+iv)=\Omega(u)+i\Omega(v)=\omega(u)+i\eta(u)+i(\omega(v)+i\eta(v))=(\omega(u)-\eta(v))+i(\eta(u)+\omega(v))=0
.
So we have
u+iv\in TY\otimes\mathbb{C}
and
(\iota^{*}\Omega)(u+iv)=0
.
Since
\iota^{*}\Omega
is non-degenerate, we get
u+iv=0
.
∎

In particular,
\iota^{*}\Omega_{\zeta}=2i\zeta\omega
is non-degenerate for
\zeta\neq 0
, so
X
is totally real in
M
with respect to
I_{\zeta}
, and is also real-analytic by the real-analyticity of
\Omega_{\zeta}
.

Similarly, for all
0<\varepsilon<r
, the circle
S^{1}_{\varepsilon}
of radius
\varepsilon
is totally real in
\mathbb{C}
, so
X\times S^{1}_{\varepsilon}
is totally real and real-analytic in
Z
.

Now, consider the map

\tau:X\times S^{1}_{\varepsilon}\longrightarrow X\times S^{1}_{\varepsilon},\quad\tau(x,\zeta)=(x,\rho_{\varepsilon}(\zeta)),

where
\rho_{\varepsilon}(\zeta)=-\varepsilon^{2}/\bar{\zeta}
, as defined in (
2.1
).
Then,
\tau
is a real-analytic involution on a totally real and real-analytic submanifold, so it has a unique extension to an anti-holomorphic involution
\tau:U\to U
covering
\rho_{\varepsilon}
, for some neighbourhood
U
of
X\times S^{1}_{\varepsilon}
in
Z
.
By shrinking
U
, if necessary, we may assume that
\tau
extends continuously to the closure
\bar{U}\subseteq Z
.
In components, we have

\tau(x,\zeta)=(\tau_{\zeta}(x),\rho_{\varepsilon}(\zeta)),\quad\text{for all }(x,\zeta)\in U,

where
\tau_{\zeta}
is a family of diffeomorphisms on neighbourhoods of
X
in
M
.

Lemma 3.3
.

We have
\tau^{*}\bar{\Omega}=\varepsilon^{2}\Omega/\pi^{2}
, where
\pi:Z\to\mathbb{C}
is the projection map.

Proof.

We need to show that
\tau_{\zeta}^{*}\bar{\Omega}_{\rho_{\varepsilon}(\zeta)}=\varepsilon^{2}\Omega_{\zeta}/\zeta^{2}
for all
\zeta\in\pi(U)
.
By definition,
\tau_{\zeta}
is holomorphic from
I_{\zeta}
to
-I_{\rho_{\varepsilon}(\zeta)}
, so
\tau_{\zeta}^{*}\bar{\Omega}_{\rho_{\varepsilon}(\zeta)}
is holomorphic with respect to
I_{\zeta}
.
Note that the right-hand side
\varepsilon^{2}\Omega_{\zeta}/\zeta^{2}
is also
I_{\zeta}
-holomorphic by definition.
Since
X
is totally real and real-analytic with respect to
I_{\zeta}
, it suffices to check the equality after pulling back by
\iota:X\hookrightarrow M
.
But
\tau_{\zeta}\circ\iota=\iota
and
\omega
is real, so

\iota^{*}(\tau_{\zeta}^{*}\bar{\Omega}_{\rho_{\varepsilon}(\zeta)})=\iota^{*}\bar{\Omega}_{\rho_{\varepsilon}(\zeta)}=\overline{2i\rho_{\varepsilon}(\zeta)\omega}=2i\tfrac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\zeta}\omega=\iota^{*}(\varepsilon^{2}\Omega_{\zeta}/\zeta^{2}),

and hence
\tau_{\zeta}^{*}\bar{\Omega}_{\rho_{\varepsilon}(\zeta)}=\varepsilon^{2}\Omega_{\zeta}/\zeta^{2}
.
∎

By Proposition
2.6
, we have a twistor space
\mathcal{Z}
whose zero fibre is
M
, and, by Corollary
2.3
, it suffices to show that every
x\in X
is contained in a real twistor line
s_{x}
.
Let
x\in X
, let
\delta>0
be small enough so that
\{x\}\times A_{\varepsilon,\delta}\subseteq U
(where
A_{\varepsilon,\delta}
is the annulus defined in §
2.2
), and let

s_{x}:D_{\varepsilon+\delta}\longrightarrow U\cup\pi^{-1}(D_{\varepsilon}),\quad s_{x}(\zeta)=(x,\zeta).

We have
s_{x}(\zeta)=\tau(s_{x}(\rho_{\varepsilon}(\zeta)))
for all
\zeta\in S^{1}_{\varepsilon}
, and since both sides are holomorphic and
S^{1}_{\varepsilon}
is totally real, this holds for all
\zeta
.
Hence,
s_{x}
defines a real holomorphic section of
\mathcal{Z}
by letting
s_{\pm}=s_{x}
as in Proposition
2.6
.

Lemma 3.4
.

The section
s_{x}
is a real twistor line, i.e. it has normal bundle
N\cong\mathcal{O}(1)^{\oplus 2n}
, where
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}M=2n
.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.4
,
N
has degree
2n
, so we can use the criterion in Lemma
2.5
.
Let
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
be a basis of
T_{x}X
and define sections
\alpha_{i}^{\pm}:D_{\varepsilon+\delta}\to\ker d\pi
for
i=1,\ldots,n
, by

\alpha_{i}^{+}(\zeta)=I_{0}v_{i}+I_{\zeta}v_{i},\quad\text{and}\quad\alpha_{i}^{-}(\zeta)=I_{0}v_{i}-I_{\zeta}v_{i}.

The sections
\alpha_{i}^{\pm}
are holomorphic since
\zeta\mapsto(v_{i},0)\in T_{x}M\times T_{\zeta}\mathbb{C}
is holomorphic as map to
TZ
and hence so is
\zeta\mapsto I(v_{i},0)=(I_{\zeta}v_{i},0)
, where
I
is the complex structure on
Z
.

Since
X
is a complex submanifold and
\tau_{\zeta}|_{X}=\mathrm{id}_{X}
, we have
d\tau_{\zeta}(I_{0}v_{i})=I_{0}v_{i}
.
Moreover,
d\tau_{\zeta}\circ I_{\zeta}=-I_{\rho_{\varepsilon}(\zeta)}\circ d\tau_{\zeta}
by the definition of
\tau
, so we get
d\tau_{\zeta}(\alpha_{i}^{+}(\zeta))=d\tau_{\zeta}(I_{0}v_{i}+I_{\zeta}v_{i})=I_{0}v_{i}-I_{\rho_{\varepsilon}(\zeta)}v_{i}=\alpha_{i}^{-}(\rho_{\varepsilon}(\zeta))
.
Similarly,
d\tau_{\zeta}(\alpha_{i}^{-}(\zeta))=\alpha_{i}^{+}(\rho_{\varepsilon}(\zeta))
.
Hence, both
\alpha_{i}^{+}
and
\alpha_{i}^{-}
extend to a global section of
N=s_{x}^{*}\ker d\pi
, so we have
2n
sections
\alpha_{1}^{\pm},\ldots,\alpha_{n}^{\pm}
.
Moreover, since for all
\zeta\neq 0
,
X
is totally real with respect to
I_{\zeta}
, i.e.
TX\cap I_{\zeta}TX=0
, the sections are linearly independent at all points
\zeta\in\mathbb{CP}^{1}\setminus\{0,\infty\}
.
Also,
\alpha_{i}^{+}(\infty)=\alpha_{i}^{-}(0)=0
, so
N\cong\mathcal{O}(1)^{\oplus 2n}
by Lemma
2.5
.
∎

By Corollary
2.3
, there is a pseudo-hyperkähler structure
(\omega_{I},\omega_{J},\omega_{K})
on a neighbourhood of
X
in
M
such that

\mathrm{ev}_{\zeta}^{*}\Omega_{\zeta}=\mathrm{ev}_{0}^{*}((\omega_{J}+i\omega_{K})+2i\zeta\omega_{I}+\zeta^{2}(\omega_{J}-i\omega_{K})),

(3.2)

for all
\zeta\in D_{r}
, where
\mathrm{ev}_{\zeta}:\mathcal{M}\to M
is the evaluation map on the space
\mathcal{M}
of real twistor lines, after identifying
M
with
Z_{\zeta}=M\times\{\zeta\}
.

Lemma 3.5
.

We have
\Omega_{0}=\omega_{J}+i\omega_{K}
and
\iota^{*}\omega_{I}=\omega
.

Proof.

The first part follows from evaluating (
3.2
) at
\zeta=0
.
Now, let
s:X\to\mathcal{M}
be the map
x\mapsto s_{x}
.
By the definition of
s_{x}
, we have
\mathrm{ev}_{\zeta}\circ s=\iota
for all
\zeta
, so

\displaystyle 2i\zeta\omega

\displaystyle=\iota^{*}\Omega_{\zeta}=s^{*}\mathrm{ev}_{\zeta}^{*}\Omega_{\zeta}

\displaystyle=s^{*}\mathrm{ev}_{0}^{*}((\omega_{J}+i\omega_{K})+2i\zeta\omega_{I}+\zeta^{2}(\omega_{J}-i\omega_{K}))

\displaystyle=\iota^{*}((\omega_{J}+i\omega_{K})+2i\zeta\omega_{I}+\zeta^{2}(\omega_{J}-i\omega_{K}))

\displaystyle=2i\zeta\iota^{*}\omega_{I},

since
X
is Lagrangian with respect to
\Omega_{0}=\omega_{J}+i\omega_{K}
.
Hence,
\omega=\iota^{*}\omega_{I}
.
∎

Lemma 3.6
.

If the pseudo-Kähler form
\omega
has signature
(p,q)
then the pseudo-hyperkähler metric has signature
(2p,2q)
.

Proof.

The signature of a non-degenerate symmetric tensor is locally constant, so it suffices to check that the pseudo-hyperkähler metric
g
has signature
(2p,2q)
at a point
x\in X
.
Since for
\zeta\neq 0
,
X
is totally real with respect to
I_{\zeta}
, we have
T_{x}M=T_{x}X\oplus I_{\zeta}T_{x}X
.
Moreover,
g(I_{\zeta}\cdot,I_{\zeta}\cdot)=g(\cdot,\cdot)
, so it suffices to show that
\iota^{*}g
has signature
(p,q)
.
But
\iota^{*}\omega_{I}=\omega
, so
\iota^{*}g
is the pseudo-Kähler metric of
\omega
.
∎

In particular, if
\omega
is a Kähler form, then
g
is positive definite, so this concludes the proof of Theorem
A
.

4.
Hyperkähler metrics on symplectic realizations

4.1.
The general case

The goal of this section is to discuss the existence of hyperkähler structures on symplectic realizations (in the sense of Definition
1.6
), and prove Theorem
B
.

Recall (see e.g.
[
31
]
) that a deformation of complex structures on a (not necessarily compact) complex manifold
X
can be obtained by solving the Maurer–Cartan equation

\bar{\partial}\phi+\tfrac{1}{2}[\phi,\phi]=0

(4.1)

for vector-valued
1
-forms
\phi\in\Omega_{X}^{0,1}(T^{1,0}X)
.
More precisely, we seek a family
\phi_{\zeta}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\phi_{n}\zeta^{n}
of solutions to (
4.1
) depending holomorphically on
\zeta
in a neighbourhood of
0
in
\mathbb{C}
.
If
1-\phi_{\zeta}\bar{\phi}_{\zeta}:T_{\mathbb{C}}X\to T_{\mathbb{C}}X
is invertible (which always holds for
\zeta
small enough if
X
is compact since
\phi_{0}=0
),
we get a new integrable almost complex structure
I_{\zeta}
by defining the
(0,1)
-part to be
T^{0,1}_{\zeta}\coloneqq(1+\phi_{\zeta})(T^{0,1}X)
.

Hitchin’s main theorem in
[
25
]
is that the presence of a holomorphic Poisson structure
\sigma
on
X
gives solutions to the Maurer–Cartan equation for each closed
(1,1)
-form.
More precisely, following Gualtieri’s review
[
22
, §5.2]
, Hitchin’s theorem can be decomposed into three parts.
The first part is that if
\omega\in\Omega_{X}^{1,1}
is a solution to

d\omega+\tfrac{1}{2}\partial i_{\sigma}(\omega\wedge\omega)=0,

(4.2)

then, the contraction
\phi\coloneqq-\sigma\omega
is a solution to the Maurer–Cartan equation (
4.1
) (note that
[
22
, Eq. 51]
reduces to (
4.2
) for a
(1,1)
-form
\omega
by
[
22
, Eq. 53]
).
The second part is that (
4.2
) admits plenty of solutions.
Namely, if
X
is compact and
H^{2}(X,\mathbb{C})\to H^{2}(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}
) is surjective (e.g.
X
is Kähler), then, for any closed
(1,1)
-form
\omega_{1}
, there exist
\omega_{n}\in\Omega_{X}^{1,1}
,
n\geq 2
, such that
\omega(\zeta)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\omega_{n}\zeta^{n}
converges to a family of solutions to (
4.2
) for
\zeta
in a neighbourhood of
0
in
\mathbb{C}
.
Hence,
\phi_{\zeta}\coloneqq-\sigma\omega(\zeta)
defines a deformation of complex structures
I_{\zeta}
.
Finally, the third part is that
\sigma
is deformed to a family of holomorphic Poisson structures
\sigma_{\zeta}
on
(X,I_{\zeta})
.

Now, we wish to lift those deformations to a symplectic realization

s:(M,\Omega_{0})\longrightarrow(X,\sigma),\quad\iota:X\lhook\joinrel\longrightarrow M

in the sense of Definition
1.6
.
Recall
[
15
, III Theorem 1.2]
that
M
has the structure of a symplectic local groupoid such that
s
is the source map, possibly after restricting to a smaller neighbourhood of
X
and
M
.
In particular, there is a
target map
, i.e. an anti-Poisson holomorphic submersion
t:M\to X
such that
\ker ds
and
\ker dt
are symplectically orthogonal and
t\circ\iota=\mathrm{id}_{X}
.
This is proved, for example, in
[
15
, III Lemme 1]
in the
C^{\infty}
case, but the same argument works in the holomorphic setting.

The maps
s
and
t
can be used to produce a two-parameter family of holomorphic symplectic structures on
M
, as the following proposition shows (the author thanks Marco Gualtieri for explaining the main idea of this proposition).

Proposition 4.1

(cf.
[
3
, Proposition 6.3]
)
.

Let
(X,\sigma)
be a (not necessarily compact) holomorphic Poisson manifold, let
\omega(\zeta)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\omega_{n}\zeta^{n}
be a family of solutions to (
4.2
) such that
1-\phi_{\zeta}\bar{\phi}_{\zeta}
is invertible for all
\zeta
in a neighbourhood
U
of
0
in
\mathbb{C}
, where
\phi_{\zeta}\coloneqq-\sigma\omega(\zeta)
, and let

\beta(\zeta)\coloneqq\omega(\zeta)+\tfrac{1}{2}i_{\sigma}(\omega(\zeta)\wedge\omega(\zeta)).

Let
s:(M,\Omega_{0})\to(X,\sigma)
be a symplectic realization with target map
t
.
Then, for all
\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2}\in U
,

\Omega_{\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2}}\coloneqq\Omega_{0}+s^{*}\beta(\zeta_{1})-t^{*}\beta(\zeta_{2})

is a holomorphic symplectic form on
M
with respect to some complex structure
I_{\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2}}
.

Remark 4.2
.

One can show that
s
is a holomorphic Poisson submersion from
\Omega_{\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2}}
to the Hitchin deformation
\sigma_{\zeta_{1}}
, and
t
a holomorphic anti-Poisson submersion from
\Omega_{\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2}}
to
\sigma_{\zeta_{2}}
.
In other words, we get a
dual pair
in the sense of
[
47
, §8]
:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(M,\Omega_{\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2}})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(X,\sigma_{\zeta_{1}})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(X,-\sigma_{\zeta_{2}})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces s}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces t}

Remark 4.3
.

The complex
2
-form
\beta(\zeta)
is a
B
-field transforming the complex Dirac structure
L_{\sigma}
of
\sigma
to that of
\sigma_{\zeta}
by gauge transformation:
e^{\beta(\zeta)}L_{\sigma}=L_{\sigma_{\zeta}}
(see e.g.
[
22
, §3]
).
Indeed,
\beta(\zeta)
is closed (as proved below) and
(1+\beta(\zeta)\sigma)^{-1}\beta(\zeta)=(1-\beta(\zeta)\sigma)\beta(\zeta)=\omega(\zeta)
, so this follows from
[
22
, Theorem 5.3]
.

Proof.

Recall (cf.
[
10
, §4]
) that a complex
2
-form
\Omega
on a smooth manifold
M
is a holomorphic symplectic form with respect to some (unique) integrable almost complex structure
I
if and only if

(1)

d\Omega=0
,

(2)

\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\ker\Omega=\tfrac{1}{2}\dim_{\mathbb{R}}M
, and

(3)

\ker\Omega\cap\overline{\ker\Omega}=0
,

where
\ker\Omega\subseteq T_{\mathbb{C}}M
.
Indeed, if we define
T^{0,1}\coloneqq\ker\Omega
and
T^{1,0}\coloneqq\overline{T^{0,1}}
, then (2) and (3) imply that
T_{\mathbb{C}}M=T^{1,0}\oplus T^{0,1}
.
Moreover, the closedness of
\Omega
implies that
[T^{0,1},T^{0,1}]\subseteq T^{0,1}
since for
X,Y\in\Gamma(T^{0,1})
we have
i_{[X,Y]}\Omega=(\mathcal{L}_{X}i_{Y}-i_{Y}\mathcal{L}_{X})\Omega=-i_{Y}(di_{X}\Omega+i_{X}d\Omega)=0
.
Hence,
T^{0,1}
defines an integrable almost complex structure
I
by the Newlander–Nirenberg theorem
[
43
]
.
Moreover,
\Omega
is a
(2,0)
-form by the definition of
T^{0,1}
, and
d\Omega=\partial\Omega+\bar{\partial}\Omega=0
implies that
\bar{\partial}\Omega=0
, so
\Omega
is holomorphic.
Also,
\ker\Omega\cap T^{1,0}=0
, so
\Omega
is non-degenerate.

We will show that
\Omega\coloneqq\Omega_{\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2}}
satisfies these three properties.

To show (1), it suffices to show that
\beta
is closed (where we omit the dependence on
\zeta
).
Note that
d\beta=d\omega+\tfrac{1}{2}\partial i_{\sigma}(\omega\wedge\omega)+i_{\sigma}(\omega\wedge\bar{\partial}\omega)
and, by (
4.2
), this simplifies to
d\beta=-\tfrac{1}{2}i_{\sigma}(\omega\wedge\partial i_{\sigma}(\omega\wedge\omega))
, which is zero by the integrability of
\sigma
.
Indeed,
\bar{\partial}\omega=0
, so we can write in coordinates
\omega=\bar{\partial}\gamma
, where
\gamma=\gamma_{i}d\bar{z}^{i}
.
Then,
\partial i_{\sigma}(\omega\wedge\omega)=\{\gamma_{i},\gamma_{j}\}d\bar{z}^{i}\wedge d\bar{z}^{j}
and
d\beta=-\tfrac{1}{2}\{\gamma_{i},\{\gamma_{j},\gamma_{k}\}\}d\bar{z}^{i}\wedge d\bar{z}^{j}\wedge d\bar{z}^{k}=0
by the Jacobi identity.

To show (2) and (3), we first introduce some notation.
We will abbreviate
\omega(\zeta_{i})
by
\omega_{i}
and let
\eta=s^{*}\omega_{1}-t^{*}\omega_{2}\in\Omega_{M}^{1,1}
.
Also, let
\tau=\Omega_{0}^{-1}
be the Poisson structure inverse to
\Omega_{0}
, and let
\psi=-\tau\circ\eta:T_{\mathbb{C}}M\to T_{\mathbb{C}}M
.
We claim that

\ker\Omega=(1+\psi)(T^{0,1}M),

(4.3)

which implies (2) (and so
\psi
is a Maurer–Cartan element).
First, we show that
\Omega=\Omega_{0}+\eta+\eta\tau\eta
, as a map
T_{\mathbb{C}}M\to T^{*}_{\mathbb{C}}M
.
Since
\ker ds
and
\ker dt
are symplectically orthogonal with respect to
\Omega_{0}
, we have
s_{*}\circ\tau\circ t^{*}=t_{*}\circ\tau\circ s^{*}=0
.
Also, since
s
is Poisson,
s_{*}\circ\tau\circ s^{*}=\sigma
and since
t
is anti-Poisson,
t_{*}\circ\tau\circ t^{*}=-\sigma
.
Then,

\displaystyle\eta\tau\eta

\displaystyle=(s^{*}\omega_{1}s_{*}-t^{*}\omega_{2}t_{*})\tau(s^{*}\omega_{1}s_{*}-t^{*}\omega_{2}t_{*})

\displaystyle=s^{*}\omega_{1}\sigma\omega_{1}s_{*}-t^{*}\omega_{2}\sigma\omega_{2}t_{*}

\displaystyle=s^{*}\tfrac{1}{2}i_{\sigma}(\omega_{1}\wedge\omega_{1})-t^{*}\tfrac{1}{2}i_{\sigma}(\omega_{2}\wedge\omega_{2}),

so
\Omega=\Omega_{0}+\eta+\eta\tau\eta
.
Hence, if
v\in T^{0,1}M
, then

\Omega(1+\psi)(v)=(\Omega_{0}+\eta+\eta\tau\eta)(1-\tau\eta)(v)=\Omega_{0}(v)-\eta\tau\eta\tau\eta(v)=0

since
\tau\eta\tau\eta=0
for a
(1,1)
-form
\eta
.
So
(1+\psi)(T^{1,0}M)\subseteq\ker\Omega
.
Conversely, let
u\in T_{\mathbb{C}}M
be such that
\Omega(u)=0
and write
u=v+w
where
v\in T^{1,0}M
and
w\in T^{0,1}M
.
Then one has
\Omega_{0}(u)+\eta(u)+\eta\tau\eta(u)=0
and the
(1,0)
-part is
\Omega_{0}(v)+\eta(w)=0
, so
v=-\tau\eta(w)
and hence
u=-\tau\eta(w)+w=(1+\psi)(w)
.
This establishes (
4.3
), and hence (2) holds.

For (3), we first claim that
s_{*}(1-\psi\bar{\psi})=(1-\phi_{1}\bar{\phi}_{1})s_{*}
, where
\phi_{i}\coloneqq\phi_{\zeta_{i}}
.
Indeed,

\displaystyle s_{*}(1-\psi\bar{\psi})

\displaystyle=s_{*}-s_{*}\tau(s^{*}\omega_{1}s_{*}-t^{*}\omega_{2}t_{*})\bar{\tau}(s^{*}\bar{\omega}_{1}s_{*}-t^{*}\bar{\omega}_{2}t_{*})

\displaystyle=s_{*}-\sigma\omega_{1}s_{*}\bar{\tau}(s^{*}\bar{\omega}_{1}s_{*}-t^{*}\bar{\omega}_{2}t_{*})

\displaystyle=s_{*}-\sigma\omega_{1}\bar{\sigma}\bar{\omega}_{1}s_{*}

\displaystyle=(1-\phi_{1}\bar{\phi}_{1})s_{*}.

A similar argument shows that
t_{*}(1-\psi\bar{\psi})=(1-\phi_{2}\bar{\phi}_{2})t_{*}
.
Now, we want to show that if
v\in T^{1,0}M
,
w\in T^{0,1}M
, and
(1+\bar{\psi})(v)=(1+\psi)(w)
, then
v=w=0
.
The
(1,0)
- and
(0,1)
-parts are
v=-\bar{\tau}\bar{\eta}w
and
-\tau\eta v=w
, respectively.
Hence,
\tau\eta\bar{\tau}\bar{\eta}w=w
, so
(1-\psi\bar{\psi})(w)=0
.
Then,
s_{*}(1-\psi\bar{\psi})(w)=(1-\phi_{1}\bar{\phi}_{1})s_{*}(w)=0
and since
1-\phi_{1}\bar{\phi}_{1}
is invertible by assumption, we get
s_{*}(w)=0
.
Similarly,
t_{*}(w)=0
.
In particular,
\bar{\eta}(w)=0
, so
v=-\bar{\tau}\bar{\eta}w=0
and also
w=-\tau\eta v=0
.
∎

Hence, if
\omega(\zeta)
is as in Proposition
4.1
and is real-analytic, we have a two-parameter real-analytic deformation of holomorphic symplectic structures
\Omega_{\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2}}
on
M
and we wish to use it with Theorem
A
to construct a hyperkähler structure near
X
in
M
.
That is, we want to find a holomorphic map
\zeta\mapsto(f(\zeta),g(\zeta))\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
such that the one-parameter deformation
\Omega_{\zeta}\coloneqq\Omega_{f(\zeta),g(\zeta)}
satisfies
\iota^{*}\Omega_{\zeta}=2i\zeta\omega
for some Kähler form
\omega
on
X
.
But

\iota^{*}\Omega_{\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2}}=\iota^{*}\Omega_{0}+\iota^{*}s^{*}\beta(\zeta_{1})-\iota^{*}t^{*}\beta(\zeta_{2})=\beta(\zeta_{1})-\beta(\zeta_{2})

since
X
is Lagrangian and
\iota
is a bisection of
s
and
t
.
In particular, taking the anti-diagonal
(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2})=(i\zeta,-i\zeta)
, we get

\iota^{*}\Omega_{i\zeta,-i\zeta}=\beta(i\zeta)-\beta(-i\zeta)=2i(\zeta\beta_{1}-\zeta^{3}\beta_{3}+\zeta^{5}\beta_{5}-\cdots),

where
\beta(\zeta)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\beta_{n}\zeta^{n}
.
We have
\beta_{1}=\omega_{1}
, so if we start with a Kähler form
\omega_{1}
, we only have to kill the higher odd terms
\beta_{3},\beta_{5},\beta_{7},\ldots
.

By definition,

\beta_{n}=\omega_{n}+\sum_{i+j=n}\tfrac{1}{2}i_{\sigma}(\omega_{i}\wedge\omega_{j}),

so
\beta_{2n+1}=0
for all
n\geq 1
if and only if
\omega_{2n+1}=0
and
\sum_{i+j=2n+1}\tfrac{1}{2}i_{\sigma}(\omega_{i}\wedge\omega_{j})=i_{\sigma}(\omega_{1}\wedge\omega_{2n})=0
for all
n\geq 1
.
This concludes the proof of Theorem
B
.

4.2.
The zero Poisson structure

In this subsection, we illustrate Theorem
B
with the special case of the zero Poisson structure, and show that it recovers the Feix–Kaledin hyperkähler metric
[
19
,
27
,
28
]
.

Let
X
be a complex manifold together with a real-analytic Kähler form
\omega
.
Then, with respect to the zero Poisson structure
\sigma=0
, the bundle map
\pi:T^{*}X\to X
is a symplectic realization, where
T^{*}X
is endowed with its canonical holomorphic symplectic structure
\Omega_{0}
.
The identity section
\iota:X\to T^{*}X
is the zero section and the target map
t
is also
\pi
.

Now,
\zeta\omega
is trivially a family of solutions to (
4.2
), and
\phi_{\zeta}=-\sigma(\zeta\omega)=0
, so

\Omega_{\zeta}=\Omega_{0}+\pi^{*}i\zeta\omega-\pi^{*}(-i\zeta\omega)=\Omega_{0}+2i\zeta\pi^{*}\omega

is a holomorphic symplectic form on
T^{*}X
for all
\zeta\in\mathbb{C}
by Proposition
4.1
.

Since
\iota^{*}\Omega_{\zeta}=2i\zeta\omega
, we get a hyperkähler metric
(\omega_{I},\omega_{J},\omega_{K})
on a neighbourhood of the zero section in
T^{*}X
by Theorem
A
.

Proposition 4.4
.

The hyperkähler structure
(\omega_{I},\omega_{J},\omega_{K})
coincides with that of Feix–Kaledin
[
19
,
27
,
28
]
.

Remark 4.5
.

Theorem
A
grew as an attempt to understand and generalize Feix’s construction
[
19
]
, so it is not surprising that we recover it.
But the quickest way to prove this correspondence is to show that the metric is
S^{1}
-invariant and use Feix’s uniqueness result
[
20
, Corollary 1]
, as we now explain.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be the space of real twistor lines and let
\mathrm{ev}_{\zeta}:\mathcal{M}\to T^{*}X
be the evaluation maps.
In the notation of §
3
, we take
\varepsilon=1
and
r=\infty
, and let
\rho\coloneqq\rho_{\varepsilon}
.
We may then view elements of
\mathcal{M}
as holomorphic maps
s:\mathbb{C}\to T^{*}X
such that
\tau_{\zeta}(s(\zeta))=s(\rho(\zeta))
for all
\zeta
.
By restricting
\mathcal{M}
to an open subset, we may assume that
\mathrm{ev}_{0}:\mathcal{M}\to T^{*}X
is a diffeomorphism onto a neighbourhood of
X
.
Then, by (
3.2
),
f_{\zeta}\coloneqq\mathrm{ev}_{\zeta}\circ\mathrm{ev}_{0}^{-1}
is a family of diffeomorphisms on neighbourhoods of
X
in
T^{*}X
such that

\Omega_{0}+2i\zeta\omega_{I}+\zeta^{2}\bar{\Omega}_{0}=f_{\zeta}^{*}\Omega_{\zeta},\quad\text{ for all }\zeta\in\mathbb{C},

(4.4)

where
\Omega_{\zeta}\coloneqq\Omega_{0}+2i\zeta\pi^{*}\omega
.
Let
\psi_{\lambda}:T^{*}X\to T^{*}X
be scalar multiplication by
\lambda\in S^{1}
.

We claim that

f_{\zeta}\circ\psi_{\lambda}=\psi_{\lambda}\circ f_{\lambda^{-1}\zeta},\quad\text{for all $\zeta\in\mathbb{C}^{*}$ and $\lambda\in S^{1}$}.

(4.5)

Indeed, we have

\psi_{\lambda}^{*}\Omega_{\zeta}=\psi_{\lambda}^{*}(\Omega_{0}+2i\zeta\pi^{*}\omega)=\lambda\Omega_{0}+2i\zeta\pi^{*}\omega=\lambda(\Omega_{0}+2i\lambda^{-1}\zeta\pi^{*}\omega)=\lambda\Omega_{\lambda^{-1}\zeta},

(4.6)

so
\psi_{\lambda}
is holomorphic from
I_{\lambda^{-1}\zeta}
to
I_{\zeta}
.
Hence,

\tau_{\zeta}\circ\psi_{\lambda}=\psi_{\lambda}\circ\tau_{\lambda\zeta},

(4.7)

since both sides are holomorphic from
I_{\lambda\zeta}
to
-I_{\rho(\zeta)}
and restrict to the identity map on the totally real and real-analytic submanifold
X
.
Now, define an action of
S^{1}
on
\mathcal{M}
by
(\lambda\cdot s)(\zeta)=\lambda s(\lambda^{-1}\zeta)
. Then, by (
4.7
),

\tau_{\zeta}((\lambda\cdot s)(\zeta))=\lambda\tau_{\lambda\zeta}(s(\lambda^{-1}\zeta))=\lambda s(\rho(\lambda^{-1}\zeta))=\lambda s(\lambda^{-1}\rho(\zeta))=(\lambda\cdot s)(\rho(\zeta)),

so
\lambda\cdot s\in\mathcal{M}
.
By definition,
\mathrm{ev}_{\zeta}(\lambda\cdot s)=\lambda\mathrm{ev}_{\lambda^{-1}\zeta}(s)
.
In particular,
\mathrm{ev}_{0}(\lambda\cdot s)=\lambda\mathrm{ev}_{0}(s)
so
\mathrm{ev}_{0}^{-1}(\lambda x)=\lambda\mathrm{ev}_{0}^{-1}(x)
for all
x\in T^{*}X
.
Then,
f_{\lambda\zeta}(\lambda x)=\mathrm{ev}_{\lambda\zeta}(\mathrm{ev}_{0}^{-1}(\lambda x))=\mathrm{ev}_{\lambda\zeta}(\lambda\mathrm{ev}_{0}^{-1}(x))=\lambda f_{\zeta}(x)
, which proves (
4.5
).

By applying
\psi_{\lambda}^{*}
to (
4.4
) and using (
4.6
), we get

\displaystyle\psi_{\lambda}^{*}\Omega_{0}+2i\zeta\psi_{\lambda}^{*}\omega_{I}+\zeta^{2}\psi_{\lambda}^{*}\bar{\Omega}_{0}

\displaystyle=f_{\lambda^{-1}\zeta}^{*}\psi_{\lambda}^{*}\Omega_{\zeta}

\displaystyle=f_{\lambda^{-1}\zeta}^{*}\lambda\Omega_{\lambda^{-1}\zeta}

\displaystyle=\lambda\Omega_{0}+2i\zeta\omega_{I}+\lambda^{-1}\zeta^{2}\bar{\Omega}_{0}.

Comparing the linear terms, we get
\psi_{\lambda}^{*}\omega_{I}=\omega_{I}
for all
\lambda\in S^{1}
.
Since
I
is also preserved by
\psi_{\lambda}
, the hyperkähler metric
g(\cdot,\cdot)=\omega_{I}(I\cdot,\cdot)
is
S^{1}
-invariant.
Also,
\iota^{*}\omega_{I}=\omega
, so the restriction of
g
to
X
is the Kähler metric of
\omega
.
Hence, the result follows from Feix’s uniqueness result
[
20
, Corollary 1]
.
∎

4.3.
Poisson surfaces

We now show that in the two-dimensional case, the equations in Theorem
B
can always be solved, thereby obtaining Theorem
C
.

Let
(X,\sigma)
be a compact holomorphic Poisson manifold of complex dimension two, and
\omega_{1}\in\Omega_{X}^{1,1}
a real-analytic Kähler form.
Use
\omega_{1}
to define
\bar{\partial}^{*}
and the Laplacian
\Delta
on differential forms.
Let
G
be the Green operator for
\Delta
(see e.g.
[
49
, §IV.5]
).

Lemma 4.6
.

For all
\gamma\in\Omega^{0,2}_{X}
, there exists
\omega\in\Omega^{1,1}_{X}
such that

\bar{\partial}\omega+\partial\gamma=0,\quad\bar{\partial}^{*}\omega=0,\quad\text{and}\quad\omega\wedge\omega_{1}=0.

In fact, we can take
\omega=\partial\bar{\partial}^{*}G\gamma
.

Proof.

We have
\bar{\partial}\gamma\in\Omega_{X}^{0,3}=0
, so
\bar{\partial}\partial\bar{\partial}^{*}G\gamma=-\partial\bar{\partial}\bar{\partial}^{*}G\gamma=-\partial\gamma
.
Hence,
\omega=\partial\bar{\partial}^{*}G\gamma
is a solution to the first equation.
Since
\bar{\partial}^{*}\partial=-\partial\bar{\partial}^{*}
on a Kähler manifold, we have
\bar{\partial}^{*}\omega=0
.
Moreover, by the Kähler identity
[\bar{\partial}^{*},L]=i\partial
, where
L(\cdot)=\cdot\wedge\omega_{1}
is the Lefschetz operator, we have
L\omega=\partial\bar{\partial}^{*}LG\gamma=0
, since
LG\gamma\in\Omega^{1,3}_{X}=0
.
∎

Now, define
\omega_{n}
recursively by

\omega_{n}\coloneqq\partial\bar{\partial}^{*}G\sum_{i+j=n}\tfrac{1}{2}i_{\sigma}(\omega_{i}\wedge\omega_{j}),

for all
n\geq 2
.
By Lemma
4.6
,

\bar{\partial}\omega_{n}+\sum_{i+j=n}\tfrac{1}{2}\partial i_{\sigma}(\omega_{i}\wedge\omega_{j})=0,\quad\bar{\partial}^{*}\omega_{n}=0,\quad\text{and}\quad\omega_{n}\wedge\omega_{1}=0.

(4.8)

Moreover,
\omega_{3}=\partial\bar{\partial}^{*}G(\omega_{2}\wedge\omega_{1})=0
, since
\omega_{2}\wedge\omega_{1}=0
, and, by induction,

\omega_{2n+1}=\partial\bar{\partial}^{*}G(\omega_{2n}\wedge\omega_{1})=0,

for all
n\geq 1
.
Hence, to apply Theorem
B
, it only remains to show that

\omega(\zeta)\coloneqq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\omega_{n}\zeta^{n}

converges to a real-analytic family for
\zeta
in a neighbourhood of
0
in
\mathbb{C}
.
Then, (
4.8
) implies that
\omega(\zeta)
is a family of solutions to
d\omega+\tfrac{1}{2}\partial i_{\sigma}(\omega\wedge\omega)=0
with the properties of Theorem
B
.
The converge of
\omega(\zeta)
is implicit in
[
25
]
, but, for completeness, we explain the argument here.
The proof is standard, using elliptic estimates as in
[
31
, §5.3]
.

For
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 2}
and
0<\alpha<1
, fix a Hölder norm
|\cdot|_{k,\alpha}
on differential forms.
Then, the Green operator
G
satisfies
|G\psi|_{k,\alpha}=c_{1}|\psi|_{k-2,\alpha}
for some
c_{1}>0
(see e.g.
[
31
, Appendix, Theorem 7.4]
).
Hence, we have

|\omega_{n}|_{k,\alpha}\leq c\sum_{i+j=n}|\omega_{i}|_{k,\alpha}|\omega_{j}|_{k,\alpha},

for some
c>0
.
This implies that
\omega(\zeta)
converges in the Hölder space
C^{k,\alpha}
for
\zeta
small enough.
Indeed, if
(a_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
is any sequence of non-negative real numbers such that
a_{n}\leq c\sum_{i+j=n}a_{i}a_{j}
, then, by induction,
a_{n}\leq c^{n-1}a_{1}^{n}C_{n-1}
, where
C_{n-1}
are the Catalan numbers.
Since
C_{n-1}=\tfrac{1}{n}{2(n-1)\choose n-1}\leq 4^{n-1}
, we get that
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}\zeta^{n}
converges for
|\zeta|<\frac{1}{4ca_{1}}
.

To prove real-analyticity, we argue as in
[
31
, p. 281]
.
Consider
\Box\coloneqq\Delta+\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\zeta\partial\overline{\zeta}}
, viewed as an elliptic differential operator on
\zeta
-dependent differential forms.
Since
\omega\coloneqq\omega(\zeta)
is holomorphic in
\zeta
, we have
\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\zeta\partial\overline{\zeta}}\omega=0
.
Moreover,
\bar{\partial}^{*}\omega=0
by (
4.8
), so
\Delta\omega=\bar{\partial}^{*}\bar{\partial}\omega=-\tfrac{1}{2}\bar{\partial}^{*}\partial i_{\sigma}(\omega\wedge\omega)
, and hence
\Box\omega+\tfrac{1}{2}\bar{\partial}^{*}\partial i_{\sigma}(\omega\wedge\omega)=0
.
Hence, by writing
\omega(\zeta)=\zeta\eta(\zeta)
,
\eta
is a solution to

\Box\eta+\tfrac{1}{2}\zeta\bar{\partial}^{*}\partial i_{\sigma}(\eta\wedge\eta)=0.

When
\zeta
is restricted to a sufficiently small neighbourhood of
0
in
\mathbb{C}
, this is a non-linear second-order
elliptic
partial differential equation with real-analytic coefficients.
Hence, its solutions are real-analytic by
[
41
]
, and this concludes the proof of Theorem
C
.
