Joint Message Detection and Channel Estimation for Unsourced Random Access in Cell-Free User-Centric Wireless Networks
Burak Çakmak, Eleni Gkiouzepi, Manfred Opper, Giuseppe Caire

Abstract
We consider unsourced random access (uRA) in a cell-free (CF) user-centric wireless network, where a large number of potential users compete for a random access slot, while only a finite subset is active. The random access users transmit codewords of length L L symbols from a shared codebook, which are received by B B geographically distributed radio units (RUs), each equipped with M M antennas. Our goal is to devise and analyze a centralized decoder to detect the transmitted messages (without prior knowledge of the active users) and estimate the corresponding channel state information. A specific challenge lies in the fact that, due to the geographically distributed nature of the CF network, there is no fixed correspondence between codewords and large-scale fading coefficients (LSFCs). This makes current activity detection approaches which make use of this fixed LSFC-codeword association not directly applicable. To overcome this problem, we propose a scheme where the access codebook is partitioned in location-based subcodes, such that users in a particular location make use of the corresponding subcode. The joint message detection and channel estimation is obtained via a novel Approximated Message Passing (AMP) algorithm for a linear superposition of matrix-valued sources corrupted by noise. The statistical asymmetry in the fading profile and message activity leads to different statistics for the matrix sources, which distinguishes the AMP formulation from previous cases. In the regime where the codebook size scales linearly with L L , while B B and M M are fixed, we present a rigorous high-dimensional (but finite-sample) analysis of the proposed AMP algorithm. Exploiting this, we then present a precise (and rigorous) large-system analysis of the message missed-detection and false-alarm rates, as well as the channel estimation mean-square error. The resulting system allows the seamless formation of user-centric clusters and very low latency beamformed uplink-downlink communication without explicit user-RU association, pilot allocation, and power control. This makes the proposed scheme highly appealing for low-latency random access communications in CF networks.

1.1
Overview of Contributions

To overcome the challenge of the unknown association between
large scale fading coefficients
(LSFCs) and codewords, we propose a location-based approach: the coverage area
{\cal D}
is partitioned into
U

\{{\cal L}_{u}:u\in[U]\}

U
is a suitable fixed integer. The locations are designed so that users within each location
{\cal L}_{u}
experience a similar LSFC profile, i.e., the collection of LSFCs between a transmitter in location
{\cal L}_{u}
and all the RUs is similar for all users in location
{\cal L}_{u}
.
The uRA codebook is partitioned in
U
subcodes, respectively assigned to the
U
locations.
Users in a given location
{\cal L}_{u}
are restricted to using the codewords of the associated
u
-th subcode.
Thus, the scheme allows general statistical asymmetry between users in different locations but enforces statistical symmetry between users within the same location. Note that conventional cellular systems also use implicit
location information, enforcing a one-to-one correspondence between locations and cells.
Thus, our approach can be seen as a generalization to the CF case of current random access protocols
for for cellular networks (e.g., see also
[
19
]
for a location-based approach to beam alignment in mmWave CF networks).

At a high level, we summarize our contributions as follows:

(i)

We introduce a (novel) multisource AMP (approximate message passing) framework for matrix-valued noisy linear observations that handles multiple independent signal sources, each of which can be described by an arbitrarily different distribution. We present a non-asymptotic (finite-sample) AMP analysis
[
20
,
21
]
that provides explicit bounds on large-system approximation errors in terms of the
\mathcal{L}^{p}
norm (Theorem 1). Our analysis yields the asymptotic consistency of the decoupling principles manifested in the AMP analysis and the replica-symmetric (RS) computation of the static problem. Consequently, the proposed AMP algorithm asymptotically achieves Bayesian optimality under the validity of the RS approach.

(ii)

Utilizing the proposed AMP framework, we devise and analyze a centralized decoder to detect the transmitted messages and estimate the corresponding channel state information. In particular, we present (rigorous) large-system analyses of the empirical missed-detection and false alarm-rates for the detection step (Theorem 2), and the empirical mean-square error for the subsequent channel estimation following the detection step (Theorem 3).

(iii)

Using the AMP large-dimensional outputs statistics, we provide expressions for the message
missed-detection and false-alarm probabilities in near closed-form (a one-dimensional integral which is easily evaluated via Gauss-Chebyshev quadrature), as well as for the channel estimation mean-square error for the active detected users.

(iv)

We present insightful results on the achievable rate of beamformed ACK transmission in the DL. These results highlight the effectiveness of the proposed scheme for uRA, ensuring seamless connectivity and low latency in CF networks.

We also compare with a previously proposed scheme for multicell networks, based on separated AMP at each RU and centralized message detection (see Section
1.2
), which can be regarded as the present state-of-the-art, and show that our centralized approach yields superior performance.

Our novel (centralized) multisource AMP framework handles
multiple
independent signal sources, each of which can be described by an arbitrarily different distribution. Interestingly, the multisource AMP is not significantly more complex than the previous multiple measurement vector approximated message passing (MMV-AMP) algorithm
[
22
]
. On the other hand, we note that our analysis is
independent
of the previous studies. In particular,
the previous MMV-AMP analysis
[
23
,
22
]
refer to the convergence
in probability
of certain quantities in the large-system limit. Our analysis yields convergence in the almost sure sense. Indeed, the latter is necessary to prove the almost sure convergence of the missed-detection and false-alarm rates, as well as the channel estimation error.

Related Works

The uRA paradigm was proposed in

In uRA, the receiver’s task is to decode the list of

2.1
Channel Model Assumptions

While our analysis for joint message detection and channel estimation applies to any random vector

In the sequel, we make the following assumptions.

2.1.1
Nominal LSFC Assumption

In general, the LSFCs are given by some distance-dependent
pathloss function indicating the signal power reduction along the propagation path
[
44
,
45
]
. In contrast, in several works treating the information theoretic analysis of wireless networks, the co-called
“Wyner model”
[
9
,
46
,
47
]

has been widely used. In the Wyner model, the LSFCs take only three values,
1 for the paths within the same cell (users within each cell to their base station),
\wp\in[0,1]
for the path across adjacent cells,
and 0 for the paths across non-adjacent cells. We take an intermediate step and consider that users in the same location
{\cal L}_{u}
have
the same LSFC, indicated as
g_{u,b}
to each
b
-th RU. The collection of LSFCs
\{g_{u,b}:b\in[B]\}
is referred to as LSFC profile and it is common to all users in location
{\cal L}_{u}
for
u\in[U]
.

This assumption, referred to as “nominal” LSFC assumption, is justified by the following rationale:

•

We use the nominal LSFC assumption to devise a suitably simple
denoising function
in the proposed AMP algorithm.
Our analysis is valid even when the true statistics of the user channels does not obey the assumption. In this case, the denoising function
derived from the nominal LSFC assumption is mismatched. Nevertheless, the analysis is still fully valid.

•

In our follow-up work
[
48
]
(published after the first submission of the present work), we considered the general case of users arbitrarily distributed over the coverage area. In this case, the nominal LSFC assumption corresponds to the case of co-located users at given positions (e.g., centroids) of the locations. For uniformly distributed users, in
[
48
]
we have compared the matched case, where the AMP denoising function uses the true user distribution, and the mismatched case, where the AMP denoising function assumes the nominal LSFC values,
and we have verified that for typical pathloss functions and CF network layouts, with an appropriate choice of the locations,
the performance loss incurred by mismatch is virtually negligible.

•

Under the nominal LSFC assumption, the AMP output statistics are Gaussians under both the active message and the inactive message assumptions, and the analysis of the missed-detection and false-alarm probabilities yields elegant and easy to evaluate near-closed form formulas, using the theory of quadratic forms of complex Gaussian RVs (see Appendix
K
).

•

In this context, we find more appropriate to favor clean and (near-)closed form results based on a generalization of the Wyner model, that has a long tradition in information theory, rather than focusing on overly specific pathloss models that clutter the results witout bringing any funamental new insight into the problem.

4.1
Message Detection

In uRA, the first task of the centralized receiver is to produce an estimate of the list of the active messages, i.e.,
the list of columns of
{\bf S}_{u}
that have been transmitted for each location

Equivalently, this consists of estimating vector of binary message activities

In random access problems, missed-detection (an active message detected as inactive) and false-alarm
(an inactive message detected as active) events play generally different roles.
In fact, in the presence of a
false-alarm
(FA) event, the system assumes that some user requests access, while in reality such user does not exist.
In this case, according to the uRA protocol sketched in Section
1

some transmission resource in the DL is wasted since the system sends an ACK message to an inexistent user,
and eventually will be released after some timeout.
In the presence of a
missed-detection
(MD) event, some user requesting access is not recognized by the system.
In this case, it will receive no ACK in the DL will try again in a subsequence RACH slot after some timeout.
Depending on whether resource waste or delayed access is the most stringent system constraint, the roles of FA and MD are clearly asymmetric. Therefore, minimizing the
average
error probability is not operationally meaningful.

7
Conclusion

We considered the problem of unsourced random access (uRA) in cell-free (CF) wireless networks. The model is an extension of what already considered in several works for the case of uRA with a massive MIMO receiver. The difference is that, in the CF case,
the total number of antennas
BM
is partitioned in
B
groups of
M
antennas each, and these are allocated to geographically separated RUs. While this may seem a minor modification, the problem is significantly
more complicated by the fact that the statistics of the channel vectors describing the propagation between
users and RUs dependents on the user (transmitter) location, via the set of distance-dependent
large-scale fading coefficients between the user and the RUs. In the uRA setting, users can transmit any codeword
from a common codebook. It is therefore not possible to associate in a fixed manner a given access codeword to a given set of large-scale propagation coefficients (unlike the problem of activity detection with known user locations and fixed association between activity signature sequences and users). We circumvent this problem by proposing a partitioned access codebook associating sub-codebooks to locations, such that users in a given location select codewords from the corresponding codebook. For this system, we propose a centralized AMP algorithm for joint uRA message detection and
CSI estimation. The rigorous analysis of this AMP scheme is novel and does not reduces immediately to
a known case for which the asymptotic SE analysis and concentration result has been rigorously proved.
In fact, our results yield immediately other known
concentration results the classical AMP and the MMV-AMP, obtained as
special cases of our general setting for
U=F=1
and for
U=1,F>1
, respectively.
In addition, we also show the consistency of the replica symmetry (non-rigorous) results with the rigorous SE analysis.

Based on our analysis, a rigorous (asymptotic) characterization of the statistics at the output of the AMP
detection/estimation scheme
are given. Using these statistics, we can provide very accurate and almost closed-form expressions for the FA and MD probabilities of the uRA messages. Also, using the asymptotic statistics, simple and almost closed-form expressions for the
achievable user rate in a subsequent transmission phase are obtained.
For simplicity, we focused here on the downlink transmission based on MRT, which requires only per-RU local combining CSI. However, in a similar manner, expressions for other forms of combining/precoding can be obtained for both downlink and
uplink transmission (in the latter case, after the RACH slot the user would append a data communication slot
as in the scheme of
[
27
]
).

An interesting extension is the case where the number of antennas per RU scales with the number of uRA messages (i.e., with
L
). In this case, we let the number of antennas per RU be equal to
mM
, where
M
is fixed (constant with
L
) and
m/L=\beta
. Then, each block of
M
antennas forms a system identical to the one presented in this paper, for which the concentration holds. However, for the sake of message detection and data transmission, the
m
group of antennas at each RU can be “pooled” together. This yields vanishing message error probability (both FA and MD) and non-vanishing per-user rate as
L\rightarrow\infty
. Again, these results will be presented in a follow-up paper.

We notice that the symbol synchronous frequency-flat channel model used in most existing uRA. MIMO papers (starting from
[
18
]
, all related papers referenced here including the present paper), is unrealistic. Propagation goes through frequency-selective multipath channels, and transmission in the RACH slot cannot be perfectly chip-level synchronous at each RU receiver, since propagation distances are different for different users and
RUs. Hence, the extension to a wideband frequency-selective asynchronous model,
where the unknown channels are vectors of
multipath impulse responses is called for.

We emphasize the broader applicability of our theoretical results. They go beyond the context of wireless networks and are relevant to the general problem of “multiple measurement vector” compressed sensing involving
the superposition of multiple signal sources, each with different statistical characteristics. This suggests the relevance of our results to a wider range of applications.

Another intriguing direction is to develop the AMP framework using randomly permuted Fourier-type codebooks
[
70
]
instead of codebooks with i.i.d. entries. From a computational perspective, randomly permuted Fourier-type codebooks and their corresponding AMP algorithms (which include matrix-vector products) can be computed very efficiently. Recently, we have introduced a new AMP framework in this direction. For further details, we refer to
[
71
]
.

Appendix A
Preliminaries: Concentration Inequalities with
\mathcal{L}^{p}
norm

We recall the notion of concentration in terms of
\mathcal{O}(\kappa)
notation introduced in (
12
)-(
13
).
In this section, we present its elementary properties which are frequently used in the following proofs.

Lemma 1
.

Consider the (scalar) random variables
{\rm a}=\mathcal{O}(\kappa)
and
{\rm b}=\mathcal{O}(\tilde{\kappa})
. Then, we have

\displaystyle{\rm a+b}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(\max(\kappa,\tilde{\kappa}))

(81)

\displaystyle{\rm ab}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(\kappa\tilde{\kappa})

(82)

\displaystyle\sqrt{\rm a}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\kappa}).

(83)

Proof.

The results (
81
) and (
82
) follow from the Minkowski inequality (i.e.,
\|{\rm a}+{\rm b}\|_{\mathcal{L}^{p}}\leq\|{\rm a}\|_{\mathcal{L}^{p}}+\|{\rm b}\|_{\mathcal{L}^{p}}
) and Hölder’s inequality (i.e.,
\|{\rm a}{\rm b}\|_{\mathcal{L}^{p}}\leq\|{a}\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2p}}\|{\rm b}\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2p}}
), respectively. The result (
83
) follows from the inequality
\|{a}^{1/2}\|_{\mathcal{L}^{p}}\leq\|{\rm a}^{1/2}\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2p}}=\|{\rm a}\|_{\mathcal{L}^{p}}^{\frac{1}{2}}
.
∎

Lemma 2
.

[
72
, Lemma 7.8]

Consider the random vector
\underline{{\bf a}}\in\mbox{\bb C}^{N}
where
\underline{{\bf a}}\sim_{\rm i.i.d.}{\rm a}

with
{\rm a}=\mathcal{O}(1)
. Then,

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n\in[N]}{\rm a}_{n}=\mathbb{E}[{\rm a}]+\mathcal{O}(N^{-\frac{1}{2}})\;.

Lemma 3
.

Consider the random vectors
\underline{{\bf a}},\underline{{\bf b}}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
where
\underline{{\bf a}}\sim_{\text{i.i.d.}}{\rm a}
and
\underline{{\bf b}}\sim_{\text{i.i.d.}}{\rm b}
with
{\rm a}=\mathcal{O}(1)
and
{\rm b}=\mathcal{O}(1)
. Then, for any
\widehat{\underline{{\bf a}}}\simeq\underline{{\bf a}}
and
\underline{\widehat{\bf b}}\simeq\underline{{\bf b}}
, we have

\frac{1}{N}\widehat{\underline{{\bf a}}}^{\sf H}\underline{\widehat{\bf b}}=\mathbb{E}[{\rm a}^{*}{\rm b}]+\mathcal{O}(N^{-\frac{1}{2}})\;.

(84)

Proof.

Let
\underline{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\delta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\delta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\delta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\delta$}}}_{a}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\widehat{\underline{{\bf a}}}-{\underline{{\bf a}}}
and
\underline{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\delta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\delta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\delta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\delta$}}}_{b}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\widehat{\underline{{\bf b}}}-{\underline{{\bf b}}}
with noting that e.g.
\underline{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\delta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\delta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\delta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\delta$}}}_{a}}=\mathcal{O}(1)
. From Lemma
2
we have
\underline{{\bf a}}=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{N})
and
\underline{{\bf b}}=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{N})
. Hence, we get from the arithmetic rules (
81
) and (
82
) that

\underline{\widehat{\bf a}}^{\sf H}\underline{\widehat{\bf b}}-{\underline{{\bf a}}}^{\sf H}\underline{{\bf b}}=\underline{{\bf a}}^{\sf H}\underline{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\delta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\delta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\delta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\delta$}}}_{b}}+\underline{{\bf b}}^{\sf H}\underline{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\delta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\delta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\delta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\delta$}}}_{a}}+\underline{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\delta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\delta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\delta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\delta$}}}_{a}}^{\sf H}\underline{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\delta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\delta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\delta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\delta$}}}_{b}}=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{N})\;.

(85)

From the product rule (
82
) we have

{\rm a}^{\star}{\rm b}=\mathcal{O}(1)
. Thus, from Lemma
2
we have the concentration

\frac{1}{N}{\underline{{\bf a}}}^{\sf H}\underline{{\bf b}}=\mathbb{E}[{\rm a}^{\star}{\rm b}]+\mathcal{O}(N^{-1/2})
. This completes the proof.
∎

Appendix B
Proof of Theorem
1

In this section, we provide a nearly self-contained proof of Theorem
1
. Auxiliary lemmas are proved in Appendix
E
in order to maintain the flow of exposition.

It is useful to define the dynamics of residuals for each
(u,t)\in[U]\times[T]

\displaystyle\widehat{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\Psi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\Psi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\Psi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\Psi$}}}}_{u}^{(t)}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\Gamma$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\Gamma$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\Gamma$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\Gamma$}}}_{u}^{(t)}-{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\Theta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\Theta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\Theta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\Theta$}}}_{u}

(86a)

\displaystyle\ \widehat{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\Phi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\Phi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\Phi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\Phi$}}}}_{u}^{(t)}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}{\bf R}_{u}^{(t)}-{\bf X}_{u}\;.

(86b)

We recall that
{\bf Y}=\sum_{u\in[U]}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\Theta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\Theta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\Theta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\Theta$}}}_{u}+{\bf N}
. Then, from the original AMP dynamics (
7
) we have the recursion of “residual-AMP” dynamics for
t=1,2,\cdots,T
as

\displaystyle\widehat{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\Psi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\Psi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\Psi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\Psi$}}}}_{u}^{(t)}

\displaystyle={\bf S}_{u}{\bf F}_{u}^{(t)}-\alpha_{u}{\bf Q}_{u}^{(t)}{\bf Z}^{(t-1)}

(87a)

\displaystyle{\bf Z}^{(t)}

\displaystyle={\bf N}-\sum_{u\in[U]}\widehat{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\Psi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\Psi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\Psi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\Psi$}}}}_{u}^{(t)}

(87b)

\displaystyle\widehat{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\Phi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\Phi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\Phi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\Phi$}}}}_{u}^{(t)}

\displaystyle={\bf S}_{u}^{\sf H}{\bf Z}^{(t)}+{\bf F}_{u}^{(t)}

(87c)

\displaystyle{\bf F}_{u}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\eta_{u,t}({\bf X}_{u}+\widehat{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\Phi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\Phi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\Phi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\Phi$}}}}_{u}^{(t)})-{\bf X}_{u}\;,

(87d)

with the initialization
{{\bf F}}_{u}^{(1)}={\bf X}_{u}^{(1)}-{\bf X}_{u}
.

Note that our goal is to verify the high-dimensional representations (for any
(u,t)\in[U]\times[T]
)

\displaystyle\widehat{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\Psi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\Psi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\Psi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\Psi$}}}}_{u}^{(t)}

\displaystyle\simeq{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\Psi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\Psi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\Psi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\Psi$}}}}_{u}^{(t)}

(88a)

\displaystyle\widehat{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\Phi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\Phi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\Phi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\Phi$}}}}_{u}^{(t)}

\displaystyle\simeq{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\Phi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\Phi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\Phi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\Phi$}}}_{u}^{(t)}.

(88b)

To this end, we use the idea of the “Householder Dice” representation of AMP dynamics introduced in
[
73
]
. Such a representation can be seen as an intermediate step in the commonly used “conditioning” AMP proof technique
[
74
]
. Namely, it clarifies how to represent the original AMP dynamics—that are coupled by a random matrix (
{\bf S}_{u}
, in our case)—as equivalent dynamics without a random matrix (i.e.,
{\bf S}_{u}
-free). This step has no approximation and requires no assumption other than the underlying random matrix assumption (i.e.,
{\bf S}_{u}\sim\mathcal{CN}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle 0$}}},{\bf I}_{N_{u}}/L)
). We also refer the reader to
[
75
,
56
]
where a similar AMP proof strategy is used.

At a high level, we have two main steps in the proof. In the first step (Section
B.1
), we use the block Gram-Schmidt process to express the dynamics of the residual-AMP (
87
) in terms of an equivalent
{\bf S}_{u}
-free dynamics, called the “Householder Dice representation”. In the second step (Section
B.2
), we use the properties of the notion of
\mathcal{L}^{p}
concentration given in Appendix
A
to establish the high-dimensional equivalence of the Householder Dice representation, and thereby it will give us the high-dimensional equivalent of the original residual-AMP dynamics (
87
).

For convenience, we will use the following notation for matrices throughout the proof: Consider matrices
{\bf A}
and
{\bf B}
, both with the same number of rows, denoted by
N
. Then, we define

\langle{\bf A},{\bf B}\rangle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\frac{1}{N}{\bf A}^{{\sf H}}{\bf B}

(89)

whenever the dependency on
N
is clear from the context.

B.1
Step-1: The Householder Dice equivalent

We will use an adaptive representation of the Gaussian random matrices (i.e.,
{\bf S}_{u}
in the context) by using the following result (see also
[
73
, Eq. (11)]
).

Lemma 4
.

Let the random matrices
{\bf V}\in\mathbb{C}^{L\times F}
,
{\bf U}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times F}
and
\tilde{{\bf S}}\in\mathbb{C}^{L\times N}
be mutually independent. Let
{\bf P}_{\bf V}^{\perp}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}{\bf I}-\frac{1}{L}{\bf V}{\bf V}^{\sf H}
be a projection matrix,
{\bf U}\sim_{\text{i.i.d.}}\mathcal{CN}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle 0$}}},{\bf I}_{F})
and
\tilde{\bf S}\sim_{\text{i.i.d.}}\mathcal{CN}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle 0$}}},{\bf I}_{N}/L)
. Let

{\bf S}=\frac{{\bf V}{\bf U}^{\sf H}}{L}+{\bf P}_{\bf V}^{\perp}\tilde{\bf S}\;.

(90)

Then,
{\bf S}\sim\tilde{\bf S}
and
{\bf S}
is (statistically) independent of
{\bf V}
.

Proof.

Let
{\rm vec}({\bf S})
denote the column vector obtained by stacking the column vectors of
{\bf S}
below one another, i.e.

{\rm vec}({\bf S})=[\underline{{\bf s}}_{1}^{\top},\underline{{\bf s}}_{2}^{\top},\cdots\underline{{\bf s}}_{N}]^{\top}
. Then, conditioned on
{\bf V}
, it is easy to verify that
{\rm vec}({{\bf S}})
is a complex Gaussian vector with zero mean and covariance

\displaystyle{\bf I}_{N}\otimes\left(\frac{1}{L^{2}}{\bf V}{\bf V}^{\sf H}+\frac{1}{L}({\bf P}_{\bf V}^{\perp})^{2}\right)

\displaystyle={\bf I}_{N}\otimes\left(\frac{1}{L^{2}}{\bf V}{\bf V}^{\sf H}+\frac{1}{L}{\bf P}_{\bf V}^{\perp}\right)

(91)

\displaystyle={\bf I}_{N}\otimes\frac{1}{L}{\bf I}_{L}\;.

(92)

Thereby, conditioned on
{\bf V}
, we have
{\bf S}\sim_{\text{i.i.d.}}\mathcal{CN}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle 0$}}},{\bf I}_{N}/L)
. Furthermore, since the conditional distribution of
{\bf S}
is invariant from
{\bf V}
,
{\bf S}
and
{\bf V}
are independent.
∎

Note that we can also consider the conjugate representation of Lemma
4
as

{\bf S}^{\sf H}=\frac{{\bf U}{\bf V}^{\sf H}}{L}+\tilde{\bf S}^{\sf H}{\bf P}_{\bf V}^{\perp}\;.

(93)

For example, let us consider a matrix
{\bf Z}\in\mbox{\bb C}^{L\times F,}
with
\text{span}({\bf Z})=\text{span}({\bf V})
. So that
{\bf P}_{{\bf V}}^{\perp}{\bf Z}={\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle 0$}}}
. Hence, we have the representation of the product
{\bf S}^{\sf H}{\bf Z}

\displaystyle{\bf S}^{\sf H}{\bf Z}

\displaystyle={\bf U}\langle{\bf V},{\bf Z}\rangle

(94)

which involves solely the lower-dimensional random element
{\bf U}\sim_{\text{i.i.d.}}\mathcal{CN}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle 0$}}},{\bf I}_{F})
.

To extend this idea to e.g. the dynamics
{\bf S}_{u}^{\sf H}{\bf Z}^{(t)}
(and similarly to the dynamics
{\bf S}_{u}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle{\bf F}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle{\bf F}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle{\bf F}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle{\bf F}$}}}_{u}^{(t)}
), we will employ the block Gram-Schmidt process to construct a set of orthogonal matrices, denoted as

{\bf V}^{(1:t)}\equiv\{{\bf V}^{(1)},{\bf V}^{(2)},\ldots,{\bf V}^{(t)}\}

such that
\text{span}({\bf V}^{(1:t)})=\text{span}({\bf Z}^{(1:t)})
. We then recursively apply Lemma
4
to obtain an
{\bf S}_{u}
-free equivalent of the dynamics
{\bf S}_{u}^{\sf H}{\bf Z}^{(t)}
.

We first introduce the block Gram-Schmidt notation:
Let
{\bf V}^{(1:t-1)}=\{{\bf V}^{(s)}\in\mbox{\bb C}^{N\times F}\}_{s\in[t-1]}
such that
\langle{\bf V}^{(i)},{\bf V}^{(j)}\rangle=\mathcal{\delta}_{ij}{\bf I}_{F}
for all
i,j
. Then, for any matrix
{\bf B}\in\mbox{\bb C}^{N\times F}
, by using the block Gram-Schmidt (orthogonalization) process,
we can always construct the new (semi-unitary) matrix

{\bf V}^{(t)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\mathcal{GS}({\bf B}\mid{\bf V}^{(1:t-1)})

such that
\langle{\bf V}^{(t)},{\bf V}^{(s)}\rangle=\delta_{st}{\bf I}_{F}
and we have the block Gram-Schmidt decomposition

{\bf B}=\sum_{1\leq s\leq t}{\bf V}^{(s)}\langle{\bf V}^{(s)},{\bf B}\rangle\;.

(95)

We refer to Appendix
L
for the explicit definition of the block Gram-Schmidt notation.

B.1.1
Iteration Step
t=1

We begin with deriving the Householder Dice representation of the residual-AMP dynamics (
87
) for the iteration step
t=1
. Let

{\bf V}_{u}^{(1)}=\mathcal{GS}({\bf F}_{u}^{(1)}\mid\emptyset)\;.

(96)

We recall that
\alpha_{u}=N_{u}/L
. Then, we use Lemma
4
to represent
{\bf S}_{u}
as

{\bf S}_{u}={\sqrt{\alpha_{u}}}\frac{{\bf G}_{u}^{(1)}({\bf V}_{u}^{(1)})^{\sf H}}{N_{u}}+{\bf S}_{u}^{(0,1)}{\bf P}_{{\bf F}_{u}^{(1)}}^{\perp}

(97)

where we introduce the arbitrary (lower-dimensional) random element
{\bf G}_{u}^{(1)}\sim{\cal N}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle 0$}}},{\bf I}_{F})
and arbitrary random matrix
{\bf S}_{u}^{(0,1)}\sim{\bf S}_{u}
.
Note that
{\bf P}_{{\bf F}_{u}^{(1)}}^{\perp}{\bf F}_{u}^{(1)}={\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle 0$}}}
. Then, from (
97
) we have

\displaystyle\widehat{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\Psi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\Psi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\Psi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\Psi$}}}}_{u}^{(1)}

\displaystyle={\bf S}_{u}{\bf F}_{u}^{(1)}={\sqrt{\alpha_{u}}}{\bf G}_{u}^{(1)}\langle{\bf V}_{u}^{(1)},{\bf F}_{u}^{(1)}\rangle\;.

(98)

To derive the
{\bf S}_{u}
-free representation of
\widehat{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\Phi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\Phi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\Phi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\Phi$}}}}_{u}^{(1)}
we first construct the
L\times F
unitary matrix

{\bf V}^{(1)}=\mathcal{GS}({\bf Z}^{(1)}\mid\emptyset)

and use Lemma
4
to represent
{\bf S}_{u}^{(0,1)}
in (
97
) as

{\bf S}_{u}^{(0,1)}=\frac{{\bf V}^{(1)}({\bf U}_{u}^{(1)})^{\sf H}}{L}+{\bf P}_{{\bf Z}^{(1)}}^{\perp}{\bf S}_{u}^{(1,1)}\;,

(99)

where we introduce a new (arbitrary) random element
{\bf U}_{u}^{(1)}\sim{\cal N}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle 0$}}},{\bf I}_{F})
and a new random matrix
{\bf S}_{u}^{(1,1)}\sim{\bf S}_{u}
. Hence, (
97
) reads as

\displaystyle{\bf S}_{u}

\displaystyle={\sqrt{\alpha_{u}}}\frac{{\bf G}_{u}^{(1)}({\bf V}_{u}^{(1)})^{\sf H}}{N_{u}}+\frac{{\bf V}^{(1)}(\widehat{\bf U}_{u}^{(1)})^{\sf H}}{L}

\displaystyle+{\bf P}_{{\bf Z}^{(1)}}^{\perp}{\bf S}_{u}^{(1,1)}{\bf P}_{{\bf F}_{u}^{(1)}}^{\perp}\;,

(100)

where for short we define
\widehat{{\bf U}}_{u}^{(1)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}{\bf P}_{{\bf F}_{u}^{(1)}}^{\perp}{{\bf U}}_{u}^{(1)}
. Note that
{\bf P}_{{\bf Z}^{(1)}}^{\perp}{\bf Z}^{(1)}={\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle 0$}}}
. Then, from (
100
) we have

\displaystyle\widehat{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\Phi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\Phi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\Phi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\Phi$}}}}_{u}^{(1)}

\displaystyle={\bf S}_{u}^{\sf H}{\bf Z}^{(1)}+{\bf F}_{u}^{(1)}

(101)

\displaystyle={\bf V}_{u}^{(1)}\frac{\langle{\bf G}_{u}^{(1)},{\bf Z}^{(1)}\rangle}{\sqrt{\alpha_{u}}}+\widehat{\bf U}_{u}^{(1)}\langle{\bf V}^{(1)},{\bf Z}^{(1)}\rangle+{\bf F}_{u}^{(1)}

(102)

\displaystyle=\widehat{\bf U}_{u}^{(1)}\langle{\bf V}^{(1)},{\bf Z}^{(1)}\rangle

\displaystyle+{\bf V}_{u}^{(1)}(\frac{\langle{\bf G}_{u}^{(1)},{\bf Z}^{(1)}\rangle}{\sqrt{\alpha_{u}}}+\langle{\bf V}_{u}^{(1)},{\bf X}_{u}^{(1)}\rangle)\;.

(103)

In step (
103
) we use the block Gram-Schmidt decomposition

{\bf F}_{u}^{(1)}={\bf V}_{u}^{(1)}\langle{\bf V}_{u}^{(1)},{\bf F}_{u}^{(1)}\rangle\;.

(104)

This completes the Householder Dice representation of the dynamics (
87
) for
t=1
.

B.1.2
Iteration Step
t=2

Moving on to the second iteration step, we fix
t=2
but mimic the notations in a manner that the arguments can be recalled for any
t>1
. We construct the matrices (for each
u\in[U]
)

\displaystyle{\bf V}_{u}^{(t)}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{GS}({\bf F}_{u}^{(t)}\mid{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle{\bf V}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle{\bf V}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle{\bf V}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle{\bf V}$}}}_{u}^{(1:t-1)}).

(105)

Then, using Lemma
4
, we represent
{\bf S}_{u}^{(t-1,t-1)}
(i.e.
{\bf S}_{u}^{(1,1)}
in (
100
)) as

{\bf S}_{u}^{(t-1,t-1)}={\sqrt{\alpha_{u}}}\frac{{\bf G}_{u}^{(t)}({\bf V}_{u}^{(t)})^{\sf H}}{N_{u}}+{\bf S}_{u}^{(t-1,t)}{\bf P}_{{\bf V}_{u}^{(t)}}^{\perp}\;,

(106)

where we introduce the new random element
{\bf G}_{u}^{(t)}\sim{\bf G}_{u}^{(1)}
and a new random matrix
{\bf S}_{u}^{(t-1,t)}\sim{\bf S}_{u}
. Then, (
100
) reads as

\displaystyle{\bf S}_{u}

\displaystyle={\sqrt{\alpha_{u}}}\sum_{s=1}^{t}\frac{\widehat{\bf G}_{u}^{(s)}({\bf V}_{u}^{(s)})^{\sf H}}{N_{u}}+\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\frac{{\bf V}^{(s)}(\widehat{\bf U}_{u}^{(s)})^{\sf H}}{L}

\displaystyle+{\bf P}_{{\bf Z}^{(1:t-1)}}^{\perp}{\bf S}_{u}^{(t-1,t)}{\bf P}_{{\bf F}_{u}^{(1:t)}}^{\perp}\;\;.

(107)

Here the matrices
{\bf P}_{{\bf F}_{u}^{(1:t)}}^{\perp}
and
{\bf P}_{{\bf Z}^{(1:t-1)}}^{\perp}
denote the projections onto the orthogonal complement of the subspace spanned by
{\bf F}_{u}^{(1:t)}
and
{\bf Z}^{(1:t-1)}
, respectively. For example,

{\bf P}_{{\bf Z}^{(1:t-1)}}^{\perp}={\bf I}-\frac{1}{L}\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}{\bf V}^{(s)}({\bf V}^{(s)})^{\sf H}\;.

Also, for convenience, we have defined

\displaystyle\widehat{{\bf G}}_{u}^{(t)}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}{\bf P}_{{\bf Z}^{(1:t-1)}}^{\perp}{{\bf G}}_{u}^{(t)}

(108)

\displaystyle\widehat{{\bf U}}_{u}^{(t)}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}{\bf P}_{{\bf F}^{(1:t)}}^{\perp}{{\bf U}}_{u}^{(t)}\;.

(109)

By using the representation (
107
) we then obtain

\displaystyle\widehat{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\Psi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\Psi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\Psi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\Psi$}}}}_{u}^{(t)}={\sqrt{\alpha_{u}}}\sum_{s=1}^{t}\widehat{\bf G}_{u}^{(s)}\langle{\bf V}_{u}^{(s)},{\bf F}_{u}^{(t)}\rangle

\displaystyle+{\alpha_{u}}\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}{\bf V}^{(s)}\langle\widehat{\bf U}_{u}^{(s)},{\bf F}_{u}^{(t)}\rangle-{\alpha_{u}}{\bf Z}^{(t-1)}{\bf Q}_{u}^{(t)}

(110)

\displaystyle={\sqrt{\alpha_{u}}}\sum_{s=1}^{t}\widehat{\bf G}_{u}^{(s)}\langle{\bf V}_{u}^{(s)},{\bf F}_{u}^{(t)}\rangle

\displaystyle+{\alpha_{u}}\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}{\bf V}^{(s)}\left(\langle\widehat{\bf U}_{u}^{(s)},{\bf F}_{u}^{(t)}\rangle-\langle{\bf V}^{(s)},{\bf Z}^{(t-1)}\rangle{\bf Q}_{u}^{(t)}\right)\;.

(111)

Here, the latter step uses the block Gram-Schmidt decomposition

{\bf Z}^{(t-1)}=\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}{\bf V}^{(s)}\langle{\bf V}^{(s)},{\bf Z}^{(t-1)}\rangle\;.

(112)

We now construct
{\bf V}^{(t)}=\mathcal{GS}({\bf Z}^{(t)}\mid{\bf V}^{(1:t-1)})
and

{\bf S}_{u}^{(t-1,t)}=\frac{{\bf V}^{(t)}({\bf U}_{u}^{(t)})^{\sf H}}{L}+{\bf P}_{{\bf V}^{(t)}}^{\perp}{\bf S}_{u}^{(t,t)}\;,

(113)

with the arbitrary random matrix
{\bf S}_{u}^{(t,t)}\sim{\bf S}_{u}
and an arbitrary random element
{{\bf U}}_{u}^{(t)}\sim{{\bf U}}_{u}^{(1)}
.Plugging this results in (
107
) leads to

\displaystyle{\bf S}_{u}

\displaystyle={\sqrt{\alpha_{u}}}\sum_{s=1}^{t}\frac{\widehat{\bf G}_{u}^{(s)}({\bf V}_{u}^{(s)})^{\sf H}}{N_{u}}+\sum_{s=1}^{t}\frac{{\bf V}^{(s)}(\widehat{\bf U}_{u}^{(s)})^{\sf H}}{L}

\displaystyle+{\bf P}_{{\bf V}^{(1:t)}}^{\perp}{\bf S}_{u}^{(t,t)}{\bf P}_{{\bf V}_{u}^{(1:t)}}^{\perp}\;.

(114)

Using this construction we get

\displaystyle\widehat{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\Phi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\Phi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\Phi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\Phi$}}}}_{u}^{(t)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{t}{\bf V}_{u}^{(s)}\frac{\langle\widehat{\bf G}_{u}^{(s)},{\bf Z}^{(t)}\rangle}{\sqrt{\alpha_{u}}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{s=1}^{t}\widehat{\bf U}_{u}^{(s)}\langle{\bf V}^{(s)},{\bf Z}^{(t)}\rangle+{\bf F}_{u}^{(t)}

(115)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\widehat{\bf U}_{u}^{(s)}\langle{\bf V}^{(s)},{\bf Z}^{(t)}\rangle

\displaystyle+\sum_{s=1}^{t}{\bf V}_{u}^{(s)}\left(\frac{\langle\widehat{\bf G}_{u}^{(s)},{\bf Z}^{(t)}\rangle}{\sqrt{\alpha_{u}}}+\langle{\bf V}_{u}^{(s)},{\bf F}_{u}^{(t)}\rangle\right).

(116)

Again, as to the latter term above, we note from block Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization that

{\bf F}_{u}^{(t)}=\sum_{s=1}^{t}{\bf V}_{u}^{(s)}\langle{\bf V}_{u}^{(s)},{\bf F}_{u}^{(t)}\rangle\;.

(117)

B.1.3
Iteration Steps
t>2

Clearly, for iteration steps
t=3,4,\ldots
, we can repeat the same arguments as for the case
t=2
. Specifically, at each
t\in~[T]
, we utilize equations (
107
) and (
114
) to compute
\widehat{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\Psi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\Psi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\Psi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\Psi$}}}}_{u}^{(t)}
and
\widehat{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\Phi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\Phi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\Phi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\Phi$}}}}_{u}^{(t)}
in the residual AMP dynamics (
87
), respectively. By following this process, we arrive at a
\{{\bf S}_{u}\}
-free representation of the dynamics (
87
): We commence with the identical initialization of
{\bf F}_{u}^{(1)}
from (
87
) for all
u\in[U]
and proceed for
t=1,2,\ldots,T
as

\displaystyle{\bf V}_{u}^{(t)}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{GS}({\bf F}_{u}^{(t)}\mid{\bf V}_{u}^{(1:t-1)})

(118a)

\displaystyle\widehat{{\bf G}}_{u}^{(t)}

\displaystyle={\bf P}_{{\bf Z}^{(1:t-1)}}^{\perp}{{\bf G}}_{u}^{(t)}

Appendix K
Computing the FA and MD probabilities

Considering the motivated fading model (
5
), i.e.
{\bf h}_{u}\in{\cal N}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle 0$}}},\hbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{u})
,
both the asymptotic of the MD rate (i.e.,
\beta_{\infty}^{\rm md}
) and the FA rate (i.e.,
\beta_{\infty}^{\rm fa}
) can be computed in closed form, as long as we have the closed-form expression of

{\rm P}(\gamma)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\mbox{\bb P}\left({\bf z}{\bf D}{\bf z}^{\sf H}\leq\gamma\right)

(293)

{\bf z}\sim\mathcal{CN}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle 0$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle 0$}}},{\bf I}_{F})
for the Hermitian symmetric PSD deterministic
{\bf D}
.
In fact, since
{\bf z}{\bf D}{\bf z}^{\sf H}\sim{\bf z}\tilde{\bf D}{\bf z}^{\sf H}
where
\tilde{\bf D}
stands for the diagonal matrix with the diagonal entries being the eigenvalues of
{\bf D}
, we can assume without loss of generality that
{\bf D}={\rm diag}(d_{1},d_{2},\cdots,d_{F})\;
.

Let
{\rm p}
denote the density function of the RV
{\bf z}{\bf D}{\bf z}^{\sf H}
. In particular, the Laplace transform of the density function reads as

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{\rm p}(s)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\mathbb{E}[{\rm e}^{-s{\bf z}{\bf D}{\bf z}^{\sf H}}]=\prod_{f\in[F]}\frac{1}{1+d_{f}s}\;.

(294)

Hence, the Laplace transform of the corresponding cumulative distribution function

{\rm P}
is given by

{\cal L}_{\rm P}(s)=\frac{1}{s}{\cal L}_{\rm p}(s)=\frac{1}{s}\prod_{f\in[F]}\left(\frac{1}{1+d_{f}{s}}\right).

Using the Laplace inversion we can write

\displaystyle{\rm P}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi{j}}\int_{c-j\infty}^{c+j\infty}\frac{1}{s}\prod_{f\in[F]}\left(\frac{1}{1+d_{f}s}\right)e^{s\gamma}\,ds

(295a)

where
c
is chosen so that the integration path is contained in the
region of convergence of the Laplace transform. Since the Laplace transform has a pole of order 1 at the origin, and
poles of order
F
at the points
-1/d_{f}
on the real negative line, the integration path must be chosen with
c>0
.

Using the Cauchy residue rule, we obtain the above Laplace inversion in analytic closed form as the sum of the residues of all poles in the left complex half-plane
{\rm Re}\{s\}\leq 0
. This yields

\displaystyle{\rm P}(\gamma)=

\displaystyle 1+\sum_{f\in[F]}{\rm Res}\left\{\prod_{f\in[F]}\left(\frac{1}{1+d_{f}s}\right){\rm e}^{s\gamma},s=\frac{-1}{d_{f}}\right\}

(296)

where the term 1 is the residue at
s=0
.
A problem with this closed-form approach is that the poles at

-1/d_{f}
may have multiplicity larger than 1. Hence, the residue computation requires
high-order differentiation which may quickly become cumbersome. For example, in our specific application case, the explicit form of the (log-)likelihood ratio test in (
43
) is given by

\displaystyle{\bf r}\left[{\hbox{diag}}\left(\frac{g_{u,1}}{\tau(\tau+g_{u,1})},\ldots,\frac{g_{u,B}}{\tau(\tau+g_{u,B})}\right)\otimes{\bf I}_{M}\right]{\bf r}^{\sf H}

\displaystyle\underset{{\cal H}_{0}}{\overset{{\cal H}_{1}}{\gtrless}}M\sum_{b=1}^{B}\log\left(1+\frac{g_{u,b}}{\tau}\right)-\log\nu_{u}

(297)

where
{\bf r}\sim{\bf z}{\bf C}^{1/2}
under hypothesis
{\cal H}_{0}
(i.e.,
a=0
), and

{\bf r}\sim{\bf z}(\hbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{u}+{\bf C})^{1/2}
under hypothesis
{\cal H}_{1}
(i.e.,
a=1
),
with
{\bf z}\sim_{\rm i.i.d.}{\cal C}{\cal N}(0,1)
,
{\bf C}=\tau{\bf I}_{F}
and
\hbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{u}
given in (
6
).
We see that in this case the matrix
{\bf D}
is given by

{\bf D}={\hbox{diag}}\left(\frac{g_{u,1}}{(\tau+g_{u,1})},\ldots,\frac{g_{u,B}}{(\tau+g_{u,B})}\right)\otimes{\bf I}_{M}\;\mbox{under ${\cal H}_{0}$}

and by

{\bf D}={\hbox{diag}}\left(\frac{g_{u,1}}{\tau},\ldots,\frac{g_{u,B}}{\tau}\right)\otimes{\bf I}_{M}\;\;\;\mbox{under ${\cal H}_{1}$}.

Hence, in both cases we have poles of multiplicity
M
(provided that the LSFCs
\{g_{u,b}\}
are distinct) or
higher if some of the LSFCs are non-distinct, e.g., due to some symmetry in the network geometry.

In order to avoid the case-by-case complication of higher order differentiation,
a more direct and yet numerically accurate approach consists of computing the Laplace inversion using a Gauss-Chebyshev quadrature, as introduced in
[
64
]
. The result is applicable once the Laplace transform
{\cal L}_{\rm p}(s)
is known. Since probabilities are real numbers, we can write

\displaystyle{\rm P}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{\mathcal{L}_{\rm p}(c+j\omega)e^{\gamma(c+j\omega)}}{c+j\omega}\;d\omega

(298a)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{c{\rm Re}\{\mathcal{L}_{\rm p}(c+j\omega)e^{\gamma(c+j\omega)}\}}{c^{2}+\omega^{2}}\;d\omega

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\frac{\omega{\rm Im}\{\mathcal{L}_{\rm p}(c+j\omega)e^{\gamma(c+j\omega)}\}}{c^{2}+\omega^{2}}\;d\omega\;.

(298b)

From the change of variables
\omega=c\sqrt{1-x^{2}}/x
we get (
298
) (at the top of next page)

\displaystyle{\rm P}(\gamma)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\left\{{\rm Re}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\rm p}\left(c+jc\frac{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}{x}\right)\exp\left(\gamma\left(c+jc\frac{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}{x}\right)\right)\right]\right.

\displaystyle+\left.\frac{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}{x}{\rm Im}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\rm p}\left(c+jc\frac{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}{x}\right)\exp\left(\gamma\left(c+jc\frac{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}{x}\right)\right)\right]\right\}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\;.

(298)

Finally, using a Gauss-Chebyshev quadrature with
v
nodes, we have

\displaystyle{\rm P}(\gamma)=\frac{1}{v}\sum_{n=1}^{v/2}\Big\{{\rm Re}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\rm p}(c+jc\tau_{n})\exp(\gamma_{u}(c+jc\tau_{n}))\right]

\displaystyle+\tau_{n}{\rm Im}[\mathcal{L}_{\rm p}(c+jc\tau_{n})\exp(\gamma_{u}(c+jc\tau_{n}))]\Big\}+E_{v},

(299)

where
\tau_{n}=\tan((2n-1)\pi/(2v))
and the error term
E_{v}
vanishes as
v\rightarrow\infty
.

Numerical evidence shows that the numerical computation of the integral is most stable by choosing the value of
c
that minimizes the function
\mathcal{L}_{\rm p}(s)e^{\gamma_{u}s}
on the real line. This value of
c
corresponds to the Chernoff bound, defined as

{\rm P}(\gamma)\leq\min_{c\geq 0}\mbox{\bb E}[e^{-c({\bf z}{\bf D}{\bf z}^{\sf H}-\gamma)}]=\min_{c\geq 0}\mathcal{L}_{\rm p}(c)e^{c\gamma}.

(300)

The Chernoff bound is easier to compute (numerically) than the Laplace inversion and serves as a sanity check to verify that the numerical computation of the Laplace inversion gives an accurate and numerically stable result.
