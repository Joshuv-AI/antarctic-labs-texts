Higher Order Generalization Error for First Order Discretization of Langevin Diffusion
Mufan (Bill) Li, Maxime Gazeau

Abstract
We propose a novel approach to analyze generalization error for discretizations of Langevin diffusion, such as the stochastic gradient Langevin dynamics (SGLD). For an ϵ \epsilon tolerance of expected generalization error, it is known that a first order discretization can reach this target if we run Ω ⁡ ( ϵ − 1 ​ log ⁡ ( ϵ − 1 ) ) \Omega(\epsilon^{-1}\log(\epsilon^{-1})) iterations with Ω ⁡ ( ϵ − 1 ) \Omega(\epsilon^{-1}) samples. In this article, we show that with additional smoothness assumptions, even first order methods can achieve arbitrarily runtime complexity. More precisely, for each N > 0 N>0 , we provide a sufficient smoothness condition on the loss function such that a first order discretization can reach ϵ \epsilon expected generalization error given Ω ( ϵ − 1 / N log ( ϵ − 1 ) ) \Omega(\epsilon^{-1/N}\log(\epsilon^{-1})) iterations with Ω ⁡ ( ϵ − 1 ) \Omega(\epsilon^{-1}) samples.

Introduction

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}

be a known loss function with respect to
parameters
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

and a single data point
z\in\mathcal{Z}
.
We define
F(x):=\mathbb{E}\,f(x,z)

where the expectation is over
z\sim\mathcal{D}

for an unknown distribution
\mathcal{D}
over
\mathcal{Z}
.
We consider the problem of minimizing an expected loss function

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}F(x)\,.

(1.1)

Since the distribution is unknown,
we rely on a sample of
n
-independent and
identically distributed (i.i.d.) data points

\mathbf{z}=\{z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}
,
and evaluate the empirical loss function denoted by

F_{\mathbf{z}}(x):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(x,z_{i})
.
In this setting, a learning algorithm is a random map

X:\mathcal{Z}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
and we use
X_{\mathbf{z}}
to denote the output.
Since the empirical loss
F_{\mathbf{z}}(X_{\mathbf{z}})
can be evaluated,
it remains to study the expected generalization error

\mathbb{E}\,F(X_{\mathbf{z}})-\mathbb{E}\,F_{\mathbf{z}}(X_{\mathbf{z}})\,,

(1.2)

where the expectation is over both
the randomness of
X
and
\mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}
.

In this article, we are interested in the class of algorithms
that can be viewed as a discretization of the (overdamped) Langevin diffusion,
defined by the stochastic differential equation (SDE)

dX(t)=-\nabla F_{\mathbf{z}}(X(t))\,dt+\sqrt{\frac{2}{\beta}}\,dW(t)\,,

(1.3)

where
\beta>0
is the inverse temperature parameter,
and
\{W(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
is a standard Brownian motion in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
It is well known that the Langevin diffusion
converges (as
t\to\infty
) to the Gibbs distribution

\rho_{\mathbf{z}}(x)\propto\exp(-\beta F_{\mathbf{z}}(x))

(
Bakry et al. 2013
)
.
Furthermore, using the uniform stability condition
introduced by
Bousquet and Elisseeff 2002
,
the Gibbs distribution is shown to have generalization error
O(n^{-1})

(
Raginsky et al. 2017
)
.
Many optimization results also rely on
the Gibbs distribution’s minimizing property

(
Raginsky et al. 2017
;
Xu et al. 2017
;
Erdogdu et al. 2018
)
.

Using this approach,
Xu et al. 2017
studied
a first order discretization of
the Langevin diffusion
\{X_{\mathbf{z},k}\}_{k\geq 0}

with step size
\eta>0
,
and showed the approximation error between
the algorithm and the Gibbs distribution
is on the order of
O(e^{-\Omega(k\eta)}+\eta)
,
where
k>0
is the number of iterations.
In terms of runtime and sample complexity for an
\epsilon
tolerance
on generalization error,
this implies we need to choose a small step size
\eta=O(\epsilon)
,
leading to
k\geq\Omega(\epsilon^{-1}\log\epsilon^{-1})

and
n\geq\Omega(\epsilon^{-1})
.
This approach corresponds to the top path of approximation steps
to generalization error in

.

In contrast to previous works,
our approach avoids viewing first order methods as
a discretization of Langevin diffusion.
Instead, we view first order methods as
higher order discretizations of a modified process,
which we construct via weak backward error analysis

(
Debussche and Faou 2011
;
Kopec 2013
;
Kopec 2015
)
.
With the explicit construction,
we can recover the stationary distribution
\pi^{N}_{\mathbf{z}}

of the modified process,
which approximates the first order methods to order
O(\eta^{N})
,
where
N>0
depends on the smoothness of
f(\cdot,z)
.
Then our main result establishes a generalization bound of
O(n^{-1})

for this distribution
\pi^{N}_{\mathbf{z}}
,
which is of the same order as Gibbs.
As a result, our approach shows that the generalization property
of Gibbs is actually a generic property of Poisson equations.
Putting these together, our results imply
we can choose a much larger stepsize of
\eta=O(\epsilon^{1/N})
,
leading to an improved runtime complexity of

k\geq\Omega(\epsilon^{-1/N}\log\epsilon^{-1})

while keeping the same sample complexity of

n\geq\Omega(\epsilon^{-1})
.
This is described in the bottom path in

Figure

.

We summarize our main contributions as follows.
We provide an explicit construction of
the modified process approximating SGLD,
as well as the stationary distribution
\pi^{N}_{\mathbf{z}}
,
up to an error of order
O(\eta^{N})
.
Under additional smoothness conditions,
we provide an improved generalization bound
for first order discretizations of Langevin diffusion.

The idea of backward error analysis traces back to
the study of numerical linear algebra
(
Wilkinson 1960
)

and numerical methods for ordinary differential equations (ODEs)

(
Hairer et al. 2006
)
.
This approach views each update step of a numerical algorithm as
the exact solution of a modified differential equation.
For example, if we solve the ODE
y^{\prime}(t)=y(t)^{2}

with the Euler update
y_{k+1}=y_{k}+\eta y_{k}^{2}

for some
\eta>0
,
then the modified equation can be written as an infinite series

(
Hairer et al. 2006
, Chapter IX, Example 1.1)

\widetilde{y}^{\prime}=\widetilde{y}^{2}-\eta\widetilde{y}^{3}+\eta^{2}\frac{3}{2}\widetilde{y}^{4}-\eta^{3}\frac{8}{3}\widetilde{y}^{5}+\eta^{4}\frac{31}{6}\widetilde{y}^{6}+\cdots\,,

where given initial condition
\widetilde{y}(0)=y_{k}
,
we will have that
\widetilde{y}(\eta)=y_{k+1}
.

However, directly extending this construction to SDEs
pathwise is not straightforward
(
Shardlow 2006
)
.
Instead,
Debussche and Faou 2011
introduced a construction approximating
the numerical method in distribution.
The authors derived a partial differential equation (PDE)
describing the evolution of the distribution for the numerical algorithm,
and consequently the stationary distribution as well.
We will provide more details in
Section

3
.
This work has been extended to implicit Langevin algorithms

(
Kopec 2013
;
Kopec 2015
)
,
higher order discretizations

(
Abdulle et al. 2012
;
Abdulle et al. 2014
;
Laurent and Vilmart 2020
)
,
and stochastic Hamiltonian systems with symplectic schemes

(
Wang et al. 2016
;
Anton 2017
;
Anton 2019
)
.

2
Main Results

Throughout the article we denote the Euclidean inner product
by
\langle x,y\rangle
for
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
and the corresponding norm by
\left|x\right|:=\langle x,x\rangle^{1/2}
.
Unless otherwise specified, all expectations
\mathbb{E}[\,\cdot\,]

are with respect to all sources of randomness
including
{\mathbf{z}}
.
We denote the conditional expectation on a random variable
using the subscript notation, more precisely we use

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}}[\,\cdot\,]:=\mathbb{E}[\,\cdot\,|{\mathbf{z}}]
.
For any measure or density
\pi
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
we denote the integral
\int\phi\,d\pi
as
\pi(\phi)
.
We also use the subscript notation on distributions
\pi_{\mathbf{z}}

(and other objects)
to denote the dependence on the dataset
\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{Z}^{n}
.

Given a multi-index
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{d})\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
,
we define
|\alpha|=\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{d}
.
For any function
\phi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
we define the short hand derivative notation

\partial_{\alpha}\phi(x)=(\partial_{x_{1}})^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots(\partial_{x_{d}})^{\alpha_{d}}\phi(x)
.
For all
k,\ell\in\mathbb{N}
,
we also define the function norm

\|\phi\|_{k,\ell}:=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\alpha|\leq k\end{subarray}}\,\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\partial_{\alpha}\phi(x)|(1+|x|^{\ell})^{-1}.

This leads to the natural function space

C^{k}_{\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):=\{\phi\in C^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):\|\phi\|_{k,\ell}<\infty\}
.

We are now ready to state the main assumptions on
the loss function
f(x,z)
.

Assumption 2.1

(Regularity)
.

There exists positive integers
N,\ell

such that
f(\cdot,z)\in C^{6N+2}_{\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and

\sup_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\|f(\cdot,z)\|_{6N+2,\ell}<\infty
.
Furthermore, there exists a constant
M>0
such that

\left|\nabla f(x,z)-\nabla f(y,z)\right|\leq M\left|x-y\right|,\quad\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,.

(2.1)

Without loss of generality,
this assumption implies
for each
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
with
|\alpha|\leq 6N+2
,
there exists a constant
M_{\alpha}>0
such that

\sup_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}|\partial_{\alpha}f(x,z)|\leq M_{\alpha}(1+|x|^{\ell})
.
For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we define

M_{k}=\sum_{|\alpha|=k}M_{\alpha}
.

Assumption 2.2

(Dissipative)
.

There exist constants
m>0
and
b\geq 0
such that

\langle x,\nabla f(x,z)\rangle\geq m\left|x\right|^{2}-b,\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},z\in\mathcal{Z}.

(2.2)

We remark this is a commonly used sufficient condition
for exponential convergence of Langevin diffusion

(
Raginsky et al. 2017
;
Bakry et al. 2008
)
,
and it can be replaced by any other sufficient condition

(
Villani 2009
;
Bakry et al. 2013
)
.

We define stochastic gradient Langevin dynamics (SGLD)
by the following update rule

X_{k+1}=X_{k}-\eta\nabla F_{\zeta_{k}}(X_{k})+\sqrt{\frac{2\eta}{\beta}}\xi_{k}\,,

(2.3)

where
X_{0}=x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
a deterministic initial condition,

\eta>0
is a constant step size (or learning rate),

\beta>0
is the inverse temperature parameter,

\{\zeta_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}

are uniform (minibatch) subsamples of
{\mathbf{z}}

(with replacement) of size
n_{b}\leq n
,
and
\{\xi_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
are
i.i.d. samples from
\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
.
Here we let
\{\zeta_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}},\{\xi_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be independent
conditioned on
{\mathbf{z}}
.

We are ready to state our first main result.

Theorem 2.3

(Approximation of SGLD)
.

Suppose
f(x,z)
satisfies
2.1
and
2.2

with order of approximation
N\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then there exist positive constants
C,\lambda,\ell^{\prime}
(depending on
N
),
such for all step sizes
0<\eta<\frac{2m}{M^{2}}

and
\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{Z}^{n}
,
we can construct a modified stationary measure
\pi^{N}_{\mathbf{z}}
,
with the property that
for all steps
k\geq 0
, initial condition
X_{0}=x
,
and test function
\phi\in C^{6N+2}_{\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
the following approximation bound on
the SGLD algorithm
\{X_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
(
2.3
) holds

\left|\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}}\,\phi(X_{k})-\pi^{N}_{\mathbf{z}}(\phi)\,\right|\leq C\left(e^{-\lambda k\eta/2}+\eta^{N}\right)(1+|x|)^{\ell^{\prime}}\,\|\phi-\rho_{\mathbf{z}}(\phi)\|_{6N+2,\ell}\,.

(2.4)

In particular, the above result holds for
\phi\in\{F,F_{\mathbf{z}}\}
.

The full proof can be found in
Section

5
.

Our second main result is on bounding the generalization error
of the approximate stationary distribution.
Here we note that beyond SGLD,
many discretizations of Langevin diffusion admits
a higher order approximate stationary distribution
\pi^{N}_{\mathbf{z}}

via the same weak backward error analysis construction.
In particular, the implicit Euler method
(
Kopec 2013
)

and weak Runge-Kutta methods
(
Laurent and Vilmart 2020
)
are well studied.
Therefore, we state the result for all such methods.

Theorem 2.4

(Generalization Bound of
\pi^{N}_{\mathbf{z}}
)
.

Suppose
\{X_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
is any discretization
of Langevin diffusion (
1.3
)
with an approximate stationary distribution
\pi^{N}_{\mathbf{z}}

as in
Theorem

2.3
.
Then there exists a constant
C>0
(depending on
N
),
such that for all choices of
k,n
and
\eta\in(0,1)

the following expected generalization bound holds

\left|\,\mathbb{E}\left[\pi^{N}_{\mathbf{z}}(F)-\pi^{N}_{\mathbf{z}}(F_{\mathbf{z}})\right]\,\right|\leq\frac{C}{n(1-\eta)}\,,

(2.5)

where the expectation is with respect to

\mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}
.

The full proof can be found in
Section

6
.
As a corollary of the two results,
we have a runtime complexity as follows.

Corollary 2.5

(Runtime Complexity)
.

Suppose
\{X_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
is any discretization
of Langevin diffusion (
1.3
)
admiting an approximate stationary distribution
\pi^{N}_{\mathbf{z}}

of the type in
Theorem

2.3
.
Then there exists a constant
C>0
(depending on
N
),
such that for all

\epsilon>0\,,\quad 0<\eta<\min\left\{\frac{2m}{M^{2}}\,,C\epsilon^{1/N}\right\}\,,\quad n\geq\frac{C}{\epsilon(1-\eta)}\,,\quad k\geq\frac{C}{\epsilon^{1/N}}\log\frac{1}{\epsilon}\,,

(2.6)

we achieve the following expected generalization bound

\left|\,\mathbb{E}\left[F(X_{k})-F_{\mathbf{z}}(X_{k})\right]\,\right|\leq\epsilon\,.

(2.7)

The proof can be found in
Section

7
.
Once again, we remark this implies a runtime complexity of

k\geq\Omega(\epsilon^{-1/N}\log(\epsilon^{-1}))

despite
\{X_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
being a first order discretization
of Langevin diffusion.

Proof Overview

In this section,
we provide a sketch of the main results.
Here we omit most of the technical details,
with the goal of explaining the core ideas clearly and concisely.

3.1
Construction of
\pi^{N}_{\mathbf{z}}

Before we go to the distributional setting,
it is instructive to build intuitions from ODEs.
In particular, we return to

(
Hairer et al. 2006
, Chapter IX, Example 1.1)
,
where we consider solving
y^{\prime}(t)=y(t)^{2}

with the Euler update
y_{k+1}=y_{k}+\eta y_{k}^{2}

for some
\eta>0
.
Following this example, we hypothesize the existence of
a modified ODE as the formal series

\widetilde{y}^{\prime}(t)=\widetilde{y}(t)^{2}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}c_{\ell}(\widetilde{y}(t))\,\eta^{\ell}\,,

(3.1)

such that
\widetilde{y}(0)=y_{k}
and
\widetilde{y}(\eta)=y_{k+1}
.
If
\widetilde{y}
has a Taylor expansion satisfying
the constraint
\widetilde{y}(\eta)=\widetilde{y}(0)+\eta\widetilde{y}(0)^{2}
,
we can solve for all the coefficients
c_{\ell}(\widetilde{y}(t))

by matching the terms with the same polynomial order of
\eta^{\ell}
.

Observe that if this formal series converges,
we have an exact reconstruction of the Euler method

\{y_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
via a modified ODE.
However, since this is often not easy (or even possible),
we alternatively consider a truncation of this series,
leading to a high order approximation.
This is exactly the approach known as backward error analysis

(
Hairer et al. 2006
)
.
We plot this particular example in
Figure

2
.

Once again, we emphasize that extending this method directly through pathwise
approximation of the diffusion will be difficult
(
Shardlow 2006
)
.
However, if we forego the pathwise information of
the Langevin diffusion
\{X(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
(
1.3
),
and only consider its distribution via
\mathbb{E}\phi(X(t))

for some test function
\phi
,
we can study the evolution via the Kolmogorov backward equation

\partial_{t}u(t,x)=L_{\mathbf{z}}\,u(t,x)\,,\quad u(0,x)=\phi(x)\,,

(3.2)

where
L_{\mathbf{z}}\,u:=\langle-\nabla F_{\mathbf{z}},\nabla u\rangle+\frac{1}{\beta}\Delta u
is the Itô generator,
and
u(t,x)=\mathbb{E}[\phi(X(t))|X_{0}=x]

is the well known stochastic representation

(
Pardoux and Răşcanu 2014
, Theorem 3.43)
.

Since the evolution of Langevin diffusion’s distribution
can be interpreted as Wasserstein gradient flow
in the space of probability distributions

(
Jordan et al. 1998
)
,
it is natural to consider extending
backward error analysis in the space of distributions.
Indeed, this is the approach taken by
Debussche and Faou 2011

using a modified PDE instead.

Similar to
Equation

3.1
,
we instead write down a modified Kolmogorov equation
as a formal series

\partial_{t}v=L_{\mathbf{z}}v+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\eta^{\ell}\,L_{\ell,\mathbf{z}}\,v\,,\quad v(0,x)=\phi(x)\,,

(3.3)

where
\{L_{\ell,\mathbf{z}}\}_{\ell\geq 1}
are differential operators
playing the same role as the coefficients
c_{\ell}
in
Equation

3.1
.
These operators are solved such that an Itô type Taylor expansion
matches the distribution of SGLD in the sense

v(\eta,x)=\mathbb{E}[\phi(X_{1})|X_{0}=x]
formally,
where
X_{1}
is the one step update of
Equation

2.3

and
\phi
is the same test function as in
Equation

3.3
.

Once again, this formal series is an exact reconstruction
of SGLD’s evolution if the series is convergent.
Similar to the modified equation for ODEs,
we avoid justifying the convergence by
taking a truncation at order
N
,
matching the regularity condition of
2.1
,
which is required for the Taylor type expansion in
\eta
.
More precisely, we construct the truncation using
functions
v_{m}(t,x)
defined recursively as
the solutions of the following equations

\partial_{t}v_{m}-L_{\mathbf{z}}v_{m}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}L_{\ell,\mathbf{z}}v_{m-\ell}\,,\quad v_{m}(0,x)=\begin{cases}\phi(x)\,,&m=0\,,\\
0\,,&m>0\,.\end{cases}

(3.4)

Using this construction,
we show that the truncation of
v
defined by

v^{(N)}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{N}\eta^{\ell}v_{\ell}

approximates
\mathbb{E}[\phi(X_{1})|X_{0}=x]

up to an error of
O(\eta^{N+1})

.

Next, we establish the convergence of the truncation terms
v_{m}

via a standard spectral gap argument
for non-homogenous parabolic PDEs
(
Pardoux et al. 2003
)
.
More precisely, we can write

\left|v_{m}(t,x)-\lim_{s\to\infty}v_{m}(s,x)\right|\leq C\,e^{-\lambda t/2}\,,

(3.5)

where
\lambda>0
is the Poincaré constant
arising as a consequence of
2.2

(
Bakry et al. 2008
;
Raginsky et al. 2017
)
.
By standard adjoint equation arguments

(
Pavliotis 2014
, Theorem 2.2)
,
we can recover the stationary measure from
(
3.4
)
by setting
\partial_{t}v_{m}=0
and
replacing all differential operators with their
corresponding adjoint.
More precisely, we have

\lim_{t\to\infty}v_{m}(t,x)=\rho_{\mathbf{z}}(\phi\,\mu_{m,\mathbf{z}})
,
where the Radon-Nikodym derivative
\mu_{m,\mathbf{z}}(x)

is the unique solution of the Poisson equation

L_{\mathbf{z}}\mu_{m,\mathbf{z}}=-\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}L_{\ell,\mathbf{z}}^{*}\mu_{m-\ell,\mathbf{z}}\,,

(3.6)

where we use
L_{\ell,\mathbf{z}}^{*}
to denote
the adjoint operator of
L_{\ell,\mathbf{z}}
in
L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}})
,
and
\mu_{0,\mathbf{z}}=1
.
Observe the above equation is exactly
stationary adjoint of (
3.4
),
where we note
L_{\mathbf{z}}^{*}=L_{\mathbf{z}}
is self-adjoint.

Using linearity of expectation over
\rho_{\mathbf{z}}
,
we can write

\lim_{t\to\infty}v^{(N)}(t,x)=\rho_{\mathbf{z}}\left(\phi\,\sum_{\ell=0}^{N}\eta^{\ell}\,\mu_{\ell,\mathbf{z}}\right)\,,

(3.7)

hence we recover the approximate stationary measure as

\pi^{N}_{\mathbf{z}}=\rho_{\mathbf{z}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{N}\eta^{\ell}\mu_{\ell,\mathbf{z}}
.

Finally, to extend the one step error bound of
O(\eta^{N+1})

to arbitrary steps, we use a telescoping argument to write

\displaystyle\left|\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}}\,\phi(X_{k})-v^{(N)}(t,x)\,\right|

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\,\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\,\left|\mathbb{E}[v^{(N)}((\ell-1)\eta,X_{k-\ell+1})-v^{(N)}(\ell\eta,X_{k-\ell})|\mathcal{F}_{k-\ell}]\right|

(3.8)

\displaystyle\leq C\,\eta^{N+1}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}e^{-\lambda\ell\eta/2}\,,

where
\{\mathcal{F}_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}

is the filtration generated by the algorithm
\{X_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
.
Here, we used the one step error bound
and exponential convergence (
3.5
).
Note that we can bound the geometric series by

\frac{1}{1-e^{-\lambda\eta/2}}=O(\eta^{-1})
,
hence leading to the error order of
O(\eta^{N})

in
Theorem

2.3
.
We plot an example construction of
\pi^{1}_{\mathbf{z}}
,
where we can compute the density explicitly in
Figure

2
.

3.2
Generalization Bound

We start by recalling the definition and generalization property of
the uniform stability
(
Bousquet and Elisseeff 2002
)
for distributions.

Definition 3.1

(Uniform Stability)
.

A collection of distributions
\{\pi_{\mathbf{z}}\}

on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
indexed by
\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{Z}^{n}

is said to be
\epsilon
-uniformly stable
if
for all
\mathbf{z},\overline{\mathbf{z}}\in\mathcal{Z}^{n}

with only one differing coordinate

\sup_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\left|\pi_{\mathbf{z}}(f(\cdot,z))-\pi_{\overline{\mathbf{z}}}(f(\cdot,z))\right|\leq\epsilon.

(3.9)

Proposition 3.2

(Generalization)
.

Suppose the collection of distributions
\{\pi_{\mathbf{z}}\}

is
\epsilon
-uniformly stable,
and that for all
(\mathbf{z},z)\in\mathcal{Z}^{n+1}
,
we also have
f(\cdot,z)\in L^{1}(\pi_{\mathbf{z}})
.
Then the expected generalization error of
\{\pi_{\mathbf{z}}\}

is bounded by
\epsilon
, or more precisely

\left|\,\mathbb{E}\left[\pi_{\mathbf{z}}(F_{\mathbf{z}})-\pi_{\mathbf{z}}(F)\right]\,\right|\leq\epsilon\,.

(3.10)

Additional details can be found in
Appendix

A
.
With this approach in mind, we introduce several additional notations.
Without loss of generality,
we let
\mathbf{z}=\{z_{1},\cdots,z_{i},\cdots,z_{n}\}

and
\overline{\mathbf{z}}=\{z_{1},\cdots,\overline{z}_{i},\cdots,z_{n}\}

such that they only differ in the
i^{\text{th}}
coordinate.
We also define
\mathbf{z}^{(i)}:=\mathbf{z}\cap\overline{\mathbf{z}}

and extend previous notations

F_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{n}f(x,z_{i})\,,\quad\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}}:=\frac{1}{Z^{(i)}}\,\exp(-\beta F_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})\,,\quad q_{z_{i}}:=\frac{Z^{(i)}}{Z}\,\exp\left(-\frac{\beta}{n}f(x,z_{i})\right)\,,

(3.11)

such that we can write
F_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}}+f(\cdot,z_{i})=F_{\mathbf{z}}
,

\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}}q_{z_{i}}=\rho_{\mathbf{z}}
,
and vice versa for the
\overline{z}_{i}
and
\overline{\mathbf{z}}
terms.
We also define the norm

\|\phi\|_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}:=\left[\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}}(\phi^{2})\right]^{1/2}
.

We start by putting
the two integrals with respect to

\pi^{N}_{\mathbf{z}},\pi^{N}_{\overline{\mathbf{z}}}

under one integral with respect to
\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}}
,
and use triangle and Cauchy-Schwarz inequalities to get

\displaystyle\left|\pi_{\mathbf{z}}(f)-\pi_{\overline{\mathbf{z}}}(f)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}}\left(f\sum_{\ell=0}^{N}\eta^{\ell}\,(\mu_{\ell,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}-\mu_{\ell,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}q_{\overline{z}_{i}})\right)\right|

(3.12)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell=0}^{N}\eta^{\ell}\,\|f\|_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}\,\|\mu_{\ell,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}-\mu_{\ell,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}q_{\overline{z}_{i}}\|_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}\,.

This implies that it is sufficient to bound the norm

\|\mu_{\ell,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}-\mu_{\ell,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}q_{\overline{z}_{i}}\|_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}

to achieve uniform stability (
Lemma

6.2
).
At the same time, we recall from (
3.6
)
that
\mu_{m,\mathbf{z}},\mu_{m,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}

solve very similar Poisson equations

\displaystyle L_{\mathbf{z}}\mu_{m,\mathbf{z}}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}L_{\ell,\mathbf{z}}^{*}\,\mu_{m-\ell,\mathbf{z}}=:G_{m,\mathbf{z}}\,,

(3.13)

\displaystyle L_{\overline{\mathbf{z}}}\mu_{m,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}L_{\ell,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}^{*}\,\mu_{m-\ell,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}=:G_{m,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}\,,

where we observe the left hand side operator only differs by

(L_{\mathbf{z}}-L_{\overline{\mathbf{z}}})\mu=\frac{1}{n}\langle\nabla f(x,\overline{z}_{i})-\nabla f(x,z_{i}),\nabla\mu\rangle
,
which is of order
O(\frac{1}{n})
.
Furthermore, the right hand side only depends on

\mu_{\ell,\mathbf{z}},\mu_{\ell,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}
for
\ell<m
.
This suggests the following induction structure

\displaystyle\mu_{\ell,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}-\mu_{\ell,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}q_{\overline{z}_{i}}=O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\text{ for all }{{0\leq\ell\leq m-1}}

(3.14)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle G_{m,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}-G_{m,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}q_{\overline{z}_{i}}=O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\mu_{\ell,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}-\mu_{\ell,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}q_{\overline{z}_{i}}=O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\text{ for all }{{0\leq\ell\leq m}}\,,

where we observe the induction step incremented
the set of
\ell\in\{0,1,\cdots,m-1\}
to
\{0,1,\cdots,m\}
.

To complete the proof of uniform stability,
we need to make the above sketch precise.
This requires the control of norms for higher order derivatives
of
\mu
and
G
terms,
which is detailed in
Lemma

6.7
.

4
Discussion

On the Poincaré Constant
.
Without a careful analysis, a non-convex potential
f(\cdot,z)
generally
lead to a Poincaré constant with exponentially poor dependence on
the inverse temperature
\beta
and dimension
d

(
Raginsky et al. 2017
)
.
However, there are many useful applications with universal Poincaré constants
(independent of
\beta,d
).
Most famously, when
F
is strongly convex,
we can use the Bakry-Émery curvature condition
to achieve an universal constant
(
Bakry et al. 2013
)
.

Cattiaux and Guillin 2021
and references within have studied
many perturbations of convex potential
f

with applications to Bayesian inference.

Menz and Schlichting 2014
;
Li and Erdogdu 2020

have also extended universal Poincaré constant to non-convex
f
,
where all critical points are either strict saddle
or the unique secondary order stationary point.
In particular, this class now contains the Burer–Monteiro relaxation
of semidefinite programs
(
Burer and Monteiro 2003
;
Boumal et al. 2016
)
.

On the Smoothness Conditions
.
In
2.1
, we assumed
f(\cdot,z)\in C^{6N+2}
.
Indeed, without additional smoothness,
there is a lower bound on the runtime complexity

(
Cao et al. 2020
)
,
and therefore higher order analysis is not appropriate in this setting.
In fact, higher order discretizations in general require higher order smoothness

(
Hairer et al. 2006
)
.
Therefore, any application that calls for a higher order discretization
can be studied using the approximation method of this work.
Furthermore, we remark that one can always consider smoothing
the Gibbs distribution via convolution with a Gaussian

(
Chaudhari et al. 2019
;
Block et al. 2020
)
,
which leads to an infinitely smooth potential.

On Further Extensions
.
While this article is focused on the analysis of generalization error,
the framework can be extended to other analyses of interest.
In general, weak backward error analysis saves the approximation error
between the discrete time algorithm to the diffusion process.
Therefore, any property of the algorithm can be studied via
the approximate stationary distribution
\pi^{N}_{\mathbf{z}}
.
For example, the expected suboptimality of Langevin discretizations
is often analyzed via the Gibbs distribution

(
Raginsky et al. 2017
;
Erdogdu et al. 2018
;
Li and Erdogdu 2020
)
,
which implies an opportunity to analyze
the suboptimality of
\pi^{N}_{\mathbf{z}}
instead.

Here, we adopt the notation

We start by stating a few technical estimates required for the main result.

Proposition 5.1

(Moment Estimates on the Continuous Process)

where we observe

For this proof, we followed similar arguments from

Let

The moments estimates, together with the Markov property,

The next result corresponds to a small modification of

Before we state the result, we will define the semi-norm

|\phi|_{l,k}:=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{d}\\
0<|\alpha|\leq l\end{subarray}}\,\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\partial_{\alpha}\phi(x)|(1+|x|^{k})^{-1}.

Proposition 5.4

(Asymptotic Expansion for SGLD)
.

Let
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{\pol}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
then for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
there exists an integer
l_{2N+2}

such that
\phi\in C^{2N+2}_{l_{2N+2}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Let
\{X_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}

be the discrete time SGLD algorithm from

.
Then for every integer

,
there exist differential operators

of order

with coefficients from

,
such that for all integer

and integer

where in particular we have

and

.

The proof can be found in
Section

5.3
.

The rest of the results will follow directly from
the steps of
Kopec 2013
,
which is an extension of
Debussche and Faou 2011

from a torus to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
We will sketch the steps here,
and check the conditions that leads to the results
of
Kopec 2013
.

Specifically, we will construct operators
\{L_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}

such that the operator

\mathcal{L}:=L+\eta L_{1}+\cdots+\eta^{j}L_{j}+\cdots\,,

satisfies the following identity in a formal sense

\exp(\eta\mathcal{L})=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{\eta^{\ell}}{\ell!}(L+\eta L_{1}+\eta^{2}+L_{2}+\cdots)^{\ell}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\eta^{j}A_{j}\,.

(5.2)

Observe that it is sufficient to the coefficients
to each term of the power series, i.e.
\eta^{j}
.
Using a formal series inverse approach from

Hairer et al. 2006
,
we obtain the following formal equivalence

L_{j}=A_{j+1}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{j}\frac{B_{\ell}}{\ell!}\sum_{n_{1}+\cdots+n_{\ell+1}=n-\ell}L_{n_{1}}\cdots L_{n_{\ell}}A_{n_{\ell+1}+1}\,,

(5.3)

where
\{B_{\ell}\}
are the Bernoulli numbers.

Using this construction, we can define the following modified PDE
as before in (
3.3

\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}=(L_{\mathbf{z}}+\eta L_{1,\mathbf{z}}+\eta^{2}L_{2,\mathbf{z}}+\cdots)v,\quad v(0,x)=\phi(x),

(5.4)

such that we can write the formal solution as

v(\eta,x)=e^{\eta\mathcal{L}}\phi(x).

Using the formal equivalence for the operators in

5.2
),
we can obtain the formal equivalence of the solutions as well, i.e.

v(\eta,x)=e^{\eta\mathcal{L}}\phi(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\eta^{j}A_{j}\phi(x)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}}[\phi(X_{1})|X_{0}=x].

Finally, we can study the stationary distribution

\pi_{\mathbf{z}}=\mu_{\mathbf{z}}\rho_{\mathbf{z}}
by writing down
the adjoint equation that
\mu_{\mathbf{z}}
must satisfy

\mathcal{L}^{*}\mu_{\mathbf{z}}=0,

where
\mathcal{L}^{*}
is the adjoint operator
with respect to

.

While the above construction is formal,

Debussche and Faou 2011
;

these statements precise for truncated series
by proving error bounds with the desired order in

.
In particular, the same authors constructed the truncation
by the following decomposition of
\pi^{N}_{\mathbf{z}}

\pi^{N}_{\mathbf{z}}=\rho_{\mathbf{z}}\mu^{N}_{\mathbf{z}}:=\rho_{\mathbf{z}}(1+\eta\mu_{1}+\cdots+\eta^{N}\mu_{N}),

(5.5)

and if each
\mu_{k}
satisfies the Poisson equation

L\mu_{k}=-\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}L_{\ell}^{*}\mu_{k-\ell}\,,

(5.6)

then we can show that

(L^{*}+\eta L_{1}^{*}+\cdots+\eta^{N}L_{N}^{*})\mu^{N}=\mathcal{O}(\eta^{N+1}),

which is sufficient close to the desired truncation to preserve
the order of approximation error.

To summarize, we will restate and prove the main result
of

We provide a sufficient condition for uniform stability,

2.1

6.2
Poisson Equation Results

Before we start the proofs, we will state a result of existence, uniqueness, and polynomial growth estimate for solutions of Poisson equations adapted from
Pardoux and Veretennikov 2001
and
Pardoux et al. 2003
to fit our assumptions.

Proposition 6.8
.

Under
2.1
and
2.2
,
for Poisson equations of the form

L_{\mathbf{z}}u=G,

where
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}Gd\rho_{\mathbf{z}}=0
,
there exist a unique function

that solves this equation. Furthermore, if we have for some constants

such that

|G(x)|\leq C(1+|x|^{k}),

then for all

, there exists a constant
C^{\prime}>0
such that

|u(x)|+|\nabla u(x)|\leq C^{\prime}(1+|x|^{k^{\prime}}).

We note here that
Pardoux and Veretennikov 2001
;
Pardoux et al. 2003
proved the above result under much more general conditions, and it is straight forward to verify that our assumptions fall under a special case of the original result.

To get higher regularity,
we will first state standard a higher regularity result from for bounded domains.

Theorem 6.9
.

(
Evans 2010
, Section 6.3.1, Theorem 3, Infinite Differentiability in the Interior)

Let

be a bounded open domain with boundary
\partial U\in C^{1}
,

L
be a uniformly elliptic operator with smooth (
C^{\infty}(U)
) coefficients,

f\in C^{\infty}(U)
,
and
u
is a weak solution of the equation

Lu=f\,.

Then we have
u\in C^{\infty}(U)
.

We will adapt this classical result to our problem.

Proposition 6.10
.

Suppose
2.1
and
2.2

for Poisson equations of the form

L_{\mathbf{z}}u=G\,,

where
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}Gd\rho_{\mathbf{z}}=0
.
If additionally
F_{\mathbf{z}},G\in C^{\infty}_{\pol}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, then we have

u\in C^{\infty}_{\pol}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Proof.

Since we already have existence and uniqueness of solutions for the Poisson equation from Proposition
6.8
,
we can restrict the solution
u
to any open bounded domain
U
with smooth boundary.
Then using Theorem
6.9

\mathbb{R}^{d}
using open bounded domains with smooth boundary

, we obtain that

.

To show polynomial growth, we will consider an induction on

,
where

is a multi-index.
We start by observing that the

.
Assuming the case is true for

,
we will prove the case for

We start by computing

\partial_{\alpha}(L_{\mathbf{z}}u)=L_{\mathbf{z}}\partial_{\alpha}u+\sum_{l=1}^{J}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}=\alpha\\
|\alpha_{1}|=l\end{subarray}}(\partial_{\alpha_{1}}L_{\mathbf{z}})(\partial_{\alpha_{2}}u)=\partial_{\alpha}G\,.

This implies

Since all the
|\alpha_{2}|<k

Therefore

must also only have polynomial growth.
Finally, applying Proposition

we obtain that there exist constants

which is the desired result.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta}\left\|\nabla\left(\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}-\mu_{k,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}q_{\overline{z_{i}}}\right)\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}}\right)}

(6.12)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\lambda^{\prime}}\left\|G_{k,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}-G_{k,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}q_{\overline{z_{i}}}\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}}\right)}+\frac{1}{\beta}\left\|\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}\nabla q_{z_{i}}-\mu_{k,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}\nabla q_{\overline{z_{i}}}\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}}\right)}\,.

From the assumption in this Lemma and from the equality

\nabla q_{z_{i}}=\frac{-\beta}{n}\nabla f(x,z_{i})q_{z_{i}},

we conclude that

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla\left(\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}-\mu_{k,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}q_{\overline{z_{i}}}\right)\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}}\right)}\leq\frac{\beta}{\lambda^{\prime}}\frac{C_{G_{k}}}{n}+\frac{1}{n}\left\|h\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}}\right)},

(6.13)

where
h=\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}\nabla f(x,z_{i})q_{z_{i}}-\mu_{k,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}\nabla f(x,\overline{z_{i}})q_{\overline{z_{i}}}
,
and we can use Lemma
6.11

to bound the norm of
h
.
We conclude the proof using the Poincaré inequality.

∎

6.6
A Couple of Corollaries

Corollary 6.14

(Generalized Zeroth Order Energy Estimate)
.

If
u_{\mathbf{z}},u_{\overline{\mathbf{z}}},G_{\mathbf{z}},G_{\overline{\mathbf{z}}}
are known functions
that satisfy the pair of PDEs

\begin{cases}L_{\mathbf{z}}u_{\mathbf{z}}=G_{\mathbf{z}},\\
L_{\overline{\mathbf{z}}}u_{\overline{\mathbf{z}}}=G_{\overline{\mathbf{z}}},\end{cases}

and there exist constants
C_{\overline{u}}>0,C_{G}>0

independent of data and
n
, such that

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u_{\mathbf{z}}d\rho_{\mathbf{z}}-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u_{\overline{\mathbf{z}}}d\rho_{\overline{\mathbf{z}}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{\overline{u}}}{n}\,,

\displaystyle\|G_{\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}-G_{\overline{\mathbf{z}}}q_{\overline{z_{i}}}\|_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{G}}{n}\,.

Then there exists a new constant
C_{u}>0

such that

\|u_{\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}-u_{\overline{\mathbf{z}}}q_{\overline{z_{i}}}\|_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}\leq\frac{C_{u}}{n}\,.

Proof.

Observe the only condition from Lemma
6.4

that we fail to satisfy is that

\overline{u}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u_{\mathbf{z}}d\rho_{\mathbf{z}}\neq 0
.
As a result, we need to use the Poincaré inequality
on the centered function,
i.e. for all
g\in C^{\infty}_{\pol}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we have

\|g\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}=\|g-\overline{g}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}+(\overline{g})^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda^{\prime}}\|\nabla g\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}+(\overline{g})^{2}\,.

Letting
g=u_{\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}-u_{\overline{\mathbf{z}}}q_{\overline{z_{i}}}
,
it is then sufficient to bound
(\overline{g})^{2}
.
To complete the proof, we can rewrite
g

to match the assumption

(\overline{g})^{2}=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u_{\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}-u_{\overline{\mathbf{z}}}q_{\overline{z_{i}}}d\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}}\right)^{2}\leq\left(\frac{C_{\overline{u}}}{n}\right)^{2}\,,

which gives us the desired bound of

\|u_{\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}-u_{\overline{\mathbf{z}}}q_{\overline{z_{i}}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}\leq\frac{C_{u-\overline{u}}^{2}+C_{\overline{u}}^{2}}{n^{2}}\,,

where
C_{u-\overline{u}}
is the same as
C_{\mu_{k}}

from Lemma
6.4
.

∎

Corollary 6.15

(First Order Energy Estimate)
.

If there exist a constant
C_{G_{k}}>0
such that

\|G_{k,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}-G_{k,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}q_{\overline{z_{i}}}\|_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}\leq\frac{C_{G_{k}}}{n},

then there exist new constant
C_{\nabla\mu_{k}}>0
such that

\|\nabla\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}-\nabla\mu_{k,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}q_{\overline{z_{i}}}\|_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}\leq\frac{C_{\nabla\mu_{k}}}{n}.

Proof.

We start by using the product rule

\nabla\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}=\nabla(\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}})-\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}\nabla q_{z_{i}}

to write

\displaystyle\|\nabla\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}-\nabla\mu_{k,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}q_{\overline{z_{i}}}\|_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}

(6.14)

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla(\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}-\mu_{k,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}q_{\overline{z_{i}}})\|_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}+\|\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}\nabla q_{z_{i}}-\mu_{k,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}\nabla q_{\overline{z_{i}}}\|_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}

\displaystyle=:T_{1}+T_{2}.

Now observe that in the proof of Lemma
6.4
,
we already proved a bound for

T_{1}=\|\nabla g\|_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}
,
which we can write as

T_{1}\leq\frac{(\lambda^{\prime})^{1/2}C_{\mu_{k}}}{n}.

To control
T_{2}
, we simply need to compute

\nabla q_{z_{i}}=\frac{-\beta}{n}\nabla f(x,z_{i})q_{z_{i}}
to get

T_{2}\leq\frac{\beta}{n}\|\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}\nabla f(x,z_{i})q_{z_{i}}-\mu_{k,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}\nabla f(x,\overline{z_{i}})q_{\overline{z_{i}}}\|_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}.

Denoting
h=|\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}\nabla f(x,z_{i})q_{z_{i}}-\mu_{k,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}\nabla f(x,\overline{z_{i}})q_{\overline{z_{i}}}|
,
we can put the two bounds together and get
the desired result

\|\nabla\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}-\nabla\mu_{k,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}q_{\overline{z_{i}}}\|_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}\leq\frac{(\lambda^{\prime})^{1/2}C_{\mu_{k}}+\beta\|h\|_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}}{n},

where we can provide a bound on
h
using
Lemma
6.11
.

∎

6.7
Energy Estimate with Weighted Norm

Lemma 6.16

(Energy Estimate with
C^{\infty}_{\pol}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
Coefficient)
.

If for all
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{\pol}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
there exists a constant
C_{\phi G_{k}}>0
such that

\|\phi G_{k,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}-\phi G_{k,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}q_{\overline{z_{i}}}\|_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}\leq\frac{C_{\phi G_{k}}}{n},

then there exists a new constant
C_{\phi\mu_{k}}>0

depending on
\phi
such that

\|\phi\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}-\phi\mu_{k,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}q_{\overline{z_{i}}}\|_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}\leq\frac{C_{\phi\mu_{k}}}{n}.

Proof.

Step 1. Reduction to a Recursive Argument on the Polynomial Degree

We will start by making the observation that
since
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{\pol}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
there exist constants
C_{\phi}>0,k_{\phi}\in\mathbb{N}

such that

|\phi(x)|\leq C_{\phi}\left(1+\sum_{j=1}^{d}|x_{j}|^{k_{\phi}}\right)=:\widehat{\phi}(x)\,.

Then observe it is sufficient to bound
the case where
\phi(x)
is exactly
a polynomial of the
\widehat{\phi}(x)
type, i.e.

\|\phi\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}-\phi\mu_{k,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}q_{\overline{z_{i}}}\|_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}\leq\|\widehat{\phi}(\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}-\mu_{k,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}q_{\overline{z_{i}}})\|_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}.

The choice for the form of
\widehat{\phi}
has
a couple of advantages.
Observe that for any first order
differential operator
\partial_{j}
,
we have that

|\partial_{j}\widehat{\phi}|=C_{\phi}k_{\phi}|x_{j}|^{k_{\phi}-1}

only depends on a single coordinate.
Furthermore,
|\partial_{j}^{k_{\phi}}\widehat{\phi}|=C_{\phi}(k_{\phi}!)

is also a constant.

This implies it is sufficient to prove a bound of the form

\displaystyle\|\nabla(\widehat{\phi}(\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}-\mu_{k,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}q_{\overline{z_{i}}}))\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}

(6.15)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\widehat{C}_{1,\phi}}{n^{2}}+\widehat{C}_{2,\phi}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\|\nabla(\partial_{j}\widehat{\phi}(\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}-\mu_{k,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}q_{\overline{z_{i}}}))\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}

\displaystyle+\widehat{C}_{3,\phi}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\|\nabla(\partial_{j}^{2}\widehat{\phi}(\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}-\mu_{k,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}q_{\overline{z_{i}}}))\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}.

Observe that since
\partial_{j}\widehat{\phi},\partial_{j}^{2}\widehat{\phi}

are lower degree polynomials,
we can recursively apply the above bound to
all terms involving
\widehat{\phi}

until they vanish,
hence recovering the desired result using Lemma
6.4
.
In particular, when
\widehat{\phi}
is a degree-
1
polynomial, the result follows directly from
Equation

6.15
.
From this point onwards, we will assume without loss of generality

\phi
is a polynomial of the form
\widehat{\phi}

defined previously.

Step 2. Energy Estimate

To prove the desired bound in
Equation

6.15
,
we will follow the same proof structure as Lemma
6.4
,
and write down
the weak form of the Poisson equation in terms of
the quantity we want to bound.
To start, we will first compute

\displaystyle L_{\mathbf{z}}(\phi\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}})

\displaystyle=-\langle\nabla F_{\mathbf{z}},\nabla(\phi\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}})\rangle+\frac{1}{\beta}(\Delta\phi\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}+2\langle\nabla\phi,\nabla\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}\rangle+\phi\Delta\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}})

\displaystyle=\phi L_{\mathbf{z}}\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}+\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}L_{\mathbf{z}}\phi+\frac{2}{\beta}\langle\nabla\phi,\nabla\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\phi G_{k,\mathbf{z}}+\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}L_{\mathbf{z}}\phi+\frac{2}{\beta}\langle\nabla\phi,\nabla\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}\rangle

Then we once again write the equation in integral form
for any test function
g\in C^{\infty}_{\pol}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

\displaystyle\quad\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle\nabla g,\nabla(\phi\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}})\rangle d\rho_{\mathbf{z}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}-g\phi G_{k,\mathbf{z}}-g\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}L_{\mathbf{z}}\phi-\frac{2}{\beta}g\langle\nabla\phi,\nabla\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}\rangle d\rho_{\mathbf{z}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}-g\phi G_{k,\mathbf{z}}+\frac{1}{\beta}\langle\nabla(g\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}),\nabla\phi\rangle-\frac{2}{\beta}g\langle\nabla\phi,\nabla\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}\rangle d\rho_{\mathbf{z}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}-g\phi G_{k,\mathbf{z}}+\frac{1}{\beta}\langle\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}\nabla g,\nabla\phi\rangle-\frac{1}{\beta}\langle g\nabla\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}},\nabla\phi\rangle d\rho_{\mathbf{z}},

where we used Green’s Theorem in the third line above.

In the same way as Lemma
6.4
,
we will use the product-rule to write

\nabla(\phi\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}})q_{z_{i}}=\nabla(\phi\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}})-\phi\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}\nabla q_{z_{i}}
,
and get the equation in the following form

\displaystyle\quad\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle\nabla g,\nabla(\phi\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}})\rangle d\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}-g\phi G_{k,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}+\frac{1}{\beta}\langle\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}\nabla g,\nabla\phi\rangle-\frac{1}{\beta}\langle g\nabla\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}},\nabla\phi\rangle+\frac{1}{\beta}\langle\nabla g,\phi\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}\nabla q_{z_{i}}\rangle d\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}}.

We can choose
g=\phi(\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}-\mu_{k,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}q_{\overline{z_{i}}})
,
and taking the difference with the
\overline{\mathbf{z}}
equation
to get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle\nabla g,\nabla g\rangle d\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}-g\phi(G_{k,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}-G_{k,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}q_{\overline{z_{i}}})d\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}}

(6.16)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle\nabla g(\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}-\mu_{k,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}q_{\overline{z_{i}}}),\nabla\phi\rangle d\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle g(\nabla\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}-\nabla\mu_{k,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}q_{\overline{z_{i}}}),\nabla\phi\rangle d\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle\nabla g,\phi\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}\nabla q_{z_{i}}-\phi\mu_{k,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}\nabla q_{\overline{z_{i}}}\rangle d\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}}

\displaystyle=:T_{1}+T_{2}+T_{3}+T_{4}.

Step 3. Controlling Terms
T_{1},T_{2},T_{3},T_{4}

Since
\overline{g}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}gd\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}}\neq 0
,
we need to use a modified Poincaré inequality, namely

\|g\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda^{\prime}}\|\nabla g\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}+(\overline{g})^{2}.

(6.17)

Observe that we can apply the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality
to
\overline{g}
and get

\displaystyle|\overline{g}|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\phi||\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}-\mu_{k,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}q_{\overline{z_{i}}}|d\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}}

(6.18)

\displaystyle\leq\|\phi\|_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}\|\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}-\mu_{k,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}q_{\overline{z_{i}}}\|_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{\mu_{k}}}{n}\|\phi\|_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})},

where we used the result of Lemma
6.4
.
Here we note the norm of
\phi
can be bounded using Lemma
6.11
.

Returning to
Equation

6.16
,
we will control
T_{1}
using Young’s inequality and the assumption, i.e.

\displaystyle T_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2c_{y_{1}}}\|g\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}+\frac{c_{y_{1}}}{2}\|\phi(G_{k,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}-G_{k,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}q_{\overline{z_{i}}})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}

(6.19)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2c_{y_{1}}\lambda^{\prime}}\|\nabla g\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}+\frac{1}{2c_{y_{1}}}\frac{C_{\mu_{k}}^{2}}{n^{2}}\|\phi\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}+\frac{c_{y_{1}}}{2}\frac{C_{G_{k}}^{2}}{n^{2}},

where we also used the modified Poincaré inequality (
6.17
) above
with the
|\overline{g}|
bound.

To control
T_{2}
, we will apply Cauchy-Schwarz and Young’s inequalities
to separate
\nabla g
, i.e.

\displaystyle T_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla g||(\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}-\mu_{k,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}q_{\overline{z_{i}}})\nabla\phi|d\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\beta c_{y_{2}}}\|\nabla g\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}+\frac{c_{y_{2}}}{2\beta}\|(\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}-\mu_{k,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}q_{\overline{z_{i}}})\nabla\phi\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}.

To convert to the desired form of
Equation

6.15
,
we observe

\displaystyle\quad\|(\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}-\mu_{k,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}q_{\overline{z_{i}}})\nabla\phi\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}(\partial_{j}\phi)^{2}(\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}-\mu_{k,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}q_{\overline{z_{i}}})^{2}d\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\|\partial_{j}\phi(\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}-\mu_{k,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}q_{\overline{z_{i}}})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\lambda^{\prime}}\|\nabla(\partial_{j}\phi(\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}-\mu_{k,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}q_{\overline{z_{i}}}))\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}+\|\partial_{j}\phi\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}\frac{C_{\mu_{k}}^{2}}{n^{2}},

where we used the modified form of the Poincaré inequality (
6.17
)
and the bound on
|\overline{g}|
.
Putting everything together, we have the following bound

\displaystyle T_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\beta c_{y_{2}}}\|\nabla g\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}+\frac{c_{y_{2}}}{2\beta}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\lambda^{\prime}}\|\nabla(\partial_{j}\phi(\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}-\mu_{k,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}q_{\overline{z_{i}}}))\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}

(6.20)

\displaystyle+\frac{c_{y_{2}}}{2\beta}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\|\partial_{j}\phi\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}\frac{C_{\mu_{k}}^{2}}{n^{2}}.

To control
T_{3}
, we will apply a similar approach as
T_{2}
.
We will start with Young’s inequality
to isolate
g
first

\displaystyle T_{3}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{2c_{y_{3}}}g^{2}+\frac{c_{y_{3}}}{2}\langle\nabla\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}-\nabla\mu_{k,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}q_{\overline{z_{i}}},\nabla\phi\rangle^{2}d\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2c_{y_{3}}\lambda^{\prime}\beta}\|\nabla g\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}+\frac{c_{y_{3}}}{2\beta}\||\nabla\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}-\nabla\mu_{k,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}q_{\overline{z_{i}}}|\,|\nabla\phi|\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}.

Next we can rewrite in terms of a sum again

\||\nabla\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}-\nabla\mu_{k,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}q_{\overline{z_{i}}}|\,|\nabla\phi|\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\|\partial_{j}\phi(\nabla\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}-\nabla\mu_{k,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}q_{\overline{z_{i}}})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}.

Using the product rule, we can write

\nabla\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}\partial_{j}\phi=\nabla(\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}\partial_{j}\phi)-\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}\nabla(q_{z_{i}}\partial_{j}\phi),

then we use the inequality
(a+b)^{2}\leq 2a^{2}+2b^{2}
twice to write

\displaystyle\quad\|\partial_{j}\phi(\nabla\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}-\nabla\mu_{k,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}q_{\overline{z_{i}}})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}

\displaystyle\leq 2\|\nabla(\partial_{j}\phi\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}-\partial_{j}\phi\mu_{k,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}q_{\overline{z_{i}}})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}+2\|\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}\nabla(q_{z_{i}}\partial_{j}\phi)-\mu_{k,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}\nabla(q_{\overline{z_{i}}}\partial_{j}\phi)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}

\displaystyle\leq 2\|\nabla(\partial_{j}\phi\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}-\partial_{j}\phi\mu_{k,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}q_{\overline{z_{i}}})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}+4\|(\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}\nabla q_{z_{i}}-\mu_{k,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}\nabla q_{\overline{z_{i}}})\partial_{j}\phi\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}

\displaystyle\quad+4\|(\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}-\mu_{k,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}q_{\overline{z_{i}}})\nabla\partial_{j}\phi\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}.

Recalling
\nabla q_{z_{i}}=q_{z_{i}}\frac{-\beta}{n}\nabla f(x,z_{i})
,
we have

\displaystyle\quad\|(\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}\nabla q_{z_{i}}-\mu_{k,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}\nabla q_{\overline{z_{i}}})\partial_{j}\phi\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\beta^{2}}{n^{2}}\|(\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}\nabla f(x,z_{i})-\mu_{k,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}q_{\overline{z_{i}}}\nabla f(x,\overline{z_{i}}))\partial_{j}\phi\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}

\displaystyle=:\frac{\beta^{2}}{n^{2}}\|h_{1,j}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}.

Since
\partial_{j}\phi
is only a function of
x_{j}
,
we also have

\displaystyle\quad\|(\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}-\mu_{k,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}q_{\overline{z_{i}}})\nabla\partial_{j}\phi\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}

\displaystyle=\|(\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}-\mu_{k,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}q_{\overline{z_{i}}})\partial_{j}^{2}\phi\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\lambda^{\prime}}\|\nabla((\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}-\mu_{k,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}q_{\overline{z_{i}}})\partial_{j}^{2}\phi)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}+\|\partial_{j}^{2}\phi\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}\frac{C_{\mu_{k}}^{2}}{n^{2}}

Putting everything together, we have the following bound

\displaystyle T_{3}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2c_{y_{3}}\lambda^{\prime}\beta}\|\nabla g\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}+\frac{c_{y_{3}}}{2\beta}\sum_{j=1}^{d}2\|\nabla(\partial_{j}\phi\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}-\partial_{j}\phi\mu_{k,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}q_{\overline{z_{i}}})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}

(6.21)

\displaystyle+\frac{c_{y_{3}}}{2\beta}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(4\frac{\beta^{2}}{n^{2}}\|h_{1,j}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}+\frac{4}{\lambda^{\prime}}\|\nabla((\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}-\mu_{k,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}q_{\overline{z_{i}}})\partial_{j}^{2}\phi)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}})}+\frac{4C_{\mu_{k}}^{2}}{n^{2}}\right),

which satisfies the desired form in
Equation

6.15
.

Lastly we have control over
T_{4}
due to
the computation
\nabla q_{z_{i}}=q_{z_{i}}\frac{-\beta}{n}\nabla f(x,z_{i})
,
which leads to

\displaystyle T_{4}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{\beta}|\nabla g|\frac{\beta}{n}|\phi(\mu_{k,\mathbf{z}}q_{z_{i}}\nabla f(x,z_{i})-\mu_{k,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}q_{\overline{z_{i}}}\nabla f(x,\overline{z_{i}}))|d\rho_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}}

(6.22)

\frac{1}{n}\phi^{\alpha}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}}\bigg(\partial_{i_{1}}F_{\zeta_{0}}\cdots\partial_{i_{l}}F_{\zeta_{0}}-\partial_{i_{1}}F_{\overline{{\zeta_{0}}}}\cdots\partial_{i_{l}}F_{\overline{{\zeta_{0}}}}\bigg)\,.

To this end, we can use the decompositions

F_{\mathbf{z}}=F_{\mathbf{z}^{(i)}}+\frac{1}{n}f(x,z_{i}),\quad F_{\zeta_{0}}=F_{\zeta_{0}^{(i)}}+\frac{1}{n_{b}}f(x,z_{i})\mathds{1}_{B},

where
\mathds{1}
is the indicator function,
and the event

is defined as

B=\{z_{i}\in\zeta_{0}\}=\{\overline{z_{i}}\in\overline{\zeta_{0}}\}
.

This leads to

\displaystyle\quad\partial_{i_{1}}F_{\zeta_{0}}\cdots\partial_{i_{l}}F_{\zeta_{0}}-\partial_{i_{1}}F_{\overline{{\zeta_{0}}}}\cdots\partial_{i_{l}}F_{\overline{{\zeta_{0}}}}

\displaystyle=\partial_{i_{1}}F_{\zeta_{0}^{(i)}}(\partial_{i_{2}}F_{\zeta_{0}}\cdots\partial_{i_{l}}F_{\zeta_{0}}-\partial_{i_{2}}F_{\overline{\zeta_{0}}}\cdots\partial_{i_{l}}F_{\overline{\zeta_{0}}})

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{n_{b}}\mathds{1}_{B}\left(\partial_{i_{1}}f(x,z_{i})\partial_{i_{2}}F_{\zeta_{0}}\cdots\partial_{i_{l}}F_{\zeta_{0}}-\partial_{i_{1}}f(x,\overline{z_{i}})\partial_{i_{2}}F_{\overline{\zeta_{0}}}\cdots\partial_{i_{l}}F_{\overline{\zeta_{0}}}\right),

where we observe that the terms inside the second bracket

-independent.
If we take the expectation
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}}
on these terms,
we will be averaging over

and only
\frac{n_{b}}{n}\binom{n}{n_{b}}=\binom{n-1}{n_{b}-1}

of these terms will be non-zero

\mathds{1}_{B}
.

Hence if we let
\phi_{\zeta_{0}}
be any
n
-independent function,
we will have

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}}\frac{1}{n_{b}}\mathds{1}_{B}\phi_{\zeta_{0}}=\frac{1}{n_{b}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\zeta_{0}\subset\mathbf{z}}\mathds{1}_{B}\phi_{\zeta_{0}}=\frac{1}{n}\widetilde{\phi},

(6.25)

where
\widetilde{\phi}
is also
n
-independent.

We can continue taking the expansion to get

\displaystyle\quad\partial_{i_{1}}F_{\zeta_{0}}\cdots\partial_{i_{l}}F_{\zeta_{0}}-\partial_{i_{1}}F_{\overline{{\zeta_{0}}}}\cdots\partial_{i_{l}}F_{\overline{{\zeta_{0}}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{b}}\mathds{1}_{B}\sum_{j=1}^{l}\partial_{i_{1}}F_{\zeta_{0}^{(i)}}\cdots\partial_{i_{j-1}}F_{\zeta_{0}^{(i)}}\partial_{i_{j}}f(x,z_{i})\partial_{i_{j+1}}F_{\zeta_{0}}\cdots\partial_{i_{l}}F_{\zeta_{0}}

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{n_{b}}\mathds{1}_{B}\sum_{j=1}^{l}\partial_{i_{1}}F_{\zeta_{0}^{(i)}}\cdots\partial_{i_{j-1}}F_{\zeta_{0}^{(i)}}\partial_{i_{j}}f(x,\overline{z_{i}})\partial_{i_{j+1}}F_{\overline{\zeta_{0}}}\cdots\partial_{i_{l}}F_{\overline{\zeta_{0}}}

\displaystyle=:\frac{1}{n_{b}}\mathds{1}_{B}\phi^{\alpha}_{\zeta_{0},\overline{\zeta_{0}}}\,,

where the sum follows from
recursively applying the first step to the terms

\partial_{i_{1}}F_{\zeta_{0}^{(i)}}(\partial_{i_{2}}F_{\zeta_{0}}\cdots\partial_{i_{l}}F_{\zeta_{0}}-\partial_{i_{2}}F_{\overline{\zeta_{0}}}\cdots\partial_{i_{l}}F_{\overline{\zeta_{0}}})
.

Computing the expectation using Equation (
6.25
),
we get that

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}}\frac{1}{n_{b}}\mathds{1}_{B}\phi^{\alpha}_{\zeta_{0},\overline{\zeta_{0}}}=\frac{1}{n}\phi^{\alpha}.

This implies we can define the desired operator as

\widehat{A}_{N}=\sum_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}\phi^{\alpha}\partial_{\alpha},

hence completing the proof for the first step.

Step 2. Finding
\widehat{L}_{N}

We will prove by induction over
N

that there exists an operator
\widehat{L}_{N}

independent of
n

\frac{1}{n}\widehat{L}_{N}=L_{N,\mathbf{z}}-L_{N,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}

For the

Now assuming the case for
N-1
,
we will prove the statement for case
N
.
We first recall the definition of
L_{N}

L_{N}=A_{N+1}+\sum_{l=1}^{N}\frac{B_{l}}{l!}\sum_{n_{1}+\cdots n_{l+1}=n-l}L_{n_{1}}\cdots L_{n_{l}}A_{n_{l+1}+1}\,.

,

\displaystyle\quad L_{n_{1},\mathbf{z}}\cdots L_{n_{l},\mathbf{z}}A_{n_{l+1}+1,\mathbf{z}}-L_{n_{1},\overline{\mathbf{z}}}\cdots L_{n_{l},\overline{\mathbf{z}}}A_{n_{l+1}+1,\overline{\mathbf{z}}}

\displaystyle\quad L_{n_{1},\mathbf{z}}\cdots L_{n_{l},\mathbf{z}}-L_{n_{1},\overline{\mathbf{z}}}\cdots L_{n_{l},\overline{\mathbf{z}}}

\displaystyle=(L_{n_{1},\mathbf{z}}-L_{n_{1},\overline{\mathbf{z}}})L_{n_{2},\mathbf{z}}\cdots L_{n_{l},\mathbf{z}}+L_{n_{1},\overline{\mathbf{z}}}(L_{n_{2},\mathbf{z}}\cdots L_{n_{l},\mathbf{z}}-L_{n_{2},\overline{\mathbf{z}}}\cdots L_{n_{l},\overline{\mathbf{z}}})

We will restate and prove the result here.

Corollary 7.1

(Runtime Complexity)
.

Suppose

is any discretization

)

\epsilon>0\,,\quad 0<\eta<\min\left\{\frac{2m}{M^{2}}\,,C\epsilon^{1/N}\right\}\,,\quad n\geq\frac{C}{\epsilon(1-\eta)}\,,\quad k\geq\frac{C}{\epsilon^{1/N}}\log\frac{1}{\epsilon}\,,

\left|\,\mathbb{E}\left[F(X_{k})-F_{\mathbf{z}}(X_{k})\right]\,\right|\leq\epsilon\,.

(7.2)

We will start by decomposing the generalization error
via an approximation step with

\displaystyle\left|\,\mathbb{E}\left[F(X_{k})-F_{\mathbf{z}}(X_{k})\right]\,\right|

.

.

\eta=O(\epsilon^{1/N})

and

is sufficient for the latter two terms.
We will then observe that

e^{-\lambda k\eta/2}=O(\epsilon)\iff k=\Omega(\eta^{-1}\log(\epsilon^{-1}))\,,

and substituting in the choice of

Appendix A
Signed Measure Results

We start this section by mentioning that the approximate stationary measure
constructed using weak backward error analysis
can be a signed measure.

Proposition A.1

(The Approximate Stationary Measure is Signed)
.

Consider the following simple Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

dX(t)=-X(t)dt+\sqrt{\frac{2}{\beta}}dW(t),

with
f(x,z)=\frac{1}{2}|x|^{2}
for all
z\in\mathcal{Z}
.
We have that whenever
\frac{2}{d}<\eta
,
the approximate density
\pi^{1}(x)
is not always positive,
specifically

\pi^{1}(0)=(1+\eta\mu_{1}(0))\rho(0)<0.

Proof.

We first write down the generator of this process

L\phi=\sum_{i=1}^{d}-x_{i}\partial_{i}\phi+\frac{1}{\beta}\partial_{ii}\phi,

and therefore leading to the following operator
A_{2}
,

\displaystyle A_{2}\phi

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}A^{\mathbf{k}}_{1}(x)\left[(-x_{i})\partial_{i}+\frac{1}{\beta}\partial_{ii}\right]\partial_{\mathbf{k}}\phi

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}x_{i}x_{j}\partial_{ij}\phi-\frac{2}{\beta}x_{i}\partial_{ijj}\phi+\frac{1}{\beta^{2}}\partial_{iijj}\phi.

Following the definition of
L_{1}
(
5.3
), we can compute

L_{1}=A_{2}+B_{1}L_{0}A_{1}=A_{2}+B_{1}L^{2},

where
B_{1}
is the first Bernoulli number.
Now observe that since
L
is self-adjoint with respect to
L^{2}(\rho)
,
i.e.
L^{\star}=L
, we have

L_{1}^{\star}1=A_{2}^{\star}+B_{1}(L^{\star})^{2}1=A_{2}^{\star}1.

Here we will compute explicitly
A_{2}^{\star}1
, and we start by writing

(A_{2}^{\star}1)\rho=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\partial_{ij}(x_{i}x_{j}\rho)+\frac{2}{\beta}\partial_{ijj}(x_{i}\rho)+\frac{1}{\beta^{2}}\partial_{iijj}\rho=:T_{1}+T_{2}+T_{3}.

We first compute one derivative of
T_{3}
to match
T_{2}
to get

T_{3}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\partial_{ijj}\frac{1}{\beta}(-x_{i}\rho),

this implies that we can combine
T_{2}
and
T_{3}

T_{2}+T_{3}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\partial_{ijj}\frac{1}{\beta}(x_{i}\rho)=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\partial_{ij}\left(\frac{\delta_{ij}}{\beta}\rho-x_{i}x_{j}\rho\right),

where
\delta_{ij}
denotes the Kronecker delta,
and we add the
T_{1}
term as well to get

T_{1}+T_{2}+T_{3}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\partial_{ij}\frac{\delta_{ij}}{\beta}\rho=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\partial_{ii}\frac{1}{\beta}\rho=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\partial_{i}\left(-x_{i}\rho\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\beta x_{i}^{2}\rho-d\rho.

This implies we have the following PDE for
\mu_{1}

L\mu_{1}=-L_{1}^{\star}1\implies\sum_{i=1}^{d}-x_{i}\partial_{i}\mu_{1}+\frac{1}{\beta}\partial_{ii}\mu_{1}=d-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\beta x_{i}^{2}.

Since the equation has a unique solution that satisfies the integral constraint

\int\mu_{1}d\rho=0

(see Proposition
6.8
),
we can explicitly guess the solution

\mu_{1}=\frac{\beta}{2}|x|^{2}-c,

where to satisfy the integral constraint, we must have

c=\int\frac{\beta}{2}|x|^{2}\frac{1}{(2\pi/\beta)^{d/2}}\exp\left(\frac{-\beta|x|^{2}}{2}\right)dx=\frac{d}{2}.

This implies

\inf_{x}\mu_{1}=\mu_{1}(0)=-\frac{d}{2}.

Finally this implies that whenever
\eta>\frac{2}{d}
,
we have

\pi^{1}(0)=\left(1-\eta\frac{d}{2}\right)\rho(0)<0.

∎

Now that we know our approximate measure
\pi^{N}
can be signed,
we can no longer define uniform stability with respect to
an expectation over the random algorithm.
Instead we naturally extend the definition based on
the integral with respect to the signed measure instead.

Definition A.2

(Uniform Stability)
.

A collection of distributions
\{\pi_{\mathbf{z}}\}

on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
indexed by
\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{Z}^{n}

is said to be
\epsilon
-uniformly stable
if
for all
\mathbf{z},\overline{\mathbf{z}}\in\mathcal{Z}^{n}

with only one differing coordinate

\sup_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\left|\pi_{\mathbf{z}}(f(\cdot,z))-\pi_{\overline{\mathbf{z}}}(f(\cdot,z))\right|\leq\epsilon.

(A.1)

Proposition A.3

(Generalization)
.

Suppose the collection of distributions
\{\pi_{\mathbf{z}}\}

is
\epsilon
-uniformly stable,
and that for all
(\mathbf{z},z)\in\mathcal{Z}^{n+1}
,
we also have
f(\cdot,z)\in L^{1}(\pi_{\mathbf{z}})
.
Then
\{\pi_{\mathbf{z}}\}
has
\epsilon
-expected generalization error,
or more precisely

\left|\,\mathbb{E}\left[\pi_{\mathbf{z}}(F_{\mathbf{z}})-\pi_{\mathbf{z}}(F)\right]\,\right|\leq\epsilon\,.

(A.2)

Proof.

it is sufficient to realize that since
f(\cdot,z)

is integrable with respect to
\pi_{\mathbf{z}}
,
all the usual manipulations are well defined.
We will include the proof for completeness
as it is an unintuitive claim.

We will start by denoting
\mathbf{z}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})
,

\widehat{\mathbf{z}}=(\overline{z_{1}},\ldots,\overline{z_{n}})
,
and also the replaced one data set

\widehat{\mathbf{z}}^{(i)}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{i-1},\overline{z_{i}},z_{i+1},\ldots,z_{n})
.

With this we can write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(x,z_{i})d\pi_{\mathbf{z}}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{z},\widehat{\mathbf{z}})\sim\mathcal{D}^{2n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(x,\overline{z_{i}})d\pi_{\widehat{\mathbf{z}}^{(i)}}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{z},\widehat{\mathbf{z}})\sim\mathcal{D}^{2n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(x,\overline{z_{i}})d\pi_{\mathbf{z}}(x)+\delta,

where we define
\delta
as

\delta:=\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{z},\widehat{\mathbf{z}})\sim\mathcal{D}^{2n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(x,\overline{z_{i}})d\pi_{\widehat{\mathbf{z}}^{(i)}}(x)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(x,\overline{z_{i}})d\pi_{\mathbf{z}}(x).

Therefore,
\epsilon
-uniform stability implies
the difference between the two integrals is at most
\epsilon
,
hence we have the generalization error is
|\delta|\leq\epsilon
.

∎

We observe that uniform stability is a stronger notion
than needed here. In fact, we will only need a sense of stability in expectation for generalization in expectation
(
Shalev-Shwartz et al. 2010
)
.
Regardless, we have shown that if
\pi^{N}_{\mathbf{z}}

is
\epsilon
-uniformly stable, then we have also have that
|T_{2}|\leq\epsilon
as desired.

At this point, we will use Mathematica

Inc.

B.3
Toy Example

In this subsection, we consider a one-dimensional problem of
optimizing a loss function
F(x)=\frac{1}{2}x^{2}

with deterministic gradient.
The Langevin update can be written as

X_{k+1}=X_{k}-\eta X_{k}+\sqrt{\frac{2\eta}{\beta}}\xi_{k},\quad X_{0}=x.

where
\xi_{k}\sim N(0,1)
are i.i.d. random variables.

In this case, the corresponding continuous time Langevin process is
described by the following SDE

dX(t)=-X(t)dt+\sqrt{\frac{2}{\beta}}dW(t),\quad X(0)=x,

where
W(t)
is a standard Brownian motion.

Observe we can compute the time-marginal distributions of both processes.
Let’s start by observing that
X_{k}
is a sum of i.i.d. normal random variables,
hence it must also be a normal random variable.
We simply need to estimate the mean and variance.

To compute the parameters, we start by rewriting the update rule as

\displaystyle X_{k+1}

\displaystyle=X_{k}-\eta X_{k}+\sqrt{\frac{2\eta}{\beta}}\xi_{k}

\displaystyle=(1-\eta)X_{k}+\sqrt{2\eta}{\beta}\xi_{k}

\displaystyle=(1-\eta)\left((1-\eta)X_{k-1}+\sqrt{\frac{2\eta}{\beta}}\xi_{k-1}\right)+\sqrt{2\eta}{\beta}\xi_{k}

\displaystyle=(1-\eta)^{k+1}X_{0}+\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}\sqrt{2\eta}{\beta}(1-\eta)^{\ell}\xi_{\ell},

this implies
\mathbb{E}X_{k}=(1-\eta)^{k+1}x
,
and we also have the variance

\mathbb{E}(X_{k}-\mathbb{E}X_{k})^{2}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}\frac{2\eta}{\beta}(1-\eta)^{2\ell}=\frac{2}{\beta(2-\eta)}\left[1-(1-\eta)^{2(k+1)}\right].

It is well known that the continuous time SDE is the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process,
which has the following solution
(
Kuo 2006
)

X(t)=X(0)e^{-t}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-(t-s)}dW(s),

which is a Gaussian process. The mean and variance can also be computed as

B.4
Plot for Figure
2
(a)

In the plot, we consider the forward Euler discretization of the ODE

\frac{dy}{dt}=y^{2},\quad y(0)=1,

which gives the following update

y_{k+1}=y_{k}+\eta y_{k}^{2},\quad y_{0}=1.

The true solution is given by

y(t)=\frac{1}{1-t},

which the modified equation is given by

\frac{dv}{dt}=v^{2}-\eta v^{3}+\eta^{2}\frac{3}{2}v^{4}+\cdots,\quad v(0)=1.

To generate the plot in Figure
2
(a),
we used a step size
\eta=\frac{1}{6}

for the forward Euler solver,
and for the modified equation we approximated the solution
using forward Euler with step size
\eta^{\prime}=\frac{1}{600}

so it is sufficiently close to the true solution.
