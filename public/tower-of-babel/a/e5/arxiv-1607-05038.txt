Abstract
. Let G G be a finite group, and let Δ ⁡ ( G ) \Delta(G) denote the prime graph built on the set of degrees of the irreducible complex characters of G G . It is well known that, whenever Δ ⁡ ( G ) \Delta(G) is connected, the diameter of Δ ⁡ ( G ) \Delta(G) is at most 3 3 . In the present paper, we provide a description of the finite solvable groups for which the diameter of this graph attains the upper bound. This also enables us to confirm a couple of conjectures proposed by M.L. Lewis.

1.
Introduction

Let
G
be a finite group; we denote by
{\rm Irr}(G)
the set of all irreducible complex characters of
G
, and write

{\rm cd}(G)=\{\chi(1)\mid\chi\in{\rm Irr}(G)\}

for the set of the degrees of such characters. The character degree graph
\Delta(G)
is thus defined as the graph with vertex set the set
\rho(G)
of all the primes that divide some
\chi(1)\in{\rm cd}(G)
, and two distinct primes
p
and
q
are adjacent if and only if
pq
divides some degree in cd
(G)
.
The study of the graph
\Delta(G)
and of the relationships between the properties of
\Delta(G)
and the structural features of the group
G
, has by now a rich literature (we recommend the survey paper
[
8
]
for a general overview of the subject), and the purpose of this paper is to contribute to one particular aspect of this research.

A fundamental result of P.P. Palfy (
[
15
]
) ensures that if
G
is a solvable group, then given any three distinct primes in
\rho(G)
, at least two of them are adjacent in
\Delta(G)
. From this it immediately follows that, for a solvable group
G
,
\Delta(G)
has at most two connected components (both inducing a complete subgraph of
\Delta(G)
), and that, when
\Delta(G)
is connected, the diameter of
\Delta(G)
is at most
3
(that this latter inequality holds in any finite group is proved in
[
11
]
).
For some time it has been unknown whether there existed solvable groups whose character degree graph has diameter
3
, until the question was settled by M.L. Lewis, who constructed in
[
9
]
a solvable group
G
such that
\Delta(G)
has
6
vertices and diameter
3
.

It was Lewis construction and his related comments (made particularly explicit in
[
8
]
) that prompted us to study in more detail solvable groups
G
such that the diameter of
\Delta(G)
is
3
.
Through our analysis in the present paper, we pin their structure down enough to show that they all closely resemble Lewis’ examples, and to allow to confirm a couple of conjectures appearing in
[
8
]
.

In the following statement, which is the main result of this paper,
{\bf F}(G)
denotes the Fitting subgroup of the group
G
and, for
i\geq 1
,
\gamma_{i}(P)
is the
i
-th term of the lower central series of the
p
-group
P
.

Theorem A
.

Let
G
be a finite solvable group such that
\Delta(G)
is connected and
{\rm diam}(\Delta(G))=3
. Then the following conclusions hold.

(a)

There exists a prime
p
such that
G=PH
, with
P
a normal non-abelian Sylow
p
-subgroup of
G
and
H
a
p
-complement.

(b)

{\bf F}(G)=P\times A
, where
A={\bf C}_{H}(P)\leq{\bf Z}(G)
,
H/A
is not nilpotent and has cyclic Sylow subgroups.

(c)

\Delta(G/\gamma_{3}(P))
is disconnected.

(d)

If
c
is the nilpotency class of
P
, then all factors
M_{1}=[P,G]/P^{\prime}
and
M_{i}=\gamma_{i}(P)/\gamma_{i+1}(P)
, for
2\leq i\leq c
, are chief factors of
G
of the same order
p^{n}
, where
n
is divisible by at least two distinct odd primes; moreover, for all
1\leq i\leq c
,
G/{\bf C}_{G}(M_{i})
embeds as an irreducible subgroup in the group of semi-linear transformations
\Gamma(p^{n})
.

In particular, we have that
\Delta(G/\gamma_{3}(P))
is a disconnected subgraph of
\Delta(G)

with the same set of vertices, thus confirming a suggestion of Lewis (
[
8
]
). In fact, it will not be hard to derive a proof of a related conjecture concerning the structure of the graph
\Delta(G)
, when
G
is solvable and
{\rm diam}(\Delta(G))=3
. Let
r,s
be two vertices of
\Delta(G)
with
d(r,s)=3
, and denote by
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
the sets consisting of
r
, respectively
s
, and all vertices adjacent to it; then
\pi_{1}\cap\pi_{2}=\emptyset
and (by Palfy’s three primes condition)
\rho(G)=\pi_{1}\cup\pi_{2}
. Moreover, denoting by
{\bf F}_{2}(G)
the second Fitting subgroup of
G
and supposing
p\in\pi_{1}
(we will see that
r\neq p\neq s
), we will show that
\pi_{1}=\pi({\bf F}_{2}(G)/{\bf Z}(G))
,
\pi_{2}=\pi(G/{\bf F}_{2}(G))
,
2\not\in\pi_{2}
,
|\pi_{2}|\geq 2
and
\pi_{1}
,
\pi_{2}
both induce complete subgraphs of
\Delta(G)
(see Remark
4.4
). Indeed,
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
are the set of vertices of the two connected components of
\Delta(G/\gamma_{3}(P))
; it follows (see Remark
4.4
) that

|\pi_{1}|\geq 2^{|\pi_{2}|}.

Hence, Conjecture 4.8 of
[
8
]
is established; in particular,
|\rho(G)|\geq 6
(indeed, it turns out that Lewis’ example has the smallest possible order).

Still in the spirit of another suggestion by Lewis (see the paragraph following 5.8 in
[
8
]
), an immediate consequence of Theorem A is the following result.

Corollary B
.

Let
G
be a finite solvable group, and assume that
\Delta(G)
is connected with diameter
3
. Then the Fitting height of
G
is precisely
3
. In fact,
G
is a nilpotent-by-metacyclic group.

As it is apparent from the above remarks, a central role in our treatment is played by solvable groups with disconnected degree graph, the main features of those we will need to have almost constantly in hand. For this, our main source is their description in
[
10
]
(although similar results also appear in
[
16
]
and
[
14
]
), and we in particular refer to the list of six subcases in section 2 (and 3) of that paper. From the same arguments that prove Theorem A, we derive a result which we believe adds to the understanding of case 2.6 in
[
10
]
.

Theorem C
.

Let
G
be a finite solvable group such that
\Delta(G)
is disconnected and
{\bf F}(G)
is not abelian. Then there is a unique prime
p
such that
P={\bf O}_{p}(G)
is not contained in
{\bf Z}(G)
and

(a)

either
p
is an isolated vertex of
\Delta(G)
, or

(b)

\Delta(G/P^{\prime})
is disconnected and, if
c
is the nilpotency class of
P
, all factors
M_{1}=[P,G]/P^{\prime}
and
M_{i}=\gamma_{i}(P)/\gamma_{i+1}(P)
, for
2\leq i\leq c
, are chief factors of
G
of the same order
p^{n}
, with
n\geq 3
; moreover, for all
1\leq i\leq c
,
G/{\bf C}_{G}(M_{i})
embeds as an irreducible subgroup in the group of semi-linear transformations
\Gamma(p^{n})
.

We conjecture that, both in the disconnected and in the diameter-three case, the chief factors
M_{i}
are pairwise non-isomorphic as
G
-modules over
{\rm{GF}}(p)
; this is true for the first pair
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
(see point (a) in the proof of Proposition
4.2
), but we were not able to prove it in general. Another question that we leave open is whether, in both Theorem A and Theorem C, one has
P=[P,G]\times{\bf Z}(G)
(again, this is true modulo
\gamma_{3}(P)
). Finally, by looking at the known examples, one might ask if it is true that, in Theorem A, not only
\Delta(G/\gamma_{3}(P))
but also
\Delta(G/\gamma_{c}(P))
is disconnected.

2.
Notation and preliminaries

Throughout this paper, every group is tacitly assumed to be a finite group. We write
{\rm V}(G)
and
{\rm E}(G)
for the sets of vertices and edges,
respectively, of the prime graph
\Delta(G)
on irreducible character degrees. We denote by
d_{G}(u,v)
the distance in
\Delta(G)

between the two (distinct) vertices
u
and
v
(i.e. the length of a shortest path joining
u

and
v
; set
d_{G}(u,v)=\infty
if there is no such path), and by
{\rm diam}(\Delta(G))
the maximum of
d_{G}(u,v)
for
u,v\in{\rm V}(G)
if
{\Delta(G)}
is connected (whereas we set
{\rm diam}(\Delta(G))=\infty
if
\Delta(G)
is not connected).

As customary, we denote by
\Gamma(p^{n})
the semi-linear group on the field
{\rm GF}(p^{n})
, and by
\Gamma_{0}(p^{n})
the subgroup of
\Gamma(p^{n})
induced by the field multiplications. If
V
is an
n
-dimensional vector space over
{\rm{GF}}(p)
, then
V
can be identified with the additive group of a field of order
p^{n}
, and in this sense we write
\Gamma(V)
and
\Gamma_{0}(V)
for
\Gamma(p^{n})
and
\Gamma_{0}(p^{n})
respectively.

Let
a>1
and
n
be positive integers. A prime
t
is called a
primitive prime divisor
for
(a,n)
if
t
divides
a^{n}-1
but
t
does not divide
a^{j}-1
for
1\leq j<n
. Recall that, by a well-known result by Zsigmondy (
[
12
, Theorem 6.2]
), such a prime always exists except when
n=6
and
a=2
, or
n=2
and
a+1
is a power of
2
.

Let
N
be a normal subgroup of
G
and let
\lambda\in{\rm Irr}(N)
. We denote by
{\rm Irr}(G|\lambda)
the set of irreducible characters
\chi
of
G
such that
\lambda
is an irreducible constituent of
\chi_{N}
. In this setting,
\chi
and
\lambda
are said to be
fully ramified
with respect to
G/N
(but sometimes, when the context is clear enough, we also say that
\lambda
is fully ramified in
G
) if
\chi_{N}=e\lambda
with
e^{2}=|G:N|
. By
[
7
, Problem 6.3]
, this is equivalent to the fact that
\chi
vanishes on
G\setminus N
with
\lambda
invariant in
G
, and also to the fact that
\chi
is the unique irreducible constituent of
\lambda^{G}
still with
\lambda
invariant in
G
.

If
A
is an abelian group, we write
\widehat{A}
to denote the dual group of A, that is, the set
{\rm Irr}(A)
endowed with multiplication of characters.

Also, we freely use without references some basic facts of Character Theory such as Clifford Correspondence, Gallagher’s Theorem, Ito-Michler’s Theorem, results concerning character extension and coprime actions (see
[
7
]
).

We shall also take into account the following well-known result concerning character degrees.

Lemma 2.1

(
[
12
, Proposition 17.3]
)
.

Let
G
be a solvable group. Let
F={\bf F}(G)
and
K={\bf F}_{2}(G)
. Then there exists
\chi\in{\rm Irr}(G)

such that
\pi(K/F)\subseteq\pi(\chi(1))
.

As mentioned in the Introduction, we will make an intensive use of the classification, provided in
[
10
]
, of solvable groups whose character degree graph is disconnected. The next statement summarizes some aspects of that classification: the groups in (a), (b) and (c) are respectively those of types 2.1, 2.4 and 2.6 (described further in 3.1, 3.4 and 3.6, respectively) in
[
10
]
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
G
be a solvable group, and set
F={\bf F}(G)
,
K={\bf F}_{2}(G)
. Assume that
\Delta(G)
has two connected components. Then the following conclusions hold.

(a)

Assume that
G
is metanilpotent. Then
G=PH
, where
P\trianglelefteq\,G
is a non-abelian Sylow
p
-subgroup for a suitable prime
p
, and
H
is an abelian
p
-complement. Moreover,
P^{\prime}\leq{\bf C}_{P}(H)
, and every non-linear irreducible character of
P
is fully ramified with respect to
P/{\bf C}_{P}(H)
. Finally, the sets of vertices of the two connected components of
\Delta(G)
are respectively
\{p\}
and
\pi(G/F)
.

(b)

Assume that
F
is abelian, and that
|{\rm V}(G)|>2
. Then
G=MH
, where
M\trianglelefteq\,G
is an elementary abelian
p
-group for a suitable prime
p
, and
H
is a complement for
M
. Also,
F=M\times{\bf Z}(G)
,
{\bf Z}(G)={\bf C}_{H}(M)
and
G/F\leq\Gamma(M)
. The subgroup

and
G/K
are cyclic groups. Finally, the sets of vertices of the two connected components of
\Delta(G)
are respectively
\pi(K/F)
and
\pi(G/K)
.

(c)

Assume that
F
is non-abelian and that, whenever
{\bf O}_{r}(G)
is non-abelian, the prime
r
is not an isolated vertex of
\Delta(G)
. Then
G=PH
, where
P\trianglelefteq\,G
is a non-abelian Sylow
p
-subgroup for a suitable prime
p
, and
H
is a
p
-complement. Also,
F=P\times U
where
U\leq{\bf Z}(G)
. The factor group
G/P^{\prime}
is a group as in
(b)
, so, in particular,
K/F
and
G/K
are cyclic groups. Finally, the sets of vertices of the two connected components of
\Delta(G)
are respectively
\{p\}\cup\pi(K/F)
and
\pi(G/K)
.

We stress that a group

as in (b) or (c) of Theorem
2.2
is such that every Sylow subgroup of
G/F
is cyclic.

We also quote the following result, which is Theorem 5.5 of
[
10
]
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
G
be a solvable group such that
\Delta(G)
is a
disconnected graph. Then there exists a unique prime
p
such
that
{\bf O}_{p}(G)
is non-central in

.

Next, another preliminary lemma.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
G

{\bf F}(G)=M\times Z
, with

and

.
Assume also that every irreducible

Observe first that, by our assumptions, every irreducible character of

\theta

be a number in
{\rm cd}(G)
,
\chi
an irreducible character of

of degree

, and
\theta

\psi\in{\rm Irr}(G/Z)

\chi=\xi\psi

Let

3.
Some proofs

We start with two lemmas concerning modules over finite fields for cyclic groups.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
G
be a cyclic group,
{\mathbb{K}}
a finite field of order
q
, and
M
a faithful irreducible
m
-dimensional
{\mathbb{K}}[G]
-module. Also, let
\epsilon
be an element of order
|G|
in the multiplicative group of
{\mathbb{F}}={\rm{GF}}(q^{m})
. Then the following hold.

(a)

If
M
is a constituent of
M\otimes_{{\mathbb{K}}}M
, then there exist
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
in
{\rm{Gal}}({\mathbb{F}}\,|\,{\mathbb{K}})
such that
\epsilon^{\sigma_{1}}\cdot\epsilon^{\sigma_{2}}=\epsilon
.

(b)

If
M
is self-contragredient, then there exists
\sigma
in
{\rm{Gal}}({\mathbb{F}}\,|\,{\mathbb{K}})
such that
\epsilon^{\sigma}=\epsilon^{-1}
.

Proof.

Observe that
{\mathbb{F}}
is a splitting field for
G
over
{\mathbb{K}}
. By
[
5
, II.3.10]
, we can identify
M
with the additive group of
{\mathbb{F}}
, and the action of a
suitable generator
x
of
G
with the multiplication by
\epsilon
. We denote by
M_{{\mathbb{F}}}

the
1
-dimensional
{\mathbb{F}}[G]
-module arising in this way. Setting
M^{{\mathbb{F}}}=M\otimes_{{\mathbb{K}}}{\mathbb{F}}
, by
[
6
, VII, 1.16 a)]
we get

M^{{\mathbb{F}}}=\bigoplus_{\sigma\in{\rm{Gal}}({\mathbb{F}}\,|\,{\mathbb{K}})}(M_{{\mathbb{F}}})^{\sigma}.

Now, if
M
is a constituent of
M\otimes_{{\mathbb{K}}}M
, then

M^{{\mathbb{F}}}
is a direct summand (as an
{\mathbb{F}}[G]
-module) of

(M\otimes_{{\mathbb{K}}}M)^{{\mathbb{F}}}\simeq M^{{\mathbb{F}}}\otimes_{{\mathbb{F}}}M^{{\mathbb{F}}}\simeq\bigoplus_{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in{\rm{Gal}}({\mathbb{F}}\,|\,{\mathbb{K}})}(M_{{\mathbb{F}}})^{\sigma_{1}}\otimes_{{\mathbb{F}}}(M_{{\mathbb{F}}})^{\sigma_{2}}.

In particular, there exist
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}

in
{\rm{Gal}}({\mathbb{F}}\,|\,{\mathbb{K}})
such that

M_{{\mathbb{F}}}\simeq(M_{{\mathbb{F}}})^{\sigma_{1}}\otimes_{{\mathbb{F}}}(M_{{\mathbb{F}}})^{\sigma_{2}}
.
Considering now the action of
x
on these two isomorphic
{\mathbb{F}}[G]
-modules, claim (a) follows.

As for (b), if
M
is
{\mathbb{K}}[G]
-isomorphic to its contragredient
module
M^{*}
, then we get

\bigoplus_{\sigma\in{\rm{Gal}}({\mathbb{F}}\,|\,{\mathbb{K}})}(M_{{\mathbb{F}}})^{\sigma}\simeq M^{{\mathbb{F}}}\simeq(M^{*})^{{\mathbb{F}}}\simeq\bigoplus_{\sigma\in{\rm{Gal}}({\mathbb{F}}\,|\,{\mathbb{K}})}((M^{*})_{{\mathbb{F}}})^{\sigma}.

In particular, there exists
\sigma
in

{\rm{Gal}}({\mathbb{F}}\,|\,{\mathbb{K}})
such that
(M_{{\mathbb{F}}})^{\sigma}
is
{\mathbb{F}}[G]
-isomorphic to
(M^{*})_{{\mathbb{F}}}
, which is in turn
{\mathbb{F}}[G]
-isomorphic to
(M_{{\mathbb{F}}})^{*}
. Claim (b) is now achieved by
comparing the action of
x
on the two relevant
{\mathbb{F}}[G]
-modules.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
G
be a cyclic group,
p
a prime, and
M
a
faithful irreducible
{\rm{GF}}(p)[G]
-module. Setting
|M|=p^{m}
, assume that there exists a divisor
r
of

m
,
1\leq r<m
, such that
\frac{p^{m}-1}{p^{r}-1}
divides
|G|
. Then the following hold.

(a)

If
M
is a constituent of
M\wedge_{{\rm{GF}}(p)}M
, then
(p,m/r)=(2,2)
.

(b)

If
M
is self-contragredient, then
(|G|,m/r)=(p^{r}+1,2)
.

Proof.

Let us first prove Claim (a). If
M
is a constituent of

M\wedge_{{\rm{GF}}(p)}M
, then it is clearly a constituent of

M\otimes_{{\rm{GF}}(p)}M
as well. Therefore, by
Lemma
3.1
(a), there exist
a,b\in\{0,...,m-1\}
(say

a\geq b
) such that
p^{a}+p^{b}\equiv 1\;({\rm mod}\;|G|)
; in
particular, setting
t=\frac{p^{m}-1}{p^{r}-1}
, we have that
t

divides
p^{a}+p^{b}-1
.

Since we have
t>p^{m-r}
, we also have
a\geq m-r
; in fact, assuming the contrary, we would get
p^{b}\leq p^{a}\leq p^{m-r-1}
, thus
p^{a}+p^{b}-1\leq 2p^{m-r-1}-1<p^{m-r}<t
, contradicting the fact that
t
divides
p^{a}+p^{b}-1
. Therefore
we can write
a=m-n
where
n
lies in
\{1,...,r\}
. Now,

t
is a divisor of
(p^{m-n}+p^{b}-1)\cdot p^{n}-(p^{m}-1)=p^{b+n}-p^{n}+1
, whence
m-r\leq b+n\leq a+n=m
.
Again, defining
\ell=m-(b+n)
, we get
0\leq\ell\leq r
and

t
divides
(p^{b+n}-p^{n}+1)\cdot p^{\ell}-(p^{m}-1)=p^{\ell}-p^{n+\ell}+1
. As a result,
t
is a
divisor of
p^{\ell}\cdot(p^{n}-1)-1
.

On the other hand, if
m/r\geq 3
, then
m-r\geq 2r
, and so

n+\ell\leq 2r\leq m-r
. This implies
p^{n+\ell}-p^{\ell}<p^{m-r}<t
, and now the only possibility is

p^{\ell}\cdot(p^{n}-1)=1,

which yields
\ell=0
,
p=2
,

n=1
. So,
p^{a}=p^{b}=2^{m-1}
. Setting
{\mathbb{F}}={\rm GF}(2^{m})
and denoting by
\sigma
the element
of
{\rm{Gal}}({\mathbb{F}}\,|\,{\rm{GF}}(2))
which maps every

to

, the conclusion so far is that
(M_{{\mathbb{F}}})^{\sigma}\otimes_{{\mathbb{F}}}(M_{{\mathbb{F}}})^{\sigma}
is the unique constituent of
M^{{\mathbb{F}}}\otimes_{{\mathbb{F}}}M^{{\mathbb{F}}}
which is isomorphic to
M_{{\mathbb{F}}}
. But
(M_{{\mathbb{F}}})^{\sigma}\otimes_{{\mathbb{F}}}(M_{{\mathbb{F}}})^{\sigma}
is not a
constituent of
M^{{\mathbb{F}}}\wedge_{{\mathbb{F}}}M^{{\mathbb{F}}}\simeq(M\wedge_{{\rm GF}(2)}M)^{{\mathbb{F}}}
, against the fact that
M
is a constituent of
M\wedge_{{\rm GF}(2)}M
.

It remains to treat the case
m/r=2
, whence
t=p^{r}+1
divides

p^{a}+p^{b}-1
with
0<a\leq 2r-1
. Note that we must have
a\geq r
, so we can write
a=r+n
with
0\leq n<r
. Now,

. In particular,

t\leq|-p^{n}+p^{b}-1|<p^{n}+p^{b}-1
. This in turn implies

,
therefore we write

where
0\leq k<r
. Finally,
p^{r}+1

divides
-p^{n}+p^{r+k}-1-p^{k}\cdot(p^{r}+1)=-p^{n}-p^{k}-1
, whence

p^{r}+1\leq p^{n}+p^{k}+1\leq 2p^{r-1}+1
. It follows that
p=2
, and
(a) is proved.

We move now to Claim (b). If
M
is self-contragredient, then
Lemma
3.1
(b) yields that there exists
k\in\{0,...,m-1\}

such that
{p^{k}}\equiv-1\;({\rm{mod}}\;|G|)
. Therefore,

\frac{p^{m}-1}{p^{r}-1}
divides
p^{k}+1
. Let us first exclude the
possibility

. Observe also that, as by

Our conclusion so far is that

Remark 3.3
.

Let

be a

-group, and

; therefore, for

is a multiple of

(and in fact

.

Lemma 3.4

Let

Proof.

We can assume that

could be fully ramified with respect to

.
Let

be a fixed primitive
p
th-root of unity and

{\mathbb{K}}=\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
. Then we can associate to every

\lambda\in\widehat{N}
an alternating bilinear form

on

Choosing a basis

\widehat{N}
of

Lemma 3.5

Lemma 3.11
.

Let

-subgroup of

Consider now the factor group

and adopt the bar convention throughout; clearly

of

Proposition 3.13

If

in

We remark that

Write

, where

. We deduce that

is disconnected with components

and
\pi_{0}
(observe that every

The main results

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
G
be a solvable group such that either
\Delta(G)
is connected of diameter
3
, or
\Delta(G)
is disconnected. In the disconnected case, assume also that
F={\bf F}(G)
is non-abelian and that, whenever
{\bf O}_{r}(G)
is non-abelian, the prime
r
is not an isolated vertex of
\Delta(G)
. Then the following conclusions hold.

(a)

Let
p
be a prime. If
{\bf O}_{p}(G)
is non-abelian, then it is a Sylow
p
-subgroup of
G
.

(b)

There exists a unique prime
p
such that
P={\bf O}_{p}(G)
is non-abelian.
Also, denoting by
U
the
p
-complement of
F
, we have
U\leq{\bf Z}(G)
.

(c)

\Delta(G/\gamma_{3}(P))
is disconnected, and
G/F
is a non-nilpotent group whose Sylow subgroups are all cyclic. If
c
is the nilpotency class of
P
, all factors
M_{1}=[P,G]/P^{\prime}
and
M_{i}=\gamma_{i}(P)/\gamma_{i+1}(P)
, for
2\leq i\leq c
, are chief factors of
G
of the same order
p^{n}
. Moreover, for all
1\leq i\leq c
,
G/{\bf C}_{G}(M_{i})
embeds in
\Gamma(p^{n})
as an irreducible subgroup.

Proof.

Let
G
be as in the assumptions and
F={\bf F}(G)
. Observe that if
\Delta(G)
is connected then, by Theorem
2.5
, there exists a prime
p

such that
P={\bf O}_{p}(G)
is non-abelian (thus
F
is not abelian in any case).
We argue by induction, and thus assume that
G
is a counterexample of minimal order.

If
M
is a normal subgroup of
G
such that
M\leq{\bf\Phi}(G)
, then the Fitting series of
G/M
is the image of the Fitting series of
G
under the natural homomorphism onto
G/M
.
Moreover, if
{\bf F}(G/M)=F/M
is non-abelian and
{\rm V}(G)={\rm V}(G/M)
, then all assumptions on
G
are inherited by
G/M
.

Observe first that, if
\Delta(G)
is disconnected, then
G
is as in (c) of Theorem
2.2
; in particular, parts (a) and (b) are already known to be true.

Thus, as it concerns the proof of (a) and (b), we may assume that
\Delta(G)
is connected. Let
p
be a prime such that
P={\bf O}_{p}(G)
is non-abelian. Setting
N=P^{\prime}
, observe
that
N\leq{\bf\Phi}(G)
. Then, in order to prove (a) and (b), we may assume that
N
is a minimal normal subgroup of
G
. In fact, if
M
is a normal subgroup
of
G
such that
1<M<N
, then
{\bf O}_{p}(G/M)=P/M
is non–abelian and
{\rm V}(G)={\rm V}(G/M)
, whence, as observed before, (a) and (b) hold in
G/M
. In particular,
P
is a Sylow
p
-subgroup of
G
, and so (a) holds in
G
.
Also, if
U
is the
p
-complement of
F
, then
UM/M
is the
p
-complement of
{\bf F}(G/M)
; property (b) in
G/M
and normality of
U
in
G
yield
[U,G]\leq M\cap U=1
, and (b) holds true in
G
.

Assuming thus
N
to be a minimal normal subgroup of
G
, we start proving claims (a) and (b) for
G
under the additional
hypothesis that
\Delta(G/N)
is disconnected.
Then, again denoting by
U

the
p
-complement of
F
, Proposition
3.13
yields
P/N\not\leq{\bf Z}(G/N)
, and Lemma
2.3
ensures that
F/N
is abelian and
UN/N
is central in
G/N
. Thus, we get
[G,U]\leq N\cap U=1
, and (b) is proved in
G
. Since Proposition
3.13
also
ensures that
p\nmid|G/P|
, then (a) is achieved as well.

For the proof of (a) and (b), we may therefore assume that
\Delta(G/{\bf O}_{p}(G)^{\prime})
is connected for every prime
p
such that
{\bf O}_{p}(G)
is
non-abelian.
Thus, let
p
be such a prime and, again, write
P={\bf O}_{p}(G)
and

N=P^{\prime}
.

Suppose, by contradiction, that (a) does not hold in
G
, that is,
p
divides
|G/P|
.
Then
{\rm V}(G/N)={\rm V}(G)
and, since
\Delta(G/N)

is connected, diam(
\Delta(G/N)
)=
3
. In
particular,
F/N
is non-abelian by Theorem
2.5
, and therefore there
exists a prime
q\neq p
such that
{\bf O}_{q}(G/N)
is non-abelian. Now,
G/N
satisfies the hypotheses of the Theorem and so, by choice of
G
,
G/N
satisfies (a), (b) and (c): in particular,
P/N
is
central in
G/N
and all the Sylow subgroups of
G/F
are cyclic.
Setting

, and taking any

3.13
, that

is an extension of

to

, such

(and therefore

\theta
) extends to

3.12

is a complete vertex of

\Delta(G)

satisfies (a).

We move next to (b). First, we prove the following claim:

and

\Delta(G/P^{\prime})

.
(Note

and

,
which a contradiction.
We thus conclude that the diameters of
\Delta(G/P^{\prime})

But in this situation, the only

Finally, we prove that (c) holds in

. Let

-complement of

(thus

, and therefore

,

and

Then
\gamma_{3}(P)=1

\Delta(G)
. Note that
|\pi_{2}|\geq 2
, as otherwise
\Delta(G)
would have a complete vertex.
Also,
\Delta(G/P^{\prime})
is a disconnected graph
with components subgraphs

\pi_{1}\setminus\{p\}
and
\pi_{2}
.

Now, we have that
G=PH
, with
P
a
p
-group,
\gamma_{3}(P)\neq 1
, and
H/{\bf C}_{H}(P)
embeds in
\Gamma(p^{n})
.
So, setting
d=|G/{\bf F}_{2}(G)|
, an application of Lemma
3.5
yields that
d
divides
n
,
(p^{n}-1)/(p^{n/d}-1)
divides
|{\bf F}_{2}(G)/{\bf F}(G)|
,
(p^{n})^{3}
divides
|P|
and
d
is coprime to
p^{n}-1
. As a consequence, on one hand

[
10
, Theorem 5.1]
yields
|\pi_{1}\setminus\{p\}|\geq 2^{|\pi_{2}|}-1
. On the other hand, we easily get
2\not\in\pi_{2}
; in particular, as
|\pi_{2}|\geq 2
, we also get that
n
is divisible by two odd primes, as stated in part (d) of Theorem A.

Also, we note that
d_{G}(p,v)\leq 2
for every
v\in{\rm V}(G)
. In
fact, assume that
d_{G}(p,v)=3
and let
p-t-r-v
be a path in
\Delta(G)
;
then we get
t\in\pi_{1}
,
r\in\pi_{2}
and, given
\chi\in{\rm Irr}(G)
such that
tr\mid\chi(1)
, the prime
p
does not divide

\chi(1)
. But now
\chi
is in fact in
{\rm Irr}(G/P^{\prime})
and hence

t
is adjacent to
r
in
\Delta(G/P^{\prime})
, a contradiction.

Finally, we sketch the proof that Lewis’ example in
[
9
]
is of the smallest possible order.
In fact, as observed above,
|G|
is a multiple of

(p^{n})^{3}\cdot\frac{p^{n}-1}{p^{n/d}-1}\cdot d\;.

The smallest value of such an integer is attained for
p=2
and
n=d=15
, that is
2^{45}\cdot(2^{15}-1)\cdot 15
, which is precisely the order of Lewis’ group.

We conclude with a proof of Corollary B.

Proof of Corollary B.

In the setting of Theorem A, we have that
G/F
is a group whose Sylow subgroups are all cyclic. This implies that
G/F
is a metacyclic group and, since it is not nilpotent, the desired conclusion follows.
