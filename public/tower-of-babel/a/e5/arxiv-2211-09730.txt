Chapter 1
Introduction

In this expository article we present Rosenlicht’s work on geometric class field theory, which classifies abelian coverings of smooth, projective, geometrically connected curves over perfect fields. Unless otherwise specified, all proofs in this expository article are either taken directly from [1], or are adaptations of proofs in [1].

Most of the author’s effort went into translating statements and proofs regarding generalised Jacobians from Weil’s language of algebraic geometry into the language of schemes (in analogy to [4], where Milne translated statements and proof regarding Jacobian varieties from Weil’s language into the language of schemes), whereas the rest of the story that don’t use Weil’s language in some essential way are mostly included here for the sake of completeness, and some proofs include added details for the sake of the author’s understanding.

1.1
Summary of the Story

Let
\displaystyle k
be a perfect field, and let
\displaystyle X
be a smooth, projective, geometrically connected curve over
\displaystyle k
. Let
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}
be a modulus (which just means an effective divisor) on
\displaystyle X
. Write
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}=\sum n_{P}P
and
\displaystyle S
for the support of
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}
(that is, the set of points where
\displaystyle n_{P}\neq 0
), and
\displaystyle v_{P}
for the valuation of a rational function at the point
\displaystyle P
. For a rational function
\displaystyle\phi
on
\displaystyle X
, we say
\displaystyle\phi\equiv 1
mod
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}
if
\displaystyle v_{P}(1-\phi)\geq n_{P}
for each
\displaystyle P
.

Chapter 2 is devoted to the following theorem:

Theorem 1
.

Let
\displaystyle f:X\dashrightarrow G
be a rational map from
\displaystyle X
to an algebraic group
\displaystyle G
which is regular away from
\displaystyle S
. This naturally extends to a map from divisors prime to
\displaystyle S
to
\displaystyle G
. There exists a modulus
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}
with support
\displaystyle S
, such that
\displaystyle f(D)=0
for every divisor
\displaystyle D=(\phi)
with
\displaystyle\phi\equiv 1
mod
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}
.

Chapter 3 is devoted to the following theorem:

Theorem 2
.

There exists an algebraic group
\displaystyle J_{\mathbf{m}}
and a principal homogeneous space
\displaystyle J_{\mathbf{m}}^{(1)}
, depending on
\displaystyle X
and
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}
, and a rational map
\displaystyle\varphi_{\mathbf{m}}:X\dashrightarrow J_{\mathbf{m}}
regular away from
\displaystyle S
, satisfying the following universal property: for every rational map
\displaystyle f:X\dashrightarrow G
to a principal homogeneous space of a connected commutative algebraic group regular away from
\displaystyle S
, there exists some modulus
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}
with support
\displaystyle S
such that there exists a unique morphism
\displaystyle\theta:J_{\mathbf{m}}^{(1)}\rightarrow H
such that
\displaystyle f=\theta\circ\varphi_{\mathbf{m}}
.

A map
\displaystyle f:H^{\prime}\rightarrow H
between principal homogeneous spaces of commutative algebraic groups is called an isogeny if it is surjective, and has finite kernel. A isogeny is called an abelian isogeny, if in addition to being an isogeny, it is an abelian covering.

Theorem 3
.

Every abelian covering of a variety is the pullback of an abelian isogeny.

Combining the above 3 theorems, we get:

Theorem 4
.

Every abelian covering of
\displaystyle X
(irreducible, non-singular, projective curve) is the pullback of an abelian isogeny
\displaystyle\theta:H\rightarrow J_{\mathbf{m}}^{(1)}
.

We will also prove that for every covering, there is a smallest
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}
we can choose in Theorem 4, and in this case, the isogeny is unique. This classifies all abelian coverings of
\displaystyle X
.

1.2
Conventions

We will work with schemes only (unlike [1], which works with varieties over universal domains). Points will always mean closed points.

Projective curves over a field
\displaystyle k
don’t have to be embedded in some
\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{k}^{n}
, they can be embedded in some projective space over a finite extension of
\displaystyle k
.

Varieties (and in particular, curves) are not assumed to be irreducible, although we will (explicitly) make this assumption most of the time.

\displaystyle k
denotes a perfect field.
\displaystyle X
denotes a smooth, projective, geometrically connected curve.
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}
denotes a modulus (effective divisor) on
\displaystyle X
, whose support is denoted
\displaystyle S
.
\displaystyle G
denotes a connected commutative algebraic group.

Write
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}=\sum n_{P}P
, and
\displaystyle v_{P}
for the valuation of a rational function at the point
\displaystyle P
. For a rational function
\displaystyle\phi
on
\displaystyle X
and
\displaystyle a\in k
, we say
\displaystyle\phi\equiv a
mod
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}
if
\displaystyle v_{P}(a-\phi)\geq n_{P}
for each
\displaystyle P
(if the inequality holds only for a point
\displaystyle P\in S

\displaystyle\phi\equiv 1
mod
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}

\displaystyle(\phi)

Let
\displaystyle f:X-S\rightarrow G

\displaystyle X-S

\displaystyle f
extends to a map from the set of divisors on
\displaystyle X
prime to
\displaystyle S
to
\displaystyle G
.

Definition 2.0.1
.

We say that
\displaystyle f
admits the modulus
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}
(or that
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}
is a modulus of
\displaystyle f
) iff
\displaystyle f((g))=0
for every function
\displaystyle g\in k(X)
such that
\displaystyle g\equiv 1
mod
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}
.

The main goal of this chapter is to prove theorem 1, which we restate here:

Theorem 2.0.2
.

Let
\displaystyle S
be a finite set on a smooth, projective, geometrically connected curve
\displaystyle X
. Let
\displaystyle G
be a connected commutative algebraic group. Every rational map
\displaystyle X\dashrightarrow G
regular on
\displaystyle X-S

\displaystyle\mathbf{m}

\displaystyle S

We will only prove it in the case of positive characteristic. The proof of the case of characteristic zero is quite different, and can be found in [1], chapter 3, no 6.

2.1
Local Symbols

In this section, we define the notion of a local symbol, prove some elementary properties of local symbols, and give a criterion for admitting a modulus in terms of traces (
Proposition

2.1.7
).

For this section and the next,
\displaystyle k
will be assumed to be algebraically closed.

Definition 2.1.1
.

Let
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}
be a modulus on
\displaystyle X
. Let
\displaystyle f:X-S\rightarrow G
. We call a “local symbol” the assignment, for each
\displaystyle P\in X
and every
\displaystyle g\in k(X)^{*}
, of an element of
\displaystyle G
, written
\displaystyle(f,g)_{P}
, satisfying:

(i)

\displaystyle(f,gg^{\prime})_{P}=(f,g)_{P}+(f,g^{\prime})_{P}
.

(ii)

\displaystyle(f,g)_{P}=0
if
\displaystyle P\in S
and if
\displaystyle g\equiv 1
mod
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}
at
\displaystyle P
.

(iii)

\displaystyle(f,g)_{P}=v_{P}(g)f(P)
if
\displaystyle P\in X-S
.

(iv)

\displaystyle\sum_{P\in X}(f,g)_{P}=0
.

Proposition 2.1.2
.

\displaystyle f
admits a modulus
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}
iff there exists a local symbol assiciated to
\displaystyle f
and
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}
, and such symbol is then unique.

Proof.

(Sketch) If a local symbol exists (with respect to a modulus
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}
), and
\displaystyle g\equiv 1
mod
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}
, then
\displaystyle f((g))=0
by properties (ii), (iii), (iv).

If
\displaystyle f
admits a modulus
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}
, then define a local symbol as follows: by (iii), define
\displaystyle(f,g)_{P}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=v_{P}(g)f(P)
if
\displaystyle P\in X-S
. For
\displaystyle P\in S
, by approximation theorem of valuations, we can find an auxiliary function
\displaystyle g_{P}
such that
\displaystyle g_{P}\equiv 1
mod
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}
at all
\displaystyle Q\in S-P
, and that
\displaystyle g/g_{P}\equiv 1
mod
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}
, and define
\displaystyle(f,g)_{P}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=-\sum_{Q\notin S}v_{Q}(g_{P})f(Q)
.

Verification that properties (i)-(iv) are satisfied can be found in [1].
∎

Proposition 2.1.3
.

Let
\displaystyle f:X-S\rightarrow G
, and let
\displaystyle\theta:G\rightarrow G^{\prime}
be an algebraic homomorphism of commutative groups. If
\displaystyle f
admits a modulus
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}
, then
\displaystyle\theta\circ f
also admits
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}
as a modulus, and the corresponding local symbols satisfy
\displaystyle(\theta\circ f,g)_{P}=\theta((f,g)_{P}).

Proof.

We check that
\displaystyle(\theta\circ f,g)_{P}=\theta((f,g)_{P})
is a local symbol.

(i)

\displaystyle\theta((f,gg^{\prime})_{P})=\theta((f,g)_{P}+(f,g^{\prime})_{P})=\theta((f,g)_{P})+\theta((f,g^{\prime})_{P})

(ii)

Let
\displaystyle g\equiv 1
mod
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}
at
\displaystyle P
.
\displaystyle\theta((f,g)_{P})=\theta(0)=0

(iii)

Let
\displaystyle P\in X-S
.
\displaystyle\theta((f,g)_{P})=\theta(v_{P}(g)f(P))=v_{P}(g)\theta(f(P))

(iv)

\displaystyle\sum_{P\in X}\theta((f,g)_{P})=\theta(\sum_{P\in X}(f,g)_{P})=\theta(0)=0
.

So by
Proposition

2.1.2
,
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}
is a modulus of
\displaystyle\theta\circ f
.
∎

Definition 2.1.4
.

Let
\displaystyle f:X-S\rightarrow G
, and let
\displaystyle\pi:X\rightarrow X^{\prime}
be a covering of curves. Put
\displaystyle S^{\prime}=\pi^{-1}(S)
, and for every
\displaystyle P^{\prime}\in X^{\prime}
, denote by
\displaystyle\pi^{-1}(P^{\prime})
the divisor of
\displaystyle X
which is the inverse image of
\displaystyle P^{\prime}
by
\displaystyle\pi
counting multiplicity (so we have
\displaystyle\pi^{-1}(P^{\prime})=\sum_{P\mapsto P^{\prime}}e_{P}P
, where
\displaystyle e_{P}
denotes the ramification index at
\displaystyle P
). If
\displaystyle P^{\prime}\notin S^{\prime}
, then
\displaystyle f(\pi^{-1}(P^{\prime}))
makes sense, and we get a map

\textrm{Tr}_{\pi}f:X^{\prime}-S^{\prime}\rightarrow G

which will be called the trace of
\displaystyle f
.

Proposition 2.1.5
.

With the setting of
Definition

2.1.4
, if
\displaystyle f
admits a modulus
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}
, then
\displaystyle f^{\prime}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\textrm{Tr}_{\pi}f
admits a modulus
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}^{\prime}
, and for every
\displaystyle P^{\prime}\in X^{\prime}
and
\displaystyle g\in k(X^{\prime})^{*}
,

(\textrm{Tr}_{\pi}f,g)_{P^{\prime}}=\sum_{P\mapsto P^{\prime}}(f,g\circ\pi)_{P}.

Proof.

We check that
\displaystyle(\textrm{Tr}_{\pi}f,g)_{P^{\prime}}=\sum_{P\mapsto P^{\prime}}(f,g\circ\pi)_{P}
is a local symbol.

(i)

\displaystyle\sum_{P\mapsto P^{\prime}}(f,gg^{\prime}\circ\pi)_{P}=\sum_{P\mapsto P^{\prime}}(f,g\circ\pi)_{P}+(f,g^{\prime}\circ\pi)_{P}=\sum_{P\mapsto P^{\prime}}(f,g\circ\pi)_{P}+\sum_{P\mapsto P^{\prime}}(f,g^{\prime}\circ\pi)_{P}

(ii)

Write
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}=\sum_{P\in S}n_{P}P
. For every
\displaystyle P^{\prime}\in S^{\prime}
, choose an integer
\displaystyle n_{P^{\prime}}
such that

n_{P^{\prime}}>n_{P}/e_{P}

for all
\displaystyle P\in S\cap\pi_{-1}(P^{\prime})
. Let
\displaystyle g\equiv 1
mod
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}^{\prime}
at
\displaystyle P
, where

\mathbf{m}^{\prime}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\sum_{P^{\prime}\in S^{\prime}}n_{P^{\prime}}P^{\prime}.

We have that for every
\displaystyle P\mapsto P^{\prime}
and
\displaystyle P\in S
,

v_{P}(1-g\circ\pi)\geq e_{P}n_{P^{\prime}}\geq n_{P},

so
\displaystyle(f,g\circ\pi)_{P}=0
. If
\displaystyle P\mapsto P^{\prime}
but
\displaystyle P\notin S
, then since
\displaystyle v_{P}(g\circ\pi)=0
, we also have
\displaystyle(f,g\circ\pi)=0
. So, for
\displaystyle P^{\prime}\in S^{\prime}
,

\sum_{P\mapsto P^{\prime}}(f,g\circ\pi)_{P}=0.

(iii)

Let
\displaystyle P^{\prime}\in X^{\prime}-S^{\prime}
.

\sum_{P\mapsto P^{\prime}}(f,g\circ\pi)_{P}=\sum_{P\mapsto P^{\prime}}v_{P}(g\circ\pi)f(P)=v_{P^{\prime}}(g)\sum_{P\mapsto P^{\prime}}e_{P}f(P)=v_{P^{\prime}}(g)\textrm{Tr}_{\pi}f.

(iv)

\displaystyle\sum_{P^{\prime}\in X^{\prime}}\sum_{P\mapsto P^{\prime}}(f,g\circ\pi)_{P}=\sum_{P}(f,g\circ\pi)_{P}=0

So by
Proposition

2.1.2
,
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}
is a modulus for
\displaystyle\textrm{Tr}_{\pi}f
.
∎

Proposition 2.1.6
.

With the setting of
Definition

2.1.4
,
\displaystyle\textrm{Tr}_{\pi}f:X^{\prime}-S^{\prime}\rightarrow G
is a regular map.

Proof.

Write
\displaystyle n
for the degree of the covering
\displaystyle X\rightarrow X^{\prime}
.

Define
\displaystyle F:(X-S)^{n}\rightarrow G
to be

F(P_{1},...,P_{n})=f(P_{1})+...+f(P_{n}).

F is invariant under permutations of
\displaystyle(X-S)^{n}
, so
\displaystyle F
factors through
\displaystyle(X-S)^{(n)}
, giving a regular map
\displaystyle F^{\prime}:(X-S)^{(n)}\rightarrow G
.

For every
\displaystyle P^{\prime}\in X^{\prime}-S^{\prime}
, we identify
\displaystyle\pi^{-1}(P^{\prime})=\sum_{P\mapsto P^{\prime}}e_{P}P
with an element of
\displaystyle(X-S)^{(n)}
. The map

\pi^{-1}:X^{\prime}-S^{\prime}\rightarrow(X-S)^{(n)}

is regular.

The composition

F^{\prime}\circ\pi^{-1}:X^{\prime}-S^{\prime}\rightarrow G

is then a regular map equal to
\displaystyle\textrm{Tr}_{\pi}f
.
∎

A non-constant
\displaystyle g\in k(X)
is a covering from
\displaystyle X
to
\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{k}^{1}
, so the above proposition applies. Write
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}=\sum n_{P}P
. We write
\displaystyle g\equiv 0
mod
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}
if
\displaystyle v_{P}\geq n_{P}
for each
\displaystyle P
. If
\displaystyle g\equiv 0
mod
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}
, then
\displaystyle g(P)=0
for all
\displaystyle P\in S
, and
\displaystyle S^{\prime}=g(S)=\{0\}\subset\mathbf{P}_{k}^{1}
. Then
\displaystyle\textrm{Tr}_{g}f
is a regular map from
\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{k}^{1}
to
\displaystyle G
.

Proposition 2.1.7
.

\displaystyle f:X\dashrightarrow G
admits a modulus
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}
iff for every non-constant rational function
\displaystyle g
with
\displaystyle g\equiv 0
mod
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}
,
\displaystyle\textrm{Tr}_{g}f
is constant.

Proof.

Assume
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}
is a modulus of
\displaystyle f
. Let
\displaystyle g
be a non-constant function with
\displaystyle g\equiv 0
mod
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}
. For every
\displaystyle a\in\mathbf{P}_{k}^{1}
,
\displaystyle\textrm{Tr}_{g}f(a)=f(g^{-1}(a))
, which is
\displaystyle f((g)_{a})
. (
\displaystyle(g)_{a}
is the divisor of zeroes of
\displaystyle g-a
when
\displaystyle a\neq\infty
, and the divisor of poles of
\displaystyle g
when
\displaystyle a=\infty
)

If in addition
\displaystyle a\neq\infty
, then since
\displaystyle g\equiv 0
mod
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}
, we have that
\displaystyle g-a
is
\displaystyle-a
mod
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}
. By linearity of
\displaystyle f
as a map from the set of divisors prime to
\displaystyle S
, and the fact that it admits
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}
as a modulus, we have that
\displaystyle f((g-a))=0
, which means
\displaystyle\textrm{Tr}_{g}f(a)=f((g)_{a})=f((g-a)_{\infty})=f((g)_{\infty})=\textrm{Tr}_{g}f(\infty)
. So
\displaystyle\textrm{Tr}_{g}f
is constant.

Conversely, assume
\displaystyle\textrm{Tr}_{\pi}g
is constant for every non-constant
\displaystyle g\equiv 0
mod
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}
. Let
\displaystyle h\equiv 1
mod
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}
. We can write
\displaystyle h=1-g
with
\displaystyle g\equiv 0
mod
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}
. If
\displaystyle g
is constant, then
\displaystyle h
is also constant, and
\displaystyle f((h))=f(0)=0
. If
\displaystyle g
is non-constant, then
\displaystyle(h)=(g)_{1}-(g)_{\infty}
(the divisors of 0’s of
\displaystyle h
which are 1’s of
\displaystyle g
, minus the divisor of poles of
\displaystyle g
). So
\displaystyle f((h))=\textrm{Tr}_{g}f(1)-\textrm{Tr}_{g}f(\infty)=0
.
∎

2.2
Proof of Theorem 1 in Positive Characteristic

Assume
\displaystyle\textrm{char}(k)>0
. We first quote two theorems about the structure of connected commutative algebraic groups.

Theorem 2.2.1
.

(Chevalley) Every connected algebraic group
\displaystyle G
contains a normal subgroup
\displaystyle R
such that
\displaystyle R
is a connected linear group, and that
\displaystyle G/R
is an Abelian variety.

Theorem 2.2.2
.

Every connected commutative linear algebraic group is isomorphic to the product of some copies of the multiplicative group
\displaystyle\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{m}}
and a unipotent group
\displaystyle U
(that is,
\displaystyle U
is isomorphic to a subgroup of the group of triangular matrices having only 1’s on the principal diagonal).

We use these 2 theorems to reduce the proof as follows: let
\displaystyle f:X-S\rightarrow G
. Use
Theorem

2.2.1
to factor
\displaystyle G
as
\displaystyle G/R=A
. Use
Theorem

2.2.2
to write
\displaystyle R=R_{1}+R_{2}
, which gives that
\displaystyle G
is embedded as a subgroup in
\displaystyle G/R_{1}\times G/R_{2}
.

If
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}_{i}
(with support
\displaystyle S
) are moduli for the composed maps
\displaystyle X-S\rightarrow G\rightarrow G/R_{i}
for each
\displaystyle i=1,2
, then
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}=\textrm{Sup}(\mathbf{m}_{1},\mathbf{m}_{2})
is a modulus for
\displaystyle f
with support
\displaystyle S
. It would thus suffice to prove Theorem 1 for
\displaystyle G/R_{1}
and
\displaystyle G/R_{2}
, which are extensions of
\displaystyle A
by
\displaystyle R_{1}
and
\displaystyle R_{2}
. Repeating this process, and using
Theorem

2.2.2
, it suffices to prove Theorem 1 for the following 2 cases:

1.

\displaystyle G
is an extension of an abelian variety
\displaystyle A
by
\displaystyle\mathbf{G}_{m}
.

2.

\displaystyle G
is an extension of an abelian variety
\displaystyle A
by a unipotent group
\displaystyle U
.

First we prove case 1.

Lemma 2.2.3
.

Every regular map
\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{k}^{1}-\{point\}\rightarrow\mathbf{G}_{m}
is constant.

Proof.

We can assume that the point is
\displaystyle\infty
. In this case, we have a regular map
\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{k}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbf{A}_{k}^{1}-\{0\}
, which is a polynomial with no zero, which is a constant.
∎

Lemma 2.2.4
.

Every rational map from
\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{k}^{1}
to an abelian variety is constant.

Proof.

(This proof uses the theory of Jacobians, and is different from the proof given in [1]) By Theorem 3.2 of [3], any such rational map is regular (since
\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{k}^{1}
is non-singular). So such a map admits the modulus
\displaystyle 0
.

By the results of next chapter (which do not depend on results of this chapter), any such map factors through the Jacobian variety
\displaystyle J
of
\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{k}^{1}
, which is a point. So such a map is constant.
∎

Proposition 2.2.5
.

Theorem 1 is true when
\displaystyle G
is the extension of an abelian variety
\displaystyle A
by
\displaystyle\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{m}}
, taking
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}=\sum_{P\in S}P
.

Proof.

We use
Proposition

2.1.7
. Let
\displaystyle g
be a non-constant rational function on
\displaystyle X
such that
\displaystyle g\equiv 0
mod
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}
.

\displaystyle\textrm{Tr}_{g}f
is a regular map from
\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{k}^{1}-\{0\}
to
\displaystyle G
(by
Proposition

2.1.6
). Composing it with the projection
\displaystyle G\rightarrow A
, we get a rational map from
\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{k}^{1}
to
\displaystyle A
which is constant by
Lemma

2.2.4
. So
\displaystyle\textrm{Tr}_{g}f
takes values in a coset of
\displaystyle\mathbf{G}_{m}
, which is isomorphic to
\displaystyle\mathbf{G}_{m}
as varieties. By
Lemma

2.2.3
,
\displaystyle\textrm{Tr}_{g}f
is constant. By
Proposition

2.1.7
, we are done.
∎

Next we prove case 2.

Write
\displaystyle p
for the characteristic of
\displaystyle k
. Assume
\displaystyle G/U=A
, where
\displaystyle U
is unipotent, and
\displaystyle A
is an abelian variety. By considering an element
\displaystyle u\in U
as
\displaystyle 1+n
, n a nilpotent matrix in
\displaystyle\mathbf{GL}_{m}
. Then whenever
\displaystyle p^{r}\geq m
, we have
\displaystyle(1+n)^{p^{r}}=1+n^{p^{r}}=1
. On the other hand, according to a theorem of Weil,
\displaystyle p^{r}A=A
(here we write the group law additively).

Put
\displaystyle U^{\prime}=G/p^{r}G,G^{\prime}=A\times U^{\prime}
, and let
\displaystyle\theta:G\rightarrow G^{\prime}
be the product of the maps
\displaystyle G\rightarrow A
and
\displaystyle G\rightarrow U^{\prime}
.
\displaystyle p^{r}U^{\prime}=0
, so it has no subgroup isomorphic to an abelian variety, or
\displaystyle\mathbf{G}_{m}
, and so by
Theorem

2.2.2
it is nilpotent.

\displaystyle\textrm{Ker}(\theta)=U\cap p^{r}G
, and
\displaystyle p^{r}G
is an abelian variety (since
\displaystyle p^{r}U=0
,
\displaystyle p^{r}:G\rightarrow G
factors as
\displaystyle G\rightarrow A\rightarrow G
). So
\displaystyle\textrm{Ker}(\theta)
being the intersection of an affine variety with a complete variety, is a finite set.

Lemma 2.2.6
.

If
\displaystyle\theta:G\rightarrow G^{\prime}
is an algebraic homomorphism of commutative groups with finite kernel, then if
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}
is a modulus for
\displaystyle\theta\circ f
, it is also a modulus for
\displaystyle f
.

Proof.

We use
Proposition

2.1.7
. If
\displaystyle g
is a non-constant rational function on
\displaystyle X
such that
\displaystyle g\equiv 0
mo
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}
, then the map
\displaystyle\textrm{Tr}_{g}(\theta\circ f):\mathbf{P}_{k}^{1}-\{0\}\rightarrow G^{\prime}
is constant (by
Proposition

2.1.7
). We also have that
\displaystyle\textrm{Tr}_{g}(\theta\circ f)=\theta\circ\textrm{Tr}_{g}f
, which shows that
\displaystyle\textrm{Tr}_{g}f
takes values in a coset of
\displaystyle\textrm{Ker}(\theta)
, which is finite. As
\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{k}^{1}-\{0\}
is connected, it follows that
\displaystyle\textrm{Tr}_{g}f
is constant, so by
Proposition

2.1.7
,
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}
is also a modulus for
\displaystyle f
.
∎

So, by the same reduction as in the beginning of this section, it suffices to prove Theorem 1 when
\displaystyle G
is an abelian variety, and when
\displaystyle G
is a unipotent group.

If
\displaystyle G
is an abelian variety, then combining
Lemma

2.2.4
and
Proposition

2.1.7
, we get Theorem 1.

Assume
\displaystyle G
is a connected, unipotent, commutative group. We consider it as embedded in a linear group
\displaystyle\mathbf{GL}_{r}
, such that each of its elements is of the form
\displaystyle 1+N
, where
\displaystyle N=(n_{ij})
is a strictly upper triangular matrix. So we can consider
\displaystyle G

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{r\times r}

Chapter 4
Class Field Theory of Curves

Global class field theory classifies abelian extensions of global fields, which in the case of global function fields, is equivalent to classifying connected coverings of smooth, projective, geometrically connected curves over finite fields.

We will work in slightly more generality, and will classify (possibly non-connected) coverings of smooth, projective, geometrically connected curves over perfect fields.

. The normal basis theorem (which extends easily to the case of a Galois algebra over a field) for
\displaystyle k(W)|k(V)
guarantees a function
\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime}\in k(W)
whose Galois orbit forms a basis for the Galois algebra
\displaystyle k(W)|k(V)
. After a base change, the Galois orbit of
\displaystyle\varphi_{\bar{k}}
is a basis for
\displaystyle\bar{k}(W)|\bar{k}(V)
. Write
\displaystyle\varphi=\varphi^{\prime}_{\bar{k}}
. The theory of discriminants gives that
\displaystyle\textrm{det}(\varphi^{st})\neq 0\in\bar{k}(W)
(this is the content of [8], chapter 3, proposition 9). In particular, there exists some
\displaystyle x\in W
such that
\displaystyle\textrm{det}(\varphi^{st}(x))\neq 0
. This proves the theorem in the case of an algebraically closed base field.

\displaystyle G^{\prime}
can be defined over
\displaystyle k
, as it is just the complement of an affine variety defined by the vanishing of a determinant. Taking the quotient by
\displaystyle\mathfrak{g}
, we see that
\displaystyle G
can also be defined over
\displaystyle k
.
\displaystyle g
is also defined over
\displaystyle k
, as on each coordinate over
\displaystyle\bar{k}
, it is simply a regular function defined by coefficients in
\displaystyle k
. So, the theorem is true over
\displaystyle k

∎

With the setting of
