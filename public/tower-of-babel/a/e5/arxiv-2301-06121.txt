Port-Hamiltonian structure of interacting particle systems and its mean-field limit
Birgit Jacob , Claudia Totzeck

Abstract
We derive a minimal port-Hamiltonian formulation of a general class of interacting particle systems driven by alignment and potential-based force dynamics which include the Cucker-Smale model with potential interaction and the second order Kuramoto model. The port-Hamiltonian structure allows to characterize conserved quantities such as Casimir functions as well as the long-time behaviour using a LaSalle-type argument on the particle level. It is then shown that the port-Hamiltonian structure is preserved in the mean-field limit and an analogue of the LaSalle invariance principle is studied in the space of probability measures equipped with the 2-Wasserstein-metric. The results on the particle and mean-field limit yield a new perspective on uniform stability of general interacting particle systems. Moreover, as the minimal port-Hamiltonian formulation is closed we identify the ports of the subsystems which admit generalized mass-spring-damper structure modelling the binary interaction of two particles. Using the information of ports we discuss the coupling of difference species in a port-Hamiltonian preserving manner.

37K45, 82C22, 93A16.

Port-Hamiltonian systems, interacting particle systems, mean-field limit, long-time behaviour

Introduction

Since the seminal works by Reynolds
[
33
]
in 1987, Vicsek, Czirók, Ben-Jacob, Cohen, Shochet
[
40
]
in 1995, and Cucker, Smale
[
19
]
in 2007, mathematical modelling of interacting particle systems and the structural analysis of these models attracts the attention of researchers from applied mathematics. One of the fascinating aspects is that simple interaction rules imposed for the binary interaction of two particles lead to collective behaviour of the whole crowd. In fact, often slight parameter changes can turn dynamics of ordinary differential equations (ODEs), where the particles form rings that resemble the milling of birds into clumps
[
20
]
.

As the analysis of these pattern formation is difficult on the particle level, where the position and velocity information of each member of the crowds is explicitly captured by the equations, more abstract formulations of the dynamics were proposed. Sending the number of particles to infinity leads to the so-called mean-field formulation of the crowd
[
24
]
. Here, the exact position and velocity information is averaged and only the probability of finding a particle at a certain time in a certain position with a certain velocity is described. The binary interaction structure on the particle level turns into an convolution that yields a nonlinear, nonlocal partial differential equation (PDE) as evolution equation on the mean-field level. Instead of a huge system of ODEs the mean-field equation requires the solution of a high-dimensional PDE. To further reduce the dimensionality of the problem, also hydro-dynamic descriptions were proposed, there the velocity information is averaged over space, leading to a coupled PDE-system that is only space-dependent
[
9
]
. In contrast to the passage from mean-field level to hydro-dynamic formulations, which in general require formal closure relations, the rigorous relationship of particle and mean-field limit is well-understood, see for example
[
24
]
. For an detailed overview of different models on the various scales we refer to
[
34
]
.

Many interacting particle systems are driven by two mechanisms: alignment in velocity and attraction/repulsion in space
[
12
]
. Alignment is the main component of (generalized) Cucker-Smale dynamics and leads to bird-like behaviour. Many different interaction kernels were proposed to fit the model closer to reality, see for example
[
1
,
11
,
18
,
28
]
.
On the other hand, there are models that incorporate only attraction/repulsion forces leading to a three-phase interaction behaviour of long-range attraction, short-range repulsion and a mid-range comfort zone, where no interaction forces are present
[
3
,
20
,
8
]
. These models often admit a gradient-structure, that means the binary interaction forces are gradients of a prescribed interaction potential like the Morse potential
[
9
]
and the force acting on one particle is the average of all these binary interaction forces.

The gradient-structure opens the toolbox of gradient flows and large deviation theory to study stability and long-time behaviour of the systems
[
4
]
. In the absence of a gradient structure, the analysis of the alignment part of the dynamics is generally more complex. However, starting with
[
13
]
stability results for different setups in the Cucker-Smale context were established
[
29
,
31
,
25
,
17
,
16
,
23
,
5
,
32
,
2
]
. In the literature stable states of alignment dynamics are often called flocking, clustering or consensus solutions.

In
[
8
]
flocking behaviour of a three-zone model was first investigated with the help of the energy of the system. Although we address a similar question, in this contribution we propose a novel viewpoint on interacting particle dynamics, namely a port-Hamiltonian one. Building on the discussion of
[
27
]
, where the port-Hamiltonian system (PHS) formulation of a Cucker-Smale dynamics with repulsion and attraction is interpreted as generalized mass-spring-damper system, we propose a minimal PHS representation of interacting particle systems that does not require an increase of the phase space. Indeed, the formulation in
[
27
]
introduces the relative positions of all particles as new variables, thereby increasing the state space dimension from
2Nd
to
N(N-1)d/2+Nd
where
N
denotes the number of particles and
d
the space dimension. In the following, the port-Hamiltonian reformulation preserves the state space dimension which allows in particular to pass to the mean-field limit.

The port-Hamiltonian formulation of interacting particles opens the door to the well-established theory of PHS for finite-dimensional systems, see

[
39
,
22
,
21
]
. Well-known facts from PHS theory include the property that PHS are closed under network interconnection, that is, coupling of port-Hamiltonian systems again leads to a port-Hamiltonian system. Furthermore, the port-Hamiltonian approach is suitable for the investigation of the qualitative solution behavior such as asymptotic stability and control questions, as it provides an energy balance. Moreover, the port-Hamiltonian structure allows to identify several conserved quantities namely the Hamiltonian and the so-called Casimir functions. The mean-field limit yields a connection to nonlinear, nonlocal infinite-dimensional PHS systems which are yet less explored.

Our main contributions are: the port-Hamiltonian formulation of an interacting particle systems with same state space dimension; structure preserving mean-field limit; characterization of long-time behavior on the particle and mean-field level and the characterization of conserved quantities such as the Hamiltonian and Casimir functions. This yields a new perspective on uniform stability and the dissipativity of interacting particle systems using the PH dissipativity inequality. Moreover, the idetification of ports allows for PH structure preserving coupling of interacting particle systems of (different) species.

The article is organized as follows: we recall some background information on interacting particle systems in Section
2
. Then we motivate derive the port-Hamiltonian reformulation in Section
3
, that is used to discuss the Casimir function and stability properties of the systems based on LaSalle theory. In Section
4
we dicuss the mean-field limit sending the number of particles
N\rightarrow\infty
and show that the PH structure is conserved in the limit. Section
5
discusses the coupling of (different) species in a PH structure preserving manner. The article concludes with a summary of the main ideas and an outlook to future work.

We recall the classical formulation of interacting particle systems in position and velocity coordinates and the corresponding mean-field limit. Let us consider
N\in\mathbb{N},N\geq 2

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}x_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\psi(|x_{j}-x_{i}|)(v_{j}-v_{i})-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\nabla\mathcal{V}(x_{i}-x_{j})=:F_{i}(t,x,v),

\displaystyle x_{i}(0)

\displaystyle=\hat{x}_{i},\qquad v_{i}(0)=\hat{v}_{i}.

(2)

This general class of interaction models contains well-known examples:

.

Let Assumption

As we are interested in the limiting behaviour as

3
Port-Hamiltonian formulation

In this section, we derive two port-Hamiltonian formulations of the interacting particle system. We emphasize that both port-Hamiltonian formulations preserve the state space dimension. This is in contrast to
[
27
]
where all relative positions between the particles are considered. First, we discuss the reformulation of the system given in
(x,v)
coordinates. Then we present a variant that exploits the translational invariance of the systems. This enables us to benefit from LaSalle’s invariance principle to analyse the flocking behaviour.

Let us define
z=(x_{1},\dots,x_{N},v_{1},\dots,v_{N})
. The Hamiltonian of the system (
1
) is given by the sum of kinetic and potential energy

\mathcal{H}^{N}(z)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Big(v_{i}^{\top}v_{i}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathcal{V}(x_{i}-x_{j})\Big),\quad z=(x_{1},\dots,x_{N},v_{1},\dots,v_{N}).

Then we calculate

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}^{N}(z)}{\partial z}=\left(-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\nabla\mathcal{V}(x_{j}-x_{1}),\ldots,-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\nabla\mathcal{V}(x_{j}-x_{N}),v\right)^{\top}

and obtain

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}z=\left(\begin{pmatrix}0&I\\
-I&0\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&\Psi(z)\end{pmatrix}\right)\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}^{N}(z)}{\partial z},\quad z(0)=z_{0},

(4)

where

\Psi(z)=\frac{1}{N}\begin{pmatrix}\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}\psi(|x_{j}-x_{1}|)&-\psi(|x_{2}-x_{1}|)&\ldots&-\psi(|x_{N}-x_{1}|)\\
-\psi(|x_{1}-x_{2}|)&\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}\psi(|x_{j}-x_{2}|)&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&&\\
&&&-\psi(|x_{N}-x_{N-1}|)\\
-\psi(|x_{1}-x_{N}|)&\ldots&-\psi(|x_{N-1}-x_{N}|)&\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}\psi(|x_{j}-x_{N}|)\end{pmatrix}.

The state space of the port-Hamiltonian system (
4
) is given by
X:=\mathbb{R}^{Nd+Nd}
.

Remark
.

For later use, we remark that
\Psi(z)
is diagonally dominant with non negative diagonal elements, hence positive semi-definite. Further,
\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{Nd}:v=(\tilde{v},\dots,\tilde{v})\text{ for some }\tilde{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
is contained in the kernel of
\Psi(z)
. Moreover, if
\psi(x)>0
for
x\in\mathbb{R}
, then the eigenspace corresponding to the zero eigenvector is spanned by
\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{Nd}:v=(\tilde{v},\dots,\tilde{v})\text{ for some }\tilde{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
.

As the positions of the particles appear only relatively in the dynamics, interacting particle systems are translational invariant w.r.t.
x
. This motivates us to consider the dynamics with center of mass shifted to zero. Let us therefore define the center of mass by
\bar{x}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}
and consider
z=(r,v)=(r_{1},\dots,r_{N},v_{1},\dots,v_{N})
with
r_{i}=x_{i}-\bar{x}
being the position relative to the center of mass. Note that the velocity of the center of mass is conserved. Indeed, using

\nabla\mathcal{V}(-r)=-\nabla\mathcal{V}(r)
, we find

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}v_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\psi(|r_{j}-r_{i}|)(v_{j}-v_{i})-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\nabla\mathcal{V}(r_{i}-r_{j})=0,

(5)

Let us denote
\bar{v}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}v_{j}(0)
. The shifted dynamics is given by

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}r_{i}

\displaystyle=v_{i}-\bar{v},

\displaystyle r_{i}(0)=(r_{0})_{i},

(6a)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}v_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\psi(|r_{i}-r_{j}|)(v_{j}-v_{i})-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\nabla\mathcal{V}(r_{i}-r_{j}),

\displaystyle v_{i}(0)=(v_{0})_{i}.

(6b)

We want to emphasize that system (
1
) contains explicit position information of the particles, which we loose in the PHS formulation as we shift by the center of mass. However, pattern formation and uniform stability which are of interest in the context of interacting particle systems are translational invariant, hence the explicit position information plays a minor role.

The mean velocity
\bar{v}
can be incorporated in the Hamiltonian leading to

\mathcal{H}^{N}(z)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Big((v_{i}-\bar{v})^{\top}(v_{i}-\bar{v})+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathcal{V}(r_{i}-r_{j})\Big)

with partial derivative

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}^{N}(z)}{\partial z}=\left(-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\nabla\mathcal{V}(x_{j}-x_{1}),\ldots,-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\nabla\mathcal{V}(x_{j}-x_{N}),v_{1}-\bar{v},\dots,v_{N}-\bar{v}\right)^{\top}.

For notational convenience, we define
v-\textbf{1}\bar{v}:=(v_{1}-\bar{v},\dots,v_{N}-\bar{v})^{\top}
.

To find the port-Hamiltonian structure we note that the upper-left part of the skew-symmetric matrix is predefined by the differential equation for
r
. Indeed, we obtain

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}r=I(v-\textbf{1}\bar{v}).

Thanks to the structure of
\Psi
, see Remark
Remark
, it holds

\Psi(z)\textbf{1}\bar{v}=0,

(7)

and hence we obtain

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}v=-I\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}^{N}(z)}{\partial r}-\Psi(z)\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}^{N}(z)}{\partial v}

with

\Psi(z)=\frac{1}{N}\begin{pmatrix}\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}\!\psi(|r_{j}-r_{1}|)&-\psi(|r_{2}-r_{1}|)&\ldots&-\psi(|r_{N}-r_{1}|)\\
-\psi(|r_{1}-r_{2}|)&\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}\!\psi(|r_{j}-r_{2}|)&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&&\\
&&&-\psi(|r_{N}\!-\!r_{N-1}|)\\
-\psi(|r_{1}-r_{N}|)&\ldots&-\psi(|r_{N-1}\!-\!r_{N}|)&\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}\!\psi(|r_{j}-r_{N}|)\end{pmatrix}.

Combining the two equations yields the port-Hamiltonian structure

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}z=\left[\begin{pmatrix}0&I\\
-I&0\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&\Psi(z)\end{pmatrix}\right]\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}^{N}(z)}{\partial z}.

(8)

The state space of the port-Hamiltonian system (
8
) is given by
X:=\mathbb{R}^{Nd+Nd}
.

The main results of the article are based on this formulation. The following theorem discusses the well-posedness and some characteristics of solutions to the port-Hamiltonian system.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let Assumption
2.1
hold, then for every initial condition
z_{0}=(r_{0},v_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{Nd+Nd}
the port-Hamiltonian system (
8
) possesses a unique global solution
z=(r,v)
satisfying the dissipativity inequality

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{H}^{N}(z)=-\langle(v-\textbf{1}\bar{v}),\Psi(z)(v-\textbf{1}\bar{v})\rangle\leq 0,

(9)

where

\bar{v}:=\sum_{j=1}^{N}v_{j}(0).

Further, the velocity of the center of mass is conserved, that is,

\bar{v}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}v_{j}(t)
,
t\geq 0
.

Proof.

The existence of a unique global solution
z=(r,v)
follows from Proposition
2.2
.
The non-negativity of
\psi
yields
\Psi(z)
is real, symmetric and diagonally dominant, hence positive semi-definite.
Thus we compute for the solution
z

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{H}^{N}(z)

\displaystyle=\left\langle\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}^{N}(z)}{\partial z},\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}z\right\rangle=\left\langle\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}^{N}(z)}{\partial z},\left[\begin{pmatrix}0&I\\
-I^{\top}&0\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&\Psi(z)\end{pmatrix}\right]\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}^{N}(z)}{\partial z}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=-\langle v-\textbf{1}\bar{v},\Psi(z)v-\textbf{1}\bar{v}\rangle\leq 0.

This shows the dissipativity inequality.
Finally, let
z_{0}=(r_{0},v_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{Nd}\times\mathbb{R}^{Nd}
. Then (
5
)
implies that
\sum_{i=1}^{N}v_{i}
is constant. This completes the proof.
∎

In the following we assume that Assumption
2.1
is satisfied, and thus the port-Hamiltonian system (
8
) has for every initial condition a unique global solution. Next we investigate conserved quantities of our port-Hamiltonian formulation of interacting particle systems.

Definition 3.2

(Definition 6.4.1 in
[
38
]
)
.

A function
C:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
that is partially differentiable is called a
Casimir function
for the port-Hamiltonian system (

) if

\left(\frac{\partial C}{\partial z}(z)\right)^{\top}\left[\begin{pmatrix}0&I\\
-I&0\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&\Psi(z)\end{pmatrix}\right]=0,\qquad z\in X.

A Casimir function is a conserved quantity as for solutions
z
we obtain

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}C(z)=\left(\frac{\partial C}{\partial z}(z)\right)^{\top}\left[\begin{pmatrix}0&I\\
-I&0\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&\Psi(z)\end{pmatrix}\right]\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}^{N}(z)}{\partial z}=0

independently of the Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}^{N}
.

Proposition 3.3
.

A function
C:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
that is partially differentiable is a Casimir function for the port-Hamiltonian system (

) if and only if

C(r,v)=\gamma

for some
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

In
[
38
]
it is shown that a function
C:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
that is partially differentiable is a Casimir function for the port-Hamiltonian system (
8
) if and only if

\left(\frac{\partial C}{\partial z}(z)\right)^{\top}\begin{pmatrix}0&I\\
-I&0\end{pmatrix}=0\quad\text{and}\quad\left(\frac{\partial C}{\partial z}(z)\right)^{\top}\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&\Psi(z)\end{pmatrix}=0,\qquad z\in X.

This is equivalent to

\frac{\partial C}{\partial r}(z)=0\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\partial C}{\partial v}(z)=0,\qquad z\in X,

and thus the statement of the proposition follows.
∎

Remark
.

Note that the system matrices of the formulations in
(x,v)
and
(r,v)
coincide. This allows to conclude that the the Casimir functions of the different formulations coincide as well.

Remark
.

We want to emphasize that, in contrast to
[
27
]
, we do not require a null space condition in order to define the Casimir function in neither of the two formulations. This is due to the different choice of the port-Hamiltonian formulation.

We conclude this section with a sufficient condition for asymptotic stability. Here the function
\mathcal{H}^{N}
serves as candidate for a suitable Lyapunov function. The following lemma will be useful.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let Assumption
2.1
hold,
\psi(x)>0
for
x\in\mathbb{R}
and we define

\mathcal{N}:=\{z=(r,v)\in X\mid-\langle v-\mathbf{1}\bar{v},\Psi(z)(v-\mathbf{1}\bar{v})\rangle=0\}.

Let
z:[0,\infty)\rightarrow X
,
z=(r,v)
, be a solution of (
6
) with
z(t)\in\mathcal{N}
for
t\geq 0
. Then the function
v
is constant,

v_{i}(t)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}v_{j}(0)\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{j=1}^{N}\nabla\mathcal{V}(r_{i}(t)-r_{j}(t))=0,\qquad i=1,\ldots,N,\,t\geq 0.

Proof.

As
\psi(x)>0
for
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we obtain
v_{i}(t)=v_{j}(t)
for
i,j=1,\ldots,N
and
t\geq 0
. This together with Theorem
3.1
implies that
v
is constant and satisfies
v_{i}(t)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}v_{j}(0)
for
i=1,\ldots,N
, and
t\geq 0
. The remaining statement follows directly from equation (
6
).
∎

Theorem 3.5
.

Let Assumption
2.1
hold,
\psi(x)>0
for
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
\nabla\mathcal{V}
bounded.
Then for every initial condition
z_{0}=(r_{0},v_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{Nd}\times\mathbb{R}^{Nd}
the corresponding solution
z
of (
8
) satisfies

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\dist(z(t),L)=0,

where

L:=\Big\{(\tilde{r},\tilde{v})\in\mathbb{R}^{Nd}\times\mathbb{R}^{Nd}\mid\tilde{v}_{i}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}v_{j}(0),\,\,\sum_{j=1}^{N}\nabla\mathcal{V}(\tilde{r}_{i}-\tilde{r}_{j})=0,\,i=1,\ldots,N\Big\}.

Proof.

Our goal is to apply LaSalle’s stability theorem
[
26
, Theorem 3.2.11]
, hence we have to show that the trajectories are contained in a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{Nd}\times\mathbb{R}^{Nd}
.

Let
\bar{v}
the mean velocity as above. We estimate

\displaystyle\|v(t)-\textbf{1}\bar{v}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|v(0)-\textbf{1}\bar{v}\|^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}2(v-\textbf{1}\bar{v})\cdot\frac{d}{ds}(v-\textbf{1}\bar{v})\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\|v(0)-\textbf{1}\bar{v}\|^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}2(v-\textbf{1}\bar{v})(-\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\mathcal{H}^{N}(z)-\Psi(z)(v-\textbf{1}\bar{v}))\mathrm{d}s,

where we used
\frac{d}{dt}\bar{v}=0
and
\Psi(z)\textbf{1}\bar{v}=0
. The boundedness of
\nabla\mathcal{V}
and the Peter-Paul inequality allow us to estimate for
\varepsilon>0

\displaystyle\|v(t)-\textbf{1}\bar{v}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|v(0)-\textbf{1}\bar{v}\|^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\|\nabla\mathcal{V}\|_{\infty}^{2}+\varepsilon\|v-\textbf{1}\bar{v}\|^{2}-2(v-\textbf{1}\bar{v})\Psi(z)(v-\textbf{1}\bar{v})\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq\|v(0)-\textbf{1}\bar{v}\|^{2}+\frac{t}{\varepsilon}\|\nabla\mathcal{V}\|_{\infty}^{2}-(2\lambda_{2}-\varepsilon)\int_{0}^{t}\|v-\textbf{1}\bar{v}\|^{2}\mathrm{d}s,

where we denote by
\lambda_{2}
the second smallest eigenvalue of
\Psi(z)
. Note that
\lambda_{2}>0
since the
(n-1)\times(n-1)
submatrix
\big(\Psi(z)_{ij}\big)_{i=1,\dots,n-1;j=1,\dots,n-1}
is strictly diagonally dominant and therefore positive definite.

We define

\alpha(t):=\|v(0)-\textbf{1}\bar{v}\|^{2}+\frac{t}{\varepsilon}\|\nabla\mathcal{V}\|_{\infty}^{2},

and then Gronwall’s inequality yields

\|v(t)-\textbf{1}\bar{v}\|^{2}\leq\alpha(t)e^{-(2\lambda_{2}-\varepsilon)t}.

Choosing
\varepsilon
such that
2\lambda_{2}>\varepsilon>0
, we find that
v(t)
relaxed towards
\textbf{1}\bar{v}
. In particular,

\sup\{\|v(t)-\textbf{1}\bar{v}\|^{2}\mid t\geq 0\}<\infty,

which shows the boundedness of the solution trajectory w.r.t. the velocity variable.

We are left to show the boundedness of the trajectory w.r.t. the positions. We estimate

\displaystyle\|r(t)\|\leq\|r(0)\|+\int_{0}^{t}\|v(s)-\textbf{1}\bar{v}\|\mathrm{d}s\leq\|r(0)\|+\int_{0}^{\infty}\alpha(s)e^{-(2\lambda_{2}-\varepsilon)s}\mathrm{d}s.

As the integral on the right hand side converges, we
conclude that also the position variables of the trajectories are bounded uniformly for all
t\geq 0
. Altogether, for any initial data the trajectories are contained in a compact set for all times. The statement follows by LaSalle’s stability theorem.
∎

Example 3.6
.

Let us consider the example of the Cucker-Smale dynamics with potential, where the alignment function

]

\psi(|r_{j}-r_{i}|)=\frac{K}{(\delta^{2}+|r_{j}-r_{i}|^{2})^{\beta}},\qquad K,\delta>0\text{ and }\beta\geq 0

and (regularized) Morse potential

.
Then

4
Mean-field limit

We obtain a candidate for the mean-field equation of the shifted dynamics following the standard derivation and moreover the mean-field Hamiltonian by rescaling with
\frac{1}{N}
. In fact, using the empirical measure

f^{N}(t,r,v)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta(r-r_{i}(t))\otimes\delta(v-v_{i}(t)),

were
(r_{i},v_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
denotes the solution of (
6
), we obtain the PDE describing the mean-field dynamics given by

\partial_{t}f^{N}+\nabla_{r}\cdot\Big(\big(v-\bar{v}\big)f^{N}\Big)=\nabla_{v}\cdot\Big(\big(\int\psi(r-\hat{r})(v-\hat{v})+\nabla\mathcal{V}(r-\hat{r})\mathrm{d}f^{N}(\hat{r},\hat{v})\big)f^{N}\Big),

(11)

where
\bar{v}=\int\hat{v}\mathrm{d}f^{N}(0,\hat{r},\hat{v})
.
Moreover, for the mean-field Hamiltonian we obtain

\lim\limits_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{N}\mathcal{H}^{N}(z)=\lim\limits_{N\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{2}\left((v-\bar{v})^{\top}(v-\bar{v})+\mathcal{V}(r-\hat{r})\right)\mathrm{d}f^{N}(t,\hat{r},\hat{v})\mathrm{d}f^{N}(t,r,v).

This motivates to define the Hamiltonian for the mean-field equation as

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(f)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{2}\left((v-\bar{v})^{\top}(v-\bar{v})+\mathcal{V}(r-\hat{r})\right)\mathrm{d}f(t,\hat{r},\hat{v})\mathrm{d}f(t,r,v)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\ast\big(v^{\top}v+\mathcal{V}(r)\big)\mathrm{d}f(t,r,v),

where
\bar{v}=\int\hat{v}\mathrm{d}f(t,\hat{r},\hat{v})
.

To check if the Hamiltonian structure is preserved in the mean-field limit we compute the variation of
\mathcal{H}(f)
in the space of probability measures, see also
[
6
]
. In order to preserve the normalization of the measures, we consider the push forward of
f
w.r.t.
(\text{id}+\varepsilon\zeta)
for
\zeta\in\mathcal{C}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathbb{R}^{2d})
,
\zeta(r,v)=(\zeta_{r}(r,v),\zeta_{v}(r,v))
and
\varepsilon>0
to find

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}((\text{id}+\varepsilon\zeta)_{\#}f)-\mathcal{H}(f)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(v+\varepsilon\zeta_{v}(r,v)-\bar{v}\right)^{\top}\left(v+\varepsilon\zeta_{v}(r,v)-\bar{v}\right)\mathrm{d}f(t,\hat{r},\hat{v})\mathrm{d}f(t,r,v)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{V}((r+\varepsilon\zeta_{r}(r,v))-\hat{r}-\varepsilon\zeta_{r}(\hat{r},\hat{v})))\mathrm{d}f(t,\hat{r},\hat{v})\mathrm{d}f(t,r,v)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left((v-\bar{v})^{\top}(v-\bar{v})+\mathcal{V}(r-\hat{r})\right)\mathrm{d}f(t,\hat{r},\hat{v})\mathrm{d}f(t,r,v)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}(v-\bar{v})^{\top}\zeta_{v}(r,v)+\nabla\mathcal{V}(r-\hat{r})\zeta_{r}(r,v)\,\mathrm{d}f(t,\hat{r},\hat{v})\mathrm{d}f(t,r,v)+o(\varepsilon),

where we used (
2
) and Fubini’s Theorem.
In the limit
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
we obtain

\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\Big(\mathcal{H}((\text{id}+\varepsilon\zeta)_{\#}f)-\mathcal{H}(f)\Big)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\begin{pmatrix}\nabla\mathcal{V}(r-\hat{r})\\
v-\bar{v}\end{pmatrix}\cdot\zeta(r,v)\mathrm{d}f(t,\hat{r},\hat{v})\mathrm{d}f(t,r,v).

Following
[
4
]
we can identify

\nabla_{f}\mathcal{H}(f)=\begin{pmatrix}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nabla\mathcal{V}(r-\hat{r})\,\mathrm{d}f(t,\hat{r},\hat{v})\right)\\
v-\bar{v}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nabla\mathcal{V}(r-\hat{r})\,\mathrm{d}f(t,\hat{r},\hat{v})\right)\\
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}(v-\hat{v})\;\mathrm{d}f(t,\hat{r},\hat{v})\end{pmatrix}=f\ast\begin{pmatrix}\nabla\mathcal{V}(r)\\
v\end{pmatrix}.

(12)

We can rewrite the mean-field equation as

\partial_{t}f=-\nabla_{(r,v)}\cdot\left(f\left(f\ast\left(\begin{pmatrix}0&I\\
-I&0\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&\psi(r)\end{pmatrix}\right)\begin{pmatrix}\nabla\mathcal{V}(r)\\
v\end{pmatrix}\right)\right).

(13)

As expected we obtain the dissipativity inequality also on the mean-field level.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\psi(x)>0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
and Assumption
2.1
hold. Then the dynamics (
13
) admits for every initial condition
f_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
a unique solution
f
in the sense of Definition
2.3
. Moreover, the solution is dissipative, i.e. it holds

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{H}(f)\leq 0.

Further, for every initial condition
f_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
there exists a vector
\bar{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\int v\,\mathrm{d}f(t,r,v)=\bar{v}\quad\text{for }t\geq 0.

Proof.

We prove the last statement first. Indeed, using integration by parts we calculate

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int v\,\mathrm{d}f(t,r,v)

\displaystyle=\int v\,\nabla_{v}\cdot\Big(\int\psi(|r-\hat{r}|)(v-\hat{v})-\nabla\mathcal{V}(r-\hat{r})\mathrm{d}f(t,\hat{r},\hat{v})f\Big)\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle=\iint\psi(|r-\hat{r}|)(v-\hat{v})-\nabla\mathcal{V}(r-\hat{r})\mathrm{d}f(t,\hat{r},\hat{v})\mathrm{d}f(t,r,v)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\iint\nabla\mathcal{V}(r-\hat{r})\mathrm{d}f(t,\hat{r},\hat{v})\mathrm{d}f(t,r,v)+\frac{1}{2}\iint\nabla\mathcal{V}(r-\hat{r})\mathrm{d}f(t,\hat{r},\hat{v})\mathrm{d}f(t,r,v)

\displaystyle=0,

which proves that the first moment with respect to the velocity is preserved. The well-posedness of (
13
) is obtained with the same arguments as (
3
) in Proposition
2.4
.

For the dissipativity we use the product rule, the symmetry of
\psi
and the anti-symmetry of
\nabla\mathcal{V}
to obtain

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{H}(f)=-\frac{1}{2}\iint(v-\hat{v})\cdot(\psi(|r-\hat{r}|)(v-\hat{v}))\mathrm{d}f(t,\hat{r},\hat{v})\mathrm{d}f(t,r,v)\leq 0.\qed

Next we investigate the characteristics and their port-Hamiltonian formulation. The characteristics
Z_{t}(z)=(R_{t}(r),V_{t}(v))
read

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}R_{t}(r)

\displaystyle=V_{t}(v)-\int V_{t}(v)\mathrm{d}f_{0}(r,v),\qquad\text{law}(z)=f(0,r,v),

(14a)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}V_{t}(v)

\displaystyle=\int\psi(|R_{t}(r)-R_{t}(\hat{r})|)(V_{t}(\hat{v})-V_{t}(v))-\nabla\mathcal{V}(R_{t}(r)-R_{t}(\hat{r}))\,\mathrm{d}f(0,\hat{r},\hat{v}),

(14b)

and we can rewrite a solution to the mean-field equation as
f(t)=Z_{t}{{}_{\#}}f(0),
where
\#
denotes the push-forward operator given by

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(z)\,\mathrm{d}f(t,z)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(Z_{t}(z))\,\mathrm{d}f(0,z).

A natural question is if the Dobrushin inequality in combination with the stability result for the ODE dynamics yields a stability result on the mean-field level. This would require the interchangebility of the limits
N\rightarrow\infty
and
t\rightarrow\infty
which is beyond the scope of this article. However, we prove the stability on the mean-field limit with the help of LaSalle’s theorem in metric spaces
[
26
, Theorem 3.2.11]
in the following. Clearly, the precompactness argument is more involved in this setting. We begin with a the mean-field analogue of Lemma
3.4
. For notational convenience we write in the following
f_{t}
for
f(t,\cdot)
as we already did in Section
2
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let Assumption
2.1
hold,
\psi(x)>0
for
x\in\mathbb{R}
and

{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathcal{N}:=\{h\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})\mid\iint(v-\hat{v})^{\top}\psi(|r-\hat{r}|)(v-\hat{v})\mathrm{d}h(\hat{r},\hat{v})\mathrm{d}h(r,v)=0\}.}

Let
f\colon[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
be a solution of (
13
) with
f_{t}\in\mathcal{N}
for
t\geq 0
. Then the support of
f_{t}
in the velocity space is concentrated at
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\bar{v}:=\int v\mathrm{d}f_{0}(r,v)}
, i.e. there exists
g_{t}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that it holds

f_{t}(r,v)=g_{t}(r)\delta(v-{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\bar{v}})\quad\text{and}\quad\int\nabla\mathcal{V}(r-\hat{r})\mathrm{d}g_{t}(\hat{r})=0,\quad\text{for all }r\in\mathrm{supp}(g_{t}),t\geq 0.

Proof.

As
\psi(x)>0
for
x\in\mathbb{R}
, the support of
f_{t}
in the velocity variable has to be concentrated at some velocity
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\bar{v}}
for all

in order to satisfy the integration condition in the definition of
\mathcal{N}
. Together with Theorem
4.1
this implies that
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\bar{v}=\int v\mathrm{d}f_{0}(r,v)}
. The remaining statement follows directly from equation (
13
).
∎

Theorem 4.3
.

Let Assumption
2.1
hold and there exists
\underline{\psi}
with
\psi(x)\geq\underline{\psi}>0
for
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Assume that
\mathcal{H}
is bounded from below and that
\nabla\mathcal{V}
is bounded. Then for every initial condition
f_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
the corresponding solution
f
of (
13
) satisfies

\lim\limits_{t\rightarrow\infty}\mathrm{dist}(f_{t},\mathcal{L})=0,

where

\mathcal{L}=\left\{f\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})\mid f=g(x)\delta(v-\hat{v}),\quad\int\nabla\mathcal{V}(x-\bar{x})\mathrm{d}g(x)=0,\quad\hat{v}=\int v\,\mathrm{d}f_{0}(x,v)\right\}.

Proof.

The proof follows the lines of the finite-dimensional result. The aim is again to employ LaSalle’s invariance theorem. Hence we have to show that the probability measure corresponding to the solution stays compactly supported for all times
t\geq 0
, since then the precompactness w.r.t.
\mathcal{W}_{2}
follows by
[
30
, Proposition 2.2.3]
.

We first consider the 2-Wasserstein distance of
f_{t}
and
\delta_{\bar{v}}
, where
\bar{v}=\int v\mathrm{d}f_{t}(r,v)
, given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{2}

\displaystyle(f_{t},\delta_{\bar{v}})^{2}

\displaystyle=\int|v-\bar{v}|^{2}\mathrm{d}f_{t}=\int|v-\bar{v}|^{2}\mathrm{d}f_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\int|v-\bar{v}|^{2}\partial_{s}f_{s}\mathrm{d}z\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\int|v-\bar{v}|^{2}\mathrm{d}f_{0}-\int_{0}^{t}\int|v-\bar{v}|^{2}\cdot\nabla_{(r,v)}\cdot\left(f\ast\left(\begin{pmatrix}0&I\\
-I&0\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&\psi(r)\end{pmatrix}\right)\begin{pmatrix}\nabla\mathcal{V}(r)\\
v\end{pmatrix}\right)\mathrm{d}f_{s}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\int|v-\bar{v}|^{2}\mathrm{d}f_{0}-2\int_{0}^{t}\int(f\ast v)(f\ast(\psi(r)v+f\ast\nabla\mathcal{V}(r)))\mathrm{d}f_{s}\mathrm{d}s.

A simple computation shows that
(f\ast v)(f\ast\psi(r)v)=\frac{1}{2}f\ast(v\psi(r)v)
which will be helpful in the following.

This and Peter and Paul inequality applied to the term with the potential interactions allows us to further estimate

\displaystyle\int|v-\bar{v}|^{2}\mathrm{d}f_{t}

\displaystyle\leq\int|v-\bar{v}|^{2}\mathrm{d}f_{0}-\int_{0}^{t}\int\int(\underline{\psi}-\varepsilon)|v-\hat{v}|^{2}\mathrm{d}\hat{f}_{s}\mathrm{d}f_{s}\mathrm{d}s+\frac{\|\nabla\mathcal{V}\|_{\infty}^{2}}{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\leq\int|v-\bar{v}|^{2}\mathrm{d}f_{0}-\int_{0}^{t}\int(\underline{\psi}-\varepsilon)|v-\bar{v}|^{2}\mathrm{d}f_{s}\mathrm{d}s+\frac{\|\nabla\mathcal{V}\|_{\infty}^{2}}{\varepsilon}.

An application of Gronwall’s inequality yields

\mathcal{W}_{2}(f_{t},\delta_{\bar{v}})^{2}=\int|v-\bar{v}|^{2}\mathrm{d}f_{t}\leq\left(\int|v-\bar{v}|^{2}\mathrm{d}f_{0}+\frac{\|\nabla\mathcal{V}\|_{\infty}^{2}}{\varepsilon}\right)e^{-(\underline{\psi}-\varepsilon)t}.

This shows that the support of
f_{t}
w.r.t.
v
strictly decays over time.

We use this result to show the boundedness of the support w.r.t.
r
. Indeed, we obtain

\displaystyle\int|r|^{2}\mathrm{d}f_{t}

\displaystyle=\int|r|^{2}\mathrm{d}f_{0}-2\int_{0}^{t}\int r\cdot(v-\bar{v})\mathrm{d}f_{s}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq 1+\int|r|^{2}\mathrm{d}f_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\left(\int|r|^{2}\mathrm{d}f_{s}\right)^{1/2}\;\left(\int|v-\bar{v}|^{2}\mathrm{d}f_{s}\right)^{1/2}\mathrm{d}s,

and thus

\displaystyle\left(\int|r|^{2}\mathrm{d}f_{t}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq 1+\int|r|^{2}\mathrm{d}f_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\left(\int|r|^{2}\mathrm{d}f_{s}\right)^{1/2}\;\left(\int|v-\bar{v}|^{2}\mathrm{d}f_{s}\right)^{1/2}\mathrm{d}s,

where we used
x>\sqrt{x}
for
x>1
.

We define
\alpha:=(1+\int|r|^{2}\mathrm{d}f_{0})
and
\beta:=\left(\int|v-\bar{v}|^{2}\mathrm{d}f_{0}+\frac{\|\nabla\mathcal{V}\|_{\infty}^{2}}{\varepsilon}\right)

Then we obtain with Gronwall inequality

\left(\int|r|^{2}\mathrm{d}f_{t}\right)^{1/2}\leq\alpha\exp(\int_{0}^{t}\beta e^{-(\underline{\psi}-\varepsilon)s}\mathrm{d}s).

Since the integral converges, also the support of
f_{t}
w.r.t.
r

As the support of

5
PHS preserving coupling of subsystems

To discuss strategies for the coupling of subsystems, we begin with the identification of the ports of the generalized mass, spring and damper components which model the interacting particle system.

5.1
Identification of ports

The interaction dynamics can be interpreted as a generalized mass-spring-damper system. In order to identify this we decouple the system into its smallest parts. This allows us then to discuss PHS preserving coupling of different subsystems.

The
i
-th mass is described by its position
x_{i}
and its velocity
v_{i}
. Its evolution is driven by its kinetic energy
H^{m}(x_{i},v_{i})=v_{i}^{\top}v_{i}
and deviations from this velocity are due to external forces
f_{i}
which are called flows in the PHS framework. Together with the effort
e_{i}=\frac{\partial}{\partial v_{i}}H^{m}(x_{i},v_{i})=v_{i}
this is leading to the dynamics

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}x_{i}=v_{i},\quad\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}v_{i}=f_{i},\quad\Rightarrow\quad\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}z_{i}=J\nabla H^{m}(z_{i})+B_{i}u_{i},\quad y_{i}=B_{i}^{\top}\nabla H^{m}(z)=v_{i},

where
z_{i}=(x_{i},v_{i})
,
J=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&I\\
-I&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
,
B_{i}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0\\
I\end{smallmatrix}\right)
and
u_{i}=f_{i}
is the input and
y_{i}=v_{i}=e_{i}
the output.

The spring and damper connecting mass
i
and mass
j
are described by the relative position
q_{ij}:=x_{i}-x_{j}
and the relative velocity
v_{ij}:=v_{i}-v_{j}
. Note that the damper is a purely dissipative element, hence it admits no Hamiltonian but the force
f_{ij}^{\mathrm{damper}}=-\psi(|q_{ij}|)v_{ij}
. The Hamiltonian of the spring is given by
H^{sd}(q_{ij},v_{ij})=\mathcal{V}(q_{ij})
. Altogether this leads to the dynamics

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}q_{ij}=v_{ij},\qquad\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}v_{ij}=-\frac{\partial}{\partial q_{ij}}H^{sd}(q_{ij},v_{ij})-\psi(|q_{ij}|)v_{ij}

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}z_{ij}=(J-R(z_{ij}))\nabla H^{sd}(z_{ij})+B_{ij}u_{ij},

where
z_{ij}=(q_{ij},v_{ij})
,
J=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&I\\
-I&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
,
R(z_{ij})=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&0\\
0&\psi(|q_{ij}|)\end{smallmatrix}\right),B_{ij}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}I&-I\\
0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
and
u_{ij}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}v_{i}\\

y_{ij}=B_{ij}^{\top}\nabla H^{sd}(z_{ij})=\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\
-I&0\end{pmatrix}\nabla H^{sd}(z_{ij})=\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\
-I&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\nabla\mathcal{V}(q_{ij})\\
0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\;\;\nabla\mathcal{V}(q_{ij})\\
-\nabla\mathcal{V}(q_{ij})\end{pmatrix}.

The flow and effort variable of the spring-damper system connecting mass
i
and mass

f_{ij}=v_{i}-v_{j}\quad\textrm{ and }\quad e_{ij}=\frac{\partial}{\partial q_{ij}}H^{sd}(q_{ij},v_{ij})+\psi(|q_{ij}|)v_{ij}=\nabla\mathcal{V}(q_{ij})+\psi(|q_{ij}|)v_{ij}.

To couple the
i

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}x_{i}=v_{i},\quad\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}v_{i}=-\nabla\mathcal{V}(q_{ij})-\psi(|q_{ij}|)v_{ij}.

5.2
Coupling of identical subsystems

On the mean-field level we can easily couple two interacting particle system of same type by adding and rescaling their probability measures. Indeed, let
f^{1},f^{2}
be two interacting particle systems with identical interaction behaviour. Then

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f^{1}=-\nabla_{(x,v)}\cdot\Big(\big(f^{1}\ast(A\nabla_{f}\mathcal{H}(f^{1}))\big)f^{1}\Big),\qquad\partial_{t}f^{2}=-\nabla_{(x,v)}\cdot\Big(\big(f^{2}\ast(A\nabla_{f}\mathcal{H}(f^{2}))\big)f^{2}\Big)

Let us now define
f^{\mathrm{sum}}=(f^{1}+f^{2})/(\int df^{1}+\int df^{2})
, where we rescale to obtain a probability measure. Note that if
f^{1}
and
f^{2}
are probability measures, it holds

f^{\mathrm{sum}}=\frac{1}{2}(f^{1}+f^{2}).

The Hamiltonian of the coupled system is given by

H(f_{t}^{\mathrm{sum}})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\frac{1}{2}v^{T}v+\mathcal{V}(x-\bar{x})\right)\mathrm{d}f^{\mathrm{sum}}(t,\bar{x},\bar{v})\mathrm{d}f^{\mathrm{sum}}(t,x,v)

leading to the dynamics

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f^{\mathrm{sum}}=-\nabla_{(x,v)}\cdot\Big(\big(f^{\mathrm{sum}}\ast(A\nabla_{f}\mathcal{H}(f^{\mathrm{sum}}))\big)f^{\mathrm{sum}}\Big).

If we sample now
N
particles from
f^{1}(0,x,v)
and
N
particles from
f^{2}(0,x,v)
we obtain the systems

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}x_{i}^{1}

\displaystyle=v_{i}^{1},

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}v_{i}^{1}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\psi(|x_{i}^{1}-x_{j}^{1}|)(v_{i}^{1}-v_{j}^{1})-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\nabla\mathcal{V}(x_{i}^{1}-x_{j}^{1})

and

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}x_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle=v_{i}^{2},

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}v_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\psi(|x_{i}^{2}-x_{j}^{2}|)(v_{i}^{2}-v_{j}^{2})-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\nabla\mathcal{V}(x_{i}^{2}-x_{j}^{2}).

Sampling
2N

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}x_{i}

\displaystyle=v_{i},

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}v_{i}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{j=1}^{2N}\psi(|x_{i}-x_{j}|)(v_{i}-v_{j})-\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{j=1}^{2N}\nabla\mathcal{V}(x_{i}-x_{j})

Remark
.

We want to stress the fact that in general the value of the Hamiltonian of the coupled systems is greater than the value of the sum of the Hamiltonians of the subsystems. This is due to the fact that additional generalized springs are needed to to define the interaction behaviour of the individuals of the different swarms. However, in case of two identical swarms as above the rescaling of the Hamiltonian yields that the values of the Hamiltonian coincide. In the following we describe one approach that allows the coupling different subsystems in a Hamiltonian preserving way. In fact, the interacting across subsystems influences only the alignment.

6
Conclusion and outlook

We derived a minimal port-Hamiltonian formulation of interacting particle systems and showed that the port-Hamiltonian structure is preserved in the mean-field limit. The Hamiltonian is used as Lyapunov function to characterize the long-time behavior of the systems on the particle and the mean-field level. Hence the PHS formulation opens a new perspective on the well-studied particle and mean-field description of interacting particle system. Moreover, the identification of Casimir functions prepares the ground to define port-Hamiltonian preserving control strategies in future work. On the other hand, the LaSalle-type argument for the long-term behavior may open the door for convergence results for general consensus dynamics for optimization and sampling tasks in the spirit of
[

,
37
]
.
