Linear Bellman Completeness Suffices for Efficient Online Reinforcement Learning with Few Actions
Noah Golowich, Ankur Moitra

Abstract
One of the most natural approaches to reinforcement learning (RL) with function approximation is value iteration , which inductively generates approximations to the optimal value function by solving a sequence of regression problems. To ensure the success of value iteration, it is typically assumed that Bellman completeness holds, which ensures that these regression problems are well-specified. We study the problem of learning an optimal policy under Bellman completeness in the online model of RL with linear function approximation. In the linear setting, while statistically efficient algorithms are known under Bellman completeness (e.g., [ JKA+17 , ZLKB20a ] ), these algorithms all rely on the principle of global optimism which requires solving a nonconvex optimization problem. In particular, it has remained open as to whether computationally efficient algorithms exist. In this paper we give the first polynomial-time algorithm for RL under linear Bellman completeness when the number of actions is any constant.

Introduction

Reinforcement learning (RL)
describes the problem of solving sequential decision-making problems in a stochastically changing environment, and is typically studied using the formalism of
Markov Decision Processes (MDPs)
. In an MDP, a learning agent must choose a sequence of
actions
over some number of time steps, each of which affects the
state
of the environment and potentially yields some
reward
to the agent. The agent aims to find a
policy
, or a mapping that describes which action to take at each state, that maximizes its expected total reward. In order for RL to yield effective learning strategies in its various application domains, including robotics
[
GHLL17
]
, economics
[
ZTS+22
]
, and healthcare
[
YLNY21
]
, it is necessary to come up with efficient strategies for exploring complex state spaces, which may be infinite or exponentially large.

A general approach to RL, which dates back decades
[
BB96
,
MR07
,
SB18
]
and yet still forms the basis for many current empirical approaches
[
HGS16
,
SWD+17
]
, involves the use of value function approximation. Recall that the optimal
value function
maps a state-action pair to the agent’s expected reward under the optimal policy starting from that state-action pair. Then this approach posits that the optimal value function belongs to some known function class
\mathcal{F}
, such as a class of linear functions or neural networks. A key question is:
under what assumptions on the class
\mathcal{F}
can we efficiently learn a near-optimal policy, i.e., one with near-maximal expected reward?

Value iteration and Bellman completeness.

A popular and time-tested approach to finding a near-optimal policy with value function approximation is
value iteration
. To explain this procedure, we consider the
finite-horizon
setting, whereby interactions with the environment occur in
episodes
lasting
H
time steps. Letting
\mathcal{X}
denote the state space and
\mathcal{A}
denote the action space, value iteration computes mappings
\hat{Q}_{h}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
in a backwards-inductive manner, i.e., for
h=H,H-1,\ldots,1
. The values
\hat{Q}_{h}(x,a)
should be interpreted as estimates of the optimal value
1

1

I.e., the value of the optimal policy.
at step
h
given that the state-action pair taken at step
h
is
(x,a)
. Since the environment’s reward functions and transitions are unknown,
\hat{Q}_{h}
must be estimated empirically.
Given a dataset
\mathcal{D}
consisting of tuples
(x_{h},a_{h},r_{h},x_{h+1})
of states, actions, and rewards drawn from the environment at step

\hat{Q}_{h}

\displaystyle\hat{Q}_{h}:=\argmin_{Q_{h}\in\mathcal{F}}\sum_{(x_{h},a_{h},r_{h},x_{h+1})\in\mathcal{D}}\left(Q_{h}(x_{h},a_{h})-\left(r_{h}+\max_{a^{\prime}}\hat{Q}_{h+1}(x_{h+1},a^{\prime})\right)\right)^{2}.

(1)

A natural condition under which LSVI might yield good estimates of the optimal value function, and thereby a near-optimal policy, is that the least-squares problem

\displaystyle Q_{h}^{\prime}(x_{h},a_{h})=\mathbb{E}\left[r_{h}+\max_{a^{\prime}}\hat{Q}_{h+1}(x_{h+1},a^{\prime})\ \mid\ (x_{h},a_{h})\right].

(2)

]
. Moreover, classical results

MS08
]
showed that if the dataset

2

Eq.

2

\hat{Q}_{h+1}\in\mathcal{F}

\mathcal{F}
.

In this paper, our focus is on finding computationally efficient algorithms. Since regression problems such as
Eq.

1

[

,

1.1
Exploration and linear Bellman completeness

The results of

Is there an algorithm which learns an

-optimal policy in an unknown linear Bellman complete MDP using

A sizeable portion of the work on computationally efficient RL in the last several years has been focused on answering

. Here too there are computationally efficient algorithms, namely
LSVI-UCB

[
JYWJ20
]
, an optimistic version of LSVI, as well as more recent rate-optimal variants
[
AJZ22

HZZG23
]

In the setting of linear Bellman completeness, which is a strict generalization of linear MDPs
[
ZLKB20a
, Proposition 3]
, the algorithm

[

]
is computationally efficient and learns a near-optimal policy in the special case that the MDP is

can be reached under some policy. The setting of linear Bellman completeness has also been studied in the special case of

[

]
(Theorem 1) showed that there is a polynomial-time learning algorithm when the transitions and rewards are deterministic, and
[

]
established a polynomial-time algorithm for the setting of deterministic transitions, stochastic rewards, and positive suboptimality gap. Finally, in recent and concurrent work,
[
WSKS24
]
established a polynomial-time algorithm for the setting of deterministic transitions and stochastic rewards (without the assumption of positive suboptimality gap). The algorithm of
[

]
has the additional advantage of working under weaker norm assumptions (namely, the second item of
2.1
is not required). Finally, we mention that in the related

setting of reinforcement learning, the analogue of

was recently resolved in concurrent work
[
GM24
]
.

Despite the above line of work,

in its full generality has remained open. Part of the reason for this is that the two principal techniques to perform computationally efficient exploration in RL both break down in the general setting of linear Bellman completeness:

1.

The first technique is
local optimism
,
3

3

“Local” is used to distinguish this technique from

which adds exploration bonuses to the reward at each state which scale inversely with how often the state has been visited. It includes the

3

2.

The second technique is to construct a

, which is a small set of policies which, roughly speaking, covers all states to the maximum extent possible. This technique includes the

Thus, in addition to generalizing a long line of work on computationally efficient learning of MDPs,

1.1.1
Main result for the online setting

Our main result is a positive answer to
1.1
in the case that
|\mathcal{A}|
is any constant:

Theorem 1.1

(Informal version of
Theorem

5.10
)
.

Suppose the ground-truth MDP satisfies linear Bellman completeness. Then for any
\epsilon>0
, there is an algorithm (
PSDP-UCB
;
Algorithm

1
) which with high probability learns an
\epsilon
-optimal policy using
(Hd|\mathcal{A}|\epsilon^{-1})^{O(|\mathcal{A}|)}
samples and time.

We remark that the exponential dependence of the sample and computational complexities on
|\mathcal{A}|
is somewhat unusual in RL. We are not aware of any prior work on an RL problem for which
\poly(|\mathcal{A}|)
dependence is possible but for which a “natural” algorithm for even the
|\mathcal{A}|=2
case does not extend to the general case.

Theorem

1.1
thus presents an intriguing challenge: to either improve the guarantee to fully answer
1.1
in the affirmative, or to find a lower bound.

Proof idea of
Theorem

1.1
.

The proof of
Theorem

1.1
proceeds via the local optimism approach discussed in
Item

1
above. Our main insight is that it is possible to design exploration bonuses for which the class of linear value functions
is
complete with respect to them, i.e., whose Bellman-backup is linear (formally, we say that such exploration bonuses are
Bellman-linear
; see
Definition

3.1
). Doing so requires extensive work to “build up” to a function which satisfies several properties required of the exploration bonuses; see
Section

3
for further details. We remark that our algorithm

takes the structure of an optimistic variant of the classical

(Policy Search by Dynamic Programming) algorithm
[
BKSN03
]
, which has not previously appeared in the literature. This choice of algorithm structure plays more nicely with the exploration bonuses we introduce.

We find the fact that local optimism provides a solution to
1.1

|\mathcal{A}|=O(1)
, to be somewhat surprising. On the one hand, it indicates that Bellman completeness is a stronger property than one might initially anticipate. On the other hand, since Bellman-completeness is satisfied in other settings for which the only known computationally efficient algorithms are policy-cover based (
Item

2
above), our results perhaps provide some hope that local optimism can be made to work in such settings as well.

Preliminaries

A finite-horizon
Markov Decision Process (MDP)
is given by a tuple
M=(H,\mathcal{X},\mathcal{A},(P_{h}^{\scriptscriptstyle{M}})_{h=1}^{H},(r_{h}^{\scriptscriptstyle{M}})_{h=1}^{H},d_{1}^{\scriptscriptstyle{M}})
, where
H\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathcal{X}
is a measure space denoting the state set,
\mathcal{A}
denotes the action set,
P_{h}^{\scriptscriptstyle{M}}(\cdot|x,a)\in\Delta(\mathcal{X})
(for
h\in[H]
) denotes the probability transition kernels,
r_{h}^{\scriptscriptstyle{M}}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow[0,1]
(for
h\in[H]
) denotes the reward functions, and
d_{1}^{\scriptscriptstyle{M}}\in\Delta(\mathcal{X})
denotes the initial state distribution. When the MDP
M
is clear from context, we will drop the superscript
M
in these notations. We let
A:=|\mathcal{A}|
denote the number of actions in the MDP.

A
policy

\pi
consists of a tuple
\pi=(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{H})
, where each
\pi_{h}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\Delta(\mathcal{A})
is a mapping from states to distributions over actions. Note that we allow policies to be nonstationary and randomized;
\Pi
denotes the set of all such policies. A policy
\pi\in\Pi
defines a distribution over
trajectories

(x_{1},a_{1},r_{1},\ldots,x_{H},a_{H},r_{H})\in(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\times[0,1]^{H}
, as follows: first,
x_{1}\sim d_{1}
, and then for each
h\in[H]
, we draw
a_{h}\sim\pi_{h}(x_{h})
, observe
r_{h}(x_{h},a_{h})
, and transition to
x_{h+1}\sim P_{h}(\cdot|x_{h},a_{h})
. Let
\mathscr{H}_{H}
denote the set of trajectories. For a function
f:\mathscr{H}_{H}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{k}
, we often write
\mathbb{E}^{\scriptscriptstyle{M,\pi}}[f(x_{1},a_{1},r_{1},\ldots,x_{H},a_{H},r_{H})]
to denote the expectation of
f
over trajectories drawn from
M
under policy
\pi
. If
M
is clear from context, we will simply write
\mathbb{E}^{\pi}[f(x_{1},a_{1},r_{1},\ldots,x_{H},a_{H},r_{H})]
. Given policies
\pi=(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{H}),\pi^{\prime}=(\pi_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,\pi_{H}^{\prime})\in\Pi
and a step
h\in[H]
, we let
\pi\circ_{h}\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi
denote the policy
(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{h-1},\pi_{h}^{\prime},\ldots,\pi_{H}^{\prime})
, i.e., which acts according to
\pi
during the first
h-1
steps and thereafter acts according to
\pi^{\prime}
.

For a function
f:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, a (randomized) policy
\pi
and
h\in[H]
, we write
f(x,\pi_{h}(x)):=\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_{h}(x)}[f(x,a)]
. The state-action value function (or
Q
-function
) and state-value function (or
V
-function
) of a policy
\pi\in\Pi
are then defined as follows: for
h\in[H],x\in\mathcal{X},a\in\mathcal{A}
,

\displaystyle Q_{h}^{\pi}(x,a):=r_{h}(x,a)+\mathbb{E}^{\pi}\left[\sum_{g=h+1}^{H}r_{g}(x_{g},a_{g})\ \mid\ (x_{h},a_{h})=(x,a)\right],\qquad V_{h}^{\pi}(x):=Q_{h}^{\pi}(x,\pi_{h}(x)).

The
optimal policy
is defined as
\pi^{\star}:=\argmax_{\pi\in\Pi}\mathbb{E}[V_{1}^{\pi}(x_{1})]
(where expectation is over
x_{1}\sim d_{1}
). It is known that there is always a deterministic optimal policy
\pi^{\star}
(i.e., so that
\pi_{h}^{\star}(x)
is a singleton for all
x,h
). We often abbreviate
Q_{h}^{\star}(x,a):=Q_{h}^{\pi^{\star}}(x,a)
and
V_{h}^{\star}(x):=V_{h}^{\pi^{\star}}(x)
.

2.1
The online learning problem

We assume that the transitions, rewards, and initial state distribution of the ground-truth MDP
M
are
unknown
to the algorithm.
To learn information about the MDP, the algorithm interacts with
M
via the
episodic online learning model
, as follows. The interaction proceeds over a series of
T
episodes. In each step
h\in[H]
of each episode, the algorithm observes the current state
x_{h}
, specifies an action
a_{h}\in\mathcal{A}
to take, and observes a reward of
r_{h}=r_{h}(x_{h},a_{h})
. Then the environment transitions to a new state
x_{h+1}\sim P_{h}(\cdot|x_{h},a_{h})
. We assume that the algorithm can query
\phi_{h}(x,a)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
for any
x,a,h
. We remark that our algorithm only needs to query
\phi_{h}(x,a)
for states
x
which are visited at some point in some episode.

The goal is as follows: for
\epsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
, to give an algorithm which interacts with the environment for
T=T(\epsilon,\delta)
episodes in the manner described above, and then to output a policy
\hat{\pi}
so that, with probability
1-\delta
,
\mathbb{E}[V_{1}^{\star}(x_{1})-V_{1}^{\hat{\pi}}(x_{1})]\leq\epsilon
. Moreover, we wish the algorithm to be computationally efficient (both in terms of the computation required when interacting with the environment over the course of
T
episodes and when outputting
\hat{\pi}
at termination).

2.2
Function approximation

In order for the above learning problem to be tractable, it is necessary to make some assumptions on the ground-truth MDP
M
being learned. While it is well-known that boundedness of
|\mathcal{X}|,|\mathcal{A}|
implies efficient learning algorithms whose computational and statistical costs scale with
\poly(|\mathcal{X}|,|\mathcal{A}|)

[
AOM17
,
JAZBJ18
]
, realistic RL environments typically have enormous state spaces. Thus we aim for weaker function approximation assumptions: a common and longstanding such assumption
[
BB96
,
MR07
,
SB18
,
YW20
,
JYWJ20
]
is
linearity
of the value functions, with respect to some known features. In particular, given
d\in\mathbb{N}
, we assume that functions
\phi_{h}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
are given for all
h\in[H]
, mapping each state-action pair
(x,a)
to a collection of
d

features
which should be interpreted as capturing all relevant information about
(x,a)
.

The weakest assumption on value function linearity is simply that
Q_{h}^{\star}
is linear, i.e., for some
w_{h}^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have
Q_{h}^{\star}(x,a)=\langle w_{h}^{\star},\phi_{h}(x,a)\rangle
for all
(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}
. Unfortunately, unless
\mathsf{NP}=\mathsf{RP}
, it is not possible to computationally efficiently learn a near-optimal policy under this assumption
[
KLLM22
,
LMK+23
]
. Accordingly, we make a stronger assumption known as
linear Bellman completeness
, which states, roughly speaking, that the Bellman backup of all linear functions is linear. To formally state this assumption (in
Definition

2.1
below), we need to introduce the following notation: for
h\in[H]
, define

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{h}:=\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\ :\ |\langle\phi_{h}(x,a),\theta\rangle|\leq 1\ \ \forall(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\}.

In words,
\mathcal{B}_{h}
denotes the set of coefficient vectors inducing bounded (linear) functions on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}
.

Definition 2.1

(Linear Bellman Completeness)
.

The MDP
M
is defined to be
linear Bellman complete
with respect to the feature mappings
(\phi_{h})_{h\in[H]}
if, for each
h\in[H]
, there is a mapping
\mathcal{T}_{h}:\mathcal{B}_{h+1}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}_{h}
so that, for all
\theta\in\mathcal{B}_{h+1}
and all
(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}
,

\displaystyle\langle\phi_{h}(x,a),\mathcal{T}_{h}\theta\rangle=\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim P_{h}(x,a)}\left[\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\langle\phi_{h+1}(x^{\prime},a^{\prime}),\theta\rangle\right].

Moreover, we require that for all
h\in[H]
,
(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}
, the reward at
(x,a)
at step
h
is given by:

\displaystyle r_{h}(x,a):=\langle\phi_{h}(x,a),\theta_{h}^{\mathsf{r}}\rangle,

for some vectors
\theta_{h}^{\mathsf{r}}\in\mathcal{B}_{h}
.

It is immediate from
Definition

2.1
that if
M
is linear Bellman complete, then there are vectors
w_{h}^{\star}\in H\cdot\mathcal{B}_{h}
so that
Q_{h}^{\star}(x,a)=\langle w_{h}^{\star},\phi_{h}(x,a)\rangle
for all
x,a
.
In addition to linear Bellman completeness, we make the following standard boundedness assumptions on the coefficient and feature vectors:

Assumption 2.1

(Boundedness)
.

We assume the following:

1.

For all
h\in[H],x\in\mathcal{X},a\in\mathcal{A}
, we have
\|\phi_{h}(x,a)\|_{2}\leq 1
.

2.

For some parameter
B\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
: for all
w_{h}\in\mathcal{B}_{h}
, it holds that
\|w_{h}\|_{2}\leq B
.

3.

For all
h\in[H]
,
\|\theta_{h}^{\mathsf{r}}\|_{2}\leq 1
(and hence
\sup_{x,a,h}|r_{h}(x,a)|\leq 1
).

Polytope of actions.

We assume that
\mathcal{A}
is finite and write
A:=|\mathcal{A}|
. Let
\mathscr{P}^{d}
denote the space of
d
-dimensional (bounded) polyhedra. For
A\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mathscr{P}_{A}^{d}\subset\mathscr{P}^{d}
denote the space of
d
-dimensional polyhedra

so that
\dim({\rm Span}\Phi)\leq A
. For each
x\in\mathcal{X}
, we define
\Phi_{h}(x):=\{\phi_{h}(x,a):\ a\in\mathcal{A}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\bar{\Phi}_{h}(x):=\mathrm{co}(\Phi_{h}(x))\in\mathscr{P}_{A}^{d}\subset\mathscr{P}^{d}
.

Gaussian smoothing.

For
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\sigma>0
, we write

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\sigma}(\theta):=\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}\cdot I_{d})(\theta)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}\sigma^{d}}\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|\theta\|_{2}^{2}\right)

to denote the probability density function of the standard normal distribution with covariance

.
Furthermore, for
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, we write
\mathsf{S}_{\sigma}f(\theta)
to denote the convolution of

\mathcal{N}_{\sigma}
, namely

\displaystyle\mathsf{S}_{\sigma}f(\theta):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(z)\mathcal{N}_{\sigma}(\theta-z)dz=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(\theta-z)\mathcal{N}_{\sigma}(z)dz=\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}\cdot I_{d})}[f(\theta-z)].

Miscellaneous notation.

Given
d\in\mathbb{N}
, we let
\mathbb{S}^{d}
denote the space of symmetric

\mathbb{S}_{+}^{d}\subset\mathbb{S}^{d}

and a matrix
T\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, we let
T\cdot\mathcal{S}:=\{Tv:\ v\in\mathcal{S}\}
. For vectors
v,v^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we let
[v,v^{\prime}]
denote the segment
[v,v^{\prime}]:=\{\lambda v+(1-\lambda)v^{\prime}:\ \lambda\in[0,1]\}
. Let
S^{d-1}=\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\ \|u\|_{2}=1\}
denote the
d
-dimensional unit sphere. For a square matrix
T
, we let
\|T\|
denote its spectral norm.

2.3
Linear policies

Under the assumption of linear Bellman completeness, there is always an optimal policy with the additional structure of being a
linear policy
. Roughly speaking, linear policies take an action defined by the argmax with respect to some fixed coefficient vector: in particular, given
w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
h\in[H]
, the associated linear policy at step
h
is
\pi_{h,w}(x):=\argmax_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\langle w,\phi_{h}(x,a)\rangle

if the argmax is unique
. It requires some care to appropriately break ties for states
x
at which the
\argmax
is not unique.
4
4

4

In particular, an appropriate tie-breaking procedure is necessary for
Lemma

4.3
to hold.
To do so, given
w\in\mathbb{R}^{d},h\in[H],x\in\mathcal{X}
, define
\mathcal{A}_{h,w}(x):=\argmax_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\langle w,\phi_{h}(x,a)\rangle\subset\mathcal{A}
, where
\argmax
is interpreted as the set of all actions maximizing
\langle w,\phi_{h}(x,a)\rangle
. Then we set, for each
a\in\mathcal{A}_{h,w}(x)
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{h,w}(x,a):=\left\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\ \|\theta\|_{2}=1,\ \langle\theta,\phi_{h}(x,a)>\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}_{h,w}(x)\backslash\{a\}}\langle\theta,\phi_{h}(x,a^{\prime})\right\}.

Let
\nu_{d}
denote the spherical measure on
S^{d-1}
.
It is straightforward to see that, for all
w
and
x
,
S^{d-1}\backslash\bigcup_{a\in\mathcal{A}_{h,w}(x)}\mathcal{G}_{h,w}(x,a)
has measure 0 with respect to
\nu_{d}
. We now define,
\pi_{h,w}:x\rightarrow\Delta(\mathcal{A})
to be the following randomized policy: for all
w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
h\in[H]
,
x\in\mathcal{X}
,
a\in\mathcal{A}
,

\displaystyle\pi_{h,w}(a|x):=\mathbbm{1}\{{a\in\mathcal{A}_{h,w}(x)}\}\cdot\nu_{d}(\mathcal{G}_{h,w}(x,a)).

(3)

It is straightfroward to see that
\pi_{h,w}(\cdot|x)\in\Delta(\mathcal{A})
. We say that a policy
\pi
is a
linear policy
if it is of the form
\pi=(\pi_{1,w_{1}},\ldots,\pi_{H,w_{H}})
, for
w_{1},\ldots,w_{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We let
\Pi^{\mathsf{lin}}_{h}:=\{\pi_{h,w}:\ w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
denote the space of linear policies at step
h
, and
\Pi^{\mathsf{lin}}:=\{(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{H}):\ \pi_{h}\in\Pi^{\mathsf{lin}}_{h}\}
denote the space of linear policies.

3
Technical overview

In this section, we overview the proof of
Theorem

1.1
(stated formally in
Theorem

5.10
), which shows how, in the online setting (
Section

2.1
),
Algorithm

1
can efficiently learn a near-optimal policy for an unknown MDP which is linear Bellman complete. To simplify our notation, we assume in this section that the parameter
B
in
2.1
is bounded by
B\leq O(1)
. The bulk of the challenge is to perform the task of
exploration
: how can we interact with the environment so as to reach state-action pairs
(x,a)
for which
\phi_{h}(x,a)
points in a new direction?

3.1
Prior work: exploration via optimism

A popular approach to exploration in RL involves the use of
optimism
, which describes, loosely speaking, the technique of perturbing the algorithm’s estimates of the MDP’s value function, typically to
increase
the estimated values, so as to induce the algorithm to visit new directions in feature space. Two distinct flavors of optimism have emerged in the literature: the first,
global optimism
,
5
5

5

See, e.g.,
[
ZLKB20a
]
, which used global optimism to computationally inefficiently learn a near-optimal policy under linear Bellman completeness; many other papers, including
[
JLM21
,
JKA+17
,
DKL+21
]
, use global optimism in settings with more general nonlinear function approximation.
constructs a confidence set consisting of all possible vectors
w=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{H})
which could be consistent with the optimal value function given the data observed so far. The global optimism procedure then chooses some
\bar{w}
in this confidence set which maximizes
\mathbb{E}[\max_{a}\langle\bar{w}_{1},\phi_{1}(x_{1},a)\rangle]
. It then executes the linear policy defined by
\bar{w}
, namely the policy
(\pi_{1,\bar{w}_{1}},\ldots,\pi_{H,\bar{w}_{H}})
, uses the resulting data to update the confidence sets, and repeats. Unfortunately, the optimization problem of finding a maximizing
\bar{w}
is nonconvex, and seems unlikely to have an efficient algorithm.

The second type of optimism-based exploration technique in RL is a more
local
approach: at each episode
t
, for some function
B_{h}^{(t)}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, an
exploration bonus
of
B_{h}^{(t)}(x,a)
is added to the reward
r_{h}(x,a)
for the pair
(x,a)
at step
h
. Then, the algorithm uses the data gathered prior to episode
t
to estimate a policy
\pi^{(t)}
which, roughly speaking, maximizes the expected sum of rewards
r_{h}(x_{h},a_{h})
and bonuses
B_{h}^{(t)}(x_{h},a_{h})
over a trajectory. This technique has proved successful for computationally efficient learning of tabular MDPs (i.e., the setting where
|\mathcal{X}|,|\mathcal{A}|
are finite)
[
AOM17
,
JAZBJ18
]
, as well as
linear MDPs

[
JYWJ20
]
, which constitute a generalization of tabular MDPs and a strict subclass of linear Bellman complete MDPs.

Unfortunately, the local optimism approach fails in the more general setting of linear Bellman completeness. At a high level, this failure of local optimism results from the fact that the exploration bonuses
B_{h}^{(t)}(x,a)
may not be linear. To illustrate, we consider the
LSVI-UCB
approach of
[
JYWJ20
]
. This approach computes optimistic
Q
-function estimates,
\hat{Q}_{h}^{(t)}
, in a backwards-inductive manner. In particular, it defines:

\displaystyle\hat{Q}_{h}^{(t)}(x,a):=\min\{\langle\hat{w}_{h}^{(t)},\phi_{h}(x,a)\rangle+B_{h}^{(t)}(x,a),H\},

(4)

where
\hat{w}_{h}^{(t)}
is the solution to a least-squares objective function whose labels are given by
\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\hat{Q}_{h+1}^{(t)}(x^{i},a)
, for various states
x^{i}
.
6
6

6

The choice of the scalar
H
in the minimum in
Eq.

4
results from the fact that the
Q
-function of any policy is bounded above in absolute value by
H
, as the reward at each step has absolute value at most 1.
The bonus
B_{h}^{(t)}(x,a)
is defined as follows: let
\Sigma_{h}^{(t)}
be the covariance matrix of features at step
h
observed prior to episode
t
. Then
B_{h}^{(t)}
is given by a scaling of the
quadratic bonus
, i.e., for some scalar
\beta_{h}
,
B_{h}^{(t)}(x,a):=\beta_{h}\cdot B^{\mathsf{quad}}_{h}(x,a;(\Sigma_{h}^{(t)})^{-1})
, where the quadratic bonus,
B^{\mathsf{quad}}_{h}(x,a;\Sigma)
, is defined for a general PSD matrix
\Sigma
, by

\displaystyle B^{\mathsf{quad}}_{h}(x,a;\Sigma):=(\phi_{h}(x,a)^{\top}\cdot\Sigma\cdot\phi_{h}(x,a))^{1/2}.

(5)

The intuition behind the quadratic bonus is as follows:
B^{\mathsf{quad}}_{h}(x,a;(\Sigma_{h}^{(t)})^{-1})
will be particularly large if
\phi_{h}(x,a)
points in the direction of eigenvectors of
\Sigma_{h}^{(t)}
with small eigenvalues, i.e., directions which have not been explored in prior episodes. Thus such
unexplored
states receive larger bonuses, and should be explored more during later episodes. More formally,
B^{\mathsf{quad}}_{h}(\cdot)
takes the same form as the standard error bound from least-squares regression (see
Lemma

5.7
), meaning that by adding it to the reward function, one can “cancel out” regression errors and thus show that
\hat{Q}_{h}^{(t)}
in
Eq.

4
is optimistic (see
[
JYWJ20
,
AJZ22
,
ZSU+22
,
HZZG23
]
).

To ensure that
\hat{w}_{h}^{(t)}
, used in the definition of
\hat{Q}_{h}^{(t)}
, is a good estimator of the future rewards and bonuses, we certainly need the regression problem to be
well-specified
, i.e., the expectation of the regression labels is a linear function in the features
\phi_{h}(x,a)
. In particular, this approach crucially relies on the fact that, in a linear MDP,
\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim P_{h}(x,a)}[\max_{a^{\prime}}\hat{Q}_{h+1}^{(t)}(x^{\prime},a^{\prime})]
, is a linear function of
\phi_{h}(x,a)
. In fact, an even stronger statement holds for linear MDPs: for
any
function
F:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, its
Bellman backup
, namely
\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim P_{h}(x,a)}[F(x^{\prime})]
, is linear in
\phi_{h}(x,a)
. Unfortunately, this fact fails to hold under the weaker assumption of linear Bellman completeness. We provide an example in
Proposition

A.1
for which
\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim P_{h}(x,a)}[\max_{a^{\prime}}\hat{Q}_{h+1}^{(t)}(x^{\prime},a^{\prime})]
is
not linear
, for some
\hat{Q}_{h+1}^{(t)}
as in
Eq.

4
.

Our main innovation is to show that if the bonus
B_{h}^{(t)}(x,a)
is carefully defined to be somewhat different from a quadratic bonus, then a variant of
Eq.

4

does
have a linear Bellman backup. In the remainder of this section, we discuss in detail how to execute this strategy: in
Section

3.2
, we first discuss the overall structure of our algorithm,
PSDP-UCB
, which is a variant of the
LSVI-UCB
algorithm discussed above but which lends itself to a simpler analysis for the setting of linear Bellman completeness. Then, in
Sections

3.3
and
3.4
, we discuss how to construct exploration bonuses for use in
PSDP-UCB
which do have a linear Bellman backup.

3.3
Implementing local optimism via Bellman-linearity

Essentially any approach to proving
Lemma

3.1
requires that
Q_{h}^{(t)}(x,a)
be a linear function of
\phi_{h}(x,a)
.
8
8

8

In particular, linearity of
Q_{h}^{(t)}
establishes that the regression problem solved in
7
of
Algorithm

1
is well-specified, which allows us to show upper bounds on the prediction error
\left|\langle\hat{w}_{h}^{(t)},\phi_{h}(x,a)\rangle-Q_{h}^{(t)}(x,a)\right|
and is in turn used to establish optimism.
In turn, as discussed in
Section

3.1
, this requires that the bonuses
F_{h+1}^{(t)}(\cdot)
have a Bellman backup which is linear in
\phi_{h}(x,a)
.
It will be useful to formalize the following notion of
Bellman-linearity
, which describes those functions whose Bellman backup is linear in the features:

Definition 3.1
.

Fix
h\in[H]
with
h>1
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
. We say that a function
G:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{k}
is
Bellman-linear at step
h
if there exists
W_{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
so that, for all
(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}
, it holds that
W_{G}\cdot\phi_{h-1}(x,a)=\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim P_{h-1}(x,a)}[G(x^{\prime})]
. In particular, in the case that
k=1
,
G:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is Bellman-linear at step
h
if there is
w_{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
so that
\langle w_{G},\phi_{h-1}(x,a)\rangle=\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim P_{h-1}(x,a)}[G(x^{\prime})]
.

If the value of
h
is clear, we will simply say the function
G
is Bellman-linear. The canonical example of a Bellman-linear function is the mapping
x\mapsto\max_{a}\langle w,\phi_{h}(x,a)\rangle
, for any
w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Given that such functions are Bellman-linear, we can construct additional Bellman-linear functions by averaging, taking limits (
Lemma

4.1
), and differentiating (
Lemma

4.2
). The following lemma shows that Bellman-linearity of the bonuses
F_{h}^{(t)}
is indeed a sufficient condition for linearity of
Q_{h}^{(t)}
:

Lemma 3.3

(Variant of
Lemma

5.1
)
.

Suppose that, in
PSDP-UCB
, for each
h\in[H],t\in[T]
,
F_{h}^{(t)}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is Bellman-linear at step
h
. Then for all
h,t
, there is some
w_{h}^{(t)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
so that
Q_{h}^{(t)}(x,a)=\langle\phi_{h}(x,a),w_{h}^{(t)}\rangle
for all
(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}
.

The proof of
Lemma

3.3
requires some work to show that the expectation of the bonuses
F_{g}^{(t)}
for

is linear at step

: this fact crucially relies on the fact that the policies
\hat{\pi}^{(t)}
are linear policies (see
Lemma

4.3
).

A Bellman-linear approximation of
B^{\mathsf{quad}}_{h}
.

In light of our discussion pertaining to the quadratic bonus

around

5

is Bellman-linear at step

Though this is not the case (

can be

Lemma 3.4

(Simplified version of

)
.

Fix
h\in[H]
and a PSD matrix

, and define

\displaystyle F^{\mathsf{normal}}_{h}(x;\Sigma):=\mathbb{E}_{w\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)}\left[\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\langle w,\phi_{h}(x,a)\rangle\right].

(9)

Then

is Bellman-linear at step

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\max_{a,a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\left((\phi_{h}(x,a)-\phi_{h}(x,a^{\prime}))^{\top}\Sigma(\phi_{h}(x,a)-\phi_{h}(x,a^{\prime}))\right)^{1/2}\leq F^{\mathsf{normal}}_{h}(x;\Sigma)\leq\sqrt{d}\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\left(\phi_{h}(x,a)^{\top}\Sigma\phi_{h}(x,a)\right)^{1/2}.

The proof of

is Bellman-linear at step

follows from the fact that
F^{\mathsf{normal}}_{h}(x;\Sigma)
is defined as an average of Bellman-linear functions. The upper and lower bounds on
F^{\mathsf{normal}}_{h}(x;\Sigma)
follow from a direct computation.

Lemma

3.4
suggests the following approach:
for some scalar
\beta_{h}
, suppose we define

\displaystyle F_{h}^{(t)}(x):=\beta_{h}\cdot F^{\mathsf{normal}}_{h}(x;(\Sigma_{h}^{(t)})^{-1}).

(10)

Since
F^{\mathsf{normal}}_{h}(\cdot;\Sigma)
is Bellman-linear and approximates the quadratic bonus (
Lemma

3.4
), we might hope that it acts similarly to the quadratic bonus in
LSVI-UCB
to induce an optimistic property of the value functions
Q_{h}^{(t)}(x,a)
in
PSDP-UCB
, thereby allowing us to establish
Lemma

3.1
.
Unfortunately, this argument encounters a few snags:

1.

The lower bound on
F^{\mathsf{normal}}_{h}(x;\Sigma)
in
Lemma

3.4
is not exactly a constant times
B^{\mathsf{quad}}_{h}(x;\Sigma)
, but instead involves a
difference
between feature vectors at the state
x
. Thus, strictly speaking,
F^{\mathsf{normal}}_{h}(x;\Sigma)
does not give a multiplicative approximation of the quadratic bonus
B^{\mathsf{quad}}_{h}(x;\Sigma)
.

2.

Even if
F^{\mathsf{normal}}_{h}(x;\Sigma)
did give a multiplicative approximation of
B^{\mathsf{quad}}_{h}(x;\Sigma)
, we would need to ensure that this multiplicative approximation is good enough for all arguments to go through.

3.

Ignoring the previous two items, we still suffer from the following exponential blowup. Roughly speaking, the proof of
Lemma

3.1
proceeds by showing that the loss in value from any sub-optimal action taken by
\hat{\pi}^{(t)}
at step
h
is canceled out by the bonus
F_{h}^{(t)}(x_{h})
. To show this, we need to show that the exploration bonus
\beta_{h}\cdot F^{\mathsf{normal}}_{h}(x_{h};(\Sigma_{h}^{(t)})^{-1})
is at least as large as the regression error in our estimate of
\hat{w}_{h}^{(t)}
from
7
. But the labels in this linear regression involve the sum of bonuses
F_{g}^{(t)}(\cdot)
for
h+1\leq g\leq H
, and thus scale with
\beta_{h+1}+\cdots+\beta_{H}
. Working through standard high-confidence bounds for linear regression (see
Lemma

5.7
for details) establishes that we would need
\beta_{h}\geq\poly(d)\cdot(\beta_{h+1}+\cdots+\beta_{H})
.
This recursion leads to
\beta_{1}\geq d^{O(H)}
, which would require exponentially many samples to cancel out exponentially large error terms.

Items

1
and
2
above turn out not to be major issues. In the case of
Item

1
, a lower bound on
F_{h}(x;\Sigma)
growing only with the difference between feature vectors still induces a sufficient exploration property on the induced value functions: see the proof of
Lemma

5.9
.
Item

2
may be handled by increasing the scaling factors
\beta_{h}
by a factor proportional to the gap between the upper and lower bounds of
Lemma

3.4
.

The main technical challenge is dealing with
Item

3
above, namely the exponential blowup of
\beta_{h}
. Typically, the solution to the above dilemma is to
truncate
the bonus, i.e., define
F_{h}^{(t)}(x):=\min\{\beta\cdot F^{\mathsf{normal}}_{h}(x;(\Sigma_{h}^{(t)})^{-1}),\bar{F}_{h}(x)\}
, for some
\bar{F}_{h}(x)\ll\beta
which is a uniform upper bound on
V_{h}^{\star}(x)
. (Note that, when using such an approach, the parameter
\beta
does not need any dependence on
h
.) Indeed, this truncation is used in
LSVI-UCB
, where the
\hat{Q}_{h}^{(t)}
function in
Eq.

4
is truncated by the constant
\bar{F}_{h}(x)\equiv H
. Unfortunately, in the setting of linear Bellman completeness, truncation, in general, breaks Bellman-linearity of
F_{h}^{(t)}(x)
.
In the following section, we discuss how to implement this truncation in a way that preserves Bellman-linearity.

3.4
Constructing truncated bonuses

Approximating
(\Sigma_{h}^{(t)})^{-1}
.

For each
h\in[H]
and
t\in[T]
, the inverse covariance matrix
(\Sigma_{h}^{(t)})^{-1}
captures which directions of feature space are explored by policies
\hat{\pi}^{(s)}\circ_{h}\tilde{\pi}^{(s)}
, for
s\leq t-1
. We split
(\Sigma_{h}^{(t)})^{-1}
into matrices representing “well-explored directions” and “unexplored directions”, as follows: for an appropriate threshold value
\sigma
, we let
\Sigma^{\prime}
denote the projection matrix onto the subspace spanned by eigenvectors of
(\Sigma_{h}^{(t)})^{-1/2}
with eigenvalues greater than
\sigma
, and
\Lambda^{\prime}
denote the projection matrix onto the subspace spanned by eigenvectors of
(\Sigma_{h}^{(t)})^{-1/2}
with eigenvalues less than
\sigma
. Formally, as defined in
Definition

5.2
, we write
(\Sigma^{\prime},\Lambda^{\prime})=\mathsf{trunc}_{\sigma}({(\Sigma_{h}^{(t)})^{-1/2}})
. We denote subspaces onto which
\Sigma^{\prime},\Lambda^{\prime}
project by
\mathcal{S}_{\Sigma^{\prime}},\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda^{\prime}}
, respectively. Note that
\Sigma^{\prime}\Lambda^{\prime}=\Lambda^{\prime}\Sigma^{\prime}=0
and
\Sigma^{\prime}+\Lambda^{\prime}=I_{d}
; we call the pair
(\Sigma^{\prime},\Lambda^{\prime})
an
orthogonal pair
(
Definition

4.1
).

If
\sigma
is chosen sufficiently small to be less than our desired error threshold, then the estimations
\hat{w}_{h}^{(t)}
produced in
7
of
Algorithm

1
are sufficiently accurate in directions spanned by
\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda^{\prime}}
. Thus, we only have to add an exploration bonus at a state
x
which scales in proportion to the width of the features
\phi_{h}(x,a),\ a\in\mathcal{A}
in directions spanned by the orthogonal complement of
\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda^{\prime}}
, namely
\mathcal{S}_{\Sigma^{\prime}}
. This leads us to aim to construct an exploration bonus which scales roughly as
x\mapsto\min\{\beta\cdot F^{\mathsf{normal}}_{h}(x;\Sigma^{\prime}),\bar{F}_{h}(x)\}
, for some parameter
\beta>1
and an appropriate choice of
\bar{F}_{h}
which should (a) be uniformly upper bounded by a parameter which does not scale with
\beta
, and (b) be an upper bound on
V_{h}^{\star}
. It turns out that condition (b) can be relaxed to require only that
\bar{F}_{h}(x)
be an upper bound on

\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}Q_{h}^{\star}(x,a)-\min_{a\in\mathcal{A}}Q_{h}^{\star}(x,a)=\max_{a,a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\langle w_{h}^{\star},\phi_{h}(x,a)-\phi_{h}(x,a^{\prime})\rangle.

Thus, to summarize, we aim to find a bonus function which satisfies the following properties:

Problem 3.2

(Bonus function)
.

Fix
h,t
(which determine
(\Sigma^{\prime},\Lambda^{\prime})=\mathsf{trunc}_{\sigma}({(\Sigma_{h}^{(t)})^{-1/2}})
as above), and some
\beta>1
. Can we find a Bellman-linear function
F_{h}^{(t)}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
which satisfies:

1.

For all
x\in\mathcal{X}
,
F_{h}^{(t)}(x)
is lower bounded as

\displaystyle F_{h}^{(t)}(x)\geq

\displaystyle BH\cdot\max_{a,a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\min\left\{\beta\cdot\|\Sigma^{\prime}\cdot(\phi_{h}(x,a)-\phi_{h}(x,a^{\prime}))\|_{2},\|\Lambda^{\prime}\cdot(\phi_{h}(x,a)-\phi_{h}(x,a^{\prime}))\|_{2}\right\}.

(11)

2.

For all
x\in\mathcal{X}
,
F_{h}^{(t)}(x)
is upper bounded as

\displaystyle F_{h}^{(t)}(x)\leq

\displaystyle\poly(d,H,B)\cdot\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\min\left\{\beta\|\Sigma^{\prime}\cdot\phi_{h}(x,a)\|_{2},\|\phi_{h}(x,a)\|_{2}\right\}.

The case
d=A=2
.

To proceed, we consider the special case in which
d=2
. Let us suppose that
\mathcal{S}_{\Sigma^{\prime}},\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda^{\prime}}
are each one dimensional, and are spanned by unit vectors
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, respectively, which are orthogonal. Let us consider the function

\displaystyle F^{\mathsf{tl}}_{h}(x;u,v):=\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\langle u,\phi_{h}(x,a)\rangle+\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\langle v,\phi_{h}(x,a)\rangle-\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\langle u+v,\phi_{h}(x,a)\rangle,

for some parameter
\beta>1
.
If we suppose that there are only 2 actions (i.e.,
|\mathcal{A}|=2
), then
x\mapsto\sum_{\epsilon_{u},\epsilon_{v}\in\{\pm 1\}}F^{\mathsf{tl}}_{h}(x;\beta\epsilon_{u}\cdot u,\epsilon_{v}\cdot v)
(scaled by a
\poly(B,H)
factor) satisfies the conditions of
3.2
. To see this, let us write
\mathcal{A}=\{0,1\}
, and set
\phi_{h}^{\Delta}(x):=\phi_{h}(x,1)-\phi_{h}(x,0)
. Fix
x\in\mathcal{X}
, and choose
\epsilon_{u},\epsilon_{v}\in\{\pm 1\}
so that
\langle\epsilon_{u}\cdot u,\phi_{h}^{\Delta}(x)\rangle
and
\langle\epsilon_{v}\cdot v,\phi_{h}^{\Delta}(x)\rangle
have opposite signs. Then

\displaystyle F^{\mathsf{tl}}_{h}(x;\beta\epsilon_{u}u,\epsilon_{v}v)=

\displaystyle|\langle\beta\epsilon_{u}u,\phi_{h}^{\Delta}(x)\rangle|+|\langle\epsilon_{v}v,\phi_{h}^{\Delta}(x)\rangle|-|\langle\beta\epsilon_{u}u+\epsilon_{v}v,\phi_{h}^{\Delta}(x)\rangle|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\min\{|\langle\beta u,\phi_{h}^{\Delta}(x)\rangle|,|\langle v,\phi_{h}^{\Delta}(x)\rangle|\},

(12)

where the inequality uses the property that for real numbers
a,b
with opposite signs, we have
|a|+|b|-|a+b|\geq\min\{|a|,|b|\}
.
By combining
Eq.

12
with non-negativity of
F^{\mathsf{tl}}_{h}(\cdot)
, we obtain the first property of
3.2
. The second property may be verified in a similar manner (see
Lemma

4.8
).

The case
d=2
and general
A
.

Let us try to extend the above argument for the case
d=2
and
A=2
to the case of general
A
(still keeping
d=2
fixed). Unfortunately, the function
x\mapsto\sum_{\epsilon_{u},\epsilon_{v}\in\{\pm 1\}}F^{\mathsf{tl}}_{h}(x;\beta\epsilon_{u}\cdot u,\epsilon_{v}\cdot v)
we used above no longer satisfies the first condition of
3.2
. To see this, suppose that
\mathcal{A}=\{0,1,2,3\}
and the feature vectors at some state
x
form a rectangle, i.e.,
\phi_{h}(x,0)=(0,0),\phi_{h}(x,1)=(u,0),\phi_{h}(x,2)=(u+v,u+v),\phi_{h}(x,3)=(0,v)
. It is straightforward to see that
F^{\mathsf{tl}}_{h}(x;u,v)=0
, whereas
\max_{a,a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\min\{|\langle\beta u,\phi_{h}(x,a)-\phi_{h}(x,a^{\prime})\rangle|,|\langle v,\phi_{h}(x,a)-\phi_{h}(x,a^{\prime})\rangle|\}\geq 1
for any
\beta\geq 1
(e.g., take
a=0,a^{\prime}=2
). Thus,
Eq.

12
cannot hold, even if we only wish for it to hold up to a
\poly(H,B,d)
factor.

The above issue runs fairly deep: it can be shown that, under some mild conditions on
F^{\mathsf{tl}}_{h}
which ensure that it be Bellman-linear,
9
9

9

Namely, that
F^{\mathsf{tl}}_{h}(x;u,v)=\int_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(w)\cdot\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\langle\phi_{h}(x,a),w\rangle d\mu(w)
, for some Borel measure
\mu
and function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
.
there is
no function

F_{h}^{(t)}
satisfying both conditions of
3.2
. To rectify this issue, we will relax the first condition of
3.2
, as follows:

Problem 3.3
.

In the setting of
3.2
, can we find a Bellman-linear function
F_{h}^{(t)}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
which satisfies the constraints of
3.2
where
Item

1
is replaced by the following constraint:

1.

For all
x\in\mathcal{X}
,
F_{h}^{(t)}(x)
is lower bounded as

\displaystyle F_{h}^{(t)}(x)\geq

\displaystyle BH\cdot\max_{a,a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\min_{\psi\in\bar{\Phi}(x)}\left\{\|\beta\Sigma^{\prime}\cdot(\phi_{h}(x,a)-\psi)\|_{2}+\|\Lambda^{\prime}\cdot(\psi-\phi_{h}(x,a^{\prime})\|_{2}\right\},

(13)

where
\bar{\Phi}_{h}(x):={\rm co}\{\phi_{h}(x,a):\ a\in\mathcal{A}\}
denotes the convex hull of the feature vectors
\phi_{h}(x,a),\ a\in\mathcal{A}
.

Note that
Eq.

13
is weaker than
Eq.

12
, as can be verified by considering the choices
\psi=\phi_{h}(x,a)
and
\psi=\phi_{h}(x,a^{\prime})
for given
a,a^{\prime}
in the outer maximum. As we discuss in
Section

5.3
, this weaker lower bound on
F_{h}^{(t)}(x)
is still sufficient to ensure optimism. Moreover, for the example discussed above in which the feature vectors at a state
x
form a rectangle, the right-hand side of
Eq.

13
is
0
: if
a,a^{\prime}
in the outer maximum are chosen to represent opposing corners of the rectangle, then taking
\psi
to be one of the other two corners yields
|\langle\beta u,\phi_{h}(x,a)-\psi\rangle|=|\langle v,\psi-\phi_{h}(x,a^{\prime})\rangle|=0
. For this example, we therefore have that
x\mapsto\sum_{\epsilon_{u},\epsilon_{v}\in\{\pm 1\}}F^{\mathsf{tl}}_{h}(x;\beta\epsilon_{u}\cdot u,\epsilon_{v}\cdot v)
satisfies the properties of
3.3
.

In fact, this holds more generally in the
d=2
case, as can be seen by the following argument: fix any
a,a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}
, and choose

\displaystyle\psi^{\star}:=\argmin_{\psi\in\bar{\Phi}_{h}(x)}\left\{|\langle\beta u,\phi_{h}(x,a)-\psi\rangle|+|\langle v,\psi-\phi_{h}(x,a^{\prime})\rangle|\right\}.

(14)

Moreover choose
\epsilon_{u},\epsilon_{v}\in\{\pm 1\}
so that
\langle\phi_{h}(x,a)-\psi^{\star},\epsilon_{u}u\rangle\geq 0
and
\langle\phi_{h}(x,a^{\prime})-\psi^{\star},\epsilon_{v}v\rangle\geq 0
. Note that
Eq.

14
is a convex optimization problem; its KKT conditions tell us that
\langle\psi^{\star}-\psi,\beta\epsilon_{u}u+\epsilon_{v}v\rangle\geq 0
for all
\psi\in\bar{\Phi}_{h}(x)
. Then, recalling that
a,a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}
were fixed, we have

\displaystyle F^{\mathsf{tl}}_{h}(x;\beta\epsilon_{u}u,\epsilon_{v}v)\geq

\displaystyle\langle\beta\epsilon_{u}u,\phi_{h}(x,a)\rangle+\langle\epsilon_{v}v,\phi_{h}(x,a^{\prime})\rangle-\max_{\bar{a}\in\mathcal{A}}\langle\beta\epsilon_{u}u+\epsilon_{v}v,\phi_{h}(x,\bar{a})\rangle

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\langle\beta\epsilon_{u}u,\phi_{h}(x,a)\rangle+\langle\epsilon_{v}v,\phi_{h}(x,a^{\prime})\rangle-\langle\beta\epsilon_{u}u+\epsilon_{v}v,\psi^{\star}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\beta\epsilon_{u}u,\phi_{h}(x,a)-\psi^{\star}\rangle+\langle\epsilon_{v}v,\phi_{h}(x,a^{\prime})-\psi^{\star}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\langle\beta u,\phi_{h}(x,a)-\psi^{\star}\rangle|+|\langle v,\phi_{h}(x,a^{\prime})-\psi^{\star}\rangle|,

(15)

where the second inequality uses the KKT conditions of
Eq.

14
and the final equality uses the choices of
\epsilon_{u},\epsilon_{v}
.
Eq.

15
, combined with non-negativity of
F^{\mathsf{tl}}_{h}(\cdot)
, verifies the first property of
3.3
, as desired; the second property (namely,
Item

2
of
3.2
) can be verified by a direct computation (
Lemma

4.8
).

The case of general
d
and general
A
.

The argument presented above for
d=2
generalizes to the case of arbitrary
d\in\mathbb{N}
. In particular, we will aim to construct a bonus of the form
G_{h}^{(t)}(x):=O(BH)\cdot\mathbb{E}_{u\sim\mathcal{D}_{\Sigma^{\prime}},v\sim\mathcal{D}_{\Lambda^{\prime}}}[F^{\mathsf{tl}}_{h}(x;u,v)]
, where
\mathcal{D}_{\Sigma^{\prime}},\mathcal{D}_{\Lambda^{\prime}}
are distributions supported on
\mathcal{S}_{\Sigma^{\prime}},\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda^{\prime}}
, respectively.
A key consideration is:
how do we choose the distributions
\mathcal{D}_{\Sigma^{\prime}},\mathcal{D}_{\Lambda^{\prime}}
?
To answer this question, it is instructive to consider how the above argument verifying
Item

1
of
3.3
generalizes to higher dimensions. In particular, given arbitrary
a,a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}
, in lieu of
Eq.

14
, we define

\displaystyle\psi^{\star}:=\argmin_{\psi\in\bar{\Phi}_{h}(x)}\left\{\|\beta\Sigma^{\prime}\cdot(\phi_{h}(x,a)-\psi)\|_{2}+\|\Lambda^{\prime}\cdot(\psi-\phi_{h}(x,a^{\prime}))\|_{2}\right\}.

(16)

Using the KKT conditions of
Eq.

16
, it can be verified that, for
u=\frac{\Sigma^{\prime}\cdot(\phi_{h}(x,a)-\psi^{\star})}{\|\Sigma^{\prime}\cdot(\phi_{h}(x,a)-\psi^{\star})\|_{2}}
and
v=\frac{\Lambda^{\prime}\cdot(\phi_{h}(x,a^{\prime})-\psi^{\star})}{\|\Lambda^{\prime}\cdot(\phi_{h}(x,a^{\prime})-\psi^{\star})\|_{2}}
, we have that

\displaystyle F^{\mathsf{tl}}_{h}(x;\beta u,v)\geq\|\beta\Sigma^{\prime}\cdot(\phi_{h}(x,a)-\psi^{\star})\|_{2}+\|\Lambda^{\prime}\cdot(\phi_{h}(x,a^{\prime})-\psi^{\star})\|_{2}.

(17)

Unfortunately these choices of
u,v
depend on the state
x
, and
\mathcal{D}_{\Sigma^{\prime}},\mathcal{D}_{\Lambda^{\prime}}
must be independent of
x
in order to ensure Bellman-linearity of the bonus function.

Fortunately, as shown in
Lemma

4.9
, under some additional technical conditions,
Eq.

17
holds up to an additive term of
O(\varepsilon)
when the left-hand side is replaced by
F^{\mathsf{tl}}_{h}(x;\beta u^{\prime},v^{\prime})
, for any vectors
u^{\prime},v^{\prime}
which are
\varepsilon
-close in
\ell_{2}
norm to
u,v
, respectively. If we take
\mathcal{D}_{\Sigma^{\prime}},\mathcal{D}_{\Lambda^{\prime}}
to be the uniform distributions on the unit sphere in the subspaces
\mathcal{S}_{\Sigma^{\prime}},\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda^{\prime}}
, respectively, then
u^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\Sigma^{\prime}},v^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\Lambda^{\prime}}
satisfy
\|u^{\prime}-u\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon,\|v^{\prime}-v\|_{2}\leq\epsilon
with probability
\varepsilon^{O(d)}
. With these choices of
\mathcal{D}_{\Sigma^{\prime}},\mathcal{D}_{\Lambda^{\prime}}
, the bonus
G_{h}^{(t)}
satisfies the constraint
Eq.

13
up to a factor of
\varepsilon^{-O(d)}
. In fact, this can easily be improved to a factor of
\varepsilon^{-O(A)}
by using the fact that closeness of
u,u^{\prime}
and of
v,v^{\prime}
only needs to hold in the subspace spanned by
\phi_{h}(x,a)
(for
a\in\mathcal{A}
), which has at most
A
dimensions – see
Lemma

4.10
.

It turns out to simplify the analysis to take
\mathcal{D}_{\Sigma^{\prime}},\mathcal{D}_{\Lambda^{\prime}}
to be the distributions
\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma^{\prime}),\mathcal{N}(0,\Lambda^{\prime})
, respectively, which permit essentially the same analysis as described above. Moreover, to satisfy certain technical conditions in
Lemma

4.9
, we add a term to the bonus function which is proportional to
F^{\mathsf{normal}}_{h}(x;\Sigma^{\prime})=\mathbb{E}_{w\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma^{\prime})}[\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\langle w,\phi_{h}(x,a)\rangle]
. To sum up, we define the bonus function as

\displaystyle F_{h}^{(t)}(x):=

\displaystyle\beta_{1}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{u^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma^{\prime})}\mathbb{E}_{v^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Lambda^{\prime})}\left[F^{\mathsf{tl}}_{h}(x;\beta u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\right]+\beta_{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{w\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma^{\prime})}\left[\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\langle w,\phi_{h}(x,a)\rangle\right],

(18)

where
\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\beta
are carefully chosen parameters bounded above by
(BHD/\epsilon)^{O(A)}
, where
\epsilon
is the desired error level of the algorithm’s output policy. The precise values of these parameters appear in the formal definition of
F_{h}^{(t)}
in
Eq.

33
. We remark that, crucially,
\beta_{2}\ll\beta_{1}\cdot\beta
– the entire point of the truncation procedure we have just described is to ensure that the size of
F_{h}^{(t)}(x)
(which may be as large as
O(\beta_{2})
) does
not
scale with
\beta
.

In
Sections

4.2
and
4.3
, we formally verify, per the arguments outlined above, that the bonus
F_{h}^{(t)}
in
Eq.

18
satisfies the properties laid out in
3.3
. Then, in
Sections

5.1
,
5.2
and
5.3
, we show how this implies the statements of
Lemmas

3.1
and
3.2
, which suffices to prove our main theorem (
Theorem

1.1
).

4
Bellman linear functions

Recall the definition of Bellman linearity in
Definition

3.1
. In this section, we discuss several properties of Bellman linear functions.

Lemma 4.1

(Limiting preserves Bellman-linearity)
.

Fix
h\in[H]
. Suppose that for each
i\in\mathbb{N}
, we are given a function
G_{i}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
which is Bellman-linear at step
h
and so that
\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X},i\in\mathbb{N}}|G_{i}(x)|\leq C
, for some
C>0
. Furthermore suppose that, for some
G:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}G_{i}(x)=G(x)
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
. Then
G
is Bellman-linear at step
h
.

Proof.

For each
i\in\mathbb{N}
, we know that there is some
w_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
so that, for all
(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}
,
\langle\phi_{h-1}(x,a),w_{i}\rangle=\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim P_{h-1}(x,a)}[G_{i}(x^{\prime})]
. For each
i,a,x
, we know that
|\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim P_{h-1}(x,a)}[G_{i}(x^{\prime})]|\leq C
by uniform boundedness of
G_{i}
, so
|\langle\phi_{h-1}(x,a),w_{i}\rangle|\leq C
for all
i,a,x
. By definition of
\mathcal{B}_{h-1}
, it follows that
w_{i}\in C\cdot\mathcal{B}_{h-1}
, which gives
\|w_{i}\|_{2}\leq CB
by
2.1
. Hence we may find a subsequence
(w_{i_{j}})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
so that
\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}w_{i_{j}}=w^{\star}
, for some
w^{\star}\in C\cdot\mathcal{B}_{h-1}
. Thus, for any
(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}
, we have

\displaystyle\langle\phi_{h-1}(x,a),w^{\star}\rangle=\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\langle\phi_{h-1}(x,a),w_{i_{j}}\rangle=\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim P_{h-1}(x,a)}[G_{i_{j}}(x^{\prime})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim P_{h-1}(x,a)}\left[\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}G_{i_{j}}(x^{\prime})\right]=\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim P_{h-1}(x,a)}[G(x^{\prime})],

where the second-to-last equality uses the dominated convergence theorem and uniform boundedness of the functions
G_{i}
.
∎

Lemma 4.2

(Differentiation preserves Bellman-linearity)
.

Fix any
h,k\in\mathbb{N}
and consider an open set
U\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
as well as a function
F:U\times\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
so that
F(u,x)
is continuously differentiable in
u
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
, and that
\max_{x\in\mathcal{X},u\in U}\max\{F(u,x),\|\nabla_{u}F(u,x)\|_{2}\}\leq C
. Suppose that, for all
u\in U
, the function
F(u,\cdot)
is Bellman-linear at step
h
. Then the function
\nabla_{u}F(u,\cdot)
is Bellman-linear at step
h
, for all
u\in U
.

Proof.

By Bellman-linearity, we know that for all
u\in U
, there is some
w_{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
so that, for all
(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\langle\phi_{h}(x,a),w_{u}\rangle=\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim P_{h-1}(x,a)}\left[F(u,x^{\prime})\right].

By the dominated convergence theorem (which may be applied as a consequence of uniform boundedness of
\|\nabla_{u}F(u,x)\|_{2}
over
u\in U
), it holds that
u\mapsto\langle\phi_{h}(x,a),w_{u}\rangle
is continuously differentiable for all
x,a
, and moreover,

\displaystyle\nabla_{u}\langle\phi_{h}(x,a),w_{u}\rangle=\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim P_{h-1}(x,a)}[\nabla_{u}F(u,x^{\prime})].

(19)

Choose
(x_{1},a_{1}),\ldots,(x_{\ell},a_{\ell})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}
so that
\{\phi_{h}(x_{i},a_{i})\}_{i=1}^{\ell}
is linearly independent and spans
\{\phi_{h}(x,a)\}_{(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}}
; then
\ell\leq d
. Let
W_{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be any matrix satisfying

\displaystyle W_{u}\cdot\phi_{h}(x_{i},a_{i})=\nabla_{u}\langle\phi_{h}(x_{i},a_{i}),w_{u}\rangle\quad\forall i\in[\ell],

which is possible by linear independence of
\phi_{h}(x_{i},a_{i}),\ i\in[\ell]
. Now consider any
(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}
; we can find
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}
so that
\phi_{h}(x,a)=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\alpha_{i}\cdot\phi_{h}(x_{i},a_{i})
. Then

\displaystyle W_{u}\cdot\phi_{h}(x,a)=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\alpha_{i}\cdot\nabla_{u}\langle\phi_{h}(x_{i},a_{i}),w_{u}\rangle=\nabla_{u}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\alpha_{i}\cdot\langle\phi_{h}(x_{i},a_{i}),w_{u}\rangle=\nabla_{u}\langle\phi_{h}(x,a),w_{u}\rangle,

which completes the proof by
Eq.

19
.
∎

Lemma

4.3
below states that the feature vectors induced by any linear policy are Bellman-linear. It is a special case of Corollary 3.2 of
[
GM24
]
(by taking the inherent Bellman error to be 0).

Lemma 4.3
.

Suppose that
M
is linear Bellman complete. Then for each
h\in[H]
and each
w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the mapping
x^{\prime}\mapsto\phi_{h+1}(x^{\prime},\pi_{h+1,w}(x^{\prime}))
is Bellman-linear at step
h+1
, i.e., there is a linear map
L_{h}(w):\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
so that, for all
(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}
,

\displaystyle L_{h}(w)^{\top}\cdot\phi_{h}(x,a)=\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim P_{h}(x,a)}[\phi_{h+1}(x^{\prime},\pi_{h+1,w}(x^{\prime}))].

Proof.

Fix
h\in[H]
. Define
V(x^{\prime},\theta):=\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\langle\phi_{h+1}(x^{\prime},a^{\prime}),\theta\rangle
and
Q(x,a,\theta)=\langle\phi_{h}(x,a),\mathcal{T}_{h}\theta\rangle
, so that
Q(x,a,\theta)=\langle\phi_{h}(x,a),\mathcal{T}_{h}\theta\rangle=\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim P_{h}(x,a)}[V(x^{\prime},\theta)]
. By Fubini’s theorem, for any
\sigma>0
, we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{S}_{\sigma}Q(x,a,\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d})}[Q(x,a,\theta-z)]=\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim P_{h}(x,a)}\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d})}[V(x^{\prime},\theta-x)]=\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim P_{h}(x,a)}[\mathsf{S}_{\sigma}V(x^{\prime},\theta)].

Linear Bellman completeness of
M
gives that for each
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the function
x^{\prime}\mapsto V(x^{\prime},\theta)
, and thus
x^{\prime}\mapsto\mathsf{S}_{\sigma}V(x^{\prime},\theta)
is Bellman-linear at step
h+1
. The function
\mathsf{S}_{\sigma}V(x^{\prime},\theta)
is infinitely differentiable as a function of
\theta
, for all
x^{\prime}
; moreover, since

\displaystyle\nabla\mathsf{S}_{\sigma}V(x^{\prime},\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d})}[\nabla V(x^{\prime},\theta-z)]=\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d})}[\phi_{h+1}(x^{\prime},\pi_{h+1,\theta-z}(x^{\prime}))],

(20)

we have that
\max\{\mathsf{S}_{\sigma}V(x^{\prime},\theta),\|\nabla\mathsf{S}_{\sigma}V(x^{\prime},\theta)\|_{2}\}
is bounded uniformly over
x^{\prime}
in the neighborhood of any
\theta
. We remark that the first equality in
Eq.

20
uses the dominated convergence theorem, and the second equality uses that the
V(x^{\prime},\theta-z)
is differentiable almost surely over
z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d})
. Thus, by
Lemma

4.2
, the function
x^{\prime}\mapsto\nabla\mathsf{S}_{\sigma}V(x^{\prime},\theta)
is Bellman-linear at step
h
, for all
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
But for
x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
and
w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\nabla\mathsf{S}_{\sigma}V(x^{\prime},w)=\phi_{h+1}(x^{\prime},\pi_{h+1,w,\sigma}(x^{\prime})),

where the equality above uses
Eq.

20
together with
Definition

A.1
. Next,
Eq.

72
ensures that for each
x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
,
\lim_{\sigma\rightarrow 0^{+}}\phi_{h+1}(x^{\prime},\pi_{h+1,w,\sigma}(x^{\prime})=\phi_{h+1}(x^{\prime},\pi_{h+1,w}(x^{\prime}))
. Then
Lemma

4.1
(applied to each coordinate of the function
x^{\prime}\mapsto\phi_{h+1}(x^{\prime},\pi_{h+1,w,\sigma}(x^{\prime})
, for
\sigma>0
) yields that
x^{\prime}\mapsto\phi_{h+1}(x^{\prime},\pi_{h+1,w}(x^{\prime}))
is Bellman-linear at step
h+1
, for any
w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
∎

Given any
\pi_{h+1}\in\Pi^{\mathsf{lin}}_{h+1}
(so that
\pi_{h+1}=\pi_{h+1,v}
for some
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
) and
w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we define
\mathcal{T}_{h}^{\pi}w:=L_{h}(v)\cdot w
. Then by
Lemma

4.3
, for all
(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}
,

\displaystyle\langle\phi_{h}(x,a),\mathcal{T}_{h}^{\pi}w\rangle=\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim P_{h}(x,a)}[\langle\phi_{h+1}(x^{\prime},\pi_{h+1}(x^{\prime})),w\rangle].

(21)

Note that
\mathcal{T}_{h}^{\pi}
depends only on
\pi_{h+1}
; thus with slight abuse of notation, we will sometimes write
\mathcal{T}_{h}^{\pi_{h+1}}w
in place of
\mathcal{T}_{h}^{\pi}w
. We also remark that by linearity of expectation, for any
\pi\in\Pi^{\mathsf{Plin}}
and
w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, there is a vector
\mathcal{T}_{h}^{\pi}w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
so that
Eq.

21
holds for the perturbed linear policy
\pi
.

As an immediate consequence of
Lemma

4.3
, we can show that the
Q
-function for a linear policy is linear.

Corollary 4.4
.

Suppose that
M
is linear Bellman complete, and that
\pi\in\Pi^{\mathsf{Plin}}
. Then for each
h\in[H]
, there is a vector
w_{h}^{\pi}\in H\cdot\mathcal{B}_{h}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
so that for all
(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}
,
Q_{h}^{\pi}(x,a)=\langle w_{h}^{\pi},\phi_{h}(x,a)\rangle
. Moreover,
\|w_{h}^{\pi}\|_{2}\leq HB
.

Proof.

Since
Q_{h}^{\pi}(x,a)=r_{h}(x,a)+\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim P_{h}(x,a)}[Q_{h+1}^{\pi}(x,\pi_{h+1}(x))]
, by induction and the fact that
r_{h}(x,a)
is linear, it suffices to show that for all
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, there is some
w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
so that
\langle\phi_{h}(x,a),w\rangle=\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim P_{h}(x,a)}[\langle\phi_{h}(x,\pi_{h+1}(x)),v\rangle]
. We may write
\pi_{h+1}=\pi_{h+1,y,\sigma}
for some
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\sigma\geq 0
; then by
Lemma

4.3
, we may take
w=\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{N}(y,\sigma^{2})}[L_{h}(z)]\cdot v
.

To see the upper bound on
\|w_{h}^{\pi}\|_{2}
, note that, by definition of
Q_{h}^{\pi}
, we have
|\langle w_{h}^{\pi},\phi_{h}(x,a)\rangle|\leq H
for all
x,a,h
. Then it follows that
\|w_{h}^{\pi}\|_{2}\leq HB
by
2.1
.
∎

4.1
Construction of Bellman-linear functions for bonuses

A central component of the proof consists of the construction of functions, to be used as exploration bonuses, which (a) are Bellman-linear and (b) approximate a truncated version of arbitrary norms induced by PSD matrices. In this section, we establish Bellman linearity of certain functions which form the building blocks of these bonuses.

Given orthogonal vectors
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we let
\Pi_{u,v}:=\frac{uu^{\top}}{\|u\|_{2}}+\frac{vv^{\top}}{\|v\|_{2}}
. Note that if
u,v
are unit vectors, then
\Pi_{u,v}
is the projection matrix onto
{\rm Span}\{u,v\}
.

Definition 4.1

(Orthogonal pair)
.

We define a tuple of
d\times d
PSD matrices
(\Sigma,\Lambda)
to be an
orthogonal pair
if the following equalities hold:

\displaystyle\Sigma^{2}=\Sigma,\ \Lambda^{2}=\Lambda,\qquad\Sigma\Lambda=\Lambda\Sigma=0,\qquad\Sigma+\Lambda=I_{d}.

For an orthogonal pair
(\Sigma,\Lambda)
,
\Sigma,\Lambda
are projections onto subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which we denote by
\mathcal{S}_{\Sigma}:=\{\Sigma v:\ v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\},\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}:=\{\Lambda v:\ v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
, respectively. Since
\Sigma\Lambda=\Lambda\Sigma=0
, the subspaces
\mathcal{S}_{\Sigma},\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}
are orthogonal. Since
\Sigma+\Lambda=I_{d}
, we have that
\dim(\mathcal{S}_{\Sigma})+\dim(\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda})=d
.

Definition 4.2

(Truncated linear bonus)
.

Consider orthogonal vectors
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For
\Phi\in\mathscr{P}^{d}
, we define

F^{\mathsf{tl}}(\Phi;u,v)=\max_{\phi\in\Phi}\langle u,\phi\rangle+\max_{\phi\in\Phi}\langle v,\phi\rangle-\max_{\phi\in\Phi}\langle u+v,\phi\rangle.

For
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
h\in[H]
, we then define
F^{\mathsf{tl}}_{h}(x;u,v):=F^{\mathsf{tl}}(\bar{\Phi}_{h}(x);u,v)
.

The following lemma is immediate from the definition of
F^{\mathsf{tl}}(\cdot)
.

Lemma 4.5

(Bellman-linearity of truncated linear bonus)
.

Given any
h>1
and vectors
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the mapping
x\mapsto F^{\mathsf{tl}}_{h}(x;u,v)
is Bellman-linear at step
h
.

We next record the following lemmas for future use, which follow as an immediate consequence of the definition of
F^{\mathsf{tl}}
.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
\Phi\in\mathscr{P}^{d}
. Suppose that
u,u^{\prime},v,v^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfy
\langle u,\phi\rangle=\langle u^{\prime},\phi\rangle
and
\langle v,\phi\rangle=\langle v^{\prime},\phi\rangle
for all
\phi\in\Phi
. Then

\displaystyle F^{\mathsf{tl}}(\Phi;u,v)=F^{\mathsf{tl}}(\Phi;u^{\prime},v^{\prime}).

Proof.

We have that
\max_{\phi\in\Phi}\langle w,\phi\rangle=\max_{\phi\in\Phi}\langle w^{\prime},\phi\rangle
for each pair
(w,w^{\prime})\in\{(u,u^{\prime}),(v,v^{\prime}),(u+v,u^{\prime}+v^{\prime})\}
.
∎

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
\Phi\in\mathscr{P}^{d}
. Suppose that
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are given and that
\alpha_{u},\alpha_{v}\geq 0
are real numbers. Then

\displaystyle F^{\mathsf{tl}}(\Phi;\alpha_{u}\cdot u,\alpha_{v}\cdot v)\geq\min\{\alpha_{u},\alpha_{v}\}\cdot F^{\mathsf{tl}}(\Phi;u,v).

Proof.

Since
F^{\mathsf{tl}}(\Phi;\alpha u,\alpha v)=\alpha\cdot F^{\mathsf{tl}}(\Phi;u,v)
for any
\alpha\geq 0
, it suffices to consider the case that
\min\{\alpha_{u},\alpha_{v}\}=1
. Without loss of generality let us suppose that
\alpha_{u}=1\leq\alpha_{v}
. Then

\displaystyle F^{\mathsf{tl}}(\Phi;u,\alpha_{v}\cdot v)-F^{\mathsf{tl}}(\Phi;u,v)=

\displaystyle(\alpha_{v}-1)\cdot\max_{\phi\in\Phi}\langle v,\phi\rangle-\max_{\phi\in\Phi}\langle u+\alpha_{v}\cdot v,\phi\rangle+\max_{\phi\in\Phi}\langle u+v,\phi\rangle\geq 0,

where the final inequality follows since
u+\alpha_{v}\cdot v=(u+v)+(\alpha_{v}-1)\cdot v
.
∎

4.2
An upper bound on the truncated linear bonus

Lemma

4.8
below shows that the truncated linear bonus
F^{\mathsf{tl}}(\Phi;u,v)
can be bounded above by the length of the projection of
\Phi
onto each of
u
and
v
.

Lemma 4.8
.

Suppose
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\Phi\in\mathscr{P}^{d}
. Then

\displaystyle 0\leq F^{\mathsf{tl}}(\Phi;u,v)\leq 2\cdot\min\left\{\max_{\phi,\phi^{\prime}\in\Phi}\langle u,\phi-\phi^{\prime}\rangle,\max_{\phi,\phi^{\prime}\in\Phi}\langle v,\phi-\phi^{\prime}\rangle\right\}.

Proof.

For any fixed
\phi
, we have

\displaystyle\langle u+v,\phi\rangle\leq\max_{\phi^{\prime}\in\Phi}\langle u,\phi^{\prime}\rangle+\max_{\phi^{\prime}\in\Phi}\langle v,\phi^{\prime}\rangle,

which implies
F^{\mathsf{tl}}(\Phi;u,v)\geq 0
.

To upper bound
F^{\mathsf{tl}}(\Phi;u,v)
, we first note that

\displaystyle F^{\mathsf{tl}}(\Phi;u,v)\leq

\displaystyle F^{\mathsf{tl}}(\Phi;u,v)+F^{\mathsf{tl}}(\Phi;-u,-v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{\phi,\phi^{\prime}\in\Phi}\langle u,\phi-\phi^{\prime}\rangle+\max_{\phi,\phi^{\prime}\in\Phi}\langle v,\phi-\phi^{\prime}\rangle-\max_{\phi,\phi^{\prime}\in\Phi}\langle u+v,\phi-\phi^{\prime}\rangle.

We will now upper bound
F^{\mathsf{tl}}(\Phi;u,v)+F^{\mathsf{tl}}(\Phi;-u,-v)
as follows. By symmetry, it is without loss of generality to assume that
\max_{\phi,\phi^{\prime}\in\Phi}\langle u,\phi-\phi^{\prime}\rangle\leq\max_{\phi,\phi^{\prime}\in\Phi}\langle v,\phi-\phi^{\prime}\rangle
. Now we have

\displaystyle\max_{\phi,\phi^{\prime}\in\Phi}\langle u+v,\phi-\phi^{\prime}\rangle\geq

\displaystyle\max_{\phi,\phi^{\prime}\in\Phi}\langle v,\phi-\phi^{\prime}\rangle-\max_{\phi,\phi^{\prime}\in\Phi}\langle-u,\phi-\phi^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{\phi,\phi^{\prime}\in\Phi}\langle v,\phi-\phi^{\prime}\rangle-\max_{\phi,\phi^{\prime}\in\Phi}\langle u,\phi-\phi^{\prime}\rangle.

Rearranging, we see that
F^{\mathsf{tl}}(\Phi;u,v)+F^{\mathsf{tl}}(\Phi;-u,-v)\leq 2\max_{\phi,\phi^{\prime}\in\Phi}\langle u,\phi-\phi^{\prime}\rangle
, as desired.
∎

4.3
A lower bound on the modified truncated linear bonus

Given
\Phi\in\mathscr{P}^{d}
and a matrix
\Gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, let
\mathcal{S}_{\Phi}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote the subspace
\mathcal{S}_{\Phi}:={\rm Span}\{\phi:\phi\in\Phi\}
, and
\mathcal{S}_{\Phi,\Gamma}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote the subspace
\mathcal{S}_{\Phi,\Gamma}:={\rm Span}\{\Gamma\cdot\phi:\ \phi\in\Phi\}=\Gamma\cdot\mathcal{S}_{\Phi}
. Let
\Pi_{\Phi,\Gamma}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
denote the matrix which projects onto
\mathcal{S}_{\Phi,\Gamma}
. Recall that for an orthogonal pair
(\Sigma,\Lambda)
(
Definition

4.1
), we have
\mathcal{S}_{\Sigma}=\Sigma\cdot\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}=\Lambda\cdot\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then for any
\Phi\in\mathscr{P}^{d}
,
\mathcal{S}_{\Phi,\Sigma}\subset\mathcal{S}_{\Sigma}
and
\mathcal{S}_{\Phi,\Lambda}\subset\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}
.

Moreover, given
\Phi\in\mathscr{P}^{d}
and
\phi_{1},\phi_{2}\in\Phi
, we define

\displaystyle\mu^{\star}_{{\Phi,\phi_{1},\phi_{2}}}:=\argmin_{\xi\in\Phi}\left\{\|\beta\Sigma\cdot(\phi_{1}-\xi)\|_{2}+\|\Lambda\cdot(\xi-\phi_{2})\|_{2}\right\}.

(22)

The point
\mu^{\star}_{{\Phi,\phi_{1},\phi_{2}}}
can be thought of as a sort of “midpoint” between
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{2}
in
\Phi
, where the distance between
\phi_{1}
and
\mu^{\star}_{{\Phi,\phi_{1},\phi_{2}}}
is measured with respect to
\beta\Sigma
and the distance between
\mu^{\star}_{{\Phi,\phi_{1},\phi_{2}}}
and
\phi_{2}
is measured with respect to
\Lambda
. As it turns out, this particular notion of midpoint will be useful in proving that our exploration bonuses induce optimistic value functions.

Finally, we introduce some notation regarding the normal cone of a convex body. For a convex subset
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
z\in\mathcal{C}
, the normal cone of
\mathcal{C}
at
z
, denoted
N_{\mathcal{C}}(z)
is defined as
N_{\mathcal{C}}(z):=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\ \langle y,z-z^{\prime}\rangle\geq 0\ \forall z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}\}
.

Lemma 4.9
.

Consider an orthogonal pair
(\Sigma,\Lambda)
and
\beta\geq 1,\zeta>0
.
Suppose that
\Phi\in\mathscr{P}^{d}
satisfies

\displaystyle\max\left\{\max_{\phi_{1},\phi_{2}\in\Phi}\|\beta\Sigma\cdot(\phi_{1}-\phi_{2})\|_{2},\ \max_{\phi_{1},\phi_{2}\in\Phi}\|\Lambda\cdot(\phi_{1}-\phi_{2})\|_{2}\right\}\leq\zeta.

(23)

Fix any
\phi_{1},\phi_{2}\in\Phi
. Then there are unit vectors
u\in\mathcal{S}_{\Phi,\Sigma}
and
v\in\mathcal{S}_{\Phi,\Lambda}
(depending on
\Phi,\phi_{1},\phi_{2}
) so that for all unit vectors
u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{\Sigma},v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}
satisfying

\displaystyle\|u-u^{\prime}\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon,\qquad\|v-v^{\prime}\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon,\qquad\langle u^{\prime},u\rangle\geq\eta,\qquad\langle v^{\prime},v\rangle\geq\eta,

(24)

it holds that

\displaystyle F^{\mathsf{tl}}(\Phi;\beta u^{\prime},v^{\prime})

\displaystyle\geq\eta\|\beta\Sigma\cdot(\phi_{1}-\mu^{\star}_{{\Phi,\phi_{1},\phi_{2}}})\|_{2}+\eta\|\Lambda\cdot(\mu^{\star}_{{\Phi,\phi_{1},\phi_{2}}}-\phi_{2})\|_{2}-2\varepsilon\zeta.

Proof.

Fix
\Phi\in\mathscr{P}^{d}
satisfying
Eq.

23
and
\phi_{1},\phi_{2}\in\Phi
.

Step 1: Choosing
u,v
.

The KKT conditions for optimality of
\mu^{\star}_{{\Phi,\phi_{1},\phi_{2}}}
(as defined in
Eq.

22
) give that, for all
z\in\bar{\Phi}
,

\displaystyle\frac{\beta^{2}\Sigma\cdot(\phi_{1}-\mu^{\star}_{{\Phi,\phi_{1},\phi_{2}}})}{\|\beta\Sigma\cdot(\phi_{1}-\mu^{\star}_{{\Phi,\phi_{1},\phi_{2}}})\|_{2}}+\frac{\Lambda\cdot(\phi_{2}-\mu^{\star}_{{\Phi,\phi_{1},\phi_{2}}})}{\|\Lambda\cdot(\phi_{2}-\mu^{\star}_{{\Phi,\phi_{1},\phi_{2}}})\|_{2}}\in N_{\Phi}(\mu^{\star}_{{\Phi,\phi_{1},\phi_{2}}}),

(25)

where
N_{\Phi}(\xi)
denotes the normal cone of
\Phi
at
\xi
.We now set

\displaystyle u:=\frac{\Sigma\cdot(\phi_{1}-\mu^{\star}_{{\Phi,\phi_{1},\phi_{2}}})}{\|\Sigma\cdot(\phi_{1}-\mu^{\star}_{{\Phi,\phi_{1},\phi_{2}}})\|_{2}},\qquad v:=\frac{\Lambda\cdot(\phi_{2}-\mu^{\star}_{{\Phi,\phi_{1},\phi_{2}}})}{\|\Lambda\cdot(\phi_{2}-\mu^{\star}_{{\Phi,\phi_{1},\phi_{2}}})\|_{2}}.

Then
Eq.

25
states that for all
z\in\Phi
,
\langle\beta u+v,\mu^{\star}_{{\Phi,\phi_{1},\phi_{2}}}-z\rangle\geq 0
. Moreover, since
\mu^{\star}_{{\Phi,\phi_{1},\phi_{2}}}\in\Phi
, we certainly have that
u\in\mathcal{S}_{\Phi,\Sigma}\subset\mathcal{S}_{\Sigma},\ v\in\mathcal{S}_{\Phi,\Lambda}\subset\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}
.

Step 2: Relating
u,v
to
u^{\prime},v^{\prime}
satisfying
Eq.

24
.

Now consider any unit vectors
u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{\Sigma},v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}
satisfying
Eq.

24
. Choose

\displaystyle\xi^{\prime}:=\argmax_{\xi\in\Phi}\langle\beta u^{\prime}+v^{\prime},\xi\rangle.

(26)

Next, we compute

\displaystyle\langle\beta u^{\prime}+v^{\prime},\xi^{\prime}-\mu^{\star}_{{\Phi,\phi_{1},\phi_{2}}}\rangle=

\displaystyle\langle(\beta u^{\prime}+v^{\prime})-(\beta u+v),\xi^{\prime}-\mu^{\star}_{{\Phi,\phi_{1},\phi_{2}}}\rangle+\langle\beta u+v,\xi^{\prime}-\mu^{\star}_{{\Phi,\phi_{1},\phi_{2}}}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\beta|\langle u-u^{\prime},\xi^{\prime}-\mu^{\star}_{{\Phi,\phi_{1},\phi_{2}}}\rangle|+|\langle v-v^{\prime},\xi^{\prime}-\mu^{\star}_{{\Phi,\phi_{1},\phi_{2}}}\rangle|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta|\langle u-u^{\prime},\Sigma\cdot(\xi^{\prime}-\mu^{\star}_{{\Phi,\phi_{1},\phi_{2}}})\rangle|+|\langle v-v^{\prime},\Lambda\cdot(\xi^{\prime}-\mu^{\star}_{{\Phi,\phi_{1},\phi_{2}}})\rangle|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta|\langle\Pi_{\Phi,\Sigma}\cdot(u-u^{\prime}),\Sigma\cdot(\xi^{\prime}-\mu^{\star}_{{\Phi,\phi_{1},\phi_{2}}})\rangle|+|\langle\Pi_{\Phi,\Lambda}\cdot(v-v^{\prime}),\Lambda\cdot(\xi^{\prime}-\mu^{\star}_{{\Phi,\phi_{1},\phi_{2}}})\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\beta\|\Pi_{\Phi,\Sigma}\cdot(u-u^{\prime})\|_{2}\cdot\|\Sigma\cdot(\xi^{\prime}-\mu^{\star}_{{\Phi,\phi_{1},\phi_{2}}})\|_{2}+\|\Pi_{\Phi,\Lambda}\cdot(v-v^{\prime})\|_{2}\cdot\|\Lambda\cdot(\xi^{\prime}-\mu^{\star}_{{\Phi,\phi_{1},\phi_{2}}})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\varepsilon\zeta,

(27)

where the first inequality uses the triangle inequality and the fact that
\langle\beta u+v,\xi^{\prime}-\mu^{\star}_{{\Phi,\phi_{1},\phi_{2}}}\rangle\leq 0
since
\xi^{\prime}\in\Phi
; the second equality uses the fact that
u-u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{\Sigma}
and
v-v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}
; the third equality uses the fact that
\Sigma\cdot(\xi^{\prime}-\mu^{\star}_{{\Phi,\phi_{1},\phi_{2}}})\in\mathcal{S}_{\Phi,\Sigma}
and
\Lambda\cdot(\xi^{\prime}-\mu^{\star}_{{\Phi,\phi_{1},\phi_{2}}})\in\mathcal{S}_{\Phi,\Lambda}
; the second inequality uses Cauchy-Schwarz; and the third inequality uses
Eq.

24
and
Eq.

23
.

We then have

\displaystyle\langle\phi_{1}-\xi^{\prime},\beta u^{\prime}\rangle+\langle\phi_{2}-\xi^{\prime},v^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\Sigma\cdot(\phi_{1}-\xi^{\prime}),\beta u^{\prime}+v^{\prime}\rangle+\langle\Lambda\cdot(\phi_{2}-\xi^{\prime}),v^{\prime}+\beta u^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\Sigma\cdot\phi_{1}+\Lambda\cdot\phi_{2}-\xi^{\prime},\beta u^{\prime}+v^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\langle\Sigma\cdot\phi_{1}+\Lambda\cdot\phi_{2}-\mu^{\star}_{{\Phi,\phi_{1},\phi_{2}}},\beta u^{\prime}+v^{\prime}\rangle-2\varepsilon\zeta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\Sigma\cdot(\phi_{1}-\mu^{\star}_{{\Phi,\phi_{1},\phi_{2}}}),\beta u^{\prime}\rangle+\langle\Lambda\cdot(\phi_{2}-\mu^{\star}_{{\Phi,\phi_{1},\phi_{2}}}),v^{\prime}\rangle-2\varepsilon\zeta

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\eta\beta\|\Sigma\cdot(\phi_{1}-\mu^{\star}_{{\Phi,\phi_{1},\phi_{2}}})\|_{2}+\eta\|\Lambda\cdot(\phi_{2}-\mu^{\star}_{{\Phi,\phi_{1},\phi_{2}}})\|_{2}-2\varepsilon\zeta,

(28)

where the first equality uses that
\Sigma u^{\prime}=u^{\prime},\Sigma v^{\prime}=v^{\prime}
, and
\Sigma v^{\prime}=\Lambda u^{\prime}=0
since
(\Sigma,\Lambda)
is an orthogonal pair, the second equality uses that
\Sigma+\Lambda=I_{d}
, the first inequality uses
Eq.

27
, the third equality again uses
\Sigma+\Lambda=I_{d}
and the fact that
\Sigma v^{\prime}=\Lambda u^{\prime}=0
, and the second inequality uses
Eq.

24
and the definition of
u,v
.

Step 3: wrapping up.

Finally, we may write

\displaystyle F^{\mathsf{tl}}(\Phi;\beta u^{\prime},v^{\prime})=

\displaystyle\max_{\phi\in\Phi}\langle\phi,\beta u^{\prime}\rangle+\max_{\phi\in\Phi}\langle\phi,v^{\prime}\rangle-\max_{\phi\in\Phi}\langle\phi,\beta u^{\prime}+v^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{\phi\in\Phi}\langle\phi,\beta u^{\prime}\rangle+\max_{\phi\in\Phi}\langle\phi,v^{\prime}\rangle-\langle\xi,\beta u^{\prime}+v^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\langle\phi_{1},\beta u^{\prime}\rangle+\langle\phi_{2},v^{\prime}\rangle-\langle\xi,\beta u^{\prime}+v^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\eta\beta\|\Sigma\cdot(\phi_{1}-\mu^{\star}_{{\Phi,\phi_{1},\phi_{2}}})\|_{2}+\eta\|\Lambda\cdot(\phi_{2}-\mu^{\star}_{{\Phi,\phi_{1},\phi_{2}}})\|_{2}-2\varepsilon\zeta,

where the second equality uses the definition of
\xi
in
Eq.

26
, and the second inequality uses
Eq.

28
.
∎

By averaging over
u^{\prime},v^{\prime}
drawn randomly from appropriate subspaces, we have the following consequence of

.

Lemma 4.10
.

There is a constant
C_{\ref{cor:optimal-perimeter}}
so that the following holds. Fix
A\in\mathbb{N}
, an orthogonal pair
(\Sigma,\Lambda)
,
\beta\geq 1
,
\zeta>0
,
\Phi\in\mathscr{P}_{A}^{d}
,
\phi_{1},\phi_{2}\in\Phi
, and suppose that
\Phi
satisfies
Eq.

Then for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\left(\frac{C_{\ref{cor:optimal-perimeter}}}{\varepsilon}\right)^{2A}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{u^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)}\mathbb{E}_{v^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Lambda)}\left[F^{\mathsf{tl}}(\Phi;\beta u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\right]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\|\beta\Sigma\cdot(\phi_{1}-\mu^{\star}_{{\Phi,\phi_{1},\phi_{2}}})\|_{2}+\|\Lambda\cdot(\mu^{\star}_{{\Phi,\phi_{1},\phi_{2}}}-\phi_{2})\|_{2}-4\varepsilon\zeta.

Proof.

Note that if
\dim(\mathcal{S}_{\Phi,\Sigma})=0
, then we could choose
\mu^{\star}_{{\Phi,\phi_{1},\phi_{2}}}=\phi_{2}
in
Eq.

22
, and thus the lemma statement becomes immediate. A symmetric argument applies to the case that
\dim(\mathcal{S}_{\Phi,\Lambda})=0
. Thus we may assume for the remainder of the proof that
\dim(\mathcal{S}_{\Phi,\Sigma}),\dim(\mathcal{S}_{\Phi,\Lambda})\geq 1
.

Note that the subspace
\mathcal{S}_{\Phi,\Sigma}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{\Sigma}
satisfies
\dim(\mathcal{S}_{\Phi,\Sigma})\leq A
, and similarly,
\dim(\mathcal{S}_{\Phi,\Lambda})\leq A
. Note that
\mathbb{E}_{u^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)}[(\Pi_{\Phi,\Sigma}u^{\prime})\cdot(\Pi_{\Phi,\Sigma}u^{\prime})^{\top}]=\Pi_{\Phi,\Sigma}\cdot\Sigma\cdot\Pi_{\Phi,\Sigma}=\Pi_{\Phi,\Sigma}
, meaning that
\Pi_{\Phi,\Sigma}u^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Pi_{\Phi,\Sigma})
. Similarly, we have that
\Pi_{\Phi,\Lambda}v^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Pi_{\Phi,\Lambda})
.

Therefore, we have that
\frac{\Pi_{\Phi,\Sigma}u^{\prime}}{\|\Pi_{\Phi,\Sigma}u^{\prime}\|_{2}}\sim\mathsf{unif}(S^{d-1}\cap\mathcal{S}_{\Phi,\Sigma})
. Since
S^{d-1}\cap\mathcal{S}_{\Phi,\Sigma}
may be covered with
(3/\varepsilon)^{A}
balls of radius
\varepsilon
, it follows that, for any

, we have

\displaystyle\PR_{u^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)}\left(\left\|\frac{\Pi_{\Phi,\Sigma}u^{\prime}}{\|\Pi_{\Phi,\Sigma}u^{\prime}\|_{2}}-u\right\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon\right)\geq(\varepsilon/3)^{A}.

Moreover, we have that
\PR_{u^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)}(\|\Pi_{\Phi,\Sigma}u^{\prime}\|_{2}\geq 1/2)\geq 1/2
: this holds because
\|\Pi_{\Phi,\Sigma}u^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}
is a chi-squared random variable with
\dim(\mathcal{S}_{\Phi,\Sigma})\geq 1
degrees of freedom. Using the fact that
\|\Pi_{\Phi,\Sigma}u^{\prime}\|_{2}
and
\frac{\Pi_{\Phi,\Sigma}u^{\prime}}{\|\Pi_{\Phi,\Sigma}u^{\prime}\|_{2}}
are independent random variables, it follows that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\cdot(\varepsilon/3)^{A}\leq

\displaystyle\PR_{u^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)}\left(\left\|\frac{\Pi_{\Phi,\Sigma}u^{\prime}}{\|\Pi_{\Phi,\Sigma}u^{\prime}\|_{2}}-u\right\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon\mbox{ and }\|\Pi_{\Phi,\Sigma}u^{\prime}\|_{2}\geq 1/2\right).

In a symmetric manner, we obtain that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\cdot(\varepsilon/3)^{A}\leq

\displaystyle\PR_{v^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Lambda)}\left(\left\|\frac{\Pi_{\Phi,\Lambda}v^{\prime}}{\|\Pi_{\Phi,\Lambda}v^{\prime}\|_{2}}-v\right\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon\mbox{ and }\|\Pi_{\Phi,\Lambda}v^{\prime}\|_{2}\geq 1/2\right).

Let us define the random variables
u^{\prime\prime}:=\frac{\Pi_{\Phi,\Sigma}u^{\prime}}{\|\Pi_{\Phi,\Sigma}u^{\prime}\|_{2}}
and
v^{\prime\prime}:=\frac{\Pi_{\Phi,\Lambda}v^{\prime}}{\|\Pi_{\Phi,\Lambda}v^{\prime}\|_{2}}
. Let
u\in\mathcal{S}_{\Phi,\Sigma}
and

be chosen according to the statement of
Lemma

4.9
given
\Phi,\phi_{1},\phi_{2}
. Then with probability at least
(1/4)\cdot(\varepsilon/3)^{2A}
over the independent draws of
u^{\prime}
and
v^{\prime}
(which induce values of
u^{\prime\prime},v^{\prime\prime}
), we have that:

\displaystyle\|u^{\prime\prime}-u\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon,\quad\|v^{\prime\prime}-v\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon,\quad\langle u^{\prime\prime},u\rangle\geq 1/2,\quad\langle v^{\prime\prime},v\rangle\geq 1/2,\quad\|\Pi_{\Phi,\Sigma}u^{\prime}\|_{2}\geq 1/2,\quad\|\Pi_{\Phi,\Lambda}v^{\prime}\|_{2}\geq 1/2,

(29)

where we have used that
\varepsilon\leq 1
and that for unit vectors
y,y^{\prime}
, we have
\langle y,y^{\prime}\rangle=\frac{2-\|y-y^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}}{2}
.

For any
\phi\in\Phi\subset\mathcal{S}_{\Phi}
, we have

\displaystyle\langle u^{\prime},\phi\rangle=\langle u^{\prime},\Sigma\phi\rangle=\langle\Pi_{\Phi,\Sigma}u^{\prime},\Sigma\phi\rangle=\langle\Pi_{\Phi,\Sigma}u^{\prime},\phi\rangle,

where the first equality uses that
u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{\Sigma}
, the second equality uses that
\Sigma\phi\in\mathcal{S}_{\Phi,\Sigma}
, and the third equality uses that
\Pi_{\Phi,\Sigma}u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{\Phi,\Sigma}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{\Sigma}
. In a similar manner, we have that, for all
\phi\in\Phi
,
\langle v^{\prime},\phi\rangle=\langle\Pi_{\Phi,\Lambda}v^{\prime},\phi\rangle
. It then follows from
Lemma

4.6
that

\displaystyle F^{\mathsf{tl}}(\Phi;\beta u^{\prime},v^{\prime})=F^{\mathsf{tl}}(\Phi;\beta\cdot\Pi_{\Phi,\Sigma}u^{\prime},\Pi_{\Phi,\Lambda}v^{\prime}).

(30)

Under the event that
\|\Pi_{\Phi,\Sigma}u^{\prime}\|_{2}\geq 1/2
and
\|\Pi_{\Phi,\Lambda}v^{\prime}\|_{2}\geq 1/2
, we have by
Lemma

4.7
that

\displaystyle F^{\mathsf{tl}}(\Phi;\beta\Pi_{\Phi,\Sigma}u^{\prime},\Pi_{\Phi,\Lambda}v^{\prime})\geq\frac{1}{2}\cdot F^{\mathsf{tl}}(\Phi;\beta u^{\prime\prime},v^{\prime\prime}).

(31)

Combining
Eqs.

30
and
31

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{u^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)}\mathbb{E}_{v^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Lambda)}\left[F^{\mathsf{tl}}(\Phi;\beta u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\right]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{u^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)}\mathbb{E}_{v^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Lambda)}\left[\widetilde{F}^{\mathsf{tl}}(\Phi;\beta u^{\prime\prime},v^{\prime\prime})\right]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\cdot\frac{1}{4}\cdot\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{3}\right)^{2A}\cdot\left(\|\beta\Sigma\cdot(\phi_{1}-\mu^{\star}_{{\Phi,\phi_{1},\phi_{2}}})\|_{2}+\|\Lambda\cdot(\mu^{\star}_{{\Phi,\phi_{1},\phi_{2}}}-\phi_{2})\|_{2}-4\varepsilon\zeta\right),

where the second inequality uses the fact that
Eq.

29
holds with probability at least

together with
Lemma

4.9
with
\eta=1/2
,
u^{\prime}
set to
u^{\prime\prime}
, and
v^{\prime}
set to
v^{\prime\prime}
. Rearranging and using that

, we obtain that

\displaystyle\left(\frac{6}{\varepsilon}\right)^{2A}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{u^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)}\mathbb{E}_{v^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Lambda)}\left[\widetilde{F}^{\mathsf{tl}}(\Phi;\beta u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\right]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\|\beta\Sigma\cdot(\phi_{1}-\mu^{\star}_{{\Phi,\phi_{1},\phi_{2}}})\|_{2}+\|\Lambda\cdot(\mu^{\star}_{{\Phi,\phi_{1},\phi_{2}}}-\phi_{2})\|_{2}-4\varepsilon\zeta,

as desired. (In particular, we may take
C_{\ref{cor:optimal-perimeter}}=6
.)
∎

5
The algorithm

The algorithm.

Algorithm

1
presents our algorithm for learning linear Bellman complete MDPs. For some
T\in\mathbb{N}
, the algorithm proceeds for
T

rounds
. In each round
t\in[T]
, the algorithm computes two policies
\hat{\pi}_{h}^{(t)},\tilde{\pi}_{h}^{(t)}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}
at step
h
, in order of decreasing
h
. For each step
h
, trajectories are gathered from the uniform mixture over
\hat{\pi}^{(s)}\circ_{h}\tilde{\pi}^{(s)}\circ_{h+1}\hat{\pi}^{(t)}
, for
s\in[t-1]
(
4
). These trajectories are then used, together with a procedure to construct an optimism-based upper confidence bonus, to define a policy
\hat{\pi}_{h}^{(t)}
(
7
and
8
), which represents the algorithm’s current optimistic estimate of the optimal policy. Finally,
\tilde{\pi}_{h}^{(t)}
is defined to be a sort of uniform policy (
10
), with respect to the current covariance matrix of features
\Sigma_{h}^{(t)}
.

\lambda_{1},\beta
used in the definition of the bonuses

(in

below), the parameters

used in
Algorithm

1
, as well as several other parameters used in the analysis of this section:

, as well as parameters

, where

.

•

.

.

Definition of bonuses.

Next we define the exploration bonus function
F_{g}^{(t)}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
used in the construction of the rewards
\hat{r}_{h}^{(t,i,h)}
in
6
. The key component in
F_{g}^{(t)}
is a linear combination of truncated linear bonuses
F^{\mathsf{tl}}_{h}(x;u,v)
, over various values of
u,v
. The vectors
u,v
are chosen to belong to the subspaces
\mathcal{S}_{\Sigma},\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}
, for matrices
\Sigma,\Lambda
forming an orthogonal pair which are obtained from the covariance matrix
\Sigma_{h}^{(t)}
via a certain truncation procedure, defined below:

Definition 5.2
.

Let
\Gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be a PSD matrix, and suppose that
\Gamma
may be diagonalized as
\Gamma=UDU^{\top}
, for a diagonal matrix
D
and an orthogonal matrix
U
. Given
\sigma>0
, we define the
\sigma
-truncated pair of
\Gamma
, denoted
(\Sigma^{\prime},\Lambda^{\prime}):=\mathsf{trunc}_{\sigma}({\Gamma})
, as
\Sigma^{\prime}=UD_{+}U^{\top},\Lambda^{\prime}=UD_{-}U^{\top}
, where
D_{+},D_{-}
are diagonal matrices whose entries are given by:

\displaystyle(D_{+})_{ii}=\begin{cases}1&:D_{ii}\geq\sigma\\
0&:D_{ii}<\sigma\end{cases},\quad(D_{-})_{ii}=\begin{cases}0&:D_{ii}\geq\sigma\\
1&:D_{ii}<\sigma\end{cases}.

(32)

In words, for
(\Sigma^{\prime},\Lambda^{\prime})=\mathsf{trunc}_{\sigma}({\Gamma})
,
\Sigma^{\prime}
contains the large components of
\Gamma
(as parametrized by
\sigma
), and
\Lambda^{\prime}
contains the small components of
\Gamma
. Note that the definition of
\mathsf{trunc}_{\sigma}({\Gamma})
does not depend on the choice of diagonalization. Moreover, note that
(\Sigma^{\prime},\Lambda^{\prime})=\mathsf{trunc}_{\sigma}({\Gamma})
is an orthogonal pair.

The bonus function
F_{h}^{(t)}
used in
Algorithm

1
is defined as follows: for each
t\in[T]
,
h\in[H]
,

\displaystyle F_{h}^{(t)}(x):=

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}\cdot\left(\frac{C_{\ref{cor:optimal-perimeter}}}{\varepsilon_{\mathsf{apx}}}\right)^{2A}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{u^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma^{\prime})}\mathbb{E}_{v^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Lambda^{\prime})}\left[F^{\mathsf{tl}}_{h}(x;\beta u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\right]

\displaystyle+2\sqrt{2\pi}\lambda_{1}\xi\cdot\mathbb{E}_{w\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma^{\prime})}\left[\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\langle w,\phi_{h}(x,a)\rangle\right]

(33)

for

\displaystyle\quad\quad(\Sigma^{\prime},\Lambda^{\prime}):=\mathsf{trunc}_{\sigma_{\mathsf{tr}}}({(\beta/\lambda_{1})\cdot(\Sigma_{h}^{(t)})^{-1/2}}).

Q
- and
V
-function definitions.

For each
h\in[H],t\in[T]
, we make the following definitions:

\displaystyle Q_{h}^{(t)}(x,a):=

\displaystyle r_{h}(x,a)+\mathbb{E}^{\hat{\pi}^{(t)}}\left[\sum_{g=h+1}^{H}r_{g}(x_{g},a_{g})+F_{g}^{(t)}(x_{g})\ \mid\ (x_{h},a_{h})=(x,a)\right]

(34)

\displaystyle V_{h}^{(t)}(x):=

\displaystyle Q_{h}^{(t)}(x,\hat{\pi}_{h}^{(t)}(x))

(35)

\displaystyle\hat{Q}_{h}^{(t)}(x,a):=

\displaystyle\langle\hat{w}_{h}^{(t)},\phi_{h}(x,a)\rangle

\displaystyle\hat{V}_{h}^{(t)}(x):=

\displaystyle\hat{Q}_{h}^{(t)}(x,\hat{\pi}_{h}^{(t)}(x))=\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\hat{Q}_{h}^{(t)}(x,a).

The functions
Q_{h}^{(t)}(\cdot)
and
V_{h}^{(t)}(\cdot)
represent the
Q
- and
V
-value functions for the policy
\hat{\pi}^{(t)}
, with respect to the rewards
\hat{r}_{g}^{(t)}
defined in
Eq.

6
. In
Lemma

5.1
below, we show that
Q_{h}^{(t)}
is a linear function, i.e., for some
w_{h}^{(t)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
Q_{h}^{(t)}(x,a)=\langle\phi_{h}(x,a),w_{h}^{(t)}\rangle
for all
(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}
. The vector
\hat{w}_{h}^{(t)}
computed in
Algorithm

1
may be viewed as an empirical approximation to
w_{h}^{(t)}
, so that
\hat{Q}_{h}^{(t)}(\cdot)
and
\hat{V}_{h}^{(t)}(\cdot)
represent empirical approximations to
Q_{h}^{(t)},V_{h}^{(t)}
, respectively.

Lemma 5.1
.

For all
t\in[T],h\in[h]
, there is some
w_{h}^{(t)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
so that
Q_{h}^{(t)}(x,a)=\langle\phi_{h}(x,a),w_{h}^{(t)}\rangle
for all
(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}
.

Proof.

For all
x,a,h,t
, we have

\displaystyle Q_{h}^{(t)}(x,a)=r_{h}(x,a)+\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim\mathbb{P}_{h}(x,a)}\left[F_{h+1}^{(t)}(x^{\prime})+Q_{h+1}^{(t)}(x^{\prime},\hat{\pi}_{h+1}^{(t)}(x^{\prime})))\right].

For fixed
t\in[T]
, we will use reverse induction on
h
to establish the stated claim. The base case
h=H
follows since
Q_{H}^{(t)}(x,a)=r_{H}(x,a)=\langle w_{H}^{(t)},\phi_{H}(x,a)\rangle
for some
w_{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, by assumption.

Now, assume for the inductive hypothesis that for all
(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}
,
Q_{h+1}^{(t)}(x,a)=\langle w_{h+1}^{(t)},\phi_{h+1}(x,a)\rangle
for some
w_{h+1}^{(t)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then
Lemma

4.3
gives that there is some
\theta^{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
so that for all
(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}
,

\displaystyle\langle\theta^{1},\phi_{h}(x,a)\rangle=\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim\mathbb{P}_{h}(x,a)}\left[\langle\phi_{h+1}(x^{\prime},\hat{\pi}_{h+1}^{(t)}(x^{\prime})),w_{h+1}^{(t)}\rangle\right]=\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim\mathbb{P}_{h}(x,a)}\left[Q_{h+1}^{(t)}(x^{\prime},\hat{\pi}_{h+1}^{(t)}(x^{\prime}))\right].

Next, note that it is immediate from the definition
Eq.

33
that, with probability 1 over
u^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma^{\prime})
and
v^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Lambda^{\prime})
, the function
F^{\mathsf{tl}}_{h+1}(\cdot;u^{\prime},v^{\prime},\beta\Sigma^{\prime},\Lambda)
is Bellman-linear at step
h+1
. Note that the function
x\mapsto\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\langle w,\phi_{h+1}(x,a)\rangle
is also Bellman-linear at step
h+1
, for all
w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Since the sum of Bellman-linear functions is Bellman-linear, the definition of
F_{h+1}^{(t)}
in
Eq.

33
gives that
F_{h+1}^{(t)}
is Bellman-linear at step
h+1
. Thus, there is some
\theta^{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
so that

\displaystyle\langle\phi_{h}(x,a),\theta^{2}\rangle=\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim\mathbb{P}_{h}(x,a)}\left[F_{h+1}^{(t)}(x^{\prime})\right]

for all
(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}
.

Finally, by assumption, there is some
\theta^{3}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
so that
r_{h}(x,a)=\langle\theta^{3},\phi_{h}(x,a)\rangle
for all
(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}
. Hence, writing
w_{h}^{(t)}=\theta^{1}+\theta^{2}+\theta^{3}
, we have that
Q_{h}^{(t)}(x,a)=\langle w_{h}^{(t)},\phi_{h}(x,a)\rangle
for all
(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}
.
∎

5.1
Boundedness of the bonuses

Next, we prove some lemmas which establish bounds on the magnitude of the bonus functions
F_{h}^{(t)}(\cdot)
, and thereby on the magnitude of the optimistic
Q
-functions
Q_{h}^{(t)}(\cdot)
.

Lemma 5.2
.

For a PSD matrix
\Gamma
and
\sigma>0
, writing
(\Sigma^{\prime},\Lambda^{\prime}):=\mathsf{trunc}_{\sigma}({\Gamma})
, we have that
\Sigma^{\prime}\preceq\frac{1}{\sigma}\cdot\Gamma
.

Proof.

We write
\Gamma=UDU^{\top}
and
\Sigma^{\prime}=UD_{+}U^{\top}
, where
D_{+}
is as defined in
Eq.

32
. Certainly
0=(D_{+})_{ii}\leq D_{ii}
if
D_{ii}<\sigma
; otherwise, we have
1=(D_{+})_{ii}\leq\frac{1}{\sigma}\cdot D_{ii}
, which implies that
D_{+}\preceq\frac{D}{\sigma}
, as desired.
∎

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
\Phi\in\mathscr{P}^{d}
be a given polyhedron. Consider any PSD matrix
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. Then

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\cdot\max_{\phi,\phi^{\prime}\in\Phi}((\phi-\phi^{\prime})^{\top}\Sigma(\phi-\phi^{\prime}))^{1/2}\leq\mathbb{E}_{w\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)}\left[\max_{\phi\in\Phi}\langle w,\phi\rangle\right]\leq\sqrt{d}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{w\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)}\left[\phi_{w}^{\top}\Sigma\phi_{w}\right]^{1/2},

where
\phi_{w}:=\argmax_{\phi\in\Phi}\langle w,\phi\rangle
.

Proof.

Define
\Psi=\{\Sigma^{1/2}\cdot\phi\ :\ \phi\in\Phi\}
, and, for
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, write
\psi_{v}:=\argmax_{\psi\in\Psi}\langle v,\psi\rangle
. Then

\displaystyle\Sigma^{1/2}\cdot\phi_{\Sigma^{1/2}v}=\Sigma^{1/2}\cdot\argmax_{\phi\in\Phi}\langle v,\Sigma^{1/2}\phi\rangle=\argmax_{\psi\in\Psi}\langle v,\psi\rangle=\psi_{v}.

We then have the following equalities:

\displaystyle\max_{\phi,\phi^{\prime}\in\Phi}((\phi-\phi^{\prime})^{\top}\Sigma(\phi-\phi^{\prime}))^{1/2}=

\displaystyle\max_{\psi,\psi^{\prime}\in\Psi}\|\psi-\psi^{\prime}\|_{2}

(36)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{w\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)}\left[\phi_{w}^{\top}\Sigma\phi_{w}\right]^{1/2}=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{v\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})}\left[\phi_{\Sigma^{1/2}v}^{\top}\Sigma\phi_{\Sigma^{1/2}v}\right]^{1/2}=\mathbb{E}_{v\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})}\left[\|\psi_{v}\|_{2}^{2}\right]^{1/2}

(37)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{w\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)}\left[\max_{\phi\in\Phi}\langle w,\phi\rangle\right]=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{v\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})}\left[\max_{\phi\in\Phi}\langle\Sigma^{1/2}v,\phi\rangle\right]=\mathbb{E}_{v\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})}\left[\max_{\psi\in\Psi}\langle v,\psi\rangle\right].

(38)

It therefore suffices to show that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\cdot\max_{\psi,\psi^{\prime}\in\Psi}\|\psi-\psi^{\prime}\|_{2}\leq\mathbb{E}_{v\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})}\left[\max_{\psi\in\Psi}\langle v,\psi\rangle\right]\leq\sqrt{d}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{v\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})}[\|\psi_{v}\|_{2}^{2}]^{1/2}.

(39)

To show the first inequality in
Eq.

39
, pick
\psi_{0},\psi_{1}\in\Psi
maximizing
\|\psi_{0}-\psi_{1}\|_{2}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{v\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})}\left[\max_{\psi\in\Psi}\langle v,\psi\rangle\right]\geq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{v\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})}\left[\max\{\langle v,\psi_{0}\rangle,\langle v,\psi_{1}\rangle\}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{v\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})}\left[\langle v,(\psi_{0}+\psi_{1})/2\rangle+\max\{\langle v,(\psi_{0}-\psi_{1})/2\rangle,\langle v,(\psi_{1}-\psi_{0})/2\rangle\}\right]

\displaystyle=

6.2
Elliptical potential

The below lemma generalizes the elliptical potential lemma.

Lemma 6.4
.

Consider any sequence
\Gamma_{1},\ldots,\Gamma_{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
of PSD matrices, and suppose that
\Tr\Gamma_{t}\leq 1
for all
t\in[T]
. Define
\Sigma_{t}=\lambda I+\sum_{s=1}^{t}\Gamma_{s}
, for some
\lambda\geq 1
. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle\Gamma_{t},\Sigma_{t-1}^{-1}\rangle\leq 2d\log(2T).

Proof.

Write
\Sigma_{0}=\lambda I
. Since
\lambda\geq 1
, we have
\Sigma_{t-1}^{-1}\preceq I_{d}
and therefore
\langle\Gamma_{t},\Sigma_{t-1}^{-1}\rangle\leq\Tr\Gamma_{t}\leq 1
for all
t\in[T]
. Thus
\langle\Gamma_{t},\Sigma_{t-1}^{-1}\rangle\leq 2\log(1+\langle\Gamma_{t},\Sigma_{t-1}^{-1}\rangle)
. Moreover, for each
t\in[T]
,

\displaystyle\det(\Sigma_{t})=

\displaystyle\det(\Sigma_{t-1}+\Gamma_{t})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\det(\Sigma_{t-1})\cdot\det\left(I+\Sigma_{t-1}^{-1/2}\Gamma_{t}\Sigma_{t-1}^{-1/2}\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\det(\Sigma_{t-1})\cdot(1+\Tr(\Sigma_{t-1}^{-1/2}\Gamma_{t}\Sigma_{t-1}^{-1/2}))=\det(\Sigma_{t-1})\cdot(1+\langle\Gamma_{t},\Sigma_{t-1}^{-1}\rangle),

where the inequality uses that for any real numbers
\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{d}\geq 0
,
\prod_{i=1}^{d}(1+\sigma_{i})\geq 1+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sigma_{i}
, which implies that
\det(I+A)\geq 1+\Tr(A)
for any PSD matrix
A
(by taking
\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{d}
to be the eigenvalues of
A
). Telescoping the above display, we obtain that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle\Gamma_{t},\Sigma_{t-1}^{-1}\rangle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log(1+\langle\Gamma_{t},\Sigma_{t-1}^{-1}\rangle)\leq\log\left(\frac{\det(\Sigma_{T})}{\det(\lambda I)}\right)\leq\log\left(\frac{\left(\frac{1}{d}\Tr(\Sigma_{T})\right)^{d}}{\det(\lambda I)}\right)\leq d\log\left(\frac{\lambda d+T}{\lambda d}\right)\leq d\log(2T).

∎

A.2
Perturbed linear policies

We begin by defining a perturbed version of linear policies.

Definition A.1

(Perturbed linear policies)
.

For
\sigma>0
,
h\in[H]
and
w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, define
\pi_{h,w,\sigma}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\Delta(\mathcal{A})
by

\displaystyle\pi_{h,w,\sigma}(x)(a)=\PR_{\theta\sim\mathcal{N}(w,\sigma^{2}\cdot I_{d})}\left(a\in\mathcal{A}_{h,\theta}(x)\right).

In words, to draw an action
a\sim\pi_{h,w,\sigma}(x)
, we draw
\theta\sim\mathcal{N}(w,\sigma^{2}\cdot I_{d})
and then play
\argmax_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\langle\phi_{h}(x,a^{\prime}),\theta\rangle
. Given
\sigma>0
, we denote the set of all
\pi_{h,w,\sigma^{\prime}}
, where
w\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\sigma^{\prime}>0
satisfy
\sigma^{\prime}/\|w\|_{2}\geq\sigma
, by
\Pi^{\mathsf{Plin},{\sigma}}_{h}
, and
\Pi^{\mathsf{Plin},{\sigma}}:=\prod_{h=1}^{H}\Pi^{\mathsf{Plin},{\sigma}}_{h}
. As a matter of convention we write
\Pi^{\mathsf{Plin},{0}}_{h}:=\Pi^{\mathsf{lin}}_{h}
and
\Pi^{\mathsf{Plin},{0}}=\Pi^{\mathsf{lin}}
. Moreover, we write
\Pi^{\mathsf{Plin}}_{h}:=\bigcup_{\sigma\geq 0}\Pi^{\mathsf{Plin},{\sigma}}_{h}
and
\Pi^{\mathsf{Plin}}=\bigcup_{\sigma\geq 0}\Pi^{\mathsf{Plin},{\sigma}}
.

Note that, for any
c>0
,
\pi_{h,cw,\sigma}=\pi_{h,w,\sigma/{c}}
. Moreover, note that tie-breaking is not an issue for perturbed linear policies, since the measure of all
\theta
so that
|\mathcal{A}_{h,\theta}(x)|>1
is 0.
The following lemma provides another interpretation of linear policies, as a limit of perturbed linear policies:

Lemma A.3
.

For any
w\in\mathbb{R}^{d},h\in[H],x\in\mathcal{X}
, we have

\displaystyle\pi_{h,w}(a|x)=\lim_{\sigma\rightarrow 0^{+}}\pi_{h,w,\sigma}(a|x)=\lim_{\sigma\rightarrow 0^{+}}\PR_{\theta\sim\mathcal{N}(w,\sigma^{2}\cdot I_{d})}\left(a\in\mathcal{A}_{h,\theta}(x)\right).

(71)

Proof.

It is straightforward to see that for any
a\not\in\mathcal{A}_{h,w}(x)
,
\lim_{\sigma\rightarrow 0^{+}}\PR_{\theta\sim\mathcal{N}(w,\sigma^{2}I_{d})}(a\in\mathcal{A}_{h,\theta}(x))=0
. This verifies
Eq.

71
for
a\not\in\mathcal{A}_{h,w}(x)
. Moreover, it also implies that for each
a\in\mathcal{A}_{h,w}(x)
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\sigma\rightarrow 0^{+}}\left|\PR_{\theta\sim\mathcal{N}(w,\sigma^{2}I_{d})}(a\in\mathcal{A}_{h,\theta}(x))-\PR_{\theta\sim\mathcal{N}(w,\sigma^{2}I_{d})}\left(\langle\theta,\phi_{h}(x,a)\rangle>\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}_{h,w}(x)\backslash\{a\}}\langle\theta,\phi_{h}(x,a^{\prime})\rangle\right)\right|=0.

Since
\langle w,\phi_{h}(x,a)\rangle=\langle w,\phi_{h}(x,a^{\prime})\rangle
for all
a,a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}_{h,w}(x)
, we have that

\displaystyle\PR_{\theta\sim\mathcal{N}(w,\sigma^{2}I_{d})}\left(\langle\theta,\phi_{h}(x,a)\rangle>\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}_{h,w}(x)\backslash\{a\}}\langle\theta,\phi_{h}(x,a^{\prime})\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\PR_{\theta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d})}\left(\langle\theta,\phi_{h}(x,a)\rangle>\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}_{h,w}(x)\backslash\{a\}}\langle\theta,\phi_{h}(x,a^{\prime})\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\PR_{\theta\sim\nu_{d}}\left(\langle\theta,\phi_{h}(x,a)\rangle>\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}_{h,w}(x)\backslash\{a\}}\langle\theta,\phi_{h}(x,a^{\prime})\rangle\right)=\nu_{d}(\mathcal{G}_{h,w}(x,a)),

where the second equality follows by rescaling. Combining the two displays above gives the result.
∎

For a randomized policy
\pi
and
h\in[H]
, we use the shorthand

\displaystyle\phi_{h}(x,\pi_{h}(x)):=\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_{h}(x)}[\phi_{h}(x,a)].

Then
Lemma

A.3
yields that, for any

, we have

\displaystyle\phi_{h}(x,\pi_{h,w}(x))=\lim_{\sigma\rightarrow 0^{+}}\phi_{h}(x,\pi_{h,w,\sigma}(x)),\qquad f_{h}(x,\pi_{h,w}(x))=\lim_{\sigma\rightarrow 0^{+}}f(x,\pi_{h,w,\sigma}(x)).

(72)
