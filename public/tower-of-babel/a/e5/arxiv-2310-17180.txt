A Forward Reachability Perspective on Robust Control Invariance and Discount Factors in Reachability Analysis * The first two authors contributed equally to this work. Thanks: Submitted to SIAM Journal on Control and Optimization.
Jason J. Choi*, Donggun Lee*, Boyang Li, Jonathan P. How, Koushil Sreenath, Sylvia L. Herbert, Claire J. Tomlin

Abstract
Control invariant sets are crucial for various methods that aim to design safe control policies for systems whose state constraints must be satisfied over an indefinite time horizon. In this article, we explore the connections among reachability, control invariance, and Control Barrier Functions (CBFs). Unlike prior formulations based on backward reachability concepts, by examining a forward reachability problem, we are able to establish a strong link between these three concepts. First, our findings show that the inevitable Forward Reachable Tube (FRT), which is the set of states such that every trajectory reaching the FRT must have passed through a given initial set of states, is precisely this initial set of states itself if it is a robust control invariant set with a differentiable boundary. We highlight that this statement may not hold if the boundary is not differentiable. Next, we formulate a differential game between the control and disturbance, where the inevitable FRT is characterized by the zero-superlevel set of the value function. By incorporating a discount factor in the cost function of the game, the barrier constraint of the CBF naturally arises as the constraint that is imposed on the optimal control policy. Combining these results, the value function of our FRT formulation serves as a CBF-like function, and conversely, any valid CBF is also a forward reachability value function inside the control invariant set, thereby revealing the inverse optimality of the CBF. This strong link we establish between the reachability problem and the barrier constraint, while guaranteeing the continuity of the value function, is not achievable by previous backward reachability-based formulations. As such, our work fills a crucial gap in the existing literature that is vital for constructing valid CBFs to ensure safety.

Introduction

Safety guarantees are essential for control design in many applications. In this article, we focus on safety problems that can be represented by ensuring that system states satisfy specific constraints over an indefinite time horizon. An effective strategy for ensuring state trajectories stay within the desired constraint region involves identifying a subset where the trajectory can remain for an infinite duration. Sets exhibiting these properties are referred to as
control invariant sets

[
11
]
, and are key to various methods for designing safe control policies
[
37
]
. The theoretical analysis of control invariance offers valuable insights for the development of safe control policies.

A typical way of characterizing a control invariant set is by using a scalar function whose zero-superlevel set defines the invariant set, known as the barrier certificate
[
35
]
. This concept has evolved into the notion of a
control barrier function
(CBF)
[
5
]
, which mandates that the function satisfy a particular differential inequality condition. By enforcing this condition, control policies ensure not just the safety at the boundary of the set, but also a smooth deceleration of trajectories as they approach the boundary. We refer to this condition as the
barrier constraint
.

This paper delves into control invariance and the barrier constraint using a reachability approach, forging a strong link between reachability, control invariance, and CBFs. We will study systems with disturbance and robust control invariance, control invariance under the worst-case effect of the disturbance as described in
[
13
,
39
]
, and similarly the robust barrier constraint. Reachability analysis in fact forms the basis for many methods that construct robust control invariant sets using dynamic programming principles
[
10
,
29
,
18
,
2
,
39
,
14
]
. These methods compute the invariant sets by eliminating states that will inevitably reach unsafe regions, corresponding to
backward
reachability problems in which one is concerned with finding states that reach a set of terminal states. However, as we will present, these backward reachability methods only have weak linkage to the barrier constraint.

The key idea of our new formulation is to use the
forward
reachability concept, in which one is concerned with finding the states that a set of initial states reaches. Our core contribution is a discovery that the optimal policy of the forward reachability problem is determined by the barrier constraint, and that CBFs are forward reachability value functions.

To see this, first, we extend the notion of the minimal forward reachable tube (FRT)
[
28
]
to
inevitable
FRT, which encompasses states that are inevitably reached from the initial set, despite the worst-case disturbance. We then verify conditions under which the inevitable FRT remains identical to the initial set. We determine that the inevitable FRT remains unchanged when the initial set is 1) robustly control invariant, and 2) has a differentiable boundary. It is notable that if either these two conditions is not met, the FRT may differ from the initial set and not be robustly control invariant, whose examples are provided in this article.

Next, we introduce a differential game formulation that characterizes the inevitable FRT as the zero-superlevel set of the value function capturing the game between the control and disturbance. The value function is then determined as a viscosity solution
[
9
]
to a particular Hamilton-Jacobi (HJ) partial differential equation called the Hamilton-Jacobi forward reachable tube variational inequality (HJ-FRT-VI). The crux of this formulation is the incorporation of a discount factor in the cost function of the value function. As a result, first, the barrier constraint emerges in the HJ-FRT-VI, thus becoming the constraint that defines the optimal control policy of the FRT value function. Consequently, the value function acts as a CBF-like function in that it satisfies the barrier constraint almost everywhere. Conversely, any valid CBF also qualifies as a viscosity solution to the HJ-FRT-VI within the control invariant set and can therefore be interpreted as a forward reachability value function. Additionally, the discount factor induces a contraction mapping in the Bellman operator of the value function, allowing the value function to be continuous and characterized as a
unique
viscosity solution to the HJ-FRT-VI.

We highlight that prior formulations relying on backward reachability, introduced in
[
18
,
2
,
39
,
14
]
, were unable to establish a connection between reachability, control invariance, and CBFs. For instance, the work in
[
14
]
incorporates the barrier constraint into a finite-horizon backward reachability problem, but its extension to the infinite-horizon setting fails to secure the continuity and boundedness of the value function. In contrast, our formulation is the only method that not only secures the linkage to the barrier constraint but also ensures the boundedness and continuity of the value function and the solution uniqueness of the corresponding HJ-PDE (Table
1
). Consequently, by adopting a forward reachability approach to control invariant sets in a manner not previously explored in the literature, our work creates a strong link between these three concepts.

Additionally, we provide a geometric interpretation of robust control invariant sets, which is used in the subsequent analyses throughout the paper. In Section

Control Invariance and Control Barrier Functions

We consider a general nonlinear time-invariant system represented by an ODE

2.1
Control Invariance

Let
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the constraint set, i.e. the set that the system must remain within to maintain safety. The main challenge of finding a control signal
\mathrm{u}\in\mathcal{U}
such that for given
\mathrm{x}(0)\in X
,
\mathrm{x}(t)\in X
for all
t\geq 0
(i.e.
\mathrm{x}(\cdot)
remains safe) is that there may be some states in
X
from which exiting the set
X
is inevitable regardless of the choice of a control signal. An effective way of ruling out these failure states is to consider a subset of
X
that is control invariant.

Definition 1
((Forward) control invariant
[
11
]
).

A set
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
in the state space is (forward) control invariant under the dynamics (
1
) if for all
x\in S
, there exists a control signal
\mathrm{u}\in\mathcal{U}
such that
\mathrm{x}(t)\in S
for all
t\geq 0
. We also say that such
\mathrm{u}
renders the trajectory
\mathrm{x}
forward invariant in
S
.

By the above definition, a trajectory starting inside a control invariant set
S
that is a subset of
X
can remain within
S
for all time, and therefore can stay safe in
X
. The control invariance of a set can be determined by a geometric relationship between the vector field and the tangent cone of the set, as defined next:

Definition 2
((Bouligand’s) tangent cone
[
15
]

Given a closed set
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the tangent cone to

at

is defined as

T_{S}(x):=\left\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\liminf_{\tau\rightarrow 0}\frac{\operatorname{dist}(x+\tau z,S)}{\tau}=0\right\},

(2)

where
\operatorname{dist}(y,S):=\min_{z\in S}{\left\lVert y-z\right\rVert}
.

The tangent cone captures the feasible directions in which one can move from the point

within the set
S
.

Lemma 3
.

(Tangential characterization of closed control invariant sets
[
6

) Let the dynamics (

\exists u\in U~\textnormal{such that}~f(x,u)\in T_{S}(x).

(3)

Verifying the tangent cone condition everywhere along the boundary of a set is practically infeasible. A modification of the lemma can be made in a special case when the set
S
has a differentiable boundary (Assumption
2

h_{S}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

Assumption 2
.

2.2
Control Barrier Functions

Lemma
4
implies that a safe control input on the boundary of the set
S
can render the trajectory
\mathrm{x}
forward invariant in
S
. Control barrier functions (CBFs), first introduced in
[
5
]
, additionally impose conditions on the input when the trajectory is strictly inside the set before reaching the boundary, which enables the trajectories to “smoothly brake” as they approach the boundary.

Definition 8
(Control Barrier Function).

A function
h_{S}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
that satisfies Assumption
3
for a closed set
S
is a control barrier function (CBF) for the dynamics (
1
) if there exists an extended class
\mathcal{K}
function
\alpha
such that, for all
x\in S
,

\displaystyle\max_{u\in U}\frac{\partial h_{S}}{\partial x}(x)\cdot f(x,u)+\alpha\left(h_{S}(x)\right)\geq 0.

(8)

Here,
\alpha:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is an extended class
\mathcal{K}
function if it is continuous and strictly increasing and satisfies
\alpha(0)=0
. We say the barrier constraint is feasible at
x
if condition (
8
) holds for
x

This paper considers a particular class
\mathcal{K}
function
\alpha(y)=\gamma y
for a constant

[

,
33
]
,

\displaystyle\max_{u\in U}\frac{\partial h_{S}}{\partial x}(x)\cdot f(x,u)+\gamma h_{S}(x)\geq 0.

(9)

This is the most common choice of class
\mathcal{K}
function used in the CBF literature, and enables us to make a connection between CBFs and reachability value functions where
\gamma
will play the role of a discount factor in the reachability formulation.

Intuitively, (
9
) ensures that
h_{S}(\mathrm{x}(t))
does not decay faster than the exponentially decaying
curve
\dot{h}_{S}(\mathrm{x}(t))=-\gamma h_{S}(\mathrm{x}(t))
. This induces the “smooth braking” mechanism to any trajectory
\mathrm{x}
approaching the boundary of
S
. If (
9
) is satisfied, (
6
) is trivially satisfied at

where
h_{S}(x)=0
. Thus, according to Lemma
4
, the existence of the CBF

is a sufficient condition for

being control invariant.

Remark 9
.

If (
6
) in Lemma
4
holds with strict inequality for a compact and control invariant set
S
, any function

satisfying Assumption

is a CBF with large enough

[

2.3
Robust Control Invariance for Systems with Disturbance

There exist various formulations of robustness with respect to disturbances or uncertainties in system dynamics
[
11
,
22
,
23
,
25
]
. In this paper, we employ the differential game-based formulation that interprets the disturbance as an adversarial agent playing against the control input
[

]
, as commonly done in the Hamilton-Jacobi reachability-based safety analysis for systems with bounded disturbance
[
13
,
18
,
39
]

For this, we consider the system dynamics

\displaystyle\dot{\mathrm{x}}(t)=f(\mathrm{x}(t),\mathrm{u}(t),\mathrm{d}(t))\;\text{for}\;t>0,\qquad\mathrm{x}(0)=x,

(10)

where
\mathrm{d}:[0,\infty)\rightarrow D
is a Lebesgue measurable disturbance signal and

is a compact set. Note that control systems (
1
) can be regarded as a special case when the disturbance set

in (
10
) is set to a singleton (e.g.

). We use
\mathcal{D}
to denote the set of Lebesgue measurable disturbance signals. We assume conditions on the dynamics, similar to Assumption
1
:

Assumption 4
(on vector field of (
10
)).

1.

f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times U\times D\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}~\textnormal{is uniformly continuous,}

2.

f(\cdot,u,d)
is Lipschitz continuous in

for each
(u,d)\in U\times D
,

3.

\exists M>0
such that
\left\lVert f(x,u,d)\right\rVert\leq M

\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},(u,d)\in U\times D
,

so that under the above conditions, the solution to the ODE (

(\mathrm{u},\mathrm{d})\in\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{D}
and initial state

[

]
.

To ensure safety under the most adversarial disturbance, we assume that the disturbance can use the control signal’s current and previous information, whereas the control is not aware of the current disturbance input. To formulate this, we recall the definition of the

17
]

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Xi_{d}\coloneqq\{&\xi_{d}:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}~|~\forall s\in[0,\infty)\text{ and }\mathrm{u},\bar{\mathrm{u}}\in\mathcal{U},\\
&\text{if }\mathrm{u}(\tau)=\bar{\mathrm{u}}(\tau)\text{ a.e. }\tau\in[0,s],\;\text{then }\xi_{d}[\mathrm{u}](\tau)=\xi_{d}[\bar{\mathrm{u}}](\tau)\text{ a.e. }\tau\in[0,s]\}.\end{split}

(11)

Using the notion of non-anticipative strategies, we define the robust control invariant set under the dynamics (

Definition 10
(Robustly (forward) control invariant
[

,

]

A set

,

, for any

.

If

is an open set, the notion of

and

x\in S

\xi_{d}\in\Xi_{d}

such that

However, at the boundary of a closed set

Remark 14
.

Another common concept of the CBF that is robust to disturbance is the notion of input-to-state safe (ISSf) CBF
[

3
Forward Reachability of Control Invariant Sets

In this section, we apply forward reachability analysis to control invariant sets. In order to do so, we first provide background on the forward reachability of a set and introduce definitions of forward reachable tubes.

3.1
Forward Reachability

Forward reachability analyzes a set’s evolution in the future; its purpose is to identify the states that trajectories from an initial set
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
reach forward in time. The forward reachable tube (FRT) of a set, roughly speaking, encompasses states that are reached by trajectories that depart from the initial set. This concept is illustrated in Figure
1
.
For autonomous systems (e.g.
\dot{\mathrm{x}}(t)=f(\mathrm{x}(t))
), the forward evolution of a set is uniquely determined (Fig

(a)). However, for systems with control and/or disturbance inputs like (
1
) and (
10
), the trajectory, and thus the forward reachable tube, can be determined in various ways according to the choice of control and disturbance signals.

(a) For an autonomous system
\dot{\mathrm{x}}(t)=f(\mathrm{x}(t))
, the forward reachable tube (FRT) of the initial set
C
is the union of the forward trajectories starting from
C
, which is uniquely determined. (b) For control systems, the viable (maximal) FRT is the collection of all possible trajectories that depart from
C
. (c) On the other hand, the inevitable (minimal) FRT, the main focus of this study, is a collection of states such that every trajectory reaching it must have passed through
C
at some point in the past.

Consider the dynamics without disturbance, satisfying (
1
). At one extreme, the control can use its best effort to get further away from the original set
C
, and at the other extreme, the control works to stay as close to
C
as possible. The former would cause the FRT to expand maximally covering all the states such that from
C
, reaching them is viable (Fig
1
(b)), and the latter would induce the FRT to grow minimally, encapsulating only the states which inevitably must have evolved from
C
(Fig
1
(c)). From this intuition, we are able to define the viable (maximal) and inevitable (minimal) FRTs of a set
C
. Here, we only define the inevitable FRT which is the focus of this study, and readers are referred to
[
28
]
for the definition of the viable FRT.

Remark 15
.

Applications of forward reachability in the safe control and verification literature primarily focused on determining the viable FRT of the set of initial states, and checking whether this set intersects with the unsafe set
[
4
,
24
,
38
]
. In this context, the utility of inevitable FRTs would be limited, since the trajectories from the initial set could still enter the unsafe region even if the inevitable FRT does not
[
28
]
. In this paper, we revisit the utility of the inevitable FRT under the specific context in which the initial set is robustly control invariant.

To introduce the formal definition of the inevitable FRT, we use a separate notation for the solution of the ODE whose terminal state is specified as
x
(as opposed to initial states being specified as
x
in (
10
)):

\displaystyle\dot{\mathrm{x}}^{-}(t)=f(\mathrm{x}^{-}(t),\mathrm{u}^{-}(t),\xi_{d}^{-}[\mathrm{u}^{-}](t)),t<0,\quad\mathrm{x}^{-}(0)=x,

(14)

where
\mathrm{u}^{-}:(-\infty,0]\rightarrow U
is a measurable backward control signal,
\mathcal{U}^{-}
is a set of measurable backward control signals,
\mathcal{D}^{-}
denotes a set of measurable backward disturbance signals
\mathrm{d}^{-}:(-\infty,0]\rightarrow D
, and
\xi_{d}^{-}
is a non-anticipative strategy for the disturbance
backward
in time:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\xi_{d}^{-}\in\Xi_{d}^{-}\coloneqq\{&\xi_{d}^{-}:\mathcal{U}^{-}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}^{-}~|~\forall s\in(-\infty,0]\text{ and }\mathrm{u}^{-},\bar{\mathrm{u}}^{-}\in\mathcal{U}^{-},\\
&\text{if }\mathrm{u}^{-}(\tau)=\bar{\mathrm{u}}^{-}(\tau)\text{ a.e. }\tau\in[s,0],\\
&\text{then }\xi_{d}^{-}[\mathrm{u}^{-}](\tau)=\xi_{d}^{-}[\bar{\mathrm{u}}^{-}](\tau)\text{ a.e. }\tau\in[s,0]\}.\end{split}

The separate notation is necessary due to the causality of the disturbance strategy being reversed in time. We will call
\mathrm{x}^{-}
the
backward trajectory of (terminal state)

x
. Evaluating whether
\mathrm{x}^{-}
reaches the set
C
when time is considered to flow backward will tell us whether

belongs to the forward reachable tube of
C
.

Definition 16
(Inevitable FRT).

For a given initial set
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
which is an open set, we define the (infinite-horizon inevitable) FRT of
C
as the following set.

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{FRT}(C)\coloneqq\bigg\{&x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}~\Big|~\exists\xi_{d}^{-}\in\Xi_{d}^{-},T>0\text{ s.t.}\forall\mathrm{u}\in\mathcal{U}^{-},\\
&\exists t\in[-T,0]\text{ s.t. }\mathrm{x}^{-}(t)\in C,\text{ where }\mathrm{x}^{-}\text{ solves }\eqref{eq:backward_dynamics}.\bigg\}\end{split}

(15)

Note that
\mathrm{FRT}(\cdot)
can be also interpreted as a set mapping,
\text{FRT}:2^{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\rightarrow 2^{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
.

In plain words,
\mathrm{FRT}(C)
is a collection of states such that
every
trajectory reaching it forward in time must have passed through
C
at some point in the past. The FRT is shaped by the control aiming to restrain the growth of the FRT, whereas the disturbance is assumed to act adversarially and attempts to grow the FRT. For control systems, the inevitable FRT is minimal
[
28
]
since it excludes any state that can be reached by a trajectory that does not evolve from
C
. For systems with disturbance, the inevitable FRT is not necessarily minimal since the disturbance strategy attempts to grow the FRT.

3.2
Forward Reachable Tubes of Control Invariant Sets

We now investigate the relationship between the inevitable FRTs and control invariance. The main theorem of the section is as follows:

Theorem 17
.

Suppose

satisfies Assumption

For the proof, we have to reason about the backward flow of the dynamics, as in (

4.1.2
The effect of the discount factor

We now discuss the effect of introducing
\gamma
to the cost function. (
20
) defines a differential game problem with a discounted supremum-over-time cost function. Larger
\gamma
will recognize the value of
h_{S}
at the current time more than the value in the past. More importantly, the discount factor would also affect the resulting optimal control policy; whereas the optimal control always has to try its best to maintain the value of
h_{S}
when there is no discount, the non-zero discount factor alleviates this conservativeness and allows the optimal control to decay the value of
h_{S}
.

As such, the discount factor introduces the “game-of-degree” aspect to the reachability problem. In this game-of-degree, the parameter
\gamma
serves as a knob that adjusts how conservative the resulting optimal policy will be. However, the fundamental nature of the reachability problem, what is called the “game-of-kind”
[
13
,
7
]
—whether or not a state is inside the FRT—remains consistent, since (
22
) holds for any value of
\gamma
and the property can be determined by the positivity of
V_{\gamma}(x)
.

The idea of introducing the discount to (
21
), the supremum-over-time cost function, is similar to introducing a discount to infinite-horizon sum-over-time cost optimal control problems
[
9
]
. In fact, many favorable properties of the value function resulting from the discount like its Lipschitz continuity, and the contraction of the corresponding Bellman backup operator hold similarly.

Proposition 25
(Lipschitz Continuity).

Suppose
f
satisfies Assumption

is Lipschitz continuous.
V_{\gamma}
is Lipschitz continuous in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
if
L_{f}<\gamma
, where

Proof.

For
x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and

, there exists

such that

\displaystyle V_{\gamma}(x_{1})\leq\inf_{\mathrm{u}^{-}\in\mathcal{U}^{-}}J_{\gamma}(x_{1},\mathrm{u}^{-},\hat{\xi}_{d}^{-})+\varepsilon,

where

is defined in (
21
).
Hence,

\displaystyle V_{\gamma}(x_{1})\leq J_{\gamma}(x_{1},\mathrm{u}^{-},\hat{\xi}_{d}^{-})+\varepsilon

for any
\mathrm{u}^{-}\in\mathcal{U}^{-}
. For
x_{2}
, there exists
\hat{\mathrm{u}}^{-}\in\mathcal{U}^{-}
such that

\displaystyle V_{\gamma}(x_{2})\geq\inf_{\mathrm{u}^{-}\in\mathcal{U}^{-}}J_{\gamma}(x_{2},\mathrm{u}^{-},\hat{\xi}_{d}^{-})\geq J_{\gamma}(x_{2},\hat{\mathrm{u}}^{-},\hat{\xi}_{d}^{-})-\varepsilon.

By combining the above two inequalities, we have

\displaystyle V_{\gamma}(x_{1})-V_{\gamma}(x_{2})

\displaystyle\leq J_{\gamma}(x_{1},\hat{\mathrm{u}}^{-},\hat{\xi}_{d}^{-})-J_{\gamma}(x_{2},\hat{\mathrm{u}}^{-},\hat{\xi}_{d}^{-})+2\varepsilon

\displaystyle=\sup_{t\in(-\infty,0]}e^{\gamma t}h_{S}(\mathrm{x}_{1}^{-}(t))-\sup_{t\in(-\infty,0]}e^{\gamma t}h_{S}(\mathrm{x}_{2}^{-}(t))+2\varepsilon,

where
\mathrm{x}_{1}^{-}
solves (
14
) for
(\hat{\mathrm{u}}^{-},\hat{\xi}_{d}^{-})
with the terminal state
x_{1}
, and
\mathrm{x}_{2}^{-}
solves (
14
) for
(\hat{\mathrm{u}}^{-},\hat{\xi}_{d}^{-})
with the terminal state
x_{2}
.
Since there exists
\hat{t}\in(-\infty,0]
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in(-\infty,0]}e^{\gamma t}h_{S}(\mathrm{x}_{1}^{-}(t))\leq e^{\gamma\hat{t}}h_{S}(\mathrm{x}_{1}^{-}(\hat{t}))+\varepsilon,

this implies

\displaystyle V_{\gamma}(x_{1})-V_{\gamma}(x_{2})

\displaystyle\leq e^{\gamma\hat{t}}h_{S}(\mathrm{x}_{1}^{-}(\hat{t}))-e^{\gamma\hat{t}}h_{S}(\mathrm{x}_{2}^{-}(\hat{t}))+3\varepsilon

\displaystyle\leq L_{h_{S}}e^{\gamma\hat{t}}e^{-L_{f}\hat{t}}\lVert x_{1}-x_{2}\rVert+3\varepsilon\leq L_{h_{S}}\lVert x_{1}-x_{2}\rVert+3\varepsilon,

where
L_{h_{S}}
is the Lipschitz constant of
h_{S}
. The second inequality is a result of Gronwall’s inequality, and the third is a result of the condition,
L_{f}<\gamma
. Using a similar argument, we can show
V_{\gamma}(x_{2})-V_{\gamma}(x_{1})\leq L_{h_{S}}\lVert x_{1}-x_{2}\rVert+3\varepsilon
, thus
|V_{\gamma}(x_{1})-V_{\gamma}(x_{2})|\leq L_{h_{S}}\lVert x_{1}-x_{2}\rVert+3\varepsilon
. Since the previous inequality holds for all
\varepsilon>0
,
|V_{\gamma}(x_{1})-V_{\gamma}(x_{2})|\leq L_{h_{S}}\lVert x_{1}-x_{2}\rVert
.

The condition
L_{f}<\gamma
implies that the discount factor has to be large enough to suppress the effect of the vector field in prohibiting continuity. Under this condition, since the value function is Lipschitz continuous, it is differentiable almost everywhere by Rademacher’s Theorem. Other infinite-horizon value functions in backward reachability formulations
[
18
,
14
]
do not have Lipschitz continuity and can even be discontinuous, which prohibits the usage of a differential inequality-based condition like the barrier constraint for deriving safe control policies from the value function.

The contraction property of the Bellman backup for the FRT value function
V_{\gamma}
will be discussed next after introducing the dynamic programming principle and the HJ PDE characterization of the value function.

4.2
Hamilton-Jacobi Characterization of the Value Function

This section provides a computational machinery for the computation of the value function
V_{\gamma}
using Hamilton-Jacobi analysis.

4.2.1
Dynamic programming for
V_{\gamma}

First, we apply Bellman’s principle of optimality to (
20
), which results in the following theorem.

Theorem 26
(Dynamic Programming principle).

Suppose
\gamma>0
. For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\displaystyle V_{\gamma}(x)=\sup_{\xi_{d}^{-}\in\Xi_{d}^{-}}\inf_{\mathrm{u}^{-}\in\mathcal{U}^{-}}\max\Big\{

\displaystyle\max_{t\in[-T,0]}e^{\gamma t}h_{S}\left(\mathrm{x}^{-}(t)\right),e^{-\gamma T}V_{\gamma}\left(\mathrm{x}^{-}(-T)\right)\Big\}

(30)

for any
T>0
, where
\mathrm{x}^{-}
solves (
14
).

Proof.

Using the one-to-one mappings
\rho_{\mathrm{u}}
and
\rho_{\xi_{d}}
in (
23
) and (
24
), the value function
V_{\gamma}
in (
20
) can be written as

\displaystyle V_{\gamma}(x)=\sup_{\xi_{d}\in\Xi_{d}}\inf_{\mathrm{u}\in\mathcal{U}}\sup_{t\in[0,\infty)}e^{-\gamma t}h_{S}(\mathrm{x}(t)),

(31)

where
\mathrm{x}
solves (
27
).

[
39
, Lemma 3]
proves that

\displaystyle V_{\gamma}(x)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\xi_{d}\in\Xi_{d}}\inf_{\mathrm{u}\in\mathcal{U}}\max\{\max_{t\in[0,T]}e^{-\gamma t}h_{S}(\mathrm{x}(t)),e^{-\gamma T}V_{\gamma}(\mathrm{x}(T))\}.

(32)

Combining (
32
) with (
23
) and (
24
), we conclude (
30
).

Building on Theorem
26
, Theorem
27
presents the Hamilton-Jacobi variational inequality for
V_{\gamma}
. See
[
9
]
for the definition of the viscosity solution in the theorem.

Theorem 27
(Forward Reachable Tube Hamilton-Jacobi
Variational Inequality).

Suppose
h_{S}
is a bounded and Lipschitz continuous function, and
\gamma>0
.
V_{\gamma}
in (
20
) is a unique viscosity solution in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
of the following Hamilton-Jacobi PDE, called forward reachable tube Hamilton-Jacobi variational inequality (FRT-HJ-VI):

\displaystyle 0=\min\Big\{\ V_{\gamma}(x)-h_{S}(x),\max_{u}\min_{d}\frac{\partial V_{\gamma}}{\partial x}\cdot f(x,u,d)+\gamma V_{\gamma}(x)\Big\}.

(33)

Proof.

Similarly to the proof of Theorem
26
, from (
31
),

[
39
, Lemma 3]
proves that
V_{\gamma}
is the unique viscosity solution to

\displaystyle 0=\min\{V_{\gamma}(x)-h_{S}(x),-\min_{u\in U}\max_{d\in D}\frac{\partial V_{\gamma}}{\partial x}\cdot(-f(x,u,d))+\gamma V_{\gamma}(x)\}

in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
This is equivalent to satisfying the following two conditions.
First, for any smooth function
v\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that
V_{\gamma}-v
has a local minimum at
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
V_{\gamma}(x_{0})=v(x_{0})
,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\min\{v(x)-h_{S}(x),-\min_{u\in U}\max_{d\in D}\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}\cdot(-f(x,u,d))+\gamma v(x)\}

\displaystyle=\min\{v(x)-h_{S}(x),\max_{u\in U}\min_{d\in D}\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}\cdot f(x,u,d)+\gamma v(x)\}.

Second, for any smooth function
v\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that
V_{\gamma}-v
has a local maximum at
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
V(x_{0})=v(x_{0})
,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\geq\min\{v(x)-h_{S}(x),-\min_{u\in U}\max_{d\in D}\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}\cdot(-f(x,u,d))+\gamma v(x)\}

\displaystyle=\min\{v(x)-h_{S}(x),\max_{u\in U}\min_{d\in D}\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}\cdot f(x,u,d)+\gamma v(x)\}.

For non-positive values of
\gamma
,
V_{\gamma}
might be unbounded and the FRT-HJ-VI might have multiple solutions. In contrast, a strictly positive value of
\gamma
guarantees the boundedness and the uniqueness of the solution of the FRT-HJ-VI. (An example in Appendix illustrates these outcomes.) In fact, the uniqueness property follows from the contraction property of the Bellman backup associated with the dynamic programming principle of
V_{\gamma}
in (
30
).

To see this, we define a Bellman backup operator
B_{T}:\text{BUC}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\rightarrow\text{BUC}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for
T>0
, where
\text{BUC}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
represents a set of bounded and uniformly continuous functions:
\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, as

\displaystyle B_{T}[V](x)\coloneqq\sup_{\xi_{d}^{-}\in\Xi_{d}^{-}}\inf_{\mathrm{u}^{-}\in\mathcal{U}^{-}}\max\Big\{

\displaystyle\max_{t\in[-T,0]}e^{\gamma t}h_{S}(\mathrm{x}^{-}(t)),e^{-\gamma T}V(\mathrm{x}^{-}(-T))\Big\}.

(34)

Then, the following holds.

Theorem 28
(Contraction mapping).

For
V^{1},V^{2}\in\text{BUC}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,

\displaystyle\lVert B_{T}[V^{1}]-B_{T}[V^{2}]\rVert_{\infty}\leq e^{-\gamma T}\lVert V^{1}-V^{2}\rVert_{\infty},

(35)

and the FRT value function
V_{\gamma}
in (
20
) is the unique fixed-point solution to
V_{\gamma}\!=\!B_{T}[V_{\gamma}]
for each
T\!>\!0
.
Also, for any
V\!\in\!\textnormal{BUC}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,

\displaystyle\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}B_{T}[V]=V_{\gamma},

(36)

Proof.

Define

\displaystyle l(\xi_{d},\mathrm{u},x)\coloneqq\max_{t\in[-T,0]}e^{\gamma t}h_{S}(\mathrm{x}(t)),

\displaystyle l^{i}(\xi_{d},\mathrm{u},x)\coloneqq e^{-\gamma T}V^{i}(\mathrm{x}(-T)),

for
i=1,2
, then

\displaystyle B_{T}[V^{i}]=\sup_{\xi_{d}\in\Xi_{d}}\inf_{\mathrm{u}\in\mathcal{U}}\max\{l(\xi_{d},\mathrm{u}),l^{i}(\xi_{d},\mathrm{u})\}.

Without loss of generality, let
B_{T}[V^{1}](x)\geq B_{T}[V^{2}](x)
. For any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\bar{\xi}_{d}
such that
B_{T}[V^{1}]-\varepsilon<\inf_{\mathrm{u}}\max\{l(\bar{\xi}_{d},\mathrm{u}),l^{1}(\bar{\xi}_{d},\mathrm{u})\}
, and there exists
\bar{\mathrm{u}}
such that
\inf_{\mathrm{u}}\max\{l(\bar{\xi}_{d},\mathrm{u}),l^{2}(\bar{\xi}_{d},\mathrm{u})\}+\varepsilon>\max\{l(\bar{\xi}_{d},\bar{\mathrm{u}}),l^{2}(\bar{\xi}_{d},\bar{\mathrm{u}})\}
.
Then,

\displaystyle B_{T}[V^{1}](x)-B_{T}[V^{2}](x)

\displaystyle<2\varepsilon+\max\{l(\bar{\xi}_{d},\bar{\mathrm{u}}),l^{1}(\bar{\xi}_{d},\bar{\mathrm{u}})\}-\max\{l(\bar{\xi}_{d},\bar{\mathrm{u}}),l^{2}(\bar{\xi}_{d},\bar{\mathrm{u}})\}

\displaystyle\leq 2\varepsilon+\lvert l^{1}(\bar{\xi}_{d},\bar{\mathrm{u}})-l^{2}(\bar{\xi}_{d},\bar{\mathrm{u}})\rvert

\displaystyle\leq 2\varepsilon+e^{-\gamma T}\max_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\lvert V^{1}(x)-V^{2}(x)\rvert

The second inequality holds since, for all
a,b,c\in\mathbb{R}
,
|\max\{a,b\}-\max\{a,c\}|\leq|b-c|
. Since the above inequality holds for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\varepsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\lVert B_{T}[V^{1}]-B_{T}[V^{2}]\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq e^{-\gamma T}\lVert V^{1}-V^{2}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

Since
V_{\gamma}
is a fixed-point solution for all
T>0
, the Banach’s contraction mapping theorem
[
16
, Chapter 9.2]
implies that
V_{\gamma}
is the unique fixed-point solution to
B_{T}[V_{\gamma}](x)=V_{\gamma}(x)
for all
T>0
.
In addition, we have

\displaystyle\lVert B_{T}[V]-V_{\gamma}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq e^{-\gamma T}\lVert V-V_{\gamma}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

for all
V\!\in\!\textnormal{BUC}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, thus we conclude (
36
).

4.2.2
Computation of
V_{\gamma}

Theorem
28
provides us various methods to compute the FRT value function
V_{\gamma}
using the operation
B_{T}[\cdot]
, which do not require any assumptions for the initial guess of the value function, besides the boundedness and uniform continuity in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For instance, the following lemma presents a finite-horizon HJ equation for this computation.

Lemma 29
(Finite horizon HJ-PDE for the computation of
V_{\gamma}
).

For a given initial value function candidate
V^{0}\in\textnormal{BUC}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, let
W:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be the unique viscosity solution to the following initial-value HJ-PDE

\displaystyle W(0,x)=\max\{h_{S}(x),V^{0}(x)\},\text{ for }x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

(37)

\displaystyle 0=\min\bigg\{W(t,x)-h_{S}(x),\frac{\partial W}{\partial t}+\max_{u}\min_{d}\frac{\partial W}{\partial x}\cdot f(x,u,d)+\gamma W(t,x)\bigg\}

(38)

for
(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then,
W(T,x)\equiv B_{T}[V^{0}](x)
.

Proof.

We will derive the HJ equation for another value function
W^{+}
defined below, and then replace
W^{+}
by
W
.
Define
W^{+}:[-T,0]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle W^{+}(t,x)=\inf_{\xi_{d}\in\Xi_{d}}\sup_{\mathrm{u}\in\mathcal{U}}\min\bigg\{\min_{s\in[t,0]}e^{-\gamma(s-t)}(-h_{S}(\mathrm{x}(s))),e^{\gamma t}(-V_{0}(\mathrm{x}(0)))\bigg\},

(39)

where
\mathrm{x}
solves (
27
).
Then,
W(T,x)=-W^{+}(-T,x)
, and

\displaystyle\begin{split}&W(t,x)=-W^{+}(-t,x),\frac{\partial W}{\partial t}(t,x)=\frac{\partial W^{+}}{\partial t}(-t,x),\\
&\frac{\partial W}{\partial x}(t,x)=-\frac{\partial W^{+}}{\partial x}(-t,x),\forall(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{n}.\end{split}

(40)

In the rest of the proof, we will utilize the technique presented in
[
14
]
. The value function in
[
14
]
is

\displaystyle\begin{split}\inf_{\xi_{d}\in\xi_{d}}\sup_{\mathrm{u}}\min_{s\in[t,0]}e^{-\gamma(s-t)}(-h_{S}(\mathrm{x}(s))),\end{split}

which is the exactly same as (
39
) except the second term of the minimization operation:
e^{\gamma t}(-V_{0}((\mathrm{x}(0)))
. This term affects the terminal condition of
W^{+}
but not the dynamic programming principle.
Thus,
W^{+}
and the value function in
[
14
]
solves the same dynamic programming principle, but their terminal conditions are different. Note that we assume
\gamma>0
, but
[
14
]
assumes
\gamma\leq 0
. However, the sign of
\gamma
does not affect any arguments in
[
14
]
’s lemmas, theorems.

Using similar arguments as in the proof of
[
14
, Theorem 2]
, we prove

\displaystyle W^{+}(t,x)=\inf_{\xi_{d}\in\Xi_{d}}\sup_{\mathrm{u}\in\mathcal{U}}\min\bigg\{\min_{s\in[t,t+\delta]}e^{-\gamma(s-t)}(-h_{S}(\mathrm{x}(s))),e^{-\gamma\delta}W^{+}(t+\delta,\mathrm{x}(t+\delta))\bigg\}.

Then,
[
14
, Theorem 3]
implies that
W^{+}
is the unique viscosity solution to the terminal-value HJ equation:

\displaystyle W^{+}(0,x)=-\max\{h_{S}(x),V_{0}(x)\}\quad\text{on }\{t=0\}\times\mathbb{R}^{n},

\displaystyle 0=\min\Big\{-\!h_{S}(x)\!-\!W^{+}(t,x),\frac{\partial W^{+}}{\partial t}+\max_{u}\min_{d}\frac{\partial W^{+}}{\partial x}\cdot(-f)(x,u,d)-\gamma W^{+}(t,x)\Big\},

in
(-T,0)\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
. By applying (
40
), we get the conclusion that
W
is the unique viscosity solution to (
38
).

In Lemma
29
, any
V^{0}\in\textnormal{BUC}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
works for the computation of
V_{\gamma}
; for instance, a straightforward choice of
V^{0}
can be
h_{S}
. As
T\rightarrow\infty
,
\frac{\partial W}{\partial t}
vanishes to 0 for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Combining Theorem
28
and Lemma
29
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}B_{T}[V^{0}]=\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}W(T,x)=V_{\gamma}(x).

(41)

The PDE (
38
) can be numerically solved forward in time from the initial condition (
37
), by using well-established time-dependent level-set methods
[
30
]
.

Theorem
28
also enables other numerical schemes that are based on time discretization, like value iteration, to produce an accurate solution of
V_{\gamma}
. The following corollary of Theorem
28
provides the guarantee that the value iteration with any initial guess of

will converge to
V_{\gamma}
with a Q-linear convergence rate specified by (
42
). For a given time step size
\Delta t
, the semi-Lagrangian approximation can be applied to the exact Bellman backup operator in (

) for its numerical approximation, and the resulting value function will converge to

when
\Delta t\rightarrow 0

[
2
]
.

Corollary 30
(Value Iteration).

For any
V^{0}\in\text{BUC}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and a time step
\Delta t>0
, define the sequence
\{V^{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
by an iteration
V^{k}:=B_{\Delta t}[V^{k-1}]
for

. Then,

\frac{\lVert V^{k+1}-V_{\gamma}\rVert_{\infty}}{\lVert V^{k}-V_{\gamma}\rVert_{\infty}}=e^{-\gamma\Delta t}<1,

and thus,
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}V^{k}=V_{\gamma}
.

Proof.

This is a direct outcome of Theorem
28
.

4.3
FRT Value Functions for Control Invariant Sets

We now revisit the forward reachability for control invariant sets, extending the analysis in Section

, and draw a connection between the FRT value function

and robust CBFs.

First, when
S
has a differentiable boundary and is robustly control invariant where Theorem

holds, the following holds:

Proposition 31
(FRT value function characterization of robust control invariant sets).

Under Assumptions
2
,
3
, and
4
,
S
is robustly control invariant if and only if

\displaystyle\mathrm{FRT}(\mathrm{Int}(S))=\mathrm{Int}(S)\;

\displaystyle=\{x~|~V_{\gamma}(x)>0\},

\displaystyle\mathrm{Int}(S)^{c}

\displaystyle=\{x~|~V_{\gamma}(x)=0\},

where

is defined as (

Proof.

The proposition holds from Theorem

and Lemma

.

When Proposition

is characterized as a strict zero-superlevel of

to maintain forward invariance of trajectories within

from the FRT-HJ-VI (

Proposition 32
(Optimal policy of
V_{\gamma}
).

Under the assumptions in Theorem
27
, we define the set-valued map policy
K_{\gamma}:S\rightarrow 2^{U}
as

K_{\gamma}(x):=\left\{u\in U:\min_{d\in D}\frac{\partial V_{\gamma}}{\partial x}\cdot f(x,u,d)+\gamma V_{\gamma}(x)\geq 0\right\},

(43)

where
V_{\gamma}
is defined as (
20
). Then,
K_{\gamma}(x)
is non-empty for every
x\in\mathrm{Int}(S)
where
\frac{\partial V_{\gamma}}{\partial x}
exists. In addition, if
V_{\gamma}
is differentiable, any element of
K_{\gamma}(x)
is an optimal control input with respect to
V_{\gamma}
in (
20
), and under Assumptions
2
,
3
, and
4
, if
S
is robustly control invariant, the trajectory under
K_{\gamma}(x)
remains forward invariant in
S
under the worst-case disturbance.

Proof.

(i) At
x\in\mathrm{Int}(S)
where
V_{\gamma}
is differentiable, the FRT-HJ-VI (
33
) implies that
K_{\gamma}
is non-empty.

(ii) For any control policy
\pi=\pi(x)\in K_{\gamma}(x)
, where
V_{\gamma}
is differentiable, consider the following equation for
V^{\pi}_{\gamma}
:

\displaystyle 0\!=\!\min\Big\{\!\ V^{\pi}_{\gamma}(x)-h_{S}(x),\min_{d}\frac{\partial V_{\gamma}}{\partial x}\cdot f(x,\pi(x),d)+\gamma V^{\pi}_{\gamma}(x)\Big\}.

(44)

For each
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\min\{y-h_{S}(x),\min_{d}\frac{\partial V_{\gamma}}{\partial x}\cdot f(x,\pi(x),d)+\gamma y\}
is monotonically increasing in

, so the equation (

Also, from the FRT-HJ-VI (
33
),

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\min\Big\{\ V_{\gamma}(x)-h_{S}(x),\max_{u}\min_{d}\frac{\partial V_{\gamma}}{\partial x}\cdot f(x,u,d)+\gamma V_{\gamma}(x)\Big\},

\displaystyle\geq\min\Big\{\ V_{\gamma}(x)-h_{S}(x),\min_{d}\frac{\partial V_{\gamma}}{\partial x}\cdot f(x,\pi(x),d)+\gamma V_{\gamma}(x)\Big\}\geq 0.

(45)

The last inequality holds since
V_{\gamma}-h_{S}\geq 0
by the FRT-HJ-VI (

) and
\min_{d}\frac{\partial V_{\gamma}}{\partial x}\cdot f(x,\pi(x),d)+\gamma V_{\gamma}(x)\geq 0

. The equation (

) and the uniqueness of (

for any
\pi
. By replacing
V_{\gamma}
by
V^{\pi}_{\gamma}

\displaystyle 0=\min\Big\{\ V^{\pi}_{\gamma}(x)-h_{S}(x),\min_{d}\frac{\partial V^{\pi}_{\gamma}}{\partial x}\cdot f(x,\pi(x),d)+\gamma V^{\pi}_{\gamma}(x)\Big\}.

The solution to the above PDE can be considered as the value function (
21
) under
\pi(x)
and worst-case disturbance, and since
V_{\gamma}\equiv V^{\pi}_{\gamma}
, we conclude that any control
u\in K_{\gamma}(x)
is an optimal control for the zero-sum game value
V_{\gamma}
in (

).

Note that the non-emptiness of
K_{\gamma}(x)
is derived from
V_{\gamma}
satisfying the FRT-HJ-VI (

). By noting that the second term of the minimum in (

) to hold, we get that

\max_{u\in U}\min_{d\in D}\frac{\partial V_{\gamma}}{\partial x}\cdot f(x,u,d)+\gamma V_{\gamma}(x)\geq 0

where

is differentiable. This corresponds to the barrier constraint in (

\alpha(y)=\gamma y
.

Since the value function is Lipschitz continuous and differentiable almost everywhere by Proposition

V_{\gamma}
satisfies (

\mathrm{Int}(S)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Note that since
V_{\gamma}
is 0 everywhere outside
\mathrm{Int}(S)
. If
V_{\gamma}
is differentiable in
\mathrm{Int}(S)

\mathrm{Int}(S)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, which constitutes the definition of the robust CBF in Definition

:

Corollary 33
.

If
V_{\gamma}
is continuously differentiable in
\mathrm{Int}(S)
,
V_{\gamma}:S\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a robust CBF.

Proof.

Proposition

implies that the barrier constraint holds at any state in the interior of

. As we constrain the domain of

to

, the gradient of
V_{\gamma}
at any state on the boundary,

is continuously differentiable, this limit exists and

in (

.

More importantly, any valid robust CBF
h
itself is the FRT value function in
\mathrm{Int}(S)
:

Theorem 34
(Inverse optimality of CBFs).

Under Assumptions
2
and
4

h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a differentiable function that satisfies Assumption
3
and is a robust CBF for a closed set
S
, satisfying

\max_{u\in U}\min_{d\in D}\frac{\partial h}{\partial x}\cdot f(x,u,d)+\gamma h(x)\geq 0,

(47)

for all
x\in S
and some
\gamma>0
.
Then,

V_{\gamma}(x)=\max\{0,h(x)\}

(48)

is the unique viscosity solution of the FRT-HJ-VI (
33
) with
h_{S}(x)=h(x)
.

Proof.

We will show the two statements as follows.

1.

For any smooth function
v\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that
V_{\gamma}-v
has a local minimum at
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and

5
Discussion

Although inspired by existing works that establish the connection between reachability and robust control invariance, our work is the first paper that connects
forward
reachability to the analysis of robust control invariance. Existing works have focused on backward reachability-based formulations that produce the largest robust control invariant set contained in a given desired safety region called the viability kernel. This is done by finding the inevitable (or minimal) backward reachable tube (BRT)
[
28
]
of the unsafe region, which becomes the complement of the viability kernel. Various value functions have been proposed to characterize the viability kernels by computing the BRTs, both for finite time
[
14
,
27
,
29
,
19
]
and infinite time
[
18
,
39
,
2
,
36
]
but none of these functions are CBFs, as indicated in Table
1
.

The time-varying value functions that characterize finite-horizon BRTs are proposed in
[
27
,
29
,
19
]
, where the discount factor is not necessary for the boundedness and continuity of the value function, and the solution uniqueness of the HJ-PDE. The work in
[
18
]
has extended these formulations to the infinite-horizon setting (Table
1
, first row). However, the value function can be discontinuous and the corresponding HJ-PDE admits non-unique solutions
[
21
]
. The work in
[
2
,
39
]
proposes formulations that resolve these issues for the infinite-horizon setting through the introduction of the discount factor. However, the differential inequality condition on the value function that emerges from the corresponding HJ-PDE differs from the barrier constraint (Table
1
, second row). Moreover, the value function flattens to zero in the interior of the computed control invariant set, thus, there is no non-zero gradient of the value function inside the control invariant set that can be useful for the synthesis of safety control. The authors in
[
40
]
extend this formulation to a new notion called Guidance-barrier functions. On the other hand, the work in
[
14
]
presents a formulation for the finite-horizon BRT wherein the differential inequality derived from the corresponding HJ-PDE matches the barrier constraint in the CBF definition. However, its extension to infinite horizon might lead to an unbounded and discontinuous value function and non-unique solutions to the corresponding HJ-PDE (Table
1
, third row).

Our formulation of forward reachability ensures compliance with the barrier constraint in the CBF definition. Additionally, the value function is both continuous and bounded in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, while the corresponding HJ-PDE has a unique solution. The central idea behind our approach is the usage of the discount factor backward in time, as
e^{\gamma t}
where
t<0
, in the definition of the discounted FRT value function (
20
). In contrast to the discount in BRT formulations leading to the emergence of
-\gamma V_{\gamma}(x)
in the corresponding HJ-PDEs (Table
1
, second row), the usage of discount in this way leads to the emergence of positive
\gamma V_{\gamma}(x)
term in the FRT-HJ-VI (
33
), and thus the satisfaction of the barrier constraint (Table
1
, last row). Moreover,
e^{\gamma t}
vanishes as
t
approaches
-\infty
, thereby ensuring continuous, bounded value functions and the solution uniqueness of the FRT-HJ-VI, resulting from the contraction mapping property outlined in Section
4.2
.

An example in Appendix illustrates the limitations of the previous approaches and compares our formulation to these using a simple one-dimensional system.

6
Conclusions

In this study, we have presented a framework that establishes a strong linkage between reachability, control invariance, and Control Barrier Functions (CBFs) through a Hamilton-Jacobi differential game formulation. Two main aspects of our approach are the use of forward reachability concept in lieu of backward reachability, and the incorporation of a discount factor in the value function. These elements induce a contraction in the Bellman backup of the value function, thereby shaping it to satisfy the barrier constraint of the CBFs. Importantly, we note that prior formulations relying on backward reachability were unable to establish this connection between reachability, control invariance, and CBFs. Thus, our work fills a crucial gap in the existing literature, presenting a new perspective on the interplay among these key concepts in the safe control literature through the lens of forward reachability.

As we look toward future research avenues, several open questions and challenges emerge. One salient assumption underlying our study is the differentiability of the boundaries of control invariant sets. A deeper understanding of the implications and limitations of this assumption is crucial for broadening the applicability of our results. For example, the relevance of such regularity properties of the boundary of the safe set for stochastic systems is elucidated in
[
12
]
. Moreover, the potential of forward reachability, especially in the context of inevitable FRT, has been discussed but not yet fully explored. For instance, our finding of the contraction mapping of the discounted FRT Bellman backup has potential ramifications for learning-based approaches. This property may pave the way for advancements in value-function-based approximate dynamic programming algorithms for safety control
[
20
,
8
]
.
