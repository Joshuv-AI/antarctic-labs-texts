Heat Equations and Hearing the Genus on p p -adic Mumford Curves via Automorphic Forms
Patrick Erik Bradley

Abstract
A self-adjoint operator is constructed on the L 2 L^{2} -functions on the K K -rational points X ⁡ ( K ) X(K) of a Mumford curve X X defined over a non-archimedean local field K K . It generates a Feller semi-group, and the corresponding heat equation describes a Markov process on X ⁡ ( K ) X(K) . Its spectrum is non-positive, contains zero and has finitely many limit points which are the only non-eigenvalues, and correspond to the zeros of a given regular differential 1 1 -form on X ⁡ ( K ) X(K) . This allows to recover the genus of X X from the spectrum. The hyperelliptic case allows in principle an explicit genus extraction.

Introduction

The Riemann theta function answers the task of inverting abelian integrals. This is also known as the Jacobi inversion problem which asks for an explicit inverse of the Abel-Jacobi map

Y^{(g)}\to\Jac(Y)

where
Y
is a compact Riemann surface of genus
g
,
\Jac(Y)
is its Jacobian variety, and
Y^{(g)}
is the
g
-fold symmetric product of
Y
. The map is determined by the natural embedding

Y\to\Jac(Y)

which is also called Abel-Jacobi map. The Riemann theta function can be used to construct holomorphic functions on the Riemann surface. In this article, the corresponding
p
-adic version of the Riemann theta function developped by Gerritzen and van der Put in
[
8
]
is used in order to obtain holomorphic functions on a Mumford curve
X
(which can be viewed as a
p
-adic analogon of a Riemann surface), whose divisor is controlled by specifying points on the universal covering of
X
, at least in the case of a hyperelliptic Mumford curve. Such a function
f
is then used in order to define the kernel function of an integral operator
\mathcal{H}_{f}
on spaces of complex-valued functions on
X
, and then study the corresponding heat equation. The general idea of an inverse problem in
p
-adic algebraic geometry is to extract information about an object in this domain (here, a Mumford curve) via a heat equation or a heat operator. In the present case, it is the genus of
X
which turns out to be revealed by the spectrum of
\mathcal{H}_{f}
. This result looks encouraging for the study of more refined inverse problems about Mumford curves in future work.

In order to obtain explicit functions on a Mumford curve, it is helpful to have an explicit uniformisation procedure, and also to be able to compute the Riemann theta function in a sufficiently explicit manner. These tasks are performed in the hyperelliptic case. Namely,
the first task relies case on a uniformisation of hyperelliptic Mumford curves by Gerritzen
[
7
]
, whereas the second task uses methods by van Steen for such curves
[
19
,
20
]
. These methods contribute towards explicit period calculations, as done e.g. in the genus
2
case by Teitelbaum
[
16
]
and others. Related to this is the problem of explicitly relating the ramification points of a finite cover

X\to\mathds{P}^{1}
to the induced ramification points in the universal covering space
\Omega
of the Mumford curve
X
. This problem is still unsolved in general, and in the hyperelliptic case it is conjectured that the
two sets of ramification points have identical relative positions

[
8
, IX.2.5.3]
.

Hearing the shape of structures via
p
-adic analysis began, to the best knowledge of the author, with reconstructing finite graphs via
p
-adic Laplacians
[
3
]
.
In
[
2
]
, the author develops Laplacian operators on a Tate curve
E_{q}
via theta functions. The difference to the case of Mumford curves of arbitrary genus considered here, is that in the former case, the multiplicative group structure of
E_{q}
can be used in order to produce
q
-invariant functions with
varying
zero
x
and pole
x^{-1}
for
K
-rational points

of

. Whereas here, this not being possible, the approach is to define an invariant function with a
fixed
set of
K
-rational zeros and poles on the Mumford curve. So, in the genus
1
case, it is possible to extract information about the existence of
2
-torsion points from the spectrum of the operator.

Heat equations on Mumford curves are already developed in
[
1
]
. However, these rely only on the skeleton or reduction graph of the curve. In contrast to this, here the geometry of
X
comes into play via choosing a regular differential
1
-form
\omega
on the
K
-rational points

for a non-archimedean local field

. This

defines in a well-known way a measure
\left\lvert\omega\right\rvert
on
X(K)
.
The idea for obtaining an integral operator is in fact, just like in previous work of the author, a slight generalisation of Zúñiga-Galindo’s method of constructing a
p
-adic operator on a finite graph
[

]
, but this time using the measure

and a compatible holomorphic function

In a private communication, M. van der Put observed the interesting fact that the space
X(K)
, viewed as a
1
-dimensional
K
-manifold for the
uninteresting (totally disconnected) topology already seems to
be sufficient to use for a diffusion process, but what should be done is to establish a diffusion process on

The results of this article can be stated in three theorems, where
V(g)\subset X(K)
denotes the zero set of a function or differential form
g
(whose zero sets are always assumed
K
-rational):

Theorem 1.

Assume that
V(\omega)\subseteq V(f)
.
Then the operator
\mathcal{H}_{f}
is self-adjoint on the Hilbert space
L^{2}(X(K)\setminus V(f),\left\lvert\omega\right\rvert)
, which further has an orthogonal
\mathcal{H}_{f}
-invariant decomposition into a part in which analogues of the Kozyrev wavelets are eigenfunctions with negative eigenvalues, and a finite-dimensional part on which
\mathcal{H}_{f}
has a non-positive spectrum containing zero, and
n
accumulation points with
n
being the size of
V(\omega)
, and these are the only non-eigenvalues in the spectrum.

Theorem 2.

There exists a probability measure
p_{t}(x,\cdot)
on the Borel
\sigma
-algebra of
X(K)\setminus V(\omega)
such that the Cauchy problem for the heat equation

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}h(x,t)-\mathcal{H}_{f}h(x,t)=0

with continuous initial condition
h_{0}(x)
has a unique
C^{1}
-solution
of the form

h(x,t)=\int_{X(K)\setminus V(\omega)}h_{0}(y)p_{t}(x,\left\lvert\omega(y)\right\rvert)

and
p_{t}(x,\cdot)
is the transition function of a Markov process whose
paths are right continuous and have no other discontinuities than jumps.

Theorem 3.

Given a regular
1
-form
\omega
on
X
, it is possible to recover the genus
g(X)
from the spectrum of
\mathcal{H}_{f}
.

The proof of Theorem 1 uses a Hilbert-Schmidt operator on an infinite graph derived from
\mathcal{H}_{f}
, and the result that Kozyrev wavelets can be extended in a useful way to Mumford curves.

The approach to proving Theorem 2 is to show that one obtains a Feller semi-group in a very general setting on a Hausdorff space endowed with a positive Radon measure from a sufficiently well-behaved kernel function. This includes the kernel function used for constructing
\mathcal{H}_{f}
.

Theorem 3 is shown by examining the tree of a good fundamental for the action of the Schottky group after removing the zeros of
\omega
.

The statement of Theorem 3 comes after investigating the case of a hyperelliptic case, in order to obtain an explicit meromorphic function in terms of the ramification divisor of the cover
X\to\mathds{P}^{1}
given by the hyperelliptic involution.
This function is to be used
for the kernel function of the operator.
It is remarked that in the hyperelliptic case, the genus extraction result of Theorem 3 can in principle be made explicit using this operator.

The following section constructs a diffusion operator on the
K
-rational points of a Mumford curve from automorphic forms and a holomorphic differential
1
-form. Self-adjointness is verified, aand the spectrum calculated.
Section 3 studies the heat equation for this operator and obtains the Feller semi-group property needed for a Markov process.
Section 4 puts the framework of
p
-adic Riemann theta functions into a setting needed for constructing kernel functions from these. In particular two forms of their functional equations are formulated and proven, even if, of course, this is implicitly contained in
[
8
]
. Further, the hyperelliptic case is studied in order to write down an explict meromorphic function together with a compatible differential
1
-form from from the equation given by the degree-
2
-cover of the projective line induced by the hyperelliptic involution. In the end, Theorem 3 is proven, and explained that in the hyperelliptic case, the genus extraction can be made explicit in principle.

1. The domain of

Since

2. The operator

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}\psi(z_{0})

\displaystyle=\int_{Z}k(z_{0},z)(\psi(z)-\psi(z_{0}))\nu(z)

3.

\rank(\eta I-\mathscr{L})

4
Kernel functions via Riemann theta functions

4.1
Riemann theta functions for Mumford curves

The Riemann theta function for a Mumford curve
X
is defined in
[
8
, VI.3]
. The presentation here follows the lines of
[
20
, §1]
.
Define

G_{\Gamma}=\Hom(\Gamma,\mathds{C}_{p}^{\times})

and identify this group with
\left(\mathds{C}_{p}^{\times}\right)^{g}
using a fixed set of generators
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{g}\in\Gamma
. If
b\in\Gamma a
for
a,b\in\Omega
, then the automorphic form
\theta(a,b;z)
does not depend on
a\in\Omega

[
8
, II.2.3.3]
.
In this case, the automorphy factor will be written as

c_{\gamma}:=c(a,\gamma a;\cdot)\in G_{\Gamma}

for
\gamma\in\Gamma
.
The group

\Lambda_{\Gamma}={\left\{c_{\gamma}\mid\gamma\in\Gamma\right\}}\subset G_{\Gamma}

is a free abelian group of rank
g
and can thus be identified with
\mathds{Z}^{g}

[
8
, VI.1.3]
.

There is an isomorphism

\displaystyle\Jac(X)\cong G_{\Gamma}/\Lambda_{\Gamma}

(14)

induced by the correspondence

D=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\left([\bar{a}_{i}]-\left[\bar{b}_{i}\right]\right)\mapsto\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}c(a_{i},b_{i};\cdot)

which takes a divisor of degree zero on the Mumford curve
X
to an automorphy factor in
G_{\Gamma}

[
8
, VI.3.9]
.
The isomorphism (
14
) reveals the Jacobian variety
\Jac(X)

as an analytic torus.

The Riemann theta function of a Mumford curve
X
of genus
g
introduced in
[
8
, VI.3]

is now given via

\xi_{c_{\gamma}}\colon G_{\Gamma}\to\mathds{C}_{p}^{\times},\;c\mapsto P(c_{\gamma},c_{\gamma})c(\gamma)

as

\theta_{\Gamma}(c)=\sum\limits_{c_{\gamma}\in\Lambda_{\Gamma}}\xi_{c_{\gamma}}(c)

The
where

P(c_{\gamma},c_{\gamma})=Q(c_{\gamma},c_{\gamma})^{\frac{1}{2}}\in\mathds{C}_{p}^{\times}\quad\text{(any choice of square root)}

with

Q\colon\Lambda_{\Gamma}\times\Lambda_{\Gamma}\to\mathds{C}_{p}^{\times}

the multiplicative bilinear form given as

Q(c_{\alpha},c_{\beta})=\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle

and where

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle\colon\Gamma^{\abelian}\times\Gamma^{\abelian}\to\mathds{C}_{p}^{\times},\;(\alpha,\beta)\mapsto\frac{u_{\alpha}(z)}{u_{\alpha}(\beta z)}

is the period pairing of
X
with
\Gamma^{\abelian}=\Gamma/[\Gamma,\Gamma]
.
Notice that the values do not depend on the choice of
z\in\Omega
. Using the map

t_{\Gamma}\colon\Omega\to G_{\Gamma},\;x\mapsto c(x,x_{0};\cdot)

for a fixed base point
x_{0}\in\Omega
, obtain the induced canonical embedding, called
Abel-Jacobi map

\bar{t}_{\Gamma}\colon X\to\Jac(X)

which extends to divisors in a canonical way.

Finally, the function of interest is given as

\vartheta=\theta_{\Gamma}\circ t_{\Gamma}\colon\Omega\to\mathds{C}_{p}

which is, according to the Riemann Vanishing Theorem
[
8
, Thm. VI.3.8]
, is a holomorphic function whose divisor is
\Gamma
-invariant, and, considered as a divisor
in
\Div(X)
, has degree
g
.

It is helpful to formulate this theory in the language of
[
20
]
or
[
16
]
, since these articles contain explicit calculations for special kinds of Mumford curves. In particular, a more general form of the functional equation for
\vartheta
, and the
\vartheta(c;\cdot)
to be defined below, than as stated in
[
8
, VI.3.3 and VI.3.4]
will be given.

Lemma 4.1
.

It holds true that

P(c_{\alpha\beta},c_{\alpha\beta})=P(c_{\alpha},c_{\alpha})c_{\alpha}(\beta)P(c_{\beta},c_{\beta})

for
\alpha,\beta\in\Gamma
.

Proof.

It holds true that

\displaystyle P(c_{\alpha\beta},c_{\alpha\beta})

\displaystyle=P(c_{\alpha},c_{\alpha})P(c_{\alpha},c_{\beta})^{2}P(c_{\beta},c_{\beta})

\displaystyle=P(c_{\alpha},c_{\alpha})Q(c_{\alpha},c_{\beta})P(c_{\beta},c_{\beta})

by the multiplicative bilinearity of
P(\cdot,\cdot)
. Now,

\displaystyle Q(c_{\alpha},c_{\beta})

\displaystyle=\frac{u_{\alpha}(z)}{u_{\alpha}(\beta z)}=c(a,\beta a;\alpha)=c_{\beta}(\alpha)

which, by symmetry of
Q(\cdot,\cdot)
also equals
c_{\alpha}(\beta)
. This proves the assertion.
∎

Corollary 4.2

(Functional Equation 1)
.

It holds true that

\theta_{\Gamma}(cc_{\alpha})=\xi_{c_{\alpha}}(c)^{-1}\theta_{\Gamma}(c)

for
\alpha\in\Gamma
and
c\in G_{\Gamma}
.

Proof.

It holds true that

\displaystyle\xi_{c_{\alpha}}(c)\xi_{c_{\gamma}}(cc_{\alpha})

\displaystyle=P(c_{\alpha},c_{\alpha})c(\alpha)P(c_{\gamma},c_{\gamma})c(\gamma)c_{\alpha}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=P(c_{\alpha\gamma},c_{\alpha\gamma})c(\alpha\gamma)=\xi_{c_{\alpha\gamma}}(c)

according to Lemma
4.1
.
This proves the assertion.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

It holds true that

t_{\Gamma}(z)\cdot c_{\alpha}=t_{\Gamma}(\alpha^{-1}z)

for
z\in\Omega
and
\alpha\in\Gamma
.

Proof.

It holds true that

\displaystyle\left(t_{\Gamma}(z)\cdot c_{\alpha}\right)(\gamma)

\displaystyle=c(z,x_{0};\gamma)c(\alpha^{-1}x_{0},x_{0};\gamma)

\displaystyle t_{\Gamma}(\alpha^{-1}z)(\gamma)

\displaystyle=c(\alpha^{-1}z,x_{0};\gamma)

The first product is

\prod\limits_{\delta\in\Gamma}\frac{x-\delta z}{x-\delta x_{0}}\cdot\frac{x-\delta\alpha^{-1}x_{0}}{x-\delta x_{0}}\cdot\frac{\gamma x-\delta x_{0}}{\gamma x-\delta z}\cdot\frac{\gamma x-\delta x_{0}}{\gamma x-\delta\alpha^{-1}x_{0}}

for
x\in\Omega
. Now, view this as four infinite products over
\delta\in\Gamma
, and substitute in the first and the third factor
\delta\mapsto\alpha^{-1}\delta
. This does not change its value, and one obtains

\prod\limits_{\delta\in\Gamma}\frac{x-\delta\alpha^{-1}z}{x-\delta x_{0}}\cdot\frac{\gamma x-\delta x_{0}}{\gamma x-\delta\alpha^{-1}}=c(\alpha^{-1}z,x_{0};\gamma)

from which the assertion now follows.
∎

Let
c\in G_{\Gamma}
, and define

\vartheta(c;z)=\theta_{\Gamma}(c\cdot t_{\Gamma}(z))

for
z\in\Omega
.

Corollary 4.4

(Functional Equation 2)
.

It holds true that

\displaystyle\vartheta(c;\alpha^{-1}z)

\displaystyle=c(\alpha)^{-1}\xi_{c_{\alpha}}(t_{\Gamma}(z))^{-1}\vartheta(c;z)

\displaystyle=c(\alpha)^{-1}P(c_{\alpha},c_{\alpha})^{-1}c(z,x_{0};\alpha)^{-1}\vartheta(c;z)

for
z\in\Omega
,
\alpha\in\Gamma
, and
c\in G_{\Gamma}
.

Proof.

One calculates, using Lemma
4.3
and Corollary
4.2
, that

\displaystyle\vartheta(c;\alpha^{-1}z)

\displaystyle=\theta_{\Gamma}(c\cdot t_{\Gamma}(\alpha^{-1}z))=\theta_{\Gamma}(c\cdot t_{\Gamma}(z)c_{\alpha})

\displaystyle=\xi_{c_{\alpha}}(c\cdot t_{\Gamma}(z))^{-1}\theta_{\Gamma}(c\cdot t_{\Gamma}(z))

\displaystyle=c(\alpha)^{-1}\xi_{c_{\alpha}}(t_{\Gamma}(z))^{-1}\vartheta(c;z)

\displaystyle=c(\alpha)^{-1}P(c_{\alpha},c_{\alpha})^{-1}c(z,x_{0};\alpha)^{-1}\vartheta(c;z)

which proves the assertion.
∎

An immediate consequence of the functional equation Corollary
4.4
is that the divisor

\divisor\left(\vartheta(c;z)\right)

is
\Gamma
-invariant.

In order to compare with
[
8
]
,
observe the following:

Lemma 4.5
.

It holds true that

\vartheta(c;z)=\sum\limits_{c_{\gamma}\in\Lambda_{\Gamma}}P(c_{\gamma},c_{\gamma})c(\gamma)u_{\gamma}(z)

for
c\in G_{\Gamma}
and

It holds true that

\vartheta(c;z)=\sum\limits_{c_{\gamma}\in\Lambda_{\Gamma}}\xi_{c_{\gamma}}(c\cdot t_{\Gamma}(z))

where further

\displaystyle c(z,x_{0};\gamma)=\frac{u_{\gamma}(z)}{u_{\gamma}(x_{0})}=u_{\gamma}(z)

(15)

because

u_{\gamma}(x_{0})=\theta(x_{0},\gamma x_{0};x_{0})=1

∎

Now, Gerritzen and van der Put in
[
8
, VI]
formulate this with respect to a fixed basis

of
\Gamma
. So, if

Using (

\omega_{0}(z)=\frac{dx(z)}{2y(z)}=\frac{x^{\prime}(z)\,dz}{\sqrt{\prod\limits_{i=1}^{2g+1}(x(z)-e_{i})}}

where
e_{0},\dots,e_{2g+1}\in\mathds{P}^{1}(K)

are the branch points of the covering
\sigma\colon X\to\mathds{P}^{1}
with

e_{0}=x(1)=\infty,\;e_{1}=x(-1)=0,\;e_{2i}=x(a_{i}),\;e_{2i+1}=x(b_{i})

for
i=1,\dots,g
. The equation corresponding to the covering map is

y^{2}=x\cdot\prod\limits_{i=2}^{2g+1}(x-e_{i})

according to

.
Observe that

x^{\prime}(z)=-4\left(\frac{1}{(z-1)^{2}}+\frac{1}{2(z-1)^{3}}\right)-4\sum\limits_{\gamma\in\Gamma\setminus 1}\left(\frac{\gamma^{\prime}(z)}{(\gamma(z)-1)^{2}}+\frac{\gamma^{\prime}(z)}{2(\gamma(z)-1)^{3}}\right)

and now obtain some absolute values. W.l.o.g. assume that the good fundamental domain
F
is obtained by punching out some holes in

\pm 1\in F
, but
0\notin F

Lemma 4.11
.

It holds true that

\displaystyle\left\lvert x(z)\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\left\lvert z-1\right\rvert^{2}},\quad\left\lvert x^{\prime}(z)\right\rvert=\frac{1}{\left\lvert z-1\right\rvert^{3}}

for
\left\lvert z-1\right\rvert<<1
.

Proof.

Assume that
\left\lvert z-1\right\rvert<C<<1
.
Since
1\in F
, it follows that
\gamma(z)
is in one of the holes of
F
for
\gamma\neq 1
. Hence,

\left\lvert\gamma(z)-1\right\rvert\geq C
. It immediately follows that

\left\lvert x(z)\right\rvert=\left\lvert 1+4\left(\frac{1}{z-1}+\frac{1}{(z-1)^{2}}\right)\right\rvert=\frac{1}{\left\lvert z-1\right\rvert^{2}}

which is the first asserted equality.

Now,
\gamma\in\Gamma\setminus 1
can be represented by a matrix

\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
1&d\end{pmatrix}

with
a,b,d\in K
.
It holds true that

\left\lvert\gamma^{\prime}(z)\right\rvert=\frac{\left\lvert\det(\gamma)\right\rvert}{\left\lvert z+d\right\rvert^{2}}

Since, by hyperbolicity,

\frac{\left\lvert a+d\right\rvert^{2}}{\left\lvert\det(\gamma)\right\rvert}>1

assume w.l.o.g. that
\left\lvert a\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert d\right\rvert
, since otherwise replace
\gamma
with
\gamma^{-1}
which is also hyperbolic.
Then

\left\lvert ad-b\right\rvert=\left\lvert\det(\gamma)\right\rvert<\left\lvert a+d\right\rvert^{2}\leq\left\lvert d\right\rvert^{2}

and

\left\lvert\gamma^{\prime}(z)\right\rvert<\frac{\left\lvert d\right\rvert^{2}}{\left\lvert z+d\right\rvert^{2}}

If
\left\lvert d\right\rvert\neq 1
, this last expression is
\leq 1
.
In all these cases, it follows that

\left\lvert x^{\prime}(z)\right\rvert=\left\lvert\frac{1}{(z-1)^{2}}+\frac{1}{(z-1)^{3}}\right\rvert=\frac{1}{\left\lvert z-1\right\rvert^{3}}

as asserted.

So, now assume that
\left\lvert d\right\rvert=1
.
If
\left\lvert 1+d\right\rvert<\left\lvert z-1\right\rvert
, then

\left\lvert\gamma^{\prime}(z)\right\rvert<\frac{1}{\left\lvert z+d\right\rvert^{2}}=\frac{1}{\left\lvert z-1+1+d\right\rvert^{2}}=\frac{1}{\left\lvert z-1\right\rvert^{2}}

which implies again the assertion for
\left\lvert x^{\prime}(z)\right\rvert
.
If

\left\lvert z-1\right\rvert<\left\lvert 1+d\right\rvert\leq 1
, then

1\leq\left\lvert\gamma^{\prime}(z)\right\rvert=\frac{1}{\left\lvert 1+d\right\rvert^{2}}<\frac{1}{\left\lvert z-1\right\rvert^{2}}

again implying the assertion for
\left\lvert x^{\prime}(z)\right\rvert
.
Now, if

\displaystyle\left\lvert 1+d\right\rvert=\left\lvert z-1\right\rvert<C

then

\left\lvert\gamma^{\prime}(z)\right\rvert

could be arbitrarily large as
C\to 0
.
Assume that there is a sequence of
\gamma_{n}\in\Gamma
represented in the above way, such that their
d
-entries tend to
-1
. Then there is a limit element
\alpha\in\PGL_{2}(K)
of this sequence. Hence, only finitely many of these
\gamma_{n}
are in
\Gamma
.
And this now implies the assertion for
\left\lvert x^{\prime}(z)\right\rvert
for
z\in F
sufficiently close to
1
. The assertions are now proven.
∎

Corollary 4.12
.

The regular differential
1
-form
\omega_{0}(z)
has a zero in
z=1
of order
2g-2
, and no other zeros.

Proof.

According to Lemma
4.11
, this is implied by

\left\lvert\omega_{0}(z)\right\rvert=\frac{\left\lvert x^{\prime}(z)\right\rvert\left\lvert dz\right\rvert}{{C\cdot\left\lvert x(z)\right\rvert^{g}}}=\frac{\left\lvert x^{\prime}(z)\right\rvert\left\lvert dz\right\rvert}{C\cdot\left\lvert x(g)\right\rvert^{\frac{2g+1}{2}}}=\left\lvert z-1\right\rvert^{2g-2}\left\lvert dz\right\rvert

for some
C>0
, if
\left\lvert z-1\right\rvert<<1
, because then

\left\lvert x(z)-e_{i}\right\rvert=\left\lvert x(z)\right\rvert

holds true.
Since the divisor of any meromorphic differential
1
-form on a projective algebraic curve defined over an algebraically closed field has degree
2g-2
by Riemann-Roch, the assertion now follows.
∎

From the theory of hyperelliptic curves, it is known that the space of
\Gamma
-invariant holomorphic differential
1
-forms
has a basis as follows:

\omega_{0},x\,\omega_{0},\cdots,x^{g-1}\,\omega_{0}

which allows to prescribe the divisor of
\omega\in H^{0}(X,\Omega_{X/K})
which has degree
2g-2
. So, in order to meet the requirement
S\subseteq V(F(z))
of Lemma
2.19
, define

\omega:=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{g}(x-e_{2i})^{m_{i}}\,\omega_{0}

with natural
m_{1},\dots,m_{g}\geq 0
such that

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{g}m_{i}=2g-2

holds true.

Corollary 4.13
.

It holds true that

\divisor(\omega)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{g}m_{i}\left[\bar{a}_{i}\right]

with
\omega
viewed as a regular differential
1
-form on
X(K)
.

Proof.

First, observe that

\divisor(x)=\left[\,\overline{-1}\,\right]-\left[\,\bar{1}\,\right]

viewed as an element of
\Div(X)
. The mulitplicities follow from the fact that the
\Gamma
-orbits of
1
and
-1
are ramification points
of the
2
-sheeted covering
X\to\mathds{P}^{1}
induced by the
\Gamma
-invariant function
x
.
In particular,
1\in F
is a simple pole of
x
.
Now,
using Corollary
4.12
, check that

\displaystyle\divisor(\omega)

\displaystyle=\divisor\left(\prod\limits_{i=1}^{g}(x-e_{2i})^{m_{i}}\right)+\divisor(\omega_{0})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{g}m_{i}\left[\bar{a}_{i}\right]-(2g-2)\left[\,\bar{1}\,\right]+\divisor(\omega_{0})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{g}m_{i}\left[\bar{a}_{i}\right]

∎

The results obtained here mean that in the case of a hyperelliptic Mumford curve, the kernel function of

can be given in terms of the ramification points

4.3
Hearing the genus of a Mumford curve

In the following, assume that
X
is a Mumford curve defined over
K
of genus
g\geq 2
,
F\subset K
a good fundamental domain for
X
, and
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{g}
corresponding generators of the Schottky group
\Gamma
, whose fixed points are all
K
-rational, and such that the fixed points of
\gamma_{1}
are
0,\infty
. Again, assume that

\divisor(F(z))=\sum\limits_{a\in V(F(z))}m_{a}[a]-\sum\limits_{b\in P(F(z))}m_{b}[b],\quad\divisor(\omega)=\sum\limits_{a\in V(\omega)}n_{a}[a]

with

\sum\limits_{a\in V(F(z))}m_{a}-\sum\limits_{b\in P(F(z))}m_{b}=0,\quad\sum\limits_{a\in V(\omega)}n_{a}=2g-2

where
P(F(z))
is the set of poles of
F(z)
.

Lemma 4.14
.

Let
x\in F\setminus S
be close to
a\in V(\omega)
, say at distance
p^{-fr_{x}}
. Assume that
y\in\dot{B}_{r_{x}+1}(a)=B_{r_{x}+1}(a)\setminus{\left\{a\right\}}

Then

\int_{\dot{B}_{r_{x}+1}(a)}H(x,y)\left\lvert\omega(y)\right\rvert=Cp^{-kfm_{a}}p^{-fn_{a}d_{x}}

for
C>0
independent of
x
, and
r_{x}>>0
.

Proof.

It holds true that

\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert=p^{-fr_{x}}

for
y\in\dot{(}B)_{r_{x}+1}(a)
.
Since
0\notin\Omega
, it does happen that
x-y
falls into a hole of
F
, if
r_{x}
becomes too large. In this case, replace
x-y
with

\mu_{1}^{n}(x-y)
for suitable
n\in\mathds{Z}
, where
\mu_{1}\in\pi O_{K}
is the multiplier of
\gamma_{1}
. So, since

\left\lvert x-y-a\right\rvert=\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert

it follows, as Lemma
x
tends to
a\in V(\omega)
, that
H(x,y)
takes only the following values:

Cp^{-fm_{a}k},\quad k=0,\dots,v(\mu_{1})-1

for
C>0
independent of
k
.
Since

\left\lvert\omega(y)\right\rvert=\left\lvert y-a\right\rvert^{n_{a}}\left\lvert dy\right\rvert

it now follows that

\int_{\dot{(}B)_{r_{x}+1}(a)}H(x,y)\left\lvert\omega(y)\right\rvert=Cp^{-fm_{a}k}\sum\limits_{\ell=r_{x}+1}^{\infty}p^{-fn_{a}\ell}(1-p^{-f})=Cp^{-fm_{a}k}p^{-fn_{a}r_{x}}

as asserted.
∎

Denote with

v(a,b,c)\in\mathscr{T}_{K}

the unique vertex in the Bruhat-Tits tree
\mathscr{T}_{K}
determined by
a,b,c\in\mathds{P}^{1}(K)

Lemma 4.15
.

Let
x\in F\setminus S
be close to
a\in V(\omega)
at distance
p^{-fr_{x}}
, and
v
a vertex of
T_{F\setminus S}
between
v_{x}=v(a,x,\infty)

\int_{U(v)}H(x,y)\left\lvert\omega(y)\right\rvert

Proof.

It holds true that

\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert=p^{-fd(v,v_{0})}

\mathscr{T}_{K}
induced by the Haar measure, i.e. each edge has length one.
It immediately follows that

under the given conditions.
∎

Theorem 4.16
.

Let
X
be a Mumford curve of genus
g
defined over a non-archimedean local field
K
, and let
F\in K(X)
, and
\omega
be a regular differential
1
-form on
X(K)
.
Assume that
\divisor F(z),\divisor(\omega)\in\Div(X(K))
. Then it is possible to recover the genus
g(X)
from the spectrum of
\mathcal{H}_{F}
.

This is Theorem 3 of the introduction.

Proof.

Notice that
V(\omega)\subseteq V(F(z))
is assumed. Assume also that
x\in F\setminus S
is close to some
a\in V(\omega)
.
If
y\in U(v)
with

v\in T_{F\setminus S}
a vertex not on the half-line beginning in
v_{0}=v(0,1,\infty)
and ending in
a
, then

\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert=\left\lvert x-z\right\rvert

for
z\in U(w)

with

w=v\wedge a

where
\wedge
is the join in the tree
T_{S}
of
{F\setminus S}
with root
v_{0}
.
By Lemmas
4.14
and
4.15
, it follows that
the set of negative degree eigenvalues of
\mathcal{H}_{F}
has
n
limit points, where
n=\left\lvert S\right\rvert
, where
S=V(\omega)
. Namely, the tree of
F\setminus S
has precisely
n
ends, and there is a convergence of the degree function

\deg_{\mathcal{H}_{f}}(x)\to\deg_{\mathcal{H}_{F}}(a)

as
x\in F\setminus S
tends to
a\in S
, assuming that
S
is a subset of the good fundamental domain
F
for
X
, and the convergence can be made explicit as follows:
Assume a sequence
x_{k}\in F\setminus S
given with

\left\lvert x_{k}-a\right\rvert=p^{-fk}

for natural
k>>0
.
Then
it holds true that

\deg_{\mathcal{H}_{f}}(x_{k+1})=\deg_{\mathcal{H}_{f}}(x_{k})+C\int\limits_{B_{k+1}(a)}\left\lvert y-a\right\rvert^{m_{a}}\left\lvert dy\right\rvert

with suitable
C>0
independent of
k
, for
k
sufficiently large.
This means that from the rate of convergence of the degree function to a limit point
a\in S
, given via

C\cdot p^{-f(k+1)n_{a}}

for
k>>0
,
the value of the natural number
n_{a}
can be estimated. Doing this for all limit points of
\deg_{\mathcal{H}_{f}}(x)
, it now follows from

\deg(\divisor(\omega))=2g-2

that the genus
g
can be extracted.
∎

Remark 4.17
.

Theorem
4.16
means that, using Example
4.10
and Corollary
4.13

in the hyperelliptic case, it is now in principle possible to explicitly construct an operator
\mathcal{H}_{F}
, calculate its negative degree eigenvalues, and then
recover the genus from that part of the spectrum. Since it has
\left\lvert V(\omega)\right\rvert
limit points, and exponential convergence to these, an explicit genus recovery method is now (in principle) feasible in this case.
Another thing which the proof of that theorem reveals, is that it is in principle possible to extract the absolute value of the multiplier
\mu_{1}
of generator
\gamma_{1}
.
