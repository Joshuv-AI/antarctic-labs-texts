Combinatorial properties of lazy expansions in Cantor real bases
Célia Cisternino

Abstract
. The lazy algorithm for a real base β \beta is generalized to the setting of Cantor bases 𝜷 = ( β n ) n ∈ ℕ \boldsymbol{\beta}=(\beta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}} introduced recently by Charlier and the author. To do so, let x 𝜷 x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}} be the greatest real number that has a 𝜷 \boldsymbol{\beta} -representation a 0 a 1 a 2 ⋯ a_{0}a_{1}a_{2}\cdots such that each letter a n a_{n} belongs to { 0 , … , ⌈ β n ⌉ − 1 } \{0,\ldots,\left\lceil\beta_{n}\right\rceil-1\} . This paper is concerned with the combinatorial properties of the lazy 𝜷 \boldsymbol{\beta} -expansions, which are defined when x 𝜷 < + ∞ x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}<+\infty . As an illustration, Cantor bases following the Thue-Morse sequence are studied and a formula giving their corresponding value of x 𝜷 x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}} is proved. First, it is shown that the lazy 𝜷 \boldsymbol{\beta} -expansions are obtained by “flipping” the digits of the greedy 𝜷 \boldsymbol{\beta} -expansions. Next, a Parry-like criterion characterizing the sequences of non-negative integers that are the lazy 𝜷 \boldsymbol{\beta} -expansions of some real number in ( x 𝜷 − 1 , x 𝜷 ] (x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-1,x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}] is proved. Moreover, the lazy 𝜷 \boldsymbol{\beta} -shift is studied and in the particular case of alternate bases, that is the periodic Cantor bases, an analogue of Bertrand-Mathis’ theorem in the lazy framework is proved: the lazy 𝜷 \boldsymbol{\beta} -shift is sofic if and only if all quasi-lazy 𝜷 ( i ) \boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i)} -expansions of x 𝜷 ( i ) − 1 x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i)}}-1 are ultimately periodic, where 𝜷 ( i ) \boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i)} is the i i -th shift of the alternate base 𝜷 \boldsymbol{\beta} .

1.
Introduction

Two well-known generalizations of the integer base representations are the Cantor and real base representations. The former was introduced by Cantor in 1869
[
3
]
. The Cantor representation of a real number
x
via a base sequence
(b_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in(\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2})^{\mathbb{N}}
is an infinite sequence
a_{0}a_{1}a_{2}\cdots
of non-negative integers such that

x=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{a_{n}}{\prod_{i=0}^{n}b_{i}}.

The latter was defined by Rényi in 1957
[
11
]
and well understood since the pioneering work of Parry in 1960
[
10
]
. A real base representation of a real number
x
via a real base
\beta>1
is an infinite sequence
a_{0}a_{1}a_{2}\cdots
of non-negative integers such that

x=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{a_{n}}{\beta^{i+1}}.

Gathering both, the notion of Cantor real bases was introduced by Charlier and the author in a recent work
[
4
]
.
Note that these type of representations involving more than one base simultaneously and independently aroused the interest of mathematicians
[
2
,
4
,
8
,
9
,
12
]
.

A
Cantor real base
is a sequence
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\beta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of real numbers greater than
1
such that
\prod_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\beta_{n}=+\infty
.
A representation of a real number
x
via a Cantor real base
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\beta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is an infinite sequence
a_{0}a_{1}a_{2}\cdots
over
\mathbb{N}
such that

x=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{a_{n}}{\prod_{i=0}^{n}\beta_{i}}.

The digits of a
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-representation can be chosen by using several appropriate algorithms. As in the real base theory, in order to represent non-negative real numbers smaller than or equal to
x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
where

x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{\left\lceil\beta_{n}\right\rceil-1}{\prod_{i=0}^{n}\beta_{i}},

the most commonly used algorithms are the greedy and the lazy ones.
In the greedy algorithm, each digit is chosen as the largest possible among
0,\ldots,\left\lceil\beta_{n}\right\rceil-1
at position
n
. At the other extreme, the lazy algorithm picks the least possible digit at each step. The so-obtained
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-representations are respectively called the greedy and lazy
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-expansions.

In the initial work
[
4
]
, the combinatorial properties of the greedy
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-expansions of real numbers in
[0,1)
were investigated. In particular, generalizations of several combinatorial results of real base expansions were obtained, such as Parry’s criterion for greedy expansions and, while considering periodic Cantor real bases, called
alternate bases
, Bertrand-Mathis’ characterization of sofic shifts.
Next, in
[
5
]
, in the particular case of alternate bases, the lazy expansions were defined and both greedy and lazy expansions were studied in terms of dynamics. These results generalize the well-known ones from the theory of real base expansions (see
[
6
,
7
,
10
,
11
]
). Note that the lazy real base expansions have been widely studied in terms of dynamics and, to the best of the author’s knowledge, not in terms of combinatorics.

The goal of this paper is to study the combinatorial properties of the lazy expansions in Cantor real bases.
In particular, the aim is to obtain a version of Parry’s theorem
[
10
]
and Bertrand-Mathis’ theorem
[
1
]
in the lazy Cantor real base framework.

This paper is organized as follows.
First, the Cantor bases and the associated greedy and lazy algorithms are introduced in Section
2
. Note that the lazy algorithm is defined when
x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}<+\infty
hence, this paper deals with Cantor bases such that
x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}<+\infty
. As an illustration, in Section
2
, the value of
x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
is studied when
\boldsymbol{\beta}
is a Cantor base defined thanks to the Thue-Morse sequence over an alphabet
\{\alpha,\beta\}
with
\alpha,\beta>1
, that is
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\alpha,\beta,\beta,\alpha,\beta,\alpha,\alpha,\beta,\cdots)
.
Next, it is shown in Section
3
that the lazy
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-expansions are obtained by “flipping” the digits of the greedy
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-expansions.
This allow us, to translate the greedy properties from
[
4
]
to their lazy analogues.
Section
4
is then concerned by first few properties of lazy
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-expansions.
Then, we define the quasi-lazy
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-expansions of
x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-1
in Section
5
and show that the same “flip” permits us to go from the quasi-greedy
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-expansion
to the quasi-lazy one.
Hence, in Section
6
, the lazy
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-admissible sequences are studied and a Parry-like criterion characterizing the lazy
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-expansions is proved. Finally, in Section
7
, the lazy
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-shift is studied and in the particular case of alternate bases, an analogue of Bertrand-Mathis’ theorem in the lazy case is proved. That is, if
\boldsymbol{\beta}
is an alternate base, we obtain that the lazy
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-shift is sofic if and only if all quasi-lazy
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i)}
-expansions of
x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i)}}-1
are ultimately periodic, where
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i)}
is the
i
-th shift of the alternate base
\boldsymbol{\beta}
.

2.
Cantor real bases

In this section, the needed definitions and conventions are given. Throughout this text, if
a
is an infinite word then for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
a_{n}
designates its letter indexed by
n
, so that
a=a_{0}a_{1}a_{2}\cdots
, that is the
(n+1)^{\text{st}}
letter of
a
. Moreover, an interval of non-negative integers
\{i,\ldots,j\}
with
1
1

1

If
i>j
, we take the convention that
[\![i,j]\!]
is the empty set.

i\leq j
is denoted
[\![i,j]\!]
and
\left\lfloor\cdot\right\rfloor
and
\left\lceil\cdot\right\rceil
respectively denote the floor and ceiling functions.

A
Cantor real base
, or simply a
Cantor base
, is a sequence
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\beta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of real numbers greater than
1
such that
\prod_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\beta_{n}=+\infty
.
For instance, any sequence
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\beta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of real numbers greater than
1
that takes only finitely many values is a Cantor base since in this case, the condition
\prod_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\beta_{n}=+\infty
is trivially satisfied.
In particular, if
\beta>1
then
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\beta,\beta,\ldots)
is a Cantor base and in this case, all notions coincides with the widely studied theory of
\beta
-expansions.
An
alternate base
is a periodic Cantor base, that is a Cantor base for which there exists
p\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
such that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\beta_{n}=\beta_{n+p}
. In this case, we simply write
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\overline{\beta_{0},\ldots,\beta_{p-1}})
and the integer
p
is called the
length
of the alternate base
\boldsymbol{\beta}
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\beta}
be a Cantor base. We define

\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(n)}=(\beta_{n},\beta_{n+1},\ldots)\quad\text{for all }n\in\mathbb{N}.

In particular
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(0)}=\boldsymbol{\beta}
.
The
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-value (partial) map

\mathrm{val}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\colon(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})^{\mathbb{N}}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
by

(2.1)

\mathrm{val}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(a)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{a_{n}}{\prod_{i=0}^{n}\beta_{i}}

for any infinite word
a
over
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, provided that the series converges. A
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-representation
of a non-negative real number
x
is an infinite word
a\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
such that
\mathrm{val}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(a)=x
.
A
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-representation is said to be
finite
if it ends with infinitely many zeros, and
infinite
otherwise.
The
length
of a finite
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-representation is the length of the longest prefix ending in a non-zero digit.
When a
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-representation is finite, we sometimes omit to write the tail of zeros.

2.1.
Greedy algorithm on
\boldsymbol{[0,1)}

For
x\in[0,1)
, a distinguished
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-representation
\varepsilon_{\boldsymbol{\beta},0}(x)\varepsilon_{\boldsymbol{\beta},1}(x)\varepsilon_{\boldsymbol{\beta},2}(x)\cdots
, called the
greedy
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-expansion
is obtained from the
greedy algorithm
. If the first
N
digits of the greedy
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-expansion of
x
are given by
\varepsilon_{\boldsymbol{\beta},0}(x),\ldots,\varepsilon_{\boldsymbol{\beta},N-1}(x)
, then the next digit
\varepsilon_{\boldsymbol{\beta},N}(x)
is the greatest integer such that

\sum_{n=0}^{N}\frac{\varepsilon_{\boldsymbol{\beta},n}(x)}{\prod_{k=0}^{n}\beta_{k}}\leq x.

In particular, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the digit
\varepsilon_{\boldsymbol{\beta},n}(x)
belongs to the alphabet
[\![0,\left\lceil\beta_{n}\right\rceil-1]\!]
.
The greedy algorithm can be equivalently defined as follows:

•

\varepsilon_{\boldsymbol{\beta},0}(x)=\left\lfloor\beta_{0}x\right\rfloor
and
r_{\boldsymbol{\beta},0}(x)=\beta_{0}x-\varepsilon_{\boldsymbol{\beta},0}(x)

•

\varepsilon_{\boldsymbol{\beta},n}(x)=\left\lfloor\beta_{n}r_{\boldsymbol{\beta},n-1}(x)\right\rfloor
and
r_{\boldsymbol{\beta},n}(x)=\beta_{n}r_{\boldsymbol{\beta},n-1}(x)-\varepsilon_{\boldsymbol{\beta},n}(x)
for
n\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
.

The obtained
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-representation is denoted by
d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(x)
and is called the
greedy
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-expansion
of
x
. When the context is clear, we simply denote
\varepsilon_{\boldsymbol{\beta},n}(x)
by
\varepsilon_{n}(x)
and
r_{\boldsymbol{\beta},n}(x)
by
r_{n}(x)
.

2.2.
Lazy algorithm on
\boldsymbol{(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-1,x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}]}

Considering a Cantor base
\boldsymbol{\beta}
, define

x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{\left\lceil\beta_{n}\right\rceil-1}{\prod_{k=0}^{n}\beta_{k}}.

Either this series converges or
x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=+\infty
.
In both cases, this corresponds to the greatest real number that has a
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-representation
a_{0}a_{1}a_{2}\cdots
such that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
the letter
a_{n}
belongs to the alphabet
[\![0,\left\lceil\beta_{n}\right\rceil-1]\!]
.

Since the greedy algorithm converges on
[0,1)
, it can be easily seen that
x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\geq 1
. Moreover, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

(2.3)

x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(n)}}=\frac{x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(n+1)}}+\left\lceil\beta_{n}\right\rceil-1}{\beta_{n}}.

Hence, it can be easily proved that
x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=1
if and only if
\beta_{n}\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

3.
Flip greedy and get lazy

In
[
5
, Section 5]
, in the alternate base framework, both greedy and lazy expansions were compared. The following result generalizes this comparison to the Cantor base expansions.

For a Cantor base
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\beta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, we let
A_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
denote the (possibly infinite) alphabet
[\![0,\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}(\left\lceil\beta_{n}\right\rceil-1)]\!]
. Note that, if the supremum is infinite, the alphabet
A_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
is made of all non-negative integers. Any greedy and lazy
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-expansion belongs to
A_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\mathbb{N}}
and more precisely to the set of infinite words
a\in A_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\mathbb{N}}
such that, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the letter
a_{n}
belongs to
[\![0,\left\lceil\beta_{n}\right\rceil-1]\!]
. From now on, let
\prod_{n\in\mathbb{N}}[\![0,\left\lceil\beta_{n}\right\rceil-1]\!]
denote this set of infinite words.

Let
\theta_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
be the map defined by

\displaystyle\theta_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\colon

\displaystyle\prod_{n\in\mathbb{N}}[\![0,\left\lceil\beta_{n}\right\rceil-1]\!]\to\prod_{n\in\mathbb{N}}[\![0,\left\lceil\beta_{n}\right\rceil-1]\!],

\displaystyle a_{0}a_{1}\cdots\mapsto(\left\lceil\beta_{0}\right\rceil-1-a_{0})(\left\lceil\beta_{1}\right\rceil-1-a_{1})\cdots.

The map
\theta_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
is continuous with respect to the topology induced by the prefix distance, bijective and the inverse map
\theta_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{-1}
is the map
\theta_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
itself.
For any infinite word
a\in\prod_{n\in\mathbb{N}}[\![0,\left\lceil\beta_{n}\right\rceil-1]\!]
, we get

(3.1)

\mathrm{val}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\theta_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(a))=x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-\mathrm{val}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(a).

Moreover, the map
\theta_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
is decreasing with respect to the lexicographic order, that is, for all infinite words
a
and
b
in
\prod_{n\in\mathbb{N}}[\![0,\left\lceil\beta_{n}\right\rceil-1]\!]
, we get

(3.2)

a<_{\mathrm{lex}}b\iff\theta_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(a)>_{\mathrm{lex}}\theta_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(b).

The map
\theta_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
is the key of the reasoning of this paper. In fact, as shown in the following result, it will allow us to “flip” the greedy expansions in order to get the lazy ones.

Proposition 7
.

For all
x\in[0,1)
and all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\xi_{\boldsymbol{\beta},n}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-x)=\left\lceil\beta_{n}\right\rceil-1-\varepsilon_{\boldsymbol{\beta},n}(x)
and
s_{\boldsymbol{\beta},n}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-x)=x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(n+1)}}-r_{\boldsymbol{\beta},n}(x)
.
In particular, we get
\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-x)=\theta_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(x))
.

Proof.

Consider
x\in[0,1)
. We proceed by induction on
n
. By (
2.3
), we have

\displaystyle\xi_{\boldsymbol{\beta},0}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-x)=

\displaystyle\lceil\beta_{0}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-x)-x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(1)}}\rceil

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\lceil\left\lceil\beta_{0}\right\rceil-1-\beta_{0}x\right\rceil

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\lceil\beta_{0}\right\rceil-1+\left\lceil-\beta_{0}x\right\rceil

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\lceil\beta_{0}\right\rceil-1-\left\lfloor\beta_{0}x\right\rfloor

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\lceil\beta_{0}\right\rceil-1-\varepsilon_{\boldsymbol{\beta},0}(x).

Moreover, we get

\displaystyle s_{\boldsymbol{\beta},0}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-x)=

\displaystyle\beta_{0}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-x)-(\left\lceil\beta_{0}\right\rceil-1-\varepsilon_{\boldsymbol{\beta},0}(x))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta_{0}x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-(\left\lceil\beta_{0}\right\rceil-1)-(\beta_{0}x-\varepsilon_{\boldsymbol{\beta},0}(x))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(1)}}-r_{\boldsymbol{\beta},0}(x)

where (
2.3
) is used again in the last equality. By induction, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
, we have

\displaystyle\xi_{\boldsymbol{\beta},n}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-x)=

\displaystyle\lceil\beta_{n}s_{\boldsymbol{\beta},n-1}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-x)-x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(n+1)}}\rceil

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lceil\beta_{n}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(n)}}-r_{\boldsymbol{\beta},n-1}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-x))-x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(n+1)}}\rceil

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\lceil\left\lceil\beta_{n}\right\rceil-1-\beta_{n}r_{\boldsymbol{\beta},n-1}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-x)\right\rceil

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\lceil\beta_{n}\right\rceil-1-\left\lfloor\beta_{n}r_{\boldsymbol{\beta},n-1}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-x)\right\rfloor

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\lceil\beta_{n}\right\rceil-1-\varepsilon_{\boldsymbol{\beta},n}(x)

and

\displaystyle s_{\boldsymbol{\beta},n}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-x)=

\displaystyle\beta_{n}s_{\boldsymbol{\beta},n-1}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-x)-\xi_{\boldsymbol{\beta},n}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta_{n}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(n)}}-r_{\boldsymbol{\beta},n-1}(x))-(\left\lceil\beta_{n}\right\rceil-1-\varepsilon_{\boldsymbol{\beta},n}(x))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(n+1)}}-r_{\boldsymbol{\beta},n}(x).

In particular, we can conclude that
\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-x)=\theta_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(x))
.
∎

4.
First properties of lazy expansions

For any alphabet
A
, the
shift operator over
A
, denoted by
\sigma_{A}
, is defined by

\sigma_{A}\colon A^{\mathbb{N}}\to A^{\mathbb{N}},\ a_{0}a_{1}a_{2}\cdots\mapsto a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}\cdots.

Throughout the text, whenever there is no ambiguity on the alphabet, we simply write
\sigma
instead of
\sigma_{A_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}
.

Lemma 10
.

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
\sigma^{n}\circ\theta_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=\theta_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(n)}}\circ\sigma^{n}
on
\prod_{n\in\mathbb{N}}[\![0,\left\lceil\beta_{n}\right\rceil-1]\!]
.

Proof.

This is a straightforward verification.
∎

Proposition 11
.

For all
x\in(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-1,x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}]
and all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\sigma^{n}(\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(x))=\ell_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(n)}}(s_{\boldsymbol{\beta},n-1}(x)).

Proof.

This is a consequence of Proposition
7
, Lemma
10
and
[
4
, Proposition 8]
since for all
x\in(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-1,x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}]
we have

\displaystyle\sigma^{n}(\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(x))

\displaystyle=\sigma^{n}\circ\theta_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-x))

\displaystyle=\theta_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(n)}}\circ\sigma^{n}(d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-x))

\displaystyle=\theta_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(n)}}(d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(n)}}(r_{\boldsymbol{\beta},n-1}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-x)))

\displaystyle=\ell_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(n)}}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(n)}}-r_{\boldsymbol{\beta},n-1}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-x))

\displaystyle=\ell_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(n)}}(s_{\boldsymbol{\beta},n-1}(x)).\qed

Proposition 12
.

Let
a
be an infinite word over
\mathbb{N}
and
x\in(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-1,x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}]
.
We have
a=\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(x)
if and only if
a\in\prod_{n\in\mathbb{N}}[\![0,\left\lceil\beta_{n}\right\rceil-1]\!]
,
\mathrm{val}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(a)=x
and for all
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
,

\sum_{n=\ell+1}^{+\infty}\frac{a_{n}}{\prod_{k=0}^{n}{\beta_{k}}}>\frac{x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(\ell+1)}}-1}{\prod_{k=0}^{\ell}{\beta_{k}}}.

Proof.

Consider
a\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
and
x\in(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-1,x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}]
. By Proposition
7
, we have
a=\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(x)
if and only if
a\in\prod_{n\in\mathbb{N}}[\![0,\left\lceil\beta_{n}\right\rceil-1]\!]
and
\theta_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(a)=d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-x)
. By
[
4
, Lemma 9]
, we get
a=\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(x)
if and only if
a\in\prod_{n\in\mathbb{N}}[\![0,\left\lceil\beta_{n}\right\rceil-1]\!]
,
\mathrm{val}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\theta_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(a))=x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-x
and for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
,

\sum_{n=N+1}^{+\infty}\frac{\left\lceil\beta_{n}\right\rceil-1-a_{n}}{\prod_{k=0}^{n}{\beta_{k}}}<\frac{1}{\prod_{k=0}^{N}{\beta_{k}}}.

We conclude the proof by (
3.1
) and by definition of
x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(N+1)}}
.
∎

Proposition 13
.

The lazy
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-expansion of a real number
x\in(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-1,x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}]
is lexicographically minimal among all
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-representations of
x
in
\prod_{n\in\mathbb{N}}[\![0,\left\lceil\beta_{n}\right\rceil-1]\!]
.

Proof.

Let
x\in(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-1,x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}]
and let
a\in\prod_{n\in\mathbb{N}}[\![0,\left\lceil\beta_{n}\right\rceil-1]\!]
be a
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-representation of
x
. Suppose that
a<_{\mathrm{lex}}\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(x)
.
By (
3.2
), we get
\theta_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(a)>_{\mathrm{lex}}\theta_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(x))
. By (
3.1
),
\theta_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(a)
is a
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-representation of
x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-x
. Moreover, by Proposition
7
and since the inverse map
\theta_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{-1}
is the map
\theta_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
itself, we have
\theta_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(x))=d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-x)
. This is absurd since, by
[
4
, Proposition 12]
,
d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-x)
is lexicographically maximal among all
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-representations of
x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-x
.
∎

Note that, contrary to
[
4
, Proposition 12]
, it cannot be stated that “the lazy
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-expansion of a real number
x\in(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-1,x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}]
is lexicographically minimal among all
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-representations of
x
”. In fact, the alphabet of the
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-representations of
x
must be fixed as shown in the following example.

Example 14
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\beta}
be the alternate base from Example

x\in(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-1,x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}]

\boldsymbol{\beta}

\prod_{n\in\mathbb{N}}[\![0,\left\lceil\beta_{n}\right\rceil-1]\!]
.

Proposition 15
.

The function
\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\colon(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-1,x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}]\to{A_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}^{\mathbb{N}}
is increasing:

\forall x,y\in(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-1,x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}],\quad x<y\iff\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(x)<_{\mathrm{lex}}\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(y).

Proof.

Consider
x,y\in(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-1,x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}]
. By
[
4
, Proposition 13]
, Proposition
7
and (
3.2
), we have

\displaystyle x<y\iff

\displaystyle x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-x>x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-y

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-x)>_{\mathrm{lex}}d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-y)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\theta_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(x))>_{\mathrm{lex}}\theta_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(y))

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(x)<_{\mathrm{lex}}\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(y).\qed

Remark 16
.

Considering two Cantor bases
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\beta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that for all

.
However, an analogous result cannot be obtained for the lazy expansions.

(or
\ell_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(x)\geq_{\mathrm{lex}}\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(x)

is a fixed interval.
Moreover, it is neither correct to say “for all

, we have
\ell_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(x_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}-x)\leq_{\mathrm{lex}}\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-x)

with

5.
Quasi-lazy expansions

In this section, we define the quasi-lazy
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-expansion of
x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-1
in order to obtain an analogous of Parry’s theorem
[
10
]
characterizing the lazy expansions of real numbers in
(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-1,x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}]
.

First, let us define the
quasi-greedy
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-expansion of
1
by

(5.1)

d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}(1)=\lim_{x\to 1^{-}}d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(x)

where the limit is taken with respect to the prefix distance of infinite words.
Note that this limit exists by left continuity of
d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
in the neighborhood of
1
.

Remark 18
.

The quasi-greedy
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-expansion of
1
obtained in (
5.1
) coincides with the one defined in
[
4
]
. In fact, let
t_{0}t_{1}\cdots
denote the quasi-greedy
d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}(1)
from
[
4
]
. By
[
4
, Theorem 26 and Corollary 36]
, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the word
t_{0}\cdots t_{n}0^{\omega}
is the greedy
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-expansion of a real number in
x_{n}\in[0,1)
. We have
\lim_{n\to+\infty}x_{n}=1
and
\lim_{n\to+\infty}d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(x_{n})=d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}(1)
.
Hence, in what follows, the results from
[
4
]
in terms of
d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}(1)
can be used.

Note that, in
[
4
]
, we made a choice of definition for the greedy
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-expansion of
1
and defined
d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}(1)
accordingly. However, in this paper I decided not to define the greedy
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-expansion of
1
. In fact, if this were the case, one would have expected to define the lazy
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-expansion of
x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-1
analogously. This would have been done by extending the lazy algorithm over
x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-1
as in
[
5
]
.
However, in that case,
\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-1)
would have not been the image of
d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(1)
, chosen as in
[
4
]
, by the map
\theta_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
when
\beta_{0}
is an integer since if
\beta_{0}\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
, we have
d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(1)=\beta_{0}0^{\omega}
whereas the first letter of
\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-1)
is
0
.

In order to get similar results from
[
4
]
for lazy expansions, we define the
quasi-lazy
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-expansion
of
x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-1
as follows:

(5.2)

\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-1)=\lim_{x\to(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-1)^{+}}\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(x).

Again, this limit exists by right continuity of
\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
in the neighborhood of
x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-1
.
Let us first prove that, similarly to Proposition
7
, the “flip” of the quasi-greedy
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-expansions of
1
is the quasi-lazy
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-expansion of
x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-1
.

Proposition 19
.

We have
\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-1)=\theta_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}(1))
.

Proof.

Consider a sequence of real numbers
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in[0,1)^{\mathbb{N}}
such that
\lim_{n\to+\infty}x_{n}=1
.
We have
(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-1,x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}]^{\mathbb{N}}
and
\lim_{n\to+\infty}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-x_{n})=x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-1
. Hence, by continuity of
\theta_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
and by Proposition
7
, we get

\displaystyle\theta_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}(1))

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to+\infty}\theta_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(x_{n}))

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to+\infty}\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-x_{n})

\displaystyle=\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-1).\qed

Example 20
.

Consider the alternate base from Example
1
. We have
d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}(1)=200(10)^{\omega}
,
d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(1)}}^{*}(1)=(10)^{\omega}
,
\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-1)=012(02)^{\omega}
and
\ell_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(1)}}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(1)}}-1)=(02)^{\omega}
.

Proposition 21
.

The quasi-lazy expansion
\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-1)
is a
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-representation of
x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-1
.

Proof.

This is direct by Proposition
19
,
[
4
, Proposition 22]
and (
3.1
).
∎

Note that, in comparison with the quasi-greedy
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-expansion of
1
which is always infinite, the quasi-lazy
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-expansion of
x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-1
can be finite.

Example 22
.

Consider an alternate base
\boldsymbol{\beta}=\overline{(\beta_{0},\ldots,\beta_{p-1})}
such that for all
i\in[\![0,p-1]\!]
,
\beta_{i}\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
. We get
d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i)}}^{*}(1)=((\beta_{i}-1)\cdots(\beta_{p-1}-1)(\beta_{0}-1)\cdots(\beta_{i-1}-1))^{\omega}
and since
x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i)}}=1
for all
i\in[\![0,p-1]\!]
, we have
\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i)}}^{*}(0)=0^{\omega}
.

The following result gives a necessary condition on the Cantor base
\boldsymbol{\beta}
to have a finite quasi-lazy
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-expansion of
x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-1
.

Proposition 23
.

If the quasi-lazy
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-expansion of
x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-1
is finite of length
n\in\mathbb{N}
, then
x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(n)}}=1
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-1)=\ell_{0}\cdots\ell_{n-1}0^{\omega}
with
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\ell_{n-1}\neq 0
(if it exists, that is if
n\neq 0
). By Proposition
19
, we get that

d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}(1)=(\left\lceil\beta_{0}\right\rceil-1-\ell_{0})\cdots(\left\lceil\beta_{n-1}\right\rceil-1-\ell_{n-1})(\left\lceil\beta_{n}\right\rceil-1)(\left\lceil\beta_{n+1}\right\rceil-1)\cdots.

However, by
[
4
, Proposition 30]
, we know that

\sigma^{n}(d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}(1))=(\left\lceil\beta_{n}\right\rceil-1)(\left\lceil\beta_{n+1}\right\rceil-1)\cdots\leq_{\mathrm{lex}}d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(n)}}^{*}(1).

Hence, we obtain that
\sigma^{n}(d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}(1))=d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(n)}}^{*}(1)
. We conclude that

\displaystyle x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(n)}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{val}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(n)}}(\sigma^{n}(d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}(1)))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{val}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(n)}}(d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(n)}}^{*}(1))

\displaystyle=1.\qed

Corollary 24
.

If the quasi-lazy
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-expansion of
x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-1
is finite of length
n\in\mathbb{N}
, then
\beta_{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
for all
k\geq n
.

As the following example shows, the necessary conditions given by the previous proposition and corollary are not sufficient.

Example 25
.

Consider the Cantor base
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\frac{4}{3},2,2,2,2,2\cdots)
. We have
x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=\frac{3}{2}
and
x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(n)}}=1
for all
n\geq 1
. However, we have
d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}(1)=(10)^{\omega}
and
\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-1)=(01)^{\omega}
.

An infinite word in
\prod_{n\in\mathbb{N}}[\![0,\left\lceil\beta_{n}\right\rceil-1]\!]
is said
ultimately maximal
if there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\geq N
, the
(n+1)^{\text{st}}
letter of
\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-1)
is
\left\lceil\beta_{n}\right\rceil-1
.

Lemma 26
.

The infinite word
\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-1)
cannot be ultimately maximal.

Proof.

This is a direct consequence of Proposition
19
since
d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}(1)
is infinite.
∎

We now prove that
\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-1)
is lexicographically smaller than all
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-representations of real numbers in
(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-1,x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}]
belonging to
\prod_{n\in\mathbb{N}}[\![0,\left\lceil\beta_{n}\right\rceil-1]\!]
.

Proposition 27
.

If
a
is an infinite word in
\prod_{n\in\mathbb{N}}[\![0,\left\lceil\beta_{n}\right\rceil-1]\!]
such that
\mathrm{val}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(a)\in(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-1,x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}]
, then
a>_{\mathrm{lex}}\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-1)
.

Proof.

Let
a
be an infinite word in

, the infinite word

6.
Admissible sequences

We let
D^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
denote the subset of
A_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\mathbb{N}}
of all lazy
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-expansions of real numbers in the interval
(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-1,x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}]
and let
S^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
denote the topological closure of
D^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
with respect to the prefix distance of infinite words:

D^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=\{\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(x)\colon x\in(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-1,x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}]\}\quad\mathrm{and}\quad S^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=\overline{D^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}.

The following result links the sets
D^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
and
S^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
with their analogous greedy ones
D_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=\{d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(x)\colon x\in[0,1)\}
and
S_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=\overline{D_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}
from
[

]
.

Proposition 30
.

The maps
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\theta_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{D_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}}\colon D_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\to D^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
and
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\theta_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{S_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}}\colon S_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\to S^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
are both bijective.

Proof.

By Proposition
7
, the map
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\theta_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{D_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}}
is well defined and surjective.
Hence, by continuity of the map
\theta_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
, the map
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\theta_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{S_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}}
is also well defined and surjective.
Moreover, since the map
\theta_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
is injective, so are the maps
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\theta_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{D_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}}
and
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\theta_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{S_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}}
.
∎

Note that, in the particular case of alternate bases, Proposition

can be deduced from

, Remark 6.3]

.

Let

\mathrm{val}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(a)\leq\mathrm{val}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(b)

Suppose that

are such that

. By Proposition

and (

), we have
\theta_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(a),\theta_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(b)\in S_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
and
\theta_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(a)>_{\mathrm{lex}}\theta_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(b)

, we
\mathrm{val}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\theta_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(a))\geq\mathrm{val}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\theta_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(b))

7.
The lazy
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-shift

This section is concerned with the study of the lazy
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-shift.
First, let us define

\Delta^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}D^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(n)}}\quad\text{and}\quad\Sigma^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=\overline{\Delta^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}.

By Proposition
30
, we get

(7.1)

\Delta^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\theta_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(n)}}(D_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(n)}}).

Proposition 36
.

The sets
\Delta^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
and
\Sigma^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
are both shift-invariant.

Proof.

Let
a
be an infinite word over
\mathbb{N}
. By (
7.1
), if
a
belongs to
\Delta^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
, then there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
and an infinite word
b\in D_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(n)}}
such that
a=\theta_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(n)}}(b)
. We obtain that
\sigma(a)=\sigma(\theta_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(n)}}(b))=\theta_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(n+1)}}(\sigma(b))
by Lemma

. By
[
4
, Theorem 26]
,
\sigma(b)\in D_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(n+1)}}

. Then, it is easily seen that if

then
\sigma(a)\in S^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(n+1)}}
.
∎

Since the set
\Sigma^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
is shift-invariant and closed with respect to the topology induced by the prefix distance on infinite words, we conclude that the subset
\Sigma^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
of
A_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\mathbb{N}}
is a subshift, which we call the

{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
-shift
.

-shift defined in

Let us now study the factors of the lazy

-shift.

Proposition 38
.

We have

Proof.

By definition, we have

Corollary 39

We have

\mathrm{Fac}(\Sigma^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}})=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\theta_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(n)}}\big(\mathrm{Pref}(D_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(n)}})\big).

The conclusion follows from Proposition
30
.
∎

\delta(q_{i_{1},(i_{1}+k_{1})\bmod p,\,k_{1}},a)=q_{i_{2},(i_{2}+k_{2})\bmod p,\,k_{2}}

if and only if

\delta^{\prime}(q_{i_{1},(i_{1}+k_{1})\bmod p,\,k_{1}},\left\lceil\beta_{i_{1}+k_{1}}\right\rceil-1-a)=q_{i_{2},(i_{2}+k_{2})\bmod p,\,k_{2}}.

Proof.

Fix
a\in A_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
,
i\in[\![0,p-1]\!]
and
k\in[\![0,m_{i}+n_{i}-1]\!]
.
By definition of the automaton
\mathcal{A}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
, from
q_{i,(i+k)\bmod p,\,k}
we have the following transitions

\delta(q_{i,(i+k)\bmod p,\,k},a)=\begin{cases}q_{i,(i+k+1)\bmod p,\,k+1}&\text{if }a=t_{k}^{(i)}\text{ and }k\neq m_{i}+n_{i}-1\\
q_{i,(i+m_{i})\bmod p,\,m_{i}}&\text{if }a=t_{k}^{(i)}\text{ and }k=m_{i}+n_{i}-1\\
q_{(i+k+1)\bmod p,(i+k+1)\bmod p,\,0}&\text{if }a\in[\![0,t_{k}^{(i)}-1]\!].\end{cases}

Similarly, by definition of
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
,
we have

\delta^{\prime}(q_{i,(i+k)\bmod p,\,k},a)=\begin{cases}q_{i,(i+k+1)\bmod p,\,k+1}&\text{if }a=\ell_{k}^{(i)}\text{ and }k\neq m_{i}+n_{i}-1\\
q_{i,(i+m_{i})\bmod p,\,m_{i}}&\text{if }a=\ell_{k}^{(i)}\text{ and }k=m_{i}+n_{i}-1\\
q_{(i+k+1)\bmod p,(i+k+1)\bmod p,\,0}&\text{if }a\in[\![\ell_{k}^{(i)}+1,\left\lceil\beta_{i+k}\right\rceil-1]\!].\end{cases}

We get the conclusion since
\ell_{k}^{(i)}=\left\lceil\beta_{i+k}\right\rceil-1-t_{k}^{(i)}
, and hence
a\in[\![0,t_{k}^{(i)}-1]\!]
if and only if
\left\lceil\beta_{i+k}\right\rceil-1-a\in[\![\ell_{k}^{(i)}+1,\left\lceil\beta_{i+k}\right\rceil-1]\!].

∎

Proof of Theorem
40
.

Suppose that, for all
i\in[\![0,p-1]\!]
,
\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i)}}^{*}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i)}}-1)
is ultimately periodic.
For all
i\in[\![0,p-1]\!]
, let

\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i)}}^{*}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i)}}-1)=\ell_{0}^{(i)}\cdots\ell_{m_{i}-1}^{(i)}\big(\ell_{m_{i}}^{(i)}\cdots\ell_{m_{i}+n_{i}-1}^{(i)}\big)^{\omega}

with
n_{i}
multiple of
p
.
By Proposition
19
, for all
i\in[\![0,p-1]\!]
, we obtain

d_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i)}}^{*}(1)=t_{0}^{(i)}\cdots t_{m_{i}-1}^{(i)}\big(t_{m_{i}}^{(i)}\cdots t_{m_{i}+n_{i}-1}^{(i)}\big)^{\omega}

with
t^{(i)}_{n}=\left\lceil\beta_{i+n}\right\rceil-1-\ell^{(i)}_{n}
for all
n\in[\![0,m_{i}+n_{i}-1]\!]
.
Let
\mathcal{A}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
and
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
be the automata associated with the greedy and lazy expansions respectively. By
[
4
, Theorem 48]
, for each
i\in[\![0,p-1]\!]
, the language accepted in
\mathcal{A}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
from the initial state
q_{i,i,0}
is precisely
\mathrm{Pref}(D_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i)}})
. Hence, by Lemma
44
, in
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
the language accepted from the initial state
q_{i,i,0}
is precisely
\theta_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i)}}(\mathrm{Pref}(D_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i)}}))
. We get the conclusion by Corollary
39
.

Conversely, suppose that there exists
j\in[\![0,p-1]\!]
such that
\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(j)}}^{*}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(j)}}-1)
is not ultimately periodic, then we prove that
\Sigma^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
is not sofic. This follows the same lines as in the greedy case (see
[
4
, Theorem 48]
). Hence, in the subsequent, the main ideas of the proof are given.
Let

\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i)}}^{*}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i)}}-1)=\ell_{0}^{(i)}\ell_{1}^{(i)}\cdots\quad\text{ for every }i\in[\![0,p-1]\!].

We define a partition
(G_{1},\ldots,G_{q})
of
[\![0,p-1]\!]
as follows.
Let
r=\Card\{\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i)}}^{*}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i)}}-1)\colon i\in[\![0,p-1]\!]\}
and let
i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}\in[\![0,p-1]\!]
be such that
\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i_{1})}}^{*}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i_{1})}}-1),\ldots,\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i_{r})}}^{*}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i_{r})}}-1)
are pairwise distinct and
\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i_{1})}}^{*}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i_{1})}}-1)<_{\mathrm{lex}}\cdots<_{\mathrm{lex}}\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i_{r})}}^{*}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i_{r})}}-1)
. Let
q\in[\![1,r]\!]
be the unique index such that
\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i_{q})}}^{*}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i_{q})}}-1)=\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(j)}}^{*}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(j)}}-1)
where
\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(j)}}^{*}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(j)}}-1)
is not ultimately periodic by assumption.
We set

G_{s}=\{i\in[\![0,p-1]\!]\colon\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i)}}^{*}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i)}}-1)=\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i_{s})}}^{*}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i_{s})}}-1)\}\quad\text{for }s\in[\![1,q-1]\!]

and

G_{q}=\{i\in[\![0,p-1]\!]\colon\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i)}}^{*}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i)}}-1)\geq_{\mathrm{lex}}\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(j)}}^{*}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(j)}}-1)\}.

For each
s\in[\![1,q-1]\!]
, we write
G_{s}=\{i_{s,1},\ldots,i_{s,\alpha_{s}}\}
where
i_{s,1}<\ldots<i_{s,\alpha_{s}}
and we use the convention that
i_{s,\alpha_{s}+1}=i_{s+1,1}
for
s\leq q-2
and
i_{q-1,\alpha_{q-1}+1}=j
. Moreover, we let
g\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
be such that for all
i,i^{\prime}\in[\![0,p-1]\!]
such that
\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i)}}^{*}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i)}}-1)\neq\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i^{\prime})}}^{*}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i^{\prime})}}-1)
, the length-
g
prefixes of
\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i)}}^{*}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i)}}-1)
and
\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i^{\prime})}}^{*}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i^{\prime})}}-1)
are distinct. Then, for
s\in[\![1,q-1]\!]
, we define
C_{s}
to be the least
c\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
such that
\ell^{(i_{s})}_{g-1+c}<\left\lceil\beta_{i_{s}+g-1+c}\right\rceil-1
. Finally, let
N\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
be such that
pN\geq\max\{g,C_{1},\ldots,C_{q-1}\}
.

For all
m\in\mathbb{N}

Now, let

be distinct. Since

\sigma^{m}\big(\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(j)}}^{*}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(j)}}-1)\big)\neq\sigma^{n}\big(\ell_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(j)}}^{*}(x_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(j)}}-1)\big)

and
w^{(n)}z\notin\mathrm{Fac}(\Sigma^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}})
.
