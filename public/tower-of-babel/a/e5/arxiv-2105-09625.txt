Limiting spectral distribution for large sample covariance matrices with graph-dependent elements.


Abstract
We obtain the limiting spectral distribution for large sample covariance matrices associated with random vectors having graph-dependent entries under the assumption that the interdependence among the entries grows with the sample size n n . Our results are tight. In particular, they give necessary and sufficient conditions for the Marchenko-Pastur theorem for sample covariance matrices with m m -dependent orthonormal elements when m = o ⁡ ( n ) m=o(n) .

Proof of Theorem

.

First, we will prove the second bound of the Theorem. Let symmetric
A=(a_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{p}
be such that
a_{ij}=0
if
d_{G}(i,j)>2
. We have

{\rm Var}(\mathbf{x}^{\top}A\,\mathbf{x})=\sum_{1\leqslant i,j,k,l\leqslant p}a_{ij}a_{kl}\mathrm{Cov}(X_{i}X_{j},X_{k}X_{l})\mathrm{I}(d_{G}(i,j)>2,d_{G}(k,l)>2)

Introduce a zero-mean Gaussian vector
\mathbf{z}=(Z_{k})_{k=1}^{p}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
with
{\mathbb{E}}\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}^{\top}=\Sigma={\mathbb{E}}\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\top}
. We will show that

if
i,j,k,l\in V
and
d_{G}(i,j)>2,

d_{G}(k,l)>2
,

\displaystyle\text{then }\mathrm{Cov}(X_{i}X_{j},X_{k}X_{l})=\mathrm{Cov}(Z_{i}Z_{j},Z_{k}Z_{l}).

(6)

Suppose for a moment that this is true. By Lemma 2.3 in
[
17
]

{\rm Var}(\mathbf{x}^{\top}A\,\mathbf{x})={\rm Var}(\mathbf{z}^{\top}A\,\mathbf{z})=2{\mathrm{tr}}((A\Sigma)^{2}).

By the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality for traces,

{\mathrm{tr}}(A\Sigma A\Sigma)\leqslant\sqrt{{\mathrm{tr}}((A\Sigma A)^{2}){\mathrm{tr}}(\Sigma^{2})}.

Furthermore,
\|A\|^{2}I_{p}-A^{2}
is a positive semidefinite matrix. Therefore,

{\mathrm{tr}}((A\Sigma A)^{2})={\mathrm{tr}}(A\Sigma A^{2}\Sigma A)\leqslant\|A\|^{2}{\mathrm{tr}}(A\Sigma^{2}A)=\|A\|^{2}{\mathrm{tr}}(\Sigma A^{2}\Sigma)\leqslant\|A\|^{4}{\mathrm{tr}}(\Sigma^{2})

and we get the desired bound
{\rm Var}(\mathbf{x}^{\top}A\,\mathbf{x})\leqslant 2\|A\|^{2}{\mathrm{tr}}(\Sigma^{2})
.

So, it remains to verify (
3

\{i,k\}
and
\{j,l\}
or the sets
\{i,l\}
and
\{j,k\}
are non-adjacent. If
\{i,j\}
and
\{k,l\}
are non-adjacent, then the claim is obvious. Suppose
\{i,j\}
and
\{k,l\}
are adjacent. W.l.o.g.
d_{G}(i,k)\leqslant 1
. We have
d_{G}(i,j)>2
,
d_{G}(k,l)>2,

d_{G}(i,l)>1
, and
d_{G}(k,j)>1
, where the last two inequalities follow from

\displaystyle 2

\displaystyle<d_{G}(k,l)\leqslant d_{G}(k,i)+d_{G}(i,l)\leqslant 1+d_{G}(i,l),

\displaystyle 2

\displaystyle<d_{G}(i,j)\leqslant d_{G}(i,k)+d_{G}(k,j)\leqslant 1+d_{G}(k,j).

So,
\{j,l\}
and
\{i,k\}
are non-adjacent. The claim is verified.

Consider the case when
\{i,k\}
and

{\mathbb{P}}(|S_{n}|>K)
arbitrarily small, as

{\mathbb{P}}(|S_{n}|>K)\leqslant\frac{{\mathbb{E}}|S_{n}|}{K}\leqslant\frac{{\mathbb{E}}T_{n}}{K}\to\frac{c}{K}.

With this, one can easily deduce that
\{\mu_{n}\}_{n\in\mathcal{N}}
is a tight collection of probability measures, where
\mu_{n}
is the distribution of
S_{n}
. By Prokhorov’s theorem, there exists an increasing sequence
(n_{l})_{l=1}^{\infty}
with
n_{l}\in\mathcal{N}
and a random variable
\sigma
such that
S_{n_{l}}
converges in distribution to
\sigma
.
In view of (
18
), the Khintchin theorem implies that
\sigma
has an infinitely divisible distribution (see Theorem 3.1 in
[
24
]
). As
|S_{n}|\leqslant T_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\rightarrow}}c
,
\sigma
should be bounded a.s. This is possible only if
\sigma
is constant a.s. (see Corollary 3 in
[
3
]
). Therefore,
S_{n_{l}}
also converges to
\sigma
in probability.

Suppose for a moment that the collection
\{T_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is uniformly integrable. Then the collection
\{S_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is uniformly integrable and, by the Lebesgue-Vitali theorem (Theorem 4.5.4 in
[
5
]
),
{\mathbb{E}}|S_{n_{l}}-\sigma|\to 0
and, in particular,
{\mathbb{E}}S_{n_{l}}-\sigma\to 0
as
l\to\infty.
The latter would contradict to the assumption (
19
). This would prove that (
19
) could not hold and
S_{n}-{\mathbb{E}}S_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\rightarrow}}0
.

To finish the proof, we need to prove the uniform integrability of
T_{n}
. This follows from
0\leqslant T_{n}\to c
and
{\mathbb{E}}T_{n}\to c.
Indeed, by the Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem,

{\mathbb{E}}T_{n}\mathrm{I}(T_{n}\leqslant 2c)\to c\mathrm{I}(c\leqslant 2c)=c
. Hence,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{k\geqslant n}{\mathbb{E}}T_{k}\mathrm{I}(T_{k}>2c)=0.

By the absolute continuity of the Lebesgue integral, we can find
(C_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
such that
2c\leqslant C_{n}\to\infty
and

\sup_{1\leqslant k\leqslant n}{\mathbb{E}}T_{k}\mathrm{I}(T_{k}>C_{n})\to 0.

As a result, we see that
{\mathbb{E}}T_{k}\mathrm{I}(T_{k}>C_{n})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
uniformly in
k
. This means that the sequence
(T_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
is uniformly integrable. The proof of the Lemma is finished.
∎
