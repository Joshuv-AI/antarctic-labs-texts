Expansion by regions meets angular integrals
Vladimir A. Smirnov, and Fabian Wunder

Abstract
We study the small-mass asymptotic behavior of so-called angular integrals, appearing in phase-space calculations in perturbative quantum field theory. For this purpose we utilize the strategy of expansion by regions, which is a universal method both for multiloop Feynman integrals and various parametric integrals. To apply the technique to angular integrals, we convert them into suitable parametric integral representations, which are accessible to existing automation tools. We use the the code asy.m to reveal regions contributing to the asymptotic expansion of angular integrals. To evaluate the contributions of these regions in an epsilon expansion we apply the method of Mellin-Barnes representation. Our approach is checked against existing results on angular integrals revealing a connection between contributing regions and angular integrals constructed from an algebraic decomposition. We explicitly calculate the previously unknown asymptotics for angular integrals with three and four denominators and formulate a conjecture for the leading asymptotics and the pole part for a general number of denominators and masses.

1.1
Definition of angular integrals

The notation employed for angular integrals follows the references
[
4
,
5
]
.
Angular integrals in
d
dimensions are defined as

\displaystyle\Omega_{j_{1},j_{2},\dots,j_{n}}(v_{1},\dots,v_{n};d)\equiv\int\mathrm{d}\Omega_{d-1}(k)\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{(v_{i}\cdot k)^{j_{i}}}

(1)

with normalized
d
-vectors

\displaystyle v_{i}=(1,\mathbf{v}_{i})\,,\quad k=(1,\mathbf{k})=(1,\dots,\cos\theta_{n}\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\sin\theta_{i},\dots,\cos\theta_{2}\sin\theta_{1},\cos\theta_{1})\,.

(2)

For the
v_{i}
one may choose an explicit parametrization of the form

\displaystyle v_{1}

\displaystyle=(1,\mathbf{0}_{d-2},\beta_{1})\,,

(3)

\displaystyle v_{2}

\displaystyle=(1,\mathbf{0}_{d-3},\beta_{2}\sin\chi_{2}^{(1)},\beta_{2}\cos\chi_{2}^{(1)})\,,

(4)

\displaystyle v_{3}

\displaystyle=(1,\mathbf{0}_{d-4},\beta_{3}\sin\chi_{3}^{(1)}\sin\chi_{3}^{(2)},\beta_{3}\sin\chi_{3}^{(1)}\cos\chi_{3}^{(2)},\beta_{3}\cos\chi_{3}^{(1)})\,,

(5)

\displaystyle v_{n}

\displaystyle=(1,\mathbf{0}_{d-1-n},\beta_{n}\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\sin\chi_{n}^{(i)},\beta_{n}\cos\chi_{n}^{(n)}\prod_{i=1}^{n-2}\sin\chi_{n}^{(i)},\dots,\beta_{n}\sin\chi_{n}^{(1)}\cos\chi_{n}^{(2)},\beta_{n}\cos\chi_{n}^{(1)})\,,

(6)

even though, in many cases, it is more convenient to keep the propagators in parametrization-free form, which preserves the explicit rotational invariance.
The angular integration measure is given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\Omega_{d-1}(k)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{d}\theta_{i}\,\sin^{2-i-2\varepsilon}\theta_{i}\,\mathrm{d}\Omega_{d-1-n}(k)\,,

(7)

the integration bound for each
\theta_{i}
is
0\leq\theta_{i}\leq\pi
.
The
d-1-n
dimensional part is transverse to all
v_{i}
in the integrand, hence it can be integrated out as

\displaystyle\Omega_{d-1-n}=\int\mathrm{d}\Omega_{d-1-n}(k)=\frac{2\pi^{\frac{d-n}{2}}}{\Gamma\left(\frac{d-n}{2}\right)}\,.

(8)

In the literature, e.g.
[
4
,
5
,
2
,
3
,
6
]
, a commonly used normalization for angular integrals in
d=4-2\varepsilon
dimensions, especially those with only a few propagators, is

\displaystyle I_{j_{1},\dots,j_{n}}(v_{1},\dots,v_{n};\varepsilon)\equiv\frac{\Omega_{j_{1},j_{2},\dots,j_{n}}(v_{1},\dots,v_{n};4-2\varepsilon)}{\Omega_{-2\varepsilon}}

(9)

with

\displaystyle\Omega_{-2\varepsilon}=2^{1-2\varepsilon}\pi^{-\varepsilon}\frac{\Gamma(1-2\varepsilon)}{\Gamma(1-\varepsilon)}.

(10)

This normalization simplifies the
\varepsilon
expansion by removing factors of the Euler-Mascheroni constant
\gamma_{\text{E}}
.

The angular integrals depend on the kinematic invariants
v_{ij}\equiv v_{i}\cdot v_{j}
.
If
v_{ii}=0
, we call the
i
th propagator
massless
, else
massive
.
A useful common notation is to indicate the number of non-zero masses in the integral with an explicit label, i.e.
I_{j_{1},\dots,j_{n}}^{(m)}
for an integral with
m

The parametric variables
\beta_{i}
and
\chi_{j}^{(i)}

, can be expressed through invariant variables
v_{ij}
via

\displaystyle\beta_{i}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-v_{ii}}\,,\;\cos\chi_{j}^{(1)}=\frac{1-v_{1j}}{\beta_{1}\beta_{j}}\,,\;\cos\chi_{j}^{(2)}=\frac{1-v_{2j}-\beta_{2}\beta_{j}\cos\chi_{2}^{(1)}\cos\chi_{j}^{(1)}}{\beta_{2}\beta_{j}\sin\chi_{2}^{(1)}\sin\chi_{j}^{(1)}}\,,

\displaystyle\cos\chi_{j}^{(3)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-v_{3j}-\beta_{3}\beta_{j}\left(\cos\chi_{3}^{(1)}\cos\chi_{j}^{(1)}+\sin\chi_{3}^{(1)}\sin\chi_{j}^{(1)}\cos\chi_{3}^{(2)}\cos\chi_{j}^{(2)}\right)}{\beta_{3}\beta_{j}\sin\chi_{3}^{(1)}\sin\chi_{j}^{(1)}\sin\chi_{3}^{(2)}\sin\chi_{j}^{(2)}}\,,\dots

(11)

1.2
Reduction identities for angular integrals

We observe that for establishing the asymptotic behavior of any angular integral with integer propagator powers
j_{1},\dots,j_{n}
it is sufficient to determine the behavior of the master integrals after IBP reduction, see
[
5
]
for the two denominator case.
The master integrals are those with
j_{i}=0,1
for
i=1,\dots,n
, i.e. there is only the master integral
I_{1,\dots,1}
with manifestly
n
denominators.
Therefore it suffices to derive the asymptotic behavior for
I_{1,\dots,1}
in section
3
.

Furthermore, any master integral with linearly dependent vectors
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
can be reduced to
n-1
-denominator angular integrals by partial fractioning identities.
Assuming the fixed vectors
v_{i}
are all in a four dimensional subspace, we have a maximum of three linearly dependent denominators, hence, under this assumption, all higher denominator angular integrals can be reduced to the three denominator case.
We will not assume linear dependence in the following, however we will use this identity as a cross-check between the three- and
four-denominator angular integrals in section
3.3
.

Another important identity for angular integrals is the
two-point splitting lemma

[
5
,
6
]
, which allows to rewrite the product of two massive denominators as a sum of two products of one massive and one auxiliary massless denominator.
An iterative use allows for writing an angular integral with any number of masses as a sum of integrals with only one non-zero mass.
Therefore, the general integral
I_{1,\dots,1}
can be expressed through a sum of
I_{1,\dots,1}^{(0)}
and
I_{1,\dots,1}^{(1)}
.
This was heavily used in
[
6
]
, however for expansion by regions it is less useful since, as we will see explicitly in section
3.1
, the splitting may change the scaling behavior of the angular integral.
Additionally, keeping all
n
masses has the benefit that the cancellation of poles between regions in the fully massive case serves as a useful consistency check.

The asymptotics in the massless limit is known for up to two denominators
[
6
]
.
For three and more denominators, much less is known in this regard.
Explicit
\varepsilon
-expansions have been published only for the massless three-denominator integral to finite order in
\varepsilon

[
4
]
.
In this work we will add the leading small mass asymptotics to all orders in
\varepsilon
to the three-denominator result and derive the asymptotic behavior for four-denominators.
Based on these findings we formulate a conjecture for the pole part for the general case of
n
denominators and
m
masses.

1.3
Integral representations compatible with
asy.m

The central idea of the present work is an application of the strategy of expansion by regions to angular integrals to obtain the asymptotic behavior of angular integrals in
d=4-2\varepsilon
dimensions in the massless limit.
This method and the corresponding computer code
asy.m
has been developed in the context of multiloop Feynman integrals, but is also applicable to certain classes of parametric integrals, for a recent example see
[
47
]
,
where the method was applied to an off-shell Wilson loop.
Of course, the code can work with parametric representations which have the same form as the Feynman parametric representation of Feynman integrals, i.e.
where the integrand is a product of two polynomials raised to powers which are linear in
d
with a delta function

\delta\left(\sum x_{i}-1\right)
where the summation can be chosen in a convenient way according to the folklore Cheng-Wu theorem.
1
1

1

For a proof see e.g.
[
40
]
, p. 46.

Note that there is no direct analog of the Cheng-Wu theorem for angular integrals since the normalization of the zero-component of the
v_{i}
vectors to
1
breaks the homogeneity of the corresponding Feynman representation.
This is however not a problem since
asy.m
also works on various other integral representations.

In this paper, we apply it to
n
-fold parametric integrals where each of the integration variables
t_{1},\dots t_{n}
runs from zero to infinity.
The integrand is supposed to be a product of polynomials
P_{i}
raised to some powers
a_{i}+\varepsilon\,b_{i}
.
The
P_{i}
are polynomials in the integration variables with coefficients depending on a set of kinematic variables
s_{1},\dots,s_{m}
.
The exponent coefficients
a_{i}
and
b_{i}
are numbers, in most practical cases integers or half-integers.

This means that we are looking for an integral representation of the form

\displaystyle I=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t_{1}\,\dots\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t_{n}\,\prod_{i=1}^{N}P_{i}^{a_{i}+\varepsilon b_{i}}(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})

(12)

for angular integrals.

1.4
Expansion by regions in a nutshell

As already mentioned above, expansion by regions has been first developed for Feynman integrals.
The original formulation
[
38
]
was based on analyzing various regions in loop momentum space, performing the corresponding expansions in small parameters within each of them, dropping out restrictions on the integration domain specifying the individual regions, and finally summing over the contributions of all regions under consideration.
Practically, one didn’t bother about possible double (and multiple) counting when collecting contributions of regions.
2
2

2

with one exception: see
[
48
]
where
regions were introduced in an ‘honest’ way, with specifying integration domains by explicit inequalities. Here interplay of
various regions (in its primary sense) was demonstrated explicitly using some characteristic two-loop examples.

Really, the notion of
region
was understood in many practical calculations as a scaling of loop integration momenta measured in powers of an expansion parameter.
However, to reveal all the relevant regions/scalings, was, in most cases, a highly non-trivial task.
It was the formulation of expansion by regions
[
41
,
43
]
in terms of parametric integrals which opened a way to systematically solve this task.
The geometric formulation gave also the possibility of a generalization of the method to a wider class of integrals such as the parametric integrals discussed in section
1.3
.

A recent review on the method of expansion by regions can be found in
[
42
]
, for some recent applications see
[
49
,
50
,
51
,
52
,
53
,
54
,
55
,
56
]
.
Expansion by regions is now implemented not only with
FIESTA
but also within the package
pySecDec
– see
[
51
]
.
Here we just want to give a very brief overview of the prescriptions as applied to parametric integral, to introduce some notation used in this work.

The prescription of expansion by regions
[
43
,
44
,
57
,
42
]
states that and
n
-fold parametric integral
I
of the structure of Eq. (
12
) with polynomials depending on a small parameter
y
has an asymptotic expansion in
y
of the form

\displaystyle I\sim\sum_{r_{i}\in r}\mathcal{M}_{r_{i}}I,

(13)

where the sum runs over a set
r
of contributing regions
r_{i}
.
These regions
r_{i}
, which correspond to facets of Newton polytopes associated with the integrand polynomials, are vectors with
n
entries each.
For the purpose of this work the set
r
will be determined automatically by
asy.m
.
The operator
\mathcal{M}_{r_{i}}
associated with each region acts on the integrand of
I
by scaling
t_{j}\to y^{(r_{i})_{j}}t_{j}
, multiplying by
y^{\sum_{j=1}^{n}(r_{i})_{j}}
, expanding in
y
at
y=0
and setting
y=1
in the end.
If necessary additional regulators
t_{j}^{\eta_{j}}
are introduced to ensure convergence of the integrals.
If there are no poles in the regulators after analytic continuation to
\eta_{j}=0
, they can be dropped directly.
This will be the case for all integrals under consideration in this work.
Generally, if poles in the regulators appear, these will cancel after combining regions.

The small parameter
y
in this work is associated with small masses
v_{ii}
in the angular integrals.
It will be introduced by multiplying each
v_{ii}
with an explicit factor of
y
.
For simplicity, we introduce the notation
C_{n,i}^{\text{LP}}
to refer to the leading power contribution from region
r_{i}
to the
n
-denominator angular integral
I_{1,\dots,1}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{r_{i}}I_{\underbrace{1,\dots,1}_{n}}\overset{\text{LP}}{\approx}C_{n,i}^{\text{LP}},

(14)

where “
\overset{\text{LP}}{\approx}
” denotes the leading power approximation in the masses
v_{ii}
.
With this notation the leading asymptotics of the integral
I_{1,\dots,1}
is given by

\displaystyle I_{1,\dots,1}\overset{\text{LP}}{\approx}\sum_{i}C_{n,i}^{\text{LP}}\,,

(15)

where the sum runs over all regions detected by
asy.m
.

2
Parametric integral representations for angular integrals

For the construction of suitable integral representations, we start with the one denominator case.
From there we successively increase the number of denominators.
Using Feynman parametrization we can systematically construct representations for more denominators, with
the integral representation of the one-denominator integral serving as the main building block.

2.1
Integral representation for one denominator

Writing the one-denominator integral

\displaystyle I_{j_{1}}(v_{11};\varepsilon)=\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\Omega_{3-2\varepsilon}(k)}{\Omega_{-2\varepsilon}}\,\frac{1}{(v_{1}\cdot k)^{j_{1}}}

(16)

in explicit spherical coordinates with
v_{1}
pointing in the
z
direction, it is
(
5
, Eq. (3.26))

\displaystyle I_{j_{1}}(v_{11};\varepsilon)=\int_{0}^{\pi}\mathrm{d}\theta_{1}\frac{\sin^{1-2\varepsilon}\theta_{1}}{(1-\beta\cos\theta_{1})^{j}}\int_{0}^{\pi}\mathrm{d}\theta_{2}\sin^{-2\varepsilon}\theta_{2}\,,

(17)

with
\beta=\sqrt{1-v_{11}}
.
Substituting
\cos\theta_{i}=1-2t_{i}
one obtains an Euler type integral representation
(
5
, Eq. (3.28))

\displaystyle I_{j_{1}}(v_{11};\varepsilon)=\frac{2^{1-4\varepsilon}}{(1-\beta)^{j_{1}}}\frac{\Gamma^{2}\!\left(\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon\right)}{\Gamma(1-2\varepsilon)}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}t_{1}\,t_{1}^{-\varepsilon}(1-t_{1})^{-\varepsilon}\left(1+\frac{2\beta}{1-\beta}\,t_{1}\right)^{-j_{1}}\,,

(18)

which can be expressed in terms of a Gauss hypergeometric function as
(
5
, Eq. (3.30))

\displaystyle I_{j_{1}}(v_{11};\varepsilon)=\frac{I_{0}(\varepsilon)}{(1-\beta)^{j_{1}}}\,{}_{2}\mathrm{F}_{1}\!\left(j_{1},1-\varepsilon,2-2\varepsilon;-\frac{2\beta}{1-\beta}\right),

(19)

where
I_{0}(\varepsilon)=\frac{2\pi}{1-2\varepsilon}
.

The Gauss hypergeometric function
{}_{2}\mathrm{F}_{1}
admits for the integral representation (see
(
58
, Eq. (15.6.2_5))
)

\displaystyle{}_{2}\mathrm{F}_{1}(a,b,c;z)=\frac{\Gamma(c)}{\Gamma(b)\Gamma(c-b)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\,t^{b-1}\,(1+t)^{a-c}\,(1+(1-z)\,t)^{-a}\,.

(20)

Hence, we have

\displaystyle I_{j_{1}}(v_{11};\varepsilon)=2\pi\,\frac{\Gamma(1-2\varepsilon)}{\Gamma^{2}(1-\varepsilon)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\,t^{-\varepsilon}\,(1+t)^{j_{1}-2+2\varepsilon}\,(1-\beta+(1+\beta)\,t)^{-{j_{1}}}\,,

(21)

which is of the desired form for use with
asy.m
.

However, we observe that the expression contains a square root
\beta=\sqrt{1-v_{11}}
of the mass parameter.
This complicates matters when we try to use the integral representation of the one denominator integral within a bigger integral, since the “mass” will depend on additional integration variables.
Fortunately, we can get rid of these square roots for the one-denominator integral by using the quadratic transformation (see
(
58
, Eq.
(15.8.13))
)

\displaystyle{}_{2}\mathrm{F}_{1}(a,b,2b;z)=\left(1-\frac{z}{2}\right)^{-a}{}_{2}\mathrm{F}_{1}\!\left(\frac{a}{2},\frac{a+1}{2},b+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{z}{2-z}\right)^{2}\right)

(22)

on the hypergeometric function in Eq. (
19
).
This gives
(
5
, Eq. (3.33))

\displaystyle I_{j_{1}}(v_{11};\varepsilon)=I_{0}(\varepsilon)\,{}_{2}\mathrm{F}_{1}\!\left(\frac{j_{1}}{2},\frac{j_{1}+1}{2},\frac{3}{2}-\varepsilon;1-v_{11}\right)\,.

(23)

Using the integral representation (
20
) on this form of the hypergeometric function, results in

\displaystyle I_{j_{1}}(v_{11};\varepsilon)=\frac{2\pi}{1-2\varepsilon}\,\frac{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{3}{2}-\varepsilon\right)}{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{j_{1}+1}{2}\right)\Gamma\!\left(1-\frac{j_{1}}{2}-\varepsilon\right)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\,t^{\frac{j_{1}-1}{2}}\,(1+t)^{\frac{j_{1}-3}{2}+\varepsilon}\,(1+v_{11}t)^{-\frac{j_{1}}{2}}\,,

(24)

which again is of a form compatible with
asy.m
.
Effectively, compared with the alternative representation from Eq. (
21
), we have interchanged the square roots in the kinematic variables for half-integers in the exponents.

By using the symmetry of
{}_{2}\mathrm{F}_{1}
in its first two arguments and interchanging the roles of
a
and
b
in Eq. (
20
) we also have the further equivalent representation

\displaystyle I_{j_{1}}(v_{11};\varepsilon)=\frac{2\pi}{1-2\varepsilon}\,\frac{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{3}{2}-\varepsilon\right)}{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{j_{1}}{2}\right)\Gamma\!\left(\frac{3-j_{1}}{2}-\varepsilon\right)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\,t^{\frac{j_{1}}{2}-1}\,(1+t)^{\frac{j_{1}}{2}-1+\varepsilon}\,(1+v_{11}t)^{-\frac{j_{1}+1}{2}}\,.

(25)

Depending on whether
j_{1}
is even or odd the square roots are distributed differently in the two forms Eq. (
24
) and (
25
).

The integral representations (
24
) and (
25
) will be the cornerstones for building up suitable integral representations for angular integrals with more denominators.
Note that we used the known full hypergeometric result for the one-denominator integral in deriving these integral representation.
Hence, in this case, the application of expansion by regions to extract the asymptotic expansion might seem circular.
Let us emphasize that for the following cases of multiple denominators no additional analytic input is required and we are able to establish integral representations also for the cases where a hypergeometric representation is not known.

2.2
Integral representation for two denominators

Now we consider the angular integral with two denominators,

\displaystyle I_{j_{1},j_{2}}(v_{12},v_{11},v_{22};\varepsilon)=\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\Omega_{3-2\varepsilon}(k)}{\Omega_{-2\varepsilon}}\,\frac{1}{(v_{1}\cdot k)^{j_{1}}(v_{2}\cdot k)^{j_{2}}}\,.

(26)

The key idea for extending the result for the one-denominator integral from the previous section to more denominators is a
Feynman parametrization
of the propagators.
This gives
(
5
, Eq. (3.69))

\displaystyle I_{j_{1},j_{2}}(v_{12},v_{11},v_{22};\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathrm{B}(j_{1},j_{2})}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}x_{1}\,x_{1}^{j_{1}-1}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}x_{2}\,x_{2}^{j_{2}-1}\,\delta(1-x_{1}-x_{2})\,I_{j_{1}+j_{2}}\!\left(v^{2};\varepsilon\right)\,,

(27)

where
v\equiv x_{1}v_{1}+x_{2}v_{2}
and
\mathrm{B}(j_{1},j_{2})=\frac{\Gamma(j_{1})\Gamma(j_{2})}{\Gamma(j_{1}+j_{2})}
denotes the Euler Beta function.
The mass
v^{2}
within the one-denominator angular integral is

\displaystyle v^{2}=(x_{1}v_{1}+x_{2}v_{2})^{2}=x_{1}^{2}v_{11}+2x_{1}x_{2}v_{12}+x_{2}^{2}v_{22}^{2}\,.

(28)

Using the delta function to integrate out
x_{2}
, we obtain

\displaystyle I_{j_{1},j_{2}}(v_{12},v_{11},v_{22};\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathrm{B}(j_{1},j_{2})}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}x_{1}\,x_{1}^{j_{1}-1}(1-x_{1})^{j_{2}-1}\,I_{j_{1}+j_{2}}\!\left(v^{2};\varepsilon\right)

(29)

where now
v^{2}=x_{1}^{2}(v_{11}-2v_{12}+v_{22})+2x_{1}(v_{12}-v_{22})+v_{22}
.

The last step is to perform a substitution such that the integration domain extends from zero to infinity.
A suitable transformation is given by

\displaystyle t=\frac{x_{1}}{1-x_{1}}\,,\qquad x_{1}=\frac{t_{1}}{1+t_{1}}\,,\qquad\mathrm{d}x_{1}=\frac{\mathrm{d}t_{1}}{(1+t_{1})^{2}}\,,

(30)

resulting in

\displaystyle I_{j_{1},j_{2}}(v_{12},v_{11},v_{22};\varepsilon)=\frac{1}{\mathrm{B}(j_{1},j_{2})}\int_{0}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t_{1}\,t_{1}^{j_{1}-1}(1+t_{1})^{-j_{1}-j_{2}}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle I_{j_{1}+j_{2}}\!\left(\frac{t_{1}^{2}v_{11}+2t_{1}v_{12}+v_{22}}{(1+t_{1})^{2}};\varepsilon\right).

(31)

Now we can plug in the integral representation (

\displaystyle I_{j_{1},j_{2}}(v_{12},v_{11},v_{22};\varepsilon)=\frac{2\pi}{1-2\varepsilon}\,\frac{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{3}{2}-\varepsilon\right)}{\mathrm{B}(j_{1},j_{2})\Gamma\!\left(\frac{j_{1}+j_{2}+1}{2}\right)\Gamma\!\left(1-\frac{j_{1}+j_{2}}{2}-\varepsilon\right)}

\displaystyle\quad\times\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t_{1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t_{2}\,t_{1}^{j_{1}-1}\,t_{2}^{\frac{j_{1}+j_{2}-1}{2}}\,(1+t_{2})^{\frac{j_{1}+j_{2}-3}{2}+\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\left[(1+t_{1})^{2}+(t_{1}^{2}v_{11}+2t_{1}v_{12}+v_{22})t_{2}\right]^{-\frac{j_{1}+j_{2}}{2}}\,.

(32)

This is of the form required by
asy.m
.

Using (

) instead of (
24
) for the one-denominator integral gives an alternative representation:

\displaystyle I_{j_{1},j_{2}}(v_{12},v_{11},v_{22};\varepsilon)=\frac{2\pi}{1-2\varepsilon}\,\frac{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{3}{2}-\varepsilon\right)}{\mathrm{B}(j_{1},j_{2})\Gamma\!\left(\frac{j_{1}+j_{2}}{2}\right)\Gamma\!\left(\frac{3-j_{1}-j_{2}}{2}-\varepsilon\right)}

\displaystyle\quad\times\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t_{1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t_{2}\,t_{1}^{j_{1}-1}\,(1+t_{1})\,t_{2}^{\frac{j_{1}+j_{2}}{2}-1}\,(1+t_{2})^{\frac{j_{1}+j_{2}}{2}-1+\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\left[(1+t_{1})^{2}+(t_{1}^{2}v_{11}+2t_{1}v_{12}+v_{22})t_{2}\right]^{-\frac{j_{1}+j_{2}+1}{2}}\,.

(33)

2.3
Integral representations for three and more denominators

The method employed for the two-denominator angular integral straightforwardly generalizes to three and more denominators.
Here, we will explicitly derive the integral representation for three denominators and afterwards give the necessary steps for the general case of

The three-denominator angular integral

\displaystyle I_{j_{1},j_{2},j_{3}}(v_{12},v_{13},v_{23},v_{11},v_{22},v_{33};\varepsilon)=\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\Omega_{3-2\varepsilon}(k)}{\Omega_{-2\varepsilon}}\,\frac{1}{(v_{1}\cdot k)^{j_{1}}(v_{2}\cdot k)^{j_{2}}(v_{3}\cdot k)^{j_{3}}}

\displaystyle I_{j_{1},j_{2},j_{3}}(v_{12},v_{13},v_{23},v_{11},v_{22},v_{33};\varepsilon)=\frac{1}{\mathrm{B}(j_{1},j_{2},j_{3})}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}x_{1}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}x_{2}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}x_{3}\,x_{1}^{j_{1}-1}x_{2}^{j_{2}-1}x_{3}^{j_{3}-1}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t_{1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t_{2}\,t_{1}^{j_{1}-1}(1+t_{1})^{-j_{1}-j_{2}-j_{3}}t_{2}^{j_{2}-1}(1+t_{2})^{-j_{2}-j_{3}}\,.

\displaystyle\times\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t_{1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t_{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t_{3}\,t_{1}^{j_{1}-1}t_{2}^{j_{2}-1}(1+t_{2})^{j_{1}}t_{3}^{\frac{j-1}{2}}(1+t_{3})^{\frac{j-3}{2}+\varepsilon}\,w_{3}^{-\frac{j}{2}}(t_{1},t_{2},t_{3})\,,

3.4
General structure of the leading asymptotics for
n
denominators

Looking at the regions found for two, three, and four denominators, we observe a rather simple pattern.
The following statements have been shown explicitly in the cases
n\leq 4
in the preceding sections and conjecturally hold true for an arbitrary number of denominators.
A formal proof for general
n
is beyond the scope of this paper.

For the
n
-denominator angular integral
I_{1,\dots,1}(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})
there are
n+1
regions contributing to its leading asymptotics.
Up to relabeling of the regions, there is one region
r_{i}
associated with each massive vector
v_{i}
which contributes with

\displaystyle C_{n,i}^{\text{LP}}=\frac{\pi}{\varepsilon}\left(\frac{v_{ii}}{4}\right)^{-\varepsilon}\frac{\Gamma(1+\varepsilon)\Gamma(1-2\varepsilon)}{\Gamma(1-\varepsilon)}\prod_{\overset{j=1}{j\neq i}}^{n}\frac{1}{v_{ij}}

(84)

and one mass-independent region
r_{n+1}
which is equal to the massless
n
-denominator integral

\displaystyle C_{n,n+1}^{\text{LP}}=I_{1,\dots,1}^{(0)}\,.

(85)

The leading power approximation of the
n
-denominator angular integral is therefore given by

\displaystyle I_{1,\dots,1}(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})

\displaystyle\overset{\text{LP}}{\approx}\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}C_{n,i}^{\text{LP}}=I_{1,\dots,1}^{(0)}+\frac{\pi}{\varepsilon}\frac{\Gamma(1+\varepsilon)\Gamma(1-2\varepsilon)}{\Gamma(1-\varepsilon)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{v_{ii}}{4}\right)^{-\varepsilon}\prod_{\overset{j=1}{j\neq i}}^{n}\frac{1}{v_{ij}}\,.

(86)

If all
n
masses
v_{ii}
are non-zero we know that
I_{1,\dots,1}
is free of collinear singularities, hence the
\varepsilon
-pole will cancel between the massive regions and the massless regions.
Therefore Eq. (
86
) implies for the pole of the
n
-denominator massless integral

\displaystyle I_{1,\dots,1}^{(0)}(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})=-\frac{\pi}{\varepsilon}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\prod_{\overset{j=1}{j\neq i}}^{n}\frac{1}{v_{ij}}+\mathcal{O}\!\left(\varepsilon^{0}\right).

(87)

For the case of
m
masses, say
v_{11},\dots,v_{mm}\neq 0
and
v_{m+1,m+1},\dots,v_{nn}=0
, the contributions of the regions
r_{m+1},\dots,r_{n}
vanish.
Hence we get for the pole of the
n
-denominator integral with
m
masses

\displaystyle I_{1,\dots,1}^{(m)}(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})

\displaystyle\overset{\text{LP}}{\approx}C_{n,n+1}^{\text{LP}}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}C_{n,i}^{\text{LP}}=-\frac{\pi}{\varepsilon}\sum_{i=m+1}^{n}\prod_{\overset{j=1}{j\neq i}}^{n}\frac{1}{v_{ij}}+\mathcal{O}\!\left(\varepsilon^{0}\right).

(88)

These predictions for the pole structure for general
n
as well as the leading asymptotics for small masses may serve as useful checks for future analytic calculations of multi-denominator angular integrals.

4
Conclusion

We have successfully applied the method of expansions by regions to angular integrals.
Using the code
asy.m
, we calculated the leading asymptotic behavior of angular integrals in the massless limit.
For the calculation of the contributions of massless regions Mellin-Barnes representations have been applied.
We established explicit results for the previously unknown cases of three and four denominators.
In both cases, we find the expected cancellation of poles and obtain the leading logarithmic contributions in all orders of the
\varepsilon
-expansion.
Based on these results we formulated a conjecture for the leading asymptotics and pole structure for a general number of denominators and masses.
These findings may serve as useful checks for future calculations of multi-denominator angular integrals.

Furthermore we observe an interesting connection between the regions detected by
asy.m
and an algebraic decomposition of angular integrals.
This allows for an identification of regions for the angular integrals in terms of angular integrals with a different scaling behavior.
