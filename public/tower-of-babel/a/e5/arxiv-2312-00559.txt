A geometric C 2 {C_{2}} -equivariant Bézout theorem
Steven R. Costenoble, Thomas Hudson

Abstract
. Classically, Bézout’s theorem says that an intersection of hypersurfaces in a projective space is rationally equivalent to a number of copies of a smaller projective space, the number depending on the degrees of the hypersurfaces. We give a generalization of that result to the context of C 2 C_{2} -equivariant hypersurfaces in C 2 C_{2} -equivariant linear projective space, expressing the intersection as a linear combination of equivariant Schubert varieties.

Introduction

The general nonequivariant Bézout theorem, as given in
[
7
]
, for example,
says the following. Suppose that we have
n
nonzero homogeneous polynomials
f_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq n
,
in
N>n
complex variables. Let
d_{i}
be the degree of
f_{i}
and let
\Delta=d_{1}d_{2}\cdots d_{n}
.
Each
f_{i}
can be thought of as a section of the line bundle
O(d_{i})
, the
d_{i}
-fold tensor power
of the dual of the tautological bundle over
\mathbb{P}^{N-1}
.
The zero locus of
f_{i}
is
H_{i}
, a hypersurface in
\mathbb{P}^{N-1}
, and the common zero locus
of all the polynomials is the intersection
\bigcap_{i}H_{i}
.
Bézout’s theorem then says that, in the Chow ring of
\mathbb{P}^{N-1}
, we have

\Bigl[\bigcap_{i}H_{i}\Bigr]=\Delta[\mathbb{P}^{N-n-1}],

that is, the common zero locus is generically rationally equivalent to
\Delta
copies of
\mathbb{P}^{N-n-1}
.
The classical case is when
n=N-1
, in which case the zero locus consists of
\Delta
points,
assuming the points are counted with multiplicity.
Our main result is Theorem
5.1
, which generalizes
Bézout’s theorem to the simplest equivariant context, considering actions
of the group
C_{2}=\{1,t\}
.
The result involves a number of interesting subspaces of equivariant projective space,
no longer just smaller projective spaces.

The usual approach to proving Bézout’s theorem nonequivariantly is to first
notice that
[H_{i}]=e(O(d_{i}))
, the Euler class of the line bundle
O(d_{i})
,
and that

\Bigl[\bigcap_{i}H_{i}\Bigr]=e(O(d_{1})\oplus\cdots\oplus O(d_{n}))=\prod_{i}e(O(d_{i})).

The Chow ring is computed to be
\mathbb{Z}[\,\widehat{c}^{\;}]/\langle\,\widehat{c}^{\;N}\rangle
,
with
\,\widehat{c}^{\;n}=[\mathbb{P}^{N-n-1}]
,
and
[H_{i}]=e(O(d_{i}))=d_{i}\,\widehat{c}^{\;}
,
from all of which Bézout’s theorem easily follows.
In place of the Chow ring we could use singular cohomology
H^{*}(\mathbb{P}^{N-1};\mathbb{Z})
, which is isomorphic
to the Chow ring in this case.

To generalize this to our equivariant context, we replace

\mathbb{P}^{N-1}
by the
C_{2}
-space
\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p+q\sigma})
,
the space of complex lines in
{\mathbb{C}}^{p+q\sigma}={\mathbb{C}}^{p}\oplus{\mathbb{C}}^{q\sigma}
, where
{\mathbb{C}}^{p}
has trivial
C_{2}
-action and

{\mathbb{C}}^{q\sigma}=({\mathbb{C}}^{\sigma})^{q}
with
{\mathbb{C}}^{\sigma}
being
{\mathbb{C}}
with
t
acting as
-1
.
In
[
3
]
we carried out the algebraic part of this project.
Equivariantly, we do not yet know the proper analogue of the Chow ring,
let alone its value on
\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p+q\sigma})
, so
in its place we use equivariant ordinary cohomology, with the extended grading developed
in
[
4
]
, and the calculation of the ordinary cohomology
of complex projective spaces given in
[
2
]
,
where we showed that the equivariant ordinary cohomology
of
\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p+q\sigma})
is free over the
RO({C_{2}})
-graded cohomology of a point and
gave an explicit basis.
In
[
3
]
we computed the Euler classes of sums of line bundles,
expressing them in terms of that basis.

In this paper, we redo the calculation in order to give a geometric interpretation
similar to the nonequivariant Bézout theorem.
There are several issues we need to address.
The first is to give geometric meaning to ordinary equivariant (co)homology.
As is well-known nonequivariantly, not all ordinary homology classes of a space
X
can be represented
by manifolds, that is, as the images of fundamental classes of smooth manifolds mapping to
X
.
However, there is the Baas-Sullivan approach that allows us to consider ordinary homology
as the bordism theory of manifolds with certain kinds of singularities.
Hastings and Waner generalized this approach to
RO(G)
-graded ordinary homology
and their results can be generalized further to the ordinary theory we use here.
This can then be used to interpret the ordinary cohomology of
G
-manifolds.
This interpretation is discussed in §
2
.

Another issue is that, nonequivariantly, the integers
\mathbb{Z}
play two coincidental roles:
On the one hand, we have the calculational fact that
H^{*}(\mathbb{P}^{N-1};\mathbb{Z})
is a free

\mathbb{Z}
-module, with basis given by the powers of
\,\widehat{c}^{\;}
.
The equivariant analogue is that the equivariant ordinary cohomology of
\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p+q\sigma})

is free over the
RO({C_{2}})
-graded cohomology of a point
(with a more complicated, but known, basis).
But note that the equivariant cohomology of a point is not simply
\mathbb{Z}
.
(See Appendix
A.1
for a review of equivariant ordinary cohomology
theory, including the structure of the cohomology of a point.)
On the other hand, because the coefficients are
\mathbb{Z}
,
the nonequivariant Bézout theorem is often thought of as
a counting result: The common zero locus of the polynomials
f_{i}
,
represented by the product of Euler classes,
is equivalent to
\Delta
copies of a projective space.
Given that the equivariant cohomology of a point is not just
\mathbb{Z}
,
our calculation in
[
3
]
, which expressed Euler classes in terms
of a basis, using coefficients from the cohomology of a point, is not suitable for counting in this way.
In §
1
we redo the algebraic calculation in a way that will
allow us to express the answer as a linear combination of classes represented by (singular) manifolds,
with integer coefficients,
which will be the form of our geometric Bézout theorem.

The singular manifolds that appear in our results are analogues of Schubert varieties.
Nonequivariantly, we can consider Schubert varieties in projective spaces, but
this is not usually done explicitly because
they are just smaller projective spaces.
Equivariantly, things get more interesting.
We believe that the Schubert varieties we introduce here will underlie similar calculations
for equivariant Grassmannians, but that is work that remains to be done. To get a feeling for what kind of equivariant spaces appear in our result, the reader may want to look at the discussion of the dimension-1 case in §
5
, where we provide an explicit description of the Schubert varieties involved in the formula.

This paper is organized as follows.
To keep this paper as self-contained as possible, in an appendix we give the facts we need about equivariant ordinary cohomology
with extended grading
and the cohomology of
\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p+q\sigma})
, summarizing results from
[
4
]

and
[
2
]
.
As mentioned above, in §
1
we redo the algebraic calculation of
[
3
]
, of the Euler class of a sum
of line bundles, in a form better suited to give us our geometric result.
In §
2
, we review the approach of Hastings and Waner to representing equivariant ordinary homology
by singular manifolds, and its generalization to ordinary homology with extended grading.
In §
3
we introduce the singular manifolds used in our Bézout theorem,
which are close analogues of nonequivariant Schubert varieties, and
in §
4
we discuss how various terms appearing in our calculations
from §
1
are represented by these Schubert varieties.
Finally, in §
5
, we put these results together to state our geometric Bézout theorem,
and give examples that show what kinds of singular manifolds appear in the codimension-1 case and the cases of
dimensions 0, 1, and 2.

1.
The Euler class of a sum of line bundles

In
[
2
, Proposition 6.12]
we gave a formula for the Euler class of a sum of line bundles over an equivariant projective space
\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p+q\sigma})
. The goal of this section is to provide an alternative expression for the same quantity that lends itself better to a geometric interpretation. We begin by establishing some notation. Let
F=L_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus L_{n}
be the direct sum of
n
line bundle
L_{i}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p+q\sigma})
. Since the Euler class is multiplicative with respect to direct sum, we have
e(F)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}e(L_{i})
.
In the nonequivariant case this computation is quite simple since one can always find
d_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
for which
L_{i}\cong O(d_{i})
and the additive formal group law in cohomology allows one to conclude that
e(L_{i})=d_{i}e(O(1))
. As a consequence, we get that the nonequivariant Euler class of
F
is given by
e(F)=d_{1}\cdots d_{n}e(O(1))^{n}
, where the product
d:=d_{1}\cdots d_{n}
is the degree of
e(F)
viewed as an element of
H^{2n}(\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p+q-1}))
.

In equivariant cohomology we do not have a formal group law at our disposal, but we can still calculate the Euler classes of line bundles. In
[
2
, Theorem 6.3]
, we showed that line bundles over equivariant projective spaces can be conveniently subdivided into four families, allowing us to describe their Euler classes in terms of the multiplicative generators
\zeta_{0}
,
\zeta_{1}
,
\widehat{c}_{\omega}=e(O(1))
,
\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}=e(\chi O(1))
, together with

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle:=e(O(2))=\tau(c)+e^{-2}\kappa\widehat{c}_{\omega}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}\qquad\text{and}

\displaystyle\chi Q

\displaystyle:=e(\chi O(2))=\zeta_{0}\widehat{c}_{\omega}+\zeta_{1}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}\,.

The exact expressions were obtained in
[
2
, Proposition 6.5]
and, by making use of
Q
and
\chi Q
, can be restated as as follows.

Proposition 1.1
.

In the cohomology of
\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p+q\sigma})
, for
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have:

i)

\displaystyle e(O(2k+1))

\displaystyle=\widehat{c}_{\omega}(1+k\tau(1)+ke^{-2}\kappa\zeta_{1}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega})=\widehat{c}_{\omega}+k\zeta_{1}Q;

ii)

\displaystyle e(O(2k))

\displaystyle=k\widehat{c}_{\omega}(\tau(\iota^{-2})\zeta_{0}+e^{-2}\kappa\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega})=kQ;

iii)

\displaystyle e(\chi O(2k+1))

\displaystyle=\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}(1+k\tau(1)+ke^{-2}\kappa\zeta_{0}\widehat{c}_{\omega})=\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}+k\zeta_{0}Q;

iv)

\displaystyle e(\chi O(2k))

\displaystyle=k\tau(1)\zeta_{1}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}+e^{2}=\chi Q+(k-1)\tau(\iota^{2}c).

Proof.

In each sequence of equalities, the first was proved in
[
2
, Proposition 6.5]
, while the second equality can be obtained by combining the definitions of
Q
and
\chi Q
with the Frobenius relation
b\tau(a)=\tau(\rho(b)a)
(equation (
A.4
)) and the values
of
\rho
given in (
A.2
) in the appendix.

For example, in (i), we have

\tau(1)\widehat{c}_{\omega}=\tau(\zeta c)=\tau(c)\zeta_{1},

which, with the definition of
Q
, gives the claimed equality.
The calculations in (ii) and (iii) are similar.

Finally, for (iv), we first need to use one of the relations in the equivariant cohomology of projective space, namely

(1.2)

\displaystyle\zeta_{1}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}-(1-\kappa)\zeta_{0}\widehat{c}_{\omega}=e^{2},

to replace
e^{2}
. The equality then follows by recalling that
\kappa=2-\tau(1)
and making use of the equalities
\rho(\xi)=\iota^{2}
,
\tau(1)\zeta_{1}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}=\tau(1)\zeta_{0}\widehat{c}_{\omega}
, and
\xi\cdot e^{-2}\kappa=0
.
∎

Let us now make more explicit the subdivision of the line bundles over
\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p+q\sigma})
. For every such line bundle
L
we say that it is of type
\dagger\in\{{\mathrm{I}},{\mathrm{II}},{\mathrm{III}},{\mathrm{IV}}\}
if it is isomorphic to one of the line bundles appearing in the corresponding roman numeral of the previous Proposition. It is also convenient to apply this subdivision to the line bundle summands of
F=L_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus L_{n}
.
Let
\mathcal{F}=\{L_{1},\ldots,L_{n}\}
and let
\mathcal{F}_{\dagger}
be the subset containing the summands of type
\dagger
. We set

\displaystyle n_{\dagger}

\displaystyle:=|\mathcal{F}_{\dagger}|\,,

\displaystyle F_{\dagger}

\displaystyle:=\bigoplus_{L_{i}\in\mathcal{F}_{\dagger}}L_{i}\text{\quad and }

\displaystyle d_{\dagger}

\displaystyle:=\prod_{L_{i}\in\mathcal{F}_{\dagger}}d_{i}.

By convention, we set
d_{\dagger}=1
whenever
\mathcal{F}_{\dagger}=\emptyset
.
As direct consequences of these notations one has

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle=n_{\mathrm{I}}+n_{\mathrm{II}}+n_{\mathrm{III}}+n_{\mathrm{IV}}\text{\quad and\ }

\displaystyle d

\displaystyle=d_{\mathrm{I}}d_{\mathrm{II}}d_{\mathrm{III}}d_{\mathrm{IV}}\,.

As in
[
3
]
, our calculations will depend only on the triples of ranks
(n,n_{0},n_{1})
and
degrees
(\Delta,\Delta_{0},\Delta_{1})
, where

\displaystyle n_{0}

\displaystyle=n_{\mathrm{I}}+n_{\mathrm{II}}

\displaystyle n_{1}

\displaystyle=n_{\mathrm{II}}+n_{\mathrm{III}}

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle=d

\displaystyle\Delta_{0}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}d_{\mathrm{I}}d_{\mathrm{II}}&\text{if\ \,$n_{0}<p$}\\
0&\text{if\,\ $n_{0}\geq p$}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\Delta_{1}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}d_{\mathrm{II}}d_{\mathrm{III}}&\text{if\ \,$n_{1}<q$}\\
0&\text{if\,\ $n_{1}\geq q$.}\end{cases}

Here,
n_{0}
is the rank of the fixed-point bundle
F^{C_{2}}
restricted to
\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p})
while

\Delta_{0}
is its nonequivariant degree when the rank is less than
p
. Similarly,

n_{1}
is the rank of
F^{C_{2}}
restricted to
\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{q\sigma})
and
\Delta_{1}
is its nonequivariant degree.
Because
d_{\mathrm{I}}
and
d_{\mathrm{III}}
are always odd,
\Delta_{0}
and
\Delta_{1}
have the same parity as
d_{\mathrm{II}}
;
in particular, they have the same parity, which is even if and only if
n_{\mathrm{II}}>0
.

In order for the Euler class to have a meaningful geometric interpretation, we impose the following
conditions, as we did in
[
3
]
.

Bézout Context 1.3
.

With notation as above, we assume that

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle<p+q

\displaystyle n-q

\displaystyle\leq n_{0}\leq n

\displaystyle\mathllap{\text{and}\qquad}n-p

\displaystyle\leq n_{1}\leq n.

We will give two versions of Bézout’s theorem, one in terms of the values above
and one in terms of the following values.
Thinking about the intersection of the hypersurfaces determined by our line bundles,
these are its nonequivariant affine dimension and the affine dimensions of its fixed sets:

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle=p+q-n

\displaystyle m_{0}

\displaystyle=p-n_{0}

\displaystyle m_{1}

\displaystyle=q-n_{1}.

Note that these depend only on the triple
(n,n_{0},n_{1})
and the values
p
and
q
.
The assumptions in context
1.3
are equivalent to the inequalities

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle<m

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq m-m_{0}\leq q

\displaystyle\mathllap{\text{and}\qquad}0

\displaystyle\leq m-m_{1}\leq p.

We will also need the quantity

\ell=n-n_{0}-n_{1}=m_{0}+m_{1}-m.

Our version of Bézout’s theorem breaks into cases depending on whether
\ell
is positive or negative.

In order to obtain
e(F)
it is convenient to first identify its factors
e(F_{\dagger})
.
We start our work with a few computational lemmas.
The first lemmas will enable us to rewrite expressions involving powers of
Q
.

Lemma 1.4
.

For
k\geq 0
one has

Q^{k}=2^{k-1}\Big(\tau(\,\widehat{c}^{\;k})+e^{-2k}\kappa\,\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;k}\,\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}^{\;k}\Big).

If
k\geq 1
we may also write this as

Q^{k}=2^{k-1}\tau(\,\widehat{c}^{\;k})+(e^{-2}\kappa)^{k}\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;k}\,\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}^{\;k}.

Proof.

The proof of the first equality is by induction on
k
, with the base case being
k=0
, for which the statement reduces to

Q^{0}=1=\frac{1}{2}\cdot 2=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\tau(1)-\tau(1)+2\Big)=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\tau(1)+\kappa\Big).

For the inductive step, we have

\displaystyle Q^{k+1}

\displaystyle=2^{k-1}\Big(\tau(\,\widehat{c}^{\;k})+e^{-2k}\kappa\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;k}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}^{\;k}\Big)\cdot\Big(\tau(\,\widehat{c}^{\;})+e^{-2}\kappa\,\widehat{c}_{\omega}\,\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}\Big)

\displaystyle=2^{k-1}\Big(\tau(\,\widehat{c}^{\;k})\tau(\,\widehat{c}^{\;})+2e^{-2k-2}\kappa\,\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;k+1}\,\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}^{\;k+1}\Big)

\displaystyle=2^{k}\Big(\tau(\,\widehat{c}^{\;k+1})+e^{-2(k+1)}\kappa\,\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;k+1}\,\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}^{\;k+1}\Big).

Here, we are using the fact that
\rho(e^{-2j}\kappa)=0
, so the Frobenius relation (
A.4
) implies that

\tau(a)\cdot e^{-2j}\kappa=0
for any
a
. Further,
\rho\tau(a)=2a
for any
a
, so the Frobenius relation
implies that
\tau(\,\widehat{c}^{\;k})\tau(\,\widehat{c}^{\;})=2\tau(\,\widehat{c}^{\;k+1})
.
Finally, we have the relation
e^{-j}\kappa\cdot e^{-k}\kappa=2e^{-j-k}\kappa
in the cohomology of a point.

When
k\geq 1
, we have
(e^{-2}\kappa)^{k}=2^{k-1}e^{-2k}\kappa
from the relation just mentioned, hence
the second equality in the statement of the lemma.
∎

Lemma 1.5
.

If
i>k\geq 0
, then

i)

\displaystyle\zeta_{0}^{i}Q^{k}

\displaystyle=\zeta_{0}^{i-k}\cdot 2^{k}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}^{\;k}

and

ii)

\displaystyle\zeta_{1}^{i}Q^{k}

\displaystyle=\zeta_{1}^{i-k}\cdot 2^{k}\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;k}.

If
0<i\leq k
, then

iii)

\displaystyle\zeta_{0}^{i}Q^{k}

\displaystyle=\zeta_{0}\cdot 2^{i-1}\,\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}^{\;i-1}Q^{k-i+1}

and

iv)

\displaystyle\zeta_{1}^{i}Q^{k}

\displaystyle=\zeta_{1}\cdot 2^{i-1}\,\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;i-1}Q^{k-i+1}.

Proof.

These equations follow by induction from the equalities

(1.6)

\displaystyle\zeta_{0}^{2}Q

\displaystyle=2\zeta_{0}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}\hbox to0.0pt{\text{\qquad and}\hss}

(1.7)

\displaystyle\zeta_{1}^{2}Q

\displaystyle=2\zeta_{1}\widehat{c}_{\omega}.

To see (
1.6
), we have

\displaystyle\zeta_{0}^{2}Q

\displaystyle=\zeta_{0}^{2}(\tau(c)+e^{-2}\kappa\widehat{c}_{\omega}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega})

\displaystyle=\tau(\iota^{4}\zeta^{-2}c)+e^{-2}\kappa\,\zeta_{0}^{2}\,\widehat{c}_{\omega}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}

\displaystyle=\tau(\iota^{4}\zeta^{-2}c)+e^{-2}\kappa\zeta_{0}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}((1-\kappa)\zeta_{1}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}+e^{2})

\displaystyle=\tau(\iota^{4}\zeta^{-2}c)+(1-\kappa)e^{-2}\kappa\,\xi\,\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}^{\;2}+\kappa\,\zeta_{0}\,\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}

\displaystyle=\tau(\iota^{4}\zeta^{-2}c)+(2-\tau(1))\zeta_{0}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}

\displaystyle=\tau(\iota^{4}\zeta^{-2}c)+2\zeta_{0}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}-\tau(\iota^{4}\zeta^{-2}c)

\displaystyle=2\zeta_{0}\,\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}.

The proof of (
1.7
) is similar.
∎

Lemma 1.8
.

\xi Q^{k}=2^{k-1}\tau(\iota^{2}c^{k})
.

Proof.

By Lemma
1.4
, the fact that
\xi\cdot e^{-k}\kappa=0
, and the Frobenius relation, we have

\xi Q^{k}=\xi\cdot 2^{k-1}\Big(\tau(c^{k})+e^{-2k}\kappa\,\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\>k}\,\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}^{\>k}\Big)=2^{k-1}\tau\big(\iota^{2}\,c^{k}\big).\qed

Lemma 1.9
.

In the cohomology of
\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p+q\sigma})
,
the class
\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;p-k}Q^{k}

\zeta_{0}

\displaystyle Q^{i}\cdot\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;p-k}Q^{k}

\displaystyle=2^{i}\,\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}^{\;i}\cdot\zeta_{0}^{-i}\,\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;p-k}Q^{k}

, and

\displaystyle Q^{i}\cdot\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}^{\;q-k}Q^{k}

\displaystyle\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;p-k}Q^{k}

\displaystyle=2^{k}\Bigl(\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;p-k}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}\tau(c^{k})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-e^{-2(k+1)}\kappa\xi\zeta_{0}^{-1}\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;p}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}^{\;k+2}+e^{-2k}\kappa\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;p}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}^{\;k+1}\Bigr)

Q\cdot\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;p-k}Q^{k}=2\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}\cdot\zeta_{0}^{-1}\,\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;p-k}Q^{k}

and the statement for

follows by iteration.
The second equality follows similarly.
∎

The next lemma will allow us to deal with powers of

Lemma 1.10
.

For

Lemma 1.11

2.
Singular manifolds

We now want to discuss how the various terms in Propositions
1.14
and
1.15

can be represented by manifolds in a way analogous to how varieties are used
to define the Chow ring. It is well known that manifolds
per se
cannot be used to define singular (co)homology,
but we can get around this by allowing limited singularities.

2.1.
Basics

In
[
8
]
, Hastings and Waner showed that ordinary
RO(G)
-graded equivariant homology could be
defined using bordism of manifolds with a certain kind of singularity, similar to the Baas-Sullivan approach nonequivariantly.
The Hastings-Waner result can be generalized to show that ordinary
RO(\Pi B)
-graded homology can be given
by bordism of singular manifolds as well.
We review the definitions and the results we need.
Our context for the moment is a finite group
G
, a
G
-space
B
, and
G
-spaces over
B
.
In the following, we use the notion of equivariant orientation
discussed in
[
5
]
.

Definition 2.1
.

For
\gamma\in RO(\Pi B)
, we define a
\gamma
-oriented manifold over
B
to be a smooth
G
-manifold
M

with a map
f\colon M\to B
together with a specified stable map
\tau_{M}\to f^{*}\gamma
of representations
of
\Pi M
, where
\tau_{M}
is the tangent representation of
M
.
We say that
M
has
dimension

\gamma
to mean that
M
is
\gamma
-orientable,
that is, there exists a
\gamma
-orientation
\tau_{M}\to f^{*}\gamma
, but we do not pick one.

Here, stable maps are defined with respect to addition of arbitrary (finite) representations of
G
.

Definition 2.2
.

We wish to define a
\gamma
-dimensional singular manifold of depth
n
over
B
, where
n\geq 0
,
which we do recursively on
n
.
A
\gamma
-dimensional manifold of depth 0 is defined to be a compact
\gamma
-dimensional manifold over
B
,
with possibly empty boundary.
For
n>0
, a singular manifold of depth (at most)
n
consists of a
G
-space
X
over
B
together with a decomposition

X=N\cup_{\partial_{1}N}M\varphi
, where

(1)

N
is a compact
\gamma
-dimensional manifold over
B
with (possibly empty) boundary
\partial N
;

(2)

\partial N=\partial_{1}N\cup_{\partial}\partial_{2}N
is a decomposition of
\partial N
into two
codimension-0 submanifolds sharing a common boundary
\partial=\partial\partial_{1}N=\partial\partial_{2}N
;

(3)

M\varphi
is the mapping cylinder of a
G
-map

\varphi\colon(\partial_{1}N,\partial\partial_{1}N)\to(P,\partial P)
,
where
(P,\partial P)
is a disjoint union of singular manifolds of depth no more than
n-1
,
each of which has dimension
\gamma-k
for some integer
k\geq 0
.
We call
P
the
singular part
of
X
.

Recursively, we define the boundary of
X
to be the
(\gamma-1)
-dimensional singular manifold

\partial X=\partial_{2}N\cup_{\partial\partial_{2}N}M(\partial\varphi)

where
\partial\varphi\colon(\partial\partial_{2}N,\emptyset)=(\partial\partial_{1}N,\emptyset)\to(\partial P,\emptyset)
is the restriction of
\varphi
.
We orient
\partial_{2}N
using the identification
{\mathbb{R}}\oplus\tau_{\partial_{2}N}=\tau_{N}|\partial_{2}N
with

{\mathbb{R}}
the inward normal.

If we wish to specify the singular parts of
X
in our notation, we will write

(X;P_{1},P_{2},\ldots,P_{n})
where
P_{1}
is the singular part of
X
,
P_{2}
is the singular part of
P_{1}
, and so on.

We say that a
\gamma
-dimensional singular manifold
N\cup_{\partial_{1}N}M\varphi
is

\gamma
-oriented
if we have specified a
\gamma
-orientation of
(N,\partial N)
.

We use the phrase “singular manifold,” without specifying the depth,
to mean a singular manifold of any finite depth.

Definition 2.3
.

A
\gamma
-dimensional singular manifold
X

has codimension-2 singularities
if its singular
part
P
has components of dimensions each no larger than
\gamma-2
.

Note that we put no further restrictions on
P
—in particular, its own singularities can
be of any non-negative codimensions.

A simple but important observation is the following.

Lemma 2.4
.

If
X=N\cup_{\partial_{1}N}M\varphi
is a
\gamma
-oriented singular manifold with codimension-2 singularities, then

\displaystyle H^{G}_{\gamma}(X_{+})

\displaystyle\cong H^{G}_{\gamma}(N,\partial_{1}N)

and

\displaystyle H^{G}_{\gamma}(X,\partial X)

\displaystyle\cong H^{G}_{\gamma}(N,\partial N).

Proof.

Consider the following part of the long exact sequence of the pair
(X,M\varphi)
:

H^{G}_{\gamma}(M\varphi_{+})\to H^{G}_{\gamma}(X_{+})\to H^{G}_{\gamma}(X,M\varphi)\to H^{G}_{\gamma-1}(M\varphi_{+}).

Because
M\varphi
is homotopy equivalent to
P
, which is a cell complex of dimension no more than
\gamma-2
,
the first and last terms are 0. Therefore the middle two terms are isomorphic.
By excision,
H^{G}_{\gamma}(X,M\varphi)\cong H^{G}_{\gamma}(N,\partial_{1}N)
, so the first isomorphism follows.

The second isomorphism follows similarly from the long exact sequence of the triple

(X,\partial X\cup M\varphi,\partial X)
.
∎

Note that
(N,\partial N)
is a smooth
\gamma
-oriented manifold with boundary,
hence has a fundamental class
[N,\partial N]\in H^{G}_{\gamma}(N,\partial N)

(
[
4
, 3.11.8]
).

Definition 2.5
.

If
X=N\cup_{\partial_{1}N}M\varphi
is a
\gamma
-oriented singular manifold with codimension-2 singularities,
we define
[X,\partial X]\in H^{G}_{\gamma}(X,\partial X)
to be the image of the
fundamental class
[N,\partial N]
under the isomorphism of Lemma
2.4
.
If we want to specify the singular parts, we will write

[X,\partial X;P_{1},P_{2},\ldots]
, or
[X;P_{1},P_{2},\ldots]
when the boundary is empty.

Up to this point we have been careful to use the letter
X
to denote a singular manifold, to prevent confusion
with the
M
used to denote a mapping cylinder. We will now start to use
M
rather than
X

to denote a general singular manifold.

Given any pair of spaces
(X,Y)
over
B
, a
\gamma
-oriented singular manifold over
(X,Y)

is a map
f\colon(M,\partial M)\to(X,Y)
over
B
with
(M,\partial M)

being a
\gamma
-oriented singular manifold with codimension-2 singularities.
We can then define oriented bordism of such singular manifolds in the usual way, using appropriate singular bordisms,
again with codimension-2 singularities.

More generally, we define a
stable

\gamma
-oriented singular manifold over
(X,Y)
to be
a
(\gamma+V)
-oriented singular manifold
(M,\partial M)\to(X,Y)\times(D(V),S(V))
over
B

for some
G
-representation

.
We call these stable manifolds because we identify such a manifold with its “suspension”

(M,\partial M)\times(D(W),S(W))\to(X,Y)\times(D(V\oplus W),S(V\oplus W))

for any representation
W
.
When
Y=\emptyset
, it is often useful to think of a stable manifold over
X
as given
by two maps, a map
(M,\partial M)\to(D(V),S(V))
(which we think of as the stable manifold) and
a map
M\to X
.
Write
\bar{\Omega}^{G}_{RO(\Pi B)}(X,Y)
for the resulting stable, oriented singular bordism groups of
(X,Y)
.
The proof given by Hastings and Waner generalizes in a straightforward way to show the following,
in which
H^{G}_{RO(\Pi B)}(X,Y)
denotes ordinary homology with Burnside ring coefficients.

Theorem 2.6

(cf.
[
8

)
.

The map

\bar{\Omega}^{G}_{RO(\Pi B)}(X,Y)\to H^{G}_{RO(\Pi B)}(X,Y)

taking a
(\gamma+V)
-oriented stable manifold

f\colon(M,\partial M)\to(X,Y)\times(D(V),S(V))
to

f_{*}[M,\partial M]\in H^{G}_{\gamma+V}((X,Y)\times(D(V),S(V)))\cong H^{G}_{\gamma}(X,Y)

is a natural isomorphism of homology theories.
∎

When
x\in H^{G}_{\gamma}(X,Y)
and

for a
(stable, oriented) singular manifold

,
we say that

is
represented by

(M,\partial M)
.
When
f
is understood, we will write
[M,\partial M]_{*}
for

.

Note that, if

is a

is represented as

, then

. That is, the map

is given simply by composition with representative
maps

The following results give some useful ways to think about singular manifolds and the elements they represent.
Suppose that
(X,Y)
is a pair of
G
-CW(
\gamma
) complexes, with
X^{\gamma+k}
being the skeleta
of
X
.
We then have that

H_{\gamma}^{G}(X,Y)\cong H_{\gamma}^{G}(X,X^{\gamma-2}\cup Y),

by looking at the long exact sequence of the evident triple.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
(X,Y)
be a pair of
G
-CW(
\gamma
) complexes and
let
M
represent an element of
H_{\gamma}^{G}(X,Y)
, so we have a map

f\colon(M,\partial M)\to((X,Y)\times(D(V),S(V)))

for some

If
M=N\cup_{\partial_{1}N}M\varphi
, where

is a
(\gamma+V)
-oriented manifold,
then we may assume that
\partial N
maps to

(X\times D(V))^{\gamma+V-2}\cup(X\times S(V))\cup(Y\times D(V))
, and then
the homology class represented by

is also represented by the map

f|N\colon(N,\partial N)\to(X\times D(V),(X\times D(V))^{\gamma+V-2}\cup(X\times S(V))\cup(Y\times D(V))).

Proof.

Because
P
is
(\gamma+V-2)
-dimensional, the restriction

is homotopic to a map
into
(X\times D(V))^{\gamma+V-2}
, with
\partial P

X\times S(V)\cup Y\times D(V)

throughout the homotopy. We can extend to a homotopy of

(X\times D(V))^{\gamma+V-2}
while
\partial_{2}N
still maps
into

This homotopy does not change the represented homology class (and can itself
be viewed as a bordism). So we may assume that

(X\times D(V))^{\gamma+V-2}\cup X\times S(V)\cup Y\times D(V)

Now

can be viewed as a bordism from

(X\times D(V),(X\times D(V))^{\gamma+V-2}\cup X\times S(V)\cup Y\times D(V))

showing that they represent the same homology element in

H_{\gamma+V}^{G}(X\times D(V),(X\times D(V))^{\gamma+V-2}\cup(X\times S(V))\cup(Y\times D(V)))\\
\cong H_{\gamma+V}^{G}((X,Y)\times(D(V),S(V)))\cong H_{\gamma}^{G}(X,Y),

∎

The gist of this lemma is that the homology class represented by a singular manifold
with codimension-2 singularities
is essentially determined by the nonsingular part.
This is the idea behind the following two lemmas.

For the first result, recall that an empty manifold can be considered to have any dimension.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
(P,\partial P)
be a
(\gamma-k)
-oriented singular manifold over
(X,Y)
,
with

, and let
(M,\partial M)
be the
\gamma
-oriented singular

for
\varphi\colon\emptyset\to P
.
Then
(M,\partial M)
is cobordant to the empty manifold, hence represents

Proof.

The restriction of
f\colon M\to X
to
M\smallsetminus P=\emptyset
agrees with the similar
restriction of the map
\emptyset\to X
, so the result is implied by Lemma
2.7

∎

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
(M,\partial M;P)
be a
\gamma
-oriented manifold with codimension-2 singularities over
(X,Y)
,
such that
(M,\partial M)
is itself a smooth
\gamma
-dimensional manifold.
Then
(M,\partial M;P)
and
(M;\partial M)
represent the same element in
H_{\gamma}^{G}(X,Y)

Proof.

Write

Then Lemma

∎

We will use this primarily in the nonequivariant case, where it says that considering a codimension-2 submanifold
as singular part doesn’t change the homology class.
Equivariantly, there will be interesting cases where we start with a smooth manifold
M
and
take a submanifold
P

equivariant dimension than

nonequivariant homology element.
See §

Finally, here is a useful general result that crystallizes the idea that it is the
nonsingular part of a singular manifold that determines what it represents in homology.

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
(M_{1},\partial M_{1})
and
(M_{2},\partial M_{2})
be two stable
\gamma
-oriented manifolds with

(X,Y)

Proof.

This follows directly from Lemma
2.7
.
∎

Although we have been talking about representing homology classes, our real interest is in representing
cohomology classes.

Definition 2.11

2.2.
Representing elements in the cohomology of a point

As first examples of how cohomology elements can be represented by singular manifolds, we look at several
elements in the cohomology of a point for
G={C_{2}}
.
(See the appendix for a summary of this cohomology.)
We regard the point as a closed 0-dimensional
{C_{2}}
-manifold, so Poincaré duality
is the identification
H_{C_{2}}^{\alpha}(S^{0})\cong H^{C_{2}}_{-\alpha}(S^{0})
.
It is easy to see in this case that products in
H_{C_{2}}^{RO({C_{2}})}(S^{0})

correspond to products of the representing manifolds.

2.2.1.
Elements of the zeroth cohomology

We start by looking at elements in

H_{C_{2}}^{0}(S^{0})\cong A({C_{2}}),

the Burnside ring.
These are represented by 0-dimensional manifolds, that is to say, finite
{C_{2}}
-sets.
There are two obvious cases: We can have a single fixed point, which represents the identity, 1,
or we could have a free orbit
{C_{2}}/e
representing the class
g=[{C_{2}}/e]\in A({C_{2}})
.
But these both assume the usual positive orientation.
Putting a negative orientation on the free orbit represents
-g
.

Orientations of a fixed point are more interesting,
because there are four stable orientations of any representation of
{C_{2}}
,
determined by the orientation of the trivial part and the nontrivial part of the (virtual) representation.
We analyze the remaining three cases using the fact that elements of
A({C_{2}})
are determined by their
nonequivariant restrictions and their fixed sets.
Consider a point with the orientation that is positive on the trivial part and negative on the nontrivial part.
This gives a negative nonequivariant orientation but a positive orientation on taking fixed sets.
The element of the Burnside ring this determines is
1-g
, thus a point with this orientation represents
1-g
.
Similarly, a point with the orientation that is negative on the trivial part and positive on the nontrivial part
represents
-1
, while a point with negative orientations on both parts represents
g-1
.
Note that the units in
A({C_{2}})
are exactly the four elements
\pm 1,\pm(1-g)
,
each of which squares to 1.

Put another way: For a point, changing the orientation of the trivial part multiplies by
-1

while changing the orientation of the nontrivial part multiplies by
1-g
.

2.2.2.
The element
\iota

Write
{\mathbb{R}}^{\sigma}
for the nontrivial 1-dimensional real representation of
{C_{2}}
.
Consider the element

\iota\in{\underline{H}}_{{C_{2}}}^{\sigma-1}(S^{0})({C_{2}}/e)\cong H^{\sigma-1}(S^{0};\mathbb{Z})\cong H_{{C_{2}}}^{\sigma-1}({C_{2}}/e_{+}).

Here is the only place, other than the appendix, where we explicitly think of cohomology as Mackey functor-valued,
so that
\iota
is an element of that Mackey functor evaluated at the free orbit
{C_{2}}/e
.
Via the indicated isomorphisms, we also think of
\iota
as
an element of nonequivariant cohomology graded on
RO({C_{2}})
via the dimension
homomorphism
RO({C_{2}})\to\mathbb{Z}
, and
as an element of the equivariant cohomology of
{C_{2}}/e_{+}
.
As an element of nonequivariant cohomology, it is represented by the
virtual manifold given by the
identity function
D({\mathbb{R}})\to D({\mathbb{R}}^{\sigma})
, with the representations there to
make the grading clear.
As an element of the equivariant cohomology of
{C_{2}}/e_{+}
, it
is represented by the
(1-\sigma)
-dimensional stable manifold

{C_{2}}\times(D({\mathbb{R}}),S({\mathbb{R}}))\to{C_{2}}\times(D({\mathbb{R}}^{\sigma}),S({\mathbb{R}}^{\sigma}))

induced by the nonequivariant identification
D({\mathbb{R}})\to D({\mathbb{R}}^{\sigma})
,
where we give
{C_{2}}\times D({\mathbb{R}})
the canonical (positive) 1-orientation.

2.2.3.
The element
\xi

The class

\xi\in H_{C_{2}}^{2\sigma-2}(S^{0})

can be represented by the
(2-2\sigma)
-dimensional stable singular manifold represented by the identity map
D({\mathbb{R}}^{2\sigma})\to D({\mathbb{R}}^{2\sigma})
,
where we give the source
M=D({\mathbb{R}}^{2\sigma})
the following
structure as a
2
-oriented singular manifold:
Let
U\subset D({\mathbb{R}}^{2\sigma})
be the open disc around the origin of radius
1/2

and let
N=M-U
, an annulus. Using the fact that
G
acts freely on
N
, we can give it
a
2
-orientation. (Note that we cannot do this on all of
M
, because of the fixed point at the origin,
where the tangent space is nontrivial.) Let
\partial_{1}N
be the inner boundary and
\partial_{2}N
be the outer
one, let
P
be a point, considered as a
2-2=0
-dimensional manifold, and let
\varphi\colon\partial_{1}N\to P
be the projection. Then

M
is homeomorphic to
N\cup_{\partial_{1}N}M\varphi
and this structure makes it a
2
-oriented singular manifold.
To emphasize this singular structure, we will use the notation introduced earlier,
M=(D({\mathbb{R}}^{2\sigma});0)
,
where
0
denotes the origin considered as the singular part of
D({\mathbb{R}}^{2\sigma})
.

To see that
(D({\mathbb{R}}^{2\sigma});0)\to D({\mathbb{R}}^{2\sigma})
represents
\xi
, we use that
\xi

is characterized by the fact that

\rho(\xi)=\iota^{2}
.
To apply
\rho
to
(D({\mathbb{R}}^{2\sigma});0)\to D({\mathbb{R}}^{2\sigma})
, we simply forget the action of
{C_{2}}
, while remembering
the singular structure. Now we are in a situation where we can apply Lemma
2.9

(with the trivial group acting),
to say that the nonequivariant map
(D({\mathbb{R}}^{2\sigma});0)\to D({\mathbb{R}}^{2\sigma})
represents the same element as
the identity map
D({\mathbb{R}}^{2})\to D({\mathbb{R}}^{2\sigma})
,
which represents
\iota^{2}
; see §
2.2.2
above.

We can also use this representation to see that
\xi^{C_{2}}=0
:
(D({\mathbb{R}}^{2\sigma});0)^{C_{2}}=(0;0)

as a (nonequivariant)
2
-oriented singular manifold and we can apply
Lemma
2.8
to conclude that
(0;0)
represents
0
.

We could try something analogous to represent an element in
H_{C_{2}}^{2-2\sigma}(S^{0})
inverse to
\xi
,
but it will not work:
Starting with the identity
D({\mathbb{R}}^{2})\to D({\mathbb{R}}^{2})
, we could try to make the source
D({\mathbb{R}}^{2})
into a singular

2\sigma
-oriented manifold. We can give the annulus

a
2\sigma
-orientation, but

would have to be
considered as a
(2\sigma-2)
-dimensional manifold, which is not possible.
This helps explain the fact that
\xi
is not invertible in
H_{C_{2}}^{RO(G)}(S^{0})
.

Positive powers
\xi^{n}
can be given a similar description, using
D({\mathbb{R}}^{2n\sigma})
with
singular part the origin.

2.2.4.
The elements
e^{n}
and
e^{-n}\kappa

For
n>0
,
e^{n}
is represented by the stable
(-n\sigma)
-dimensional manifold

0\to D({\mathbb{R}}^{n\sigma})
.
To see this, we use that
e^{n}\in H_{C_{2}}^{n\sigma}(S^{0})
is characterized by the fact that

(e^{n})^{C_{2}}=1
, and verify that

[0\to D({\mathbb{R}}^{n\sigma})]^{C_{2}}=[0\to 0]=1\in H^{0}(S^{0}).

Similarly, for
n>0
,
e^{-n}\kappa\in H_{C_{2}}^{-n\sigma}(S^{0})
is characterized by

(e^{-n}\kappa)^{C_{2}}=2
. We can represent it by the
{C_{2}}
-manifold
S({\mathbb{R}}^{1+n\sigma})
,
the unit sphere in
{\mathbb{R}}^{1+n\sigma}
,
with the following orientation:
Embed
S({\mathbb{R}}^{1+n\sigma})\hookrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{1+n\sigma}
and use as the orientation the identification of the stable tangent bundle
(thought of as the sum of the tangent bundle with the normal bundle, which is trivial) with

S({\mathbb{R}}^{1+n\sigma})\times{\mathbb{R}}^{1+n\sigma}
.
Nonequivariantly, this gives the usual orientation, but equivariantly it does something interesting.
Call the fixed point
(1,0)\in S({\mathbb{R}}\oplus{\mathbb{R}}^{n\sigma})
the north pole and
(-1,0)
the south pole.
Recalling the discussion of orientations of points in Section
2.2.1
,
the orientation at the north pole is positive in both its trivial and nontrivial parts.
However, at the south pole, if we transport the nonequivariant orientation of the nontrivial part
(that is, the tangent space)
from north to south, we see that the map to
{\mathbb{R}}^{n\sigma}
reverses orientation, while on the trivial part
the south pole is oriented in the positive direction. That is, the south pole is oriented positively
in the trivial direction and negatively in the nontrivial direction, the opposite of how we would usually
think about it in terms of an outward normal direction.
Thus, when taking fixed points, the two fixed points embedded in
({\mathbb{R}}^{1+n\sigma})^{C_{2}}={\mathbb{R}}

will both be oriented in the positive direction, hence their sum represents 2 rather than 0,
making
S({\mathbb{R}}^{1+n\sigma})
with this orientation represent
e^{-n}\kappa
as claimed.

We can also check from this description that
e^{n}\cdot e^{-n}\kappa=\kappa
:
The product is represented by the map
S({\mathbb{R}}^{1+n\sigma})\to 0\hookrightarrow D({\mathbb{R}}^{n\sigma})
, and there is an obvious homotopy
to a map transverse to the origin with discs of the form
D({\mathbb{R}}^{n\sigma})
at the two poles both
mapping by the identity. The inverse image of the origin is then the two poles,
with the north pole still carrying the orientation that is positive in both the trivial and nontrivial parts
and the south pole oriented in the positive direction on the trivial part and the negative direction on the nontrivial part.
Thus, from our discussion in Section
2.2.1
, the north pole represents 1 while the south pole represents
1-g
,
so their sum represents
2-g=\kappa
as claimed.

This discussion also gives us the case
n=0
, where we can write
\kappa=[S({\mathbb{R}})]^{*}
, where

S({\mathbb{R}})
is oriented as above.

2.3.
Representing elements in the cohomology of
\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p+q\sigma})

We now turn to the cohomology of finite projective spaces, as summarized in the Appendix.
Throughout we will write
B=\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{\infty+\infty\sigma})
and grade cohomology on
RO(\Pi B)

We noted in
[
2

that, if
0\leq p^{\prime}\leq p
and

\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;p-p^{\prime}}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}^{\;q-q^{\prime}}=[\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p^{\prime}+q^{\prime}\sigma})]^{*}=i_{!}(1)

for

So it suffices in many cases to examine how to represent elements

that are
not multiples of
\widehat{c}_{\omega}
or
\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}

In particular, if we want to represent the standard basis given in the Appendix,
then the only thing left to consider is powers of
\zeta_{0}
and

2.3.1.
The elements
\zeta_{0}^{k}
and
\zeta_{1}^{k}

For the purpose of representing the terms that appear in

1.14
and
1.15

we need to look at
\zeta_{0}^{k}
for

and at
\zeta_{1}^{k}
for

We continue to use the notation from Definition

Proposition 2.13
.

If
0<k\leq q
then

\zeta_{0}^{k}=[\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p+q\sigma});\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p+(q-k)\sigma})]^{*}.

If
0<k\leq p
, then

\zeta_{1}^{k}=[\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p+q\sigma});\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{(p-k)+q\sigma})]^{*}.

Proof.

Consider the case of
\zeta_{0}^{k}
; the case of
\zeta_{1}^{k}
is similar.
From
[
2
]
, we know that
\zeta_{0}^{k}
generates

H_{C_{2}}^{k(\chi\omega-2)}(\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p+q\sigma})_{+})\cong A({C_{2}})
, hence is determined by its restriction to
nonequivariant cohomology,

\rho(\zeta_{0}^{k})=1\in H^{0}(\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p+q})_{+})

and its fixed points,

(\zeta_{0}^{k})^{C_{2}}=(0,1)\in H^{-2k}(\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p})_{+})\oplus H^{0}(\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{q\sigma})_{+}).

Because
\zeta_{0}^{k}
lives in grading
k(\chi\omega-2)
, we need its representing manifold to have this codimension.
The dimension of the ambient
\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p+q\sigma})
is
p\omega+q\chi\omega-2
, so the representing manifold needs to have dimension

p\omega+q\chi\omega-2-k(\chi\omega-2)=p\omega+(q-k)\chi\omega+2(k-1).

The complement
\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p+q\sigma})\smallsetminus\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p+(q-k)\sigma})
has no fixed points over
\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p})
and, over

\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{q\sigma})
,
\chi\omega=2
, so we have

p\omega+q\chi\omega-2=p\omega+(q-k)\chi\omega+2(k-1)\qquad\text{away from $\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p})$},

which is part of what we need. The other thing we need is for the singular part to have integer codimension of at least two, but
the dimension of
\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p+(q-k)\sigma})
is
p\omega+(q-k)\chi\omega-2
, so its codimension is
2k
, hence it satisfies that requirement.

At this point, we know that
(\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p+q\sigma});\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p+(q-k)\sigma}))
is a singular manifold of the right dimension to represent
\zeta_{0}^{k}
;
now we have to check that it has the right restriction and fixed points.
Its nonequivariant restriction is the singular manifold
(\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p+q});\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p+q-k}))
.
By Lemma
2.9
(applied with the ambient group being trivial), this represents the same element
as the smooth manifold
\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p+q})
, which represents the identity, as required.
The fixed points are

(\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p+q\sigma});\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p+(q-k)\sigma}))^{C_{2}}=(\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p});\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p}))\sqcup(\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{q\sigma});\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{(p-k)\sigma}))

with the first component mapping to
\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p})
and the second to
\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{q\sigma})
.
Using Lemma
2.8
, the first component represents 0.
By Lemma
2.9
, the second represents 1.
Hence we have verified that we have the correct restriction and fixed points, so

\zeta_{0}^{k}=[\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p+q\sigma});\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p+(q-k)\sigma})]^{*}
as claimed.
∎

From the equation

3.
Schubert varieties

In this section we present some notations commonly used for Schubert varieties of Grassmannians and explain how these can be generalised to the equivariant world.
We will use them only in the special case of projective spaces, but we suspect they
will be useful more generally.

Let
\Gr(D,N)
be the Grassmannian of
D
-dimensional
{\mathbb{C}}
-linear spaces in
{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
. Inside of each Grassmannian there exists an important family of subvarieties known as Schubert varieties, which are usually indexed by partitions. In general a partition

\underline{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{l})
of length
l
is an
l
-tuple of positive integers

\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{l}>0\,,

the
parts
of the partition.
To specify a Schubert variety of
\Gr(D,N)
, a partition must satisfy two extra conditions, on its parts and its length:

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}\leq N-D\,\quad\text{and }\quad l\leq D\,.

Note that we allow the empty partition, of length 0.

Besides a partition, we must also choose a reference flag

V_{\bullet}=\Big(0=V_{0}\subset V_{1}\subset V_{2}\subset\cdots\subset V_{N-1}\subset V_{N}={\mathbb{C}}^{N}\Big)

with
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}V_{i}=i
.
We then define the Schubert variety

Y_{\underline{\lambda}}(V_{\bullet}):=\Bigl\{W\in\Gr(D,N)\ \Bigm|\ \dim_{\mathbb{C}}(W\cap V_{N-D+i-\lambda_{i}})\geq i,\ \forall\,i\Bigr\}\,.

The sum of the parts of a partition is the codimension of the associated Schubert variety, in other words, one has

|\underline{\lambda}|:=\sum_{i=1}^{l}\lambda_{i}=\text{codim}\Big(Y_{\underline{\lambda}}(V_{\bullet})\,,\Gr(D,N)\Big)\,.

Nonequivariantly, although Schubert varieties do depend on the choice of
V_{\bullet}
, their fundamental classes are independent of it and, as a consequence, the flag is usually omitted from the notation.
Equivariantly, different flags can give significantly different Schubert varieties,
so we will suppress
V_{\bullet}
from the notation only when it is well understood.

In the special case in which
D=1
this picture becomes much simpler, with the Grassmannian being
\mathbb{P}^{N-1}
and non-empty partitions
\underline{\lambda}
reducing to single integers
\lambda_{1}
such that

0<\lambda_{1}\leq N-1.

In this case, a point
W
of the Schubert variety is a complex line and the defining condition implies that
Y_{\underline{\lambda}}
is just the smaller projective space

\mathbb{P}^{N-1-\lambda_{1}}:=\mathbb{P}(V_{N-\lambda_{1}}).

(When
\underline{\lambda}
is empty,
Y_{\underline{\lambda}}=\mathbb{P}^{N-1}
is the whole projective space.)

So, nonequivariantly, the Schubert varieties in a projective space
\mathbb{P}^{m}
are very simple: they are
sub-projective spaces
\mathbb{P}^{k}
for
k\leq m
. Equivariantly, there are three classes of objects
mapping to
\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p+q\sigma})

we can consider as Schubert varieties, all of which will appear in our version of Bézout’s theorem.

3.1.
Free Schubert varieties

The first type of Schubert variety we consider is one induced from a nonequivariant variety.
If
\lambda_{1}\leq p+q-1
and

\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p+q-\lambda_{1}})=Y_{\underline{\lambda}}\to\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p+q\sigma})

is a standard nonequivariant inclusion, then we have the induced equivariant map

{C_{2}}\times Y_{\underline{\lambda}}\to\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p+q\sigma}).

The cohomology element
[{C_{2}}\times Y_{\underline{\lambda}}]^{*}
does not depend on which inclusion map we start with, as all such
are homotopic, so we will write

\mathbb{F}_{\underline{\lambda}}={C_{2}}\times Y_{\underline{\lambda}}

generically and consider the element
[\mathbb{F}_{\underline{\lambda}}]^{*}
in the cohomology of
\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p+q\sigma})
.

Because
[\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p+q-\lambda_{1}})]^{*}
can represent the nonequivariant class

\iota^{k}\zeta^{j}\,\widehat{c}^{\;\lambda_{1}}
for any
k
and
j
,
we can write

[\mathbb{F}_{\underline{\lambda}}]^{*}=\tau(\iota^{k}\zeta^{j}\,\widehat{c}^{\;\lambda_{1}}),

interpreting the equivariant dimension of
\mathbb{F}_{\underline{\lambda}}
as appropriate.
Thus, the fundamental classes of free Schubert varieties represent the images under the transfer of the additive generators of
the nonequivariant cohomology of projective space.

In some of the formulas it will be more convenient to refer to these Schubert varieties in terms of dimension as opposed to codimension.
To do so, assuming that an ambient space
\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p+q\sigma})
has been fixed, we will use the notation

\mathbb{F}^{p+q-\lambda_{1}}=\mathbb{F}_{\underline{\lambda}}\,,

where
p+q-\lambda_{1}
is the affine dimension.
We will also use the simplified notation

\mathbb{F}_{\lambda_{1}}=\mathbb{F}_{(\lambda_{1})}.

3.2.
Invariant Schubert varieties

Next, consider the case of an equivariant flag
V_{\bullet}
in
{\mathbb{C}}^{p+q\sigma}
, that is, one for which, for every
i\in\{1,\ldots,p+q\}
, we have

tV_{i}=V_{i}.

Note that we do not require
V_{i}
to be fixed pointwise, just that it is mapped to itself by the action. Thus, each
V_{i}
is an equivariant complex affine space, so there exist
0\leq p_{i}\leq p
and
0\leq q_{i}\leq q
, satisfying
p_{i}+q_{i}=i
, for which

V_{i}={\mathbb{C}}^{p_{i}+q_{i}\sigma}.

As in the nonequivariant case, we define the Schubert variety associated to the non-empty partition
\underline{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1})
to be

Y_{\underline{\lambda}}(V_{\bullet}):=\Bigl\{W\in\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p+q\sigma})\ \Bigm|\ \dim_{\mathbb{C}}(W\cap V_{p+q-\lambda_{1}})\geq 1\Bigr\}=\mathbb{P}(V_{p+q-\lambda_{1}})\,.

We will refer to these as the
invariant Schubert varieties
and we will write

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}^{+}=p-p_{p+q-\lambda_{1}}

and

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}^{-}=q-q_{p+q-\lambda_{1}}

to denote, respectively, the codimensions of the components of the fixed sets of
Y_{\underline{\lambda}}(V_{\bullet})
in
\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p})
and
\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{q\sigma})
.
Note that
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{1}^{+}+\lambda_{1}^{-}.

For the sake of readability we will usually drop the reference to the flag
V_{\bullet}
, but the reader should be aware that the dependence on the flag is crucial, even at the level of fundamental classes.
In fact, we saw in §
2.3
that

(3.1)

\displaystyle[\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p^{\prime}+q^{\prime}\sigma})]^{*}=\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;p-p^{\prime}}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}^{\;q-q^{\prime}}

and, in Corollaries
2.14
and
2.16
,
we pointed out that we can represent these elements multiplied by (small) powers of
\zeta_{0}
and
\zeta_{1}

by adding suitable singular parts consisting of smaller invariant Schubert varieties. Equality (
3.1
) can also be rewritten as

[Y_{\underline{\lambda}}(V_{\bullet})]^{*}=\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;\lambda_{1}^{+}}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}^{\;\lambda_{1}^{-}}

and similarly for the products with (small) powers of
\zeta_{0}
and
\zeta_{1}
.
Because this class depends only on
\lambda_{1}
,
\lambda_{1}^{+}
, and
\lambda_{1}^{-}
,
and assuming that the ambient space
\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p+q\sigma})
is understood, we will write
Y_{\lambda_{1}}^{\lambda_{1}^{+},\lambda_{1}^{-}}
instead of the more precise
Y_{\underline{\lambda}}(V_{\bullet})
.
With this notation,

Y_{\lambda_{1}}^{\lambda_{1}^{+},\lambda_{1}^{-}}=\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p-\lambda_{1}^{+}+(q-\lambda_{1}^{-})\sigma})

and

[Y_{\lambda_{1}}^{\lambda_{1}^{+},\lambda_{1}^{-}}]^{*}=\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;\lambda_{1}^{+}}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}^{\;\lambda_{1}^{-}}.

3.3.
Binate Schubert varieties

Finally, we consider the case in which the flag
V_{\bullet}
is not necessarily invariant under the action of
C_{2}
. For each level of the flag we can consider the intersection of
V_{i}
with its translate

I(V_{i}):=V_{i}\cap tV_{i}={\mathbb{C}}^{p_{i}+q_{i}\sigma}\,,

a
{C_{2}}
-subspace of
{\mathbb{C}}^{p+q\sigma}
.
We no longer need have that
i=p_{i}+q_{i}
, but
we have the following relationship between these numbers.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
i
,
p_{i}
, and
q_{i}
be nonnegative integers.
There exists a nonequivariant subspace
V_{i}\subseteq{\mathbb{C}}^{p+q\sigma}
of dimension
i

such that
I(V_{i})={\mathbb{C}}^{p_{i}+q_{i}\sigma}
if and only if

(3.3)

\displaystyle p_{i}+q_{i}

\displaystyle\leq i

\displaystyle i-q_{i}

\displaystyle\leq p

\displaystyle\mathllap{\text{and}}\quad i-p_{i}

\displaystyle\leq q.

Proof.

Note that (
3.3
) implies that
i\leq p+q
,
p_{i}\leq p
, and
q_{i}\leq q
,
so we do not need to include these as separate assumptions.

First, assume that there exists such a subspace
V_{i}
.
By definition, we have
{\mathbb{C}}^{p_{i}+q_{i}\sigma}\subseteq V_{i}
, hence we must have
p_{i}+q_{i}\leq i
.
Write

V_{i}=I(V_{i})\oplus W,

so that
W\cap tW=0
. We claim that
W+tW={\mathbb{C}}^{k+k\sigma}
where
k=\dim W
.
To see this,
note that
W+tW={\mathbb{C}}^{k+k^{\prime}\sigma}
for some
k
and
k^{\prime}
. Suppose that
k>k^{\prime}
, so
k>\dim W
.
Then
W\cap{\mathbb{C}}^{k}\neq 0
, because
\dim(W+{\mathbb{C}}^{k})\leq k+k^{\prime}=2\dim W<\dim W+k
.
But then we would have

W\cap tW\supseteq W\cap{\mathbb{C}}^{k}\neq 0,

a contradiction. Similarly, we cannot have
k<k^{\prime}
.
Now,
\dim W=i-(p_{i}+q_{i})
, so we get

\displaystyle V_{i}+tV_{i}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{C}}^{p_{i}+q_{i}\sigma}\oplus{\mathbb{C}}^{i-(p_{i}+q_{i})+(i-(p_{i}+q_{i}))\sigma}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{C}}^{i-q_{i}+(i-p_{i})\sigma}.

Because
V_{i}+tV_{i}\subseteq{\mathbb{C}}^{p+q\sigma}
,
it follows that we must have
i-q_{i}\leq p
and
i-p_{i}\leq q
.

Conversely, suppose that
i
,
p_{i}
, and
q_{i}
satisfy (
3.3
).
Let

\displaystyle u_{j}

\displaystyle=(0,\ldots,0,1,0\ldots,0;0,\ldots,0)

\displaystyle 1\leq j\leq p

\displaystyle v_{j}

\displaystyle=(0,\ldots,0;0,\ldots,0,1,0,\ldots,0)

\displaystyle 1\leq j\leq q

be the standard basis elements of
{\mathbb{C}}^{p+q\sigma}
.
Then we can take
V_{i}
to be the subspace of
{\mathbb{C}}^{p+q\sigma}
with basis

\{u_{j}\mid 1\leq j\leq p_{i}\}\cup\{v_{j}\mid 1\leq j\leq q_{i}\}\cup\{u_{p_{i}+j}+v_{q_{i}+j}\mid 1\leq j\leq i-(p_{i}+q_{i})\},

which is possible because of (
3.3
).
∎

We define the
binate Schubert variety
specified by
V_{\bullet}
and
\underline{\lambda}

to be the union of the following two non equivariant Schubert varieties:

\mathbb{Y}_{\underline{{\lambda}}}(V_{\bullet}):=Y_{\underline{{\lambda}}}(V_{\bullet})\cup tY_{\underline{{\lambda}}}(V_{\bullet}).

In general such a
C_{2}
-space will consist of two projective spaces,
\mathbb{P}(V_{p+q-\lambda_{1}})
and
t\mathbb{P}(V_{p+q-\lambda_{1}})
, which are swapped by the action and whose common intersection is
\mathbb{P}(I(V_{p+q-\lambda_{1}}))
. As in the previous section we can replace the reference to the flag by keeping track of
\lambda_{1}^{+}
and
\lambda_{1}^{-}
, defined the same way and again the codimensions of the fixed sets of
\mathbb{P}(I(V_{p+q-\lambda_{1}}))
in the components of the fixed set of
\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p+q\sigma})
, and we will write
\mathbb{Y}_{\lambda_{1}}^{\lambda_{1}^{+},\lambda_{1}^{-}}
instead of
\mathbb{Y}_{\underline{{\lambda}}}(V_{\bullet})
.
Now the subscript
\lambda_{1}
is not superfluous, as
it is possible to have
\lambda_{1}^{+}+\lambda_{1}^{-}>\lambda_{1}
.

We would like to able to interpret binate Schubert varieties as singular manifolds. However, in the dimension dictated
by the singularity having integer codimension, the resulting singular manifold turns out to represent the same
cohomology class as a free Schubert variety.
We can get something more interesting as follows,
which will lead to Corollaries
4.2
and
4.4
.

For convenience, and assuming that we have fixed an ambient projective space
\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p+q\sigma})
, we will now switch to an equivalent notation which highlights the affine dimension of the variety and of its fixed sets:

\mathbb{X}_{{p_{i}},{q_{i}}}^{i}:=\mathbb{Y}_{p+q-i}^{p-p_{i},q-q_{i}}\,.

For the purposes of Corollaries
4.2
and
4.4
, we would like this to be a singular manifold of (real) equivariant dimension

(3.4)

p_{i}\omega+q_{i}\chi\omega-2+2(i-p_{i}-q_{i})\sigma

(which implies a nonequivariant dimension of
2(i-1)
).
The singular part should be the intersection

\mathbb{P}(V_{i})\cap t\mathbb{P}(V_{i})=\mathbb{P}(I(V_{i}))=\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p_{i}+q_{i}\sigma}).

The action of
{C_{2}}
is free on the complement of the intersection, so we may consider the complement
to have dimension (
3.4
).
However, the intersection has dimension

p_{i}\omega+q_{i}\chi\omega-2,

and the difference of this from (
3.4
) is
2(i-p_{i}-q_{i})\sigma

which, when
p_{i}+q_{i}\neq i
, is not an integer.
Hence,
(\mathbb{X}_{{p_{i}},{q_{i}}}^{i};\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p_{i}+q_{i}\sigma}))
does
not form a singular manifold with codimension-2 singularities in our desired dimension.
We can fix this by taking a “desingularization.”
With those corollaries in mind, we are lead to the following definition.

Definition 3.5
.

Assume that
i
,
p_{i}
, and
q_{i}
are nonnegative and satisfy (
3.3
). Define

\widetilde{\mathbb{Y}}_{p+q-i}^{p-p_{i},q-q_{i}}=\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{p_{i}},{q_{i}}}^{i}=\begin{cases}{C_{2}}\times\mathbb{P}^{i-1}\sqcup\Bigl(S({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2(i-p_{i}-q_{i})\sigma})\times\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p_{i}+q_{i}\sigma})\Bigr)\\
\mspace{300mu}\text{if $i>p_{i}+q_{i}$}\\
\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p_{i}+q_{i}\sigma})\sqcup\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p_{i}+q_{i}\sigma})\\
\mspace{300mu}\text{if $i=p_{i}+q_{i}$.}\end{cases}

There is a map
\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{p_{i}},{q_{i}}}^{i}\to\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p+q\sigma})
defined as follows.
When
i>p_{i}+q_{i}
, we map one copy of
\mathbb{P}^{i-1}
onto
\mathbb{P}(V_{i})
, so that the other
maps to
t\mathbb{P}(V_{i})
.
On
S({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2(i-p_{i}-q_{i})\sigma})\times\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p_{i}+q_{i}\sigma})
, we take the map to be projection to the second factor followed by inclusion.
In the case of
i=p_{i}+q_{i}
, the map is given by the usual inclusion of
\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p_{i}+q_{i}\sigma})
on each component.

Because
S({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2(i-p_{i}-q_{i})\sigma})
has dimension
2(i-p_{i}-q_{i})\sigma
, the space

\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{p_{i}},{q_{i}}}^{i}
has equivariant dimension (
3.4
).
By definition, the image of
\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{p_{i}},{q_{i}}}^{i}
in
\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p+q\sigma})
is the binate Schubert variety

\mathbb{X}_{{p_{i}},{q_{i}}}^{i}
.

Note that, in this definition, we are using the orientation on
S({\mathbb{R}}^{1+n\sigma})

discussed in §
2.2.4
.
Using that, we could consider the case
i=p_{i}+q_{i}
to be a special case of the first part
of the definition, because, with this orientation,
S({\mathbb{R}})
represents
\kappa=2-g
,
hence the first expression represents the same element as
\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p_{i}+q_{i}\sigma})\sqcup\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p_{i}+q_{i}\sigma})

in the cohomology of
\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p+q\sigma})

when
i=p_{i}+q_{i}
.

We extend the definition as follows.

Definition 3.6
.

Assume that
i
is nonnegative and that
i
,
p_{i}
, and
q_{i}
satisfy (
3.3
),
with
p_{i}
and
q_{i}
possibly negative. Let

\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{p_{i}},{q_{i}}}^{i}=\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{\max(p_{i},0)},{\max(q_{i},0)}}^{i},

but with dimension still given by (
3.4
).
Similarly, we set

\widetilde{\mathbb{Y}}_{\lambda_{1}}^{\lambda_{1}^{+},\lambda_{1}^{-}}=\widetilde{\mathbb{Y}}_{\lambda_{1}}^{\min(\lambda_{1}^{+},p),\min(\lambda_{1}^{-},q)}.

We need to explain how we can continue to use (
3.4
) as the dimension when

p_{i}<0
or
q_{i}<0
. Suppose, for example, that
p_{i}<0
and
q_{i}\geq 0
, so we are setting

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{p_{i}},{q_{i}}}^{i}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{0},{q_{i}}}^{i}

\displaystyle={C_{2}}\times\mathbb{P}^{i-1}\sqcup\Bigl(S({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2(i-q_{i})\sigma})\times\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{q_{i}\sigma})\Bigr).

Because
{C_{2}}\times\mathbb{P}^{i-1}
is free, we may interpret its dimension as any equivariant dimension
whose underlying nonequivariant dimension is
2(i-1)
, as is true for (
3.4
).
The piece
S({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2(i-q_{i})\sigma})\times\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{q_{i}\sigma})
maps entirely into
\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{q\sigma})
,
where we have
\omega=2\sigma
and
\chi\omega=2
. Now
S({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2(i-q_{i})\sigma})\times\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{q_{i}\sigma})

has natural dimension
2(q_{i}-1)+2(i-q_{i})\sigma
, which agrees with (
3.4
)
on substituting there
\omega=2\sigma
and
\chi\omega=2
,
showing that
\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{0},{q_{i}}}^{i}
has the desired dimension.
A similar argument works for the case where
p_{i}\geq 0
and
q_{i}<0
.
If both
p_{i}
and
q_{i}
are negative, then we are looking at
\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{0},{0}}^{i}
, which is free,
so again we may interpret it to have the dimension given by (
3.4
).

Finally, we identify the element
[\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{p_{i}},{q_{i}}}^{i}]^{*}
.

Proposition 3.7
.

In the cohomology of
\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p+q\sigma})
we have

\displaystyle[\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{p_{i}},{q_{i}}}^{i}]^{*}

\displaystyle=\tau(\iota^{2(q-(i-p_{i}))}\zeta^{p-p_{i}-(q-q_{i})}\,\widehat{c}^{\;p+q-i})

\displaystyle\qquad{}+\begin{cases}e^{-2(i-p_{i}-q_{i})}\kappa\,\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;p-p_{i}}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}^{\;q-q_{i}}&p_{i}\geq 0\text{ and }q_{i}\geq 0\\
e^{-2(i-q_{i})}\kappa\,\zeta_{0}^{p_{i}}\,\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;p}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}^{\;q-q_{i}}&p_{i}<0\text{ and }q_{i}\geq 0\\
e^{-2(i-p_{i})}\kappa\,\zeta_{1}^{q_{i}}\,\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;p-p_{i}}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}^{\;q}&p_{i}\geq 0\text{ and }q_{i}<0\\
0&p_{i}<0\text{ and }q_{i}<0.\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;p-(i-q_{i})}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}^{\,q-(i-p_{i})}\bigl(\tau(\,\widehat{c}^{\;k})+e^{-2k}\kappa\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;k}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}^{\;k}\bigr)

where
k=i-p_{i}-q_{i}
. In particular, if
k=0
, one has

[\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{p_{i}},{q_{i}}}^{i}]^{*}=2[\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p_{i}+q_{i}\sigma})]^{*}.

In the alternate notation, we have

\displaystyle[\widetilde{\mathbb{Y}}_{\lambda_{1}}^{\lambda_{1}^{+},\lambda_{1}^{-}}]^{*}

\displaystyle=\tau\big(\iota^{2(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{1}^{+})}\zeta^{\lambda_{1}^{+}-\lambda_{1}^{-}}\,\widehat{c}^{\;\lambda}\big)

\displaystyle\qquad{}+\begin{cases}e^{-2(\lambda_{1}^{+}+\lambda_{1}^{-}-\lambda_{1})}\kappa\,\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;\lambda_{1}^{+}}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}^{\;\lambda_{1}^{-}}&p\geq\lambda_{1}^{+}\text{ and }q\geq\lambda_{1}^{-}\\
e^{-2(p+\lambda_{1}^{-}-\lambda_{1})}\kappa\,\zeta_{0}^{p-\lambda_{1}^{+}}\,\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;p}\,\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}^{\;\lambda_{1}^{-}}&p<\lambda_{1}^{+}\text{ and }q\geq\lambda_{1}^{-}\\
e^{-2(q+\lambda_{1}^{+}-\lambda_{1})}\kappa\,\zeta_{1}^{q-\lambda_{1}^{-}}\,\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;\lambda_{1}^{+}}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}^{\;q}&p\geq\lambda_{1}^{+}\text{ and }q<\lambda_{1}^{-}\\
0&p<\lambda_{1}^{+}\text{ and }q<\lambda_{1}^{-}.\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{1}^{-}}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}^{\,\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{1}^{+}}\bigl(\tau(\,\widehat{c}^{\;k})+e^{-2k}\kappa\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;k}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}^{\;k}\bigr)

where
k=\lambda_{1}^{+}+\lambda_{1}^{-}-\lambda_{1}
.
In the special case
k=0
one has

[\widetilde{\mathbb{Y}}_{\lambda_{1}}^{\lambda_{1}^{+},\lambda_{1}^{-}}]^{*}=2[Y_{\lambda_{1}}^{\lambda_{1}^{+},\lambda_{1}^{-}}]^{*}.

Proof.

We show the first equality, case by case.
Suppose that

p_{i}\geq 0
and
q_{i}\geq 0
.
If
p_{i}+q_{i}=i
, so
k=0
, the equality follows from
the second part of Definition
3.5
and the equality

[\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p_{i}+q_{i}\sigma})\sqcup\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p_{i}+q_{i}\sigma})]^{*}=2[\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p_{i}+q_{i}\sigma})]^{*}=2\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;p-p_{i}}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}^{\;q-q_{i}}.

Note that
\tau(\,\widehat{c}^{\;0})+e^{0}\kappa=g+(2-g)=2
.

So assume that
p_{i}+q_{i}<i
. We then have that

\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{p_{i}},{q_{i}}}^{i}={C_{2}}\times\mathbb{P}^{i-1}\sqcup\Bigl(S({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2(i-p_{i}-q_{i})\sigma})\times\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p_{i}+q_{i}\sigma})\Bigr).

Interpreting the dimension of the free part
{C_{2}}\times\mathbb{P}^{i-1}
to be given by (
3.4
),
we have

[{C_{2}}\times\mathbb{P}^{i-1}]^{*}=\tau(\iota^{2(q-(i-p_{i}))}\zeta^{p-p_{i}-(q-q_{i})}\,\widehat{c}^{\;p+q-i}),

the factors of
\iota
and
\zeta
being what is necessary to put this element in the correct grading.
For the other piece, we have

[S({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2(i-p_{i}-q_{i})\sigma})]^{*}=e^{-2(i-p_{i}-q_{i})}\kappa

by §
2.2.4
and

[\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p_{i}+q_{i}\sigma})]^{*}=\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;p-p_{i}}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}^{\;q-q_{i}}

by §
2.3
. Putting this all together verifies this case of the proposition.

Now consider the case where
p_{i}<0
and
q_{i}\geq 0
. We are then setting

\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{p_{i}},{q_{i}}}^{i}=\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{0},{q_{i}}}^{i}={C_{2}}\times\mathbb{P}^{i-1}\sqcup\Bigl(S({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2(i-q_{i})\sigma})\times\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{q_{i}\sigma})\Bigr),

but with the dimension taken to be given by (
3.4
), as discussed above.
The free part represents the same element as above, by the same argument.
The other part is

[S({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2(i-q_{i})\sigma})\times\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{q_{i}\sigma})]^{*}=e^{-2(i-q_{i})}\kappa\zeta_{0}^{p_{i}}\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;p}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}^{\;q-q_{i}},

where the factor of
\zeta_{0}^{p_{i}}
is is there to put the element in the correct grading.
Notice that we are allowed to insert this negative power of
\zeta_{0}
because we have the factor of
\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;p}
,
which is infinitely divisible by
\zeta_{0}
.

The argument for the case
p_{i}\geq 0
and
q_{i}<0
is similar.

When
p_{i}<0
and
q_{i}<0
, we have
\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{p_{i}},{q_{i}}}^{i}=\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{0},{0}}^{i}
, which is free
because
\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{0+0\sigma})
is empty.

For the last equality, we have first that

\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;p-(i-p_{i})}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}^{\;q-(i-p_{i})}\tau(\,\widehat{c}^{\;i-p_{i}-q_{i}})=\tau(\iota^{2(q-(i-p_{i}))}\zeta^{p-p_{i}-(q-q_{i})}\,\widehat{c}^{\;p+q-i}),

using the facts that

\rho(\widehat{c}_{\omega})=\zeta\,\widehat{c}^{\;}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\rho(\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega})=\iota^{2}\zeta^{-1}\,\widehat{c}^{\;}.

Now consider the term

\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;p-(i-q_{i})}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}^{\;q-(i-p_{i})}\cdot e^{-2k}\kappa\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;k}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}^{\;k}=e^{-2(i-p_{i}-q_{i})}\kappa\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;p-p_{i}}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}^{\;q-q_{i}}.

If
p_{i}\geq 0
and
q_{i}\geq 0
, this matches the formula we already proved.
If
p_{i}<0
and
q_{i}\geq 0
, we have

e^{-2(i-p_{i}-q_{i})}\kappa\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;p-p_{i}}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}^{\;q-q_{i}}=e^{-2(i-q_{i})}\kappa\zeta_{0}^{p_{i}}\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;p}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}^{\;q-q_{i}}

by iterating the calculation

\displaystyle e^{-2k}\kappa\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;p+1}

\displaystyle=e^{-2k}\kappa\zeta_{0}^{-1}\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;p}\cdot\zeta_{0}\widehat{c}_{\omega}

\displaystyle=e^{-2k}\kappa\zeta_{0}^{-1}\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;p}((1-\kappa)\zeta_{1}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}+e^{2})

\displaystyle=e^{-2k}\kappa\xi\zeta_{0}^{-2}\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;p}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}+e^{-2(k-1)}\kappa\zeta_{0}^{-1}\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;p}

\displaystyle=e^{-2(k-1)}\kappa\zeta_{0}^{-1}\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;p}.

The case
p_{i}\geq 0
and
q_{i}<0
is similar, and, in the case where both are negative,
we use that
\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;p}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}^{\;q}=0
.
∎

4.
Representing elements using Schubert varieties

Nonequivariantly, it is well known that the Euler class of a smooth bundle is represented
by the zero locus of a section of the bundle transverse to the zero section,
where we use “represented” in the sense of Definition
2.11
.
The same can be shown equivariantly and can be improved to allow cases where the
section is not transverse, but the zero locus is a singular manifold
with codimension-2 singularities as in Definition
2.3
.
We will not give the proof here, but take this as motivation for the following discussions.
Our main goal is to show how to represent various terms that appear
in Proposition
1.14
.

4.1.
The Euler class of
\chi O(2)

At the beginning of §
1
we introduced the Euler class

\chi Q=e(\chi O(2))=\zeta_{0}\widehat{c}_{\omega}+\zeta_{1}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}.

The following gives two ways to interpret this geometrically.

Proposition 4.1
.

\displaystyle\chi Q

\displaystyle=[Y_{1}^{1,0};Y_{2}^{1,1}]^{*}+[Y_{1}^{0,1};Y_{2}^{1,1}]^{*}

\displaystyle=[\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{(p-1)+q\sigma});\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{(p-1)+(q-1)\sigma})]^{*}+[\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p+(q-1)\sigma});\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{(p-1)+(q-1)\sigma})]^{*}

\displaystyle=[\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{(p-1)+q\sigma})\cup\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p+(q-1)\sigma});\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{(p-1)+(q-1)\sigma})]^{*}.

Proof.

The first two expressions follow from Corollary
2.14
.
They equal the third expression by Lemma
2.10
.
∎

We can motivate the third expression in the proposition by looking at sections, as suggested above.
Writing the coordinates of a point in
\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p+q\sigma})
as
[x_{1}:\cdots:x_{p}:y_{1}:\cdots:y_{q}]
,
the polynomial
x_{1}y_{1}
gives a section of
\chi O(2)
.
Its zero locus is precisely
\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{(p-1)+q\sigma})\cup\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p+(q-1)\sigma})
,
which is not a smooth manifold, but if we take
\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{(p-1)+(q-1)\sigma})
, the intersection of these two hyperplanes,
as singular part, we get a singular manifold with codimension-2 singularities.

4.2.
The Euler class of
O(2)

Fundamental to the formula in Proposition
1.14

is the element

Q=e(O(2))=\tau(c)+e^{-2}\kappa\widehat{c}_{\omega}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}.

Proposition
3.7
gives us the following as an immediate corollary.

Corollary 4.2
.

Q=[\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{p-1},{q-1}}^{p+q-1}]^{*}=[\widetilde{\mathbb{Y}}_{1}^{1,1}]^{*}.

∎

We can interpret this result in terms of a section of
O(2)
.
Assume for simplicity that
p>0
and
q>0
.
The section
s=x_{1}^{2}+y_{1}^{2}
has zero locus

\mathbb{P}(W_{0})\cup\mathbb{P}(W_{1})
where

\displaystyle W_{0}

\displaystyle=\{(x,y)\in{\mathbb{C}}^{p+q\sigma}\mid x_{1}=iy_{1}\}

\displaystyle W_{1}

\displaystyle=\{(x,y)\in{\mathbb{C}}^{p+q\sigma}\mid x_{1}=-iy_{1}\}.

Using the notation of §
3.3
,
W_{0}
is a model for
V_{p-1,q-1}^{p+q-1}

and
W_{1}=tW_{0}=tV_{p-1,q-1}^{p+q-1}
, so the zero locus of
s
can be identified with

\mathbb{X}_{{p-1},{q-1}}^{p+q-1}
.
Our first thought is to consider
\mathbb{X}_{{p-1},{q-1}}^{p+q-1}
as a singular manifold with singular part

\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p-1+(q-1)\sigma})
, but, as discussed in §
3.3
, the dimensions are wrong and this
does not give us a singular manifold with codimension-2 singularities in the correct dimension.

We can correct this problem by considering
\tilde{P}
, the blowup of
\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p+q\sigma})
along
\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p-1+(q-1)\sigma})
.
Explicitly, this can be modeled as the subspace of
\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p+q\sigma})\times\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{1+\sigma})
defined by

\tilde{P}=\{([x:y],[u:v])\in\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p+q\sigma})\times\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{1+\sigma})\mid x_{1}v=y_{1}u\}.

The proper transforms of
\mathbb{P}(W_{0})
and
\mathbb{P}(W_{1})
are

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbb{P}}(W_{0})

\displaystyle=\{([x:y],[u:v])\in\tilde{X}\mid x_{1}=iy_{1}\text{ and }[u,v]=[1:i]\}

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbb{P}}(W_{1})

\displaystyle=\{([x:y],[u:v])\in\tilde{X}\mid x_{1}=-iy_{1}\text{ and }[u,v]=[1:-i]\}.

We then have
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}(W_{0})
and
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}(W_{1})
disjoint and

\tilde{\mathbb{P}}(W_{0})\cup\tilde{\mathbb{P}}(W_{1})\approx{C_{2}}\times\mathbb{P}^{p+q-2}
.
The exceptional divisor in
\tilde{P}
is
E=\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p-1+(q-1)\sigma})\times\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{1+\sigma})
,
which intersects
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}(W_{0})
in
\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p-1+(q-1)\sigma})\times[1:i]
and intersects

\tilde{\mathbb{P}}(W_{1})
in
\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p-1+(q-1)\sigma})\times[1:-i]
.
These intersections are freely interchanged by
{C_{2}}
giving a copy of

{C_{2}}\times\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p-1+(q-1)\sigma})={C_{2}}\times\mathbb{P}^{p+q-3}
.
This allows us to consider

(\tilde{\mathbb{P}}(W_{0})\cup\tilde{\mathbb{P}}(W_{1})\cup E;{C_{2}}\times\mathbb{P}^{p+q-3})

as a singular manifold with codimension-2 singularities.
We can remove the singularities using Lemma
2.9
, and the result
is exactly
\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{p-1},{q-1}}^{p+q-1}
.

Note that this differs from the nonequivariant algebro-geometric analysis, which would allow us
to replace
\mathbb{P}(W_{0})\cup\mathbb{P}(W_{1})
with
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}(W_{0})\cup\tilde{\mathbb{P}}(W_{1})
, with no contribution from the
exceptional divisor
E
. Equivariantly, and using ordinary cohomology, we cannot neglect
the exceptional divisor as it contributes the term
e^{-2}\kappa\widehat{c}_{\omega}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}
to
Q
.
Note that
e^{-2}\kappa
restricts to 0 nonequivariantly, recovering the classical result.

One further thought about this example: We could also consider the section of
O(2)
given by
the polynomial
\sum_{i=1}^{p}x_{i}^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{q}y_{j}^{2}
. Assuming
p+q>2
, this is a smooth
section with zero locus the Fermat hypersurface, which we could use as a representative of
Q
.
Taking the proper transform in
\tilde{P}
and letting the polynomial deform to
x_{1}^{2}+y_{1}^{2}
,
we can see that the Fermat hypersurface deforms to
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}(W_{0})\cup\tilde{\mathbb{P}}(W_{1})\cup E
.

4.3.
Powers of
Q

We also need to interpret
Q^{k}
for
2\leq k<p+q
.
By Lemma
1.4
, we can write

(4.3)

Q^{k}=2^{k-1}(\tau(c^{k})+e^{-2k}\kappa\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;k}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}^{\;k}).

Proposition
3.7
has the following corollary.

Corollary 4.4
.

For
0\leq k<p+q
,

Q^{k}=2^{k-1}[\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{p-k},{q-k}}^{p+q-k}]^{*}=2^{k-1}[\widetilde{\mathbb{Y}}_{k}^{k,k}]^{*}.

∎

We don’t have a good interpretation of this representation of
Q^{k}
in terms of sections of
kO(2)
.
One issue is that the sphere
S({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2k})
that appears would not arise via any
algebro-geometric construction.

4.4.
Powers of
Q
times
\zeta

The remaining terms that need to be interpreted have the form
\zeta_{0}Q^{k}
or
\zeta_{1}Q^{k}
with
1\leq k<p+q
.

Proposition 4.5
.

For
0\leq k<p+q
,

\displaystyle\zeta_{0}Q^{k}

\displaystyle=2^{k-1}[\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{p-k},{q-k}}^{p+q-k};\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{p-k},{q-k-1}}^{p+q-k-1}]^{*}=2^{k-1}[\widetilde{\mathbb{Y}}_{k}^{k,k};\widetilde{\mathbb{Y}}_{k+1}^{k,k+1}]^{*}

\displaystyle\zeta_{1}Q^{k}

\displaystyle=2^{k-1}[\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{p-k},{q-k}}^{p+q-k};\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{p-k-1},{q-k}}^{p+q-k-1}]^{*}=2^{k-1}[\widetilde{\mathbb{Y}}_{k}^{k,k};\widetilde{\mathbb{Y}}_{k+1}^{k+1,k}]^{*}

Proof.

We look at
\zeta_{0}Q^{k}
, the case of
\zeta_{1}Q^{k}
being similar.

We have

Q^{k}=2^{k-1}[\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{p-k},{q-k}}^{p+q-k}]^{*}

from Corollary
4.4
, so we need to determine
\zeta_{0}[\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{p-k},{q-k}}^{p+q-k}]^{*}
.

If
k=0
, the result follows from Proposition
2.13
, so assume that
k>0
.
Consider the case
k\leq p
and
k\leq q
, so

\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{p-k},{q-k}}^{p+q-k}={C_{2}}\times\mathbb{P}^{p+q-k-1}\sqcup\Bigl(S({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2k\sigma})\times\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p-k+(q-k)\sigma})\Bigr).

For the free part, we have

\displaystyle\zeta_{0}[{C_{2}}\times\mathbb{P}^{p+q-k-1}]^{*}

\displaystyle=[{C_{2}}\times\mathbb{P}^{p+q-k-1}]^{*}

\displaystyle=[{C_{2}}\times\mathbb{P}^{p+q-k-1};{C_{2}}\times\mathbb{P}^{p+q-k-2}]^{*}

where the first equality involves a reinterpretation of dimension and the second follows
from Lemma
2.9
.
For the other part, we have

\zeta_{0}[\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p-k+(q-k)\sigma})]^{*}=[\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p-k+(q-k)\sigma});\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p-k+(q-k-1)\sigma})]^{*}

by Corollary
2.14
. Putting these representations together shows the proposition
in this case.

The other cases, where
k>p
or
k>q
, follow similarly.
∎

We note the following special cases where the representation simplifies.

Corollary 4.6
.

If
k\geq p
, one has

[\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{p-k},{q-k}}^{p+q-k};\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{p-k},{q-k-1}}^{p+q-k-1}]^{*}=[\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{p-k},{q-k}}^{p+q-k}]^{*},

which implies

\zeta_{0}Q^{k}=2^{k-1}[\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{p-k},{q-k}}^{p+q-k}]^{*}=2^{k-1}[\widetilde{\mathbb{Y}}_{k}^{k,k}]^{*}.

Similarly, if
k\geq q
, one has

[\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{p-k},{q-k}}^{p+q-k};\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{p-k-1},{q-k}}^{p+q-k-1}]^{*}=[\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{p-k},{q-k}}^{p+q-k}]^{*},

which implies

\zeta_{1}Q^{k}=2^{k-1}[\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{p-k},{q-k}}^{p+q-k}]^{*}=2^{k-1}[\widetilde{\mathbb{Y}}_{k}^{k,k}]^{*}.

Proof.

Consider the case
k\geq p
. Then we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{p-k},{q-k}}^{p+q-k}

\displaystyle={C_{2}}\times\mathbb{P}^{p+q-k}\sqcup\Bigl(S({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2k\sigma})\times\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{(q-k)\sigma})\Bigr)

and

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{p-k},{q-k-1}}^{p+q-k-1}

\displaystyle={C_{2}}\times\mathbb{P}^{p+q-k-1}\sqcup\Bigl(S({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2k\sigma})\times\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{(q-k-1)\sigma})\Bigr)

The equality

[\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{p-k},{q-k}}^{p+q-k};\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{p-k},{q-k-1}}^{p+q-k-1}]^{*}=[\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{p-k},{q-k}}^{p+q-k}]^{*}

then follows from Lemma
2.9
.
Note that this does not work in the case
k<p
because it would require a reinterpretation of the dimension
of
\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{p-k},{q-k}}^{p+q-k}
that is not possible.
The case
k\geq q
is similar.
∎

5.
A geometric
{C_{2}}
-Bézout theorem

We can now state and prove our equivariant version of Bézout’s theorem,
using the equivariant Schubert varieties introduced in §
3
.

Theorem 5.1

(Bézout’s Theorem)
.

Assume Context
1.3
and the notation around it.

(1)

If
\ell=m_{0}+m_{1}-m\leq 0
, let
\Delta_{\min}=\min(\Delta_{0},\Delta_{1})
and
\Delta_{\max}=\max(\Delta_{0},\Delta_{1})
. Then

\displaystyle e(F)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta_{\min}}{2}[\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{m_{0}},{m_{1}}}^{m}]^{*}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{\Delta_{0}-\Delta_{\min}}{2}[\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{m_{0}},{m_{1}-1}}^{m};\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{m_{0}-1},{m_{1}-1}}^{m-1}]^{*}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{\Delta_{1}-\Delta_{\min}}{2}[\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{m_{0}-1},{m_{1}}}^{m};\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{m_{0}-1},{m_{1}-1}}^{m-1}]^{*}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{\Delta-\Delta_{\max}}{2}[\mathbb{F}^{m}]^{*}.

This formula simplifies in the following special cases:

e(F)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{\Delta_{1}}{2}[\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{0},{m_{1}}}^{m}]^{*}+\dfrac{\Delta-\Delta_{1}}{2}[\mathbb{F}^{m}]^{*}&\text{if $m_{0}\leq 0$}\\[12.91663pt]
\dfrac{\Delta_{0}}{2}[\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{m_{0}},{0}}^{m}]^{*}+\dfrac{\Delta-\Delta_{0}}{2}[\mathbb{F}^{m}]^{*}&\text{if $m_{1}\leq 0$}\\[12.91663pt]
\dfrac{\Delta}{2}[\mathbb{F}^{m}]^{*}&\text{if $m_{0}\leq 0$ and $m_{1}\leq 0$.}\end{cases}

(Here, we need to interpret
\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{0},{m_{1}}}^{m}
as having the correct dimension, and
similarly for
\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{m_{0}},{0}}^{m}
.)

(2)

If
\ell>0
, let
\epsilon=\overline{\Delta}_{0}
. Then

\displaystyle e(F)

\displaystyle=\epsilon\sum_{j=1}^{\ell-1}\overline{\binom{\ell}{j}}[\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{m_{0}-j+(m-m_{0}+j)\sigma});

\displaystyle\mspace{120mu}\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{m-m_{1}+(m-m_{0}+j)\sigma})\cup\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{m_{0}-j+(m-m_{0})\sigma});

\displaystyle\mspace{120mu}\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{m-m_{1}+(m-m_{0})\sigma})]^{*}

\displaystyle\qquad+\Delta_{0}[\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{m_{0}+(m-m_{0})\sigma});\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{m-m_{1}+(m-m_{0})\sigma})]^{*}

\displaystyle\qquad+\Delta_{1}[\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{m-m_{1}+m_{1}\sigma});\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{m-m_{1}+(m-m_{0})\sigma})]^{*}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{\Delta-\Delta_{0}-\Delta_{1}-\epsilon(2^{\beta(\ell)}-2)}{2}[\mathbb{F}^{m}]^{*}

We can also write the result in terms of codimensions, as follows.

(1)

If
\ell=n-n_{0}-n_{1}\leq 0
, let
\Delta_{\min}=\min(\Delta_{0},\Delta_{1})
and
\Delta_{\max}=\max(\Delta_{0},\Delta_{1})
. Then

\displaystyle e(F)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta_{\min}}{2}[\widetilde{\mathbb{Y}}_{n}^{n_{0},n_{1}}]^{*}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{\Delta_{0}-\Delta_{\min}}{2}[\widetilde{\mathbb{Y}}_{n}^{n_{0},n_{1}+1};\widetilde{\mathbb{Y}}_{n+1}^{n_{0}+1,n_{1}+1}]^{*}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{\Delta_{1}-\Delta_{\min}}{2}[\widetilde{\mathbb{Y}}_{n}^{n_{0}+1,n_{1}};\widetilde{\mathbb{Y}}_{n+1}^{n_{0}+1,n_{1}+1}]^{*}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{\Delta-\Delta_{\max}}{2}[\mathbb{F}_{n}]^{*}.

This formula simplifies in the following special cases:

e(F)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{\Delta_{1}}{2}[\widetilde{\mathbb{Y}}_{n}^{p,n_{1}}]^{*}+\dfrac{\Delta-\Delta_{1}}{2}[\mathbb{F}_{n}]^{*}&\text{if $n_{0}\geq p$}\\[12.91663pt]
\dfrac{\Delta_{0}}{2}[\widetilde{\mathbb{Y}}_{n}^{n_{0},q}]^{*}+\dfrac{\Delta-\Delta_{0}}{2}[\mathbb{F}_{n}]^{*}&\text{if $n_{1}\geq q$}\\[12.91663pt]
\dfrac{\Delta}{2}[\mathbb{F}_{n}]^{*}&\text{if $n_{0}\geq p$ and $n_{1}\geq q$.}\end{cases}

(Here, we need to interpret
\widetilde{\mathbb{Y}}_{n}^{p,n_{1}}
as having the correct dimension, and
similarly for
\widetilde{\mathbb{Y}}_{n}^{n_{0},q}
.)

(2)

If
\ell>0
, let
\epsilon=\overline{\Delta}_{0}
. Then

\displaystyle e(F)

\displaystyle=\epsilon\sum_{j=1}^{\ell-1}\overline{\binom{\ell}{j}}\Big[Y_{n}^{n_{0}+j,n-n_{0}-j};\ Y_{n+\ell-j}^{n-n_{1},n-n_{0}-j}\cup Y_{n+j}^{n_{0}+j,n-n_{0}};\ Y_{n+\ell}^{n-n_{1},n-n_{0}}\Big]^{*}

\displaystyle\qquad+\Delta_{0}[Y_{n}^{n_{0},n-n_{0}};Y_{n+\ell}^{n-n_{1},n-n_{0}}]^{*}+\Delta_{1}[Y_{n}^{n-n_{1},n_{1}};Y_{n+\ell}^{n-n_{1},n-n_{0}}]^{*}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{\Delta-\Delta_{0}-\Delta_{1}-\epsilon(2^{\beta(\ell)}-2)}{2}[\mathbb{F}_{n}]^{*}.

Proof.

Suppose first that
m_{0}+m_{1}\leq m
.
Then
e(F)
is given by Proposition
1.14
:

\displaystyle e(F)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta_{\min}}{2^{\ell}}\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;k_{1}}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}^{\;k_{0}}Q^{\ell}+\frac{\Delta_{0}-\Delta_{\min}}{2^{\ell+1}}\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;k_{1}-1}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}^{\;k_{0}}\zeta_{1}Q^{\ell+1}

\displaystyle\qquad{}+\frac{\Delta_{1}-\Delta_{\min}}{2^{\ell+1}}\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;k_{1}}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}^{\;k_{0}-1}\zeta_{0}Q^{\ell+1}+\frac{\Delta-\Delta_{\max}}{2}\tau(\iota^{2k_{0}}\zeta^{k_{1}-k_{0}}\,\widehat{c}^{\;p+q-m})

where

\displaystyle k_{0}

\displaystyle=q-(m-m_{0})

\displaystyle k_{1}

\displaystyle=p-(m-m_{1})

\displaystyle\mathllap{\text{and}\qquad}\ell

\displaystyle=m-m_{0}-m_{1}.

By Corollary
4.4
and Proposition
4.5
, we have

\displaystyle\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;k_{1}}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}^{\;k_{0}}Q^{\ell}

\displaystyle=2^{\ell-1}[\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{m_{0}},{m_{1}}}^{m}]^{*}

\displaystyle\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;k_{1}-1}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}^{\;k_{0}}\zeta_{1}Q^{\ell+1}

\displaystyle=2^{\ell}[\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{m_{0}},{m_{1}-1}}^{m};\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{m_{0}-1},{m_{1}-1}}^{m}]^{*}

and

\displaystyle\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;k_{1}}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}^{\;k_{0}-1}\zeta_{0}Q^{\ell+1}

\displaystyle=2^{\ell}[\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{m_{0}-1},{m_{1}}}^{m};\widetilde{\mathbb{X}}_{{m_{0}-1},{m_{1}-1}}^{m}]^{*}.

The first follows from Corollary
4.4
when we think of
\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;k_{1}}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}^{\;k_{0}}Q^{\ell}

as the pushforward of
Q^{\ell}
from the cohomology of
\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{m-m_{1}+(m-m_{0})\sigma})
,
and similarly for the other two.
They can also be derived directly from Proposition
3.7
.
This gives the general formula in part (1) of the theorem.

If
m_{0}\leq 0
, then
\Delta_{\min}=\Delta_{0}=0
.
(Here, we are implicitly assuming that
\Delta_{1}\geq 0
, but a similar simplification
can be given if
\Delta_{1}<0
.)
Together with Corollary
4.6
,
we get the simplified formula given in part (1). The other simplifications follow similarly.
These simplifications also correspond to the simplified formulas in Proposition
1.14
.

Now consider the case where
m_{0}+m_{1}>m
, so that
e(F)
is given by
Proposition
1.15
:

\displaystyle e(F)

\displaystyle=\epsilon\xi\sum_{j=1}^{\ell-1}\overline{\binom{\ell}{j}}\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;p-m_{0}+j}\;\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}^{\;k_{0}-j}\,\zeta_{0}^{j-1}\,\zeta_{1}^{\;\ell-j-1}

\displaystyle\qquad{}+\Delta_{0}\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;p-m_{0}}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}^{\;k_{0}}\zeta_{1}^{\;\ell}+\Delta_{1}\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;k_{1}}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}^{\;q-m_{1}}\zeta_{0}^{\;\ell}

\displaystyle\qquad{}+\frac{\Delta-\Delta_{0}-\Delta_{1}-\epsilon(2^{\beta(\ell)}-2)}{2}\tau(\iota^{2k_{0}}\zeta^{k_{1}-k_{0}}\,\widehat{c}^{\;p+q-m})

where
k_{0}
and
k_{1}
are as above, but now
\ell=m_{0}+m_{1}-m
.
By Corollary
2.16
, we have

\displaystyle\xi\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;p-m_{0}+j}\;\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}^{\;k_{0}-j}\,

\displaystyle\zeta_{0}^{j-1}\,\zeta_{1}^{\;\ell-j-1}

We also
gave an explicit basis for
H_{C_{2}}^{RO(\Pi B)}(\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p+q\sigma})_{+})
over
{\mathbb{H}}
,
which we can describe as follows.
We define sets
F_{p,q}(m)
, recursively on
p
and
q
, that give bases for
H_{C_{2}}^{m\omega+RO({C_{2}})}(\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{p+q\sigma})_{+})

(The superscript indicates the sub-
{\mathbb{H}}
-module of groups graded on the coset

m\omega+RO({C_{2}})\subseteq RO(\Pi B)
.)
For
m\in\mathbb{Z}
, let

\displaystyle F_{p,0}(m)

\displaystyle:=\{\zeta_{1}^{m},\zeta_{1}^{m-1}\widehat{c}_{\omega},\zeta_{1}^{m-2}\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;2},\ldots,\zeta_{1}^{m-p+1}\widehat{c}_{\omega}^{\;p-1}\}

and

\displaystyle F_{0,q}(m)

\displaystyle:=\{\zeta_{0}^{m},\zeta_{0}^{m-1}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega},\zeta_{0}^{m-2}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}^{\;2},\ldots,\zeta_{0}^{m-q+1}\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}^{\;q-1}\}.

(Note that
\zeta_{1}
is invertible in the first case and
\zeta_{0}
is invertible in the second.)
For
p,q>0
we then define

F_{p,q}(m):=\begin{cases}\{\zeta_{1}^{m}\}\cup i_{!}F_{p-1,q}(m-1)&\text{if\ \,$m\geq 0$}\\
\{\zeta_{0}^{|m|}\}\cup j_{!}F_{p,q-1}(m+1)&\text{if\,\ $m<0$,}\end{cases}

The pushforward
i_{!}
is given algebraically by multiplication by
\widehat{c}_{\omega}
and
j_{!}
is multiplication by
\widehat{c}_{\chi\omega}
.
