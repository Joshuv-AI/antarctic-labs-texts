A Proof of the Riemann Hypothesis
Using Bombieri’s Equivalence Theorem

Abstract




Abstract
:
The Riemann Hypothesis states that within the strip region of the complex plane
0<{\rm Re}(s)<1
, the Riemann
\xi(s)
function has zeros only on the critical line
{\rm Re}(s)=\frac{1}{2}
and none elsewhere. To prove the Riemann Hypothesis, we need to identify which points
s
make the complex function
\xi(s)=0
, which is evidently a challenging task. Bombieri proposed a proposition in the official description of the Millennium Prize Problems stating that “The Riemann hypothesis is equivalent to the statement that all local maxima of
\xi(t)
(on the critical line) are positive and all local minima are negative.” This provides a direction for proving the Riemann Hypothesis. In this paper, we follow Bombieri’s approach to study the Riemann Hypothesis. First, we prove that the function
\xi(s)
on the critical line (where it is a real function of a single real variable) satisfies a special differential equation. This ensures that it meets Bombieri’s equivalence condition. Then, since we have not been able to find the original proof of Bombieri’s equivalence theorem, we provide an independent proof for the sufficiency part of the theorem. We find that if the function
\xi(s)
on the critical line satisfies Bombieri’s equivalence condition, then by applying the Cauchy-Riemann equations to
\xi(s)
, we can prove that it has no zeros outside this critical line. Therefore, we can conclude that the Riemann Hypothesis is true. To further validate our findings, we discuss Pólya’s counterexample, which is misleading for understanding the research methods and results of this paper. However, we demonstrate that this counterexample does not invalidate Bombieri’s equivalence theorem or the judgments proposed in this paper, nor does it affect our proof.

Key words:
Riemann hypothesis.

1.
Introduction

In 1859, Riemann [1] used the method of analytic continuation to extend the
\zeta(s)
function on the complex field
{\mathbb{C}}
to the following form of the function
\xi(s)
:

\xi(s)=\frac{1}{2}s(s-1)\pi^{-s/2}\Gamma\Big(\frac{s}{2}\Big)\zeta(s),

(1)

Riemann also proved that the
\xi(s)
function can equivalently be expressed in another integral form of an analytic function:

\displaystyle\xi(s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}s(s-1)\int\limits_{1}^{\infty}\Psi(\tau)\Big(\tau^{s/2-1}+\tau^{-(1+s)/2}\Big){\rm d}\tau,

\displaystyle\Psi(\tau)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}e^{-\pi n^{2}\tau}=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\theta(\tau)-1\Big),

(2)

where
\theta(\tau)
is the third kind of Jacobi
\theta
function.
Riemann discovered that if every zero of the
\xi(s)
function (which corresponds to the non-trivial zeros of the
\zeta(s)
function) lies within the critical strip
0<\text{Re}(s)<1
, then the prime number theorem could be proven. To further advance this proof, Riemann boldly proposed a hypothesis, which stated that all zeros would most likely lie on the central line
\text{Re}(s)=\frac{1}{2}
of the critical strip. This hypothesis later became known as the Riemann Hypothesis, which holds a foundational status in analytic number theory [2-3]. Due to its importance, the Riemann Hypothesis was listed as the 8th problem in the famous 23 problems presented by Hilbert in 1900, and in 2000, it was selected as one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems by the Clay Mathematics Institute [4].

The Riemann Hypothesis can also be stated as follows: except on the critical line where the real part of
s
is
\frac{1}{2}
, the function
\xi(s)
has no zeros within the entire critical strip
0<\text{Re}(s)<1
. Clearly, determining whether a point within the critical strip is a zero of the complex function is an extremely challenging task. So far, progress in this research direction has been minimal.
In 1896, Hadamard [5] and de la Vallée Poussin [6] independently proved that
\xi(s)
has no zeros on the boundaries
\text{Re}(s)=0
and
\text{Re}(s)=1
, and they used this result to prove the prime number theorem. In 1914, Bohr and Landau [7, p.193] showed that almost all zeros of
\xi(s)
lie within a small
\varepsilon
neighborhood near the critical line. Of course, the phrase “almost all” implies that there may be some exceptional zeros outside this neighborhood.

In 1956, Lehmer [8] computed some zeros of the Riemann
\zeta(s)
function on the critical line
s=\frac{1}{2}+{\rm i}t
. He discovered that, in certain regions, two zeros are very close to each other, and the amplitude of the curve between these zeros is very small. Later, in 1974, Edwards [7, p.175] referred to these cases as “approximate counterexamples” to the Riemann Hypothesis. He proved that if the function
\xi(t)
has a small positive local minimum or a small negative local maximum, then the Riemann Hypothesis would be disproven. Edwards’ theorem provides a necessary condition for the Riemann Hypothesis.

In 2000, Bombieri proposed a statement in the official description of the Millennium Prize Problems [4]:
“The Riemann hypothesis is equivalent to the statement that all local maxima of
\xi(t)
(on the critical line) are positive and all local minima are negative.” This statement elevates Edwards’ theorem to a condition that is both necessary and sufficient.
If Bombieri’s assertion is correct, it would provide a more effective approach to studying the Riemann Hypothesis. This is because, on the critical line, the function
\xi(s)
becomes a real function of a single real variable. The geometric properties of this function’s curve are simpler than the surface geometric properties of the complex function
\xi(s)
within the critical strip, allowing for the application of more advanced mathematical tools.
So far, we know that Hardy [9] has proven that
\xi(s)
has infinitely many zeros on the critical line. Additionally, several researchers, including Platt and Trudgian [10], have shown through numerical computations that the Riemann Hypothesis holds within the range
{\rm Im}(s)\leq 3\times 10^{12}
.

We will follow Bombieri’s approach to investigate the Riemann Hypothesis.

First, in Section 2, we simplify the complex function
\xi(s)
using Jensen’s method. We use some different notations in the formulas compared to other authors, in order to provide the best perspective for studying the Riemann Hypothesis in subsequent sections. We then list several important properties of the Jensen function, which are essential for handling the
\xi(s)
function on the critical line.

In Section 3, we prove that on the critical line, the function
\xi(s)
(which is a real function of a single real variable at this point) satisfies a special differential equation. Consequently, the function must exhibit geometric properties near its zeros: it is either increasing and concave down, or decreasing and concave up. This ensures that all of the function’s zeros are simple zeros, with all local maxima being greater than zero and all local minima being less than zero. Therefore, it satisfies Bombieri’s equivalence condition.

Furthermore, in Section 4, since we have not yet found the original proof of the sufficiency part of Bombieri’s equivalence theorem, we independently provide a proof regarding this part. We demonstrate that if the function
\xi(s)
satisfies Bombieri’s equivalence condition on the critical line, then according to the Cauchy-Riemann equations, it must have no zeros outside this critical line. Thus, we can conclude that the Riemann Hypothesis is correct.

Finally, in Section 5, we explore Pólya’s counterexample, which has caused some misunderstandings regarding the use of the Jensen form of the
\xi(s)
function in studying the Riemann Hypothesis. We analyze it and show that it does not invalidate Bombieri’s equivalence theorem or the judgments proposed in this paper. Therefore, Pólya’s counterexample does not conflict with this research.

2.
Integral form
\xi(s)
and properties of Jensen’s function

The Riemann
\xi(s)
function can be expressed in two equivalent forms: (
1
) and (
2
). Our research primarily focuses on (
2
), but we also use (
1
) in Lemma 1 for quick and convenient results. In his original paper on the hypothesis [1], Riemann made a transformation of (
2
), rewriting it in another integral form that is still relatively complex. In 1911, at the Copenhagen conference [11], Jensen transformed this formula into an elegant form of the Fourier cosine transform. Subsequently, many authors, including Titchmarsh [12], Wintner [13], Haviland [14], Spira [15], Matiyasevich [16], and Pólya [17], have employed the Jensen form of the formula.

We will also use the Jensen form of the
\xi(s)
function but will need to change some symbols in the equations. This will help us apply fundamental calculus knowledge, such as differential equations and the Cauchy-Riemann equations, to handle the
\xi(s)
function further in the article.
Starting from (
2
), we introduce a new complex variable
z=x+{\rm i}y
for the transformation:

s=\frac{1}{2}(z+1).

(3)

At this point,
\xi(s)
can be rewritten in the following form:

\xi(s)=\Xi(z)=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{8}\big(z^{2}-1\big)\int\limits_{1}^{\infty}\Psi(\tau)\tau^{-3/4}\big(\tau^{z/4}+\tau^{-z/4}\big){\rm d}\tau.

(4)

This representation of
\Xi(z)
is different from those used by Jensen [11, 18] and Landau [19], who set (
3
) as
s=\frac{1}{2}+{\rm i}z
. By using (
3
), the strip region
0<{\rm Re}(s)<1
in the
s
plane, where we originally focused on finding the zeros of the function
\xi(s)
, has now become
-1<{\rm Re}(z)<1
in the
z
plane. Meanwhile, the critical line
{\rm Re}(s)=\frac{1}{2}
also transforms to
{\rm Re}(z)=x=0
.

Moreover, we change the integration variable
\tau
to
t
as follows:

\tau=e^{4t},

(5)

therefore,

\Xi(z)=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\big(z^{2}-1\big)\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\Psi(e^{4t})e^{t}\big(e^{zt}+e^{-zt}\big){\rm d}t.

(6)

Using the hyperbolic function
\cosh(w)=\frac{1}{2}(e^{w}+e^{-w})
, we obtain:

\Xi(z)=\frac{1}{2}+\big(z^{2}-1\big)\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}F(t)\cosh(zt){\rm d}t,

(7)

where

F(t)=\Psi(e^{4t})e^{t}=\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\exp\big(t-\pi n^{2}e^{4t}\big).

(8)

We continue to simplify the function
F(t)
. Taking its first and second derivatives, we obtain:

F^{\prime}(t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\exp\big(t-\pi n^{2}e^{4t}\big)\;\big(1-4\pi n^{2}e^{4t}\big),

(9)

\displaystyle F^{\prime\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\exp\big(t-\pi n^{2}e^{4t}\big)\;\Big[\big(1-4\pi n^{2}e^{4t}\big)^{2}-16\pi n^{2}e^{4t}\Big]

(10)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle F(t)+G(t),

where

G(t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\exp\big(t-\pi n^{2}e^{4t}\big)\;\Big[16\pi^{2}n^{4}e^{8t}-24\pi n^{2}e^{4t}\Big]

(11)

is referred to as the Jensen function [11], which differs from Jensen’s original formula by only a constant factor of
8
. At
t=0
, we have [20]:

F(0)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\exp\big(-\pi n^{2}\big)=\frac{1}{2}\big(\theta(1)-1\big),

(12)

F^{\prime}(0)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\exp\big(-\pi n^{2}\big)\;\big(1-4\pi n^{2}\big)=-\frac{1}{2},

(13)

and as
t\to\infty
,

\lim_{t\to\infty}F(t)\sinh(zt)=0,\quad\lim_{t\to\infty}F^{\prime}(t)\cosh(zt)=0,

(14)

where the value of the
\theta
function
\theta(1)
in (
12
) is a finite value that can be found in the works of Whittaker and Watson [21], and equations (
13
) and (
14
) should have originally been discovered by Riemann, as they are essential for handling the integral representation of the
\xi(s)
function.
With these preparations, we can further simplify (
7
). Using integration by parts, we have:

\displaystyle\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}F(t)\,\cosh(zt){\rm d}t

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{z^{2}}F^{\prime}(0)+\frac{1}{z^{2}}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}[F(t)+G(t)]\,\cosh(zt){\rm d}t.

(15)

Thus,

(z^{2}-1)\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}F(t)\,\cosh(zt){\rm d}t=F^{\prime}(0)+\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}G(t)\,\cosh(zt){\rm d}t.

(16)

Substituting (
16
) into (
7
), we obtain an integral representation of the Riemann
\xi(s)
function:

\Xi(z)=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}G(t)\,\cosh(zt){\rm d}t.

(17)

This formula is consistent with Jensen’s original formula [11], differing only in symbolic representation. In the following chapters, we will primarily use this formula.

Now, we review several properties of the Jensen function. It can be easily verified that for
0<t<\infty
, the Jensen function
G(t)>0
, and as
t\to\infty
,
G(t)
approaches
0
very rapidly. Wintner [13] proved that
G(t)
is strictly decreasing, i.e.,
G^{\prime}(t)<0
, in a brief two-page note. This important property was later independently proven by Spira [15] in 1971. In Wintner’s note, he also pointed out a property attributed to Jensen, Hurwitz, and others: if the domain of
G(t)
is extended to the negative
t
axis, it remains an even function, meaning
G(-t)=G(t)
, and
G^{\prime}(0)=0
.
Since this property is not easily seen from
(\ref {G_func})
, we briefly repeat the proof as follows. Referring to (
5
) and (
8
), we have:

F(t)=\Psi(\tau)e^{t},\quad\tau(t)=e^{4t},\quad\tau^{\prime}(t)=4\tau(t),\quad\tau(-t)=\frac{1}{\tau(t)},

(18)

and we can calculate:

G(t)=F^{\prime\prime}(t)-F(t)=\Big[16\tau^{2}\Psi^{\prime\prime}(\tau)+24\tau\Psi^{\prime}(\tau)\Big]e^{t}.

(19)

By differentiating the Jacobi identity provided by Riemann in his paper [1]:

2\Psi(\tau)+1=\tau^{-1/2}\Big[2\Psi\Big(\frac{1}{\tau}\Big)+1\Big],

(20)

we obtain:

\Big[16\tau^{2}\Psi^{\prime\prime}(\tau)+24\tau\Psi^{\prime}(\tau)\Big]e^{t}=\Big[\frac{16}{\tau^{2}}\Psi^{\prime\prime}\Big(\frac{1}{\tau}\Big)+\frac{24}{\tau}\Psi^{\prime}\Big(\frac{1}{\tau}\Big)\Big]e^{-t}=G(-t).

(21)

Thus,
G(t)
behaves similarly to a Gaussian function. We computed its bell curve [20] and presented it in Figure 1. This property has inspired many researchers to construct other similar functions
G(t)
to study the Riemann Hypothesis. For example, see reference [17].

Since

is a smooth (infinitely differentiable) even function, Jensen [18] proved that its derivatives of any order at

and

are given as follows:

G^{(2k-1)}(0)=0,\hskip 28.45274ptG^{(2k)}(0)=\text{exists},\hskip 28.45274pt\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}G^{(k)}(t)=0.

(22)

Although Jensen did not provide a formula for the even-order derivatives, it can be seen from equations
(\ref {2})
and
(\ref {G_tau})
that
G^{(2k)}(0)
is related to
\theta^{(2k+2)}(1)
,
\theta^{(2k+1)}(1)
,

,
\theta^{\prime}(1)
. Recently, Romik [22] found a formula for calculating all derivatives of the

G(0)\approx 3.5736,\hskip 14.22636ptG^{\prime\prime}(0)\approx-267.6880,\hskip 14.22636ptG^{(4)}(0)\approx 51978.4213,\ \cdots.

(23)

Jensen [18] and other mathematicians have also discovered many other properties of
G(t)
and its derivatives. Gélinas collected these properties and made a comprehensive inductive summary in a reading note [23]. In it, Gélinas also derived more interesting formulas for the
G(t)
function. For example, for
t\geq 0
, there are:

(2\pi\tau-3)\pi\tau e^{t-\pi\tau}\ <\ \frac{1}{8}G(t)\ <\ (2\pi\tau-3)\pi\tau e^{t-\pi\tau}+32\pi^{2}\tau^{2}e^{t-4\pi\tau},

(24)

and

4G(t)\ <\ \frac{\ -G^{\prime}(t)\ }{\pi\tau-\pi}\ <\ 4G(t)+8(8\pi-18)\pi\tau e^{t-\pi\tau}.

These two inequalities will play an important role in the subsequent work of this paper.

We will use
(\ref {xi_G})
to study the Riemann Hypothesis. Since
z=x+{\rm i}y
, the function
\Xi(z)
can be separated into its real and imaginary parts, as follows:

\displaystyle\Xi(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle u(x,y)+{\rm i}v(x,y)

(26)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}G(t)\cosh(xt)\cos(yt){\rm d}t+{\rm i}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}G(t)\sinh(xt)\sin(yt){\rm d}t,

Thus,
\Xi(z)=0
is equivalent to
u(x,y)=v(x,y)=0
.

3.
Bombieri’s equivalence condition

As mentioned in the introduction, we will study the Riemann Hypothesis based on Bombieri’s equivalence theorem. In this section, we focus on the critical line, which is the line
x=0
, at which the
\Xi(z)
function becomes the real function
U(y)
of a single real variable
y
:

\displaystyle u(0,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Xi({\rm i}y)=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}G(t)\cos(yt){\rm d}t=:U(y),

\displaystyle v(0,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

(27)

We will prove that the function
U(y)
satisfies Bombieri’s equivalence condition, which states that all local maxima of
U(y)
are positive and all local minima are negative.

Lemma 1.

For sufficiently large
y
, the
k
-th derivative of
U(y)
satisfies the following asymptotic equation:

U^{(k)}(y)=(-1)^{k}\Big(\frac{\pi}{8}-\frac{1}{4y}\Big)^{k}U(y)\Big(1+{\mathcal{O}}(y^{-1})\Big).

(28)

Proof.
According to Titchmarsh’s conclusion in reference [24], when the modulus of
s
is sufficiently large, the
\xi(s)
function has the following asymptotic property:

\log\xi(s)=\frac{1}{2}s\log{s}+{\mathcal{O}}(1).

(29)

Therefore, we substitute
s=(1+{\rm i}y)/2
into equation
(\ref {Titch})
, where
y
is sufficiently large,

\displaystyle\log U(y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log\xi\Big(\frac{1+{\rm i}y}{2}\Big)=-\frac{y}{4}\arctan\Big(\frac{y}{1}\Big)+\frac{1}{4}\log\sqrt{1+y^{2}}+{\mathcal{O}}(1)

(30)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\pi}{8}y+\frac{1}{4}\log y+{\mathcal{O}}(y^{-1}).

Taking the derivative of
U(y)
, we obtain

U^{\prime}(y)=-\Big(\frac{\pi}{8}-\frac{1}{4y}\Big)U(y)\Big(1+{\mathcal{O}}(y^{-1})\Big).

(31)

By repeatedly differentiating this equation, we arrive at equation
(\ref {Deriv U^n(y)})
.

\square

Remark.
Titchmarsh provided the estimate
(\ref {Titch})
through the analysis of equation
(\ref {1})
, but during the computation, he overlooked the term
s(s-1)
. If we include this term,
(\ref {Titch})
will become

\log\xi(s)=\frac{1}{2}s\log{s}+2\log{s}+{\mathcal{O}}(1).

(32)

Then, through a similar derivation,
(\ref {Deriv U^n(y)})
will be adjusted to

U^{(k)}(y)=(-1)^{k}\Big(\frac{\pi}{8}-\frac{9}{4y}\Big)^{k}U(y)\Big(1+{\mathcal{O}}(y^{-1})\Big).

(33)

Since the purpose of Lemma 1 is to determine a decay factor for the higher-order derivatives of
U(y)
, which will provide a basis for handling the higher-order derivative terms in the series when proving Lemma 2 later, this adjustment does not change the upper bound of the decay factor,
\pi/8
, and will not affect later usage.

Lemma 2.

All zeros of the function
U(y)
defined in
(\ref {u(0,y)})
are simple zeros.

Proof.
This proof is somewhat lengthy, so we will first outline the strategy. Suppose
U(y)
has an
n
-th order zero at
y_{0}
. We will perform a Taylor expansion of
U(y)
around the point
y_{0}
, yielding

U(y)\approx\frac{1}{n!}U^{(n)}(y_{0})(y-y_{0})^{n},

where
U^{(n)}(y_{0})\neq 0
. This implies that in the interval
y_{0}<y<y_{0}+\delta
, where
\delta>0
is sufficiently small, if
U(y)>0
, then for all derivatives
1\leq k\leq n
, we have
U^{(k)}(y)>0
. Therefore, we only need to show that the case
n\geq 2
for
U(y)
cannot hold, which allows us to conclude that
y_{0}
is a first-order (simple) zero.

We differentiate
U(y)
in
(\ref {u(0,y)})
, and then apply integration by parts to obtain

yU^{\prime}(y)+U(y)=-\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}G^{\prime}(t)t\cos(yt)\;\!{\rm d}t.

(34)

We differentiate
U(y)
again to get the second derivative,

U^{\prime\prime}(y)=-\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}G(t)t^{2}\cos(yt)\;\!{\rm d}t.

(35)

We need to relate the function
U(y)
with its various derivatives. These two equations inspire us to establish a relationship between
G^{\prime}(t)t
and
G(t)t^{2}
. Using the left inequality from
(\ref {Jensen_1})
, we have

\pi\tau e^{t-\pi\tau}<\frac{1}{\ 8(2\pi\tau-3)\ }G(t).

(36)

Substituting this into the right inequality from
(\ref {Jensen_2})
, we obtain

4G(t)<\frac{\ -G^{\prime}(t)\ }{\pi\tau-\pi}<4G(t)+\frac{\ 8\pi-18\ }{2\pi\tau-3}G(t).

(37)

Since
\tau=e^{4t}=1+4t+\cdots
, we reorganize
(\ref {Jensen_3})
to get

16\pi\;\!G(t)<-\frac{G^{\prime}(t)}{t}<16\pi\frac{(e^{4t}-1)}{4t}\bigg[1+\frac{\ 4\pi-9\ }{4\pi e^{4t}-6}\bigg]G(t).

(38)

This equation indicates that

is at least
16\pi
times
G(t)
. Figure 2 (taken from [20]) shows a comparison of three curves and also verifies

and

Theorem 1.

The function
U(y):=u(0,y)
defined in
(\ref {u(0,y)})
satisfies Bombieri’s equivalence conditions. Specifically, between each pair of adjacent zeros,
U(y)
has exactly one stationary point
y_{m}
that satisfies
U^{\prime}(y_{m})=0
, and at this point,
U(y_{m})
is either a positive local maximum or a negative local minimum.

Proof.
According to Lemma 2, all zeros of
U(y)
are simple zeros. By Rolle’s theorem, there exists at least one point
y_{m}
between each pair of adjacent zeros such that
U^{\prime}(y_{m})=0
. In the neighborhood
|y-y_{m}|<\delta
,
U^{\prime}(y)
can be approximated as:

U^{\prime}(y)=\frac{1}{n!}U^{(n+1)}(y_{m})(y-y_{m})^{n}\Big(1+{\mathcal{O}}(y-y_{m})\Big),

(67)

where
n\geq 1
and
U^{(n+1)}(y_{m})\neq 0
. If
n=2
, then
U^{\prime\prime\prime}(y_{m})\neq 0
. Without loss of generality, assume
U^{\prime\prime\prime}(y_{m})>0
. According to
(\ref {U'(y)_m})
, in the region
y_{m}<y<y_{m}+\delta
, we would have
U^{\prime}(y)>0
,
U^{\prime\prime}(y)>0
, and
U^{\prime\prime\prime}(y)>0
, which contradicts
(\ref {ODE_3})
. A similar argument applies if
U^{\prime\prime\prime}(y_{m})<0
.
Therefore,
n\neq 2
. Likewise, when
n\geq 3
, the same contradiction arises.

In summary, the only possible case is
n=1
. In the neighborhood of
y_{m}
,
(\ref {ODE})
simplifies to:

yU^{\prime}(y)+U(y)\ =-c\;\!U^{\prime\prime}(y)+{\mathcal{O}}\Big(\frac{U^{\prime\prime}(y)}{y}\Big),

(68)

which is the same as
(\ref {ODE_1})
, but from
(\ref {U'(y)_m})
we see that there is no fixed relationship between
U(y)
and
U^{\prime}(y)
. Since
U^{\prime}(y_{m})=0
, if
U(y_{m})>0
, then
(\ref {ODE_m})
requires
U^{\prime\prime}(y_{m})<0
, indicating that
U(y_{m})
is a positive local maximum. Conversely, if
U(y_{m})<0
, then
(\ref {ODE_m})
requires
U^{\prime\prime}(y_{m})>0
, meaning
U(y_{m})
is a negative local minimum. Therefore,
U(y)
cannot exhibit positive local minima or negative local maxima. Furthermore, there is exactly one stationary point
y_{m}
between each pair of adjacent zeros.

\square

4.
Zeros outside the critical line

According to Theorem 1, the function
\Xi(z)
satisfies Bombieri’s equivalence conditions. Based on Bombieri’s equivalence theorem, the Riemann Hypothesis holds, meaning that apart from the critical line
{\rm Re}(z)=0
, the function
\Xi(z)
has no zeros in the entire
z
-plane. Therefore, as long as Bombieri’s equivalence theorem is correct, this work is complete. However, while we believe that Bombieri has an original proof for his theorem, we have so far been unable to find this proof in the public literature. To complete this article, we will provide an independent proof of the sufficiency portion of that theorem. Of course, if Bombieri’s original proof is found later, he should receive precedence in recognition.

Our next task is to search for zeros of the
\Xi(z)
function outside the critical line. As Pólya describes in references [17,20], the traditional method is to find a function
A(z)
such that
|A(z)\Xi(z)-1|=B(z)
is bounded and less than
1
, thereby proving that
\Xi(z)
has no zeros. However, finding such an
A(z)
is by no means easy.
Our approach stems from numerical studies of the zero-value contour lines of the
\Xi(z)
function (both its real and imaginary parts) in the critical strip. Figure 5 shows some zero-value contour lines of the function
u(x,y)
, generated by Gélinas in 2017 using the Pari/GP and Gnuplot systems (see references [20,25]). The figure indicates that these contour lines divide the critical strip into several subregions. In each subregion,
u(x,y)\neq 0
, and
u(x,y)=0
occurs only on the boundary lines of the subregions (i.e., on the zero-value contour lines). According to
(\ref {u+iv})
,
\Xi(z)=0
is equivalent to
u(x,y)=v(x,y)=0
. If we can prove that the function
v(x,y)\neq 0
on these contour lines, then we can ensure that
\Xi(z)
is non-zero throughout the entire critical strip.

Similarly, we can use the zero-value contour lines of the
v(x,y)
function to partition the critical strip. In this way, we find that combining Theorem 1 with the Cauchy-Riemann equations allows us to conveniently demonstrate that
u(x,y)\neq 0
on the zero-value contour lines of the
v(x,y)
function. This conclusion verifies the sufficiency of Bombieri’s equivalence theorem, thus completing the proof of the Riemann Hypothesis.

Lemma 3.

There exists an
\varepsilon(y)>0
that forms two
\varepsilon
regions
\omega=\{(x,y):0<|x|<\varepsilon(y)\}
on either side of the critical line
x=0
. In these regions, the function
\Xi(z)
has no zeros.

Proof.
We first mention a similar result. In 1914, Bohr and Landau [7, p.193] proved a conclusion: that almost all zeros of the function
\Xi(z)
lie within a small region
|x|<\varepsilon
. However, their proof did not confirm whether individual zeros could exist outside the critical line
x=0
. In contrast, this lemma further clarifies that there are no zeros in the
\varepsilon
regions outside the critical line.

on the critical line
x=0
are countable and are all isolated points. Combining this with Theorem 1, it can be seen that the stationary points of this function are likewise isolated. Therefore, along the critical line, the distance between any two adjacent stationary points
y_{m}
and
y_{m+1}
of the curve
u(0,y)
satisfies
|y_{m+1}-y_{m}|>0
. Based on this, we can construct the following piecewise-defined function:

\varepsilon(y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\varepsilon_{2}(y_{m}),&{\rm if\ }|y-y_{m}|<\varepsilon_{2}(y_{m}),\\[8.53581pt]
\varepsilon_{1}(y),&{\rm otherwise}\end{array}\right.

(71)

Then, we establish two
\varepsilon
regions on either side of the critical line
x=0
:

\omega=\{(x,y):0<|x|<\varepsilon(y)\},

(72)

Clearly, within this region, the functions
u(x,y)
and
v(x,y)
cannot both be zero simultaneously, thus
\Xi(z)
has no zeros in this region.

\square

Lemma 4.

The function
\Xi(z)
has no zeros in the region
|x|>0
.

Proof.
This is the final step in proving the Riemann Hypothesis.
To prove this lemma, we will use the zero-value contour lines of the function
v(x,y)
to partition the region into several subregions. Since
v(x,y)\neq 0
in these subregions, our goal is to show that along all these zero-value contour lines of
v(x,y)
, we always have
u(x,y)\neq 0
. Given the symmetry of the function
\Xi(z)
, we will only consider the right half-plane where
x>0
, which also includes the strip
0<x<1
.

According to Lemma 3, there exists an
\varepsilon
region
\omega=\{(x,y):0<x<\varepsilon(y)\}
adjacent to the critical line
x=0
. Since along the line
x=0
,
v_{x}(0,y)
can be either greater than
0
or less than
0
, from
(\ref {v(x,y) not=0})
, it follows that the region
\omega
can be partitioned into multiple subregions
\omega_{k}
(
k=\cdots\!,-1,0,1,\cdots
). If the function
v(x,y)
is positive in
\omega_{k}
, then it must be negative in
\omega_{k+1}
. The boundary between
\omega_{k}
and
\omega_{k+1}
is a curve denoted as
y=\varphi_{k}(x)
, where
v(x,\varphi_{k}(x))=0
. This curve begins at a point where
v_{x}(0,y_{m})=-u_{y}(0,y_{m})=0
, which indicates that at this point,
u(0,y_{m})
reaches a positive local maximum or a negative local minimum.

Specifically, we represent
\omega_{k}
as

\omega_{k}=\{(x,y):0<x<\varepsilon(y),\ \varphi_{k-1}(x)<y<\varphi_{k}(x),\ (-1)^{k}v(x,y)>0\}.

(73)

Since
v(x,y)
is analytic (continuous and differentiable) for
x>0
, we can extend each subregion
\omega_{k}
to fill the entire half-plane:

\Omega_{k}=\{(x,y):x>0,\ \varphi_{k-1}(x)<y<\varphi_{k}(x),\ (-1)^{k}v(x,y)>0\}.

(74)

The boundary between
\Omega_{k}
and
\Omega_{k+1}
is still denoted as
y=\varphi_{k}(x)
, where
v(x,\varphi_{k}(x))=0
. These boundary curves are the zero-value contour lines of the function
v(x,y)
. Although these curves may have complex geometric shapes, they can be categorized into three situations: (i) the curves do not intersect or branch; (ii) some curves intersect; (iii) some curves branch.
We will prove that not all of these situations can occur. If such situations do exist, then
u(x,y)
will not equal zero on those curves, which implies that
\Xi(z)\neq 0
throughout the entire half-plane.

5.
The counterexample from Pólya

After the passing of J.L.W.V. Jensen, Pólya was authorized to handle his Nachlass. Pólya provided detailed proofs of all the interesting properties of the Riemann
\xi(s)
function discovered by Jensen, and he wrote an important paper in German, published in a Danish journal in 1927 [18].
Finally, Pólya needed to confirm whether Jensen had found a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis. To address this question, Pólya devised a disastrous function at the end of that article. This function also took the form of a Fourier cosine transform and featured a Gaussian-type kernel function similar to Jensen’s function
G(t)
. Therefore, Pólya’s function was very similar to the Riemann
\xi(s)
function studied by Jensen but had zeros outside the critical line. From that point on, any method that used Jensen’s function as the integral kernel for a Fourier transform to prove the Riemann Hypothesis was considered equally applicable to Pólya’s counterexample and, thus, deemed invalid.

Here we use the notation from the article to review the mathematical expression of Pólya’s counterexample. We use the subscript
1
to denote the function in Pólya’s counterexample. Compared to
(\ref {xi_G})
, Pólya’s counterexample is a linear combination of two equations:

\Xi_{1}(z)=e^{\frac{1}{2}z^{2}}\Big(\cosh(z)+\alpha\Big)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi e}}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{1}{2}t^{2}}\Big(\cosh(t)+\alpha\sqrt{e}\Big)\cosh(zt){\rm d}t,

(79)

where
\alpha
is a parameter. When
-1<\alpha<1
, the function
\Xi_{1}(z)=e^{\frac{1}{2}z^{2}}\big(\cosh(z)+\alpha\big)
has infinitely many simple zeros on the
y
-axis, which is consistent with the behavior of the Riemann
\Xi(z)
function. However, it can be observed that when
\alpha=1
,
\Xi_{1}(z)
on the
y
-axis becomes

\Xi_{1}({\rm i}y)=e^{\frac{1}{2}({\rm i}y)^{2}}\big(\cosh({\rm i}y)+1\big)=e^{-\frac{1}{2}y^{2}}\big(\cos(y)+1\big)=2e^{-\frac{1}{2}y^{2}}\cos^{2}\Big(\frac{y}{2}\Big),

(80)

and thus it has infinitely many second-order zeros on the
y
-axis, specifically at

y_{k}=(2k+1)\pi,\hskip 28.45274pt(k=0,\pm 1,\pm 2,\cdots).

When
\alpha>1
, the function
\Xi_{1}(z)
no longer has zeros on the
y
-axis. From
\cosh(z)+\alpha=0
, the zeros can be found in the region where
x\neq 0
, expressed as

z_{k}=\ln(\alpha\pm\sqrt{\alpha^{2}-1})+{\rm i}2k\pi,\hskip 28.45274pt(k=0,\pm 1,\pm 2,\cdots).

Therefore, it is necessary to verify whether Pólya’s example invalidates the methods and results obtained in this paper.

We first verify whether Pólya’s counterexample undermines the Bombieri equivalence theorem followed and proved in this paper. In fact, we can restate Theorem 2 as follows: if
\Xi(z)
has zeros outside the critical line, then it must have a positive local minimum or a negative local maximum on the critical line. Pólya’s counterexample is precisely this case. From
(\ref {counter})
we obtain

\Xi_{1}({\rm i}y)=U_{1}(y)=e^{-\frac{1}{2}y^{2}}\left(\cos(y)+\alpha\right).

(81)

When
\alpha>1
, this expression indeed has infinitely many positive local minima. Therefore, Pólya’s counterexample, in the case of
\alpha>1
, has zeros outside the critical line, which does not contradict the theorem presented in this paper.

Secondly, we need to prove that the criterion used in this paper for determining the simple zeros of the function
U(y)
also applies to detecting the occurrence of second-order zeros in
U_{1}(y)
from Pólya’s counterexample when
\alpha=1
. To demonstrate this, we directly analyze the geometric shape of Pólya’s
U_{1}(y)
function.
Differentiating
(\ref {Polya_U})
, we find:

\displaystyle U_{1}^{\prime}(y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-yU_{1}(y)-e^{-\frac{1}{2}y^{2}}\sin(y),

\displaystyle U_{1}^{\prime\prime}(y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(y^{2}-1)U_{1}(y)+e^{-\frac{1}{2}y^{2}}(2y\sin(y)+\alpha).

(82)

According to the standard proposed in this paper, a necessary condition for the zero of
U_{1}(y)
to be a second-order zero is to satisfy
U_{1}(y)>0
,
U_{1}^{\prime}(y)>0
, and
U_{1}^{\prime\prime}(y)>0
near the zero. Therefore, we derive the following three equations to solve for the critical value
\alpha_{c}
where a second-order zero might occur:

\displaystyle\cos(y)+\alpha

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

\displaystyle-\sin(y)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 0,

\displaystyle 2y\sin(y)+\alpha

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 0.

(83)

By solving the above system of equations in the vicinity of
\alpha=1
, we obtain the result [20]:
\alpha_{c}\approx 0.98617
. This means that when
\alpha\geq\alpha_{c}
, a strictly increasing and upward concave curvature shape has already formed near the zero. As
\alpha
approaches
1
, this will ultimately change the zero from first order to second order. Figure 10 shows the graph of
U_{1}(y)
based on calculations from [20], illustrating the geometric form of the curve near the corresponding zero, which can be compared to Figure 4.

Thus, for the study of the Riemann Hypothesis and Pólya’s counterexample, the theorems and criteria used in this paper are consistent. The exceptional cases arising in Pólya’s counterexample are entirely determined by the properties of its
\Xi_{1}(z)
function and do not undermine the theorems and criteria presented in this paper. This also indicates that the validity of the Riemann Hypothesis is independent of whether there is a Gaussian-type integral kernel function, and Pólya’s counterexample does not affect the work and results of this paper.

Finally, we will compare the functions in Pólya’s counterexample with the corresponding functions in this paper to illustrate that the proof methods in this paper have a degree of independence and cannot be interchanged with the research methods of Pólya’s counterexample.

We first compare the higher-order derivatives of
U_{1}(y)
and
U(y)
. In Pólya’s example, as shown in
(\ref {Polya_U})
,
U_{1}(y)
has a bounding function of
e^{-\frac{1}{2}y^{2}}
, so
U_{1}^{\prime}(y)/U_{1}(y)\approx-y
. In our work, based on Lemma 1, the bound is
U(y)=e^{-(\pi/8)y}
, thus
U^{\prime}(y)/U(y)\approx-\pi/8
. In contrast, for the function
U_{1}(y)
, the higher the order of differentiation, the larger the value of the derivative. This makes it impossible for us to handle the series in
(\ref {ODE})
.

We further compare the kernel function
G_{1}(t)
in Pólya’s counterexample with the kernel function
G(t)
in the Riemann Hypothesis (i.e., Jensen’s function
(\ref {G_func})
). In Pólya’s example, from
(\ref {counter})
, the integral kernel is given by

G_{1}(t)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi e}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}t^{2}}\left(\cosh(t)+\alpha\sqrt{e}\right).

(84)

From
(\ref {polya_G(t)})
, we can calculate the relationship between
-G_{1}^{\prime}(t)/t
and
G_{1}(t)
:

\displaystyle G_{1}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle tG_{1}(t)-\frac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi e}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}t^{2}}\sinh(t)

(85)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle tG_{1}(t)-\frac{\sinh(t)}{\cosh(t)+\alpha\sqrt{e}}G_{1}(t).

This equation shows a significant difference compared to
(\ref {Jensen_4})
:
-G_{1}^{\prime}(t)/t
is smaller than
G_{1}(t)
, while
-G^{\prime}(t)/t
is more than
16\pi
times
G(t)
.
Thus, Pólya’s
G_{1}(t)
function decreases much more slowly as
t
increases compared to Jensen’s
G(t)
. Thanks to the favorable property of Jensen’s function
G(t)
, we calculated the first term
a_{0}=74.9076
for the series
a(t)
in
(\ref {Taylor_3})
. This number is sufficiently large to ensure that
c>0
in
(\ref {c})
, allowing for the effective differential equation
(\ref {ODE_1})
, which enables the subsequent proofs to proceed smoothly.
However, if we switch to Pólya’s counterexample, the situation is entirely different. From
(\ref {polya_G'(t)})
, we can calculate

(a_{0})_{1}=\lim\limits_{t\to 0}\frac{-G_{1}^{\prime}(t)}{tG_{1}(t)}=1-\frac{1}{1+\alpha\sqrt{e}}<1.

(86)

This coefficient is too small. If we were to directly apply the method of constructing the differential equation used in this paper to analyze Pólya’s counterexample
\Xi_{1}(z)
for zeros on the critical line, we would encounter a completely different scenario.

In summary, the proof methods in this paper are specifically designed for the Riemann
\xi(s)
function and possess a degree of independence; their validity should not be judged based on whether they can be parallelly applied to Pólya’s counterexample.
