Constraining the General Oscillatory Inflaton Potential with Freeze-in Dark Matter and Gravitational Waves Preprint: IPARCOS-UCM-23-124
Jose A. R. Cembranos, Mindaugas Karčiauskas

Abstract
The reheating phase after inflation is one of the least observationally constrained epochs in the evolution of the Universe. The forthcoming gravitational wave observatories will enable us to constrain at least some of the non-standard scenarios. For example, models where the radiation bath is produced by the perturbative inflaton decay that oscillates around a minimum of the potential of the form V ∝ ϕ 2 ​ n V\propto\phi^{2n} , with n > 2 n>2 . In such scenarios a part of the inflationary gravitational wave spectrum becomes blue tilted, making it observable, depending on the inflation energy scale and the reheating temperature. The degeneracy between the latter two parameters can be broken if dark matter in the Universe is produced via the freeze-in mechanism. The combination of the independent measurement of dark matter mass with gravitational wave observations makes it possible to constrain the reheating temperature and the energy scale at the end of inflation, at least within some parameter ranges.



I
Introduction

Freeze-out is a popular mechanism to explain the production of Dark
Matter (DM)
(
Lee and Weinberg 1977
;
Scherrer and Turner 1986
;
Srednicki, Watkins, and Olive 1988
;
Gondolo and Gelmini 1991
;
Kolb and Turner 1990
)
.
According to this mechanism DM is in thermal equilibrium with the
hot plasma of the early Universe. As the Universe expands and cools
down, the DM interaction rate becomes smaller than the expansion rate.
This makes DM particles decouple from the rest of plasma, leaving
the comoving particle number density constant thereafter. The final
DM abundance in this scenario is not very sensitive to the physics
of the early Universe; at least not for models with a standard thermal
history after the freeze-out.

An alternative DM production mechanism is the so called “freeze-in”
mechanism (see e.g.
(
Chung, Kolb, and Riotto 1999
)
). According to this paradigm,
the interactions of DM are so weak that they are never in thermal
equilibrium with the cosmic plasma. However, such interactions are
strong enough that the scattering of the thermal bath particles can
produce DM in sufficient abundance. After the production, DM particles
travel unimpeded. In contrast to the freeze-out scenario, the DM abundance
from the freeze-in scenarios is very sensitive to the details of the
early Universe physics. This offers an opportunity: if Nature did
select this scenario for DM production, by measuring DM properties
will enable us to learn and constrain the physics of the early Universe.

The potential of such studies is, for example, investigated in Refs.
(
Feldstein, Ibe, and Yanagida 2014
;
Bhattiprolu et al. 2023
)
,
where it is demonstrated how the measurement of DM particle mass could
give us information about the maximum themperature and the reheating
temperature after inflation. Any new method to constraint the epoch
of reheating by observations is very welcome, as this remains to be
a very poorly understood period due to the lack of direct observational
signatures. The authors of Ref.
(
Feldstein, Ibe, and Yanagida 2014
)
assumed
hypercharged minimal DM and a standard reheating scenario. According
to such a scenario the inflaton oscillates around the minimum of the
quadratic potential after inflation and perturbatively decays into
relativistic particles
(
Lyth and Liddle 2009
)
. The sensitivity of the
freeze-in mechanism to the early Universe physics also means that
any change in this standard reheating scenario could affect the properties
of DM particles. For example, the effects of the non-standard expansion
history after inflation is studied in Refs.
(
D’Eramo, Fernandez, and Profumo 2018
;
Bernal, Rubio, and Veermäe 2020
;
Ghoshal, Heurtier, and Paul 2022
;
Barman et al. 2022a
;
Barman et al. 2023
)

and the authors of Ref.
Becker et al. 2024
investigate other
ways how freeze-in DM could be used to constrain the reheating epoch.

In the past several years the detection of Gravitational Waves (GW)

(
Abbott et al. 2016a
;
Abbott et al. 2016b
;
Abbott et al. 2017a
)

opened a new window to investigate the Universe. More recently, the
detection of the stochastic GW background by pulsar timing array collaborations
NANOGrav, EPTA/InPTA, PPTA and CPTA has reached another big milestone
in the GW astronomy
(
Agazie et al. 2023
;
Xu et al. 2023
;
Antoniadis et al. 2023
;
Reardon et al. 2023
)
.
Although the detected background signal is generated by astrophysical
sources, it demonstrates the potential to study the physics of the
early Universe too. There are numerous processes that could operate
in the early Universe and produce a detectable background of GW
(
Guzzetti et al. 2016
;
Caprini and Figueroa 2018
)
.
One of such processes is the non-standard expansion history after
inflation
(
Giovannini 1998
;
Giovannini 1999
;
Figueroa and Tanin 2019
;
Haque et al. 2021
)
.
Due to vacuum fluctuations, inflation leaves the Universe filled with
the primordial GW background that is (quasi) scale invariant
(
Lyth and Liddle 2009
)
.
This spectrum is modified as the Universe undergoes the various phases
of its evolution
(
Saikawa and Shirai 2018
)
. In particular, any epoch,
where the expansion rate deviates from the radiation dominated one,
tilts the spectrum in the range of subhorizon modes. In many models
the tilt can be large enough that the inflationary GW signal falls
within the sensitivity limits of future observatories (see for example

(
Haque et al. 2021
;
Dimopoulos 2023
)
).

In this work we investigate how the combination of DM searches and
GW observations could shed some light on inflation and the reheating
epochs. In Section
II
we compute the freeze-in
production of DM particles during reheating. In contrast to the previous
calculations we allow for the inflaton to oscillate around a non-quadratic
potential. This modifies the expansion rate during reheating and the
final density of DM. The non-standard expansion rate also affects
the spectrum of the background GW. The computation of such a spectrum
is summarised in Section
III
. In Section
IV

we find the relation between that spectrum and the DM properties.
We also explore the allowed parameter range and determine regions
which are accessible by future observations.

II
The Freeze-in DM Production with an Arbitrary Inflaton Potential

II.1
The Setup

We consider a similar setup as discussed in Ref.
(
Chung, Kolb, and Riotto 1999
)
.
The model consists of three components: an inflaton field, radiation
and the non-relativistic DM. After inflation the inflaton
\phi

oscillates around the minimum of the potential and initially dominates
the energy budget of the Universe. To simplify the discussion, we
assume that the inflaton decays almost exclusively into light degrees
of freedom (radiation) with the decay rate
\Gamma_{\phi}
. The thermalisation
rate is taken to be fast enough so that we can assume these degrees
of freedom to be in a local thermodynamic equilibrium. We can then
relate the energy density of radiation to the thermodynamic temperature
by

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{r}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\pi^{2}g_{*}\left(T\right)}{30}T^{4}\,,

(1)

where
g_{*}\left(T\right)
is the number of effective relativistic
degrees of freedom at a given temperature. The Universe is reheated
with the temperature
T_{\mathrm{reh}}
when radiation takes over other contributions
to the energy budget. At that moment the Universe becomes radiation
dominated. It is important to recognise that
T_{\mathrm{reh}}
is not the
maximum temperature, as we will see later.

The heavy and stable DM particles are non-relativistic throughout
the whole of reheating process. Moreover, they are so weakly coupled
to radiation that DM particles never come into thermal equilibrium.
Nevertheless, the interaction is sufficiently strong that the thermal
bath produces DM particles, which are then freely diluted by the expansion
of the Universe.

The Boltzman equations (in terms of energy densities) for such a setup
can be written as
(
Chung, Kolb, and Riotto 1999
)

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}_{\phi}+3H\left(1+w_{\phi}\right)\rho_{\phi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\Gamma_{\phi}\rho_{\phi}\,,

(2)

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}_{\mathrm{r}}+4H\rho_{\mathrm{r}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\phi}\rho_{\phi}\,,

(3)

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}_{\mathrm{DM}}+3H\rho_{\mathrm{DM}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\left\langle\sigma\left|v\right|\right\rangle}{M_{\mathrm{DM}}}\left(\rho_{\mathrm{DM}}^{\mathrm{eq}}\right)^{2}\,,

(4)

where
\rho_{\phi}
,
\rho_{\mathrm{r}}
and
\rho_{\mathrm{DM}}
are the energy
densities of the inflaton, radiation and DM respectively.
M_{\mathrm{DM}}

is the mass of DM particles and
\left\langle\sigma\left|v\right|\right\rangle

is the thermal average cross section for annihilation of DM. As DM
is assumed to be far from equilibrium, only the
\left(\rho_{\mathrm{DM}}^{\mathrm{eq}}\right)^{2}

term dominates the R.H.S. of eq. (
4
), which is the non-relativistic
equilibrium energy density, given by

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{DM}}^{\mathrm{eq}}\left(T\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{M_{\mathrm{DM}}^{5/2}T^{3/2}}{\left(2\pi\right)^{3/2}}\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-M_{\mathrm{DM}}/T}\,.

(5)

The inflaton decay rate
\Gamma_{\phi}
is assumed to be much lower
than the Hubble parameter at the end of inflation,
H_{\mathrm{I}}
,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\phi}

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{I}}\,.

(6)

This leads to a long period of reheating, before the inflaton decays
into radiation completely.

Equations (
2
)-(
4
) differ from analogous equations
in Ref.
(
Chung, Kolb, and Riotto 1999
)
by that we do not assume the inflaton
to be oscillating in a quadratic potential after inflation. We rather
allow for a much flatter form of the potential at the minimum, such
that

\displaystyle V\left(\phi\right)

\displaystyle\propto

\displaystyle\phi^{2n}\,,

(7)

where
n
is a natural number. Moreover, since we are interested
in observable stochastic background of GWs, we only consider values

n>2
. These types of potentials can be encountered, for example,
in
\alpha
-attractor
(
Ueno and Yamamoto 2016
;
Di Marco, Cabella, and Vittorio 2017
)
or monodromy

(
McAllister et al. 2014
)
models of inflation. As it is well known
(see
(
Turner 1983
;
Shtanov, Traschen, and Brandenberger 1995
;
Lozanov and Amin 2017
;
Cembranos, Maroto, and Núñez Jareño 2016
)
),
an oscillating scalar field in such a potential leads to an effective
equation of state of the form

\displaystyle w_{\phi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{n-1}{n+1}\,.

(8)

This value of
w_{\phi}
is used in eq. (
2
) above.
n=2

corresponds to
w_{\phi}=1/3
; as
n
increases,
w_{\phi}
approaches
1. However,
w_{\phi}=1
describes a non-oscillatory inflaton potential

(
Felder, Kofman, and Linde 1999
)
, hence we cannot assume the standard perturbative
reheating scenario and apply our calculations to estimate DM abundances.
The
w_{\phi}=1
case rather serves as an upper limit for the allowed
range of
w_{\phi}
values. With non-oscillatory potentials one has
to employ other ways to reheat the universe. In summary, the range
of
w_{\phi}
values that will be used in this work is
1/3<w_{\phi}<1
.

Inflaton oscillations in a non-quadratic potential also induce the
time dependence of the decay rate
\Gamma_{\phi}

(
Shtanov, Traschen, and Brandenberger 1995
;
Garcia et al. 2020
;
Ahmed, Grzadkowski, and Socha 2022
;
Barman et al. 2022b
;
Banerjee and Chowdhury 2022
;
Barman et al. 2023
)
,
which we parametrise by

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\phi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\mathrm{I}}a^{\nu}\,,

(9)

where
\Gamma_{\mathrm{I}}
is the inflaton decay rate at the end of inflation
and we normalised the scale factor to equal unity at that moment
a_{\mathrm{I}}=1
.
The scaling
\nu
depends on the shape of the potential in eq. (
7
)
as well as on the dominant inflaton decay channel. According to
(
Shtanov, Traschen, and Brandenberger 1995
)

if the inflaton decays primarily into fermions then
\nu=-3w_{\phi}

and
\nu=3w_{\phi}
if it decays into scalar particles. One can device
a more complicated behaviour of
\Gamma_{\phi}
(see e.g.
(
Co, Gonzalez, and Harigaya 2020
)
)
but we consider only this generic case in the current work.

II.2
Evolution of the Inflaton and Radiation During Reheating

The condition in eq. (
6
) ensures that before the end of
reheating the R.H.S. of eq. (
2
) is negligible. Here,
we define the
moment of reheating
to be the moment when radiation
energy density takes over that of the inflaton, i.e. reheating happens
at
a_{\mathrm{reh}}
when
\rho_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{reh}}=\rho_{\phi}^{\mathrm{reh}}
. The interval
between inflation and this point we call the
period

of
reheating
.

It is also a sufficiently good approximation to assume that the oscillating
inflaton dominates the energy budget of the Universe before radiation
takes over. In other words, we can approximate
\rho\simeq\rho_{\phi}

during reheating, where
\rho
is the total energy density. Solving
eq. (
2
) then leads to

\displaystyle\rho_{\phi}

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{I}}a^{-3\left(1+w_{\phi}\right)}\,,

(10)

where
a
is the scale factor and
\rho_{\mathrm{I}}
is the energy density
at the end of inflation. Plugging this result into the Friedman equation

3m_{\mathrm{Pl}}^{2}H^{2}=\rho
we can also compute the scaling of the Hubble
parameter

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{I}}a^{-\frac{3}{2}\left(1+w_{\phi}\right)}\,.

(11)

The evolution of the radiation energy density is governed by eq. (
3
).
To find the solution of this equation during reheating, we can plug
eqs. (
10
), (
11
) and (
9
) into eq. (
3
).
At the lowest order in
\Gamma_{\phi}/H\ll 1
the integral is equal
to

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{r}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\rho_{\mathrm{I}}}{4\alpha}\frac{\Gamma_{\mathrm{I}}}{H_{\mathrm{I}}}\left(a^{4\alpha}-1\right)a^{-4}\,,

(12)

where
\alpha
is defined as

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{1}{8}\left(5-3w_{\phi}+2\nu\right)\,.

(13)

With the upper bound
w_{\phi}<1
, that were specified in the paragraph
bellow eq. (
8
), and for
-3w_{\phi}\leq\nu\leq 3w_{\phi}
, the
range of possible
\alpha
values is
-1/2<\alpha<1
. Notice,
that the above solution is not valid if
\alpha=0
(
2\nu=3w_{\phi}-5
).
In that case
\rho_{\mathrm{r}}\propto a^{-4}\ln a
. But since we are mainly
interested in
\nu=\pm 3w_{\phi}
values, this case will not be analysed
any further.

Whichever the sign of
\alpha
, the maximum value of
\rho_{\mathrm{r}}

is achieved at

\displaystyle a_{\max}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(1-\alpha\right)^{-\frac{1}{4\alpha}}\>.

(14)

After this point the evolution of radiation does depend on the sign
of
\alpha
. In the case of
\alpha>0
, the first term in eq. (
12
)
dominates. We call this case the “lasting inflaton decay case”,
or the “
lasting decay
” briefly. In this case the production
of radiation by the oscillating inflaton field continues to be significant
all the way up until reheating is complete. Newly produced radiation
continuously sources the radiation bath and makes its energy density
decrease as
\rho_{\mathrm{r}}\propto a^{-\frac{3}{2}\left(1+w_{\phi}\right)+\nu}
,
i.e. slower than
a^{-4}
.

In the opposite regime
\alpha<0
, which we call the “
brief
decay
” case, the radiation production is very inefficient. After
reaching
\rho_{\mathrm{r}}^{\max}
, the energy density of the radiation
bath cannot keep up with the expansion of the Universe and decays
adiabatically,
\rho_{\mathrm{r}}\propto a^{-4}
.

Whichever the decay regime, the lasting or the brief one, we can compute
the maximum temperature of the Universe. If we assume that the number
of effective relativistic degrees of freedom
g_{*}\left(T\right)

in eq. (
1
) is constant during reheating, which we denote
by
g_{*}
, the maximum value of
\rho_{\mathrm{r}}
in eq. (
12
)
can be related to the maximum temperature of radiation, which is

\displaystyle T_{\max}^{4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{I}}\frac{15}{2\pi^{2}g_{*}}\frac{\Gamma_{\mathrm{I}}}{H_{\mathrm{I}}}\left(1-\alpha\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}-1}\,.

(15)

Figure 1:
The evolution of the inflaton and radiation energy
densities
\rho_{\phi}
and
\rho_{\mathrm{r}}
respectively during
reheating. The dotted lines represent approximate scalings derived
in the text.

To check if the above described scenario is correct, we also numerically
integrate the exact system of equations (
2
)-(
4
).
A few examples of those solutions are shown in Figure
1

together with a few lines illustrating analytic solutions in eqs. (
10
)
and (
12
). We can clearly see that after the initial burst
of radiation production, the latter passively decays away as
a^{-4}

in the brief decay regime (
\alpha<0
). This is demonstrated in the
upper row of the figure. Conversely, for
\alpha>0
(the lower row
of the figure) the radiation production continues to be significant
until the moment of reheating.

As was mentioned above, we define the moment of reheating, to be the
instance when the radiation energy density starts dominating over
the inflaton one. This can happen in both, brief or lasting, decay
scenarios. In the former case, even if the production of radiation
is negligible after
a_{\max}
, the radiation redshifts away slower
than the inflaton (see the upper row of Figure
1
),
eventually taking it over.

In the lasting decay case several things can happen. First, as in
the previous case,
\rho_{\mathrm{r}}
might take over
\rho_{\phi}
at
a=a_{\mathrm{rd}}
.
But the evolution of
\rho_{\mathrm{r}}
was computed assuming
\Gamma_{\phi}/H\ll 1
,
which scales as
\Gamma_{\phi}/H\propto a^{\nu+\frac{3}{2}\left(1+w_{\phi}\right)}
.
For the most of parameter values this ratio is growing with time.
It is therefore possible that
\Gamma_{\phi}/H
becomes larger than
1 at
a=a_{\mathrm{dec}}<a_{\mathrm{rd}}
, before the domination of
\rho_{\mathrm{r}}

in eq. (
12
). When the
\Gamma_{\phi}<H
bound is violated,
the inflaton decays away in less than a Hubble time, giving its energy
to radiation. Hence, in the lasting decay scenario we can define the
moment of reheating to be
a_{\mathrm{reh}}=\min\left[a_{\mathrm{rd}},a_{\mathrm{dec}}\right]
.
In practice, however, it is sufficient to use the approximation
a_{\mathrm{reh}}\simeq a_{\mathrm{rd}}
.
To see this, notice that
a_{\mathrm{rd}}/a_{\mathrm{dec}}=\left(4\alpha\right)^{1/\left(4\alpha-1+3w_{\phi}\right)}
,
which is never much larger than 1 for the interesting parameter values.
In conclusion, for the rest of the paper the moment of reheating will
be determined using eq. (
12
) and finding the moment
\rho_{\mathrm{r}}\left(a_{\mathrm{reh}}\right)\simeq\rho_{\phi}\left(a_{\mathrm{reh}}\right)
.

With this in mind, we find the scale factor at reheating to be

\displaystyle a_{\mathrm{reh}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(4\left|\alpha\right|\frac{H_{\mathrm{I}}}{\Gamma_{\mathrm{I}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{4\vartheta-1+3w_{\phi}}}\,,

(16)

where we introduced the parameter

\displaystyle\vartheta

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\max\left\{0,\alpha\right\}\,

(17)

to take into account both cases,
\alpha>0
and
\alpha<0
. It is
easy to compute the temperature of the Universe at
a_{\mathrm{reh}}
, which
is given by

\displaystyle T_{\mathrm{reh}}^{4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{I}}\left(\frac{30}{\pi^{2}g_{*}}\right)\left(\frac{\Gamma_{\mathrm{I}}}{4\left|\alpha\right|H_{\mathrm{I}}}\right)^{\frac{3\left(1+w_{\phi}\right)}{4\vartheta+3w_{\phi}-1}}\,.

(18)

II.3
Freeze-In Production of Dark Matter

Next, we integrate eq. (
4
), which specifies the evolution
of DM. Before the epoch of matter-radiation equality DM is subdominant
to other components of the Universe. This allows us to substitute

H
and
T
by the values computed in the previous section when
we integrate eq. (
4
) next. Moreover, for concreteness,
we assume that the DM annihilation is dominated by the s-wave channel,
that is very common for renormalizable interactions. In such a case,
the thermal average cross section is just a constant value. To do
the integration it is first convenient to rewrite eq. (
4
)
as

\displaystyle a^{3}\rho_{\mathrm{DM}}^{\mathrm{reh}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\kappa\intop_{1}^{a}\mathrm{e}^{-g\left(a^{\prime}\right)}\mathrm{d}a^{\prime}\,,

(19)

where we used
\rho_{\mathrm{DM}}\left(a_{\mathrm{I}}=1\right)=0
. The dimension-4
constant
\kappa
in the above expression depends on the DM cross
section, and is given by

\displaystyle\kappa

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{M_{\mathrm{DM}}^{2}\left\langle\sigma\left|v\right|\right\rangle}{\left(\lambda\pi\right)^{3}}\cdot\frac{M_{\mathrm{DM}}^{5}}{H_{\mathrm{I}}}\,.

(20)

While the
g\left(a\right)
function is defined as

\displaystyle g\left(a\right)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle-\lambda\left(\frac{a^{4\alpha}-1}{4\alpha}\right)^{-\frac{1}{4}}a+\frac{3}{4}\ln\left(\frac{a^{4\alpha}-1}{4\alpha}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left(1+3w_{\phi}\right)\ln a\,.

(21)

where

\displaystyle\lambda

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\sqrt{2}a_{\max}^{\alpha-1}x_{\max}

(22)

and we introduced a dimensionless parameter

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{M_{\mathrm{DM}}}{T}\,,

(23)

with
x_{\max}\equiv x\left(T_{\max}\right)
and
T_{\max}
given
in eq. (
15
).

If the mass of DM particles is sufficiently smaller than the maximal
temperature,
M_{\mathrm{DM}}<T_{\max}
, the integral in eq. (
19
)
can be evaluated using the Laplace approximation. In that case we
can take the freeze-in scale factor to be
a_{\mathrm{fi}}>1
. The approximate
value of
a_{\mathrm{fi}}
, which depends on the sign of
\alpha
, is

\displaystyle a_{\mathrm{fi}}

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle\left[\frac{6\vartheta+1+3w_{\phi}}{2\sqrt{2}\lambda\left|\alpha\right|^{\frac{1}{4}}\left(1-\theta\right)}\right]^{\frac{1}{1-\vartheta}}\,.

(24)

At
a_{\mathrm{fi}}
the comoving energy density of DM particles freezes
in and remains constants thereafter. That value is given by

\displaystyle a^{3}\rho_{\mathrm{DM}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{16a_{\mathrm{fi}}^{4}}{\pi^{5/2}}\cdot M_{\mathrm{DM}}^{2}\left\langle\sigma\left|v\right|\right\rangle\frac{M_{\mathrm{DM}}^{5}}{H_{\mathrm{I}}}\left(\frac{1-\vartheta}{6\vartheta+1+3w_{\phi}}\right)^{\frac{7}{2}}\left(\frac{a_{\mathrm{fi}}^{1-\vartheta}}{\mathrm{e}}\right)^{\frac{6\vartheta+1+3w_{\phi}}{2\left(1-\vartheta\right)}}\frac{a_{\mathrm{fi}}^{-3\vartheta}}{1-\vartheta}\,.

(25)

Utilising the constancy of
a^{3}\rho_{\mathrm{DM}}
we can estimate the
present day DM abundance

\displaystyle\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{DM}}}{\Omega_{\mathrm{r}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\rho_{\mathrm{DM}}^{\mathrm{reh}}}{\rho_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{reh}}}\left[\frac{g_{*}\left(T_{\mathrm{td}}\right)}{g_{*}}\right]^{\frac{1}{3}}\left(\frac{a_{\mathrm{td}}}{a_{\mathrm{reh}}}\right)\,,

(26)

where ‘
\mathrm{td}
’ denotes values evaluated today (remember that
g_{*}\equiv g_{*}\left(T_{\mathrm{reh}}\right)
).

\Omega_{\mathrm{DM}}
and
\Omega_{\mathrm{r}}
denote the current values of DM
and radiation respectively. Plugging eq. (
1
) into the above
expression we find

\displaystyle\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{DM}}}{\Omega_{\mathrm{r}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{30}{\pi^{2}g_{*}}\frac{\rho_{\mathrm{DM}}^{\mathrm{reh}}}{T_{\mathrm{reh}}^{3}T_{\mathrm{td}}}\,.

(27)

Computing
\rho_{\mathrm{DM}}^{\mathrm{reh}}
from eq. (
25
) we can
write

\displaystyle x_{\mathrm{reh}}\equiv\frac{M_{\mathrm{DM}}}{T_{\mathrm{reh}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\frac{g_{*}}{80\sqrt{3\pi}}\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{r}}m_{\mathrm{Pl}}}{\Omega_{\mathrm{DM}}T_{\mathrm{td}}}M_{\mathrm{DM}}^{2}\left\langle\sigma\left|v\right|\right\rangle\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{6\vartheta+1+3w_{\phi}}{2\left(1-\vartheta\right)}}}{1-\vartheta}\cdot\left(\frac{6\vartheta+1+3w_{\phi}}{4\left(1-\vartheta\right)}\right)^{\frac{7\vartheta+2+3w_{\phi}}{2\left(1-\vartheta\right)}}\right]^{\frac{2\left(1-\vartheta\right)}{10\vartheta-1+3w_{\phi}}}\,,

(28)

where
T_{\mathrm{reh}}
is given in eq. (
18
). The
\Omega_{\mathrm{r}}m_{\mathrm{Pl}}/\Omega_{\mathrm{DM}}T_{\mathrm{td}}

factor in this expression is fixed by observations. Taking
T_{\mathrm{td}}=2.725\,\text{K}

(
Fixsen 2009
)
, which translates to
\Omega_{\mathrm{r}}h^{2}\simeq 2.5\times 10^{-5}
,
and
\Omega_{\mathrm{DM}}h^{2}\simeq 0.12

(
Aghanim et al. 2020
)
, where

h
is the dimensionless Hubble parameter today defined as
H_{\mathrm{td}}=100h\text{ km/s/Mpc}
,
we can compute this factor to be

\displaystyle\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{DM}}}{\Omega_{\mathrm{r}}}\frac{T_{\mathrm{td}}}{m_{\mathrm{Pl}}}

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle 4.6\times 10^{-28}\,.

(29)

Eq. (
28
) is one of the main equations of this work. It gives
the relation between DM particle mass and the reheating temperature.
For later convenience we can also compute the
x_{\max}
value, which
is

\displaystyle x_{\max}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x_{\mathrm{reh}}\frac{a_{\max}^{1-\alpha}}{\left|\alpha\right|^{\frac{1}{4}}}\left(\frac{\pi^{2}g_{*}}{30}\frac{M_{\mathrm{DM}}^{4}}{x_{\mathrm{reh}}^{4}\rho_{\mathrm{I}}}\right)^{\frac{1-\vartheta}{3\left(1+w_{\phi}\right)}}\,.

(30)

III
Gravitational Waves

III.1
The Spectrum

After inflation is over, the Universe is unavoidably filled with a
background of primordial GWs that have an almost scale invariant spectrum

(
Lyth and Liddle 2009
)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{h}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{8}{m_{\mathrm{Pl}}^{2}}\left(\frac{H_{k}}{2\pi}\right)^{2}\,,

(31)

where
H_{k}
is the Hubble parameter during inflation at the moment
when the
k
mode exits the horizon. For our purpose it is sufficient
to assume that
H_{k}\simeq\mathrm{const}
during inflation, which
makes
\mathcal{P}_{h}
in eq. (
31
) scale invariant.

After inflation, when those modes reenter the horizon, they behave
as radiation, in particular, they are redshifted according to
\rho_{hk}\left(k<aH\right)\propto a^{-4}
.
On the contrary, the behaviour of the dominant scalar field energy
density depends on the shape of the potential in eq. (
7
) .
For
n>2
, or equivalently
w_{\phi}>\frac{1}{3}
in eq. (
8
),
the energy density of the oscillating inflaton dilutes faster than
radiation. In those cases the relative contribution of GWs to the
total energy budget of the Universe increases. The earlier the mode
reenters the horizon before the end of reheating, the longer the relative
energy density of that modes grows. This modifies the primordial spectrum
of GWs by making the large frequency part blue tilted. More concretely,
the GW spectrum today can be written as
(
Figueroa and Tanin 2019
;
Haque et al. 2021
)

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mathrm{GW}}\left(f\right)

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mathrm{\mathrm{GW}}}^{\mathrm{rd}}\times\begin{cases}1&f<f_{\mathrm{reh}}\\
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\frac{f}{f_{\mathrm{reh}}}\right)^{-2\frac{1-3w_{\phi}}{1+3w_{\phi}}}&f>f_{\mathrm{reh}}\end{cases}\,,

(32)

where
f
denotes the present day frequency of GW modes in units
of Hz and
f_{\mathrm{reh}}
is the frequency of the mode that reenters the
horizon at the end of reheating.
\Omega_{\mathrm{\mathrm{GW}}}^{\mathrm{rd}}
is
the present day amplitude of GW modes that reenter the horizon after
reheating, that is, during the radiation domination. Because the radiation
dominated background scales with the same power as GWs at this epoch,
the slope of the GW spectrum is not modified for such frequencies.
Hence,
\Omega_{\mathrm{\mathrm{GW}}}^{\mathrm{rd}}
is given by

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mathrm{\mathrm{GW}}}^{\mathrm{rd}}

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{k}\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{r}}}{36\pi^{2}}\frac{\rho_{\mathrm{I}}}{m_{\mathrm{Pl}}^{4}}\,.

(33)

In Ref.
(
Figueroa and Tanin 2019
)

\mathcal{G}_{k}
is approximated
by
\mathcal{G}_{k}\simeq 0.39
, which we also adopt in this work.
The
w_{\phi}
dependent constant
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{s}}
is
given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{s}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}\left(1+3w_{\phi}\right)^{\frac{4}{1+3w_{\phi}}}\Gamma^{2}\left(\frac{5+3w_{\phi}}{2\left(1+3w_{\phi}\right)}\right)\,,

(34)

where
\Gamma
denotes the Gamma function. In eq. (
32
) we
do not include the modes that reenter the horizon after matter-radiation
equality. These modes are of a very low frequency and will play no
role in our analysis.

As can be seen from eq. (
28
), the DM abundance today fixes
the ratio between the reheating temperature and DM particle mass.
In its turn, knowing
T_{\mathrm{reh}}
, we can compute
k_{\mathrm{reh}}\equiv a_{\mathrm{reh}}H_{\mathrm{reh}}

and therefore the corresponding frequency
f_{\mathrm{reh}}
of that mode
today
(
Figueroa and Tanin 2019
)

\displaystyle T_{\mathrm{reh}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 9\times 10^{-12}\left(\frac{200}{g_{*}}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}\left(\frac{f_{\mathrm{reh}}}{\text{Hz}}\right)m_{\mathrm{Pl}}\,,

(35)

where we used
\mathcal{G}_{k}\simeq 0.39
as stated above. We can
invert this expression and write

\displaystyle f_{\mathrm{reh}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{10^{11}}{x_{\mathrm{reh}}}\left(\frac{g_{*}}{200}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}\frac{M_{\mathrm{DM}}}{m_{\mathrm{Pl}}}\,\text{ Hz}\,.

(36)

where
x_{\mathrm{reh}}
is given in eq. (
28
). The above expression
is another main result of this work, which relates the DM mass with
the GW frequency corresponding to reheating.

III.2
The Bound from the Big Bang Nucleosynthesis

The epoch of the Big Bang Nucleosynthesis (BBN) can be used to put
some of the tightest bounds on new physics at that period. Any modification
of the standard scenario results in different ratios of light element
abundances in the Universe, which are tightly constrained by observations

(
Workman et al. 2022
)
. In particular, gravitons are relativistic
species. If their contribution to the overall energy density during
BBN is too large, the thermal history of the Universe changes and
the very successful standard BBN predictions are modified. This limits
the amount of GWs at BBN. The bound becomes especially severe for
strongly blue tilted spectra
(
Ferreira and Joyce 1998
;
Tashiro, Chiba, and Sasaki 2004
)
.

To compute the total energy density of GWs we can integrate the spectral
energy distribution over all frequencies

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mathrm{GW}}^{\mathrm{BBN}}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\intop_{f_{\mathrm{BBN}}}^{f_{\max}}\Omega_{\mathrm{GW}}\left(f\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}f}{f}\,,

(37)

where
\Omega_{\mathrm{GW}}\left(f\right)
is the GW density parameter today.

f_{\mathrm{BBN}}
corresponds to the frequency of modes that reenter the
horizon at BBN. The largest frequency
f_{\max}
is taken for
k_{\max}

modes that exit the horizon at the end of inflation. To leave BBN
predictions intact, the upper limit
\Omega_{\mathrm{GW}}^{\mathrm{BBN}}h^{2}<1.12\times 10^{-6}

must be satisfied
(
Caprini and Figueroa 2018
)
. The function
\Omega_{\mathrm{GW}}\left(f\right)

is provided in eq. (
32
). As can be seen, in the region
f>f_{\mathrm{reh}}

the spectrum is blue tilted for
w_{\phi}>1/3
. Thus, in practice,
the integral in eq. (
37
) is dominated by the largest frequency
modes. Using this fact, we can write the bound in eq. (
37
)
as
(
Figueroa and Tanin 2019
)

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mathrm{GW}}\left(f_{\max}\right)h^{2}

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mathrm{GW}}^{\mathrm{BBN}}h^{2}=2.24\times 10^{-6}\times\frac{3w_{\phi}-1}{3w_{\phi}+1}\,.

(38)

The value of
f_{\max}
can be computed in terms of
f_{\mathrm{reh}}
.
By construction we have
f_{\max}/f_{\mathrm{reh}}=k_{\max}/k_{\mathrm{reh}}=H_{\mathrm{I}}/a_{\mathrm{reh}}H_{\mathrm{reh}}
,
where the Hubble parameter scales as in eq. (
11
) during reheating.
Using the Friedman equation
\rho_{\mathrm{I}}=3m_{\mathrm{Pl}}^{2}H_{\mathrm{I}}^{2}
and
eq. (
1
) we find

\displaystyle\frac{f_{\max}}{f_{\mathrm{reh}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(4\times 10^{10}\>\frac{\rho_{\mathrm{I}}^{1/4}}{m_{\mathrm{Pl}}}\frac{\text{Hz}}{f_{\mathrm{reh}}}\right)^{\frac{2\left(1+3w_{\phi}\right)}{3\left(1+w_{\phi}\right)}}\,,

(39)

where we also employed eq. (
35
). Plugging this value into
the bound in eq. (
38
), we finally obtain the upper limit
of the inflation energy scale that is consistent with BBN bound

\displaystyle\frac{\rho_{\mathrm{I}}^{1/4}}{m_{\mathrm{Pl}}}

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\left(\frac{36\pi^{2}}{\mathcal{G}_{k}\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{s}}}\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{GW}}^{\mathrm{BBN}}}{\Omega_{\mathrm{r}}}\right)^{\frac{3\left(1+w_{\phi}\right)}{8\left(1+3w_{\phi}\right)}}\left(2\times 10^{-11}\frac{f_{\mathrm{reh}}}{\text{Hz}}\right)^{\frac{3w_{\phi}-1}{2\left(1+3w_{\phi}\right)}}\,.

(40)

IV
The Parameter Space

To find models that are compatible with observations we scan over
the range of
M_{\mathrm{DM}}
and
\rho_{\mathrm{I}}
parameters for given values
of
w_{\phi}
and
\nu
. We also identify the regions of this parameter
space that could be probed by the future GW observatories. Bellow
we explain how the upper and lower limits of
M_{\mathrm{DM}}
and
\rho_{\mathrm{I}}

are computed.

First, to compute eq. (
25
) we assumed the lower bound
of
M_{\mathrm{DM}}
to be
M_{\mathrm{DM}}>T_{\mathrm{reh}}
. But to ensure that the universe
is radiation dominated at BBN we must also take
T_{\mathrm{reh}}>T_{\mathrm{BBN}}\simeq 1\text{ MeV}
.
Conveniently, the bound
M_{\mathrm{DM}}>T_{\mathrm{reh}}>T_{\mathrm{BBN}}
can be written
as

\displaystyle x_{\mathrm{reh}}

\displaystyle>1\,;

(41)

\displaystyle M_{\mathrm{DM}}

\displaystyle>x_{\mathrm{\mathrm{reh}}}T_{\mathrm{BBN}}\,.

The second inequality provides us with the lower bound for
M_{\mathrm{DM}}

values. As we will see bellow, in some cases this allows for very
small
M_{\mathrm{DM}}
, which, one might worry, must be excluded by DM detection
experiments. However, that depends on the DM cross section. To remain
generic, we do not take into account such experiments in our analysis.

To set the upper bound, we note that to compute eq. (
25
),
we also assumed
M_{\mathrm{DM}}<T_{\max}
. For larger
M_{\mathrm{DM}}
values
DM production is exponentially suppressed. Using the value of
x_{\max}

in eq. (
30
) we can write this bound as

\displaystyle M_{\mathrm{DM}}

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle x_{\mathrm{reh}}\left(\frac{30\rho_{\mathrm{I}}}{\pi^{2}g_{*}}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}\left(\frac{\left|\alpha\right|^{\frac{1}{4}}}{x_{\mathrm{reh}}a_{\max}^{1-\alpha}}\right)^{\frac{3\left(1+w_{\phi}\right)}{4\left(1-\vartheta\right)}}\,.

(42)

As can be seen from the above inequality, the largest allowed DM particle
mass depends on the energy scale of inflation
\rho_{\mathrm{I}}
. There
are two possible upper limits for
\rho_{\mathrm{I}}
. First,
\rho_{\mathrm{I}}

is limited by the observational constraints on the GWs at
the pivot scale
k_{*}=0.05\text{ Mpc}^{-1}
, that are measured from
CMB observations
(
Ade et al. 2021
)
. This constraint is usually
expressed in terms of the tensor-to-scalar ratio

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{A_{h}}{A_{\mathrm{s}}}\,,

(43)

where
A_{h}
and
A_{\mathrm{s}}
are the amplitudes of the tensor
and scalar spectra at
k_{*}
respectively.
A_{h}
is defined as

A_{h}=\mathcal{P}_{h}\left(k_{*}\right)
, where
\mathcal{P}_{h}

is given in eq. (
31
) and
A_{\mathrm{s}}
is determined by
the Planck normalisation
\ln\left(10^{10}A_{\mathrm{s}}\right)=3.043\pm 0.014

(
Aghanim et al. 2020
)
. Using eq. (
31
) we can write the inflation
energy scale
\rho_{*}
as

\displaystyle\rho_{*}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{3}{2}r\pi^{2}A_{\mathrm{s}}m_{\mathrm{Pl}}^{4}\,.

(44)

At the end of inflation
\rho_{\mathrm{I}}<\rho_{*}
. Hence, plugging in

r<0.036

(
Ade et al. 2021
)
results in

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{I}}^{1/4}

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle 5.8\times 10^{-3}m_{\mathrm{Pl}}\,.

(45)

The above constraint might not be the tightest one. In certain parameter
regions, the requirement that GW do not spoil the predictions of BBN,
as it is expressed in eq. (
40
), might provide more
stringent constraint. Hence, the maximum value of
\rho_{\mathrm{I}}
will
be determined by the tighter bound of the two.

Plugging the upper bound of
\rho_{\mathrm{I}}
into eq. (
42
),
we can find the maximum value of allowed DM particle mass. If the
constraint in eq. (
45
) is tighter than the one in eq. (
40
),
the upper bound on
M_{\mathrm{DM}}
is

\displaystyle\frac{M_{\mathrm{DM}}}{m_{\mathrm{Pl}}}

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle 5.8\times 10^{-3}x_{\mathrm{reh}}\left(\frac{30}{\pi^{2}g_{*}}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}\left(\frac{\left|\alpha\right|^{\frac{1}{4}}}{x_{\mathrm{reh}}a_{\max}^{1-\alpha}}\right)^{\frac{3\left(1+w_{\phi}\right)}{4\left(1-\vartheta\right)}}\,.

(46)

In the opposite case, plugging the upper value of eq. (
40

into (
42

\displaystyle\frac{M_{\mathrm{DM}}}{m_{\mathrm{Pl}}}

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle 0.81^{-\frac{5+9w_{\phi}}{3\left(1+w_{\phi}\right)}}x_{\mathrm{reh}}\left(\frac{1080}{g_{*}\mathcal{G}_{k}\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{s}}}\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{GW}}^{\mathrm{BBN}}}{\Omega_{\mathrm{r}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}\left(\frac{\left|\alpha\right|^{\frac{1}{4}}}{x_{\mathrm{reh}}a_{\max}^{1-\alpha}}\right)^{\frac{2\left(1+3w_{\phi}\right)}{4\left(1-\vartheta\right)}}\,.

(47)

46

V
Summary and Conclusions

The properties of DM particles, that are required to reproduce the
present day abundance, depend on the thermal history of the Universe.
This is especially true if DM is produced via the so called freeze-in
mechanism. We can use this sensitivity to constrain the earliest epochs
of the evolution of the Universe.

In this work we study the freeze-in DM production mechanism and how
it is affected by the non-standard expansion history during reheating.
In order to reproduce the observed present day abundance, DM particle
mass is related to the reheating temperature as shown in eq. (
28
).
The relation, among other parameters, also depends on the effective
equation of state,
w_{\phi}
, during reheating. To fix that value
another set of observations is needed, which is provided by GW observatories.

Observations of GWs give us a new tool to study the Universe and the
very early stages of its evolution. Quantum vacuum fluctuations during
inflation leave the Universe filled with a stochastic GW background.
The primordial spectrum of this background is (quasi-)flat. The ensuing
evolution modifies a part of that spectrum. Any period where the expansion
rate of the Universe deviates from the radiation dominated one, tilts
the spectrum within the range of subhorizon modes. In particular,
the expansion rate that is faster than the radiation dominated one,
makes the corresponding part of the spectrum blue tilted. Depending
on the magnitude of the deviation and the scale of inflation, the
tilt can be sufficiently large to make such GW signal directly observable.
In such cases, we can use eq. (
28
) to constrain the inflation
energy scale, the reheating temperature and the expansion rate during
the reheating.

In Figure
3
we show how the spectrum of GWs is related
to the main three parameters of the model: DM particle mass
M_{\mathrm{DM}}
,
the expansion rate during reheating and inflation energy scale. In
principle, the spectrum depends on the number of relativistic degrees
of freedom during reheating,
g_{*}
, and the thermal average cross
section for annihilation of DM
\left\langle\sigma\left|v\right|\right\rangle
.
However, as we show in Figure
4
, this dependence is
very strongly suppressed.

Finally, we show the allowed parameter space of such models for several
expansion rates (parametrised by
w_{\phi}
) in Figure
5
.
We can see that in the case of large enough inflation energy scale
and
3\leq n\leq 5

\left(1/2\leq w_{\phi}\leq 2/3\right)
, such models
produce GWs that are detectable by future observatories. We can also
look at this result differently: if DM are produced by the freeze-in
process, the measurement of the
M_{\mathrm{DM}}
and the GW spectrum will
allow us to constrain the reheating temperature and inflation energy
scale. For some values of
M_{\mathrm{DM}}
the latter constraint can more
than an order of magnitude tighter than the predicted bound from the
future CMB Stage-4 project.
