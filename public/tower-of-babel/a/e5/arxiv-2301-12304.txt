Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process and generalizations: particle’s dynamics under comb constraints and stochastic resetting
Pece Trajanovski 1 , Petar Jolakoski 1 , Kiril Zelenkovski 1 , Alexander Iomin 2,3 , Ljupco Kocarev 1,4 , Trifce Sandev 1,5,6

Abstract
The Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process is interpreted as Brownian motion in a harmonic potential. This Gaussian Markov process has a bounded variance and admits a stationary probability distribution, in contrast to the standard Brownian motion. It also tends to a drift towards its mean function, and such a process is called mean-reverting. Two examples of the generalized Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process are considered. In the first one, we study the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process on a comb model, as an example of the harmonically bounded random motion in the topologically constrained geometry. The main dynamical characteristics (as the first and the second moments) and the probability density function are studied in the framework of both the Langevin stochastic equation and the Fokker-Planck equation. The second example is devoted to the study of the effects of stochastic resetting on the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, including stochastic resetting in the comb geometry. Here, the non-equilibrium stationary state is the main question in task, where the two divergent forces, namely the resetting and the drift towards the mean, lead to compelling results both in the case of the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process with resetting and its generalization on the two dimensional comb structure.

Introduction

Statistical treatment of Brownian motion independently suggested by Einstein

In contemporary studies, it has been established that non-Markov anomalous transport is more general and ubiquitous topic across different fields of science. Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process

In this section we set the scene for the generalisations of the O-U process. We do that by laying out the results for the probability density function (PDF) and the first two moments of the displacement for the standard O-U process, as well explaining the properties of the process. We define the standard O-U process in terms of the modified stochastic Langevin equation

III
Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process with resetting

In this section we consider the problem of the O-U process in presence of stochastic Poissonian resetting
Evans2011
. This means that between two consecutive resetting events, the particle undergoes the O-U process driven towards the long term mean value
\mu
. The resetting of the particle is done to the initial position
x=x_{0}
, and the process is randomly repeated. The interplay of these two random phenomena results in a completely new renewal process with effects different from the standard O-U process.

Before studying the analytic properties of the PDF of the O-U process with resetting in the framework of a renewal equation, we simulate the random trajectory by means of a discretized Langevin equation.

III.1
Langevin equation approach

To define the one dimensional O-U process with Poissonian resetting, we take into account the Langevin equation (
1
) for the O-U process and follow the concept of Poisonnian resetting. Namely, let us consider resetting with the rate
r
to a fixed position. In our case, it is the initial position
x(0)=x_{0}
. If we suppose that at the time
t=\tau\Delta t
the random particle is at the position
x(t)=x(\tau\Delta t)
, then for the next small time interval
\Delta t
its dynamics is defined either by reset to the position
x_{0}
with the probability
r\Delta t
, or by the O-U motion according to the Langevin Eq. (
1
). Therefore, this dichotomous process can be simulated in the framework of the discretized Langevin equation,

\displaystyle x(\tau\Delta t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}x(0),\,\,\textrm{with prob.}\,\,r\,\Delta t,\\
\\
x[(\tau-1)\Delta t]+\lambda\,\big[\mu-x[(\tau-1)\Delta t]\big]+&\\
\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\,\xi[(\tau-1)\Delta t],\,\,\textrm{with prob.}\,\,(1-r\,\Delta t),&\end{array}\right.

where
x(0)=x_{0}
is the initial particle’s position. Here, we introduce the probability
r\Delta t
for the diffusing particle to be reset to the initial position
x_{0}
, therefore the process is starting from the beginning, and respectively the probability
(1-r\Delta t)
for the process to continue evolving according to the Langevin equation (
III.1
)
Evans2011
;
Evans2020
. The properties of the discretized white noise are defined by zero mean
\langle\xi(\tau\Delta t)\rangle=0
, and the correlation function
\langle\xi(\tau\Delta t)\xi(\tau^{\prime}\Delta t)\rangle=\delta((\tau-\tau^{\prime})\Delta t)
.

III.2
Probability density function and non-equilibrium stationary state

From the Langevin description of the O-U process with stochastic resetting one can find the governing Fokker-Planck equation, which reads

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}P_{r}(x,t)=L_{FP}P_{r}(x,t)-r\,P_{r}(x,t)+r\,\delta(x-x_{0}),

(14)

with the initial condition
P_{r}(x,t=0)=P(x,t=0)=\delta(x-x_{0})
and zero boundary conditions at infinity. Here,
L_{FP}
is defined in Eq. (
3
) and
-r
is the loss of the probability at the
position
x
due to the reset to the initial position
x=x_{0}
, while the gain of the probability takes place with the rate
+r
at the initial position
x_{0}
.

To find the solution to Eq. (
14

\mathcal{L}[P_{r}(x,t)]=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-st}P_{r}(x,t)\,dt=\hat{P}_{r}(x,s)
,
which yields the expression

\displaystyle s\,\hat{P}_{r}(x,s)-\delta(x-x_{0})=\frac{s}{s+r}\,L_{FP}\hat{P}_{r}(x,s).

(15)

Then the inverse Laplace transform yields Eq.(
14

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}P_{r}(x,t)=\frac{d}{dt}\int_{0}^{t}\eta(t-t^{\prime})\,L_{FP}P_{r}(x,t^{\prime})\,dt^{\prime}.

(16)

Here

\hat{\eta}(s)=\frac{1}{s+r}

Then, we look for the solution of Eq. (

\displaystyle P_{r}(x,t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}P_{0}(x,u)\,h(u,t)\,du,

(17)

where

is the O-U solution of Eq. (
2

\displaystyle\hat{h}(u,s)=\frac{1}{s\,\hat{\eta}(s)}e^{-\frac{u}{\hat{\eta}(s)}},

(18)

we obtain the following expression for the Laplace image of the PDF

\displaystyle\hat{P}_{r}(x,s)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}P_{0}(x,u)\,\hat{h}(u,s)\,du

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{s\,\hat{\eta}(s)}\int_{0}^{\infty}P_{0}(x,u)\,e^{-\frac{u}{\hat{\eta}(s)}}\,du

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{s\,\hat{\eta}(s)}\,\hat{P}_{0}\left(x,1/\hat{\eta}(s)\right)=\frac{s+r}{s}\,\hat{P}_{0}(x,s+r).

\displaystyle\langle x^{2}(t)\rangle_{r}=e^{-rt}\,\langle x^{2}(t)\rangle+\int_{0}^{t}r\,e^{-rt^{\prime}}\,\langle x^{2}(t^{\prime})\rangle\,dt^{\prime},

IV
Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process on comb

In this section, we employ a comb model for the O-U process. We follow the phenomenological Fokker-Planck equation, suggested in Ref.
Arkhincheev1991
and extensively explored in a variety of applications, see Refs.
Iomin2018
;
book_ws
. According to the comb model, the two dimensional transport consists of two independent processes, shown in Fig.
5
. The first is the O-U process, which takes place along the
x
axis exactly at
y=0
, and this axis is called the backbone, and the corresponding motion is the backbone transport. In the
y
direction, there is Brownian motion with the diffusion coefficient
\sigma^{2}_{y}/2
, which is the side-branched motion and the direction is called fingers, or side-branches.
The corresponding Fokker-Planck equation for this process is

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}P(x,y,t)=\delta(y)\,L_{FP,x}P(x,y,t)+\frac{\sigma_{y}^{2}}{2}\,\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial y^{2}}P(x,y,t),

(34)

with the initial condition
P(x,y,t=0)=\delta(x-x_{0})\,\delta(y)
and zero boundary conditions at infinity. The Fokker-Planck operator on the backbone reads
2
2

2

Note that the complete form of the Fokker-Planck operator contains
\delta(y)
, as well. We however keep its present form
of Eq. (
3
) to separate the O-U process and to stress it in the ensuing analysis.

\displaystyle L_{FP,x}\equiv\lambda\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(x-\mu)+\frac{\sigma_{x}^{2}}{2}\,\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}.

(35)

Note that
\lambda\delta(y)
now is the rate of mean-reversion, such that
\lambda
is the velocity of mean-reversion. Bearing this change in mind, we also admit that the backbone diffusion coefficient is
\delta(y)\sigma_{x}^{2}/2
. In what follows we keep the same notation for the velocity of mean-reversion to stress the O-U process along the backbone.

Two dimensional comb structure. The backbone along the
x
axis has continuously distributed fingers (or branches) along the
y
axis. The O-U transport takes place along the backbone, while Brownian motion is in fingers.

IV.1
Fokker-Planck equations for the marginal probability density functions

Inferring the differential equations for the movement of the particle along the backbone and the fingers separately, we introduce the corresponding marginal PDFs as follows

\displaystyle p_{1}(x,t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}P(x,y,t)\,dy,

(36)

and

\displaystyle p_{2}(y,t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}P(x,y,t)\,dx.

(37)

Introducing new notations
3
3

3

Note also that the diffusion coefficient in the fingers is
\mathcal{D}_{y}=\sigma_{y}^{2}/2
, while the backbone diffusion coefficient is
\delta(y)\,\sigma_{x}^{2}/2=\delta(y)\,\mathcal{D}_{x}
.

\mathcal{D}_{x}=\frac{\sigma_{x}^{2}}{2}
,
\mathcal{D}_{y}=\frac{\sigma_{y}^{2}}{2}
, in Eqs. (
34
) and (
35
), we arrive at the comb equation as follows

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}P(x,y,t)=\delta(y)\,L_{FP,x}P(x,y,t)+\mathcal{D}_{y}\,\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial y^{2}}P(x,y,t).

(38)

The Laplace transform of Eq. (
38
) yields

\displaystyle s\,\hat{P}(x,y,s)-\delta(x-x_{0})\,\delta(y)

\displaystyle=\delta(y)\,L_{FP,x}\hat{P}(x,y,s)

\displaystyle+\mathcal{D}_{y}\,\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial y^{2}}\hat{P}(x,y,s).

(39)

The solution
\hat{P}(x,y,s)
is presented in the following form

\displaystyle\hat{P}(x,y,s)=\hat{g}(x,s)\,e^{-\hat{r}(x,s)\,|y|},

(40)

that yields the backbone marginal PDF as follows
4
4

4

Do not confuse
\hat{r}(x,s)

with the reset rate
r
.

\displaystyle\hat{p}_{1}(x,s)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hat{g}(x,s)\,e^{-\hat{r}(x,s)|y|}\,dy=\frac{2\hat{g}(x,s)}{\hat{r}(x,s)}.

(41)

Taking into account the expression for the step sign function
\frac{d}{dy}|y|=2\theta(y)-1
, where
\theta(y)
is the Heaviside theta function, one obtains

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\hat{P}(x,y,s)=-\hat{g}(x,s)\,\hat{r}(x,s)\,e^{-\hat{r}(x,s)|y|}\big[2\theta(y)-1\big].

(42)

By using the property of the Heaviside function:
\frac{d}{dy}\theta(y)=\delta(y)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial y^{2}}\hat{P}(x,y,s)

\displaystyle=-\hat{r}(x,s)\,\hat{g}(x,s)\,\big\{2\delta(y)

\displaystyle-\hat{r}(x,s)\left[2\theta(y)-1\right]^{2}\big\}\,e^{-\hat{r}(x,s)|y|}.

(43)

Taking into account Eqs. (
40
) and (
IV.1
) and using the property
f(y)\,\delta(y)=f(0)\,\delta(y)
, we obtain Eq. (
IV.1
) as follows

\displaystyle s\,\hat{g}(x,s)\,e^{-\hat{r}(x,s)|y|}-\delta(x-x_{0})\,\delta(y)=\lambda\,\delta(y)\,\hat{g}(x,s)

\displaystyle+\lambda\,\delta(y)\,(x-\mu)\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\hat{g}(x,s)+\mathcal{D}_{x}\,\delta(y)\,\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}\hat{g}(x,s)

\displaystyle+\mathcal{D}_{y}\,\hat{r}^{2}(x,s)\,\hat{g}(x,s)\,e^{-\hat{r}(x,s)|y|}-2\delta(y)\,\mathcal{D}_{y}\,\hat{r}(x,s)\,\hat{g}(x,s).

(44)

Thus, we arrive at the system of two equations

s=\mathcal{D}_{y}\,\hat{r}^{2}(x,s)\quad\rightarrow\quad\hat{r}(x,s)=\sqrt{\frac{s}{\mathcal{D}_{y}}},

(45)

and

\displaystyle-\delta(x-x_{0})

\displaystyle=\lambda\,\hat{g}(x,s)+\lambda\,(x-\mu)\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\hat{g}(x,s)

\displaystyle+\mathcal{D}_{x}\,\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}\hat{g}(x,s)-2\mathcal{D}_{y}\,\hat{r}(x,s)\,\hat{g}(x,s).

(46)

From Eqs. (
41
) and (
45
) we derive

\hat{g}(x,s)=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{s}{\mathcal{D}_{y}}}\,\hat{p}_{1}(x,s).

(47)

Now by substituting for
\hat{g}(x,s)
in Eq. (
IV.1
) we get

\displaystyle s^{1/2}\,\hat{p}_{1}(x,s)

\displaystyle-s^{-1/2}\,\delta(x-x_{0})=\frac{\lambda}{2\sqrt{{\mathcal{D}_{y}}}}\,\hat{p}_{1}(x,s)

\displaystyle+\frac{\lambda\,(x-\mu)}{2\sqrt{\mathcal{D}_{y}}}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\,\hat{p}_{1}(x,s)+\frac{\mathcal{D}_{x}}{2\sqrt{{\mathcal{D}_{y}}}}\,\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}\hat{p}_{1}(x,s),

(48)

which by the inverse Laplace transform yields the following time fractional diffusion equation

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}p_{1}(x,t)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\mathcal{D}_{y}}}\,{{}_{\text{RL}}}D_{t}^{1/2}L_{FP,x}p_{1}(x,t),

(49)

where
{{}_{\text{RL}}}D_{t}^{\mu}
is the Riemann-Liouville fractional derivative (
C
) of order
\mu=1/2
.
It can be also written in terms of the Caputo fractional derivative, as follows

\displaystyle{{}_{\text{C}}}D_{t}^{1/2}p_{1}(x,t)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\mathcal{D}_{y}}}\,L_{FP,x}\,p_{1}(x,t),

(50)

where
{{}_{\text{C}}}D_{t}^{\mu}
is the Caputo fractional derivative (
C
) of order
\mu=1/2
. Analytical properties of the fractional O-U process, described by the
fractional Fokker-Planck equation (
49

MeKl2000

Integrating Eq. (
34

, we obtain the Fokker-Planck equation for the marginal PDF along fingers, which reads

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}p_{2}(y,t)=\mathcal{D}_{y}\,\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial y^{2}}p_{2}(y,t).

(51)

The solution to this equation is the Gaussian PDF, as expected, since the particle performs Brownian motion along the fingers.

IV.2
First moment and mean squared displacement

The MSD along the backbone can be found by multiplying both sides of the Eq. (

x^{2}
and integrating with respect to
x

\displaystyle{{}_{\text{C}}}D_{t}^{1/2}\langle x^{2}(t)\rangle_{\text{c}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda}{2\sqrt{\mathcal{D}_{y}}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x^{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\big[(x-\mu)\,p_{1}(x,t)\big]dx

\displaystyle+\frac{\mathcal{D}_{x}}{2\sqrt{\mathcal{D}_{y}}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x^{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}p_{1}(x,t)\,dx

(52)

that yields

\displaystyle{{}_{\text{C}}}D_{t}^{1/2}\langle x^{2}(t)\rangle_{\text{c}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{\mathcal{D}_{y}}}\,\langle x^{2}(t)\rangle_{\text{c}}

\displaystyle+\frac{\lambda\mu}{2\sqrt{\mathcal{D}_{y}}}\,\langle x(t)\rangle_{\text{c}}+\frac{\mathcal{D}_{x}}{\sqrt{\mathcal{D}_{y}}}.

(53)

Equation for the mean value
\langle x(t)\rangle
is obtained in the same way, and it reads

\displaystyle{{}_{\text{C}}}D_{t}^{1/2}\langle x(t)\rangle_{\text{c}}=-\frac{\lambda}{2\sqrt{\mathcal{D}_{y}}}\,\langle x(t)\rangle_{\text{c}}+\frac{\lambda\mu}{2\sqrt{\mathcal{D}_{y}}}.

(54)

In Laplace space, the mean value is

\displaystyle\langle\hat{x}(s)\rangle_{\text{c}}=\frac{x_{0}\,s^{-1/2}}{s^{1/2}+\frac{\lambda}{2\sqrt{\mathcal{D}_{y}}}}+\frac{\lambda\mu}{2\sqrt{\mathcal{D}_{y}}}\,\frac{s^{-1}}{s^{1/2}+\frac{\lambda}{2\sqrt{\mathcal{D}_{y}}}}.

(55)

Performing the inverse Laplace transform, we get

\displaystyle\langle x(t)\rangle_{\text{c}}

\displaystyle=x_{0}\,E_{1/2}\left(-\frac{\lambda}{2\sqrt{\mathcal{D}_{y}}}t^{1/2}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{\lambda\mu}{2\sqrt{\mathcal{D}_{y}}}\,t^{1/2}\,E_{1/2,3/2}\left(-\frac{\lambda}{2\sqrt{\mathcal{D}_{y}}}t^{1/2}\right),

(56)

where
E_{\alpha}(z)
and
E_{\alpha,\beta}(z)
are the one and two parameter Mittag-Leffler functions, respectively, see Eqs. (
120
) and (
119
) in Appendix
C

Now the exact expression for the MSD in Eq. (
IV.2
) can be obtained. Performing the Laplace transform of Eq. (
IV.2
) and taking into account
Eq. (
55
), we obtain the Laplace image of the MSD as follows

\displaystyle\langle\hat{x}^{2}(s)\rangle_{\text{c}}

\displaystyle=x_{0}^{2}\,\frac{s^{-1/2}}{s^{1/2}+\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{\mathcal{D}_{y}}}}+\frac{\mathcal{D}_{x}}{\sqrt{\mathcal{D}_{y}}}\,\frac{s^{-1}}{s^{1/2}+\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{\mathcal{D}_{y}}}}

\displaystyle+\frac{\lambda\mu x_{0}}{\sqrt{\mathcal{D}_{y}}}\,\frac{s^{-1/2}}{\left(s^{1/2}+\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{\mathcal{D}_{y}}}\right)\left(s^{1/2}+\frac{\lambda}{2\sqrt{\mathcal{D}_{y}}}\right)}

\displaystyle+\frac{\lambda^{2}\mu^{2}}{2\sqrt{\mathcal{D}_{y}}}\,\frac{s^{-1}}{\left(s^{1/2}+\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{D_{y}}}\right)\left(s^{1/2}+\frac{\lambda}{2\sqrt{\mathcal{D}_{y}}}\right)}.

(57)

The inverse Laplace transform yields the expression for the MSD along the backbone,

\displaystyle\langle x^{2}(t)\rangle_{\text{c}}=x_{0}^{2}\,E_{1/2}\left(-\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{\mathcal{D}_{y}}}\,t^{1/2}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{\mathcal{D}_{x}}{\sqrt{\mathcal{D}_{y}}}\,t^{1/2}\,E_{1/2,3/2}\left(-\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{\mathcal{D}_{y}}}\,t^{1/2}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{\lambda\mu x_{0}}{2\sqrt{\mathcal{D}_{y}}}\,t^{1/2}\,E_{(1/2,1),3/2}\left(-\frac{3\lambda}{2\sqrt{\mathcal{D}_{y}}}\,t^{1/2}\,\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2\mathcal{D}_{y}}\,t\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{\lambda^{2}\mu^{2}}{4\mathcal{D}_{y}}\,t\,E_{(1/2,1),2}\left(-\frac{3\lambda}{2\sqrt{\mathcal{D}_{y}}}\,t^{1/2},-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2\mathcal{D}_{y}}\,t\right),

(58)

where
E_{(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}),\beta}(z;\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
is the multinomial Mittag-Leffler function, see Eq. (
C

\displaystyle\langle x^{2}(t)\rangle_{\text{c}}\sim\mu^{2}\sqrt{\mathcal{D}_{y}}+\frac{\mathcal{D}_{x}}{\lambda}.

(59)

However, the transition to the constant MSD is slower (of the power-law decay) than the one for the one dimensional O-U process (of the exponential decay) due to the fact that the particle is hindered in the fingers before it turns back to the backbone transport. In this section we extend the problem of the O-U process on a comb by introducing stochastic resetting
prr

r

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}P_{r}(x,y,t)

\displaystyle=\delta(y)\,L_{FP,x}P_{r}(x,y,t)+\frac{\sigma_{y}^{2}}{2}\,\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial y^{2}}P_{r}(x,y,t)

p_{1}(x,t)
and
p_{2}(y,t)

VII
Summary

In this work, we investigated the O-U process in presence of stochastic resetting to the initial position of the particle. We used the Langevin equation approach to perform the numerical simulation and the Fokker-Planck equation to find analytical results for the PDF, NESS and the MSD. We also performed a thorough analysis of the compound effect of the comb geometry and stochastic resetting on the statistical properties of the O-U process. Even though the standard O-U process, with and without resetting, has been considered before, a geometry impact was an open question and the suggested paper on the detailed study of the influence of a comb structure and its anomalous properties can be the answer to the question. We found the corresponding Fokker-Planck equations for the marginal PDFs along the backbone and fingers of the comb in absence and presence of Poissonian resetting. The corresponding mean displacement and the MSD of the particle are calculated exactly by using the one parameter, two parameter and multinomial Mittag-Leffler functions, and the obtained results are confirmed by numerical simulations performed in the framework of the coupled Langevin equations. We also introduced the O-U process on a fractal grid structure, and we have showed that the fractal dimension of the fractal backbone structure has an influence on the PDF and MSD.

In conclusion, we admit that the O–U process is one of several classical approaches used to model interest rates, currency exchange rates, and commodity prices stochastically. The parameter
\mu
represents the equilibrium or mean value supported by fundamentals;
\sigma
plays the role of the degree of volatility around it caused by shocks, and
\lambda
becomes the rate by which these shocks dissipate and the variable reverts towards the mean. However, a plethora of studies
mantegna1995scaling
;
mantegna1999introduction
;
bassler2007nonstationary
;
gopikrishnan1999scaling
show that the distribution of returns
\log[x(t+dt)/x(t)]
has a sharper maximum and fatter tails, thus further suggesting that a simple O-U trajectory may not be an adequate representation for these types of asset dynamics, due to asymmetries found when comparing its properties with empirical distributions. In addition, an empirical trajectory of interest rates or currency exchange rates may exhibit approximately constant values between two points in time, due to market inactivity. These constant periods can be considered to be trapping of particles, as it is done in physical systems that manifest anomalous diffusion (subdiffusion)
scalas2000fractional
;
raberto2002waiting
. This empirical investigation represents a potential research avenue for application of the models considered in this work and the further analysis we are leaving for future work.

The equation for the standard O-U process is

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}P_{0}(x,t)=L_{FP}P_{0}(x,t),

(95)

\displaystyle L_{FP}\equiv\lambda\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(x-\mu)+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\,\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}},

(96)

in Fourier space. Equation (

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\tilde{P}_{0}(k,t)}{\partial t}=-\lambda k\,\frac{\partial\tilde{P}_{0}(k,t)}{\partial k}-\biggr[\frac{k^{2}\sigma^{2}}{2}+i\mu\lambda k\biggr]\tilde{P}_{0}(k,t).

(97)

The Lagrange–Charpit equations for this equation are

\displaystyle\frac{\partial t}{\partial u}=1;\,\,\frac{\partial k}{\partial u}=\lambda k;\,\,\frac{\partial\tilde{P}_{0}}{\partial u}=-\biggr[\frac{k^{2}\sigma^{2}}{2}+i\mu\lambda k\biggr]\tilde{P}_{0}(k,t),

(98)

and the parametrization invariant form of the Lagrange–Charpit equations is

\displaystyle\frac{dt}{1}=\frac{dk}{\lambda k}=\frac{d\tilde{P}_{0}(k,t)}{-\left(\frac{k^{2}\sigma^{2}}{2}+i\mu\lambda k\right)\tilde{P}_{0}(k,t)}.

(99)

From the first two terms with integration we get

\displaystyle k=k_{0}\,e^{\lambda t}\quad\rightarrow\quad k_{0}=k\,e^{-\lambda t},

(100)

and then again from the last two terms of
99
by integrating with separation of the variables we get the expression

\displaystyle\tilde{P}_{0}(k,t)=C\,e^{-\frac{i\mu\lambda k+\frac{\sigma^{2}k^{2}}{4}}{\lambda}}=C\,e^{-\frac{i\mu k_{0}e^{\lambda t}\lambda+\frac{k_{0}^{2}e^{2\lambda t}\sigma^{2}}{4}}{\lambda}}.

\displaystyle C=e^{ik_{0}x_{0}}\,e^{\frac{i\mu\lambda k_{0}+\frac{\sigma^{2}k_{0}^{2}}{4}}{\lambda}}.

(102)

Inserting the coefficient

\displaystyle\hat{P}_{0}(k,t)=e^{ikx_{0}\,e^{-\lambda t}}\,e^{-\frac{i\mu k\lambda}{\lambda}(e^{\lambda t}-1)e^{-\lambda t}-\frac{k^{2}\sigma^{2}}{4\lambda}(e^{2\lambda t}-1)e^{-2\lambda t}}.

\displaystyle P_{0}(x,t)=\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{\left[x-x_{0}e^{-\lambda t}-\mu\left(1-e^{-\lambda t}\right)\right]^{2}}{\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\lambda}e^{-2\lambda t}\left(e^{2\lambda t}-1\right)}\right)}{\sqrt{2\pi\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2\lambda}e^{-2\lambda t}\left(e^{2\lambda t}-1\right)}}.

(104)
