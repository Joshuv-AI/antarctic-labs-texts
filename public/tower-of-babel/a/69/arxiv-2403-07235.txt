Hessian estimates for shrinkers, expanders, translators, and rotators of the Lagrangian Mean Curvature Flow
Arunima Bhattacharya and Jeremy Wall

Abstract
. In this paper, we prove interior Hessian estimates for shrinkers, expanders, translators, and rotators of the Lagrangian mean curvature flow under the assumption that the Lagrangian phase is hypercritical. We further extend our results to a broader class of Lagrangian mean curvature type equations.



1.
Introduction

A family of Lagrangian submanifolds
X(x,t):\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
evolves by
Lagrangian mean curvature flow
if it solves

(1.1)

\displaystyle(X_{t})^{\bot}=\Delta_{g}X=\vec{H},

where
\vec{H}
denotes the mean curvature vector of the Lagrangian submanifold.
The mean curvature vector of the Lagrangian submanifold
(x,Du(x))\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
is determined by the Lagrangian angle or phase
\Theta
, by Harvey-Lawson
[
HL82
, Proposition 2.17]
. The Lagrangian angle is given by

(1.2)

\Theta=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\arctan\lambda_{i},

where
\lambda_{i}
are the eigenvalues of the Hessian
D^{2}u
. This angle acts as the potential of the mean curvature vector

(1.3)

\displaystyle\vec{H}=J\nabla_{g}\Theta,

where
g=I_{n}+(D^{2}u)^{2}
is the induced metric on
(x,Du(x))
, and
J
is the almost complex structure on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Thus, equation (
1.2
) is the potential equation for prescribed
Lagrangian mean curvature
. When the Lagrangian phase
\Theta
is constant,
u
solves the
special Lagrangian equation
of Harvey-Lawson
[
HL82
]
. In this case,
H=0
, and
(x,Du(x))
is a volume-minimizing Lagrangian submanifold.

After a change of coordinates, one can locally write
X(x,t)=(x,Du(x,t))
, such that
\Delta_{g}X=(J\bar{\nabla}\Theta(x,t))^{\bot}
, where
\bar{\nabla}=(\partial_{x},\partial_{y})
is the ambient gradient. This means a local potential
u(x,t)
evolves by the parabolic equation

(1.4)

\displaystyle

\displaystyle u_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\arctan\lambda_{i},

\displaystyle u(x,0):=u(x).

Symmetry reductions of (
1.1
) reduce (
1.4
) to an elliptic equation for
u(x)
. This is illustrated, for instance, in the work of Chau-Chen-He
[
CCH12
]
. These solutions model singularities of the mean curvature flow.

If
u(x)
solves

(1.5)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\arctan\lambda_{i}=s_{1}+s_{2}(x\cdot Du(x)-2u(x)),

then
X(x,t)=\sqrt{1-2s_{2}t}\,(x,Du(x))
is a
shrinker
or
expander
solution of (
1.1
), if
s_{2}>0
or
s_{2}<0
, respectively. The mean curvature of the initial submanifold
(x,Du(x))
is given by
H=-s_{2}X^{\bot}
. Entire smooth solutions to (
1.5
) for
s_{2}>0
are quadratic polynomials, by Chau-Chen-Yuan
[
CCY12
]
; see also Huang-Wang
[
HW11
]
for the smooth convex case. The circle
x^{2}+u^{\prime}(x)^{2}=1
is a closed example of a shrinker
s_{2}=1,s_{1}=0
in one dimension. We refer the reader to the work of Joyce-Lee-Tsui
[
JLT10
]
, for other non-graphical examples.

If
u(x)
solves

(1.6)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\arctan\lambda_{i}=t_{1}+t_{2}\cdot x+t_{3}\cdot Du(x),

then
X(x,t)=(x,Du(x))+t(-t_{3},t_{2})
is a
translator
solution of (
1.1
), with constant mean curvature
H=(-t_{3},t_{2})^{\bot}
. For example, in one dimension, the grim reaper curve
(x,u^{\prime}(x))=(x,-\ln\cos(x))
, for
t_{2}=1,t_{3}=t_{1}=0
. Entire solutions to (
1.6
) with Hessian bounds are quadratic polynomials, by Chau-Chen-He
[
CCH12
]
; see also Ngyuen-Yuan
[
NY11
]
for entire ancient solutions to (
1.6
) with Hessian conditions.

The Hamiltonian vector field
A\cdot z=J\bar{\nabla}\Theta
has a real potential given by
\Theta(x,y)=\frac{1}{2i}\langle z,A\cdot z\rangle_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}
if
A\in SU(n)
is skew-adjoint. Since
\exp(tA)\in U(n)
preserves the symplectic form

dz\wedge d\bar{z}=\sum dz^{i}\wedge d\bar{z}^{i}
, the Hamiltonian flow
X(x,t)=\exp(tA)(x,Du(x))
is a Lagrangian immersion with
X_{t}=AX=J\bar{\nabla}\Theta
. For
A=r_{2}J
and
\Theta(x,y)=r_{1}+\frac{r_{2}}{2}|z|^{2}
, if
u(x)
solves

(1.7)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\arctan\lambda_{i}=r_{1}+\frac{r_{2}}{2}(|x|^{2}+|Du(x)|^{2}),

then
X(x,t)=\exp(r_{2}tJ)(x,Du(x))
is a
rotator
solution of (
1.1
), with mean curvature
H=r_{2}(JX)^{\bot}
. The Yin-Yang curve of Altschuler
[
Alt91
]
is one such example in one dimension. We also refer the reader to the notes of Yuan
[
Yua
, pg. 3]
.

A broader class of equations of interest that generalize equations (
1.5
), (
1.6
), (
1.7
), among others, are the

Lagrangian mean curvature type equations

(1.8)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\arctan\lambda_{i}=\Theta(x,u(x),Du(x)).

The study of Lagrangian mean curvature-type equations is driven by a geometric interest, particularly because of the notable special cases illustrated above; see
[
Yua20
,
BS20
]
for a detailed discussion.

In this paper, we prove interior Hessian estimates for shrinkers, expanders, translators, and rotators of the Lagrangian mean curvature flow and further extend these results to the broader class of Lagrangian mean curvature-type equations. We assume the Lagrangian phase to be hypercritical, i.e.
|\Theta|\geq(n-1)\frac{\pi}{2}
. This results in the convexity of the potential of the initial Lagrangian submanifold. For certain
\Theta=\Theta(x)
, smooth convex solutions were constructed by Wang-Huang-Bao
[
WHB23
]
satisfying
Du(\Omega_{1})=\Omega_{2}
for prescribed uniformly convex smooth domains
\Omega_{i}
, following Brendle-Warren
[
BW10
]
for the constant
\Theta
case; see also Huang
[
Hua15
]
for a construction using Lagrangian mean curvature flow.

Notations.
Before we present our main results, we clarify some terminology.

I.

By
B_{R}
we denote a ball of radius
R
centered at the origin.

II.

We denote the oscillation of
u
in
B_{R}
by
\mathrm{osc}_{B_{R}}(u)
.

III.

Let
\Gamma_{R}=B_{R}\times u(B_{R})\times Du(B_{R})\subset B_{R}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Let
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}
be constants such that for
\Theta(x,z,p)
, we have the following structure conditions

(1.9)

\displaystyle|\Theta_{x}|,|\Theta_{z}|,|\Theta_{p}|

\displaystyle\leq\nu_{1},

\displaystyle|\Theta_{xx}|,|\Theta_{xz}|,|\Theta_{xp}|,|\Theta_{zz}|,|\Theta_{zp}|

\displaystyle\leq\nu_{2}

for all
(x,z,p)\in\Gamma_{R}
. In the above partial derivatives, the variables
x,z,p
are treated as independent of each other. Observe that this indicates that the above partial derivatives do not have any
D^{2}u
or
D^{3}u
terms.

Our main results are the following:

Theorem 1.1
.

If
u
is a
C^{4}
solution of any of these equations: (
1.5
), (
1.6
), and (
1.7
) on
B_{R}(0)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
where
|\Theta|\geq(n-1)\frac{\pi}{2}
, then we have

|D^{2}u(0)|\leq C_{1}\exp[C_{2}(\mathrm{osc}_{B_{R}}(u)/R^{2})^{4n-2}]

where
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are positive constants depending on
n
and the following:

(1)

s_{2}
for (
1.5
)

(2)

t_{2},t_{3}
for (
1.6
)

(3)

r_{2}
for (
1.7
).

Remark 1.1
.

In the case of equation (
1.6
), since there is no gradient dependence in the derivative of the phase, the precise estimate obtained is

|D^{2}u(0)|\leq C_{1}\exp[C_{2}(\mathrm{osc}_{B_{R}}(u)/R^{2})^{3n-2}].

Theorem 1.2
.

Suppose that
u
is a
C^{4}
solution of (
1.8
) on
B_{R}(0)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
|\Theta|\geq(n-1)\frac{\pi}{2}
,
\Theta(x,z,p)\in C^{2}(\Gamma_{R})
is partially convex in the
p
variable, and satisfies the structure conditions given by (
1.9
). Then we have

|D^{2}u(0)|\leq C_{1}\exp[C_{2}(\mathrm{osc}_{B_{R}}(u)/R^{2})^{4n-2}]

where
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are positive constants depending on
n
,
\nu_{1}
,
\nu_{2}
.

Remark 1.2
.

From the singular solutions constructed in
[
BS20
, (1.13)]
, it is evident that the Hessian estimates in Theorem
1.2
will not hold without partial convexity of
\Theta
in the gradient variable
Du
.

One application of the above results is that
C^{0}
viscosity solutions to (
1.5
),(
1.6
), and (
1.7
) with
|\Theta|\geq(n-1)\frac{\pi}{2}
are analytic inside the domain of the solution, as explained in Remark
5.1
.

The concavity of the arctangent operator in (
1.2
) is closely associated with the range of the Lagrangian phase.
The phase
(n-2)\frac{\pi}{2}
is called critical because the level set
\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{n}|\lambda
satisfying
\eqref{sl}\}
is convex only when
|\Theta|\geq(n-2)\frac{\pi}{2}

[
Yua02
, Lemma 2.2]
. The arctangent operator is concave if
u
is convex. The concavity of the level set is evident for
|\Theta|\geq(n-1)\frac{\pi}{2}
since that implies
\lambda>0
, making
F
concave. The phase
|\Theta|\geq(n-1)\frac{\pi}{2}
is called hypercritical. The phase
|\Theta|\geq(n-2)\frac{\pi}{2}+\delta
is called supercritical. The phase
|\Theta|\geq(n-2)\frac{\pi}{2}
is called critical and supercritical.
For solutions of the special Lagrangian equation with critical and supercritical phase
|\Theta|\geq(n-2)\frac{\pi}{2}
, Hessian estimates have been obtained by Warren-Yuan
[
WY09
,
WY10
]
, Wang-Yuan
[
WY14
]
; see also Li
[
Li19
]
for a compactness approach and Zhou
[
Zho22
]
for estimates requiring Hessian constraints which generalize criticality. The singular
C^{1,\alpha}
solutions to (
1.2
) constructed by Nadirashvili-Vlăduţ
[
NV10
]
and Wang-Yuan
[
WY13
]
show that interior regularity is not possible for subcritical phases
|\Theta|<(n-2)\frac{\pi}{2}
, without an additional convexity condition, as in Bao-Chen
[
BC03
]
, Chen-Warren-Yuan
[
CWY09
]
, and Chen-Shankar-Yuan
[
CSY23
]
, and that the Dirichlet problem is not classically solvable for arbitrary smooth boundary data.
In
[
MS23
]
, viscosity solutions to (
1.2
) that are Lipschitz but not
C^{1}
were constructed.

If the Lagrangian phase varies
\Theta=\Theta(x)
, then there is less clarity. Hessian estimates for convex smooth solutions with
C^{1,1}
phase
\Theta=\Theta(x)
were obtained by Warren in
[
War08
, Theorem 8]
. For
C^{1,1}
supercritical phase, interior Hessian and gradient estimates were established by Bhattacharya in
[
Bha21
]
. For
C^{1,1}
critical and supercritical phase, interior Hessian and gradient estimates were established by Bhattacharya
[
Bha21
,
Bha22
]
and Bhattacharya-Mooney-Shankar
[
BMS22
]
(for
C^{2}
phase) respectively. See also Lu
[
Lu22
]
. Recently in
[
Zho23
]
, Zhou established interior Hessian estimates for supercritical
C^{0,1}
phase. For convex viscosity solutions, interior regularity was established for
C^{2}
phase by Bhattacharya-Shankar in
[
BS23
,
BS20
]
. If
\Theta
is merely in
C^{\alpha}
and supercritical, counterexamples to Hessian estimates exist as shown in
[
Bha24
]
.

While our knowledge is still limited when it comes to the variable Lagrangian phase
\Theta(x)
, it narrows even further when the Lagrangian phase is dependent on both the potential and the gradient of the potential of the Lagrangian submanifold, i.e.,
\Theta(x,u,Du)
. Applying the integral method of
[
Bha21
]
to the current problem poses numerous challenges. For instance, establishing the Jacobi-type inequality becomes significantly more intricate due to the presence of the gradient term
Du
in
\Theta
. Consequently, it is by no means a straightforward process to combine the derivatives of
\Theta
into a single constant term as in
[
Bha21
]
.
Next, due to the presence of the gradient term in the phase, the Michael-Simon Sobolev inequality cannot be used to estimate the integral of the volume form by a weighted volume of the non-minimal Lagrangian graph. We circumvent this issue by using the Lewy-Yuan rotation
[
Yua02
, p.122]
, which is reminiscent of the technique used in
[
CWY09
]
. This rotation results in a uniformly elliptic Jacobi inequality on the rotated Lagrangian graph, which allows the use of a local maximum principle
[
GT01
, Theorem 9.20]
. However, the constants appearing in our Jacobi inequality are dependent on the oscillation of the potential. Therefore we need an explicit dependence of the constants arising in the local maximum principle on
\mathrm{osc}(u)
. To address this, we state and prove a version of the local maximum principle
[
GT01
, Theorem 9.20]
applied to our specific equation (see Appendix).
Next, rotating back to the original coordinates and keeping track of the constants appearing at each step, we bound the slope of the gradient graph
(x,Du(x))
at the origin by an exponential function of the oscillation of
u
. Note that since the Michael-Simon mean value
[
MS73
, Theorem 3.4]
and Sobolev inequalities
[
MS73
, Theorem 2.1]
are not employed, there is no explicit dependence on the mean curvature bound in our final estimate.

The critical and supercritical phase case
|\Theta|\geq(n-2)\frac{\pi}{2}
introduces new challenges requiring new techniques, which we present along with the supercritical phase case
|\Theta|\geq(n-2)\frac{\pi}{2}+\delta
in forthcoming work
[
BW24
]
.

2.
Preliminaries

For the convenience of the readers, we recall some preliminary results. We first introduce some notations that will be used in this paper.
The induced Riemannian metric on the Lagrangian submanifold
X=(x,Du(x))\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
is given by

g=I_{n}+(D^{2}u)^{2}.

We denote

\displaystyle\partial_{i}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\text{ , }\partial_{ij}=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}\text{ , }u_{i}=\partial_{i}u\text{ , }u_{ij}=\partial_{ij}u.

Note that for the functions defined below, the subscripts on the left do not represent partial derivatives

\displaystyle h_{ijk}=\sqrt{g^{ii}}\sqrt{g^{jj}}\sqrt{g^{kk}}u_{ijk},\quad g^{ii}=\frac{1}{1+\lambda_{i}^{2}}.

Here
(g^{ij})
is the inverse of the matrix
g
and
h_{ijk}
denotes the second fundamental form when the Hessian of
u
is diagonalized.
The volume form, gradient, and inner product with respect to the metric
g
are given by

\displaystyle dv_{g}=\sqrt{\det g}dx

\displaystyle=Vdx\text{ , }\qquad\nabla_{g}v=g^{ij}v_{i}X_{j},

\displaystyle\langle\nabla_{g}v,\nabla_{g}w\rangle_{g}

\displaystyle=g^{ij}v_{i}w_{j}\text{ , }\quad|\nabla_{g}v|^{2}=\langle\nabla_{g}v,\nabla_{g}v\rangle_{g}.

Next, we derive the Laplace-Beltrami operator on the non-minimal submanifold
(x,Du(x))
. Taking variations of the energy functional
\int|\nabla_{g}v|^{2}dv_{g}
with respect to
v
, one gets the Laplace-Beltrami operator of the metric
g
:

(2.1)

\displaystyle\Delta_{g}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\partial_{i}(\sqrt{g}g^{ij}\partial_{j})

\displaystyle=g^{ij}\partial_{ij}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\partial_{i}(\sqrt{g}g^{ij})\partial_{j}

\displaystyle=g^{ij}\partial_{ij}-g^{jp}u_{pq}(\partial_{q}\Theta)\partial_{j}.

The last equation follows from the following computation:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\partial_{i}(\sqrt{g}g^{ij})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\partial_{i}(\sqrt{g})g^{ij}+\partial_{i}g^{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{i}\ln g)g^{ij}+\partial_{k}g^{kj}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}g^{kl}\partial_{i}g_{kl}g^{ij}-g^{kl}\partial_{k}g_{lb}g^{bj}

(2.2)

\displaystyle=-g^{jp}g^{ab}u_{abq}u_{pq}=-g^{jp}u_{pq}\partial_{q}\Theta

where the last equation follows from (
2.3
) and (
2.4
) below.
The first
derivative of the metric
g
is given by

(2.3)

\displaystyle\partial_{i}g_{ab}=\partial_{i}(\delta_{ab}+u_{ak}u_{kb})=u_{aik}u_{kb}+u_{bik}u_{ka}\overset{\text{at }x_{0}}{=}u_{abi}(\lambda_{a}+\lambda_{b}),

assuming the Hessian of
u
is diagonalized at
x_{0}
.
On taking the gradient of both sides of the Lagrangian mean curvature type equation (
1.8
), we get

(2.4)

\sum_{a,b=1}^{n}g^{ab}u_{jab}=\partial_{j}\Theta(x,u(x),Du(x)).

For the general phase
\Theta(x,u(x),Du(x))
, assuming the Hessian
D^{2}u
is diagonalized at
x_{0}
, we get

(2.5)

\displaystyle\partial_{i}\Theta(x,u(x),Du(x))

\displaystyle=\Theta_{x_{i}}+\Theta_{u}u_{i}+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Theta_{u_{k}}u_{ki}

(2.6)

\displaystyle\overset{x_{0}}{=}\Theta_{x_{i}}+\Theta_{u}u_{i}+\Theta_{u_{i}}\lambda_{i}.

So from (
2.6
) and (
1.3
), we get, at the point
x_{0}\in B_{R}
,

\displaystyle|\vec{H}|_{g}^{2}=g^{ii}(\partial_{i}\Theta)^{2}

\displaystyle=g^{ii}\bigg(\Theta_{x_{i}}^{2}+\Theta_{u}^{2}u_{i}^{2}+\Theta_{u_{i}}^{2}\lambda_{i}^{2}+2\Theta_{x_{i}}\Theta_{u}u_{i}+2\Theta_{x_{i}}\Theta_{u_{i}}\lambda_{i}+2\Theta_{u}\Theta_{u_{i}}u_{i}\lambda_{i}\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq 3g^{ii}\bigg(\Theta_{x_{i}}^{2}+\Theta_{u}^{2}u_{i}^{2}+\Theta_{u_{i}}^{2}\lambda_{i}^{2}\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq C(\nu_{1},n,\mathrm{osc}_{B_{R+1}}(u)).

Taking the
j
-th partial derivative of (
2.5
), we get

\displaystyle\partial_{ij}\Theta(x,u(x),Du(x))

\displaystyle=\Theta_{x_{i}x_{j}}+\Theta_{x_{i}u}u_{j}+\sum_{r=1}^{n}\Theta_{x_{i}u_{r}}u_{rj}

\displaystyle\qquad+\left(\Theta_{ux_{j}}+\Theta_{uu}u_{j}+\sum_{s=1}^{n}\Theta_{uu_{s}}u_{sj}\right)u_{i}+\Theta_{u}u_{ij}

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\Theta_{u_{k}x_{j}}+\Theta_{u_{k}u}u_{j}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\Theta_{u_{k}u_{\ell}}u_{\ell j}\right)u_{ki}+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Theta_{u_{k}}u_{kij}

(2.7)

\displaystyle\overset{x_{0}}{=}\Theta_{x_{i}x_{j}}+\Theta_{x_{i}u}u_{j}+\Theta_{x_{i}u_{j}}\lambda_{j}

\displaystyle\qquad+\left(\Theta_{ux_{j}}+\Theta_{uu}u_{j}+\Theta_{uu_{j}}\lambda_{j}\right)u_{i}+\Theta_{u}\lambda_{i}\delta_{ij}

\displaystyle\qquad+\left(\Theta_{u_{i}x_{j}}+\Theta_{u_{i}u}u_{j}+\Theta_{u_{i}u_{j}}\lambda_{j}\right)\lambda_{i}+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Theta_{u_{k}}u_{kij}.

Observe that when
\Theta
is constant, one can choose harmonic co-ordinates
\Delta_{g}x=0
, which
reduces the Laplace-Beltrami operator on the minimal submanifold
\{(x,Du(x))|x\in B_{R}(0)\}
to the linearized operator of (
1.2
) at
u
.

3.
The slope as a subsolution to a fully nonlinear PDE

In this section, we prove a Jacobi-type inequality for the slope of the gradient graph
(x,Du(x))
, i.e., we show that a certain function of the slope of the gradient graph
(x,Du(x))
is almost strongly subharmonic.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
u
be a
C^{4}
convex solution of (
1.8
) in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Suppose that the Hessian
D^{2}u
is diagonalized at point
x_{0}
. Then we have the following at
x_{0}

\frac{1}{n}|\nabla_{g}\log\sqrt{\det g}|^{2}_{g}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}^{2}h_{iii}^{2}+\sum_{i\neq j}\lambda_{j}^{2}h_{jji}^{2}

and

\displaystyle\Delta_{g}\log\sqrt{\det g}

\displaystyle\overset{x_{0}}{=}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1+\lambda_{i}^{2})h_{iii}^{2}+\sum_{j\neq i}(3+\lambda_{j}^{2}+2\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j})h_{jji}^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+2\sum_{i<j<k}(3+\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}+\lambda_{j}\lambda_{k}+\lambda_{k}\lambda_{i})h_{ijk}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i=1}^{n}g^{ii}\lambda_{i}\partial_{ii}\Theta-\sum_{i=1}^{n}g^{ii}\lambda_{i}(\partial_{i}\Theta)\partial_{i}\log\sqrt{\det g}.

Proof.

We compute some derivatives of the metric
g
. We have

\displaystyle\partial_{j}g_{ab}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}(u_{akj}u_{kb}+u_{ak}u_{kbj})

(3.1)

\displaystyle\overset{x_{0}}{=}u_{abj}(\lambda_{a}+\lambda_{b})

and

\displaystyle\partial_{i}g^{ab}

\displaystyle=-g^{ak}\partial_{i}g_{kl}g^{lb}

\displaystyle\overset{x_{0}}{=}-g^{aa}\partial_{i}g_{ab}g^{bb}

(3.2)

\displaystyle\overset{x_{0}}{=}-g^{aa}g^{bb}u_{abi}(\lambda_{a}+\lambda_{b}).

Hence

\displaystyle\partial_{ij}g_{ab}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}(u_{akji}u_{kb}+u_{akj}u_{kbi}+u_{aki}u_{kbj}+u_{ak}u_{kbij})

\displaystyle\overset{x_{0}}{=}u_{abji}(\lambda_{a}+\lambda_{b})+\sum_{k=1}^{n}(u_{akj}u_{kbi}+u_{aki}u_{kbj}).

In order to substitute the 4th order derivatives, we take the partial derivative of (
2.4
) and get

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}g^{ij}u_{ijk\ell}

\displaystyle=\partial_{k\ell}\Theta-\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\partial_{\ell}g^{ij}u_{ijk}

\displaystyle\overset{x_{0}}{=}\partial_{k\ell}\Theta+\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}g^{ii}g^{jj}u_{ij\ell}u_{ijk}(\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j}).

Thus, we have

(3.3)

\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}g^{ij}\partial_{ij}g_{ab}\overset{x_{0}}{=}(\lambda_{a}+\lambda_{b})\partial_{ab}\Theta+\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}g^{ii}g^{jj}u_{ija}u_{ijb}(\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j})(\lambda_{a}+\lambda_{b})+\sum_{i,k=1}^{n}2g^{ii}u_{aki}u_{bki}.

Next, we compute the norm of the gradient:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}|\nabla_{g}\log\sqrt{\det g}|^{2}_{g}

\displaystyle\overset{x_{0}}{=}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{n}g^{ii}\left(\partial_{i}\log\sqrt{\det g}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\overset{x_{0}}{=}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{n}g^{ii}\left(\sum_{a,b=1}^{n}\frac{1}{2}g^{ab}\partial_{i}g_{ab}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\overset{x_{0}}{=}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{n}g^{ii}\left(\sum_{a,b=1}^{n}\frac{1}{2}g^{ab}u_{abi}(\lambda_{a}+\lambda_{b})\right)^{2}\quad\text{from \eqref{djg_ab@p}}

(3.4)

\displaystyle\overset{x_{0}}{=}\sum_{i,a=1}^{n}h_{aai}^{2}\lambda_{a}^{2}

\displaystyle\overset{x_{0}}{=}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}^{2}h_{iii}^{2}+\sum_{i\neq j}\lambda_{j}^{2}h_{jji}^{2}.

). From the above calculations, we observe that

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}g^{ij}\partial_{ij}\log\sqrt{\det g}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}g^{ij}\partial_{j}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det g}}\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\det g}}\partial_{i}\det g\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j,a,b=1}^{n}g^{ij}\partial_{j}\left(\frac{1}{2\det g}\det g\;g^{ab}\partial_{i}g_{ab}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j,a,b=1}^{n}g^{ij}\frac{1}{2}\partial_{j}\left(g^{ab}\partial_{i}g_{ab}\right)

(3.5)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j,a,b=1}^{n}g^{ij}\frac{1}{2}\left((\partial_{j}g^{ab})\partial_{i}g_{ab}+g^{ab}\partial_{ij}g_{ab}\right).

Using (
3.1
) and (
3.2
), we see that the first term of (
3.5
) becomes

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j,a,b=1}^{n}\frac{1}{2}g^{ij}(\partial_{j}g^{ab})\partial_{i}g_{ab}\overset{x_{0}}{=}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,a,b=1}^{n}g^{ii}g^{aa}g^{bb}u_{abi}^{2}(\lambda_{a}+\lambda_{b})^{2}.

Using (
3.3

\displaystyle\overset{x_{0}}{=}\sum_{a=1}^{n}g^{aa}\lambda_{a}\partial_{aa}\Theta+\sum_{i,j,a=1}^{n}g^{aa}g^{ii}g^{jj}u_{ija}^{2}(\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j})\lambda_{a}+\sum_{i,k,a=1}^{n}g^{aa}g^{ii}u_{aki}^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,a,b=1}^{n}g^{ii}g^{aa}g^{bb}u_{abi}^{2}(\lambda_{a}+\lambda_{b})^{2}

\displaystyle\overset{x_{0}}{=}\sum_{a=1}^{n}g^{aa}\lambda_{a}\partial_{aa}\Theta+\sum_{a,b,c=1}^{n}g^{aa}g^{bb}g^{cc}u_{abc}^{2}(\lambda_{b}+\lambda_{c})\lambda_{a}

\displaystyle\overset{x_{0}}{=}\sum_{a=1}^{n}g^{aa}\lambda_{a}\partial_{aa}\Theta+\sum_{a,b,c=1}^{n}h_{abc}^{2}(1+\lambda_{b}\lambda_{c})

\displaystyle\overset{x_{0}}{=}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g^{ii}\lambda_{i}\partial_{ii}\Theta+\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1+\lambda_{i}^{2})h_{iii}^{2}+\sum_{j\neq i}(3+\lambda_{j}^{2}+2\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j})h_{jji}^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+2\sum_{i<j<k}(3+\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}+\lambda_{j}\lambda_{k}+\lambda_{k}\lambda_{i})h_{ijk}^{2}.

Altogether, we get

\displaystyle\overset{x_{0}}{=}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1+\lambda_{i}^{2})h_{iii}^{2}+\sum_{j\neq i}(3+\lambda_{j}^{2}+2\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j})h_{jji}^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+2\sum_{i<j<k}(3+\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}+\lambda_{j}\lambda_{k}+\lambda_{k}\lambda_{i})h_{ijk}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i=1}^{n}g^{ii}\lambda_{i}\partial_{ii}\Theta-\sum_{i=1}^{n}g^{ii}\lambda_{i}(\partial_{i}\Theta)\partial_{i}\log\sqrt{\det g}.

∎
