FaceDet3D: Facial Expressions with 3D Geometric Detail Prediction
ShahRukh Athar 1, Albert Pumarola 2, Francesc Moreno-Noguer 2, Dimitris Samaras 1 1 Stony Brook University 2 Institut de Robòtica i Informàtica Industrial, CSIC-UPC

Abstract
Facial Expressions induce a variety of high-level details on the 3D face geometry. For example, a smile causes the wrinkling of cheeks or the formation of dimples, while being angry often causes wrinkling of the forehead. Morphable Models (3DMMs) of the human face fail to capture such fine details in their PCA-based representations and consequently cannot generate such details when used to edit expressions. In this work, we introduce FaceDet3D, a first-of-its-kind method that generates - from a single image - geometric facial details that are consistent with any desired target expression. The facial details are represented as a vertex displacement map and used then by a Neural Renderer to photo-realistically render novel images of any single image in any desired expression and view. Introduction

Modelling the geometry of the human face continues to attract great interest
in the computer vision and computer graphics communities. Strong PCA-based priors make 3D morphable models (3DMMs)
[
3

Additionally, the lack of diversity in the texture space of most available 3DMMs make it very hard to generate realistic renderings that capture the large variations of color and texture we observe in human faces. ,

]
and videos
[

]
. However, despite using more expressive shape and expression spaces, these models still fail to capture fine details in geometry.

Recent methods that accurately estimate facial geometric details from single images
[
6
,
34
]
, while being unable to predict novel details under expression change, have nonetheless enabled the large scale annotation of unpaired image data with facial geometric details. Thus, it is now possible to train facial detail prediction methods using unsupervised adversarial losses
[
7
,
24
]
. Similarly, Neural Rendering
[
31
]
has made it possible to render 3D geometries with photo-realistic detail via the use of high-dimensional Neural Texture Maps (NTMs). However, NTMs are able to store fine details of the output image, causing the rendered details to be completely independent of the geometric details. They do not change even if the geometric details do, making neural rendering unsuitable for rendering facial geometric details.
In this paper we introduce FaceDet3D, which is, to the best of our knowledge, the first generative model that predicts facial geometric details for any target expression and renders them realistically to the image space.
The model is made up of two components: 1) A detail prediction network that infers plausible geometric facial details of a person as their expression changes, given a
single
image of that person.
2) A rendering network that overcomes the aforementioned shortcoming of neural rendering and explicitly conditions the rendered facial details on the
details
of the 3D face geometry. This conditioning is achieved through the use of the novel
Augmented Wrinkle Loss
and the
Detailed Shading Loss
during training.

Our method is trained using only a large scale in-the-wild image dataset and a much smaller video dataset captured in controlled conditions, without any 3D data as supervision for the target expression geometry. An exhaustive evaluation shows that once trained, our method is able to generate plausible facial details for any desired anatomically consistent facial expression and render it realistically in any desired view direction.

Related Work

We next describe the most related methods in facial geometry estimation, geometric facial detail estimation and facial expression editing and reanimation.

Facial Geometry Estimation

Traditional facial geometry estimation methods fit statistical 3D face models to a given image, going back to the original 3D Face Morphable Model
[
3
]
. However, the PCA-based prior used by 3DMMs is not flexible enough to represent fine facial geometric details such as wrinkles and skin bumps. Recent methods
[
28
,
29
,
33
,
32
,
39
,
5
,
9
,
16
,
20
,
11
]
leverage the power of deep-learning and large-scale image and video datasets to regress parameters that generate realistic 3D reconstructions or learn complex representations for face shape, expression and texture. These methods generate more realistic results than traditional 3DMM fitting, but still cannot capture fine facial details. In our work we use the Basel Face Model
[
12
]
as the underlying geometry on top of which we predict geometric details.

Geometric Facial Details Estimation.

Over the past few years there has been significant improvement in the realism of 3D face geometries estimated from single images. In
[
26
]
, the regressed correspondence and depth maps are registered onto a template mesh, which is further refined to generate the detailed facial geometry. In
[
34
]
facial details are modelled with bump maps on top of a 3DMM base. Similarly, in Facial Details Synthesis (FDS)
[
6
]
, details are represented as vertex displacements of an underlying 3DMM, trained using a combination of 3D data and in-the-wild images. These methods, however, can only estimate the facial details of the expression manifested in an image, but cannot predict novel facial details for a different expression, which is the motivation of our method.

Facial Expression Editing.

The success of image-to-image translation networks
[
15
]
, adversarial training
[
13
]
and cycle-consistency losses
[
38
]
have led to the development of expression editing methods that use large-scale in-the-wild training datasets. In
[
27
]
, an unsupervised face model disentangles the input face into albedo, normals and shading. Expressions are then edited by traversals in the disentangled latent space. In
[
7
]
, expressions are edited via adversarial losses coupled with cycle consistency. In
[
24
]
, a network edits input images to target expressions, represented as Action Units
[
10
]
.
[
2
]
extends this work by explicitly modeling skin motion followed by texture hallucination. In
[
8
]
, template images are used for editing. While these methods give photo-realistic results, they are restricted to the 2D Image space and cannot be used to manipulate 3D viewpoint.

Facial Reanimation Methods.

Facial renanimation methods use driving parameters from a source video to reanimate a source image or video.
[
19
]
, performs the reanmatiion using the parameters of a 3DMM extracted from the source video. In
[
31
]
, Neural Rendering is used to reanimate videos using expression parameters extracted from a source video. However, this renderer is needs to be retrained for each target video. In
[
30
]
, audio parameters drive the target video. In
[
36
]
, facial landmarks from a source video are used to animate single images. While all these methods generate realistic re-animations, they do not model any facial geometric detail. More closely related to our work is
[
23
]
, which regresses a set of FACS
[
10
]
based textures from single images and are able to generate realistic renderings in any desired expression. Nevertheless, they do not predict any facial geometric detail but instead generate the details in the texture space.

FaceDet3D

3.1
Problem Formulation

A face image
I_{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times 3}
has expression
\mathbf{x}
represented by Action Units
[
10
]
. Its shape and expression parameters in the Basel Face Model (BFM)
[
12
]
space are
\{\alpha_{s},\alpha_{e}\}
. A vertex displacement UV map
\mathcal{D}(I_{\mathbf{x}})\in\mathbb{R}^{H_{\mathcal{D}}\times W_{\mathcal{D}}\times 3}
encodes the facial details of the person shown in
I_{\mathbf{x}}
with the expression
\mathbf{x}
. We want to: (1) Generate facial details, represented by a vertex displacement map,
\mathcal{D}(I_{\mathbf{y}})
as the expression of that person changes to
\mathbf{y}
; (2) Render an image of the geometry
with
these details.

We use FDS
[
6
]
to extract the texture map, initial detail map and geometry of the input image
I_{\mathbf{x}}
:

\mathcal{T}(I_{\mathbf{x}}),\mathcal{D}(I_{\mathbf{x}}),\alpha_{s},\alpha_{e}\leftarrow FDS(I_{\mathbf{x}})

(1)

where,
\mathcal{T}(I_{\mathbf{x}})\in\mathbb{R}^{H_{\mathcal{T}}\times W_{\mathcal{T}}\times 3}
is the texture map.

Next, we use the
detail prediction network
,
DetP(\cdot)
, to predict the plausible facial detail map of the person in
I_{\mathbf{x}}
for expression
\mathbf{y}
as:

\mathcal{\widetilde{D}}(I_{\mathbf{y}})=DetP(\mathcal{D}(I_{\mathbf{x}}),\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\hat{\alpha_{e}},Age(I_{\mathbf{x}}),FaceID(I_{\mathbf{x}}))

(2)

where,
\mathbf{y}
,
\hat{\alpha_{e}}
are the target AUs and expression parameters,
Age(I_{\mathbf{x}})
are features extracted from an age prediction network and
FaceID(I_{\mathbf{x}})
is the facial embedding of
I_{\bf{x}}
extracted using
[
25
]
.
Note, we
do not have access
to the ground truth image
I_{\mathbf{y}}
, thus we
predict a plausible detail map
of
I_{\mathbf{y}}
(i.e
\mathcal{\widetilde{D}}(I_{y})
) using
DetP
. Once we have
\mathcal{\widetilde{D}}(I_{y})
, we use it to displace the vertices along their normal direction giving us the detailed geometry. Finally, we render this detailed face geometry using a
rendering network

R(\cdot)
:

\tilde{I}_{\bf{y}}=R(\mathcal{T}(I_{\mathbf{x}}),\mathcal{\widetilde{D}}({I}_{\mathbf{y}}),\alpha_{s},\hat{\alpha_{e}},\mathbf{y},c,l,\gamma)

(3)

where,
c
are the desired camera and view parameters,
\gamma
is the albedo PCA-space parameters of BFM
[
12
]
, and
l
are the lighting parameters.

3.2
Detail Prediction

Figure 2
:
Detail Prediction Network.

DetP
first extracts from its various inputs high-dimensional features and concatenates them. This concatenated feature map is then used to predict a coarse detail map
\mathcal{D}(I_{\mathbf{y}})^{C}
. The coarse detail map is then refined via a refinement module to predict a fine detail map
\mathcal{D}(I_{\mathbf{y}})^{F}
which is then added to the coarse detail map,
\mathcal{D}(I_{\mathbf{y}})^{C}
, to give
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}(I_{\mathbf{y}})
.

DetP
takes the input detail map,
\mathcal{D}(I_{\mathbf{x}})
, the target expression parameters
\hat{\alpha_{e}}
, the shape parameters
\alpha_{shape}
, the face embedding
FaceID(I_{\mathbf{x}})
, the action unit
\mathbf{x}
, and the age features
Age(I_{\mathbf{x}})
and extracts features from each of them. There features are concatenated in the channel dimension and passed though the
Decoder
which receives the target action unit
\mathbf{y}
via Adaptive Instance Normalization
[
14
]
layers. The
Decoder
gives as output a hallucination
\mathcal{D}(I_{\mathbf{y}})^{C\text{-}Hal}
and a mask
\mathcal{D}(I_{\mathbf{y}})^{C\text{-}Mask}
which are combined together to give
\mathcal{D}(I_{\mathbf{y}})^{C}
:

\begin{split}\mathcal{D}(I_{\mathbf{y}})^{C}&=\mathcal{D}(I_{\mathbf{y}})^{C\text{-}Mask}\odot\mathcal{D}(I_{\mathbf{y}})^{C\text{-}Hal}\\
&+(1\shortminus\mathcal{D}(I_{\mathbf{y}})^{C\text{-}Mask})\odot\mathcal{D}(I_{\mathbf{x}})\end{split}

(4)

\mathcal{D}(I_{\mathbf{y}})^{C}
then goes through a refinement module that generates
\mathcal{D}(I_{\mathbf{y}})^{F\text{-}Hal}
and a mask
\mathcal{D}(I_{\mathbf{y}})^{F\text{-}Mask}
which are combined to give
\mathcal{D}(I_{\mathbf{y}})^{F}
:

\begin{split}\mathcal{D}(I_{\mathbf{y}})^{F}&=\mathcal{D}(I_{\mathbf{y}})^{F\text{-}Mask}\odot\mathcal{D}(I_{\mathbf{y}})^{F\text{-}Hal}\\
&+(1\shortminus\mathcal{D}(I_{\mathbf{y}})^{F\text{-}Mask})\odot\mathcal{D}(I_{\mathbf{x}})\end{split}

(5)

Finally,
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}(I_{\mathbf{y}})=\mathcal{D}(I_{\mathbf{y}})^{C}+\mathcal{D}(I_{\mathbf{y}})^{F}
. With this two-stage approach
\mathcal{D}(I_{\mathbf{y}})^{C}
is able to capture coarse-scale detail changes while
\mathcal{D}(I_{\mathbf{y}})^{F}
captures the finer scale changes. The masking mechanism used in both stages ensures the preservation of the details that are not meant to be changed with the expression.

3.2.1
Training Losses

Due to the lack of a large scale in-the-wild dataset of paired data with expression change or 3D data, we cannot directly perform a regression using ground-truth geometric facial details,
\mathcal{D}(I_{\mathbf{y}})
of the image
I_{\mathbf{y}}
. Therefore, we instead use an adversarial training paradigm along with cycle-consistency losses similar to
[
2
,
24
]
to predict the plausible facial geometric details
\mathcal{\widetilde{D}}(I_{y})
and to ensure the prediction’s fidelity to the target expression and input features. In order to speed up convergence, we weakly supervise the adversarial training using randomly sampled frames from videos of the MUG
[
1
]
and the ADFES datasets
[
35
]
, ensuring that the sampling is sparse enough such that there is significant change in expression with frames sampled from each video.

Expression Adversarial Loss.

In order to ensure the predicted facial geometric details,
\mathcal{\widetilde{D}}(I_{\mathbf{y}})
, are consistent with the target expression
\{\mathbf{y},\hat{\alpha}_{e}\}
, we use an expression discriminator
\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Exp}}
. Given
\mathcal{D}(I_{\mathbf{x}})
of some image
I_{\mathbf{x}}
manifesting expression
\{\mathbf{x},\alpha_{e}\}
,
\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Exp}}
, outputs the following

\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Exp}}(\mathcal{D}(I_{x}))=\{r,\hat{\mathbf{x}},\hat{\alpha_{e}}\}

(6)

where
r
is a realism score and
\hat{\mathbf{x}}\text{ and }\hat{\alpha_{e}}
are the predicted AUs and expression parameters, respectively. For brevity, we will use
\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Exp}}(\mathcal{D}(I_{\mathbf{x}}))
and
\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Exp}}(\mathcal{D})
interchangeably. We use the Non-Saturating adversarial loss
[
13
]
along with the
R1
gradient penalty
[
22
]
to train
\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Exp}}
. Specifically, let
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{D}}
be the distribution of real facial geometric detail maps, the adversarial loss for
\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Exp}}
can be written as:

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}^{\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Exp}}}_{Adv}&=-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{\widetilde{D}}\sim DetP(.)}\left[\text{log }\left(1\shortminus\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Exp}}^{r}(\mathcal{\widetilde{D}})\right)\right]\\
&-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}\sim\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{D}}}\left[\text{log }\left(\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Exp}}^{r}(\mathcal{D})\right)\right]\\
&+\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}\sim\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{D}}}\left[||\nabla\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Exp}}^{r}(\mathcal{D})||_{2}^{2}\right]\end{split}

(7)

where
\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Exp}}^{r}
is the realism output head of
\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Exp}}
. In addition,
\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Exp}}
is trained to minimize the error of the predicted AU and expression parameters

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}^{\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Exp}}}_{Exp}&=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}\sim\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{D}}}\left[||[\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Exp}}^{\textrm{AU}}(\mathcal{D})\shortminus\mathbf{x}||_{2}^{2}\right]\\
&+\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}\sim\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{D}}}\left[||[\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Exp}}^{\alpha_{e}}(\mathcal{D})-\alpha_{e}||_{2}^{2}\right]\end{split}

(8)

where
\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Exp}}^{\textrm{AU}}\text{ and }\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Exp}}^{\alpha_{e}}
are the AU and expression parameter output head of
\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Exp}}
respectively. The Detail Prediction Network,
DetP
is trained to minimize the adversarial loss

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}^{DetP}_{ExpAdv}&=\underset{I_{\mathbf{x}},\{\mathbf{y},\hat{\alpha}_{e}\}}{\shortminus\mathbb{E}}\text{log }\left(\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Exp}}^{r}(DetP(\cdot))\right)\\
\end{split}

(9)

and the expression losses

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}^{DetP}_{AU}&=\underset{I_{\mathbf{x}},\{\mathbf{y},\hat{\alpha}_{e}\}}{\mathbb{E}}||\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Exp}}^{\textrm{AU}}(DetP(\cdot))\shortminus\mathbf{y}||_{2}^{2}\\
\end{split}

(10)

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}^{DetP}_{\alpha_{e}}&=\underset{I_{\mathbf{x}},\{\mathbf{y},\hat{\alpha}_{e}\}}{\mathbb{E}}||\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Exp}}^{\alpha_{e}}(DetP(\cdot))\shortminus\hat{\alpha}_{e}||_{2}^{2}\\
\end{split}

(11)

where,
DetP(\cdot)
is to be read as in Eq. (
2
) and
\mathbf{y}
and
\hat{\alpha}_{e}
are the target AU and expression parameters respectively.

FaceID Loss.

The FaceID loss ensures that facial details characteristic of the subject’s identity, that are invariant to expression change, are preserved in the predicted details. A face embedding detection network,
\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Face}}
, is trained to predict the
FaceNet

[
25
]
features of an image
I_{\mathbf{x}}
from its detail map
\mathcal{D}(I_{\mathbf{x}})
as follows

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}^{\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Face}}}_{ID}&=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}\sim\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{D}}}||\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Face}}^{\textrm{ID}}(\mathcal{D}(I_{\mathbf{x}}))\shortminus FaceNet(I_{\mathbf{x}})||_{2}^{2}\end{split}

(12)

where
\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Face}}^{\textrm{ID}}
is the face embedding output head of
\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Face}}
. Additionally,
\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Face}}
is also trained with an adversarial loss with the same loss as shown in Eq. (
7
). The detail prediction network,
DetP
is trained to minimize both the FaceID loss and the adversarial loss

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}^{DetP}_{ID}&=\underset{I_{\mathbf{x}},\{\mathbf{y},\hat{\alpha}_{e}\}}{\mathbb{E}}||\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Face}}^{\textrm{ID}}(DetP(\cdot))\shortminus FaceNet(I_{\mathbf{x}})||_{2}^{2}\\
\end{split}

(13)

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}^{DetP}_{IDAdv}&=\underset{I_{\mathbf{x}},\{\mathbf{y},\hat{\alpha}_{e}\}}{\shortminus\mathbb{E}}\text{log }\left(\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Face}}^{\textrm{r}}(DetP(.)\right)\\
\end{split}

(14)

where
\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Face}}^{\textrm{r}}
is the realism output head of
\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Face}}
.

Age Loss.

The Age Loss ensures that the facial details predicted are consistent with the subject’s age by ensuring the age feature embedding of the predicted detail map matches that of the input details. An age feature prediction network,
\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Age}}
is trained to predict the features of an image
I_{\mathbf{x}}
extracted using a pre-trained age prediction network,
AgeNet
, from its detail map
\mathcal{D}(I_{\mathbf{x}})
as follows

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}^{\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Age}}}_{Age}&=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}\sim\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{D}}}||\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Age}}^{\textrm{Age}}(\mathcal{D}(I_{\mathbf{x}}))\shortminus AgeNet(I_{\mathbf{x}})||_{2}^{2}\end{split}

(15)

where
\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Age}}^{\textrm{Age}}
is the age feature output head of
\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Age}}
. Additionally,
\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Age}}
is also trained with an adversarial loss of the same type as shown in Eq. (
7
). The detail prediction network,
DetP
is trained to minimize both the Age loss and the adversarial loss

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}^{DetP}_{Age}&=\underset{I_{\mathbf{x}},\{\mathbf{y},\hat{\alpha}_{e}\}}{\mathbb{E}}||\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Age}}^{\textrm{Age}}(DetP(.))\shortminus AgeNet(I_{\mathbf{x}})||_{2}^{2}\\
\end{split}

(16)

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}^{DetP}_{AgeAdv}&=\underset{I_{\mathbf{x}},\{\mathbf{y},\hat{\alpha}_{e}\}}{-\mathbb{E}}\text{log }\left(\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Age}}^{\textrm{r}}(DetP(.)\right)\\
\end{split}

(17)

where
\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Age}}^{\textrm{r}}
is the realism output head of
\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Age}}
.

where,

Superresolution Losses

The detail maps generated by FDS
[
6
]
are of resolution
4096\times 4096
and thus cannot be used directly for training due to GPU-memory constraints. To get around this, we train
DetP
on detail maps downsampled to
256\times 256
. Simultaneously, we finetune a superresolution network,
RCAN

[
37
]
, to super-resolve downsampled
256\times 256
patches of
\mathcal{D}(I_{\bf{x}})
by a factor of 4

\mathcal{L}_{SR}^{RCAN}=L1(RCAN(\mathcal{D}(I_{\bf{x}})_{256}^{P},\mathcal{D}(I_{\bf{x}})_{1024}^{P})

(19)

where
\mathcal{D}(I_{\bf{x}})_{1024}^{P}
is a randomly sampled patch of resolution
1024\times 1024
from the full-resolution detail map
\mathcal{D}(I_{\bf{x}})
and
\mathcal{D}(I_{\bf{x}})_{256}^{P}
is its downsampled version. During inference, we use
RCAN
twice on the predicted detail map
\mathcal{\widetilde{D}}(I_{\bf{y}})
to upsample it to
4096\times 4096

\mathcal{\widetilde{D}}(I_{\bf{y}})^{HR}=RCAN(RCAN(\mathcal{\widetilde{D}}(I_{\bf{y}})))

(20)

In the interest of brevity, we will use
\mathcal{\widetilde{D}}(I_{\bf{y}})
in lieu of
\mathcal{\widetilde{D}}(I_{\bf{y}})^{HR}
in the remainder of this text.

3.3
Rendering Network

Figure 3
:
The Rendering Network.
The Rendering Network,
R
first predicts a Neural Texture Map
NTM
from the given input texture map
\mathcal{T}
using
NTex_{\phi}
. The
NTM
is then rasterized using both the proxy (geometry w/o details) and the detailed geometry and input into an image rendering network
Tex2Im
.
Tex2Im
generates a rendering of the details
\hat{I_{\mathbf{y}}}^{\mathcal{D}}
and a low-resolution image
\hat{I_{\mathbf{y}}}^{LR}
that contains only detail-invariant image textures. They are added together to generate the final rendered image
\hat{I_{\mathbf{y}}}
.

The rendering network,
R
, consists of two subnetworks: (1) The Neural Texture prediction network
NTex_{\phi}
and (2) The image rendering network
Tex2Im
.

Neural Texture Prediction.

NTex_{\phi}
predicts the Neural Textures given the texture map
\mathcal{T}(I_{\bf{x}})
of image
I_{\bf{x}}
:

NTM=NTex_{\phi}(\mathcal{T}(I_{\bf{x}}));\hskip 8.5359ptNTM\in\mathbb{R}^{F\times H\times W}

(21)

where
NTM
is the predicted neural texture map with
F
channels. Unlike in
[
31
]
, where the
NTM
is identity specific,
NTex_{\phi}
can be used on any
\mathcal{T}(I_{\bf{x}})
regardless of identity to generate its corresponding neural texture map. Through training,
NTex_{\phi}
learns to extract the appropriate high-dimensional texture features from
\mathcal{T}(I_{\bf{x}})
such that
NTM
can be used to generate a realistic render of the person in
I_{\bf{x}}
in any desired expression and view.

Image rendering.

The image rendering network,
Tex2Im
, consists of two branches, the low-res image renderer
g_{\theta}
and the detail renderer
g_{\omega}
. The low-res image renderer generates identity-specific image textures that are invariant to the predicted geometric details, such as the skin-tone or eye color. The detail renderer
g_{\omega}
renders the facial geometric details obtained from the detail prediction network,
DetP
, and adds them to the low-res image generated by
g_{\theta}
. The separation of the image rendering network into two branches allows each branch to focus on its respective task and leads to high-quality renderings.

The low-res image renderer,
g_{\theta}
inputs the
NTM
sampled using the UV map rasterized by the proxy geometry,
G_{P}=\{0\times\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}(I_{\mathbf{y}}),\alpha_{s},\hat{\alpha_{e}}\}
, i.e the geometry
without
any details, along with the shaded albedo also rasterized by
G_{P}
:

\hat{I_{\bf{y}}^{LR}}=g_{\theta}(\small{\texttt{Sample}}(NTM,\small{UV\shortminus Raster}(G_{P},c)),y,\gamma,l)

(22)

where
\gamma
are the coefficients of the albedo PCA-space of the BFM
[
12
]
and
l
are the lighting parameters. Since
g_{\theta}
only uses inputs dependent on
G_{P}
it generates the image textures that are invariant to details predicted by
DetP
.

The detail renderer,
g_{\omega}
takes as input the
NTM
sampled using the UV map rasterized by the
detailed
geometry,
G_{\mathcal{D}}=\{\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}(I_{\mathbf{y}}),\alpha_{s},\hat{\alpha_{e}}\}
along with the shaded albedo also rasterized by
G_{\mathcal{D}}
:

\hat{I_{\bf{y}}^{\mathcal{D}}}=g_{\theta}(\small{\texttt{Sample}}(NTM,\small{UV\shortminus Raster}(G_{\mathcal{D}},c)),y,\gamma,l)

(23)

where
\gamma
and
l
are the albedo PCA-space and lighting parameters. The final output image is calculated as:

\hat{I_{\mathbf{y}}}=\hat{I_{\bf{y}}^{LR}}+\hat{I_{\bf{y}}^{\mathcal{D}}}

(24)

Since all of the detail invariant textures are already generated by
g_{\theta}
in
\hat{I_{\bf{y}}^{LR}}
, the detail renderer,
g_{\omega}
can solely focus on realistically rendering the details predicted by
DetP
.

3.3.1
Training Losses

The renderings generated by
R
must: (1) faithfully render the geometric details onto the
RGB
space and (2) be realistic. Neural Rendering
[
31
]
is designed to address (2) as the high-dimensional neural texture map is able store the fine details of the output texture. Consequently, the details on the rendered image become entirely conditional on the input texture map,
\mathcal{T}(I_{\mathbf{x}})
, and ignore the detailed geometry
G_{\mathcal{D}}
. This significantly hurts (1) causing the details on the rendered image to remain unchanged even if the facial geometric details change due to changes in facial expression. In order to ensure the output renderings satisfy (2) we use, along with the branched architecture discussed in Sect
3.3
, an
Augmented Wrinkle Loss
and the
Detailed Shading Loss
to ensure the geometric details are faithfully rendered onto the output image.

Photometric Loss.

The Photometric Loss ensures the rendered images are realistic by re-rendering a given image
I_{\bf{x}}
, producing
\hat{I}_{\bf{x}}
and comparing it to the ground truth. More specifically, given the texture map
\mathcal{T}(I_{\mathbf{x}})
, detailed geometry
G_{\mathcal{D}}=\{\mathcal{D}(I_{\mathbf{x}}),\alpha_{s},\alpha_{e}\}
and action unit
\bf{x}
,
I_{\bf{x}}
is re-rendered using
R
as follows

\begin{split}\hat{I}_{\mathbf{x}}=R(\mathcal{T}(I_{\mathbf{x}}),G_{\mathcal{D}},\mathbf{x},c,l,\gamma)\end{split}

(25)

where
c
and
l
are the camera and lighting parameters of
I_{\bf{x}}
. The re-rendered image
\hat{I}_{\mathbf{x}}
is then compared to
I_{\bf{x}}

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{Photo}&=MSE(\hat{I}_{\mathbf{x}},I_{\bf{x}})+L1(\hat{I}_{\mathbf{x}},I_{\bf{x}})\\
&+LapLoss(\hat{I}_{\mathbf{x}},I_{\bf{x}})+PcptL(\hat{I_{\bf{x}}},I_{\bf{x}})\end{split}

(26)

where
LapLoss
is the Laplacian Loss
[
21
,
4
]
and
PcptL
is the perceptual loss
[
17
]
. In order to ensure the low-res rendering captures the image textures that are invariant to facial details as much as possible, the photometric loss is also applied to the low-res output of
g_{\theta}
i.e
\hat{I_{\bf{y}}^{LR}}

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{Photo}^{LR}&=MSE(\hat{I_{\bf{x}}^{LR}},I_{\bf{x}})+L1(\hat{I_{\bf{x}}^{LR}},I_{\bf{x}})\\
&+LapLoss(\hat{I_{\bf{x}}^{LR}},I_{\bf{x}})+PcptL(\hat{I_{\bf{x}}^{LR}},I_{\bf{x}})\end{split}

(27)

Augmented Wrinkle Loss.

In order to enforce the rendering of geometric details onto the rendered image we add ‘fake’ wrinkles to an image
I_{\bf{x}}
and force
R
to generate the same. Given the the detailed geometry of
I_{\bf{x}}
,
G_{\mathcal{D}}=\{\mathcal{D}(I_{\mathbf{x}}),\alpha_{s},\alpha_{e}\}
, a geometry with ‘fake’ details
G_{\mathcal{D}}^{*}=\{\mathcal{D}(I^{*}_{\mathbf{z}}),\alpha_{s},\alpha_{e}\}
using the geometric details from some random image
I^{*}_{\mathbf{z}}
and the lighting
l
of
I_{\bf{x}}
, the ‘fake’ wrinkles are added as follows

\begin{split}&Shading(I_{\bf{}_{x}})=L_{Sph}(G_{\mathcal{D}},l);Shading^{*}(I_{\bf{}_{x}})=L_{Sph}(G_{\mathcal{D}}^{*},l)\\
&I_{\bf{}_{x}}^{*}=Shading^{*}(I_{\bf{}_{x}})\times\left(\frac{I_{\bf{}_{x}}}{Shading(I_{\bf{}_{x}})}\right)\end{split}

(28)

where
L_{Sph}
is the spherical harmonic lighting function and
l
are the coefficients of the first 9 spherical harmonics.
The artificially wrinkled image
I_{\bf{}_{x}}^{*}
is now re-rendered using
R

\begin{split}\hat{I}_{\mathbf{x}}^{*}&=R(\mathcal{T}(I_{\mathbf{x}}),G_{\mathcal{D}}^{*},\mathbf{x},c,l)\\
\mathcal{L}_{AugW}&=LapLoss(\hat{I}_{\mathbf{x}}^{*},I_{\bf{}_{x}}^{*})\end{split}

(29)

In order to faithfully reconstruct
I_{\bf{}_{x}}^{*}
,
R
is forced to rely on the detailed geometry
G_{\mathcal{D}}^{*}
, since the input texture map
\mathcal{T}(I_{\mathbf{x}})
, and consequently the neural texture map
contain no
information about the ‘fake’ wrinkles.

Detailed Shading Loss.

In addition to the Augmented Wrinkle Loss, we also try to predict the shading of the detailed facial geometry from the output rendering
\hat{I}_{\mathbf{x}}

\begin{split}&\hat{Shading}(\hat{I}_{\mathbf{x}})=f_{\theta}(\hat{I}_{\mathbf{x}})\\
&\mathcal{L}_{DSL}=LapLoss(\hat{Shading}(\hat{I}_{\mathbf{x}}),Shading^{*}(I_{\bf{}_{x}}))\end{split}

(30)

where
f_{\theta}
is a small convolutional network (CNN) with only two layers and the shading
Shading^{*}(I_{\bf{}_{x}})
is calculated as in Eq. (
28
). We calculate this loss only over the skin region. Since,
f_{\theta}
is a small CNN with limited representational capacity, the details must be quite visible on the rendered image
\hat{I}_{\mathbf{x}}
in order for them to be picked up by
f_{\theta}
to generate an accurate shading
\hat{Shading}(\hat{I}_{\mathbf{x}})
.

Expression Adversarial Loss.

In order to ensure that the rendered output conforms to the target expression we use an expression adversarial loss. Given a rendered image,
\hat{I}_{\mathbf{x}}=R(\mathcal{T}(I_{\mathbf{x}}),G_{\mathcal{D}},\mathbf{x},c,l)
, manifesting the expression encoded by AU
\bf{x}
an expression discriminator,
\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Exp}}^{\textrm{RGB}}
, outputs

\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Exp}}^{\textrm{RGB}}(\hat{I}_{\mathbf{x}})=\{r,\hat{\bf{x}}\}

(31)

where
r
is a realism score and
\hat{\bf{x}}
is the predicted AU. We use the Non-Saturating adversarial loss
[
13
]
along with the
R1
gradient penalty
[
22
]
to train
\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Exp}}
. More specifically, let
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{I}}
be the distribution of real images, the adversarial loss can then be written as

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}^{\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Exp}}^{\textrm{RGB}}}_{Adv}&=-\mathbb{E}_{\hat{I_{\bf{x}}}\sim R(.)}\left[\text{log }\left(1\shortminus\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Exp}}^{\textrm{RGB},\textrm{r}}(\hat{I_{\bf{x}}})\right)\right]\\
&-\mathbb{E}_{I_{\bf{x}}\sim\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{I}}}\left[\text{log }\left(\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Exp}}^{\textrm{RGB},\textrm{r}}(I_{\bf{x}})\right)\right]\\
&+\mathbb{E}_{I_{\bf{x}}\sim\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{I}}}\left[||\nabla\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Exp}}^{\textrm{RGB},\textrm{r}}(I_{\bf{x}})||_{2}^{2}\right]\end{split}

(32)

where
\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Exp}}^{\textrm{RGB},\textrm{r}}
is the realism output head of
\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Exp}}^{\textrm{RGB}}
. In addition,
\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Exp}}^{\textrm{RGB}}
is trained to minimize the predicted AU error

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}^{\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Exp}}^{\textrm{RGB}}}_{Exp}&=\mathbb{E}_{I_{\bf{x}}\sim\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{I}}}\left[||[\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Exp}}^{\textrm{RGB},\textrm{AU}}(I_{\bf{x}})\shortminus\mathbf{x}||_{2}^{2}\right]\end{split}

(33)

where
\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Exp}}^{\textrm{RGB},\textrm{AU}}
is the AU output head of
\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Exp}}^{\textrm{RGB}}
. The Rendering Network,
R
is trained to minimize adversarial loss

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}^{R}_{ExpAdv}&=\shortminus\mathbb{E}_{I_{\mathbf{x}}}\text{log }\left(\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Exp}}^{\textrm{RGB},\textrm{r}}(R(.)\right)\\
\end{split}

(34)

and AU loss

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}^{R}_{AU}&=\mathbb{E}_{I_{\mathbf{x}}}||\textrm{D}_{\textrm{Exp}}^{\textrm{RGB},\textrm{AU}}(R(\cdot))\shortminus\mathbf{y}||_{2}^{2}\\
\end{split}

(35)

where
R(\cdot)
is to be read as in Eq. (
3
).

5
Conclusion and Future Work

We have presented FaceDet3D, a method capable of predicting, from a single image, plausible facial geometric details as the expression changes and render them photo-realistically. Using adversarial losses along with weak supervision we train a detail prediction network capable of predicting plausible facial details for any target expression. A rendering network then renders these details photo-realistically in a manner that is consistent with the target expression and the predicted detailed geometry. The
Augmented Wrinkle Loss
and
Detailed Shading Loss
force the rendering network to use cues from the detailed geometry and not rely solely on the neural texture map to generate the details, ensuring consistency in the appearance of the rendered details across a variety of views.
Due to the lack of explicit disentanglement of the lighting within our method, the renderings we generate are entangled with the input lighting conditions and therefore cannot be controlled independently. The detail prediction network relies on the detail map estimated by FDS
[
6
]
as input to predict the plausible details of the target expression, therefore it cannot handle occlusions such as glasses or make-up very well as FDS
[
6
]
fails in those conditions. In future work, we plan to incorporate explicit disentanglement of lighting in order to enable greater control over the final rendering along with explicit modelling occlusions and joint training of
DetP
and
R
.
