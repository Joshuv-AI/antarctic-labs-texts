Wigner-Smith Time Delay Matrix for Acoustic Scattering: Theory and Phenomenology
Utkarsh R. Patel, Yiqian Mao, Eric Michielssen

Abstract
The Wigner-Smith (WS) time delay matrix relates a lossless system’s scattering matrix to its frequency derivative. First proposed in the realm of quantum mechanics to characterize time delays experienced by particles during a collision, this article extends the use of WS time delay techniques to acoustic scattering problems governed by the Helmholtz equation. Expression for the entries of the WS time delay matrix involving renormalized volume integrals of energy densities are derived, and shown to hold true independent of the scatterer’s geometry, boundary condition (sound-soft or sound-hard), and excitation. Numerical examples show that the eigenmodes of the WS time delay matrix describe distinct scattering phenomena characterized by well-defined time delays.



I
Introduction

The concept of the Wigner-Smith (WS) time delay matrix
\mathbf{Q}
was proposed by Felix Smith to characterize time delays experienced by quantum particles during a collision
Smith_1960
.
Specifically, Smith showed that the matrix

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}=j\mathbf{S}^{\dagger}\frac{\partial\mathbf{S}}{\partial k}

(1)

where
\mathbf{S}
is the scattering matrix of a lossless potential well,
\partial/\partial k
denotes the derivative w.r.t. wavenumber
k
, and
\,{}^{\dagger}
represents the adjoint operation,
fully characterizes the time particles dwell in a system.

Since its introduction in 1960, the WS time delay matrix has found many applications in quantum mechanics, photonics, and electromagnetics.
In quantum mechanics, the WS time delay matrix
\mathbf{Q}
has been used to study photoionization and photoemission time delays
Gallmann_2017
;
Hockett_2016
, to analyze traversal times during quantum tunneling
Buttiker_1982
;
Wardlaw_1988
, and to characterize quantum mechanical decay mechanisms
Dittes_2000
.
In photonics,
\mathbf{Q}
has been used to study wave propagation in multimode fibers
Carpenter_2015
, to shape light flow in disordered media and complex cavities
Brandstotter_2019
;
Gerardin_2016
;
Bohm_2018
, and to optimize light storage in highly scattering media
Durand_2019
.
In electromagnetics, WS time delays have been used for focusing energy in a microwave cavity
Amb2017focus
, to characterize
group delays of fields interacting with a two-port waveguide
Winful_2003
, to decompose electromagnetic fields in terms of modes with well-defined time delays
TAP1_WS
, and to characterize the frequency sensitivity of antenna input impedances
TAP2_WS
.

Applications of WS time delay concepts to acoustic scattering problems have been few and far in between, however. Exceptions include the use of
\mathbf{Q}
to study acoustic resonances in fluid-loaded elastic plates
Frank2006
, the study of scattering from two-dimensional fluid slabs in an elastic medium
Rembert2007four
, and the characterization of elastic wave propagation in disordered media
gerardin2014full
.

This work, along with its companion paper “Wigner-Smith Time Delay Matrix for Acoustic Scattering: Computational Aspects”, extends the authors’ work
TAP1_WS
on WS methods for Maxwell’s equations (electromagnetics) to the Helmholtz equation (acoustics).
The WS time delay matrix of a lossless acoustic scatterer possesses several interesting properties that carry over wholesale from its electromagnetic counterpart, and are summarized next – the reader is referred to the section on “WS Theory: Systems Perspective” in reference
TAP1_WS
for a detailed derivation and justification of the statements that follow.

•

First, the WS time delay matrix
\mathbf{Q}
is Hermitian and hence its diagonal elements are purely real. This property can be derived by using the symmetry and unitary properties of the scattering matrix, i.e.
\mathbf{S}^{T}=\mathbf{S}
and
\mathbf{S}^{\dagger}\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{I}
, where
\mathbf{I}
is an identity matrix and
\,{}^{T}
denotes the transpose operation.

•

Second, the
n
-th diagonal element of
\mathbf{Q}
represents the average time delay experienced by an incoming narrowband wave packet that enters the system via its
n
-th port prior to exiting via any other port.

•

Third,
\mathbf{Q}
and
\mathbf{S}
can be simultaneously diagonalized as
\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{W}\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.29167pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\mathbf{Q}$\kern-0.50003pt}}}\mathbf{W}^{\dagger}
and
\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{W}^{*}\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.29167pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\mathbf{S}$\kern-0.50003pt}}}\mathbf{W}^{\dagger}
.
\mathbf{W}
is a unitary matrix whose columns are eigenvectors of
\mathbf{Q}
and are often called “WS modes”.

\mathbf{Q}

is a real diagonal matrix that collects
\mathbf{Q}
’s eigenvalues, often called “WS time delays” or “WS dwell times”.

•

Fourth, the simultaneous diagonalization of
\mathbf{Q}
and
\mathbf{S}
implies that WS modes are decoupled and are characterized by well-defined time delays. In other words, an incoming field composed of narrowband wave packets that enter the system weighed by the entries of
\mathbf{Q}
’s
n
-th eigenvector exit the system with a time delay characterized by
\mathbf{Q}
’s
n
-th eigenvalue.

This paper elucidates the definition and use of the WS time delay matrix for acoustic scattering problems governed by the Helmholtz equation. Its contributions are twofold.

•

First, it demonstrates that the elements of
\mathbf{Q}
can be expressed as renormalized volume integrals of energy density-like quantities involving fluid potentials. The derived volume integral expressions for elements of
\mathbf{Q}
provide an alternative to Eqn. (
1
), allowing
\mathbf{Q}
’s computation without knowledge of
\mathbf{S}
and
\partial\mathbf{S}/\partial k
. When used in conjunction with Eqn. (
1
), the volume integral expression for
\mathbf{Q}
can be used to compute
\partial\mathbf{S}/\partial k
provided
\mathbf{S}
is known.

•

Second, it demonstrates via numerical experimentation that WS modes can be used to untangle various wave components, including those associated with corner/edge diffraction, surface waves, ballistic scattering, and resonant modes, and that each of these is characterized by distinct and well-defined time delays/dwell times and stored energies.

The two topics above are detailed in Secs. II and Sec. III below.
Conclusions and avenues for future research are provided in Sec. IV. Throughout this paper, the superscript
′
denotes
{\partial}/{\partial}k
.

II.1
Setup

Consider a closed scatterer
\Omega_{s}
with surface
d\Omega_{s}
that resides in a lossless and homogeneous medium with (real) wavenumber
k
and is circumscribed by a sphere of radius
a
centered at the origin (Fig.
1
).
Let
\Omega
and
d\Omega
denote the volume and surface of a sphere of radius
R\gg a
and
kR\gg 1
, also centered about the origin.
The fluid velocity potential
\phi(\mathbf{x})
in a source-free medium
\mathbb{R}^{3}\backslash\Omega_{s}
satisfies the Helmholtz equation

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\phi(\mathbf{x})+k^{2}\phi(\mathbf{x})=0

(2)

subject to the Dirichlet boundary condition

\displaystyle\phi(\mathbf{x})=0,\quad\mathbf{x}\in d\Omega_{s}

(3)

for a sound-soft scatterer or the Neumann boundary condition

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\phi(\mathbf{x})}{\partial n(\mathbf{x})}=0,\quad\mathbf{x}\in d\Omega_{s}

(4)

for a sound-hard scatterer; here
\partial/\partial n(\mathbf{x})
denotes the outward normal derivative on
d\Omega_{s}
.

On
d\Omega
, the velocity potential may be expanded in terms of
M
orthonormal modes
{\cal X}_{p}(\theta,\phi)
.
While there exist many different choices for modes
{\cal X}_{p}(\theta,\phi)
, this paper adopts a specific realization based on spherical harmonics
{\cal X}_{p}(\theta,\phi)
with
p=(l,m)
, where
l=0,\ldots,l_{max}
,
m=-l,\ldots,l
, detailed in Appendix
A
;
M=(l_{max}+1)^{2}
,
l_{max}=ka+c(ka)^{1/3}
and typically
2<c<4

Wiscombe1980
; these modes are orthonormal, i.e.

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}{\cal X}_{p}(\theta,\phi){\cal X}_{q}^{*}(\theta,\phi)\sin\theta d\theta d\phi=\delta_{p,q}

(5)

where
\delta_{l,l^{\prime}}
is a Kronecker delta function.

Assume that the scatterer is excited by a radially-travelling
incoming
(
i
) unit-power field
\phi_{p}^{i}(\mathbf{x})
. Using large argument approximations, this field on
d\Omega
reads

\displaystyle\phi_{p,\infty}^{i}(\mathbf{x})={\cal X}_{p}(\theta,\phi)\frac{e^{jkr}}{r}\,.

(6)

Next, let
\phi_{p}(\mathbf{x})
denote the
total
field throughout
\Omega
due to the excitation in Eqn. (
6
). Near
d\Omega
, this field is

\displaystyle\phi_{p,\infty}(\mathbf{x})=\phi_{p,\infty}^{i}(\mathbf{x})+\phi_{p,\infty}^{o}(\mathbf{x})

(7)

where
\phi_{p,\infty}^{o}(\mathbf{x})
, the
outgoing
field near
d\Omega
, can be expressed as

\displaystyle\phi_{p,\infty}^{o}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbf{S}_{mp}{\cal X}_{m}^{*}(\theta,\phi)\frac{e^{-jkr}}{r}\,.

(8)

That is,
\phi_{p,\infty}^{o}(\mathbf{x})
is the superposition of
M
outgoing modes weighed by wavenumber-dependent scattering coefficients
\mathbf{S}_{mp}
.
The above construct guarantees that
\mathbf{S}
is symmetric and unitary, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}^{\dagger}\mathbf{S}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{I}

(9a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}^{T}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{S}\,.

(9b)

In preparation of the derivation of the WS relationship, consider two velocity potentials,
\phi_{p}(\mathbf{x})
and
\phi_{q}(\mathbf{x})
.
Taking the derivative w.r.t
k
of Eqn. (
2
) with
\phi(\mathbf{x})=\phi_{p}(\mathbf{x})
yields

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\phi_{p}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x})+2k\phi_{p}(\mathbf{x})+k^{2}\phi_{p}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=0\,.

(10)

Multiplying the conjugate of Eqn. (
2
) with
\phi(\mathbf{x})=\phi_{q}(\mathbf{x})
by
\phi_{p}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x})
and subtracting from it Eqn. (
10
) multiplied by
\phi_{q}^{*}(\mathbf{x})
yields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2k}(\phi_{p}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x})\nabla^{2}\phi_{q}^{*}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle-\phi_{q}^{*}(\mathbf{x})\nabla^{2}\phi_{p}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x}))

\displaystyle=\phi_{p}(\mathbf{x})\phi_{q}^{*}(\mathbf{x})\,.

(11)

Using the identity
\psi\nabla^{2}\gamma-\gamma\nabla^{2}\psi=\nabla\cdot\left(\psi\nabla\gamma-\gamma\nabla\psi\right)
, Eqn. (
11
) simplifies to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2k}\nabla\cdot\left(\phi_{p}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x})\nabla\phi_{q}^{*}(\mathbf{x})-\phi_{q}^{*}(\mathbf{x})\nabla\phi_{p}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x})\right)

\displaystyle=\phi_{p}(\mathbf{x})\phi_{q}^{*}(\mathbf{x})\,.

(12)

Next, the RHS of Eqn. (
12
) is rewritten using Eqn. (
2
) with
\phi(\mathbf{x})=\phi_{q}^{*}(\mathbf{x})
and the vector identity
\phi\nabla^{2}\gamma=\nabla\cdot(\phi\nabla\gamma)-\nabla\gamma\cdot\nabla\phi
to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2k}\nabla\cdot

\displaystyle\left(\phi_{p}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x})\nabla\phi_{q}^{*}(\mathbf{x})-\phi_{q}^{*}(\mathbf{x})\nabla\phi_{p}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\phi_{p}(\mathbf{x})\phi_{q}^{*}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2k^{2}}\nabla\cdot(\phi_{p}(\mathbf{x})\nabla\phi_{q}^{*}(\mathbf{x}))

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2k^{2}}\nabla\phi_{q}^{*}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\nabla\phi_{p}(\mathbf{x})\,.

(13)

Finally, integrating Eqn. (
13
) over
\Omega
and using the divergence theorem yields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2k}

\displaystyle\int_{d\Omega}\bm{\hat{r}}\cdot\bigg[\phi_{p,\infty}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x})\nabla\phi_{q,\infty}^{*}(\mathbf{x})-\phi_{q,\infty}^{*}(\mathbf{x})\nabla\phi_{p,\infty}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{k}\nabla\phi_{q,\infty}^{*}(\mathbf{x})\phi_{p,\infty}(\mathbf{x})\bigg]d\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\phi_{p}(\mathbf{x})\phi_{q}^{*}(\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2k^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\nabla\phi_{q}^{*}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\nabla\phi_{p}(\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{x}\,.

(14)

The above equation assumes a hard or soft-source boundary condition to eliminate the integration over
d\Omega_{s}
on the LHS.

II.2
WS Relationship

The evaluation of the LHS of Eqn. (
14
) requires expressions for
\phi_{p,\infty}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x})
,
\bm{\hat{r}}\cdot\nabla\phi_{p,\infty}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x})
,
\phi_{q,\infty}^{*}(\mathbf{x})
, and
\bm{\hat{r}}\cdot\nabla\phi_{q,\infty}^{*}(\mathbf{x})
.
Substituting Eqns. (
6
) and (
8
) into Eqn. (
7
) and differentiating w.r.t.
k
yields

\displaystyle\phi_{p,\infty}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=j{\cal X}_{p}(\theta,\phi)e^{jkr}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{n=1}^{M}\mathbf{S}_{np}^{\prime}{\cal X}_{n}^{*}(\theta,\phi)\frac{e^{-jkr}}{r}

\displaystyle\quad-j\sum_{n=1}^{M}\mathbf{S}_{np}{\cal X}_{n}^{*}(\theta,\phi)e^{-jkr}\,.

(15)

Taking the gradient of Eqn. (
15
), dotting the result with
\bm{\hat{r}}
, and using the large argument behavior of
r\rightarrow\infty
produces

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{r}}

\displaystyle\cdot\nabla\phi_{p,\infty}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=-k{\cal X}_{p}(\theta,\phi)e^{jkr}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{n=1}^{M}\mathbf{S}_{np}^{\prime}{\cal X}_{n}^{*}(\theta,\phi)\frac{e^{-jkr}}{r}\left(-jk\right)

\displaystyle\quad-k\sum_{n=1}^{M}\mathbf{S}_{np}{\cal X}_{n}^{*}(\theta,\phi)e^{-jkr}.

(16)

Likewise, substituting Eqns. (
6
) and (
8
) into Eqn. (
7
) while changing

to

, and conjugating the result yields

\displaystyle\phi_{q,\infty}^{*}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle={\cal X}_{q}^{*}(\theta,\phi)\frac{e^{-jkr}}{r}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbf{S}_{mq}^{*}{\cal X}_{m}(\theta,\phi)\frac{e^{jkr}}{r}\,.

(17)

Finally, taking the gradient of Eqn. (
17
), dotting it with
\bm{\hat{r}}
and using the large argument approximation for

results in

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{r}}\cdot\nabla\phi_{q,\infty}^{*}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle={\cal X}_{q}^{*}(\theta,\phi)\frac{e^{-jkr}}{r}\left(-jk\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbf{S}_{mq}^{*}{\cal X}_{m}(\theta,\phi)\frac{e^{jkr}}{r}\left(jk\right)\,.

(18)

Next, substituting Eqns. (
15
), (
16
), (
17
), and (
18
) into the LHS of Eqn. (

) and evaluating the resulting integral as detailed in Appendix

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2k}

\displaystyle\int_{d\Omega}\bm{\hat{r}}\cdot\bigg[\phi_{p,\infty}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x})\nabla\phi_{q,\infty}^{*}(\mathbf{x})-\phi_{q,\infty}^{*}(\mathbf{x})\nabla\phi_{p,\infty}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{k}\nabla\phi_{q,\infty}^{*}(\mathbf{x})\phi_{p,\infty}(\mathbf{x})\bigg]d\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=2R\delta_{p,q}+j\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbf{S}_{mq}^{*}\mathbf{S}_{mp}^{\prime}.

(19)

By using Eqn. (

), Eqn. (
14
) simplifies to

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{qp}=2R\delta_{p,q}+j\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbf{S}_{mq}^{*}\mathbf{S}_{mp}^{\prime}

where
\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{qp}
reads

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{qp}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\phi_{p}(\mathbf{x})\phi_{q}^{*}(\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2k^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\nabla\phi_{q}^{*}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\nabla\phi_{p}(\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{x}.

(21)

To obtain a WS relationship that is independent of
R
, consider the quantity
\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{qp,\infty}
obtained by replacing
(\phi_{p},\nabla\phi_{p})
and
(\phi_{q},\nabla\phi_{q})
by
(\phi_{p,\infty},\nabla\phi_{p,\infty})
and
(\phi_{q,\infty},\nabla\phi_{q,\infty})
, i.e.

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{qp,\infty}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\phi_{p,\infty}(\mathbf{x})\phi_{q,\infty}^{*}(\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2k^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\nabla\phi_{q,\infty}^{*}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\nabla\phi_{p,\infty}(\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{x}.

(22)

The quantities
\phi_{p,\infty}
,
\phi_{q,\infty}
in Eqn. (

) are the same as those in Eqn. (
7
) though their use is extended to all of
\mathbf{x}\in\Omega
; a similar interpretation holds true for their gradients.
Evaluating the integral in Eqn. (
22

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{qp,\infty}

\displaystyle=2R\delta_{p,q}\,.

(23)

Finally, subtracting
\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{qp,\infty}
from both sides of Eqn. (
20
) yields the WS relationship

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}_{qp}=j\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbf{S}_{mq}^{*}\mathbf{S}_{mp}^{\prime}\,,

(24)

where the
(q,p)
-th entry of the WS time delay matrix
\mathbf{Q}
is

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}_{qp}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\left[\phi_{p}(\mathbf{x})\phi_{q}^{*}(\mathbf{x})-\phi_{p,\infty}(\mathbf{x})\phi_{q,\infty}^{*}(\mathbf{x})\right]d\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2k^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\big[\nabla\phi_{q}^{*}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\nabla\phi_{p}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad-\nabla\phi_{q,\infty}^{*}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\nabla\phi_{p,\infty}(\mathbf{x})\big]d\mathbf{x}\,.

(25)

In matrix form, Eqn. (

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}=j\mathbf{S}^{\dagger}\mathbf{S}^{\prime}\,,

(26)

showing that the entries of the WS time delay matrix are renormalized volume integrals of energy-like densities.

II.3
Alternative Formulations

The derivation above casts elements of the WS time delay matrix in terms of integrals involving velocity potentials
\phi_{p}(\mathbf{x})
and
\phi_{q}(\mathbf{x})
,
and
their gradients
\nabla\phi_{p}(\mathbf{x})
and
\nabla\phi_{q}(\mathbf{x})
.
It is often convenient to compute the entries of the WS time delay matrix from just the scalar fields
or
their gradients.
The first alternative formulation, obtained by integrating Eqn. (
12
), reads

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}^{a}

\displaystyle=j\mathbf{S}^{\dagger}\mathbf{S}^{\prime}

(27)

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}^{a}_{qp}=\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{qp}^{a}+\frac{j}{2k}(-1)^{m}\mathbf{S}_{q,\tilde{p}}-\frac{j}{2k}(-1)^{m}\mathbf{S}_{\tilde{p},q}^{*}

(28)

and

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{qp}^{a}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left[\phi_{p}(\mathbf{x})\phi_{q}^{*}(\mathbf{x})-\phi_{p,\infty}(\mathbf{x})\phi_{q,\infty}^{*}(\mathbf{x})\right]d\mathbf{x}\,.

(29)

The derivation of Eqn. (
28
) requires use of the conjugation property of spherical harmonics with
{p}=(l,m)
,
{q}=(l^{\prime},m^{\prime})
, and
\tilde{p}=(l,-m)
(see Eqn. (
35
) in Appendix
A
).
The second alternative formulation, obtained by integrating two times Eqn. (
13
) and subtracting Eqn. (
12
), reads

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}^{b}

\displaystyle=j\mathbf{S}^{\dagger}\mathbf{S}^{\prime}

(30)

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}^{b}_{qp}=\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{qp}^{b}-\frac{j}{2k}(-1)^{m}\mathbf{S}_{q,\tilde{p}}+\frac{j}{2k}(-1)^{m}\mathbf{S}^{*}_{\tilde{p},q}

(31)

and

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{qp}^{b}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\bigg[\nabla\phi_{p}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\nabla\phi_{q}^{*}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\quad\quad-\nabla\phi_{p,\infty}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\nabla\phi_{q,\infty}^{*}(\mathbf{x})\bigg]d\mathbf{x}\,.

(32)

It is easily verified that the symmetric formulation in Eqn. (
26
) is obtained by adding Eqns. (
27
) and (
30
).

IV
Conclusion

This paper elucidated a WS theory for acoustic scattering problems governed by the Helmholtz equation involving sound-soft or sound-hard objects of arbitrary geometry.
The entries of the WS time delay matrix were cast in terms of renormalized volume integrals involving energy densities. Numerical examples show that the eigenvectors of the of WS time delay matrix can be used to untangle scattering phenomena into canonical contributions characterized by well-defined energies and dwell times.

Current research extending the methodology in this paper is focused on

1.

The development of fast frequency-sweep methods for computing a scatterer’s response to broadband excitations;

2.

The design of acoustic systems and devices resulting in fields that exhibit desired group delays;

3.

The phenomenological study of acoustic waves interacting with complicated structures and metamaterials.
