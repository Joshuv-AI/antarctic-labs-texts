Solving sums of squares in global fields
Przemysław Koprowski

Abstract
. The problem of writing a totally positive element as a sum of squares has a long history in mathematics, going back to Bachet and Lagrange. While for some specific rings (like integers or polynomials over the rationals), there are known methods for decomposing an element into a sum of squares, in general, for many other important rings and fields, the problem is still widely open. In this paper, we present an explicit algorithm for decomposing an element of an arbitrary global field (either a number field or a global function field) into a sum of squares of minimal length.

1.
Introduction

One of the fundamental results in classical number theory is the famous Lagrange Four Squares Theorem, stating that every positive integer is a sum of four squares. Over centuries, Lagrange theorem has been generalized in numerous directions. A remarkable result by Siegel says that a totally positive element of any global field is a sum of four squares. Hence, from the existential standpoint, the theory over an arbitrary number field is as complete as over the rationals. It is no longer so if we consider the computational aspect of the problem. There is a number of algorithms for decomposing a positive rational number into a sum of four (or less) squares, see for example
[
2
,
18
,
19
,
20
,
21
]
. On the other hand, for number fields, or more generally global fields, there is only the recent paper by M.-K. Darkey-Mensah and B. Rothkegel
[
7
]
, where the authors give an algorithm for computing the
length
(i.e. the minimal number of summands needed to represent given
a
as a sum of squares) of an element of a global field. The author is not aware of any algorithm that would actually solve the problem of representing an algebraic number as a sum of squares of minimal length. Thus, the goal of this paper is to fill this gap and propose such algorithms, working over arbitrary global fields (so either number fields or global function fields).

Recall that over a field of characteristic
2
, every sum of squares is a square. Hence the problem of decomposing an element into a sum of squares is meaningful only over fields of characteristic distinct from
2
. Therefore, in what follows the term
global field
will always mean “global field of characteristic
\neq 2
”. It can be either a number field (then the characteristic is just zero) or an algebraic function field over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{p^{n}}
, for some odd prime
p
.

Throughout this paper we use the following notation and terminology. By
K
we always denote a global field and
K^{\times}
stands for the group of nonzero elements. We write
\langle a_{1},\dotsc,a_{n}\rangle
for the quadratic form
a_{1}x_{1}^{2}+\dotsb+a_{n}x_{n}^{2}
. The
length
of an element

, denoted
\ell(a)
, is the minimal number of summands needed to express

as a sum of squares. If
a
is not a sum of squares, we put
\ell(a):=\infty
. Since a representation of
-1
as a sum of squares plays a special role, the length of
-1
is called the
level
of
K
and denoted
s(K)
. In particular,
s(K)=\infty
if and only if
K
is formally real. It is known (see e.g.,
[
15
, Example XI.2.4 (8)]
) that the level of a global field can take only one of the following values:
1
,
2
,
4
or
\infty
. Another invariant related to sums of squares is the
Pythagoras number
of
K
, denoted
P(K)
hereafter. By definition, the Pythagoras number is the supremum of the lengths of all the elements of K that are sums of squares.

An equivalency class of valuations is called a

of
K
. We always use fraktur letters
\mathfrak{d}
,
\mathfrak{p}
,
\mathfrak{q}

induces a map
\nicefrac{{K^{\times}}}{{K^{\times 2}}}\to\nicefrac{{\mathbb{Z}}}{{2\mathbb{Z}}}

. Abusing the notation harmlessly, we denote the latter map by

Let
S
be a finite set of places of

, containing all archimedean places. A square-class

is said to be

. The set of
S
-singular square classes form a subgroup of
\nicefrac{{K^{\times}}}{{K^{\times 2}}}
, that we denote
\mathbb{E}_{S}
. Observe that
\mathbb{E}_{S}
is an elementary

. We will use this fact extensively.

For a real place
\mathfrak{r}
, by

we denote the sign function on

, associated to the ordering of

, induced by the unique ordering of the completion
K_{\mathfrak{r}}
. Given a finite prime
\mathfrak{p}
and two elements

, we write

for their Hilbert symbol at
\mathfrak{p}
. Further, if

is a quadratic form (with coefficients in

), then

stands for the associated quadratic form over

. We write

for the Hasse invariant of

. By definition (see e.g.,

s_{\mathfrak{p}}q:=\prod_{i<j}(a_{i},a_{j})_{\mathfrak{p}}.

2.
Algorithms

We may assume that the length of
a
is known a priori, since it can be calculated using
[

Algorithm 1
.

Given an element
a\in K^{\times}
of length
\ell(a)=2
, this algorithm outputs
c_{1},c_{2}\in K^{\times}
such that
a=c_{1}^{2}+c_{2}^{2}
.

(1)

If
-1
is a square in
K
, then output

c_{1}:=\frac{a+1}{2},\qquad c_{2}:=\frac{a-1}{2}\cdot\sqrt{-1}

and quit.

(2)

Otherwise set
L:=K\bigl(\sqrt{-1}\bigr)
and solve the norm equation
N_{L/K}\left(x\right)=a
. Denote a solution by
c_{1}+c_{2}\cdot\sqrt{-1}
.

(3)

Output
c_{1},c_{2}
.

Proof of correctness.

If
-1
is a square in
K
, then the correctness of the output of step (
1
) follows by a direct calculation. Otherwise, when
-1
is not a square, then
L:=K\bigl(\sqrt{-1}\bigr)
is a proper extension of
K
and we have

a=c_{1}^{2}+c_{2}^{2}=N_{L/K}\left(c_{1}+c_{2}\cdot\sqrt{-1}\right)

as desired.
∎

We may now focus on sums of three squares. First of all, observe that if

is a formally real number field. In particular,

is always a proper extension of

Algorithm 2
.

Let

c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}\in K^{\times}
such that
a=c_{1}^{2}+c_{2}^{2}+c_{3}^{2}

(1)

Let

.

(2)

Solve the norm equation

be a solution.

(3)

Output

c_{1}:=\frac{a+1}{2},\qquad c_{2}:=\frac{a-1}{2}\cdot d_{1},\qquad c_{3}:=\frac{a-1}{2}\cdot d_{2}.

Proof of correctness.

By assumption,
-1
is a sum of two squares in

) is solvable. By a direct computation we obtain that

is the sum of squares of the elements

constructed in step (

∎

We can now concentrate on fields of level strictly greater than

Algorithm 4

Let

Proof of correctness.

The proof is very similar to that of Algorithm
3
. Since
a
is a sum of four squares, there is some
c\in K^{\times}
such that the two forms
\langle 1,1,1,c\rangle
and
\langle 1,-a,-c\rangle
are isotropic. In particular,
c
must be totally negative. Again, there is a positive-density set of primes of
K
such that appending to
S
any prime
\mathfrak{q}
from this set, we may find an
\bigl(S\cup\{\mathfrak{q}\}\bigr)
-singular element
b
such that:

(
\textup{C}_{1}
)

\sgn_{\mathfrak{r}}b=\sgn_{\mathfrak{r}}c
for every real place
\mathfrak{r}
of
K
,

(
\textup{C}_{2}
)

b\equiv c\pmod{\mathfrak{p}^{1+\ord_{\mathfrak{p}}4}}
for every prime
\mathfrak{p}\in S\setminus D
,

(
\textup{C}_{3}
)

b\equiv g_{i}\pmod{\mathfrak{d}_{i}^{1+\ord_{\mathfrak{d}_{i}}4}}
for the primes
\mathfrak{d}_{1},\dotsc,\mathfrak{d}_{d}\in D
,

(
\textup{C}_{4}
)

\ord_{\mathfrak{q}}b=1
.

By the Local Square Theorem we have

•

b\cdot K_{\mathfrak{p}}^{\times 2}=c\cdot K_{\mathfrak{p}}^{\times 2}
for every
\mathfrak{p}\in S\setminus D
,

•

b\cdot K_{\mathfrak{d}_{i}}^{\times 2}=g_{i}\cdot K_{\mathfrak{d}_{i}}^{\times 2}
for
\mathfrak{d}_{1},\dotsc,\mathfrak{d}_{d}\in D
.

Denote

q_{1}:=\langle 1,1,1,b\rangle\qquad\text{and}\qquad q_{2}:=\langle 1,-a,-b\rangle.

As in the proof of correctness of Algorithm
3
, we will show that the coordinates
\xi_{1},\dotsc,\xi_{k}
of
b
in
\mathbb{E}_{S\cup\{\mathfrak{q}\}}
form a solution to (

♠

‣
6g
). And every solution to (

♠

‣
6g
) results in an element

for which
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
are isotropic.

For archimedean places of
K
, as well as the non-archimedean ones, but distinct from
\mathfrak{q}
and not sitting in
S
, the arguments are the same as used in the previous proof. Take a prime
{\mathfrak{p}_{i}}\in S\setminus D
. Then
q_{1}\otimes K_{\mathfrak{p}_{i}}
is isotropic since it contains three units. For the form
q_{2}
we use the same arguments as in the previous proof to show that
q_{2}\otimes K_{\mathfrak{p}_{i}}
is isotropic, since
b\cdot K_{\mathfrak{p}_{i}}^{\times 2}=c\cdot K_{\mathfrak{p}_{i}}^{\times 2}
, and this isotropy is equivalent to the condition

\sum_{j\leq k}\xi_{j}c_{ij}=w_{i}.

What really distinguishes sums of four squares from sums of three squares is the local behavior of
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
at the dyadic primes
\mathfrak{d}_{1},\dotsc,\mathfrak{d}_{d}\in D
. Fix a prime
{\mathfrak{d}_{i}}\in D
. We know that
b\cdot K_{\mathfrak{d}_{i}}^{\times 2}=g_{i}\cdot K_{\mathfrak{d}_{i}}^{\times 2}
and
(g_{i},h_{i})_{\mathfrak{d}_{i}}=-1
. Therefore we have

(-1)^{v_{i}}=-1=(h_{i},b)_{\mathfrak{d}_{i}}=\prod_{j\leq k}(h_{i},\kappa_{j})^{\xi_{j}}_{\mathfrak{d}_{i}}=\prod_{j\leq k}(-1)^{\xi_{j}b_{ij}}.

This means that the following
\mathbb{F}_{2}
-linear condition holds

\sum_{j\leq k}\xi_{j}b_{ij}=v_{i}.

On the other hand, if
\xi_{1},\dotsc,\xi_{k}\in\{0,1\}
satisfy the following equality, and
b=\kappa_{1}^{\xi_{1}}\dotsm\kappa_{k}^{\xi_{k}}

(h_{i},b)=-1

is not a local square at

Similar arguments apply to the form

\displaystyle s_{\mathfrak{d}_{i}}q_{2}

\displaystyle=(-a,-b)_{\mathfrak{d}_{i}}

\displaystyle=(-1,-\det q_{2})_{\mathfrak{d}_{i}}\cdot(a,g_{i})_{\mathfrak{d}_{i}}

Therefore,

\sum_{j\leq k}\xi_{j}c_{ij}=w_{i}.

Conversely, if the above equality holds for some

Remark 1
.

In Algorithms
3
and
4
we keep enlarging the set
S
until we happen to come across a prime
\mathfrak{q}
from a certain positive-density set. There are two basic strategies to do it, with different worst-case scenarios and average behaviors. One method is to select primes of
K
at random. The probability that we will find a “good” prime
\mathfrak{q}
is given by the density of this set, and so is positive. However, in the worst case scenario, we may hypothetically keep picking the primes that are constantly not in this set. Hence, while this method is better-behaved in practice, it does not warrant that the algorithms terminate in finite time. In theory it may take indefinitely long to find a correct prime
\mathfrak{q}
. The other approach uses a purely deterministic exhaustive search. Start with a (rational) prime
p=3
and try all the primes of
K
extending
p
. Then go to the next prime number, take the primes of
K
extending it, and so on. Although slower in practice, this procedure guaranties that the two algorithms terminate in finite time.

Remark 2
.

In step (
4
) of Algorithm
4
, for every
{\mathfrak{d}_{i}}\in D
with
i\leq|D|
, we need to find elements
g_{i},h_{i}\in K
such that

(a,g_{i})_{\mathfrak{d}_{i}}=1\qquad\text{and}\qquad(g_{i},h_{i})_{\mathfrak{d}_{i}}=-1.

The element
g_{i}
is not used again in the algorithm. It is needed only to find
h_{i}
and can be discarded afterwards. (On the other hand it is very important in the proof of correctness of the algorithm.)

Here again, a fast and efficient method is a probabilistic one. Let
n_{i}:=(K_{\mathfrak{d}_{i}}:\mathbb{Q}_{2})
. If
a\notin K_{\mathfrak{d}_{i}}^{\times 2}
then the probability that a random element
g_{i}\in K
satisfies the first of the above two conditions is
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
. However, the probability that
g_{i}
itself is a local square (and so the second condition can never be satisfied) is
2^{-n_{i}-2}
. Thus, the probability that we pick a “good”
g_{i}
at random equals
(\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2^{n_{i}+2}}})\geq\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}
. On the other hand, if
a\in K_{\mathfrak{d}_{i}}^{\times 2}
, then
every

g_{i}\in K
satisfies the first condition. Nevertheless,
g_{i}
still cannot be a local square at
{\mathfrak{d}_{i}}
, for the second condition to be satisfiable. Therefore, the probability of finding a “good”
g_{i}
at random is
(1-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2^{n_{i}+2}}})\geq\nicefrac{{3}}{{4}}
. In both cases, once we selected
g_{i}
, an element
h_{i}
satisfying the second condition can be found by a random search with probability
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
.
