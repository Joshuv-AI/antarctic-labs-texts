Modeling same-order modes of multicell cavities
Olof Troeng

Abstract
We derive the transfer function of a multicell cavity with parasitic same-order modes (from power coupler to pickup probe). The derived model is discussed and compared to measurement data.



I
Introduction

Multicell elliptical cavities are suitable for accelerating particle beams with velocities greater than
0.5c
.
However, these cavities intrinsically have parasitic electromagnetic modes that are close in frequency to the accelerating mode.
These so-called
same-order modes
, or fundamental passband modes, interact both with the rf system and the beam.
Their interaction with the rf system may, without counter-measures, give instability in the field control loop
Schilcher 1998
, and their interaction with the beam drives emittance growth
Ainsworth and Molloy 2012
.
Dynamic models of same-order modes and these interactions are necessary for design and analysis of field control algorithms.

The regular geometry of multicell elliptical cavities makes it possible to derive the same-order-mode dynamics from a small number of cavity parameters. Such dynamic models have been used for studying how beating of same-order modes affects the beam
Ferrario et al. 1996
.
The steady-state models in
Doolittle 1989
;
Padamsee et al. 2008
;
Wangler 2008
;
Sekutowicz 2010
capture the shapes and resonance frequencies of the modes but not their dynamics and decay rates.

For field control analysis in the frequency domain, it is necessary to know the transfer function from the rf drive to the pickup-probe signal.
In
Schilcher 1998
, a real-coefficient, two-input two-output transfer function from rf drive to pickup signal (valid for superconducting cavities) was presented without motivation.
Such real-coefficient, two-input two-output models are common in the field control literature. However, as we discussed in
Troeng 2019
, the equivalent, complex-coefficient, single-input single-output (SISO) representation gives more intuition and simplifies analysis.

In this paper we: (1) derive a complex baseband model of a multicell cavity along the lines of
Ferrario et al. 1996
, but using the energy-based parameterization from
Troeng 2020
; (2) normalize that model to make it suitable for field control analysis; (3) derive the complex-coefficient SISO transfer function from power coupler to pickup signal (also valid for normal-conducting cavities); and (4) fit the transfer-function model to measurement data from a 6-cell niobium cavity taken at room temperature and cryogenic temperature.

Remark 1:

An incorrect transfer-function model for cavities with parasitic modes was proposed in
Vogel 2007
.
It was based on taking the transfer function of the individual modes from
Schilcher 1998
and multiplying them with the modes’ coupling strengths to the rf system, although that effect was already accounted for in
Schilcher 1998
. The incorrect model in
Vogel 2007
predicts that the resonance peaks have different magnitudes, while both the analysis in this paper and
Liepe 2001
[Fig. 5.9] indicate that they have similar magnitudes.

Also the stability analysis in
Vogel 2007
is problematic.
It is not possible to analyze closed-loop stability of MIMO systems with strong cross couplings (as from same-order modes) using loop-by-loop analysis
(
Zhou et al. 1996
, Sec. 8.6)
,
Skogestad and Postlethwaite 2007
.
The complex-signal perspective in
Troeng 2019
together with the model in this paper enables correct and intuitive analysis.

II.1
Cavity model with parasitic modes

We start by considering parasitic modes of a general cavity using the energy-based parameterization in
Haus 1983
;
Troeng 2020
. Let
\mathbf{A}_{k}
be the complex envelope of mode
k
, with its magnitude (units
\sqrt{J}
) scaled so that
\left|\mathbf{A}_{k}\right|^{2}
is the mode energy. This unambiguously defines the mode amplitude without reference to the effective voltage of the accelerating mode.
If coupling between the modes can be neglected then their complex envelopes evolve according to
Troeng 2020

\frac{d\mathbf{A}_{k}}{dt}=(-\gamma_{k}+i\Delta\omega_{k})\mathbf{A}_{k}+\sqrt{2{\gamma_{\text{ext}}}_{k}}\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{g}}+\frac{\bm{\alpha}_{k}}{2}\mathbf{I}_{\mathbf{b}},

(1a)

where
\gamma_{k}={\gamma_{0}}_{k}+{\gamma_{\text{ext}}}_{k}
is the decay rate of the mode amplitudes (
{\gamma_{0}}_{k}
corresponds to resistive losses and
{\gamma_{\text{ext}}}_{k}
to decay through the power coupler);
\Delta\omega_{k}=\omega_{k}-\omega_{\text{rf{}}}
is the offset of the mode frequency
\omega_{k}
relative to the nominal rf frequency
\omega_{\text{rf{}}}
;
\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{g}}
(units
\sqrt{W}
) is the envelope of the forward wave from the rf amplifier with
\left|\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{g}}\right|^{2}
equal to the power in the wave; and
\mathbf{I}_{\mathbf{b}}
is the beam phasor, with
\left|\mathbf{I}_{\mathbf{b}}\right|
equal to the dc beam current.
The complex envelopes
\mathbf{A}_{k}
are defined relative to
\omega_{\text{rf{}}}
, with their phases chosen so that the coefficients in front of
\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{g}}
are real.
The voltage sensed by the pickup probe is a linear combination

\mathbf{V}_{\text{pu}}=\sum_{k=a,1,2,\ldots}^{N}\bm{C}_{k}\mathbf{A}_{k}

(1b)

of the mode amplitudes, where
\bm{C}_{k}
are complex coefficients.
Here we have used the subscript
a
to indicate the mode intended for acceleration. We will use a different labeling when discussing same-order modes.

The cavity–beam-coupling parameters
\bm{\alpha}_{k}
are in general complex. However, the parameter
\bm{\alpha}_{a}=\alpha_{a}
for the accelerating mode is real due to the definition of
\mathbf{I}_{\mathbf{b}}
.

Normalized model with parasitic modes

In many situations, e.g., field control analysis, it is easier to work with normalized models.
Therefore, we introduce the following dimension-free, normalized variables similarly to in

(2b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{i}_{\mathbf{b}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{\gamma_{a}A_{a0}}\frac{\alpha_{a}}{2}\mathbf{I}_{\mathbf{b}}

(3a)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{a}_{a}+\sum_{k=1,2,\ldots}^{N}\bm{c}_{k}\mathbf{a}_{k}

The normalization (

Remark 2:

While
\Delta\omega_{\pi}
is negligible relative to both
\Delta\omega_{N-1}
and typical field control bandwidths, its precise tuning, typically to a value slightly larger than 0, is crucial for minimizing the drive power

;

;

Remark 3:

Typical cell-to-cell coupling factors
k_{cc}
are on the order of
0.01
, which gives

\displaystyle\Delta\omega_{n}

\displaystyle\approx\omega_{\text{cell}}\left(\sqrt{1+2R_{n}^{2}k_{cc}}-\sqrt{1+4k_{cc}}\right)

\displaystyle\approx(R_{n}^{2}-2)k_{cc}\omega_{\text{cell}}.

This shows that the baseband frequencies of the same-order modes are approximately proportional to
R_{n}^{2}-2
.

Remark 4:

There are two definitions of the cell-to-cell coupling factor
k_{cc}
in the accelerator literature.
The
full-passband-width
definition, for which
k_{cc}\approx(\omega_{\pi}-\omega_{1})/\omega_{\pi}

Doolittle 1989
;
Liepe 2001
;
Wangler 2008
;
Sekutowicz 2010
and the
half-passband-width
definition
Ferrario et al. 1996
;
Padamsee et al. 2008

The first definition corresponds to the per-cycle decay of a cell’s energy due to coupling to a neighboring cell and the latter definition corresponds to the decay of the field amplitude.
In this paper we use the half-passband-width definition since it gives slightly more convenient expressions.

II.3
​​Transfer function from rf drive to pickup probe​

The transfer function from
\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{g}}
to
\mathbf{v}_{\text{pu}}
in
Fig.

2
is important for field control analysis. It is given by

\displaystyle P_{\text{cav}}(s)

\displaystyle=\gamma_{\pi}\sum_{n=1}^{N}(-1)^{N-n}\frac{R_{n}^{2}}{s+\gamma_{n}-i\Delta\omega_{n}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma_{\pi}}{{\gamma_{\text{ext}}}_{\pi}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}(-1)^{N-n}\frac{{\gamma_{\text{ext}}}_{n}}{s+\gamma_{n}-i\Delta\omega_{n}},

(9)

where
\gamma_{n}\coloneqq\gamma_{0}+{\gamma_{\text{ext}}}_{n}
is the total decay rate of the
n\pi/N
mode. For superconducting cavities with
\gamma_{0}\ll{\gamma_{\text{ext}}}_{n}
we have that
{\gamma_{\text{ext}}}_{n}\approx\gamma_{n}
and (

(10)

9

First, since the numbers
\gamma_{n}
are small relative to the differences between the numbers
\Delta\omega_{n}

has sharp resonance peaks at (baseband) frequencies
\Delta\omega_{n}
.
Also note that
\Delta\omega_{n}<0

For superconducting cavities (

For normal conducting cavities (
\gamma_{0}\gg{\gamma_{\text{ext}}}_{n}

IV
Conclusion

We have derived a complex-coefficient transfer function model that is valid for both normal conducting and superconducting cavities. The model was seen to give a good fit to measurement data.
