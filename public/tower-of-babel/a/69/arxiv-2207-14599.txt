A Dual Ramsey theorem for trees Thanks: 2000 Mathematics Subject Classification : 05D10.
Stevo Todorcevic, Konstantinos Tyros

Abstract
. We prove a dualization of the Graham–Rothschild Theorem for variable words indexed by homogeneous trees.



1.
Introduction

In 1971, R. Graham and B. Rothschild (see
[
1
]
) proved a higher dimensional analogue of the celebrated Hales-Jewett Theorem (see
[
3
]
) about colorings of parameter words indexed by
\{0,...,n-1\}
where
n
is a positive integer number. To state the Graham–Rothschild Theorem we need to introduce some pieces of notation.
Let
\Lambda
be a finite nonempty set, which we view as an alphabet, let
m,n
be positive integers with
m\leqslant n

and let
v_{0},...,v_{m-1}
be symbols not belonging to
\Lambda
.
We view the elements of the set
\Lambda^{n}
of all functions from the set
\{0,...,n-1\}
into
\Lambda
as constant words.
By the term
m
-parameter word we mean a map

w=w(v_{0},...,v_{m-1}):\{0,...,n-1\}\to\Lambda\cup\{v_{0},...,v_{m-1}\}
such that every
v_{i}
is attained at least once
and for every integer with
0\leqslant i<m-2
we have
\min w^{-1}(\{v_{i}\})<\min w^{-1}(\{v_{i+1}\})
. Given an
m
-parameter word
w=w(v_{0},...,v_{m-1})
and
a_{0},...,a_{m-1}
in
\Lambda
we denote by
w(a_{0},...,a_{m-1})
the element of
\Lambda^{n}

resulting by substituting every occurrence of each
v_{i}
by
a_{i}
and set
[w]_{\Lambda}=\{w(a_{0},...,a_{m-1}):a_{0},...,a_{m-1}\in\Lambda\}
. Moreover, if
k
is a positive integer with
k\leqslant m
then we denote by
[w]_{k,\Lambda}
the set of all
k
-parameter words
\tilde{w}
satisfying
[\tilde{w}]_{\Lambda}\subseteq[w]_{\Lambda}
.
The Graham–Rothschild Theorem states the following.

Theorem 1

(Graham–Rothschild)
.

Let
\ell,k,m
and
r
be positive integers with
k\leqslant m
. Then there exists a positive integer
n_{0}
with the following property. For every finite alphabet
\Lambda
with
\ell
elements, every integer
n
with
n\geqslant n_{0}
and every
r
-coloring of the
k
-parameter words, there exists an
m
-parameter word
w
such that the set
[w]_{k,\Lambda}
is monochromatic.

One of the immediate consequences of the Graham–Rothschild Theorem is a finite form of the Milliken–Taylor Theorem
(see
[
5
]
,
[
9
]
).
Let
k,m
be positive integers with
k\leqslant m
and let
\mathbf{F}=(F_{i})_{i=0}^{m-1}
be a sequence of pairwise
disjoint finite nonempty subsets of the natural numbers such that
(\min F_{i})_{i=0}^{m-1}
is an increasing sequence.
By
\mathrm{DS}_{k}(\mathbf{F})
we denote the set of all pairwise disjoint sequences
(G_{j})_{j=0}^{k-1}
of nonempty sets such that
(\min G_{j})_{j=0}^{m-1}
is an increasing sequence and each
G_{j}
belongs to the set
\{\bigcup_{i\in U}F_{i}:U\subseteq\{0,...,m-1\}\}
. For simplicity, if
n
is a positive integer, then we set

\mathrm{DS}_{k}(n)=\mathrm{DS}_{k}((\{i\})_{i=0}^{n-1})
. The finite form of the Milliken–Taylor Theorem is as follows.

Theorem 2
.

(Milliken–Taylor)
Let
k,m
and
r
be positive integers. Then there exists a positive integer
n_{0}
with the following property.
For every positive integer
n
with
n_{0}\leqslant n
and every
r
-coloring of
\mathrm{DS}_{k}(n)
there exists

\mathbf{F}
in
\mathrm{DS}_{m}(n)
such that the set
\mathrm{DS}_{k}(\mathbf{F})
is monochromatic.

Notice that the case
m=1
of the above result boils down to Folkman’s Theorem (see
[
2
]
; p.82),
which is a finite form of Hindman’s Theorem (see
[
4
]
). The main result of the present work is a
Graham–Rothschild Theorem for variable words indexed by homogeneous trees.
To state our result we need to introduce some pieces of notation.

1.1.
Complete Skew trees

Let
b
and
n
be positive integers. We denote by
b^{<n}
the set of all sequences in
\{0,...,b-1\}
of length less than
n
. For every element
s
in
b^{<n}
we denote by
|s|
its length, while for every
i
in
\{0,...,|s|-1\}
we denote by
s(i)
the
i
-th element of
s
. By
\sqsubseteq
we denote the initial segment ordering and by
\leqslant_{\mathrm{lex}}
we denote the
lexicographical order on the set
b^{<n}
.

We view the subsets of
b^{<n}
as subtrees.
For every subtree
S
of
b^{<n}
and
s
in
S
, we define the following. We set
\mathrm{Pred}_{S}(s)=\{t\in S:\;t\sqsubset s\}
and we denote by
\mathrm{ImSucc}_{S}(s)
the set of all
t
in
S
such that
s\sqsubset t
and there is no
t^{\prime}
in
S
satisfying
s\sqsubset t^{\prime}\sqsubset t
. Finally, we set
\mathrm{h}_{S}(s)
to be the cardinality of the set
\mathrm{Pred}_{S}(s)
.

Let
k
be a positive integer. A
k
-complete skew subtree
S
of
b^{<n}
is a subtree with the following properties.

(i)

S
has a minimum with respect to
\sqsubseteq
and every maximal chain with respect to
\sqsubseteq

is of cardinality
k
.

(ii)

For every
s
and
s^{\prime}
in
S
with
\mathrm{h}_{S}(s)=\mathrm{h}_{S}(s^{\prime})
and
s\leqslant_{\mathrm{lex}}s^{\prime}
, we have that
|s|\leqslant|s^{\prime}|
.

(iii)

For every
s
and
s^{\prime}
in
S
with
\mathrm{h}_{S}(s)<\mathrm{h}_{S}(s^{\prime})
, we have that
|s|<|s^{\prime}|
.

(iv)

For every non maximal
s
in
S
with respect to
\sqsubseteq
and every
i
in
\{0,...,b-1\}
,
there is unique
t
in
\mathrm{ImSucc}_{S}(s)
such that
s^{\smallfrown}(i)\sqsubseteq t
.

1.2.
Variable words indexed by trees

.
Let
b,n
be positive integers and let
\Lambda
be a finite alphabet. By
\mathrm{W}(b,n,\Lambda)
we denote the set of all maps
from
b^{<n}
to
\Lambda
.
Let
k
be a positive integer and
T
a
k
-complete skew subtree of
b^{<n}
. A
T
-variable word
f
on
\mathrm{W}(b,n,\Lambda)
is a map from
b^{<n}
into
\Lambda\cup\{v_{t}:t\in T\}
such that for every
t
in
T
the set
f^{-1}(v_{t})
is non empty and has
t
as a
\sqsubseteq
-minimum. We denote by
\mathrm{W}_{v,T}(b,n,\Lambda)
the set of all
T
-variable words on
\mathrm{W}(b,n,\Lambda)
and by
\mathrm{W}_{v,k}(b,n,\Lambda)

the union of
\mathrm{W}_{v,T}(b,n,\Lambda)
over all possible
k
-complete skew subtrees
T
of
b^{<n}
.
Observe that for every
f
in
\mathrm{W}_{v,k}(b,n,\Lambda)
, there exists unique
k
-complete skew subtree
T
of
b^{<n}
,
which we denote by
\mathrm{ws}(f)
such that
f
is a
T
-variable word.

Let
f
in
\mathrm{W}_{v,k}(b,n,\Lambda)
.
If
(\alpha_{t})_{t\in\mathrm{ws}(f)}\in\Lambda^{\mathrm{ws}(f)}
, then we denote by
f\big((\alpha_{t})_{t\in\mathrm{ws}(f)}\big)
the element of

\mathrm{W}(b,n,\Lambda)
resulting by substituting each occurrence of
v_{t}
in
f
by
\alpha_{t}
for all
t
in
\mathrm{ws}(f)
.
Moreover, we define the span of
f
as

[f]_{\Lambda}=\{f\big((\alpha_{t})_{t\in\mathrm{ws}(f)}\big):(\alpha_{t})_{t\in\mathrm{ws}(f)}\in\Lambda^{\mathrm{ws}(f)}\}.

Finally, if
k^{\prime}
is a positive integer with
k^{\prime}\leqslant k
, then we set

\mathrm{W}_{v,k^{\prime}}(f)=\{g\in\mathrm{W}_{v,k^{\prime}}(b,n,\Lambda):[g]_{\Lambda}\subseteq[f]_{\Lambda}\}.

1.3.
Main result

The main result of the present work states the following.

Theorem 3
.

For every choice of positive integers
b,\ell,k,m
and
r
there exists a positive integer
n_{0}
with the following property. For every integer
n
with
n\geqslant n_{0}
, every finite alphabet
\Lambda
with
\ell
elements and every
r
-coloring of
\mathrm{W}_{v,k}(b,n,\Lambda)
there exists
f
in
\mathrm{W}_{v,m}(b,n,\Lambda)
such that the set
\mathrm{W}_{v,k}(f)
is monochromatic.
We denote the least such
n_{0}
by
\mathrm{TGR}(k,m,b,\ell,r)
.

Notice that the case
b=1
of Theorem
3
yields Theorem
1
.
The proof of Theorem
3
requires the development of a Hales–Jewett type theorem for trees
(see Theorem
13
), which is of its own interest.
The proof of the latter result utilizes a double induction scheme resulting non primitive recursive bounds.

1.4.
A Milliken–Taylor theorem for trees

As the Graham–Rothschild Theorem yields the Milliken-Taylor Theorem,
it is expected that Theorem
3
yields some form of the Milliken-Taylor Theorem for trees.
To state the latter we need some pieces of notation.
Let
b
and
n
be positive integers. We set

\mathcal{U}_{1}(b^{<n})=\{U\subseteq b^{<n}:\;U\;\text{has a minimum}\}.

The subspaces under consideration are collections of pairwise disjoint elements of
\mathcal{U}_{1}(b^{<n})
such that their minimums form a complete skew subtree. More precisely, if
k
is a positive integer, by
\mathcal{U}_{k}(b^{<n})
we denote the set of all collections
(U_{t})_{t\in T}
, where
T
is a
k
-complete skew subtree of
b^{<n}
and
U_{t}
belongs to
\mathcal{U}_{1}(b^{<n})
with
\min U_{t}=t
for all
t
in
T
. We denote by
\mathcal{U}(b^{<n})
the union of
\mathcal{U}_{k}(b^{<n})
over all positive integers
k
. For
\mathbf{U}
and
\mathbf{V}
in
\mathcal{U}(b^{<n})
, we say that
\mathbf{U}
is a subspace of

\mathbf{V}
, we write
\mathbf{U}\leq\mathbf{V}
, if every member of
\mathbf{U}
is a union of members of
\mathbf{V}
. We denote by
\mathcal{U}(\mathbf{U})
the set of all subspaces of
\mathbf{U}
, while if
k
is a positive integer, we set
\mathcal{U}_{k}(\mathbf{U})=\mathcal{U}(\mathbf{U})\cap\mathcal{U}_{k}(b^{<n})
.
Theorem
3
has the following immediate consequence.

Theorem 4
.

Let
k,m,b,r
be positive integers with
k\leqslant m
. Then there exists a positive integer
n_{0}
with the following property. For every integer
n
with
n\geqslant n_{0}
and every coloring of
\mathcal{U}_{k}(b^{<n})
with
r
colors, there exists
\mathbf{U}
in
\mathcal{U}_{m}(b^{<n})
such that the set
\mathcal{U}_{k}(\mathbf{U})
is monochromatic.

Theorem
4
is the result announced in
[
11
]
and can be viewed as a finite and multidimensional version of the main result in
[
11
]
.

2.
Background Material

One of the main tools that we will need in the present work is a variant of the Hales–Jewett Theorem, that is, Theorem
8
below. Although its proof is an straightforward modification of Shelah’s proof of the
Hales–Jewett Theorem, we include its proof for the sake of completeness in an appendix.
Let us start with some basic notation that we will make use for the rest of the paper.
By
\mathbb{N}
we denote the set of the natural numbers starting from 0.
For every two sets
X
and
Y
by
Y^{X}
we denote the set of all maps from
X
into
Y
.
For every choice of sets
X,Y,Z
with
Z\subseteq X
and
f
in
Y^{X}
we denote by
f\upharpoonright Z
the restriction of
f
to
Z
.
For every two sequences of finite length
\mathbf{a}=(a_{i})_{i=0}^{n-1}
and
\mathbf{b}=(b_{i})_{i=0}^{m-1}
we denote their concatenation by

\mathbf{a}^{\smallfrown}\mathbf{b}
, that is, the sequence
(z_{i})_{i=0}^{n+m-1}
satisfying
z_{i}=a_{i}
for every
i=0,...,n-1
and
z_{i}=b_{i}
for all
i=n,...,n+m-1
.

2.1.
The Hales–Jewett Theorem

One of the most central results in Combinatorics is the Hales–Jewett Theorem
[
3
]
. To state it we need some notation.
Let
N
be a positive integer and
\Lambda
a finite alphabet. We view the elements of the set
\Lambda^{N}
of all functions from the set
\{0,...,N-1\}
into
\Lambda
as constant words of length
N
over the alphabet
\Lambda
. Also, let a symbol
v\not\in\Lambda
. A variable word
w(v)
of length
N
over
\Lambda
is a function from
\{0,...,N-1\}
into
\Lambda\cup\{v\}
such that the symbol
v
occurs at least once. For a variable word
w(v)
over
\Lambda
and a letter
a\in\Lambda
we denote by
w(a)
the constant word resulting by substituting each occurrence of
v
by
a
.
A combinatorial line is a set of the form
\{w(a):\;a\in\Lambda\}
, where
w(v)
is a variable word over
\Lambda
.

Theorem 5

(Hales–Jewett)
.

Let
k
and
r
be positive integers. Then there exists a positive integer
N_{0}
with the following property.
For every positive integer
N
with
N\geqslant N_{0}
, every alphabet
\Lambda
with
k
elements and every
r
-coloring of
\Lambda^{N}
, there exists a monochromatic combinatorial line. We denote the least such
N_{0}
by
\mathrm{HJ}(k,r)
.

Moreover, the numbers
\mathrm{HJ}(k,r)
are upper bounded by a primitive recursive function belonging to the class
\mathcal{E}^{5}
of Grzegorczyk’s hierarchy.

The bounds mentioned in the above theorem are due to Shelah
[
7
]
.
We will need a multidimensional version of this Theorem. Let
N
be a positive integer and
\Lambda
be a finite alphabet. Also let
m
be a positive integer and
v_{0},...,v_{m-1}
distinct symbols not belonging to
\Lambda
. An
m
-dimensional variable word of length
N
over
\Lambda
is a function
w:\{0,...,N-1\}\to\Lambda\cup\{v_{0},...,v_{m-1}\}
such that each
v_{i}
occurs and their supports are in block position, i.e.
\max\mathrm{supp}_{w}(v_{i})<\min\mathrm{supp}_{w}(v_{i+1})
for all
i=0,...,m-2
, where
\mathrm{supp}_{w}(v_{i})=\big\{j\in\{0,...,N-1\}:w(j)=v_{i}\big\}
which we will refer to as the wildcard set of
v_{i}
in
w
. In order to highlight the role of the variables, we will denote such an
m
-dimensional variable word by
w(v_{0},...,v_{m-1})
. Thus a variable word is just an
1
-dimensional variable word.

Similarly to the case of a single variable word, we consider substitutions also for
m
-variable
words. In particular, if
a_{0},...,a_{m-1}
are elements of
\Lambda
, then by
w(a_{0},...,a_{m-1})
we denote the constant word resulting by substituting each occurrence of
v_{i}
by
a_{i}
for all
i=0,...,m-1
. An
m
-dimensional combinatorial subspace
S
of
\Lambda^{N}
, is a set of the form
\{w(a_{0},...,a_{m-1}):a_{0},...,a_{m-1}\in\Lambda\}
, where
w(v_{0},...,v_{m-1})
is an
m
-dimensional variable word of length
N
over
\Lambda
. In this case, we say that
w(v_{0},...,v_{m-1})
generates
S
. Let us observe that for every
m
-dimensional combinatorial subspace
S
of
\Lambda^{N}
there is unique
m
-dimensional word of length
N
over
\Lambda
generating
S
. The multidimensional version of Theorem
5
is the following.

Theorem 6

(Hales–Jewett)
.

Let
k,m,r
be positive integers. Then there exists a positive integer
N_{0}
with the following property.
For every positive integer
N
with
N\geqslant N_{0}
, every alphabet
\Lambda
with
k
elements and every
r
-coloring of
\Lambda^{N}
, there exists a monochromatic
m
-dimensional combinatorial subspace. We denote the least such
N_{0}
by
\mathrm{MHJ}(k,m,r)
.

Moreover, the numbers
\mathrm{MHJ}(k,m,r)
are upper bounded by a primitive recursive function belonging to the class
\mathcal{E}^{5}
of Grzegorczyk’s hierarchy.

However, we will need a mild strengthening of Theorem
6
.
To introduce this strengthening, let us denote for every subset
A
of the natural numbers and every positive integer
\kappa
the set of all subsets of
A
with exactly
\kappa
elements by
A^{(\kappa)}
.
In our case, the underling structure is the Cartesian product
\Lambda^{N}\times\{0,...,N-1\}^{(\kappa)}
.
First, for every
m
-dimensional variable word
w
over
\Lambda
of length
N
, let us set
\ell^{w}_{i}=\min\mathrm{supp}_{w}(v_{i})
for all
i=0,...,m-1
.
An
m
-dimensional
\kappa^{*}
combinatorial subspace is a set of the form
X=S\times\{\ell^{w}_{i}:i=0,...,m-1\}^{(\kappa)}
, where
w(v_{1},...,v_{m-1})
is an
m
-dimensional variable word over
\Lambda
of length
N
and
S
the combinatorial subspace generated by
w
. In this case, we say that
w(v_{0},...,v_{m-1})
generates the
\kappa^{*}
combinatorial subspace
X
. Also, let us observe that for every
m
-dimensional
\kappa^{*}
combinatorial subspace
X
there is unique
m
-dimensional variable word generating
X
. We consider the following special type of colorings.

Definition 7
.

Let
\kappa,m,N
be positive integers with
\kappa\leqslant m\leqslant N
and let
\Lambda
be a finite alphabet. Also let
w(v_{0},...,v_{m-1})
be an
m
-dimensional variable word over
\Lambda
of length
N
and let
X
be the
\kappa^{*}
combinatorial subspace generated by
w
. We say that a finite coloring
c
of
\Lambda^{N}\times\{0,...,N-1\}^{(\kappa)}
is strongly
∗
insensitive in
X
if

c(w(a_{0},...,a_{m-1}),\{\ell_{i}^{w}:i\in F\})=c(w(a_{0}^{\prime},...,a_{m-1}^{\prime}),\{\ell_{i}^{w}:i\in F\})

for every choice of
F
in
\{0,...,m-1\}^{(\kappa)}
and
a_{0},...,a_{m-1},a_{0}^{\prime},...,a_{m-1}^{\prime}
in
\Lambda
with
a_{i}=a_{i}^{\prime}
for all
i
in
F
.

It is clear that it is not expected the existence of a monochromatic
\kappa^{*}
combinatorial subspace given an arbitrary coloring. However, we have the following.

Theorem 8
.

Let
k,\kappa,m,r
be positive integers with
\kappa\leqslant m
. Then there exist integers
n_{0},q_{0},...,q_{m}
with
0=q_{0}<q_{1}<...<q_{m}=n_{0}
satisfying the following property. For every integer
N
with
N\geqslant n_{0}
, every finite alphabet
\Lambda
with
k
elements and every
r
-coloring
c
of
\Lambda^{N}\times\{0,...,N-1\}^{(\kappa)}
, there exists an
m
-dimensional
\kappa^{*}
combinatorial subspace
X
such that
c
is strongly
∗
insensitive in
X
.

Moreover, if
w(v_{0},...,v_{m-1})
is the
m
-dimensional variable word generating
X
, then the wildcard set of
v_{i}
in
w
is contained in the interval
[q_{i},q_{i+1})
for all
i=0,...,m-1
. We denote the least such
n_{0}
by
\mathrm{MHJ}^{*}(k,\kappa,m,r)
.

Finally, the numbers
\mathrm{MHJ}^{*}(k,\kappa,m,r)
are upper bounded by a primitive recursive function belonging to the class
\mathcal{E}^{5}
of Grzegorczyk’s hierarchy.

The proof of Theorem
8
is a straightforward modification of Shelah’s proof
[
7
]
of the Hales–Jewett Theorem. For the sake of completeness we include its proof in an appendix.
Theorem
8
has the following easy consequence which is useful for us below.

Corollary 9
.

Let
k,\kappa,m,r
be positive integers with
\kappa\leqslant m
. Then there exists a positive integer
q
with the following property. For every integer
N
with
N\geqslant mq
, every alphabet finite
\Lambda
with at most
k
elements, every collection
(G_{j})_{j=0}^{\kappa-1}
of non empty and pairwise disjoint subsets of
\{0,...,m-1\}
and every
r
-coloring of
\Lambda^{N}\times\prod_{j=0}^{\kappa-1}\bigcup_{i\in G_{j}}I_{i}
, where
I_{i}=\{iq,...,(i+1)q-1\}
for all
i=0,...,m-1
, there exists an
m
-dimensional variable word
w(v_{0},...,v_{m-1})
satisfying the following.

(a)

The wildcard set of
v_{i}
in
w
is contained in
I_{i}
for all
i=0,...,m-1
.

(b)

For every choice of
\alpha_{0},...,\alpha_{m-1},\alpha^{\prime}_{0},...,\alpha^{\prime}_{m-1}
in
\Lambda
and
(p_{0},...,p_{\kappa-1})
in
\prod_{i=0}^{\kappa-1}G_{i}
with
\alpha_{p_{i}}=\alpha^{\prime}_{p_{i}}
for all
i=0,...,\kappa-1
, we have that

c(w(\alpha_{0},...,\alpha_{m-1}),(\ell^{w}_{p_{i}})_{i=0}^{\kappa-1})=c(w(\alpha^{\prime}_{0},...,\alpha^{\prime}_{m-1}),(\ell^{w}_{p_{i}})_{i=0}^{\kappa-1}).

We denote the least such
q
by
\mathrm{Q}(k,\kappa,m,r)
.

Finally, the numbers
\mathrm{Q}(k,\kappa,m,r)
are upper bounded by a primitive recursive function belonging to the class
\mathcal{E}^{5}
of Grzegorczyk’s hierarchy.

As mentioned above, Corollary
9
is an easy consequence of Theorem
8
and in particular we have that
\mathrm{Q}(k,\kappa,m,r)\leqslant\mathrm{MHJ}^{*}(k,\kappa,m,r)
for every choice of
k,\kappa,m
and
r
as in the relevant statements.

3.
Skew, complete skew and vector complete skew subtrees

In this section we gather all the tree related notions and notation.

3.1.
Tree notation

Let us fix positive integers
b
and
n
. We denote by
b^{<n}
(resp.
b^{\leqslant n}
) the set of all sequences in
\{0,...,b-1\}
of length less than (resp. at most)
n
. We also denote by
\emptyset
the empty sequence, as well as, the empty set. For every element
s
in
b^{<n}
we denote by
|s|
its length, while for every
i
in
\{0,...,|s|-1\}
we denote by
s(i)
the
i
-th element of
s
and for every
i
in
\{0,...,|s|\}
we denote by
s\upharpoonright i
the initial segment of
s
of length
i
. In particular,
s=(s(0),...,s(|s|-1))
for each non empty
s
in
b^{<n}
. Moreover, for every
s,t
in
b^{<n}
, we denote by
s\wedge t
their longest common initial segment.

Let us observe that
b^{<n}
with the initial segment ordering
\sqsubseteq
is a uniquely rooted and balanced tree, that is, all maximal chains have the same length, with fixed branching number
b
, i.e. every non-maximal node has exactly
b
immediate successors.
We will also consider the lexicographical order on the set
b^{<n}
defined as follows. For every
s,t
in
b^{<n}
, we set
s\leqslant_{\mathrm{lex}}t
if either

(i)

we have that
s\sqsubseteq t
, or

(ii)

we have that
s\not\sqsubseteq t
and
t\not\sqsubseteq s
and
s(i)<t(i)
, where
i=|s\wedge t|
.

Finally, we consider one more linear order
\preccurlyeq
on
b^{<n}
defined as follows. For every
s,t
in
b^{<n}
, we set
s\preccurlyeq t
if either

(i)

|s|<|t|
, or

(ii)

|s|=|t|
and
t\leqslant_{\mathrm{lex}}s
.

A subtree
S
of
b^{<n}
is a subset of
b^{<n}
endowed with
\sqsubseteq
.
We say that a subtree
S
of
b^{<n}
is a subtree of some subtree
T
of
b^{<n}
, if
S
is a subset of
T
.
For every subtree
S
of
b^{<n}
and
s
in
S
, we define the following. The set of the predecessors of
s
inside
S
is defined as
\mathrm{Pred}_{S}(s)=\{t\in S:\;t\sqsubset s\}
and the set of the successors of
s
inside
S
is defined as
\mathrm{Succ}_{S}(s)=\{t\in S:\;s\sqsubseteq t\}
. Moreover, we consider the set of the immediate successors
\mathrm{ImSucc}_{S}(s)
of
s
in
S
to be the set of all
t
in
S
such that
s\sqsubset t
and there is no
t^{\prime}
in
S
satisfying
s\sqsubset t^{\prime}\sqsubset t
. The height of
s
in
S
, denoted by
\mathrm{h}_{S}(s)
, is the cardinality of the set
\mathrm{Pred}_{S}(s)
, while the height of
S
, denoted by
\mathrm{h}_{S}
, is the maximal length of a chain in
S
. If
m
is a non negative integer with
m<\mathrm{h}_{S}
, we define the
m
-th level of
S
, denoted by
S(m)
to be the set of all
t
in
S
such that
\mathrm{h}_{S}(t)=m
. Finally, the level set of
S
, denoted by
L(S)
, is the set
\{m<n:b^{m}\cap S\neq\emptyset\}
.

3.1.1.
Skew subtrees

A skew subtree
S
of
b^{<n}
is a subtree that satisfies the following.

(i)

S
has a minimum, called the root of
S
and denoted by
\mathrm{r}_{S}
.

(ii)

For every two nodes
s,s^{\prime}
in
S
with
\mathrm{h}_{S}(s)=\mathrm{h}_{S}(s^{\prime})
and
s\leqslant_{\mathrm{lex}}s^{\prime}
, we have that
|s|\leqslant|s^{\prime}|
.

(iii)

For every two nodes
s,s^{\prime}
in
S
with
\mathrm{h}_{S}(s)<\mathrm{h}_{S}(s^{\prime})
, we have that
|s|<|s^{\prime}|
.

(iv)

There exist
i_{S}
in
\{0,...,b-1\}
and
s_{S}
in
S
satisfying the following.

(a)

For every
s^{\prime}
in
S
with
s^{\prime}\preccurlyeq s_{S}
and
s^{\prime}\neq s_{S}
, we have that for every
i
in
\{0,...,b-1\}
, there is unique
t
in
\mathrm{ImSucc}_{S}(s^{\prime})
such that
s^{\prime\smallfrown}(i)\sqsubseteq t
. Thus, in particular, the set
\mathrm{ImSucc}_{S}(s^{\prime})
is of cardinality
b
.

(b)

For every
s^{\prime}
in
S
with
s_{S}\preccurlyeq s^{\prime}
and
s^{\prime}\neq s_{S}
, we have that the set
\mathrm{ImSucc}_{S}(s^{\prime})
is empty

(c)

The set
\mathrm{ImSucc}_{S}(s_{S})
is of cardinality
i_{S}+1
and for every

i
in
\{0,...,i_{s}\}
, there is unique
t
in
\mathrm{ImSucc}_{S}(s_{S})
such that
s_{S}^{\smallfrown}(i)\sqsubseteq t
.

Let us observe that by the condition (iv) of the definition of a skew subtree, we have that every skew subtree has at least two nodes. We will also view singletons as skew subtrees.

3.1.2.
Complete skew subtrees

We will say that a skew subtree of
b^{<n}
is
k
-complete, where
k
is a positive integer, if every maximal chain of itself is of length
k
and every non maximal node has exactly
b
immediate successors in the subtree. Moreover, we call a skew subtree complete, if it is
k
-complete for some
k
.
Let us also observe that if
S
is a
k
-complete skew subtree, then
\mathrm{h}_{S}=k
and there exists unique isomorphism
\mathrm{I}_{S}:b^{<k}\to S
, that is,
\mathrm{I}_{S}
is bijective and preserves
\sqsubseteq,\leqslant_{\mathrm{lex}}
and
\preccurlyeq
.

3.2.
Vector tree notation

Let us fix positive integers
b,n,d
. We define
\mathbf{T}_{b,n}^{d}
to be the

-tuple having each component equal to

. We view
\mathbf{T}_{b,n}^{d}
as a vector tree. A vector subtree of
\mathbf{T}_{b,n}^{d}
is a
d
-tuple such that each component is a subtree of
b^{<n}
. For
\mathbf{S}_{1},\mathbf{S}_{2}
vector subtrees of
\mathbf{T}_{b,n}^{d}
, we say that
\mathbf{S}_{1}
is a vector subtree of
\mathbf{S}_{2}
, if every component of
\mathbf{S}_{1}
is a subtree of the corresponding component of
\mathbf{S}_{2}
.

3.2.1.
Vector skew subtrees

Let
k
be a positive integer. A vector subtree
\mathbf{S}=(S_{1},...,S_{d})
of
\mathbf{T}_{b,n}^{d}
is called a vector skew subtree of
\mathbf{T}_{b,n}^{d}
of height

, if each

is a skew subtree of
b^{<n}
and the following are satisfied.

(i)

There exists
j_{0}
in
[d]
such that
S_{i}
is
k
-complete skew subtree of
b^{<n}
for all
i
in
[d]
with

and
S_{i}
is
(k-1)
-complete skew subtree of

for all
i
in
[d]
with

.
Moreover,

is of height

.

(ii)

For every triple of integers
i,j,m
with

and

, we have that
|s_{i}|\leqslant|s_{j}|
for each
s_{i},s_{j}
satisfying
s_{i}\in S_{i}(m)
and
s_{j}\in S_{j}(m)
.

(iii)

For every integer

with

, we have that
|s|<|s^{\prime}|
for each

satisfying

and

.

3.2.2.
Vector complete skew subtrees

A vector skew subtree
\mathbf{S}=(S_{1},...,S_{d})
of
\mathbf{T}_{b,n}^{d}
is called vector
k
-complete if
each
S_{i}
is a
k
-complete skew subtree of

.
A vector subtree is called vector complete skew subtree of
\mathbf{T}_{b,n}^{d}
,
if it is a vector
k
-complete skew subtree of
\mathbf{T}_{b,n}^{d}
for some positive integer

.
If
\mathbf{S}=(S_{1},...,S_{d})
is either a vector strong subtree or a vector skew subtree of
\mathbf{T}^{d}_{b,n}
, then we set
\mathrm{h}_{\mathbf{S}}=\mathrm{h}_{S_{1}}
. Finally, for every positive integer

and every
\mathbf{S}
vector complete skew subtree of

Remark 1
.

An important property of the vector complete skew subtrees is the following. Fix positive integers

and pick a non-maximal node

of

. Let

and
(t_{i})_{i=1}^{d}
the

-minimal elements of

-increasing order. Also let

Actually, one can obtain primitive recursive bounds for the numbers

]
.

4.
A Hales-Jewett type theorem for mixed products of words indexed by trees.

4.1.
Spaces of Word and subspaces

Let
\Lambda
be a finite alphabet. We consider three different types of spaces. Let
b,n
be positive integers. We set
\mathrm{W}(b,n,\Lambda)=\Lambda^{(b^{<n})}
, that is the set of all maps from
b^{<n}
into
\Lambda
. We also set

\mathrm{W}^{\uparrow}(b,n,\Lambda)=\mathrm{W}(b,n,\Lambda)\times b^{n}\;\text{and}\;\mathrm{W}^{\bullet}(b,n,\Lambda)=\mathrm{W}(b,n,\Lambda)\times b^{<n}.

In order to define variable words we reserve for every
s
in
b^{<n}
a distinct symbol
v_{s}
not belonging to
\Lambda
that we view as a variable.
Let
k
be a positive integer and
T
a
k
-complete skew subtree of
b^{<n}
. A
T
-variable word
f
on
\mathrm{W}(b,n,\Lambda)
is a map from
b^{<n}
into
\Lambda\cup\{v_{t}:t\in T\}
such that for every
t
in
T
the set
f^{-1}(v_{t})
is non empty and has
t
as a
\sqsubseteq
-minimum. We denote by
\mathrm{W}_{v,T}(b,n,\Lambda)
the set of all
T
-variable words on
\mathrm{W}(b,n,\Lambda)
.
For a positive integer
k
, we denote by
\mathrm{W}_{v,k}(b,n,\Lambda)

the union of
\mathrm{W}_{v,T}(b,n,\Lambda)
over all possible
k
-complete skew subtrees
T
of
b^{<n}
. Finally, we denote by
\mathrm{W}_{v}(b,n,\Lambda)
the union of
\mathrm{W}_{v,k}(b,n,\Lambda)
over all positive integers
k
with
k\leqslant n
.
We view the elements of
\mathrm{W}_{v}(b,n,\Lambda)
as variable words.
Observe that for every
f
in
\mathrm{W}_{v}(b,n,\Lambda)
, there exists unique
k
, which we denote by
\mathrm{h}(f)
, and unique
k
-complete skew subtree
T
of
b^{<n}
, which we denote by
\mathrm{ws}(f)
such that
f
is a
T
-variable word.

As usual, for variable words we consider substitutions. Let
f
in
\mathrm{W}_{v}(b,n,\Lambda)
.
If
(\alpha_{t})_{t\in\mathrm{ws}(f)}\in\Lambda^{\mathrm{ws}(f)}
, then we denote by
f\big((\alpha_{t})_{t\in\mathrm{ws}(f)}\big)
the constant word resulting by substituting each occurrence of
v_{t}
in
f
by
\alpha_{t}
for all
t
in
\mathrm{ws}(f)
.
Moreover, we define the span of
f
as

[f]_{\Lambda}=\{f\big((\alpha_{t})_{t\in\mathrm{ws}(f)}\big):(\alpha_{t})_{t\in\mathrm{ws}(f)}\in\Lambda^{\mathrm{ws}(f)}\}.

We view the span of a variable word as a combinatorial subspace of
\mathrm{W}(b,n,\Lambda)
.

The subspaces of
\mathrm{W}^{\uparrow}(b,n,\Lambda)
are described by pairs of the form
(f,X)
, where
f
belongs to
\mathrm{W}_{v}(b,n,\Lambda)
and
X
is a subset of
b^{n}
of cardinality
b^{\mathrm{h}(f)}
, such that
\mathrm{ws}(f)\cup X
is a skew subtree of
b^{\leqslant n}
. We denote the set of all such pairs as
W_{v}^{\uparrow}(b,n,\Lambda)
. For
(f,X)
in
W_{v}^{\uparrow}(b,n,\Lambda)
, we define its span as

[(f,X)]_{\Lambda}^{\uparrow}=[f]_{\Lambda}\times X.

The subspaces of
\mathrm{W}^{\bullet}(b,n,\Lambda)
are described again by variable words. In particular, if
f
belongs to
\mathrm{W}_{v}(b,n,\Lambda)
, then we define

[f]_{\Lambda}^{\bullet}=[f]_{\Lambda}\times\mathrm{ws}(f).

Actually, we deal with mixed products of the above spaces. More precisely, let us also fix some positive integer
d
and a partition
\mathbf{D}=(D_{0},D_{1},D_{2})
of
[d]
. We define

\mathrm{W}^{d}(b,n,\Lambda)=\prod_{i\in[d]}\mathrm{W}(b,n,\Lambda)

and

\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{D}}(b,n,\Lambda)=\Big(\prod_{i\in D_{0}}\mathrm{W}(b,n,\Lambda)\Big)\times\Big(\prod_{i\in D_{1}}\mathrm{W}^{\uparrow}(b,n,\Lambda)\Big)\times\Big(\prod_{i\in D_{2}}\mathrm{W}^{\bullet}(b,n,\Lambda)\Big).

Observe that the set
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{D}}(b,n,\Lambda)
can naturally be identified with the Cartesian product

\mathrm{W}^{d}(b,n,\Lambda)\times(b^{n})^{D_{1}}\times(b^{<n})^{D_{2}}
. More precisely this identification is achieved by corresponding to
each element
((w_{i})_{i\in D_{0}},(w_{i},x_{i})_{i\in D_{1}},(w_{i},t_{i})_{i\in D_{2}})
of the set
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{D}}(b,n,\Lambda)
the
triple
((w_{i})_{i\in[d]},(x_{i})_{i\in D_{1}},(t_{i})_{i\in D_{2}})
. We keep in mind this identification and when we refer to
element of
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{D}}(b,n,\Lambda)
, we describe them as triples of the form

((w_{i})_{i\in[d]},(x_{i})_{i\in D_{1}},(t_{i})_{i\in D_{2}}),

where
w_{i}
belongs to
\mathrm{W}(b,n,\Lambda)
for each
i
in
[d]
,
x_{i}
belongs to
b^{n}
for each
i
in
D_{1}
and

t_{i}
belongs to
b^{<n}
for each
i
in
D_{2}
.

A variable word of
\mathrm{W}^{d}(b,n,\Lambda)
is a
d
-tuple
(f_{i})_{i=1}^{d}
of variable words, i.e. of elements of
\mathrm{W}_{v}(b,n,\Lambda)
, such that
(\mathrm{ws}(f_{i}))_{i=1}^{d}
forms a vector complete skew subtree of
\mathbf{T}_{b,n}^{d}
. The span of a variable word
(f_{i})_{i=1}^{d}
of
\mathrm{W}^{d}(b,n,\Lambda)
is defined as

[(f_{i})_{i=1}^{d}]_{\Lambda}=\prod_{i\in[d]}[f_{i}]_{\Lambda}.

A variable word of
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{D}}(b,n,\Lambda)
is a pair
\big((f_{i})_{i=1}^{d},(X_{i})_{i\in D_{1}}\big)
such that
(f_{i})_{i=1}^{d}
is a variable word of
\mathrm{W}^{d}(b,n,\Lambda)
and
(f_{i},X_{i})
belongs to
\mathrm{W}_{v}^{\uparrow}(b,n,\Lambda)
for all
i
in
D_{1}
. Let us denote the set of all such pairs by
\mathrm{W}_{v}^{\mathbf{D}}(b,n,\Lambda)
. Finally, for
\big((f_{i})_{i=1}^{d},(X_{i})_{i\in D_{1}}\big)
in
\mathrm{W}_{v}^{\mathbf{D}}(b,n,\Lambda)
, we define its span as

\big[\big((f_{i})_{i=1}^{d},(X_{i})_{i\in D_{1}}\big)\big]_{\Lambda}=\Big(\prod_{i\in D_{0}}[f_{i}]_{\Lambda}\Big)\times\Big(\prod_{i\in D_{1}}[(f_{i},X_{i})]^{\uparrow}_{\Lambda}\Big)\times\Big(\prod_{i\in D_{2}}[f_{i}]^{\bullet}_{\Lambda}\Big)

and again when we refer to elements of
\big[\big((f_{i})_{i=1}^{d},(X_{i})_{i\in D_{1}}\big)\big]_{\Lambda}
, we describe them as triples of the form

((w_{i})_{i\in[d]},(x_{i})_{i\in D_{1}},(t_{i})_{i\in D_{2}}),

where
w_{i}
belongs to
[f_{i}]_{\Lambda}
for each
i
in
[d]
,
x_{i}
belongs to
X_{i}
for each
i
in
D_{1}
and

t_{i}
belongs to
\mathrm{ws}(f_{i})
for each
i
in
D_{2}
.
We also view such spans as combinatorial subspaces of
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{D}}(b,n,\Lambda)
.
We say that
\big[\big((f_{i})_{i=1}^{d},(X_{i})_{i\in D_{1}}\big)\big]_{\Lambda}
is the combinatorial subspace generated by

\big((f_{i})_{i=1}^{d},(X_{i})_{i\in D_{1}}\big)
and its dimension is
\mathrm{h}(f_{1})
.

4.2.
Hales–Jewett for mixed products

The main result of this section is Theorem
13
below.
Roughly speaking, the theorem states that given any arbitrary finite coloring of
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{D}}(b,n,\Lambda)
,
there exists a combinatorial subspace of
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{D}}(b,n,\Lambda)
such that the restriction of the color on
the subspace becomes “canonical”. The next definition describes the appropriate notion of “canonical”
color required in our case.

Definition 11
.

Let
d,b,n
be positive integers, let
\mathbf{D}=(D_{0},D_{1},D_{2})
be a partition of
[d]
and let

be a finite alphabet.
Also, let

We say that the combinatorial subspace generated by
((f_{i})_{i\in[d]},(X_{i})_{i\in D_{1}})
is

-good, if
for every choice of elements
((w_{i})_{i\in[d]},(x_{i})_{i\in D_{1}},(t_{i})_{i\in D_{2}})
and
((w^{\prime}_{i})_{i\in[d]},(x^{\prime}_{i})_{i\in D_{1}},(t^{\prime}_{i})_{i\in D_{2}})

be positive integers. We say that

with the following property. For every finite alphabet

, every partition

of

and every

-coloring

Theorem 13

For every choice of non negative integers

with
