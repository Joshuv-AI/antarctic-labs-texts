On a new method for the stochastic perturbation of the disease transmission coefficient in SIS Models
Alberto Lanconelli, Berk Tan Perçin

Abstract
In this study we investigate a novel approach to stochastically perturb the disease transmission coefficient, which is a key parameter in susceptible-infected-susceptible (SIS) models. Motivated by the papers [ 5 ] and [ 2 ] , we perturb the disease transmission coefficient with a Gaussian white noise, formally modelled as the time derivative of a mean reverting Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process. We remark that, thanks to a suitable representation of the solution to the deterministic SIS model, this perturbation is rigorous and supported by a Wong-Zakai approximation argument that consists in smoothing the singular Gaussian white noise and then taking limit of the solution from the approximated model. We prove that the stochastic version of the classic SIS model obtained this way preserves a crucial feature of the deterministic equation: the reproduction number dictating the two possible asymptotic regimes for the infection, i.e. extinction and persistence, remains unchanged. We then identify the class of perturbing noises for which this property holds and propose simple sufficient conditions for that. All the theoretical discoveries are illustrated and discussed with the help of several numerical simulations.



Key words and phrases: SIS epidemic model, Itô and Stratonovich stochastic differential equations, Wong-Zakai approximation, extinction, persistence.

AMS 2000 classification: 60H10, 60H30, 92D30.

Introduction

There are key biological parameters to analyse in epidemiology. The pathogen’s type, infection target, pathogen’s vulnerability to immune system, contagiousness and its life time outside of the host are certainly some of these key parameters. However, they are not enough to fully describe how an epidemic evolves. A key element, which makes epidemiology a very complex area, is the interaction inside the population and its unpredictability.

To address this feature several mathematical models have been proposed and analysed in the literature: see e.g.
[
6
,
5
,
15
,
7
]
and the references quoted there. In the classic susceptible-infected-susceptible (SIS) model there is no long term immunity for the infection. One example of such case is Gonorrhea, as described in
[
6
]
, where individuals who are recovered from the infection become infected again and again. This means that long term immunity is not effective in prevention from re-infection.

From a mathematical point of view, the SIS model (see e.g.
[
3
]
) is a very handy tool that describes the average evolution of an infection with no immunity. It consists of the system of ordinary differential equations

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{dS(t)}{dt}=\mu N-\beta S(t)I(t)+\gamma I(t)-\mu S(t),&S(0)=s_{0}\in]0,N[;\\
\frac{dI(t)}{dt}=\beta S(t)I(t)-(\mu+\gamma)I(t),&I(0)=i_{0}\in]0,N[,\end{cases}

(1.1)

where
S(t)
and
I(t)
denote the number of
susceptibles
and
infecteds
at time
t
, respectively. Here,
N:=s_{0}+i_{0}
is the initial size of the population amongst whom the disease is spreading,
\mu
denotes the per capita death rate,
\gamma
is the rate at which infected individuals become cured and
\beta
stands for the disease transmission coefficient. If we sum the equations in (
1.1
), we get that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}(S(t)+I(t))=\mu(N-(S(t)+I(t))),\quad S(0)+I(0)=N,

which yields

\displaystyle S(t)+I(t)=S(0)+I(0)=N,\quad\mbox{ for all $t\geq 0$}.

Therefore, system (
1.1
) can be reduced to the differential equation

\displaystyle\frac{dI(t)}{dt}=\beta I(t)(N-I(t))-(\mu+\gamma)I(t),\quad I(0)=i_{0}\in]0,N[,

(1.2)

with
S(t):=N-I(t)
. Furthermore, equation (
1.2
) can be solved explicitly as

\displaystyle I(t)=\frac{i_{0}e^{[\beta N-(\mu+\gamma)]t}}{1+\beta\int_{0}^{t}i_{0}e^{[\beta N-(\mu+\gamma)]s}ds},\quad t\geq 0.

(1.3)

This explicit representation easily identifies two different asymptotic regimes for the solution
I(t)
, namely

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to+\infty}I(t)=\begin{cases}0,&\mbox{ if $R^{D}_{0}\leq 1$};\\
N\left(1-\frac{1}{R_{0}^{D}}\right),&\mbox{ if $R^{D}_{0}>1$},\end{cases}

(1.4)

where

\displaystyle R^{D}_{0}:=\frac{\beta N}{\mu+\gamma}.

This ratio is known as
basic reproduction number
of the infection and determines whether the disease will become extinct, i.e.
I(t)
will tend to zero as
t
goes to infinity, or will be persistent, i.e.
I(t)
will tend to a positive limit as
t
increases.

As seen from (
1.3
) and identity
S(t)=N-I(t)
, the infected and susceptible populations will have
smooth
flows to each other with rates specified by the model. This description is very good at yielding overall results for a population but it fails to capture its heterogeneity, hence realism. In reality, individuals have different recovering rates or get infected at different rates, thus creating a distortion in the smooth flow between the two populations. To describe this randomness, several approaches have been proposed in the literature.

One example is the approach of Allen
[
1
]
: here one starts with a discrete Markov chain whose transition probabilities reflect the dynamical behaviour of the deterministic model; then, via a suitable scaling on the one-step transition probability, one obtains a forward Fokker-Planck equation which is canonically associated with a stochastic differential equation.

Another common method for introducing stochasticity is the so-called
parameter perturbation
approach
[
5
,
14
,
4
]
: it amounts at perturbing one of the parameters of the model equation with a suitable source of randomness, usually a Gaussian white noise. One of the most representative papers in this direction is
[
5
]
: here the authors formally perturb equation (
1.2
), rewritten in the form

\displaystyle dI(t)=\beta I(t)(N-I(t))dt-(\mu+\gamma)I(t)dt,\quad I(0)=i_{0}\in]0,N[,

through the replacement

\displaystyle\beta dt\mapsto\beta dt+\sigma dB_{t}

(1.5)

with
\{B_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
being a standard one dimensional Brownian motion and
\sigma
an additional parameter of the model. Since
\beta
is a parameter for disease transmission rate, the term
\beta dt
can be interpreted as number of transmissions in time interval
[t,t+dt]
, as stated in
[
5
]
. This way, the authors propose the model

\displaystyle dI(t)=[\beta I(t)(N-I(t))-(\mu+\gamma)I(t)]dt+\sigma I(t)(N-I(t))dB(t),

(1.6)

interpreted as an Itô-type stochastic differential equation, which will encapsulate the randomness in the disease transmission; moreover, they identify a
stochastic reproduction number

\displaystyle R_{0}^{S}:=R^{D}_{0}-\frac{\sigma^{2}N^{2}}{2(\mu+\gamma)},

which, in contrast to (
1.4
), characterizes the following asymptotic behaviours:

•

if
R_{0}^{S}<1
and
\sigma^{2}<\frac{\beta}{N}
or if
\sigma^{2}>\max\{\frac{\beta}{N},\frac{\beta^{2}}{2(\mu+\gamma)}\}
, then the
disease will become extinct
, i.e.

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to+\infty}I(t)=0;

•

if
R_{0}^{S}>1
, then
the disease will be persistent
, i.e.

\displaystyle\liminf_{t\to+\infty}I(t)\leq\xi\leq\limsup_{t\to+\infty}I(t),

where
\xi:=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\left(\sqrt{\beta^{2}-2\sigma^{2}(\mu+\gamma)}-(\beta-\sigma^{2}N)\right)
.

(see also
[
14
]
). It is worth mentioning that going from (
1.2
) to (
1.6
), as described in
[
5
]
, one has to accept some reasonable but heuristic manipulations of the infinitesimal quantities
dt
and
dB_{t}
.

The aim of this paper is to propose a different method for perturbing the disease transmission rate in the SIS model (
1.2
). Our idea stems from the following simple observation: if we let
\beta
in (
1.2
) to be a function of time, then the solution formula (
1.3
) takes the form

\displaystyle I(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{i_{0}e^{N\int_{0}^{t}\beta(s)ds-(\mu+\gamma)t}}{1+i_{0}\int_{0}^{t}\beta(s)e^{N\int_{0}^{s}\beta(r)dr-(\mu+\gamma)s}ds}

\displaystyle=\frac{i_{0}e^{N\int_{0}^{t}\beta(s)ds-(\mu+\gamma)t}}{1+\frac{i_{0}}{N}\left(e^{N\int_{0}^{t}\beta(r)dr-(\mu+\gamma)t}-1+\int_{0}^{t}e^{N\int\limits_{0}^{s}\beta(r)dr-(\gamma+\mu)s}(\gamma+\mu)ds\right)},

(1.7)

where in the second equality we performed an integration by parts in the denominator.
Equation (
1
) now depends on the function
\beta(t)
only through its integral
\int_{0}^{t}\beta(s)ds
. In
[
2
]
the authors utilized this approach to mimic the perturbation proposed in
[
5
]
; in this case, the
singular
perturbation

\displaystyle\beta(t)\mapsto\beta(t)+\sigma\frac{dB_{t}}{dt},

formally employed on the differential equation (
1.2
) by the authors in
[
5
]
, becomes the well defined transformation

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\beta(s)ds\mapsto\int_{0}^{t}\beta(s)ds+\sigma B_{t},

if directly applied on the explicit solution (
1
). As shown in
[
2
]
, this different procedure of parameter perturbation results in an alternative stochastic SIS model which surprisingly exhibits the same asymptotic regimes of its deterministic counterpart (
1.2
). It is important to remark that this new parameter perturbation approach, which directly acts on the explicit solution (
1
), is also justified via Wong-Zakai theorem at the level of differential equations, thus ruling out the necessity of having a closed form expression for the solution.

In the current paper we employ the just mentioned approach to the case where the perturbation is modelled as a mean reverting Ornstein -Uhlenbeck process. This choice is suggested, but not investigated, both in
[
1
]
and
[
5
]
. From a modelling point of view it is motivated by the fact that the variance of a mean reverting Ornstein -Uhlenbeck process is bounded in time, while the one of a Brownian motion, utilized in
[
5
]
, is not. This feature seems to be more realistic and hence desirable. However, from a mathematical point of view, the perturbation with a mean reverting Ornstein -Uhlenbeck process makes the analysis of the model more demanding since in this case equation (
1.6
) becomes a stochastic differential equation with random coefficients.

This problem is discussed in
[
4
]
with an approach that follows
[
5
]
. Here, we introduce the model working directly on the explicit representation (
1
) and cross-validate the proposal from a differential equations’ perspective passing through the Wong-Zakai theorem. We prove that our model fulfils some basic biological constraints, i.e. the solution is global and lives in the interval
]0,N[
with probability one. Then, we analyse the asymptotic behaviour and discover that the threshold for the different regimes coincides with the one for the deterministic SIS model; in other words, the parameters describing the mean reverting Ornstein -Uhlenbeck process do not play any role in the limiting behaviour of the solution. We also identify a class of perturbations for which this invariance is preserved thus offering a complete analysis of our approach.

The paper is organized as follows: in Section 2 we introduce the model and its cross-validation via the Wong-Zakai theorem; Section 3 is devoted to the analysis of our model: support of the solution, extinction, persistence and discussion of several numerical simulations. Lastly, in Section 4 we address the problem of finding a general class of perturbations for which the results from Section 3 remain the same; numerical simulations are also presented for this enlarged framework.

2
Stochastic parameter perturbation with a mean reverting Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process

Let
\{Y_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
be a mean reverting Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process driven by a standard one dimensional Brownian motion
\{B_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
; this means that
\{Y_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
is the unique strong solution of the stochastic differential equation

\displaystyle dY_{t}=-\alpha Y_{t}dt+\sigma dB_{t},\quad Y_{0}=0

(2.1)

where the parameters
\alpha
and
\sigma
are positive real number. the process
\{Y_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
can be explicitly represented as

\displaystyle Y_{t}=\sigma\int\limits_{0}^{t}e^{-\alpha(t-s)}dB_{s},\quad t\geq 0,

(2.2)

entailing that
Y_{t}
is a Gaussian random variable with mean zero and variance
\frac{1-e^{-2\alpha t}}{2\alpha}\sigma^{2}
. We also recall the ergodic property of
\{Y_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
:

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to+\infty}\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}Y_{s}ds=0\quad\mbox{almost surely}.

(2.3)

We now perturb (
1
) via the substitution

\displaystyle\beta(t)\mapsto\beta+\frac{dY_{t}}{dt}

(2.4)

or more rigorously

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\beta(s)ds\mapsto\int_{0}^{t}\left(\beta+\frac{dY_{s}}{ds}\right)ds=\beta t+Y_{t}.

(2.5)

This gives

\displaystyle\mathtt{I}_{t}:=\frac{i_{0}e^{\nu t+NY_{t}}}{1+\frac{i_{0}}{N}\left(e^{\nu t+NY_{t}}-1+\int_{0}^{t}e^{\nu s+NY_{s}}(\gamma+\mu)ds\right)},\quad t\geq 0,

(2.6)

where to ease the notation we set
\nu:=N\beta-(\gamma+\mu)
and
\mathtt{I}_{t}
instead of
I(t,Y_{t})
; note that
R_{0}^{D}\leq 1
is equivalent to
\nu\leq 0
. The stochastic process (
2.6
) is the object of our investigation. Observe that an application of the Itô formula gives

\displaystyle d\mathtt{I}_{t}=

\displaystyle\left[\mathtt{I}_{t}(N-\mathtt{I}_{t})\left(\frac{\nu}{N}-\alpha Y_{t}+\frac{N-2\mathtt{I}_{t}}{2}\sigma^{2}\right)-\frac{\gamma+\mu}{N}\mathtt{I}_{t}^{2}\right]dt+\sigma\mathtt{I}_{t}(N-\mathtt{I}_{t})dB_{t}.

(2.7)

This equation can be considered either as a one dimensional stochastic differential equation with random coefficients (for the presence of
\{Y_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
) or, if coupled with (
2.1
), as a two dimensional system of stochastic differential equations. The local Lipschitz continuity of the coefficients of such system entails path-wise uniqueness and hence that the couple
\{(\mathtt{I}_{t},Y_{t})\}_{t\geq 0}
, with
\mathtt{I}_{t}
defined in (
2.6
) and
Y_{t}
defined in (
2.2
), is its unique solution (see e.g. Theorem 2.5, Chapter 5 in
[
8
]
).

2.1
Cross-validation of the model via Wong-Zakai theorem

We obtained the stochastic process (
2.6
) perturbing the explicit solution (
1
) with the transformation (
2.5
). One can however derive the stochastic differential equation (
2.7
), which is uniquely solved by (
2.6
), through a parameter perturbation procedure acting on the deterministic equation (
1.2
), which resembles the approach employed in
[
5
]
.

Let
\{B^{\pi}_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
be the polygonal approximation of the Brownian motion
\{B_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
, relative to the partition
\pi
. This means that
\{B^{\pi}_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
is a continuous piecewise linear random function converging to
\{B_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
almost surely and uniformly on
[0,T]
, as the mesh of the partition tends to zero. Now, replace (
2.1
) with

\displaystyle\frac{dY^{\pi}_{t}}{dt}=-\alpha Y^{\pi}_{t}+\sigma\frac{dB^{\pi}_{t}}{dt},\quad Y_{0}^{\pi}=0,

(2.8)

which gives a smooth approximation of
\{Y_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
. Using
\{Y^{\pi}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
instead of
\{Y_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
allows for a rigorous implementation of the transformation (
2.4
) in (
1.2
), that means

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathtt{I}_{t}^{\pi}}{dt}=

\displaystyle\beta\mathtt{I}_{t}^{\pi}(N-\mathtt{I}_{t}^{\pi})-(\mu+\gamma)\mathtt{I}_{t}^{\pi}+\mathtt{I}_{t}^{\pi}(N-\mathtt{I}_{t}^{\pi})\frac{dY^{\pi}_{t}}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta\mathtt{I}_{t}^{\pi}(N-\mathtt{I}_{t}^{\pi})-(\mu+\gamma)\mathtt{I}_{t}^{\pi}-\alpha\mathtt{I}_{t}^{\pi}(N-\mathtt{I}_{t}^{\pi})Y^{\pi}_{t}

\displaystyle+\sigma\mathtt{I}_{t}^{\pi}(N-\mathtt{I}_{t}^{\pi})\frac{dB^{\pi}_{t}}{dt}.

(2.9)

According to the Wong-Zakai Theorem
[
13
]
,
[
11
]

the unique solution
\{\mathtt{I}_{t}^{\pi}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
of the random ordinary differential equation (
2.1
) converges, as the mesh of the partition
\pi
tends to zero, to the solution of the Stratonovich stochastic differential equation

\displaystyle d\mathtt{I}_{t}=

\displaystyle\left[\beta\mathtt{I}_{t}(N-\mathtt{I}_{t})-(\mu+\gamma)\mathtt{I}_{t}-\alpha\mathtt{I}_{t}(N-\mathtt{I}_{t})Y_{t}\right]dt+\sigma\mathtt{I}_{t}(N-\mathtt{I}_{t})\circ dB_{t},

which in turn is equivalent to the Itô SDE

\displaystyle d\mathtt{I}_{t}=

\displaystyle\left[\beta\mathtt{I}_{t}(N-\mathtt{I}_{t})-(\mu+\gamma)\mathtt{I}_{t}-\alpha\mathtt{I}_{t}(N-\mathtt{I}_{t})Y_{t}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\mathtt{I}(t)(N-\mathtt{I}(t))(N-2\mathtt{I}(t))\right]dt

\displaystyle+\sigma\mathtt{I}_{t}(N-\mathtt{I}_{t})dB_{t}.

The stochastic differential equation above coincides with (
2.7
) thus validating our parameter perturbation approach also from a model equation pont of view.

3
Analysis of the stochastically perturbed SIS Model

In this section we analyse the stochastic process (
2.6
) which we recall to be the unique strong solution of the SDE (
2.7
). We will in particular show that such process lives in the interval
]0,N[
, for all
t\geq 0
, almost surely and we will provide sufficient conditions for extinction and persistence.

3.1
Support of the solution

We start with the following.

Proposition 3.1
.

For the stochastic process
\{\mathtt{I}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
defined in (
2.6
) we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathtt{I}_{t}\in]0,N[)=1,\quad\mbox{for all $t\geq 0$}.

Proof.

First of all, we observe that
\mathtt{I}_{t}
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\mathtt{I}_{t}=\frac{Ni_{0}e^{\nu t+NY_{t}}}{N-i_{0}+i_{0}e^{\nu t+NY_{t}}+i_{0}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\nu s+NY_{s}}(\gamma+\mu)ds}.

Since by assumption
0<i_{0}<N
, we see that
\mathtt{I}_{t}
is a ratio of almost sure positive quantities; this yields
\mathtt{I}_{t}>0
for all
t\geq 0
almost surely. On the other hand, the last identity also gives

\displaystyle\mathtt{I}_{t}

\displaystyle<\frac{Ni_{0}e^{\nu t+NY_{t}}}{i_{0}e^{\nu t+NY_{t}}+i_{0}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\nu s+NY_{s}}(\gamma+\mu)ds}

\displaystyle=\frac{N}{1+(\gamma+\mu)\int\limits_{0}^{t}e^{\nu(s-t)+N(Y_{s}-Y_{t})}ds}

\displaystyle<N.

The proof is complete.
∎

3.2
Extinction of the infection

We now provide a sufficient condition for extinction; remarkably, the parameters describing the stochastic perturbation, i.e.
\alpha
and
\sigma
, do not play role in that.

Theorem 3.2
.

If
R^{D}_{0}=\frac{\beta N}{\gamma+\mu}\leq 1
, or equivalently
\nu=\beta N-(\gamma+\nu)\leq 0
, then

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathtt{I}_{t}=0\quad\mbox{ almost surely}.

Proof.

We take
G(x):=\ln\left(\frac{x}{N-x}\right)
for
x\in]0,N[
and observe that
G
is a strictly increasing function that maps the interval
]0,N[
into
]-\infty,+\infty[
. An application of the Itô formula gives

\displaystyle dG(\mathtt{I}_{t})=\left[\nu-N\alpha Y_{t}-(\gamma+\mu)\frac{\mathtt{I}_{t}}{N-\mathtt{I}_{t}}\right]dt+\sigma NdB_{t},

which corresponds to the integral equation

\displaystyle G(\mathtt{I}_{t})=\ln\left(\frac{i_{0}}{N-i_{0}}\right)-\alpha N\int\limits_{0}^{t}Y_{s}ds+\int\limits_{0}^{t}f(\mathtt{I}_{s})ds+\sigma NB_{t},

(3.1)

where

\displaystyle f(x):=\nu-(\gamma+\mu)\frac{x}{N-x},\quad\mbox{ for $x\in]0,N[$}.

(3.2)

It is useful to note that
f
is monotone decreasing on the interval
]0,N[
and that
f(x)<\nu
, for
x\in]0,N[
. Therefore, from equation (
3.1
) we get

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}G(\mathtt{I}_{t})\leq

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\ln\left(\frac{i_{0}}{N-i_{0}}\right)-\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\alpha N}{t}\int\limits_{0}^{t}Y_{s}ds

\displaystyle+\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\int\limits_{0}^{t}f(\mathtt{I}_{s})ds+\sigma N\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{B_{t}}{t}.

(3.3)

By recalling (
2.3
), it is easy to notice that the first two terms in right hand side above are equal to zero. Moreover, by the strong law of large numbers for martingales (see for instance
[
10
]
) we also have

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{B_{t}}{t}=0,\quad\mbox{almost surely}.

Therefore, inequality (
3.2
) now reads

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}G(\mathtt{I}_{t})\leq\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\int\limits_{0}^{t}f(\mathtt{I}_{s})ds<\nu,

that means

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\ln\left(\frac{\mathtt{I}_{t}}{N-\mathtt{I}_{t}}\right)<0,\quad\mbox{almost surely}.

Since the last statement implies our thesis, the proof is complete.
∎

In the stochastic SIS model obtained by this parameter perturbation method from the deterministic one, the limiting behaviour of both models are the same for
R^{D}_{0}\leq 1
.

3.3
Persistence of the Infection

We now turn to the problem of finding sufficient condition for persistence of the disease. Again, the parameters describing the stochastic perturbation, i.e.
\alpha
and
\sigma
, do not influence the threshold.

Theorem 3.3
.

If
R^{D}_{0}>1
, or equivalently
\nu>0
, then we have with probability one

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\to\infty}\mathtt{I}_{t}\geq x^{*}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\liminf_{t\to\infty}\mathtt{I}_{t}\leq x^{*},

(3.4)

where
x^{*}=N\left(1-\frac{1}{R^{D}_{0}}\right)
.

Proof.

The assumption of the theorem implies that the function in (
3.2
) has a unique root in
]0,N[
given by
x^{*}=N(1-1/R^{D}_{0})
, as shown in figure
1
.

Figure 1
:
The plot of
f(x)
from (
3.2
) with
\nu=20
,
\gamma+\mu=0.2
and
N=10

We follow the proof of Theorem 5.1 in
[
5
]
. Assume the first inequality in (
3.4
) to be false. Then, there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{1})>\varepsilon\quad\mbox{ where }\quad\Omega_{1}:=\left\{\limsup_{t\to\infty}\mathtt{I}_{t}\leq x^{*}-\varepsilon\right\}.

(3.5)

Therefore, for all
\omega\in\Omega_{1}
there exists
T(\omega)\geq 0
such that

\displaystyle\mathtt{I}_{t}\leq x^{*}-\varepsilon,\quad\mbox{ for all $t\geq T(\omega)$},

and the monotonicity of
f
yields

\displaystyle f(\mathtt{I}_{t})\geq f(x^{*}-\varepsilon)>0,\quad\mbox{ for all $t\geq T(\omega)$}.

(3.6)

Therefore, using identities (
3.1
), (
2.3
) and the strong law of large numbers for martingales, we can write for all
\omega\in\Omega_{1}
that

\displaystyle\liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}G(\mathtt{I}_{t})\geq

\displaystyle\liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\ln\left(\frac{i_{0}}{N-i_{0}}\right)-\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\alpha N}{t}\int\limits_{0}^{t}Y_{s}ds

\displaystyle+\liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\int\limits_{0}^{t}f(\mathtt{I}_{s})ds+N\sigma\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{B_{t}}{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\int\limits_{0}^{t}f(\mathtt{I}_{s})ds

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\int\limits_{0}^{T}f(\mathtt{I}_{s})ds+f(x^{*}-\varepsilon)\liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac{t-T}{t}

and hence

\displaystyle\liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\ln\left(\frac{\mathtt{I}_{t}}{N-\mathtt{I}_{t}}\right)\geq f(x^{*}-\varepsilon)>0.

This gives
\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}\mathtt{I}_{t}=N
which contradicts
(
3.5
).

To prove the second inequality in (
3.4
) we proceed as before and assume that there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{2})>\varepsilon\quad\mbox{ where }\quad\Omega_{2}=\left\{\liminf_{t\to\infty}\mathtt{I}_{t}\geq x^{*}+\varepsilon\right\}.

(3.7)

Therefore, for all
\omega\in\Omega_{2}
, there exists
T(\omega)\geq 0
such that

\displaystyle\mathtt{I}_{t}\geq x^{*}-\varepsilon,\quad\mbox{ for all $t\geq T(\omega)$}.

The monotonicity of
f
gives

\displaystyle f(\mathtt{I}_{t})\leq f(x^{*}-\varepsilon)<0,\quad\mbox{ for all $t\geq T(\omega)$},

(3.8)

and

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\ln\left(\frac{\mathtt{I}_{t}}{N-\mathtt{I}_{t}}\right)\leq\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\int\limits_{0}^{t}f(\mathtt{I}_{s})ds\leq f(x^{*}+\varepsilon)<0.

This implies
\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}\mathtt{I}_{t}=0
, contradicting (
3.7
).
∎

3.4
Trajectory simulations

In this section we present various simulations; we consider two different values of
\sigma
to emphasize that, according to our theoretical results, the limiting behaviour of the solution doesn’t depend on them.

For the first example, we set

4
Stochastic parameter perturbation with a general process

In this section we try to understand to which extent the results of the previous sections are determined by the choice of the particular perturbation
\{Y_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
in (
2.1
). To this aim we consider
\{Z_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
, solution of the stochastic differential equation

\displaystyle dZ_{t}=b(t,Z_{t})dt+\sigma dB_{t},\quad Z_{0}=0,

(4.1)

where
\sigma
is positive real number and the function
b:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}
is assumed to be globally Lipschitz continuous in
z
, uniformly in
t
; we also introduce the corresponding perturbation of the parameter
\beta
, namely

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\beta(s)ds\mapsto\int_{0}^{t}\left(\beta+\frac{dZ_{s}}{ds}\right)ds=\beta t+Z_{t}.

(4.2)

If we employ such transformation in (
1
), which is the solution of the deterministic SIS model with a time dependent transmission coefficient
\beta
, we get the stochastic process

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{t}:=\frac{i_{0}e^{\nu t+NZ_{t}}}{1+\frac{i_{0}}{N}\left(e^{\nu t+NZ_{t}}-1+\int_{0}^{t}e^{\nu s+NZ_{s}}(\gamma+\mu)ds\right)},\quad t\geq 0.

(4.3)

where to ease the notation we set
\mathcal{I}_{t}
instead of
I(t,Z_{t})
. Moreover, an application of the Itô formula yields

\displaystyle d\mathcal{I}_{t}=

\displaystyle\left[\mathcal{I}_{t}(N-\mathcal{I}_{t})\left(\frac{\nu}{N}+b(t,Z_{t})+\frac{\sigma^{2}N}{2}-\sigma^{2}\mathcal{I}_{t}\right)-\frac{\gamma+\mu}{N}\mathcal{I}_{t}^{2}\right]dt

\displaystyle+\sigma\mathcal{I}_{t}(N-\mathcal{I}_{t})dB_{t}

(4.4)

Remark 4.1
.

The restriction to constant diffusion coefficients imposed in (
4.1
) is due the necessity of cross validating the model (
4.3
) also from a differential equations’ point of view. In fact, if we smooth the process
\{Z_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
as

\displaystyle\frac{dZ^{\pi}_{t}}{dt}=b(t,Z^{\pi}_{t})+\sigma\frac{dB^{\pi}_{t}}{dt},\quad Z^{\pi}_{0}=0,

and perturb correspondingly the parameter
\beta
in equation (
1.2
), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathcal{I}_{t}^{\pi}}{dt}=

\displaystyle\beta\mathcal{I}_{t}^{\pi}(N-\mathcal{I}_{t}^{\pi})-(\mu+\gamma)\mathcal{I}_{t}^{\pi}+b(t,Z^{\pi}_{t})\mathcal{I}_{t}^{\pi}(N-\mathcal{I}_{t}^{\pi})

\displaystyle+\sigma\mathcal{I}_{t}^{\pi}(N-\mathcal{I}_{t}^{\pi})\frac{dB^{\pi}_{t}}{dt}.

According to the Wong-Zakai Theorem the unique solution
\{\mathcal{I}_{t}^{\pi}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
of the random ordinary differential equation above converges, as the mesh of the partition
\pi
tends to zero, to the solution of the Stratonovich stochastic differential equation

\displaystyle d\mathcal{I}_{t}=

\displaystyle\left[\beta\mathcal{I}_{t}(N-\mathcal{I}_{t})-(\mu+\gamma)\mathcal{I}_{t}+b(t,Z_{t})\mathcal{I}_{t}(N-\mathcal{I}_{t})\right]dt+\sigma\mathcal{I}_{t}(N-\mathcal{I}_{t})\circ dB_{t},

which in turn is equivalent to the Itô SDE

\displaystyle d\mathcal{I}_{t}=

\displaystyle\left[\beta\mathcal{I}_{t}(N-\mathcal{I}_{t})-(\mu+\gamma)\mathcal{I}_{t}+b(t,Z_{t})\mathcal{I}_{t}(N-\mathcal{I}_{t})+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\mathcal{I}(t)(N-\mathcal{I}(t))(N-2\mathcal{I}(t))\right]dt

\displaystyle+\sigma\mathcal{I}_{t}(N-\mathcal{I}_{t})dB_{t}.

This SDE coincides with (
4
) thus validating that model. If we allow
\sigma
in (
4.1
) to depend also on
Z
, then this match wouldn’t take place for the presence of an additional drift term in the equation for
\{Z_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
.

We now start to analyse the properties of
\{\mathcal{I}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
by stating the analogue of Proposition
3.1
.

Proposition 4.2
.

For the stochastic process
\{\mathcal{I}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
defined in (
4.3
) we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{I}_{t}\in]0,N[)=1,\quad\mbox{for all $t\geq 0$}.

Proof.

Looking through the proof of Proposition
3.1
one easily see that
the same conclusion holds for
\{\mathcal{I}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
.
∎

The next theorem provides a sufficient condition on the stochastic process
\{Z_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
which guarantees extinction for
\{\mathcal{I}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Assume that
R^{D}_{0}=\frac{\beta N}{\gamma+\mu}\leq 1
, or equivalently
\nu=\beta N-(\gamma+\nu)\leq 0
. If

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{Z_{t}}{t}\leq 0,\quad\mbox{ almost surely},

(4.5)

then

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathcal{I}_{t}=0\quad\mbox{ almost surely}.

Proof.

We take
G(y):=\ln\left(\frac{y}{N-y}\right)
, for
y\in]0,N[
; an application of the Itô formula gives

\displaystyle dG(\mathcal{I}_{t})=\left[\nu-(\gamma+\mu)\frac{\mathcal{I}_{t}}{N-\mathcal{I}_{t}}\right]dt+NdZ_{t}.

this yields

\displaystyle G(\mathcal{I}_{t})=\ln\left(\frac{i_{0}}{N-i_{0}}\right)+\int\limits_{0}^{t}f(\mathcal{I}_{s})ds+NZ_{t},

where
f
is defined as (
3.2
) again. By utilizing the monotonicity of
f
, we get

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}G(\mathcal{I}_{t})<\nu+N\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{Z_{t}}{t}.

Now, if

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{Z_{t}}{t}\leq 0,\quad\mbox{ almost surely},

then
\nu\leq 0
implies immediately that

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\ln\left(\frac{\mathcal{I}_{t}}{N-\mathcal{I}_{t}}\right)<0.

The last inequality entails the statement of our theorem and competes the proof.
∎

We now state the analogue of Theorem
3.3
.

Theorem 4.4
.

Assume that
R^{D}_{0}>1
, or equivalently
\nu>0
. If

\displaystyle 0\leq\liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac{Z_{t}}{t}\leq\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{Z_{t}}{t}<+\infty,

(4.6)

then the infection is persistent. More precisely, for all
x\in]0,N[
we have

\displaystyle\liminf_{t\to\infty}\mathcal{I}_{t}\leq x\leq\limsup_{t\to\infty}\mathcal{I}_{t},\quad\mbox{almost surely}.

(4.7)

Proof.

We now proceed by contradiction as in the proof of Theorem
3.3
. Similarly, we take
G(y):=\ln\left(\frac{y}{N-y}\right)
, for
y\in]0,N[
; an application of the Itô formula gives

\displaystyle dG(\mathcal{I}_{t})=\left[\nu-(\gamma+\mu)\frac{\mathcal{I}_{t}}{N-\mathcal{I}_{t}}\right]dt+NdZ_{t}.

We now fix
0<x<N
and first prove that

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\to\infty}\mathcal{I}_{t}\geq x,\quad\mbox{ almost surely}.

Assume to the contrary that the event
\left\{\limsup_{t\to\infty}\mathcal{I}_{t}<x\right\}
has positive probability. This means that there exists
T(\omega)\geq 0
such that
\mathcal{I}_{t}<x
, for all
t\geq T(\omega)
; this, together with the monotonicity of (
3.2
), gives

\displaystyle\liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\ln\left(\frac{\mathcal{I}_{t}}{N-\mathcal{I}_{t}}\right)\geq

\displaystyle\liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}f(\mathcal{I}_{s})ds+N\liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac{Z_{t}}{t}

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle f(x)+N\liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac{Z_{t}}{t}.

Now, the core of Theorem
3.3
is the left hand side above being positive and this is guaranteed if

\displaystyle N\liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac{Z_{t}}{t}\geq-f(x).

(4.8)

In this case
\liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\ln\left(\frac{\mathcal{I}_{t}}{N-\mathcal{I}_{t}}\right)>0
and hence
\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathcal{I}_{t}=N
, contradicting our initial assumption. Since the range of
f
is
]-\infty,\nu]
, condition (
4.8
) is implied by the first inequality in (
4.6
) and positivity of
\nu
.

We now prove that

\displaystyle\liminf_{t\to\infty}\mathcal{I}_{t}\leq x,\quad\mbox{almost surely}.

Assume to the contrary that the event
\left\{\liminf_{t\to\infty}\mathcal{I}_{t}>x\right\}
has positive probability; this implies the existence of
T(\omega)>0
such that
\mathcal{I}_{t}>x
, for all
t\geq T(\omega)
. Then,

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\ln\left(\frac{\mathcal{I}_{t}}{N-\mathcal{I}_{t}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{t\to\infty}\int_{0}^{t}f(\mathcal{I}_{s})ds+N\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{Z_{t}}{t}

\displaystyle<f(x)+N\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{Z_{t}}{t}.

Now, the core of Theorem
3.3
is the left hand side above being negative and this is guaranteed if

\displaystyle N\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{Z_{t}}{t}\leq-f(x)

(4.9)

In this case
\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\ln\left(\frac{\mathcal{I}_{t}}{N-\mathcal{I}_{t}}\right)<0
and hence
\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathcal{I}_{t}=0
which contradicts our initial assumption. Since the range of
f
is
]-\infty,\nu]
, condition (
4.9
) is implied by the last inequality in (
4.6
).
∎

) and (

5
Discussion

In this study we propose a new perturbation method for the disease transmission coefficient in SIS model. Our approach consists in acting directly on the explicit solution of the deterministic problem, thus avoiding delicate manipulations of differential quantities such as
dB_{t}
. Nevertheless, our model is cross validated at the level of differential equations once we smooth the perturbation and use the Wong-Zakai theorem. We first use this method with a perturbation of mean reverting type and properties of the corresponding model are analysed. Then, generalization to different sources of randomness are investigated.

When the deterministic SIS model
1.1
is perturbed with a mean reverting Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process
\{Y_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
, the solution
\{\mathtt{I}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
is shown to preserve the deterministic model’s regimes for extinction and persistence. Namely:

•

if
R^{D}_{0}\leq 1
, then we have extinction of infection;

•

if
R^{D}_{0}>1
, then we have persistence of infection.

Then, we identified some simple sufficient conditions on the class of possible perturbations which entail the same key feature for the corresponding models.
This study emphasizes that the methodology of perturbation of deterministic models is crucial in generating different stochastic versions. For further studies one can apply the same rationale to other parameters in the deterministic SIS model, i.e.
\gamma+\mu
, or to other models, such as Lotka-Volterra type of equations (
[
9
]
,
[
12
]
).
