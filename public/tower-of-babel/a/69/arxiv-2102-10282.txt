On unit-regular elements in various monoids of transformations
Mosarof Sarkar, Shubh N. Singh

Abstract
. Let X X be an arbitrary set and let T ⁡ ( X ) T(X) denote the full transformation monoid on X X . We prove that an element of T ⁡ ( X ) T(X) is unit-regular if and only if it is semi-balanced. 1.
Introduction

We assume that the reader is familiar with the basic terminology of algebraic semigroup theory. An element
x
of a monoid
M
is said to be
unit-regular
in
M
if there exists a unit
u\in M
such that
xux=x
; and monoids consisting entirely of unit-regular elements are called
unit-regular
. The concept of unit-regularity, which is a stronger form of regularity, first appeared in the context of rings
[
12
]
. There are several natural and motivating examples of such monoids. The unit-regularity in a monoid has received wide attention by a number of authors (see, e.g.,
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
15
,
16
,
19
,
20
,
32
]
).

Throughout this paper, let
X
be an arbitrary nonempty set,
\mathcal{P}=\{X_{i}|\;i\in I\}
be a partition of
X
,
E
be the equivalence relation induced by
\mathcal{P}
, and
T(X)
be the full transformation monoid on
X
. It is well-known that
T(X)
is regular (cf.
[
18
, p. 63, Exercise 2.6.15]
). It is proven in
[
7
, Proposition 5]
that
T(X)
is unit-regular if and only if
X
is finite. Pei
[
24
, Corollary 2.3]
characterized regular elements in the monoid
T(X,\mathcal{P})
, under composition, defined as

\displaystyle T(X,\mathcal{P})

\displaystyle=\{f\in T(X)\mid(\forall X_{i}\in\mathcal{P})(\exists X_{j}\in\mathcal{P})\;X_{i}f\subseteq X_{j}\}

\displaystyle=\{f\in T(X)\mid(x,y)\in E\Longrightarrow(xf,yf)\in E\}.

Pei
[
24
, Proposition 2.4]
also showed that
T(X,\mathcal{P})
is regular if and only if
\mathcal{P}
is trivial. For finite
X
, the authors
[
28
, Theorem 3.4]
characterized unit-regular elements in
T(X,\mathcal{P})
. Several interesting results of
T(X,\mathcal{P})
and its submonoids have also been investigated (see. e.g.,
[
1
,
2
,

,

,

,

In recent years, the submonoids

\displaystyle\Sigma(X,\mathcal{P})

\displaystyle=\{f\in T(X,\mathcal{P})\mid(\forall X_{i}\in\mathcal{P})\;Xf\cap X_{i}\neq\emptyset\},

\displaystyle\ \Gamma(X,\mathcal{P})

\displaystyle=\{f\in T(X,\mathcal{P})\mid(\forall X_{i}\in\mathcal{P})(\exists X_{j}\in\mathcal{P})\;X_{i}f=X_{j}\},

\displaystyle\ T_{E^{*}}(X)

\displaystyle=\{f\in T(X)\mid(\forall x,y\in X)(x,y)\in E\Longleftrightarrow(xf,yf)\in E\}

of
T(X,\mathcal{P})
have also been considered. Moreover, the regular elements in
\Gamma(X,\mathcal{P})
and
T_{E^{*}}(X)
have been characterized (cf.
[
28
, Theorem 5.5]
and
[
8
, Theorem 3.1]
, respectively). For finite

, the authors
[

, Proposition 3.5]
characterized unit-regular elements in
\Sigma(X,\mathcal{P})
and showed in
[

Let

\displaystyle\Omega(X,\mathcal{P})

\displaystyle=\{f\in T(X,\mathcal{P})\mid(\forall x,y\in X,x\neq y)(x,y)\in E\Longrightarrow xf\neq yf\}.

It is routine to verify that
\Omega(X,\mathcal{P})
is a submonoid of
T(X,\mathcal{P})
. Clearly, if
E=\{(x,x)\mid x\in X\}
, then
\Omega(X,\mathcal{P})=T(X)
; and if
E=X\times X
, then
\Omega(X,\mathcal{P})=\{f\in T(X)\mid f\mbox{ is injective}\}
.

The rest of this paper is structured as follows. In the next section, we provide definitions and notation used throughout the paper. In Section

,
\Gamma(X,\mathcal{P})

. In Section

.

2.
Preliminaries and Notation

Let
X
be a set. The cardinality of
X
is denoted by
|X|
. For
A,B\subseteq X
, we write
A\setminus B
to denote the set of elements of
A
that are not in
B
. A
partition
of
X
is a collection of nonempty disjoint subsets, called
blocks
, whose union is
X
. A partition is called
trivial
if it has only singleton blocks or a single block. We denote by
\Delta(X)
the equivalence relation
\{(x,x)\mid x\in X\}
on
X
. A
cross-section
of an equivalence relation
E
on
X
is a subset of
X
which intersects every equivalence class of
E
in exactly one point. The letter
I
will be reserved for an indexing set.

We write maps to the right of their arguments, and compose them accordingly. The composition of maps will be denoted simply by juxtaposition. Let
f\colon X\to Y
be a map. We denote by
Af
the image of a subset
A\subseteq X
under
f
. The preimage of a subset
B\subseteq Y
under
f
is denoted by
Bf^{-1}
. We write
\mbox{dom}(f)
,
\mbox{codom}(f)
, and
\mbox{im}(f)
for the domain, codomain, and image of
f
, respectively. The
defect
of
f
, denoted by
\mbox{d}(f)
, is defined by
\mbox{d}(f)=|Y\setminus\mbox{im}(f)|
. The
kernel
of
f
, denoted by
\ker(f)
, is an equivalence relation on
X
defined by
\ker(f)=\{(x,y)\in X\times X|\;xf=yf\}
. We denote by
\pi(f)
the partition of
X
induced by
\ker(f)
, namely
\pi(f)=\{yf^{-1}|\;y\in Xf\}
. Note that a map
f
is injective if and only if
\ker(f)=\Delta(X)
. We write
T_{f}
to denote any cross-section of
\ker(f)
. Note that
|T_{f}|=|\mbox{im}(f)|
. The
collapse
of
f
, denoted by
\mbox{c}(f)
, is defined by
\mbox{c}(f)=|X\setminus T_{f}|
(cf.
[
17
, p. 1356]
). If
A\subseteq X
and
B\subseteq Y
such that
Af\subseteq B
, then there is a map
g\colon A\to B
defined by
xg=xf
for all
x\in A
and, in this case, we say that the map
g

is induced by

f
.

Let
M
be a monoid with identity
e
. An element
x\in M
is called
unit
if there exists
y\in M
such that
xy=yx=e
. The set
U(M)
of all units in
M
forms a subgroup of
M
, and is called the
group of units
of
M
. An element
x\in M
is said to be
regular
in
M
if there exists
y\in M
such that
xyx=x
. Such an element
y
is called an
inner inverse
for
x
, and it need not be unique. Denote
U(x)=\{y\in U(M)\;|\;x=xyx\}
. If in addition, the inner inverse
y
is a unit, then
x
is said to be
unit-regular
in
M
. By
\mbox{reg}(M)
and
\mbox{ureg}(M)
, we mean the sets of all regular and unit-regular elements in
M
, respectively. If
\mbox{reg}(M)=M
(resp.
\mbox{ureg}(M)=M)
, we say that the monoid
M
is
regular
(resp.
unit-regular
). Note that
U(M)\subseteq\mbox{ureg}(M)\subseteq\mbox{reg}(M)
, and if
M
is unit-regular then any submonoid of
M
containing
U(M)
is also unit-regular.

Let
X
be a nonempty set. A
selfmap
on
X
is a map from
X
into
X
. A
permutation
of
X
is a bijective selfmap on
X
. We say that a selfmap
f
on
X

preserves
a partition
\mathcal{P}
of
X
if for every
X_{i}\in\mathcal{P}
, there exists
X_{j}\in\mathcal{P}
such that
X_{i}f\subseteq X_{j}
. We denote by
S(X)
the symmetric group on
X
. We write
\Omega(X)
(resp.
\Gamma(X)
) to denote the submonoids of
T(X)
consisting of all injective (resp. surjective) selfmaps on
X
. Note that the monoids
T(X)
,
\Omega(X)
, and
\Gamma(X)
have the symmetric group
S(X)
as their group of units; and
\Omega(X)=\Gamma(X)=S(X)
if and only if
X
is finite.

We refer the reader to the standard book
[
18
]
for additional information from algebraic semigroup theory.

3.
Unit-regular elements in
T(X)

In this section, we characterize the unit-regular elements in
T(X)
. We then provide an alternative proof of Proposition 5 in
[
7
]
which characterizes the unit-regularity of
T(X)
. We also characterize the regularity of
\Omega(X)
and
\Gamma(X)
. We begin by proving the following lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
f\colon X\to Y
and
g\colon Y\to X
be maps. If

fgf=f
, then
\mbox{im}(fg)
is a cross-section of the equivalence relation
\ker(f)
.

Proof.

Let
yf^{-1}\in\pi(f)
. It suffices to show that
|(Xf)g\cap yf^{-1}|=1
. Since
y\in Xf
, there exists
x\in X
such that
xf=y
. Write
yg=z
. Clearly
z=yg\in(Xf)g
. Since
fgf=f
, we obtain

zf=(yg)f=(xf)gf=xf=y.

It follows that
z\in yf^{-1}
, so
|(Xf)g\cap yf^{-1}|\geq 1
.

Moreover, suppose that
z_{1},z_{2}\in(Xf)g\cap yf^{-1}
. Then
z_{1}f=y=z_{2}f
. As
z_{1},z_{2}\in(Xf)g
, there exist
y_{1},y_{2}\in Xf
such that
y_{1}g=z_{1}
and
y_{2}g=z_{2}
. Since
y_{1},y_{2}\in Xf
, there exist
x_{1},x_{2}\in X
such that
x_{1}f=y_{1}
and
x_{2}f=y_{2}
. Since
fgf=f
, we obtain

y_{1}=x_{1}f=(x_{1}f)gf=(y_{1}g)f=z_{1}f=y.

Similarly, we obtain
y_{2}=y
. It follows that
y_{1}=y_{2}
, so
z_{1}=z_{2}
. Hence
|(Xf)g\cap yf^{-1}|=1
. This completes the proof.
∎

The following corollary is a direct consequence of Lemma
3.1
by replacing
Y
with
X
.

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
f,g\in T(X)
. If
fgf=f
, then
\mbox{im}(fg)
is a cross-section of the equivalence relation
\ker(f)
.

Before we begin to prove one of the main theorems, we recall an important definition from
[
17
]
.

Definition 3.3
.

[
17
, p. 1356]

An element
f
of
T(X)
is said to be
semi-balanced
if
\mbox{c}(f)=\mbox{d}(f)
.

The following theorem gives a characterization of unit-regular elements in
T(X)
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
f\in T(X)
. Then
f\in\ureg(T(X))
if and only if
f
is semi-balanced.

Proof.

Suppose first that
f\in\ureg(T(X))
. Then there exists
g\in S(X)
such that
fgf=f
. Therefore, by Corollary
3.2
, the set
\mbox{im}(fg)
is a cross-section of the equivalence relation
\ker(f)
. Write
\mbox{im}(fg)=T_{f}
. Since
g
is bijective, we obtain

(X\setminus Xf)g=Xg\setminus(Xf)g=X\setminus T_{f}.

It follows that
\mbox{c}(f)=|X\setminus T_{f}|=|X\setminus Xf|=\mbox{d}(f)
, so
f
is semi-balanced.

Conversely, suppose that
f
is semi-balanced. Note that
|T_{f}|=|Xf|
and
|T_{f}\cap yf^{-1}|=1
for all
yf^{-1}\in\pi(f)
. Therefore, we choose a bijection
g_{1}\colon Xf\to T_{f}
such that
yg_{1}=x
whenever
xf=y
and
x\in T_{f}
. Since
|X\setminus T_{f}|=|X\setminus Xf|
, we arbitrarily choose any bijection
g_{2}\colon X\setminus Xf\to X\setminus T_{f}
. Using these two bijections, we now define a map
g\colon X\to X
by

yg=\begin{cases}yg_{1}&\text{if $y\in Xf$},\\
yg_{2}&\text{if $y\in X\setminus Xf$}.\end{cases}

Clearly
g\in S(X)
. One can also verify in a routine manner that
fgf=f
. Hence
f\in\ureg(T(X))
.
∎

The following simple lemma will be useful in the sequel.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
f\colon X\to Y
be a map. Then

(i)

f
is injective if and only if
\mbox{c}(f)=0
;

(ii)

f
is surjective if and only if
\mbox{d}(f)=0
.

If
X
is finite, then every element of
T(X)
is semi-balanced (cf.
[
17
, p. 1356]
). However, we have the following lemma.

Lemma 3.6
.

Every element of
T(X)
is semi-balanced if and only if
X
is finite.

Proof.

Suppose that every element of
T(X)
is semi-balanced. Assume, to the contrary, that
X
is an infinite set. Choose an arbitrary element
x
of
X
. Note that the sets
X
and
X\setminus\{x\}
have the same cardinality. Therefore there exists is a bijective map
g\colon X\to X\setminus\{x\}
, so the map
g\colon X\to X
is injective but not surjective. Then we get
\mbox{c}(g)=0
and
\mbox{d}(g)\geq 1
by Lemma
3.5
. This leads to a contradiction. Hence
X
is finite.

The converse is immediate.
∎

From Lemma
3.6
and Theorem
3.4
, we have the corollary below that characterizes unit-regularity of
T(X)
.

Corollary 3.7
.

The monoid
T(X)
is unit-regular if and only if
X
is finite.

Proof.

Suppose that
T(X)
is unit-regular. Then, by Theorem
3.4
, every element of
T(X)
is semi-balanced. Hence
X
is finite by Lemma
3.6
.

Conversely, suppose that
X
is finite and let
f\in T(X)
. Then
f
is semi-balanced by Lemma
3.6
. It follows from Theorem
3.4
that
f\in\ureg(T(X))
. Since
f
is an arbitrary element, the monoid
T(X)
is unit-regular.
∎

If
X
is a finite set and
f\in T(X)
, the next proposition gives the cardinality of the set
U(f)
.

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
X
be a finite set and
f\in T(X)
. Then

|U(f)|=\mbox{d}(f)!\displaystyle\prod_{x\in Xf}|xf^{-1}|.

Proof.

Let
f\in T(X)
. Since
X
is finite, there exists
g\in S(X)
such that
fgf=f
by Corollary
3.7
. Then
\mbox{im}(fg)
is a cross-section of
\ker(f)
by Corollary
3.2
. Write
T_{f}=\mbox{im}(fg)
. Note that
|T_{f}|=|Xf|
and
T_{f}
depends on
g
. Observe that any such bijection
g\in S(X)
can be only of the following form:

yg=\begin{cases}yg_{1}&\text{if $y\in Xf$},\\
yg_{2}&\text{if $y\in X\setminus Xf$},\end{cases}

where
g_{1}\colon Xf\to T_{f}
is a bijection defined by
yg_{1}=x
whenever
xf=y
and
x\in T_{f}
; and
g_{2}\colon X\setminus Xf\to X\setminus T_{f}
is any bijection. Note that for every cross-section
T_{f}
of
\ker(f)
there is unique such bijection
g_{1}
and
\mbox{d}(f)!
number of such bijections
g_{2}
.

By the multiplication principle, the total number of possible cross-sections
T_{f}
of
\ker(f)
is
\prod_{y\in Xf}|yf^{-1}|
. Hence

|U(f)|=\mbox{d}(f)!\;\prod_{y\in Xf}|yf^{-1}|

by the multiplication principle.
∎

We now characterize regularity of
\Omega(X)
in the following proposition.

Proposition 3.9
.

The monoid
\Omega(X)
is regular if and only if
\Omega(X)=S(X)
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\Omega(X)
is regular. Since
S(X)
is the group of units of
\Omega(X)
, we have
S(X)\subseteq\Omega(X)
. For the reverse inclusion, let
f\in\Omega(X)
. Then
\mbox{c}(f)=0
by Lemma
3.5
(i). Since
f\in\reg(\Omega(X))
, there exists
g\in\Omega(X)
such that
fgf=f
. It follows from Corollary
3.2
that
\mbox{im}(fg)
is a cross-section of
\ker(f)
. Since
\mbox{c}(f)=0
and
\mbox{im}(fg)
is a cross-section of
\ker(f)
, it follows that
(Xf)g=X
and therefore
\mbox{d}(fg)=0
. Since
g
is injective, we subsequently have
\mbox{d}(f)=0
by
[
17
, Theorem 2.2(iv)]
. Hence
f
is surjective by Lemma
3.5
(ii) and consequently
f\in S(X)
.

The converse is immediate.
∎

The next proposition provides a characterization of regularity of
\Gamma(X)
.

Proposition 3.10
.

The monoid
\Gamma(X)
is regular if and only if
\Gamma(X)=S(X)
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\Gamma(X)
is regular. Since
S(X)
is the group of units of
\Gamma(X)
, we have
S(X)\subseteq\Gamma(X)
. For the reverse inclusion, let
f\in\Gamma(X)
. Since
f\in\reg(\Gamma(X))
, there exists
g\in\Gamma(X)
such that
fgf=f
. It follows from Corollary
3.2
that
\mbox{im}(fg)
is a cross-section of
\ker(f)
. Since
f,g\in\Gamma(X)
, we obtain
\mbox{im}(fg)=(Xf)g=Xg=X
. But
\mbox{im}(fg)
is a cross-section of
\ker(f)
, we therefore have
\mbox{c}(f)=0
. Hence
f
is injective by Lemma
3.5
(i) and consequently
f\in S(X)
.

The converse is immediate.
∎

The following corollary is obvious from Proposition
3.9
and
3.10
.

Corollary 3.11
.

Let
M=\Omega(X),\;\Gamma(X)
. If
M
is regular, then
M
is unit-regular.

4.
Unit-regular elements in
T(X,\mathcal{P})

In this section, we characterize unit-regular elements in
T(X,\mathcal{P})
. For finite
X
, recall that the authors
[
28
, Theorem 3.4]
characterized unit-regular elements in
T(X,\mathcal{P})
. We begin by considering an important definition corresponding to a map of
T(X,\mathcal{P})
from
[
27
]
.

Definition 4.1
.

(
[
27
, p. 220]
)
Let
\mathcal{P}=\{X_{i}|\;i\in I\}
be a partition of
X
and let
f\in T(X,\mathcal{P})
. The
character
of
f
, denoted by
\chi^{(f)}
, is a map
\chi^{(f)}\colon I\to I
defined by

i\chi^{(f)}=j\mbox{ whenever }X_{i}f\subseteq X_{j}.

In case of finite
X
, the map
\chi^{(f)}
has also been studied with the notation
\overline{f}
(see, e.g.,
[
1
,
10
,
11
]
). The authors gave a characterization of elements of
\Sigma(X,\mathcal{P})
and
T_{E^{*}}(X)
in terms of
\chi^{(f)}
in
[
29
, Theorem 3.2]
and
[
29
, Theorem 3.4]
, respectively.

Note that the monoid
T(I)
is regular (cf.
[
18
, p. 63, Exercise 2.6.15]
), but need not be unit-regular (cf.
[
7
, Proposition 5]
). We now prove the following lemma.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=\{X_{i}|\;i\in I\}
be a partition of
X
and let
f\in T(X,\mathcal{P})
. If
f\in\ureg(T(X,\mathcal{P}))
, then
\chi^{(f)}\in\ureg(T(I))
.

Proof.

If
f\in\ureg(T(X,\mathcal{P}))
, then there exists
g\in S(X,\mathcal{P})
such that
fgf=f
. Note that
\chi^{(g)}\in S(I)
by
[
29
, Theorem 5.8]
. We also have from
[
27
, Lemma 2.3]
that

\chi^{(f)}\chi^{(g)}\chi^{(f)}=\chi^{(fgf)}=\chi^{(f)}
.
Hence
\chi^{(f)}\in\ureg(T(I))
.
∎

Before proving Theorem
4.4
, we recall a crucial lemma from
[
29
]
.

Lemma 4.3
.

[
29
, Lemma 5.2]

Let
\mathcal{P}=\{X_{i}|\;i\in I\}
be a partition of
X
and let
f\in T(X)
. Then
f\in T(X,\mathcal{P})
if and only if there exists a unique family

B(f,I)=\{f_{i}|\;f_{i}\mbox{ is induced by }f\mbox{and }\mbox{dom}(f_{i})=X_{i}\;\forall i\in I\}.

The following theorem characterizes unit-regular elements in
T(X,\mathcal{P})
.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=\{X_{i}|\;i\in I\}
be a partition of
X
and let
f\in T(X,\mathcal{P})
. Then
f\in\ureg(T(X,\mathcal{P}))
if and only if

(1)

\chi^{(f)}\in\ureg(T(I))
;

(2)

for each
j\in I\chi^{(f)}
there exists
i\in I
such that

(i)

|X_{i}|=|X_{j}|
,

(ii)

X_{i}f=X_{j}\cap Xf
, and

(iii)

\mbox{c}(f_{i})=\mbox{d}(f_{i})
where
f_{i}\in B(f,I)
.

Proof.

Suppose first that
f\in\ureg(T(X,\mathcal{P}))
. Then there exists
g\in S(X,\mathcal{P})
such that
fgf=f
.

(1)

Lemma
4.2
takes care of this part.

(2)

Let
j\in I\chi^{(f)}
and let
j\chi^{(g)}=i
.

(i)

Since
g\in S(X,\mathcal{P})
and
j\chi^{(g)}=i
, we get
|X_{i}|=|X_{j}|
by
[
29
, Lemma 3.6(ii)]
.

(ii)

We first show that
X_{i}f\subseteq X_{j}\cap Xf
. Since
X_{i}f\subseteq Xf
, it suffices to prove that
X_{i}f\subseteq X_{j}
. Since
j\in I\chi^{(f)}
,
there exists
k\in I
such that
k\chi^{(f)}=j
and so
X_{k}f\subseteq X_{j}
by definition of
\chi^{(f)}
. Since
j\chi^{(g)}=i
, we also have
X_{j}g=X_{i}
by definition of
\chi^{(g)}
and
[
29
, Lemma 3.6]
. Then

X_{k}f=(X_{k}f)gf\subseteq(X_{j}g)f=X_{i}f
.
This implies that
X_{k}f\subseteq X_{j}\cap X_{i}f
. Since
f
preserves
\mathcal{P}
, we thus get
X_{i}f\subseteq X_{j}
.

For the reverse inclusion, let
y\in X_{j}\cap Xf
. Since
y\in Xf
, there exists
x\in X
such that
xf=y
. Also
y\in X_{j}
and
j\chi^{(g)}=i
, we see that
yg\in X_{i}
. Then

y=xf=(xf)gf=(yg)f\in X_{i}f
. Hence
X_{j}\cap Xf\subseteq X_{i}f
as required.

(iii)

Since
j\chi^{(g)}=i
, we have from
[
29
, Lemma 3.6]
that
X_{j}g=X_{i}
and so
X_{j}g_{j}=X_{i}
where
g_{j}\in B(g,I)
.
Also, by (ii), we have
X_{i}f=X_{j}\cap Xf
and so
X_{i}f_{i}\subseteq X_{j}
where
f_{i}\in B(f,I)
. Since
fgf=f
, we see that
f_{i}g_{j}f_{i}=f_{i}
.
Then
(X_{i}f_{i})g_{j}\subseteq X_{i}
is a cross-section of
\ker(f_{i})
by Lemma
3.1
. Write
(X_{i}f_{i})g_{j}=T_{f_{i}}
. Since
g\in S(X,\mathcal{P})
, the map
g_{j}\in B(g,I)
is bijective by
[
29
, Theorem 5.8]
. Therefore
(X_{j}\setminus X_{i}f_{i})g_{j}=X_{j}g_{j}\setminus(X_{i}f_{i})g_{j}=X_{i}\setminus T_{f_{i}}
and hence
\mbox{c}(f_{i})=\mbox{d}(f_{i})
.

Conversely, suppose that the given conditions hold. By (2), let
T_{\chi^{(f)}}\subseteq I
be a cross-section of
\ker(\chi^{(f)})
such that the preimage
j(\chi^{(f)})^{-1}\cap T_{\chi^{(f)}}
of every element
j\in I\chi^{(f)}
satisfies all the three conditions of (2). By (1), let
\chi^{(g)}\in S(I)
be the following bijection that satisfies
\chi^{(f)}\chi^{(g)}\chi^{(f)}=\chi^{(f)}
.

j\chi^{(g)}=\begin{cases}i\in T_{\chi^{(f)}}&\text{if $j\in I\chi^{(f)}$ and $i\chi^{(f)}=j$},\\
i\in I\setminus T_{\chi^{(f)}}&\text{if $j\in I\setminus I\chi^{(f)}$ and $|X_{i}|=|X_{j}|$}.\end{cases}

Using the above bijection
\chi^{(g)}
, we now construct a bijection of
S(X,\mathcal{P})
whose character is equal to
\chi^{(g)}
.

Let
j\in I\chi^{(f)}
. Then there exists
i\in T_{\chi^{(f)}}
such that
i\chi^{(f)}=j
. By (2), we then have
|X_{i}|=|X_{j}|
,
X_{j}\cap Xf=X_{i}f
, and
\mbox{c}(f_{i})=\mbox{d}(f_{i})
where
f_{i}\in B(f,I)
. Therefore, we can choose a bijection
g_{j}\colon X_{j}\to X_{i}
such that
yg_{j}=x
whenever
x\in T_{f_{i}}
and
xf_{i}=y
.

Let
j\in I\setminus I\chi^{(f)}
. Then we arbitrarily choose a bijection
h_{j}\colon X_{j}\to X_{j\chi^{(g)}}
. Using these bijections, we define a map
g\colon X\to X
by

xg=\begin{cases}xg_{j}&\text{if $x\in X_{j}$ where $j\in I\chi^{(f)}$},\\
xh_{j}&\text{if $x\in X_{j}$ where $j\in I\setminus I\chi^{(f)}$}.\end{cases}

Since
\chi^{(g)}\in S(I)
and each map of
B(g,I)
is bijective, it follows from
[
29
, Theorem 5.8]
that
g\in S(X,\mathcal{P})
. We can also verify in a routine manner that
fgf=f
. Hence
f\in\ureg(T(X,\mathcal{P}))
.
∎

5.
Unit-regular elements in
\Sigma(X,\mathcal{P})
,
\Gamma(X,\mathcal{P})
, and
T_{E^{*}}(X)

In this section, we first give a characterization of a regular element
f
in
\Sigma(X,\mathcal{P})
in term of its character
\chi^{(f)}
. We then characterize unit-regular elements in
\Sigma(X,\mathcal{P})
,
\Gamma(X,\mathcal{P})
, and
T_{E^{*}}(X)
, respectively.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=\{X_{i}|\;i\in I\}
be a partition of
X
and let
f\in\Sigma(X,\mathcal{P})
. Then
f\in\reg(\Sigma(X,\mathcal{P}))
if and only if
\chi^{(f)}\in\reg(\Gamma(I))
.

Proof.

Suppose first that
f\in\reg(\Sigma(X,\mathcal{P}))
. Then there exists
g\in\Sigma(X,\mathcal{P})
such that
fgf=f
. Therefore

\chi^{(f)}=\chi^{(fgf)}=\chi^{(f)}\chi^{(g)}\chi^{(f)}

by
[
27
, Lemma 2.3]
. Since
f,g\in\Sigma(X,\mathcal{P})
, we have
\chi^{(f)},\chi^{(g)}\in\Gamma(I)
by Theorem
[
29
, Theorem 3.4]
and so
\chi^{(f)}\in\reg(\Gamma(I))
.

Conversely, suppose that
\chi^{(f)}\in\reg(\Gamma(I))
. Then there exists
h\in\Gamma(I)
such that
\chi^{(f)}h\chi^{(f)}=\chi^{(f)}
. Since
\chi^{(f)}\in\reg(\Gamma(I))
, it follows from Proposition
3.10
that
\chi^{(f)}\in S(I)
and so
h=(\chi^{(f)})^{-1}
.

For each
i\in I
, let
x_{i}
be a fixed element of
X_{i}
. Moreover, for each
x\in Xf
, let
x^{\prime}
be a fixed element of
xf^{-1}
. Define a map
g\colon X\to X
by

xg=\begin{cases}x^{\prime}&\text{if $x\in X_{i}\cap Xf$},\\
x_{i(\chi^{(f)})^{-1}}&\text{if $x\in X_{i}\setminus Xf$}.\end{cases}

Observe that
g\in T(X,\mathcal{P})
and
\chi^{(g)}=h\in\Gamma(I)
. Therefore
g\in\Sigma(X,\mathcal{P})
by
[
29
, Theorem 3.2]
. Since
g
maps each element
x
of
Xf
to a fixed element
x^{\prime}
of
xf^{-1}
, we can verify in a routine manner that
fgf=f
. Hence
f\in\reg(\Sigma(X,\mathcal{P}))
.
∎

We now have the following corollary from Proposition
3.10
and Proposition
5.1
.

Corollary 5.2
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=\{X_{i}|\;i\in I\}
be a partition of
X
and let

, then
\chi^{(f)}\in S(I)
.

If
f\in\ureg(\Sigma(X,\mathcal{P}))
, then
f\in\reg(\Sigma(X,\mathcal{P}))
. Therefore
\chi^{(f)}\in\reg(\Gamma(I))
by Proposition
5.1
and so
\chi^{(f)}\in S(I)
by Proposition
3.10
.
∎

Let

be a monoid and let

be a submonoid of

. Then

\mbox{ureg}(N)\subseteq\mbox{ureg}(M)

Recall that, for finite

.
The following proposition characterizes unit-regular elements in

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=\{X_{i}|\;i\in I\}
be a partition of

∎

6.
The Submonoid
\Omega(X,\mathcal{P})

In this section, we characterize regular and unit-regular elements in
\Omega(X,\mathcal{}P)
, respectively. Let us first make the following useful remark.

Remark 6.1
.

Let
f\in T(X,\mathcal{P})
. Then
f\in\Omega(X,\mathcal{P})
if and only if every map in
B(f,I)
is injective.

The next theorem characterizes regular elements in
\Omega(X,\mathcal{P})
.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=\{X_{i}|\;i\in I\}
be a partition of
X
and let
f\in\Omega(X,\mathcal{P})
. Then
f\in\reg(\Omega(X,\mathcal{P}))
if and only if for every
j\in I\chi^{(f)}
there exists
i\in I
such that the map
f_{i}\in B(f,I)
from
X_{i}
to
X_{j}
is surjective.

Proof.

Suppose first that
f\in\reg(\Omega(X,\mathcal{P}))
, and let
j\in I\chi^{(f)}
. Then there exists
i\in I
such that
i\chi^{(f)}=j
and so
X_{i}f\subseteq X_{j}
by definition of
\chi^{(f)}
. Then there is a map
f_{i}\in B(f,I)
from
X_{i}
to
X_{j}
. If
f_{i}
is surjective, then we are done. Otherwise, suppose that
f_{i}
is not surjective.

Recall that
f\in\reg(\Omega(X,\mathcal{P}))
. There exists
g\in\Omega(X,\mathcal{P})
such that
fgf=f
. Since
j\in I\chi^{(f)}\subseteq I
, there exists
l\in I\chi^{(g)}
such that
j\chi^{(g)}=l
and so
X_{j}g\subseteq X_{l}
by definition of
\chi^{(g)}
. Then there is a map
g_{j}\in B(g,I)
from
X_{j}
to
X_{l}
. Recall that
X_{i}f\subseteq X_{j}
,
X_{j}g\subseteq X_{l}
, and
fgf=f
. We obtain

X_{i}f=(X_{i}f)gf\subseteq(X_{j}g)f\subseteq X_{l}f.

Since
f
preserves
\mathcal{P}
, we get
X_{l}f\subseteq X_{j}
. Therefore there is a map
f_{l}\in B(f,I)
from
X_{l}
to
X_{j}
. We now claim that
f_{l}
is surjective.

Assume, to the contrary, that there exists
y\in X_{j}\setminus X_{l}f_{l}
. Write
yg_{j}=z
and
zf_{l}=w
. Clearly
z\in X_{l}
,
w\in X_{j}
, and
y\neq w
.
Since
f,g\in\Omega(X,\mathcal{P})
, the maps
g_{j}
and
f_{l}
are injective by Remark
6.1
and so
(wg_{j})f_{l}\neq(yg_{j})f_{l}
. But
(yg_{j})f_{l}=zf_{l}=w
, and

(wg_{j})f_{l}=(wg_{j})f=(wg)f=(zf_{l})gf=(zf)gf=zf=zf_{l}=w

which is a contradiction. Hence the map
f_{l}\in B(f,I)
is surjective as required.

Conversely, suppose that the given condition holds. By Remark
6.1
, we know that every map in
B(f,I)
is injective. Then, by hypothesis, for every
j\in I\chi^{(f)}
there exists
i\in I
such that the map
f_{i}\in B(f,I)
from
X_{i}
to
X_{j}
is bijective. Denote by
h_{j}
the inverse map of
f_{i}\colon X_{i}\to X_{j}
. Note that the map
h_{j}\colon X_{j}\to X_{i}
is bijective. We now define a map
g\colon X\to X
by

xg=\begin{cases}xh_{j}&\text{if $x\in X_{j}$ where $j\in I\chi^{(f)}$},\\
x&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Clearly
g\in\Omega(X,\mathcal{P})
. We can verify in a routine manner that

. Hence
f\in\reg(\Omega(X,\mathcal{P}))
.
∎

The following proposition characterizes unit-regular elements in
\Omega(X,\mathcal{P})
.

.

Let
\mathcal{P}=\{X_{i}|\;i\in I\}

(ii)

(i).

(i)

Then Lemma
4.2

(ii)

Note that

6.2

Conversely, suppose that the given conditions hold. Since

there exists

such that the map

(ii).
∎
