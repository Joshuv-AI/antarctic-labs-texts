Weak Carleson conditions in uniformly rectifiable metric spaces: the WCD and alpha numbers
Jared Krandel

Abstract
. We investigate characterizations of uniformly rectifiable (UR) metric spaces by so-called weak Carleson conditions for flatness coefficients which measure the extent to which Hausdorff measure on the metric space differs from Hausdorff measure on a normed space. First, we show that UR metric spaces satisfy David and Semmes’s weak constant density condition, a quantitative regularity property which implies most balls in the space support a measure with nearly constant density in a neighborhood of scales and locations. Second, we introduce a metric space variant of Tolsa’s alpha numbers that measure a local normalized L 1 L_{1} mass transport cost between the space’s Hausdorff measure and Hausdorff measure on a normed space. We show that a weak Carleson condition for these alpha numbers gives a characterization of metric uniform rectifiability. We derive both results as corollaries of a more general abstract result which gives a tool for transferring weak Carleson conditions to spaces with very big pieces of spaces with a given weak Carleson condition.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Overview

Rectifiability properties of sets and measures are fundamental topics of interest in geometric measure theory. Although these properties were initially studied for subsets of Euclidean space, in recent years there have been significant developments in rectifiability properties of metric spaces.

Definition 1.1

(
n
-rectifiability)
.

Let
X
be a metric space. We say that
E\subseteq X
is
n
-rectifiable if there exist Borel sets
A_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
and Lipschitz maps
f_{i}:A_{i}\rightarrow X
such that

\mathscr{H}^{n}\left(E\setminus\bigcup_{i}f_{i}(A_{i})\right)=0.

The class of
uniformly
n
-rectifiable
(UR) metric spaces give a strictly stronger notion of rectifiability which is
quantitative
in nature.

Definition 1.2

(uniform
n
-rectifiability)
.

A metric space
X
is
uniformly
n
-rectifiable
if
X
has
Big Pieces of Lipschitz images of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(BPLI) and there exists a constant
C_{0}>0
such that
X
is
Ahlfors
(C_{0},n)
-regular
. By having BPLI, we mean there exist constants
L,\theta>0
such that for all
x\in X
and
0<r<\mathrm{diam}(X)
, there exists an
L
-Lipschitz map
f:A_{x,r}\subseteq B(0,r)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow X
such that

(1.1)

\mathscr{H}^{n}(B(x,r)\cap f(A_{x,r}))\geq\theta r^{n}.

By being Ahlfors
(C_{0},n)
-regular, we mean that for all
x\in X
and
0<r<\mathrm{diam}(X)
,

(1.2)

C_{0}^{-1}r^{n}\leq\mathscr{H}^{n}(B(x,r))\leq C_{0}r^{n}.

This is a stronger form of
n
-rectifiability in which one enforces Ahlfors
n
-regularity and requires a uniform percentage of the measure of each ball to be covered by a single Lipschitz image. In the case when
X
is a subset of some Euclidean space with the induced metric, this class of sets were introduced and studied in detail by David and Semmes
[
DS91
]
,
[
DS93
]
. In their work, David and Semmes provide numerous analytic and geometric characterizations of these sets involving boundedness of singular integral operators, quantitative control over numerous coefficients measuring local non-flatness, quantitative approximation by Lipschitz graphs, and more.

It has long been an open question whether there exist analogous characterizations of UR metric spaces similar to those studied by David and Semmes in Euclidean spaces. Recent work by Bate, Hyde, and Schul has given significant progress, giving metric versions of the well-known BWGL and corona decomposition
[
BHS23
]
. Fassler and Violo have also managed to give a version of the so-called strong geometric lemma characterization of UR for one-dimensional sets
[
FV23
]
.

In this paper, we give some progress on characterizations of UR metric spaces by showing (1) UR metric spaces satisfy the
weak constant density condition
of David and Semmes, and (2) metric uniform rectifiability is characterized by a
weak Carleson condition
for a metric variant of Tolsa’s
\alpha
number. We derive both results as an application of a more general, abstract tool for transferring weak Carleson conditions from coefficients on spaces in a given class
\mathscr{Y}
to spaces with
very big pieces
of spaces in
\mathscr{Y}
. In our applications, we apply this result by first showing that
subsets
of bi-Lipschitz images of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
have well-controlled densities and alpha numbers. Then, we use the fact that UR metric spaces have very big pieces of bi-Lipschitz images to apply the abstract transference result

1.2.
The weak constant density condition

One of many interesting characterizations of
n
-rectifiability in Euclidean spaces involves the Hausdorff density, which measures how the
\mathscr{H}^{n}
measure of small balls around a point compare to the
\mathscr{H}^{n}
measure of balls of equal radius in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be
\mathscr{H}^{n}
measurable with
\mathscr{H}^{n}(E)<\infty
. The set
E
is
n
-rectifiable if and only if for
\mathscr{H}^{n}
-a.e.
x\in E
,

(1.3)

\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\frac{\mathscr{H}^{n}(E\cap B(x,r))}{(2r)^{n}}=1.

The backward direction was proven by Besicovitch for
n=1,\ d=2

[
Bes28
]
,
[
Bes38
]
, Marstrand for
n=2,\ d=3

[
Mar61
]
and Mattila for general
n\leq d

[
Mat75
]
. Later, Preiss showed that any measure in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
whose
n
-dimensional density merely exists and is positive and finite
\mathscr{H}^{n}
-a.e. is
n
-rectifiable, generalizing this result significantly
[
Pre87
]
.

The forward direction of Theorem
1.3
follows more readily from the almost everywhere differentiability of Lipschitz maps, but it was not until Kircheim gave a notion of metric differentiability for maps from
\mathbb{R}^{n}
into
X
that Theorem
1.3
received the following one-sided metric space analog.

Theorem 1.4

(See
[
Kir94
]
Theorem 9)
.

Let
E\subseteq X
be
n
-rectifiable with
\mathscr{H}^{n}(E)<\infty
. Equation (
1.3
) holds at
\mathscr{H}^{n}
-a.e.
x\in E
.

It follows from work of Preiss and Tišer
[
PT92
]
that the converse of Theorem
1.4
holds when
n=1
, but it remains an interesting and difficult open question whether the converse holds for general
n
.

The weak constant density condition (WCD) provides an analog of (
1.3
) in the world of uniform rectifiability. David and Semmes introduced the WCD as a way of quantifying (
1.3
) by requiring that in almost every
ball
, there exists a measure supported on the set with
nearly constant
density nearby.

Definition 1.5

(weak constant density condition, Carleson sets and measures)
.

Let
X
be Ahlfors
n
-regular, let
C_{0},\epsilon_{0}>0
, and define

\displaystyle\mathscr{G}_{\text{cd}}(\epsilon_{0})

\displaystyle=\Set{(x,r)\in X\times\mathbb{R}^{+}}{\begin{array}[]{l}\exists\mu\text{ a $C_{0}$-regular measure with }\mathrm{spt}\mu=X\\
\text{such that }\forall y\in B(x,r),\ 0<t\leq r,\\
|\mu(B(y,t))-t^{n}|\leq\epsilon_{0}r^{n}\end{array}},

(1.7)

\displaystyle\mathscr{B}_{\text{cd}}(\epsilon_{0})

\displaystyle=X\times\mathbb{R}^{+}\setminus\mathscr{G}_{\text{cd}}(\epsilon_{0}).

We say that
X
satisfies the weak constant density condition if for all
\epsilon_{0}>0
,
\mathscr{B}_{\text{cd}}(\epsilon_{0})
is a Carleson set. That is, there exists a constant
C>0
such that for all
z\in X
and
0<r<\mathrm{diam}(X)
,

\int_{B(z,r)}\int_{0}^{r}\chi_{\mathscr{B}_{\text{cd}}(\epsilon_{0})}(x,t)d\mathscr{H}^{n}(x)\frac{dt}{t}\leq Cr^{n}.

If this holds, we say that
\chi_{\mathscr{B}_{\text{cd}}(\epsilon_{0})}d\mathscr{H}^{n}(x)\frac{dt}{t}
is a
Carleson measure
and say that
\mathscr{B}_{\text{cd}}(\epsilon_{0})
is
C
-Carleson
.

For related quantitative conditions involving densities, see
[
CGLT16
]
,
[
AH22
]
, and
[
TT15
]
. The work of David, Semmes, and Tolsa combine to prove the following Theorem:

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be Ahlfors
n
-regular. The set
E
is uniformly
n
-rectifiable if and only if
E
satisfies the WCD.

David and Semmes proved the forward implication in Chapter 6 of
[
DS91
]
using a characterization of uniform rectifiability (condition C2 of
[
DS91
]
) more closely related to the boundedness of singular integral operators. We will say more about this when we discuss our result.

They proved the reverse implication only in the case
n=1,2,
and
d-1
. Their proof uses the fact that if a measure is very close to having constant density in a large neighborhood of scales and locations, then its support is well-approximated by the support of an
n
-uniform measure
, a measure
\mu
for which there exists
c>0
such that
\mu(B(x,r))=cr^{n}
for all
x\in\mathrm{spt}(\mu)
and
r>0
. Because uniform measures in Euclidean space are completely classfied for
n=1,2
(they are all multiples of Hausdorff measure on a plane) and for
n=d-1
(they are Hausdorff measure on products of planes and light cones
[
KP87
]
), David and Semmes are able to show that a WCD set is very close to flat on most balls which are good for the WCD. The absence of a classification for uniform measures in intermediate dimensions prevented a direct adaptation of their arguments. However, Tolsa completed the proof of the reverse direction in Theorem
1.6
in
[
Tol15
]
by replacing elements of David and Semmes’s argument specific to their examples of uniform measures with general flatness properties of uniform measures derived by Preiss
[
Pre87
]
in addition to new arguments using the Riesz transform.

In general, classifying uniform measures is a difficult open problem, but see
[
Nim22
]
for an interesting family of examples. For further studies of uniform measures in Euclidean spaces see
[
Pre87
]
(and
[
DL08
]
for a more gentle presentation of Preiss),
[
KP02
]
,
[
Nim17
]
, and
[
Nim19
]
. For research into uniform measures in the Heisenberg group see
[
CMT20
]
and
[
Mer22
]
and for a related result in
\ell_{\infty}^{3}
, see
[
Lor03
]
.

Just as Theorem
1.6
provides a quantitative analog of Theorem
1.3
, one might expect a quantitative analog of Kircheim’s result, Theorem
1.4
, to hold for
uniformly rectifiable metric spaces
, i.e., metric spaces which are Ahlfors
n
-regular and have big pieces of Lipschitz images of subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In this paper, we provide such an analog by proving the following theorem.

Theorem A
.

Uniformly
n
-rectifiable metric spaces satisfy the WCD.

We note here that the naive converse of Theorem
1.6
is false: There exist Ahlfors regular metric spaces which satisfy the WCD, yet are not uniformly rectifiable. Indeed, the metric space
(X,d)=(\mathbb{R},|\cdot|_{\text{Euc}}^{1/2})
is in fact
2
-uniform:
\mathscr{H}^{2}(B(x,r))=2r^{2}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
r\geq 0
, hence
X
satisfies the weak constant density condition, yet
X
is purely
2
-unrectifiable (notice that the Hausdorff
2
-density is everywhere 1/2 so that this space does not give a counterexample to the potential converse of Theorem
1.4
). Some different examples of this failure even in the case
n=1
are given by Bate
[
Bat23
]
. He proves that every
1
-uniform metric measure space is either
\mathbb{R}
, a particular union of disjoint circles of radius
d
, or a purely unrectifiable “limit” of the circle spaces. These last two spaces are examples of
1
-uniform spaces which are not uniformly rectifiable.

Analyzing connectedness plays a special role in the proof because any
1
-uniform connected component must be locally isometric to
\mathbb{R}
, implying any connected
1
-uniform space is itself
\mathbb{R}
. From these examples, it seems reasonable to think that some connectedness and topological conditions are necessary hypotheses for any type of converse to hold. It also follows from work of Schul
[
Sch07
]
,
[
Sch09
]
, and Fassler and Violo
[
FV23
]
(see also
[
Hah05
]
) that any Ahlfors
1
-regular connected subset of a metric space is uniformly
1
-rectifiable, although perhaps adding some form of weaker hypothesis could provide an interesting converse to our result in the one-dimensional case using Bate’s classification.

1.4.
Outline of the paper

In section
4
, we give a broad definition of a coefficient (such as
\alpha
) and prove Theorem
4.10
, an abstract result which gives conditions under which one can “transfer” a weak Carleson condition under very big pieces. This result is used in both the proofs of Theorems
A
and
B
.

In section
5
, we develop more tools used in the proofs of Theorems
A
and
B
. The main results are Lemma
5.11
and Corollary
5.12
which allow us to quantitatively control the variation of means of
L^{\infty}
functions on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
over a general class of subsets of the domain which we call sandwichable (the motivating example of such a class is
L
-bi-Lipschitz images of a fixed ball). Corollary
5.12
is similar to results given by David and Semmes (See
[
DS93
]
Lemma IV.2.2.14 and Corollary IV.2.2.19, and see Remark
5.13
for a discussion of the difference with our result), although our proof proceeds by contradiction, a method which differs significantly from their proofs. These results are applied to control the variation of the Jacobian of bi-Lipschitz mappings
g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\Sigma
over normed balls in its domain, controlling the variation of the
\mathscr{H}^{n}
measure of balls in
\Sigma
.

In section
6
, we prove Theorems
A
and
B
. The structure of both proofs are similar. For the proofs of the Carleson conditions in both theorems, we apply our transference theorem
4.10
using Bate, Hyde, and Schul’s result that UR spaces have very big pieces of bi-Lipschitz images. The main difficulty is showing that suitable versions of our coefficients defined on
subsets
of bi-Lipschitz images have the necessary Carleson conditions (See Proposition
6.18
and Proposition
6.33
.) For the WCD, this method of proof differs significantly from the original Euclidean proof of David and Semmes which uses a Carleson condition for integrals of smooth odd functions defined on the ambient Euclidean space to show that UR subsets have a form of quantitative symmetry from which one can deduce near uniformity of Hausdorff measure (See section 6 of
[
DS91
]
.).

Preliminaries

and

. If we write

.

. The Hausdorff

Definition 3.1

(one-third trick lattices)
.

The following family of dyadic systems were introduced by Okikiolu
[
Oki92
]
. For any

and cube
Q_{0}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, define the shifted dyadic lattice

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{j}^{e}(Q_{0})

\displaystyle=\Set{Q+\frac{\ell(Q)}{3}e}{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{j}(Q_{0})},

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{e}(Q_{0})

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{j\geq 0}\mathcal{D}_{j}^{e}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

and set

\tilde{\mathcal{D}}(Q_{0})=\bigcup_{e\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\mathcal{D}^{e}(Q_{0}).

\tilde{\mathcal{D}}(Q_{0})
has the following property: For any

and
j\geq 0
, there exists
Q\in\tilde{\mathcal{D}}(Q_{0})
such that
x\in\frac{2}{3}Q
(See
[
Ler03
]
Proposition 3.2).

We will also need a version of “cubes” associated to a metric space. David
[
Dav88
]
introduced this idea first, and it was later generalized by
[

]
and
[

]
. The following formulation draws most from the latter two.

Theorem 3.2

(Christ-David cubes)
.

Let
X
be a doubling metric space. Let
X_{k}
be a nested sequence of maximal
\rho^{k}
-nets for
X
where
\rho<1/1000
and let
c_{0}=1/500
. For each
k\in\mathbb{Z}
there is a collection
\mathscr{D}_{k}
of “cubes,” which are Borel subsets of
X
such that the following hold.

(i)

X=\bigcup_{Q\in\mathscr{D}_{k}}Q
.

(ii)

If
Q,Q^{\prime}\in\mathscr{D}=\bigcup\mathscr{D}_{k}
and
Q\cap Q^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
, then
Q\subseteq Q^{\prime}
or
Q^{\prime}\subseteq Q
.

(iii)

For
Q\in\mathscr{D}
, let
k(Q)
be the unique integer so that
Q\in\mathscr{D}_{k}
and set
\ell(Q)=5\rho^{k(Q)}
. Then there is
x_{Q}\in X_{k}
so that

B(x_{Q},c_{0}\ell(Q))\subseteq Q\subseteq B(x_{Q},\ell(Q))

and

X_{k}=\{x_{Q}:Q\in\mathscr{D}_{k}\}.

(iv)

If
X
is Ahlfors
n
-regular, then there exists
C\geq 1
such that

\mathscr{H}^{n}(\Set{x\in Q}{d(x,X\setminus Q)\leq\eta\rho^{k}})\lesssim\eta^{1/C}\ell(Q)^{n}

for all
Q\in\mathscr{D}
and
\eta>0
.

In addition, we define

B_{Q}=B(x_{Q},\ell(Q)).

In analogy to the dyadic cube notation, for any
R\in\mathscr{D}
and
k\in\mathbb{Z}
we also write

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}(R)

\displaystyle=\Set{Q\in\mathscr{D}}{Q\subseteq R},

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}_{k}(R)

\displaystyle=\Set{Q\in\mathscr{D}(R)}{\ell(Q)=\rho^{-k}\ell(R)}.

4.
Transference of weak Carleson conditions under very big pieces

In this section, we prove an abstract lemma that allows one to transfer weak Carleson conditions for a variety of beta-type coefficients from very big pieces to their approximated spaces. We begin by discussing what we mean by a coefficient and by weak Carleson conditions.

Definition 4.1

(Beta number-type coefficients)
.

Let
X
be a metric space. We say that
\beta:X\times[0,\mathrm{diam}(X))\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a
coefficient
on
X
if there exists an increasing, doubling function
f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with
f(1)=1
such that for any
x,y\in X
and
t<r
satisfying
B(y,t)\subseteq B(x,r)
, we have

(4.1)

\beta(y,t)\leq f\left(\frac{r}{t}\right)\beta(x,r).

We denote the set of coefficients on
X
by
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Co}(X)
. We often identify the pair
(x,r)
with the ball
B(x,r)
and use the convention
\beta(B(x,r))=\beta(x,r)
. If
\mathscr{D}(X)
is a Christ-David lattice on
X
and
Q\in\mathscr{D}(X)
, we define

\beta(Q)=\beta(B_{Q}).

Definition 4.2

(weak Carleson conditions)
.

Let
X
be a doubling metric space, let
\mathscr{D}(X)
be a Christ-David lattice for
X
, and let
\beta\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Co}(X)
. We say that
\beta\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Co}(X)
satisfies a
weak Carleson condition
on
X
if it has the following property: For all
\epsilon>0
, there exists a constant
C>0
such that for any
R\in\mathscr{D}(X)
,

(4.2)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q\subseteq R\\
\beta(Q)>\epsilon\end{subarray}}\ell(Q)^{n}\leq C\ell(R)^{n}.

We define

\mathrm{WC}(X)=\{\beta\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Co}(X):\beta\text{ satisfies a weak Carleson condition on $X$}\}.

Remark 4.3
.

Using the “monotonicity” property in the definition of a coefficient, it is standard to show that
\beta\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Co}(X)
satisfying a weak Carleson condition as in Definition
4.2
is equivalent to a form of Carleson measure statement as in Definition
1.5
. For
\epsilon>0
, if we define

\mathscr{B}(\epsilon)=\{(x,t)\in X\times\mathbb{R}^{+}:\beta(x,t)>\epsilon\}

then
\beta
has a weak Carleson condition on
X
if and only if
\chi_{\mathscr{B}(\epsilon)}(x,t)d\mathscr{H}^{n}(x)\frac{dt}{t}
is a Carleson measure for every
\epsilon>0
.

We want to use (
4.1
) to show that it actually suffices to control the value of coefficients on tiny balls
\{c_{0}B_{Q}\}_{Q\in\mathscr{D}}
in a fixed lattice rather than the full balls. The next two lemmas accomplish this.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
X
be a doubling metric space with doubling constant
C_{d}
. There exists
N(C_{d})<\infty
Christ-David systems of cubes
\{\mathscr{D}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
for
X
such that the following holds: For any
x\in X,\ 0<t<\mathrm{diam}(X)
, there exists
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
and
Q\in\mathscr{D}_{i}
with
t\leq\ell(Q)\leq\frac{5}{\rho c_{0}}t
such that
B(x,t)\subseteq\frac{c_{0}}{2}B_{Q}
.

Proof.

Fix
\rho<\frac{1}{1000}
. For each
k
, let
\tilde{X}_{k}
be a maximal
c_{0}\rho^{k}
-net for
X
. We now iteratively construct maximal
\rho^{k}
-nets
X_{k}^{1},X_{k}^{2},\ldots,X_{k}^{N},\ldots
in the following way. Let
X_{k}^{1}
be a completion of a maximal
\rho^{k}
-separated subset of
\tilde{X}_{k}
to a maximal
\rho^{k}
-net for
X
. Given
X_{k}^{i}
for any
i>0
, construct
X_{k}^{i+1}
by completing a maximal
\rho^{k}
-separated subset of
Y_{k}^{i}\vcentcolon=\tilde{X}_{k}\setminus(X_{k}^{1}\cup X_{k}^{2}\cup\ldots\cup X_{k}^{i})
to a maximal
\rho^{k}
-net for
X
. We claim that this process terminates in
N(C_{d})
steps, giving for each
k\in\mathbb{Z}
a collection of maximal
\rho^{k}
-nets
X_{k}^{1},\ldots,X_{k}^{N}
. Indeed, let
B
be a ball of radius
2\rho^{k}
. By doubling, there exists
N(C_{d})<\infty
such that
\#(B\cap\tilde{X}_{k})\leq N(C_{d})
. Suppose that
\frac{1}{2}B\cap Y_{k}^{j}\not=\varnothing
for some
j>0
. Then, because
X_{k}^{j+1}
is maximal, there exists some
x\in B\cap Y_{k}^{j}
such that
x\in X_{k}^{j+1}
. Therefore,
\#(B\cap Y_{k}^{j+1})<\#(B\cap Y_{k}^{j})
whenever
\frac{1}{2}B\cap Y_{k}^{j}\not=\varnothing
. This means
\frac{1}{2}B\cap Y_{k}^{N+1}=\varnothing
for any such
B
, implying
Y_{k}^{N+1}=\varnothing
and
\tilde{X}_{k}\subseteq\cup_{i=1}^{N}X_{k}^{i}
as desired.

We now show that the lemma follows from this. Recall that Theorem
3.2
takes as input a collection
\{X_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
of maximal
\rho^{k}
-nets for
X
and outputs a system of cubes
\mathscr{D}
such that every
x^{\alpha}_{k}\in X_{k}
is the “center” of a cube
Q_{k}^{\alpha}\in\mathscr{D}
with
B_{X}(x_{k}^{\alpha},c_{0}5\rho^{k})=c_{0}B_{Q_{k}^{\alpha}}\subseteq Q_{k}^{\alpha}
. We apply Theorem
3.2
to the collection
\{X_{k}^{i}\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
for every
1\leq i\leq N
and receive a Christ-David system
\mathscr{D}_{i}
such that each point
\tilde{x}_{k}\in\tilde{X}_{k}
is the center of some
Q\in\mathscr{D}_{i}
for some
i
. So, let
x\in X
,
0<t<\mathrm{diam}(X)
, and let
k\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
c_{0}\rho^{k-1}\leq t<c_{0}\rho^{k}
. Because
\tilde{X}_{k}
is a maximal
c_{0}\rho^{k}
-net for
X
, there exists
\tilde{x}_{k}\in\tilde{X}_{k}
such that
d(x,\tilde{x}_{k})<c_{0}\rho^{k}
. Because
\tilde{X}_{k}\subseteq\cup_{i=1}^{N}X_{k}^{i}
, there then exists
1\leq i\leq N
and
Q\in\mathscr{D}_{i}
such that
\tilde{x}_{k}=x_{Q}
so that
x\in B(x_{Q},c_{0}\rho^{k})=\frac{1}{5}B(x_{Q},c_{0}\ell(Q))=\frac{c_{0}}{5}B_{Q}
. Similarly,
\frac{\rho c_{0}}{5}\ell(Q)\leq t<\frac{c_{0}}{5}\ell(Q)
.
∎

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
X
be doubling, let
\beta\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Co}(X)
, and let
\mathscr{D}(X)
be any Christ-David lattice for
X
. Suppose that the collection

\mathscr{B}_{0}=\{Q\in\mathscr{D}(X):\beta(c_{0}B_{Q})>\epsilon\}

is Carleson for any
\epsilon>0
. Then
\beta\in\mathrm{WC}(X)
.

Proof.

Using Lemma
4.4
, we get a collection of
N(C_{0},n)
Christ-David lattices
\{\mathscr{D}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
such that for any
x\in X
and
0<t<\mathrm{diam}(X)
, there exists
1\leq i\leq N
such that there is
Q\in\mathscr{D}_{i}
with
B(x,t)\subseteq\frac{c_{0}}{2}B_{Q}
and
t\asymp\ell(Q)
. Therefore, for any
Q\in\mathscr{D}(X)
, there exists
\tilde{Q}\in\cup_{i}\mathscr{D}_{i}
such that
B_{Q}\subseteq c_{0}B_{\tilde{Q}}
and
\ell(Q)\asymp\ell(\tilde{Q})
. Define

\mathscr{B}_{\epsilon^{\prime}}=\{Q\in\mathscr{D}(X):\beta(\tilde{Q})>\epsilon^{\prime}\}.

Notice that if
Q\in\mathscr{D}\setminus\mathscr{B}_{\epsilon^{\prime}}
, then

\beta(Q)=\beta(B_{Q})\leq f\left(\frac{c_{0}\ell(\tilde{Q})}{\ell(Q)}\right)\beta(c_{0}B_{\tilde{Q}})\lesssim\beta(\tilde{Q})\leq C\epsilon^{\prime}<\epsilon

as long as
\epsilon^{\prime}
is small enough. Therefore, it suffices to show that
\mathscr{B}_{\epsilon^{\prime}}
is Carleson. Fix
R\in\mathscr{D}(X)
and observe for each
1\leq i\leq N
, there exists a collection of at most
N^{\prime}(C_{0},n)
cubes
\mathscr{Q}_{i}
such that for all
Q\in\mathscr{D}(R)
, there exists
i
and
Q^{\prime}\in\mathscr{D}_{i}
such that
\tilde{Q}\subseteq Q_{i}
. We also have that any such
Q_{i}
has
\ell(Q_{i})\asymp_{C_{0},n}\ell(R)
. Using the fact that the mapping
Q\mapsto\tilde{Q}
is also at most
C(C_{0},n)
to one, we can estimate

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q\subseteq R\\
Q\in\mathscr{B}_{\epsilon^{\prime}}\end{subarray}}\ell(Q)^{n}\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q\subseteq R\\
Q\in\mathscr{B}_{\epsilon^{\prime}}\end{subarray}}\ell(\tilde{Q})^{n}\leq C\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{Q}_{i}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q^{\prime}\subseteq Q\\
\beta(Q^{\prime})>\epsilon^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\ell(Q^{\prime})^{n}\lesssim\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{Q}_{i}}\ell(Q)^{n}\lesssim\ell(R)^{n}.

∎

Remark 4.6
.

It is not much more work to show that if one instead defines
\beta(Q)=\beta(AB_{Q})
for some
A\geq 1
, then Lemma
4.5
holds with constants additionally depending on
A
.

Reducing weak Carleson conditions to controlling
\beta(c_{0}B_{Q})
rather than
\beta(B_{Q})
is convenient for us because we are interested in approximation of spaces by very big pieces, and the following lemma gives a simple way of finding large families of cubes which have almost all of their measure contained in a given very big piece
Y
. That is, cubes
Q
such that
\mathscr{H}^{n}(Q\setminus Y)\leq\epsilon\mathscr{H}^{n}(Q)
. For such a cube, we can immediately control the measure of
c_{0}B_{Q}\subseteq Q
outside of
Y
in a way we cannot for
B_{Q}
.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
X
be a doubling metric space and let
\mathscr{D}(X)
be a Christ-David lattice. Let
\epsilon>0
, let
F\subseteq X\text{ be $\mathscr{H}^{n}$ measurable}
, and let
R\in\mathscr{D}(X)
be such that
\mathscr{H}^{n}(R\setminus F)\leq\epsilon\mathscr{H}^{n}(R)
. Define

(4.3)

\tilde{R}=\Set{x\in R}{\begin{array}[]{l}\text{For all $Q\in\mathscr{D}$ such that $x\in Q\subseteq R$, }\\
\mathscr{H}^{n}(Q\cap F)\geq(1-2\epsilon)\mathscr{H}^{n}(Q)\end{array}}.

We have
\mathscr{H}^{n}(\tilde{R})\geq\epsilon\mathscr{H}^{n}(R)
.

Proof.

This proof is essentially contained in the proof of Lemma IV.2.2.38 in
[
DS93
]
, but we need to be precise about the constant
\epsilon
. If
x\in R\setminus\tilde{R}
, then
x
is contained in some cube
Q
such that
\mathscr{H}^{n}(Q\cap F)\leq(1-2\epsilon)\mathscr{H}^{n}(Q)
. Let
\{Q_{i}\}_{i}
be a maximal disjoint family of such cubes so that
R\setminus\tilde{R}=\bigcup_{i}Q_{i}
. Then

(4.4)

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}^{n}((R\setminus\tilde{R})\cap F)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\mathscr{H}^{n}(Q_{i}\cap F)\leq(1-2\epsilon)\sum_{i}\mathscr{H}^{n}(Q_{i})

\displaystyle\leq(1-2\epsilon)\mathscr{H}^{n}(R\setminus\tilde{R})\leq(1-2\epsilon)\mathscr{H}^{n}(R).

On the other hand,

(4.5)

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}^{n}((R\setminus\tilde{R})\cap F)

\displaystyle=\mathscr{H}^{n}((R\cap F)\setminus\tilde{R})\geq\mathscr{H}^{n}(R\cap F)-\mathscr{H}^{n}(\tilde{R})

\displaystyle\geq(1-\epsilon)\mathscr{H}^{n}(R)-\mathscr{H}^{n}(\tilde{R}).

Combining (
4.4
) and (
4.5
) and rearranging gives

\mathscr{H}^{n}(\tilde{R})\geq(1-\epsilon)\mathscr{H}^{n}(R)-(1-2\epsilon)\mathscr{H}^{n}(R)=\epsilon\mathscr{H}^{n}(R).\qed

The last ingredient for our transference theorem is the following abstract version of the John-Nirenberg-Stromberg lemma.

Lemma 4.8

(
[
BHS23
]
Lemma 4.2.8,
[
DS93
]
Lemma IV.1.12)
.

Let
X
be an Ahlfors
n
-regular metric space and
\mathscr{D}
a system of Christ-David cubes for
X
. Let
\alpha:\mathscr{D}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
be given and suppose there are
N,\eta>0
such that

(4.6)

\mathscr{H}^{n}\left(\Set{x\in R}{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q\subseteq R\\
x\in Q\end{subarray}}\alpha(Q)\leq N}\right)\geq\eta\ell(R)^{n}

for all
R\in\mathscr{D}
. Then,

\sum_{Q\subseteq R}\alpha(Q)\ell(Q)^{n}\lesssim_{N,\eta}\ell(R)^{n}

for all
R\in\mathscr{D}
.

For proving weak Carleson conditions, we can apply this lemma with
\alpha(Q)=\chi_{\mathscr{B}}(Q)
where
\mathscr{B}
is a bad collection of cubes which we want to show has a packing condition. If we can show that (
4.6
) holds for this choice of
\alpha
, then we will conclude

(4.7)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q\subseteq R\\
Q\in\mathscr{B}\end{subarray}}\ell(Q)^{n}=\sum_{Q\subseteq R}\chi_{\mathscr{B}}(Q)\ell(Q)^{n}\lesssim_{N,\eta}\ell(R)^{n}.

The theorem of this section is a tool for transferring weak Carleson conditions from
very big pieces
to those pieces’ approximated spaces.

Definition 4.9

(very big pieces)
.

Let
Z
be a metric space, let
X\subseteq Z
be Ahlfors
n
-regular, and let
\mathscr{Y}
be a class of Ahlfors
n
-regular subsets of
Z
. We say that
X\in\mathrm{VBP}(\mathscr{Y})
if for every
\epsilon>0
, there exists
C_{0}>0
such that for every
x\in X
and
0<r<\mathrm{diam}(X)
, there exists an Ahlfors
n
-regular space
Y\in\mathscr{Y}
with constant
C_{0}
such that

\mathscr{H}^{n}(B(x,r)\cap X\cap Y)\geq(1-\epsilon)\mathscr{H}^{n}(B(x,r)\cap X).

Theorem 4.10

(transference under VBP)
.

Let
X\subseteq Z
be Ahlfors
n
-regular and let
\mathscr{Y}
be a class of Ahlfors
n
-regular spaces in
Z
such that
X\in\mathrm{VBP}(\mathscr{Y})
. Suppose
\beta\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Co}(X)
is such that for any
Y\in\mathscr{Y}
, there exists a coefficient
\beta_{Y}\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Co}(Z)
such that
\beta_{Y}\in\mathrm{WC}(X)\cup\mathrm{WC}(Y)
with constant depending only on
n
, the regularity constants for
X
and
Y
, and the
\mathrm{VBP}
constant such that for any
Q\in\mathscr{D}(X)
,

(4.8)

\beta(c_{0}B_{Q})\lesssim\frac{\mathscr{H}^{n}(Q\setminus Y)}{\ell(Q)^{n}}+\beta_{Y}(c_{0}B_{Q}).

Then
\beta\in\mathrm{WC}(X)
.

Remark 4.11
.

If
Q
is such that
\mathscr{H}^{n}(Q\setminus Y)\gtrsim\ell(Q)^{n}
, then (
4.8
) is trivially satisfied and there is no need for
\beta_{Y}
near
Q
. Although we take
\beta_{Y}\in\mathrm{WC}(X)\cup\mathrm{WC}(Y)
for technical reasons, in practice
\beta_{Y}
is only used in places where
X
and
Y
have some overlap. One should think of
\beta_{Y}
as being a controlled coefficient on
Y
which only sees
Y\cap X
.

Proof of Theorem
4.10
.

Let
\mathscr{D}(X)
be a Christ-David lattice for
X
and fix
0<\epsilon^{\prime}<\epsilon
. By Lemma
4.5
, it suffices to show that the collection

\mathscr{B}_{\epsilon}=\{Q\in\mathscr{D}(X):\beta(c_{0}B_{Q})>\epsilon\}

is Carleson. By Lemma
4.8
, it further suffices to show that there exist
N,\eta>0
such that for all
R\in\mathscr{D}(X)
,

(4.9)

\mathscr{H}^{n}\left(\Set{x\in R}{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q\subseteq R\\
x\in Q\end{subarray}}\chi_{\mathscr{B}}(Q)\leq N}\right)\geq\eta\ell(R)^{n}.

Fix
R\in\mathscr{D}(X)
and let
Y\in\mathscr{Y}
be such that

\mathscr{H}^{n}(B_{R}\cap X\setminus Y)\leq\epsilon^{\prime}\mathscr{H}^{n}(R).

Lemma
4.7
implies that the set

\tilde{R}=\Set{x\in R}{\begin{array}[]{l}\text{For all $Q\in\mathscr{D}$ such that $x\in Q\subseteq R$, }\\
\mathscr{H}^{n}(Q\cap Y)\geq(1-2\epsilon^{\prime})\mathscr{H}^{n}(Q)\end{array}}

has
\mathscr{H}^{n}(\tilde{R})\geq\epsilon^{\prime}\mathscr{H}^{n}(R)
. Let
\mathscr{D}(\tilde{R})=\{Q\in\mathscr{D}(X):\exists x\in\tilde{R},\ x\in Q\subseteq R\}
. For any
Q\in\mathscr{D}(\tilde{R})
, we have that
c_{0}B_{Q}\cap X\subseteq Q
, so that

\mathscr{H}^{n}(c_{0}B_{Q}\cap X\setminus Y)\leq 2\epsilon^{\prime}\mathscr{H}^{n}(Q)\lesssim\epsilon^{\prime}\ell(Q)^{n}.

Assume first that
\beta_{Y}\in\mathrm{WC}(X)
satisfies (
4.8
) and define

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathscr{G}}

\displaystyle=\{Q\in\mathscr{D}(\tilde{R}):\beta_{Y}(c_{0}B_{Q})\leq\epsilon^{\prime}\},

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathscr{B}}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mathscr{D}}(R)\setminus\tilde{\mathscr{G}}.

Notice that for any
Q\in\tilde{\mathscr{G}}
, we have

\beta(c_{0}B_{Q})\lesssim\frac{\mathscr{H}^{n}(c_{0}B_{Q}\cap X\setminus Y)}{\ell(Q)^{n}}+\beta_{Y}(c_{0}B_{Q})\lesssim\epsilon^{\prime}+\epsilon^{\prime}\leq\epsilon

as long as
\epsilon^{\prime}
is small enough, implying that
\chi_{\mathscr{B}_{\epsilon}}(Q)\leq\chi_{\tilde{\mathscr{B}}}(Q)
for any
Q\in\tilde{\mathscr{D}}
. Therefore, for any
x\in\tilde{R}

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q\subseteq R\\
x\in Q\end{subarray}}\chi_{\mathscr{B}_{\epsilon}}(Q)\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q\subseteq R\\
x\in Q\end{subarray}}\chi_{\tilde{\mathscr{B}}}(Q)

This means that the left-hand side of (
4.9
) is bounded below by the same expression with
R
replaced by
\tilde{R}
and
\mathscr{B}
replaced by
\tilde{\mathscr{B}}
. We now focus on proving this version of (
4.9
). We can use Chebyshev’s inequality to estimate

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}^{n}\left(\Set{x\in\tilde{R}}{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q\subseteq R\\
x\in Q\end{subarray}}\chi_{\tilde{\mathscr{B}}}(Q)>N}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{N}\int_{\tilde{R}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q\subseteq R\\
x\in Q\end{subarray}}\chi_{\tilde{\mathscr{B}}}(Q)\lesssim\frac{1}{N}\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{D}(\tilde{R})\cap\tilde{\mathscr{B}}}\ell(Q)^{n}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q\subseteq R\\
\beta_{Y}(c_{0}B_{Q})>\epsilon^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\ell(Q)^{n}\lesssim\frac{1}{N}\ell(R)^{n}

where the final inequality follows from the fact that
\beta_{Y}
satisfies a weak Carleson condition. The result follows by taking
N
sufficiently large since the left hand side of (
4.9
) is bounded below by

\mathscr{H}^{n}(\tilde{R})-\frac{C}{N}\ell(R)^{n}\geq\epsilon\mathscr{H}^{n}(R)-\frac{C}{N}\ell(R)^{n}\gtrsim_{\epsilon}\ell(R)^{n}.

This completes the proof in the case
\beta_{Y}\in\mathrm{WC}(X)
. If instead
\beta_{Y}\in\mathrm{WC}(Y)
, the only additional complication is in showing
\tilde{\mathscr{B}}
is Carleson. This follows because for each
Q\in\mathscr{D}(X)
with
c_{0}B_{Q}\cap Y\not=\varnothing
, there exists
Q^{\prime}\in\mathscr{D}(Y)
with
\ell(Q^{\prime})\lesssim\ell(Q)
and
5B_{Q^{\prime}}\supseteq B_{Q}
where the mapping
Q\mapsto Q^{\prime}
is at most
C
to one with
C
depending on
n
and the regularity constant for
Y
. The fact that
\tilde{\mathscr{B}}
is Carleson now follows from the fact that
\beta_{Y}(c_{0}B_{Q})\lesssim\beta_{Y}(10B_{Q^{\prime}})
and the Carleson condition for
\beta_{Y}
on
Y
.
∎

5.
Oscillation of means

In this section, we introduce the notion of a sandwichable family of sets, review necessary facts about dyadic decompositions of
L^{2}
functions, and prove Lemma
5.12
, one of our main tools for the proof of Theorem
A
.

Definition 5.1
.

We follow the presentation of
[
Tol12
]
. Given
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
Q\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, define

\Delta_{Q}h(x)=\begin{cases}\fint_{P}h(z)dz-\fint_{Q}h(z)dz&\text{ if $x\in P$, where $P$ is a child of $Q$},\\
0&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}

If
h\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, then

h=\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\Delta_{Q}h\quad\text{ and }\quad h\chi_{Q}=\fint_{Q}h+\sum_{R\subseteq Q}\Delta_{R}h

where the sums converge in
L^{2}
and
\langle\Delta_{Q}h,\Delta_{Q^{\prime}}h\rangle_{L^{2}}=0
when
Q\not=Q^{\prime}
so that
\|h\|_{2}=\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}}\|\Delta_{Q}h\|_{2}^{2}
. One can view
\Delta_{Q}h
as a projection of
h
onto the subspace of
L^{2}
formed by the Haar wavelets
h_{Q}^{\epsilon},\ \epsilon\in\{0,1\}^{n}\setminus\{(0,0,\ldots,0)\}
associated to
Q
.

We now use the wavelet-like decomposition of
h
to define coefficients
\Delta_{k}^{h}(Q)
which, roughly speaking, measure the variation in means of
h
from
Q
through to its
k
-th generation descendants.

Definition 5.2
.

For any
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
Q\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and
h\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, define

(5.1)

\Delta_{k}^{h}(Q)^{2}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\sum_{R\in\mathcal{D}_{j}(Q)}\|\Delta_{R}h\|_{2}^{2}.

Remark 5.3

(Properties of
\Delta_{k}^{h}
)
.

Notice that if
h\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, then
\Delta^{h}_{k}
has a type of strong Carleson condition since for any
Q_{0}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

\sum_{Q\subseteq Q_{0}}\Delta^{h}_{k}(Q)^{2}=\sum_{Q\subseteq Q_{0}}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\sum_{R\in\mathcal{D}_{j}(Q)}\|\Delta_{R}h\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim_{k,n}\sum_{R\subseteq Q_{0}}\|\Delta_{R}h\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim_{\|h\|_{\infty}}\ell(Q_{0})^{n}.

This implies the weak Carleson condition

(5.2)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q\subseteq Q_{0}\\
\Delta_{k}^{h}(Q)>\delta\ell(Q)^{n/2}\end{subarray}}\ell(Q)^{n}\lesssim_{\delta}\sum_{Q\subseteq Q_{0}}\Delta_{k}^{h}(Q)^{2}\lesssim_{k,n,\|h\|_{\infty}}\ell(Q_{0})^{n}.

\Delta^{h}_{k}
also scales appropriately in the following manner: Let
Q,\tilde{Q}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and let
T:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the affine map sending
\tilde{Q}
onto
Q
by

(5.3)

T(x)=x_{Q}+\left(\frac{x-x_{\tilde{Q}}}{\ell(\tilde{Q})}\right)\ell(Q)

where
x_{\tilde{Q}}
is the center of
\tilde{Q}
. Let
\tilde{h}\in L^{2}(\tilde{Q})
and set
h=\tilde{h}\circ T^{-1}
. Notice that

\displaystyle\|h\|_{L^{2}(Q)}^{2}=\int_{T(\tilde{Q})}(\tilde{h}\circ T^{-1})^{2}=\int_{\tilde{Q}}\tilde{h}^{2}\frac{\ell(Q)^{n}}{\ell(\tilde{Q})^{n}}=\frac{\ell(Q)^{n}}{\ell(\tilde{Q})^{n}}\|\tilde{h}\|_{2}^{2}.

Similarly, notice that if
V\subseteq Q
and
\tilde{V}\subseteq\tilde{Q}
with
T(\tilde{V})=V
, then

\displaystyle\|\Delta_{V}h\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{V}(\Delta_{V}h(x))^{2}dx=\int_{T(\tilde{V})}(\Delta_{\tilde{V}}\tilde{h}(T^{-1}(x)))^{2}dx

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\ell(Q)}{\ell(\tilde{Q})}\right)^{n}\int_{\tilde{V}}(\Delta_{\tilde{V}}\tilde{h})^{2}dx=\left(\frac{\ell(Q)}{\ell(\tilde{Q})}\right)^{n}\|\Delta_{\tilde{V}}\tilde{h}\|_{2}^{2}

which gives
\Delta_{k}^{h}(Q)^{2}=\left(\frac{\ell(Q)}{\ell(\tilde{Q})}\right)^{n}\Delta_{k}^{\tilde{h}}(\tilde{Q})^{2}
.

The
\Delta_{k}^{h}
coefficients are useful for controlling the oscillation of averages of
h
over certain subsets in the domain. The most abstract families of subsets which we control averages over here are called
\triangle
-compact families.

Definition 5.4

(
d_{\triangle},\ \triangle
-compactness)
.

Define a function
d_{\triangle}
on pairs of Lebesgue measurable subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
by

d_{\triangle}(A,B)=\mathscr{L}(A\triangle B).

It is well-known that
d_{\triangle}
is a pseudometric which becomes a metric on equivalence classes of subsets formed by the relation
A\sim B\iff\mathscr{L}(A\triangle B)=0
. We say that a family
\mathscr{E}
of
\mathscr{L}
-measurable subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is
\triangle
-compact
if it is subsequentially compact with respect to
d_{\triangle}
. That is, for every sequence
E_{j}\in\mathscr{E}
, there exists a subsequence
E_{j_{k}}
such that there exist a
\mathscr{L}
-measurable
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
d_{\triangle}(E_{j_{k}},E)\rightarrow 0
as
k\rightarrow\infty
.

Roughy speaking, any class of
\triangle
-compact sets with Lebesgue measure bounded from below have a subsequence whose measure concentrates around some limit set. This definition arises in our context because for any such concentrating sequence one can “exchange” integrals over any
E_{j_{k}},E_{j_{m}}
for large
k
and
m
with small cost. It will actually be most convenient to introduce another class of sets that is equivalent to the class of
\triangle
-compact sets (see Lemma
5.6
) but that more directly lays out the “concentration” properties we want for a well-chosen subsequence in the proof of Lemma
5.11
.

Definition 5.5

(sandwichable families)
.

Let
L\geq 1
and let
\mathscr{E}_{L}
be a family of
\mathscr{L}
-measurable subsets of
[0,1]^{n}
with
\mathscr{L}(E)\gtrsim_{L,n}1
for all
E\in\mathscr{E}_{L}
.
We say that the family
\mathscr{E}_{L}
is
sandwichable
if for every sequence
E_{j}\in\mathscr{E}_{L}
, there exists a subsequence
E_{j_{k}}
with the following property: There exist families of “lower” subsets
\{L_{\epsilon}\}_{0<\epsilon<\frac{1}{2}},
and “upper” subsets
\{U_{\epsilon}\}_{0<\epsilon<\frac{1}{2}}
such that:

(1)

For all
0<\epsilon_{0}<\frac{1}{2}
, there exists
k_{0}>0
such that for all
k\geq k_{0}
, we have
L_{\epsilon}\subseteq E_{j_{k}}\subseteq U_{\epsilon}
,

(2)

For any
\epsilon^{\prime}<\epsilon
,
L_{\epsilon}\subseteq L_{\epsilon^{\prime}}
and
U_{\epsilon^{\prime}}\subseteq U_{\epsilon}
,

(3)

\frac{\mathscr{L}(L_{\epsilon})}{\mathscr{L}(U_{\epsilon})}\geq 1-\epsilon
.

We also call such a sequence
sandwichable
. For any given
Q\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, let
T_{Q}
be the affine map sending
[0,1]^{n}
onto
Q
and define

\mathscr{E}_{L}(Q)=T_{Q}(\mathscr{E}_{L}).

The motivating example of a sandwichable or family is that of
L
bi-Lipschitz images of a fixed
n
-dimensional Euclidean ball (see Lemma
5.8
). The results involving sandwichable families that follow are more general than what is necessary for our applications (it would suffice to consider only the family of normed balls
L
-bi-Lipschitz to a fixed Euclidean ball), but we believe these more general results could be of independent interest. We now show this definition is equivalent to the more intuitive
\triangle
-compactness we introduced first.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
L>0
and let
\mathscr{E}_{L}
be a family of
\mathscr{L}
-measurable subsets of
[0,1]^{n}
such that
\mathscr{L}(E)\gtrsim_{L,n}1
for all
E\in\mathscr{E}_{L}
. The family
\mathscr{E}_{L}
is sandwichable if and only if
\mathscr{E}_{L}
is
\triangle
-compact.

Proof.

First, suppose
\mathscr{E}_{L}
is sandwichable and let
E_{j}\in\mathscr{E}_{L}
be a sandiwchable sequence. Define
E=\bigcap_{0<\epsilon<1/2}U_{\epsilon}
. We want to show that
d_{\triangle}(E_{j},E)\rightarrow 0
. Fix
\epsilon_{0}>0
and choose
j_{0}>0
so that property
1
is satisfied for
\epsilon_{0}
. Then for any
k\geq j_{0}

\displaystyle d_{\triangle}(E_{k},E)

\displaystyle=\mathscr{L}(E_{k}\triangle E)=\mathscr{L}\left(E_{k}\setminus\bigcap_{0<\epsilon<1/2}U_{\epsilon}\right)+\mathscr{L}\left(\bigcap_{\epsilon>0}U_{\epsilon}\setminus E_{k}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 0+\mathscr{L}\left(U_{\epsilon_{0}}\setminus E_{k}\right)\leq\mathscr{L}(U_{\epsilon_{0}}\setminus L_{\epsilon_{0}})

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon_{0}\mathscr{L}(U_{\epsilon_{0}})\leq\epsilon_{0}.

Now, let
\mathscr{E}_{L}
be sub-sequentially compact with respect to
d_{\triangle}
. Let
E_{j}
be a subsequence with limit
E
such that
\mathscr{L}(E_{j}\triangle E)\leq 2^{-j}
and fix
\epsilon>0
. Let
j_{0}
be the smallest integer such that
2^{-j_{0}}\leq c\epsilon
for
0<c<1
to be determined later. Define

\displaystyle L_{\epsilon}

\displaystyle=\bigcap_{j\geq j_{0}}E_{j},

\displaystyle U_{\epsilon}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{j\geq j_{0}}E_{j}.

It is immediate that
L_{\epsilon}
and
U_{\epsilon}
satisfy properties
1
and
2
in the definition of sandwichable. To verify the final property, notice that

\displaystyle E\triangle U_{\epsilon}=\bigcup_{j\geq j_{0}}E\triangle E_{j}

and

\displaystyle E\triangle L_{\epsilon}

\displaystyle=\left(E\setminus\bigcap_{j\geq j_{0}}E_{j}\right)\cup\left(\bigcap_{j\geq j_{0}}E_{j}\setminus E\right)\subseteq\left(\bigcup_{j\geq j_{0}}E\setminus E_{j}\right)\cup(E_{j_{0}}\setminus E).

These imply

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}(U_{\epsilon}\setminus L_{\epsilon})

\displaystyle\leq d_{\triangle}(U_{\epsilon},L_{\epsilon})\leq d_{\triangle}(U_{\epsilon},E)+d_{\triangle}(E,L_{\epsilon})=\mathscr{L}(E\triangle U_{\epsilon})+\mathscr{L}(E\triangle L_{\epsilon})

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{j\geq j_{0}}\mathscr{L}(E\triangle E_{j})\right)+\left(\sum_{j\geq j_{0}}\mathscr{L}(E\triangle E_{j})+\mathscr{L}(E_{j_{0}}\triangle E)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j\geq j_{0}}2^{-j}+2\sum_{j\geq j_{0}}2^{-j}\lesssim 2^{-j_{0}}\leq c\epsilon.

Because
\mathscr{L}(E)\gtrsim_{L,n}1
we have
\mathscr{L}(L_{\epsilon})\gtrsim_{L,n}1
for small enough
\epsilon
, and property
3
follows upon choosing
c
small enough.
∎

We now aim to show, as promised, that bi-Lipschitz images of Euclidean balls are sandwichable.

Definition 5.7
.

Let
L\geq 1
and define

\mathrm{BiLip}_{L}=\{E\subseteq[0,1]^{n}:\exists f:B(0,1)\twoheadrightarrow E,\ f\text{ $L$-bi-Lipschitz}\}.

Lemma 5.8
.

\mathrm{BiLip}_{L}
is a sandwichable family.

Proof.

Let
E_{j}\in\mathrm{BiLip}_{L}(Q)
and
\epsilon>0
. By Arzela-Ascoli, the corresponding family
f_{j}:B(0,\ell(Q))\twoheadrightarrow E_{j}
of
L
-bi-Lipschitz maps has a convergent subsequence in the sup norm which we relabel as
f_{j}
. Let
f:B(0,\ell(Q))\twoheadrightarrow E
be the
L
-bi-Lipschitz limit mapping and define

\displaystyle L_{\epsilon}

\displaystyle=\{y\in E:\mathrm{dist}(y,\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus E)\geq\epsilon\ell(Q)\},

\displaystyle U_{\epsilon}

\displaystyle=\{y\in Q:\mathrm{dist}(y,E)\leq\epsilon\ell(Q)\}.

Now, let
k_{0}
be such that
\lVert f_{k}-f\rVert_{\infty}<\frac{\epsilon}{5}\ell(Q)
for all
k\geq k_{0}
. Then for any
y=f_{k}(x)\in E_{k}
, we have that

\mathrm{dist}(y,E)\leq|f_{k}(x)-f(x)|\leq\frac{\epsilon}{5}\ell(Q)

so that
E_{k}\subseteq U_{\epsilon}
. Similarly, for any
y=f(x)\in L_{\epsilon}
let
y^{\prime}=f_{k}(x^{\prime})\in f_{k}(\partial B)
such that
\mathrm{dist}(y,f_{k}(\partial B))=|y-y^{\prime}|
. Then

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}(y,f_{k}(\partial B))

\displaystyle=|y-y^{\prime}|\geq|f(x)-f(x^{\prime})|-|f(x^{\prime})-f_{k}(x^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{dist}(y,\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus E)-\frac{\epsilon}{5}\ell(Q)\geq\frac{4\epsilon}{5}\ell(Q).

Since
f_{k}(\partial B)
separates
\mathbb{R}^{n}
into the two components
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus E_{k}
and
\mathring{E_{k}}
, the fact that
\mathrm{dist}(y,E_{k})\leq|f_{k}(x)-f(x)|\leq\frac{\epsilon}{5}\ell(Q)
combined with the above displayed equation implies
y\in E_{k}
. This shows
L_{\epsilon}\subseteq E_{k}
. We now need to estimate
\frac{\mathscr{L}(L_{\epsilon})}{\mathscr{L}(U_{\epsilon})}
. Let
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
be an
\epsilon\ell(Q)
-net for
f(\partial B)
and observe that
U_{\epsilon}\setminus L_{\epsilon}\subseteq N_{\epsilon\ell(Q)}(f(\partial B))\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}B(x_{i},2\epsilon)
. Using the Ahlfors
n-1
-regularity of
f(\partial B)
and the disjointness of
{B(x_{i},\epsilon\ell(Q)/4)}_{i=1}^{N}
, we get

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}(U_{\epsilon}\setminus L_{\epsilon})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathscr{L}(B(x_{i},2\epsilon\ell(Q)))\lesssim_{L,n}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\epsilon\ell(Q)\mathscr{H}^{n-1}(B(x_{i},\epsilon\ell(Q)/4)\cap f(\partial B))

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon\ell(Q)\mathscr{H}^{n-1}(f(\partial B))\lesssim_{L,n}\epsilon\ell(Q)^{n}.

From which we conclude
\frac{\mathscr{L}(L_{\epsilon})}{\mathscr{L}(U_{\epsilon})}\geq 1-C(L,n)\epsilon
using that
\mathscr{L}(L_{\epsilon})\gtrsim_{L,n}\ell(Q)^{n}
.
∎

We will need to control averages over general bi-Lipschitz images of balls as well as over subclasses of normed balls.

Definition 5.9

(normed balls)
.

Given
L>0
, we define the set of norms on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
which are
L
-bi-Lipschitz to the Euclidean norm by

\mathcal{N}_{L}=\{\|\cdot\|\ :\ L^{-1}\|x\|\leq|x|\leq L\|x\|\}.

We define a collection of
L
-bi-Lipschitz normed balls inside
[0,1]^{n}
by

\mathcal{B}_{L}=\{B_{\|\cdot\|}(x,r)\subseteq[0,1]^{n}\ :\ \|\cdot\|\in\mathcal{N}_{L},\ r\geq L^{-1}\}.

Remark 5.10
.

The fact that balls in
\mathcal{B}_{L}
are uniformly bi-Lipschitz to
B(0,1)
implies that
\mathcal{B}_{L}
is a sandwichable family by Lemma
5.8
.

The following lemma gives our main tool for controlling averages of
L^{2}
functions.

Lemma 5.11
.

Let
L\geq 1
and let
\mathscr{E}_{L}
be a sandwichable family. For all
\epsilon,M>0,\ n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exist
k(\epsilon,M,n,\mathscr{E}_{L})\in\mathbb{N}
and
\delta(\epsilon,M,n,\mathscr{E}_{L})>0
such that the following holds:
Suppose
h\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
Q\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
are such that

(1)

h\geq 0

\mathscr{L}^{n}
-a.e.,

(2)

\|h\|_{L^{2}(Q)}^{2}\leq M\ell(Q)^{n}
,

(3)

\Delta_{k}^{h}(Q)^{2}\leq\delta\ell(Q)^{n}
.

Then, for any
E\in\mathscr{E}_{L}(Q)
, we have

(5.4)

\left|\fint_{E}h-\fint_{Q}h\right|\leq\epsilon.

Proof.

Suppose the conclusion of the lemma is false. Then there exist
\epsilon,M,L,n
and a sequence of maps
\tilde{h}_{j}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and cubes
\tilde{Q}_{j}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
\tilde{h}_{j}\geq 0
,
\|\tilde{h}_{j}\|_{2}\leq M\ell(Q_{j})^{n}
, and subsets
\tilde{E}_{j}\in\mathscr{E}_{L}(\tilde{Q}_{j})
so that (
5.4
) does not hold for
\tilde{h}_{j},\tilde{Q}_{j},\tilde{E}_{j}
, yet
\Delta_{j}^{\tilde{h}_{j}}(\tilde{Q}_{j})\leq\frac{1}{j}\ell(\tilde{Q}_{j})^{n/2}
. For any
j\in\mathbb{N}
, let
T_{j}
be the affine transformation sending
\tilde{Q}_{j}
onto
Q=[0,1]^{n}
as in (
5.3
) and define
h_{j}:Q\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as in Remark
5.3
by

h_{j}=\tilde{h}_{j}\circ T_{j}^{-1}.

It follows from Remark
5.3
that
\|h_{j}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}^{2}\leq M
and
\Delta_{j}^{h_{j}}(Q)^{2}\leq\frac{1}{j}
. We also define
E_{j}=T_{j}(\tilde{E}_{j})\in\mathscr{E}_{L}(Q)
to be the appropriately translated and scaled copy of
\tilde{E}_{j}
.

By the weak compactness of bounded closed balls in
L^{2}
, there exists some
h\in L^{2}(Q)
such that
h_{j}\rightharpoonup h
in
L^{2}
for some subsequence of
h_{j}
. By further refining subsequences we can further assume the subsequence
E_{j}\in\mathscr{E}_{L}
is sandwichable. Let
c=\int_{Q}h
and let
c_{j}=\int_{Q}h_{j}
.

We will first show that
h=c
by showing that
\Delta_{V}h=0
for all
V\subseteq Q
. By weak convergence we have

(5.5)

c_{j}=\int_{Q}h_{j}\rightarrow\int_{Q}h=c.

Write
h_{j}=c_{j}+\sum_{R\subseteq Q}\Delta_{R}h_{j}
and
h=c+\sum_{R\subseteq Q}\Delta_{R}h
. Fix
V\subseteq Q
and observe

\displaystyle\int_{Q}h_{j}\Delta_{V}h

\displaystyle=\int_{Q}\left(c_{j}+\sum_{R\subseteq Q}\Delta_{R}h_{j}\right)\Delta_{V}h=c_{j}\int_{Q}\Delta_{V}h+\sum_{R\subseteq Q}\langle\Delta_{R}h_{j},\Delta_{V}h\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\Delta_{V}h_{j},\Delta_{V}h\rangle.

where the final equality follows since
\langle\Delta_{R}f_{1},\Delta_{V}f_{2}\rangle=0
whenever

and

. Similarly, we have

\displaystyle\int_{Q}h\Delta_{V}h=\langle h,\Delta_{V}h\rangle=\langle\Delta_{V}h,\Delta_{V}h\rangle=\|\Delta_{V}h\|_{2}^{2}

Using weak convergence again, we get

\displaystyle\langle\Delta_{V}h_{j},\Delta_{V}h\rangle=\int_{Q}h_{j}\Delta_{V}h\rightarrow\int_{Q}h\Delta_{V}h=\|\Delta_{V}h\|_{2}^{2}.

Using Cauchy-Schwarz, we can now conclude that
\|\Delta_{V}h\|_{2}\leq\lim_{j}\|\Delta_{V}h_{j}\|_{2}
. We claim that
\|\Delta_{V}h\|_{2}=0
. Indeed, if

is sufficiently large, then both
V\in\mathcal{D}_{j^{\prime}}(Q)
for some
j^{\prime}\leq j
and
\|\Delta_{V}h\|_{2}\leq 2\|\Delta_{V}h_{j}\|_{2}
. This means

\|\Delta_{V}h\|_{2}^{2}\leq 4\|\Delta_{V}h_{j}\|^{2}\leq 4\sum_{k=0}^{j}\sum_{R\in\mathcal{D}_{k}(Q)}\|\Delta_{R}h_{j}\|_{2}^{2}=4\Delta_{j}^{h_{j}}(Q)\leq\frac{4}{j}.

for all large
j
. This shows that
\Delta_{V}h=0
for all
V\subseteq Q
, hence

as desired.

We will now show how this leads to a contradiction. Let
\eta>0
be small, let the families
\{L_{\epsilon^{\prime}}\}_{\epsilon^{\prime}},\{U_{\epsilon^{\prime}}\}_{\epsilon^{\prime}}
satisfy the sandwichable property for the sequence
E_{j}
, and let
j_{0}>0
be such that
L_{\eta}\subseteq E_{j}\subseteq U_{\eta}
for all
j\geq j_{0}
. Using the fact that
h_{j}\geq 0
, for all
j\geq j_{0}

(5.6)

\int_{L_{\eta}}h_{j}\leq\int_{E_{j}}h_{j}\leq\int_{U_{\eta}}h_{j}

so that

\frac{\mathscr{L}(L_{\eta})}{\mathscr{L}(E_{j})}\fint_{L_{\eta}}h_{j}-\fint_{Q}h_{j}\leq\fint_{E_{j}}h_{j}-\fint_{Q}h_{j}\leq\frac{\mathscr{L}(U_{\eta})}{\mathscr{L}(E_{j})}\fint_{U_{\eta}}h_{j}-\fint_{Q}h_{j}.

Because
\mathscr{L}(L_{\eta})\leq\mathscr{L}(E_{j})\leq\mathscr{L}(U_{\eta})
and
\frac{\mathscr{L}(U_{\eta})}{\mathscr{L}(L_{\eta})}\leq(1+2\eta)
for small enough
\eta
, we can assume without loss of generality that

\limsup_{j}\left|\fint_{E_{j}}h_{j}-\fint_{Q}h_{j}\right|\leq\limsup_{j}\left|\frac{\mathscr{L}(U_{\eta})}{\mathscr{L}(L_{\eta})}\fint_{U_{\eta}}h_{j}-\fint_{Q}h_{j}\right|\leq\left|(1+c^{\prime}\eta)c-c\right|\lesssim_{n}\eta c.

Since this holds for all

, we get
\limsup_{j}\left|\fint_{E_{j}}h_{j}-\fint_{Q}h_{j}\right|=0
. On the other hand, by hypothesis

\left|\fint_{E_{j}}h_{j}-\fint_{Q}h_{j}\right|>\epsilon

for all
j
. This gives a contradiction.
∎

|\mathscr{H}^{n}(g(B\cap P))-c_{P,Q}\mathscr{L}(B\cap P)|\leq\delta\mathscr{L}(B).

Applying this inequality to the ball
B_{2}
and using (
6.4
), this implies the existence of a constant
c_{2}(n,L)
so that

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}^{n}(B(y,r)\cap E)

\displaystyle\leq\mathscr{H}^{n}(g(B_{2}\cap P))\leq(c_{P,Q}+\delta)\mathscr{L}(B_{2})\leq(c_{P,Q}+\delta)c_{\|\cdot\|_{Q}}(1+c_{1}\delta)^{n}r^{n}

\displaystyle\leq a_{Q}r^{n}+c_{2}\delta\ell(Q)^{n}.

A similar computation using
\mathscr{H}^{n}(g(B_{1}\cap P))
gives a similar lower bound for
\mathscr{H}^{n}(B(y,r)\cap E)
. This shows that

\mathrm{Osc}_{E}(Q)\ell(Q)^{n}\leq\left|\mathscr{H}^{n}(B(y,r)\cap E)-a_{Q}r^{n}\right|\leq c_{2}(n,L)\delta\ell(Q)^{n}<\epsilon\ell(Q)^{n}

where the final inequality follows by choosing
\delta
small enough.
∎

Proposition 6.18
.

For any
\mathscr{H}^{n}
-measurable subset
E\subseteq\Sigma
,
\mathrm{Osc}_{E}\in\mathrm{WC}(\Sigma)
. In particular,
\Sigma
satisfies the WCD.

Proof.

Let
\epsilon>0
. By Lemma
6.17
, there exist
\delta>0
such that
Q\in\mathscr{G}(\delta)\implies\mathrm{Osc}_{E}(Q)<\epsilon
. Therefore,
\mathrm{Osc}_{E}(Q)\geq\epsilon\implies Q\in\mathscr{B}(\delta)
. But Lemma
6.16
shows that
\mathscr{B}(\delta)
is Carleson, implying
\mathrm{Osc}_{E}\in\mathrm{WC}(\Sigma)
. When
E=\Sigma
, this is equivalent to the WCD on
\Sigma
by Remark
6.10
.
∎

Theorem 6.19
.

Any uniformly
n
-rectifiable metric space
X
satisfies the WCD.

Proof.

We plan to use Theorem
4.10
. Without loss of generality, assume
X\subseteq\ell_{\infty}
and recall that Theorem
6.7
implies that
X\in\mathrm{VBP}(\mathscr{S})
, so Theorem
4.10
implies that it suffices to show that for any
\Sigma\in\mathscr{S}
there exists
\beta_{\Sigma}\in\mathrm{WC}(\Sigma)
such that
\mathrm{Osc}
satisfies (
4.8
). We claim
\mathrm{Osc}_{X\cap\Sigma}
is such a coefficient. Indeed, Lemma
6.11
(with Remark
6.12
) implies that
\mathrm{Osc}_{X\cap\Sigma}\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Co}(\ell_{\infty})
and Lemma
6.13
implies that for any
Q\in\mathscr{D}(X)

(6.6)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Osc}\left(\frac{c_{0}}{2}B_{Q}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\mathscr{H}^{n}(Q\setminus\Sigma)}{\ell(Q)^{n}}+\mathrm{Osc}_{\Sigma\cap X}\left(\frac{c_{0}}{2}B_{Q}\right).

By taking

, Proposition
6.18
implies
\mathrm{Osc}_{X\cap\Sigma}\in\mathrm{WC}(\Sigma)
.
∎

6.3.
Alpha numbers in Ahlfors
n
-regular metric spaces

In this section, we define a metric space variant of Tolsa’s alpha numbers and prove Theorem
B
, characterizing uniformly rectifiable metric spaces in terms of a weak Carleson condition for
\alpha
.

6.3.1.
Preliminaries with metric alpha numbers

Definition 6.20

(distances between measures)
.

Let
Z
be a metric space and fix finite Borel measures
\mu,\nu
in
Z
. Given a ball
B=B(x,r)\subseteq Z
, we define

(6.7)

\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(B)=\{f:Z\rightarrow\mathbb{R}:\mathrm{Lip}(f)\leq 1,\ \forall z\in Z\ |f(z)|\leq\max\{0,r-\mathrm{dist}(z,x)\}\}.

The second condition in (
6.7
) is similar to requiring
\mathrm{spt}(f)\subseteq B
, but ensures the correct decay in
|f(z)|
as
z
nears the boundary of
Z
. In the case
Z=\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\mathrm{spt}(f)\subseteq B
implies this condition, but this implication does not hold in general because
Z
could have holes. We also define

\mathrm{dist}_{B}(\mu,\nu)=\sup\left\{\int fd\mu-\int fd\nu:f\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(B)\right\}.

Definition 6.21

(metric alpha numbers)
.

Let
X
be an Ahlfors
n
-regular metric space and let
x\in X,0<r<\mathrm{diam}(X)
. We define
\alpha:X\times[0,\mathrm{diam}(X))\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

\alpha_{X}(x,r)=\frac{1}{r^{n+1}}\inf_{c\geq 0,\|\cdot\|,Z}\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\iota_{1}:B(x,r)\rightarrow Z\\
\iota_{2}:(\mathbb{R}^{n},\|\cdot\|)\rightarrow Z\end{subarray}}\mathrm{dist}_{B(\iota_{1}(x),r)}\left(\iota_{1}\#[\mathscr{H}^{n}_{X}],c\iota_{2}\#[\mathscr{H}^{n}_{\|\cdot\|}]\right)

where
\|\cdot\|
is a norm on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
Z
ranges over all complete, separable metric spaces, and
\iota_{1},\iota_{2}
range over all isometric embeddings of
B(x,r)
and
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\|\cdot\|)
respectively into
Z
. For any
\mathscr{H}^{n}
-measurable subset
E\subseteq X
, we also define

\alpha_{E,X}(x,r)=\frac{1}{r^{n+1}}\inf_{c\geq 0,\|\cdot\|,Z}\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\iota_{1}:B(x,r)\rightarrow Z\\
\iota_{2}:(\mathbb{R}^{n},\|\cdot\|)\rightarrow Z\end{subarray}}\mathrm{dist}_{B(\iota_{1}(x),r)}\left(\iota_{1}\#[\mathscr{H}^{n}_{E}],c\iota_{2}\#[\mathscr{H}^{n}_{\|\cdot\|}]\right)

where the infima range over the same parameters as in the definition of
\alpha
. It is a minor, but important technical detail for the proof of Lemma
6.23
that we do not take the infimum of
\iota_{1}
over the a priori larger family of isometric embeddings
\iota:B(x,r)\cap E\rightarrow Z
.

We note that
\alpha_{E,X}(x,r)=0
if
\mathscr{H}^{n}(E\cap B(x,r))=0
. We will often abuse notation and drop the iotas, identifying subsets of
X
and
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with their isometric images in
Z
. For any Christ-David lattice
\mathscr{D}(X)
and
Q\in\mathscr{D}(X)
, define

\alpha_{E,X}(Q)=\alpha_{E,X}(5B_{Q}).

For any
Q\in\mathscr{D}(X)
, let
c_{Q},Z_{Q},\iota_{1}^{Q},\iota_{2}^{Q},\|\cdot\|_{Q}
denote some parameters almost minimizing
\alpha_{E,X}(Q)
in the sense that

(6.8)

\alpha_{E,X}(Q)\ell(Q)^{n+1}\geq\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{dist}_{B(\iota_{1}^{Q}(x_{Q}),\ell(Q))}(\iota_{1}^{Q}\#[\mathscr{H}^{n}_{E}],c_{Q}\iota_{2}^{Q}\#[\mathscr{H}^{n}_{\|\cdot\|_{Q}}]).

To shorten formulas, we will often drop the isometric embeddings
\iota_{1},\iota_{2}
. We also define

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}_{Q}

\displaystyle=\iota_{2}^{Q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\subseteq Z_{Q},

\displaystyle E_{Q}

\displaystyle=\iota_{1}^{Q}(E\cap 5B_{Q})\subseteq Z_{Q},

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}^{n}_{E_{Q}}

\displaystyle=\iota_{1}^{Q}\#[\mathscr{H}^{n}_{E}],

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}^{n}_{\|\cdot\|_{Q}}

\displaystyle=\iota_{2}^{Q}\#[\mathscr{H}^{n}_{\|\cdot\|_{Q}}].

Lemma 6.22
.

Let
E\subseteq X
be
\mathscr{H}^{n}
-measurable. For any
x\in X
and
0<r<\mathrm{diam}(X)
, we have

\alpha_{X}(x,r)\leq\frac{\mathscr{H}^{n}(B(x,r)\setminus E)}{r^{n}}+\alpha_{E,X}(x,r)

Proof.

Notice that for any fixed
c,\|\cdot\|,Z,\iota_{1},\iota_{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}_{B}(\mathscr{H}^{n}_{X},c\mathscr{H}^{n}_{\|\cdot\|})

\displaystyle=\sup_{f\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(B(\iota_{1}(x),r))}\left|\int fd\mathscr{H}^{n}_{X}-c\int fd\mathscr{H}^{n}_{\|\cdot\|}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{f\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(B(\iota_{1}(x),r))}\left|\int_{X\cap B\setminus E}fd\mathscr{H}^{n}_{X}\right|+\left|\int_{E\cap B}fd\mathscr{H}^{n}_{X}-c\int fd\mathscr{H}^{n}_{\|\cdot\|}\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim r\mathscr{H}^{n}(X\cap B\setminus E)+\mathrm{dist}_{B}(\mathscr{H}^{n}_{E},c\mathscr{H}^{n}_{\|\cdot\|}).

The result follows by dividing both sides by
r^{n+1}
and taking infimums.
∎

Lemma 6.23
.

Let
X
be an Ahlfors
n
-regular metric space, and let
E\subseteq X
be
\mathscr{H}^{n}
-measurable. We have
\alpha_{E,X}\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Co}(X)
.

Proof.

Let
x,y\in X
and
t<r
be such that
B(y,t)\subseteq B(x,r)
. Fix any metric space
Z
, norm
\|\cdot\|
, constant
c>0
, and let
\iota_{1}:B(x,r)\rightarrow Z
and
\iota_{2}:(\mathbb{R}^{n},\|\cdot\|)\rightarrow Z
be any isometric embeddings. For any
f\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(B(\iota_{1}(y),t))
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{t^{n+1}}\int fd\mathscr{H}^{n}_{E}-c\int fd\mathscr{H}^{n}_{\|\cdot\|}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{r^{n+1}}{t^{n+1}}\frac{1}{r^{n+1}}\mathrm{dist}_{B(\iota_{1}(x),r)}(\mathscr{H}^{n}_{E},c\mathscr{H}^{n}_{\|\cdot\|})

where the final line follows because every
f\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(B(\iota_{1}(y),t))
can be extended to
\tilde{f}\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(B(\iota_{1}(x),r))
by setting
\tilde{f}=0
in
B(\iota_{1}(x),r)\setminus B(\iota_{1}(y),t)
using the second condition in (
6.7
). By taking infimums, we get
\alpha_{E,X}(y,t)\leq\left(\frac{r}{t}\right)^{n+1}\alpha_{E,X}(x,r)
.
∎

Remark 6.24
.

If
Z
is a metric space such that
X\subseteq Z
, one can naturally view
\alpha_{E,X}
as a function from
Z\times\mathbb{R}^{+}
to
\mathbb{R}
defined on
(z,t)
by replacing
E
above with
B(z,t)\cap E
and measuring
\mathrm{dist}
inside
B(\iota_{1}(x),2r)
for any
x\in E\cap B(z,t)
. A similar proof then shows
\alpha_{E,X}\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Co}(Z)
.

Lemma 6.25

(cf.
[
Tol09
]
Lemma 3.1)
.

Let
X
be an Ahlfors
n
-regular metric space, and let
E\subseteq X
be
\mathscr{H}^{n}
-measurable. There exists
c_{1}>0
such that for any
Q\in\mathscr{D}(X)
, if
\mathscr{H}^{n}(E\cap c_{0}B_{Q})\gtrsim\ell(Q)^{n}
and
\alpha_{E,X}(Q)\leq c_{1}
then the following hold:

(1)

\mathbb{R}^{n}_{Q}\cap B(x_{Q},\frac{1}{5}\ell(Q))\not=\varnothing
,
and

(2)

c_{Q}\asymp 1
.

Proof.

The proof mostly follows the proof of
[
Tol09
]
Lemma 3.1. We first prove
1
. Let
\varphi:Z_{Q}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be Lipschitz satisfying

(1)

\chi_{B(x_{Q},\frac{1}{100}\ell(Q))}\leq\varphi\leq 100\max\{0,\frac{1}{10}-\frac{\mathrm{dist}(\cdot,x_{Q})}{\ell(Q)}\}\leq 100\chi_{B(x_{Q},\frac{1}{10}\ell(Q))}

and

(2)

\mathrm{Lip}(\varphi)\lesssim\ell(Q)^{-1}
.

Then
\|\varphi\cdot\mathrm{dist}(\cdot,\mathbb{R}^{n}_{Q})\|_{\infty}\lesssim\ell(Q)
,
\mathrm{Lip}(\varphi\cdot\mathrm{dist}(\cdot,\mathbb{R}^{n}_{Q}))\lesssim 1
, and
\varphi\cdot\mathrm{dist}(\cdot,\mathbb{R}^{n}_{Q})\lesssim\max\{0,5\ell(Q)-\mathrm{dist}(\cdot,x_{Q})\}
so that
c\varphi\cdot\mathrm{dist}(\cdot,\mathbb{R}^{n}_{Q})\in\mathrm{Lip}(5B_{Q})
for some controlled constant
c>0
. This means

\left|\int\varphi(x)\mathrm{dist}(x,\mathbb{R}^{n}_{Q})\ d\mathscr{H}^{n}_{E_{Q}}(x)\right|\lesssim\alpha(Q)\ell(Q)^{n+1}.

But, using the fact that
\mathscr{H}^{n}(E\cap c_{0}B_{Q})\gtrsim\ell(Q)^{n}
, we also have

\displaystyle\int\varphi(x)\mathrm{dist}(x,\mathbb{R}^{n}_{Q})\ d\mathscr{H}^{n}_{E_{Q}}(x)

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{dist}(\mathrm{spt}(\varphi),\mathbb{R}^{n}_{Q})\int\varphi\ d\mathscr{H}^{n}_{E_{Q}}

\displaystyle\gtrsim\mathrm{dist}(\mathrm{spt}(\varphi),\mathbb{R}^{n}_{Q})\ell(Q)^{n}.

By choosing
c_{1}
sufficiently small, we get
\mathrm{dist}(\mathrm{spt}(\varphi),\mathbb{R}^{n}_{Q})\leq\frac{1}{10}\ell(Q)
from which we get
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{Q}\cap B(x_{Q},\frac{1}{5}\ell(Q))\not=\varnothing
.

Now, we prove
2
. Indeed, if
\psi:Z_{Q}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is Lipschitz with

(1)

\chi_{B(x_{Q},\frac{1}{2}\ell(Q))}\leq\psi\leq 10\max\{0,1-\frac{\mathrm{dist}(\cdot,x_{Q})}{\ell(Q)}\}\leq 10\chi_{B(x_{Q},\ell(Q))}

and

(2)

\mathrm{Lip}(\psi)\lesssim\ell(Q)^{-1}
,

then

\left|\int\psi\ d\mathscr{H}^{n}_{E_{Q}}-c_{Q}\int\psi\ d\mathscr{H}^{n}_{\|\cdot\|_{Q}}\right|\lesssim\alpha_{E,X}(Q)\ell(Q)^{n}.

This gives

(6.9)

\int\psi\ d\mathscr{H}^{n}_{E_{Q}}-C\alpha_{E,X}(Q)\ell(Q)^{n}\leq c_{Q}\int\psi\ d\mathscr{H}^{n}_{\|\cdot\|_{Q}}\leq\int\psi\ d\mathscr{H}^{n}_{E_{Q}}+C\alpha_{E,X}(Q)\ell(Q)^{n}.

Observe that the fact that
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{Q}\cap B(x_{Q},\frac{1}{5}\ell(Q))\not=\varnothing
implies
\int\psi\ d\mathscr{H}^{n}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\|\cdot\|_{Q}}}\asymp\ell(Q)^{n}
because
B(x_{Q},\frac{1}{2}\ell(Q))
contains a ball of radius
\gtrsim\ell(Q)
contained in
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\|\cdot\|_{Q}}
. Using this and the upper Ahlfors
n
-regularity of
X
(and hence of
E
), the second inequality of (
6.9
) implies
c_{Q}\lesssim 1
. The first inequality of (
6.9
) implies

c_{Q}\ell(Q)^{n}\gtrsim c_{Q}\int\psi\ d\mathscr{H}^{n}_{\|\cdot\|_{Q}}\geq\mathscr{H}^{n}_{E}\left(\frac{1}{2}B_{Q}\right)-C\alpha_{E,X}(Q)\ell(Q)^{n}\gtrsim\ell(Q)^{n}

which gives
c_{Q}\gtrsim 1
as long as
c_{1}
is sufficiently small.
∎

For the next lemma, we introduce a Gromov-Hausdorff variant of the bilateral

number of David and Semmes.

Definition 6.26

(Gromov-Hausdorff

)
.

Let

and

and define

\displaystyle b\beta_{1}^{\mathrm{GH}}(x,r)=\frac{1}{r^{n}}\inf_{\|\cdot\|,Z}\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\iota_{1}:B(x,r)\rightarrow Z\\
\iota_{2}:(\mathbb{R}^{n},\|\cdot\|)\rightarrow Z\end{subarray}}\int_{B(\iota_{1}(x),r)}

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{dist}(y,\iota_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}{r}\ d\iota_{1}\#[\mathscr{H}^{n}_{X}](y)

\displaystyle+\int_{B(\iota_{1}(x),r)}\frac{\mathrm{dist}(x,\iota_{1}(X))}{r}\ d\iota_{2}\#[\mathscr{H}^{n}_{\|\cdot\|}](x)

where the infima are taken over all norms
\|\cdot\|
, complete, separable metric spaces
Z
, and isometric embeddings
\iota_{1}
and
\iota_{2}
.

Lemma 6.27

(cf.
[
Tol09
]
Lemma 3.2)
.

Let
X
be Ahlfors
n
-regular. For any
Q\in\mathscr{D}(X)
,

\alpha_{X}(Q)\gtrsim b\beta_{1}^{\mathrm{GH}}(3B_{Q})\gtrsim\xi(B_{Q})^{n+1}.

Proof.

The proof mostly follows the proof of Lemma 3.2 in
[
Tol09
]
. We may assume that
\alpha(Q)\leq c_{1}
. Let
\varphi:Z_{Q}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfy
\chi_{B(x_{Q},3\ell(Q))}\leq\varphi\leq\max\{0,5-\frac{\mathrm{dist}(\cdot,x_{Q})}{\ell(Q)}\}

has
\mathrm{Lip}(f)\lesssim 1
and

so that

\displaystyle=\left|\int\varphi(z)\mathrm{dist}(z,\mathbb{R}^{n}_{Q})\ d\mathscr{H}^{n}_{X_{Q}}(z)+c_{Q}\int\varphi(z)\mathrm{dist}(z,X_{Q})\ d\mathscr{H}^{n}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{Q}}(z)\right|.

(6.10)

\displaystyle\geq\min(1,c_{Q})b\beta_{1}^{\mathrm{GH}}(3B_{Q})\ell(Q)^{n+1}\gtrsim b\beta_{1}^{\mathrm{GH}}(3B_{Q})\ell(Q)^{n+1}.

where the last line follows because we’ve assumed

6.3.2.
Alpha numbers in uniformly rectifiable metric spaces

Unless stated otherwise, for the rest of this section fix a complete metric space
(Z,d)
, an Ahlfors
n
-regular metric space
X\subseteq Z
, and an
L
-bi-Lipschitz mapping
g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow Z
. Set
\Sigma=g(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and let
E\subseteq X\cap\Sigma
be
\mathscr{H}^{n}
-measurable.

We wish to control
\alpha_{E,X}
in terms of controlled quantities associated to the bi-Lipschitz image
\Sigma
. Given any cube
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let
\|\cdot\|_{I}
be a norm such that
\sup_{x,y\in I}|\mathrm{dist}(g(x),g(y))-\|x-y\|_{I}|\leq 2\mathrm{md}_{g}(I)\ell(I)
. Let

\tilde{Z}=(X\cup\Sigma)\sqcup\mathbb{R}^{n}

and define a metric
\zeta_{I}:\tilde{Z}\times\tilde{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

(6.11)

\zeta_{I}(x,y)=\begin{cases}d(x,y)&\text{ if $x,y\in X\cup\Sigma$},\\
\|x-y\|_{I}&\text{ if $x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$},\\
\inf_{u\in I}\|x-u\|_{I}+2\mathrm{md}_{g}(I)\ell(I)+d(g(u),y)&\text{ if $x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$ and $y\in X\cup\Sigma$}.\end{cases}

For proof that
\zeta_{I}
is a metric, the reader can see
[
Bat23
]
Lemma 2.24 for a nearly identical case. The basic idea is that since the mapping
g
makes an error of at most
2\mathrm{md}_{g}(I_{Q})\ell(Q)
, we must add this quantity when passing between
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
X\cup\Sigma\subseteq Z
to prevent shortcuts which break the triangle inequality. The metric space
(\tilde{Z},\zeta_{I})
will serve as a test space for controlling
\alpha_{E,X}
near scales and locations in
E
nearby
g(I)
. We begin by controlling
\alpha_{E,X}
by a distance of measures more directly adapted to the dyadic lattices in the domain of
g
.

For any cube
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we let
h_{I}=\frac{d\mathscr{L}^{n}}{d\mathscr{H}^{n}_{\|\cdot\|_{I}}}
be the Radon-Nikodym derivative of
\mathscr{L}^{n}
with respect to
\mathscr{H}^{n}_{\|\cdot\|_{I}}
. We define

P\vcentcolon=g^{-1}(E),

and we set

c_{P,I}\vcentcolon=h_{I}\fint_{I}\mathscr{J}_{g}\chi_{P}(u)d\mathscr{L}(u).

With this setup, we can define cube-adapted alpha numbers.

Definition 6.28
.

Let
\mu
and
\nu
be Borel measures of bounded support in
Z
. Set
D=\max\{\mathrm{diam}(\mathrm{spt}(\mu)),\mathrm{diam}(\mathrm{spt}(\nu))\}
and define

\widetilde{\mathrm{dist}}_{Z}(\mu,\nu)\vcentcolon=\sup\left\{\int fd\mu-\int fd\nu:f:Z\rightarrow[-D,D],\ \mathrm{Lip}(f)\leq 1\right\}.

Definition 6.29
.

For any cube
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, set

\tilde{\alpha}_{E}(I)\vcentcolon=\frac{1}{\ell(I)^{n+1}}\widetilde{\mathrm{dist}}_{(\tilde{Z},\zeta_{I})}(\mathscr{H}^{n}|_{g(I)\cap E},c_{I,P}\mathscr{H}^{n}|_{I}).

where we identify
g(I)\cap E
and
I
with their isometric embeddings inside
\tilde{Z}

Now, fix
R\in\mathscr{D}(X)
and let
Q\in\mathscr{D}(R)
. Let
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}=\tilde{\mathcal{D}}(I_{R})
so that whenever
Q\cap E\not=\varnothing
there always exists some
I_{Q}\in\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
such that
g^{-1}(10B_{Q})\subseteq I_{Q}
and
\ell(I_{Q})\lesssim_{L}\ell(Q)
.

Lemma 6.30
.

For any
Q\in\mathscr{D}(R)
,

\alpha_{E,X}(Q)\lesssim_{L,n}\tilde{\alpha}_{E}(I_{Q})

Proof.

For convenience, we write
\|\cdot\|_{Q}\vcentcolon=\|\cdot\|_{I_{Q}},c_{P,Q}\vcentcolon=c_{P,I_{Q}}
, and so on. Because
X
and
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\|\cdot\|_{Q})
embed isometrically in the complete metric space (
\tilde{Z}_{Q},\zeta_{Q}
) and
\ell(I_{Q})\asymp_{L}\ell(Q)
, we only need to show

\mathrm{dist}_{5B_{Q}}(\mathscr{H}^{n}_{E},c_{P,Q}\mathscr{H}^{n}_{\|\cdot\|_{Q}})\lesssim_{L,n}\widetilde{\mathrm{dist}}_{\tilde{Z}_{Q}}(\mathscr{H}^{n}|_{g(I_{Q})\cap E},c_{P,Q}\mathscr{H}^{n}|_{I_{Q}})

where all sets, balls, and measures are taken in
(\tilde{Z}_{Q},\zeta_{Q})
. Any
f\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(5B_{Q})
is also an admissible Lipschitz function for the supremum in the definition of
\widetilde{\mathrm{dist}}
. Therefore, using the facts that
f=0
outside of
5B_{Q}
,
5B_{Q}\cap E\subseteq g(I_{Q})\cap E
, and
5B_{Q}\cap\mathbb{R}^{n}\subseteq I_{Q}
, for any
f\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(5B_{Q})
we have

\displaystyle\int_{5B_{Q}}fd\mathscr{H}^{n}_{E}-c_{P,Q}\int_{5B_{Q}}fd\mathscr{H}^{n}_{\|\cdot\|_{Q}}

\displaystyle=\int fd\mathscr{H}^{n}|_{g(I_{Q})\cap E}-c_{P,Q}\int fd\mathscr{H}^{n}|_{I_{Q}}

\displaystyle\leq\widetilde{\mathrm{dist}}_{\tilde{Z}_{Q}}(\mathscr{H}^{n}|_{g(I_{Q})\cap E},c_{P,Q}\mathscr{H}^{n}|_{I_{Q}}).\qed

We can now record our primary estimate for
\alpha_{E,X}
. It is a simple consequence of the following lemma that
\alpha
has a weak Carleson condition on bi-Lipschitz images.

Lemma 6.31

(cf.
[
Tol09
]
(4.1) )
.

For any
Q\in\mathscr{D}(R)
, we have

(6.12)

\alpha_{E,X}(Q)\lesssim_{L,n}\mathrm{md}_{g}(I_{Q})+\sum_{I\subseteq I_{Q}}\frac{\ell(I)^{1+n/2}}{\ell(I_{Q})^{1+n}}\|\Delta_{I}(\mathscr{J}_{g}\chi_{g^{-1}(E)})\|_{2}.

Proof.

The proof follows the strategy of that of Theorem 1.1 in
[
Tol09
]
. By Lemma
6.30
, we only need to show the desired bound for
\tilde{\alpha}_{E}(I_{Q})
in place of
\alpha_{E,X}(Q)
.

Recall
P=g^{-1}(E)
. Using the definition of
c_{P,Q}
and the area formula, we have

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{g(I_{Q})\cap E}f(z)

\displaystyle d\mathscr{H}^{n}(z)-c_{P,Q}\int_{I_{Q}}f(u)d\mathscr{H}^{n}_{\|\cdot\|_{Q}}(u)\bigg|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{I_{Q}\cap P}f(g(u))\mathscr{J}_{g}(u)d\mathscr{L}(u)-\fint_{I_{Q}}(\mathscr{J}_{g}\chi_{P})d\mathscr{L}\int_{I_{Q}}f(u)h_{Q}d\mathscr{H}^{n}_{\|\cdot\|_{Q}}(u)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\int_{I_{Q}}[f(g(u))-f(u)]\mathscr{J}_{g}\chi_{P}(u)d\mathscr{L}(u)\right|+\left|\int_{I_{Q}}\left[\mathscr{J}_{g}\chi_{P}(u)-\fint_{I_{Q}}\mathscr{J}_{g}\chi_{P}\right]f(u)d\mathscr{L}(u)\right|

\displaystyle\leq C(L,n)\mathrm{md}_{g}(I_{Q})\ell(Q)^{n+1}+\sum_{I\subseteq I_{Q}}\left|\int_{I_{Q}}f(u)\Delta_{I}(\mathscr{J}_{g}\chi_{P})(u)d\mathscr{L}(u)\right|

where the final inequality uses that
g
is a
C(L)\mathrm{md}_{g}(I_{Q})\ell(Q)
-isometry,
\|f\|_{\infty}+\|\mathscr{J}_{g}\|_{\infty}\lesssim_{L,n}1
, and
\mathscr{H}^{n}(I_{Q})\lesssim_{L,n}\ell(Q)^{n}
. For any cube
I
, let
u_{I}
denote the center of
I
. Since
\Delta_{I}(\mathscr{J}_{g}\chi_{P})
has mean zero, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{I\subseteq I_{Q}}\left|\int_{I_{Q}}f(u)\Delta_{I}(\mathscr{J}_{g}\chi_{P})(u)d\mathscr{L}(u)\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{I\subseteq I_{Q}}\left|\int_{I_{Q}}[f(u)-f(u_{I})]\Delta_{I}(\mathscr{J}_{g}\chi_{P})(u)d\mathscr{L}(u)\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{I\subseteq I_{Q}}\ell(I)\|\Delta_{I}(\mathscr{J}_{g}\chi_{P})\|_{1}\leq\sum_{I\subseteq I_{Q}}\ell(I)^{1+n/2}\|\Delta_{I}(\mathscr{J}_{g}\chi_{P})\|_{2}.

where the final line follows from Cauchy-Schwarz.
∎

Remark 6.32
.

The second term in (
6.12
) satisfies the following strong Carleson condition: For any
J\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

\sum_{I\subseteq J}\left(\sum_{I^{\prime}\subseteq I}\frac{\ell(I^{\prime})^{1+n/2}}{\ell(I)^{1+n}}\|\Delta_{I^{\prime}}(\mathscr{J}_{g}\chi_{P})\|_{2}\right)^{2}\ell(I)^{n}\lesssim_{L,n}\ell(J)^{n}.

The primary tool for the simple proof is Cauchy-Schwarz (see the proof of Theorem 1.1 in
[
Tol09
]
).

Proposition 6.33
.

\alpha_{E,X}\in\mathrm{WC}(X)
.

Proof.

Fix
\epsilon>0
and set

\displaystyle\mathscr{G}

\displaystyle=\left\{Q\in\mathscr{D}(R):\mathrm{md}_{g}(I_{Q})<\epsilon,\ \sum_{I\subseteq I_{Q}}\frac{\ell(I)^{1+n/2}}{\ell(I_{Q})^{1+n}}\|\Delta_{I_{Q}}(\mathscr{J}_{g}\chi_{P})\|_{2}<\epsilon\right\}

\displaystyle\textflorin\mathscr{B}

\displaystyle=\mathscr{D}(R)\setminus\mathscr{G}.

By Lemma
6.31
, for any
Q\in\mathscr{G}
we have
\alpha_{E,X}(Q)\lesssim_{L,n}\epsilon
. Therefore, it suffices to show that
\mathscr{B}
is Carleson. This follows from Theorem
6.4
, Remark
6.32
, and the fact that
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
consists of finitely many shifted lattices.
∎

Corollary 6.34
.

\alpha_{E,\Sigma}\in\mathrm{WC}(\Sigma)
.

Proof.

Take
X=Z=\Sigma
in Proposition
6.33

∎

Proposition 6.35
.

Let
X
be a uniformly
n
-rectifiable metric space. Then
\alpha_{X}\in\mathrm{WC}(X)
.

Proof.

We will use Theorem
4.10
. Without loss of generality, assume
X\subseteq\ell_{\infty}
and recall that Theorem
6.7
implies that
X\in\mathrm{VBP}(\mathscr{S})
inside
\ell_{\infty}
, so Theorem
4.10
implies that it suffices to show that for any
\Sigma\in\mathscr{S}
there exists
\beta_{\Sigma}\in\mathrm{WC}(X)
satisfying (
4.8
). We claim that
\alpha_{X\cap\Sigma,X}
is such a coefficient. Indeed,
\alpha_{X\cap\Sigma,X}\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Co}(\ell_{\infty})
by Lemma
6.23
(with Remark
6.24
) and Lemma
6.22
implies that for any
Q\in\mathscr{D}(X)

\alpha_{X}(c_{0}B_{Q})\lesssim\frac{\mathscr{H}^{n}(Q\setminus\Sigma)}{\ell(Q)^{n}}+\alpha_{X\cap\Sigma,X}(c_{0}B_{Q}).

Since
\alpha_{X\cap\Sigma,X}\in\mathrm{WC}(X)
by Proposition
6.33
, the result follows.
∎

It now follows easily that a weak Carleson condition for
\alpha
is equivalent to uniform rectifiability for Ahlfors regular metric spaces.

Proof of Theorem
B
.

The forward direction is exactly the statement of Proposition
6.35
. For the backward direction, notice that Lemma
6.27
immediately gives a weak Carleson condition for
\xi
. This is exactly the BWGL, which is equivalent to uniform rectifiability by Theorem
6.6
.
