The Discontinuity Problem
Vasco Brattka

Abstract
. Matthias Schröder has asked the question whether there is a weakest discontinuous problem in the continuous version of the Weihrauch lattice. Such a problem can be considered as the weakest unsolvable problem. We introduce the discontinuity problem , and we show that it is reducible exactly to the effectively discontinuous problems , defined in a suitable way. However, in which sense this answers Schröder’s question sensitively depends on the axiomatic framework that is chosen, and it is a positive answer if we work in Zermelo-Fraenkel set theory with dependent choice and the axiom of determinacy 𝖠𝖣 \mathsf{AD} . On the other hand, using the full axiom of choice, one can construct problems which are discontinuous, but not effectively so. Hence, the exact situation at the bottom of the Weihrauch lattice sensitively depends on the axiomatic setting that we choose. We prove our result using a variant of Wadge games for mathematical problems. While the existence of a winning strategy for player II characterizes continuity of the problem (as already shown by Nobrega and Pauly), the existence of a winning strategy for player I characterizes effective discontinuity of the problem. By Weihrauch determinacy we understand the condition that every problem is either continuous or effectively discontinuous. This notion of determinacy is a fairly strong notion, as it is not only implied by the axiom of determinacy 𝖠𝖣 \mathsf{AD} , but it also implies Wadge determinacy. We close with a brief discussion of generalized notions of productivity.

3.
Effectively Discontinuous Problems

We now introduce a concept of
computable
(and
effective
)
discontinuity
.
These are strengthenings of the concept of discontinuity in the sense that the discontinuity is witnessed by
a continuous function
D:{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
.
A computably discontinuous problem
f
is supposed to have no continuous realizer
\Phi_{q}
and the computable discontinuity function
D:{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}

computes for every candidate
\Phi_{q}
a witnessing input
D(q)
that shows that the candidate
\Phi_{q}
fails to realize
f
on that particular input.

Definition 14

(Computable discontinuity)
.

Let
(X,\delta_{X})
and
(Y,\delta_{Y})
be represented spaces.
A problem
f:\subseteq X\rightrightarrows Y
is called
computably discontinuous
(
effectively discontinuous
) if there is a computable (continuous)
D:{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
such that for all
q\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
we obtain

D(q)\in{\mathrm{dom}}(f\delta_{X})\mbox{ and }\delta_{Y}\Phi_{q}D(q)\not\in f\delta_{X}D(q).

In this case the function
D
is called a
discontinuity function
of
f
.

We emphasize that we consider the condition
\delta_{Y}\Phi_{q}D(q)\not\in f\delta_{X}D(q)
also as satisfied when the left-hand side is undefined.
If one does not apply this convention, then one has to write out the condition somewhat more detailed as

D(q)\in{\mathrm{dom}}(f\delta_{X})\mbox{ and }(D(q)\in{\mathrm{dom}}(\delta_{Y}\Phi_{q})\Longrightarrow\delta_{Y}\Phi_{q}D(q)\not\in f\delta_{X}D(q)).

Clearly, every computably discontinuous problem
f
is effectively discontinuous, and every
effectively discontinuous problem is discontinuous.
The definition of computable (effective) discontinuity is such that a problem
f:\subseteq X\rightrightarrows Y
has the respective
property if and only if its
realizer version

f^{\mathrm{r}}:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\rightrightarrows{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
with
f^{\mathrm{r}}:=\delta_{Y}^{-1}\circ f\circ\delta_{X}

has the property. This implies that it suffices to study the concepts of computable and effective discontinuity on Baire space
{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
.
We will prove that effective and computable discontinuity can be both characterized in terms of the following
discontinuity problem.

Definition 15

(Discontinuity problem)
.

We define the
discontinuity problem
by

\mathsf{DIS}:{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\rightrightarrows{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}},p\mapsto\{q\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}:{\mathsf{{U}}}(p)\not=q\}

We note that
\mathsf{DIS}
is total, i.e., for instances
p\not\in{\mathrm{dom}}({\mathsf{{U}}})
the problem
\mathsf{DIS}
can provide arbitrary
q\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}

as solutions.
It is a direct consequence of the parameterized recursion theorem from Corollary
13

\mathsf{DIS}
is computably discontinuous.

Proposition 16

\mathsf{DIS}

Proof.

By Corollary
13

D:{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}

such that
{\mathsf{{U}}}D(p)={\mathsf{{U}}}\langle p,D(p)\rangle=\Phi_{p}D(p)
for all
p\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
. This function

is hence a computable discontinuity function for
\mathsf{DIS}
.
∎

In particular,

.
By the parameterized recursion theorem from Corollary

.
Let

and let us assume that

.
Since

is realizable, there is a realizer

follows
by the choice of

in contradiction to the choice of

Hence the assumption was wrong, i.e.,

.

“
\Longleftarrow
”
Let

.
By the smn-theorem (Theorem

Now for every realizer

.

(2) The proof is literally the same as above, except that

We note that the proof of “

Now we can ask the question whether every discontinuous problem is automatically computably discontinuous, i.e.,
whether the discontinuity problem
\mathsf{DIS}
is the smallest discontinuous problem with respect to the computable version of Weihrauch reducibility.
This is clearly not the case, as the following example shows. In fact, we can infer this from the existence of immune sets.
We recall that a set
A\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}
is called

if it is infinite but does not contain any infinite c.e. subset.

Example 18
.

Let
A\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}
and let
f_{A}:\subseteq[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}
be defined by
{\mathrm{dom}}(f_{A}):=\{2^{-n}:n\in A\}\cup\{0\}
and

f_{A}(x):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\mbox{if $x=0$}\\
0&\mbox{otherwise}\end{array}\right..

Then we obtain:

(1)

f_{A}
discontinuous
\iff

A
infinite.

(2)

f_{A}
computably discontinuous
\iff

A
contains an infinite c.e. subset.

(3)

f_{A}
discontinuous and not computably discontinuous
\iff

A
is immune.

Proof.

(1) is obvious and (3) follows from (1) and (2). For the proof of (2), we note that given a computable discontinuity function
D:{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
for
f_{A}
, we get an infinite c.e. subset of
A
as follows.
Given
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
A_{n}=\{0,1,2,...,n\}
we can compute a name
q
of a realizer
\Phi_{q}
of the continuous
function
f_{A_{n}}
, and
D(q)
has to provide a name of an input
2^{-k}\in{\mathbb{N}}
to
f_{A}
on which the realizer
\Phi_{q}
fails.
But this means that
k\in A\setminus A_{n}
. The collection of all those
k
for
n=0,1,2,...
forms an infinite c.e. subset of
A
. On the other hand, given an infinite c.e. subset
B\subseteq A
and a potential realizer
\Phi_{q}
of
f_{A}
,
we can evaluate
\Phi_{q}
successively on names of the compact interval
[0,2^{-k}]
with
k=0,1,2,...
. Then either
\Phi_{q}
eventually produces an output that excludes
0
or that excludes
1
or neither of this ever happens. In the first case, we can find some
m\in B
with

m>k
and produce a name of
2^{-m}
as output of
D(q)
, since this is a value on which
\Phi_{q}
fails to realize
f_{A}(2^{-m})=0
.
In the second case, we produce a name of
0
as output of
D(q)
, since this is then a value on which
\Phi_{q}
fails to realize
f_{A}(0)=1
. As long as neither the first nor the second case occurs, we produce the open interval
(-2^{-k},2^{-k+1})
as approximation of an output of
D(q)
. If the first and the second case never occurs, then this produces a name of
0
as output of
D(q)
, which is then not in the domain of
\Phi_{q}
. Altogether,
D
is a computable discontinuity function for
f_{A}
.
∎

The following result shows that with respect to the computable version of Weihrauch reducibility
we can even get an infinite descending chain
of discontinuous problems that are not computably discontinuous.
If we restrict the discontinuity problem to the Turing cone
[p]:=\{q\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}:p\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{T}}}q\}
,
we obtain an effectively discontinuous problem
\mathsf{DIS}|_{[p]}
. With increasing complexity of

these problems get weaker.
By
p^{(n)}
we denote the
n
–th
Turing jump
of
p\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
.

Proposition 19
.

\mathsf{DIS}|_{[p^{(n+1)}]}\mathop{<_{\mathrm{W}}}\mathsf{DIS}|_{[p^{(n)}]}
and
\mathsf{DIS}|_{[p^{(n)}]}
is effectively discontinuous for all
p\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, but not computably so for
n\geq 1
.

Proof.

It is clear that
\mathsf{DIS}|_{[p^{(n+1)}]}\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{sW}}}\mathsf{DIS}|_{[p^{(n)}]}
holds for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, as the
former problem is a restriction of the latter one. We have
\mathsf{DIS}|_{[p^{(n)}]}\not\leq_{\mathrm{W}}\mathsf{DIS}|_{[p^{(n+1)}]}
,
as an instance
q\mathop{\equiv_{\mathrm{T}}}p^{(n)}
of
\mathsf{DIS}|_{[p^{(n)}]}
cannot be computably mapped to an
instance of
\mathsf{DIS}|_{[p^{(n+1)}]}
.

On the other hand, every function
\Phi_{q}
has names of Turing degree above any
p\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
, and we can even continuously
determine such names by adding redundant information in the code

that encodes

(for instance by repeating the

exactly

times).
In other words, for every
p\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
there is a continuous function
R_{p}:{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
such that
\Phi_{q}=\Phi_{R_{p}(q)}
and

.
Hence
\mathsf{DIS}\leq_{\mathrm{W}}^{*}\mathsf{DIS}|_{[p]}
holds for every

. That is,
\mathsf{DIS}|_{[p]}
is effectively discontinuous by Theorem

.
∎

Hence, we even have an infinite descending chain of effectively discontinuous problems below
\mathsf{DIS}

with respect to the computable version of Weihrauch reducibility.
In particular, an effectively discontinuous problem does not need to be computably discontinuous.
The next question is whether there are discontinuous problems, which are not even effectively discontinuous.
The following proposition provides a sufficient condition for such an example.

Proposition 20
.

Let
\iota:{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
be injective and
B:={\mathrm{range}}(\iota)
. We consider the problem

f:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\rightrightarrows{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
with the domain
{\mathrm{dom}}(f):=B
and

f(p):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\setminus\{\Phi_{\iota^{-1}(p)}(p)\}&\mbox{if $p\in{\mathrm{dom}}(\Phi_{\iota^{-1}(p)})$}\\
{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}&\mbox{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

for all
p\in B
. Then
f
is discontinuous. Moreover, if
f
is effectively discontinuous, then there is a continuous
embedding
g:2^{\mathbb{N}}\hookrightarrow B
.

Proof.

A function
D:{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
is a discontinuity function for
f
if and only if
{\mathrm{range}}(D)\subseteq B
and

D(q)\in{\mathrm{dom}}(\Phi_{q})\Longrightarrow\Phi_{q}D(q)=\Phi_{\iota^{-1}D(q)}D(q)

holds for all
q\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
. Clearly,
D=\iota
is a discontinuity function
for
f
, albeit not necessarily a continuous one. Nevertheless, this shows that
f
is discontinuous.

Let us now assume that
D:{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
is a continuous function with the above property.
By the smn-theorem (Theorem
11
) there exists a computable total function
R:{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
such that

\Phi_{R(q)}(p)=q
for all
p,q\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
. Then we obtain

q=\Phi_{R(q)}DR(q)=\Phi_{\iota^{-1}DR(q)}DR(q)

for all
q\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
, which is only possible if
DR:{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
is injective.
In particular,
DR|_{2^{\mathbb{N}}}:2^{\mathbb{N}}\to B
is a continuous embedding of Cantor space into
B
.
∎

Hence, the existence of an injective map
\iota:{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
with a range
B={\mathrm{range}}(\iota)

into which Cantor space cannot be continuously embedded, is sufficient to guarantee the existence
of a problem
f
that is discontinuous, but not effectively discontinuous.

For this purpose it is sufficient to show that there exists a set
B\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
, which violates the perfect subset property.
A set
B
satisfies the
perfect subset property
if it is either countable or it contains a non-empty
perfect
subset, which
is a subset that is closed and has no isolated points. We recall that every
A\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
into which Cantor space can be continuously embedded contains a perfect subset.
It is a well-known fact that there are so-called
Bernstein sets

[
4
]
, which violate the perfect subset property,
at least if we assume the axiom of choice
[
12
, Exercise 8.24]
.

Fact 21

(Bernstein set)
.

Assuming
\mathsf{AC}
there exists a
Bernstein set

B\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
, which is a set
B
such that
B
as well as its complement
{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\setminus B
have non-empty intersection
with every uncountable closed set
A\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
.

The construction of a Bernstein set

provided in
[
12
, Exercise 8.24]
works equally well for
B\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}

and is by transfinite recursion.
This construction necessarily requires the axiom of choice.
Indeed, if we assume
\mathsf{BP}
, i.e., that every subset
B\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
has the Baire property,
then no Bernstein set can exist
[
12
]
.
From the point of view of computability theory Bernstein sets play a similar rôle as immune sets.
Actually, together with Proposition
20
and Fact
21

we directly obtain the following conclusion.

Corollary 22
.

If we assume
\mathsf{AC}
, then there exists a problem
f:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\rightrightarrows{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}

that is discontinuous, but not effectively discontinuous.

Similarly as in the case of Proposition
19
, one could object that the problem
f
constructed here
is not genuinely less discontinuous than
\mathsf{DIS}
, but only “simpler” as instances are artificially made harder.
In other words, the problem
f
considered here has a very complicated domain, which in the case of our proof of Corollary

is a Bernstein set.

With the next result we dispel this objection by constructing a total problem
f:{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\rightrightarrows{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}

that is discontinuous, but not effectively so. Again, the construction is based on the axiom of choice,
and we directly perform a transfinite recursion.
We can arrange this construction even such that
f
is parallelizable.
We recall that
f:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\rightrightarrows{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
is

if and only if
f\mathop{\equiv_{\mathrm{W}}}\langle f\rangle
,
where
\langle f\rangle
is defined by
\langle f\rangle:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\rightrightarrows{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}},\langle p_{0},p_{1},...\rangle\mapsto\langle f(p_{0}),f(p_{1}),...\rangle
.
By
|X|
we denote the

of a set

.

Theorem 23
.

Assuming
\mathsf{AC}
, there exists a total parallelizable
f:{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\rightrightarrows{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
that is discontinuous, but not
effectively discontinuous.

Proof.

It suffices to construct a total
f:{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\rightrightarrows{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
that is discontinuous and such that

\langle f\rangle:{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\rightrightarrows{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}},\langle p_{0},p_{1},...\rangle\mapsto\langle f(p_{0}),f(p_{1}),...\rangle

is not effectively discontinuous. Then
\langle f\rangle
has the desired properties, since
it is discontinuous and parallelizable.
We note that the set
\mathsf{C}({\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}},{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}})
of total continuous functions
g:{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
has continuum cardinality.
Hence, by the axiom of choice there is a transfinite enumeration
(r_{\xi})_{\xi<2^{\aleph_{0}}}
of
r_{\xi}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}

such that
\mathsf{C}({\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}},{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}})=\{\Phi_{r_{\xi}}:\xi<2^{\aleph_{0}}\}
.
A problem
f:{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\rightrightarrows{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
is discontinuous and
\langle f\rangle
is not effectively discontinuous if
the following two requirements are satisfied:

(1)

(\forall\xi<2^{\aleph_{0}})(\exists q\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}})\left(\Phi_{q}\Phi_{r_{\xi}}(q)\in\langle f\rangle(\Phi_{r_{\xi}}(q))\mbox{ and }\Phi_{q}\mbox{ total}\right)
,

(2)

(\forall\xi<2^{\aleph_{0}})(\exists p\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}})\;\Phi_{r_{\xi}}(p)\not\in f(p)
.

The second condition guarantees that
f
has no continuous realizer
\Phi_{r_{\xi}}
, the
first condition guarantees that no
\Phi_{r_{\xi}}
is a discontinuity function for
\langle f\rangle
.
We build

by transfinite recursion.
For this purpose we construct two increasing sequences
(N_{\xi})_{\xi<2^{\aleph_{0}}}
and
(P_{\xi})_{\xi<2^{\aleph_{0}}}

of sets

ensures that

is discontinuous and

ensures that
\langle f\rangle
is not effectively discontinuous.
The construction will be such that
|N_{\xi}|=|\xi|
and
|P_{\xi}|\leq|\xi|\cdot\aleph_{0}
.
During the construction we frequently use the axiom of choice without further mention.
The transfinite recursion goes as follows.
We start with
N_{0}:=P_{0}:=\emptyset
.
For each
0<\xi<2^{\aleph_{0}}
we first choose
some

that does not appear as a first component in

and we define
N_{\xi}:=\{(p,\Phi_{r_{\xi}}(p))\}\cup\bigcup_{\lambda<\xi}N_{\lambda}
.
Such a choice is possible, as
|M|\leq|\xi|+|\xi|\cdot\aleph_{0}=\max(|\xi|,\aleph_{0})<2^{\aleph_{0}}

the usual rules of cardinal arithmetic
[
11
, Corollary 3.7.8]
.
Secondly, we also choose some
q\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
such that
\Phi_{q}
is total and such that no
\pi_{i}\Phi_{q}\Phi_{r_{\xi}}(q)
with
i\in{\mathbb{N}}

appears in any second component of

.
Here
\pi_{i}:{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}},\langle p_{0},p_{1},...\rangle\mapsto p_{i}
denotes the projection
on the

–th component.
Such a choice is possible, as
|N_{\xi}|\leq|\xi|<2^{\aleph_{0}}
and
\Phi_{q}
for

includes all the constant total functions.
We define

P_{\xi}:=\{(\pi_{i}\Phi_{r_{\xi}}(q),\pi_{i}\Phi_{q}\Phi_{r_{\xi}}(q)):i\in{\mathbb{N}}\}\cup\bigcup_{\lambda<\xi}P_{\lambda}
.
The construction guarantees that
|N_{\xi}|=|\xi|
and
|P_{\xi}|\leq|\xi|\cdot\aleph_{0}
.
This ends the transfinite recursion.
We now define
P:=\bigcup_{\xi<2^{\aleph_{0}}}P_{\xi}
and
N:=\bigcup_{\xi<2^{\aleph_{0}}}N_{\xi}
.
The construction guarantees that
P
is the graph of a partial problem
f:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\rightrightarrows{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}

and

is the graph of a single-valued function
g:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
with

.
Actually,
P
guarantees that
f
satisfies condition (1) and

guarantees that
f
satisfies condition (2).
We still need to extend

to the graph of a total problem
f:{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\rightrightarrows{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
without affecting the conditions (1) and (2).
For this purpose, we choose for every

p\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
that does not yet appear in a first component of

some

such that

(p,s)\not\in N
and we add
(p,s)
to

. This is possible since

is the graph of a single-valued function

.
Now

is the graph of a suitable total problem
f:{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\rightrightarrows{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
.
∎

In Corollary

we will see that without the axiom of choice (

) we cannot construct

In this section we want to characterize continuity and effective discontinuity using games.
It is common in descriptive set theory and computability theory to use games to characterize reducibilities and other properties
[

,

,

]
(see
[

,

]
for historical surveys).

Wadge
[

]
introduced games on subsets

to characterize the reducibility that is named after him.
Nobrega and Pauly
[
20
,
21
]
have used a modification of Wadge games for problems

in order to characterize lower cones in the Weihrauch lattice.
We consider similar generalized

We warn the reader that the extension of the notion of a Wadge game from sets

versions of Wadge and Lipschitz games, defined as follows. We recall that by
{f^{\mathrm{r}}:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}}
we denote the realizer version
of a problem

(see section

Definition 25

(Wadge game of problems)
.

Let
f:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\rightrightarrows{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
be a problem. In a
Wadge game

f

two players I and II consecutively play words, with player I starting:

•

Player I:
x_{0}\quad x_{1}\quad x_{2}\quad...\quad=:x
,

•

Player II:
\quad y_{0}\quad y_{1}\quad y_{2}\quad...\quad=:y
,

with
x_{i},y_{i}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
.
The concatenated sequences
(x,y)\in({\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\cup{\mathbb{N}}^{*})^{2}
are called a

of the game
f
.
We say that Player II
wins

(x,y)
of
f
, if

(x,y)\in{\mathrm{graph}}(f)
or
x\not\in{\mathrm{dom}}(f)
. Otherwise Player I
wins
.
A Wadge game is called a

if
x_{i},y_{i}\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
A
Wadge

of a general problem

is understood to be the corresponding game of
the realizer version

.

We allow both players to play arbitrary words, including the empty word.
One can see that player I does not take any advantage of playing words and he could be restricted to natural numbers in a Wadge game, without loss of generality.
Likewise, player II does not take any advantage of playing arbitrary words, it would suffice to allow numbers and the empty word, where the empty word essentially corresponds to skipping the corresponding move.
This shows that our notion of a Wadge game for problems corresponds to the one of Nobrega and Pauly
[
20
,
21
]
.
For simplicity we have allowed arbitrary words for both players.
This does not only lead to a more symmetric definition, but it also simplifies the proof of Theorem

As usual we define winning strategies for games to be word functions that determine moves for one player depending on the
moves of the other player.

Definition 26

(Winning strategy)
.

Let

be a problem and let
{\sigma:{\mathbb{N}}^{**}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{*}}
be a function.
We consider the Wadge game

.

(1)

x_{0},x_{1},...\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
.

(2)

\sigma
is called a

for Player I in the game

, if Player I
wins every run of
f

with his moves being determined by

x_{i}:=\sigma(y_{0},...,y_{i-1}),

while Player II plays
y_{0},y_{1},...\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
.

Winning strategies for Lipschitz games are defined analogously with functions of type

.

Now our main observation on Wadge games is that winning of player II characterizes continuity of the problem
and winning of player I effective discontinuity. Nobrega and Pauly have proved a general version of the first observation for lower cones in the Weihrauch lattice

Theorem 27

(Wadge games)

(1) If

Definition 30

In this section we briefly want to discuss the question what computable discontinuity means for subsets and how

Here, for every subset

The codomain

It is well-known that for any represented space

As a side remark we mention that the characteristic function in this form can also nicely be used to embed Wadge reducibility and many-one reducibility
into the strong version of Weihrauch reducibility.
We recall that for

. Likewise, we say for

that

is

to

in symbols

such that

. Since the only continuous functions

are the identity and the two constant functions, we directly get the following characterization of Wadge and many-one reducibility in terms of (continuous) strong Weihrauch reducibility.

Proposition 45

(Reducibilities)

We obtain

(1)

\chi_{A}\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{sW}}}\chi_{B}\iff A\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{m}}}B
for all
A,B\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}
.

(2)

\chi_{A}\leq_{\mathrm{sW}}^{*}\chi_{B}\iff A\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{W}}}B\iff\frac{B}{A}\leq_{\mathrm{W}}^{*}{\mathrm{id}}
for all
A,B\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
.

Hence, the corresponding reducibility structures can be embedded into the corresponding strong versions of the Weihrauch lattice.
By
{\mathcal{O}}(X)
we denote the set of open subsets of
X
equipped with the representation
\delta_{{\mathcal{O}}(X)}
,
defined by
\delta_{{\mathcal{O}}(X)}(p)=U:\iff\Phi_{p}
is a realizer of
\chi_{U}
.
Now one can ask what it means for
\chi_{A}
to be computably discontinuous.
The following result answers this question for Baire space
X={\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
using the
symmetric difference problem

\Delta_{A}:\subseteq{\mathcal{O}}(X)\rightrightarrows X,U\mapsto A\Delta U
. Here
A\Delta U:=(A\setminus U)\cup(U\setminus A)
denotes
the
symmetric difference
of
A,U\subseteq X
. The problem
\Delta_{A}
is total for non-open
A\subseteq X
.

Proposition 46

(Symmetric difference)
.

Let
A\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
not be open. Then the problem
\Delta_{A}
is computable if and only if
\chi_{A}
is computably discontinuous.

Proof.

If
\chi_{A}:{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{S}}
is computably discontinuous, then there is a computable discontinuity function
D:{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}

for
\chi_{A}
. Given a name
q
of a realizer
\Phi_{q}
of the continuous function
\chi_{U}:{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{S}}
for any given
U\in{\mathcal{O}}({\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}})
,
the value
D(q)
is a name for some point
x\in X
with
\chi_{U}(x)\not=\chi_{A}(x)
, i.e.,
x\in A\Delta U
.
That is,
\Delta_{A}
is realized by
D
and hence computable.
On the other hand, let
\Delta_{A}
be computable and
A\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
not open.
Given a name
q
of some potential realizer
\Phi_{q}
of
\chi_{A}
, we can convert
q
computably into a name
r=G(q)
of some total function

\Phi_{r}
with
\delta_{\mathbb{S}}\Phi_{r}=\delta_{\mathbb{S}}\Phi_{q}
, because
\delta_{\mathbb{S}}
is precomplete (the idea is that
\Phi_{r}
produces zero output as long as
\Phi_{q}
makes no other information available).
Let us denote by
G:{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
the corresponding computable function.
Now there is some open
U\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
such that
\Phi_{r}
is a realizer of
\chi_{U}
, namely
U:=\Phi_{r}^{-1}({\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\setminus\{000...\})=\Phi_{r}^{-1}\delta_{\mathbb{S}}^{-1}(\{1\})
.
Since
A
is not open,
\Delta_{A}(U)
is defined and non-empty.
Let
F
be a computable realizer of
\Delta_{A}
and
D:=FG
. Then
F(r)\in\Delta_{A}(U)=(A\setminus U)\cup(U\setminus A)
,
i.e.,
\chi_{U}D(q)\not=\chi_{A}D(q)
. This implies
\delta_{\mathbb{S}}\Phi_{q}D(q)\not=\chi_{A}D(q)
, since either
D(q)\not\in{\mathrm{dom}}(\Phi_{q})
or otherwise

\delta_{\mathbb{S}}\Phi_{q}D(q)=\delta_{\mathbb{S}}\Phi_{r}D(q)=\chi_{U}D(q)
. That is
D
is a computable discontinuity function of
\chi_{A}
.
∎

An analogous statement holds if we replace computable by continuous in both occurrences. However,
in this case the statement is void as any
\chi_{A}
for non open
A
is effectively discontinuous
by (a suitable extension of) Proposition
24
.
We can also summarize Proposition
46
as follows.

Corollary 47

(Symmetric difference)
.

\Delta_{A}\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{W}}}{\mathrm{id}}\iff\mathsf{DIS}\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{W}}}\chi_{A}
for non-open
A\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
.

Proposition
46

shows that the notion of computable discontinuity is also formally related to the notion of productivity.
Weihrauch has introduced a topological and a computability theoretic notion of productivity for subsets
A\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}

[
28
, Definition 4.4]
,

[
29
, Definition 3.2.24]
.
If, in a similar way, we transfer the definition of
completely productive sets
, as originally defined by Dekker
[
10
]
,
then we could say that non-open
A\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
is
completely productive
if
\Delta_{A}
is computable.
Using this terminology, non-open
A\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
is completely productive if and only if
\chi_{A}
is computably discontinuous by Proposition
46
.
Myhill
[
18
]
proved that a set
A\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}
is productive if and only if
A
is completely productive (see also the proofs in
[
24
, Theorem VII, §11.3]
,
[
29
, Corollary 2.6.8]
).
It is clear that complete productivity for
A\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
implies productivity.
We leave it as a task to the reader to study whether productivity also implies complete productivity for Baire space
{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
(or even more general spaces).

6.
Conclusions

We have introduced the discontinuity problem
\mathsf{DIS}
and we have provided some evidence that one can consider it
as the simplest natural unsolvable problem with respect to the continuous version of Weihrauch reducibility.
At least in
\mathsf{ZF}+\mathsf{DC}+\mathsf{AD}
it turns out that it actually induces the minimal discontinuous Weihrauch degree.

More results on the discontinuity problem will be provided in a forthcoming article
[
6
]
.
While the original definition of
\mathsf{DIS}
is in terms of a universal function
{\mathsf{{U}}}
,
it is useful to have a characterization in purely set-theoretic terms.
In
[
6
]
we prove that the discontinuity problem is equivalent to the
range non-equality problem

defined by

\mathsf{NRNG}:{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\rightrightarrows 2^{\mathbb{N}},p\mapsto\{A\in 2^{\mathbb{N}}:A\not={\mathrm{range}}(p-1)\}
.
Here for
p\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
the finite or infinite sequence
p-1\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\cup{\mathbb{N}}^{*}

is the sequence that is obtained as concatenation of
p(0)-1,p(1)-1,p(2)-1,...
,
where
-1
is identified with the empty word
\varepsilon
.

Proposition 48
.

\mathsf{DIS}\mathop{\equiv_{\mathrm{sW}}}\mathsf{NRNG}
.

In
[
6
]
we also discuss algebraic properties of the discontinuity problem
\mathsf{DIS}
and one important property is that
the parallelization
\widehat{\mathsf{DIS}}
of the discontinuity problem is equivalent to the non-computability problem
\mathsf{NON}
.

Theorem 49

(Non-computability is parallelized discontinuity)
.

\mathsf{NON}\mathop{\equiv_{\mathrm{sW}}}\widehat{\mathsf{DIS}}
.

The
non-computability problem
is defined with the help of Turing reducibility
\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{T}}}
by

\mathsf{NON}:{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\rightrightarrows{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}},p\mapsto\{q\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}:q\not\leq_{\mathrm{T}}p\}
.
This result supports the slogan that “non-computability is the parallelization of discontinuity” and underlines
that the discontinuity problem is a natural one.
The discontinuity problem can itself be obtained by summation (a dual operation to parallelization introduced in
[
6
]
)
starting from other natural problems such as
\mathsf{LPO},\mathsf{LLPO}
etc. Hence, it is nicely related in an algebraic
way to other natural problems in the Weihrauch lattice.
