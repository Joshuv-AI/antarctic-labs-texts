Convex-compact subgroups of the Goeritz group
Bena Tshishiku

Abstract
Let G < Mod 2 G<\Mod_{2} be the Goeritz subgroup of the genus-2 mapping class group. We show that finitely-generated, purely pseudo-Anosov subgroups of G G are convex cocompact in Mod 2 \Mod_{2} , addressing a case of a general question of Farb–Mosher. We also give a simple criterion to determine if a Goeritz mapping class is pseudo-Anosov, which we use to give very explicit convex-cocompact subgroups. In our analysis, a central role is played by the primitive disk complex 𝒫 \mathcal{P} . In particular, we (1) establish a version of the Masur–Minksy distance-formula for 𝒫 \mathcal{P} , (2) classify subsurfaces X ⊂ S X\subset S that are infinite-diameter holes of 𝒫 \mathcal{P} , and (3) show that 𝒫 \mathcal{P} is quasi-isometric to a coned-off Cayley graph for G G .



Key words and phrases.
Geometric group theory, convex cocompactness, mapping class groups, curve complexes, 3-manifolds, Heegaard splittings

Characterizing pseudo-Anosov elements of the Goeritz group.

(Pseudo-Anosov characterization)

Let

Refined Nielsen–Thurston classification.

Theorem
B
also classifies the ways an element of the Goeritz group can be reducible (in terms of what structure is preserved). This provides a refinement of the Nielsen–Thurston classification for reducible elements of the Goeritz group. We illustrate this with the following result.

Corollary 1.2

(Classification of canonical reduction systems for Goeritz elements)
.

Let
g\in\mathbb{G}
be an infinite-order, reducible element. Then the canonical reduction system
\CRS(g)
is either

(i)

(weakly reducing pair) a pair of curves
c
and
\widehat{c}
that bound primitive disks in
V
and
\widehat{V}
respectively,

(ii)

(reducing curve) a curve
c
that bounds disks in both
V
and
\widehat{V}
, or

(iii)

(figure-8 curve) a curve
c
whose embedding in
S^{3}
is the figure-8 knot.

Explicit convex cocompact examples.

Using Theorems
A
and
B
, one may give explicit examples of convex cocompact subgroups of
G<\Mod(S)
. We illustrate this with the following corollary, which is motivated by Problem 3.6 of
[
Mos06

Corollary 1.3

(Explicit convex cocompact subgroups)
.

Let
\beta,\delta\in\mathbb{G}
be the elements pictured in Figure
1
. For each
n\geq 2
, the subgroup

G_{n}=\langle\beta^{n}\delta,\delta\beta^{n}\rangle<\Mod(S)

is purely pseudo-Anosov and hence convex cocompact by Theorem
A
.

Proof of Corollary
1.3
.

By the presentation (
1
), the subgroup
\langle\beta,\delta\rangle\subset\mathbb{G}
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}*\mathbb{Z}_{3}
. This implies that nontrivial products of the generators of
G_{n}
are cyclically reduced with no cancellation. From this one quickly deduces that no word in
G_{n}
is conjugate into any of the subgroups listed in Theorem
B
. Therefore
G_{n}
is purely pseudo-Anosov.
∎

Explicit examples are rare in the study of convex cocompact subgroups of mapping class groups. More general constructions of convex cocompact groups, e.g. using ping-pong as in
[

Min11

[

, §7]
and
[
Whi00
, §6]
(the latter examples are convex cocompact by
[
KLS09
]
).

Techniques.

The main technical results used in the proofs of Theorems
A
and
B
are Theorems
C
and
D
below.

To show a group
G<\Mod(S)
is convex cocompact, it suffices to show that the orbit map (with respect to some basepoint) of
G
acting on the curve complex
\mathcal{C}(S)
is a quasi-isometric embedding
[
KL08
]
. For studying subgroups
G<\mathbb{G}
, it is convenient to choose the basepoint so that the orbit is contained in the primitive disk complex
\mathcal{P}(V)\subset\mathcal{C}(S)
associated to the Heegaard splitting
S^{3}=V\cup_{S}\widehat{V}
(see §
2
for the definition). Theorem
C
relates the distance in
\mathcal{P}(V)
to the distance in
\mathcal{C}(S)
and more generally
\mathcal{C}(X)
, where
X\subset S
is a subsurface whose complement does not support a primitive disk; such subsurfaces are called
holes
for
\mathcal{P}(V)
; see §
2
.

Theorem C

(Distance formula)
.

Given
c>0
, there is
K>0
so that for any two vertices
D,E
of
\mathcal{P}(V)
,

\frac{1}{K}\sum_{X}\{d_{X}(D,E)\}_{c}-K\leq d_{\mathcal{P}(V)}(D,E)\leq K\sum_{X}\{d_{X}(D,E)\}_{c}+K,

(3)

where the sums range over subsurfaces
X\subset S
that are
holes
for
\mathcal{P}(V)
,
d_{X}(D,E)
is defined by
subsurface projection
, and
\{\cdot\}_{c}
is the
cutoff function
. Definitions appear in §
2
.

Distance formulas as in (
3
) originate in the work of Masur–Minsky
[
MM00
]
. We arrive at (
3
) by closely following the work of Masur–Schleimer
[
MS13
]
, who prove an analogous result for the disk complex
\mathcal{D}(V)
and provide an axiomatic approach to distance formulas more generally.

To apply Theorem
C
, it is necessary to classify the holes
X\subset S
with the property that the image of the subsurface projection
\pi_{X}:\mathcal{P}(V)\rightarrow\mathcal{C}(X)
has infinite diameter. For brevity we say that
X

has infinite diameter
(with respect to
\mathcal{P}(V)
).

Theorem D

(Classification of large holes for
\mathcal{P}(V)
)
.

Let
X\subset S
be a hole for
\mathcal{P}(V)
with diameter
\geq 61
. Then either
X=S
or
X
is a genus-
1
Seifert surface for the figure-
8
knot. In either case there exists
g\in\mathbb{G}
that preserves
X
and so that
g\bigr|_{X}
is pseudo-Anosov. Consequently, every hole of diameter
\geq 61
has infinite diameter.

Remark 1.4

(Genus-1 fibered knots)
.

The proof of Theorem
D
uses the classification of genus-1 fibered knots in
S^{3}
. The only such knots are the figure-8 and the (left/right-handed) trefoil
[
GAn70
]
. The appendix contains a modern proof of this result.

Remark 1.5

(Relation to the work of Masur–Schleimer)
.

Our proofs of the distance formula and classification of holes for
\mathcal{P}(V)
(Theorems
C
and
D
) build on the work of
[
MS13
]
, who prove similar results for the disk complex
\mathcal{D}(V)
. The primitive disk complex
\mathcal{P}(V)
is a subcomplex of
\mathcal{D}(V)
. As such, the techniques of
[
MS13
]
are useful for this paper; however, we note that the results of
[
MS13
]
cannot be applied directly to prove our main results, since e.g. there are holes for
\mathcal{P}(V)
that are not holes for
\mathcal{D}(V)
, and there are holes for
\mathcal{D}(V)
that have infinite diameter with respect to
\mathcal{D}(V)
, but finite diameter with respect to
\mathcal{P}(V)
. See Remark
2.12
. Many of the arguments of
[
MS13
]
“break” when restricting only to primitive disks, and some of the work in the current paper involves finding ways to “repair” these arguments.

Remark 1.6

(Holes for the reducing sphere complex)
.

Using our analysis one could also classify holes for the complex
\mathcal{R}(V,\widehat{V})
of reducing spheres for the Heegaard splitting. This problem was posed by Schleimer
[
Sch20
, §7]
. One might try to use
\mathcal{R}(V,\widehat{V})
to prove Theorem
A
(indeed, this is one motivation for Schleimer’s problem), but it seems more convenient to work with the primitive disks instead of reducing spheres. For example, it’s easier to construct paths between primitive disks using surgery.

Remark 1.7

(The assumption
g=2
)
.

Our approach to proving each of Theorems
A
–
D
fails to extend to genus
g\geq 3
. A fundamental difference between
g=2
and
g\geq 3
is that in genus 2, every surgery path between a pair of primitive disks is a path of
primitive
disks, but this is not true in higher genus; see
[
CKL20
, Thm. 1.1]
.

Given Theorem

2.1
Surfaces and curves

We use
\Sigma_{g,b}
to denote the compact oriented surface of genus
g
with
b

curve

A disk

Primitive disk complex.

For a compact surface
X

\mathcal{C}(X)
to denote the curve complex, which is the simplicial complex with a vertex for each isotopy class of essential, non-peripheral simple closed curve on
X

\mathcal{C}(X)
as the fewest edges in an edge path in
\mathcal{C}(X)

The key complex of interest in this paper is the

\mathcal{P}(V)

whose vertices are isotopy classes of simple closed curves on

]

\mathcal{P}(V)

By definition,
\mathcal{P}(V)
is a subcomplex of the (more familiar) disk complex
\mathcal{D}(V)\subset\mathcal{C}(S)
whose vertices are curves on
S
that bound disks in
V
. The disk complex will be helpful for our study of

To understand

Heegaard marking complex.

We define the
Heegaard marking complex

\mathcal{M}(V,\widehat{V})
as the graph with

•

a vertex for each pair
(R,D)
, where
R
is a reducing curve, and
D\subset V
is a primitive disk that is disjoint from the primitive disks
D^{\prime},D^{\prime\prime}\subset V
that are disjoint from
R
.

•

an edge between pairs of vertices if they differ by one of the following moves:

–

twist: replace
(R,D)
by
(R,\beta_{R}(D))
, where
\beta_{R}\in\mathbb{G}
is a half-twist about
R
(analogous to

in Figure

–

3-cycle: replace
(R,D)
by
(\rho(R),\rho(D))
, where
\rho\in\mathbb{G}
is the order-3 element defined as follows. Let
D^{\prime},D^{\prime\prime}\subset V
be the two primitive disks that are disjoint from

. Let
\rho\in\mathbb{G}
be a mapping class that cyclically permutes

and preserves the two components

(analogous to
\delta
in Figure

A vertex of
\mathcal{M}(V,\widehat{V})
naturally determines a clean, complete marking in the sense of

of

. Let

Goeritz group action.

The Goeritz group

obviously acts on all of our complexes. The following computation will be used in §

Proposition 2.5

(Primitive disk stabilizer)
.

Let
E=E_{2}
be the primitive disk pictured in Figure
3
. The stabilizer
\mathbb{G}_{E}<\mathbb{G}
is the subgroup generated by
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\delta
.

Proof.

The following proof is based on the computation in
[
Sch04
]
of a generating set for
\mathbb{G}
. Let
\mathcal{R}_{E}\subset\mathcal{R}(V,\widehat{V})
denote the subcomplex spanned by reducing spheres that are disjoint from
E
. Let
P,Q
be the reducing curves pictured in Figure

.

We prove the proposition by showing that (1)
\mathcal{R}_{E}
is connected, (2)
\mathbb{G}_{E}
acts transitively on vertices and edges of
\mathcal{R}_{E}
, and (3)
\mathbb{G}_{E}
contains an edge inversion
P\leftrightarrow Q
. Once we show this, it is a basic result of geometric group theory that
\mathbb{G}_{E}
is generated by the stabilizer of
P
and the edge inversion
[
FM12
, Lem. 4.10]
. The stabilizer of
P
in
\mathbb{G}
is
\langle\alpha,\beta,\gamma\rangle\cong(\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z})\rtimes\mathbb{Z}_{2}
; see
[
Sch04
, §2]
. It is easy to see that the intersection of this group with
\mathbb{G}_{E}
is
\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle
. In addition
\gamma\delta
inverts
P
and
Q
, so it only remains to show that
\mathcal{R}_{E}
is connected, and that
\mathbb{G}_{E}
acts transitively on its vertices and edges.

One can show that
\mathcal{R}_{E}
is connected in the same way that Scharlemann shows that
\mathcal{R}(V,\widehat{V})
is connected. Given
R\in\mathcal{R}_{E}
, Scharlemann
[
Sch04
, §3]
gives a surgery procedure that replaces
R
by an adjacent curve with fewer intersections with
P
. This construction does not create intersections with
E
. (The main input of the surgery operation is an arc of
R
on one side of
P
whose slope (defined in
[
Sch04
, §3]
) is
\infty
and an arc on the other side that has slope 0 and is disjoint from
R
. By our choice of

, the arc of slope
\infty
is not on the same side of
P
as
E
. Since
E
is disjoint from arcs of slope 0, the surgery procedure does not create any new intersections with
E
. See
[
Sch04
]
for details.)

Next we show that
\mathbb{G}_{E}
acts transitively on vertices
\mathcal{R}_{E}
. Fix a vertex

we show that there is

with

. By Scharlemann’s result, there exists

with

. There are two possibilities for

(each of the solid tori components of

contains exactly one primitive disk). If

, then

, and so either

or

has the desired properties

and

(note that

acts transitively on edges follows by observing that

acts transitively on reducing spheres that are adjacent to

∎

To summarize, we have a sequence of forgetful maps

\mathcal{M}(V,\widehat{V})\rightarrow\mathcal{R}(V,\widehat{V})\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(V),

and each is distance non-increasing. Up to quasi-isometry, each map is the inclusion of a space in a coned-off space. Identifying
\mathcal{M}(V,\widehat{V})
with
\mathbb{G}
(up to quasi-isometry), the space
\mathcal{R}(V,\widehat{V})
is quasi-isometric to the cone off of
\mathbb{G}
by cosets of

\mathcal{P}(V)
is quasi-isometric to the cone off of
\mathbb{G}
by cosets of
\mathbb{G}_{E}=\langle\alpha,\beta,\gamma\delta\rangle

is contained in
\mathbb{G}_{E}
, we can also view
\mathcal{P}(V)
as a cone off of
\mathcal{R}(V,\widehat{V})
, up to quasi-isometry. This is discussed further in §
5
.

To end this section, we record the following lemma for later use. The proof is elementary.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
G
be a finitely generated group. Fix complexes
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
with isometric
G
actions, and assume there is a distance non-increasing

\pi:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
. If the orbit map

is a quasi-isometric embedding, then so is the orbit map

2.3
Surgery of primitive disks

In this section we explain the construction of surgery paths in
\mathcal{P}(V)
.

Surgery.

Primitive disks are manipulated by surgery along bigons (boundary compressions). Some care must be taken to ensure that the disks resulting from surgery are primitive. Below we mostly follow the terminology in
[
MS13
, §8]
.

Fix a primitive disk
D
. A
surgery bigon
for
D
is a triple
(B,a,b)
, where
B\subset V
is a closed disk whose boundary is decomposed into two arcs
a,b
with
a=B\cap D
,
b=B\cap S
, and
B\cap\partial D=\partial a=\partial b
. Sometimes we refer to the surgery bigon as
B
, leaving
a
and
b
implicit.

To surger
D
along
(B,a,b)
, observe that
a
decomposes
D
into closed disks
D=D_{1}\cup D_{2}
whose intersection is
a
. The disks obtained from
D
by surgery along
B
are
D^{\prime}=D_{1}\cup B
and
D^{\prime\prime}=D_{2}\cup B
. After an isotopy,
D,D^{\prime},D^{\prime\prime}
are pairwise disjoint. Furthermore,
D^{\prime},D^{\prime\prime}
are not isotopic, since otherwise
\partial D\subset S
would be separating (hence not primitive).

A surgery bigon
(B,a,b)
for
D
is called a
boundary compression
if
b
is an essential arc in
S\setminus\partial D
. This implies that
\partial D^{\prime}
and
\partial D^{\prime\prime}
are essential in
S
.

We call a boundary compression
(B,a,b)
for
D

primitive
if there exists
\widehat{D}_{b}\subset\widehat{V}
so that
\partial D,\partial\widehat{D}_{b}
are disjoint and
b,\partial\widehat{D}_{b}
intersect transversely in a single point. If
B
is a primitive boundary compression, then
D^{\prime},D^{\prime\prime}
are both primitive disks; this follows from the definitions. Below we will only ever be interested in primitive boundary compressions.

Example 2.7
.

The following example gives a basic but important construction of primitive boundary compressions. Fix primitive disks
D,E\subset V
with
i(\partial D,\partial E)\neq 0
. We assume
\partial D,\partial E
are in minimal position and
D,E
intersect transversely. Since
V
is irreducible, we can isotope

so that the 1-manifold

has no closed components, i.e. each component of
D\cap E
is a proper arc. An outermost arc

of

cuts off a bigon

, which is always a primitive boundary compression for

by
[

, Thm. 2.3]

satisfy

which implies that neither of
D^{\prime},D^{\prime\prime}
is isotopic to
D
. Furthermore, neither of
D^{\prime},D^{\prime\prime}
is isotopic to
E
because
D^{\prime},D^{\prime\prime}
are disjoint from
D
and
i(\partial D,\partial E)\neq 0
.

Remark 2.8
.

We can repeat the above discussion with a single primitive disk
D
replaced by a collection
\Delta\subset S
of
k\geq 1
disjoint primitive disks. A primitive boundary compression for
\Delta
is a primitive boundary compression
B
for some component
D\subset\Delta
such that
B
is disjoint
\Delta\setminus D
. Surgering
D
along

results in a collection
\Delta^{\prime}
of

disjoint primitive disks. Furthermore, generalizing Example
2.7
, if

is a primitive disk with nonempty minimal intersection with
\Delta
, then an outermost bigon of

is a primitive boundary compression for
\Delta
, and
i(\Delta^{\prime},E)<i(\Delta,E)
.

Subsurfaces and surgery sequences.

A subsurface
X\subset S
is
essential
if every component of
\partial X
is essential in
S
. By convention, we will always assume our subsurfaces are connected.

We say a primitive disk

is
supported in

if
\partial D\subset X
and
\partial D
is not parallel to a component of
\partial X
. More generally,
D

cuts

if
\partial D
cannot be isotoped to be disjoint from
X
.

If there is a primitive disk supported in
X
, we say

is
primitively compressible
. Otherwise,
X
is
primitively incompressible
. Similarly, if
D
cuts

and there exists a primitive boundary compression
(B,a,b)
for

with

, then we say that

is
primitively boundary compressible
into

.

Definition 2.9

(Surgery sequences)
.

Fix a subsurface
X\subset S
and primitive disk
D
, and assume
\partial X,\partial D
are in minimal position and have nonempty intersection. A
primitive compression sequence
is a sequence
\{\Delta_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}
, where (i)
\Delta_{1}=\{D\}
, (ii)
\Delta_{k+1}
is a collection of
k+1
disjoint disks obtained from
\Delta_{k}
by a primitive boundary compression supported in
S\sm\partial X
, and (iii) for each
k
, each
D_{k}\in\Delta_{k}
intersects
\partial X
nontrivially.

Remark 2.10
.

If
S\setminus n(\partial X)
supports a primitive disk
E
and
\{\Delta_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}
is a surgery sequence for
X
, then either (i)
E
is disjoint from
\Delta_{n}
or (ii)
\Delta_{n}
can be primitively boundary compressed into
S\setminus n(\partial X)
; cf. Example
2.7
and Remark
2.8
. This motivates the following definition, which agrees with
[
MS13
, Definition 11.2]
.

Definition 2.11
.

A primitive compression sequence
\{\Delta_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}
for
X,D
is
maximal
if either
X
supports a primitive disk that is disjoint from
\Delta_{n}
or there is no primitive boundary compression for
\Delta_{n}
supported in
S\setminus n(\partial X)
. In the former case, the surgery sequence is said to
end in
S\setminus n(\partial X)
and in the latter case it is said to
end essentially
.

Whether or not a maximal primitive compression sequence for
(X,D)
ends essentially or ends in
S\setminus n(\partial X)
depends on whether or not
S\setminus\partial X
is primitively compressible. By Remark
2.10
, if
S\setminus n(\partial X)
is primitively compressible, then every maximal surgery sequence ends in
S\setminus n(\partial X)
, and the converse is also true, trivially. Then if
S\setminus\partial X
is primitively incompressible, and
\{\Delta_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}
is a maximal primitive compression sequence, then
\Delta_{n}
cannot be primitively boundary compressed into
S\setminus n(\partial X)
.

Any surgery sequence can be extended to a maximal sequence. If
\{\Delta_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}
is not maximal then, there exists a primitive boundary compression for
\Delta_{n}
supported in
S\setminus n(\partial X)
, which produces a collection
\Delta_{n+1}
. Since the given sequence is not maximal, every disk in
\Delta_{n+1}
intersects
\partial X
, which extends the sequence. Since each
D_{k}\in\Delta_{k}
intersects
\partial X
and the total intersection
i(\Delta_{k},\partial X)
is bounded above by
i(\partial D,\partial X)
, every surgery sequence is finite and can be extended to a maximal sequence.

General position.

For various arguments it is helpful to put curves on
S
or disks in
V
in a “general position”. Precise notions follow.

Recall that simple closed curves
a,b\subset S
are said to be in
minimal position
if they meet transversely and
S\setminus(a\cup b)
has no bigon components. Equivalently,
a,b
realize the geometric intersection number for the corresponding isotopy classes
[
FM12
, §1.2]
.

Disks
D,E\subset V
that intersecting transversely can be isotoped so that
D\cap E
is a union of arcs. This is because
V
is irreducible: if
c\subset D\cap E
is a circle, then
c
bounds a disk in both
D
and
E
, giving a 2-sphere in
V
that can be filled by a 3-ball; using these 3-balls, circular components of
D\cap E
can be removed by a sequence of isotopies. Then we say that
D\cap E
is
circle free
.

If given simple closed curves
a,b\subset S
and a subsurface
X\subset S
, we can isotope
a,b
so that
a,b,\partial X
are (pairwise) in minimal position. Then
S\setminus(a\cup b\cup\partial X)
generally will contain triangular components, and we can always isotope further so that these triangle components are all in
X
or all in
S\setminus X
(the same observation appears in
[
MS13
, §10]
).

Combining the preceding paragraphs, we say
D,E,X
are in
standard position
if (1) each pair of
\partial D,\partial E,\partial X
are in minimal position, (2) the triangles of
S\setminus(\partial D\cup\partial E\cup\partial X)
are either all in
X
or all in
S\setminus X
, and (3)
D\cap E
is circle free. Any triple
D,E,X
can be put in standard position.

2.5

An

is a fiber bundle with fiber
I=[0,1]
. In this paper

will always be a compact surface
B
. The
horiztonal boundary

\partial_{h}T\subset T
is the union of endpoints of each fiber, i.e. it is the total space of the induced bundle with fiber
\partial I
; the restriction
p:\partial_{h}T\rightarrow B
is a 2-fold covering map. The
vertical boundary

\partial_{v}T
is defined as
p^{-1}(\partial B)
. A subset of
T
is called
vertical
if it is a union of fibers.

If
T
is orientable and
\partial_{h}T
is connected, then the base
B
is non-orientable. Indeed, if
\partial_{h}T
is connected, then there exists a curve
c\subset B
so that
p^{-1}(c)
is a Möbius band. In order for
T
to be orientable,
c
must be 1-sided. From this it follows that
\partial_{h}T\rightarrow B
is the orientation double cover of
B
.

For example, if
\partial_{h}T=\Sigma_{0,2n}
, then
B=\mathbb{R}P^{2}\setminus(\bigcup_{n}D^{2})
, and if
\partial_{h}T=\Sigma_{1,2n}
, then
B=(\mathbb{R}P^{2}\#\mathbb{R}P^{2})\setminus(\bigcup_{n}D^{2})
.

Lemma 2.14
.

Let

be a fibered link

\mathring{F}\rightarrow S^{3}\setminus L\xrightarrow{\pi}S^{1}.

The union of two fibers with

is a Heegaard surface.

Proof.

Identify
S^{1}
with
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
. For
\theta\in S^{1}

\mathring{F}_{\theta}=\pi^{-1}(\theta)
, and denote
F_{\theta}=F_{\theta}\cup L
(a compact surface with boundary

\Sigma=F_{0}\cup F_{1/2}

is a Heegaard surface.

Consider the interval

. Then
\pi^{-1}(J)\cong\mathring{F}\times J
. We want to show that
W:=(\mathring{F}\times J)\cup K
is a handlebody.

First observe that
F\times J
is a handlebody. Choose a maximal collection of disjoint essential arcs
a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\subset F
whose complement is a disk. Then
a_{1}\times J,\ldots,a_{m}\times J
is a collection of disjoint disks whose complement in

is a 3-ball.

Next observe that
W
is homeomorphic to
F\times J\cup C(q)
, where

is the mapping cylinder of the projection

. Observe that

Proposition 2.19

(Fiber-preserving homeomorphisms)
.

Let
F
be a compact surface, and fix
M=(F\times I)\cup_{\phi}(\widehat{F}\times I)
as above. Fix an orientation-preserving homeomorphisms
g:M\rightarrow M
that preserves this Heegaard splitting. If
g
preserves
F\times 0\cup F\times 1
, then
g
is isotopic, through homeomorphisms preserving the Heegaard splitting, to a homeomorphism whose restriction to each of
F\times I
and
\widehat{F}\times I
is fiber preserving.

Proof.

There are two cases: either the components of
F\times 0\cup F\times 1
are preserved, or they are interchanged. We explain the case each component is preserved; the other case is similar.

Since a homeomorphism of a handlebody that is the identity on the boundary is isotopic to the identity
[
Hat76
, §1]
, it suffices to show that there exists a fiber-preserving homeomorphism
f
so that
f^{-1}\circ g\bigr|_{\Sigma}
is isotopic to the identity, where
\Sigma=\partial(F\times I)
.

Set
g_{i}=g\bigr|_{F\times\{i\}}
for
i=0,1
.
Observe that
g_{0}
and
g_{1}
are isotopic homeomorphisms of
F
, since they induce the same (outer) automorphism of
\pi_{1}(F)
(identifying
\pi_{1}(F\times 1)=\pi_{1}(F\times I)=\pi_{1}(F\times 0)
. Note that this isotopy is not necessarily the identity on
\partial F
. In any case, by extension we can isotope
g
to a homeomorphism (still denoted
g
) satisfying
g_{0}=g_{1}
.

Let
f=g_{0}\times\id
on
F\times I
and
\widehat{F}\times I
. By construction,
f^{-1}\circ g
is the identity on
F\times\{0,1\}
, so
f^{-1}\circ g\bigr|_{\Sigma}
is supported on
(\partial F)\times I
. Then the isotopy class of
f^{-1}\circ g\bigr|_{\Sigma}
is a multitwist.

By
[
Oer02
, Thm. 1.11]
, a multitwist about
c\subset\Sigma
extends to a handlebody only if the curves in
c
bound a union of essential disks and annuli. Since no curve in
c=\partial F\times\{1/2\}
bounds a disk in
F\times I
, and no two curves of
c
bound an annulus in
F\times I
, we conclude that
f^{-1}\circ g\bigr|_{\Sigma}
is isotopic to the identity.
∎

Remark 2.20
.

Assume that
f
is fiber preserving on
F\times I
and
\widehat{F}\times I
. If
f
preserves the components of

, then we can write

on

and

on

, and the gluing

forces the relation

in

. Together these imply that
\psi\>\phi\>\psi^{-1}=\phi
.

Suppose instead that

interchanges the components of

on

and

We also observe that the homeomorphism

2.7
Heegaard splittings and Mayer–Vietoris

Here we make a simple observation that will be useful in multiple arguments.

Lemma 2.21
.

Let
M
be a
3
-manifold with a Heegaard splitting
M=W\cup_{\Sigma}\widehat{W}
. Suppose there exists a multicurve
c\subset\Sigma
that bounds a surface in
W
and
\widehat{W}
. If
c
can be oriented so that the associated homology class
[c]\in H_{1}(\Sigma)
is nonzero, then
H_{2}(M)\neq 0
.

Proof.

The Mayer–Vietoris sequence associated to the Heegaard splitting contains the exact sequence

H_{2}(M)\xrightarrow{d}H_{1}(\Sigma)\xrightarrow{j}H_{1}(W)\oplus H_{1}(\widehat{W})

By assumption there is a nonzero class
H_{1}(\Sigma)
in
\ker(j)=\im(d)
, so
H_{2}(M)\neq 0
.
∎

3.2
The hole
X
is primitively compressible

Recall from §
2
that
X\subset S
is primitively compressible if there exists a primitive disk
E\subset V
such that
\partial E\subset X
is nonperipheral.

Proposition 3.3

(Primitively compressible holes)
.

Let
X\subset S
be a non-annular subsurface that is primitively compressible. If
X
is a hole for
\mathcal{P}(V)
, then either
X=S
or
X
has diameter
\leq 13
.

Proof of Proposition
3.3
.

Fix
X
as in the statement, and suppose that there exist primitive disks
D_{1},E_{1}
with
d_{X}(D_{1},E_{1})\geq 14
. We will show
X=S
, proceeding by contradiction. Since
X\neq\Sigma_{0,2}
(by assumption) and
X\neq\Sigma_{0,3}
(because
\mathcal{C}(\Sigma_{0,3})
is empty),
X
is homeomorphic to one of
\Sigma_{0,4},\Sigma_{1,1}
, and
\Sigma_{1,2}
.

By Lemma
2.13
, there exist disks
D,E
supported in
X
with
d_{X}(D_{1},D),d_{X}(E_{1},E)\leq 6
. Then
d_{X}(D,E)\geq 2
by the triangle inequality. Since
\partial D,\partial E
are vertices of
\mathcal{C}(X)
, this implies that
D
and
E
have nonzero intersection number. After an isotopy, we may assume that
D\cap E
is circle free (c.f. §
2.3
).

Consider an outermost bigon
(B,a,b)
cut off from
E
by
D\cap E
, and the surgered disks
D^{\prime},D^{\prime\prime}
. The disks
D,D^{\prime},D^{\prime\prime}
are disjoint, and no two are isotopic (Example
2.7
). Furthermore,
\partial D,\partial D^{\prime},\partial D^{\prime\prime}
bound a subsurface
Y\cong\Sigma_{0,3}
that is contained in
X
. Since
X
is homeomorphic to one of
\Sigma_{0,4}
,
\Sigma_{1,1}
, or
\Sigma_{1,2}
, one of the boundary component of
Y
is parallel to a boundary component of
X
. This means the corresponding disk (
D^{\prime}
or
D^{\prime\prime}
) can be isotoped to be disjoint from
X
, contradicting the assumption that
X
is a hole.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

By the proof of Proposition
3.3
, if
X\subset S
is a hole and there exists primitive disks
D,E
supported in
X
such that
i(\partial D,\partial E)\neq 0
, then there exist a triple
(D,D^{\prime},D^{\prime\prime})
of disjoint disks supported in
X
, and this implies that
X=S
. We will use this observation later.

3.3
The hole
X
is primitively incompressible

In this section we prove that if
X
is primitively incompressible with sufficiently large diameter, then
X
is a genus-1 Seifert surface for a fibered knot
K\subset S\subset S^{3}
. This is the content Theorem
3.5
, although we formulate the theorem slightly differently.

Theorem 3.5

(Primitively incompressible holes)
.

If
X\subset S
is a primitively incompressible hole for
\mathcal{P}(V)
with diameter
\geq 61
, then
V
and
\widehat{V}
are homeomorphic to the
I
-bundle
\Sigma_{1,1}\times I
with
X
a component of the horizontal boundary of each.

Theorem
3.5
is similar to
[
MS13
, Thm. 12.1]
, which we state below in a special case.

Theorem 3.6

(Masur–Schleimer)
.

Suppose
X
is an incompressible hole for
\mathcal{D}(V)
with diameter
\geq 57
. Then there is an
I
-bundle
T
with an embedding
T\hookrightarrow V
so that
\partial_{h}T\subset S
and with
X
a component of
\partial_{h}T
.

We cannot use Theorem
3.6
directly to prove Theorem
3.5
since a primitively incompressible hole is not necessarily incompressible. However, we will use some arguments from
[
MS13
, Thm. 12.1]
in our proof.

Proof of Theorem
3.5
.

Fix
X
as in the statement of the Theorem. We divide the proof into several steps:

1.

Assuming that
X
has diameter
\geq 57
with respect to
\mathcal{P}(V)
, we build an embedded
I
-bundle
T\hookrightarrow V
that contains
X
as a component of its horizontal boundary. For this we follow the argument of
[
MS13
, §12]
as far as possible. We show that either
T\hookrightarrow V
is (isotopic to) a homeomorphism or every component of
\partial X
is nullhomologous in
V
.

2.

We show that if
X
has diameter
\geq 61
, then
X
is also a primitively incompressible hole for
\mathcal{P}(\widehat{V})
with diameter
\geq 57
. Our proof of this relies on details of the proof of Step 1.

3.

Combining Steps 1 and 2, we have embedded
I
-bundles
T\hookrightarrow V
and
\widehat{T}\hookrightarrow\widehat{V}
, each with
X
as a component of the horizontal boundary. We use the topology of
S^{3}=V\cup\widehat{V}
to rule out all cases where either
T\hookrightarrow V
or
\widehat{T}\hookrightarrow\widehat{V}
is not a homeomorphism, and from this we conclude.

Step 1.

In this step we construct an embedded
I
-bundle
T\hookrightarrow V
. The assumption needed in the construction is that
X
is a primitively incompressible hole for
\mathcal{P}(V)
with diameter
\geq 57
.
Part of the construction follows
[
MS13
, §12]
and we refer the reader there for more details. We mostly use their notation. We also remark that a similar construction appeared in earlier work of Oertel; cf.
[
Oer02
, Lem. 2.13]
.

Fix primitive disks
D_{0},E_{0}\subset V
so that
d_{X}(D_{0},E_{0})\geq 57
. By Lemma
2.13
, we can find primitive disks
D,E
that cannot be primitively boundary compressed into
S\setminus n(\partial X)
and with
d_{X}(D,E)\geq 45
.

We put
D,E,X
in standard position with triangular components of
S\setminus(\partial D\cup\partial E\cup\partial X)
contained in
S\setminus X
(c.f. §
2.3
).

Give
D
the structure of a polygon with vertices
\partial D\cap\partial X
, and similarly for
E
. The edges of these polygons alternate between
X
and
Y:=\overline{S\setminus X}
. Set
\Gamma=D\cap E
. The assumption that
D,E
cannot be primitively boundary compressed into

implies that every arc in
\Gamma
is a
diagonal
, i.e. it connects a pair of distinct sides. (This argument is the same as
[
MS13
, first claim of §12.5]
, although our assumption is slightly different.)

A side with not many different diagonals.

A counting argument shows that there are sides
a\subset\partial D\cap X
and
b\subset\partial E\cap X
meeting at most
8
different types of diagonals (two diagonals have the same type if they are parallel, i.e. their endpoints share the same sides). See
[
MS13
, Lem. 12.4]
.

Arcs and rectangles.

We decompose

into at most
8
subarcs
\{a_{i}\}
, each containing one parallel collection of diagonals, and we choose rectangles

with one side of

equal to
a_{i}
and so that

contains all the diagonals in
\Gamma
meeting
a_{i}
. We write
a_{i}^{\prime}\subset\partial D
for the side of
R_{i}
that’s parallel to
a_{i}
. We do the same for

, getting subarcs
\{b_{j}\}
, rectangles
Q_{j}\subset E
, and parallel subarcs

.

Large arcs.

If
|a_{i}\cap b_{j}|\geq 3
for some

, then
a_{i}
and
b_{j}
are called

. Similarly, say
a_{i}^{\prime}
and
b_{j}^{\prime}
are large if
|a_{i}^{\prime}\cap b_{j}^{\prime}|\geq 3
. Note that
|a_{i}\cap b_{j}|=|a_{i}^{\prime}\cap b_{j}^{\prime}|
, so if
a_{i}
and
b_{j}
are large, then
a_{i}^{\prime}
and
b_{j}^{\prime}
are large (and vice versa).

Graphs
\Theta,\Theta^{\prime}\subset S
.

Define
\Theta\subset X
the union of all the large
a_{i}
and
b_{j}
, and define
\Theta^{\prime}
the union of the large
a_{i}^{\prime}
and
b_{j}^{\prime}
. The set
\Theta
(and
\Theta^{\prime}
similarly) has the structure of a graph where every vertex has degree either
1
(coming from endpoints of
a_{i}
and
b_{j}
) or
4
(coming from intersections of
a_{i}
and
b_{j}
). In addition,
\Theta
and
\Theta^{\prime}
are isomorphic as graphs in an obvious way.

Define

as a small regular neighborhood of
\Theta,\Theta^{\prime}
respectively. We have the following properties, which are proved in
[
MS13
]
. We give some brief explanation of the proofs to help assure the reader that these proofs hold in our setting.

•

[
MS13
, Claim 12.7]
: The graph
\Theta
is nonempty.

Proof sketch. If
\Theta=\vn
, then all the
a_{i},b_{j}
are small. From this one deduces that
|a\cap b|\leq 128
. Since
a,b
are components of
\partial D\cap X
and
\partial E\cap X
, this implies that

can’t be large. In fact,

, contradicting our assumption.

•

[
MS13
, Claim 12.8]
: No component of either
\Theta
or
\Theta^{\prime}
is contained in a disk in
S
or an annulus in

that’s peripheral in

.

Proof sketch. If some component of
\Theta
is contained in a disk or peripheral annulus, then one argues that either some

cut a bigon from

, or

cut a triangle contained in

. Both of these contradict our assumption that

are in standard position. The argument for
\Theta^{\prime}
is the same.

•

[
MS13
, Claim 12.9]
: Let

be a component of

, and let

be a component of
\partial Z_{1}
. Then

is either inessential or peripheral in

.

Proof sketch. Supposing that

is both essential and non-peripheral, one can deduce that

(counting separately intersections with

coming from large and small

). Similarly,

, and this implies that

, a contradiction.

•

\Theta
(and hence also
\Theta^{\prime}
) is connected, and
\Theta
fills
X
.

Proof sketch. This follows from the previous items: any component

fills

by
[
MS13
, Claim 12.9]
. Then any other component must be contained in a disk or peripheral annulus in

, which contradicts
[
MS13
, Claim 12.8]
. Thus

is connected and fills

, and this implies the same for

Let
\mathcal{R}=\{R_{i}\}
and
\mathcal{Q}=\{Q_{j}\}
be the large rectangles. Observe that

is an

-bundle whose horizontal boundary is

. We can thicken

to an
I
-bundle

with horizontal boundary

.

Case 1:

\Theta
and
\Theta^{\prime}
intersect nontrivially. Then

is a connected subsurface of

. Since complementary components of

in

are disks and peripheral annuli, the same is also true for complementary components of

in

.

In this case, the horizontal boundary

is orientable (because

The vertical boundary

is a union of annuli, each connecting a pair of components of

. If

is such an annulus, and both components of

bound disks in

, then the union of

to preserve the

-bundle

with the following properties:

(1)

For every component

of

is peripheral in

(2)

\partial X
is parallel to a boundary component of some annulus

.

Next we use

to show that every component of
\partial X
is null-homologous in

. We separate into cases, depending on whether

is homeomorphic to
\Sigma_{1,1}
,
\Sigma_{1,2}
, or
\Sigma_{0,4}
(we do not need to consider
\Sigma_{0,2}
,
\Sigma_{0,3}
, or
\Sigma_{2}
).

Case 1(a):

. By properties (1) and (2),
\partial_{v}T
has a single component

. One component of
\partial A
bounds a disk in

, and the other is parallel to
\partial X
. Thus

bounds a disk in

.

Case 1(b):

X\cong\Sigma_{1,2}
. Then
\partial_{v}T
has either one or two components.

If
\partial_{v}T
has one component

, then the two components of
\partial A
are parallel to the two components of
\partial X
. If

is compressible, then each component of

bounds a disk in

. Otherwise, if

is incompressible, then

can be isotoped into

. From this we conclude that
\partial X
bounds an embedded
(\mathbb{R}P^{2}\#\mathbb{R}P^{2})\setminus D^{2}
in

because in this case

is a bundle over
(\mathbb{R}P^{2}\#\mathbb{R}P^{2})\setminus D^{2}
, and

If
\partial_{v}T
has two components

.

Case 1(c):

. In this case

If

has four components, then every component of

bounds a disk in

.

If
\partial_{v}T
has three components, then two components of

. The other two components of

. In this case,

.

, so

is an

-bundle over
\mathbb{R}P^{2}\setminus(D^{2}\cup D^{2})
.

First suppose that

\geq 57

, then two components of

Overall, we find that in every case, every component of
\partial X

. Note that the only case when

has a component that is separating in

Case 2:

\Theta
and
\Theta^{\prime}
are disjoint. In this case

with horizontal boundary

Claim.

\xi(Y)\geq\xi(X)
, where

Since

is homeomorphic to one of
\Sigma_{1,1},\Sigma_{1,2},\Sigma_{0,4}\subset\Sigma_{2}

and

.

Since

(disjoint subsurfaces glued along boundary),

and

. Since

As a consequence, we also conclude that

fills

Every component

-bundle

.

Step 2.

In this step we show that if

4
Distance formula for
\mathcal{P}(V)

Our proof of the distance formula (Theorem
C
) follows closely the approach of
[
MS13
]
. There are two parts: an upper and a lower bound on
d_{\mathcal{P}(V)}(D,E)
. The lower bound follows directly from
[
MM00
, Thms. 6.10, 6.12]
, as explained in
[
MS13
, Thm. 5.14]
. To prove the upper bound requires additional work, and for this we use the axiomatic approach of
[
MS13
, §13]
.

The general axiom-based proof of the upper bound
[
MS13
, Thm. 13.1]
is an inductive argument, which, in our case, necessitates the definition of relative versions of
\mathcal{P}(V)
. If
X\subset S
is a hole for
\mathcal{P}(V)
, then we define
\mathcal{P}(V;X)\subset\mathcal{P}(V)
as the subcomplex spanned by primitive disks supported in
X
. Note that we do not necessarily assume that
X
has infinite diameter. If
X
is primitively incompressible, then
\mathcal{P}(V;X)
is empty, which is undesirable, so when
X
also has infinite diameter, we define
\mathcal{P}(V;X)
as the complex spanned by vertical primitive disks in the
I
-bundle constructed in the proof of Theorem
D
. By the nature of the induction, we will not need to deal with the case
X
is a primitively incompressible hole with bounded diameter, so we keep
\mathcal{P}(V;X)
empty in this case.

For the induction, we need to prove the following more general version of the distance formula upper bound.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
X\subset S
be a hole for
\mathcal{P}(V)
. Given
c\geq 0
, there is a constant
A
depending only on
c
and the topological type of
X
, such that for any two vertices
D,E\in\mathcal{P}(V;X)
, we have

d_{\mathcal{P}(V;X)}(D,E)\leq A\sum\{d_{Y}(D,E)\}_{c}+A,

where the sum is over all holes
Y\subset X
for
\mathcal{P}(V)
.

Given our previous work, the most interesting case of Theorem
4.1
is when
X=S
, as we now explain. If
X\subsetneq S
is an infinite-diameter hole, then the distance formula holds by our classification of such
X
in Theorem
D
and by the computation of
\mathcal{P}(V;X)
given in §
3.5
; see Remark
3.9
. In the remaining case, when
X
is a primitively compressible hole, Theorem
4.1
holds trivially by the following proposition.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
X\subsetneq S
be a primitively compressible hole for
\mathcal{P}(V)
. Then
\mathcal{P}(V;X)
is a connected, graph with at most two vertices.

Proof.

As noted in Remark
3.4
, if a hole
X\subset S
supports two primitive disks with nonzero intersection number, then
X=S
. Therefore, our assumption
X\neq S
implies that
\mathcal{P}(V;X)
is either a single vertex or is two vertices connected by an edge.
∎

It remains to prove Theorem
4.1
for
X=S
. It is here that we use the axiomatic approach of
[
MS13
]
.

4.1
Axiomatic approach to the distance formula for
\mathcal{P}(V)

This section explains a proof of Theorem
4.1
in the main case of interest
X=S
.

Our approach is to use
[
MS13
, Thm. 13.1]
, which states that there are seven axioms that together imply the desired upper bound. Instead of writing the full statement of the axioms with all the necessary definitions (this takes multiple pages in
[
MS13
]
), we give precise references to
[
MS13
]
and follow their notation as closely as possible. Our goal is to explain why the axioms hold for
\mathcal{P}(V)
, focusing on the parts that are the least straight-forward. Ultimately, the argument is very similar to the proof in
[
MS13
, §18-19]
that the axioms are satisfied for the disk complex
\mathcal{D}(V)
, using our analogous results. In fact, there are even some simplifications because we only work in genus 2 and because the classification of large holes (Theorem
D
) is simpler than the corresponding result for
\mathcal{D}(V)
, c.f.
[
MS13
, Thm. 1.1]
.

Below we list the axioms and explain why they hold for
\mathcal{P}(V)
. See
[
MS13
, §13]
for statements of the axioms, and see
[
MS13
, §18-19]
for additional information about why the axioms hold for
\mathcal{D}(V)
.

Large Holes Axiom.

This is stated in
[
MS13
, Axiom 13.3]
. Here we may take “large” to mean diameter
\geq 61
, which implies the hole has infinite diameter (Theorem
D
). This axiom states that any two large holes either overlap
[
MS13
, Defn. 2.8]
or are paired
[
MS13
, Defn. 5.6]
. This holds trivially for
\mathcal{P}(V)
because all proper holes are homeomorphic to
\Sigma_{1,1}
, so the only way two large holes can be disjoint is if they are paired, cf. Remark
3.10
.

Marking Path, Accessibility Intervals, Combinatorial Sequence Axioms.

These three axioms concern properties of the following data associated to a pair of primitive disks
D,E\subset V
. These axioms hold for the exact same reason as they hold for
\mathcal{D}(V)
. Start with a surgery path
D=D_{0},\ldots,D_{N}=E
as defined in §
2.3
.

•

The
marking path
is defined as follows. The sequence
\{D_{n}\}
induces a nested sequence of train tracks
\tau_{0}\succ\cdots\succ\tau_{N}
by the construction explained in
[
MM04
, §4]
. A train track
\tau
has an associated a vertex cycle
\vertex(\tau)\subset\mathcal{C}(S)
(see e.g.
[
MM04
, §3]
), which is a marking in the sense of
[
MS13
, §2.9]
. The marking path is defined as
\mu_{n}=\vertex(\tau_{n})
.

•

Define the
combinatorial sequence
as follows. By
[
MM04
, Thm. 1.3]
, the subset
\bigcup_{n=0}^{N}\vertex(\tau_{n})\subset\mathcal{C}(S)
is an unparameterized quasi-geodesic, which means that there is a subsequence
\tau_{0}\succ\cdots\succ\tau_{K}
such that for any choice of
v_{k}\in\vertex(\tau_{k})
, the sequence
v_{0},\ldots,v_{K}
is a quasi-geodesic in
\mathcal{C}(S)
. The combinatorial sequence, is the associated sequence
D=D_{0},\ldots,D_{K}=E
of primitive disks.

•

By construction of the combinatorial sequence, there is an increasing
re-indexing function

r:[0,K]\rightarrow[0,N]
.

•

Given the sequence
\{\tau_{n}\}_{n=0}^{N}
, for each subsurface
X\subset S
, an
accessibility interval

J_{X}\subset[0,N]
is defined in
[
MMS12
, §5.2]
.

The Marking Path Axiom
[
MS13
, Axiom 13.4]
states that the
\mu_{n}
have nested supports and that the projection
\pi_{X}(\mu_{n})
to any subsurface
X\subset S
is an unparameterized quasi-geodesic in
\mathcal{C}(X)
with constant depending only on
\mathcal{P}(V)
. The former property holds by construction, and the latter property holds as a consequence of a general result proved by Masur–Mosher–Schleimer
[
MMS12
, Thm. 5.5]
.

The Accessibility Intervals Axiom (see
[
MS13
, Axiom 13.5]
) is verified in
[
MMS12
, Thm. 5.3]
, just as for
\mathcal{D}(V)
; see
[
MS13
, §18]
.

The Combinatorial Sequence Axiom (see
[
MS13
, Axiom 13.6]
) follows from the construction of
\{D_{k}\}_{k=0}^{K}
and
\{\mu_{n}\}_{n=0}^{N}
, just as for
\mathcal{D}(V)
. Details are in
[
MM04
, §3-4]
; see also
[
MS13
, Thm. 19.3]
.

Replacement Axiom.

This is stated in
[
MS13
, Axiom 13.7]
. The proof of this axiom for
\mathcal{P}(V)
follows quickly from the following claim (cf.
[
MS13
, §19.5]
).

Claim.
Let
Y\subsetneq S
be a large incompressible hole for
\mathcal{P}(V)
with corresponding
I
-bundle
T
, and fix a primitive disk
D
such that
i(\partial D,\partial Y)<K
for some constant
K
, then there exists a primitive disk
D^{\prime}
that is vertical in
T
and such that
d_{\mathcal{P}(V)}(D,D^{\prime})\leq\log_{2}(K)
.

Proof of Claim.
The proof is similar to
[
MS13
, §19.5]
. Consider a maximal sequence of primitive compressions of
D
into
S\setminus\partial Y
. Each compression replaces
D
with two disks, and we choose the one with at most half as many intersections with
\partial Y
. This process ends after
\leq\log_{2}(K)
steps with a disk
D^{\prime}
with no primitive compression into
S\setminus\partial Y
, and by the argument in §
3.5
,
D^{\prime}
can be isotoped to be vertical. This proves the claim.

Straight Intervals Axiom.

See
[
MS13
, Axiom 13.13]
. This axiom holds for for
\mathcal{P}(V)
by the exact same argument as
[
MS13
, §19.7]
.

Shortcut Intervals Axiom.

See
[
MS13
, Axiom 13.14]
. The proof of this axiom ultimately boils down to the following claim (cf.
[
MS13
, §19.8]
).

Claim.
Let
Z
be a non-hole for
\mathcal{P}(V)
, and fix disks
D,E
such that
i(\partial D,\partial Z)<K
and
i(\partial E,\partial Z)<K
. Then
d_{\mathcal{P}(V)}(D,E)<K^{\prime}
for some constant
K^{\prime}
depending only on
K
.

Proof of Claim.
Let
Y\subset S
be the (closure of the) complement of
Z
. Note that
Y
is primitively compressible because
Z
is a non-hole for
\mathcal{P}(V)
and
Y\cup Z=S
. As in the argument for the Replacement Axiom, we choose maximal compression sequences
D\leadsto D^{\prime}
and
E\leadsto E^{\prime}
of
D
and
E
into
S\setminus\partial Z
with each sequence of length
\leq\log_{2}(K)
. It suffices to give a uniform bound on
d_{\mathcal{P}(V)}(D^{\prime},E^{\prime})
. If
D^{\prime}
and
E^{\prime}
are disjoint, then we are done, so we assume
D^{\prime},E^{\prime}
intersect nontrivially. Then
D^{\prime},E^{\prime}
lie in the same component of
S\setminus\partial Z
. If this component is
Z
, then
d_{\mathcal{P}(V)}(D^{\prime},E^{\prime})\leq 2
since
Y
is primitively compressible. Next suppose
D^{\prime},E^{\prime}
are contained in a component
Y^{\prime}
of
Y
. Observe that
Y^{\prime}
is not a hole for
\mathcal{P}(V)
since if it were, this would imply
Y=S
by Remark
3.4
. Then
Y
is not a hole, so again we conclude that
d_{\mathcal{P}(V)}(D^{\prime},E^{\prime})\leq 2
.

4.2
Consequences of the distance formula

In this section we explain several consequences of the distance formula that we use in Section
5
to prove Theorem
A
. The results in this section appear in a similar form in
[
BBKL20
]
, although we need to formulate them differently, replacing the assumption that
G
is quasi-isometrically embedded in
\Mod(S)
with the assumption that
G<\mathbb{G}
and the orbit map
G\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(V)
is quasi-isometrically embedded. In fact, the results we prove work more generally:

In this section (and this section only), we let
S
denote any closed oriented surface of genus
\geq 2
, and we fix a subgroup
\mathbb{G}<\Mod(S)
that acts on a subcomplex
\mathcal{P}\subset\mathcal{C}(S)
. We assume that
\mathcal{P}
has a distance formula. Similar to
[
BBKL20
, §2]
, we express the distance formula in the following form, which is equivalent to (
3
): given
\beta>0
(sufficiently large), there is
\lambda>0
so that

\frac{1}{\lambda}d_{\mathcal{P}}(u,u^{\prime})\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}X\subset S\\
\text{ hole for $\mathcal{P}$}\end{subarray}}\{d_{X}(u,u^{\prime})\}_{\beta}\leq\lambda\>d_{\mathcal{P}}(u,u^{\prime})

(5)

for any vertices
u,u^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{P}
satisfying either
\sum_{X}\{d_{X}(u,u^{\prime})\}_{\beta}\neq 0
or
d_{\mathcal{P}}(u,u^{\prime})\geq\lambda
.

We also assume that there is complex
\mathcal{M}
whose vertices are markings (i.e. vertices of
\mathcal{M}(S)
; see §
2.2
) such that (1)
\mathcal{M}
is quasi-isometric to
\mathbb{G}
, and (2) there is a uniform bound on the distance in
\mathcal{M}(S)
between adjacent markings in
\mathcal{M}
.

All of these assumptions hold for
\mathbb{G}
the genus-2 Goeritz group,
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}(V)
the primitive disk complex, and
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}(V,\widehat{V})
the Heegaard marking complex. See Theorem
C
and Lemmas
2.3
and
2.4
.

The main result needed for the proof of Theorem
A
is Proposition
4.3
. This result and its proof are similar to the main theorem of
[
BBKL20
]
.

Proposition 4.3
.

Fix a finitely-generated subgroup
G<\mathbb{G}
. If the orbit map
G\rightarrow\mathcal{P}
is a q.i. embedding, but the orbit map
G\rightarrow\mathcal{C}(S)
is not a q.i. embedding, then
G
contains a reducible element.

To prove Proposition
4.3
, we use two additional results. The first, Proposition
4.4
, is proved in
[
BBKL20
, Prop. 3.1]
. The second, Proposition
4.5
, is similar to
[
BBKL20
, Prop. 4.1]
but with a slightly different assumption. We sketch the proof below.

For a subsurface
Z\subset S
, we write
\mathcal{M}(Z)
for the marking complex; see
[
BBKL20
, §2]
. Here “marking” refers to the clean, complete markings defined in
[
MM00
, §2.5]
; this differs from the more flexible notion of markings
[
MS13
, §2.9]
that is used in §
4.1
.

Proposition 4.4

(Reducibility criterion)
.

Let
G<\Mod(S)
be finitely generated, and let
|g|
denote the word length of
g\in G
with respect to a finite generating set. Fix a marking
\mu
. For any
c>0
there exists
R=R(c)>0
so that if
|g|>R
and there exists a proper subsurface
Z\subset S
with
d_{\mathcal{M}(Z)}(\mu,g\mu)\geq c|g|
, then
G
contains a reducible element.

Proposition 4.5

(Linearly summing projections)
.

Fix
G<\mathbb{G}
with finite generating set
\Omega
, and fix a marking
\mu\in\mathcal{M}
. Assume that the orbit map
G\rightarrow\mathcal{P}
is a quasi-isometric embedding. Then there exists
K,C>0
with the following property. For
g\in G
, if
|g|>C
and
d_{S}(\mu,g\mu)<\frac{|g|}{K}
, then we can find subsurfaces
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{k}\subset S
and write
g=g_{1}\cdots g_{k}
geodesically in
G
with
k\leq d_{S}(\mu,g\mu)
so that

|g|\leq K\sum_{j=1}^{k}d_{\mathcal{M}(Z_{j})}(\mu,g_{j}\mu).

Furthermore, by increasing
K
we can also arrange that
d_{\mathcal{M}(Z_{j})}(\mu,g_{j}\mu)\leq K|g_{j}|
for each
j
.

Proof of Proposition
4.3
.

Fix a marking
\mu\in\mathcal{M}
. Let
K,C
be the constants from Proposition
4.5
, and fix
M>K
(to be chosen more precisely later). Since
G\rightarrow\mathcal{C}(S)
is not a q.i. embedding, we can find group elements
g\in G
with
|g|
arbitrarily large and
d_{S}(\mu,g\mu)\leq\frac{|g|}{M}
. Then we can apply Proposition
4.5
: write
g=g_{1}\cdots g_{k}
with
k\leq d_{S}(\mu,g\mu)
and take
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{k}\subset S
so that
|g|\leq K\sum_{j=1}^{k}d_{\mathcal{M}(Z_{j})}(\mu,g_{j}\mu)
.

For
t>0
, let
R(\frac{1}{t})
be the constant from Proposition
4.4
, and consider the partition of
\{1,\ldots,k\}
into

J_{\leq}(t)=\{j:d_{\mathcal{M}(Z_{j})}(\mu,g_{j}\mu)\leq\frac{|g_{j}|}{t}\}\>\>\>\text{ and }\>\>\>J_{>}(t)=\{j:d_{\mathcal{M}(Z_{j})}(\mu,g_{j}\mu)>\frac{|g_{j}|}{t}\}.

If
|g_{j}|\leq R(\frac{1}{t})
for each
j\in J_{>}(t)
, then using the conclusions of Proposition
4.5
and the assumption
d_{S}(\mu,g\mu)\leq\frac{|g|}{M}
, we obtain the following estimate (cf.
[
BBKL20
, §5]
):

\begin{array}[]{rcl}|g|&\leq&K\sum_{j=1}^{k}d_{\mathcal{M}(Z_{j})}(\mu,g_{j}\mu)\\[5.69054pt]
&=&K\left[\sum_{j\in J_{\leq}(t)}d_{\mathcal{M}(Z_{j})}(\mu,g_{j}\mu)+\sum_{j\in J_{>}(t)}d_{\mathcal{M}(Z_{j})}(\mu,g_{j}\mu)\right]\\[5.69054pt]
&\leq&K\left[\frac{|g|}{t}+\sum_{j\in J_{>}(t)}K|g_{j}|\right]\\[5.69054pt]
&\leq&K\left[\frac{|g|}{t}+k\cdot K\cdot R(\frac{1}{t})\right]\\[5.69054pt]
&\leq&K\left[\frac{|g|}{t}+d_{S}(\mu,g\mu)\cdot K\cdot R(\frac{1}{t})\right]\\[5.69054pt]
&\leq&K\left[\frac{|g|}{t}+\frac{|g|}{M}\cdot K\cdot R(\frac{1}{t})\right]=\left(\frac{K}{t}+\frac{K\cdot R(\frac{1}{t})}{M}\right)\cdot|g|\\[5.69054pt]
\end{array}

Now choose
t>2K
and any
M>2K\cdot R(\frac{1}{t})
, so that
\left(\frac{K}{t}+\frac{K\cdot R(\frac{1}{t})}{M}\right)<1
. By the computation above, there must be some
j\in J_{>}(t)
with
|g_{j}|>R(\frac{1}{t})
. Then we may apply Proposition
4.4
to conclude that
G
contains a reducible element.
∎

It remains to explain the proof of Proposition
4.5
. Before this, we recall the distance formula for the marking complex due to
[
MM00
]
; see also
[
BBKL20
, Thm. 2.7]
for the form that we state it.
Let
Z\subset S
be a subsurface. Given
\beta>0
(sufficiently large), there is
\kappa>0
so that

\frac{1}{\kappa}d_{\mathcal{M}(Z)}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})\leq\sum_{Y\subset Z}\{d_{Y}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})\}_{\beta}\leq\kappa\>d_{\mathcal{M}(Z)}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})

(6)

for every
\mu,\mu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}(S)
so that either the middle term is nonzero or
d_{\mathcal{M}(Z)}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})\geq\kappa
.

Proof of Proposition
4.5
.

We explain the last statement first (this is one difference between our argument and the argument in
[
BBKL20
, §4]
). Since the orbit map
G\rightarrow\mathcal{P}
is a quasi-isometric embedding, so too is the orbit map
G\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
(see Lemma
2.6
). Then by Lemma
2.3
,
d_{\mathcal{M}(S)}(\mu,g_{j}\mu)
is coarsely bounded above by
|g_{j}|
. Combining this with the fact that subsurface projection of markings is coarsely Lipschitz (see
[
BBKL20
, Prop. 2.3]
), one deduces the last sentence of the proposition.

The rest of the proof is very similar to
[
BBKL20
, §4]
. The only difference is that we substitute, in the appropriate place, the distance formula for
\mathcal{P}
(
5
) for the Masur–Minsky distance formula (
6
). (Note, however, that we still use the Masur–Minsky distance formula in a different part of the argument.) Since precise details are contained in
[
BBKL20
, §4]
, we will only sketch the argument. In particular, we will be imprecise with some of the constants (e.g. we will not carefully specify
C
).

Fix
\beta\gg 0
sufficiently large (precisely how large will be evident at the end of the argument). Since
G\rightarrow\mathcal{P}
is a q.i. embedding, by the distance formula (
5
), there is a constant
K_{1}
so that if
|g|\gg 0
, then

|g|\leq K_{1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}X\subset S\\
\text{ hole for $\mathcal{P}$}\end{subarray}}\big\{d_{X}(\mu,g\mu)\big\}_{\beta}

BBF factors and subsurface order.

By
[
BBF15
]
, it is possible to decompose the set of essential subsurfaces of
S
into finitely many equivalence classes (called BBF factors), one of which is
\{S\}
, so that surfaces in the same BBF factor overlap (i.e. are neither disjoint nor nested). Combining this fact with the preceding inequality, there is a constant
K_{2}\geq K_{1}
and a BBF factor
\mathbb{Y}
so that

|g|\leq K_{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y\in\mathbb{Y}\\
\text{hole}\end{subarray}}\{d_{Y}(\mu,g\mu)\}_{\beta}.

(7)

Ultimately, we will choose our constant
K
to be larger than
K_{2}
, for then the assumption
d_{S}(\mu,g\mu)<\frac{|g|}{K}
implies that
\mathbb{Y}\neq\{S\}
in (
7
). Assuming this, let
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}\in\mathbb{Y}
be the holes with
d_{Y}(\mu,g\mu)\geq\beta
. If
\beta
is large enough, it’s possible to put the
Y_{i}
in increasing order with respect to the subsurface order, which is defined using the Behrstock inequality, c.f.
[
BBKL20
, Prop. 2.6]
.

Maximal subsurfaces.

Define
0=i_{0}<i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}=n
inductively so that
i_{j}
is the largest index so that

Y_{i_{j-1}+1}\cup\cdots\cup Y_{i_{j}}

is a proper subsurface. Denote this subsurface by
Z_{j}^{\prime}
. If
\beta
is sufficiently large, then the number
k
of these maximal subsurfaces is bounded above by
d_{S}(\mu,g\mu)
by
[
BBKL20
, Lem. 4.4]
.

We use this to decompose the sum in (
7
)

\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{Y_{i}}(\mu,g\mu)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\>\sum_{\ell=i_{j-1}+1}^{i_{j}}d_{Y_{\ell}}(\mu,g\mu)

Prefixes of
g
.

Next we bound the preceding sum. Write
g=s_{1}\cdots s_{m}
in the generating set
\Omega
. Let
b>0
be an upper bound on
d_{\mathcal{M}(Z)}(\mu,s\mu)
and
d_{Z}(\mu,s\mu)
for
s\in\Omega
and any subsurface
Z\subset S
. For
1\leq j<k
define
g_{j}^{\prime}
as the largest prefix of
g=s_{1}\cdots s_{m}
so that
d_{Y_{i_{j}}}(g_{j}^{\prime}\mu,g\mu)\geq 2b
. Set also
g_{0}=\id
and
g_{k}=g
. By
[
BBKL20
, Lem. 4.3]
,
g_{j-1}^{\prime}
is a prefix of
g_{j}^{\prime}
, and

d_{Y_{\ell}}(\mu,g\mu)-5b\leq d_{Y_{\ell}}(g_{j-1}^{\prime}\mu,g_{j}^{\prime}\mu)

(8)

for
i_{j-1}<\ell\leq i_{j}
. (Note: here our notation differs from
[
BBKL20
]
; it would agree if we wrote
g_{i_{j}}^{\prime}
instead of
g_{j}^{\prime}
.) Since
d_{Y_{\ell}}(\mu,g\mu)\geq\beta
by construction, if we choose
\beta
sufficiently large, we can assume that
d_{Y_{\ell}}(\mu,g\mu)-5b\geq\frac{1}{2}d_{Y_{\ell}}(\mu,g\mu)
. Combining this with (
8
) gives

d_{Y_{\ell}}(\mu,g\mu)\leq 2\>d_{Y_{\ell}}(g_{j-1}^{\prime}\mu,g_{j}^{\prime}\mu).

Compare with
[
BBKL20
, Eqn. (5)]
. Denoting
g_{j}=(g_{j-1}^{\prime})^{-1}g_{j}^{\prime}
and
Z_{j}=(g_{j-1}^{\prime})^{-1}(Z_{j}^{\prime})
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\sum_{\ell={i_{j-1}+1}}^{i_{j}}d_{Y_{\ell}}(\mu,g\mu)&\leq&2\sum_{\ell}d_{Y_{\ell}}(g_{j-1}^{\prime}\mu,g_{j}^{\prime}\mu)\\[5.69054pt]
&=&2\sum_{\ell}d_{(g_{j-1}^{\prime})^{-1}(Y_{\ell})}(\mu,g_{j}\mu)\\[5.69054pt]
&\leq&2\sum_{Y\subset Z_{j}}\{d_{Y}(\mu,g_{j}\mu)\}_{\beta}\\[5.69054pt]
&\leq&2\kappa\>d_{\mathcal{M}(Z_{j})}(\mu,g_{j}\mu)\end{array}

The last inequality uses the distance formula for the marking complex (

). (We know that the distance formula applies because the sum on the left-hand side above is nonzero.)

Conclusion.

Now summing over

, we obtain

|g|\leq K_{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{Y_{i}}(\mu,g\mu)\leq 2\kappa K_{2}\sum_{j=1}^{k}d_{\mathcal{M}(Z_{j})}(\mu,g_{j}\mu)

which is the desired inequality. Therefore, if
K=2\kappa K_{2}
and
|g|
is sufficiently large with
d_{S}(\mu,g\mu)\leq\frac{|g|}{K}
, then we obtain
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{k}\subset S
and
g=g_{1}\cdots g_{k}
with the desired properties.∎

5
Purely pseudo-Anosov implies convex cocompact

This section contains the proof of Theorem
A
.

Fix
G<\mathbb{G}
finitely-generated, purely pseudo-Anosov. To show that
G
is convex cocompact in
\Mod(S)
, it suffices to show that the orbit map
G\rightarrow\mathcal{C}(S)
is a q.i. embedding; furthermore, by Proposition
4.3
, it suffices to show the orbit map
G\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(V)
is a q.i. embedding. We proceed in the following steps.

•

Step 1.
We show that
\mathcal{P}(V)
is quasi-isometric to a certain coned-off Cayley graph
\Cone(\Gamma_{\mathbb{G}},\mathbb{G}_{E})
.

•

Step 2.
We show that
G\rightarrow\Cone(\Gamma_{\mathbb{G}},\mathbb{G}_{E})
is a q.i. embedding, using in particular a result of
[
AM21
]
and the fact that
\mathbb{G}
is virtually free.

6
Characterization of Goeritz pseudo-Anosov elements

This section contains the proof of Theorem
B
. For this, we fix
g\in\mathbb{G}
and assume
g
is not pseudo-Anosov. If
g
has finite order, then
g
is conjugate into one of the vertex groups in (
1
) by the theory of graphs of groups
[
SW79
, Cor. 3.8]
. These vertex groups appear as (ii) and (iii) in the statement of Theorem
B
. Therefore, the most interesting case of Theorem
B
is when
g
has infinite order.

Assuming
g\in\mathbb{G}
is reducible and infinite order, to prove Theorem
B
, it suffices to show that
g
(or a power) stabilizes either a finite set of primitive disks or an infinite-diameter genus-1 subsurface (these are classified in §
3
). We prove this with a case-by-case analysis of the canonical reduction system
\CRS(g)
. As such, the proof will also immediately prove Corollary
1.2
.

Recall that a
reduction system
of a reducible mapping class
g
is a multicurve that is preserved by
g
. Reduction systems for
g
are partially ordered by inclusion, and the
canonical reduction
system

\CRS(g)
is defined as the intersection of all maximal reduction systems of
g
. See
[
FM12
, §13.2]
for more information and also
[
BLM83
, Lem. 2.6]
for basic properties of
\CRS(g)
.

In general, there are the following possibilities for a multicurve, like
\CRS(g)
, on a genus-2 surface:

(A)

a separating curve

(B)

a nonseparating curve

(C)

two curves: one separating, one nonseparating

(D)

two nonseparating curves

(E)

three curves: one separating, two nonseparating

(F)

three nonseparating curves

To analyze these possibilities we use some results of Oertel
[
Oer02
]
about the structure of homeomorphisms of handlebodies:

•

Fact 1. A multitwist of
S=\partial V
extends to the handlebody
V
if and only if there is a collection of essential disks and annuli whose boundary is the multicurve
[
Oer02
, Thm. 1.11]
(an essential annulus is incompressible and not boundary parallel).

•

Fact 2. If
X\subset S
is an essential subsurface that is preserved by a homeomorphism
g:V\rightarrow V
, then the
characteristic compression body

Q_{X}\subset V
is also preserved by
g
. See
[
Oer02
, Cor. 2.2]
for this statement and
[
Oer02
, §2]
for the definition of the characteristic compression body; the main example we will use is that if
X\cong\Sigma_{1,1}
supports a disk, then
Q_{X}
is a solid torus.

Case (A).

Suppose that
\CRS(g)=\{c\}
, where
c
is separating. We are done if
c
is a reducing curve (since then we are in case (ii) of Theorem
B
), so assume not. Let
X_{1},X_{2}\cong\Sigma_{1,1}
be the subsurfaces that
c
bounds in
S
. We can write
g^{2}=h_{1}\circ h_{2}\circ T_{c}^{n}
, where
h_{i}
is supported on
X_{i}
, and
h_{i}\bigr|_{X_{i}}\in\Mod(X_{i})
is either the identity or pseudo-Anosov. Suppose that
X_{1}
is primitively compressible in
V
. Then the characteristic compression body
Q_{X_{1}}
is a solid torus. From this we conclude that
X_{1}
supports a unique primitive disk
D
, and hence either
\{D\}
or
\{D,gD\}
is preserved by
g
(depending on whether
g(X_{1})=X_{1}
or
g(X_{1})=X_{2}
). We conclude similarly if
X_{1}
(or
X_{2}
) is primitively compressible in
\widehat{V}
. Thus it remains to consider the case
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
are primitively incompressible in both
V
and
\widehat{V}
. Since
c
is not reducing, we can assume (without loss of generality) that
c
does not bound a disk in
V
. Since
T_{c}^{n}\notin\mathbb{G}
(by Fact 1), we can assume that
h_{1}
, say, is pseudo-Anosov. Then
X_{1}
is an infinite-diameter hole for
\mathcal{P}(V)
that is preserved by
g^{2}
.

Case (B).

Suppose that
\CRS(g)=\{c\}
, where
c
is nonseparating. Let
X=S\setminus n(c)\cong\Sigma_{1,2}
. We can write
g^{2}=h\circ T_{c}^{n}
, where
h
is supported on
X
and preserves each component of
\partial X
, and
h\bigr|_{X}\in\Mod(X)
is either the identity or a pseudo-Anosov. By Fact 1,
T_{c}^{n}\notin\mathbb{G}
, so
h\neq\id
. If
c
does not bound a primitive disk, then
X
is a hole for
\mathcal{P}(V)
, and
X
has infinite diameter since
g\bigr|_{X}=h
is pseudo-Anosov. Since
X\cong\Sigma_{1,2}
, this contradicts Theorem
D
(classification of holes). Therefore,
c
bounds a primitive disk, which is fixed by
g
.

Case (C).

Suppose that
\CRS(g)=\{c_{1},c_{2}\}
, where
c_{1}
is separating and
c_{2}
is nonseparating. Let
X,Y\cong\Sigma_{1,1}
be the subsurfaces that
c_{1}
bounds, and assume
Y
contains
c_{2}
. We can write
g^{2}=h\circ T_{c_{1}}^{n_{1}}\circ T_{c_{2}}^{n_{2}}
, where
h
preserves
X
, and
h\bigr|_{X}\in\Mod(X)
is either pseudo-Anosov or the identity. We consider these cases separately.

First assume that
h
is pseudo-Anosov. Then
X
has infinite diameter with respect to
\mathcal{P}(V)
. Observe that
Y
must support a primitive disk; otherwise
X
is an infinite diameter hole for
\mathcal{P}(V)
, which implies (by Theorem
D
and §
3.6
) that
g
acts as a pseudo-Anosov on both
X
and
Y
contrary to our assumption about
\CRS(g)
. Let
D
be a primitive disk supported in
Y
. The canonical compression body
Q_{Y}
is a solid torus preserved by
g
, and
D
is the unique disk (up to isotopy) in this solid torus, so
g(D)=D
.

If
h\bigr|_{X}\in\Mod(X)
is the identity, then
g^{2}
is a multitwist. By Fact 1,
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
either bound disks in
V
or bound an essential annulus in
V
, and the same holds for
\widehat{V}
. In any case, (after properly orienting
c_{1},c_{2}
) the homology class
c_{1}-c_{2}\in H_{1}(S)
is a nonzero element in the kernel of the homomorphism
H_{1}(S)\rightarrow H_{1}(V)\oplus H_{1}(\widehat{V})
. By Lemma
2.21
this implies that
H_{2}(S^{3})\neq 0
, a contradiction.

Case (D).

Suppose that
\CRS(g)=\{c_{1},c_{2}\}
, where both
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
are nonseparating. Since
g
preserves
\CRS(g)
, we are done if at least one of
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
bounds a primitive disk in
V
or
\widehat{V}
, so we assume that neither bounds a primitive disk. Denoting
X=S\setminus n(c_{1}\cup c_{2})
, we can write
g^{4}=h\circ T_{c_{1}}^{n_{1}}\circ T_{c_{2}}^{n_{2}}
, where
h
is supported on
X
and preserves each component of
\partial X
, and
h\bigr|_{X}\in\Mod(X)
is either pseudo-Anosov or the identity. In fact,
h\bigr|_{X}
is not pseudo-Anosov because otherwise
X\cong\Sigma_{0,4}
would be an infinite-diameter hole for
\mathcal{P}(V)
, contrary to Theorem
D
. Then
g^{4}
is a multitwist, and by applying Fact 1 in the same way as in Case (C), we conclude that
H_{2}(S^{3})\neq 0
, a contradiction.

Cases (E) and (F).

Suppose that
\CRS(g)=\{c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}\}
, as in (E) or (F). Each complementary component of
\CRS(g)
in
S
is a pair of pants, which has finite mapping class group. Thus a power of
g
is a nontrivial multitwist about the curves in
\CRS(g)
. Then, as in Cases (C) and (D), we apply Fact 1 arrive at a contradiction. Here there are two cases. In the first case
c_{1}
, say, bounds disks in both
V
and
\widehat{V}
, and
c_{2}
and
c_{3}
bound annuli in each of
V
and
\widehat{V}
; this implies that
c_{1}
is separating, so both
c_{2},c_{3}
are nonseparating, and this implies that
c_{2}-c_{3}
is a nontrivial element in the kernel of
H_{1}(S)\rightarrow H_{1}(V)\oplus H_{1}(\widehat{V})
. In the second case, without loss of generality,
c_{1}
bounds a disk in
V
,
c_{3}
bounds a disk in
\widehat{V}
,
c_{2}
and
c_{3}
bound an annulus in
V
, and
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
bound an annulus in
\widehat{V}
. At most one of the
c_{i}
is separating, so
c_{1}-c_{2}+c_{3}
is a nontrivial element of the kernel of
H_{1}(S)\rightarrow H_{1}(V)\oplus H_{1}(\widehat{V})
.

This completes the proof of Theorem
B
. ∎

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}I&I\\
\tau&I\end{array}\right),

where
I
denotes the
2\times 2
identity matrix, and
\tau
is the matrix for the action of
\phi
on
H_{1}(F)
. Then
j
is an isomorphism if and only if

\pm 1=\det\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}I&I\\
\tau&I\end{array}\right)=\det(I-\tau)=2-\tr(\tau).

Consequently, either
\tr(\tau)=3
or
\tr(\tau)=1
. It is well-known that this implies
\tau
is conjugate to one of the matrices in (
9
). We briefly explain this below.

If
\tr(\tau)=3
, then
\tau
is conjugate in
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
to
\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}2&1\\
1&1\end{array}\right)
. This is because
\tau
is a hyperbolic matrix with characteristic polynomial
x^{2}-3x+1
, and conjugacy classes of these matrices correspond to narrow ideal classes in
\mathbb{Z}[\lambda]
, where
\lambda
is a root of the characteristic polynomial
[
Wal84
]
. Here
\mathbb{Z}[\lambda]=\mathbb{Z}\left[\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}\right]
is the ring of integers in
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{5})
. It is well-known that the narrow class number of this ring is
1
.

If
\tr(\tau)=1
, then since
\tau
satisfies its characteristic polynomial
x^{2}-x+1
, it follows that
\tau
has order
6
. From the action of
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
on the dual tree to the Farey graph, it follows
\tau
is conjugate to either
\left(\begin{array}[]{rr}0&1\\
-1&1\end{array}\right)
or its inverse.
