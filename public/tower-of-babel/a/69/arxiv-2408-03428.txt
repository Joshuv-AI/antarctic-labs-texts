Two-dimensional solitary water waves with constant vorticity, Part II: the deep capillary case
James Rowan, Lizhe Wan

Abstract
. We consider the two-dimensional capillary water waves with nonzero constant vorticity in infinite depth. We first derive the Babenko equation that describes the profile of the solitary wave. When the velocity c c is close to a critical velocity and a sign condition involving the physical parameters is met, the Babenko equation can be reduced to the stationary focusing cubic nonlinear Schrödinger equation plus perturbative error. We show the existence of a critical value of a dimensionless physical parameter below which at least two families of velocities satisfy the focusing condition and above which only one does. This gives the existence of small-amplitude solitary wave solutions for the water wave system with constant vorticity.

We consider two-dimensional water waves with constant vorticity on deep water without viscosity.
The fluid occupies a time-dependent domain
\Omega_{t}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}

A natural question in any water wave model is the existence and properties of solitary wave solutions.

Before stating the exact existence result, we give some heuristics by looking at the linearization equations of the water wave (

The linearized system is given by

\tau^{2}+\gamma\tau+g\xi+\sigma\xi^{3}=0,\quad\xi\leq 0.

Remark 1.3
.

We make the following remarks about the main results.

(1)

To see why the equation (
1.5
) has exactly two roots, we consider the function

f(x)=x^{4}-4\sigma\gamma x-4\sigma g.

Then
f^{{}^{\prime}}(x)=4x^{3}-4\sigma\gamma
, so that
f(x)
is decreasing on
(-\infty,\sqrt[3]{\sigma\gamma})
, and
f(x)
is increasing on
(\sqrt[3]{\sigma\gamma},\infty)
.
Also
f(0)=-4\sigma g<0
, and
f(x)
is positive when
|x|
is large.
The range of velocity
c
satisfies the sign condition

(1.8)

g+c\gamma>0,

as well as the coercivity condition

(1.9)

c^{4}<4\sigma(g+c\gamma).

These two conditions ensure that the Babenko equation (
1.10
) that describes the profile of solitary waves is a second-order quasilinear elliptic equation.

(2)

In the zero surface tension case
\sigma=0
, the Babenko equation (
1.10
) degenerates to a first-order elliptic equation.
The sign conditions for capillary and pure gravity water waves with constant vorticity are different.
In the pure gravity case, we assume

as in
[

]

This is to be expected, due to the different convexities of the dispersion relations for the equations linearized around the zero solution.
In both cases, the sign condition ensures that the solitary wave velocity is not an allowed group velocity for linear dispersive waves.

(3)

Our result also holds in the zero vorticity case
\gamma=0
, where the profile of the solitary waves can be approximated by a stationary focusing NLS equation, which is consistent with the result in Groves-Wahlén
[
20
]
.

(4)

The condition (
1.6
) is always satisfied when
c
is near whichever of
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
is larger in absolute value, so that there is always at least one family of solitary wave solutions.

Next, we discuss further the number of possible families of gravity-capillary solitary waves with nonzero constant vorticity.
The minimum number of families depends on the values of the physical parameters
g,

\sigma
, and
\gamma

Proposition 1.4
.

Define the dimensionless parameter
V=\frac{\sigma\gamma^{4}}{g^{3}}
.
There exists a critical value
V_{*}\approx 0.11034
such that if
V<V_{*}
, both possible velocities
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
satisfy condition (
1.6
), so that there are at least two families of solitary waves, while if
V>V_{*}
, only the family near the velocity with the same sign as the vorticity satisfies the condition (
1.6
).

Remark 1.5
.

The above proposition states that a solitary wave with favorable vorticity always exists, while a solitary wave with adverse vorticity only exists when the vorticity is small relative to gravity.
This result is also consistent with the zero gravity case, where
V
can be seen as infinity.

Our main result on the existence of solitary wave solutions of the two-dimensional gravity-capillary waves is based on studying an elliptic equation called the
Babenko equation
which describes the profile of solitary waves.
Starting from the formulation of the problem as the critical point of the total energy subject to the constraint of fixed momentum, we derive the Babenko equation:

(1.10)

\displaystyle(g+c\gamma-c^{2}|D|)U-\sigma\left(\dfrac{U_{\alpha}}{J^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right)_{\alpha}+\sigma|D|\left(\dfrac{1+|D|U}{J^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}U^{2}-gU|D|U-\frac{1}{2}g|D|U^{2}+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}U|D|U^{2}-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}U^{2}|D|U-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{6}|D|U^{3},

for
U=\Im W
.
Historically, Babenko studied periodic traveling waves in
[
4
,
5
]
, but the technique of building a single elliptic equation to solve works for the solitary wave case as well.

Working in holomorphic coordinates makes the nonlocal operators that arise in the Babenko equation simpler to work with.
In holomorphic coordinates, the Dirichlet-Neumann operator is reduced to the differential operator
|D|
, which drastically simplifies the required estimates in the proof.
For comparison, in
[
19
,
20
,
21
]
, a considerate amount of computations are needed to deal with the analyticity of the Dirichlet-Neumann operator and related nonlocal operators.
In the nonexistence result
[
27
]
, the Babenko equation is written in holomorphic coordinates in the complex-valued form.
Here, we consider a real-valued Babenko equation instead since it allows us to work directly with the wave profile.

Indeed, the existence of a solitary wave for (
1.1
) is equivalent to the existence of a nontrivial solution of Babenko equation (
1.10
).
This equation arises from looking for a critical point of the energy of (
1.1
) subject to a constraint on the momentum, with the velocity
c
arising as a Lagrange multiplier.
A full derivation will be given in Section
2
.

For a further comparison with the constant vorticity solitary waves result
[
21
]
in finite depth, in that paper the solitary waves are studied as solutions of a constrained minimization problem with fixed momentum instead of writing down a variant of the Babenko equation.
As a result of the purely variational approach, the main ingredient of
[
21
]
is the concentration compactness principle.
One needs to have a-priori estimates for the energy functional in order to show that only the concentration case can happen.
In this article, we instead use the ideas of NLS approximation and implicit function theorem in Groves
[
19
]
.
This is a direct approximation result that gives the approximate equation and the uniqueness for small solutions.

The rest of the article is organized as follows.
In Section
2
we use the variational approach to derive the Babenko equation (
1.10
), which is a quasilinear second-order equation that describes the profile of the solitary waves of (
1.1
).
In Section
3
, we decompose the profile of the solitary waves into a frequency-localized part
U_{1}
and a remainder part
U_{2}
.
We further show that
U_{2}
can be expressed as an implicit function of
U_{1}
.
Then in Section
4
, we derive an equation (
4.1
) for
U_{1}^{+}=\chi^{+}(D)U
and show that using a rescaling, it is equivalent to the equation (
4.8
).
In Section

, by taking the limit as

Thus, we show that (

Finally, in Section

By a combination of asymptotic analysis and interval arithmetic, we show the existence of a single critical value of the dimensionless parameter

2.
The derivation of the Babenko equation

In this section, we derive the Babenko equation of (
1.1
), which describes the profile of solitary waves.
The system (
1.1
) is expressed in holomorphic coordinates, and both
W
and
Q
are holomorphic functions.
An advantage of using holomorphic coordinates is that the Dirichlet-Neumann operator that arises in the Zakharov-Craig-Sulem formulation of water waves reduces to the simple differential operator
|D|
.
This helps us avoid delicate analysis of the Dirichlet-Neumann operator, and will greatly simplify our later computations.

For a holomorphic function, its real part equals the Hilbert transform of its imaginary part.
Therefore, the entire system can be expressed using only the imaginary parts of
(W,Q)
, and it suffices to consider just
(\Im W,\Im Q)
.
Here, instead of simply plugging the ansatz (
1.2
) into (
1.1
), we use a variational approach to derive the real-valued equation satisfied by the solitary wave profiles.

According to the computation carried out in the Appendix of
[
26
]
and Section
1
of
[
25
]
,
the total energy of (
1.1
) is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int|D|\Im Q\cdot\Im Q+g(\Im W)^{2}(1+|D|\Im W)+\gamma|D|\Im Q\cdot(\Im W)^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{3}(\Im W)^{3}(1+|D|\Im W)+2\sigma\left(J^{\frac{1}{2}}-1-|D|\Im W\right)d\alpha,

and the horizontal momentum is

\mathcal{P}=-\int|D|\Im Q\Im W+\frac{\gamma}{2}(\Im W)^{2}(1+|D|\Im W)\,d\alpha,

where the Jacobian

J=|1+W_{\alpha}|^{2}=(1+|D|\Im W)^{2}+(\Im W_{\alpha})^{2}.

Here, the differential operator
|D|
is defined by

|D|f(\alpha)=\partial_{\alpha}Hf(\alpha).

An alternative definition of operator
|D|
is given via the Fourier transform

\widehat{|D|f}(\xi)=|\xi|\hat{f}(\xi).

In
[
8
]
, it was observed by Benjamin and Olver that the solitary waves can be characterized as a critical point of the total energy subject to the constraint of a fixed momentum.
The velocity
c
can be viewed as the corresponding Lagrange multiplier.
In order to compute the functional derivative, we will need the following result.

Lemma 2.1
.

Given a function
f(x)
defined on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let
L\left(f(x),\nabla f(x),|D|f(x)\right)
be a
C^{2}_{0}
functional of
f
,
\nabla f(x)
, and
|D|f(x)
, and consider the functional defined by

\mathcal{J}(f)=\int L\left(f(x),\nabla f(x),|D|f(x)\right)dx.

Then the functional derivative of
\mathcal{J}(f)
is given by

\frac{\delta\mathcal{J}}{\delta f}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial f}-\operatorname{div}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial\nabla f}\right)+|D|\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial|D|f}\right).

Proof.

For any smooth test function
\phi(x)
and small constant
\epsilon
, we have

\displaystyle\delta\mathcal{J}(f;\epsilon\phi)=\mathcal{J}(f+\epsilon\phi)-\mathcal{J}(f)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int L\left(f(x)+\epsilon\phi(x),\nabla f(x)+\epsilon\nabla\phi(x),|D|f(x)+\epsilon|D|\phi(x)\right)-L\left(f(x),\nabla f(x),|D|f(x)\right)\,dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\frac{\partial L}{\partial f}\epsilon\phi+\frac{\partial L}{\partial\nabla f}\epsilon\nabla\phi+\frac{\partial f}{\partial|D|f}\epsilon|D|\phi\,dx+O(\epsilon^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon\int\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial f}-\operatorname{div}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial\nabla f}\right)+|D|\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial|D|f}\right)\right)\phi\,dx+O(\epsilon^{2}),

using integration by parts and the self-adjoint property of the operator
|D|
.
The result follows from the definition

\int\frac{\delta\mathcal{J}}{\delta f}\phi(x)\,dx=\frac{\delta\mathcal{J}(f;\epsilon\phi)}{\epsilon}\Bigg|_{\epsilon=0}.

∎

Taking the functional derivatives of the energy
\mathcal{E}
and momentum
\mathcal{P}
with respect to
\Im Q,\Im W
, and using the results in
[
8
]
, we get two equations.
The first equation is on the functional derivatives with respect to
\Im Q
,

\frac{\delta\mathcal{E}}{\delta\Im Q}=c\frac{\delta\mathcal{P}}{\delta\Im Q},

which can be simplified to

(2.1)

\Im Q=-\frac{\gamma}{2}(\Im W)^{2}-c\Im W.

The second equation is on the functional derivative with respect to
\Im W
,

\frac{\delta\mathcal{E}}{\delta\Im W}=c\frac{\delta\mathcal{P}}{\delta\Im W}.

By computation,

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\mathcal{E}}{\delta\Im W}=

\displaystyle g\Im W(1+|D|\Im W)+\frac{1}{2}g|D|(\Im W)^{2}+\gamma|D|\Im Q\Im W+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}(\Im W)^{2}(1+|D|\Im W)

\displaystyle+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{6}|D|(\Im W)^{3}-\sigma\left(\dfrac{\Im W_{\alpha}}{J^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right)_{\alpha}+\sigma|D|\left(\dfrac{1+|D|\Im W}{J^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right),

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\mathcal{P}}{\delta\Im W}=

\displaystyle-|D|\Im Q-\gamma\Im W(1+|D|\Im W)-\frac{\gamma}{2}|D|(\Im W)^{2},

which leads to the second equation

\displaystyle g\Im W(1+|D|\Im W)+\frac{1}{2}g|D|(\Im W)^{2}+\gamma|D|\Im Q\Im W+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}(\Im W)^{2}(1+|D|\Im W)+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{6}|D|(\Im W)^{3}

\displaystyle+c|D|\Im Q+c\gamma\Im W(1+|D|\Im W)+c\frac{\gamma}{2}|D|(\Im W)^{2}=\sigma\left(\dfrac{\Im W_{\alpha}}{J^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right)_{\alpha}-\sigma|D|\left(\dfrac{1+|D|\Im W}{J^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right).

Therefore by eliminating
\Im Q
in the second equation using (
2.1
), we obtain that
U:=\Im W
solves the Babenko equation (
1.10
):

\displaystyle(g+c\gamma-c^{2}|D|)U-\sigma\left(\dfrac{U_{\alpha}}{J^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right)_{\alpha}+\sigma|D|\left(\dfrac{1+|D|U}{J^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}U^{2}-gU|D|U-\frac{1}{2}g|D|U^{2}+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}U|D|U^{2}-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}U^{2}|D|U-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{6}|D|U^{3}.

Note that in this derivation,
c
is the Lagrange multiplier, while
\sigma,g
and
\gamma
are three fixed physical parameters.

As a consequence of (
2.1
) and the properties of holomorphic functions, we immediately obtain the following result that gives the solitary wave solutions
(W,Q)
once we solve a nonzero
U
in (
1.10
).

Corollary 2.2
.

If
u\neq 0
solves (
1.10
), then (
1.1
) has a solitary wave solution

\left(Hu(\alpha+ct)+iu(\alpha+ct),cHu(\alpha+ct)-\frac{\gamma}{2}H(u^{2})(\alpha+ct)+icu(\alpha+ct)-\frac{i\gamma}{2}u^{2}(\alpha+ct)\right).

3.
Functional setting and reduction

The Babenko equation (
1.10
) is a quasilinear elliptic equation under conditions (
1.8
) and (
1.9
).
The constrained minimization method in general cannot solve quasilinear equations like this.
Nevertheless, as pointed out in Section
1
, when
c
is slightly larger than
c_{1}
or slightly smaller than
c_{2}
, we expect a possibility of solitary waves with small amplitude bifurcating from the linear waves with frequency either
\omega_{1}
or
\omega_{2}
.
For these cases, we prove in this section that it suffices to consider only the part of
U
whose frequencies are localized near
\omega_{1}
or
\omega_{2}
.

For clarity and simplicity, we will perform the analysis in subspaces of
H^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, which will ultimately lead to the existence of
H^{2}
solitary wave solutions.
At the end of this section, we briefly outline the idea to generalize the analysis to
H^{s}(\mathbb{R})
,
s\geq 2
.

3.1.
Functional setting

We first define the function spaces we will be using later.
These function spaces are the same as in
[
19
]
.

Let
\chi(\xi)
be the characteristic function of the set
(-|\omega|-\delta,-|\omega|+\delta)\cup(|\omega|-\delta,|\omega|+\delta)
for some small positive constant
\delta
, where
\omega
is either
\omega_{1}
or
\omega_{2}
depending on whether the velocity
c
is close to
c_{1}
or
c_{2}
.
One can decompose

(3.1)

U=U_{1}+U_{2}:=\chi(D)U+(1-\chi(D))U.

For our analysis below, we use the function space
\mathcal{X}=H^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.
It can be decomposed into the direct sum

\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{X}_{1}+\mathcal{X}_{2}:=\chi(D)\mathcal{X}+(1-\chi(D))\mathcal{X}.

For the space
\mathcal{X}_{1}
, we define the associated scaled norm

\|u\|_{\mathfrak{E}_{\omega}}:=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1+\epsilon^{-2}(|\xi|-|\omega|)^{2})|\hat{u}(\xi)|^{2})d\xi\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Using the fact that
U_{1}\in\mathcal{X}_{1}
is frequency-localized, we have the following estimates for the rescaled norm:

Lemma 3.1

(
[
19
]
)
.

For each
u\in\mathcal{X}_{1}
,

(3.2)

\displaystyle\|\hat{u}\|_{L^{1}}\lesssim\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\|u\|_{\mathfrak{E}_{\omega}},\quad\|u\|_{W^{n,\infty}}\lesssim\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\|u\|_{\mathfrak{E}_{\omega}},

(3.3)

\displaystyle\|u\|_{H^{s_{1}}}\approx\|u\|_{H^{s_{2}}}\lesssim\|u\|_{\mathfrak{C}_{k}},\quad s_{1},s_{2}\geq 0.

Due to the estimate (
3.2
), we will work with the function space
\mathcal{Z}
defined by

\mathcal{Z}=\{U\in\mathcal{S}^{{}^{\prime}}:\|U\|_{\mathcal{Z}}:=\|\hat{U}_{1}\|_{L^{1}}+\|U_{2}\|_{H^{2}}<+\infty\}.

Then we have the inequality

\|U\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim\|U\|_{\mathcal{Z}}\lesssim\|U_{1}\|_{\mathfrak{E}_{\omega}}+\|U_{2}\|_{H^{2}}.

Let
M<1
be a small positive number.
In the following, we will abuse notation and write
B_{M}
for a ball of radius
M
in
\mathcal{Z}
.
Using the Sobolev embedding and (
3.2
), for any
U\in B_{M}
,
\|U\|_{W^{1,\infty}}
is also small.

To estimate the capillary terms, we have the following approximation result:

Lemma 3.2
.

For all
U\in B_{M}
, the capillary terms have the following expansion:

(3.4)

-\sigma\left(\dfrac{U_{\alpha}}{J^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right)_{\alpha}+\sigma|D|\left(\dfrac{1+|D|U}{J^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right)=-\sigma U_{\alpha\alpha}-\frac{\sigma}{2}|D|(|D|U)^{2}+\frac{\sigma}{2}\partial_{\alpha}(U_{\alpha})^{3}+\tilde{R}(U),

where the remainder
\tilde{R}(U)
consists of quartic and higher order terms in
U
containing more than four derivatives and satisfies the estimates

(3.5)

\|\tilde{R}(U)\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim\|U\|_{\mathcal{Z}}^{3}\|U\|_{H^{2}},\quad\|d\tilde{R}[U](V)\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim(\|U\|_{\mathcal{Z}}+\|U\|_{W^{1,\infty}})^{3}\|V\|_{H^{2}}.

Proof.

Observe that the two capillary terms on the left of (
3.4
) are the critical point of the capillary functional

I_{cap}(U)=\int\sigma\left(J^{\frac{1}{2}}-1-|D|U\right)d\alpha.

Recall the Taylor expansion

(1+x)^{\frac{1}{2}}=1+\frac{1}{2}x-\frac{1}{8}x^{2}+\frac{1}{16}x^{3}-\frac{5}{128}x^{4}+r(x),\quad|x|<1,

where
r(x)
contains all quintic and higher order terms.
Since
\|U\|_{W^{1,\infty}}
is small, one can apply the Taylor expansion up to the quartic term,

J^{\frac{1}{2}}-1-|D|U=\frac{1}{2}U_{\alpha}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}|D|U(U_{\alpha})^{2}-\frac{1}{8}U_{\alpha}^{4}+r_{*}(U),

where
r_{*}(U)
are quintic and higher order terms of
U
with more than four derivatives.

Using the identity of the Hilbert transform

u^{2}=(Hu)^{2}-2H(uHu),\quad u\in L^{2},

one can rewrite the cubic integral

-\frac{1}{2}\int|D|U(U_{\alpha})^{2}\,d\alpha=\int-\frac{1}{2}(|D|U)^{3}+|D|U(U_{\alpha})^{2}\,d\alpha=-\frac{1}{6}\int(|D|U)^{3}\,d\alpha.

The functional derivative of
\int-\frac{\sigma}{6}(|D|U)^{3}-\frac{\sigma}{8}U_{\alpha}^{4}\,d\alpha

is just
-\frac{\sigma}{2}|D|(|D|U)^{2}+\frac{\sigma}{2}\partial_{\alpha}(U_{\alpha})^{3}
,
and the functional derivative of
\sigma\int r_{*}(U)\,d\alpha
gives the remainder term
\tilde{R}(u)
.
Corresponding bounds follow from direct computation.
∎

3.2.
Reduction

One can write the Babenko equation (
1.10
) as

\displaystyle\left(g+c_{*}\gamma-c_{*}^{2}|D|+\sigma|D|^{2}\right)U=(c_{*}-c)\gamma U+(c^{2}-c^{2}_{*})|D|U+\sigma\left(\dfrac{U_{\alpha}}{J^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right)_{\alpha}-\sigma|D|\left(\dfrac{1+|D|U}{J^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle+\sigma|D|^{2}U-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}U^{2}-gU|D|U-\frac{1}{2}g|D|U^{2}+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}U|D|U^{2}-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}U^{2}|D|U-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{6}|D|U^{3},

where
c_{*}
is either
c_{1}
or
c_{2}
so that
|c-c_{*}|\lesssim\epsilon^{2}
.
The operator

L(D):=g+c_{*}\gamma-c_{*}^{2}|D|+\sigma|D|^{2}

is a second order elliptic operator whose symbol

\ell(\xi):=g+c_{*}\gamma-c_{*}^{2}|\xi|+\sigma|\xi|^{2}\geq 0

is nonnegative and equals zero only at
\xi=\pm\omega
.
Recall the frequency decomposition
U=U_{1}+U_{2}=\chi(D)U+(1-\chi(D))U
.
Applying
(1-\chi(D))
to the Babenko equation and using (
3.1
), we get
U_{2}=F(U_{1})+U_{3}
, where
F(U_{1})
is

(3.6)

F(U_{1})=-(1-\chi(D))L(D)^{-1}\left[\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}U_{1}^{2}+gU_{1}|D|U_{1}+\frac{1}{2}g|D|U_{1}^{2}-\frac{\sigma}{2}|D|(|D|U_{1})^{2}\right],

and
U_{3}
is given by

(3.7)

\displaystyle U_{3}=

\displaystyle\frac{1-\chi(D)}{L(D)}\Big[-\frac{\sigma}{2}\partial_{\alpha}(U_{\alpha})^{3}+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}U|D|U^{2}-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}U^{2}|D|U

\displaystyle-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{6}|D|U^{3}-\tilde{R}(U)\Big]+F(U)-F(U_{1}).

Here,
\tilde{R}(U)
is the last term of (
3.4
).

Note that

f\mapsto\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left[\dfrac{1-\chi(\xi)}{\ell(\xi)}\hat{f}(\xi)\right]

is a bounded linear operator from
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
to
\mathcal{X}_{2}
.

In the following, we show that
U_{3}
can be expressed implicitly as a function of
U_{1}
, so that
U
is an implicit function of
U_{1}
.
The key lemma of the proof is the following implicit function theorem in Banach spaces.

Lemma 3.3

(Implicit function theorem
[
19
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{E}_{1}
,
\mathcal{E}_{2}
be two Banach spaces,
X_{1},X_{2}
be closed convex sets in, respectively,
\mathcal{E}_{1}
,
\mathcal{E}_{2}
containing the origin and
\mathcal{G}:X_{1}\times X_{2}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}_{2}
be a smooth function.
Suppose that there exists a continuous function
r:X_{1}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
, such that

\|\mathcal{G}(x_{1},0)\|\leq\frac{r}{2},\quad\|d_{2}\mathcal{G}[x_{1},x_{2}]\|\leq\frac{1}{3}

for each
x_{2}\in\bar{B}_{r}(0)\subset X_{2}
and each
x_{1}\in X_{1}
.

Under these hypotheses there exists for each
x_{1}\in X_{1}
a unique solution
x_{2}=x_{2}(x_{1})
of the fixed-point equation
x_{2}=\mathcal{G}(x_{1},x_{2})
satisfying
x_{2}(x_{1})\in\bar{B}_{r}(0)
.
Moreover
x_{2}(x_{1})
is a smooth function of
x_{1}\in X_{1}
, and in particular satisfies the estimate

\|dx_{2}[x_{1}]\|\leq 2\|d_{1}\mathcal{G}[x_{1},x_{2}(x_{1})]\|.

To adapt the above implicit function theorem to the equation (
3.7
), we choose the function spaces
\mathcal{E}_{1}=\mathcal{X}_{1}=\chi(D)H^{2}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}=\mathcal{X}_{2}=(1-\chi(D))H^{2}
and the sets

X_{1}=\{U_{1}\in\mathcal{X}_{1}:\|U_{1}\|_{\mathfrak{E}_{\omega}}\leq R_{1}\},\quad X_{2}=\{U_{3}\in\mathcal{X}_{2}:\|U_{3}\|_{H^{2}}\leq R_{2}\}.

For the given small ball
B_{M}
in
\mathcal{Z}
, by (
3.2
), we pick
R_{1}<\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{-\frac{1}{2}}M<\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\ll 1
such that
\|\hat{U}_{1}\|_{L^{1}}<\frac{M}{2}
.
The value of
R_{2}
is chosen such that
\|F(U_{1})+U_{3}\|_{H^{2}}<\frac{M}{2}
for all
U_{1}\in X_{1}
and
U_{3}\in X_{2}
.
As a consequence,
U=U_{1}+U_{2}\in B_{M}
.

Let
x_{1}=U_{1}
,
x_{2}=U_{3}
, and
\mathcal{G}(U_{1},U_{3})
be the right-hand side of (
3.7
).
We further write
\mathcal{G}(U_{1},U_{3})=\mathcal{G}_{1}(U_{1},U_{3})+\mathcal{G}_{2}(U_{1},U_{3})+\mathcal{G}_{3}(U_{1},U_{3})
, where
U=U_{1}+U_{2}
and

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{1}(U_{1},U_{3})=

\displaystyle-\frac{1-\chi(D)}{L(D)}\tilde{R}(U),

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{2}(U_{1},U_{3})=

\displaystyle\frac{1-\chi(D)}{L(D)}\Big[-\frac{\sigma}{2}\partial_{\alpha}(U_{\alpha})^{3}+\sigma\left((|D|U)^{2}U_{\alpha}\right)_{\alpha}-\sigma|D|\left(|D|UU_{\alpha}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{\sigma}{2}|D|(|D|U)^{3}+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}U|D|U^{2}-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}U^{2}|D|U-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{6}|D|U^{3}\Big],

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{3}(U_{1},U_{3})=

\displaystyle F(U)-F(U_{1}).

In the following, we estimate
\mathcal{G}_{i}(U_{1},U_{3})
,
d_{1}\mathcal{G}_{i}(U_{1},U_{3})
and
d_{2}\mathcal{G}_{i}(U_{1},U_{3})
successively for
i=1,2,3
in order to apply Lemma
3.3
.
Before that, we first give an estimate for
F(U_{1})
.

Lemma 3.4
.

For each
U_{1}\in X_{1}
,

(3.8)

\|F(U_{1})\|_{H^{2}}\lesssim\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\|U_{1}\|_{\mathfrak{E}_{\omega}}^{2},\quad\|dF[U_{1}]\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X}_{1},\mathcal{X}_{2})}\lesssim\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\|U_{1}\|_{\mathfrak{E}_{\omega}}.

As a consequence of
R_{1}\ll 1
,
\|U_{2}\|_{H^{2}}\lesssim\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\|U_{1}\|_{\mathfrak{E}_{\omega}}+\|U_{3}\|_{H^{2}}
.

Proof.

It follows that for the
H^{2}
norm of
F(U_{1})
,

\|F(U_{1})\|_{H^{2}}\lesssim\|U_{1}^{2}+U_{1}|D|U_{1}\|_{L^{2}}+\|U_{1}^{2}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}+\|(|D|U_{1})^{2}\|_{H^{1}}\lesssim\|U\|_{W^{2,\infty}}\|U\|_{H^{1}}\lesssim\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\|U_{1}\|_{\mathfrak{E}_{\omega}}^{2}.

Taking the derivative,

dF[U_{1}](V)=-\frac{1-\chi(D)}{L(D)}\left[\gamma^{2}U_{1}V+gV|D|U_{1}+gU_{1}|D|V+g|D|(U_{1}V)-\sigma|D|(|D|U_{1}|D|V)\right].

Hence, for any
V\in\mathcal{X}_{1}
,

\|dF[U_{1}]V\|_{H^{2}}\lesssim\|U_{1}\|_{W^{2,\infty}}\|V\|_{H^{2}}\lesssim\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\|U_{1}\|_{\mathfrak{E}_{\omega}}\|V\|_{H^{2}},

which gives the second estimate of (
3.8
).
∎

Lemma 3.5
.

For each
U_{1}\in X_{1}
and
U_{3}\in X_{2}
,

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{G}_{1}(U_{1},U_{3})\|_{H^{2}}\lesssim(\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\|U_{1}\|_{\mathfrak{E}_{\omega}}+\|U_{3}\|_{H^{2}})^{3}(\|U_{1}\|_{\mathfrak{E}_{\omega}}+\|U_{3}\|_{H^{2}}),

\displaystyle\|d_{1}\mathcal{G}_{1}(U_{1},U_{3})\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X}_{1},\mathcal{X}_{2})}\lesssim(\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\|U_{1}\|_{\mathfrak{E}_{\omega}}+\|U_{3}\|_{H^{2}})^{3}(1+\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\|U_{1}\|_{\mathfrak{E}_{\omega}}),

\displaystyle\|d_{2}\mathcal{G}_{1}(U_{1},U_{3})\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X}_{2},\mathcal{X}_{2})}\lesssim(\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\|U_{1}\|_{\mathfrak{E}_{\omega}}+\|U_{3}\|_{H^{2}})^{3}.

Proof.

We use (
3.5
) to get

\|(1-\chi(D))L^{-1}(D)\tilde{R}(U)\|_{H^{2}}\lesssim\|U\|_{\mathcal{Z}}^{3}\|U\|_{H^{2}}\lesssim(\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\|U_{1}\|_{\mathfrak{E}_{\omega}}+\|U_{3}\|_{H^{2}})^{3}(\|U_{1}\|_{\mathfrak{E}_{\omega}}+\|U_{3}\|_{H^{2}}).

The estimates for the derivative follow from the derivative estimate (
3.5
).
∎

Lemma 3.6
.

For each
U_{1}\in X_{1}
and
U_{3}\in X_{2}
,

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{G}_{2}(U_{1},U_{3})\|_{H^{2}}\lesssim(\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\|U_{1}\|_{\mathfrak{E}_{\omega}}+\|U_{3}\|_{H^{2}})^{2}(\|U_{1}\|_{\mathfrak{E}_{\omega}}+\|U_{3}\|_{H^{2}}),

\displaystyle\|d_{2}\mathcal{G}_{2}(U_{1},U_{3})\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X}_{2},\mathcal{X}_{2})}\lesssim(\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\|U_{1}\|_{\mathfrak{E}_{\omega}}+\|U_{3}\|_{H^{2}})^{2},

\displaystyle\|d_{1}\mathcal{G}_{2}(U_{1},U_{3})\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X}_{1},\mathcal{X}_{2})}\lesssim(\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\|U_{1}\|_{\mathfrak{E}_{\omega}}+\|U_{3}\|_{H^{2}})(\|U_{1}\|_{\mathfrak{E}_{\omega}}+\|U_{3}\|_{H^{2}}).

Proof.

Using the fact that
(1-\chi(D))L^{-1}(D)
is bounded from
L^{2}
to
H^{2}
and (
3.8
), we compute,

\displaystyle\|(1-\chi(D))L^{-1}(D)\partial_{\alpha}(U_{\alpha})^{3}\|_{H^{2}}\lesssim(\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\|U_{1}\|_{\mathfrak{E}_{\omega}}+\|U_{3}\|_{H^{2}})^{2}(\|U_{1}\|_{\mathfrak{E}_{\omega}}+\|U_{3}\|_{H^{2}}),

\displaystyle\|(1-\chi(D))L^{-1}(D)(U|D|U^{2}-U^{2}|D|U)\|_{H^{2}}\lesssim\|U|D|U^{2}\|_{L^{2}}+\|U^{2}|D|U\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\|U\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\|U_{\alpha}\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim(\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\|U_{1}\|_{\mathfrak{E}_{\omega}}+\|U_{3}\|_{H^{2}})^{2}(\|U_{1}\|_{\mathfrak{E}_{\omega}}+\|U_{3}\|_{H^{2}}),

\displaystyle\|(1-\chi(D))L^{-1}(D)(|D|U^{3})\|_{H^{2}}\lesssim\||D|U^{3}\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim\|U^{2}U_{\alpha}\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\|U\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\|U_{\alpha}\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim(\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\|U_{1}\|_{\mathfrak{E}_{\omega}}+\|U_{3}\|_{H^{2}})^{2}(\|U_{1}\|_{\mathfrak{E}_{\omega}}+\|U_{3}\|_{H^{2}}).

Combining these estimates, we obtain the first bound.

As for the expression for the operator
d_{2}\mathcal{G}_{2}(U_{1},U_{3})
, we apply the chain rule.

\displaystyle d_{2}\mathcal{G}_{2}(U_{1},U_{3})V=

\displaystyle\frac{1-\chi(D)}{L(D)}\Big[-\frac{3\sigma}{2}\partial_{\alpha}(U^{2}_{\alpha}V_{\alpha})-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}V|D|U^{2}

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\gamma^{2}U|D|(UV)+\gamma^{2}UV|D|U+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}U^{2}|D|V+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}|D|(U^{2}V)\Big],

where
U=U_{1}+F(U_{1})+U_{3}
, for each
V\in\mathcal{X}_{1}
.
Hence,

\displaystyle\|\partial_{\alpha}(U^{2}_{\alpha}V_{\alpha})\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim(\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\|U_{1}\|_{\mathfrak{E}_{\omega}}+\|U_{2}\|_{H^{2}})^{2}\|V\|_{H^{2}},

\displaystyle\|(V|D|U^{2}+2U|D|(UV)-2UV|D|U+U^{2}|D|V+|D|(U^{2}V))\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle(\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\|U_{1}\|_{\mathfrak{E}_{\omega}}+\|U_{2}\|_{H^{2}})^{2}\|V\|_{H^{2}}.

The estimate for
d_{1}\mathcal{G}_{2}(U_{1},U_{3})
is similar as that of
d_{2}\mathcal{G}_{2}(U_{1},U_{3})
.
∎

Lemma 3.7
.

For each
U_{1}\in X_{1}
and
U_{2}\in X_{2}
,
\mathcal{G}_{3}(U_{1},U_{3})=F(U)-F(U_{1})
satisfies the estimate

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{G}_{3}(U_{1},U_{3})\|_{H^{2}}\lesssim\epsilon\|U_{1}\|^{3}_{\mathfrak{E}_{\omega}}+\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\|U_{1}\|^{2}_{\mathfrak{E}_{\omega}}\|U_{3}\|_{H^{2}}+\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\|U_{1}\|_{\mathfrak{E}_{\omega}}\|U_{3}\|_{H^{2}}+\|U_{3}\|^{2}_{H^{2}},

\displaystyle\|d_{2}\mathcal{G}_{3}(U_{1},U_{3})\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathcal{X}_{2},\mathcal{X}_{2}))}\lesssim\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\|U_{1}\|_{\mathfrak{E}_{\omega}}+\|U_{3}\|_{H^{2}},

\displaystyle\|d_{1}\mathcal{G}_{3}(U_{1},U_{3})\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathcal{X}_{1},\mathcal{X}_{2}))}\lesssim\epsilon\|U_{1}\|^{2}_{\mathfrak{E}_{\omega}}+\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\|U_{1}\|_{\mathfrak{E}_{\omega}}\|U_{3}\|_{H^{2}}+\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\|U_{3}\|_{H^{2}}.

Proof.

Each term of

\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}U^{2}+gU|D|U+\frac{1}{2}g|D|U^{2}-\frac{\sigma}{2}|D|(|D|U)^{2}

in the expression of
F(U)
is a quadratic term of
U
with at most three derivatives.
Hence, for each term in
\mathcal{G}_{2}(U_{1},U_{3})
, it can be expressed by

(1-\chi(D))L(D)^{-1}[2m(U_{1},F(U_{1})+U_{3})+m(F(U_{1})+U_{3},F(U_{1})+U_{3})],

where
m(u,v)
are bilinear terms with at most three derivatives that satisfy

\|m(u,v)\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim(\|u\|_{\mathcal{Z}}+\|u\|_{W^{1,\infty}})\|v\|_{H^{2}},\quad\forall u,v\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R}).

Then the estimates follows directly from (
3.2
) and (
3.8
).
∎

Putting the results in Lemma
3.5
, Lemma
3.6
and Lemma
3.7
together, for each
U_{1}\in X_{1}
, we choose
M
and
\epsilon
small enough.
Let
r(U_{1})=C\epsilon\|U_{1}\|^{2}_{\mathfrak{E}_{k}}
for some large constant
C
, then for
U_{3}\in\overline{B}_{r(U_{1})}(0)\subseteq B_{\frac{M}{2}}(0)\subseteq X_{2}
,

\|\mathcal{G}(U_{1},0)\|_{H^{2}}\lesssim\epsilon\|U_{1}\|_{\mathfrak{E}_{\omega}}^{3}\lesssim\frac{1}{2}r(U_{1}).

Moreover,

\displaystyle\|d_{2}\mathcal{G}(U_{1},U_{3})\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X}_{2},\mathcal{X}_{2})}\lesssim(\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\|U_{1}\|_{\mathfrak{E}_{\omega}}+\|U_{3}\|_{H^{2}})(1+\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\|U_{1}\|_{\mathfrak{E}_{\omega}}+\|U_{3}\|_{H^{2}})\ll\frac{1}{3},

\displaystyle\|d_{1}\mathcal{G}(U_{1},U_{3})\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X}_{1},\mathcal{X}_{2})}\lesssim(\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\|U_{1}\|_{\mathfrak{E}_{\omega}}+\|U_{3}\|_{H^{2}})(\|U_{1}\|_{\mathfrak{E}_{\omega}}+\|U_{3}\|_{H^{2}}).

It then follows from Lemma
3.3
and the assumption
\|U_{1}\|_{\mathfrak{E}_{\omega}}\leq\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}
that
U_{3}
can be expressed as implicitly as a function of
U_{1}
:

Proposition 3.8
.

The equation (
3.7
) has a unique solution
U_{3}\in X_{2}
which depends smoothly upon
U_{1}\in X_{1}
.
In addition,

\|U_{3}(U_{1})\|_{H^{2}}\lesssim\epsilon\|U_{1}\|^{2}_{\mathfrak{E}_{k}},\quad\|dU_{3}(U_{1})\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X}_{1},\mathcal{X}_{2})}\leq\epsilon\|U_{1}\|_{\mathfrak{E}_{k}}.

This means that
U_{2}
, and thus
U
itself, is an implicit function of
U_{1}
.

To further show that
U_{2}
is in any
H^{s}(\mathbb{R})
, we just need to repeat the above analysis and work in the function space
\mathcal{X}^{s}=H^{s}(\mathbb{R})

\rho\in B_{R}(0)\subseteq\chi_{0}(\epsilon D)H^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, where the value

.
Let

,

solves the equation

(4.8)

\epsilon^{-2}\tilde{L}(|\omega|+\epsilon D)\rho+a_{1}\rho-a_{2}\chi_{0}(\epsilon D)(|\rho|^{2}\rho)+\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\underline{O}_{0}^{\epsilon}(\|\rho\|_{H^{1}})=0,

where the symbol

of

satisfies the identities

Positive coefficients

and

5.
Solving the reduced equation

In this section, we use the method in Section

of
[

to solve the reduced equation (

Writing (

we apply the following implicit function theorem for the limit as

Proposition 5.1

(Implicit function theorem II
[
19
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a Banach space,
X_{0}
and
\Lambda_{0}
be open neighbourhoods of respectively

in
\mathcal{X}
and the origin in
\mathbb{R}
, and
\mathcal{F}:X_{0}\times\Lambda_{0}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}
be a function which is differentiable with respect to

for each
\lambda\in\Lambda_{0}
.
Furthermore, suppose that
\mathcal{F}(x^{*},0)=0
,
d_{1}\mathcal{F}[x^{*},0]:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}
is an isomorphism,

\lim_{x\rightarrow x^{*}}\|d_{1}\mathcal{F}[x,0]-d_{1}\mathcal{F}[x^{*},0]\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X})}=0,

and

\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0}\|\mathcal{F}(x,\lambda)-\mathcal{F}(x,0)\|_{\mathcal{X}}=0,\quad\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0}\|d_{1}\mathcal{F}[x,\lambda]-d_{1}\mathcal{F}[x,0]\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X})}=0

uniform over
x\in X_{0}
.

There exist open neighbourhoods
x^{*}\in\mathcal{X}
and
\Lambda
of
0
in
\mathbb{R}
(with

,
\Lambda\subseteq\Lambda_{0}
) and a uniquely determined mapping

(1)

(2)

\mathcal{F}(h(\lambda),\lambda)=0
for

(3)

satisfies

To apply the above implicit function theorem to the fixed-point equation (

Lemma 5.2

Let

\left|\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{\tilde{\ell}(|\omega|+\epsilon\xi)+\epsilon^{2}a_{1}}-\frac{1}{a_{1}+\sigma\xi^{2}}\right|\lesssim\frac{\epsilon}{(1+\xi^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}}.

Proof.

Using direct computation,

\left|\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{\tilde{\ell}(|\omega|+\epsilon\xi)+\epsilon^{2}a_{1}}-\frac{1}{a_{1}+\sigma\xi^{2}}\right|=\left|\frac{\epsilon^{2}\xi^{2}\sigma-\tilde{\ell}(|\omega|+\epsilon\xi)}{(\tilde{\ell}(|\omega|+\epsilon\xi)+\epsilon^{2}a_{1})(a_{1}+\sigma\xi^{2})}\right|.

By (

|\epsilon^{2}\xi^{2}\sigma-\tilde{\ell}(|\omega|+\epsilon\xi)|\lesssim\epsilon^{3}|\xi|^{3},\quad|s|\lesssim\delta.

On the other hand, for any

Hence,

\left|\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{\tilde{\ell}(|\omega|+\epsilon\xi)+\epsilon^{2}a_{1}}-\frac{1}{a_{1}+\sigma\xi^{2}}\right|\lesssim\frac{\epsilon|\xi|^{3}}{(1+\xi^{2})^{2}}\lesssim\frac{\epsilon}{(1+\xi^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}},\quad|\xi|<\frac{\delta}{\epsilon}.

∎

6.
The range of parameters with two solitary waves

In this section, we compute a lower bound for the number of families of gravity-capillary solitary water wave solutions with nonzero vorticity and prove Proposition
1.4
.
In this section, the parameters
g
and
\sigma
are positive, and the vorticity
\gamma
is nonzero.

We will do this by analyzing when the coefficient on the cubic term in (
4.7
) is positive.
We will find an explicit formula for the value of the critical frequency
\omega
that is closer to the origin in terms of the physical parameters of the problem.
Then a combination of asymptotic arguments and computer-assisted root-finding algorithm will be used to describe when the stationary cubic NLS equation is focusing.

To simplify our computations, we rescale the space and time coordinates,

\tilde{\alpha}=\frac{\gamma^{2}\alpha}{g},\quad\tilde{t}=\gamma t

and choose the rescaled unknowns and velocity

\tilde{W}=\frac{\gamma^{2}W}{g},\quad\tilde{Q}=\frac{\gamma^{3}Q}{g^{2}},\quad\tilde{c}=\frac{\gamma c}{g}.

Using the rescaled variables and unknowns and dividing through by the common factor
\frac{g^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}
,
\tilde{U}=\Im\tilde{W}(\tilde{\alpha})
solves the nondimensionalized Babenko equation

(6.1)

\displaystyle(1+\tilde{c}-\tilde{c}^{2}|D|)\tilde{U}-V\left(\frac{\tilde{U}_{\alpha}}{J^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right)_{\alpha}+V|D|\left(\frac{1+|D|\tilde{U}}{J^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\tilde{U}^{2}-\tilde{U}|D|\tilde{U}-\frac{1}{2}|D|\tilde{U}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\tilde{U}|D|\tilde{U}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\tilde{U}^{2}|D|\tilde{U}-\frac{1}{6}|D|\tilde{U}^{3},

where
V
denotes the dimensionless parameter

V=\frac{\sigma\gamma^{4}}{g^{3}},

which measures the relative strength of the vorticity.
Compared to the original Babenko equation (
1.10
), the coefficients of (

.
It is then equivalent to consider the number of families of solutions for (

To address the question of the sign of the coefficient on the cubic term in (

The condition

\tilde{\omega}

\tilde{c}^{\pm}(k)
have critical points.
In other words,
\tilde{\omega}

A direct computation shows these (possible)

Solving the resolvent cubic by radicals and making the intermediate definitions

\displaystyle z_{0}

\displaystyle=32G^{3}+27G^{2}+3\sqrt{3}\sqrt{27G^{4}+64G^{5}},\quad z_{1}=2^{\frac{5}{3}}z_{0}^{-\frac{1}{3}}G,

Lemma 6.3

Description of proof.

By the intermediate value theorem and the two preceding lemmas,

Since we have reduced to searching in a finite interval, we can use interval arithmetic to find all the roots of

The interval arithmetic form of Newton’s method produces a nested sequence of closed intervals in which the root(s) of

At each step, the full range of possible endpoints of Newton steps starting at the midpoint of the interval is constructed.
By taking the intersection of this set with the interval, the procedure avoids divergence.
When

Our numerical computation was done using the
