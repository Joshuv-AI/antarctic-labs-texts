Sparse Bayesian Inference with Regularized Gaussian Distributions Thanks:
Jasper M. Everink, Yiqiu Dong, Martin S. Andersen

Abstract
Regularization is a common tool in variational inverse problems to impose assumptions on the parameters of the problem. One such assumption is sparsity, which is commonly promoted using lasso and total variation-like regularization. Although the solutions to many such regularized inverse problems can be considered as points of maximum probability of well-chosen posterior distributions, samples from these distributions are generally not sparse. In this paper, we present a framework for implicitly defining a probability distribution that combines the effects of sparsity imposing regularization with Gaussian distributions. Unlike continuous distributions, these implicit distributions can assign positive probability to sparse vectors. We study these regularized distributions for various regularization functions including total variation regularization and piecewise linear convex functions. We apply the developed theory to uncertainty quantification for Bayesian linear inverse problems and derive a Gibbs sampler for a Bayesian hierarchical model. To illustrate the difference between our sparsity-inducing framework and continuous distributions, we apply our framework to small-scale deblurring and computed tomography examples.

Introduction

A common method for reconstructing a signal
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
from noisy measurements
\bm{b}=A\bm{x}+\bm{e}
, where
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is a linear forward operator and
\bm{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is noise, is to solve a regularized linear least squares problem of the form

\argmin_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\frac{\lambda}{2}\|A\bm{x}-\bm{b}\|^{2}_{2}+f(\bm{x})\right\},

(1)

where the function
f
is chosen to make the optimization problem well-posed and to improve the reconstruction by penalizing unwanted behavior. A common choice is to promote sparsity in the reconstruction by choosing
l_{1}
-based regularization functions of the form
f(\bm{x})=\gamma\|D\bm{x}\|_{1}
, where
\gamma>0
is the strength of the regularization and
D
is a linear operator representing the basis in which the reconstruction is to be sparse. Such regularization functions have been studied in areas like compressed sensing with the basis pursuit algorithm
[
7
]
and in image processing with total variation regularization
[
9
]
.

Reconstructing the signal using (
1
) can be motivated by its solution being the maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimate of a suitable posterior distribution, i.e.,

\argmin_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\frac{\lambda}{2}\|A\bm{x}-\bm{b}\|^{2}_{2}+f(\bm{x})\right\}=\argmax_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\pi(\bm{x}\,|\,\bm{b}),

(2)

where,

\pi(\bm{x}\,|\,\bm{b})\,\propto\,\pi(\bm{b}\,|\,\bm{x})\pi(\bm{x})\,\propto\,\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda}{2}\|A\bm{x}-\bm{b}\|^{2}_{2}-f(\bm{x})\right).

(3)

The likelihood function
\pi(\bm{b}\,|\,\bm{x})\,\propto\,\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda}{2}\|A\bm{x}-\bm{b}\|^{2}_{2}\right)
is obtained from assuming that the error
\bm{e}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\lambda^{-1}I)
, i.e., normally distributed with mean zero and covariance
\lambda^{-1}I
, whilst the prior
\pi(\bm{x})\,\propto\,\exp(-f(\bm{x}))
describes any further assumptions we make on the reconstruction.

Although the MAP estimate (
2
) can have guaranteed sparsity for suitable regularization functions
f
, the corresponding posterior distribution (
3
) assigns zero probability to these sparse solutions. This is because sparsity is represented by low-dimensional subspaces which are always assigned zero probability by continuous probability distributions like (
3
).

A method for creating probability distributions that assign positive probability to low-dimensional subspaces is by using variable dimension models, see
[
14
]
. In these methods, a variety of models
M_{i}
are constructed, each having their own probability distribution
\pi(\bm{x}\,|\,M_{i})
, possibly supported on a low-dimensional subspace. These model distributions are then combined with a model prior distribution
\pi(M_{i})
. For sparsity, each model distribution would be supported on a sparse subspace and the model prior distribution corresponds to assigning a prior distribution on the sparsity of the reconstruction.

Sampling from these variable dimension models is quite challenging. A common method for sampling such distributions are Reversible Jump Markov Chain Monte Carlo (RJMCMC) method
[
8
]
. This method requires a lot of tuning and easily fails for large dimensional problems. An alternative method is the Shrinkage-Thresholding Metropolis adjusted Langevin
algorithm (STMALA)
[
17
]
which provides an efficient proposal for an RJMCMC-like algorithm based on MALA. Although this method works better on larger scale problems, it still requires difficult parameter tuning and can easily result in highly correlated samples.

In
[
5
]
, we showed that for a closed and convex set
\mathbf{C}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the distribution of the constrained linear least squares problem

\argmin_{\bm{x}\in\mathbf{C}}\left\{\frac{\lambda}{2}\|A\bm{x}-\hat{\bm{b}}\|^{2}_{2}+\frac{\delta}{2}\|L\bm{x}-\hat{\bm{c}}\|^{2}_{2}\right\},

(4)

with
{\text{Null}}(A)\cap{\text{Null}}(L)=\{\bm{0}\}
,
\hat{\bm{b}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{b},\lambda^{-1}I)
and
\hat{\bm{c}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{c},\delta^{-1}I)
, is identical to sampling from the Gaussian posterior

\pi(\bm{x}\,|\,\bm{b})\,\propto\,\pi(\bm{b}\,|\,\bm{x})\pi(\bm{x})\,\propto\,\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda}{2}\|A\bm{x}-\bm{b}\|^{2}_{2}-\frac{\delta}{2}\|L\bm{x}-\bm{c}\|^{2}_{2}\right),

(5)

and projecting the sample onto the constraints set
\mathbf{C}
with respect to the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\lambda A^{T}A+\delta L^{T}L}
. Furthermore, if the constraint set
\mathbf{C}
is polyhedral, then the resulting projected Gaussian assigns positive probability to the various low-dimensional faces of
\mathbf{C}
. The structure of this projected Gaussian distribution is similar to those of variable dimension models and results in independent samples, however, the corresponding probability distribution is implicit and for each sample, an instance of optimization problem (
4
) has to be solved accurately.

This method is similar to other optimization-based sampling methods like Perturbation-Optimization
[
12
]
for sampling from Gaussian distributions and Randomize-Then-Optimize (RTO)
[
19
]
for non-linear inverse problems. However, these methods are restricted to sampling from explicitly described continuous probability distributions, whilst our method results in a probability distribution for which we generally do not have an explicit expression.

In this paper, we generalize most of the results in
[
5
]
about (
4
), by studying the optimization problem

\argmin_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\frac{\lambda}{2}\|A\bm{x}-\hat{\bm{b}}\|^{2}_{2}+\frac{\delta}{2}\|L\bm{x}-\hat{\bm{c}}\|^{2}_{2}+f(\bm{x})\right\},

(6)

where
f
is a sparsity promoting regularization. The case
f(\bm{x})=\|D\bm{x}\|_{1}
, where
D
is a diagonal matrix, has been studied before in
[
6
]
. We mainly consider the class of convex piecewise linear functions
f
. This class includes
l_{1}
-norm based regularization functions and the polyhedral constraints considered in
[
5
]
. We derive a characterization of the distribution obtained through the regularized and randomized linear least squares problem (
6
). The properties we derive are applied to Bayesian linear inverse problems and hierarchical models and tested using some numerical experiments.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we transform problem (
6
) into a general framework using proximal operators and study the properties of the resulting regularized Gaussian distributions. We will apply this theory in Section
3
to Bayesian linear inverse problems and derive an algorithm for Bayesian hierarchical models in Section
4
. Finally, numerical experiments based on these models are covered in Section
5
.

2
Regularized Multivariate Distributions

We discuss a theory for combining the effects of regularization to probability distributions. In Subsection
2.1
, the definition of a regularized Gaussian is presented together with some general theory about regularized distributions. In Subsection
2.2
, the focus will be on regularization functions that introduce sparsity when combined with Gaussian distributions.

2.1
General theory

If
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
has rank
n
, then the regularized least squares problem with random data
\hat{\bm{b}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{b},\lambda^{-1}I)
can be transformed as follows,

\displaystyle\argmin_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\frac{\lambda}{2}\|A\bm{x}-\hat{\bm{b}}\|^{2}_{2}+f(\bm{x})\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\argmin_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{x}-(A^{T}A)^{-1}(A^{T}\hat{\bm{b}})\|^{2}_{\lambda A^{T}A}+f(\bm{x})\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\argmin_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\star}\|^{2}_{\Sigma^{-1}}+f(\bm{x})\right\},

(7)

where
\bm{x}^{\star}=(A^{T}A)^{-1}(A^{T}\hat{\bm{b}})
has a Gaussian distribution with density

\pi_{\bm{x}^{\star}}(\bm{x})\,\propto\,\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda}{2}\|A\bm{x}-\bm{b}\|^{2}_{2}\right),

(8)

and covariance
\Sigma=(\lambda A^{T}A)^{-1}
. Due to this transformation, we can study the general setting of optimization problem (
7
), where
\bm{x}^{\star}
follows any Gaussian distribution. This leads to the following assumption and definition of the object that will be the focus of study in this section.

Assumption 1
.

Let
\bm{x}^{\star}
be a Gaussian random vector satisfying
\bm{x}^{\star}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu},\Sigma)
with mean
\bm{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and covariance matrix
\Sigma\in\mathbb{S}_{++}^{n}
, where
\mathbb{S}_{++}^{n}
denotes the set of symmetric positive definite real matrices of order
n
.

Definition 2
.

Under Assumption
1
. If
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
is a proper lower semi-continuous convex function, then the Gaussian
\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu},\Sigma)
regularized by
f
is defined by

\prox_{f}^{\Sigma^{-1}}(\bm{x}^{\star}):=\argmin_{\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{z}-\bm{x}^{\star}\|^{2}_{\Sigma^{-1}}+f(\bm{z})\right\},

(9)

where
\prox_{f}^{\Sigma^{-1}}
is the proximal operator with respect to the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}
.

Due to its relation to regularized linear least squares, the focus of this work is on regularized Gaussian distributions, but the proximal operator can be applied to any probability distribution. If
\pi
is any probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then by continuity of the proximal operator
\prox_{f}^{\Sigma^{-1}}
, the pushforward measure
\pi\circ\left[\prox_{f}^{\Sigma^{-1}}\right]^{-1}
is a well-defined probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Furthermore, some of the results presented in this work do not rely on
\pi
being Gaussian and will therefore easily generalize to other distributions.

For most proper lower semi-continuous convex functions
f
, we cannot compute an explicit expression for
\prox_{f}^{\Sigma^{-1}}
, however, for some simple examples an explicit expression does exist. Let
f(\bm{z})=\frac{1}{2}\|D\bm{z}-\bm{d}\|_{2}^{2}
for any
D\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times n}
and
\bm{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, i.e., generalized Tikhonov regularization. For such regularization, the regularized Gaussian is

\prox_{f}^{\Sigma^{-1}}(\bm{x}^{\star})=(\Sigma^{-1}+D^{T}D)^{-1}(\Sigma^{-1}\bm{x}^{\star}+D^{T}\bm{d}),

which is again a Gaussian distribution. However, in general, the regularized Gaussian is not a Gaussian anymore. For a more complicated example of an explicit description of a regularized Gaussian, see
[
17
, Lemma II.2]
. Let
f(\bm{x})=\chi_{\mathbf{C}}(\bm{x})
be the characteristic function of a closed and convex set
\mathbf{C}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, i.e.,
f(\bm{x})
is zero on
\mathbf{C}
and infinite otherwise. Then
\prox_{f}^{\Sigma^{-1}}(\bm{x}^{\star})=\Pi^{\Sigma^{-1}}_{\mathbf{C}}(\bm{x}^{\star})
, where
\Pi^{\Sigma^{-1}}_{\mathbf{C}}(\bm{x})
denotes the oblique projection of
\bm{x}
onto
\mathbf{C}
. In this case,
\prox_{f}^{\Sigma^{-1}}(\bm{x}^{\star})
has the same density as
\bm{x}^{\star}
on the interior of
\mathbf{C}
, but all the mass outside of
\mathbf{C}
gets transported to the boundary of
\mathbf{C}
. We have studied this projection setting specifically in
[
5
]
.

In general, the following proposition describes the support of the regularized Gaussian.

Proposition 3
.

Under Assumption
1
. If
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
is a proper lower semi-continuous convex function, then,

\supp(\prox_{f}^{\Sigma^{-1}}(\bm{x}^{\star}))=\cl(\dom(f)),

where
\cl(\mathbf{C})
denotes the closure of
\mathbf{C}
and
\supp(\bm{z}^{\star})
denotes the support of the random vector
\bm{z}^{\star}
, i.e, the set of points for which any open set around that point is assigned positive probability by
\bm{z}^{\star}
.

Proof.

Use that
\range(\prox_{f}^{\Sigma^{-1}})=\dom(f)
in Lemma
15
.

Note that for the case of
f(\bm{x})=\chi_{\mathbf{C}}(\bm{x})
the projection
\Pi^{\Sigma^{-1}}_{\mathbf{C}}
maps all samples outside the constraint set
\mathbf{C}
to the boundary
\bd(\mathbf{C})
, which is a set with Lebesgue measure zero. Furthermore, it can be shown
[
5
, Lemma 2.4]
that
\Pi^{\Sigma^{-1}}_{\mathbf{C}}(\bm{x}^{\star})
assigns positive probability to
\bd(\mathbf{C})
if
\mathbf{C}\neq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, thus
\Pi^{\Sigma^{-1}}_{\mathbf{C}}(\bm{x}^{\star})
does not have a density with respect to the Lebesgue measure and is therefore not a continuous random variable. The following proposition gives a condition on the smoothness of
f
such that the regularized Gaussian

is a continuous random variable.

Proposition 4
.

Under Assumption
1

f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}

\prox_{f}^{\Sigma^{-1}}(\bm{x}^{\star})

Proof.

Note that the optimality condition associated with

\bm{x}\in\bm{z}+\Sigma\partial f(\bm{z}),

(10)

\left[\prox_{f}^{\Sigma^{-1}}\right]^{-1}(\bm{z})=\bm{z}+\Sigma\partial f(\bm{z}).

(11)

\left[\prox_{f}^{\Sigma^{-1}}\right]^{-1}(\bm{z})=\bm{z}+\Sigma\nabla f(\bm{z})

2.2
Sparsity

Although Theorem
5
could apply to any surface, commonly, the focus is put on linear subspaces. Particularly, if we are interested in solutions that lie in a subspace defined by the linear system
D\bm{x}=\bm{d}
, we add as regularization function
\|D\bm{x}-\bm{d}\|
, where the norm is non-differentiable at zero, hence the regularization function is non-differentiable in the specified subspace. The following theorem shows that for these kinds of regularization functions, the regularized Gaussian has positive probability on these subspaces.

Theorem 6
.

Let
p\geq 1
,
D_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{c_{i}\times n}
and
\bm{d}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{c_{i}}
for
i=1,\dots,k

(12)

For any
\mathbf{I}\subseteq\{1,\dots,k\}

\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{I}}

\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,|\,D_{i}\bm{x}-\bm{d}_{i}=0,\forall i\in\mathbf{I}\text{ and }D_{i}\bm{x}-\bm{d}_{i}\neq 0,\forall i\not\in\mathbf{I}\}
. Then,

\mathbb{P}\left(\prox_{f}^{\Sigma^{-1}}(\bm{x}^{\star})\in\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{I}}\right)>0.

Proof.

Assume first that

\bm{x}\in\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{I}}

\bm{x}\mapsto\|D_{i}\bm{x}-\bm{d}_{i}\|_{p}

We will show that the regularized Gaussian assigns positive probability to each set of the polyhedral partition. Furthermore, the regularized Gaussian can be described by a mixture of possibly low-dimensional densities on each set and these densities are proportional to the original Gaussian up to translation. The following lemma describes an orthogonality property which holds due to the translation component
\nabla g
in (
13
) being zero.

Lemma 9
.

Consider the polyhedral partition
\{\mathbf{F}_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbf{J}}
of Definition
8
. The subdifferential
\partial f(\bm{x})
for
\bm{x}\in\mathbf{F}_{i}
does not depend on
\bm{x}
, hence will be denoted by
\partial f_{\mathbf{F}_{j}}
. Furthermore, parameterize
\mathbf{F}_{j}
as
\bm{x}_{j}+F_{j}\bm{u}
for fixed
\bm{x}_{j}\in\mathbf{F}_{j}
,
F_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times\dim(\mathbf{F}_{j})}
, and local coordinates
\bm{u}\in\mathbf{U}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\dim(\mathbf{F}_{j})}
. Then, there exists
\bm{d}\in\{\bm{0},\bm{a}_{1},\dots,\bm{a}_{|\mathbf{I}|}\}
such that
(\partial f_{\mathbf{F}_{j}}-\bm{d})^{T}F_{j}\bm{u}=\{0\}
.

Proof.

For each
\mathbf{F}_{j}
, there exists a largest, non-empty set of active affine functions
\hat{\mathbf{I}}\subseteq\mathbf{I}
, such that
f|_{\mathbf{F}_{j}}(\bm{x})=\bm{a}_{i}^{T}\bm{x}+b_{i}
for all
i\in\hat{\mathbf{I}}
.
For the first part, note that for
\bm{x}\in\mathbf{F}_{j}
,
\partial f(\bm{x})=N_{\mathbf{\dom(f)}}(\bm{x})+\conv\{\bm{a}_{i}\,|\,i\in\hat{\mathbf{I}}\}
, where
\conv\{\mathbf{S}\}
denotes the convex hull of
\mathbf{S}
. Hence,
\partial f(\bm{x})
is independent of
\bm{x}\in\mathbf{F}_{j}
.

For the second part, note that for any
j,k\in\hat{\mathbf{I}}
and
\bm{u}\in\mathbf{U}
,

\displaystyle\bm{a}_{j}^{T}(\bm{x}_{j}+F_{j}\bm{u})+b_{j}

\displaystyle=\bm{a}_{k}^{T}(\bm{x}_{j}+F_{j}\bm{u})+b_{k}\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\bm{a}_{j}^{T}\bm{x}_{j}+b_{j}

\displaystyle=\bm{a}_{k}^{T}\bm{x}_{j}+b_{k}.

From these equations we obtain

0=(\bm{a}^{T}_{j}\bm{x}_{j}+b_{j})-(\bm{a}^{T}_{k}\bm{x}_{j}+b_{k})+(\bm{a}_{j}-\bm{a}_{k})^{T}F_{j}\bm{u}=(\bm{a}_{j}-\bm{a}_{k})^{T}F_{j}\bm{u}.

Therefore, for any
\bm{v}\in\conv\{\bm{a}_{j}\,|\,j\in\hat{\mathbf{I}}\}
we have
(\bm{v}-\bm{a}_{k})^{T}F_{j}\bm{u}=0
.
Furthermore, for any
\bm{n}\in N_{\mathbf{\dom(f)}}(\bm{x}_{j}+F_{j}\bm{u})
we have
\bm{n}^{T}F_{j}\bm{u}=0
.
Thus, we can conclude that for any
\bm{v}\in\partial f(\bm{x})=N_{\dom(f)}(\bm{x})+\conv\{\bm{a}_{i}\,|\,i\in\hat{\mathbf{I}}\}
, we have
(\bm{v}-\bm{a}_{k})^{T}F_{j}\bm{u}=0
.

3
Application to Bayesian linear inverse problems

In this section, we will discuss how to apply the concept of a regularized Gaussian and the theory discussed in Section
2
to linear inverse problems. At the beginning of Subsection
2.1
, we transformed the randomized regularized linear least squares problem

\argmin_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\frac{\lambda}{2}\|A\bm{x}-\hat{\bm{b}}\|_{2}^{2}+f(\bm{x})\right\},

with
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and
\hat{\bm{b}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{b},\lambda^{-1}I)
into the proximal framework of Definition
2
. This transformation required the assumption that
A
has rank
n
. However, inverse problems often have less data than variables, in which case this transformation cannot be applied. This problem can be addressed by introducing a second randomized linear least squares term, resulting in a problem of the form

\argmin_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\frac{\lambda}{2}\|A\bm{x}-\hat{\bm{b}}\|^{2}_{2}+\frac{\delta}{2}\|L\bm{x}-\hat{\bm{c}}\|^{2}_{2}+f(\bm{x})\right\},

(14)

with
\hat{\bm{b}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{b},\lambda^{-1}I),\hat{\bm{c}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{c},\delta^{-1}I)
and assuming
{\text{Null}}(A)\cap{\text{Null}}(L)=\{\bm{0}\}
.
Because (
14
) is equivalent to

\prox_{f}^{\Sigma^{-1}}(\bm{x}^{\star}),\quad\text{with }\Sigma^{-1}=\delta L^{T}L+\lambda A^{T}A\text{ and }\bm{x}^{\star}\sim\mathcal{N}(\Sigma(\lambda A^{T}\bm{b}+\delta L^{T}\bm{c}),\Sigma),

(15)

this problem fits into the framework and theory discussed in Section
2
.

In Subsection
3.1
, we discuss this setting where the framework of Section
2
applies from a Bayesian viewpoint.
In subsection
3.2
, we discuss the setting where the randomized linear least squares term is underdetermined.

3.1
Overdetermined randomization

Let us now return to the reconstruction of a signal
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
from noisy measurements
\bm{b}=A\bm{x}+\bm{e}
, with linear forward operator
A:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
and independent Gaussian noise
\bm{e}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\lambda^{-1}I)
. If we assume a priori that

\pi(\bm{x})\,\propto\,\exp\left(-\frac{\delta}{2}\|L\bm{x}-\bm{c}\|^{2}_{2}\right),

(16)

then we can compute the posterior up to scaling, i.e.,

\pi(\bm{x}\,|\,\bm{b})\,\propto\,\pi(\bm{b}\,|\,\bm{x})\pi(\bm{x})\,\propto\,\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda}{2}\|A\bm{x}-\bm{b}\|^{2}_{2}-\frac{\delta}{2}\|L\bm{x}-\bm{c}\|^{2}_{2}\right).

We can now define a regularized posterior
\bm{x}^{\star}
using the regularized and randomized linear least squares problem

\bm{x}^{\star}=\argmin_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\frac{\lambda}{2}\|A\bm{x}-\hat{\bm{b}}\|^{2}_{2}+\frac{\delta}{2}\|L\bm{x}-\hat{\bm{c}}\|^{2}_{2}+f(\bm{x})\right\}.

Under Assumption
7
, we can conclude from Theorem
10
, that the distribution of this regularized posterior conditioned on a set
\mathbf{F}_{i}
in the polyhedral partition from Definition
8
satisfies

\pi(\bm{x}\,|\,\mathbf{F}_{i},\bm{b})\,\propto\,\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda}{2}\|A\bm{x}-\bm{b}\|^{2}_{2}-\frac{\delta}{2}\|L\bm{x}-\bm{c}\|^{2}_{2}-f(\bm{x})\right)\,\propto\,\pi(\bm{b}\,|\,\bm{x})\pi(\bm{x})\exp(-f(\bm{x})).

(17)

Note that
\pi(\bm{b}\,|\,\bm{x})=\pi(\bm{b}\,|\,\bm{x},\mathbf{F}_{i})
for
\bm{x}\in\mathbf{F}_{i}
, then we can compute the conditional prior corresponding to the regularized posterior, precisely,

\pi(\bm{x}\,|\,\mathbf{F}_{i})\,\propto\,\frac{\pi(\bm{x}\,|\,\mathbf{F}_{i},\bm{b})}{\pi(\bm{b}\,|\,\bm{x},\mathbf{F}_{i})}\,\propto\,\pi(\bm{x})\exp(-f(\bm{x}))\,\propto\,\exp\left(-\frac{\delta}{2}\|L\bm{x}-\bm{c}\|^{2}_{2}-f(\bm{x})\right).

(18)

Thus, conditioned on the set
\mathbf{F}_{i}
, the prior is the same as if we would have chosen the prior
\exp\left(-\frac{\delta}{2}\|L\bm{x}-\bm{c}\|^{2}_{2}-f(\bm{x})\right)
on the whole of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. However, the regularized posterior assigns positive probability to sparse sets. Furthermore, this shows that the regularized posterior corresponds to imposing a variable dimension model as prior on
\bm{x}
, where the models are associated with the sets
\mathbf{F}_{i}
. Our method therefore implicitly defines the model prior
\pi(\mathbf{F}_{i})
, which generally depends on the forward operator and data.

3.2
Underdetermined randomization

In this subsection, we consider the setting where the linear inverse problem is under-determined and we do not add an explicit prior, i.e., we consider optimization problems of the form

\argmin_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\frac{\lambda}{2}\|A\bm{x}-\hat{\bm{b}}\|^{2}_{2}+f(\bm{x})\right\},

(19)

where
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
has rank less than
n
.

If
g(\bm{x})=f(\bm{x})-\frac{\delta}{2}\|L\bm{x}-\bm{c}\|
is still a proper lower semi-continuous convex function for some
L
such that
{\text{Null}}(A)\cap{\text{Null}}(L)=\{\bm{0}\}
, then
optimization problem (
19
) is equivalent to

\bm{z}^{\star}=\argmin_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\frac{\lambda}{2}\|A\bm{x}-\hat{\bm{b}}\|^{2}_{2}+\frac{\delta}{2}\|L\bm{x}-\bm{c}\|^{2}_{2}+g(\bm{x})\right\},

(20)

i.e., the same optimization problem as when we add an explicit prior, but without the randomization of the prior least squares term. Such a case might arise if
f
is strongly convex.

As a results,
\bm{z}^{\star}
can be written as

\bm{z}^{\star}=\prox_{g}^{\lambda A^{T}A+\delta L^{T}L}\left(\bm{x}_{\text{deg}}^{\star}\right),

where
\bm{x}_{\text{deg}}^{\star}=(\lambda A^{T}A+\delta L^{T}L)^{-1}(A^{T}\hat{\bm{b}}+L^{T}\bm{c})
is a degenerate Gaussian distribution.

Because
\prox_{f}^{\lambda A^{T}A+\delta L^{T}L}
is still a continuous function, we can conclude from Lemma
15
that
\bm{z}^{\star}
is not necessarily supported on the whole of
\dom(f)
and therefore, the support of
\bm{z}^{\star}
might not include the true signal
\bm{x}_{0}\in\dom(f)
. The following Lemma characterizes which signals can be reconstructed.

Lemma 11
.

Assume that
f
is such that optimization problem (
19
) is well-posed for any perturbation
\hat{\bm{b}}
, i.e., there exists a unique solution that depends continuously on the perturbation
\hat{\bm{b}}
. Then, for any
\bm{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, there exists
\bm{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that

\bm{x}_{0}=\argmin_{\bm{x}}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\|A\bm{x}-\bm{q}\|_{2}^{2}+f(\bm{x})\right\},

(21)

if and only if
\partial f(\bm{x}_{0})\cap\range(A^{T})\neq\emptyset
.

Furthermore, if
\hat{\bm{q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a random variable such that
\support(\hat{\bm{q}})=\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
then the random variable

\bm{x}^{\star}=\argmin_{\bm{x}}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\|A\bm{x}-\hat{\bm{q}}\|_{2}^{2}+f(\bm{x})\right\},

(22)

satisfies

\support(\bm{x}^{\star})=\closure\left(\left\{\bm{x}\in\dom(f)\,|\,\partial f(\bm{x})\cap\range(A^{T})\neq\emptyset\right\}\right).

Proof.

The first part follows from the observation that the optimality condition of (
21
) can be written as

A^{T}(\bm{q}-A\bm{x}_{0})\in\partial f(\bm{x}_{0}).

For the second part, by assumption the solution map
\bm{q}\mapsto\argmin_{\bm{x}}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\|A\bm{x}-\bm{q}\|_{2}^{2}+f(\bm{x})\right\}
is continuous, hence the conclusion follows from the first part combined with Lemma
15
.

Whilst for general regularization functions
f
, the set
\support(\bm{x}^{\star})
can be very complicated, the following Corollary describes a few cases where
\support(\bm{x}^{\star})
is well-behaved.

Corollary 12
.

Under Assumption
7
consider the polyhedral partition
\{\mathbf{F}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbf{J}}
of Definition
8
.
Assume (
21
) is well-posed, then if
\partial f(\bm{x})\cap\range(A^{T})\neq\emptyset
holds for any
\bm{x}\in\mathbf{F}_{i}
, then it holds for all
\bm{x}\in\mathbf{F}_{i}
. Thus, there exists
\tilde{\mathbf{J}}\subset\mathbf{J}
such that

\support(\bm{x}^{\star})=\bigcup_{j\in\tilde{\mathbf{J}}}\closure\left(\mathbf{F}_{j}\right).

Furthermore,
f
is symmetric and positive homogeneous, then
\support(\bm{x}^{\star})
is a linear subspace and if
f=\chi_{\mathbf{C}}+g
, where
\mathbf{C}
is a closed convex set and
g
is symmetric and positive homogeneous, then
\support(\bm{x}^{\star})
is a convex set.

As an example, by the second part of Corollary
12
, the support
\support(\bm{x}^{\star})
is a linear subspace. The convexity of
\support(\bm{x}^{\star})
guarantees that certain point estimates like the empirical mean stays within the support. However, note that the support does not depend on the data used for reconstruction, because the perturbed data has full support.

Whilst these methods with low-dimensional randomization can still give good results, the low-dimensional support of the distributions cannot guarantee positive mass around the truth, which can be guaranteed by adding the randomized linear least squares regularization as described in the beginning of Subsection
3.1
.

4
Bayesian hierarchical model

Consider the setting of Subsection
3.1
with
\bm{c}=\bm{0}
. To make the model more robust, we will add prior distributions on the hyperparameters
\lambda
and
\delta
. More precisely, let the conditional distribution
\pi(\bm{x}\,|\,\lambda,\delta,\bm{b})
be defined by

\argmin_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\frac{\lambda}{2}\|A\bm{x}-\hat{\bm{b}}\|^{2}_{2}+\frac{\delta}{2}\|L\bm{x}-\hat{\bm{c}}\|^{2}_{2}+\sqrt{\delta}f(\bm{x})\right\},

(23)

with
\hat{\bm{b}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{b},\lambda^{-1}I)
and
\hat{\bm{c}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\delta^{-1}I)
.
Furthermore, let
\alpha_{\lambda},\alpha_{\delta},\beta_{\lambda},\beta_{\delta}>0
and define the hyperpriors

\lambda\sim\Gamma(\alpha_{\lambda},\beta_{\lambda})\quad\text{and}\quad\delta\sim\Gamma(\alpha_{\delta},\beta_{\delta}),\\

or equivalently

\pi(\lambda)\,\propto\,\lambda^{\alpha_{\lambda}-1}\exp(-\beta_{\lambda}\lambda)\quad\text{and}\quad\pi(\delta)\,\propto\,\delta^{\alpha_{\delta}-1}\exp(-\beta_{\delta}\delta).

(24)

To be able to compute the conditional distribution of
\delta
given
\bm{x}
, we need to know how the conditional prior (
18
) depends on
\delta
, which is hidden in the proportionality. This proportionality can be computed under some restrictions on the regularization function
f
, as stated in the following lemma.

Lemma 13
.

Assume that the epigraph of
f
is a polyhedral cone up to an additive constant. Equivalently,
f
satisfies Assumption
7
and is positive homogeneous, i.e.,
f(\gamma\bm{x})=\gamma f(\bm{x})
for
\gamma\geq 0
. Then the conditional prior
\pi(\bm{x}\,|\,\delta,\mathbf{F}_{j})
satisfies

\pi(\bm{x}\,|\,\delta,\mathbf{F}_{j})\,\propto\,\delta^{-\dim(\mathbf{F}_{j})/2}\exp\left(-\frac{\delta}{2}\|L\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2}-\sqrt{\delta}f(\bm{x})\right).

(25)

Proof.

We can obtain the dependence on
\delta
by computing the normalization constant
K
using that any set
\mathbf{F}_{j}
is invariant under multiplication by a positive constant,

\displaystyle 1=\int_{\mathbf{F}_{j}}\pi(\bm{x}\,|\,\delta,\mathbf{F}_{i})\text{d}\bm{x}

\displaystyle=K\int_{\mathbf{F}_{j}}\exp\left(-\frac{\delta}{2}\|L\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2}-\sqrt{\delta}f(\bm{x})\right)\text{d}\bm{x}

\displaystyle=K\int_{\mathbf{F}_{j}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|L\sqrt{\delta}\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2}-f(\sqrt{\delta}\bm{x})\right)\text{d}\bm{x}

\displaystyle=K\delta^{-\text{dim}(\mathbf{F}_{j})/2}\int_{\mathbf{F}_{j}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|L\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2}-f(\bm{x})\right)\text{d}\bm{x}.

Hence we obtain that

\pi(\bm{x}\,|\,\delta,\mathbf{F}_{j})\,\propto\,\delta^{\dim(\mathbf{F}_{j})/2}\exp\left(-\frac{\delta}{2}\|L\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2}-\sqrt{\delta}f(\bm{x})\right).

Note that in both optimization problem (
23
) and the conditional prior (
25
), the regularization function is scaled by the hyperparameter
\delta
. While the additional condition that the epigraph of
f
is a polyhedral cone can be quite restrictive, many important regularization functions do satisfy this condition, e.g., nonnegativity constraints and
l_{1}
-norm based regularization, like
\|Dx\|_{1}
for any matrix
D
.

Using Lemma
13
, we can obtain the distribution of the (hyper)parameters given
\mathbf{F}_{j}
,

\displaystyle\pi(\bm{x},\lambda,\delta\,|\,\bm{b},\mathbf{F}_{j})\,\propto\,

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}\left\{\bm{x}\in\mathbf{F}_{j}\right\}\pi(\bm{b}\,|\,\bm{x},\lambda,\delta)\pi(\bm{x}\,|\,\delta,\mathbf{F}_{j})\pi(\lambda)\pi(\delta)

\displaystyle\,\propto\,

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}\left\{\bm{x}\in\mathbf{F}_{j}\right\}\lambda^{m/2+\alpha_{\lambda}-1}\delta^{\text{dim}(\mathbf{F}_{j})/2+\alpha_{\delta}-1}

\displaystyle\times\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda}{2}\|A\bm{x}-\bm{b}\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{\delta}{2}\|L\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2}-\sqrt{\delta}f(\bm{x})-\beta_{\lambda}\lambda-\beta_{\delta}\delta\right),

(26)

where
\mathbf{1}\left\{\bm{x}\in\mathbf{F}_{j}\right\}
is
1
if
\bm{x}\in\mathbf{F}_{j}
and
0
otherwise.

Given
\bm{x}
, denote the set
\mathbf{F}
which contains
\bm{x}
by
\mathbf{F}(\bm{x})
, then we obtain the following conditional distributions of the hyperparameters,

\displaystyle\pi(\lambda\,|\,\bm{x},\bm{b})

\displaystyle\,\propto\,\lambda^{m/2+\alpha_{\lambda}-1}\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda}{2}\|A\bm{x}-\bm{b}\|_{2}^{2}-\beta_{\lambda}\lambda\right),\text{ for }\lambda>0\quad\text{and}

(27)

\displaystyle\pi(\delta\,|\,\bm{x},\bm{b})

\displaystyle\,\propto\,\delta^{\text{dim}(\mathbf{F}(\bm{x}))/2+\alpha_{\delta}-1}\exp\left(-\frac{\delta}{2}\|L\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2}-\sqrt{\delta}f(\bm{x})-\beta_{\delta}\delta\right),\text{ for }\delta>0,

(28)

of which the first conditional distribution satisfies
\lambda\,|\,\bm{x},\bm{b}\sim\Gamma(m/2+\alpha_{\lambda},\frac{1}{2}\|A\bm{x}-\bm{b}\|_{2}^{2}+\beta_{\lambda})
.

These conditional distribution can be used to derive the Polyhedral Cone Epigraph Hierarchical Gibbs Sampler described in Algorithm

.

Proof.

For

or

6
Conclusions and Further Research

We have presented a framework for combining regularization with Gaussian distributions. By repeatedly solving a regularized linear least squares for different Gaussian perturbations of the data, we obtain samples from a probability distribution that is equivalent to post-processing a Gaussian posterior with a proximal operator. If the regularization function is sufficiently smooth, then this distribution is a continuous distribution. However, for sparsity inducing regularization functions, e.g., total variation and lasso, the regularized distribution assigns positive probability to low-dimensional subspaces corresponding to the sparsity modeled with the regularization function. Such distributions behave similarly to varying dimension models, but are implicitly defined by the solution of a randomized optimization problem.

We applied the theory to Bayesian linear inverse problems and discussed how the regularized Gaussian can then be interpreted as an implicitly defined posterior distribution by looking into the underlying prior distribution. This implicit prior perspective allowed us to derive Gibbs samplers for some Bayesian hierarchical models that include the regularized Gaussian based on regularization functions whose epigraph are polyhedral cones, e.g. non-negative total variation. We also provided some results on the support of the distribution in the general case where the linear least squares term is underdetermined.

Because sampling from this distribution only requires solving regularized linear least squares problems, we only need tools from optimization theory in order to obtain samples. For some small scale deblurring and computed tomography examples, we showed the difference between our framework and continuous distributions, and highlighted the robustness of the Gibbs sampler for the Bayesian hierarchical model.

Obtaining a single independent sample from the regularized distribution has approximately the same computational cost as solving a similar regularized variational inverse problem. It is therefore computationally expensive to obtain many exact independent samples. However, we have shown in a small numerical example that inaccurate samples can still give good results at a fraction of the computational cost. To make the approach more practical, further research could include a more in-depth study on the effect that inaccurately solving the optimization problem has on the distribution of the samples.

In this work, we have not considered all possible regularization functions. A big part of the theory focused on low-dimensional subspaces, for which we only provided results for sufficiently smooth functions or non-differentiable functions with sufficiently well-behaved subdifferential. Furthermore, most theory provided relies on the linear least squares term to be strongly convex, providing only some simple properties for the general case. Further investigating these and more general properties of regularized distributions is a topic for further study.

Lemma 15
.

Let
\mu
be a probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
g:D\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
be continuous, then

\supp(\mu\circ g^{-1})=\cl(g(\supp(\mu))).

Proof.

Let
\bm{y}=g(\bm{x})\in g(\supp(\mu))
and let
\mathbf{N}_{\bm{y}}
be any open neighborhood of
\bm{y}
, then

\displaystyle(\mu\circ g^{-1})(\mathbf{N}_{\bm{y}})=\mu(g^{-1}(\mathbf{N}_{\bm{y}}))=\mu(\hat{\mathbf{N}}_{\bm{x}})>0,

where
\hat{\mathbf{N}}_{\bm{x}}
is an open neighborhood of
\bm{x}
. By the support of
\mu\circ g^{-1}
being closed, we get
\cl(g(\supp(\mu)))\subseteq\supp(\mu\circ g^{-1})
.

Let
\bm{y}\in\supp(\mu\circ g^{-1})
and assume that
\bm{y}\not\in\cl(g(\supp(\mu)))
, then there exists an open neighborhood
\mathbf{N}_{\bm{y}}
around
\bm{y}
such that
\mathbf{N}_{\bm{y}}\cap g(\supp(\mu))=\emptyset
. Hence,
g^{-1}(\mathbf{N}_{\bm{y}})\cap\supp(\mu)=\emptyset
. This would imply that
(\mu\circ g^{-1})(\mathbf{N}_{\bm{y}})=0
, which contradicts
\bm{y}\in\supp(\mu\circ g^{-1})
. Therefore we can conclude that
\supp(\mu\circ g^{-1})\subseteq\cl(g(\supp(\mu)))
.

Lemma 16
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
be a locally Lipschitz continuous function with
\dom(f)
open. Let
\mathbf{U}\subseteq\dom(f)
with
\text{Leb}(\mathbf{U})=0
, then
\text{Leb}(f(\mathbf{U}))=0
, i.e., locally Lipschitz continuous functions preserve sets of Lebesgue measure zero.

Proof.

It follows from
[
16
, Lemma 7.25]
that Lipschitz continuous functions preserve sets of Lebesgue measure zero. If
f
is not Lipschitz continuous, consider the covering
\dom(f)=\cup_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbf{K}_{i}
, where
\mathbf{K}_{i}
is an increasing sequence of compact sets. Because

\text{Leb}(f(\mathbf{U}))\leq\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\text{Leb}(f(\mathbf{U}\cap\mathbf{K}_{i})),

it is enough to show that
\text{Leb}(f(\mathbf{U}\cap\mathbf{K}))=0
for any compact set
\mathbf{K}
.

Because
f
is locally Lipschitz continuous, for every
\bm{u}\in\mathbf{U}\cap\mathbf{K}
there exists an open ball
\mathbf{B}(\bm{u},\epsilon)
on which
f
is Lipschitz continuous. Furthermore, because the set of all these balls is an open cover of the compact set
\cl(\mathbf{U}\cap\mathbf{K})\subset\dom(f)
, there exist a finite subcover
\{\mathbf{B}(\bm{u}_{i},\epsilon_{i})\}_{i=1}^{k}
. Therefore we can conclude that

\text{Leb}(f(\mathbf{U}\cap\mathbf{K}))\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\text{Leb}(f(\mathbf{U}\cap\mathbf{K}\cap\mathbf{B}(\bm{u}_{i},\epsilon_{i})))=0.

Corollary 17
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
be a locally Lipschitz continuous function with
\dom(f)
open and let
\mu
be a Borel measure on
\dom(f)
. If
\mu\ll\text{Leb}
, then
\mu\circ f\ll\text{Leb}
, i.e., if
\mu
has a density with respect to the Lebesgue measure, then so does
\mu\circ f
.

Lemma 18
.

Let
\mathbf{U}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}
and
\mathbf{V}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{l}
be compact sets with continuous functions
\phi:\mathbf{U}\times\mathbf{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\psi:\mathbf{U}\times\mathbf{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
. If
h:\mathbf{U}\times\mathbf{V}\rightarrow\phi(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V})+\psi(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V})
defined by
h(\bm{u},\bm{v})=\phi(\bm{u},\bm{v})+\psi(\bm{u},\bm{v})
is one-to-one, then
h
is a homeomorphism between
\mathbf{U}\times\mathbf{V}
and the Minkowski sum
\phi(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V})+\psi(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V})
.

Proof.

Note that
h
is a bijective continuous function from the compact set
\mathbf{U}\times\mathbf{V}
to the Hausdorff space
\phi(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V})+\psi(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V})\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, hence by
[
18
, Proposition 5.2.5]
,
h
is a homeomorphism.
