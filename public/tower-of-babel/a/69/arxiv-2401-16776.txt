Leveraging Nested MLMC for Sequential Neural Posterior Estimation with Intractable Likelihoods
Xiliang Yang, Yifei Xiong, Zhijian He

Abstract
Sequential neural posterior estimation (SNPE) techniques have been recently proposed for dealing with simulation-based models with intractable likelihoods. They are devoted to learning the posterior from adaptively proposed simulations using neural network-based conditional density estimators. As a SNPE technique, the automatic posterior transformation (APT) method proposed by Greenberg et al. (2019) performs notably and scales to high dimensional data. However, the APT method bears the computation of an expectation of the logarithm of an intractable normalizing constant, i.e., a nested expectation. Although atomic APT was proposed to solve this by discretizing the normalizing constant, it remains challenging to analyze the convergence of learning. In this paper, we propose a nested APT method to estimate the involved nested expectation instead. This facilitates establishing the convergence analysis. Since the nested estimators for the loss function and its gradient are biased, we make use of unbiased multi-level Monte Carlo (MLMC) estimators for debiasing. To further reduce the excessive variance of the unbiased estimators, this paper also develops some truncated MLMC estimators by taking account of the trade-off between the bias and the average cost. Numerical experiments for approximating complex posteriors with multimodal in moderate dimensions are provided.

Posterior density estimation approaches approximate the posterior of interest

Out of this reason, there has been a growing interest in employing neural networks to represent probability density recently, particularly normalizing flows

. Many approaches have been developed to address this issue.

Among the SNPE methods, automatic posterior transformation (APT) requires the computation of the expectation of the logarithm of an intractable normalizing constant, which is a nested expectation. They then propose to use the atomic APT to discretize the normalizing constant. However, it remains challenging to utilize the existing analysis techniques
[
6
]
to perform convergence analysis to the best of our knowledge. To address this limitation, we propose the nested APT method, an method enable the convergence analysis without scarifying the performance. We conduct a thorough analysis of the bias and variance of the loss estimators, moreover, we provide a convergence result for this biased estimator in the case of stochastic gradient descent (SGD). To evaluate the effectiveness of the nested APT, we conduct a series of comprehensive numerical experiments.

Since nested APT is a biased estimator, we undertake a comprehensive exploration of a range of unbiased methods based on multi-level Monte Carlo (MLMC)

Additionally, as we use stochastic gradient-based optimization methods to update the parameters of the neural network, it is well known that the unbiased method suffers from excessive variance in the gradient. To address this issue, we employ several variants of truncated MLMC methods to diminish the variance. Furthermore, we compute the order of the variance and average cost for the estimators for the gradient of the loss function. Finally, we utilize these results to provide the convergence analysis of truncated MLMC and nested APT.

2
Nested APT

2.1
Problem formulation

Let
p(\theta)
represent the prior distribution of the model parameter of interest. Given an observed sample
x_{o}
, our objective is to perform inference on the posterior distribution
p(\theta|x_{o})\propto p(\theta)p(x_{o}|\theta)
. However, in many cases, the likelihood function
p(x|\theta)
either lacks an explicit expression or presents challenges in direct evaluation. Instead, it can be expressed using a ‘simulator’ approach, wherein, given a fixed model parameter
\theta
, we can sample
x
from
p(x|\theta)
.

Since our objective is to approximate the posterior of interest
p(\theta|x_{o})
using tractable density estimators, the KL divergence serves as a primary measure of the discrepancy between two densities. The KL divergence is defined as

\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(p(\theta)\|q(\theta)\right)=\int p(\theta)\log\frac{p(\theta)}{q(\theta)}\mathrm{d}\theta,

which is not less than
0
by using Jensen’s inequality and attains its minima when
q(\theta)
agrees with
p(\theta)
, making it suitable as a loss function. When the likelihood
p(x|\theta)
is tractable, one can directly approximate
p(\theta|x_{o})
by minimizing the KL divergence between the target distribution and the proposed estimator
q(\theta)
within a certain family of distributions. In the likelihood-free context, this can be viewed as a problem of conditional density estimation. Within this framework, a conditional density estimator
q_{F(x,\phi)}(\theta)
based on a neural network
[
48
,
25
]
, is utilized to approximate
p(\theta|x)
over the admissible set of tuning parameter
\phi\in\Phi
. To this end, we focus on minimizing the following
average KL divergence
under the marginal distribution
p(x)=\int p(\theta)p(x|\theta)\mathrm{d}\theta

\displaystyle\quad\ \mathbb{E}_{p(x)}\left[\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(p(\theta|x)\|q_{F(x,\phi)}(\theta)\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\iint p(x)p(\theta|x)\left(\log p(\theta|x)-\log q_{F(x,\phi)}(\theta)\right)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}\theta

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{p(\theta,x)}\left[\log q_{F(x,\phi)}(\theta)\right]+\iint p(\theta,x)\log p(\theta|x)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}\theta

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{L}(\phi)+\iint p(\theta,x)\log p(\theta|x)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}\theta,

where the term

\mathcal{L}(\phi):=-\mathbb{E}_{p(\theta,x)}\left[\log q_{F(x,\phi)}(\theta)\right],

(1)

is used as the loss function. However, since
\mathcal{L}(\phi)
is intractable, we use its empirical estimator instead

\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{N}(\phi)=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log q_{F(x_{i},\phi)}(\theta_{i}),

(2)

where the training data
\{(\theta_{i},x_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
is sampled from the joint probability density
p(\theta,x)=p(\theta)p(x|\theta)
. After training, given the observation
x_{o}
, the posterior
p(\theta|x_{o})
can be approximated by
q_{F(x_{o},\phi)}(\theta)
.

Since our aim is to conduct conditional density estimation at
x_{o}
, it is essential to utilize a proposal distribution
\tilde{p}(\theta)
that provides more informative priors regarding
x_{o}
in comparison to the prior distribution
p(\theta)
. After initializing
\tilde{p}(\theta)
as
p(\theta)
, we then want the approximation of
p(\theta|x_{o})
to serve as a good proposal in the following simulation. This conditional density estimation with adaptively chosen proposal is called
sequential neural posterior estimation
(SNPE). However, after replacing
p(\theta,x)
with
\tilde{p}(\theta,x)=\tilde{p}(\theta)p(x|\theta)
in (
1
), it is observed that
q_{F(x,\phi)}(\theta)
approximates the
proposal posterior
:

\tilde{p}(\theta|x)=p(\theta|x)\frac{\tilde{p}(\theta)p(x)}{p(\theta)\tilde{p}(x)},

(3)

where
\tilde{p}(x)=\int\tilde{p}(\theta)p(x|\theta)\mathrm{d}\theta
. Hence, we need to adjust loss function
\mathcal{L}(\phi)
to make
q_{F(x,\phi)}(\theta)
approximate the true posterior
p(\theta|x)
.

In APT
[
25
]
, the proposal distribution
\tilde{p}(\theta)
is initialized as the prior distribution
p(\theta)
. Consequently, (
1
) can be directly used for the loss function. In the subsequent rounds,
[
25
]
proposed to replace
q_{F(x,\phi)}(\theta),\ p(\theta,x)
with
\tilde{q}_{F(x,\phi)}(\theta)
,
\tilde{p}(\theta,x)
in (
1
) respectively. Explicitly, the loss function proposed in APT is

\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{p}(\theta,x)}\left[-\log\tilde{q}_{F(x,\phi)}(\theta)\right],

(4)

where

\displaystyle\tilde{q}_{F(x,\phi)}(\theta)

\displaystyle=q_{F(x,\phi)}(\theta)\frac{\tilde{p}(\theta)}{p(\theta)}\frac{1}{Z(x,\phi)},

(5)

\displaystyle Z(x,\phi)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{q_{F(x,\phi)}(\theta^{\prime})}{p(\theta^{\prime})}\tilde{p}(\theta^{\prime})\mathrm{d}\theta^{\prime},

Z(x,\phi)
here denotes the normalizing constant. Proposition 1 in
[
48
]
shows that if
q_{F(x,\phi)}(\theta)
is expressive enough that
\tilde{q}_{F(x,\phi^{*})}(\theta)=\tilde{q}(\theta)
for some parameter
\phi^{*}
, then
q_{F(x,\phi^{*})}(\theta)=q(\theta|x)
.

Since the integral
Z(x,\phi)
is usually intractable in practice, APT proposes the use of ‘atomic’ proposals, APT with such proposal is known as atomic APT. Specifically, they assume that
\tilde{p}(\theta)=U_{\Theta}
, where
U_{\Theta}
is a uniform distribution over a finite set
\Theta=\{\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{M}\}
. The uniform setting of the proposal distribution allows for the analytical computation of
Z(x,\phi)
. With this method, (
3
) and (
5
) can be reformulated as

\displaystyle\tilde{p}(\theta|x)=\frac{p(\theta|x)/p(\theta)}{\sum_{\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta}p(\theta^{\prime}|x)/p(\theta^{\prime})},\quad\tilde{q}_{x,\phi}(\theta)=\frac{q_{F(x,\phi)}(\theta)/p(\theta)}{\sum_{\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta}q_{F(x,\phi)}(\theta^{\prime})/p(\theta^{\prime})}.

(6)

Proposition 1 in
[
25
]
provides the consistency guarantees of atomic APT: given that each
\Theta
is constructed by sampling
\theta^{\prime}
from a distribution that covers the target
p(\theta|x_{o})
, atomic APT is able to recover the full posterior.

However, to the best of our knowledge, the use of atomic proposals makes it challenging to analyze its convergence behaviour. Therefore unable to explain the unexpected low performance in some tasks
[
13
]
with the existing convergence results
[
6
]
, both in terms of insight and theory. As an alternative approach to estimate
Z(x,\phi)
, the nested estimation, which enjoys a comprehensive theoretical framework and comparable performance, is studied in the next section.

2.2
Nested APT method

For ease of presentation, we denote
g_{\phi}(x,\theta):=q_{F(x,\phi)}(\theta)/p(\theta)
. We reformulate (
4
) as

\displaystyle\quad\ \mathbb{E}_{\tilde{p}(\theta,x)}\left[-\log\tilde{q}_{F(x,\phi)}(\theta)\right]

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{p}(\theta,x)}\left[\log q_{F(x,\phi)}(\theta)+\log\frac{\tilde{p}(\theta)}{p(\theta)}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{p}(x)}[\log Z(x,\phi)]

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{p}(\theta,x)}\left[\log\frac{q_{F(x,\phi)}(\theta)}{p(\theta)}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{p}(x)}[\log Z(x,\phi)]-\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{p}(\theta)}\left[\log\tilde{p}(\theta)\right]

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{p}(\theta,x)}\left[\log g_{\phi}(x,\theta)\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{p}(x)}\left[\log\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{p}(\theta^{\prime})}\left[g_{\phi}(x,\theta^{\prime})\right]\right]-\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{p}(\theta)}\left[\log\tilde{p}(\theta)\right]

\displaystyle:=\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\phi)-\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{p}(\theta)}\left[\log\tilde{p}(\theta)\right],

where

\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\phi)=-\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{p}(\theta,x)}\left[\log g_{\phi}(x,\theta)\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{p}(x)}\left[\log\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{p}(\theta^{\prime})}\left[g_{\phi}(x,\theta^{\prime})\right]\right],

(7)

is selected as the loss function,
-\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{p}(\theta)}\left[\log\tilde{p}(\theta)\right]
in the last column is dropped for it is independent of
\phi
. We employ stochastic gradient methods to optimize the loss function in this paper, therefore
\nabla_{\phi}\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\phi)
is of interest. By allowing the interchange of expectation and gradient operator, the gradient of (
7
) is given by

\displaystyle\nabla_{\phi}\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\phi)=-\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{p}(\theta,x)}\left[\nabla_{\phi}\log g_{\phi}(x,\theta)\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{p}(x)}\left[\nabla_{\phi}\log\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{p}(\theta^{\prime})}\left[g_{\phi}(x,\theta^{\prime})\right]\right].

(8)

Notice that (
8
) holds a similar formulation of the gradient use in the optimization procedure of Bayesian experimental design
[
9
,
16
,
34
,
37
]
.
Given any query point
(\theta,x)\sim\tilde{p}(\theta,x)
, we denote the corresponding query of the loss and gradient as

\displaystyle\psi_{\phi}

\displaystyle=-\log g_{\phi}(x,\theta)+\log Z(x,\phi),

(9)

\displaystyle\rho_{\phi}

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{\phi}\log g_{\phi}(x,\theta)+\nabla_{\phi}\log Z(x,\phi),

(10)

so that
\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{p}(\theta,x)}[\psi_{\phi}]=\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\phi)
and
\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{p}(\theta,x)}[\rho_{\phi}]=\nabla_{\phi}\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\phi)
. With the abuse of notation aiming at easing representation, unless otherwise specified in the following paper,
\mathcal{L}(\phi)
and
\nabla_{\phi}\mathcal{L}(\phi)
will be used to represent (
7
) and (
8
) respectively.

Given the intractability of the normalizing constant
Z(x,\phi)=\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{p}(\theta^{\prime})}\left[g_{\phi}(x,\theta^{\prime})\right]
, it remains an obstacle to derive an estimator for the loss function. A simple choice is leveraging its empirical estimator based on
M
samples, which is given by

\displaystyle\hat{Z}_{M}(x,\phi)=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}g_{\phi}(x,\theta^{\prime}_{j}),

(11)

where
\theta^{\prime}_{1},\cdots,\theta^{\prime}_{M}\sim\tilde{p}(\theta^{\prime})
independently,
M
in the subscript denotes the number of the samples used for the estimation of
Z(x,\phi)
. We thus arrive at queries for nested estimators of (
9
) and (
10
) respectively

\displaystyle\psi_{\phi,M}

\displaystyle=-\log g_{\phi}(x,\theta)+\log\hat{Z}_{M}(x,\phi)=\log\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\frac{g_{\phi}(x,\theta^{\prime}_{j})}{g_{\phi}(x,\theta)},

(12)

\displaystyle\rho_{\phi,M}

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{\phi}\log g_{\phi}(x,\theta)+\nabla_{\phi}\log\hat{Z}_{M}(x,\phi).

(13)

Nested estimators for the loss function (
7
) and its gradient (
8
) are then given by the mean of
N
iid copies of their queries

\displaystyle\hat{{\mathcal{L}}}^{\mathrm{Ne}}(\phi)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\psi_{\phi,M}^{(i)}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\frac{g_{\phi}(x_{i},\theta^{\prime}_{ij})}{g_{\phi}(x_{i},\theta_{i})},

(14)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\phi}\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\text{Ne}}(\phi)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\rho^{(i)}_{\phi,M},

(15)

where
\theta_{ij}^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}\tilde{p}(\theta^{\prime})
constitute the inner sample with a size of
M
and
(\theta_{i},x_{i})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}\tilde{p}(\theta,x)
constitute the outer sample with a size of
N
,
\psi^{(i)}_{\phi,M},\ \rho^{(i)}_{\phi,M}
are iid copies of
\psi_{\phi,M}
and
\rho_{\phi,M}
respectively. Due to the nonlinearity of the logarithm, (
14
) is a biased estimator. Using similar arguments in
[
53
]
, it is not difficult to evaluate that the order of variance of the estimator of nested APT
\hat{{\mathcal{L}}}^{\mathrm{Ne}}(\phi)
is of
O(1/N)
, and
O(1/M)
for its bias.

3.1
Basic idea of MLMC

Nested simulation combined with the MLMC method has been studied for other applications
[
18
,
19
,
22
,
30
]
. In this paper, MLMC is used to derive an unbiased estimator for APT. Increasing
M
for
\rho_{\phi,M}
eventually recovers the desired
\rho_{\phi}
, this allows the exchange of expectation and limitation that

\displaystyle\lim_{M\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\rho_{\phi,M}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\rho_{\phi}\right]=\nabla_{\phi}\mathcal{L}(\phi).

(16)

Then we take a monotonically increasing sequence for inner sample size
\{M_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{\infty}
with
M_{\ell}=2^{\ell}M_{0}=2M_{\ell-1}
, then (

) can be reformulated as

\nabla_{\phi}\mathcal{L}(\phi)=\mathbb{E}\left[\rho_{\phi,M_{0}}\right]+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\rho_{\phi,M_{\ell}}-\rho_{\phi,M_{\ell-1}}\right].

(17)

Set
\{\Delta\rho_{\phi,\ell}\}_{\ell=0}^{\infty}
to be a sequence of random variables satisfying

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta\rho_{\phi,0}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\rho_{\phi,M_{0}}\right],\quad\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta\rho_{\phi,\ell}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\rho_{\phi,M_{\ell}}-\rho_{\phi,M_{\ell-1}}\right]\quad(\ell\geq 1),

then (

, i.e.,
\nabla_{\phi}\mathcal{L}(\phi)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta\rho_{\phi,\ell}\right]

In the case of the loss function, we also introduce the similar sequence
\{\Delta\psi_{\phi,\ell}\}_{\ell=0}^{\infty}
satisfying

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta\psi_{\phi,0}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\psi_{\phi,M_{0}}\right],\quad\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta\psi_{\phi,\ell}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\psi_{\phi,M_{\ell}}-\psi_{\phi,M_{\ell-1}}\right]\quad(\ell\geq 1),

(18)

similarly we also have
\mathcal{L}(\phi)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta\psi_{\phi,\ell}\right]

3.1.1
Antithetic construction

Motivated by the prevailing literature on MLMC
[

,

,
19
,

]

\{\Delta\rho_{\phi,\ell}\}_{\ell=0}^{\infty}
with antithetic coupling, leading to a faster rate of convergence for smooth functions. The fundamental concept of antithetic coupling involves the selection of two non-overlapping subsets, each with a size of
M_{\ell-1}
from the

inner samples
\{\theta_{j}^{\prime}\}_{j=1}^{M_{\ell}}
utilized for computing
\rho_{\phi,M_{\ell}}
given in (

\rho_{\phi,M_{\ell-1}}

\rho^{(a)}_{\phi,M_{\ell-1}}
and

\rho^{(a)}_{\phi,M_{\ell-1}}=-\frac{\nabla_{\phi}g_{\phi}(x,\theta)}{g_{\phi}(x,\theta)}+\frac{\nabla g^{(a)}_{\phi,M_{\ell-1}}(x)}{g^{(a)}_{\phi,M_{\ell-1}}(x)},

where

g^{(a)}_{\phi,M_{\ell-1}}(x)=\frac{1}{M_{\ell-1}}\sum_{j=1}^{M_{\ell-1}}g_{\phi}(x,\theta_{j}^{\prime}),\ \nabla g^{(a)}_{\phi,M_{\ell-1}}(x)=\frac{1}{M_{\ell-1}}\sum_{j=1}^{M_{\ell-1}}\nabla_{\phi}g_{\phi}(x,\theta_{j}^{\prime}).

The notations
\rho^{(b)}_{\phi,M_{\ell-1}}

are defined in the similar way by using

Then the antithetic construction of

is then given as

\displaystyle\Delta\rho_{\phi,0}=\rho_{\phi,M_{0}},\quad\Delta\rho_{\phi,\ell}=\rho_{\phi,M_{\ell}}-\frac{1}{2}\left(\rho^{(a)}_{\phi,M_{\ell-1}}+\rho^{(b)}_{\phi,M_{\ell-1}}\right)\quad(\ell\geq 1).

(19)

The antithetic construction of

can be similarly given.

3.2
Unbiased MLMC for APT

3.2.1
RU-MLMC

Recall that both loss function and its gradient can be represented as the summation of the expected value of the random variable sequence, and this topic has been well studied. We introduce a non-negative integer-valued random variable
L
that independent of the two random variable sequences, with its probability mass function
\mathbb{P}(L=\ell)=w_{\ell}
. We then have

\mathbb{E}\left[{\omega_{L}}^{-1}\Delta\rho_{\phi,L}\right]=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[{\omega_{\ell}}^{-1}\Delta\rho_{\phi,\ell}\right]P(L=\ell)=\nabla_{\phi}\mathcal{L}(\phi).

The equivalence between
\nabla_{\phi}\mathcal{L}(\phi)
and the expectation of the query

\displaystyle V_{\mathrm{RU}}={\omega_{L}}^{-1}{\Delta\rho_{\phi,L}}

(20)

leads to an unbiased Monte Carlo estimator of the gradient
\nabla_{\phi}\mathcal{L}(\phi)
.
We similarly define
U_{\mathrm{RU}}={\omega_{L}}^{-1}{\Delta\psi_{\phi,L}}
for the query of the estimation of the loss function
\mathcal{L}(\phi)
. This method is known as randomized unbiased multilevel Monte Carlo (RU-MLMC) method
[
51
]
. In this study,
we take
L
as a geometric distribution
\mathrm{Ge}(p)
with
\omega_{\ell}=(1-p)^{\ell}p
and
p=1-2^{-\alpha}
. To ensure a finite variance and finite expected computational cost for
V_{\mathrm{RU}},U_{\mathrm{RU}}
, it is required that
\alpha\in(1,\min\left(r_{1},r_{2}\right))
. This is further examined with the following theorem.

Theorem 3.5
.

Under the setting of Theorems
3.2
and
3.3
, let
L\sim\mathrm{Ge}(p)
with
p=1-2^{-\alpha}
and
1<\alpha<\min\left(r_{1},r_{2}\right)
, where
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
are from Theorems
3.2
and
3.3
respectively. Then we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left[U_{\mathrm{RU}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{A}{(1-2^{\alpha-r_{1}})(1-2^{-\alpha})},

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left[V_{\mathrm{RU}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{B}{(1-2^{\alpha-r_{2}})(1-2^{-\alpha})},

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cost}_{\mathrm{RU}}

\displaystyle\propto M_{0}\frac{2^{\alpha}-1}{2^{\alpha}-2},

where the constants
A,B
are independent of
\alpha
and
r_{1}
.

Proof.

The proof of this theorem follows in line of Theorem
3.6
.
∎

This theorem indicates that a large
\alpha
leads to less expected total computational burden, but larger variance for the estimators of loss and its gradient.

3.2.2
GRR-MLMC

The Russian roulette (RR) estimator
[
44
]
is also employed to estimate the sum of an infinite series, wherein the evaluation of any term in the series only demands a finite amount of computation. This estimator relies on randomized truncation and assigns a higher weight to each term to accommodate the possibility of not computing them.
The query of the gradient of RR-MLMC estimator is given by

V_{\mathrm{RR}}:=\sum_{j=0}^{L}\frac{\Delta\rho_{\phi,j}}{p_{j}},

(21)

where
p_{j}=\mathbb{P}(L\geq j)=\sum_{\ell=j}^{\infty}w_{\ell}
for
j\geq 0
.
If all
p_{j}>0
, then
V_{\mathrm{RR}}
is unbiased since

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[V_{\mathrm{RR}}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(L=\ell)\sum_{j=0}^{\ell}\frac{\mathbb{E}[\Delta\rho_{\phi,j}]}{p_{j}}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}[\Delta\rho_{\phi,j}]}{p_{j}}\sum_{\ell\geq j}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(L=\ell)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}[\Delta\rho_{\phi,j}]=\nabla_{\phi}\mathcal{L}(\phi).

Motivated by
[
43
]
, in order to trade lower variance with higher cost based on the conventional RR-MLMC, one way is to ensure that the first
{\underline{m}}
terms of the infinite series are always computed, where this
{\underline{m}}
is called the
base level
. In this case, the random index
L
is set to have a lower bound
{\underline{m}}
, i.e.,
\mathbb{P}(L\geq{\underline{m}})=0
, implying
p_{j}=1
for all
j\leq{\underline{m}}
. We call this modified estimator as generalized Russian roulette (GRR) estimator. When
{\underline{m}}=0
, this degenerates to the conventional RR-MLMC. The associated quaery for GRR-MLMC estimator of the gradient is

\displaystyle V_{\mathrm{GRR}}:=\rho_{\phi,M_{{\underline{m}}}}+\sum_{j={\underline{m}}+1}^{L}\frac{\Delta\rho_{\phi,j}}{p_{j}}.

(22)

In this case, the probability mass function of
L
is chosen as

\mathbb{P}(L={\underline{m}})=1-\sum_{\ell>{\underline{m}}}w_{\ell}
and
\mathbb{P}(L=\ell)=w_{\ell}
for
\ell>{\underline{m}}
, where
w_{\ell}=(1-p)^{\ell}p
and
p=1-2^{-\alpha}
. We denote this distribution by
\mathrm{Ge}(p,{\underline{m}})
. This implies that
V_{\mathrm{GRR}}
is also unbiased since all
p_{j}>0
,

Similarly, we define the query for GRR estimator of the loss function:

\displaystyle U_{\mathrm{GRR}}:=\psi_{\phi,M_{{\underline{m}}}}+\sum_{j={\underline{m}}+1}^{L}\frac{\Delta\psi_{\phi,j}}{p_{j}}.

(23)

U_{\mathrm{GRR}}
and the following theorem is established aiming at evaluating their order.

Theorem 3.6
.

Under the setting of Theorems
3.2
and
3.3
, let
L\sim\mathrm{Ge}(p,{\underline{m}})
with
p=1-2^{-\alpha}
and
1<\alpha<\min\left(r_{1},r_{2}\right)
, where
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
are from Theorems
3.2
and
3.3
, and the base level
{\underline{m}}\geq 1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left[U_{\mathrm{GRR}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq A\left(\frac{1}{2^{{\underline{m}}}}+\frac{2^{(\alpha-r_{1})({\underline{m}}+1)}}{1-2^{\alpha-r_{1}}}\right),

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left[V_{\mathrm{GRR}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq B\left(\frac{1}{2^{{\underline{m}}}}+\frac{2^{(\alpha-r_{2})({\underline{m}}+1)}}{1-2^{\alpha-r_{2}}}\right),

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cost}_{\mathrm{GRR}}

\displaystyle\propto M_{0}2^{{\underline{m}}}+M_{0}\frac{2^{(1-\alpha)({\underline{m}}+1)}}{1-2^{(1-\alpha)}},

where the constants

The proof of this theorem is detailed in Appendix

Truncated MLMC for APT

When treating
\nabla_{\phi}\mathcal{L}(\phi)
as the summation of the infinite random variable sequence
\{\Delta\rho_{\phi,\ell}\}_{\ell=0}^{\infty}
, we notice that
\Delta\rho_{\phi,0}=\rho_{\phi,0}
is just the nested APT estimator with a bias of order
O(1/M_{0})
. For
\Delta\rho_{\phi,\ell}
with
\ell\geq 1

\ell

\Delta\rho_{\phi,\ell}
is shown in Figure

We observe that the truncated RU-MLMC method enjoys a more stable loss compared to the original one, indicating that truncation helps reducing variance. Therefore, in order to mitigate the variance of the RU-MLMC, we propose to truncate the distribution of
L
by setting the largest value of
L
as
\overline{m}

]

3.3.1
TGRR-MLMC

TGRR-MLMC stands for the truncated version of GRR-MLMC. Let
{\overline{m}}\geq{\underline{m}}
be a truncated level, and let
L
be a nonnegative
integer-value random index taking values in
\{{\underline{m}},\dots,{\overline{m}}\}
. The query for the estimation of the gradient of TGRR-MLMC is then defined as

\displaystyle V_{\mathrm{TGRR}}=\rho_{\phi,M_{{\underline{m}}}}+\sum_{j={\underline{m}}+1}^{L}\frac{\Delta\rho_{\phi,j}}{p_{j}},

(24)

where
p_{j}=\mathbb{P}(L\geq j)>0
for
{\underline{m}}\leq j\leq{\overline{m}}
.
This estimator is biased:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[V_{\mathrm{TGRR}}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\rho_{\phi,M_{{\underline{m}}}}]+\sum_{\ell={\underline{m}}+1}^{{\overline{m}}}\mathbb{P}(\tilde{L}=\ell)\sum_{j={\underline{m}}+1}^{\ell}\frac{\mathbb{E}[\Delta\rho_{\phi,j}]}{p_{j}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\rho_{\phi,M_{{\underline{m}}}}]+\sum_{j={\underline{m}}+1}^{{\overline{m}}}\frac{\mathbb{E}[\Delta\rho_{\phi,j}]}{p_{j}}\sum_{\ell\geq j}^{{\overline{m}}}\mathbb{P}(\tilde{L}=\ell)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\rho_{\phi,M_{{\underline{m}}}}]+\sum_{j={\underline{m}}+1}^{{\overline{m}}}\mathbb{E}[\Delta\rho_{\phi,j}]=\mathbb{E}[\rho_{\phi,M_{{\overline{m}}}}].

We should note that if
{\overline{m}}={\underline{m}}=m

\overline{m}

Convergence results of SGD

In this section, we analyze the convergence of the methods proposed in this paper with some existing techniques. For simplicity, we only study the widely used SGD with constant step size
\gamma
in this paper. Our result should extend to boarder cases. The parameter update procedure of SGD is presented as

\phi_{t+1}=\phi_{t}-\gamma_{t}\nabla_{\phi}\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\phi_{t}),

leveraging the information of an estimator of the gradient of the loss function at the current state
\phi_{t}
. The gradient estimator for TGRR-MLMC is

\displaystyle\nabla_{\phi}\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\text{TGRR}}(\phi):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}V_{\mathrm{TGRR}}^{(i)},

where
V_{\mathrm{TGRR}}^{(i)}
are iid copies of
V_{\mathrm{TGRR}}
. The gradient estimators for other MLMC methods follows a similar form. The gradient estimator for nested APT is stated previously in (
15
). Since some of them are biased estimators for
\nabla_{\phi}\mathcal{L}(\phi)
, we take the following decomposition

\nabla_{\phi}\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\phi)=\nabla_{\phi}\mathcal{L}(\phi)+b(\phi)+\eta(\phi),

(26)

where
b(\phi)
and
\eta(\phi)
denote the bias and the noise of gradient estimator
\nabla_{\phi}\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\phi)
respectively.

Assumption 4.1
.

There exist constants
U_{b}<\infty
and
U_{\eta}<\infty
such that

\displaystyle\underset{\phi\in\Phi}{\sup}\left\|b(\phi)\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\underset{\phi\in\Phi}{\sup}\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla_{\phi}\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\phi)\right]-\nabla_{\phi}\mathcal{L}(\phi)\right\|^{2}_{2}\leq U_{b},

\displaystyle\underset{\phi\in\Phi}{\sup}\ \mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\eta(\phi)\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\underset{\phi\in\Phi}{\sup}\ \mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla_{\phi}\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\phi)-\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla_{\phi}\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\phi)\right]\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]\leq U_{\eta}.

Following the similar procedure to derive (
45
), we find that the order of
U_{b}
for nested APT is
\mathcal{O}(1/M)
, while
\mathcal{O}(1/(M_{0})^{\overline{m}})
for TGRR. Intuitively speaking, when it is set that
M_{0}^{\overline{m}}=M
and
\underline{m}<\overline{m}
,
M
inner samples are always required for each outer sample in nested APT, while the inner sample size for TGRR-MLMC is determined by the distribution
\mathrm{Ge}(p,\underline{m},\overline{m})
whose maximal output is
M_{0}^{\overline{m}}=M
. Therefore, this truncated MLMC method takes less cost than nested APT to achieve the same level of bias.

We then focus on studying how
U_{b}
and
U_{\eta}
would affect the
optimal gap
at any
t>0
, which is defined as

\displaystyle G_{t}=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{L}(\phi_{t})\right]-\mathcal{L}(\phi^{*}),

(27)

where
\phi^{*}=\underset{\phi\in\Phi}{\arg\min}\,\mathcal{L}(\phi)
. Moving forward, we introduce the following two commonly used assumptions when analyzing the convergence of SGD, as outlined in
[
6
]
.

Assumption 4.2
.

The objective function
\mathcal{L}(\phi)
is differentiable and there exists a constant
K
such that for every
\phi,\phi^{\prime}

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\phi)\leq\mathcal{L}(\phi^{\prime})+\nabla_{\phi}\mathcal{L}(\phi)^{T}(\phi-\phi^{\prime})+\frac{K}{2}\left\|\phi-\phi^{\prime}\right\|^{2}_{2}.

Assumption 4.3
.

The objective function
\mathcal{L}(\phi)
is differentiable and there exists
\mu>0
such that

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla\mathcal{L}(\phi)\right\|_{2}^{2}\geq 2\mu(\mathcal{L}(\phi)-\mathcal{L}(\phi^{*})).

In situations where a fixed number of iterations are exclusively employed for parameter updates, the evaluation of the impact of variance and bias on the upper bound of the optimal gap is undertaken through the application of the theorem presented below, following
[
1
]
.

Theorem 4.4
.

Under Assumptions
4.2
and
4.3
and the constant step size
\gamma\leq\min\left\{1/K,1/\mu\right\}
, for a fixed
T
steps of SGD, the upper bound of the optimal gap (
27
) is given as

\displaystyle G_{T}\leq(1-\gamma\mu)^{T}G_{0}+\frac{1}{2\mu}\left(U_{b}+U_{\eta}\right).

(28)

Since
G_{0}
depends solely on the initialization of
\phi
, our focus lies on the last two terms of (
28
), where
U_{b}
and
U_{\eta}
have the same impact on the optimal gap
G_{T}
. This implies that unbiased methods with large variance may ultimately achieve the same result as those biased ones at the same iteration step
T
. Hence in cases where variance dominates bias, methods with smaller variance are preferable.

On the other hand, when a sufficiently large iteration step
T
is given and proper learning rate
\gamma
is chosen, the upper bound of the optimal gap can be smaller than any given
\epsilon>0
except for the parts of bias, which is examined in the following corollary.

Corollary 4.5
.

Suppose that Assumptions
4.2
and
4.3
are satisfied and
\gamma=\min\left\{1/K,1/\mu,\epsilon\mu/(K\eta)\right\},\,T=\max\left\{(K/\mu)\ln(2G_{0}/\epsilon),K\eta/(\epsilon\mu^{2})\ln(2G_{0}{\epsilon})\right\}
.
Then the optimal gap satisfies
G_{T}\leq\epsilon+U_{b}/(2\mu)
for any
\epsilon>0
.

Proof.

It follows directly from (
28
) by scaling
\gamma
and
T
.
∎

In this case, unbiased methods are always favorable, as they are able to converge to an arbitrarily small neighborhood of 0, while biased methods can only converge to a neighborhood of the
U_{b}
. Moreover, for biased method with
U_{b}\gg U_{\eta}
, the optimal gap for
t_{1}\gg t_{2}
tends to be
G_{t_{1}}\approx G_{t_{2}}
, indicating that increasing the iteration step can be futile in attempting to reduce the optimal gap.

In this section, numerical experiments are conducted to compare the efficiency of two unbiased MLMC methods and a biased MLMC method for three models. To handle the curse of dimensionality, we propose to use low-dimensional summary statistics instead of the full data. However, this introduces additional bias, as detailed in

\displaystyle a\sim U(-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2})

\displaystyle,\quad r_{2}\sim\mathcal{N}(0.1,0.01^{2}),

\displaystyle p=(r_{2}\cos(a)+0.25,r_{2}\sin(a))

[

In this paper, we employ neural spline flows (NSFs)

6
Concluding remarks

We develop a series of efficient nested MLMC methods, which are improvement over the unbiased MLMC methods. The latter suffer from excessive variance at the cost of unbiasedness, which, based on the convergence analysis, severely affects the method’s performance. Through experiments on standard benchmark for likelihood-free estimation and theoretical analysis, we valid the inferior performance of unbiased methods when variance dominates bias. In this paper, our choice for hyper-parameter
\alpha
deviates from the mainstream choice. While
values of
\alpha
closer to
r_{2}
is favored, aiming at cost minimization, given the fact that variance is the major focus, our focus lies on the asymptotic inefficiency by considering both variance and average cost. However, given that a thorough validation for this strategy through ablation experiments is still lacking, its improvement is still unclear. Quasi-Monte Carlo (QMC) and Randomized Quasi-Monte Carlo (RQMC) are well known variance reduction techniques, its application in nested MLMC methods has already been investigated in
[
30
]
. Given the high sensitivity of this problem to variance, using RQMC is likely to be beneficial, and existing results
[
30
]
can be appropriately utilized.
