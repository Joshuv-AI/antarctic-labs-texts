Computational
homogenization of higher-order continua.

Abstract




Abstract.
We introduce a novel computational framework for the multiscale simulation of higher-order continua that allows for the consideration of first-, second- and third-order effects at both micro- and macro-level. In line with classical two-scale approaches, we describe the microstructure via representative volume elements (RVE) that are attached at each integration point of the macroscopic problem. To take account of the extended continuity requirements of independent fields at micro- and macro-level, we discretize both scales via isogeometric analysis (IGA). As a result, we obtain an IGA
2
-method that is conceptually similar to the well-known FE
2
-method. We demonstrate the functionality and accuracy of this novel multiscale method by means of a series of multiscale simulations involving different kinds of higher-order continua.

Keywords
: higher-order gradient material, representative volume element, energetic criteria, consistent linearization, NURBS,
\text{IGA}^{2}
-method, multigrid.

1
Introduction

Whether or not morphological features of a material are visible depends on the observed length scale. While a material may appear perfectly homogeneous at one scale, it may be heterogeneous at another. A typical example for such a
material
is a composite, whose phases are distinguishable only at a small length scale and whose heterogeneous properties are linked to homogeneous properties at a larger scale. In general, the involved length scales are considered
separated
if
their contrast
is sufficiently high.
†
†

†

More strictly speaking, separation of scales is present when the wavelengths of physical fields at the higher scale are very much larger than the dimensions of heterogeneities at the lower scale
(
[
70
]
).

In such cases, it is reasonable to describe the
homogenized
behavior with classical, first-order theories. In contrast to that, when the scales are not clearly separated, the description of the

homogenized
behavior needs to be based on generalized, higher-order theories.

Generalized theories for materials are nowadays well-established. They trace back to the seminal work of
[
14
]
, who investigated the emergence and significance of couple stresses for the modeling of the size-dependent response of materials more than a hundred years ago. In their theory, the Cosserat brothers linked
couple stresses
to the gradient of a microscopic rotation field and classical
force stresses
to the gradient of the macroscopic translation field (i.e., the displacements). In that context, the microscopic rotation field is understood independent from the macroscopic rotation field. An extended theory based on the consideration of both macroscopic translational and macroscopic rotational degrees of freedom was later developed by
[
78
,
79
,
64
]
. We refer to
[
4
]
and
[
22
]
for rigorous expositions of the Cosserats’ couple-stress theory as well as to
[
23
,
24
,
37
]
for further developments and generalizations.

Next to classical couple-stress theories, there exist a number of further approaches to the modeling of size effects in materials. An important branch is given by so-called
strain-gradient theories
, which for linear elastic solids have first been proposed by
[
61
]
. Associated theories are based on the incorporation of higher-order gradients of the displacement field into the material description. We refer to
[
63
]
for further specifications based on the first gradient of strain and to
[
62
]
for an extension involving the second gradient of strain. The interested reader is further referred to
[
35
]
and
[
48
]
for underlying virtual-work and variational principles, respectively, to
[
43
]
for extensions towards fully nonlinear settings, and to
[
83
,
6
,
27
]
for possible numerical implementations. A general overview of gradient-extended continua is available through the monographs of
[
56
,
5
,
9
]
. Next to gradient-theories for elastic materials, there exists a rich theory on gradient-extended models for dissipative solids. In these cases, the gradient extensions are classicaly linked to internal variables like the plastic strains (
[
1
,
28
]
) and the damage field (
[
67
]
). We refer to
[
58
]
for associated variational treatments.

The above mentioned formulations have in common that they incorporate the notion of microstructure (and its size) in a phenomenological way. In contrast to that, microstructural information about morphology and material properties can be accounted for in an explicit manner by means of
homogenization methods
. As in case of phenomenological material modeling, size effects may be incorporated in related schemes, depending on the existence of scale separation. If the considered scales are clearly separated,
classical
or
first-order
homogenization schemes are applicable; if they are not,
generalized
or
higher-order
schemes become necessary.

Associated computational homogenization schemes have been developed in the framework of couple-stress and micromorphic theories by

The present work is devoted to the
multiscale computational homogenization of gradient-extended continua
and unites ingredients of the works of

[
52
]

with respect to the gradient extensions at the homogenized scale, of

[
59
]

with regard to the algorithmic linearization of the macroscopic field equations, and of

[

,
41
]

with regard to spatial discretizations. In contrast to the contribution of

[
52
]
,
which combines a Cauchy continuum at the lower length scale with a gradient-extended continuum at the larger length scale, we will take into account
gradient-extended continua at both scales
. This endeavor poses additional challenges not only for the theoretical treatment, but also for the numerical implementation.

From a theoretical perspective, we are dealing with overall three spatial scales given by (i) a
macroscopic scale
, at which the homogenized, gradient-extended behavior will be obtained through computational homogenization of (ii) a
mesoscopic scale
, at which we assume the presence of representative volume elements (
\cal RVE
), which are themselves characterized by size dependent material response at each mesoscopic material point and thus inherently linked to (iii) a
microscopic scale
, at which we assume the existence of a microstructure that we capture with phenomenological, gradient-extended material models. The latter could be motivated, for example, through the presence of microscopic fibers with spatial extensions and distributions that could still be distinguished from further morphological entities like holes, inclusions, etc. at the level of the
\cal RVE
. We refer the interested reader to

[
73
,
47
]

for associated analytical, numerical and experimental details.

From a numerical perspective, challenges arise because
the gradient extensions come along with the requirement of
C^{1}
-continuous approximations of independent fields at both scales. Such a requirement can be captured in an elegant way by employing
isogeometric analysis
(IGA) in the sense of
[
13
,
41
]
. A further algorithmic feature of the proposed implementation is due to the linearization of the macroscopic boundary value problem. Here, we employ the approach advocated by
[
59
]
, which was originally developed in the context of first-order homogenization. As we will see, the associated gradient extensions result in settings that remind of the linearized structures appearing in the coupled homogenization schemes considered by
[
72
,
46
,
45
]
.

As the present work proposes a computational multiscale method based on numerical discretizations involving isogeometric analysis at two scales, we denote it as IGA
2
-method in analogy to the well-known FE
2
-methods mentioned above. We refer to
[
71
]
for a review of FE
2
-methods and to
[
55
]

for a general overview of computational multiscale techniques. As already mentioned, the motivation behind using IGA instead of classical finite elements is due to the straightforward and elegant implementation of
C^{1}
-continuous independent fields. In case of classical FE methods, the contruction and implementation of higher-order element continuities is usually cumbersome. It could, for example, be realized by the use of Hermite shape functions, which however come with a complex algebraic structure and a high number of degrees of freedom, in particular in three spatial dimensions. Alternative FE approximations are given by mixed and non-conforming methods. While mixed methods can be implemented with standard
C^{0}
-type shape functions, they need to satisfy the inf-sup condition
(
[
12
]
).
Non-conforming finite elements indeed allow for a more or less straightforward numerical implementation at a reasonable amount of degrees of freedom
(
[
77
]
),
still their finite-element function space is not a subspace of the solution space
(
[
16
]
).
In contrast to that, IGA-based schemes do not suffer from such limitations, but allow for an elegant implementation of higher-order continuities.

‡
‡

‡

Alternative schemes with even
C^{\infty}
-continuous interpolations at the microscopic level have been proposed by
[
66
]
and were recently implemented in the framework of so-called FE-FFT methods. As the name suggests, associated schemes combine macroscopic solvers based on finite elements with microscopic solvers based on spectral methods (Fast Fourier Transforms; FFT), see
[
76
,
49
,
36
]
.

As IGA-based multiscale methods have thus far been limited to homogeneous macroscopic problems (
[
3
,
80
]
), we believe that the here proposed IGA
2
-method provides a useful and innovative framework for the modeling of higher-order continua across length scales.

The outline of the paper is as follows. In Section 2 we discuss fundamental concepts of the multiscale modeling of higher-gradient continua. In that consequence, suitable boundary conditions based on an energetically consistent scale transition are derived. In Section 3 we discuss the numerical implementation of the proposed scheme. Here, we put an emphasis on the consistent linearization of the macroscopic field equations and the IGA-based discretization of representative volume elements (RVE). In Section 4 we present a number of benchmark tests to demonstrate the performance and accuracy of the proposed multiscale technique. We close the paper with a summary and a conclusion in Section 5.

2
Preliminaries and problem description

In this section, we present the basic concepts for the homogenization of second- and third-gradient media for the macro- and microcontinuum. Moreover, suitable boundary conditions with respect to energetic criteria for the scale transition are provided. As higher-order tensor notations and operations on them are required, a brief summary is given in Appendix
A

) follows immediately that the geometrical boundaries for a second-order problem are not periodic within the
\mathcal{RVE}
in contrast to a first-order problem. To be specific, the boundary deformation emanating from
\bar{\boldsymbol{F}}
is periodic whereas the deformation emanating from
\bar{\mathfrak{F}}
is not due to the quadratic formulation in
\boldsymbol{X}
. The latter term does not drop out if (
21
), left, is formulated in the total deformation
\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{X})
.

Remark 1
.

Third-gradient medium: The proposed formulation at hand can be extended in a straightforward manner towards a macroscopic third-gradient medium with hyperstress
\bar{\mathbb{P}}
and the conjugate deformation measure
\bar{\mathbb{F}}=\bar{\nabla}^{3}\bar{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}
. The corresponding application of the energetic criterion reads

\frac{1}{V}\int\limits_{\mathcal{RVE}}\bigl(\boldsymbol{P}:\nabla\delta\boldsymbol{\varphi}+\mathfrak{P}\,\vdots\,\nabla^{2}\delta\boldsymbol{\varphi}\bigr)\,\operatorname{d}\!V=\bar{\boldsymbol{P}}:\delta\bar{\boldsymbol{F}}+\bar{\mathfrak{P}}\,\vdots\,\delta\bar{\mathfrak{F}}+\bar{\mathbb{P}}::\delta\bar{\mathbb{F}}\,.

(22)

The mapping of the microscopic position reads

\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{X})=\bar{\boldsymbol{F}}\,\boldsymbol{X}+\frac{1}{2}\,\bar{\mathfrak{F}}:(\boldsymbol{X}\otimes\boldsymbol{X})+\frac{1}{6}\,\bar{\mathbb{F}}\,\vdots\,(\boldsymbol{X}\otimes\boldsymbol{X}\otimes\boldsymbol{X})+\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}}\,.

(23)

Insertion yields the relations

\displaystyle\bar{\boldsymbol{P}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{V}\int\limits_{\mathcal{RVE}}\boldsymbol{P}\,\operatorname{d}\!V,

(24)

\displaystyle\bar{\mathfrak{P}}

\displaystyle={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\underbrace{\frac{1}{V}\int\limits_{\mathcal{RVE}}\boldsymbol{P}\otimes\boldsymbol{X}\,\operatorname{d}\!V}_{\bar{\mathfrak{P}}^{\boldsymbol{P}}}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{V}\int\limits_{\mathcal{RVE}}\mathfrak{P}\,\operatorname{d}\!V}_{\bar{\mathfrak{P}}^{\mathfrak{P}}}},

\displaystyle\bar{\mathbb{P}}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\frac{1}{V}\int\limits_{\mathcal{RVE}}\frac{1}{2}\,\boldsymbol{P}\otimes\boldsymbol{X}\otimes\boldsymbol{X}\,\operatorname{d}\!V}_{\bar{\mathbb{P}}^{\boldsymbol{P}}}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{V}\int\limits_{\mathcal{RVE}}\mathfrak{P}\otimes\boldsymbol{X}\,\operatorname{d}\!V}_{\bar{\mathbb{P}}^{\mathfrak{P}}}\,,

where we have again made use of
\int\limits_{\mathcal{RVE}}\boldsymbol{X}\operatorname{d}\!V=\boldsymbol{0}
, see Appendices
B
to
D
. This yields the set of Dirichlet boundary conditions

\displaystyle\bar{\boldsymbol{F}}\,\boldsymbol{X}+\frac{1}{2}\,\bar{\mathfrak{F}}:(\boldsymbol{X}\otimes\boldsymbol{X})+\frac{1}{6}\,\bar{\mathbb{F}}\,\vdots\,(\boldsymbol{X}\otimes\boldsymbol{X}\otimes\boldsymbol{X})-\boldsymbol{\varphi}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0}\,,

(25)

\displaystyle\bar{\boldsymbol{F}}+\bar{\mathfrak{F}}\,\boldsymbol{X}+\frac{1}{2}\,\bar{\mathbb{F}}\,:\,(\boldsymbol{X}\otimes\boldsymbol{X})-\boldsymbol{F}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0}\,,

where we omit again further discussion on possible (periodic) Neumann conditions. With this at hand, a first-gradient medium within the
\mathcal{RVE}
can be established by removing all terms related to
\mathfrak{P}
. An extension towards a third-gradient medium within the
\mathcal{RVE}
seems plausible, but up to now constitutive equations for this need further investigations. Moreover, we note here, that a typical
\mathcal{RVE}
is in the range of
\upmu\mathrm{m}
, and thus, inhomogeneities in the first Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor are weighted with
\upmu\mathrm{m}^{2}
in
\bar{\mathbb{P}}
, which is often negligible and the reason, why we do not further take this into account here. For further information on scale separation, see Schröder
[
71
]
.

3
Consistent linearization and discretization

For the computation of the macroscopic boundary value problem with attached
mesoscopic

\mathcal{RVE}
s, we introduce here the
\text{IGA}^{2}
-method, analogous to the
\text{FE}^{2}
-method, see Schröder
[
71
]
and references therein. We omit here details on the spline-based discretization of the macroscale within the concept of
IGA
, as numerous papers have already presented this and instead focus on the
mesoscopic

\mathcal{RVE}
, assuming that the discrete macroscopic quantities of the deformations (
\bar{\boldsymbol{F}}
,
\bar{\mathfrak{F}}
) are known at the particular Newton step. Note that higher-order continua at the macroscale require appropriate continuity of the spline based discretization.

Thus, in a first step the macroscopic quantities (
\bar{\boldsymbol{F}}
,
\bar{\mathfrak{F}}
) are transferred to the
mesoscale

\mathcal{RVE}
at every material point, see Figure
2
. After that, the boundary value problem on the
mesoscale
is solved using suitable boundary conditions and the homogenization is performed using volumetric averaged
mesoscopic
quantities as well as the linearization of these quantities. In the last step, the macroscopic boundary value problem is solved and the next Newton iteration starts.

3.1
Linearization of macroscopic stresses and hyperstresses

Since the macroscopic boundary value problem is solved with a Newton-Raphson iteration, we need a consistent linearization of the macroscopic field equations. Therefore, it is necessary to linearize the stresses
\bar{\boldsymbol{P}}
and
\bar{\mathfrak{P}}
, evaluated via the incremental relations:

\Delta\bar{\boldsymbol{P}}:=\frac{\partial\bar{\boldsymbol{P}}}{\partial\bar{\boldsymbol{F}}}:\Delta\bar{\boldsymbol{F}}+\frac{\partial\bar{\boldsymbol{P}}}{\partial\bar{\mathfrak{F}}}\,\vdots\,\Delta\bar{\mathfrak{F}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Delta\bar{\mathfrak{P}}:=\frac{\partial\bar{\mathfrak{P}}}{\partial\bar{\boldsymbol{F}}}:\Delta\bar{\boldsymbol{F}}+\frac{\partial\bar{\mathfrak{P}}}{\partial\bar{\mathfrak{F}}}\,\vdots\,\Delta\bar{\mathfrak{F}}\,.

(26)

However, the macroscopic quantities are given by the averaged
mesoscopic
stresses and hyperstresses, hence
\bar{\boldsymbol{P}}:=\bar{\boldsymbol{P}}(\boldsymbol{P}(\boldsymbol{F},\mathfrak{F}))
and
\bar{\mathfrak{P}}:=\bar{\mathfrak{P}}(\boldsymbol{P}(\boldsymbol{F},\mathfrak{F}),\mathfrak{P}(\boldsymbol{F},\mathfrak{F}))
, see (
17
). Thus we have to use the chain rule for the partial derivative of the macroscopic stresses with respect to the corresponding deformations and end up after some calculations with:

\displaystyle\left[\Delta\bar{\boldsymbol{P}}\right]_{iJ}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{V}\int\limits_{\mathcal{RVE}}\left[\mathbb{C}\right]_{iJsT}\,\operatorname{d}\!V\,\left[\Delta\bar{\boldsymbol{F}}\right]_{sT}

(27)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{V}\int\limits_{\mathcal{RVE}}\bigl(\left[\mathbb{C}\right]_{iJsT}\,\left[\boldsymbol{X}\right]_{U}+\left[\mathsf{D}\right]_{iJsTU}\bigr)\operatorname{d}\!V\,\left[\Delta\bar{\mathfrak{F}}\right]_{sTU}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{V}\int\limits_{\mathcal{RVE}}\bigl(\left[\mathbb{C}\right]_{iJsT}\,\left[\Delta\tilde{\boldsymbol{F}}\right]_{sT}+\left[\mathsf{D}\right]_{iJsTU}\,\left[\Delta\tilde{\mathfrak{F}}\right]_{sTU}\bigr)\operatorname{d}\!V

and

\displaystyle\left[\Delta\bar{\mathfrak{P}}\right]_{iJK}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{V}\int\limits_{\mathcal{RVE}}\bigl(\left[\mathbb{C}\right]_{iJsT}\,\left[\boldsymbol{X}\right]_{K}+\left[\mathsf{E}\right]_{iJKsT}\bigr)\,\operatorname{d}\!V\,\left[\Delta\bar{\boldsymbol{F}}\right]_{sT}

(28)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{V}\int\limits_{\mathcal{RVE}}\bigl(\left[\mathbb{C}\right]_{iJsT}\,\left[\boldsymbol{X}\right]_{K}\,\left[\boldsymbol{X}\right]_{U}+\left[\mathsf{D}\right]_{iJsTU}\,\left[\boldsymbol{X}\right]_{K}\bigr.

\displaystyle+\bigl.\left[\mathsf{E}\right]_{iJKsT}\,\left[\boldsymbol{X}\right]_{U}+\left[\mathtt{G}\right]_{iJKsTU}\bigr)\operatorname{d}\!V\,\left[\Delta\bar{\mathfrak{F}}\right]_{sTU}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{V}\int\limits_{\mathcal{RVE}}\bigl(\left[\mathbb{C}\right]_{iJsT}\,\left[\boldsymbol{X}\right]_{K}+\left[\mathsf{E}\right]_{iJKsT}\bigr)\,\left[\Delta\tilde{\boldsymbol{F}}\right]_{sT}\,\operatorname{d}\!V

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{V}\int\limits_{\mathcal{RVE}}\bigl(\left[\mathsf{D}\right]_{iJsTU}\,\left[\boldsymbol{X}\right]_{K}+\left[\mathtt{G}\right]_{iJKsTU}\bigr)\,\left[\Delta\tilde{\mathfrak{F}}\right]_{sTU}\operatorname{d}\!V\,,

where the derivatives of the stresses are defined by:

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}:=\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{P}}{\partial\boldsymbol{F}}\quad,\quad\mathsf{D}:=\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{P}}{\partial\mathfrak{F}}\quad,\quad\mathsf{E}:=\frac{\partial\mathfrak{P}}{\partial\boldsymbol{F}}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathtt{G}:=\frac{\partial\mathfrak{P}}{\partial\mathfrak{F}}\,,

(29)

see Appendix
E
for more details. It is obvious, that the linearizations of the macroscopic stresses
\bar{\boldsymbol{P}}
and
\bar{\mathfrak{P}}
depend on the sensitivity of the
mesoscopic
fluctuations
\Delta\tilde{\boldsymbol{F}}
and
\Delta\tilde{\mathfrak{F}}
, defined in (
12
). The correlation between these sensitivities and the change of the corresponding macroscopic fields
\Delta\bar{\boldsymbol{F}}
and
\Delta\bar{\mathfrak{F}}
can be done in the discrete setting by linearization of the virtual work of the
mesoscopic
boundary value problem in the solution point, as shown next.

3.2
Linearization of
mesoscopic
boundary value problem

The relationship between these sensitivities and the macroscopic fields follows from the
mesoscopic
boundary value problem. With regard to (
14
) and assuming that
\delta\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}}=\boldsymbol{0}
holds on the whole boundary, we obtain

G:=\int\limits_{\mathcal{RVE}}\left(\boldsymbol{P}:\delta\tilde{\boldsymbol{F}}+\mathfrak{P}\,\vdots\,\delta\tilde{\mathfrak{F}}\right)\,\operatorname{d}\!V\,.

(30)

Solving the problem such that
G=0
, it follows immediately that
\Delta G=0
. Hence, the linearization in the equilibrium state reads

\Delta G:=\int\limits_{\mathcal{RVE}}\left(\delta\tilde{\boldsymbol{F}}:\left[\mathbb{C}:\Delta\boldsymbol{F}+\mathsf{D}\,\vdots\,\Delta\mathfrak{F}\right]+\delta\tilde{\mathfrak{F}}\,\vdots\,\left[\mathsf{E}:\Delta\boldsymbol{F}+\mathtt{G}\,\vdots\,\Delta\mathfrak{F}\right]\right)\,\operatorname{d}\!V=0\,,

(31)

where

\Delta\boldsymbol{F}=\Delta\bar{\boldsymbol{F}}+\Delta\bar{\mathfrak{F}}\,\boldsymbol{X}+\Delta\tilde{\boldsymbol{F}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Delta\mathfrak{F}=\Delta\bar{\mathfrak{F}}+\Delta\tilde{\mathfrak{F}}\,.

(32)

This can be evaluated in the discrete setting, as will be shown next.

3.3
Mesoscopic
finite element approximation

Next, we have to approximate the fluctuation field, the virtual and the incremental fluctuation fields:

\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\scriptstyle\mathrm{h}}=\sum\limits_{A\in\mathcal{I}}R^{A}\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{q}}^{A}\quad,\quad\delta\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\scriptstyle\mathrm{h}}=\sum\limits_{A\in\mathcal{I}}R^{A}\,\delta\tilde{\boldsymbol{q}}^{A}\quad\text{and}\quad\Delta\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\scriptstyle\mathrm{h}}=\sum\limits_{A\in\mathcal{I}}R^{A}\,\Delta\tilde{\boldsymbol{q}}^{A}\,,

(33)

where
R^{A}:\mathcal{RVE}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are B-Spline
¶
¶

¶

B-Splines are used without loss of generality, NURBS can also be applied if necessary.
based shape functions of order
p
with associated control points
A\in\mathcal{I}={1,\ldots,m}
with the overall number of control points
m
. Furthermore,
\left[\tilde{\boldsymbol{q}}^{A},\,\delta\tilde{\boldsymbol{q}}^{A},\,\Delta\tilde{\boldsymbol{q}}^{A}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. So, the deformation tensors lead to the approximation

\tilde{\boldsymbol{F}}^{\scriptstyle\mathrm{h}}=\sum\limits_{A\in\mathcal{I}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{q}}^{A}\otimes\nabla R^{A}\quad\text{and}\quad\tilde{\mathfrak{F}}^{\scriptstyle\mathrm{h}}=\sum\limits_{A\in\mathcal{I}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{q}}^{A}\otimes\nabla^{2}R^{A}\,,

(34)

which are given analogously for the virtual (
\delta\tilde{\boldsymbol{F}}^{\scriptstyle\mathrm{h}}
,
\delta\tilde{\mathfrak{F}}^{\scriptstyle\mathrm{h}}
) and the incremental (
\Delta\tilde{\boldsymbol{F}}^{\scriptstyle\mathrm{h}}
,
\Delta\tilde{\mathfrak{F}}^{\scriptstyle\mathrm{h}}
) deformation tensors. Note that we can also discretize the displacement field
\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{X})
using (
11
) as well.

For the boundary conditions we first introduce Dirichlet conditions as presented in (
20
). For the implementation of a first-order mesoscale continuum is straightforward, as we only have to deal with linear conditions in
\boldsymbol{X}
. Using open knot vectors, which are interpolatory at the boundaries, the control points of the spline has to be distributed linearly along the boundaries of the
\mathcal{RVE}
. For higher-order problems, we obtain quadratic (second-order formulations) and cubic (third-order formulations) boundaries in
\boldsymbol{X}
. Therefore, we make use of a least-square optimization for the ease of implementation. However, the problem itself can be solved exactly, i.e. quadratic
or higher order
splines can reproduce a quadratic boundary, c.f.
[
15
]
. Introducing a set of evaluation points
\hat{\boldsymbol{q}}_{i}
along the boundary and a set of control points
\tilde{\boldsymbol{q}}_{j}
for the splines-based discretization of the discrete boundary
\partial\mathcal{RVE}^{{}^{\scriptstyle\mathrm{h}}}
, the least-square problem reads

\{\tilde{\boldsymbol{q}}_{j}\}=\underbrace{\text{min}}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{q}}_{i}\in\partial\mathcal{RVE}^{{}^{\scriptstyle\mathrm{h}}}}\|\hat{\boldsymbol{q}}_{i}-\sum\limits_{j}R^{j}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i})\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{q}}^{j}\|.

(35)

Note, that
\hat{\boldsymbol{q}}_{i}=\bar{\boldsymbol{F}}\,{\boldsymbol{q}}_{i}+\frac{1}{2}\,\bar{\mathfrak{F}}:({\boldsymbol{q}}_{i}\otimes{\boldsymbol{q}}_{i})\,
and
\nabla\hat{\boldsymbol{q}}_{i}=\bar{\boldsymbol{F}}+\bar{\mathfrak{F}}\,{\boldsymbol{q}}_{i}\,
, with the position of the evaluation point in the reference configuration
{\boldsymbol{q}}_{i}
.
We refer to the textbook
[
15
]
and the discussion therein on the enforcement of Dirichlet conditions for further information on the evaluation of the least-square problem.
For second-order boundaries, the least-square problem is expanded by the constraint
\nabla\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}}(\boldsymbol{X})=\boldsymbol{0}
on all surfaces to

\{\tilde{\boldsymbol{q}}_{j}\}=\underbrace{\text{min}}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{q}}_{i}\in\partial\mathcal{RVE}^{{}^{\scriptstyle\mathrm{h}}}}\begin{Vmatrix}\hat{\boldsymbol{q}}_{i}-\sum\limits_{j}R^{j}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i})\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{q}}^{j}\\
\nabla\hat{\boldsymbol{q}}_{i}-\sum\limits_{j}\nabla R^{j}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i})\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{q}}^{j}\end{Vmatrix}.

(36)

For periodic boundary conditions, we have to ensure that (
21
), left, is valid.
For general higher-order domain decomposition problems using non-conforming meshes, we refer to our previous developments in
[
19
,
20
]
, applied here on conforming meshes.
For the ease of implementation, we note that a least-square optimization using

\{\tilde{\boldsymbol{q}}_{j}^{-}\}=\underbrace{\text{min}}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{q}}_{i}^{-}\in\partial\mathcal{RVE}^{{}^{\scriptstyle\mathrm{h}}}}\begin{Vmatrix}\sum\limits_{k}R^{k}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}^{+})\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{q}}^{k}-\sum\limits_{j}R^{j}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}^{-})\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{q}}^{j}+\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{q}}_{i}^{+}-\hat{\boldsymbol{q}}_{i}^{-}\right)\\
\sum\limits_{k}\nabla R^{k}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}^{+})\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{q}}^{k}-\sum\limits_{j}\nabla R^{j}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}^{-})\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{q}}^{j}+\left(\nabla\hat{\boldsymbol{q}}_{i}^{+}-\nabla\hat{\boldsymbol{q}}_{i}^{-}\right)\end{Vmatrix}\,,

(37)

can also be applied, leaving a nodal dependency in the form
\tilde{\boldsymbol{q}}_{j}^{-}:=\tilde{\boldsymbol{q}}_{j}^{-}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{q}}_{j}^{+})
for the set of opposing evaluation points
\{\boldsymbol{q}_{i}^{+},\boldsymbol{q}_{i}^{-}\}
.

Next, we can establish a relationship between the mesoscopic sensitivities and the change of corresponding macroscopic fields. For this, we discretize the last two sections in reverse order and insert the approximations in a first step in the equilibrium state of the mesoscopic boundary value problem (
31
)

\displaystyle\Delta G^{\scriptstyle\mathrm{h}}:=

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathcal{RVE}^{\scriptstyle\mathrm{h}}}\delta\tilde{\boldsymbol{F}}^{\scriptstyle\mathrm{h}}:\left[\mathbb{C}^{\scriptstyle\mathrm{h}}:\left(\Delta\bar{\boldsymbol{F}}+\Delta\bar{\mathfrak{F}}\,\boldsymbol{X}^{\scriptstyle\mathrm{h}}+\Delta\tilde{\boldsymbol{F}}^{\scriptstyle\mathrm{h}}\right)+\mathsf{D}^{\scriptstyle\mathrm{h}}\,\vdots\,\left(\Delta\bar{\mathfrak{F}}+\Delta\tilde{\mathfrak{F}}^{\scriptstyle\mathrm{h}}\right)\right]\,\operatorname{d}\!V

(38)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathcal{RVE}^{\scriptstyle\mathrm{h}}}\delta\tilde{\mathfrak{F}}^{\scriptstyle\mathrm{h}}\,\vdots\,\left[\mathsf{E}^{\scriptstyle\mathrm{h}}:\left(\Delta\bar{\boldsymbol{F}}+\Delta\bar{\mathfrak{F}}\,\boldsymbol{X}^{\scriptstyle\mathrm{h}}+\Delta\tilde{\boldsymbol{F}}^{\scriptstyle\mathrm{h}}\right)+\mathtt{G}^{\scriptstyle\mathrm{h}}\,\vdots\,\left(\Delta\bar{\mathfrak{F}}+\Delta\tilde{\mathfrak{F}}^{\scriptstyle\mathrm{h}}\right)\right]\,\operatorname{d}\!V=0\,,

where the discrete derivatives of the stresses are defined by

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}^{\scriptstyle\mathrm{h}}:=\mathbb{C}\left(\boldsymbol{F}^{\scriptstyle\mathrm{h}},\,\mathfrak{F}^{\scriptstyle\mathrm{h}}\right)\,,\,\mathsf{D}^{\scriptstyle\mathrm{h}}:=\mathsf{D}\left(\boldsymbol{F}^{\scriptstyle\mathrm{h}},\,\mathfrak{F}^{\scriptstyle\mathrm{h}}\right)\,,\,\mathsf{E}^{\scriptstyle\mathrm{h}}:=\mathsf{E}\left(\boldsymbol{F}^{\scriptstyle\mathrm{h}},\,\mathfrak{F}^{\scriptstyle\mathrm{h}}\right)\,\text{and}\,\mathtt{G}^{\scriptstyle\mathrm{h}}:=\mathtt{G}\left(\boldsymbol{F}^{\scriptstyle\mathrm{h}},\,\mathfrak{F}^{\scriptstyle\mathrm{h}}\right)\,.

(39)

F

\left[\Delta\tilde{\boldsymbol{q}}\right]^{B}_{s}=-\left(\left[\boldsymbol{K}\right]^{AB}_{ls}\right)^{-1}\,\left(\left[\mathfrak{L}\right]^{A}_{lrT}\,\left[\Delta\bar{\boldsymbol{F}}\right]_{rT}+\left[\mathbb{M}\right]^{A}_{lrTU}\,\left[\Delta\bar{\mathfrak{F}}\right]_{rTU}\right)\,.

(40)

Here,
\boldsymbol{K}
,
\mathfrak{L}
and
\mathbb{M}

In a second step, we discretize the macroscopic stresses (

\displaystyle\left[\Delta\bar{\boldsymbol{P}}\right]^{\scriptstyle\mathrm{h}}_{iJ}=

5
Conclusions

In this work, we could demonstrate a generalization for the numerical homogenization of higher-order strain gradient materials. This approach allows to homogenize first- and second-gradient materials on the
mesoscale, containing representative quantities of the microstructure,
towards second- and third-gradient materials on the macroscale. Suitable Dirichlet and periodic boundary conditions have been applied on the
mesoscale
to ensure an energetic consistent formulation, analogously to the Hill-Mandel criterion for first-gradient materials.

On both scales, the IGA concept using NURBS based shape functions has proven to be very well suited for these kind of formulations. Hence, we could implement an IGA
2
-method and demonstrate the accuracy even for highly anisotropic strain gradient materials on the
mesoscale
. Eventually, we could derive a generalized framework for a consistent linearization of the macroscale values. The Newton-Raphson iteration for this highly nonlinear problem could be improved by calculating nested meshes on the micro- and the macroscale. With this framework at hand, novel computational investigations and predictions
of
the constitutive relations of materials with specific microstructures as now widely used in additive manufacturing are feasible.

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\mathcal{RVE}}\underbrace{\left(\nabla\cdot{\boldsymbol{I}}\right)}_{\boldsymbol{0}}\cdot\boldsymbol{a}\,\operatorname{d}\!V+\boldsymbol{I}:\int\limits_{\mathcal{RVE}}\nabla\boldsymbol{a}\,\operatorname{d}\!V\,,

or for a tensor
\boldsymbol{A}
and the unit tensor
\boldsymbol{I}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\mathcal{RVE}}\nabla\cdot(\boldsymbol{A}\,\boldsymbol{I})\,\operatorname{d}\!V\,,

(64)

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\partial\mathcal{RVE}}\boldsymbol{A}\otimes\boldsymbol{N}\,\operatorname{d}\!A:\boldsymbol{I}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\mathcal{RVE}}\nabla\boldsymbol{A}\,\operatorname{d}\!V:\boldsymbol{I}+\int\limits_{\mathcal{RVE}}\boldsymbol{A}\,\underbrace{\left(\nabla\cdot{\boldsymbol{I}}\right)}_{\boldsymbol{0}}\,\operatorname{d}\!V\,,

respectively.

Appendix C
Macroscopic stresses

For the derivation of the macroscopic stresses we use the partial integration and the Gaussian integral theorem for the integral of the left side of the energetic criterion (

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathcal{RVE}}\left(\boldsymbol{P}:\nabla\delta\boldsymbol{\varphi}+\mathfrak{P}\,\vdots\,\nabla^{2}\delta\boldsymbol{\varphi}\right)\,\operatorname{d}\!V

The macroscopic stresses are defined in terms of surface integrals since macroscopic values defined by volume integrals could lead to nonphysical results, see Schröder & Keip
[

. Inserting the variations of the material points

in the last equation, we can split the integral into three parts depending on the variation of the macroscopic deformation gradient
\bar{\boldsymbol{F}}
, the second gradient
\bar{\mathfrak{F}}
and the third gradient
\bar{\mathbb{F}}

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathcal{RVE}}\left(\boldsymbol{P}:\nabla\delta\boldsymbol{\varphi}+\mathfrak{P}\,\vdots\,\nabla^{2}\delta\boldsymbol{\varphi}\right)\operatorname{d}\!V

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\partial\mathcal{RVE}}\left(\mathfrak{P}\,\boldsymbol{N}+\left[\boldsymbol{P}-\nabla\cdot\mathfrak{P}\right]\,\boldsymbol{N}\otimes\boldsymbol{X}\right)\,\operatorname{d}\!A:\delta\bar{\boldsymbol{F}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\partial\mathcal{RVE}}\left(\mathfrak{P}\,\boldsymbol{N}\otimes\boldsymbol{X}+\frac{1}{2}\,\left[\boldsymbol{P}-\nabla\cdot\mathfrak{P}\right]\,\boldsymbol{N}\otimes\boldsymbol{X}\otimes\boldsymbol{X}\right)\,\operatorname{d}\!A\,\vdots\,\delta\bar{\mathfrak{F}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\partial\mathcal{RVE}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\,\mathfrak{P}\,\boldsymbol{N}\otimes\boldsymbol{X}\otimes\boldsymbol{X}+\frac{1}{6}\,\left[\boldsymbol{P}-\nabla\cdot\mathfrak{P}\right]\,\boldsymbol{N}\otimes\boldsymbol{X}\otimes\boldsymbol{X}\otimes\boldsymbol{X}\right)\,\operatorname{d}\!A\,::\,\delta\bar{\mathbb{F}}

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathcal{RVE}}\left(\boldsymbol{P}:\nabla\delta\boldsymbol{\varphi}+\mathfrak{P}\,\vdots\,\nabla^{2}\delta\boldsymbol{\varphi}\right)\operatorname{d}\!V

(70)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathcal{RVE}}\left(\nabla\cdot\mathfrak{P}+\left[\nabla\cdot\left(\boldsymbol{X}\otimes\left[\boldsymbol{P}-\nabla\cdot\mathfrak{P}\right]\right)\right]^{\scriptstyle\mathrm{T}\mathrm{}}\right)\,\operatorname{d}\!V:\delta\bar{\boldsymbol{F}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathcal{RVE}}\left(\left[\nabla\cdot\left(\boldsymbol{X}\otimes\mathfrak{P}\right)\right]^{\scriptstyle\mathrm{T}\mathrm{1}}+\frac{1}{2}\,\left[\nabla\cdot\left(\boldsymbol{X}\otimes\boldsymbol{X}\otimes\left[\boldsymbol{P}-\nabla\cdot\mathfrak{P}\right]\right)\right]^{\scriptstyle\mathrm{T}\mathrm{2}}\right)\,\operatorname{d}\!V\,\vdots\,\delta\bar{\mathfrak{F}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathcal{RVE}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\,\left[\nabla\cdot\left(\boldsymbol{X}\otimes\boldsymbol{X}\otimes\mathfrak{P}\right)\right]^{\scriptstyle\mathrm{T}\mathrm{2}}+\frac{1}{6}\,\left[\nabla\cdot\left(\boldsymbol{X}\otimes\boldsymbol{X}\otimes\boldsymbol{X}\otimes\left[\boldsymbol{P}-\nabla\cdot\mathfrak{P}\right]\right)\right]^{\scriptstyle\mathrm{T}\mathrm{3}}\right)\,\operatorname{d}\!V::\delta\bar{\mathbb{F}}
