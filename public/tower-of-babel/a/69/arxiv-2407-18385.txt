Combinatorial transfer: a new method for constructing infinite families of nonabelian difference sets, partial difference sets, and relative difference sets
Eric Swartz, James A. Davis, John Polhill, Ken W. Smith

Abstract
. For nearly a century, mathematicians have been developing techniques for constructing abelian automorphism groups of combinatorial objects, and, conversely, constructing combinatorial objects from abelian groups. While abelian groups are a natural place to start, recent computational evidence strongly indicates that the vast majority of transitive automorphism groups of combinatorial objects are nonabelian. This observation is the guiding motivation for this paper. We propose a new method for constructing nonabelian automorphism groups of combinatorial objects, which could be called the combinatorial transfer method , and we demonstrate its power by finding (1) the first infinite families of nonabelian Denniston partial difference sets (including nonabelian Denniston PDSs of odd order), (2) the first infinite family of Spence difference sets in groups with a Sylow 3-subgroup that is non-normal and not elementary abelian, (3) the first infinite families of McFarland difference sets in groups with a Sylow p p -subgroup that is non-normal and is not elementary abelian, (4) new infinite families of partial difference sets in nonabelian p p -groups with large exponent, (5) an infinite family of semiregular relative difference sets whose forbidden subgroup is nonabelian, and (6) a converse to Dillon’s Dihedral Trick in the PDS setting. We hope this paper will lead to more techniques to explore this largely unexplored topic.



1.
Introduction

A subset
D
of a finite group
G
is called a
(v,k,\lambda)
-difference set
(DS) if
|G|=v
,
|D|=k
, and, for all
g\in G\backslash\{1\}
, we can express
g
in exactly
\lambda
ways as
d_{1}d_{2}^{-1}
, where
d_{1},d_{2}\in D
. A subset
D
of
G
is called a
(v,k.\lambda,\mu)
-partial difference set
(PDS) if
|G|=v
,
|D|=k
,
g\in D
can be expressed as
d_{1}d_{2}^{-1}
with
d_{1},d_{2}\in D
in exactly
\lambda
different ways, and
g\in G\backslash(D\cup\{1\})
can be expressed as
d_{1}d_{2}^{-1}
with
d_{1},d_{2}\in D
in exactly
\mu
different ways.

Difference sets and partial difference sets have proven to be extremely useful in applications. Difference sets have found uses, for instance, in coding theory
[
38
,
39
]
and imaging with coded masks
[
32
]
; see
[
31
]
for the proceedings of a NATO conference dedicated to such applications. Similarly, partial difference sets have found applications in coding theory; see, for instance,
[
25
, Section 8]
and
[
35
]
. For more information about DSs and PDSs, see
[
29
]
and
[
25
]
, respectively.

The study of difference sets in (noncyclic) abelian groups was initiated by the seminal works of McFarland
[
28
]
and Turyn
[
36
]
in the 1960s, and we now have a deep understanding of how to construct difference sets in that context. However, computational evidence indicates that difference sets in nonabelian groups will dwarf the number of difference sets in abelian groups (as noted in
[
30
]
, of the 330159 distinct
(64,28,12)
-DSs, there are 105269 nonisomorphic symmetric designs, of which only 748 arise from an abelian group), and there has been relatively little done to develop techniques that will work in that context. (Beyond sporadic examples, see
[
8
,
13
,
14
,
28
,
33
]
for the known examples of DSs in nonabelian groups and
[
7
,
15
,
16
,
30
,
34
]
for the known examples of PDSs in nonabelian groups.) This paper develops a technique that has so far only been used in an ad hoc way into a more general theory. Our hope is that it will motivate further exploration in this vast largely unexplored area.

A method that has been used for the construction of new (P)DSs, which could be called the
combinatorial transfer method
, is the following:

(1)

take a known example of a (P)DS
D
in a group
G
;

(2)

construct a related combinatorial object
\Gamma
, such as a symmetric design or (possibly directed) Cayley graph;

(3)

determine the full automorphism group
{\rm Aut}(\Gamma)
of
\Gamma
; and, finally,

(4)

search
{\rm Aut}(\Gamma)
for subgroups with the appropriate regular action on
\Gamma
.

The combinatorial transfer method is remarkably effective for known, “small” examples, and it has yielded many new examples of (P)DSs (see, for example,
[
22
]
). On the other hand, current implementations of this process have significant drawbacks. For one thing, the vast majority of known examples of (P)DSs come from a purely algebraic construction. Once the related combinatorial object
\Gamma
is constructed, we often completely forget about the algebraic construction of the (P)DS when determining the full automorphism group of
\Gamma
. While at the very least inefficient, this also makes the method extremely ad hoc, unless the associated combinatorial object
\Gamma
itself independently has an algebraic structure, such as being related to a bilinear or quadratic form; see, for instance
[
7
, Section 3]
,
[
15
]
,
[
16
]
, and
[
30
, Theorem 4.10]
. The success of these recent constructions indicates that we should not be ignoring the algebraic structure that already exists.

The goal here is to take advantage of the power of the combinatorial transfer method to create new, infinite families of examples without “forgetting” the original construction of the (P)DS. The following result is our main method of combinatorial transfer. The idea is roughly as follows: given a (P)DS
D
in a group
G
, if
{\rm Aut}(G)_{D}
is the subgroup of automorphisms of
G
that fix
D
setwise, we prove that a new group
\mathcal{G}\leqslant{\rm Aut}(G)_{D}\ltimes G
contains a (P)DS with the same parameters as
D
if it satisfies certain conditions. Since many (P)DSs are constructed algebraically using (for example) cosets of subgroups or “hyperplanes” (when a group is viewed as a vector space), it is often possible to “arrange” these coset representatives and hyperplanes in such a way to guarantee the existence of such a group
\mathcal{G}
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
G
be a group containing a (P)DS
D
and let
{\rm Aut}(G)_{D}
be the subgroup of automorphisms of
G
that stabilizes
D
setwise. Let
\mathcal{G}\leqslant{\rm Aut}(G)_{D}\ltimes G
be a subgroup satisfying:

(i)

|\mathcal{G}|=|G|
,

(ii)

1\times X=(1\times G)\cap\mathcal{G}
is a normal subgroup of
G
and
\mathcal{G}
, and

(iii)

the action of
\mathcal{G}
on
[G:X]
, the set of right cosets of
X
in
G
, given by

\left(Xh\right)^{(\phi,g)}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}X^{\phi}h^{\phi}g=Xh^{\phi}g

is transitive on
[G:X]
.

Then,
\mathcal{G}
contains a (P)DS
\mathcal{D}
with the same parameters as
D
.

We prove a similar result for relative difference sets. Recall that
R
is an
(m,u,k,\lambda)
-relative difference set
(RDS) in
G
(relative to the subgroup
U\leqslant G
of order

) provided
|R|=k
,
|G|=mu
, and the set

\{r_{1}r_{2}^{-1}:r_{1}\neq r_{2}\in R\}

contains each element of

exactly
\lambda
times and contains no element of
U
. The subgroup

is called the
forbidden subgroup
with respect to

. Much like DSs and PDSs, RDSs have been useful in applications, such as the construction of mutually unbiased bases in
\mathbb{C}^{n}

[
19
]
, which have applications to quantum information processing.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
G
be a group containing an RDS
R
with forbidden subgroup
U
and let
{\rm Aut}(G)_{R}
be the subgroup of automorphisms of
G
that stabilizes
R
setwise. Let
\mathcal{G}\leqslant{\rm Aut}(G)_{R}\ltimes G
be a subgroup satisfying:

(i)

|\mathcal{G}|=|G|
,

(ii)

1\times X\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}(1\times G)\cap\mathcal{G}
is a normal subgroup of
G
and
\mathcal{G}
, and

(iii)

the action of
\mathcal{G}
on
[G:X]
, the set of right cosets of
X
in
G
, given by

\left(Xh\right)^{(\phi,g)}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}X^{\phi}h^{\phi}g=Xh^{\phi}g

is transitive on
[G:X]
.

Then,
\mathcal{G}
contains a RDS
\mathcal{R}
with the same parameters as
R
with respect to the forbidden subgroup

\mathcal{U}=\{(\phi,g)\in\mathcal{G}:g\in U\}.

The proofs of Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
are given in Section
2
. We are then able to use Theorems these results to prove the existence of DSs, PDSs, and RDSs in many nonabelian settings, all of which are new (with the exception of some constructed in Sections

and

), including the following:

(1)

We prove a converse to Dillon’s Dihedral Trick
[

in the PDS setting: whenever there exists a regular PDS (respectively, reversible DS) in an abelian group

with index

subgroup

, there exists a regular PDS (respectively, reversible DS) in the generalized dihedral extension of

. In particular, as a corollary, we prove that the existence of a reversible DS in an abelian group

as a subgroup of index

(Section

(2)

We prove that, whenever

(3)

We prove the existence of an infinite family of DSs with Spence parameters in groups with a non-normal, nonabelian Sylow

(4)

We prove the existence of two infinite families of nonabelian

, where

(5)

We construct new examples of DSs with McFarland parameters in nonabelian groups that have non-normal, nonabelian Sylow

(6)

We construct new examples of RDSs in nonabelian

Finally, in Section

2.
Proofs of Theorems
1.1
and
1.2

This section is dedicated to the proofs of Theorem
1.1
and
1.2
(which are quite similar).

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

We will assume that the group
G
contains a
(v,k,\lambda)
-DS (respectively,
(v,k,\lambda,\mu)
-PDS)
D
. We can construct a (possibly directed, possibly containing loops) Cayley graph
\Gamma\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}{\rm Cay}(G,D)
, and we may view
G
as a subgroup of
A\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}{\rm Aut}(\Gamma)
, where
G
acts regularly on the vertices of
\Gamma
; that is,
|V(\Gamma)|=|G|
, and, for (base vertex)
\alpha\in V(\Gamma)
,
\alpha^{G}=V(\Gamma)
. (See, for example,
[
4
, 10.14 Proposition]
for the equivalence between the existence of a regular PDS
D
in
G
and a
strongly regular Cayley graph

{\rm Cay}(G,D)
.)

We define
A_{\alpha}
to be the stabilizer of
\alpha
in
A
; note that
A_{\alpha}\cap G=\{1\}
. By the Frattini Argument, we have

A=A_{\alpha}G;

indeed, since
G
is transitive on
V(\Gamma)
, for any
a\in A
, there exists a unique
g\in G
such that
\alpha^{g}=\alpha^{a}
. Thus,
\alpha^{ag^{-1}}=\alpha
, meaning
ag^{-1}\in A_{\alpha}
and
a\in A_{\alpha}G
, as desired.

Since
\alpha
is our base vertex of
\Gamma
, we may identify the vertices in
V(\Gamma)
with elements of
G
in the natural way: if
\beta=\alpha^{g}
for
g\in G
, then we identify
\beta
with
g
. Using this identification, we may identify
{\rm Aut}(G)_{D}
naturally with a subgroup of
A_{\alpha}
: for
\phi\in{\rm Aut}(G)_{D}
, we define

(\alpha^{g})^{\phi}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\alpha^{g^{\phi}}.

Indeed,
\alpha^{g}\sim\alpha^{h}
if and only if
gh^{-1}\in D
(by the definition of our Cayley graph) if and only
(gh^{-1})^{\phi}=g^{\phi}\left(h^{\phi}\right)^{-1}\in D
(since
\phi\in{\rm Aut}(G)_{D}
) if and only if
\alpha^{g^{\phi}}\sim\alpha^{h^{\phi}}
(by the definition of our Cayley graph), and so defining
(\alpha^{g})^{\phi}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\alpha^{g^{\phi}}
does identify the elements of
{\rm Aut}(G)_{D}
with elements of
A
. Furthermore, for any
\phi\in{\rm Aut}(G)_{D}
, since
1_{G}^{\phi}=1_{G}
, we have

\alpha^{\phi}=(\alpha^{1_{G}})^{\phi}=\alpha^{1_{G}^{\phi}}=\alpha^{1_{G}}=\alpha,

and so
{\rm Aut}(G)_{D}
is in fact identified with a subgroup of
A_{\alpha}
. This discussion shows that we may indeed identify
{\rm Aut}(G)_{D}\ltimes G
as a subgroup of
A
in a natural way and will henceforth view
{\rm Aut}(G)_{D}\ltimes G\leqslant A
.

Now, let
\mathcal{G}
be a subgroup of
{\rm Aut}(G)_{D}\ltimes G
with
|\mathcal{G}|=|G|
. Since
|\mathcal{G}|=|V(\Gamma)|
, if we can show that
\mathcal{G}
is transitive on
V(\Gamma)
, then we can identify
\Gamma
as a (possibly directed, possibly containing loops) Cayley graph
{\rm Cay}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{D})
, where

\mathcal{D}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\{h\in\mathcal{G}:\alpha^{h}\sim\alpha\}.

Note that, since
\alpha^{(\phi,g)}=(\alpha^{\phi})^{g}=\alpha^{g}
for all
\phi\in{\rm Aut}(G)_{D}
,
g\in G
, it follows that

\mathcal{D}=\{(\phi,d)\in\mathcal{G}:d\in D\}.

Since the defining properties of being a DS (respectively, PDS) can be expressed entirely in terms of (directed) walks on the (possibly directed, possibly containing loops) graph
\Gamma
, this implies that
\mathcal{D}
is a DS (respectively, PDS) of
\mathcal{G}
with the same parameters as
D
.

Let
1\times X\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}(1\times G)\cap\mathcal{G}
, and assume
1\times X
is normal in both
1\times G
and
\mathcal{G}
. In particular, for any
(\phi,g)\in\mathcal{G}
and any
(1,x)\in 1\times X
, we have

(\phi,g)^{-1}(1,x)(\phi,g)=(1,g^{-1}x^{\phi}g)\in X.

Because
X\lhd G
, this implies that
X
is
\phi
-invariant for any
\phi
such that
(\phi,g)\in\mathcal{G}
. Now,
X\leqslant G
, so
X
acts semiregularly on
V(\Gamma)
, i.e.,
X
has
|G:X|
orbits of size
|X|
on
V(\Gamma)
. Since
1\times X\lhd\mathcal{G}
,
\mathcal{G}
has a faithful action on the orbits of
X
on
V(\Gamma)
: for
\beta\in V(\Gamma)
and
(\phi,g)\in\mathcal{G}
,

(\beta^{X})^{(\phi,g)}=\beta^{X(\phi,g)}=\left(\beta^{(\phi,g)}\right)^{X}.

Finally, if we assume the natural action of
\mathcal{G}
is transitive on
[G:X]
, then (recalling the identification of
G
with
V(\Gamma)
)
\mathcal{G}
will indeed be transitive on
V(\Gamma)
: let
\beta\in V(\Gamma)
. Then,
\beta\in\alpha^{Xg}
for some
g\in G
, and, by assumption
X^{(\phi,h)}=Xg
for some
(\phi,h)\in\mathcal{G}
, so
\alpha^{(\phi,h)}\in\alpha^{Xg}
. Since
1\times X\leqslant\mathcal{G}
is transitive on
\alpha^{Xg}=\left(\alpha^{g}\right)^{X}
(since
X
is normal in both
G
and
\mathcal{G}
), there exists an element
(\psi,y)\in\mathcal{G}
such that
\alpha^{(\psi,y)}=\beta
, i.e.,
\mathcal{G}
is transitive on
V(\Gamma)
, as desired.
∎

We now prove Theorem
1.2
; we omit the details that apply mutatis mutandis from Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Let
\Gamma={\rm Cay}(G,R)
. As in the proof of Theorem
1.1
, if
\mathcal{G}\leqslant{\rm Aut}(G)_{R}\ltimes G
has
|\mathcal{G}|=|G|
and
\mathcal{G}
is transitive on the vertices of
\Gamma
, we can then express
\Gamma
as
{\rm Cay}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{R})
, where

\mathcal{R}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\{r\in\mathcal{G}:\alpha^{r}\sim\alpha\}

for some base vertex
\alpha\in\Gamma
identified with
1\in G
; indeed, proceeding exactly as in the proof of Theorem
1.1
,
\mathcal{G}
will indeed be transitive on
V(\Gamma)
.

Define

\mathcal{U}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\{w\in\mathcal{G}:\alpha^{w}\in\Upsilon\},

where
\Upsilon\subseteq V(\Gamma)
is set of vertices whose (directed) distance from
\alpha
is either zero or more than two. Note that

U=\{g\in G:\alpha^{g}\in\Upsilon\},

so

\mathcal{U}=\{(\phi,g)\in\mathcal{G}:g\in U\}.

Since
|\mathcal{G}|=|G|
and
\mathcal{G}
is transitive on
V(\Gamma)
, we have
|\mathcal{U}|=|U|
, and there is a unique element
(\phi,w)\in\mathcal{U}
for each
w\in U
. Moreover, for each
\phi\in{\rm Aut}(G)_{R}
, since
R
is an RDS with unique forbidden subgroup
U
, we must have
U^{\phi}=U
, and, hence, for any
(\phi_{1},w_{1}),(\phi_{2},w_{2})\in\mathcal{U}
, we have

(\phi_{1},w_{1})(\phi_{2},w_{2})=(\phi_{1}\phi_{2},w_{1}^{\phi_{2}}w_{2})\in\mathcal{U},

implying that
\mathcal{U}\leqslant\mathcal{G}
since
U
is a finite subgroup of
G
. Since products
r_{1}r_{2}^{-1}
,
r_{1}\neq r_{2}\in R
, correspond to directed walks of length two starting at
\alpha
, this will imply that
\mathcal{R}
is a RDS of
\mathcal{G}
with the same parameters as
D
.
∎

3.
A partial converse to Dillon’s Dihedral Trick

Part of the inspiration of this paper is Dillon’s Dihedral Trick (see
[
29
, Section 7.3]
for an excellent account of this result); we want to be able to determine immediately the existence of a (P)DS in one group based on the existence of one in another group.

Theorem 3.1

(Dillon’s Dihedral Trick)
.

[
13
, Section 4, Theorem]

Let
H
be an abelian group, and let
G
be the generalized dihedral extension of
H
; i.e.,
G=\langle H,g\rangle
,
g^{2}=1
, and
ghg=h^{-1}
for all
h\in H
. If
G
contains a DS, then so does every abelian group which contains
H
as a subgroup of index
2
.

The proof of this result relies on calculations in the group ring
\mathbb{Z}[G]
. We know that a full converse to Dillon’s Dihedral Trick does not hold; for example,
C_{8}\times C_{2}
contains a
(16,6,2)
-DS but the dihedral group of order
16
does not. On the other hand, Theorem
1.1
can be used to prove a converse to Dillon’s Dihedral Trick that holds for regular PDSs (as well as
reversible
DSs).

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
X
be an abelian group, and let
G
be any abelian group containing
X
as a subgroup of index
2
. If
G
contains a regular PDS (respectively, reversible DS)
D
, then so does the generalized dihedral extension
\mathcal{G}
of
X
.

Proof.

Let
G=\langle X,y\rangle
, where
y^{2}\in X
. Let
\phi
be the inversion automorphism, i.e.,
g^{\phi}=g^{-1}
for all
g\in G
. Since
D
is a regular PDS (respectively, reversible DS),
D
is closed under inversion, and hence
\phi\in{\rm Aut}(G)_{D}
; moreover,
X^{\phi}=X
. Define

\mathcal{G}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\langle(1,x),(\phi,y):x\in X\rangle.

Note that

(\phi,y)^{2}=(\phi^{2},y^{\phi}y)=(1,y^{-1}y)=(1,1),

and so
1\times X
is an index
2
subgroup of
\mathcal{G}
, showing that
|\mathcal{G}|=|G|
and
1\times X
is normal in
\mathcal{G}
. Furthermore,
G=X\cup Xy
, and

X^{(\phi,y)}=X^{\phi}y=Xy.

By Theorem
1.1
,
\mathcal{G}
contains a PDS (or reversible DS) with the same parameters as
D
.

Finally, it remains to show that
\mathcal{G}
is isomorphic to the generalized dihedral extension of
X
. Indeed, for any
(1,x)\in 1\times X\cong X
, we have

(\phi,y)(1,x)(\phi,y)=(\phi^{2},y^{\phi}x^{\phi}y)=(1,y^{-1}x^{-1}y)=(1,x^{-1}),

proving the result.
∎

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
G
be an abelian group containing a reversible DS
D
, and let
X
be a subgroup of index
2
in
G
. Then, every abelian group containing
X
as a subgroup of index
2
contains a DS with the same parameters as
D
.

Proof.

Let
G
be an abelian group containing a reversible DS
D
, and let
X
be a subgroup of index
2
in
G
. By Theorem
3.2
, the generalized dihedral extension
\mathcal{G}
of
X
contains a DS with the same parameters as
D
. By Dillon’s Dihedral Trick (Theorem
3.1
), every abelian group containing
X
as a subgroup of index
2
contains a DS with the same parameters as
D
.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

The DS obtained from Dillon’s Dihedral Trick may not be reversible, even if the DS in the generalized dihedral extension is reversible.

Example 3.5
.

Let

G=\langle a,b,c:a^{4}=b^{2}=c^{2}=[a,b]=[a,c]=[b,c]=1\rangle\cong C_{4}\times C_{4}\times C_{2}.

Then, the subset

D=\{1,a,b,c,a^{3},a^{2}bc\}

is a reversible
(16,6,2)
-DS in
G
. If
\phi
is the inversion map on
G
, then

\mathcal{G}=\langle(1,a),(1,b),(\phi,c)\rangle\cong C_{2}\ltimes(C_{4}\times C_{2})

is isomorphic to the generalized dihedral extension of
\langle a,b\rangle\cong C_{4}\times C_{2}
, and, by Theorem
3.2
,
\mathcal{G}
contains a
(16,6,2)
-DS; namely,

\mathcal{D}=\{(1,1),(1,a),(1,b),(1,a^{3}),(\phi,c),(\phi,a^{2}bc)\}.

(Note that
(1,a^{2}b)(\phi,c)=(\phi,a^{2}bc)
.) To simplify notation, we let
h=(1,a)
,
x=(1,b)
,
g=(\phi,c)
, so

\mathcal{D}=\{1,h,x,h^{3},g,h^{2}xg\}.

We remark that
\mathcal{D}
is actually a reversible DS in
\mathcal{G}
. We define
H\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\langle h,x\rangle\cong C_{4}\times C_{2}
and note that
\mathcal{G}
is the generalized dihedral extension of
H
.

Define

K\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\langle k,h,x:k^{2}=h,h^{4}=x^{2}=[k,x]=1\rangle\cong C_{8}\times C_{2}.

Clearly,
K
contains
H
as an index
2
subgroup. By the proof of Dillon’s Dihedral Trick (
[
13
, pp. 17–18]
), the subset
S
of
K
obtained by replacing “
g
” with “
k
” in
\mathcal{D}
will be a
(16,6,2)
-DS in
K
; indeed,

S\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\{1,h,x,h^{3},k,h^{2}xk\}

is a
(16,6,2)
-DS of
K
. However,
k\in S
but
k^{-1}\notin S
, so the DS
S
obtained using Dillon’s Dihedral Trick here is not reversible. This means we cannot use Theorem
3.2
to obtain a DS in the dihedral group of order
16
. (In fact, the dihedral group of order
16
does not contain a
(16,6,2)
-DS.)

4.
PDSs in nonabelian
p
-groups

Throughout this section, let
p
be a prime, let
n\geqslant 2
be a positive integer, and define

G\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\langle x,y:x^{p^{n}}=y^{p^{n}}=[x,y]=1\rangle\cong C_{p^{n}}\times C_{p^{n}}.

These groups contain PDSs related to partial congruence partitions, Paley PDSs, as well as reversible Hadamard difference sets when
p=2
(which are also PDSs, since the DSs are reversible). When
p
is odd, the regular PDSs in such groups were completely classified in
[
23
]
. When
p
is even, a great deal is known, but the situation is more complicated; see
[
27
]
.

Given a PDS
D
in the group
G
, it is relatively easy to find an automorphism of
G
preserving
D
using
multipliers
. If
m
is an integer, we define

D^{(m)}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\{d^{m}:d\in D\}.

Lemma 4.1

(Ma’s Multiplier Theorem)
.

If
D=D^{(-1)}
is a PDS in an abelian group
G
and
m
is an integer relatively prime to the order of
G
, then
D^{(m)}=D.

We assume that
D
is a regular PDS in
G
, so that
D^{(-1)}=D
. Define an automorphism
\phi
of
G
by

g^{\phi}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}g^{p^{n-1}+1}

for all
g\in G
. Since
p^{n-1}+1
is coprime to
p
, it is coprime to
|G|
, and, by Ma’s Multiplier Theorem,
\phi
is indeed an automorphism of
G
fixing
D
. Moreover, for any
g\in G
,

g^{\phi^{p}}=g^{(p^{n-1}+1)^{p}}=g^{\sum_{i=0}^{p}{p\choose i}p^{i(n-1)}}=g,

so
\phi
is an order
p
automorphism of
G
.

The following result can be considered a generalization of the results in
[
7
, Section 4]
; note also that Theorem
1.1
allows for a considerably simpler proof of those results.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
G
and
\phi
be as defined above, suppose that
G
contains a regular PDS
D
, and define

\mathcal{G}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\langle(1,x),(\phi,y)\rangle.

Then,
\mathcal{G}
is a nonabelian group isomorphic to
C_{p^{n}}\rtimes_{p^{n-1}+1}C_{p^{n}}
that contains a regular PDS with the same parameters as
D
.

Proof.

First, when
p
is odd, we note that

(\phi,y)^{p}=\left(\phi^{p},\prod_{i=0}^{p-1}y^{(p^{n-1}+1)^{i}}\right)=\left(1,y^{\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}\limits(ip^{n-1}+1)}\right)=\left(1,y^{\frac{p(p-1)}{2}p^{n-1}+p}\right)=(1,y^{p}),

whereas when
p=2
we have

(\phi,y)^{4}=\left((\phi,y)^{2}\right)^{2}=\left((\phi^{2},y^{\phi}\cdot y)\right)^{2}=(1,y^{2^{n-1}+2})^{2}=(1,y^{4}),

which shows both that
(\phi,y)^{p}\in 1\times G
and
|(\phi,y)|=|y|
. Moreover, if

X\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\langle x,y^{p}\rangle,

then
\mathcal{G}\cap(1\times G)=1\times X
, which shows
|\mathcal{G}|=|G|
, and
1\times X
is normal in
\mathcal{G}
since it is an index
p
subgroup of a
p
-group. Moreover,
x^{\phi}\in\langle x\rangle\subseteq X
and

(y^{p})^{\phi}=\left(y^{p}\right)^{p^{n-1}+1}=y^{p^{n}+p}=y^{p}\in X,

so
X
is
\phi
-invariant. Now,

G=\bigcup_{i=0}^{p-1}Xy^{i},

and since

X^{(\phi,y)}=X^{\phi}y=Xy

and
y^{p}\in X
, we proceed by induction and see that

X^{(\phi,y)^{i}}=\left(Xy^{i-1}\right)^{(\phi,y)}=X^{\phi}(y^{i-1})^{\phi}y=X\left(y^{(p^{n-1}+1)(i-1)}\right)y=X\left(y^{p^{n-1}}\right)^{i-1}y^{i}=Xy^{i}.

Thus,
\mathcal{G}
is indeed transitive on the cosets of
X
in
G
. By Theorem
1.1
,
\mathcal{G}
contains a PDS with the same parameters as
D
.

Finally, we note that

(\phi,y)^{-1}(1,x)(\phi,y)=(\phi,y)^{-1}(\phi,x^{p^{n-1}+1}y)=(\phi,y)^{-1}(\phi,y)(1,x^{p^{n-1}+1})=(1,x^{p^{n-1}+1}),

showing that
\mathcal{G}
is nonabelian and
\langle(1,x)\rangle\cong C_{p^{n}}
is a normal subgroup of
\mathcal{G}
, implying that
\mathcal{G}\cong C_{p^{n}}\rtimes_{p^{n-1}+1}C_{p^{n}}
and completing the proof.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

Theorem
4.2
provides evidence that multipliers can likely be used in conjunction with Theorem
1.1
to construct many examples of PDSs in nonabelian groups whenever we have a corresponding PDS in an abelian group.

5.
Nonabelian Spence DSs

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
d
be a positive integer. There exists a group
\mathcal{G}
with a nonabelian, non-normal Sylow
3
-subgroup that contains a DS with Spence parameters; namely,
\mathcal{G}
contains a DS
\mathcal{D}
with parameters

\left(\frac{3^{3d}(3^{3d}-1)}{2},\frac{3^{3d-1}(3^{3d}+1)}{2},\frac{3^{3d-1}(3^{3d-1}+1)}{2}\right).

Proof.

Let
r\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}(3^{3d}-1)/2.
We begin with the abelian group

G\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\langle a_{1},\dots,a_{3d},w:a_{i}^{3}=w^{r}=[a_{i},a_{j}]=[a_{i},w]=1\rangle\cong C_{3}^{3d}\times C_{r}.

Note that, if
\varphi
denotes the Euler phi function, we have
\varphi(r)
is divisible by
3
; indeed, there is an automorphism
\rho
of
\langle w\rangle\cong C_{r}
given by
\rho:w\mapsto w^{3^{d}}
. Moreover, if

H\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\langle a_{1},\dots,a_{3d}\rangle\cong C_{3}^{3d},

then we may identify
H
with the additive group of the finite field
\mathbb{F}={\rm GF}(3^{3d})
, and there exists a Singer cycle
\sigma
of
\mathbb{F}
of order
2r=3^{3d}-1
, corresponding to multiplication by a primitive element
\theta
of the field, fixing the identity and cyclically permuting the units. Picking out a distinguished hyperplane (maximal subgroup)
H_{0}
of
H
, we may choose the indices of the other hyperplanes in terms of the action of
\sigma
; that is, for
1\leqslant i\leqslant r-1
, we may define

H_{i}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}H_{0}^{\sigma^{i}}=H_{0}\cdot\theta^{i}=\{h\cdot\theta^{i}:h\in H_{0}\}.

Indeed,
\sigma^{r}
acts as the additive inversion map on
\mathbb{F}
(multiplication by
-1
), and so
\sigma^{r}
fixes each hyperplane setwise. Noting that the Frobenius automorphism
F:x\mapsto x^{3}
is an automorphism of
\mathbb{F}
of order
3d
, the automorphism
F^{d}:x\mapsto x^{3^{d}}
is an automorphism of order
3
of
\mathbb{F}
. Since
3
is coprime to
r
,
F^{d}
must fix at least one hyperplane; indeed, without loss of generality we will assume that
F^{d}
fixes the hyperplane

H_{0}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\langle a_{1},\dots,a_{3d-1}\rangle.

Thus, we have

H_{i}^{F^{d}}=\left(H_{0}\cdot\theta^{i}\right)^{F^{d}}=H_{0}^{F^{d}}\cdot\left(\theta^{i}\right)^{F^{d}}=H_{0}\left(\theta^{3^{d}}\right)^{i}=H_{i\cdot 3^{d}},

where the calculation of the subscript
i\cdot 3^{d}
is done modulo
r
. (Indeed,
F^{d}
is acting on
\mathbb{F}^{*}/\langle-1\rangle\cong C_{r}
exactly as
\rho
is acting on
\langle w\rangle
.)

By
[
33
, Theorem 1]
, if we denote the
r
elements of
\langle w\rangle
by
k_{0},\dots,k_{r-1}
(in any order), then the set

D\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}H_{0}^{c}k_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}H_{j}k_{j}

is a difference set (here,
H_{0}^{c}
denotes the set-theoretic complement). Here, we will specifically choose
k_{i}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}w^{i}
for each
i
, i.e., we will consider

D=H_{0}^{c}+\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}H_{j}w^{j}

By the above discussion, we may define an automorphism
\phi
of
G
, where, if
g=hw^{i}
for
h\in H
, then

g^{\phi}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}h^{F^{d}}(w^{i})^{\rho}.

Thus,

\displaystyle D^{\phi}

\displaystyle=\left(H_{0}^{c}+\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}H_{j}w^{j}\right)^{\phi}

\displaystyle=H_{0}^{c}+\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}H_{j\cdot 3^{d}}w^{j\cdot 3^{d}}

\displaystyle=D,

and so
\phi\in{\rm Aut}(G)_{D}.

Define
\mathcal{G}\leqslant{\rm Aut}(G)_{D}\ltimes G
by

\mathcal{G}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\langle(1,a_{1}),\dots,(1,a_{3d-1}),(\phi,a_{3d}),(1,w)\rangle.

Note that, if

X\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\langle a_{1},\dots,a_{3d-1},w\rangle=\langle H,w\rangle,

then
\mathcal{G}\cap(1\times G)=1\times X
and
|G:X|=3
. Moreover,

(\phi,a_{3d})^{-1}(1\times X)(\phi,a_{3d})=1\times a_{3d}^{-1}X^{\phi}a_{3d}=1\times X,

and so
1\times X
is a normal subgroup of both
1\times G
and
\mathcal{G}
.

Next, examining the action of
(\phi,a_{3d})
on

[G:X]=\left\{X,Xa_{3d},Xa_{3d}^{2}\right\},

we see that

X^{(\phi,a_{3d})}=X^{\phi}a_{3d}=Xa_{3d};

since the orbit of
\mathcal{G}
containing
X
has size
1
or size
3
, we see that
\mathcal{G}
is in fact transitive on
[G:X]
under this action.

We note that
H_{0}^{\phi}=H_{0}
and
a_{3d}\notin H_{0}
, so
a_{3d}^{\phi}=ha_{3d}^{i}
for some
h\in H_{0}
and
i=1,2
. Moreover,
\phi
has order
3
, so we must have
i=1
, i.e.,
a_{3d}^{\phi}=ha_{3d}
for some
h\in H_{0}
. With this in mind, we have

(\phi,a_{3d})^{3}=(\phi^{3},(a_{3d})^{\phi^{2}}(a_{3d})^{\phi}a_{3d})=(1,h^{\phi}ha_{3d}^{3})=(1,h^{\phi}h)\in 1\times H_{0}\leqslant 1\times X,

and so the order of
(\phi,a_{3d})
is
9
, and
|\mathcal{G}:(1\times X)|=3
. Therefore,
\mathcal{G}
satisfies the criteria of Theorem
1.1
, and so
\mathcal{G}
contains a DS with Spence parameters.

To finish the proof, we will show that
\mathcal{G}
contains a nonabelian, non-normal Sylow
3
-subgroup. First, by order considerations, we see that

P_{3}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\langle H_{0},(\phi,a_{3d})\rangle

is a Sylow
3
-subgroup of
\mathcal{G}
. To see that
P_{3}
is nonabelian, we first take
h\in H_{0}
such that
h^{\phi}=h^{F^{d}}=h^{3^{d}}\neq h
; such an
h
exists because
F^{d}
fixes exactly
3^{d}
elements of
H
and
|H|=3^{3d-1}.
Then,

(\phi,a_{3d})(1,h)=(\phi,ha_{3d}),

whereas

(1,h)(\phi,a_{3d})=(\phi,h^{\phi}a_{3d}),

and we see that
P_{3}
is nonabelian. Finally, we note that

(1,w)(\phi,a_{3d})(1,w)^{-1}=(\phi,w^{\rho}a_{3d}w^{-1})=(\phi,a_{3d}w^{3^{d}-1})\notin P_{3},

and so
\mathcal{G}
indeed has a nonabelian, non-normal Sylow
3
-subgroup, as desired.
∎

Remark 5.2
.

When
d=1
in Theorem

, we obtain the nonabelian Spence DS first noted in

, as we see in the following example.

Example 5.3
.

We consider the concrete example when
d=1
and
r=13
. In this instance,
\mathbb{F}={\rm GF}(27)={\rm GF}(3)(\theta)
, where
\theta^{3}=\theta+2
. The map
\sigma:\mathbb{F}\to\mathbb{F}
defined by
x^{\sigma}=x\cdot\theta
is an additive automorphism. If we consider
\mathbb{F}
as a
3
-dimensional vector space over
{\rm GF}(3)
, then the hyperplanes are the
2
-dimensional subspaces of
\mathbb{F}
. We define

H_{0}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\langle 1,\theta\rangle=\{a+b\theta:a,b\in{\rm GF}(3)\}.

With this “base hyperplane,” we then define the other twelve hyperplanes by

H_{i}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}H_{0}^{\sigma^{i}}=H_{0}\cdot\theta^{i}=\{h\cdot\theta^{i}:h\in H_{0}\}.

We may further consider the Frobenius mapping
F:\mathbb{F}\to\mathbb{F}
defined by
x^{F}=x^{3}

x^{F^{3}}=x^{27}=x
for all

F^{1}=F
is an element of order

in the automorphism group of

H_{0}^{F}=\langle 1^{F},\theta^{F}\rangle=\langle 1,\theta^{3}\rangle=\langle 1,\theta+2\rangle=H_{0},

and the other twelve hyperplanes are permuted using the permutation

\pi\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}(1\;3\;9)(2\;6\;5)(4\;12\;10)(7\;8\;11)

on the indices, i.e.,
H_{i}^{F}=H_{i^{\pi}}.
Noting that
\theta^{13}=-1

K\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\mathbb{F}^{*}/\langle-1\rangle=\langle\overline{\theta}\rangle\cong C_{13}

via the permutation

Putting this all together, the Frobenius map

is a Spence DS that is invariant under the action of

\displaystyle(F,(\theta^{2},\overline{1}))^{3}

we see that

6.
Nonabelian Denniston PDSs

This section is dedicated to the construction of Denniston PDSs in nonabelian groups.

6.1.
A nonabelian
2
-group containing a Denniston PDS whenever the elementary abelian group of the same order does

In
[
12
]
, Denniston proved an existence result concerning maximal arcs in projective spaces, which can be rephrased in terms of PDSs as follows (see
[
25
, Theorems 8.1, 9.1, 9.3, Example 9.4]
):

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
Q:{\rm GF}(2^{m})^{2}\to{\rm GF}(2^{m})
be a quadratic form such that
Q(x)=0
if and only if
x=0
, and let
K
be a subgroup of the additive group of
{\rm GF}(2^{m})
with
2^{r}
elements, where
1\leqslant r<m
. Then

D\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\{(c,ca,cb):c\in{\rm GF}(2^{m})^{\times},Q(a,b)\in K\}

is a PDS in the additive group
G=({\rm GF}(2^{m})^{3},+)
with parameters

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle=2^{3m}

\displaystyle k

\displaystyle=(2^{m+r}-2^{m}+2^{r})(2^{m}-1),

\displaystyle\lambda

\displaystyle=2^{m}-2^{r}+(2^{m+r}-2^{m}+2^{r})(2^{r}-2),

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=(2^{m+r}-2^{m}+2^{r})(2^{r}-1).

By
[
2
, Theorem 3.28]
, if
x=(x_{1},x_{2})
, we may choose
Q(x)=x_{1}x_{2}
or
Q(x)=x_{1}^{2}+\alpha x_{1}x_{2}+x_{2}^{2}
, where
\alpha\in{\rm GF}(2^{m})
satisfies
{\rm tr}(\alpha^{-1})=1
(
{\rm tr}
is the standard field trace as defined in Section
6
). Since we want
Q(x)=0
if and only if
x=0
, our form must be equivalent to
Q(x)=x_{1}^{2}+\alpha x_{1}x_{2}+x_{2}^{2}
, and in this case the matrix

M\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&0&1\\
0&1&0\\
\end{pmatrix}

is an automorphism of
G
that fixes
D
setwise since switching the second and third coordinates stabilizes
Q
.

With this setup, we can prove the existence of a nonabelian group containing Denniston PDSs.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
m\geqslant 2
be a positive integer. There exists a nonabelian group
\mathcal{G}
that contains a PDS with Denniston parameters

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle=2^{3m}

\displaystyle k

\displaystyle=(2^{m+r}-2^{m}+2^{r})(2^{m}-1),

\displaystyle\lambda

\displaystyle=2^{m}-2^{r}+(2^{m+r}-2^{m}+2^{r})(2^{r}-2),

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=(2^{m+r}-2^{m}+2^{r})(2^{r}-1).

for each positive integer
r
,
1\leqslant r<m
.

Proof.

Let
G
,
D
, and
M
be as defined above. Define
u\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}(0,1,1)\in G
, and let
X
be the complement hyperplane to
\langle u\rangle
in
G
(as a
{\rm GF}(2)
-vector space), such that

G=X\oplus\langle u\rangle.

Since
M
stabilizes
u
,
M
stabilizes
X
setwise. Let
x_{1},\dots x_{3m-1}
be a
{\rm GF}(2)
-basis for
X
, and define

\mathcal{G}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\langle(1,x_{i}),(M,u):1\leqslant i\leqslant 3m-1\rangle.

Note that
\mathcal{G}\cap(1\times G)=1\times X
, and, since
M
stabilizes
X
,

(M,u)^{-1}(1\times X)(M,u)=1\times X,

meaning
1\times X
is a normal subgroup of
\mathcal{G}
. Furthermore,
G=X\cup(X+u)

We follow the notation of

We first construct a Galois ring over

The ring

is a finite local ring with unique maximal ideal

R/2R\cong{\rm GF}(2^{t})

{\rm GF}(2)
-vector spaces,

are isomorphic, with an explicit isomorphism given by
\phi(2h^{i})=g^{i}

If

{\rm tr}(\alpha)=\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}\alpha^{2^{i}},

then
H_{0}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\{r\in{\rm GF}(2^{t}):{\rm tr}(r)=0\}

are

are of the form

. More explicitly,
K_{0}=\{r\in 2R:{\rm tr}(\phi(r))=0\}
and

for

.

We are now ready to define our PDS. Define

G\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}(R,+)\times({\rm GF}(2^{t}),+)\cong C_{4}^{t}\times C_{2}^{t}.

Let

be any element of

Theorem 6.3
.

[
10

The set

(2^{3t},(2^{2t-1}-2^{t-1})(2^{t}-1),2^{t-1}+(2^{2t-1}-2^{t-1})(2^{t-1}-2),(2^{2t-1}-2^{t-1})(2^{t-1}-1))

We will explicitly define automorphisms of

when

implies that the inversion map

Lemma 6.4
.

The automorphism
\psi_{\ell}
,
1\leqslant\ell\leqslant t-2
, fixes
D
setwise.

Proof.

It suffices to prove that
\psi_{\ell}
fixes each
(E_{i},g_{i})

\psi_{\ell}
fixes
(h^{i}+h^{2i-j}+2a_{i,j}+K_{j},g^{i})

and

\psi_{\ell}
fixes the element

and the set

(h^{i}+h^{2i-j},g^{i})^{\psi_{\ell}}-(h^{i}+h^{2i-j},g^{i})\in K_{j}=h^{j}K_{0}

\displaystyle(h^{i}+h^{2i-j},g^{i})^{\psi_{\ell}}

\displaystyle-(h^{i}+h^{2i-j},g^{i})=(2h^{i+2^{\ell-1}}+2h^{2i-j+2^{\ell-1}}+2f_{\ell}(g^{i}),0)

\displaystyle=\left(2h^{i+2^{\ell-1}}+2h^{2i-j+2^{\ell-1}}+\phi^{-1}({\rm tr}(g))\cdot h^{i}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leqslant k\leqslant t-1,\\

Theorem 6.5
.

Let

be a positive integer. For each

,

contains a PDS with parameters

(2^{3t},(2^{2t-1}-2^{t-1})(2^{t}-1),2^{t-1}+(2^{2t-1}-2^{t-1})(2^{t-1}-2),(2^{2t-1}-2^{t-1})(2^{t-1}-1)).

Proof.

Let
G
and
D
be as defined above, and let

be a fixed integer,

. Define

by

\mathcal{G}=\langle(1,(h^{i},0)),(1,(0,g^{j})),(\psi_{\ell},(0,g^{\ell})):0\leqslant i\leqslant t-1,k\leqslant j\leqslant t-1,0\leqslant\ell\leqslant k-1\rangle.

, where

, and

be as defined above. Define
X
to be a complement hyperplane to
(0,1)\in G=({\rm GF}(p^{m}),+)\times({\rm GF}(p^{2m}),+)
, where
G
is considered as a
{\rm GF}(p)
-vector space. Thus,

G=X\oplus\langle(0,1)\rangle.

Since
\phi
stabilizes
(0,1)
,
\phi
stabilizes
X
setwise. Let
x_{1},\dots x_{3m-1}
be a
{\rm GF}(p)
-basis for
X
, and define

\mathcal{G}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\langle(1,x_{i}),(\phi,(0,1)):1\leqslant i\leqslant 3m-1\rangle.

Note that
\mathcal{G}\cap(1\times G)=1\times X
, and, since
\phi
stabilizes
X
,

(\phi,(0,1))^{-1}(1\times X)(\phi,(0,1))=1\times X,

meaning
1\times X
is a normal subgroup of
\mathcal{G}
. Furthermore,
G=\bigcup_{i=0}^{p-1}\limits X+i(0,1)
, and

X^{(\phi^{i},i(0,1))}=X^{\phi^{i}}+i(0,1)=X+i(0,1),

meaning
\mathcal{G}
is transitive on the cosets of
X
in
G
. Therefore, by Theorem
1.1
,
\mathcal{G}
contains a PDS with the same parameters as
D
.

Finally, we note that

(1,(0,\alpha))\cdot(\phi,(0,1))=(\phi,(0,\alpha)^{\phi}+(0,1))=(\phi,(0,\alpha^{p^{2t}}+1),

whereas

(\phi,(0,1))\cdot(1,(0,\alpha))=(\phi,(0,\alpha+1)),

which are not equal since
\alpha
is a primitive element with multiplicative order
p^{2m}-1
, and so
\mathcal{G}
is indeed nonabelian.
∎

Remark 6.9
.

Define
q\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}p^{t}
. Then,
p^{m}=q^{p}
, and so
{\rm GF}(p^{m})
is a degree
p
extension of
{\rm GF}(q)
. Define
{\rm tr}_{q}
to be the trace map of
{\rm GF}(p^{m})
over
{\rm GF}(q)
, i.e.,

{\rm tr}_{q}(\beta)=\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}\beta^{q^{i}};

similarly,
p^{2m}=(q^{2})^{p}
,
{\rm GF}(p^{2m})
is a degree
p
extension of
{\rm GF}(q^{2})
, and we define
{\rm tr}_{q^{2}}
to be the trace map of
{\rm GF}(p^{2m})
over
{\rm GF}(q^{2})
.

Note first that, for any
(\phi^{i},(\beta,\gamma))\in\mathcal{G}
, since
p
is odd, direct calculation shows that

(\phi^{i},(\beta,\gamma))^{p}=((\phi^{i})^{p},({\rm tr}_{q}(\beta),{\rm tr}_{q^{2}}(\gamma)))=(1,({\rm tr}_{q}(\beta),{\rm tr}_{q^{2}}(\gamma)))\in 1\times G,

so every element of
\mathcal{G}
has order at most
p^{2}
. On the other hand, let
\theta\in{\rm GF}(p^{m})
be any element with
{\rm tr}_{q}(\theta)\neq 0
. Then,
(\phi,(\theta,1))=((\phi,(0,1))(1,(\theta,0))\in\mathcal{G}
, and

(\phi,(\theta,1))^{p}=(1,({\rm tr}_{q}(\theta),{\rm tr}_{q^{2}}(1)))=(1,({\rm tr}_{q}(\theta),0))\neq(1,(0,0))=1_{\mathcal{G}},

so
\mathcal{G}
has exponent
p^{2}
.

7.
Nonabelian DSs with McFarland parameters

In
[
28
]
, McFarland presents a very general construction for difference sets. Let
q
be a prime power and let
V
be an
(s+1)
-dimensional
{\rm GF}(q)
-vector space. Define

r\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\frac{q^{s+1}-1}{q-1},

and let
H_{1},\dots,H_{r}
be the distinct hyperplanes of
V
. Let
E=(V,+)
, the additive group of
V
, and consider the
H_{i}
as subgroups of
E
. Let
K
be any group of order
r+1
,
k_{1},\dots,k_{r}
any
r
distinct elements of
K
, and
e_{1},\dots,e_{r}
any
r
elements of
E
(not necessarily distinct).

Theorem 7.1
.

[
28
]

Using the notation above, if
G=E\times K
, then the set

D\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}(H_{i}+e_{i},k_{i})

is a difference set in
G
with parameters

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle=q^{s+1}\left(\frac{q^{s+1}-1}{q-1}+1\right),

\displaystyle k

\displaystyle=q^{s}\left(\frac{q^{s+1}-1}{q-1}\right),

\displaystyle\lambda

\displaystyle=q^{s}\left(\frac{q^{s}-1}{q-1}\right).

These results were generalized by Dillon, who was able to significantly weaken the necessary conditions of Theorem
7.1
.

Theorem 7.2
.

[
13
, Section 2, Theorem]

Let
q
be a prime power and let
s
be any positive integer. Let
E
be an elementary abelian group of order
q^{s+1}
(which we regard as vector space of dimension
s+1
over
{\rm GF}(q)
), and let
H_{1},\dots,H_{r}
,
r=(q^{s+1}-1)/(q-1)
, be the
s
-dimensional subspaces of
E
. Let
G
be a group of order
q^{s+1}(r+1)
which contains
E
as a normal subgroup and let
g_{1},\dots,g_{r}
be elements of
G
lying in distinct cosets of
E
. Let

D\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}g_{i}H_{i}.

If the map

H_{i}\mapsto g_{i}H_{i}g_{i}^{-1}

is a permutation of the hyperplanes of
E
, then
D
is a difference set with McFarland parameters.

Drisko
[
14
]
was further able to provide conditions guaranteeing in certain instances the existence of the coset representatives
g_{i}
that are necessary for Theorem
7.2
:

Corollary 7.3
.

[
14
, Corollary 8]

Suppose
q
is a prime,
r=(q^{s+1}-1)/(q-1)
, and
r+1
is a prime power. Then any group
G
of order
q^{s+1}(r+1)
which has a normal elementary abelian subgroup
E
of order
q^{s+1}
has a difference set with McFarland parameters.

Remark 7.4
.

It is likely that many McFarland DSs constructed using Theorem
1.1
from a group
G=E\times K
, where
E
is elementary abelian, also can be shown to exist using the results of Dillon (Theorem
7.2
) and Drisko (Corollary
7.3
). On the other hand, in the new groups created by Theorem
1.1
, it may not be obvious from the outset that the coset representatives necessary to apply Theorem
7.2
exist, and it is also possible that the new groups created by Theorem
1.1
do not contain an elementary abelian subgroup of order
q^{s+1}
and will be genuinely new.

With this discussion in mind, we present some constructions, some of which may also follow from Theorem
7.2
.

7.1.
Constructions for even values of
q

In this subsection, we let
q=2^{d}
for some positive integer
d
. We fix
s=1
,
V={\rm GF}(q)^{2}
,
r=q+1
,
E=(V,+)\cong C_{2}^{2d}
, and
K=C_{r+1}=C_{q+2}
. Then,

G=E\times K\cong C_{2}^{2d}\times C_{q+2}.

Define an automorphism
\phi
of
G
as follows: if
E
is identified with
V={\rm GF}(q)^{2}
and
K
is viewed additively, then define

\left((a,b),x\right)^{\phi}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\left((b,a),-x\right).

It is clear that
\phi
is an order
2
automorphism. Note that
\langle(1,1)\rangle
is the unique
{\rm GF}(q)
-hyperplane of
V
fixed by swapping the coordinates. Moreover,
(q+2)/2=q/2+1
is odd, so
C_{q+2}
contains a unique order
2
element
u
that will be fixed under the mapping
x\mapsto-x
.

By Theorem
7.1
, we may assign the nonidentity elements of
K
to the hyperplanes
H_{i}
of
E
however we want, and we will obtain a difference set with McFarland parameters. We choose the following assignment: first, if
H_{1}=\langle(1,1)\rangle
, we choose
k_{1}=u
; next, we order the other
H_{i}
so that, if
H_{i}=\langle(a,b)\rangle
, then
H_{r+2-i}=\langle(b,a)\rangle
; finally, we order the remaining
k_{i}
so that, if
k_{i}=x
, then
k_{r+2-i}=-x
. Define

D\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}(H_{i},k_{i}).

Then, by Theorem
7.1
,
D
is a DS with McFarland parameters, and our choice of
H_{i}
and
k_{i}
shows that
D
is invariant under
\phi
, since
(H_{1},k_{1})^{\phi}=(H_{1},k_{1})
and
(H_{i},k_{i})^{\phi}=(H_{r+2-i},k_{r+2-i})
for
2\leqslant i\leqslant r
. We will now use Theorem
1.1
to construct different nonabelian groups containing McFarland DSs.

For ease of notation, we write elements in
\langle\phi\rangle\ltimes G
in the form
(a,b,c)
, where
a\in\langle\phi\rangle
,
b\in E
, and
c\in K
. We will also denote the identity element of
E=(V,+)
simply by
0
. (We will use similar notation when referring to elements of
G
, and we will use notation along these lines for the rest of Section
7
.)

Theorem 7.5
.

Let
G=E\times K
,
D
, and
\phi
be as above. Let
e_{1},\dots e_{2d}
be generators for
E
, and let
K=\langle u,y\rangle
, so that
|y|=(q+2)/2
. Define

\mathcal{G}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\langle(\phi,0,u),(1,0,y),(1,e_{i},0):1\leqslant i\leqslant 2d\rangle.

Then,
\mathcal{G}
is a nonabelian group containing a DS with McFarland parameters.

Proof.

Note that, if

X=\langle(1,0,y),(1,e_{i},0):1\leqslant i\leqslant 2d\rangle,

then
1\times X=(1\times G)\cap\mathcal{G}
and
X^{\phi}=X
. Moreover,
(\phi,0,u)^{2}=(1,0,0)
and

(\phi,0,u)^{-1}(1\times X)(\phi,0,u)=1\times X

so
|\mathcal{G}|=|G|
and
1\times X
is a normal subgroup of
\mathcal{G}
. Finally,
G=X\cup X+(0,u)
, and

X^{(\phi,0,u)}=X^{\phi}+(0,u)=X+(0,u),

so
\mathcal{G}
is transitive on the cosets of
X
in
G
. Thus, by Theorem
1.1
,
\mathcal{G}
contains a DS with McFarland parameters. Finally, if
e_{1}=(1,0)
and
e_{2}=(0,1)
, then

(\phi,0,u)\cdot(1,e_{1},0)=(\phi,e_{1},u),

whereas

(1,e_{1},0)\cdot(\phi,0,u)=(\phi,e_{2},u),

so
\mathcal{G}
is nonabelian.
∎

Remark 7.6
.

If
\mathcal{G}
is the nonabelian group constructed in Theorem
7.5
and
K
is the dihedral group of order
r+2
, then
\mathcal{G}\cong E\rtimes K
, and
\mathcal{G}
can be seen to have a McFarland DS by applying Theorem
7.2
.

Theorem 7.7
.

Let
G=E\times K
,
D
, and
\phi
be as above. Let
e_{1},\dots,e_{2d}
be generators for
E
, where
e_{1}=(1,0)
and
e_{2}=(0,1)
, and let
K=\langle u,y\rangle
, so that
|y|=(q+2)/2
. Define

\mathcal{G}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\langle(\phi,e_{1},0),(1,0,y),(1,0,u),(1,e_{1}+e_{2},0),(1,e_{i},0):3\leqslant i\leqslant 2d\rangle.

Then,
\mathcal{G}
is a nonabelian group containing a DS with McFarland parameters.

Proof.

Note that, if

X=\langle(1,0,y),(1,0,u),(1,e_{1}+e_{2},0),(1,e_{i},0):3\leqslant i\leqslant 2d\rangle,

then
1\times X=(1\times G)\cap\mathcal{G}
and
X^{\phi}=X
. Moreover,

(\phi,e_{1},0)^{2}=(\phi^{2},e_{2}+e_{1},0)=(1,e_{1}+e_{2},0)\in X

and

(\phi,e_{1},0)^{-1}(1\times X)(\phi,e_{1},0)=1\times X

so
|\mathcal{G}|=|G|
and
1\times X
is a normal subgroup of
\mathcal{G}
. Furthermore,
G=X\cup X+(e_{1},0)
, and

X^{(\phi,e_{1},0)}=X^{\phi}+(e_{1},0)=X+(e_{1},0),

so
\mathcal{G}
is transitive on the cosets of
X
in
G
. Thus, by Theorem
1.1
,
\mathcal{G}
contains a DS with McFarland parameters. Finally,

(\phi,e_{1},0)\cdot(1,0,y)=(\phi,e_{1},y),

whereas

(1,0,y)\cdot(\phi,e_{1},0)=(\phi,e_{1},-y),

so
\mathcal{G}
is nonabelian.
∎

Remark 7.8
.

The group
\mathcal{G}
does contain a normal, elementary abelian group

\mathcal{E}=\langle(1,0,u),(1,e_{1}+e_{2},0),(1,e_{i},0):3\leqslant i\leqslant 2d\rangle

of order
q^{2}
. It is possible that the coset representatives of

in

satisfy the hypotheses of Theorem
7.2
, but this is not immediately clear to the authors.

Finally, we modify the above constructions to create a group that does not have a normal, elementary abelian subgroup of order
q^{2}
. As above, we let
V={\rm GF}(q)^{2}
, where
q=2^{d}
, and
E=(V,+)
. Define
K^{\prime}=\langle y,u\rangle\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}D_{(q+2)/2}
, the dihedral group of order
q+2
, where
y^{\frac{q+2}{2}}=u^{2}=u^{-1}xux=1
. Now, we define

G^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}E\times K^{\prime}.

Define an automorphism
\psi
of
G^{\prime}
by

((a,b),x)^{\psi}=((b,a),u^{-1}xu).

Indeed,
\psi
is an order
2
automorphism of
G^{\prime}
. As in the previous cases,
\langle(1,1)\rangle
is the unique
{\rm GF}(q)
-hyperplane of
V
fixed by swapping the coordinates, and, since
(q+2)/2
is odd, the only nonidentity element of

fixed under conjugation by

is

itself.

Proceeding as before, we may assign the nonidentity elements of
K
to the hyperplanes
H_{i}
of
E
however we want, and we will obtain a difference set with McFarland parameters. We choose the following assignment: first, if
H_{1}=\langle(1,1)\rangle
, we choose
k_{1}=u
; next, we order the other
H_{i}
so that, if
H_{i}=\langle(a,b)\rangle
, then
H_{r+2-i}=\langle(b,a)\rangle
; finally, we order the remaining
k_{i}
so that, if
k_{i}=x
, then
k_{r+2-i}=u^{-1}xu
. Define

D^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}(H_{i},k_{i}).

Then, by Theorem
7.1
,
D^{\prime}
is a DS with McFarland parameters, and our choice of
H_{i}
and
k_{i}
shows that
D^{\prime}
is invariant under
\psi
, since
(H_{1},k_{1})^{\psi}=(H_{1},k_{1})
and
(H_{i},k_{i})^{\psi}=(H_{r+2-i},k_{r+2-i})
for
2\leqslant i\leqslant r
. Note that we will again use coordinate notation similar to that used in Theorems
7.5
and
7.7
.

Theorem 7.9
.

Let
G^{\prime}=E\times K^{\prime}
,
D^{\prime}
,
y
,
u
, and
\psi
be as above. Let
e_{1},\dots,e_{2d}
be generators for
E
, where
e_{1}=(1,0)
and
e_{2}=(0,1)
. Define

\mathcal{G}^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\langle(\psi,e_{1},1),(1,0,y),(1,0,u),(1,e_{1}+e_{2},1),(1,e_{i},1):3\leqslant i\leqslant 2d\rangle.

Then,
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
is a nonabelian group containing a DS with McFarland parameters that does not have a normal Sylow
2
-subgroup and that does not contain a normal elementary abelian subgroup of order

.

Proof.

Note that, if

X=\langle(1,0,y),(1,0,u),(1,e_{1}+e_{2},1),(1,e_{i},1):3\leqslant i\leqslant 2d\rangle,

then
1\times X=(1\times G^{\prime})\cap\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
and

. Moreover,

(\psi,e_{1},1)^{2}=(\psi^{2},e_{2}+e_{1},1)=(1,e_{1}+e_{2},1)\in X

and

(\psi,e_{1},1)^{-1}(1\times X)(\psi,e_{1},1)=1\times X

so
|\mathcal{G}^{\prime}|=|G^{\prime}|
and

is a normal subgroup of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
. Furthermore,

, and

so
\mathcal{G}
is transitive on the cosets of

in
G
. Thus, by Theorem
1.1
,
\mathcal{G}
contains a DS with McFarland parameters. Finally, since a Sylow
2
-subgroup of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
has order
2q^{2}
and
(\psi,e_{1},1)
has order

, we see that

\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}=\langle(1,0,u),(\psi,e_{1},1),(1,e_{i},1):3\leqslant i\leqslant 2d\rangle

is a Sylow

-subgroup of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
, and hence

\mathcal{E}^{\prime}=\langle(1,0,u),(1,e_{1}+e_{2},1),(1,e_{i},1):3\leqslant i\leqslant 2d\rangle

is the unique elementary abelian subgroup of order

in
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
. However,

(1,0,y)^{-1}(1,0,u)(1,0,y)=(1,0,uy^{2})\notin\mathcal{Q}^{\prime},

so neither
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
nor
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
is normal in
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
, completing the proof.
∎

7.2.
A construction for odd values of
q

We present another construction, which is similar in spirit to Drisko’s (Corollary
7.3
).

Theorem 7.10
.

Let
q
be an odd prime,
2\leqslant s<q
. Define

r\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\frac{q^{s+1}-1}{q-1}

and assume
r+1=2p
, where
p
is an odd prime. Then, there exists a group
\mathcal{G}
with a non-normal, nonabelian Sylow
q
-subgroup containing a DS with McFarland parameters.

Proof.

First, let
V={\rm GF}(q)^{s+1}
,
E=(V,+)
,
K=(\mathbb{Z}/(r+1)\mathbb{Z},+)\cong C_{r+1}=C_{2p}
, and
G=E\times K
. Note that
{\rm Aut}(K)\cong C_{p-1}
, and
{\rm Aut}(K)
has exactly four orbits on
K
:
\{0\}
,
\{p\}
, and two orbits of length
p-1
. Now, since
2p=r+1
, we have

p=\frac{r+1}{2}=\frac{q(q^{s-1}+\cdots+q+1)}{2}+1,

meaning
q
divides
p-1
. Hence, there is an automorphism
\sigma
of
K
of order
q
that fixes
0,p\in\mathbb{Z}/2p\mathbb{Z}
and partitions the remaining elements into orbits of size
q
.

Next, let
e_{1},\dots,e_{s+1}
be a basis for
V
. Consider the matrix
M=(m_{i,j})
, where
1\leqslant i,j\leqslant s+1
, with

m_{i,j}=\begin{cases}1,\text{ if }i\leqslant j\leqslant i+1,\\
0,\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

That is

M=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&0&\cdots&0\\
0&1&1&\ddots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&0&1&1\\
0&\cdots&\cdots&0&1\end{pmatrix}.

Now, an induction argument shows that, for
n\geqslant 1
,

M^{n}=\begin{pmatrix}1&{n\choose 1}&{n\choose 2}&\cdots&{n\choose s}\\
0&1&{n\choose 1}&\ddots&{n\choose{s-1}}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&0&1&{n\choose 1}\\
0&\cdots&\cdots&0&1\end{pmatrix},

where we define
{n\choose t}
to be
0
when
t>n
. Since
s<q
, this shows that the matrix
M
has multiplicative order
q
. Moreover, we note that
M
is already in Jordan Canonical Form, and, indeed, if
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{s+1})
is an eigenvector for
M
, then

(\lambda x_{1},\dots,\lambda x_{s+1})=\lambda x=xM=(x_{1},x_{1}+x_{2},x_{2}+x_{3},\dots,x_{s}+x_{s+1}),

then
\lambda=1
and
x_{1}=\cdots=x_{s}=0
. That is,
M
fixes a unique
1
-dimensional subspace of
V
, and hence
M
fixes a unique hyperplane
H_{1}
of
V
(and the remaining
r-1
hyperplanes are partitioned into orbits of length
q
).

We now define an automorphism
\phi
of
G
by

(u,y)^{\phi}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}(uM,y^{\sigma}).

It is clear from the construction that
\phi
has order
q
. Now, we assign nonidentity elements of
K
to hyperplanes of
E=(V,+)
as follows: first, let
H_{1}
be the unique hyperplane
\langle e_{s+1}\rangle^{\perp}
fixed by
M
and choose
k_{1}=p\in K=(\mathbb{Z}/(r+1)\mathbb{Z},+)=(\mathbb{Z}/2p\mathbb{Z},+)
. Next, we note that the remaining
r-1
hyperplanes are partitioned into
\langle M\rangle
-orbits of size
q
and the remaining
r-1
nonidentity elements of
K
are partitioned into
\langle\sigma\rangle
-orbits of size
q
. We choose representatives for each
\langle M\rangle
-orbit of size
q
and for each
\langle\sigma\rangle
-orbit of size
q
, and then we choose any bijection we want between the representatives of orbits of hyperplanes and the representatives of orbits of elements of
K
, and if
H_{i}\leftrightarrow k_{i}
, then we extend this to a bijection between hyperplanes and nonidentity elements of
K
by defining
H_{i}M^{j}\leftrightarrow k_{i}^{\sigma^{j}}
. Define

D\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}(H_{i},k_{i}).

Then, by Theorem
7.1
,
D
is a DS with McFarland parameters, and our choice of
H_{i}
and
k_{i}
shows that
D
is invariant under
\phi
, since
(H_{1},k_{1})^{\phi}=(H_{1},k_{1})
and

(H_{i},k_{i})^{\phi}=(H_{i}M,k_{i}^{\sigma})

for
2\leqslant i\leqslant r
.

Using coordinate notation similar to that used in Subsection
7.1
, define

\mathcal{G}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\langle(1,0,y),(1,e_{i},0),(\phi,e_{s+1},0):y\in K,1\leqslant i\leqslant s\rangle.

Now, if
X\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}(1\times G)\cap\mathcal{G}
, then it is clear that

X=\langle(1,0,y),(1,e_{i},0):y\in K,1\leqslant i\leqslant s\rangle

and
X^{\phi}=X
;
moreover,
(\phi,e_{s+1},0)
is an order
q
element, and

(\phi,e_{s+1},0)^{-1}(1\times X)(\phi,e_{s+1},0)=1\times X,

so, indeed,
1\times X
is normal in
\mathcal{G}
and
|\mathcal{G}|=|G|
. Next, noting that

G=\bigcup_{i=0}^{q-1}X(ie_{s+1},0),

we see that

X^{(\phi,e_{s+1},0)^{i}}=X^{(\phi^{i},ie_{s+1},0)}=X^{\phi^{i}}(ie_{s+1},0)=X(ie_{s+1},0),

and so
\mathcal{G}
is indeed transitive on the cosets of
X
in
G
. By Theorem
1.1
,
\mathcal{G}
contains a DS with McFarland parameters.

Finally, note that

\mathcal{Q}=\langle(\phi,e_{s+1},0),(1,e_{i},0):1\leqslant i\leqslant s\rangle

is a Sylow
q
-subgroup of
\mathcal{G}
. Since
e_{1}M\neq e_{1}
, we have

(\phi,e_{s+1},0)\cdot(1,e_{1},0)=(\phi,e_{1}+e_{s+1},0)\neq(\phi,e_{1}M+e_{s+1},0)=(1,e_{1},0)\cdot(\phi,e_{s+1},0),

and so
\mathcal{Q}
is nonabelian. Moreover, since

(1,0,y)^{-1}(\phi,e_{s+1},0)(1,0,y)=(\phi,e_{s+1},(-y)^{\sigma}+y)\notin\mathcal{Q},

we see that
\mathcal{G}
does not contain a normal Sylow
q
-subgroup.
∎

8.
RDSs in nonabelian
2
-groups

Let
q=2^{d}
, where
d
is a positive integer, and define
V\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}{\rm GF}(q^{2})^{2}.
Note that
V
contains
q^{2}+1

{\rm GF}(q^{2})
-hyperplanes (
1
-dimensional
{\rm GF}(q^{2})
-subspaces)
H_{0},\dots,H_{q^{2}}
. Define

G\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\langle w\rangle\times(V,+)\cong C_{q^{2}}\times C_{2}^{4d}.

Consider the automorphism
\phi:G\to G
defined by

(w^{i},(x,y))^{\phi}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}(w^{-i},(x^{q},y^{q}));

that is,
\phi
acts as inversion on
\langle w\rangle
and as a power of the Frobenius map on each coordinate of the elements of
V
. Clearly,
\phi
is an order
2
automorphism. Note that inversion has
q^{2}/2+1
orbits on
\langle w\rangle
, namely,
\{1\}
,
\{w^{q^{2}/2}\}
, and
q^{2}/2-1
orbits of the form
\{w^{i},w^{-i}\}
,
1\leqslant i\leqslant q^{2}/2-1
. Moreover, the map
(x,y)\mapsto(x^{q},y^{q})
has
q^{2}/2+2
orbits on hyperplanes, namely,
\{\langle(1,0)\rangle\}
,
\{\langle(0,1)\rangle\}
,
\{\langle(1,1)\rangle\}
, and
q^{2}/2-1
orbits of the form
\{\langle(1,\alpha^{i})\rangle,\langle(1,(\alpha^{i})^{q})\rangle\},
where
\alpha
is a primitive element of
{\rm GF}(q^{2})
.

We enumerate the hyperplanes as follows: we define
H_{0}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\langle(1,0)\rangle
,
H_{q^{2}/2}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\langle(0,1)\rangle
,
H_{q^{2}}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\langle(1,1)\rangle
, and, for the remaining hyperplanes, we assume that
\{H_{i},H_{q^{2}-i}\}
is an orbit of length two under the map
(x,y)\mapsto(x^{q},y^{q})
for each
i
,
1\leqslant i\leqslant q^{2}/2-1
.

We view the
H_{i}
as subgroups of
(V,+)
, and define

R\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\bigcup_{i=1}^{q^{2}}(w^{i},H_{i}).

Then,
R
is a
(q^{4},q^{2},q^{4},q^{2})
-RDS with respect to the forbidden subgroup
(1,H_{0})
. Moreover, by construction,

(w^{q^{2}/2},H_{q^{2}/2})^{\phi}=(w^{q^{2}/2},H_{q^{2}/2}),

(w^{q^{2}},H_{q^{2}})^{\phi}=(1,H_{q^{2}})^{\phi}=(1,H_{q^{2}}),

and, for each
1\leqslant i\leqslant q^{2}/2-1
,

(w^{i},H_{i})^{\phi}=(w^{q^{2}-i},H_{q^{2}-i}),

and so
R^{\phi}=R
.

For ease of notation, we write elements in
\langle\phi\rangle\ltimes G
in the form
(a,b,c,d)
, where
a\in\langle\phi\rangle
,
b\in\langle w\rangle
, and
(c,d)\in V
. (We will use similar notation when referring to elements of
G
.)

Theorem 8.1
.

Let
q=2^{d}
,
V={\rm GF}(q^{2})^{2}
,
\langle w\rangle\cong C_{q^{2}}
, and
G\cong\langle w\rangle\times(V,+)
. Then,

\mathcal{G}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\langle(\phi,w,0,0),(1,w^{2},0,0),(1,1,s,t):(s,t)\in V\rangle

is a nonabelian group containing a
(q^{4},q^{2},q^{4},q^{2})
-RDS.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be as in the statement. First,

\mathcal{G}\cap(1\times G)=1\times X,

where
X=\langle(w^{2},0,0),(1,s,t):(s,t)\in V\rangle
. It is clear that
|X|=|G|/2
, and, since

(\phi,w,0,0)^{2}=(\phi^{2},w^{-1}w,0,0)=(1,1,0,0)\in 1\times X,

we have
|\mathcal{G}|=|G|
and
1\times X
is normal in
\mathcal{G}
, and we see that
X^{\phi}=X
from the definition of
\phi
.
Moreover, since
G=X\cup X(w,0,0)
, we have

X^{(\phi,w,0,0)}=X^{\phi}(w,0,0)=X(w,0,0),

and hence the action of
\mathcal{G}
on right cosets of
X
in
G
is transitive. Therefore, by Theorem
1.2
,
\mathcal{G}
contains an RDS
\mathcal{R}
with the same parameters as
R
.

Finally, take
\alpha\in{\rm GF}(q^{2})\backslash{\rm GF}(q)
, which implies
\alpha^{q}\neq\alpha
. Since

(\phi,w,0,0)(1,1,\alpha,0)=(\phi,w,\alpha,0)

whereas

(1,1,\alpha,0)(\phi,w,0,0)=(\phi,w,\alpha^{q},0),

we see that
\mathcal{G}
is nonabelian, completing the proof.
∎

Define
{\rm tr}:{\rm GF}(q^{2})\to{\rm GF}(q)
to be the trace map, so
{\rm tr}(\alpha)=\alpha^{q}+\alpha
, and, given
t\in V
, define
B(t)\subset V
to be a subset such that
B(t)\cup\{t\}
is a
{\rm GF}(2)
-basis for
V
.

Theorem 8.2
.

Let
q=2^{d}
,
V={\rm GF}(q^{2})^{2}
,
\langle w\rangle\cong C_{q^{2}}
, and
G\cong\langle w\rangle\times(V,+)
. If
\beta\in{\rm GF}(q^{2})\backslash{\rm GF}(q)
, then

\mathcal{G}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\langle(\phi,1,\beta,0),(1,w,0,0),(1,1,s,t)):(s,t)\in B((\beta,0))\rangle

is a nonabelian group containing a
(q^{4},q^{2},q^{4},q^{2})
-RDS.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be as in the statement, and let
B=B((\beta,0))
.. Note that, since
\beta\notin{\rm GF}(q)
and
B\cup\{(\beta,0)\}
is a
{\rm GF}(2)
-basis for
V
, the subspace generated by
B
contains all elements of
V
of the form
(\alpha,0)
, where
\alpha\in{\rm GF}(q)
.

First,

\mathcal{G}\cap(1\times G)=1\times X,

where
X=\langle(w,0,0),(1,s,t):(s,t)\in B\rangle
. It is clear that
|X|=|G|/2
, and, since
{\rm tr}(\beta)\in{\rm GF}(q)
, we have

(\phi,1,\beta,0)^{2}=(\phi^{2},1,\beta^{q}+\beta,0)=(1,1,{\rm tr}(\beta),0)\in 1\times X,

and so
|\mathcal{G}|=|G|
,
1\times X
is normal in
\mathcal{G}
.
Now, assume
(1,\gamma,0)\notin X
. Since
\gamma=\gamma^{q^{2}}=(\gamma^{q})^{q}
and
(1,\gamma^{q}+\gamma,0)\in X
(since
\gamma^{q}+\gamma\in{\rm GF}(q)
, we have
(1,\gamma^{q},0)\notin X
as well. This shows that
X^{\phi}=X
. Moreover, since
G=X\cup X(1,\beta,0)
, we have

X^{(\phi,1,\beta,0)}=X^{\phi}(1,\beta,0)=X(1,\beta,0),

and hence the action of
\mathcal{G}
on right cosets of
X
in
G
is transitive. Therefore, by Theorem
1.2
,
\mathcal{G}
contains an RDS
\mathcal{R}
with the same parameters as
R
.

Finally, since

(\phi,1,\beta,0)(1,w,0,0)=(\phi,w,\beta,0)

whereas

(1,w,0,0)(\phi,1,\beta,0)=(\phi,w^{-1},\beta,0),

we see that
\mathcal{G}
is nonabelian, completing the proof.
∎

Remark 8.3
.

In Theorem
8.2
, we have
(\phi,1,\beta,0)\in\mathcal{U}
, the forbidden subgroup in
\mathcal{G}
with respect to
\mathcal{R}
(see the proof of Theorem
1.2
). In particular, we have

(\phi,1,\beta,0)^{2}=(1,1,{\rm tr}(\beta),0),

which shows that
(\phi,1,\beta,0)
has order
4
; that is, the forbidden subgroup
\mathcal{U}
is not elementary abelian. In fact, if
q>2
, then, as
{\rm GF}(2)
-vector spaces,
\dim({\rm GF}(q^{2}))>\dim({\rm GF}(q))+1
, so there exists
\gamma\in{\rm GF}(q^{2})\backslash{\rm GF}(q)
such that
(1,1,\gamma,0)\in(1\times X)\cap\mathcal{U}
and
\gamma^{q}\neq\gamma
. Thus, we have

(\phi,1,\beta,0)(1,1,\gamma,0)=(\phi,1,\beta+\gamma,0)

whereas

(1,1,\gamma,0)(\phi,1,\beta,0)=(\phi,1,\beta+\gamma^{q},0),

so the forbidden subgroup
\mathcal{U}
is nonabelian.

9.
Future Work

We mention here a number of potential future directions.

(1)

It seems likely to the authors that Theorem
1.1
can be applied in settings in which the (P)DS is constructed using hyperplanes and coset representatives, such as those in the Hadamard
[
37
]
, Davis-Jedwab
[
9
]
, and Chen
[
5
]
families. For example, computation in GAP
[
18
]
has shown that there are
(320,88,24)
-DSs in the Davis-Jedwab family in SmallGroup(320, 1142), SmallGroup(320, 1146), SmallGroup(320, 1151), SmallGroup(320, 1463), SmallGroup(320, 1511), and SmallGroup(320,1512), so the authors believe that an infinite family can be obtained in nonabelian groups using Theorem
1.1
.

(2)

As noted in Remark
4.3
, since PDSs in abelian groups obey Ma’s Multiplier Theorem (Lemma
4.1
). Determine the extent to which such automorphisms can be used in conjunction with Theorem
1.1
to construct new examples.

(3)

Other than the converse to Dillon’s Dihedral Trick (Theorem
3.2
), the constructions of PDSs in this paper were in groups of prime-power order. Use Theorem
1.1
to generate PDSs – such as PDSs with Paley parameters – in groups that do not have prime-power order.

(4)

Theorem
1.2
was only used in Section
8
, although there are a number of RDS constructions (see, for example,
[
26
]
). While it is known that RDSs exist in certain nonabelian groups (see
[
17
,
20
]
), it would be interesting to know the extent to which Theorem
1.2
can be used to construct new examples. For example, the authors suggest examining RDSs with parameters
(p^{2p},p^{p},p^{2p},p^{p})
, where
p
is prime.

(5)

We used Theorem
1.1
to construct several infinite families, but these examples are not necessarily the only infinite families for these (P)DSs that can be constructed this way. It would be quite interesting to see the extent to which Theorem
1.1
can used, and a classification result in the style of
[
16
]
for any of these families would be especially interesting.

(6)

While Theorem
1.1
provides a method for constructing new examples, it is not necessarily exhaustive. In order to guaranteed that all examples can be constructed using Theorem
1.1
, it must be the case that
{\rm Aut}({\rm Cay}(G,D))\cong{\rm Aut}(G)_{D}\ltimes G
; however, this is not always the case. For example, SmallGroup(100,11) and SmallGroup(100,12) each contain a
(100,36,14,12)
-PDS that give rise to the same SRG (see
[
22
]
), whereas the full automorphism group of this Cayley graph is isomorphic to
J_{2}.2
and does not contain a normal subgroup of order 100. It is worth noting, however, that computation in GAP
[
18
]
shows that one can obtain SmallGroup(100,11) by applying Theorem
1.1
to SmallGroup(100,12) (and vice versa). It would be interesting to know of any cases where Theorem
1.1
fails to construct all examples.

(7)

There has been significant interest in related combinatorial objects such as
external difference families
,
external partial difference families
, and
disjoint partial difference families
in recent years due to applications in information security and cryptography; see, for instance
[
21
]
. Is it possible to prove results analogous to Theorem
1.1
and
1.2
in such settings?

(8)

In recent work
[
1
]
, the groups of order 256 containing a Hadamard DS were classified. The next open case among
2
-groups is order 1024. It would be interesting to see which groups can be guaranteed to contain a Hadamard DS using Theorem
1.1
.
