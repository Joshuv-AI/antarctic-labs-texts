A Riemann–Hilbert Approach for the Novikov Equation This paper is a contribution to the Special Issue on Asymptotics and Universality in Random Matrices, Random Growth Processes, Integrable Systems and Statistical Physics in honor of Percy Deift and Craig Tracy. The full collection is available at http://www.emis.de/journals/SIGMA/Deift-Tracy.html
Anne BOUTET DE MONVEL †, Dmitry SHEPELSKY ‡ and Lech ZIELINSKI §

Abstract
We develop the inverse scattering transform method for the Novikov equation u t − u t ​ x ​ x + 4 ​ u 2 ​ u x = 3 ​ u ​ u x ​ u x ​ x + u 2 ​ u x ​ x ​ x u_{t}-u_{txx}+4u^{2}u_{x}=3uu_{x}u_{xx}+u^{2}u_{xxx} considered on the line x ∈ ( − ∞ , ∞ ) x\in(-\infty,\infty) in the case of non-zero constant background. The approach is based on the analysis of an associated Riemann–Hilbert (RH) problem, which in this case is a 3 × 3 3\times 3 matrix problem. The structure of this RH problem shares many common features with the case of the Degasperis–Procesi (DP) equation having quadratic nonlinear terms (see [Boutet de Monvel A., Shepelsky D., Nonlinearity 26 (2013), 2081–2107, arXiv:1107.5995]) and thus the Novikov equation can be viewed as a “modified DP equation”, in analogy with the relationship between the Korteweg–de Vries (KdV) equation and the modified Korteweg–de Vries (mKdV) equation. We present parametric formulas giving the solution of the Cauchy problem for the Novikov equation in terms of the solution of the RH problem and discuss the possibilities to use the developed formalism for further studying of the Novikov equation.

Introduction

1.1.
In this paper we present an inverse scattering approach, based on an appropriate Riemann–Hilbert problem formulation, for the initial value problem for the Novikov equation
[
29
,
37
,
39
]

\displaystyle u_{t}-u_{txx}+4u^{2}u_{x}=3uu_{x}u_{xx}+u^{2}u_{xxx},\qquad

\displaystyle-\infty<x<+\infty,\quad t>0,

(1.1)

\displaystyle u(x,0)=u_{0}(x),

\displaystyle-\infty<x<+\infty,

where
u_{0}(x)
is assumed to decay to a non-zero constant:

\displaystyle u_{0}(x)\to\varkappa>0,\qquad x\to\pm\infty.

The solution
u(x,t)
is also assumed to decay to
\varkappa
for all
t>0
:

\displaystyle u(x,t)\to\varkappa,\qquad x\to\pm\infty.

Introducing the momentum variable

\displaystyle m\coloneqq u-u_{xx},

the Novikov equation (
1.1
) can be written as

\displaystyle m_{t}+(m_{x}u+3mu_{x})u=0

(1.2)

or, equivalently,

\displaystyle\big(m^{\frac{2}{3}}\big)_{t}+\big(u^{2}m^{\frac{2}{3}}\big)_{x}=0.

(1.3)

1.2.
The Novikov equation (
1.1
) was obtained in the search for a classification of integrable generalized Camassa–Holm equations of the form

\displaystyle\big(1-\partial_{x}^{2}\big)u_{t}=F(u,u_{x},u_{xx},u_{xxx},\dots),\qquad u=u(x,t),\qquad\partial_{x}=\partial/\partial x

possessing infinite hierarchies of higher symmetries
[
39
]
(see also
[
37
]
). Equation (
1.1
) listed by Novikov in
[
39
, equation (31)]
attracted further considerable attention in the literature, first of all, as an example of nonlinear equation admitting, like the Camassa–Holm (CH)
[
13
,
14
]
and the Degasperis–Procesi (DP)
[
22
,
23
]
equations, peaked solutions (peakons), but having cubic (rather than quadratic) nonlinear terms. Another integrable Camassa–Holm type equation with cubic nonlinearities was discovered by Fokas
[
25
]
and Qiao
[
41
,
42
]
.

Hone, Lundmark, and Szmigielski
[
28
]
obtained explicit formulas for multipeakon solutions of (
1.1
). Some smooth and nonsmooth soliton solutions were presented by Pan and Yi in
[
40
]
. For studies concerned with blow-up phenomenon and the Cauchy problem for (
1.1
) we refer the reader to
[
16
,
17
,
26
,
27
,
30
,
32
,
38
,
43
,
44
]
.

1.3.
In
[
39
]
Novikov presented a scalar Lax pair for (
1.1
), which involves the third order derivative with respect to
x
. Hone and Wang
[
29
]
proposed a
3\times 3
matrix Lax pair for (
1.1
), which allowed presenting explicit formulas for peakon solutions
[
28
,
29
]
on zero background. Recently, Matsuno
[
36
]
presented parametric representations of smooth multisoliton solutions (as well as singular solitons with single cusp and double peaks) of (
1.1
) on a constant (non-zero) background, using a Hirota-type, purely algebraic procedure. He also demonstrated that a smooth soliton converges to a peakon in the limit where the constant background tends to
0
while the velocity of the soliton is fixed. Furthermore, he performed the asymptotic analysis of pure multisoliton solutions and noticed that the formulas for the phase shifts of the solitons as well as their peakon limits coincide with those for the Degasperis–Procesi (DP) equation
[
34
,
35
]

\displaystyle u_{t}-u_{txx}+4uu_{x}=3u_{x}u_{xx}+uu_{xxx},

(1.4)

which, in terms of
m
reads

\displaystyle\big(m^{\frac{1}{3}}\big)_{t}+\big(um^{\frac{1}{3}}\big)_{x}=0.

(1.5)

Comparing with the Degasperis–Procesi equation, it is natural to view (at least formally) the Novikov equation as a “modified DP equation”, in analogy with the relationship between the Korteweg–de Vries (KdV) equation

\displaystyle u_{t}+6uu_{x}+u_{xxx}=0

and the modified Korteweg–de Vries (mKdV) equation

\displaystyle u_{t}+6u^{2}u_{x}+u_{xxx}=0.

The subsequent analysis presented in the paper supports this point of view. Indeed, as we will show below, the implementation of the inverse scattering transform method involves a Riemann–Hilbert problem of the same structure as in the case of the DP equation (recall that this is true when comparing the KdV and the mKdV equations).

1.4.
Recall that for the DP equation, the transformation
u(x,t)\mapsto\tilde{u}(x-\varkappa t,t)+\varkappa
reduces (
1.4
) and (
1.5
) to

\displaystyle\tilde{u}_{t}-\tilde{u}_{txx}+3\varkappa\tilde{u}_{x}+4\tilde{u}\tilde{u}_{x}=3\tilde{u}_{x}\tilde{u}_{xx}+\tilde{u}\tilde{u}_{xxx}

(1.6)

and

\displaystyle\bigl(\tilde{m}^{\frac{1}{3}}\bigr)_{t}+\bigl(\tilde{u}\tilde{m}^{\frac{1}{3}}\bigr)_{x}=0,

respectively. Here
\tilde{m}\coloneqq\tilde{u}-\tilde{u}_{xx}+\varkappa
and thus
\tilde{u}\to 0
as
x\to\pm\infty
provided
u\to\varkappa
as
x\to\pm\infty
. Therefore, the study of the Cauchy problem for the DP equation in the form (
1.4
) in the case of non-zero constant background is equivalent to the study of the Cauchy problem on zero background for the DP equation in the form (
1.6
), i.e., for the DP equation with
non-zero linear dispersion term
. For the latter problem, the Riemann–Hilbert approach, which is a variant of the inverse scattering transform method, has been applied in
[
10
]
, which allows obtaining a useful representation of the solution in a form suitable for the analysis of its long time behavior. Notice that the further transformation
\tilde{u}\mapsto\tilde{\tilde{u}}\colon\tilde{u}(x,t)=\varkappa\tilde{\tilde{u}}(x,\varkappa t)
, which preserves the zero background, allows reducing the study of (
1.6
) with any
\varkappa>0
to the case of (
1.6
) with
\varkappa=1
.

Similar arguments for the Novikov equation lead to the following: The transformations
u(x,t)=\tilde{u}(x-\varkappa^{2}t,t)+\varkappa
and
u(x,t)=\varkappa\tilde{\tilde{u}}(x-\varkappa^{2}t,\varkappa^{2}t)+\varkappa
reduce the Cauchy problem for (
1.1
) (or (
1.3
)) on a non-zero constant background
u\to\varkappa
as
x\to\pm\infty
to the Cauchy problem on zero background (
\tilde{u}\to 0
and
\tilde{\tilde{u}}\to 0
as
x\to\pm\infty
) for the equations

\displaystyle\bigl(\tilde{m}^{\frac{2}{3}}\bigr)_{t}+\bigl(\big(\tilde{u}^{2}+2\varkappa\tilde{u}\big)\tilde{m}^{\frac{2}{3}}\bigr)_{x}=0,

(1.7)

and

\displaystyle\bigl(\tilde{\tilde{m}}^{\frac{2}{3}}\bigr)_{t}+\bigl(\big(\tilde{\tilde{u}}^{2}+2\tilde{\tilde{u}}\big)\tilde{\tilde{m}}^{\frac{2}{3}}\bigr)_{x}=0,

(1.8)

respectively. Here
\tilde{m}\coloneqq\tilde{u}-\tilde{u}_{xx}+\varkappa
and
\tilde{\tilde{m}}=\tilde{\tilde{u}}-\tilde{\tilde{u}}_{xx}+1
.

1.5.
Henceforth, we consider the Cauchy problem for equation (
1.8
) on zero background, which, to simplify notations, will be written as

\displaystyle\bigl(\hat{m}^{2/3}\bigr)_{t}+\bigl(\big(u^{2}+2u\big)\hat{m}^{2/3}\bigr)_{x}=0,\qquad

\displaystyle-\infty<x<\infty,\quad t>0,

(1.9a)

\displaystyle\hat{m}\equiv m+1=u-u_{xx}+1,

(1.9b)

\displaystyle u(x,0)=u_{0}(x),

\displaystyle-\infty<x<\infty,

(1.9c)

where
u_{0}(x)
is sufficiently smooth and decays fast as
x\to\pm\infty
. Moreover, we assume that
u_{0}(x)
satisfies the sign condition

\displaystyle u_{0}(x)-(u_{0})_{xx}(x)+1>0.

(1.9d)

Then there exists
[
32
]
a unique global solution
u(x,t)
of (
1.9
), such that
u(x,t)\to 0
as
x\to\pm\infty
for all
t
.

Notice that the Novikov equation (
1.9a
), being written in terms of
u
only, contains linear as well as quadratic dispersion terms:

\displaystyle u_{t}-u_{txx}+4u^{2}u_{x}+8uu_{x}+3u_{x}=3uu_{x}u_{xx}+u^{2}u_{xxx}+3u_{x}u_{xx}+2uu_{xxx}.

1.6.
The analysis of Camassa–Holm-type equations by using the inverse scattering approach was initiated in
[
18
,
21
,
31
,
33
]
for the Camassa–Holm equation itself:

\displaystyle u_{t}-u_{txx}+3uu_{x}=2u_{x}u_{xx}+uu_{xxx}.

A version of the inverse scattering method for the CH equation based on a Riemann–Hilbert (RH) factorization problem was proposed in
[
5
,
8
]
(another RH formulation of the inverse scattering transform is presented in
[
19
]
). The RH approach has proved its efficiency in the study of the long-time behavior of solutions of both initial value problems
[
2
,
3
,
7
]
and initial boundary value problems
[
9
]
for the CH equation. In
[
4
,
10
]
it has been adapted to the study of the Degasperis–Procesi equation.

In the present paper we develop the RH approach to the Novikov equation in the form (
1.9a
) on zero background, following the main ideas developed in
[
8
,
10
]
. To the best of our knowledge, no equations of the Camassa–Holm type with
cubic
nonlinearity have been treated before by the inverse scattering method in the form of a RH problem.

A major difference between the implementations of the RH method to the CH equation, on one hand, and to the DP as well as Novikov equation, on the other hand, is that in the latter cases, the spatial equations of the associated Lax pairs are of the third order, which implies that when rewriting them in matrix form, one has to deal with
3\times 3
matrix-valued equations, while in the case of the CH equation, they have a
2\times 2
matrix structure, as in the cases of the most known integrable equations (KdV, mKdV, nonlinear Schrödinger, sine-Gordon, etc.). Hence, the construction and analysis of the associated RH problem become considerably more complicated.

In our approach, we propose (Section
3
) an associated RH problem and give (Theorem
4.1
) a representation of the solution
u(x,t)
of the initial value problem (
1.9
) in terms of the solution of this RH problem evaluated at a distinguished point of the plane of the spectral parameter. Remarkably, the formulas for
u(x,t)
obtained in this way have the same structure as the parametric formulas obtained in
[
36
]
for pure multisoliton solutions.

Assumptions
.

Recall that we assume that the initial function
u_{0}(x)
in (
1.9c
) is sufficiently smooth with fast decay at
\pm\infty
and satisfies the sign condition (
1.9d
).

Then, similarly to the case of the CH equation (see, e.g.,
[
18
]
), the solution
\hat{m}(x,t)
of (

) satisfies the sign condition
\hat{m}(x,t)>0
for all

and all

.

2.1
Lax pairs

2.1.1
A first Lax pair

The Lax pair found by Hone and Wang
[
29
]
for the Novikov equation in the form (
1.1
) (or (
1.2
)) reads

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{1}\\
\psi_{2}\\
\psi_{3}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0&zm&1\\
0&0&zm\\
1&0&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{1}\\
\psi_{2}\\
\psi_{3}\end{pmatrix},\qquad m\coloneqq u-u_{xx},

(2.1a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{1}\\
\psi_{2}\\
\psi_{3}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-uu_{x}&\frac{u_{x}}{z}-u^{2}mz&u_{x}^{2}\vskip 2.84526pt\\
\frac{u}{z}&-\frac{1}{z^{2}}&-\frac{u_{x}}{z}-u^{2}mz\vskip 2.84526pt\\
-u^{2}&\frac{u}{z}&uu_{x}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{1}\\
\psi_{2}\\
\psi_{3}\end{pmatrix},

(2.1b)

where
z
is the
spectral parameter
.

2.1.2
A modified Lax pair

For the Novikov equation in the form (
1.9a
), the Lax pair (
2.1
) has to be appropriately modified. While for the Camassa–Holm and Degasperis–Procesi equations the corresponding modification (when passing from the equation with zero linear dispersive term to that with a non-zero one) consists simply in replacing
m
by
\hat{m}=m+1
, the modification for the Novikov equation turns out to be more involved.

Lemma 2.1
.

The Novikov equation (
1.9a
) admits as Lax pair the system

\displaystyle\Phi_{x}=U\Phi,\qquad\Phi_{t}=V\Phi,

(2.2a)

where
\Phi\equiv\Phi(x,t;z)
and

\displaystyle U(x,t;z)=\begin{pmatrix}0&z\hat{m}&1\\
0&0&z\hat{m}\\
1&0&0\end{pmatrix},

(2.2b)

\displaystyle V(x,t;z)=\begin{pmatrix}-(u+1)u_{x}+\frac{1}{3z^{2}}&\frac{u_{x}}{z}-(u^{2}+2u)\hat{m}z&u_{x}^{2}+1\\[2.84526pt]
\frac{u+1}{z}&-\frac{2}{3z^{2}}&-\frac{u_{x}}{z}-(u^{2}+2u)\hat{m}z\\[2.84526pt]
-u^{2}-2u&\frac{u+1}{z}&(u+1)u_{x}+\frac{1}{3z^{2}}\end{pmatrix}.

(2.2c)

Remark 2.2
.

The freedom in adding to
V
a constant (independent of
(x,t)
) term
c\cdot I
, where
I
is the
3\times 3
identity matrix, has been used in (
2.2c
) in order to make
V
traceless, which provides that the determinant of a matrix solution to the equation
\Phi_{t}=V\Phi
is independent of
t
. The same property holds for the equation
\Phi_{x}=U\Phi
whose coefficient
U
is obviously traceless.

The coefficient matrices
U
and
V
in (
2.2
) have singularities (in the extended complex
z
-plane) at
z=0
and at
z=\infty
. In order to control the behavior of solutions to (
2.2
) as functions of the spectral parameter
z
(which is crucial for the Riemann–Hilbert method), we follow a strategy similar to that adopted for the CH equation
[
5
,
8
]

and the DP equation
[
10
]
.

2.1.3
A Lax pair appropriate for large
\boldsymbol{z}

In order to control the large
z
behavior of the solutions of (
2.2
), we will transform this Lax pair as follows (cf.
[
1
]
):

Lemma 2.3
.

The Lax pair (
2.2
) can be transformed into a new Lax pair

\displaystyle\hat{\Phi}_{x}-Q_{x}\hat{\Phi}=\hat{U}\hat{\Phi},\qquad\hat{\Phi}_{t}-Q_{t}\hat{\Phi}=\hat{V}\hat{\Phi},

(2.3)

whose coefficients
Q(x,t;z)
,
\hat{U}(x,t;z)
, and
\hat{V}(x,t;z)
have the following properties:

(i)

Q
is diagonal;

(ii)

\hat{U}=\mathrm{O}(1)
and
\hat{V}=\mathrm{O}(1)
as
z\to\infty
, whereas
Q_{x}
is growing;

(iii)

the diagonal parts of
\hat{U}
and
\hat{V}
decay as
z\to\infty
;

(iv)

\hat{U}\to 0
and
\hat{V}\to 0
as
x\to\pm\infty
.

Proof.

As in the case of the DP equation
[
10
]
, we perform this transformation into two steps:

•

We transform (
2.2
) into a system where the leading terms are represented as products of
(x,t)
-independent (matrix-valued) and
(x,t)
-dependent (scalar) factors.

•

We diagonalize the
(x,t)
-independent factors.

First step.
Introducing
\tilde{\Phi}\equiv\tilde{\Phi}(x,t;z)
by

\displaystyle\tilde{\Phi}=D^{-1}\Phi,

where

D(x,t)=\diag\big\{q(x,t),1,q^{-1}(x,t)\big\}

and (
\hat{m}
is as in (
1.9b
))

\displaystyle q=q(x,t)\coloneqq\hat{m}^{1/3}(x,t),

(2.4)

transforms (
2.2
) into the new Lax pair

\displaystyle\tilde{\Phi}_{x}=\tilde{U}\tilde{\Phi},\qquad\tilde{\Phi}_{t}=\tilde{V}\tilde{\Phi},

(2.5a)

where

\displaystyle\tilde{U}(x,t;z)=q^{2}(x,t)\begin{pmatrix}0&z&1\\
0&0&z\\
1&0&0\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{q_{x}}{q}&0&\frac{1}{q^{2}}-q^{2}\\
0&0&0\\
0&0&\frac{q_{x}}{q}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\tilde{U}(x,t;z)}{}\equiv q^{2}(x,t)U_{\infty}(z)+\tilde{U}^{(1)}(x,t)

(2.5b)

\displaystyle\tilde{V}(x,t;z)=-\big(u^{2}+2u\big)q^{2}\begin{pmatrix}0&z&1\\
0&0&z\\
1&0&0\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{3z^{2}}&0&1\vskip 2.84526pt\\
\frac{1}{z}&-\frac{2}{3z^{2}}&0\vskip 2.84526pt\\
0&\frac{1}{z}&\frac{1}{3z^{2}}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\tilde{V}(x,t;z)=}{}+\begin{pmatrix}(u^{2}+2u)\frac{q_{x}}{q}&0&(u^{2}+2u)q^{2}+\frac{u_{x}^{2}+1}{q^{2}}-1\\
0&0&0\\
0&0&-(u^{2}+2u)\frac{q_{x}}{q}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\tilde{V}(x,t;z)=}{}+\frac{1}{z}\begin{pmatrix}0&\frac{u_{x}}{q}&0\\
(u+1)q-1&0&-\frac{u_{x}}{q}\\
0&(u+1)q-1&0\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\tilde{V}(x,t;z)}{}\equiv-\big(u^{2}(x,t)+2u(x,t)\big)q^{2}(x,t)U_{\infty}(z)+V_{\infty}(z)+\tilde{V}^{(1)}(x,t)+\frac{1}{z}\tilde{V}^{(2)}(x,t).

(2.5c)

Second step.
The commutator of
U_{\infty}
and
V_{\infty}
vanishes identically, i.e.,
[U_{\infty},V_{\infty}]\equiv 0
, which allows simultaneous diagonalization of
U_{\infty}
and
V_{\infty}
. Indeed, we have (for
z\neq 0
)

\displaystyle P^{-1}(z)U_{\infty}(z)P(z)=\Lambda(z),\qquad P^{-1}(z)V_{\infty}(z)P(z)=A(z),

where

\displaystyle\Lambda(z)=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}(z)&0&0\\
0&\lambda_{2}(z)&0\\
0&0&\lambda_{3}(z)\end{pmatrix},

(2.6a)

\displaystyle A(z)=\frac{1}{3z^{2}}I+\Lambda^{-1}(z)\equiv\begin{pmatrix}A_{1}(z)&0&0\\
0&A_{2}(z)&0\\
0&0&A_{3}(z)\end{pmatrix},

(2.6b)

\displaystyle P(z)=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}^{2}(z)&\lambda_{2}^{2}(z)&\lambda_{3}^{2}(z)\\
z&z&z\\
\lambda_{1}(z)&\lambda_{2}(z)&\lambda_{3}(z)\end{pmatrix},

(2.6c)

\displaystyle P^{-1}(z)=\left(\begin{matrix}(3\lambda_{1}^{2}(z)-1)^{-1}\!&0&0\\
0&(3\lambda_{2}^{2}(z)-1)^{-1}&0\\
0&0&\!(3\lambda_{3}^{2}(z)-1)^{-1}\end{matrix}\right)\!\left(\begin{matrix}1&\frac{z}{\lambda_{1}(z)}&\lambda_{1}(z)\\
1&\frac{z}{\lambda_{2}(z)}&\lambda_{2}(z)\\
1&\frac{z}{\lambda_{3}(z)}&\lambda_{3}(z)\end{matrix}\right).\!\!\!

(2.6d)

Here
\lambda_{1}(z)
,
\lambda_{2}(z)
, and
\lambda_{3}(z)
are the solutions of the algebraic equation

\displaystyle\lambda^{3}-\lambda=z^{2},

enumerated in such a way that
\lambda_{j}(z)\sim\omega^{j}z^{2/3}
as
z\to\infty
, where
\omega=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{2\mathrm{i}\pi}{3}}
.

Now, introducing
\hat{\Phi}\equiv\hat{\Phi}(x,t;z)
by

\displaystyle\hat{\Phi}=P^{-1}\tilde{\Phi}

transforms the Lax pair (
2.5
) into the new Lax pair:

\displaystyle\hat{\Phi}_{x}-q^{2}\Lambda(z)\hat{\Phi}=\hat{U}\hat{\Phi},

(2.7a)

\displaystyle\hat{\Phi}_{t}+\left(\big(u^{2}+2u\big)q^{2}\Lambda(z)-A(z)\right)\hat{\Phi}=\hat{V}\hat{\Phi},

(2.7b)

where

\displaystyle\hat{U}(x,t;z)=P^{-1}(z)\tilde{U}^{(1)}(x,t)P(z),

(2.7c)

\displaystyle\hat{V}(x,t;z)=P^{-1}(z)\left(\tilde{V}^{(1)}(x,t)+\frac{1}{z}\tilde{V}^{(2)}(x,t)\right)P(z).

(2.7d)

Here
\hat{U}(x,t;z)=\mathrm{O}(1)
and
\hat{V}(x,t;z)=\mathrm{O}(1)
as
z\to\infty
due to the fact that
\tilde{U}^{(1)}
and
\tilde{V}^{(1)}
are upper triangular matrices. Moreover, the fact that
\tilde{U}^{(1)}
and
\tilde{V}^{(1)}
are traceless implies that the diagonal entries of
\hat{U}(x,t;z)
and
\hat{V}(x,t;z)
are
\mathrm{O}(z^{-2/3})
as
z\to\infty
.

Indeed, we can write
\hat{U}=\hat{U}^{(1)}\hat{U}^{(2)}
, where

\displaystyle\hat{U}^{(1)}=\left(\begin{matrix}\frac{1}{3\lambda_{1}^{2}-1}&0&0\\
0&\frac{1}{3\lambda_{2}^{2}-1}&0\\
0&0&\frac{1}{3\lambda_{3}^{2}-1}\end{matrix}\right),

(2.8a)

.

We can also write

where

has the form of

with

\displaystyle\hat{V}^{(2)}=\left(\begin{matrix}2c_{6}&\begin{matrix}c_{5}\left(\frac{1}{\lambda_{2}}-\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}}\right)\\

Finally, in order to write (

\displaystyle Q_{x}=q^{2}\Lambda(z),

(2.9a)

\displaystyle Q_{t}=-\left(\big(u^{2}+2u\big)q^{2}\right)\Lambda(z)+A(z).

(2.9b)

\displaystyle y(x,t)\coloneqq x-\int_{x}^{\infty}(q^{2}(\xi,t)-1)\mathrm{d}\xi.

This solution

\displaystyle Q(x,t;z)\sim x\Lambda(z)+tA(z)\qquad\text{as}\quad x\to+\infty.

2.2
Eigenfunctions

2.2.1
Fredholm integral equations

Introducing the
3\times 3
matrix-valued function

by

\displaystyle M=\hat{\Phi}\mathrm{e}^{-Q}

reduces the Lax pair (
2.3
) to the system

\displaystyle M_{x}-[Q_{x},M]=\hat{U}M,\qquad M_{t}-[Q_{t},M]=\hat{V}M.

(2.13)

Assume that the coefficients in (
2.13
), which are expressed in terms of

, are given. Then particular solutions of (

) having well-controlled properties as functions of the spectral parameter

\displaystyle M(x,t;z)=I

\displaystyle{}+\int_{(x^{*},t^{*})}^{(x,t)}\mathrm{e}^{Q(x,t;z)-Q(\xi,\tau;z)}\big(\hat{U}M(\xi,\tau;z)\mathrm{d}\xi+\hat{V}M(\xi,\tau;z)\mathrm{d}\tau\big)\mathrm{e}^{-Q(x,t;z)+Q(\xi,\tau;z)},

where the initial points of integration

can be chosen differently for different matrix entries of the equation.

being diagonal, (

\displaystyle M_{jl}(x,t;z)=I_{jl}

\displaystyle{}+\int_{(x_{jl}^{*},t_{jl}^{*})}^{(x,t)}\mathrm{e}^{Q_{jj}(x,t;z)-Q_{jj}(\xi,\tau;z)}\bigl((\hat{U}M)_{jl}(\xi,\tau;z)\mathrm{d}\xi+(\hat{V}M)_{jl}(\xi,\tau;z)\mathrm{d}\tau\bigr)\mathrm{e}^{-Q_{ll}(x,t;z)+Q_{ll}(\xi,\tau;z)}.

Notice that choosing the
(x_{jl}^{*},t_{jl}^{*})
appropriately allows obtaining eigenfunctions which are piecewise analytic w.r.t. the spectral parameter
z
and thus can be used in the construction of Riemann–Hilbert problems associated with initial value problems
[
8
]
as well as initial boundary value problems
[

]
.

\displaystyle M(x,t;z)=I+\int_{(\pm)\infty}^{x}\mathrm{e}^{Q(x,t;z)-Q(\xi,t;z)}[\hat{U}M(\xi,t;z)]\mathrm{e}^{-Q(x,t;z)+Q(\xi,t;z)}\mathrm{d}\xi,

or, in view of (

\displaystyle M(x,t;z)=I+\int_{(\pm)\infty}^{x}\mathrm{e}^{-\left(\int_{x}^{\xi}q^{2}(\zeta,t)\mathrm{d}\zeta\right)\Lambda(z)}[\hat{U}M(\xi,t;z)]\mathrm{e}^{\big(\int_{x}^{\xi}q^{2}(\zeta,t)\mathrm{d}\zeta\big)\Lambda(z)}\mathrm{d}\xi.

Since

2.2.2
A new spectral parameter

As in the case of the Degasperis–Procesi equation (see

\displaystyle z^{2}(k)=\frac{1}{3\sqrt{3}}\left(k^{3}+\frac{1}{k^{3}}\right).

We thus have

\displaystyle\lambda_{j}=\lambda_{j}(z(k))=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\left(\omega^{j}k+\frac{1}{\omega^{j}k}\right),\qquad\text{where}\quad\omega=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{2\mathrm{i}\pi}{3}}.

(2.18)

In what follows, we will work in the complex

for

for

and

for

The

In order that (

\displaystyle M_{jl}(x,t;k)=I_{jl}+\int_{\infty_{j,l}}^{x}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{j}(k)\int_{x}^{\xi}q^{2}(\zeta,t)\mathrm{d}\zeta}[(\hat{U}M)_{jl}(\xi,t;k)]\mathrm{e}^{\lambda_{l}(k)\int_{x}^{\xi}q^{2}(\zeta,t)\mathrm{d}\zeta}\mathrm{d}\xi,

In spite of the fact that some of the coefficients in (

Proposition 2.5

(analyticity)
.

Let

)

(ii)

(iii)

The proof follows the same lines as in

Proposition 2.6

(symmetries)
.

The solution
M(x,t;k)
of (
2.20
) satisfies the symmetry relations:

(S1)

\Gamma_{1}\overline{M(x,t;\bar{k})}\Gamma_{1}=M(x,t;k)
, where
\Gamma_{1}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1&0\\
1&0&0\\
0&0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)
;

(S2)

\Gamma_{2}\overline{M(x,t;\bar{k}\omega^{2})}\Gamma_{2}=M(x,t;k)
, where
\Gamma_{2}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&0&1\\
0&1&0\\
1&0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
;

(S3)

\Gamma_{3}\overline{M(x,t;\bar{k}\omega)}\Gamma_{3}=M(x,t;k)
, where
\Gamma_{3}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&0&1\\
0&1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
;

(S4)

M(x,t;\frac{1}{k})=\overline{M(x,t;\bar{k})}
.

Proof.

Indeed, the diagonal entries of the matrix
\Lambda(k)=\diag(\lambda_{j}(k))
satisfy the following relations:

\displaystyle\overline{\lambda_{1}(\bar{k})}=\lambda_{2}(k),\quad

\displaystyle\overline{\lambda_{3}(\bar{k})}=\lambda_{3}(k),

\displaystyle\overline{\lambda_{2}(\bar{k}\omega)}=\lambda_{3}(k),\qquad

\displaystyle\overline{\lambda_{1}(\bar{k}\omega)}=\lambda_{1}(k),

\displaystyle\overline{\lambda_{1}(\bar{k}\omega^{2})}=\lambda_{3}(k),\qquad

\displaystyle\overline{\lambda_{2}(\bar{k}\omega^{2})}=\lambda_{2}(k),\qquad

\displaystyle\lambda_{j}(1/k)=\overline{\lambda_{j}(\bar{k})},\quad j=1,2,3.

∎

(i-1) Let

Starting from a solution

3.1
A Lax pair appropriate for small

Coming back to the original Lax pair (

\displaystyle\tilde{\Phi}^{(0)}_{t}-A(k)\tilde{\Phi}^{(0)}=\tilde{V}^{(0)}\tilde{\Phi}^{(0)},

4
Riemann–Hilbert problem

In Section
3
we have shown how we can express the solution
u
of the Cauchy problem for the Novikov equation by evaluating certain eigenfunctions – solutions of the Lax pair equations. Notice they were defined using the solution
u
itself.

In the framework of the Riemann–Hilbert approach to the Cauchy problem for an integrable nonlinear equation, one is looking for obtaining these eigenfunctions in terms of the solution of an appropriate factorization problem, of Riemann–Hilbert type. The factorization problem is formulated in the complex plane of a spectral parameter
k
whereas
x
and
t
play the role of parameters, and the data for this problem are uniquely determined, in spectral terms, by the initial data for the Cauchy problem.

4.1
RH problem satisfied by
\boldsymbol{\hat{M}}

Let
M(x,t;k)
be as in Section
3
, solution of the system of integral equations (
2.20
). The key observation is that the limiting values
M_{\pm}(x,t;k)
(on
l_{\nu}
) of
M(x,t;k^{\prime})
as
k^{\prime}\to k
from the positive or negative side of
l_{\nu}
,
\nu=1,\dots,6
are related as follows:

\displaystyle M_{+}(x,t;k)=M_{-}(x,t;k)\mathrm{e}^{Q(x,t;k)}S_{0}(k)\mathrm{e}^{-Q(x,t;k)},\qquad k\in l_{\nu}.

(4.1)

We indeed have
M_{\pm}=\hat{\Phi}_{\pm}\mathrm{e}^{-Q}
where
\hat{\Phi}_{\pm}
are two solutions of the system of ordinary differential equations (
2.7
). They are then related by
\hat{\Phi}_{+}=\hat{\Phi}_{-}S_{0}
where
S_{0}
is a matrix independent of
(x,t)
. Considering (
4.1
) at
t=0
we see that
S_{0}(k)
is completely determined by
u(x,0)
, i.e., by the initial data for the Cauchy problem (
1.9
), via the solution
M(x,0;k)
of the system (
2.20
) whose coefficients are determined by
u(x,0)
:

\displaystyle S_{0}(k)=\mathrm{e}^{-Q(x,0;k)}M_{-}^{-1}(x,0;k)M_{+}(0,0;k)\mathrm{e}^{Q(x,0;k)}

(recall that
Q(x,0;k)=\left(x-\int_{x}^{\infty}(q^{2}(\xi,0)-1)\mathrm{d}\xi\right)\Lambda(k)
and thus

is also determined by
u(x,0)
).

Moreover,
S_{0}(k)
has a special matrix structure: for
k\in l_{1}\cup l_{4}\equiv\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle S_{0}(k)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
-r(k)&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&\overline{r(k)}&0\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix},

(4.2)

where
r(k)\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
and
r(k)=\mathrm{O}(k^{-1})
as
k\to\pm\infty
(this structure follows from the analysis of the behavior of

as

; for the details, see
[
10
, Section 3.1]
), whereas the expression of

for the other parts of
\Sigma
follows from the symmetries stated in Proposition
2.6
. Thus the jump matrices on all parts of the contour are determined in terms of a single scalar function, the
reflection coefficient

r(k)
.

On the other hand, in order to have explicit exponentials in the r.h.s. of (
4.1
), one replaces the pair of parameters
(x,t)
by
(y,t)
, where
y=y(x,t)
was introduced in (
2.12
), see also (
2.11
). Following this observation, (
4.1
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\hat{M}_{+}(y,t;k)=\hat{M}_{-}(y,t;k)\mathrm{e}^{y\Lambda(k)+tA(k)}S_{0}(k)\mathrm{e}^{-y\Lambda(k)-tA(k)},\qquad k\in\Sigma

(4.3)

where
\Sigma=l_{1}\cup\dots\cup l_{6}
and

\displaystyle M(x,t;k)=\hat{M}(y(x,t),t;k).

(4.4)

Now (
4.3
) says that
\hat{M}
– as in (
4.4
) – is solution of a factorization problem:

Factorization problem
.

Given
S_{0}(k)
,
k\in\Sigma
, find a piece-wise (w.r.t.
\Sigma
) meromorphic (in
k
),
3\times 3
matrix-valued function
\hat{M}(y,t;k)
(
y
and
t
are parameters), whose limiting values satisfy the jump condition (
4.3
).

This condition, being supplemented by conditions at possible poles of
\hat{M}(y,t;k)
, by a normalization condition, and by certain structural conditions, will constitute the RH problem satisfied by
\hat{M}
.

Additional conditions
.

The dependence on
k
of the eigenvalues
\lambda_{j}(k)
in (

) is exactly the same as in the case of the DP equation, which implies that most conditions involved in the RH problem for the Novikov equation have exactly the same form as for the Degasperis–Procesi equation, cf.
[
10
]
.

1.

For the residue conditions at poles (if any)

4.2
Main theorem

Starting directly from the main RH problem we show how its solution gives a representation of the solution of the Cauchy problem for the Novikov equation.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
u_{0}(x)
satisfy assumptions made for the Cauchy problem (
1.9
) for the Novikov equation. Let
\hat{M}(y,t;k)
be the solution of the associated main RH problem
(1)–(6)
. By evaluating
\hat{M}
at
k=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\mathrm{i}\pi}{6}}
we get the parametric representation (
3.5
) for the solution

of the Cauchy problem (
1.9
):

\displaystyle u(x,t)=\hat{u}(y(x,t),t),

where
x(y,t)
and
\hat{u}(y,t)
are given by

\displaystyle x(y,t)=y+\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{\hat{M}_{33}\big(y,t;\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\mathrm{i}\pi}{6}}\big)}{\hat{M}_{11}\big(y,t;\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\mathrm{i}\pi}{6}}\big)},

\displaystyle\hat{u}(y,t)=\frac{1}{2}\hat{N}_{1}(y,t)\left(\frac{\hat{M}_{33}\big(y,t;\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\mathrm{i}\pi}{6}}\big)}{\hat{M}_{11}\big(y,t;\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\mathrm{i}\pi}{6}}\big)}\right)^{1/2}+\frac{1}{2}\hat{N}_{3}(y,t)\left(\frac{\hat{M}_{33}\big(y,t;\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\mathrm{i}\pi}{6}}\big)}{\hat{M}_{11}\big(y,t;\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\mathrm{i}\pi}{6}}\big)}\right)^{-1/2}-1,

with
\hat{N}_{k}(y,t):=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{3}\hat{M}_{jk}\big(y,t;\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\mathrm{i}\pi}{6}}\big)
,

4.3.1
Soliton-type solutions

In the case
r(k)\equiv 0
, solving the RH problem reduces to solving a system of linear algebraic equations associated with the residue conditions. Following this way, one can describe not only smooth classical solutions, but also singular and/or multivalued solutions associated with breaks of bijectivity in the change of variables
x\mapsto y
(see, e.g.,
[
11
,
12
]
).

4.3.2
Long-time asymptotics

Noticing that the form and the
k
-dependence of the jump matrix, see (
4.3
) and (
4.2
), are the same as in the case of the DP equation, a reasonable conjecture is that the long-time analysis for the Novikov equation can be done following the same steps as for the DP equation
[
10
]
, by adapting the nonlinear steepest descent method by Deift and Zhou
[
24
]
.

But there is a problem that prevents from transferring the long-time analysis literally from the case of the DP equation to the Novikov equation. Indeed, in
[
4
,
10
]
the long-time analysis was actually done for a matrix RH problem regular at the points
\varkappa_{1},\dots,\varkappa_{6}
whereas the original RH problem, as in the case of the Novikov equation, was singular at these points. This discrepancy disappeared in
[
4
,
10
]
after left multiplying by the row vector
(\begin{matrix}1&1&1\end{matrix})
and thus passing from the matrix to a (regular) row vector RH problem and exploiting the uniqueness of the solution of the latter problem. In turn, the solution of the row vector RH problem evaluated at
\kappa_{1},\dots,\kappa_{6}
, and particularly, at
k=\kappa_{1}=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\mathrm{i}\pi}{6}}
, gave, in a simple way, the quantity
\mathrm{e}^{x(y,t)-y}
, from which it was straightforward to deduce a parametric representation for
u(x,t)
, see (
3.5
).

Notice that a similar situation takes place for other Camassa–Holm-type equations including the Camassa–Holm equation itself, see
[
5
,
8
]
. On the contrary, in the case of the Novikov equation, the situation is different. Indeed, the solution of the similar row vector RH problem cannot be directly used to recover
\mathrm{e}^{x(y,t)-y}
since, being evaluated at
k=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\mathrm{i}\pi}{6}}
, it has the form

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}(1+u+u_{x})\mathrm{e}^{y-x}&1&(1+u-u_{x})\mathrm{e}^{x-y}\end{pmatrix},

left multiplying (
3.4
) by the row vector
(\begin{matrix}1&1&1\end{matrix})
. This issue has forced us to use instead the solution of the matrix RH problem (
3.4
) in (
3.5
). But now there is an (open) problem of relating the solution of the original (singular) problem to the solution of the limiting (as
t\to\infty
) regular RH problem. In
[
4
,
10
]
, it was the latter problem that was used for obtaining the main asymptotic term of
u(x,t)
in an explicit form, with parameters determined by the initial data in terms of the associated reflection coefficient.
