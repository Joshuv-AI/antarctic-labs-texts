Some applications of representation theory to the sum–product phenomenon
I.D. Shkredov

Abstract




Annotation.

In our paper, we introduce a new method for estimating incidences via representation theory.
We obtain several applications to various sums with multiplicative characters and to Zaremba’s conjecture from number theory.

1
Introduction

Given two finite sets
A
and
B
of an abelian ring, define the sumset, and the product set of
A
and
B
as

A+B=\{a+b:a\in A,\,b\in B\},\quad\quad A\cdot B=\{ab:a\in A,\,b\in B\}\,.

(1)

The sum-product phenomena was introduced by Erdős and Szemerédi in paper
[
9
]
where they proved that for an arbitrary finite subset
A
of integers one has

\max\{|A+A|,|A\cdot A|\}\gg|A|^{1+c}\,.

(2)

Here
c>0
is an absolute constant and
Erdős and Szemerédi conjectured that any
c<1
is admissible, at the cost of the implicit constant.
As a general heuristic, the conjecture suggests that either
A+A
or
AA
is significantly larger then the original set, unless
A
is close to a subring.
Even more generally speaking, the sum–product phenomenon predicts that the
an arbitrary subset of a ring cannot have good additive and multiplicative structures simultaneously.
The interested reader may consult
[
38
]
for a rather thorough treatment of sumsets and related questions, including some prior work on the sum-product problem.
The sum-product phenomenon has been extensively studied in the last few decades, the current records as of writing being

[
31
]
for real numbers, and
[
23
]
for sufficiently small sets in finite fields.

In our paper we consider the case of the ring
\mathbb{Z}_{q}:=\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}
and we have deal with

large
sets
A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{q}
(basically, it means that
|A|>q^{1-\kappa}
for a certain constant
\kappa>0
).
In the case of a prime
q
the behaviour of the maximum from (
2
) is fully known thanks to the beautiful result of Garaev
[
13
]
who used some classical exponential sums bounds in his proof. Another approach was suggested
in

[
42
]
and in
[
28
]
where some finite geometry considerations were applied.
For example, Vinh
[
42
]
proved that for an arbitrary prime
q
and any two sets
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{q}\times\mathbb{Z}_{q}
,
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{q}\times\mathbb{Z}_{q}
one has

\left||\{(a_{1},a_{2})\in\mathcal{A},\,(b_{1},b_{2})\in\mathcal{B}~:~a_{1}b_{1}-a_{2}b_{2}\equiv 1\pmod{q}\}|-\frac{|\mathcal{A}|\mathcal{B}|}{q}\right|\leqslant\sqrt{q|\mathcal{A}||\mathcal{B}|}\,.

(3)

In the proof he used the fact that equation (
3
) can be interpreted as a question about points/lines incidences.
Clearly, the result above has the sum–product flavour and indeed one can use (
3
) to derive some lower bounds for the maximum from (
2
) (in the case of large subsets of
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
, of course).

In this paper we introduce a new method of estimating sum–product quantities as in (
3
) which does not use any exponential sums, as well as any considerations from the incidence geometry. It turns out that representation theory
makes it possible
to obtain (almost automatically) asymptotic formulae for the number of solutions to systems of equations that are preserved by the actions of certain groups.
For example, equation (
3
) can be interpreted as the equation
\det\left({\begin{array}[]{cc}a&b\\
c&d\\
\end{array}}\right)=1
, where
(a_{1},a_{2})\in\mathcal{A}
and
(b_{1},b_{2})\in\mathcal{B}
and hence the equation respects the usual action of
{\rm SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{q})
.
The advantage of
our
approach is its generality and (relative) simplicity.
First of all, having a certain equation, the method makes it possible to obtain
an asymptotic formula for the number of solutions to the equation
for composite
q
due to the fact that representation theory for composite
q
is usually not so complicated and can be reduced to the case of prime powers.
We should mention that the question about the sum–product phenomenon for general
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
and large sets is considered to be difficult and there are few results in this direction, see

[
39
]
and paper

[
29
]
, where the case of finite valuation rings was considered (also, see
[
8
]
).
Another
statement
of the problem concerning the sum–product results in
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
is contained in

[
10
]
,
[
14
]
,

[
35
]
.
Let us remark that
in
[
10
]
Fish
also uses the property of equation invariance, but combines it with classical Fourier analysis.
Secondly, due to the obvious observation that representation theory deals with some facts concerning the acting group but not with sets, in all our results all the sets involved (as
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}
in (
3
)) are absolutely general and do not require to have a special structure, for example, to be Cartesian products of some other sets.
The last constraint is sometimes crucial for Fourier analysis manipulations, see, e.g.,
[
1
]
,
[
41
]
, although it usually allows to obtain better error terms in
asymptotic formulae.

To be more specific
let us mention
just one result here (see Theorem
24
of Section
3
below).
Given positive integers
q
,
n,m
,
d=n+m
, an element
\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}
and sets
\mathcal{A}\subseteq(\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{q})^{n}
,
\mathcal{B}\subseteq(\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{q})^{m}
define by
\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
the number of solutions to the equation

\det(a_{1},\dots,a_{n},b_{1},\dots,b_{m})\equiv\lambda\pmod{q}\,,\quad\quad(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\in\mathcal{A},\,\quad(b_{1},\dots,b_{m})\in\mathcal{B}\,.

(4)

Theorem 1

Let
q
be an odd prime number and
\lambda\neq 0
. Then

\left|\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})-\frac{|\mathcal{A}||\mathcal{B}|}{q-1}\right|\ll q^{d^{2}/2-d/4-3/4}\sqrt{|\mathcal{A}||\mathcal{B}|}\,.

(5)

In Section
4
we obtain further applications of our approach to some problems of number theory.
Our main observation is that the representation theory of
{\rm SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{q})

makes it easy
to insert
multiplicative
characters into all formulae with incidences and, therefore, to obtain non–trivial estimates for the corresponding exponential sums. In the author opinion this is a rather interesting phenomenon due to the widely–known fact that results with multiplicative characters are usually very difficult to obtain.
As an example, we formulate the following theorem concerning summation over a hyperbolic surface.
Denote by
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{C}
the unit disk.

Theorem 2

Let
q
be a prime number,
\delta>0
be a real number,
A,B,X,Y\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{q}
be sets, let
\chi
be a non–principal multiplicative character and
|X||Y|\geqslant q^{\delta}
.
Also, let

c_{A}:A\to\mathcal{D}
,
c_{B}:B\to\mathcal{D}

be some weights.
Then there is
\varepsilon(\delta)>0
such that

\sum_{a\in A,\,b\in B,\,x\in X,\,y\in Y~:~(a+x)(b+y)=1}c_{A}(a)c_{B}(b)\chi(a+x)\leqslant\sqrt{|A||B|}(|X||Y|)^{1-\varepsilon(\delta)}\,.

Another application of the approach allows us to generalize
[
34
, Theorem 4]
(also, see Theorem 33 from this paper). Let
\chi
be a non–principal multiplicative character over a finite field
\mathbb{F}
.
Consider the Kloosterman sum twisted by the character
\chi
, namely,

K_{\chi}(n,m)=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}\setminus\{0\}}\chi(x)e(nx+mx^{-1})\,,

where
e(\cdot)
is an additive character on
\mathbb{F}
.
We are interested in bilinear forms of Kloosterman sums (motivation can be found, say, in
[
21
]
,
[
34
]
,
[
36
]
)
that is, the sums of the form

S_{\chi}(\alpha,\beta)=\sum_{n,m}\alpha(n)\beta(m)K_{\chi}(n,m)\,,

where
\alpha:\mathbb{F}\to\mathbb{C}
,
\beta:\mathbb{F}\to\mathbb{C}
are arbitrary functions.

Theorem 3

Let
c>0
,
\chi
be a non–principal multiplicative character and
q
be a prime number.
Let
t_{1},t_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
,
N,M
be integers,
N,M\leqslant q^{1-c}

and let

\alpha,\beta:\mathbb{Z}_{q}\to\mathbb{C}
be functions supported on
\{1,\dots,N\}+t_{1}
and
\{1,\dots,M\}+t_{2}
, respectively.
Then there exists
\delta=\delta(c)>0

such that

S_{\chi}(\alpha,\beta)\lesssim\|\alpha\|_{2}\|\beta\|_{2}q^{1-\delta}\,.

(6)

Besides, if

M^{2}<pN
, then

S_{\chi}(\{1,\dots,N\}+t_{1},\beta)\lesssim\|\beta\|_{2}\left(N^{3/7}M^{1/7}p^{13/14}+N^{3/4}p^{3/4}+N^{1/4}p^{13/12}\right)\,.

(7)

Our bounds
(
6
), (
7
) (also, see much more general Corollary
40
below)
are non–trivial and do not seem to be covered by
[
21
]
,
[
36
]
results, which require some additional restrictions on
\chi
.

Finally, we obtain an application to Zaremba’s
conjecture

[
44
]
.
Recall the main result of
[
25
]
.

Theorem 4

Let
q
be a positive
sufficiently large
integer
with sufficiently large prime factors.
Then there is a positive integer
a
,
(a,q)=1
and

M=O(\log q/\log\log q)

(8)

such that

\frac{a}{q}=[0;c_{1},\dots,c_{s}]=\cfrac{1}{c_{1}+\cfrac{1}{c_{2}+\cfrac{1}{c_{3}+\cdots+\cfrac{1}{c_{s}}}}}\,,\quad\quad c_{j}\leqslant M\,,\quad\quad\forall j\in[s]\,.

(9)

Also, if
q
is a sufficiently large square–free number, then (
8
), (
9
) take place.

Finally, if
q=p^{n}
,
p
is an arbitrary prime, then (
8
), (
9
) hold for sufficiently large
n
.

Using an idea from representation theory, one can generalize Theorem
4
.

Theorem 5

Let
q
be a
sufficiently large prime number and

\Gamma\leqslant\mathbb{Z}_{q}

be a multiplicative subgroup,

|\Gamma|\gg\frac{q}{\log^{\kappa}q}\,,

(10)

\kappa>0

Then there is

a\in\Gamma
and

M=O(\log q/\log\log q)

(11)

such that

\frac{a}{q}=[0;c_{1},\dots,c_{s}]\,,\quad\quad c_{j}\leqslant M\,,\quad\quad\forall j\in[s]\,.

Some results of this type
concerning

[

]
and
[

Definitions and preliminaries

Let
{\mathbf{G}}
be a group (commutative or not) and
A,B
be some subsets of
{\mathbf{G}}
.
The sumset (and the product set) of
A
and
B
was defined in (
1
). Let us write
A\dotplus B
if for finite sets
A
,
B
one has
|A+B|=|A||B|
.
We use a representation function notation such as
r_{AB}(x)
or
r_{AB^{-1}}(x)
, which counts the number of ways
x\in{\mathbf{G}}
can be expressed as the product
ab
or
ab^{-1}
with
a\in A
,
b\in B
, respectively.
For example,
|A|=r_{AA^{-1}}(1)
.
Let us write
r^{(k)}_{A}
for
r_{A\dots A}
, where the set
A
is taken
k
times.
Having real functions
f_{1},\dots,f_{2k}:{\mathbf{G}}\to\mathbb{C}

(let
k
be an even number for concreteness), we put

\mathsf{T}_{k}(f_{1},\dots,f_{2k})=\sum_{a_{1}a^{-1}_{2}\dots a_{k-1}a^{-1}_{k}=a_{k+1}a^{-1}_{k+2}\dots a_{2k-1}a^{-1}_{2k}}f_{1}(a_{1})\dots f_{2k}(a_{2k})\,.

We denote the Fourier transform of a function
f:{\mathbf{G}}\to\mathbb{C}
by
\widehat{f},
namely,

\widehat{f}(\xi)=\sum_{x\in{\mathbf{G}}}f(x)\xi(x)\,,

(12)

where

\xi

is an additive character on
{\mathbf{G}}
.
In this paper we use the same letter to denote a set
A\subseteq{\mathbf{G}}
and its characteristic function
A:{\mathbf{G}}\to\{0,1\}
.
Finally, if
|{\mathbf{G}}|<\infty
, then we consider the balanced function
f_{A}
of
A
, namely,
f_{A}(x):=A(x)-|A|/|{\mathbf{G}}|
.

In this paper we
have deal with
the group
{\rm SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{q})\leqslant{\rm GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{q})
of matrices

g=\left({\begin{array}[]{cc}a&b\\
c&d\\
\end{array}}\right)=(ab|cd)=(a,b|c,d)\,,\quad\quad a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}\,,\quad\quad\det(g)=ad-bc=1\,,

which acts on the project line (in the case of a prime number
q
) via the formula
gx=\frac{ax+b}{cx+d}
and naturally acting on
\mathbb{Z}_{q}\times\mathbb{Z}_{q}
for an arbitrary
q
.

Now we give a simplified version of the special case of
[
20
, Theorem 6]

(also, see

[
17
, Theorem 3]
).
Let
p
be a prime number,
d
be a positive integer,
V(\mathbb{Z}_{p^{d}})
be a vector space over
\mathbb{Z}_{p^{d}}
,
{\rm dim}\,V(\mathbb{Z}_{p^{d}})=n
on which a non–degenerate symmetric bilinear form
\Phi(\cdot,\cdot)
is given.
The group of isometries of

is called the

V(\mathbb{Z}_{p^{d}})

O_{n}(\mathbb{Z}_{p^{d}})
and the subgroup of isometries with determinant one is called the

Theorem 6

Let
p
be a prime number,
p\geqslant 5
,
d
be a positive integer,

V(\mathbb{Z}_{p^{d}})
be a vector space over
\mathbb{Z}_{p^{d}}
,
and
\Phi(x_{1},\dots,x_{n};y_{1},\dots,y_{n})=x_{1}y_{1}+\dots+x_{n}y_{n}
defined on
V(\mathbb{Z}_{p^{d}})\times V(\mathbb{Z}_{p^{d}})
.
Suppose that
\Gamma
is a normal subgroup of
SO_{n}(\mathbb{Z}_{p^{d}})
, where
n\geqslant 3
,

.
Then
\Gamma
is a congruence subgroup with the quotient isomorphic to
SO_{n}(\mathbb{Z}_{p^{r}})
,

.

Indeed, in
[
20
, Theorem 6]
it requires to calculate the center of

, which is trivial as one can easily check (or consult
[
17
, Lemma 1]
for general

).
Further one needs to find an isotropic vector
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\neq 0
such that
\Phi(x,x)=0
and this is an obvious task to do as the equation

has a nonzero solution (and hence a solution modulo

by Hensel’s lemma).
Finally, notice that in the case

one can in principle use
[

(in
[

the author considers the case

Basic facts of representation theory can be found in
[
18
]
. Recall that a representation
\rho
of a group
{\mathbf{G}}

is called

We need some number–theoretic functions. Given a positive integer

we write
\tau(n)
for the number of all divisors of

.

The signs
\ll

, we write
\ll_{M}
and

.
If

, then we write
a\sim_{M}b
.
All logarithms are to base

.
We write

.
By

\mathbb{F}_{p}=\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}

.
Finally, let us denote by

.

3
Applications to incidence problems

We start with the simplest question about points/hyperplanes incidences (see equation (
13
) below).
This problem was considered before in

[
43
]
,
[
40
]
, where the authors obtained better asymptotic formulae for the quantity
\mathcal{I}_{\lambda}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
using other approaches.
We commence with equation (
13
) because it allows us to transparently demonstrate our method, and because
we will use some of the calculations from the proof below.
As we will see the proof of Theorem
7
exploits some facts about representation theory of
SO_{n}(\mathbb{Z}_{q})
, which preserves the distance
x_{1}^{2}+\dots+x_{n}^{2}
in
\mathbb{Z}^{n}_{q}
.
Thus, our approach is applicable in principle to all distance problems, for example, to the well–known
Erdős–Falconer distance problem
see, e.g.,
[
16
]
.

Given positive integers
q,n\geqslant 2
, an element
\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}
and sets
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{n}_{q}
,
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{n}_{q}
consisting of tuples all coprime to
q
,
define by
\mathcal{I}_{\lambda}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
the number of solutions to the equation

a_{1}b_{1}+\dots+a_{n}b_{n}\equiv\lambda\pmod{q}\,.

(13)

Theorem 7

Let
q,n\geqslant 2
be positive integers,
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{n}_{q}
,
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{n}_{q}
be sets and
\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}_{q}
.
Let
m
be the least prime divisor of
q
and suppose that
m\geqslant 5
.
Then

\left|\mathcal{I}_{\lambda}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})-\frac{|\mathcal{A}||\mathcal{B}|}{q\prod_{p|q}(1-p^{-n})}\right|\leqslant 2q^{n-1}\sqrt{|\mathcal{A}||\mathcal{B}|}\cdot(\Theta(n)m^{-n_{*}})^{1/4}\,,

(14)

where
n_{*}=1
for
n=2,3
and
n_{*}=n-3
for
n\geqslant 4

and further,

\Theta(2)\ll\min\{\tau(q),\log_{m}q\}
,

\Theta(3)\ll\min\{\log\omega(q),1+\omega(q)/m\}
and
\Theta(n)\ll 1
for
n\geqslant 4
.

P r o o f.

Let
q=p^{\omega_{1}}_{1}\dots p^{\omega_{t}}_{t}
, where
m=p_{1}<p_{2}<\dots<p_{t}
are primes and
\omega_{j}
are positive integers.
Also, let
a=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
,
b=(b_{1},\dots,b_{n})
and let
M(a,b)=1
iff the pair
(a,b)
satisfies our equation (
13
).
Considering the unitary decomposition of the hermitian matrix
M(a,b)
, we obtain

M(a,b)=\sum_{j=1}^{q^{n}}\mu_{j}u_{j}(a)\overline{u}_{j}(b)\,,

(15)

where
\mu_{1}\geqslant\mu_{2}\geqslant\dots
are the eigenvalues and
u_{j}
are correspondent orthonormal eigenfunctions.
Clearly,

\mathcal{I}_{\lambda}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A},b\in\mathcal{B}}M(a,b)=\sum_{j=1}^{q^{n}}\mu_{j}\langle\mathcal{A},u_{j}\rangle\overline{\langle\mathcal{B},u_{j}\rangle}\,.

Let
N=J_{n}(q)
.
By the definition of the Jordan totient function the number of vectors
a=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
such that
a_{1},\dots,a_{n},q
are coprime is exactly
N
.
It is easy to see that
\mu_{1}=q^{n-1}
and
u_{1}(x)=N^{-1/2}(1,\dots,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
Indeed, we fix
b
and thanks to the Chinese remainder theorem we need to we solve linear equation (
13
) modulo
p^{\omega_{j}}_{j}
,
j\in[t]
.
Since
\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}_{q}
and hence
\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}_{p^{\omega_{j}}_{j}}
for all
j\in[t]
, it follows that not all coefficients of (
13
) are divided by
p_{j}
and hence there are
p^{\omega_{j}(n-1)}_{j}
solutions modulo
p^{\omega_{j}}_{j}
. Hence there are
q^{n-1}
solutions in total.
Thus
we obtain

\mathcal{I}_{\lambda}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})-\frac{q^{n-1}|\mathcal{A}||\mathcal{B}|}{N}=\sum_{j=2}^{q^{n}}\mu_{j}\langle\mathcal{A},u_{j}\rangle\overline{\langle\mathcal{B},u_{j}\rangle}:=\mathcal{E}\,.

(16)

By the orthonormality of
u_{j}
and the Hölder inequality, we get

|\mathcal{E}|\leqslant|\mu_{2}|\sqrt{|\mathcal{A}||\mathcal{B}|}\,.

(17)

Thus it remains
to estimate
the second eigenvalue
\mu_{2}

and to do this we calculate the rectangular norm of the matrix
M
, that is

\sum_{j=1}^{q^{n}}|\mu_{j}|^{4}=\sum_{a,a^{\prime}}\left|\sum_{b}M(a,b)M(a^{\prime},b)\right|^{2}:=\sigma\,,

and then
|\mu_{2}|
.
Fixing a pair
(a,a^{\prime})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}_{q}\times\mathbb{Z}^{n}_{q}
, we need to solve the system of two linear equations

a_{1}b_{1}+\dots+a_{n}b_{n}\equiv\lambda\pmod{q}\,,\quad\quad a^{\prime}_{1}b_{1}+\dots+a^{\prime}_{n}b_{n}\equiv\lambda\pmod{q}\,.

(18)

It implies, in particular, that

\sum_{j=2}^{n}b_{j}(a^{\prime}_{1}a_{j}-a_{1}a^{\prime}_{j})\equiv\lambda(a_{1}^{\prime}-a_{1})\pmod{q}\,,

(19)

and if
a_{1}^{\prime}-a_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{*}
, say, then we obtain
q^{n-2}
solutions by the previous argument.
If not, then consider all possible determinants of
2\times 2
matrices consisting of the elements of the matrix
(1,a_{1},\dots,a_{n}|1,a^{\prime}_{1},\dots,a^{\prime}_{n})
.
Further given a tuple
(r_{1},\dots,r_{n})
, where
0\leqslant r_{j}\leqslant\omega_{j}
we consider the set
\mathcal{A}(r_{1},\dots,r_{n})
of pairs

(a,a^{\prime})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}_{q}\times\mathbb{Z}^{n}_{q}

such that
p^{r_{j}}_{j}
is the maximal divisor of all these determinants.
If
(a,a^{\prime})\in\mathcal{A}(r_{1},\dots,r_{n})
, then
a_{j}\equiv a^{\prime}_{j}\pmod{p^{r_{j}}_{j}}
and hence

|\mathcal{A}(r_{1},\dots,r_{n})|\leqslant\frac{q^{2n}}{\prod_{j=1}^{t}p^{r_{j}n}_{j}}\,.

(20)

To solve (
19
) (recall that we consider the case when
(a,a^{\prime})\in\mathcal{A}(r_{1},\dots,r_{n})
)
one can use
the Chinese remainder theorem again and we see that there are

\prod_{j=1}^{t}p^{\omega_{j}(n-2)+r_{j}}_{j}=q^{n-2}\prod_{j=1}^{t}p^{r_{j}}_{j}

solutions to equation (
19
).
Combining the last bound with (
20
), we obtain

\sigma\leqslant q^{4n-4}\sum_{r_{1}\leqslant\omega_{1},\dots,r_{t}\leqslant\omega_{t}}\,\prod_{j=1}^{t}p_{j}^{-r_{j}(n-2)}=q^{4n-4}\Theta(n)\,,

where
\Theta(n)=O(1)
for
n\geqslant 4
,
\Theta(3)=O(\log t)
and
\Theta(2)\ll\prod_{j=1}^{t}(1+\omega_{j})=\tau(q)
.
Let us remark other bounds for
\Theta(2)

P r o o f.

We combine an appropriate version of Corollary
11
and the well–known Bourgain–Gamburd machine
[
6
]
applied to equation (
37
) see, e.g.,
[
24
]
.
Indeed, for any
x\in A\cap A^{-1}
, we have
x=i+\lambda
such that

1=(i+\lambda)(i^{\prime}+\lambda^{\prime})
, where
i,i^{\prime}\in I
and
\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}\in\Lambda
.
Thus we in very deed arrive to equation
(
37
). Now
I(i)\leqslant N^{-1}(I*\overline{I})(i)
, where
\overline{I}=[-N,N]
and hence the number of solutions to the equation
1=(i+\lambda)(i^{\prime}+\lambda^{\prime})
can be bounded above as
1=(j+a)(j^{\prime}+a^{\prime})
with
a,a^{\prime}\in A
and
j,j^{\prime}\in\overline{I}
(times
N^{-2}
, of course).
In particular (see
[
24
]
or just Proposition
34
and Corollary
11
above), we get for an absolute constant
c\in(0,1]
that

|A\cap A^{-1}|\leqslant\frac{|A|^{2}|\overline{I}|^{2}}{N^{2}p}+O(N^{-2}|A|\cdot N^{2-c})\ll\frac{|A|^{2}}{p}

(42)

and hence the second estimate of (
41
) follows from the first one. Here we have used the conditions that
|A|>p^{1-\epsilon}
and
N\geqslant p^{\epsilon/c}
, which is satisfied if we put

c_{*}=c/4
, say.

Similarly, let
h\in[N]
be an integer parameter and write
I(i)=h^{-1}(H*I)(i)+\varepsilon(i)
, where
H=[h]
and
\|\varepsilon\|_{\infty}=1
,
|\mathsf{supp}(\varepsilon)|\leqslant 2h
.
In particular, we have
\|\varepsilon\|^{2}_{2}\leqslant 2h
and one can threat
\varepsilon
as a sum of two functions
\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}
with supports on some shifts of the interval
H
.
Put
\tilde{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}:H\to[-1,1]
.
As always let us write

\sum_{x\in A\cap A^{-1}}\chi(x)=\sum_{1=(i+\lambda)(i^{\prime}+\lambda^{\prime})}\chi(\lambda+i)\Lambda(\lambda)\Lambda(\lambda^{\prime})I(i)I(i^{\prime})

=\sum_{1=(i+\lambda)(i^{\prime}+\lambda^{\prime})}\chi(\lambda+i)\Lambda(\lambda)\Lambda(\lambda^{\prime})(h^{-1}(H*I)(i)+\varepsilon(i))(h^{-1}(H*I)(i^{\prime})+\varepsilon(i^{\prime}))

=h^{-2}\sum_{a,a^{\prime},h,h^{\prime}~:~(a+h)(a^{\prime}+h^{\prime})=1}A(a)A(a^{\prime})H(h)H(h^{\prime})\chi(a+h)+\mathcal{E}=\sigma+\mathcal{E}\,,

where the error term
\mathcal{E}
can be estimated as (there are better bounds as the set
A
is
I
–invariant and not just
H
–invariant)

|\mathcal{E}|\leqslant 2h^{-1}\sum_{a,a^{\prime},h,h^{\prime}~:~(a+h)(a^{\prime}+h^{\prime})=1}\Lambda(a)A(a^{\prime})|\tilde{\varepsilon}(h)|H(h^{\prime})+\sum_{a,a^{\prime},h,h^{\prime}~:~(a+h)(a^{\prime}+h^{\prime})=1}\Lambda(a)\Lambda(a^{\prime})|\tilde{\varepsilon}(h)\tilde{\varepsilon}(h^{\prime})|

\ll\frac{|A|^{2}}{p}\left(\frac{h}{N}+\frac{h^{2}}{N^{2}}\right)+|A|\cdot\left(\frac{h^{1-c}}{\sqrt{N}}+\frac{h^{2-c}}{N}\right)\ll\frac{|A|^{2}h}{pN}+\frac{|A|h^{1-c}}{\sqrt{N}}\,.

(43)

Here we have assumed that
h\leqslant\sqrt{N}
and applied the well–known Bourgain–Gamburd machine
[
6
]
,
[
24
]
.
Recall that this result replaces Corollary
11
in the case when
X
,
Y

are intervals and
\chi\equiv 1
(that is why we need two additional main terms in (
43
)).
It remains to estimate the sum
\sigma
and to do this we can use
the Bourgain–Gamburd machine one more time, namely, we
apply our
Corollary
11
and get
\sigma\ll|A|h^{-c}
.
Finally, combining the estimate for
\sigma
and bound (
43
) for the error term
\mathcal{E}
, choosing the parameter

, we obtain

\sum_{x\in A\cap A^{-1}}\chi(x)\ll|A|h^{-c}+\frac{|A|^{2}h}{pN}+\frac{|A|h^{1-c}}{\sqrt{N}}\ll|A|h^{-c}\ll|A|N^{-c/2}\ll\frac{|A|^{2}}{p}\cdot N^{-c/4}

thanks to our assumptions
|A|>p^{1-\epsilon}
and
N\geqslant p^{4\epsilon/c}
.
The same calculations show that there is an asymptotic formula for
|A\cap A^{-1}|
and, in particular, the inverse inequality to (
42
) takes place. It gives us the first inequality in (

).
This completes the proof.

Theorem 14

Let
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{F}_{p}
,

,

,

(44)

Let us make a final remark.
Loosely,
Theorem
5
gives us a non–trivial bound for the
multiplicative energy
of the set
Z_{M}
, see formula (
46
) below.
Nevertheless, the last fact follows from the circumstance that
Z_{M}
is an Ahlfors–David set,
[
4
]
, that is
for an arbitrary
z\in Z_{M}
one has

|Z_{M}\cap(\mathcal{D}+z)|\sim_{M}|\mathcal{D}|^{w_{M}}N^{1-w_{M}}

(45)

for any interval
\mathcal{D}
,
|\mathcal{D}|\geqslant N
with the center at the origin.
Recall that
in
[
4
]
a non–trivial upper bound was obtained for the
additive energy
of any Ahlfors–David set.
Let us briefly
prove an upper estimate for the multiplicative energy of
an arbitrary
Ahlfors–David set
Z_{M}
, having large Hausdorff dimension
w_{M}
.
The advantage of bound (
47
) below that our power saving can be expressed in terms of
|Z_{M}|
but not just
N
.

Namely, write
Z=Z_{M}
,
w=w_{M}
and then
|Z|\sim_{M}p^{w}N^{1-w}
.
Also, put
\delta\sim_{M}\Delta^{w}N^{1-w}
, where

\Delta
is a parameter.
By the points/planes incidences in
\mathbb{F}_{p}
(see
[
30
]
) and property (
45
) one has

\mathsf{E}^{\times}(Z):=|\{(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3},z_{4})\in Z^{4}~:~z_{1}z_{2}=z_{3}z_{4}\}|

(46)

\ll\delta^{-2}|\{(z_{1},z_{2},z^{\prime}_{1},z^{\prime}_{2},d,d^{\prime})\in Z^{4}\times[\Delta]^{2}~:~z_{1}(z_{2}+d)\equiv z^{\prime}_{1}(z^{\prime}_{2}+d^{\prime})\}|

\ll\delta^{-2}\left(\frac{|Z|^{4}\Delta^{2}}{p}+|Z|^{3}\Delta^{3/2}\right)\ll\delta^{-2}p^{3}\,,

where the optimal choice for
\Delta
is
\Delta=(p/|Z|)^{2}
.
Thus

\mathsf{E}^{\times}(Z)\ll_{M}|Z|^{3}(p/|Z|)^{3-4w}N^{-2(1-w)}\sim_{M}|Z|^{3}\cdot|Z|^{-\frac{(4w-3)(1-w)}{w}}N^{\frac{-(1-w)(3-2w)}{w}}<|Z|^{3}

(47)

for
w>3/4
.
Thus, we have a power saving in terms of
|Z|
for the multiplicative energy of any Ahlfors–David set.
