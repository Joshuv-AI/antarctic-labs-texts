Abundance of Dirichlet-improvable pairs with respect to arbitrary norms Thanks: D.K. was supported by NSF grant DMS-1900560.
Dmitry Kleinbock and Anurag Rao

Abstract
. In the paper [ AS ] of Akhunzhanov–Shatskov the two-dimensional Dirichlet spectrum with respect to Euclidean norm was defined. We consider an analogous definition for arbitrary norms on ℝ 2 \mathbb{R}^{2} and prove that, for each such norm, the set of Dirichlet improvable pairs contains the set of badly approximable pairs, hence is hyperplane absolute winning. To prove this we make a careful study of some classical results in the geometry of numbers due to Chalk–Rogers and Mahler to establish a Hajós–Minkowski type result for the critical locus of a cylinder. As a corollary, using a recent result of the first named author with Mirzadeh [ KM ] , we conclude that for any norm on ℝ 2 \mathbb{R}^{2} the top of the Dirichlet spectrum is not an isolated point.

11J13; 11J83, 11H06, 37A17

1.
Introduction

For a pair of real numbers
{\bf x}=(x_{1},x_{2})
, the authors of
[
AS
]
considered the quantity

(1.1)

c_{2}({\bf x}):=\limsup_{t\to\infty}\ t\left(\min_{1\leq q\leq t}\operatorname{dist}_{2}(q{\bf x},{\mathbb{Z}}^{2})\right)^{2}

where
\operatorname{dist}_{2}
denotes the distance function with respect to the Euclidean norm.
Equivalently,
c_{2}({\bf x})
is the infimum of
c>0
such that the system

\begin{cases}\|q{\bf x}-{\bf p}\|_{2}^{2}&\leq c/t\\
\qquad|q|&\leq t,\end{cases}

where
\|\cdot\|_{2}
is the Euclidean norm on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
,
has a
nontrivial integer solution
({\bf p},q)
for all large enough
t>0
.

The main result of
[
AS
]
was a description of the so-called
Dirichlet spectrum
with respect to the Euclidean norm on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
:

(1.2)

{\mathbb{D}}_{2}:=\left\{c_{2}({\bf x}):{\bf x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\right\}=\left[0,{2}/{\sqrt{3}}\right].

Note that the set-up described above is a Euclidean modification of the classical set-up of improving Dirichlet’s Theorem initiated by Davenport and Schmidt
[
DS
]
.
There the distance
\operatorname{dist}_{\infty}
with respect to the supremum norm is used in place of
\operatorname{dist}_{2}
in (
1.1
). It is worth pointing out that, to the best our knowledge, the complete structure of the supremum-norm analogue of (
1.2
), that is the set

(1.3)

{\mathbb{D}}_{\infty}:=\left\{c_{\infty}({\bf x}):{\bf x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\right\},\text{ where }c_{\infty}({\bf x}):=\limsup_{t\to\infty}\ t\cdot\left(\min_{1\leq q\leq t}\operatorname{dist}_{\infty}(q{\bf x},{\mathbb{Z}}^{2})\right)^{2},

is not clear. What is known though is that
{\mathbb{D}}_{\infty}
is a subset of
[0,1]
containing both endpoints, that
1
is not an isolated point of
{\mathbb{D}}_{\infty}
, and that the set of
Dirichlet-improvable
pairs

\mathbf{DI}_{\infty}:=\left\{{\bf x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}:c_{2}({\bf x})<1\right\}

has measure zero but nevertheless it is quite big: namely
[
DS
, Theorem 2]
it contains the set of
badly approximable
pairs

\mathbf{BA}:=\left\{{\bf x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}:\liminf_{t\to\infty}\ t\left(\min_{1\leq q\leq t}\operatorname{dist}(q{\bf x},{\mathbb{Z}}^{2})\right)^{2}>0\right\}.

The latter set is known to be of full Hausdorff dimension; this was proved in
[
S
]
by showing that
\mathbf{BA}
is a winning set of Schmidt’s game, and later upgraded to an even stronger hyperplane absolute winning property, see
[
BFKRW
]
.

In this paper we show that
\mathbf{BA}
is also a subset of the set of
Euclidean Dirichlet-improvable
pairs

\mathbf{DI}_{2}:=\left\{{\bf x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}:c_{2}({\bf x})<{2}/{\sqrt{3}}\right\}.

Moreover, the same is true if the Euclidean norm is replaced by any norm. Namely, for a norm
\nu
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
and
{\bf x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
let us define

(1.4)

c_{\nu}({\bf x})=\limsup_{t\to\infty}\ t\cdot\left(\min_{1\leq q\leq t}\operatorname{dist}_{\nu}(q{\bf x},{\mathbb{Z}}^{2})\right)^{2}\text{ and }c_{\nu}:=\sup_{{\bf x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}c_{\nu}({\bf x}),

where
\operatorname{dist}_{\nu}
is the distance function induced by
\nu
.
Our main result is as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

For any norm
\nu
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, the set

\mathbf{DI}_{\nu}:=\left\{{\bf x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}:c_{\nu}({\bf x})<c_{\nu}\right\}

contains
\mathbf{BA}
(hence is hyperplane absolute winning).

Note that it has already been proved in
[
KR
]
that for any norm
\nu
the Lebesgue measure of
\mathbf{DI}_{\nu}
is zero.

Theorem
1.1
can be used to derive a corollary concerning
the
Dirichlet spectrum
with respect to an arbitrary norm
\nu
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, defined similarly to (
1.2
) and (
1.3
) as

{\mathbb{D}}_{\nu}:=\left\{c_{\nu}({\bf x}):{\bf x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\right\}.

It is a
subset of
[0,c_{\nu}]
containing both of the endpoints.

Corollary 1.2
.

c_{\nu}
is an accumulation point of
{\mathbb{D}}_{\nu}
.

The structure of this paper is as follows.
In the subsequent section we show that the set
\mathbf{DI}_{\nu}
can be identified with a set of three-dimensional unimodular lattices having a certain dynamical property.
This property is that of avoiding a certain compact subset
\mathcal{L}_{\eta}
of the space of all unimodular lattices in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
under a diagonal flow. More precisely,
\mathcal{L}_{\eta}
is the
critical locus
(see §
2
) of the norm
\eta
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
given by

(1.5)

{\eta(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}):=\max\{\nu(x_{1},x_{2}),|x_{3}|\}.}

The next two sections are then justifiably spent on a detailed study of the work
[
CR1
,
CR2
]
of Chalk–Rogers in order to establish a structure theorem for these critical loci.
The resulting structure allows us to
apply the argument implicitly contained in the paper of Davenport and Schmidt
to conclude the proof of Theorem
1.1
in §
5
.
Corollary
1.2
is then derived from Theorem
1.1
with the help of a recent result—a solution of a special case of the Dimension Drop Conjecture—due to the first-named author and Mirzadeh
[
KM
]
. Several open questions are mentioned at the very end of the paper.

2.
A dynamical restatement

As promised, we describe how Dirichlet-improvability is related to dynamics and how the constant
c_{\nu}
of equation (
1.4
) is related to critical lattices.
This is a special case of the uniform Diophantine approximation problem discussed in
[
KR
]
.
For
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and a norm
\eta
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, let
\Delta_{\eta}
be the infimum covolume over all lattices in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
intersecting the unit norm ball
B_{\eta}(1)\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
trivially.
This is called the
critical determinant
of the norm
\eta
.
The constant
\Delta_{\eta}
is positive and is actually attained by a lattice.
These facts follow from the Minkowski convex body theorem and Mahler’s compactness criterion respectively.

For us it will be convenient to work with
unimodular
(covolume one) lattices; we will denote by
X_{n}
the space of all such lattices in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Clearly
X_{n}
can be
identified with the homogeneous space
\operatorname{SL}_{n}({\mathbb{R}})/\operatorname{SL}_{n}({\mathbb{Z}})
via
g\mapsto g{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
.
Then one can define the
critical radius
of the norm
\eta
as follows:

r_{\eta}:=\sup\left\{r:\Lambda\cap B_{\eta}(r)=\{0\}\text{ for some }\Lambda\in X_{n}\right\}.

It is clear that we have
r_{\eta}=\Delta_{\eta}^{-1/n}
.

Moreover, for
r>0
let us define

\mathcal{K}_{\eta}(r):=\big\{\Lambda\in{X_{n}}:\Lambda\cap B_{\eta}\left({r}\right)=\{0\}\big\}.

Obviously we have the containment

r_{1}<r_{2}\implies\mathcal{K}_{\eta}(r_{1})\supset\mathcal{K}_{\eta}(r_{2}),

and it follows from the definition of
r_{\eta}
that

(2.1)

{r_{\eta}<r\implies\mathcal{K}_{\eta}(r)=\varnothing,}

and

(2.2)

{r<r_{\eta}\implies\mathcal{K}_{\eta}(r)\text{ has non-empty interior}.}

Thus,
\{\mathcal{K}_{\eta}(r):0<r<r_{\eta}\}
is a family of compact neighborhoods of the set

\mathcal{L}_{\eta}:=\mathcal{K}_{\eta}(r_{\eta}),

which is called the
critical locus
of
\eta
.

Now let us specialize to
n=3
. For
{\bf x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
let us define

(2.3)

\Lambda_{\bf x}:=u_{\bf x}\cdot{\mathbb{Z}}^{3}\in{X_{3}},\text{ where }u_{\bf x}:=\left[{\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&0&x_{1}\\
0&1&x_{2}\\
0&0&1\end{array}}\right]\in\operatorname{SL}_{3}({\mathbb{R}}).

The flow of interest here is given by the one-parameter subgroup

(2.4)

a_{s}:=\left[{\begin{array}[]{ccc}e^{s/2}&0&0\\
0&e^{s/2}&0\\
0&0&e^{-s}\end{array}}\right].

Recall that we started with a norm
\nu
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, and let us take
\eta
of the form (
1.5
).
Then a standard argument usually referred to as the
Dani correspondence
gives the next proposition.
It is a special case of a general correspondence described in
[
KR
, Proposition 2.1]
; we include the elementary proof for the sake of keeping the paper self-contained.

Proposition 2.1
.

For
\nu
and
\eta
related via (
1.5
) and
c>0
the following are equivalent:

(i)

c_{\nu}({\bf x})<c
;

(ii)

there is an
r<c^{1/3}
such that for all sufficiently large
s
,
a_{s}\Lambda_{\bf x}\notin\mathcal{K}_{\eta}(r)
.

Proof.

Say
\rm(i)
holds.
Then for any
c_{\nu}({\bf x})<d<c
, the system

\begin{cases}\nu(q{\bf x}-{\bf p})&\leq(d/t)^{1/2}\\
\qquad|q|&\leq t\end{cases}

has a nontrivial integer solution
({\bf p},q)
for all sufficiently large
t
.
Equivalently, for sufficiently large
t
,

\Lambda_{\bf x}\cap\left[{\begin{array}[]{ccc}(d/t)^{1/2}&0&0\\
0&(d/t)^{1/2}&0\\
0&0&t\end{array}}\right]\cdot\overline{B_{\eta}(1)}\neq\{0\}.

By substituting
\ln\frac{t}{d^{1/3}}
for
s
, we see that for all sufficiently large
s
,

a_{s}\Lambda_{\bf x}\cap\overline{B_{\eta}(d^{1/3})}\neq\{0\}.

Choosing
d<r^{3}<c
we see that
\rm(ii)
is true.

Now say that
\rm(ii)
holds.
By defining
t
via the equation
\ln\frac{t}{r}=s
, we may trace backwards in the implications above to see that the system

\begin{cases}\nu(q{\bf x}-{\bf p})&<(r^{3}/t)^{1/2}\\
\qquad|q|&<\,t\end{cases}

has a nontrivial solution for all sufficiently large
t
.
Thus we have that
c_{\nu}({\bf x})\leq r^{3}<c
, and hence
\rm(i)
holds.
∎

In view of (
2.1
) we have
c_{\nu}({\bf x})\leq r_{\eta}^{3}
for all
{\bf x}
. Moreover, an application of the above proposition with
c=r_{\eta}^{3}
, in view of (
2.2
) and the ergodicity of the
a_{s}
-action on
{X_{3}}
, shows that
c_{\nu}({\bf x})=r_{\eta}^{3}
for Lebesgue almost all
{\bf x}
.
See
[
KR
, Proposition 3.2]
for additional details.
It follows that
c_{\nu}=r_{\eta}^{3}=\Delta_{\eta}^{-1}
. This last fact and another application of Proposition
2.1
show that

Corollary 2.2
.

{\bf x}\in\mathbf{DI}_{\nu}

if and only if the forward
a_{s}
-orbit of
\Lambda_{\bf x}
eventually avoids an open neighborhood of
\mathcal{L}_{\eta}
; that is,

(2.5)

{\overline{\left\{a_{s}\Lambda_{\bf x}:s\geq s_{0}\right\}}\cap\mathcal{L}_{\eta}=\varnothing\text{ for some }s_{0}>0.}

Thus our main result is reduced to proving that any
{\bf x}\in\mathbf{BA}
satisfies (
2.5
).

3.
The critical locus of a cylinder: results of Chalk–Rogers and Mahler

Let us now slightly change our definitions of
critical determinant
and
critical locus
so that they now refer to objects corresponding to a convex symmetric bounded domain rather than a norm.

For a convex symmetric bounded domain
B\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, we say a lattice
\Lambda
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is
B
-admissible
if

B\cap\Lambda=\{0\}.

We denote by
d(\Lambda)
the covolume of a lattice
\Lambda
, and by
\Delta(B)
the infimum covolume over all
B
-admissible lattices and refer to it as the
critical determinant
of
B
.
The (nonempty compact) set
\mathcal{L}(B)
denotes the set of
B
-admissible lattices having covolume exactly
\Delta(B)
, and we refer to it as the
critical locus
of
B
. To reconcile it with the previous notation, if
B
is the unit ball with respect to a norm
\eta
, then
\mathcal{L}_{\eta}=\frac{1}{\Delta(B)^{1/n}}\mathcal{L}(B)
consists of lattices in
\mathcal{L}(B)
scaled so that their covolume becomes equal to
1
.
Our goal is to prove a theorem which gives the structure of
\mathcal{L}(B)
when
B
is a cylindrical convex symmetric bounded domain in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
.

Let
B
be a convex symmetric bounded domain in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
.
Let
\nu
be the Minkowski norm associated to
B
.
Let
{C=C_{B}}
be defined as the cylinder
B\times[-1,1]
.
We can also write
C
as

(3.1)

C=\{(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}):\nu(x_{1},x_{2})<1,\ |{x_{3}}|<1\}.

We fix notation
{\pi_{+}}
,
{\pi_{-}}
for the projections from
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
to the
x_{3}
-axis and the
(x_{1},x_{2})
-plane respectively.

It is a well-known fact that

(3.2)

{\Delta(B)=\Delta(C_{B}).}

In fact, it is very well known; around the
1940
s three independent proofs appeared.
Mahler in
[
M1
]
gave a proof for the cylinder over the disc, which was based on a (non-lattice) packing result for discs in a polgyon.
This packing result admits a generalization (due to Fejes-Tóth) which settles the issue for arbitrary cylinders.
See
[
C
, §IX.5.4]
for the theorem of Fejes-Tóth as well as the following section for the application to cylinders.
Another argument was given by Yeh in
[
Y
]
and was based on results of Minkowski on the configuration of critical lattices for three-dimensional domains.

The argument we discuss here is given by Chalk and Rogers in
[
CR1
]
.
It so happens that this argument is especially convenient for making a precise description of
\mathcal{L}(C)
in terms of
\mathcal{L}(B)
.
We need to make a preparatory study of the argument therein before giving the proof of the following theorem.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
B\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
be a convex symmetric bounded domain which is not a parallelogram, and let
{C=C_{B}}
.
Then the critical locus
\mathcal{L}(C)
is the union of the two sets

(3.3)

\left\{\left[{\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
&*&1\end{array}}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{c | c}M&\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
0\end{array}\\
\hline\cr\begin{array}[]{c c}0&0\end{array}&1\end{array}\right]{\mathbb{Z}}^{3}:M{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\in\mathcal{L}(B)\right\}

and

(3.4)

\left\{\left[{\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&0&*\\
0&1&*\\
0&0&1\end{array}}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{c | c}M&\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
0\end{array}\\
\hline\cr\begin{array}[]{c c}0&0\end{array}&1\end{array}\right]{\mathbb{Z}}^{3}:M{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\in\mathcal{L}(B)\right\}.

The critical locus for cylinders over parallelograms and, more generally, for parallelopideds in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is the content of the Hajós–Minkowski theorem.
See
[
H
]
for the proof.
A more expository treatment is given in
[
SS
]
.

Theorem 3.2
.

If
C
is the unit ball for the supremum norm on
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
, the critical locus
\mathcal{L}(C)
is the union of manifolds

(3.5)

P\left\{\left[{\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&*&*\\
0&1&*\\
0&0&1\end{array}}\right]{\mathbb{Z}}^{3}\right\}

over all
3\times 3
permutation matrices
P
.

The following lemma appears as Lemma
1
in
[
CR1
]
.
We write out the entire proof since we need an explicit form of the deformation, see (
3.9
) below, appearing therein.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
C
be as in (
3.1
).
Then, given any critical lattice
\Lambda
of
C
, there is a path of
C
-critical lattices
\Lambda(t)
with
\Lambda(0)=\Lambda
, and with
\Lambda(1)
having three linearly independent points satisfying

(3.6)

\nu\big({\pi_{-}}(*)\big)\leq 1,\ |{\pi_{+}}(*)|=1.

In particular, three independent lattice points on the top of the cylinder can be found.
Moreover, this path is given by

\{L_{t}\Lambda\}
,
where
L_{t}
is a smooth family of lower triangular unipotents having
(2,1)
entry zero.

Proof.

Let
\Lambda
be
C
-critical. Then
\Lambda
must contain three independent points on the boundary
\partial C
:
for if the points of
\Lambda\cap\partial C
were contained in a plane,
[
L
, Theorem 7.8]
would enable us to construct an admissible lattice of smaller covolume, a contradiction.
So, let

{\bf p}_{1},{\bf p}_{2},{\bf p}_{3}

be any three independent points in
\Lambda\cap\partial C
.
Let
n
be the number of linearly independent points of
\Lambda\cap\partial C
satisfying (
3.6
). Clearly

n
cannot be
0
, for otherwise it would permit a contraction in the vertical direction.
In each of the cases
n=1,2
, we show that a shear can be applied to obtain a lattice
\Lambda^{\prime}
with
3
independent points on
\partial C
satisfying (
3.6
).

Say
n=1
.
Among the three independent points
{\bf p}_{i}
in
\Lambda\cap\partial C
, we can assume without loss of generality that
{\bf p}_{1}
satisfies (
3.6
) and that
{\bf p}_{2},{\bf p}_{3}
satisfy

(3.7)

\nu\big({\pi_{-}}(*)\big)=1,\ |{\pi_{+}}(*)|<1.

Let
{\bf q}_{1},\ {\bf q}_{2},\ {\bf q}_{3}
be any basis for
\Lambda
.
This gives us an integral
3\times 3
matrix
M
with

\left[{\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\bf p}_{1}&{\bf p}_{2}&{\bf p}_{3}\\
\end{array}}\right]=\left[{\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\bf q}_{1}&{\bf q}_{2}&{\bf q}_{3}\\
\end{array}}\right]M

from which we can write

\Lambda=\left[{\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\bf q}_{1}&{\bf q}_{2}&{\bf q}_{3}\\
\end{array}}\right]{\mathbb{Z}}^{3}=\left[{\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\bf p}_{1}&{\bf p}_{2}&{\bf p}_{3}\\
\end{array}}\right]M^{-1}{\mathbb{Z}}^{3}.

Consider the continuous family of lattices

(3.8)

\Lambda(t)=\left[{\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\bf p}_{1}&{\bf p}_{2}&{\bf p}_{3}+t{\bf e}_{3}\\
\end{array}}\right]M^{-1}{\mathbb{Z}}^{3}

where
{\bf e}_{3}
is the third standard basis vector in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
.
Writing
P=\left[{\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\bf p}_{1}&{\bf p}_{2}&{\bf p}_{3}\\
\end{array}}\right]
and
Q=\left[{\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\bf q}_{1}&{\bf q}_{2}&{\bf q}_{3}\\
\end{array}}\right]
, we see that
M^{-1}=P^{-1}Q
, whence the lattice
\Lambda(t)
can actually be written as

(3.9)

\begin{split}\Lambda(t)&=\left[{\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\bf p}_{1}&{\bf p}_{2}&{\bf p}_{3}+t{\bf e}_{3}\\
\end{array}}\right]P^{-1}Q{\mathbb{Z}}^{3}\\
&=\left[{\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
t(P^{-1})_{31}&t(P^{-1})_{32}&1+t(P^{-1})_{33}\end{array}}\right]\Lambda,\end{split}

where the constants on the bottom row indicate the corresponding entries in the matrix
P^{-1}
.
Without loss of generality, we assume that the covolume function

d\big(\Lambda(t)\big)=\left|1+t(P^{-1})_{33}\right|d(\Lambda)

has nonpositive derivative at
0
, i.e.
(P^{-1})_{33}\leq 0
.
(If this is not the case, the argument continues by considering
-t
instead of
t
.)
Let
\tau
be the largest (possibly infinite) real number such that

0\leq t\leq\tau\text{ implies }\Lambda(t)\text{ is $C$-admissible}.

By the assumption on the derivative of the covolume,
\Lambda(t)
must actually be
C
-critical for all
0\leq t\leq\tau
.
So, the covolume function is constant in this (possibly singleton or even infinite) interval.
We consider cases wherein
\tau
is either infinite or not.

(a)

\tau=+\infty
. Every
\Lambda(t)
with
t\geq 0
is
C
-critical.
Note, in this case, since the covolume is constant, we must have
(P^{-1})_{33}=0
in (
3.9
).
Choosing
t_{0}
appropriately, we have
{\bf p}_{1}
and
{\bf p}_{3}+t_{0}{\bf e}_{3}
(which belong to
\Lambda(t)
) satisfying (
3.6
).
The crucial observation here is that

(3.10)

\Lambda(t_{0})=L(t_{0})\Lambda

where
L(t)
is a curve in the lower triangular unipotents with
(2,1)
entry
0
.

(b)

\tau<\infty
. Consider
\Lambda(\tau)
, which is
C
-critical.
Suppose we have no lattice point of
\Lambda(\tau)
satisfying (
3.6
) and not proportional to
{\bf p}_{1}
.
Choose
\rho>0
so that every lattice point in
\Lambda(\tau)

\rho
-close (in some fixed norm
\|\cdot\|
) to
C
is actually in the closure
\overline{C}
.
Write

\Lambda(\tau)=N(\tau){\mathbb{Z}}^{3},\ \ \Lambda(\tau+\varepsilon)=N(\tau+\varepsilon){\mathbb{Z}}^{3}

where
N(\cdot)
is the product of matrices appearing in the formula (
3.8
).
Let
\delta>0
be such that for all
\varepsilon\in[0,\delta]
, we have that

d\big(\Lambda(\tau+\varepsilon)\big)\geq\Delta(C)/2
and that

(3.11)

\left(N(\tau+\varepsilon){\bf u}\in C\text{ with }{\bf u}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{3}\right)\ \text{ implies }\ \left(\|N(\tau+\varepsilon){\bf u}-N(\tau){\bf u}\|\leq\rho\right).

Now if we have
\varepsilon
in this range, and a nonzero point
N(\tau+\varepsilon){\bf u}
lying in
C
, then (
3.11
) and the definition of
\rho
show that
N(\tau){\bf u}\in\overline{C}
.
Since
\Lambda(\tau)
is
C
-admissible,
N(\tau){\bf u}
is in
\partial C
.
By the assumption on
\Lambda(\tau)
at the start of this case, we see that

(3.12)

\text{either }{\pi_{+}}\big(N(\tau){\bf u}\big)<1,\text{ or }N(\tau){\bf u}=\pm{\bf p}_{1}.

The second eventuality is prevented since, by definition of
N(\cdot)
, we would then necessarily have
N(\tau+\varepsilon){{\bf u}}=\pm{\bf p}_{1}
, contrary to
N(\tau+\varepsilon){{\bf u}}
lying in
C
.
Thus we are in the first case where
{\pi_{+}}\big(N(\tau){\bf u}\big)<1
, and this implies that
\nu\big({\pi_{-}}(N(\tau){\bf u})\big)=1
.
This is again a contradiction, since
{\pi_{-}}\big(N(\tau+\varepsilon){\bf u}\big)={\pi_{-}}\big(N(\tau){\bf u}\big)
, and
N(\tau+\varepsilon){{\bf u}}
was presumed to be in
C
.

What this shows is that

0&0&1\end{array}}\right]\left[{\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&1/2&0\\
0&\sqrt{3}/2&0\\
0&0&1\end{array}}\right]{\mathbb{Z}}^{3}:x,y,t\in{\mathbb{R}}\right\}.

Each is the orbit of a subgroup of
\operatorname{SL}_{3}({\mathbb{R}})
, and the same is true for their intersection.
Letting
\nu
stand for the Euclidean norm in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, on scaling each lattice above by

\Delta_{\nu}^{-1/3}=\left(\sqrt{3}/2\right)^{-1/3},

we get the critical locus
\mathcal{L}_{\eta}
associated to the norm (
1.5
).
As mentioned before, this normalization is done to ensure that the critical locus consists of unimodular lattices.

5.
The interaction of the critical locus and the flow

In order to use the results of the previous sections for the proof of our main result, it will be convenient to introduce the following two subsets of
X_{3}
:

\mathcal{Z}_{+}:=\left\{\left[{\begin{array}[]{ccc}*&*&0\\
&*&0\\
&*&*\end{array}}\right]{\mathbb{Z}}^{3}\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{Z}_{-}:=\left\{\left[{\begin{array}[]{ccc}*&*&*\\
&*&*\\
0&*&*\end{array}}\right]{\mathbb{Z}}^{3}\right\}.

In words,
\mathcal{Z}_{+}
(resp.,
\mathcal{Z}_{-}
) consist of lattices having a nonzero vector on the
x_{3}
-axis (resp., on the
(x_{1},x_{2})
-plane).

Now observe that a combination of Theorems
3.1
and
3.2
readily produces the following

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
\eta
be the norm on
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
of the form (
1.5
). Then its critical locus
\mathcal{L}_{\eta}
is contained in
\mathcal{Z}_{+}\cup\mathcal{Z}_{-}
.

Proof.

Let
B:=B_{\nu}\left({1}/{\Delta_{\nu}^{1/3}}\right)
, and recall that we have the equality
\Delta_{\eta}=\Delta_{\nu}
.
Note that if
g\in\operatorname{GL}_{3}({\mathbb{R}})
and if
D
is any domain, we have

(5.1)

\Delta(gD)=|\det(g)|\Delta(D)\text{ and }\mathcal{L}(gD)=g\mathcal{L}(D).

Using the matrix

(5.2)

g=\left[{\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&\Delta_{\nu}^{1/3}\end{array}}\right],

we see that

gB_{\eta}\left({1}/{\Delta_{\eta}^{1/3}}\right)=B\times[-1,1].

Suppose that
B
is not a parallelogram. Then by Theorem
3.1
,

\mathcal{L}_{\eta}=\left\{\left[\begin{array}[]{c | c}M&\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
0\end{array}\\
\hline\cr\begin{array}[]{c c}*&*\end{array}&\Delta_{\nu}^{-1/3}\end{array}\right]{\mathbb{Z}}^{3}:M{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\in\mathcal{L}(B)\right\}\bigcup\left\{\left[\begin{array}[]{c | c}M&\begin{array}[]{c}*\\
\end{array}\\
\hline\cr\begin{array}[]{c c}0&0\end{array}&\Delta_{\nu}^{-1/3}\end{array}\right]{\mathbb{Z}}^{3}:M{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\in\mathcal{L}(B)\right\}.

This finishes the proof in this case, since the sets in the right hand side of the above equation are contained in
\mathcal{Z}_{+}
and
\mathcal{Z}_{-}
respectively.

In the remaining parallelogram case we have
B_{\eta}\left({1}/{\Delta_{\eta}^{1/3}}\right)=gC
, where
g
is a linear transformation of the form

\left[\begin{array}[]{c | c}*&\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
0\end{array}\\
\hline\cr\begin{array}[]{c c}0&0\end{array}&*\end{array}\right]
, and
C
is a ball for the supremum norm in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
. Theorem
3.2
implies that every lattice in
\mathcal{L}(C)
contains at least one vector from the standard basis of
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
; hence
\mathcal{L}(C)\subset\mathcal{Z}_{+}\cup\mathcal{Z}_{-}
. Since
g
leaves both the
(x_{1},x_{2})
-plane and the
x_{3}
-axis invariant, it also preserves the sets
\mathcal{Z}_{+}
and
\mathcal{Z}_{-}
, which, in view of (
5.1
), finishes the proof of the proposition.
∎

We are now ready for the

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Recall that
{\bf x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
is badly approximable if and only if the orbit
\{a_{s}\Lambda_{\bf x}:s>0\}
is bounded (see
[
D
, Theorem 2.20]
).
Take
{\bf x}\in\mathbf{BA}
and let
\mathcal{K}\subset X_{3}
be a compact set containing
\{a_{s}\Lambda_{\bf x}:s\geq 0\}
. In view of Corollary
2.2
, we need to prove that the forward
a_{s}
-orbit of
\Lambda_{\bf x}
eventually avoids an open neighborhood of
\mathcal{L}_{\eta}
. Suppose that it is not the case, that is, we have
a_{s_{k}}\Lambda_{\bf x}\to\Lambda\in\mathcal{L}_{\nu}
for some sequence
s_{k}\to\infty
. In view of the preceding proposition we are left to consider two cases.

Case 1.

\Lambda\in\mathcal{Z}_{+}
. Since the
x_{3}
-axis is a contracting eigenspace for
a_{s}
for
s>0
, it follows that the forward
a_{s}
-trajectory of
\Lambda
is divergent, which implies that

(5.3)

\{s>0:a_{s}\Lambda\in\mathcal{K}\}\text{ is bounded.}

By continuity of the action and in view of (
5.3
), there is a
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
and an open neighborhood
\mathcal{V}
of
\Lambda
such that
a_{s_{k}}\mathcal{V}\cap\mathcal{K}=\varnothing
.
This implies that
a_{s_{k}+s_{m}}\Lambda_{\bf x}\notin\mathcal{K}
for large enough
m
, a contradiction.

Case 2.

\Lambda\in\mathcal{Z}_{-}
. In this case, since the
(x_{1},x_{2})
-plane is a contracting eigenspace for
a_{s}
for
s<0
, the backward
a_{s}
-trajectory of
\Lambda
is divergent, That is,

(5.4)

\{s<0:a_{s}\Lambda\in\mathcal{K}\}\text{ is bounded.}

Again by continuity of the action and in view of (
5.4
), there is a neighborhood
\mathcal{V}
of
\Lambda
and
s<0
such that
a_{s}\mathcal{V}\cap\mathcal{K}=\varnothing
.
This implies that there exist infinitely many
k
for which
a_{s+s_{k}}\Lambda_{\bf x}\notin\mathcal{K}
.
The flow time
s+s_{k}
will eventually be positive, contradicting the fact that the forward orbit of
\Lambda_{\bf x}
is contained in
K
.

∎

Proof of Corollary
1.2
.

Suppose, to the contrary, that
c_{\nu}
is an isolated point of
{\mathbb{D}}_{\nu}
. This means that there exists
c<c_{\nu}
such that
c_{\nu}({\bf x})<c
for all
{\bf x}\in\mathbf{DI}_{\nu}
. Proposition
2.1
then implies that there exists
r<r_{\eta}
, where
\eta
is again defined via (
1.5
), such that

(5.5)

{\mathbf{DI}_{\nu}\subset\left\{{\bf x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}:a_{s}u_{\bf x}\cdot{\mathbb{Z}}^{3}\notin\mathcal{K}_{\eta}(r)\text{ for all sufficiently large }s\right\}.}

The exceptional sets similar to that in the right hand side of (
5.5
) were recently investigated in
[
KM
]
, where, in particular, the so-called Dimension Drop Conjecture was solved for the
a_{s}
-action on
X_{3}
. More precisely,
since
\mathcal{K}_{\eta}(r)
has non-empty interior, in view of
[
KM
, Theorem 1.2]

the set in the right hand side of (
5.5
) has less than full Hausdorff dimension, which contradicts Theorem
1.1
. ∎

We end the paper with a few remarks and questions.

Remark 5.2
.

Let
B\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be a bounded convex symmetric domain, and let
C_{B}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
be the cylinder over it.
It would be natural to attempt to obtain a structure theorem for
\mathcal{L}(C_{B})
similar to Theorem
3.1
.
However, even the equality of critical determinants
is not known in this generality.
The only higher-dimensional example known to the authors can be found in
[
W
]
: it establishes (
3.2
) for the special case of
B
being a Euclidean ball in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
.

Remark 5.3
.

Corollary
2.2

suggests that one can consider a Diophantine condition on
{\bf x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
equivalent to the forward
a_{s}
-orbit of
\Lambda_{\bf x}
eventually avoiding some open neighborhood of
\mathcal{L}_{\eta}
,
where
\eta
is an arbitrary (not necessarily cylindrical) norm on
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
. This variation on the theme of improving Dirichlet’s theorem has been thoroughly explored in
[
KR
]
in a much more general set-up of
m\times n
matrices. In particular, the full Hausdorff dimension (and, even stronger, the hyperplane absolute winning property) of the sets of lattices avoiding the critical locus has been established in several special cases, such as: an arbitrary norm on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}

[
KR
, Theorem 1.3]
and the Euclidean norm on
{\mathbb{R}}^{m+n}

[
KR
, Theorem 3.7]
. In both cases a connection with badly approximable matrices is no longer clear, and the proof is based on results from
[
AGK
]
of the first named author with An and Guan. Whether or not a similar full dimension or winning result holds for an arbitrary norm on
{\mathbb{R}}^{m+n}
is an open question.
