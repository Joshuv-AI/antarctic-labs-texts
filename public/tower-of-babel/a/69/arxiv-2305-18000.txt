Dissipation, quantum coherence, and asymmetry of finite-time cross-correlations
Tan Van Vu, Van Tuan Vo, Keiji Saito

Abstract
Recent studies have revealed a deep connection between the asymmetry of cross-correlations and thermodynamic quantities in the short-time limit. In this study, we address the finite-time domain of the asymmetry for both open classical and quantum systems. Focusing on Markovian dynamics, we show that the asymmetry observed in finite-time cross-correlations is upper bounded by dissipation. We prove that, for classical systems in a steady state with arbitrary operational durations, the asymmetry exhibits, at most, linear growth over time, with the growth speed determined by the rates of entropy production and dynamical activity. In the long-time regime, the asymmetry exhibits exponential decay, with the decay rate determined by the spectral gap of the transition matrix. Remarkably, for quantum cases, quantum coherence is equally important as dissipation in constraining the asymmetry of correlations. We demonstrate an example where only quantum coherence bounds the asymmetry while the entropy production rate vanishes. Furthermore, we generalize the short-time bounds on correlation asymmetry, as reported by Shiraishi [Phys. Rev. E 108, L042103 (2023)] and Ohga et al. [Phys. Rev. Lett. 131, 077101 (2023)], to encompass finite-time scenarios. These findings offer novel insights into the thermodynamic aspects of correlation asymmetry.



I
Introduction

Asymmetry, which refers to the absence of symmetry, constitutes a fundamental concept in physics.
The presence of asymmetry typically results in nontrivial and crucial consequences for a given system and has thus drawn considerable attention in various scientific disciplines.
Nonequilibrium thermodynamics is one such field where the concept of asymmetry is crucial
Sekimoto 2010
;
Seifert 2012
.
For instance, the violation of time-reversal symmetry indicates the existence of nonequilibrium conditions, and the degree of such a violation is closely related to entropy production, with asymmetry being reflected by fluctuation theorems
Gallavotti and Cohen 1995
;
Jarzynski 1997
;
Kurchan 1998
;
Crooks 1999
;
Saito and Utsumi 2008
;
Esposito et al. 2009
.
As manifested in the thermodynamic uncertainty relation
Barato and Seifert 2015
;
Gingrich et al. 2016
;
Hasegawa and Van Vu 2019
;
Timpanaro et al. 2019
;
Horowitz and Gingrich 2020
and the entropic bound
Dechant and Sasa 2018
, the asymmetry of arbitrary currents is constrained by dissipation.
Another intriguing phenomenon is the relaxation asymmetry, which asserts that the heating process is faster than the cooling one
Lapolla and Godec 2020
;
Van Vu and Hasegawa 2021
;
Manikandan 2021
.
Furthermore, asymmetry can be harnessed to enhance the performance of heat engines
Mondal et al. 2020
;
Harunari et al. 2021
.

Cross-correlation is a fundamental quantity that embodies both temporal and spatial information pertaining to physical systems.
Fluctuation-dissipation theorems
Kubo et al. 1991
establish that the response of a nonequilibrium steady-state system to a small perturbation can be expressed in terms of cross-correlation
Seifert and Speck 2010
.
The investigation of correlation properties has progressed in various directions, including linear response theory
Crisanti and Ritort 2003
;
Marconi et al. 2008
, speed limits for auto-correlation
Mohan and Pati 2022
;
Carabba et al. 2022
;
Hasegawa 2023
;
Dechant et al. 2023
;
Mori and Shirai 2023
, and thermodynamic inference of entropy production using the correlation between different observables
Dechant and Sasa 2021
, to name a few.
Recently, a deep connection between the asymmetry of cross-correlations and thermodynamic quantities has been reported for steady-state systems in the
short-time
limit
Ohga et al. 2023
;
Shiraishi 2023
, providing novel insights into the circulation of fluctuation
Tomita and Tomita 1974
and coherent oscillations
Barato and Seifert 2017
.
From a dynamic standpoint, correlation asymmetry can be related to physical quantities in nonequilibrium systems such as odd viscosity in fluid dynamics
Fruchart et al. 2023
, kinetic fluxes in reaction networks
Qian and Elson 2004
, and the temporal ordering of cellular events in living cells
Sisan et al. 2010
.
Simultaneously, correlation asymmetry should also contain information on the time-reversal symmetry breaking for the whole time regime
Onsager 1931a
;
Onsager 1931b
.
Thus, it is quite important to unveil the in-depth relationship between the asymmetry and the thermodynamic costs in the entire
finite-time
domain, as well as to explore quantum effects on correlation.

In the present paper, we address these open problems by considering classical and quantum Markov processes of discrete-state systems whose initial state is a stationary state.
We prove that the asymmetry exhibited in finite-time cross-correlations is always bounded from above by the thermodynamic costs for the whole time regime.
Specifically, for classical Markov jump processes, we show that the asymmetry grows at most linear in time, with the velocity determined by the rates of entropy production and dynamical activity; in the long-time regime, it exponentially decays with the rate determined by the spectral gap of the transition matrix [cf. Eq. (
7

These provide a basic picture of the asymmetry in generic Markov processes.
Variants of this structure are found in other cases with multi-time and multi-observables, as well as in other dynamics such as discrete-time Markov chains, continuous-state overdamped Langevin systems, and open quantum systems.
Remarkably, by considering the Lindblad master equations for open quantum systems, we find that quantum coherence plays a crucial role in the asymmetry of correlations [cf. Eq. (
11
)].
We demonstrate that degeneracy in the spectrum of the steady-state density matrix can lead to a nontrivial phenomenon wherein only quantum coherence is responsible for a finite asymmetry with zero entropy production.
In addition, we provide finite-time generalizations for the short-time bounds reported in Refs.
Ohga et al. 2023
;
Shiraishi 2023
[cf. Eqs. (
13
) and (
14
)].
These findings provide thermodynamic bounds on physically relevant quantities in various dynamics and further deepen our understanding of the asymmetry of correlations from the thermodynamic perspective.

II
Setup

We consider a Markov jump process described by the master equation:

\ket{\dot p_t}={W}\ket{p_t},

(1)

where the dot
\cdot
denotes the time derivative,
\ket{p_t}=[p_{1}(t),\dots,p_{N}(t)]^{\top}
is the probability distribution at time
t
, and
{W}=[w_{mn}]\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
denotes the time-independent transition matrix with
w_{mn}\geq 0
being the jump rate from state
n
to
m\,(\neq n)
and
w_{nn}=-\sum_{m(\neq n)}w_{mn}
.
We assume the local detailed balance
\ln(w_{mn}/w_{nm})=\Delta s_{mn}
, i.e., the log of the ratio of transition rates is related to the entropy change in the environment
\Delta s_{mn}
.
Hereafter, we consider the case that the system is in a nonequilibrium steady state
\ket{\pi}
.
Let
j_{mn}\coloneqq w_{mn}\pi_{n}-w_{nm}\pi_{m}
be the steady-state probability current; then, the master equation (
1
) implies
\sum_{m}j_{nm}=0
.
Two quantities of importance are the rates of entropy production and dynamical activity, defined as

\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{m>n}j_{mn}\ln\frac{w_{mn}\pi_{n}}{w_{nm}\pi_{m}},

(2)

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{m>n}(w_{mn}\pi_{n}+w_{nm}\pi_{m}).

(3)

Qualitatively,
\sigma
quantifies the degree of thermodynamic irreversibility, whereas
\gamma
reflects the timescale of the system
Maes 2020
.
Another relevant quantity is dynamical state mobility
Van Vu and Saito 2023
, which characterizes the response of probability currents against thermodynamic forces and is defined as

\kappa\coloneqq\sum_{m>n}\frac{j_{mn}}{\ln(w_{mn}\pi_n/w_{nm}\pi_m)}.

(4)

The relation
\kappa\leq\gamma/2
holds in general.

Next, we introduce cross-correlation and some notations to be used in this study.
Let
\ket{a}=[a_{1},\dots,a_{N}]^{\top}
and
\ket{b}=[b_{1},\dots,b_{N}]^{\top}
be arbitrary observables.
The two-time cross-correlation between these two observables can be defined as

C_{ba}^{\tau}\coloneqq\expectationvalue{b(\tau)a(0)},

(5)

where
a(t)\,[b(t)]
takes the value of
a_{n}\,[b_{n}]
if the system is in state
n
at time
t
and the average
\expectationvalue{\cdot}
is over all stochastic trajectories of time period
\tau
.
Defining
\Pi={\rm diag}(\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{N})
, then the cross-correlation can be analytically expressed as
C_{ba}^{\tau}=\matrixelement{b}{e^{{W}\tau}\Pi}{a}
.
We are interested in the asymmetry of cross-correlations

\delta C_{ba}^{\tau}\coloneqq C_{ba}^{\tau}-C_{ab}^{\tau},

(6)

which vanishes in equilibrium. However, it is not the case for nonequilibrium situations.
Qualitatively,
\delta C_{ba}^{\tau}
reflects symmetry breaking in the causality of observations and can reveal essential aspects of system dynamics, such as the extent to which the system deviates from equilibrium.
Several thermodynamic bounds for this quantity have recently been derived in the
\tau\to 0
limit
Ohga et al. 2023
;
Shiraishi 2023
.
In this study, we focus on the entire
finite-time
regime.

Let
\{\lambda_{n}\}
be the set of eigenvalues and
\{\ket{v_n^l},\ket{v_n^r}\}
be the left and right eigenvectors of
{W}
, respectively (i.e.,
\bra{v_n^l}{W}=\bra{v_n^l}\lambda_{n}
and
{W}\ket{v_n^r}=\lambda_{n}\ket{v_n^r}
).
The largest eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}=0
is associated with the eigenvector
\bra{v_1^l}\propto\bra{1}
, whereas all other eigenvalues have a negative real part,
0>\Re{\lambda_2}\geq\dots\geq\Re{\lambda_N}
.
Here,
\ket{1}
denotes the all-one vector.
The eigenvectors are normalized,
\innerproduct{v_n^l}{v_n^l}=1
.
Since
\{\ket{v_n^l}\}
forms a basis of
\mathbb{C}^{N}
, we can always find coefficients
\{\tilde{z}_{n}\}
for any vector
\ket{z}
such that
\ket{z}=\sum_{n}\tilde{z}_{n}\ket{v_n^l}
.
Hereafter, we define the
\ell_{1}
-norm
\|z\|_{*}\coloneqq\sum_{n\geq 2}|\tilde{z}_{n}|
.
An important quantity is the spectral gap
g\coloneqq-\Re{\lambda_2}>0
, which corresponds to the slowest decay mode and characterizes the relaxation speed of the system.

III.1
First main result and quantum generalization

Our first main result is a thermodynamic bound on the asymmetry of finite-time cross-correlations:

\frac{|\delta C_{ba}^{\tau}|}{\|a\|_{*}\|b\|_{*}}\leq\tau e^{-g\tau}\sigma\Phi\quantity(\frac{\sigma}{2\gamma})^{-1}\leq 2\tau e^{-g\tau}\sqrt{\sigma\kappa},

(7)

where
\Phi(u)
denotes the inverse function of
u\tanh(u)
and satisfies
\Phi(u)\geq\max\{u,\sqrt{u}\}\,\forall u\geq 0
.
The proof is presented in Appendix
A.1
.
Bound (
7
) implies that the thermodynamic costs govern the asymmetry over the whole time domain.
In the small-time regime, the asymmetry of cross-correlations increases at most linear in time with speed constrained by the entropy production and dynamical activity rates.
On the other hand, in the large-time regime, the asymmetry exponentially decays with the rate of the spectral gap.
The result can be analogously generalized to other scenarios, such as discrete-time Markov chains, continuous-state overdamped Langevin systems, and multiple observables, as shown in Appendix
B
.

Some remarks on this finding are given in order.
(i) First, it is of fundamental importance as it relates two physically important concepts (i.e., dissipation and correlation asymmetry) for
arbitrary
times.
While the bound may not be tight, it essentially captures both the dynamic and thermodynamic properties of correlation asymmetry.
An improved saturable bound, which implicitly exhibits the exponential decay, can be found in Appendix
A.2
.
(ii) Second, notice that
\delta C_{ba}^{\tau}
is identical with the difference between cross-correlations in the original dynamics and the dual dynamics
Crooks 2000
with transition rates
\widetilde{{W}}=\Pi{W}^{\dagger}\Pi^{-1}

fnt a
.
Therefore, the bound (
7
) can also be interpreted as a thermodynamic bound for the discrepancy between these dynamics in terms of observables.
(iii) Third, in other relevant contexts,
\delta C_{ba}^{\tau}
can be employed as a natural measure to study interactions in complex systems
Nolte et al. 2006
, dynamic co-localization, diffusion, binding in living cells
Bacia et al. 2006
, and information flow in biological systems
Ohga et al. 2023
;
Horowitz and Esposito 2014
.
Thus, the inequality (
7
) not only provides information on the temporal behavior of such a measure but also describes the constraints imposed by the thermodynamic costs.
Particularly, in the case of active fluids, it has been shown that odd viscosity can be expressed as the time integral of correlation asymmetry
Hargus et al. 2020
.
Our finding thus yields a thermodynamic bound on odd viscosity, indicating that it is constrained by dissipation and the reciprocal of the spectral gap.
(iv) Last, an effective approach to estimating the spectral gap
g
approximately, without knowing the details of the underlying dynamics, can be deduced from our result.
In practice, calculating the spectral gap
g
requires knowledge of the transition matrix, which is generally unavailable in experiments.
The bound derived here suggests that
g
can be estimated as the decay slope of the correlation asymmetry, which is experimentally accessible.

Next, we extend the result (
7
) to quantum cases, which include autonomous thermal engines
Mitchison 2019
;
Manzano et al. 2021
and quantum measurement processes
Wiseman and Milburn 2009
.
We consider a Markovian open quantum system, which is weakly coupled to a single or multiple reservoirs.
The time evolution of the reduced density matrix is described by the Lindblad equation,
\dot{\varrho}_{t}=\mathcal{L}(\varrho_{t})

Lindblad 1976
;
Gorini et al. 1976
, where

\mathcal{L}(\varrho)\coloneqq-i[H,\varrho]+\sum_{k}(L_{k}\varrho L_{k}^{\dagger}-\{L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k},\varrho\}/2)

(8)

with
H
is the time-independent Hamiltonian and
\{L_{k}\}
denote jump operators.
In order to be thermodynamically consistent, we assume the local detailed balance condition
Horowitz and Parrondo 2013
;
Manzano and Zambrini 2022
; that is, the jump operators come in pairs
(k,k^{\prime})
such that
L_{k}=e^{\Delta s_{k}/2}L_{k^{\prime}}^{\dagger}
, where
\Delta s_{k}
denotes the entropy change in the environment.
Let
\pi
be the steady state and
\pi=\sum_{n}\pi_{n}\outerproduct{n}{n}
be its spectral decomposition.
The rates of irreversible entropy production and dynamical activity are given by
\sigma=\sum_{k}\tr{L_k\pi L_k^\dagger}\Delta s_{k}
and
\gamma=\sum_{k}\tr{L_k\pi L_k^\dagger}
, respectively.
The system is measured by the eigenbasis
\{\outerproduct{n}{n}\}
at both the initial and final times.
Note that this two-point measurement scheme does not alter the steady state of the system.
Define observables
{A}\coloneqq\sum_{n}a_{n}\outerproduct{n}{n}
and
{B}\coloneqq\sum_{n}b_{n}\outerproduct{n}{n}
.
As will be shown later, quantum properties emerge even for this measurement basis.
In this case, the cross-correlation can be analytically calculated as
fnt b

C_{ba}^{\tau}=\expectationvalue{b(\tau)a(0)}=\tr{{B} e^{\mca{L}\tau}({A}\pi)}.

(9)

Let
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}
be an adjoint superoperator of
\mathcal{L}
, defined as

\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\varrho)\coloneqq i[H,\varrho]+\sum_{k}(L_{k}^{\dagger}\varrho L_{k}-\{L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k},\varrho\}/2).

(10)

Note that both
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}
and
\mathcal{L}
have the same eigenvalue spectrum,
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}({V}_{n})=\lambda_{n}{V}_{n}
, where
0=\lambda_{1}>\Re{\lambda_2}\geq\dots
and the eigenvectors are normalized such that
\|{V}_{n}\|_{\infty}=1
.
For any operator
{X}
, let
{X}_{t}\coloneqq e^{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}t}({X})
be the time-evolved operator in the Heisenberg picture, and
\|X\|_{*}\coloneqq\sum_{n\geq 2}|z_{n}^{x}|
be the
\ell_{1}
-norm of
X
, where
X=\sum_{n}z_{n}^{x}V_{n}
.
The definition of the spectral gap is analogous to the classical case,
g\coloneqq-\Re{\lambda_2}
.

With the above setup in place, we obtain a quantum extension of the classical result (
7
), indicating that the asymmetry of cross-correlations is limited by both dissipation and quantum coherence (see Appendix
C.1
for the proof):

|\delta C_{ba}^{\tau}|\leq\tau e^{-g\tau}\quantity[\mca{C} + \|A\|_*\|B\|_*\sigma\Phi\qty(\frac{\sigma}{2\gamma})^{-1}].

(11)

Here,
\mathcal{C}
is a quantum coherence term that quantifies the amount of quantum coherence generated in the time-evolved observables in the Heisenberg picture, given by

\mathcal{C}\coloneqq\Theta\int_{0}^{1}\differential{s}\quantity[ \|B\|_*{C}_{\ell_1}(A_{(1-s)\tau}) + \|A\|_*{C}_{\ell_1}(B_{s\tau}) ],

(12)

where
\Theta\coloneqq 4\|H\|_{\infty}+3\sum_{k}\|L_{k}\|_{\infty}^{2}
,
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
denotes the operator norm, and
{C}_{\ell_{1}}(X_{t})\coloneqq e^{gt}\sum_{m\neq n}|\matrixelement{m}{X_t}{n}|
is the
\ell_{1}
-norm of quantum coherence in the eigenbasis
Baumgratz et al. 2014
.
Note that
{C}_{\ell_{1}}(X_{t})
is always upper bounded by
N(N-1)\|X\|_{*}
for all

.
Roughly speaking, a nonvanishing value of
\mathcal{C}
signifies the ability to generate quantum coherence in the original dynamics.
In the classical limit (e.g.,
H=0
and
L_{k}\propto\outerproduct{m}{n}
),
\mathcal{C}
vanishes.
Bound (
11
) establishes a qualitative and quantitative relationship between asymmetry, thermodynamic irreversibility, and quantum coherence.
Remarkably,
\delta C_{ba}^{\tau}
can be nonzero even when
\sigma=0
, indicating that quantum coherence is inevitable in the bound (see Appendix

for an analytical analysis).
While this bound may not be strict, it yields a crucial implication that coherent manipulations can be exploited to break the symmetry of correlations even in the absence of dissipation.
Note that
C_{\ell_{1}}
defined for operators is different from the conventional quantum coherence in quantum states; nonetheless, they share the same essence of quantum coherence in quantum dynamics.
Specifically, the coherence term
C_{\ell_{1}}(A_{t}/B_{t})
is exactly a weighted sum of quantum coherence generated at time

with respect to initial incoherent states
\{\outerproduct{n}{n}\}
in the Heisenberg picture

.
Due to the importance of the Heisenberg picture in both dynamic and thermodynamic aspects, this kind of quantum coherence is also physically relevant as the conventional one in the Schrödinger picture.

III.2
Second and third main results

In the sequel, we revisit
classical
systems and focus on some quantifications of the normalized asymmetry that have been considered in the literature.
Our second main result is a thermodynamic bound for the normalized asymmetry only in terms of entropy production (see Appendix

\frac{|\delta C_{ba}^{\tau}|^{2}}{D_{a}^{\tau}+D_{b}^{\tau}}\leq\tau\sigma\min\quantity{\|a^2+b^2\|_\infty,\qty[\frac{\max_c\ell_c}{2N\tan(\pi/N)}]^2},

(13)

where
D_{a}^{\tau}\coloneqq C_{aa}^{0}-C_{aa}^{\tau}
is the decay of auto-correlation

;
Pietzonka et al. 2016

\ell_{c}\coloneqq\sum_{i}\sqrt{(a_{n_{i}}-a_{n_{i+1}})^{2}+(b_{n_{i}}-b_{n_{i+1}})^{2}}
is the length of cycle

Here,

In the short-time limit, bound (

Therefore, it can be regarded as a finite-time generalization of Shiraishi’s bound.
Since the normalized asymmetry is experimentally measurable, our bound can be used to estimate entropy production based on trajectory data obtained from experiments, similar to the tool provided by the thermodynamic uncertainty relation

;
Manikandan et al. 2020
;

It is noteworthy that this bound is tight and saturable in the short-time limit

This leads us to anticipate the existence of exponentially decaying bounds.
Investigating such bounds is left as future work.

So far, we have demonstrated that entropy production serves as a limit for the asymmetry of cross-correlations in finite time.
Our third primary finding is an additional constraint expressed in terms of thermodynamic affinity, given by

\frac{|\delta C_{ba}^{\tau}|}{2\sqrt{D_{a}^{\tau}D_{b}^{\tau}}}\leq\max_{c}\frac{\tanh(\mca{F}_c^\tau/2|c|)}{\tan(\pi/|c|)}\leq\max_{c}\frac{\mathcal{F}_{c}^{\tau}}{2\pi},

IV
Summary and discussion

We derived finite-time thermodynamic bounds for the asymmetry of cross-correlations in terms of entropy production and thermodynamic affinity.
The results universally apply to various dynamics, from discrete to continuous time and space domains.
At the fundamental level, our findings indicate that dissipation limits the asymmetry of cross-correlations across the entire time regime.
This not only generalizes the principle of microscopic reversibility to nonequilibrium situations but also yields thermodynamic constraints on physical functions, such as viscosity in active fluids and signal transduction in biological systems.
In the context of quantum systems, we have elucidated the pivotal role of quantum coherence in breaking the symmetry of correlations, offering fresh insights into the relationship between asymmetry, dissipation, and quantum coherence.
From a practical standpoint, our results can be applied to infer the spectral gap and dissipative quantities, such as entropy production and coarse-grained thermodynamic affinity.

It is also worthwhile to explore applications of our results in biochemical systems, where the concepts of correlation and symmetry breaking play crucial roles in the performance of systems
Govern and ten
Wolde 2014
;
Cao et al. 2015
;
Barato and Seifert 2017
;
Seara et al. 2021
;
Leighton and Sivak 2022
;
Liang et al. 2022
.
Furthermore, it would be intriguing to develop our bounds in other quantum scenarios, such as when the system is measured in a basis different from the eigenbasis of the steady state.
Quantum measurements have a unique impact that can influence the asymmetry of correlations, causing them to persist even when the system is initially in equilibriums.
Such investigations could provide valuable insights into the behavior of quantum systems and potentially lead to advancements in harnessing the merits of quantum measurements.

Appendix A
Proof of Eq. (
7
) and an improved bound

A.1
Proof of Eq. (
7
)

For convenience, we define
J\coloneqq[j_{mn}]
, which is the matrix of probability currents.
By simple algebraic calculations, we can transform the asymmetry of cross-correlations as follows:

\displaystyle\delta C_{ba}^{\tau}

\displaystyle=\matrixelement{b}{e^{{W}\tau}\Pi}{a}-\matrixelement{a}{e^{{W}\tau}\Pi}{b}

\displaystyle=\matrixelement{b}{e^{{W}\tau}\Pi}{a}-\matrixelement{b}{\Pi e^{{W}^\dagger\tau}}{a}

\displaystyle=\matrixelement{b}{(e^{{W}\tau}\Pi-\Pi e^{{W}^\dagger\tau})}{a}.

(16)

Here, we use the fact
z=z^{\dagger}
for any
z\in\mathbb{R}
in the second line.
Note that for any matrices
{X}
and
{Y}
, the following equality always holds:

e^{{X}}\Pi-\Pi e^{{Y}}=\int_{0}^{1}\differential{s}e^{s{X}}({X}\Pi-\Pi{Y})e^{(1-s){Y}}.

(17)

Applying
{X}={{W}}\tau
and
{Y}={{W}}^{\dagger}\tau
and noting that
{J}={{W}}\Pi-\Pi{{W}}^{\dagger}
, we can proceed Eq. (
16
) further as follows:

\displaystyle\delta C_{ba}^{\tau}

\displaystyle=\tau\int_{0}^{1}\differential{s}\matrixelement{b}{e^{s{{W}}\tau}{J} e^{(1-s){{W}}^\dagger\tau}}{a}

\displaystyle=\tau\int_{0}^{1}\differential{s}\tr{{J} e^{(1-s){{W}}^\dagger\tau}\dyad{a}{b}e^{s{{W}}\tau}}.

(18)

Noting that
\ket{a}=\sum_{n}\tilde{a}_{n}\ket{v_n^l}
,
\ket{b}=\sum_{n}\tilde{b}_{n}\ket{v_n^l}
, and
\int_{0}^{1}\differential{s}e^{(1-s)x+sy}=(e^{x}-e^{y})/(x-y)
, we can simplify the terms in Eq. (
18
) as

\displaystyle\delta C_{ba}^{\tau}

\displaystyle=\tau\int_{0}^{1}\differential{s}\sum_{m,n}\tr{{J}\tilde{a}_m\tilde{b}_n^*e^{(1-s)\lambda_m^*\tau+s\lambda_n\tau}\dyad{v_m^l}{v_n^l}}

\displaystyle=\tau\tr{{J}\sum_{m,n\ge 2}\tilde{a}_m\tilde{b}_n^*\frac{e^{\lambda_m^*\tau}-e^{\lambda_n\tau}}{(\lambda_m^*-\lambda_n)\tau}\dyad{v_m^l}{v_n^l}},

(19)

where we use the facts that
\bra{v_1^l}{J}=\bra{0}
and
{J}\ket{v_1^l}=\ket{0}
to obtain the last line.
Here,
\ket{0}
denotes the all-zero vector.
Before proceeding further, we note some useful inequalities (see Appendix
E
for the proof).
For any matrices
{X}
and
{Y}
and orthogonal basis
\{\ket{n}\}
, we have
\quantity|\tr{{X}{Y}}|\leq\|{Y}\|_{\infty}\sum_{m,n}|\matrixelement{m}{{X}}{n}|
, where
\|{Y}\|_{\infty}
denotes the operator norm of
{Y}
.
In addition, for any complex numbers
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
with a negative real part (i.e.,
\Re{z_1}\leq 0
and
\Re{z_2}\leq 0
), we always have
|e^{z_{1}}-e^{z_{2}}|/|z_{1}-z_{2}|\leq 1
.
Using the expression (
19
) and applying the above inequalities, we can evaluate as follows:

\displaystyle|\delta C_{ba}^{\tau}|

\displaystyle\leq\tau\sum_{m,n}|j_{mn}|\left\|\sum_{m,n\geq 2}\tilde{a}_{m}\tilde{b}_{n}^{*}\frac{e^{\lambda_{m}^{*}\tau}-e^{\lambda_{n}\tau}}{(\lambda_{m}^{*}-\lambda_{n})\tau}\outerproduct{v_m^l}{v_n^l}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\tau\sum_{m,n}|j_{mn}|\sum_{m,n\geq 2}|\tilde{a}_{m}\tilde{b}_{n}^{*}|\quantity|\frac{e^{\lambda_m^*\tau}-e^{\lambda_n\tau}}{(\lambda_m^*-\lambda_n)\tau}|.

(20)

Next, we evaluate the last term in Eq. (
20
).
For
m\geq n\geq 2
, since
\Re{\lambda_m^*-\lambda_n}\leq 0
and
\Re{\lambda_n}\leq-g
, we thus have

\displaystyle\quantity|\frac{e^{\lambda_m^*\tau}-e^{\lambda_n\tau}}{(\lambda_m^*-\lambda_n)\tau}|

\displaystyle=|e^{\lambda_{n}\tau}|\quantity|\frac{e^{(\lambda_m^*-\lambda_n)\tau}-1}{(\lambda_m^*-\lambda_n)\tau}|\leq e^{-g\tau}.

(21)

Likewise, for
n\geq m\geq 2
, since
\Re{\lambda_n-\lambda_m^*}\leq 0
and
\Re{\lambda_m^*}\leq-g
, we also obtain the same argument.
Applying these inequalities to Eq. (
20
), we readily obtain

|\delta C_{ba}^{\tau}|\leq\tau e^{-g\tau}\|a\|_{*}\|b\|_{*}\sum_{m,n}|j_{mn}|.

(22)

Furthermore, we can prove that the sum of absolute probability currents is bounded from above by the rates of entropy production, dynamical activity, and dynamical state mobility as
Van Vu and Saito 2023

\sum_{m,n}|j_{mn}|\leq\sigma\Phi\quantity(\frac{\sigma}{2\gamma})^{-1}\leq 2\sqrt{\sigma\kappa}.

(23)

Combining Eqs. (
22
) and (
23
) yields the desired result (
7
) in the main text.

A.2
A quantitative improvement of bound (
7
) and numerical demonstration

Here we demonstrate that the bound (
7
) can be quantitatively improved; however, this improvement comes at the cost of
implicitly
exhibiting the exponential decay.
To this end, we define
\ket{x(t)}\coloneqq(e^{W^{\dagger}t}-\outerproduct{1}{\pi})\ket{x}
for
x\in\{a,b\}
.
Using this notation and Eq. (
18
), the correlation asymmetry can be calculated as

\displaystyle\delta C_{ba}^{\tau}

\displaystyle=\tau\int_{0}^{1}\differential{s}\matrixelement{b(s\tau)}{J}{a((1-s)\tau)}

\displaystyle=\tau\sum_{m\neq n}\int_{0}^{1}\differential{s}j_{mn}a_{n}((1-s)\tau)b_{m}(s\tau)

\displaystyle=\tau\sum_{m>n}\int_{0}^{1}\differential{s}j_{mn}\quantity[a_n((1-s)\tau)b_m(s\tau)-a_m((1-s)\tau)b_n(s\tau)].

(24)

Defining
\gamma_{mn}\coloneqq w_{mn}\pi_{n}+w_{nm}\pi_{m}
and applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we can evaluate the asymmetry from above as

\displaystyle|\delta C_{ba}^{\tau}|

\displaystyle\leq\tau\int_{0}^{1}\differential{s}\sqrt{\sum_{m>n}\frac{j_{mn}^{2}}{\gamma_{mn}}\sum_{m>n}\quantity[a_n((1-s)\tau)b_m(s\tau)-a_m((1-s)\tau)b_n(s\tau)]^{2}\gamma_{mn}}

\displaystyle=\tau\sqrt{\sum_{m>n}\frac{j_{mn}^{2}}{\gamma_{mn}}}\int_{0}^{1}\differential{s}\sqrt{\sum_{m>n}\quantity[a_n((1-s)\tau)b_m(s\tau)-a_m((1-s)\tau)b_n(s\tau)]^{2}\gamma_{mn}}.

(25)

Note that the first term in Eq. (
25
) is the pseudo entropy production rate and can be upper bounded as
Vo et al. 2022

\sum_{m>n}\frac{j_{mn}^{2}}{\gamma_{mn}}\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4\gamma}\Phi\quantity(\frac{\sigma}{2\gamma})^{-2}.

(26)

Defining

D_{ba}^{\tau}\coloneqq\int_{0}^{1}\differential{s}\sqrt{\sum_{m>n}\quantity[a_n((1-s)\tau)b_m(s\tau)-a_m((1-s)\tau)b_n(s\tau)]^{2}\gamma_{mn}},

(27)

we immediately obtain an improvement for Eq. (
7
) as

|\delta C_{ba}^{\tau}|\leq\tau D_{ba}^{\tau}\frac{\sigma}{2\sqrt{\gamma}}\Phi\quantity(\frac{\sigma}{2\gamma})^{-1},

(28)

where
D_{ba}^{\tau}
exponentially decays over time.
Notably, this improved bound is tight and can be saturated, for example, in a three-state biochemical oscillation (see Fig.
3
for the numerical demonstration).
We can also prove that

D_{ba}^{\tau}\leq 2e^{-g\tau}\|a\|_{*}\|b\|_{*}\sqrt{\gamma}.

(29)

Using this relation, the bound (
7
) in the main text can be recovered.

e^{W^{\dagger}t}=\outerproduct{1}{\pi}+\sum_{n\geq 2}e^{\lambda_{n}^{*}t}\outerproduct{v_n^l}{v_n^r}.

(30)

We begin by upper bounding the term inside the integral in Eq. (

) as follows:

\displaystyle\sum_{m>n}\quantity[a_n((1-s)\tau)b_m(s\tau)-a_m((1-s)\tau)b_n(s\tau)]^{2}\gamma_{mn}

\displaystyle\leq 4\|a((1-s)\tau)\|_{\infty}^{2}\|b(s\tau)\|_{\infty}^{2}\sum_{m>n}\gamma_{mn}

(31)

\displaystyle=4\gamma\|\sum_{n\geq 2}e^{\lambda_{n}^{*}(1-s)\tau}\ket{v_n^l}\innerproduct{v_n^r}{a}\|_{\infty}^{2}\|\sum_{n\geq 2}e^{\lambda_{n}^{*}s\tau}\ket{v_n^l}\innerproduct{v_n^r}{b}\|_{\infty}^{2}

Appendix B
Generalizations of Eq. (
7
) to other cases

B.1
Generalization to discrete-time Markov chains

Here we provide a generalization of Eq. (
7
) for discrete-time Markov chains.
We consider a time-homogeneous irreducible Markov chain whose dynamics is governed by the master equation:

\ket{p_{t_i}}={R}\ket{p_{t_{i-1}}}.

(34)

Here,
{R}=[R_{mn}]
is the stochastic matrix with
R_{mn}\geq 0
the transition probability from state
n
to
m
.
The normalization condition
\sum_{m}R_{mn}=1
is satisfied for all
n
.
We consider a finite duration
\tau
of
K
steps (i.e.,
t_{0}=0
and
t_{K}=\tau
).
The system is in a nonequilibrium steady state
\ket{\pi}
(i.e.,
\ket{\pi}={R}\ket{\pi}
).
The steady-state entropy production and dynamical activity at each time step are given by

\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{m\neq n}R_{mn}\pi_{n}\ln\frac{R_{mn}\pi_{n}}{R_{nm}\pi_{m}},

(35)

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{m\neq n}R_{mn}\pi_{n}.

(36)

The cross-correlation between the observables can be expressed as

C_{ba}^{\tau}\coloneqq\expectationvalue{b(\tau)a(0)}=\matrixelement{b}{{R}^K\Pi}{a}.

(37)

Let
\{\lambda_{n}\}
be the set of eigenvalues of
{R}
and
\{\ket{v_n^l},\ket{v_n^r}\}
be the left and right eigenvectors, respectively (i.e.,
\bra{v_n^l}{R}=\bra{v_n^l}\lambda_{n}
and
{R}\ket{v_n^r}=\lambda_{n}\ket{v_n^r}
).
Note that
1=\lambda_{1}>|\lambda_{2}|\geq\dots
and
\ket{v_1}\propto\ket{1}
.
The spectral gap can thus be defined as
g\coloneqq-\ln|\lambda_{2}|>0
.
Evidently,
|\lambda_{n}|\leq e^{-g}
for any
n\geq 2
.

Now, we can calculate the asymmetry of cross-correlations as follows:

\displaystyle\delta C_{ba}^{\tau}

\displaystyle=\matrixelement{b}{{R}^K\Pi}{a}-\matrixelement{a}{{R}^K\Pi}{b}

\displaystyle=\matrixelement{b}{{R}^K\Pi- \Pi({R}^\dagger)^K}{a}.

(38)

Note that for any matrices
{X}
and
{Y}
, the following relation holds:

{X}^{K}\Pi-\Pi{Y}^{K}=\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}{X}^{k}({X}\Pi-\Pi{Y}){Y}^{K-1-k}.

(39)

Applying this relation for
{X}={R}
and
{Y}={R}^{\dagger}
and noting that
{J}={R}\Pi-\Pi{R}^{\dagger}
, we can proceed further as

\displaystyle\delta C_{ba}^{\tau}

\displaystyle=\matrixelement{b}{{R}^K\Pi- \Pi({R}^\dagger)^K}{a}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\matrixelement{b}{{R}^k({R}\Pi-\Pi{R}^\dagger)({R}^\dagger)^{K-1-k}}{a}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\tr{{J}({R}^\dagger)^{K-1-k}\dyad{a}{b}{R}^k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\sum_{m,n}\tr{{J} a_mb_n^*(\lambda_m^*)^{K-1-k}\lambda_n^{k}\dyad{v_m^l}{v_n^l}}.

(40)

Note that
{J}\ket{1}=\ket{0}
and
\bra{1}{J}=\bra{0}
.
Consequently, the asymmetry of cross-correlations can be upper bounded as

\displaystyle|\delta C_{ba}^{\tau}|

\displaystyle=\quantity|\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\sum_{m,n\ge 2}\tr{{J} a_mb_n^*(\lambda_m^*)^{K-1-k}\lambda_n^{k}\dyad{v_m^l}{v_n^l}}|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m,n}|j_{mn}|\left\|\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\sum_{m,n\geq 2}a_{m}b_{n}^{*}(\lambda_{m}^{*})^{K-1-k}\lambda_{n}^{k}\outerproduct{v_m^l}{v_n^l}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq Ke^{-(K-1)g}\sum_{m,n}|j_{mn}|\sum_{m,n\geq 2}|a_{m}b_{n}^{*}|

\displaystyle\leq Ke^{-(K-1)g}\|a\|_{*}\|b\|_{*}\sigma\Phi\quantity(\frac{\sigma}{2\gamma})^{-1},

(41)

which yields the desired generalization for discrete-time systems:

\frac{|\delta C_{ba}^{\tau}|}{\|a\|_{*}\|b\|_{*}}\leq Ke^{-(K-1)g}\sigma\Phi\quantity(\frac{\sigma}{2\gamma})^{-1}.

(42)

B.2
Generalization to overdamped Langevin dynamics

We consider a
d
-dimensional overdamped Langevin system.
Let
p_{t}({\bf\it x})
denote the probability density function of finding the system in state
{\bf\it x}
at time
t
.
The time evolution of
p_{t}({\bf\it x})
is described by the Fokker-Planck equation:

\dot{p}_{t}({\bf\it x})=\mathcal{L}[p_{t}({\bf\it x})]=-{\bf\it\nabla}\cdot{\bf\it j}_{t}({\bf\it x}),

(43)

where
\mathcal{L}[p({\bf\it x})]\coloneqq-{\bf\it\nabla}\cdot[{\bf\it f}({\bf\it x})p({\bf\it x})-{D}{\bf\it\nabla}p({\bf\it x})]
is the Fokker-Planck operator,
{\bf\it f}({\bf\it x})
is the force vector, and
{D}={\rm diag}(D_{1},\dots,D_{d})
is the matrix of diffusion coefficients.
Consider the adjoint operator
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}
, which is defined as

\tilde{\mathcal{L}}[p({\bf\it x})]\coloneqq{\bf\it f}({\bf\it x})\cdot{\bf\it\nabla}p({\bf\it x})+{\bf\it\nabla}\cdot{D}{\bf\it\nabla}p({\bf\it x}).

(44)

The operator
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}
is also known as the generator of the backward Fokker-Planck equation.
Define the inner product

\expectationvalue{u(\vb*{x}),v(\vb*{x})}\coloneqq\int\differential{\vb*{x}}u({\bf\it x})v({\bf\it x}).

(45)

For any functions
u({\bf\it x})
and
v({\bf\it x})
such that
u({\bf\it x}){\bf\it f}({\bf\it x})v({\bf\it x})
,
u({\bf\it x}){D}{\bf\it\nabla}v({\bf\it x})
, and
v({\bf\it x}){D}{\bf\it\nabla}u({\bf\it x})
vanish at infinity, we prove that

\expectationvalue{u(\vb*{x}),\mca{L}[v(\vb*{x})]}=\expectationvalue{v(\vb*{x}),\tilde{\mca{L}}[u(\vb*{x})]}.

(46)

Indeed, by exploiting the boundary conditions, we can show as

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{u(\vb*{x}),\mca{L}[v(\vb*{x})]}

\displaystyle=-\int\differential{\vb*{x}}u({\bf\it x}){\bf\it\nabla}\cdot[{\bf\it f}({\bf\it x})v({\bf\it x})-{D}{\bf\it\nabla}v({\bf\it x})]

\displaystyle=\int\differential{\vb*{x}}[{\bf\it f}({\bf\it x})v({\bf\it x})-{D}{\bf\it\nabla}v({\bf\it x})]\cdot{\bf\it\nabla}u({\bf\it x})

\displaystyle=\int\differential{\vb*{x}}[v({\bf\it x}){\bf\it f}({\bf\it x})\cdot{\bf\it\nabla}u({\bf\it x})+v({\bf\it x}){\bf\it\nabla}\cdot{D}{\bf\it\nabla}u({\bf\it x})]

\displaystyle=\int\differential{\vb*{x}}v({\bf\it x})\quantity[\vb*{f}(\vb*{x})\cdot\vb*{\nabla}u(\vb*{x})+\vb*{\nabla}\cdot{D}\vb*{\nabla}u(\vb*{x})]

\displaystyle=\expectationvalue{v(\vb*{x}),\tilde{\mca{L}}[u(\vb*{x})]}.

(47)

Using this relation, we can immediately derive that

\expectationvalue{u(\vb*{x}),e^{\mca{L}\tau}[v(\vb*{x})]}=\expectationvalue{v(\vb*{x}),e^{\tilde{\mca{L}}\tau}[u(\vb*{x})]}.

(48)

We consider the case where the system is in a nonequilibrium steady state
\pi({\bf\it x})
(i.e.,
\mathcal{L}[\pi({\bf\it x})]=-{\bf\it\nabla}\cdot{\bf\it j}({\bf\it x})=0
).
The entropy production rate can be calculated as

\sigma=\int\differential{\vb*{x}}\frac{{\bf\it j}({\bf\it x})\cdot{D}^{-1}{\bf\it j}({\bf\it x})}{\pi({\bf\it x})}.

(49)

The cross-correlation between observables
a({\bf\it x})
and
b({\bf\it x})
can be expressed as

C_{ba}^{\tau}\coloneqq\expectationvalue{b(\vb*{x}_\tau)a(\vb*{x}_0)}=\int\differential{\vb*{x}}b({\bf\it x})e^{\mathcal{L}\tau}\quantity[a(\vb*{x})\pi(\vb*{x})].

(50)

Using the equality (
48
), the asymmetry of cross-correlations can thus be calculated as

\displaystyle\delta C_{ba}^{\tau}

\displaystyle=\int\differential{\vb*{x}}b({\bf\it x})e^{\mathcal{L}\tau}\quantity[a(\vb*{x})\pi(\vb*{x})]-\int\differential{\vb*{x}}a({\bf\it x})e^{\mathcal{L}\tau}\quantity[b(\vb*{x})\pi(\vb*{x})]

\displaystyle=\int\differential{\vb*{x}}b({\bf\it x})\quantity{ e^{\mca{L}\tau}[a(\vb*{x})\pi(\vb*{x})] - \pi(\vb*{x})e^{\tilde{\mca{L}}\tau}[a(\vb*{x})] }

\displaystyle=\int\differential{\vb*{x}}b({\bf\it x})\int_{0}^{1}\differential{s}\frac{d}{ds}{e^{s\mathcal{L}\tau}\quantity{e^{(1-s)\tilde{\mca{L}}\tau}[a(\vb*{x})]\pi(\vb*{x})}}

\displaystyle=\tau\int\differential{\vb*{x}}b({\bf\it x})\int_{0}^{1}\differential{s}e^{s\mathcal{L}\tau}\quantity{\mca{L}[\pi(\vb*{x})\circ] - \pi(\vb*{x})\tilde{\mca{L}}[\circ]}\quantity[e^{(1-s)\tilde{\mca{L}}\tau}a(\vb*{x})].

(51)

From the stationarity, we also obtain that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}[\pi({\bf\it x})q({\bf\it x})]-\pi({\bf\it x})\tilde{\mathcal{L}}[q({\bf\it x})]

\displaystyle=-{\bf\it\nabla}\cdot\quantity{\vb*{f}(\vb*{x})\pi(\vb*{x})q(\vb*{x})-{D}\vb*{\nabla}[\pi(\vb*{x})q(\vb*{x})]}-\pi({\bf\it x})\quantity[\vb*{f}(\vb*{x})\cdot\vb*{\nabla}q(\vb*{x})+\vb*{\nabla}\cdot{D}\vb*{\nabla}q(\vb*{x})]

\displaystyle=-2{\bf\it f}({\bf\it x})\pi({\bf\it x})\cdot{\bf\it\nabla}q({\bf\it x})-q({\bf\it x}){\bf\it\nabla}\cdot[{\bf\it f}({\bf\it x})\pi({\bf\it x})]+{\bf\it\nabla}\cdot{D}{\bf\it\nabla}[\pi({\bf\it x})q({\bf\it x})]-\pi({\bf\it x}){\bf\it\nabla}\cdot{D}{\bf\it\nabla}q({\bf\it x})

\displaystyle=-2{\bf\it f}({\bf\it x})\pi({\bf\it x})\cdot{\bf\it\nabla}q({\bf\it x})-q({\bf\it x}){\bf\it\nabla}\cdot[{\bf\it f}({\bf\it x})\pi({\bf\it x})]+{\bf\it\nabla}\pi({\bf\it x})\cdot{D}{\bf\it\nabla}q({\bf\it x})+{\bf\it\nabla}q({\bf\it x})\cdot{D}{\bf\it\nabla}\pi({\bf\it x})+q({\bf\it x}){\bf\it\nabla}\cdot{D}{\bf\it\nabla}\pi({\bf\it x})

\displaystyle=-2{\bf\it\nabla}q({\bf\it x})\cdot[{\bf\it f}({\bf\it x})\pi({\bf\it x})-{D}{\bf\it\nabla}\pi({\bf\it x})]-q({\bf\it x}){\bf\it\nabla}\cdot[{\bf\it f}({\bf\it x})\pi({\bf\it x})-{D}{\bf\it\nabla}\pi({\bf\it x})]

\displaystyle=-2{\bf\it\nabla}q({\bf\it x})\cdot{\bf\it j}({\bf\it x}).

(52)

Let
\{\lambda_{n}\}
be the discrete spectrum of operator
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}
and
\{\varphi_{n}({\bf\it x})\}
be the set of corresponding eigenfunctions:

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{L}}\varphi_{n}({\bf\it x})=\lambda_{n}\varphi_{n}({\bf\it x}).

(53)

Assume that
a({\bf\it x})
and
b({\bf\it x})
can be expanded in terms of the eigenfunctions as

\displaystyle a({\bf\it x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}a_{n}\varphi_{n}({\bf\it x}),

(54)

\displaystyle b({\bf\it x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}b_{n}\varphi_{n}({\bf\it x}).

(55)

The eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}=0
corresponds to the eigenfunction
\varphi_{1}({\bf\it x})=1
.
Other eigenvalues have a negative real part,
0>\Re{\lambda_2}\geq\Re{\lambda_3}\geq\dots
; thus, the spectral gap can be defined as
g\coloneqq-\Re{\lambda_2}>0
.
Using Eqs. (
51
) and (
52
), we can evaluate the asymmetry of cross-correlations as

\displaystyle\delta C_{ba}^{\tau}

\displaystyle=-\tau\int_{0}^{1}\differential{s}\int\differential{\vb*{x}}b({\bf\it x})e^{s\mathcal{L}\tau}\quantity[\qty{2\vb*{\nabla}e^{(1-s)\tilde{\mca{L}}\tau}a(\vb*{x})}\cdot\vb*{j}(\vb*{x})]

\displaystyle=-\tau\int_{0}^{1}\differential{s}\int\differential{\vb*{x}}\quantity[\qty{2\vb*{\nabla}e^{(1-s)\tilde{\mca{L}}\tau}a(\vb*{x})}\cdot\vb*{j}(\vb*{x})]e^{s\tilde{\mathcal{L}}\tau}[b({\bf\it x})]

\displaystyle=-\tau\int_{0}^{1}\differential{s}\int\differential{\vb*{x}}\quantity[\qty{\sum_n2\vb*{\nabla}e^{(1-s)\tau\lambda_n} a_n\varphi_n(\vb*{x})}\cdot\vb*{j}(\vb*{x})]\sum_{m}e^{s\tau\lambda_{m}}b_{m}\varphi_{m}({\bf\it x})

\displaystyle=-\tau\int\differential{\vb*{x}}\int_{0}^{1}\differential{s}\sum_{m,n}e^{s\tau\lambda_{m}+(1-s)\tau\lambda_{n}}b_{m}a_{n}\quantity[2\varphi_m(\vb*{x})\vb*{\nabla}\varphi_n(\vb*{x})]\cdot{\bf\it j}({\bf\it x})

\displaystyle=-\tau\int\differential{\vb*{x}}\sum_{m,n}\frac{e^{\lambda_{m}\tau}-e^{\lambda_{n}\tau}}{(\lambda_{m}-\lambda_{n})\tau}b_{m}a_{n}\quantity[2\varphi_m(\vb*{x})\vb*{\nabla}\varphi_n(\vb*{x})]\cdot{\bf\it j}({\bf\it x})

\displaystyle=-\tau\int\differential{\vb*{x}}\sum_{m,n\geq 2}\frac{e^{\lambda_{m}\tau}-e^{\lambda_{n}\tau}}{(\lambda_{m}-\lambda_{n})\tau}b_{m}a_{n}\quantity[2\varphi_m(\vb*{x})\vb*{\nabla}\varphi_n(\vb*{x})]\cdot{\bf\it j}({\bf\it x})

\displaystyle=\tau\int\differential{\vb*{x}}\sum_{m,n\geq 2}\frac{e^{\lambda_{m}\tau}-e^{\lambda_{n}\tau}}{(\lambda_{m}-\lambda_{n})\tau}b_{m}a_{n}\quantity[\varphi_n(\vb*{x})\vb*{\nabla}\varphi_m(\vb*{x})-\varphi_m(\vb*{x})\vb*{\nabla}\varphi_n(\vb*{x})]\cdot{\bf\it j}({\bf\it x})

\displaystyle=\tau e^{-g\tau}\int\differential{\vb*{x}}{\bf\it\varphi}_{\tau}({\bf\it x})\cdot{\bf\it j}({\bf\it x}),

(56)

where we use the fact
\int\differential{\vb*{x}}[\varphi_{1}({\bf\it x}){\bf\it\nabla}\varphi_{n}({\bf\it x})]\cdot{\bf\it j}({\bf\it x})=\int\differential{\vb*{x}}[\varphi_{m}({\bf\it x}){\bf\it\nabla}\varphi_{1}({\bf\it x})]\cdot{\bf\it j}({\bf\it x})=0
and define

{\bf\it\varphi}_{\tau}({\bf\it x})\coloneqq\sum_{m,n\geq 2}\frac{e^{(\lambda_{m}+g)\tau}-e^{(\lambda_{n}+g)\tau}}{(\lambda_{m}-\lambda_{n})\tau}b_{m}a_{n}\quantity[\varphi_n(\vb*{x})\vb*{\nabla}\varphi_m(\vb*{x})-\varphi_m(\vb*{x})\vb*{\nabla}\varphi_n(\vb*{x})].

(57)

Applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle|\delta C_{ba}^{\tau}|

\displaystyle\leq\tau e^{-g\tau}\quantity[\int\dd{\vb*{x}}\frac{\vb*{j}(\vb*{x})\cdot{D}^{-1}\vb*{j}(\vb*{x})}{\pi(\vb*{x})}]^{1/2}\quantity[\int\dd{\vb*{x}}\pi(\vb*{x})\vb*{\varphi}_\tau(\vb*{x})\cdot{D}\vb*{\varphi}_\tau(\vb*{x})]^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\tau e^{-g\tau}\sqrt{\sigma}\quantity[\int\dd{\vb*{x}}\pi(\vb*{x})\vb*{\varphi}_\tau(\vb*{x})\cdot{D}\vb*{\varphi}_\tau(\vb*{x})]^{1/2}.

(58)

For any vector
{\bf\it z}=[z_{1},\dots,z_{d}]^{\top}
, define
|{\bf\it z}|\coloneqq[|z_{1}|,\dots,|z_{d}|]^{\top}
.
Since

\quantity| \frac{e^{(\lambda_m+g)\tau}-e^{(\lambda_n+g)\tau}}{(\lambda_m-\lambda_n)\tau} |\leq 1~\forall m,n\geq 2,

(59)

we can upper bound the last term in Eq. (
58
) as

\displaystyle\int\differential{\vb*{x}}\pi({\bf\it x}){\bf\it\varphi}_{\tau}({\bf\it x})\cdot{D}{\bf\it\varphi}_{\tau}({\bf\it x})\leq\int\differential{\vb*{x}}\pi({\bf\it x}){\bf\it\phi}({\bf\it x})\cdot{D}{\bf\it\phi}({\bf\it x}),

(60)

where we define

{\bf\it\phi}({\bf\it x})\coloneqq\sum_{m,n\geq 2}|b_{m}||a_{n}||\varphi_{n}({\bf\it x}){\bf\it\nabla}\varphi_{m}({\bf\it x})-\varphi_{m}({\bf\it x}){\bf\it\nabla}\varphi_{n}({\bf\it x})|.

(61)

Defining
\chi_{ba}\coloneqq\quantity[\int\dd{\vb*{x}}\pi(\vb*{x})\vb*{\phi}(\vb*{x})\cdot{D}\vb*{\phi}(\vb*{x})]^{1/2}
, we obtain the following thermodynamic bound on the asymmetry of cross-correlations:

\displaystyle\frac{|\delta C_{ba}^{\tau}|}{\chi_{ba}}

\displaystyle\leq\tau e^{-g\tau}\sqrt{\sigma}.

(62)

B.3
Generalization to multiple observables

The result (
7
) can be generalized to the case of multi-time and multi-observables, where the observables
(\ket{o^1},\dots,\ket{o^M})\eqqcolon{\bf\it o}
are respectively measured at times
(t_{1},\dots,t_{M})\eqqcolon{\bf\it\tau}
.
Here,
M\geq 2
is an arbitrary integer number and
0=t_{1}<t_{2}<\dots<t_{M}=\tau
.
In this case, the cross-correlation can be defined as

C_{{\bf\it o}}^{{\bf\it\tau}}\coloneqq\expectationvalue{o^1(t_1)\dots o^M(t_M)},

(63)

where
o^{m}(t)
takes the value of
o^{m}_{n}
if the system is in state
n
at time
t
.
Define the reversed observation times
\tilde{{\bf\it\tau}}\coloneqq(\tau-t_{1},\dots,\tau-t_{M})
.
Then, the following asymmetry of cross-correlations is a quantity of interest:

\delta C_{{\bf\it o}}^{{\bf\it\tau}}\coloneqq C_{{\bf\it o}}^{{\bf\it\tau}}-C_{{\bf\it o}}^{\tilde{{\bf\it\tau}}}.

(64)

We find that this asymmetry is consistently limited by dissipation and decreases exponentially at the rate of the spectral gap:

\frac{|\delta C_{{\bf\it o}}^{{\bf\it\tau}}|}{\chi_{{\bf\it o}}}\leq\sigma\Phi\quantity(\frac{\sigma}{2\gamma})^{-1}\sum_{k=2}^{M}(t_{k}-t_{k-1})e^{-g(t_{k}-t_{k-1})}.

(65)

Here,
\chi_{{\bf\it o}}\coloneqq\|o^{1}\|_{*}\|o^{M}\|_{*}\prod_{m=2}^{M-1}\|o^{m}\|_{\infty}\quantity(\sum_{n=1}^N\|v_n^r\|_2)^{M-2}
,
\|z\|_{2}\coloneqq\sqrt{\innerproduct{z}{z}}
denotes
\ell_{2}
-norm, and
\|z\|_{\infty}=\max_{n}|z_{n}|
for any vector
\ket{z}
.
Interestingly, bound (
65
) indicates that the degree of decay also depends on the time interval between consecutive measurements.

In what follows, we present the proof of Eq. (
65
).
For convenience, we define
{O}_{m}\coloneqq{\rm diag}(o^{m}_{1},\dots,o^{m}_{N})
.
Then,
C_{{\bf\it o}}^{{\bf\it\tau}}
can be explicitly expressed as

C_{{\bf\it o}}^{{\bf\it\tau}}=\matrixelement{o^M}{\prod_{k=M-1}^{1}e^{{W}(t_{k+1}-t_k)}{O}_k}{\pi}=\matrixelement{o^M}{e^{{W}(t_{M}-t_{M-1})}{O}_{M-1}\dots{O}_2e^{{W}(t_{2}-t_{1})}\Pi}{o^1}.

(66)

Consequently, the asymmetry can be calculated as follows:

\displaystyle\delta C_{{\bf\it o}}^{{\bf\it\tau}}

\displaystyle=\matrixelement{o^M}{e^{{W}(t_{M}-t_{M-1})}{O}_{M-1}\dots{O}_{2}e^{{W}(t_{2}-t_{1})}\Pi}{o^1}-\matrixelement{o^1}{e^{{W}(t_{2}-t_{1})}{O}_{2}\dots{O}_{M-1}e^{{W}(t_{M}-t_{M-1})}\Pi}{o^M}

\displaystyle=\matrixelement{o^M}{e^{{W}(t_{M}-t_{M-1})}{O}_{M-1}\dots{O}_{2}e^{{W}(t_{2}-t_{1})}\Pi}{o^1}-\matrixelement{o^M}{\Pi e^{{W}^\dagger(t_{M}-t_{M-1})}{O}_{M-1}\dots{O}_{2}e^{{W}^\dagger(t_{2}-t_{1})}}{o^1}

\displaystyle=\matrixelement{o^M}{e^{{W}(t_{M}-t_{M-1})}{O}_{M-1}\dots{O}_{2}e^{{W}(t_{2}-t_{1})}\Pi-\Pi e^{{W}^\dagger(t_{M}-t_{M-1})}{O}_{M-1}\dots{O}_{2}e^{{W}^\dagger(t_{2}-t_{1})}}{o^1}.

(67)

For simplicity, we define
\Delta_{m}\coloneqq t_{m}-t_{m-1}>0
.
Noticing that
[{O}_{k},\Pi]=0
for all
k
and
e^{{W}t}=\sum_{i}e^{\lambda_{i}t}\outerproduct{v_i^r}{v_i^l}
, we can further transform
\delta C_{{\bf\it o}}^{{\bf\it\tau}}
as

\displaystyle\delta C_{{\bf\it o}}^{{\bf\it\tau}}

\displaystyle=\matrixelement{o^M}{\sum_{k=2}^{M}e^{{W}\Delta_{M}}{O}_{M-1}\dots e^{{W}\Delta_{k+1}}{O}_{k}\qty[e^{{W}\Delta_{k}}\Pi-\Pi e^{{W}^\dagger\Delta_{k}}]{O}_{k-1}e^{{W}^\dagger\Delta_{k-1}}\dots{O}_{2}e^{{W}^\dagger\Delta_{2}}}{o^1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=2}^{M}\Delta_{k}\int_{0}^{1}\differential{s}\matrixelement{o^M}{\prod_{m=M-1}^{k} e^{{W}\Delta_{m+1}}{O}_{m}\qty[e^{s{W}\Delta_{k}}{J} e^{(1-s){W}^\dagger\Delta_{k}}]\prod_{m=k-1}^{2}{O}_{m}e^{{W}^\dagger\Delta_{m}}}{o^1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=2}^{M}\Delta_{k}\sum_{i,j}\frac{e^{\lambda_{i}\Delta_{k}}-e^{\lambda_{j}^{*}\Delta_{k}}}{(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}^{*})\Delta_{k}}\matrixelement{o^M}{\prod_{m=M-1}^{k} e^{{W}\Delta_{m+1}}{O}_{m}}{v_i^r}\matrixelement{v_i^l}{{J}}{v_j^l}\matrixelement{v_j^r}{\prod_{m=k-1}^{2}{O}_{m}e^{{W}^\dagger\Delta_{m}}}{o^1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=2}^{M}\Delta_{k}\sum_{i,j\geq 2}\frac{e^{\lambda_{i}\Delta_{k}}-e^{\lambda_{j}^{*}\Delta_{k}}}{(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}^{*})\Delta_{k}}\matrixelement{o^M}{\prod_{m=M-1}^{k} e^{{W}\Delta_{m+1}}{O}_{m}}{v_i^r}\matrixelement{v_i^l}{{J}}{v_j^l}\matrixelement{v_j^r}{\prod_{m=k-1}^{2}{O}_{m}e^{{W}^\dagger\Delta_{m}}}{o^1}.

(68)

Next, we upper bound the terms in Eq. (
68
).
Note that for any
i,j\geq 2
, the following inequality holds:

\quantity|\frac{e^{\lambda_i\Delta_k}-e^{\lambda_j^*\Delta_k}}{(\lambda_i-\lambda_j^*)\Delta_k}|\leq e^{-g\Delta_{k}}.

(69)

In addition, since
\Re{\lambda_n}\leq 0
, we can evaluate as follows:

\displaystyle|\matrixelement{o^M}{\prod_{m=M-1}^{k} e^{{W}\Delta_{m+1}}{O}_{m}}{v_i^r}|

\displaystyle=\quantity|\sum_{1\le i_{M-1},\dots,i_k\le N}e^{\sum_{m=M-1}^k\lambda_{i_m}\Delta_{m+1}}\braket{o^M}{v_{i_{M-1}}^r}\prod_{m=M-1}^{k}\mel{v_{i_m}^l}{{O}_{m}}{v_{i_{m-1}}^r}|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{1\leq i_{M-1},\dots,i_{k}\leq N}\quantity|\braket{o^M}{v_{i_{M-1}}^r}\prod_{m=M-1}^{k}\mel{v_{i_m}^l}{{O}_{m}}{v_{i_{m-1}}^r}|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{1\leq i_{M-1},\dots,i_{k}\leq N}|\innerproduct{o^M}{v_{i_{M-1}}^r}|\prod_{m=M-1}^{k}\|{O}_{m}\|_{\infty}\|v_{i_{m}}^{l}\|_{2}\|v_{i_{m-1}}^{r}\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\prod_{m=M-1}^{k}\|{O}_{m}\|_{\infty}\quantity(\sum_{1\le i_{M-1},\dots,i_k\le N}|\tilde{o}_{i_{M-1}}^M|\prod_{m=M-1}^{k}\|v_{i_{m-1}}^r\|_2)

\displaystyle\leq\|o^{M}\|_{*}\quantity(\sum_{n=1}^N\|v_n^r\|_2)^{M-1-k}\|v_{i}^{r}\|_{2}\prod_{m=M-1}^{k}\|{O}_{m}\|_{\infty}.

(70)

where
i_{k-1}\equiv i
.
Likewise, we can also obtain

\displaystyle|\matrixelement{v_j^r}{\prod_{m=k-1}^{2}{O}_{m}e^{{W}^\dagger(t_{m}-t_{m-1})}}{o^1}|

\displaystyle\leq\|o^{1}\|_{*}\quantity(\sum_{n=1}^N\|v_n^r\|_2)^{k-3}\|v_{j}^{r}\|_{2}\prod_{m=k-1}^{2}\|{O}_{m}\|_{\infty}.

(71)

By combining Eqs. (
70
) and (
71
), we arrive at the following inequality:

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j\geq 2}|\matrixelement{o^M}{\prod_{m=M-1}^{k} e^{{W}(t_{m+1}-t_{m})}{O}_{m}}{v_i^r}\matrixelement{v_j^r}{\prod_{m=k-1}^{2}{O}_{m}e^{{W}^\dagger(t_{m}-t_{m-1})}}{o^1}|\leq\|o^{1}\|_{*}\|o^{M}\|_{*}\prod_{m=2}^{M-1}\|{O}_{m}\|_{\infty}\quantity(\sum_{n=1}^N\|v_n^r\|_2)^{M-2}.

(72)

Noting that
|\matrixelement{v_i^l}{{J}}{v_j^l}|\leq\sum_{m,n}|j_{mn}|\leq\sigma\Phi\quantity(\sigma/2\gamma)^{-1}
and using Eqs. (
69
) and (
72
), we obtain

|\delta C_{{\bf\it o}}^{{\bf\it\tau}}|\leq\|o^{1}\|_{*}\|o^{M}\|_{*}\prod_{m=2}^{M-1}\|{O}_{m}\|_{\infty}\quantity(\sum_{n=1}^N\|v_n^r\|_2)^{M-2}\sum_{k=2}^{M}(t_{k}-t_{k-1})e^{-g(t_{k}-t_{k-1})}\sigma\Phi\quantity(\sigma/2\gamma)^{-1}.

(73)

Defining
\chi_{{\bf\it o}}\coloneqq\|o^{1}\|_{*}\|o^{M}\|_{*}\prod_{m=2}^{M-1}\|{O}_{m}\|_{\infty}\quantity(\sum_{n=1}^N\|v_n^r\|_2)^{M-2}
, the generalization of Eq. (

\frac{|\delta C_{{\bf\it o}}^{{\bf\it\tau}}|}{\chi_{{\bf\it o}}}\leq\sigma\Phi\quantity(\frac{\sigma}{2\gamma})^{-1}\sum_{k=2}^{M}(t_{k}-t_{k-1})e^{-g(t_{k}-t_{k-1})}.

(74)

Proof of Eq. (

For convenience, we define

Using these terms, the rates of entropy production and dynamical activity can be expressed as

(75)

\displaystyle\gamma

(76)

By simple algebraic calculations, it can be shown that the relation
\expectationvalue{{X},\mca{L}({Y})}=\expectationvalue{\tilde{\mca{L}}({X}),{Y}}

{X}
and
{Y}

(78)

for any

Noting that
{A}

{B}

\displaystyle\delta C_{ba}^{\tau}

\displaystyle=\expectationvalue{{B},e^{\mca{L}\tau}({A}\pi)}-\expectationvalue{{A},e^{\mca{L}\tau}({B}\pi)}

\displaystyle=\expectationvalue{{B},e^{\mca{L}\tau}({A}\pi)}-\expectationvalue{e^{\tilde{\mca{L}}\tau}({A}),{B}\pi}

(79)

[{A},\pi]=0
, the following equality holds:

\displaystyle e^{\mathcal{L}\tau}({A}\pi)-\pi e^{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}\tau}({A})

(80)

Using this equality, we can proceed further as

\displaystyle\delta C_{ba}^{\tau}

\displaystyle=\tau\int_{0}^{1}\differential{s}\expectationvalue{{B},e^{s\mca{L}\tau}\qty[ \mca{L} (\pi e^{(1-s)\tilde{\mca{L}}\tau}({A})) - \pi\tilde{\mca{L}}(e^{(1-s)\tilde{\mca{L}}\tau}({A})) ]}

(81)

{X}

(82)

\bar{{B}}_{s\tau}

\bar{{A}}_{(1-s)\tau}\bar{{B}}_{s\tau}

\displaystyle\leq e^{-g\tau}\|A\|_{*}\|B\|_{*}\sigma\Phi\quantity(\sigma/2\gamma)^{-1}.

(92)

,
\bar{{B}}_{s\tau}
, and
\bar{{A}}_{(1-s)\tau}\bar{{B}}_{s\tau}

\displaystyle\Big|\sum_{k,n,m(\neq n)}\pi_{n}\matrixelement{n}{\bar{{A}}_{(1-s)\tau}}{m}\matrixelement{m}{L_k^\dagger\bar{{B}}_{s\tau}L_k}{n}\Big|

(93)

(94)

\displaystyle\leq e^{-g\tau}\|A\|_{*}\sum_{k}\|L_{k}\|_{\infty}^{2}C_{\ell_{1}}(B_{s\tau}),

(95)

\displaystyle\leq e^{-g\tau}\|B\|_{*}\sum_{k}\|L_{k}\|_{\infty}^{2}C_{\ell_{1}}(A_{(1-s)\tau}),

(96)

and

\displaystyle\Big|\sum_{k,n,m(\neq n)}\pi_{n}\matrixelement{n}{\bar{{A}}_{(1-s)\tau}\bar{{B}}_{s\tau}}{m}\matrixelement{m}{L_k^\dagger L_k}{n}\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k}\|L_{k}\|_{\infty}^{2}\sum_{n\neq m}\pi_{n}|\matrixelement{n}{\bar{{A}}_{(1-s)\tau}\bar{{B}}_{s\tau}}{m}|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k}\|L_{k}\|_{\infty}^{2}\sum_{n\neq m}\Big[\pi_{n}|\matrixelement{n}{\bar{{A}}_{(1-s)\tau}}{m}\matrixelement{m}{\bar{{B}}_{s\tau}}{m}|+\sum_{m^{\prime}(\neq m)}\pi_{n}|\matrixelement{n}{\bar{{A}}_{(1-s)\tau}}{m'}\matrixelement{m'}{\bar{{B}}_{s\tau}}{m}|\Big]

(97)

\displaystyle\Big|\sum_{k}\expectationvalue{\bar{{B}}_{s\tau},L_k\pi\bar{{A}}_{(1-s)\tau}L_k^\dagger-\pi L_k^\dagger\bar{{A}}_{(1-s)\tau}L_k+(\pi L_k^\dagger L_kA_{(1-s)\tau}-L_k^\dagger L_k\pi\bar{{A}}_{(1-s)\tau})/2}\Big|

(98)

Finally, by inserting Eqs. (

) and (

) to Eq. (

|\delta C_{ba}^{\tau}|\leq\tau e^{-g\tau}\quantity[\mca{C} + \|A\|_*\|B\|_*\sigma\Phi\qty(\frac{\sigma}{2\gamma})^{-1}],

(99)

where

is the quantum coherence term given by

\mathcal{C}\coloneqq(4\|H\|_{\infty}+3\sum_{k}\|L_{k}\|_{\infty}^{2})\int_{0}^{1}\differential{s}\quantity[ \|B\|_*{C}_{\ell_1}(A_{(1-s)\tau}) + \|A\|_*{C}_{\ell_1}(B_{s\tau}) ].

(100)

C.2
Analytical demonstration of the critical role of quantum coherence

Here we show that quantum coherence plays a pivotal role in constraining the asymmetry of cross-correlations.
Specifically, we present a case wherein the asymmetry of cross-correlations can persist even when irreversible entropy production is zero.
This implies that the quantum coherence term
\mathcal{C}
is inevitable in the derived bound (
11
).

We consider a three-level maser—the prototype for quantum heat engines that rely on quantum coherence to perform work
Scovil and Schulz-DuBois 1959
;
Kalaee et al. 2021
;
Van Vu and Saito 2022
.
The engine is simultaneously coupled to a hot and a cold heat bath and interacts with a classical electric field.
The Markovian dynamics is governed by the local master equation with the Hamiltonian
H_{t}=H_{0}+V_{t}
and jump operators
L_{1}=\sqrt{\alpha_{h}(N_{h}+1)}\sigma_{13}
,
L_{1^{\prime}}=\sqrt{\alpha_{h}N_{h}}\sigma_{31}
,
L_{2}=\sqrt{\alpha_{c}(N_{c}+1)}\sigma_{23}
, and
L_{2^{\prime}}=\sqrt{\alpha_{c}N_{c}}\sigma_{32}
.
Here,
H_{0}=\omega_{1}\sigma_{11}+\omega_{2}\sigma_{22}+\omega_{3}\sigma_{33}
is the bare Hamiltonian,
V_{t}=\Omega\quantity( e^{i\omega_0t}\sigma_{12}+ e^{-i\omega_0t}\sigma_{21})
is the external classical field,
\sigma_{ij}=\outerproduct{\epsilon_i}{\epsilon_j}
, and
\alpha_{x}
and
N_{x}
are the decay rate and the thermal occupation number for
x\in\{h,c\}
, respectively.
To remove the time dependence of the full Hamiltonian, it is convenient to rewrite operators in the rotating frame
X\to U_{t}^{\dagger}XU_{t}
, where
U_{t}=e^{-i\bar{H}t}
and
\bar{H}=\omega_{1}\sigma_{11}+(\omega_{1}+\omega_{0})\sigma_{22}+\omega_{3}\sigma_{33}
.
In this rotating frame, the master equation reads

\dot{{\varrho}}_{t}=-i[H,{\varrho}_{t}]+\sum_{k=1}^{2}\quantity(\mca{D}[L_{k}]{\varrho}_t+ \mca{D}[L_{k'}]{\varrho}_t),

(101)

where
H=-\Delta\sigma_{22}+\Omega(\sigma_{12}+\sigma_{21})
and
\Delta=\omega_{0}+\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}
.
It was shown that the master equation (
101
) is valid when the driving field is weak
Geva and Kosloff 1996
.

After some algebraic calculations, we can show that the steady-state density matrix reads

\pi=\pi_{11}\outerproduct{\epsilon_1}{\epsilon_1}+\pi_{22}\outerproduct{\epsilon_2}{\epsilon_2}+\pi_{12}\outerproduct{\epsilon_1}{\epsilon_2}+\pi_{12}^{*}\outerproduct{\epsilon_2}{\epsilon_1}+(1-\pi_{11}-\pi_{22})\outerproduct{\epsilon_3}{\epsilon_3},

(102)

where

\displaystyle\pi_{11}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\quantity{\alpha_c\alpha_hN_c(N_h+1)[4\Delta^2+(\alpha_cN_c+\alpha_hN_h)^2]+4\Omega^2(\alpha_cN_c+\alpha_hN_h)(\alpha_c+\alpha_h+\alpha_cN_c+\alpha_hN_h)},

(103)

\displaystyle\pi_{22}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\quantity{\alpha_c\alpha_hN_h(N_c+1)[4\Delta^2+(\alpha_cN_c+\alpha_hN_h)^2]+4\Omega^2(\alpha_cN_c+\alpha_hN_h)(\alpha_c+\alpha_h+\alpha_cN_c+\alpha_hN_h)},

(104)

\displaystyle\pi_{12}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\quantity{2i\alpha_c\alpha_h\Omega(N_c-N_h)(-2i\Delta+\alpha_cN_c+\alpha_hN_h)},

(105)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{c}\alpha_{h}(3N_{c}N_{h}+N_{c}+N_{h})[4\Delta^{2}+(\alpha_{c}N_{c}+\alpha_{h}N_{h})^{2}]+4\Omega^{2}(\alpha_{c}N_{c}+\alpha_{h}N_{h})[\alpha_{c}(3N_{c}+2)+\alpha_{h}(3N_{h}+2)].

(106)

Likewise, the irreversible entropy production rate is given by

\sigma=4(N_{c}-N_{h})\alpha_{c}\alpha_{h}(\alpha_{c}N_{c}+\alpha_{h}N_{h})\Omega^{2}\ln\left(\frac{N_{c}N_{h}+N_{c}}{N_{c}N_{h}+N_{h}}\right).

(107)

Now we consider the simple case of
N_{h}=N_{c}
.
In this case, we have
\sigma=0
,
\pi_{11}=\pi_{22}
, and
\pi_{12}=0
, which yield

\pi=\pi_{11}(\outerproduct{\epsilon_1}{\epsilon_1}+\outerproduct{\epsilon_2}{\epsilon_2})+(1-2\pi_{11})\outerproduct{\epsilon_3}{\epsilon_3}.

(108)

For any real numbers
\theta
and
\phi
, the density matrix
\pi
can also be written as

\pi=\pi_{11}(\outerproduct{1}{1}+\outerproduct{2}{2})+(1-2\pi_{11})\outerproduct{3}{3},

(109)

where
\ket{1}=e^{i\phi}\cos(\theta)\ket{\epsilon_1}+\sin(\theta)\ket{\epsilon_2}
,
\ket{2}=-\sin(\theta)\ket{\epsilon_1}+e^{-i\phi}\cos(\theta)\ket{\epsilon_2}
, and
\ket{3}=\ket{\epsilon_3}
.
We consider observables
A=\outerproduct{1}{1}
and
B=\outerproduct{3}{3}
.
We need only show that the asymmetry of cross-correlations is nonzero for this measurement basis.
It is thus sufficient to prove for the short-time regime
\tau\ll 1
.
In this region of operational time
\tau
, the asymmetry of cross-correlations can be analytically expanded in terms of
\tau
as

\displaystyle\delta C_{ba}^{\tau}

\displaystyle=\tr{B[\mbb{1}+\tau\mca{L}+\tau^2\mca{L}^2/2](A\pi)-B\pi[\mbb{1}+\tau\tilde{\mca{L}}+\tau^2\tilde{\mca{L}}^2/2](A)}+O(\tau^{3})

\displaystyle=\tr{B[\mca{L}(A\pi)-\pi\tilde{\mca{L}}(A)]}\tau+\tr{B[\mca{L}^2(A\pi)-\pi\tilde{\mca{L}}^2(A)]}\frac{\tau^{2}}{2}+O(\tau^{3})

\displaystyle=\frac{N_{h}(N_{h}+1)\Omega(\alpha_{c}-\alpha_{h})\sin(2\theta)\sin(\phi)}{3N_{h}+2}\tau^{2}+O(\tau^{3}).

(110)

As can be observed, although the first-order term is zero, the second-order term does not vanish and
\delta C_{ba}^{\tau}
is thus nonzero (i.e.,
|\delta C_{ba}^{\tau}|>0
).
Since
\sigma=0
, the quantum coherence term
\mathcal{C}
is the only term that bounds
\delta C_{ba}^{\tau}
in this case:

|\delta C_{ba}^{\tau}|\leq\tau e^{-g\tau}\mathcal{C}.

(111)

D.1
Proof of Eq. (
13
)

Define
{T}\coloneqq e^{{W}\tau}\Pi-\Pi
, which satisfies
{T}\ket{1}=\ket{0}
and
{T}^{\dagger}\ket{1}=\ket{0}
.
In other words,
T_{nn}=-\sum_{m(\neq n)}T_{mn}=-\sum_{m(\neq n)}T_{nm}=-\sum_{m(\neq n)}(T_{mn}+T_{nm})/2
.
For
m\neq n
, we have
T_{mn}=[e^{W\tau}]_{mn}\pi_{n}\geq 0
, which is nothing but the joint probability of observing the initial and final states.
First, we prove that

\tau\sigma\geq D({T}||{T}^{\dagger})=\sum_{m,n}T_{mn}\ln\frac{T_{mn}}{T_{nm}}.

(112)

To this end, let
\Gamma
be a stochastic trajectory of system states and
p(\Gamma)
be the path probability of finding
\Gamma
.
Utilizing the phase-space representation of the total entropy production and the monotonicity of the Kullback-Leibler divergence under coarse-graining, Eq. (
112
) can be proved as

\displaystyle\tau\sigma=D(p(\Gamma)||p(\tilde{\Gamma}))

\displaystyle\geq D(\Lambda[p(\Gamma)]||\Lambda[p(\tilde{\Gamma})])

\displaystyle=D({T}||{T}^{\dagger}),

(113)

where
\Lambda
is a coarse-grained map that reduces the path probability to the probability of observing only the initial and final states.
For convenience, we define
\ell_{mn}\coloneqq\sqrt{(a_{m}-a_{n})^{2}+(b_{m}-b_{n})^{2}}
,
\Omega_{mn}\coloneqq(a_{n}b_{m}-a_{m}b_{n})/2
, and
J_{mn}\coloneqq T_{mn}-T_{nm}
.
Note that
J_{mn}
differs from
j_{mn}
.
Using these quantities, we can express the asymmetry of cross-correlations as

\displaystyle\delta C_{ba}^{\tau}=\matrixelement{b}{{T}-{T}^\dagger}{a}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m>n}(T_{mn}-T_{nm})(a_{n}b_{m}-a_{m}b_{n})

\displaystyle=2\sum_{m>n}J_{mn}\Omega_{mn}.

(114)

Likewise, we can calculate the decay of auto-correlation as

\displaystyle D_{a}^{\tau}=-\matrixelement{a}{{T}}{a}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\neq n}\quantity(\frac{T_{mn}+T_{nm}}{2}a_n^2-T_{mn}a_ma_n)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m>n}(T_{mn}+T_{nm})(a_{m}-a_{n})^{2},

(115)

which leads to

\displaystyle D_{a}^{\tau}+D_{b}^{\tau}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m>n}(T_{mn}+T_{nm})\ell_{mn}^{2}.

(116)

Using Eqs. (
114
) and (
116
), we can prove the first argument of Eq. (
13
) as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{|\delta C_{ba}^{\tau}|^{2}}{D_{a}^{\tau}+D_{b}^{\tau}}

\displaystyle=\frac{8\quantity(\sum_{m>n}J_{mn}\Omega_{mn})^{2}}{\sum_{m>n}(T_{mn}+T_{nm})\ell_{mn}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\|a^{2}+b^{2}\|_{\infty}\quantity(\sum_{m>n}|J_{mn}|\ell_{mn})^{2}}{\sum_{m>n}(T_{mn}+T_{nm})\ell_{mn}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\|a^{2}+b^{2}\|_{\infty}\sum_{m>n}\frac{\quantity(T_{mn}-T_{nm})^{2}}{T_{mn}+T_{nm}}

\displaystyle\leq\|a^{2}+b^{2}\|_{\infty}\sum_{m>n}(T_{mn}-T_{nm})\ln\frac{T_{mn}}{T_{nm}}

\displaystyle=\|a^{2}+b^{2}\|_{\infty}D({T}||{T}^{\dagger})

\displaystyle\leq\|a^{2}+b^{2}\|_{\infty}\tau\sigma.

(117)

Here, we use the triangle inequality and
|\Omega_{mn}|\leq\ell_{mn}\|a^{2}+b^{2}\|_{\infty}^{1/2}/2

Shiraishi 2023
in the second line, the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality in the third line, inequality
2(x-y)^{2}/(x+y)\leq(x-y)\ln(x/y)
in the fourth line, and Eq. (
112
) in the last line.

The second argument of Eq. (
13
) can be proved by a similar strategy as in Ref.
Shiraishi 2023
.
For any directed edge
e=(m\leftarrow n)
, we define
x_{e}\coloneqq x_{mn}
for an arbitrary variable
x
, its reversed edge
\tilde{e}\coloneqq(n\leftarrow m)
, and
\mathcal{E}\coloneqq\{e\,|\,J_{e}>0\}
.
The discrete isoperimetric inequality
Ohga et al. 2023
;
Fan et al. 1955
implies that

4|c|\tan\frac{\pi}{|c|}|\Omega_{c}|\leq\ell_{c}^{2},

(118)

where we define
X_{c}\coloneqq\sum_{e\in c}X_{e}
for variable
X
and cycle
c\in\mathcal{C}
.
Since
\sum_{m}J_{mn}=0
, we can always find a uniform decomposition of cycles
\mathcal{C}
with appropriate orientations and associated positive currents
\{J^{c}\}_{c\in\mathcal{C}}
such that
J_{e}=\sum_{c}J^{c}S_{e}^{c}
for any
e\in\mathcal{E}
, where
S_{e}^{c}=1
if
e\in c
and zero otherwise
Pietzonka et al. 2016
.
Using this decomposition, equality
\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}J^{c}X_{c}=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}J_{e}X_{e}
can be derived; thus,
\delta C_{ba}^{\tau}=2\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}J_{e}\Omega_{e}=2\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}J^{c}\Omega_{c}
.
By utilizing this equality, inequality (
118
), and the monotonicity of function
x\tan(\pi/x)
over
[3,\infty)
, the asymmetry of cross-correlations can be upper bounded as

\displaystyle|\delta C_{ba}^{\tau}|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}J^{c}\ell_{c}^{2}\quantity(2|c|\tan\frac{\pi}{|c|})^{-1}

(119)

\displaystyle\leq\quantity(2N\tan\frac{\pi}{N})^{-1}\max_{c}\ell_{c}\sum_{m>n}|J_{mn}|\ell_{mn}.

(120)

Subsequently, following the same procedure as in Eq. (
117
) leads to the desired result.

D.2
Proof of Eq. (
14
)

We follow the approach in Ref.
Ohga et al. 2023
.
Note that observables
a
and
b
can be arbitrarily rescaled without altering the ratio
|\delta C_{ba}^{\tau}|/\sqrt{D_{a}^{\tau}D_{b}^{\tau}}
.
Therefore, we can assume
D_{a}^{\tau}=D_{b}^{\tau}
without loss of generality.
Noticing that
\mathcal{F}_{c}^{\tau}=\sum_{e\in c}\ln(T_e/T_{\tilde{e}})
and applying Jensen’s inequality, we can lower bound
\mathcal{F}_{c}^{\tau}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{c}^{\tau}

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in c}\ln\frac{(T_{e}+T_{\tilde{e}})+J_{e}}{(T_{e}+T_{\tilde{e}})-J_{e}}

\displaystyle=2|c|\sum_{e\in c}\frac{1}{|c|}\atanh\quantity(\frac{J_e}{T_e+T_{\tilde{e}}})

\displaystyle\geq 2|c|\atanh\quantity(\frac{1}{|c|}\sum_{e\in c}\frac{J_e}{T_e+T_{\tilde{e}}}),

(121)

which yields

\frac{1}{|c|}\sum_{e\in c}\frac{J_{e}}{T_{e}+T_{\tilde{e}}}\leq\tanh\quantity(\frac{\mca{F}_c^\tau}{2|c|}).

(122)

Consequently, by applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in c}\frac{(T_{e}+T_{\tilde{e}})}{J_{e}}\ell_{e}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\quantity(\sum_{e\in c}\ell_e)^{2}}{\sum_{e\in c}J_{e}/(T_{e}+T_{\tilde{e}})}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\ell_{c}^{2}}{|c|\tanh(\mca{F}_c^\tau/2|c|)}.

(123)

Using this inequality, we can lower bound the denominator as

\displaystyle 2\sqrt{D_{a}^{\tau}D_{b}^{\tau}}

\displaystyle=D_{a}^{\tau}+D_{b}^{\tau}\geq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}(T_{e}+T_{\tilde{e}})\ell_{e}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}J^{c}\sum_{e\in c}\frac{(T_{e}+T_{\tilde{e}})}{J_{e}}\ell_{e}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}J^{c}\ell_{c}^{2}\quantity[|c|\tanh(\mca{F}_c^\tau/2|c|)]^{-1}.

(124)

By combining Eqs. (
119
) and (
124
) and noticing that
(\sum_{c}x_{c})/(\sum_{c}y_{c})\leq\max_{c}(x_{c}/y_{c})
for positive numbers
\{x_{c}\}
and
\{y_{c}\}
, Eq. (
14
) is immediately derived.

D.2.1

Here we analytically demonstrate that the equality of the bound (

Without loss of generality, we can assume that
w_{+}=\omega>1
and
w_{-}=1

Note that the observables are given by
\ket{a}=[\sin(2\pi n/3)]_{n}^{\top}
and
\ket{b}=[\cos(2\pi n/3)]_{n}^{\top}

For this unicyclic system, by performing simple algebraic calculations, we can derive that

\displaystyle\frac{|\delta C_{ba}^{\tau}|}{2\sqrt{D_{a}^{\tau}D_{b}^{\tau}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\quantity|\sin[\sqrt{3}(\omega-1)\tau/2]|}{e^{3(\omega+1)\tau/2}-\cos[\sqrt{3}(\omega-1)\tau/2]},

(125)

\displaystyle\max_{c}\frac{\tanh(\mca{F}_c^\tau/2|c|)}{\tan(\pi/|c|)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\tanh\quantity(\frac{1}{2}\qty|\log\frac{e^{3(\omega+1)\tau/2}-\cos[\sqrt{3}(\omega-1)\tau/2]+\sqrt{3}\sin[\sqrt{3}(\omega-1)\tau/2]}{e^{3(\omega+1)\tau/2}-\cos[\sqrt{3}(\omega-1)\tau/2]-\sqrt{3}\sin[\sqrt{3}(\omega-1)\tau/2]}|).

(126)

By separately considering two cases:
\sin[\sqrt{3}(\omega-1)\tau/2]\geq 0

Proposition 1
.

For any matrices
{X}
and
{Y}
and orthogonal basis
\{\ket{n}\}
, we have

\quantity|\tr{{X}{Y}}|\leq\|{Y}\|_{\infty}\sum_{m,n}|\matrixelement{m}{{X}}{n}|,

(128)

where
\|{Y}\|_{\infty}
denotes the operator norm of
{Y}
.

Proof.

By applying the inequalities
\tr{{X}{Y}}\leq\|{Y}\|_{\infty}\|{X}\|_{1}
and
\|{X}\|_{1}\leq\sum_{m,n}|\matrixelement{m}{{X}}{n}|
, one can immediately complete the proof.

Proposition 2
.

For any complex numbers
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
with a negative real part, the following inequality holds:

\quantity|\frac{e^{z_1}-e^{z_2}}{z_1-z_2}|\leq 1.

(129)

Proof.

Since
\Re{z_1}\leq 0
and
\Re{z_2}\leq 0
, we have
|e^{sz_{1}+(1-s)z_{2}}|\leq 1
for any
0\leq s\leq 1
.
Consequently, we can prove Eq. (
129
) as follows:

\displaystyle\quantity|\frac{e^{z_1}-e^{z_2}}{z_1-z_2}|

\displaystyle=\quantity|\int_0^1\dd{s}e^{sz_1+(1-s)z_2}|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\differential{s}\quantity|e^{sz_1+(1-s)z_2}|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\differential{s}

\displaystyle=1.

(130)

∎
