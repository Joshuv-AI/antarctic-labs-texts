On a globally convergent semismooth ∗ Newton method in nonsmooth nonconvex optimization
Helmut Gfrerer

Abstract




Abstract.
In this paper we present GSSN, a globalized SCD semismooth
∗
Newton method for solving nonsmooth nonconvex optimization problems. The global convergence properties of the method are ensured by the proximal gradient method, whereas locally superlinear convergence is established via the SCD semismooth
∗
Newton method under quite weak assumptions. The Newton direction is based on the SC (subspace containing) derivative of the subdifferential mapping and can be computed by the (approximate) solution of an equality-constrained quadratic program. Special attention is given to the efficient numerical implementation of the overall method.

Key words.
Nonsmooth optimization, nonconvex, semismooth
∗
Newton method, proximal gradient method, global convergence, superlinear convergence

AMS Subject classification.
90C26, 65K05, 90C06.

1
Introduction

This paper deals with nonsmooth and nonconvex optimization problems of the form

\min\varphi(x):=f(x)+g(x),

(1.1)

where
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a continuously differentiable function with strictly continuous gradient
\nabla f
and
g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}:=\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
is a proper lsc prox-bounded function. Throughout the paper we will assume these properties on
f
and
g
without further mention.

For solving such problems, first-order methods are very popular, because they are easy to implement, have low computational cost per iterate and global convergence properties can be shown under very mild assumptions. However, the rate of convergence is at most linear and is very sensitive to some condition number of the problem. Thus, in some cases first-order methods can produce only solutions with low or medium accuracy in reasonable time.

On the other hand, second-order methods are locally superlinearly convergent under suitable smoothness and regularity assumptions at the solution. Having at hand a starting point sufficiently close to the solution, these second-order methods produce in few iterations a solution with high accuracy. However, the computational cost per iterate is higher and it may be a difficult task to globalize the second-order methods.

Such a globalization can be done by a hybrid of first- and second-order methods. In a series of recent papers
[
35
,
39
,
38
,
1
]
this was done by using
forward-backward splitting (FBS)
(also called
proximal gradient method
(PGM)) as the first-order method. One step of FBS yields a sufficient decrease in the forward-backward envelope (FBE), which can be used to make a line search along a direction obtained by the second-order method. In the mentioned papers, this is done by using a quasi-Newton method for computing a fixed point of the forward-backward operator. This quasi-Newton technique avoids the computation of derivatives for the forward-backward operator. A crucial assumption for guaranteeing superlinear convergence is that the forward-backward operator is strictly differentiable at the solution.

Another approach uses the fact that under suitable assumptions on
g
(convexity or uniform prox-regularity) the FBE is continuously differentiable and a descent method combined with some semismooth Newton method can be applied in order to minimize the FBE, cf.
[
28
,
39
,
19
]
. Here, the difficult part is the computation of one element of the Clarke generalized derivative of the forward-backward operator and the limiting coderivative of the subdifferential
\partial g
, respectively.

In the Truncated Nonsmooth Newton Multigrid (TNNMG) method
[
16
]
, global convergence is ensured by the convergence properties of a nonlinear pre-smoother, whereas the subsequent linear correction step is crucial for fast convergence of the method.

Concerning second-order methods, a lot of active investigations have been done in direction of proximal Newton methods, see, e.g.,
[
18
,
21
,
26
]
and
[
30
,
31
]
for the infinite dimensional case. Here, in each iteration one has to solve a nonsmooth problem of the form

\min_{d}f({x}^{(k)})+\langle\nabla f({x}^{(k)}),d\rangle+\frac{1}{2}d^{T}{H}^{(k)}d+g({x}^{(k)}+d),

where
{H}^{(k)}
is some approximation of the Hessian
\nabla^{2}f({x}^{(k)})
.

Other second-order methods rely on the observation, that in many cases
\mathrm{gph}\,\partial\varphi
, the graph of the subdifferential mapping, is a smooth manifold locally near the solution, see, e.g.,
[
3
,
23
]
. To this class also belongs the theory of
\cal VU
decomposition as introduced in
[
22
]
. We refer the reader also to the survey
[
37
]
and the references therein.

In this paper we want to globalize the SCD semismooth
∗
Newton method for solving the first-order optimality conditions
0\in\partial\varphi(x)
. The semismooth
∗
Newton method aims at solving inclusions
0\in F(x)
with set-valued mappings
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
. It was introduced in
[
12
]
and is mainly motivated by the semismooth
∗
property which was defined by means of limiting coderivatives. The semismooth
∗
property can be considered as a generalization of the classical semismooth property for single-valued functions
[
32
]
. Let us mention that the class of mappings having the semismoothness
∗
property is rather broad. As it follows from a result by Jourani
[
17
]
, every mapping, whose graph is a closed subanalytic set, is semismooth
∗
.

In the very recent paper
[
13
]
the SCD variant (Subspace Containing Derivative) of the semismooth
∗
Newton method has been developed which avoids the use of limiting coderivatives and the SC derivative is comparatively easy to compute. As the name already indicates, the elements of the SC derivative are subspaces. This seems to be quite natural, since for single-valued smooth mappings the Fréchet derivative is a linear mapping, whose graph is a subspace. In fact, for single-valued Lipschitzian mappings
F
the elements of the SC derivative are exactly the graphs of the linear mappings induced by the elements of the so-called B-Jacobian. However, the SC derivative of a set-valued mapping may contain subspaces which are not the graph of a linear mapping. Using the SC derivative, we can then define the SCD semismoothness
∗
property which is weaker than the above mentioned one of semismoothness
∗
.

The SCD semismooth
∗
Newton direction is calculated as the solution of a linear system which is formed by means of an arbitrary basis of a subspace belonging to the SC derivative. Note that we need not to know the whole SC derivative but only one element for computing the Newton direction.

In order that the SC derivative of
\partial\varphi
exists we have to impose a very mild assumption on
g
, namely that
g
is prox-regular and subdifferentially continuous at
x
for
x^{*}
for
(x,x^{*})
belonging to a dense subset of
\mathrm{gph}\,\partial g
. Although there exist examples of proper lsc functions which does not fulfill this assumption, we claim that nearly all functions used in applications have this property.

To ensure superlinear convergence of the SCD semismooth
∗
Newton method, we have to impose the semismooth
∗
property together with a certain regularity condition on the subdifferential
\partial\varphi
at the solution. This regularity condition is called SCD regularity and is weaker than metric regularity. On the other hand, together with the semismooth
∗
property, SCD regularity implies strong metric subregularity. SCD regularity also guarantees the unique solvability of the linear system defining the Newton direction. Let us mention that the assumed semismooth
∗
property is weaker than the smoothness assumptions used in other papers for ensuring superlinear convergence like strict differentiability of the forward-backward operator
[
38
]
or the graph of the subdifferential mapping being a smooth manifold around the solution
[
3
,
23
]
. The SCD semismooth
∗
Newton method has been already successfully applied to some challenging applications
[
14
,
15
]
.

The aim of this paper is to globalize the SCD semismooth
∗
Newton method for solving the inclusion
0\in\partial\varphi(x)
and the obtained algorithm we will call GSSN (Globalized SCD semismooth
∗
Newton method). For globalization we use a hybrid with FBS. In particular, our algorithm is a modification of PANOC
+
developed by De Marchi and Themelis
[
8
]
and does not require that
\nabla f
is globally Lipschitz. We are able to show that every accumulation point of the produced sequence is a fixed point of the forward-backward operator and therefore stationary for
\varphi
. Further, if one iterate is sufficiently close to a local minimizer where
\partial\varphi
is both SCD semismooth
∗
and SCD regular, then the whole sequence converges superlinearly to that minimizer.

For the purpose of solving large scale problems we also treat the situation that the linear system defining the Newton direction is only solved approximately by some iterative method. We will show that the SCD Newton direction is a stationary solution of a quadratic function and therefore some CG method can be applied.

The paper is organized in the following way. In the preliminary Section 2 we recall first some basic notions from variational analysis followed by some properties of the forward-backward envelope. In addition we recall some basic results on SCD mappings and the SCD semismooth
∗
Newton method for solving inclusions
0\in F(x)
. In Section 3 we analyze the SCD semismooth
∗
Newton method more in detail for the case when
F
is the subdifferential
\partial\varphi
of an lsc function
\varphi
. It appears that the subspaces of the SC derivative of
\partial\varphi
have a certain basis representation which can be efficiently exploited for computation of the Newton direction. In Section 4 we present a globally convergent algorithm which acts as a template for GSSN. In Section 5 we establish superlinear convergence results. The analysis is also done for large-scale problems when the Newton direction cannot be computed exactly but is only approximated by some iterative method. Finally, in Section 6 we present some numerical experiments for large-scale problems which demonstrate the effectiveness of our method.

Our notation is basically standard. For an element
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\|u\|
denotes its Euclidean norm,

{\cal B}_{\epsilon}(u)
denotes the closed ball around
u
with radius
\epsilon
and
{\cal B}
stands for the closed unit ball. For a matrix
A
,
{\rm rge\;}A
signifies its range and
\|A\|
denotes its spectral norm. Given a set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{s}
, we define the distance
from a point
x
to
\Omega
by

{\rm dist}(x,\Omega):=\inf\{\|y-x\|\,\mid\,y\in\Omega\}
, the respective indicator function is denoted by

\delta_{\Omega}
and
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Omega}}{{x\rightarrow\bar{x}}}
means convergence within
\Omega
.
When a mapping
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
is differentiable at
x
, we denote by
\nabla F(x)
its Jacobian. We denote by
\mathbb{R}_{+}
(
\mathbb{R}_{++}
) the set of all nonnegative (positive) real numbers and by
\mathbb{R}_{-}
(
\mathbb{R}_{--}
) the set of all nonpositive (negative) real numbers.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Variational analysis

Given a set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a point
\bar{x}\in A
, the
tangent cone
to
A
at
\bar{x}
is defined as

T_{A}(\bar{x}):=\limsup_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{A-\bar{x}}{t}.

We call
A

geometrically derivable
at
\bar{x}
, if
T_{A}(\bar{x})=\lim_{t\downarrow 0}(A-\bar{x})/t
, i.e., for every
u\in T_{A}(\bar{x})
and every sequence
t_{k}\downarrow 0
there exits a sequence
u_{k}\to u
such that
\bar{x}+t_{k}u_{k}\in A
.

Consider now a function
q:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
and a point
\bar{x}\in\mathrm{dom}\,q:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,\mid\,q(x)<\infty\}
.
The
regular subdifferential
of
q
at
\bar{x}
is given by

\widehat{\partial}q(\bar{x}):=\Big\{x^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,\mid\,\liminf_{x\to\bar{x}}\frac{q(x)-q(\bar{x})-\langle x^{*},x-\bar{x}\rangle}{\|x-\bar{x}\|}\geq 0\Big\}

while the
(limiting) subdifferential
is defined by

\partial q(\bar{x}):=\{x^{*}\,\mid\,\exists(x_{k},x_{k}^{*})\to(\bar{x},x^{*})\mbox{ with }x_{k}^{*}\in\widehat{\partial}q(x_{k})\ \forall k\mbox{ and }\lim_{k\to\infty}q(x_{k})=q(\bar{x})\}.

Given
{\bar{x}^{\ast}}\in\partial q(\bar{x})
, the function
q
is called
prox-regular
at
\bar{x}
for
{\bar{x}^{\ast}}
if there
exist
\epsilon>0
and
\rho\geq 0
such that for all
x^{\prime},x\in{\cal B}_{\epsilon}(\bar{x})
with
|q(x)-q(\bar{x})|\leq\epsilon
one has

q(x^{\prime})\geq q(x)+\langle x^{*},x^{\prime}-x\rangle-\frac{\rho}{2}\|x^{\prime}-x\|^{2}\quad\mbox{whenever}\quad x^{*}\in\partial q(x)\cap{\cal B}_{\epsilon}({\bar{x}^{\ast}}).

When this holds for all
{\bar{x}^{\ast}}\in\partial q(\bar{x})
,
q
is said to be prox-regular at
\bar{x}
. Further,
q
is called
subdifferentially continuous
at
\bar{x}
for
{\bar{x}^{\ast}}
if for any sequence
(x_{k},x_{k}^{*})\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{{\mathrm{gph}\,\partial q}}(\bar{x},{\bar{x}^{\ast}})
we have
\lim_{k\to\infty}q(x_{k})=q(\bar{x})
. When this holds for all
{\bar{x}^{\ast}}\in\partial q(\bar{x})
,
q
is said to be subdifferentially continuous at
\bar{x}
.

The function
q
is said to be
prox-bounded
if there exists some
\lambda>0
such that
\inf_{z}\frac{1}{2\lambda}\|z-x\|^{2}+q(z)>-\infty
for some
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The supremum of the set of all such
\lambda
is the threshold
\lambda_{q}
for the prox-boundedness of
q
.
Further, the
proximal mapping

{\rm prox}_{\lambda q}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
is defined by

{\rm prox}_{\lambda q}(x):=\mathop{\rm arg\,min}_{z}\frac{1}{2\lambda}\|z-x\|^{2}+q(z).

Given a mapping
F:D\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
with
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we say that
F
is
strictly continuous
at
\bar{x}\in D
if

{\rm Lip\,}F(\bar{x}):=\limsup_{{\textstyle{x,x^{\prime}\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{D}\bar{x}\atop x\not=x^{\prime}}}}\frac{\|F(x)-F(x^{\prime})\|}{\|x-x^{\prime}\|}<\infty.

The mapping
F
is called strictly continuous if it is strictly continuous at every point
x\in D
.

2.2
On the forward-backward envelope

Consider the problem (
1.1
). Given
\lambda>0
, we define the function
\psi_{\lambda}^{\rm FB}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
by

\psi_{\lambda}^{\rm FB}(x,z):=\ell_{f}(x,z)+\frac{1}{2\lambda}\|z-x\|^{2}+g(z)\mbox{ with }\ell_{f}(x,z):=f(x)+\langle\nabla f(x),z-x\rangle.

Further we define the mapping
T_{\lambda}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
and the function
\varphi_{\lambda}^{\rm FB}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
by

\displaystyle T_{\lambda}(x)

\displaystyle:=\mathop{\rm arg\,min}_{z}\psi_{\lambda}^{\rm FB}(x,z),

\displaystyle\varphi_{\lambda}^{\rm FB}(x)

\displaystyle:=\min_{z}\psi_{\lambda}^{\rm FB}(x,z).

Clearly, for any
z\in T_{\lambda}(x)
there holds the first-order optimality condition

0\in\widehat{\partial}_{z}\psi_{\lambda}^{\rm FB}(x,z)=\nabla f(x)+\frac{1}{\lambda}(z-x)+\widehat{\partial}g(z).

(2.2)

In particular, whenever
x\in T_{\lambda}(x)
, we have
0\in\nabla f(x)+\widehat{\partial}g(x)=\widehat{\partial}\varphi(x)
, i.e.,
x
is a stationary point for
\varphi
.

The function
\varphi_{\lambda}^{\rm FB}
is the so-called
forward-backward envelope
, which has been introduced in
[
28
]
under the name
composite Moreau envelope
, and has been successfully used in a series of papers for the solution of nonsmooth optimization problems, cf.
[
35
,
38
,
36
]
.

In what follows we will also use of the well-known descent lemma:

Lemma 2.1

(see, e.g.,
[
4
, Lemma 5.7]
)
.

Let
\nabla f
be Lipschitz continuous with constant
L
on the convex set
D
. Then for any
x,y\in D
one has

f(y)\leq\ell_{f}(x,y)+\frac{L}{2}\|y-x\|^{2}.

Note that by the proof of
[
4
, Lemma 5.7]
we even have
|f(y)-\ell_{f}(x,y)|\leq\frac{L}{2}\|y-x\|^{2}
,
x,y\in D
.

Let us state some basic properties of
T_{\lambda}
and
\varphi_{\lambda}^{\rm FB}
. First at all note that

T_{\lambda}(x)={\rm prox}_{\lambda g}(x-\lambda\nabla f(x)).

Lemma 2.2

(cf.
[
8
, Lemma 2.2]
)
.

Let
\lambda\in(0,\lambda_{g})
, where
\lambda_{g}
denotes the threshold of prox-boundedness of
g
.

(i)

\varphi_{\lambda}^{\rm FB}
is everywhere finite and strictly continuous.

(ii)

For every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
one has
\varphi_{\lambda}^{\rm FB}(x)\leq\varphi(x)
, where equality holds if and only if
x\in T_{\lambda}(x)
.

(iii)

If
\tilde{x}\in T_{\lambda}(x)
and
f(\tilde{x})\leq\ell(x,\tilde{x})+\frac{L}{2}\|\tilde{x}-x\|^{2}
then

\displaystyle\varphi(\tilde{x})\leq\varphi_{\lambda}^{\rm FB}(x)-\frac{1-\lambda L}{2\lambda}\|\tilde{x}-x\|^{2}.

Lemma 2.3
.

(i)

For every bounded set
M
and every
\alpha>0
there exists some
\hat{\lambda}_{M,\alpha}>0
such that for every
\lambda\in(0,\hat{\lambda}_{M,\alpha}]
and every pair
(x,z)\in M\times M
one has

f(z)\leq\ell_{f}(x,z)+\frac{\alpha}{2\lambda}\|z-x\|^{2}.

(ii)

For every bounded set
\bar{M}
and every
\bar{\lambda}\in(0,\lambda_{g})
the set

S:=\bigcup_{{\textstyle{x\in\bar{M}\atop\lambda\in(0,\bar{\lambda}]}}}T_{\lambda}(x)

(2.3)

is bounded.

(iii)

If
\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}
then for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
there holds
\varphi^{\rm FB}_{\lambda_{2}}(x)\leq\varphi^{\rm FB}_{\lambda_{1}}(x)
.

Proof.

ad (i): Since
\nabla f
is assumed to be strictly continuous,
\nabla f
is Lipschitz continuous on the convex compact set
{\rm cl\,}{\rm conv\,}M
with some constant
L
, cf.
[
33
, Theorem 9.2]
. By Lemma
2.1
there holds
f(z)\leq\ell(x,z)+\frac{L}{2}\|z-x\|^{2}
for all
x,z\in{\rm cl\,}{\rm conv\,}M
and the assertion follows with
\hat{\lambda}_{M,\alpha}=\alpha/L
.

ad (ii): Assume on the contrary that the set
S
is unbounded, i.e., there exist sequences
(x_{k},\lambda_{k})\in\bar{M}\times(0,\bar{\lambda}]
and
z_{k}\in T_{\lambda_{k}}(x_{k})
with
\|z_{k}\|\to\infty
as
k\to\infty
. Taking any
\tilde{z}\in\mathrm{dom}\,g
we have

f(x_{k})+\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),z_{k}-x_{k}\rangle+\frac{1}{2\lambda_{k}}\|z_{k}-x_{k}\|^{2}+g(z_{k})\leq f(x_{k})+\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),\tilde{z}-x_{k}\rangle+\frac{1}{2\lambda_{k}}\|\tilde{z}-x_{k}\|^{2}+g(\tilde{z}).

A rearranging and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality yield

\displaystyle g(\tilde{z})

\displaystyle\geq\langle\nabla f(x_{k})-\frac{x_{k}}{\lambda_{k}},z_{k}-\tilde{z}\rangle+\frac{1}{2\lambda_{k}}(\|z_{k}\|^{2}-\|\tilde{z}\|^{2})+g(z_{k})

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{1}{\lambda_{k}}\|\lambda_{k}\nabla f(x_{k})-x_{k}\|\|z_{k}-\tilde{z}\|+\frac{1}{2\lambda_{k}}(\|z_{k}\|^{2}-\|\tilde{z}\|^{2})+g(z_{k}).

Since the sequences
x_{k}
and
\lambda_{k}
are bounded and
\nabla f
is continuous, the sequence
\|\lambda_{k}\nabla f(x_{k})-x_{k}\|
is bounded as well. By taking into account
\lim_{k\to\infty}\|z_{k}\|=\infty
, for arbitrary
\epsilon>0
we can find some
k_{\epsilon}
such that for any
k\geq k_{\epsilon}
one has

\|\lambda_{k}\nabla f(x_{k})-x_{k}\|\|z_{k}-\tilde{z}\|\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}\|z_{k}\|^{2}\mbox{ and }\|\tilde{z}\|^{2}\leq\epsilon\|z_{k}\|^{2},

resulting in

g(\tilde{z})\geq\frac{1-2\epsilon}{2\lambda_{k}}\|z_{k}\|^{2}+g(z_{k})\geq\frac{1-2\epsilon}{2\bar{\lambda}}\|z_{k}\|^{2}+g(z_{k}).

Taking
\epsilon>0
small enough such that
\bar{\lambda}/(1-2\epsilon)<\lambda_{g}
we obtain
\liminf_{k\to\infty}g(z_{k})/\|z_{k}\|^{2}<-1/(2\lambda_{g})
. However,
by
[
33
, Exercise 1.24]
there holds
\liminf_{k\to\infty}g(z_{k})/\|z_{k}\|^{2}\geq-1/(2\lambda_{g})
, a contradiction.

ad (iii): This is an easy consequence of
\psi^{\rm FB}_{\lambda_{2}}(x,\cdot)-\psi^{\rm FB}_{\lambda_{1}}(x,\cdot)=\big(\frac{1}{\lambda_{2}}-\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}}\big)\|\cdot-x\|^{2}\leq 0
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

2.3
On SCD mappings

In this subsection we want to recall the basic definitions and features of the SCD property introduced in the recent paper
[
13
]
.

In what follows we denote by
{\cal Z}_{n}
the metric space of all
n
-dimensional subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
equipped with the metric

d_{\cal Z}(L_{1},L_{2}):=\|P_{L_{1}}-P_{L_{2}}\|

where
P_{L_{i}}
is the symmetric
2n\times 2n
matrix representing the orthogonal projection on
L_{i}
,
i=1,2
.
{\cal Z}_{n}
is a compact metric space, cf.
[
13
, Lemma 3.1]

A sequence
L_{k}\in{\cal Z}_{n}
converges in
{\cal Z}_{n}
to some
L\in{\cal Z}_{n}
, i.e.
\lim_{k\to\infty}d_{\cal Z}(L_{k},L)=0
, if and only if
\lim_{k\to\infty}L_{k}=L
in the sense of Painlevé-Kuratowski convergence, cf.
[
13
, Lemma 3.1]
.

We treat every element of
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
as a column vector. In order to keep notation simple we write
(u,v)
instead of
\begin{pmatrix}u\\
v\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
when this does not lead to confusion.

We will also work with bases for the subspaces
L\in{\cal Z}_{n}
. Let
{\cal M}_{n}
denote the collection of all
2n\times n
matrices with full column rank
n
and for
L\in{\cal Z}_{n}
we define

{\cal M}(L):=\{Z\in{\cal M}_{n}\,\mid\,{\rm rge\;}Z=L\},

i.e., the columns of
Z\in{\cal M}(L)
are a basis for
L
.
We can partition every matrix
Z\in{\cal M}(L)
into two
n\times n
matrices
A
and
B
and we will write
Z=(A,B)
instead of
Z=\begin{pmatrix}A\\
B\end{pmatrix}
. It follows that
{\rm rge\;}(A,B):=\{(Au,Bu)\,\mid\,u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\}\doteq\{\begin{pmatrix}Au\\
Bu\end{pmatrix}\,\mid\,u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\}=L
.

Further, for every
L\in{\cal Z}_{n}
we can define the adjoint subspace

\displaystyle L^{*}

\displaystyle:=\{(-v^{*},u^{*})\,\mid\,(u^{*},v^{*})\in L^{\perp}\},

where
L^{\perp}
denotes as usual the orthogonal complement of
L
. Then it can be shown that
(L^{*})^{*}=L
and
d_{\cal Z}(L_{1},L_{2})=d_{\cal Z}(L_{1}^{*},L_{2}^{*})
. Thus the mapping
L\to L^{*}
defines an isometry on
{\cal Z}_{n}
.

Definition 2.4
.

Consider a mapping
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

1.

We call
F
graphically smooth of dimension
n
at
(x,y)\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
, if

T_{\mathrm{gph}\,F}(x,y)\in{\cal Z}_{n}.

Further we denote by
{\cal O}_{F}
the set of all points belonging to
\mathrm{gph}\,F
where
F
is graphically smooth of dimension
n
.

2.

We associate with
F
the SC (subspace containing) derivative
{\mathcal{S}}F:\mathrm{gph}\,F\rightrightarrows{\cal Z}_{n}
and the adjoint SC derivative
{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F:\mathrm{gph}\,F\rightrightarrows{\cal Z}_{n}
, given by

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}F(x,y)

\displaystyle:=\{L\in{\cal Z}_{n}\,\mid\,\exists(x_{k},y_{k})\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{{{\cal O}_{F}}}(x,y):\ \lim_{k\to\infty}d_{\cal Z}(L,T_{\mathrm{gph}\,F}(x_{k},y_{k}))=0\},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F(x,y)

\displaystyle:=\{L^{*}\,\mid\,L\in{\mathcal{S}}F(x,y)\}.

3.

We say that
F
has the SCD (subspace containing derivative) property at
(x,y)\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
, if
{\mathcal{S}}F(x,y)\not=\emptyset
. We say that
F
has the SCD property around
(x,y)\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
, if there is a neighborhood
W
of
(x,y)
such that
F
has the SCD property at every
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in\mathrm{gph}\,F\cap W
.
Finally, we call
F
an SCD mapping if

F
has the SCD property at every point of its graph.

The (adjoint) SC derivative was introduced in
[
13
]
. Note that the definition of
{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F
in
[
13
]
is different but equivalent to the definition above, cf.
[
13
, Remark 3.5]
.

In this paper we are concerned with the SCD property of subdifferential mappings.

Proposition 2.5

(cf.
[
13
, Proposition 3.26]
)
.

Suppose that
q:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is prox-regular and subdifferentially continuous at
\bar{x}
for
\bar{x}^{*}\in\partial q(\bar{x})
. Then
\partial q
has the SCD property around
(\bar{x},{\bar{x}^{\ast}})
. Further, there exists some
\lambda>0
such that for every
(x,x^{*})\in\mathrm{gph}\,\partial q
sufficiently close to
(\bar{x},{\bar{x}^{\ast}})
there holds
{\mathcal{S}}\partial q(x,x^{*})={\mathcal{S}}^{*}\partial q(x,x^{*})
and for every
L\in{\mathcal{S}}^{*}\partial q(x,x^{*})
there is a symmetric positive semidefinite
n\times n
matrix
B
such that
L=L^{*}={\rm rge\;}(B,\frac{1}{\lambda}(I-B))
.

Next we turn to the notion of SCD regularity.

Definition 2.6
.

1.

We denote by
{\cal Z}_{n}^{\rm reg}
the collection of all subspaces
L\in{\cal Z}_{n}
such that

(y^{*},0)\in L\ \Rightarrow\ y^{*}=0.

2.

A mapping
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called SCD regular at
(x,y)\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
, if
F
has the SCD property at
(x,y)
and

(y^{*},0)\in L^{*}\Rightarrow\ y^{*}=0\ \forall L^{*}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F(x,y),

(2.4)

i.e.,
L^{*}\in{\cal Z}_{n}^{\rm reg}
for all
L^{*}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F(x,y)
. Further, we will denote by

{\rm scd\,reg\;}F(x,y):=\sup\{\|y^{*}\|\,\mid\,(y^{*},x^{*})\in L^{*},L^{*}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F(x,y),\|x^{*}\|\leq 1\}

the modulus of SCD regularity of
F
at
(x,y)
.

Since the elements of
{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F(x,y)
are contained in
\mathrm{gph}\,D^{*}F(x,y)
, cf.
[
13
, Lemma 3.7]
, where
D^{*}F(x,y)
denotes the limiting coderivative of
F
at
(x,y)
, it follows from the Mordukhovich criterion, see, e.g.,
[
25
, Theorem 3.3]
, that SCD regularity is weaker than metric regularity. We refer to
[
13
]
for an example demonstrating that SCD regularity is strictly weaker than metric regularity.

In the following proposition we state some basic properties of subspaces
L\in{\cal Z}_{n}^{\rm reg}
.

Proposition 2.7

(cf.
[
13
, Proposition 4.2]
)
.

Given a
2n\times n
matrix
Z=(A,B)
, there holds
{\rm rge\;}Z\in{\cal Z}_{n}^{\rm reg}
if and only if the
n\times n
matrix
B
is nonsingular. Thus,
for every
L^{*}\in{\cal Z}_{n}^{\rm reg}
there is a unique
n\times n
matrix
C_{L^{*}}
such that
L^{*}={\rm rge\;}(C_{L^{*}},I)
. Further,
L={\rm rge\;}(C_{L^{*}}^{T},I)\in{\cal Z}_{n}^{\rm reg}
,
C_{L}=C_{L^{*}}^{T}
,

\langle x^{*},C_{L^{*}}^{T}v\rangle=\langle y^{*},v\rangle\ \forall(y^{*},x^{*})\in L^{*}\ \forall v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

and

\|y^{*}\|\leq\|C_{L^{*}}\|\|x^{*}\|\ \forall(y^{*},x^{*})\in L^{*}.

Note that for every
L^{*}\in{\cal Z}_{n}^{{\rm reg\,}}
and every
(A,B)\in{\cal M}(L^{*})
the matrix
B
is nonsingular and
C_{L^{*}}=AB^{-1}
.

The next lemma follows from
[
13
, Lemma 4.7, Proposition 4.8]
.

Lemma 2.8
.

Assume that
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
is SCD regular at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
. Then

{\rm scd\,reg\;}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})=\sup\{\|C_{L^{*}}\|\,\mid\,L^{*}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})\}<\infty.

Moreover, if
F
has the SCD property around
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
, then
F
is SCD regular at every
(x,y)\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
sufficiently close to
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
and

\limsup_{(x,y)\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{\mathrm{gph}\,F}(\bar{x},\bar{y})}{\rm scd\,reg\;}F(x,y)\leq{\rm scd\,reg\;}F(\bar{x},\bar{y}).

As an example of SCD regularity we refer to the subdifferential mapping around tilt-stable local minimizers.

Definition 2.9

(
tilt-stable minimizers).

Let
q:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
, and let
\bar{x}\in\mathrm{dom}\,q
. Then
\bar{x}
is a tilt-stable local minimizer of
q
if there is a number
\gamma>0
such that the mapping

M_{\gamma}(x^{\ast}):=\mathop{\rm arg\,min}\big\{q(x)-\langle x^{\ast},x\rangle\,\mid\,x\in{\cal B}_{\gamma}(\bar{x})\big\},\quad x^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

(2.5)

is single-valued and Lipschitz continuous in some neighborhood of
\bar{x}^{*}=0\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
M_{\gamma}(0)=\{\bar{x}\}
.

Theorem 2.10

(
[
13
, Theorem 7.9]
)
.

For a function
q:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
having
0\in\partial q(\bar{x})
and such that
q
is both prox-regular and subdifferentially continuous at
\bar{x}
for
{\bar{x}^{\ast}}=0
, the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

\bar{x}
is a tilt-stable local minimizer of
q
.

(ii)

\partial q
is SCD regular at
(\bar{x},0)
and
C_{L}
is positive semidefinite for every

.

Note that the subdifferential mapping

can be SCD regular also at local minimizers which are not tilt-stable, cf.
[

, Example 6.5]
.

2.4
On the SCD semismooth
∗
Newton method

In this subsection we outline the SCD semismooth
∗
Newton method presented in the recent paper
[
13
]
for solving the inclusion

0\in F(x),

where
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a set-valued mapping.

Definition 2.11
.

We say that
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
is SCD semismooth
∗
at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
if
F
has the SCD property around
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
and
for every
\epsilon>0
there is some
\delta>0
such that

\displaystyle|\langle x^{*},x-\bar{x}\rangle-\langle y^{*},y-\bar{y}\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon\|(x,y)-(\bar{x},\bar{y})\|\|(x^{*},y^{*})\|

(2.6)

holds for all
(x,y)\in\mathrm{gph}\,F\cap{\cal B}_{\delta}(\bar{x},\bar{y})
and all
(y^{*},x^{*})\in L^{*}
,
L^{*}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F(x,y)
.

The SCD semismooth
∗
property has also a useful primal interpretation:

Proposition 2.12
.

Let the mapping
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}
and the pair
(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
be given. Then the following two statements are equivalent:

1.

F
is SCD semismooth
∗
at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
.

2.

For every
\epsilon>0
there is some
\delta>0
such that for every
(x,y)\in\mathrm{gph}\,F\cap{\cal B}_{\delta}(\bar{x},\bar{y})
and every
L\in{\mathcal{S}}F(x,y)
there holds

{\rm dist}((x-\bar{x},y-\bar{y}),L)\leq\epsilon\|(x-\bar{x},y-\bar{y})\|.

Proof.

Consider
(x,y)\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
and
L\in{\mathcal{S}}F(x,y)
. By the well-known distance formula we have

\displaystyle{\rm dist}((x-\bar{x},y-\bar{y}),L)

\displaystyle=\sup\{\langle x^{*},x-\bar{x}\rangle+\langle y^{*},y-\bar{y}\rangle\,\mid\,(x^{*},y^{*})\in L^{\perp},\|(x^{*},y^{*})\|=1\}

\displaystyle=\sup\{|\langle x^{*},x-\bar{x}\rangle-\langle y^{*},y-\bar{y}\rangle|\,\mid\,(y^{*},x^{*})\in L^{*},\|(y^{*},x^{*})\|=1\}

and the equivalence follows now from Definition
2.6
.
∎

The notion of SCD semismoothness
∗
is weaker than the one of semismoothness
∗
introduced in
[
12
]
. Thus, we obtain from
[
13
, Proposition 2.10]
the following
result.

Proposition 2.13
.

Let
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an SCD mapping. Each of the following two conditions is sufficient for
F
to be SCD semismooth
∗
at every point of its graph:

(i)

\mathrm{gph}\,
F is the union of finitely many convex sets.

(ii)

\mathrm{gph}\,F
is a closed subanalytic set.

Example 2.14
.

Consider
g(x)=\|x\|
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Since

\mathrm{gph}\,\partial g=\left\{(x,x^{*})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\,\mid\,\sum_{i=1}^{n}{x_{i}^{*}}^{2}\leq 1,\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}x_{i}^{*}\Big)^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2},\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}x_{i}^{*}\geq 0\right\}

is a closed subanalytic set and the subdifferential of any proper lsc convex function is an SCD mapping by Proposition
2.5
,
\partial g
is SCD semismooth
∗
at every point of its graph.

The basic idea behind the SCD semismooth
∗
Newton method is as follows. Assume that the mapping
F
is SCD semismooth
∗
at
(\bar{x},0)\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
and consider a point
(x,y)\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
close to
(\bar{x},0)
together with a subspace
L^{*}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F(x,y)
and
n
pairs
(y_{i}^{*},x_{i}^{*})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, forming a basis of
L^{*}
. Then, by (
2.6
), there holds

\langle x_{i}^{*},\bar{x}-x\rangle=-\langle y_{i}^{*},y\rangle+o(\|(x,y)-(\bar{x},0)\|)\|(y_{i}^{*},x_{i}^{*})\|,\ i=1,\ldots,n

and we expect that, under suitable assumptions, a unique solution of the system of linear equations

\langle x_{i}^{*},\Delta x\rangle=-\langle y_{i}^{*},y\rangle,\ i=1,\ldots,n

exists and satisfies
\|x+\Delta x-\bar{x}\|=o(\|(x,y)-(\bar{x},0)\|)
.

Let us formalize this idea. Consider an iterate
{x}^{(k)}
. Since we are dealing with multifunctions, we cannot expect that
F({x}^{(k)})\not=\emptyset
or that
0
is close to
F({x}^{(k)})
, even if
{x}^{(k)}
is very close to the solution
\bar{x}
. Hence, we first perform a so-called
approximation step
by finding a pair
({\hat{x}}^{(k)},{\hat{y}}^{(k)})
satisfying

\|({\hat{x}}^{(k)},{\hat{y}}^{(k)})-(\bar{x},0)\|\leq\eta\|{x}^{(k)}-\bar{x}\|

(2.7)

for some parameter
\eta>0
. This bound involves the unknown solution
\bar{x}
. However, if we compute
({\hat{x}}^{(k)},{\hat{y}}^{(k)})
as some approximate projection of
({x}^{(k)},0)
on
\mathrm{gph}\,F
, i.e.,

\|({\hat{x}}^{(k)},{\hat{y}}^{(k)})-({x}^{(k)},0)\|\leq\beta{\rm dist}(({x}^{(k)},0),\mathrm{gph}\,F)

for some
\beta\geq 1
, then

\displaystyle\|({\hat{x}}^{(k)},{\hat{y}}^{(k)})-(\bar{x},0)\|

\displaystyle\leq\|({\hat{x}}^{(k)},{\hat{y}}^{(k)})-({x}^{(k)},0)\|+\|({x}^{(k)},0)-(\bar{x},0)\|

\displaystyle\leq\beta{\rm dist}(({x}^{(k)},0),\mathrm{gph}\,F)+\|({x}^{(k)},0)-(\bar{x},0)\|\leq(\beta+1)\|({x}^{(k)},0)-(\bar{x},0)\|

and therefore (
2.7
) holds with
\eta=\beta+1
. Then we perform the so-called
Newton step
. We choose some subspace
{L^{*}}^{(k)}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F({\hat{x}}^{(k)},{\hat{y}}^{(k)})
together with some basis
({B}^{(k)},{A}^{(k)})\in{\cal M}({L^{*}}^{(k)})
and compute the Newton direction
\Delta{x}^{(k)}
as solution of the linear system

{{A}^{(k)}}^{T}\Delta x=-{{B}^{(k)}}^{T}{\hat{y}}^{(k)}.

(2.8)

By Proposition
2.7
, the matrix
{A}^{(k)}
is nonsingular if and only if
{L^{*}}^{(k)}\in{\cal Z}_{n}^{\rm reg}
and in this case we have

\Delta{x}^{(k)}=-{{A}^{(k)}}^{-T}{{B}^{(k)}}^{T}{\hat{y}}^{(k)}=-C_{{L^{*}}^{(k)}}^{T}{\hat{y}}^{(k)}.

An equivalent approach for computing the Newton direction is based on the relation
C_{L}=C_{L^{*}}^{T}
. Choosing a subspace
{L}^{(k)}\in{\mathcal{S}}F({\hat{x}}^{(k)},{\hat{y}}^{(k)})
and a basis
({X}^{(k)},{Y}^{(k)})\in{\cal M}({L}^{(k)})
, the Newton direction is given by

{\Delta x}^{(k)}={X}^{(k)}p\quad\mbox{where $p$ solves}\quad{Y}^{(k)}p=-{\hat{y}}^{(k)}.

Finally we get the new iterate as
{x}^{(k+1)}={\hat{x}}^{(k)}+\Delta{x}^{(k)}
.

The following proposition provides the key estimate for the semismooth
∗
Newton method for SCD mappings.

Proposition 2.15

(cf.
[
13
, Proposition 5.3]
)
.

Assume that
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
is SCD semismooth
∗
at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
. Then for every
\epsilon>0
there is some
\delta>0
such that the estimate

\|x-C_{L^{*}}^{T}(y-\bar{y})-\bar{x}\|\leq\epsilon\sqrt{n(1+\|C_{L^{*}}\|^{2})}\|(x,y)-(\bar{x},\bar{y})\|

holds for every
(x,y)\in\mathrm{gph}\,F\cap{\cal B}_{\delta}(\bar{x},\bar{y})
and every
L^{*}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F(x,y)\cap{\cal Z}_{n}^{\rm reg}
.

In order to state a convergence result, we introduce the following set-valued mappings

\displaystyle{\cal A}_{\eta,\bar{x}}(x):=\{(\hat{x},\hat{y})\in\mathrm{gph}\,F\,\mid\,\|(\hat{x},\hat{y})-(\bar{x},0)\|\leq\eta\|x-\bar{x}\|\},

\displaystyle{\cal N}_{\eta,\bar{x}}(x):=\{\hat{x}-C_{L^{*}}^{T}\hat{y}\,\mid\,(\hat{x},\hat{y})\in{\cal A}_{\eta,\bar{x}}(x),L^{*}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{*}F(\hat{x},\hat{y})\cap{\cal Z}_{n}^{\rm reg}\}.

Note that the Newton iteration described above can be shortly written as
{x}^{(k+1)}\in{\cal N}_{\eta,\bar{x}}({x}^{(k)})
.

Proposition 2.16

(
[
15
, Proposition 4.3]
)
.

Assume that
F
is SCD semismooth
∗
and SCD regular at
(\bar{x},0)\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
and let
\eta>0
. Then there is some
\bar{\delta}>0
such that for every
x\in{\cal B}_{\bar{\delta}}(\bar{x})
the mapping
F
is SCD regular at every point
(\hat{x},\hat{y})\in{\cal A}_{\eta,\bar{x}}(x)
. Moreover, for every
\epsilon>0
there is some
\delta\in(0,\bar{\delta}]
such that

\|z-\bar{x}\|\leq\epsilon\|x-\bar{x}\|\ \forall x\in{\cal B}_{\delta}(\bar{x})\ \forall z\in{\cal N}_{\eta,\bar{x}}(x).

It now easily follows that, under the assumptions of Proposition
2.16
, for every starting point
{x}^{(0)}
sufficiently close to the solution
\bar{x}
the SCD semismooth
∗
Newton method is well-defined and any sequence
{x}^{(k)}
produced by this method converges superlinearly to
\bar{x}
.

We also need the following proposition which easily follows from
[
13
, Theorem 6.2]
.

Proposition 2.17
.

Assume that
F
is SCD semismooth
∗
at
(\bar{x},0)\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
and SCD regular at
(\bar{x},0)
. Then for every
\kappa>{\rm scd\,reg\;}F(\bar{x},0)
there is some neighborhood
U
of
\bar{x}
such that

\|x-\bar{x}\|\leq\kappa{\rm dist}(0,F(x))\ \forall x\in U,

i.e.,
F
is strongly metrically subregular at
(\bar{x},0)
, cf.
[
10
, Chapter 3.9]
.

3
On the SCD semismooth
∗
Newton step in optimization

As explained in the previous section, an iteration of the SCD semismooth
∗
Newton method consists of an approximation step and a Newton step. In this section we analyze the Newton step for solving the inclusion
0\in\partial\varphi(x)
for rather general lsc functions
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
more in detail. The discussion of the approximation step is shifted to the next sections, where we will show that the approximation step can be realized by our globalization procedure, i.e., by essentially one step of PGM.

Proposition
2.5
states that the SC derivative of prox-regular and subdifferentially continuous functions
\varphi
have the favourable property that every subspace
L
belonging to the SC derivative is self-adjoint, i.e.,
L=L^{*}
. Since we want to work with such subspaces, we make the following assumption, which we assume from now on for the rest of this section.

Assumption 1
.

The set

{\rm proxreg\,}\varphi:=\{(x,x^{*})\in\mathrm{gph}\,\partial\varphi\,\mid\,\mbox{ $\varphi$ is prox-regular and subdifferentially continuous at $x$ for $x^{*}$}\}

is dense in
\mathrm{gph}\,\partial\varphi
.

Lemma 3.1
.

\partial\varphi
is an SCD mapping.

Proof.

Consider an arbitrary pair
(\bar{x},{\bar{x}^{\ast}})\in\mathrm{gph}\,\partial\varphi
. By Assumption
1
, there exists a sequence
(x_{k},x_{k}^{*})
in
{\rm proxreg\,}\varphi
converging to
(\bar{x},{\bar{x}^{\ast}})
and, by Proposition
2.5
, for every
k
there is some
L_{k}\in{\mathcal{S}}\partial\varphi(x_{k},x_{k}^{*})
. The metric space
{\cal Z}_{n}
is compact and therefore the sequence
L_{k}
has a limit point
\bar{L}\in{\cal Z}_{n}
. Since
(x_{k},x_{k}^{*})\to(\bar{x},{\bar{x}^{\ast}})
, it follows that
\bar{L}\in{\mathcal{S}}\partial\varphi(\bar{x},{\bar{x}^{\ast}})\not=\emptyset
by
[
13
, Lemma 3.13]
. Since this holds for arbitrary
(\bar{x},{\bar{x}^{\ast}})\in\mathrm{gph}\,\partial\varphi
, the subdifferential
\partial\varphi
is an SCD mapping.
∎

In order to implement the SCD semismooth
∗
Newton step, we need a basis for the selected subspace
{L}^{(k)}
. Instead of the basis representation provided by Proposition
2.5
we will use another one, which seems to be more appropriate.

Definition 3.2
.

We denote by
{\cal Z}_{n}^{P,W}
the collection of all subspaces
L\in{\cal Z}_{n}
such that there are symmetric
n\times n
matrices
P
and
W
with the following properties:

(i)

L={\rm rge\;}(P,W)
,

(ii)

P^{2}=P
, i.e..
P
represents the orthogonal projection onto some subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

iii)

W(I-P)=I-P
.

Note that for symmetric matrices
W,P
fulfilling conditions (ii) and (iii) of the above definition we have

\displaystyle(I-P)W=\big(W(I-P)\big)^{T}=(I-P)^{T}=I-P=W(I-P),

(3.9)

\displaystyle PW=WP=PWP,

(3.10)

\displaystyle W=WP+W(I-P)=PWP+(I-P).

(3.11)

Lemma 3.3
.

Every subspace
L\in{\cal Z}_{n}^{P,W}
is self-adjoint, i.e.,
L=L^{*}
.

Proof.

Let
(P,W)\in{\cal M}(L)
be according to Definition
3.2
. For every
(u,v)\in L
there is some
p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
(u,v)=(Pp,Wp)
implying

\langle Ws,u\rangle+\langle-Ps,v\rangle=\langle Ws,Pp\rangle-\langle Ps,Wp\rangle=\langle s,(WP-PW)p\rangle=0\ \forall s\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

by symmetry of
P
,
W
and (
3.10
). Hence,
{\rm rge\;}(W,-P)\subset L^{\perp}
. Further, since
L={\rm rge\;}(P,W)
is an
n
-dimensional subspace, so are
L^{\perp}
and
{\rm rge\;}(W,-P)
as well and we conclude
L^{\perp}={\rm rge\;}(W,-P)
. Now the equality
L=L^{*}
follows from the definition of
L^{*}
.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

For every
L\in{\cal Z}_{n}^{P,W}
there exists exactly one pair
(P,W)
of symmetric
n\times n
matrices fulfilling properties (i), (ii) and (iii) of Definition
3.2
.

Proof.

Consider two pairs
(P_{i},W_{i})
,
i=1,2
, fulfilling (i)–(iii). The matrices
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
represent the orthogonal projections onto the same subspace and thus
P_{1}=P_{2}=:P
. Since
{\rm rge\;}(P,W_{1})={\rm rge\;}(P,W_{2})
, for every
z_{1}
there is some
z_{2}
with
Pz_{1}=Pz_{2}
and
W_{1}z_{1}=W_{2}z_{2}
. Hence, by using (
3.9
), we have

(I-P)z_{1}=(I-P)W_{1}z_{1}=(I-P)W_{2}z_{2}=(I-P)z_{2}

and together with
Pz_{1}=Pz_{2}
the equality
z_{1}=z_{2}
follows. Hence
W_{1}=W_{2}
.
∎

Lemma 3.5
.

{\cal Z}_{n}^{P,W}
is closed in
{\cal Z}_{n}
.

Proof.

Consider a sequence
L_{k}
in
{\cal Z}_{n}^{P,W}
and assume that
L_{k}
converges to some
L\in{\cal Z}_{n}
. In order to prove the lemma, we have to show
L\in{\cal Z}_{n}^{P,W}
. Let
(P_{k},W_{k})
be the symmetric
n\times n
matrices associated with
L_{k}
according to Definition
3.2
. By (
3.10
), for each
k
the symmetric matrices
P_{k}
and
W_{k}
commute and can thus be diagonalized with the same orthogonal matrix
Q_{k}
, i.e., there are diagonal matrices
D^{k}={\rm diag}(d^{k}_{1},\ldots,d^{k}_{n})
and
E^{k}={\rm diag}(e^{k}_{1},\ldots,e^{k}_{n})
such that
P_{k}=Q_{k}D^{k}Q_{k}^{T}
and
W_{k}=Q_{k}E^{k}Q_{k}^{T}
. Since
P_{k}
represents an orthogonal projection, we have
d^{k}_{j}\in\{0,1\}
,
j=1,\ldots,n
and from

W_{k}(I-P_{k})=Q_{k}E^{k}Q_{k}^{T}(I-Q_{k}D^{k}Q_{k}^{T})=Q_{k}E^{k}(I-D^{k})Q_{k}^{T}=I-P_{k}=Q_{k}(I-D^{k})Q_{k}^{T}

we deduce that
E^{k}(I-D^{k})=I-D^{k}
implying
e^{k}_{j}(1-d^{k}_{j})=1-d^{k}_{j}
,
j=1,\ldots,n
. By passing to a subsequence we may assume that the orthogonal matrices
Q_{k}
converge to some orthogonal matrix
Q
, the diagonal matrices
D^{k}
are constant
D^{k}\equiv D
for all
k
and the diagonal matrices
E^{k}
converge to some diagonal matrix
E
with entries
e_{j}\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{\pm\infty\}
. Let
I:=\{j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\,\mid\,e_{j}\in\mathbb{R}\}
and for each

and the diagonal matrices

. Since

.
For every

is bounded and, since

converges to some

and that

converges to some

ad (iii)
\ \Rightarrow\
(ii): This obviously holds.

Finally, equation (

At the end of this section we provide some sum rule.

Lemma 3.16
.

Assume that
\varphi=f+g
, where
f
is twice continuously differentiable at
\bar{x}
and
g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is a proper lsc function. Given
{\bar{x}^{\ast}}\in\partial g(\bar{x})
, for every
(P,W)\in{\cal M}_{P,W}\partial g(\bar{x},{\bar{x}^{\ast}})
one has

(P,P\nabla^{2}f(\bar{x})P+W)\in{\cal M}_{P,W}\partial\varphi(\bar{x},\nabla f(\bar{x})+{\bar{x}^{\ast}}).

Proof.

By
[
13
, Proposition 3.15]
we have
L={\rm rge\;}(P,\nabla^{2}f(\bar{x})P+W)\in{\mathcal{S}}\partial\varphi(\bar{x},\nabla f(\bar{x})+{\bar{x}^{\ast}})
. Let
(Pp,(\nabla^{2}f(\bar{x})P+W)p)\in L
and set
\tilde{p}:=p+(I-P)\nabla^{2}f(\bar{x})Pp
. Then
P\tilde{p}=Pp
and

\displaystyle(P\nabla^{2}f(\bar{x})P+W)\tilde{p}

\displaystyle=P\nabla^{2}f(\bar{x})Pp+Wp+W(I-P)\nabla^{2}f(\bar{x})Pp

\displaystyle=P\nabla^{2}f(\bar{x})Pp+Wp+(I-P)\nabla^{2}f(\bar{x})Pp=(\nabla^{2}f(\bar{x})P+W)p,

verifying
L\subset{\rm rge\;}(P,P\nabla^{2}f(\bar{x})P+W)
. On the other hand, consider

(Pp,(P\nabla^{2}f(\bar{x})P+W)p)\in{\rm rge\;}(P,P\nabla^{2}f(\bar{x})P+W)

and set
\tilde{p}:=p-(I-P)\nabla^{2}f(\bar{x})Pp
. Then
P\tilde{p}=Pp
and

\displaystyle(\nabla^{2}f(\bar{x})P+W)\tilde{p}

\displaystyle=\nabla^{2}f(\bar{x})Pp+Wp-W(I-P)\nabla^{2}f(\bar{x})Pp=\nabla^{2}f(\bar{x})Pp+Wp-(I-P)\nabla^{2}f(\bar{x})Pp

\displaystyle=(P\nabla^{2}f(\bar{x})P+W)p

showing the reverse inclusion
{\rm rge\;}(P,P\nabla^{2}f(\bar{x})P+W)\subset L
. Hence equality holds and it is easy to see that
P\nabla^{2}f(\bar{x})P+W
is symmetric and satisfies
(P\nabla^{2}f(\bar{x})P+W)(I-P)=W(I-P)=I-P
. Thus
(P,P\nabla^{2}f(\bar{x})P+W)\in{\cal M}_{P,W}\partial\varphi(\bar{x},\nabla f(\bar{x})+{\bar{x}^{\ast}})
.
∎

4
Globalizing the SCD semismooth
∗
Newton method

The suggested globalization of the SCD semismooth
∗
Newton method is done by combining it with some globally convergent method and to this end we use
forward-backward splitting
(also called
proximal gradient method
). One iteration of forward-backward splitting can be described by

{x}^{(k+1)}\in T_{{\lambda}^{(k)}}({x}^{(k)}),

where for convergence of the method it is important that the parameter
{\lambda}^{(k)}
is chosen adequately. If
\nabla f
is globally Lipschitz with constant
L
, a constant choice
{\lambda}^{(k)}\in(0,\frac{2}{L})
ensures convergence. In case of unknown Lipschitz modulus
L
some backtracking procedure for computing
{\lambda}^{(k)}
is available, see, e.g.,
[
4
, Section 10.3.3]
.

Very recently, De Marchi and Themelis
[
8
]
presented the algorithm PANOC
+
based on forward-backward splitting, where global convergence is established under the weaker assumption of strict continuity of the gradient
\nabla f
. The following algorithm BasGSSN (Basic Globalized SemiSmooth
∗
Newton method) is motivated by the algorithm PANOC
+
.

Algorithm 1

(Algorithm BasGSSN)
.

Input:
Starting point
{x}^{(0)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
0<\lambda_{0}\leq\bar{\lambda}<\lambda_{g}
, parameters
\alpha,\beta\in(0,1)
,
\bar{\rho}>0
and
\sigma\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
.

1. Set
k\leftarrow 0
,
{\lambda}^{(0)}\leftarrow\lambda_{0}
, compute
{z}^{(0)}\in T_{{\lambda}^{(0)}}({x}^{(0)})
and set
{\eta}^{(0)}\leftarrow\frac{1}{2{\lambda}^{(0)}}\|{z}^{(0)}-{x}^{(0)}\|^{2}
;

2.

while

f({z}^{(0)})>\ell_{f}({x}^{(0)},{z}^{(0)})+\alpha{\eta}^{(0)}

{\lambda}^{(0)}\leftarrow{\lambda}^{(0)}/2
, compute
{z}^{(0)}\in T_{{\lambda}^{(0)}}({x}^{(0)})
,
{\eta}^{(0)}\leftarrow\frac{1}{2{\lambda}^{(0)}}\|{z}^{(0)}-{x}^{(0)}\|^{2}
;

end while

3.
if

{x}^{(k)}=={z}^{(k)}
or another convergence criterion is fulfilled
then stop
;

4. Compute a search direction
{s}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\|{s}^{(k)}\|\leq\bar{\rho}
;

5.

Set
{\tau}^{(k)}\leftarrow 1
,
{x}^{(k+1)}\leftarrow{z}^{(k)}+{\tau}^{(k)}{s}^{(k)}
,
{\lambda}^{(k+1)}\leftarrow{\lambda}^{(k)}
,
compute
{z}^{(k+1)}\in T_{{\lambda}^{(k+1)}}({x}^{(k+1)})
and set
{\eta}^{(k+1)}\leftarrow\frac{1}{2{\lambda}^{(k+1)}}\|{z}^{(k+1)}-{x}^{(k+1)}\|^{2}
;

6.

while

\varphi_{{\lambda}^{(k+1)}}^{\rm FB}({x}^{(k+1)})>\varphi_{{\lambda}^{(k)}}^{\rm FB}({x}^{(k)})-\beta(1-\alpha){\eta}^{(k)}\ \vee\ f({z}^{(k+1)})>\ell_{f}({x}^{(k+1)},{z}^{(k+1)})+\alpha{\eta}^{(k+1)}

if

\varphi_{{\lambda}^{(k+1)}}^{\rm FB}({x}^{(k+1)})>\varphi_{{\lambda}^{(k)}}^{\rm FB}({x}^{(k)})-\beta(1-\alpha){\eta}^{(k)}

then

{\tau}^{(k)}\leftarrow{\tau}^{(k)}/2
,
{x}^{(k+1)}\leftarrow{z}^{(k)}+{\tau}^{(k)}{s}^{(k)}
;

else

{\lambda}^{(k+1)}\leftarrow{\lambda}^{(k+1)}/2
;

compute
{z}^{(k+1)}\in T_{{\lambda}^{(k+1)}}({x}^{(k+1)})
,
{\eta}^{(k+1)}\leftarrow\frac{1}{2{\lambda}^{(k+1)}}\|{z}^{(k+1)}-{x}^{(k+1)}\|^{2}
;

end while

7.

while

f({z}^{(k+1)})\leq\ell_{f}({x}^{(k+1)},{z}^{(k+1)})+\sigma\alpha{\eta}^{(k+1)}\ \wedge\ 2{\lambda}^{(k+1)}\leq\bar{\lambda}

do

compute
\tilde{z}\in T_{2{\lambda}^{(k+1)}}({x}^{(k+1)})
and
\tilde{\eta}\leftarrow\frac{1}{4{\lambda}^{(k+1)}}\|\tilde{z}-{x}^{(k+1)}\|^{2}
.

if

f(\tilde{z})>\ell_{f}({x}^{(k+1)},\tilde{z})+\alpha\tilde{\eta}

then

proceed with step 8.;

else

set
{\lambda}^{(k+1)}\leftarrow 2{\lambda}^{(k+1)}
,
{z}^{(k+1)}\leftarrow\tilde{z}
,
{\eta}^{(k+1)}\leftarrow\tilde{\eta}
;

end while

8. Set
k\leftarrow k+1
and go to step 3.;

The search direction
{s}^{(k)}
will be an (inexact) SCD semismooth
∗
Newton direction and we will discuss later how to compute it in practice.

Remark 4.1
.

Apart from using a different notation there are the following significant differences to PANOC
+
from
[
8
]
:

1.

In PANOC
+
the search direction is of the form
{s}^{(k)}={d}^{(k)}+{x}^{(k)}-{z}^{(k)}
with
\|{d}^{(k)}\|\leq D\|{z}^{(k)}-{x}^{(k)}\|
. We do not use such a restriction on our search direction
{s}^{(k)}
because we do not know how to choose the constant
D
in practice without destroying the superlinear convergence properties of the SCD semismooth
∗
Newton method. Further, in our numerical experiments for the nonconvex regression problem (Subsection
6.2
) we observed that our method can escape from non-optimal stationary points and in this case the ratio
\|{s}^{(k)}\|/\|{z}^{(k)}-{x}^{(k)}\|
can be arbitrarily large. We refer to Subsection
6.2
for more details on this phenomenon.

2.

If in the
while
-loop in step 6. both conditions are fulfilled, we decrease
{\tau}^{(k)}
whereas in PANOC
+
the parameter
{\lambda}^{(k+1)}
is reduced. This exchange does not affect the convergence analysis and is mainly motivated by performance reasons. We observed that for poor search direction
{s}^{(k)}

{x}^{(k+1)}
can be far away from a solution and therefore it seems reasonable to favor the reduction of the step size
{\tau}^{(k)}
. Therefore we possibly decrease

only at those points which are candidates for the next iterate, i.e., a sufficient reduction in
\varphi_{{\lambda}^{(k+1)}}^{\rm FB}({x}^{(k+1)})
has been achieved.

3.

We also add the possibility to increase
\lambda
in step 7 in contrary to PANOC
+
, where the produced sequence

is monotonically decreasing. Again, this modification is mainly motivated by performance reasons since it is well-known that the method of proximal gradients works better with larger values of

(4.25)

where
{\rm typ\_subgr}
denotes the magnitude of a “typical” subgradient. If such a value is not at hand it can be estimated by the value
\|{z^{*}}^{(0)}\|
obtained by running steps 1. and 2. of Algorithm BasGSSN with a randomly generated starting point
{x}^{(0)}
.

In our implementation we use an alternative termination criterion. As we are looking for a solution of the inclusion

0\in F(x,z):=\begin{pmatrix}\nabla f(x)+\partial g(z)\\
x-z\end{pmatrix}

and
(\frac{1}{{\lambda}^{(k)}}({x}^{(k)}-{z}^{(k)}),{x}^{(k)}-{z}^{(k)})^{T}\in F({x}^{(k)},{z}^{(k)})
by (
4.23
), a stopping criterion of the form

{r}^{(k)}:=\left(1+\frac{1}{{\lambda}^{(k)}}\right)\|{x}^{(k)}-{z}^{(k)}\|\leq\epsilon\max\left\{{\rm typ\_val},{r}^{(0)}\right\}

(4.26)

can be used, where
{\rm typ\_val}
is the magnitude of a “typical” value of
(1+\frac{1}{\lambda}){\rm dist}(x,T_{\lambda}(x))
.

Note that by Theorem
4.4
we can only ensure that the termination criterion (
4.26
) is achieved in finite time if the sequence
{\lambda}^{(k)}
has an accumulation point which is bounded away from
0
. Apart from the case when
\nabla f
is Lipschitzian on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, by Lemma
4.3
(vi) this is also the case when the produced sequence
{x}^{(k)}
(or
{z}^{(k)}
) has at least one accumulation point. From (
4.20
), (
4.21
) we immediately obtain that
\varphi({z}^{(k)})\leq\varphi_{{\lambda}^{(0)}}^{\rm FB}({x}^{(0)})

\forall k
. Hence, if
\varphi
is level bounded, the produced sequence
{z}^{(k)}
remains bounded and finite termination is guaranteed.
In case that
\varphi
is not level-bounded or is even not bounded below, we can enforce finiteness of Algorithm
1
by adding the stopping conditions

{\eta}^{(k)}\leq\epsilon\big(\varphi_{{\lambda}^{(0)}}^{\rm FB}({x}^{(0)})-\varphi_{{\lambda}^{(k)}}^{\rm FB}({x}^{(k)})\big)\quad\vee\quad\varphi({z}^{(k)})\leq\varphi_{\min}.

(4.27)

Remark 4.5
.

Since Algorithm
1
is a modification of PANOC
+
, many of the assertions of Lemma
4.3
and Theorem
4.4
follow also from
[
8
]
. The only difference is that we can prove stationarity of accumulation points of the sequence
{x}^{(k)}
(or
{z}^{(k)}
) whereas in
[
8
]
this is only shown under the additional assumption that
{x}^{(k)}
remains bounded. On the other hand, since we do not impose some bound of the form

on the search directions

5
Combining BasGSSN with the SCD semismooth
∗
Newton method (GSSN)

We will refer to GSSN (Globalized SCD Semismooth
∗
Newton method) when we use a realization of the basic Algorithm
1
with search direction
{s}^{(k)}
related to the SCD semismooth
∗
Newton direction given by (
3.14
). To this aim we assume throughout this section that Assumption
1
is fulfilled.

Given the iterate
{x}^{(k)}
,
{z}^{(k)}\in T_{{\lambda}^{(k)}}({x}^{(k)})
we will take the point
({z}^{(k)},{z^{*}}^{(k)})\in\mathrm{gph}\,\partial\varphi
, with
{z^{*}}^{(k)}
given by (
4.24
),
as approximation step in the SCD semismooth
∗

0\in\partial\varphi(x)
.
Selecting two matrices

{s}^{(k)}
in Algorithm

the SCD semismooth
∗
Newton direction given as the solution of the linear equation

{W}^{(k)}s=-{P}^{(k)}{z^{*}}^{(k)}.

(5.28)

However, from the viewpoint of global convergence this is not feasible. We do not know in general, whether this system has a solution and, if it has a solution, its norm is less or equal than the given radius
\bar{\rho}
. Further it is also possible that the problem dimension
n
is so large that we cannot solve the linear system (

) exactly and we must confine ourselves with some approximate solution produced by an iterative method.

Hence, for the moment we assume that our search direction

,
\|{s}^{(k)}\|\leq\bar{\rho}
and is some approximate solution of (

). We denote the relative residual by

{\xi}^{(k)}:=\frac{\|{W}^{(k)}{s}^{(k)}+{P}^{(k)}{z^{*}}^{(k)}\|}{\|{z^{*}}^{(k)}\|}.

(5.29)

We now prove a very general result on superlinear convergence of our method. It only depends on the relative residuals
{\xi}^{(k)}
but not on the way how we actually compute
{s}^{(k)}
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Consider the iterates generated by Algorithm
1
and suppose that
{s}^{(k)}\in{\rm rge\;}{P}^{(k)}

\forall k
as described above. Assume that
\varphi({z}^{(k)})
is bounded below and assume that the sequence
{x}^{(k)}
has a limit point
\bar{x}
which is a local minimizer for
\varphi
. Further suppose that
\partial\varphi
is both SCD regular and SCD semismooth
∗
at
(\bar{x},0)
.
If for every subsequence
{x}^{(k_{i})}\to\bar{x}
the corresponding subsequence of relative residuals

converges to
0
,
then
{x}^{(k)}
converges superlinearly to
\bar{x}
and for all
k
sufficiently large there holds
{x}^{(k+1)}={z}^{(k)}+{s}^{(k)}
.

Proof.

By Proposition
2.17
and
[
9
, Corollary 3.5]
it follows that the quadratic growth condition holds at the local minimizer
\bar{x}
, i.e., there exists some reals
C,\delta>0
such that

\varphi(x)\geq\varphi(\bar{x})+C\|x-\bar{x}\|^{2}\ \forall x\in{\cal B}_{\delta}(\bar{x}).

Consider the set

S:=\bigcup_{{\textstyle{x\in{\cal B}_{\delta/2}(\bar{x})\atop\lambda\in(0,\bar{\lambda}]}}}T_{\lambda}(x),

which is bounded by Lemma
2.3
(ii), and let
L
denote the Lipschitz constant of
\nabla f
on the compact convex set

By Lemma
4.3
(vi) there is some
\underline{\lambda}>0
such that

. By Theorem
4.4

and hence there is some index
k_{\delta}\geq 1
such that
\|z_{k}-x_{k}\|<\delta/2

\forall k\geq k_{\delta}
.
Next consider the index set
K:=\{k\geq k_{\delta}\,\mid\,{x}^{(k)}\in{\cal B}_{\delta/2}(\bar{x})\}
and we claim that

\|{z}^{(k)}-\bar{x}\|\leq c_{1}\|{x}^{(k)}-\bar{x}\|\mbox{ with }c_{1}:=\sqrt{\frac{L+1/\underline{\lambda}}{2C}}\ \forall k\in K.

Indeed, for arbitrarily fixed
k\in K
we have
{z}^{(k)}\in{\cal B}_{\delta}(\bar{x})
and
{\lambda}^{(k)}>\underline{\lambda}
implying

\displaystyle\varphi_{{\lambda}^{(k)}}^{\rm FB}({x}^{(k)})

\displaystyle\leq\psi_{{\lambda}^{(k)}}^{\rm FB}\big({x}^{(k)},\bar{x}\big)

\displaystyle=\varphi(\bar{x})+f({x}^{(k)})+\nabla f({x}^{(k)})(\bar{x}-{x}^{(k)})-f(\bar{x})+\frac{1}{2{\lambda}^{(k)}}\|\bar{x}-{x}^{(k)}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\varphi(\bar{x})+\frac{L+1/\underline{\lambda}}{2}\|\bar{x}-{x}^{(k)}\|^{2}.

Using the bound (
4.21
) and the quadratic growth condition we conclude that

\displaystyle C\|{z}^{(k)}-\bar{x}\|^{2}\leq\varphi({z}^{(k)})-\varphi(\bar{x})\leq\varphi_{{\lambda}^{(k)}}^{\rm FB}({x}^{(k)})-\varphi(\bar{x})-(1-\alpha){\eta}^{(k)}\leq\frac{L+1/\underline{\lambda}}{2}\|\bar{x}-{x}^{(k)}\|^{2}

and the claimed inequality follows.

Further, by (
4.24
) we have

\displaystyle\|{z^{*}}^{(k)}\|

\displaystyle=\|\nabla f({z}^{(k)})+{z_{g}^{*}}^{(k)}\|=\|\nabla f({z}^{(k)})-\nabla f({x}^{(k)})-\frac{1}{{\lambda}^{(k)}}({z}^{(k)}-{x}^{(k)})\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla f({z}^{(k)})-\nabla f({x}^{(k)})\|+\frac{1}{{\lambda}^{(k)}}\|({z}^{(k)}-{x}^{(k)})\|

\displaystyle\leq c_{2}\|{z}^{(k)}-{x}^{(k)}\|

\displaystyle\leq c_{2}(\|{z}^{(k)}-\bar{x}\|+\|{x}^{(k)}-\bar{x}\|)\leq c_{3}\|{x}^{(k)}-\bar{x}\|

(5.32)

with constants
c_{2}:=\left(L+1/\underline{\lambda}\right)
and
c_{3}:=c_{2}(c_{1}+1)
,
implying

\|({z}^{(k)},{z^{*}}^{(k)})-(\bar{x},0)\|\leq(c_{1}+c_{3})\|{x}^{(k)}-\bar{x}\|.

Thus the bound (
2.7
) holds with
\eta:=c_{1}+c_{3}
and we have
({z}^{(k)},{z^{*}}^{(k)})\in{\cal A}_{\eta,\bar{x}}({x}^{(k)})
for all
k\in K

Set
\kappa:={\rm scd\,reg\;}\partial\varphi(\bar{x},0)

with

\epsilon(\eta+c_{3})<\frac{1}{2},\quad(L+1/\underline{\lambda})\epsilon^{2}\leq C,\quad 4(L+1/\bar{\lambda})\epsilon^{2}c_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{(1-\alpha)(1-\beta)}{2\bar{\lambda}}.

(5.33)

By Proposition
2.15
and Lemma
2.8
we can find some radius

(5.34)

Further we can choose
\delta_{z}
so small that for every iteration index

with

(\kappa+1){\xi}^{(k)}<\epsilon.

(5.35)

Set
\delta_{x}:=\min\{\delta,\delta_{z}/\eta\}
and consider an iteration

∗

by (

{P}^{(k)}
and
{W}^{(k)}

\|{s}^{(k)}-{\bar{s}}^{(k)}\|=\|{P}^{(k)}{s}^{(k)}-{\bar{s}}^{(k)}\|=\|{{W}^{(k)}}^{-1}{P}^{(k)}({W}^{(k)}{s}^{(k)}+{P}^{(k)}{z^{*}}^{(k)})\|\leq\|C_{{L}^{(k)}}\|{\xi}^{(k)}\|{z^{*}}^{(k)}\|

\displaystyle\|{z}^{(k)}+{s}^{(k)}-\bar{x}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|{z}^{(k)}+{\bar{s}}^{(k)}-\bar{x}\|+\|{s}^{(k)}-{\bar{s}}^{(k)}\|\leq\epsilon\|({z}^{(k)}-\bar{x},{z^{*}}^{(k)})\|+\|C_{{L}^{(k)}}\|{\xi}^{(k)}\|{z^{*}}^{(k)}\|

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon\|{z}^{(k)}-\bar{x}\|+\big(\epsilon+(\kappa+1){\xi}^{(k)}\big)c_{2}\|{z}^{(k)}-{x}^{(k)}\|

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\epsilon c_{1}+\big(\epsilon+(\kappa+1){\xi}^{(k)}\big)c_{3}\Big)\|{x}^{(k)}-\bar{x}\|=\big(\epsilon\eta+(\kappa+1){\xi}^{(k)}c_{3})\|{x}^{(k)}-\bar{x}\|

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon(\eta+c_{3})\|{x}^{(k)}-\bar{x}\|<\frac{1}{2}\|{x}^{(k)}-\bar{x}\|

by (
5.31
)-(
5.33

4.2
,
we will now show that for every

resulting in

and
{\lambda}^{(k+1,j)}=2^{-j}{\lambda}^{(k+1,0)}\geq 1/{\underline{\lambda}}
. Indeed, since
{z}^{(k)}\in{\cal B}_{\delta_{z}}(\bar{x})
and
{x}^{(k+1,0)}={z}^{(k)}+{s}^{(k)}\in{\cal B}_{\delta_{x}/2}(\bar{x})

\displaystyle\varphi^{\rm FB}_{{\lambda}^{(k+1,j)}}({z}^{(k)}+{s}^{(k)})

.

1.

small:

if

else

{\rho}^{(k+1)}\leftarrow{\rho}^{(k)}

(5.38)

{s}^{(k)}

For large
n
the computation of the exact solution of the Newton system (
5.28
) might be very time consuming and it seems to be advantageous to compute only an approximate solution by means of an iterative method. This is our second approach. Since we are dealing with quadratic optimization problems, the use of conjugate gradients (CG) seems to be appropriate. Motivated by Lemma
3.13
, we try to find an approximate solution of the subproblem

\min\frac{1}{2}s^{T}{W}^{(k)}s+\langle{z^{*}}^{(k)},s\rangle\quad\mbox{subject to}\quad s\in{\rm rge\;}{P}^{(k)},\ \|s\|\leq{\rho}^{(k)}

(5.40)

Consider an
n\times m
matrix
{Z}^{(k)}
, where
m=\dim{\rm rge\;}{P}^{(k)}
, whose columns form an basis for
{\rm rge\;}{P}^{(k)}
and therefore
{P}^{(k)}={Z}^{(k)}\big({{Z}^{(k)}}^{T}{Z}^{(k)}\big)^{-1}{{Z}^{(k)}}^{T}
. Then the problem above can be equivalently rewritten as

\min_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\frac{1}{2}u^{T}{{Z}^{(k)}}^{T}{W}^{(k)}{Z}^{(k)}u+\langle{{Z}^{(k)}}^{T}{z^{*}}^{(k)},u\rangle\quad\mbox{subject to}\quad\|{Z}^{(k)}u\|\leq{\rho}^{(k)}.

(5.41)

Any solution
s
to (
5.40
) induces a solution
u=\big({{Z}^{(k)}}^{T}{Z}^{(k)}\big)^{-1}{{Z}^{(k)}}^{T}s
to (
5.41
) and, vice versa,
s={{Z}^{(k)}}u
is a solution to (
5.40
) for every solution
u
to (
5.41
). Consider the following algorithm:

.

Preconditioned Trust Region CG

Input:
n\times n
matrix

,
n\times m
matrix

, subgradient

,

;

2.
while

\|{Z}^{(k)}{u}^{(j)}\|\leq{\rho}^{(k)}\ \wedge\|{W}^{(k)}{Z}^{(k)}{u}^{(j)}+{P}^{(k)}{z^{*}}^{(k)}\|\geq{\bar{\xi}}^{(k)}\|{z^{*}}^{(k)}\|

3.

In Algorithm

{\rho}^{(k)}
or we get a sufficient decrease in the gradient.

Note that (
5.42
) is in fact a two-dimensional problem, which is computationally inexpensive to solve. After some
algebraic manipulation it can be reduced to finding the roots of a fourth degree polynomial. However, in our implementation we used an iterative procedure (see, e.g,
[
27
, Chapter 4.3]
) for solving this subproblem. The condition
\langle{y}^{(j)},{p}^{(j)}\rangle=\langle{{Z}^{(k)}}^{T}{W}^{(k)}{Z}^{(k)}{p}^{(j)},{p}^{(j)}\rangle\leq 0
implies that the matrix
{{Z}^{(k)}}^{T}{W}^{(k)}{Z}^{(k)}
is not positive semidefinite. Further, if
\langle{y}^{(j)},{p}^{(j)}\rangle<0
then the solution
{s}^{(k)}
of (
5.42
) satisfies
\|{s}^{(k)}\|={\rho}^{(k)}
.

As an alternative to Algorithm
2
one could also use the conjugate gradient (CG) algorithm due to Steihaug
[
34
]
for solving (
5.41
).

In case when
{s}^{(k)}
is computed by Algorithm
2
, we need some stronger assumption on the minimizer
\bar{x}
to guarantee that the matrix
{W}^{(k)}
is positive definite for
{x}^{(k)}
close to
\bar{x}
and therefore
{s}^{(k)}
is not computed by (
5.42
).

Corollary 5.4
.

Consider the iterates generated by Algorithm
1
with
{s}^{(k)}
computed by Algorithm
2
with tolerances
{\bar{\xi}}^{(k)}:=\chi(\|{z^{*}}^{(k)}\|)
, where
\chi:(0,\infty)\to(0,1)
is a monotonically increasing function with
\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\chi(t)=0
. Assume that
\varphi({z}^{(k)})
is bounded below and assume that the sequence
{x}^{(k)}
has a limit point
\bar{x}
which is a tilt-stable local minimizer for
\varphi
. Further suppose that
\varphi
is prox-regular and subdifferentially continuous at
\bar{x}
for
0
and that
\partial\varphi
is SCD semismooth
∗
at
(\bar{x},0)
. Then
{x}^{(k)}
converges superlinearly to
\bar{x}
.

Proof.

Since
\partial\varphi
is SCD regular at
(\bar{x},0)
by Theorem
2.10
, the real
\beta:=2/{\rm scd\,reg\;}\partial\varphi(\bar{x},0)
is positive, By Proposition
3.15
together with Lemma
2.8
we can find a neighborhood
U\times V
of
(\bar{x},0)
such that for every
(x,x^{*})\in\partial\varphi\cap(U\times V)
and every
(P,W)\in{\cal M}_{P,W}\partial\varphi(x,x^{*})
the matrix
W
is positive definite and there holds

s^{T}PWPs\geq\beta\|Ps\|^{2}\ \forall s\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Thus, whenever
({z}^{(k)},{z^{*}}^{(k)})\in{\rm int\,}(U\times V)
then the matrix
{W}^{(k)}
is positive definite and the obtained search direction
{s}^{(k)}
fulfills
{{s}^{(k)}}^{T}{P}^{(k)}{W}^{(k)}{P}^{(k)}{s}^{(k)}\geq\beta\|{P}^{(k)}{s}^{(k)}\|^{2}
. Hence the condition in the
if
statement in step 4. of Algorithm
2
is never fulfilled. Since
{P}^{(k)}{s}^{(k)}={s}^{(k)}
and the objective function values at the iterates produced by the CG-method are strictly decreasing, we obtain

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle>\frac{1}{2}{{s}^{(k)}}^{T}{W}^{(k)}{s}^{(k)}+\langle{z^{*}}^{(k)},{s}^{(k)}\rangle\geq\frac{1}{2}\beta\|{s}^{(k)}\|^{2}-\|{z^{*}}^{(k)}\|\|{s}^{(k)}\|.

Thus
\|{s}^{(k)}\|<2\|{z^{*}}^{(k)}\|/\beta
showing that
\|{s}^{(k)}\|<\underline{\rho}<{\rho}^{(k)}
whenever
\|{z^{*}}^{(k)}\|<\underline{\rho}\beta/2
and in this case the relative residual fulfills
{\xi}^{(k)}\leq{\bar{\xi}}^{(k)}
. Since for every subsequence
{x}^{(k_{i})}\to\bar{x}
we have
{z^{*}}^{(k_{i})}\to 0
by (
5.31
), we conclude
{\xi}^{(k_{i})}\to 0
and the assertion follows from Theorem
5.1
.
∎

Note that the assumption of a tilt-stable local minimizer (together with the other assumptions) is only sufficient for superlinear convergence but not necessary. Without this assumption
it is possible that some of the matrices
{W}^{(k)}
are not positive definite, but as soon as we encounter a positive definite matrix
{W}^{(k)}
at some iterate
{z}^{(k)}
close to
\bar{x}
, this results in a big improvement of
{z}^{(k)}
. Let us illustrate this behavior in the following example.

Example 5.5
.

Consider the function
\varphi(x_{1},x_{2})=\frac{1}{2}x_{1}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}x_{2}^{2}+\delta_{C}(x_{1},x_{2})
, where

C:=\{(x_{1},x_{2})\,\mid\,-x_{1}\leq 2x_{2}\leq x_{1}\}.

Then
\bar{x}=(0,0)
is a strict local minimizer which is not tilt-stable, cf.
[
13
, Example 7.10]
. Now consider an iterate
{z}^{(k)}
satisfying
0<2{z_{2}}^{(k)}<{z_{1}}^{(k)}
. Then easy calculations show that
{z^{*}}^{(k)}=({z_{1}}^{(k)},-{z_{2}}^{(k)})
,
{P}^{(k)}=I
and that
{W}^{(k)}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{smallmatrix}\right)
is not positive definite. Note that the direction computed by (
5.39
) points to the exact solution. If we use Algorithm
2
it might be that the search direction
{s}^{(k)}
is computed by (
5.42
) yielding a search direction
{s}^{(k)}
with
\|{s}^{(k)}\|={\rho}^{(k)}
. However it can be easily verified that in this case the next iterate
{z}^{(k+1)}
either belongs to the set
T:=\{(x_{1},x_{2})\,\mid\,0\leq 2x_{2}=x_{1}\}\subset{\rm bd\,}C
or is at least closer to
T
as
{z}^{(k)}
. Due to the use of the proximal mapping, one can show that one of the subsequent iterates
{z}^{(j)}
,
j\geq k+1
must actually belong to
T
. If
{z}^{(j)}\not=\bar{x}
, then
{P}^{(j)}=\frac{1}{5}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}4&2\\
2&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)
and
{W}^{(j)}={P}^{(j)}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{smallmatrix}\right){P}^{(j)}+(I-{P}^{(j)})
is positive definite and the search direction
{s}^{(j)}
points towards the solution.

6.1
Signorini problem with Tresca friction

We consider an elastic body represented by the domain

\Omega=\{(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\,\mid\,(x_{1},x_{2})\in(0,2)\times(0,1),d(x_{1},x_{2})<x_{3}<1\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3},

where
d:(0,2)\times(0,1)\to(0,1)
is a function describing the bottom surface of the body.
The body is made of elastic, homogeneous, and isotropic material.
The boundary consists of three parts
\Gamma_{u}=\{0\}\times[0,1]^{2}
,

\Gamma_{c}:=\{(x_{1},x_{2},d(x_{1},x_{2}))\,\mid\,(x_{1},x_{2})\in(0,2)\times(0,1)\}
and
\Gamma_{p}=\partial\Omega\setminus(\Gamma_{c}\cup\Gamma_{u})
.
Zero displacements are prescribed on
\Gamma_{u}
, surface tractions act on
\Gamma_{p}
, and the body is subject to volume forces. We seek a displacement field
and a corresponding stress field satisfying the Lamé system of PDEs in
\Omega
, the homogeneous Dirichlet
boundary conditions on
\Gamma_{u}
, and the Neumann boundary conditions on
\Gamma_{p}
. The body is unilaterally
supported along
\Gamma_{c}
by some flat rigid foundation given by the half space
\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}_{-}
and the initial gap
between the body and the rigid foundation is given by
d(x_{1},x_{2})
,
(x_{1},x_{2})\in[0,2]\times[0,1]
. In the contact zone
\Gamma_{c}
, we consider
a Tresca friction condition.
This problem can be described by partial differential equations and boundary conditions for the
displacements, which we are looking for. We refer the reader to, e.g.,
[
11
]
, where also a weak formulation
can be found. We consider here only the discrete algebraic problem, which arises after some
suitable finite element approximation.
Let
n
denote the number of degrees of freedom of the nodal displacement vector and let
p
denote
the number of nodes
x_{i}
lying in the contact zone
\Gamma_{c}
. After some suitable reordering of the variables, such that
the first
3p
positions are occupied by the displacements of the nodes lying in the contact part of the
boundary, we arrive at the following nodal block structure for an arbitrary vector
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
:

y=(y^{1},\ldots,y^{p},y^{R})\mbox{ with }y^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{3},\ i=1,\ldots,p,\ y^{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-3p}.

In what follows,
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and
l\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
are the stiffness matrix and the load vector, respectively. The matrix
A
is sparse, symmetric and positive definite and has no more than 81 nonzero elements in each row. Given a vector
z=(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, we denote by
z_{12}:=(z_{1},z_{2})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
the vector formed by the first
two components. Using this notation, the displacement vector
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a solution of the problem

\min_{v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{1}{2}v^{T}Av-\langle l,v\rangle+\sum_{i=1}^{p}\big({\cal F}_{i}\|v^{i}_{12}\|+\delta_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}(v_{3}^{i}+d(x^{i}_{12}))\big),

(6.44)

where
\delta_{R_{+}}
denotes the indicator function of
\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
{\cal F}_{i}>0
is the given friction coefficient.

This problem is of the form (
1.1
) with the convex quadratic function
f(v)=\frac{1}{2}v^{T}Av-\langle l,v\rangle
and the nonsmooth convex function
g(v)=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\big({\cal F}_{i}\|v^{i}_{12}\|+\delta_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}(v_{3}+d(x_{i}))\big)
. Clearly, the proximal mapping
{\rm prox}_{\lambda g}
is single-valued and given by
\bar{v}={\rm prox}_{\lambda g}(v)
with

\bar{v}_{12}^{i}=\begin{cases}0&\mbox{if $\|v^{i}_{12}\|\leq\lambda{\cal F}_{i}$,}\\
\left(1-\frac{\lambda{\cal F}_{i}}{\|v^{i}_{12}\|}\right)v^{i}_{12}&\mbox{else,}\end{cases}\quad\bar{v}^{i}_{3}=\max\{v_{3}^{i},-d(x^{i}_{1,2})\},\ i=1,\ldots,p,\ \bar{v}^{R}=v^{R}.

The corresponding subgradient
\bar{v}^{*}\in\partial g(\bar{v})
can be easily computed by
\bar{v}^{*}=(v-\bar{v})/\lambda
.

Given
(v,v^{*})\in\mathrm{gph}\,\partial g
, we can compute an element

(P,W)=\left(\begin{pmatrix}P^{1}&&&0\\
&\ddots&&\\
&&P^{p}&\\
0&&&P^{R}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}W^{1}&&&0\\
&\ddots&&\\
&&W^{p}&\\
0&&&W^{R}\end{pmatrix}\right)\in{\cal M}_{P,W}\partial g(v,v^{*})

as block diagonal matrices formed by the
3\times 3
blocks

P^{i}=\begin{pmatrix}P^{i}_{12}&0\\
0&P^{i}_{3}\end{pmatrix},\ W^{i}=\begin{pmatrix}W^{i}_{12}&0\\
0&W^{i}_{3}\end{pmatrix},\ i=1,\ldots,p

and the
(n-3p)\times(n-3p)
matrices
P^{R}=I
,
W^{R}=0
, where

(P^{i}_{12},W^{i}_{12})=\begin{cases}\left(I,\frac{{\cal F}_{i}}{\|v^{i}_{12}\|}\left(I-\frac{v^{i}_{12}{v^{i}_{12}}^{T}}{\|v^{i}_{12}\|^{2}}\right)\right)&\mbox{if $v^{i}_{12}\not=0$,}\\
(0,I)&\mbox{if $v^{i}_{12}=0$,}\end{cases}\quad\mbox{and}\quad(P^{i}_{3},W^{i}_{3})=\begin{cases}\left(1,0\right)&\mbox{if $v^{i}_{3}+d(x^{i}_{12})>0$,}\\
(0,1)&\mbox{if $v^{i}_{3}+d(x^{i}_{12})=0$,}\end{cases}

cf. Example
3.11
. We see that
P
is a diagonal matrix with diagonal elements belonging to
\{0,1\}
and therefore the unit vectors given by the nonzero columns of
P
form an orthogonal basis for
{\rm rge\;}P
. Writing these unit vectors into an
n\times m
matrix
Z
and using Lemma
3.16
, problem (
5.41
) is of the form

\min_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\frac{1}{2}u^{T}Z^{T}(A+W)Zu+\langle u,Z^{T}(Av-l+v^{*})\rangle\quad\mbox{subject to}\quad\|u\|\leq\rho.

The SCD semismooth
∗
Newton direction is then given by
s=Zu
, where
u
denotes an (approximate) solution of this quadratic auxiliary problem.
Of course, the
m\times m
matrix
Z^{T}(A+W)Z
is the submatrix of
A+W
formed by the rows and columns corresponding to the unit vectors in
Z
.

6.2
(Nonconvex) sparse approximation

Now let us consider the problem of finding a sparse solution
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
to a least-squares problem
Ax=b
, where
A
is an
m\times n
matrix and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Sparsity can be induced by
considering the
\ell_{q}(q\in[0,1])
-regularized problem

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{1}{2}\|Ax-b\|^{2}+\mu\|x\|_{q}^{q},

(6.45)

where
\mu>0
is the regularization parameter and

\|x\|_{q}^{q}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}|^{q}.

Thus the smooth part is given by
f(x)=\frac{1}{2}\|Ax-b\|^{2}
and the nonsmooth part by
g(x)=\mu\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}|^{q}
.
In case
q=1
we obtain the well-known lasso problem which is convex, whereas the resulting problem is nonconvex when
q\in[0,1)
. In our numerical experiments we restrict ourselves to the cases
q\in\{1,\frac{1}{2}\}
for which the proximal mapping of
|\cdot|^{q}
is explicitly known. We would like to point out that we can apply our method also when the proximal mapping can only be computed numerically, because usually only a few evaluations of the proximal mapping are required.

Due to the separable structure of
g
only the proximal mapping of the one-dimensional functions
|\cdot|^{q}
is required in order to compute
{\rm prox}_{\lambda g}
. Given an element
(x,x^{*})\in\mathrm{gph}\,\partial g
, we can choose the matrices
(P,W)\in{\cal M}_{P,W}\partial g(x,x^{*})
as diagonal matrices with entries

(P_{ii},W_{ii})=\begin{cases}(1,q(q-1)\mu|x_{i}|^{q-2})&\mbox{if $x_{i}\not=0$,}\\
(0,1)&\mbox{else,}\end{cases}

cf. Example
3.9
. Note that in case when
x_{i}\not=0
we have
W_{ii}=\mu\nabla^{2}|\cdot|^{q}(x_{i})
. As for the Signorini problem, the nonzero columns of
P
, which correspond to the nonzero components of
x
, form an orthogonal basis for
{\rm rge\;}P
and can be used to build the matrix
{Z}^{(k)}
in (
5.41
).

Although in our convergence analysis the semismooth
∗
Newton direction
{s}^{(k)}
is only used for ensuring superlinear convergence, in practice
{s}^{(k)}
can be also used to accelerate convergence from poor starting points. To this aim we observed that the trust region concept according to (
5.38
) is not adequate for the function
\|\cdot\|_{q}^{q}
if
q>0
. Instead of requiring that
\|{s}^{(k)}\|^{2}
is sufficiently small, it seems to be better to bound the relative quantities

{\nu_{-}}^{(k)}:=\min\{{s}^{(k)}_{i}/{z}^{(k)}_{i}\,\mid\,{z}^{(k)}_{i}\not=0\},\quad{\nu_{+}}^{(k)}:=\max\{{s}^{(k)}_{i}/{z}^{(k)}_{i}\,\mid\,{z}^{(k)}_{i}\not=0\}.

Clearly, if for a nonzero component
{z}^{(k)}_{i}
there holds
{s}^{(k)}_{i}/{z}^{(k)}_{i}\ll-1
then the quantity

|{z}^{(k)}_{i}|^{q}+\nabla|\cdot|^{q}({z}^{(k)}_{i}){s}^{(k)}_{i}+\frac{1}{2}\nabla^{2}|\cdot|^{q}({z}^{(k)}_{i}){{s}^{(k)}_{i}}^{2}

cannot be a good approximation for
|{z}^{(k)}_{i}+{s}^{(k)}_{i}|^{q}
and the same holds true when
{s}^{(k)}_{i}/{z}^{(k)}_{i}\gg 1
in case when
q=\frac{1}{2}
.
We thus add to the matrix
{W}^{(k)}
in (
5.41
) a diagonal matrix
{D}^{(k)}
with entries
{D}^{(k)}_{ii}={\zeta}^{(k)}/{{z}^{(k)}_{i}}^{2}
for the nonzero components of
{z}^{(k)}
, the remaining diagonal elements of
{D}^{(k)}
can be set arbitrarily, e.g. zero. The parameter
{\zeta}^{(k)}
is updated by the rule

{\zeta}^{(k+1)}=\begin{cases}2{\zeta}^{(k)}&\mbox{if ${{s}^{(k)}}^{T}{W}^{(k)}{s}^{(k)}\leq 0$}\\
{\zeta}^{(k)}\sqrt{{\nu}^{(k)}_{\epsilon}}&\mbox{if ${{s}^{(k)}}^{T}{W}^{(k)}{s}^{(k)}>0$ and ${\nu_{\epsilon}}^{(k)}\not\in[0.8,1.2]$,}\\
{\zeta}^{(k)}&\mbox{else,}\end{cases}

where

\displaystyle{\nu_{\epsilon}}^{(k)}:=\begin{cases}-{\nu}^{(k)}_{\epsilon-}&\mbox{if $q=1$,}\\
\max\{-{\nu}^{(k)}_{\epsilon-},{\nu}^{(k)}_{\epsilon+}\}&\mbox{if $q=\frac{1}{2}$,}\end{cases}

\displaystyle{\nu}^{(k)}_{\epsilon-}:=\min\{{s}^{(k)}_{i}/({z}^{(k)}_{i}+\epsilon{\rm sign\,}{z}^{(k)}_{i})\,\mid\,{z}^{(k)}_{i}\not=0\},\quad{\nu}^{(k)}_{\epsilon+}:=\max\{{s}^{(k)}_{i}/({z}^{(k)}_{i}+\epsilon{\rm sign\,}{z}^{(k)}_{i})\,\mid\,{z}^{(k)}_{i}\not=0\}.

For the considered test examples we used
\epsilon=10^{-4}
.
With this modification the superlinear convergence is not affected because of
{\zeta}^{(k)}\to 0
whenever
{s}^{(k)}\to 0
, but for some test problems we observed a considerable reduction both in the number of iterations and the number of backtracking steps. The maximum speedup (factor 8) was achieved for the instance
triazines4
,
\lambda_{c}=10^{-4}
, cf. Table
2
below.

In Algorithm
2
we used the diagonal for preconditioning in case when the matrix
A
is available. In case when we have at our disposal only functions for evaluating the matrix-vector products
Ax
and
A^{T}y
we replace the diagonal of
A^{T}A
by
\sigma I
, where
\sigma
is an estimate of the mean value of the diagonal elements of
A^{T}A
. We estimate
\sigma
by choosing randomly 10 vectors
x_{i}
and set
\sigma:=\frac{1}{10}\sum_{i=1}^{10}\|Ax_{i}\|^{2}/\|x_{i}\|^{2}
.

Let us now turn to the nonconvex case
q=\frac{1}{2}
. We observed that the considered test problems have many local minima and, dependent on the chosen starting point, our method may converge to any of these minima. Therefore we implemented the following heuristic to obtain a better solution:

Algorithm 3

(Heuristic for the nonconvex case
q=\frac{1}{2}
)
.

Input:
Starting point
{x}^{(0)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

Initialization:

j\leftarrow 0
,
\varphi_{\rm opt}\leftarrow\infty
,
j_{\rm opt}\leftarrow 0
;

1.

Compute a (local) solution
{\bar{x}}^{(j)}
using
{x}^{(j)}
as starting point. Let
{\lambda}^{(j)}
denote the final parameter
{\lambda}^{(k)}
when the method terminates.

2.

if
\varphi({\bar{x}}^{(j)})<\varphi_{\rm opt}
then
\varphi_{\rm opt}\leftarrow\varphi({\bar{x}}^{(j)})
,
j_{\rm opt}\leftarrow j
.

3.

if
j>\max\{j_{\rm opt}+2,10\}\vee\big(j>1\wedge\varphi({\bar{x}}^{(j)})==\varphi({\bar{x}}^{(j-1)})\big)
then stop.

4.

Compute
{z}^{(j)}\in T_{1000{\lambda}^{(j)}}({\bar{x}}^{(j)})
, denote
{I}^{(j)}:=\{i\,\mid\,{z}^{(j)}_{i}\not=0\}
and compute
an (approximate) solution
{\triangle x}^{(j)}
of the system

\min_{\triangle x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{1}{2}\|A({\bar{x}}^{(j)}+\triangle x)-b\|^{2}\quad\mbox{ s.t. }\quad\triangle x_{i}=0,\ i\not\in{I}^{(j)}.

(6.46)

5.

Set
{x}^{(j+1)}\leftarrow{\bar{x}}^{(j)}+\frac{1}{4}{\triangle x}^{(j)}
,
j\leftarrow j+1
and go to Step 1.

In fact, we solve the system (
6.46
) rather imprecisely by means of a CG method stopping when we decreased the initial residual by a factor of
0.1
. The idea behind Algorithm
3
is to use a starting point
{x}^{(j+1)}
having a smaller residual than the previously obtained local solution
{\bar{x}}^{(j)}
, i.e.,
\|A{x}^{(j+1)}-b\|<\|A{\bar{x}}^{(j)}-b\|
.

In most cases, the first iteration of the heuristic is the most time consuming one. In Table
4
we present some results of Algorithm
3
when applied to the nine test problems from the LIBSVM data set with starting point as the corresponding solution of the convex case with
\lambda_{c}=10^{-4}
. For each problem we report our findings for two values of
\lambda_{c}
. We compare our method with the very recent method HpgSRN
[
40
]
, a regularized Newton method for
\ell_{q}
-norm composite optimization problems. In
[
40
]
, the reader can find a comparison of HpgSRN with other methods, including ZeroFPR, showing that HpgSRN is superior to ZeroFPR on this problem class. Note that HpgSRN uses also a proximal gradient approach for ensuring global convergence and therefore the parameter
{\lambda}^{(j)}
in Algorithm
3
is defined. We state for the first iteration the objective function value
\varphi({x}^{(1)})
and the time
t_{1}
needed to compute the first local minimizer
{x}^{(1)}
. Finally we give the best obtained objective function value
\varphi_{\rm opt}
and the total time

we obtained even a better objective function value
\varphi_{\rm opt}
with the randomly chosen starting point. This shows that the heuristic of Algorithm
3
, although very simple, works well for our test problems. It is beyond the scope of this paper to find better methods approximating the global minimizer in the nonconvex case.

Finally we want to mention a remarkable property of our method in the nonconvex case
q=\frac{1}{2}
. We observed that the function not only has a lot of local minima but also many stationary points which are locally non-optimal. Let us therefore have a closer look on second-order necessary conditions for a local minimizer. If
\bar{x}
is a local minimizer, then it is clearly also a local minimizer for the problem

\min\frac{1}{2}\|Ax-b\|^{2}+\mu\sum_{i\in I(\bar{x})}\sqrt{|x_{i}|}\quad\mbox{subject to}\quad x_{i}=0,\ i\not\in I(\bar{x}),

where
I(\bar{x})=\{i\,\mid\,\bar{x}_{i}\not=0\}
. The objective for this problem is twice continuously differentiable near
\bar{x}
and therefore the second-order necessary KKT-conditions hold at
\bar{x}
. Thus the matrix
A_{I(\bar{x})}^{T}A_{I(\bar{x})}+\bar{W}_{I(\bar{x})}
must be positive semidefinite, where
A_{I(\bar{x})}
denotes the matrix with columns
A_{i}
,
i\in I(\bar{x})
and
\bar{W}_{I(\bar{x})}
is a diagonal matrix with entries
-\frac{\mu}{4}|\bar{x}_{i}|^{-3/2}
,
i\in I(\bar{x})
. Whenever the number of nonzeros at the returned solution was small enough, i.e., less than 5000, we tested this condition. We observed that it was always fulfilled for the solutions returned by GSSN, whereas it failed to hold in rare cases for HpgSRN and ZeroFPR. However, we want to point out that this behaviour of GSSN is due to the high accuracy in our termination criterion (
6.43
). We observed that sometimes the iterates produced by GSSN get very close to a non-optimal stationary point, until the CG-method of Algorithm
2
terminated with a direction of negative curvature and the method could escape from the stationary point along the search direction computed by (
5.42
).

7
Conclusion

We presented GSSN, a globalized SCD semismooth
∗
Newton method, which is a hybrid of some variant of a proximal gradient method ensuring global convergence and the SCD semismooth
∗
Newton method yielding fast convergence near the solution. The method can be applied to nonconvex nonsmooth optimization problem, where the nonsmooth part can be rather general. Compared with other methods, GSSN has the advantage that superlinear convergence can be assured under weak assumptions.
