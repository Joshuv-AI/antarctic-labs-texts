Abstract




Abstract

In this paper we prove the existence of normalized solutions
(\lambda,u)\subset(0,\infty)\times H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
to the following Schrödinger-Poisson equation

\begin{cases}-\Delta u+V(x)u+\lambda u+(|x|^{-1}\ast u^{2})u=|u|^{p-2}u\quad\text{in}\quad\mathbb{R}^{3},\\
\displaystyle u>0,\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}u^{2}dx=a^{2},\end{cases}

where
a>0
is fixed,
p\in(\frac{10}{3},6)
is a given exponent and the potential
V
satisfies some suitable conditions. Since the
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
-norm of
u
is fixed,
\lambda
appears as a Lagrange multiplier. For
V(x)\geq 0
, our solutions are obtained by using a mountain-pass argument on bounded domains and a limit process introduced by Bartsch et al
[
3
]
. For
V(x)\leq 0
, we directly construct an entire mountain-pass solution with positive energy.

Key words:
Schrödinger-Poisson equation; Normalized solution; Variational Methods.

35J10, 35J50, 35J60.

Introduction

Consider the following time-dependent Schrödinger-Poisson equation

i\partial_{t}\psi+\Delta\psi-(|x|^{-1}\ast|\psi|^{2})\psi+g(|\psi|)\psi=0\qquad\text{in}~(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{3},

(1.1)

where the function
\psi=\psi(t,x):(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is complex valued and
g
is real valued. This class of Schrödinger-type equations with a repulsive nonlocal Coulomb potential are obtained as an approximation of the Hartree-Fock equation which is used to describe a quantum mechanical system of many particles, see
[
23
,
24
,
28
]
for more physical background.

A stationary wave of (
1.1
) is a solution of the form
\psi(t,x)=e^{i\lambda t}u(x)
, where
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
denotes the frequency. It is well-known that
e^{i\lambda t}u(x)
solves (
1.1
) if and only if
(\lambda,u)
is a pair of solutions of the following equation

-\Delta u+\lambda u+(|x|^{-1}\ast u^{2})u=g(|u|)u\qquad\text{in}\,{\mathbb{R}}^{3},

(1.2)

We note that
\phi_{u}:=|x|^{-1}\ast u^{2}
is the convolution of
u^{2}
with the Green function of
-\Delta
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
, hence it fulfills

-\Delta\phi_{u}=u^{2}\qquad\text{in}\,{\mathbb{R}}^{3}.

If
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
is fixed in (
1.2
), this kind of problem is known as the
fixed frequency problem
, which has received many
scholars’ attention, and a great part of the literature is focused on existence, nonexistence and multiplicity of solutions to (
1.2
) or similar problems, see
[
21
,
26
,
31
,
32
,
34
]
and the references therein. In those papers the authors apply variational methods. Similar methods to find solutions are applied in
[
17
,
33
,
35
,
36
]
.

On the other hand, it is also interesting to study the
fixed mass problem
, that is looking for solutions with prescribed
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
-norm
\|u\|_{2}=a>0
. These kind of solutions, which have recently gained interest in physics, are known as
normalized solutions
. Indeed, from the physical point of view, this approach turns out to be more meaningful since it offers a better insight of the variational properties of the stationary solutions of (
1.2
), such as stability or instability. Compared to the fixed frequency case, the study of normalized solutions is more complicated because the terms in the corresponding energy functional scale differently, readers are invited to see
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
20
]
for more details. Now we recall some known results in this direction. In
[
8
]
, Bellazzini and Siciliano considered the following problem

\begin{cases}-\Delta u+\lambda u+(|x|^{-1}\ast u^{2})u=|u|^{p-2}u&\text{in}~\mathbb{R}^{3},\\
\displaystyle u>0,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}u^{2}dx=a^{2},\end{cases}

(1.3)

for
p\in(2,3)
and proved the existence of normalized solutions for
a>0
small. After that, in
[
7
]
, they treated the case
p\in(3,\frac{10}{3})
and proved that (
1.3
) admits normalized solutions if
a>0
is large enough. Later, in
[
20
]
, Jeanjean and Luo identified a threshold value of
a>0
which separates existence and nonexistence of normalized solutions of (
1.3
). It is noteworthy to point out that, in the
L^{2}
-subcritical case

2<p<\frac{10}{3}
, one can obtain normalized solutions by considering the minimization problem

\alpha(a):=\inf_{u\in\mathcal{S}_{a}}I(u),

where

I(u)
is the energy functional corresponding to (
1.3
) defined by

I(u)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\nabla u|^{2}dx+\frac{1}{4}B(u)-\frac{1}{p}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|u|^{p}dx\qquad\forall\,u\in H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{3}),

being

B(u):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{|u(x)|^{2}|u(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|}dxdy\qquad\forall\,u\in H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{3}),

(1.4)

and
\mathcal{S}_{a}
is the
L^{2}
-constraint

\mathcal{S}_{a}=\{u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3}):~\|u\|_{2}^{2}=a^{2}\}.

(1.5)

However, in the
L^{2}
-supercritical case

\frac{10}{3}<p\leq 6
, the functional
I
is no more bounded from below on
\mathcal{S}_{a}
and the minimization method fails. For quite a long time,
[
6
]
was the only paper dealing with the existence of normalized solutions for the
L^{2}
-supercritical case. The main difficulties and challenges are based on several aspects: (i) the classical mountain-pass theorem is not applicable to construct a
(PS)
sequence; (ii) since
\lambda
is unknown, the Nehari manifold cannot be used anymore, which brings more obstacles to prove the boundedness of the
(PS)
sequence; (iii) the compactness of the
(PS)
sequence is more challenging even if one considers the radial case since the embedding of
H^{1}_{r}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})\hookrightarrow L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
is not compact.

Since we are interested in the
L^{2}
-supercritical case
, we now give more related results that inspire us to study our problem. In
[
6
]
, Bellazzini, Jeanjean and Luo studied the normalized solutions by introducing a Nehari-Pohozaev manifold. The benefit of working on the Nehari-Pohozaev manifold is that
I(u)
is coercive. They proved the existence of solutions to (
1.3
) for
a>0
sufficiently small. The smallness condition about
a
is crucial and it is used to show that the Lagrange multiplier
\lambda
is positive which leads to compactness. Later, Luo
[
27
]
studied the multiplicity of normalized solutions by using the Fountain Theorem established by Bartsch and De Valeriola
[
2
]
. Chen
[
15
]
et al. generalized the results to more general non-linearities. Very recently, Jeanjean and Le in
[
19
]
investigated the following Schrödinger-Poisson-Slater equation

-\Delta u+\lambda u-\gamma(|x|^{-1}\ast|u|^{2})u-b|u|^{p-2}u=0\qquad\text{in}~\mathbb{R}^{3},

(1.6)

with
p\in(\frac{10}{3},6]
,
\gamma,b\in\mathbb{R}
and
\|u\|_{2}=a
for some given
a>0
. They obtained existence and nonexistence results in the cases
(\gamma<0,b<0)
,
(\gamma>0,b>0)
and
(\gamma>0,b<0)
. In particular, for the case
\gamma>0,b>0
, they obtained two normalized solutions, one being a local minimizer and the other one being a mountain pass type solution for
a>0
small.

The existence and multiplicity of normalized solutions to the autonomous Schrödinger-Poisson equation are widely studied in the literature, but the non-autonomous case, that is
V(x)\neq 0
, is less understood. From the perspective of physics, the presence of
V(x)
is very important because it represents an external potential that influences the behavior of the stationary waves. From the mathematical point of view, the presence of
V
brings more difficulties, especially in recovering compactness. In fact,
V
is not required to be radial, hence the solutions are not expected to be radial, thus we cannot use the compactness of the embedding
H^{1}_{rad}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})\subset L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
for
2<p<6
. For this reason, we are led to consider the following problem

\begin{cases}-\Delta u+V(x)u+\lambda u+(|x|^{-1}\ast|u|^{2})u=|u|^{p-2}u&\text{in}~\mathbb{R}^{3},\\
\displaystyle u>0,~\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}u^{2}dx=a^{2},\end{cases}

(
\mathcal{P}
)

where
a>0
,
V(x)
is a fixed potential,
\frac{10}{3}<p<6
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
is a Lagrange multiplier. The study of normalized solutions of (

P

) is equivalent to looking for critical points of the functional

J_{V}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\nabla u|^{2}dx+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}V(x)u^{2}dx+\frac{1}{4}B(u)-\frac{1}{p}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|u|^{p}dx

(1.7)

on the constraint
\mathcal{S}_{a}
. Our purpose is to find suitable conditions on
V
to prove existence of normalized solutions to (

P

). Basically, we will distinguish two cases:
V(x)\geq 0
and
V(x)\leq 0
. In both cases we will use a mountain-pass argument, with some relevant differences. In fact, in the case
V(x)\geq 0
, we will construct solutions in domains of the form
\Omega_{r}:=r\Omega
, where
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
is a fixed bounded convex open set and then we will pass to the limit as
r\to\infty
to prove existence of entire solutions in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
. On the other hand, if
V(x)\leq 0
, we directly construct entire solutions using the mountain-pass geometry of the functional
J_{V}
and a classical Ghoussoub min-max principle described in
[
16
, Section 5]
, which enables us to construct a
(PS)
sequence.

Before formulating our main theorems, we first recall some known results. For the limit equation (
1.3
), the existence of normalized solutions is studied in
[
6
]
, at least for
a>0
small enough. We state the following result established by Bellazzini, Jeanjean and Luo.

Theorem 1.1
.

(
[
6
]
)
Let
p\in(\frac{10}{3},6)
. Then there exists
a_{0}>0
such that for any
a\in(0,a_{0})
, there exists a solution
(u_{a},\lambda_{a})\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\times(0,\infty)
of (
1.3
) with
I(u_{a})=c_{a}
, where

c_{a}=\inf_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{a}}\max_{t\in[0,1]}I(g(t))>0

\mathcal{G}_{a}=\{g\in C([0,1],\mathcal{S}_{a}):g(0)\in A_{K_{a}},I(g(1))<0\}

and
A_{K_{a}}=\{u\in\mathcal{S}_{a}:\|\nabla u\|_{2}^{2}\leq K_{a}\}
, for some constant
K_{a}>0
small enough.

Remark 1.1
.

In
[
6
]
, in order to obtain
a bounded
(PS)
sequence, the authors introduced the auxiliary functional

P(u):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\nabla u|^{2}dx+\frac{1}{4}B(u)-\frac{3(p-2)}{2p}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|u|^{p}dx.

(1.8)

given by a linear combination of the Nehari and the Pohozaev constraints. This is relevant because, introducing the scaling

u^{t}(x):=t^{\frac{3}{2}}u(tx)~\text{for}~t>0,\,u\in\mathcal{S}_{a},

(1.9)

we can see that
u^{t}\in\mathcal{S}_{a}
for any
t>0
so that, differentiating
I(u^{t})
with respect to
t
, we can prove that any solution
u
to (
1.3
) fulfills
P(u)=0
(see also Lemma
2.2
below). Moreover, in
[
6
]
the authors also proved that, setting

\mathcal{V}(a):=\{u\in\mathcal{S}_{a}:~P(u)=0\},

the solution

constructed in Theorem
1.1

satisfies

c_{a}=I(u_{a})=\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\inf_{u\in\mathcal{V}(a)}I(u).

(1.10)

As we mentioned above, the geometry of
J_{V}
is significantly influenced by the properties of
V
. In the sequel, we will consider both non-negative and non-positive potentials separately. First, we impose the following conditions on

:

(V_{1})

V\in C^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{3}),\,V\geq 0
,
\lim_{|x|\to\infty}V(x)=0
and the function

\|V\|_{\infty}<\frac{2\theta c_{a}}{a^{2}}\quad\text{and}\quad\|W\|_{\infty}<\frac{c_{a}}{a^{2}}\eta.

(1.11)

Alternatively, we can assume that

(V_{1})^{\prime}

V\in W^{3/2}_{loc}({\mathbb{R}}^{3}),\,V\geq 0,\,\lim_{|x|\to\infty}V(x)=0

(V_{2})

\limsup_{|y|\to\infty}\left(|y|^{\alpha}{{\rm ess}\sup}_{x\in B_{\delta|y|}(y)}\nabla V(y)\cdotp x\right)<0.

•

We note that assumption

•

The proof of Theorem
1.2
: differentiable non-negative potential

Throughout this Section we will assume that
(V_{1})
holds. The strategy to prove Theorem
1.2
is the following: first we fix a bounded convex open set
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
and solve the problem

\begin{cases}-\Delta u+V(x)u+\lambda u+(|x|^{-1}\ast u^{2})u=|u|^{p-2}u\quad\text{in }\quad\Omega_{r}\\
\displaystyle\displaystyle u\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{r}),\,\int_{\Omega_{r}}u^{2}dx=a^{2},\end{cases}

(2.1)

where
\Omega_{r}:=r\Omega
for
r>0
large enough, where

\phi_{u}(x)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\frac{u^{2}(y)}{|x-y|}dy,\qquad\forall\,x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}.

is well defined by extending
u
to
0
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\setminus\Omega_{r}
. In other words
\phi_{u}=|x|^{-1}\ast u
is the convolution of
u
with the Green function of the Laplacian in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
, hence it satisfies the equation

-\Delta\phi_{u}=u^{2}\qquad\text{in}\,{\mathbb{R}}^{3}.

Then we will prove that our solutions are bounded uniformly in
r
in
H^{1}(\Omega_{r})
, so that we can pass to the limit as
r\to\infty
and prove existence of a normalized solution to (

P

) in the whole
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
. We will see that the construction of solutions in a large bounded domain does not require condition
(V_{2})
, which is only used to pass to the limit to have a normalized solution in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Remark 2.4
.

The proof of Proposition
2.4
is very similar to the one of
[
25
, Theorem 1.3]
. In any case, here we stress that such a technique provides a bound of the
L^{\bar{q}}(\Omega)
norm which is uniform in
\Omega
and
\bar{q}
. Such uniformity
will play a crucial role in the proof of Theorem
1.2
when considering the limit as
r\to\infty
.

Proof of Proposition
2.4
.

First we set, for
M>0
and
x\in\Omega
,
v_{M}(x):=\min\{u(x),M\}
and we test equation (
2.24
) with
v:=v_{M}^{2\chi+1}
, where
\chi>0
will be fixed below. A direct computation shows that

\int_{\Omega}(2\chi+1)v_{M}^{2\chi}|\nabla v_{M}|^{2}dx\leq\int_{\Omega}u^{p-1}vdx.

Using the Sobolev embedding
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\subset L^{6}(\Omega)
, we can see that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u^{p-1}vdx\geq\int_{\Omega}(2\chi+1)v_{M}^{2\chi}|\nabla v_{M}|^{2}dx

\displaystyle=\frac{2\chi+1}{(\chi+1)^{2}}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla(v_{M}^{\chi+1})|^{2}dx\geq S\frac{2\chi+1}{(\chi+1)^{2}}\|v_{M}\|^{2(\chi+1)}_{6(\chi+1)}.

On the other hand, by the definition of
v_{M}
and the Holder inequality, one have

\int_{\Omega}u^{p-1}vdx\leq\int_{\Omega}u^{p-2}u^{2(\chi+1)}dx\leq\|u\|_{6}^{p-2}\|u\|^{2(\chi+1)}_{\gamma_{0}(\chi+1)},

where we have set
\gamma_{0}:=\frac{12}{8-p}\in(2,6)
since
p\in(2,6)
. As a consequence, we have the estimate

\|v_{M}\|_{6(\chi+1)}\leq\left(\frac{\chi+1}{(S(2\chi+1))^{1/2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\chi+1}}\|u\|_{6}^{\frac{p-2}{2(\chi+1)}}\|u\|_{\gamma_{0}(\chi+1)}\qquad\forall\,M>0,\,\chi>0.

Using the fact that
u\geq 0
,
v_{M}\to u
as
M\to\infty
point-wise in
\Omega
and the Fatou lemma, we have as
M\to\infty
,

\|u\|_{6(\chi+1)}\leq\left(\frac{\chi+1}{(S(2\chi+1))^{1/2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\chi+1}}\|u\|_{6}^{\frac{p-2}{2(\chi+1)}}\|u\|_{\gamma_{0}(\chi+1)}\qquad\forall\,\chi>0.

(2.25)

Applying (
2.25
) with
\chi=\chi_{1}>0
such that
1+\chi_{1}=\gamma_{0}/6
we have

\|u\|_{6(\chi_{1}+1)}\leq\left(\frac{\chi_{1}+1}{(S(2\chi_{1}+1))^{1/2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\chi_{1}+1}}\|u\|_{6}^{1+\frac{p-2}{2(\chi_{1}+1)}}.

By induction, for any
n>0
we can choose
\chi_{n}>0
such that
1+\chi_{n}=(6/\gamma_{0})^{n}
, so that, by (
2.25
)

\|u\|_{6(\chi_{n}+1)}\leq S^{-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\chi_{k}+1}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\chi_{k}+1}{(2\chi_{k}+1)^{1/2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\chi_{k}+1}}\|u\|_{6}^{1+\frac{p-2}{2}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\chi_{k}+1}\right)},

for any
n\geq 1
. We note that
\chi_{n}\to\infty
. Using the properties of the function
\varphi(y):=\left(\frac{y+1}{(2y+1)^{1/2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\sqrt{y+1}}}
and the properties of the geometric series we conclude that

\|u\|_{6(\chi_{n}+1)}\leq c\|u\|_{6}^{1+\frac{p-2}{6-p}(1-(\gamma_{0}/6)^{n})}\qquad\forall\,n\geq 1,

for some constant
c=c(p)>0
depending on
p
but not on
\Omega
. We refer to the proof of
[
25
, Theorem 1.3]
for the details.

Taking
\bar{q}>6
,
n\geq 1
such that
6(\chi_{n}+1)>\bar{q}
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
defined by

\frac{1}{\bar{q}}=\frac{\alpha}{6}+\frac{1-\alpha}{6(\chi_{n}+1)},

an interpolation inequality gives

\|u\|_{\bar{q}}\leq\|u\|_{6}^{\alpha}\|u\|^{1-\alpha}_{6(\chi_{n}+1)}\leq c\|u\|_{6}^{1+\frac{p-2}{6-p}(1-(\gamma_{0}/6)^{n})(1-\alpha)}\leq c\max\{\|u\|_{6},\|u\|_{6}^{1+\frac{p-2}{6-p}}\}=:g(\|u\|_{6}),

where
c>0
is independent of
q
and
\Omega
. This concludes the proof for
\bar{q}\in[6,\infty)
.

In orded to treat the case
q=\infty
, we note that the function
v(x):=\min\{u(x),g(\|u\|_{6})+1\}
is bounded in
\Omega
and satisfies
\|v\|_{\bar{q}}\leq\|u\|_{\bar{q}}\leq g(\|u\|_{6})
for any
\bar{q}\geq 6
, hence

\|v\|_{\infty}=\lim_{\bar{q}\to\infty}\|v\|_{\bar{q}}\leq g(\|u\|_{6}).

This yields that
v(x)=\min\{u(x),g(\|u\|_{6})+1\}\leq g(\|u\|_{6})
, hence
v=u
, so in particular
u\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
and
\|u\|_{\infty}\leq g(\|u\|_{6})
.
∎

Lemma 2.6

(Splitting Lemma)

Let

(PS)

,

(2.27)

and

(2.28)

Furthermore, one has

\|u_{n}\|_{2}^{2}=\|u\|_{2}^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\|w^{j}\|_{2}^{2}+o(1)

(2.29)

and

J_{V,\lambda}(u_{n})=J_{V,\lambda}(u)+\sum_{j=1}^{k}I_{\lambda}(w^{j})+o(1)

(2.30)

Proof of the Splitting Lemma.

The proof of Lemma

.

Setting

, we have
\psi^{1}_{n}\to 0

and strongly in

, hence we have

(2.31)

\|\psi^{1}_{n}\|^{2}_{2}=\|u_{n}\|^{2}_{2}-\|u\|^{2}_{2}+o(1)\qquad\text{as}\,n\to\infty

(2.32)

and, thanks to the Brézis-Lieb Theorem (see
[
13

),

\|u_{n}\|^{p}_{p}=\|u\|^{p}_{p}+\|\psi^{1}_{n}\|^{p}_{p}+o(1)\qquad\text{as}\,n\to\infty,

(2.33)

which yields that

\nabla I_{\lambda}(\psi^{1}_{n})=\nabla J_{V,\lambda}(u_{n})-\nabla J_{V,\lambda}(u)+o(1)\qquad\text{in}\,H^{-1}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})\,\text{as}\,n\to\infty,

(2.34)

since
\psi^{1}_{n}\rightharpoonup 0
weakly in
H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
,

B(u_{n})-B(u)=B(u_{n}-u)+o(1)\qquad\text{as}\,n\to\infty

due to
[
39

and the mapping

v\in H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})\mapsto\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}V\psi v\in{\mathbb{R}}

is in
H^{-1}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
, since
V\in L^{\bar{q}}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
for some
\bar{q}\geq\frac{3}{2}
.

If
\psi^{1}_{n}\to 0
strongly in
H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
we are done. Otherwise we have

I_{\lambda}(\psi^{1}_{n})+\frac{1}{4}B(\psi^{1}_{n})\geq\kappa>0

(2.35)

for some
\kappa>0
and for

large enough. In order to prove (
2.35

2.34

I_{\lambda}(\psi^{1}_{n})+\frac{1}{4}B(\psi^{1}_{n})=\frac{p-2}{2p}\left(\|\psi^{1}_{n}\|^{2}_{2}+\lambda\|\psi^{1}_{n}\|^{2}_{2}+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}V(\psi^{1}_{n})^{2}dx+B(\psi^{1}_{n})\right)+o(1)=\frac{p-2}{2p}\|\psi^{1}_{n}\|^{p}_{p}+o(1)\qquad\text{as}\,n\to\infty

(2.36)

As a consequence if we assume that, up to a subsequence,

I_{\lambda}(\psi^{1}_{n})+\frac{1}{4}B(\psi^{1}_{n})\to 0\qquad\text{as}\,n\to\infty,

then (
2.36
) gives

0\leq(1-S^{-1}\|V^{-}\|_{\frac{3}{2}})\|\psi^{1}_{n}\|^{2}_{2}+\lambda\|\psi^{1}_{n}\|^{2}_{2}\leq\|\psi^{1}_{n}\|^{2}_{2}+\lambda\|\psi^{1}_{n}\|^{2}_{2}+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}V(\psi^{1}_{n})^{2}dx+B(\psi^{1}_{n})\to 0\qquad\text{as}\,n\to\infty.

Here we have used the lower bound

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}V(\psi^{1}_{n})^{2}dx\geq-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}V^{-}(\psi^{1}_{n})^{2}dx\geq-S^{-1}\|V^{-}\|_{\frac{3}{2}}\|\psi^{1}_{n}\|^{2}_{2}.

Now we decompose
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
in the union of countably many closed unit cubes

whose interiors are disjoint and we prove that there exists

such that

In fact, using (
2.36

, we can see that

\displaystyle\frac{2p}{p-2}\left(I_{\lambda}(\psi^{1}_{n})+\frac{1}{4}B(\psi^{1}_{n})\right)+o(1)

\displaystyle=\|\psi^{1}_{n}\|^{p}_{p}=\sum_{i}\|\psi^{1}_{n}\|^{p}_{L^{p}(Q-i)}\leq d_{n}^{p-2}\sum_{i}\|\psi^{1}_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{p}(Q-i)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{p}d_{n}^{p-2}\sum_{i}\left(\|\psi^{1}_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(Q_{i})}+\lambda\|\nabla\psi^{1}_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(Q_{i})}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{C}_{p}d_{n}^{p-2}\frac{2p}{p-2}\left(I_{\lambda}(\psi^{1}_{n})+\frac{1}{4}B(\psi^{1}_{n})\right),

which shows that

0<\kappa\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\left(I_{\lambda}(\psi^{1}_{n})+\frac{1}{4}B(\psi^{1}_{n})\right)\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}(1-\tilde{C}_{p}d_{n}^{p-2})\left(I_{\lambda}(\psi^{1}_{n})+\frac{1}{4}B(\psi^{1}_{n})\right)\leq 0

if

, so that (

) is true.

Taking
y^{1}_{n}
to be the centre of the hypercube

in

As a consequence, the sequence

.

u_{n}=u+w^{1}(\cdotp-y^{1}_{n})+\psi^{2}_{n}(\cdotp-y^{1}_{n}),\qquad\psi^{2}_{n}\rightharpoonup 0\,\text{as}\,n\to\infty\,\text{weakly}\,\text{in}\,H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{3}).

Then by induction for

, we find sequences

, sequences

\displaystyle\|\nabla\psi^{j}_{n}\|^{2}_{2}

\displaystyle=\|\nabla\psi^{j-1}_{n}\|^{2}_{2}-\|\nabla w^{j}\|^{2}_{2}+o(1)=\|\nabla u_{n}\|^{2}_{2}-\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}\|\nabla w^{i}\|^{2}_{2}+o(1)

\displaystyle\|\psi^{j}_{n}\|^{2}_{2}

\displaystyle=\|\psi^{j-1}_{n}\|^{2}_{2}-\|w^{j}\|^{2}_{2}+o(1)=\|u_{n}\|^{2}_{2}-\|u\|^{2}_{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}\|w^{i}\|^{2}_{2}+o(1)+o(1)

\displaystyle=\|\psi^{j-1}_{n}\|^{p}_{p}-\|w^{j}\|^{p}_{p}+o(1)=\|u_{n}\|^{p}_{p}-\|u\|^{p}_{p}-\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}\|w^{i}\|^{p}_{p}+o(1)+o(1)

\displaystyle B(\psi^{j}_{n})

\displaystyle=B(\psi^{j-1}_{n})-B(w^{j})+o(1)=B(u_{n})-B(u)-\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}B(w^{j})+o(1)

\displaystyle 0<c_{a}<I_{\lambda}(w)+\frac{1}{4}B(w)=\frac{p-2}{2p}\left(\|\nabla w\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|w\|^{2}_{2}+B(w)\right)

\displaystyle\leq c(p)\left(\|\nabla w\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|w\|^{2}_{2}+\|\nabla w\|_{2}\|w\|^{3}_{2}\right)\leq 2c(p)\left(\|\nabla w\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|w\|^{2}_{2}+\|w\|^{2}_{H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})}\|w\|^{2}_{2}\right)

again, we get

\displaystyle J_{V}(v)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{3p-10}{6(p-2)}\|\nabla v\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{(p-4)^{+}}{2(p-2)}S^{-1}\|V\|_{\frac{3}{2}}\|\nabla v\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{2}{3(p-2)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}V(x)v\nabla v\cdot xdx

\displaystyle\geq\Big(\frac{3p-10}{6(p-2)}-\frac{(p-4)^{+}}{2(p-2)}S^{-1}\|V\|_{\frac{3}{2}}-\frac{2}{3(p-2)}S^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|\tilde{W}\|_{3}\Big)\|\nabla u\|_{2}^{2}>0.

This proves the lemma.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

By Lemma
4.1
and
4.2
, we know there is
a_{*}>0
such that for any
a\in(0,a_{*})
, there exists a bounded
(PS)
sequence
\{u_{n}\}\subset\mathcal{S}_{a}
for
J_{V}
at level
m_{V,a}
and the associated Lagrange multipliers
\lambda_{n}\rightarrow\tilde{\lambda}>0
. Then there exists
\tilde{u}\in H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
such that, along a subsequence,
u_{n}\rightharpoonup\tilde{u}~\text{in}~H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

To finish the proof of Theorem
1.4
, we need to prove as
n\rightarrow\infty
,

u_{n}\rightarrow\tilde{u}\quad\text{strongly\quad in}\quad H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{3}).

Since
\{u_{n}\}\subset\mathcal{S}_{a}
is a
(PS)
sequence of
J_{V}
, then for any
\psi\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, it holds

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\nabla u_{n}\nabla\psi dx+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}V(x)u_{n}\psi dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(|x|^{-1}*u_{n}^{2})u_{n}\psi dx

\displaystyle=-\lambda_{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}u_{n}\psi dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|u_{n}|^{p-2}u_{n}\psi dx+o(1)\|\psi\|

and using the fact
\lambda_{n}\rightarrow\tilde{\lambda}
, one gets

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\nabla u_{n}\nabla\psi dx+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}V(x)u_{n}\psi dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(|x|^{-1}*u_{n}^{2})u_{n}\psi dx

\displaystyle=-\tilde{\lambda}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}u_{n}\psi dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|u_{n}|^{p-2}u_{n}\psi dx+o(1)\|\psi\|.

The above results show that
\{u_{n}\}
is actually a
(PS)
sequence for
J_{V,\tilde{\lambda}}
at level
m_{V,a}+\frac{\tilde{\lambda}}{2}a^{2}
. Since
(V_{4})
holds, we have

where

If

m_{V,a}=J_{V}(u)+\sum_{j=1}^{k}J_{\infty}(w^{j}).

(4.21)
