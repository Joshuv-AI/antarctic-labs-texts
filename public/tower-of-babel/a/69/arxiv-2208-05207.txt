On the irreducible spin representations of symmetric and alternating groups which remain irreducible in characteristic 3 3
Matthew Fayers, Lucia Morotti

Abstract
. For any finite group G G and any prime p p one can ask which ordinary irreducible representations remain irreducible in characteristic p p , or more generally, which representations remain homogeneous in characteristic p p . In this paper we address this question when G G is a proper double cover of the symmetric or alternating group. We obtain a classification when p = 3 p=3 except in the case of a certain family of partitions relating to spin RoCK blocks. Our techniques involve induction and restriction, degree calculations, decomposing projective characters and recent results of Kleshchev and Livesey on spin RoCK blocks.



1.
Introduction

In the representation theory of finite groups it is interesting and useful to classify the ordinary irreducible representations of a group
G
that remain irreducible modulo a prime
p
. This classification has been carried out for various families of groups, including the symmetric groups
[
JM2
,
L
,
F1
,
F2
]
, the alternating groups
[
F3
,
F4
]
and the finite general linear and special linear groups
[
JM1
,
KT
]
in non-defining characteristic, as well as the double covers of the symmetric and alternating groups when
p=2

[
F5
,
F6
]
. In this paper we address the same problem for the double covers in odd characteristic, obtaining an almost-complete classification in the case
p=3
.

In fact in this setting it is more natural to classify representations which remain
homogeneous
(that is, with all composition factors isomorphic) modulo
p
. To state our main theorem, let
\hat{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
denote a proper double cover of the symmetric group
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, and
\hat{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}
the corresponding double cover of the alternating group
\mathfrak{A}_{n}
. Irreducible representations of these groups on which the central involution
z
acts trivially correspond to representations of the quotient groups
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
and
\mathfrak{A}_{n}
, so we concentrate on representations on which
z
acts non-trivially, also called
spin
representations. The spin representations of
\mathbb{C}\hat{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
are modules for the twisted group algebra
\mathcal{T}_{n}
, which is usually studied as a superalgebra, with irreducible supermodules labelled by strict partitions of
n
. We let
{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}_{0}
be the irreducible supermodule labelled by a strict partition
\lambda
, and let
{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}_{p}
be a reduction modulo
p
of
{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}_{0}
. Our main result is the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

If
\lambda
is a strict partition and
{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}_{3}
is homogeneous as a supermodule then one of the following holds:

(1)

\lambda=\nu+3\alpha
where
\nu
is a
3
-bar core and
\alpha
is a partition with
l(\alpha)\leqslant l(\nu)
;

(2)

\lambda=(3a)
with
a\geqslant 2
;

(3)

\lambda=\nu\sqcup(3)
with
\nu
a
3
-bar core;

(4)

\lambda
is one of the partitions
(2,1)
,
(3,2,1)
,
(4,3,2)
,
(4,3,2,1)
,
(5,3,2,1)
,
(5,4,3,1)
,
(5,4,3,2)
,
(5,4,3,2,1)
,
(7,4,3,2,1)
,
(8,5,3,2,1)
.

Furthermore in cases (
2
)–(
4
)
{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}_{3}
is homogeneous.

This theorem gives a classification of supermodules which are homogeneous modulo
3
, except for partitions appearing in case (
1
). For this case we make a precise conjecture (
3.7
below) saying exactly which
{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}
are homogeneous, and we hope to resolve this in future work.

A classification of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
-supermodules which reduce homogeneously directly leads to a classification of spin representations for
\hat{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
and
\hat{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}
which remain irreducible in characteristic
p
(see
3.2
). From our almost-complete classification for
p=3
in
1.1
, we can deduce the following. Recall that a strict partition
\lambda
is
even
if it has an even number of positive even parts, and
odd
otherwise. For each even strict partition
\lambda
of
n
we have an irreducible
\mathbb{C}\hat{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
-module
{\mathrm{S}}^{\lambda}
, and a pair of irreducible
\mathbb{C}\hat{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}
-modules
{\mathrm{T}}^{\lambda,\pm}
. For each odd strict partition
\lambda
of
n
, we have a pair of irreducible
\mathbb{C}\hat{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
-modules
{\mathrm{S}}^{\lambda,\pm}
and an irreducible
\mathbb{C}\hat{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}
-module
{\mathrm{T}}^{\lambda}
. All irreducible spin modules for
\mathbb{C}\hat{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
and
\mathbb{C}\hat{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}
arise in this way.

Theorem 1.2
.

Suppose
M
is an irreducible spin module for
\mathbb{C}\hat{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
or
\mathbb{C}\hat{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}
. If the reduction of
M
modulo
3
is irreducible, then one of the following holds:

(1)

M
is one of the modules
{\mathrm{S}}^{\lambda(,\pm)}
or
{\mathrm{T}}^{\lambda(,\pm)}
, where
\nu
is a
3
-bar core and
\alpha
is a partition with
l(\alpha)\leqslant l(\nu)
and
\lambda=\nu+3\alpha
;

(2)

M={\mathrm{S}}^{(6a),\pm}
or
M={\mathrm{T}}^{(6a-3),\pm}
for
a\geqslant 1
;

(3)

M={\mathrm{S}}^{\nu\sqcup(3),\pm}
, where
\nu
is an odd
3
-bar core;

(4)

M={\mathrm{T}}^{\nu\sqcup(3),\pm}
, where
\nu
is an even
3
-bar core;

(5)

M
is one of
{\mathrm{S}}^{(2,1),\pm}
,
{\mathrm{T}}^{(2,1)}
,
{\mathrm{S}}^{(3,2,1),\pm}
,
{\mathrm{T}}^{(4,3,2),\pm}
,
{\mathrm{T}}^{(4,3,2,1),\pm}
,
{\mathrm{S}}^{(5,3,2,1),\pm}
,
{\mathrm{S}}^{(5,4,3,1),\pm}
,
{\mathrm{T}}^{(5,4,3,2),\pm}
,
{\mathrm{T}}^{(5,4,3,2,1),\pm}
,
{\mathrm{T}}^{(7,4,3,2,1),\pm}
,
{\mathrm{T}}^{(8,5,3,2,1),\pm}
.

Furthermore in cases (
2
–
5
)
M
is irreducible modulo
3
.

Our approach to the representation theory of
\hat{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
follows the Brundan–Kleshchev approach to double covers via supermodules, using in particular their branching rules for irreducible supermodules and their analogue of James’s regularisation theorem. We also make use of the very recent study by Kleshchev and Livesey of RoCK blocks for double covers of symmetric groups; this leads us to an analysis of modules for a certain wreath product algebra, for which we emulate results of Chuang and Tan. Finally, we make extensive use of known decomposition numbers, which have been worked out by Maas
[
Ma
]
.

It is natural to ask what the corresponding results are for
p\geqslant 5
, and it seems likely that many of our techniques will help in finding a general classification. With this in mind, we prove our results for arbitrary odd
p
as far as possible until
Section

5
(especially results on induction and restriction in
Section

4
). However, in
Sections

6
and
7
we will restrict attention to characteristic
3
. The main difficulty in generalising
1.1
seems to be in generalising the list of “exceptional” partitions appearing in (
4
), which appears to grow as
p
gets larger. But with limited data available for small values of
n
, it is hard to make a conjecture.

2.
Background

In this section we describe the background results we shall need. Throughout this paper we fix an odd prime
p
, and we will state results for general
p
as far as is convenient. Later in the paper we will specialise to the case
p=3
.

2.1.
Partitions

A
composition
of
n
is a sequence
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots)
of non-negative integers such that the sum
|\lambda|=\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}+\cdots
equals
n
. A composition
\lambda
is called a
partition
if it is weakly decreasing. When writing partitions, we usually group together equal parts with a superscript and omit trailing zeroes. The unique partition of
0
is written as
\varnothing
. The
length
of a partition
\lambda
, denoted
l(\lambda)
, is the number of non-zero parts of
\lambda
. We write
{\mathcal{P}}
for the set of all partitions, and
{\mathcal{P}}(n)
for the set of partitions of
n
.

We adopt natural conventions for adding together compositions and multiplying by scalars. Suppose
\lambda,\mu
are compositions and
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Then we write
\lambda+m\mu
for the composition
(\lambda_{1}+m\mu_{1},\lambda_{2}+m\mu_{2},\dots)
. We also define
\lambda\sqcup\mu
to be the partition whose parts are the combined parts of
\lambda
and
\mu
, written in decreasing order.

Often we will consider partitions some of whose parts form an arithmetic progression with common difference
3
; so if
x\geqslant y
with
x\equiv y\text{ }(\text{\rm mod }3)\,
, then we write
x\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\dots.}}y
to mean the sequence
x,x-3,\dots,y
. For example, we may write
(18,\ 17\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\dots.}}5,\ 1)
for the partition
(18,17,14,11,8,5,1)
.

The
dominance order

\trianglelefteqslant
is a partial order on partitions defined by setting
\lambda\trianglelefteqslant\mu
if
|\lambda|=|\mu|
and
\lambda_{1}+\dots+\lambda_{r}\leqslant\mu_{1}+\dots+\mu_{r}
for all
r
.

A partition
\lambda
is called
strict
if
\lambda_{r}>\lambda_{r+1}
for all
1\leqslant r<l(\lambda)
, or
p
-strict
if for all
r
either
\lambda_{r}>\lambda_{r+1}
or
\lambda_{r}\equiv 0\text{ }(\text{\rm mod }p)\,
. A
p
-strict partition is called
restricted
if for all
r
either
\lambda_{r}-\lambda_{r+1}<p
or
\lambda_{r}-\lambda_{r+1}=p
and
\lambda_{r}\not\equiv 0\text{ }(\text{\rm mod }p)\,
. We write
{\mathcal{P}_{0}}
for the set of all strict partitions, and
{\mathcal{P}_{0}}(n)
for the set of strict partitions of
n
. We write
\mathcal{RP}_{p}
for the set of all restricted
p
-strict partitions, and
\mathcal{RP}_{p}(n)
for the set of restricted
p
-strict partitions of
n
. We say that a strict partition is
even
is it has an even number of positive even parts, and
odd
otherwise.

For any partition
\lambda
, we write
l_{p}(\lambda)
for the number of positive parts of
\lambda
divisible by
p
.

2.4.
p
-bar cores

Next we recall the notion of
p
-bar cores. Suppose
\lambda
is a
p
-strict partition.
Removing a
p
-bar
from
\lambda
means either

\diamond

replacing
\lambda_{r}
with
\lambda_{r}-p
and then re-ordering, for some
r
for which
\lambda_{r}\geqslant p
and either
p\mid\lambda_{r}
or
\lambda_{r}-p
is not a part of
\lambda
, or

\diamond

deleting two parts whose sum is
p
.

The
p
-bar core of
\lambda
is the strict partition obtained by repeatedly removing
p
-bars until none remain. The
p
-bar-weight of
\lambda
is the number of
p
-bars removed to reach the
p
-bar core. In general, we say that a strict partition is a
p
-bar core if it equals its own
p
-bar core.

For example, the
3
-bar cores are the partitions
(3l-1\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\dots.}}2)
for
l\geqslant 0
and
(3l-2\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\dots.}}1)
for
l\geqslant 1
.

2.5.
The double cover of the symmetric group

Now we set out the background on representation theory that we need. We work over a sufficiently large field
\mathbb{F}
of characteristic not equal to
2
. Let
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
denote the symmetric group on
\{1,\dots,n\}
, and
\hat{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
the double cover of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
specified in
[
K
]
. The group algebra
\mathbb{F}\hat{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
decomposes as a direct sum of
\mathbb{F}\mathfrak{S}_{n}
and a twisted group algebra
\mathcal{T}_{n}
. For
n\geqslant 4
the group
\hat{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
is a Schur cover of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, and representations of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
are called
spin
representations of
\hat{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
.

In practice it is easier to treat
\mathcal{T}_{n}
as a superalgebra (that is, a
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
-graded algebra), and consider its supermodules. Much of the representation theory of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
as an algebra can then be recovered by forgetting the
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
-grading. We refer to
[
K
, Chapter 12]
for the essentials on superalgebras and supermodules. In particular, we note that irreducible supermodules come in two types: an irreducible supermodule
D
is of
type M
if it is irreducible as a module, and of
type Q
if it is reducible as a module (in which case it is a direct sum of two irreducible modules). In any block of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
all the irreducible modules have the same type, and we say that the block is of type M or type Q accordingly.

Given modules or supermodules
M,N
, we write
M\sim N
to mean that
M
and
N
have the same composition factors. We may extend this notation linearly, writing
M\sim N+N^{\prime}
or
M\sim aN
for
a\geqslant 0
.

The classification of irreducible supermodules in characteristic
0
can be derived from the work of Schur. For each
\lambda\in{\mathcal{P}_{0}}(n)
there is an irreducible supermodule
{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}_{0}
, of type M if
\lambda
is even, and of type Q if
\lambda
is odd. These supermodules give all the irreducible
\mathcal{T}_{n}
-supermodules up to isomorphism when
\mathbb{F}
has characteristic
0
.

The classification of irreducible supermodules in odd characteristic
p
is much more recent, and is due to Brundan and Kleshchev
[
BK1
,
BK2
]
. (The constructions in the two papers are different but they have been shown to be equivalent in
[
KS
]
.) For each
\lambda\in\mathcal{RP}_{p}(n)
there is an irreducible supermodule
{\mathbf{D}}^{\lambda}
, of type M if
\lambda
is
p
-even, or of type Q if
\lambda
is
p
-odd. We write
{\mathbf{P}}^{\lambda}
for the projective cover of
{\mathbf{D}}^{\lambda}
.

For
p>0
, we will write
{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}_{p}
(or simply
{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}
if
p
is understood) for a
p
-modular reduction of
{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}_{0}
. Though
{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}
is not well-defined as a supermodule, its composition factors are. If
P
is a projective supermodule for
\hat{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
in characteristic
p
, then
P
lifts to a
\mathbb{C}\hat{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
-module
P_{0}
; we write
[P:{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}]
for the composition multiplicity of
{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}_{0}
in
P_{0}
.

The
decomposition number problem
asks for the composition multiplicities
d_{\lambda\mu}=[{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}:{\mathbf{D}}^{\mu}]
for
\lambda\in{\mathcal{P}_{0}}(n)
and
\mu\in\mathcal{RP}_{p}(n)
. By Brauer reciprocity
d_{\lambda\mu}
can also be recovered from the multiplicity
[{\mathbf{P}}^{\mu}:{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}]
; specifically,
d_{\lambda\mu}=2^{x-y}[{\mathbf{P}}^{\mu}:{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}]
, where
x=1
if
\lambda
is odd and
0
otherwise, while
y=1
if
\mu
is
p
-odd and
0
otherwise.

We will also need the classification of the blocks of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
, which was proved by Humphreys
[
H
]
. In fact in this paper we consider superblocks; since these are almost always the same as blocks, we will abuse notation by using the term “block” to mean “superblock”. Given a strict partition
\lambda
and a restricted
p
-strict partition
\mu
, the supermodules
{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}
and
{\mathbf{D}}^{\mu}
lie in the same block of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
if and only if
\lambda
and
\mu
have the same
p
-bar core. This automatically means that
\lambda
and
\mu
have the same
p
-bar-weight, and it customary to label a block by its bar core and bar-weight, meaning the common
p
-bar core and
p
-bar-weight of the partitions labelling modules in the block.

An alternative description of the blocks follows from a combinatorial lemma of Morris and Yaseen
[
MY
, Theorem 5]
:
{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}
and

2.7.
Jucys–Murphy elements and weight spaces

Later we shall need to make use of weight spaces; we briefly explain the background, following
[
K
, Section 22]
.

There are elements
y_{1},\dots,y_{n}\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
(defined originally by Sergeev) which are analogues of the Jucys–Murphy elements in
\mathbb{F}\mathfrak{S}_{n}
; in particular, the squares
y_{1}^{2},\dots,y_{n}^{2}
generate a large commutative subalgebra of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
. Given a
\mathcal{T}_{n}
-supermodule
M
and a tuple
i=(i_{1},\dots,i_{n})\in I^{n}
, we define the
i
-weight space

M[i]=\left\{\left.m\in M\ \right|\ \smash{(y_{r}^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}i_{r}(i_{r}+1))^{N}m=0\text{ for $N\gg 0$ and $1\leqslant r\leqslant n$}}\right\}.

Then
M
is the direct sum of the weight spaces
M[i]
. We say that
i
is a
weight
of
M
if
M[i]\neq 0
.

In the case where
M={\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}
for
\lambda\in{\mathcal{P}_{0}}(n)
we can describe the weights explicitly. To explain this, we need to introduce
standard shifted tableaux
. A standard shifted
\lambda
-tableau is a bijection
t
from the Young diagram of
\lambda
to the set
\{1,\dots,n\}
such that

t(r,c)<t(r,c+1),\qquad t(r,c+1)<t(r+1,c)

for all admissible
r,c
.

Given a shifted tableau
t
, we define
i_{k}
to be the residue of the node
t^{-1}(k)
for each
k
, and then define the tuple
i^{t}=(i_{1},\dots,i_{n})\in I^{n}
. Now the following result follows from the branching rules for the modules
{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}
.

Proposition 2.4
.

Suppose
\lambda\in{\mathcal{P}_{0}}(n)
and
t
is a standard shifted
\lambda
-tableau. Then
i^{t}
is a weight of
{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}
.

We use induction on
n
, with the case
n=0
being trivial. Assuming
n\geqslant 1
, let
(r,c)
be the node for which
t(r,c)=n
. Let
\lambda^{-}
be the partition obtained from
\lambda
by removing the node
(r,c)
. Then the definition of a standard shifted tableau means that
\lambda^{-}\in{\mathcal{P}_{0}}(n-1)
and that the tableau
t^{-}
obtained by restricting
t
to
\lambda^{-}
is a standard shifted
\lambda^{-}
-tableau. Let
i
be the residue of
(r,c)
. Then the branching rule means that
{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda^{-}}
appears in
\mathrm{e}_{i}{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}
, so by
[
K
, Lemma 22.3.14]
if
(i_{1},\dots,i_{n-1})
is a weight of
{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda^{-}}
then
(i_{1},\dots,i_{n-1},i)
is a weight of
{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}
. By induction

is a weight of
{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda^{-}}
, and so
i^{t}
is a weight of
{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}
.
∎

3.
Irreducible and homogeneous modules

Our motivation in this paper is to classify the irreducible representations of
\hat{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
in characteristic
0
which remain irreducible when reduced modulo
p
. In fact the answer to this question depends on whether we consider modules or supermodules, but we address both questions (as well as the corresponding question for the double cover of the alternating group) by considering the more general question of homogeneous modules.

We say that a strict partition
\lambda
is
homogeneous
if the composition factors of
{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}
(as a supermodule) are all isomorphic. The following
Section
due to Brundan and Kleshchev is a very useful tool for classifying homogeneous partitions. Recall that
\lambda^{\mathrm{reg}}
denotes the regularisation of
\lambda
, and
l_{p}(\lambda)
the number of positive parts of
\lambda
divisible by
p
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose
\lambda\in{\mathcal{P}_{0}}
. Then

\displaystyle[{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}:{\mathbf{D}}^{\lambda^{\mathrm{reg}}}]

\displaystyle=2^{\frac{l_{p}(\lambda)+x-y}{2}},

\displaystyle[{\mathbf{P}}^{\lambda^{\mathrm{reg}}}:{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}]

\displaystyle=2^{\frac{l_{p}(\lambda)+y-x}{2}},

where
x
is
0
if
\lambda
is even and
1
if
\lambda
is odd, and
y
is
0
if
\lambda
is
p
-even and
1
if
\lambda
is
p
-odd. All other composition factors of
{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}
are of the form
{\mathbf{D}}^{\mu}
with
\mu\vartriangleleft\lambda^{\mathrm{reg}}
.

The corresponding result for the algebraic supergroup
Q(n)
is proved in
[
BK4
, Theorem 1.2]
, and the result for
\mathcal{T}_{n}
follows by
[
BK2
, Theorem 10.8]
.
∎

Suppose
\lambda
is a strict partition. If
\lambda
is even, then
{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}_{0}
is irreducible as a module, and we write this module as
{\mathrm{S}}^{\lambda}_{0}
. If
\lambda
is odd, then as a module
{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}_{0}
splits as the direct sum of two non-isomorphic irreducible modules which we write as
{\mathrm{S}}^{\lambda,+}_{0}
and
{\mathrm{S}}^{\lambda,-}_{0}
. These modules give all the irreducible spin representations of
\hat{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
in characteristic
0
. We also consider the double cover
\hat{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}
of the alternating group: if
\lambda
is even, then
{\mathrm{S}}^{\lambda}_{0}{\downarrow}_{\hat{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}}
splits as the direct sum of two non-isomorphic irreducible modules which we write as
{\mathrm{T}}^{\lambda,+}_{0}
and
{\mathrm{T}}^{\lambda,-}_{0}
, while if
\lambda
is odd, then
{\mathrm{S}}^{\lambda,+}_{0}{\downarrow}_{\hat{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}}\cong{\mathrm{S}}^{\lambda,-}_{0}{\downarrow}_{\hat{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}}
is an irreducible module which we write as
{\mathrm{T}}^{\lambda}_{0}
. These modules give all the irreducible spin representations of
\hat{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}
in characteristic
0
. We write
{\mathrm{S}}^{\lambda}_{p}
for a
p
-modular reduction of
{\mathrm{S}}^{\lambda}_{0}
, and similarly for the other modules just defined (though we may simply write
{\mathrm{S}}^{\lambda}
if
p
is understood).

A classification of homogeneous partitions also directly allows us to classify which modules
S^{\lambda(,\pm)}_{p}
and
T^{\lambda(,\pm)}_{p}
are
almost homogeneous
(by which we mean that all composition factors of
S_{p}^{\lambda(,\pm)}
or
T_{p}^{\lambda(,\pm)}
are indexed by the same partition), as
S_{p}^{\lambda(,\pm)}
has a composition factor indexed by
\mu
if and only if
{\mathbf{S}}_{p}^{\lambda}
has a composition factor
{\mathbf{D}}_{p}^{\mu}
and similarly for
T_{p}^{\lambda(,\pm)}
.

3.1
tells us that if
\lambda
is homogeneous then all the composition factors of
{\mathbf{S}}_{p}^{\lambda}
are isomorphic to
{\mathbf{D}}^{\lambda^{\mathrm{reg}}}
, and gives the number of composition factors. So a classification of homogeneous partitions also allows us to answer the question of irreducibility in different settings, as follows.

Proposition 3.2
.

Suppose
\lambda\in{\mathcal{P}_{0}}
.

(1)

(a)

If
\lambda
is even, then
{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}_{p}
is an irreducible supermodule if and only if
\lambda
is homogeneous and
l_{p}(\lambda)\leqslant 1
.

(b)

If
\lambda
is odd, then
{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}_{p}
is an irreducible supermodule if and only if
\lambda
is homogeneous and
l_{p}(\lambda)=0
.

(2)

(a)

If
\lambda
is even, then
{\mathrm{S}}^{\lambda}_{p}
is irreducible if and only if
\lambda
is homogeneous and
l_{p}(\lambda)=0
.

(b)

If
\lambda
is odd, then
{\mathrm{S}}^{\lambda,\pm}_{p}
is irreducible if and only if
\lambda
is homogeneous and
l_{p}(\lambda)\leqslant 1
.

(3)

(a)

If
\lambda
is even, then
{\mathrm{T}}^{\lambda,\pm}_{p}
is irreducible if and only if
\lambda
is homogeneous and

.

(b)

If
\lambda

{\mathrm{T}}^{\lambda}_{p}

\lambda
is homogeneous and

.

In this proof, for any supermodule

for the underlying module.

(1)

This follows immediately from

(2)

(a)

In this case

(b)

First note that

If

can be irreducible only in the first case, with

(3)

We begin by observing that if

has composition length

is irreducible and therefore invariant under tensoring with the sign module, so its restriction to

is

-odd, then
\underline{{\mathbf{D}}^{\mu}}
is a direct sum of two non-isomorphic modules; these modules are images of each other under tensoring with the sign module, so remain irreducible on restriction to

.

(a)

As in

(

{\mathrm{T}}^{\lambda,+}
is irreducible if and only if

In this case

the number of addable nodes of
\lambda
in
\mathcal{L}_{l}
. Define
\operatorname{srem}_{l}(\lambda)
and
\operatorname{rem}_{l}(\lambda)
similarly, for strictly-removable nodes. Define all these numbers to be
0
when
l<0
.

We begin by considering ladders comprising nodes of residue
\frac{1}{2}(p-1)
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Suppose
\lambda
is a
p
-strict partition, and
l\in\mathbb{N}
with
l\equiv\frac{1}{2}(p-1)\ (\operatorname{mod}\,p-1)
. Then

\operatorname{rem}_{l-p+1}(\lambda)-\operatorname{add}_{l}(\lambda)=\begin{cases}\operatorname{lad}_{l}(\lambda)-\operatorname{lad}_{l-1}(\lambda)-\operatorname{lad}_{l-p+2}(\lambda)+\operatorname{lad}_{l-p+1}(\lambda)&\text{if }p\geqslant 5\\
\operatorname{lad}_{l}(\lambda)-\operatorname{lad}_{l-1}(\lambda)+\operatorname{lad}_{l-2}(\lambda)&\text{if }p=3.\end{cases}

This is proved in a similar way to
[
F6
, Lemma 4.15]
. For each node
(r,c)\in\mathcal{L}_{l}
, we can consider the set of (up to) four nodes

\{(r-1,c),(r-1,c+1),(r,c-1),(r,c)\}\cap\mathbb{N}^{2}.

By examining the possible intersections of this set with
[\lambda]
, we can show that the lemma holds when restricted to just these nodes. Then by summing over all nodes
(r,c)\in\mathcal{L}_{l}
, we obtain the result.
∎

When
\lambda
is a strict partition and
\mathcal{L}_{l}
consists of nodes of non-zero residue, it is easy to see that
\operatorname{add}_{l}(\lambda)=\operatorname{sadd}_{l}(\lambda)
and
\operatorname{rem}_{l-p+1}(\lambda)=\operatorname{srem}_{l-p+1}(\lambda)
. As a consequence, we obtain

\operatorname{sadd}_{l}(\lambda)-\operatorname{srem}_{l-p+1}(\lambda)=\operatorname{add}_{l}(\lambda^{\mathrm{reg}})-\operatorname{rem}_{l-p+1}(\lambda^{\mathrm{reg}})

for any strict partition
\lambda
when
l\equiv\frac{1}{2}(p-1)\ (\operatorname{mod}\,p-1)
, because
\operatorname{lad}_{k}(\lambda^{\mathrm{reg}})=\operatorname{lad}_{k}(\lambda)
for every
k
. Now we can obtain a precise criterion for when
\hat{\epsilon}_{i}(\lambda)>\epsilon_{i}(\lambda^{\mathrm{reg}})
in the case
i=\frac{1}{2}(p-1)
. This is analogous to
[
F4
, Proposition 4.9]
.

Proposition 4.5
.

Suppose
\lambda
is a strict partition and that
i=\frac{1}{2}(p-1)
. Then
\hat{\epsilon}_{i}(\lambda)>\epsilon_{i}(\lambda^{\mathrm{reg}})
if and only if
\lambda
has a strictly-removable
i
-node and a strictly-addable
i
-node in a longer ladder.

The proof is essentially the same as for
[
F4
, Proposition 4.9]
. The crucial point is that when we read the addable and removable
i
-nodes of a restricted
p
-strict partition from left to right, the nodes in longer ladders come first, and within each ladder the removable nodes come before the addable nodes.
∎

For residues
i\neq\frac{1}{2}(p-1)
, it is harder to give a necessary and sufficient criterion for
\hat{\epsilon}_{i}(\lambda)>\epsilon_{i}(\lambda^{\mathrm{reg}})
, but it suffices for our purposes to show that the condition in
4.5
is sufficient.

We consider the case
i=0
. Take
l\equiv 0\ (\operatorname{mod}\,p-1)
, and define
\operatorname{c}_{l}(\lambda)
to be the number of nodes
(r,c)\in\mathcal{L}_{l}
such that
c\equiv 0\ (\operatorname{mod}\,3)
,
r\geqslant 2
and
(\lambda_{r-1},\lambda_{r},\lambda_{r+1})=(c+1,c,c-1)
.

Lemma 4.6
.

Suppose
\lambda
is a
p
-strict partition and
l\equiv 0\ (\operatorname{mod}\,p-1)
. Then

\displaystyle\operatorname{rem}_{l-p+1}(\lambda)-\operatorname{add}_{l}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{lad}_{l}(\lambda)-2\operatorname{lad}_{l-1}(\lambda)-2\operatorname{lad}_{l-p+2}(\lambda)+\operatorname{lad}_{l-p+1}(\lambda)-\delta_{l0}.

Furthermore, if
\lambda
is strict, then

\displaystyle\operatorname{srem}_{l-p+1}(\lambda)-\operatorname{sadd}_{l}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{lad}_{l}(\lambda)-2\operatorname{lad}_{l-1}(\lambda)-2\operatorname{lad}_{l-p+2}(\lambda)+\operatorname{lad}_{l-p+1}(\lambda)-\operatorname{c}_{l}(\lambda)+\operatorname{c}_{l-p+1}(\lambda)-\delta_{l0}.

Suppose
(r,c)
is a node in
\mathcal{L}_{l}
, with
c\equiv 1\ (\operatorname{mod}\,p)
. Then (as in
[
F6
, Lemma 4.15]
) we can consider the set of nodes

X_{(r,c)}=\{(r-2,c+1),(r-1,c-1),(r-1,c),(r-1,c+1),(r,c-2),(r,c-1),(r,c),(r+1,c-2)\}\cap\mathbb{N}^{2}.

By considering the intersection of
X_{(r,c)}
with
[\lambda]
, we can show that if
p\geqslant 5
, then the two formulæ hold when restricted to
X_{(r,c)}
. Now summing over all
(r,c)
gives the result. (In particular, the calculation for the second formula is identical to that in
[
F6
, Lemma 4.15]
.)

For
p=3
, if we restrict attention to
X_{(r,c)}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\operatorname{rem}_{l-2}(\lambda)-\operatorname{add}_{l}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{lad}_{l}(\lambda)-2\operatorname{lad}_{l-1}(\lambda)+\operatorname{lad}_{l-2}(\lambda)-\delta_{l0}

and (if
\lambda
is strict)

\displaystyle\operatorname{srem}_{l-2}(\lambda)-\operatorname{sadd}_{l}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{lad}_{l}(\lambda)-2\operatorname{lad}_{l-1}(\lambda)+\operatorname{lad}_{l-2}(\lambda)-\operatorname{c}_{l}(\lambda)+\operatorname{c}_{l-2}(\lambda)-\delta_{l0}.

Now summing again gives the result, but here we have to take account of the fact that the sets
X_{(r,c)}
overlap, so that each node in
\mathcal{L}_{l-1}
must be counted twice.
∎

Now we can give a sufficient (but not necessary) condition for
\hat{\epsilon}_{0}(\lambda)>\epsilon_{0}(\lambda^{\mathrm{reg}})
.

Proposition 4.7
.

Suppose
\lambda
is a strict partition, and that
\lambda
has a strictly-removable
0
-node and a strictly-addable
0
-node in a longer ladder. Then
\hat{\epsilon}_{0}(\lambda)>\epsilon_{0}(\lambda^{\mathrm{reg}})
.

We follow the proof of
[
F6
, Proposition 4.17]
. As there, given an integer
u
we write
[u]
to denote a string of
u
plus signs if
u\geqslant 0
, or a string of
-u
minus signs if
u\leqslant 0
. Let
\mu=\lambda^{\mathrm{reg}}
. When we read the addable and removable
0
-nodes from left to right, the nodes in longer ladders come earlier, and within each ladder the removable nodes come before the addable nodes. This means that the
0
-signature of
\mu
is

\dots[\operatorname{add}_{3p-3}(\mu)][-\operatorname{rem}_{2p-2}(\mu)][\operatorname{add}_{2p-2}(\mu)][-\operatorname{rem}_{p-1}(\mu)][\operatorname{add}_{p-1}(\mu)][-\operatorname{rem}_{0}(\mu)][\operatorname{add}_{0}(\mu)].

So the reduced
0
-signature is the reduction of the sequence

\dots[\operatorname{add}_{3p-3}(\mu)-\operatorname{rem}_{2p-2}(\mu)][\operatorname{add}_{2p-2}(\mu)-\operatorname{rem}_{p-1}(\mu)][\operatorname{add}_{p-1}(\mu)-\operatorname{rem}_{0}(\mu)][\operatorname{add}_{0}(\mu)].

By
Lemma

4.6
this is the same as the sequence

\displaystyle\dots

\displaystyle[\operatorname{sadd}_{3p-3}(\lambda)-\operatorname{srem}_{2p-2}(\lambda)-\operatorname{c}_{3p-3}(\lambda)+\operatorname{c}_{2p-2}(\lambda)]

\displaystyle[\operatorname{sadd}_{2p-2}(\lambda)-\operatorname{srem}_{p-1}(\lambda)-\operatorname{c}_{2p-2}(\lambda)+\operatorname{c}_{p-1}(\lambda)]

\displaystyle[\operatorname{sadd}_{p-1}(\lambda)-\operatorname{srem}_{0}(\lambda)-\operatorname{c}_{p-1}(\lambda)+\operatorname{c}_{0}(\lambda)]

\displaystyle[\operatorname{sadd}_{0}(\lambda)-\operatorname{c}_{0}(\lambda)].

Now the proof proceeds exactly as in
[
F6
, Proposition 4.17]
.
∎

It remains to consider residues
i
such that
1\leqslant i\leqslant\frac{1}{2}(p-3)
. Take
l\in\mathbb{N}
with
l\not\equiv 0\ (\operatorname{mod}\,\frac{1}{2}(p-1))
. For a
p
-strict partition
\lambda
, define
\operatorname{d}_{l}(\lambda)
to be the number of nodes
(r,c)\in\mathcal{L}_{l}
such that
(\lambda_{r},\lambda_{r+1})=(c,c-1)
. Now we have the following lemma.

Lemma 4.8
.

Suppose
\lambda
is a
p
-strict partition, and
l\in\mathbb{N}
with
l\not\equiv 0\ (\operatorname{mod}\,\frac{1}{2}(p-1))
. Let
k<l
be maximal such that
k+l\equiv 0\ (\operatorname{mod}\,p-1)
. Then

\operatorname{rem}_{k}(\lambda)-\operatorname{add}_{l}(\lambda)=\begin{cases}\operatorname{lad}_{l}(\lambda)-\operatorname{lad}_{l-1}(\lambda)-\operatorname{lad}_{k+1}(\lambda)+\operatorname{lad}_{k}(\lambda)-\operatorname{d}_{k}(\lambda)+\operatorname{d}_{l-p+1}(\lambda)&\text{if }l\not\equiv 1\ (\operatorname{mod}\,p-1)\\
\operatorname{lad}_{l}(\lambda)-\operatorname{lad}_{l-1}(\lambda)+\operatorname{lad}_{k}(\lambda)-\operatorname{d}_{k}(\lambda)+\operatorname{d}_{l-p+1}(\lambda)&\text{if }l\equiv 1\ (\operatorname{mod}\,p-1).\end{cases}

This is proved in a similar way to
Lemmas

4.4
and
4.6
. For each node
(r,c)\in\mathcal{L}_{l}
, we let
b<c
be maximal such that
b+c\equiv 1\ (\operatorname{mod}\,p)
, and consider the set

X_{(r,c)}=\{(r,b),(r,b+1),(r,c-1),(r,c)\}\cap\mathbb{N}^{2}.

It is easy to check that the formula holds when restricted to the set
X_{(r,c)}
, and summing over
(r,c)
gives the result.
∎

As with
Lemma

4.4
, we can replace
\operatorname{rem}_{k}(\lambda)
and
\operatorname{add}_{l}(\lambda)
with
\operatorname{srem}_{k}(\lambda)
and
\operatorname{sadd}_{l}(\lambda)
when
\lambda
is strict, because
\mathcal{L}_{k}
and
\mathcal{L}_{l}
consist of nodes of non-zero residue. So we obtain

\operatorname{sadd}_{l}(\lambda)-\operatorname{srem}_{k}(\lambda)+\operatorname{d}_{l-p+1}(\lambda)-\operatorname{d}_{k}(\lambda)=\operatorname{add}_{l}(\lambda^{\mathrm{reg}})-\operatorname{rem}_{k}(\lambda^{\mathrm{reg}})+\operatorname{d}_{l-p+1}(\lambda^{\mathrm{reg}})-\operatorname{d}_{k}(\lambda^{\mathrm{reg}})

when
\lambda
is strict. To exploit this, we need to examine the function
\operatorname{d}_{l}(\lambda)
a little more closely.

Lemma 4.9
.

Suppose
\lambda
is a
p
-strict partition and
l\in\mathbb{N}
with
l\not\equiv 0\ (\operatorname{mod}\,\frac{1}{2}(p-1))
. Then
\operatorname{d}_{l}(\lambda^{\mathrm{reg}})\leqslant\operatorname{d}_{l}(\lambda)
.

Let
\mu=\lambda^{\mathrm{reg}}
. In fact it is possible to determine
\operatorname{d}_{l}(\mu)
quite explicitly. Suppose
\operatorname{d}_{l}(\mu)\geqslant 1
, and take a node
(r,c)\in\mathcal{L}_{l}
such that
(\mu_{r},\mu_{r+1})=(c,c-1)
. Note in particular that the node
(r,c+1)
belongs to
\mathcal{L}_{l+1}\setminus[\mu]
, while
(r+1,c-1)
belongs to
\mathcal{L}_{l+p-2}\cap[\mu]
. Now the node
(r+1,c+1-p)
(if there is such a node) belongs to
[\mu]
, which means that
(r,c+1)
is the leftmost node in
\mathcal{L}_{l+1}\setminus[\mu]
. Similarly,
(r+1,c-1)
is the rightmost node in
\mathcal{L}_{l+p-2}\cap[\mu]
. This in particular shows that
(r,c)
is unique, so that
\operatorname{d}_{l}(\mu)=1
. We also obtain
\operatorname{lad}_{l+p-2}(\mu)>\operatorname{lad}_{l+1}(\mu)
. Conversely, if
\operatorname{lad}_{l+p-2}(\mu)>\operatorname{lad}_{l+1}(\mu)
and if there is at least one node in ladder
l+1
which does not belong to
[\mu]
, then we obtain
\operatorname{d}_{l}(\mu)\geqslant 1
(by taking
(r,c)
such that
(r,c+1)
is the leftmost node of
\mathcal{L}_{l+1}
not in
\mu
).

So we have shown that
\operatorname{d}_{l}(\mu)
equals
1
if
\operatorname{lad}_{l+p-2}(\mu)>\operatorname{lad}_{l+1}(\mu)
and there is at least one node of
\mathcal{L}_{l+1}
not in
[\mu]
, and
0
otherwise. So to prove the
Lemma
it suffices to show that if
\operatorname{lad}_{l+p-2}(\lambda)>\operatorname{lad}_{l+1}(\lambda)
and there is at least one node of
\mathcal{L}_{l+1}
which is not in
\lambda
, then
\operatorname{d}_{l}(\lambda)\geqslant 1
. If there is a node
(s,d)\in\mathcal{L}_{l+p-2}
such that
s\geqslant 2
and
(s-1,c+2)\notin[\lambda]
, then we can deduce
\operatorname{d}_{l}(\lambda)\geqslant 1
by taking
(r,c)=(s-1,c+1)
, and we are done. So assume there is no such node
(s,d)
. Now the assumption that
\operatorname{lad}_{l+p-2}(\lambda)>\operatorname{lad}_{l+1}(\lambda)
means that
[\lambda]
contains the unique node of
\mathcal{L}_{l+p-2}
in row
1
, and that for each node
(s,d)\in\mathcal{L}_{l+p-2}
with
s\geqslant 2
, the node
(s,d)
lies in
[\lambda]
if and only if
(s-1,c+2)
does. But now we find that because
[\lambda]
contains that unique node of
\mathcal{L}_{l+p-2}
in row
1
, it also contains the unique node of
\mathcal{L}_{l+1}
in row
1
, and therefore contains the unique node of
\mathcal{L}_{l+p-2}
in row
2
, and therefore the unique node of
\mathcal{L}_{l+1}
in row
2
, and so on, so that
[\lambda]
contains every node of
\mathcal{L}_{l+1}
, contrary to assumption.
∎

Now we can prove the final result concerning
\hat{\epsilon}_{i}(\lambda)>\epsilon_{i}(\lambda^{\mathrm{reg}})
.

Proposition 4.10
.

Suppose
\lambda
is a strict partition and
i\in I
, and that
\lambda
has a strictly-removable
i
-node and a strictly-addable
i
-node in a longer ladder. Then
\hat{\epsilon}_{i}(\lambda)>\epsilon_{i}(\lambda^{\mathrm{reg}})
.

The cases
i=0
and
i=\frac{1}{2}(p-1)
are dealt with in
4.5
and
4.7
. So take
i\neq 0,\frac{1}{2}(p-1)
. We follow the structure of the proof of
4.7
. Let
\mu=\lambda^{\mathrm{reg}}
, and let
l_{1}<l_{2}<\cdots
be the ladders that contain nodes of residue
i
. As in
4.7
, when we read the addable and removable
i
-nodes from left to right, the nodes in longer ladders come earlier, and within each ladder the removable nodes come before the addable nodes. This means that the
i
-signature of
\mu
is

\dots[\operatorname{add}_{l_{4}}(\mu)][-\operatorname{rem}_{l_{3}}(\mu)][\operatorname{add}_{l_{3}}(\mu)][-\operatorname{rem}_{l_{2}}(\mu)][\operatorname{add}_{l_{2}}(\mu)][-\operatorname{rem}_{l_{1}}(\mu)][\operatorname{add}_{l_{1}}(\mu)].

So the reduced
i
-signature is the reduction of the sequence

\dots[\operatorname{add}_{l_{4}}(\mu)-\operatorname{rem}_{l_{3}}(\mu)][\operatorname{add}_{l_{3}}(\mu)-\operatorname{rem}_{l_{2}}(\mu)][\operatorname{add}_{l_{2}}(\mu)-\operatorname{rem}_{l_{1}}(\mu)][\operatorname{add}_{l_{1}}(\mu)].

By
Lemma

4.8
this is the same as the sequence

\displaystyle\dots

\displaystyle[\operatorname{sadd}_{l_{4}}(\lambda)-\operatorname{srem}_{l_{3}}(\lambda)-\operatorname{b}_{l_{3}}(\lambda)+\operatorname{b}_{l_{2}}(\lambda)]

\displaystyle[\operatorname{sadd}_{l_{3}}(\lambda)-\operatorname{srem}_{l_{2}}(\lambda)-\operatorname{b}_{l_{2}}(\lambda)+\operatorname{b}_{l_{1}}(\lambda)]

\displaystyle[\operatorname{sadd}_{l_{2}}(\lambda)-\operatorname{srem}_{l_{1}}(\lambda)-\operatorname{b}_{l_{1}}(\lambda)]

\displaystyle[\operatorname{sadd}_{l_{1}}(\lambda)],

where
\operatorname{b}_{l}(\lambda)=\operatorname{d}_{l}(\lambda)-\operatorname{d}_{l}(\mu)
for each
l
. Since by
Lemma

4.9
each
\operatorname{b}_{l}(\lambda)
is non-negative, we can complete the proof exactly as in
[
F6
, Proposition 4.17]
.
∎

5.
Dimension arguments

In the proof of our main theorem we will often use arguments involving dimensions, similar to those in
[
F5
,
F6
]
.

5.1.
The bar-length formula

First we recall Schur’s “bar-length formula”
[
S
]
for the dimension of
{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}
: if
\lambda\in{\mathcal{P}_{0}}(n)
, then

\dim{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}=2^{\lceil\frac{1}{2}(n-l(\lambda))\rceil}\frac{|\lambda|!}{\prod_{1\leqslant r\leqslant l(\lambda)}\lambda_{r}!}\frac{\prod_{1\leqslant r<s\leqslant l(\lambda)}(\lambda_{r}-\lambda_{s})}{\prod_{1\leqslant r<s\leqslant l(\lambda)}(\lambda_{r}+\lambda_{s})}.

Now define

\operatorname{ddim}(\lambda)=\frac{\dim{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}}{[{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}:{\mathbf{D}}^{\lambda^{\mathrm{reg}}}]}.

Using
3.1
, we get

\operatorname{ddim}(\lambda)=2^{\lceil\frac{1}{2}(|\lambda|-l(\lambda)-l_{p}(\lambda))\rceil}\frac{|\lambda|!}{\prod_{1\leqslant r\leqslant l(\lambda)}\lambda_{r}!}\frac{\prod_{1\leqslant r<s\leqslant l(\lambda)}(\lambda_{r}-\lambda_{s})}{\prod_{1\leqslant r<s\leqslant l(\lambda)}(\lambda_{r}+\lambda_{s})}.

Now the following lemma follows from
3.1
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Suppose
\lambda
and
\mu
are strict partitions with
\lambda^{\mathrm{reg}}=\mu^{\mathrm{reg}}
and
\operatorname{ddim}(\lambda)>\operatorname{ddim}(\mu)
. Then
\lambda
is not homogeneous.

To help us use this, we have an analogue of
[
F6
, Lemma 4.19]
, which is proved in the same way.

Lemma 5.2
.

Suppose
\lambda
and
\mu
are strict partitions with
\mu\vartriangleright\lambda
, and
m>\mu_{1}
. Define

\displaystyle\lambda^{+}

\displaystyle=(m,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots),

\displaystyle\mu^{+}

\displaystyle=(m,\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\dots).

Then

\frac{\operatorname{ddim}(\lambda^{+})}{\operatorname{ddim}(\mu^{+})}>\frac{\operatorname{ddim}(\lambda)}{\operatorname{ddim}(\mu)}.

In addition, if
\lambda^{\mathrm{reg}}=\mu^{\mathrm{reg}}
, then
(\lambda^{+})^{\mathrm{reg}}=(\mu^{+})^{\mathrm{reg}}
.

The next few lemmas apply these results in specific situations.
For the remainder of
Section

5.1
, we take
p=3
.
These results typically work as follows: we define two families of strict partitions
\lambda_{(l)}
,
\mu_{(l)}
, and we wish to show that
\operatorname{ddim}(\lambda_{(l)})>\operatorname{ddim}(\mu_{(l)})
for all
l
greater than or equal to some threshold
l_{0}
. The bar-length formula will allow us to calculate the ratio
r_{l}=\operatorname{ddim}(\lambda_{(l)})/\operatorname{ddim}(\mu_{(l)})
, and to express
r_{l+1}/r_{l}
as a rational function of
l
. We show that the value of this rational function is greater than
1
for all
l\geqslant l_{0}
; in each case, this can be shown by subtracting the denominator from the numerator, and checking that all derivatives of the resulting polynomial are positive at
l=l_{0}
. We deduce
r_{l_{0}}<r_{l_{0}+1}<\cdots
, and we can check directly that
r_{l_{0}}>1
to get the result.

Lemma 5.3
.

Suppose
p=3
,
l\geqslant 3
and define

\lambda_{(l)}=(3l+1\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\dots.}}10,\ 6,4,3,1).

Then
\lambda_{(l)}
is not homogeneous.

Define

\mu_{(l)}=(3l+4,\ 3l-2\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\dots.}}7,\ 3,1)

for each
l\geqslant 3
. Then
\lambda_{(l)}^{\mathrm{reg}}=\mu_{(l)}^{\mathrm{reg}}
, and the bar-length formula gives

\frac{\operatorname{ddim}(\lambda_{(l+1)})\operatorname{ddim}(\mu_{(l)})}{\operatorname{ddim}(\mu_{(l+1)})\operatorname{ddim}(\lambda_{(l)})}=\frac{(l+1)(3l+1)(3l+8)}{l(3l+5)(3l+7)}>1.

So

\frac{\operatorname{ddim}(\lambda_{(3)})}{\operatorname{ddim}(\mu_{(3)})}<\frac{\operatorname{ddim}(\lambda_{(4)})}{\operatorname{ddim}(\mu_{(4)})}<\frac{\operatorname{ddim}(\lambda_{(5)})}{\operatorname{ddim}(\mu_{(5)})}\dots.

The bar-length formula also gives
\operatorname{ddim}(\lambda_{(3)})=\operatorname{ddim}(\mu_{(3)})
, so we are done by
5.1
, except in the case
l=3
, where we can achieve the same result using the partition
(13,7,4)
instead of
\mu_{(3)}
.
∎

Lemma 5.4
.

Suppose
p=3
,
l\geqslant 4
, and define

\displaystyle\lambda_{(l)}

\displaystyle=(3l,3l-3,3l-7,3l-8,\ 3l-12\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\dots.}}3),

\displaystyle\mu_{(l)}

\displaystyle=(3l,3l-3,3l-5,\ 3l-9\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\dots.}}6,\ 2).

Then
\operatorname{ddim}(\lambda_{(l)})>\operatorname{ddim}(\mu_{(l)})
.

The bar-length formula gives

\frac{\operatorname{ddim}(\lambda_{(l+1)})\operatorname{ddim}(\mu_{(l)})}{\operatorname{ddim}(\mu_{(l+1)})\operatorname{ddim}(\lambda_{(l)})}=\frac{(l-3)l^{3}(2l-5)^{2}(2l-1)(3l-7)(3l-5)(3l-4)^{2}(3l+5)(6l-11)^{2}(6l-7)(6l+1)}{(l-2)^{4}(l+2)(2l-3)^{2}(3l-8)(3l-1)(3l+1)^{2}(6l-17)(6l-13)(6l-5)^{2}(6l-1)}.

This fraction is always greater than
1
when
l\geqslant 5
. Since
\operatorname{ddim}(\lambda_{(l)})>\operatorname{ddim}(\mu_{(l)})
for
l=4,5
, the same is true for all
l\geqslant 4
.
∎

Lemma 5.5
.

Suppose
p=3
,
l=4
or
l\geqslant 7
, and define

\displaystyle\lambda_{(l)}

\displaystyle=(3l+1,3l-3,3l-7,3l-8,\ 3l-12\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\dots.}}3),

\displaystyle\mu_{(l)}

\displaystyle=(3l+1,3l-3,3l-5,\ 3l-9\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\dots.}}6,\ 2)

Then
\operatorname{ddim}(\lambda_{(l)})>\operatorname{ddim}(\mu_{(l)})
.

For
l=4
the lemma can be easily checked. For
l\geqslant 7
the bar-length formula gives

\frac{\operatorname{ddim}(\lambda_{(l+1)})\operatorname{ddim}(\mu_{(l)})}{\operatorname{ddim}(\mu_{(l+1)})\operatorname{ddim}(\lambda_{(l)})}=\frac{(l-3)(l-1)^{2}l(l+2)(2l-5)^{2}(3l-7)(3l-5)(3l-1)^{2}(3l+1)(3l+4)(6l-11)^{2}(6l-7)}{(l-2)^{4}(l+1)^{2}(2l-3)(3l-8)(3l-2)^{3}(3l+7)(6l-17)(6l-13)(6l-5)(6l-1)}.

Since this fraction is always greater than
1
and
\operatorname{ddim}(\lambda_{(7)})>\operatorname{ddim}(\mu_{(7)})
, the result follows.
∎

Lemma 5.6
.

Suppose
p=3
,
l\geqslant 6
, and define

\displaystyle\lambda_{(l)}

\displaystyle=(3l-1,3l-2,3l-7,3l-8,\ 3l-12\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\dots.}}3),

\displaystyle\mu_{(l)}

\displaystyle=(3l-1,3l-2,3l-6,3l-8\ 3l-12\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\dots.}}6,\ 2).

Then
\operatorname{ddim}(\lambda_{(l)})>\operatorname{ddim}(\mu_{(l)})
.

The bar-length formula gives

\frac{\operatorname{ddim}(\lambda_{(l+1)})\operatorname{ddim}(\mu_{(l)})}{\operatorname{ddim}(\mu_{(l+1)})\operatorname{ddim}(\lambda_{(l)})}=\frac{(l-4)(l-1)^{3}(l+1)(2l-5)^{2}(3l-10)(3l-8)(3l-4)(3l-1)(3l+2)(6l-1)}{(l-3)(l-2)^{2}l^{3}(2l-1)(3l-11)^{2}(3l-5)(3l+5)(6l-13)(6l-7)}>1.

Since
\operatorname{ddim}(\lambda_{(6)})>\operatorname{ddim}(\mu_{(6)})
, the result follows.
∎

Lemma 5.7
.

Suppose
p=3
,
l\geqslant 3
and define

\lambda_{(l)}=(3l\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\dots.}}3).

Then

5.2.
Projective modules

Now we describe a technique we will use to show that certain partitions are not homogeneous using projective modules. The basic lemma we use is the following; for this lemma, we can take
p
to be any odd prime.

Lemma 5.15
.

Suppose
\lambda
and
\nu
are strict partitions with
\lambda^{\mathrm{reg}}=\nu^{\mathrm{reg}}
.

(1)

If there is a projective supermodule
P
such that

\frac{[P:{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}]}{[{\mathbf{P}}^{\lambda^{\mathrm{reg}}}:{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}]}>\frac{[P:{\mathbf{S}}^{\nu}]}{[{\mathbf{P}}^{\lambda^{\mathrm{reg}}}:{\mathbf{S}}^{\nu}]},

then
\lambda
is not homogeneous.

(2)

If
\operatorname{ddim}(\lambda)>\operatorname{ddim}(\nu)
then there is a projective supermodule
P
such that

\frac{[P:{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}]}{[{\mathbf{P}}^{\lambda^{\mathrm{reg}}}:{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}]}>\frac{[P:{\mathbf{S}}^{\nu}]}{[{\mathbf{P}}^{\lambda^{\mathrm{reg}}}:{\mathbf{S}}^{\nu}]}.

(1)

If
\lambda
is homogeneous then by Brauer reciprocity
[{\mathbf{P}}^{\mu}:{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}]=0
when
\mu\not=\lambda^{\mathrm{reg}}
. So if
P
is any projective supermodule and we write
P=\bigoplus_{\mu}({\mathbf{P}}^{\mu})^{\oplus c_{\mu}}
, then

\displaystyle[P:{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}]=c_{\lambda^{\mathrm{reg}}}[{\mathbf{P}}^{\lambda^{\mathrm{reg}}}:{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}],

\displaystyle[P:{\mathbf{S}}^{\nu}]\geqslant c_{\lambda^{\mathrm{reg}}}[{\mathbf{P}}^{\lambda^{\mathrm{reg}}}:{\mathbf{S}}^{\nu}],

giving

\frac{[P:{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}]}{[{\mathbf{P}}^{\lambda^{\mathrm{reg}}}:{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}]}\leqslant\frac{[P:{\mathbf{S}}^{\nu}]}{[{\mathbf{P}}^{\lambda^{\mathrm{reg}}}:{\mathbf{S}}^{\nu}]}.

(2)

Recall that

\operatorname{ddim}(\lambda)=\frac{\dim{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}}{[{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}:{\mathbf{D}}^{\lambda^{\mathrm{reg}}}]}=\frac{\sum_{\mu}[{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}:{\mathbf{D}}^{\mu}]\dim{\mathbf{D}}^{\mu}}{[{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}:{\mathbf{D}}^{\lambda^{\mathrm{reg}}}]}.

So if
\operatorname{ddim}(\lambda)>\operatorname{ddim}(\nu)
then there is some
\mu
for which

\frac{[{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}:{\mathbf{D}}^{\mu}]}{[{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}:{\mathbf{D}}^{\lambda^{\mathrm{reg}}}]}>\frac{[{\mathbf{S}}^{\nu}:{\mathbf{D}}^{\mu}]}{[{\mathbf{S}}^{\nu}:{\mathbf{D}}^{\lambda^{\mathrm{reg}}}]}.

By Brauer reciprocity, this is the same as saying

\frac{[{\mathbf{P}}^{\mu}:{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}]}{[{\mathbf{P}}^{\lambda^{\mathrm{reg}}}:{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}]}>\frac{[{\mathbf{P}}^{\mu}:{\mathbf{S}}^{\nu}]}{[{\mathbf{P}}^{\lambda^{\mathrm{reg}}}:{\mathbf{S}}^{\nu}]}.\qed

The way we will apply
Lemma

5.15
is as follows. Suppose we have a strict partition
\lambda
that we want to prove is not homogeneous. We will find another strict partition
\nu
with
\lambda^{\mathrm{reg}}=\nu^{\mathrm{reg}}
, and a pair of smaller partitions
\lambda_{-}
and
\nu_{-}
with
\lambda_{-}^{\mathrm{reg}}=\nu_{-}^{\mathrm{reg}}
and
\operatorname{ddim}(\lambda_{-})>\operatorname{ddim}(\nu_{-})
. Using part (ii) of
Lemma

5.15
, we will take a projective module
Q
for which

\frac{[Q:{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda_{-}}]}{[{\mathbf{P}}^{\lambda_{-}^{\mathrm{reg}}}:{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda_{-}}]}>\frac{[Q:{\mathbf{S}}^{\nu_{-}}]}{[{\mathbf{P}}^{\lambda_{-}^{\mathrm{reg}}}:{\mathbf{S}}^{\nu_{-}}]},

and apply a suitable induction functor to obtain a projective module
P
with

\frac{[P:{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}]}{[{\mathbf{P}}^{\lambda^{\mathrm{reg}}}:{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}]}>\frac{[P:{\mathbf{S}}^{\nu}]}{[{\mathbf{P}}^{\lambda^{\mathrm{reg}}}:{\mathbf{S}}^{\nu}]}.

Then part (i) of
Lemma

5.15
gives the desired result.

The specific cases where we use this technique are in the following
Section
.

Proposition 5.16
.

Suppose

, and

. Also define

6.
Wreath products and RoCK blocks

In this section we analyse certain wreath product algebras that arise in the work of Kleshchev and Livesey
[
KL
]
on RoCK blocks, and use these to derive information on modules labelled by special partitions.

6.1.
Wreath products

First we recall some more theory of superalgebras; let
\mathbb{F}
be an arbitrary field throughout this section. For any vector superspace
V
and
0\not=v
a homogeneous vector in
V
we let
\overline{v}\in\{0,1\}
be the parity of
v
. We use the usual tensor product rule for superalgebras
A
and
B
: if
a_{1},a_{2}\in A
and
b_{1},b_{2}\in B
are all homogeneous, then
(a_{1}\otimes b_{1})(a_{2}\otimes b_{2})=(-1)^{\overline{b_{1}}\overline{a_{2}}}(a_{1}a_{2})\otimes(b_{1}b_{2})
in
A\otimes B
.

We will be mainly concerned with the superalgebra
A_{\mathbb{F}}=\mathbb{F}[u]/(u^{3})
, where the generator
u
is homogeneous of odd degree. We usually write write
A_{\mathbb{F}}
just as
A
if
\mathbb{F}
is understood. For any
d\geqslant 0
we define the wreath superproduct
A\wr_{\text{s}}\mathfrak{S}_{d}
as in
[
KL
, §2.2a]
: as a vector superspace this is
A^{\otimes d}\otimes\mathbb{F}\mathfrak{S}_{d}
with
\mathfrak{S}_{d}
in even degree. The multiplication rule is defined by the tensor product rule above for multiplying in
A^{\otimes d}
, together with

(1^{\otimes d};\sigma)(v_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes v_{d};\pi)=(-1)^{[\sigma;v_{1},\dots,v_{d}]}(v_{\sigma^{-1}(1)}\otimes\dots\otimes v_{\sigma^{-1}(d)};\sigma\pi),

for
\sigma,\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{d}
and homogeneous
v_{1},\dots,v_{d}\in A
, where

[\sigma;v_{1},\dots,v_{d}]=|\{(i,j)\mid 1\leqslant i<j\leqslant d,\ \sigma(i)>\sigma(j),\ \overline{v_{i}}=1=\overline{v_{j}}\}|.

Our aim in this section is to develop a small part of the representation theory of
A\wr_{\text{s}}\mathfrak{S}_{d}
(mimicking the work of Chuang and Tan
[
CT
]
) to be able to apply the results from
[
KL
]
; in particular, we will be interested in finding bounds for the diagonal Cartan invariants of
A\wr_{\text{s}}\mathfrak{S}_{d}
.

We begin by constructing some
A\wr_{\text{s}}\mathfrak{S}_{d}
-supermodules. (We will view
A\wr_{\text{s}}\mathfrak{S}_{d}
as a superalgebra, although as we shall see every simple supermodule is of type M, so it makes little difference whether we consider modules or supermodules.) For any
A
-supermodule
V
and
\mathbb{F}\mathfrak{S}_{d}
-module
W
(viewed as a supermodule concentrated in degree
0
) we define a module
T(V,W)
, which is
V^{\otimes d}\otimes W
as a vector superspace, with the action of
A\wr_{\text{s}}\mathfrak{S}_{d}
given by

\displaystyle(1^{\otimes(i-1)}\otimes u\otimes 1^{\otimes(d-i)};1)v_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes v_{d}\otimes w

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\overline{v_{d}}}v_{1}\otimes v_{i-1}\otimes\dots\otimes uv_{i}\otimes v_{i+1}\otimes\dots\otimes v_{d}\otimes w

\displaystyle(1\otimes\dots\otimes 1;\sigma)v_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes v_{d}\otimes w

\displaystyle=(-1)^{[\sigma;v_{1},\dots,v_{d}]}v_{\sigma^{-1}(1)}\otimes\dots\otimes v_{\sigma^{-1}(d)}\otimes\sigma w

for homogeneous
v_{1},\dots,v_{d}\in V
,
w\in W
and
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{d}
.

This construction allows us to classify the simple supermodules for
A\wr_{\text{s}}\mathfrak{S}_{d}
: let
J(R)
denote the Jacobson radical of a ring
R
. It is clear that any element of
A\wr_{\text{s}}\mathfrak{S}_{d}
of the form
(1^{\otimes(i-1)}\otimes u\otimes 1^{\otimes(d-i)};1)
or of the form
(1^{\otimes d};m)
with
m\in J(\mathbb{F}\mathfrak{S}_{d})
lies in
J(A\wr_{\text{s}}\mathfrak{S}_{d})
. Hence
A\wr_{\text{s}}\mathfrak{S}_{d}/J(A\wr_{\text{s}}\mathfrak{S}_{d})\cong\mathbb{F}\mathfrak{S}_{d}/J(\mathbb{F}\mathfrak{S}_{d})
, and every simple
A\wr_{\text{s}}\mathfrak{S}_{d}
-module arises by taking a simple
\mathbb{F}\mathfrak{S}_{d}
-module and letting
u
act as
0
. In other words, the simple
A\wr_{\text{s}}\mathfrak{S}_{d}
-modules are the modules
T(\mathbb{F},S)
as
S
ranges over the simple
\mathbb{F}\mathfrak{S}_{d}
-modules, where
\mathbb{F}
is regarded as an
A
-module with
u
acting as
0
. In particular, if
\operatorname{char}(\mathbb{F})=0
then the simple
\mathbb{F}\mathfrak{S}_{d}
-modules are the
Specht modules

\mathscr{S}^{\lambda}
for partitions
\lambda
of
d
, while if
\operatorname{char}(\mathbb{F})=p>0
then the simple
\mathbb{F}\mathfrak{S}_{d}
-modules are the
James modules

\mathscr{D}^{\lambda}
for
p
-regular partitions
\lambda
.

We will adapt techniques from
[
CT
]
to find the composition factors of
T(A,\mathscr{S}^{\delta})
in characteristic
0
, which will enable us to find the diagonal Cartan invariants. First we introduce another construction: if
M
is an
A\wr_{\text{s}}\mathfrak{S}_{d}
-module and
W
an
\mathbb{F}\mathfrak{S}_{d}
-module then we define the
A\wr_{\text{s}}\mathfrak{S}_{d}
-module
M\oslash W
to be the vector space
M\otimes W
with

(u_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes u_{d};\sigma)(m\otimes w)=((u_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes u_{d};\sigma)m)\otimes\sigma w.

It is clear from the definition that
T(V,W)\oslash W^{\prime}\cong T(V,W\otimes W^{\prime})
.

The regular
A
-module has three composition factors all isomorphic to
\mathbb{F}
; we will use this to find filtrations and composition factors of the modules
T(A,\mathscr{S}^{\nu})
. Given partitions
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\nu
with
|\alpha|+|\beta|+|\gamma|=|\nu|
, let
c^{\nu}_{\alpha\beta\gamma}
denote the corresponding Littlewood–Richardson coefficient, i.e. the multiplicity of
\mathscr{S}^{\nu}
as a composition factor of
\mathscr{S}^{\alpha}\otimes\mathscr{S}^{\beta}\otimes\mathscr{S}^{\gamma}\uparrow_{\mathfrak{S}_{|\alpha|,|\beta|,|\gamma|}}^{\mathfrak{S}_{|}\nu|}
in characteristic
0
; here and below,
\mathfrak{S}_{a,b,c}
denotes the Young subgroup
\mathfrak{S}_{a}\times\mathfrak{S}_{b}\times\mathfrak{S}_{c}\leqslant\mathfrak{S}_{a+b+c}
. For a partition
\beta
let
\beta^{\prime}
denote the conjugate (or transpose) partition.

Now we can give the composition factors of the modules of the modules
T(A,\mathscr{S}^{\nu})
in characteristic
0
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Suppose
\operatorname{char}(\mathbb{F})=0
, and let
\nu,\pi
be partitions of
d
. Then

[T(A,\mathscr{S}^{\nu}):T(\mathbb{F},\mathscr{S}^{\pi})]=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in{\mathcal{P}}\\
|\alpha|+|\beta|+|\gamma|=d\end{subarray}}c^{\nu}_{\alpha\beta\gamma}c^{\pi}_{\alpha\beta^{\prime}\gamma}.

We adapt the results in
[
CT
, §4]
to wreath superproducts, specialising to the case of
A\wr_{\text{s}}\mathfrak{S}_{d}
.

Because
\mathscr{S}^{(d)}
is the trivial
\mathfrak{S}_{d}
-module, we can write
T(A,\mathscr{S}^{\nu})=T(A,\mathscr{S}^{(d)})\oslash\mathscr{S}^{\nu}
. So if
0\leqslant M_{1}\leqslant\dots\leqslant M_{h}=T(A,\mathscr{S}^{(d)})
is a filtration of
T(A,\mathscr{S}^{(d)})
, then

0\leqslant M_{1}\oslash\mathscr{S}^{\nu}\leqslant\dots\leqslant M_{h}\oslash\mathscr{S}^{\nu}

is a filtration of
T(A,\mathscr{S}^{\nu})
. We thus start by finding a filtration of
T(A,\mathscr{S}^{(d)})
. As vector spaces we identify
T(A,\mathscr{S}^{(d)})
and
A^{\otimes d}
.

The vector space
A^{\otimes d}
has a basis
U=\{u^{e_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes u^{e_{d}}\mid 0\leqslant e_{i}\leqslant 2\text{ for each }i\}
. For any composition
(a,b,c)
of
d

U_{a,b,c}
be the subset of

consisting of all basis elements
u^{e_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes u^{e_{d}}
in which
1,u,u^{2}
appear
a,b,c
times respectively.

From the filtration
0\subseteq\langle u^{2}\rangle\subseteq\langle u,u^{2}\rangle\subseteq A
of

0=M_{0}\leqslant\dots\leqslant M_{2d+1}=T(A,\mathscr{S}^{(d)})

where
M_{i}=\langle U_{a,b,c}\mid b+2c\geqslant 2d+1-i\rangle
. For any
(a,b,c)
with
b+2c=2d+1-i
, let
V_{a,b,c}=(\langle U_{a,b,c}\rangle_{\mathbb{F}}\oplus M_{i-1})/M_{i-1}
. Then

\frac{M_{i}}{M_{i-1}}\cong\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}a,b,c\geqslant 0\\
b+2c=2d+1-i\end{subarray}}V_{a,b,c},

so that
T(A,\mathscr{S}^{(d)})
is filtered by the modules
V_{a,b,c}
as
(a,b,c)
ranges over compositions of
d
. We will show that

(6.2)

V_{a,b,c}\cong T\left(\mathbb{F},(\mathscr{S}^{(a)}\otimes\mathscr{S}^{(1^{b})}\otimes\mathscr{S}^{(c)}){\uparrow}_{\mathfrak{S}_{a,b,c}}^{\mathfrak{S}_{d}}\right).

It is easy to see that any vector of the form
(1^{\otimes(i-1)}\otimes u\otimes 1^{\otimes(d-i)};1)
acts as 0 on both modules. So we only need to compare the action of the elements
(1^{\otimes d};\sigma)
.

Let
v
be the image of
1^{\otimes a}\otimes u^{\otimes b}\otimes(u^{2})^{\otimes c}
in
V_{a,b,c}
and
R
be a set of representatives of
\mathfrak{S}_{d}/\mathfrak{S}_{a,b,c}
. Then
\{(1^{\otimes d};\pi)v\mid\pi\in R\}
is a basis of
V_{a,b,c}
. To work out the action of
\mathfrak{S}_{d}
on this basis, take
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{d}
and
\pi\in R
, and let
\rho\in R
be such that
\sigma\pi\in\rho\mathfrak{S}_{a,b,c}
. Then
\rho^{-1}\sigma\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{a,b,c}
; we let
\overline{\sigma}
be the permutation induced by
\rho^{-1}\sigma\pi
on
\{a+1,\dots,a+b\}
.

Then

(1^{\otimes d};\sigma)(1^{\otimes d};\pi)v=(1^{\otimes d};\rho)(1^{\otimes d};\rho^{-1}\sigma\pi)v=(1^{\otimes d};\rho)(-1)^{\mathbf{\mathrm{sgn}}(\overline{\sigma})}v=(-1)^{\mathbf{\mathrm{sgn}}(\overline{\sigma})}(1^{\otimes d};\rho)v.

This action coincides with the action of
\mathfrak{S}_{d}
on
(\mathscr{S}^{(a)}\otimes\mathscr{S}^{(1^{b})}\otimes\mathscr{S}^{(c)}){\uparrow}_{\mathfrak{S}_{a,b,c}}^{\mathfrak{S}_{d}}
(with basis
\{\pi w\mid\pi\in R\}
where

is any non-zero element of
\mathscr{S}^{(a)}\otimes\mathscr{S}^{(1^{b})}\otimes\mathscr{S}^{(c)}
), so that (
6.2
) follows.

Thus
T(A,\mathscr{S}^{(d)})
has a filtration with a factor isomorphic to
T(\mathbb{F},(\mathscr{S}^{(a)}\otimes\mathscr{S}^{(1^{b})}\otimes\mathscr{S}^{(c)}){\uparrow}_{\mathfrak{S}_{a,b,c}}^{\mathfrak{S}_{d}})
for each composition
(a,b,c)
of
d
. Hence
T(A,\mathscr{S}^{\nu})\cong T(A,\mathscr{S}^{(n)})\oslash\mathscr{S}^{\nu}
has a filtration with a factor isomorphic to

\displaystyle T(\mathbb{F},(\mathscr{S}^{(a)}\otimes\mathscr{S}^{(1^{b})}\otimes\mathscr{S}^{(c)}){\uparrow}_{\mathfrak{S}_{a,b,c}}^{\mathfrak{S}_{d}})\oslash\mathscr{S}^{\nu}

\displaystyle\cong T(\mathbb{F},((\mathscr{S}^{(a)}\otimes\mathscr{S}^{(1^{b})}\otimes\mathscr{S}^{(c)}){\uparrow}_{\mathfrak{S}_{a,b,c}}^{\mathfrak{S}_{d}})\otimes\mathscr{S}^{\nu})

\displaystyle\cong T(\mathbb{F},((\mathscr{S}^{(a)}\otimes\mathscr{S}^{(1^{b})}\otimes\mathscr{S}^{(c)})\otimes\mathscr{S}^{\nu}{\downarrow}^{\mathfrak{S}_{d}}_{\mathfrak{S}_{a,b,c}}){\uparrow}_{\mathfrak{S}_{a,b,c}}^{\mathfrak{S}_{d}})

for each
(a,b,c)
. Now

\displaystyle(\mathscr{S}^{(a)}\otimes\mathscr{S}^{(1^{b})}\otimes\mathscr{S}^{(c)})\otimes\mathscr{S}^{\nu}{\downarrow}^{\mathfrak{S}_{d}}_{\mathfrak{S}_{a,b,c}}

\displaystyle\cong\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in{\mathcal{P}}(a)\\
\beta\in{\mathcal{P}}(b)\\
\gamma\in{\mathcal{P}}(c)\end{subarray}}(\mathscr{S}^{(a)}\otimes\mathscr{S}^{(1^{b})}\otimes\mathscr{S}^{(c)})\otimes(\mathscr{S}^{\alpha}\otimes\mathscr{S}^{\beta}\otimes\mathscr{S}^{\gamma})^{\oplus c^{\nu}_{\alpha\beta\gamma}}

\displaystyle\cong\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in{\mathcal{P}}(a)\\
\beta\in{\mathcal{P}}(b)\\
\gamma\in{\mathcal{P}}(c)\end{subarray}}(\mathscr{S}^{\alpha}\otimes\mathscr{S}^{\beta^{\prime}}\otimes\mathscr{S}^{\gamma})^{\oplus c^{\nu}_{\alpha\beta\gamma}}

so that

\left[T(\mathbb{F},(\mathscr{S}^{(a)}\otimes\mathscr{S}^{(1^{b})}\otimes\mathscr{S}^{(c)}){\uparrow}_{\mathfrak{S}_{a,b,c}}^{\mathfrak{S}_{d}})\oslash\mathscr{S}^{\nu}:T(\mathbb{F},\mathscr{S}^{\pi})\right]=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in{\mathcal{P}}(a)\\
\beta\in{\mathcal{P}}(b)\\
\gamma\in{\mathcal{P}}(c)\end{subarray}}c^{\nu}_{\alpha\beta\gamma}c^{\pi}_{\alpha\beta^{\prime}\gamma},

and the result follows.
∎

Now we turn to Cartan invariants.

Lemma 6.3
.

The projective
A\wr_{\text{s}}\mathfrak{S}_{d}
-modules are precisely the modules
T(A,P)
, for
P
a projective
\mathbb{F}\mathfrak{S}_{d}
-module. If
S
is a simple
\mathbb{F}\mathfrak{S}_{d}
-module with projective cover
P(S)
, then
T(A,P(S))
is the projective cover of
T(\mathbb{F},S)
.

As
T(A,\mathbb{F}\mathfrak{S}_{d})\cong A\wr_{\text{s}}\mathfrak{S}_{d}
and
T(A,P\oplus Q)=T(A,P)\oplus T(A,Q)
it is clear that the modules
T(A,P)
are projective, and we have a direct sum decomposition

A\wr_{\text{s}}\mathfrak{S}_{d}=\bigoplus_{S}T(A,P(S))^{\oplus\dim S},

summing over all simple
\mathbb{F}\mathfrak{S}_{d}
-modules
S
. The simple
A\wr_{\text{s}}\mathfrak{S}_{d}
-modules are the modules
T(\mathbb{F},S)
, with
\dim T(\mathbb{F},S)=\dim S
for each
S
. So the summands in the expression above are indecomposable, and therefore every projective module occurs as
T(A,P)
for some projective
P
. Moreover, it is easy to check that
T(\mathbb{F},S)
occurs as a quotient of
T(A,P(S))
, and therefore
T(A,P(S))
is the projective cover of
T(\mathbb{F},S)
.
∎

Now we can explicitly find the diagonal Cartan invariants for
A\wr_{\text{s}}\mathfrak{S}_{d}
in characteristic
0
. Let
C=(c_{\nu,\pi})
be the Cartan matrix of
A\wr_{\text{s}}\mathfrak{S}_{d}
with rows and columns indexed by partitions of
d
, so that

is the multiplicity of
T(\mathbb{F},\mathscr{S}^{\nu})
as a composition factor of the projective cover of

.

Lemma 6.4
.

Suppose
\operatorname{char}(\mathbb{F})=0
and
\nu\in{\mathcal{P}}(d)
. Then
c_{\nu,\nu}\geqslant 2d+1
, with equality if and only if
\gamma=(d)
or
(1^{d})
.

Because
\mathbb{F}\mathfrak{S}_{d}
is semisimple,
\mathscr{S}^{\nu}
is it own projective cover, and therefore
T(A,\mathscr{S}^{\nu})
is the projective cover of
T(\mathbb{F},\mathscr{S}^{\nu})
. So by
Lemma

6.1

c_{\nu,\nu}=[T(A,\mathscr{S}^{\nu}):T(\mathbb{F},\mathscr{S}^{\nu})]=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in{\mathcal{P}}\\
|\alpha|+|\beta|+|\gamma|=d\end{subarray}}c^{\nu}_{\alpha\beta\gamma}c^{\nu}_{\alpha\beta^{\prime}\gamma}.

We first recall the following simple fact about the Littlewood–Richardson coefficients: if
\beta\subseteq\nu
and
0\leqslant i\leqslant d-|\beta|
, there exist partitions
\alpha\in{\mathcal{P}}(i)
and
\gamma\in{\mathcal{P}}(d-|\beta|-i)
for which
c^{\nu}_{\alpha\beta\gamma}>0
.

Assume first that
\gamma\not=(d),(1^{d})
. Then
d\geqslant 3
and
\varnothing,(1),(2,1)\subseteq\gamma
. So (using the fact above)

\displaystyle c_{\gamma,\gamma}

\displaystyle\geqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,\gamma\in{\mathcal{P}}\\
|\alpha|+|\gamma|=d\end{subarray}}(c^{\nu}_{\alpha\varnothing\gamma})^{2}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,\gamma\in{\mathcal{P}}\\
|\alpha|+|\gamma|=d-1\end{subarray}}(c^{\nu}_{\alpha(1)\gamma})^{2}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,\gamma\in{\mathcal{P}}\\
|\alpha|+|\gamma|=d-3\end{subarray}}(c^{\nu}_{\alpha(2,1)\gamma})^{2}

\displaystyle\geqslant(d+1)+d+(d-2)

\displaystyle>2d+1.

Now assume that
\gamma=(d)
(the case
\gamma=(1^{d})
being similar). In this case we use the fact that

c^{(d)}_{\alpha\beta\gamma}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\alpha=(|\alpha|),\ \beta=(|\beta|),\ \gamma=(|\gamma|)\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

and

c^{(d)}_{\alpha\beta^{\prime}\gamma}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\alpha=(|\alpha|),\ \beta=(1^{|\beta|}),\ \gamma=(|\gamma|)\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

which gives

c_{(d),(d)}=\sum_{i=0}^{d}(c^{(d)}_{(i)\varnothing(d-i)})^{2}+\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}(c^{(d)}_{(i)(1)(d-i-1)})^{2}=(d+1)+d=2d+1.\qed

Now we prove a similar result in characteristic
3
. Given
\pi\in{\mathcal{P}}(d)
, let
\overline{\mathscr{S}}^{\pi}
denote a
3
-modular reduction of
\mathscr{S}^{\pi}
. Recall that if
\mu
is a
3
-regular partition then
\mathscr{D}^{\mu}
denotes the James module for
\mathfrak{S}_{d}
in characteristic
3
. Let
\overline{C}
denote the Cartan matrix for
A\wr_{\text{s}}\mathfrak{S}_{d}
in characteristic
3
, with entries
\overline{c}_{\mu,\pi}=[T(\mathbb{F},P(\mathscr{D}^{\pi}))):T(\mathbb{F},\mathscr{D}^{\mu})]
.

Lemma 6.5
.

Suppose
\operatorname{char}(\mathbb{F})=3
,
d\geqslant 3
and
\mu
is a
3
-regular partition of
d
. Then
\overline{c}_{\gamma,\gamma}>2d+1
.

In this proof
A
denotes the algebra
\mathbb{F}(u)/(u^{3})
Given
\pi\in{\mathcal{P}}(d)
, let
d_{\pi\mu}=[\overline{\mathscr{S}}^{\pi}:\mathscr{D}^{\mu}]
be the corresponding decomposition number for
\mathbb{F}\mathfrak{S}_{d}
. Then it is clear that

. So, taking the result of

6.1
and reducing modulo

[T(A,\overline{\mathscr{S}}^{\nu}):T(\mathbb{F},\mathscr{D}^{\mu})]=\sum_{\pi\in{\mathcal{P}}(d)}[T(A_{\mathbb{C}},\mathscr{S}^{\nu}):T(\mathbb{C},\mathscr{S}^{\pi})]d_{\pi\mu}.

So by Brauer reciprocity

\overline{c}_{\mu,\mu}=[T(A,P(\mathscr{D}^{\mu})):T(\mathbb{F},\mathscr{D}^{\mu})]=\sum_{\nu\in{\mathcal{P}}(d)}d_{\nu\mu}\sum_{\pi\in{\mathcal{P}}(d)}[T(A_{\mathbb{C}},\mathscr{S}^{\nu}):T(\mathbb{C},\mathscr{S}^{\pi})]d_{\pi\mu}.

Taking just the terms with

gives

\overline{c}_{\mu,\mu}\geqslant\sum_{\nu\in{\mathcal{P}}(d)}[T(A_{\mathbb{C}},\mathscr{S}^{\nu}):T(\mathbb{C},\mathscr{S}^{\nu})]d_{\nu\mu}^{2}=\sum_{\nu\in{\mathcal{P}}(d)}c_{\mu,\mu}d_{\nu\mu}^{2}.

If

again using

∎

6.2.
Special partitions and RoCK blocks

Now we consider special partitions more closely. Even though our main theorem excludes the case of special partitions, we still need to know something about modules labelled by special partitions in order to prove our main result. To do this, we combine our analysis of wreath products in
Section

6
with the results of Kleshchev and Livesey.

Recall that a strict partition is special if it has the form
\nu+3\alpha
with
\nu
a 3-bar core and
\alpha
a partition with
l(\alpha)\leqslant l(\nu)
. In the case where
\lambda=\nu+3\alpha
where the non-zero parts of
\nu
are congruent to
1
modulo
3
and
l(\nu)\geqslant|\alpha|
, the block of
\mathcal{T}_{|\lambda|}
containing
{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}
is a
spin RoCK block
as defined in
[
KL
]
. We first prove that when considering partitions of the above given form we may always assume that we are in a RoCK block.

Lemma 6.6
.

Let
\alpha
be a partition. If there exists a 3-bar core
\nu
with
l(\nu)\geqslant l(\alpha)
such that
\nu+3\alpha
is homogeneous, then
\pi+3\alpha
is homogeneous for all 3-bar cores
\pi
with
l(\pi)\geqslant l(\alpha)
.

It is enough to prove that if
l\geqslant l(\lambda)
and

\pi=(3l-2\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\dots.}}1),\qquad\nu=(3l-1\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\dots.}}2),\qquad\psi=(3l+1\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\dots.}}1),

then
\nu+3\alpha
is homogeneous if and only if
\pi+3\alpha
is, and similarly with
\psi
in place of
\pi
.

It follows by the definition that all parts of
\nu+3\alpha
are congruent to
2
modulo
3
, so that
(\nu+3\alpha)^{-1}=\pi+3\alpha
and
(\pi+3\alpha)^{+1}=\nu+3\alpha
. Similarly we find that
(\nu+3\alpha)^{+0}=\psi+3\alpha
and
(\psi+3\alpha)^{-0}=\nu+3\alpha
. The lemma then follows from
Lemmas

4.1
and
4.2
.
∎

Next we prove a column removal result for
\alpha
.

Lemma 6.7
.

Suppose
\alpha
is a partition. Let
l=l(\alpha)
and

\nu=(3l-2\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\dots.}}1),\qquad\pi=(3l-1\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\dots.}}2),\qquad\beta=(\alpha_{1}-1,\dots,\alpha_{l}-1).

If
\nu+3\alpha
is homogeneous then so is
\pi+3\beta
.

Note that all parts of
\nu+3\alpha
are congruent to
1
modulo
3
, so
\pi+3\beta=(\nu+3\alpha)^{-0}
. The lemma then follows from
Lemma

4.1
.
∎

The converse does not necessarily hold, as the following
Section
shows.

Theorem 6.8
.

Let
\nu=(3l-2\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\dots.}}1)
, where
l\geqslant d\geqslant 3
. Then
\nu+(3^{d})
is not homogeneous.

To prove
6.8
, we will use results on RoCK blocks proved recently by Kleshchev and Livesey, specialising to the case
p=3
. So we fix
\nu=(3l-2\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\dots.}}1)
and
d\leqslant l
, and let
\mathcal{B}^{\nu,d}
be the block of
\mathcal{T}_{|\nu|+3d}
with bar core
\nu
and bar-weight
d
. (We will abuse notation by saying that a
3
-strict partition
\lambda
lies in
\mathcal{B}^{\nu,d}
if it has
3
-bar core
\nu
and
3
-bar-weight
d
.)

Lemma 6.9
.

The
3
-strict partitions in
\mathcal{B}^{\nu,d}
are the partitions
(\nu+3\alpha)\sqcup 3\beta
, for partitions
\alpha,\beta
with
|\alpha|+|\beta|=d
. Such a partition is restricted if and only if
\alpha=\varnothing
.

The first statement is easily proved by induction on
d
: a partition in
\mathcal{B}^{\nu,d}
is obtained by adding a
3
-bar to a partition in
\mathcal{B}^{\nu,d-1}
. By induction such a partition can be written in the form
(\nu+3\alpha^{-})\sqcup 3\beta^{-}
where
|\alpha^{-}|+|\beta^{-}|=d-1
. Since
d-1<l
, the last non-zero part of this partition equals

. So the only way to add a
3
-bar is to increase one of the parts by
3
. Increasing one of the parts congruent to
1
modulo
3
corresponds to adding a node to
\alpha^{-}
, while increasing one of the parts divisible by
3
corresponds to adding a node to
\beta
. So we obtain a partition of the required form.

The second statement is easy to check.
∎

Now we compute the diagonal Cartan entry for the simple module
{\mathbf{D}}^{\nu\sqcup(3d)}
.

Lemma 6.10
.

Let
\nu
and

be as above, and let

[{\mathbf{P}}^{\mu}:{\mathbf{D}}^{\mu}]=2d+1
.

Lemma

6.9

is the most dominant restricted

-strict partition in

. So

gives

{\mathbf{P}}^{\mu}\sim\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\in{\mathcal{P}_{0}}\\

where the coefficient
[{\mathbf{P}}^{\lambda^{\mathrm{reg}}}:{\mathbf{S}}^{\lambda}]
is given in
3.1
. So to obtain the formula for
[{\mathbf{P}}^{\mu}]
we need to find which strict partitions
\lambda

. We claim that

if and only if

Now we summarise the results we need from
[

]

Next we want to consider the

Let

. We set
a_{r}=\lambda_{r}-\lambda^{-0}_{r}
for
1\leqslant r\leqslant l
and
a_{l+1}=\lambda_{l+1}+1
.

The assumption that
\lambda^{-1}=\lambda
means that if
r\leqslant l
with
a_{r}=0
, then
a_{r+1}=2
. Furthermore, if
a_{l}=0
, then
\alpha_{l}
must be
0
.

If
a_{1}=\dots=a_{l}=2
, then
\lambda=(3l+1\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\dots.}}4)+3\alpha=(3l-2\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\dots.}}1)+3(\alpha+(1^{l}))
or
(3l+1\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\dots.}}1)+3\alpha
, so
\lambda
is special, contrary to assumption. So we assume there is some
r\leqslant l
such that
a_{r}\leqslant 1
.

If
r<s\leqslant l
and
\alpha_{r}>\alpha_{s}
, then the strictly-addable nodes of
\lambda^{-0}
in row
r
lie in a longer ladder than the strictly-addable nodes in row
s
. So using
4.7
, if
a_{s}>0
, then
a_{r}=2
. Similarly, if
a_{l+1}=2
and
\alpha_{r}>0
, then
a_{r}=2
. In particular, if
\alpha_{l}>0
then
a_{l+1}=1
(since otherwise
a_{1}=\dots=a_{l}=2
, contrary to the last paragraph).

Combining these statements, we obtain
a_{r}=2
for every
r
for which
\alpha_{r}>\alpha_{l}
.

Case 6.1

Suppose
a_{r}=1
for a single value
r
, and
a_{s}=2
for all
s\neq r
. So

\lambda=(3l+1\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\dots.}}1)+3\alpha-\delta_{r},

where
\delta_{r}
is the composition which has a
1
in position
r
and
0
elsewhere. The assumptions above mean that
\alpha_{r}=0
and
a_{l+1}=2
. Now we use a dimension argument. Let
\mu=(3l+1\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\dots.}}4)+3\alpha
. Then
\mu^{\mathrm{reg}}=\lambda^{\mathrm{reg}}
and
\operatorname{ddim}(\lambda)>\operatorname{ddim}(\mu)
by
Lemmas

5.2
and
5.13
as
\alpha_{r}=0
. So
\lambda
is not homogeneous.

Case 6.2

Suppose that there exists
r
such that
a_{r}=2
and either
r=1
or
a_{r-1}>0
. Consider the partition
\lambda^{+1}
. We claim that this partition has a strictly-addable
0
-node
(r,3(l-r+2+\alpha_{r}))
: this follows since
\lambda_{r}=3(l-r+2+\alpha_{r})-2
, and
\lambda_{r-1}\geqslant 3(l-r+2+\alpha_{r})
if
r\geqslant 2
, and
\lambda_{r-2}\geqslant 3(l-r+2+\alpha_{r})+2
if
r\geqslant 3
.

If there is any
s\leqslant l
for which
(a_{s},a_{s+1})
equals
(0,2)
,
(1,0)
or
(1,1)
, then
\lambda^{+1}
has a strictly-removable
0
-node in either row
s
or row
s+1
. Now the fact that
a_{r}=2
and
a_{s}\leqslant 1
and the argument at the start of Case 6 mean that
\alpha_{s}\leqslant\alpha_{r}
, and therefore the strictly-removable
0
-node in row
s
or
s+1
lies in a shorter ladder than the strictly-addable
0
-node in row
r
. So by
4.7

\lambda^{+1}
is not homogeneous, and hence neither is
\lambda
.

So we can assume that
a_{s}\geqslant 1
for all
s
, and that there are no two consecutive values of
s
with
a_{s}=1
. As observed above, there is at least one
1\leqslant s\leqslant l
with
a_{s}=1
. If there is exactly one
1\leqslant s\leqslant l+1
for which
a_{s}=1
, then we are in case Case 6.1, so assume instead that there are at least two values of
s
for which
a_{s}=1
.

Now we define a sequence of partitions
\nu^{(1)},\nu^{(2)},\dots
by

\nu^{(1)}=\lambda^{+1},\qquad\nu^{(k+1)}=((\nu^{(k)})^{+0})^{+1}\text{ for }k\geqslant 1.

Observe that
\lambda^{+1}
has no parts congruent to
1
modulo
3
, and so by
Lemma

7.2

\nu^{(k)}
has no parts congruent to
1
modulo
3
, for any
k
. Now we make the following observations, for
k\geqslant 1
.

Claim:

If
a_{1}=2
or
a_{l+1}=2
or the tuple
(a_{1},\dots,a_{l+1})
contains
k
consecutive
2
s, then
\nu^{(k)}
has a strictly-addable
0
-node
(s,c)
for which
3s+c\geqslant 3(\alpha_{l}+l+k+1)
.

Proof:

If
a_{1}=2
, then we can take
(s,c)=(1,3(l+k+\alpha_{1}))
. If
a_{l+1}=2
, then we can take
(s,c)=(l+k+1,1)
by
Lemma

7.3
and induction on
k
.

Assume now that
k<r\leqslant l
such that
a_{r-k}=1
and
a_{r-k+1}=\dots=a_{r}=2
. Then
\alpha_{r-k}=\dots=\alpha_{r}=\alpha_{l}
, so we can take
(s,c)=(r,3(\alpha_{l}+l+k-r+1))
by
Lemma

7.4
.

Claim:

If there is
k\leqslant r\leqslant l
such that
(a_{r-k+1},\dots,a_{r+1})=(1,2,2,\dots,2,1)
, then
\nu^{(k)}
has a strictly-removable
0
-node
(s,c)
for which
3s+c=3(\alpha_{l}+l+k)
.

Proof:

In this case
\alpha_{r-1}=\dots=\alpha_{r}=\alpha_{l}
. We can take
(s,c)=(r,3(\alpha_{l}+l-r+k))
by
Lemma

7.4
, since

\nu^{(k)}_{r+1}\leqslant\nu^{(1)}_{r+1}+3(k-2)+2\leqslant 3(\alpha_{l}+l-r+k-2)+2.

As a result,
4.7
shows that
\nu^{(k)}
is not homogeneous for some
k
(and hence
\lambda
is not homogeneous) unless the tuple
(a_{1},\dots,a_{l+1})
has the form
(1,2^{t},1,2^{t},\dots,1,2^{t},1)
for some
t\geqslant 1
. So assume this is the case. Now the fact that
a_{1}<2
implies
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{l}
. By
6.14
we can then assume that
\alpha=(1^{2})
or
\varnothing
. In the first case
\lambda=(9,7)
, which can be ruled out looking at the known decomposition matrices. So we may assume that
\alpha=\varnothing
. If there are at least three values of
s
for which
a_{s}=1
, then we use a degree argument: specifically, we claim that there is a partition
\mu
such that
\mu\vartriangleright\lambda
,
\mu^{\mathrm{reg}}=\lambda^{\mathrm{reg}}
,
\mu_{1}=\lambda_{1}
and
\operatorname{ddim}(\mu)>\operatorname{ddim}(\lambda)
, which will show that
\lambda
is not homogeneous. We prove this by induction on the number of
1
s in the tuple
(a_{1},\dots,a_{l+1})
. If there are at least four
1
s, then we can use
Lemma

5.2
and induction, so assume
(a_{1},\dots,a_{l+1})=(1,2^{t},1,2^{t},1)
. Then we use
Lemma

5.10
.

We are left with the case where
(a_{1},\dots,a_{l+1})=(1,2^{l-1},1)
. Suppose first that
l\geqslant 6
. Then

\nu^{(k)}=(6l-4\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\dots.}}3l+2,\ 3l,\ 3l-4\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{\dots.}}2).

Now by
Lemma

5.11

\nu^{(k)}
is not homogeneous, and hence neither is
\lambda
.

So we are left with the cases
2\leqslant l\leqslant 5
. If
l=2
then
\lambda=(6,4)
, which can be dealt with using known decomposition matrices. For
3\leqslant l\leqslant 5
,
5.16
shows that
\lambda
is not homogeneous.

Case 6.3

Suppose that
a_{1}<2
and there is no
r\geqslant 2
for which
a_{r}=2
and
a_{r-1}>0
. Suppose in addition that
l\geqslant 3
and there is
r\geqslant 4
such that
a_{r}\neq 1
. Since
a_{1}<2
we have
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{l}
. Since
l\geqslant 3
we can then assume from
6.14
that
\alpha=\varnothing
. Now
\lambda
is not homogeneous by
5.14
.

Case 6.4

If neither Case 6.2 nor Case 6.3 applies, then the tuple
(a_{1},\dots,a_{l+1})
has the form
(1,1,\dots,1)
,
(0,2,1,1,\dots,1)
or
(1,0,2,1,1,\dots,1)
. Again
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{l}
, so by
6.14

\alpha=(1^{2})
or
\varnothing
. In the first case
\lambda\in\{(9,6),(8,7)\}
(as
a_{3}=1
in this case by assumption) which can both be ruled out looking at known decomposition matrices. So we may again assume that
\alpha=\varnothing
. These cases can be dealt with using
Lemmas

5.7
,
5.8
and
5.9
, except for the following small cases.

\diamond

(6,3)
,
(5,4)
and
(8,7,3)
can be shown to be not homogeneous using known decomposition matrices.

\diamond

(11,10,6,3)
,
(14,13,9,6,3)
and
(17,16,12,9,6,3)
are not homogeneous by
5.16
.

This completes the proof of
3.5
.
