Energy versus Output Quality of Non-volatile Writes in Intermittent Computing
Rei Barjami, Antonio Miele, Luca Mottola

Abstract
. We explore how to improve the energy performance of battery-less IOT ( IOT ) devices at the cost of a reduction in the quality of the output. Battery-less IOT devices are extremely resource-constrained energy-harvesting devices. Due to erratic energy patterns from the ambient, their executions become intermittent ; periods of active computation are interleaved by periods of recharging small energy buffers. To cross periods of energy unavailability, a device persists application and system state onto NVM ( NVM ) in anticipation of energy failures. We purposely control the energy invested in these operations, representing a major energy overhead, when using STT-MRAM ( STT-MRAM ) as NVM . As a result, we abate the corresponding overhead, yet introduce write errors. Based on 1.9 + 1.9+ trillion experimental data points, we illustrate whether this is a gamble worth taking, when, and where. We measure the energy consumption and quality of output obtained from the execution of nine diverse benchmarks on top of seven different platforms. Our results allow us to draw three key observations : i) the trade-off between energy saving and reduction of output quality is program-specific ; ii) the same trade-off is a function of a platform’s specific compute efficiency and power figures ; and iii) data encoding and input size impact a program’s resilience to errors. As a paradigmatic example, we reveal cases where we achieve up to 50% reduction in energy consumption with negligible effects on output quality, as opposed to settings where a minimal energy gain causes drastic drops in output quality.



1.
Introduction

Ambient energy harvesting allows
IOT
devices to eliminate their dependency on traditional batteries
(
Bhatti et al. 2016
)
.
This enables drastic reductions of maintenance costs and previously unattainable deployments, operating completely unattended for multiple years
(
Chen et al. 2017
;
Chiang et al. 2018
;
Ikeda et al. 2020
;
Afanasov et al. 2020
;
Denby et al. 2023
)

However, energy from the environment is generally erratic, causing frequent and unanticipated energy failures.
For example, harvesting energy from RF transmissions to compute a simple CRC application may lead to
16
energy failures over a
6
second period
(
Bhatti et al. 2016
)
.
Executions thus become
intermittent
, as they consist of intervals of active operation interleaved by possibly long periods of recharging energy buffers
(
Bhatti et al. 2016
)
.

Figure 1.
Intermittent execution.

Computing intermittently.
Fig.
1
shows an example execution.
The ambient charges an onboard small energy buffer, such as a capacitor, until the energy buffer voltage level
V_{\mathit{on}}
is reached, causing the device to power on.
The device senses, computes, and communicates as long as the energy buffer voltage level charge remains above a threshold
V_{\mathit{off}}
.
The device then switches off, waiting for the energy buffer to reach
V_{\mathit{on}}
again.

Battery-less
IOT
devices are
extremely resource constrained

(
Hester and Sorber 2017b
)
: they typically feature 16- or 32-bit
MCU
with kilobytes of main memory.
Applications run on bare hardware
without operating system support
.
Because of this, energy failures normally cause a device to lose application and system state.
To ensure forward progress across energy failures, a variety of techniques exists that make use of
persistent state
stored on
NVM
, as we elaborate in Sec.
2
.
Persistent state is loaded back from
NVM
when energy is back, so executions resume close to the point of energy failure rather than performing a complete reboot.

The price to pay to dump application and system state on
NVM
is
enormous
, especially in
energy
.
The corresponding overhead may reach up to 350% the cost of the application processing, mainly due to the use of energy-hungry
NVM
technology
(
Van Der Woude and Hicks 2016
)
.
Using persistent state to cross energy failures has further implications.
When using
FRAM
(
FRAM
) as
NVM
, for example, wait cycles may be necessary to synchronize read/write operations with the
MCU
, further increasing energy consumption
(
Instruments 2018
)
.
Finally, if the system employs stateful peripherals, their state is to be retained across energy failures too
(
Berthou et al. 2018
;
Arreola et al. 2018
;
Maeng and Lucia 2019
;
Branco et al. 2019
)
.
This increases the size of persistent state that must include information that may not be reflected in the system’s main memory, adding to the energy overhead.

Trading energy for output quality.
Our work starts from the observation that a number of
IOT
applications, as in smart health
(
Solanas et al. 2014
)
, ambient intelligence
(
Cook et al. 2009
)
, and environment monitoring
(
Othman and Shazali 2012
)

inherently tolerate approximate results
.
This stems from the nature of data processing in these applications, including computer vision, machine learning, signal processing, and pattern identification.
These algorithms offer probabilistic guarantees in the first place and are resilient to data errors, for example, due to sensor inaccuracies
(
Mittal 2016
)
.

Modern
NVM
technology, such as
STT-MRAM
, is a stepping stone to take advantage of these characteristics.
We can pilot
STT-MRAM
chips during write operations to reduce energy consumption, specifically, by applying a variable current intensity.
Due to
stochastic switching

(
Devolder et al. 2008
)
of
STT-MRAM
cells, further described in Sec.
2
, lower current intensity applied during write operations causes
write errors
that are randomly distributed.
Write errors inevitably affect the
quality of the output
the system eventually produces.

Using the knob provided by controlling energy consumption of
STT-MRAM
at the cost of write errors, here we seek to answer a fundamental question, as we articulate in Sec.
3
:

What are the applications, system settings, and hardware platforms where the trade-off between energy savings and reduction in output quality is favorable?

Our technique resonates with approximate computing techniques
(
Mittal 2016
)
aimed at
introducing controlled errors to save resources
.
What we do here is fundamentally different, however.
Application requirements normally dictate rigid lower bounds on output quality
(
Han and Orshansky 2013
)
.
Approximate computing techniques aim at ensuring this minimum quality by saving the greatest resources, for example, energy.
In our case, the problem is reversed.
Applications may not impose minimum accuracy requirements and be satisfied with whatever is attainable, whereas
a strict upper bound on energy consumption
exists due to the finite energy buffers.
The notion of output quality, however, the way it is measured, and the definition of acceptable bounds are inherently application-specific.
This is not only germane to our work, but a general characteristic of
approximate computing

(
Mittal 2016
;
Han and Orshansky 2013
)
.

Results and take-aways.
Gathering experimental data to answer the question is a challenge per se.
MCUs with integrated
STT-MRAM
are commercially unavailable, forcing system designers to build ad-hoc integrations of off-the-shelf MCUs and
STT-MRAM
chips.
The extreme variety of
MCU
employed for battery-less
IOT
devices depending on computing power, energy consumption, and form factor would also make this a one-off exercise.
Even if concrete hardware was at disposal, the number of different benchmarks, system configurations, and inputs required to build a solid experimental foundation would render running tests on real hardware an endless effort.

To address this issue, we design an experimental framework, described in Sec.
4
, by combining state-of-the-art
MCU
emulators, accurate
NVM
estimation tools, carefully-selected profiling on real hardware, and executions on mainstream computing machines.
Based on existing literature and the widespread use of these instruments
(
Hester and Sorber 2017b
;
Eriksson et al. 2009
;
Dong et al. 2012
)
, the energy figures we obtain, along with the corresponding estimation of the output quality, are arguably on par with real hardware.

Based on
1.9+
trillion experimental data points, we draw
three fundamental observations
, further discussed in Sec.
5
:

(1)

the trade-off between energy saving and reduction of output quality is
program-specific
: we test benchmarks where we achieve up to 50% reduction in energy consumption with negligible effects on output quality, as opposed to settings where a minimal energy gain causes drastic drops in output quality.

(2)

the same trade-off is a function of a
platform’s specific compute efficiency and power figures
: in the same setting as the previous point, changing the target platform halves the reduction in energy consumption.

(3)

data encoding
and
input size
impact a program’s resilience to errors: in the case of neural network benchmarks, as an example, non-quantized networks quickly degrade the output quality as even a single bit error may have disastrous effects.

We end the paper in Sec.
6
with brief concluding remarks.

2.
Related Work and Background

Our work intersects different areas, including energy harvesting, low-power embedded computing, and hardware architectures.
We briefly survey existing literature relevant to the following discussion and contrast that with our own work.

2.1.
Intermittent Computing

Operations to persist application, system, and peripheral states entirely represent
an overhead
since they consume energy exclusively to let a program cross energy failures, yet without contributing to making progress in the application logic.
Taming this overhead is the primary focus of a major fraction of existing works in intermittent computing.

Some solutions employ a form of checkpointing
(
Ransford et al. 2011
;
Balsamo et al. 2015
;
Balsamo et al. 2016
;
Maeng and Lucia 2018
;
Bhatti and Mottola 2017
;
Ahmed et al. 2019
;
Van Der Woude and Hicks 2016
)
.
This consists in replicating the application state on
NVM
at specific points in the code, where it is retrieved back once the system resumes with sufficient energy.
Systems such as Hibernus
(
Balsamo et al. 2015
;
Balsamo et al. 2016
)
operate based on interrupts fired from a hardware device that prompts the application to take a checkpoint, for example, whenever the energy level falls below a threshold.
Differently, systems exist that place function calls in application code to proactively checkpoint
(
Ransford et al. 2011
;
Bhatti and Mottola 2017
;
Van Der Woude and Hicks 2016
;
Maeng and Lucia 2018
)
.
The specific placement is a function of program structure and energy patterns.

Other approaches offer abstractions that programmers use to define and manage persistent state
(
Lucia and Ransford 2015
;
Maeng et al. 2017
;
Colin and Lucia 2016
;
Majid et al. 2020
;
Yildirim et al. 2018
)
.
Most of these employ a
task
abstraction to structure the code.
A task is a single computational unit with defined inputs and outputs, which runs with
transactional semantics
and commits outputs on
NVM
upon completion.
Albeit requiring programmers to re-factor existing code, tasks allow one to precisely map the energy consumption of different code segments to the available hardware, for example, when using multiple capacitors
(
Colin et al. 2018
)
, as we illustrate next.

Our work is orthogonal to most existing literature in intermittent computing.
Saving energy by controlling the energy invested in
NVM
writes applies to both checkpoint and task-based systems, regardless of whether the data represents application, system, or peripheral states.
The key point is only to apply this technique to data that is error resilient: as we elaborate next, we cannot afford any error when persisting program control data, whereas data encoding images, audio, or signals is inherently subject to errors.

2.2.
Data Errors and Non-volatile Writes

As hinted above,
IOT
applications in diverse domains inherently tolerate approximate results.
The underlying data pipelines are resilient to errors by design.
For instance,
NN
used for image classification generally work properly also in the presence of noisy input images, as they provide probabilistic estimates in the first place.
The corresponding data pipelines also often provide various parameters, such as image resolution, that can be tuned to trade resource consumption against output quality.
These features become a stepping stone to design approximate computing techniques
(
Mittal 2016
)
aimed at
purposely introducing data errors to save resources
.

NVM
represent an alternative to
SRAM
(
SRAM
) technology with higher density and speed, lower energy consumption, and the ability to persist data.
As discussed in the International Technology Roadmap for Semiconductors report
(
International Technology Roadmap for Semiconductors (2013) ITRS
)
, due to higher energy efficiency compared to other
NVM
,
STT-MRAM
technology is an asset, especially for resource-constrained systems like energy-harvesting devices.
However,
STT-MRAM
suffers from
stochastic switching

(
Devolder et al. 2008
)
: during write operations, a memory cell may fail to commute to the new input value, thus causing a
write error
.
This phenomenon is shown to depend on the current intensity applied during writes
(
Monazzah et al. 2020
)
; the lower the intensity, the higher is the probability of an error.
Being the write errors stochastic in nature, errors are introduced in a random way, yielding a “salt and pepper” pattern in saved data.

Efforts exist where stochastic switching of
STT-MRAM
is used as a knob to gain resources when the memory is deployed as cache or scratchpad
(
Sayed et al. 2017
;
Ranjan et al. 2015
;
Monazzah et al. 2020
;
Seyedfaraji et al. 2022
)
.
To structure the analysis, these works define a limited set of
QL

(
Monazzah et al. 2020
)
, each one of them characterized by a specific write current intensity and thus by a defined probability of write errors per single memory bit write.
We do the same, as further explained in Sec.
4
, and define five distinct
QL
to configure the system ahead of running tests and discuss the corresponding results.
In this work, the setting of quality level is the tunable knob to trade energy consumption for output quality.

Unlike existing literature, however, here we explore the use of
STT-MRAM
with current scaling in an intermittent system, using
STT-MRAM
to persist states.
Current scaling resonates with near-threshold computing
(
Dreslinski et al. 2010
)
, which however controls supply voltage and not current, and is most often applied to computing units and not memories, especially low-power ones.
Overall, we may regard this technique as an instance of approximate intermittent computing
(
Bambusi et al. 2022
)
working close to the hardware and not requiring changes to algorithms.

3.
Problem and Objectives

We describe the essential features of the platforms we target and their configuration, articulate the benefits we obtain from controlling the
QL
in
STT-MRAM
writes, and weigh them against the cost of reducing the output quality.
We conclude by formulating the problem we tackle and the corresponding objectives.

3.1.
Platforms and Configurations

Figure 2.
Abstract representation of target platform.

We consider a platform composed of a resource-constrained
MCU
using
STT-MRAM
as a non-volatile scratchpad memory, as abstractly depicted in Fig.
2
.
Concrete examples of target
MCU
are the MSP430 or Cortex M series, as seen in real deployments of battery-less
IOT
systems
(
Chen et al. 2017
;
Chiang et al. 2018
;
Ikeda et al. 2020
;
Afanasov et al. 2020
;
Denby et al. 2023
)
.
To achieve energy efficiency, these MCUs are extremely primitive in hardware features and lack, for example, functionality such as
DMA
(
DMA
).

Using the
STT-MRAM
as a scratchpad memory, while tuning the current intensity during write operations, requires extreme care.
If errors are introduced on arbitrary program data, chances are that errors may also corrupt data affecting the program’s execution flow.
This would be the case, for example, if errors affected control variables in a program, say causing unwanted jumps in the code.
We exclude this kind of data from write operations where we tune the current intensity, and tune write currents only with data that is potentially resilient to errors, as in the case of imagery or sensor data.
This may be achieved on the same
STT-MRAM
chip: it is possible to divide the memory into segments, each with a different write current
(
Monazzah et al. 2020
)
.
We reserve one segment to control data with a (probabilistic) guarantee that no errors are introduced.

In line with state-of-the-art intermittent systems, we consider task-based programming, capacitors as energy buffers, and a multi-capacitor setup
(
Colin et al. 2018
)
, as shown in Fig.
2
.
The latter allows designers to optimize capacitor size based on the energy demands of the tasks in a program.
A large capacitor is used to store the energy required for the execution of energy-hungry tasks, whereas smaller capacitors are used for tasks with lower energy demands.
This improves the general performance because it better matches energy availability and demand.
Large capacitors are characterized by slower recharge times and high leakage.
They are only used when necessary, that is, to support energy-hungry tasks.
If tasks can run to completion with smaller energy budgets, they rely on smaller capacitors with shorter recharge times and reduced leakage
(
Colin et al. 2018
;
Hester and Sorber 2017a
)
.

3.2.
Benefits

Figure 3.
At the top, we have an example of task execution, while at the bottom, we explore two methods to enhance the system’s performance. In the bottom-left, we consider reducing the capacitor dimension, and in the bottom-right, we consider prolonging the code execution.

Intermittent systems persist the state on
NVM
to cross energy failures.
The upper side of Fig.
3
qualitatively illustrates the energy patterns during an example task execution.
The
MCU
consumes a variable portion of the available energy for executing code, represented as the blue area in Fig.
3
, thus making progress in the application logic.
In anticipation of an energy failure, application, system, and peripheral state are persisted on
NVM
when the task completes.
Because of the lack of features such as
DMA
, the
MCU
remains active performing the data transfer. During these operations, the energy consumed by the
MCU
adds to the energy spent in
NVM
writes.

Persisting the state thus subtracts energy that could be rather invested in useful processing and hence represents an overhead that should be minimized.
This overhead corresponds to the red area in Fig.
3
, which is a function of current intensity, write latency, and operating voltage of the
STT-MRAM
chip.
All other memory operating settings staying the same, we can shrink the red area by piloting the current intensity.

One may invest the energy we save to improve the system’s performance in two ways:

(1)

Using a smaller capacitor while keeping the same energy invested in executing code, as shown in the bottom left Fig.
3
. If the red area shrinks, the total amount of energy required to complete a task, represented as the sum of the red and blue areas, also diminishes.
As smaller capacitors recharge faster and enjoy reduced leakage, the system throughput increases.

(2)

Shifting the energy gained by shrinking the red area to the blue area, as shown in the bottom right of Fig.
3
, that is, to code execution.
Keeping capacitors fixed, gains in the energy required for
STT-MRAM
writes may be invested in making a task accomplish additional useful work; by doing so, a program may finish the workload sooner, again increasing the system’s throughput.

3.3.
Drawbacks

Tuning the
STT-MRAM
write current comes with a major drawback, write errors may occur that affect the quality of the output, possibly rendering the system useless.

Fully understanding the impact of these errors is non-trivial.
Task-based programming is a form of data-flow programming
(
Halbwachs et al. 1991
)
.
A single write error occurrence when persisting the output of a task early in the pipeline may propagate as an incorrect value throughout multiple tasks downstream, each taking as input the output of the previous one.
This would create a snowball effect, intuitively described in Fig.
4
, ultimately heavily affecting the final output.

Figure 4.
Error snowball effect, the red area represents corrupted data.

Two aspects are, therefore, to be taken into account:
i)
a single write error does not necessarily result in only one erroneous value in the final output, and
ii)
not all write errors bear the same impact on the final output.
This is especially true when dealing with bit flips, as it happens on
STT-MRAM
; trivially, if the bit switch occurs on the most relevant bit of a value, the effect is certainly more disruptive than if it occurs on the least significant one.

These observations lead us to realize that providing a statistically sound assessment of the effects of tuning write currents of
STT-MRAM
output quality requires running a substantial number of experiments.
Each experiment may have errors that affect the execution differently, emphasizing the need for a comprehensive evaluation.

3.4.
Problem

The problem we address is therefore to
quantify the trade-off between the energy savings enabled by tuning write currents of
STT-MRAM
and the reduction in the output quality
.
This is a multi-faceted problem, because
i)
the factors ultimately determining the energy savings are multiple, and
ii)
the notion of output quality is application-specific.

As for
i)
, the energy consumed by a battery-less
IOT
device to execute a given task given a sufficient energy budget is

(1)

E_{total}=E_{MCUcompute}+E_{MCUpersist}+E_{storage}

where
E_{MCUcompute}
is the energy spent by the MCU in useful computations, shown in blue in Fig.
3
,
E_{MCUpersist}
is the energy spent by the MCU while the
STT-MRAM
writes data, shown in green in Fig.
3
, and
E_{storage}
is the energy spent by the
STT-MRAM
when writing data, shown in red in Fig.
3
.

Each term in Eq.
1
may be rewritten, piece by piece, depending on the characteristics of the hardware at hand, leading to

(2)

\begin{split}E_{total}&=E_{cycle}\cdot\#\mathit{cycles}+E_{cycle}\cdot\#\mathit{memAccesses}\cdot+E_{memWrite}\cdot\#\mathit{memAccesses}\\
&=E_{cycle}\cdot\#\mathit{cycles}+(E_{cycle}+E_{memWrite})\cdot\#\mathit{memAccesses}\end{split}

where
E_{cycle}
is the MCU energy for a single compute cycle,
\#\mathit{cycles}
is the number of MCU cycles to complete the tasks chosen for execution,
\#\mathit{memAccesses}
is the number of memory accesses during state persistence operations, and
E_{memWrite}
is the write energy for a single memory access.

Given a task
T
of a program, we make explicit how the quantities of Eq.
2
depend on multiple software and hardware factors as

(3)

\begin{split}E_{cycle}=f(\mathit{MCU_{activePower},\mathit{MCU_{frequency}}}),\\
\#\mathit{cycles}=f(MCU_{ISA},T),\\
\#\mathit{memAccesses}=f(T),\\
E_{memWrite}=f(\mathit{QL}).\end{split}

where
MCU_{activePower}
and
MCU_{ISA}
capture the specific power figures and instruction set architecture of the chosen
MCU
,
\mathit{MCU_{frequency}}
is the
MCU
operating frequency, and
\mathit{QL}
is the selected
QL
for
STT-MRAM
writes, corresponding to a specific current setting, as in Sec.
2
.

To understand the trade-offs at hand, we must quantify the energy gains as the
QL
changes, depending on the executing task and target
MCU
.
The latter two are the key experimental parameters driving the measurements we take, based on the methodology explained next.

4.2.
Framework

Figure 5.
Experimental framework.

Commercial
MCU
do not integrate
STT-MRAM
chips yet.
Even if such integration was available, however, the number of different benchmarks, system configurations, and inputs we test would make employing real hardware impractical and extremely time consuming.

Conceptual design.
We combine state-of-the-art
MCU
emulators, accurate
NVM
estimation tools, carefully-selected profiling on real hardware, and executions on mainstream computing machines, as shown in Fig.
5
.
Given a benchmark and a target hardware platform, the framework allows us to measure the energy consumption of the hardware and the corresponding
QoR
, as defined in Tab.
1
, while applying the different
QL
to
STT-MRAM
writes.

We measure the system’s energy consumption by means of the energy model presented in Sec.
3
.
In fact, the development boards hosting
MCU
generally do not provide power sensors for the specific chip, while they also integrate many other debug and utility components that would not be present in a deployed system; as a consequence, ammeters and the like, when hooked to the board, would provide biased measurements.

We compute the system’s energy consumption as the sum of
MCU
and
STT-MRAM
energy.
The
MCU
we consider feature very simple architectures with no dynamic voltage/frequency scaling; their energy consumption is a simple function of power consumption and the number of cycles to execute a workload.
Power consumption is retrieved from an
MCU
datasheet.
To compute the number of cycles, we profile a benchmark’s execution to extract a trace of machine instructions and memory accesses.
We perform multiple runs to average out the effects of different inputs.

The model we employ for
STT-MRAM
computes the energy consumption by multiplying the energy consumption of a single bit read and write by the overall number of performed operations.
The energy consumption of read operations is retrieved from the
STT-MRAM
datasheet.
For write operations, energy consumption is specific to each
QL
; we explain next how we obtain this value and the corresponding error probability for a single bit write.
Finally, we extract the number of write/read operations from the execution trace above.

The crucial issue is that the evaluation of the
QoR
of a benchmark at a given
QL
requires write errors to be somehow simulated.
Since write errors are stochastic, as common practice in reliability analysis of faulty digital systems
(
Bolchini et al. 2023
)
, we perform a large number of runs of the considered benchmark on the target architecture.
For each such execution, we compute the selected quality metric by comparing the concrete benchmark output, which may be corrupted by write errors on
NVM
, with the
golden
counterpart, that is, the output we collect during the profiling execution above, where no
NVM
write errors occur.
We use multiple different inputs, as explained next, to factor out any bias due to the specific input, and average the
QoR
over all inputs we test.

The outcome of the framework is, for a given benchmark and
MCU
, the corresponding energy consumption, and resulting
QoR
for a specified
QL
.
By performing the presented flow for all considered
QL
, it is possible to obtain a mapping between each
QL
, the corresponding energy consumption, and the resulting
QoR
.
This mapping is the basis for the analytical evaluation of the trade-off between energy savings and output quality.

Implementation.

We use NVSIM
(
Dong et al. 2012
)
to characterize the
STT-MRAM
and obtain its energy figures.
NVSIM is a widely used
NVM
simulator (e.g., in
(
Monazzah et al. 2020
)
) that allows system designers to characterize
STT-MRAM
devices, and therefore to estimate read/write currents and energy consumption for a given chip configuration.
We borrow the mapping between the energy invested for write operations at a given
QL
and the corresponding
WER
(
WER
) for a single bit from existing characterizations
(
Monazzah et al. 2020
)
.

For MSP430
MCU
, we profile the benchmark executions with MSPSim
(
Eriksson et al. 2009
)
, a widely-used cycle-accurate MSP430 emulator.
MSPSim provides functionality to extract the execution trace.
On the other hand, we use real development kits from ST Microelectronics to profile the benchmarks on top of Cortex M
MCU
.
We use hardware counters and on-board debugging facilities to count the number of executed cycles.

As hinted earlier, a solid evaluation of the
QoR
for a benchmark at different
QL
requires a sufficiently large number of runs, which would be unfeasible on real hardware.
We define an application-level error injection approach to this end.
Being
STT-MRAM
operations directly mapped to variable writes, we instrument the benchmark source code with an error injection facility corrupting the bits during each variable assignment with the set
WER
probability.
As this error injection strategy is independent of the hardware running the benchmark, as long as the data encoding is the same, it is possible to execute error simulations on any high-ended workstations and yet obtain the same results of the target
MCU
, but with multi-fold reductions of the experiment time.
We instrument
OnlyWrites
,
FFT
,
PicoJpeg
, and
Susan
in a semi-automated way, as their source code is quite simple.
The
NN
we consider are implemented with TensorFlow; here we integrate the the error simulation facility in a completely automated way by relying on an existing technique designed to achieve an almost identical effect
(
Bolchini et al. 2023
)
.

The

Quantization is commonly performed when deploying a
NN

This choice is guided by key critical considerations:

(1)

Non-quantized
NN
are more demanding in terms of resources, necessitating a minimum of four times the memory compared to their quantized counterparts. In the context of intermittent systems, which inherently face resource constraints, as others did already, we observe that several
NN
chosen for our evaluation would exceed the storage capacity of the selected platforms otherwise.
This is not just our own observation
(
Gobieski et al. 2019
;
Islam and Nirjon 2020
)
.

(2)

NN
with 8-bit integer quantization exhibit better error resilience compared to their non-quantized counterpart, because intermediate values have a restricted numerical range. This property is widely discussed in the investigation on
NN
resiliency
(
Chen et al. 2021
)
, when it is observed that restricting the numerical range of the
NN
intermediate values reduces the deviation caused by bit flips to a level that can be tolerated.

Fig.

compares the results of error simulation for two quantized
NN
compared to their non-quantized counterparts.
Our experiments confirm the error resilience properties of
NN
.
We note that the accuracy of non-quantized networks, even at a
Q1
setting, is lower than the accuracy achieved at
Q3
on the quantized network. This trend holds true for other networks we experimented with, demonstrating a consistent behavior across multiple settings.

Take-away.
Quantization of
NN
is necessary not just to meet memory requirements of battery-less
IOT
devices, but it
becomes an asset to limit the degradation of output quality
, compared to their non-quantized counterparts, when state persistence operations are subject to errors.

6.
Conclusion

We explored the trade-off between the energy gains attainable by piloting the write current of
STT-MRAM
and the reduction of output quality due to the resulting write errors.
We target battery-less
IOT
devices powered by energy harvesting, where computations become intermittent because of energy failures.
We use
STT-MRAM
to persist the state necessary to cross energy failures.
We measured the energy consumption at different current settings and the output quality obtained from the execution of nine diverse benchmarks on top of seven different platforms.
Based on
1.972\cdot 10^{12}
experimental data points, we drew
four key observations
and
corresponding take-aways
:

i)

the trade-off between energy saving and reduction of output quality is
program-specific
;

ii)

the same trade-off is a function of a
platform’s specific compute efficiency and power figures
; and

iii)

data encoding
and
input size
impact a program’s resilience to errors.
