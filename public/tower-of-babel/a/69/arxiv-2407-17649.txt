Quantum Algorithms for Representation-Theoretic Multiplicities
Martín Larocca, Vojtech Havlicek

Abstract
Kostka, Littlewood-Richardson, Plethysm and Kronecker coefficients are the multiplicities of irreducible representations in the decomposition of representations of the symmetric group that play an important role in representation theory, geometric complexity and algebraic combinatorics. We give quantum algorithms for computing these coefficients whenever the ratio of dimensions of the representations is polynomial and study the computational complexity of this problem. We show that there is an efficient classical algorithm for computing the Kostka numbers under this restriction and conjecture the existence of an analogous algorithm for the Littlewood-Richardson coefficients. We argue why such classical algorithm does not straightforwardly work for the Plethysm and Kronecker coefficients and conjecture that our quantum algorithms lead to superpolynomial speedups for these problems. The conjecture about Kronecker coefficients was disproved by Greta Panova in [arXiv:2502.20253] with a classical solution which, if optimal, points to a 𝒪 ⁡ ( n 4 + 2 ​ k ) \mathcal{O}(n^{4+2k}) vs Ω ~ ​ ( n 4 ​ k 2 + 1 ) \tilde{\Omega}(n^{4k^{2}+1}) polynomial gap in quantum vs classical computational complexity for an integer parameter k k .

Introduction.–

Is there a computational problem that admits efficient quantum algorithms but no efficient randomized one?
Quantum computing is driven by the hypothesis that the answer is yes, to a large extent because –
“…our best description of the physical world is quantum-mechanical and quantum mechanics seems hard to simulate by classical computers…”

[
2
]
.
This intuition often comes with limited backing for specific computational problems and supporting it rigorously faces significant challenges in computational complexity theory. It would be ideal to prove that a decision problem admits efficient quantum algorithms but no randomized one, but doing that unconditionally entails showing that
{\mathsf{BPP}}\subset{\mathsf{BQP}}
and in consequence separating
{\mathsf{BPP}}
and
{\mathsf{PSPACE}}

{\mathsf{BPP}}

[

-hard or

-complete, but the evidence for their hardness is that we use factoring and discrete logarithm for public-key cryptography
[

Kostka Numbers.—

The Kostka number

Theorem 2

Littlewood-Richardson Coefficients.–

The quantum algorithm for Littlewood-Richardson (LR) coefficients fixes
H=S_{a}\times S_{b}\subseteq G=S_{n}
for
a+b=n
. The irreducible representations of
S_{a}\times S_{b}
are a tensor product of irreducibles
r_{S_{a}}^{\lambda}\otimes r_{S_{b}}^{\mu}
, labeled by pairs
\alpha=(\lambda,\mu)
with
\lambda,\mu\vdash a,b
. We have
c^{\nu}_{\lambda\mu}={\rm\mathbb{mult}}(r^{\lambda}_{S_{a}}\otimes r^{\mu}_{S_{b}},r^{\nu}_{S_{n}}\downarrow^{S_{n}}_{S_{a}\times S_{b}})
, and from Thm.
1
, the algorithm samples from:

\displaystyle p_{\nu}(\lambda,\mu)

\displaystyle=c^{\nu}_{\lambda\mu}\frac{d_{\lambda}d_{\mu}}{d_{\nu}}.

(12)

This computes the LR coefficient in
\mathcal{O}\left(n^{4}\left(\frac{d_{\nu}}{d_{\lambda}d_{\mu}}\right)^{2}\right)
gate complexity.

LR coefficients count combinatorial objects called the LR tableaux. A skew shape
\nu/\lambda
is a diagram where
\lambda
is subtracted from
\nu

and a skew semistandard Young Tableau is a skew shape filled with integers such that the entries are non-decreasing along every row and strictly increasing along every column. The LR tableau is a skew Young tableau of shape
\nu/\lambda
whose row-reading evaluates to
\mu
. The LR coefficient
c^{\nu}_{\lambda\mu}
is the number of such tableaux; this fact is called the LR rule. See
[
24
,
25
,
26
]
and the Supp. Mat. for details (esp. definition of ‘row reading’). The quantum algorithm in Thm.
1
is only efficient for
c^{\nu}_{\lambda\mu}\leq{\rm poly}(n)
in which case it counts only up to
{\rm poly}(n)
many LR tableaux.

2

Frobenius’s reciprocity

Discussion.–

We gave quantum algorithms for Kostka, Littlewood-Richardson, Kronecker and Plethysm coefficients, efficient on a nontrivial subset of inputs.
We gave a polynomial-time classical algorithm for computing of the Kostka numbers
K^{\mu}_{\nu}
whenever
d_{\nu}\leq{\rm poly}(n)
, ruling out a superpolynomial quantum speedup for this problem and conjectured the existence of a similar classical algorithm for the Littlewood-Richardson coefficients.
We explained why such classical algorithm does not straightforwardly extend to the Plethysm and Kronecker coefficients and conjectured that our quantum algorithms efficiently solve problems that are hard for classical algorithms. Going against our thesis is a result in Ref.
[
44
]
in which Jordan presented a quantum algorithm for approximating
S_{n}
characters to a similar precision as our Thm.
1
and gave an efficient classical algorithm based on the Roichman’s rule
[
45
]
. Our Conjecture
2
followed a similar fate and was disproved by an algorithm in
[
27
, Theorem 1.]
. The runtime of this algorithm is
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{4k^{2}+1})
which, if tight, suggests a possible
{\mathcal{O}}(n^{4+2k})
vs
\tilde{\Omega}(n^{4k^{2}+1})
speedup on inputs with
d_{\nu}=d_{\lambda}
and
d_{\mu}\leq n^{k}
. It is thus important to address Question
1
and understand if the large polynomial gap for computing Kronecker coefficient closes. Conjecture
1
was put under similar scrutiny: there are significant limitations to regimes where the algorithm can offer a superpolynomial speedup, but large polynomial speedups may be still possible.

We note that, Ref.
[
46
]
showed that some of the multiplicity coefficients considered here appear naturally in spin chains. It remains an interesting question if this approach could provide more insight into this class of quantum algorithms.

We can obtain a characterization of
S_{n}
-irreps
r^{\lambda}_{S_{n}}(g)
in the following way. First, note that
S_{n}
is generated by adjacent transpositions
S_{n}=\langle\{\sigma_{i}=(i,i+1)\}_{i\in[n-1]}\rangle
. Thus, any
g\in S_{n}
can be decomposed into a sequence of adjacent transpositions, i.e., as
g=\prod_{k=1}^{N}\sigma_{i_{k}}
, and

r^{\lambda}(g)=\prod_{k=1}^{N}r^{\lambda}(\sigma_{i_{k}})

(29)

where
N
is at most
\binom{n}{2}\in\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
. To see this, think of “sorting” a permutation using adjacent swaps (as in bubble sort). Each adjacent swap removes at most one inversion. The worst-case scenario is the reverse permutation, which has
\binom{n}{2}
inversions. Instead, the
k
-cycle
(1,2,\cdots,k)
can be written as
(1,2)\cdots(k-1,k)
, i.e., using
k-1
adjacent transpositions – so cycles require
\mathcal{O}(n)
adjacent transpositions in worst case.

Once we’ve decomposed into adjacent transpositions
\sigma_{i}
, we can leverage the fact that their action on irrep basis elements
\ket{T}\in\mathcal{S}^{\lambda}
is sparse (see e.g., Eq. 28 in Ref.
[
50
]
):

r^{\lambda}_{S_{n}}(\sigma_{i})\ket{T}=\frac{1}{\Delta_{i}(T)}\ket{T}+\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{\Delta_{i}(T)}^{2}}\ket{\sg_i \cdot T}

(30)

in terms of the axial distance
\Delta_{i}(T)={\rm cont}_{i+1}(T)-{\rm cont}_{i}(T)
, where
{\rm cont}_{i}(T)={\rm col}_{i}(T)-{\rm row}_{i}(T)
is the content of cell
i
in
T
, the difference between the column and row indexes of the box with
i
in
T
.

For our purposes, it will be handy to have a re-derivation of Eq. (
30
) in terms of Yamanouchi words. The row index of
i
in
x
is given by
{\rm row}_{i}(x)=x_{i}
, since the letter
x_{i}
directly tells you the row number where
i
should be placed. The column index of
i
in
x
is given by
{\rm col}_{i}(x)=t_{i}(x^{i})-x_{i}
. To see this, note that the tableau is filled in so that within each row the entries are strictly increasing from left to right. This means that when you place the number
i
in row
x_{i}
, you put it in the leftmost free (i.e., next available) box in that row. In other words, the column in which
i
is placed is determined by how many times the row
x_{i}
has already been used for earlier entries. Finally, the content of
i
in
x
is given by

\displaystyle{\rm cont}_{i}(x)

\displaystyle={\rm col}_{i}(x)-{\rm row}_{i}(x)

(31)

\displaystyle=t_{i}(x^{i})-2x_{i}\,,

(32)

and the axial distance is

\displaystyle\Delta_{i}(x)

\displaystyle={\rm cont}_{i+1}(x)-{\rm cont}_{i}(x)

(33)

\displaystyle=(t_{i+1}(x^{i+1})-t_{i}(x^{i}))+2(x_{i}-x_{i+1})\,.

(34)

We thus have

r^{\lambda}_{S_{n}}(\sigma_{i})\ket{x}=\frac{1}{\Delta_{i}(x)}\ket{x}+\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{\Delta_{i}(x)}^{2}}\ket{\sg_i \cdot x}

(35)

where
\ket{\sg_i\cdot x}=\ket{x_1 \cdots x_{i+1}x_i \cdots x_n}
. Note that
\sigma_{i}\cdot x
could, or could not, be a Yamanouchi word. In case its not, the coefficient
\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{\Delta_{i}^{2}}}
will vanish. We will typically encounter the situation where, given a Yamanouchi word
x
of type
\lambda\vdash n
, we are asked to produce the unique Yamanouchi word
x_{\mu}
of type
\mu\in\lambda+\Box
with prefix
x_{\mu}^{n}=x
. This is illustrated by the following example:

Example 1

Let
\lambda=(3,2)
and
\mu=(3,3)\in\lambda+\Box
. Then:

\displaystyle x=12112

\displaystyle\mapsto x^{\prime}=121122,

\displaystyle x=12121

\displaystyle\mapsto x^{\prime}=121212\,.

I.2
The hook-length formula

The dimension
d_{\lambda}
of an
S_{n}
irreducible representation
\lambda
can be computed in time
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
using the hook-length formula. It states that:

\displaystyle d_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\frac{n!}{\prod_{(i,j)\in\lambda}h_{\lambda}(i,j)},

(37)

where the product runs over all boxes in
\lambda
and
h_{\lambda}(i,j)
is the hook-length of the
(i,j)
-th box: the number of boxes to the right of the box at position
(i,j)
in
\lambda
plus the number of boxes below
(i,j)
plus
1
. Although we state the runtime of our algorithm in terms of
S_{n}
irrep dimension ratios – unfortunately, there is not closed analytic formula for the runtime as a function of
n
–, we remark that given some input partitions one should be able to use Eq. (
37
) to efficiently estimate the number of samples needed and therefore the total runtime of the algorithm.

Fact 1

(Young’s Rule and Young’s Orthogonal Basis
[
25
]
)
.

Let
r^{\lambda}_{S_{n}}
be an irreducible representation of
S_{n}
. Its restriction to
S_{n-1}
contains
S_{n-1}
-irreducible representations with multiplicity at most

in the following way:

\displaystyle U\left(r^{\lambda}_{S_{n}}(h)\downarrow^{S_{n}}_{S_{n-1}}\right)U^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\bigoplus_{\nu\in\lambda-\Box}r^{\nu}_{S_{n-1}}(h),\,\forall h\,\in S_{n-1}.

(38)

Here
\nu-\Box

S_{n-1}
that can be obtained by deleting one box from the
\nu

and

such that

in Eq. (

Example 2
.

An irreducible representation

\displaystyle r^{(3,2,1)}_{S_{6}}\downarrow^{S_{6}}_{S_{5}}=r^{(2,2,1)}_{S_{5}}\oplus r^{(3,1,1)}_{S_{5}}\oplus r^{(3,2)}_{S_{5}}.

(39)

\displaystyle r^{\lambda}_{S_{n}}(th)=r^{\lambda}_{S_{n}}(t)r^{\lambda}_{S_{n}}(h)=r^{\lambda}_{S_{n}}(t)\left[r^{\lambda}_{S_{n}}(h)\downarrow^{S_{n}}_{S_{n-1}}\right]=r_{S_{n}}^{\lambda}(t)\left[\bigoplus_{\nu\in\lambda-\Box}r^{\nu}_{S_{n-1}}(h)\right],

(40)

One can recurse this as:

\displaystyle r^{\lambda_{n}}_{S_{n}}(g)

\displaystyle=r^{\lambda_{n}}_{S_{n}}(t_{n})\left[\bigoplus_{\lambda_{n-1}\in\lambda_{n}-\Box}r^{\lambda_{n-1}}_{S_{n-1}}(t_{n-1})\right]\left[\bigoplus_{\lambda_{n-2}\in\lambda_{n-1}-\Box}r^{\lambda_{n-2}}_{S_{n-2}}(t_{n-2})\right]\ldots\left[\bigoplus_{\lambda_{1}\in\lambda_{2}-\Box}r^{\lambda_{1}}_{S_{1}}(t_{1})\right],

\ket{f}=\sum_{g\in S_{n}}f(g)\ket{g}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\ket{F^j}
. Let us also define, for each
j\in[n]
,
f^{j}_{n-1}:S_{n-1}\xrightarrow{}\mathbb{C}
such that
f^{j}_{n-1}(h)=f(t_{n}^{j}h)
for all
h\in S_{n-1}
.

Consider a
transversals register

\mathcal{H}_{T_{n}}={\rm span}\{\ket{t_n^1},\cdots,\ket{t_n^n},\ket{*}\}
, where we use the cycles
t_{n}^{i}\equiv(i,i+1,\cdots,n-1,n)
as a choice of transversals for the cosets of
S_{n-1}
in
S_{n}
, and where
\ket{*}
is a dummy state. Once a choice of transversals is made, any
g\in S_{n}
has a unique decomposition
g=t_{n}t_{n-1}\cdots t_{2}
, where
t_{n}\in T_{n}
,
t_{n-1}\in T_{n-1}
and so on. Note the transversals register
\mathcal{H}_{T_{n}}
is
n+1
-dimensional, and denote
\mathcal{H}_{T_{n}}^{S_{n}/S_{n-1}}
the
n
-dimensional subspace that excludes the span of the dummy state:
\mathcal{H}_{T_{n}}^{S_{n}/S_{n-1}}={\rm span}\{t_{n}^{j}\}_{j\in[n]}\cong\mathbb{C}[S_{n}/S_{n-1}]
. Consider the composition of multiple transversal registers for the tower
S_{n}\supset S_{n-1}\supset\cdots\supset S_{2}
into

\mathcal{H}_{T^{n}}=\mathcal{H}_{T_{n}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{T_{n-1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathcal{H}_{T_{2}}

(43)

and note that it contains a subspace
\mathcal{H}_{T^{n}}^{S_{n}}=\mathcal{H}_{T^{n}}^{S_{n}/S_{n-1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathcal{H}_{T^{2}}^{S_{2}/S_{1}}\cong\mathbb{C}[S_{n}]\subset\mathcal{H}_{T^{n}}
. That is, any
f:S_{n}\xrightarrow{}\mathbb{C}
can be encoded as a vector
\ket{f}_{T^{n}}=\sum_{g}f(g)\ket{g}_{T^{n}}\in\mathcal{H}_{T^{n}}^{S_{n}}
, with each
\ket{g}_{T^{n}}=\ket{t_n}_{T_{n}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\ket{t_2}_{T_{2}}
corresponding to its unique factorization
g=t_{n}\cdots t_{2}
.

Next, consider the following
word registers

\mathcal{H}_{W_{j}}=\mathcal{H}_{X_{j}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{Y_{j}}={\rm span}\{\ket{x_j}\otimes\ket{y_j}\}_{x_{j},y_{j}=0}^{j}\,

where again
x_{0}=y_{0}=*
are meant to be dummy registers, such that the composition of word registers

\mathcal{H}_{W^{n}}=\mathcal{H}_{W_{n}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{W_{n-1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathcal{H}_{W_{2}}

(44)

also contains a subspace
\mathcal{H}_{W^{n}}^{S_{n}}
isomorphic to
\mathbb{C}[S_{n}]
, given by the span of all valid Yamanouchi words (see Eq. (
26
)):

\mathcal{H}_{W^{n}}\supset\mathcal{H}_{W^{n}}^{S_{n}}={\rm span}\{\ket{x,y}\}_{x,y\in\mathcal{Y}_{n}}\cong\mathbb{C}[S_{n}]\,.

(45)

In addition, define the subspace
\mathcal{H}_{W^{n}}^{S_{n-1}}={\rm span}\{\ket{x^{n-1},y^{n-1}}_{W^{n-1}}\otimes\ket{*,*}\}_{x,y\in\mathcal{Y}_{n}}\cong\mathbb{C}[S_{n-1}]\subset\mathcal{H}_{W^{n}}
and note that
\mathcal{H}_{W^{n}}^{S_{n-1}}
and
\mathcal{H}_{W^{n}}^{S_{n}}
are orthogonal. Finally, for each
t_{n}^{i}\in T_{n}
, let

\mathcal{H}_{i}={\rm span}\Big\{\ket{ \widehat{t_n^i h}}_{W^{n}}\,,\quad h\in T^{n-1}\cong S_{n-1}\Big\}\cong\mathbb{C}[S_{n-1}]\subset\mathcal{H}_{W^{n}}^{S_{n}}\,,

(46)

where
\mathcal{H}_{i}\cong\mathbb{C}[S_{n-1}]
and

\ket{\,\widehat{g}\,}_{W^{n}}=V_{{\rm QFT}_{n}}\ket{g}_{T^{n}}=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\sqrt{\frac{d_{\lambda}}{n!}}\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{Y}^{\lambda}_{n}}[r^{\lambda}_{S_{n}}(g)]_{x,y}\ket{x,y}_{W^{n}}\,,

(47)

Here,
V_{{\rm QFT}_{n}}:\mathcal{H}_{T^{n}}^{S_{n}}\xrightarrow{}\mathcal{H}_{W^{n}}^{S_{n}}
is an isometry implementing the desired Fourier transform over
S_{n}
. Note that these
\mathcal{H}_{i}
subspaces are isomorphic to
\mathcal{H}_{W^{n}}^{S_{n-1}}
, and orthogonal to it.

We prove by induction the
S_{n}
-QFT construction works.
Starting with an arbitrary
\ket{f}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ket{t_n^i}_{T_{n}}\otimes\ket{f^{n-1}_i}_{T^{n-1}}\in\mathcal{H}_{T^{n}}^{S_{n}}
, we assume access to
V_{{\rm QFT}_{n-1}}
and use it on
\ket{f}
to prepare
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ket{t_n^i}_{T_{n}}\otimes\ket{ \hat{f}^{n-1}_i}_{W^{n-1}}
. Following, we tensor with an ancillary word register
\mathcal{H}_{W_{n}}
initialized in its dummy state
\ket{**}_{W_{n}}
to get

\ket{\phi_0}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ket{t_n^i}_{T_{n}}\otimes\ket{ \hat{f}^{n-1}_i,**}_{W^{n}}\,.

(48)

For each
l\in[n]
, consider the unitary operation
Z_{l}=R_{l}V_{E}S_{l}V_{E}R_{l}\in\textsf{U}(\mathcal{H}_{T_{n}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{W^{n}})
, where
R_{l}
,
V_{E}
and
S_{l}
are specified in Definition
1
. The following lemma considers applying the sequence
Z_{n}\cdots Z_{1}
on
\ket{\phi_0}
.

Lemma 1

(From Ref.
[
1
]
)
.

The state after
Z_{l}\cdots Z_{1}
,

\ket{\phi_l}\equiv Z_{l}\cdots Z_{1}\ket{\phi_0}
, is

\ket{\phi_l}=\sum_{j\in[1,l-1]}\ket{*}_{T_{n}}\otimes\ket{\hat{F}_j}_{W^{n}}+\ket{t_n^l}_{T_{n}}\otimes\ket{ \hat{f}^{n-1}_l, **}_{W^{n}}+\sum_{j\in[l+1,n]}\ket{t_n^j}_{T_{n}}\otimes\ket{ \hat{f}^{n-1}_j, **}_{W^{n}}\,.

(49)

Having established that
Z_{l}\cdots Z_{1}\ket{\phi_0}
produces
\ket{\phi_l}
, we conclude that
Z_{n}\cdots Z_{1}
produces

\ket{\phi_{n}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ket{*}_{T_{n}}\otimes\ket{\hat{F}_i}_{W^{n}}=\ket{*}_{T_{n}}\otimes\ket{\hat{f}}_{W^{n}}\,.

(50)

We return the register
W^{n}
which contains the desired
\ket{\hat{f}}
. A sketch of the circuit implementation is provided in Fig.
3
.

Details and Proof of Lemma
1
.

We now give details on the iteration and then follow with the proof of the lemma.

Definition 1
.

Consider the following operations:

•

For any
\pi\in S_{n}
, let
R(\pi)
be the unitary in
\textsf{U}(\mathcal{H}_{W^{n}})
acting nontrivially only in the subspace
\mathcal{H}^{S_{n}}_{W^{n}}
, where its action on a basis
\{\ket{x,y}\}_{x,y\in\mathcal{Y}_{n}}
of
\mathcal{H}^{S_{n}}_{W^{n}}
is

\displaystyle R(\pi)\ket{x,y}_{W^{n}}

\displaystyle=r^{\lambda}(\pi)\ket{x,y}_{W^{n}}=\ket{\pi\cdot x,y}_{W^{n}}\,.

(51)

where
\lambda
is the type of
x
(and of
y
). The action
\pi\cdot x
of a permutation
\pi
on a word
x
is described in the previous section. We will use the shorthand
R_{i}\equiv R(t_{n}^{i})
for the transversal
t_{n}^{i}
, in which case we have that
R_{i}
is an involution – it is own inverse,
R_{i}^{\dagger}=R_{i}
and thus
R_{i}^{2}=I_{W^{n}}
. Let us stress that
R(\pi)
acts trivially outside of
\mathcal{H}_{W^{n}}^{S_{n}}
, in particular on
\mathcal{H}_{W^{n}}^{S_{n-1}}
:

R(\pi)\Big(\ket{\hat{f}^{n-1}_i}_{W^{n-1}}\otimes\ket{**}_{W_{n}}\Big)=\ket{\hat{f}^{n-1}_i}_{W^{n-1}}\otimes\ket{**}_{W_{n}}\,.

(52)

That is, one can use a control on the
W_{n}
register not being
\ket{**}
. The natural way to implement
R_{i}
is by decomposing
t_{n}^{i}
into adjacent transpositions as

t_{n}^{i}=(i,i+1,\cdots,n-1,n)=(i,i+1)(i+1,i+2)\cdots(n-1,n)\,.

(53)

Thus, we write
t_{n}^{i}
in terms of
n-i-1
adjacent transpositions each of which have
2
-sparse matrices in Young’s Orthogonal representation (see Eq.(
30
)) and are thus easy to implement. For example, Ref.
[
53
]
gives an explicit compilation for the action of adjacent transpositions on Yamanouchi registers using
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
elementary gates.

•

Consider the “embedding” unitary
V_{E}
on
\mathcal{H}_{T^{n}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{W^{n}}
that acts only nontrivially on the subspace
\mathcal{H}_{T_{n}}^{S_{n}/S_{n-1}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{W^{n}}^{S_{n-1}}
, whose action is given by

\displaystyle V_{E}\Big(\ket{t_n^i}_{T_{n}}\otimes\ket{x^{n-1}y^{n-1},**}_{W^{n}}\Big)=\ket{t_n^i}_{T_{n}}\otimes\sum_{\lambda\in\lambda_{n-1}+\Box}\sqrt{\frac{d_{\lambda}}{nd_{\lambda_{n-1}}}}\ket{x_\lm,y_\lm}_{W^{n}}

(54)

where
x_{\lambda},y_{\lambda}
are the unique Yamanouchi words with type
\lambda
and prefixes
x^{n-1}
and
y^{j-1}
. Note that
\mathcal{H}_{W^{n}}^{S_{n-1}}
and
\mathcal{H}_{W^{n}}^{S_{n}}
are orthogonal but this unitary is effectively rotating the subspace
\mathcal{H}_{W^{n}}^{S_{n-1}}
inside of
\mathcal{H}_{W^{n}}^{S_{n}}
, more precisely mapping into
\mathcal{H}_{n}\subset\mathcal{H}_{W^{n}}^{S_{n}}
(see Eq. (
46
)), provided the register
T_{n}
is not in the dummy state. Note that for any
f^{n-1}:\mathbb{C}[S_{n-1}]\xrightarrow{}\mathbb{C}
, we have

V_{E}\Big(\ket{t_n^i}_{W_{n}}\otimes\ket{\hat{f}^{n-1}_i,**}_{W^{n-1}}\Big)=\ket{t_n^i}_{W_{n}}\otimes\ket{\widehat{()f^{n-1}_i}}_{W^{n}}\,,

(56)

that is, it embeds
\ket{\hat{f}^{n-1}_i,**}_{W^{n-1}}\in\mathcal{H}_{W^{n}}^{S_{n-1}}
into
\ket{\widehat{()f^{n-1}_i}}_{W^{n}}\in\mathcal{H}_{n}\subset\mathcal{H}_{W^{n}}^{S_{n}}
where
()
represent the identity permutation. Here,
\widehat{()f^{n-1}_{i}}
is the Fourier transform of a function
f
such
f(g)=f^{n-1}_{i}(g)
if
g=()h
for some
h\in T^{n-1}
and
f(g)=0
else. We can write
V_{E}
explicitly in therms of the orthonormal bases of
\mathcal{H}_{W^{n}}^{S_{n-1}}
and of
\mathcal{H}_{n}
,

V_{E}=\outerproduct{*}{*}_{T_{n}}\otimes I_{W^{n}}+\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\outerproduct{t_n^i}{t_n^i}_{T_{n}}\Big)\otimes\Big(\sum_{h\in S_{n-1}}\outerproduct{\hat{h},**}{\widehat{()h}}+\outerproduct{\widehat{()h}}{\hat{h},**}+P_{1}\Big)\,.

(57)

where we use
P
to denote the orthogonal projector
P:\mathcal{H}_{W^{n}}\xrightarrow{}\Big(\mathcal{H}_{W^{n}}^{S_{n-1}}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{n}\Big)^{\perp}
into the complement (denoted with the
\perp
) of
\Big(\mathcal{H}_{W^{n}}^{S_{n-1}}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{n}\Big)
in
\mathcal{H}_{W^{n}}
. Note that
V_{E}
is too an involution,
V_{E}=V_{E}^{\dagger}
and
V_{E}^{2}=I_{T_{n}}\otimes I_{W^{n}}
.

•

Finally,
S_{i}
is a swap between the states
\ket{t_n^i}_{T_{n}}
and
\ket{*}_{T_{n}}
in the transversals register, conditioned on the state of the word register
\mathcal{H}_{W^{n}}
being within its subspace
\mathcal{H}_{n}\subset\mathcal{H}_{W^{n}}^{S_{n}}
. That is, for any
h\in S_{n-1}
,

\displaystyle S_{i}\Big(\ket{t_n^i}_{T_{n}}\otimes\ket{\widehat{()h}}_{W^{n}}\Big)=\ket{*}_{T_{n}}\otimes\ket{\widehat{()h}}_{W^{n}}

(58)

\displaystyle S_{i}\Big(\ket{*}_{T_{n}}\otimes\ket{\widehat{()h}}_{W^{n}}\Big)=\ket{t_n^i}_{T_{n}}\otimes\ket{\widehat{()h}}_{W^{n}}

(59)

Again, one can write

S_{i}=\Big(\outerproduct{t_n^i}{*}+\outerproduct{*}{t_n^i}\Big)\otimes Q+\Big(\sum_{j\neq i}\outerproduct{t_n^j}{t_n^j}\Big)\otimes Q^{\perp}

(60)

with
Q
the orthogonal projector into
\mathcal{H}_{n}
and
Q^{\perp}
such that
Q+Q^{\perp}=I_{W^{n}}
. Once more, we note
S_{i}
is involutory.

Our algorithm is a variation on Harrow’s generalized phase estimation (GPE) when applied to restricted representations

as an

-rep (by restriction). In this target register

\displaystyle\rho^{\alpha}_{G}

\displaystyle=U^{\dagger}\left(\ket{\a}\bra{\a}\otimes\frac{I_{d_{\alpha}}}{d_{\alpha}}\otimes\frac{I_{d_{\alpha}}}{d_{\alpha}}\right)U=\frac{\Pi_{\alpha}^{G}}{d_{\alpha}^{2}},

where

is the

-rep arising from the restriction of the regular representation of

. We then weak-Fourier-sample on the control register,
finding the outcome

with probability

,
where

with

-QFT and

-QFT).
Note that

(83)

where
\Pi_{\beta}^{H}=V^{\dagger}(\outerproduct{\b}{\b}\otimes I_{d_{\beta}}\otimes I_{d_{\beta}})V

\displaystyle\Tr[\Pi_{\beta}^{H}\outerproduct{h}{g}]

\displaystyle=\Tr[\Big(\outerproduct{\b}{\b}\otimes I_{d_{\beta}}\otimes I_{d_{\beta}}\Big)\Big(V\outerproduct{h}{g}V^{\dagger}\Big)]

(88)

\displaystyle p_{\alpha}(\beta)

\displaystyle=\Tr[E_{\beta}\rho^{\alpha}_{G}E_{\beta}^{\dagger}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d_{\alpha}^{2}|H|}\sum_{h,g\in H}\frac{d_{\beta}d_{\alpha}}{|H|}\chi^{\beta}_{H}(hg^{-1})\chi^{\alpha}_{G}(hg^{-1})^{*}

\displaystyle=\frac{d_{\beta}}{d_{\alpha}}{\rm\mathbb{mult}}(r^{\beta}_{H},r^{\alpha}_{G}\downarrow^{G}_{H})\,.

Lemma 2
.

Algorithm
1
finds
{\rm\mathbb{mult}}(r^{\beta}_{H},r^{\alpha}_{G}\downarrow^{G}_{H})
with constant probability after
\mathcal{O}((d_{\alpha}/d_{\beta})^{2})
shots.

Proof.

In each run, the algorithm samples the output distribution:

\displaystyle p(\beta^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{d_{\beta}^{\prime}}{d_{\alpha}}{\rm\mathbb{mult}}(r^{\beta^{\prime}}_{H},r^{\alpha}_{G}\downarrow^{G}_{H})

(94)

Let

be a boolean random variable such that
x_{i}=1
if the
i
-th sample is
\beta
and
x_{i}=0
otherwise. It follows that
\mathbb{E}[x_{i}]=\frac{d_{\beta}}{d_{\alpha}}{\rm\mathbb{mult}}(r^{\beta}_{H},r^{\alpha}_{G}\downarrow^{G}_{H})
. Let

be the number of samples taken from the distribution and let

, so that

. Chernoff-Hoeffding inequality gives:

\displaystyle\text{Pr}\left[\left|X-\mathbb{E}[X]\right|>a\right]=\text{Pr}\left[\left|\frac{d_{\alpha}}{Nd_{\beta}}X-{\rm\mathbb{mult}}(r^{\beta}_{H},r^{\alpha}_{G}\downarrow^{G}_{H})\right|>\frac{d_{\alpha}}{Nd_{\beta}}a\right]\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{2a^{2}}{N}\right).

(95)

Because

, we can round

to the nearest integer and get the correct value of the multiplicity coefficient. We can thus set

and set:

\displaystyle 2\exp\left(-\frac{2a^{2}}{N}\right)=C

(96)

for some small number

\displaystyle N\leq(d_{\alpha}/d_{\beta})^{2}\ln(4/C^2).

(97)

and proves the assertion.

I.7
Kronecker Coefficients: Details

Kronecker coefficients are the multiplicities of
S_{n}
irreducible representations in their tensors:

\displaystyle g_{\mu\nu\lambda}

\displaystyle={\rm\mathbb{mult}}(r^{\lambda}_{S_{n}},r^{\mu}_{S_{n}}\otimes r^{\nu}_{S_{n}}),\;\mu,\nu,\lambda\vdash n,

(108)

that play an important role in representation theory, algebraic combinatorics and many other areas. It is a major open problem (Stanley’s Problem 10
[
29
]
), whether the coefficients can be expressed as a sum of combinatorial objects or not. This question plays an important role in the Geometric Complexity Theory approach to
{\mathsf{P}}
vs
{\mathsf{NP}}
of Mulmuley and Sohoni
[
58
]
and has attracted a lot of interest for this reason (see
[
31
]
and references therein). The coefficients also appeared in works on quantum marginal problem (see for example Refs.
[
14
,
8
,
59
]
). The coefficients can be also computed using the character formula:

\displaystyle g_{\mu\nu\lambda}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\pi\in S_{n}}\chi^{\mu}(\pi)\chi^{\nu}(\pi)\chi^{\lambda}(\pi),

(109)

which shows that the coefficients are symmetric under permutation of its inputs. They have been shown to be
\#{\mathsf{P}}
-hard to compute
[
15
]
, but are known to be in
\#{\mathsf{P}}
on some sets of restricted inputs
[
31
]
and in
{\mathsf{P}}
for inputs with constant length
[
33
]
. See Ref.
[
1
]
for additional background on the coefficients in context of quantum computation.

Kronecker Coefficients: Inputs with a possible polynomial speedup.

A nontrivial set of inputs in which the quantum algorithm runs in polynomial time is when
d_{\mu}\leq{\rm poly}(n)
and
d_{\lambda}=d_{\nu}
is arbitrary. A nice set of partitions that satisfy this condition is
\mu=(n-k,\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\ldots)
, where
\alpha
is a partition of
k
(for a constant
k
), such that
\alpha_{1}<n-k+1
. A simple application of the hook length formula shows that the dimension of such partition is upper bounded by
(d_{\alpha}n^{k}/k!)
, which is polynomial of degree
k
in
n
.

Lemma 5
.

Let
\mu=(n-k,\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\ldots)
, where
\alpha
is a partition of
k
(for a constant
k
), such that
\alpha_{1}<n-k+1
. Then
d_{\mu}\leq\frac{k!n^{k}}{d_{\alpha}}
.

Proof.

Using the hook-length formula, we have:

\displaystyle d_{\mu}

\displaystyle=\frac{n!}{\Pi_{(i,j)\in\mu}h(i,j)}

\displaystyle d_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{k!}{\Pi_{(i,j)\in\alpha}h(i,j)},

(110)

where
h(i,j)
is the hook length between boxes
i,j
in the corresponding partition (the number of boxes to the right of
(i,j)
plus the number of boxes below
(i,j)
plus one). The product
\Pi_{(i,j)\in\mu}h(i,j)
factorizes as:

\displaystyle\Pi_{(i,j)\in\mu}h(i,j)=\Pi_{(1,j),j\in[n-k]}h(1,j)\times\Pi_{(i,j)\in\alpha}h(i,j)=\Pi_{(1,j),j\in[n-k]}h(1,j)\times\frac{k!}{d_{\alpha}}

(111)

where the first factor is the product of hook-lengths of boxes in the first row of
\mu
. Notice that
h(1,j)\geq n-k-j+1
, from which:

\displaystyle d_{\mu}\leq\frac{n!d_{\alpha}}{k!(n-k)!}\leq\frac{n^{k}d_{\alpha}}{k!}

(112)

Since
k
is constant, then
d_{\alpha}
is constant and the dimension of
\mu
is bounded by a polynomial.
∎

Corollary 1
.

\mu=(n-k,\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\ldots)
and
\nu=\lambda
be arbitrary partitions of
n
. Then the Algorithm
1
computes the Kronecker coefficient
g_{\mu\lambda\lambda}
in polynomial time.

Proof.

Reorder the partitions so that the algorithms estimates
g_{\mu\lambda\lambda}
by sampling a distribution;

\displaystyle\frac{d_{\lambda}}{d_{\lambda}d_{\mu}}g_{\mu\lambda\lambda}=\frac{1}{d_{\mu}}g_{\mu\lambda\lambda}.

(113)

We have that
d_{\mu}\leq n^{k}d_{\alpha}/k!
from above, which means that we can resolve the (integer) coefficient
g_{\mu\lambda\lambda}
with polynomially many shots (following Lemma
2
, see also below).
∎

While we were updating this manuscript, Greta Panova showed that the problem of computing Kronecker coefficients on this class of partitions can be solved in polynomial time by classical algorithm
[
27
, Theorem 1]
in time
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{4k^{2}+1})
. In the case of Kronecker coefficients, we now claim that each run of Algorithm
1
requires
\mathcal{O}(n^{4})
of elementary quantum gates. For
d_{\mu}\leq n^{k}
, the Kronecker coefficient can be estimated with high probability with at most
n^{k}
many shots; counting each shot as one elementary operation then leads to
\mathcal{O}(n^{2k+4})
runtime. Assuming her algorithm is optimal, this suggest a large polynomial speedup in computing the Kronecker coefficients for the partitions in Lemma
5
.

Lemma 6
.

The Kronecker coefficient algorithm requires
\mathcal{O}(n^{4})
elementary gates per shot.

Proof.

The algorithm has three key components: a preparation of the two input states
\rho_{S_{n}}^{\lambda}
and
\rho_{S_{n}}^{\mu}
(which can be done by
S_{n}
-Fourier transform), a controlled application of two copies of the regular representation and an inverse
S_{n}
-QFT on the control register. Following the results of
[
23
]

S_{n}
-QFT can be decomposed into
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{4})
-many quantum gates (if not better), which means that both preparation of the input states as well as the inverse
S_{n}
-QFT require
\mathcal{O}(n^{4})
many gates. It remains to argue that the controlled operation of the regular representation is also
\mathcal{O}(n^{4})
. As discussed in
[
23
]
, each
\pi\in S_{n}
has a unique factorization into cyclic permutations as:

\displaystyle\pi

\displaystyle=(1,2,\ldots n)^{i_{n}}(1,2\ldots n-1)^{i_{n-1}}\ldots(1,2,3)^{i_{3}}(1,2)^{i_{2}},

(114)

for integers
i_{2},\ldots i_{n}
. This suggests that we can implement any
\pi
by a sequence of controlled shift operators, each applied at most
n
times. Each cyclic shift decomposes into at most
n
transpositions.
Each transposition
t\in S_{n}
acts as:

\displaystyle R(t)\ket{h}=\ket{th}.

(115)

Assuming that a swap gate between two qubits is an elementary operation, we now argue that a controlled action by a transposition in the regular representation is
n
. To that end, encode each permutation as a vectorized permutation matrix using
n^{2}
qubits, for example:

\displaystyle(132)\mapsto\ket{010|001|100},

(116)

where the vertical
|
separators were added to visually separate the rows of the permutation matrix. A transposition matrix acts by swapping exactly two rows (each with at most
n
qubits), which can be implemented using up to
n
controlled swaps. This means that the controlled action of
R(t)
can be implemented with at most
\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
(cycles
\times
transpositions
\times
controlled swaps) elementary gates. The leading contribution to the gate complexity is then the
S_{n}
-QFT, which gives
{\mathcal{O}}(n^{4})
elementary gates per round.
∎

We thank Dmitry Grinko for pointing out the factorization from
[
23
]
.

Lemma 7
.

Let
d_{\lambda}=d_{\nu}\geq d_{\mu}
, such that
d_{\mu}\leq n^{k}
. Then algorithm
1
uses
\mathcal{O}(n^{2k+4})
gates and finds
g_{\mu\lambda\lambda}
with constant probability.

Proof.

Lemma
2
with
d_{\beta}:=d_{\lambda}
,
d_{\alpha}=d_{\lambda}d_{\mu}
and
{\rm\mathbb{mult}}(r^{\lambda}_{S_{n}},r^{\mu}_{S_{n}}\otimes r^{\lambda}{S_{n}})=g_{\mu\lambda\lambda}
implies that we need
\mathcal{O}(n^{2k})
-many shots to estimate the Kronecker coefficient with high probability. Combining this with Lemma
6
, leads to
\mathcal{O}(n^{2k+4})
gate complexity for the algorithm.
∎

Numerical Results: The Christandl, Doran, Walter agorithm

We studied the Christandl, Doran and Walter
[
33
]
algorithm numerically and compared its performance to a naive, brute-force, implementation of Kronecker computation. The ‘brute-force’ implementation evaluates the character formula for the Kronecker coefficient:

\displaystyle g_{\mu\nu\lambda}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\pi\in S_{n}}\frac{\chi^{\mu}(\pi)\chi^{\nu}(\pi)\chi^{\lambda}(\pi)}{n!}=\sum_{\gamma\in C}\frac{c(\gamma)\chi^{\mu}(\gamma)\chi^{\nu}(\gamma)\chi^{\lambda}(\gamma)}{n!},

(117)

where
C
is the set of conjugacy classes of
S_{n}
,
c(\gamma)
is the size of the conjugacy class with element
\gamma
and
\chi^{\mu}(\gamma)
is the value of the character on the conjugacy class
\gamma
. We implemented this using Sage’s
SymmetricGroupRepresentation
on a given conjugacy class, which we convert to character table. We assume that we precomputed sizes of the conjugacy classes and we time the evaluation of the characters and the summation over the character table. We also do not time the normalization of the character sum that gives Kronecker coefficients. As is, the algorithm is quite inefficient and one may object that the choice of not timing the conjugacy class precomputation is arbitrary. We invite the interested reader to suggest improvements to our methodology. See the stub of the Sage/Python code used for the ‘brute-force’ implementation:

def evaluate_character_formula(mu,nu, ell):
""" Simple algorithm for the Kronecker """
n = sum(mu)
conjugacy_class_sizes = list(map(lambda x: len(x.list()), SymmetricGroup(n).conjugacy_classes()))

start = timeit.default_timer()
a = SymmetricGroupRepresentation(mu).to_character().values()
b = SymmetricGroupRepresentation(nu).to_character().values()
c = SymmetricGroupRepresentation(ell).to_character().values()

# Kronecker is not normalized!
sum([x*y*z*cc_size for x in a for y in b for z in c for cc_size in conjugacy_class_sizes])
end = timeit.default_timer()

return end - start

I.8
Plethysm Coefficients: Details

Semidirect Product Groups.

Given a finite group
F
with an action on another finite group
H
, that is, provided a group homomorphism
\phi:F\xrightarrow{}{\rm Aut}(H)
exists, the
semidirect product
group
H\rtimes_{\phi}F
is the group with set being the cartesian product
H\times F
, i.e., all pairs
(h,f)
for
h\in H
and
f\in F
, and with a group operation
\circ:(H\rtimes_{\phi}F)\times(H\rtimes_{\phi}F)\xrightarrow{}H\rtimes_{\phi}F
given by

\displaystyle(h^{\prime},e_{F})\circ(h,f)=(h^{\prime}\circ h,f)

(118)

\displaystyle(e_{H},f^{\prime})\circ(h,f)=(\phi(f^{\prime})\circ h,f^{\prime}\circ f)\,,

(119)

for all
h,h^{\prime}\in H
and
f,f^{\prime}\in F
, where
e_{H}
and
e_{F}
are used to denote the identity element on each group.

Perhaps the simplest example for a semidirect product group is given by the
Dihedral group

D_{n}=Z_{n}\rtimes_{\phi}Z_{2}
, which consists of the semidirect product between cyclic groups of order
2
and
n
, where
Z_{2}=\{0,1\}
and
Z_{n}=\{i\}_{i\in[n]}
, and where
\phi(0)\cdot i=i
and
\phi(1)\cdot i=-i\,
.

Wreath Product Groups.

A special kind of semidirect product groups, when
H=G^{\times n}
and
F\subseteq S_{n}
acts on
H
by permuting the copies of
G
in
H
, are called
wreath product
groups, and denoted
G\wr F=G^{\times n}\rtimes S_{n}
. Let us focus on the case
F=S_{n}
. Intuitively,
G\wr S_{n}
corresponds to the set of
n\times n
permutation matrices with "ones" replaced by elements of
G
. Given
U={\rm span}\{\ket{u}\}_{u}
and
V={\rm span}\{\ket{v}\}_{v}

G
and
S_{n}
modules respectively, the wreath product
U\wr V
is a
G\wr S_{n}
module with vector space
U^{\otimes n}\otimes V
and group action

\displaystyle(g_{1},\cdots,g_{n},e_{S_{n}})\cdot\ket{u_1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\ket{u_n}\otimes\ket{v}=\Big(g_{1}\cdot\ket{u_1}\Big)\otimes\cdots\otimes\Big(g_{n}\cdot\ket{u_n}\Big)\otimes\ket{v}\,\text{ and }

(120)

\displaystyle(e_{G},\cdots,e_{G},\sigma)\cdot\ket{u_1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\ket{u_n}\otimes\ket{v}=\ket{u_{\sg^{-1}(1)}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\ket{u_{\sg^{-1}(n)}}\otimes\Big(\sigma\cdot\ket{v}\Big)\,.

(121)

In fact, one can show
U\wr V
is irreducible whenever
U
and
V
are
[
60
]
, yet not all irreps of
G\wr S_{n}
come from the wreath of irreps of
G
and
S_{n}
. Although we will only care for such subset of irreducibles, let us mention that the strategy to obtain a complete set of irreps for
G\wr S_{n}
consists of considering representations of
G\wr S_{n}
that are induced from certain representation of its Young-subgroup-like subgroups
G\wr S_{\lambda}
for
\lambda\vdash n
. We will not need this here so we refer the reader to Ref.
[
60
]
for further details.

Plethysm Coefficients.

Consider the irreducible decomposition of the reducible
\textsf{SU}(d)
module obtained by composing Schur functors
\mathbb{S}^{\lambda}
and
\mathbb{S}^{\mu}
on
\mathbb{C}^{d}

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}^{\lambda}(\mathbb{S}^{\mu}(\mathbb{C}^{d}))\cong\bigoplus_{\nu}\mathbb{C}^{a_{\lambda\mu}^{\nu}}\otimes\mathbb{S}^{\nu}(\mathbb{C}^{d})

(122)

where
a_{\lambda\mu}^{\nu}
are the Plethysm coefficients. The computational complexity of the map
a:\mathcal{P}_{n,d}^{\times 3}\xrightarrow{}\mathbb{N}
was studied by Fischer and Ikenmeyer
[
16
]
.
Most importantly (at least for our purposes), the plethysm coefficients also appear as multiplicities in the restriction of
S_{n}
irreps to the wreath product subgroup
S_{a}\wr S_{b}\subset S_{ab}
(where
ab=n
), i.e., the subgroup with set
S_{a}^{\times b}\times S_{b}
with
S_{b}
permuting the copies of
S_{a}
:

a^{\nu}_{\lambda\mu}={\rm\mathbb{mult}}(r_{S_{a}\wr S_{b}}^{\lambda\mu},r_{S_{ab}}^{\nu}\downarrow^{S_{ab}}_{S_{a}\wr S_{b}})\,,

(123)

where
r_{S_{a}\wr S_{b}}^{\lambda\mu}
denotes the
S_{a}\wr S_{b}
-irrep
r_{S_{a}}^{\lambda}\wr r_{S_{b}}^{\mu}
of dimension
d_{\lambda}^{b}d_{\mu}
.

I.9
Induction Algorithm

Our induction algorithm uses a subgroup register
\mathcal{S}=\mathbb{C}[H]={\rm span}\{\ket{h}\}_{h\in H}
and a coset register
\mathcal{V}=\mathbb{C}[G/H]={\rm span}{\ket{t_i}}_{i\in[N]}
.
We begin with a maximally-mixed state over some isotypic in
\mathcal{S}

\displaystyle\rho^{\beta}_{H}

\displaystyle=V^{\dagger}\left(\ket{\b}\bra{\b}\otimes\frac{I_{d_{\beta}}}{d_{\beta}}\otimes\frac{I_{d_{\beta}}}{d_{\beta}}\right)V=\Pi_{\beta}^{H}/d_{\beta}^{2},

(124)

in the subgroup register
\mathcal{S}
, where
V
is the
H
-QFT. This could be prepared with
H
-QFT or given access to some oracle.

We make use of an embedding unitary
U_{E}
acting on
\mathcal{V}\otimes\mathcal{S}
mapping a pair
(t,h)
of transversal and subgroup elements to the corresponding
g=th

\displaystyle U_{E}\Big(\ket{t}_{\mathcal{V}}\otimes\ket{h}_{\mathcal{S}}\Big)=\ket{g}.

(125)

The mapping
(t,h)\xrightarrow{}g
is unique and the above transformation is unitary. It can be efficiently implemented as a quantum circuit as long as there is an efficient classical circuit for group multiplication. This way, we can “match the bases” of the
H
-regular representation and the
G
-regular representation. Note that Ref.
[
61
]
used similar trick for QFT over induced representations. Let:

\displaystyle R(g)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in G}\ket{ga}\bra{a},

\displaystyle S(h)

\displaystyle=\sum_{b\in H}\ket{hb}\bra{b},

\displaystyle I_{N}

\displaystyle=\sum_{c\in|G|/|H|}\ket{c}\bra{c}.

(126)

As depicted in Fig.
2
, we initialize the state
\rho^{\beta}_{H}\otimes I_{N}/N
in
\mathcal{S}\otimes\mathcal{V}
, then apply the embedding unitary and finally weak Fourier sample
G
-irreps. The probability of obtaining
G
-irrep
\alpha
is given by

\displaystyle p_{\beta}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\Tr\left[\Pi^{\alpha}_{G}U_{E}(\rho^{\beta}_{H}\otimes I_{N})U_{E}^{\dagger}\right]=\frac{d_{\alpha}d_{\beta}}{N|G||H|d_{\beta}^{2}}\sum_{g\in G}\sum_{h\in H}\chi_{G}^{\alpha}(g^{-1})\chi_{H}^{\beta}(h)\Tr[R(g)U_{E}(S(h^{-1})\otimes I_{N})U_{E}^{\dagger}]\,,

(127)

where we’ve used that for a finite group
F
and a representation
T
, the projector into its
\gamma
-isotypic subspace is
[
49
]
:

\displaystyle\Pi^{\gamma}_{F}=\frac{d_{\gamma}}{|F|}\sum_{f\in F}\chi^{\gamma}_{F}(f^{-1})T(f).

(128)

We now unwrap
\Tr[R(g)U_{E}(S(h^{-1})\otimes I_{N})U_{E}^{\dagger}]
to get
U_{E}(S(h^{-1})\otimes 1)U_{E}^{\dagger}=\sum_{b\in H}\sum_{i\in[N]}\ket{h^{-1}bt_i}\bra{bt_i}\,
. Notice that
bc
is an element of
G
and that the register has dimension
|G|
and we assume that we match the basis of
R(g)
. It follows from orthogonality that:

\displaystyle\Tr[R(g)U_{E}(S(h^{-1})\otimes I_{N})U_{E}^{\dagger}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in G}\sum_{b\in H}\sum_{i\in[N]}\innerproduct{bt_i}{ga}\innerproduct{a}{h^{-1}bt_i}

(129)

\displaystyle=\sum_{b\in H}\sum_{i\in[N]}\innerproduct{g^{-1}bt_i}{h^{-1}b t_i}=|G|\delta_{hg}.

(130)

Plugging this back gives:

\displaystyle p_{\beta}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\frac{d_{\alpha}}{Nd_{\beta}|H|}\sum_{h\in H}\chi_{G}^{\alpha}(g^{-1})\chi_{H}^{\beta}(h)

(131)

\displaystyle=\frac{d_{\alpha}}{Nd_{\beta}}{\rm\mathbb{mult}}(r^{\beta}_{H},r^{\alpha}_{G}\downarrow^{G}_{H})

(132)

\displaystyle=\frac{d_{\alpha}}{Nd_{\beta}}{\rm\mathbb{mult}}(r^{\alpha}_{G},r^{\beta}_{H}\uparrow^{G}_{H})\,.

(133)

where the last line follows from Frobenius’s reciprocity.

I.10
Connection to the Hidden Subgroup Problem

We briefly remark that Thm.
1
has an interesting relationship to the standard approach to the Hidden Subgroup Problem (HSP). In HSP, one aims to find a subgroup
H\subseteq G
using oracle access to a function
f_{H}:G\xrightarrow{}[N]
for
N=|G|/|H|
that takes distinct values on cosets of
H
in
G
:

G=\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}t_{i}H\quad\text{ and }\quad f(t_{i}H)=i\,,

(135)

where
\{t_{i}\}
is a set of
N
elements each chosen from a distinct coset
i
of
H
in
G
.
The standard approach to HSP prepares the state
\sum_{g\in G}\ket{g}_{\mathcal{C}}\otimes\ket{f(g)}_{\mathcal{T}}
and ignores
\mathcal{T}
to obtain the hidden subgroup state:

\rho_{G}^{G/H}=\frac{|H|}{|G|}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\outerproduct{t_i H}{t_i H},

(136)

where
\ket{t_i H}=\sum_{h}\ket{t_i h}/\sqrt{|H|}
is the
coset state
.
\rho^{G/H}_{G}
is the maximally-mixed state over the subspace
\mathbb{C}[G/H]={\rm span}\{\ket{t_i}\}_{i=1}^{N}\subseteq\mathbb{C}[G]
of left cosets of
H
on
G
. Such subspace
\mathbb{C}[G/H]
is a
G
-representation that is induced from the trivial irrep of
H
,
r^{\rm triv}_{H}\uparrow_{H}^{G}
. One approach to take from here is to weak Fourier-sample: apply
G
-QFT to
\rho_{G}^{G/H}
and measure the
G
-irrep register, obtaining the output irrep
\alpha
of
G
with probability:

\displaystyle p_{G/H}(\alpha)

\displaystyle={\rm\mathbb{mult}}(r^{\alpha}_{G},r^{\rm triv}_{H}\uparrow_{H}^{G})\frac{d_{\alpha}}{\text{dim}(r^{\rm triv}_{H}\uparrow_{H}^{G})}={\rm\mathbb{mult}}(r^{\alpha}_{G},r^{\rm triv}_{H}\uparrow_{H}^{G})\frac{d_{\alpha}|H|}{|G|}.

(137)

For
G=S_{n}
and
H=S_{\mu}
,
one obtains
S_{n}
-irrep
\nu
with probability
p_{S_{n}/S_{\mu}}(\nu)=K^{\mu}_{\nu}d_{\nu}/(n!/\mu!)
.
Compare this with the distribution sampled in Thm.
1
, which would be
K_{\nu}^{\mu}/d_{\nu}
.

I.11
Weak Fourier Sampling of the Hidden Subgroup State

For reference, we show how to arrive at Eq. (
137
). We have that:

\displaystyle p_{G/H}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\Tr\left(\rho_{G}^{G/H}\Pi_{\alpha}\right)=\frac{|H|}{|G|}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Tr\left(\ket{t_i H}\bra{t_iH}\Pi_{\alpha}\right)=\frac{|H|}{|G|}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_{i},

(138)

so by linearity, we can just analyze
\alpha_{i}
:

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|H|}\sum_{k,\ell\in[{d_{\alpha}}]}\sum_{h,h^{\prime}\in H}\innerproduct{t_i h}{U | \alpha, k, \ell}\innerproduct{\alpha, k, \ell}{U^\dag| t_i h'},

(139)

where
U
is the
G
-QFT with matrix elements (see also Eq.
7
):

\displaystyle U\ket{g}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha}\sqrt{\frac{d_{\alpha}}{|G|}}\sum_{ij}\innerproduct{i}{r^\alpha_G(g)|j}\ket{\alpha, i, j},

(140)

This gives:

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{d_{\alpha}}{|H||G|}\sum_{k,\ell}\sum_{h,h^{\prime}\in H}\Tr(r_G^\alpha(t_i h) r_G^\alpha(t_i h')^\dag)

(141)

\displaystyle=\frac{d_{\alpha}}{|H||G|}\sum_{k,\ell}\sum_{h,h^{\prime}\in H}\Tr(r_G^\alpha(t_i h h'^{-1} t_i^{-1}) )

(142)

\displaystyle=\frac{d_{\alpha}}{|G|}\sum_{h\in H}\chi_{G}^{\alpha}(h),

(143)

where
\chi_{G}^{\alpha}
the irreducible character
\alpha
of
G
. Character orthogonality leads to:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|H|}\sum_{h\in H}\chi_{G}^{\alpha}(h)=(\chi_{G}^{\alpha},\chi^{{\rm triv}}_{H})_{H}={\rm\mathbb{mult}}(r^{{\rm triv}}_{H},r^{\alpha}\downarrow^{G}_{H}).

(144)

It follows that:

\displaystyle\Tr\left(\rho_{H}\Pi_{\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{|H|d_{\alpha}}{|G|}{\rm\mathbb{mult}}(r^{{\rm triv}}_{H},r^{\alpha}_{G}\downarrow^{G}_{H}).

(145)
