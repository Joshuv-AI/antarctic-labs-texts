The Sprague-Grundy Functions of Saturations of Misère Nim
Yuki Irie Research Alliance Center for Mathematical Sciences Tohoku University Miyagi, Japan yirie@tohoku.ac.jp

Abstract




We consider misère Nim as a normal-play game obtained from Nim by removing the terminal position.
While explicit formulas are known for the Sprague-Grundy functions of Nim and Welter’s game,
no explicit formula is known for that of misère Nim.
All three of these games can be considered as position restrictions of Nim.
What are the differences between them?
We point out that Nim and Welter’s game are saturated, but misère Nim is not.
Moreover, we present explicit formulas for the Sprague-Grundy functions of saturations
of misère Nim, which are obtained from misère Nim by adjoining some moves.

Introduction

The loser in Nim is the winner in misère Nim.
Nim is a two-player game played with heaps of coins.
Two players alternately choose a heap and take at least one coin from it.
The player who takes the last coin wins in Nim and loses in misère Nim.
In general, the player who moves last wins in the normal-play convention and loses in the misère-play convention,
which has been extensively studied by using misère Sprague-Grundy functions, genera, and misère quotients
(see, for example,

In this paper, we will consider misère Nim as a normal-play game

Impartial games including Nim and misère Nim can be analyzed
using their (normal) Sprague-Grundy functions
[

Sprague-Grundy functions are defined recursively, and

However, explicit formulas are known for the Sprague-Grundy functions of some games such as Nim
[

and Welter’s game
[

, which is a position restriction of Nim.
Though misère Nim is also a position restriction of Nim,
no explicit formula is currently available for its Sprague-Grundy function.

,

, and

Let

We denote by

the

expansion of

if
n_{\geqslant L}^{{\beta}}
is the integer quotient

is the unique integer satisfying

1.2
Subtraction games

We define subtraction games, their misère versions, and Sprague-Grundy functions.

Fix a positive integer
k
and let
\Omega
denote
\set{0,1,\ldots,k-1}
.
Let
{\mathcal{P}}\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{k}
and
{\mathscr{C}}\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{k}\setminus\set{(0,\ldots,0)}
.
Define
\Gamma({\mathcal{P}},{\mathscr{C}})
to be the digraph with vertex set
{\mathcal{P}}
and edge set

\set{(X,Y)\in{\mathcal{P}}^{2}:X-Y\in{\mathscr{C}}}.

We call
\Gamma({\mathcal{P}},{\mathscr{C}})
a
subtraction game
or a
take-away game
.
The vertex set
{\mathcal{P}}
is called the
position set
of
\Gamma({\mathcal{P}},{\mathscr{C}})
.

Remark 1
.

We can consider
\Gamma({\mathcal{P}},{\mathscr{C}})
as a two-player game as follows.
Before the game begins, we pick an initial position
X_{0}\in{\mathcal{P}}
.
The first player subtracts some
C_{0}\in{\mathscr{C}}
from
X_{0}
so that
X_{0}-C_{0}\in{\mathcal{P}}
.
Let
X_{1}=X_{0}-C_{0}
.
Similarly, the second player subtracts some
C_{1}\in{\mathscr{C}}
from
X_{1}
so that
X_{1}-C_{1}\in{\mathcal{P}}
.
In this way, the two players alternately subtract some
C\in{\mathscr{C}}
from the current position.
The player who subtracts last wins.

Example 2

(Nim)
.

Let

{\mathscr{C}}_{[1]}=\set{{C}\in\mathbb{N}^{k}:\mathop{\operator@font wt}\nolimits(C)=1},

where
\mathop{\operator@font wt}\nolimits(C)
is the Hamming weight of
C
, that is, the number of nonzero components of
C
.
The subtraction game
\Gamma(\mathbb{N}^{k},{\mathscr{C}}_{[1]})
is called
Nim
.
For example, in Nim, the first player will win if we start from
(1,0)
;
indeed, he can subtract
(1,0)\in{\mathscr{C}}_{[1]}
from
(1,0)
,
but the second player cannot subtract any
C\in{\mathscr{C}}_{[1]}
from
(0,0)
.

We next define the misère version of a subtraction game.
Let
X
be a position in a subtraction game
\Gamma({\mathcal{P}},{\mathscr{C}})
.
If
X-C\in{\mathcal{P}}
for some
C\in{\mathscr{C}}
, then
X-C
is called an
option
of
X
(in
\Gamma({\mathcal{P}},{\mathscr{C}})
).
If
X
has no options, then
X
is called a
terminal position
.
Let
{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}
be the set of non-terminal positions in
\Gamma({\mathcal{P}},{\mathscr{C}})
.
The subtraction game
\Gamma({\mathcal{P}}^{\prime},{\mathscr{C}})
is called the
misère version
of
\Gamma({\mathcal{P}},{\mathscr{C}})

[
11
]
.

Example 3

(misère Nim)
.

Let
{\mathcal{P}}_{\text{Mis}}={\mathcal{P}}_{\text{Mis}}^{k}=\mathbb{N}^{k}\setminus\set{(0,\ldots,0)}
.
Then
{\mathcal{P}}_{\text{Mis}}
is the set of non-terminal positions of Nim,
so the misère version of Nim is
\Gamma({\mathcal{P}}_{\text{Mis}},{\mathscr{C}}_{[1]})
.
We call
\Gamma({\mathcal{P}}_{\text{Mis}},{\mathscr{C}}_{[1]})

misère Nim
.
4
4

4

Misère Nim is usually defined to be Nim in misère play, so the definition of misère Nim used in this paper is
slightly different from the standard one. However, for
X\in\mathbb{N}^{k}\setminus\set{(0,\ldots,0)}
, the outcomes of
X
in the two of these misère Nim are the same.
In other words, when the initial position is
X
, the first player can win in one of the two misère Nim if and only if he can win in the other misère Nim.

In misère Nim,
the first player will lose if we start from
(1,0)
because this position is terminal.

We now define Sprague-Grundy functions.
See, for example,
[
2
,
5
,
1
,
14
]
for details.
Let
\Gamma=\Gamma({\mathcal{P}},{\mathscr{C}})
and
X\in{\mathcal{P}}
.
The
Sprague-Grundy value

\mathop{\operator@font sg}\nolimits(X)
of
X
is defined to be
the minimum nonnegative integer
n
such that
n
is not equal to the Sprague-Grundy value of any option of
X
, that is,

\mathop{\operator@font sg}\nolimits(X)=\mathop{\operator@font sg}\nolimits_{\Gamma}(X)=\mathop{\operator@font mex}\nolimits\set{\mathop{\operator@font sg}\nolimits_{\Gamma}(Y):Y\text{\ is an option of\ }X},

where
\mathop{\operator@font mex}\nolimits S=\min\set{n\in\mathbb{N}:n\not\in S}
.
Note that if
X
is a terminal position, then
\mathop{\operator@font sg}\nolimits(X)=\mathop{\operator@font mex}\nolimits\emptyset=0
.
The nonnegative integer-valued function
\mathop{\operator@font sg}\nolimits:{\mathcal{P}}\ni X\mapsto\mathop{\operator@font sg}\nolimits(X)\in\mathbb{N}

is called the
Sprague-Grundy function
of the subtraction game
\Gamma
.
For a position
X
,
the following two statements are equivalent:

(1)

\mathop{\operator@font sg}\nolimits(X)=0
.

(2)

The second player can win when the initial position is
X
.

Example 4
.

Let
k=2
.
We calculate the Sprague-Grundy values of some positions in misère Nim (see Table
7
in Example
7
).
Since
(0,1)
and
(1,0)
are terminal positions, their Sprague-Grundy values are 0.
Hence
\mathop{\operator@font sg}\nolimits((0,2))=\mathop{\operator@font sg}\nolimits((1,1))=\mathop{\operator@font sg}\nolimits((2,0))=\mathop{\operator@font mex}\nolimits\set{0}=1
.
This implies that
\mathop{\operator@font sg}\nolimits((1,2))=\mathop{\operator@font sg}\nolimits((2,1))=\mathop{\operator@font mex}\nolimits\set{0,1}=2
,
so
\mathop{\operator@font sg}\nolimits((2,2))=\mathop{\operator@font mex}\nolimits\set{1,2}=0

We can verify that the second player can win when
(2,2)
is the initial position.

Remark 5
.

The Sprague-Grundy function of misère Nim is different from
the misère Sprague-Grundy function
\mathscr{G}^{-}
of Nim defined in
[
5
]
.
The domain of the former function is
\mathbb{N}^{k}\setminus\set{(0,\ldots,0)}
and that of the latter one is
\mathbb{N}^{k}

Here, for

\mathop{\operator@font mex}\nolimits\set{\mathscr{G}^{-}(Y):Y\text{\ is an option of\ }X}&\text{\ \ {otherwise}}.\end{cases}

The value
\mathscr{G}^{-}(X)
is generally not equal to
\mathop{\operator@font sg}\nolimits_{\Gamma}(X)
,
where
\Gamma
is misère Nim
\Gamma({\mathcal{P}}_{\text{Mis}},{\mathscr{C}}_{[1]})
.
For example,
\mathscr{G}^{-}((0,2))=2\neq 1=\mathop{\operator@font sg}\nolimits_{\Gamma}((0,2))
.
However,
\mathscr{G}^{-}(X)=0
if and only if
\mathop{\operator@font sg}\nolimits_{\Gamma}(X)=0
.
5
5

5

As we have mentioned,
\mathscr{G}^{-}(X)
can be written down explicitly.
If
\max X\geqslant 2
, then
\mathscr{G}^{-}(X)=\sigma^{2}(X)

\sigma^{2}(X)
is the Nim sum of the components of
X

If
\max X<2

\mathscr{G}^{-}(X)=1-\sigma^{2}(X)

1.3
{\beta}
-Saturations

We define
{\beta}
-saturations of subtraction games.

Elements in
\mathbb{N}^{k}
will be denoted by upper-case letters, and components of them by lower-case letters with superscripts.
For example,
C=(c^{0},\ldots,c^{k-1})\in\mathbb{N}^{k}

Define

{\mathscr{C}}^{{\beta}}={\mathscr{C}}^{{\beta},k}=\Set{{C}\in\mathbb{N}^{k}\setminus\set{(0,\ldots,0)}:\mathop{\operator@font ord}\nolimits_{{\beta}}\left(\sum_{i\in\Omega}{c}^{i}\right)=\mathop{\operator@font mord}\nolimits_{{\beta}}(C)},

where

\mathop{\operator@font mord}\nolimits_{{\beta}}(C)=\min\set{\mathop{\operator@font ord}\nolimits_{{\beta}}(c^{i}):i\in\Omega}.

For example,
(2,2,6)\in{\mathscr{C}}^{2}
and
(2,2,4)\not\in{\mathscr{C}}^{2}
because

\mathop{\operator@font ord}\nolimits_{2}(2+2+6)=1=\mathop{\operator@font mord}\nolimits_{2}((2,2,6))\ \text{\ \ {and} \ }\ \mathop{\operator@font ord}\nolimits_{2}(2+2+4)=3>1=\mathop{\operator@font mord}\nolimits_{2}((2,2,4)).

A subtraction game

{\beta}
-saturated

is equal to that of

is
{\beta}
-saturated,

{\beta}
-saturation
of
\Gamma({\mathcal{P}},{\mathscr{C}}_{[1]})

1.4
A formula for
{\beta}
-saturations of misère Nim

We present an explicit formula for the Sprague-Grundy functions of
{\beta}
-saturations of misère Nim.

Let
\llbracket{\beta}_{L}\rrbracket
denote
\set{0,1,\ldots,{\beta}_{L}-1}
equipped with the following two operations:
for
a,b\in\llbracket{\beta}_{L}\rrbracket
,

a\oplus b=(a+b)\bmod{\beta}_{L}\quad\text{\ \ {and} \ }\quad a\ominus b=(a-b)\bmod{\beta}_{L}.

In other words,
\llbracket{\beta}_{L}\rrbracket
is the additive group of integers modulo
{\beta}_{L}
.
For example, in
\llbracket 5\rrbracket
,
2\oplus 4=1
and
2\ominus 4=3
.
For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we will think of
n_{L}^{{\beta}}
as an element of
\llbracket{\beta}_{L}\rrbracket
.
Let
\mathbb{Z}_{{\beta}}
denote
\prod_{L\in\mathbb{N}}\llbracket{\beta}_{L}\rrbracket
equipped with the following two operations:
for
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}_{{\beta}}
,

n\oplus m=[{n_{L}\oplus m_{L}}]_{L\in\mathbb{N}}\quad\text{\ \ {and} \ }\quad n\ominus m=[{n_{L}\ominus m_{L}}]_{L\in\mathbb{N}},

where
n=[{n_{L}}]_{L\in\mathbb{N}}
and
m=[{m_{L}}]_{L\in\mathbb{N}}
.
For
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{{\beta}}
, define

\mathop{\operator@font ord}\nolimits_{{\beta}}(n)=\begin{cases}\min\Set{L\in\mathbb{N}:n_{L}\neq 0}&\text{\ \ {if} \ }n\neq[{0,0,\ldots}],\\
\infty&\text{\ \ {if} \ }n=[{0,0,\ldots}].\end{cases}

Consider the map
\Phi:\mathbb{Z}\ni n\mapsto[{n^{{\beta}}_{L}}]_{L\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{{\beta}}
.
For
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, we identify
\Phi(n)
with
n
.
Let
{\mathcal{N}}_{{\beta}}
denote
\Phi(\mathbb{N})
.
For
n\in{\mathcal{N}}_{{\beta}}
, it is convenient to write
n=[{n_{0},n_{1},\ldots,n_{L-1}}]
when
n_{\geqslant L}=0
.
For example, if
{\beta}=10
, then
24=[{4,2,0,\ldots}]=[{4,2}]\in{\mathcal{N}}_{10}
.

For
X\in{\mathcal{N}}_{{\beta}}^{k}
, define

\sigma^{{\beta}}(X)=\sigma^{{\beta},k}(X)=x^{0}\oplus\cdots\oplus x^{k-1}\text{\ (}\in{\mathcal{N}}_{{\beta}}\text{)}.

(1.1)

Let
\sigma^{{\beta}}_{L}(X)=(\sigma^{{\beta}}(X))_{L}
(
=
the
L
th digit of

) for

.

\phi^{2}(X)=\sigma^{2}(X)\oplus(2^{1+1}-1)=3\quad\text{\ \ {and} \ }\quad\phi^{2}(Y)=\sigma^{2}(Y)\oplus(2^{0+1}-1)=0.

1.5
The weight of
\phi^{{\beta},k}

We give the minimum of the
weight
of
{\mathscr{C}}
such that

\Gamma({\mathcal{P}}_{\text{Mis}},{\mathscr{C}})
is a
{\beta}
-saturation of misère Nim.

Let
{\mathcal{P}}\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{k}
.
For a nonnegative integer-valued function
\psi:{\mathcal{P}}\to\mathbb{N}
,
let
\Delta(\psi)
be the set of
{\mathscr{C}}\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{k}\setminus\set{(0,\ldots,0)}

such that the Sprague-Grundy function of
\Gamma({\mathcal{P}},{\mathscr{C}})
equals
\psi
.
Note that if
{\mathscr{C}},{\mathscr{D}}\in\Delta(\psi)
and
{\mathscr{C}}\subseteq{\mathscr{E}}\subseteq{\mathscr{D}}
, then
{\mathscr{E}}\in\Delta(\psi)
.
By definition,
\Gamma({\mathcal{P}},{\mathscr{C}})
is
{\beta}
-saturated if and only if

{\mathscr{C}}\in\Delta(\psi^{{\beta}})
, where
\psi^{{\beta}}
is the Sprague-Grundy function of
\Gamma({\mathcal{P}},{\mathscr{C}}^{{\beta}})
.
If
\Delta(\psi)\neq\emptyset
, then define

\mathop{\operator@font wt}\nolimits(\psi)=\min_{{\mathscr{C}}\in\Delta(\psi)}\mathop{\operator@font wt}\nolimits({\mathscr{C}}),

where
\mathop{\operator@font wt}\nolimits({\mathscr{C}})=\max\set{\mathop{\operator@font wt}\nolimits(C):C\in{\mathscr{C}}}
and
\max\emptyset=0
.
For example, if
\psi^{2}
is the Sprague-Grundy function of a 2-saturation of Nim or that of a 2-saturation of Welter’s game,
then

\mathop{\operator@font wt}\nolimits(\psi^{2})=1

since
{\mathscr{C}}_{[1]}\in\Delta(\psi^{2})
. In other words, Nim and Welter’s game themselves are 2-saturated.
However, as we have seen in Example
7
, if
k=2
, then
{\mathscr{C}}_{[1]}\not\in\Delta(\phi^{2,2})
,
so
\mathop{\operator@font wt}\nolimits(\phi^{2,2})=2
. In fact, if
k\geqslant 2
, then

\mathop{\operator@font wt}\nolimits(\phi^{2,k})=2.

Let
{B}
be the supremum of
\set{{\beta}_{L}:L\geqslant 1}
in
\mathbb{N}\cup\set{\infty}
.
In general, we will prove that

\displaystyle\mathop{\operator@font wt}\nolimits(\phi^{{\beta},k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max\bigg\{\min\Big\{{\beta}_{L}-\delta(L),k-\delta(L)[{\beta}_{0}<2k]\Big\}:L\in\mathbb{N}\bigg\}

(1.3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}k&\text{\ \ {if} \ }{B}\geqslant k\ \ \text{{\ }or \ }\ \ \ {\beta}_{0}\geqslant 2k,\\
k-1&\text{\ \ {if} \ }{B}<k\ \text{\ \ {and} \ }\ k\leqslant{\beta}_{0}<2k,\\
\max\set{{\beta}_{0}-1,{B}}&\text{\ \ {if} \ }{B}<k\ \text{\ \ {and} \ }\ {\beta}_{0}<k,\end{cases}

where
\delta(L)=[L=0]
and
[\ ]
is the Iverson bracket notation, that is,
[P]=1
if a statement
P
holds, and
[P]=0
otherwise.
In particular, if
{\beta}=b
for some
b\in\mathbb{N}
, then

\mathop{\operator@font wt}\nolimits(\phi^{b,k})=\min\set{b,k}.

y^{0}_{L}=m_{L}\oplus{[L\leqslant M]}\ominus y^{1}_{L}\ominus\cdots\ominus y^{k-1}_{L}=n_{L}\ominus x^{1}_{L}\ominus\cdots\ominus x^{k-1}_{L}=x^{0}_{L}\ominus[L\leqslant N]=x^{0}_{L}.

We also see that

y^{0}_{R}=m_{R}\oplus{[R\leqslant M]}\ominus y^{1}_{R}\ominus\cdots\ominus y^{k-1}_{R}=m_{R}\oplus{[R\leqslant M]}\ominus u^{1}\ominus\cdots\ominus u^{k-1}=u^{0}

by (C1).
Hence if
u^{0}<x^{0}_{R}
, then
y^{0}<x^{0}
.
Suppose that
u^{0}=x^{0}_{R}
. Then
M=R
by (C3).
Since
M=\mathop{\operator@font mord}\nolimits_{{\beta}}(Y)
,

y^{0}=[{\underbrace{0,\ldots,0}_{M},{u}^{0},x^{0}_{M+1},x^{0}_{M+2},\ldots}]=[{\underbrace{0,\ldots,0}_{M},x^{0}_{M},x^{0}_{M+1},x^{0}_{M+2},\ldots}]\leqslant x^{0}.

(b) If
{u}^{0}\neq x^{0}_{R}
, then
\mathop{\operator@font wt}\nolimits(C)=\mathop{\operator@font wt}\nolimits(x_{R}-{u})
, and hence
\mathop{\operator@font wt}\nolimits(C)\leqslant w
by (C4).
Suppose that
{u}^{0}=x^{0}_{R}
.
By (C2),
{u}=x_{R}
, so
\mathop{\operator@font wt}\nolimits(C)=1\leqslant w
.

(c) For
i\geqslant 1
, we know that
c^{i}=x^{i}-y^{i}=(x^{i}_{R}-u^{i}){\beta}^{R}
.
Hence if
\mathop{\operator@font ord}\nolimits_{{\beta}}(c^{0})<R
, then

\mathop{\operator@font mord}\nolimits_{{\beta}}(C)=\mathop{\operator@font ord}\nolimits_{{\beta}}(c^{0})=\mathop{\operator@font ord}\nolimits_{{\beta}}\left(\sum_{i\in\Omega}c^{i}\right).

Suppose that
\mathop{\operator@font ord}\nolimits_{{\beta}}(c^{0})\geqslant R
. Then
R\leqslant\mathop{\operator@font mord}\nolimits_{{\beta}}(C)\leqslant\mathop{\operator@font ord}\nolimits_{{\beta}}(\sum c^{i})
,
so we need only show that
\mathop{\operator@font ord}\nolimits_{{\beta}}(\sum c^{i})=R
, that is,

\bigoplus_{i\in\Omega}c^{i}_{R}\neq 0.

(2.10)

We first show that
M=N
.
Since
Y_{<R}=X_{<R}
and
M,N\leqslant R
,

M<R
or

, then
M=N
.
If
M\geqslant R
and
N\geqslant R
, then
M=R=N
.
We now show (
2.10
).
Since
y^{i}_{R}=x^{i}_{R}\ominus c^{i}_{R}
, we see that

\displaystyle m_{R}=\bigoplus_{i\in\Omega}y^{i}_{R}\ominus{[R\leqslant M]}

\displaystyle=\bigoplus_{i\in\Omega}x^{i}_{R}\ominus\left(\bigoplus_{i\in\Omega}c^{i}_{R}\right)\ominus{[R\leqslant N]}

\displaystyle=n_{R}\ominus\left(\bigoplus_{i\in\Omega}c^{i}_{R}\right).

Since
m_{R}\neq n_{R}
, it follows that (
2.10
) holds, so
\mathop{\operator@font ord}\nolimits_{{\beta}}(\sum c^{i})=R=\mathop{\operator@font mord}\nolimits_{{\beta}}(C)
.
Therefore
C\in{\mathscr{C}}^{{\beta}}_{[w]}
.

It remains to prove the claim.
We first show that
x_{R}\neq(0,\ldots,0)
.
If
R=N
, then the assertion is obvious.
If
R>N
, then
{[R\leqslant N]}=0
, so

, which implies that

We next show that

(2.11)

Since

,

n_{R}+{[R\leqslant N]}=n_{R}\oplus{[R\leqslant N]}
, then (
2.11
) holds.
Suppose that
n_{R}+{[R\leqslant N]}\neq n_{R}\oplus{[R\leqslant N]}
.
Then
{[R\leqslant N]}=1
and
n_{R}\oplus{[R\leqslant N]}=0
.
Hence
\bigoplus_{i}x^{i}_{R}=0
.
Since
x_{R}\neq(0,\ldots,0)
, it follows that
\sum x^{i}_{R}\geqslant{\beta}_{R}=n_{R}+{[R\leqslant N]}
.

We now construct
{u}
satisfying (C1)–(C4).
Since
{[R\leqslant M]},{[R\leqslant N]}\in\set{0,1}

By Lemma
15
, there exists
{u}\in\llbracket{\beta}_{R}\rrbracket^{k}
satisfying (C1), (C2), and

\mathop{\operator@font wt}\nolimits(x_{R}-u)\leqslant\min\set{{\beta}_{R}-1,k}.

Case i

(

)
.

Since
\min\set{{\beta}_{R}-1,k}\leqslant w

{u}
satisfies (C4).
If

{u}=x_{R}

It follows that

Suppose that
{[R\leqslant M]}=1
and
{[R\leqslant N]}=0
.
Then

and

. Hence
u
satisfies (C3).
Suppose that
{[R\leqslant M]}=0

{[R\leqslant N]}=1

By (
2.12

m_{R}=\bigoplus_{i\in\Omega}x^{i}_{R}=n_{R}\oplus 1<n_{R}.

We can now find

\mathop{\operator@font wt}\nolimits(x_{R}-\tilde{{u}})\leqslant\min\set{{\beta}_{R},k}\leqslant w,

Case ii

(

)
.
