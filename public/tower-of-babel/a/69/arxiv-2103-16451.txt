Abstract
. We propose a data-driven portfolio selection model that integrates side information, conditional estimation, and robustness using the framework of distributionally robust optimization. Conditioning on the observed side information, the portfolio manager solves an allocation problem that minimizes the worst-case conditional risk-return trade-off, subject to all possible perturbations of the covariate-return probability distribution in an optimal transport ambiguity set. Despite the nonlinearity of the objective function in the probability measure, we show that the distributionally robust portfolio allocation with a side information problem can be reformulated as a finite-dimensional optimization problem. If portfolio decisions are made based on either the mean-variance or the mean-Conditional Value-at-Risk criterion, the reformulation can be further simplified to second-order or semi-definite cone programs. Empirical studies in the US equity market demonstrate the advantage of our integrative framework against other benchmarks.



1.
Introduction

We study distributionally robust portfolio decision rules, which are informed by conditioning on observed side information.
In the presence of contextual information that might be relevant for predicting future returns, our objective is to direct the statistical task (i.e., conditional estimation) towards the downstream task of selecting a portfolio that is robust to distributional shifts (including local conditioning). Our ultimate goal is to provide a tractable, non-parametric, data-driven approach that bypasses the need for computing global conditional expectations. At the same time, our framework is flexible to accommodate a wide range of portfolio optimization selection criteria, as well as extend to other diverse applications. In order to explain our methodology and contribution in detail, let us consider a conventional single-period portfolio optimization selection problem of the form

\min\limits_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}~\mathcal{R}_{\mathbb{P}}[Y^{\top}\alpha]-\eta\cdot\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[Y^{\top}\alpha],

where the real-valued vector
\alpha
denotes the portfolio allocation choice within a feasible region
\mathcal{A}
, and the random vector
Y
denotes the assets’ future return under a probability measure
\mathbb{P}
. The return of the portfolio is denoted by
Y^{\top}\alpha
. At the same time,
\mathcal{R}_{\mathbb{P}}[\cdot]
captures the risk associated with
\alpha
, and
\eta\geq 0
is a parameter that weights the preference between the portfolio return and the associated risk.

Different choices of the measure of risk
\mathcal{R}_{\mathbb{P}}[\cdot]
lead to different portfolio optimization models, including the mean-variance model
[
58
]
, the mean-standard deviation model
[
50
]
, the mean-Value-at-Risk model
[
8
]
, and the mean-Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) model
[
71
]
. The measure of risk
\mathcal{R}_{\mathbb{P}}[\cdot]
often is chosen to belong to certain classes to induce desirable properties. For instance, the class of coherent risk measures
[
3
]
or the class of convex risk measures
[
39
]
are examples of risk measure classes that are often used in portfolio optimization
[
57
]
. In our setting, we consider a family of risk measures that admits a stochastic optimization representation of the form

\mathcal{R}_{\mathbb{P}}[Y^{\top}\alpha]=\min_{\beta\in\mathcal{B}}\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[r(Y^{\top}\alpha,\beta)]
for some auxiliary function
r
and finite-dimensional statistical variable
\beta
residing within an appropriate feasible region
\mathcal{B}
. Hence, we focus on a generic portfolio allocation that minimizes an expected loss of the form

(1)

\min\limits_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A},~\beta\in\mathcal{B}}~\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)],\qquad\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)\triangleq r(Y^{\top}\alpha,\beta)-\eta\cdot Y^{\top}\alpha.

In problem (
1
), optimizing over
\beta
characterizes the improvement of risk estimation, which is entangled with the procedure of finding the optimal portfolio allocation
\alpha
. For example, consider when
\mathcal{R}_{\mathbb{P}}
is the variance of the portfolio return, then by setting
\mathcal{B}=\mathbb{R}
and choosing
\ell
as a quadratic function, we can rewrite the variance in the form
\mathcal{R}_{\mathbb{P}}[Y^{\top}\alpha]=\min_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}}~\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[(Y^{\top}\alpha-\beta)^{2}]
. This further implies that the optimal solution of
\beta
coincides with the expected return
\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[Y^{\top}\alpha]
, and one thus can view
\beta
as an auxiliary statistical variable representing the estimation of the expected portfolio return.

Despite the simplicity and popularity of the portfolio optimization models (
1
), they are challenging to solve because the distribution
\mathbb{P}
of the random stock returns is unknown to the portfolio managers. To overcome this issue, problem (
1
) is usually solved by resorting to a plug-in estimator
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}
of
\mathbb{P}
, and this estimator is usually inferred from the available data. Unfortunately, solving (
1
) using an estimated distribution
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}
may amplify the statistical estimation error. The corresponding portfolio allocation is vulnerable to the estimation errors of the input:
even small changes in the input parameters can result in large changes in the weights of the optimal portfolio

[
23
,
20
]
. Consequently, in terms of out-of-sample performance, the advantage of deploying the optimal portfolio weights is overwhelmed by the offset of estimation error
[
27
]
. To mitigate the estimation error, one may resort, at least, to the following two strategies:

(1)

reduce the estimation bias by incorporating side information into the portfolio allocation problems;

(2)

diminish the impact of estimation variance by employing robust portfolio optimization models.

Both the above-mentioned strategies have led to successful stories. Starting from the capital asset-pricing model
[
75
,
13
]
, numerous studies exploit side information to explain and/or predict the cross-sectional variation of the stock returns. The predictive side information may include macro-economic factors
[
38
]
, firms’ financial statements
[
35
]
, and historical trading data
[
44
]
. Alternatively, robust optimization formulations are applied to the portfolio allocation tasks to alleviate the impact of statistical error due to input uncertainty
[
66
,
25
,
85
]
.

In this paper, we endeavor to unify both strategies of leveraging side information and enhancing robustness. In order to incorporate the side information into portfolio allocation, we use a random vector
X
to denote the side information that is correlated with the stock return
Y
, and consider the following conditional stochastic optimization (CSO) problem:

(2)

\min\limits_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A},~\beta\in\mathcal{B}}~\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)|X\in\mathcal{N}],

where the set
\mathcal{N}
represents our prior (or most current) knowledge about the covariate
X
.
In problem (
2
), the distribution
\mathbb{P}
is lifted to a
joint
distribution between the covariate
X
and the return
Y
. Problem (
2
) is thus a joint portfolio allocation (over
\alpha
) and statistical estimation (over
\beta
) problem, that minimizes the conditional expected loss of the portfolio, given that the outcome of
X
belongs to a set
\mathcal{N}
. We will apply the distributionally robust framework to promote robust solutions and hedge against the estimation error of
\mathbb{P}
. Instead of assessing the expected loss with respect to a single distribution, the distributionally robust formulation minimizes the expected conditional loss uniformly over a distributional ambiguity set that represents the portfolio managers’ ambiguity regarding the underlying distribution
\mathbb{P}
.

We propose a data-driven portfolio optimization framework that
simultaneously exploits side information and robustifies over sampling error
and
model misspecification
. We formulate the distributionally robust portfolio optimization problem with side information as

(3)

\min\limits_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A},~\beta\in\mathcal{B}}~\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B},\mathbb{Q}(X\in\mathcal{N})\geq\varepsilon}~\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)|X\in\mathcal{N}].

We can view the min-max problem (
3
) as a two-player zero-sum game: the portfolio manager chooses the portfolio allocation
\alpha
and the estimation parameters
\beta
so as to minimize the conditional expected loss, while the fictitious adversary chooses the joint distribution
\mathbb{Q}
of
(X,Y)
so as to maximize the incurring loss. A typical choice of the ambiguity set
\mathbb{B}
is a neighborhood around a nominal distribution
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}
. In a data-driven setting, a popular choice of
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}
is the empirical distribution, defined as a uniform distribution supported on the available data. As such, the data influences the optimization problem (
3
) via the channel of the ambiguity set prescription. Given

\varepsilon>0
as an input parameter, the constraint
\mathbb{Q}(X\in\mathcal{N})\geq\varepsilon
is a shorthand for
\mathbb{Q}((X,Y)\in\mathcal{N}\times\mathcal{Y})\geq\varepsilon
, and it controls the probability mass requirement on the set
\mathcal{N}\times\mathcal{Y}
and avoids conditioning on sets of measure zero. In the limit, we also will study the case
\mathbb{Q}(X\in\mathcal{N})>0
with strict inequality.

Intuitively, one can describe problem (
3
) as a zero-sum game between two players. The outer player is the statistical portfolio manager who optimizes over the allocation
\alpha
, and the estimator
\beta
to minimize the conditional expected loss, and the inner player can be regarded as a fictitious adversary whose goal is to increase the resulting loss by choosing an adversarial distribution.

Our formulation (
3
) has several benefits. First, our model is an end-to-end framework that directly generates investment decisions using data. This approach, in the same spirit as
[
52
]
and
[
28
]
, integrates the sequential pipeline of return prediction and portfolio optimization, which is conventionally employed by portfolio managers. Therefore, our model, which consolidates return prediction and portfolio optimization, directly minimizes the conditional expected loss instead of the prediction error of return. Second, the robustness of the random return is directed by the portfolio choice. Indeed, the adversary who solves the supremum problem in (
3
) will aim to maximize the portfolio loss and not the statistical loss. Thirdly, our proposed model does not require imposing any parametric family for prediction, and thus it reduces the risk of model misspecification (see
[
80
,
34
]
for examples of how this risk is typically mitigated).

1.1.
Main Contributions

We study a robust portfolio optimization strategy that integrates conditional estimation and decision-making. This strategy leverages side information to improve the ex-ante return prediction. Additionally, it robustifies the over-estimation error to improve the out-of-sample performance of the portfolio allocation. Our contributions are summarized as follows.

(1)

We present a comprehensive modeling framework for distributionally robust optimization for conditional decision-making, which provides flexibility for selecting different conditional sets and adjusting probability mass requirements. We study specifically the case where the set
\mathbb{B}
will be prescribed using the notions of optimal transport. We show that the qualitative behavior of the distributionally robust portfolio optimization problem with side information depends on whether the set
\mathcal{N}
is a singleton and whether the probability mass requirement
\varepsilon
is zero.

(2)

We derive finite-dimensional reformulations
1

1

To be precise, finite-dimensional optimization problems refer to problems with a finite number of variables and constraints.
of the distributionally robust portfolio optimization problem with side information. Further, we also provide tractable reformulations for the distributionally robust mean-variance problem and mean-CVaR problem as convex conic (second-order cone or semi-definite cone) problems. These
tractable reformulations are particularly interesting given that the conditional expectation is a nonlinear function of the probability measure (thus not amenable to traditional reformulations based on semi-infinite linear programming duality arguments) and that minimizing the worst-case conditional expected loss is equivalent to a min-max optimization problem with a fractional objective function.

(3)

We conduct extensive numerical experiments in the US equity markets. The results demonstrate the advantage of the distributionally robust portfolio optimization formulation, which has
a lower out-of-sample expected loss
compared to other benchmarks.

The distributionally robust optimization problem with side information presented in (
3
) is generic, and our contributions are related to the vast literature in estimation-informed decision-making tasks. Moreover, by an appropriate choice of the loss function
\ell
, the sets
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B},\mathcal{N})
and the ambiguity set
\mathbb{B}
, the formulation (
3

the contextual two-stage stochastic linear programming.

2.
Problem Setup

This section delineates the details of our robustification of the conditional portfolio allocation problem. To this end, we will construct the ambiguity set using the optimal transport cost.

Definition 2.1

(Optimal transport cost)
.

Fix any integer
k
and let
\Xi\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}
.
Let
\mathbb{D}
be a nonnegative and continuous function on
\Xi\times\Xi
. The optimal transport cost between two distributions
\mathbb{Q}_{1}
and
\mathbb{Q}_{2}
supported on
\Xi
is defined as

\mathds{W}(\mathbb{Q}_{1},\mathbb{Q}_{2})\triangleq\min\left\{\mathds{E}_{\pi}[\mathbb{D}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})]:\pi\in\Pi(\mathbb{Q}_{1},\mathbb{Q}_{2})\right\},

where
\Pi(\mathbb{Q}_{1},\mathbb{Q}_{2})
is the set of all probability measures on
\Xi\times\Xi
with marginals
\mathbb{Q}_{1}
and
\mathbb{Q}_{2}
, respectively.

Intuitively, the optimal transport cost
\mathds{W}(\mathbb{Q}_{1},\mathbb{Q}_{2})
computes the cheapest cost of transporting the mass from an initial distribution
\mathbb{Q}_{1}
to a target distribution
\mathbb{Q}_{2}
, given a function
\mathbb{D}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})
prescribing the cost of transporting a unit of mass from position
\xi_{1}
to position
\xi_{2}
. The function
\mathbb{D}
is called the ground transport cost function. The existence of an optimal joint distribution
\pi^{\star}
that attains the minimal value in the definition is guaranteed by
[
81
, Theorem 4.1]
. The class of optimal transport cost is rich enough to encompass common probability metrics. In particular, if
\mathbb{D}
is a metric on
\Xi
, then
\mathds{W}
coincides with the type-1 Wasserstein distance. In this paper, we do not restrict the ground transportation cost
\mathbb{D}
to be a metric, and as a consequence,
\mathds{W}
is not necessarily a proper distance on the space of probability measure. Nevertheless, we will usually refer to
\mathds{W}
as an optimal transport distance with a slight abuse of terminology. A comprehensive introduction to the theory of optimal transport can be found in
[
81
]
.

We now consider the covariate space
\mathcal{X}
, equipped with a ground cost
\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}
, and the return space
\mathcal{Y}
, equipped with a ground cost
\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}
. The joint sample space
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
is endowed with a ground cost
\mathbb{D}
, which is additively separable using
\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}
and
\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}
. We suppose that the portfolio manager possesses
N
historical data samples, where each sample is a pair of covariate-return
(\widehat{x}_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
. Using the optimal transport cost in Definition
2.1
, we define the
joint ambiguity set
of the distributions of
(X,Y)
as

\mathbb{B}_{\rho}=\left\{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}):\mathds{W}(\mathbb{Q},\widehat{\mathbb{P}})\leq\rho\right\}.

The set
\mathbb{B}_{\rho}
contains all distributions that are of optimal transport distance less than or equal to
\rho
from the empirical distribution

\widehat{\mathbb{P}}\triangleq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}\delta_{(\widehat{x}_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})},

defined as the distribution that makes every member of the dataset
(\widehat{x}_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
equiprobable.
3
3

3

While other discrete distributions can be used as the nominal one, the empirical distribution is often considered in cases where observations are assumed to be independently and identically distributed.

In this form,
\mathbb{B}_{\rho}
is a non-parametric ambiguity set.
We propose to solve the distributionally robust conditional portfolio allocation problem

(4)

\min\limits_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A},~\beta\in\mathcal{B}}~\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho},\mathbb{Q}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))\geq\varepsilon}~\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)|X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})].

Problem (
4
) is a special instance of problem (
3
) with two particular design choices. First, the ambiguity set is chosen as the optimal transport ambiguity set
\mathbb{B}_{\rho}
. Second,
the side information input is specifically modeled as a neighborhood around a covariate
x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}
, and the size of this neighborhood is controlled by a parameter
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. This neighborhood is prescribed using the ground cost
\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}
on
\mathcal{X}
as

\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\triangleq\left\{x\in\mathcal{X}:\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(x,x_{0})\leq\gamma\right\}.

The set
\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\times\mathcal{Y}
is called the fiber set. When
\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})
collapses into a singleton, we obtain a
singular
fiber set
\{x_{0}\}\times\mathcal{Y}
, and this singular case represents conditioning on the event
X=x_{0}
. In this perspective, our formulation is a generalization of the conventional conditional decision-making problem in the literature, which usually focuses on conditioning on
X=x_{0}
. Throughout this paper, we use
\mathbb{Q}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))
as a shorthand for
\mathbb{Q}((X,Y)\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\times\mathcal{Y})
. The constraint
\mathbb{Q}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))\geq\varepsilon
indicates a minimum amount
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
of probability mass to be assigned to the fiber set
\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\times\mathcal{Y}
. The feasible set
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
are assumed to be simple in the sense that they are representable using second-order cone constraints. Overall, problem (
4
) involves three parameters: the ambiguity size
\rho\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, the fiber size
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, and the probability mass requirement
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
.

It is now instructive to discuss the difficulty level of problem (
4
). By the definition of the conditional expectation, problem (
4
) is equivalent to

\min\limits_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A},~\beta\in\mathcal{B}}~\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho},\mathbb{Q}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))\geq\varepsilon}~\frac{\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})}(X)]}{\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})}(X)]}.

In this form, one can observe that the objective function is a fractional, and thus is a non-linear, function of the probability measure
\mathbb{Q}
. Problem (
4
) hence belongs to the class of distributionally robust fractional optimization problems
[
45
,
83
]
. Thus, problem (
4
) is fundamentally different and also fundamentally more difficult to solve compared to existing models in the field of distributionally robust optimization that simply minimizes the worst-case expected loss
[
68
]
.

It is important to emphasize that the auxiliary variable
\beta\in\mathcal{B}
is intentionally regrouped to the feasible set of the outer minimization problem of formulation (
4
). This regrouping of portfolio allocation variables
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
and the estimation variable
\beta\in\mathcal{B}
is a modeling choice. In the expected loss minimization perspective, this regrouping is without any loss of optimality under conditions such as the compactness of
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{Y}
, as we illustrate in the following technical lemmas.

Lemma 2.2

(Mean-variance loss function)
.

The robustified conditional mean-variance portfolio allocation problem is

\min\limits_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}~\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho},\mathbb{Q}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))\geq\varepsilon}~\mathrm{Variance}_{\mathbb{Q}}[Y^{\top}\alpha|X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})]-\eta\cdot\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[Y^{\top}\alpha|X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})].

If
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{Y}
are compact, then the above optimization problem is equivalent to (
4
) with
\ell
representing the mean-variance loss function of the form

(5)

\ell(y,\alpha,\beta)=(y^{\top}\alpha-\beta)^{2}-\eta\cdot y^{\top}\alpha

and the set
\mathcal{B}
defined as

\mathcal{B}=\big[\inf_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A},~y\in\mathcal{Y}}y^{\top}\alpha,\;\sup_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A},~y\in\mathcal{Y}}y^{\top}\alpha\big].

Lemma 2.3

(Mean-CVaR loss function)
.

The robustified conditional mean-CVaR portfolio allocation problem with risk tolerance
\tau\in(0,1)
is

\min\limits_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}~\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho},\mathbb{Q}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))\geq\varepsilon}~\mathrm{CVaR}^{1-\tau}_{\mathbb{Q}}[Y^{\top}\alpha|X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})]-\eta\cdot\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[Y^{\top}\alpha|X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})].

If
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{Y}
are compact, the above optimization problem is equivalent to problem (
4
) with
\ell
representing the mean-CVaR loss function of the form

(6)

\ell(y,\alpha,\beta)=-\eta y^{\top}\alpha+\beta+\frac{1}{\tau}(-y^{\top}\alpha-\beta)^{+}=\max\left\{-\eta y^{\top}\alpha+\beta,-(\eta+\frac{1}{\tau})y^{\top}\alpha+(1-\frac{1}{\tau})\beta\right\}

and
\mathcal{B}
as defined in Lemma
2.2
.

Theoretically, the assumptions on the compactness of the sets
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{Y}
may be restrictive. However, in practice and especially in the portfolio decision setting, the feasible allocation set
\mathcal{A}
is usually compact. Moreover, we empirically observe that restricting
\mathcal{Y}
to be inside a ball of sufficiently large diameter does not alter the numerical solution. Alternatively, weak duality also implies that problem (
4
) is a conservative formulation of the robustified risk measure minimization problem, see Remark
A.3
for an example. Thus even when the compactness assumptions do not hold, the optimal solution of (
4
) can still be considered a robust, or more risk-averse, portfolio allocation.

Throughout this paper, we make the following regularity assumption.

Assumption 2.4

(Regularity conditions)
.

The following assumptions hold.

(i)

Separable ground cost: The joint space
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
is endowed with a separable cost function
\mathbb{D}
of the form

\mathbb{D}\big((x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\big)=\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(x,x^{\prime})+\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(y,y^{\prime})\quad\forall(x,y),~(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}.

The individual ground transport costs
\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}
and
\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}
are symmetric, non-negative, and continuous on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}
, respectively. Moreover,
\mathbb{D}\big((x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\big)=0
if and only if
(x,y)=(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
.

(ii)

Projection: For any
i\in[N]
, there exists a unique projection
\widehat{x}_{i}^{p}\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})
of
\widehat{x}_{i}
onto
\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})
under the cost
\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}
, that is,

(7)

0\leq\kappa_{i}\triangleq\min\limits_{x\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})}\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(x,\widehat{x}_{i})=\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(\widehat{x}_{i}^{p},\widehat{x}_{i}).

(iii)

Vicinity: For any
x\in\partial\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})
and for any radius
r>0
, the neighborhood set
\{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}\backslash\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}):\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(x^{\prime},x)\leq r\}
around the boundary point
x
is non-empty.

The conditions on
\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}
and
\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}
in Assumption
2.4
(i)
are trivially satisfied if these individual ground costs are chosen as continuous functions of norms on
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
, respectively. Assumption
2.4
(ii)
asserts the existence of the projection of any training sample point
(\widehat{x}_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})
onto the set
\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\times\mathcal{Y}
. It is easy to see that
\kappa_{i}=0
and
\widehat{x}_{i}^{p}=\widehat{x}_{i}
whenever
\widehat{x}_{i}\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})
thanks to the choice of
\mathbb{D}
in Assumption
2.4
(i)
. Assumption
2.4
(iii)
indicates that any points on the boundary of the fiber set can be shifted outside the fiber with an arbitrarily small cost. Assumption
2.4
(iii)
holds whenever the set
\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})
lies in the interior of the set
\mathcal{X}
.

2.1.
Feasibility condition

Notice that for a fixed amount of fiber probability
\varepsilon
, if the transportation budget
\rho
is small, the feasible set of the inner supremum problem in (
4
) may be empty. We define the minimum value of the radius
\rho
so that this feasible set is non-empty as

(8)

\rho_{\min}(x_{0},\gamma,\varepsilon)\triangleq\inf\left\{\mathds{W}(\mathbb{Q},\widehat{\mathbb{P}}):\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}),~\mathbb{Q}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))\geq\varepsilon\right\}.

The value
\kappa_{i}
defined in Assumption
2.4
(ii)
signifies the unit cost of moving a point mass from the observation
(\widehat{x}_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})
to the fiber set
\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\times\mathcal{Y}
. The magnitude of
\kappa_{i}
depends on
\gamma
, however, this dependence is implicit. Using this definition of
\kappa
, the next proposition asserts that the value of
\rho_{\min}(x_{0},\gamma,\varepsilon)
can be computed by solving a finite-dimensional optimization problem.

Proposition 2.5

(Minimum radius)
.

The value
\rho_{\min}(x_{0},\gamma,\varepsilon)
equals the optimal value of a linear program

(9)

\rho_{\min}(x_{0},\gamma,\varepsilon)=\min\left\{\displaystyle N^{-1}\sum_{i\in[N]}\kappa_{i}\upsilon_{i}:\upsilon\in[0,1]^{N},\,\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i}\geq N\varepsilon\right\},

where
\kappa
are defined as in (
7
).
Furthermore, there exists a measure
\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho}
such that
\mathbb{Q}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))\geq\varepsilon
if and only if
\rho\geq\rho_{\min}(x_{0},\gamma,\varepsilon)
.

Notice that the minimization problem (
9
) can be formulated as a fractional knapsack problem, and it can be solved in time
O(N\log N)
using a greedy heuristic
[
24
]
, see also
[
51
, Proposition 17.1]
.

2.2.
Discussion and roadmap

Problem (
4
) is governed by three parameters: the ambiguity size
\rho
, the fiber size
\gamma
, and the fiber probability requirement
\varepsilon
. Figure
1
gives an overview of the results corresponding to all combinations of parameter choices for
(\rho,\gamma,\varepsilon)
.

Figure 1
.
Schematic overview of the results in this paper.

The parameter
\gamma
determines the size of the neighborhood around
x_{0}
. The case with
\gamma=0
results in a singular fiber set
\{x_{0}\}\times\mathcal{Y}
: in this case, either problem (
4
) leads to uninformative conditioning result in Section
3.1
or we need to impose a strictly positive mass in order to get informative conditioning result (
\varepsilon>0
) in Section
3.2
. Notice that imposing the constraint that
\mathbb{Q}(X=x_{0})\geq\varepsilon>0
will automatically eliminate distributions with a density around
x_{0}
. Thus, if the modeler has a prior belief that the data-generating distribution admits a density around
x_{0}
, then it is more reasonable to use
\gamma>0
. The parameter
\gamma
prescribes a neighborhood in the covariate space
\mathcal{X}
, and it resembles the kernel bandwidth in the field of nonparametric statistics. One can rely on this resemblance and tune
\gamma
based on the number of samples
N
using the same theoretical guidance for choosing the bandwidth. For example, if
\mathcal{X}
is one-dimensional, then
[
37
, Section 3.2]
suggests that
\gamma
should be of the order of
1/N^{1/5}
. For higher dimensions, the rate can be obtained by imposing suitable assumptions on the data-generating distribution.

Adding a neighborhood around
x_{0}
also implies that the modeler wishes to hedge against potential misspecification or perturbation of the covariate
x_{0}
. In a portfolio optimization setting, the covariate
x_{0}
may represent a combination of macroeconomics (inflation, GDP growth, etc.) and/or market indices (VIX, etc.), which are notoriously difficult to assign an exact value due to time lags, noisy or constantly-updated measurements. It is thus imperative to take the effects of an uncertain covariate
x_{0}
into account.

From a technical viewpoint, the parameter
\varepsilon
helps to avoid the ill-posedness of the optimization problem. As we will show in Sections
3.1
and
4.1
, the conditioning in problem (
4
) may become
un
informative if
\varepsilon=0
, and this issue can be eliminated by imposing a strictly positive value of
\varepsilon
. From a modeling viewpoint, the parameter
\varepsilon
can capture the prior information of the modeler on the magnitude of the density function around
x_{0}
, which translates integrally to a lower-confidence bound on the probability value assigned to the set
\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\times\mathcal{Y}
. Alternatively, the parameter
\varepsilon
can be tuned using a similar idea as tuning the kernel bandwidth in the nonparametric statistics literature: if the neighborhood around
x_{0}
is of a radius
\gamma>0
and if the data-generating distribution admits a density, then we expect
\varepsilon
to scale in the order of
\gamma^{n}
, where
n
is the dimension of the covariate space
\mathcal{X}
.

Finally, the parameter
\rho\in\mathbb{R}_{++}
hedges against possible error in the finite sample estimation, equivalently known as the epistemic uncertainty. It is possible to tune
\rho
so that problem (
4
) possesses some desirable theoretical guarantees. For example, under the condition
\gamma>0
and
\varepsilon=0
of Section
4.1
, problem (
4
) satisfies the finite sample guarantee provided that
\rho
is chosen so that
\mathbb{B}_{\rho}
contains the data-generating distribution with high probability (e.g., by choosing
\rho
that satisfies the finite guarantee from
[
40
]
). Alternatively,
\rho
can be also tuned using the robust Wasserstein profile inference (RWPI) approach with the goal of recovering the correct decision
[
17
,
76
]
.

The most comprehensive and general case in this paper is presented in Section
4.2
with all parameters
(\rho,\gamma,\varepsilon)
being non-zero. In addition, the results in Section
4.2
also provide the reformulations for a specific case with
\varepsilon=0
; see the dashed arrow. Thus, the combination of non-zero parameters
(\rho,\gamma,\varepsilon)
presented in Section
4.2
is useful on two fronts: first, it equips the modeler with the most flexible setting to capture different prior information, and second, it serves as a reformulating auxiliary combination to resolve the problem under a null fiber probability assumption.

While the statistical performance guarantees (either in asymptotic or finite sample regime) of problem (
4
) are also of high theoretical relevance, our paper focuses mainly on practical relevance. Towards this goal, our main focus is to provide the reformulations for problem (
4
) via a complete and thorough analysis for each combination of the parameters
(\rho,\gamma,\varepsilon)
. We leave the statistical performance guarantee of problem (
4
) open for further research.

3.
Tractable Reformulations for Singular Fiber Set

We study in this section the case where the radius
\gamma
is zero, which implies that
\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})=\{x_{0}\}
and the fiber set becomes
\{x_{0}\}\times\mathcal{Y}
. In this case, we simply recover the conventional portfolio allocation problem conditional on
X=x_{0}
. Interestingly, the qualitative behavior of the robustified conditional portfolio allocation problem (
4
) depends on whether
\varepsilon=0
or
\varepsilon>0
. We will explore these two cases in the subsequent subsections.

3.1.
Null fiber probability
\varepsilon=0

We consider now the situation when
\gamma=\varepsilon=0
.
Notice that the probability mass constraint in this case should be taken as a strict inequality of the form
\mathbb{Q}(X=x_{0})>0
to avoid conditioning on sets of measure zero. This is equivalent to viewing the mass constraint in the limit as
\varepsilon
tends to zero. If we use the type-
\infty
Wasserstein distance to dictate the set
\mathbb{B}_{\rho}
, then the results from
[
63
]
can be utilized in order the compute the worst-case conditional expected loss. However, in this paper, we use the optimal transport of Definition
2.1
to prescribe
\mathbb{B}_{\rho}
, and the worst-case expected loss becomes uninformative, as is shown in the following result.

Proposition 3.1

(Uninformative solution when
\varepsilon=\gamma=0
)
.

Suppose that
\varepsilon=\gamma=0
. For any
x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}
and
\rho\in\mathbb{R}_{++}
, the worst-case conditional expected loss becomes

\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho},\mathbb{Q}(X=x_{0})>0}~\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)|X=x_{0}]=\sup\limits_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}~\ell(y,\alpha,\beta).

The result in Proposition
3.1
can be justified heuristically as follows. Consider an adversary who can move sample points on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
to maximize the loss subject to the optimal transport distance budget constraint. If
\widehat{x}_{i}=x_{0}
, the adversary can slightly perturb
\widehat{x}_{i}
within an infinitesimal distance so that the sample no longer belongs to the fiber set. Because of Assumption
2.4
on the continuity of the ground metric and the non-emptiness of the neighborhood, this perturbation costs an infinitesimally small amount of energy. The adversary can repeat until there is no sample point lying on the fiber set. Now, the adversary can pick any sample, slice out a tiny amount of mass, and move that slice to any location on
\{x_{0}\}\times\mathcal{Y}
. Because the ground metric is continuous by Assumption
2.4
(i)
and the slice can be arbitrarily small, this would cost an infinitesimally small amount of energy. As the thin slice can be put on any point on
\{x_{0}\}\times\mathcal{Y}
, the resulting distribution will generate the robust conditional expected loss.

Proposition
3.1
reveals that the conditioning problem with
\varepsilon=\gamma=0
results in a robust optimization formulation, which can be overly conservative. This result is also negative in the sense that the worst-case conditional expected loss depends only on the support
\mathcal{Y}
, and it does
not
depend on the data
(\widehat{x}_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})
that were collected. Thus, in this case, the notion of data-driven decision-making becomes obsolete, and thus we do not pursue the reformulation any further. These observations also highlight the qualitative difference between using the optimal transport ambiguity set
\mathbb{B}_{\rho}
as in this paper and using the
\infty
-Wasserstein ambiguity set as in
[
63
, §2]
.

3.2.
Strictly Positive Fiber Probability
\varepsilon>0

We now study the situation with a singular fiber set prescribed by
\gamma=0
, but the probability mass requirement is set to a strictly positive value
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
. The robust conditional portfolio allocation problem (
4
) can now be rewritten explicitly as

(10)

\min\limits_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A},~\beta\in\mathcal{B}}~\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho},\mathbb{Q}(X=x_{0})\geq\varepsilon}~\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)|X=x_{0}].

The next theorem asserts that the worst-case conditional expected loss admits a finite-dimensional reformulation.

Theorem 3.2

(Worst-case conditional expected loss when
\gamma=0
)
.

Suppose that
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
and
\rho>\rho_{\min}(x_{0},0,\varepsilon)
. For any feasible solution
(\alpha,\beta)
, we have

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho},\mathbb{Q}(X=x_{0})\geq\varepsilon}~\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)|X=x_{0}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda_{1}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\\
\lambda_{2}\in\mathbb{R}\end{subarray}}\left\{\rho\lambda_{1}+\varepsilon\lambda_{2}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}\left(\sup_{y_{i}\in\mathcal{Y}}\big\{\varepsilon^{-1}\ell(y_{i},\alpha,\beta)-[\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{0},\widehat{x}_{i})+\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(y_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})]\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}\big\}\right)^{+}\right\}.

It is instructive to discuss the insights that lead to the result presented in Theorem
3.2
. Notice that the supremum problem in the statement of Theorem
3.2
has a fractional objective function. The first step of the proof establishes that without any loss of optimality, the
inequality
constraint
\mathbb{Q}(X=x_{0})\geq\varepsilon
can be reduced to an
equality
constraint of the form
\mathbb{Q}(X=x_{0})=\varepsilon
(see Proposition
A.5
for a formal statement of this result). Leveraging this result, we derive the equivalence

\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho},\mathbb{Q}(X=x_{0})\geq\varepsilon}~\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)|X=x_{0}]=\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho},\mathbb{Q}(X=x_{0})=\varepsilon}~\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)\mathbbm{1}_{\{x_{0}\}}(X)],

where the right-hand side problem is linear in the probability measure
\mathbb{Q}
. At this point, duality techniques can be applied to reformulate the original problem into a finite-dimensional optimization problem.

We acknowledge that a similar duality result has been proposed in
[
33
, Theorem 1]
using the trimming approach.
Notice that the trimming procedure in
[
33
]
imposed that the fictitious adversary can only choose a distribution
\mathbb{Q}
satisfying the strict fiber constraint
\mathbb{Q}(X=x_{0})=\varepsilon
. One advantage of this equality constraint is that the conditional expectation is trivially reduced to a linear function of
\mathbb{Q}
. In direct comparison, our approach endows the adversary with a bigger feasible set, wherein we only impose an
in
equality
\mathbb{Q}(X=x_{0})\geq\varepsilon
. As a result, our adversary is considered more powerful than the trimming adversary, and our problem is significantly harder than the trimming problem because there is no trivial way to reduce the fractional objective function into a linear function of
\mathbb{Q}
. We contribute an insight that despite a bigger set with
\mathbb{Q}(X=x_{0})\geq\varepsilon
, it is still optimal for our adversary to choose the distribution with an equality value
\mathbb{Q}(X=x_{0})=\varepsilon
, and only after that, the reduction of the conditional expectation to a linear function of
\mathbb{Q}
follows.

By combining the infimum reformulation of Theorem
3.2
with the outer infimum problem of problem (
10
), the portfolio allocation problem (
10
) is thus reformulatable as a finite-dimensional optimization problem. More specifically, problem (
10
) becomes

(11)

\begin{array}[]{cll}\inf&\displaystyle\rho\lambda_{1}+\varepsilon\lambda_{2}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}\theta_{i}\\
\st&\alpha\in\mathcal{A},\;\beta\in\mathcal{B},\;\lambda_{1}\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\;\lambda_{2}\in\mathbb{R},\;\theta\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}\\
&\theta_{i}\geq\sup_{y_{i}\in\mathcal{Y}}\big\{\varepsilon^{-1}\ell(y_{i},\alpha,\beta)-\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(y_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})\lambda_{1}\big\}-\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{0},\widehat{x}_{i})\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}\quad\forall i\in[N].\end{array}

In Propositions
3.3
and
3.4
, we provide a second-order cone reformulation of problem (
11
) tailored for the mean-variance and mean-CVaR objective functions for a special instance with
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\mathcal{Y}=\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(x,\widehat{x})=\|x-\widehat{x}\|^{2}
and
\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(y,\widehat{y})=\|y-\widehat{y}\|_{2}^{2}
. Notice that
\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}
is constructed from an arbitrary norm on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
while
\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}
is constructed as the
squared
Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

Proposition 3.3

(Mean-variance loss function)
.

Suppose that
\ell
is the mean-variance loss function of the form (
5
),
\gamma=0
,
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
and
\rho>\rho_{\min}(x_{0},0,\varepsilon)
. Suppose in addition that
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathcal{Y}=\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(x,\widehat{x})=\|x-\widehat{x}\|^{2}
and
\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(y,\widehat{y})=\|y-\widehat{y}\|_{2}^{2}
. The distributionally robust portfolio allocation model with side information (
10
) is equivalent to the second-order cone program

\begin{array}[]{cll}\inf&\displaystyle\rho\lambda_{1}+\varepsilon\lambda_{2}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}\theta_{i}\\
\st&\alpha\in\mathcal{A},\;\beta\in\mathcal{B},\;\lambda_{1}\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\;\lambda_{2}\in\mathbb{R},\;\theta\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N},\;z\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N},\;w\in\mathbb{R}\\
&0\leq w\leq 1,\quad\left\|\begin{bmatrix}2\alpha\\
1-w-\varepsilon\lambda_{1}\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{2}\leq 1-w+\varepsilon\lambda_{1}\\
&z_{i}=\varepsilon\theta_{i}+\varepsilon\|x_{0}-\widehat{x}_{i}\|^{2}\lambda_{1}+\varepsilon\lambda_{2}+\frac{1}{4}\eta^{2}+\eta\beta,\quad\left\|\begin{bmatrix}2\,\widehat{y}_{i}^{\top}\alpha-2\beta-\eta\\
z_{i}-w\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{2}\leq z_{i}+w&\forall i\in[N].\end{array}

Proposition 3.4

(Mean-CVaR loss function)
.

Suppose that
\ell
is the mean-CVaR loss function of the form (
6
),
\gamma=0
,
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
and
\rho>\rho_{\min}(x_{0},0,\varepsilon)
. Suppose in addition that
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\mathcal{Y}=\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(x,\widehat{x})=\|x-\widehat{x}\|^{2}
and
\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(y,\widehat{y})=\|y-\widehat{y}\|_{2}^{2}
. The distributionally robust portfolio allocation model with side information (
10
) is equivalent to the second-order cone program

\begin{array}[]{cll}\inf&\displaystyle\rho\lambda_{1}+\varepsilon\lambda_{2}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}\theta_{i}\\
\st&\alpha\in\mathcal{A},\;\beta\in\mathcal{B},\;\lambda_{1}\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\;\lambda_{2}\in\mathbb{R},\;\theta\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N},\;z\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N},\;\tilde{z}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}\\
&\left.\!\!\!\begin{array}[]{l}z_{i}=\theta_{i}+\lambda_{1}\|x_{0}-\widehat{x}_{i}\|^{2}+\lambda_{2}+\varepsilon^{-1}\eta\widehat{y}_{i}^{\top}\alpha-\varepsilon^{-1}\beta\\
\tilde{z}_{i}=\theta_{i}+\lambda_{1}\|x_{0}-\widehat{x}_{i}\|^{2}+\lambda_{2}+\varepsilon^{-1}(\eta+\frac{1}{\tau})\widehat{y}_{i}^{\top}\alpha-\varepsilon^{-1}(1-\frac{1}{\tau})\beta\\
\left\|\begin{bmatrix}\varepsilon^{-1}\eta\alpha\\
z_{i}-\lambda_{1}\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{2}\leq z_{i}+\lambda_{1},\quad\left\|\begin{bmatrix}\varepsilon^{-1}(\eta+\tau^{-1})\alpha\\
\tilde{z}_{i}-\lambda_{1}\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{2}\leq\tilde{z}_{i}+\lambda_{1}\end{array}\right\}\forall i\in[N].\end{array}

Both Proposition
3.3
and
3.4
leverage the fact that
\mathcal{Y}=\mathbb{R}^{m}
in order to simplify the semi-infinite constraints into second-order cone constraints. We re-emphasize that under the assumption
\mathcal{Y}=\mathbb{R}^{m}
of Proposition
3.3
and
3.4
, formulation (
10
) is a conservative approximation of the distributionally robust mean-variance and mean-CVaR portfolio allocation problem, respectively. In case the set
\mathcal{Y}
is an ellipsoid of the form
\mathcal{Y}=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:y^{\top}Qy+2q^{\top}y+q_{0}\leq 0\}
with a non-empty interior, then semi-definite cone constraint counterparts are also available by employing the S-lemma
[
67
]
.

4.
Tractable Reformulations for Nonsingular Conditioning Set

We now focus on the portfolio allocation conditional on
X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})
for some radius
\gamma>0
. A fiber set of the form
\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\times\mathcal{Y}
was first used in
[
63
]
in the conditional estimation setting with the aim to hedge against noisy covariate information
x_{0}
and also to improve the statistical performance of the solution approach. The results from
[
63
]
rely heavily on the specification of the ambiguity set using the type-
\infty
Wasserstein distance, and these results are not transferrable to the ambiguity set under investigation in this paper. As a parallel counterpart to Section
3
, we will study two separate cases depending on whether the probability requirement
\varepsilon
is zero or strictly positive. We start by discussing the case when
\varepsilon=0
.

4.1.
Null fiber probability
\varepsilon=0

Similar to Section
3.1
, we consider the probability mass constraint of the form
\mathbb{Q}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))>0
with a strict inequality to avoid conditioning on sets of measure zero. Problem (
4
) becomes

(12)

\min\limits_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A},~\beta\in\mathcal{B}}~\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho},\mathbb{Q}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))>0}~\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)|X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})].

Problem (
12
) is of particular interest thanks to its finite sample guarantee: if the samples are independently and identically distributed, and the radius
\rho
is chosen judiciously, then the (unknown) data-generating distribution belongs to
\mathbb{B}_{\rho}
with high probability. As such, the optimal value of problem (
12
) constitutes an upper bound on the worst-case conditional expected loss under the data-generating distribution. One possible way to choose
\rho
to obtain the finite sample guarantee is by using the seminal result from
[
40
]
.
4
4

4

The formal statement of the finite sample guarantee is omitted for brevity. Interested readers may refer to
[
59
]
for similar finite sample guarantee results.

The complication of conditioning on sets of measure zero, as highlighted in Section
3.1
for
\varepsilon=0
, still arises when the radius
\rho
is big enough. To illustrate this problem, we define the following quantity

\rho_{\max}(x_{0},\gamma)\triangleq\inf\left\{\mathds{W}(\mathbb{Q},\widehat{\mathbb{P}}):\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}),~\mathbb{Q}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))=0\right\}.

Intuitively,
\rho_{\max}(x_{0},\gamma)
indicates the minimum budget required to transport all the training samples out of the fiber
\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\times\mathcal{Y}
. If the empirical distribution
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}
satisfies
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))=0
, which means that there is no training samples
(\widehat{x}_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})
falling inside the fiber set
\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\times\mathcal{Y}
, then it is trivial that
\rho_{\max}(x_{0},\gamma)=0
. If
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))>0
, then the value of
\rho_{\max}(x_{0},\gamma)
is known in closed form. To this end, define the following index sets

(13)

\mathcal{I}_{1}=\{i\in[N]:\widehat{x}_{i}\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\},\qquad\mathcal{I}_{2}=\{i\in[N]:\widehat{x}_{i}\not\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\}.

The sets
\mathcal{I}_{1}
and
\mathcal{I}_{2}
divide the training samples into two mutually exclusive sets dependent on whether the training samples fall inside or outside the fiber. For any
i\in[N]
, let
d_{i}
be the distance from
\widehat{x}_{i}
to the boundary
\partial\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})
of the set
\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})
, that is,

(14)

\forall i\in[N]:\qquad d_{i}=\min\limits_{x\in\partial\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})}\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(x,\widehat{x}_{i}).

Note that the distance
d_{i}
defined above is closely related to the values of
\kappa_{i}
defined in (
7
). Indeed, if
\widehat{x}_{i}\not\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})
then
d_{i}
is equal to
\kappa_{i}
. However, if
\widehat{x}_{i}
is in the interior of the set
\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})
then
d_{i}>0
while
\kappa_{i}=0
. Evaluating
d_{i}
is, unfortunately, difficult in general because the set
\partial\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})
may be non-convex. However,
d_{i}
can be computed efficiently under certain choices of
\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}
. For example, when
\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}
is the Euclidean norm, then it is easy to see that

d_{i}=\min\limits_{x:\|x-x_{0}\|_{2}=\gamma}~\|x-\widehat{x}_{i}\|_{2}=\gamma-\|x_{0}-\widehat{x}_{i}\|_{2}

for any
i\in\mathcal{I}_{1}
implying that
\|x_{0}-\widehat{x}_{i}\|_{2}\leq\gamma
.

Using the definition of the set
\mathcal{I}_{1}
and the boundary projection distance
d_{i}
, the maximum radius
\rho_{\max}(x_{0},\gamma)
can be computed in closed form, as asserted by the next proposition.

Proposition 4.1

(Expression for
\rho_{\max}(x_{0},\gamma)
)
.

We have

\rho_{\max}(x_{0},\gamma)=N^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{1}}d_{i}
.

The result of Proposition
4.1
is also intuitive: if
\widehat{x}_{i}\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})
then
i\in\mathcal{I}_{1}
, and we will need a sample-wise budget of
d_{i}/N
to transport
(\widehat{x}_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})
out of the fiber set
\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\times\mathcal{Y}
. The value
\rho_{\max}(x_{0},\gamma)
is thus obtained by summing all
d_{i}/N
over the set
\mathcal{I}_{1}
. If
\mathcal{I}_{1}=\emptyset
then there is no training sample inside the fiber set, and thus
\rho_{\max}(x_{0},\gamma)=0
. Proposition
4.1
also highlights that computing
\rho_{\max}(x_{0},\gamma)
necessitates evaluating
|\mathcal{I}_{1}|
values
d_{i}
for each
i\in\mathcal{I}_{1}
.

The computation of
\rho_{\max}(x_{0},\gamma)
provides a natural upper bound on the radius
\rho
. Indeed, if the radius
\rho
prescribing the ambiguity set is bigger than
\rho_{\max}(x_{0},\gamma)
, we recover the robust worst-case conditional loss. This robust loss is uninformative because it depends only on the support
\mathcal{Y}
, and it is
in
dependent on the training samples. This negative result is reminiscent of Proposition
3.1
and highlights the sophistication of the distributionally robust conditional decision-making problem.

Proposition 4.2

(Uninformative solution when
\rho
is sufficiently large)
.

When
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}_{++}
,
\varepsilon=0
, and
\rho>\rho_{\max}(x_{0},\gamma)
, we have

\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho},\mathbb{Q}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))>0}~\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)|X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})]=\sup\limits_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}~\ell(y,\alpha,\beta).

We now provide a heuristic justification for the result of Proposition
4.2
. To form the worst-case distribution, the adversary first moves all the samples with index in
\mathcal{I}_{1}
out of the fiber, which would cost an amount of energy
\rho_{\max}(x_{0},\gamma)
. After that, the adversary can pick any sample, slice out an infinitesimally small amount of mass, and then move that slice to any point in the fiber
\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\times\mathcal{Y}
. Because
\rho>\rho_{\max}(x_{0},\gamma)
and because the ground metric is continuous, this new arrangement of the samples is feasible and constitutes the worst-case distribution that maximizes the conditional expected loss.

Consider the case where the ambiguity size
\rho
is strictly smaller than the maximum value
\rho_{\max}(x_{0},\gamma)
. It can be shown that in this situation, any distribution
\mathbb{Q}
that is feasible in the supremum problem of (
10
) should satisfy
\mathbb{Q}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))\geq\underline{\varepsilon}
for some strictly positive lower bound
\underline{\varepsilon}
. Further, this value
\underline{\varepsilon}
can be quantified by solving a linear optimization problem.

Proposition 4.3

(Strictly positive probability requirement equivalence)
.

Suppose that
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}_{++}
,
\varepsilon=0
and
\rho<\rho_{\max}(x_{0},\gamma)
. Then there exists an
\underline{\varepsilon}>0
, such that the distributionally robust portfolio allocation model with side information (
10
) is equivalent to

\min_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A},~\beta\in\mathcal{B}}\;\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho},\mathbb{Q}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))\geq\underline{\varepsilon}}~\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)|X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})].

In particular, this equivalence holds for

\underline{\varepsilon}=\min\left\{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{1}}p_{i}:p\in[0,1]^{N},~\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{1}}d_{i}(1-p_{i})\leq\rho\right\}.

The linear program that defines
\underline{\varepsilon}
in Proposition
4.3
can be further shown to be a fractional knapsack problem, and it can be solved efficiently to optimality using a greedy heuristic
[
51
, Proposition 17.1]
. The conditions of Proposition
4.3
are satisfied only when
\rho_{\max}(x_{0},\gamma)>0
, which further implies that
\mathcal{I}_{1}\neq\emptyset
and there exists at least one training sample in the fiber set
\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\times\mathcal{Y}
. With an ambiguity size
\rho
which is strictly smaller than
\rho_{\max}(x_{0},\gamma)
, it is not possible for the adversary to remove all the samples out of the fiber set
\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\times\mathcal{Y}
. The lower bound value
\underline{\varepsilon}
represents the lowest possible amount of probability mass left on the fiber.

The result of Proposition
4.3
indicates that the portfolio allocation problem when
\rho<\rho_{\max}(x_{0},\gamma)
can be obtained by solving the general problem with a strictly positive probability mass requirement. This general case is our subject of study in the next subsection.

4.2.
Strictly positive fiber probability
\varepsilon>0

We now consider the last and most general case with a nonsingular fiber set
\gamma>0
and a strictly positive fiber probability
\varepsilon>0
. More specifically, we aim to solve the portfolio allocation of the form

(15)

\min\limits_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A},~\beta\in\mathcal{B}}~\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho},\mathbb{Q}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))\geq\varepsilon}~\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)|X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})].

Notice that problem (
15
) is also relevant to the case with null probability requirement of Section
4.1
because problem (
15
) is equivalent to problem (
12
) when
\rho<\rho_{\max}(x_{0},\gamma)
by choosing a proper value of
\varepsilon
(cf. Proposition
4.3
).
As the first step towards solving (
15
), we define the following feasible set for the dual variables

(16)

\mathcal{V}\triangleq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(\lambda,s,\nu^{+},\nu^{-},\phi,\varphi,\psi)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}\text{ such that: }\\
\phi-d_{i}\varphi+\psi_{i}-s_{i}\geq 0&\forall i\in\mathcal{I}_{1}\\
\phi+d_{i}\varphi+\psi_{i}-s_{i}\geq 0&\forall i\in\mathcal{I}_{2}\\
\nu^{+}-\nu^{-}+(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{1}}d_{i}-N\rho)\varphi-\sum_{i\in[N]}\psi_{i}\geq 0\\
\varphi-\lambda_{i}\geq 0&\forall i\in[N]\end{array}\right\}.

The next theorem presents the finite-dimensional reformulation of the worst-case conditional expected loss.

Theorem 4.4

(Worst-case conditional expected loss for
\varepsilon>0
)
.

Suppose that
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}_{++}
,
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}_{++}
, and
\rho>\rho_{\min}(x_{0},\gamma,\varepsilon)
. Let the parameters
d_{i}
be defined as in (
14
). For any feasible solution
(\alpha,\beta)
, we have

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho},\mathbb{Q}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))\geq\varepsilon}~\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)|X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})]=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cll}\inf&\phi+(N\varepsilon)^{-1}\nu^{+}-N^{-1}\nu^{-}\\
\st&(\lambda,s,\nu^{+},\nu^{-},\phi,\varphi,\psi)\in\mathcal{V}\\
&s_{i}\geq\sup\limits_{y_{i}\in\mathcal{Y}}~\{\ell(y_{i},\alpha,\beta)-\lambda_{i}\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(y_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})\}&\forall i\in[N].\end{array}\right.

We can join the minimization operator over
(\alpha,\beta)
to the result of Theorem
4.4
, and the portfolio allocation problem with side information (
15
) is equivalent to the following finite-dimensional optimization problem

(17)

\begin{array}[]{cll}\min&\phi+(N\varepsilon)^{-1}\nu^{+}-N^{-1}\nu^{-}\\
\st&\alpha\in\mathcal{A},~\beta\in\mathcal{B},~(\lambda,s,\nu^{+},\nu^{-},\phi,\varphi,\psi)\in\mathcal{V}\\
&s_{i}\geq\sup\limits_{y_{i}\in\mathcal{Y}}~\{\ell(y_{i},\alpha,\beta)-\lambda_{i}\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(y_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})\}&\forall i\in[N].\end{array}

The last constraint of problem (
17
) is a semi-infinite constraint that can be reformulated into a set of semi-definite constraints under specific situations. These results are highlighted in Proposition
4.5
and
4.6
.

Proposition 4.5

(Mean-variance loss function)
.

Suppose that
\ell
is the mean-variance loss function of the form (
5
),
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}_{++}
,
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
and
\rho>\rho_{\min}(x_{0},\gamma,\varepsilon)
. Suppose in addition that
\mathcal{Y}=\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(y,\widehat{y})=\|y-\widehat{y}\|_{2}^{2}
. Let the parameters
d_{i}
be defined as in (
14
). The distributionally robust portfolio allocation model with side information (
17
) is equivalent to the semi-definite optimization problem

\begin{array}[]{cll}\min&\phi+(N\varepsilon)^{-1}\nu^{+}-N^{-1}\nu^{-}\\
\st&\alpha\in\mathcal{A},~\beta\in\mathcal{B},~t\in\mathbb{R}_{+},~A_{i}\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{m}\quad\forall i\in[N]\\
&(\lambda,s,\nu^{+},\nu^{-},\phi,\varphi,\psi)\in\mathcal{V}\\
&\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{i}I-A_{i}&\alpha\\
\alpha^{\top}&1\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0,~\begin{bmatrix}A_{i}&(\beta+\eta/2)\alpha-\lambda_{i}\widehat{y}_{i}\\
(\beta+\eta/2)\alpha^{\top}-\lambda_{i}\widehat{y}_{i}^{\top}&s_{i}+\lambda_{i}\|\widehat{y}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}-t\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0&\forall i\in[N]\\
&\begin{bmatrix}t&\beta\\
\beta&1\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0.\end{array}

Proposition 4.6

(Mean-CVaR loss function)
.

Suppose that
\ell
is the mean-CVaR loss function of the form (
6
),
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}_{++}
,
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
and
\rho>\rho_{\min}(x_{0},\gamma,\varepsilon)
. Suppose in addition that
\mathcal{Y}=\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(y,\widehat{y})=\|y-\widehat{y}\|_{2}^{2}
. Let the parameters
d_{i}
be defined as in (
14
). The distributionally robust portfolio allocation model with side information (
17
) is equivalent to the semi-definite optimization problem

\begin{array}[]{cll}\min&\phi+(N\varepsilon)^{-1}\nu^{+}-N^{-1}\nu^{-}\\
\st&\alpha\in\mathcal{A},~\beta\in\mathcal{B},~(\lambda,s,\nu^{+},\nu^{-},\phi,\varphi,\psi)\in\mathcal{V}\\
&\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{i}I&\frac{\eta}{2}\alpha-\lambda_{i}\widehat{y}_{i}\\
\frac{\eta}{2}\alpha^{\top}-\lambda_{i}\widehat{y}_{i}^{\top}&s_{i}+\lambda_{i}\|\widehat{y}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}-\beta\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0,~\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{i}I&\frac{\eta\tau+1}{2\tau}\alpha-\lambda_{i}\widehat{y}_{i}\\
\frac{\tau+1}{2\tau}\alpha^{\top}-\lambda_{i}\widehat{y}_{i}^{\top}&s_{i}+\lambda_{i}\|\widehat{y}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}-(1-1/\tau)\beta\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0&\forall i\in[N].\end{array}

Both Proposition
4.5
and
4.6
leverage the fact that
\mathcal{Y}=\mathbb{R}^{m}
to reformulate the problem using semi-definite constraint. Again, under the assumption
\mathcal{Y}=\mathbb{R}^{m}
, the reformulation (
17
) is a conservative approximation of the distributionally robust conditional portfolio allocation problem; thus the reformulations in Proposition
4.5
and
4.6
are also conservative approximations of the distributionally robust conditional mean-variance and mean-CVaR portfolio allocation problems, respectively. In case
\mathcal{Y}
is an ellipsoid with a non-empty interior of the form
\mathcal{Y}=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:y^{\top}Qy+2q^{\top}y+q_{0}\leq 0\}
for some symmetric matrix
Q
, then the S-lemma
[
67
]
can also be applied to devise a similar semi-definite optimization problem, the details can be found in Appendix
B
.

Despite having a fractional objective function, the results in this section assert that distributionally robust portfolio allocation with side information problems can be solved efficiently using convex conic programming solvers. This is in stark contrast with existing results in distributionally robust fractional programming where only
non
convex reformulations are available, and the optimal solution is obtained by solving a sequence of convex optimization problems after bisection
[
45
,
83
]
.

5.
Numerical Experiment

We conduct a comprehensive numerical experiment using real data with the following methods: (i) the equal-weighted model, (ii) the unconditional model, (iii) the distributionally robust unconditional model, (iv) the conditional model, (v) the distributionally robust conditional model with type-
\infty
Wasserstein ambiguity set, and (vi) our proposed optimal transport conditional model. We experiment with two popular portfolio allocation strategies: the mean-variance criterion in Section
5.1
and the mean-CVaR criterion in Section
5.2
. The details about the optimization models for the mean-variance and mean-CVaR problems will be provided in the corresponding section.

Asset return and side information:
For the asset returns, we take the historical S&P500 constituents data from January 01, 2017, to March 31, 2023.
5
5

5

The data is downloaded from the Wharton Research Data Services:
https://wrds-www.wharton.upenn.edu
.
To ensure a stable portfolio construction, we retain only assets that have been in the S&P500 since 2010, which gives a universe of 399 assets in total. The sampling frequency is one sample per day: at day
t
, the response variable
Y
is defined as the 1-day stock percentage return from day
t
to day
t+1
. For the side information, we follow
[
22
]
and use five publicly available market indices: (i) Volatility Index (VIX), (ii) 10-year Treasury Yield Index (TNX), (iii) Crude Oil Index (CL=F), (iv) S&P 500 (GSPC), (v) Dow Jones Index (DJI) to construct the side information covariate
X\in\mathbb{R}^{5}
.
6
6

6

Side information indices can be downloaded from yfinance:
https://pypi.org/project/yfinance/
.

We preprocess the side information using the Gaussian kernel bandwidth selection method. Namely, over the period from January 1, 2017 to December 31, 2020, we pick the side information indices as the predictors and pick the unweighted average stock return (averaged over the asset universe ) as the target values. We then use the least-squares cross-validation method from
[
54
]
to estimate the optimal Gaussian kernel bandwidth for each side information index in the Nadaraya-Watson kernel prediction. Then for each side information index, we divide the raw values by the corresponding optimal bandwidth.

Parameter tuning:
We tune the hyperparameters of each portfolio allocation method as follows. At each trading day
t
in the period between January 1, 2019 and December 31, 2020, we randomly sample
m=50
stocks from the asset universe to create a
stock pool
instance. Then, we use the historical observations of the side information indices and the asset returns in the stock pool in the two-year window (
252\times 2
observations) before
t
to form the nominal distribution
\hat{\mathbb{P}}
. The covariate
x_{0}
for time
t
is chosen as the observed side information at time
t
. We then find the optimal portfolio allocation of each method by solving the corresponding optimization problem with varying hyperparameter values. The validation block consists of the subsequent
60
trading days after
t
: we evaluate the expected loss of the portfolio by calculating the objective value
\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)]
using these 60 samples. We pick the hyperparameters that
minimize
the average expected loss over the whole tuning period from January 1, 2019, to December 31, 2020.

Out-of-sample performance computation:
To obtain the out-of-sample performance, we evaluate each method with the optimal hyperparameters in a rolling horizon scheme on the two-year test period from January 1, 2021, to December 31, 2022. On each trading day in the test period, we randomly sample
m=50
assets from the universe to form the stock pool, and we pick the prior two-year window data on the side information and asset returns to form the nominal distribution. We solve the allocation problem associated with each method using the corresponding tuned hyperparameters to obtain the portfolio allocation, and then we calculate the realized daily percentage return and expected loss of this portfolio based on the empirical distribution of the subsequent 60 days.

If we denote the daily percentage return of a portfolio by
r_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,60
, then the annualized Sharpe ratio is computed as:

\mbox{annualized Sharpe ratio}:=\sqrt{252}\times\left(\widehat{\mbox{mean}}(\{r_{i}\}_{i=1,\ldots,60})\Big/\widehat{\mbox{std}}(\{r_{i}\}_{i=1,\ldots,60})\right),

where
\widehat{\mbox{mean}}
and
\widehat{\mbox{std}}
denote the empirical mean and standard deviation estimator, respectively.

The test procedure is repeated for trading days from the test period, resulting in
252\times 2
independent test experiments. For each optimally tuned method, we compute
252\times 2
Sharpe ratios, and will report on the average value. Other performance metrics of the portfolios, such as the expected loss
\ell(\cdot)
values, the mean returns, the CVaR values, and the standard deviations, are computed using the empirical distribution of the 60 subsequent realized returns, and averaged over
252\times 2
test experiments.

Feasible set.
All methods share the same feasible set for the optimization problems. If an asset in the stock pool is not tradable at day
t
, we impose its weight as zero.
In addition, we prohibit short selling by enforcing the investment weight vector
\alpha
to be non-negative.
Thus, the feasible region for the portfolio allocation problem is a subset of the non-negative simplex

\mathcal{A}=\left\{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m}\mid\sum_{i\in[m]}\alpha_{i}=1,~\alpha_{j}=0\mbox{ if stock }j\mbox{ is not tradable}\right\}.

Notice that the feasible set
\mathcal{B}
is set to
\mathbb{R}
throughout the experiment. The ground costs are chosen with
\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(x,\widehat{x})=\|x-\widehat{x}\|_{2}^{2}
and
\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(y,\widehat{y})=\|y-\widehat{y}\|_{2}^{2}
. All optimization problems are solved using the MOSEK quadratic program solver
[
60
]
.
Due to the computational complexity of the hyperparameter fine-tuning process, we do not consider baselines that are reformulated as semi-definite programs in the experiment. We also provide computational solution time on the mean-variance and mean-CVaR models with
m=50
and
399
assets in Appendix
D
.
All codes, data, and results are available at:

https://github.com/shanshanwang2019/Robustifying-Conditional-Portfolio-Decisions-via-Optimal-Transport
.

5.1.
Mean-Variance Portfolio Allocation Problems

We first consider the portfolio allocation problem using the mean-variance criterion as in Lemma
2.2
. Below, the loss function
\ell
is prescribed in (
5
). We compare the following methods:

(i)

the Equal Weighted model (EW): in this case, the portfolio allocation solves

(EW)

\min\limits_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}~\|\alpha\|_{2}.

If all stocks are tradable, then the EW allocation coincides with the
1/m
-portfolio, which is renowned for its robustness
[
27
]
. This portfolio is parameter-free, and was previously shown to be the limit of the distributionally robust portfolio allocations as the ambiguity size increases
[
65
]
.

(ii)

the unconditional Mean-Variance model (MV)
[
58
]
: the portfolio allocation solves

(MV)

\min\limits_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A},~\beta\in\mathbb{R}}\mathds{E}_{\widehat{\mathbb{P}}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)]

with the distribution
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}
is the empirical distribution supported on the available return data.

(iii)

the Distributionally Robust unconditional Mean-Variance model (DRMV): the portfolio allocation solves

(DRMV)

\min\limits_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}~\sqrt{\alpha^{\top}\textrm{Variance}_{\widehat{\mathbb{P}}}(Y)\alpha}-\eta\cdot\alpha^{\top}\mathds{E}_{\widehat{\mathbb{P}}}[Y]+\sqrt{(1+\eta^{2})\rho}\|\alpha\|_{2}.

The above optimization problem is the reformulation of the distributionally robust mean-variance portfolio allocation with a Wasserstein ambiguity set on the distributions of the asset returns
Y

[
14
]
. The tuning parameter for this method is
\rho\in\{0.05,0.1,0.25\}
.

(iv)

the Conditional Mean-Variance model (CMV): the portfolio allocation solves

(CMV)

\min\limits_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A},~\beta\in\mathbb{R}}\mathds{E}_{\widehat{\mathbb{P}}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)|X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})].

The parameter
\gamma
is set to the
a
-quantile of the empirical distribution of the distance between
x_{0}
and the training covariate vectors
7
7

7

More precisely, this is the empirical distribution supported on
\Delta_{i}=\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(\widehat{x}_{i},x_{0})
for
i=1,\ldots,N
.
, where the quantile value is in the range
a\in\{5\%,10\%,25\%\}
. Notice that by the choice of
\gamma
, the empirical distribution satisfies
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))>0
and the conditional expectation is well-defined.

(v)

the Distributionally Robust Conditional Mean-Variance model (DRCMV) with type-
\infty
Wasserstein ambiguity set
[
63
]
, in which the portfolio allocation solves

(DRCMV)

\min\limits_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A},~\beta\in\mathbb{R}}\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}^{\infty}_{\rho},\mathbb{Q}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))>0}~\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)|X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})].

x_{0}
and the training covariate vectors, where the quantile value is in the range

[
63

\varepsilon\in\{0.05,0.1,0.15\}

Summary of Empirical Findings

The empirical experiments presented above indicate that OTCMV and OTCMV can significantly impact the out-of-sample performance of portfolios produced in an environment where side information can be exploited. As with any machine/statistical learning method, the performance of OTCMV and OTCMC will depend on the nature of the application, the quality of the data, and the choice of side information that is used. We expect that additional empirical evaluation with other data and application environments would benefit our understanding of the value of our proposed OTCMV and OTCMC approaches.

Lemma A.1

(Interchange)
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{Y}
and
\mathcal{B}
are compact and that
\ell
is continuous in
Y
and convex in
\beta
. Then we have

\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho},\mathbb{Q}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))\geq\varepsilon}\inf\limits_{\beta\in\mathcal{B}}~\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)|X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})]=\inf\limits_{\beta\in\mathcal{B}}\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho},\mathbb{Q}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))\geq\varepsilon}~\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)|X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})].

A similar result on the interchange of the infimum and the supremum operators is obtained in
[
63
, Lemma B.3]
. Nevertheless, there is a subtle distinction in the conditions of the two results: Lemma
A.1
requires that
\mathcal{Y}
and
\mathcal{B}
are compact, while
[
63
, Lemma B.3]
does not require these compactness conditions. This distinction emphasizes the qualitative difference between an
\infty
-Wasserstein ambiguity set of
[
63
]
and the optimal transport ambiguity set
\mathbb{B}_{\rho}
in this paper. Indeed, the weak-compactness condition is automatically satisfied by the
\infty
-Wasserstein set in
[
63
]
. Still, this condition is not satisfied by the set
\mathbb{B}_{\rho}
, which leads to the additional requirement on the compactness of
\mathcal{B}
in Lemma
A.1
. Further, Lemma
A.1
requires the compactness of
\mathcal{Y}
for the upper-continuity of the objective function in
\mathbb{Q}
. On the contrary, the continuity condition is automatically satisfied by the
\infty
-Wasserstein set in
[
63
]
.

The proof of Lemma
A.1
follows from the convexity of the conditional ambiguity set. To this end, define the following ambiguity set

\mathcal{B}_{x_{0},\gamma,\varepsilon}(\mathbb{B}_{\rho})\triangleq\left\{\mu_{x_{0}}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{Y}):\begin{array}[]{l}\exists\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho}\text{ such that }\mathbb{Q}(\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\times\mathcal{Y})\geq\varepsilon\\
~\mathbb{Q}(\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\times A)=\mu_{x_{0}}(A)\mathbb{Q}(\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\times\mathcal{Y})~~\forall A\subseteq\mathcal{Y}~\text{ measurable}\end{array}\right\}.

(Convexity of
\mathcal{B}_{x_{0},\varepsilon,\gamma}(\mathbb{B}_{\rho})

.

If
\mathbb{B}_{\rho}
is convex, then the conditional ambiguity set
\mathcal{B}_{x_{0},\gamma,\varepsilon}(\mathbb{B}_{\rho})
is also convex.

The proof of Lemma

Proof of Lemma
A.2
.

Let
\mu_{0}^{0},~\mu_{0}^{1}\in\mathcal{B}_{x_{0},\gamma,\varepsilon}(\mathbb{B}_{\rho})
be two arbitrary probability measures supported on
\mathcal{Y}
. Associated with each
\mu_{0}^{j}
,
j\in\{0,1\}

\mathbb{Q}^{j}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})
such that

\mathbb{Q}^{j}(\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\times\mathcal{Y})\geq\varepsilon\quad\text{and}\quad\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{Q}^{j}(\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\times A)}{\mathbb{Q}^{j}(\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\times\mathcal{Y})}=\mu_{0}^{j}(A)\quad\forall A\subseteq\mathcal{Y}~\text{measurable}.

Select any
\lambda\in(0,1)
. We now show that
\mu_{0}^{\lambda}=\lambda\mu_{0}^{1}+(1-\lambda)\mu_{0}^{0}\in\mathcal{B}_{x_{0},\gamma,\varepsilon}(\mathbb{B}_{\rho})
. Indeed, consider the joint measure

\mathbb{Q}^{\lambda}=\theta\mathbb{Q}^{1}+(1-\theta)\mathbb{Q}^{0},

where
\theta
is defined as

\theta=\frac{\lambda\mathbb{Q}^{0}(\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\times\mathcal{Y})}{\lambda\mathbb{Q}^{0}(\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\times\mathcal{Y})+(1-\lambda)\mathbb{Q}^{1}(\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\times\mathcal{Y})}\in[0,1].

By definition, we have
\mathbb{Q}^{\lambda}(\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\times\mathcal{Y})\geq\varepsilon
, and by the convexity of
\mathbb{B}_{\rho}
, we have
\mathbb{Q}^{\lambda}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho}
. Moreover, for any set
A\subseteq\mathcal{Y}
measurable, we find

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{Q}^{\lambda}(\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\times A)}{\mathbb{Q}^{\lambda}(\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\times\mathcal{Y})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\theta\mathbb{Q}^{1}(\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\times A)+(1-\theta)\mathbb{Q}^{0}(\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\times A)}{\theta\mathbb{Q}^{1}(\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\times\mathcal{Y})+(1-\theta)\mathbb{Q}^{0}(\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\times\mathcal{Y})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda\mathbb{Q}^{0}(\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\times\mathcal{Y})\mathbb{Q}^{1}(\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\times A)+(1-\lambda)\mathbb{Q}^{1}(\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\times\mathcal{Y})\mathbb{Q}^{0}(\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\times A)}{\mathbb{Q}^{0}(\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\times\mathcal{Y})\mathbb{Q}^{1}(\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\times\mathcal{Y})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda\mathbb{Q}^{1}(\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\times A)}{\mathbb{Q}^{1}(\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\times\mathcal{Y})}+\frac{(1-\lambda)\mathbb{Q}^{0}(\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\times A)}{\mathbb{Q}^{0}(\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\times\mathcal{Y})}

\displaystyle=\lambda\mu_{0}^{1}(A)+(1-\lambda)\mu_{0}^{0}(A),

where the second equality holds thanks to the definition of
\theta
. This line of argument implies that
\mu_{0}^{\lambda}\in\mathcal{B}_{x_{0},\gamma,\varepsilon}(\mathbb{B}_{\rho})
, and further asserts the convexity of
\mathcal{B}_{x_{0},\gamma,\varepsilon}(\mathbb{B}_{\rho})
.
∎

Proof of Lemma

.

By rewriting the conditional expectation using the conditional measure

, we have for any value of
\alpha

\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho},\mathbb{Q}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))\geq\varepsilon}\inf\limits_{\beta\in\mathcal{B}}~\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)|X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})]=\sup\limits_{\mu_{0}\in\mathcal{B}_{x_{0},\gamma,\varepsilon}(\mathbb{B}_{\rho})}~\inf\limits_{\beta\in\mathcal{B}}~\mathds{E}_{\mu_{0}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)].

The set
\mathcal{B}_{x_{0},\gamma,\varepsilon}(\mathbb{B}_{\rho})
is convex by Lemma
A.2
.
Moreover, because
\ell
is continuous in
Y
, the mapping
\mu_{0}\mapsto\mathds{E}_{\mu_{0}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)]
is upper semicontinuous in the weak topology thanks to the compactness of
\mathcal{Y}
. Finally,
\mathcal{B}
is compact and
\ell
is convex in
\beta
. The interchangeability of the supremum and the infimum operators is now a consequence of Sion’s minimax theorem
[
77
]
.
∎

We are ready to prove the results in Section
2
.

Proof of Lemma
2.2
.

It is well known that

\mathrm{Variance}_{\mathbb{Q}}[Y^{\top}\alpha|X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})]=\min\limits_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}}~\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[(Y^{\top}\alpha-\beta)^{2}|X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})]

for any probability measure
\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})
, where the optimal
\beta
is the conditional mean of
Y^{\top}\alpha
given
X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})
under
\mathbb{Q}
. When
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{Y}
are compact, the random variable
Y^{\top}\alpha
has a bounded mean for any
\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho}
. More precisely, we have

\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[Y^{\top}\alpha|X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})]\in\mathcal{B},

where
\mathcal{B}
is defined as in the statement of the lemma. Therefore, it is without any loss of optimality to restrict
\beta\in\mathcal{B}
. We thus find

\displaystyle\min\limits_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}~\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho},\mathbb{Q}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))\geq\varepsilon}~\mathrm{Variance}_{\mathbb{Q}}[Y^{\top}\alpha|X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})]-\eta\cdot\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[Y^{\top}\alpha|X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min\limits_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}~\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho},\mathbb{Q}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))\geq\varepsilon}~\min_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}}~\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)|X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min\limits_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}~\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho},\mathbb{Q}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))\geq\varepsilon}~\min_{\beta\in\mathcal{B}}~\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)|X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min\limits_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}~\min_{\beta\in\mathcal{B}}~\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho},\mathbb{Q}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))\geq\varepsilon}~\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)|X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})],

where the last equality follows from Lemma
A.1
. This completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Lemma
2.3
.

It is well known that

\mathrm{CVaR}^{1-\tau}_{\mathbb{Q}}[Y^{\top}\alpha|X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})]=\min\limits_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}}~\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\beta+\frac{1}{\tau}(-Y^{\top}\alpha-\beta)^{+}|X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})]

for any probability measure
\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})
, where the optimal
\beta
is the conditional
(1-\tau)
-quantile of
Y^{\top}\alpha
given
X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})
under
\mathbb{Q}
. When
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{Y}
are compact, the random variable
Y^{\top}\alpha
has uniformly bounded support
\mathcal{B}
for any
\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho}
and
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
, where
\mathcal{B}
is defined as in the statement of the lemma. Therefore, it is without any loss of optimality to restrict
\beta\in\mathcal{B}
. The rest of the proof follows the same argument in the proof of Lemma
2.2
.
∎

The proofs of Lemmas
2.2
and
2.3
rely on a strong duality result. If strong duality fails, one can still resort to weak duality to obtain a conservative approximation of the decision problem. This fact is highlighted in the next remark.

Remark A.3

(Conservative approximation under weak duality)
.

If the conditions of Lemma
A.1
do not hold, then the robustified conditional mean-variance portfolio problem still admits

\displaystyle\min\limits_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}~\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho},\mathbb{Q}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))\geq\varepsilon}~\mathrm{Variance}_{\mathbb{Q}}[Y^{\top}\alpha|X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})]-\eta\cdot\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[Y^{\top}\alpha|X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min\limits_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}~\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho},\mathbb{Q}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))\geq\varepsilon}~\min_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}}~\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)|X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min\limits_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}~\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho},\mathbb{Q}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))\geq\varepsilon}~\min_{\beta\in\mathcal{B}}~\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)|X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\min\limits_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}~\min_{\beta\in\mathcal{B}}~\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho},\mathbb{Q}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))\geq\varepsilon}~\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)|X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})],

where the inequality is from weak duality. Thus, the ultimate min-max problem is a conservative approximation of the robustified conditional mean-variance portfolio problem. A similar argument also holds for the mean-CVaR problem.

Proof of Proposition
2.5
.

Using the definition of the optimal transport cost, we can compute
\rho_{\min}(x_{0},\gamma,\varepsilon)
as

\rho_{\min}(x_{0},\gamma,\varepsilon)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\inf&\displaystyle\int_{(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})\times(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})}[\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(x,x^{\prime})+\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(y,y^{\prime})]~\pi\big((\mathrm{d}x\times\mathrm{d}y),(\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}\times\mathrm{d}y^{\prime})\big)\\
\st&\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}),~\pi\in\Pi(\mathbb{Q},\widehat{\mathbb{P}})\\
&\displaystyle\int_{(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})\times(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})}(x)~\pi\big((\mathrm{d}x\times\mathrm{d}y),(\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}\times\mathrm{d}y^{\prime})\big)\geq\varepsilon.\end{array}\right.

Because
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}
is an empirical measure, any joint probability measure
\pi\in\Pi(\mathbb{Q},\widehat{\mathbb{P}})
can be written as
\pi=N^{-1}\sum_{i\in[N]}\pi_{i}\otimes\delta_{(\widehat{x}_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})}
using the collection of probability measures
\{\pi_{i}\}_{i\in[N]}
, and
\otimes
denotes the Kronecker product of two probability measures. One thus can reformulate
\rho_{\min}(x_{0},\gamma,\varepsilon)
as

\displaystyle\rho_{\min}(x_{0},\gamma,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\inf&\displaystyle N^{-1}\sum_{i\in[N]}\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}[\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(x,\widehat{x}_{i})+\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(y,\widehat{y}_{i})]~\pi_{i}(\mathrm{d}x\times\mathrm{d}y)\\
\st&\pi_{i}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})~~\forall i\in[N],~\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[N]}\pi_{i}(\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\times\mathcal{Y})\geq N\varepsilon.\end{array}\right.

Let
\{\pi_{i}^{\star}\}_{i\in[N]}
be an optimal solution of the above optimization problem. We now show that
\pi_{i}^{\star}
should be of the form

\pi_{i}^{\star}=\upsilon_{i}\delta_{(\widehat{x}_{i}^{p},\widehat{y}_{i})}+(1-\upsilon_{i})\delta_{(\widehat{x}_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})}

for some
\upsilon_{i}\in[0,1]
and where
\widehat{x}_{i}^{p}
is the projection of
\widehat{x}_{i}
onto
\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})
defined in (
7
). Suppose otherwise, then denote
P_{i}=\pi_{i}^{\star}(\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\times\mathcal{Y})
. Consider now

\pi_{i}^{\prime}=P_{i}\delta_{(\widehat{x}_{i}^{p},\widehat{y}_{i})}+(1-P_{i})\delta_{(\widehat{x}_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})}.

It is trivial that
\sum_{i\in[N]}\pi_{i}^{\prime}(\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\times\mathcal{Y})\geq\sum_{i\in[N]}P_{i}=\sum_{i\in[N]}\pi_{i}^{\star}(\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\times\mathcal{Y})\geq N\varepsilon
. Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[N]}\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}[\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(x,\widehat{x}_{i})+\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(y,\widehat{y}_{i})]~\pi_{i}^{\prime}(\mathrm{d}x\times\mathrm{d}y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[N]}[\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(\widehat{x}_{i}^{p},\widehat{x}_{i})+\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(\widehat{y}_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})]P_{i}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in[N]}\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}[\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(x,\widehat{x}_{i})+\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(y,\widehat{y}_{i})]~\pi_{i}^{\star}(\mathrm{d}x\times\mathrm{d}y),

which implies that
\{\pi_{i}^{\prime}\}_{i\in[N]}
is at least as good as
\{\pi_{i}^{\star}\}_{i\in[N]}
in the optimization problem (
A.1
).

Next, by restricting the decision variables to
\pi_{i}=\upsilon_{i}\delta_{(\widehat{x}_{i}^{p},\widehat{y}_{i})}+(1-\upsilon_{i})\delta_{(\widehat{x}_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})}
, one now can consider the equivalent reformulation

\displaystyle\rho_{\min}(x_{0},\gamma,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\inf\left\{\displaystyle N^{-1}\sum_{i\in[N]}\kappa_{i}\upsilon_{i}:\upsilon\in[0,1]^{N},~\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i}\geq N\varepsilon\right\}.

Because the feasible set is compact and the objective function is continuous, the minimization operator is justified thanks to Weierstrass’ maximum value theorem
[
1
, Corollary 2.35]
.

It remains to show the existence of a measure
\mathbb{Q}^{\star}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho}
with
\mathbb{Q}^{\star}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))\geq\varepsilon
if and only if
\rho\geq\rho_{\min}(x_{0},\gamma,\varepsilon)
. The definition of
\rho_{\min}(x_{0},\gamma,\varepsilon)
immediately proves the “only if” part, because (
8
) implies that

\mathbb{B}_{\rho}\cap\Big\{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}):~\mathbb{Q}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))\geq\varepsilon\Big\}=\emptyset\quad\forall\rho<\rho_{\min}(x_{0},\gamma,\varepsilon).

Now we prove the “if” part. Given a minimizer
\{\upsilon_{i}^{\star}\}_{i\in[N]}
that solves (
9
), there exists a collection of probability measures
\{\pi_{i}^{\star}\}_{i\in[N]}
defined as
\pi_{i}^{\star}\triangleq\upsilon_{i}^{\star}\delta_{(\widehat{x}_{i}^{p},\widehat{y}_{i})}+(1-\upsilon_{i}^{\star})\delta_{(\widehat{x}_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})}
such that

\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}[\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(x,\widehat{x}_{i})+\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(y,\widehat{y}_{i})]~\pi_{i}^{\star}(\mathrm{d}x\times\mathrm{d}y)=\kappa_{i}\upsilon_{i}^{\star}.

The probability measure
\mathbb{Q}^{\star}\triangleq N^{-1}\sum_{i\in[N]}\pi_{i}^{\star}
satisfies

\mathds{W}(\mathbb{Q}^{\star},\widehat{\mathbb{P}})\leq\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}[\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(x,\widehat{x}_{i})+\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(y,\widehat{y}_{i})]~\pi_{i}^{\star}(\mathrm{d}x\times\mathrm{d}y)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}\kappa_{i}\upsilon_{i}^{\star}=\rho_{\min}(x_{0},\gamma,\varepsilon)\leq\rho,

where the last equality is from the optimality of
\upsilon^{\star}
. This implies that
\mathbb{Q}^{\star}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho}
and completes the proof.
∎

A.2.
Proofs of Section
3

We first collect the fundamental results that facilitate the proofs of Section
3
. For any
N\in\mathbb{N}
, given some
c\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
d\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, consider the following two optimization problems

(20a)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\sup\limits_{\upsilon\in[0,1]^{N}}&\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{i}\upsilon_{i}c_{i}}{N\sum_{i}\upsilon_{i}}\\
\st&\displaystyle\sum_{i}\upsilon_{i}\geq\varepsilon N,~\sum_{i}\upsilon_{i}d_{i}\leq\rho\end{array}\right.

and

(20b)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\sup\limits_{\upsilon\in[0,1]^{N}}&\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{i}\upsilon_{i}c_{i}}{N\sum_{i}\upsilon_{i}}\\
\st&\displaystyle\sum_{i}\upsilon_{i}=\varepsilon N,~\sum_{i}\upsilon_{i}d_{i}\leq\rho,\end{array}\right.

where the summations are taken over
i\in[N]
. Notice that the summation constraint of
\upsilon_{i}
in (
20a
) is an
in
equality constraint, while in (
20b
) it is an equality constraint. The next result asserts that the inequality in (
20a
) can be strengthened to an equality as in (
20b
) without any loss of optimality.

Lemma A.4
.

The optimal values of problems (
20a
) and (
20b
) are equal.

Proof of Lemma
A.4
.

Let
\bar{\upsilon}\in[0,1]^{N}
be such that
\sum_{i}\bar{\upsilon}_{i}>\varepsilon N
and
\sum_{i}\bar{\upsilon}_{i}d_{i}\leq\rho
. One can construct
\upsilon^{\prime}=(\varepsilon N/\sum_{i}\bar{\upsilon}_{i})\bar{\upsilon}
which satisfies
\upsilon^{\prime}\in[0,1]^{N}
,
\sum_{i}\upsilon_{i}^{\prime}=\varepsilon N
, and
\sum_{i}\upsilon_{i}^{\prime}d_{i}\leq\rho
since
d\geq 0
. Furthermore, it reaches the same objective value

\frac{1}{N\sum_{i}\upsilon_{i}^{\prime}}\sum_{i}\upsilon_{i}^{\prime}c_{i}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon N^{2}}\sum_{i}\frac{\varepsilon N}{\sum_{i}\bar{\upsilon}_{i}}\bar{\upsilon}_{i}c_{i}=\frac{1}{N\sum_{i}\bar{\upsilon}_{i}}\sum_{i}\bar{\upsilon}_{i}c_{i},

which finishes the proof.
∎

Proposition A.5

(Equivalent representation)
.

Given
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
and
\rho>\rho_{\min}(x_{0},0,\varepsilon)
, then for any feasible solution
(\alpha,\beta)
,
we have

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho},\mathbb{Q}(X=x_{0})\geq\varepsilon}~\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)|X=x_{0}]=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\sup&\displaystyle(N\varepsilon)^{-1}\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i}\mathds{E}_{\mu_{0}^{i}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)]\\
\st&\upsilon\in[0,\,1]^{N},\;\mu_{0}^{i}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{Y})\qquad\forall i\in[N]\\
&\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i}=N\varepsilon\\
&\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i}\big(\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{0},\widehat{x}_{i})+\mathds{E}_{\mu_{0}^{i}}[\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(Y,\widehat{y}_{i})]\big)\leq N\rho.\end{array}\right.

Proof of Proposition
A.5
.

By exploiting the definition of the optimal transport cost and the fact that any joint probability measure
\pi\in\Pi(\mathbb{Q},\widehat{\mathbb{P}})
can be written as
\pi=N^{-1}\sum_{i\in[N]}\pi_{i}\otimes\delta_{(\widehat{x}_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})}
, where each
\pi_{i}
is a probability measure on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
, we have

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho},\mathbb{Q}(X=x_{0})\geq\varepsilon}~\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)|X=x_{0}]=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\sup&\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)|X=x_{0}]\\
\st&\pi_{i}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})\quad\forall i\in[N]\\
&\sum_{i\in[N]}\pi_{i}(\{x_{0}\}\times\mathcal{Y})\geq N\varepsilon\\
&\mathbb{Q}=N^{-1}\sum_{i\in[N]}\pi_{i},~\sum_{i\in[N]}\mathds{W}(\pi_{i},\delta_{(\widehat{x}_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})})\leq N\rho\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccll}\sup\limits_{\upsilon\in\mathcal{U}}&\sup&\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)|X=x_{0}]\\
&\st&\pi_{i}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})&\forall i\in[N]\\
&&\pi_{i}(\{x_{0}\}\times\mathcal{Y})=\upsilon_{i}&\forall i\in[N]\\
&&\mathbb{Q}=N^{-1}\sum_{i\in[N]}\pi_{i},~\sum_{i\in[N]}\mathds{W}(\pi_{i},\delta_{(\widehat{x}_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})})\leq N\rho,\end{array}\right.

where the set
\mathcal{U}
is defined as

\mathcal{U}\triangleq\left\{\upsilon\in[0,1]^{N}:\sum_{i\in[N]}~\upsilon_{i}\geq N\varepsilon\right\}.

Define the following two functions
g,h:\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}
as

(21)

g(\upsilon)\triangleq\left\{\begin{array}[]{cll}\sup&\displaystyle(\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i})^{-1}\sum_{i\in[N]}\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\ell(y,\alpha,\beta)\pi_{i}(\{x_{0}\}\times\mathrm{d}y)\\
\st&\pi_{i}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})&\forall i\in[N]\\
&\pi_{i}(\{x_{0}\}\times\mathcal{Y})=\upsilon_{i}&\forall i\in[N]\\
&\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[N]}\mathds{W}(\pi_{i},\delta_{(\widehat{x}_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})})\leq N\rho\end{array}\right.

and

(22)

h(\upsilon)\triangleq\left\{\begin{array}[]{cll}\sup&\displaystyle(\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i})^{-1}\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i}\mathds{E}_{\mu_{0}^{i}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)]\\
\st&\mu_{0}^{i}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{Y})&\forall i\in[N]\\
&\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i}\mathds{E}_{\mu_{0}^{i}}[\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{0},\widehat{x}_{i})+\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(Y,\widehat{y}_{i})]\leq N\rho.\end{array}\right.

We can show that
\sup_{\upsilon\in\mathcal{U}}g(\upsilon)=\sup_{\upsilon\in\mathcal{U}}h(\upsilon)
.
First, to show
\sup_{\upsilon\in\mathcal{U}}g(\upsilon)\leq\sup_{\upsilon\in\mathcal{U}}h(\upsilon)
, we fix an arbitrary value
\upsilon\in\mathcal{U}
. For any
\{\pi_{i}\}_{i\in[N]}
that is feasible for (
21
), define
\mu_{0}^{i}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{Y})
such that

\upsilon_{i}\mu_{0}^{i}(S)=\pi_{i}(\{x_{0}\}\times S)\qquad\forall S\subseteq\mathcal{Y}\text{ measurable}\qquad\forall i\in[N].

One can verify that
\{\mu_{0}^{i}\}_{i\in[N]}
is a feasible solution to (
22
), notably because

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i}\mathds{E}_{\mu_{0}^{i}}[\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{0},\widehat{x}_{i})+\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(Y,\widehat{y}_{i})]\leq\sum_{i\in[N]}\mathds{E}_{\pi_{i}}[\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(X,\widehat{x}_{i})+\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(Y,\widehat{y}_{i})]=\sum_{i\in[N]}\mathds{W}(\pi_{i},\delta_{(\widehat{x}_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})})\leq N\rho,

where the first inequality follows from the non-negativity of
\mathbb{D}(\cdot,\widehat{x})
and
\mathbb{D}(\cdot,\widehat{y})
by Assumption
2.4
(i)
, and the second inequality is from the feasibility of
\{\pi_{i}\}_{i\in[N]}
in (
21
). Moreover, the optimal value of
\{\pi_{i}\}_{i\in[N]}
in (
21
) and the optimal value of
\{\mu_{0}^{i}\}_{i\in[N]}
in (
22
) coincide because

\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\ell(y,\alpha,\beta)\pi_{i}(\{x_{0}\}\times\mathrm{d}y)=\upsilon_{i}\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\ell(y,\alpha,\beta)\mu_{0}^{i}(\mathrm{d}y)=\upsilon_{i}\mathds{E}_{\mu_{0}^{i}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)]\qquad\forall i\in[N].

This implies that
g(\upsilon)\leq h(\upsilon)
for any
\upsilon\in\mathcal{U}
, and thus we have

(23)

\sup\limits_{\upsilon\in\mathcal{U}}~g(\upsilon)\leq\sup\limits_{\upsilon\in\mathcal{U}}~h(\upsilon).

Next, we will establish the reverse direction of the inequality in (
23
). To this end, consider any
\{\mu_{0}^{i}\}_{i\in[N]}
that is feasible for (
22
), we will construct explicitly a sequence of probability families
\{\pi_{i,k}\}_{i\in[N],k\in\mathbb{N}}
that is feasible for (
21
) and attains the same objective value in the limit as
k\to\infty
.

In doing so, we start by supposing that
\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i}\mathds{E}_{\mu_{0}^{i}}[\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{0},\widehat{x}_{i})+\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(Y,\widehat{y}_{i})]<N\rho
. Let us define the measures
\{\pi_{i}\}_{i\in[N]}
as

\pi_{i}=\upsilon_{i}\delta_{x_{0}}\otimes\mu_{0}^{i}+(1-\upsilon_{i})\delta_{(x_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})},

for some
x_{i}\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\{\widehat{x}_{i}\}
that satisfy

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[N]}(1-\upsilon_{i})\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{i},\widehat{x}_{i})

\displaystyle\leq N\rho-\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i}\big(\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{0},\widehat{x}_{i})+\mathds{E}_{\mu_{0}^{i}}[\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(Y,\widehat{y}_{i})]\big).

Notice that under the condition of this case, the right-hand side is strictly positive, and the existence of such
x_{i}
’s is guaranteed thanks to the continuity of
\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}
and the fact that
\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(\widehat{x},\widehat{x})=0
by Assumption
2.4
(i)
.
It is now easy to verify that
\{\pi_{i}\}_{i\in[N]}
is feasible for (
21
), and moreover, the objective value of
\{\pi_{i}\}_{i\in[N]}
in (
21
) amounts to

(\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i})^{-1}\sum_{i\in[N]}\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\ell(y,\alpha,\beta)\pi_{i}(\{x_{0}\}\times\mathrm{d}y)=(\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i})^{-1}\sum_{i\in[N]}\Big[\upsilon_{i}\mathds{E}_{\mu_{0}^{i}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)]\Big].

The case where
\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i}\mathds{E}_{\mu_{0}^{i}}[\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{0},\widehat{x}_{i})+\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(Y,\widehat{y}_{i})]=N\rho
is more complex. In particular, we start by focusing on the situation where there exists some
\widehat{i}
for which
v_{\widehat{i}}\neq 0
and
\mu_{0}^{\widehat{i}}\neq\delta_{\widehat{y}_{\widehat{i}}}
. Here, we can construct a sequence of measure

\{\pi_{i,k}\}_{i\in[N],k\in\mathbb{N}}
as

\pi_{i,k}=\begin{cases}(\upsilon_{i}-\gamma_{k})\delta_{x_{0}}\otimes\mu_{0}^{i}+\gamma_{k}\delta_{(x_{0},\widehat{y}_{i})}+(1-\upsilon_{i})\delta_{(x_{i,k},\widehat{y}_{i})}&\text{if }i=\widehat{i},\\
\upsilon_{i}\delta_{x_{0}}\otimes\mu_{0}^{i}+(1-\upsilon_{i})\delta_{(x_{i,k},\widehat{y}_{i})}&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

for some
\gamma_{k}\in[0,\upsilon_{\widehat{i}}]
,
\lim_{k\to\infty}\gamma_{k}=0
, and some
x_{i,k}\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\{\widehat{x}_{i}\}

x_{i,k}\xrightarrow{k\to\infty}\widehat{x}_{i}\quad\forall k.

Furthermore, let the sequences
\gamma_{k}
and
x_{i,k}
satisfy for any
k\in\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[N]}(1-\upsilon_{i})\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{i,k},\widehat{x}_{i})

\displaystyle\leq\gamma_{k}\mathds{E}_{\mu_{0}^{\widehat{i}}}[\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(Y,\widehat{y}_{\widehat{i}})].

Notice that under the condition of this case, the right-hand side is strictly positive, and the existence of the sequence
(x_{i,k},\gamma_{k})
is again guaranteed thanks to the continuity of
\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}
and the fact that
\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(\widehat{x},\widehat{x})=0
by Assumption
2.4
(i)
.
It is now easy to verify that for any
k
,
\{\pi_{i,k}\}_{i\in[N]}
is feasible for (
21
):

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[N]}\mathds{W}(\pi_{i,k},\delta_{(\widehat{x}_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i}(\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{0},\widehat{x}_{i}))+\mathds{E}_{\mu_{0}^{i}}[\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(Y,\widehat{y}_{i})])+(1-\upsilon_{i})\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{i,k},\widehat{x}_{i})-\gamma_{k}\mathds{E}_{\mu_{0}^{\widehat{i}}}[\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(Y,\widehat{y}_{\widehat{i}})]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i}(\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{0},\widehat{x}_{i})+\mathds{E}_{\mu_{0}^{i}}[\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(Y,\widehat{y}_{i})]))\leq N\rho\,.

Moreover, the objective value of
\{\pi_{i,k}\}_{i\in[N]}
in (
21
) amounts to

(\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i})^{-1}\left(\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i}\mathds{E}_{\mu_{0}^{i}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)]+\gamma_{k}(\ell(\widehat{y}_{i},\alpha,\beta)-\mathds{E}_{\mu_{0}^{\widehat{i}}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)])\right)\xrightarrow{k\to\infty}(\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i})^{-1}\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i}\mathds{E}_{\mu_{0}^{i}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)],

where the limit holds because
\lim_{k\to\infty}\gamma_{k}=0
.

In the final case, we have
\mu_{0}^{i}=\delta_{\widehat{y}_{i}}
for all
i\in[N]
and
\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i}\kappa_{i}=N\rho
. Since we have assumed that
\rho>\rho_{\min}(x_{0},0,\varepsilon)
, it must be that there exists an
\widehat{i}
for which
v_{\widehat{i}}>0
and
\widehat{x}_{\widehat{i}}\neq x_{0}
. Indeed, otherwise we would have that
N\rho=\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i}\kappa_{i}=0\leq N\rho_{\min}(x_{0},0,\varepsilon)
which is a contradiction. Now let us one final time construct a sequence of measures

\pi_{i,k}=\begin{cases}(\upsilon_{i}-\gamma_{k})\delta_{(x_{0},\widehat{y}_{i})}+(1-\upsilon_{i}+\gamma_{k})\delta_{(\widehat{x}_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})}&\text{if }i=\widehat{i},\\
\upsilon_{i}\delta_{(x_{0},\widehat{y}_{i})}+(1-\upsilon_{i})\delta_{(\widehat{x}_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})}&\text{if }\widehat{x}_{i}\neq x_{0},\\
\upsilon_{i}\delta_{(x_{0},\widehat{y}_{i})}+(1-\upsilon_{i})\delta_{(x_{i,k},\widehat{y}_{i})}&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

for some
\gamma_{k}\in(0,\;v_{\widehat{i}})
and some
x_{i,k}\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\{x_{0}\}
, such that
x_{i,k}\xrightarrow{k\rightarrow\infty}\widehat{x}_{i}
, and that

\gamma_{k}\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{0},\widehat{x}_{\widehat{i}})\geq\sum_{i:\widehat{x}_{i}=x_{0}}(1-v_{i})\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{0},x_{i,k})\,.

Indeed, it is easy to verify that
\{\pi_{i,k}\}_{i\in[N]}
is always feasible for (
21
), and moreover, the objective value of
\{\pi_{i,k}\}_{i\in[N]}
in (
21
) amounts to

\displaystyle(\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i})^{-1}

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i}\ell(\widehat{y}_{i},\alpha,\beta)-\gamma_{k}\ell(\widehat{y}_{\widehat{i}},\alpha,\beta)\right)

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{k\rightarrow\infty}(\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i})^{-1}\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i}\ell(\widehat{y}_{i},\alpha,\beta)=(\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i})^{-1}\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i}\mathds{E}_{\mu_{0}^{i}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)]\,,

where the limit holds because
\lim_{k\to\infty}\gamma_{k}\to 0
.

Combining the three cases, we can establish that

\sup\limits_{\upsilon\in\mathcal{U}}~g(\upsilon)\geq\sup\limits_{\upsilon\in\mathcal{U}}~h(\upsilon),

and by considering the above inequality along with (
23

\sup\limits_{\upsilon\in\mathcal{U}}~g(\upsilon)=\sup\limits_{\upsilon\in\mathcal{U}}~h(\upsilon).

One now can rewrite

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{\upsilon\in\mathcal{U}}~h(\upsilon)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccl}\sup\limits_{\mu_{0}^{i}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{Y})~\forall i}&\sup&\displaystyle(\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i})^{-1}\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i}\mathds{E}_{\mu_{0}^{i}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)]\\
&\st&\upsilon\in[0,1]^{N}\\
&&\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i}\mathds{E}_{\mu_{0}^{i}}[\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{0},\widehat{x}_{i})+\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(Y,\widehat{y}_{i})]\leq N\rho\\
&&\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i}\geq N\varepsilon.\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccl}\sup\limits_{\mu_{0}^{i}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{Y})~\forall i}&\sup&\displaystyle(\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i})^{-1}\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i}\mathds{E}_{\mu_{0}^{i}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)]\\
&\st&\upsilon\in[0,1]^{N}\\
&&\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i}\mathds{E}_{\mu_{0}^{i}}[\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{0},\widehat{x}_{i})+\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(Y,\widehat{y}_{i})]\leq N\rho\\
&&\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i}=N\varepsilon.\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccl}\sup\limits_{\upsilon\in[0,1]^{N},\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i}=N\varepsilon}&\sup&\displaystyle(\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i})^{-1}\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i}\mathds{E}_{\mu_{0}^{i}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)]\\
&\st&\mu_{0}^{i}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{Y})\quad\forall i\in[N]\\
&&\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i}\mathds{E}_{\mu_{0}^{i}}[\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{0},\widehat{x}_{i})+\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(Y,\widehat{y}_{i})]\leq N\rho,\end{array}\right.

where equality (

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

Because
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}
is an empirical measure, any joint probability measure
\pi\in\Pi(\mathbb{Q},\widehat{\mathbb{P}})
can be written as
\pi=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}\pi_{i}\otimes\delta_{(\widehat{x}_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})}
, where each
\pi_{i}
is a probability measure on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
. Thus, by the definition of the optimal transport cost, we find

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}_{\rho}=\left\{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}):\begin{array}[]{l}\exists~\pi_{i}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})~\forall i\in[N]\text{ such that }\mathbb{Q}=N^{-1}\sum_{i\in[N]}\pi_{i}\\
\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}\int_{\Xi}[\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(x,\widehat{x}_{i})+\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(y,\widehat{y}_{i})]\pi_{i}(\mathrm{d}x\times\mathrm{d}y)\leq\rho\end{array}\right\}.

Fix any arbitrary
y_{0}\in\mathcal{Y}
. For any
i\in[N]
, let
(x_{i}^{\prime},y_{i}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
be such that
x_{i}^{\prime}\neq x_{0}
and

\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{i}^{\prime},\widehat{x}_{i})+\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(y_{i}^{\prime},\widehat{y}_{i})\leq\frac{\rho}{2N}.

Consider the following set of probability measures
\{\pi_{i}\}_{i\in[N]}
defined through

\forall i\in[N-1]:\quad\pi_{i}=\begin{cases}\delta_{(x_{i}^{\prime},y_{i}^{\prime})}&\text{if }i\in[N-1],\\
\upsilon\delta_{(x_{0},y_{0})}+(1-\upsilon)\delta_{(x_{N}^{\prime},y_{N}^{\prime})}&\text{if }i=N,\end{cases}

for some
\upsilon\in(0,1]
satisfying

\upsilon[\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{0},\widehat{x}_{N})+\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(y_{0},\widehat{y}_{N})]\leq\rho/(2N)
.
Given this specific construction of
\{\pi_{i}\}_{i\in[N]}
, we can verify that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}[\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(x,\widehat{x}_{i})+\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(y,\widehat{y}_{i})]\pi_{i}(\mathrm{d}x\times\mathrm{d}y)\leq\rho,\quad\text{ and }\quad\sum_{i\in[N]}\pi_{i}(X=x_{0})=\upsilon>0.

As such, the measure
\mathbb{Q}^{\prime}=N^{-1}\sum_{i\in[N]}\pi_{i}
satisfies
\mathbb{Q}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho}
and
\mathbb{Q}^{\prime}(X=x_{0})>0
. We thus have

\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho},\mathbb{Q}(X=x_{0})>0}~\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)|X=x_{0}]\geq\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{Q}^{\prime}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)|X=x_{0}]=\ell(y_{0},\alpha,\beta).

Because the choice of
y_{0}
is arbitrary, we find

\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho},\mathbb{Q}(X=x_{0})>0}~\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)|X=x_{0}]\geq\sup\limits_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}~\ell(y,\alpha,\beta).

Moreover, because the distribution of
Y
given
X=x_{0}
is supported on
\mathcal{Y}
, we have

\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho},\mathbb{Q}(X=x_{0})>0}~\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)|X=x_{0}]\leq\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho}}\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)|X=x_{0}]\leq\sup_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}~\ell(y,\alpha,\beta).

This observation establishes the postulated equality and completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.2
.

By applying Proposition
A.5
, we have

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho}\\
\mathbb{Q}(X=x_{0})\geq\varepsilon\end{subarray}}\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)|X=x_{0}]=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccl}\sup\limits_{\upsilon\in[0,1]^{N},\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i}=N\varepsilon}&\sup&\displaystyle(N\varepsilon)^{-1}\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i}\mathds{E}_{\mu_{0}^{i}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)]\\
&\st&\mu_{0}^{i}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{Y})~\forall i\in[N]\\
&&\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i}\big(\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{0},\widehat{x}_{i})+\mathds{E}_{\mu_{0}^{i}}[\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(Y,\widehat{y}_{i})]\big)\leq N\rho.\end{array}\right.

For any
\upsilon
satisfying
\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i}\kappa_{i}>N\rho
, the inner supremum subproblem is infeasible, thus, without loss of optimality, we can add the constraint
\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i}\kappa_{i}\leq N\rho
into the outer supremum. Denote temporarily by
\mathcal{U}
the set

\mathcal{U}=\left\{\upsilon\in[0,1]^{N}:\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i}=N\varepsilon,~\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i}\kappa_{i}\leq N\rho\right\}.

For any
\upsilon\in\mathcal{U}
, strong duality holds because
\mu_{0}^{i}:=\delta_{\widehat{y}_{i}}
constitutes a Slater point. The duality result for moment problem
[
74
, Proposition 3.4]
implies that the inner supremum problem is equivalent to

\begin{array}[]{cll}\inf&\displaystyle\lambda_{1}\big(N\rho-\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i}\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{0},\widehat{x}_{i})\big)+\sum_{i\in[N]}\theta_{i}\\
\st&\lambda_{1}\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\;\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\\
&\upsilon_{i}\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(y_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})\lambda_{1}+\theta_{i}\geq(N\varepsilon)^{-1}\upsilon_{i}\ell(y_{i},\alpha,\beta)&\forall y_{i}\in\mathcal{Y},\;\forall i\in[N].\end{array}

By rescaling
\theta_{i}\leftarrow\upsilon_{i}\theta_{i}
, we have the equivalent form

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho},\mathbb{Q}(X=x_{0})\geq\varepsilon}~\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)|X=x_{0}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccl}\sup\limits_{\upsilon\in[0,1]^{N},\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i}=N\varepsilon}&\inf&\displaystyle\lambda_{1}\big(N\rho-\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i}\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{0},\widehat{x}_{i})\big)+\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i}\theta_{i}\\
&\st&\lambda_{1}\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\;\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\\
&&\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(y_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})\lambda_{1}+\theta_{i}\geq(N\varepsilon)^{-1}\ell(y_{i},\alpha,\beta)\quad\forall y_{i}\in\mathcal{Y},\;\forall i\in[N]\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\inf&\sup\limits_{\upsilon\in[0,1]^{N},\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i}=N\varepsilon}\;\displaystyle\lambda_{1}\big(N\rho-\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i}\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{0},\widehat{x}_{i})\big)+\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i}\theta_{i}\\
\st&\lambda_{1}\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\;\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\\
&\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(y_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})\lambda_{1}+\theta_{i}\geq(N\varepsilon)^{-1}\ell(y_{i},\alpha,\beta)\quad\forall y_{i}\in\mathcal{Y},\;\forall i\in[N].\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{cll}\inf&\displaystyle N\rho\lambda_{1}+N\varepsilon\lambda_{2}+\sum_{i\in[N]}\vartheta_{i}\\
\st&\lambda_{1}\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\;\lambda_{2}\in\mathbb{R},\;\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\;\vartheta\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}\\
&\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(y_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})\lambda_{1}+\nu_{i}\geq(N\varepsilon)^{-1}\ell(y_{i},\alpha,\beta)&\forall y_{i}\in\mathcal{Y},\;\forall i\in[N]\\
&\lambda_{2}+\vartheta_{i}\geq\theta_{i}-\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{0},\widehat{x}_{i})\lambda_{1}&\forall i\in[N],\end{array}\right.

where the second equality follows from interchanging the min-max operators using Sion’s minimax theorem
[
77
]
. By eliminating
\theta
, we have

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho},\mathbb{Q}(X=x_{0})\geq\varepsilon}~\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)|X=x_{0}]

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cll}\inf&\displaystyle N\rho\lambda_{1}+N\varepsilon\lambda_{2}+\sum_{i\in[N]}\vartheta_{i}\\
\st&\lambda_{1}\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\;\lambda_{2}\in\mathbb{R},\;\vartheta\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}\\
&\vartheta_{i}\geq\sup_{y_{i}\in\mathcal{Y}}\left\{(N\varepsilon)^{-1}\ell(y_{i},\alpha,\beta)-[\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{0},\widehat{x}_{i})+\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(y_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})]\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}\right\}&\forall i\in[N],\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cll}\inf&\displaystyle\rho\lambda_{1}+\varepsilon\lambda_{2}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}\vartheta_{i}\\
\st&\lambda_{1}\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\;\lambda_{2}\in\mathbb{R},\;\vartheta\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}\\
&\vartheta_{i}\geq\sup_{y_{i}\in\mathcal{Y}}\big\{\varepsilon^{-1}\ell(y_{i},\alpha,\beta)-[\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{0},\widehat{x}_{i})+\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(y_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})]\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}\big\}\quad\forall i\in[N]\end{array}\right.

where the second equality follows by rescaling the dual variables. Eliminating
\vartheta
leads to the desired result.
∎

Proof of Proposition
3.3
.

By exploiting the quadratic form of
\ell
, the supremum problem in the last constraint of (
11
) is a quadratic optimization problem that admits a closed-form expression as

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{y_{i}\in\mathcal{Y}}\big\{\varepsilon^{-1}\ell(y_{i},\alpha,\beta)-\lambda_{1}\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(y_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})\big\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{y_{i}\in\mathcal{Y}}\big\{\varepsilon^{-1}(y_{i}^{\top}\alpha-\beta)^{2}-\varepsilon^{-1}\eta\cdot y_{i}^{\top}\alpha-\lambda_{1}\cdot\|y_{i}-\widehat{y}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\big\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{4}\varepsilon^{-1}\eta^{2}-\varepsilon^{-1}\eta\beta+\sup\limits_{y_{i}\in\mathcal{Y}}\big\{\varepsilon^{-1}(y_{i}^{\top}\alpha-\beta-\frac{1}{2}\eta)^{2}-\lambda_{1}\cdot\|y_{i}-\widehat{y}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\big\}.

Let
\Delta_{i}=y_{i}^{\top}\alpha-\widehat{y}_{i}^{\top}\alpha
, then we have

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{y_{i}\in\mathcal{Y}}\big\{\varepsilon^{-1}\ell(y_{i},\alpha,\beta)-\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(y_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})\lambda_{1}\big\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{4}\varepsilon^{-1}\eta^{2}-\varepsilon^{-1}\eta\beta+\sup\limits_{\Delta_{i}\in\mathbb{R}}\big\{\varepsilon^{-1}(\Delta_{i}+\widehat{y}_{i}^{\top}\alpha-\beta-\frac{1}{2}\eta)^{2}-\lambda_{1}\cdot\inf\limits_{y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:y_{i}^{\top}\alpha-\widehat{y}_{i}^{\top}\alpha=\Delta_{i}}\|y_{i}-\widehat{y}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\big\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{4}\varepsilon^{-1}\eta^{2}-\varepsilon^{-1}\eta\beta+\sup\limits_{\Delta_{i}\in\mathbb{R}}\big\{\varepsilon^{-1}(\Delta_{i}+\widehat{y}_{i}^{\top}\alpha-\beta-\frac{1}{2}\eta)^{2}-\lambda_{1}\Delta^{2}_{i}\|\alpha\|_{2}^{-2}\big\},

where the last equality is obtained by applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, i.e.,
\Delta_{i}=(y_{i}-\widehat{y}_{i})^{\top}\alpha\leq\|y_{i}-\widehat{y}_{i}\|_{2}\|\alpha\|_{2}
, which implies that the minimum for
\|y_{i}-\widehat{y}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}
is
\Delta_{i}\|\alpha\|_{2}^{-2}
. By combining cases, we find

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{y_{i}\in\mathcal{Y}}\big\{\varepsilon^{-1}\ell(y_{i},\alpha,\beta)-\lambda_{1}\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(y_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})\big\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\varepsilon^{-1}\cdot\frac{(\widehat{y}_{i}^{\top}\alpha-\beta-\frac{1}{2}\eta)^{2}}{1-\varepsilon^{-1}\|\alpha\|_{2}^{2}/\lambda_{1}}-\frac{1}{4}\varepsilon^{-1}\eta^{2}-\varepsilon^{-1}\eta\beta&\mbox{if }\lambda_{1}>\varepsilon^{-1}\|\alpha\|_{2}^{2},\\
-\frac{1}{4}\varepsilon^{-1}\eta^{2}-\varepsilon^{-1}\eta\beta&\mbox{if }\lambda_{1}=\varepsilon^{-1}\|\alpha\|_{2}^{2}\mbox{ and }\widehat{y}_{i}^{\top}\alpha=\beta+\eta/2,\\
+\infty&\mbox{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Consider the case of
\lambda_{1}>\varepsilon^{-1}\|\alpha\|^{2}
first, in which the last constraint of (
11
) is satisfied if and only if there exist ancillary variables
w\in(0,1)
and
z\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}
, such that

\displaystyle 1-\varepsilon^{-1}\|\alpha\|_{2}^{2}/\lambda_{1}\geq w\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\varepsilon(1-w)\lambda_{1}\geq\|\alpha\|_{2}^{2}

and

\displaystyle\theta_{i}\geq\varepsilon^{-1}\cdot\frac{(\widehat{y}_{i}^{\top}\alpha-\beta-\frac{1}{2}\eta)^{2}}{1-\varepsilon^{-1}\|\alpha\|_{2}^{2}/\lambda_{1}}-\frac{1}{4}\varepsilon^{-1}\eta^{2}-\varepsilon^{-1}\eta\beta\Longleftrightarrow\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}z_{i}=\varepsilon\theta_{i}+\varepsilon\|x_{0}-\widehat{x}_{i}\|^{2}\lambda_{1}+\varepsilon\lambda_{2}+\frac{1}{4}\eta^{2}+\eta\beta\\
z_{i}w\geq\big(\widehat{y}_{i}^{\top}\alpha-\beta-\frac{1}{2}\eta\big)^{2}\end{array}\right.\;\forall i\in[N].

Using the equivalent reformulation between hyperbolic constraint and second-order cone constraint
[
56
]
, we have

\displaystyle\varepsilon(1-w)\lambda_{1}\geq\|\alpha\|_{2}^{2}\Longleftrightarrow\left\|\begin{bmatrix}2\alpha\\
1-w-\varepsilon\lambda_{1}\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{2}\leq 1-w+\varepsilon\lambda_{1}

and

\displaystyle z_{i}w\geq\big(\widehat{y}_{i}^{\top}\alpha-\beta-\frac{1}{2}\eta\big)^{2}\Longleftrightarrow\left\|\begin{bmatrix}2\widehat{y}_{i}^{\top}\alpha-2\beta-\eta\\
z_{i}-w\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{2}\leq z_{i}+w\quad\forall i\in[N].

Now we consider the case of
\lambda_{1}=\varepsilon^{-1}\|\alpha\|_{2}^{2}
, where the last constraint of (
11
) is equivalent to the cone constraints when
w=0
. Finally notice that
w=1
recovers the original constraints when
\alpha=0
.
Combining all of the above cases and using them to replace the last constraint of (
11
) completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Proposition
3.4
.

Notice that
\ell
is a pointwise maximum of two linear functions of

. In this case, the supremum problem in the last constraint of (

) can be written as

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{y_{i}\in\mathcal{Y}}\big\{\varepsilon^{-1}\ell(y_{i},\alpha,\beta)-\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(y_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})\lambda_{1}\big\}

\displaystyle=

Therefore, using additional variables

and

,

Using the equivalent reformulation between hyperbolic constraint and second-order cone constraint
[
56
]
, we have for each

\displaystyle 4\lambda_{1}z_{i}\geq\varepsilon^{-2}\eta^{2}\|\alpha\|_{2}^{2}\Longleftrightarrow\left\|\begin{bmatrix}\varepsilon^{-1}\eta\alpha\\
z_{i}-\lambda_{1}\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{2}\leq z_{i}+\lambda_{1},

and

\displaystyle 4\lambda_{1}\tilde{z}_{i}\geq\varepsilon^{-2}(\eta+\tau^{-1})^{2}\|\alpha\|_{2}^{2}\Longleftrightarrow\left\|\begin{bmatrix}\varepsilon^{-1}(\eta+\tau^{-1})\alpha\\

Proof of Proposition
4.1
.

Without any loss of optimality, we can substitute the constraint
\mathbb{Q}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))=0
by the inequality constraint
\mathbb{Q}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))\leq 0
to obtain

\displaystyle\rho_{\max}(x_{0},\gamma)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\inf\limits_{\pi\in\mathcal{M}((\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})\times(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}))}&\mathds{E}_{\pi}[\mathbb{D}(\xi,\xi^{\prime})]\\
\st&\mathds{E}_{\pi}[\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\times\mathcal{Y}}(\xi)]\leq 0,\quad\mathds{E}_{\pi}[\mathbbm{1}_{(\widehat{x}_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})}(\xi^{\prime})]=\frac{1}{N}\qquad\forall i\in[N],\end{array}\right.

where
\xi
represents the joint random vector
(X,Y)
. By a weak duality result
[
78
, Section 2.2]
, we have

\displaystyle\rho_{\max}(x_{0},\gamma)

\displaystyle\geq\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\sup\limits_{b\in\mathbb{R}^{N},~\zeta\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}&\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}b_{i}\\
\st&\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[N]}b_{i}\mathbbm{1}_{(\widehat{x}_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})-\zeta\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})}(x)\leq\mathbb{D}\big((x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\big)\quad\forall(x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\end{array}\right.

If
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\neq(\widehat{x}_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})
for all
i\in[N]
then the constraint does not involve the variables
b_{i}
and thus does not affect the optimal value. Suppose that
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=(\widehat{x}_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})
for some
i\in[N]
then the constraint becomes

b_{i}-\zeta\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})}(x)\leq\mathbb{D}\big((x,y),(\widehat{x}_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})\big)\quad\forall(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}.

Screening the above constraint for each
i\in[N]
, we obtain the following bound

\displaystyle\rho_{\max}(x_{0},\gamma)

\displaystyle\geq\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\sup\limits_{b\in\mathbb{R}^{N},~\zeta\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}&\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}b_{i}\\
\st&\displaystyle b_{i}-\zeta\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})}(x_{i})\leq\mathbb{D}\big((x_{i},y_{i}),(\widehat{x}_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})\big)\quad\forall(x_{i},y_{i})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y},~\forall i\in[N]\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle=\sup\limits_{\zeta\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}~\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}\inf\limits_{(x_{i},y_{i})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}\left\{\mathbb{D}\big((x_{i},y_{i}),(\widehat{x}_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})\big)+\zeta\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})}(x_{i})\right\}

\displaystyle=\sup\limits_{\zeta\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}~\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}\inf\limits_{x_{i}\in\mathcal{X}}\left\{\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{i},\widehat{x}_{i})+\zeta\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})}(x_{i})\right\},

where the last equality follows from the decomposition of
\mathbb{D}
and the fact that the minimizer in
y_{i}
is
\widehat{y}_{i}
. For any
\zeta\geq 0
, we have for any
i\in[N]

\inf\limits_{x_{i}\in\mathcal{X}}\left\{\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{i},\widehat{x}_{i})+\zeta\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})}(x_{i})\right\}=\begin{cases}\min\{\zeta,d_{i}\}&\text{if }\widehat{x}_{i}\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}),\\
0&\text{if }\widehat{x}_{i}\not\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}),\end{cases}

which implies that

\rho_{\max}(x_{0},\gamma)\geq\sup\limits_{\zeta\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}~\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{1}}\min(\zeta,d_{i})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{1}}d_{i}.

In the last step, we show that the above inequality is tight. For any value
\rho
such that
\rho>\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{1}}d_{i}
, Assumption
2.4
(iii)
and the continuity of
\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}
and
\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}
imply that there exists
(x_{i}^{\prime},y_{i}^{\prime})\in(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})\backslash(\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\times\mathcal{Y})
such that

\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{i}^{\prime},\widehat{x}_{i})+\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(y_{i}^{\prime},\widehat{y}_{i})\leq d_{i}+\frac{1}{N}(\rho-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{1}}d_{i}).

The distribution

\mathbb{Q}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{N}\big(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{1}}\delta_{(x_{i}^{\prime},y_{i}^{\prime})}+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{2}}\delta_{(\widehat{x}_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})}\big)

thus satisfies
\mathds{W}(\mathbb{Q}^{\prime},\widehat{\mathbb{P}})\leq\rho
and
\mathbb{Q}^{\prime}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))\leq 0
. This implies that
\rho_{\max}(x_{0},\gamma)=N^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{1}}d_{i}
and completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Proposition
4.2
.

For simplicity, we assume that
\rho_{\max}(x_{0},\gamma)>0
, which implies that
\mathcal{I}_{1}\neq\emptyset
. Let’s consider some index
j\in\mathcal{I}_{1}
.
Assumption
2.4
(iii)
and the continuity of
\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}
and
\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}
imply that there exists
x_{i}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}\backslash\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})
such that

\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{i}^{\prime},\widehat{x}_{i})\leq d_{i}+\frac{1}{2N}(\rho-\rho_{\max}(x_{0},\gamma)).

Fix an arbitrary value
y_{0}\in\mathcal{Y}
. Consider the distribution

\mathbb{Q}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{N}\big(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{1}\setminus\{j\}}\delta_{(x_{i}^{\prime},\widehat{y}_{i})}+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{2}}\delta_{(\widehat{x}_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})}+\upsilon\delta_{(\widehat{x}_{j},y_{0})}+(1-\upsilon)\delta_{(\widehat{x}_{j},\widehat{y}_{j})}\big)

in which
\upsilon\in\mathbb{R}_{++}
is chosen so that

\upsilon\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(y_{0},\widehat{y}_{j})\leq\frac{1}{2}(\rho-\rho_{\max}(x_{0},\gamma)).

It is easy to verify that
\mathbb{Q}^{\prime}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))=\upsilon/N>0
,
\mathds{W}(\mathbb{Q}^{\prime},\widehat{\mathbb{P}})\leq\rho
and that

\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{Q}^{\prime}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)|X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})]=\ell(y_{0},\alpha,\beta).

As
y_{0}
is chosen arbitrarily, a similar argument as in the proof of Proposition
3.1
leads to the necessary result.

Note that alternatively, the case where
\rho_{\max}(x_{0},\gamma)=0
implies that all samples
\{\widehat{x}_{i}\}_{i\in[N]}
lie in the set
(\mathcal{X}\setminus\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))\cup\partial\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})
. If
\mathcal{I}_{1}\neq\emptyset
, then the same argument as before applies. Otherwise, fixing
j
to be such that
\widehat{x}_{j}\notin\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})
, the same results is obtained with

\mathbb{Q}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{N}\big(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{2}\setminus\{j\}}\delta_{(\widehat{x}_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})}+\upsilon\delta_{(\widehat{x}_{j}^{*},y_{0})}+(1-\upsilon)\delta_{(\widehat{x}_{j},\widehat{y}_{j})}\big)

where
\widehat{x}_{j}^{\star}
is the projection of
\widehat{x}_{j}
onto
\partial\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})
. The proof is complete.
∎

Proof of Proposition
4.3
.

We start by proving by contradiction that, when
\rho<\rho_{\max}(x_{0},\gamma)
, we necessarily have that
\inf_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho}}\mathbb{Q}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))>0
. Let us assume that

\inf_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho}}\mathbb{Q}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))=0
.
This implies that for all
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a
\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho}
such that
\mathbb{Q}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))\leq\varepsilon
. Based on the following representation of
\mathbb{B}_{\rho}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}_{\rho}=\left\{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}):\begin{array}[]{l}\exists~\pi_{i}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})~\forall i\in[N]\text{ such that }\mathbb{Q}=N^{-1}\sum_{i\in[N]}\pi_{i}\\
\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}\mathds{E}_{\pi_{i}}[\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(X,\widehat{x}_{i})+\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(Y,\widehat{y}_{i})]\leq\rho\end{array}\right\},

it must be that there exists an assignment for
\{\pi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
that satisfies

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}\mathds{E}_{\pi_{i}}[\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(X,\widehat{x}_{i})+\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(Y,\widehat{y}_{i})]\leq\rho,\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}\pi_{i}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))\leq\varepsilon.

We can work out the following steps:

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}\mathds{E}_{\pi_{i}}[\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(X,\widehat{x}_{i})+\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(Y,\widehat{y}_{i})]\geq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}\mathds{E}_{\pi_{i}}[\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(X,\widehat{x}_{i})|X\notin\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})]\pi_{i}(X\notin\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}d_{i}(1-\pi_{i}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))\geq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}d_{i}-\left(\max_{i}d_{i}\right)\pi_{i}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))\geq\rho_{\max}-\left(\max_{i}d_{i}\right)\varepsilon\,.

Given that this is the case for all
\varepsilon
, we conclude that
\rho\geq\rho_{\max}
, which contradicts our assumption about
\rho
.

Next, we turn to how to evaluate
\underline{\varepsilon}:=\inf_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho}}\mathbb{Q}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))
. Given any
\bar{\mathbb{Q}}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho}
, associated to some
\{\bar{\pi}_{i}\}_{i\in[N]}
based on the representation in (
A.3
), one can construct a new measure
\bar{\mathbb{Q}}^{\star}
of the form:

\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{\star}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{1}}\big((1-p_{i})\delta_{(\widehat{x}_{i}^{\star},\widehat{y}_{i})}+p_{i}\delta_{(\widehat{x}_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})}\big)+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{2}}\delta_{(\widehat{x}_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})},

where
\widehat{x}_{i}^{\star}\in\mathcal{X}\backslash\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})
is a point arbitrarily close to the projection of
\widehat{x}_{i}
onto
\partial\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})
, based on
\bar{\mathbb{Q}}^{\star}\triangleq\mathbb{Q}_{\bar{p}}^{\star}
with
\bar{p}_{i}\triangleq\bar{\pi}_{i}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))
. It is easy to see how
\bar{\mathbb{Q}}^{\star}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho}
and achieves

\bar{\mathbb{Q}}^{\star}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))\leq\bar{\mathbb{Q}}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))
.
Hence, using similar arguments as in the proof of Proposition
4.2
, we have that

\displaystyle\underline{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\inf&\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{\star}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))\\
\st&p\in[0,1]^{N},~\widehat{x}_{i}^{\star}\in\mathcal{X}\backslash\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})~\forall i\in\mathcal{I}_{1}\\
&\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{\star}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{1}}\big((1-p_{i})\delta_{(\widehat{x}_{i}^{\star},\widehat{y}_{i})}+p_{i}\delta_{(\widehat{x}_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})}\big)+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{2}}\delta_{(\widehat{x}_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})}\\
&\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{1}}d_{i}(1-p_{i})\leq\rho\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle=\min\left\{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{1}}p_{i}:p\in[0,1]^{N},~\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{1}}d_{i}(1-p_{i})\leq\rho\right\}\,.

This completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4.4
.

By exploiting the definition of the optimal transport cost and the fact that any joint probability measure
\pi\in\Pi(\mathbb{Q},\widehat{\mathbb{P}})
can be written as
\pi=N^{-1}\sum_{i\in[N]}\pi_{i}\otimes\delta_{(\widehat{x}_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})}
, where each
\pi_{i}
is a probability measure on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
, we have

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho},\mathbb{Q}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))\geq\varepsilon}~\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)|X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})]=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\sup&\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)|X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})]\\
\st&\pi_{i}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})\quad\forall i\in[N]\\
&\sum_{i\in[N]}\pi_{i}(\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\times\mathcal{Y})\geq N\varepsilon\\
&\mathbb{Q}=N^{-1}\sum_{i\in[N]}\pi_{i},~\sum_{i\in[N]}\mathds{W}(\pi_{i},\delta_{(\widehat{x}_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})})\leq N\rho\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\sup&\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{i\in[N]}\mathds{E}_{\pi_{i}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})}(X)]}{\sum_{i\in[N]}\pi_{i}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))}\\
\st&\pi_{i}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})\quad\forall i\in[N]\\
&\sum_{i\in[N]}\pi_{i}(\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\times\mathcal{Y})\geq N\varepsilon\\
&\sum_{i\in[N]}\mathds{W}(\pi_{i},\delta_{(\widehat{x}_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})})\leq N\rho.\end{array}\right.

For any
i\in\mathcal{I}
, let
\widehat{x}_{i}^{\star}=\arg\min\limits_{x\in\partial\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})}\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(x,\widehat{x}_{i})
be again the projection of
\widehat{x}_{i}
onto
\partial\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})
. Moreover, for any
i\in\mathcal{I}_{1}
, let
(x_{i}^{\prime},y_{i}^{\prime})
be a point on
(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})\backslash(\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\times\mathcal{Y})
. Using a continuity and greedy argument, we can choose
(x_{i}^{\prime},y_{i}^{\prime})
sufficiently close to
(\widehat{x}_{i}^{\star},\widehat{y}_{i})
so that it suffices to consider the conditional distribution
\pi_{i}
of the form

(27)

\pi_{i}(\mathrm{d}x\times\mathrm{d}y)=\begin{cases}p_{i}\delta_{\widehat{x}_{i}}(\mathrm{d}x)\mu_{0}^{i}(\mathrm{d}y)+(1-p_{i})\delta_{(x_{i}^{\prime},y_{i}^{\prime})}(\mathrm{d}x\times\mathrm{d}y)&\text{if }i\in\mathcal{I}_{1},\\
p_{i}\delta_{\widehat{x}_{i}^{\star}}(\mathrm{d}x)\mu_{0}^{i}(\mathrm{d}y)+(1-p_{i})\delta_{(\widehat{x}_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})}(\mathrm{d}x\times\mathrm{d}y)&\text{if }i\in\mathcal{I}_{2},\end{cases}

for a parameter
p_{i}\in[0,1]
and a conditional measure
\mu_{0}^{i}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{Y})
. Intuitively,
p_{i}
represents the portion of the sample point
(\widehat{x}_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})

that is transported to the fiber
\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\times\mathcal{Y}
, and
\mu_{0}^{i}
is the conditional distribution of
Y
given
X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})
that is obtained by transporting the sample point
(\widehat{x}_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})
. Using this representation, we can rewrite
\mathbb{Q}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))=N^{-1}\sum_{i\in[N]}p_{i}
. By exploiting the definition of
d_{i}
, the worst-case conditional expected loss can be re-expressed as

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho},\mathbb{Q}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))\geq\varepsilon}~\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)|X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\sup&\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{i\in[N]}p_{i}\mathds{E}_{\mu_{0}^{i}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)]}{\sum_{i\in[N]}p_{i}}\\
\st&p_{i}\in[0,1],~\mu_{0}^{i}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{Y})\quad\forall i\in[N]\\
&\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{1}}(p_{i}\mathds{E}_{\mu_{0}^{i}}[\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(Y,\widehat{y}_{i})]+(1-p_{i})d_{i})+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{2}}\big(p_{i}\mathds{E}_{\mu_{0}^{i}}[\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(Y,\widehat{y}_{i})]+p_{i}d_{i}\big)\leq N\rho\\
&\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}p_{i}\geq N\varepsilon.\end{array}\right.

For any
p=(p_{i})_{i=1,\ldots,N}\in[0,1]^{N}
such that
\sum_{i\in[N]}p_{i}\geq N\varepsilon
, define the function

f(p)\triangleq\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\sup\limits_{q\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}}&\displaystyle\sup\limits_{\mu_{0}^{i}:\mathds{E}_{\mu_{0}^{i}}[p_{i}\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(Y,\widehat{y}_{i})]\leq q_{i}~\forall i}~\sum_{i\in[N]}\mathds{E}_{\mu_{0}^{i}}[p_{i}\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)]\\
\st&\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{1}}(q_{i}+(1-p_{i})d_{i})+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{2}}(q_{i}+p_{i}d_{i})\leq N\rho.\end{array}\right.

Using the general Charnes-Cooper variable transformation

\upsilon_{i}=\frac{p_{i}}{\sum_{i\in[N]}p_{i}},\quad t=\frac{1}{\sum_{i\in[N]}p_{i}},

the equivalent characterization
[
73
, Lemma 1]
implies that

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho},\mathbb{Q}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))\geq\varepsilon}~\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)|X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})]

\displaystyle=\sup\limits_{p\in[0,1]^{N},\sum_{i\in[N]}p_{i}\geq\varepsilon}\frac{f(p)}{\sum_{i\in[N]}{p_{i}}}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\sup\limits_{\upsilon\in[0,1]^{N},~t\in\mathbb{R}}&tf(\upsilon/t)\\
\st&\upsilon_{i}\leq t\quad\forall i\in[N],~\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i}=1\\
&N^{-1}\leq t\leq(N\varepsilon)^{-1},\end{array}\right.

where the fact that
t\geq N^{-1}
is implied by
v_{i}\leq t
and
\sum_{i\in[N]}v_{i}=1
yet makes explicit the fact that
t>0
.

For any feasible value of
\upsilon
and
t
, its corresponding objective value can be evaluated as

\displaystyle tf(\upsilon/t)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\sup\limits_{q\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}}&\displaystyle\sup\limits_{\mu_{0}^{i}:\mathds{E}_{\mu_{0}^{i}}[\upsilon_{i}\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(Y,\widehat{y}_{i})]\leq q_{i}t~\forall i}~\sum_{i\in[N]}\mathds{E}_{\mu_{0}^{i}}[\upsilon_{i}\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)]\\
\st&\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{1}}(q_{i}+(1-\frac{\upsilon_{i}}{t})d_{i})+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{2}}(q_{i}+\frac{\upsilon_{i}}{t}d_{i})\leq N\rho\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\sup\limits_{q\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}}&\displaystyle\sup\limits_{\mu_{0}^{i}:\mathds{E}_{\mu_{0}^{i}}[\upsilon_{i}\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(Y,\widehat{y}_{i})]\leq q_{i}t~\forall i}~\sum_{i\in[N]}\mathds{E}_{\mu_{0}^{i}}[\upsilon_{i}\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)]\\
\st&\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{1}}(q_{i}t+(t-\upsilon_{i})d_{i})+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{2}}(q_{i}t+\upsilon_{i}d_{i})\leq N\rho t\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\sup\limits_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}}&\displaystyle\sup\limits_{\mu_{0}^{i}:\mathds{E}_{\mu_{0}^{i}}[\upsilon_{i}\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(Y,\widehat{y}_{i})]\leq\theta_{i}~\forall i}~\sum_{i\in[N]}\mathds{E}_{\mu_{0}^{i}}[\upsilon_{i}\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)]\\
\st&\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{1}}(\theta_{i}+(t-\upsilon_{i})d_{i})+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{2}}(\theta_{i}+\upsilon_{i}d_{i})\leq N\rho t,\end{array}\right.

where the last equality follows from the change of variable
\theta_{i}\leftarrow q_{i}t
. The inner supremum problems over
\mu_{0}^{i}
are separable, and can be written using standard conic duality results
[
16
, Theorem 1]
as

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{\mu_{0}^{i}:\mathds{E}_{\mu_{0}^{i}}[\upsilon_{i}\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(Y,\widehat{y}_{i})]\leq\theta_{i}~\forall i}~\sum_{i\in[N]}\mathds{E}_{\mu_{0}^{i}}[\upsilon_{i}\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)]=\inf\limits_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}}\sum_{i\in[N]}\theta_{i}\lambda_{i}+\upsilon_{i}\sup\limits_{y_{i}\in\mathcal{Y}}\{\ell(y_{i},\alpha,\beta)-\lambda_{i}\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(y_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})\}.

Thus we find for any feasible
(\upsilon,t)

\displaystyle tf(\upsilon/t)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\sup\limits_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}}&\displaystyle\inf\limits_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}}\sum_{i\in[N]}\theta_{i}\lambda_{i}+\upsilon_{i}\sup\limits_{y_{i}\in\mathcal{Y}}\{\ell(y_{i},\alpha,\beta)-\lambda_{i}\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(y_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})\}\\
\st&\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{1}}(\theta_{i}+(t-\upsilon_{i})d_{i})+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{2}}(\theta_{i}+\upsilon_{i}d_{i})\leq N\rho t,\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccl}\displaystyle\inf\limits_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}}&\sup\limits_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}}&\sum_{i\in[N]}\theta_{i}\lambda_{i}+\upsilon_{i}\sup\limits_{y_{i}\in\mathcal{Y}}\{\ell(y_{i},\alpha,\beta)-\lambda_{i}\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(y_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})\}\\
&\st&\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{1}}(\theta_{i}+(t-\upsilon_{i})d_{i})+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{2}}(\theta_{i}+\upsilon_{i}d_{i})\leq N\rho t\end{array}\right.

where the second equality holds thanks to Sion’s minimax theorem because the feasible set for
\theta
is compact, and the function

(\theta,\lambda)\mapsto\sum_{i\in[N]}\theta_{i}\lambda_{i}+\upsilon_{i}\sup\limits_{y_{i}\in\mathcal{Y}}\{\ell(y_{i},\alpha,\beta)-\lambda_{i}\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(y_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})\}

is convex in
\lambda
, affine in
\theta
and jointly continuous in both
\theta
and
\lambda
. Let
\mathcal{Q}(\upsilon,t)
be the feasible set for
\theta
, that is,

\mathcal{Q}(\upsilon,t)=\left\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}:\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{1}}(\theta_{i}+(t-\upsilon_{i})d_{i})+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{2}}(\theta_{i}+\upsilon_{i}d_{i})\leq N\rho t\right\}.

By rejoining (
A.3
), (
A.3
) and the definition of
\mathcal{Q}(\upsilon,t)
above, we have

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho},\mathbb{Q}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))\geq\varepsilon}~\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)|X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{(\upsilon,t)\in\Upsilon}\inf\limits_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}}\sup\limits_{\theta\in\mathcal{Q}(\upsilon,t)}~\sum_{i\in[N]}\theta_{i}\lambda_{i}+\upsilon_{i}\sup\limits_{y_{i}\in\mathcal{Y}}\{\ell(y_{i},\alpha)-\lambda_{i}\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(y_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})\},

where the feasible set
\Upsilon
of
(\upsilon,t)
in the above problem is understood to be the feasible set of (
A.3
), that is,

\Upsilon\triangleq\left\{(\upsilon,t)\in[0,1]^{N}\times\mathbb{R}:\upsilon_{i}\leq t\quad\forall i\in[N],~\sum_{i\in[N]}\upsilon_{i}=1,~N^{-1}\leq t\leq(N\varepsilon)^{-1}\right\}.

Since
\Upsilon
is compact, Sion’s minimax theorem
[
77
]
applies and we obtain

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho},\mathbb{Q}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))\geq\varepsilon}~\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)|X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})]

\displaystyle=\inf\limits_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}}\sup\limits_{(\upsilon,t)\in\Upsilon}\sup\limits_{\theta\in\mathcal{Q}(\upsilon,t)}~\sum_{i\in[N]}\theta_{i}\lambda_{i}+\upsilon_{i}\sup\limits_{y_{i}\in\mathcal{Y}}\{\ell(y_{i},\alpha,\beta)-\lambda_{i}\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(y_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})\}

\displaystyle=\inf\limits_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}}\sup\limits_{(\upsilon,t)\in\Upsilon}\sup\limits_{\theta\in\mathcal{Q}(\upsilon,t)}~\sum_{i\in[N]}\theta_{i}\lambda_{i}+\upsilon_{i}\inf_{s_{i}\in\mathcal{S}_{i}(\alpha,\beta,\lambda_{i})}s_{i}

(35)

\displaystyle=\inf\limits_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N},s_{i}\in\mathcal{S}_{i}(\alpha,\beta,\lambda_{i})\forall i}\sup\limits_{(\upsilon,t)\in\Upsilon}\sup\limits_{\theta\in\mathcal{Q}(\upsilon,t)}~\sum_{i\in[N]}\theta_{i}\lambda_{i}+\upsilon_{i}s_{i}

where
\mathcal{S}_{i}(\alpha,\beta,\lambda_{i})\triangleq\{s\in\mathbb{R}:s\geq\ell(y_{i},\alpha,\beta)-\lambda_{i}\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(y_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})\,\forall y_{i}\in\mathcal{Y}\}
, and where we exploited the fact that the supremum over
y_{i}\in\mathcal{Y}
is not affected by the choice of
v
,
t
, and
\theta
, and that each
v_{i}\geq 0
to get rid of the apparent bilinearity in
v_{i}
and
y_{i}
. Given that the inner two layers of supremum represent a linear program, linear programming duality can be applied to obtain

(36)

\begin{array}[]{cll}\inf&\phi+(N\varepsilon)^{-1}\nu^{+}-N^{-1}\nu^{-}\\
\st&\nu^{+}\in\mathbb{R}_{+},~\nu^{-}\in\mathbb{R}_{+},~\phi\in\mathbb{R},~\varphi\in\mathbb{R}_{+},~\psi\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}\\
&\phi-d_{i}\varphi+\psi_{i}-s_{i}\geq 0&\forall i\in\mathcal{I}_{1}\\
&\phi+d_{i}\varphi+\psi_{i}-s_{i}\geq 0&\forall i\in\mathcal{I}_{2}\\
&\nu^{+}-\nu^{-}+(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{1}}d_{i}-N\rho)\varphi-\sum_{i\in[N]}\psi_{i}\geq 0\\
&\varphi-\lambda_{i}\geq 0&\forall i\in[N].\end{array}

One can confirm that strong linear programming duality necessarily applied given that
\rho>\rho_{\min}(x_{0},\gamma,\varepsilon)
implies the existence of a feasible
\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho}
such that
\mathbb{Q}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))\geq\varepsilon
. This probability measure can be used to create a feasible triplet
(\upsilon,t,\theta)
for the supremum problem

\displaystyle t\triangleq 1/(N\mathbb{Q}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))),\quad v_{i}\triangleq\pi_{i}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))/(N\mathbb{Q}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))),\quad\theta_{i}

\displaystyle\triangleq 0,

with
\{\pi_{i}\}_{i\in[N]}
as the set of probability measures that certify that
\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho}
. Rejoining the infimum operation in (
36
) into (
35
) and exploiting the definition of the set
\mathcal{V}
complete the proof.
∎

Proof of Proposition
4.5
.

For each
i\in[N]
, by the definition of the loss function
\ell
in (
5
) and by the choice of the transport cost
\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}
, the constraint

\sup\limits_{y_{i}\in\mathcal{Y}}~\ell(y_{i},\alpha,\beta)-\lambda_{i}\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(y_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})\leq s_{i}

is equivalent to

y_{i}^{\top}(\alpha\alpha^{\top}-\lambda_{i}I)y_{i}+\big(2\lambda_{i}\widehat{y}_{i}-(2\beta+\eta)\alpha\big)^{\top}y_{i}+\beta^{2}-\lambda_{i}\|\widehat{y}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}-s_{i}\leq 0\quad\forall y_{i}\in\mathcal{Y}.

This constraint can be further linearized in
\alpha
and
\beta
using the fact that it is equivalent to:

\exists A_{i}\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{m},t\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\,-A_{i}\succeq\alpha\alpha^{\top}-\lambda_{i}I,~t\geq\beta^{2},~-y_{i}^{\top}A_{i}y_{i}+\big(2\lambda_{i}\widehat{y}_{i}-(2\beta+\eta)\alpha\big)^{\top}y_{i}+t-\lambda_{i}\|\widehat{y}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}-s_{i}\leq 0\quad\forall y_{i}\in\mathcal{Y}.

Because
\mathcal{Y}=\mathbb{R}^{m}
, the set of constraints indexed by
y_{i}
can be further written as the semi-definite constraint

\begin{bmatrix}A_{i}&(\beta+\eta/2)\alpha-\lambda\widehat{y}_{i}\\
(\beta+\eta/2)\alpha^{\top}-\lambda_{i}\widehat{y}_{i}^{\top}&s_{i}+\lambda\|\widehat{y}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}-t\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0

while using Schur complement, we also obtain two additional linear matrix inequalities to capture the two other constraints on
A_{i}
and
t
as

\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{i}I-A_{i}&\alpha\\
\alpha^{\top}&1\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0,~\begin{bmatrix}t&\beta\\
\beta&1\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0\,.

This completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Proposition
4.6
.

Notice that
\ell
is a pointwise maximum of two linear functions of
y_{i}
. In this case, the epigraphical formulation of the constraint

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{y_{i}\in\mathcal{Y}}\big\{\ell(y_{i},\alpha,\beta)-\lambda_{i}\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(y_{i},\widehat{y}_{i})\big\}\leq s_{i}

is equivalent to the following set of two semi-infinite constraint

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\eta y_{i}^{\top}\alpha+\beta-\lambda_{i}\|y_{i}-\widehat{y}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\leq s_{i}&\forall y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\\
-(\eta+\frac{1}{\tau})y_{i}^{\top}\alpha+(1-\frac{1}{\tau})\beta-\lambda_{i}\|y_{i}-\widehat{y}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\leq s_{i}&\forall y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.\end{array}\right.

The proof now follows from the same line of argument as the proof of Proposition
4.5
.
∎

Appendix C
Portfolio Allocation with type-
\infty
Optimal Transport Cost Ambiguity Set

In this appendix, we elaborate the reformulations for the conditional portfolio allocation problems using the type-
\infty
Wasserstein ambiguity set.
We first revisit the definition of the type-
\infty
optimal transport distance.

Definition C.1

(Type-
\infty
optimal transport cost)
.

Let
\mathbb{D}
be a nonnegative and continuous function on
\Xi\times\Xi
. The type-
\infty
optimal transport cost between two distributions
\mathbb{Q}_{1}
and
\mathbb{Q}_{2}
supported on
\Xi
is defined as

\mathds{W}_{\infty}(\mathbb{Q}_{1},\mathbb{Q}_{2})\triangleq\inf\left\{\mathrm{ess}\sup\limits_{\pi}\big\{\mathbb{D}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2}):(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\in\Xi\times\Xi\big\}:\pi\in\Pi(\mathbb{Q}_{1},\mathbb{Q}_{2})\right\}.

The type-
\infty
ambiguity set can be formally defined as

\mathbb{B}^{\infty}_{\rho}=\left\{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}):\mathds{W}_{\infty}(\mathbb{Q},\widehat{\mathbb{P}})\leq\rho\right\}.

We now provide the reformulation for the mean-variance portfolio allocation problem. To this end, recall that the parameters
\kappa
are defined as in (
7
). In addition, define the following set

\mathcal{J}\triangleq\left\{i\in[N]:\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{0},\widehat{x}_{i})\leq\rho+\gamma\right\},

and
\mathcal{J}
is decomposed further into two disjoint subsets

\mathcal{J}_{1}=\left\{i\in\mathcal{J}:\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{0},\widehat{x}_{i})+\rho\leq\gamma\right\}~~\text{and}~~\mathcal{J}_{2}=\mathcal{J}\backslash\mathcal{J}_{1}.

Proposition C.2

(Mean-variance loss function)
.

Suppose that
\ell
is the mean-variance loss function of the form (
5
),
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Suppose in addition that
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathcal{Y}=\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(x,\widehat{x})=\|x-\widehat{x}\|^{2}
,
\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(y,\widehat{y})=\|y-\widehat{y}\|_{2}^{2}
and
\rho>\min_{i\in[N]}~\kappa_{i}
. The distributionally robust portfolio allocation model with side information

\min\limits_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A},~\beta\in\mathbb{R}}\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathbb{B}^{\infty}_{\rho},\mathbb{Q}(X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0}))>0}~\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)|X\in\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(x_{0})]

is equivalent to the second-order cone program

\begin{array}[]{cll}\min&\lambda\\
\st&\alpha\in\mathcal{A},~\beta\in\mathbb{R},~\lambda\in\mathbb{R},~u_{i}\in\mathbb{R}~\forall i\in\mathcal{J}_{1},~u_{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}~\forall i\in\mathcal{J}_{2},~t\in\mathbb{R}^{N},~z\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}\\
&\sum_{i\in\mathcal{J}}u_{i}\leq 0\\
&\!\!\!\!\left.\begin{array}[]{l}\left\|\begin{bmatrix}2z_{i}\\
1-\lambda-u_{i}-\eta\beta-\frac{1}{4}\eta^{2}\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{2}\leq 1+\lambda+u_{i}+\eta\beta+\frac{1}{4}\eta^{2}\\
\widehat{y}_{i}^{\top}\alpha-\beta-0.5\eta\leq t_{i},~-\widehat{y}_{i}^{\top}\alpha+\beta+\frac{1}{2}\eta\leq t_{i}\\
t_{i}+(\rho-\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(\widehat{x}_{i}^{p},\widehat{x}_{i}))^{1/2}\|\alpha\|_{2}\leq z_{i}\end{array}\right\}\quad\forall i\in\mathcal{J}.\end{array}

Proof of Proposition
C.2
.

Using
[
63
, Theorem 2.3]
, we have

\begin{array}[]{cll}\min&\lambda\\
\st&\alpha\in\mathcal{A},~\beta\in\mathbb{R},~\lambda\in\mathbb{R},~u_{i}\in\mathbb{R}~\forall i\in\mathcal{J}_{1},~u_{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}~\forall i\in\mathcal{J}_{2}\\
&\lambda+u_{i}\geq v_{i}^{\star}(\alpha,\beta)\quad\forall i\in\mathcal{J}\\
&\sum_{i\in\mathcal{J}}u_{i}\leq 0,\end{array}

where for each
i\in\mathcal{J}
, the value
v_{i}^{\star}(\alpha,\beta)
is

\displaystyle v_{i}^{\star}(\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle=\sup\left\{\ell(y_{i},\alpha,\beta):y_{i}\in\mathcal{Y},~\|y_{i}-\widehat{y}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\rho-\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(\widehat{x}_{i}^{p},\widehat{x}_{i})\right\}

\displaystyle=\Big(|\widehat{y}_{i}^{\top}\alpha-\beta-\frac{1}{2}\eta|+\|\alpha\|_{2}(\rho-\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(\widehat{x}_{i}^{p},\widehat{x}_{i}))^{1/2}\Big)^{2}-\eta\beta-\frac{1}{4}\eta^{2}.

Formulating each constraint
\lambda+u_{i}\geq v_{i}^{\star}(\alpha,\beta)
using an epigraphical formulation of the form

and formulating them as linear or second-order cone constraints completes the proof.
∎

Appendix E
Implications for Contextual Two-stage Stochastic Linear programs

In this appendix, we explore how our distributionally robust conditional decision-making framework can be exploited in a two-stage stochastic linear optimization problem.
Let us redefine the loss function as
\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)\triangleq r(h(Y,\alpha),\beta)-\eta\cdot h(Y,\alpha)
,
where
h(Y,\alpha)
captures the optimal profit generated by a linear recourse problem with right-hand side uncertainty:

\displaystyle h(y,\alpha)\triangleq\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\max&c^{\top}\upsilon\\
\st&\upsilon\in\mathbb{R}^{m},~A\alpha+B\upsilon\leq Cy,\end{array}\right.

constrained by
K
linear constraints prescribed by the matrices
A
,
B
and
C
of appropriate dimensions.
This formulation can capture many interesting operational management problems, including multi-item newsvendor problems
[
2
]
, facility location problems
[
72
]
, and scheduling problems
[
30
]
.

In the case that
r(\cdot,\beta)
is non-increasing for all
\beta
, such as for the CVaR formulation, one can obtain a conservative approximation for problem (
1
) by employing linear decision rules. Namely,

\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[\ell(Y,\alpha,\beta)]=\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[r(h(Y,\alpha),\beta)-\eta\cdot h(Y,\alpha)]\leq\mathds{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[r(c^{\top}(\upsilon+{\Upsilon}Y),\beta)-\eta\cdot c^{\top}(\upsilon+{\Upsilon}Y)]

as long as

(37)

A\alpha+B(\upsilon+{\Upsilon}y)\leq Cy\quad\forall y\in\mathcal{Y}

since in this case
h(y,\alpha)\geq c^{\top}(\upsilon+{\Upsilon}Y)
.

It is therefore not surprising that Propositions
3.4

and
4.6
have natural extensions to provide conservative approximation models in the form of second-order cone and semi-definite programs. For example, Proposition
3.4
can be extended to the following.

Corollary E.1

(Mean-CVaR TSLP for single fiber set)
.

Suppose that
\ell
is the mean-CVaR loss function of the form
\ell(y,\alpha,\beta)=-\eta h(y,\alpha)+\beta+\frac{1}{\tau}(-h(y,\alpha)-\beta)^{+}
,
\gamma=0
,
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
and
\rho>\rho_{\min}(x_{0},0,\varepsilon)
. Suppose in addition that
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\mathcal{Y}
is polyhedral,
\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{X}}(x,\widehat{x})=\|x-\widehat{x}\|^{2}
and
\mathbb{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}(y,\widehat{y})=\|y-\widehat{y}\|_{2}^{2}
. The distributionally robust two-stage stochastic linear program with side information is conservatively approximated by the second-order cone program

(38)

\begin{array}[]{cll}\inf&\displaystyle\rho\lambda_{1}+\varepsilon\lambda_{2}-(1+\eta)c^{\top}\upsilon+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}\theta_{i}\\
\st&\alpha\in\mathcal{A},\;\beta\in\mathcal{B},\;\lambda_{1}\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\;\lambda_{2}\in\mathbb{R},\;\theta\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N},\;z\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N},\;\tilde{z}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N},\;\upsilon\in\mathbb{R}^{m},\;{\Upsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}\\
&\left.\!\!\!\begin{array}[]{l}z_{i}=\theta_{i}+\lambda_{1}\|x_{0}-\widehat{x}_{i}\|^{2}+\lambda_{2}+\varepsilon^{-1}\eta\widehat{y}_{i}^{\top}{\Upsilon}^{\top}c-\varepsilon^{-1}\beta\\
\tilde{z}_{i}=\theta_{i}+\lambda_{1}\|x_{0}-\widehat{x}_{i}\|^{2}+\lambda_{2}+\varepsilon^{-1}(\eta+\frac{1}{\tau})\widehat{y}_{i}^{\top}{\Upsilon}^{\top}c-\varepsilon^{-1}(1-\frac{1}{\tau})\beta\\
\left\|\begin{bmatrix}\varepsilon^{-1}\eta{\Upsilon}^{\top}c\\
z_{i}-\lambda_{1}\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{2}\leq z_{i}+\lambda_{1},\quad\left\|\begin{bmatrix}\varepsilon^{-1}(\eta+\tau^{-1}){\Upsilon}^{\top}c\\
\tilde{z}_{i}-\lambda_{1}\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{2}\leq\tilde{z}_{i}+\lambda_{1}\end{array}\right\}\forall i\in[N]\\
&e_{k}^{\top}(A\alpha+B\upsilon)+\delta^{*}((\Upsilon^{\top}B^{\top}-C^{\top})e_{k}|\mathcal{Y})\leq 0\qquad\forall k\in[K],\end{array}

where
e_{k}
is the
k
-th column of the identity matrix, and
\delta^{*}(z|\mathcal{Y})\triangleq\sup_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}z^{\top}y
is a linear programming representable support function of
\mathcal{Y}
.

Proof of Corollary
E.1
.
