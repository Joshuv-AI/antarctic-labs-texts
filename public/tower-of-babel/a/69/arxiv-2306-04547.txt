Power-closed ideals of polynomial and Laurent polynomial rings
Geir Agnarsson, Jim Lawrence

Abstract
. We investigate the structure of power-closed ideals of the complex polynomial ring R = ℂ ⁡ [ x 1 , … , x d ] R=\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}] and the Laurent polynomial ring R ± = ℂ ​ [ x 1 , … , x d ] ± = M − 1 ​ ℂ ​ [ x 1 , … , x d ] R^{\pm}=\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]^{\pm}=M^{-1}\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}] , where M M is the multiplicative sub-monoid M = [ x 1 , … , x d ] M=[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}] of R R . Here, an ideal I I is power-closed if f ⁡ ( x 1 , … , x d ) ∈ I f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in I implies f ⁡ ( x 1 i , … , x d i ) ∈ I f(x_{1}^{i},\ldots,x_{d}^{i})\in I for each natural i i . In particular, we investigate related closure and interior operators on the set of ideals of R R and R ± R^{\pm} . Finally, we give a complete description of principal power-closed ideals and of the radicals of general power-closed ideals of R R and R ± R^{\pm} .

1.
Introduction

The original motivation for the questions studied in this paper came
largely from
the theory of convex sets and in particular from results concerning
Minkowski addition of convex sets.
Let
{\cal S}
denote the additive group of functions on a Euclidean
space that is generated by the indicator functions
[P]
of convex
polytopes
P
.
If
P
and
Q
are nonempty convex polytopes then their

Minkowski sum
is the set
P+Q=\{p+q:p\in P,q\in Q\}
,
which is also a convex polytope.
A well-defined product,
\cdot
, on
{\cal S}
is uniquely determined by the
condition that, for nonempty convex polytopes
P
and
Q
,

[P]\cdot[Q]=[P+Q]
.
With this product,
{\cal S}
is a commutative ring.
Its multiplicative identity is the indicator function of the singleton set

\{0\}
.
The ring structure on
{\cal S}
has been considered by several authors,
e. g.,
[
Gro74
,
McM94
,
Law85
,
FS92
,
Mor93
]
.

In
[
Law85
,
FS92
]
,

{\cal S}
and its subrings that are generated by
indicator functions of convex polytopes are called
Minkowski rings
.
(The paper
[
Law85
]
is unpublished; however, preprints were
rather widely
disseminated, and
[
FS92
]
was based upon it.)
These rings possess a rather striking property.
For each
k\in\mbox{$\mathbb{N}$}
and for each convex polytope
P
, the power
[P]^{k}

in the Minkowski ring is simply
[kP]
, the indicator function of
the dilation of
P
by the factor
k
.
The function that takes
[P]
to
[kP]
extends to a homomorphism,

\psi_{k}:{\cal S}\rightarrow{\cal S}
. Taking that as a cue, we consider
rings
S
, commutative rings with identity, that mimic the Minkowski
rings in the following way: The ring
S
possesses a distinguished
subset
G
that generates
S
as a ring, together with operators

\psi_{k}:S\rightarrow S
for
k\in\mbox{$\mathbb{N}$}
such that each operator is
a homomorphism of
S
to itself and such that, for each
g\in G
,

\psi_{k}(g)=g^{k}
. (Notice that
G
can be taken to be closed
under multiplication if so desired.)
We dub these
Adams-Minkowski rings
.

A reader familiar with them may have already recognized that these rings
are related to
\lambda
-rings, a notion that
was originally motivated by rings of vector bundles and operations on them,
i.e.,
K
-theory.

Terminology
applying to
\lambda
-rings varies; we use the second section
of
[
AT69
]
. (For much more on the topic of
\lambda
-rings,
see the book,
[
Yau10
]
.)
Special
\lambda
-rings which are generated by elements of dimension
at most 1 are Adams-Minkowski rings, where the set of elements of degree at
most 1 can be taken as the distinguished generating
set and the operators
\psi_{k}
are the Adams operations (whose presence
prompted the “Adams” in the name).
(Special
\lambda
-rings are sometimes called
\lambda
-rings, in which
case, usually,
\lambda
-rings are called pre-
\lambda
-rings.)
Conversely, Adams-Minkowski rings in which division by natural numbers
is possible are special
\lambda
-rings which are generated by elements
of dimension at most 1 and for which the Adams operations are the
homomorphisms
\psi_{k}
.
As shown in
[
AT69
]
, any special

\lambda
-ring may be obtained as a subring of
such a special
\lambda
-ring,
closed under the operations; indeed the special
\lambda
-rings may be
characterized as such subrings.

Although many results here will apply to Adams-Minkowski rings in
general, or to large subclasses,
the scope in this paper will be considerably reduced
in order to accommodate its most acute results.
First, attention is restricted to Noetherian Adams-Minkowski rings

S
. The polynomial ring
\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
is such a ring, with
G

taken to be
G=\{1,x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}\}
;
and, of course, all other Noetherian Adams-Minkowski rings
can be obtained as homomorphic images of such
polynomial rings. We are interested in the ideals that are the kernels of
certain of these homomorphisms, and it will be useful to consider
their analogues
in the ring
R=\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
of polynomials with complex
coefficients, instead. Also, we will address their analogues in the
rings of Laurent polynomials having complex coefficients.

2.
Definitions and observations

Although some results will be valid in Adams-Minkowski
rings more generally, we will consider
the polynomial ring
R=\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
over the complex
field
\mathbb{C}
in
d
variables.

For a polynomial
f=f(\tilde{x})=f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in R
let

f^{(i)}(\tilde{x})=f(x_{1}^{i},\ldots,x_{d}^{i})
.

Definition 2.1
.

An ideal
I
of
R=\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
is

power-closed
if
f\in I
implies

f^{(i)}\in I
for each
i\in\mbox{$\mathbb{N}$}
.

Notice that
R
itself is power-closed, and the intersection of an
arbitrary family of power-closed ideals is power-closed. Therefore
the function that takes an arbitrary subset
S
of
R
to the
smallest power-closed ideal containing it (i.e., the intersection
of the collection of power-closed ideals containing it) is a
closure operator on
R
: It is enlarging, monotone, and idempotent.
For a given
S
,
this ideal will be denoted by
S^{(*)}
. It follows that the collection
of power-closed
ideals forms a complete lattice in which the meet operation is intersection.
This is of course also the meet operation in the lattice of all ideals of

R
, and it will be seen shortly that the join operation in the lattice
of power-closed ideals is also the join operation (namely, sum) in the
lattice of ideals. It will follow
that the lattice of power-closed ideals is a complete sublattice of
the lattice of ideals.

Many types of ideals are power-closed ideals, as the following examples show.
(i) Each monomial ideal generated by a finite set of monomials
from the monoid
[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
is a power-closed ideal.
(ii) Each ideal of
R
that is generated by a finite set of binomials
each of which is a difference of two monomials is a power-closed ideal.
(iii) In particular each toric ideal,
being a prime ideal generated by binomials, is a power-closed ideal.
(iv) More generally, every ideal generated by polynomials each being
a product of monomials and binomials from

R=\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
is a power-closed ideal.
We will see in the next section that there are power-closed ideals of

R
that are not of the most general type (iv) mentioned here above.

For a subset
S\subseteq R
we can also consider the ideal

S^{(*)}
of
R
constructed “from below”.
For a subset
S\subseteq R
and
i\geq 1
let

S^{(i)}:=(f^{(\ell)}:f\in S,1\leq\ell\leq i)
.
In this way we have a nested family of ideals containing
S
; starting
with the set
S
:

S\subseteq S^{(1)}\subseteq S^{(2)}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq S^{(i)}\subseteq\cdots.

The union is an ideal
S^{(\star)}=\bigcup_{i\geq 0}S^{(i)}

of
R
that contains
S
.
An element
g
of
R
is in
S^{(\star)}
if and only if

g=h_{1}f_{1}^{(\ell_{1})}+\cdots+h_{k}f_{k}^{(\ell_{k})}

for some
h_{1},\ldots,h_{k}\in R
,
f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}\in S
and positive integers

\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{k}
. For each
i\geq 1
we have

g^{(i)}=h_{1}^{(i)}f_{1}^{(i\ell_{1})}+\cdots+h_{k}^{(i)}f_{k}^{(i\ell_{k})}\in S^{(\star)}
;
it follows that
S^{(\star)}
is a power-closed ideal. Clearly, any
power-closed ideal that contains
S
must contain
S^{(\star)}
and
hence
S^{(\star)}=S^{(*)}
from above.

Since
R
is Noetherian, every ideal
I=(S)
is finitely generated,
so
S
contains a finite subset
S^{\prime}\subseteq S
with
I=(S^{\prime})
.
Consequentially, there is an
N\in\mbox{$\mathbb{N}$}
with
I^{(*)}=I^{(N)}
.
In a similar vein as the above, we can show that
I^{(*)}
can be written
in terms of any set of generators for
I
. We have the following.

Claim 2.2
.

For an ideal
I
of
R
we have that
I^{(*)}
is a power-closed ideal
and contains

I
. Further, if
I=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})\in R
then for some
N\in\mbox{$\mathbb{N}$}

I^{(*)}=I^{(N)}=(f_{1},f_{1}^{(2)},\ldots,f_{1}^{(N)},f_{2},f_{2}^{(2)},\ldots,f_{2}^{(N)},\ldots,f_{n},f_{n}^{(2)},\ldots,f_{n}^{(N)}).

As a consequence, for any
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\in R
, the ideal

\sum_{i}(f_{i})^{(*)}
is power-closed, and every power-closed ideal is of this
form. The sum of any collection of power-closed ideals is again
power-closed. Notice that this implies that the join operation in the lattice
of power-closed ideals is sum.

From the above discussion and Claim
2.2

we see that for any ideal
I
we can obtain the
power-closure

I^{(*)}
of
I
directly
from the generators of
I
.
As mentioned earlier in this section
S\mapsto S^{(*)}
is a closure
operator on subsets of
R
and therefore also on the set of ideals
of
R
. That
I\mapsto I^{(*)}
is a closure operator for ideals
I
of
R

follows from the power-closedness of
I^{(*)}
; that is,

I^{(**)}:=(I^{(*)})^{(*)}=I^{(*)}
. We summarize in the following
phrased in terms of subsets
S\subseteq R
, but it also holds when
restricting to ideals of
R
.

Proposition 2.3
.

For each set
S\subseteq R
the set
S^{(*)}
is a power-closed ideal and the
operation
S\mapsto S^{(*)}
is a closure operator:
(i)
S\subseteq S^{(*)}
for each subset
S
;
(ii)
S\subseteq T
implies
S^{(*)}\subseteq T^{(*)}
; and
(iii)
S^{(**)}:=(S^{(*)})^{(*)}=S^{(*)}
for each
S
.
The set of power-closed ideals is a complete sublattice of the (complete)
lattice of ideals of
R
: If
\cal S
is a family of power-closed ideals
of
R
then both
\bigcap_{S\in{\cal S}}S
and

\sum_{S\in{\cal S}}S
are power-closed ideals.

For any subsets
S,T\subseteq R
one can always define the sum

S+T=\{f+g:f\in S,\ \ g\in T\}
since
R
is a ring and closed
under addition. By the above Proposition
2.3

we have
S^{(*)}+T^{(*)}\subseteq(S+T)^{(*)}
for any subsets

S,T\in R
and generally also

\sum_{S\in{\cal S}}S^{(*)}\subseteq\left(\sum_{S\in{\cal S}}S\right)^{(*)}

for any family
{\cal S}
of subsets of
R
.

Conversely, since for any
f,g\in R
we have
(f+g)^{(i)}=f^{(i)}+g^{(i)}

we have
(S+T)^{(*)}\subseteq S^{(*)}+T^{(*)}
for any subsets

S,T\subseteq R
and therefore the following.

Observation 2.4
.

The closure operator
S\mapsto S^{(*)}
commutes with the sum of sets
in
R
; that is

\left(\sum_{S\in{\cal S}}S\right)^{(*)}=\sum_{S\in{\cal S}}S^{(*)}

for any family
{\cal S}
of subsets of
R
.

As with the sum we also have
(fg)^{(i)}=f^{(i)}g^{(i)}
for any
f,g\in R
.
Suppose both
I
and
J
are power-closed ideals and consider the
power-closure
(IJ)^{(*)}
of the product ideal
IJ
. If
F\in(IJ)^{(*)}
,
then there are
f_{i}\in I
and
g_{i}\in J
such that

F=\sum_{i}(f_{i}g_{i})^{(k_{i})}
. (Note that in this sum, some elements

f_{i}
and
g_{i}
might be repeated with different
k_{i}
.)
Since
I
and
J
are power-closed we get

F=\sum_{i}(f_{i}g_{i})^{(k_{i})}=\sum_{i}f_{i}^{(k_{i})}g_{i}^{(k_{i})}\in IJ.

Observation 2.5
.

For power-closed ideals
I
and
J
of
R
we have that

IJ
is also power-closed. In particular, for any
n\in\mbox{$\mathbb{N}$}

the ideal
I^{n}
is also power-closed.

Remark:
For ideals
I
and
J
of
R
we clearly have the
containment
(I\cap J)^{(*)}\subseteq I^{(*)}\cap J^{(*)}
.
However, we will see in Observation
3.5
in
the next Section
3
that
unlike the sum, the intersection does not commute with power-closure.
That is, we do not have the equality
(I\cap J)^{(*)}=I^{(*)}\cap J^{(*)}

in
R
in general.

The collection of ideals of
R
, partially ordered by inclusion,
forms a complete lattice in which the meet operation is intersection and the
join operation is sum.
We conclude this section with a few semi-dual observations about
power-closed ideals in this lattice.

For a given ideal
I
of
R
, let

{\cal{MC}}(I)=\{J\subseteq I:\mbox{$J$ is a power-closed ideal of $R$}\}.

If

(1)

I^{{(\circ)}}:=\sum_{J\in{\cal{MC}}(I)}J

we see that
I^{{(\circ)}}
is the the unique maximum power-closed
ideal contained in

I
. Call
I^{{(\circ)}}
the
power-interior
of the ideal
I
.

The power-interior ideal can also be given constructively “from below”:
If
f_{1}^{(i)},\ldots,f_{n}^{(i)}\in I
for each
i\in\mbox{$\mathbb{N}$}
,
then by analogous argument to prove Claim
2.2
we have that

(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})^{(*)}\subseteq I
. Considering a general
element of the power-interior of
I
as defined in (
1
)
we have the following.

Observation 2.6
.

For an ideal
I
of
R
, the power-interior ideal
I^{{(\circ)}}
of
I

as defined in (
1
) can be given as

I^{{(\circ)}}=\{f\in I:f^{(i)}\in I\mbox{ for each $i$ }\}
.

Note that since
R
is Noetherian

I^{{(\circ)}}=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})
for finitely many elements

f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\in I
satisfying

f_{1}^{(i)},\ldots,f_{n}^{(i)}\in I
for each
i\in\mbox{$\mathbb{N}$}
.

By Observation
2.6
we have for every element
\overline{f}

in the quotient ring
R/I^{{(\circ)}}
that
\overline{f}=0
implies that

\overline{f^{(i)}}=0
for every
i\in\mbox{$\mathbb{N}$}
. The ring
R/I^{{(\circ)}}
is
the universal quotient ring of
R
with this property.

Proposition 2.7
.

For an ideal
I
of
R
, every ring homomorphism
\phi:R\rightarrow R/I

such that
\phi(f)=0
implies
\phi(f^{(i)})=0
for each
i\in\mbox{$\mathbb{N}$}

factors through
R/I^{{(\circ)}}
. That is, there is a unique homomorphism

\overline{\phi}:R/I^{{(\circ)}}\rightarrow R/I
such that

\phi=\overline{\phi}\circ\pi
, where

\pi:R\twoheadrightarrow R/I^{{(\circ)}}

is the natural surjection.

The power-interior operation
I\mapsto I^{(\circ)}
does satisfy many
of the usual interior operation for open sets in topology. It is routine
to verify that when we dually replace “
(*)
” with “
(\circ)
”,
“
\subseteq
” with “
\supseteq
”, and “
+
” with “
\cap
” in
Proposition
2.3
,
then we have valid statements. We iterate this and summarize in the
following.

Proposition 2.8
.

For each ideal
I
the ideal
I^{(\circ)}
is power-closed and the
operation
I\mapsto I^{(\circ)}
is an interior operator:
(i)
I\supseteq I^{(\circ)}
for each ideal
I
;
(ii)
I\supseteq J
implies
I^{(\circ)}\supseteq J^{(\circ)}
for ideals

I
and
J
of
R
.
(iii) We have the idempotent property

I^{(\circ\circ)}:=(I^{(\circ)})^{(\circ)}=I^{(\circ)}
for each ideal
I
.
(iv) The interior operator
I\mapsto I^{(\circ)}
commutes with the intersection
of ideals in
R
; that is

\left(\bigcap_{I\in{\cal I}}I\right)^{(\circ)}=\bigcap_{I\in{\cal I}}I^{(\circ)}

for any family
{\cal I}
of ideals of
R
.

Remark:
For ideals
I
and
J
of
R
we clearly have

I^{(\circ)}+J^{(\circ)}\subseteq(I+J)^{(\circ)}
. However, we do not
have the other containment in general as we will see in the following example.

Example 1
.

Consider
\alpha\in\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}
such that

\langle\alpha\rangle=\{\alpha^{n}:n\in\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}\}
forms an infinite
multiplicative subgroup of
{\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}}^{*}
. If

I=(x-\alpha)\subseteq\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}[x]
and
J=(x+\alpha)\subseteq\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}[x]
,
then
I+J=\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}[x]=R
and hence
(I+J)^{(\circ)}=\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}[x]
as well.
By definition we have

I^{(\circ)}=\{f\in I:f^{(i)}\in I\}=\{f\in\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}[x]:f(\alpha^{i})=0\mbox{ for all }i\in\mbox{$\mathbb{N}$}\},

and so
I^{(\circ)}=\{0\}
since
\langle\alpha\rangle
is infinite.
Similarly we have
J^{(\circ)}=\{0\}
and hence

I^{(\circ)}+J^{(\circ)}=\{0\}\neq\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}[x]
.

Remark:
For any ideal
I
of
R
we have that

I^{{(\circ)}}\subseteq I\subseteq I^{(*)}
, so
I
is always between two
power-closed ideals. The ideal
I
is power-closed if and only
if
I^{{(\circ)}}=I^{(*)}
, in which case they both equal
I
.

As for the compositions of the power-closure and power-interior operations
we have the following directly by definitions.

Observation 2.9
.

For each ideal
I
of
R
we have

I^{(\circ*)}:=(I^{(\circ)})^{(*)}=I^{(\circ)},\ \ I^{(*\circ)}:=(I^{(*)})^{(\circ)}=I^{(*)}.

3.
Some explicit results

As the title of this section indicates, we will delve into some refinements
of what what was observed in the previous section. In particular, we will
strengthen Claim
2.2
among other results.

Recall the elementary symmetric functions of

R=\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]

[
Hun00
, p. 252]
:

\displaystyle\sigma_{0}(d)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1,

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}(d)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i},

\displaystyle\sigma_{2}(d)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq d}x_{i}x_{j},

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}(d)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}\leq d}x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{k}},

\displaystyle\sigma_{d}(d)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x_{1}\cdots x_{d}.

We also put
\sigma_{i}(d)=0
for
i>d
. Whenever

i,d\geq 1

we have the relation

\sigma_{i}(d)=x_{d}\sigma_{i-1}(d-1)+\sigma_{i}(d-1)
.
Using these relations we get

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}(-1)^{i}x_{d}^{d-i}\sigma_{i+1}(d)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}(-1)^{i}x_{d}^{d-i}[x_{d}\sigma_{i}(d-1)+\sigma_{i+1}(d-1)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma_{0}(d-1)x_{d}^{d+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x_{d}^{d+1},

and hence by symmetry we get for any
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,d\}

that

\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}(-1)^{i}x_{\ell}^{d-i}\sigma_{i+1}(d)=x_{\ell}^{d+1}

and therefore the following.

Claim 3.1
.

For any
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
and
n\geq d+1
we have

x_{\ell}^{n}=\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}(-1)^{i}x_{\ell}^{n-i-1}\sigma_{i+1}(d).

From this we obtain the following.

Lemma 3.2
.

If
f=a_{1}x_{1}+\cdots+a_{d}x_{d}\in R
then for all
n\geq d+1

we have
f^{(n)}\in(f^{(n-d)},\ldots,f^{(n-1)})
and
therefore
f^{(n)}\in(f,f^{(2)},\ldots,f^{(d)})\subseteq R
.
In particular,
(f)^{(*)}=(f,f^{(2)},\ldots,f^{(d)})
.

Remark:
Note that Claim
3.1
yields
Newton’s Identities
[
New
]
by summing both the left
and the right hand side when
\ell
goes from
1
to
d
.

Example 2
.

Suppose
\alpha\in\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}
is a constant that is nonzero and
not a root of unity. By the above Lemma
3.2
the ideal

I^{(*)}=(y-\alpha x,y^{2}-\alpha x^{2})\subseteq R={\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}}[x,y]

is power-closed. We now argue that
I^{(*)}
cannot be generated by
products of monomials and binomials. Using the
degree lexicographical order (or
deglex
for short)
on
[x,y]
for which
x<y
, we obtain a
Gröbner basis
B=\{y-\alpha x,x^{2}\}
for
the ideal
I^{(*)}
of
\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}[x,y]
. There is an associated system
of reductions

(2)

x^{2}\mapsto 0,\ \ y\mapsto\alpha x

for the elements of the quotient ring
R/I^{(*)}=\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}[x,y]/I^{(*)}
,
which is, in particular, a two dimensional vector space over
\mathbb{C}

spanned by images of
1
and
x
.

Suppose that
(y-\alpha x,x^{2})=I^{(*)}=(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})

where each
g_{i}
is a product of a monomial and binomials.
Since
g_{i}\in I^{(*)}
for each
i
it must reduce to zero by
the reductions in (
2
) from
B
. Such reduction can
be done by first reducing each
y
in each
g_{i}
to
\alpha x

and then reducing each
x^{2}
to zero.

For
f\in\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}[x,y]
,
f\neq 0
, let
\delta(f)
denote
the minimum total degree
of monomials in the expression of
f
as a linear combination of monomials;
that is, writing
f(x,y)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}c_{i}x^{a_{i}}y^{b_{i}}
, where
c_{1}\neq 0
and

a_{1}+b_{1}\leq\cdots\leq a_{k}+b_{k}
, we have
\delta(f)=a_{1}+b_{1}
.
An element
g\in\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}[x,y]
that is a product of
monomials and binomials has the form

g=\pm x^{e}y^{f}\prod_{i=1}^{k}(x^{a_{i}}y^{b_{i}}-x^{c_{i}}y^{d_{i}})

where we may arrange that
a_{i}+b_{i}\leq c_{i}+d_{i}
for
each
i
, in which case we have
\delta(g)=e+f+\sum_{i=1}^{k}(a_{i}+b_{i})
.
For each

5.1.
Power-polynomials

A polynomial in

Definition 5.1

Claim 5.2

A polynomial

\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}[x]\subseteq\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}[x]^{\pm}

if and only if

divides

Example 6

Let

Every root of a power-polynomial

5.2.
Generation and computation of power-polynomials

Here we discuss some further properties of power-polynomials.
In particular, we take a slightly more systematic approach and discuss
how a given polynomial in one variable in either

or

To add to the discussion we started with Proposition

6.
Applications to general principal ideals

In this section we will use results in the previous section
to characterize all principal power-closed ideals in
the usual polynomial ring
R=\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]

as well as in the corresponding Laurent polynomial ring

R^{\pm}=\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]^{\pm}
. For a
d
-tuple
\tilde{p}\in{\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}}^{d}

let
{\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}\nolimits}(\tilde{p})=\{i\in[d]:p_{i}\neq 0\}
denote

\tilde{p}
. For an arbitrary collection of tuples

\tilde{p}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{p}_{k}
let
\gcd(\tilde{p}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{p}_{k})

denote the greatest common divisor of all the coordinates collectively:

\gcd(\tilde{p}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{p}_{k})=\gcd(\{p_{i\/j}:1\leq i\leq k,\ \ 1\leq j\leq d\})
.

Definition 6.1
.

Call a pair of
d
-tuples
\tilde{p},\tilde{q}\in{\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}}^{d}

primitive
if
(i)
{\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}\nolimits}(\tilde{p})\cap{\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}\nolimits}(\tilde{q})=\emptyset
and
(ii)
\gcd(\tilde{p},\tilde{q})=1
.

power primitive
if
\tilde{p}
and
\tilde{q}

are primitive.

Consider an arbitrary and fixed power-primitive binomial

.
By renaming the variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}
we can assume
that
b
has the form

b=x_{1}^{p_{1}}\cdots x_{\ell}^{p_{h}}-y_{1}^{q_{1}}\cdots y_{k}^{q_{k}}

where
\gcd(\tilde{p},\tilde{q})=1
and where all the coordinates

are positive integers.
Note that

b=\upsilon\left(\frac{y_{1}^{q_{1}}\cdots y_{k}^{q_{k}}}{x_{1}^{p_{1}}\cdots x_{h}^{p_{h}}}-1\right)

for some unit
\upsilon\in R^{\pm}
. Clearly

is irreducible
in
R^{\pm}
if and only if

\frac{y_{1}^{q_{1}}\cdots y_{k}^{q_{k}}}{x_{1}^{p_{1}}\cdots x_{h}^{p_{h}}}-1
is irreducible
in
R^{\pm}
. The valuation

\nu(b):=\sum_{i=1}^{h}p_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}q_{i}\in\mbox{$\mathbb{N}$}

is well defined for all binomials in

of the form
\frac{y_{1}^{q_{1}}\cdots y_{k}^{q_{k}}}{x_{1}^{p_{1}}\cdots x_{h}^{p_{h}}}-1

Lemma 6.2
.

A binomial

is irreducible in both

if and only if

Proof.

It is evident that the condition

is necessary

to be irreducible.
Also, it is clear that every binomial

with
\nu(b)=1
is irreducible.

If
q_{1}\geq p_{1}
then in terms of new variables

and

, where

if and only if

The case when

7.
The radical

We have seen that the power-closure is a closure operator.
We turn now to consider a familiar operator, the
radical,
\sqrt{I}
, defined here in the usual way for ideals
I
.
It can be viewed as a closure operator on the lattice of ideals since, if

I
is an ideal then
\sqrt{I}
is also an ideal, and the operator is
enlarging, monotone, and idempotent.
See particularly
[
AM69
, p. 9]
.

As usual, given a subset (usually, an ideal)

I\subseteq{\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
, the zero locus, or the affine variety,
of
I
is
Z(I)=\{\tilde{w}\in({\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}}^{*})^{d}:f(\tilde{w})=0
for
each
f\in I\}
; and the
radical
, or
nilradical
,
\sqrt{I}
,
of an ideal
I
is

\{f\in{\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]:f^{n}\in I\mbox{ for some $n\in{\mbox{$\mathbb{N}$}}$ }\}
.
If
I=\sqrt{I}
then the ideal
I
is said to be
radical
.
Given a subset

Z\subseteq({\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}}^{*})^{d}
,
I(Z)
denotes the set of polynomials

f\in{\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
such that
f(\tilde{w})=0
for each

\tilde{w}\in Z
.

By Hilbert’s Nullstellensatz
[
AM69
, p. 85]
,

\mathbb{C}
being algebraically closed, we have

\sqrt{I^{(*)}}=I(Z(I^{(*)}))
.

The functions
I(\cdot)
and
Z(\cdot)
form a Galois connection
between the set of ideals in
\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
and the
collection of subsets of
\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}^{d}
,
each partially ordered by inclusion. Using Hilbert’s theorem it is seen
that this Galois connection yields a bijective order-reversing correspondence
between the set of radical ideals and the set of zero loci
of ideals
I
of
\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
. We will establish a
similar correspondence between the set of radical power-closed ideals
of
\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]^{\pm}
and certain subsets of the
d
-dimensional
torus group
T^{d}\subseteq(\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}^{*})^{d}
.

Let
T
denote the
circle group
, consisting of complex numbers
of unit modulus, under multiplication; then
T^{d}\subseteq\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}^{d}

is the
d
-dimensional torus group
.
(Here we depart from the more common terminology in algebraic geometry,
wherein the torus is considered to be complex algebraic manifold

(\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}^{*})^{d}
.)

If
f(\tilde{x})
is a binomial then the set
of solutions
\tilde{x}\in(\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}^{*})^{d}
to the equation
f(\tilde{x})=0
is
a topologically closed subgroup of the group
(\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}^{*})^{d}

(under componentwise multiplication). The intersection of this subgroup
with
T^{d}
, that is, the set of solutions in
T^{d}
to
f(\tilde{x})=0
, is
a topological group that is up to isomorphism the product of a
finite cyclic group with the
(d-1)
-dimensional torus group.
Two binomials yield the same subgroup in this way if and only if
they differ by a multiplicative factor of
\pm 1
times a monomial.
Any proper subgroup of
T^{d}
can be obtained
as the intersection of such subgroups.

For
\tilde{w}=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{d})
, let
\tilde{w}^{(j)}
denote

(w_{1}^{j},\ldots,w_{d}^{j})
. Given an ideal
I
, let
Z_{T}(I)
be the set of

\tilde{w}\in T^{d}
for which
f(\tilde{w}^{(j)})=0
for each
f\in I
and

j\in\mbox{$\mathbb{N}$}
. When
I
is a power-closed ideal,
Z_{T}(I)=Z(I)\cap T^{d}
.

For
S\subseteq\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}^{d}
, let

I_{T}(S)=\{f\in\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]:f(\tilde{w}^{(j)})=0

for each
\tilde{w}\in S
and
j\in\mbox{$\mathbb{N}$}\}
.
Also write
I_{T}(\tilde{w})=I_{T}(\{\tilde{w}\})
.

For
f\in\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
, the
monomial support
of
f
is
the set of monomials in
[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
appearing with nonzero
coefficient in its representation as a linear combination of
distinct monomials.

Lemma 7.1
.

Suppose
\tilde{w}\in\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}^{d}
and let
I

be the ideal
I_{T}(\tilde{w})
. If
f
is a nonzero polynomial in

I=I_{T}(\tilde{w})
having minimal monomial support then
f
is a
constant multiple of a monomial or a binomial.

Proof.

Let
f
be a nonzero polynomial in
I
having minimal nonempty support,
where, in terms of the linear basis of monomials,

f(\tilde{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}\tilde{x}^{\tilde{p}_{i}}
.
Then
\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}(\tilde{w}^{\tilde{p}_{i}})^{j}=f(\tilde{w}^{(j)})=0

(
j=1,2,\ldots
).
Let
V=V({\tilde{w}}^{\tilde{p}_{1}},\ldots,{\tilde{w}}^{\tilde{p}_{k}})

denote the
k\times k
Vandermonde matrix for which

V_{i\/j}=(\tilde{w}^{\tilde{p}_{i}})^{j-1}
, and let

D=D({\tilde{w}}^{\tilde{p}_{1}},\ldots,{\tilde{w}}^{\tilde{p}_{k}})

denote the
k\times k
diagonal matrix having

D_{i\/i}=\tilde{w}^{\tilde{p}_{i}}
. As is immediate from the well-known
expression for the determinant of a Vandermonde matrix,
the determinant of
V
is

\prod_{i<j}(\tilde{w}^{\tilde{p}_{j}}-\tilde{w}^{\tilde{p}_{i}})
;
and of course the determinant of
D
is the product of the

k
complex numbers on the diagonal. The
j
-th entry of the vector

V^{t}D\tilde{a}^{t}
is
f(\tilde{w}^{(j)})=0
; that is,

V^{t}D\tilde{a}^{t}=\tilde{0}
.
From this it follows that the determinant of
V^{t}D

is zero, and that either one of the
k
monomials
\tilde{x}^{\tilde{p}_{i}}

yields
0
when evaluated at
\tilde{w}
, or some two of the monomials
yield the same value at
\tilde{w}
. In either case,
there is a polynomial
g(\tilde{x})
, either the difference of
two monomials or itself a monomial, which yields 0 when evaluated at

\tilde{w}
; but clearly then, for such a polynomial,

g(\tilde{w}^{(j)})=0
for
j\in\mbox{$\mathbb{N}$}
.
Then
g\in I
and its support is contained in that of
f
.
By the minimality assumption,
f=cg
, for some constant
c
.

Lemma 7.2

If

As already noted,

Theorem 7.3
.

If
I
is an ideal of
\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]^{\pm}
that is radical
and power-closed, then

is generated by products of binomials.

Proof.

Since

.
Since

is power-closed,

is closed under the positive power operators.
Then
I
is the intersection of the ideals
I_{T}(\tilde{w})
where

\tilde{w}\in Z(I)

By Lemma

each ideal

To conclude this episode, we have the following statement.

Theorem 7.4
.

There is a bijective, order-reversing correspondence between the set of
subsets
Z\subseteq T^{d}
that are closed both topologically and under
the mappings
{\tilde{w}}\rightarrow{\tilde{w}}^{(j)}
for

and the set of

\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]^{\pm}
.

Proof.

Clearly

, which maps subsets of

to ideals, and

\phi(S)=Z_{T}(I_{T}(S))\supseteq S

\tau(I)=I_{T}((Z_{T}(I))\supset I
, for each
I
. Thus

(I(\cdot),Z(\cdot))
form a Galois connection; and
\phi
and

are both closure operators. The lattices of
\phi

and of
\tau
-closed ideals are dually isomorphic.

We need further only to identify the closed sets.

Given an ideal
I
, the set

is a topologically closed subset of

that is also closed under the mappings

\tilde{w}\mapsto\tilde{w}^{(j)}
for
j\in\mbox{$\mathbb{N}$}
. By a
theorem of
[

,
such sets are finite unions of closed subgroups of

.
If
f
is a binomial, then

is a closed subgroup of
T^{d}
.
As a topological group, it is isomorphic to the product of a finite
cyclic group and a
(d-1)
-dimensional torus group. Any such group
can be obtained as the set

for some binomial

; and any closed
subgroup of

can be obtained as the finite intersection of such subgroups.
If

, where the

is of the form

, for some ideal
I
. If

Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{m}
are closed subgroups of
T^{d}
and

(

then

.
It follows that the sets in the image of

are precisely the finite
unions of closed subgroups of
T^{d}
.

For any set
S\subseteq T^{d}
,

is a radical power-closed ideal.

.

Definition 7.7

Call an ideal
