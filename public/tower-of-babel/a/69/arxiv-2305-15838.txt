Decomposition of first order Lipschitz functions by Clifford algebra-valued harmonic functions
Lianet De la Cruz Toranzo, Ricardo Abreu Blaya, Swanhild Bernstein

Abstract
In this paper we solve the problem on finding a sectionally Clifford algebra-valued harmonic function, zero at infinity and satisfying certain boundary value condition related to higher order Lipschitz functions. Our main tool are the Hardy projections related to a singular integral operator arising in bimonogenic function theory, which turns out to be an involution operator on the first order Lipschitz classes. Our result generalizes the classical Hardy decomposition of Hölder continuous functions on a simple closed curve in the complex plane.



1
Introduction

A classical boundary value problem in Complex analysis is to find a sectionally holomorphic function
\Phi(z)
, zero at infinity and satisfying the given boundary condition
\Phi(t)^{+}-\Phi(t)^{-}=\varphi(t),

\,t\in\Gamma,

where
\varphi
satisfies the Hölder condition on
\Gamma
, i.e.
\varphi\in{\bf C}^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma)=\{f:|f(t)-f(\tau)|\leq c|t-\tau|^{\alpha};\,t,\tau\in\Gamma\},\,0<\alpha\leq 1.
Here
\Gamma
is a closed smooth curve in the complex plane and
\Phi(t)^{\pm}
are the limiting values as approaching the curve from the interior
\Omega_{+}
and the exterior
\Omega_{-}
domain respectively.
This problem is immediately solved by the Cauchy transform,

\mathcal{C}\varphi(z):=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma}\frac{\varphi(\zeta)}{\zeta-z}d\zeta,z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\Gamma,

which has continuous limiting values as approaching
\Gamma
from the interior or exterior domain
[
3
,
5
]
. Those involve both the identity and the singular integral operator

\mathcal{S}\varphi(t)=p.v.\frac{1}{\pi i}\!\int_{\Gamma}\!\frac{\varphi(\zeta)}{\zeta-t}d\zeta=\lim\limits_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\pi i}\!\int_{\Gamma\setminus\Gamma_{\epsilon}}\!\!\frac{\varphi(\zeta)}{\zeta-t}d\zeta,\,\,t\in\Gamma,\,\,\Gamma_{\epsilon}=B_{\epsilon}(t)\cap\Gamma

and they are expressed by the classical Plemelj-Sokhotski formula:

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{+}\varphi(t)=\lim\limits_{z\to t\atop z\in\Omega_{+}}\mathcal{C}\varphi(z)=\frac{1}{2}\varphi(t)+\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma}\frac{\varphi(\zeta)}{\zeta-t}d\zeta=\frac{1}{2}\big[I+\mathcal{S}\big]\varphi,\\
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{-}\varphi(t)=\lim\limits_{z\to t\atop z\in\Omega_{-}}\mathcal{C}\varphi(z)=-\frac{1}{2}\varphi(t)+\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma}\frac{\varphi(\zeta)}{\zeta-t}d\zeta=\frac{1}{2}\big[-I+\mathcal{S}\big]\varphi.\end{cases}

(1)

The limiting values of the Cauchy transform with density function satisfying the Hölder condition, also verify this
[
3
,
5
]
. Therefore, the singular integral operator keeps invariant the Hölder class (Plemelj-Privalov theorem) and it yields the Hardy decomposition of Hölder functions:
{\bf C}^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma)={\bf C}^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma)^{+}\oplus{\bf C}^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma)^{-},
where the uniqueness may be seen as a consequence of the involution property for the singular operator, i.e.
\mathcal{S}^{2}=I
.

In the present work, we aim to obtain a similar decomposition for first order Lipschitz functions in the framework of Clifford analysis instead, which is a multidimensional function theory where the so-called monogenic functions play a similar roll to that by the holomorphic ones in Complex analysis. Our main result is Theorem
2
and it will be stated and proved in Section
4
.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Clifford algebras and Clifford analysis

Denote by
\{e_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
an orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
governed by the multiplication rules

\displaystyle e_{i}^{2}=-1,\quad e_{i}e_{j}=-e_{j}e_{i},\,\,\forall i,j=1,2,\dots,m;\,\,i<j.

(2)

Then the real Clifford algebra
\mathbb{R}_{0,m}
is generated by
\{e_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
over the field of real numbers
\mathbb{R}
, so that an element
a\in\mathbb{R}_{0,m}
may be written as

a=\sum_{A}a_{A}e_{A},

where
a_{A}\in\mathbb{R}
and
A
runs over all the possible ordered sets
A=\{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}\leq m\}
or
A=\emptyset
and
e_{A}:=e_{i_{1}}e_{i_{2}}\cdots e_{i_{k}},\,\,e_{0}=e_{\emptyset}=1.
If we identify
\underline{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
by
\underline{x}=x_{1}e_{1}+\dots+x_{m}e_{m};\,x_{i}\in\mathbb{R},i=\overline{1,m}
then we easily see that
\mathbb{R}^{m}
is embedded in
\mathbb{R}_{0,m}
.

A norm for
a\in\mathbb{R}_{0,m}
may be defined by
\|a\|^{2}=\sum_{A}|a_{A}|^{2},
so that the Clifford algebra can be seen as a
2^{n}
-Euclidean space with Euclidean metric. In particular, for
\underline{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
we have
\|\underline{x}\|=|\underline{x}|
, where
|\cdot|
denotes the usual Euclidean norm.

We will consider functions defined on subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
and taking values
in
\mathbb{R}_{0,m}
, namely
f=\sum_{A}f_{A}e_{A}
. We shall say that an
\mathbb{R}_{0,m}
-valued function belongs to certain classical class of functions, if each of its real components
f_{A}
do so.

From now on,
\Omega
stands for a Jordan domain, i.e. a bounded oriented connected open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
whose boundary is a compact topological surface. For simplicity, we assume
\partial\Omega
to be sufficiently smooth, e.g. Liapunov surface. By
\Gamma
we denote the boundary of
\Omega
, while
\Omega_{+}=\Omega
,
\Omega_{-}=\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus{\Omega\cup\Gamma}
if necessary.

An
\mathbb{R}_{0,m}
-valued function
f
in
{\bf C}(\Omega)
is called left (resp. right) monogenic if
{\cal D}_{\underline{x}}\,f=0
(resp.
f\,{\cal D}_{\underline{x}}=0
) in
\Omega
, where
{\cal D}_{\underline{x}}
is the Dirac operator

{\cal D}_{\underline{x}}={\partial_{x_{1}}}e_{1}+{\partial_{x_{2}}}e_{2}+\cdots+{\partial_{x_{m}}}e_{m}.

It is easy to check that the so-called Clifford-Cauchy kernel
E_{0}(\underline{x})=-\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma_{m}}\frac{\underline{x}}{|\underline{x}|^{m}}\,\,(\underline{x}\neq 0),

where
\sigma_{m}
stands for the surface area of the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, is a two-sided monogenic function.

If
f\in{\bf C}^{1}(\Omega_{+}\cup\Gamma)
is monogenic in
\Omega_{+}
, then the following representation formula holds

f(\underline{x})=\int_{\Gamma}E_{0}(\underline{y}-\underline{x})n(\underline{y})f(\underline{y})d\underline{y},\quad\underline{x}\in\Omega_{+},

where
n(\underline{y})
stands for the outward pointing unit normal at
\underline{y}\in\Gamma.

This formula gives rise to the cliffordian Cauchy type transform and its singular version

\mathcal{C}_{0}=\int_{\Gamma}E_{0}(\underline{y}-\underline{x})n(\underline{y})f(\underline{y})d\underline{y},\,(\underline{x}\in\Omega_{+})\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathcal{S}_{0}=2\int_{\Gamma}E_{0}(\underline{y}-\underline{x})n(\underline{y})f(\underline{y})d\underline{y},\,(\underline{x}\in\Gamma).

When
f
satisfies the Hölder condition then, as in (
1
), both operators are connected by the cliffordian Plemelj-Sokhotski formula
[
4
]
, namely

[\mathcal{C}_{0}f]^{+}=\frac{1}{2}[I+\mathcal{S}_{0}]f\quad\mbox{and }\quad[\mathcal{C}_{0}f]^{-}=\frac{1}{2}[-I+\mathcal{S}_{0}]f.

(3)

Note that
{\cal D}_{\underline{x}}
factorizes the Laplace operator
\triangle_{m}
in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
in the sense that
{\cal D}_{\underline{x}}^{2}=-\triangle_{m}.
The fundamental solution of
{\cal D}_{\underline{x}}
is thus given by
E_{0}(\underline{x})={\cal D}_{\underline{x}}E_{1}(\underline{x}),
where

E_{1}(\underline{x})=\frac{1}{(m-2)\sigma_{m}{|\underline{x}|}^{m-2}}\,\,(\underline{x}\neq 0,\,m>2)

is the fundamental solution of the Laplacian in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
(
m\geq 3
).

In addition, an
\mathbb{R}_{0,m}
-valued function
f
in
{\bf C}^{k}(\Omega)
is called polymonogenic of order
k
or simply
k
-monogenic (left) if it satisfies
{\cal D}_{\underline{x}}^{k}\,f=0
in
\Omega
.

2.2
Higher order Lipschitz functions and Whitney extension theorem

Given an appropriate space of functions defined on a non-empty closed set
{\bf E}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
, how can these be extended to
\mathbb{R}^{m}
? The study of these problems and linear properties of some related extension operators in terms of Banach spaces, led to the higher order Lipschitz functions, defined by E. Stein in
[
7
]
, but going back to the works of H. Whitney
[
8
]
, who introduced a notion of differentiation in a general set (
{\bf C}^{m}
-continuity in terms of
f_{(k)}
). The most appropriate spaces are the ones given in terms of the modulus of continuity, in particular, the Lipschitz spaces
{\mbox{Lip}}(\alpha,{\bf E})
with exponent
0<\alpha\leq 1
composed by those functions
f
defined on
{\bf E}
such that

\displaystyle|f(x)|\leq M\quad\mbox{and}\quad|f(x)-f(y)|\leq M|x-y|^{\alpha}\quad\mbox{for}\quad x,\,y\in{\bf E},

(4)

which are Banach spaces with the smallest
M
in the above definition as norm. In fact, the linear extension operator
{\cal{E}}_{0}
defined by

{\cal{E}}_{0}(f)(x)=\begin{cases}f(x),&x\in{\bf E}\\
\sum\limits_{i}f(p_{i})\varphi_{i}^{*}(x),&x\in{\bf E}^{c}\end{cases}

(5)

maps
{\mbox{Lip}}(\alpha,{\bf E})
continuously into
{\mbox{Lip}}(\alpha,\mathbb{R}^{m})
for
0<\alpha\leq 1
and the norm is independent of the closed set
{\bf E}
. This definition deals with the ideas on decomposition of open sets into cubes and partition of the unity as well. It calls for more explanation, but it is beyond the scope of this paper. For more details we refer the reader to
[
7
, Ch. VI]
.

When
\alpha>1
, the space
{\mbox{Lip}}(\alpha,{\bf E})
is constituted by constants only. However, it can be generalized as follows.

Definition 1

(Higher order Lipschitz functions)
.

Let
{\bf E}
be a closed subset of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
k
a nonnegative integer and
0<\alpha\leq 1
. We shall say that a real valued function
f
, defined in
{\bf E}
, belongs to the higher order Lipschitz class
{\mbox{Lip}}(k+\alpha,{\bf E})
if there exist real valued functions
f^{(j)}
,
0<|j|\leq k
, defined on
{\bf E}
, with
f^{(0)}=f
, and so that if

f^{(j)}(x)=\sum_{|j+l|\leq k}\frac{f^{(j+l)}(y)}{l!}(x-y)^{l}+R_{j}(x,y),\,\,x,y\in{\bf E}

then

|f^{(j)}(x)|\leq M,\,\,\,|R_{j}(x,y)|\leq M|x-y|^{k+\alpha-|j|},\,\,x,y\in{\bf E},|j|\leq k,

where
M
is a positive constant and
j=(j_{1},\dots,j_{m})
and
l=(l_{1},\dots,l_{m})
are
m
-dimensional multi-indices in
{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{m}
.

We recall the multi-indices conventions

\underline{x}^{j}:=x_{1}^{j_{1}}\cdots x_{m}^{j_{m}},\,j!:=j_{1}!\cdots j_{m}!,\,|j|:=j_{1}+\cdots+j_{m},\,\partial^{j}:=\frac{\partial^{|j|}}{\partial^{j_{1}}_{x_{1}}\dots\partial^{j_{m}}_{x_{m}}}.

First, some remarks concerning this definition are in order. We note that the function
f^{(0)}=f
does not necessarily determine the elements
f^{(j)}
for an arbitrary
{\bf E}
. Therefore, an element of
{\mbox{Lip}}(k+\alpha,{\bf E})
has to be interpreted as a collection of functions
\{f^{(j)}:{\bf E}\mapsto\mathbb{R},\,|j|\leq k\}
. However, if
{\bf E}=\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
f\in{\mbox{Lip}}(k+\alpha,\mathbb{R}^{m})
, then according to the definition
f
is continuous and bounded and has continuous and bounded partial derivatives
\partial^{j}f
up to the order
k
and the functions
f^{(j)}:=\partial^{j}f
for
|j|=k
belong to the space
{\mbox{Lip}}(\alpha,\mathbb{R}^{m})
. Moreover, when
k=0
, the Lipschitz class becomes the usual class
{\bf C}_{b}^{0,\alpha}({\bf E})
of bounded Hölder continuous functions in
{\bf E}
, which is consistent with (
4
).

A generalization of (
5
) is given by the linear extension operator

{\cal{E}}_{k}(f^{(j)})(x)=\begin{cases}f^{(0)}(x),&x\in{\bf E}\\
{\sum\limits_{i}}^{\prime}P(x,p_{i})\varphi_{i}^{*}(x),&x\in{\bf E}^{c}\end{cases}

(6)

where
P(x,y)
denotes the Taylor expansion of
f
about
y\in{\bf E},
i.e.
P(x,y)=\displaystyle\sum_{|l|\leq k}\frac{f^{(l)}(y)}{l!}(x-y)^{l}
.

For
0<\alpha\leq 1
, the operator
{\cal E}_{k}
maps
{\mbox{Lip}}(k+\alpha,{\bf E})
continuously into
{\mbox{Lip}}(k+\alpha,\mathbb{R}^{m})
and the norm is independent of the closed set
{\bf E}
.

By using the Clifford norm
\|\cdot\|
the
\mathbb{R}_{0,m}
-valued class of functions
{\mbox{Lip}}(k+\alpha,{\bf E})
may also be defined by means of the corresponding compatibility conditions

R_{j}(\underline{x},\underline{y})=f^{(j)}(\underline{x})-\sum_{|l|\leq k-|j|}\frac{f^{(j+l)}(\underline{y})}{l!}(\underline{x}-\underline{y})^{l},\,\,\underline{x},\underline{y}\in{\bf E}

\|f^{(j)}(\underline{x})\|\leq M,\;\|R_{j}(\underline{x},\underline{y})\|\leq M|\underline{x}-\underline{y}|^{k+\alpha-|j|},\,\,\underline{x},\underline{y}\in{\bf E},|j|\leq k,

where the functions
f^{(j)}
and
R_{j}(\underline{x},\underline{y})
are
\mathbb{R}_{0,m}
-valued as well.

As we mentioned before, the higher order Lipschitz class is connected with the works of H. Whitney, namely his celebrated extension theorem
[
8
, Thm. I]
, which turns out to be an appropriate way to define our multidimensional singular integral operator later.

Theorem 1

(Whitney Extension Theorem)
.

Let
f\in{\mbox{Lip}}(k+\alpha,{\bf E})
. Then, there exists a function
\tilde{f}\in{\mbox{Lip}}(k+\alpha,\mathbb{R}^{m})
satisfying

(i)

\tilde{f}|_{\bf E}=f^{(0)}
,

(ii)

\partial^{j}\tilde{f}|_{\bf E}=f^{(j)}
,
0<|j|\leq k
,

(iii)

\tilde{f}\in{\bf C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus{\bf E})
.

3
Auxiliary results

Our focus in this paper will be on the particular case of bimonogenic functions (i.e.
k
-monogenic with
k=2
), which are nothing more than
\mathbb{R}_{0,m}
-valued harmonic functions.

Bimonogenic functions enjoy a representation formula
[
6
, Theorem 7]
, namely if
f
is bimonogenic in
\Omega
, then

f(\underline{x})=\int_{\Gamma}E_{0}(\underline{y}-\underline{x})n(\underline{y})f(\underline{y})d\underline{y}-\int_{\Gamma}E_{1}(\underline{y}-\underline{x})n(\underline{y}){\cal D}_{\underline{y}}f(\underline{y})d\underline{y},x\in\Omega.

(7)

Now assume
f\in{\bf C}^{2}(\Omega_{-})\cap{\bf C}^{1}(\Omega_{-}\cup\Gamma)
is bimonogenic in
\Omega_{-}
,
f(\infty)
exists and
{\cal D}_{\underline{y}}f(\underline{y})=\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{O}\big(\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\underline{y}|}\big)
as
|\underline{y}|\to\infty
. Consider the ball
B_{R}(\underline{x})
with center in
\underline{x}\in\Omega_{-}
and radius
R
sufficiently big such that
\Omega_{+}\cup\Gamma\subset B_{R}(\underline{x})
, then if (
7
) is applied to the domain
B_{R}(\underline{x})\setminus\Omega_{+}\cup\Gamma
we obtain

f(\underline{x})=\int_{\Gamma^{*}}E_{0}(\underline{y}-\underline{x})n(\underline{y})f(\underline{y})d\underline{y}-\int_{\Gamma^{*}}E_{1}(\underline{y}-\underline{x})n(\underline{y}){\cal D}_{\underline{y}}f(\underline{y})d\underline{y},

where
\Gamma^{*}=-\Gamma\cup C_{R}(\underline{x})
and
C_{R}(\underline{x})=\partial B_{R}(\underline{x})
. Then it follows from the identity

\int_{C_{R}(\underline{x})}E_{0}(\underline{y}-\underline{x})n(\underline{y})f(\underline{y})d\underline{y}=\int_{C_{R}(\underline{x})}E_{0}(\underline{y}-\underline{x})n(\underline{y})[f(\underline{y})-f(\infty)]d\underline{y}+\Big(\int_{C_{R}(\underline{x})}E_{0}(\underline{y}-\underline{x})n(\underline{y})d\underline{y}\Big)f(\infty),

the continuity of
f
and the fact that
\int_{C_{R}(\underline{x})}E_{0}(\underline{y}-\underline{x})n(\underline{y})d\underline{y}=1
that

\int_{C_{R}(\underline{x})}E_{0}(\underline{y}-\underline{x})n(\underline{y})f(\underline{y})d\underline{y}\to f(\infty),\,\,\mbox{as}\,\,R\to\infty.

On the other hand, our assumption on the behavior at infinity implies
|\underline{y}|\|{\cal D}_{\underline{y}}f(\underline{y})\|\leq\epsilon
as
|\underline{y}|\to\infty
and it does so
\|{\cal D}_{\underline{y}}f(\underline{y})\|\to 0
as
|\underline{y}|\to\infty
, hence
|\underline{y}-\underline{x}|\|{\cal D}_{\underline{y}}f(\underline{y})\|\leq|\underline{y}|\|{\cal D}_{\underline{y}}f(\underline{y})\|+|\underline{x}|\|{\cal D}_{\underline{y}}f(\underline{y})\|\to 0
as
|\underline{y}|\to\infty
, that is,
{\cal D}_{\underline{y}}f(\underline{y})=\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{O}\big(\frac{1}{|\underline{y}-\underline{x}|}\big)
as
|\underline{y}|\to\infty
and therefore

\Big\|\int_{C_{R}(\underline{x})}E_{1}(\underline{y}-\underline{x})n(\underline{y}){\cal D}_{\underline{y}}f(\underline{y})d\underline{y}\Big\|\leq\int_{C_{R}(\underline{x})}|E_{1}(\underline{y}-\underline{x})|\|{\cal D}_{\underline{y}}f(\underline{y})\|d\underline{y}\leq\frac{c\epsilon}{R^{m-1}}\int_{C_{R}(\underline{x})}d\underline{y}\to 0\,(\epsilon\to 0,\,R\to\infty).

Under the above-mentioned assumptions, we arrive at a representation formula in the exterior domain

\displaystyle f(\underline{x})=-\int_{\Gamma}E_{0}(\underline{y}-\underline{x})n(\underline{y})f(\underline{y})d\underline{y}+\int_{\Gamma}E_{1}(\underline{y}-\underline{x})n(\underline{y}){\cal D}_{\underline{y}}f(\underline{y})d\underline{y}+f(\infty),\,\,\underline{x}\in\Omega_{-}.

(8)

Combining (
7
) with the Whitney extension theorem (component-wise applied to
\mathbb{R}_{0,m}
-valued functions) we introduce a Cauchy type transform of a first order Lipschitz function by

[\mathcal{C}_{1}f]^{(0)}(\underline{x})=\int_{\Gamma}E_{0}(\underline{y}-\underline{x})n(\underline{y})\tilde{f}(\underline{y})d\underline{y}-\int_{\Gamma}E_{1}(\underline{y}-\underline{x})n(\underline{y}){\cal D}_{\underline{y}}\tilde{f}(\underline{y})d\underline{y},\,\,\underline{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus\Gamma

and we do so its singular version

[\mathcal{S}_{1}f]^{(0)}(\underline{z})=2\int_{\Gamma}E_{0}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y})\tilde{f}(\underline{y})d\underline{y}-\int_{\Gamma}E_{1}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y}){\cal D}_{\underline{y}}\tilde{f}(\underline{y})d\underline{y},\,\,\underline{z}\in\Gamma

(9)

where
\tilde{f}
denotes the
\mathbb{R}_{0,m}
-valued Whitney extension of
f\in{\mbox{Lip}}(1+\alpha,\Gamma)
. Note that
[\mathcal{C}_{1}f]^{(0)}
is bimonogenic in
\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus\Gamma
.

Due to the weakly singularity of the kernel
E_{1}
we have from (
3
)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\big([\mathcal{C}_{1}f]^{(0)}\big)^{+}(\underline{z})-\big([\mathcal{C}_{1}f]^{(0)}\big)^{-}(\underline{z})=\tilde{f}|_{\Gamma}=f^{(0)}(\underline{z}),&\,\underline{z}\in\Gamma\\
\big([\mathcal{C}_{1}f]^{(0)}\big)^{+}(\underline{z})+\big([\mathcal{C}_{1}f]^{(0)}\big)^{-}(\underline{z})=[\mathcal{S}_{1}f]^{(0)}(\underline{z}),&\,\underline{z}\in\Gamma\end{cases}

(10)

where, as usual,
\big([\mathcal{C}_{1}f]^{(0)}\big)^{\pm}(\underline{z})=\lim\limits_{\underline{x}\to\underline{z}\atop\underline{x}\in\Omega_{\pm}}[\mathcal{C}_{1}f]^{(0)}(\underline{x}).

If we set

\displaystyle[\mathcal{S}_{1}f]^{(j)}(\underline{z})=2\int_{\Gamma}E_{0}^{(j)}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y})R(\underline{y},\underline{z})d\underline{y}-2\int_{\Gamma}E_{1}^{(j)}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y}){\cal D}_{\underline{y}}R(\underline{y},\underline{z})d\underline{y}+f^{(j)}(\underline{z}),\,|j|=1,

then it follows that the first order Lipschitz class
{\mbox{Lip}}(1+\alpha,\Gamma)
behaves invariant under the action of this singular integral operator
[
2
]
, that is, the Whitney data
\mathcal{S}_{1}f:=\{[\mathcal{S}_{1}f]^{(j)},\,0\leq|j|\leq 1\}
belongs to
{\mbox{Lip}}(1+\alpha,\Gamma)
, whenever
f\in{\mbox{Lip}}(1+\alpha,\Gamma)
.

We include for later reference the following lemma which was recently proved in
[
1
]
.

Lemma 1
.

Let
f,g\in{\mbox{Lip}}(1+\alpha,\Gamma)
such that
f^{(0)}(\underline{x})=g^{(0)}(\underline{x})
and
\sum\limits_{|j|=1}e_{(j)}f^{(j)}(\underline{x})=\sum\limits_{|j|=1}e_{(j)}g^{(j)}(\underline{x})
for all
\underline{x}\in\Gamma
. Then
f\equiv g
in
\Gamma
, i.e.
f^{(j)}=g^{(j)}\,\,\forall|j|\leq k.

4
Main results

Theorem 2
.

The singular integral operator
\mathcal{S}_{1}
is an involution. Namely,

[\mathcal{S}_{1}^{2}f]^{(j)}=f^{(j)}\,\,\forall\,\,|j|\leq 1.

(11)

Proof.

Let us first show that

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{|j|=1}e_{(j)}[\mathcal{S}_{1}f]^{(j)}(\underline{z})=2\int_{\Gamma}E_{0}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y})\Big(\sum\limits_{|j|=1}e_{(j)}f^{(j)}(\underline{y})\Big)d\underline{y}.

(12)

Indeed,

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{|j|=1}e_{(j)}[\mathcal{S}_{1}f]^{(j)}(\underline{z})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\int_{\Gamma}\sum\limits_{|j|=1}e_{(j)}E_{0}^{(j)}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y})R(\underline{y},\underline{z})d\underline{y}-2\int_{\Gamma}\sum\limits_{|j|=1}e_{(j)}E_{1}^{(j)}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y}){\cal D}_{\underline{y}}R(\underline{y},\underline{z})d\underline{y}

(13)

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{|j|=1}e_{(j)}f^{(j)}(\underline{z})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\int_{\Gamma}{\cal D}_{\underline{z}}E_{0}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y})R(\underline{y},\underline{z})d\underline{y}-2\int_{\Gamma}{\cal D}_{\underline{z}}E_{1}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y}){\cal D}_{\underline{y}}R(\underline{y},\underline{z})d\underline{y}

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{|j|=1}e_{(j)}f^{(j)}(\underline{z})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\int_{\Gamma}E_{0}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y}){\cal D}_{\underline{y}}R(\underline{y},\underline{z})d\underline{y}+\sum\limits_{|j|=1}e_{(j)}f^{(j)}(\underline{z}).

Note that if
\exists i:j_{i}>l_{i}
, then
\partial_{\underline{y}}^{(j)}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})^{l}=0
. Thus, since
f^{(0)}(\underline{y})=f^{(0)}(\underline{z})+\displaystyle\sum\limits_{|l|=1}f^{(l)}(\underline{z})(\underline{y}-\underline{z})^{l}+R(\underline{y},\underline{z})
, we have

\displaystyle{\cal D}_{\underline{y}}R(\underline{y},\underline{z})=\sum\limits_{|j|=1}e_{(j)}\partial_{\underline{y}}^{(j)}\Big[\tilde{f}(\underline{y})-\tilde{f}(\underline{z})-\sum\limits_{|l|=1}\partial_{\underline{z}}^{(l)}\tilde{f}(\underline{z})(\underline{y}-\underline{z})^{l}\Big]=\sum\limits_{|j|=1}e_{(j)}\partial_{\underline{y}}^{(j)}\tilde{f}(\underline{y})-\sum\limits_{|j|=1}e_{(j)}\partial_{\underline{z}}^{(j)}\tilde{f}(\underline{z}).

When this is substituted in (
13
) we get

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{|j|=1}e_{(j)}[\mathcal{S}_{1}f]^{(j)}(\underline{z})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\int_{\Gamma}E_{0}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y})\Bigg\{\sum\limits_{|j|=1}e_{(j)}\partial_{\underline{y}}^{(j)}\tilde{f}(\underline{y})-\sum\limits_{|j|=1}e_{(j)}\partial_{\underline{z}}^{(j)}\tilde{f}(\underline{z})\Bigg\}+\sum\limits_{|j|=1}e_{(j)}f^{(j)}(\underline{z}).

Then (

Let us prove (

\displaystyle[\mathcal{S}_{1}^{2}f]^{(0)}(\underline{z})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sum\limits_{s=0}^{1}\int_{\Gamma}(-1)^{s}E_{s}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y}){\cal D}_{\underline{y}}^{s}\widetilde{\mathcal{S}_{k}f}(\underline{y})d\underline{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\int_{\Gamma}E_{0}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y})\widetilde{\mathcal{S}_{1}f}(\underline{y})d\underline{y}-2\int_{\Gamma}E_{1}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y}){\cal D}_{\underline{y}}\widetilde{\mathcal{S}_{1}f}(\underline{y})d\underline{y}

\displaystyle 2\int_{\Gamma}E_{0}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y})\widetilde{\mathcal{S}_{1}f}(\underline{y})d\underline{y}-2\int_{\Gamma}E_{1}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y})\sum\limits_{|j|=1}e_{(j)}\partial^{(j)}_{\underline{y}}\widetilde{\mathcal{S}_{1}f}(\underline{y})d\underline{y}.

On substituting (

\partial_{\underline{y}}^{(j)}\widetilde{\mathcal{S}_{1}f}|_{\Gamma}=[\mathcal{S}_{1}f]^{(j)}

\displaystyle[\mathcal{S}_{k}^{2}f]^{(0)}(\underline{z})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\int_{\Gamma}E_{0}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y})[\mathcal{S}_{k}f]^{(0)}(\underline{y})d\underline{y}-2\int_{\Gamma}E_{1}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y})\sum\limits_{|j|=1}e_{(j)}[\mathcal{S}_{1}f]^{(j)}(\underline{y})d\underline{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\int_{\Gamma}E_{0}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y})\Big[2\int_{\Gamma}E_{0}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})n(\underline{\zeta})\tilde{f}(\underline{\zeta})d\underline{\zeta}-2\int_{\Gamma}E_{1}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})n(\underline{\zeta}){\cal D}_{\underline{\zeta}}\tilde{f}(\underline{\zeta})d\underline{\zeta}\Big]d\underline{y}

Since
2\int_{\Gamma}E_{0}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y})\Big[2\int_{\Gamma}E_{0}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})n(\underline{\zeta})\tilde{f}(\underline{\zeta})d\underline{\zeta}\Big]d\underline{y}=f^{(0)}(\underline{z})
according to the classical involution property and
\sum_{|j|=1}e_{(j)}f^{(j)}(\underline{y})=\sum_{|j|=1}e_{(j)}\partial_{\underline{y}}^{(j)}\tilde{f}(\underline{y})={\cal D}_{\underline{y}}\tilde{f}(\underline{y}),
it is therefore enough to show that the following expression vanishes

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle 2\int_{\Gamma}E_{0}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y})\Big[2\int_{\Gamma}-E_{1}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})n(\underline{\zeta}){\cal D}_{\underline{\zeta}}\tilde{f}(\underline{\zeta})d\underline{\zeta}\Big]d\underline{y}-2\int_{\Gamma}E_{1}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y})\Big[2\int_{\Gamma}E_{0}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})n(\underline{\zeta}){\cal D}_{\underline{\zeta}}\tilde{f}(\underline{\zeta})d\underline{\zeta}\Big]d\underline{y}

\displaystyle 2\sum\limits_{s=0}^{1}\int_{\Gamma}(-1)^{s}E_{s}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y})\Big[2\int_{\Gamma}-{\cal D}_{\underline{y}}^{s}E_{1}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})n(\underline{\zeta}){\cal D}_{\underline{\zeta}}\tilde{f}(\underline{\zeta})d\underline{\zeta}\Big]d\underline{y}.

Fubini’s theorem leads to

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4\int_{\Gamma}\int_{\Gamma}\sum\limits_{s=0}^{1}(-1)^{s}E_{s}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y})\Big[-{\cal D}_{\underline{y}}^{s}E_{1}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})d\underline{y}\Big]n(\underline{\zeta}){\cal D}_{\underline{\zeta}}d\underline{\zeta}.

Define
F(\underline{y}):=-E_{1}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})
. It suffices to show that

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{s=0}^{1}\int_{\Gamma}(-1)^{s}E_{s}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y}){\cal D}_{\underline{y}}^{s}F(\underline{y})d\underline{y}=0.

Let
\Gamma^{*}=\Gamma\setminus(\Gamma_{\epsilon}(\underline{z})\cup\Gamma_{\epsilon}(\underline{\zeta}))\cup-C^{*}_{\epsilon}(\underline{z})\cup-C^{*}_{\epsilon}(\underline{\zeta})
and
\Omega_{*}
its interior. Clearly,
F(\underline{y})
is bimonogenic on
\Omega_{*}
and continuous on
\Omega_{*}\cup\Gamma^{*}
, so we can apply Cauchy’s formula (for
\underline{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus\overline{\Omega}_{*}
) to get

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{s=0}^{1}\int_{\Gamma^{*}}(-1)^{s}E_{s}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y}){\cal D}_{\underline{y}}^{s}F(\underline{y})d\underline{y}=0.

Then we are reduced to proving that

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\epsilon\to 0}\Bigg\{\sum\limits_{s=0}^{1}\int_{C^{*}_{\epsilon}(\underline{z})\cup C^{*}_{\epsilon}(\underline{\zeta})}(-1)^{s}E_{s}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y}){\cal D}_{\underline{y}}^{s}F(\underline{y})d\underline{y}\Bigg\}=0.

We compute

\displaystyle\int_{C^{*}_{\epsilon}(\underline{z})\cup C^{*}_{\epsilon}(\underline{\zeta})}\bigg[E_{0}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y})E_{1}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})+E_{1}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y})E_{0}(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})\bigg]d\underline{y}=

(15)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{m}\Biggl\{\int_{C^{*}_{\epsilon}(\underline{z})}\frac{-(\underline{y}-\underline{z})}{|\underline{y}-\underline{z}|^{m}}\frac{\underline{y}-\underline{z}}{|\underline{y}-\underline{z}|}\frac{1}{|\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y}|^{m-2}}d\underline{y}+\int_{C^{*}_{\epsilon}(\underline{z})}\frac{1}{|\underline{y}-\underline{z}|^{m-2}}\frac{\underline{y}-\underline{z}}{|\underline{y}-\underline{z}|}\frac{-(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})}{|\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y}|^{m}}d\underline{y}

\displaystyle+\int_{C^{*}_{\epsilon}(\underline{\zeta})}\frac{-(\underline{y}-\underline{z})}{|\underline{y}-\underline{z}|^{m}}\frac{\underline{y}-\underline{\zeta}}{|\underline{y}-\underline{\zeta}|}\frac{1}{|\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y}|^{m-2}}d\underline{y}+\int_{C^{*}_{\epsilon}(\underline{\zeta})}\frac{1}{|\underline{y}-\underline{z}|^{m-2}}\frac{\underline{y}-\underline{\zeta}}{|\underline{y}-\underline{\zeta}|}\frac{-(\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y})}{|\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y}|^{m}}d\underline{y}\Biggr\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{c_{m}}{\epsilon^{m-1}}\Bigg\{\int_{C^{*}_{\epsilon}(\underline{z})}\frac{d\underline{y}}{|\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y}|^{m-2}}-\int_{C^{*}_{\epsilon}(\underline{\zeta})}\frac{d\underline{y}}{|\underline{y}-\underline{z}|^{m-2}}

\displaystyle+\int_{C^{*}_{\epsilon}(\underline{z})}\frac{(\underline{y}-\underline{z})(\underline{y}-\underline{\zeta})}{|\underline{\zeta}-\underline{y}|^{m}}d\underline{y}-\int_{C^{*}_{\epsilon}(\underline{\zeta})}\frac{(\underline{y}-\underline{z})(\underline{y}-\underline{\zeta})}{|\underline{y}-\underline{z}|^{m}}d\underline{y}\Bigg\}.

After the change of variable
\underline{y}=\underline{z}-\underline{t}+\underline{\zeta}
, we get that the first and second expressions in curly brackets are equal. This observation applies to the third and fourth terms as well
and
[\mathcal{S}_{1}^{2}f]^{(0)}=f^{(0)}
as claimed.
Now that we have the above claim, we set
G=\{[\mathcal{S}_{1}^{2}f]^{(j)}(\underline{z})-f^{(j)}(\underline{z}),\,|j|\leq 1\}
. Clearly,
G^{(0)}=0
. By Plemelj-Privalov’s theorem
[

]
,
G\in{\mbox{Lip}}(1+\alpha,\Gamma)
and according to Lemma
1
it suffices to show
\sum\limits_{|j|=1}e_{(j)}G^{(j)}=0.

On applying (
12
) twice, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{|j|=1}e_{(j)}G^{(j)}(\underline{z})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{|j|=1}e_{(j)}\big[\mathcal{S}_{1}(\mathcal{S}_{1}f)\big]^{(j)}(\underline{z})-\sum\limits_{|j|=1}e_{(j)}f^{(j)}(\underline{z})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\int_{\Gamma}E_{0}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y})\Bigg(\sum\limits_{|j|=1}e_{(j)}(\mathcal{S}_{1}f)^{(j)}(\underline{y})\Bigg)d\underline{y}-\sum\limits_{|j|=1}e_{(j)}f^{(j)}(\underline{z})

It is immediate that the operators
\mathcal{P}^{+}=\frac{1}{2}(I+\mathcal{S}_{1})
and
\mathcal{P}^{-}=\frac{1}{2}(I-\mathcal{S}_{1})

are projections on

, that is

\mathcal{P}^{+}\mathcal{P}^{+}=\mathcal{P}^{+},\,\mathcal{P}^{-}\mathcal{P}^{-}=\mathcal{P}^{-},\,\mathcal{P}^{+}\mathcal{P}^{-}=0,\,\mathcal{P}^{-}\mathcal{P}^{+}=0.

Consequently,
{\mbox{Lip}}(1+\alpha,\Gamma)={\mbox{Lip}}^{+}(1+\alpha,\Gamma)\oplus{\mbox{Lip}}^{-}(1+\alpha,\Gamma),

where
{\mbox{Lip}}^{\pm}(1+\alpha,\Gamma):=\mbox{im}\mathcal{P}^{\pm}
.

Theorem 3
.

The Whitney data
f\in\emph{{\mbox{Lip}}}(1+\alpha,\Gamma)
belongs to
\emph{{\mbox{Lip}}}^{+}(1+\alpha,\Gamma)
if and only if there exists a bimonogenic function
F
in
\Omega_{+}
which together with
{\cal D}_{\underline{x}}F
continuously extends to
\Gamma
and such that

F|_{\Gamma}=f^{(0)},\,\,{\cal D}_{\underline{x}}F|_{\Gamma}=\sum\limits_{|j|=1}e_{(j)}f^{(j)}.

(16)

Proof.

The proof proceeds along the same lines as the proof of
[
1
, Theorem 6]
, but we use (
12
) instead. Let us prove necessity. By definition, if
f\in{\mbox{Lip}}^{+}(1+\alpha,\Gamma)
there exists
g\in{\mbox{Lip}}(1+\alpha,\Gamma)
such that
f=\frac{1}{2}(g+\mathcal{S}_{1}g)
, i.e.

f^{(j)}(\underline{x})=\frac{1}{2}[I^{(j)}+\mathcal{S}_{1}^{(j)}]g,|j|\leq 1
, where
I^{(j)}g=g^{(j)}
is the identity operator. Let us introduce the function
F
given by the Cauchy type transform
F(\underline{x})=[\mathcal{C}_{1}g]^{(0)}(\underline{x}),\,\underline{x}\in\Omega_{+}
, which is clearly bimonogenic in
\Omega_{+}
. Thus, for
\underline{z}\in\Gamma
we get from (
10
) that

F(\underline{z})=\lim_{\underline{x}\to\underline{z}\atop\underline{x}\in\Omega_{+}}F(\underline{x})=\big([\mathcal{C}_{1}g]^{(0)}\big)^{+}(\underline{z})=\frac{1}{2}[I^{(0)}+\mathcal{S}_{1}^{(0)}]g=f^{(0)}(\underline{z}).

On the other hand,

{\cal D}_{\underline{x}}F(\underline{x})={\cal D}_{\underline{x}}\Big[\int_{\Gamma}E_{0}(\underline{y}-\underline{x})n(\underline{y})\tilde{g}(\underline{y})d\underline{y}-\int_{\Gamma}E_{1}(\underline{y}-\underline{x})n(\underline{y}){\cal D}_{\underline{y}}\tilde{g}(\underline{y})d\underline{y}\Big]=\mathcal{C}_{0}[{\cal D}\tilde{g}](\underline{x})=\mathcal{C}_{0}\Big[\sum\limits_{|j|=1}e_{(j)}\partial_{\underline{x}}^{(j)}\tilde{g}\Big](\underline{x}).

From (
3
) we have for
\underline{z}\in\Gamma

\displaystyle[{\cal D}_{\underline{x}}F](\underline{z})=\lim_{\underline{x}\to\underline{z}\atop\underline{x}\in\Omega_{+}}{\cal D}_{\underline{x}}F(\underline{x})=\Big[\mathcal{C}_{0}({\cal D}_{\underline{x}}\tilde{g})\Big]^{+}(\underline{z})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}[I+\mathcal{S}_{0}]\Big(\sum\limits_{|j|=1}e_{(j)}g^{(j)}\Big)(\underline{z})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Big[\sum\limits_{|j|=1}e_{(j)}g^{(j)}(\underline{z})+2\int_{\Gamma}E_{0}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y})\Big(\sum\limits_{|j|=1}e_{(j)}g^{(j)}(\underline{y})\Big)d\underline{y}\Big].

Hence it follows from (
12
) that
{\cal D}_{\underline{x}}F|_{\Gamma}=\sum\limits_{|j|=1}e_{(j)}f^{(j)}(\underline{z}).

We now prove sufficiency. Assume there exists such bimonogenic function
F
satisfying (
16
). It turns out that nothing more need be done to prove that
[\mathcal{P}^{+}f]^{(j)}=f^{(j)}
for all
0\leq|j|\leq 1
. Indeed, in that case,
f\in\mbox{im}\mathcal{P}^{+}
as claimed.
Combining our assumptions with (
7
) gives

\displaystyle F(\underline{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}E_{0}(\underline{y}-\underline{x})n(\underline{y})F(\underline{y})d\underline{y}-\int_{\Gamma}E_{1}(\underline{y}-\underline{x})n(\underline{y}){\cal D}_{\underline{y}}F(\underline{y})d\underline{y},\,\,\underline{x}\in\Omega_{+}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}E_{0}(\underline{y}-\underline{x})n(\underline{y})f^{(0)}(\underline{y})d\underline{y}-\int_{\Gamma}E_{1}(\underline{y}-\underline{x})n(\underline{y})\Big(\sum\limits_{|j|=1}e_{(j)}f^{(j)}(\underline{y})\Big)d\underline{y}=[\mathcal{C}_{1}f]^{(0)}(\underline{x}).

It is easy to see from this and (

\displaystyle[\mathcal{P}^{+}f]^{(0)}(\underline{z}):=\frac{1}{2}[I^{(0)}+\mathcal{S}_{1}^{(0)}]f(\underline{z})=\big([\mathcal{C}_{1}f]^{(0)}\big)^{+}(\underline{z})=F(\underline{z})=f^{(0)}(\underline{z}).

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{|j|=1}e_{(j)}[\mathcal{P}^{+}f]^{(j)}(\underline{z})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{|j|=1}e_{(j)}\frac{1}{2}[I^{(j)}+\mathcal{S}_{1}^{(j)}]f(\underline{z})

(17)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Big[\sum\limits_{|j|=1}e_{(j)}f^{(j)}(\underline{z})+\sum\limits_{|j|=1}e_{(j)}[\mathcal{S}_{1}f]^{(j)}(\underline{z})\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Big[\sum\limits_{|j|=1}e_{(j)}f^{(j)}(\underline{z})+2\int_{\Gamma}E_{0}(\underline{y}-\underline{z})n(\underline{y})\Big(\sum\limits_{|j|=1}e_{(j)}f^{(j)}(\underline{y})\Big)d\underline{y}\Big]

the last equality being a consequence of (

\Gamma
and due to (
16

{\cal D}_{\underline{x}}F
then (
3
)
yields

\sum\limits_{|j|=1}e_{(j)}f^{(j)}(\underline{z})=\Big[\mathcal{C}_{0}\big(\sum\limits_{|j|=1}e_{(j)}f^{(j)}\big)\Big]^{+}(\underline{z})=\frac{1}{2}\big[I+\mathcal{S}_{0}\big]\big(\sum\limits_{|j|=1}e_{(j)}f^{(j)}\big)(\underline{z}),

or equivalently

\frac{1}{2}\Big[\sum\limits_{|j|=1}e_{(j)}f^{(j)}(\underline{z})\Big]=\mathcal{S}_{0}\Big(\sum\limits_{|j|=1}e_{(j)}f^{(j)}\Big)(\underline{z}),

which gives

The Whitney data
