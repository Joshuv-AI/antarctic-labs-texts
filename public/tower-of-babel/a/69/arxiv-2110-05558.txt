Abstract
. In this paper we study systems of autonomous algebraic ODEs in several differential indeterminates. We develop a notion of algebraic dimension of such systems by considering them as algebraic systems. Afterwards we apply differential elimination and analyze the behavior of the dimension in the resulting Thomas decomposition. For such systems of algebraic dimension one, we show that all formal Puiseux series solutions can be approximated up to an arbitrary order by convergent solutions. We show that the existence of Puiseux series and algebraic solutions can be decided algorithmically. Moreover, we present a symbolic algorithm to compute all algebraic solutions. The output can either be represented by triangular systems or by their minimal polynomials.



1.
Introduction

In
[
6
]
, we have studied local solutions of first order autonomous algebraic ordinary differential equations (shortly AODEs).
Therein we prove that every fractional power series solution of such equations is convergent, and an algorithm for computing these solutions is provided.
In
[
5
]
, we generalized these results to the case of systems of higher order autonomous AODEs in one unknown function whose associated algebraic set is of dimension one, i.e. the algebraic set is a finite union of curves and, maybe, points.
Here, in the current paper, we show that every component of a fractional power series solution vector of a system of higher order autonomous AODEs in several unknown functions, with the expected dimension, is convergent.
In
[
8
]
it is shown that for general systems of AODEs the existence of non-constant formal power series solutions can not be decided algorithmically.
Nevertheless, for the systems treated in the paper and formal Puiseux series solutions, this undecidability property does not hold.
Moreover, all algebraic solutions can be computed algorithmically.

We follow the idea in
[
5
]
and triangularize the given system and perform differential elimination.
In contrast to
[
5
]
, we use in the current paper the so-called Thomas decomposition (see e.g.
[
13
,
3
]
).
In the literature there are several other methods to triangularize differential systems and to obtain resolvent representations of them, see for instance
[
7
,
4
]
and references therein, but the Thomas decomposition seems to be the most adequate and direct method for our reasonings.

In the Thomas decomposition of the systems under investigation, we obtain subsystems involving the unknown functions and only one derivative of one of the functions.
In the case where the first solution component of such a subsystem is an algebraic Puiseux series, the subsystem can be further simplified in order to obtain subsystems involving only the unknown functions.
As a consequence, all solution components are algebraic and for every component of a possible solution its minimal polynomials can be found.
In the representation in terms of the minimal polynomials, however, not all possible root combinations indeed define a solution of the given differential system.
So we alternatively represent the solutions as the triangular set involving no derivatives.

The main algorithm presented here generalizes both the algorithms in
[
11
]
and
[
5
]
, in the following sense.
In
[
11
]
, rational solutions of systems of autonomous AODEs of dimension one with one differential unknown are computed.
Additionally, algebraic and formal Puiseux series solutions for such systems were considered in
[
5
]
.
We present here an extension to systems of this kind involving several differential unknowns.

The structure of the paper is as follows.
In Section
2
we recall some necessary concepts such as simple systems, the Thomas decomposition and algebraic solutions of first order autonomous AODEs.
In Section
3
we introduce, and briefly discuss, a notion of dimension when dealing with differential systems.
Following our algebraic definition, which can be computed by just using well-known methods from computational Algebraic Geometry, the dimension does not increase when applying algebraic and differential elimination methods for obtaining a Thomas decomposition (see Theorem
3.4
).
Consequently, differential systems of algebraic dimension one decompose into simple subsystems of a very specific type, namely into triangular systems involving no derivatives of the unknown functions except for possibly the first derivative of one variable.
In Section
4
we study formal Puiseux series solutions of such systems with constant coefficients.
The main result is Theorem
4.3
, where it is shown that the Artin approximation theorem for algebraic systems (see
[
2
]
) holds for autonomous differential systems of algebraic dimension one.
Additionally we show in Theorem
4.4
that the existence of constant and non-constant formal Puiseux series solutions can be decided.
In Section
4.1
we focus on algebraic solutions.
In Theorem
4.9
is shown that all algebraic solutions of systems of autonomous AODEs of algebraic dimension one can be given by a finite number of simple systems involving no derivatives.
Alternatively to the representation by simple systems, the algebraic solutions can be represented by their minimal polynomials and Proposition
4.10
gives a degree bound on them.
In Section
5
, by using the algorithms in
[
5
,
3
]
, we derive an algorithm for determining all algebraic solutions of the original system.
This is also illustrated by examples.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section we first recall the notion of simple systems and Thomas decomposition
in a form that is adapted to systems of ordinary differential equations.
For further details we refer to
[
13
]
.
Afterwards we recall some results on algebraic solutions of first order autonomous AODEs.
We follow the work
[
1
]
, where the authors give a necessary and sufficient condition on algebraic general solutions of such equations.
They indicate how to use these results in order to compute all algebraic solutions.

[

2.1.
Algebraic and Differential Thomas Decomposition

Let
K
be a field of characteristic zero
and
K[z_{0},\ldots,z_{m}]
the polynomial ring in
m+1
indeterminates with ordering
z_{0}<\cdots<z_{m}
.
For
F\in K[z_{0},\ldots,z_{m}]\setminus K
we denote by
\lv(F)
the
leading variable

of
F
with respect to
<
.
An algebraic system is given by

(2.1)

\mathcal{S}=\{F_{1}=0,\ldots,F_{M}=0,U_{1}\neq 0,\ldots,U_{N}\neq 0\},

where
F_{i}
,
U_{j}\in K[z_{0},\ldots,z_{m}]
are polynomials.
Moreover, let
L\supseteq K
be a field extension and let us denote by
\overline{L}
the algebraic closure of
L
.
For given
S\subset K[z_{0},\ldots,z_{m}]
, we introduce the set

\mathcal{V}_{\overline{L}}(\mathcal{S}):=\mathbb{V}_{\overline{L}}(\{F_{1},\ldots,F_{M}\})\setminus\bigcup_{1\leq i\leq N}\mathbb{V}_{\overline{L}}(\{U_{i}\})\subseteq{\overline{L}}^{m+1},

where
\mathbb{V}_{\overline{L}}(\Delta)
is the zero set defined by the polynomials in
\Delta
.
Each non-constant polynomial is considered recursively as a univariate polynomial
in its leading variable with coefficients
that are univariate polynomials in lower ranked indeterminates, etc. Correspondingly, a sequence
of projections is defined by

\begin{array}[]{ccccccccc}\overline{L}^{m+1}&\!\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\pi_{m}}}{{\longrightarrow}}&\!\overline{L}^{m}&\!\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\pi_{m-1}}}{{\longrightarrow}}&\!\overline{L}^{m-1}&\!\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\pi_{m-2}}}{{\longrightarrow}}&\!\ldots&\!\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\pi_{1}}}{{\longrightarrow}}&\!\overline{L}\\[1.99997pt]
(z_{0},\ldots,z_{m})&\!\longmapsto&\!(z_{0},\ldots,z_{m-1})&\!\longmapsto&\!(z_{0},\ldots,z_{m-2})&\!\longmapsto&\!\ldots&\!\longmapsto&\!z_{0}\end{array}

The
initial

\init(F)
of a non-constant polynomial
F
is the coefficient of the highest power of
\lv(F)
in
F
,
which is a polynomial in indeterminates that are ranked lower than
\lv(F)
.
Similarly, the
discriminant
of
F
is also defined with respect to
\lv(F)
.

Definition 2.1
.

The algebraic system
\mathcal{S}
is said to be
simple
(with respect to
<
) if

(1)

the polynomials
F_{1},\ldots,F_{M},U_{1},\ldots,U_{N}
are not in
K
and have
pairwise distinct leading variables;

(2)

the initial and discriminant of each of these polynomials (say, with leading variable
z_{j+1}
)
do not admit roots
(a_{0},\ldots,a_{j})\in\overline{L}^{j+1}
in

(\pi_{j+1}\circ\ldots\circ\pi_{m})(\mathcal{V}_{\overline{L}}(\mathcal{S}))
.

An
algebraic Thomas decomposition
of a system
\mathcal{S}
as in (
2.1
) is
a finite collection of (algebraic) simple systems
\mathcal{S}_{i}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{\overline{L}}(\mathcal{S})=\dot{\bigcup}\,\mathcal{V}_{\overline{L}}(\mathcal{S}_{i}),

where
\dot{\cup}
denotes the disjoint union, see
[
13
, Definition 2.2.10]
.

Now let
K
be a differential field of characteristic zero,
endowed with the derivation
{}^{\prime}={\rm d}/{\rm d}x
, and consider
the differential polynomial ring
K\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}\}
in
differential indeterminates
y_{1}
, …,
y_{n}
representing unknown functions of
x
.
In other words, we consider the polynomial ring
over
K
in
y_{1}
, …,
y_{n}
and all their derivatives
y^{\prime}_{1}
,
y^{\prime\prime}_{1}
, …,

y^{\prime}_{n}
,
y^{\prime\prime}_{n}
, …, which are linearly ordered by the
ranking

(2.2)

y_{1}<y^{\prime}_{1}<y^{\prime\prime}_{1}<\ldots<y_{2}<y^{\prime}_{2}<y^{\prime\prime}_{2}<\ldots<y_{n}<y^{\prime}_{n}<y^{\prime\prime}_{n}<\ldots

(more generally, any ranking on the differential polynomial ring
K\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}\}
is assumed to satisfy
y^{(l)}_{j}<y^{(k)}_{j}

whenever
k>l
.)
Then for any differential polynomial
F\in K\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}\}\setminus K

its leading variable
\lv(F)
(with respect to
<
) and its initial
\init(F)

are defined as above. The
separant

\sepa(F)
of
F
is defined as the
partial derivative of
F
with respect to
\lv(F)
, which is also the initial of any
proper derivative of
F
.

Let
F
,
G\in K\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}\}\setminus K
. Then
F
can be reduced modulo
G

if the leading variables of
F
and
G
involve the same differential indeterminate,
say,
\lv(F)=y^{(k)}_{j}
and
\lv(G)=y^{(l)}_{j}
, and if
k>l
or

k=l
and the degree
d_{F}=\deg(F,y^{(k)}_{j})
of
F
in
y^{(k)}_{j}
is greater than or equal to the
degree
d_{G}=\deg(G,y^{(k)}_{j})
. In this case a (differential) pseudo-reduction
of
F
modulo
G
is possible as follows.

•

If
k=l
, then let

R=\init(G)\,F-\init(F)\,(y^{(k)}_{j})^{d_{F}-d_{G}}\,G.

•

If
k>l
, then let

R=\sepa(G)\,F-\init(F)\,(y^{(k)}_{j})^{d_{F}-1}G^{(k-l)}.

Then
R
is either constant or has leading variable
\lv(R)=\lv(F)
and degree in
\lv(F)
less than
d_{F}

or has leading variable
\lv(R)
ranked lower than
\lv(F)
.
If none of the above reduction steps can be performed, then
F

is said to be
(differentially) reduced modulo
G
.

Let
G_{1}
, …,
G_{r}\in K\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}\}\setminus K
(typically with
pairwise distinct leading variables). Iterated pseudo-reduction of
F

modulo each of the polynomials
G_{1}
, …,
G_{r}
yields, after finitely many steps, a
differential polynomial
R
, called

(differential) pseudo-remainder
of
F
modulo
G_{1}
, …,
G_{r}
,
that is reduced modulo
G_{1}
, …,
G_{r}
.
Taking the recursive representation
of (differential) polynomials into account, pseudo-reductions similar to the ones defined above
may be applied to ensure that all coefficients of
R
and their coefficients, etc.,
are (differentially) reduced modulo
G_{1}
, …,
G_{r}
as well. If we would like to emphasize
this property, we call
R

completely reduced modulo

G_{1}
, …,
G_{r}
.

A
differential system
is given by

(2.3)

\mathcal{S}=\{F_{1}=0,\ldots,F_{M}=0,U_{1}\neq 0,\ldots,U_{N}\neq 0\},

where
F_{1}
, …,
F_{M}
,
U_{1}
, …,
U_{N}\in K\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}\}
are differential polynomials.

Definition 2.2
.

The differential system
\mathcal{S}
is said to be
simple
(with respect to
<
) if

(1)

\mathcal{S}
is simple as an algebraic system;

(2)

each
F_{i}
is reduced modulo
F_{1}
, …,
F_{i-1}
,
F_{i+1}
, …,
F_{M}
;

(3)

each
U_{j}
is reduced modulo
F_{1}
, …,
F_{M}
.

If
F_{i}
and
U_{j}
in (
2
) and (
3
), respectively, are completely reduced
modulo
F_{1}
, …,
F_{i-1}
,
F_{i+1}
, …,
F_{M}
and modulo
F_{1}
, …,
F_{M}
, respectively,
then we call
\mathcal{S}
a
simple differential system with completely reduced equations and inequations
.

Let
L
be a differential extension field of
K
.
The solutions in
L
of the system of differential equations
\{F_{1}=0,\ldots,F_{M}=0\}
, fulfilling the inequations in
\mathcal{S}
, will be denoted by
\Sol_{L}(\mathcal{S})
.

A
(differential) Thomas decomposition

[
13
, Algorithm 2.2.56]

of a system (
2.3
) is a finite collection of simple (differential) systems
\mathcal{S}_{i}
such that

(2.4)

\Sol_{L}(\mathcal{S})=\dot{\bigcup}\,\Sol_{L}(\mathcal{S}_{i}).

Simple (differential) systems are formally integrable in the sense that they incorporate
all integrability conditions for formal power series solutions.

The computation of a Thomas decomposition of a differential system can be understood as an iteration of two phases (which can also be interwoven as described in
[
3
]
).
The first phase achieves simplicity in the algebraic sense by applying Euclidean division with case distinctions so as to ensure the triangular shape and that polynomials have non-vanishing initials and discriminants.
Splittings of the systems arise from these case distinctions.
In the second phase, differential pseudo-reductions are applied in order to ensure the remaining conditions in Definition
2.2
.
When non-zero pseudo-remainders are obtained, this process has to be restarted.

In the proof of Theorem
3.4
, we will use this approach.
More details can be found in
[
13
]
.

Implementations of the algebraic and differential Thomas decomposition methods have been
developed by T. Bächler and M. Lange-Hegermann. The implementation for differential systems
has been incorporated into Maple’s standard library since Maple 2018 and was also published
in the Computer Physics Communications library
[
9
]
.

2.2.
Algebraic Solutions of First Order Autonomous AODEs

Let
K
be an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero.
Let
K\langle\langle x\rangle\rangle
be the field of formal Puiseux series expanded around any
x_{0}\in K
or around infinity.
Since the equations and systems of consideration are invariant under translation of the independent variable, we can assume without loss of generality that the formal Puiseux series are expanded around zero or around infinity such as in
[
5
]
.

In this subsection we consider a subclass of formal Puiseux series, namely algebraic series.
These are
y(x)\in K\langle\langle x\rangle\rangle
such that there exists a non-zero
Q\in K[x,y]
with
Q(x,y(x))=0
.
Note that since the field of formal Puiseux series is algebraically closed, all algebraic solutions can be represented as (formal) Puiseux series.
In
[
1
]
it is stated, without an explicit proof, that if there exists one (non-constant) algebraic solution of a first order autonomous AODE, then all of them are algebraic.
For the convenience of the reader we provide a complete proof of this fact (see Theorem
2.4
) and additionally show that the minimal polynomials are equal up to a shift and the multiplication with a constant (Theorem
2.6
).

For
d_{x},d_{y}\in\mathbb{N}
, we say that a formal Puiseux series
y(x)\in K\langle\langle x\rangle\rangle
is
(d_{x},d_{y})
-algebraic
if and only if
y(x)
is algebraic over
K(x)
with a minimal polynomial
Q(x,y)\in K[x,y]
such that

\deg(Q,x)\leq d_{x},~\deg(Q,y)\leq d_{y}.

Rational functions
y(x)=\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}\in K(x)\setminus\{0\}
are
(d_{x},1)
-algebraic, where
\gcd(f,g)=1
and
d_{x}
is the maximum of the degrees of
f(x)
and
g(x)
, since they have the minimal polynomial

Q(x,y)=g(x)\,y-f(x).

Lemma 2.5 in
[
1
]
can be rewritten as follows.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
d_{x},d_{y}>0
.
Then there exists an autonomous differential polynomial
G\in K\{y\}
of order less or equal to
(d_{x}+1)(d_{y}+1)-1
such that for any
y(x)\in K\langle\langle x\rangle\rangle
,
y(x)
is
(d_{x},d_{y})
-algebraic over
K(x)
if and only if
y(x)\in\Sol_{K\langle\langle x\rangle\rangle}(G)
.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
F\in K[y,y^{\prime}]
be an irreducible polynomial.
Let
y(x)\in K\langle\langle x\rangle\rangle
be a non-constant algebraic solution of the differential equation
F(y,y^{\prime})=0
.
Then all non-constant formal Puiseux series solutions of
F(y,y^{\prime})=0
are algebraic over
K(x)
.

Proof.

Let
Q(x,y)\in K[x,y]
be the minimal polynomial of
y(x)

and let
G\in K[y,\ldots,y^{(d)}]
, where
d=(\deg(Q,x)+1)(\deg(Q,y)+1)-1
, be as in Lemma
2.3
.
Then we can compute the differential pseudo-remainder of
G
with respect to
F

(2.5)

\displaystyle R(y,y^{\prime})=I(y)^{k_{1}}\,S(y,y^{\prime})^{k_{2}}\,G-\sum_{0\leq i\leq d-1}G_{i}\,F^{(i)},

where
I=\init(F)\in K[y],S=\sepa(F)\in K[y,y^{\prime}],G_{i}\in K\{y\},k_{1},k_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
and
\deg(R,y^{\prime})<\deg(F,y^{\prime})
.
By plugging
y(x)
into equation (
2.5
), we obtain that
R(y(x),y^{\prime}(x))=0.

Let us show that
R
is the zero-polynomial.
Assume that
R
has positive degree in
y^{\prime}
, otherwise
R
depends only on
y
and the statement follows.
Consider the resultant

\displaystyle H(y)=\mathrm{res}(F,R,y^{\prime})=A_{1}(y,y^{\prime})\,F(y,y^{\prime})+A_{2}(y,y^{\prime})\,R(y,y^{\prime})

for some
A_{1},A_{2}\in K[y,y^{\prime}]
with
\deg(A_{1},y^{\prime})<\deg(R,y^{\prime}),\deg(A_{2},y^{\prime})<\deg(F,y^{\prime}).

Since
H(y(x))=0
and
y(x)
is non-constant,
H
is the zero polynomial.
Because
F
is an irreducible polynomial,
F
divides
A_{2}
or
R
, which is a contradiction to the degree conditions.
Hence,
R(y,y^{\prime})=0
.
Now let
\bar{y}(x)
be a non-constant Puiseux series solution of the differential equation
F(y,y^{\prime})=0
.
Substituting
\bar{y}(x)
into equation (
2.5
),

I(\bar{y}(x))^{k_{1}}\,S(\bar{y}(x),\bar{y}^{\prime}(x))^{k_{2}}\,G(\bar{y}(x),\ldots,\bar{y}^{(d)}(x))=0.

Similar as before, since
\bar{y}(x)
is non-constant and
F
is irreducible, it follows that the first two factors are non-zero and
\bar{y}(x)
is a solution of
G=0
.
Then the statement follows from Lemma
2.3
.
∎

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
F\in K[y,y^{\prime}]
be an irreducible polynomial.
Let
y_{1}(x),y_{2}(x)
be non-constant algebraic solutions of the differential equation
F(y,y^{\prime})=0
and let
Q_{1},Q_{2}\in K[x,y]
be their minimal polynomials.
Then there is
c\in K
such that

Q_{1}(x,y)=Q_{2}(x+c,y).

Proof.

From Theorem 3.4 in
[
1
]
we know that

d=\deg(Q_{i},x)=\deg(F,y^{\prime}).

For the proof we need
y_{0}\in K
such that all curve points
\mathbf{p}_{0}=(y_{0},p_{0})\in\mathbb{V}_{K}(F)
and
(x_{0},y_{0})\in\mathbb{V}_{K}(Q_{i})
, for
i\in\{1,2\}
, are somehow generic points.
To be precise, let
y_{0}\in K
be such that
the number of different roots of
F(y_{0},z)\in K[z]
and of
Q_{1}(x,y_{0}),Q_{2}(x,y_{0})\in K[x]
are maximal, namely equal to
d
.
Note that by the irreducibility of
F
and
Q_{i}
, there are only finitely many exceptional values for
y_{0}\in K
where this is not fulfilled.
Let
p_{1},\ldots,p_{d}\in K
,
x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}\in K
and
x_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,x_{d}^{\prime}\in K
be the distinct roots of
P(y_{0},z)
,
Q_{1}(x,y_{0})
and
Q_{2}(x,y_{0})
, respectively.
Centered at
(x_{j},y_{0})\in\mathbb{V}_{K}(Q_{1})
, for
j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
, we can compute by the Newton polygon method for algebraic equations the Puiseux expansions.
Since every point
(x_{j},y_{0})\in\mathbb{V}_{K}(Q_{1})
is regular, the expansion is unique and a formal power series

\varphi_{j}(x)=y_{0}+\sum_{k\geq 1}a_{j,k}(x-x_{j})^{k}.

Moreover, from
[
1
, Lemma 2.4]
it follows that
\varphi_{j}(x)
are solutions of the differential equation
F(y,y^{\prime})=0
.
In particular,

F(\varphi_{j}(x_{j}),\varphi_{j}^{\prime}(x_{j}))=F(y_{0},a_{j,1})=0.

By the choice of
y_{0}
,
\{a_{1,1},\ldots,a_{d,1}\}=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{d}\}.

Similarly, for
Q_{2}
and its Puiseux expansions

\psi_{j}(x)=y_{0}+\sum_{k\geq 1}a^{\prime}_{j,k}(x-x_{j}^{\prime})^{k}

we obtain
\{a^{\prime}_{1,1},\ldots,a^{\prime}_{d,1}\}=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{d}\}.

Without loss of generality we can assume that
a_{1,1}=p_{1}=a^{\prime}_{1,1}
.
Since
F
is independent of
x
and Cauchy-Kovalevskaya’s Theorem is applicable, because
(y_{0},p_{1})
is a regular curve point with
\tfrac{\partial F}{\partial z}(y_{0},p_{1})\neq 0
, all coefficients
a_{1,k}=a^{\prime}_{1,k}
coincide and

y(x)=y_{0}+\sum_{k\geq 1}a_{1,k}\,x^{k}

is the unique solution of the differential equation
F(y,y^{\prime})=0
with the initial condition
(y(0),y^{\prime}(0))=(y_{0},p_{1})
centered around the origin.
Now the irreducible polynomials
\bar{Q}_{1}(x,y)=Q_{1}(x+x_{1},y)
and
\bar{Q}_{2}(x,y)=Q_{2}(x+x_{1}^{\prime},y)
have the common root
y(x)
, which is only possible if they divide each other.
Since minimal polynomials are monic, the statement follows for
c=x_{1}^{\prime}-x_{1}
.
∎

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
F\in K[y,y^{\prime}]
be irreducible and let
y(x)
be a non-constant algebraic solution of the differential equation
F(y,y^{\prime})=0
with minimal polynomial
Q\in K[x,y]
.
Then all non-constant formal Puiseux series solutions of the differential equation
F(y,y^{\prime})=0
are algebraic and given by
Q(x+c,y)
, where
c\in K
.

Proof.

First let us prove that, for
c\in K
, all roots of
Q(x+c,y)
are indeed solutions of the differential equation
F(y,y^{\prime})=0
.
The pseudo-remainder of
F
with respect to
Q
is

R(x,y)=I(x)^{k_{1}}\,S(x,y)^{k_{2}}\,F(y,y^{\prime})-A_{1}\,Q(x,y)-A_{2}\,Q^{\prime}(x,y,y^{\prime})

where
I=\init(Q)\in K[x],S=\sepa(Q)\in K[x,y],A_{1},A_{2}\in K[x,y,y^{\prime}],k_{1},k_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
and
\deg(R,y)<\deg(Q,y)
.
Since
Q
is irreducible with the root
y(x)
,
R
is the zero-polynomial (compare to the proof of Theorem
2.4
).
In the above equation, substitute
x
by
x+c
and set
\bar{Q}(x,y)=Q(x+c,y)
.
Note that
\bar{Q}^{\prime}(x,y,y^{\prime})=Q^{\prime}(x+c,y,y^{\prime})
and the initial
\bar{I}
and the separant
\bar{S}
of
\bar{Q}
are
I(x+c)
and
S(x+c,y)
, respectively.
Then

0=\bar{I}(x)^{k_{1}}\,\bar{S}(x,y)^{k_{2}}\,F(y,y^{\prime})-\bar{A}_{1}\,\bar{Q}-\bar{A}_{2}\,\bar{Q}^{\prime},

where
\bar{A}_{1}(x,y,y^{\prime})=A_{1}(x+c,y,y^{\prime}),\bar{A}_{2}(x,y,y^{\prime})=A_{2}(x+c,y,y^{\prime})
.
Let
\bar{y}(x)
be a non-constant root of
\bar{Q}
.
Hence,
\bar{Q}^{\prime}(x,\bar{y}(x),\bar{y}^{\prime}(x))=0
,
\bar{I}(x)\,\bar{S}(x,\bar{y}(x))\neq 0
and consequently,
F(\bar{y}(x),\bar{y}^{\prime}(x))=0.

The converse direction is Lemma
2.5
.
∎

3.
Algebraic-Geometric Dimension of Differential Systems

Let
K
be an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero, considered as differential field of constants under the derivation
′
,
and let
L
be a differential extension field of
K
.
For
n\geq 1
we denote by
L\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}\}
the differential polynomial ring in the indeterminates
y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}
.
We consider systems of algebraic ordinary differential equations
in several differential indeterminates of algebraic dimension
equal to one in the following sense.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
F_{1},\ldots,F_{M}
,
U_{1},\ldots,U_{N}\in L[y_{1},\ldots,y_{1}^{(m_{1})},\ldots,y_{n},\ldots,y_{n}^{(m_{n})}]
be differential polynomials effectively depending on
y_{1}^{(m_{1})},\ldots,y_{n}^{(m_{n})}
for some
m_{1}
, …,
m_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then we define the
(algebraic) dimension
of the corresponding system

(3.1)

\mathcal{S}=\{F_{1}=0,\ldots,F_{M}=0,U_{1}\neq 0,\ldots,U_{N}\neq 0\}

as the dimension of
\mathcal{V}_{\overline{L}}(\mathcal{S})\subset\overline{L}^{m_{1}+\cdots+m_{n}+n}
(see Subsection
2.1
for the precise definition of
\mathcal{V}_{\overline{L}}(\mathcal{S})
).

In what follows we often let
L=K(x)
with
{}^{\prime}=\textrm{d}/\textrm{d}x
.
In that case we would not consider systems (
3.1
)
with equations or inequations involving only
x
.

Example 3.2
.

The differential systems
\{y_{1}^{\prime 2}+y_{2}^{3}=0,2y_{1}-y_{1}^{\prime}y_{2}=0,y_{1}\neq 0\}
and
\{y^{\prime}+x=0\}
have algebraic dimension one, whereas the system

\{y^{\prime}+y=0,y^{\prime\prime}\neq 0\}
has algebraic dimension two.

Let us remark that
\mathcal{V}_{\overline{L}}(\mathcal{S})
may be a strict subset of
\mathcal{V}_{\overline{L}}(\{F_{1},\ldots,F_{M}\})
.

Note that our definition of dimension is purely algebraic and does not correspond to notions of differential dimension
presented, e.g., in
[
12
]
or
[
10
]
.
In particular, it is not necessary to consider the differential ideal generated by
\mathcal{S}
nor its set of generic solutions.
As Example
3.5
shows, there are some systems which have algebraic dimension equal to one, but their differential dimension is bigger.

Remark 3.3
.

There are several methods for computing the dimension of an algebraic set, which makes it easy to verify an assumption on the dimension imposed on a system (
3.1
).
For example, for a given system
\mathcal{S}
as in (
3.1
), an
algebraic
Thomas decomposition
\mathcal{S}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{S}_{r}
of
\mathcal{S}

can be computed (cf., e.g.,
[
13
, Subsection 2.2.1]
).
The dimension of
\mathcal{V}_{\overline{L}}(\mathcal{S}_{i})
is the difference of the dimension of the ambient affine space
and the number of (distinct) leading variables of the equations in
\mathcal{S}_{i}
.
Then the dimension of
\mathcal{V}_{\overline{L}}(\mathcal{S})
is the maximum of the dimensions of the
\mathcal{V}_{\overline{L}}(\mathcal{S}_{i})
.
Note that each system is a finite set of equations and inequations, so that only finitely
many indeterminates occur, and for our purposes, the coordinate ring of the
ambient affine space is of the form
K[y_{1},\ldots,y_{1}^{(m_{1})},\ldots,y_{n},\ldots,y_{n}^{(m_{n})}]
for certain
m_{1}
, …,
m_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
.

We are going to explain, in the following theorem, how the algebraic dimension is affected by the construction of a
differential
Thomas decomposition.
The resulting simple differential systems may refer to different ambient affine spaces in general.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a differential system as in (
3.1
), of algebraic dimension
d
.
Then there exists a Thomas decomposition of
\mathcal{S}
(with respect to
<
)
with completely reduced equations and inequations
whose simple differential systems all
have algebraic dimension less than or equal to
d
.

Proof.

We recall that a Thomas decomposition of a
differential
system
\mathcal{S}
(consisting of
ordinary differential polynomials) can be obtained as follows.
We repeat (if necessary) two stages, namely the computation of an algebraic Thomas decomposition and
differential pseudo-reductions, in a loop.
In the first stage, iterated Euclidean pseudo-reduction is applied to pairs of left hand sides of equations and
inequations of the system, in order to obtain a system that is simple in the algebraic sense (cf. Definition
2.1
).
In general this process requires case distinctions so as to ensure non-vanishing (on the solution
set of the system) of initials and discriminants of the polynomials involved, which leads to a splitting of the system into
subsystems, whose solution sets form a partition of the solution set of the original system.
Let
\mathcal{S}_{1}
, …,
\mathcal{S}_{r}
be the resulting (sub)systems, which are simple in the algebraic sense.
Due to the above mentioned partition of the solution set of
\mathcal{S}
,
if
\mathcal{S}
has algebraic dimension
d
, then each
\mathcal{S}_{i}

has algebraic dimension at most
d
(in the same ambient affine space as for
\mathcal{S}
).

For each system
\mathcal{S}_{i}
obtained so far, the second stage applies differential pseudo-reductions modulo the
equations in
\mathcal{S}_{i}
as explained in Subsection
2.1
, so as to work towards a simple differential
system with completely reduced equations and inequations. Pseudo-reductions with respect to the greatest variable
(with respect to
<
) are performed first.
In this step of our reasoning we may consider reductions modulo
proper
derivatives of equations
G=0
in
\mathcal{S}_{i}

only. Let
\mathrm{Coord}(\mathcal{S}_{i})
be the set of coordinates of the ambient affine space for
\mathcal{S}_{i}
, say,

\mathrm{Coord}(\mathcal{S}_{i})=\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{1}^{(m_{1})},\ldots,y_{n},\ldots,y_{n}^{(m_{n})}\}
,
and let
\mathrm{LV}^{=}(\mathcal{S}_{i})
be the set of leading variables of equations in
\mathcal{S}_{i}
.
The pseudo-remainder
R
of a reduction of
F
modulo
G
, where
F=0
and
G=0
are
equations in
\mathcal{S}_{i}
, is either zero or not.

If
R
is zero, then
F=0
is omitted from
\mathcal{S}_{i}
.
The leading variable of
F
may occur in equations of
\mathcal{S}_{i}
whose leading variables are ranked higher than
\lv(F)
and possibly in inequations.
Differential pseudo-reductions modulo
G=0
eliminate
\lv(F)
from
\mathcal{S}_{i}
altogether, because any proper derivative of
G
has degree one in its leading variable.
We call the resulting system
\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{i}
.
The cardinality
|\mathrm{LV}^{=}(\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{i})|
is
|\mathrm{LV}^{=}(\mathcal{S}_{i})|-1
and
|\mathrm{Coord}(\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{i})|<|\mathrm{Coord}(\mathcal{S}_{i})|
.
Hence, the algebraic dimension of
\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{i}
is the same or smaller as that of
\mathcal{S}_{i}
.

If
R
is a non-zero element of
L
, then the system
\mathcal{S}_{i}
is inconsistent (as differential system)
and is discarded.

Otherwise
R
is a differential polynomial with a leading variable
\lv(R)
that is ranked lower
than
\lv(F)
.
After elimination of
\lv(F)
from
\mathcal{S}_{i}
(using
G
) as described above and replacing
F=0
by
R=0
, we obtain a new system
\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{i}
with
|\mathrm{Coord}(\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{i})|<|\mathrm{Coord}(\mathcal{S}_{i})|
.
The indeterminate
\lv(R)
is certainly an element of
\mathrm{Coord}(\mathcal{S}_{i})
,
but either
\lv(R)
is an element of
\mathrm{LV}^{=}(\mathcal{S}_{i})
or not.
If not, then
|\mathrm{LV}^{=}(\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{i})|=|\mathrm{LV}^{=}(\mathcal{S}_{i})|
so that
the algebraic dimension of the system drops.
If
\lv(R)
is an element of
\mathrm{LV}^{=}(\mathcal{S}_{i})
, then the
algebraic dimension either does not change or drops.

For differential reductions of inequations
F\neq 0
modulo (
proper
derivatives of)
equations
G=0
in
\mathcal{S}_{i}
we have a similar case distinction.
If the pseudo-remainder
R
is zero, then the system
is inconsistent and is discarded. If
R
is a non-zero element of
L
, then the
inequation
F\neq 0
is omitted from
\mathcal{S}_{i}
.
Otherwise
R
is a differential polynomial with a leading variable
\lv(R)
that is ranked lower
than
\lv(F)
. In both of these cases, after elimination of further occurrences of
\lv(F)

in
\mathcal{S}_{i}
, for the resulting system
\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{i}
it holds that
|\mathrm{Coord}(\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{i})|<|\mathrm{Coord}(\mathcal{S}_{i})|
. As a consequence, the
algebraic dimension of the system drops at least by one as well, because

\mathrm{LV}^{=}(\mathcal{S}_{i})=\mathrm{LV}^{=}(\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{i})
.

If any differential reduction was performed on a system
\mathcal{S}_{i}
we start over
with the modified system
\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{i}
and compute an algebraic Thomas decomposition of it, where the ambient affine space may now be different to the one for the
original system
\mathcal{S}
. This step may lead to further splittings. Differential
pseudo-reduction might have to be performed again, etc.

This loop is a special case for ordinary differential polynomials
of the Thomas Algorithm and it terminates with finitely many simple differential
systems after finitely many steps.
∎

Example 3.5
.

Let
\mathcal{S}=\{F_{1}=z^{\prime 2}+z=0,\ F_{2}=yz^{\prime}=0\}
be a system of autonomous AODEs,
defined over
K=\mathbb{C}
. Let us check that
\mathcal{S}
has algebraic dimension one.
For that we compute an algebraic Thomas decomposition of
\mathcal{S}
with respect to
y<z<z^{\prime}
:

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{1}=\{y=0,z^{\prime 2}+z=0\},\quad\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{2}=\{z=0,z^{\prime}=0,y\neq 0\},

which are of dimension one because the set of leading variables has two elements.
A differential Thomas decomposition is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{1}=\{y=0,z^{\prime 2}+z=0,z\neq 0\},\ \mathcal{S}_{2}=\{y=0,z=0\},\ \mathcal{S}_{3}=\{z=0,y\neq 0\}.

Note that
\mathcal{S}_{3}
is of dimension two in
\mathbb{C}[y,z,z^{\prime}]
, but this does not contradict Theorem
3.4
because
\mathcal{S}_{3}
is, seen as independent system, of algebraic dimension one.

In the following we show that, if the given differential system is of algebraic dimension one, the construction of a differential Thomas decomposition results in simple systems of a very particular form.
This leads to results on convergence and on the computation of algebraic solutions (cf. Section
4
and Section
4.1
.)

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a differential system as in (
3.1
)
and let the ranking
<
on
L\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}\}
be defined as in (
2.2
).
If
\mathcal{S}
is simple (with respect to
<
) and of algebraic dimension
at most one, then it is of one of the following types:

(I)

\left\{\quad\begin{array}[]{rcll}G_{s}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{s})&=&0,&\quad s\in\{1,\ldots,t-1\},\\[1.99997pt]
G_{t}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{t},y^{\prime}_{t})&=&0,\\[1.99997pt]
G_{s}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{t},y_{t}^{\prime},y_{t+1},\ldots,y_{s})&=&0,&\quad s\in\{t+1,\ldots,n\},\\[1.99997pt]
U(y_{1},\ldots,y_{t})&\neq&0,\end{array}\right.

for a unique
t\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, where
\lv(G_{t})=y_{t}^{\prime}
;

(II)

\left\{\quad\begin{array}[]{rcll}G_{s}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{s})&=&0,&\quad s\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\setminus\{t\},\\[1.99997pt]
U(y_{1},\ldots,y_{t})&\neq&0,\end{array}\right.

for a unique
t\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
;

(III)

\left\{\quad\begin{array}[]{rcll}G_{s}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{s})&=&0,&\quad s\in\{1,\ldots,n\},\end{array}\right.

where, in each of the systems (
I
), (
II
), (
III
),
for all
s
in the admissible range, we have
G_{s}\in L[y_{1},\ldots,y_{s}]
with
either
\lv(G_{s})=y_{s}
or
G_{s}
is the zero polynomial and all other equations in the system
are independent of
y_{s}
, and
U
is a non-zero element of
L
or
U\in L[y_{1},\ldots,y_{t}]
with
\lv(U_{t})=y_{t}
.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{S}
is
of algebraic dimension at most one,
there exist a minimal subset
\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{q}\}

of
\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}\}
, say, of cardinality
q
, and non-negative integers
m_{1}
, …,
m_{q}

such that all equations and inequations
of
\mathcal{S}
are elements
of
L[z_{1},\ldots,z_{1}^{(m_{1})},\ldots,z_{q},\ldots,z_{q}^{(m_{q})}]\setminus L

and
\mathcal{V}_{\overline{L}}(\mathcal{S})\subset\overline{L}^{m_{1}+\cdots+m_{q}+q}
is of dimension at most one.

Considered as an algebraic system,
\mathcal{S}
admits solutions

(\eta_{1,0},\ldots,\eta_{1,m_{1}},\eta_{2,0},\ldots,\eta_{2,m_{2}},\ldots,\eta_{q,0},\ldots,\eta_{q,m_{q}})\in\overline{L}^{m_{1}+\cdots+m_{q}+q},

where the coordinates of the affine space are arranged in accordance with the ranking (
2.2
).
By simplicity of
\mathcal{S}
, every solution is obtained as follows (cf. also

[
13
, Remark 2.2.5]
). Suppose that all coordinates of a solution
preceding
\eta_{k,l}
are already determined. If there is an equation
G_{j}=0
in
\mathcal{S}

with
\lv(G_{j})=z_{k}^{(l)}
and degree
d
in
\lv(G_{j})
, then there are exactly
d

different
\eta_{k,l}\in\overline{L}
such that
G_{j}(\eta_{1,0},\ldots,\eta_{k,l})=0
,
because the initial and discriminant of the polynomial
G_{j}
in
\lv(G_{j})
do not
vanish when evaluated at the preceding coordinates, and
\overline{L}
is algebraically closed.
Similarly, if there is an inequation
G_{j}\neq 0
in
\mathcal{S}

with
\lv(G_{j})=z_{k}^{(l)}
and degree
d
in
\lv(G_{j})
, then all
\eta_{k,l}\in\overline{L}

except
d
many satisfy
G_{j}(\eta_{1,0},\ldots,\eta_{k,l})\neq 0
.
If there is neither an equation nor an inequation in
\mathcal{S}

with leading variable
z_{k}^{(l)}
, then
\eta_{k,l}\in\overline{L}
can be chosen arbitrarily.

We enumerate the differential polynomials
G_{j}
occurring
as left hand sides of equations and inequations in
\mathcal{S}

in increasing order with respect to their pairwise distinct leading variables
\lv(G_{j})
.
Since in
\mathcal{S}
each equation is differentially
reduced modulo the other equations and each inequation is differentially reduced
modulo the equations, for every
k\in\{1,\ldots,q\}
there exists at most
one non-negative integer
\overline{m}_{k}\leq m_{k}
such that
z_{k}^{(\overline{m}_{k})}
is
the leading variable of some equation
G_{j}=0
, and if
\overline{m}_{k}
exists
and
z_{k}^{(n_{k})}
is the leading variable of an inequation in
\mathcal{S}
, then
n_{k}<\overline{m}_{k}
.

Suppose that all
\overline{m}_{1}
, …,
\overline{m}_{q}
exist,
i.e., for all
k\in\{1,\ldots,q\}

some equation in
\mathcal{S}
has leading variable
z_{k}^{(\overline{m}_{k})}
.
If
\overline{m}_{1}=m_{1}
, …,
\overline{m}_{q}=m_{q}
, then, since
\mathcal{S}

is of algebraic dimension at most one, either
m_{1}=\ldots=m_{q}=0
,
in which case
\mathcal{S}
is a system of type (
III
),
or
m_{j}=1
for a unique
j\in\{1,\ldots,q\}
and
m_{k}=0
for all
k\neq j
,
in which case
\mathcal{S}
is a system of type (
I
) with
t=j
.
On the other hand, if
\overline{m}_{j}<m_{j}
for some
j\in\{1,\ldots,q\}
,
then no equation in
\mathcal{S}
involves
z_{j}^{(m_{j})}
, and since
\mathcal{S}
is
of algebraic dimension at most one and all

\overline{m}_{1}
, …,
\overline{m}_{q}
exist,
\mathcal{S}
contains no inequation
and is of type (
III
).

Now suppose that
\overline{m}_{j}
does not exist.
According to the recursive solution procedure recalled above,
the coordinates
\eta_{j,0}
,
\eta_{j,1}
, …,
\eta_{j,m_{j}}
attain
arbitrary values in
L
, possibly with the exception of finitely many values
if
\mathcal{S}
contains an inequation with leading variable
z_{j}^{(n_{j})}
.
Since
\mathcal{S}
is of algebraic dimension at most one,
we conclude that at most one
\overline{m}_{j}
may not exist, and in that case
we have
m_{j}=0
, and
\mathcal{S}
is of type (
II
) with
t=j
.
∎

Note that a differential system of type (
I
)
or (
II
) or (
III
) in Proposition
3.6
is of algebraic dimension
1
,
1
or
0
, respectively.
In Example
3.5
, the systems
\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{2}
and
\mathcal{S}_{3}
are of type (
I
), (
III
) and (
II
), respectively.

Corollary 3.7
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a differential system as in (
3.1
)
and let the ranking
<
on
L\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}\}
be defined as in (
2.2
).
If
\mathcal{S}
is of algebraic dimension one, then there exists
a Thomas decomposition of
\mathcal{S}
(with respect to
<
) each of whose simple differential systems
is of one of the types (
I
), (
II
) and (
III
) from Proposition
3.6
.

Proof.

This is a combination of Theorem
3.4
and
Proposition
3.6
.
∎

4.
Solutions of Differential Systems of Dimension One

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a differential system as in (
3.1
) with coefficients in the algebraically closed field
K
, i.e. the differential polynomials in
\mathcal{S}
have constant coefficients.
We are interested in formal Puiseux series solution vectors

Y(x)=(y_{1}(x),\ldots,y_{n}(x))\in K\langle\langle x\rangle\rangle^{n}

of
\mathcal{S}
, expanded around
x_{0}\in\{0,\infty\}
.

Remark 4.1
.

The components of the solution
Y(x)=(y_{1}(x),\ldots,y_{n}(x))\in K\langle\langle x\rangle\rangle^{n}
can be assumed to have non-negative order.
Otherwise, choose
I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}
as the set of indices where the order is negative and perform the change of variable
\tilde{y}_{i}=1/y_{i}
in
\mathcal{S}
for every
i\in I
.
More precisely, let
\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{1}^{(m_{1})},\ldots,y_{n},\ldots,y_{n}^{(m_{n})}\}
be the ambient space for
\mathcal{S}
and let
m=m_{1}+\cdots+m_{n}+n
.
If the differential polynomials
F_{1},\ldots,F_{M}\in\mathcal{S}
have some
y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}
as factors, divide these equations by such factors.
Then we define for every
i\in I
the mapping

\Phi_{i}:K^{m_{i}+1}\setminus\mathbb{V}_{K}(u_{0})\rightarrow K^{m_{i}+1}\setminus\mathbb{V}_{K}(w_{0});(u_{0},\ldots,u_{m_{i}})\mapsto(w_{0},\ldots,w_{m_{i}}),

where
w_{0}=1/u_{0}
and

w_{j}=\dfrac{-u_{0}^{j-1}\,u_{j}+R_{j}(u_{0},\ldots,u_{j-1})}{u_{0}^{j+1}}

for some polynomial
R_{j}
.
Since the equality above is linear in
u_{j}
,
\Phi_{i}
is birational.
For all
i\notin I
let us define
\Phi_{i}
as the identity map on
K^{m_{i}+1}
.
Then the mapping

\boldsymbol{\Phi}(u_{1,0},\ldots,u_{n,m_{n}})=(\Phi_{1}(u_{1,0},\ldots,u_{1,m_{1}}),\ldots,\Phi_{n}(u_{n,0},\ldots,u_{n,m_{n}}))

is birational on
K^{m}\setminus\bigcup_{i\in I}\mathbb{V}_{K}(y_{i})
.
Let us apply
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
to
\mathcal{S}
and call the resulting system
\mathcal{S}^{*}
.
The Zariski closure of
\boldsymbol{\Phi}(\mathcal{V}_{K}(\mathcal{S}))
is the Zariski closure of
\mathcal{V}_{K}(\mathcal{S}^{*})
.
In particular,
\dim(\mathcal{V}_{K}(\mathcal{S}))=\dim(\mathcal{V}_{K}(\mathcal{S}^{*}))
and one may proceed with
\mathcal{S}^{*}
instead of
\mathcal{S}
.

In the following we will again impose that the given differential system is of algebraic dimension one.
This allows to use in particular Proposition
3.6
.

Remark 4.2
.

Clearly, by definition of Thomas decomposition (cf. (
2.4
)),
the set of solution vectors of
\mathcal{S}
is partitioned into the subsets of solution vectors of the simple differential systems in Proposition
3.6
.
Systems of type (
II
) or (
III
) are algebraic systems.
Of main interest for our further study of differential systems of dimension are systems of type (
I
).

Following the discussion in Proposition
3.6
, the construction of a differential Thomas decomposition of
\mathcal{S}
may eliminate certain differential indeterminates
y_{s}
in some subsystems
\mathcal{S}_{i}
, see e.g. Example
3.5
.
Then the solutions of
\mathcal{S}_{i}
do not include the components corresponding to the
y_{s}
and when prolonging to a solution of
\mathcal{S}
, these components can be chosen arbitrarily.
In the following we may speak in this case about the
free variables

y_{s}
.
Additionally, for systems of type (
II
),
y_{t}
can be chosen arbitrarily except for a finite number of constants possibly excluded by the inequation
U(y_{1},\ldots,y_{t})\neq 0
.
In this situation we call
y_{t}
a
parametric variable
.

In systems
\mathcal{S}_{i}
of the type (
III
), the components which are not free variables are constants.
The same holds for the first
1\leq s<t
components of systems
\mathcal{S}_{i}
of the type (
II
).
The parametric variable
y_{t}
can be chosen (almost) arbitrary.
The following components depend on this choice and, for example, if
y_{t}
and free variables are chosen constant, every of the remaining components is again constant or a free variable.
In systems
\mathcal{S}_{i}
is of the type (
I
), the first
1\leq s<t
components of a solution are constants
\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{t-1}\in\overline{L}
or a free variable, which might be chosen constant.
Plug these values into
\mathcal{S}
in order to obtain a system of type (
I
) where
G_{t}(\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{t-1},y_{t},y_{t}^{\prime})\in\overline{L}[y_{t},y_{t}^{\prime}]
is the polynomial with lowest ranked leading variable.
Hence, we may assume occasionally that
t=1
.

For showing that the components of the solutions are convergent or algebraic, we may replace the components of the free variables in a solution by zero or any other suitable function.
A parametric variable
y_{t}
can be substituted by almost every constant and every non-constant suitable function such as
x
.
In this case, the next components
y_{t+1}(x),y_{t+2}(x),\ldots
have to be substituted accordingly.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
K=\mathbb{C}
.
Let
\mathcal{S}
be a differential system as is (
3.1
), of algebraic dimension one, and let
Y(x)=(y_{1}(x),\ldots,y_{n}(x))\in\mathbb{C}\langle\langle x\rangle\rangle^{n}
be a solution of
\mathcal{S}
.
Let
N
be a non-negative integer.
Then there exists a solution
\bar{Y}(x)=(\bar{y}_{1}(x),\ldots,\bar{y}_{n}(x))
of
\mathcal{S}
with convergent components such that
Y(x)\equiv\bar{Y}(x)\mod x^{N}
.

Proof.

By Corollary
3.7
, we may restrict our attention to simple systems
\bar{\mathcal{S}}
of types (
I
), (
II
) and (
III
).
If
Y(x)
is a solution of a system of type (
II
) or (
III
), the result follows by the Artin approximation theorem (see
[
2
, Theorem 1.2]
).

In the case that
\bar{\mathcal{S}}
is of type (
I
), the components in
Y(x)
corresponding to the free variables will be substituted by its truncations up to order
N
.
For
1\leq s<t
, the component
y_{s}(x)
is either a constant or corresponds to a free variable.
Plugging them into
G_{t}
, we obtain the autonomous differential equation
H_{t}(y_{t},y_{t}^{\prime})=G_{t}(y_{1}(x),\ldots,y_{t-1}(x),y_{t},y_{t}^{\prime})=0
with leading variable
y_{t}^{\prime}
.
Applying
[
6
, Theorem 11]
to
H_{t}(y_{t},y_{t}^{\prime})=0
, the component
y_{t}(x)
is convergent.
For
s>t
, let us proof by induction that
y_{s}(x)
is convergent.
If
y_{s}
is a free variable, then
y_{s}(x)
is convergent.
Otherwise, let us consider

H_{s}(x,y_{s})=G_{s}(y_{1}(x),\ldots,y_{t}(x),y_{t}^{\prime}(x),\ldots,y_{s-1}(x),y_{s})=0.

Since the system is simple, the leading variable of
H_{s}
is
y_{s}
and,
by Puiseux’s Theorem,
y_{s}(x)
is convergent.
∎

In the proof of Theorem
4.3
we have evaluated the polynomials
G_{s}
at Puiseux series in order to obtain the equations
H_{s}(x,y_{s})=0
.
In case that
y_{t}(x)
is algebraic over
K(x)
, these evaluations can be performed by using its minimal polynomial which leads to algorithmic computations as we show in the following section.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a differential system as is (
3.1
), of algebraic dimension one.
Then it can be decided algorithmically whether
\mathcal{S}
has a formal Puiseux series solution.
Moreover, it can be decided whether a formal Puiseux series solution with a non-constant component exists.

Proof.

By Corollary
3.7
, there exists a Thomas decomposition of
\mathcal{S}
into simple systems of the type (
I
), (
II
) or (
III
) with the same solution set as
\mathcal{S}
.
The third system has constant solutions.
There are no other formal Puiseux series solutions if and only if it has no free variables (see Remark
4.2
).
In systems of type (
II
), for the first
1\leq s<t
equations there exist constant solutions
\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{t-1}
.
Then any non-constant formal Puiseux series
y_{t}(x)
fulfills the inequality
U\neq 0
.
Since the field of formal Puiseux series is algebraically closed, the tuple
(\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{t-1},y_{t}(x))
can be prolonged to a non-constant solution (cf. proof of Theorem
4.3
).

In the first
1\leq s<t
equations of systems of the type (
I
), we again obtain constant solution components
\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{t-1}
.
The differential equation
G_{t}=0
has infinitely many non-constant formal power series solutions
y_{t}(x)
(see e.g.
[
6
]
).
Take one of them and again prolong
(\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{t-1},y_{t}(x))
to a non-constant solution.

The system
\mathcal{S}
does not have any solution if and only if all subsystems are inconsistent and discarded in the process of computing the Thomas decomposition.
Moreover,
\mathcal{S}
has only constant solutions if and only if all subsystems in the Thomas decomposition are of type (
III
) and do not involve free variables.
∎

4.1.
Algebraic Solutions

The Thomas decomposition of a given differential system
\mathcal{S}
leads to simple subsystems
\mathcal{S}_{i}
with a disjoint solution set.
In the case where
\mathcal{S}
is of algebraic dimension one, the
\mathcal{S}_{i}
can be assumed to be of a specific type (see Proposition
3.6
).
In this section, after some possible further decomposition of the
\mathcal{S}_{i}
and using the results from Section
2.2
,
we show that the existence of algebraic solutions can be decided and, in the affirmative case, all of them can be determined.
The algebraic solutions are expressed either as a simple system (involving no derivatives) or by their minimal polynomials.
In the first case, all of the solutions can be recovered whereas in the latter case there might be combinations of roots which are not a solution of the given system
\mathcal{S}
.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a simple system as in (
I
) with
t=1
,
let
P_{1}\in K(x)[y_{1}]
be a polynomial with
\lv(P_{1})=y_{1}

and let
<
be as in (
2.2
).
Then the simple differential systems in any Thomas decomposition of
\mathcal{S}\cup\{P_{1}=0\}
with respect to
<
are of the following type:

(IV)

\left\{\quad\begin{array}[]{rcll}H_{s}(x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{s})&=&0,&\quad s\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\end{array}\right.

where
H_{s}\in K(x)[y_{1},\ldots,y_{s}]
with either
\lv(H_{s})=y_{s}
or
H_{s}
is the zero-polynomial
and all other polynomials are independent of
y_{s}
.

Proof.

Since
\lv(P_{1})=y_{1}
is the lowest ranked variable and
P_{1}
is already completely reduced modulo
\mathcal{S}
, the system
\mathcal{S}\cup\{P_{1}=0\}
is either inconsistent or has algebraic dimension zero.
From now on we assume the latter.

Let
\mathcal{S}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{S}_{r}
be the simple subsystems of a Thomas decomposition of
\mathcal{S}\cup\{P_{1}=0\}
with respect to
<
.
During the construction of the Thomas decomposition, the polynomial
G_{1}
from (
I
) was reduced by
P_{1}^{\prime}
, which has degree one in
y_{1}^{\prime}
, and the derivative is eliminated.

Fix
i\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
and let
\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{q}\}
be the minimal subset of
\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}\}
such that the elements in
\mathcal{S}_{i}
are in
K(x)[z_{1},\ldots,z_{q}]
.
Since the algebraic dimension of
\mathcal{S}_{i}
is zero, for every
s\in\{1,\ldots,q\}
there exists a unique equation
H_{s}=0
with
H_{s}\in K(x)[z_{1},\ldots,z_{s}]
and
\lv(H_{s})=z_{s}
.
Since the set of leading variables of
\mathcal{S}_{i}
is
\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{q}\}
, the system contains only (algebraic) equations and
\mathcal{S}_{i}
is of the type (
IV
).
∎

Let us note that in Lemma
4.5
we do not necessarily choose
P_{1}
to be irreducible.

Corollary 4.6
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a differential system of the type (
I
) and
\mathcal{H}
be an algebraic subsystem of
\mathcal{S}
of the form (
IV
).
Let
Y(x)=(y_{1}(x),\ldots,y_{n}(x))\in K\langle\langle x\rangle\rangle^{n}
be a solution of
\mathcal{H}
.
Then, for every
1\leq s\leq n
, the component
y_{s}(x)
is algebraic or
y_{s}
is a free variable.

Proof.

Replace in
Y(x)
all the components which are not appearing in
\mathcal{H}
by zero and call the resulting vector
\tilde{Y}(x)=(\tilde{y}_{1}(x),\ldots,\tilde{y}_{n}(x))
, which is a solution of
\mathcal{H}
, and therefore of
\mathcal{S}
.
Since
\mathcal{H}
is simple and of algebraic dimension zero, the tower of field extensions

K\subseteq K(\tilde{y}_{1}(x))\subseteq\cdots\subseteq K(\tilde{y}_{1}(x),\ldots,\tilde{y}_{n}(x))

is algebraic.
∎

Due to Corollary
4.6
, we may speak in the following about algebraic solutions instead of formal Puiseux series solutions where all components are algebraic.
Let us now show that all of the algebraic solutions can be found algorithmically.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
Y(x)=(y_{1}(x),\ldots,y_{n}(x))\in K\langle\langle x\rangle\rangle^{n}
be an algebraic solution of a system
\mathcal{S}
as in (
I
).
Then there exists a simple algebraic subsystem
\mathcal{H}
of
\mathcal{S}
, of the form (
IV
), having
Y(x)
as solution.

Proof.

Let us assume that
t=1
(cf. Remark
4.2
).
The algebraic Puiseux series
y_{1}(x)
is a solution of the differential equation
G_{1}(y,y^{\prime})=0
from
\mathcal{S}
.
There is a factor
F_{1}(y,y^{\prime})
of the polynomial
G_{1}
such that
y_{1}(x)
is a solution of the differential equation
F_{1}(y_{1},y_{1}^{\prime})=0
.
Let
Q_{1}(x,y_{1})\in K[x,y_{1}]
be the minimal polynomial of
y_{1}(x)
.
Let
\mathcal{H}
be the algebraic system obtained when
applying Lemma
4.5
to
\mathcal{S}\cup\{Q_{1}=0\}
.
Then,
\mathcal{H}
is among the subsystems derived from the Thomas decomposition of
\mathcal{S}\cup\{Q_{1}=0\}
,
and hence
\mathcal{H}
is a simple algebraic subsystem of
\mathcal{S}
of the type (
IV
) having
Y(x)
as solution.
∎

For details regarding the computation of
Q_{1}
figuring in the previous proof we refer to Section
2.2
.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
\mathcal{H}=\{H_{1}(x,y_{1}),\ldots,H_{n}(x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})\}
be a simple algebraic subsystem of a differential system
\mathcal{S}
of type (
I
).
Then, for
c\in K
, every solution of the system
\mathcal{H}(c)
, defined by

\left\{\quad\bar{H}_{s}(x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{s})=H_{s}(x+c,y_{1},\ldots,y_{s})=0,\quad s\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\right.,

is an algebraic solution of
\mathcal{S}
.

Proof.

The system
\mathcal{H}(c)
is of the type (
IV
).
By Corollary
4.6
, its solutions are algebraic.
We need to show that the solutions of
\mathcal{H}(c)
are solutions of
\mathcal{S}
.
Let
P_{1}\in K(x)[y_{1}]
be the polynomial used for obtaining
\mathcal{H}
(see Lemma
4.5
).
We may assume that
P_{1}
is square-free.
Since
y_{1}
is the lowest ranked variable, the initial of
P_{1}
does not vanish on the solution set of
\mathcal{S}
.
Define
\bar{P}_{1}(x,y_{1})=P_{1}(x+c,y_{1})
where
c
is a new variable with
c^{\prime}=0
.
The reduction steps in the process of obtaining
\mathcal{H}
in a Thomas decomposition of
\mathcal{S}\cup\{P_{1}=0\}
are as follows:

(1)

Eliminate
y_{1}^{\prime}
in
G_{1}(y_{1},y_{1}^{\prime})
by
P_{1}^{\prime}
resulting into the polynomial
R_{1}(x,y_{1})
.

(2)

Compute the greatest common divisor of
R_{1}
and
P_{1}
, say
T_{1}
.

(3)

Possibly reduce
G_{2},\ldots,G_{n}
by
T_{1}
.

The chain rule implies that elimination of
y_{1}^{\prime}
in
G_{1}(y_{1},y_{1}^{\prime})
by
\bar{P}_{1}^{\prime}
yields
R_{1}(x+c,y_{1})
.
Similarly, as
x+c
can be considered as a new variable, the greatest common divisor of
R_{1}(x+c,y_{1})
and
\bar{P}_{1}
is
T_{1}(x+c,y_{1})
.
Hence, this process results in the simple system
\mathcal{H}(c)
which can be obtained from
\mathcal{H}
by substituting
x
by
x+c
.
∎

Theorem 4.9
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a differential system as in (
3.1
), of algebraic dimension one.
Then there exist a finite number of simple algebraic subsystems
\mathcal{H}_{k}
of
\mathcal{S}
such that any algebraic solution of
\mathcal{S}
is among the solutions of some
\mathcal{H}_{k}(c)
,
c\in K
, which are defined as in Lemma
4.8
.

Proof.

Let us first construct a set of simple algebraic subsystems
\mathcal{H}_{k}
of
\mathcal{S}
.
By Corollary
3.7
,
\mathcal{S}
can be decomposed into simple systems of the form (
I
),(
II
) and (
III
).
Systems of the second and third type are already algebraic systems and we add them.

Let us consider systems
\bar{\mathcal{S}}
of the type (
I
) with
t=1
.
By possibly further splitting systems according to the factorization of
G_{1}(y_{1},y_{1}^{\prime})
, we may additionally assume that
G_{1}
in
\bar{\mathcal{S}}
is irreducible.
The differential equation
G_{1}=0
might have a non-constant algebraic solution, given by its minimal polynomial
Q_{1}\in K[x,y_{1}]
, or does not have any.
In the first case, let
\mathcal{H}_{k}
be the simple algebraic subsystems obtained by a Thomas decomposition of
\bar{\mathcal{S}}\cup\{Q_{1}=0\}
.
In the latter case, we discard
\bar{\mathcal{S}}
.
Additionally, compute the constant solutions
y_{1}(x)=c
of
G_{1}
and let
\mathcal{H}_{k}
be the simple algebraic subsystems obtained by a Thomas decomposition of
\bar{\mathcal{S}}\cup\{y_{1}-c=0\}
.

Let
\bar{\mathcal{S}}
be of the type (
I
) with
t>1
.
For
1\leq s<t
, compute the constant solutions
y_{s}(x)=c_{s}
and plug them into
\bar{\mathcal{S}}
.
Now proceed with the differential equation
G_{t}(y_{t},y_{t}^{\prime})=0
as above in order to obtain a non-trivial minimal polynomial
Q_{t}(x,y_{t})
of a non-constant algebraic solution, if it exists, and the corresponding simple algebraic subsystems
\mathcal{H}_{k}
.

Let
Y(x)=(y_{1}(x),\ldots,y_{n}(x))
be an algebraic solution of
\mathcal{S}
and hence, of a subsystem
\bar{\mathcal{S}}
.
If all components of
Y(x)
are constant, then it is a constant solution of some system
\bar{\mathcal{S}}
and all of them are kept in
\mathcal{H}_{k}
.
So let
y_{t}(x)
be the first non-constant component.
Then, by Theorem
2.6
, there exists
c\in K
such that
Q_{t}(x+c,y_{t}(x))=0
for the minimal polynomial
Q_{t}
corresponding to
\bar{\mathcal{S}}
.
By Lemma
4.8
, the simple algebraic systems of
\bar{\mathcal{S}}\cup\{Q_{t}(x+c,y_{1})=0\}
are
\mathcal{H}_{k}(-c)
and exactly one of them has
Y(x)
as solution.
∎

Based on Corollary
4.6
, an algebraic solution
(y_{1}(x),\ldots,y_{n}(x))
of a given system
\mathcal{S}
as in (
I
) could be alternatively represented by a list of minimal polynomials
(Q_{1}(x,y_{1}),\ldots,Q_{n}(x,y_{n}))
, called a
minimal polynomial system
of
\mathcal{S}
.
The following proposition gives a degree bound on the minimal polynomials.

Proposition 4.10
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a differential system as in (
I
) with an algebraic solution
(y_{1}(x),\ldots,y_{n}(x))
.
Then there exists a minimal polynomial system
(Q_{1}(x,y_{1}),\ldots,Q_{n}(x,y_{n}))
of
\mathcal{S}
.
Moreover, every
Q_{s}\in K[x,y_{s}]
fulfills

(4.1)

\deg_{y_{s}}(Q_{s})\leq(\deg_{y_{1}}(G_{1})+\deg_{y_{1}^{\prime}}(G_{1}))\,\deg_{y_{2}}(G_{2})\cdots\deg_{y_{n}}(G_{n}).

Proof.

Without loss of generality we can assume that
t=1
in
\mathcal{S}
(cf. Remark
4.2
).
By the primitive element theorem, there exist minimal polynomials
Q_{1}\in K[x,y_{1}]
, …,
Q_{n}\in K[x,y_{n}]
of
y_{1}(x),\ldots,y_{n}(x)
, respectively.
Using
[
5
, Theorem 6]
, we have for
Q_{1}(x,y_{1})
the degree bound

\deg_{y_{1}}(Q_{1})\leq\deg_{y_{1}}(G_{1})+\deg_{y_{1}^{\prime}}(G_{1}).

Applying the multiplicative formula for the degree to the tower of field extensions, the statement follows.
∎

Let us note that, in contrast to the situation for simple subsystems of the type (
IV
), not every combination of roots of the minimal polynomial system indeed defines a solution as it can be seen in Example
5.3
.
