Beyond The Fermi’s Golden Rule: Discrete-Time Decoherence Of Quantum Mesoscopic Devices Due To Bandlimited Quantum Noise
Evgeny A. Polyakov

Abstract
We are in the midst of second quantum revolution where the mesoscopic quantum devices are actively employed for technological purposes. Despite this fact, the description of their real-time dynamics beyond the Fermi’s golden rule remains a formiddable theoretical problem. This is due to the rapid spread of entanglement within the degrees of freedom of the surrounding environment. This is accompanied with a quantum noise (QN) acting on the mesoscopic device. In this work we propose a possible way out: to exploit the fact that this QN is usually bandlimited. This is because its spectral density is often contained in peaks of localized modes and resonances, and may be constrained by bandgaps. Inspired by the Kotelnikov sampling theorem from the theory of classical bandlimited signals, we put forward and explore the idea that when the QN spectral density has effective bandwidth B B , the quantum noise becomes a discrete-time process, with an elementary time step τ ∝ B − 1 \tau\propto B^{-1} . After each time step τ \tau , one new QN degree of freedom (DoF) gets coupled to the device for the first time, and one new QN DoF get irreversibly decoupled. Only a bounded number of QN DoFs are significantly coupled at any time moment. We call these DoFs the Kotelnikov modes . As a result, the real-time dissipative quantum motion has a natural structure of a discrete-time matrix product state, with a bounded bond dimension. This yields a microscopically derived collision model. The temporal entanglement entropy appears to be bounded (area-law scaling) in the frame of Kotelnikov modes. The irreversibly decoupled modes can be traced out as soon as they occur during the real-time evolution. This leads to a novel bandlimited input-output formalism and to quantum jump Monte Carlo simulation techniques for real-time motion of open quantum systems. We illustrate this idea on a spin-boson model.



I
Introduction

The last decade has witnessed a rapid develoment of quantum technology

(
Dowling and Milburn 2003
)
. This technology is concerned with measurement,
control, communication, and computation based on quantum mesoscopic
degrees of freedom. The motivation behind this development is two-fold.
The first is miniaturization: the technological devices on a nanometer
scale must be designed according to the laws of quantum physics. The
second one is the promise to achieve a significant supremacy over
the technology based on the laws of classical physics
(
Dowling and Milburn 2003
)
.
The role of the quantum mesoscopic degrees of freedom can be played
by e.g. selected electron levels of trapped ions
(
Bruzewicz et al. 2019
)
,
semiconductor quantum dots
(
Scarlino et al. 2019
)
, superconducting
circuits
(
Kjaergaard et al. 2020
)
, spin of electrons in nitrogen-vacancy
centers
(
Childress and Hanson 2013
)
, Majorana zero modes
(
Smith et al. 2020
)
.
In any physical device, these dedicated degrees of freedom are always
coupled to an infinite number of other degrees of freedom. The latter
are called the environment. This way we arrive to the model of open
quantum system (OQS)
(
Breuer and Petruccione 2007
)
, which is thus a topic of
active research. Part of the environment can be used to control the
open quantum system (e.g. transmission line resonator in superconducting
circuits
(
Blais et al. 2004
;
Krantz et al. 2019
)
). The other part is responsible
for irreversible scattering of the quantum information (decoherence)

(
Breuer and Petruccione 2007
;
Krantz et al. 2019
)
. The latter is the major obstacle to
the creation of scalable quantum computers
(
Krantz et al. 2019
)
, which
stimulates the research on decoherence phenomena.

In the field of condensed matter the open quantum system is referred
to as the quatum impurity model (QIM), especially in the solid state
context. The role of QIM may be played by a single lattice site, localized
spin, or some other defect. QIM provides a model of solid state mesoscopic
quantum devices
(
Scarlino et al. 2019
)
. Besides that, at low temperatures
QIM develops complicated many-body correlations with the itinerant
electrons. The latter play the role of the environment, and QIM becomes
an open quantum system. These correlations significantly affect the
transport properties of materials
(
Hewson 1997
)
and properties
of quantum devices
(
Pustilnik and Glazman 2004
)
. Currently there is an active
investigation of non-equilibrium properties of QIM
(
Thoenniss et al. 2022
;
Sonner et al. 2021
)
,
in particular under periodical driving
(
Lerose et al. 2021a
)
.

Finally, besides the technological importance, the model of open quantum
system plays important role in the microscopic foundations of non-equilibrium
thermodynamics
(
Campisi and Hanggi 2011
;
Andrieux and Gaspard 2008
;
Talkner and Hanggi 2020
)
: how
the time arrow emerges from the reversible microscopic dynamics
(
Batalhao et al. 2015
;
Batalhao et al. 2018
)
;
statistics of work, heat, and entropy production
(
Talkner and Hanggi 2020
;
Andrieux and Gaspard 2008
;
Manzano et al. 2019
;
Elouard and Mohammady 2018
;
Deffner and Lutz 2011
;
Esposito et al. 2010
)
;
how the thermodynamics is related to quantum trajectories
(
Breuer 2003
;
Vu and Saito 2022
;
Hasegawa 2021
;
Horowitz and Parrondo 2013
)
;
the studies of quantum heat engines
(
Menczel et al. 2020
;
Popovic et al. 2021
;
Peterson et al. 2019
)
.

All of the above shows that at the moment the actual problem is to
study the non-stationary real-time motion of OQS/QIM, in order to
clarify open questions in the research fields mentioned above.

The description of real-time motion of OQS/QIM is clear and simple
when we can adopt the Fermi’s golden rule (the so-called Markovian
approximation
(
Breuer and Petruccione 2007
)
): OQS experiences sudden transitions
(quantum jumps)
\omega_{1}\to\omega_{2}
between the energy eigenstates

\left|\omega_{1}\right\rangle
and
\left|\omega_{2}\right\rangle

with a rate
\Gamma_{1\to 2}=\left|\left\langle\omega_{2}\left|\widehat{V}\right|\omega_{1}\right\rangle\right|^{2}J\left(\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}\right)2\pi/\hbar
.
Here
\widehat{V}
is coupling to the environment, and
J\left(\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}\right)

is the effective density of degrees of freedom (DoFs) of the environment
seen by OQS. The Markovian approximation is valid when the density
of states
J\left(\omega\right)
is smooth on the scales of OQS lineshapes.
The resulting stochastic dynamics is described by the so-called Lindblad
master equation
(
Breuer and Petruccione 2007
)
. Due to such a clear probabilistic
picture, this Markovian approximation is well understood nowadays.
It is a workhorse in describing the quantum optics experiments
(
Jacobs 2014
)

and the physical mechanisms of decoherence in qubits
(
Glazman and Catelani 2021
)
.
There exist efficient numerical simulation techniques
(
Breuer and Petruccione 2007
)
.
The Markovian approximation is also used in the simulations of quantum
heat engines
(
Campisi et al. 2015
)
and in foundations of non-equilibrium
quantum thermodynamics
(
Rivas 2020
)
. The Fermi’s golden rule
corresponds to a white (delta-correlated in time) quantum noise
(
Plenio and Knight 1998
;
Gardiner and Zoller 2004
)
.

The real-time motion of OQS becomes a formiddable theoretical problem
outside the applicability of the Fermi’s golden rule (the so-called
non-Markovian regime). This happens when the spectral density
J\left(\omega\right)

of the environment is not flat, but on the contrary, is structured
on the scales of OQS lineshapes. The structured
J\left(\omega\right)

naturally occurs in quantum devices which are based on solid state
impurities. For example, in the nitrogen-vacancy centers in the diamond

(
Alkauskas et al. 2014
)
, the impurity (defect) is coupled to the environment
of acoustic phonons. The corresponding
J\left(\omega\right)
develops
a number of peaks corresponding to vibrational resonances and localized
modes. Analogous situation occurs for impurities in semiconductors

(
Alkauskas et al. 2016
)
. The structured
J\left(\omega\right)
also
occurs in the photonic band gap materials
(
Yablonovich 1987
)

where
J\left(\omega\right)
is organized into allowed and forbidden
(
J\left(\omega\right)\approx 0
) bands. Finally, there is experimental
progress in creating artificial environments with a structured band-gapped

J\left(\omega\right)

(
Ferreira et al. 2021
)
. The motivation for
the latter is to introduce effects into the motion of OQS which are
beyond the Markovian approximation e.g. the information backflow
(
Bylicka et al. 2017
)
.
One hopes to exploit such non-Markovian effects as a resource for
the quantum technologies (the so-called reservoir enegineering)
(
Reich et al. 2015
)
.

The deep reason behind the difficulty of non-Markovian regime is the
apparent absence of the full-fledged quantum jumps. Indeed, the Fermi’s
golden rule provides us with quantum jumps which are completed and
irreversible elements of classical reality. As a result, they have
a probabilistic interpretation, which leads to a beautiful formal
picture (the divisibility of quantum Markovian motion and the concept
of dynamical semigroups)
(
Breuer and Petruccione 2007
)
, and to efficient Monte-Carlo
simulation methods
(
Breuer and Petruccione 2007
)
. On the contrary, beyound the
Markovian approximation, every excitation which is emitted by OQS
can always be reabsorbed back (return to OQS) after a time interval

t
with some amplitude
C_{\textrm{q}}\left(t\right)
. This amplitude
slowly decreases as a certain inverse power
C_{\textrm{q}}\left(t\right)\propto t^{-p}
,

p>0

(
Polyakov 2022
;
Polyakov and Rubtsov 2019
;
Polyakov and Rubtsov 2018a
)
. As a result,
one fails to introduce the concept of full-fledged non-Markovian quantum
jumps
(
Piilo et al. 2008
;
Piilo et al. 2009
)
: they appear to be never complete
in finite time. For any finite time, there is a non-nebligible amplitude
that the excitation will return. Therefore, the entanglement rapidly
propagates through the surrounding enviromnent, with a combinatorial
growth of the complexity of real-time quantum motion (the so-called
temporal entanglement barrier
(
Polyakov and Rubtsov 2019
;
Lerose et al. 2021b
;
Frias-Perez and Banuls 2022
;
Lerose et al. 2022
)
).

In literature there are many attemps to solve the puzzle of non-Markovian
real-time motion. Nowadays the most activity is concentrated on combining
the Feynman-Vernon influence functional
(
Feynman and Vernon 1963
)
with
the tensor network techniques
(
Strathearn et al. 2018
;
Lerose et al. 2022
;
Lerose et al. 2021b
;
Lerose et al. 2021a
;
Luchnikov et al. 2019
;
Somoza et al. 2019
;
Jorgensen and Pollock 2019
)
.
At the heart of these attemps is a bruteforce numerical compression
of many-body quantum states using smart linear algebra machinery and
the singular value decomposition (SVD) of high-rank tensors.

In this work we continue another line of research: our aim is to find
a clear visual physical principle, which could replace/extend the
Fermi’s golden rule in the non-Markovian regime. We started in
(
Polyakov and Rubtsov 2018a
)
,
where we have employed the time-domain wavefunction to identify how
OQS/QIM forgets about the previously emitted quanta. We have found
that all the emitted spectral content is forgotten except progressively
small vicinities of frequencies
\omega_{k}
where
J\left(\omega_{k}\right)

has sharp features (e.g. band edges, sharp peaks). In
(
Polyakov and Rubtsov 2019
)

we have implemented this approach in a numerical method and conjectured
that the time domain should be employed in a properly formulated real-time
renormalization group (RTRG). Such RTRG was proposed in
(
Polyakov 2020
)

which was recently published as
(
Polyakov 2022
)
. The present
paper further develops this approach by taking into account the spectral
properties of the quantum noise.

This work is structured as follows. In sec.
II
we
present the model of open quantum system and state the problem considered.
Then in sec.
III
we introduce the
concept of bandlimited quantum noise and bear ananlogies with the
classical Kotelnikov sampling. In sec.
IV

we introduce the quantum analog of Kolelnikov sampling in the context
of interaction quench. The resulting discrete-time model, simulation
techniques, and physical picture are described. In sec.
V

we derive the discrete-time model and provide a numerical recipe to
find its coupling constants. We conclude in
VI
.
There are two appendices with some details.

II
The model

In this work we study the real time motion of a small open quantum
system with a Hamiltonian
\widehat{H}_{\textrm{s}}
. It is surrounded
by a quantum environment
\widehat{H}_{\textrm{b}}
. We assume there
is some local site in the environment which is represented by annihilation

\widehat{a}_{0}
and creation
\widehat{a}_{0}^{\dagger}
operators,

\left[\widehat{a}_{0},\widehat{a}_{0}^{\dagger}\right]_{\xi}=1
,
with
\xi=\pm 1
depending on the statistics of the environment’s
excitations (fermions or bosons). OQS is coupled to this site via
some operators
\widehat{V}
,
\widehat{V}^{\dagger}
. The joint
Hamiltonian for this model is

\widehat{H}=\widehat{H}_{\textrm{s}}+\widehat{V}^{\dagger}\widehat{a}_{0}+\widehat{V}\widehat{a}_{0}^{\dagger}+\widehat{H}_{\textrm{b}}.

(1)

Here for simplicity we assume the bilinear coupling between the OQS
operators
\widehat{V},\,\,\widehat{V}^{\dagger}
and between the
environment’s
\widehat{a}_{0}
and
\widehat{a}_{0}^{\dagger}
.

We study the joint quantum state
\left|\Psi\left(t\right)\right\rangle

of OQS and environment. The state
\left|\Psi\left(t\right)\right\rangle

may appear as a result of the interaction quench. That is, we assume
that initially the environment is in its ground state
\left|0\right\rangle_{\textrm{b}}
,
and OQS is in some state
\left|\psi\right\rangle_{\textrm{s}}
:

\left|\Psi\left(0\right)\right\rangle=\left|\psi\right\rangle_{\textrm{s}}\otimes\left|0\right\rangle_{\textrm{b}}
.
Then at
t=0
we couple OQS to the environment and track the resulting
evolution.

In this work we consider a specific form of the environment, a semi-infinite
chain of sites

\widehat{H}_{\textrm{b}}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left\{\varepsilon_{j}\widehat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}\widehat{a}_{j}+h_{j}\widehat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}\widehat{a}_{j+1}+h_{j}\widehat{a}_{j+1}^{\dagger}\widehat{a}_{j}\right\},

(2)

with
\left[\widehat{a}_{k},\widehat{a}_{l}^{\dagger}\right]_{\xi}=\delta_{kl}
.
Here the coupling site operators
\widehat{a}_{0}
and
\widehat{a}_{0}^{\dagger}

appear as the first sites of the chain. This chain representation
is general: given a spectral density
J\left(\omega\right)
, there
is an algorithm how to compute
\varepsilon_{j},h_{j}
so that the
resulting chain reproduces
J\left(\omega\right)

(
Tamascelli et al. 2019
;
Prior et al. 2010
;
Chin et al. 2010
;
Tamascelli et al. 2015
;
Kohn et al. 2018
)

as seen by OQS.

III
Bandlimited quantum noise.
Analogy to the classical Kotelnikov sampling theorem

A good physical principle requires good assumpitions. In this work
we assume that the spectral density
J\left(\omega\right)
of the
environment is effectively bounded to some frequency range
\left[0,B\right]
,
see Fig.
1
.

Figure 1:
In this work we consider the quantum environment
whose spectral density
J\left(\omega\right)
is effectively bounded
to some frequency range
\left[0,B\right]
, but otherwise can be
arbitrary complex.

In most cases (e.g., environment with non-interacting quasiparticles)
this means that the quantum field emitted into the environment also
has a bandlimited spectrum. From the discipline of classical signal
processing we know the Kotelnikov sampling theorem
(
Kotelnikov 1933
)
,
which is the following.

Figure 2:
The classical Kotelnikov sampling
theorem states that a) if a signal
z\left(t\right)
has a spectral
density
J_{\textrm{cl}}\left(\omega\right)
which is limited to
a frequency range
\left[-B,B\right]
, then b) the signal
z\left(t\right)

is completely determined (without loss of information) by a sequence
of its values
\ldots,\,z\left(t_{-1}\right),z\left(t_{0}\right),z\left(t_{1}\right),\ldots z\left(t_{k}\right)\ldots
,
at discrete times moments which are spaced
\tau=1/2B
seconds apart.

Consider a random classical signal
z\left(t\right)
. For each time
moment
t
the value of the signal
z\left(t\right)
is a complex
number. Its time correlator is

C_{\textrm{cl}}\left(t\right)=\overline{z^{*}\left(t\right)z\left(0\right)},

(3)

where the averaging
\overline{\left[\cdot\right]}
is over the signal
samples. The spectral density
J_{\textrm{cl}}\left(\omega\right)

of the signal is provided by the causal Fourier transform of the correlator

J_{\textrm{cl}}\left(\omega\right)=\textrm{Re}\intop_{0}^{+\infty}dt\,C_{\textrm{cl}}\left(t\right)e^{i\omega t-0t}.

(4)

Suppose that the signal turns out to have a finite band halfwidth

B
, see Fig.
2
, a). Then the signal
can be faithfully (without loss of information) represented by its
values at discrete time moments
t_{k}=k\tau
,
\tau=1/2B
, with
integer
k
, Fig.
2
b):

z\left(t\right)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}z\left(t_{k}\right)\frac{\sin\left(2\pi B\left(t-t_{k}\right)\right)}{2\pi B\left(t-t_{k}\right)}.

(5)

The message of the Kotelnikov sampling is that in each finite time
interval
T
there is only a finite number
m\left(t\right)\propto TB

of significant DoFs of the signal, see
A
.
These DoFs are given by the sinc basis functions,
\kappa_{k}\left(t\right)=\sin\left(2\pi B\left(t-t_{k}\right)\right)/2\pi B\left(t-t_{k}\right)
.
Each DoF
\kappa_{k}
is in one-to-one correspondence with its time
moment
t_{k}=k\tau
.

Now we return to the quantum case. Analogously to the classical correlator
eq. (
3
), we introduce the correlator
of the zero-point quantum noise
(
Polyakov 2022
;
Polyakov and Rubtsov 2019
;
Polyakov and Rubtsov 2018b
)
,

C_{\textrm{q}}\left(t\right)=\prescript{}{\textrm{b}}{\left\langle 0\right|}\widehat{a}_{0}\left(t\right)\widehat{a}_{0}^{\dagger}\left(0\right)\left|0\right\rangle_{\textrm{b}}

(6)

for the vacuum state
\left|0\right\rangle_{\textrm{b}}
of environment.
Here we employ the partial interaction picture with respect to the
free motion of environment,
\widehat{a}_{0}\left(t\right)=\exp\left(it\widehat{H}_{\textrm{b}}\right)\widehat{a}_{0}\exp\left(-it\widehat{H}_{\textrm{b}}\right)
.

Analogously to the classical spectral density eq. (
4
),
the spectral density of the zero-point quantum noise (which coincides
with the spectral density of the environment)
J\left(\omega\right)

is also a Fourier transform
(
Polyakov 2022
;
Polyakov and Rubtsov 2019
)

J\left(\omega\right)=\textrm{Re}\intop_{0}^{+\infty}dt\,C_{\textrm{q}}\left(t;0\right)e^{i\omega t-0t}.

(7)

If
J\left(\omega\right)
is bandlimited, we expect that, analogously
to the classical case, the quantum noise should be faithfully represented
by a discrete time process.

IV
Kotelnikov sampling of bandlimited
quantum noise: the resulting model

In this section we present the resulting model of quantum dissipative
motion under bandlimited quantum noise. It is ready for use in calculations
of specific problems. We also discuss some physical implications.
In the following sections we justify this model and present the algorithm
how to calculate the numerical values of the model constants.

IV.1
Forward quantum Kotelnikov sampling

Notice that the statement of the real-time problem is slightly different
from that of the original classical Kotelnikov theorem eq. (
5
):
we have the initial value problem instead of the signal on the whole
time axis. Therefore, we need a causal variant of the Kotelnikov theorem,
which we call the
forward Kotelnikov sampling
, see appendix

A
. According to the latter,
we expect that the DoFs of the environment
\widehat{a}_{0},\widehat{a}_{1},\ldots

can be arranged in a sequence,
\kappa_{k}^{\textrm{in}},

k=1,2,3\ldots

by some change of frame (Bogoliubov transform)
W
,

\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\widehat{\kappa}_{1}^{\textrm{in}}\\
\widehat{\kappa}_{2}^{\textrm{in}}\\
\vdots\end{array}\right]=W\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\widehat{a}_{0}\\
\widehat{a}_{1}\\
\vdots\end{array}\right]

(8)

for some unitary matrix
W
. This sequence can be chosen so that

\kappa_{k}^{\textrm{in}}
couple to OQS sequentially, one after
the other, at discrete time moments
t_{k}^{\textrm{in}}.
Let us
denote
m_{\textrm{in}}\left(t\right)
the number of modes which
are already coupled to OQS at the time moment
t
. The function
m_{\textrm{in}}\left(t\right)

is piecewise constant and increases by jumps at the time moments
t_{k}^{\textrm{in}}

when a new mode is coupled. For convenience of notation we define

m_{\textrm{in}}\left(t\right)
to be left-continuous:
m_{\textrm{in}}\left(t_{k}^{\textrm{in}}+0\right)=m_{\textrm{in}}\left(t_{k}^{\textrm{in}}\right)=m_{\textrm{in}}\left(t_{k}^{\textrm{in}}-0\right)+1
.
Let us discuss the expected rate of such coupling events. The interaction
quench at
t=0
(i.e. sudden switching-on of the coupling
\widehat{V}^{\dagger}\widehat{a}_{0}+\widehat{V}\widehat{a}_{0}^{\dagger}
)
is a broadband event. Therefore, we expect that near
t=0
the modes

\kappa_{k}^{\textrm{in}}
will appear at a high rate. But fast enough
the high-frequency part of the quench spectrum will become decoupled

(
Polyakov and Rubtsov 2018a
;
Polyakov 2022
)
, and we reach the Kotelnikov asymptotic
rate
t_{k+1}^{\textrm{in}}-t_{k}^{\textrm{in}}\propto\tau=1/B
,
see Fig.
3
.

IV.2
Backward quantum Kotelnikov
sampling

Observe that our arrangement
\kappa_{1}^{\textrm{in}},\kappa_{2}^{\textrm{in}},\ldots

of the DoFs behaves asymmetrically in time. Since the quantum mechanics
has the time-reversal symmetry, we expect that the time-reversed process
is also happening: at time intervals
\propto 1/B
some modes
\kappa_{l}^{\textrm{out}}

get irreversibly decoupled from OQS. We call them the
outgoing
Kotelnikov modes
. Here we should distinguish between the two cases:
(i) the modes which never become coupled to OQS, and (ii) the modes
which couple to OQS but after some time irreversibly decouple. We
are not interested in (i): we discard them from our model. However
we need to track the evolution of (ii). This consideration determines
the way we find
\kappa_{l}^{\textrm{out}}
: the mode
\kappa_{l}^{\textrm{out}}

which decouples at time moment
t_{l}^{\textrm{out}}
should be a
linear combination of
\kappa_{1}^{\textrm{in}}\ldots\kappa_{m\left(t_{l}^{\textrm{out}}\right)}^{\textrm{in}}
.
In other words, the newly decoupled mode should emerge in the subspace
of modes that are coupled to OQS by the time
t_{l}^{\textrm{out}}
.
This is because the modes which are not incduled in this linear combination
have not yet been coupled to OQS.

This picture leads to the following iterative construction of the
stream of outgoing modes. The first outgoing mode
\kappa_{1}^{\textrm{out}}

is found by switching to the frame where it appears as an independent
DoF,

\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\kappa_{1}^{\textrm{out}\dagger}\\
\widehat{\kappa}_{1}^{\textrm{rel}\dagger}\\
\vdots\\
\widehat{\kappa}_{m\left(t_{1}^{\textrm{out}}\right)-1}^{\textrm{rel}\dagger}\end{array}\right]=U_{1}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\widehat{\kappa}_{1}^{\textrm{in}\dagger}\\
\vdots\\
\kappa_{m\left(t_{1}^{\textrm{out}}\right)}^{\textrm{in}\dagger}\end{array}\right]

(12)

where
U_{1}
is
m_{\textrm{in}}\left(t_{1}^{\textrm{out}}\right)\times m_{\textrm{in}}\left(t_{1}^{\textrm{out}}\right)

unitary matrix. Here
m_{\textrm{in}}\left(t_{1}^{\textrm{out}}\right)

is the number of modes which have coupled before the time of the first
decoupling
t_{1}^{\textrm{out}}
. We place the new outgoing mode
in the first component of the vector on the left hand side. Observe
that the remaining
m_{\textrm{in}}\left(t_{1}^{\textrm{out}}\right)-1

modes can also be rotated somehow. We call them the
relevant
modes
since they are coupled to OQS thus are important for the future
evolution. We mark them with a superscript “rel”. The subsequent
outgoing modes are found recurrently. Namely, by the time moment
t_{k}^{\textrm{out}}

we have
k-1
outgoing modes
\kappa_{1}^{\textrm{out}}\ldots\kappa_{k-1}^{\textrm{out}}

and
r\left(t_{k}^{\textrm{out}}\right)
relevant modes
\widehat{\kappa}_{1}^{\textrm{rel}}\ldots\widehat{\kappa}_{r\left(t_{k}^{\textrm{out}}\right)}^{\textrm{rel}}
,
which obviously satisfy

r\left(t_{k}^{\textrm{out}}\right)+k-1=m_{\textrm{in}}\left(t_{k}^{\textrm{out}}\right).

(13)

Then the newly formed outgoing mode is a linear combination of relevant
modes:

\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\widehat{\kappa}_{k}^{\textrm{out}\dagger}\\
\widehat{\kappa}_{1}^{\textrm{rel}\prime\dagger}\\
\vdots\\
\widehat{\kappa}_{r\left(t_{k}^{\textrm{out}}\right)-1}^{\textrm{rel}\prime\dagger}\end{array}\right]=U_{k}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\widehat{\kappa}_{1}^{\textrm{rel}\dagger}\\
\vdots\\
\widehat{\kappa}_{r\left(t_{k}^{\textrm{out}}\right)}^{\textrm{rel}\dagger}\end{array}\right],

(14)

were
U_{k}
is
r\left(t_{k}^{\textrm{out}}\right)\times r\left(t_{k}^{\textrm{out}}\right)

unitary matrix. We get a picture in which the coupling and decoupling
events are interspersed. In the course of a coupling event at
t=t_{k}^{\textrm{in}}
,
the number of relevant modes increases by one:
r\left(t_{k}^{\textrm{out}}+0\right)=r\left(t_{k}^{\textrm{out}}\right)=r\left(t_{k}^{\textrm{out}}-0\right)+1
,
with the new mode
\widehat{\kappa}_{r\left(t_{k}^{\textrm{out}}\right)+1}^{\textrm{rel}}\equiv\kappa_{m\left(t_{1}^{\textrm{out}}\right)}^{\textrm{in}}
.
In the course of decoupling event
t=t_{k}^{\textrm{out}}
, we rotate
the frame, eq. (
14
).

An important feature of this picture is that all these events have
a finite duration
\propto\tau=1/B
. Therefore, we can do all the
rotations smoothly in conjunction with the Hamiltonian evolution.
Namely, let us introduce a time interval
\left[t_{k}^{*},t_{k}^{\textrm{out}}\right]
,
where
t_{k}^{*}
is the time of the last event (whatever it is)
before
t_{k}^{\textrm{out}}
. All the time axis
\left[0,+\infty\right)

becomes covered with such intervals: every time moment
t
belongs
to some
\left[t_{k}^{*},t_{k}^{\textrm{out}}\right]
. We can introduce
the generator
D\left(t\right)
of
U_{k}=\exp\left(-i\left(t_{k}^{\textrm{out}}-t_{k}^{*}\right)D\left(t\right)\right)
,
where
U_{k}
is from eq. (
14
).
Here for convenience of notation we define
D\left(t\right)
as a
piecewise constant function on intervals
\left[t_{k}^{*},t_{k}^{\textrm{out}}\right]
.
Then the joint state
\left|\Psi\left(t\right)\right\rangle
evolves
on
\left[t_{k}^{*},t_{k}^{\textrm{out}}\right]
according to the
Schrodinger equation

i\partial_{t}\left|\Psi\left(t\right)\right\rangle=\widehat{H}_{\textrm{eff}}\left(t\right)\left|\Psi\left(t\right)\right\rangle,

(15)

with the effective Hamiltonian in the moving frame

\widehat{H}_{\textrm{eff}}\left(t\right)=\widehat{H}_{\textrm{s}}+\sum_{l=1}^{r\left(t\right)}\left(\widehat{V}^{\dagger}\chi_{l}^{\textrm{rel}}\left(t\right)\widehat{\kappa}_{l}^{\textrm{rel}}+\widehat{V}\chi_{l}^{\textrm{rel}*}\left(t\right)\widehat{\kappa}_{l}^{\textrm{rel}\dagger}\right)\\
-\sum_{kl=1}^{r\left(t\right)}D_{lk}\left(t\right)\widehat{\kappa}_{k}^{\textrm{rel}\dagger}\widehat{\kappa}_{l}^{\textrm{rel}}.

(16)

Here the first line describes the interaction of OQS with
r
relevant
modes, and the coupling constants

\chi_{l}^{\textrm{rel}}\left(t\right)=\left[\widehat{a}_{0}\left(t\right),\widehat{\kappa}_{l}^{\textrm{rel}\dagger}\right]_{\xi}

(17)

represent the amplitude to absorb a quantum from the relevant mode

\kappa_{l}^{\textrm{rel}}
. The second line of eq. (
16
)
gradually exercises the frame rotation eq. (
14
).

The equation eq. (
16

applications. The constants of the model,
\chi_{l}^{\textrm{rel}}\left(t\right)

and
D_{kl}\left(t\right)

IV.3
Tracing out the irreversibly decoupled modes

The merit of the presented model is that the irreversibly decoupled

motion:

\widehat{\rho}_{\textrm{rel}}\left(t\right)=\textrm{Tr}_{\kappa_{1}^{\textrm{out}}\ldots\kappa_{k}^{\textrm{out}}}\left\{\left|\Psi\left(t\right)\right\rangle\left\langle\Psi\left(t\right)\right|\right\},

\partial_{t}\widehat{\rho}_{\textrm{rel}}\left(t\right)=-i\left[\widehat{\rho}_{\textrm{rel}}\left(t\right),\widehat{H}_{\textrm{eff}}\left(t\right)\right].

(19)

IV.5
Monte Carlo sampling of quantum
jumps

There is alternative to the partial trace in eq. (
18
).
When a new outgoing mode
\kappa_{k}^{\textrm{out}}
is formed at

t=t_{k}^{\textrm{out}}
, we can apply the von Neumann measurement
model
(
Neumann 1996
)
: the mode
\kappa_{k}^{\textrm{out}}

is an auxiliary probe which was previously coupled to OQS. It is entangled
with OQS according to the Schmidt decomposition

\left|\Psi\left(t_{k}^{\textrm{out}}\right)\right\rangle\\
=\sum_{p}c_{p}\left(k\right)\left|\Psi_{\textrm{collapsed}}^{\left(p\right)}\left(t_{k}^{\textrm{out}}\right)\right\rangle_{\textrm{rel}}\otimes\left|\textrm{jump}^{\left(p\right)}\left(t_{k}^{\textrm{out}}\right)\right\rangle_{\kappa_{k}^{\textrm{out}}},

(20)

were we consider a biparticle system: one part labeled “rel” is
the open system with relevant modes, and the second part labeled
\kappa_{k}^{\textrm{out}}

is the newly formed outgoing mode. Since the probe
\kappa_{k}^{\textrm{out}}

is irreversibly decoupled, the entanglement structure (
20
)
is invariant under the future evolution. Then, according to the von
Neumann measurement model, we can interpret eq. (
20
)
as a
p
th quantum jump at time
t=t_{k}^{\textrm{out}}
,

\left|\Psi\left(t_{k}^{\textrm{out}}\right)\right\rangle\to\left|\Psi_{\textrm{collapsed}}^{\left(p\right)}\left(t_{k}^{\textrm{out}}\right)\right\rangle_{\textrm{rel}},

(21)

with a probability
P_{k}\left(p\right)=\left|c_{p}\left(k\right)\right|^{2}
.
These jumps are irreversible and non-Markovian since they happen during
a finite time interval
t_{k}^{\textrm{out}}-t_{k}^{\textrm{*}}\propto 1/B
.

At a time
t
there are
m_{\textrm{out}}\left(t\right)
decoupled
modes. Each of them is accompanied with a quantum jump, which are
obtained by a recurrent application of the von Neumann rule eq. (
20
)-(
21
).
Therefore, before the time
t
there are
m_{\textrm{out}}\left(t\right)

jumps. They are characterized by a history
h
of choices,
h=\left(p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{k}\right)=\left(p_{k}\right)_{k:\,t_{k}^{\textrm{out}}\leq t}

, which occurs with the probability
P\left(p_{1}p_{2}\ldots p_{k}\right)=\prod_{k:\,t_{k}^{\textrm{out}}\leq t}\left|c_{p_{k}}\left(k\right)\right|^{2}
.
Then the reduced density matrix eq. (
18
)
is computed as an average over the histories
h
of jumps before

t
,

\widehat{\rho}_{\textrm{rel}}\left(t\right)=\\
\overline{\left[\left|\Psi_{\textrm{collapsed}}^{\left(h\right)}\left(t\right)\right\rangle_{\textrm{rel}}\,\,\,\prescript{}{\textrm{rel}}{\left\langle\Psi_{\textrm{collapsed}}^{\left(h\right)}\left(t\right)\right|}\right]}_{\textrm{over all }h}.

(22)

Here by
\left|\Psi_{\textrm{collapsed}}^{\left(h\right)}\left(t\right)\right\rangle_{\textrm{rel}}

we denote a particular trajectory of jumps,

\left|\Psi\left(t_{1}^{\textrm{out}}\right)\right\rangle\to\left|\Psi_{\textrm{collapsed}}^{\left(p_{1}\right)}\left(t_{1}^{\textrm{out}}\right)\right\rangle_{\textrm{rel}},\\
\left|\Psi_{\textrm{collapsed}}^{\left(p_{1}\right)}\left(t_{2}^{\textrm{out}}\right)\right\rangle_{\textrm{rel}}\to\left|\Psi_{\textrm{collapsed}}^{\left(p_{1}p_{2}\right)}\left(t_{2}^{\textrm{out}}\right)\right\rangle_{\textrm{rel}},\\
\left|\Psi_{\textrm{collapsed}}^{\left(p_{1}p_{2}\right)}\left(t_{3}^{\textrm{out}}\right)\right\rangle_{\textrm{rel}}\to\left|\Psi_{\textrm{collapsed}}^{\left(p_{1}p_{2}p_{3}\right)}\left(t_{3}^{\textrm{out}}\right)\right\rangle_{\textrm{rel}},\\
\textrm{etc}.

(23)

This procedure results in a novel Monte Carlo simulation technique
of real-time motion. It has a benefit with respect to the density
matrix formulation (
18
)-(
19
)
since the density matrix has a quadratically higher dimension than
that of wavefunction,
\dim\widehat{\rho}_{\textrm{rel}}\left(t\right)=\left(\dim\left|\Psi_{\textrm{collapsed}}^{\left(h\right)}\left(t\right)\right\rangle_{\textrm{rel}}\right)^{2}
.

with this technique. The averaging was done over

randomly

IV.7
Geometric arrow of time

Suppose we know that the environment is in some global equilibrium
state
\textrm{$\widehat{\rho}$}_{\textrm{b}}^{\textrm{eq}}
,
and that OQS was coupled to the environment at some time moment in
the past. Then our streams of incoming and outgoing Kotelnikov modes
define an arrow of time. Indeed, at any time moment
t
the future
incoming modes
\kappa_{k}^{\textrm{in}},

t_{k}^{\textrm{in}}>t
,
did not have an opportunity to significantly interact with OQS. Therefore,
the partial density matrix of the incoming stream should coincide
with the equilibrium one at all times:

\textrm{$\widehat{\rho}$}_{\textrm{in}}\left(t\right)\equiv\textrm{Tr}_{\textrm{all }\kappa_{k}^{\textrm{in}}:\,t_{k}^{\textrm{in}}\leq t}\textrm{Tr}_{\textrm{s}}\left\{\textrm{$\widehat{\rho}$}\left(t\right)\right\}\\
=\textrm{Tr}_{\textrm{all }\kappa_{k}^{\textrm{in}}:\,t_{k}^{\textrm{in}}\leq t}\left\{\textrm{$\widehat{\rho}$}_{\textrm{b}}^{\textrm{eq}}\right\}\equiv\textrm{$\widehat{\rho}$}_{\textrm{in}}^{\textrm{eq}}\left(t\right),

(24)

where
\textrm{$\widehat{\rho}$}\left(t\right)
is the
joint density of OQS and environment due to the coupling quench. At
the same time, the outgoing stream carries away the disturbance due
to the coupling quench, therefore its partical density matrix in general
differs from the equilibrium one:

\textrm{$\widehat{\rho}$}_{\textrm{out}}\left(t\right)\equiv\textrm{Tr}_{\textrm{all}\kappa_{k}^{\textrm{out}}:\,t_{k}^{\textrm{out}}\geq t}\textrm{Tr}_{\textrm{s}}\left\{\textrm{$\widehat{\rho}$}\left(t\right)\right\}\\
\neq\textrm{Tr}_{\textrm{all}\kappa_{k}^{\textrm{out}}:\,t_{k}^{\textrm{out}}\geq t}\left\{\textrm{$\widehat{\rho}$}_{\textrm{b}}^{\textrm{eq}}\right\}\equiv\textrm{$\widehat{\rho}$}_{\textrm{out}}^{\textrm{eq}}\left(t\right).

(25)

We show in section
V.3.3
that under
the time reversal
\textrm{$\widehat{\rho}$}_{\textrm{in}}\left(t\right)

and
\textrm{$\widehat{\rho}$}_{\textrm{out}}\left(t\right)

are swapped. However, in the latter case
\textrm{$\widehat{\rho}$}_{\textrm{in}}\left(t\right)\neq\textrm{$\widehat{\rho}$}_{\textrm{in}}^{\textrm{eq}}
,
so that we detect the “wrong” time direction.

We call this arrow of time geometric because it is revealed by a specific
arrangement of the microscopic degrees of freedom. By itself, this
arrangement is time-symmetric (
\kappa_{k}^{\textrm{out}}
and
\kappa_{k}^{\textrm{in}}

swap under the time reversal). However the boundary condition (global
equilibrium of uncoupled environment at
t=-\infty
) is asymmetric,
which leads to the emergence of the preferred time direction.

IV.8
Entropy of the ensemble of classical records

As we have discussed above, the frame of outgoing modes
\kappa_{k}^{\textrm{out}}

reveals the emerging asymptotically invariant entanglement structure:

\left|\Psi\left(t\right)\right\rangle=\sum_{p_{1}\ldots p_{k}}c_{p_{1}}\left(1\right)\ldots c_{p_{k}}\left(k\right)\left|\Psi_{\textrm{collapsed}}^{\left(p_{1}\ldots p_{k}\right)}\left(t\right)\right\rangle_{\textrm{rel}}\\
\otimes\left|\textrm{jump}^{\left(p_{1}\right)}\left(t_{1}^{\textrm{out}}\right)\right\rangle_{\kappa_{1}^{\textrm{out}}}\ldots\otimes\left|\textrm{jump}^{\left(p_{k}\right)}\left(t_{k}^{\textrm{out}}\right)\right\rangle_{\kappa_{k}^{\textrm{out}}}

(26)

for
t_{k}^{\textrm{out}}\leq t\leq t_{k+1}^{\textrm{out}}
. This
structure encodes the classical ensemble of jump histories
\left(p_{1}\ldots p_{k}\right)

with probabilities

P\left(p_{1}p_{2}\ldots p_{k}\right)=\prod_{k:\,t_{k}^{\textrm{out}}\leq t}\left|c_{p_{k}}\left(k\right)\right|^{2}.

(27)

The entropy of such ensemble is

S_{\textrm{jump}}\left(\Psi\left(t\right)\right)=-\sum_{p_{1}\ldots p_{k}}P\left(p_{1}\ldots p_{k}\right)\ln P\left(p_{1}\ldots p_{k}\right).

(28)

Informally, we can say that for a given pure state
\left|\Psi\left(t\right)\right\rangle
,
the entropy
S_{\textrm{jump}}\left(\Psi\left(t\right)\right)
quantifies
how much classical reality was generated in this pure state due to
non-Markovian decoherence.

V
Variational principle and numerical
scheme

In this section we provide a recipe how to compute the constants
\chi_{l}^{\textrm{rel}}\left(t\right)

and
D_{lk}\left(t\right)
of the model eq. (
15
)-(
16
).
First we introduce a variational principle for the real time motion
which yields the computational scheme which in its turn is presented
at the end of this section.

We begin by noting that the classical Kotelnikov theorem eq. (
5
)
can be reformulated in a statistical form: on every finite time interval

T
there is a finite number
m\left(t\right)\propto BT
of independent
statistically significant DoFs for a signal with effective bandwidth

B
. See appendix
A
. Also,
this statistical reformulation yields the desired causal variant of
the Kotelnikov theorem.

Thererore, we proceed by constructing measures of statistical significance
of environment DoFs on time intervals.

V.1
Measure of retarded statistical significance

Returning to the coupling quench of OQS, we face the problem how to
identify the statistically significant DoFs of environment on a time
interval
\left[0,t\right]
. We need to identify these DoFs
before

sovling the many body problem eq.(
1
). The simplest
trick we can do is to replace OQS in eq. (
1
) with
a classical white noise
\xi\left(t\right)
:

\widehat{H}\to\widehat{H}_{\xi+}\left(t\right)=\xi\left(t\right)^{*}\widehat{a}_{0}\left(t\right)+\xi\left(t\right)\widehat{a}_{0}^{\dagger}\left(t\right)

(29)

(here we consider a specific case of bosonic environment),
\overline{\xi^{*}\left(t\right)\xi\left(t^{\prime}\right)}=\delta\left(t-t^{\prime}\right)
.
Since the white noise
\xi\left(t\right)
has infinite bandwidth
and uniform spectral density, it efficiently lights up all the available
DoFs of the environment. The intensity of such interaction is modulated
with
J\left(\omega\right)
. Then, given a trial environment DoF

\phi
, with the corresponding creation operator
\widehat{\phi}^{\dagger}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\left\langle i\left|\phi\right.\right\rangle\widehat{a}_{i}^{\dagger}
,
we can estimate its statistical significance in the following way.
We compute its average occupation
n_{+}\left(t;\phi\right)
by averaging
over the ensemble of noisy evolutions of the environment
\left|\Psi_{\xi+}\left(t\right)\right\rangle_{\textrm{b}}

under the Hamiltonian eq. (
29
),

n_{+}\left(t;\phi\right)=\left\langle\phi\right|\widehat{\rho}_{+}\left(t\right)\left|\phi\right\rangle,

(30)

where the one-body reduced density matrix
\widehat{\rho}_{+}\left(t\right)

is
\left\langle i\right|\widehat{\rho}_{+}\left(t\right)\left|j\right\rangle=\overline{\left\langle\Psi_{\xi+}\left(t\right)\right|\widehat{a}_{i}^{\dagger}\widehat{a}_{j}\left|\Psi_{\xi+}\left(t\right)\right\rangle}
.
Here for each noise realization we solve the Schrodinger equation

i\partial_{t}\left|\Psi_{\xi+}\left(t\right)\right\rangle_{\textrm{b}}=\widehat{H}_{\xi+}\left(t\right)\left|\Psi_{\xi+}\left(t\right)\right\rangle_{\textrm{b}}

with the vacuum initial condition
\left|\Psi_{\xi+}\left(0\right)\right\rangle_{\textrm{b}}=\left|0\right\rangle_{\textrm{b}}
.
If
n_{+}\left(t;\phi\right)
is negligible, then no other open system
can excite this DoF, and
\phi
can be safely discarded. Indeed,
unlike white noise, OQS interacts only with those DoFs which are close
to its transition frequencies. Therefore, OQS is a less efficient
illuminator than the white noise.

We call
n_{+}\left(t;\phi\right)
the measure of retarded statistical
significance, i.e. the statistical significance of
\phi
for the
past quantum motion up to time
t
. The modes
\phi
with negligible

n_{+}\left(t;\phi\right)
can be discarded from the Hamiltonian

\widehat{H}_{\textrm{b}}
when solving the Schrodinger equation
on
\left[0,t\right]
.

In appendix
B
we show that
\widehat{\rho}_{+}\left(t\right)

has a simple closed solution

\left\langle i\right|\widehat{\rho}_{+}\left(t\right)\left|j\right\rangle=\intop_{0}^{t}d\tau\phi_{i}^{*}\left(\tau\right)\phi_{j}\left(\tau\right),

(31)

where
\phi_{j}\left(\tau\right)
is a free one-particle wavepacket
in the environment, which is initially located on the site
0
of
the environment (which is coupled to OQS),
\phi_{j}\left(0\right)=\delta_{j0}
,
and at later times its free motion under
\widehat{H}_{\textrm{b}}

is given by a first-quantized Schrodinger equation

\partial_{\tau}\phi_{j}\left(\tau\right)=i\varepsilon_{j}\phi_{j}\left(\tau\right)+ih_{j}\phi_{j+1}\left(\tau\right)+ih_{j-1}\phi_{j-1}\left(\tau\right).

(32)

These equations can be easily solved on a computer.

For a given arbitrary trial mode
\phi
, the measure
n_{+}\left(t;\phi\right)

is a monotonically increasing function of
t
, which follows from
eq. (
31
). Therefore, given a significance
treshold
r_{\textrm{cut}}
, there is a unique time moment
t_{\phi}^{\textrm{in}}\in\left[0,\infty\right]

such that before
t_{\phi}^{\textrm{in}}
the mode
\phi
is effectively
decoupled,

n_{+}\left(t;\phi\right)<r_{\textrm{cut}}\,\,\textrm{for}\,\,t<t_{\phi}^{\textrm{in}},

(33)

and becomes coupled after this time moment,

n_{+}\left(t;\phi\right)>r_{\textrm{cut}}\,\,\textrm{for}\,\,t>t_{\phi}^{\textrm{in}}.

(34)

If the mode
\phi
is always significant, we formally put
t_{\phi}^{\textrm{in}}=0
.
If the mode
\phi
is never significant, we formally put
t_{\phi}^{\textrm{in}}=+\infty.

V.2
Measure of advanced statistical significance

Let us assume that we consider the quantum motion on a maximal time
interval
\left[0,T\right]
. Analogously to the previous section,
we can introduce the measure of statistical significance for the future
quantum motion
n_{-}\left(t;\phi\right)
on the time interval
\left[t,T\right]
.
We call
n_{-}\left(t;\phi\right)
the measure of advanced statistical
significance.

In order to define
n_{-}\left(t;\phi\right)
, we again employ the
white noise trick. We consider the ensemble of noisy evolutions of
the environment
\left|\Psi_{\xi-}\left(\tau\right)\right\rangle_{\textrm{b}}
,
but this time the noise is switched on only after
\tau=t:

\widehat{H}_{\xi-}\left(\tau\right)=\theta\left(\tau-t\right)\left\{\xi\left(\tau\right)^{*}\widehat{a}_{0}\left(t\right)+\xi\left(\tau\right)\widehat{a}_{0}^{\dagger}\left(t\right)\right\}.

(35)

We solve the Schrodinger equation
i\partial_{\tau}\left|\Psi_{\xi-}\left(\tau\right)\right\rangle_{\textrm{b}}=\widehat{H}_{\xi-}\left(\tau\right)\left|\Psi_{\xi-}\left(\tau\right)\right\rangle_{\textrm{b}}

with the vacuum initial condition
\left|\Psi_{\xi-}\left(0\right)\right\rangle_{\textrm{b}}=\left|0\right\rangle_{\textrm{b}}
.
Then, given a trial DoF
\phi
, with the corresponding creation operator

\widehat{\phi}^{\dagger}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\left\langle i\left|\phi\right.\right\rangle\widehat{a}_{i}^{\dagger}
,
we can estimate its advanced statistical significance as average occupation

n_{-}\left(t;\phi\right)=\left\langle\phi\right|\widehat{\rho}_{-}\left(t\right)\left|\phi\right\rangle,

(36)

where the one-body reduced density matrix
\widehat{\rho}_{-}\left(t\right)

is
\left\langle i\right|\widehat{\rho}_{-}\left(t\right)\left|j\right\rangle=\overline{\left\langle\Psi_{\xi-}\left(t\right)\right|\widehat{a}_{i}^{\dagger}\widehat{a}_{j}\left|\Psi_{\xi-}\left(t\right)\right\rangle}
.
The modes
\phi
with negligible
n_{-}\left(t;\phi\right)
can
be discarded from the Hamiltonian
\widehat{H}_{\textrm{b}}
when
solving the Schrodinger equation
after
the time
t
.

Analogously to appendix
B
, one can show that

\widehat{\rho}_{-}\left(t\right)
has a simple closed solution

\left\langle i\right|\widehat{\rho}_{-}\left(t\right)\left|j\right\rangle=\intop_{t}^{T}d\tau\phi_{i}^{*}\left(\tau\right)\phi_{j}\left(\tau\right),

(37)

where
\phi_{j}\left(\tau\right)
is the same as in eq. (
32
).
This equation can also be easily solved on a computer.

The measure
n_{-}\left(t;\phi\right)
is a monotonically
decreasing

function of
t
, which follows from eq. (
37
).
Therefore, given a significance treshold
r_{\textrm{cut}}
, there
is a unique time moment
t_{\phi}^{\textrm{out}}\in\left[0,\infty\right]

such that
after

t_{\phi}^{\textrm{out}}
the mode
\phi

is irreversibly decoupled,

n_{-}\left(t;\phi\right)<r_{\textrm{cut}}\,\,\textrm{for}\,\,t>t_{\phi}^{\textrm{out}},

(38)

and is coupled before this time moment,

n_{-}\left(t;\phi\right)>r_{\textrm{cut}}\,\,\textrm{for}\,\,t<t_{\phi}^{\textrm{out}}.

(39)

If the mode is always significant, we formally put
t_{\phi}^{\textrm{out}}=+\infty
.
If the mode is never significant, we formally put
t_{\phi}^{\textrm{out}}=0.

V.3
Streams of incoming and outgoing Kotelnikov modes

Having defined meaningful, easily computable measures of significance,
the problem of finding the streams of incoming
\kappa_{l}^{\textrm{in}}

and outgoing
\kappa_{l}^{\textrm{out}}
Kotelnikov modes becomes
purely technical.

V.3.1
Incoming Kotelnikov modes

Given a maximal time interval
\left[0,T\right]
, we define the stream
of incoming
\kappa_{l}^{\textrm{in}}
modes,
l=1\ldots m_{\textrm{in}}\left(T\right),

as a sequence of independent DoFs such that (I.1) it is the set of
all independent DoFs which are significant at
t=T
. That is, for
any other trial independent DoF
\phi
we will necessarily have
n_{+}\left(T;\phi\right)<r_{\textrm{cut}}
.
(I.2) It is the slowest coupling sequence. That is, we can always
rotate the frame via some unitary matrix
U
and arrange the set

\kappa_{l}^{\textrm{in}}
as another sequence
\kappa_{l}^{\textrm{in}\prime}=\sum_{k}U_{lk}\kappa_{k}^{\textrm{in}}
.
Then the slowest coupling means that the sequence
\kappa_{l}^{\textrm{in}}

couples at a sequence of times
t_{k}^{\textrm{in}}
which are the
most delayed:
t_{k}^{\textrm{in}}\geq t_{k}^{\textrm{in}\prime}
,
where
t_{k}^{\textrm{in}\prime}
are the coupling time for any other
arrangement
\kappa_{l}^{\textrm{in}\prime}
. We want to have (I.1)
in order to have a faithful representation of the quantum motion.
We want to have (I.2) because we want to slow down the growth of complexity
as much as possible. It is evident that the lowest bound for the growth
rate is nothing but the Kotelnikov sampling rate
\propto B
.

V.3.2
Outgoing Kotelnikov modes

Given a maximal time interval
\left[0,T\right]
, we define the stream
of outgoing
\kappa_{l}^{\textrm{out}}
modes,
l=1\ldots m_{\textrm{in}}\left(T\right),

as a sequence of independent DoFs such that (O.1) it is the set of
all independent DoFs which are significant at
t=T
. That is, for
any other trial independent DoF
\phi
we will necessarily have
n_{+}\left(T;\phi\right)<r_{\textrm{cut}}
.
(O.2) It is the fastest
decoupling
sequence. That is, we
can always rotate the frame via some unitary matrix
U
and arrange
the set
\kappa_{l}^{\textrm{out}}
as another sequence
\kappa_{l}^{\textrm{out}\prime}=\sum_{k}U_{lk}\kappa_{k}^{\textrm{out}}
.
Then the fastest decoupling means that the sequence
\kappa_{l}^{\textrm{out}}

decouples at a sequence of times
t_{k}^{\textrm{out}}
which are
the earliest times:
t_{k}^{\textrm{out}}\leq t_{k}^{\textrm{out}\prime}
,
where
t_{k}^{\textrm{out}\prime}
are the coupling time for any
other arrangement
\kappa_{l}^{\textrm{out}\prime}
. Again, we want
to have (O.1) in order to have a faithful representation of the quantum
motion. We want to have (O.2) because we want to
speed up

the
decay
of complexity as much as possible. It is evident
that the maximal decay rate is bounded from above by bandwidth, i.e.
it is the Kotelnikov sampling rate
\propto B
.

V.3.3
Time reversal symmetry between
the streams of incoming and outgoing modes

These definitions are manifestly time reversal invariant. Let is define
the time reversal operator
\mathcal{T}
on the time interval
\left[0,T\right]
:

\mathcal{T}\left[\phi\left(t\right)\right]=\phi^{*}\left(T-t\right)
.
Then
\mathcal{T}\widehat{\rho}_{\pm}\left(t\right)\mathcal{T}=\widehat{\rho}_{\mp}^{*}\left(T-t\right)
,
and the measures of statistical significance swap:
n_{\pm}\left(t;\phi\right)=n_{\mp}\left(T-t;\mathcal{T}\phi\right)
.
The slowest coupling becomes the fastest decoupling, and vice versa,
so that the incoming and the outgoing Kotelnikov modes also swap:

\mathcal{T}\kappa_{l}^{\textrm{in}}=\kappa_{m_{\textrm{in}}\left(T\right)-l+1}^{\textrm{out}}
,
with
t_{l}^{\textrm{in}}=T-t_{m_{\textrm{in}}\left(T\right)-l+1}^{\textrm{out}}
.
Observe that the initial condition for eq. (
32
)
is not time reversal invariant. But this does not matter: the time
reversal results in a unitarily displaced initial condition. That
is, the frame of the whole environment is just unitarily displaced,
but the model constants
\chi_{l}^{\textrm{rel}}\left(t\right)
and

D_{lk}\left(t\right)
in eq. (
16
)
are invariant under such a change, and
\widehat{H}_{\textrm{eff}}\left(t\right)

is the same.

V.3.4
Composition of incoming and outgoing streams

As we pointed out in sec.
IV.2
,
we are only interested in the outgoing modes which were previously
coupled to OQS. Therefore, we impose an additional constraint on the
definition of outgoing modes: (O.3)
\kappa_{k}^{\textrm{out}}
should
be a linear combination of all
\kappa_{l}^{\textrm{in}}
with
t_{l}^{\textrm{in}}\leq t_{k}^{\textrm{out}}
.
This definition is not required for the correctness of the model.
But it is required to achieve the balance of complexity: due to (O.3)
the number of relevant modes saturates, Fig.
6
.
Observe that formally this requirement breaks the strict time reversal
symmetry between the incoming and outgoing streams:
\mathcal{T}\kappa_{l}^{\textrm{in}}\neq\kappa_{m_{\textrm{in}}\left(T\right)-l+1}^{\textrm{out}}.

However this symmetry holds in a more general sense,
\mathcal{T}\kappa_{l}^{\textrm{in}}=\kappa_{m_{\textrm{in}}\left(T\right)-l+1}^{\textrm{out}\prime}

and
\mathcal{T}\kappa_{l}^{\textrm{out}}=\kappa_{m_{\textrm{in}}\left(T\right)-l+1}^{\textrm{in}\prime}
,
where
\kappa_{k}^{\textrm{in$\prime$}}
and
\kappa_{k}^{\textrm{out}\prime}

are some other incoiming and outgoing streams which satisfy the requirements
(I.1, O.1) and suboptimally satisfy theirs corresponding requirements
(I.2, O.2).

(I.2)

eq. (

VI
CONCLUSION

In this work we propose the concept of bandlimited quantum noise.
On a spin-boson model we demonstrate that the bandlimited quantum
noise is effectively a discrete time process, which yields simple
and intuitive equations of motion. These equations of motion are ready
to be applied to specific problems. A numerical procedure to find
the necessary coupling constants for a given spectral density
J\left(\omega\right)

is presented.

The merit of the proposed approach is that it exploits the balance
of complexity which is characteristic of the quantum dissipative motion

(
Polyakov 2022
)
: only a bounded number of degrees of freedom
are coupled to OQS at any given instant of time. Moreover, the population
of these coupled degrees of freedom is bounded. This yields a microscopically-derived
matrix product state (MPS) for the quantum dissipative evolution,
with a bounded bond dimension. Interesting enough, we do not use SVD
anywhere. In this work we provide an alternative way of how to obtain
the MPS structures.

In this work we also propose a Monte Carlo simulation technique which
samples the pure-state evolution interrupted by random quantum jumps.
This has a benefit with respect to the density matrix methods due
to a quadratically smaller dimension of the state vector. Importantly,
these quantum jumps are irreversible and completed. There is no need
to simulate the reversed quantum jumps
(
Piilo et al. 2008
;
Piilo et al. 2009
)
.

The model of bandlimited quantum noise also yields some conceptual
insights. In particular, it shows that the classical records are stored
in the asymptotically invariant entanglement structures. Given a pure
state, we can compute the entropy of the ensemble of classical records
which this pure state contains. It also presents a novel discrete-time
input-output formalism.

The concept of bandlimited quantum noise is expected to be applicable
to any kind of open system and environment. The work is in progress
to cover more situations.

Appendix A
CLASSICAL KOTELNIKOV SAMPLING:
VARIATIONAL FORMULATION

Here we reformulate the classical Kotelnikov sampling theorem so that
it could be easily generalized to the case of quantum noise. We begin
by noting that the ensemble of random classical signal samples
z\left(t\right)

on a finite time interval
\left[0,T\right]
is analogous to a many
body state since it generates an inifinite hierarchy of correlators

C_{\textrm{cl}}\left(t_{1},t_{2},\ldots t_{p}\left|t_{1}^{\prime},t_{2}^{\prime},\ldots t_{q}^{\prime}\right.\right)\\
=\overline{z^{*}\left(t_{1}\right)z^{*}\left(t_{2}\right)\ldots z^{*}\left(t_{p}\right)z\left(t_{1}^{\prime}\right)z\left(t_{2}^{\prime}\right)\ldots z\left(t_{q}^{\prime}\right)}.

(45)

Then the time correlator
C_{\textrm{cl}}\left(t\left|t^{\prime}\right.\right)=\overline{z^{*}\left(t\right)z\left(t^{\prime}\right)}

can be interpreted as a one-body reduced density matrix
\widehat{\rho}_{\textrm{cl}}

with matrix elements

\left\langle t\left|\widehat{\rho}_{\textrm{cl}}\right|t^{\prime}\right\rangle=\overline{z^{*}\left(t\right)z\left(t^{\prime}\right)}.

(46)

Suppose we are given some normalized temporal function
\phi\left(t\right)

denoted as
\left|\phi\right\rangle
. We may consider it as a DoF
and ask a question: is it significant for
z\left(t\right)
or we
may discard it? In other words, we seek for a minimal number
m

of DoFs
\left|\kappa_{k}\right\rangle
,
k=1\ldots m
, which play
the most significant role in the representation of the signal, so
that it effectively becomes
m
-dimensional,
z\left(t\right)\approx\sum_{k=1}^{m}z_{m}\left\langle t\left|\kappa_{k}\right.\right\rangle
.
To answer this question it is enough to consider the variance

\mathcal{K}\left[\phi\right]=\left\langle\phi\left|\widehat{\rho}_{\textrm{cl}}\right|\phi\right\rangle.

(47)

If
\overline{z\left(t\right)}=0
, then a negligible
\mathcal{K}\left[\phi\right]

indicates that the signal is effectively zero along the direction
of
\left|\phi\right\rangle
, and it is irrelevant for the representation
of
z\left(t\right)
. Then the most significant DoF
\left|\kappa_{1}\right\rangle

is found variationally

\left|\kappa_{1}\right\rangle=\textrm{arg}\underset{\kappa_{1}}{\max}\frac{\left\langle\kappa_{1}\right|\widehat{\rho}_{\textrm{cl}}\left|\kappa_{1}\right\rangle}{\left\langle\kappa_{1}\left|\kappa_{1}\right.\right\rangle}.

(48)

Next we find the DoF
\left|\kappa_{2}\right\rangle
which is the
most singificant after
\left|\kappa_{1}\right\rangle
. It satisfies
the same variational principle under the constraint
\left|\kappa_{1}\right\rangle\perp\left|\kappa_{2}\right\rangle

since it should be a new independent DoF. Repeating this recursively,
we recognize this as the Rayleigh-Ritz formulation of the eigenvalue
problem,

\widehat{\rho}_{\textrm{cl}}\left|\kappa_{l}\right\rangle=\pi_{l}\left|\kappa_{l}\right\rangle,

(49)

where the eigenvectors
\left|\kappa_{l}\right\rangle
are sorted
in the descending order of
\pi_{l}
. By choosing some relative significance
treshold
r_{\textrm{cut}}
(e.g.
r_{\textrm{cut}}=10^{-4}\ldots 10^{-7}
),
we keep only the first
m
modes which surpass this treshold,
\pi_{k}/\pi_{1}>r_{\textrm{cut}}

for
k=1\ldots m
. I

It can be shown by a numerical computation that indeed
\left|\kappa_{l}\right\rangle

span the same subspace as the sinc basis in eq. (
5
),
and
m=2BT
. We call the modes
\left|\kappa_{l}\right\rangle

surpassing the significance treshold the
Kotelnikov modes
.

We conclude that the message of the Kotelnikov sampling theorem is
that a bandlimited signal has a finite number
m
of statistically
significant DoFs on a finite interval
T
, and
m
scales as
\propto BT
.
These DoFs are found as eigenvectors of the largest eigenvalues of
the one body reduced density matrix.

Appendix B
SOLUTION FOR
\widehat{\rho}_{+}\left(t\right)

We interpret the equation (
29
) as a stochastic
equation in the Stratonovich sense

id\left|\Psi_{\xi+}\right\rangle=\left\{dz^{*}\widehat{a}_{0}\left(t\right)+dz\widehat{a}_{0}^{\dagger}\left(t\right)\right\}\left|\Psi_{\xi+}\right\rangle.

(50)

where
dz
is increment of complex white noise (Wiener increment).
The corresponding Ito form is

d\left|\Psi_{\xi+}\right\rangle\\
=\left\{-idz^{*}\widehat{a}_{0}\left(t\right)-idz\widehat{a}_{0}^{\dagger}\left(t\right)-\left(\widehat{a}_{0}^{\dagger}\left(t\right)\widehat{a}_{0}\left(t\right)+\frac{1}{2}\right)dt\right\}\left|\Psi_{\xi+}\right\rangle.

(51)

The one-body density matrix is

\rho_{+kl}\left(t\right)=\overline{\left\langle\Psi_{\xi+}\left(t\right)\left|\widehat{a}_{k}^{\dagger}\widehat{a}_{l}\right|\Psi_{\xi+}\left(t\right)\right\rangle},

(52)

where
\overline{\left(\cdot\right)}
is stochastic average (over
noise
z
realizations). Using the identity

\widehat{a}_{0}^{\dagger}\left(t\right)=\sum_{j}\widehat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}\phi_{j}\left(t\right),

(53)

where
\phi_{j}\left(t\right)
satisfies eq. (
32
),
we apply the Ito formula for the increment
d\rho_{+kl}\left(t\right)
.
After some algebra we obtain:

d\rho_{+kl}\left(t\right)=dt\phi_{k}^{*}\left(t\right)\phi_{l}\left(t\right).

(54)

Therefore, we get the equation (
31
).
