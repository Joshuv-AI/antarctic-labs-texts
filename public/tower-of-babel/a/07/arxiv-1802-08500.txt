Abstract
. We investigate the isomorphism problem in the setting of definable sets (equivalent to sets with atoms): given two definable relational structures, are they related by a definable isomorphism? Under mild assumptions on the underlying structure of atoms, we prove decidability of the problem. The core result is parameter-elimination: existence of an isomorphism definable with parameters implies existence of an isomorphism definable without parameters.

Introduction

We consider
hereditarily first-order definable sets

amenable to algorithmic manipulation.
We drop the qualifiers
herediatarily first-order

\displaystyle=\left\{\,(\{a,b\},\{c,d\})\,|\,a,b,c,d\in\mathsf{Atoms},a\neq b\wedge a\neq c\wedge a\neq d\wedge b\neq c\wedge b\neq d\wedge c\neq d\,\right\}.

We remark that in the pure set

Thus, as long as complexity issues are ignored and decidability is the only concern, in the case of the pure set, we can safely restrict to quantifier-free formulas,
however, in general, definable sets may include arbitrary first-order formulas.

A definable function

is simply
a function whose domain
X

Y

A relational structure is definable if its signature, universe,
and interpretation function
that maps each relation symbol to a relation on the

1
1

1

A structure over a finite signature is definable in

.
Finally, a definable isomorphism between definable structures over the same signature is a definable bijective

Contribution

The classical
isomorphism problem
asks whether two given
finite
structures are isomorphic.
In this paper, we consider its counterpart in the setting of definable sets
(the problem is called
definable isomorphism problem
in the sequel):
given two definable structures
{\mathbb{A}},{\mathbb{B}}
over the same definable signature
\Sigma
,
all over the same fixed structure
\mathsf{Atoms}
, are they related by a definable isomorphism?
Note that definable structures can be meaningfully considered as input to a computational problem since they are finitely described using the set-builder notation and first-order formulas in the language of
\mathsf{Atoms}
.
The structure
\mathsf{Atoms}
is considered here in a parametric manner, not as a part of input: every structure
\mathsf{Atoms}
induces a different decision problem.

As our main result we prove, under a certain assumptions on the structure
\mathsf{Atoms}
,
that the definable isomorphism problem is decidable.
The key technical difficulty is to show that every two

S
-definable structures related by a definable isomorphism are also related by an
S
-definable one.
(When
S=\emptyset
this is parameter elimination:
existence of an isomorphism defined with parameters enforces existence of one defined without parameters.)
Having this, the problem reduces to testing whether two
S
-definable structures are related by an
S
-definable isomorphism,
which in turn reduces to the first-order satisfiability problem in
\mathsf{Atoms}
.

As witnessed by Example
2
below, existence of an isomorphism does not guarantee existence of a definable one.
Therefore we do not solve the
isomorphism problem
for definable structures, which asks whether two given definable structures (over the same signature) are isomorphic.
In fact, the decidability status of the latter problem remains an intriguing open question, even for
\mathsf{Atoms}
being the pure set.

As a notational convention, when writing set-builder expressions (

Effectivity

We will additionally impose certain computability assumptions on
\mathsf{Atoms}
.
First, we fix an encoding of elements of

as strings,

We then assume that

More precisely, fix a finite field

Let

A concrete example is

The main property that will ensure the decidability of the definable isomorphism problem is the following

-definable directed graphs
{\mathbb{A}}=(A,E)
and
{\mathbb{B}}=(B,F)
,
where

and

are sets of nodes, and

and

together with an

of graphs.
Assume w.l.o.g. that the node sets
A
and
B
are disjoint.
By Lemma

,
the set
A
of nodes of
{\mathbb{A}}
, being itself
\emptyset
-definable, splits into finitely many
\emptyset
-orbits.
Likewise the set
B
of nodes of
{\mathbb{B}}
splits into finitely many
\emptyset
-orbits,
but a priori it is not clear whether the numbers of
\emptyset
-orbits inside
A
and

As a side conclusion of our proof, it will be made clear that they really are.

We apply denseness of
\mathsf{Atoms}
.
Fix in the sequel an embedding
H:\mathsf{Atoms}\to\mathsf{Atoms}
such that every automorphism of
H(\mathsf{Atoms})
extends
to an
S
-automorphism of
\mathsf{Atoms}
.
Atoms in
H(\mathsf{Atoms})
will be called
S
-independent
.
Along the same lines, a node

will be called
S
-independent
if
\mathrm{sup}(x)\subseteq H(\mathsf{Atoms})
.
By
\omega
-categoricity and denseness of atoms we have:

Claim
\thethm
.

Every
\emptyset
-orbit inside
A\cup B
contains an
S
-independent node.

(The claim, as well as few other claims formulated below, will be proved below once the proof of Theorem
3
is outlined.)

We are going to construct an
\emptyset
-definable bijection
h:A\to B
which will be later shown to be an isomorphism;
to this end we will define inductively a sequence of
\emptyset
-definable partial bijections

h_{i}:A\to B,

for
i=0,1,\ldots,m
, where
m
is the number of
\emptyset
-orbits inside
A
(or
B
, as made explicit below),
such that the domain
\text{Dom}(h_{i+1})
of every
h_{i+1}
extends
the domain
\text{Dom}(h_{i})
of
h_{i}
by one
\emptyset
-orbit inside
A
.
The required bijection will be
h=h_{m}
.
The order of adding
\emptyset
-orbits to the domain of
h
will be relevant for showing that
h
is an isomorphism.

We start easily by taking as
h_{0}
the empty function.
For the induction step, suppose that
h_{n}
is already defined.
Among the remaining
\emptyset
-orbits of
A
and
B
, i.e., among those which are not included in

\text{Dom}(h_{n})\cup\text{Codom}(h_{n})
, choose an orbit

O
whose elements have maximal dimension (cf. Corollary
5
).
W.l.o.g. assume that
O\subseteq A
(if
O\subseteq B
, change the roles of
{\mathbb{A}}
and
{\mathbb{B}}
, and replace

f
and all
h_{i}
for
i\leq n
by their inverses).
Choose an arbitrary
S
-independent node
x_{0}\in O
.
We observe that the
S
-orbit of
x_{0}
does not depend on the choice of
x_{0}
:

Claim
\thethm
.

All
S
-independent nodes in
O
belong to the same
S
-orbit.

We call this
S
-orbit
M\subseteq O
the
starting

S
-orbit inside
O
, and all its elements
starting nodes
.

Intuitively, an obvious idea would be to declare
h_{n+1}(x_{0})=f(x_{0})
and then to close
under atom automorphisms to lift the definition of
h_{n+1}
to the full orbit
O
.
However, it might be the case that
y_{0}=f(x_{0})\in B
is already contained in
\text{Codom}(h_{n})
.
In such case we return back to
A
using
{h_{n}}^{-1}
, which yields
x_{1}:={h_{n}}^{-1}(y_{0})\in\text{Dom}(h_{n})
.
Continuing in this way, we define a sequence
x_{0},y_{0},x_{1},y_{1},x_{2},\ldots
of nodes, alternating between
nodes of
{\mathbb{A}}
and nodes of
{\mathbb{B}}
, by the following equalities:

\displaystyle h_{n}(x_{i+1})\quad=\quad y_{i}\quad

\displaystyle=\quad f(x_{i}).

(3)

In words,
y_{i}
is obtained from
x_{i}
by applying
f
, and
x_{i+1}
is obtained from
y_{i}
by applying

(h_{n})^{-1}
. Clearly, the latter application is well-defined only when
y_{i}\in\text{Codom}(h_{n})
.
We thus stop generating the sequence as soon as
y_{i}\notin\text{Codom}(h_{n})
.
We need however to prove that this will eventually happen, i.e., that the sequence is finite:

Claim
\thethm
.

y_{l}\notin\text{Codom}(h_{n})
, for some
l\geq 0
.

(In particular, when
n=0
the claim necessarily holds for
l=0
.)
The
S
-orbit
M^{\prime}
of
y_{l}
will be called the starting
S
-orbit inside the
\emptyset
-orbit
O^{\prime}
of
y_{l}

(
{\mathbb{A}}
and
{\mathbb{B}}
are treated symmetrically here).
The number
l
will be called the length of the starting orbit
M
, and
n+1
will be called its order; we write

\text{order}(M)=n+1
, and
\text{length}(M)=l
.
By abuse of notation, we will also assign the same order and length to the
\emptyset
-orbit
O
, and to every element of
O
.

We are now ready to define the bijection
h_{n+1}
with
\text{Dom}(h_{n+1})=\text{Dom}(h_{n})\cup O
.
It agrees with
h_{n}
on
\text{Dom}(h_{n})
; on the orbit
O
, we define
h_{n+1}

by extending the mapping
x_{0}\mapsto y_{l}
to all of
O
.
We claim that there is a (unique)
\emptyset
-definable bijection between the
\emptyset
-orbit
O
of
x_{0}
and
the
\emptyset
-orbit
O^{\prime}
of
y_{l}
that maps
x_{0}
to
y_{l}
:

Claim
\thethm
.

The set of pairs
\left\{\,(\pi x_{0},\pi y_{l})\,|\,\pi\text{ is an atom automorphism}\,\right\}
is an
\emptyset
-definable bijection between
O
and
O^{\prime}
.

This completes the induction step of the definition of
h
.

Before proving that
h
is an isomorphism, we formulate a concise equality describing
h
; the equality will be useful later.
The definition of
h_{n+1}
in the induction step does not depend on the choice of the
S
-independent node
x_{0}\in M
.
To see this, observe that Claim
6
holds, with the same value
l
, for every other choice of
x_{0}\in M
; indeed, both

f
and
(h_{n})^{-1}
are
S
-definable, and hence by Lemma
5
they preserve the relation of belonging to the same
S
-orbit;
thus, no matter which
S
-independent node
x_{0}\in M
is chosen, it will always belong to the same
S
-orbit (by Claim
6
), hence the node

y_{l}
will always belong to the same
S
-orbit (and hence to the same
\emptyset
-orbit) inside
B
.
Thus by Lemma
5
and by the definition of
h
we have,
for every starting node
x\in A
, the following equality:

\displaystyle h(x)=\Big[f\circ(h^{-1}\circ f)^{l}\Big](x)

where
l=\text{length}(x)
; or equivalently (as
f
and
h
are bijections)

\displaystyle(h^{-1}\circ f)^{l+1}(x)=x.

(4)

(In the special case of starting nodes
x
of
\text{order}(x)=1
(recall that 1 is the minimal possible order) we have
\text{length}(x)=0
,
and thus the above equality reduces to
h(x)=f(x)
. Thus
h
agrees with
f
on
the starting
S
-orbit of order 1.)

Now we shall prove that
h
is an isomorphism, i.e., that for every two nodes
x,x^{\prime}\in A
,

\displaystyle(x,x^{\prime})\in E\quad\text{ if, and only if }\quad(h(x),h(x^{\prime}))\in F.

(5)

The proof is by induction on the orders of
x
and
x^{\prime}
.
Let
n=\text{order}(x)
and
n^{\prime}=\text{order}(x^{\prime})
.
For the induction step, suppose that (
5
) holds for all pairs
y,y^{\prime}
of starting nodes
with
\langle m,m^{\prime}\rangle=\langle\text{order}(y),\text{order}(y^{\prime})\rangle
pointwise strictly smaller than
\langle n,n^{\prime}\rangle
.
Thus we assume that the claim holds for
y,y^{\prime}
whenever
m\leq n
,
m^{\prime}\leq n^{\prime}
, but either
m<n
or
m^{\prime}<n^{\prime}
.
Let
l=\text{length}(x)
and
l^{\prime}=\text{length}(x^{\prime})
.

We first prove (
5
) in the special case when both
x
and
x^{\prime}
are starting nodes.
(Recall that in the special case
n=n^{\prime}=1
we have
h(x)=f(x)
and
h(x^{\prime})=f(x^{\prime})
, and the claim follows since
f
is an isomorphism.)
Consider two sequences of nodes,

\displaystyle x_{0},y_{0},x_{1},y_{1},\ldots,\qquad\text{ and }\quad x^{\prime}_{0},y^{\prime}_{0},x^{\prime}_{1},y^{\prime}_{1},\ldots

where
x_{0}=x
and
x^{\prime}_{0}=x^{\prime}
,
alternating between nodes of
{\mathbb{A}}
and
{\mathbb{B}}
, determined by the equalities analogous to (
3
):

\displaystyle h(x_{i+1})\quad=\quad y_{i}\quad

\displaystyle=\quad f(x_{i})

\displaystyle h(x^{\prime}_{i+1})\quad=\quad y^{\prime}_{i}\quad

\displaystyle=\quad f(x^{\prime}_{i}).

Analogously as before, the sequence is obtained by alternating applications of
f
and
h^{-1}
.
In particular,
x_{i+1}=h^{-1}(f(x_{i}))
and
x^{\prime}_{i+1}=h^{-1}(f(x^{\prime}_{i}))
.
Consider the smallest
k>0
such that
x_{k}=x
and
x^{\prime}_{k}=x^{\prime}
.
Observe that equality (
4
)
guarantees that such
k
exists, for instance
k=(l+1)(l^{\prime}+1)
works.
Note that the equality (
4
) implies also that

\displaystyle h(x)=y_{k-1}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad h(x^{\prime})=y^{\prime}_{k-1}.

(6)

Since
f
is an isomorphism, for every
i\geq 0
we have:

\displaystyle(x_{i},x^{\prime}_{i})\in E\quad\text{ if, and only if }\quad(y_{i},y^{\prime}_{i})\in F.

(7)

Using the inductive assumption we want to prove additionally

\displaystyle(x_{i+1},x^{\prime}_{i+1})\in E\quad\text{ if, and only if }\quad(y_{i},y^{\prime}_{i})\in F,

(8)

for every
i
such that
0<i+1<k
.
By the definition of
h
we know that
\text{order}(x_{i})\leq n
and
\text{order}(x^{\prime}_{i})\leq n^{\prime}
for every
i\geq 0
.
Furthermore, observe that for every
0<i<k
we have
\text{order}(x_{i})<n
or
\text{order}(x^{\prime}_{i})<n^{\prime}
; indeed, the equalities

\text{order}(x_{i})=n
and
\text{order}(x^{\prime}_{i})=n^{\prime}
, together with the bijectivity of
(h^{-1}\circ f)^{i}
, would imply
x_{i}=x
and
x^{\prime}_{i}=x^{\prime}
.
In consequence, the induction assumption (
5
) applies for every pair
(x_{i+1},x^{\prime}_{i+1})
where
0<i+1<k
, which
proves the equivalences (
8
).
Combining (
7
) with (
8
) we get

\displaystyle(x,x^{\prime})\in E\quad\text{ if, and only if }\quad(y_{k-1},y^{\prime}_{k-1})\in F

which, by (
6
), is exactly (
5
), as required.

Now we proceed to proving (
5
) for arbitrary nodes
x,x^{\prime}\in A
. To this end we will use the following fact,
easy to prove using
\omega
-categoricity and denseness of
\mathsf{Atoms}
:

Claim
\thethm
.

For every two nodes
x,x^{\prime}\in A
there is an atom automorphism
\pi
such that both
\pi x
and
\pi x^{\prime}
are starting nodes.

For any
\pi
in Claim

we have the following sequence of equivalences:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} (x,x^{\prime})\in E&\ \text{ iff }\\
(\pi x,\pi x^{\prime})\in E&\ \text{ iff }\\
(h(\pi x),h(\pi x^{\prime}))\in F&\ \text{ iff }\\
(\pi h(x),\pi h(x^{\prime}))\in F&\ \text{ iff }\\
(h(x),h(x^{\prime}))\in F&.\end{aligned}

(9)

The first and the last equivalence hold as the relations
E
and
F
, being
\emptyset
-definable, are closed under atom automorphisms
(cf. Lemma
5
);
the second equivalence has been treated previously as both
\pi x
an
\pi x^{\prime}
are starting nodes;
and the third equivalence holds as the function
h
is
\emptyset
-definable (cf. Lemma
5
).
Theorem
3
is thus proved, once we prove the yet unproven Claims

–
6
.

Proof of Claim
6
.

Consider an
\emptyset
-orbit
O\subseteq A\cup B
. We will use the embedding
H:\mathsf{Atoms}\to\mathsf{Atoms}
.
Take any node
x\in O
and consider the restriction of
H
to the finite set
\mathrm{sup}(x)\subseteq\mathsf{Atoms}
.
As
H
is an embedding, the restriction is a finite partial isomorphism which extends, by
\omega
-categoricity of
\mathsf{Atoms}

(c.f. Lemma

),
to an atom automorphism, say
\pi
.
Then
\pi x
is an
S
-independent node in

, as
\mathrm{sup}(\pi x)=\pi\mathrm{sup}(x)\subseteq H(\mathsf{Atoms})

(cf. Lemma

).
∎

Proof of Claim
6
.

Let
x,x^{\prime}\in O
be two
S
-independent nodes, thus
\mathrm{sup}(x)\cup\mathrm{sup}(x^{\prime})\subseteq H(\mathsf{Atoms})
.
Take an atom automorphism
\pi
such that
\pi x=x^{\prime}
.
By Lemma

we have
\pi\mathrm{sup}(x)=\mathrm{sup}(x^{\prime})
.
The restriction of
\pi
to
\mathrm{sup}(x)
is a finite partial isomorphism, and hence it extends to an automorphism of

, which in turn
extends to an atom

-automorphism

, by denseness. Thus
\tau x=x^{\prime}

(we use Lemma

here) and hence

and
x^{\prime}
are in the same
S
-orbit.
∎

Proof of Claim
6
.

It is enough to prove that the nodes
y_{0},y_{1},\ldots
belong to different
S
-orbits inside
B
(as

has finitely many
S
-orbits, and so does

, and the claim follows).
With this aim consider the sequence of
S
-orbits:

\text{orbit}_{S}(x_{0}),\text{orbit}_{S}(y_{0}),\text{orbit}_{S}(x_{1}),\ldots

and suppose, towards contradiction, that some
S
-orbit
O
in
B
repeats in the sequence, say

\text{orbit}_{S}(y_{k})=\text{orbit}_{S}(y_{j})
for some indices
j<k
.
Recall that
A
and
B
are disjoint, and that
y_{i}=f(x_{i})
and
x_{i+1}=h_{n}^{-1}(y_{i})
, for every
i
.
Since
f
is
S
-definable, and
h_{n}
, being
\emptyset
-definable, is also
S
-definable, by Lemma
5

they both (preserve and) reflect the relation of belonging to the same
S
-orbit; therefore

\text{orbit}_{S}(y_{k-\alpha})=\text{orbit}_{S}(y_{j-\alpha})
for
\alpha=1,\ldots,j
, and in consequence
the first orbit
O
necessarily repeats in the sequence:

\displaystyle\text{orbit}_{S}(x_{0})\ =\ \text{orbit}_{S}(x_{i})

(10)

for
i=k-j>0
. Recall that
x_{i}=(h_{n}^{-1}\circ f)^{i}
to observe that the equality (
10
) is impossible:

x_{i}\in\text{Dom}(h_{n})
while
x_{0}\notin\text{Dom}(h_{n})
, and
\text{Dom}(h_{n})
, being the union of
\emptyset
-orbits,
is also the union of
S
-orbits.
∎

Proof of Claim
6
.

Relying on Lemma
5
it is enough to demonstrate that the set of pairs

\left\{\,(\pi x_{0},\pi y_{l})\,|\,\pi\text{ is an atom automorphism}\,\right\}

is (the graph of) a bijection. In other words, it is enough to prove that for every two atom automorphism
\pi,\pi^{\prime}
,
the equality
\pi x_{0}=\pi^{\prime}x_{0}
holds if, and only if the equality
\pi y_{l}=\pi^{\prime}y_{l}
holds.
This is equivalent to the following condition:

\displaystyle\text{for every atom automorphism }\pi,\ \pi x_{0}=x_{0}\text{ iff }\pi y_{l}=y_{l}.

(11)

Recall that
y_{l}=g(x_{0})
, where
g=\Big[f\circ(h_{n}^{-1}\circ f)^{l}\Big]
; as both
f
and
h_{n}
are
S
-definable (partial) bijections,
their composition
g
is also an
S
-definable bijection, and hence the condition (
11
) holds for all atom
S
-automorphisms
\pi
.
Our aim is to prove (
11
) for all atom automorphisms, using
S
-independence of
x_{0}
.

Define the
S
-support
as
\mathrm{supp}_{S}(x)\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\ \mathrm{sup}(x)-S
.
Let
U=\mathrm{sup}(x_{0})
. We claim that
\mathrm{sup}(y_{l})=U
as well, i.e.,
y_{l}
is
S
-independent too.
To demonstrate this, we first observe using Lemma
5
that the mapping
g
, being
S
-definable, can only decrease the
S
-support:

\mathrm{supp}_{S}(x_{0})\supseteq\mathrm{supp}_{S}(y_{l}).

But
g^{-1}
, being also
S
-definable, has the same property and hence

\mathrm{supp}_{S}(x_{0})=\mathrm{supp}_{S}(y_{l}).

Furthermore,
x_{0}
is
S
-independent and thus satisfies
\mathrm{supp}_{S}(x_{0})=\mathrm{sup}(x_{0})
, which yields

U=\mathrm{supp}_{S}(y_{l})\subseteq\mathrm{sup}(y_{l})
. Finally, the dimension of
y_{l}
is at most equal to the dimension of
x_{0}

(because the node
x_{0}
has been chosen as one with the maximal dimension), i.e., to the cardinality of
U
,
therefore we deduce the equality
U=\mathrm{sup}(y_{l})
.

We are now ready to prove (
11
); we focus on the left-to-right implication, as the other one is proved similarly.
Consider any atom automorphism
\pi
satisfying
\pi x_{0}=x_{0}
.
By Lemma
5
we know that
\pi
preserves the set
U
, i.e.,
\pi U=U
.
We claim that some atom
S
-automorphism
\pi^{\prime}
coincides with
\pi
on
U
.
Indeed, the restriction of
\pi
to
U
extends, as a finite partial isomorphism inside
H(\mathsf{Atoms})
, to an automorphism of
H(\mathsf{Atoms})
;
and the latter extends to an atom
S
-automorphism
\pi^{\prime}
, by denseness of
\mathsf{Atoms}
.
As
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
coincide on
U=\mathrm{sup}(x_{0})
,
by Lemma
5
we have
\pi^{\prime}x_{0}=\pi x_{0}=x_{0}
. We can apply (
11
) to the atom
S
-automorphism

\pi^{\prime}
, thus obtaining
\pi^{\prime}y_{l}=y_{l}
.
Finally, again by Lemma
5
applied to
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
, coinciding on
U=\mathrm{sup}(y_{l})
, we deduce

\pi y_{l}=\pi^{\prime}y_{l}
. This entails
\pi y_{l}=y_{l}
as required.
∎

Proof of Claim
6
.

Similarly as in the proof of Claim
6
, we use the embedding
H:\mathsf{Atoms}\to\mathsf{Atoms}
.
The restriction of
H
to the finite set
\mathrm{sup}(x)\cup\mathrm{sup}(x^{\prime})\subseteq\mathsf{Atoms}
is necessarily a finite partial isomorphism which extends,
by
\omega
-categoricity of
\mathsf{Atoms}
, to an atom automorphism
\pi
, such that the nodes

and
\pi x^{\prime}
are both

, also starting.
∎

The general case

The additional assumptions imposed in the proof are not essential, and the proof easily adapts to the general case.
To get rid of the assumption
T=\emptyset
, one just needs to replace the atoms by the structure
\mathsf{Atoms}^{\prime}
obtained from
\mathsf{Atoms}
by introducing constant symbols for each element of
T
. Then, every set
x
which is
T
-definable in
\mathsf{Atoms}
becomes
\emptyset
-definable in
\mathsf{Atoms}^{\prime}
. Moreover,
\mathsf{Atoms}^{\prime}
remains effectively
\omega
-categorical, dense, and still has least supports.

One also easily gets rid of the assumption that the signature has just one binary symbol.
First, to deal with (possibly infinitely) many symbols, in the inductive argument towards
h
being an isomorphism one treats each symbol separately.
In case symbols of arity other than 2, say
r
, the induction is with respect to the
r
-tuples of orders, instead of pairs thereof.
The inductive argument itself, as well as Claim
6
, adapt easily to
r
-tuples.
Finally, in case of a (possibly infinite)
T
-definable signature, one needs to modify the sequence of equalities (
9
) appropriately, in order to
take into account the action of atom automorphisms on signature symbols. For a signature symbol
\sigma
, denote by
\sigma^{{\mathbb{A}}}
,

\sigma^{{\mathbb{B}}}
the interpretation
of
\sigma
in
{\mathbb{A}}
,
{\mathbb{B}}
, respectively. Then the sequence of equalities (
9
) is adapted as follows:

\displaystyle(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r})\in\sigma^{{\mathbb{A}}}

iff

\displaystyle(\pi x_{1},\ldots,\pi x_{r})\in\pi(\sigma^{{\mathbb{A}}})

iff

\displaystyle(\pi x_{1},\ldots,\pi x_{r})\in(\pi\sigma)^{{\mathbb{A}}}

iff

\displaystyle(h(\pi x_{1}),\ldots,h(\pi x_{r}))\in(\pi\sigma)^{{\mathbb{B}}}

iff

\displaystyle(h(\pi x_{1}),\ldots,h(\pi x_{r}))\in\pi(\sigma^{{\mathbb{B}}})

iff

\displaystyle(\pi h(x_{1}),\ldots,\pi h(x_{r}))\in\pi(\sigma^{{\mathbb{B}}})

iff

\displaystyle(h(x_{1}),\ldots,h(x_{r}))\in\sigma^{{\mathbb{B}}}

.

We consider here the action
\pi\sigma
on the signature symbol
\sigma
, as well as the action on the interpretation of the signature symbol,

\pi(\sigma^{{\mathbb{A}}})
or
\pi(\sigma^{{\mathbb{B}}})
. In particular,
(\pi\sigma)^{{\mathbb{A}}}
denotes the interpretation of the signature symbol
\pi\sigma
in
{\mathbb{A}}
.
As
\pi
is a
T
-automorphism, the first, the second, the fourth and the last equivalence follow by
T
-definability of
{\mathbb{A}}
and
{\mathbb{B}}

(cf. Lemma
5
);
the third equivalence is proved previously, as
\pi x_{1},\ldots,\pi x_{r}
are starting nodes;
and the fifth equivalence holds since the function
h
is
T
-definable (cf. Lemma
5
).
