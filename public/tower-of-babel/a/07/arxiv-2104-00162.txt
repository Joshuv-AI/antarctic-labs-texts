Point-free Construction of Real Exponentiation
Ming Ng ORCID:0000-0002-2170-0247, Steven Vickers ORCID:0000-0003-1907-9014

Abstract
. We define a point-free construction of real exponentiation and logarithms, i.e. we construct the maps exp : ( 0 , ∞ ) × ℝ → ( 0 , ∞ ) , ( x , ζ ) ↦ x ζ \exp\colon(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\!(0,\infty),\,(x,\zeta)\mapsto x^{\zeta} and log : ( 1 , ∞ ) × ( 0 , ∞ ) → ℝ , ( b , y ) ↦ log b ⁡ ( y ) \log\colon(1,\infty)\times(0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\,(b,y)\mapsto\log_{b}(y) , and we develop familiar algebraic rules for them. The point-free approach is constructive, and defines the points of a space as models of a geometric theory, rather than as elements of a set — in particular, this allows geometric constructions to be applied to points living in toposes other than Set \mathrm{Set} . Our geometric development includes new lifting and gluing techniques in point-free topology, which highlight how properties of ℚ \mathbb{Q} determine properties of real exponentiation. This work is motivated by our broader research programme of developing a version of adelic geometry via topos theory. In particular, we wish to construct the classifying topos of places of ℚ \mathbb{Q} , which will provide a geometric perspective into the subtle relationship between ℝ \mathbb{R} and ℚ p \mathbb{Q}_{p} , a question of longstanding number-theoretic interest.

Introduction

Our primary objective here is self-explanatory: we wish to develop an account of exponential and logarithmic functions

1.
Preliminaries in Point-free Topology and Topos Theory

The goal of this section is to give a quick introduction to the basic ideas and constructions used in this paper, as well as fixing some important notation and terminology. We shall also introduce two important techniques that we will rely heavily on in this paper — the first is a Lifting Lemma that allows us to lift certain results from the rationals to the reals, the second is a gluing technique that will allow us to (constructively) justify performing certain kinds of case-splitting of the reals.

1.1.
Point-Free Topology and Geometric Logic

The defining feature of a point-free space is that its points are not described as elements of a set (as in a
point-set

This general principle covers the various interpretations of “point-free”, including locales (using the geometric theory of completely prime filters), and formal topology (which presents the geometric theory directly, with the base as signature and covers as axioms).

1.2.
Brief explanation of the topos theory

So far, we have not tried to explain what
[\mathbb{T}]
,
the space of models of
\mathbb{T}
, actually is mathematically.
This is not trivial, as it is supposed to be the space of
all

The solution, a fundamental part of topos theory, is to form a category
\mathcal{S}[\mathbb{T}]

which is “the geometric mathematics freely generated by the generic (or universal) model

of
\mathbb{T}

•

“A category

[\mathbb{T}]

•

•

“Freely generated” means that for any model
M

\mathcal{S}[\mathbb{T}]\to\mathcal{S}[\mathbb{T}^{\prime}]
, unique up to isomorphism, that takes

to

and preserves colimits and finite limits (and hence all geometric constructions).

{obs}

[Conservativity of the generic model]
This definition means that any geometric construction applied to the generic point
U_{\mathbb{T}}
can be transported along the relevant functor taking

to any other model
M
. In particular, for any geometric sequent
\sigma
, the sequent
\sigma
holds for
U_{\mathbb{T}}
iff it holds for any
\mathbb{T}
-model in any topos.

Let’s unpack this in the context of point-free topology. Consider a

.
To define it, we declare “let

[\mathbb{T}]

[\mathbb{T}^{\prime}]

In the particular case where

in

.

We thus see that the generic point

In short, a map

1.3.
The Geometric Theory of Reals

There are two important different types of reals in point-free topology: Dedekind reals and the so-called ‘one-sided reals’. Much of the technical analysis of this paper involves exploiting the relationship between these two notions of reals, as well as their specific characteristics.

We start with definitions. Denote by
\mathbb{Q}
the set of rationals, by

{defi}

The geometric theory of Dedekind Reals, with space

, comprises two relations

which satisfy the following axioms:

(1)

\exists r\in\mathbb{Q}.R(r)

(2)

\forall r\in\mathbb{Q}.\big(R(r)\Leftrightarrow\exists r^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Q}.(r^{\prime}<r\land R(r^{\prime}))\big)
​​​ (
\Leftarrow

\Rightarrow

(3)

\exists q\in\mathbb{Q}.L(q)
(Left Inhabitedness)

(4)

\forall q\in\mathbb{Q}.\big(L(q)\Leftrightarrow\exists q^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Q}.(q^{\prime}>q\land L(q^{\prime}))\big)
(
\Leftarrow

\forall q,r\in\mathbb{Q}.(L(q)\land R(r)\Rightarrow q<r)

The two relations

{conv}

The one-sided reals occupy a kind of computational sweet spot in our point-free calculations. On the one hand, they correspond more closely to our intuitive notion of a ‘real number’ compared to the mere set of

This is a particularly powerful insight once we realise that a Dedekind real is entirely determined by its left or right sections. Hence, in order to show that certain identities hold on the level of the Dedekinds, it is often enough to show that they hold for either their left or right Dedekind sections, which is typically easier.

We make these claims precise in the following two lemmas:

(2)

(3)

Proof 1.2
.

We proceed in stages.

Step 0: Set-up.

We define

{defi}

Discussion 14

(Stability under pullback)
.

A reasonable question: why do we ask for the coequaliser to be stable in the Gluing Principle? The short answer: geometricity. To elaborate, saying that a construction is geometric is equivalent to saying that it’s stable under pullback along geometric morphisms. The Gluing Principle is meant to provide a geometric justification for the case-splitting along
x\leq 1
and
x\geq 1
— the pushout property gives us a framework for gluing the two cases together, while its stability under pullback tells us that the gluing is geometric.

2.
Rational Exponents

In this section, we develop the consequences of two pairs of basic
exponent laws:

\displaystyle x^{\zeta+\zeta^{\prime}}=x^{\zeta}x^{\zeta^{\prime}}\text{, }\quad x^{0}=1

(2)

\displaystyle x^{\zeta\cdot\zeta^{\prime}}=(x^{\zeta})^{\zeta^{\prime}}\text{, }\quad x^{1}=x

(3)

In describing a map
(x,\zeta)\mapsto x^{\zeta}
as an
exponentiation
, we shall mean that it satisfies the above exponent laws. They are enough to prescribe what
x^{\zeta}
has to be for
\zeta
rational. With
a
a natural number,
x^{a}

must be by repeated multiplication; for
b
a positive natural number,
x^{\frac{1}{b}}
must be a radical, and
x^{\frac{a}{b}}
combines those; and
x^{-\zeta}
is
(x^{\zeta})^{-1}
.
For completeness, we shall also prove the following
base product
law:

\displaystyle(xy)^{\zeta}=x^{\zeta}y^{\zeta}\text{, }\quad 1^{\zeta}=1

(4)

These identities recover the familiar (and standard) algebraic properties of exponentiation. Hereafter, we shall refer to Equations (
2
)–(
4
) collectively as
the Basic Equations
.

Before proceeding, however, first some obligatory remarks about the appropriate range for the base
x
. Clearly, without complex numbers we cannot hope to deal with radicals of negative reals, so we shall have to assume
x\geq 0
. Further, in later sections, we shall also find two additional problems with the case
x=0
. The first (Section
2.5
) is for negative exponents and Dedekind base
x
since
x
will need to be invertible. The second (Section
3
) is that while our definition
x^{0}=1
is OK for rational exponents (cf. Discussion
5
), this causes continuity issues for real exponents; indeed, this reflects the classical fact that
0^{0}
is not well-defined. Nonetheless, so long as we work with just non-negative rational exponents, the assumption that
x\geq 0
is OK.

2.1.
Natural Number Exponentiation of Discrete Monoids

Let
M
be a set equipped with a multiplicative monoid structure. Let
x\in M
be an element of such a set-based multiplicative monoid. By the universal property of
\mathbb{N}
being the free monoid generated by 1, we obtain a unique monoid homomorphism corresponding to the set-based function sending
1
to
x
in
M
. This yields the following map:

\displaystyle M\times\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle\rightarrow M

\displaystyle(x,a)

\displaystyle\mapsto x^{a}

Proposition 15
.

If
M
is commutative, then exponentiation (as defined above) satisfies the Basic Equations.

Proof 2.1
.

Let
x\in M
, and
a,a^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
.
Equation (
2
), and the second part of Equation (
3
), come straight from the definition.
The others are by induction on
a
or
a^{\prime}
.

Obviously it will be our aim to show these equations for real exponentiation. As our first step towards this goal, recall that
Q
denotes the set of non-negative rationals. It is known geometrically that
Q
is a monoid with respect to multiplication, hence we obtain the following exponentiation map as a special case of the previous construction:

\displaystyle Q\times\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle\rightarrow Q

\displaystyle(x,a)

\displaystyle\mapsto x^{a}

We finish this subsection by establishing some important (and useful!) algebraic properties of this exponentiation map:

Lemma 16

(Monotonicity)
.

Denote
\mathbb{N}_{+}
to be the set of positive natural numbers.
If
a\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
, then the map
\text{(---)}^{a}
on non-negative rational base preserves and reflects the strict order, and is also unbounded.

Proof 2.2
.

We prove that
\text{(---)}^{a}
preserves the strict order (i.e. is strictly monotonic) on
Q
by induction on
a
. The base case is trivial since
x<y\implies x^{1}<y^{1}
for any
x,y\in Q
by the basic exponent laws. For the inductive step, suppose that
x<y
implies that
x^{a}<y^{a}
. Then this yields:
x^{a+1}=x^{a}\cdot x<x^{a}\cdot y<y^{a}\cdot y=y^{a+1}
. To prove that
\text{(---)}^{a}
reflects the strict order, we have to show that
x^{a}<y^{a}\implies x<y
. Decidability of
<
on
Q
means that either
x<y
or
x\geq y
. Since monotonicity means
x\geq y\implies x^{a}\geq y^{a}
, contradicting our hypothesis that
x^{a}<y^{a}
, this means that the remaining case
x<y
must be true.
Finally, we show that
\text{(---)}^{a}
is unbounded. Given any
x\in Q
such that
1<x
, note that monotonicity implies that
1<x^{a}
. A simple inductive argument easily shows that
x<x^{a}
.

Lemma 17
.

For any pair of non-negative rationals
q,r\in Q
such that
q<r
, and any positive natural number
a\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
, there exists a positive rational
s\in Q_{+}
so that
q<s^{a}<r
.

Proof 2.3
.

Given
q,r\in Q
such that
q<r
, denote
\epsilon:=\frac{r-q}{2}
. By Lemma
16
, we know that
\text{(---)}^{a}
is unbounded on
Q
— thus there exists some
v\in Q
such that
v^{a}>r
. Consider the function
\text{(---)}^{a}
on the set of rationals from
0
to
v
. For any two rationals
x,y
such that
0\leq x<y\leq v
, we have:

\displaystyle y^{a}-x^{a}

\displaystyle=(y-x)\cdot(y^{a-1}+y^{a-2}\cdot x+\dots+y\cdot x^{a-2}+x^{a-1})

\displaystyle\leq(y-x)\cdot(v^{a-1}+v^{a-2}\cdot v+\dots+v\cdot v^{a-2}+v^{a-1})

\displaystyle=(y-x)\cdot a\cdot v^{a-1}\text{.}

By the Archimedean property (Fact
3
), there exists some
M\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\frac{1}{M}<\frac{\epsilon}{a\cdot v^{a}}
. Denoting
s_{i}:=\frac{i\cdot v}{M}
, it is clear that:

\displaystyle\bigcup_{0\leq i\leq M}[s_{i},s_{i+1}]=[0,v]

and that
s_{i+1}-s_{i}=\frac{v\cdot(i+1-i)}{M}=\frac{v}{M}<\frac{\epsilon}{a\cdot v^{a-1}}
, and so
s_{i+1}^{a}-s_{i}^{a}<\epsilon
.
Recalling that
\epsilon:=\frac{r-q}{2}
, this consequently yields:

q<s_{j+1}^{a}\leq s_{j}^{a}+\epsilon\leq q+\epsilon<r

proving the lemma, i.e.
\exists s\in Q_{+}
such that
q<s^{a}<r
.

2.2.
Natural Number Exponentiation of Non-negative Reals

Unfortunately, the previous exponentiation map is geometric only for monoid structures on sets, as opposed to topological monoids. Hence, in order to generalise

to the case where

Proposition 18

The map
\text{(---)}^{a}
that sends

\displaystyle\overrightarrow{[0,\infty]}\times\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\overrightarrow{[0,\infty]}

\displaystyle\overleftarrow{[0,\infty]}\times\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\overleftarrow{[0,\infty]}\text{.}

Each map is unique subject to being monoid homomorphism for

.

Let us fix some

Natural number exponentiation on the one-sided reals (as per Proposition

\displaystyle[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{N}

Proof 2.5
.

It remains to verify the inhabitedness, separatedness and locatedness axioms. Right inhabitedness essentially follows from the unboundedness of

In the case where

The Basic Equations follow immediately, because we know they hold for the lower and upper parts.

Lemma 20

(Monotonicity)
.

Let

be a positive natural number. Then the map
\text{(---)}^{a}
preserves and reflects non-strict order on the non-negative reals. Further, it is also an unbounded map that preserves and reflects strict order on the non-negative Dedekind reals.

Proof 2.6
.

Consider

\displaystyle\implies q<q^{\prime}\,\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\!\text{[since $\text{(---)}^{a}$ reflects strict order on $Q$]}

\displaystyle\implies q<y\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\!\!\!\!\!\quad\text{[by downward closure of lower reals]}

5.
Comparisons with other approaches and Future Applications

5.1.
Comparisons with Other Possible Approaches

It is reasonable to ask: why did we choose to develop point-free real exponentiation in the way we did? For instance, why did we choose to work with Dedekind reals as opposed to Cauchy reals? Or why did we not choose to define exponentiation via power series — e.g. by first defining the functions

\exp(x):=\displaystyle\sum^{\infty}_{n=0}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}\quad\quad\text{for $x\in\mathbb{R}$}

\displaystyle\ln(x):=\int^{x}_{1}t^{-1}dt\quad\quad\text{for $x\in(0,\infty$)},

before defining
x^{\zeta}\!:=\exp\,(\zeta\ln(x))
, for
x\in(0,\infty)
and
\zeta\in\mathbb{R}
? The answer to both questions is that we felt that our chosen approach would be comparatively cleaner in the point-free setting. Indeed, whilst there does exist a point-free account of quotiented Cauchy reals
[
Vic98
, Theorem 7.8]
and of integration
[
Vic07b
]
, both descriptions involve rather complicated and technical details. In contrast, by choosing to define real exponentiation by successively lifting it from the rational case to the reals, our construction explicitly (and geometrically) highlights how many familiar algebraic properties of real exponentiation are in fact inherited from the properties of
\mathbb{Q}
as one might expect — something which would likely to have been obscured in the other two approaches.

One may also reasonably ask: why even construct a point-free account of real exponentiation in the first place? One simple answer is that point-free topology is a school of constructive mathematics with many attractive features, and so it’s worth translating familiar ideas from real analysis into this setting and demonstrating how they (more or less) work in the ways we expect them to. From a methodological standpoint, it is also instructive to illustrate how point-wise reasoning works in a point-free setting. A slightly deeper answer, however, comes from our interest in applying ideas from geometric logic to questions arising from number theory, which we explain more fully in the next subsection.

5.2.
Applications to Number Theory and Arithmetic Geometry

Suppose we have some object

One would thus like to improve our understanding of the completions of
