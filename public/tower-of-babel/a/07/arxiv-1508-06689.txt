A Hypergeometric Integral with Applications to the Fundamental Solution of Laplace’s Equation on Hyperspheres This paper is a contribution to the Special Issue on Orthogonal Polynomials, Special Functions and Applications. Abstract
We consider Poisson’s equation on the n n -dimensional sphere in the situation where the inhomogeneous term has zero integral. Using a number of classical and modern hypergeometric identities, we integrate this equation to produce the form of the fundamental solutions for any number of dimensions in terms of generalised hypergeometric functions, with different closed forms for even and odd-dimensional cases.

Introduction

The calculation of fundamental solutions is difficult even for simple geometries. However, in the case of Laplace’s equation on the
n
-dimensional sphere, the sphere’s homogeneity allows us to reduce the partial differential equation to an ordinary one, rendering the computation tractable. The author is aware of two previous calculations of this type in the literature,
[
2
]
and
[
13
]
, which disagree. In this paper, we shall proceed along similar lines to
[
2
]
; in the process, it will become apparent that the calculation in
[
2
]
is in fact the correct solution to a subtly different problem. We shall also check the consistency of our results with those of
[
13
]
in the case
n=2
.

The even- and odd-dimensional cases are treated separately: one may be expressed as a hypergeometric polynomial in
\cot{\tfrac{1}{2}\theta}
, the other as a sum of an even polynomial in
\cot{\theta}
and an odd polynomial multiplied by
\pi-\theta
. There is reason to expect this disparity, given the other ways in which spheres of even and odd dimension are different, such as the hairy ball theorem and the volume in terms of factorials.

The last section gives some applications of our result, including the dipole potential on the sphere, the azimuthal Fourier expansion, fundamental solutions on real projective space by considering it as a quotient of the sphere, and a similar calculation explaining the problem that
[
2
]
is actually solving.

3
The ordinary differential equation for
\boldsymbol{G_{n}}

3.1
The Laplace–Beltrami operator on a hyperspherical manifold

To be explicit, let the Green’s function for the
n
-dimensional hypersphere be denoted
G_{n}
. One standard coordinate chart on the
n
-dimensional hypersphere is
(\theta_{i},\varphi)
,
0<\theta_{i}<\pi
,
0<\varphi<2\pi
; this shall suffice for our purposes. (Our derivation does not depend on
\varphi
, and excluding the point antipodal to
\theta=0
will also not matter, since the function is constructed to be smooth there.) The metric is given by
[
9
, p. 52, Example 7.4]

\displaystyle g=\operatorname{diag}\big(R^{2}\sin^{2}{\theta_{1}},R^{2}\sin^{2}{\theta_{1}}\sin^{2}{\theta_{2}},\dotsc\big).

Taking
\theta_{1}=\theta
, since a fundamental solution in a homogeneous set does not depend on direction, our fundamental solution reduces to
G_{n}=G_{n}(\theta)
, so we find that the Laplacian reduces to

\displaystyle\Delta{f(\theta)}=\frac{1}{R^{2}\sin^{n-1}{\theta}}\frac{d}{d\theta}\big(\sin^{n-1}{\theta}f^{\prime}(\theta)\big).

Therefore we need to solve the ordinary differential equation

\displaystyle f^{\prime\prime}+(n-1)\cot{\theta}f^{\prime}=aR^{2},

(3.1)

for
\theta\in(0,\pi)
, where
a
is the constant defined in the previous section. (Since the interval does not contain
\theta=0
, the
\delta(\theta)
term is not present.) The theory in the previous section dictates that, with
S_{n}
the volume of the unit
n
-sphere,

\displaystyle a=\frac{1}{S_{n}R^{n}}=\frac{\Gamma((n+1)/2)}{2\pi^{(n+1)/2}R^{n}},

(3.2)

which is the reciprocal of the volume of the
n
-sphere; we discuss the relationship of this global constraint with the behaviour as
\theta\downarrow 0
in Section
3.3
below.

3.2
Formal solution

Recall that a first-order differential equation

\displaystyle y^{\prime}+gy=u

may be written in the form

\displaystyle e^{-g}(ye^{g})^{\prime}=u.

It is then simple to show that for suitable limits on the integrals,

\displaystyle y(x)=e^{-g(x)}\int_{x_{0}}^{x}e^{g(t)}u(t)\,dt

is the solution, where
x_{0}
is some constant.

We conclude that (
3.1
), with
a
as in (
3.2
), has the formal solution

\displaystyle G_{n}(\theta)=\frac{1}{S_{n}R^{n-2}}\int_{\theta}^{\pi}\csc^{n-1}{\phi}\int_{\phi}^{\pi}\sin^{n-1}{\psi}\,d\psi\,d\phi.

(3.3)

(Since the integrand is a smooth function of
\phi
for
0<\phi\leqslant\pi
, the integral is smooth and bounded on
[\varepsilon,\pi]
for any sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
.)

3.3
The behaviour as
\boldsymbol{\theta\downarrow 0}
: consistency with fundamental solutions

on flat space

In this section we consider the limit for
n\neq 2
; the two-dimensional case is analogous using
\log{s}
. The integral (
3.3
) was determined by the global behaviour of fundamental solutions (
viz.
smoothness away from the pole at
\theta=0
). On the other hand, the arc-length on the hypersphere of radius
R
is given by
s=R\theta
, so we expect to find that in the flat-space limit as
R\to\infty
,

\displaystyle G_{n}(s/R)\sim\frac{1}{(n-2)S_{n-1}s^{n-2}},

which shall give us the local behaviour as
\theta\downarrow 0
. We wish to verify that our solution has this local behaviour. To agree with the flat-space fundamental solution, our fundamental solution should also exhibit a singularity of order
\theta^{2-n}
near
\theta=0
, with coefficient
1/((n-2)S_{n-1}R^{n-2})
. We therefore need to have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{S_{n}R^{n-2}}\int_{\theta}^{\pi}\csc^{n-1}{\phi}\int_{\phi}^{\pi}\sin^{n-1}{\psi}\,d\psi\,d\phi\sim\frac{1}{(n-2)S_{n-1}(R\theta)^{n-2}}

as
\theta\downarrow 0
. We can drop the part of the
\phi
integral from
\pi/2
to
\pi
as irrelevant, it being just a constant, and then we need to examine

\displaystyle\frac{1}{S_{n}R^{n-2}}\int_{\theta}^{\pi/2}\csc^{n-1}{\phi}\int_{\phi}^{\pi}\sin^{n-1}{\psi}\,d\psi\,d\phi.

It is apparent that as
\theta\downarrow 0
, the integrand behaves as

\displaystyle\csc^{n-1}{\phi}\int_{\phi}^{\pi}\sin^{n-1}{\psi}\,d\psi\sim\phi^{1-n}\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin^{n-1}{\psi}\,d\psi=\phi^{1-n}\frac{\pi^{1/2}\Gamma(n/2)}{\Gamma((n+1)/2)}.

A result in asymptotic analysis shows that if
F
is sufficiently well-behaved and
F(x)\sim f(x)
as
x\downarrow x_{0}
, then for
x\in(x_{0},x_{0}+\varepsilon)
,
\varepsilon>0
, we have
\int_{x}^{x_{0}+\varepsilon}F\sim\int_{x}^{x_{0}+\varepsilon}f
as
x\downarrow x_{0}
. It is easy to see that the integrand behaves as such, and hence we find

\displaystyle\frac{R^{2-n}}{S_{n}}\int_{\theta}^{\pi/2}\csc^{n-1}{\phi}\int_{\phi}^{\pi}\sin^{n-1}{\psi}\,d\psi\,d\phi\sim\frac{\theta^{2-n}R^{2-n}}{(n-2)S_{n}}\frac{\pi^{1/2}\Gamma(n/2)}{\Gamma((n+1)/2)}=\frac{(R\theta)^{2-n}}{(n-2)S_{n-1}},

as expected, using the recurrence relation for the hyperspherical volumes.

4
Calculating explicit expressions for our fundamental solution

In the following sections we shall consider two separate ways of determining our fundamental solution explicitly: the first proves that the solutions are finite sums of various trigonometric functions by deriving recurrence relations, the second uses hypergeometric functions and identities to produce closed form expressions for these sums, and recurrence relations that determine the explicit form of the solution. Even and odd dimensions are treated separately since the form of the solution expression is quite different in each case.

We shall use the notation

\displaystyle I_{m}(\phi):=\int_{\phi}^{\pi}\sin^{m-1}{\psi}\,d\psi,\qquad J_{m}(\theta):=\int_{\theta}^{\pi}\frac{I_{m}(\phi)}{\sin^{m-1}{\phi}}\,d\phi.

(4.1)

It is apparent that
J_{m}(\theta)
shall be proportional to our fundamental solution: in particular,

\displaystyle G_{n}(\theta)=\frac{R^{2-n}}{S_{n}}J_{n}(\theta).

4.1
Recurrence relation

Proposition 4.1
.

The integral
I_{m}(\phi)
satisfies the recurrence relation

\displaystyle I_{m}(\phi)=\frac{m-2}{m-1}I_{m-2}(\phi)+\frac{1}{m-1}\sin^{m-2}{\phi}\cos{\phi}

(4.2)

for
m>0
, with basis cases

\displaystyle I_{1}(\phi)=\pi-\phi,\qquad I_{2}(\phi)=1+\cos{\phi}.

Proof.

It is easy to verify the basis cases directly. We can carry out a standard calculation to find

\displaystyle I_{m}(\phi)

\displaystyle=\int_{\phi}^{\pi}\sin^{m-3}{\psi}\big(1-\cos^{2}{\psi}\big)\,d\psi=I_{m-2}(\phi)-\int_{\phi}^{\pi}\sin^{m-3}{\psi}\cos^{2}{\psi}\,d\psi

\displaystyle=I_{m-2}(\phi)-\left[\frac{1}{m-2}\sin^{m-2}{\psi}\cos{\psi}\right]_{\phi}^{\pi}+\frac{1}{m-2}\int_{\phi}^{\pi}\sin^{m-2}{\psi}(-\sin{\psi})\,d\psi

\displaystyle=I_{m-2}(\phi)+\frac{1}{m-2}\sin^{m-2}{\phi}\cos{\phi}-\frac{1}{m-2}I_{m}(\phi),

and we therefore have

\displaystyle I_{m}(\phi)=\frac{m-2}{m-1}I_{m-2}(\phi)+\frac{1}{m-1}\sin^{m-2}{\phi}\cos{\phi}

for
m>0
, as required.
∎

We may then verify, for example,

\displaystyle I_{3}(\phi)=\frac{2-1}{2}I_{1}(\phi)+\frac{1}{2}\sin^{2-1}{\phi}\cos{\phi}=\frac{1}{2}(\pi-\phi)+\frac{1}{2}\sin{\phi}\cos{\phi}.

We can use this to find a recurrence relation for
J_{m}(\theta)
.

Proposition 4.2
.

For
m>0
, the integral
J_{m}
satisfies the recurrence relation

\displaystyle J_{m}(\theta)=\frac{m-3}{m-1}J_{m-2}(\theta)+\frac{1}{m-1}\frac{\cos{\theta}}{\sin^{m-2}{\theta}}I_{m-2}(\theta)+\frac{1}{(m-1)(m-2)}

with basis cases

\displaystyle J_{1}(\theta)=\frac{1}{2}(\pi-\theta)^{2},\qquad J_{2}(\theta)=\log{\csc^{2}{\tfrac{1}{2}\theta}}.

Proof.

The basis cases are simple: for example,

\displaystyle J_{2}(\theta)=\int_{\theta}^{\pi}\frac{1+\cos{\phi}}{\sin{\phi}}\,d\phi=\int_{\theta}^{\pi}\cot{\tfrac{1}{2}\phi}\,d\phi=\log{\csc^{2}{\tfrac{1}{2}\theta}}.

For the general case,
[
2
]
notes the useful trigonometric/partial fraction identity

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sin^{m}{\phi}}=\frac{1}{\sin^{m-2}{\phi}}+\frac{\cos^{2}{\phi}}{\sin^{m}{\phi}},

which is a trivial consequence of Pythagoras’s identity. We therefore write

\displaystyle J_{m}(\theta)=\int_{\theta}^{\pi}\frac{I_{m}(\phi)}{\sin^{m-3}{\phi}}\,d\phi+\int_{\theta}^{\pi}\frac{I_{m}(\phi)\cos^{2}{\phi}}{\sin^{m-1}{\phi}}\,d\phi.

The next step is the most delicate part: we integrate the last integral by parts, being careful to note that near
\theta=\pi
,
I_{m}(\phi)\sim\frac{1}{m}(\pi-\phi)^{m}
:

\displaystyle\int_{\theta}^{\pi}\frac{\cos{\phi}}{\sin^{m-1}{\phi}}I_{m}(\phi)\cos{\phi}\,d\phi

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\left[-\frac{1}{m-2}\frac{I_{m}(\phi)\cos{\phi}}{\sin^{m-2}{\phi}}\right]_{\theta}^{\pi}+\frac{1}{m-2}\int_{\theta}^{\pi}\frac{I^{\prime}_{m}(\phi)\cos{\phi}-I_{m}(\phi)\sin{\phi}}{\sin^{m-2}{\phi}}\,d\phi

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\frac{1}{m-2}\frac{\cos{\theta}}{\sin^{m-2}{\theta}}I_{m}(\phi)-\frac{1}{m-2}\int_{\theta}^{\pi}\frac{\sin^{m-1}{\phi}\cos{\phi}}{\sin^{m-2}{\phi}}\,d\phi-\frac{1}{m-2}\int_{\theta}^{\pi}\frac{I_{m}(\phi)}{\sin^{m-3}{\phi}}\,d\phi

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\frac{1}{m-2}\frac{\cos{\theta}}{\sin^{m-2}{\theta}}I_{m}(\phi)-\frac{1}{m-2}\int_{\theta}^{\pi}\sin{\phi}\cos{\phi}\,d\phi-\frac{1}{m-2}\int_{\theta}^{\pi}\frac{I_{m}(\phi)}{\sin^{m-3}{\phi}}\,d\phi.

We therefore have

\displaystyle J_{m}(\theta)=\frac{1}{m-2}\left((m-3)\int_{\theta}^{\pi}\frac{I_{m}(\phi)}{\sin^{m-3}{\phi}}\,d\phi+\frac{\cos{\theta}}{\sin^{m-2}{\theta}}I_{m}(\theta)-\int_{\theta}^{\pi}\sin{\phi}\cos{\phi}\,d\phi\right).

The first integral is substantially easier: it splits as

\displaystyle\int_{\theta}^{\pi}\frac{I_{m}(\phi)}{\sin^{m-3}{\phi}}\,d\phi

\displaystyle=\frac{m-2}{m-1}\int_{\theta}^{\pi}\frac{I_{m-2}(\phi)}{\sin^{m-3}{\phi}}\,d\phi+\frac{1}{m-1}\int_{\theta}^{\pi}\frac{\sin^{m-2}{\phi}\cos{\phi}}{\sin^{m-3}{\phi}}\,d\phi

\displaystyle=\frac{m-2}{m-1}J_{m-2}(\theta)+\frac{1}{m-1}\int_{\theta}^{\pi}\sin{\phi}\cos{\phi}\,d\phi,

and hence

\displaystyle J_{m}(\theta)=\frac{m-3}{m-1}J_{m-2}(\theta)+\frac{1}{m-2}\frac{\cos{\theta}}{\sin^{m-2}{\theta}}I_{m}(\theta)-\frac{2}{(m-1)(m-2)}\int_{\theta}^{\pi}\sin{\phi}\cos{\phi}\,d\phi.

Computing the last integral, we obtain the recurrence relation

\displaystyle J_{m}(\theta)=\frac{m-3}{m-1}J_{m-2}(\theta)+\frac{1}{m-2}\frac{\cos{\theta}}{\sin^{m-2}{\theta}}I_{m}(\theta)+\frac{1}{(m-1)(m-2)}\sin^{2}{\theta}.

We can simplify the answer by using (
4.2
) to give

\displaystyle J_{m}(\theta)=\frac{m-3}{m-1}J_{m-2}(\theta)+\frac{1}{m-1}\frac{\cos{\theta}}{\sin^{m-2}{\theta}}I_{m-2}(\theta)+\frac{1}{(m-1)(m-2)},

as required.
∎

We shall now consider another way to obtain this recurrence, which will additionally produce closed-form results.

4.2
Hypergeometric functions

4.2.1
\boldsymbol{n}
even: stereographic projection

Note that stereographic projection
S^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
can be defined in terms of spherical coordinates
(\theta,\theta_{2},\dotsc,\theta_{n-1},\varphi)
on
S^{n}
and spherical coordinates
(r,\theta^{\prime}_{2},\dotsc,\theta^{\prime}_{n-1},\varphi^{\prime})
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
by

\displaystyle r=\cot{\tfrac{1}{2}\theta},\qquad\theta^{\prime}_{i}=\theta_{i},\quad 2\leqslant i\leqslant n-1,\qquad\varphi^{\prime}=\varphi.

The metric is transformed as

\displaystyle d\theta^{2}+\sin^{2}{\theta}(d\Sigma_{n-1})^{2}=\frac{4}{(1+r^{2})^{2}}\left(dr^{2}+r^{2}(d\Sigma^{\prime}_{n-1})^{2}\right),

where
(d\Sigma_{n-1})^{2}
is the metric on the
(n-1)
-dimensional sphere defined by
(\theta_{2},\theta_{3},\dotsc,\theta_{n-1},\varphi)
, and likewise
(d\Sigma_{n-1}^{\prime})^{2}
in the primed variables. The Laplacian becomes

\displaystyle\Delta{f}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sin^{n-1}{\theta}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left(\sin^{n-1}{\theta}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta}\right)+\frac{1}{\sin^{2}{\theta}}\Delta_{\Sigma_{n-1}}{f}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1+r^{2})^{n}}{4r^{n-1}}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(\frac{r^{n-1}}{(1+r^{2})^{n-2}}\frac{\partial f}{\partial r}\right)+\frac{1}{r^{2}}\Delta_{\Sigma^{\prime}_{n-1}}{f},

where
\Delta_{\Sigma_{n-1}}
is the Laplacian restricted to the
(n-1)
-dimensional sphere, and likewise
\Delta_{\Sigma^{\prime}_{n-1}}
in the primed variables.

Therefore in the new coordinates, the Green’s function is given by the integral expression

\displaystyle G_{n}(r)=4\frac{R^{2-n}}{S_{n}}\int_{0}^{r}\frac{(1+x^{2})^{n-2}}{x^{n-2}}\left(\int_{0}^{x}\frac{y^{n}}{(1+y^{2})^{n}}\,\frac{dy}{y}\right)\frac{dx}{x}.

(4.3)

We observe that projection from the antipodal point is just
r^{\prime}=\tan{\tfrac{1}{2}\theta=1/r}
. This fact is neatly captured in the
r\mapsto 1/r
symmetry of the integrands in (
4.3

Theorem 4.3
.

The Green’s function for the
n

\displaystyle G_{n}(\theta)=\frac{R^{2-n}}{S_{n}}\left(\frac{n-2}{n(n-1)}r^{2}\,{}_{3}F_{2}\biggl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{1,1,2-n/2}{2,1+n/2};-r^{2}\biggr]+\frac{1}{n-1}\log\big(1+r^{2}\big)\right),

where
r=\cot{\frac{1}{2}\theta}

Proof.

Consider first the inner integral in (
4.3
). This can easily be transformed to one of hypergeometric Euler type

\displaystyle(b-a)(1-z)F=(c-a)F(a^{-})-(c-b)F(b^{-}),

\displaystyle(1-n/2)\big(1+x^{2}\big)\,{}_{2}F_{1}\biggl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{1,2-n/2}{1+n/2};-x^{2}\biggr]=n/2-(n-1)\,{}_{2}F_{1}\biggl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{1,1-n/2}{1+n/2};-x^{2}\biggr];

\displaystyle G_{n}(\theta)

\displaystyle=4\frac{R^{2-n}}{S_{n}}\int_{0}^{r}\left(\frac{n-2}{n(n-1)}x\,{}_{2}F_{1}\biggl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{1,2-n/2}{1+n/2};-x^{2}\biggr]+\frac{1}{2(n-1)}\frac{x}{1+x^{2}}\right)dx

\displaystyle=4\frac{R^{2-n}}{S_{n}}\left[\frac{n-2}{2n(n-1)}x^{2}\,{}_{3}F_{2}\biggl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{1,1,2-n/2}{2,1+n/2};-x^{2}\biggr]+\frac{1}{4(n-1)}\log{(1+x^{2})}\right]_{x=0}^{r}

\displaystyle=\frac{R^{2-n}}{S_{n}}\left(\frac{n-2}{n(n-1)}r^{2}\,{}_{3}F_{2}\biggl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{1,1,2-n/2}{2,1+n/2};-r^{2}\biggr]+\frac{1}{n-1}\log{(1+r^{2})}\right),

as required.
∎

Corollary 4.4
.

Suppose
n=2m\geqslant 2
is a positive even integer. Then the Green’s function for the
n
-dimensional sphere of radius
R
is

\displaystyle G_{2m}(\theta)=\frac{R^{2-2m}}{S_{2m}}\left(\frac{m-1}{m(2m-1)}r^{2}\,{}_{3}F_{2}\biggl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{1,1,2-m}{2,1+m};-r^{2}\biggr]+\frac{1}{2m-1}\log\big(1+r^{2}\big)\right),

(4.5)

where
r=\cot{\frac{1}{2}\theta}
.

In particular, notice that since
2-m
is a negative integer, the hypergeometric term is explicitly a polynomial.

To verify the local behaviour, we extract from (
4.5
) the most singular term as
1/r\to 0
. Firstly, we have
r=\cot{\frac{1}{2}\theta}\sim 2/\theta
as
\theta\to 0
. As discussed in Section
3.3
, the Green’s function should act like the fundamental solution for flat
n
-dimensional space near
1/r=0
,
viz.
,

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\pi}\log{s},\quad n=2,\qquad\frac{1}{(n-2)S_{n-1}}s^{2-n},\quad n\neq 2,

where
S_{n-1}=2\pi^{n/2}/\Gamma(n/2)
is the volume of the
(n-1)
-dimensional unit hypersphere, and we recall that
s
is the arc-length, given by
s=\left\lvert x\right\rvert
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, and
s=R\theta
on hyperspheres
S^{n}
.

For
S^{2}
(i.e.,
m=1
), we find that

\displaystyle G_{2}\sim\frac{-2}{4\pi(2\cdot 1-1)}\log{r^{-1}}\sim-\frac{1}{2\pi}\log{s},

which is consistent with Section
3.3
.

For
m\geqslant 2
, the most singular term is the largest power of
r
in the hypergeometric polynomial, which has degree
m-2
, and can be simplified as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{R^{2-2m}}{S_{2m}}\frac{m-1}{m(2m-1)}\frac{(1)_{m-2}(1)_{m-2}(2-m)_{m-2}}{(2)_{m-2}(1+m)_{m-2}}\frac{(-1)^{m}r^{2(m-2)-2}}{(m-2)!}

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\frac{\Gamma(m+1/2)}{2\pi^{m+1/2}}\frac{2\Gamma(m-1)\Gamma(m+1)}{\Gamma(2m+1)}\left(\frac{r}{R}\right)^{2m-2}

\displaystyle\qquad{}\sim\frac{\Gamma(m-1)}{\pi^{m}}\frac{m\Gamma(m)\Gamma(m+1/2)}{\sqrt{\pi}2m\Gamma(2m)}\frac{2^{2m-2}}{(R\theta)^{2m-2}}

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\frac{\Gamma(m-1)}{\pi^{m}}\frac{1}{2^{2m}}\frac{2^{2m-2}}{(R\theta)^{2m-2}}=\frac{\Gamma(m)}{(2m-2)2\pi^{m}}s^{2-2m}=\frac{1}{(n-2)S_{n-1}}s^{2-n},

using the duplication formula for the
\Gamma
-function; this is also in agreement with Section
3.3
.

Remark 4.5
.

We can verify that some simple cases are in agreement with the corresponding results in
[
13
]
. Their notation differs somewhat from ours, and they have the opposite sign convention for the Laplacian (i.e.,
\Delta
instead of
-\Delta
). Specifically, their even-dimensional Green’s function for the Laplace case (corresponding to
L=0
in their paper) is given in their paper’s equation (4.41) by

\displaystyle\bar{G}_{0}^{(2n+2)}(\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime})=\frac{1}{(2n+1)S_{2n+2}}\log{\frac{1-\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime}}{2}}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\bar{G}_{0}^{(2n+2)}(\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{n}') =}{}-\frac{n!}{(2n+1)!!S_{2n+2}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{(2n-2k-1)!!}{k(n-k)!}\frac{C_{k}^{(n-k+1/2)}(\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime})}{(1-\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime})^{k}}+\text{const},

(4.6)

where
n
is our
m-1
, and

\displaystyle C_{\lambda}^{(\alpha)}(x)=\frac{\Gamma(\lambda+2\alpha)}{\Gamma(\lambda+1)\Gamma(2\alpha)}{}_{2}F_{1}\biggl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{-\lambda,\lambda+2\alpha}{\alpha+1/2};\frac{1-x}{2}\biggr]

is the Gegenbauer function (of the first kind), which for
\lambda\in{\mathbb{Z}}
is obviously a polynomial; we have also used that
C_{0}^{(\alpha)}(x)=1
for
\alpha\neq 0
to simplify their expression into (
4.6
). It is easy to see that
\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime}=\cos{\theta}
in our notation. Putting
n=0
in (
4.6
) gives the two-dimensional case,

\displaystyle\bar{G}_{0}^{(2)}=\frac{1}{S_{2}}\log{\frac{1-\cos{\theta}}{2}}+\text{const},

(4.7)

whereas if we put
m=1
in our expression (
4.5
), we obtain

\displaystyle G_{2}(\theta)=\frac{1}{4\pi}\log{(1+\cot^{2}{\tfrac{1}{2}\theta})}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\log{\csc{\tfrac{1}{2}\theta}}=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\log{\frac{1-\cos{\theta}}{2}},

which agrees with (
4.7
) when we take the opposite sign (to agree with the difference in the Laplacians).

Similarly, taking
n=1
in (
4.6
) gives the four-dimensional case,

\displaystyle\bar{G}_{0}^{(4)}(\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3S_{4}}\log{\frac{1-\cos{\theta}}{2}}-\frac{1}{3S_{4}}\frac{1}{1}\frac{C_{1}^{(1/2)}(\cos{\theta})}{1-\cos{\theta}}+\text{const}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3S_{4}}\left(\log{\frac{1-\cos{\theta}}{2}}-\frac{\cos{\theta}}{1-\cos{\theta}}\right)+\text{const}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{3S_{4}}\left(-\log{\frac{1-\cos{\theta}}{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\cot^{2}{\tfrac{1}{2}\theta}\right)+\text{const},

where the constants differ between the last two lines (essentially we have used
1-\cos{\theta}=2\sin^{2}{\frac{1}{2}\theta}
again). Our corresponding result is

\displaystyle G_{4}(\theta)=\frac{1}{3S_{4}}\left(-\log{\frac{1-\cos{\theta}}{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\cot^{2}{\tfrac{1}{2}\theta}\right),

which is once again in agreement when we take the opposite sign convention.

4.2.2
\boldsymbol{n}
odd: a recurrence relation

Unfortunately the simple hypergeometric polynomial formula found in the previous section does not extend to the odd case. Instead the Green’s function has a quite different character, as we shall see in Theorem
4.7
.

The discussion will be carried out for
\pi/2<\theta<\pi
initially, then the end result will be obtained by analytic continuation.

Lemma 4.6

(reduction of
J_{2m+1}
to hypergeometric form)
.

Let
\pi/2<\theta<\pi
. With
J_{n}(\theta)
as defined in (
4.1
), we have

\displaystyle J_{2m+1}(\theta)=\frac{1}{4m}\left(\log{(1+T)}-\frac{T}{2m+1}\,{}_{3}F_{2}\biggl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{1,1,3/2}{2,m+3/2};-T\biggr]\right),

where
T=\tan^{2}{\theta}
.

Proof.

We shall make the substitution

\displaystyle u=\frac{\tan^{2}{\psi}}{\tan^{2}{\phi}},\qquad d\psi=\frac{du}{2u^{1/2}\tan{\phi}(1+u\tan^{2}{\phi})},

in the definitions of
I_{2m+1}
and
J_{2m+1}
from (
4.1
). This also gives

\displaystyle\sin{\psi}=u^{1/2}\tan{\phi}\big(1+u\tan^{2}{\phi}\big)^{-1/2}.

Then, returning to the notation of Section
4.1
and taking a general odd
n=2m+1
,

\displaystyle I_{2m+1}(\phi)=\int_{\phi}^{\pi}\sin^{2m}{\psi}\,d\psi=-\frac{1}{2}\tan^{2m+1}{\phi}\int_{0}^{1}u^{m-1/2}\big(1+u\tan^{2}{\phi}\big)^{-m-1/2}\,du,

and the usual Euler integral produces

\displaystyle I_{2m+1}(\phi)=-\frac{\tan^{2m+1}{\phi}}{2m+1}\,{}_{2}F_{1}\biggl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{m+1/2,m+1}{m+3/2};-\tan^{2}{\phi}\biggr],

which can be converted to

\displaystyle I_{2m+1}(\phi)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2m+1}\tan{\phi}\frac{\sin^{2m}{\phi}}{\cos^{2m}{\phi}}\frac{1}{(1+\tan^{2}{\phi})^{m}}\,{}_{2}F_{1}\biggl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{1,1/2}{m+3/2};-\tan^{2}{\phi}\biggr]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2m+1}\tan{\phi}\sin^{2m}{\phi}\,{}_{2}F_{1}\biggl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{1,1/2}{m+3/2};-\tan^{2}{\phi}\biggr]

using an Euler transformation.

We then have

\displaystyle J_{2m+1}(\theta)=-\frac{1}{2m+1}\int_{\theta}^{\pi}\tan{\phi}\,{}_{2}F_{1}\biggl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{1,1/2}{m+3/2};-\tan^{2}{\phi}\biggr]\,d\phi.

Substituting

\displaystyle v=\frac{\tan^{2}{\phi}}{\tan^{2}{\theta}}=:T^{-1}\tan^{2}{\phi},\qquad\tan{\phi}\,d\phi=\frac{dv}{2(1+Tv)},

the integral becomes

\displaystyle J_{m}(\theta)=\frac{1}{2(2m+1)}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{T}{1+Tv}\,{}_{2}F_{1}\biggl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{1,1/2}{m+3/2};-Tv\biggr]\,dv.

The same contiguous relation as before (see (
4.4
) above) allows us to write this as

\displaystyle J_{2m+1}(\theta)=\frac{T}{4m}\int_{0}^{1}\left(\frac{1}{1+Tv}-\frac{1}{2m+1}\,{}_{2}F_{1}\biggl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{1,3/2}{m+3/2};-Tv\biggr]\right)dv,

which integrates immediately to

\displaystyle J_{2m+1}(\theta)=\frac{1}{4m}\left(\log{(1+T)}-\frac{T}{2m+1}\,{}_{3}F_{2}\biggl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{1,1,3/2}{2,m+3/2};-T\biggr]\right),

as required.
∎

This lemma provides a form of the Green’s function, but rather an unilluminating one: indeed, it is not even obvious that there is a finite form to the expansion. We shall now derive the following explicitly finite form.

Theorem 4.7
.

Suppose that
n=2m+1\geqslant 3
is a positive odd integer. Then the Green’s function for the sphere of dimension
2m+1
is

\displaystyle G_{2m+1}(\theta)=\frac{R^{1-2m}}{S_{2m+1}}\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\left(\binom{k-1/2}{k}y^{k}\left(1+(\pi-\theta)\cot{\theta}\right)-\frac{1}{3}\sum_{l=1}^{k}\frac{\binom{k-1/2}{k}}{\binom{l+1/2}{l-1}}y^{k-l}\right),

where
y=\csc^{2}{\theta}
.

Hence the solution can be expressed as an even polynomial in
\cot{\theta}
, added to an odd polynomial in
\cot{\theta}
multiplied by
\pi-\theta
.

Proof.

We shall employ an argument using reduction formulae, based on the contiguous relations for hypergeometric functions.

We consider the hypergeometric expression in
J_{2m+1}
derived in the previous lemma: write

\displaystyle A(m):=-\frac{T}{2m+1}\,{}_{3}F_{2}\biggl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{1,1,3/2}{2,m+3/2};-T\biggr].

By the
{}_{3}F_{2}
contiguous relation

\displaystyle(b-a-1)F=(b-1)F(b^{-})-aF(a^{+}),

where
F=F(a,\dotsc;b,\dotsc;z)

[
12
, equation (15)]
,
we know
A
satisfies

\displaystyle A(m)-A(m-1)=\frac{2T}{(2m+1)(2m-1)}{}_{2}F_{1}\biggl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{1,3/2}{m+3/2};-T\biggr].

Hence we can write down
A(m)
as a sum of hypergeometric functions:

\displaystyle A(m)=A(0)+\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\frac{2T}{(2k+3)(2k+1)}{}_{2}F_{1}\biggl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{1,3/2}{k+5/2};-T\biggr]

(4.8)

and we also find that

\displaystyle A(0)=-\frac{1}{2}\log{(1+T)},

which cancels the other logarithm term, confirming our suspicion of the initial solution.
This is still not obviously finitary. Now consider the term in the sum

\displaystyle B(k):=\frac{2T}{(2k+3)(2k+1)}\,{}_{2}F_{1}\biggl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{1,3/2}{k+5/2};-T\biggr].

We have the other contiguous relation for a hypergeometric function
[
8
, equation (9)]

\displaystyle(1-a+(c-b-1)z)F=(c-a)F(a^{-})-(c-1)(1-z)F(c^{-}),

so after rearranging,
B(k)
satisfies

\displaystyle B(k)=-\frac{2}{(2k+1)(2k)}+y\frac{2k-1}{2k}B(k-1),

(4.9)

where we have written
y=1+1/T=\csc^{2}(\theta)
. We also have

\displaystyle B(0)=2\left(1-\frac{\arctan{\sqrt{T}}}{\sqrt{T}}\right).

Now split
B(k)
as
B(k)=\alpha(k)\beta(k)
, where
\alpha(0)=B(0)
, and
\alpha(k)
solves the homogeneous version of (
4.9
):

\displaystyle\alpha(k)=\frac{2k+1}{2k}y\alpha(k-1).

We can show that

\displaystyle\alpha(k)=y^{k}\binom{k-1/2}{k}B(0)

(for example, by checking the recurrence relation and initial conditions). Now,
\beta
satisfies the recurrence relation

\displaystyle\alpha(k)\beta(k)=-\frac{2}{(2k+1)(2k)}+y\frac{2k-1}{2k}\alpha(k-1)\beta(k-1)=-\frac{2}{(2k+1)(2k)}+\alpha(k)\beta(k-1),

and hence we obtain the simple recurrence for
\beta
:

\displaystyle\beta(k)-\beta(k-1)=-\frac{2}{(2k+1)(2k)\alpha(k)}.

Then, since
\beta(0)=1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\beta(k)=1-\sum_{l=1}^{k}\frac{2}{(2l+1)(2l)\alpha(l)},

and since

\displaystyle(2l+1)(2l)\binom{l-1/2}{l}=3\binom{l+1/2}{l-1},

we find that

\displaystyle B(k)=\alpha(k)-\!\sum_{l=1}^{k}\!\frac{2\alpha(k)}{(2l+1)(2l)\alpha(l)}=2\binom{k\!-\!1/2}{k}y^{k}\!\left(\!1-\frac{\arctan{\sqrt{T}}}{\sqrt{T}}\!\right)\!-\frac{2}{3}\!\sum_{l=1}^{k}\!\frac{\binom{k-1/2}{k}}{\binom{l+1/2}{l-1}}y^{k-l}.

Now we can solve (
4.8
) to obtain

\displaystyle A(m)=-\frac{1}{2}\log{(1+T)}+2\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\left(\binom{k-1/2}{k}y^{k}\left(1-\frac{\arctan{\sqrt{T}}}{\sqrt{T}}\right)-\frac{1}{3}\sum_{l=1}^{k}\frac{\binom{k-1/2}{k}}{\binom{l+1/2}{l-1}}y^{k-l}\right).

One can see from the expansion near
\theta=\pi
that the correct branch of
\arctan{(\tan{\theta})}
to choose is
\theta-\pi
, so we can substitute back to find that

\displaystyle J_{2m+1}(\theta)=\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\left(\binom{k-1/2}{k}y^{k}\left(1+(\pi-\theta)\cot{\theta}\right)-\frac{1}{3}\sum_{l=1}^{k}\frac{\binom{k-1/2}{k}}{\binom{l+1/2}{l-1}}y^{k-l}\right).

Hence,

\displaystyle G_{2m+1}(\theta)=\frac{R^{1-2m}}{S_{2m+1}}\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\left(\binom{k-1/2}{k}y^{k}\left(1+(\pi-\theta)\cot{\theta}\right)-\frac{1}{3}\sum_{l=1}^{k}\frac{\binom{k-1/2}{k}}{\binom{l+1/2}{l-1}}y^{k-l}\right),

as required.
∎

The most singular term in
G_{2m+1}(\theta)
is

\displaystyle\frac{R^{1-2m}}{S_{2m+1}}\frac{1}{2m}\binom{m-3/2}{m-1}\pi\csc^{2m-2}{\theta}\cot{\theta}\sim\frac{1}{S_{2m+1}}\frac{1}{2m}\binom{m-3/2}{m-1}\pi\frac{1}{(R\theta)^{2m-1}},

and then the coefficient of
(R\theta)^{2-(2m+1)}
is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{S_{2m+1}}\frac{1}{2m}\binom{m-3/2}{m-1}\pi

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(m+1)}{2\pi^{m+1}}\frac{\Gamma(m-1/2)\pi}{2m\Gamma(m)\Gamma(1/2)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(m-1/2)}{4\pi^{m+1/2}}=\frac{\Gamma(m+1/2)}{(2m-1)2\pi^{m+1/2}}=\frac{1}{((2m+1)-2)S_{2m}},

as it should be to agree with the flat-space results.

Remark 4.8
.

As in the even-dimensional case, we can compare this with the result in
[
13
]
. The three-dimensional case is given in that paper’s equation (4.23):

\displaystyle\bar{G}_{0}^{(3)}(\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime})=-\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\frac{\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime}}{\sqrt{1-\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime}}}\arccos{(-\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime})}+\text{const}.

Since
\arccos{(-\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime})}=\pi-\theta
, substituting in gives

\displaystyle\bar{G}_{0}^{(3)}(\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime})=-\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}(\pi-\theta)\frac{\cos{\theta}}{\left\lvert\sin{\theta}\right\rvert}+\text{const}=-\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}(\pi-\theta)\cot{\theta}+\text{const},

(4.10)

since
\sin{\theta}\geqslant 0
for
0\leqslant\theta\leqslant\pi
.

Whereas, if we put
m=1
(and
R=1
) in our result we find that

\displaystyle G_{3}(\theta)=\frac{1}{2S_{3}}\binom{-1/2}{0}y^{0}(1+(\pi-\theta)\cot{\theta})-0=\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\left((\pi-\theta)\cot{\theta}+1\right),

which agrees with (
4.10
) when the sign convention difference is understood.

A similar calculation using
[
13
, equation (4.34)]
shows that the five-dimensional results also agree up to a constant, ours being

\displaystyle G_{5}(\theta)=\frac{1}{S_{5}}\left(\frac{1}{8}\big(2+\csc^{2}{\theta}\big)(\pi-\theta)\cot{\theta}+\frac{1}{24}\big(5+3\csc^{2}{\theta}\big)\right).

5
Applications

5.1
The dipole potential on a sphere

In Euclidean space, the conventional way to define a dipole potential from a fundamental solution is by considering two point charges with charge
q
and
-q
at
a
and
P(d)
a distance
d
apart. We then take the limit
q\to\infty
,
d\downarrow 0
, so that
qd
is constant, and the path has a well-defined tangent vector
p
when
d
becomes zero. To understand this on the sphere, it is advantageous to make this a little more formal:

Definition 5.1
.

Let
\gamma\colon(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\to M
be a path on the manifold
M
, with
\gamma(0)=a
and
\dot{\gamma}(0)=p\in T_{a}(M)
. Let
G(x,y)
be the Green’s function for the Laplace–Beltrami operator on
M
. The
dipole potential at
a
with dipole moment
p
is given by

\displaystyle H(x,a,p)=\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{G(x,\gamma(t))-G(x,a)}{t}=p\cdot\nabla_{a}G(x,a).

The limit definition indicates that this describes two point charges of charge
\pm t^{-1}
being brought together; the second equality shows that
H
is well-defined by prescribing
a
and
p
. Moreover, since the total charge is always zero, the dipole potential is also consistent on compact manifolds, such as the hypersphere. Since two fundamental solutions are subtracted from one another, we also lose any dependence on the constant we took in the definition of our particular fundamental solution, so this is in a sense
the
dipole potential.

We therefore need to find the gradient of
G(x,a)
. Since this involves moving the base point of the coordinates, and hence different definitions of the angle
\theta
, it is advantageous to start by recalling that on the sphere of radius
R
,
\theta
can actually be defined as a particular solution to the equation

\displaystyle\cos{\theta}=\frac{x\cdot a}{R^{2}},

since it is the angle between the vectors
x,a\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
. We then find that

\displaystyle\nabla_{a}\theta=\frac{1}{R^{2}\sin{\theta}}x,

and so the chain rule gives

\displaystyle p\cdot\nabla_{a}G(x,a)=G_{n}^{\prime}(\theta)(p\cdot\nabla_{a})\theta=\frac{G_{n}^{\prime}(\theta)}{R^{2}\sin{\theta}}(p\cdot x),

and using the group of rotations on the hypersphere, we can always use the spherical symmetry to place
a
,
p
and
x
in the same three-dimensional space, with
a
along the axis
\theta=0
and
p
in the plane
\theta=\phi=0
, so that
p\cdot x=R\left\lvert p\right\rvert\sin{\theta}\cos{\phi}
, and then

\displaystyle p\cdot\nabla_{a}G(x,a)=\frac{G_{n}^{\prime}(\theta)}{R}\left\lvert p\right\rvert\cos{\phi}.

We should now verify that this function has the correct behaviour as
R\to\infty
, in that it becomes a dipole at the origin in the Euclidean space with distance
s=R\theta
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{R}G_{n}^{\prime}(\theta)\left\lvert p\right\rvert\cos{\phi}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left\lvert p\right\rvert\cos{\phi}}{S_{n}R^{n-1}}\csc^{n-1}(\theta)\int_{\theta}^{\pi}\sin^{n-1}{\theta^{\prime}}\,d\theta^{\prime}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{\left\lvert p\right\rvert\cos{\phi}}{S_{n}(R\theta)^{n-1}}\frac{\pi^{1/2}\Gamma(n/2)}{\Gamma((n+1)/2)}=\frac{s^{1-n}\left\lvert p\right\rvert\cos{\phi}}{S_{n-1}},

similarly to the calculation in Section
3.3
. It is easy to check that the dipole potential at the origin in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is given by

\displaystyle\left.(p\cdot\nabla_{a})\frac{\left\lvert x-a\right\rvert^{2-n}}{(n-2)S_{n-1}}\right\rvert_{a=0}=\frac{\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{-n}p\cdot x}{S_{n-1}}=\frac{s^{1-n}\left\lvert p\right\rvert\cos{\phi}}{S_{n-1}},

with which the limit agrees.

5.2
Fourier expansion of our fundamental solution on
\boldsymbol{S^{2}}

The form of the
n=2
result means that we can quite easily derive an azimuthal Fourier expansion. We follow
[
4
]
, with some simplifications. We have

\displaystyle\cos{d(x,x^{\prime})}=\cos{\theta}\cos{\theta^{\prime}}+\sin{\theta}\sin{\theta^{\prime}}\cos{(\phi-\phi^{\prime})},

with
x\!=\!(\cos{\phi}\sin{\theta},\sin{\phi}\sin{\theta},\cos{\theta})
in standard spherical coordinates (and similarly
x^{\prime}
),
d(x,x^{\prime})
the spherical distance between them, and inserting this into our fundamental solution gives

\displaystyle G_{2}(x,x^{\prime})=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\log{\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\cos{\theta}\cos{\theta^{\prime}}-\sin{\theta}\sin{\theta^{\prime}}\cos{(\phi-\phi^{\prime})}\right)}.

We can find the expansion of this quite easily using the following:

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
A>B>0
and
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Then

\displaystyle-\log{(A+B\cos{t})}=\log{\frac{2\big(A-\sqrt{A^{2}-B^{2}}\big)}{B^{2}}}+2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\cos{kt}}{k}\left(\frac{\sqrt{A^{2}-B^{2}}-A}{B}\right)^{k}.

(5.1)

Proof.

We begin with the well-known formula

\displaystyle-\log{(1+r^{2}-2r\cos{t})}=2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{r^{k}\cos{kt}}{k},\qquad\left\lvert r\right\rvert<1,

which can by proven by applying Euler’s formula to the right-hand side and computing the sum directly. We then have

\displaystyle-\log{\left(1-\frac{2r}{1+r^{2}}\cos{t}\right)}=\log\big(1+r^{2}\big)+2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{r^{k}\cos{kt}}{k},

5.3
A fundamental solution on
\boldsymbol{{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}^{n}}

The real projective space
{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}^{n}
can be considered as the quotient of the sphere
S^{n}
by the group
\{1,-1\}
, where
-1
is the antipodal map
x\mapsto-x
, isomorphic to
Z_{2}
:
{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}^{n}\cong S^{n}/Z_{2}
. This is realised in our spherical coordinates by associating the points

\displaystyle(\theta_{i},\varphi)\sim(\pi-\theta_{i},\pm\pi+\varphi).

Therefore our fundamental solution for
S^{n}
can be made into one for
{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}^{n}
by setting

\displaystyle G_{{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}^{n}}(x,x^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}(G_{S^{n}}(x,x^{\prime})+G_{S^{n}}(-x,x^{\prime})),

or in terms of angles,

\displaystyle G_{{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}^{n}}(\theta_{1})=\frac{1}{2}(G_{S^{n}}(\theta_{1})+G_{S^{n}}(\pi-\theta_{1})).

This clearly has all the correct properties of a fundamental solution, such as matching singularities and smoothness.

5.4
Interpretation of prior results

In this section we shall discuss the results of
[
2
]
, and in particular, how our construction enables a consistent explanation of some of the properties of their solution. We shall apply a similar procedure to the previous section, but consider the functions odd under the group action.

Consider first the equation

\displaystyle-\Delta{G(x,x^{\prime})}=\delta(x,x^{\prime})-\delta(-x,x^{\prime});

(5.4)

this is consistent on the sphere, because the integral of the right-hand side is zero. It is essentially the opposite limit of the two point charge case to the dipole considered above: the separation of the charges is now the largest possible. Linearity allows us to conclude that

\displaystyle\tilde{G}(x,x^{\prime}):=G(x,x^{\prime})-G(-x,x^{\prime})

(5.5)

is a solution to (
5.4
), where
G
is the generalised Green’s function discussed in Section
2
, since

\displaystyle-\Delta{\tilde{G}(x,x^{\prime})}=\big(\delta(x,x^{\prime})-(S_{n})^{-1}\big)-\big(\delta(-x,x^{\prime})-(S_{n})^{-1}\big)=\delta(x,x^{\prime})-\delta(-x,x^{\prime}).

In fact, (
5.4
) is the equation that the Green’s function found in
[
2
]
satisfies: to see this we only have to observe its oddness under the transformation
x\mapsto-x
, or in our terms,
\theta\mapsto\pi-\theta
. This is readily apparent if we consider
the form
[
2
, equation (29)]

\displaystyle\cos{\theta}\,{}_{2}F_{1}\biggl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{1/2,n/2}{3/2};\cos^{2}{\theta}\biggr].

Hence it must have a singularity at
\theta=\pi
of the same nature and opposite sign as it does at
\theta=0
, where it was designed to have a singularity that produces a
\delta
-function, so it must satisfy (
5.4
).

An instructive and simple example is given by the nontrivial case in two dimensions: we have

\displaystyle G(x,x^{\prime})-G(-x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi}\log{\csc^{2}{\tfrac{1}{2}\theta}}-\frac{1}{4\pi}\log{\csc^{2}{\tfrac{1}{2}(\pi-\theta)}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\log{\sin^{2}{\tfrac{1}{2}\theta}}+\frac{1}{4\pi}\log{\cos^{2}{\tfrac{1}{2}\theta}}=\frac{1}{4\pi}\log{\cot^{2}{\tfrac{1}{2}\theta}},

which is precisely the dimension-two Green’s function calculated in
[
2
]
. It also follows immediately that this satisfies (
5.4
).

We now proceed to prove the relationship in the general case: we recall that our integral, from (
4.1
), is given by

\displaystyle J_{n}(\theta)=\int_{\theta}^{\pi}\csc^{n-1}{\phi}\int_{\phi}^{\pi}\sin^{n-1}{\psi}\,d\psi\,d\phi,

and Cohl’s is

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{n}(\theta):=\int_{\theta}^{\pi/2}\frac{dx}{\sin^{n-1}{x}},

up to a constant.

Proposition 5.4
.

For
\theta\in(0,\pi)
, we have

\displaystyle J_{n}(\theta)-J_{n}(\pi-\theta)=\frac{\pi^{1/2}\Gamma(n/2)}{\Gamma((n+1)/2)}\mathcal{I}_{n}(\theta)

(5.6)

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle J_{n}(\theta)=\int_{\theta}^{\pi}\csc^{n-1}{\phi}\left(\int_{\pi/2}^{\pi}\sin^{n-1}{\psi}\,d\psi+\int_{\phi}^{\pi/2}\sin^{n-1}{\psi}\,d\psi\right)d\phi,

so the difference is, using
\int_{a}^{b}=\int_{a}^{c}+\int_{c}^{b}
,

\displaystyle J_{n}(\theta)-J_{n}(\pi-\theta)=\int_{\theta}^{\pi-\theta}\csc^{n-1}{\phi}\left(\int_{\pi/2}^{\pi}\sin^{n-1}{\psi}\,d\psi+\int_{\phi}^{\pi/2}\sin^{n-1}{\psi}\,d\psi\right)d\phi,

valid for
0<\theta<\pi
when we recall the standard definition
\int_{a}^{b}=-\int_{b}^{a}
if
b<a
.
The first integral in the bracket is the same as

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\sin^{n-1}{\psi}\,d\psi=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(n/2)}{2\Gamma((n+1)/2)}

using the Beta-function; the second is odd about
\phi=\pi/2
. A simple way to see this is to change variables and notice that
\cos^{n-1}{x}
is an even function, so it has an odd antiderivative. Since
\csc^{n-1}{\phi}
is even about
\phi=\pi/2
(again, a change of variables will show this easily), the second term’s integrand is odd overall, and hence the integral over a region symmetric about
\phi=\pi/2
is zero. We are therefore left with

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(n/2)}{2\Gamma((n+1)/2)}\int_{\theta}^{\pi-\theta}\csc^{n-1}{\phi}\,d\phi;

this time, since the integrand is even, the answer is double the integral from
\theta
to
\pi/2
, which gives the result.
∎

With that resolved, we just have to check the constants. In our case,

\displaystyle G_{n}(\theta)=\frac{\Gamma((n+1)/2)}{2\pi^{(n+1)/2}R^{n-2}}J_{n}(\theta).

Using (
5.6
), we have

\displaystyle G_{n}(\theta)-G_{n}(\pi-\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi^{1/2}\Gamma(n/2)}{\Gamma((n+1)/2)}(J_{n}(\theta)-J_{n}(\pi-\theta))

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi^{1/2}\Gamma(n/2)}{\Gamma((n+1)/2)}\frac{\Gamma((n+1)/2)}{2\pi^{(n+1)/2}R^{n-2}}\mathcal{I}_{n}=\frac{\Gamma(n/2)}{2\pi^{n/2}R^{n-2}}\mathcal{I}_{n},

which is the same as Cohl’s solution. Hence the function in Cohl’s paper is related to ours in the way we specified in (
5.5
).
