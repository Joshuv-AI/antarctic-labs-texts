GlyphPattern: An Abstract Pattern Recognition for Vision-Language Models
Zixuan Wu, Yoolim Kim, Carolyn Jane Anderson

Abstract
Vision-Language Models (VLMs) building upon the foundation of powerful large language models have made rapid progress in reasoning across visual and textual data. While VLMs perform well on vision tasks that they are trained on, our results highlight key challenges in abstract pattern recognition. We present GlyphPattern, a 954 item dataset that pairs 318 human-written descriptions of visual patterns from 40 writing systems with three visual presentation styles. GlyphPattern evaluates abstract pattern recognition in VLMs, requiring models to understand and judge natural language descriptions of visual patterns. GlyphPattern patterns are drawn from a large-scale cognitive science investigation of human writing systems; as a result, they are rich in spatial reference and compositionality. Our experiments show that GlyphPattern is challenging for state-of-the-art VLMs (GPT-4o achieves only 55% accuracy), with marginal gains from few-shot prompting. Our detailed error analysis reveals challenges at multiple levels, including visual processing, natural language understanding, and pattern generalization.

Introduction

A key aspect of intelligence is the ability to recognize abstract patterns.
Bongard 1967
proposed a task to measure abstract pattern recognition in artificial intelligence using a kind of partitioned diagram, now known as a
Bongard problem
. Each diagram show two sets of items; the goal is to describe the pattern that distinguishes them. Bongard problems have inspired a number of visual benchmarks
(
Nie et al. 2020
;
Jiang et al. 2022
;
Spratley, Ehinger, and Miller 2023
;
Wu et al. 2024
)
. However, existing benchmarks are easier than classical Bongard problems because they do not involve a natural language description of the pattern.

We present GlyphPattern, an abstract pattern recognition benchmark for vision-language models (VLMs) that challenges models to understand natural language pattern descriptions. GlyphPattern tests the ability of VLMs to recognize visual properties that distinguish two groups of symbols, pairing together human-written and human-verified patterns with three different visual styles designed to test the robustness of abstract pattern recognition in VLMs, for a benchmark of 954 items.

We use GlyphPattern to test the pattern recognition ability of six VLMs in a multiple choice task. Our zero-shot results show that this is a challenging task for all models: the state-of-the-art GPT-4o model achieves only 55.6% accuracy. We find that although few-shot prompting does not help GPT-4o, it enables Gemini 1.5 to match GPT-4o’s performance. For the best-performing models, we also explore a free response format and find that models generate valid rules less than 16% of the time.

GlyphPattern’s rich human-written pattern set and multiple visual styles provide insight into the impact of different aspects of the task, including the length and syntactic complexity of the rules, the visual saliency of different writing systems, and the visual presentation style. Our fine-grained error analysis reveals that models “fail to see” key portions of the image and fail to understand certain terms in the natural language descriptions. We hope that GlyphPattern will guide improvements in VLMs by highlighting weaknesses in both the vision and language capabilities of current models. Our key contributions are as follows:

•

A 954 item abstract pattern recognition benchmark for VLMs based on human-written descriptions of patterns in visual properties of writing system characters.

•

Benchmarking results for six VLMs, showing that GlyphPattern is challenging for state-of-the-art models: Gemini 1.5 and GPT-4o achieve accuracy around 55%.

•

A comparison of zero-shot, few-shot, and free response results for the two best-performing models.

•

A detailed analysis of factors that impact model performance, including image style, pattern complexity, and human-identified cross-linguistic pattern types.

•

An exploration of error sources, revealing mistakes that arise at multiple stages, including image-processing, language understanding, and abstract pattern reasoning.

2
Related Work

There is a long history of assessing intelligence through the ability to recognize abstract patterns, both in non-verbal tests of human intelligence and in AI.
Bongard 1967
’s influential work on pattern recognition in AI proposed a test using a diagram that partitions items into two groups. So-called
Bongard problems
evaluate an agent’s few-shot pattern recognition ability: the goal is to recognize and describe the pattern from a handful of examples.

2.1
Abstract Pattern Recognition Benchmarks

General-purpose visual understanding requires the ability to recognize patterns from a handful of examples. A number of abstract pattern recognition benchmarks for few-shot image classification have been inspired by intelligence tests.
Barrett et al. 2018
adapt IQ test items to measure few-shot pattern recognition in computer vision models. The model is prompted with a grid of example images, and asked to select the next member of the sequence.
Zhang et al. 2019
and
Teney et al. 2020
use a similar format inspired by Raven’s Progressive Matrices
(
Raven 1936
)
.
Zerroug et al. 2022
, by contrast, use an odd-one-out classification task.

There are several Bongard-inspired image classification benchmarks.
Nie et al. 2020
propose Bongard-Logo, a benchmark where models classify images as members or non-members of a pattern set. Bongard-HOI
(
Jiang et al. 2022
)
and Bongard-OpenWorld
(
Wu et al. 2024
)
use similar formats but with real-world images. Most similar to our work,
Spratley, Ehinger, and Miller 2023
present a dataset of Unicode character patterns; unlike our patterns, which are easily solved by humans, their automatically-identified patterns have only a 55% human solver success rate.

These benchmarks are a simplification of Bongard’s original task, because they test whether the model can
continue
a pattern rather than
identify
it. Our GlyphPattern benchmark comes closer by challenging VLMs to understand and even produce natural language descriptions of abstract patterns.

2.2
Benchmarking Vision-Language Models

Our focus is on benchmarking VLMs, which may struggle with image processing, natural language understanding, or relating the two modalities.

Compositionality in VLMs

An important feature of GlyphPattern is that its human-written patterns are linguistically rich in compositionality and spatial relations. Compositionality is a key evaluation area for VLMs. Existing benchmarks explore compositionality in object attributes
(
Ray et al. 2023
)
, spatial relations
(
Liu, Emerson, and Collier 2023
;
Kamath, Hessel, and Chang 2023
)
, and visual concepts
(
Suhr et al. 2019
;
Ma et al. 2023
)
. A popular and challenging benchmark is Winoground
(
Thrush et al. 2022
)
, which targets linguistic compositionality with pairs of captions containing identical words in different syntactic structures.
Diwan et al. 2022
argue that it is text-image linking that makes Winoground challenging.

Abstract Pattern Recognition in VLMs

The increasing prominence of LLM-based systems has led to widespread interest in multimodality. However, recent work on abstract pattern recognition reveals surprising weaknesses of VLMs.
Ji et al. 2022
find poor zero-shot performance on tangram puzzle-based tasks.
Rahmanzadehgervi et al. 2024
show that VLMs struggle with extremely easy visual reasoning tasks, like judging if circles overlap, which they attribute to visual processing issues.
Wu, Brantley, and Artzi 2024
ask VLMs to verify captions for displays of geometric shapes from the NLVR dataset
(
Suhr et al. 2017
)
, and find that even state-of-the-art models perform little better than randomly.

Our findings converge with and expand on
Wu, Brantley, and Artzi 2024
’s findings: using a different task and data domain, we find similar weaknesses, including inconsistent gains from few-shot prompting. However, our fine-grained analysis allows us to dig deeper into the sources of errors.

Computer Vision and Writing Systems

GlyphPattern items come from a diverse set of human writing systems. There is previous work on computer vision systems for recognizing characters in writing systems, including
Magre and Brown 2022
’s MNIST
(
Deng 2012
)
inspired dataset and
Lake, Salakhutdinov, and Tenenbaum 2015
’s work on discovering schemas for recognizing characters. Our goal is to probe abstract pattern recognition in VLMs, rather than to improve their ability to transcribe writing systems.

3
Dataset

GlyphPattern uses patterns from human writing systems to benchmark abstract pattern recognition in VLMs. It pairs 318 human-written descriptions of patterns that partition characters in 40 human writing systems with three different visual presentation styles, for a total of 954 items.

3.1
Online Data Collection

We draw on human-written patterns collected as part of the online applet
Glyph
created by the Minds and Traditions Max Planck Independent Research Group, which explores cross-linguistic universals in the visual properties of human writing systems. We use the publicly-available data collected from the online applet. In
Glyph
, players select a subset of characters in an unfamiliar writing system and write a description of the pattern that separates the selected characters (the
included characters
) from the rest of the script (the
excluded characters
). Players must verify their rule by re-selecting the included characters after at least three minutes using their previously written rule.

3.2
Data Cleaning

We use a subset of 318 filtered, validated, and standardized player-written rules produced by the
Glyph
team. Rules were independently validated by five researchers, who also identified redundant rules and grouped the rules into categories. Rules were then summarized by ChatGPT in order to standardize wording. We use this publicly-available subset of rules, but have an additional rewording step so that all rules are compatible with our prompt prefixes.

3.3
Image Construction

The Glyph dataset associates each rule with a script and a partition of characters. We retrieve the Unicode symbols for the included and excluded characters, and generate images in three visual styles (Figure
2
). In the
color
style, the included characters are shown in red, while the excluded are black. In the
left-right
style, a dashed line partitions the image into two halves. The included characters are on the right side and the excluded on the left. In the
circle
style, the characters are arranged along the circumference of a circle: the included characters are inside and the excluded outside.

Exploring three ways of visualizing the partition allows us to measure how sensitive VLMs are to visual properties of the images. If a model performs well on one style but not the others, it would indicate that the model understands the pattern, but fails to apply it to certain kinds of images.

3.4
Benchmark Construction

We present GlyphPattern as a multiple choice Visual Question Answering task. Given an image and a multiple choice question with four pattern options, the goal is to pick the pattern that explains which characters are included and excluded. We randomly sample the three other answer options and manually verify that they are not correct patterns for the image, replacing them if so.

4.2
Multiple Choice Experiments

We benchmark all models using the multiple choice format of GlyphPattern. We use a chat template for chat models, with a system prompt explaining the task. For non-chat models, we suffix the instructions to the prompt.

Answers-Only Baseline

In multiple choice tasks, information about the correct answer may be gleaned from the answer options. We measure this in a pre-experiment: we prompt GPT-4o with the answer choices alone, excluding the question and using an empty image. Accuracy is close to random. We refer to this as the
Answers-Only
baseline.

Zero-Shot Prompting

We conduct zero-shot experiments by presenting the model with question and answer options, followed by the image.

Few-Shot Prompting

For models that support few-shot prompting, we explore prompting with one, three, and five examples. We format the few-shot prompt as a multi-turn message series. To avoid biasing the model, we select as examples only patterns that do not reoccur with other writing systems.

\alpha
=0.05 using mixed-effects logistic regression models for each VLM (see Supplementary Materials).

5.2
Does Few-shot Prompting Help?

Table
1

5.3
Can Models Describe Patterns?

The multiple choice version of GlyphPattern explores whether VLMs can
understand and assess
patterns described in natural language. We can also use GlyphPattern to assess whether models can
describe
patterns.

We explore the pattern description ability of the two best-performing VLMs, GPT-4o and Gemini 1.5, in a generation task. We prompt models with three image/pattern pairs and ask them to produce a rule for a fourth image. The authors independently assessed the validity of the patterns, with inter-annotator agreement of
\kappa
=0.89 for GPT-4o and 0.88 for Gemini 1.5.

Table

Manual inspection reveals that models typically generate patterns similar to human ones: both models generated rules related to enclosed spaces, symmetry, and line type, which are common in GlyphPattern. Despite generating human-like rules, models struggle to apply them. This points to issues in pattern recognition or in text/image mapping.

6
Exploring Model Errors

We have shown that GlyphPattern is a challenging benchmark for VLMs. The rich human-written rules and multiple visual styles of GlyphPattern allow us to explore how various aspects of the task impact performance. In this section, we highlight key challenges that VLMs face in abstract pattern recognition, focusing on the two best-performing models, Gemini 1.5 and GPT-4o.

6.1
Are Complex Descriptions Harder?

One potential source of error is in understanding textual descriptions of patterns. A model may be able to correctly distinguish visual properties of included versus excluded characters, but fail to understand how they are described in text. To explore this possibility, we investigate the
complexity
of the pattern descriptions. Are simpler descriptions easier?

Figure

plots the performance of the two best-performing models against the number of characters in each rule as a proxy for complexity. Contrary to expectations, we observe that accuracy increases slightly with description length, suggesting that longer patterns are not more challenging. Mixed-effects models reveal a weak but statistically reliable correlation for both Gemini 1.5 (
\widehat{\beta}
=0.003;
p
=0.001) and GPT-4o (
\widehat{\beta}
=0.003;
p
=0.002).

Another possibility is that models struggle with compositional language in the patterns. We quantify the syntactic complexity of each rule as the maximum depth of their dependency parse tree. Figure

6.2
Do Models Understand Cross-linguistically Common Patterns?

Figure 7:
Accuracy by writing system.

The
Glyph
project aims to explore cross-linguistic universals in visual properties of human writing systems. Towards this end, they have identified categories of rules that players commonly propose across languages. These categories represent visual properties of writing systems that are salient for humans. Are they also visually salient to VLMs?

Figure
6
plots performance by rule category for the two best-performing models. We observe significant variation in accuracy by rule category, showing that many rule categories commonly identified by humans pose challenges for models.

The easiest kind of patterns for GPT-4o involve diagonal lines and specific character mentions: for instance,
Resemble the letter E
. Compared to rules that did not belong to a category, accuracy was higher in these categories (diagonal:
\widehat{\beta}
=0.46 and
p
=0.002; specific: (
\widehat{\beta}
=0.37 and
p
<0.001). For Gemini 1.5, the easiest patterns were ones referring to vertical (
\widehat{\beta}
=0.28;
p
=0.009) lines.

One surprising finding is the large difference for both models between rules that refer to diagonal and vertical lines versus horizontal lines. Accuracy was low in the horizontal category for both models, though only significantly different for GPT-4o (Gemini 1.5:
\widehat{\beta}
=-0.08 and
p
=0.45; GPT-4o:
\widehat{\beta}
=-0.23;
p
=0.03). It is not clear why this line orientation is so much more challenging than the others.

It is less surprising that models struggle with the Stroke category, which consists of rules that describe how many pen strokes it would take to draw the character. These rules require grounding in the experience of drawing letters by hand, which VLMs lack. Our results show that symmetry is also a difficult property for both models.

6.3
What Kinds of Patterns Do Models Generate?

Our free response experiment allows us to examine what kinds of patterns Gemini 1.5 and GPT-4o are most successful at generating. As discussed in Section
5.3
, models often generate rules using similar language as humans even though their rules are usually incorrect for the target image.

We categorize the valid patterns generated by each model according to the cross-linguistically common categories from Section
6.2
(full results in Supplementary Materials). We find that the most common correctly generated patterns are ones that refer to enclosed spaces or specific characters. Gemini 1.5 is more successful at identifying symmetry than GPT-4o. Qualitatively, we note that models seem to struggle especially to generate relative spatial terms. There are many generated patterns that would be correct if “horizontal” and “vertical” or “left” and “right” were swapped. This suggests that relative spatial language remains a challenge. Alternatively, these struggles could spring from a loss of spatial information in image-processing.

6.5
How Does Reasoning Go Awry?

When VLMs make mistakes, they can stem from issues in natural language understanding, image processing, or the vision-language mapping. To explore sources of error in GlyphPattern decisions, we prompt Gemini 1.5 and GPT-4o to explain their reasoning for the multiple-choice task and manually inspect the results. We find issues stemming from multiple aspects of the task:

Errors in image-processing

In one case, GPT-4o correctly identifies the four excluded characters when they are presented in the color style. However, in the circle style, GPT-4o lists all 22 characters in the script as falling inside the circle, indicating an error in recognizing the circle itself (see Supplementary Materials). This is similar to the image-processing errors in
Rahmanzadehgervi et al. 2024
.

Errors in natural language understanding

In other cases, models fail to understand the language that human pattern writers use. For the example in Figure
8
, GPT-4o is able to map the included characters to their Unicode symbols and identifies that these characters contain loops. However, GPT-4o rejects the correct option because it does not understand “closed end” as the human writer intended. Gemini 1.5 also misunderstands this description and incorrectly identifies it as applying to multiple characters in the excluded set.

Target Pattern
:
Characters that have exactly one closed end.

Gemini 1.5 Response
:
The characters ‘S’, ‘2’, ‘C’, and ‘3’ have exactly one closed end, but they are not colored red.

GPT-4o Response
:
The red characters do not have exactly one closed end. They have loops, which means they have more than one closed end.

Example of natural language understanding failure

Text/image mapping issues

Models sometimes fail to map the properties described in natural language to the character images. For the example in Figure
9
with a curved bottom pattern, Gemini 1.5 correctly picks out a red character that is similar to “O”, but does not seem to understand the visual properties of the character or its Latin equivalent. GPT-4o makes a similar mistake for the same pattern, incorrectly deciding that the “L”-like character lacks a curved bottom but does have “enclosed spaces or loops” (one of the distractor options). Here, the models either fail to understand the property description, or to map it to the visual properties of the characters.

Target Pattern
:
Characters that have a curved bottom.

Gemini 1.5 Response
:
While some red characters have curved bottoms, others do not. For example, the red character ”O” does not have a curved bottom.

GPT-4o Response
:
Not all red characters have a curved bottom. For example, the character in second row, second column from the left (which looks like an upside-down ”L”) does not have a curved bottom.

Example of incorrect character property reasoning

Reasoning errors

Other errors arise from failures to reason about the excluded characters. For instance, GPT-4o chooses the pattern
Characters that look like Latin capital letters
for the item in Figure
2
. Its explanation (
The characters on the right side, “U” and “W”, look like Latin capital letters
) correctly judges that the included characters fit the pattern, but fails consider that excluded characters do too.

Thus, GlyphPattern shows that VLM errors arise across the image-processing, text-image mapping, and reasoning aspects of the abstract pattern recognition task.

7
Limitations

Our work reveals weaknesses in abstract pattern recognition for VLMs. GlyphPattern moves closer to Bongard’s original task by requiring models to understand natural language pattern descriptions; however, evaluating the ability to describe patterns is challenging due to the lack of automated scoring.

8
Conclusion

We present GlyphPattern, an abstract pattern recognition benchmark consisting of 954 items across three visual presentation styles. GlyphPattern challenges models to understand visually-presented patterns paired with human-validated natural language descriptions, revealing significant gaps in VLM capabilities. We find that even the best performing models, Gemini 1.5 and GPT-4o, solve only a little more than half of GlyphPattern problems.

Because GlyphPattern is based on human-written descriptions of visual properties of writing systems, it facilitates exploration of different aspects of the abstract pattern recognition task: from pattern length, complexity, and visual presentation style, to differences across cross-linguistically common pattern types and human writing systems. Our detailed error analysis reveals that errors arise at multiple levels of processing, including image-processing, natural language understanding, and reasoning. We hope that the GlyphPattern benchmark will serve to guide future work on improving pattern recognition in VLMs by providing a challenging, targeted and diverse evaluation set.

Appendix A
Ethical Statement

Our work does not involve any data collection from human subjects. We rely on publicly-available human data from the Glyph project, which was originally collected under the oversight of the Ethikkommission affiliated with Universitätsklinikum, ensuring adherence to ethical guidelines and standards.

Our findings have implications for the visual salience of character sets across human writing systems, and we hope that they complement ongoing cognitive scientific efforts to understand the visual properties of human writing systems. Although our results show that some writing systems are more challenging for VLMs, this should not be misinterpreted to suggest that some writing systems are better or more efficient than others.
