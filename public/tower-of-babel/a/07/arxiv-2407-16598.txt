A tale of three approaches: dynamical phase transitions for weakly bound Brownian particles
Lucianno Defaveri, Eli Barkai, David A. Kessler

Abstract
We investigate a system of Brownian particles weakly bound by attractive parity-symmetric potentials that grow at large distances as V ⁡ ( x ) ∼ | x | α V(x)\sim|x|^{\alpha} , with 0 < α < 1 0<\alpha<1 . The probability density function P ⁡ ( x , t ) P(x,t) at long times reaches the Boltzmann-Gibbs equilibrium state, with all moments finite. However, the system’s relaxation is not exponential, as is usual for a confining system with a well-defined equilibrium, but instead follows a stretched exponential e − const ​ t ν e^{-\mathrm{const}\,t^{\nu}} with exponent ν = α / ( 2 + α ) \nu=\alpha/(2+\alpha) . This problem is studied from three perspectives. First, we propose a straightforward and general scaling rate-function solution for P ⁡ ( x , t ) P(x,t) . This rate-function, which is an important tool from large deviation theory, also displays anomalous time scaling and a dynamical phase transition. Second, through the eigenfunctions of the Fokker-Planck operator, we obtain, using the WKB method, more complete solutions that reproduce the rate function approach. Finally, we show how the alternative path-integral formalism allows us to recover the same results, with the above rate-function being the solution of the classical Hamilton-Jacobi equation describing the most probable path. Properties such as parity, the role of initial conditions, and the dynamical phase transition are thoroughly studied in all three approaches.



I
Introduction

Brownian particles in contact with a heat bath at temperature
T
that are also subject to a binding potential field
V(x)
will at long times reach a stationary equilibrium state. In this equilibrium state, the particles attain the standard Boltzmann-Gibbs expression for the probability density function
P(x,t)
, that is,

P(x,t\to\infty)=P_{\text{eq}}(x)=\frac{1}{Z}e^{-\frac{V(x)}{k_{B}T}}\,,

(1)

where
k_{B}T
is the temperature times the Boltzmann constant and
Z
is the Boltzmann-Gibbs normalizing partition function

\displaystyle Z=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{V(x)}{k_{B}T}}\,dx\,.

(2)

In this work, we consider a class of even potentials behaving for large
x
as
V(x)\approx V_{0}\,|x/\ell|^{\alpha}
. Here,
\alpha
denotes the scaling exponent,
V_{0}
is a positive constant indicating the strength of the potential, and
\ell
represents a characteristic length scale. The time-dependent probability density of the aforementioned Brownian particles is described by the Fokker-Planck equation (FPE),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}P(x,t)

\displaystyle=D\left\{\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}P(x,t)-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left[\frac{F(x)}{k_{B}T}P(x,t)\right]\right\}\,,

(3)

where we have the force
F(x)=-V^{\prime}(x)
and
D
is the diffusion coefficient.

When
\alpha>1
, the system exponentially relaxes towards the Boltzmann-Gibbs equilibrium state in Eq. (
1
). The rate of relaxation is governed by the first non-zero eigenvalue associated with the Fokker-Planck operator (see Eq. (
20
) below). For such systems, the eigenvalue spectrum is discrete
[
1
]
, starting at 0, which is the time-independent equilibrium state, as in Eq. (
1
). We note for future reference that odd/even observables will, at long times, relax to their equilibrium values at different rates, governed respectively by the lowest nonzero odd/even eigenvalue.

If
\alpha<0
, the potential is not binding, the partition function in Eq. (
2
) diverges and the equilibrium state is non-normalizable. Despite this, the Boltzmann-Gibbs factor
e^{-V(x)/k_{B}T}
plays a key role in calculating averages, and such systems have been extensively studied using tools from infinite ergodic theory
[
2
,
3
]
.

The logarithmic potential limit is obtained when
\alpha\to 0
(and
V_{0}=\tilde{V}_{0}/\alpha\to\infty
), and so
V(x)\sim\tilde{V}_{0}\ln|x/\ell|

[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
]
. These systems possess a normalizable equilibrium state for
\tilde{V}_{0}/k_{B}T>1
, with a power-law tail, leading to anomalous statistics and higher-order moments that grow as various powers of the time. This interesting case has also been studied extensively
[
8
]
due to its numerous applications to optical lattices and long-range systems. For smaller
V_{0}
, on the other hand, the equilibrium state is non-normalizable and the behavior is similar to the
\alpha<0
case.

In this paper, we consider the case of
0<\alpha<1
. For concreteness, we will consider the family of potentials

\displaystyle V(x)

\displaystyle=V_{0}\left(1+\frac{x^{2}}{\ell^{2}}\right)^{a/2}\,,

(4)

though the results extend directly to the general case of even potentials which behave as
V(x)\sim x^{\alpha}(1+{\cal O}(1/x))
as
x\to\infty
. For this range of
\alpha
, we have that the potential is confining as
V(x\to\infty)\to\infty
. However, the force, which behaves as
F(x)=-\alpha(V_{0}/\ell)/(x/\ell)^{1-\alpha}
decays to zero. This means that the particles will experience two distinct regions, a binding region for
x
of order
\ell
, where the potential is strongly felt and a free region for large
x
, where the force is negligible and the motion approximates that of a freely diffusing particle. We present a schematic representation in Fig.
1
. For the sake of simplicity, throughout this paper, we will rescale position and time to dimensionless variables by using
x\to x/\ell
and
t\to Dt/\ell^{2}
.

We shall see that for the family of potentials in Eq. (
4
), the spectrum
\lambda_{k}
of the relevant Fokker-Planck operator is mixed. As is the case of the logarithmic potential for
\tilde{V_{0}}/kT>1
, it has a continuous spectrum extending from zero along with a discrete normalized ground-state at eigenvalue zero. As opposed to the logarithmic case, however, for
0<\alpha<1
the dominant contribution to
P(x,t)
for large times comes not from around
\lambda_{k}=0
, but rather from the vicinity of a finite
\lambda_{k*}(t)
which scales as a negative power of the time. This leads to a stretched-exponential relaxation to equilibrium
[
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
]
. The existence of a continuum of modes has been long understood as a prerequisite for stretched-exponential behavior. However, it is not sufficient, as the logarithmic potential case demonstrates. The class of weak power-law potentials thus forms an interesting and natural example for stretched exponential relaxation. Interestingly, we will show that this relaxation is related to a dynamical phase transition.

Specific cases of this class of potentials have been studied to model the effective dynamics of the radius
r
of spherical droplets in Ising ferromagnets bellow critical temperatures
[
14
,
15
]
. The effective dynamics of the radius follows a Langevin equation, with the effective potential scaling with the radius as
r^{2/3}
(
\alpha=2/3
) for

r
(
\alpha=1
) for
d=3

II
Scaling solution

In order to obtain a complete time-dependent solution of the FPE, Eq. (
3
), we must make use of methods such as the eigenfunction approach, which is a difficult task. While the eigenfunction approach, which we will pursue in detail in the next section, is a viable method in the long-time limit, a scaling approach offers a simpler way to express the long-time PDF.
For long times, we can define a new scaled position variable
z\equiv x/t^{\gamma}
as we define in Eq. (
5
), and assume
[
37
]

\displaystyle P(x,t)\sim e^{-t^{\nu}\mathcal{I}(z)}\,,

(6)

where
\nu
and
\gamma
are exponents to be determined and
\mathcal{I}(z)
plays the role of a rate function in the large-deviation formalism
[
38
,
16
]
. However, since
\nu\neq 1
and
\gamma\neq 1
, the rate function will be termed anomalous
[
21
,
30
]
. By plugging Eq. (
6
) into the FPE (
3
) and considering only the leading terms in
1/t
, we obtain for the time derivative

\displaystyle\frac{1}{P(x,t)}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}P(x,t)\to\frac{z\gamma\mathcal{I}^{\prime}(z)-\nu\mathcal{I}(z)}{t^{1-\nu}}\,,

(7)

and for the Laplacian term

\displaystyle\frac{1}{P(x,t)}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}P(x,t)\to\frac{\mathcal{I}^{\prime}(z)^{2}}{t^{2\gamma-2\nu}}\,,

(8)

and finally, for the force field term

\displaystyle\frac{1}{P(x,t)}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left[\frac{F(x)}{k_{B}T}P(x,t)\right]\to\frac{V_{0}}{k_{B}T}\frac{\alpha}{z^{1-\alpha}}\frac{\mathcal{I}^{\prime}(z)}{t^{(2-\alpha)\gamma-\nu}}\,.

(9)

We are interested in the scaling regime where all terms, and hence
the force, the diffusion and the time-dependence contribute equally. This rules out other possible choices whre ones of these effects is negligible.
For example, the trivial
\nu=\gamma=0
solution eliminates the time derivative term, and the rate function would assume the value
t^{\nu}\mathcal{I}(z)=V(x)/k_{B}T
, which is equivalent to the Boltzmann-Gibbs probability, see Eq. (
1
). Another example is
\nu=\gamma=1
where, for
\alpha<1
, the force field term is smaller than the other two by
t^{1-\alpha}
, and the rate function becomes
\mathcal{I}(z)=z^{2}/4
. This describes diffusion in the absence of the force.
Rather, for the exponents

\displaystyle\nu=\frac{\alpha}{2-\alpha}~\mathrm{and}~\gamma=\frac{1}{2-\alpha}\,.

(10)

we have that Eqs. (
7
), (
8
) and (
9
) will have the same time dependence.
This results in the non-linear odinary differential equation for
\mathcal{I}(z)
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{\prime}(z)^{2}-\left(\frac{V_{0}}{k_{B}T}\frac{\alpha}{z^{1-\alpha}}+\frac{z}{2-\alpha}\right)\mathcal{I}^{\prime}(z)+\frac{\alpha\mathcal{I}(z)}{2-\alpha}=0\,.

(11)

Note that, because the previous equation is quadratic with
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}(z)
, we have two possible differential equations to choose for every point. We may write them explicitly as

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{\prime}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{V_{0}}{k_{B}T}\frac{\alpha}{z^{1-\alpha}}+\frac{z}{2-\alpha}\right)\pm\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\left(\frac{V_{0}}{k_{B}T}\frac{\alpha}{z^{1-\alpha}}+\frac{z}{2-\alpha}\right)^{2}-\frac{4\alpha\mathcal{I}(z)}{2-\alpha}}\,.

(12)

\Delta(z,\mathcal{I})

\displaystyle\Delta(z,\mathcal{I})=\left(\frac{V_{0}}{k_{B}T}\frac{\alpha}{z^{1-\alpha}}+\frac{z}{2-\alpha}\right)^{2}-\frac{4\alpha\mathcal{I}}{2-\alpha}\geq 0\,,

z

III
Scaling and the dynamical phase transition

In this Section, we provide a clear physical meaning to the scaling and to the critical transition point
z_{c}
. First, we will consider localized initial conditions, that is, the particle starts the motion at position
x_{0}
with PDF
P(x,0)=\delta(x-x_{0})
,
x_{0}\neq 0
. The particle then undergoes Brownian motion under the influence of the external potential, as defined in Eq. (
4
). We remind the reader that the force
F(x)
, related to that potential is a restoring force, acting to impede the motion of free diffusion.

The PDF at a fixed position
x
, as time goes to infinity, will converge to the Boltzmann-Gibbs equilibrium. However, we cannot expect that, for a fixed time
t
, regardless of how large, the PDF has reached equilibrium for all values of
x
. This is a consequence of the fact that the PDF cannot spread faster than what is allowed by free diffusion, which decays exponentially as a Gaussian, that is,
e^{-x^{2}/4t}
. Given that the equilibrium distribution decays as a power of
\alpha
,
e^{-V_{0}x^{\alpha}/k_{B}T}
, for
0<\alpha<2
, the more restrictive cutoff is the Gaussian free-particle, and we expect it to dominate the behavior at sufficiently large
x
.

An important question is: what is sufficiently large in this case? The answer to this question is given by the critical point obtained in the previous Section. At the point
z=z_{c}
, of
x=x_{c}(t)=z_{c}t^{\gamma}
, the rate function for cases (b) and (c) in the previous Section undergoes a continuous, but non-analytic, change. The case described in item (b) applies to our current problem of localized initial conditions. This is exactly the point where we shift from the bound region,
x<x_{c}(t)
, where the PDF has converged to the equilibrium expectation, and the free region,
x>x_{c}(t)
, where the statistics resemble more closely those of a free-particle.

This non-analytical transition observed in the rate function for cases (b) and (d) (see previous section) is referred to as a dynamical phase transition
[
28
,
21
,
35
,
36
]
. This is due to the rate function playing a similar role to thermodynamic potentials in equilibrium statistical physics
[
21
,
36
]
. In our problem, we have a second-order dynamical phase transition, since both
\mathcal{I}(z)
and
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}(z)
are continuous and only
\mathcal{I}^{\prime\prime}(z)
is discontinuous
[
39
]
.

IV
Eigenfunction expansion

As we stated before, the scaling approach, albeit simple and straightforward, is limited in the amount of information we are able to glean from its results. For example, we could obtain the scaling exponents
\nu
and
\gamma
, we also could find an expression for the critical point
z_{c}
. However, to calculate ensemble averages of observables, we must obtain pre-exponential corrections which we can calculate through the eigenfunction approach. In this Section, we will obtain a formal solution of the FPE  (
3
) via an eigenfunction expansion of the Fokker-Planck operator

\displaystyle\hat{L}_{\mathrm{FP}}(x)

\displaystyle=\left\{\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left[\frac{F(x)}{k_{B}T}\,\,\right]\right\}\,,

(20)

as

\displaystyle P(x,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}\,{P_{k}(x)}e^{-\lambda_{k}t}\,,

(21)

where
\lambda_{k}
are the eigenvalues and
a_{k}
are coefficients given by the initial conditions
[
1
]
. We can see from the previous equation that the Fokker-Planck operator is not self-adjoint, and therefore we must take care to distinguish right eigenfunctions,
P_{k}(x)
and left eigenfunctions
Q_{k}(x)
, which are solutions to the adjoint FP operator. A simple way to avoid this inconvenience is to map the FPE into a Schrödinger equation via the similarity transformation
\psi_{k}(x)\equiv e^{-V(x)/2k_{B}T}P_{k}(x)=e^{V(x)/2k_{B}T}Q_{k}(x)

[
1
]
. The problem now reads

\displaystyle-\lambda_{k}P_{k}(x)=\hat{L}_{\mathrm{FP}}(x)P_{k}(x)\longrightarrow\lambda_{k}\psi_{k}(x)=\hat{H}_{S}(x)\psi_{k}(x)\,

(22)

where the Schrödinger operator is defined as
\hat{H}_{S}(x)\equiv-e^{V(x)/2k_{B}T}\hat{L}_{\mathrm{FP}}(x)e^{-V(x)/2k_{B}T}=-\partial_{x}^{2}+u_{s}(x)
, with the effective potential

\displaystyle u_{S}(x)\equiv\frac{V^{\prime}(x)^{2}}{4(k_{B}T)^{2}}-\frac{V^{\prime\prime}(x)}{2k_{B}T}\,.

(23)

Since the Schrödinger operator
\hat{H}_{S}(x)
is self-adjoint, the eigenfunctions
\psi_{k}(x)
are orthogonal. For simplicity, we will define the scaled potential
v(x)\equiv V(x)/k_{B}T
and the inverse temperature
\beta\equiv V_{0}/k_{B}T
. We imagine placing the system between reflecting walls at
x=\pm L
, so that the spectrum is guaranteed to be discrete. At the end of the calculation, we will take the limit
L\to\infty
.

In the large
x
limit, the effective potential becomes

\displaystyle u_{S}(x)\sim\frac{\beta^{2}\alpha^{2}}{4x^{2-2\alpha}}+\frac{\beta\alpha(1-\alpha)}{2x^{2-\alpha}}\,.

(24)

The crucial point here is the large-
x
behavior of
u_{S}(x)
, which is
\sim x^{2(\alpha-1)}
, since the
-v^{\prime\prime}(x)
term goes as
x^{\alpha-2}
, which decays faster. Since both terms decay to zero at large
x
, we essentially have an unbound particle, and we have that the eigenvalues are
\lambda_{k}\propto 1/L^{2}
. In the infinite-
L
limit, the eigenvalue spectrum becomes a continuum that extends down to
\lambda=0^{+}
. This is what differentiates this problem from a “classic”
\alpha>1
problem, where
u_{S}(x)
grows with
x
and so the spectrum is discrete near
0
, even in the limit
L\to\infty
.

Since we are particularly interested in the long-time regime, as we see from Eq. (
21
), only the small
\lambda_{k}
states contribute to the sum. Thus, the task at hand is to find the continuum state at energy
\lambda_{k}
, which we will now represent as
\lambda_{k}\equiv k^{2}
. We highlight that, since the ground state is
\lambda_{k}=0
, all the excited states that form the continuum must be positive and no negative eigenvalue solutions exist. It is also important to highlight that, since we are using scaled dimensionless variables for position and time, the eigenvalue spectrum
\lambda_{k}
is also dimensionless. For completeness, in physical units, the relationship between the dimensionless scale and the physical one is
\lambda_{k}\to\ell^{2}k^{2}
. To accomplish this, we have to solve the problem in 3 different regimes of
x
and perform asymptotic matching. We will also focus our derivation on
x>0
, as the solutions will either be symmetric (even) or anti-symmetric (odd). The calculations are straightforward, but as we must use several strategies over different scales, we outline the entire procedure

•

Since the effective potential
u_{S}(x)
is even, the parity is obtained by imposing
\psi^{\mathrm{even}}(0)=1
,
{\psi^{\mathrm{even}}}^{\prime}(0)=0
for the even eigenfunction, and
\psi^{\mathrm{odd}}(0)=0
and
{\psi^{\mathrm{odd}}}^{\prime}(0)=1
for the odd eigenfunction.

•

We perform a small
k
expansion in the region near the origin. This solution is valid only for
x
’s which are not too large, in particular,
kx^{\alpha-1}\ll 1
. This range of
x
values is called Region I.

•

In order to extend our solution to larger values of
x
, we will now make use of a WKB solution. We can see in Fig.
3
that there are two turning points, although only one of them (the rightmost one) is highlighted. The first turning point is not important to our analysis, as it is found in Region I for any small
k
value, and we will only make use of the WKB method outside of that region. The second turning point, which is the relevant one, is the one we highlight (see Fig.
3
).

•

The solution near the origin is then matched with the WKB solution on Region II, to the left of the relevant turning point.

•

We use the standard matching of eigenfunctions in the vicinity of the turning point to get to the right side of the relevant turning point.

•

For larger
x
, beyond the turning point, we use the oscillating WKB solution, in agreement with the expected free-particle behavior. This is unlike the non-oscillating solution found in Region II.

•

The eigenvalue spectrum is obtained by imposing the boundary conditions.

•

Finally, we obtain the value of
a_{k}
(see Eq. (
21
)) through the initial conditions. We point out that
a_{k}
ensures the normalization of
P(x,t)
.

The many regions are outlined in Fig.
3
.

x_{\mathrm{TP}}

) and (

IV.1
Region I

In Region I,
kx^{\alpha}\ll 1
, the
k^{2}
term can be treated as a perturbation. The even solutions are obtained by setting
{\psi_{k}^{\mathrm{even}}}(0)=1
. To leading order, all even solutions are approximately the ground state,

\displaystyle\psi_{0}^{\mathrm{even}}(x)=e^{-v(x)/2+v(0)/2}\,.

(25)

This solution is equivalent to the Boltzmann-Gibbs solution in Eq. (
1
). For
k>0
, we can calculate
\psi_{k}^{\mathrm{even}}
to order
k^{2}
:

\psi_{k}^{\mathrm{even}}(x)\approx e^{-v(x)/2+v(0)/2}\left[1-k^{2}\int_{0}^{x}dy_{1}e^{v(y_{1})}\int_{0}^{y_{1}}dy_{2}e^{-v(y_{2})}\right]\,.

(26)

At large
x
, the inner integral can be approximated asymptotically as

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{y_{1}}e^{-v(y_{2})}dy_{2}=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-v(y_{2})}dy_{2}-\int_{y_{1}}^{\infty}e^{-v(y_{2})}dy_{2}\approx\frac{Z}{2}-\frac{{y_{1}}^{1-\alpha}}{\beta\alpha}e^{-\beta x^{\alpha}}\,,

(27)

where
Z
is the Boltzmann-Gibbs partition function, as in Eq. (
1
).
The outer integral is dominated by the large
y_{1}
region, for which the second term of the inner integral becomes negligible. The asymptotics of the outer integral is then

\displaystyle\frac{Z}{2}\int_{0}^{x}e^{v(y)}dy\approx\frac{Z}{2}\left[\frac{x^{1-\alpha}}{\beta\alpha}-\frac{(1-\alpha)}{\beta^{2}\alpha^{2}}x^{1-2\alpha}\right]e^{\beta x^{\alpha}}\,.

(28)

We can now write that the asymptotic expression of the even eigenfunctions is

\displaystyle\psi_{k}^{\mathrm{even}}(x)

\displaystyle\approx e^{v(x)/2+v(0)/2}\left[1-\frac{Zk^{2}}{2}\left(\frac{x^{1-\alpha}}{\beta\alpha}-\frac{(1-\alpha)}{\beta^{2}\alpha^{2}}x^{1-2\alpha}\right)e^{\beta x^{\alpha}}\right]

\displaystyle\approx-\frac{Zk^{2}}{2}e^{\beta x^{\alpha}/2+v(0)/2}\left[\frac{x^{1-\alpha}}{\beta\alpha}-\frac{(1-\alpha)}{\beta^{2}\alpha^{2}}x^{1-2\alpha}\right]\,.

(29)

Note that the perturbation has induced the presence of the exponentially growing mode, which dominates at large
x
. Subsequent terms in the perturbation theory have additional factors of
(kx^{1-\alpha})^{2}
, so this first-order perturbation theory is reliable as long as

kx^{1-\alpha}\ll 1
.
The approximate odd solution is dominated by the growing, odd, solution of the homogeneous equation and so the leading order is
k
independent:

\displaystyle\psi^{\mathrm{odd}}_{k}(x)\approx e^{-\frac{v(x)}{2}-\frac{v(0)}{2}}\int_{0}^{x}e^{v(y)}dy\,,

(30)

where we have set the normalization by the condition
{\psi_{k}^{\mathrm{odd}}}^{\prime}(0)=1
. At large
x
, we use the asymptotic form of the integral to write Eq. (
30
) as

\displaystyle\psi_{k}^{\mathrm{odd}}(x)

\displaystyle\approx e^{-\frac{\beta x^{\alpha}}{2}-\frac{v(0)}{2}}\left[\frac{x^{1-a}}{\beta a}e^{\beta x^{\alpha}}-\frac{1-\alpha}{\beta^{2}\alpha^{2}}x^{1-2\alpha}e^{\beta x^{\alpha}}\right]

\displaystyle\approx e^{\frac{\beta x^{\alpha}}{2}-\frac{v(0)}{2}}\left[\frac{x^{1-\alpha}}{\beta\alpha}-\frac{1-\alpha}{\beta^{2}\alpha^{2}}x^{1-2\alpha}\right]\,.

(31)

We can see the validity of Eqs. (
26
) and (
30
) is shown numerically in Fig.
4
.

IV.2
Region II

The next region, which we label Region II, is characterized by
x\gg 1
, whereas we have seen the growing mode is dominant. In this region, we will use the WKB approximation to find the eigenfunctions, which will be valid until we approach too closely to the turning point. From the definition of
u_{S}(x)
in Eq. (
23
), we see that there are in fact two turning points, that is,
u_{S}(x_{\mathrm{TP}})=k^{2}
. The first turning point occurs for
x\sim O(1)
, and is inside Region I, and so is described by that solution and need not concern us further. Here, we are only interested in the second turning point,
x_{\text{TP}}\sim(\beta\alpha/2k)^{1/(1-\alpha)}
. This turning point marks the boundary between Regions II and III, which we will treat in the next subsection.

For the values of
x
we are interested in, the effective Schrödinger potential is larger than the eigenvalue.
u_{S}(x<x_{\mathrm{TP}})>k^{2}
. We also know that the eigenfunction is growing in that region, so we choose the WKB solution

\displaystyle\psi_{k}(x)\approx A_{k}\frac{\exp\left[-\int_{x}^{x_{\text{TP}}}\sqrt{u_{S}(y)-k^{2}}\,dy\right]}{[u_{S}(x)-k^{2}]^{1/4}}\,.\,,

(32)

where
A_{k}
is a constant. To determine
A_{k}
, we need to evaluate
\psi_{k}(x)
far to the left of the turning point, at some
1\ll x\ll x_{\mathrm{TP}}
, and match this solution to the asymptotic solutions in Region I. Note that Eqs. (
29
) and (
31
) have the same functional dependency on
x
. As a consequence, in Region II both even and odd eigenfunctions will follow Eq. (
32
), with the only difference being the specific value of
A_{k}
.

In the overlap region of interest,
u_{S}(x)\gg k^{2}
, we are far to the left of the turning point. This allows us to write the position as a function of the energy
u
,

\displaystyle x(u)

\displaystyle\approx\left(\frac{\beta\alpha}{2\sqrt{u}}\right)^{1/(1-\alpha)}+\frac{1}{2\sqrt{u}}\,,

(33)

and therefore,

\displaystyle\left|\frac{dx}{du}\right|

\displaystyle\approx\left(\frac{\beta\alpha}{2}\right)^{1/(1-\alpha)}\frac{u^{\frac{2\alpha-3}{2-2\alpha}}}{2(1-\alpha)}+\frac{1}{4u^{3/2}}\,.

(34)

Let us label the quantity in the exponential of Eq. (
32
), the WKB action, as
-S(x,k)
. We have that

\displaystyle Q(x)\equiv\int_{x}^{x_{\mathrm{TP}}}\sqrt{u_{S}(y)-k^{2}}dy\approx\int_{k^{2}}^{u_{S}(x)}\sqrt{u-k^{2}}\left|\frac{dy}{du}\right|du\,,

(35)

which we use to split the WKB action into two parts as
Q(x)\approx Q_{1}(x)+Q_{2}(x)
, defined from each term of
\left|{dx}/{du}\right|
respectively. We expand the first part of the action as

\displaystyle Q_{1}(x)

\displaystyle\approx\int_{k^{2}}^{u_{S}(x)}\sqrt{u-k^{2}}\left(\frac{\beta\alpha}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-a}}\frac{u^{\frac{2\alpha-3}{2-2\alpha}}}{2(1-\alpha)}du

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\left(\frac{\beta\alpha}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}}{2(1-\alpha)}\left[\int_{k^{2}}^{\infty}\sqrt{u-k^{2}}\,u^{\frac{2\alpha-3}{2-2\alpha}}du-\int_{u_{S}(x)}^{\infty}\sqrt{u-k^{2}}\,u^{\frac{2\alpha-3}{2-2\alpha}}du\right]

\displaystyle\approx\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{4}}\left(\frac{\alpha\beta}{2k^{\alpha}}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{\alpha}{2-2\alpha}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2-2\alpha}\right)}-\left(\frac{\alpha\beta}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}\left[\frac{u_{S}(x)^{-\frac{\alpha}{2-2\alpha}}}{\alpha}\right.

\displaystyle~\left.-\frac{k^{2}\,u_{S}(x)^{-(2-\alpha)/(2-2\alpha)}}{2(2-\alpha)}\right]\,.

(36)

In the limit
x\gg 1
, we can write
u_{S}(x)
as in Eq. (
24
), so we may explicitly write

\displaystyle u_{S}(x)^{-1}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{4x^{2-2\alpha}}{\beta^{2}\alpha^{2}}

(37)

\displaystyle u_{S}(x)^{-1/2}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{2x^{1-\alpha}}{\beta\alpha}\left(1-\frac{1-\alpha}{\beta\alpha}x^{-\alpha}\right)

(38)

\displaystyle u_{S}(x)^{-\alpha/(2-2\alpha)}

\displaystyle\approx\left(\frac{2}{\beta\alpha}\right)^{\alpha/(1-\alpha)}\left\{x^{\alpha}-\frac{1}{\beta}\right\}\,,

(39)

\displaystyle u_{S}(x)^{-(2-\alpha)/(2-2\alpha)}

\displaystyle\approx\left(\frac{2}{\beta\alpha}\right)^{(2-\alpha)/(1-\alpha)}x^{2-\alpha}\left\{1-\frac{2-\alpha}{\beta\alpha}x^{-\alpha}\right\}\,.

(40)

Using the previous expressions, we can write that

\displaystyle Q_{1}(x)\approx\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{4}}\left(\frac{\alpha\beta}{2k^{\alpha}}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{\alpha}{2-2\alpha}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2-2\alpha}\right)}-\frac{\beta x^{\alpha}}{2}+\frac{1}{2}+\frac{k^{2}x^{2-\alpha}}{\beta\alpha(2-\alpha)}-\frac{k^{2}x^{2-2\alpha}}{\beta^{2}\alpha^{2}}\,,

(41)

and

\displaystyle Q_{2}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{k^{2}}^{u_{S}(x)}\frac{\sqrt{u-k^{2}}}{4u^{3/2}}du=

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\left[-2\sqrt{1-\frac{k^{2}}{u_{S}(x)}}-\ln\left(\frac{k^{2}}{u_{S}(x)}\right)+2\ln\left(1+\sqrt{1-\frac{k^{2}}{u_{S}(x)}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\approx-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{4}\ln\left(\frac{k^{2}}{4u_{S}(x)}\right)+\frac{k^{2}x^{2-2\alpha}}{2\beta^{2}\alpha^{2}}\,.

(42)

Now we will write the same expansion for the WKB denominator, which we will promote to the exponent as

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{4}\ln\left(u_{S}(x)-k^{2}\right)\approx-\frac{1}{4}\ln u_{S}(x)+\frac{k^{2}x^{2-2\alpha}}{\beta^{2}\alpha^{2}}\,.

(43)

Combining all previous expressions, we reach the complete WKB solution as

\displaystyle\psi_{k}(x)

\displaystyle\approx A_{k}\exp\left\{-Q(x,k)-\frac{1}{4}\ln\left(u_{S}(x)-k^{2}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{A_{k}\,\sqrt{\frac{k}{2}}}{u_{S}(x)^{-1/2}}e^{\left\{-\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{4}}\left(\frac{\alpha\beta}{2k^{\alpha}}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{\alpha}{2-2\alpha}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2-2\alpha}\right)}+\frac{\beta x^{\alpha}}{2}-\frac{k^{2}x^{2-\alpha}}{\beta\alpha(2-\alpha)}-\frac{k^{2}x^{2-2\alpha}}{\beta^{2}\alpha^{2}}\right\}}

\displaystyle\psi_{k}(x)

\displaystyle\approx A_{k}\sqrt{2k}\left[\frac{x^{1-\alpha}}{\beta\alpha}-\frac{1-\alpha}{\beta^{2}\alpha^{2}}x^{1-2\alpha}\right]e^{\left\{-\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{4}}\left(\frac{\alpha\beta}{2k^{\alpha}}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{\alpha}{2-2\alpha}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2-2\alpha}\right)}+\frac{\beta x^{\alpha}}{2}-\frac{k^{2}x^{2-\alpha}}{\beta\alpha(2-\alpha)}-\frac{k^{2}x^{2-2\alpha}}{\beta^{2}\alpha^{2}}\right\}}\,.

As stated in the previous Section, the approximation on Region I is only valid for
x^{2-2\alpha}k^{2}\ll 1
. In this limit, the
k^{2}
terms in the exponent of Eq. (
) can be neglected and we see that the WKB solution has the same functional dependence on
x
that in Eqs. (
29
) and (
31
). The constant
A_{k}
is finally obtained, for both odd and even eigenfunctions, by matching these equations. We reach that

\displaystyle A_{k}^{\mathrm{odd}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2k}}\exp\left\{\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{4}}\left(\frac{\alpha\beta}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{\alpha}{2-2\alpha}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2-2\alpha}\right)}\,k^{-\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}-\frac{v(0)}{2}\right\}

(45)

\displaystyle A_{k}^{\mathrm{even}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{Z\,k^{3/2}}{{2}^{3/2}}\exp\left\{\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{4}}\left(\frac{\alpha\beta}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{\alpha}{2-2\alpha}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2-2\alpha}\right)}\,k^{-\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}+\frac{v(0)}{2}\right\}\,,

(46)

and the WKB solutions, far to the left of the turning point, are

\displaystyle\psi_{k}^{\mathrm{odd}}(x)

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{x^{1-\alpha}}{\beta\alpha}-\frac{1-\alpha}{\beta^{2}\alpha^{2}}x^{1-2\alpha}\right]e^{\left\{\frac{\beta x^{\alpha}}{2}-\frac{v(0)}{2}-\frac{k^{2}x^{2-\alpha}}{\beta\alpha(2-\alpha)}+\frac{k^{2}x^{2-2\alpha}}{2\beta^{2}\alpha^{2}}\right\}}

(47)

\displaystyle\psi_{k}^{\mathrm{even}}(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{Z\,k^{2}}{2}\left[\frac{x^{1-\alpha}}{\beta\alpha}-\frac{1-\alpha}{\beta^{2}\alpha^{2}}x^{1-2\alpha}\right]e^{\left\{\frac{\beta x^{\alpha}}{2}+\frac{v(0)}{2}-\frac{k^{2}x^{2-\alpha}}{\beta\alpha(2-\alpha)}+\frac{k^{2}x^{2-2\alpha}}{2\beta^{2}\alpha^{2}}\right\}}.

(48)

In Fig.
5
we show numerically the validity of the previous equations.

IV.3
Region III

The next and final region, which we label Region III, is the region to the right of the turning point. Here, the effective potential is less than the energy eigenvalue,
u_{S}(x)<k^{2}
, and the WKB solution is oscillating, as

\displaystyle\psi_{k}(x)\approx 2A_{k}\frac{\sin\left(\int_{x_{\text{TP}}}^{x}\sqrt{k^{2}-u_{S}(y)}\,dy+\frac{\pi}{4}\right)}{(k^{2}-u_{S}(x))^{1/4}}\,,

(49)

where the constant
2A_{k}
and the phase
\pi/4
are obtained through the WKB connection formula to cross the turning point.

Since our system is placed inside reflective walls at
x=\pm L
, we know that for these points, Eq. (
49
) must follow the no-flux boundary condition. The crucial property in this case is that the difference between the phases of two consecutive eigenvalues
k
and
k+\Delta k
must be equal to
\pi
. In the limit of large
L
, specifically
L\gg x_{\mathrm{TP}}
, we have

\displaystyle\Delta k\int_{x_{\text{TP}}}^{L}\frac{k}{\sqrt{k^{2}-u_{S}(y)}}\,dy\approx L\,\Delta k\approx\pi\,.

(50)

Clearly, the density of states is continuous, as the spacing
\Delta k\approx\pi/L
goes to zero as
L\to\infty
, and it stretches from
k=0^{+}
to infinity. The complete eigenvalue spectrum is mixed, as we have the discrete ground state for
k=0
, and the continuum states with
k>0
. In the limit of
L\to\infty
, we can replace the sum in
k
by an integral as

\displaystyle\sum_{k}\to\int L\frac{dk}{\pi}\,.

(51)

IV.4
Initial conditions

The last step to complete the eigenfunction expansion is to obtain the constants
a_{k}
in Eq. (
21
). We use the initial condition
P(x,0)=\delta(x-x_{0})=\sum_{k}a_{k}e^{-v(x)/2}\psi_{k}(x)
and the orthogonality of the eigenfunctions
\psi_{k}(x)
to write

\displaystyle a_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{v(x)/2}P(x,0)\psi_{k}(x)dx}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(\psi_{k}(x))^{2}dx}=\frac{e^{v(x_{0})/2}\psi_{k}(x_{0})}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(\psi_{k}(x))^{2}dx}\,.

(52)

The largest contribution to the integral on the denominator arises from Region III. This can be seen in Figs.
3
and
4
. In this region, the function behaves as a squared sine function, which we can replace by its mean value 1/2, to find

\displaystyle\int_{-L}^{L}(\psi_{k}(x))^{2}dx\approx\frac{4A_{k}^{2}}{k}L\,.

(53)

The previous equation is true for both odd, see Eq. (
45
), and even, see Eq. (
46
) eigenfunctions. For simplicity, we will take the limit of
x_{0}\to 0
, so that we may write
\psi^{\mathrm{even}}_{k}(x_{0})\approx{\psi^{\mathrm{odd}}_{k}}^{\prime}(x_{0})/x_{0}\approx 1
.

Finally, the odd PDF can be written as

\displaystyle P^{\mathrm{odd}}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dk}{\pi}\frac{k}{4{A^{\mathrm{odd}}_{k}}^{2}}e^{-\frac{v(x)}{2}+\frac{v(x_{0})}{2}}\psi_{k}(x_{0})\psi_{k}(x)e^{-k^{2}t}

\displaystyle=x_{0}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dk}{\pi}\frac{k}{4{A^{\mathrm{odd}}_{k}}^{2}}e^{-\frac{v(x)}{2}+\frac{v(0)}{2}}\psi_{k}(x)e^{-k^{2}t}\,,

(54)

and the even PDF can be written as

\displaystyle P^{\mathrm{even}}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-v(x)}}{Z}+\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dk}{\pi}\frac{k}{4{A^{\mathrm{even}}_{k}}^{2}}e^{-\frac{v(x)}{2}+\frac{v(x_{0})}{2}}\psi_{k}(x_{0})\psi_{k}(x)e^{-k^{2}t}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-v(x)}}{Z}+\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dk}{\pi}\frac{k}{4{A^{\mathrm{even}}_{k}}^{2}}e^{-\frac{v(x)}{2}+\frac{v(x_{0})}{2}}\psi_{k}(x_{0})\psi_{k}(x)e^{-k^{2}t}\,.

(55)

V
The equivalence between the eigenfunctions and the scaled rate function approach

The eigenfunction solution provides a more complete solution for the PDF, compared to the simpler scaling approach used in Sec.
II
. However, it is not clear from expressions such as Eq. (
54
), that the results from the scaling approach would emerge. This section is dedicated to showing the equivalence of both approaches.

First, we must introduce scaled variables for the position
z\equiv x/t^{\gamma}
and for the eigenvalues
\kappa\equiv k\,t^{\zeta}
. The scaling exponents
\gamma
and
\zeta
must be obtained. From Eqs. (
54
) and  (
55
), in the regime of small
k
and restricting the values of
x
where the WKB solution is valid, we can define the
x
and
k
contributions in the exponent as an action, first for the left of the turning point,

\displaystyle S_{L}(x,k)=k^{2}t+\frac{v(x)}{2}+\int_{0}^{x_{\text{TP}}}\sqrt{u_{S}(y)-k^{2}}\,dy+\int_{x}^{x_{\text{TP}}}\sqrt{u_{S}(y)-k^{2}}\,dy\,,

(56)

and the solution right of the turning point,

\displaystyle S_{R}(x,k)=k^{2}t+\frac{v(x)}{2}+\int_{0}^{x_{\text{TP}}}\sqrt{u_{S}(y)-k^{2}}\,dy+i\int_{x}^{x_{\text{TP}}}\sqrt{k^{2}-u_{S}(y)}\,dy\,.

(57)

Clearly, the actions defined in the previous equation depend on time, but we omitted
t
in the argument list for simplicity.
We take the first two terms of the previous equations, the eigenvalue, and the potential (divided by 2) contribution, and write them using the scaled variables

\displaystyle\frac{\beta x^{\alpha}}{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta z^{\alpha}}{2}t^{\alpha\gamma}

(58)

\displaystyle k^{2}t

\displaystyle=\kappa^{2}t^{1-2\zeta}\,.

(59)

At this point, we will impose that the scaling exponents must ensure that all relevant terms, i.e. terms that depend on
x
and
k
, contribute equally to the action. We thus obtain that
1-2\zeta=\alpha\gamma
. Next, we must ensure that the Schrödinger potential and the eigenvalues have the same dependency in time, that is

\displaystyle u_{S}(x)

where we introduce the

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{L}(z,\kappa)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{z_{\text{TP}}}\sqrt{\frac{\beta^{2}\alpha^{2}}{4y^{2-2\alpha}}-\kappa^{2}}\,dy=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{4}}\left(\frac{\alpha\beta}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{\alpha}{2-2\alpha}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2-2\alpha}\right)}\kappa^{-\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\,,

From Fig.

. The exponent of the

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial\kappa}\mathcal{S}(z,\kappa)\right|_{\kappa=\kappa^{*}}=0\,.

(70)

For now, we will focus on solving the odd part of the PDF. This will allow us to calculate the integral without concerning ourselves with the Boltzmann-Gibbs ground state. The integral of the
k
-density can be approximated using Laplace’s method and we can write, up to pre-exponential terms, that

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{odd}}(x,t)\approx e^{-t^{\nu}\mathcal{S}(z,\kappa^{*})}\,.

(71)

The eigenfunction expansion and the rate function scaling approach must yield the same expression for the PDF, that is,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(z)=\mathcal{S}(z,\kappa^{*})\,.

(72)

A direct way to show that this is true is to demonstrate that
\mathcal{S}(z,\kappa^{*})
is also a solution of the differential Eq. (

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\mathcal{S}(z,\kappa^{*})=\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\mathcal{S}(z,\kappa)\right|_{\kappa=\kappa^{*}}+\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial\kappa}\mathcal{S}(z,\kappa)\right|_{\kappa=\kappa^{*}}\frac{\partial\kappa^{*}}{\partial z}=\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\mathcal{S}(z,\kappa)\right|_{\kappa=\kappa^{*}}\,,

(73)

where the last equality is true due to
\kappa^{*}
being the point of maximal contribution. We will now show that Eq. (

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\left[\frac{z}{\alpha}\sqrt{\frac{\beta^{2}\alpha^{2}}{4z^{2-2\alpha}}-\kappa^{2}}\right]=\sqrt{\frac{\beta^{2}\alpha^{2}}{4z^{2-2\alpha}}-\kappa^{2}}-\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\frac{\kappa^{2}}{\sqrt{\frac{\beta^{2}\alpha^{2}}{4z^{2-2\alpha}}-\kappa^{2}}}\,,

(74)

which allows us to write

\displaystyle\left[\frac{1-\alpha}{2-\alpha}\right]{\kappa}\frac{\partial S}{\partial\kappa}

\displaystyle=\kappa^{2}+\frac{z\frac{\partial\mathcal{S}}{\partial z}-\alpha\mathcal{S}}{2-\alpha}\,,

(75)

and

\displaystyle\left[\frac{\partial\mathcal{S}}{\partial z}\right]^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta\alpha}{z^{1-\alpha}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{S}}{\partial z}-\kappa^{2}\,.

(76)

Combining both previous equations, we can write the equivalent of Eq. (

\displaystyle\left[\frac{\partial\mathcal{S}}{\partial z}\right]^{2}-\left(\frac{\beta\alpha}{z^{1-\alpha}}+\frac{z}{2-\alpha}\right)\frac{\partial\mathcal{S}}{\partial z}+\frac{\alpha\mathcal{S}}{2-\alpha}=\left[\frac{1-\alpha}{2-\alpha}\right]{\kappa}\frac{\partial S}{\partial\kappa}\,.

(77)

It is clear that

\kappa=\kappa^{*}
yields the excited solutions and
\kappa=0
is equivalent to the Boltzmann-Gibbs solution. Therefore, we have that

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{BG}}(z)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{S}(z,0)\,,

(78)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{cont}}(z)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{S}(z,\kappa^{*})\,.

(79)

In these cases, Eq. (
77

11

VI
The solvable
V
-shaped potential (
\alpha=1
)

In this section, we shall treat the analytically solvable
V
-shaped potential, which is equivalent to
\alpha=1

[
40
,
41
,
42
]
, defined below. As we will see, the eigenvalue spectrum in this case is different than the
0<\alpha<1
case. Here, the spectrum has a gap between the ground state and the beginning of the excited state. Despite that, we can use it to gather valuable insights into the behavior of the solution, as this potential has a simple analytical solution. The
V
-shaped potential is defined as

\displaystyle V(x)

\displaystyle=V_{0}|x/\ell|\,.

(91)

VI.1
Using the rate-function approach

This potential is equivalent to the
\alpha=1
case for our family of potentials, see Eq. (
4
). Here, the scaling exponents are
\gamma=\nu=1
, and the scaled position variable for this example is
z\equiv x/t
. Following the scaling approach, we find that the differential equation for the rate function
\mathcal{I}(z)
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{\prime}(z)^{2}-\left(\frac{V_{0}}{k_{B}T}+z\right)\mathcal{I}^{\prime}(z)+\mathcal{I}(z)=0\,,

(92)

which is equivalent to Eq. (
11
), for
\alpha=1
. As we saw, one immediate solution is the Boltzmann-Gibbs equivalent, that is,
\mathcal{I}(z)=V_{0}z/k_{B}T
. From the form of Eq. (
92
), we can propose a polynomial solution as
\mathcal{I}(z)=b_{2}z^{2}+b_{1}z+b_{0}
. Plugging that expression into Eq. (
92
), we find that

\displaystyle b_{0}+b_{1}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{b_{1}V_{0}}{k_{B}T}\,

(93)

\displaystyle b_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{V_{0}}{2k_{B}T}\,.

(94)

Imposing diffusive behavior for large
z
, we find that
b_{2}=1/4
,
b_{1}=0
, and
b_{0}=V_{0}^{2}/4(k_{B}T)^{2}
. The complete solution is

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{BG}}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{V_{0}}{k_{B}T}z

(95)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{cont}}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{z^{2}}{4}+\frac{V_{0}}{2k_{B}T}z+\frac{V_{0}^{2}}{4(k_{B}T)^{2}}\,.

(96)

In this particular case, we emphasize that the rate function related to the continuum contributions,
\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{cont}}
, is also a zero discriminant line. The critical point is found through
\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{BG}}(z_{c})=\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{cont}}(z_{c})
, that is,

\displaystyle z_{c}=\frac{V_{0}}{k_{B}T}\,.

(97)

Note that, in this case, the scaling exponents are identical to unity, so here this is an exponential (not stretched) relaxation.

VI.2
Using the eigenfunctions approach

For the
V
-shaped potential, we have that the Schrödinger equivalent potential is

\displaystyle u_{S}(x)=\frac{V_{0}^{2}}{4(k_{B}T)^{2}}-\frac{V_{0}}{2k_{B}T}\delta(x)\,,

(98)

where
\delta(x)
is the Dirac delta function. For
x\neq 0
, the Schrödinger equation is equivalent to that of a constant potential (with intensity
{V_{0}^{2}}/{4(k_{B}T)^{2}}
). The eigenvalue spectrum has the form

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}=\frac{V_{0}^{2}}{4(k_{B}T)^{2}}+k^{2}\,.

(99)

We see here that there is a gap
\frac{V_{0}^{2}}{4(k_{B}T)^{2}}
between the ground state and the first excited state. The complete normalized eigenfunctions are obtained using the Dirac delta function to match the
x>0
and
x<0
regions. Explicitly, the normalized even and odd eigenfunctions are

\displaystyle\psi_{k}^{\mathrm{even}}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{k\cos(kx)+\left(\frac{V_{0}}{2k_{B}T}\right)\sin(k|x|)}{\sqrt{\pi\left(k^{2}+\frac{V_{0}^{2}}{4(k_{B}T)^{2}}\right)}}\,,

(100)

\displaystyle\psi_{k}^{\mathrm{odd}}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sin(kx)\,.

(101)

To obtain the PDF, we must perform an integral over positive
k
. Let us consider a localized initial condition
P(x,t=0)=\delta(x-x_{0})
. For a small initial position
x_{0}
, we can write
\psi_{k}(x_{0})=\sin(kx_{0})/\sqrt{\pi}\approx kx_{0}/\sqrt{\pi}
. The odd part of the probability density is written as

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{odd}}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}dk\psi^{\mathrm{odd}}_{k}(x)\psi^{\mathrm{odd}}_{k}(x_{0})e^{-\lambda_{k}t-\frac{V_{0}|x|}{2k_{B}T}}

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{V_{0}|x|}{2k_{B}T}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dk}{2\pi}kx_{0}\sin(kx)\exp\left(-k^{2}t-\frac{V_{0}^{2}t}{4(k_{B}T)^{2}}\right)\,

\displaystyle=x_{0}e^{-\frac{V_{0}|x|}{2k_{B}T}-\frac{V_{0}^{2}t}{4(k_{B}T)^{2}}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dk}{2\pi i}\exp\left(-k^{2}t+ikx\right).

(102)

The last integral is a very simple Gaussian integral. Despite this, we will go into detail as to how this integral is calculated as it will be useful to understanding the dynamical phase transition in the even PDF. First we introduce the same scaled position variable
z\equiv x/t
, so that

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{odd}}

\displaystyle=x_{0}e^{-\frac{V_{0}|x|}{2k_{B}T}-\frac{V_{0}^{2}t}{4(k_{B}T)^{2}}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dk}{2\pi i}k\exp\left(t[-k^{2}+ikz]\right).

(103)

Extending the variable
k
into the complex plane, we find that the value
k^{*}
that maximizes the argument inside the exponential is

\displaystyle k^{*}=\pm\frac{iz}{2}\,.

(104)

Since there are no poles in the pre-factor of the integral in Eq. (
103
), we make use of Cauchy’s integral theorem to close the contour with a new path that crosses the maximum point
k^{*}
. We then use Laplace’s method to obtain the integral. Despite that, in the particular case of the
V
-shaped potential, the integral is Gaussian and this result is analytical, we detail the steps to calculate it as we will use them in the next section where we treat
0<\alpha<1
. We write the integration result as

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{odd}}(x,t)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{x_{0}x}{4\sqrt{\pi}t^{3/2}}\exp\left(-t\left[\frac{z^{2}}{4}+\frac{V_{0}}{2k_{B}T}|z|+\frac{V_{0}^{2}}{4(k_{B}T)^{2}}\right]\right)\,,

(105)

where we can clearly see that the expression in the exponent is identical to
\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{cont}}(z)
in Eq. (
96
).

We now turn to the contribution from the continuum modes to the even part of the PDF,
P^{*}_{\mathrm{even}}(x,t)=P_{\mathrm{even}}(x,t)-P_{\mathrm{eq}}(x)
. In that case, we have

\displaystyle P^{*}_{\mathrm{even}}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}dk\psi^{\mathrm{even}}_{k}(x)\psi^{\mathrm{even}}_{k}(0)e^{-\lambda_{k}t-\frac{V_{0}|x|}{2k_{B}T}}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dk}{2\pi}\frac{k^{2}\cos(kx)+k\left(\frac{V_{0}}{2k_{B}T}\right)\sin(k|x|)}{\left(k^{2}+\frac{V_{0}^{2}}{4(k_{B}T)^{2}}\right)}e^{-k^{2}t-\frac{V_{0}^{2}}{4(k_{B}T)^{2}}t-\frac{V_{0}|x|}{2k_{B}T}}\,,

(106)

where, for simplicity, we take
x_{0}=0
. Note that we could employ Laplace’s method to solve the Gaussian integrals in the same way as we did for the odd part. However, unlike the odd part, here the pre-factor contains a pole at
\bar{k}=\pm iV_{0}/k_{B}T
. For a critical value
z_{c}
of the scaled variable, we will have that
k^{*}=\bar{k}
, explicitly

\displaystyle\frac{iz_{c}}{2}=\pm i\frac{V_{0}}{2k_{B}T}\to z_{c}=\pm\frac{V_{0}}{k_{B}T}\,.

(107)

This is the same critical point as obtained in Eq. (
97
) through the rate function approach. For
|z|<|z_{c}|
, the pole will lie outside the integration contour, and will not contribute to the final result. On the other hand, if
|z|>|z_{c}|
, we must include the contribution from the residue of
\bar{k}
, which is equivalent to the (negative) Boltzmann-Gibbs solution,

\displaystyle(2\pi i)\mathrm{Res}_{k=\bar{k}}\left\{\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{k^{2}\cos(kx)+k\left(\frac{V_{0}}{2k_{B}T}\right)\sin(k|x|)}{\left(k^{2}+\frac{V_{0}^{2}}{4(k_{B}T)^{2}}\right)}e^{-k^{2}t-\frac{V_{0}^{2}}{4(k_{B}T)^{2}}t-\frac{V_{0}|x|}{2k_{B}T}}\right\}=-\frac{V_{0}}{2k_{B}T}e^{-\frac{V_{0}|x|}{k_{B}T}}\,.

For
z>z_{c}
, this negative Boltzmann-Gibbs contribution will dominate. In the long-time limit, we have

\displaystyle P^{*}_{\mathrm{even}}(x,t)

\displaystyle\approx\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}\frac{x\exp\left(-t\left[\frac{z^{2}}{4}+\frac{V_{0}}{2k_{B}T}|z|+\frac{V_{0}^{2}}{4(k_{B}T)^{2}}\right]\right)}{\sqrt{\pi t}\left(x-\frac{V_{0}}{k_{B}T}t\right)}&\text{if }x<\frac{V_{0}t}{k_{B}T}\\
-\frac{V_{0}}{2k_{B}T}e^{-\frac{V_{0}|x|}{k_{B}T}}&\text{if }x>\frac{V_{0}t}{k_{B}T}\end{array}\right.\,.

The dynamical phase transition occurs when the integration path envelops the Boltzmann-Gibbs pole. The complete even part of the PDF is then

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{even}}(x,t)

\displaystyle\approx\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}\frac{V_{0}}{2k_{B}T}e^{-\frac{V_{0}|x|}{k_{B}T}}&\text{if }x<\frac{V_{0}t}{k_{B}T}\\
\frac{x\exp\left(-t\left[\frac{z^{2}}{4}+\frac{V_{0}}{2k_{B}T}|z|+\frac{V_{0}^{2}}{4(k_{B}T)^{2}}\right]\right)}{\sqrt{\pi t}\left(x-\frac{V_{0}}{k_{B}T}t\right)}&\text{if }x>\frac{V_{0}t}{k_{B}T}\end{array}\right.\,.

The dynamical phase transition for

In our analysis of the

, we understood that the negative Boltzmann-Gibbs contribution, necessary to cancel out the equilibrium ground state at large distances, arises from a pole in the complex plane. This is impossible in the odd part of the PDF, which we could easily verify in the
V
-shaped potential (see Eq. (
101
)).

In the case of general exponent
\alpha
, we don’t have access to a complete analytical expression for the
k
-density which would allow us to find this pole. As we showed in the previous section, the WKB solution becomes more accurate the longer time
t
we consider. Despite that, we are still limited to a piece-wise expression, the turning point being the point of switch. The Boltzmann-Gibbs contribution is found when the right WKB solution dominates the integral, and we must perform analytical continuation to find the maximal eigenvalues
\kappa^{*}
.
We understand that the pole must be found at
\kappa=0
of the analytical continuation of
\mathcal{S}_{R}(z,\kappa)
.

Let us express the excited contributions of the even PDF in the usual coordinates. Using that, for the even eigenfunctions, we have
\psi_{k}^{\mathrm{even}}(x_{0})\approx 1
, the expression for the PDF becomes

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{even}}^{*}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dk}{\pi}\frac{k}{4A_{k}^{2}}\psi_{k}(x)\psi_{k}(x_{0})e^{-k^{2}t-\frac{V(x)}{2k_{B}T}+\frac{V(0)}{2k_{B}T}}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dk}{\pi}e^{-k^{2}t-\frac{V(x)}{2k_{B}T}+\frac{V(0)}{2k_{B}T}}\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}\frac{k}{4A_{k}}\frac{\exp\left(\int_{x}^{x_{\text{TP}}}\sqrt{u_{S}(y)-k^{2}}dy\right)}{\left(u_{S}(x)-k^{2}\right)^{1/4}}&\text{if }x<x_{\text{TP}}(k)\\
\frac{k}{2A_{k}}\frac{\sin\left(\int_{x_{\text{TP}}}^{x}\sqrt{k^{2}-u_{S}(y)}dy+\pi/4\right)}{\left(k^{2}-u_{S}(x)\right)^{1/4}}&\text{if }x>x_{\text{TP}}(k)\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle=-\frac{2^{3/2}}{Z}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dk}{\pi}\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}\frac{1}{4\sqrt{k}}\frac{\exp\left(-S_{L}(x,k)\right)}{\left(u_{S}(x)-k^{2}\right)^{1/4}}&\text{if }x<x_{\text{TP}}(k)\\
\frac{1}{2\sqrt{k}}\frac{\mathrm{Im}\exp\left(-S_{R}(x,k)\right)}{\left(k^{2}-u_{S}(x)\right)^{1/4}}&\text{if }x>x_{\text{TP}}(k)\end{array}\right.\,,

This is the function we are interested in

\displaystyle\int_{k_{\text{TP}}(x)}^{\infty}\frac{dk}{\pi}e^{-k^{2}t-\frac{V(x)}{2k_{B}T}+\frac{V(0)}{2k_{B}T}}\frac{k}{2A_{k}}\frac{\sin\left(\int_{x_{\text{TP}}}^{x}\sqrt{k^{2}-u_{S}(y)}dy+\pi/4\right)}{\left(k^{2}-u_{S}(x)\right)^{1/4}}\,,

(117)

which we can write as the imaginary part of

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{k}{2A_{k}}\frac{e^{-k^{2}t-\frac{V(x)}{2k_{B}T}+\frac{V(0)}{2k_{B}T}+i\int_{x_{\text{TP}}}^{x}\sqrt{k^{2}-u_{S}(y)}dy+i\pi/4}}{\left(k^{2}-u_{S}(x)\right)^{1/4}}

(118)

as we are hunting for a pole in the region of
k<k_{\text{TP}}(x)
, we re-write the square roots as

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{k}{2A_{k}}\frac{e^{-k^{2}t-\frac{V(x)}{2k_{B}T}+\frac{V(0)}{2k_{B}T}+i\int_{x_{\text{TP}}}^{x}i\sqrt{u_{S}(y)-k^{2}}dy+i\pi/4}}{(-1)^{1/4}\left(u_{S}(x)-k^{2}\right)^{1/4}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{k}{2A_{k}}\frac{e^{-k^{2}t-\frac{V(x)}{2k_{B}T}+\frac{V(0)}{2k_{B}T}-\int_{x_{\text{TP}}}^{x}\sqrt{u_{S}(y)-k^{2}}dy+i\pi/4}}{e^{i\pi/4}\left(u_{S}(x)-k^{2}\right)^{1/4}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{k}{2A_{k}}\frac{e^{-k^{2}t-\frac{V(x)}{2k_{B}T}+\frac{V(0)}{2k_{B}T}-\int_{x_{\text{TP}}}^{x}\sqrt{u_{S}(y)-k^{2}}dy}}{\left(u_{S}(x)-k^{2}\right)^{1/4}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{k}{2\pi}\frac{{2}^{3/2}}{Z\,k^{3/2}}e^{-\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{4}}\left(\frac{\alpha\beta}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{\alpha}{2-2\alpha}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2-2\alpha}\right)}\,k^{-\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}-\frac{v(0)}{2}}\frac{e^{-k^{2}t-\frac{v(x)}{2}+\frac{v(0)}{2}-\int_{x_{\text{TP}}}^{x}\sqrt{u_{S}(y)-k^{2}}dy}}{\left(u_{S}(x)-k^{2}\right)^{1/4}}\,

(119)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{{2}^{1/2}}{Z\,k^{1/2}}e^{-\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{4}}\left(\frac{\alpha\beta}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{\alpha}{2-2\alpha}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2-2\alpha}\right)}\,k^{-\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}}\frac{e^{-k^{2}t-\frac{v(x)}{2}-\int_{x_{\text{TP}}}^{x}\sqrt{u_{S}(y)-k^{2}}dy}}{\left(u_{S}(x)-k^{2}\right)^{1/4}}\,.

(120)

We now return to the function
Q(x)
in Eq. (
35
). In the exponent, the contribution from the integral is equivalent to
-Q(x)
, while for the left WKB solution we would have
+Q(x)
. This small difference is essential to show that the pole is found at
k=0
. Let us make use of the solution we already found for
Q(x)
to write

\displaystyle Q(x)\approx\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{4}}\left(\frac{\alpha\beta}{2k^{\alpha}}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{\alpha}{2-2\alpha}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2-2\alpha}\right)}-\frac{v(x)}{2}-\frac{1}{4}\ln\left(\frac{k^{2}}{4u_{S}(x)}\right)+O(k^{2})\,,

(121)

Combining all terms into the exponent, we obtain

\displaystyle=-\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{4}}\left(\frac{\alpha\beta}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{\alpha}{2-2\alpha}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2-2\alpha}\right)}\,k^{-\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}-k^{2}t-\frac{v(x)}{2}+Q(x)-\frac{1}{4}\ln\left(u_{S}(x)-k^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=-k^{2}t-v(x)-\frac{1}{4}\ln\left(\frac{k^{2}}{4u_{S}(x)}\right)-\frac{1}{4}\ln\left(u_{S}(x)-k^{2}\right)+O(k^{2})\,,

(122)

now we take the small
k
limit to expand the second Log in the previous equation

\displaystyle=-k^{2}t-v(x)-\frac{1}{4}\ln\left(\frac{k^{2}}{4u_{S}(x)}\right)-\frac{1}{4}\ln\left(u_{S}(x)\right)+O(k^{2})\,

\displaystyle=-k^{2}t-v(x)-\frac{1}{4}\ln\left(\frac{k^{2}}{4}\right)+O(k^{2})

\displaystyle=-k^{2}t-v(x)-\ln\left(\sqrt{\frac{k}{2}}\right)+O(k^{2})\,.

(123)

We plug the exponent back into the original expression to find

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{{2}^{1/2}}{Z\,k^{1/2}}e^{-k^{2}t-v(x)-\ln\left(\sqrt{\frac{k}{2}}\right)+O(k^{2})}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{{2}}{Z\,k}e^{-k^{2}t-v(x)+O(k^{2})}\,.

(124)

The residue at
k=0
is

\displaystyle\mathrm{Res}

\displaystyle=-\frac{4i}{Z}e^{-v(x)}\,,

(125)

and therefore, the integral, which is
2\pi i\,\mathrm{Res}
, is equivalent to
4
times the Boltzmann-Gibbs equilibrium distribution. The imaginary unit is not important since we are interested in the imaginary part. We also have changed the integration limit from
(k_{\text{TP}}(x),\infty)
to
(-\infty,\infty)
. This is done by closing the contour through the imaginary plane, crossing the point of local maxima
\kappa^{*}
, and will require us to divide the result by a factor of 2. Further, since we are not just enveloping the pole, but crossing it, we must divide by an additional factor of 2. This allows us to recover the Boltzmann-Gibbs equilibrium solution.

VIII
The stretched exponential relaxation

The complete eigenfunction expansion allows us to find the complete expression for the PDF. In the long-time limit, the WKB solution for the eigenfunctions becomes more accurate. We also have that the eigenfunctions always decay exponentially, indicating that for calculating observable averages, smaller values of
x
are more important than larger ones. This means that in the relevant limit
x\ll x_{\text{TP}}
, we can write the eigenfunctions as

\displaystyle\psi_{k}(x)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{x^{1-\alpha}}{\beta\alpha}e^{-\frac{V(0)}{2k_{B}T}}\exp\left\{\frac{\beta x^{\alpha}}{2}-\frac{k^{2}x^{2-\alpha}}{\beta\alpha(2-\alpha)}\right\}

(126)

For finite values of
x
, the maximum contribution comes from
\kappa^{*}(z=0)
. We obtain the even part of the PDF as

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{odd}}(x,t)

\displaystyle\approx x_{0}\left(\frac{x^{1-\alpha}}{\alpha\beta}\right)\frac{\nu\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{cont}}(0)}{t^{3/2-\nu}}\sqrt{\frac{\gamma-\nu}{4\pi}}e^{\frac{V(0)}{k_{B}T}}e^{-\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{cont}}(0)t^{\nu}-\frac{\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{cont}}(0)x^{2-\alpha}}{\beta(2-\alpha)^{2}\,t^{1-\nu}}}\,.

(127)

See Eq. (
81
) for the definition of
\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{cont}}(0)
. We can now calculate the ensemble average of the asymmetry operator
\mathcal{AS}(x,t)
, defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{AS}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rr}-1~~~\mathrm{for}~~~x>0\\
0~~~\mathrm{for}~~~x=0\\
+1~~~\mathrm{for}~~~x>0\end{array}\right.\,,

and the ensemble mean is

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{AS}\rangle(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{2x_{0}}{\alpha\beta}\frac{\nu\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{cont}}(0)}{t^{3/2-\nu}}\sqrt{\frac{\gamma-\nu}{4\pi}}e^{\frac{V(0)}{k_{B}T}}e^{-\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{cont}}(0)t^{\nu}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\,x^{1-\alpha}e^{-\frac{\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{cont}}(0)x^{2-\alpha}}{\beta(2-\alpha)^{2}\,t^{1-\nu}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{x_{0}}{\alpha\beta}\frac{\nu\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{cont}}(0)}{t^{3/2-\nu}}\sqrt{\frac{\gamma-\nu}{\pi}}e^{\frac{V(0)}{k_{B}T}}e^{-\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{cont}}(0)t^{\nu}}\frac{\beta(2-\alpha)t^{1-\nu}}{\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{cont}}(0)}

\displaystyle=\frac{x_{0}}{t^{1/2}}\sqrt{\frac{\gamma-\nu}{\pi}}\,\,e^{\frac{V(0)}{k_{B}T}}e^{-\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{cont}}(0)t^{\nu}}\,.

(131)

The validity of the previous Eq. is shown numerically in Fig.
8
. We extend this result to all odd moments

\displaystyle\langle x^{2n+1}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{x_{0}}{\alpha\beta}\frac{\nu\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{cont}}(0)}{t^{3/2-\nu}}\sqrt{\frac{\gamma-\nu}{\pi}}e^{v(0)}e^{-\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{cont}}(0)t^{\nu}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\,x^{2n+2-\alpha}e^{-\frac{\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{cont}}(0)x^{2-\alpha}}{\beta(2-\alpha)^{2}\,t^{1-\nu}}}

\displaystyle=\left[{x_{0}}\left(\frac{\beta(2-\alpha)^{2}}{\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{cont}}(0)}\right)^{\frac{2n+1}{2-\alpha}}\sqrt{\frac{\gamma-\nu}{\pi}}e^{v(0)}\Gamma\left(\frac{3+2n-\alpha}{2-\alpha}\right)\right]t^{\frac{8n(1-\alpha)-\alpha^{2}}{2(2-\alpha)^{2}}}e^{-\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{cont}}(0)t^{\nu}}\,.

(132)

The even contributions will converge to the Boltzmann-Gibbs expectation. We can calculate how observables relax to this value using the
excited even part of the PDF,

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{even}}^{*}(x,t)

\displaystyle\approx-\frac{1}{Z}\left(\frac{x^{1-\alpha}}{\alpha\beta}\right)\sqrt{\frac{\gamma-\nu}{\pi t}}e^{v(0)}e^{-\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{cont}}(0)t^{\nu}-\frac{\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{cont}}(0)x^{2-\alpha}}{\beta(2-\alpha)^{2}\,t^{1-\nu}}}\,,

(133)

to obtain the excited even moments

\displaystyle\langle x^{2n}\rangle^{*}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{Z}\frac{e^{v(0)}}{\alpha\beta}\sqrt{\frac{\gamma-\nu}{\pi t}}e^{-\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{cont}}(0)t^{\nu}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\,x^{2n+1-\alpha}e^{-\frac{\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{cont}}(0)x^{2-\alpha}}{\beta(2-\alpha)^{2}\,t^{1-\nu}}}

\displaystyle=-\left[\left(\frac{\beta(2-\alpha)^{2}}{\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{cont}}(0)}\right)^{-1-\frac{2n}{2-\alpha}}\frac{2e^{v(0)}\Gamma\left(1+\frac{2n}{2-\alpha}\right)}{Z(2-\alpha)\alpha\beta}\sqrt{\frac{\gamma-\nu}{\pi}}\right]t^{\frac{4+3\alpha^{2}+8n-8\alpha(n+1)}{2(2-\alpha)^{2}}}e^{-\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{cont}}(0)t^{\nu}}\,.

(134)

It is clear that the relaxation of all observables is through a stretched exponential. Remarkably, the rate function, which typically is used to calculate the statistics of rare events, in our case controls the rate of the stretched exponential via
\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{cont}}(0)
.

IX
Path integral approach

We now present another way of obtaining the scaling rate function. It is possible to express the probability density
P(x,t)
, with initial condition
P(x,0)=\delta(x)
, using a path integral formulation. For a stochastic process that is governed by the Langevin Eq.

\displaystyle\Gamma\dot{x}=-\partial_{x}V(x)+\eta(t)\,,

(135)

where the damping constant
\Gamma
obeys Einstein’s relation,
\Gamma\equiv k_{B}T/D
, and
\eta(t)
is an unbiased normal-distributed stochastic force,
\langle\eta(t)\rangle=0
, with variance

\displaystyle\langle\eta(t)\eta(t^{\prime})\rangle=2k_{B}T\,\Gamma\,\delta(t-t^{\prime})\,.

(136)

The Langevin equation describes the system on the level of the individual possible trajectories. It is possible to extract the PDF
P(x,t)
, of a packet of particles. This PDF is equivalent to one that is obtained from the FPE (
3
), as these two systems are entirely equivalent.

One alternative way to obtain the probability density
P(x,t)
is through the path-integral formalism. In this case, the probability density at position
x
and time
t
is found by considering the contribution from all possible paths
x(t^{\prime})
, with initial position
x(0)=0
, and final position
x(t)=x
.

\displaystyle P(x,t)

\displaystyle=\int\mathcal{D}x(t^{\prime})e^{-\mathcal{A}[x(t^{\prime}),\dot{x}(t^{\prime})]}

(137)

where
\mathcal{D}x(t^{\prime})
is the Wiener measure, which will weight all possible trajectories and the action
\mathcal{A}[x(t^{\prime}),\dot{x}(t^{\prime}),t]
being

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}\big[x(t^{\prime}),\dot{x}(t^{\prime}),t\big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\Big[\dot{x}(t^{\prime})-f\big(x(t^{\prime})\big)\Big]^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,f^{\prime}\big(x(t^{\prime})\big)\,,

(138)

where we now express the position in units of
\ell
and time in units of
\ell^{2}/D
and we remind that
f(x)\equiv-\partial_{x}v(x)
. The second term in the previous equation is due to the Stratonovich interpretation of stochastic calculus.

IX.1
Equivalence with the scaling rate function

There is a trajectory
x^{*}(t^{\prime})
which minimizes the action in Eq. (
138
), and therefore is the maximum contribution to the integral in Eq. (
137
). We can define the action as a function of the final position
x
and time
t
, as
\mathcal{A}^{*}(x,t)\equiv\mathcal{A}[x^{*}(t^{\prime}),\dot{x}^{*}(t^{\prime}),t]
. Our goal here is to show that this action, in the proper scaled variables, is equivalent to the rate function we obtained by solving the differential equation in Eq. (
11
).

We are interested in the limit of
t\to\infty
, let us introduce the scaled time as
\tau\equiv t^{\prime}/t
, so that
\tau\in[0,1]
. Second, we scale the position as
z(\tau)\equiv x(t^{\prime})/t^{\gamma}
, and the final position
z\equiv x/t^{\gamma}
. The action, in these new variables, becomes

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}\big[x(t^{\prime}),\dot{x}(t^{\prime})\big]

\displaystyle=\frac{t^{\nu}}{4}\int_{0}^{1}d\tau\Big[\dot{z}(\tau)+\beta\alpha\big(z(\tau)\big)^{\alpha-1}\Big]^{2}=t^{\nu}\tilde{\mathcal{A}}\big[z(\tau),\dot{z}(\tau)\big]\,,

(139)

where we identify the scaled action
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}\big[z(\tau),\dot{z}(\tau)\big]
. To obtain the previous equation, we neglected terms of order
1/t
. We observe the expected scaling for the rate function. In the scaled variables, we have that

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{*}(x,t)=t^{\nu}\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{*}(z)\,.

(140)

To completely show the equivalence between the action at the maximal trajectory and the rate function, we first construct the equivalent Lagrangian function from Eq. (
138
),

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(x,\dot{x})=\frac{1}{4}\Big[\dot{x}-f\big(x\big)\Big]^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\,f^{\prime}\big(x\big)\,.

(141)

The corresponding Hamiltonian function is

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(x,p)=p^{2}+p\,f(x)-\frac{1}{2}\,f^{\prime}\big(x\big)\,,

(142)

where the generalized moment is
p=(\dot{x}-f(x))/2
. Through the Hamilton-Jacobi equation, we know that the action must respect the equation

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial\mathcal{A}^{*}}{\partial t}=\mathcal{H}\left(x,\frac{\partial\mathcal{A}^{*}}{\partial x}\right)=\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{A}^{*}}{\partial x}\right)^{2}+f(x)\frac{\partial\mathcal{A}^{*}}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{A}^{*}}{\partial x^{2}}\,.

(143)

By finally expressing the previous equation in the scaled variables. The partial derivatives become

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{A}^{*}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\big[t^{\nu}\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{*}(x/t^{\gamma})\big]=\frac{z\gamma\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{*\prime}(z)-\nu\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{*}(z)}{t^{1-\nu}}\,,

(144)

and

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{A}^{*}}{\partial x}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(t^{\nu}\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{*}(x/t^{\gamma})\right)=\frac{1}{t^{\gamma-\nu}}\frac{\partial\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{*}}{\partial z}\,,

(145)

leading to the differential equation

\displaystyle\left[\frac{\partial\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{*}}{\partial z}\right]^{2}-\left(\frac{\beta\alpha}{z^{1-\alpha}}+\frac{z}{2-\alpha}\right)\frac{\partial\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{*}}{\partial z}+\frac{\alpha\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{*}}{2-\alpha}=0\,,

(146)

which is identical to the one in Eq. (
11
) for the rate function. We can finally conclude that

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(z)=\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{*}(z)\,.

(147)

IX.2
Equivalence with the WKB eigenfunctions

For a system with fixed energy, that is,
\mathcal{H}(p,x)=E
, we can find the action as

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{*}(x,t)=-Et+\int_{0}^{x}dx^{\prime}p(x^{\prime})\,,

(148)

which is a solution to the Hamilton-Jacobi equation, see Eq. (
143
). Alternatively, it can be seen as the minimization of the abbreviated action, the integral term in the previous equation, where the energy is equivalent to the Lagrange multiplier for the time constraint. This can also be clearly seen in the previous equation.

The two possible values for the generalized moment
p
can be obtained, as a function of
x
and
E
, as

\displaystyle p(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{f(x)}{2}\pm\sqrt{\frac{f(x)^{2}}{4}+\frac{f^{\prime}(x)}{2}+E}

\displaystyle=-\frac{f(x)}{2}\pm\sqrt{u_{S}(x)+E}\,,

(149)

where we identified the effective potential of the Schrödinger equation inside the square root. The choice of sign in the square root term is set by the initial condition. For a positive value of
x
, we take the positive sign and vice-versa. Performing the change to the scaled variables, the scaled energy, which we define as
\epsilon
, assumes the same scaling as
\kappa^{2}
, as

\displaystyle Et\to\epsilon t^{\nu}\,,

(150)

and the action becomes

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{\star}(z)

\displaystyle=-\epsilon+\int_{0}^{z}dy\,\left(\frac{\alpha\beta}{2}y^{\alpha-1}+\sqrt{\frac{\beta^{2}\alpha^{2}}{4y^{2-2\alpha}}+\epsilon(z)}\right)

\displaystyle=-\epsilon+\frac{\beta z^{\alpha}}{2}+\int_{0}^{z}\sqrt{\frac{\beta^{2}\alpha^{2}}{4y^{2-2\alpha}}+\epsilon(z)}\,\,dy\,,

(151)

which shows a clear resemblance with Eqs. (
67
) and (
84
), with
{\kappa^{\star}}^{2}
having the same role as
\epsilon
. Next, we will look at the actual optimal paths to clearly show that indeed Eq. (
151
) is equivalent to Eqs. (
67
) and (
84
).

IX.3
Instantons

We shall obtain the optimal trajectory,
z^{\star}(\tau)
, or instanton, that minimizes
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}
. Using the Euler-Lagrange equation, we find that the optimal path must satisfy

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}z^{*}}{d\tau^{2}}=-\beta^{2}\alpha^{2}(1-\alpha)z^{2\alpha-3}\,,

(152)

with
z^{*}(0)=0
and
z^{*}(1)=z
. We multiply both sides of the previous equation by
\dot{z}
to obtain an effective energy description as

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\tau}\left[\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{d{z}^{*}}{d\tau}\right)^{2}-\frac{\beta^{2}\alpha^{2}}{2{z^{*}}^{2-2\alpha}}\right]=0\longrightarrow\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{d{z}^{*}}{d\tau}\right)^{2}-\frac{\beta^{2}\alpha^{2}}{2{z^{*}}^{2-2\alpha}}=2\epsilon(z)\,,

(153)

where the constant
\epsilon(z)
is set by the target final position
z
and plays the same role as the total energy in Eq. (
151
). The connection between the action in the previous equation and the action from the WKB approach will be explained in detail further. It is easier to solve this problem backward in time, starting at
z
until reaching the origin. This is ideal as we are interested in the
z>0
range, and as
z\to 0
, we have to be more careful to understand the role of the fine structure of the potential.

From Eq. (
151
), we can see that
\epsilon=0
is equivalent to the potential, and therefore, the Boltzmann-Gibbs rate function. The actual trajectory is obtained from Eq. (
153
),

\displaystyle\frac{dz^{*}}{d\tau}=2\sqrt{\frac{\beta^{2}\alpha^{2}}{{4z^{*}}^{2-2\alpha}}}=\frac{\beta\alpha}{{z^{*}}^{1-\alpha}}\,.

(154)

In the backwards time description, the previous equation is equivalent to a particle that starts at position
z
with kinetic energy identical to the negative of the potential energy, that is,
\dot{z}^{*}(1)=f(z)
(which is a negative value). For
z<z_{c}
, the particle reaches the origin at a finite time
\delta\tau(z)
, which is obtained directly from Eq. (
154
) as

\displaystyle\delta\tau(z)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{z}\frac{y^{1-\alpha}}{\beta\alpha}dy=\frac{z^{2-\alpha}}{\beta\alpha(2-\alpha)}=\left[\frac{z}{z_{c}}\right]^{2-\alpha}\,.

(155)

Clearly, from the previous equation, for
z=z_{c}
, the particle reaches the origin exactly at
\tau=1
. If the particle is closer to the origin than the critical
z_{c}
, the particle will reach the origin at a finite time and then become stuck at
z=0
, which is also a solution to the Euler-Lagrange equation.

The complete solution, for
z<z_{c}
, can be written as

\displaystyle z^{*}(\tau)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}0~~~\mathrm{for}~~~\tau\leq\tau_{0}\\
z_{c}\left(\tau-\tau_{0}\right)^{1/(2-\alpha)}~~~\mathrm{for}~~~\tau>\tau_{0}\end{array}\right.\,,

where
\tau_{0}=\left[{z}/{z_{c}}\right]^{2-\alpha}
is the instant where the particle leaves the origin. In the actual coordinates, being
x
and
t
, this break at
\tau=\tau_{0}
is smooth, as we can see from the numerical integrations of the Euler-Lagrange equations at finite time. The break only occurs in the
t\to\infty
scaling limit. The action can be calculated directly as

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{A}}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\int_{0}^{1}d\tau\Big[\dot{z}^{*}+\beta\alpha{z^{*}}^{\alpha-1}\Big]^{2}=\frac{1}{4}\int_{\tau_{0}}^{1}d\tau\Big[\dot{z}^{*}+\beta\alpha{z^{*}}^{\alpha-1}\Big]^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{z_{c}^{2}}{(2-\alpha)^{2}}\int_{\tau_{0}}^{1}\frac{d\tau}{(\tau-\tau_{0})^{\frac{2-2\alpha}{2-\alpha}}}=\beta z^{\alpha}\,,

(158)

which, as expected, is equivalent to the potential.

In the other case, for positions larger than the critical point,
z>z_{c}
, the particle requires the energy to be bigger than zero in order to reach the origin in the available time frame. This sharp transition is the dynamical phase transition: the system goes from the zero energy Boltzmann-Gibbs solution to a finite energy one. And, for very large value of
z
, the necessary energy becomes much greater than the potential and we obtain a straight line solution, which is equivalent to the free-particle. We will go into more detail in the next subsection, but the energy
\epsilon
is equivalent to
-{\kappa^{*}}^{2}
, which is a positive number since for
z>z_{c}
, the critical point is a purely imaginary number. We show this numerically in Fig.

IX.4
Instantons for the asymmetric part

The results we obtained thus far are consistent with the rate function of the complete solution. However, as we have found in both scaling rate function and eigenfunction approaches, there is also a distinct solution that will govern the odd part of the PDF. It is not immediately clear how to obtain these contributions through the path-integral formulation. Let us start by considering an initial condition that will cause the PDF to contain an odd part. Placing the particles initially at
x=x_{0}
, that is,

\displaystyle P(x,0)=\delta(x-x_{0})=\underbrace{\frac{\delta(x-x_{0})+\delta(x+x_{0})}{2}}_{\mathrm{Symmetric}}+\underbrace{\frac{\delta(x-x_{0})-\delta(x+x_{0})}{2}}_{\mathrm{Asymmetric}}\,,

(159)

we have the even (symmetric) and odd (asymmetric) contributions. We will now consider the paths for all possible delta functions in the previous equations. When we consider the very long time limit,
t\to\infty
, for positions that scale with
t^{\gamma}
, the contributions from paths that connect
x_{0}\to x
and
x_{0}\to-x
(
-x_{0}\to x
and
-x_{0}\to-x
) become identical and are equivalent to the instanton trajectories we discussed in the previous subsection, as the respective deltas just add their own contributions. Therefore, the symmetric part will behave as described before, exactly as expected. However, since the asymmetric terms are the difference between deltas, these identical paths will actually be canceled out.

In that case, we must look for asymmetric paths that never cross the origin, meaning for
+x_{0}
(
-x_{0}
), we will only have optimal paths for
x>0
(
x<0
). In the scaled variable,
x_{0}/t^{\gamma}\to 0
, and the problem becomes almost identical to the previous one, with the difference that we now want the particle to be as close as possible to
z=0
, without actually touching the origin. For
\epsilon=0
, we saw previously that, if
z<z_{c}
, the particle has more than enough time to reach the origin. Therefore, there is a smaller (negative) energy value where the particle will reach the region near the origin at exactly the available time.

Let us calculate the energy for the specific example
z=0
, which we remind that we mean actually near zero. When
z<z_{\dagger}
, the particle initially has a positive velocity, pointing away from the origin. It then reaches the maximum point, with zero velocity, which as we will see is equivalent to the
z_{\text{TP}}
, and finally returns to the origin.

We use the Eq. (
153
) for the energy balance to write the general expression

\displaystyle\frac{dz^{*}}{2\sqrt{\epsilon(z)+\frac{\beta^{2}\alpha^{2}}{{4z^{*}}^{2-2\alpha}}}}=d\tau\,.

(160)

The integral of
d\tau
on the right of the previous equation will give us 1, which is the total time. On the left-hand side, the integral must be performed on the ascending path of the trajectory
z\to z_{\text{TP}}
and then, with the opposite sign, at the descending side of the trajectory
z_{\text{TP}}\to 0
. Explicitly, we have

\displaystyle\int_{z}^{z_{\text{TP}}}\frac{dz^{*}}{2\sqrt{\epsilon(z)+\frac{\beta^{2}\alpha^{2}}{{4z^{*}}^{2-2\alpha}}}}+\int_{0}^{z_{\text{TP}}}\frac{dz^{*}}{2\sqrt{\epsilon(z)+\frac{\beta^{2}\alpha^{2}}{{4z^{*}}^{2-2\alpha}}}}=1\,,

(161)

where we highlight that the value of the energy is related to the turning point as
z_{\text{TP}}
, as
\epsilon(z)=\beta^{2}\alpha^{2}/(4z_{\text{TP}}^{2-2\alpha})
. In the case of
z=0
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{z_{\text{TP}}}\frac{dz^{*}}{\sqrt{-\frac{\beta^{2}\alpha^{2}}{{4z_{\text{TP}}}^{2-2\alpha}}+\frac{\beta^{2}\alpha^{2}}{{4z^{*}}^{2-2\alpha}}}}=1\,,

(162)

and we obtain that

\displaystyle z_{\text{TP}}(0)

\displaystyle=\left[\left(\frac{2}{\beta\alpha}\right)\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{4}}\left(\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\right)\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{\alpha}{2-2\alpha}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2-2\alpha}\right)}\right]^{-1/(2-\alpha)}\,,

(163)

and the energy is

\displaystyle\epsilon(0)=-\left[\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{4}}\left(\frac{\beta\alpha}{2}\right)^{1/(1-\alpha)}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{\alpha}{2-2\alpha}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2-2\alpha}\right)}\right]^{(2-2\alpha)/(2-\alpha)}=-\kappa^{*}(0)^{2}\,,

(164)

which is equivalent to Eq. (
81
) (last equality of the previous equation). Another point we can find exactly is the
z=z_{\dagger}
, where the particle starts at the top of the trajectory and then we have
z_{\text{TP}}=z=z_{\dagger}
. We can find the value of
z_{\dagger}
through

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{z_{\dagger}}\frac{dz^{*}}{2\sqrt{-\frac{\beta^{2}\alpha^{2}}{{4z_{\dagger}}^{2-2\alpha}}+\frac{\beta^{2}\alpha^{2}}{{4z^{*}}^{2-2\alpha}}}}=1\,,

(165)

and we immediately recover the
z_{\dagger}
obtained from the WKB action, and

\displaystyle\epsilon(z_{\dagger})=-\left[\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{16}}\left(\frac{\beta\alpha}{2}\right)^{1/(1-\alpha)}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{\alpha}{2-2\alpha}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2-2\alpha}\right)}\right]^{(2-2\alpha)/(2-\alpha)}=-\kappa^{*}(z_{\dagger})^{2}\,.

(166)

Using numerical simulations at finite times, we show that, as time increases, the effective energy approaches our predictions both for the symmetric and asymmetric case, see Fig.
10
. The dynamical phase transition happens for the symmetric as the effective energy switches from
\epsilon=0
to a finite value for
z>z_{c}
.

X
Final remarks

In this paper, we present a comprehensive study of weakly bound Brownian particles. We employ three distinct analytical approaches: scaling rate-function solutions, eigenfunction expansions (through the WKB method), and path integral formalism, demonstrating their equivalence. Our findings are summarized by the equation

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(z)=\mathcal{S}(z,\kappa^{)}=\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{(}z),.

(167)

The rate function
\mathcal{I}(z)
, obtained using a scaling ansatz, is determined through the non-linear differential equation (
11
). The action
\mathcal{S}
is derived using an eigenfunction approach (see Eqs. (
67
) and (
84
)), which involves a semi-classical approximation (WKB method). We find that this action at the critical point
\kappa^{*}
(see Eq. (
70
)) is identical to
\mathcal{I}(z)
. The action
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{*}(z)
(see Eq.,(
140
)) is obtained through the path-integral method, using the path of minimal action, and is equivalent to the previous two rate functions.

In all three approaches we have a dynamical phase transition occurring at a critical point
z_{c}
(Eq. (
15
)), where the rate function switches between two analytical expressions: the equilibrium rate function
\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{BG}}(z)
, and the rate function from the continuum modes
\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{cont}}(z)
(see Fig.
2
). This second-order transition is characterized by a discontinuity in the second derivative. We also showed that the appearance of the rate function is connected with parity and initial conditions.

The dynamical exponents
\nu
and
\gamma
, as functions of
\alpha
(see Eq. (
10
)), are consistent across all approaches. These exponents, along with properties such as the dynamical phase transition and stretched exponential relaxation, are expected to remain valid in general dimensions. However, the role of parity in the various rate functions requires careful investigation.

We note that dynamical phase transitions also occur for
1<\alpha<2
. In this case, the scaling exponents are larger than one, indicating that
x_{c}(t)
grows super-linearly with time, as
\gamma>1
. For
\alpha\geq 2
, the equilibrium becomes more confining than the Gaussian free-particle, causing the scaling approach to break down and eliminating the dynamical phase transition. The harmonic oscillator represents a special case where the exponents diverge.

Our findings hint that the multi-valued nature of the rate function may be present in more systems. In our analysis using the path integral formalism, it becomes clear that the choice between symmetric and asymmetric paths may lead to different paths and therefore different actions. The rate function at
z=0
,
\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{cont}}(0)
, governs the rate of relaxation of the stretched exponential, which is easier to access through experiments. This is remarkable, as the rate function is usually related to fluctuations and rare events. Therefore, a possible extension to this work is to search for physical systems where the rate function can play a meaningful role that can be experimentally probed.

Finally, given that the Boltzmann measure,
e^{-V(x)/k_{B}T}
, is sub-exponential, statistical features of the system are described by the big jump principle
[
43
]
. This principle may become apparent when time-integrated observables of the underlying processes are considered, although further research is required in this area.
