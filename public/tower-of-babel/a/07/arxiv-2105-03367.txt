Abstract
We provide a new analysis technique to measure the effect of the isotropic polarization rotation, induced by e.g. the isotropic cosmic birefringence from axion-like particles and a miscalibration of CMB polarization angle, via mode coupling in the cosmic microwave background (CMB). Several secondary effects such as gravitational lensing and CMB optical-depth anisotropies lead to mode coupling in observed CMB anisotropies, i.e., non-zero off-diagonal elements in the observed CMB covariance. To derive the mode coupling, however, we usually assume no parity violation in the observed CMB anisotropies. We first derive a new contribution to the CMB mode coupling arising from parity violation in observed CMB. Since the isotropic polarization rotation leads to parity violation in the observed CMB anisotropies, we then discuss the use of the new mode coupling for constraining the isotropic polarization angle. We find that constraints on the isotropic polarization angle by measuring the new mode-coupling contribution are comparable to that using the E ​ B EB cross-power spectrum in future high-sensitivity polarization experiments such as CMB-S4 and LiteBIRD. Thus, this technique can be used to cross-check results obtained by the use of the E ​ B EB cross-power spectrum.

Introduction

The cosmic microwave background (CMB) anisotropies have been measured precisely over the past decades, and its angular power spectrum has been used to tightly constrain cosmological parameters (e.g.
Planck Collaboration 2020a
). Precise measurements of CMB anisotropies also allow us to extract information involved in statistics beyond the angular power spectrum. For example, the gravitational lensing effect on CMB leads to mode coupling between different multipoles of observed CMB anisotropies. Multiple works have developed analysis methods to extract gravitational lensing signals from the mode coupling
(
Hu &
Okamoto 2002
;
Okamoto &
Hu 2003
)

et al. 2017
;

;

;
POLARBEAR Collaboration 2020
;

), and cross-power spectra between CMB lensing and other cosmological probes (e.g.
Larsen
et al. 2016
;
Omori
et al. 2019
;
Namikawa et al. 2019
;
Darwish
et al. 2020
;
Robertson
et al. 2020
and references therein). In addition to lensing, mode coupling in observed CMB anisotropies can be produced by several secondary effects, including the anisotropic polarization rotation, the so-called
anisotropic
cosmic birefringence (e.g.,
Kamionkowski 2009
;
Gluscevic et al. 2012
;
POLARBEAR Collaboration 2015
;
Bicep2 / Keck Array
Collaboration 2017
;
Planck Collaboration 2016
;
Namikawa
et al. 2020
;
Bianchini
et al. 2020
), the anisotropic CMB optical-depth (e.g.,

;
Gluscevic
et al. 2013
;
Namikawa 2018
;
Roy
et al. 2018
;
Namikawa et al. 2021
), and instrumental and astrophysical systematics
(
Yadav
et al. 2010
;
Namikawa
et al. 2013
;
Namikawa &
Takahashi 2014
;
Osborne
et al. 2014
;

;
Sailer
et al. 2020
)
.

When deriving the mode coupling, however, we usually assume no parity violation in the observed CMB anisotropies. In the presence of parity violation, the temperature-
B
and
EB
cross-power spectra do not vanish. These parity-odd power spectra are further distorted by the above secondary effects, and the observed CMB anisotropies have new mode coupling by parity violation. Extracting this new contribution in mode coupling allows us to extract information on parity violation in the observed CMB anisotropies.

One of the sources of parity violation is the
isotropic
cosmic birefringence in which the CMB linear polarization is rotated by a same angle in all of the line-of-sight directions. Several types of beyond-the-Standard-Model physics can source the isotropic cosmic birefringence such as axion-like particles (ALPs) that couple to photons through a so-called Chern-Simons term (see e.g.,
Carroll 1998
;
Li & Zhang 2008
;
Pospelov
et al. 2009
;
Finelli &
Galaverni 2009
;
Hlozek
et al. 2018
;
Fujita
et al. 2021b
;
Fujita
et al. 2021a
;
Takahashi &
Yin 2021
and a review,
Marsh 2016

(
Capparelli et al. 2020
)
.

Another source of the parity-odd spectra is a miscalibration of the instrumental polarization angle. Calibration of the instrumental polarization angle is accomplished using either hardware calibrators or by measurements of polarized astrophysical sources, but accuracy of these methods is worse than
\mathcal{O}(0.1)\,
deg
(
Kaufman et al. 2014
;
POLARBEAR Collaboration 2014
;
Koopman
et al. 2016
;
Planck Collaboration 2016
;
Cornelison
et al. 2020
)
. A miscalibration of the polarization angle by
\mathcal{O}(0.1)\,
deg could produce a non-negligible bias in e.g. measuring the inflationary gravitational waves. To circumvent this situation, in future CMB experiments,
Keating
et al. 2013
proposed to use the
EB
cross-power spectrum to determine the instrumental polarization angle with the assumption that there are no other sources of parity violation (hereafter, the
EB
spectrum method).

In this paper, we first derive new CMB mode coupling by the presence of parity violation in the observed CMB anisotropies, i.e., by assuming the non-zero temperature-
B
and
EB
parity-odd spectra. We derive a quadratic estimator to extract this new mode coupling analogues to the gravitational lensing.
If the parity-odd spectra are generated by the polarization rotation, the new mode coupling depends on the polarization angle.
Thus, we discuss constraining the polarization angle by extracting the new mode coupling via the quadratic estimator and compare its sensitivity to the
EB
spectrum method.

This paper is organized as follows. In Sec.
2

3
, we consider constraints on the isotropic polarization angle by extracting the mode coupling. Sec.
4

CMB mode coupling: Gravitational lensing

The lensed CMB temperature anisotropies,

2.2
CMB mode coupling: Other sources

Mode coupling is led by not only the gravitational lensing but also the anisotropies in CMB optical depth, anisotropic cosmic birefringence, observational effects such as survey window, masking and inhomogeneous noise, and extragalactic foregrounds.
If multiple effects introduce mode coupling, the right-hand-side of
Eq.

13
becomes the sum of all these effects:

\displaystyle\sum_{x}\sum_{LM}\begin{pmatrix}\ell&\ell^{\prime}&L\\
m&m^{\prime}&M\end{pmatrix}f^{x,({\rm XY})}_{\ell L\ell^{\prime}}x^{*}_{LM}\,,

(22)

where
x
denotes a field causing the mode coupling, e.g., the anisotropic birefringence angle, anisotropic CMB optical depth, lensing potential,
\phi
, and curl mode
\varpi
. The functional form of
f^{x,(\rm XY)}_{\ell L\ell^{\prime}}
depends on the field,
x
.

In general, we can decompose mode coupling terms into two groups in terms of parity symmetry; one has the same parity symmetry as the lensing potential such as masking and point sources (parity even), and the other has the opposite parity symmetry to the lensing potential such as the curl mode and anisotropic cosmic birefringence (parity odd).
Analogues to the simultaneous estimate of the lensing potential and curl mode
(
Namikawa
et al. 2012
)
, the difference of the parity symmetry allows us to separately estimate the mode coupling terms from the parity even and parity odd groups.

2.3
CMB mode coupling from polarization rotation

Next, we generalize the mode coupling described in the previous section to the case when parity violation exists in the observed CMB anisotropies. As an example, we first consider a case in the presence of the isotropic polarization rotation in which the observed polarization is given by:

\displaystyle P^{\prime}(\hat{\bm{n}})=\hskip 1.00006pt{\rm e}^{2{\rm i}\hskip 0.40974pt\beta}\widetilde{P}(\hat{\bm{n}})\,.

(23)

Here,
\widetilde{P}
is the lensed CMB anisotropies, and
\beta
is the isotropic polarization angle which does not depend on observed line-of-sight directions.

For example,
\beta
equals to a miscalibrated polarization angle. The polarization angle,
\beta
, is also interpreted as the isotropic cosmic birefringence angle induced by the ALPs as follows. We first note that, in the absence of lensing, the isotropic cosmic birefringence effect on CMB by the ALPs is given by:

(24)

where

(

et al. 1990
;

Sikivie 1992

In the presence of lensing, the path of CMB photons is deflected and the CMB polarization is rotated along the deflected path. If the ALP fields do not depend on spatial coordinates,
\beta
does not depend on the path of CMB photons, i.e., observed CMB anisotropies at each direction are rotated by the same angle,

), we find that the kernel functions arising from the non-zero odd-parity spectra are given by:

\displaystyle f^{\widetilde{x},(\rm\Theta E)}_{\ell L\ell^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=p_{\ell L\ell^{\prime}}W^{x,-}_{\ell^{\prime}L\ell}C^{\rm\Theta B}_{\ell}\,,

(40)

\displaystyle f^{\widetilde{x},(\rm\Theta B)}_{\ell L\ell^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=W^{x,0}_{\ell L\ell^{\prime}}C^{\rm\Theta B}_{\ell^{\prime}}+p_{\ell L\ell^{\prime}}W^{x,+}_{\ell^{\prime}L\ell}C^{\rm\Theta B}_{\ell}\,,

(41)

\displaystyle f^{\widetilde{x},(\rm EE)}_{\ell L\ell^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=W^{x,-}_{\ell L\ell^{\prime}}C^{\rm EB}_{\ell^{\prime}}+p_{\ell L\ell^{\prime}}W^{x,-}_{\ell^{\prime}L\ell}C^{\rm EB}_{\ell}\,,

(42)

\displaystyle f^{\widetilde{x},(\rm EB)}_{\ell L\ell^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=W^{x,+}_{\ell L\ell^{\prime}}C^{\rm EB}_{\ell^{\prime}}+p_{\ell L\ell^{\prime}}W^{x,+}_{\ell^{\prime}L\ell}C^{\rm EB}_{\ell}\,,

(43)

\displaystyle f^{\widetilde{x},(\rm BB)}_{\ell L\ell^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=-W^{x,-}_{\ell L\ell^{\prime}}C^{\rm EB}_{\ell^{\prime}}-p_{\ell L\ell^{\prime}}W^{x,-}_{\ell^{\prime}L\ell}C^{\rm EB}_{\ell}\,,

(44)

where
Eq.

13
has the following new term:

\displaystyle\langle X^{\prime}_{\ell m}Y^{\prime}_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm CMB}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x}\sum_{LM}\begin{pmatrix}\ell&\ell^{\prime}&L\\
m&m^{\prime}&M\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\qquad\times[f^{x,({\rm XY})}_{\ell L\ell^{\prime}}+f^{\widetilde{x},({\rm XY})}_{\ell L\ell^{\prime}}]x^{*}_{LM}\,.

(45)

Note that, substituting
C^{\rm\Theta B}=2\beta C^{\rm\Theta E}
and
C^{\rm EB}=2\beta(C^{\rm EE}-C^{\rm BB})
into the above kernel functions, dividing the kernel functions by
\beta
, and choosing
x=\phi
, we reproduce the same kernel function as that obtained in
Eqs.

34
,
35
,
36
,
37
and
38
.

Finally, the quadratic estimator of
\widetilde{x}
is the same as that of
x
but with the kernel functions given by
Eqs.

40
,
41
,
42
,
43
and
44
in
Eqs.

20
and
21
. Due to the property of parity symmetry,
Eq.

39
holds for
x
and
\widetilde{x}
. This means that the quadratic estimator of
\widetilde{x}
is not biased by the presence of
x
at the leading order of
x
.

Here, we estimate sensitivity of a measurement of
\widetilde{\phi}
to the isotropic polarization angle. We consider a situation where the reconstructed
\widetilde{\phi}
is cross-correlated with a mass tracer of the large-scale structure,
m
, such as the cosmic infrared background (CIB) and galaxies. Because the cross-power spectrum probes
\beta C_{L}^{\phi\rm m}
and
C_{L}^{\phi\rm m}
is also observable, we can estimate the isotropic polarization angle,
\beta

We compute the expected
1\,\sigma
uncertainties of
\beta
from a measurement of the cross-correlation between
\widetilde{\phi}
and a mass tracer,
m
, as:

\displaystyle\sigma^{-2}(\beta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{L}\frac{(2L+1)f_{\rm sky}}{(C_{L}^{\widetilde{\phi}\widetilde{\phi}}+N_{L}^{\widetilde{\phi}\widetilde{\phi}})(C_{L}^{\rm mm}+N_{L}^{\rm mm})+C_{L}^{\widetilde{\phi}\rm m}}\left(\dfrac{\partial C_{L}^{\widetilde{\phi}\rm m}}{\partial\beta}\right)^{2}\,,

(46)

where
f_{\rm sky}
is the observed sky fraction and
N_{L}^{\widetilde{\phi}\widetilde{\phi}}
is the reconstruction noise spectrum. The reconstruction noise is dominated by the
BB
estimator at lower noise experiments and
EB
estimator at higher noise experiments. Thus, we evaluate a minimum variance reconstruction noise as
1/N_{L}^{\widetilde{\phi}\widetilde{\phi}}=1/A_{L}^{\widetilde{\phi},(EB)}+1/A_{L}^{\widetilde{\phi},(BB)}
. In the small angle limit,
\beta\ll 1
, the angular power spectra,
C_{L}^{\widetilde{\phi}\widetilde{\phi}}
and
C_{L}^{\widetilde{\phi}\rm m}
, are negligible in the denominator, and the above equation becomes:

\displaystyle\sigma^{-2}(\beta)

\displaystyle\simeq\sum_{L}\frac{(2L+1)f_{\rm sky}(C_{L}^{\phi\rm m})^{2}}{N_{L}^{\widetilde{\phi}\widetilde{\phi}}(C_{L}^{\rm mm}+N_{L}^{\rm mm})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{L}(2L+1)f_{\rm sky}\rho^{2}_{L}\frac{C_{L}^{\phi\phi}}{N_{L}^{\widetilde{\phi}\widetilde{\phi}}}\,.

(47)

Here,
\rho_{L}\equiv C_{L}^{\phi\rm m}/\sqrt{C^{\phi\phi}_{L}(C^{\rm mm}_{L}+N^{\rm mm}_{L})}
is the correlation coefficient between the true CMB lensing potential and the observed mass tracer.

with the
EB

\displaystyle\sigma^{-2}(\beta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell}\frac{(2\ell+1)f_{\rm sky}}{(C^{\rm EE}_{\ell}+N^{\rm P}_{\ell})(C^{\rm BB}_{\ell}+N^{\rm P}_{\ell})+C^{\rm EB}_{\ell}}\left(\dfrac{\partial C^{\rm EB}_{\ell}}{\partial\beta}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\simeq 4\sum_{\ell}\frac{(2\ell+1)f_{\rm sky}(C^{\rm EE}_{\ell})^{2}}{(C^{\rm EE}_{\ell}+N^{\rm P}_{\ell})(C^{\rm BB}_{\ell}+N^{\rm P}_{\ell})}\,,

(48)

where

is the CMB polarization noise power spectrum.

4
Summary and Discussion

We have explored the mode coupling involved in observed CMB anisotropies when the
\Theta B
and
EB
spectra are non-zero. We first derived the estimator for
\widetilde{\phi}
to extract the mode coupling introduced by lensing. We then generalized it to other mode-coupling cases.
As an example of the application of the new mode coupling measurement, we explored the constraint on the isotropic polarization angle by reconstructing
\widetilde{\phi}
. We showed that the polarization angle can be constrained by cross-correlating the reconstructed
\widetilde{\phi}
and mass tracers of the large-scale structure. The expected constraints on the polarization angle would be comparable to that obtained from the
EB
spectrum method in future high precision polarization measurements such as CMB-S4
(
CMB-S4
Collaboration 2019
)
and LiteBIRD
(
Hazumi
et al. 2019
)
.

The method discussed in this paper can be used to cross-check the isotropic polarization angle measured from the
EB
spectrum method because the cross-power spectrum between
\widetilde{\phi}
and a mass tracer have a different response to observational systematics compared to the
EB
spectrum. For example, the cross-power spectrum does not have a bias from the instrumental noise since the CMB noise and mass tracers does not correlate. Similarly, pointing errors could introduce a bias in the
EB
spectrum
(
Shimon
et al. 2008
)
but do not in the cross-power spectrum with a mass tracer. The temperature-to-polarization leakage, on the other hand, could lead to a bias in the cross-power spectrum. The temperature-to-polarization leakage leads to non-zero correlations in the observed odd-parity spectra,
\Theta B
and
EB

(
Planck Collaboration 2016
)
. The kernel functions given in
Eqs.

40
,
41
,
42
,
43
and
44
then become non-zero even without the polarization rotation and introduce new mode-coupling terms in
Eqs.

29
,
30
,
31
,
32
and
33
which are proportional to
\phi
as analogous to the isotropic polarization rotation. However, the kernel functions are different from that of the isotropic polarization rotation. Therefore, we can distinguish these contributions by constructing a bias-hardened estimator
(
Namikawa
et al. 2013
;
Namikawa &
Takahashi 2014
)
.

To constrain the cosmological signals of the isotropic cosmic birefringence, we need to mitigate an error of the polarization angle. The current accuracy of the polarization angle calibration is typically worse than
\sim 0.1
deg which is larger than the statistical errors in ongoing and future CMB experiments. Thus, we need to develop methods to avoid the large instrumental systematic bias in estimating the isotropic cosmic birefringence signals. In the
EB
spectrum method,
Minami et al. 2019
proposes to use the Galactic foregrounds which partially breaks the degeneracy between a bias from a miscalibration angle and signals of the isotropic cosmic birefringence. The method is already demonstrated with Planck data
(
Minami &
Komatsu 2020
)
. Analogous to the
EB
spectrum method, we can use the Galactic foregrounds involved in
\widetilde{\phi}
. For example, the cross-power spectrum between
\widetilde{\phi}
and foreground-dominant frequency maps contains a Galactic foreground bispectrum. Although we need a model for the intrinsic Galactic foreground bispectrum, the joint measurements of the Galactic foreground bispectrum and
C_{L}^{\widetilde{\phi}m}
would break the degeneracy between a bias from an instrumental polarization angle error and the isotropic cosmic birefringence signal.
