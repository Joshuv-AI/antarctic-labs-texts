Long time behaviour of solutions to non-local and non-linear dispersion problems
Maciej Tadej maciej.tadej@math.uni.wroc.pl

Abstract
This paper explores a non-linear, non-local model describing the evolution of a single species. We investigate scenarios where the spatial domain is either an arbitrary bounded and open subset of the n n -dimensional Euclidean space or a periodic environment modeled by n n -dimensional torus. The analysis includes the study of spectrum of the linear, bounded operator in the considered equation, which is a scaled, non-local analogue of classical Laplacian with Neumann boundaries. In particular we show the explicit formulas for eigenvalues and eigenfunctions. Moreover we show the asymptotic behaviour of eigenvalues. Within the context of the non-linear evolution problem, we establish the existence of an invariant region, give a criterion for convergence to the mean mass, and construct spatially heterogeneous steady states.



Keywords
:
non-local dispersion, spectral analysis, asymptotics, equilibria, stability

Introduction

1.1
Statement and main results

In this paper we study properties of solutions
u:=u(x,t)
of the following non-linear, non-local model describing the evolution of a density of single species.

\displaystyle u_{t}(x,t)=\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}u(x,t)+f(u(x,t))

(x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,\infty),

(1.1)

\displaystyle u(x,0)=u_{0}(x)

x\in\Omega,

(1.2)

where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a bounded and open domain. We assume that an initial datum
u_{0}\in L^{2}\left(\Omega\right)
, a scaling parameter
\varepsilon>0
and the force term
f\in C^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)
. The dispersion operator
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}:L^{2}\left(\Omega\right)\rightarrow L^{2}\left(\Omega\right)
is given by the formula

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}u(x,t)=\int_{\Omega}J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\left(u(y,t)-u(x,t)\right)\>dy,

(1.3)

where
J_{\varepsilon}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is a properly scaled version of the integral kernel
J:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. Here, we distinguishh two frameworks stated in the following assumptions.

Assumption 1.1

(General case)
.

Denote by
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
an open and bounded region. We consider the integral kernels
J\in C(\mathbb{R}^{n})
satisfying the following properties

(J1)

J(z)\geq 0
for
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
J(0)>0

(J2)

J(z)=J(-z)
for
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

(J3)

J(z)\rightarrow 0
as
|z|\rightarrow\infty
,

(J4)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}J(z)|z|^{2}dz<\infty
.

(J5)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}J(z)dz=1
.

We introduce
\varepsilon>0
and
m\in[0,2]
to define the re-scaled kernel
J_{\varepsilon}\in C(\mathbb{R}^{n})

J_{\varepsilon}(z)=\frac{C_{norm}}{\varepsilon^{m+n}}J\left(\frac{z}{\varepsilon}\right),\quad C_{norm}=\left(\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}J(z)|z|^{2}\>dz\right)^{-1}.

(1.4)

Now we define the setting where dispersion outside the domain is identified by the reflection of boundaries.

Assumption 1.2

(Periodic case)
.

Denote by
\mathbb{T}^{n}=(-\pi,\pi)^{n}
an
n
-dimensional torus. We consider the integral kernels
J\in C(\mathbb{R}^{n})
satisfying claims
(J1)
-
(J3)
of Assumption
1.4
. Next we introduce
\varepsilon>0
and
m\in[0,2]
to define the re-scaled, truncated and periodized kernel

given by

J_{\varepsilon}(z)=\frac{C_{norm}^{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon^{m+n}}J_{per}\left(\frac{z}{\varepsilon}\right),\quad C_{norm}^{\varepsilon}=\left(\frac{1}{2}\int_{\left(-\frac{\pi}{\varepsilon},\frac{\pi}{\varepsilon}\right)^{n}}J_{per}(z)|z|^{2}\>dz\right)^{-1}.

(1.5)

We extended the kernel periodically in such a way that

-periodic function and so

2
Spectral analysis

2.1
Main results of the section

Here we perform the spectral analysis of the operator
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}:L^{2}(\Omega)\rightarrow L^{2}(\Omega)
given by formula (
1.3
). For that we decompose the dispersion operator

\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}u(x)=\int_{\Omega}J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)u(y)\>dy+b_{\varepsilon}(x)u(x),

(2.1)

where

b_{\varepsilon}(x)=-\int_{\Omega}J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\>dy.

(2.2)

We characterise the spectrum
\sigma(\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon})
on an arbitrary bounded domain
\Omega
.

Theorem 2.1

(General case)
.

Let
J_{\varepsilon}
and
\Omega
satisfy Assumption
1.4
. Denote
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}:L^{2}\left(\Omega\right)\rightarrow L^{2}\left(\Omega\right)
as in a formula (
2.1
). Then the spectrum of
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}
is given by

\sigma(\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon})=b_{\varepsilon}\left[\>\overline{\Omega}\>\right]\cup\left\{\beta_{k}\right\}_{k=0}^{\infty},

(2.3)

where

b_{\varepsilon}\left[\>\overline{\Omega}\>\right]=\left\{-\int_{\Omega}J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\>dy\>\Big|\>x\in\overline{\Omega}\right\}

and the sequence
\left\{\beta_{k}\right\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
is a set of isolated eigenvalues of
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}
.

Next we consider the spatial domain
\Omega=\mathbb{T}^{n}
being an
n
-dimensional torus. In this approach we conclude the following

Remark 2.1
.

Let
J_{\varepsilon}
and
\mathbb{T}^{n}
satisfy Assumption
1.2
. Denote the set
b_{\varepsilon}\left[\>\overline{\Omega}\>\right]
as in Theorem
2.1
. Then this set is a single point. In particular we have

b_{\varepsilon}\left[\>\overline{\Omega}\>\right]=\left\{-\hat{J}_{\varepsilon}(0)\right\},

where
\hat{J}_{\varepsilon}(k)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}J_{\varepsilon}(z)e^{ikz}\>dz
is a
k
-th Fourier coefficient of the kernel
J_{\varepsilon}
.

In this setting we will also derive the explicit formulas for the eigenvalues
\left\{\beta_{k}\right\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
and their corresponding eigenfunctions
\left\{\varphi_{k}\right\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
from the Theorem
2.1
.

Theorem 2.2

(Periodic case)
.

Let
J_{\varepsilon}
and
\mathbb{T}^{n}
satisfy Assumption
1.2
. Denote
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}:L^{2}\left(\mathbb{T}^{n}\right)\rightarrow L^{2}\left(\mathbb{T}^{n}\right)
as in a formula (
2.1
). Then the spectrum of
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}
is given by

\sigma(\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon})=\left\{-\hat{J}_{\varepsilon}(0)\right\}\cup\left\{\beta\right\}_{k=0}^{\infty}.

(2.4)

Eigenvalues and the corresponding eigenfunctions are given explicitly as follows

\beta_{k}(\varepsilon):=\hat{J}_{\varepsilon}(k)-\hat{J}_{\varepsilon}(0)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}J_{\varepsilon}(z)(\cos(k\cdot z)-1)\>dz,

(2.5)

\varphi_{k}^{1}(x)=\cos(k\cdot x),\quad\varphi_{k}^{2}(x)=\sin(k\cdot x),

(2.6)

where
\hat{J}_{\varepsilon}(k)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}J_{\varepsilon}(z)e^{ikz}\>dz
is a
k
-th Fourier coefficient of the kernel
J_{\varepsilon}
. Moreover the following properties hold

(i)

The eigenvalues
\{\beta_{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
are real, bounded and non-positive for
k\neq 0
.

(ii)

The limit eigenvalue is an element of the spectrum. In particular

\beta_{k}\xrightarrow{|k|\rightarrow\infty}\beta_{\infty}(\varepsilon)=-\hat{J}_{\varepsilon}(0)=b_{\varepsilon}(0).

(2.7)

When
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
then the limit eigenvalue is bounded in a following way

-\varepsilon^{-m}\leq\beta_{\infty}(\varepsilon)\leq-\frac{\varepsilon^{2-m}}{\pi^{2}}.

(2.8)

(iii)

When
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
and
m=2
then the eigenvalues
\{\beta_{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
are given explicitly by

\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\beta_{k}(\varepsilon)=-\frac{|k|^{2}}{n}

(2.9)

If
m\in[0,2)
then the limit becomes zero

2.2
Proof of Theorem
2.1

Firstly we show that the set
b_{\varepsilon}\left[\>\overline{\Omega}\>\right]
belongs to the spectrum of
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
J_{\varepsilon}
and
\Omega
satisfy Assumption
1.4
. Then for
b_{\varepsilon}
defined as in formula (
2.2
) we obtain the inclusion

b_{\varepsilon}\left[\>\overline{\Omega}\>\right]\subseteq\sigma(\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}).

Proof.

Let
\lambda\in b_{\varepsilon}\left[\>\Omega\>\right]
. Suppose that
\lambda\notin\sigma(\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon})
. Then for all
v\in L^{2}\left(\Omega\right)
there is a solution
u\in L^{2}\left(\Omega\right)
to the equation

\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}u(x)-\lambda u(x)=v(x).

As a consequence of bounded inverse theorem, there exists
C>0
such that

C\|u\|_{2}\leq\|\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}u-\lambda u\|_{2}.

(2.10)

By the continuity of
J
, there exists
x_{\lambda}\in\Omega
such that
b_{\varepsilon}(x_{\lambda})=\lambda
. Hence we take
u_{R}^{\lambda}(x)=R^{-n/2}\mathds{1}_{B(x_{\lambda},R)}
and easily verify that it is a function of unit norm and it satisfies the upper bound of a following form

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}u_{R}^{\lambda}-\lambda u_{R}^{\lambda}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\left(\int_{\Omega}J_{\varepsilon}(\cdot-y)u_{R}^{\lambda}(y)\>dy\right)\right\|_{2}+\|\left(b_{\varepsilon}-\lambda\right)u_{R}^{\lambda}\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{\Omega}\left(\int_{B(x_{\lambda},R)}J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)R^{-n/2}\>dy\right)^{2}\>dx\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle+\left(\int_{B(x_{\lambda},R)}\left(b_{\varepsilon}(x)-\lambda\right)^{2}dx\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\|J_{\varepsilon}\|_{\infty}|\Omega|^{1/2}R^{n/2}+\left(\int_{B(x_{\lambda},R)}\left(b_{\varepsilon}(x)-\lambda\right)^{2}\>dx\right)^{1/2}\xrightarrow{R\rightarrow 0}0.

But it contradicts (
2.10
), which yields
\lambda\in\sigma(\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon})
. We conclude saying that
\sigma(\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon})
must be a closed set since it is a spectrum of bounded linear operator.
∎

Next, we show that every other element of the spectrum is an eigenvalue of
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
J_{\varepsilon}
and
\Omega
satisfy Assumption
1.4
,
\varepsilon>0
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Then every
\lambda\in\sigma(\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon})\setminus b_{\varepsilon}\left[\>\overline{\Omega}\>\right]
is an eigenvalue of the operator
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}
given in (
1.3
).

Proof.

Denote a set
D=\mathbb{C}\setminus b_{\varepsilon}\left[\>\overline{\Omega}\>\right]
. This set is open and connected. Now we re-write equation (
1.6
) using formula (
2.1
) to obtain

\int_{\Omega}J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)u(y)\>dy=\left(\beta-b_{\varepsilon}(x)\right)u(x).

We divide both sides of the equation to get the fixed point problem and denote the analytic, operator valued function
F:D\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(L^{2}(\Omega))
as follows

F(\beta)[u](x):=\int_{\Omega}\frac{J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)}{\beta-b_{\varepsilon}(x)}u(y)\>dy=u(x).

Now, let us verify the convergence of the difference quotient

\displaystyle\frac{F(\beta)-F(\beta_{0})}{\beta-\beta_{0}}[u](x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\beta-\beta_{0}}\left(\int_{\Omega}\frac{J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)}{\beta-b_{\varepsilon}(x)}u(y)\>dy-\int_{\Omega}\frac{J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)}{\beta_{0}-b_{\varepsilon}(x)}u(y)\>dy\right)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}\frac{J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)}{(\beta-b_{\varepsilon}(x))(\beta_{0}-b_{\varepsilon}(x))}u(y)\>dy

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\beta_{0}\rightarrow\beta}-\int_{\Omega}\frac{J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)}{(\beta-b_{\varepsilon}(x))^{2}}u(y)\>dy,

which proves that
F:D\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(L^{2}\left(\Omega\right))
is an analytic operator, as claimed.

We emphasize that since
\beta
never intersects the range of
b_{\varepsilon}
then the integral kernel
K(x,y)=\frac{J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)}{\beta-b_{\varepsilon}(x)}
has a finite
L^{2}(\Omega\times\Omega)
norm and so it is a Hilbert-Schmidt operator. In particular it is a compact operator.

Now, we verify that
F
is a contraction for some
\beta\in D
. For instance we can take
\beta_{0}\in D
such that

\Re(\beta_{0})=-\inf_{x\in\Omega}-b_{\varepsilon}(x)+2\|J_{\varepsilon}\|_{\infty}|\Omega|^{\frac{1}{2}},\quad\Im(\beta_{0})=0.

Having that we can bound the operator norm of
F(\beta_{0})
to obtain the following

\displaystyle\|F(\beta_{0})\|

\displaystyle=\sup_{\|u\|_{2}=1}\|F(\beta_{0})[u]\|_{2}\leq\sup_{\|u\|_{2}=1}\left(\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\|J_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}u^{2}(y)}{(\beta_{0}-b_{\varepsilon}(x))^{2}}\>dy\>dx\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\frac{1}{2}

Hence
(I-F(\beta_{0}))^{-1}
exists. But now, by the Analytical Fredholm Theorem
[
15
]
, we know that
(I-F(\beta))^{-1}
exists for all
\beta\in D\setminus\left\{\beta_{k}\right\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
. On the other hand, for any element
\beta_{k}\in\left\{\beta_{k}\right\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
there exists a corresponding function
\varphi_{k}\in L^{2}\left(\Omega\right)
such that
(\beta_{k},\varphi_{k})
solves the equation (
1.6
). Thus
\beta_{k}
is an eigenvalue.
∎

Combining the results obtained in the Lemma
2.1
and
2.2
we finish the proof of Theorem
2.1
.

2.3
Proof of Theorem
2.2

Firstly we observe that by Theorem
2.1
and Remark
2.1
the spectrum is given by formula (
2.4
) as claimed. To find the latter part of the spectrum we denote the following complex valued function defined for
x\in\mathbb{T}^{n},k\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}

\psi_{k}(x)=e^{ik\cdot x}.

We plug
\psi_{k}
into the equation (
1.6
) and use the periodicity of
J_{\varepsilon}
to obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}\psi_{k}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)e^{ik\cdot y}\>dy-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\>dy\>\psi_{k}(x)

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)e^{ik\cdot(y-x)}\>dy-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\>dy\right)\psi_{k}(x)

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}+x}J_{\varepsilon}(z)e^{ik\cdot z}\>dz-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}+x}J_{\varepsilon}(z)\>dz\right)\psi_{k}(x)

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}J_{\varepsilon}(z)e^{ik\cdot z}\>dz-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}J_{\varepsilon}(z)\>dz\right)\psi_{k}(x)

\displaystyle=\left(\hat{J}_{\varepsilon}(k)-\hat{J}_{\varepsilon}(0)\right)\psi_{k}(x).

Using the symmetry of
J
, we verify formula (
2.5
). Indeed we have that

\beta_{k}(\varepsilon):=\hat{J}_{\varepsilon}(k)-\hat{J}_{\varepsilon}(0)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}J_{\varepsilon}(z)(\cos(k\cdot z)-1)\>dz,

as asserted in the Theorem. We conclude that both real and imaginary part of
\psi_{k}
solves the equation (
1.6
) and so

\varphi_{k}^{1}(x):=\Re(\psi_{k})=\cos(k\cdot x),

\varphi_{k}^{2}(x):=\Im(\psi_{k})=\sin(k\cdot x),

which is exactly formula (
2.6
) as we claimed.

(i)
It follows immediately from formula (
2.5
).

(ii)
The Riemann-Lebesgue Lemma applied to formula (
2.5
) yields

\beta_{\infty}(\varepsilon):=\lim_{|k|\rightarrow\infty}\beta_{k}(\varepsilon)=-\hat{J}_{\varepsilon}(0)=b_{\varepsilon}(0).

Next we compute

\displaystyle\beta_{\infty}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}J_{\varepsilon}(x)\>dx

\displaystyle=-\frac{C_{norm}^{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon^{m+n}}\int_{\left(-\pi,\pi\right)^{n}}J_{per}\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)\>dx

\displaystyle=-\frac{C_{norm}^{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon^{m}}\int_{\left(-\frac{\pi}{\varepsilon},\frac{\pi}{\varepsilon}\right)^{n}}J_{per}\left(z\right)\>dz

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{m}}\frac{\int_{\left(-\frac{\pi}{\varepsilon},\frac{\pi}{\varepsilon}\right)^{n}}J_{per}\left(z\right)\>dz}{\int_{\left(-\frac{\pi}{\varepsilon},\frac{\pi}{\varepsilon}\right)^{n}}J_{per}\left(z\right)|z|^{2}\>dz}=:-\frac{\rho(\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon^{m}},

where we denoted

\rho(\varepsilon)=\frac{\int_{\left(-\frac{\pi}{\varepsilon},\frac{\pi}{\varepsilon}\right)^{n}}J_{per}\left(z\right)\>dz}{\int_{\left(-\frac{\pi}{\varepsilon},\frac{\pi}{\varepsilon}\right)^{n}}J_{per}\left(z\right)|z|^{2}\>dz}.

We bound this quantity from below in the following way

\displaystyle\rho(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\int_{\left(-\frac{\pi}{\varepsilon},\frac{\pi}{\varepsilon}\right)^{n}}J_{per}\left(z\right)\>dz}{\frac{\pi^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\left(-\frac{\pi}{\varepsilon},\frac{\pi}{\varepsilon}\right)^{n}}J_{per}(z)\>dz}=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\pi^{2}}.

Reversely we see that for
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small we have

\displaystyle\rho(\varepsilon)=\frac{\int_{|z|<1}J_{per}\left(z\right)\>dz+\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}\left(-\frac{\pi}{\varepsilon},\frac{\pi}{\varepsilon}\right)^{n}\\
|z|>1\end{subarray}}J_{per}\left(z\right)\>dz}{\int_{|z|<1}J_{per}\left(z\right)|z|^{2}\>dz+\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}\left(-\frac{\pi}{\varepsilon},\frac{\pi}{\varepsilon}\right)^{n}\\
|z|>1\end{subarray}}J_{per}\left(z\right)|z|^{2}\>dz}\leq 1.

Hence we obtained the bound for a limit eigenvalue

-\varepsilon^{-m}\leq\beta_{\infty}(\varepsilon)\leq-\frac{\varepsilon^{2-m}}{\pi^{2}}.

(iii)
We use the Taylor series expansion to re-write
\beta_{k}
with
k\neq 0
.

\displaystyle\beta_{k}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\int_{(-\pi,\pi)^{n}}J_{\varepsilon}(x)\cos(k\cdot x)\>dx-\int_{(-\pi,\pi)^{n}}J_{\varepsilon}(x)\>dx

\displaystyle=-\int_{(-\pi,\pi)^{n}}J_{\varepsilon}(x)\frac{(k\cdot x)^{2}}{2}\>dx+\sum_{l=2}^{\infty}(-1)^{l}\int_{(-\pi,\pi)^{n}}J_{\varepsilon}(x)\frac{(k\cdot x)^{2l}}{(2l)!}\>dx

Substitution
z=x/\varepsilon
gives a simplified formula in the asymptotic form.

\displaystyle\beta_{k}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\varepsilon^{2-m}C_{norm}^{\varepsilon}}{2}\int_{\left(-\frac{\pi}{\varepsilon},\frac{\pi}{\varepsilon}\right)^{n}}J_{per}(z)(k\cdot z)^{2}\>dz+o(\varepsilon^{4-m})

\displaystyle=-\frac{\varepsilon^{2-m}C_{norm}^{\varepsilon}}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\left(-\frac{\pi}{\varepsilon},\frac{\pi}{\varepsilon}\right)^{n}}J_{per}(z)k_{i}^{2}z_{i}^{2}\>dz

\displaystyle-\frac{\varepsilon^{2-m}C_{norm}^{\varepsilon}}{2}\sum_{i\neq j}^{n}\int_{\left(-\frac{\pi}{\varepsilon},\frac{\pi}{\varepsilon}\right)^{n}}J_{per}(z)k_{i}k_{j}z_{i}z_{j}\>dz+o(\varepsilon^{4-m})

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon^{2-m}\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}^{2}\frac{\int_{\left(-\frac{\pi}{\varepsilon},\frac{\pi}{\varepsilon}\right)^{n}}J_{per}z_{i}^{2}\>dz}{\int_{\left(-\frac{\pi}{\varepsilon},\frac{\pi}{\varepsilon}\right)^{n}}J_{per}|z|^{2}\>dz}+o(\varepsilon^{4-m})

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon^{2-m}\frac{|k|^{2}}{n}+o(\varepsilon^{4-m})

This ends the proof of Theorem

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left(u_{t}-u_{\Omega}^{\prime}\right)v\>dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}u+f(u)-\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}f(u)\>dy\right)v\>dx

\displaystyle=\left\langle\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}u,v\right\rangle+\int_{\Omega}\left(f(u)-f(u_{\Omega})-\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}\left(f(u)-f(u_{\Omega})\right)\>dy\>\right)v\>dx

\displaystyle=\langle\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}v,v\rangle+\max_{u\in\Gamma}|f^{\prime}(u)|E_{v}(t)

\displaystyle\frac{E_{v}^{\prime}(t)}{E_{v}(t)}

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(\sup_{v\in L^{2}(\Omega),\int_{\Omega}v=0}\frac{\langle\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}v,v\rangle}{\|v\|_{2}^{2}}+\max_{u\in\Gamma}|f^{\prime}(u)|\right)

\displaystyle\left|\langle\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}u,u\rangle\right|

f\in C^{2}_{b}(\mathbb{R})
is such that there exists
u_{1},u_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
satisfying
f(u_{1})=f(u_{2})=0
and
f^{\prime}(u_{1})\neq 0
,
f^{\prime}(u_{2})\neq 0
. Then there exists unique perturbation
\varphi
such that
U=\bar{U}+\varphi
is a solution to steady problem (
1.10
) with
\hat{U}
defined as in Theorem
4.2
.

Proof.

Plug
U=\tilde{U}+\varphi
into equation (
1.10
) and re-write the obtained formula to get fixed point problem

\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}\varphi=\frac{1}{f^{\prime}(\tilde{U})}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}[\tilde{U}+\varphi]+f(\tilde{U}+\varphi)-f(\tilde{U})-f^{\prime}(\tilde{U})\varphi\right)=\varphi.

We show that the mapping
\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}
is a Banach contraction for some large value of
\varepsilon>0
. For the Banach space we choose the closed ball
\overline{B(0,R)}\subset L^{\infty}(\Omega)
with a small radius
R>0
.
Let
\varphi\in\overline{B(0,R)}
, then the norm is bounded as follows

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}\varphi\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\frac{1}{f^{\prime}(\tilde{U})}\right\|_{\infty}\left(C(\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon})(\|\tilde{U}\|_{\infty}+R)+\|f^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}R^{2}\right).

Here, the quadratic term is estimated using the Mean Value Theorem and the constant
C(\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon})
is given by the formula

C(\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}):=2\sup_{x\in\Omega}\int_{\Omega}J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\>dy=O(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{m}}).

Thus in order to have
\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}:\overline{B(0,R)}\rightarrow\overline{B(0,R)}
the following must be fulfilled

\left\|\frac{1}{f^{\prime}(\tilde{U})}\right\|_{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{m}}(\|\tilde{U}\|_{\infty}+R)+\|f^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}R^{2}\right)\leq R.

(4.8)

Now we derive the contractivity criterion. Take
\varphi,\psi\in\overline{B(0,R)}
and estimate

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}\varphi-\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}\psi\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\frac{1}{f^{\prime}(\tilde{U})}\right\|_{\infty}\left(C(\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon})+\|f^{\prime}(\zeta)-f^{\prime}(\tilde{U})\|_{\infty}\right)\|\varphi-\psi\|_{\infty},

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\frac{1}{f^{\prime}(\tilde{U})}\right\|_{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{m}}+2\|f^{\prime}\|_{\infty}R\right)\|\varphi-\psi\|_{\infty}.

The criterion of the following form has to hold true to get contractivity

\left\|\frac{1}{f^{\prime}(\tilde{U})}\right\|_{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{m}}+2\|f^{\prime}\|_{\infty}R\right)<1.

(4.9)

Observe that conditions (
4.8
) and (
4.9
) are satisfied for a sufficiently large values of dispersion parameter
\varepsilon>0
and small radius
R>0
. Now the proof proceeds by the standard Banach fixed point argument.
∎

Next, we emphasize that the obtained discontinuous solution is stable.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\Omega,J_{\varepsilon}
satisfy either Assumption
1.4
or
1.2
. Assume that
f\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
is such that there exists
u_{1},u_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
satisfying
f(u_{1})=f(u_{2})=0
and
f^{\prime}(u_{1})<0
,
f^{\prime}(u_{2})<0
. Then solution
U
given by Lemma
4.4
is linearly, exponentially stable in
L^{2}(\Omega)
.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.4
the function
U=\tilde{U}+\varphi
is a steady state, where the jump function
\hat{U}
and small perturbation
\psi
are defined in the lemma. We linearise problem (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) around
U
by introducing the new function
\psi=u-U
. This functions satisfies the following problem

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\psi_{t}(x,t)&=\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}\psi(x,t)+f^{\prime}(U(x))\psi(x,t),\\
\psi(x,0)&=\psi_{0}(x),\end{cases}

In order to proceed we define the energy of a linearized system as follows

E_{\phi}(t):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\phi(x,t)|^{2}\>dx.

We verify that the eigenvalue of linearization operator is strictly negative

\displaystyle\begin{split}\gamma_{0}&=\sup_{\varphi\in L^{2}\left(\Omega\right),\varphi\neq 0}\frac{\langle\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}\varphi+f^{\prime}(U)\varphi,\varphi\rangle}{\|\varphi\|_{2}^{2}}\\
&\leq\sup_{\varphi\in L^{2}\left(\Omega\right),\|\varphi\|_{2}=1}\langle f^{\prime}(U)\varphi,\varphi\rangle\\
&=\sup_{\varphi\in L^{2}\left(\Omega\right),\|\varphi\|_{2}=1}\int_{\Omega}f^{\prime}(U)\varphi^{2}(x)\>dx<0,\end{split}

(4.10)

where last strict inequality holds true by continuity of
f^{\prime}
and perturbation
\varphi
being defined in sufficiently small ball. Next we differentiate the energy
E_{\varphi}
.

\displaystyle E_{\varphi}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\varphi_{t}(x,t)\varphi(x,t)\>dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}\varphi(x,t)+f^{\prime}(U)\varphi(x,t)\right)\varphi(x,t)\>dx

\displaystyle=\frac{\langle\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}\varphi+f^{\prime}(U)\varphi,\varphi\rangle}{\|\varphi\|_{2}^{2}}\|\varphi\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma_{0}E_{\varphi}(t).

When solved, this inequality yields the following limit

\|\varphi(\cdot,t)-0\|_{2}^{2}=E_{\varphi}(t)\leq\|\psi_{0}\|_{2}^{2}e^{\gamma_{0}t}.

(4.11)

Inequality (
4.10
) and (
4.11
) yields the stability of zero in a linearized problem.
∎

Combining lemmas
4.4
and
4.5
completes the proof of Theorem
4.2
.
