Double shrinkage priors for a normal mean matrix
Takeru Matsuda, Fumiyasu Komaki, William E. Strawderman

Abstract
We consider estimation of a normal mean matrix under the Frobenius loss. Motivated by the Efron–Morris estimator, a generalization of Stein’s prior has been recently developed, which is superharmonic and shrinks the singular values towards zero. The generalized Bayes estimator with respect to this prior is minimax and dominates the maximum likelihood estimator. However, here we show that it is inadmissible by using Brown’s condition. Then, we develop two types of priors that provide improved generalized Bayes estimators and examine their performance numerically. The proposed priors attain risk reduction by adding scalar shrinkage or column-wise shrinkage to singular value shrinkage. Parallel results for Bayesian predictive densities are also given.

Introduction

Suppose that we have independent matrix observations
Y^{(1)},\dots,Y^{(N)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
whose entries are independent normal random variables
Y^{(t)}_{ij}\sim{\rm N}(M_{ij},1)
, where
M\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
is an unknown mean matrix.
In the notation of
Gupta and Nagar 2000
, this is expressed as
Y^{(t)}\sim{\rm N}_{n,p}(M,I_{n},I_{p})
for
t=1,\dots,N
, where
I_{k}
denotes the
k
-dimensional identity matrix.
We consider estimation of
M
under the Frobenius loss

\displaystyle l(M,\hat{M})=\|\hat{M}-M\|_{{\rm F}}^{2}=\sum_{a=1}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{p}(\hat{M}_{ai}-M_{ai})^{2}.

By sufficiency reduction, it suffices to consider the average
{Y}=(Y^{(1)}+\dots+Y^{(N)})/N\sim{\rm N}(M,I_{n},N^{-1}I_{p})
in estimation of
M
.
We assume
n-p-1>0
in the following.
Note that vectorization reduces this problem to estimation of a normal mean vector
{\rm vec}(M)
from
{\rm vec}({Y})\sim{\rm N}_{np}({\rm vec}(M),N^{-1}I_{np})
under the quadratic loss, which has been well studied in shrinkage estimation theory
(
Fourdrinier et al. 2018
)
.

Efron and Morris 1972
proposed an empirical Bayes estimator:

\displaystyle\hat{M}_{{\rm EM}}=Y\left(I_{p}-\frac{n-p-1}{N}(Y^{\top}Y)^{-1}\right).

(1)

This estimator can be viewed as a generalization of the James–Stein estimator (
p=1
) for a normal mean vector.

Efron and Morris 1972
showed that
\hat{M}_{{\rm EM}}
is minimax and dominates the maximum likelihood estimator
\hat{M}=Y
under the Frobenius loss.
Let
Y=U\Lambda V^{\top}
,
U\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
,
V\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
,
\Lambda={\rm diag}(\sigma_{1}(Y),\ldots,\sigma_{p}(Y))
be the singular value decomposition of
Y
, where
U^{\top}U=V^{\top}V=I_{p}
and

\sigma_{1}(Y)\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_{p}(Y)\geq 0
are the singular values of
Y
.

Stein 1974
pointed out that
\hat{M}_{{\rm EM}}
does not change the singular vectors but shrinks the singular values of
Y
towards zero:

\displaystyle\hat{M}_{{\rm EM}}=U\hat{\Lambda}_{{\rm EM}}V^{\top},\quad\hat{\Lambda}_{{\rm EM}}={\rm diag}(\sigma_{1}(\hat{M}_{{\rm EM}}),\ldots,\sigma_{p}(\hat{M}_{{\rm EM}})),

where

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}(\hat{M}_{{\rm EM}})=\left(1-\frac{n-p-1}{N\sigma_{i}(Y)^{2}}\right)\sigma_{i}(Y),\quad i=1,\ldots,p.

See
Tsukuma and Kubokawa 2020
;
Yuasa and Kubokawa 2023a
;
Yuasa and Kubokawa 2023b
for recent developments around the Efron–Morris estimator.

As a Bayesian counterpart of
\hat{M}_{{\rm EM}}
,
Matsuda and Komaki 2015
proposed a singular value shrinkage prior

\displaystyle\pi_{{\rm SVS}}(M)=\det(M^{\top}M)^{-(n-p-1)/2},

(2)

and showed that the generalized Bayes estimator
\hat{M}_{{\rm SVS}}
with respect to
\pi_{{\rm SVS}}
dominates the maximum likelihood estimator
\hat{M}=Y
under the Frobenius loss.
This prior can be viewed as a generalization of Stein’s prior
\pi(\mu)=\|\mu\|^{2-n}
for a normal mean vector
\mu
(
p=1
) by
Stein 1974
.
Similarly to
\hat{M}_{{\rm EM}}
in (
1
) ,
\hat{M}_{{\rm SVS}}
shrinks the singular values towards zero.
Thus, it works well when the true matrix is close to low-rank.
See
Matsuda and Strawderman 2022
and
Matsuda 2023
for details on the risk behavior of
\hat{M}_{{\rm EM}}
and
\hat{M}_{{\rm SVS}}
.

In this paper, we show that the generalized Bayes estimator with respect to the singular value shrinkage prior
\pi_{{\rm SVS}}
in (
2
) is inadmissible under the Frobenius loss.
Then, we develop two types of priors that provide improved generalized Bayes estimators asymptotically.
The first type adds scalar shrinkage while the second type adds column-wise shrinkage.
We conduct numerical experiments and confirm the effectiveness of the proposed priors in finite samples.
We also provide parallel results for Bayesian prediction as well as a similar improvement of the blockwise Stein prior, which was conjectured by
Brown and Zhao 2009
.

This paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
, we prove the inadmissibility of the generalized Bayes estimator with respect to the singular value shrinkage prior
\pi_{{\rm SVS}}
in (
2
).
In Sections
3
and
4
, we provide two types of priors that asymptotically dominate the singular value shrinkage prior
\pi_{{\rm SVS}}
in (
2
) by adding scalar or column-wise shrinkage, respectively.
Numerical results are also given.
In Section
5
, we provide parallel results for Bayesian prediction.
Technical details and similar results for the blockwise Stein prior are given in the Appendix.

2
Inadmissibility of the singular value shrinkage prior

Here, we show that the generalized Bayes estimator with respect to the singular value shrinkage prior
\pi_{{\rm SVS}}
in (
2
) is inadmissible under the Frobenius loss.
Since
N
does not affect admissibility results, we fix
N=1
for convenience in this section.

For estimation of a normal mean vector under the quadratic loss,
Brown 1971
derived the following sufficient condition for inadmissibility of generalized Bayes estimators.

Lemma 2.1
.

(
Brown 1971
)

In estimation of
\theta
from
Y\sim\mathrm{N}_{d}(\theta,I_{d})
under the quadratic loss, the generalized Bayes estimator of
\theta
with respect to a prior
\pi(\theta)
is inadmissible if

\int_{c}^{\infty}{r^{1-d}\underline{m}(r)}{\rm d}r<\infty

for some
c>0
, where

\underline{m}(r)=\int\frac{1}{m_{\pi}(y)}{\rm d}U_{r}(y),

m_{\pi}(y)=\int p(y\mid\theta)\pi(\theta){\rm d}\theta,

p(y\mid\theta)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{\|y-\theta\|^{2}}{2}\right),

and
U_{r}
is the uniform measure on the sphere of radius
r
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

After vectorization, estimation of a normal mean matrix
M
from
Y\sim{\rm N}_{n,p}(M,I_{n},I_{p})
under the Frobenius loss reduces to estimation of a normal mean vector
{\rm vec}(M)
from
{\rm vec}(Y)\sim{\rm N}_{np}({\rm vec}(M),I_{np})
under the quadratic loss.
Then, by using Brown’s condition in Lemma
2.1
, we obtain the following.

Theorem 2.1
.

When
p\geq 2
, the generalized Bayes estimator with respect to
\pi_{{\rm SVS}}
in (
2
) is inadmissible under the Frobenius loss.

Proof.

From
n-p-1>0
and the AM-GM inequality

\displaystyle\left(\prod_{i=1}^{p}\sigma_{i}(M)^{2}\right)^{1/p}\leq\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\sigma_{i}(M)^{2},

we have

\displaystyle\pi_{{\rm SVS}}(M)=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{p}\sigma_{i}(M)^{2}\right)^{-(n-p-1)/2}

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\sigma_{i}(M)^{2}\right)^{-p(n-p-1)/2}

\displaystyle=A_{n,p}\|M\|_{{\rm F}}^{-p(n-p-1)},

where
A_{n,p}=p^{p(n-p-1)/2}
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle m_{{\rm SVS}}(Y)

\displaystyle=\int\pi_{{\rm SVS}}(M)p(Y\mid M){\rm d}M

\displaystyle\geq A_{n,p}\int\|M\|_{{\rm F}}^{-p(n-p-1)}p(Y\mid M){\rm d}M

\displaystyle=A_{n,p}{\rm E}[\|Y+Z\|_{{\rm F}}^{-p(n-p-1)}]

\displaystyle\geq A_{n,p}{\rm E}[(\|Y\|_{{\rm F}}+\|Z\|_{{\rm F}})^{-p(n-p-1)}],

(3)

where
Z=M-Y\sim{\rm N}_{n,p}(O,I_{n},I_{p})
and we used the triangle inequality.
As
\|Y\|_{{\rm F}}\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\|Y\|_{{\rm F}}^{p(n-p-1)}{\rm E}[(\|Y\|_{{\rm F}}+\|Z\|_{{\rm F}})^{-p(n-p-1)}]={\rm E}\left[\left(1+\frac{\|Z\|_{{\rm F}}}{\|Y\|_{{\rm F}}}\right)^{-p(n-p-1)}\right]\to 1,

which yields

\displaystyle{\rm E}[(\|Y\|_{{\rm F}}+\|Z\|_{{\rm F}})^{-p(n-p-1)}]=O(\|Y\|_{{\rm F}}^{-p(n-p-1)}).

(4)

Now, we apply Lemma
2.1
by noting that estimation of a normal mean matrix
M
from
Y\sim{\rm N}_{n,p}(M,I_{n},I_{p})
under the Frobenius loss is equivalent to estimation of a normal mean vector
{\rm vec}(M)
from
{\rm vec}(Y)\sim{\rm N}_{np}({\rm vec}(M),I_{np})
under the quadratic loss.
Let
U_{r}
be the uniform measure on the sphere of radius
r
in
\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
, where the Frobenius norm is adopted for radius.
Then, from (
3
) and (
4
),

\displaystyle\underline{m}_{{\rm SVS}}(r)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{1}{m_{{\rm SVS}}(Y)}{\rm d}U_{r}(Y)\leq Cr^{p(n-p-1)}

for some constant
C
.
Therefore, since
-p^{2}-p+1<-1
when
p\geq 2
,

\int_{1}^{\infty}r^{1-np}\underline{m}_{{\rm SVS}}(r){\rm d}r\leq C\int_{1}^{\infty}r^{-p^{2}-p+1}{\rm d}r<\infty.

From Lemma
2.1
, it implies the inadmissibility of the generelized Bayes estimator with respect to
\pi_{{\rm SVS}}
under the Frobenius loss.
∎

3
Improvement by additional scalar shrinkage

Here, motivated by the result of
Efron and Morris 1976
, we develop a class of priors for which the generalized Bayes estimators asymptotically dominate that with respect to the singular value shrinkage prior
\pi_{{\rm SVS}}
in (
2
).

Efron and Morris 1976
proved that the estimator

\displaystyle\hat{M}_{{\rm MEM}}=Y\left(I_{p}-\frac{n-p-1}{N}(Y^{\top}Y)^{-1}-\frac{p^{2}+p-2}{N\|Y\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}I_{p}\right)

(5)

dominates
\hat{M}_{{\rm EM}}
in (
1
) under the Frobenius loss.
This estimator shrinks the singular values of
Y
more strongly than
\hat{M}_{{\rm EM}}
:

\displaystyle\hat{M}_{{\rm MEM}}=U\hat{\Lambda}_{{\rm MEM}}V^{\top},\quad\hat{\Lambda}_{{\rm MEM}}={\rm diag}(\sigma_{1}(\hat{M}_{{\rm MEM}}),\ldots,\sigma_{p}(\hat{M}_{{\rm MEM}})),

where

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}(\hat{M}_{{\rm MEM}})=\left(1-\frac{n-p-1}{N\sigma_{i}(Y)^{2}}-\frac{p^{2}+p-2}{N\|Y\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}\right)\sigma_{i}(Y),\quad i=1,\ldots,p.

(6)

In other words,
\hat{M}_{{\rm MEM}}
adds scalar shrinkage to
\hat{M}_{{\rm EM}}
.

Konno 1990
;
Konno 1991
showed corresponding results in the unknown covariance setting.
By extending these results,
Tsukuma and Kubokawa 2007
derived a general method for improving matrix mean estimators by adding scalar shrinkage.

Motivated by
\hat{M}_{{\rm MEM}}
in (
5
), we construct priors by adding scalar shrinkage to
\pi_{{\rm SVS}}
in (
2
):

\displaystyle\pi_{{\rm MSVS1}}(M)=\pi_{{\rm SVS}}(M)\|M\|_{{\rm F}}^{-\gamma},

(7)

where
\gamma\geq 0
.
Note that
Tsukuma and Kubokawa 2017
studied this type of prior in the context of Bayesian prediction.
Let

\displaystyle m_{{\rm MSVS1}}(Y)=\int p(Y\mid M)\pi_{{\rm MSVS1}}(M){\rm d}M

be the marginal density of
Y
under the prior
\pi_{{\rm MSVS1}}(M)
.

Lemma 3.1
.

If
0\leq\gamma<p^{2}+p
, then
m_{{\rm MSVS1}}(Y)<\infty
for every
Y
.

Proof.

Since
m_{{\rm MSVS1}}(Y)
is interpreted as the expectation of
\pi_{{\rm MSVS1}}(M)
under
M\sim{\rm N}_{n,p}(Y,I_{n},I_{p})
, it suffices to show that
\pi_{{\rm MSVS1}}(M)
is locally integrable at every
M
.

First, consider
M\neq O
.
Since

\displaystyle m_{{\rm SVS}}(Y)

\displaystyle=\int\pi_{{\rm SVS}}(M)p(Y\mid M){\rm d}M<\infty

for every
Y
from Lemma 1 of
Matsuda and Komaki 2015
,
\pi_{{\rm SVS}}(M)
is locally integrable at
M
.
Also,
\|M\|_{{\rm F}}>c
for some
c>0
in a neighborhood of
M
.
Thus,
\pi_{{\rm MSVS1}}(M)=\pi_{{\rm SVS}}(M)\|M\|_{{\rm F}}^{-\gamma}
is locally integrable at
M
if
\gamma\geq 0
.

Next, consider
M=O
and take its neighborhood
A=\{M\mid\|M\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq\varepsilon\}
for
\varepsilon>0
.
To evaluate the integral on
A
, we use the variable transformation from
M
to
(r,U)
, where
r=\|M\|_{\mathrm{F}}
and
U=M/r
so that
M=rU
.
We have
{\rm d}M=r^{np-1}{\rm d}r{\rm d}U
.
Also, from
\det(M^{\top}M)=r^{2p}\det(U^{\top}U)
,

\displaystyle\pi_{{\rm MSVS1}}(M)=r^{-p(n-p-1)-\gamma}\det(U^{\top}U)^{-(n-p-1)/2}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\int_{A}\pi_{{\rm MSVS1}}(M){\rm d}M

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}r^{-p(n-p-1)-\gamma+np-1}{\rm d}r\int\det(U^{\top}U)^{-(n-p-1)/2}{\rm d}U

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}r^{p^{2}+p-\gamma-1}{\rm d}r\int\det(U^{\top}U)^{-(n-p-1)/2}{\rm d}U.

The integral with respect to
r
is finite if
p^{2}+p-\gamma-1>-1
, which is equivalent to
\gamma<p^{2}+p
.
The integral with respect to
U
is finite due to the local integrability of
\pi_{\mathrm{SVS}}
, which corresponds to
\gamma=0
, at
M=O
.
Therefore,
\pi_{{\rm MSVS1}}(M)
is locally integrable at
M=O
if
0\leq\gamma<p^{2}+p
.

Hence,
\pi_{{\rm MSVS1}}(M)
is locally integrable at every
M
if
0\leq\gamma<p^{2}+p
.
∎

From Lemma
3.1
, the generalized Bayes estimator with respect to
\pi_{{\rm MSVS1}}
is well-defined when
0\leq\gamma<p^{2}+p
.
We denote it by
\hat{M}_{{\rm MSVS1}}
.

Theorem 3.1
.

For every
M
,

\displaystyle N^{2}({\rm E}_{M}[\|\hat{M}_{{{\rm MSVS1}}}-M\|_{{\rm F}}^{2}]-{\rm E}_{M}[\|\hat{M}_{{{\rm SVS}}}-M\|_{{\rm F}}^{2}])\to\frac{\gamma(\gamma-2p^{2}-2p+4)}{{\rm tr}(M^{\top}M)}

(8)

as
N\to\infty
.
Therefore, if
p\geq 2
and
0<\gamma<p^{2}+p
, then the generalized Bayes estimator with respect to
\pi_{{\rm MSVS1}}
in (
7
) asymptotically dominates that with respect to
\pi_{{\rm SVS}}
in (
2
) under the Frobenius loss.

Proof.

Let
K=M^{\top}M
and
K^{ij}
be the
(i,j)
th entry of
K^{-1}
.
From

\displaystyle\frac{\partial K_{jk}}{\partial M_{ai}}=\delta_{ik}M_{aj}+\delta_{ij}M_{ak},\quad\frac{\partial}{\partial K_{ij}}\det K=K^{ij}\det K,

(9)

we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial M_{ai}}\det K

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k}\frac{\partial K_{jk}}{\partial M_{ai}}\frac{\partial}{\partial K_{jk}}\det K=2\sum_{j}M_{aj}K^{ij}\det K.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial M_{ai}}\log\pi_{{\rm SVS}}(M)=-(n-p-1)\sum_{j}M_{aj}K^{ij}.

(10)

Let
\pi_{{\rm S}}(M)=\|M\|_{{\rm F}}^{-\gamma}=({\rm tr}K)^{-\gamma/2}
.
Since

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial M_{ai}}{\rm tr}K=2M_{ai}

from (
9
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial M_{ai}}\log\pi_{{\rm S}}(M)

\displaystyle=-\gamma M_{ai}({\rm tr}K)^{-1},

(11)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial M_{ai}^{2}}\log\pi_{{\rm S}}(M)

\displaystyle=-\gamma({\rm tr}K-2M_{ai}^{2})({\rm tr}K)^{-2}.

(12)

By using (
10
), (
11
), and (
12
), we obtain

\displaystyle{\rm tr}(\widetilde{\nabla}\log\pi_{{\rm SVS}}(M)^{\top}\widetilde{\nabla}\log\pi_{{\rm S}}(M))

\displaystyle=\gamma p(n-p-1)({\rm tr}K)^{-1},

\displaystyle{\rm tr}(\widetilde{\nabla}\log\pi_{{\rm S}}(M)^{\top}\widetilde{\nabla}\log\pi_{{\rm S}}(M))

\displaystyle=\gamma^{2}({\rm tr}K)^{-1},

\displaystyle{\rm tr}(\widetilde{\Delta}\log\pi_{{\rm S}}(M))

\displaystyle=-\gamma(np-2)({\rm tr}K)^{-1},

where we used the matrix derivative notations (
28
) and (
29
).
Therefore, from Lemma
A.2
,

\displaystyle{\rm E}_{M}[\|\hat{M}_{{\rm MSVS1}}-M\|_{{\rm F}}^{2}]-{\rm E}_{M}[\|\hat{M}_{{\rm SVS}}-M\|_{{\rm F}}^{2}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N^{2}}{\rm tr}(2\widetilde{\nabla}\log\pi_{{\rm SVS}}(M)^{\top}\widetilde{\nabla}\log\pi_{{\rm S}}(M)+\widetilde{\nabla}\log\pi_{{\rm S}}(M)^{\top}\widetilde{\nabla}\log\pi_{{\rm S}}(M)+2\widetilde{\Delta}\log\pi_{{\rm S}}(M))

\displaystyle\quad+o(N^{-2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N^{2}}\gamma(\gamma-2p^{2}-2p+4)({\rm tr}K)^{-1}+o(N^{-2}).

Hence, we obtain (
8
).
∎

From (
8
), the choice
\gamma=p^{2}+p-2
attains the minimum risk among
0<\gamma<p^{2}+p
.
Note that
p^{2}+p-2
also appears in the singular value decomposition form of the modified Efron–Morris estimator
\hat{M}_{{\rm MEM}}
in (
6
).

Now, we examine the performance of
\pi_{{\rm MSVS1}}
in (

) by Monte Carlo simulation.

Improvement by additional scalar shrinkage holds even under the matrix quadratic loss
(
Matsuda and Strawderman 2022
;
Matsuda 2024
)
.

Theorem 3.2
.

For every
M
,

\displaystyle N^{2}({\rm E}_{M}[(\hat{M}_{{{\rm MSVS1}}}-M)^{\top}

\displaystyle(\hat{M}_{{{\rm MSVS1}}}-M)]-{\rm E}_{M}[(\hat{M}_{{{\rm SVS}}}-M)^{\top}(\hat{M}_{{{\rm SVS}}}-M)])

\displaystyle\to\gamma({\rm tr}K)^{-2}(-2(p+1)({\rm tr}K)I_{p}+(\gamma+4)K)

(13)

as
N\to\infty
.
Therefore, if
p\geq 2
and
0<\gamma<2p-2
, then the generalized Bayes estimator with respect to
\pi_{{\rm MSVS1}}
in (
7
) asymptotically dominates that with respect to
\pi_{{\rm SVS}}
in (
2
) under the matrix quadratic loss.

Proof.

We use the same notation with the proof of Theorem
3.1
.
By using (
10
), (
11
), and (
12
), we obtain

\displaystyle(\widetilde{\nabla}\log\pi_{{\rm SVS}}(M)^{\top}\widetilde{\nabla}\log\pi_{{\rm S}}(M))

\displaystyle=\gamma(n-p-1)({\rm tr}K)^{-1}I_{p},

\displaystyle(\widetilde{\nabla}\log\pi_{{\rm S}}(M)^{\top}\widetilde{\nabla}\log\pi_{{\rm S}}(M))

\displaystyle=\gamma^{2}({\rm tr}K)^{-2}K,

\displaystyle(\widetilde{\Delta}\log\pi_{{\rm S}}(M))

\displaystyle=-n\gamma({\rm tr}K)^{-1}I_{p}+2\gamma({\rm tr}K)^{-2}K.

Therefore, from Lemma
A.3
,

\displaystyle{\rm E}_{M}[(\hat{M}_{{{\rm MSVS1}}}-M)^{\top}(\hat{M}_{{{\rm MSVS1}}}-M)]-{\rm E}_{M}[(\hat{M}_{{{\rm SVS}}}-M)^{\top}(\hat{M}_{{{\rm SVS}}}-M)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N^{2}}(2\widetilde{\nabla}\log\pi_{{\rm SVS}}(M)^{\top}\widetilde{\nabla}\log\pi_{{\rm S}}(M)+\widetilde{\nabla}\log\pi_{{\rm S}}(M)^{\top}\widetilde{\nabla}\log\pi_{{\rm S}}(M)+2\widetilde{\Delta}\log\pi_{{\rm S}}(M))

\displaystyle\quad+o(N^{-2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N^{2}}\gamma({\rm tr}K)^{-2}(-2(p+1)({\rm tr}K)I_{p}+(\gamma+4)K)+o(N^{-2}).

Hence, we obtain (
13
).
Since
K\preceq({\rm tr}K)I_{p}
from
K\succeq O
,

\displaystyle-2(p+1)({\rm tr}K)I_{p}+(\gamma+4)K\preceq(\gamma-2p+2)({\rm tr}K)I_{p}\prec O

if
0<\gamma<2p-2
.
∎

The generalized Bayes estimator with respect to
\pi_{{\rm MSVS1}}
in (
7
) attains minimaxity in some cases as follows.

Theorem 3.3
.

If
p\geq 2
,
p+2\leq n<2p+2-2/p
and

, then the generalized Bayes estimator with respect to

in (

Proof.

From Proposition

,

\displaystyle\Delta\pi_{{\rm MSVS1}}(M)

\displaystyle=\gamma(\gamma+np-2p^{2}-2p+2)\|M\|_{{\rm F}}^{-2}\pi_{{\rm MSVS1}}(M)\leq 0.

Thus,
\pi_{{\rm MSVS1}}(M)
is superharmonic, which indicates the minimaxity of the generalized Bayes estimator with respect to

in (

) under the Frobenius loss from Stein’s classical result
(

;

)
.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

(
Brown 1971
)

In estimation of

from

under the quadratic loss, the generalized Bayes estimator of

with respect to a prior

is admissible if

is uniformly bounded and

\displaystyle\int_{c}^{\infty}\frac{r^{1-d}}{\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{m}(r)}{\rm d}r=\infty,

(14)

where

\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{m}(r)=\int{m_{\pi}(y)}{\rm d}U_{r}(y)

and

is the uniform measure on the sphere of radius

in

4
Improvement by additional column-wise shrinkage

Here, instead of scalar shrinkage, we consider priors with additional column-wise shrinkage:

\displaystyle\pi_{{\rm MSVS2}}(M)=\pi_{{\rm SVS}}(M)\prod_{i=1}^{p}\|M_{\cdot i}\|^{-\gamma_{i}},

(15)

where
\gamma_{i}\geq 0
for every
i
and
\|M_{\cdot i}\|
denotes the norm of the
i
-th column vector of
M
.
Let

\displaystyle m_{{\rm MSVS2}}(Y)=\int p(Y\mid M)\pi_{{\rm MSVS2}}(M){\rm d}M

be the marginal density of
Y
under the prior
\pi_{{\rm MSVS2}}(M)
.

Lemma 4.1
.

If
0\leq\gamma_{i}\leq p
for every
i
, then
m_{{\rm MSVS2}}(Y)<\infty
for every
Y
.

Proof.

Similarly to Lemma
3.1
, it suffices to show that
\pi_{{\rm MSVS2}}(M)
is locally integrable at
M=O
.
Consider the neighborhood of
M=O
defined by
A=\{M\mid\|M\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq\varepsilon\}
for
\varepsilon>0
.
To evaluate the integral on
A
, we use the variable transformation from
M
to
(r_{1},\dots,r_{p},u_{1},\dots,u_{p})
, where each
r_{i}\in[0,\infty)
and
u_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\|u_{i}\|=1
are defined by
r_{i}=\|M_{\cdot i}\|
and
u_{i}=M_{\cdot i}/r_{i}
for the
i
-th column vector
M_{\cdot i}
of
M
so that
M_{\cdot i}=r_{i}u_{i}
(polar coordinate).
Since
{\rm d}M_{\cdot i}=r_{i}^{n-1}{\rm d}r_{i}{\rm d}u_{i}
,

\displaystyle{\rm d}M=r_{1}^{n-1}\dots r_{p}^{n-1}{\rm d}r_{1}\dots{\rm d}r_{p}{\rm d}u_{1}\dots{\rm d}u_{p}.

Also, from
M^{\top}M=D(U^{\top}U)D
with
D={\rm diag}(r_{1},\dots,r_{p})
and
U=(u_{1}\dots u_{p})
,

\displaystyle\pi_{{\rm MSVS2}}(M)

\displaystyle=\det(D)^{-(n-p-1)}\det(U^{\top}U)^{-(n-p-1)/2}\prod_{i=1}^{p}r_{i}^{-\gamma_{i}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{p}r_{i}^{-(n-p-1)-\gamma_{i}}\cdot\det(U^{\top}U)^{-(n-p-1)/2}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\int_{A}\pi_{{\rm MSVS2}}(M){\rm d}M

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\|r\|\leq\varepsilon}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{p}r_{i}^{p-\gamma_{i}}\right){\rm d}r_{1}\dots{\rm d}r_{p}\int\det(U^{\top}U)^{-(n-p-1)/2}{\rm d}u_{1}\dots{\rm d}u_{p}.

(16)

By variable transformation from
r=(r_{1},\dots,r_{p})
to
s=\|r\|
and
v=r/s
, the first integral in (
16
) is reduced to

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}s^{p^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{p}\gamma_{i}+p-1}{\rm d}s\int_{\|v\|=1}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{p}v_{i}^{p-\gamma_{i}}\right){\rm d}v.

The integral with respect to
s
is finite if
p^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{p}\gamma_{i}+p-1>-1
, which is equivalent to
\sum_{i=1}^{p}\gamma_{i}<p^{2}+p
.
The integral with respect to
v
is finite if
p-\gamma_{i}\geq 0
for every
i
.
On the other hand, the second integral in (
16
) is finite due to the local integrability of
\pi_{\mathrm{SVS}}
, which corresponds to
\gamma=0
, at
M=O
.
Therefore,
\pi_{{\rm MSVS2}}(M)
is locally integrable at
M=O
if
0\leq\gamma_{i}\leq p
for every
i
.
∎

From Lemma
4.1
, the generalized Bayes estimator with respect to
\pi_{{\rm MSVS2}}
is well-defined when
0\leq\gamma\leq p
.
We denote it by
\hat{M}_{{\rm MSVS2}}
.

Theorem 4.1
.

For every
M
,

\displaystyle N^{2}({\rm E}_{M}[\|\hat{M}_{{{\rm MSVS2}}}-M\|_{{\rm F}}^{2}]-{\rm E}_{M}[\|\hat{M}_{{{\rm SVS}}}-M\|_{{\rm F}}^{2}])\to\sum_{i=1}^{p}\gamma_{i}(\gamma_{i}-2p+2)\|M_{\cdot i}\|^{-2}

(17)

as
N\to\infty
.
Therefore, if
p\geq 2
and
0<\gamma_{i}\leq p
for every
i
, then the generalized Bayes estimator with respect to
\pi_{{\rm MSVS2}}
in (
15
) asymptotically dominates that with respect to
\pi_{{\rm SVS}}
in (
2
) under the Frobenius loss.

Proof.

Let

\displaystyle\pi_{{\rm CS}}(M)=\prod_{i=1}^{p}\|M_{\cdot i}\|^{-\gamma_{i}}.

Then,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial M_{ai}}\log\pi_{{\rm CS}}(M)=-\gamma_{i}M_{ai}\|M_{\cdot i}\|^{-2},

(18)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial M_{ai}^{2}}\log\pi_{{\rm CS}}(M)=-\gamma_{i}\left(\|M_{\cdot i}\|^{2}-2M_{ai}^{2}\right)\|M_{\cdot i}\|^{-4}.

(19)

From (
10
), (
18
), and (
19
),

\displaystyle{\rm tr}(\widetilde{\nabla}\log\pi_{{\rm SVS}}(M)^{\top}\widetilde{\nabla}\log\pi_{{\rm CS}}(M))=(n-p-1)\sum_{i=1}^{p}\gamma_{i}\|M_{\cdot i}\|^{-2},

(20)

\displaystyle{\rm tr}(\widetilde{\nabla}\log\pi_{{\rm CS}}(M)^{\top}\widetilde{\nabla}\log\pi_{{\rm CS}}(M))=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\gamma_{i}^{2}\|M_{\cdot i}\|^{-2},

(21)

\displaystyle{\rm tr}(\widetilde{\Delta}\log\pi_{{\rm CS}}(M))=-(n-2)\sum_{i=1}^{p}\gamma_{i}\|M_{\cdot i}\|^{-2},

(22)

where we used the matrix derivative notations (
28
) and (
29
).
Therefore, from Lemma
A.2
,

\displaystyle{\rm E}_{M}[\|\hat{M}_{{\rm MSVS2}}-M\|_{{\rm F}}^{2}]-{\rm E}_{M}[\|\hat{M}_{{\rm SVS}}-M\|_{{\rm F}}^{2}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N^{2}}{\rm tr}(2\widetilde{\nabla}\log\pi_{{\rm SVS}}(M)^{\top}\widetilde{\nabla}\log\pi_{{\rm CS}}(M)+\widetilde{\nabla}\log\pi_{{\rm CS}}(M)^{\top}\widetilde{\nabla}\log\pi_{{\rm CS}}(M)+2\widetilde{\Delta}\log\pi_{{\rm CS}}(M))

\displaystyle\quad+o(N^{-2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\gamma_{i}(\gamma_{i}-2p+2)\|M_{\cdot i}\|^{-2}+o(N^{-2}).

Hence, we obtain (
17
).
∎

Theorem 4.2
.

For every

,

\displaystyle N^{2}({\rm E}_{M}[(\hat{M}_{{{\rm MSVS2}}}-M)^{\top}

\displaystyle(\hat{M}_{{{\rm MSVS2}}}-M)]-{\rm E}_{M}[(\hat{M}_{{{\rm SVS}}}-M)^{\top}(\hat{M}_{{{\rm SVS}}}-M)])

\displaystyle\to-2(p-1)D+DM^{\top}MD

(23)

as
N\to\infty
, where
D={\rm diag}(\gamma_{1}\|M_{\cdot 1}\|^{-2},\dots,\gamma_{p}\|M_{\cdot p}\|^{-2})
.
Therefore, if
p\geq 2
and
0<\gamma_{1}=\dots=\gamma_{p}<2-2/p
, then the generalized Bayes estimator with respect to
\pi_{{\rm MSVS2}}
in (
15
) asymptotically dominates that with respect to
\pi_{{\rm SVS}}
in (
2
) under the matrix quadratic loss.

Proof.

We use the same notation with the proof of Theorem
4.1
.
By using (
10
), (
18
), and (
19
), we obtain

\displaystyle\widetilde{\nabla}\log\pi_{{\rm SVS}}(M)^{\top}\widetilde{\nabla}\log\pi_{{\rm CS}}(M)=(n-p-1)D,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\nabla}\log\pi_{{\rm CS}}(M)^{\top}\widetilde{\nabla}\log\pi_{{\rm CS}}(M)=DM^{\top}MD,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Delta}\log\pi_{{\rm CS}}(M)=-(n-2)D.

Therefore, from Lemma
A.3
,

\displaystyle{\rm E}_{M}[(\hat{M}_{{{\rm MSVS2}}}-M)^{\top}(\hat{M}_{{{\rm MSVS2}}}-M)]-{\rm E}_{M}[(\hat{M}_{{{\rm SVS}}}-M)^{\top}(\hat{M}_{{{\rm SVS}}}-M)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N^{2}}(2\widetilde{\nabla}\log\pi_{{\rm SVS}}(M)^{\top}\widetilde{\nabla}\log\pi_{{\rm CS}}(M)+\widetilde{\nabla}\log\pi_{{\rm CS}}(M)^{\top}\widetilde{\nabla}\log\pi_{{\rm CS}}(M)+2\widetilde{\Delta}\log\pi_{{\rm CS}}(M))

\displaystyle\quad+o(N^{-2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2(p-1)D+DM^{\top}MD.

Hence, we obtain (
23
).

Suppose that
p\geq 2
and
0<\gamma_{1}=\dots=\gamma_{p}<2-2/p
.
Let
\|\cdot\|
be the operater norm.
Since
D\succeq O
is diagonal and
\|M\|^{2}\leq\|M\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\|M_{\cdot i}\|^{2}
,

\displaystyle\|D^{1/2}M^{\top}MD^{1/2}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|D^{1/2}\|\|M^{\top}M\|\|D^{1/2}\|

\displaystyle=(\max_{i}D_{ii})\|M\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{1}\frac{\|M\|^{2}}{\min_{i}\|M_{\cdot i}\|^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma_{1}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{p}\|M_{\cdot i}\|^{2}}{\min_{i}\|M_{\cdot i}\|^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma_{1}p

\displaystyle<2(p-1),

which yields
D^{1/2}M^{\top}MD^{1/2}\prec 2(p-1)I_{p}
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle-2(p-1)D+DM^{\top}MD=D^{1/2}(-2(p-1)I_{p}+D^{1/2}M^{\top}MD^{1/2})D^{1/2}\prec O.

5
Bayesian prediction

Here, we consider Bayesian prediction and provide parallel results to those in Sections
3
and
4
.
Suppose that we observe
Y\sim{\rm N}_{n,p}(M,I_{n},N^{-1}I_{p})
and predict
\widetilde{Y}\sim{\rm N}_{n,p}(M,I_{n},I_{p})
by a predictive density
\hat{p}(\widetilde{Y}\mid Y)
.
We evaluate predictive densities by the Kullback–Leibler loss:

\displaystyle D({p}(\cdot\mid M),\hat{p}(\cdot\mid Y))=\int{p}(\widetilde{Y}\mid M)\log\frac{{p}(\widetilde{Y}\mid M)}{\hat{p}(\widetilde{Y}\mid Y)}{\rm d}\widetilde{Y}.

The Bayesian predictive density based on a prior
\pi(M)
is defined as

\displaystyle\hat{p}_{\pi}(\widetilde{Y}\mid Y)

\displaystyle=\int{p}(\widetilde{Y}\mid M)\pi(M\mid Y){\rm d}M,

where
\pi(M\mid Y)
is the posterior distribution of
M
given
Y
, and it minimizes the Bayes risk
(
Aitchison 1975
)
:

\displaystyle\hat{p}_{\pi}(\widetilde{Y}\mid Y)

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\hat{p}}\int D({p}(\cdot\mid M),\hat{p}(\cdot\mid Y))p(Y\mid M)\pi(M){\rm d}Y{\rm d}M.

The Bayesian predictive density with respect to the uniform prior is minimax.
However, it is inadmissible and dominated by Bayesian predictive densities based on superharmonic priors
(
Komaki 2001
;
George, Liang and Xu 2006
)
.
In particular, the Bayesian predictive density based on the singular value shrinkage prior
\pi_{{\rm SVS}}
in (
2
) is minimax and dominates that based on the uniform prior
(
Matsuda and Komaki 2015
)
.

The asymptotic expansion of the difference between the Kullback–Leibler risk of two Bayesian predictive densities is obtained as follows.

Lemma 5.1
.

As
N\to\infty
, the difference between the Kullback–Leibler risk of
p_{\pi_{1}}(\widetilde{Y}\mid Y)
and
p_{\pi_{1}\pi_{2}}(\widetilde{Y}\mid Y)
is expanded as

\displaystyle{\rm E}_{M}[D(p(\widetilde{Y}\mid M),p_{\pi_{1}\pi_{2}}(\widetilde{Y}\mid Y))]-{\rm E}_{M}[D(p(\widetilde{Y}\mid M),p_{\pi_{1}}(\widetilde{Y}\mid Y))]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2N^{2}}{\rm tr}(2(\widetilde{\nabla}\log\pi_{1}(M))^{\top}(\widetilde{\nabla}\log\pi_{2}(M))+(\widetilde{\nabla}\log\pi_{2}(M))^{\top}(\widetilde{\nabla}\log\pi_{2}(M))+2\widetilde{\Delta}\log\pi_{2}(M))

\displaystyle\quad+o(N^{-2}).

(24)

Proof.

For the normal model with known covariance, the information geometrical quantities
(
Amari 1985
)
are given by

\displaystyle g_{ij}=g^{ij}=\delta_{ij},\quad\Gamma_{ij}^{k}=0,\quad T_{ijk}=0.

Also, the Jeffreys prior coincides with the uniform prior
\pi(M)\equiv 1
.
Therefore, from equation (3) of
Komaki 2006
, the Kullback–Leibler risk of the Bayesian predictive density

p_{\pi}(\widetilde{Y}\mid Y)
based on a prior
\pi(M)
is expanded as

\displaystyle{\rm E}_{M}[D(p(\widetilde{Y}\mid M),p_{\pi}(\widetilde{Y}\mid Y))]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{np}{2N}+\frac{1}{2N^{2}}{\rm tr}((\widetilde{\nabla}\log\pi(M))^{\top}(\widetilde{\nabla}\log\pi(M))+2\widetilde{\Delta}\log\pi(M))+g(M)+o(N^{-2}),

(25)

where
g(M)
is a function independent of
\pi(M)
.
Substituting
\pi=\pi_{1}\pi_{2}
and
\pi=\pi_{1}
into (
25
) and taking difference, we obtain (
24
).
∎

By comparing Lemma
5.1
to Lemma
A.2
, we obtain the following connection between estimation and prediction.

Proposition 5.1
.

For every
M
,

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}N^{2}({\rm E}_{M}[D(p(\widetilde{Y}\mid M),\hat{p}_{\pi_{1}\pi_{2}}(\widetilde{Y}\mid Y))]-{\rm E}_{M}[D(p(\widetilde{Y}\mid M),\hat{p}_{\pi_{1}}(\widetilde{Y}\mid Y))])

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\lim_{N\to\infty}N^{2}({\rm E}_{M}[\|\hat{M}^{\pi_{1}\pi_{2}}-M\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}]-{\rm E}_{M}[\|\hat{M}^{\pi_{1}}-M\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}]).

Therefore, if
\hat{\theta}^{\pi_{1}\pi_{2}}
asymptotically dominates
\hat{\theta}^{\pi_{1}}
under the quadratic loss, then
\hat{p}_{\pi_{1}\pi_{2}}(\widetilde{Y}\mid Y)
asymptotically dominates
\hat{p}_{\pi_{1}}(\widetilde{Y}\mid Y)
under the Kullback–Leibler loss.

Therefore, Theorems
3.1
and
4.1
are extended to Bayesian prediction as follows.
Other results in the previous sections can be extended to Bayesian prediction similarly.

.

For every
M
,

\displaystyle N^{2}({\rm E}_{M}[D(p(\cdot\mid M),\hat{p}_{{\rm MSVS1}}(\cdot\mid Y))]-{\rm E}_{M}[D(p(\cdot\mid M),\hat{p}_{{\rm SVS}}(\cdot\mid Y))])

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma(\gamma-2p^{2}-2p+4)}{2{\rm tr}(M^{\top}M)}

as

.
Therefore, if

and

, then the Bayesian predictive density with respect to

in (

) asymptotically dominates that with respect to
\pi_{{\rm SVS}}

.

For every
M
,

\displaystyle N^{2}({\rm E}_{M}[D(p(\cdot\mid M),\hat{p}_{{\rm MSVS2}}(\cdot\mid Y))]-{\rm E}_{M}[D(p(\cdot\mid M),\hat{p}_{{\rm SVS}}(\cdot\mid Y))])

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\gamma_{i}(\gamma_{i}-2p+2)\|M_{\cdot i}\|^{-2}

as

.
Therefore, if

Appendix A
Asymptotic expansion of risk

Here, we provide asymptotic expansion formulas for estimators of a normal mean vector.
Consider the problem of estimating
\theta
from the observation
Y\sim{\rm N}_{d}(\theta,N^{-1}I_{d})
under the quadratic loss
l(\theta,\hat{\theta})=\|\hat{\theta}-\theta\|^{2}
.
As shown in
Stein 1974
, the generalized Bayes estimator
\hat{\theta}^{\pi}
with respect to a prior
\pi(\theta)
is expressed as

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}^{\pi}(y)=y+\frac{1}{N}\nabla_{y}\log m_{\pi}(y),

where

\displaystyle m_{\pi}(y)=\int p(y\mid\theta)\pi(\theta){\rm d}\theta.

The asymptotic difference between the quadratic risk of two generalized Bayes estimators as
N\to\infty
is given as follows.

Lemma A.1
.

As
N\to\infty
, the difference between the quadratic risk of
\hat{\theta}^{\pi_{1}}
and
\hat{\theta}^{\pi_{1}\pi_{2}}
is expanded as

\displaystyle{\rm E}_{\theta}[\|\hat{\theta}^{\pi_{1}\pi_{2}}-\theta\|^{2}]-{\rm E}_{\theta}[\|\hat{\theta}^{\pi_{1}}-\theta\|^{2}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N^{2}}(2\nabla\log\pi_{1}(\theta)^{\top}\nabla\log\pi_{2}(\theta)+\|\nabla\log\pi_{2}(\theta)\|^{2}+2\Delta\log\pi_{2}(\theta))+o(N^{-2}).

(26)

Proof.

By using Stein’s lemma
(
Fourdrinier et al. 2018
)
and
m_{\pi}(y)=\pi(y)+o(1)
as
N\to\infty
, the quadratic risk of the generalized Bayes estimator
\hat{\theta}^{\pi}
is calculated as

\displaystyle{\rm E}_{\theta}[\|\hat{\theta}^{\pi}(y)-\theta\|^{2}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm E}_{\theta}[\|y-\theta\|^{2}]+\frac{2}{N}{\rm E}_{\theta}[(y-\theta)^{\top}\nabla\log m_{\pi}(y)]+\frac{1}{N^{2}}{\rm E}_{\theta}[\|\nabla\log m_{\pi}(y)\|^{2}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{d}{N}+\frac{1}{N^{2}}{\rm E}_{\theta}[\|\nabla\log m_{\pi}(y)\|^{2}+2\Delta\log m_{\pi}(y)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{d}{N}+\frac{1}{N^{2}}\left(\|\nabla\log\pi(\theta)\|^{2}+2\Delta\log\pi(\theta)\right)+o(N^{-2}).

(27)

Substituting
\pi=\pi_{1}\pi_{2}
and
\pi=\pi_{1}
into (
27
) and taking difference, we obtain (
26
).
∎

We extend the above formula to matrices by using the matrix derivative notations from
Matsuda and Strawderman 2022
.
For a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}\to\mathbb{R}
, its matrix gradient
\widetilde{\nabla}f:\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
is defined as

\displaystyle(\widetilde{\nabla}f(X))_{ai}=\frac{\partial}{\partial X_{ai}}f(X).

(28)

For a
C^{2}
function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}\to\mathbb{R}
, its matrix Laplacian
\widetilde{\Delta}f:\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}\to\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
is defined as

\displaystyle(\widetilde{\Delta}f(X))_{ij}=\sum_{a=1}^{n}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial X_{ai}\partial X_{aj}}f(X).

(29)

Then, the above formulas can be straightforwardly extended to matrix-variate normal distributions as follows.

Lemma A.2
.

As
N\to\infty
, the difference between the Frobenius risk of
\hat{M}^{\pi_{1}}
and
\hat{M}^{\pi_{1}\pi_{2}}
is expanded as

\displaystyle{\rm E}_{M}[\|\hat{M}^{\pi_{1}\pi_{2}}-M\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}]-{\rm E}_{M}[\|\hat{M}^{\pi_{1}}-M\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N^{2}}{\rm tr}(2\widetilde{\nabla}\log\pi_{1}(M)^{\top}\widetilde{\nabla}\log\pi_{2}(M)+\widetilde{\nabla}\log\pi_{2}(M)^{\top}\widetilde{\nabla}\log\pi_{2}(M)+2\widetilde{\Delta}\log\pi_{2}(M))

\displaystyle\quad+o(N^{-2}).

Lemma A.3
.

As
N\to\infty
, the difference between the matrix quadratic risk of
\hat{M}^{\pi_{1}}
and
\hat{M}^{\pi_{1}\pi_{2}}
is expanded as

\displaystyle{\rm E}_{M}[(\hat{M}^{\pi_{1}\pi_{2}}-M)^{\top}(\hat{M}^{\pi_{1}\pi_{2}}-M)-(\hat{M}^{\pi_{1}}-M)^{\top}(\hat{M}^{\pi_{1}}-M)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N^{2}}(2\widetilde{\nabla}\log\pi_{1}(M)^{\top}\widetilde{\nabla}\log\pi_{2}(M)+\widetilde{\nabla}\log\pi_{2}(M)^{\top}\widetilde{\nabla}\log\pi_{2}(M)+2\widetilde{\Delta}\log\pi_{2}(M))

\displaystyle\quad+o(N^{-2}).

Komaki 2006
derived the asymptotic expansion of the Kullback–Leibler risk of Bayesian predictive densities.
For the normal model as discussed in Section
5
, the result shows that Stein’s prior dominates the Jeffreys prior in
O(N^{-1})
term at the origin and
O(N^{-2})
term at other points, which is reminiscent of superefficiency theory.
A similar phenomenon should exist in estimation as well.
Unlike Stein’s prior, the priors for a normal mean matrix such as
\pi_{\mathrm{SVS}}
diverge at many points such as low-rank matrices.
It is an interesting future problem to investigate the asymptotic risk of such priors in detail.

Appendix B
Laplacian of
\pi_{{\rm MSVS1}}
and

Lemma B.1
.

(
Stein 1974
;
Matsuda and Strawderman 2019
)

Suppose that
f:\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}\to\mathbb{R}
is represented as
f(X)=\widetilde{f}(\sigma)
, where
n\geq p
and
\sigma=(\sigma_{1}(X),\ldots,\sigma_{p}(X))
denotes the singular values of
X
. If
f
is twice weakly differentiable, then its Laplacian is

\displaystyle\Delta f

\displaystyle=\sum_{a=1}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial X_{ai}^{2}}=2\sum_{i<j}\frac{\sigma_{i}{\partial\widetilde{f}}/{\partial\sigma_{i}}-\sigma_{j}{\partial\widetilde{f}}/{\partial\sigma_{j}}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}-\sigma_{j}^{2}}+(n-p)\sum_{i=1}^{p}\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}}\frac{\partial\widetilde{f}}{\partial\sigma_{i}}+\sum_{i=1}^{p}\frac{\partial^{2}\widetilde{f}}{\partial\sigma_{i}^{2}}.

Proposition B.1
.

The Laplacian of
\pi_{{\rm MSVS1}}
in (
7
) is given by

\displaystyle\Delta\pi_{{\rm MSVS1}}(M)

\displaystyle=\gamma(\gamma+np-2p^{2}-2p+2)\|M\|_{{\rm F}}^{-2}\pi_{{\rm MSVS1}}(M).

Proof.

Let

\displaystyle\widetilde{f}(\sigma)=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{p}\sigma_{i}^{-(n-p-1)}\right)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{p}\sigma_{i}^{2}\right)^{-\gamma/2}

so that
\pi_{{\rm MSVS1}}(M)=\widetilde{f}(\sigma)
with
\sigma=(\sigma_{1}(M),\dots,\sigma_{p}(M))
.
From Lemma
B.1
and

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\widetilde{f}}{\partial\sigma_{i}}=-(n-p-1)\sigma_{i}^{-1}\widetilde{f}-\gamma\sigma_{i}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{p}\sigma_{j}^{2}\right)^{-1}\widetilde{f},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\widetilde{f}}{\partial\sigma_{i}^{2}}=(n-p)(n-p-1)\sigma_{i}^{-2}\widetilde{f}+(2n-2p-3)\gamma\left(\sum_{j=1}^{p}\sigma_{j}^{2}\right)^{-1}\widetilde{f}+\gamma(\gamma+2)\sigma_{i}^{2}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{p}\sigma_{j}^{2}\right)^{-2}\widetilde{f},

we have

\displaystyle\Delta\pi_{{\rm MSVS1}}(M)

\displaystyle=2\sum_{i<j}\frac{\sigma_{i}{\partial\widetilde{f}}/{\partial\sigma_{i}}-\sigma_{j}{\partial\widetilde{f}}/{\partial\sigma_{j}}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}-\sigma_{j}^{2}}+(n-p)\sum_{i=1}^{p}\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}}\frac{\partial\widetilde{f}}{\partial\sigma_{i}}+\sum_{i=1}^{p}\frac{\partial^{2}\widetilde{f}}{\partial\sigma_{i}^{2}}

\displaystyle=-2\cdot\frac{p(p-1)}{2}\gamma\left(\sum_{i=1}^{p}\sigma_{i}^{2}\right)^{-1}\widetilde{f}-(n-p)(n-p-1)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{p}\sigma_{i}^{-2}\right)\widetilde{f}

\displaystyle\quad-p(n-p)\gamma\left(\sum_{i=1}^{p}\sigma_{i}^{2}\right)^{-1}\widetilde{f}+(n-p)(n-p-1)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{p}\sigma_{i}^{-2}\right)\widetilde{f}

\displaystyle\quad+\gamma(p(2n-2p-3)+\gamma+2)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{p}\sigma_{i}^{2}\right)^{-1}\widetilde{f}

\displaystyle=\gamma(\gamma+np-2p^{2}-2p+2)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{p}\sigma_{i}^{2}\right)^{-1}\widetilde{f}.

∎

.

The Laplacian of
\pi_{{\rm MSVS2}}

\displaystyle\Delta\pi_{{\rm MSVS2}}(M)

\displaystyle=\gamma(\gamma+n-2p)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{p}\|M_{\cdot i}\|^{-2}\right)\pi_{{\rm MSVS2}}(M).

From

\displaystyle\Delta\log f=\frac{\Delta f}{f}-\|\nabla\log f\|^{2}

and (

),

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta\pi_{{\rm MSVS2}}(M)}{\pi_{{\rm MSVS2}}(M)}

Appendix C
Improving on the block-wise Stein prior

Here, we develop priors that asymptotically dominate the block-wise Stein prior in estimation and prediction.
Suppose that we observe
Y\sim{\rm N}_{d}(\theta,N^{-1}I_{d})
and estimate
\theta
or predict
\widetilde{Y}\sim{\rm N}_{d}(\theta,I_{d})
.
We assume that the
d
-dimensional mean vector
\theta
is split into
B
disjoint blocks
\theta^{(1)},\dots,\theta^{(B)}
with size
d_{1},\dots,d_{B}
, where
d_{1}+\dots+d_{B}=d
.
For example. such a situation appears in balanced ANOVA and wavelet regression
(
Brown and Zhao 2009
)
.
Then, the block-wise Stein prior is defined as

\displaystyle\pi_{{\rm BS}}(\theta)=\prod_{b=1}^{B}\|\theta^{(b)}\|^{R_{b}},\quad R_{b}=-(d_{b}-2)_{+},

(30)

which puts Stein’s prior on each block.
Since it is superharmonic, the generalized Bayes estimator
\hat{\theta}^{\pi_{{\rm BS}}}
with respect to
\pi_{{\rm BS}}
is minimax.
However,
Brown and Zhao 2009
showed that
\hat{\theta}^{\pi_{{\rm BS}}}
is inadmissible and dominated by an estimator with additional James–Stein type shrinkage defined by

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}(y)=\hat{\theta}^{\pi_{{\rm BS}}}(y)-\frac{R_{\#}+d-2}{\|y\|^{2}}y,

where
R_{\#}=\sum_{b}R_{b}>2-d
.
From this result,
Brown and Zhao 2009
conjectured that the block-wise Stein prior can be improved by multiplying a Stein-type shrinkage prior in Remark 3.2.
Following their conjecture, we construct priors by adding scalar shrinkage to the block-wise Stein priors:

\displaystyle\pi_{{\rm MBS}}(\theta)=\pi_{{\rm BS}}(\theta)\|\theta\|^{-\gamma},

(31)

where
\gamma\geq 0
.
Let

\displaystyle m_{{\rm MBS}}(y)=\int p(y\mid\theta)\pi_{{\rm MBS}}(\theta){\rm d}\theta.

Lemma C.1
.

If
0\leq\gamma<B(R_{b}+d_{b})
for every
b
, then
m_{{\rm MBS}}(y)<\infty
for every
y
.

Proof.

Since
m_{{\rm MBS}}(y)
is interpreted as the expectation of
\pi_{{\rm MBS}}(\theta)
under
\theta\sim{\rm N}_{d}(y,I_{d})
, it suffices to show that
\pi_{{\rm MBS}}(\theta)
is locally integrable at every
\theta
.

First, consider
\theta\neq 0
.
Since
m_{{\rm BS}}(y)<\infty
for every
y

(
Brown and Zhao 2009
)
,
\pi_{{\rm BS}}(\theta)
is locally integrable at
\theta
.
Also,
\|\theta\|>c
for some
c>0
in a neighborhood of
\theta
.
Thus,
\pi_{{\rm MBS}}(\theta)=\pi_{{\rm BS}}(\theta)\|\theta\|^{-\gamma}
is locally integrable at
\theta
.

Next, consider
\theta=0
and take the neighborhood
A=\{\theta\mid\|\theta^{(1)}\|\leq s,\dots,\|\theta^{(B)}\|\leq s\}
for
s>0
.
From the AM-GM inequality,

\displaystyle\|\theta\|^{2}=\sum_{b}\|\theta^{(b)}\|^{2}\geq B\left(\prod_{b}\|\theta^{(b)}\|^{2}\right)^{1/B}=B\prod_{b}\|\theta^{(b)}\|^{2/B}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\int_{A}\pi_{{\rm MBS}}(\theta){\rm d}\theta\leq\

\displaystyle C\int_{0}^{s}\cdots\int_{0}^{s}\prod_{b}r_{b}^{R_{b}+d_{b}-1-\gamma/B}{\rm d}r_{1}\cdots{\rm d}r_{B},

where
r_{b}=\|\theta^{(b)}\|
and
C
is a constant.
Therefore,
\pi_{{\rm MBS}}(\theta)
is locally integrable at
\theta=0
if
R_{b}+d_{b}-1-\gamma/B>-1
for every
b
, which is equivalent to
\gamma<B(R_{b}+d_{b})
for every
b
.
∎

From Lemma
C.1
, the generalized Bayes estimator with respect to
\pi_{{\rm MBS}}
is well-defined when
0\leq\gamma<B(R_{b}+d_{b})
for every
b
.
We denote it by
\hat{\theta}_{{\rm MBS}}
.

Theorem C.1
.

For every
M
,

\displaystyle N^{2}({\rm E}_{\theta}[\|\hat{\theta}_{{{\rm MBS}}}-\theta\|^{2}]-{\rm E}_{\theta}[\|\hat{\theta}_{{{\rm BS}}}-\theta\|^{2}])\to\gamma(\gamma-2(R_{\#}+d-2))\|\theta\|^{-2}

(32)

as
N\to\infty
.
Therefore, if
0<\gamma<2(R_{\#}+d-2)
, then the generalized Bayes estimator with respect to
\pi_{{\rm MBS}}
in (
31
) asymptotically dominates that with respect to
\pi_{{\rm BS}}
in (
30
) under the Frobenius loss.

Proof.

Let
\pi_{{\rm S}}(\theta)=\|\theta\|^{-\gamma}
.
By straightforward calculation, we obtain

\displaystyle\nabla\log\pi_{{\rm BS}}(\theta)^{\top}\nabla\log\pi_{{\rm S}}(\theta)=-\gamma R_{\#}\|\theta\|^{-2},

\displaystyle\nabla\log\pi_{{\rm S}}(\theta)^{\top}\nabla\log\pi_{{\rm S}}(\theta)=\gamma^{2}\|\theta\|^{-2},

\displaystyle\Delta\log\pi_{{\rm S}}(\theta)=-\gamma(d-2)\|\theta\|^{-2}.

Therefore, from Lemma
A.1
,

\displaystyle{\rm E}_{\theta}[\|\hat{\theta}_{{{\rm MBS}}}-\theta\|^{2}]-{\rm E}_{\theta}[\|\hat{\theta}_{{{\rm BS}}}-\theta\|^{2}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N^{2}}\left(2\nabla\log\pi_{{\rm BS}}(\theta)^{\top}\nabla\log\pi_{{\rm S}}(\theta)+\|\nabla\log\pi_{{\rm S}}(\theta)\|^{2}+2\Delta\log\pi_{{\rm S}}(\theta)\right)+o(N^{-2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N^{2}}\gamma(\gamma-2(R_{\#}+d-2))\|\theta\|^{-2}+o(N^{-2}).

Hence, we obtain (
32
).
∎

From (
32
), the choice
\gamma=R_{\#}+d-2
is optimal.
As discussed in Section
5
, Theorem
C.1
is extended to Bayesian prediction as follows.

Theorem C.2
.

For every
M
,

\displaystyle N^{2}({\rm E}_{\theta}[D(p(\cdot\mid\theta),\hat{p}_{{\rm MBS}}(\cdot\mid y))]-{\rm E}_{\theta}[D(p(\cdot\mid\theta),\hat{p}_{{\rm BS}}(\cdot\mid y))])\to\frac{\gamma(\gamma-2(R_{\#}+d-2))}{2}\|\theta\|^{-2}

as
N\to\infty
.
Therefore, if
p\geq 2
and
0<\gamma<p^{2}+p
, then the Bayesian predictive density with respect to
\pi_{{\rm MBS}}
in (
31
) asymptotically dominates that with respect to
\pi_{{\rm BS}}
in (
30
) under the Kullback–Leibler loss.
