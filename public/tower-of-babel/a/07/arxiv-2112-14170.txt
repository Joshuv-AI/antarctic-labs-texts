Anomalous Surface Conductivity of Weyl Semimetals
Hridis K. Pal, Osakpolor Eki Obakpolor, Pavan Hosur

Abstract
We calculate the surface dc conductivity of Weyl semimetals and show that it contains an anomalous contribution in addition to a Drude contribution from the Fermi arc. The anomalous part is independent of the surface scattering time, and appears at nonzero temperature and doping (away from the Weyl nodes), increasing quadratically with both with a universal ratio of coefficients. Microscopically, it results from the contribution of the gapless bulk to the surface conductivity. We argue that this can be interpreted as the conductivity of an effective interacting surface fluid that coexists with the Fermi arc metal. In a certain regime of low temperatures, the temperature dependence of the surface conductivity is dominated by the anomalous response, which can be probed experimentally to unravel the unusual behavior.

In this work, we calculate a basic surface transport property of a WSM, namely, the dc conductivity
\sigma_{\textrm{surf}}
. Unlike previous studies of surface transport in WSMs that exclusively focus on the contributions of the FAs
(
Gorbar et al. 2016
;
Wilson et al. 2018
;
Resta et al. 2018
;
Mukherjee et al. 2019
;
Breitkreiz and Brouwer 2019
)
, we include the nontrivial influence of the bulk on the surface in a controlled manner and show that
\sigma_{\textrm{surf}}
is comprised of two qualitatively different contributions:

\sigma_{\textrm{surf}}=\sigma_{\textrm{norm}}+\sigma_{\textrm{anom}}.

(1)

Here,
\sigma_{\textrm{norm}}
is a normal, Drude-like conductivity arising from the FA. It is proportional to the scattering time (
\tau
) and has a negligible dependence on temperature (
T
) besides the
T
-dependence of
\tau
. In contrast,
\sigma_{\textrm{anom}}
is an anomalous term arising from the bulk states. It is
\tau
-independent, but has a characteristic dependence on both
T
and doping around the Weyl nodes,
\mu
:
\sigma_{\textrm{anom}}\propto\mu^{2}+k_{B}^{2}T^{2}\pi^{2}/3
. Crucially, the coefficients have a universal ratio,
k_{B}^{2}\pi^{2}/3
, independent of material parameters.
In a regime of low temperatures described later, the
T
-dependent part of
\sigma_{\textrm{surf}}
is dominated by
\sigma_{\textrm{anom}}
which provides immediate experimental access to
\sigma_{\textrm{anom}}
.
Note that
\sigma_{\textrm{anom}}
is
\tau
-independent even if the bulk quasiparticles have a nonzero lifetime. This is reminiscent of the universal minimal conductivity of a 2D Dirac fermion that only depends on fundamental constants
Katsnelson and Novoselov 2007
. As espoused later, the electrical response of the bulk states on the surface mimics that of an interacting 2D fluid that coexists with the FA metal on the surface. This, in turn, gives rise to unusual form of
\sigma_{\textrm{anom}}
in Eq. (
1
). Thus, the daunting problem of the 2D FAs coexisting with the 3D bulk states reduces to a tractable, strictly 2D, effective two-fluid problem.

Model.
We employ a variant of the model introduced in Ref.
Hosur 2012
for generating a WSM with arbitrary configurations of FAs and Weyl nodes. The model consists of a finite stack of metallic bilayers whose Hamiltonian as a function of
\mathbf{k}=(k_{x},k_{y})
is:

H_{\mathbf{k}}=\sum_{z=1}^{L}\psi_{z,\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}[(-1)^{z}\xi_{\mathbf{k}}-\mu]\psi_{z,\mathbf{k}}-\sum_{z=1}^{L-1}\psi_{z,\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}t_{z,\mathbf{k}}\psi_{z+1,\mathbf{k}}+\mathrm{H.c.},

(2)

where
\psi_{z,\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}
creates an electron with momentum
\hbar\mathbf{k}
in layer
z
,
\xi_{\mathbf{k}}
denotes a metallic dispersion with Fermi surface along
\xi_{\mathbf{k}}=0
,
\mu
is the bulk chemical potential and the interlayer hopping modulates as
t_{z,\mathbf{k}}=t_{\perp}+(-1)^{z}\delta t_{\mathbf{k}}
. If
|\delta t_{\mathbf{k}}|<|t_{\perp}|\ \forall\ \mathbf{k}
,
H_{\mathbf{k}}
produces bulk Weyl nodes in the
k_{z}=\pi/2c
plane, where
2c
is the lattice constant along
z
, at discrete points
\mathbf{K}_{j}\equiv(K_{jx},K_{jy})
such that
\xi_{\mathbf{K}_{j}}=\delta t_{\mathbf{K}_{j}}=0
. Near the
j^{th}
node, the bulk Hamiltonian is
H_{\text{Weyl},j}(\mathbf{q})=\hbar\mathbf{q}\cdot\left(\mathbf{v}_{j}\sigma_{z}+\mathbf{u}_{j}\sigma_{x}+\mathbf{w}_{j}\sigma_{y}\right)-\mu
, where
\sigma_{\alpha}
are bilayer Pauli matrices and

\mathbf{v}_{j}=\frac{1}{\hbar}\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\mathbf{K}_{j}}\xi_{\mathbf{K}_{j}}
,

\mathbf{u}_{j}=\frac{2}{\hbar}\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\mathbf{K}_{j}}\delta t_{\mathbf{K}_{j}}

and
\mathbf{w}_{j}=-\frac{2}{\hbar}t_{\perp}c\hat{\mathbf{z}}
are Weyl velocities.

The surface (
z=1
) Matsubara Green’s function in the clean, non-interacting limit when
L\to\infty
is
(
Obakpolor and Hosur 2021
;
Hosur 2012
)
(see Supplemental Material [SM]
SM
for details)

\displaystyle G_{\mathbf{k}}(iE_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{a_{\mathbf{k}}(iE_{n})+b_{\mathbf{k}}(iE_{n})}{2(t_{\perp}+\delta t_{\mathbf{k}})^{2}(iE_{n}+\mu-\xi_{\mathbf{k}})},

(3)

\displaystyle a_{\mathbf{k}}(iE_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(iE_{n}+\mu)^{2}-\xi_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}+4t_{\perp}\delta t_{\mathbf{k}},

\displaystyle b_{\mathbf{k}}(iE_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\prod_{\lambda=\pm}\left[(iE_{n}+\mu)^{2}-E_{\mathbf{k}\lambda}^{2}\right]},

where
E_{\mathbf{k}+}=\sqrt{\xi_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}+4t_{\perp}^{2}}
and
E_{\mathbf{k}-}=\sqrt{\xi_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}+4\delta t_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}}
are the extremeties of the bulk conduction band at
\mathbf{k}
. For real energies,
G_{\mathbf{k}}(E)
has BCs when
|E+\mu|\in[E_{\mathbf{k}-},E_{\mathbf{k}+}]
, i.e.,
E
lies within the bulk bands, which we dub the BC-region. When
|E+\mu|\not\in[E_{\mathbf{k}-},E_{\mathbf{k}+}]
, dubbed the N-region,

has simple poles along

1
). Importantly, the poles and BCs are generic features of surface Green’s functions in WSMs and are not specific to the current model

.

Surface conductivity.
Since our goal here is to investigate surface transport, we need to introduce a nonzero quasiparticle lifetime
\tau_{\mathbf{k}}
into Eq. (
24
) that captures the effect of scattering required to relax momentum gained due to the external field. To this effect, we revisit Eq. (
15
): a nonzero lifetime is introduced in each layer and the surface (
z=1
) Matsubara Green’s function is reevaluated—see SM
SM
for details. We assume scattering to be weak such that
1/\tau_{\mathbf{k}}\rightarrow 0
. Analytically continuing, the retarded Green’s functions in the N- and BC-regions are found to be

\displaystyle G^{R}_{\mathbf{k}}(E)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{a_{\mathbf{k}}(E)+b_{\mathbf{k}}(E)}{2(t_{\perp}+\delta t_{\mathbf{k}})^{2}(E+\mu-\xi_{\mathbf{k}}+\frac{i\hbar}{2\tau_{\mathbf{k}}})},E\in N

(4)

\displaystyle G^{R}_{\mathbf{k}}(E)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{a_{\mathbf{k}}(E)+i\ \mathrm{sgn}(E+\mu)|b_{\mathbf{k}}(E)|}{2(t_{\perp}+\delta t_{\mathbf{k}})^{2}(E+\mu-\xi_{\mathbf{k}}+\frac{i\hbar}{2\tau_{\mathbf{k}}})},E\in BC

(5)

Note that we have assumed only
E
-independent scattering processes for simplicity, but relaxing this assumption will not change our qualitative results.

The longitudinal dc conductivity along
x
-direction due to the motion of electrons only on the surface is given by

\sigma_{\textrm{surf}}=\frac{e^{2}\hbar}{2}\intop_{E,\mathbf{k}}\left(-\frac{\partial f}{\partial E}\right)\left[v_{\mathbf{k},x}A_{\mathbf{k}}(E)\right]^{2},

(6)

where
f(E)=1/[1+\exp(E/k_{B}T)]
,
\intop_{E,\mathbf{k}}=\int\frac{d^{2}kdE}{(2\pi)^{3}}
,
v_{\mathbf{k},x}=\frac{1}{\hbar}\partial_{k_{x}}\xi_{\mathbf{k}}
is the
x
-component of the in-plane velocity, and
A_{\mathbf{k}}(E)=-2\textrm{Im}G^{R}_{\mathbf{k}}(E)
is the surface spectral function. Due to the distinct forms of
G^{R}_{\mathbf{k}}(E)
in the N- and BC-regions, it is convenient to split the
E
-integral as
\int_{E}=\int_{E\in N}+\int_{E\in BC}\equiv\int_{N}+\int_{BC}
. The two terms yield
\sigma_{\textrm{norm}}
and
\sigma_{\textrm{anom}}
respectively, which leads to the decomposition of
\sigma_{\textrm{surf}}
stated in Eq. (
1
). Moreover, in the limit
1/\tau_{\mathbf{k}}\to 0
,
\int_{N}
receives contributions only from a sharp quasiparticle peak in
A_{\mathbf{k}}(E)
due to the FAs while
\int_{BC}
depends only on a broad feature in
A_{\mathbf{k}}(E)
that captures the surface projection of the bulk states. Thus,
\sigma_{\textrm{norm}}
and
\sigma_{\textrm{anom}}
are in one-to-one correspondence with the FA and the bulk contributions to surface transport.

In the N-region, Eqs. (
24
), (
25
) and (
6
) give
A_{\mathbf{k}}(E)=2\pi W_{\mathbf{k}}\delta_{\hbar/2\tau_{\mathbf{k}}}(E+\mu-\xi_{\mathbf{k}})

W_{\mathbf{k}}=R\left[\frac{4t_{\perp}\delta t_{\mathbf{k}}}{(t_{\perp}+\delta t_{\mathbf{k}})^{2}}\right]
with
R(x)=x\Theta(x)
and
\delta_{\eta}(x)=\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{x}{x^{2}+\eta^{2}}
. For
1/\tau_{\mathbf{k}}\to 0
, Eq. (
6
) then yields,

\sigma_{\textrm{norm}}=e^{2}\int_{N}\left(-\frac{\partial f}{\partial E}\right)\intop_{\mathbf{k}}2\pi\tau_{\mathbf{k}}v_{\mathbf{k},x}^{2}W_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}\delta(E+\mu-\xi_{\mathbf{k}}).

(7)

The factor
W_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}
above ensures that
\sigma_{\textrm{norm}}
gets contributions only from the metallic FAs. Additionally, the contribution varies as one traverses the FA: it is largest near the middle of the FA (maximum
t_{\perp}\delta t_{\mathbf{k}}
), vanishes at the Weyl nodes (
\delta t_{\mathbf{k}}=0
), and remains zero in regions lacking FAs (
t_{\perp}\delta t_{\mathbf{k}}<0
). At
T=0
, we find

\sigma_{\textrm{norm}}=2e^{2}\nu_{F}D,

(8)

where
\nu_{F}=\intop_{\mathbf{k}}{W}_{\mathbf{k}}\delta(\mu-\xi_{\mathbf{k}})
and
D=\frac{1}{2}\langle v_{F,x}^{2}\tau\rangle=(2\nu_{F})^{-1}\intop_{\mathbf{k}}\delta(\mu-\xi_{\mathbf{k}})(v_{\mathbf{k},x}{W}_{\mathbf{k}})^{2}\tau_{\mathbf{k}}

are the suitably weighted means of the density of states per unit area (DOS) and the diffusion constant due to the FAs. Clearly,
\sigma_{\textrm{norm}}
is the Drude conductivity, scaling linearly with
\tau
. At nonzero
T
, temperature enters the conductivity mainly through
\tau_{\mathbf{k}}
which is model-dependent, and is discussed later. Temperature may also enter via corrections to the DOS arising from the Sommerfeld expansion. However, such corrections vanish exactly for an ordinary 2D parabolic dispersion. By extension, the corrections are expected to be small for generic 2D metals including the FA metal, which we ignore henceforth.

\displaystyle\sigma_{\textrm{anom}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2e^{2}\hbar\int_{BC}\left(-\frac{\partial f}{\partial E}\right)\intop_{\mathbf{k}}v_{\mathbf{k},x}^{2}\times

(9)

\displaystyle\frac{\left[(E+\mu)^{2}-E_{\mathbf{k}-}^{2}\right]\left[E_{\mathbf{k}+}^{2}-(E+\mu)^{2}\right]}{(E+\mu-\xi_{\mathbf{k}})^{2}(t_{\perp}+\delta t_{\mathbf{k}})^{2}}.

At low

E_{\mathbf{k}-}\lesssim k_{B}T+|\mu|\lesssim E_{\mathbf{k}+}

and vanishes only at the Weyl nodes, where the bulk bands touch. As a result, the dominant contribution to
\sigma_{\textrm{anom}}
comes from the vicinity of the Weyl nodes. Thus, we can linearize around the

\xi_{\mathbf{k}}\approx\hbar\mathbf{v}_{j}\cdot\mathbf{q}

where we assumed

. If the Weyl nodes are at different energies, we must replace

, the chemical potential relative to the energy of the

Effective interacting surface fluid.
We now reinterpret
\sigma_{\textrm{anom}}
as the conductivity of an effective interacting surface fluid. To this end, we consider the surface particle density at
1/\tau_{\mathbf{k}}\to 0
,
n=\intop_{\mathbf{k},E}A_{\mathbf{k}}(E)=n_{\textrm{norm}}+n_{\textrm{anom}}
where
n_{\textrm{norm}}=\intop_{\mathbf{k}}f(\xi_{\mathbf{k}}-\mu)W_{\mathbf{k}}
and

n_{\textrm{anom}}=\intop_{\mathbf{k}}\intop_{BC}f(E)\frac{\textrm{sgn}(E+\mu)|b_{\mathbf{k}}|}{(t_{\perp}+\delta t_{\mathbf{k}})^{2}(E+\mu-\xi_{\mathbf{k}})}.

(11)

As in the case of
\sigma_{\textrm{anom}}
, the integral receives contributions only from the regions near the Weyl nodes. Carrying out this integral by linearizing around the Weyl nodes as before, we get
n_{\textrm{anom}}=\sum_{j}n_{\textrm{anom},j}
, with

n_{\textrm{anom},j}=\frac{\mu\left(\mu^{2}+k_{B}^{2}T^{2}\pi^{2}\right)}{6\pi^{2}\hbar^{3}}\frac{c}{|(\mathbf{u}_{j}\times\mathbf{v}_{j})\cdot\mathbf{w}_{j}|}.

(12)

This, in turn, gives an effective DOS from the BC,
\nu_{\textrm{anom},j}=dn_{\textrm{anom},j}/d\mu
, in terms of which
Eq. (
10
) can be expressed as

\sigma_{\textrm{anom}}=\sum_{j}e^{2}\nu_{\textrm{anom},j}v_{j,x}^{2}\frac{2\hbar}{t_{\perp}}.

(13)

Written as above,
\sigma_{\textrm{anom}}
resembles the Drude conductivity of a 2D metal, similar to
\sigma_{\textrm{norm}}
in Eq. (
8
), except that the DOS on the FA is replaced by an effective DOS on the surface due to the bulk and
\tilde{\tau}=2\hbar/t_{\perp}
plays the role of lifetime in lieu of
\tau_{\mathbf{k}}
.

The appearance of a new effective lifetime can be traced to the fact that
\textrm{Im}G^{R}\neq 0
when
E\in BC
even when
1/\tau_{\mathbf{k}}=0
. In fact, one can write Eq. (
26
) as

\displaystyle G^{R}_{\mathbf{k}}(E)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{Z_{\mathbf{k}}(E)}{E+\mu-\xi_{\mathbf{k}}+\frac{i\hbar}{2\tilde{\tau}_{\mathbf{k}}(E)}},

\displaystyle Z_{\mathbf{k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{a_{\mathbf{k}}(E)^{2}+|b_{\mathbf{k}}(E)|^{2}}{2a_{\mathbf{k}}(E)(t_{\perp}+\delta t_{\mathbf{k}})^{2}},

\displaystyle\frac{\hbar}{2\tilde{\tau}_{\mathbf{k}}(E)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{sgn}(E+\mu)(E+\mu-\xi_{\mathbf{k}})\frac{|b_{\mathbf{k}}(E)|}{a_{\mathbf{k}}(E)},

(14)

in the region
|E+\mu|>E_{\mathbf{k}-}
. This resembles an interacting Green’s function where
Z_{\mathbf{k}}(E)
is the quasiparticle residue and
\tilde{\tau}_{\mathbf{k}}(E)
is the effective lifetime. At a Weyl node at
E=0
, we find
\tilde{\tau}_{\mathbf{k}}(0)=\hbar/4t_{\perp}
which shows up as the scattering time in the anomalous contribution upto pre-factors. Thus, the bulk states effectively induce an interacting 2D fluid on the surface near the Weyl nodes, leading to the additional anomalous contribution
\sigma_{\textrm{anom}}
on the surface.

Experiment.
Our main results, Eqs. (
1
), (
8
), and (
10
), imply that the surface of a WSM, in spite of being a metal, exhibits a nonmetallic signature in transport: the conductivity (resistivity) increases (decreases) with increase in
T
with a characteristic quadratic dependence. This can be studied experimentally to verify our theory. As an example we consider the commonly studied Weyl semimetal TaAs: for the W1 Weyl pockets, in-plane velocity
\approx 3\times 10^{5}\text{ m/s}
, out-of-plane velocity
\approx 3\times 10^{4}\text{ m/s}
,
\tau\sim 0.1\text{ps}
,
c\sim 10\AA
,
t_{\perp}\sim 0.01\text{eV}
, and
\nu_{F}\sim 0.002-0.02\text{ eV}^{-1}\AA^{-2}
, depending on the size of the FA
Huang et al. 2015b
;
Ramshaw et al. 2018
. This yields
\sigma_{\textrm{norm}}\sim 10^{-4}-10^{-3}\Omega^{-1}
and
\sigma_{\textrm{anom}}(T)-\sigma_{\textrm{anom}}(0)\sim 10^{-9}T^{2}\Omega^{-1}\text{K}^{-2}
, which evaluates to
\sim 10^{-7}\Omega^{-1}
for
T\sim 10^{1}\text{K}
. A change in resistance of
1
part in
10^{3}-10^{4}
is easily measurable, implying that our predicted effect is readily amenable to experimental verification; a similar estimate can be obtained for other Weyl materials. In arriving at this, however, we assumed

\tau
to be
T
-independent in Eq. (
8
). In reality, this is not true —
\tau
does depend on
T
and is, in fact, the chief source of
T
-dependence in the
\sigma_{\textrm{norm}}
, which can compete with the
T^{2}
-dependence in
\sigma_{\textrm{anom}}
. Nevertheless, below a certain temperature
T_{D}
whose exact value will depend on the microscopics, the
T
-dependence of
\sigma_{\text{surf}}
can be dominated by
\sigma_{\textrm{anom}}
even with a
T
-dependent
\tau
. For example, if one assumes the usual form of electron-phonon scattering with the scattering time given by
\hbar/\tau_{e-ph}\sim k_{B}T^{4}/T_{BG}^{3}
, (
T_{BG}
is the Bloch-Gruneisen temperature for the FA states), then
T_{D}\sim(\hbar k_{B}^{1/2}/\tau)(T_{BG}/t_{\perp})^{3/2}
. For
T_{BG}\sim 100\text{ K}
and
t_{\perp}\sim 0.01\text{ eV}
, one gets
T_{D}\sim 65\text{ K}
. On the other hand, below an even lower temperature, say
T_{Q}
, quantum corrections, not considered in this work, will become important. The predicted effect will, therefore, be observable for
T_{Q}\lesssim T\lesssim T_{D}
, and is illustrated in terms of resistivity in Fig.
3
.

Discussion.
The expression for the surface conductivity used in Eq. (
6
) considers electrons restricted to move along the top layer. Nevertheless, electrons on the surface can tunnel to other layers and reappear on the surface. Such processes also contribute to the surface conductivity, but have been ignored so far. Including these effects, however, does not change the qualitative results. As shown in SM
SM
, the
\tau
- and
T
-dependences of
\sigma_{\textrm{norm}}
and
\sigma_{\textrm{anom}}
remain unchanged at low temperature and doping. The only consequence of these extra terms is a renormalization of
W_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}
in
\sigma_{\textrm{norm}}
:
W_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}\rightarrow\tilde{W}_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}=W_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}/(1-W_{\mathbf{k}}^{2})
in Eq. (
7
) while
\sigma_{\textrm{anom}}
in Eq. (
10
) receives corrections that are suppressed by powers of
|\mu|/t_{\perp}
and
k_{B}T/t_{\perp}
.

Another aspect of our calculations that need scrutiny is that our starting model in Eq. (
15
) defines a WSM which assumes the bilayer basis in real space to be identical to the band basis in energy space. In general, this need not be true. However, in such cases, the bilayer basis and the low energy band basis can be related by a
\mathbf{k}
-dependent unitary transformation
U_{\mathbf{k}}
. In SM
SM
we show that
U_{\mathbf{k}}
does not modify the salient qualitative features
\sigma_{\textrm{surf}}
. This is essentially because
U_{\mathbf{k}}
does not change the analytic structure of
G_{\mathbf{k}}(E)
, which continues to retain poles along the FAs and BCs within the bulk bands.

Finally, we note that, while
\sigma_{\textrm{surf}}
stems from topological features such as the FAs and inseparability of the surface from the bulk, the response itself is non-topological as it does not descend from a topological response theory. Indeed, as shown in SM
SM
, an ordinary metal also acquires a
T^{2}
term in the surface conductivity. However, this term vanishes as the bulk Fermi surface shrinks to zero, in contrast to
\sigma_{\textrm{anom}}
which is non-zero at finite
T
even when the Weyl nodes are undoped. Additionally, the
\mu
and
T
dependences in ordinary metals are essentially unrelated whereas
\sigma_{\textrm{anom}}
only depends on the combination
\mu^{2}+k_{B}^{2}T^{2}\pi^{2}/3
according to (
10
). Both these properties stem from the innate linear dispersion of Weyl fermions. In this sense,
\sigma_{\textrm{surf}}
is the surface counterpart of the bulk longitudinal conductivity that stems from topological Weyl fermions but is itself non-topological
Burkov and Balents 2011
;
Hosur et al. 2012
. Nevertheless, it paves the way to search for other – possibly topological – surface responses to complement the extensively studied bulk topological responses originating from the Weyl nodes.

Conclusion.
We have shown that the dc surface conductivity of a WSM contains an anomalous contribution at nonzero temperature and doping in addition to the expected Drude contribution from the FAs. The novel contribution is manifestly non-Drude in character, being independent of the in-plane scattering time, stems from the intrinsic inseparability of the surface from the bulk, and dominates the temperature dependence at low temperatures. Moreover, its temperature and doping dependences are locked to each other by a universal, dimensionless ratio:
k_{B}^{2}\pi^{2}/3
. We argued that the bulk states when projected on the surface mimics a correlated liquid, and the anomalous conductivity can be understood as a response of this liquid.

II
Surface Green’s function

The Hamiltonian for a slab of a Weyl semimetal (WSM) used in the main text is

H_{\mathbf{k}}=\sum_{z=1}^{L}\psi_{z,\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}[(-1)^{z}\xi_{\mathbf{k}}-\mu]\psi_{z,\mathbf{k}}-\sum_{z=1}^{L-1}\psi_{z,\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}t_{z,\mathbf{k}}\psi_{z+1,\mathbf{k}}+\mathrm{H.c.},

(15)

where
\psi_{z,\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}
creates an electron with 2D momentum
\hbar\mathbf{k}
in layer
z
,
\xi_{\mathbf{k}}=\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}-\varepsilon_{F}^{2D}
denotes a metallic dispersion in each layer with a Fermi surface along
\xi_{\mathbf{k}}=0
,
\mu
is the bulk chemical potential and the interlayer hopping modulates as
t_{z,\mathbf{k}}=t_{\perp}+(-1)^{z}\delta t_{\mathbf{k}}
. In the bulk, it results in the 3D Bloch Hamiltonian and dispersion:

\displaystyle H_{\mathbf{k}}(k_{z})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mu+\xi_{\mathbf{k}}\sigma_{z}-\left[t_{\perp}-\delta t_{\mathbf{k}}+(t_{\perp}+\delta t_{\mathbf{k}})\cos(2k_{z}c)\right]\sigma_{x}+t_{\perp}\sin(2k_{z}c)\sigma_{y}

(16)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}^{\pm}(k_{z})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mu\pm\sqrt{\xi_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}+\left[t_{\perp}-\delta t_{\mathbf{k}}+(t_{\perp}+\delta t_{\mathbf{k}})\cos(2k_{z}c)\right]^{2}+t_{\perp}^{2}\sin^{2}(2k_{z}c)}

(17)

The Green’s function
\hat{G}_{L}(E,\mathbf{k})
is found by computing
(E-H_{\mathbf{k}})^{-1}
. Written explicitly in the layer basis,

\hat{G}_{L}(E,\mathbf{k})=\begin{pmatrix}E-\xi_{\mathbf{k}}-\mu&t_{\perp}-\delta t_{\mathbf{k}}&&&&&\\
t_{\perp}-\delta t_{\mathbf{k}}&E+\xi_{\mathbf{k}}-\mu&t_{\perp}+\delta t_{\mathbf{k}}&&&&\\
&t_{\perp}+\delta t_{\mathbf{k}}&E-\xi_{\mathbf{k}}-\mu&t_{\perp}-\delta t_{\mathbf{k}}&&&\\
&&t_{\perp}-\delta t_{\mathbf{k}}&E+\xi_{\mathbf{k}}-\mu&t_{\perp}+\delta t_{\mathbf{k}}&&\\
&&&&\ddots&&\end{pmatrix}^{-1}.

(18)

The above can be written in the form

\hat{G}_{L}(E,\mathbf{k})=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}X&Y\\
Y^{\dagger}&Z\end{array}\right)^{-1},

(19)

where

X=\begin{pmatrix}E-\xi_{\mathbf{k}}-\mu&t_{\perp}-\delta t_{\mathbf{k}}\\
t_{\perp}-\delta t_{\mathbf{k}}&E+\xi_{\mathbf{k}}-\mu\end{pmatrix};\ \ Y=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0&\cdots\\
t_{\perp}-\delta t_{\mathbf{k}}&0&0&\cdots\end{pmatrix};\ \ Z=\begin{pmatrix}E-\xi_{\mathbf{k}}-\mu&t_{\perp}-\delta t_{\mathbf{k}}&&&&&\\
t_{\perp}-\delta t_{\mathbf{k}}&E+\xi_{\mathbf{k}}-\mu&t_{\perp}+\delta t_{\mathbf{k}}&&&&\\
&t_{\perp}+\delta t_{\mathbf{k}}&\ddots&&&&\end{pmatrix}.

(20)

We now use the identity for block matrix inversion,

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}X&Y\\
Y^{\dagger}&Z\end{array}\right)^{-1}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}(X-YZ^{-1}Y^{\dagger})^{-1}&-(X-YZ^{-1}Y^{\dagger})^{-1}YZ^{-1}\\
-Z^{-1}Y^{\dagger}(X-YZ^{-1}Y^{\dagger})^{-1}&Z^{-1}+Z^{-1}Y^{\dagger}(X-YZ^{-1}Y^{\dagger})^{-1}YZ^{-1}\end{array}\right)

(21)

to calculate Eq. (
18
). Since we are interested in the surface Green’s function
G_{\mathbf{k}}(E)\equiv\hat{G}_{L}^{11}(E,\mathbf{k})
, Eq. (
45
) implies

\hat{G}_{L}^{11}(E,\mathbf{k})=[(X-YZ^{-1}Y^{\dagger})^{-1}]^{11}.

(22)

But
Z^{-1}\equiv\hat{G}_{L-2}(E,\mathbf{k})
. Using this in Eq. (
22
), and approximating
\hat{G}_{L}^{11}(E,\mathbf{k})=\hat{G}_{L-2}^{11}(E,\mathbf{k})
as
L\rightarrow\infty
, we find the expression for surface Green’s function for a semi-infinite Weyl semimetal:

\displaystyle G_{\mathbf{k}}(E)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{a_{\mathbf{k}}(E)+b_{\mathbf{k}}(E)}{2(t_{\perp}+\delta t_{\mathbf{k}})^{2}(E+\mu-\xi_{\mathbf{k}})},

(23)

\displaystyle a_{\mathbf{k}}(E)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(E+\mu)^{2}-\xi_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}+4t_{\perp}\delta t_{\mathbf{k}},

\displaystyle b_{\mathbf{k}}(E)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\left[(E+\mu)^{2}-\xi_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}-4t_{\perp}^{2}\right]\left[(E+\mu)^{2}-\xi_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}-4\delta t_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}\right]}.

This expression, in the Matsubara representation, has been quoted in the main text.

The above surface Green’s function is derived for a clean system. For transport, we need to find the equivalent expression in the presence of scattering. To incorporate such effects, we take a phenomenological route without getting into the microscopics. In the presence of scattering, energy eigenstates in each layer will gain a self-energy term. Since there are two kinds of layers, electron-like and hole-like, we introduce two kinds of self-energies. This is equivalent to making the following substitution in Eq. (
15
):
(-1)^{z}\xi_{\mathbf{k}}\rightarrow(-1)^{z}\xi_{\mathbf{k}}+\Sigma_{z}
, with
\Sigma_{z}=\Sigma_{e(h)}
for
z
even(odd). Going through the same steps as before, we find

\displaystyle G_{\mathbf{k}}(E)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{a_{\mathbf{k}}(E)+b_{\mathbf{k}}(E)}{2(t_{\perp}+\delta t_{\mathbf{k}})^{2}(E+\mu-\xi_{\mathbf{k}}+\Sigma_{e})},

(24)

\displaystyle a_{\mathbf{k}}(E)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(E+\mu+\frac{\Sigma_{e}+\Sigma_{h}}{2}\right)^{2}-\left(\xi_{\mathbf{k}}-\frac{\Sigma_{e}-\Sigma_{h}}{2}\right)^{2}+4t_{\perp}\delta t_{\mathbf{k}},

\displaystyle b_{\mathbf{k}}(E)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\left[\left(E+\mu+\frac{\Sigma_{e}+\Sigma_{h}}{2}\right)^{2}-\left(\xi_{\mathbf{k}}-\frac{\Sigma_{e}-\Sigma_{h}}{2}\right)^{2}-4t_{\perp}^{2}\right]\right.

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\left.\left[\left(E+\mu+\frac{\Sigma_{e}+\Sigma_{h}}{2}\right)^{2}-\left(\xi_{\mathbf{k}}-\frac{\Sigma_{e}-\Sigma_{h}}{2}\right)^{2}-4\delta t_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}\right]\right\}^{1/2}.

In the presence of weak scattering, all self-energy terms in the numerator of Eq. (
24
) can be neglected leaving only the one in the denominator. Rewriting
\mathrm{Im}[\Sigma_{e}]
as
(i\hbar/2\tau_{\mathbf{k}})\mathrm{sgn}(E+\mu)
and analytically continuing, one can get the retarded Green’s functions as usual. However, note that the Green’s function has branch cuts (BCs) in addition to poles: the former occur when
|E+\mu|\in[E_{\mathbf{k}-},E_{\mathbf{k}+}]
– the BC region – and the latter occur when
|E+\mu|\not\in[E_{\mathbf{k}-},E_{\mathbf{k}+}]
– the normal (N) region, where
E_{\mathbf{k}+}=\sqrt{\xi_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}+4t_{\perp}^{2}}=\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}(0)+\mu
and
E_{\mathbf{k}-}=\sqrt{\xi_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}+4\delta t_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}}=\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}(\pi/2c)+\mu
. Accordingly, analytic continuation gives rise to two kinds of expressions in the two regions:

\displaystyle G^{R}_{\mathbf{k}}(E)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{a_{\mathbf{k}}(E)+b_{\mathbf{k}}(E)}{2(t_{\perp}+\delta t_{\mathbf{k}})^{2}(E+\mu-\xi_{\mathbf{k}}+\frac{i\hbar}{2\tau_{\mathbf{k}}})},E\in N,

(25)

\displaystyle G^{R}_{\mathbf{k}}(E)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{a_{\mathbf{k}}(E)+i\ \mathrm{sgn}(E+\mu)|b_{\mathbf{k}}(E)|}{2(t_{\perp}+\delta t_{\mathbf{k}})^{2}(E+\mu-\xi_{\mathbf{k}}+\frac{i\hbar}{2\tau_{\mathbf{k}}})},E\in BC.

(26)

These have been quoted as (4) and (5) in the main text.

III
General expression for
\sigma_{\text{surf}}

In this section, we derive a general expression for
\sigma_{\text{surf}}

in terms of spectral functions that includes all possible interlayer
processes and is applicable to arbitrary tight-binding Hamiltonians.
We then rederive the results for
\sigma_{\text{norm}}
and
\sigma_{\text{anom}}

presented in the main paper in Sec.
IV
and analyze
corrections to these results in Sec.
V
.

According to the Kubo formula, the surface ac conductivity as a function
of Matsubara frequency is given by

\sigma(i\omega_{n})=e^{2}\frac{T}{\omega_{n}}\sum_{iE_{n}}\intop_{\mathbf{k}}\left[\hat{v}_{\mathbf{k},x}\hat{G}_{\mathbf{k}}(iE_{n})\hat{v}_{\mathbf{k},x}\hat{G}_{\mathbf{k}}(iE_{n}+i\hbar\omega_{n})\right]_{11}

(27)

where
\hat{v}_{\mathbf{k},x}
and
\hat{G}_{\mathbf{k}}(iE_{n})

are matrices in layer space,
\left[\dots\right]_{11}
denotes the

(1,1)
matrix element and the surface is defined to be the
z=1

layer. Evaluating the Matsubara sum via appropriate contour integrals
and analytically continuing to real frequencies,
i\omega_{n}\to\omega+i0^{+}
,
gives the real part of the conductivity

\text{Re}\sigma(\omega)=\frac{e^{2}\hbar}{4}\intop_{E,\mathbf{k}}\frac{f(E-\hbar\omega/2)-f(E+\hbar\omega/2)}{\omega}\left[\hat{v}_{\mathbf{k},x}\hat{A}_{\mathbf{k}}(E-\hbar\omega/2)\hat{v}_{\mathbf{k},x}\hat{A}_{\mathbf{k}}(E+\hbar\omega/2)+\textrm{h.c.}\right]_{11}

(28)

where
\hat{A}_{\mathbf{k}}(E)=-i\left[\hat{G}_{\mathbf{k}}(E+i0^{+})-\hat{G}_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}(E+i0^{+})\right]

is the spectral function matrix in layer space and we have dropped
a term
\propto\delta(\omega)
that implies a static limit. In the
physical dc limit,
\omega\to 0
, we get

\sigma_{\text{surf}}^{\text{tot}}=\text{Re}\sigma(0)=\frac{e^{2}\hbar}{4}\intop_{E,\mathbf{k}}\left(-\frac{\partial f}{\partial E}\right)\left[\hat{v}_{\mathbf{k},x}\hat{A}_{\mathbf{k}}(E)\hat{v}_{\mathbf{k},x}\hat{A}_{\mathbf{k}}(E)+\textrm{h.c.}\right]_{11}

(29)

The factor
-\partial_{E}f
above restricts the integrand to the
regime
|E|\lesssim T
; the contributions from larger
|E|
decay
exponentially since
-\partial_{E}f\approx T^{-1}e^{-|E|/T}\text{ for }|E|\gtrsim T
.
Physically, this is the statement that longitudinal dc transport on
WSM surfaces, just like in ordinary metals, is dominated by bands
near the Fermi level with exponentially small corrections from higher
bands. In the rest of the discussion, we will assume
|E|\lesssim T
.

IV
Derivation of main results

Now, we focus on the layering model described in Eq. (2) and restrict
to the
(1,1)
matrix elements of both
\hat{v}_{\mathbf{k},x}

and
\hat{A}_{\mathbf{k}}(E)
. In the main paper, we denoted
v_{\mathbf{k},x}^{11}=\frac{1}{\hbar}\partial_{k_{x}}\xi_{\mathbf{k}}

and
A_{\mathbf{k}}^{11}(E)
as simply
v_{\mathbf{k},x}
and
A_{\mathbf{k}}(E)
,
respectively. Explicitly,

\displaystyle A_{\mathbf{k}}(E)

\displaystyle=2\pi\delta_{\hbar/2\tau_{\mathbf{k}}}(E+\mu-\xi_{\mathbf{k}})W_{\mathbf{k}}+\frac{\text{sgn}(E+\mu)\sqrt{R\left[\left((E+\mu)^{2}-E_{\mathbf{k}-}^{2}\right)\left(E_{\mathbf{k}+}^{2}-(E+\mu)^{2}\right)\right]}}{(t_{\perp}+\delta t_{\mathbf{k}})^{2}(E+\mu-\xi_{\mathbf{k}})}

\displaystyle\equiv A_{\text{norm},\mathbf{k}}(E)+A_{\text{anom},\mathbf{k}}(E)

(30)

in the limit of small
\hbar/2\tau_{\mathbf{k}}
, where
\delta_{\hbar/2\tau}(E)=\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{\hbar/2\tau}{E^{2}+(\hbar/2\tau)^{2}}
.
Clearly,
A_{\text{norm},\mathbf{k}}(E)
captures quasiparticle poles
at the Fermi arc and is non-vanishing only in this region, while
A_{\text{anom},\mathbf{k}}(E)

is non-zero only when
E\in BC
. These matrix elements yield the
contributions to
\sigma_{\text{surf}}^{\text{tot}}
from processes
on the top layer only, which we denoted
\sigma_{\text{surf}}
in
the main paper.
\sigma_{\text{surf}}
then decomposes as

\displaystyle\sigma_{\text{surf}}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{2}\hbar}{2}\intop_{E,\mathbf{k}}\left(-\frac{\partial f}{\partial E}\right)v_{\mathbf{k},x}^{2}\left[A_{\text{norm},\mathbf{k}}^{2}(E)+2A_{\text{norm},\mathbf{k}}(E)A_{\text{anom},\mathbf{k}}(E)+A_{\text{anom},\mathbf{k}}^{2}(E)\right]

\displaystyle\equiv\sigma_{\text{norm}}+\sigma_{\text{cross}}+\sigma_{\text{anom}}

(31)

Since the pole and one branch point coincide precisely at the Weyl
nodes, but the branch cuts are separated in energy from the poles
everywhere else,
\sigma_{\text{cross}}=e^{2}\hbar\intop_{E,\mathbf{k}}\left(-\frac{\partial f}{\partial E}\right)v_{\mathbf{k},x}^{2}A_{\text{norm},\mathbf{k}}(E)A_{\text{anom},\mathbf{k}}(E)

vanishes when
1/\tau_{\mathbf{k}}=0
. For non-zero
1/\tau_{\mathbf{k}}
,
the leading
\tau
-dependence is determined by the broadening of
the pole into the branch cut region and is expected to be subleading,

\leq O(1/\tau)
. Thus, neglecting
\sigma_{\text{cross}}
,
\sigma_{\text{surf}}

decomposes into
\sigma_{\text{norm}}
and
\sigma_{\text{anom}}

as stated in Eq. (1). There, we used an alternate argument for such
a decomposition, namely, by splitting the energy integral as
\intop_{E}=\intop_{N}+\intop_{BC}
.

IV.1
\sigma_{\text{norm}}

The explicit expression for
\sigma_{\text{norm}}
according to (
31
)
is

\sigma_{\text{norm}}=\frac{e^{2}\hbar}{2}\intop_{E,\mathbf{k}}\left(-\frac{\partial f}{\partial E}\right)v_{\mathbf{k},x}^{2}A_{\text{norm},\mathbf{k}}^{2}(E)

(32)

Writing
A_{\text{norm},\mathbf{k}}^{2}(E)=2\pi W_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}\delta(E+\mu-\xi_{\mathbf{k}})\times 2\pi\delta_{\hbar/2\tau}(0)=4\pi W_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}\tau_{\mathbf{k}}\delta(E+\mu-\xi_{\mathbf{k}})/\hbar
,
we recover Eq. (7).

IV.2
\sigma_{\text{anom}}

From (
31
),
\sigma_{\text{anom}}
is given
by

\displaystyle\sigma_{\text{anom}}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{2}\hbar}{2}\intop_{E,\mathbf{k}}\left(-\frac{\partial f}{\partial E}\right)v_{\mathbf{k},x}^{2}A_{\text{anom},\mathbf{k}}^{2}(E)

(33)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{2}\hbar}{2}\intop_{E\in BC}\intop_{\mathbf{k}}\left(-\frac{\partial f}{\partial E}\right)v_{\mathbf{k},x}^{2}\frac{\left((E+\mu)^{2}-E_{\mathbf{k}-}^{2}\right)\left(E_{\mathbf{k}+}^{2}-(E+\mu)^{2}\right)}{(t_{\perp}+\delta t_{\mathbf{k}})^{4}(E+\mu-\xi_{\mathbf{k}})^{2}}

(34)

As argued in the main paper, the combination
\intop_{E\in BC}\partial_{E}f

is negligible far from the Weyl nodes. Linearizing around the nodes
as

\xi_{\mathbf{k}}\approx\hbar\mathbf{v}_{j}\cdot\mathbf{q}_{2D}\,,\,\delta t_{\mathbf{k}}\approx\frac{\hbar}{2}\mathbf{u}_{j}\cdot\mathbf{q}_{2D}

(35)

and reversing the order of the
E
and momentum integrals gives

\sigma_{\text{anom}}=2e^{2}\hbar\sum_{j}\frac{v_{j,x}^{2}}{t_{\perp}^{2}}\intop_{E}\left(-\frac{\partial f}{\partial E}\right)\intop_{\hbar\sqrt{(\mathbf{v}_{j}\cdot\mathbf{q}_{2D})^{2}+(\mathbf{u}_{j}\cdot\mathbf{q}_{2D})^{2}}<|E+\mu|}\frac{d^{2}q}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{(E+\mu)^{2}-\hbar^{2}(\mathbf{v}_{j}\cdot\mathbf{q}_{2D})^{2}-\hbar^{2}(\mathbf{u}_{j}\cdot\mathbf{q}_{2D})^{2}}{(E+\mu-\hbar\mathbf{v}_{j}\cdot\mathbf{q}_{2D})^{2}}

(36)

Note,
\mathbf{v}_{j}
and
\mathbf{u}_{j}
are not necessarily
orthogonal. To simplify the above integrals, we define
q_{\parallel}

and
q_{\perp}
as the components of
\mathbf{q}_{2D}
parallel
and perpendicular to
\mathbf{v}_{j}
, respectively, and perform
suitable rotation and scaling transformations:

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}Q\cos\phi\\
Q\sin\phi\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}V_{j}&0\\
0&U_{j}\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\cos\theta_{j}&\sin\theta_{j}\\
-\sin\theta_{j}&\cos\theta_{j}\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}q_{\parallel}\\
q_{\perp}\end{array}\right)

(37)

so that
(\mathbf{v}_{j}\cdot\mathbf{q}_{2D})^{2}+(\mathbf{u}_{j}\cdot\mathbf{q}_{2D})^{2}=Q^{2}
.
These transformations imply the following relations that we will need
shortly:

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\frac{1}{v_{j}}=\left|\frac{\cos\theta_{j}}{V_{j}}+i\frac{\sin\theta_{j}}{U_{j}}\right|\,,\,U_{j}V_{j}=|\mathbf{v}_{j}\times\mathbf{u}_{j}|

(38)

We now get

\displaystyle\sigma_{\text{anom}}

\displaystyle=2e^{2}\hbar\sum_{j}\frac{v_{j,x}^{2}}{t_{\perp}^{2}}\intop_{E}\left(-\frac{\partial f}{\partial E}\right)\intop_{\hbar Q<|E+\mu|}\frac{d^{2}Q}{(2\pi)^{2}U_{j}V_{j}}\frac{(E+\mu)^{2}-\hbar^{2}Q^{2}}{[E+\mu-\hbar v_{j}Q(\cos\phi\cos\theta_{j}/V_{j}+\sin\phi\sin\theta_{j}/U_{j})]^{2}}

(39)

The
\phi
-integral is most easily performed via contour methods.
Defining
\zeta=e^{i\phi}
,
\beta=(E+\mu)/\hbar Q
and
e^{i\alpha}=v_{j}\left(\frac{\cos\theta_{j}}{V_{j}}-i\frac{\sin\theta_{j}}{U_{j}}\right)
,

\displaystyle\sigma_{\text{anom}}

\displaystyle=2e^{2}\hbar\sum_{j}\frac{v_{j,x}^{2}}{t_{\perp}^{2}}\intop_{E}\left(-\frac{\partial f}{\partial E}\right)\intop_{\hbar Q<|E+\mu|}\frac{QdQ}{(2\pi)^{2}iU_{j}V_{j}}\oint\frac{4(\beta^{2}-1)\zeta d\zeta}{\left(2\beta\zeta-\left[\zeta^{2}e^{i\alpha}+e^{-i\alpha}\right]\right)^{2}}

(40)

The poles of the
\zeta
-integrand in both expressions are at

\zeta_{\pm}=e^{-i\alpha}\left(\beta\pm\sqrt{\beta^{2}-1}\right)

(41)

of which exactly one is within the unit circle in the complex plane.
Integrating over the unit circle now gives

\displaystyle\sigma_{\text{anom}}

\displaystyle=e^{2}\hbar\sum_{j}\frac{v_{j,x}^{2}}{t_{\perp}^{2}}\intop_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dE}{2\pi}\left(-\frac{\partial f}{\partial E}\right)\intop_{\hbar Q<|E+\mu|}\frac{QdQ}{2\pi U_{j}V_{j}}\frac{2|\beta|}{\sqrt{\beta^{2}-1}}

(42)

The
Q
and
E
integrals are trivial, and yield

\sigma_{\text{anom}}=\frac{e^{2}}{\hbar\pi^{2}}\sum_{j}\frac{v_{j,x}^{2}}{2t_{\perp}^{2}|\mathbf{u}_{j}\times\mathbf{v}_{j}|}\left(\mu^{2}+\frac{T^{2}\pi^{2}}{3}\right)

(43)

where we have used a Sommerfeld expansion for
f(E)
and the relation

U_{j}V_{j}=|\mathbf{u}_{j}\times\mathbf{v}_{j}|
. Recalling that

\mathbf{w}_{j}=-\frac{2}{\hbar}t_{\perp}c\hat{\mathbf{z}}
gives
Eq. (10).

Other matrix elements of

p\times p
block of
\hat{G}_{N}(E)
and use the
identity for block matrix inversion

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}X&Y\\
Y^{\dagger}&Z\end{array}\right)^{-1}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}(X-YZ^{-1}Y^{\dagger})^{-1}&-(X-YZ^{-1}Y^{\dagger})^{-1}YZ^{-1}\\
-Z^{-1}Y^{\dagger}(X-YZ^{-1}Y^{\dagger})^{-1}&Z^{-1}+Z^{-1}Y^{\dagger}(X-YZ^{-1}Y^{\dagger})^{-1}YZ^{-1}\end{array}\right)

(45)

with
X=E-H_{p}^{0}
,
Y=\left(H_{p}^{-},0,0,\dots\right)
,
Z=E-H_{N}^{0}
.
The triangularity of
H_{p}^{\pm}
ensures that
\hat{G}_{N+p,p}(E)

depends only on
\hat{G}_{N,p}(E)
, i.e., it does not depend on the
elements of
\hat{G}_{N}(E)
outside the top-left
p\times p
block.
In particular,

\hat{G}_{N+p,p}(E)=\left(E-H_{p}^{0}-H_{p}^{-}G_{N,p}(E)H_{p}^{+}\right)^{-1}

(46)

For
N\gg p
, we expect
\hat{G}_{N+p,p}(E)\approx\hat{G}_{N,p}(E)
,
which gives the self-consistent equation

\hat{G}_{N,p}^{-1}(E)=E-H_{p}^{0}-H_{p}^{-}G_{N,p}(E)H_{p}^{+}

(47)

Focusing on the layering model in Eq. (2), choosing
p=2
(one bilayer
unit cell) and
N=L-2
gives a
2\times 2
symmetric matrix Green’s
function for the surface unit cell,
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{k}}(E)
,
where we have reinstated the
\mathbf{k}
and
E
dependences. In
the main paper, we have denoted
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{k}}^{11}(E)\equiv G_{\mathbf{k}}(E)
.
Some algebra on (
47
) gives the other matrix elements of

\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{k}}(E)
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{k}}^{12}(E)\equiv G_{\mathbf{k}}^{12}(E)

\displaystyle=-\frac{(E+\mu)^{2}-\xi_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}-4t_{\perp}^{2}-4\delta t_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}+b_{\mathbf{k}}(E)}{2(t_{\perp}-\delta t_{\mathbf{k}})(t_{\perp}+\delta t_{\mathbf{k}})^{2}}

(48)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{k}}^{22}(E)\equiv G_{\mathbf{k}}^{22}(E)

\displaystyle=-\frac{E+\mu-\xi_{\mathbf{k}}}{t_{\perp}-\delta t_{\mathbf{k}}}\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{k}}^{12}(E)

(49)

In the presence of a non-zero
\tau_{\mathbf{k}}
, we must ideally
replace
E\to E\pm i\hbar/2\tau_{\mathbf{k}}
for the retarded and
advanced parts of the Green’s functions. However, we see that
G_{\mathbf{k}}^{12}(E)

and
G_{\mathbf{k}}^{22}(E)
have no poles and we can safely set

1/\tau_{\mathbf{k}}=0
to leading order. On the other hand, these
matrix elements develop branch cuts along
|E+\mu|\in(E_{\mathbf{k}-},E_{\mathbf{k}+})
,
just as
G_{\mathbf{k}}(E)
does, when
b_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}(E)<0
.
In fact, these matrix elements are purely real except on the branch
cuts. The spectral functions matrix elements
A_{\mathbf{k}}^{zz^{\prime}}(E)=-2\text{Im}\left(G_{\mathbf{k}}^{R}(E)\right)^{zz^{\prime}}

are given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\mathbf{k}}^{12}(E)\equiv A_{\mathbf{k}}^{12}(E)

\displaystyle=-\frac{E+\mu-\xi_{\mathbf{k}}}{t_{\perp}-\delta t_{\mathbf{k}}}A_{\text{anom},\mathbf{k}}

(50)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\mathbf{k}}^{22}(E)\equiv A_{\mathbf{k}}^{22}(E)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{E+\mu-\xi_{\mathbf{k}}}{t_{\perp}-\delta t_{\mathbf{k}}}\right)^{2}A_{\text{anom},\mathbf{k}}

(51)

Assuming
|E|\lesssim T
,
(T,|\mu|)\ll|t_{\perp}|
, and the absence
of fine-tuned regions away from the Weyl nodes with
|\delta t_{\mathbf{k}}|\lesssim(T,|\mu|)
,
this means

\left|\mathcal{A}_{\mathbf{k}}^{22}(E)\right|\ll\left|\mathcal{A}_{\mathbf{k}}^{12}(E)\right|\ll\left|A_{\text{anom},\mathbf{k}}(E)\right|\ll\left|A_{\text{norm},\mathbf{k}}(E)\right|

(52)

The off diagonal block of (
45
) yields
a recursion relation for the off-diagonal matrix elements of
\hat{G}

in the first two rows and columns:

\displaystyle G_{\mathbf{k}}^{1z}(E)

\displaystyle=G_{\mathbf{k}}^{z1}(E)=\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{k}}^{12}(E)(t_{\perp}+\delta t_{\mathbf{k}})G_{\mathbf{k}}^{1,z-2}(E)\,;\,z\geq 3

(53)

\displaystyle G_{\mathbf{k}}^{2z}(E)

\displaystyle=G_{\mathbf{k}}^{z2}(E)=\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{k}}^{22}(E)(t_{\perp}-\delta t_{\mathbf{k}})G_{\mathbf{k}}^{1,z-2}(E)\,;\,z\geq 3

(54)

The recursion relations yield the closed form expressions

\displaystyle G_{\mathbf{k}}^{1z}(E)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\left[\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{k}}^{12}(E)(t_{\perp}+\delta t_{\mathbf{k}})\right]^{(z-1)/2}G_{\mathbf{k}}(E)&z\in\text{odd}\\
\left[\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{k}}^{12}(E)(t_{\perp}+\delta t_{\mathbf{k}})\right]^{z/2-1}\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{k}}^{12}(E)&z\in\text{even}\end{cases}

(55)

\displaystyle G_{\mathbf{k}}^{2z}(E)

\displaystyle=-\frac{E+\mu-\xi_{\mathbf{k}}}{t_{\perp}-\delta t_{\mathbf{k}}}G_{\mathbf{k}}^{1z}(E)\,\,z\geq 2

(56)

Now,
G_{\mathbf{k}}^{1z}(E)
inherits a simple pole from
G_{\mathbf{k}}(E)

for odd
z\geq 3
but lacks poles for even
z
, while
G_{\mathbf{k}}^{2z}(E)

lacks poles for any
z
. Using (
55
), (4) and (5), the
corresponding matrix elements of
\hat{A}_{\mathbf{k}}(E)
are

\displaystyle A_{\mathbf{k}}^{1z}(E)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}-2\pi\left(-W_{\mathbf{k}}\right)^{(z+1)/2}\delta_{\hbar/2\tau_{\mathbf{k}}}(E+\mu-\xi_{\mathbf{k}})&z\in\text{odd};E\in N\\
0&z\in\text{even};E\in N\\
-\frac{z+1}{E+\mu-\xi_{\mathbf{k}}}\frac{(2\delta t_{\mathbf{k}})^{(z-1)/2}\text{sgn}(E+\mu)\sqrt{(E+\mu)^{2}-E_{\mathbf{k}-}^{2}}}{(-t_{\perp})^{(z+1)/2}}&z\in\text{odd};E\in BC\\
\frac{z+1}{t_{\perp}}\frac{(2\delta t_{\mathbf{k}})^{z/2-1}\text{sgn}(E+\mu)\sqrt{(E+\mu)^{2}-E_{\mathbf{k}-}^{2}}}{(-t_{\perp})^{z/2}}&z\in\text{even};E\in BC\end{cases}

(57)

\displaystyle A_{\mathbf{k}}^{2z}(E)

\displaystyle=-\frac{E+\mu-\xi_{\mathbf{k}}}{t_{\perp}-\delta t_{\mathbf{k}}}A_{\mathbf{k}}^{1z}(E)\,\,z=2,3,4\dots;\text{ any }E

(58)

V.3
Corrections due to unitary transformations

The 3D Bloch Hamiltonian generated by Eq. (2) is

H_{3D}(\mathbf{k},k_{z})=\xi_{\mathbf{k}}\sigma_{z}-\left[(t_{\perp}+\delta t_{\mathbf{k}})+(t_{\perp}-\delta t_{\mathbf{k}})\cos(2k_{z}c)\right]\sigma_{x}+(t_{\perp}+\delta t_{\mathbf{k}})\sin(2k_{z}c)\sigma_{y}

(74)

where
\sigma_{i}
are Pauli matrices in the bilayer basis and
\sigma_{z}=\pm 1

correspond to the two layers within a unit cell. At
\delta t_{\mathbf{k}}=0
,

H_{3D}(\mathbf{k},k_{z})
realizes a nodal line semimetal with a
line node along
\xi_{\mathbf{k}}=0
at
k_{z}=\pi/2c
. In the nodal
plane,
k_{z}=2\pi/c
, the Bloch Hamiltonian reduces to a diagonal
form,
H_{3D}(\mathbf{k},k_{z})=\xi_{\mathbf{k}}\sigma_{z}
. In other
words, the two bands that intersect along the line node live on different
layers in the nodal plane, which results in the top layer being purely
hole-like. Note, this conclusion does not require the material to
be layered or quasi-2D; it holds even when
t_{\perp}
is of the
same order or even larger than the typical scale of
\xi_{\mathbf{k}}

(denoted
t_{\parallel}
in the main text).

More generally, the low energy band basis in the bulk would be related
to the bilayer basis by a unitary transformation
U_{\mathbf{k}}
.
Then, the surface conductivity due to motion of electrons on the top
layer is given by

\tilde{\sigma}_{\text{surf}}=\frac{e^{2}\hbar}{2}\intop_{\mathbf{k},E}\left(\tilde{v}_{\mathbf{k},x}^{11}\tilde{A}_{\mathbf{k},x}^{11}\right)^{2}

(75)

where
\tilde{v}_{\mathbf{k},x}^{11}=\frac{1}{\hbar}\partial_{k_{x}}\left(U_{\mathbf{k}}\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{k}}U_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\right)_{11}
,

\tilde{A}_{\mathbf{k}}^{11}(E)=-2\text{Im}\left[U_{\mathbf{k}}\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{k}}^{R}(E)U_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\right]_{11}

and
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{k}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}-\xi_{\mathbf{k}}-\mu&-t_{\perp}+\delta t_{\mathbf{k}}\\
-t_{\perp}+\delta t_{\mathbf{k}}&\xi_{\mathbf{k}}-\mu\end{array}\right)
is the Hamiltonian of the topmost bilayer in Eq. (2). Parameterizing

U_{\mathbf{k}}=\exp\left(i\boldsymbol{\sigma}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{\mathbf{k}}\theta_{\mathbf{k}}\right)

gives

\displaystyle\tilde{v}_{\mathbf{k},x}^{11}

\displaystyle=-v_{\mathbf{k},x}\left(\cos 2\theta+2\sin^{2}\theta n_{z}^{2}\right)-\frac{\xi_{\mathbf{k}}}{\hbar}\frac{\partial}{\partial k_{x}}\left(\cos 2\theta+2\sin^{2}\theta n_{z}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial}{\partial k_{x}}\left[(t_{\perp}-\delta t_{\mathbf{k}})\left(n_{y}\sin 2\theta+2n_{z}n_{x}\sin^{2}\theta\right)\right]

\displaystyle\equiv-v_{\mathbf{k},x}^{\text{eff}}

(76)

and

\displaystyle\tilde{A}_{\mathbf{k}}^{11}(E)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}_{\mathbf{k}}^{11}(E)(\cos\theta_{\mathbf{k}}+n_{\mathbf{k},z}^{2}\sin^{2}\theta_{\mathbf{k}})

\displaystyle+\mathcal{A}_{\mathbf{k}}^{22}(E)\sin\theta_{\mathbf{k}}(1-n_{\mathbf{k},z}^{2}\sin\theta_{\mathbf{k}})-\mathcal{A}_{\mathbf{k}}^{12}(E)\left(2n_{\mathbf{k},y}\sin 2\theta_{\mathbf{k}}+n_{\mathbf{k},x}n_{\mathbf{k},z}\sin^{2}\theta_{\mathbf{k}}\right)

\displaystyle\approx A_{\mathbf{k}}(E)(\cos\theta_{\mathbf{k}}+n_{\mathbf{k},z}^{2}\sin^{2}\theta_{\mathbf{k}})

(77)

\displaystyle\equiv A_{\mathbf{k}}^{\text{eff}}(E)

(78)

using (
52
). While
v_{\mathbf{k},x}^{\text{eff}}

differs from
v_{\mathbf{k},x}
by an
O(1)
factor as well as additive
corrections,
A_{\mathbf{k}}(E)
merely acquires an
O(1)
prefactor
due to the unitary transformation. Crucially, the qualitative results,
namely, the decomposition of
\sigma_{\text{surf}}
into
\sigma_{\text{norm}}

and
\sigma_{\text{anom}}
and their
T
,
\tau
and
\mu
dependences,
are determined governed by
A_{\mathbf{k}}(E)
while
v_{\mathbf{k},x}(E)

only affects the quantitative behavior. Thus,
U_{\mathbf{k}}
is
expected to leave the qualitative results unchanged.

VI
Comparison with ordinary metals

In this section, we calculate the surface conductivity of an ordinary metal and compare it with the results obtained for a WSM. Our approach is similar to that used for the WSM. We start with a minimal tight-binding model for an ordinary metal:

H_{\text{metal}}=\sum_{z}(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}-\mu)\psi_{z,\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\psi_{z,\mathbf{k}}-\sum_{z}t_{\perp}\left(\psi_{z,\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\psi_{z+1,\mathbf{k}}+\text{h.c.}\right),

(79)

which yields the 3D Bloch Hamiltonian
H_{\mathbf{k}}=\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}-2t_{\perp}\cos k_{z}-\mu

in the bulk with a Fermi surface defined by
\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}-2t_{\perp}\cos k_{z}=\mu
.
Solving Eq. (
47
) in the limit
N\to\infty
with
p=1
,

H_{p}^{0}=\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}-\mu
and
H_{p}^{\pm}=-t_{\perp}
,
and selecting the imaginary part at complex energy
E+i\hbar/2\tau_{\mathbf{k}}

gives the surface spectral function,

A_{\mathbf{k}}^{\text{triv}}(E)=\frac{\text{sgn}(E+\mu-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}})\sqrt{R\left[4t_{\perp}^{2}-(E+\mu-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}})^{2}\right]}}{t_{\perp}^{2}}

(80)

In writing
A_{\mathbf{k}}^{\text{triv}}(E)
, we have taken the limit

\hbar/2\tau_{\mathbf{k}}\to 0
because the surface Green’s function
lacks poles. Its only non-analyticities are square root branch cuts
when
-2|t_{\perp}|<E+\mu-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}<2|t_{\perp}|
,
i.e., when
E
lies within the bulk bands. According to (
29
),
the leading term in the corresponding surface conductivity is

\displaystyle\sigma_{\text{surf}}^{\text{triv}}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{2}}{2\hbar}\intop\frac{dE}{2\pi}\left(-\frac{\partial f}{\partial E}\right)\intop_{\mathbf{k}}\left(\frac{\partial\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}}{\partial k_{x}}\right)^{2}\frac{R\left[4t_{\perp}^{2}-(E+\mu-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}})^{2}\right]}{t_{\perp}^{4}}

(81)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{2}\hbar}{4\pi t_{\perp}^{4}}\intop_{\mathbf{k}}v_{\mathbf{k},x}^{2}\left(\left[4t_{\perp}^{2}-(\mu-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}})^{2}\right]-\frac{T^{2}\pi^{2}}{6}\right)\Theta\left[4t_{\perp}^{2}-(\mu-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}})^{2}\right]

(82)

where
v_{\mathbf{k},x}=\frac{1}{\hbar}\partial_{k_{x}}\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}

and we have performed a Sommerfeld expansion for
f(E)
. If there
are multiple trivial Fermi surfaces, each will contribute additively
to
\sigma_{\text{surf}}^{\text{triv}}
. The temperature dependent
part,
\Delta\sigma_{\text{surf}}^{\text{triv}}(T)\equiv\sigma_{\text{surf}}^{\text{triv}}(T)-\sigma_{\text{surf}}^{\text{triv}}(0)

is given by

\Delta\sigma_{\text{surf}}^{\text{triv}}(T)=-\frac{e^{2}\hbar}{96\pi}\frac{T^{2}\left\langle v_{x}^{2}\right\rangle_{FS}A_{FS}}{t_{\perp}^{4}}

(83)

where
A_{FS}
is the area of the surface projection of the Fermi
surface and
\left\langle v_{x}^{2}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{A_{FS}}\int d^{2}kv_{\mathbf{k},x}^{2}

is the mean square velocity in this area.

Thus, a trivial Fermi surface also produces a
\tau
-independent
surface conductivity whose leading
T
dependence at low temperatures
is
\sim T^{2}
. However, the surface conductivity due to a trivial
Fermi surface is proportional to its cross-sectional area, so it vanishes
if the Fermi pocket is emptied. Physically, the dependence on
A_{FS}

indicates that
\Delta\sigma_{\text{surf}}^{\text{triv}}(T)
is a
surface manifestation of quasiparticle excitations above the bulk
Fermi surface. The
\mu
-dependence of
\sigma_{\text{surf}}^{\text{triv}}(T)

is rather non-universal and depends on band structure details. In
contrast, (i) the
T
-dependent part of
\sigma_{\text{anom}}
in
WSMs, namely,
\Delta\sigma_{\text{anom}}(T)=\frac{e^{2}}{6\hbar}\sum_{j}\frac{T^{2}v_{j,x}^{2}}{t_{\perp}^{2}|\mathbf{u}_{j}\times\mathbf{v}_{j}|}
,
is independent of doping around the Weyl node and persists even as
the Fermi surface around a Weyl node shrinks to a point, and (ii)

\sigma_{\text{anom}}
only depends on a particular combination of

T
and
\mu
, namely,
\mu^{2}+T^{2}\pi^{2}/3
.
Both these features of
\sigma_{\text{anom}}
are consequences
of the linear dispersion that is innate to a Weyl node.
