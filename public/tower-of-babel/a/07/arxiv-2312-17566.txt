Abstract

Establishing the frequentist properties of Bayesian approaches widens their appeal and offers new understanding. In hypothesis testing, Bayesian model averaging addresses the problem that conclusions are sensitive to variable selection. But Bayesian false discovery rate (FDR) guarantees are sensitive to subjective prior assumptions. Here we show that Bayesian model-averaged hypothesis testing is a closed testing procedure that controls the frequentist familywise error rate (FWER) in the strong sense. To quantify the FWER, we use the theory of regular variation and likelihood asymptotics to derive a chi-squared tail approximation for the model-averaged posterior odds. Convergence is pointwise as the sample size grows and, in a simplified setting subject to a minimum effect size assumption, uniform. The ‘Doublethink’ method computes simultaneous posterior odds and asymptotic
p
-values for model-averaged hypothesis testing. We explore Doublethink through a Mendelian randomization study and simulations, comparing to approaches like LASSO, stepwise regression, the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure, the harmonic mean
p
-value and
e
-values. We consider the limitations of the approach, including finite-sample inflation, and mitigations, like testing groups of correlated variables. We discuss the benefits of Doublethink, including post-hoc variable selection, and its wider implications for the theory and practice of hypothesis testing.

Keywords
: Bayesian model averaging, closed testing procedure, false discovery rate, familywise error rate, hypothesis tests, post-hoc variable selection

Introduction

Hypothesis testing is a fundamental scientific approach for identifying variables that affect an outcome of interest
(
53
;
18
;
50
)
.
In observational studies, however, the statistical evidence that one variable directly affects an outcome typically depends on which other variables are included in the model, because of confounding and mediation
(
68
;
52
)
. Uncertainty in model selection can, therefore, strongly influence conclusions and should be accounted for, particularly in big data settings featuring thousands of variables. Bayesian model averaging provides a solution
(
56
)
, but in many settings Bayes has not become mainstream
(
26
)
.

The advantages of Bayesian methods, like model-averaging and interpretability, are counterbalanced by various concerns
(
21
)
, chiefly the sensitivity to debatable prior assumptions. Whereas Bernstein-von Mises theorem
(
9
)
shows that Bayesian and frequentist parameter estimates and confidence regions can converge for large, informative samples, the influence of the prior on hypothesis tests remains uncomfortably direct. The posterior odds of one hypothesis versus another can be doubled by simply doubling the prior odds. Bayes factors are similarly manipulable through the prior on effect sizes
(
20
)
. This is partly a problem of scale: in large samples, the prior contributes only a constant term to the log posterior odds and gets overwhelmed, and indeed, Bayesian hypothesis testing is consistent by Doob’s theorem
(
14
;
35
;
51
)
. Yet, even in large samples, priors influence testing at the boundary of statistical significance, which can lead different researchers to different conclusions. Likewise, Bayesian false discovery rate (FDR) guarantees are contingent on the prior, which may be disputed.

Establishing the frequentist properties of Bayesian tests can broaden their appeal and afford new insights
(
2
)
. There is long-standing interest in bridging this Bayesian-frequentist divide
(
25
)
.
Formalizing earlier ideas expressed by
24
,
60
introduced methods that convert
p
-values to maximum Bayes factors and, by implication, Bayes factors to minimum
p
-values.
79
derived the null distribution of Bayes factors in linear regression, enabling direct interconversion of Bayesian and frequentist measures of evidence.
For models in which chi-squared statistics are sufficient, Johnson’s
(
31
;
32
)
approach (see also
72
;
29
) enabled one-to-one interconversion between Bayes factors and
p
-values via the maximized likelihood ratio (MLR), similarly to the Bayesian information criterion (BIC;
59
), to which it converges.

70
developed conversion of Bayes factors involving a simple null and a possibly model-averaged alternative hypothesis (
e
-values) to maximum
p
-values, together with a closed testing procedure (CTP;
43
) to control the familywise error rate (FWER). FWER is considered standard control in frequentist multiple testing
(
67
)
.
23
developed general
p
-value-based CTPs for FWER control that allow multilevel testing, meaning that thresholds for arbitrary combinations of hypotheses are pre-determined and significant groups of variables can be freely identified post-hoc
(
45
;
22
)
.

In this paper, we investigate the frequentist properties of Bayesian model-averaged hypothesis tests involving nested models, with model uncertainty in both the null and alternative hypotheses. Perhaps surprisingly, we show that Bayesian tests are CTPs that control both the Bayesian FDR and the frequentist FWER in the strong sense at some rate. We quantify a large-sample asymptotic false positive rate and FWER, which enables interconversion of Bayesian model-averaged posterior odds and asymptotic
p
-values, on which we base a new method ‘Doublethink’. We investigate the limitations of Doublethink, including finite-sample inflation, and mitigation by grouping correlated variables. We demonstrate the benefits of the approach, including multilevel testing and post-hoc variable selection, through a simulation study benchmarking alternative approaches and a Mendelian randomization study of age-related macular degeneration. Finally, we discuss the broader implications of this work.

Bayesian hypothesis testing is a closed testing procedure that controls the familywise error rate in the strong sense

Definition 1

(
Frequentist familywise error rate control).

43

\boldsymbol{y}

\Omega

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta\in\omega_{\boldsymbol{s}}}\alpha_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\theta}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\Pr\left(\psi_{\boldsymbol{s}}({\boldsymbol{y}})=1;{\boldsymbol{x}},\theta\right),\qquad\theta\in\omega_{\boldsymbol{s}}.

By definition, a CTP rejects

\displaystyle\phi_{\boldsymbol{s}}({\boldsymbol{y}})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\underset{\omega_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}\in\Omega\ \theta\in\omega_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}}{\max\ \sup}\ \Pr\left(\phi_{\boldsymbol{s}}({\boldsymbol{y}})=1;{\boldsymbol{x}},\theta\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\max_{\omega_{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\Omega}\ \alpha_{\boldsymbol{s}}.

Definition 2

(
Bayesian false discovery rate control).

A Bayesian test rejects the null hypothesis that
\theta\in\omega_{\boldsymbol{s}}
when the posterior odds

(4)

\displaystyle\mathrm{PO}_{\omega_{\boldsymbol{s}}^{\mathsf{c}}:\omega_{\boldsymbol{s}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\int_{\omega_{\boldsymbol{s}}^{\mathsf{c}}}p({\boldsymbol{y}};{\boldsymbol{x}},\theta)\ \mathrm{d}\Pi(\theta)}{\int_{\omega_{\boldsymbol{s}}}p({\boldsymbol{y}};{\boldsymbol{x}},\theta)\ \mathrm{d}\Pi(\theta)},

\displaystyle\mathrm{FDR}_{\boldsymbol{s}}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\Pr\left(\theta\in\omega_{\boldsymbol{s}}\mid{\boldsymbol{y}},\,{\boldsymbol{x}}\right)\,\psi_{\boldsymbol{s}}({\boldsymbol{y}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{1+\mathrm{PO}_{\omega_{\boldsymbol{s}}^{\mathsf{c}}:\omega_{\boldsymbol{s}}}}\,\mathbb{I}\left(\mathrm{PO}_{\omega_{\boldsymbol{s}}^{\mathsf{c}}:\omega_{\boldsymbol{s}}}\geq\tau\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+\tau}

and, for the set of hypotheses defined by
\Omega=\{\omega_{\boldsymbol{s}}\}
, globally at level

(6)

\displaystyle\mathrm{FDR}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{\boldsymbol{s}}\mathrm{FDR}_{\boldsymbol{s}}}{\max\left\{1,\sum_{\boldsymbol{s}}\psi_{\boldsymbol{s}}(\boldsymbol{y})\right\}}\ \leq\ \dfrac{1}{1+\tau}.

Theorem 1

(
Bayesian hypothesis tests simultaneously control the Bayesian FDR and the frequentist FWER).

Bayesian hypothesis tests are a type of CTP known as a shortcut CTP. That is,
\phi_{\boldsymbol{s}}({\boldsymbol{y}})=\psi_{\boldsymbol{s}}({\boldsymbol{y}})=1
(rejection of
\theta\in\omega_{\boldsymbol{s}}
) automatically implies
\phi_{\boldsymbol{r}}({\boldsymbol{y}})=\psi_{\boldsymbol{r}}({\boldsymbol{y}})=1
for all intersection hypotheses
\omega_{\boldsymbol{r}}\subset\omega_{\boldsymbol{s}}
. Therefore, they simultaneously control the frequentist FWER in the strong sense at or below level
\max\{\alpha_{\boldsymbol{s}}:\omega_{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\Omega\}
and the Bayesian FDR at or below level
1/(1+\tau)
.

Proof.

By definition,
\omega_{\boldsymbol{r}}\subset\omega_{\boldsymbol{s}}
and
\omega_{\boldsymbol{r}}^{\mathsf{c}}\supset\omega_{\boldsymbol{s}}^{\mathsf{c}}
, so
{\int_{\omega_{\boldsymbol{r}}}p({\boldsymbol{y}};{\boldsymbol{x}},\theta)\ \mathrm{d}\Pi(\theta)}\leq{\int_{\omega_{\boldsymbol{s}}}p({\boldsymbol{y}};{\boldsymbol{x}},\theta)\ \mathrm{d}\Pi(\theta)}
and
{\int_{\omega_{\boldsymbol{r}}^{\mathsf{c}}}p({\boldsymbol{y}};{\boldsymbol{x}},\theta)\ \mathrm{d}\Pi(\theta)}\geq{\int_{\omega_{\boldsymbol{s}}^{\mathsf{c}}}p({\boldsymbol{y}};{\boldsymbol{x}},\theta)\ \mathrm{d}\Pi(\theta)}
. Therefore

\displaystyle\mathrm{PO}_{\omega_{\boldsymbol{r}}^{\mathsf{c}}:\omega_{\boldsymbol{r}}}=\frac{{\int_{\omega_{\boldsymbol{r}}^{\mathsf{c}}}p({\boldsymbol{y}};{\boldsymbol{x}},\theta)\ \mathrm{d}\Pi(\theta)}}{{\int_{\omega_{\boldsymbol{r}}}p({\boldsymbol{y}};{\boldsymbol{x}},\theta)\ \mathrm{d}\Pi(\theta)}}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{{\int_{\omega_{\boldsymbol{s}}^{\mathsf{c}}}p({\boldsymbol{y}};{\boldsymbol{x}},\theta)\ \mathrm{d}\Pi(\theta)}}{{\int_{\omega_{\boldsymbol{s}}}p({\boldsymbol{y}};{\boldsymbol{x}},\theta)\ \mathrm{d}\Pi(\theta)}}=\mathrm{PO}_{\omega_{\boldsymbol{s}}^{\mathsf{c}}:\omega_{\boldsymbol{s}}}

so

(7)

\displaystyle\psi_{\boldsymbol{s}}(\boldsymbol{y})=1\iff\mathrm{PO}_{\omega_{\boldsymbol{s}}^{\mathsf{c}}:\omega_{\boldsymbol{s}}}\geq\tau\implies\mathrm{PO}_{\omega_{\boldsymbol{r}}^{\mathsf{c}}:\omega_{\boldsymbol{r}}}\geq\tau\iff\psi_{\boldsymbol{r}}({\boldsymbol{y}})=1.

(

\omega_{\boldsymbol{s}}=\{\theta:\,\beta_{j}=0\;\,\forall\ s_{j}=0\}\subseteq\Theta=\mathbb{R}^{\nu+\zeta}
. The null hypotheses are
\Omega=\mathcal{S}\setminus{\boldsymbol{1}}
. We define models in terms of variable selection. Unlike the null hypotheses, the models are disjoint with respect to
\beta
, such that model
\boldsymbol{s}
has parameter space
\Theta_{\boldsymbol{s}}=\{\theta\,:\,\beta_{j}=0\ \forall\ s_{j}=0\ ;\ \beta_{j}\neq 0\ \forall\ s_{j}=1\}\subseteq\Theta

\omega_{\boldsymbol{r}}\subset\omega_{\boldsymbol{s}}

In general, the finite sample properties of frequentist methods, including FWER, are difficult to obtain. Instead, it is standard to use approximations based on the asymptotic distribution of methods as the sample size,

There are two sources of inflation in the simplified two-variable model, illustrated by Figure

. The first source (Figure

A) are higher-order tests involving more than one-degree-of-freedom (here,
\mathrm{PO}_{\boldsymbol{1}:\boldsymbol{0}}

. As the sample size increases, this source of inflation recedes and the FPR tends to its asymptotic level.

The second source (Figure

x_{\cdot 1}
here), and variables not being tested, that have non-zero effect (

here). Inflation peaks at an effect size

\xi_{n}

To trial Doublethink in a real data analysis, we applied it to a Mendelian randomization study of age-related macular degeneration (AMD).

Example 2

\bf y

Theorem 5

(
Testing based on a sub-analysis).

Suppose we conduct a sub-analysis in which we exclude a subset of variables
\{j\in\{1,\dots,\nu\}:{v}^{-}_{j}=0\}
, in a Bayesian model-averaged setting (Definition
7
). Let
\Theta^{-}=\omega_{{\boldsymbol{v}}^{-}}
define a reduced parameter space and let
\mathcal{S}^{-}=\{{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\mathcal{S}:\Theta_{\boldsymbol{s}}\subseteq\Theta^{-}\}
define a reduced model space. Let
\mathcal{O}^{-}_{{\boldsymbol{v}}}\;=\mathcal{O}_{{\boldsymbol{v}}}\,\cap\,\mathcal{S}^{-}
and
\mathcal{A}^{-}_{{\boldsymbol{v}}}=\mathcal{A}_{\boldsymbol{v}}\cap\mathcal{S}^{-}
be the sets of models compatible with any null and corresponding alternative hypothesis, respectively. In the sub-analysis, the model-averaged posterior odds against the null hypothesis
\omega_{\boldsymbol{v}}\subseteq\Theta^{-}
are

(40)

\displaystyle\mathrm{PO}_{\mathcal{A}^{-}_{\boldsymbol{v}}:\mathcal{O}^{-}_{\boldsymbol{v}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\mathcal{A}^{-}_{\boldsymbol{v}}}\mathrm{PO}_{\boldsymbol{s}}}{\sum_{{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\mathcal{O}^{-}_{\boldsymbol{v}}}\mathrm{PO}_{\boldsymbol{s}}}.

Now
\mathrm{PO}_{\mathcal{A}^{-}_{\boldsymbol{v}}:\mathcal{O}^{-}_{\boldsymbol{v}}}
is a lower bound on the posterior odds against the intersection null hypothesis
\omega_{{\boldsymbol{v}}\odot{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\prime}}:=\omega_{\boldsymbol{v}}\ \cap\ \omega_{{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\prime}}
, where
\odot
denotes elementwise multiplication, i.e.

(41)

\displaystyle\mathrm{PO}_{\mathcal{A}^{-}_{\boldsymbol{v}}:\mathcal{O}^{-}_{\boldsymbol{v}}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{PO}_{\mathcal{A}_{{\boldsymbol{v}}\odot{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\prime}}:\mathcal{O}_{{\boldsymbol{v}}\odot{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\prime}}}

but it is not a bound on the posterior odds against the null hypothesis
\omega_{\boldsymbol{v}}
, i.e.

(42)

\displaystyle\mathrm{PO}_{\mathcal{A}^{-}_{\boldsymbol{v}}:\mathcal{O}^{-}_{\boldsymbol{v}}}

\displaystyle\nleq\mathrm{PO}_{\mathcal{A}_{\boldsymbol{v}}:\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{v}}}.

Therefore rejection of a null hypothesis
\omega_{\boldsymbol{v}}
in a sub-analysis only implies rejection of the intersection null hypothesis
\omega_{{\boldsymbol{v}}\odot{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\prime}}
in the full analysis and does not imply rejection of
\omega_{{\boldsymbol{v}}}
in the full analysis.

Proof.

\mathcal{A}^{-}_{\boldsymbol{v}}\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{{\boldsymbol{v}}\odot{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\prime}}
and
\mathcal{O}^{-}_{\boldsymbol{v}}=\mathcal{O}_{{\boldsymbol{v}}\odot{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\prime}}
whereas
\mathcal{A}^{-}_{\boldsymbol{v}}\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
and
\mathcal{O}^{-}_{\boldsymbol{v}}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
.

7.
Inflation between highly correlated variables: simulation approach

In human GWAS, genetic variants that are physically linked tend to be correlated, so that signals of association often manifest as clusters of significant genetic variants.
When several linked genetic variants are significant, the assumption is that most are not causally associated. So we reject the intersection hypothesis that none of the clustered variants are associated, but we do not reject all the elementary hypotheses, as a straightforward interpretation of statistical significance would suggest, because that would probably produce many false positives among the clustered variants. In this section we formalize the idea and show through simulation that the way statistical significance is interpreted has a direct bearing on FWER control.

Definition 9

(
Frequentist control of FWER
ρ
).

In the regression problem (Definition
3
), where the binary vector
{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\mathcal{S}=\{0,1\}^{\nu}
indexes the null hypotheses such that
\beta_{j}=0\ \forall\ s_{j}=0
, where
\beta_{j}
is the coefficient for variable
x_{\cdot j}
, define the correlation coefficient between variables
j
and
k
to be
\rho_{j,k}
. Define the maximum absolute correlation between variables whose regression coefficients are constrained versus unconstrained under null hypothesis
\boldsymbol{s}
to be

(43)

\displaystyle\rho^{\mathrm{max}}_{\boldsymbol{s}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}0&\boldsymbol{s}\in\{\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{1}\}\\
\max_{j,k\,:\,s_{j}\neq s_{k}}|\rho_{j,k}|&\textrm{otherwise}\end{cases}

The idea is to group the most correlated variables indivisibly, in order to exclude null hypotheses with the highest
\rho^{\mathrm{max}}_{\boldsymbol{s}}
from the set of all tested hypotheses,
\Omega
, because they are most susceptible to inflation. We define
\Omega_{\rho}=\{\omega_{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\Omega:\rho^{\mathrm{max}}_{\boldsymbol{s}}\leq\rho\}
. By definition,
\Omega_{\rho}
remains closed under intersection. We define

(44)

\displaystyle\mathrm{FWER}_{\rho}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\underset{\omega_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}\in\Omega_{\rho}\ \theta\in\omega_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}}{\max\ \sup}\ \Pr\left(\phi_{\boldsymbol{s}}({\boldsymbol{y}})=1;{\boldsymbol{x}},\theta\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\max_{\omega_{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\Omega_{\rho}}\ \alpha_{\boldsymbol{s}}.

This implies the regular FWER (Definition
1
) equals FWER
1
. Since
\mathrm{FWER}_{\rho}\leq\mathrm{FWER}_{1}
, it is a less stringent error rate to control, and control of
\mathrm{FWER}_{1}
implies control of
\mathrm{FWER}_{\rho}
.

Definition 10

(
Asymptotically equivalent test statistic).

Lemma
1
implies that the model-averaged posterior odds against the null hypothesis
\omega_{\boldsymbol{v}}
, denoted
\mathrm{PO}_{\mathcal{A}_{\boldsymbol{v}}:\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{v}}}
, are asymptotically equivalent to the posterior odds of alternatives differing from the true model
\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}
,
\theta\in\Theta_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}}
by one degree-of-freedom:

(45)

\displaystyle\mathrm{PO}_{\mathcal{A}_{\boldsymbol{v}}:\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{v}}}

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\sum_{j\,:\,v_{j}=0}\mathrm{PO}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}+{\boldsymbol{e}}_{j}:\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}}\ =:\ \tilde{\mathrm{PO}}_{\mathcal{A}_{\boldsymbol{v}}:\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{v}}}

as
n\rightarrow\infty
, assuming that
\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
, where
\{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{j}\}_{k}=\mathbb{I}(j=k)
.
This allows us to partition the error of Doublethink into (a) the approximation to the distribution of
\tilde{\mathrm{PO}}_{\mathcal{A}_{\boldsymbol{v}}:\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{v}}}
and (b) the rate of convergence to the asymptotically equivalent test statistic.

Remark 1

(
The
\alpha\leq 0.025

in Doublethink; equivalently,

Definition 11

(
Bayes FWER).

For the purpose of simulation it is convenient to define the FWER as an expectation over a (possibly degenerate) distribution of parameter values, rather than in a minimax sense (Definition
1
). We define the Bayes FWER in a Bayes risk sense (see, e.g.
6
) as

(46)

\displaystyle\mathrm{BFWER}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\int_{\Theta}\Pr\left(\mathrm{PO}_{\mathcal{A}_{{\boldsymbol{v}}(\theta)}:\mathcal{O}_{{\boldsymbol{v}}(\theta)}}\geq\tau;{\boldsymbol{x}},\theta\right)\mathrm{d}\Pi(\theta),

where the notation
{\boldsymbol{v}}(\theta)
,
v_{j}(\theta)=\mathbb{I}(\beta_{j}\neq 0)
, emphasizes that the intersection of the true null hypotheses depends on the parameter
\theta
. By analogy, we can define BFWER
ρ
with reference to Definition
9
, and the asymptotically equivalent Bayes FWER,

(47)

\displaystyle\mathrm{AFWER}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\int_{\Theta}\Pr\left(\tilde{\mathrm{PO}}_{\mathcal{A}_{{\boldsymbol{v}}(\theta)}:\mathcal{O}_{{\boldsymbol{v}}(\theta)}}\geq\tau;{\boldsymbol{x}},\theta\right)\mathrm{d}\Pi(\theta),

with the caveat that the asymptotically equivalent test (Definition
10
), does not actually define a CTP, and therefore AFWER is not a familywise error rate, for finite
n
.

Definition 12

(
Worst-case Bayes FWER in Doublethink).

The
e
-value approach of

yields a worst-case inflation for BFWER in Doublethink when
\Pi
is the ‘true’ distribution of parameter values, which provides a benchmark for simulation performance.

(48)

\displaystyle\mathrm{BFWER}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Pr\left(\mathrm{PO}_{\mathcal{A}_{{\boldsymbol{v}}(\theta)}:\mathcal{O}_{{\boldsymbol{v}}(\theta)}}\geq\tau;{\boldsymbol{x}},\Pi\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{PO}_{\mathcal{A}_{{\boldsymbol{v}}(\theta)}:\mathcal{O}_{{\boldsymbol{v}}(\theta)}};{\boldsymbol{x}},\Pi\right]/\tau\qquad\textrm{(by Markov's inequality)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mu^{\nu-|{\boldsymbol{v}}(\theta)|};\Pi\right]/\tau

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\left(\ 1+\frac{\mu}{1+\mu}\right)^{\nu}-1\right]/\tau\ \ \sim\ \ \frac{\nu\,\mu}{\tau}\ \textrm{when}\ \mu\ll 1.

9.
Discussion

Often, the goal of hypothesis testing is to identify a few influential variables among many candidates, reflecting the scientific principal of explaining complex observed patterns by simple unobserved processes. For accurately estimating parameters, quantifying their uncertainty, and controlling false positives, model choice is vital, as the simulations showed. Bayesian model averaging is especially useful when the model is uncertain, but for decades frequentists and Bayesians have debated hypothesis testing, seemingly irreconcilably (e.g.
15
;
5
;
2
;
21
).

Perhaps it is surprising, therefore, that Bayesian hypothesis testing can be formulated as a closed testing procedure
(
43
)
that simultaneously controls the Bayesian FDR and the strong-sense frequentist FWER (Theorem
1
). Particularly surprising because FWER, in contrast to FDR, is often characterized as “an extremely strict criterion which is not always appropriate”
(
64
)
. Theorem
1
is general, independent of prior or modelling assumptions, complementing known results like Bayesian consistency
(
14
;
58
)
and the frequentist admissibility of Bayesian procedures
(
74
;
6
)
. It addresses both the broad question of when Bayesian and frequentist philosophies converge, and the practical question of how to choose significance thresholds. Whereas it may often be convenient to pre-determine one or the other, reporting both the FWER and FDR thresholds offers an opportunity to bridge Bayesian and frequentist approaches to hypothesis testing

In big data regression settings, Theorems
2
and
3
extend work by
31
;
32
to interconvert FPR, FWER and FDR thresholds. Corollaries
2
and
3
interconvert the unadjusted and adjusted
p
-values and posterior odds. These results are less general than Theorem
1
, comprising large-sample limits that require assumptions including an appropriate family of prior distributions and independent observations. Clearly their utility depends on the relevance of the Johnson model (Definition
6
) to the problem at hand. In general the convergence results are pointwise, rather than uniform, with respect to the parameters, subjecting their use to finite-sample inflation. Keeping in mind such caveats, these results nevertheless afford insights into the relationship between the frequentist approach to multiple testing and Bayesian prior assumptions. Corollary
4
states that the FWER threshold is asymptotically proportional to (a) the number of variables,
\nu
; (b) the prior odds,
\mu
; and (c) the square root of the prior precision
h
; and asymptotically inversely proportional to (d) the Bayesian threshold,
\tau
; and (e) the square root of the sample size,
n
. The FWER and FDR bounds – which are not necessarily tight – are approximately inversely proportional.

Corollary
4
recapitulates the Jeffreys-Lindley paradox
(
30
;
39
)
: Bayesian inference is consistent for a fixed FDR threshold,
\tau
,
(
14
)
, whereas the practice of fixing the FWER threshold,
\alpha
, irrespective of sample size,
n
(e.g. at 0.05 or 0.005
(
3
)
), is not consistent because there exists a tangible probability of falsely rejecting a null hypothesis no matter how large the sample
(
51
)
. This can be solved by inversely scaling
\alpha
with
\sqrt{n}
, originally advocated by Jeffreys
(
71
)
, from which point one could report both the FWER and FDR bound.

Interconverting model-averaged posterior odds and adjusted
p
-values using Doublethink (Corollary
3
) has several limitations that stem from the underlying assumptions. These assumptions include (a) a large sample size, which implies that a Normal approximation to the likelihood is reasonable locally; (b) data that comprise independent realizations of a process satisfying technical conditions which imply locally unique, consistent and asymptotically Normal maximum likelihood estimators
(
10
)
; (c) a specific family of conjugate priors parameterized by
\mu
and
h
; (d) local alternatives, which implies that the Normal approximation holds in the vicinity of null and alternative hypotheses; and (e) technical assumptions like joint convergence to the limiting distributions. The local alternatives assumption is convenient for deriving an asymptotic distribution for the maximized likelihood ratio
(
10
;
31
;
32
)
, yet it implies that non-zero parameter values scale with
\sqrt{n}
, contrary to consistency and our prior, though this may be a side-issue for large
n
, because the evidence quickly becomes decisive
(
35
)
. The conjugate prior is convenient for pursuing joint Bayesian-frequentist inference (e.g.
78
;
38
), but its covariance structure, based on the Fisher information, is otherwise hard to justify.
35
investigated other priors for which the BIC (and by implication the Johnson model) offer a reasonable approximation. The independent data assumption restricts the generality of the approach. It is not recommended to apply Doublethink with
\alpha>0.025
;
p
-values larger than
0.025
should be interpreted as 1 (Remark 1), but this is hardly limiting if
\alpha
is to scale inversely with
\sqrt{n}
.

Of principal concern, the large sample assumption means the asymptotic results may be inaccurate for finite samples, as evidenced by the simulations. Two sources of inflation were found: (a) the contribution of higher-order tests involving more than one-degree-of-freedom to the posterior odds, which vanishes asymptotically, and (b) the lack of convergence of the posterior odds to the asymptotically equivalent test statistic (Definition
10
). The latter is caused by correlation between variables that have zero and non-zero effects. The simplified two-variable model (Definition
8
) attributed this inflation to specific parameter values that scale inversely with
\sqrt{n}
, and therefore threaten inflation regardless of
n
. In simulations, not only Doublethink but all methods were affected by such inflation (Figure
8
), which invalidates the fundamental assumption that test statistics are well calibrated under the null. To address it, we formalized the practice of making inferences about indivisible groups of correlated variables, rather than the individual variables concerned, through the notion of FWER
ρ
(Definition
9
). In simulations, this mitigated, but did not eradicate, inflation at
\rho=0.43
.

Hypothesis testing, particularly multiple testing, has been considered a long and difficult-to-reconcile conflict between Bayesian and frequentist approaches
(
2
)
, in constrast to parameter estimation (e.g.
14
;
9
). Doublethink is connected to other approaches that bridge this gap, notably
e
-values
(
70
)
. Application of the
e
-value approach to the Doublethink model (Definition 7) allows interconversion of the Bayesian FDR and the Bayes FWER (Definition
11
), assuming the prior is correctly specified. The
e
-value bound on the Bayes FWER (Definition
12
) is then approximately proportional to (a) the number of variables,
\nu
; and (b) the prior odds,
\mu
; but not (c) the square root of the prior precision
h
; and inversely proportional to (d) the Bayesian threshold,
\tau
; but not (e) the square root of the sample size,
n
. Therefore the
e
-value bound on the Bayes FWER is conservative, in the large-sample limit, by a factor of approximately
\sqrt{h/n}
, but conversely, the Doublethink threshold on the Bayes FWER could be anti-conservative, in a finite sample, by the corresponding amount.

Another related approach, which extends FWER, is the false discovery proportion (FDP) approach of
23
;
22
. The FDP approach comprises a ‘multilevel’ closed testing procedure
(
45
)
in which all possible combinations of hypotheses are simultaneously controlled via pre-determined thresholds. This means arbitrary combinations of hypotheses can be tested and significant groups of variables freely identified post-hoc. The approach is inherent to Bayesian control of both the FDR and, by Theorem
1
, the FWER. Multilevel testing challenges concepts like fishing for significance, data dredging, and
p
-hacking
(
1
)
.

The Bayesian FDR considered here motivated, but remains distinct from, frequentist FDR concepts. Doublethink controls both the ‘local’ and ‘global’ Bayesian FDR
(
16
)
, rather than a frequentist FDR controlled by Benjamini and Hochberg’s
(
4
)
or Storey’s
(
63
;
64
)
procedures. One connection to Theorem
1
is that controlling the frequentist FDR controls the FWER at the same level, but only in the weak sense
(
4
)
. Whereas Corollary
4
indicates the FWER bound is
O(\sqrt{n})
smaller than the Bayesian FDR bound.

Doublethink belongs to a class of combination tests that exploit heavy-tailed distributions, including the Cauchy combination test
(
40
)
and the HMP procedure (
76
), through its reliance on the theory of regular variation
(
34
;
48
)
. The HMP offers model averaging, starting with
p
-values, whereas Doublethink pursues joint Bayesian-frequentist model-averaging, beginning with nested maximized likelihood ratios. A theoretical advance over the HMP is the ability of Doublethink to consider model uncertainty in the null hypothesis, as well as in the alternative hypothesis. Another is the clarification that the relevant limit is
n\rightarrow\infty
, rather than
\nu\rightarrow\infty
. By Corollary
1
, the model-averaged deviance asymptotically follows a chi-squared distribution with one degree of freedom. This mirroring of the null distribution of the classical likelihood ratio test statistic
(
75
;
73
)
emerges from the self-similarity or fractal property of sums of heavy-tailed random variables
(
42
;
65
)
. A practical advance, demonstrated by the simulations, is the greater power of testing groups of correlated variables using a coherent Bayesian approach, rather than by combining
p
-values derived from tests of the individual variables.

This paper focuses on the frequentist properties of Bayesian model averaging. It implies a limited defence of
p
-values, specifically those based on likelihood ratio tests. Despite their flaws, here
p
-values offer a useful complement to Bayesian reasoning because their distribution under the null hypothesis is defined independently of a prior. The ability to interconvert model-averaged
p
-values with Bayesian sufficient statistics like the model-averaged deviance or posterior odds implies a reprieve from the central criticisms of
p
-values – their lack of evidentiary value and violation of the likelihood principal (see, e.g.
7
). Simultaneous Bayesian-frequentist hypothesis testing addresses another major flaw of
p
-values – their indifference towards power – because the prior determines the performance of the test under the alternative hypothesis. By characterizing the asymptotic properties of Bayesian tests under the null hypothesis, even when the prior is wrong, we have aimed to exploit the advantages of Bayesian model-averaged hypothesis testing while mitigating the subjective influence of the prior by controlling both the FDR and the FWER at a quantifiable level.

Background theory

B.1.
Closed testing procedures control the familywise error rate in the strong sense

14
introduced the closed testing procedure (CTP) to control the frequentist family-wise error rate (FWER) in the strong sense. Suppose that random element
\boldsymbol{y}
has a probability mass or density function
p({\boldsymbol{y}};{\boldsymbol{x}},\theta)
that depends on auxiliary data
\boldsymbol{x}
and parameters
\theta\in\Theta
.
The aim is to test a set of hypotheses
defined by
\Omega=\{\omega_{\boldsymbol{s}}\}
, where
\omega_{\boldsymbol{s}}\subset\Theta
and
\boldsymbol{s}
is an index.

\Omega
must be closed under intersection, meaning
\omega_{\boldsymbol{s}},\omega_{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}}\in\Omega
implies
\omega_{\boldsymbol{s}}\cap\omega_{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}}\in\Omega
. A ‘local’ test controls the false positive rate (FPR) at level
\alpha_{\boldsymbol{s}}
by rejecting
\theta\in\omega_{\boldsymbol{s}}
when
\psi_{\boldsymbol{s}}({\boldsymbol{y}})=1
(rather than 0) such that:

(B1)

\displaystyle\alpha_{\boldsymbol{s}}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta\in\omega_{\boldsymbol{s}}}\Pr\left(\psi_{\boldsymbol{s}}({\boldsymbol{y}})=1;{\boldsymbol{x}},\theta\right).

A CTP rejects
\theta\in\omega_{\boldsymbol{s}}
when
\phi_{\boldsymbol{s}}({\boldsymbol{y}})=1
, which indicates rejection of all intersection hypotheses in
\Omega
:

(B2)

\displaystyle\phi_{\boldsymbol{s}}({\boldsymbol{y}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min_{\omega_{\boldsymbol{r}}\subseteq\omega_{\boldsymbol{s}}}\psi_{\boldsymbol{r}}({\boldsymbol{y}}).

By construction, rejection of the null hypothesis
\omega_{\boldsymbol{s}}
by the CTP implies rejection of any and all intersection hypotheses too:

(B3)

\displaystyle\phi_{\boldsymbol{s}}({\boldsymbol{y}})=1

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\phi_{\boldsymbol{r}}({\boldsymbol{y}})=1\qquad\forall\ \omega_{\boldsymbol{r}}\subseteq\omega_{\boldsymbol{s}}.

If this is an inherent property of a set of local tests (achieved, for example, by setting the local significance thresholds appropriately), then we have a procedure

(B4)

\displaystyle\phi_{\boldsymbol{s}}({\boldsymbol{y}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\psi_{\boldsymbol{s}}({\boldsymbol{y}})

known as a shortcut CTP. Shortcut procedures are more computationally efficient (c.f. Equation
B2
).

The purpose of the CTP is to control the FWER, at a level determined by the local tests. The FPR of test
\phi
is related to the FPR of local test
\psi
by

(B5)

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta\in\omega_{\boldsymbol{s}}}\Pr\left(\phi_{\boldsymbol{s}}(\boldsymbol{y})=1;\theta\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta\in\omega_{\boldsymbol{s}}}\Pr\left(\bigcap_{\omega_{\boldsymbol{r}}\subseteq\omega_{\boldsymbol{s}}}\left\{\psi_{\boldsymbol{r}}(\boldsymbol{y})=1\right\};\theta\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta\in\omega_{\boldsymbol{s}}}\Pr\left(\psi_{\boldsymbol{s}}(\boldsymbol{y})=1;\theta\right)\ =\ \alpha_{\boldsymbol{s}}.

Let
\omega_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}}
denote the intersection of all true null hypotheses in
\Omega
. The CTP controls the FWER given
\theta\in\omega_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}}
at level

(B6)

\displaystyle\mathrm{FWER}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta\in\omega_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}}}\Pr\left(\bigcup_{\omega_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}}\subseteq\omega_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}}\left\{\phi_{\boldsymbol{s}}({\boldsymbol{y}})=1\right\};{\boldsymbol{x}},\theta\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta\in\omega_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}}}\Pr\left(\phi_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}}({\boldsymbol{y}})=1;{\boldsymbol{x}},\theta\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\alpha_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}}

by the logic of the CTP (Equation
B3
) and the FPR of test
\phi
(Equation
B5
). In other words, for a familywise error to occur, it is necessary and sufficient that
\theta\in\omega_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}}
be rejected by test
\phi_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}}(\boldsymbol{y})
. Since
\omega_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}}
is unknown, sometimes the weak-sense FWER is used, defined as
\mathrm{FWER}_{\boldsymbol{0}}
, but this may be anti-conservative when some null hypotheses are false. More often the strong-sense FWER is used, defined as

(B7)

\displaystyle\mathrm{FWER}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\max_{\omega_{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\Omega}\ \mathrm{FWER}_{\boldsymbol{s}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\max_{\omega_{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\Omega}\ \alpha_{\boldsymbol{s}}.

B.3.4.
Classical hypothesis tests: power

To evaluate the power of the likelihood ratio test – the probability of rejecting the null hypothesis (model
{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{0}}
) when, in fact, it is false – it is necessary to consider the likelihood ratio test statistic when the alternative model (model
\boldsymbol{s}
) is true. Following the approach of earlier works (
2
,
10
and
9
), a local alternatives assumption can be followed, whereby,

(B40)

\displaystyle\textrm{under model }\boldsymbol{0}\textrm{:}\quad\theta^{\boldsymbol{0}}_{\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{s}}}=\begin{pmatrix}{\boldsymbol{0}}\\
{\gamma}\end{pmatrix}

(B41)

\displaystyle\textrm{under model }\boldsymbol{s}\textrm{:}\quad\theta^{\boldsymbol{s},n}_{\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{s}}}=\begin{pmatrix}{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\boldsymbol{s},n}_{\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{s}}}}\\
{\gamma}\end{pmatrix}

Where

(B42)

\displaystyle\beta^{\boldsymbol{s},n}_{i}={q_{i}^{n}}/n^{1/2},\textrm{ with}\;\lim_{n\to\infty}{q_{i}^{n}}=q_{i},\;i=1,2...,|\boldsymbol{s}|.

In simple terms, therefore, the local alternatives hypothesis assumes that
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\boldsymbol{s},n}_{\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{s}}}
is a sequence of alternatives that converge to the null vector at rate
n^{1/2}
and that interest focuses on small departures from the null. The fact that larger departures from the null become self-evident in large sample sizes motivates this scaling assumption; the focus of the statistical analysis is necessarily on the ‘grey area’ of small departures from the null. More importantly, the assumption is convenient because it implies the tail of the distribution of the MLE is still approximately normal near the null value, which greatly simplifies the theory.

Equation
B32
gives an expression for the maximized likelihood ratio.

(B43)

\displaystyle R_{\boldsymbol{s}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left\{\frac{n}{2}\left\{\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\boldsymbol{s}}_{\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{s}}}\right\}^{T}\left[\left(\left[\mathcal{I}(\tilde{\theta})_{\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{s}}}\right]^{-1}\right)_{\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{s}}}\right]^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\boldsymbol{s}}_{\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{s}}}+o_{p}(1)\right\}

\displaystyle\exp\left\{\frac{n}{2}\left\{\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\boldsymbol{s}}_{\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{s}}}\right\}^{T}\left[\left(\left[\mathcal{I}(\theta^{\boldsymbol{s},n})_{\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{s}}}\right]^{-1}\right)_{\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{s}}}\right]^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\boldsymbol{s}}_{\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{s}}}+o_{p}(1)\right\}

\displaystyle\int_{\Theta_{\boldsymbol{s}}}\ f_{\mathcal{N}_{|\boldsymbol{s}|+\zeta}}\left(\theta\,\big|\tfrac{n}{n+h}\,\hat{\theta}^{\boldsymbol{s}}_{\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{s}}},\,\tfrac{n}{n+h}\left[n\,\mathcal{I}({\boldsymbol{0}})_{\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{s}}}\right]^{-1}\right)\,d\theta

\displaystyle\propto

\displaystyle f_{\mathcal{N}_{|\boldsymbol{s}|+\zeta}}\left(\hat{\theta}^{\boldsymbol{s}}_{\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{s}}}\,\big|\,\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right)

Where

(B63)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\left(\tfrac{1}{h}+\tfrac{1}{n}\right)\left[\mathcal{I}({\boldsymbol{0}})_{\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{s}}}\right]^{-1}

Equation

\displaystyle{m(\boldsymbol{y}|\,\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{s})}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle c\,{f_{\mathcal{N}_{|\boldsymbol{s}|+\zeta}}\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\boldsymbol{s}}_{\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{s}}}\,\bigg|\,\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{s}}}\right)}\times

\displaystyle f_{\mathcal{N}_{|\boldsymbol{s}|+\zeta}}\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\boldsymbol{s}}\,\bigg|\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathcal{F}_{0},\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{s}}}\{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{s}}}\}^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\boldsymbol{s}}_{\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{s}}}\left[\left\{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\right\}_{\mathcal{F}_{0},\mathcal{F}_{0}}\right]^{-1}\right)

(B65)

\displaystyle m(\boldsymbol{y}|\,\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{0})

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle c\,{f_{\mathcal{N}_{|\boldsymbol{s}|+\zeta}}\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\boldsymbol{s}}_{\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{s}}}\,\bigg|\,\boldsymbol{0},\tfrac{h}{n+h}\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{s}}}\right)}\times

\displaystyle{f_{\mathcal{N}_{|\boldsymbol{s}|+\zeta}}\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\boldsymbol{s}}\,\bigg|\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathcal{F}_{0},\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{s}}}\{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{s}}}\}^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\boldsymbol{s}}_{\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{s}}},\left[\left\{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\right\}_{\mathcal{F}_{0},\mathcal{F}_{0}}\right]^{-1}\right)}

Thus the Bayes factor for the test between model
{\boldsymbol{s}}
and model
\boldsymbol{0}
, defined as

(B66)

\displaystyle\mathrm{BF}_{\boldsymbol{s}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{m(\boldsymbol{y}|\,\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{s})}{m(\boldsymbol{y}|\,\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{0})}

can be simplified (via Equations

and
B65

(B67)

\displaystyle\mathrm{BF}_{\boldsymbol{s}}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{f_{\mathcal{N}}\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\boldsymbol{s}}_{\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{s}}}\,\bigg|\,\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{s}}}\right)}{f_{\mathcal{N}}\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\boldsymbol{s}}_{\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{s}}}\,\bigg|\,\boldsymbol{0},\frac{h}{n+h}\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{s}}}\right)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(\textrm{det}(\frac{h}{n+h}\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{s}}}))^{1/2}}{(\textrm{det}({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{s}}}})^{1/2}}\exp\left\{-\tfrac{1}{2}\left(1-\tfrac{n+h}{h}\right)\left\{\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\boldsymbol{s}}_{\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{s}}}\right\}^{T}\{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{s}}}\}^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\boldsymbol{s}}_{\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{s}}}\right\}

and therefore,

\displaystyle\mathrm{BF}_{\boldsymbol{s}}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\left(\tfrac{h}{n+h}\right)^{|\boldsymbol{s}|/2}\exp\left\{\tfrac{n}{2h}\left\{\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\boldsymbol{s}}_{\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{s}}}\right\}^{T}\{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{s}}}\}^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\boldsymbol{s}}_{\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{s}}}\right\}

\displaystyle\left(\tfrac{h}{n+h}\right)^{|\boldsymbol{s}|/2}\exp\left\{\tfrac{n}{2h}{\left(\tfrac{1}{h}+\tfrac{1}{n}\right)}^{-1}\left\{\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\boldsymbol{s}}_{\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{s}}}\right\}^{T}\left[\left(\left[\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{0})_{\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{s}}}\right]^{-1}\right)_{\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{s}}}\right]^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\boldsymbol{s}}_{\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{s}}}\right\}

Through comparison of Equation
B68
with Equation
B36
, it is possible to write the Bayes factor as a function of the MLR,
{R_{\boldsymbol{s}}}
, under the null hypothesis. This expression has been termed the likelihood ratio test-based Bayes factor (
10
,
9
).

(B69)

\displaystyle\mathrm{BF}_{\boldsymbol{s}}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\left(\tfrac{h}{n+h}\right)^{|\boldsymbol{s}|/2}\,({R_{\boldsymbol{s}}})^{n/(n+h)}

It is noteworthy that the local alternatives assumption employed above implies that the hyperparameter,
h
, be proportional to the sample size (
h\propto n
), such that
\beta^{\boldsymbol{s},n}_{i}
shrinks with
n
, as given by definition (Equation
B42
).

Following the methods of Johnson and colleagues, however (
10
;
9
), this local alternatives assumption is dropped from this point forward. Instead,
h
is assumed to be fixed at a constant to remove the dependence of the prior on
n
. Furthermore, as derived previously (see Lemma 1 of
10
) and repeated in Section
B.4.5
for completeness, when
h
is a constant, the logarithm of the Bayes factor is consistent, allowing for consistency of hypothesis testing.

Notice further that because the Bayes Factor depends only on a single hyper-parameter,
h
, is it easy to calculate. For
n\gg h
, the main role of the hyper-parameter
h
is to modify the penalty on the number of free parameters in the Bayesian interpretation of the maximized likelihood ratio,
R_{\boldsymbol{s}}
.

The posterior odds for the test of model
\boldsymbol{s}
to
\boldsymbol{0}
is defined to be the product of model prior odds,
{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{s}:\boldsymbol{0}}={\mu}^{|\boldsymbol{s}|}
(see MT Equation
19
) and the Bayes Factor (Equation
B69
) and can be written as follows,

(B70)

\displaystyle\mathrm{PO}_{\boldsymbol{s}}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle{{\mu}^{|\boldsymbol{s}|}}\left(\tfrac{h}{n+h}\right)^{|\boldsymbol{s}|/{2}}\,(R_{\boldsymbol{s}})^{n/(n+h)}

In the Bayesian framework, the null hypothesis, model
\boldsymbol{0}
, is rejected in favor of the alternative hypothesis, model
\boldsymbol{s}
, in the sense that the smallest
100\left(1-1/(1+\tau)\right)\%
posterior model credibility set excludes model
\boldsymbol{0}
, when
\mathrm{PO}_{\boldsymbol{s}}\geq\tau
. Model
\boldsymbol{0}
is therefore rejected when the following inequality holds

(B71)

\displaystyle\mu^{|\boldsymbol{s}|}\left(\tfrac{h}{n+h}\right)^{{|\boldsymbol{s}|}/{2}}\,R_{\boldsymbol{s}}^{n/(n+h)}\geq{\tau}

B.4.4.
Classical power of the Bayesian test

Since the Bayes factor is an increasing function of the MLR, the prior implies that
\bar{\delta}_{\boldsymbol{s}}=|\boldsymbol{s}|\,n/h
, and Equation
B51
can be used to calculate the classical power of the Bayesian hypothesis test.

(B72)

\displaystyle\Pr\left(\chi^{2}_{|\boldsymbol{s}|}\geq\left(1+\tfrac{n}{h}\right)^{-1}\,Q^{-1}_{\chi^{2}_{|\boldsymbol{s}|}}\left(1-\tfrac{1}{1+\tau}\right)\right).

B.4.5.
The logarithm of the Bayes factor is consistent

As demonstrated by
10
, and repeated here for completeness, the logarithm of the Bayes factor (Equation
B69
), as given below, is consistent.

(B73)

\displaystyle\log(\mathrm{BF}_{\boldsymbol{s}})

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle-{\frac{|\boldsymbol{s}|}{2}}\textrm{log}\left(\tfrac{n}{h}+1\right)+\left(\tfrac{n}{n+h}\right)\textrm{log}\left({R_{\boldsymbol{s}}}\right)

This means that under model
\boldsymbol{0}

(B74)

\displaystyle{\log(\mathrm{BF}}_{\boldsymbol{s}})\overset{p}{\mathop{\to}}-\infty,\qquad n\to\infty

and under model
\boldsymbol{s}

(B75)

\displaystyle{\log(\mathrm{BF}}_{\boldsymbol{s}})\overset{p}{\mathop{\to}}\infty,\qquad n\to\infty

This result follows from noticing that, when the null hypothesis is true,
2\log R_{\boldsymbol{s}}\ \overset{d}{\mathop{\to}}\ \chi^{2}_{|\boldsymbol{s}|}
as
n\to\infty
(Equation
B37
), i.e. can be written as the sum of squares of
|\boldsymbol{s}|
independent standard Normal distributions. The second term on the right-hand side of Equation
B73
is thus bounded in probability, whereas the first tends to -
\infty
as
n\to\infty
. Therefore,
\log(\mathrm{BF}_{\boldsymbol{s}})\overset{p}{\mathop{\to}}-\infty
.

When the alternative hypothesis is true, however,
2\log R_{\boldsymbol{s}}\ \overset{d}{\mathop{\to}}\ \chi^{2}_{|\boldsymbol{s}|,\,\delta}
as
n\to\infty
(Equation
B46
) i.e. represents the sum of independent and identically distributed random variables that each has an expectation that is a linear function of
n
(Equation
B47
and MT Equation
B9
) and finite variance. The second term on the right-hand side of Equation
B73
is therefore
O_{p}(n)
. As the first term is
O\left(-\log{(n)}\right)
, the term on the right-hand side dominates the term on the left-hand side, and
\log(\mathrm{BF}_{\boldsymbol{s}})\overset{p}{\mathop{\to}}\infty
.

Based upon the consistency result demonstrated here, we argue that, whereas the local alternative hypothesis is useful for motivating the asymptotic theory on which the approximate methods are based, in practice
h
should be fixed, for example, at
h=1
, as in the unit information prior
(
12
)
, and further that in the classical significance threshold,
\alpha
, should scale with
n^{-1/2}
, to also make the classical approach consistent.

Appendix C
Proof of Lemma

Step 1. Define the scaled posterior odds.

To study the large-sample behaviour of the model-averaged posterior odds (MT Eqn.
21
), we can define the scaled odds as

(C1)

\displaystyle\kappa_{n}\ \mathrm{PO}_{\mathcal{A}_{\boldsymbol{v}}:\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{v}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\kappa_{n}\ \dfrac{\sum_{\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathcal{A}_{\boldsymbol{v}}}{\mathrm{PO}}_{\boldsymbol{s}}}{\sum_{\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{v}}}{\mathrm{PO}}_{\boldsymbol{s}}},\qquad\kappa_{n}=\left(\mu\ \sqrt{\tfrac{h}{n+h}}\right)^{-1}.

As a reminder of notation, we are interested in testing the null hypothesis
\omega_{\boldsymbol{v}}=\{\theta:\beta_{j}=0\ \forall\ v_{j}=0\}
. The null hypothesis encompasses all the models

\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{v}}=\{{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\mathcal{S}:\Theta_{\boldsymbol{s}}\subseteq\omega_{\boldsymbol{v}}\}
where an individual model
\boldsymbol{s}
is defined by its parameter space
\Theta_{\boldsymbol{s}}=\{\theta:\beta_{j}=0\ \forall\ s_{j}=0;\ \beta_{j}\neq 0\ \forall\ s_{j}=1\}
.
\mathcal{S}=\{0,1\}^{\nu}
is the state space of all models and the alternative hypothesis encompasses the remaining models
\mathcal{A}_{\boldsymbol{v}}=\{\mathcal{S}\setminus\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{v}}\}
. The models are disjoint.

It will be convenient to define the set of ‘basic’ alternative models,

(C2)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{v}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{\boldsymbol{t}:\boldsymbol{t}\in\mathcal{A}_{\boldsymbol{v}},\;t_{j}=0\ \forall\ v_{j}=1\},

the index set of variables-of-interest, to which the null hypothesis pertains,

(C3)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{V}_{\boldsymbol{v}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{j\in\{1,\dots,\nu\}:v_{j}=0\}

and the posterior probability of model
\boldsymbol{s}
versus the other null models in
\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
,

(C4)

\displaystyle w_{\boldsymbol{s}}=\dfrac{\mathrm{PO}_{\boldsymbol{s}}}{\sum_{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{v}}}\mathrm{PO}_{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}}},

using which we can rewrite the scaled model-averaged posterior odds as

(C5)

\displaystyle\kappa_{n}\ \mathrm{PO}_{\mathcal{A}_{\boldsymbol{v}}:\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{v}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{t}\in\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{v}}}\ \sum_{\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{v}}}w_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}\ \kappa_{n}\ \mathrm{PO}_{\boldsymbol{s}+\boldsymbol{t}:\boldsymbol{s}}.

In the Doublethink model (MT Definition
7
), the prior odds of variable inclusion are
\mu
, independently for each variable, and the posterior odds are

(C6)

\displaystyle\mathrm{PO}_{\boldsymbol{s}}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle{\kappa_{n}}^{-|\boldsymbol{s}|}\ {R_{n,\boldsymbol{s}}}^{n/(n+h)}.

Notice that, in the proof, we use subscript
n
to highlight the finite-sample distributional properties of the MLR, and we drop the subscript
n
for the limiting distribution.

Step 2. Simplification of the posterior odds.

Step 2a. Independent case.
If we assume that the likelihood factorizes due to independence such that

(C7)

\displaystyle R_{n,{\boldsymbol{s}}}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{\nu}{R_{n,\boldsymbol{e}_{j}}}^{\boldsymbol{s}_{j}}

where
\{\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\}_{i}=\mathbb{I}(i=j)
, then since the prior factorizes due to independence such that

(C8)

\displaystyle\mu_{\boldsymbol{s}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mu^{|\boldsymbol{s}|},

the posterior odds have the property that

(C9)

\displaystyle\mathrm{PO}_{{\boldsymbol{s}}+\boldsymbol{t}:{\boldsymbol{s}}}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\mathrm{PO}_{{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}}+\boldsymbol{t}:{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}}}

for all
\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
and all
\boldsymbol{t}\in\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
.

Assuming the null hypothesis
\boldsymbol{v}
is true in the sense that the true parameters
\tilde{\theta}\in\omega_{\boldsymbol{v}}
, then we can define the true model
\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
such that
\tilde{\theta}\in\Theta_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}}
, where
\Theta_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}}\subseteq\omega_{\boldsymbol{v}}
. This allows us to simplify the scaled model-averaged posterior odds (Equation
C5
) to

(C10)

\displaystyle\kappa_{n}\ \mathrm{PO}_{\mathcal{A}_{\boldsymbol{v}}:\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{v}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{t}\in\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{v}}}\kappa_{n}\ \mathrm{PO}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}+\boldsymbol{t}:\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}}.

By the Johnson model, we have

(C11)

\displaystyle\kappa_{n}\ \mathrm{PO}_{\mathcal{A}_{\boldsymbol{v}}:\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{v}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{t}\in\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{v}}}{\kappa_{n}}^{1-|\boldsymbol{t}|}\ {R_{n,\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}+\boldsymbol{t}:\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}}}^{n/(n+h)}.

By Wilks’ theorem
(
24
;
23
)
, assuming standard regularity conditions, (MT Definition
4
),

(C12)

\displaystyle 2\log R_{n,\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}+\boldsymbol{t}:\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}}

\displaystyle\overset{d}{\rightarrow}

\displaystyle\chi^{2}_{|\boldsymbol{t}|},\qquad n\rightarrow\infty

where
\chi^{2}_{\nu}
represents a chi-squared distribution with
\nu
degrees of freedom. Equivalently,

(C13)

\displaystyle R_{n,\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}+\boldsymbol{t}:\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}}

\displaystyle\overset{d}{\rightarrow}

\displaystyle\textrm{LG}\left(\tfrac{|\boldsymbol{t}|}{2},1\right),\qquad n\rightarrow\infty,

where
\mathrm{LG}(a,b)
represents a log-gamma distribution with shape parameter
a
and rate parameter
b
. It follows that

(C14)

\displaystyle{R_{n,\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}+\boldsymbol{t}:\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}}}^{n/(n+h)}

\displaystyle\overset{d}{\rightarrow}

\displaystyle\textrm{LG}\left(\tfrac{|\boldsymbol{t}|}{2},1\right),\qquad n\rightarrow\infty.

Slutsky’s
(
19
)
theorem (see e.g.
21
) states that if
A_{n}\overset{d}{\rightarrow}A
,
B_{n}\overset{p}{\rightarrow}b
and

A_{n},B_{n},C_{n}

A

b
and

,
and if

A_{n}\ B_{n}+C_{n}\overset{d}{\rightarrow}A\ b+c
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. If

and
A_{n}
is bounded in probability,
then
A_{n}\ B_{n}+C_{n}\overset{p}{\rightarrow}c
.

We rewrite the scaled posterior odds (Equation
C11
) as

(C15)

\displaystyle\kappa_{n}\ \mathrm{PO}_{\mathcal{A}_{\boldsymbol{v}}:\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{v}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A_{n}+B_{n},

where

\displaystyle A_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{V}_{\boldsymbol{v}}}{R_{n,\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}+\boldsymbol{e}_{j}:\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}}}^{n/(n+h)},

\displaystyle B_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\phantom{\,:}\boldsymbol{t}\in\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{v}}\,:}}{{|\boldsymbol{t}|>1}}}{\kappa_{n}}^{1-|\boldsymbol{t}|}\ {R_{n,\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}+\boldsymbol{t}:\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}}}^{n/(n+h)}.

Noting that
\kappa_{n}^{1-|\boldsymbol{t}|}\overset{}{\rightarrow}\mathbb{I}(|\boldsymbol{t}|=1)
, then combining Wilks’ and Slutsky’s theorems, we have for
|\boldsymbol{t}|>1

(C16)

\displaystyle{\kappa_{n}}^{1-|\boldsymbol{t}|}\ {R_{n,\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}+\boldsymbol{t}:\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}}}^{n/(n+h)}

\displaystyle\overset{p}{\rightarrow}

\displaystyle 0,\qquad\qquad n\rightarrow\infty,

assuming
{R_{n,\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}+\boldsymbol{t}:\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}}}^{n/(n+h)}
is bounded in probability. Therefore by Slutsky’s theorem,
B_{n}\overset{p}{\rightarrow}b=0
.

By Wilks’ theorem, we have for
|\boldsymbol{t}|=1

(C17)

\displaystyle{R_{n,\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}+\boldsymbol{t}:\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}}}^{n/(n+h)}

\displaystyle\overset{d}{\rightarrow}

\displaystyle R_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}+\boldsymbol{t}:\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}},\qquad n\rightarrow\infty.

Since the independence assumption implies joint convergence in distribution of the summands in

to a limiting distribution

. This simplifies the scaled model-averaged posterior odds to the sum of maximized likelihood ratios involving one degree-of-freedom tests:

(C18)

\displaystyle\kappa_{n}\ \mathrm{PO}_{\mathcal{A}_{\boldsymbol{v}}:\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{v}}}

\displaystyle\overset{d}{\rightarrow}

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{V}_{\boldsymbol{v}}}R_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}+\boldsymbol{e}_{j}:\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}},\qquad n\rightarrow\infty.

Step 2b. General case.

C5
) as

(C19)

\displaystyle\kappa_{n}\ \mathrm{PO}_{\mathcal{A}_{\boldsymbol{v}}:\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{v}}}

\displaystyle A_{n}\ w_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}}+B_{n}\ w_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}}+C_{n},

\displaystyle A_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{V}_{\boldsymbol{v}}}{R_{n,\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}+\boldsymbol{e}_{j}:\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}}}^{n/(n+h)}

\displaystyle B_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\phantom{\,:}\boldsymbol{t}\in\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{v}}\,:}}{{|\boldsymbol{t}|>1}}}\kappa_{n}^{1-|\boldsymbol{t}|}\ {R_{n,\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}+\boldsymbol{t}:\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}}}^{n/(n+h)}

Assuming that

\displaystyle\overset{d}{\rightarrow}

\displaystyle\overset{p}{\rightarrow}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{t}\in\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{v}}}\kappa_{n}^{1-|\boldsymbol{t}|}\ {R_{n,\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}+\boldsymbol{t}:\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}}}^{n/(n+h)}\ w_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}}\ \left(\dfrac{\sum_{\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{v}}}\mathrm{PO}_{\boldsymbol{s}+\boldsymbol{t}}}{\mathrm{PO}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}+\boldsymbol{t}}}-1\right).
