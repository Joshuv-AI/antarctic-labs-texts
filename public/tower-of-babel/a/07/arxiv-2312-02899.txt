Ultra hypercyclic weighted backward shifts
Martin Liu, David Walmsley, James Xue

Abstract
Recently, new topological properties that an operator acting on a topological vector space can have were introduced: strong hypercyclicity and hypermixing. We introduce a new property called ultra hypercyclicity, and we compare it to the other common topological properties, including the new ones. For the family of weighted backward shifts on c 0 c_{0} and ℓ p \ell^{p} , we characterize ultra hypercyclicity in terms of the weight sequence. We further show that the family of strongly hypercyclic weighted backward shifts is distinct from that of the ultra hypercyclic weighted backward shifts, and frame the question of how to characterize strongly hypercyclic weighted backward shifts in terms of properties of the underlying weight sequence.

Introduction

Let
T:X\to X
be a continuous linear map (henceforth an
operator
) on a separable Banach space
X
. We are interested in the dynamics, or long-term behavior, of
T
; given some initial point
x\in X
, what can we say about the
orbit of
x
under
T
, given by
\orb(T,x)=\{x,Tx,T^{2}x,T^{3}x,\ldots\}
? For example, is
x

periodic
, meaning
T^{n}x=x
for some

? Or, for quite different behavior, could
\orb(T,x)
be dense in

? It turns out
\orb(x,T)
being dense is equivalent to each
return set

N(x,U)\coloneqq\{n\in\mathbb{N}:T^{n}x\in U\}
being nonempty for any nomempty open set
U
in
X
. With a more topological mindset, we might ask how the iterates of
T
“connect” two given nonempty open sets
U
and
V
in
X
; given nonempty open subsets
U
and
V
of
X
, the

is defined as

. We say

is

(resp.

; resp.

) if for all nonempty open subsets

and
V

is nonempty (resp. contains arbitrarily long intervals; resp. is cofinite). In our setting of a separable Banach space

, it turns out hypercyclicity is equivalent to topological transitivity by the Birkhoff transitivity theorem
[
6
]
. Hypercyclicity has become a very active area of research in operator theory that has many connections to other branches of mathematics. For more background on its history, central ideas, and a detailed introduction, we refer the interested reader to the monographs
[

,

]
.

2
Weighted backward shifts

The family of weighted backward shifts on
\ell^{p}
and
c_{0}
is the testing ground for any new notion in linear dynamics due to its simple yet rich structure. Recall that
c_{0}
is the space of all bounded sequences
(x_{n})_{n\geq 0}
in
\mathbb{C}
for which
x_{n}\to 0
, and the norm in
c_{0}
is given by
\|x\|=\sup_{n\geq 0}|x_{n}|
. For
1\leq p<\infty
,
\ell^{p}
is the space of all complex sequences
(x_{n})_{n\geq 0}
for which
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|x_{n}|^{p}<\infty
, and the norm on
\ell^{p}
is given by
\|x\|=(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|x_{n}|^{p})^{1/p}
.

Let
X
be
c_{0}
or one of the
\ell^{p}
spaces,
1\leq p<\infty
. We denote by
e_{n}
,
n\geq 0
, the canonical basis vector of
X
whose only nonzero term is a 1 in the
n
th position. Every
x=(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots)
in
X
can thus be written as
x=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}x_{i}e_{i}
. If
w=(w_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is a bounded sequence of positive numbers, the weighted backward shift
B_{w}
is defined on the basis of
X
by
B_{w}e_{0}=0
,
B_{w}e_{n}=w_{n}e_{n-1}
for
n\geq 1
. That
(w_{n})_{n}
is bounded is equivalent to
B_{w}
being continuous. We consider here only surjective weighted backward shifts; it is a straightforward exercise to show that
B_{w}
is surjective if and only if
\inf\{w_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}>0
. Each weighted backward shift has an associated weighted forward shift
S
defined on the basis of
X
by
Se_{n}=\frac{e_{n+1}}{w_{n+1}}
. When
B_{w}
is surjective,
S
is defined on all of
X
, and the forward shift is a right inverse for
B_{w}
on
X
, meaning
B_{w}S=I
, where
I
is the identity operator on
X
.

For simplicity in notations, we put
M_{i}^{j}=w_{i}w_{i+1}\cdots w_{i+j-1}
for
i,j\geq 1
; that is to say,
M_{i}^{j}
is the product of
j
consecutive weights, starting with the
i
th weight. Then for any
x\in X
, a formula for
S^{n}x
is given by

\displaystyle S^{n}x=(0,0,\ldots,0,\frac{x_{0}}{M_{1}^{n}},\frac{x_{1}}{M_{2}^{n}},\frac{x_{2}}{M_{3}^{n}},\ldots).

(2)

Furthermore, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
i<j
, by writing out the products in the following expressions, we have

\displaystyle M_{i}^{n}M_{i+n}^{j-i}=M_{i}^{n+j-i}=M_{i}^{j-i}M_{j}^{n}

(3)

Characterizing conditions for
B_{w}
to be hypercyclic or mixing are, respectively,

\displaystyle\text{hypercyclic: }\sup_{n\geq 1}M_{1}^{n}=\infty,\hskip 18.06749pt\text{mixing: }\lim_{n\to\infty}M_{1}^{n}=\infty.

For this result and much more background on the dynamics of backward shifts, we refer the interested reader to
[
8
, Section 4.1]
and the references therein.

A characterization of hypermixing weighted backward shifts was obtained by Ansari in

.

Theorem 2.1

(
[
2
, Theorems 4.1 and 4.4]
)
.

For a weighted backward shift
B_{w}
on
c_{0}
or
\ell^{p}
, the following are equivalent.

(i)

B_{w}
is hypermixing.

(ii)

B_{w}
is surjective and
S^{n}x\to 0
for every nonzero vector
x\in X
.

(iii)

\sup_{n\geq 1}M_{1}^{n}=\infty
and
\inf_{n,k\geq 1}M_{n}^{k}>0
.

Our goal is to develop theorems analogous to Ansari’s for the ultra hypercyclicity and strong hypercyclicity properties. Towards this goal, we first record a simple fact about surjective backward shifts.

Lemma 2.2
.

Suppose
B_{w}
is surjective on
\ell^{p}
or
c_{0}
. Then
S^{n_{k}}e_{j}\to 0
for all
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
if and only if
S^{n_{k}}e_{j}\to 0
for some
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. In terms of weights,
M_{j}^{n_{k}}\to\infty
for all
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
if and only if
M_{j}^{n_{k}}\to\infty
for some
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

Proof.

Since
B_{w}
is continuous and surjective,
\mu=\displaystyle\sup_{n\geq 1}|w_{n}|<\infty
and
\displaystyle\delta=\inf_{n\geq 1}|w_{n}|>0
. It suffices to show
M_{j}^{n_{k}}\to\infty
for some
j
implies
M_{i}^{n_{k}}\to\infty
for all
i
. Suppose
M_{j}^{n_{k}}\to\infty
and let
i\not=j
. We first consider the case when
i<j
. Rearranging equation (
3
), we have
M_{j}^{n_{k}}=\displaystyle\frac{M_{i+n_{k}}^{j-i}}{M_{i}^{j-i}}M_{i}^{n_{k}}\leq\left(\frac{\mu}{\delta}\right)^{j-i}M_{i}^{n_{k}}
, which proves
M_{i}^{n_{k}}\to\infty
. The case
i<j
is similar.
∎

The similarities between the definition of ultra hypercyclic and hypermixing lead immediately to the following “ultra hypercyclicity criterion.” Its proof is entirely similar to the proof of the hypermixing criterion in
[
2
, Theorem 2.3]
, so we omit it.

Theorem 2.3
.

Suppose
X
is a first countable topological vector space and
T:X\to X
is a surjective operator with right inverse map
S
. Then
T
is ultra hypercyclic for
(n_{k})
if and only if, for every nonzero vector
x\in X
and any
y\in X
, there exists a sequence
(u_{k})
in
X
such that
u_{k}\in\ker T^{n_{k}}
and
S^{n_{k}}x+u_{k}\to y
.

We employ the previous two results to characterize ultra hypercyclic weighted backward shifts on
c_{0}
and
\ell^{p}
,
1\leq p<\infty
.

Theorem 2.4
.

Suppose
B_{w}
is a weighted backward shift on
c_{0}
or
\ell^{p}
,
1\leq p<\infty
, and
(n_{k})
is an increasing sequence in
\mathbb{N}
. Then the following are equivalent.

(i)

B_{w}
is surjective and is ultra hypercyclic for
(n_{k})
.

(ii)

B_{w}
is surjective and for all nonzero
x\in c_{0}
,
S^{n_{k}}x\to 0
.

(iii)

\displaystyle\inf_{n\geq 1}w_{n}>0
,
\displaystyle\sup_{k\geq 1}M_{1}^{n_{k}}=\infty
, and
\displaystyle\limsup_{k\to\infty}\big(\inf_{i\geq 1}M_{i}^{n_{k}}\big)>0
.

Proof.

(i)
\implies
(ii). Assume
B_{w}
is surjective and ultra hypercyclic for
(n_{k})
. Let
x=(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots)\in X
be nonzero. Since the forward shift
S
is a right inverse for
B_{w}
on
X
, Theorem
2.3
implies the existence of a sequence of vectors
(u_{k})_{k}
in
\ker B_{w}^{n_{k}}
such that
S^{n_{k}}x+u_{k}\to 0
. The vector
u_{k}
must have the form
u_{k}=(u_{k,0},u_{k,1},\ldots,u_{k,n_{k}-1},0,0,\ldots)
. Then

\displaystyle S^{n_{k}}x+u_{k}=(u_{k,0},u_{k,1},\ldots,u_{k,n_{k}-1},\frac{x_{0}}{M_{1}^{n_{k}}},\frac{x_{1}}{M_{2}^{n_{k}}},\ldots).

Hence
\|S^{n_{k}}x\|\leq\|S^{n_{k}}x+u_{k}\|\to 0
.

(ii)
\implies
(i). We again use the ultra hypercyclicity criterion. Let
x\in c_{0}
be nonzero and let
y=(y_{0},y_{1},y_{2},\ldots)\in c_{0}
. Then
S^{n_{k}}x\to 0
, and the sequence of vectors
(u_{k})_{k}
given by
u_{k}=(y_{0},y_{1},\ldots,y_{n_{k}-1},0,0,\ldots)
belongs to
\ker B_{w}^{n_{k}}
and converges to

. Thus
S^{n_{k}}x+u_{k}\to 0+y=y
, which shows
B_{w}
is ultra hypercyclic for
(n_{k})
by Theorem
2.3
.

(ii)
\implies
(iii). We prove the contrapositive. We can assume
B_{w}
is surjective. Furthermore, we can assume
\sup_{k\geq 1}M_{1}^{n_{k}}=\infty
. If not, then by Lemma
B_{w}
does not satisfy the Hypercyclicity Criterion for
(n_{k})
, which implies
B_{w}
is not hypercyclic for
(n_{k})
and hence not ultra hypercyclic for
(n_{k})
.

So suppose that
\inf_{i}M_{i}^{n_{k}}\to 0
as
k\to\infty
. The rest of the argument is similar to
[
2
, Theorem 4.4]
. There exists an increasing sequence
(i_{k})
in
\mathbb{N}
such that
M_{i_{k}}^{n_{k}}\to 0
. Then there exists a subsequence converging to zero fast enough such that

\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}(M_{i_{k_{l}}}^{n_{k_{l}}})^{p}<\infty.

Then one can construct a vector
x\in\ell^{p}
for which
S^{n_{k_{l}}}x
always has a 1, so
S^{n_{k}}x\not\to 0
, so
B_{w}
is not ultra hypercyclic for
(n_{k})
.

(iii)
\implies
(ii). Let
X
be
c_{0}
or
\ell^{p}
,
p\geq 1
, and denote by
\|\cdot\|
the usual norm on
X
. Let
(n_{k})
be the sequence described in (iii). There exists some
r>0
for which
\inf_{i}M_{i}^{n_{k}}\geq r
for all
k
. One can quickly check that for any
x\in\ell^{p}
,
\|S^{n_{k}}x\|<\frac{\|x\|}{r}
.

Let
x=(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots)\in\ell^{p}
. Let
\epsilon>0
be given. There exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\|\sum_{i=N+1}^{\infty}x_{i}\|<\frac{1}{2}r\epsilon
. Let
x_{N}=\sum_{i=N+1}^{\infty}x_{i}
and
x_{N}^{\prime}=\sum_{i=0}^{N}x_{i}
, so that
x=x_{N}^{\prime}+x_{N}
. Then

\|S^{n_{k}}x_{N}\|\leq\frac{\|x_{N}\|}{r}<\frac{\epsilon}{2}.

Furthermore,
M_{j}^{n_{k}}\to\infty
for each
j=1,2,\ldots,N
by Lemma
2.2
, for large enough
n_{k}
, we can ensure

M_{j}^{n_{k}}\geq\frac{2\|x\|}{\epsilon}.

Hence
\|S^{n_{k}}x_{N}^{\prime}\|<\|x\|\frac{\epsilon}{2\|x\|}=\frac{\epsilon}{2}
for large enough
n_{k}
, and the two previous inequalities imply

\|S^{n_{k}}x\|\leq\|S^{n_{k}}x_{N}\|+\|S^{n_{k}}x_{N}^{\prime}\|<\epsilon,

provided
n_{k}
is large enough.
∎

Theorem 2.5

That (i)

(ii)
\implies
(iii). It suffices to show that if
\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\big(\inf_{i\geq 1}M_{i}^{n}\big)=0

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\big(\inf_{i\geq 1}M_{i}^{n}\big)=0

We next derive a sufficient condition for a weighted backward shift on
c_{0}
to be strongly hypercyclic.

Theorem 2.6
.

Suppose there exists a function
f:(0,1)\to(0,\infty)
with
\lim_{x\to 0^{+}}f(x)=+\infty
such that for all
\epsilon\in(0,1)
and for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that the following conditions hold:

•

M_{i}^{n}>f(\epsilon)
for all
i\leq N
, and

•

\epsilon<M_{i}^{n}
for all
i
.

Then
B_{w}
is strongly hypercyclic.

Proof.

Let
x=(x_{1},x_{1},x_{2},\ldots)\in c_{0}
be non-zero. We must show there exists an increasing sequence
(n_{k})
in
\mathbb{N}
such that
\|S^{n_{k}}x\|\to 0
. It suffices to show there exists a (not necessarily increasing) sequence
(n_{k})
in
\mathbb{N}
such that
\|S^{n_{k}}x\|\to 0
. Indeed, a necessary condition for
\|S^{n_{k}}x\|\to 0
is that no value in the sequence
(n_{k})
repeat infinitely often (otherwise
\|S^{n_{k}}x\|
would contain a constant subsequence), and if no value repeats infinitely often, then we must have
\lim_{k\to\infty}n_{k}=+\infty
, and so we may pass to an increasing subsequence of
(n_{k})
if necessary.

We construct the sequence
(n_{k})
inductively. Let
\epsilon_{1}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{\max(2,\|x\|)}
. Since
x\in c_{0}
, there exists a value
N_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
|x_{i-1}|\leq(\epsilon_{1})^{2}
whenever
i-1\geq N_{1}
. Then by our assumption, there exists
n_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

(A)

M_{i}^{n_{1}}>f(\epsilon_{1})
for all
i\leq N_{1}
, and

(B)

\epsilon_{1}<M_{i}^{n_{1}}
for all
i
.

Then for
i\leq N_{1}
, inequality (A) implies

\frac{|x_{i-1}|}{M_{i}^{n_{1}}}\leq\frac{\|x\|}{f(\epsilon_{1})}.

Furthermore, (B) implies that for
i-1\geq N_{1}
,

\frac{|x_{i-1}|}{M_{i}^{n_{1}}}\leq\frac{(\epsilon_{1})^{2}}{\epsilon_{1}}=\epsilon_{1}.

Since

S^{n_{1}}x=(0,0,\ldots,0,\frac{x_{0}}{M_{1}^{n_{1}}},\frac{x_{1}}{M_{2}^{n_{1}}},\ldots,\frac{x_{N_{1}-1}}{M_{N_{1}}^{n_{1}}},\frac{x_{N_{1}}}{M_{N_{1}+1}^{n_{1}}},\frac{x_{N_{1}+1}}{M_{N_{1}+2}^{n_{1}}},\ldots),

combining the previous two inequalities yields

\displaystyle\|S^{n_{1}}x\|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\left(\frac{x_{0}}{M_{1}^{n_{1}}},\frac{x_{1}}{M_{2}^{n_{1}}},\ldots,\frac{x_{N_{1}-1}}{M_{N_{1}}^{n_{1}}},0,0,\ldots\right)\right\|+\left\|\left(0,\cdots,0,\frac{x_{N_{1}}}{M_{N_{1}+1}^{n_{1}}},\frac{x_{N_{1}+1}}{M_{N_{1}+2}^{n_{1}}},\ldots\right)\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|x\|}{f(\epsilon_{1})}+\epsilon_{1}.

Now suppose positive numbers
\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{k-1}
, positive integers
N_{1},\ldots,N_{k-1}
, and positive integers
n_{1},\ldots,n_{k-1}
have been chosen. We define
\epsilon_{k}
and
N_{k}
as follows. Let
\epsilon_{k}=\min(\frac{1}{k\|x\|},\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{k-1})
. Then since
x\in c_{0}
, there exists an integer
N_{k}>\max(N_{1},\ldots,N_{k-1})
such that
|x_{i-1}|<(\epsilon_{k})^{2}
whenever
i-1\geq N_{k}
.

Then by our hypothesis, there exists
n_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
such that both

(A’)

M_{i}^{n_{k}}>f(\epsilon_{k})
for all
i\leq N_{k}
, and

(B’)

\epsilon_{k}<M_{i}^{n_{k}}
for all
i
.

By an entirely similar argument as above, one can show

\|S^{n_{k}}x\|\leq\frac{\|x\|}{f(\epsilon_{k})}+\epsilon_{k}.

Since
\epsilon_{k}\to 0
,
f(\epsilon_{k})\to+\infty
, and hence
\|S^{n_{k}}x\|\to 0
, as desired.
∎

Example 2.7
.

Consider the weight sequence
w=(w_{n})
defined as follows: if
n
is odd then
w_{n}=2
, and if
n
is even then
w_{n}=(2w_{\frac{n}{2}})^{-1}
. In other words

w_{n}=\begin{cases}2&\text{ if }w_{\frac{n}{2}}=\frac{1}{4};\\
\frac{1}{4}&\text{ if }w_{\frac{n}{2}}=2.\end{cases}

We will show
B_{w}
is strongly hypercyclic but not ultra hypercyclic. First, it is immediate from the definition of
(w_{n})
that, for every
i\in\mathbb{N}
:

w_{2i-1}w_{2i}=\frac{1}{w_{i}}.

(4)

Also, for every
i,k\in\mathbb{N}
, since
2i+2k-1
and
2i-1
are both odd, we have

M_{2i}^{2k}=\frac{w_{2i+2k-1}}{w_{2i-1}}M_{2i-1}^{2k}=\frac{2}{2}M_{2i-1}^{2k}=M_{2i-1}^{2k}.

Consequently, the identity
M_{2i-1}^{2k}=(M_{i}^{k})^{-1}
follows from replacing each
w_{j}
in the product
M_{i}^{k}
with
(w_{2j-1}w_{2j})^{-1}
using (
4
). Therefore for every
i,k\in\mathbb{N}
,

M_{2i-1}^{2k}=M_{2i}^{2k}=\frac{1}{M_{i}^{k}}.

(5)

From repeated applications of (
5
) we yield the following for every
i,k\in\mathbb{N}
:

M_{j}^{4^{m}k}=M_{i}^{k}\hskip 11.74988pt\text{for }j\in\{4^{m}(i-1)+1,\dots,4^{m}i\}.

(6)

Now we are ready to prove
B_{w}
is strongly hypercyclic but not ultra hypercyclic. We set
a_{1}=1
, and define
a_{k}=4a_{k-1}+1
for every
k\geq 2
. We claim that for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

M_{1}^{a_{k}}=2^{k}.

(7)

To see this, we induct on
k
. The base case
k=1
is clear. Let
k\geq 2
and assume
M_{1}^{a_{i}}=2^{i}
for every positive integer
i<k
. Since
a_{k}
is odd, by definition of
(w_{n})
, we have
w_{a_{k}}=2
. By (
6
), we also have
M_{1}^{4a_{k-1}}=M_{1}^{a_{k-1}}
, so

M_{1}^{a_{k}}=M_{1}^{a_{k}-1}\cdot w_{a_{k}}=M_{1}^{4a_{k-1}}\cdot 2=M_{1}^{a_{k-1}}\cdot 2=2^{k-1}\cdot 2=2^{k}.

This proves (
7
). We also claim for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

\min_{i}M_{i}^{a_{k}}\geq 4^{-k}.

(8)

Again we induct on
k
, and the base case is clear from definition of
(w_{n})
. Let
k\geq 2
and assume
\min_{i}M_{i}^{a_{j}}\geq 4^{-j}
for every positive integer
j<k
. Since
M_{i}^{a_{k}}=M_{i}^{a_{k}-1}w_{i+a_{k}-1}
for every positive integer
i
and
\min_{i}w_{i}=\frac{1}{4}
, we have

\min_{i}M_{i}^{a_{k}}\geq\min_{i}\frac{1}{4}M_{i}^{a_{k}-1}=\min_{i}\frac{1}{4}M_{i}^{4a_{k-1}}=\min_{i}\frac{1}{4}M_{i}^{a_{k-1}}=\frac{1}{4^{k}},

where the second-to-last equality follows from (
6
). This proves (
8
).

Next we verify the conditions of Theorem
2.6
are satisfied, which will imply that
B_{w}
is strongly hypercyclic. Define
f:(0,1)\to(0,\infty)
by
f(x)=(4\sqrt{x})^{-1}
. It is clear that
\lim_{x\to 0^{+}}f(x)=\infty
. Let
\epsilon\in(0,1)
and let
N\in\mathbb{N}
. Choose a positive integer
k
such that
2^{k-1}\leq(4\sqrt{\epsilon})^{-1}<2^{k}
, and choose a positive integer
m
such that
4^{m}\geq N
. Choose
n=4^{m}a_{k}
. By (
6
) and (
7
) we have:

M_{i}^{n}=2^{k}>\frac{1}{4\sqrt{\epsilon}}=f(\epsilon)\hskip 11.74988pt\text{for }i\in\{1,2,\dots,4^{m}\}.

Note that
2^{k-1}\leq\frac{1}{4\sqrt{\epsilon}}
implies

\epsilon\leq\frac{1}{4^{k+1}}<\frac{1}{4^{k}},

so by (
6
) and (
8
), we have for every
i\in\mathbb{N}
,

M_{i}^{n}\geq\frac{1}{4^{k}}>\epsilon.

Therefore the conditions of Theorem
2.6
are satisfied, which proves
B_{w}
is strongly hypercyclic.

Now we show that
B_{w}
is not ultra hypercyclic by showing condition (iii) of Theorem
2.4
is not satisfied. Note that (
5
) implies for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\frac{1}{M_{1}^{n}}=M_{1}^{2n}=M_{1}^{n}M_{n+1}^{n},

so for every
n\in\mathbb{N}

M_{n+1}^{n}=\frac{1}{(M_{1}^{n})^{2}}.

(9)

Therefore, if
(n_{k})
is such that
\lim_{k\to\infty}M_{1}^{n_{k}}=\infty
, then we must also have
\lim_{k\to\infty}M_{n_{k}+1}^{n_{k}}=0
. In particular, this implies if
(n_{k})
satisfies
\sup_{k\geq 1}M_{1}^{n_{k}}=\infty
, then we must have
\limsup_{k\to\infty}\left(\inf_{i}M_{i}^{n_{k}}\right)=0.
Thus
B_{w}
cannot be ultra hypercyclic by Theorem
2.4
.

This example shows that the family of ultra hypercyclic weighted backward shifts is a proper subset of the strongly hypercyclic weighted backward shifts. We invite the reader to try and characterize the strongly hypercyclic weighted backward shifts, and we ask whether condition (iii) in Theorem
2.5
is sufficient for strong hypercyclicity.
