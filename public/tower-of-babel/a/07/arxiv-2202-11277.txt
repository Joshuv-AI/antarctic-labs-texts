Minimax Optimal Quantization of Linear Models: Information-Theoretic Limits and Algorithms
Rajarshi Saha, Mert Pilanci, Andrea J. Goldsmith

Abstract
High-dimensional models often have a large memory footprint and must be quantized after training before being deployed on resource-constrained edge devices for inference tasks. In this work, we develop an information-theoretic framework for the problem of quantizing a linear regressor learned from training data ( 𝐗 , 𝐲 ) (\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y}) , for some underlying statistical relationship 𝐲 = 𝐗 ​ 𝜽 + 𝐯 \mathbf{y}=\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\theta}+\mathbf{v} . The learned model, which is an estimate of the latent parameter 𝜽 ∈ ℝ d \boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d} , is constrained to be representable using only B ​ d Bd bits, where B ∈ ( 0 , ∞ ) B\in(0,\infty) is a pre-specified budget and d d is the dimension. We derive an information-theoretic lower bound for the minimax risk under this setting and propose a matching upper bound using randomized embedding-based algorithms which is tight up to constant factors. The lower and upper bounds together characterize the minimum threshold bit-budget required to achieve a performance risk comparable to the unquantized setting. We also propose randomized Hadamard embeddings that are computationally efficient and are optimal up to a mild logarithmic factor of the lower bound. Our model quantization strategy can be generalized and we show its efficacy by extending the method and upper-bounds to two-layer ReLU neural networks for non-linear regression. Numerical simulations show the improved performance of our proposed scheme as well as its closeness to the lower bound.

The sizes of the trained models in high dimensional learning problems have witnessed a tremendous increase, easily consisting of at least

1.1
Significance and Related Work

Model compression techniques employ lossless and lossy source coding schemes to quantize the model parameters subject to bit-budget constraints in order to make them deployable on memory-constrained devices
[
6
,
7
]
.
We develop an information-theoretic framework for the problem of quantizing linear regressors.
Related to our work,
[
8
]
adopts a rate-distortion theoretic approach to answer the question:
What is the minimum number of bits required to compress a model to achieve a target distortion?

Their result for linear models
[
8
]
[§4.1] assumes the model parameters follow a Gaussian distribution and the different input features are uncorrelated.
Similarly,
[
9
]
provides empirical evidence to approximate the distribution of model parameters as an i.i.d. Laplacian, and consequently proposes a weight pruning scheme to compress a model without deteriorating performance.
However, modeling parameters as an i.i.d. sequence also ignores correlations between them, leading to sub-optimal performance in worst-case scenarios.

In contrast to these works, we answer the question:
What is the minimum achievable risk when a model is quantized according to a
pre-specified
bit budget of
B
bits per dimension?

Moreover, we adopt a minimax approach, and we do not assume any prior distributional knowledge about the latent parameter
\boldsymbol{\theta}
or the feature matrix
\mathbf{X}
.
Consequently, our proposed model quantization strategies are minimax optimal, i.e. optimal in a worst-case sense.
Our work can be seen as a generalization of Gaussian sequence models
[
10
]
to general least-squares regression models.

We also propose randomized subspace embedding-based model quantization algorithms that have a near optimal performance (up to constant factors) with respect to our minimax lower bound.
We note that our lower bound expression is comprised of two terms that can be interpreted as the sum of the
unavoidable learning risk
that is persistent even in the absence of any bit-budget constraint, and an
excess quantization risk
that depends on the bit-budget.
This suggests that we can have tight upper bounds that also have this separation property, by first learning the model parameters agnostic to any bit-budget constraints, and then subsequently quantizing them.
We use the idea of democratic (or Kashin) representations
[
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
]
to show that this hypothesis is indeed true.

A Minimax Risk Formulation for Comparing Learning Codes

Given training data
(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y})

Inspired from

4
Algorithms for Learning and Quantizing Linear Models

We now propose several learning codes that achieve

We first discuss the
\ell_{2}
-risk of a straightforward strategy in §

\boldsymbol{\theta}
from training data
(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y})

Such a naïve strategy is highly sub-optimal as its
\ell_{2}

Consequently, we propose a randomized projection based learning code called

in §
4.2

Although optimal, this scheme is not practically feasible and simply exhibits the
existence of a good learning code
, much like Shannon’s achievability proof for channel coding theorem
[
16
]
[Ch. 7].
We also propose:
Democratic Quantization (DQ)
and

in §
4.3
and §
4.4
.
Compared to
RCM
,

and
NDQ
introduce a slightly more structure into the codebooks generated, making them practically feasible.

DQ
achieves a dimension-independent worst-case
\ell_{2}
-risk with a computational complexity of
O(d^{2})
multiplications.
On the other hand,
NDQ
has a complexity of
O(d\log d)

Remark 1

In the absence of any bit-budget constraints, the least squares problem (

We analyze our proposed learning codes by assuming that the pseudoinverse

However, in practice, unconstrained least-squares problems can be solved via one of several optimization algorithms such as gradient descent, conjugate gradient, etc, and all of them eventually converge to the estimate given by the closed form solution.

4.1
Naive Learning and Quantization

We first look at a naïve strategy for learning and quantizing
\boldsymbol{\theta}
from
(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y})
.
We do this in two steps: learning and encoding

1.

the magnitude,
\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\rVert_{2}
, and

2.

the direction,
\boldsymbol{\theta}/\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\rVert_{2}
.

Learning and Encoding the Magnitude
.
For this, we make use of the fact that
(1/d)\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq c^{2}
.
Let
\sigma_{1}\geq\sigma_{2}\geq\ldots\geq\sigma_{d}
denote the singular values of
\mathbf{X}
.
Then an estimate of
\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\rVert_{2}^{2}
is given by,
\widehat{b}^{2}\triangleq\frac{1}{d}\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\rVert_{2}^{2}-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sigma_{i}^{-2}
.
This choice of
\widehat{b}^{2}
becomes apparent in the proof of Lemma
G
.
Now, we generate a codebook
{\cal B}=\{1/\sqrt{d},2/\sqrt{d},\ldots,\lceil c^{2}\sqrt{d}\rceil/\sqrt{d}\}
for encoding the magnitude, and encode
\widehat{b}^{2}
as,
\varphi_{{\cal B}}(\widehat{b}^{2})=\mathop{\rm arg\hskip 2.84526ptmin}_{b}\{\lvert b-\widehat{b}^{2}\rvert:b\in{\cal B}\}
.

\varphi_{{\cal B}}(\widehat{b}^{2})
returns an index from
\{1,\ldots,\lceil c^{2}\sqrt{d}\rceil\}
.
To decode, let
\widetilde{b}^{2}=\psi_{{\cal B}}(\varphi_{{\cal B}}(\widehat{b}^{2}))
, where
\psi_{{\cal B}}(i)
returns the
i^{th}
element in the codebook
{\cal B}
.
The strategy for learning and encoding
\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\rVert_{2}
is the same for other learning codes proposed subsequently in §
4.2
,
4.3
, and
4.4
.
For this reason, we state Lemma
G
in App.
G
that gives a tail probability bound on the deviation of the estimate
\widehat{b}^{2}
from
\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\rVert_{2}^{2}
.
We show that as
d\to\infty
,
\widehat{b}^{2}\to\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\rVert_{2}^{2}
with high probability (w.h.p.) and we use it in our analysis.

Uniform scalar quantization to encode the direction
.
To do this, we uniformly quantize the
shape

\mathbf{s}\triangleq\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}/\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\rVert_{2}
by allocating
B
-bits to each coordinate.
Let
\textbf{B}_{\infty}^{d}(1)
denote the
\ell_{\infty}
-ball of radius
1
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Since
\lVert\mathbf{s}\rVert_{\infty}\leq 1
, a
uniform quantizer
, denoted as
\text{Q}_{u,B}(\cdot)
, constructs the quantization lattice by choosing
M=2^{B}
points
\{u_{i}\}_{i=1}^{M}
along every dimension, given by
u_{i}=-1+(2i-1)\Delta/2
for
i=1,\ldots,M
, where
\Delta=2/M
is the
resolution
.
For
\mathbf{z}\equiv[z_{1}\ldots z_{d}]\in\textbf{B}_{\infty}^{d}(1)
,
\text{Q}_{u,B}
is defined as,

\text{Q}_{u,B}(\mathbf{z})\triangleq[z_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,z_{d}^{\prime}]^{\top};\hskip 2.84526ptz_{j}^{\prime}\triangleq\mathop{\rm arg\hskip 2.84526ptmin}_{u\in\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{M}\}}\lvert u-z_{j}\rvert

(7)

The worst-case quantization error of a uniform quantizer is
\Delta\sqrt{d}/2
.
We get the uniform scalar quantized direction as
\widetilde{\mathbf{s}}\triangleq\text{Q}_{u,B}(\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}/\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\rVert_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Getting the quantized model
.
Using the quantized magnitude
\widetilde{b}^{2}\in\mathbb{R}
and the quantized direction
\widetilde{\mathbf{s}}\triangleq\text{Q}_{u,B}(\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}/\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\rVert_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is obtained by multiplying and appropriately scaling as,

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\triangleq\sqrt{\frac{d\widetilde{b}^{4}}{\widetilde{b}^{2}+\sigma^{2}\xi/d}}\cdot\widetilde{\mathbf{s}}=\sqrt{\frac{d\widetilde{b}^{4}}{\widetilde{b}^{2}+\sigma^{2}\xi/d}}\cdot\text{Q}_{u,B}\left(\frac{\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}}{\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\rVert_{2}}\right),

(8)

where
\xi=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sigma_{i}^{-2}
.
Thm.
4.1
below gives a guarantee for this naïve strategy while showing that it can be highly suboptimal for large
d
due to the
O(d)
scaling of second term of the risk.

Theorem 4.1
.

For
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\theta}+\mathbf{v}
, where
\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq dc^{2}
, the output
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
of the naïve strategy satisfies

\mathbb{P}\left(R(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\boldsymbol{\theta})>\frac{2c^{2}\sigma^{2}}{\sigma^{2}+c^{2}\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}}+\frac{2{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}d}c^{4}\sigma_{\mathrm{max}}^{2}2^{-2B}}{\sigma^{2}+c^{2}\sigma_{\mathrm{max}}^{2}}+\frac{C_{n}}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\to 0

as
d\to\infty
for some constant
C_{n}
independent of
d
.

Its proof is given in App.
H
.
This (and all subsequent strategies) requires
\log c^{2}+(1/2)\log d
bits to quantize the magnitude and
dB
bits to quantize the direction.
The number of bits required per dimension is then
B+(\log c^{2})/d+(\log d)/(2d)\to B
as
d\to\infty
.
To ensure that the
excess risk due to quantization is of the same order of magnitude as the unavoidable learning risk
, we require,

\left(\frac{2c^{2}\sigma^{2}}{\sigma^{2}+c^{2}\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}}-\frac{c^{2}\sigma^{2}}{\sigma^{2}+c^{2}\sigma_{\mathrm{max}}^{2}}\right)+\frac{2dc^{4}\sigma_{\mathrm{max}}^{2}2^{-2B}}{\sigma^{2}+c^{2}\sigma_{\mathrm{max}}^{2}}\lesssim\frac{2K_{r}c^{2}\sigma^{2}}{\sigma^{2}+c^{2}\sigma_{\mathrm{max}}^{2}},

(9)

for some constant
K_{r}
.
This implies a minimum bit-budget requirement for the
naïve strategy
to be
B_{*}=\Omega\left(\log\left(d\omega^{2}\sigma_{\mathrm{max}}^{2}\right)\right)
, that scales logarithmically with
d
compared
B_{thr}=\Omega(1)
in eq. (
6
).

4.2
Random Correlation Maximization (RCM)

We now propose and analyze
RCM
, a random source coding scheme that achieves the lower bound (with an additive
\sqrt{(\log d)/d}
term) of Thm.
3.4
for well-conditioned matrices
\mathbf{X}
.

RCM
estimates
\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\rVert_{2}
exactly as in §
4.1
.
The difference lies in the learning strategy of the direction of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
.

Maximize random correlations to learn the direction
.
We do this by first generating a codebook
{\cal Y}
that consists of
2^{dB}
i.i.d. vectors drawn uniformly at random on the
d
-dimensional unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.
We learn and encode the direction of
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
by
\varphi_{{\cal Y}}\left(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y}\right)=\mathop{\rm arg\hskip 2.84526ptmax}_{\overline{\mathbf{y}}\in{\cal Y}}\left\langle\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y},\overline{\mathbf{y}}\right\rangle
, and decode it as
\widetilde{\mathbf{s}}=\psi_{{\cal Y}}\left(\varphi_{{\cal Y}}\left(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y}\right)\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
\psi_{{\cal Y}}\left(i\right)
returns the
i^{th}
element in the codebook
{\cal Y}
.

Getting the quantized model
.
Similar to §
4.1
, the model
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is obtained (after appropriate scaling) as,

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\sqrt{\frac{d\widetilde{b}^{4}(1-2^{-2B})}{\widetilde{b}^{2}+\sigma^{2}\xi/d}}\cdot\widetilde{\mathbf{s}}.

(10)

The following Thm.
4.2
shows that as
d\to\infty
, with high probability over the construction of the random codebook
{\cal Y}
, the
\ell_{2}
-risk of
RCM
is very close to the lower bound.

Theorem 4.2
.

For
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\theta}+\mathbf{v}
, where
\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq dc^{2}
, the output
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
of
RCM
satisfies,

\mathbb{P}\left(R(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\boldsymbol{\theta})>\frac{c^{2}\sigma^{2}}{\left(\sigma^{2}+c^{2}\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}\right)}+\frac{c^{4}\sigma_{\mathrm{max}}^{2}2^{-2B}}{\left(\sigma^{2}+c^{2}\sigma_{\mathrm{max}}^{2}\right)}+C\sqrt{\frac{\log d}{d}}\right)\to 0

as
d\to\infty
for some constant
C
independent of
d
.

Its proof is delegated to App.
I
.
As in (
9
), we can calculate
B_{*}
for
RCM
, given in Table
1
.
Thm.
4.2
guarantees the
existence of a good quantizer
with performance close to the lower bound of Thm.
3.4
.
However, implementing it requires exponential complexity since the codebook
{\cal Y}
comprises of
2^{dB}
random directions, and solving
\mathop{\rm arg\hskip 2.84526ptmax}_{\overline{\mathbf{y}}\in{\cal Y}}\left\langle\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y},\overline{\mathbf{y}}\right\rangle
requires us to search over all of them.

4.3
Democratic Quantization (DQ)

Since
RCM
is practically infeasible, we now propose
DQ
that gives us a close to optimal polynomial-time scheme.
In
DQ
, the scheme for estimating and quantizing the magnitude remains the same as in §
4.1
.
But instead of uniformly quantizing
\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}/\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\rVert_{2}
, we quantize the solution of:

\mathbf{s}_{d}\triangleq\mathop{\rm arg\hskip 2.84526ptmin}_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{\infty}\hskip 5.69054pt\text{subject to}\hskip 5.69054pt\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{S}\mathbf{x}.

(11)

Here,
D\geq d
and
\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times D}
is a
wide matrix
.
The solution of (
11
) is called the
democratic embedding
of
\mathbf{s}
w.r.t.
\mathbf{S}
.
It can be shown that w.h.p.,
\lVert\mathbf{s}_{d}\rVert_{\infty}\leq K_{u}/\sqrt{d}
for some constant
K_{u}
that depends on the choice of
\mathbf{S}
.
Compared to the naïve strategy which uniformly quantized
\mathbf{s}
to get
\widetilde{\mathbf{s}}
,
DQ
uniformly quantizes the random embedding
\mathbf{s}_{d}
instead.
Previously,
\lVert\mathbf{s}\rVert_{\infty}\leq 1
required the
naïve
quantizer to have a dynamic range of
[-1,+1]
, but
\lVert\mathbf{s}_{d}\rVert_{\infty}\leq K_{u}/\sqrt{d}
lets us choose the dynamic range of the uniform quantizer as
[-K_{u}/\sqrt{d},+K_{u}/\sqrt{d}]
instead.
This reduced dynamic range significantly improves the resolution.
From the quantized embedding, an estimate of
\mathbf{s}
can be obtained by taking the inverse embedding.
We denote this by
\text{Q}_{d,B}(\cdot)
and the decoded direction is,

\widetilde{\mathbf{s}}=\text{Q}_{d,B}(\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}/\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\rVert_{2})\triangleq\mathbf{S}\cdot\text{Q}_{u,B}(\mathbf{s}_{d}).

(12)

We discuss democratic embeddings in detail and relevant choices of
\mathbf{S}
in App.
J
.
In particular, we show that the worst case quantization error is
\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{s}}-\text{Q}_{d,B}(\mathbf{s})\rVert_{2}\leq O(1)
, a constant that does not scale with dimension, unlike
O(\sqrt{d})
for naïve uniform quantizers.

Getting the quantized model
.
As before in §
4.1
,
DQ
obtains the final quantized model according to eq. (
8
), where
\widetilde{\mathbf{s}}
is given by (
12
).
Thm.
4.3
below shows that the
\ell_{2}
-risk of this strategy is close to the lower bound of Thm.
3.4
up to constant factors.

Theorem 4.3
.

For
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\theta}+\mathbf{v}
, where
\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq dc^{2}
, the output
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
of the
DQ
satisfies,

\mathbb{P}\left(R(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\boldsymbol{\theta})>\frac{2c^{2}\sigma^{2}}{\sigma^{2}+c^{2}\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}}+\frac{16K_{u}^{2}c^{4}\sigma_{\mathrm{max}}^{2}2^{-2B/\lambda}}{\sigma^{2}+c^{2}\sigma_{\mathrm{max}}^{2}}+\frac{C_{dq}}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\to 0,

as
d\to\infty
for some constant
C_{dq}
independent of
d
.

We prove this in App.
K
.
Here,
K_{u}
is a constant (precisely, the upper Kashin constant of
\mathbf{S}
as defined in App.
J
) and
\lambda=D/d
, that depends on the choice of user, and is usually chosen very close to
1
.

4.4
Near-Democratic Quantization (NDQ)

Although
DQ
requires
O(d^{2})
multiplications which is much better than
RCM
, it can still be computationally prohibitive for large
d
, especially when compared to the
O(d)
complexity of the naïve strategy.
To get a
near-democratic embedding
, an
\ell_{2}
relaxed version of (
11
) is solved as follows,

\mathbf{s}_{nd}\triangleq\mathop{\rm arg\hskip 2.84526ptmin}_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}\hskip 5.69054pt\text{subject to}\hskip 5.69054pt\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{S}\mathbf{x}.

(13)

The solution of (
13
) can be found in a closed form (App.
L
) as
\mathbf{s}_{nd}=\mathbf{S}^{\top}\mathbf{s}
.
Although
\mathbf{S}
can be one of several random matrices as discussed in App.
J
, we specifically consider the construction
\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times D}
.
Here,
\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times D}
is the sampling matrix obtained by randomly selecting
d
rows of
\mathbf{I}_{D}
,
\mathbf{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times D}
is a diagonal matrix whose entries are randomly chosen to be
\pm 1
with equal probability, and
\mathbf{H}
is the
D\times D
Hadamard matrix with normalized entries, i.e.
H_{ij}=\pm 1/\sqrt{D}
.
We let
D=2^{\lceil\log d\rceil}
so that
\mathbf{H}
can be constructed.
Using the fast Walsh-Hadamard transform,
\mathbf{s}_{nd}
can be computed with just
O(d\log d)
additions and sign flips, which is near-linear complexity.

We show in App.
L
that
\lVert\mathbf{s}_{nd}\rVert_{\infty}\leq 2\sqrt{(\log D)/D}
, and we again reduce the dynamic range.

NDQ
uniformly quantizes
\mathbf{s}_{nd}
using
B
bits per dimension, and takes an inverse embedding.
Precisely,

\widetilde{\mathbf{s}}=\text{Q}_{nd,B}\left(\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}/\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\rVert_{2}\right)\triangleq\mathbf{S}\cdot\text{Q}_{u,B}(\mathbf{s}_{nd}).

(14)

The worst-case quantization error can be shown to be
\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{s}}-\text{Q}_{nd,B}(\mathbf{s})\rVert_{2}\leq O(\sqrt{\log d})
, where
\text{Q}_{nd,B}
denotes
NDQ
.
Compared to
DQ
, the logarithmic factor is a mild price to pay considering the significant computational savings.
The final quantized model is obtained in the same way as before according to eq. (
8
), where
\widetilde{\mathbf{s}}
is given by (
14
).
Thm.
4.4
below gives an upper bound to the risk.

Theorem 4.4
.

For data
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\theta}+\mathbf{v}
, where
\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq dc^{2}
, the output
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
of
NDQ
satisfies,

\mathbb{P}\left(R(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\boldsymbol{\theta})>\frac{2c^{2}\sigma^{2}}{\sigma^{2}+c^{2}\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}}+\frac{64c^{4}\sigma_{\mathrm{max}}^{2}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\log(2\lambda d)}2^{-\frac{2B}{\lambda}}}{\sigma^{2}+c^{2}\sigma_{\mathrm{max}}^{2}}+\frac{C_{nd}}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\to 0

as
d\to\infty
for some constant
C_{nd}
independent of
d
.

The proof of
4.4
is provided in App.
M
.
It also implies that the minimum budget requirement for
NDQ
is
B_{*}=\Omega\left(\log\log d\right)
, very close to the lower bound of
B_{thr}=\Omega(1)
for practical values of
d
.

Remark 2
.

We can assume that both the encoder and decoder know
\mathbf{S}
.
If quantization constraints are imposed by a communication channel
[
2
]
,
\mathbf{S}
need not be communicated between them; it can be generated with shared randomness using initially agreed upon seeds.
Hence, it does not count towards the total bit-requirement of the quantized model.
Moreover, computing
\mathbf{P}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{H}(\cdot)
requires Hadamard transform and sign-flips which can be done efficiently on hardware.
Although specifying
\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{D}
for sampling & sign-flips requires
d\log(D)
bits, it can be done using in-place computation or circuits and does not require memory hardware like RAM.

5
Numerical Simulations

To numerically validate our results, we compare learning codes for several different settings.

Linear Regression
.
In Fig.
3
, we consider learning and subsequently quantizing an estimate
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
from the training data
(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y})
, where the response
\mathbf{y}
is generated according to
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\theta}+\mathbf{v}
.
We consider
n=d=128,c=1,\sigma=1
, and all the plots have been averaged over
10
realizations.
In Figs.
3(a)
,
3(b)
, and
3(c)
,
\mathbf{X}
is either an identity matrix, or a perturbed orthonormal matrix.
When
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{I}_{n}
,
\sigma_{\mathrm{max}}=\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}
, and for perturbed orthonormal, we have
\sigma_{\mathrm{max}}\approx\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}
.
The coordinates of the latent parameter
\boldsymbol{\theta}
are drawn i.i.d., either from Student-t, or from
{\cal N}(0,1)
and then cubed, i.e.
{\cal N}(0,1)^{3}
.

We plot the results of naïve uniform quantization and near-democratic model quantization.
For
NDQ
, we distinguish between the cases when
\mathbf{S}
used to obtain the near-democratic embedding in (
13
) is

1.

Random orthonormal, and

2.

Randomized Hadamard (
\mathbf{P}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{H}
).

A random Haar orthonormal matrix is constructed by first obtaining an
n\times d
matrix with i.i.d.
{\cal N}(0,1)
entries, computing its singular value decomposition, and then multiplying the matrices of its left and right singular vectors.
For comparison, we also plot the
lower bound
of Thm.
3.4
.
There is a noticeable difference in performance between the
naïve
and
randomized embedding-based
strategies, especially at low bit-budgets
B
.
When
B<1
, i.e. less than
1
-bit is available to quantize each coordinate, some coordinates are randomly set to
0
, so that the average bit requirement of the learning code is still
B
.

Remark 3
.

We consider a minimax framework for our lower bound.
In our setup, for some small
\varepsilon>0
, consider
\boldsymbol{\theta}=[\left(\varepsilon/(d-1)\right)^{1/2},\ldots,(c^{2}d-\varepsilon)^{1/2},\ldots,\left(\varepsilon/(d-1)\right)^{1/2}]
where just one arbitrary entry is large and
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rVert_{\infty}\approx c\sqrt{d}
.
The worst-case
\boldsymbol{\theta}
satisfies
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rVert_{2}\approx\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rVert_{\infty}c\sqrt{d}
, as naïve quantization yields the worst case error
\left\lVert Q(\boldsymbol{\theta})-\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rVert_{2}\approx 2^{(1-B)}c\sqrt{d}
.

Student-t
and
{\cal N}(0,1)^{3}
are heavy-tailed distributions.
When
\boldsymbol{\theta}
is sampled from either of them, its coordinate magnitudes are distributed very non-uniformly, i.e. close to worst-case, and we compare this case with the minimax lower bound.

Neural-Network
.
Our proposed randomized embedding-based quantization schemes can be extended beyond linear regression models, to other general settings where no prior distributional information about the model weights is known.
To show this, in Fig.
4(a)
, we train a
2
-layer densely connected ReLU neural network (
32
neurons) with scalar output for non-linear regression to predict the fuel efficiency of cars from parameters like
horsepower, weight
, etc for the
auto_mpg
dataset from UCI Machine Learning repository
[
18
]
.
We do layer-wise quantization and treat the weights and biases of each layer separately.
To quantize the model parameters, we first vectorize the weight matrix of any specific layer, and then use randomized Hadamard-based
NDQ
.

Conclusions

In this work, we considered model quantization for least-squares regression, where the solution is constrained to be representable using a pre-specified budget of
B
-bits per dimension.
For the setting when the observations are noisy linear measurements of some true regressor, we obtained an information-theoretic lower bound for the minimax
\ell_{2}
-risk of learning under quantization constraints.
We saw that the
\ell_{2}
-risk can be separated as the sum of two terms: the
unavoidable learning risk
and the
excess quantization risk
.
We subsequently proposed several algorithms for model quantization and compared their performance in terms of the worst-case
\ell_{2}
-risk and computational requirements.
Our results showed the tightness of our lower bound as well as the minimax optimality of our proposed algorithms.
Although naïve quantization is computationally simple, its error is sub-optimal for large
d
.
Contrastingly,
RCM
and
DQ
yield small quantization errors, but require exponential and quadratic computations, respectively.
We proposed
randomized Hadamard
-based
NDQ
as a practically feasible strategy with near-optimal error.
Finally, we showed that our method and upper bounds can be extended for non-linear regression using
2
-layer densely connected ReLU neural networks.

We compare the performance of different quantization schemes using
coefficient of determination
, denoted as

as our evaluation metric.
The

It is defined as follows.
For a given feature matrix
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
with rows
\{\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n}\}
, suppose a given model

is the mean response.

(16)

On the other hand, a trivial baseline model, which always predicts
\overline{y}

Regression models that have worse predictions than this baseline will have negative

Proof of Lemma
3.1
: An inequality between the mutual information
I(\mathbf{y};{\mathsf{Q}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y}))
and the bit-budget
B

Given training data
(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y})
,
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\triangleq{\mathsf{Q}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y})
is the output of the learning code
{\mathsf{Q}}\in{\cal Q}_{n,d,B}
and serves as an estimate of the latent parameter
\boldsymbol{\theta}
that generated the training data according to (
NPM
).
For any such learning code
{\mathsf{Q}}
, since
{\mathsf{Q}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y})
takes at most
2^{dB}
values,

I(\mathbf{y};{\mathsf{Q}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y}))=H({\mathsf{Q}}(\mathbf{y}))-H({\mathsf{Q}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y})|\mathbf{y})\leq H({\mathsf{Q}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y}))\leq dB.

(17)

The last inequality holds true since entropy of a discrete random variable is maximized when it is distributed uniformly over the domain.

Appendix D
Proof of Lemma
3.2
: Worst-case risk over the parameter space
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
is asymptotically lower bounded by the Bayes risk over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for Gaussian prior

The proof of this lemma parallels the arguments in the proof of Thm. 3.1 in
[
10
]
.
Let
\overline{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}:=\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\boldsymbol{\Theta}
.
We have,

\displaystyle\int_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}R({\mathsf{Q}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y}),\boldsymbol{\theta})dF(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\boldsymbol{\Theta}}R({\mathsf{Q}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y}),\boldsymbol{\theta})dF(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\int_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\overline{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}}R({\mathsf{Q}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y}),\boldsymbol{\theta})dF(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\boldsymbol{\Theta}}R({\mathsf{Q}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y}),\boldsymbol{\theta})+\int_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\overline{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}}R({\mathsf{Q}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y}),\boldsymbol{\theta})dF(\boldsymbol{\theta})

(18)

It then suffices to show that
\int_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\overline{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}}R({\mathsf{Q}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y}),\boldsymbol{\theta})dF(\boldsymbol{\theta})\to 0
as
d\to\infty
.
For a fixed
\delta\in(0,1)
, define the distribution
F_{\delta}\equiv{\cal N}(\mathbf{0},c^{2}\delta^{2}\mathbf{I}_{d})
.
Suppose
\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim F_{\delta}
.
We then have,

\displaystyle\int_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\overline{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}}R({\mathsf{Q}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y}),\boldsymbol{\theta})dF_{\delta}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\int_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\overline{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}}\left[\left\lVert{\mathsf{Q}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y})-\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right]dF_{\delta}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{d}\int_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\overline{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}dF_{\delta}(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\frac{2}{d}\int_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\overline{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}}\left[\left\lVert{\mathsf{Q}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y})\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right]dF_{\delta}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\frac{2}{d}\sqrt{\int_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\overline{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}}dF_{\delta}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}\sqrt{\int_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rVert_{2}^{4}dF_{\delta}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}}_{T_{1}}+\underbrace{2c^{2}\int_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\overline{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}}dF_{\delta}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}_{T_{2}},

(19)

where the last inequality follows from Cauchy-Schwarz inequality for probability measures and the fact that
{\mathsf{Q}}:\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\boldsymbol{\Theta}
, implying
\frac{1}{d}\lVert{\mathsf{Q}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y})\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq c^{2}
.
To upper bound
T_{2}
, note that,

\displaystyle\int_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\overline{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}}dF_{\delta}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim F_{\delta}}\left[\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\theta_{i}^{2}>c^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim F_{\delta}}\left[\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(\left(\frac{\theta_{i}}{\delta c}\right)^{2}-1\right)>\frac{1-\delta^{2}}{\delta^{2}}\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{(i)}}}{{\leq}}2\exp\left(-\frac{d(1-\delta^{2})^{2}}{8\delta^{4}}\right).

Here,
\mathrm{(i)}
follows from the concentration inequality: If
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{d}\sim{\cal N}(0,1)
and
t\in(0,1)
, then
\mathbb{P}\left(\left\lvert\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}(Z_{i}^{2}-1)\right\rvert>t\right)\leq 2e^{-dt^{2}/8}
.
On the other hand, to bound
T_{1}
, note that,

\displaystyle\int_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rVert_{2}^{4}dF_{\delta}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim F_{\delta}}\left[\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rVert_{2}^{4}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}_{F_{\delta}}\left[\theta_{i}^{4}\right]+\sum_{i\neq j}\mathbb{E}_{F_{\delta}}\left[\theta_{i}^{2}\right]\cdot\mathbb{E}_{F_{\delta}}\left[\theta_{j}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{F_{\delta}}\left[\theta_{1}^{4}\right]\cdot d+\binom{d}{2}c^{4}\delta^{4}=O(d^{2}).

So, the R.H.S. of (
D
) can be upper bounded by,

\int_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\overline{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}}R({\mathsf{Q}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y}),\boldsymbol{\theta})dF(\boldsymbol{\theta})\leq\frac{2}{d}\sqrt{2}\exp\left(-\frac{d(1-\delta^{2})^{2}}{16\delta^{4}}\right)O(d)+2c^{2}\exp\left(-\frac{d(1-\delta^{2})^{2}}{8\delta^{4}}\right)

for any
\delta\in(0,1)
.
This implies
\int_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\overline{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}}R({\mathsf{Q}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y}),\boldsymbol{\theta})dF(\boldsymbol{\theta})\to 0
as
d\to\infty
.

Appendix E
Proof of Lemma

: Lower bound on mutual information

for Gaussian prior over
\boldsymbol{\theta}

Given the training data
(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y})
, for a prior distribution
\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim F_{\delta}\equiv{\cal N}(\mathbf{0},c^{2}\delta^{2}\mathbf{I}_{d})
, the Bayes optimal estimator is the maximum likelihood (ML) estimator denoted as
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
.
Since
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\theta}+\mathbf{v}
where
\mathbf{v}\sim{\cal N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n})
, it can be easily shown
[
26
]
that the posterior distribution of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
given
\mathbf{y}
is
{\cal N}(\overline{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\overline{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})
where,

\displaystyle\overline{\boldsymbol{\mu}}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{c^{2}\delta^{2}}\mathbf{I}_{d}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{y},

(20)

\displaystyle\overline{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}\left(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{c^{2}\delta^{2}}\mathbf{I}_{d}\right)^{-1}.

(21)

The ML estimator is then given by
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y}\right]=\overline{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
.
To begin with, we state the following two lemmas that will be required for our analysis.
Their proofs are deferred to §
E.1
and §
E.2
.

{lemma_boxed}

For the linear model
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\theta}+\mathbf{v}
and Gaussian prior
\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim F_{\delta}\equiv{\cal N}(\mathbf{0},c^{2}\delta^{2}\mathbf{I}_{d})
, the differential entropy of the ML estimator
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y}\right]
is given by,

h(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\frac{1}{2}\log\left((2\pi e)^{d}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\frac{c^{4}\delta^{4}\sigma_{i}^{2}}{\sigma^{2}+c^{2}\delta^{2}\sigma_{i}^{2}}\right),

(22)

where
\{\sigma_{i}\}_{i\in[d]}
denote the singular values of the feature matrix
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
.

{lemma_boxed}

Under conditions of Lemma
E
, the
\ell_{2}
-risk of the ML estimator
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is given by,

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}}\left[\lVert\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}\rVert_{2}^{2}\right]=\textrm{Tr}\left[\textrm{Cov}\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{c^{2}\delta^{2}\sigma^{2}}{\sigma^{2}+c^{2}\delta^{2}\sigma_{i}^{2}},

(23)

where
\{\sigma_{i}\}_{i\in[d]}
denote the singular values of the feature matrix
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
,
\textrm{Tr}\left[\cdot\right]
denotes the trace of a matrix, and
\textrm{Cov}\left(\cdot\right)
is the covariance.

For a prior distribution
\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim F_{\delta}\equiv{\cal N}(\mathbf{0},c^{2}\delta^{2}\mathbf{I}_{d})
, the Bayes risk for any estimate
\boldsymbol{\zeta}\in\boldsymbol{\Theta}
is,

\int_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}R(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\theta})dF_{\delta}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim F_{\delta}}\left[\frac{1}{d}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\zeta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{d}\textrm{Tr}\left[\textrm{Cov}\left(\boldsymbol{\zeta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)\right]

(24)

Consider the decomposition of this error about the Bayes optimal estimator
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y}\right]=\overline{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
.
Writing
\boldsymbol{\zeta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\boldsymbol{\zeta}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})+(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta})
, we have,

\textrm{Cov}\left(\boldsymbol{\zeta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)=\textrm{Cov}\left(\boldsymbol{\zeta}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right)+\textrm{Cov}\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)-\mathbb{E}\left[(\boldsymbol{\zeta}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta})^{\top}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta})(\boldsymbol{\zeta}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})^{\top}\right]

(25)

Note that the first of the cross terms is

\mathbb{E}\left[(\boldsymbol{\zeta}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta})^{\top}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{\zeta}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\big|\mathbf{y}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}\big|\mathbf{y}\right]^{\top}\right]=\mathbf{0}

(26)

This holds true because we require
p(\boldsymbol{\zeta}|\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})=p(\boldsymbol{\zeta}|\mathbf{y})
, i.e. given
\mathbf{X}
,
\boldsymbol{\theta}\to\mathbf{y}\to\boldsymbol{\zeta}
forms a Markov chain.
So,
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}
are conditionally independent given
\mathbf{y}
, and since
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is a deterministic function of
(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y})
, consequently,
(\boldsymbol{\zeta}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
and
(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta})
are also conditionally independent given
\mathbf{y}
.
Moreover, from our choice of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y}\right]
, we have
\mathbf{E}\left[\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}\big|\mathbf{y}\right]=\mathbf{0}
.
Similarly, the other cross-term also vanishes and we have from eqs. (
24

25
),

\int_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}R(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\theta})dF_{\delta}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{1}{d}\textrm{Tr}\left[\textrm{Cov}\left(\boldsymbol{\zeta}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right)\right]+\frac{1}{d}\textrm{Tr}\left[\textrm{Cov}\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)\right].

(27)

We will use eq. (
27

Now, to derive a lower bound on

\displaystyle I(\mathbf{y};\boldsymbol{\zeta})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{(i)}}}{{\geq}}I\left(\left(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{c^{2}\delta^{2}}\mathbf{I}_{d}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{y};\boldsymbol{\zeta}\right)

\displaystyle=h(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})-h(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}|\boldsymbol{\zeta})=h(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})-h(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\zeta}|\boldsymbol{\zeta})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{(ii)}}}{{\geq}}h(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})-h(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\zeta})

Inequality

[

\mathrm{(ii)}

\displaystyle h(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\zeta})

\displaystyle\leq h\left({\cal N}\left(\mathbf{0},\textrm{Cov}\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\zeta}\right)\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log\left((2\pi e)^{d}\left|\textrm{Cov}\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\zeta}\right)\right|\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{(i)}}}{{\leq}}\frac{1}{2}\log\left((2\pi e)^{d}\left(\frac{1}{d}\textrm{Tr}\left[\textrm{Cov}\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\zeta}\right)\right]\right)^{d}\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{(ii)}}}{{=}}\frac{d}{2}\log(2\pi e)+\frac{d}{2}\log\left(\int_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}R(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\theta})dF_{\delta}(\boldsymbol{\theta})-\frac{1}{d}\textrm{Tr}\left[\textrm{Cov}\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)\right]\right).

\geq

\mathrm{(ii)}

27

So, (

(29)

From Lemmas

\displaystyle I(\mathbf{y};\boldsymbol{\zeta})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\log\left((2\pi e)^{d}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\frac{c^{4}\delta^{4}\sigma_{i}^{2}}{\sigma^{2}+c^{2}\delta^{2}\sigma_{i}^{2}}\right)-\frac{d}{2}\log(2\pi e)

\displaystyle\hskip 99.58464pt-\frac{d}{2}\log\left(\int_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}R(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\theta})dF_{\delta}(\boldsymbol{\theta})-\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{c^{2}\delta^{2}\sigma^{2}}{\sigma^{2}+c^{2}\delta^{2}\sigma_{i}^{2}}\right)

E.1
Proof of Lemma
E
: Differential entropy of ML estimator for Gaussian prior

The ML estimator is given by
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\left(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{c^{2}\delta^{2}}\mathbf{I}_{d}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{y}
.
Since
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\theta}+\mathbf{v}
where
\mathbf{v}\sim{\cal N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n})
, and the prior is
\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim{\cal N}(\mathbf{0},c^{2}\delta^{2}\mathbf{I}_{d})
, this implies that
\mathbf{y}
is a Gaussian vector with zero mean and covariance given by,

\displaystyle\textrm{Cov}\left(\mathbf{y}\right)=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[(\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\theta}+\mathbf{v})(\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\theta}+\mathbf{v})^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{X}\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{\theta}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}\right]\mathbf{X}^{\top}+\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{v}\mathbf{v}^{\top}\right]=c^{2}\delta^{2}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}+\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n}

(30)

So, for fixed
\mathbf{X}
,
\textrm{Cov}\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right)
is given by,

\displaystyle\textrm{Cov}\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{c^{2}\delta^{2}}\mathbf{I}_{d}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}^{\top}\right]\mathbf{X}\left(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{c^{2}\delta^{2}}\mathbf{I}_{d}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{c^{2}\delta^{2}}\mathbf{I}_{d}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}(c^{2}\delta^{2}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}+\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n})\mathbf{X}\left(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{c^{2}\delta^{2}}\mathbf{I}_{d}\right)^{-1}.

(31)

Consider the singular value decomposition of
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}(n\geq d)
to be
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^{\top}
, where
\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
, and
\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
.
Here
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
is a diagonal matrix comprising of singular values
\geq\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{d}
such that
\sigma_{1}\leq\sigma_{\mathrm{max}}
and
\sigma_{d}\geq\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}
.
Then,
\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{V}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\top}\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^{\top}=\mathbf{V}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}\mathbf{V}^{\top}
, where
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
.
Note that without loss of generality, we can assume
\mathbf{X}
is full column rank, i.e. all its singular values are positive.
This implies
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}
is full rank since
n\geq d
, giving us,

\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{c^{2}\delta^{2}}\mathbf{I}_{d}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{V}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{c^{2}}\mathbf{I}_{d}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{V}^{\top},

\displaystyle\text{ and, }\hskip 22.76219ptc^{2}\delta^{2}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}+\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{U}(c^{2}\delta^{2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\top}+\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n})\mathbf{U}^{\top}.

(32)

Substituting these expressions in eq. (
E.1
) yields,

\textrm{Cov}\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right)=\mathbf{V}\left[\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{c^{2}\delta^{2}}\mathbf{I}_{d}\right)^{-1}\left(c^{2}\delta^{2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}^{2}+\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}\right)\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{c^{2}\delta^{2}}\mathbf{I}_{d}\right)^{-1}\right]\mathbf{V}^{\top}

(33)

Since the determinant of a matrix is the product of its eigenvalues, this gives us:

\displaystyle\left\lvert\textrm{Cov}\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right)\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left[\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left(\sigma_{i}^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{c^{2}\delta^{2}}\right)\right]^{-1}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\sigma_{i}^{2}\left(c^{2}\delta^{2}\sigma_{i}^{2}+\sigma^{2}\right)\left[\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left(\sigma_{i}^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{c^{2}\delta^{2}}\right)\right]^{-1}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\frac{c^{4}\delta^{4}\sigma_{i}^{2}}{\sigma^{2}+c^{2}\delta^{2}\sigma_{i}^{2}}.

(34)

Since the ML estimator
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is a Gaussian random variable, substituting (
E.1
) in the expression for the entropy
h(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\frac{1}{2}\log\left((2\pi e)^{d}\left\lvert\textrm{Cov}\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right)\right\rvert\right)
completes the proof.

E.2
Proof of Lemma
E
:
\ell_{2}
-risk of ML estimator under Gaussian prior

To find a closed form expression for
\textrm{Tr}\left[\textrm{Cov}\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)\right]
, where
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\theta}+\mathbf{v}
,
\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim{\cal N}(\mathbf{0},c^{2}\delta^{2}\mathbf{I}_{d})
,
\mathbf{v}\sim{\cal N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n})
, and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y}\right]=\left(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{c^{2}\delta^{2}}\mathbf{I}_{d}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{y}
, note that:

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\left(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{c^{2}\delta^{2}}\mathbf{I}_{d}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{y}=\left(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{c^{2}\delta^{2}}\mathbf{I}_{d}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\theta}+\mathbf{v}\right)

So,

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}=\underbrace{\left(\left(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{c^{2}\delta^{2}}\mathbf{I}_{d}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{I}_{d}\right)\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{=\mathbf{u}_{1}}+\underbrace{\left(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{c^{2}\delta^{2}}\mathbf{I}_{d}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{v}}_{=\mathbf{u}_{2}}

Since
\boldsymbol{\theta}
and
\mathbf{v}
are independent of each other, so are
\mathbf{u}_{1}
and
\mathbf{u}_{2}
, and hence their covariances can be computed separately and added up (i.e. no cross terms).
Considering the SVD as
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^{\top}
, we have,

\displaystyle\textrm{Cov}\left(\mathbf{u}_{1}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{u}_{1}\mathbf{u}_{1}^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=c^{2}\delta^{2}\left[\left(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{c^{2}\delta^{2}}\mathbf{I}_{d}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{I}_{d}\right]\left[\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\left(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{c^{2}\delta^{2}}\mathbf{I}_{d}\right)^{-1}-\mathbf{I}_{d}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{V}\left[c^{2}\delta^{2}\left(\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{c^{2}\delta^{2}}\mathbf{I}_{d}\right)^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}-\mathbf{I}_{d}\right)\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{c^{2}\delta^{2}}\mathbf{I}_{d}\right)^{-1}-\mathbf{I}_{d}\right)\right]\mathbf{V}^{\top}

Similarly,

\displaystyle\textrm{Cov}\left(\mathbf{u}_{2}\right)=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{u}_{2}\mathbf{u}_{2}^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}\left(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{c^{2}\delta^{2}}\mathbf{I}_{d}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\left(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{c^{2}\delta^{2}}\mathbf{I}_{d}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle\textrm{Tr}\left[\textrm{Cov}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left[c^{2}\delta^{2}\left(1-\sigma_{i}^{2}\left(\sigma_{i}^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{c^{2}\delta^{2}}\right)^{-1}\right)^{2}+\sigma^{2}\sigma_{i}^{2}\left(\sigma_{i}^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{c^{2}\delta^{2}}\right)^{-2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{c^{2}\delta^{2}\sigma^{2}}{\sigma^{2}+c^{2}\delta^{2}\sigma_{i}^{2}}.

This completes the proof of the lemma.

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}=\boldsymbol{\theta}+\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{v}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{(i)}}}{{\implies}}\frac{1}{d}\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\rVert_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{d}\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\rVert_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{d}\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{v}\rVert_{2}^{2}-\frac{2}{d}\left\langle\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{v}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{(ii)}}}{{\implies}}\frac{1}{d}\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\rVert_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{d}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{v}}\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\rVert_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{d}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{v}}\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{v}\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\implies b^{2}=\frac{1}{d}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{v}}\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\rVert_{2}^{2}-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}.

Since we have a single realization of the noise vector
\mathbf{v}
, giving us access to just one realization
\mathbf{y}
, a reasonable estimate of
b^{2}
is obtained by simply using
\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\rVert_{2}^{2}
as an approximation for the expected value,
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{v}}\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\rVert_{2}^{2}
.
In this section, we present Lemma
G
that gives a tail probability bound on the deviation of the estimated magnitude
\widehat{b}^{2}
and the true magnitude of the model,
b^{2}
.
Since
b^{2}
is encoded using a scalar quantizer of resolution
\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}
to get
\widetilde{b}^{2}
, it follows from Lemma
G
that
\widetilde{b}^{2}-b^{2}=O_{P}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right)
, where
O_{P}(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}})
denotes a quantity which approaches
0
with high probability as
d\to\infty
.

{lemma_boxed}

Suppose that
\mathbf{y}\sim{\cal N}\left(\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\theta},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n}\right)
for
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d},(n\geq d)
, and
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\frac{1}{d}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=b^{2}
.
Then for
t\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left\lvert\frac{1}{d}\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\rVert_{2}^{2}-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}-b^{2}\right\rvert\geq t\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{dt^{2}\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}}{24\sigma^{4}}\right)+\frac{8\sigma b}{\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}t\sqrt{2\pi d}}\exp\left(-\frac{dt^{2}\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}}{32\sigma^{2}b^{2}}\right).

In other words,
\widehat{b}^{2}-b^{2}=O_{P}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right)
, where
\widehat{b}^{2}\triangleq\frac{1}{d}\left\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}
, where
\sigma_{\mathrm{max}}
and
\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}
are upper and lower bounds to the maximum and minimum singular values of
\mathbf{X}
, respectively.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\theta}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
, where
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\in{\cal N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n})
.
Then,
\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\left(\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\theta}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\right)=\boldsymbol{\theta}+\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
.
To study the concentration of
\frac{1}{d}\left\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}
around
b^{2}
, note that:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}\left\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{d}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}+\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{d}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{d}\left\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\frac{2}{d}\left\langle\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}\left\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}-b^{2}=\frac{1}{d}\left\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\frac{2}{d}\left\langle\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\right\rangle-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}.

So, it suffices to find an upper bound on the tail probability,

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\lvert\frac{1}{d}\left\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}+\frac{2}{d}\left\langle\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\right\rangle\right\rvert\geq t\right).

We have,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left\lvert\frac{1}{d}\left\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}+\frac{2}{d}\left\langle\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\right\rangle\right\rvert\geq t\right)

\displaystyle\leq\hskip 5.69054pt

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left\lvert\frac{1}{d}\left\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}\right\rvert\geq\frac{t}{2}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(\left\lvert\frac{2}{d}\left\langle\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\right\rangle\right\rvert\geq\frac{t}{2}\right)

(36)

Since
\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim{\cal N}\left(\mathbf{0},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}{\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}}^{\top}\right)
, the first term in (
G
) can be upper bounded using Chernoff’s method (cf.
[
27
]
).

Fact
: For a multivariate normal distribution
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\sim{\cal N}\left(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{C}\right)
with covariance matrix
\mathbf{C}
, the moment generating function of
\left\lVert\mathbf{z}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}
is given by,

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\pm s\left\lVert\mathbf{z}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}\right]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\left|\mathbf{I}\pm 2s\mathbf{C}\right|}}

(37)

Moreover,
\mathbb{E}\left\lVert\mathbf{z}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=\textrm{Tr}\left[\textrm{Cov}\left(\mathbf{y}\right)\right]=\textrm{Tr}\left[\mathbf{C}\right]
.
Using Chernoff’s bound for
\mathbf{z}\triangleq\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
with
\mathbf{C}=\sigma^{2}\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}{\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}}^{\top}
gives us,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\left\lVert\mathbf{z}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\geq\left(1+\varepsilon\right)\mathbb{E}\left\lVert\mathbf{z}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right]\leq\min_{s>0}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[e^{s\left\lVert\mathbf{z}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}\right]}{e^{s(1+\varepsilon)\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\mathbf{z}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right]}}

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\left\lVert\mathbf{z}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}-\mathbb{E}\left\lVert\mathbf{z}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\geq\varepsilon\mathbb{E}\left\lVert\mathbf{z}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right]\leq\min_{s>0}\frac{\left|\mathbf{I}-2s\mathbf{C}\right|^{-\frac{1}{2}}}{e^{s\left(1+\varepsilon\right)\textrm{Tr}\left[\mathbf{C}\right]}}

(38)

To evaluate
\textrm{Tr}\left[\mathbf{C}\right]
, consider the SVD of
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^{\top}
, where
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
consists of the singular values of
\mathbf{X}
, i.e.
\sigma_{1}\geq\ldots\geq\sigma_{d}
.
The SVD of
\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
is given by
\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}=\mathbf{V}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\prime}\mathbf{U}^{\top}
, where the diagonal entries of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
are
\sigma_{1}^{-1},\sigma_{2}^{-1},\ldots,\sigma_{d}^{-1}
.
This yields the eigenvalue decomposition of
\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}{\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
to be
\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}{\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}}^{\top}=\mathbf{V}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\top}\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^{\top}=\mathbf{V}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}\mathbf{V}^{\top}
, where
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}=\mathrm{diag}\{\sigma_{1}^{-2},\ldots,\sigma_{d}^{-2}\}
.
So,

\textrm{Tr}\left[\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}{\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}}^{\top}\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}\implies\mathbb{E}\left\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=\textrm{Tr}\left[\mathbf{C}\right]=\sigma^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}\geq\frac{\sigma^{2}d}{\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}}.

(39)

Moreover, the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{I}-2s\mathbf{C}
are given by
\left(1-2s\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}\right)
for
i=1,2,\ldots,d
, meaning,

\left|\mathbf{I}-2s\mathbf{C}\right|=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left(1-2s\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}\right)\leq\left(1-2s\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}}\right)^{d}.

(40)

From eq. (
G
), setting
s=\frac{\varepsilon\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}}{2\left(1+\varepsilon\right)\sigma^{2}}
, we get,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\left\lVert\mathbf{z}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}-\mathbb{E}\left\lVert\mathbf{z}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\geq\varepsilon\mathbb{E}\left\lVert\mathbf{z}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right]\leq\frac{\left(1-2s\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}}\right)^{-\frac{d}{2}}}{e^{s\left(1+\varepsilon\right)\frac{\sigma^{2}d}{\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}}}}

\displaystyle=\left(1-2s\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}}\right)^{-\frac{d}{2}}e^{-s\left(1+\varepsilon\right)\frac{\sigma^{2}d}{\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}}}

\displaystyle=\left(\left(1+\varepsilon\right)e^{-\varepsilon}\right)^{\frac{d}{2}}.

(41)

Utilizing the inequalities
\left(1+\varepsilon\right)e^{-\varepsilon}=e^{-\varepsilon+\log\left(1+\varepsilon\right)}
, and
\log\left(1+\varepsilon\right)\leq\varepsilon-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{3}
, and from symmetry, we have,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\left\lVert\mathbf{z}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}-\mathbb{E}\left\lVert\mathbf{z}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\geq\varepsilon\mathbb{E}\left\lVert\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right]\leq\left(e^{-\left(\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}-\frac{\varepsilon^{3}}{3}\right)}\right)^{\frac{d}{2}}\leq e^{-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}d}{6}}

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\left\lvert\left\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}-\sigma^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}\right\rvert\geq\varepsilon\sigma^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}\right]\leq 2e^{-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}d}{6}}

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\left\lvert\left\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}-\sigma^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}\right\rvert\geq\varepsilon\frac{\sigma^{2}d}{\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}}\right]\leq 2e^{-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}d}{6}}.

(42)

The last inequality follows from the fact that
\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}-\frac{\varepsilon^{3}}{3}\geq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{3}
for
0<\varepsilon\leq 1/2
.
Setting
\varepsilon=\frac{t}{2}\cdot\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}
, the first term of (
G
) can be bounded as,

\mathbb{P}\left[\left\lvert\frac{1}{d}\left\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}\right\rvert\geq\frac{t}{2}\right]\leq 2e^{-\frac{dt^{2}\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}^{4}}{24\sigma^{4}}}.

(43)

To upper bound the second term in (
G
), let us consider the tail probability
\mathbb{P}\left(\left\langle{\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\right\rangle\geq\frac{dt}{4}\right)
.
Note that
Z=\left\langle{\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\right\rangle\sim{\cal N}\left(0,\widetilde{\sigma}^{2}\right)
, where
\widetilde{\sigma}^{2}=\left({\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)^{\top}\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{\top}\right]{\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta}=\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}{\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta}
.
Recalling the SVD of
\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}
to be
\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}=\mathbf{V}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\prime}\mathbf{U}^{\top}
, it can be shown that,

\widetilde{\sigma}^{2}=\sigma^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sigma_{i}^{-2}\left(\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)^{2}\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}}\left\lVert\mathbf{V}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=\frac{\sigma^{2}db^{2}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}}.

Here,
\{\mathbf{v}_{i}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},i=1,\ldots,d
denote the columns of the matrix
\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
.
Using the tail probability expression of a normal random variable yields an upper bound on the second term of (
G
) as follows,

\mathbb{P}\left[\left\lvert\frac{2}{d}\left\langle\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\right\rangle\right\rvert\geq\frac{t}{2}\right]\leq\frac{8\sigma b}{\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}t\sqrt{2\pi d}}\exp\left(-\frac{dt^{2}\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}}{32\sigma^{2}b^{2}}\right)\sim O_{P}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right).

(44)

Equations (
43
) and (
44
) together complete the proof of Lemma
G
.
∎

Appendix H
Proof of Theorem
4.1
: Naïve Quantizer guarantee

For the purposes of this proof, let us denote the scaling factor used in the naive learning and quantization strategy as
\widetilde{\gamma}
, i.e.,

\widetilde{\gamma}\triangleq\sqrt{\frac{d\widetilde{b}^{4}}{\widetilde{b}^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d}\xi}}.

(45)

Then the quantized model learned by the naive strategy, given by
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\widetilde{\gamma}\widetilde{\mathbf{s}}
, has an
\ell_{2}
-risk,

R\left(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)=\frac{1}{d}\lVert\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\underbrace{\frac{2}{d}\lVert\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\widehat{\gamma}\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\rVert_{2}^{2}}_{A_{1}}+\underbrace{\frac{2}{d}\left\lVert\widehat{\gamma}\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}_{A_{2}},

(46)

where
\widehat{\gamma}\triangleq\frac{\widehat{b}^{2}}{\widehat{b}^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d}\xi}
.

Analyzing the term
A_{1}
.
The first term is eq. (
46
) can be upper bounded as,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}\left\lVert\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\widehat{\gamma}\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\left\lVert\widetilde{\gamma}\widetilde{\mathbf{s}}-\widehat{\gamma}\left\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{2}\cdot\frac{\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}}{\left\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{2}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\left\lVert\widehat{\gamma}\left\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{2}\cdot\widetilde{\mathbf{s}}-\widehat{\gamma}\left\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{2}\cdot\frac{\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}}{\left\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{2}}+\left(\widetilde{\gamma}-\widehat{\gamma}\left\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{2}\right)\cdot\widetilde{\mathbf{s}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{d}\widehat{\gamma}^{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\cdot\left\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{s}}-\frac{\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}}{\left\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{2}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\frac{2}{d}\left(\widetilde{\gamma}-\widehat{\gamma}\left\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{2}\right)^{2}\left\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{s}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

(47)

The second term in (
H
) is small.
Since
\widetilde{\mathbf{s}}=\text{Q}_{u,B}\left(\frac{\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}}{\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\rVert_{2}}\right)
, this implies
\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{s}}\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq 1
.
Furthermore, using the facts
\widehat{\gamma}=\widehat{b}^{2}/(\widehat{b}^{2}+\sigma^{2}\xi/d)
, and
\widehat{b}^{2}=\frac{1}{d}\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\rVert_{2}^{2}-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d}\xi
, we get,

\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\left(\widetilde{\gamma}-\widehat{\gamma}\left\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{2}\right)=\sqrt{\frac{\widetilde{b}^{4}}{\widetilde{b}^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d}\xi}}-\sqrt{\frac{\widehat{b}^{4}}{\widehat{b}^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d}\xi}}=O_{P}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right).

Whereas, the first term in eq. (
H
) satisfies,

\frac{2}{d}\widehat{\gamma}^{2}\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\rVert_{2}^{2}\cdot\left\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{s}}-\frac{\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}}{\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\rVert_{2}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{2}{d}\cdot\frac{d\widehat{b}^{4}}{\left(\widehat{b}^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d}\xi\right)}\cdot 2^{-2B}d=\frac{2db^{4}}{b^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d}\xi}\cdot 2^{-2B}+O_{P}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right).

The first inequality follows from the fact that for a uniform scalar quantizer, the worst-case quantization error is given as,

\sup_{\mathbf{z}\in\textbf{B}_{\infty}^{d}(1)}\left\lVert\text{Q}_{u,B}(\mathbf{z})-\mathbf{z}\right\rVert=\frac{\Delta}{2}\sqrt{d}=\frac{\sqrt{d}}{M}=2^{-B}\sqrt{d}.

Consequently,

A_{1}=\frac{2db^{4}}{\left(b^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d}\xi\right)}\cdot 2^{-2B}+O_{P}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right).

(48)

Analyzing the term
A_{2}
.
Term
A_{2}
has nothing to do with the quantization scheme used for model compression and is essentially the unavoidable loss for the linear estimate
\widehat{\gamma}\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}
, which is present even if there is no bit budget constraint.
Suppose
\mathbf{A}
is a
d\times d
orthonormal matrix such that
\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\theta}=\left(\sqrt{d}b,0,\ldots,0\right)^{\top}\triangleq\boldsymbol{\tau}
.
Let
\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}
.
Then,
\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}\triangleq\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\theta}+\mathbf{A}\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{v}\sim{\cal N}\left(\boldsymbol{\tau},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}{\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\right)
.
We have,

\displaystyle A_{2}=\frac{1}{d}\left\lVert\widehat{\gamma}\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{d}\left\lVert\widehat{\gamma}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\left\lVert\widehat{\gamma}\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}-\boldsymbol{\tau}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\widehat{\gamma}^{2}\left\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+b^{2}-2\widehat{\gamma}\frac{1}{d}\left\langle\widetilde{\mathbf{y}},\boldsymbol{\tau}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{b^{2}\cdot\frac{\sigma^{2}\xi}{d}}{\left(b^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d}\xi\right)}+O_{P}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right)

(49)

The last equality follows from the fact that
\left\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{2}=\left\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert_{2}
, and
\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\left\langle\widetilde{\mathbf{y}},\boldsymbol{\tau}\right\rangle=\frac{\widetilde{y}_{1}}{\sqrt{d}}
concentrates around its mean
b
, as
\frac{\widetilde{y}_{1}}{\sqrt{d}}=b+O_{P}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right)
.
Here,
\widetilde{y}_{1}
is the first coordinate of
\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}
.

Completing the proof of Thm
4.1
.
From eqs. (
48
) and (
H
), eq. (
46
) simplifies to

R\left(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)\leq\frac{2b^{2}\cdot\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d}\xi}{b^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d}\xi}+\frac{2db^{4}}{\left(b^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d}\xi\right)}\cdot 2^{-2B}+O_{P}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right).

(50)

A uniform high-probability bound for all models in the euclidean ball
\frac{1}{d}\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq c^{2}
can be obtained as follows:

\mathbb{P}\left(R\left(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)>\frac{2c^{2}\sigma^{2}}{\sigma^{2}+c^{2}\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}}+\frac{2dc^{4}\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}}{\sigma^{2}+c^{2}\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}}\cdot 2^{-2B}+\frac{C_{n}}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\to 0,

(51)

as
d\to\infty
, for some constant
C_{n}
independent of dimension
d
.
This completes the proof.

Appendix I
Proof of Theorem
4.2
: Random Correlation Maximization (
RCM
) guarantee

Random Correlation Maximization (
RCM
) learns and encodes the magnitude and direction of the model separately and then multiplies them with appropriate scaling.
Recall Lemma
G
that gives a tail bound on
\widehat{b}^{2}-b^{2}
.
We use this throughout the analysis of Thm.
4.2
.
We also state the following two lemmas from
[
28
]
and
[
10
]
without proofs.
The proofs can be found in the respective references.

{lemma_boxed}

[
[
28
]
]

Suppose that
\mathbf{z}
is uniformly distributed on the
d
-dimensional unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.
For a fixed
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}=1
, the inner product
\rho=\left\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}\right\rangle
between
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{z}
has the probability density function

f\left(\rho\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{d-1}{2}\right)}\left(1-\rho^{2}\right)^{\frac{d-3}{2}}I\left(|\rho|<1\right).

Here,
I(\cdot)
denotes the indicator function.

{lemma_boxed}
[
[
10
]
]

Suppose that
p=e^{d\beta}
and
\mathbf{z}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{z}_{p}
are independent and identically distributed with a uniform distribution on the
d
-dimensional sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.
For a fixed unit vector
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
\rho_{i}=\left\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}_{i}\right\rangle
and
L_{d}=\max_{1\leq i\leq p}\rho_{i}
.
Then
L_{d}\to\sqrt{1-e^{-2\beta}}
in probability as
d\to\infty
.
Furthermore,
L_{d}-\sqrt{1-e^{-2\beta}}=O_{P}\left(\frac{\log d}{d}\right)
.

The proof of Lemma
I
utilizes Lemma
I
.

To prove Thm.
4.2
, we alternatively prove the following Lemma
I
that provides a high probability bound for
RCM
when the magnitude of the true model
\boldsymbol{\theta}
is known.

{lemma_boxed}

For a true parameter
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfying
\frac{1}{d}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=b^{2}\leq c^{2}
, as
d\to\infty
, the output of
RCM
satisfies,

\mathbb{P}\left(R\left(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)>\frac{b^{2}\cdot\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}}}{\left(b^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}}\right)}+\frac{b^{4}\cdot 2^{-2B}}{\left(b^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}}\right)}+C\sqrt{\frac{\log d}{d}}\right)\to 0

The probability measure in Lemma

due to the additive model

Note that as a direct consequence of Lemma

As before, let us denote

The

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\left\lVert\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\widehat{\gamma}\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}+\widehat{\gamma}\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\frac{1}{d}\left\lVert\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\widehat{\gamma}\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}_{A_{1}}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{d}\left\lVert\widehat{\gamma}\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}_{A_{2}}+\underbrace{\frac{2}{d}\left\langle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\widehat{\gamma}\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y},\widehat{\gamma}\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rangle}_{A_{3}}.

Analyzing the term

The second term in (

\frac{1}{d}\widehat{\gamma}^{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=\frac{\widehat{b}^{4}}{\left(\widehat{b}^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d}\xi\right)^{2}}\cdot\left(\widehat{b}^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d}\xi\right)=\frac{b^{4}}{b^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d}\xi}+O_{P}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right).

Whereas, the third (cross) term can be seen to be,

\displaystyle\frac{2}{d}\left\langle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\widehat{\gamma}\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{d}\frac{\widehat{b}^{2}}{\left(\widehat{b}^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d}\xi\right)}\sqrt{\frac{d\widetilde{b}^{4}\left(1-2^{-2B}\right)}{\left(\widetilde{b}^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d}\xi\right)}}\cdot\left\langle\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y},\widetilde{\mathbf{s}}\right\rangle

So,
\frac{2}{d}\left\langle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\widehat{\gamma}\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\right\rangle=\frac{2b^{4}}{b^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d}\xi}\left(1-2^{-2B}\right)+O_{P}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right)
.
Combining all the three terms together yields,

A_{1}=\frac{b^{4}\cdot 2^{-2B}}{\left(b^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d}\xi\right)}+O_{P}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right).

(55)

Analyzing the term
A_{2}
.
Term

has nothing to do with the quantization scheme used for model compression and can be analyzed similar to eq. (

, i.e.

A_{2}=\frac{b^{2}\cdot\frac{\sigma^{2}\xi}{d}}{\left(b^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d}\xi\right)}+O_{P}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right).

Analyzing the term
A_{3}
.
Let us define,

\overline{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\triangleq\sqrt{\frac{d\widehat{b}^{4}\left(1-2^{-2B}\right)}{\widehat{b}^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d}\xi}}\cdot\widetilde{\mathbf{s}}.

(56)

The term
A_{3}
can be written as,

\displaystyle\frac{2}{d}\left\langle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\widehat{\gamma}\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y},\widehat{\gamma}\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rangle=\frac{2}{d}\left\langle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\overline{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\widehat{\gamma}\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rangle

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{d}\left\langle\overline{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\widehat{\gamma}\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rangle

(57)

\displaystyle-\frac{2}{d}\left\langle\widehat{\gamma}\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y},\widehat{\gamma}\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rangle.

The first term in eq. (

) is
O_{P}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right)
, since
\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\lVert\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\overline{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\rVert_{2}=O_{P}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right)

\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\left\lVert\widehat{\gamma}\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rVert_{2}

The third term in eq. (

\displaystyle\frac{2}{d}\left\langle\widehat{\gamma}\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y},\widehat{\gamma}\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rangle=\frac{2}{d}\left\langle\widehat{\gamma}\widetilde{\mathbf{y}},\widehat{\gamma}\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}-\boldsymbol{\tau}\right\rangle=\frac{2}{d}\widehat{\gamma}^{2}\left\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}-2\widehat{\gamma}b\frac{\widetilde{y}_{1}}{\sqrt{d}}=O_{P}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right).

We now consider the second term,

\frac{2}{d}\left\langle\overline{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\widehat{\gamma}\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rangle=\frac{2}{d}\left\langle\overline{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\gamma^{\prime}\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rangle+\frac{2}{d}\left(\widehat{\gamma}-\gamma^{\prime}\right)\left\langle\overline{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\right\rangle,

(59)

where
\gamma^{\prime}\triangleq\frac{\left\langle\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\right\rangle}{\left\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}
.
Note that this choice of
\gamma^{\prime}
ensures that
\gamma^{\prime}\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}
is the projection of the true model
\boldsymbol{\theta}
on to
\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}

\left\langle\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y},\gamma^{\prime}\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rangle=0
.
Furthermore, since
\widehat{\gamma}\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}
can be interpreted as the linear estimator of

from
\mathbf{y}

, which uses the unquantized magnitude

59

\boldsymbol{\theta}
on
\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}
.
We now show that
\widehat{\gamma}\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}
is not too far from this projection because,

\displaystyle\gamma^{\prime}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{d}\theta_{i}\left(\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\right)_{i}}{\left\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}=\frac{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\theta_{i}\left(\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{v}\right)_{i}}{d\left(\widehat{b}^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d}\xi\right)}=\frac{b^{2}}{\widehat{b}^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d}\xi}+\frac{1}{\widehat{b}^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d}\xi}\cdot\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\theta_{i}\left(\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{v}\right)_{i}

Moreover, since we considered the SVD of
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^{\top}
, the SVD of
\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}
is,
\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{V}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\prime}\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{v}
, where
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\prime}
consists of singular values
\sigma_{1}^{-1},\ldots,\sigma_{d}^{-1}
.
Since Gaussian distribution is rotation invariant, and
\mathbf{V}^{\top}
is a unitary transform,
\mathbf{v}\sim{\cal N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n})
implies that
\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{v}\sim{\cal N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n})
.
Furthermore, since this is followed by multiplication by a diagonal matrix, which simply scales the variance of each coordinate differently, and another unitary transform
\mathbf{U}
, it holds that,
\left(\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{v}\right)_{i}\sim{\cal N}\left(0,\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}\right)
.
Consequently,
\sum_{i=1}^{d}\theta_{i}\left(\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{v}\right)_{i}\sim{\cal N}\left(0,\sum_{i=1}^{d}\theta_{i}^{2}\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}\right)\implies
The variance of
\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\theta_{i}\left(\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{v}\right)_{i}
can be upper bounded by
\frac{\sigma^{2}b^{2}}{d\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}}
.
So,
\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\theta_{i}\left(\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{v}\right)_{i}
concentrates around its mean
0
as
O_{P}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right)
, meaning,
\widehat{\gamma}-\gamma^{\prime}=\frac{\widehat{b}^{2}-b^{2}}{\widehat{b}^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d}\xi}=O_{P}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right)
.
Moreover, the coefficient of
(\widehat{\gamma}-\gamma^{\prime})
,

\frac{2}{d}\left\langle\overline{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\right\rangle\leq\frac{2}{d}\lVert\overline{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\rVert_{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{2}=\frac{2}{d}\sqrt{\frac{d\widehat{b}^{4}\left(1-2^{-2B}\right)}{\widehat{b}^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d}\xi}}\sqrt{d\left(\widehat{b}^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d}\xi\right)}=2\widehat{b}^{2}\left(1-2^{-2B}\right)

is finite.
Therefore, the second term in eq. (
59
) is
O_{P}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right)
.
The final term left to analyze is the first term in eq. (
59

\frac{2}{d}\left\langle\overline{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\gamma^{\prime}\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rangle=\sqrt{\frac{4\widehat{b}^{4}\left(1-2^{-2B}\right)}{\widehat{b}^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d}\xi}}\left\langle\widetilde{\mathbf{s}},\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\left(\gamma^{\prime}\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)\right\rangle

Note that the scaling factor
\sqrt{\frac{4\widehat{b}^{4}\left(1-2^{-2B}\right)}{\widehat{b}^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d}\xi}}=\sqrt{\frac{4b^{4}\left(1-2^{-2B}\right)}{b^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d}\xi}}+O_{P}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right)
.
To analyze the term
\left\langle\widetilde{\mathbf{s}},\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\left(\gamma^{\prime}\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)\right\rangle

[

]

{lemma_boxed}

[]Near-orthogonality of vectors in high dimensions;
[
29
, Prop. 1]
,
[
10
, Lemma A.4]

Let
\mathbf{u}
be uniformly distributed on the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.
Let
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a fixed unit vector.
Then,

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\lvert\left\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{x}\right\rangle\right\rvert>\epsilon\right)\leq K\sqrt{d}\left(1-\epsilon^{2}\right)^{\frac{d-2}{2}}

for all
d\geq 2
and
0<\epsilon<1
, where
K
is a universal constant.
Therefore,

\left\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{x}\right\rangle=O_{P}\left(\frac{\log d}{d}\right).

From the construction of our codebook
{\cal Y}
, the vector
\widetilde{\mathbf{s}}
is uniformly distributed on the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.
Consequently, for any vector
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, from symmetry, the projection of
\widetilde{\mathbf{s}}
on to subspace orthogonal
\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}
, i.e.
\mathrm{Proj}_{\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}^{\perp}}\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{s}}\right)
has a spherical distribution in
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
.
Furthermore, its length can be calculated as follows,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Proj}_{\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}^{\perp}}\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{s}}\right)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\mathbf{s}}-\frac{\left\langle\widetilde{\mathbf{s}},\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\right\rangle}{\left\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle\implies\left\lVert\mathrm{Proj}_{\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}^{\perp}}\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{s}}\right)\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{s}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\frac{\left\langle\widetilde{\mathbf{s}},\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\right\rangle^{2}}{\left\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}-2\cdot\frac{\left\langle\widetilde{\mathbf{s}},\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\right\rangle^{2}}{\left\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{\left\langle\widetilde{\mathbf{s}},\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\right\rangle^{2}}{\left\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}

\displaystyle=1-\left\langle\widetilde{\mathbf{s}},\frac{\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}}{\left\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{2}}\right\rangle^{2}=1-\sqrt{1-2^{-2B}}+O_{P}\left(\frac{\log d}{d}\right).

The final equality is due to Lemma
I
.
So, we can write, with high probability,
\mathrm{Proj}_{\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}^{\perp}}\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{s}}\right)=L\cdot\mathbf{u}
where,
\mathbf{u}
follows the uniform distribution on sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
and
L=1-\sqrt{1-2^{-2B}}+O_{P}\left(\frac{\log d}{d}\right)
.
Note that the projection of
\widetilde{\mathbf{s}}
onto the orthogonal space of
\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}
, i.e.
\mathrm{Proj}_{\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}^{\perp}}\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{s}}\right)
, is independent of
\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}
.
So, conditioning on a particular value of
\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and recalling that
\left\langle\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y},\gamma^{\prime}\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rangle=0
, we have,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left\langle\widetilde{\mathbf{s}},\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\left(\gamma^{\prime}\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)\right\rangle>t\hskip 2.84526pt\Bigg|\hskip 2.84526pt\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{w}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\left\langle\mathrm{Proj}_{\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}^{\perp}}\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{s}}\right),\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\mathrm{Proj}_{\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}^{\perp}}\left(\gamma^{\prime}\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)\right\rangle>t\hskip 2.84526pt\Bigg|\hskip 2.84526pt\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{w}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(L\left\lVert\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\left(\gamma^{\prime}\mathbf{w}-\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)\right\rVert_{2}\left\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{e}\right\rangle>t\right)

\displaystyle\leq K_{1}\sqrt{d}\left(1-\frac{t^{2}}{K_{2}\left\lVert\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\left(\gamma^{\prime}\mathbf{w}-\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}\right)^{\frac{d-2}{2}},

where,
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
are positive constants.
Here,
\mathbf{e}
is the unit vector in the direction of
(\gamma^{\prime}\mathbf{w}-\boldsymbol{\theta})
, and the last inequality follows from Lemma
I.1
.
We then have,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left\langle\widetilde{\mathbf{s}},\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\left(\widehat{\gamma}\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)\right\rangle>t\right)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\text{\Large$\int$}}\nolimits\mathbb{P}\left(\left\langle\widetilde{\mathbf{s}},\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\left(\gamma^{\prime}\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)\right\rangle>t\hskip 2.84526pt\Bigg|\hskip 2.84526pt\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{w}\right)p_{\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}}\left(\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{w}\right)d\mathbf{w}

\displaystyle\leq K_{1}\sqrt{d}\mathop{\text{\Large$\int$}}\nolimits\left(1-\frac{t^{2}}{K_{2}\left\lVert\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\left(\gamma^{\prime}\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}\right)^{\frac{d-2}{2}}p_{\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}}\left(\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{w}\right)d\mathbf{w}

\displaystyle\leq K_{1}\sqrt{d}\left(1-\frac{t^{2}}{K_{2}^{\prime}}\right)^{\frac{d-2}{2}}+\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{d}\left\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}>b^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d}\xi+K_{3}\right),

for positive constants
K_{1},K_{2}
and
K_{3}
.
This implies
\left\langle\widetilde{\mathbf{s}},\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\left(\gamma^{\prime}\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)\right\rangle=O_{P}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log d}{d}}\right)
and thus
A_{3}=O_{P}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log d}{d}}\right)
.

Completing the proof of Lemma
I
.
From eq. (
I.1
), we have that
R\left(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)=A_{1}+A_{2}+A_{3}
.
From eq. (
55
),
A_{1}=\frac{b^{4}\cdot 2^{-2B}}{\left(b^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d}\xi\right)}+O_{P}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right)
.
From eq. (

),
A_{2}=\frac{b^{2}\cdot\frac{\sigma^{2}\xi}{d}}{\left(b^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d}\xi\right)}+O_{P}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right)
.
Finally, as just seen above,
A_{3}=O_{P}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log d}{d}}\right)
.
Therefore, for Random Correlation Maximization (
RCM
), the risk is given by,

R\left(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)=\frac{b^{2}\cdot\frac{\sigma^{2}\xi}{d}}{\left(b^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d}\xi\right)}+\frac{b^{4}\cdot 2^{-2B}}{\left(b^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d}\xi\right)}+O_{P}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log d}{d}}\right)

(60)

In other words,

\mathbb{P}\left(R\left(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)>\frac{b^{2}\cdot\frac{\sigma^{2}\xi}{d}}{\left(b^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d}\xi\right)}+\frac{b^{4}\cdot 2^{-2B}}{\left(b^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d}\xi\right)}+C\sqrt{\frac{\log d}{d}}\right)\to 0

(61)

as

, for some constant

independent of

.
This also implies,

\mathbb{P}\left({R\left(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)>\frac{b^{2}\cdot\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}}}{\left(b^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}}\right)}+\frac{b^{4}\cdot 2^{-2B}}{\left(b^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{max}}^{2}}\right)}+C\sqrt{\frac{\log d}{d}}}\right)\to 0

(62)

which completes the proof.

Appendix J
Democratic Embeddings and Quantization

For a given wide matrix
\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times D}
where
d\leq D
, and for a given vector
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the system of equations
\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{S}\mathbf{x}
is under-determined for
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
, with the set
{\cal X}=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}\hskip 2.84526pt|\hskip 2.84526pt\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{S}\mathbf{x}\}
as the solution space.
The
democratic embedding
of
\mathbf{z}
with respect to
\mathbf{S}
is defined to be the solution of the following linear program,

\mathbf{x}_{d}=\mathop{\rm arg\hskip 2.84526ptmin}_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}}\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_{\infty}\text{ subject to }\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{S}\mathbf{x}.

(63)

In order to characterize the solution of the optimization problem (
63
) (done in Lemma
J
), we review certain definitions from
[
11
,
12
]
.

{definition_boxed}

(Frame)

A matrix
\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times D}
with
d\leq D
is called a
frame
if there exist
A,B
with
0<A\leq B<\infty
, such that for any vector
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have
A\left\lVert\mathbf{z}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\left\lVert\mathbf{S}^{\top}\mathbf{z}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq B\left\lVert\mathbf{z}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}
, where
A
and
B
are called
lower
and
upper frame bounds
respectively.

Note that the definition of a
frame
includes all full-rank matrices
[
30
,
31
]
.
However, for obtaining democratic embeddings with desirable properties (as specified in Lemma
J
below), we are interested in frames that have a small value of the upper frame bound
B
.
Explicit expressions for
B
that hold true with high probability, can be obtained for special classes of frames that satisfy the uncertainty principle defined below in Def.
J
.

{definition_boxed}

(Uncertainty principle (UP))

A frame
\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times D}
satisfies the Uncertainty Principle with parameters
\eta,\delta
, with
\eta>0,\delta\in(0,1)
if
\left\lVert\mathbf{S}\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\eta\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}
holds for all (sparse) vectors
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
satisfying
\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{0}\leq\delta D
, where
\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{0}
denotes the number of non-zero elements in
\mathbf{x}
.

{lemma_boxed}

[
Democratic Embeddings
;
[
12
]
]

Let
\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times D}
be a frame with bounds
A,B
(cf. Def.

[

{lemma_boxed}

[
Worst-case
\ell_{2}
error of democratic quantization
]

Given a frame
\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times D}
with upper Kashin constant
K_{u}
and a uniform scalar quantizer
\text{Q}_{u,B}
, for any
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\left\lVert\mathbf{z}\right\rVert_{2}\leq 1
, let
\widetilde{\mathbf{z}}=\text{Q}_{d,B}(\mathbf{z})=\mathbf{S}\cdot\text{Q}_{u,B}(\mathbf{x}_{d})
.
Then, for
\lambda=D/d
,

\sup_{\lVert\mathbf{z}\rVert_{2}\leq 1}\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{z}}-\mathbf{z}\rVert_{2}\leq 2^{\left(1-\frac{B}{\lambda}\right)}K_{u}.

(66)

Proof.

From Lemma
J
, we know that the democratic representation of
\mathbf{z}
with respect to
\mathbf{S}
, i.e.
\mathbf{x}_{d}
, satisfies,
\left\lVert\mathbf{x}_{d}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\frac{K_{u}}{\sqrt{D}}
.
Let us denote
\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}=\text{Q}_{u,B}(\mathbf{x}_{d})
.
Then, since
\mathbf{x}_{d},\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
, we have
\left\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}_{d}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\frac{2K_{u}/\sqrt{D}}{2^{dB/D}}\sqrt{D}=2^{\left(1-\frac{dB}{D}\right)}K_{u}
.
This inequality follows since we can exploit Lemma
J
and choose the dynamic range of the uniform quantizer
\text{Q}_{u,B}
to be
\left[-\frac{K_{u}}{\sqrt{D}},+\frac{K_{u}}{\sqrt{D}}\right]
.
Moreover, since we originally had a bit-budget of
dB
-bits but we are now using it to quantize a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{D}
, the effective average number of bits per dimension is
dB/D
, implying
2^{dB/D}
quantization points per dimension.

Moreover,
\left\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{z}}-\mathbf{z}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\left\lVert\mathbf{S}\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}_{d}\right)\right\rVert_{2}\leq\left\lVert\mathbf{S}\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}_{d}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\left\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}_{d}\right\rVert_{2}\leq 2^{\left(1-\frac{dB}{D}\right)}K_{u}

\mathbf{S}\mathbf{S}^{\top}=1\implies\left\lVert\mathbf{S}\right\rVert_{2}\leq 1

Appendix L
Near-Democratic Embeddings and Quantization

To reduce the computational complexity of obtaining the democratic embeddings, an alternative would be to solve a relaxation of (
11
).
The
near-democratic embedding
of
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with respect to
\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times D}
is defined to be the solution of the following convex program,

\mathbf{x}_{nd}=\mathop{\rm arg\hskip 2.84526ptmin}_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}}\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_{2}\text{ subject to }\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{S}\mathbf{x}.

(70)

To solve this in closed form, the Lagrangian is
L(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\nu})=\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{x}+\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{S}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right)
for
\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Computing the derivative and setting it to zero,
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}L(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\nu})=2\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{S}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\nu}=0\implies\mathbf{x}_{nd}=-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{S}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\nu}
.
So,
\boldsymbol{\nu}=-2\left(\mathbf{S}\mathbf{S}^{\top}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{z}
and
\mathbf{x}_{nd}=-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{S}^{\top}\left(-2\left(\mathbf{S}\mathbf{S}^{\top}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{z}\right)=\mathbf{S}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{S}\mathbf{S}^{\top}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{S}^{\top}\mathbf{z}
.
The last equality follows from the fact that we choose our frames
\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times D}
to be Parseval, i.e. they satisfy
\mathbf{S}\mathbf{S}^{\top}=\mathbf{I}_{d}
.

Remark 4
.

The frame
\mathbf{S}
can be chosen to be either a random Haar orthonormal matrix as in Thm.
J
, or the Randomized Hadamard frame
\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{H}
as described in §
4.4
.
Choosing
\mathbf{S}
to be random orthonormal still requires
O(d^{2})
multiplications to find the near-democratic embedding but does not require the explicit knowledge of the UP parameters
\eta
and
\delta
.
On the other hand, when
\mathbf{S}
is taken to be randomized Hadamard,
\mathbf{x}_{nd}
can be computed using only
O(d\log d)
additions and sign flips using the fast Walsh-Hadamard transform.

The following lemmas
L
and
L
provide an upper bound to the
\ell_{\infty}
-norm of the solution of (
70
) for orthonormal and Hadamard frames respectively.
Their proofs are reproduced here from
[
14
]
for completeness.

{lemma_boxed}

For a random orthonormal frame
\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times D}
, with probability as least
1-\frac{1}{2D}
, the near-democratic embedding of
\mathbf{z}
with respect to
\mathbf{S}
satisfies,

\left\lVert\mathbf{x}_{nd}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq 2\sqrt{\frac{\lambda\log(2D)}{D}}\left\lVert\mathbf{z}\right\rVert_{2},

(71)

where
\lambda=D/d
.

Proof.

A random orthonormal matrix is obtained by first generating a random Gaussian matrix
\mathbf{G}
, each of whose entries are drawn i.i.d. from
{\cal N}(0,1)
, computing its singular value decomposition
\mathbf{G}=\underline{\mathbf{U}}\underline{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\underline{\mathbf{V}}^{\top}
, letting
\widetilde{\mathbf{S}}=\underline{\mathbf{U}}\underline{\mathbf{V}}^{\top}
, and randomly selecting
d
rows of
\widetilde{\mathbf{S}}
, i.e.
\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{P}\widetilde{\mathbf{S}}
.
Let
\{\mathbf{s}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\{\widetilde{\mathbf{s}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
denote the columns of
\mathbf{S}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{S}}
respectively.
We have,
\left\lVert\mathbf{s}_{i}\right\rVert_{2}=\left\lVert\mathbf{P}\widetilde{\mathbf{s}}_{i}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\left\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{s}}_{i}\right\rVert_{2}=1
.
For any
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{z}}=\mathbf{z}/\left\lVert\mathbf{z}\right\rVert_{2}
,

\left\lVert\mathbf{S}^{\top}\mathbf{z}\right\rVert_{\infty}=\max_{i\in[D]}|\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{z}|=\left\lVert\mathbf{z}\right\rVert_{2}\max_{i\in[D]}\left\lVert\mathbf{s}_{i}\right\rVert_{2}|\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{i}^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{z}}|\leq\left\lVert\mathbf{z}\right\rVert_{2}\max_{i\in[D]}|\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{i}^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{z}}|,

where
\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{i}=\mathbf{s}_{i}/\left\lVert\mathbf{s}_{i}\right\rVert_{2}
.
Note that
\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is uniformly random on the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, i.e.
\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{i}
has identical distribution as
\mathbf{g}/\left\lVert\mathbf{g}\right\rVert_{2}
where
\mathbf{g}\sim{\cal N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}_{d})
.
Due to rotational invariance of Gaussian distribution, for any fixed
\widehat{\mathbf{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{z}}\right\rVert_{2}=1
,
\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{i}^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{z}}
has identical distribution as
\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{e}_{1}
, where
\mathbf{e}_{1}
is
[1,0,\ldots,0]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Concentration of measure for uniform distribution on the unit sphere implies,

\mathbb{P}\left[|\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{i}^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{z}}|\geq t\right]=\mathbb{P}\left[|s_{1}|\geq t\right]\leq 2e^{-dt^{2}/2}.

A subsequent union bound gives us,

\mathbb{P}\left[\max_{i\in[D]}|\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{i}^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{z}}|\geq t\right]\leq 2De^{-dt^{2}/2}.

Choosing
t=2\sqrt{\frac{\log(2D)}{d}}
yields,

\mathbb{P}\left[\left\lVert\mathbf{S}^{\top}\mathbf{z}\right\rVert_{\infty}\geq 2\sqrt{\frac{\lambda\log(2D)}{D}}\left\lVert\mathbf{z}\right\rVert_{2}\right]\leq\frac{1}{2D}.

This completes the proof.
∎

{lemma_boxed}

For
\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times D}
, with probability at least
1-\frac{1}{2D}
, the near-democratic embedding of
\mathbf{z}
with respect to
\mathbf{S}
satisfies,

\left\lVert\mathbf{x}_{nd}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq 2\sqrt{\frac{\log\left(2D\right)}{D}}\left\lVert\mathbf{z}\right\rVert_{2}.

(72)

Proof.

For the purposes of this proof, let
\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{P}^{\top}\mathbf{z}=[w_{1},\ldots,w_{D}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
and
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{H}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{w}=[u_{1},\ldots,u_{N}]^{\top}
.
Note that
u_{j}
is of the form
\sum_{i=1}^{D}a_{i}w_{i}
with each
a_{i}=\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{D}}
chosen i.i.d.
A Chernoff-type argument gives,

\mathbb{P}[u_{j}>t]=\mathbb{P}\left[e^{\lambda u_{j}}>e^{\lambda t}\right]\leq e^{-\lambda t}\prod_{i=1}^{D}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\lambda a_{i}w_{i}}\right].

Moreover,

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\lambda a_{i}w_{i}}\right]=\frac{1}{2}e^{\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{D}}w_{i}}+\frac{1}{2}e^{-\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{D}}w_{i}}=\cosh\left(\frac{\lambda w_{i}}{\sqrt{D}}\right)\leq e^{\lambda^{2}w_{i}^{2}/(2D)},

where the last inequality follows from a bound on hyperbolic cosine.
This gives us,

\mathbb{P}\left[u_{j}>t\right]\leq e^{\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2D}\left\lVert\mathbf{w}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}-\lambda t}.

Letting
\lambda=tD/\left\lVert\mathbf{w}\right\rVert_{2}
yields
\mathbb{P}\left[u_{j}>t\right]\leq e^{-t^{2}D/(2\left\lVert\mathbf{w}\right\rVert_{2}^{2})}
.
Similarly,
\mathbb{P}\left[u_{j}<-t\right]\leq e^{-t^{2}D/(2\left\lVert\mathbf{w}\right\rVert_{2}^{2})}
.
Since
\left\lVert\mathbf{u}\right\rVert_{\infty}=\max_{j\in[D],s\in\{\pm 1\}}su_{j}
, union bound yields,

\mathbb{P}\left[\left\lVert\mathbf{H}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{w}\right\rVert_{\infty}>t\right]\leq e^{-\frac{t^{2}D}{2\left\lVert\mathbf{w}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}+\log(2D)}.

Setting
t=2\left\lVert\mathbf{w}\right\rVert_{2}\sqrt{\frac{\log(2D)}{D}}
gives us,

\mathbb{P}\left[\left\lVert\mathbf{H}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{w}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq 2\left\lVert\mathbf{w}\right\rVert_{2}\sqrt{\frac{\log(2D)}{D}}\right]\geq 1-\frac{1}{2D}.

As
\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{P}^{\top}\mathbf{z}\implies\left\lVert\mathbf{w}\right\rVert_{2}=\left\lVert\mathbf{z}\right\rVert_{2}
and this completes the proof.
∎

D
, when
d\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 2.5pt\kern-5.27776pt$\displaystyle\not$\hss}{\rightarrow}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 2.5pt\kern-5.27776pt$\textstyle\not$\hss}{\rightarrow}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 2.04861pt\kern-4.45831pt$\scriptstyle\not$\hss}{\rightarrow}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 1.84029pt\kern-3.95834pt$\scriptscriptstyle\not$\hss}{\rightarrow}}}\infty
, this term can be upper bounded as before, i.e.

\displaystyle\int_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\overline{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}}R({\mathsf{Q}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y}),\boldsymbol{\theta})dF_{\delta}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{d}\sqrt{2}\exp\left(-\frac{d(1-\delta^{2})^{2}}{16\delta^{4}}\right)Cd+2c^{2}\exp\left(-\frac{d(1-\delta^{2})^{2}}{8\delta^{4}}\right),

for some constant, independent of
d
that can be evaluated.
This gives us, for a fixed
\delta\in(0,1)
,

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\boldsymbol{\Theta}}R({\mathsf{Q}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y}),\boldsymbol{\theta})\geq\int_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}R({\mathsf{Q}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y}),\boldsymbol{\theta})dF_{\delta}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle-\frac{2}{d}\sqrt{2}\exp\left(-\frac{d(1-\delta^{2})^{2}}{16\delta^{4}}\right)Cd

\displaystyle-2c^{2}\exp\left(-\frac{d(1-\delta^{2})^{2}}{8\delta^{4}}\right)

(77)

Moreover, for the choice of prior
\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim{\cal N}\left(\mathbf{0},c^{2}\delta^{2}\mathbf{I}_{d}\right)
, Lemma
3.3
can be modified to lower bound the first term in eq. (
N
) as,

\displaystyle\int_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}R({\mathsf{Q}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y}),\boldsymbol{\theta})dF_{\delta}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\geq

\displaystyle\frac{c^{2}\delta^{2}\sigma^{2}}{\sigma^{2}+c^{2}\delta^{2}\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}}+\frac{c^{4}\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}}{\sigma^{2}+c^{2}\delta^{2}\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}}\cdot 2^{-2B}

\displaystyle-\frac{2}{d}\sqrt{2}\exp\left(-\frac{d(1-\delta^{2})^{2}}{16\delta^{4}}\right)Cd-2c^{2}\exp\left(-\frac{d(1-\delta^{2})^{2}}{8\delta^{4}}\right)

(78)

Note that inequality (
N
) holds true for any
\delta\in(0,1)
.
The particular choice of
\delta\in(0,1)
can be optimized to maximize the right hand side expression so as to ensure that the lower bound is tight.
It is worthwhile to note that since the last two terms are exponentially decaying in
d
while the first two terms are independent of
d
, for large enough (but finite)
d
, the lower bound is non-negative.
Moreover, for the optimal choice of
\delta
, it is difficult to obtain a neat closed form expression for the R.H.S. of eq. (
N
).

Appendix O
Extension to general noise models

Although we assume model
NPM
for deriving tight lower and upper bounds, our proposed model quantization algorithms are not limited to Gaussian noise.
For any arbitrary distribution of the noise
\mathbf{v}
, suppose our quantized model is given by
Q(\mathbf{X}^{\dagger})\mathbf{y}
, where
Q
can be
Q_{u,B}
, or
Q_{d,B}
, or
Q_{nd,B}
.
The
\ell_{2}
-risk can be upper bounded by,

\frac{1}{d}\mathbb{E}\lVert Q(\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y})-\boldsymbol{\theta}\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{2}{d}\mathbb{E}\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol{\theta}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\frac{2}{d}\mathbb{E}\lVert Q(\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y})-\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\rVert_{2}^{2}.

(79)

The first term,
\mathbb{E}\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol{\theta}\rVert_{2}^{2}=\mathbb{E}\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{v}\rVert_{2}^{2}=\textrm{Tr}\left[{\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}}^{\top}\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\textrm{Cov}\left(\mathbf{v}\right)\right]
contributes to the inherent learning risk, and is independent of the quantization scheme used.
For
naïve quantization
, in the worst-case the second term is,

\sup_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y}}\mathbb{E}\lVert Q_{u,B}(\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y})-\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\rVert_{2}^{2}=2^{-2B}d.

(80)

For
democratic quantization
, from Lemma
J
, in the worst case, we have,

\sup_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y}}\mathbb{E}\lVert Q_{u,B}(\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y})-\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq 2^{2\left(1-\frac{B}{\lambda}\right)}K_{u},

(81)

and for
near-democratic quantization
, from Lemma
L
,

\sup_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y}}\mathbb{E}\lVert Q_{u,B}(\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y})-\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq 2^{2\left(2-\frac{B}{\lambda}\right)}\log(2\lambda d).

(82)

These results do not require the Gaussianity of noise
\mathbf{v}
, and clearly, the scaling of the
\ell_{2}
-risk with dimension for
DQ
and
NDQ
are better than that of the naïve strategy.

Appendix P
Extension to
2
-Layer scalar output ReLU Neural Networks

A scalar output
2
-layer neural network can be represented as:

f(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{w}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}\right)_{+}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(\mathbf{w}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{x}\right)_{+}w_{j}\in\mathbb{R}.

(83)

Here,
f(\mathbf{x})
denotes the scalar output of the neural network,
(\cdot)_{+}=\max\{\cdot,0\}
is the ReLU activation,
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
denotes the weight matrix for the first layer,
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the input,
m
is the number of neurons in the first (hidden) layer.

\{\mathbf{w}_{1}^{\top},\ldots,\mathbf{w}_{m}^{\top}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote the rows of the weight matrix
\mathbf{W}
, whereas
\mathbf{w}\triangleq[w_{1},\ldots,w_{m}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
denote the weights of the second dense layer.
Note that for every
j
, we have
w_{j}=\lvert w_{j}\rvert\cdot\text{Sign}(w_{j})
.
Consequently, eq. (
83
) can be re-parametrized by absorbing
\lvert w_{j}\rvert
within the
(\cdot)_{+}
function.
Setting
\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{j}=\mathbf{w}_{j}\lvert w_{j}\rvert
, we get,

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{x}\right)_{+}\cdot\text{Sign}(w_{j})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j:w_{j}\geq 0}\left(\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{x}\right)_{+}-\sum_{j:w_{j}<0}\left(\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{x}\right)_{+}

(84)

Let us denote the set
\{j:w_{j}\geq 0\}
as
\mathrm{M}_{+}
and
\{j:w_{j}<0\}
as
\mathrm{M}_{-}
.
Suppose we quantize the weights of each layer to get
\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}
instead of the unquantized weights,
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{w}}
.
Here,
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}
is the matrix whose rows are
\{\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{1}^{\top},\ldots,\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{m}^{\top}\}
.
The output of the quantized neural network can be written as
\widetilde{f}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{x}\right)_{+}\cdot\text{Sign}(\widetilde{w}_{j})
, where
\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}_{1}^{\top},\ldots,\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}_{m}^{\top}
are the rows of
\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}
, and
\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}\triangleq[\widetilde{w}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{w}_{m}]
.
We then have the error in the scalar output of the quantized neural network as,

\displaystyle\left\lvert\widetilde{f}(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{x})\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\sum_{j\in M_{+}}\left[\left(\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{x}\right)_{+}-\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{x}\right)_{+}\right]-\sum_{j\in M_{-}}\left[\left(\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{x}\right)_{+}-\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{x}\right)_{+}\right]\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j\in M_{+}}\left\lvert\left(\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{x}\right)_{+}-\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{x}\right)_{+}\right\rvert+\sum_{j\in M_{-}}\left\lvert\left(\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{x}\right)_{+}-\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{x}\right)_{+}\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left\lvert\left(\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{x}\right)_{+}-\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{x}\right)_{+}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left\lvert\left(\left(\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{j}-\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}_{j}\right)^{\top}\mathbf{x}\right)_{+}\right\rvert\leq\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{j}-\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}_{j}\right\rVert_{2}\cdot\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{m}\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{j}-\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}_{j}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}\cdot\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}=\sqrt{m}\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{W}}-\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}\right\rVert_{\rm F}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}.

We essentially vectorize
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
and apply
DQ
or
NDQ
for quantizing the resultant vector.
Since
\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{W}}-\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}\rVert_{\rm F}=\lVert\mathrm{Vec}(\widehat{\mathbf{W}})-\mathrm{Vec}(\widetilde{\mathbf{W}})\rVert_{2}
, this suggests us that vectorizing the weight matrices and subsequently using
democratic
or
near-democratic
quantization strategies should prove beneficial over the naïve quantizaition strategy.
For simplicity of analysis, we assume
\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}\leq 1
and
\lVert\mathrm{Vec}(\widehat{\mathbf{W}})\rVert_{2}\leq 1
.
This assumption can be relaxed by taking in account appropriate scaling that depends on the Frobenius norm of the weight matrix.

From Lemma
L
, which is a guarantee on the quantization error of
NDQ
for any general vector input, if we use
NDQ
with a randomized Hadamard frame to quantize
\mathrm{Vec}(\widehat{\mathbf{W}})\in\mathbb{R}^{md}
with a bit-budget of
B
-bits per weight (which we do in our numerical simulations), we get,

\displaystyle\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}-\widehat{\mathbf{W}}\rVert_{\rm F}

\displaystyle=\lVert\mathrm{Vec}(\widetilde{\mathbf{W}})-\mathrm{Vec}(\widehat{\mathbf{W}})\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=\lVert Q_{nd,B}(\mathrm{Vec}(\widehat{\mathbf{W}}))-\mathrm{Vec}(\widehat{\mathbf{W}})\rVert_{2}\leq 2^{(2-B)}\sqrt{\log(2md)}.

This implies, to ensure
\left\lvert\widehat{f}(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{x})\right\rvert\leq\epsilon
for some error tolerance
\epsilon>0
, it suffices to ensure that

\displaystyle 4\sqrt{m}\cdot 2^{-B}\sqrt{\log\left(2md\right)}\leq\epsilon

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle B\geq\log_{2}\left(\frac{4}{\epsilon}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}m+\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}\log_{2}md

(85)

Let us define
B^{*}_{nd}(\epsilon)
to be the minimum bit budget required to achieve a modeling error of
\epsilon
, i.e. to ensure
\lvert\widetilde{f}(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{x})\rvert\leq\epsilon
, where
\widetilde{f}(\cdot)
and
f(\cdot)
denote the neural network functions with quantized and unquantized weights, respectively.
Then, from inequality (
P
), it follows that,

B^{*}_{nd}(\epsilon)\leq\log_{2}\left(\frac{4}{\epsilon}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}m+\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}\log_{2}md

(86)

Inequality (
P
) gives us an upper bound on the minimum number of bits required to achieve a given error
\epsilon
.
A similar upper bound could be obtained for naïve quantization by noting that the worst-case naïve quantizer error is upper bounded by

\lVert Q_{n,B}(\mathrm{Vec}(\widehat{\mathbf{W}}))-\mathrm{Vec}(\widehat{\mathbf{W}})\rVert_{2}\leq 2^{-B}\sqrt{md}.

This gives us,

\sqrt{m}\cdot 2^{-B}\sqrt{d}\leq\epsilon\implies B^{*}_{n}(\epsilon)\leq\log_{2}\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}(md)

(87)

Similarly, for democratic quantizer, Lemma
J
tells us,

\lVert Q_{d,B}(\mathrm{Vec}(\widehat{\mathbf{W}}))-\mathrm{Vec}(\widehat{\mathbf{W}})\rVert_{2}\leq 2^{\left(1-B\right)}K_{u},

giving us,

2\sqrt{m}\cdot 2^{-B}K_{u}\leq\epsilon\implies B_{d}^{*}(\epsilon)\leq\log_{2}\left(\frac{2K_{u}}{\epsilon}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}m.
