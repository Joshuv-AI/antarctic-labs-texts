Dichotomy of Early and Late Phase Implicit Biases Can Provably Induce Grokking
Kaifeng Lyu, Jikai Jin, Zhiyuan Li, Simon S. Du, Jason D. Lee, Wei Hu

Abstract
Recent work by Power et al. 2022 highlighted a surprising “grokking” phenomenon in learning arithmetic tasks: a neural net first “memorizes” the training set, resulting in perfect training accuracy but near-random test accuracy, and after training for sufficiently longer, it suddenly transitions to perfect test accuracy. This paper studies the grokking phenomenon in theoretical setups and shows that it can be induced by a dichotomy of early and late phase implicit biases. Specifically, when training homogeneous neural nets with large initialization and small weight decay on both classification and regression tasks, we prove that the training process gets trapped at a solution corresponding to a kernel predictor for a long time, and then a very sharp transition to min-norm/max-margin predictors occurs, leading to a dramatic change in test accuracy.

Introduction

The generalization behavior of modern over-parameterized neural nets has been puzzling: these nets have the capacity to overfit the training set, and yet they frequently exhibit a small gap between training and test performance when trained by popular gradient-based optimizers.
A common view now is that the network architectures and training pipelines can automatically induce regularization effects to avoid or mitigate overfitting throughout the training trajectory.

Recently,
Power et al. 2022
discovered an even more perplexing generalization phenomenon called
grokking
:
when training a neural net to learn modular arithmetic operations, it first “memorizes” the training set with zero training error and near-random test error, and then training for much longer leads to a sharp transition from no generalization to perfect generalization. See
Section

2
for our reproduction of this phenomenon.
Beyond modular arithmetic, grokking has been reported in learning group operations
(
Chughtai et al. 2023
)
, learning sparse parity
(
Barak et al. 2022
;
Bhattamishra et al. 2023
)
, learning greatest common divisor
(
Charton 2024
)
, and image classification
(
Liu et al. 2023
;
Radhakrishnan et al. 2022
)
.

Different viewpoints on the mechanism of grokking have been proposed, including the
slingshot mechanism (cyclic phase transitions)
(
Thilak et al. 2022
)
, random walk among minimizers
(
Millidge 2022
)
, slow formulation of good representations
(
Liu et al. 2022
)
, the scale of initialization
(
Liu et al. 2023
)
, and the simplicity of the generalizable solution
(
Nanda et al. 2023
;
Varma et al. 2023
)
.
However, existing studies failed to address two crucial aspects for gaining a comprehensive understanding of grokking:

1.

No prior work has rigorously proved grokking in a neural network setting.

2.

No prior work has provided a quantitative explanation as to why the transition from memorization to generalization is often sharp, instead of gradual.

Our Contributions.

In this work, we address these limitations by identifying simple yet insightful theoretical setups where grokking with sharp transition can be rigorously proved and its mechanism can be intuitively understood.
Our main intuition is that optimizers may implicitly induce different biases in early and late phases; grokking happens if the early phase bias implies an overfitting solution and late phase bias implies a generalizable solution.

More specifically, we focus on neural nets with large initialization and small weight decay. This is inspired by a recent work
(
Liu et al. 2023
)
showing that training with these two tricks can induce grokking on many tasks, even beyond learning modular arithmetic. Our theoretical analysis attributes this to the following
dichotomy
of early and late phase implicit biases:
the large initialization induces a very strong early phase implicit bias towards kernel predictors, but over time, it decays and competes with a late phase implicit bias towards min-norm/max-margin predictors induced by the small weight decay, resulting in a transition that turns out to be provably sharp in between.

This implicit bias result holds for homogeneous neural nets,
a broad class of neural nets that include commonly used MLPs and CNNs with homogeneous activation.
We further exemplify this dichotomy in sparse linear classification with diagonal linear nets and low-rank matrix completion with over-parameterized models, where we provide theory and experiments showing that the kernel predictor does not generalize well but the min-norm/max-margin predictors do, hence grokking happens (see
Figures

1
and
3
).

Based on these theoretical insights, we are able to construct examples where the early phase implicit bias leads to a generalizable solution, and the late phase bias leads to overfitting. This gives rise to a new phenomenon which we call “
misgrokking
”: a neural net first achieves perfect training and test accuracies, but training for a longer time leads to a sudden big drop in test accuracy. See
Figure

1
.

Our proof of the kernel regime in the early phase is related to the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) regime studied in the literature
(
Jacot et al. 2018
;
Chizat et al. 2019
)
.
However, our result is qualitatively different from existing NTK analyses in that in our setting, the weight norm changes significantly (due to weight decay), while in the usual NTK regime, the weight changes only by a small amount, and so does its norm.
Our proof relies on a careful analysis of the norm and direction of the weight, which enables us to obtain a tight bound on the time spent in the kernel regime.
This novel result of a kernel regime in homogeneous neural nets under weight decay may be of independent interest.
We further analyze the late phase using a gradient convergence argument, and it turns out that a slightly longer time suffices for convergence to the max-margin/min-norm predictor, thus exhibiting a sharp transition between the two phases.

Concretely, our contributions can be summarized as follows:

1.

For training homogeneous neural nets with large initialization and small weight decay on classification and regression tasks, we prove a very sharp transition from optimal solutions of kernel SVM/regression to KKT solutions of global max-margin/min-norm problems associated with the neural net.

2.

For classification and regression tasks respectively, we provide concrete examples with diagonal linear nets and overparameterized matrix completion, showing either grokking or misgrokking.

2
Motivating Experiment: Grokking in Modular Addition

In this section, we provide experiments to show that the initialization scale and weight decay are important factors in the grokking phenomenon, in the sense that enlarging the initialization scale or reducing the weight decay delays the sharp transition in test accuracy.

Grokking in Modular Addition.

Following many previous works
(
Nanda et al. 2023
;
Gromov 2023
)
,
we focus on
modular addition
, which belongs to the modular arithmetic tasks where the grokking phenomenon was first observed
(
Power et al. 2022
)
.
The task is to learn the addition operation over
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
: randomly split
\{(a,b,c):a+b\equiv c\pmod{p}\}
into training and test sets, and train a neural net on the training set to predict
c
given input pair
(a,b)
.
The grokking phenomenon has been observed in learning this task under many training regimes, including training transformers and MLPs on cross-entropy and squared loss with various optimizers (full-batch GD, SGD, Adam, AdamW, etc.)
(
Power et al. 2022
;
Liu et al. 2022
;
Thilak et al. 2022
;
Gromov 2023
)
. For simplicity, we focus on a simple setting without involving too many confounding factors: training a two-layer ReLU net with full-batch GD. More specifically, we represent the input
(a,b)\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}\times\mathbb{Z}_{p}
as the concatenation of the one-hot representations of
a
and
b
, resulting in an input vector
{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2p}
, and then the neural net processes the input as follows:

f({\bm{\theta}};{\bm{x}})={\bm{W}}_{2}\,\mathrm{ReLU}({\bm{W}}_{1}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{b}}_{1}),

(1)

where
{\bm{\theta}}=({\bm{W}}_{1},{\bm{b}}_{1},{\bm{W}}_{2})
is the parameter vector consisting of
{\bm{W}}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{h\times 2p},{\bm{b}}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{h},{\bm{W}}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times h}
,
h
is the width, and
\mathrm{ReLU}({\bm{v}})
stands for the ReLU activation that maps each entry
v_{i}
of a vector
{\bm{v}}
to
\max\{v_{i},0\}
.
The neural net output
f({\bm{\theta}};{\bm{x}})\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is treated as logits and is fed into the standard cross-entropy loss in training. We set the modulus
p
as
97
, the training data size as
40\%
of the total number of data (
p^{2}
), the width
h
as
1024
, the learning rate as
0.002
and weight decay as
10^{-4}
.
Figure

2
successfully reproduces the grokking phenomenon: the training accuracy reaches
100\%
in
10^{6}
steps while the test accuracy remains close to random guessing, but after training for one additional order of magnitude in the number of steps, the test accuracy suddenly jumps to
100\%
.

A natural question is how this grokking phenomenon depends on the training tricks in the pipeline.
Liu et al. 2023
showed that, empirically, using a large initialization scale and a small but non-zero weight decay can induce grokking on various tasks even beyond modular arithmetic, including image classification on MNIST and sentiment classification on IMDB.
Indeed, our experiments on modular addition can confirm the importance of initialization scale and weight decay.

Effect of Initialization Scale.

To study the effect of initialization, we scale up the standard He initialization
(
He et al. 2015
)
by a factor of
\alpha>0
, and then run the same training procedure. However, the model at large initialization produces a very large random number for each training sample, which induces training instability. To remove this confounding factor, we set the weights
{\bm{W}}_{2}
in the second layer to
{\bm{0}}
so that the initial model output is always
0
. Results in
Figure

2
show that the sharp transition in test accuracy is delayed when
\alpha
increases from
1
to
100
.

Effect of Weight Decay.

In
Figure

2
, we run experiments with different weight decay values. We observe that increasing weight decay makes the transition happen earlier. However, when weight decay surpasses a small threshold, the regularization strength can become so strong that it even hinders the optimization of the training loss and causes the training accuracy to collapse eventually. This collapse due to weight decay is also consistent with the observation in
Lewkowycz and Gur-Ari 2020
.
Conversely, as weight decay decreases, the grokking phenomenon becomes more significant because now it takes more time for the test accuracy to exhibit the sharp transition. But the weight decay has to be non-zero: we will discuss in
Section

B.1
that training without weight decay leads to perfect generalization in the end, but the transition in test accuracy is no longer sharp.

Explanation?

Liu et al. 2023
built their intuition upon an empirical observation from
Fort and Scherlis 2019
: there is a narrow range of weight norm, called
Goldilocks zone
, where the generalization inside the zone is better than the outside. It is argued that the weight norm can take a longer time to enter this Goldilocks zone when training with a larger initial weight norm or smaller weight decay, hence causing the delayed generalization. However,
Fort and Scherlis 2019
;
Liu et al. 2023
did not provide much explanation on why such a narrow Goldilocks zone could exist in the first place. When talking about MLPs and CNNs with ReLU activation, which are commonly used in practice, this becomes even more mysterious: the output functions
f({\bm{\theta}};{\bm{x}})
of these neural nets are homogeneous to their parameters, i.e.,
f(c{\bm{\theta}};{\bm{x}})=c^{L}f({\bm{\theta}};{\bm{x}})
for all
c>0

(
Lyu and Li 2020
;
Ji and Telgarsky 2020a
;
Kunin et al. 2023
)
. As a result, it is impossible to explain the effect of norm just from the expressive power, since all classifiers that can be represented with a certain norm can also be represented by all other norms. This motivates us to dive deep into the training dynamics in grokking via rigorous theoretical analysis.

3
Grokking with Large Initialization and Small Weight Decay

In this section, we present our theory on homogeneous neural nets with large initialization and small weight decay, which attributes grokking to a dichotomy of early and late phase implicit biases.

3.1
Theoretical Setup

We focus on training models for classification and regression. Let
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{X}}
be the input distribution. For every input
{\bm{x}}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{X}}
, let
y^{*}({\bm{x}})
be the classification/regression target of
{\bm{x}}
.
We parameterize the model with
{\bm{\theta}}
and use
f({\bm{\theta}};{\bm{x}})
to denote the model output on input
{\bm{x}}
.
We train the model by optimizing an empirical loss on a dataset
\{({\bm{x}}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
where the data points are drawn i.i.d. as
{\bm{x}}_{i}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{X}},y_{i}=y^{*}({\bm{x}}_{i})
.
For each
i\in[n]
, we write
f_{i}({\bm{\theta}}):=f({\bm{\theta}};{\bm{x}}_{i})
for short.
We assume the model is
homogeneous
with respect to its parameters
{\bm{\theta}}
, a common assumption in analyzing neural networks
(
Lyu and Li 2020
;
Ji and Telgarsky 2020a
;
Telgarsky 2023
;
Woodworth et al. 2020
)
.

Assumption 3.1
.

For all
{\bm{x}}
,
f({\bm{\theta}};{\bm{x}})
is
L
-homogeneous (i.e.,
f_{i}(c{\bm{\theta}})=c^{L}f_{i}({\bm{\theta}})
for all
c>0
) and
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-smooth with respect to
{\bm{\theta}}
.

The two-layer net in our motivating experiment and other MLPs and CNNs with ReLU activation are indeed
L
-homogeneous. However, we also need to assume the
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-smoothness to ease the definition and analysis of gradient flow, which excludes the use of any non-smooth activation. This can be potentially addressed by defining and analyzing gradient flow via Clarke’s subdifferential
(
Clarke 1975
;
Clarke 2001
;
Lyu and Li 2020
;
Ji and Telgarsky 2020a
)
, but we choose to make this assumption for simplicity. Note that our concrete examples of grokking on sparse linear classification and matrix completion indeed satisfy this assumption.

As motivated in
Section

2
, we consider training with large initialization and small weight decay. Mathematically, we start training from
\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}
, where
\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a fixed vector and
\alpha
is a large factor controlling the initialization scale.
We also assume a small but non-zero weight decay
\lambda>0
. To study the asymptotics more conveniently, we regard
\lambda
as a function of
\alpha

with order
\lambda(\alpha)=\Theta(\alpha^{-p})
for some positive
p=\Theta(1)
.

To avoid loss explosion at initialization, we deterministically set or randomly sample
\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}
in a way that the initial output of the model is zero. This can be done by setting the last layer weights to zero (same as our experiments in
Section

2
), using the symmetrized initialization in
Chizat et al. 2019
,
or using the “difference trick” in
Hu et al. 2020
.
This zero-output initialization is also used in
many previous studies of implicit bias
(
Chizat et al. 2019
;
Woodworth et al. 2020
;
Moroshko et al. 2020
)
for the sake of mathematical simplicity. We note that our analysis should be easily extended to random initialization with small outputs, just as in
Chizat et al. 2019
;
Arora et al. 2019b
.

Assumption 3.2
.

f(\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}};{\bm{x}})=0
for all
{\bm{x}}
.

We define the vanilla training loss as the average over the loss values of each individual sample:

\mathcal{L}({\bm{\theta}})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(f_{i}({\bm{\theta}});y_{i}).

(2)

With weight decay
\lambda
, it becomes the following regularized training loss:

\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}({\bm{\theta}})=\mathcal{L}({\bm{\theta}})+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|{\bm{\theta}}\|_{2}^{2}.

(3)

For simplicity, in this paper, we consider minimizing the loss via gradient flow
\frac{\mathrm{d}{\bm{\theta}}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\nabla\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}({\bm{\theta}})
, which is the continuous counterpart of gradient descent and stochastic gradient descent when the learning rate goes to
0
. We use
{\bm{\theta}}(t;{\bm{\theta}}_{0})
to denote the parameter at time
t
by running gradient flow from
{\bm{\theta}}_{0}
, and we write
{\bm{\theta}}(t)
when the initialization
{\bm{\theta}}_{0}
is clear from the context.

3.2
Grokking in Classification

In this subsection, we study the grokking phenomenon on classification tasks.
For simplicity, we restrict to binary classification problems, where
y^{*}({\bm{x}})\in\{-1,+1\}
.
While in practice it is standard to use the logistic loss
\ell(\hat{y},y)=\log(1+e^{-y\hat{y}})
(a.k.a. binary cross-entropy loss), we follow the implicit bias literature to analyze the exponential loss

\ell(\hat{y};y)=e^{-y\hat{y}}
as a simple surrogate, which has the same tail behavior as the logistic loss
when
y\hat{y}\to+\infty
and thus usually leads to the same implicit bias
(
Soudry et al. 2018
;
Nacson et al. 2019b
;
Lyu and Li 2020
;
Chizat and Bach 2020
)
.

By carefully analyzing the training dynamics, we rigorously prove that there is a sharp transition around
\frac{1}{\lambda}\log\alpha
. Before this point, gradient flow fits the training data perfectly while maintaining the parameter direction within a local region near the initial direction, which causes the classifier to behave like a kernel classifier based on Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK)
(
Jacot et al. 2018
;
Arora et al. 2019b
;
Arora et al. 2019c
)
. After the transition, however, the gradient flow escapes the local region and makes efforts to maximize the margin.
Following the nomenclature in
(
Moroshko et al. 2020
)
, we call the first regime the
kernel regime
and the second regime the
rich regime
.
The key difference to the existing works analyzing the kernel and rich regimes
(
Moroshko et al. 2020
;
Telgarsky 2023
)
is that the transition in our case is provably sharp, which is a crucial ingredient for the grokking phenomenon.

3.2.1
Kernel Regime

First, we show that gradient flow gets stuck in the kernel regime
over the initial period of
\frac{1-c}{\lambda}\log\alpha
,
where the model behaves as if it were optimizing over a linearized model. More specifically, for all
{\bm{x}}
, we define
\nabla f(\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}};{\bm{x}})
as the NTK feature of
{\bm{x}}
. As we are considering over-parameterized models, where the dimension of
{\bm{\theta}}
is larger than the number of data
n
, it is natural to assume that the NTK features of the training data are linearly separable
(
Ji and Telgarsky 2020b
;
Telgarsky 2023
)
:

Assumption 3.3
.

There exists
{\bm{h}}
such that
y_{i}\left\langle{\nabla f_{i}(\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}})},{{\bm{h}}}\right\rangle>0
for all
i\in[n]
.

Let
{\bm{h}}^{*}_{\mathrm{ntk}}
be the unit vector so that a linear classifier with weight
{\bm{h}}^{*}_{\mathrm{ntk}}
can attain the max
L^{2}
-margin on the NTK features of the training data,

\{(\nabla f_{i}(\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}),y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, and
\gamma_{\mathrm{ntk}}
be the corresponding
L^{2}
-margin.
That is,
{\bm{h}}^{*}_{\mathrm{ntk}}
is the unique unit vector that points to the direction of
the unique optimal solution
to the following constrained optimization problem:

\displaystyle\min\quad\frac{1}{2}\|{\bm{h}}\|_{2}^{2}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad y_{i}\left\langle{\nabla f_{i}(\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}})},{{\bm{h}}}\right\rangle\geq 1,\quad\forall i\in[n],

(K1)

and
\gamma_{\mathrm{ntk}}:=\min_{i\in[n]}\{y_{i}\langle{\nabla f_{i}(\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}})},{{\bm{h}}^{*}_{\mathrm{ntk}}}\rangle\}
.

The following theorem states that for any
c\in(0,1)
, the solution found by gradient flow at time
\frac{1-c}{\lambda}\log\alpha
represents the same classifier as the max
L^{2}
-margin linear classifier on the NTK features:

Theorem 3.4
.

For any all constants
c\in(0,1)
, letting

T^{-}_{\mathrm{c}}(\alpha):=\frac{1-c}{\lambda}\log\alpha
, it holds that

\displaystyle\forall{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\qquad\lim_{\alpha\to+\infty}\frac{1}{Z(\alpha)}f({\bm{\theta}}(T^{-}_{\mathrm{c}}(\alpha);\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}});{\bm{x}})=\left\langle{\nabla f(\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}};{\bm{x}})},{{\bm{h}}^{*}_{\mathrm{ntk}}}\right\rangle,

where
Z(\alpha):=\frac{1}{\gamma_{\mathrm{ntk}}}\log\frac{\alpha^{c}}{\lambda}
is a normalizing factor.

Key Proof Insight.

The standard NTK-based dynamical analysis requires
{\bm{\theta}}(t;\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}})\approx\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}
, but the weight decay in our case can significantly change the norm, hence violating this condition. The key ingredient of our proof is a much more careful dynamical analysis showing that
{\bm{\theta}}(t;\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}})\approx\alpha e^{-\lambda t}\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}
, i.e., the norm of
{\bm{\theta}}(t;\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}})
decays but the direction remains close to
\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}
. Then by
L
-homogeneity and Taylor expansion, we have

\displaystyle f({\bm{\theta}};{\bm{x}})=\left(\alpha e^{-\lambda t}\right)^{L}f(\tfrac{e^{\lambda t}}{\alpha}{\bm{\theta}};{\bm{x}})

\displaystyle\approx\left(\alpha e^{-\lambda t}\right)^{L}\left(f(\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}};{\bm{x}})+\left\langle{\nabla f(\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}};{\bm{x}})},{\tfrac{e^{\lambda t}}{\alpha}{\bm{\theta}}-\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}}\right\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=\alpha^{L}e^{-L\lambda t}\left\langle{\nabla f(\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}};{\bm{x}})},{\tfrac{e^{\lambda t}}{\alpha}{\bm{\theta}}-\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}}\right\rangle.

The large scaling factor
\alpha^{L}e^{-L\lambda t}
above enables fitting the dataset even when the change in direction
\tfrac{e^{\lambda t}}{\alpha}{\bm{\theta}}-\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}
is very small. Indeed, we show that
\tfrac{e^{\lambda t}}{\alpha}{\bm{\theta}}-\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}\approx\frac{1}{\gamma_{\mathrm{ntk}}}\alpha^{L}e^{-L\lambda t}\log\frac{\alpha^{c}}{\lambda}{\bm{h}}^{*}_{\mathrm{ntk}}
at time
\frac{1-c}{\lambda}\log\alpha
by closely tracking the dynamics, hence completing the proof.
See
Section

C.2
for details.
When
\alpha^{L}e^{-L\lambda t}
is no longer a large scaling factor, namely
t=\frac{1}{\lambda}(\log\alpha+\omega(1))
, this analysis breaks and the dynamics enter the rich regime.

3.2.2
Rich Regime

Next, we show that continuing the gradient flow for a slightly long time to time
\frac{1+c}{\lambda}\log\alpha
, it is able to escape the kernel regime. More specifically, consider the following constrained optimization problem that aims at maximizing the margin of the predictor
f({\bm{\theta}};{\bm{x}})
on the training data
\{({\bm{x}}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
:

\displaystyle\min\quad\frac{1}{2}\|{\bm{\theta}}\|_{2}^{2}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad y_{i}f_{i}({\bm{\theta}})\geq 1,\quad\forall i\in[n].

(R1)

Then we have the following directional convergence result:

Theorem 3.5
.

For all constants
c>0

and for every sequence
\{\alpha_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
with
\alpha_{k}\to+\infty
,
letting

T^{+}_{\mathrm{c}}(\alpha):=\frac{1+c}{\lambda}\log\alpha
,
there exists a time sequence
\{t_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}

such that

\frac{1}{\lambda}\log\alpha_{k}\leq t_{k}\leq T^{+}_{\mathrm{c}}(\alpha_{k})

and every limit point of

\left\{\frac{{\bm{\theta}}(t_{k};\alpha_{k}\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}})}{\|{\bm{\theta}}(t_{k};\alpha_{k}\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}})\|_{2}}:k\geq 1\right\}

is along the direction of a KKT point of (
R1
).

For the problem
Equation

R1
, the KKT condition is a first-order necessary condition for global optimality, but it is not sufficient in general since
f_{i}({\bm{\theta}})
can be highly non-convex. Nonetheless, since gradient flow can easily get trapped at spurious minima with only first-order information, the KKT condition is widely adopted as a surrogate for global optimality in theoretical analysis
(
Lyu and Li 2020
;
Wang et al. 2021
;
Kunin et al. 2023
)
.

Key Proof Insight.

The key is to use the loss convergence and norm decay bounds from the kernel regime as a starting point to obtain a small upper bound for the gradient
\nabla\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}({\bm{\theta}})
. Then we connect the gradient upper bounds with KKT conditions. See
Section

C.3
for the proof.

3.2.3
Example: Linear Classification with Diagonal Linear Nets

Now, we exemplify how our implicit bias results imply a sharp transition in test accuracy in a concrete setting: training diagonal linear nets for linear classification. Let
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{X}}
be a distribution over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
y^{*}({\bm{x}})=\sign(\left\langle{{\bm{w}}^{*}},{{\bm{x}}}\right\rangle)\in\{\pm 1\}
be the ground-truth target for binary classification, where
{\bm{w}}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is an unknown vector.
Following
Moroshko et al. 2020
, we consider training a so-called two-layer “diagonal” linear net to learn
y^{*}
. On input
{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the model outputs the following function
f({\bm{\theta}};{\bm{x}})
, where
{\bm{\theta}}:=({\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{2d}
is the model parameter.

\displaystyle f({\bm{\theta}};{\bm{x}})=\sum_{k=1}^{d}u_{k}^{2}x_{k}-\sum_{k=1}^{d}v_{k}^{2}x_{k}.

(4)

This model is considered a diagonal net because it can be seen as a sparsely-connected two-layer net with
2d
hidden neurons, each of which only inputs and outputs a single scalar. More specifically,
the input
{\bm{x}}
is first expanded to an
2d
-dimensional vector
({\bm{x}},-{\bm{x}})
. Then the
k
-th hidden neuron in the first half inputs
x_{k}
and outputs
u_{k}x_{k}
, and the
k
-th hidden neuron in the second half inputs
-x_{k}
and outputs
v_{k}x_{k}
.
The output neuron in the second layer shares the same weights as the hidden neurons and takes a weighted sum over the hidden neuron outputs according to their weights:
f({\bm{\theta}};{\bm{x}})=\sum_{k=1}^{d}u_{k}\cdot(u_{k}\cdot x_{k})+\sum_{k=1}^{d}v_{k}\cdot(v_{k}\cdot(-x_{k}))
.
Note that the model output is always linear with the input.
For convenience, we can write
f({\bm{\theta}};{\bm{x}})=\left\langle{{\bm{w}}({\bm{\theta}})},{{\bm{x}}}\right\rangle

with effective weight
{\bm{w}}({\bm{\theta}}):={\bm{u}}^{\odot 2}-{\bm{v}}^{\odot 2}
.

In this setup, we can understand the early and late phase implicit biases very concretely.
We start training from a large initialization:
{\bm{\theta}}(0)=\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}
, where
\alpha>0
controls the initialization scale and
\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}=({\bm{1}},{\bm{1}})
. That is,
u_{k}=v_{k}=\alpha
for all
1\leq k\leq d
at
t=0
.
As noted in
Moroshko et al. 2020
, the kernel feature for each data point is
\nabla f(\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}};{\bm{x}})=(2{\bm{x}},-2{\bm{x}})
, so the max
L^{2}
-margin kernel classifier is the max
L^{2}
-margin linear classifier. This leads to the following corollary:

Corollary 3.6
(Diagonal linear nets, kernel regime).

Let
T^{-}_{\mathrm{c}}(\alpha):=\frac{1-c}{\lambda}\log\alpha
.
For all constants
c\in(0,1)
, as
\alpha\to+\infty
, the normalized effective weight vector
\frac{{\bm{w}}({\bm{\theta}}(T^{-}_{\mathrm{c}}(\alpha);\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}))}{\|{\bm{w}}({\bm{\theta}}(T^{-}_{\mathrm{c}}(\alpha);\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}))\|_{2}}
converges to the max
L^{2}
-margin direction, namely
{\bm{w}}^{*}_{2}:=\argmax_{{\bm{w}}\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}\{\min_{i\in[n]}y_{i}\langle{{\bm{w}}},{{\bm{x}}_{i}}\rangle\}
.

In contrast, if we train slightly longer from
T^{-}_{\mathrm{c}}(\alpha)
to
T^{+}_{\mathrm{c}}(\alpha)
, we can obtain a KKT point of (
R1
).

Corollary 3.7
(Diagonal linear nets, rich regime).

Let
T^{+}_{\mathrm{c}}(\alpha):=\frac{1+c}{\lambda}\log\alpha
.
For all constants
c\in(0,1)
, and for every sequence
\{\alpha_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
with
\alpha_{k}\to+\infty
,
there exists a time sequence
\{t_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}

such that

\frac{1}{\lambda}\log\alpha_{k}\leq t_{k}\leq T^{+}_{\mathrm{c}}(\alpha_{k})

and every limit point of
\left\{\frac{{\bm{\theta}}(t_{k};\alpha_{k}\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}})}{\|{\bm{\theta}}(t_{k};\alpha_{k}\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}})\|_{2}}:k\geq 1\right\}

is along the direction of a KKT point of the problem

\min\|{\bm{u}}\|_{2}^{2}+\|{\bm{v}}\|_{2}^{2}
s.t.
y_{i}\langle{{\bm{u}}^{\odot 2}-{\bm{v}}^{\odot 2}},{{\bm{x}}_{i}}\rangle\geq 1
.

As noted in these two works, in the diagonal linear case, optimizing (
R1
) to the global optimum is equivalent to finding the max
L^{1}
-margin linear classifier:
\min\|{\bm{w}}\|_{1}
s.t.
y_{i}\langle{{\bm{w}}},{{\bm{x}}_{i}}\rangle\geq 1,\forall i\in[n]
, or equivalently,
{\bm{w}}^{*}_{1}:=\argmax_{{\bm{w}}\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}\{\min_{i\in[n]}y_{i}\langle{{\bm{w}}},{{\bm{x}}_{i}}\rangle\}
.
Therefore, our theory suggests a sharp transition at time
\frac{1}{\lambda}\log\alpha
from max
L^{2}
-margin linear classifier to max
L^{1}
-margin linear classifier.

While this corollary only shows KKT conditions rather than global optimality, we believe that one may be able to obtain the global optimality using insights from existing analysis of training diagonal linear nets without weight decay
(
Gunasekar et al. 2018b
;
Moroshko et al. 2020
)
.

Empirical Validation: Grokking.

As it is well known that maximizing the
L^{1}
-margin can better encourage sparsity than maximizing the
L^{2}
-margin, our theory predicts that the grokking phenomenon can be observed in training diagonal linear nets for
k
-sparse linear classification. To verify this, we specifically consider the following task: sample
n

\{\pm 1\}^{d}
for a very large

-sparse weight, where the non-zero coordinates are sampled uniformly from
\{\pm 1\}^{k}

Applying standard generalization bounds based on Rademacher complexity can show that the max
L^{2}
-margin linear classifier needs
\mathcal{O}(kd)
to generalize, while the max
L^{1}
-margin linear classifier only needs
\mathcal{O}(k^{2}\log d)
. See
Section

E.2
for the proof.
This suggests that the grokking phenomenon can be observed in this setting.
In
Figure

, we run experiments with

Empirical Validation: Misgrokking.

From the above example, we can see that grokking can be induced by the mismatch between the early-phase implicit bias and data and a good match between the late-phase implicit bias and data. However, this match and mismatch can largely depend on data. Conversely, if we consider the case where the labels in the linear classification problem are generated by a linear classifier with a large
L^{2}
-margin, the early phase implicit bias can make the neural net generalize easily, but the late phase implicit bias can destroy this good generalization since the ground-truth weight vector may not have a large

-margin.
To justify this, we first sample a unit-norm vector

(i.e., a Gaussian distribution that is separated by a margin in the middle). We take

3.3
Grokking in Regression

In this subsection, we study the grokking phenomenon on regression tasks with squared loss
\ell(\hat{y},y)=(\hat{y}-y)^{2}

\frac{1}{\lambda}\log\alpha

3.3.1
Kernel Regime

Similar to the classification setting, we first show that gradient flow gets stuck in the kernel regime over the initial period of
\frac{1-c}{\lambda}\log\alpha
time.
For all
{\bm{x}}
, we define
\nabla f(\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}};{\bm{x}})
as the NTK feature of
{\bm{x}}
. For over-parameterized models, where the dimension of

is larger than the number of data

;
Chizat et al. 2019

Assumption 3.8

The NTK features of training samples

Now we let
{\bm{h}}^{*}_{\mathrm{ntk}}
be the vector with minimum norm such that the linear predictor

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \min\quad\frac{1}{2}\|{\bm{h}}\|_{2}^{2}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\left\langle{\nabla f_{i}(\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}})},{{\bm{h}}}\right\rangle=y_{i},\quad\forall i\in[n].\end{aligned}

(K2)

Then we have the following result that is analogous to
Theorem

C.2
for the proof.

Theorem 3.9

For all constants
c\in(0,1)
, letting

T^{-}_{\mathrm{c}}(\alpha):=\frac{1-c}{\lambda}\log\alpha

\displaystyle\forall{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\qquad\lim_{\alpha\to+\infty}f({\bm{\theta}}(T^{-}_{\mathrm{c}}(\alpha);\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}});{\bm{x}})=\left\langle{\nabla f(\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}};{\bm{x}})},{{\bm{h}}^{*}_{\mathrm{ntk}}}\right\rangle.

3.3.2
Rich Regime

Similar to the classification setting, we then show that gradient flow is able to escape the kernel regime at time
\frac{1+c}{\lambda}\log\alpha

\displaystyle\min\quad\frac{1}{2}\|{\bm{\theta}}\|_{2}^{2}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad f_{i}({\bm{\theta}})=y_{i},\quad\forall i\in[n].

(R2)

Then we have the following convergence result. See
Section

D.2
for the proof.

Theorem 3.10
.

For any constant
c>0
, letting

T^{+}_{\mathrm{c}}(\alpha):=\frac{1+c}{\lambda}\log\alpha
,
for any sequence of
\{\alpha_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
with
\alpha_{k}\to+\infty
,
there exists a time sequence
\{t_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}

satisfying

\frac{1}{\lambda}\log\alpha_{k}\leq t_{k}\leq T^{+}_{\mathrm{c}}(\alpha_{k})

\|{\bm{\theta}}(t_{k};\alpha_{k}\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}})\|_{2}

4
Conclusion

In this paper, we show that the grokking phenomenon provably occurs in several setups.
Our results suggest that the sharp transition in test accuracy may stem from a dichotomy of implicit biases between the early and late training phases. While the early phase bias guides the optimizer to overfitting solutions, it is quickly corrected when the late phase bias takes effect.

Some limitations of our work are as follows. First, our work only studies the training dynamics with large initialization and small weight decay, but these may not be the only source of the dichotomy of the implicit biases. We further discuss in
Appendix

B
that the late phase implicit bias can also be induced by implicit margin maximization and sharpness reduction, though the transition may not be as sharp as the weight decay case. Also, our work focuses on understanding the cause of grokking but does not study how to make neural nets generalize without so much delay in time. We leave it to future work to explore the other sources of grokking and practical methods to eliminate grokking.

Appendix A
Additional Related Works

Identifying Mechanisms that Cause Grokking.

Some works attempted to identify mechanisms that cause grokking through experiments.

Davies et al. 2022
hypothesized that both grokking and double descent are the result of the existence of two patterns: one pattern is faster to learn but generalizes poorly, and the other pattern is slower to learn but generalizes well. However, it is unclear how these notions can be rigorously defined for general training dynamics.
Similar to the work of
Liu et al. 2023
that we discussed in
Section

2
,

Varma et al. 2023
hypothesized that grokking happens when the generalizable solutions have a smaller parameter norm than the overfitting solutions, but the former takes a longer time to learn. They also demonstrated that violating a part of this condition indeed changes the generalization behavior from grokking to “ungrokking” and “semi-grokking”, two interesting phenomena they named in the paper.
For training with adaptive gradient methods,
Thilak et al. 2022
identified the Slingshot mechanism, which refers to a cyclic behavior of the last-layer parameter norm that empirically causes grokking.
Notsawo Jr et al. 2023
also discussed a similar relationship between the oscillations in training loss and grokking. For the task of learning sparse parity,
Merrill et al. 2023
empirically observed that the number of active neurons significantly decrease as the test accuracy starts to improve.
While many explanations of grokking may make sense intuitively, none of these works provides rigorous justification their claims with mathematical analysis for neural net training, while our work is grounded by theoretical analyses of implicit biases in the kernel and rich regimes.

Progress Measures of Grokking.

Efforts have been made to find progress measures that are improving smoothly and are predictive of the time to perfect generalization.

Nanda et al. 2023
,
Chughtai et al. 2023
,
Gromov 2023
focused on the tasks of learning modular arithmetic or more general group operations, and attempted to reverse engineer the weights of neural nets after grokking. It was found that the weights can exhibit special structures to make the neural net internally compute trigonometric functions or other representations of group elements. These works also made efforts to define progress measures for grokking based on this understanding of the final weights. In a related study,
Morwani et al. 2024
derived analytical formulas for the weights of two-layer neural nets, assuming the margin is ultimately maximized.

Hu et al. 2023
computed a variety of metrics throughout training and fit a Hidden Markov Model (HMM) over them to study the phase transition in grokking.
A common issue with these works is that why progress measures themselves can make progress is still not well understood and requires new insights into the training dynamics, which is the focus of our work.

Dynamical Analysis of Grokking.

A few recent works were devoted to theoretically analyze the training dynamics in the grokking phenomenon.

Liu et al. 2022
attributed the delayed generalization in grokking to the slow learning speed of the input embeddings. They especially analyzed the dynamics of the input embeddings in a related optimization problem, and showed that the eigenvalues of the coefficient matrix in the ODE can be used to predict the convergence. However, it is unclear how this relates to the training dynamics of the original problem and why the neural net overfits the dataset before the predicted convergence time.

Žunkovič and Ilievski 2022
analyzed a simple linear classification problem, where the loss is the squared loss with explicit
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
regularization. Assuming specific input distributions, they proved sharp transition in test accuracy and derived an estimate of the grokking time, which depends on the initialization and regularization strength.
Levi et al. 2024
leveraged random matrix theory to analyze grokking in a standard linear regression setting, where the test accuracy can provably exhibit sharp transition if the accuracy is defined as the fraction of points whose regression loss is smaller than a small threshold.
All these works are limited to linear models, while our work is able to analyze deep homogeneous neural nets.

Concurrent Works.

Concurrent to our work,
Kumar et al. 2024
hypothesized that grokking is due to a transition from lazy to rich regimes, and observed that manipulating the scale parameter for the output and the task-model alignment between NTK and ground-truth labels can control the time to escape the lazy regime and the test accuracy when staying in the lazy regime. This observation is consistent with our work, but we provide rigorous analyses for both lazy and rich regimes with quantitative bounds.

Xu et al. 2024
proved that training two-layer neural nets on XOR cluster data with noisy labels can exhibit grokking. Both their work and our work go beyond linear models, but their analysis does not show whether the transition in test accuracy is sharp or not, while our work provides quantitative bounds for the sharp transition in grokking.

Implicit Bias.

A line of works seek to characterize the implicit bias of optimization algorithms that drive them to generalizable solutions. Several forms of implicit bias have been considered, including equivalence to NTK
[
Du et al. 2019b
,
Du et al. 2019a
,
Allen-Zhu et al. 2019
,
Zou et al. 2020
,
Chizat et al. 2019
,
Arora et al. 2019b
,
Ji and Telgarsky 2020b
,
Cao and Gu 2019
]
, margin maximization
[
Soudry et al. 2018
,
Nacson et al. 2019a
,
Lyu and Li 2020
,
Ji and Telgarsky 2020a
]
, parameter norm minimization
[
Gunasekar et al. 2017
,
Gunasekar et al. 2018a
,
Arora et al. 2019a
]
and sharpness minimization
[
Blanc et al. 2020
,
Damian et al. 2021
,
HaoChen et al. 2021
,
Li et al. 2022
,
Lyu et al. 2022
,
Gu et al. 2023
]
. In this work, we characterize the early phase implicit bias based on NTK and late phase implicit bias based on margin maximization.

Appendix B
Discussion: Other Implicit Biases Can Also Induce Grokking

The main paper focuses on the grokking phenomenon induced by large initialization and small weight decay, where the transition in the implicit bias is very sharp:
\frac{1-c}{\lambda}\log\alpha
leads to the kernel predictor, but increasing it slightly to
\frac{1+c}{\lambda}\log\alpha
leads to a KKT solution of min-norm/max-margin problems associated with the neural net.
In this section, we discuss two other possible sources of grokking: implicit margin maximization and sharpness reduction. These two sources can also cause the neural net to generalize eventually, but the transition in test accuracy is not as sharp as the case of using large initialization and small weight decay.

In

We empirically show that training two-layer neural nets without weight decay indeed leads to grokking, but the transition is no longer sharp.
More specifically, we use the same setting as in

We focus on analyzing the training dynamics of full-batch gradient descent with learning rate so small that the trajectory is close to the gradient flow, which ensures that gradient noise/training instability is not a potential source of implicit regularization.

and 1-SAM
[
Wen et al. 2023
]
. The high-level idea here is that the minimizers of the training loss connect as a Riemannian manifold due to the overparametrization in the model. Training usually contains two phases for algorithms which implicitly minimize the sharpness when the learning rate is small, where in the first phase the algorithm minimizes the training loss and finds a minimizer with poor generalization, while in the second phase the algorithm implicitly minimizes the sharpness along the manifold of the minimizers, and the model groks when the sharpness is sufficiently small. The second phase is typically longer than the first phase by magnitude and this causes grokking.

As a concrete example,
Li et al. 2022
shows that grokking happens when training two-layer diagonal linear networks
Equation

4
by label noise SGD (
6
) when the learning rate is sufficiently small. We consider the same initialization as in
Section

3.2.3
, where
{\bm{\theta}}(0)=\alpha({\bm{1}},{\bm{1}})
, where
\alpha>0
controls the initialization scale. Different from
Section

3.2.3
, we consider a
\ell_{2}
loss, and the label is generated by a sparse linear ground-truth, that is,
{\bm{y}}^{*}({\bm{x}})={\bm{x}}^{\top}{\bm{w}}^{*}
, where
{\bm{w}}^{*}
is
\kappa
-sparse. We also assume data distribution is the isotropic Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,I)
.

\displaystyle{\bm{\theta}}(t+1)={\bm{\theta}}(t)-\eta\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}(f(\theta;{\bm{x}}_{i_{t}})-y_{i_{t}}+\xi_{t})^{2},\text{where }\xi_{t}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,1)

(6)

Theorem B.1
.

There exists
C_{1}>0,C_{2}\in(0,1)
, such that for any
n,d
satisfying
C_{1}\kappa\ln d\leq n\leq d(1-C_{2})
, the following holds with
1-e^{-\Omega(n)}
probability with respect to randomness of training data:

1.

The gradient flow starting of
\mathcal{L}
from
{\bm{\theta}}(0)
has a limit,
{\bm{\theta}}_{GF}
, which achieves zero training loss. The expected population loss
{\bm{\theta}}_{GF}
is at least
\Omega(\|{\bm{w}}^{*}\|_{2}^{2})
. (Theorem 6.7)

2.

For any

and

,
{\bm{\theta}}(T/\eta^{\alpha})
converges to
{\bm{\theta}}_{GF}
in distribution as

. (Implication of Theorem B.9)

3.

For any

converges to some

in distribution as

and

The above theorem shows that a sharp transition of the implicit biases can be seen in the log time scale: when

Appendix C
Proofs for Classification with L2 Regularization

C.1
Preliminaries

Let
F_{\mathrm{LL}}:[1,+\infty)\to[0,+\infty),x\mapsto x\log x
be the linear-times-log function,
and
F_{\mathrm{LL}}^{-1}:[0,+\infty)\to[1,+\infty)
be its inverse function.

Lemma C.1
.

For all
y>0
,
F_{\mathrm{LL}}^{-1}(y)\geq\frac{y}{\log(y+1)}
.

Proof.

Since
F_{\mathrm{LL}}
is an increasing function, it suffices to show
F_{\mathrm{LL}}(\frac{y}{\log(y+1)})\leq y
,
and this can be indeed proved by

\displaystyle F_{\mathrm{LL}}\left(\frac{y}{\log(y+1)}\right)\leq\frac{y}{\log(y+1)}\log\frac{y}{\log(y+1)}=y\cdot\frac{\log\frac{y}{\log(y+1)}}{\log(y+1)}\leq y,

where the last inequality holds because
\frac{y}{\log(y+1)}\leq y+1
.

Lemma C.2
.

For all
y>0
and
\beta\in(0,1)
,

\displaystyle F_{\mathrm{LL}}^{-1}(\beta y)-1\geq\beta(F_{\mathrm{LL}}^{-1}(y)-1).

Proof.

By concavity of
F_{\mathrm{LL}}^{-1}
,
F_{\mathrm{LL}}^{-1}(\beta y)\geq\beta F_{\mathrm{LL}}^{-1}(y)+(1-\beta)F_{\mathrm{LL}}^{-1}(0)
.
Rearranging the terms gives the desired result.

Lemma C.3
.

The following holds for
r_{1},\dots,r_{n}>0
and
\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\log\frac{1}{n\mathcal{L}}\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}\log\frac{1}{r_{i}}\leq\mathcal{L}\log\frac{1}{\mathcal{L}}.

Proof.

For proving the first inequality, note that

\log\frac{1}{r_{i}}\geq\log\frac{1}{n\mathcal{L}}
,
so

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}\log\frac{1}{r_{i}}\geq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}\log\frac{1}{n\mathcal{L}}=\mathcal{L}\log\frac{1}{n\mathcal{L}}.

For the other inequality,
by Jensen’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}\log\frac{1}{r_{i}}=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}F_{\mathrm{LL}}(r_{i})\leq-F_{\mathrm{LL}}(\mathcal{L})=\mathcal{L}\log\frac{1}{\mathcal{L}},

which completes the proof.

C.2
Kernel Regime

Now we present the proof of
Theorem

3.4
for the kernel regime.
We follow the notations in
Section

3.2.1
and define
{\bm{h}}^{*}_{\mathrm{ntk}}
as the unique unit vector
along the direction of the optimal solution to
Equation

K1

and
\gamma_{\mathrm{ntk}}:=\min_{i\in[n]}\{y_{i}\langle{\nabla f_{i}(\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}})},{{\bm{h}}^{*}_{\mathrm{ntk}}}\rangle\}
.
The kernel regime is a training regime where the parameter does not move very far from the initialization. Mathematically, let
\epsilon_{\max}>0
be a small constant so that

\max_{i\in[n]}\{\|\nabla f_{i}({\bm{\theta}})-\nabla f_{i}(\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}})\|_{2}:i\in[n]\}<\frac{\gamma_{\mathrm{ntk}}}{2}

holds for all
\|{\bm{\theta}}-\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}\|_{2}<\epsilon_{\max}
,
and let
T_{\max}:=\inf\left\{t\geq 0:\left\|\frac{e^{\lambda t}}{\alpha}{\bm{\theta}}(t)-\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}\right\|_{2}>\epsilon_{\max}\right\}
be the time that
{\bm{\theta}}(t)
moves far enough to change the NTK features significantly.

A key difference to existing analyses of the kernel regime is that our setting has weight decay, so the norm can change a lot even when the parameter direction has not changed much yet. Therefore, we normalize the parameter by multiplying a factor of
\frac{e^{\lambda t}}{\alpha}
and compare it to the initial parameter direction. We consider
{\bm{\theta}}(t)
as moving too far only if
\|\frac{e^{\lambda t}}{\alpha}{\bm{\theta}}(t)-\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}\|_{2}
is too large, which is different from existing analyses in that they usually consider the absolute moving distance
\left\|{\bm{\theta}}(t)-{\bm{\theta}}(0)\right\|_{2}
.

In the following, we first derive an upper bound for the loss convergence rate, and then use it to lower bound the training time in the kernel regime
T_{\max}\geq\frac{1}{\lambda}(\log\alpha-\frac{1}{L}\log\log A+\Omega(1))
.
Finally, we establish the implicit bias towards the kernel predictor with a more careful analysis of the trajectory.

More notations are needed for the proof.
Let
q_{i}({\bm{\theta}}):=y_{i}f_{i}({\bm{\theta}})
be the output margin of the neural net at the
i
-th training sample. Let
r_{i}({\bm{\theta}})=\ell(f_{i}({\bm{\theta}}),y_{i})=\exp(-q_{i}({\bm{\theta}}))
be the loss incurred at the
i
-th training sample.
Let
A:=\frac{\alpha^{2(L-1)}}{\lambda}
, which is a factor that will appear very often in our proof. Let
G_{\mathrm{ntk}}:=\sup\{\|\nabla f_{i}({\bm{\theta}})\|_{2}:\|{\bm{\theta}}-\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}\|_{2}<\epsilon_{\max},i\in[n]\}
be the maximum norm of NTK features.
The following lemma gives an upper bound for the training loss over time.

Lemma C.4
.

For all
0\leq t\leq T_{\max}
,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{\gamma_{\mathrm{ntk}}^{2}}{4}\alpha^{2(L-1)}e^{-2(L-1)\lambda t}\mathcal{L}^{2}+\lambda L\cdot\mathcal{L}\log\frac{1}{\mathcal{L}},

(7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}({\bm{\theta}}(t))

\displaystyle\leq\max\left\{\frac{1}{1+\frac{\gamma_{\mathrm{ntk}}^{2}}{16(L-1)}A(1-e^{-2(L-1)\lambda t})},\frac{e^{2(L-1)\lambda t}}{F_{\mathrm{LL}}^{-1}(\frac{\gamma_{\mathrm{ntk}}^{2}}{8L}A)-1}\right\}.

(8)

Proof.

By the update rule of
{\bm{\theta}}
, the following holds for all
t>0
:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\left\langle{\nabla\mathcal{L}({\bm{\theta}})},{\frac{\mathrm{d}{\bm{\theta}}}{\mathrm{d}t}}\right\rangle=\underbrace{-\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}\nabla q_{i}({\bm{\theta}})\right\|_{2}^{2}}_{=:V_{1}}+\underbrace{\frac{\lambda L}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}q_{i}}_{=:V_{2}}.

For
V_{1}
, we have

\displaystyle-V_{1}=\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}\nabla q_{i}({\bm{\theta}})\right\|_{2}^{2}\geq\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}\left\langle{\nabla q_{i}({\bm{\theta}})},{{\bm{h}}^{*}_{\mathrm{ntk}}}\right\rangle\right)^{2}.

(9)

Note that for all
t\leq T_{\max}
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}\left\langle{\nabla q_{i}({\bm{\theta}})},{{\bm{h}}^{*}_{\mathrm{ntk}}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}\left(\left\langle{\nabla q_{i}(\alpha e^{-\lambda t}\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}})},{{\bm{h}}^{*}_{\mathrm{ntk}}}\right\rangle-\|\nabla f_{i}({\bm{\theta}})-\nabla f_{i}(\alpha e^{-\lambda t}\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}})\|_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}\cdot\alpha^{L-1}e^{-(L-1)\lambda t}\left(\gamma_{\mathrm{ntk}}-\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{\mathrm{ntk}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma_{\mathrm{ntk}}}{2}\alpha^{L-1}e^{-(L-1)\lambda t}\mathcal{L},

where the second inequality is due to

\left\langle{\nabla q_{i}(\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}})},{{\bm{h}}^{*}_{\mathrm{ntk}}}\right\rangle\geq\gamma_{\mathrm{ntk}}
,

\left\|\nabla f_{i}(\frac{e^{\lambda t}}{\alpha}{\bm{\theta}}(t))-\nabla f_{i}(\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}})\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{\gamma_{\mathrm{ntk}}}{2}

and the
(L-1)
-homogeneity of
\nabla f_{i}
.
Together with (

we obtain the following upper bound for

\displaystyle V_{1}\leq-\frac{\gamma_{\mathrm{ntk}}^{2}}{4}\alpha^{2(L-1)}e^{-2(L-1)\lambda t}\mathcal{L}^{2}.

(10)

For
V_{2}
, by

C.3

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}\log\frac{1}{r_{i}}\leq\mathcal{L}\log\frac{1}{\mathcal{L}}
.
So we have the following upper bound for

\displaystyle V_{2}\leq\lambda L\cdot\mathcal{L}\log\frac{1}{\mathcal{L}}.

(11)

Combining this with (
10

7
).

For proving (
8
),
we first divide by

on both sides of (

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\frac{1}{\mathcal{L}}\geq\frac{\gamma_{\mathrm{ntk}}^{2}}{4}\alpha^{2(L-1)}e^{-2(L-1)\lambda t}-\lambda L\cdot\frac{1}{\mathcal{L}}\log\frac{1}{\mathcal{L}}.

(12)

Let

For all

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}({\bm{\theta}}(t))\leq\frac{1}{F_{\mathrm{LL}}^{-1}\left(\frac{\gamma_{\mathrm{ntk}}^{2}}{8L}Ae^{-2(L-1)\lambda t}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{1+\left(F_{\mathrm{LL}}^{-1}(\frac{\gamma_{\mathrm{ntk}}^{2}}{8L}A)-1\right)e^{-2(L-1)\lambda t}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\left(F_{\mathrm{LL}}^{-1}(\frac{\gamma_{\mathrm{ntk}}^{2}}{8L}A)-1\right)e^{-2(L-1)\lambda t}}\leq\frac{e^{2(L-1)\lambda t}}{F_{\mathrm{LL}}^{-1}(\frac{\gamma_{\mathrm{ntk}}^{2}}{8L}A)-1}.

Otherwise, let

be the largest number in

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathcal{L}({\bm{\theta}}(t))}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathcal{L}({\bm{\theta}}(t^{\prime}))}+\int_{t^{\prime}}^{t}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\frac{1}{\mathcal{L}}\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\mathcal{L}({\bm{\theta}}(t^{\prime}))}+\int_{t^{\prime}}^{t}\frac{\gamma_{\mathrm{ntk}}^{2}}{8}\alpha^{2(L-1)}e^{-2(L-1)\lambda\tau}\,\mathrm{d}\tau

There exists a constant

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{1}{\alpha}e^{\lambda t}{\bm{\theta}}(t)-\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}\right\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\alpha^{-L}\log A\right)\cdot e^{L\lambda t}.

By definition of

Applying the gradient upper bounds gives

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha}\left\|e^{\lambda t}{\bm{\theta}}(t)-\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\geq-\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}\nabla q_{i}({\bm{\theta}})\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq-\alpha^{2(L-1)}e^{-2(L-1)\lambda t}\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}\nabla q_{i}(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}e^{\lambda t}{\bm{\theta}})\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq-\alpha^{2(L-1)}e^{-2(L-1)\lambda t}G_{\mathrm{ntk}}^{2}\mathcal{L}^{2},

where the last inequality holds
because
\|\nabla q_{i}(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}e^{\lambda t}{\bm{\theta}})\|_{2}\leq\|\nabla q_{i}(\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}})\|_{2}+\|\nabla q_{i}(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}e^{\lambda t}{\bm{\theta}})-\nabla q_{i}(\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}})\|_{2}\leq G_{\mathrm{ntk}}
.

For

, by
Lemma

C.3
we have

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}\log\frac{1}{r_{i}}\geq\mathcal{L}\log\frac{1}{n\mathcal{L}}
.
So
V_{2}\geq\lambda L\cdot\mathcal{L}\log\frac{1}{n\mathcal{L}}
.
Combining the inequalities for
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
together
proves (
16
).

For proving (
8
),
we first divide by
\mathcal{L}^{2}
on both sides of (
16
)
to get

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\frac{1}{\mathcal{L}}\leq G_{\mathrm{ntk}}^{2}\alpha^{2(L-1)}e^{-2(L-1)\lambda t}-\lambda L\cdot\frac{1}{\mathcal{L}}\log\frac{1}{n\mathcal{L}}.~

(18)

Let
\mathcal{E}:=\{t\in[0,T_{\max}]:F_{\mathrm{LL}}(\frac{1}{n\mathcal{L}})\leq\frac{2}{nL}G_{\mathrm{ntk}}^{2}Ae^{-2(L-1)\lambda t}\}
.
It is easy to see that
0\in\mathcal{E}
.
For all
t\in[0,T_{\max}]
, if
t\in\mathcal{E}
, then
by
Lemma

C.2
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}({\bm{\theta}}(t))\geq\frac{n}{F_{\mathrm{LL}}^{-1}\left(\frac{2}{nL}G_{\mathrm{ntk}}^{2}Ae^{-2(L-1)\lambda t}\right)}.

If no
t
is at the boundary of
\mathcal{E}
, then we are done.
Otherwise, let
t>0
be one on the boundary of
\mathcal{E}

Then at time
t
,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\frac{1}{n\mathcal{L}}=-\frac{\lambda L}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{n\mathcal{L}}\log\frac{1}{n\mathcal{L}}.

So as long as
1+\log\frac{1}{n\mathcal{L}({\bm{\theta}}(t))}>2(L-1)\lambda
,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}F_{\mathrm{LL}}(\frac{1}{n\mathcal{L}})

\displaystyle=(1+\log\frac{1}{n\mathcal{L}})\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\frac{1}{n\mathcal{L}}

\displaystyle=-(1+\log\frac{1}{n\mathcal{L}})\cdot\frac{\lambda L}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{n\mathcal{L}}\log\frac{1}{n\mathcal{L}}

\displaystyle=-(1+\log\frac{1}{n\mathcal{L}})\cdot\frac{2}{nL}G_{\mathrm{ntk}}^{2}Ae^{-2(L-1)\lambda t}<\left(\frac{2}{nL}G_{\mathrm{ntk}}^{2}Ae^{-2(L-1)\lambda t}\right)^{\prime},

which means
t
is not actually on the boundary.
So
1+\log\frac{1}{n\mathcal{L}({\bm{\theta}}(t))}\leq 2(L-1)\lambda
.
Repeating this argument with inequalities and taking some integrals can show that every point outside
\mathcal{E}

satisfies
1+\log\frac{1}{n\mathcal{L}({\bm{\theta}}(t))}\leq 2(L-1)\lambda
.

Lemma C.8
.

For any constant
c\in(0,1)
,
letting
T^{-}_{\mathrm{c}}(\alpha):=\frac{1-c}{\lambda}\log\alpha
, it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\|{\bm{h}}\|_{2}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{2(L-1)}e^{-2(L-1)\lambda t}\left\langle{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}({\bm{\theta}})\nabla q_{i}(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}e^{\lambda t}{\bm{\theta}})},{\frac{{\bm{h}}}{\|{\bm{h}}\|_{2}}}\right\rangle-L\lambda\|{\bm{h}}\|_{2},

(19)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\log\frac{1}{\mathcal{L}}

\displaystyle\geq\left(\gamma_{\mathrm{ntk}}+\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{-L}\log A)\cdot e^{L\lambda t}\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}\|{\bm{h}}\|_{2}}{\mathrm{d}t}.

(20)

Proof.

By update rule,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}{\bm{h}}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{L}e^{-L\lambda t}\frac{\mathrm{d}{\bm{\delta}}}{\mathrm{d}t}-L\lambda\alpha^{L}e^{-L\lambda t}{\bm{\delta}}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{L}e^{-L\lambda t}\left(\frac{e^{\lambda t}}{\alpha}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}({\bm{\theta}}(t))\nabla q_{i}({\bm{\theta}}(t))\right)-L\lambda{\bm{h}}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{2(L-1)}e^{-2(L-1)\lambda t}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}({\bm{\theta}}(t))\nabla q_{i}(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}e^{\lambda t}{\bm{\theta}}(t))-L\lambda{\bm{h}}.

Then we can deduce (

from

For proving (

For

By

C.5

\left\|{\bm{\delta}}(t)\right\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\alpha^{-L}\log A\right)\cdot e^{L\lambda t}

One way to give a lower bound for

\displaystyle V_{3}

\displaystyle\geq\left\langle{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}({\bm{\theta}})\nabla q_{i}(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}e^{\lambda t}{\bm{\theta}})},{{\bm{h}}^{*}_{\mathrm{ntk}}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}({\bm{\theta}})\left(\left\langle{\nabla q_{i}(\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}})},{{\bm{h}}^{*}_{\mathrm{ntk}}}\right\rangle+\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{-L}\log A)\cdot e^{L\lambda t}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathcal{L}({\bm{\theta}})\cdot\left(\gamma_{\mathrm{ntk}}+\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{-L}\log A)\cdot e^{L\lambda t}\right)>0.

Another way to give a lower bound for
V_{3}
is to
consider the projection of

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}({\bm{\theta}})\nabla q_{i}(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}e^{\lambda t}{\bm{\theta}})

\displaystyle V_{3}

Putting these two lower bounds together gives:

\displaystyle V_{1}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{2(L-1)}e^{-2(L-1)\lambda t}\frac{V_{3}^{2}}{\mathcal{L}({\bm{\theta}})}

\displaystyle\geq\left(\gamma_{\mathrm{ntk}}+\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{-L}\log A)\cdot e^{L\lambda t}\right)\alpha^{2(L-1)}e^{-2(L-1)\lambda t}\left\langle{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}({\bm{\theta}})\nabla q_{i}(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}e^{\lambda t}{\bm{\theta}})},{\tfrac{{\bm{h}}}{\|{\bm{h}}\|_{2}}}\right\rangle.

For

\displaystyle V_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda}{\mathcal{L}({\bm{\theta}})}\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}({\bm{\theta}})\left\langle{{\bm{\theta}}},{\nabla q_{i}({\bm{\theta}})}\right\rangle

C.3
Rich Regime

Now we proceed to prove

First, we derive the following bound for the training loss at the end of the kernel regime.

Lemma C.10

At

Proof.

By

Also we have the norm bound

We can conclude the proof by noting that
\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}=\mathcal{L}+\|{\bm{\theta}}\|_{2}^{2}

The loss bound then implies the following norm bound.

Lemma C.11
.

If

\displaystyle\|{\bm{\theta}}(t)\|_{2}=\Theta((\log\tfrac{1}{\lambda})^{1/L}).

Proof.

For all

and the Lipschitzness of

\displaystyle\Omega(\log(1/\lambda))\leq\log\frac{1}{n\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}({\bm{\theta}}(0))}\leq\log\frac{1}{n\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}({\bm{\theta}}(t))}\leq q_{i}({\bm{\theta}}(t))\leq B_{0}\|{\bm{\theta}}(t)\|_{2}^{L}.

So
\|{\bm{\theta}}(t)\|_{2}=\Omega((\log\frac{1}{\lambda})^{1/L})
.
Also note
\|{\bm{\theta}}(t)\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{2}{\lambda}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}({\bm{\theta}}(t))\leq\frac{2}{\lambda}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}({\bm{\theta}}(0))=\mathcal{O}((\log\frac{1}{\lambda})^{1/L})

So we have the tight norm bound

\|{\bm{\theta}}(t)\|_{2}=\Theta((\log\frac{1}{\lambda})^{1/L})
.

Appendix D
Proofs for Regression with L2 Regularization

The analyses for the regression task are similar to those for the classification task, but the use of squared loss simplifies the analysis.

We define
s_{i}({\bm{\theta}}):=f_{i}({\bm{\theta}})-y_{i}
. In the regression setting, the gradients of the vanilla and regularized square loss can be written as

\nabla\mathcal{L}({\bm{\theta}})=2\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}\nabla f_{i}({\bm{\theta}}),

(23)

and

\nabla\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}({\bm{\theta}})=2\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}\nabla f_{i}({\bm{\theta}})+\lambda{\bm{\theta}}.

(24)

D.1
Kernel Regime

Now we present the proof of
Theorem

3.9
for the kernel regime.
Following
Section

3.3.1
, we define
{\bm{h}}^{*}_{\mathrm{ntk}}
as the solution to
Equation

K2
.
Additionally, let
{\bm{\Phi}}({\bm{\theta}})\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times n}

be the matrix where the
i
-th column is
\nabla f_{i}({\bm{\theta}})
.
Let
{\bm{K}}({\bm{\theta}}):={\bm{\Phi}}({\bm{\theta}})^{\top}{\bm{\Phi}}({\bm{\theta}})
be the NTK matrix at
{\bm{\theta}}
.
Let
\nu_{\mathrm{ntk}}>0
be the minimum eigenvalue of
{\bm{K}}(\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}})
.
Let
\epsilon_{\max}
be a small constant so that

\lambda_{\min}({\bm{K}}({\bm{\theta}}))>\frac{\nu_{\mathrm{ntk}}}{2}

holds for all
\|{\bm{\theta}}-\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}\|_{2}<\epsilon_{\max}
.
Let

,

{\bm{\theta}}(t)

First, we derive an upper bound for the loss convergence.

Lemma D.1
.

For all

,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{\nu_{\mathrm{ntk}}}{2n}\alpha^{2(L-1)}e^{-2(L-1)\lambda t}\mathcal{L}+\frac{\lambda L}{\sqrt{n}}\|{\bm{y}}\|_{2}\sqrt{\mathcal{L}},

(25)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}({\bm{\theta}}(t))

\displaystyle\leq\max\left\{\frac{1}{n}\|{\bm{y}}\|_{2}^{2}\exp\left(-\frac{\nu_{\mathrm{ntk}}A}{8n(L-1)}\left(1-e^{-2(L-1)\lambda t}\right)\right),\frac{16nL^{2}\|{\bm{y}}\|_{2}^{2}\lambda^{2}}{\nu_{\mathrm{ntk}}^{2}\alpha^{4(L-1)}}e^{4(L-1)\lambda t}.\right\}.

(26)

Proof.

By the update rule of
{\bm{\theta}}
, the following holds for all
t>0
:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\left\langle{\nabla\mathcal{L}({\bm{\theta}})},{\frac{\mathrm{d}{\bm{\theta}}}{\mathrm{d}t}}\right\rangle=\underbrace{-\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}\nabla f_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}}_{=:V_{1}}-\underbrace{\frac{\lambda L}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}f_{i}}_{=:V_{2}}.

For
V_{1}
, we have

\displaystyle-V_{1}=\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}\nabla f_{i}({\bm{\theta}})\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}}{\bm{s}}({\bm{\theta}})^{\top}{\bm{K}}({\bm{\theta}}){\bm{s}}({\bm{\theta}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\alpha^{2(L-1)}e^{-2(L-1)\lambda t}{\bm{s}}({\bm{\theta}})^{\top}{\bm{K}}(\tfrac{e^{\lambda t}}{\alpha}{\bm{\theta}}){\bm{s}}({\bm{\theta}})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\nu_{\mathrm{ntk}}}{2n^{2}}\alpha^{2(L-1)}e^{-2(L-1)\lambda t}\|{\bm{s}}({\bm{\theta}})\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\nu_{\mathrm{ntk}}}{2n}\alpha^{2(L-1)}e^{-2(L-1)\lambda t}\mathcal{L}.

For
V_{2}
, we have

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}f_{i}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(s_{i}^{2}+s_{i}y_{i})\geq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}y_{i}\geq-\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\|{\bm{y}}\|_{2}\sqrt{\mathcal{L}}
. So

V_{2}\leq\frac{\lambda L}{\sqrt{n}}\|{\bm{y}}\|_{2}\sqrt{\mathcal{L}}
.
Combining the inequalities for
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
together proves (
25
).

For proving (
26
),
we first divide by
\mathcal{L}
on both sides of (
25
) to get

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\log\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{\nu_{\mathrm{ntk}}}{2n}\alpha^{2(L-1)}e^{-2(L-1)\lambda t}+\frac{\lambda L}{\sqrt{n}}\|{\bm{y}}\|_{2}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{L}}}.

Let
\mathcal{E}:=\{t\in[0,T_{\max}]:\frac{\lambda L}{\sqrt{n}}\|{\bm{y}}\|_{2}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{L}}}\geq\frac{\nu_{\mathrm{ntk}}}{4n}\alpha^{2(L-1)}e^{-2(L-1)\lambda t}\}
.
For all
t\in[0,T_{\max}]
,
if
t\in\mathcal{E}
,
then

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\lambda^{2}L^{2}}{n}\|{\bm{y}}\|_{2}^{2}\cdot\frac{1}{\left(\frac{\nu_{\mathrm{ntk}}}{4n}\alpha^{2(L-1)}e^{-2(L-1)\lambda t}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{16nL^{2}\|{\bm{y}}\|_{2}^{2}}{\nu_{\mathrm{ntk}}^{2}}\cdot\frac{\lambda^{2}}{\alpha^{4(L-1)}}e^{4(L-1)\lambda t}.

Otherwise, let
t^{\prime}
be the largest number in
\mathcal{E}\cup\{0\}
that is smaller than
t
. Then

\displaystyle\log\mathcal{L}({\bm{\theta}}(t))

\displaystyle=\log\mathcal{L}({\bm{\theta}}(t^{\prime}))+\int_{t^{\prime}}^{t}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\log\mathcal{L}({\bm{\theta}}(\tau))\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\leq\log\mathcal{L}({\bm{\theta}}(t^{\prime}))-\int_{t^{\prime}}^{t}\left(\frac{\nu_{\mathrm{ntk}}}{4n}\alpha^{2(L-1)}e^{-2(L-1)\lambda\tau}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\leq\log\mathcal{L}({\bm{\theta}}(t^{\prime})).

If
t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}
, then
\mathcal{L}({\bm{\theta}}(t))\leq\mathcal{L}({\bm{\theta}}(t^{\prime}))\leq\frac{16nL^{2}\|{\bm{y}}\|_{2}^{2}}{\nu_{\mathrm{ntk}}^{2}}\cdot\frac{\lambda^{2}}{\alpha^{4(L-1)}}e^{4(L-1)\lambda t^{\prime}}\leq\frac{16nL^{2}\|{\bm{y}}\|_{2}^{2}}{\nu_{\mathrm{ntk}}^{2}}\cdot\frac{\lambda^{2}}{\alpha^{4(L-1)}}e^{4(L-1)\lambda t}
. Otherwise,
t^{\prime}=0
and we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}({\bm{\theta}}(t))

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{L}({\bm{\theta}}(0))\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{t}\left(\frac{\nu_{\mathrm{ntk}}}{4n}\alpha^{2(L-1)}e^{-2(L-1)\lambda\tau}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\tau\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\|{\bm{y}}\|_{2}^{2}\exp\left(-\frac{\nu_{\mathrm{ntk}}A}{8n(L-1)}\left(1-e^{-2(L-1)\lambda t}\right)\right)

which concludes the proof.

Now we derive a lower bound for the time that the dynamics stay in the kernel regime.

Lemma D.2
.

There exists a constant
\Delta T
such that

T_{\max}\geq\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(\log\alpha+\Delta T\right)
when
\alpha
is sufficiently large.
Furthermore,
for all
t\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(\log\alpha+\Delta T\right)
,

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{1}{\alpha}e^{\lambda t}{\bm{\theta}}(t)-\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}\right\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\alpha^{-L}\right)\cdot e^{L\lambda t}.

Proof.

By definition of
T_{\max}
,
to prove the first claim,
it suffices to show that
there exists a constant
\Delta T

such that

\left\|\frac{1}{\alpha}e^{\lambda t}{\bm{\theta}}(t)-\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}\right\|_{2}\leq\epsilon_{\max}

for all
t\leq\min\{\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(\log\alpha+\Delta T\right),T_{\max}\}
.

When
t\leq T_{\max}
, we have the following gradient upper bound:

\displaystyle\|\nabla\mathcal{L}({\bm{\theta}}(t))\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}\nabla f_{i}({\bm{\theta}}(t))\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\|{\bm{s}}({\bm{\theta}})\|_{2}\|\Phi({\bm{\theta}})\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\alpha^{L-1}e^{-(L-1)\lambda t}\|\Phi(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}e^{-\lambda t}{\bm{\theta}})\|_{2}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\|{\bm{s}}({\bm{\theta}})\|_{2}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq G_{\mathrm{ntk}}\alpha^{L-1}e^{-(L-1)\lambda t}\sqrt{\mathcal{L}}.

By update rule,
e^{\lambda t}{\bm{\theta}}(t)-\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}=\int_{0}^{t}e^{\lambda\tau}\nabla\mathcal{L}({\bm{\theta}}(\tau))\mathrm{d}\tau
.
Applying the gradient upper bound gives

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha}\left\|e^{\lambda t}{\bm{\theta}}(t)-\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{0}^{t}\left\|e^{\lambda\tau}\nabla\mathcal{L}({\bm{\theta}}(\tau))\right\|_{2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\lambda\tau}G_{\mathrm{ntk}}\alpha^{L-1}e^{-(L-1)\lambda\tau}\sqrt{\mathcal{L}({\bm{\theta}}(\tau))}\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle=G_{\mathrm{ntk}}\alpha^{L-2}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-(L-2)\lambda\tau}\sqrt{\mathcal{L}({\bm{\theta}}(\tau))}\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

Now we substitute
\mathcal{L}
in

\int_{0}^{t}e^{-(L-2)\lambda\tau}\sqrt{\mathcal{L}({\bm{\theta}}(\tau))}\,\mathrm{d}\tau

with the loss upper bound in
Lemma

D.1
.
Then for all
t\leq T_{\max}
, we have
\int_{0}^{t}e^{-(L-2)\lambda\tau}\sqrt{\mathcal{L}({\bm{\theta}}(\tau))}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\leq\max\{I_{1},I_{2}\}
,
where

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\|{\bm{y}}\|_{2}\exp\left(-\frac{\nu_{\mathrm{ntk}}A}{16n(L-1)}\left(1-e^{-2(L-1)\lambda\tau}\right)-(L-2)\lambda\tau\right),

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{4\sqrt{n}L\|{\bm{y}}\|_{2}}{\nu_{\mathrm{ntk}}}\cdot\frac{\lambda}{\alpha^{2(L-1)}}e^{L\lambda\tau}\mathrm{d}\tau.

We now bound
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
.
For
I_{1}
, we divide the integral into two parts. Let
t_{0}=\frac{1}{20(L-1)\lambda}
, for
\tau\in\left[0,t_{0}\right]
, we have
1-e^{-2(L-1)\lambda\tau}\geq\frac{1}{2}\cdot 2(L-1)\lambda\tau=(L-1)\lambda\tau
, so that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t_{0}}\exp\left(-\frac{\nu_{\mathrm{ntk}}A}{16n(L-1)}\left(1-e^{-2(L-1)\lambda\tau}\right)-(L-2)\lambda\tau\right)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t_{0}}\exp\left(-\frac{\nu_{\mathrm{ntk}}A\lambda\tau}{16n}\right)\mathrm{d}\tau=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{A\lambda}\right)=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{1}{\alpha^{2(L-1)}}\right).

On the other hand, we have

\displaystyle\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\exp\left(-\frac{\nu_{\mathrm{ntk}}A}{16n(L-1)}\left(1-e^{-2(L-1)\lambda\tau}\right)-(L-2)\lambda\tau\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\Omega(A)\right)\cdot\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\exp\left(-(L-2)\lambda\tau\right)\mathrm{d}\tau\leq\exp\left(-\Omega(A)\right)\cdot\mathcal{O}\!\left(\lambda^{-1}\right)=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{1}{\alpha^{2(L-1)}}\right).

Thus, we have

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{1}{\alpha^{2(L-1)}}\right).

For
I_{2}
, it holds for all
t\leq T_{\max}
that

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{1}{\alpha^{2(L-1)}}\right)\cdot e^{L\lambda t}.

Putting all these together, we have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{1}{\alpha}\left\|e^{\lambda t}{\bm{\theta}}(t)-\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}\right\|_{2}&=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\alpha^{L-2}\right)\cdot\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{1}{\alpha^{2(L-1)}}\right)\cdot e^{L\lambda t}\\
&=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\alpha^{-L}\right)\cdot e^{L\lambda t}.\end{aligned}

(27)

Therefore, there exists a constant
C>0
such that
when
\alpha
is sufficiently large,

\left\|\frac{1}{\alpha}e^{\lambda t}{\bm{\theta}}(t)-\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}\right\|_{2}\leq C\alpha^{-L}e^{L\lambda t}

for all
t\leq\min\{T_{\max},\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{\lambda}\log\alpha)\}
.
Setting
\Delta T:=\frac{1}{L}\log\frac{\epsilon_{\max}}{C}
,
we obtain the following bound for all
t\leq\min\{T_{\max},\frac{1}{\lambda}(\log\alpha+\Delta T)\}
:

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{1}{\alpha}e^{\lambda t}{\bm{\theta}}(t)-\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}\right\|_{2}\leq C\alpha^{-L}\exp\left(L\log\alpha+\log\frac{\epsilon_{\max}}{C}\right)=\epsilon_{\max},

which implies

T_{\max}\geq\frac{1}{\lambda}(\log\alpha+\Delta T)
.
Combining this with (
13
) prove the second claim.

Let
{\bm{\delta}}(t;\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}):=\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}e^{\lambda t}{\bm{\theta}}(t;\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}})-\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}\right)

and
{\bm{h}}(t;\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}):=\alpha^{L}e^{-L\lambda t}{\bm{\delta}}(t;\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}})
.
In the following, we show via a series of lemmas that for any
{\bm{v}}
that is in the orthogonal complement of
{\bm{\Phi}}
,

\frac{\left\langle{{\bm{\delta}}},{{\bm{v}}}\right\rangle}{\|{\bm{\delta}}\|_{2}}\to 0
, which is a crucial property for deriving the implicit bias.

Lemma D.3
.

For any vector
{\bm{v}}
that lies in the orthogonal space of the column space of
\Phi
, we have
\left\langle{{\bm{\delta}}(T_{c}^{-}(\alpha);\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}})},{{\bm{v}}}\right\rangle=\mathcal{O}\left(\alpha^{-2cL}\right)
where
T_{c}^{-}(\alpha):=\frac{1-c}{\lambda}\log\alpha
.

Proof.

By update rule,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}{\bm{\delta}}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\tfrac{1}{\alpha}e^{\lambda t}\left(-2\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}\nabla f_{i}({\bm{\theta}}(t;\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}))\right).

Then we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\left\langle{{\bm{\delta}}},{{\bm{v}}}\right\rangle}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=-2\frac{1}{\alpha}e^{\lambda t}\left\langle{\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}\nabla f_{i}({\bm{\theta}}(t;\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}))},{{\bm{v}}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=-2\frac{1}{\alpha}e^{\lambda t}\left\langle{\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}\left(\nabla f_{i}({\bm{\theta}}(t;\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}))-\nabla f_{i}(\alpha e^{-\lambda t}\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}})\right)},{{\bm{v}}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=-2\alpha^{L-2}e^{-(L-2)\lambda t}\left\langle{\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}\left(\nabla f_{i}(\alpha^{-1}e^{\lambda t}{\bm{\theta}}(t;\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}))-\nabla f_{i}(\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}})\right)},{{\bm{v}}}\right\rangle

where the second equation is due to our choice of
{\bm{v}}
and the last equation follows from the homogeneity of
f_{i}
. Now, since
f_{i}
is locally smooth in a neighbourhood of
\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}
and
\left\|\frac{1}{\alpha}e^{\lambda t}{\bm{\theta}}(t)-\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}\right\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\alpha^{-L}\right)\cdot e^{L\lambda t}
by
Lemma

D.2
, we have

\|\nabla f_{i}(\alpha^{-1}e^{\lambda t}{\bm{\theta}}(t;\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}})-\nabla f_{i}(\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}})\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\alpha^{-L}e^{L\lambda t}\right).

Let
t_{0}=\frac{1}{20(L-1)\lambda}
, then for
t\in[0,t_{0}]
, we have
1-e^{2(L-1)\lambda\tau}\geq(L-1)\lambda\tau
, so that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\left\langle{{\bm{\delta}}},{{\bm{v}}}\right\rangle}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\alpha^{-2}e^{2\lambda t}\sqrt{\mathcal{L}\left({\bm{\theta}}(t;\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}})\right)}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\alpha^{-2}e^{2\lambda t}\max\left\{\exp\left(-\frac{\nu_{\mathrm{ntk}}A\lambda t}{16n}+\lambda t\right),\alpha^{-2(L-1)}\lambda e^{2(L-1)\lambda t}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\max\left\{\alpha^{-2}\exp\left(-\frac{\nu_{\mathrm{ntk}}A\lambda t}{32n}\right),\lambda\alpha^{-2L}e^{2L\lambda t}\right\}\right)

for sufficiently large
\alpha
, since
A=\frac{\alpha^{2(L-1)}}{\lambda}
. On the other hand, for
t\in\left[t_{0},T_{c}^{-}(\alpha)\right]
, the second term in (
26
) dominates the first term, so that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\left\langle{{\bm{\delta}}},{{\bm{v}}}\right\rangle}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\alpha^{-2}e^{2\lambda t}\sqrt{\mathcal{L}\left({\bm{\theta}}(t;\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}})\right)}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\alpha^{-2}e^{2\lambda t}\cdot\alpha^{-2(L-1)}\lambda e^{2(L-1)\lambda t}\right)=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\lambda\alpha^{-2L}e^{2L\lambda t}\right).

Hence,

\displaystyle\left\langle{{\bm{\delta}}(T_{c}^{-}(\alpha);\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}})},{{\bm{v}}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\max\left\{\alpha^{-2}\int_{0}^{T_{c}^{-}(\alpha)}\exp\left(-\frac{\nu_{\mathrm{ntk}}A\lambda t}{32n}\right)\mathrm{d}t,\lambda\alpha^{-2L}\int_{0}^{T_{c}^{-}(\alpha)}e^{2L\lambda t}\mathrm{d}t\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\max\left\{\alpha^{-2}\frac{1}{A\lambda},\alpha^{-2L}\alpha^{2L(1-c)}\right\}\right)=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\alpha^{-2cL}\right),

which proves the claim.

Lemma D.4
.

For
{\bm{\theta}}={\bm{\theta}}(t)
with
t\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(\log\alpha+\Delta T\right)
as defined in
Lemma

D.2
, we have

\displaystyle f(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}e^{\lambda t}{\bm{\theta}};{\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=\left\langle{\nabla f(\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}};{\bm{x}})},{{\bm{\delta}}}\right\rangle+\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{-L})\cdot e^{L\lambda t}\|{\bm{\delta}}\|_{2},

(28)

\displaystyle f({\bm{\theta}};{\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=\left\langle{\nabla f(\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}};{\bm{x}})},{{\bm{h}}}\right\rangle+\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{-L})\cdot e^{L\lambda t}\|{\bm{h}}\|_{2}.

(29)

Proof.

By
Lemma

D.2
and Taylor expansion, we have

\displaystyle f(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}e^{\lambda t}{\bm{\theta}};{\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=f(\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}};{\bm{x}})+\left\langle{\nabla f(\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}};{\bm{x}})},{{\bm{\delta}}}\right\rangle+\mathcal{O}(\|{\bm{\delta}}\|_{2}^{2})

\displaystyle=f(\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}};{\bm{x}})+\left\langle{\nabla f(\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}};{\bm{x}})},{{\bm{\delta}}}\right\rangle+\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{-L})\cdot e^{L\lambda t}\|{\bm{\delta}}\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\langle{\nabla f(\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}};{\bm{x}})},{{\bm{\delta}}}\right\rangle+\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{-L})\cdot e^{L\lambda t}\|{\bm{\delta}}\|_{2},

which proves (
28
).
Combining this with the
L
-homogeneity of
q_{i}

proves (
29
).

Lemma D.5
.

\|{\bm{h}}(T_{c}^{-}(\alpha);\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}})\|_{2}=\Theta(1)
.

Proof.

By
Lemma

D.2
we have
\|{\bm{\delta}}(T_{c}^{-}(\alpha);\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}})\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}(e^{-cL})
, so that
\|{\bm{h}}(T_{c}^{-}(\alpha);\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}})\|_{2}=\alpha^{L}e^{-L\lambda T_{c}^{-}}\|{\bm{\delta}}(T_{c}^{-}(\alpha);\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}})\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}(1)
.

On the other hand, recall that
T_{c}^{-}(\alpha)=\frac{1-c}{\lambda}\log\alpha
, so by
Equation

26
we have

|s_{i}|\leq\sqrt{n\mathcal{L}({\bm{\theta}}(T_{c}^{-}(\alpha);\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}))}=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\max\left\{e^{-\Omega(A)},\lambda\alpha^{-2(L-1)c}\right\}\right)=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\alpha^{-2(L-1)c}\right).

Thus, for sufficiently large
\alpha
, we have
\left|f_{i}({\bm{\theta}}(T_{c}^{-}(\alpha);\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}))\right|\geq\frac{1}{2}y_{i}
.

By
Lemma

D.4
, we have that

\frac{1}{2}y_{i}\leq\|\nabla f_{i}(\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}})\|_{2}\|{\bm{h}}\|_{2}+\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{-cL})\|{\bm{h}}\|_{2}\Rightarrow\|{\bm{h}}\|_{2}=\Omega(1)

as desired.

Corollary D.6
.

\lim_{\alpha\to 0}\left\langle{\frac{{\bm{\delta}}}{\|{\bm{\delta}}\|_{2}}(T_{c}^{-}(\alpha);\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}})},{{\bm{v}}}\right\rangle=0
.

Proof.

By the previous lemma, we have
\|{\bm{\delta}}(T_{c}^{-}(\alpha);\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}})\|_{2}=\Omega\left(\alpha^{-L}e^{L\lambda T_{c}^{-}(\alpha)}\right)=\Omega(\alpha^{-cL})
. On the other hand,
\left\langle{{\bm{\delta}}(T_{c}^{-}(\alpha);\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}})},{{\bm{v}}}\right\rangle=\mathcal{O}\left(\alpha^{-2cL}\right)
by
Lemma

D.3
. The conclusion immediately follows.

Theorem D.7
.

For any constant
c\in(0,1)
, letting

T^{-}_{\mathrm{c}}(\alpha):=\frac{1-c}{\lambda}\log\alpha
, it holds that

\displaystyle\forall{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\qquad\lim_{\alpha\to+\infty}f({\bm{\theta}}(T^{-}_{\mathrm{c}}(\alpha);\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}});{\bm{x}})=\left\langle{\nabla f(\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}};{\bm{x}})},{{\bm{h}}^{*}_{\mathrm{ntk}}}\right\rangle.

Proof.

By
Lemmas

D.5
and
D.4
we have

\displaystyle f({\bm{\theta}}(T^{-}_{\mathrm{c}}(\alpha);\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}});{\bm{x}})

(30)

\displaystyle=\left\langle{\nabla f(\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}};{\bm{x}})},{{\bm{h}}({\bm{\theta}}(T^{-}_{\mathrm{c}}(\alpha);\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}))}\right\rangle+\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{-L})\cdot e^{L\lambda T_{c}^{-}(\alpha)}\|{\bm{h}}(T^{-}_{\mathrm{c}}(\alpha);\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}})\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\langle{\nabla f(\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}};{\bm{x}})},{{\bm{h}}({\bm{\theta}}(T^{-}_{\mathrm{c}}(\alpha);\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}))}\right\rangle+\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{-cL})

By
Lemma

D.1
we have for
\forall i\in[n]
,
\left|f_{i}({\bm{\theta}}(T^{-}_{\mathrm{c}}(\alpha);\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}))-y_{i}\right|=\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{-2(L-1)})
. Since
y_{i}=\left\langle{\nabla f_{i}(\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}})},{{\bm{h}}^{*}_{\mathrm{ntk}}}\right\rangle
, we have

\lim_{\alpha\to+\infty}\left\langle{\nabla f_{i}(\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}})},{{\bm{h}}({\bm{\theta}}(T^{-}_{\mathrm{c}}(\alpha);\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}))-{\bm{h}}^{*}_{\mathrm{ntk}}}\right\rangle=0.

(31)

By
Corollaries

D.6
and
D.5
we have that for any direction
{\bm{v}}
orthogonal to the column space of
\Phi
, we have
\lim_{\alpha\to+\infty}\left\langle{{\bm{h}}({\bm{\theta}}(T^{-}_{\mathrm{c}}(\alpha);\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}))},{{\bm{v}}}\right\rangle=0
. Let
{\bm{P}}_{\Phi}
be the projection operator onto the column space of
\Phi
, then

\lim_{\alpha\to+\infty}({\bm{I}}-{\bm{P}}_{\Phi}){\bm{h}}({\bm{\theta}}(T^{-}_{\mathrm{c}}(\alpha);\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}))=0.

(32)

Combined with
Equation

31
, we deduce that

\lim_{\alpha\to+\infty}\left\langle{\nabla f_{i}(\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}})},{{\bm{P}}_{\Phi}{\bm{h}}({\bm{\theta}}(T^{-}_{\mathrm{c}}(\alpha);\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}))-{\bm{h}}^{*}_{\mathrm{ntk}}}\right\rangle=0.

The above holds for all
\nabla f_{i}(\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}),i\in[d]
, which are exactly the columns of
\Phi
. Note also that
{\bm{h}}^{*}_{\mathrm{ntk}}
also lies in the column space of
\Phi
, so we actually have
\lim_{\alpha\to+\infty}\|{\bm{P}}_{\Phi}{\bm{h}}({\bm{\theta}}(T^{-}_{\mathrm{c}}(\alpha);\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}))-{\bm{h}}^{*}_{\mathrm{ntk}}\|_{2}=0
. Hence, we can use
Equation

32
to deduce that
\lim_{\alpha\to+\infty}\|{\bm{h}}({\bm{\theta}}(T^{-}_{\mathrm{c}}(\alpha);\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}))-{\bm{h}}^{*}_{\mathrm{ntk}}\|_{2}=0
. Finally, plugging this into
Equation

30
gives the desired result.

D.2
Rich Regime

Now we proceed to prove
Theorem

3.10
for the rich regime.
First, we derive a norm bound.

Lemma D.8
.

For any constant
c>0
, let
T_{c}^{+}(\alpha):=\frac{1+c}{\lambda}\log\alpha
, then we have that

\max_{\frac{1}{\lambda}\log\alpha\leq t\leq T_{c}^{+}(\alpha)}\|{\bm{\theta}}\left(t;\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}\right)\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}(1).

Proof.

We know from
Lemmas

D.2
and

Since

Now we prove
Theorem

3.10
.

Proof.

By
Lemma

C.5
, we know that for

, we have
\|{\bm{\theta}}(t;\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}})-\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}(1)\Rightarrow\|{\bm{\theta}}(t;\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}})\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}(1)
. Since

\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}\left({\bm{\theta}}\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}\log\alpha;\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}\right)\right)-\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}\left({\bm{\theta}}\left(T_{c}^{+}(\alpha);\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}\right)\right)=\int_{\frac{1}{\lambda}\log\alpha}^{T_{c}^{+}(\alpha)}\|\nabla\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}({\bm{\theta}}(t;\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}))\|_{2}^{2}\mathrm{d}t

and
0\leq\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}\left({\bm{\theta}}\left(T_{c}^{+}(\alpha);\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}\right)\right),\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}\left({\bm{\theta}}\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}\log\alpha;\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}\right)\right)\leq\mathcal{O}(\lambda)
by
Lemma

D.1
, we deduce that there exists

such that
\left\|\nabla\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}({\bm{\theta}}(t;\alpha\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\mathrm{init}}))\right\|_{2}\leq\mathcal{O}\!\left(\sqrt{\lambda}{\frac{c}{\lambda}\log\alpha}\right)=\mathcal{O}(\lambda(\log\frac{1}{\lambda})^{-1/2})
.

For any sequence
\{\alpha_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
with
\alpha_{k}\to+\infty
, we choose

. Let

(33)

E.1

We base our generalization analyses on Rademacher complexity.

Definition E.1

(34)

Rademacher complexity is useful in deriving margin-based generalization bounds for classification. The following bound is standard in the literature
Koltchinskii and Panchenko 2002

Mohri et al. 2018

Theorem E.2
(Theorem 5.8,
Mohri et al. 2018

.
q>0

\delta>0

1-\delta

\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{X}}}[\mathbbm{1}_{[y^{*}({\bm{x}})f({\bm{x}})\leq 0]}]\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}_{[y_{i}f({\bm{x}}_{i})\leq q]}+\frac{2}{q}\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{S}(\mathcal{F})+3\sqrt{\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{2n}}.

(35)

Let

with bounded weight in

The following theorem from

For a set of samples

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sqrt{2\log(2d)}\cdot\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|{\bm{x}}_{i}\|_{\infty}^{2}\right)^{1/2},

(36)

\bm{G}_{1}=\left(\bm{g}_{i}:i\in[d]\right),\quad\bm{G}_{2}=\left((-1)^{\mathbb{I}\{i>t\}}\bm{g}_{i}:i\in[d]\right),\quad\text{and}\quad\bm{B}=\mathrm{diag}{|b_{i}|:i\in[d]},

\bm{G}_{1}

\bm{W}^{*}=\bm{G}_{1}\bm{B}\bm{G}_{2}^{\top}
. This gives a singular value decomposition of
\bm{W}^{*}
. By

we can write down the explicit form of
\partial\|\bm{W}^{*}\|_{*}

\partial\|\bm{W}^{*}\|_{*}=\left\{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{T}}(-1)^{\mathbb{I}\{i>t\}}\bm{g}_{i}\bm{g}_{i}^{\top}+\bm{E}:\|\bm{E}\|\leq 1\text{ and }\bm{E}\bm{g}_{i}=0,\forall i\in\mathcal{T}\right\}.

Here
\mathcal{T}=\left\{i\in[d]:b_{i}\neq 0\right\}\subset[s]
. We choose

\displaystyle\bm{E}

\displaystyle=-\left(\lambda^{-1}\bm{M}+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{T}}(-1)^{\mathbb{I}\{i>t\}}\bm{g}_{i}\bm{g}_{i}^{\top}\right)

\displaystyle=-\lambda^{-1}\left(-\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{t}\bm{f}_{i}\bm{f}_{i}^{\top}+\lambda\sum_{i=t+1}^{s}\bm{f}_{i}\bm{f}_{i}^{\top}+\sum_{i>s}m_{i}\bm{f}_{i}\bm{f}_{i}^{\top}\right)-\sum_{i\in\mathcal{T}}(-1)^{\mathbb{I}\{i>t\}}\bm{g}_{i}\bm{g}_{i}^{\top}

\displaystyle=-\lambda^{-1}\left(-\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{t}\bm{g}_{i}\bm{g}_{i}^{\top}+\lambda\sum_{i=t+1}^{s}\bm{g}_{i}\bm{g}_{i}^{\top}+\sum_{i>s}m_{i}\bm{g}_{i}\bm{g}_{i}^{\top}\right)-\sum_{i\in\mathcal{T}}(-1)^{\mathbb{I}\{i>t\}}\bm{g}_{i}\bm{g}_{i}^{\top}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[s]-\mathcal{T}}(-1)^{\mathbb{I}\{i>t\}}\bm{g}_{i}\bm{g}_{i}^{\top}-\lambda^{-1}\sum_{i>s}m_{i}\bm{g}_{i}\bm{g}_{i}^{\top}

where the third equation holds because of the definition

\bm{g}_{i}=\bm{f}_{i}
when
i>s

51

m_{i}\in[-\lambda,\lambda]

i\in[d]

\|\bm{E}\|\leq 1

\bm{E}\bm{g}_{i}=0
when
i\in\mathcal{T}
(since
\{\bm{g}_{i}\}

0\in\bm{M}+\lambda\partial\|\bm{W}^{*}\|

To summarize, we have shown that

\bm{W}^{*}
. Therefore,
\bm{W}^{*}

which concludes the proof of

Claim 2.

(\bm{U}_{*},\bm{V}_{*})
solves the problem

\mathrm{minimize}\quad\|\bm{U}\|_{F}^{2}+\|\bm{V}\|_{F}^{2}\quad s.t.\quad\bm{U}\bm{U}^{\top}-\bm{V}\bm{V}^{\top}=\bm{W}^{*}.

(53)

Proof of Claim 2.
We will use the notations in the proof of Claim 1 for convenience. note that

\|\bm{U}_{*}\|_{F}^{2}=\mathrm{tr}{\bm{U}_{*}\bm{U}_{*}^{\top}}=\sum_{i=1}^{t}b_{i}

and similarly

\|\bm{V}_{*}\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{i=t+1}^{s}b_{i}.

Thus
\|\bm{U}_{*}\|_{F}^{2}+\|\bm{V}_{*}\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{s}b_{i}=\|\bm{W}\|_{*}
. On the other hand, for any
(\bm{U},\bm{V})
satisfying
\bm{U}\bm{U}^{\top}-\bm{V}\bm{V}^{\top}=\bm{W}^{*}

\|\bm{W}^{*}\|_{*}=\left\|\bm{U}\bm{U}^{\top}-\bm{V}\bm{V}^{\top}\right\|_{*}\leq\left\|\bm{U}\bm{U}^{\top}\right\|_{*}+\left\|\bm{V}\bm{V}^{\top}\right\|_{*}=\|\bm{U}\|_{F}^{2}+\|\bm{V}\|_{F}^{2}.

Hence
(\bm{U}_{*},\bm{V}_{*})
is a minimizer of
Equation

53
, as desired.

We can now prove that
(\bm{U}_{*},\bm{V}_{*})
is a global minimizer of
\mathcal{L}
, which completes the proof of

F.3
. Indeed, for any
(\bm{U},\bm{V})

\mathcal{L}(\bm{U},\bm{V})\geq\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{U}\bm{U}^{\top}-\bm{V}\bm{V}^{\top})\geq\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{W}^{*})=\mathcal{L}(\bm{U}_{*},\bm{V}_{*}).

The last step follows from

.

Lemma F.4
.

[
Watson 1992

Let
\bm{A}=\bm{L}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{R}^{\top}

\partial\|\bm{A}\|_{*}=\left\{\bm{L}\bm{R}^{\top}+\bm{E}:\left\|\bm{E}\right\|\leq 1,\bm{L}^{\top}\bm{E}=0\text{ and }\bm{E}\bm{R}=0\right\}.

Theorem F.5
.

Suppose that
\mathrm{rank}({\bm{X}}^{*})=r=\mathcal{O}(1)
,
{\bm{X}}^{*}={\bm{V}}_{{\bm{X}}^{*}}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{{\bm{X}}^{*}}{\bm{V}}_{{\bm{X}}^{*}}^{\top}
