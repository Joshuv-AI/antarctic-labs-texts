Smoothness of solutions of hyperbolic stochastic partial differential equations with L ∞ L^{\infty} -vector fields Thanks: The project on which this publication is based has been carried out with funding provided by the Alexander von Humboldt Foundation, under the programme financed by the German Federal Ministry of Education and Research entitled German Research Chair No 01DG15010.
Antoine-Marie Bogso, Moustapha Dieye, Olivier Menoukeu-Pamen, Frank Proske

Abstract
. In this paper we are interested in a quasi-linear hyperbolic stochastic differential equation (HSPDE) when the vector field is merely bounded and measurable. Although the deterministic counterpart of such equation may be ill-posed (in the sense that uniqueness or even existence might not be valid), we show for the first time that the corresponding HSPDE has a unique (Malliavin differentiable) strong solution. Our approach for proving this result rests on: 1) tools from Malliavin calculus and 2) variational techniques introduced in Davie [ 16 ] non trivially extended to the case of SDEs in the plane by using an algorithm for the selection of certain rectangles. As a by product, we also obtain the Sobolev differentiability of the solution with respect to its initial value. The results derived here constitute a significant improvement of those in the current literature on SDEs on the plane and can be regarded as an analogous equivalent of the pioneering works by Zvonkin [ 48 ] and Veretennikov [ 41 ] in the case of one-parameter SDEs with singular drift.

Introduction

In this article we are interested in studying solutions
X_{s,t},0\leq s,t\leq T
to the following hyperbolic stochastic
partial differential equation (HSPDE)

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\dfrac{\partial^{2}X(s,t)}{\partial s\partial t}=b(s,t,X(s,t))+\dfrac{\partial^{2}W_{s,t}}{\partial s\partial t},&(s,t)\in\mathcal{T}^{2}\\
&\\
X(s,0)=X(0,t)=x&\end{array}\right.,

where

,

2.1

is a Borel measurable function and

This equation can also be regarded as the following stochastic differential equation (SDE) in the plane

(1.2)

The objective of this paper is two-fold: first we construct a unique (Malliavin
differentiable) strong solution to the SDE (

In order to overcome this difficulty, we devise an algorithm for the selection of specific rectangles with respect to the Wiener sheet, which are used in connection with an integration by parts argument (see for example Section
3

These results shed light on the regularisation effect of Gaussian white noise on ill-posed singular (noiseless) HPDEs, which do not admit existence, uniqueness or regularity of solutions, in general.

2.1.
Weak and strong solutions to SDEs in the plane

[

Definition 2.1

We call a filtered probability space any probability space

with a family

(3)

\mathcal{F}_{s_{1},t_{1}}=\bigcap\limits_{\{(s_{2},t_{2}):\,(s_{1},t_{1})\prec(s_{2},t_{2})\}}\mathcal{F}_{s_{2},t_{2}}.

Definition 2.2
.

We call a one-dimensional
(\mathcal{F}_{s,t})
-Brownian sheet on a filtered probability space

(3)

2.2.

. We now introduce a set similar to the Cameron-Martin space in the one parameter case.

\displaystyle H:=

\displaystyle\Big\{h\in\Omega;\,\text{there exists }\dot{h}^{i}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{T}^{2}),i=1\ldots,d,\text{ such that }

(2.1)

\displaystyle h^{i}(z)=\int_{R_{z}}\dot{h}^{i}(r)\mathrm{d}r,\text{ for any }z\in\mathcal{T}^{2}\text{ and for any }i\Big\}.

The set
H
is a Hilbert space with the inner product

\langle h_{1},h_{2}\rangle_{H}=\int_{\mathcal{T}^{2}}\underset{i=1}{\overset{d}{\sum}}\dot{h}_{1}^{i}(r)\dot{h}_{2}^{i}(r)\mathrm{d}r.

A Wiener functional is any measurable function defined on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
. A Wiener functional
F:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is smooth if there exist some
n\geq 1
and an infinitely differentiable function
f
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

(1)

f
and all its derivatives are at most of polynomial growth,

(2)

F(\omega)=f(\omega(z_{1}),\ldots,\omega(z_{n}))
for some
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\in\mathcal{T}^{2}
.

Every smooth functional F is Fréchet-differentiable, and the derivative of
F
in the direction of any vector
h\in H
is given by

\displaystyle DF(h)=

\displaystyle\underset{j=1}{\overset{d}{\sum}}\underset{i=1}{\overset{n}{\sum}}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}^{j}}(\omega(z_{1}),\ldots,\omega(z_{n}))h^{j}(z_{i})

(2.2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{T}^{2}}\underset{j=1}{\overset{d}{\sum}}\underset{i=1}{\overset{n}{\sum}}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}^{j}}(\omega(z_{1}),\ldots,\omega(z_{n}))1_{R_{z_{i}}}(r)\dot{h}^{j}(r)\mathrm{d}r.

We denote by
\mathcal{D}_{1,2}
the closed hull of family of smooth functionals with respect to the following norm:

(2.3)

\left\|F\right\|_{1,2}^{2}:=\left\|F\right\|_{L^{2}(P)}^{2}+\mathbb{E}[\left\|DF\right\|_{HS}^{2}],

where
\|\cdot\|
is the Hilbert-Schmidt norm defined by

\left\|DF\right\|_{HS}=\Big(\int_{\mathcal{T}^{2}}\underset{j=1}{\overset{d}{\sum}}\Big\{\underset{i=1}{\overset{n}{\sum}}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}^{j}}(\omega(z_{1}),\ldots,\omega(z_{n}))1_{R_{z_{i}}(r)}\Big\}^{2}\mathrm{d}r\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Let
W=\{W(z);\,z\in\mathcal{T}^{2}\}
be a Brownian sheet on the above probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
. Again for each
z\in\mathcal{T}^{2}
,
\mathcal{F}_{z}
is the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the random variables
\{W(\xi);\xi\preceq z\}
completed with respect to
\mathbb{P}
. We say that a stochastic process
X=\{X(z),z\in\mathcal{T}^{2}\}
is adapted if
X(z)
is
\mathcal{F}_{z}
-measurable for all
z\in\mathcal{T}^{2}
. For
h\in L^{2}(\mathcal{T}^{2})
, we define

W(h)=\int_{\mathcal{T}^{2}}h(z)\dot{W}(\mathrm{d}z).

We recall that
\dot{W}=(\dot{W}^{(1)},\ldots,\dot{W}^{(d)})
is the mean-zero Gaussian process indexed by the Borel field
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{T}^{2})
on
\mathcal{T}^{2}
with covariance functions

\mathbb{E}\Big[\dot{W}^{(i)}(A)\dot{W}^{(j)}(B)\Big]=\delta_{i,j}\text{Leb}(A\cap B),\quad\forall\,A,B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{T}^{2}).

where
\text{Leb}(\cdot)
denotes the Lebesgue measure on
\mathcal{T}^{2}
and
\delta_{i,j}=1
if
i=j
and
\delta_{i,j}=0
otherwise.

Let
C^{\infty}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be the space of infinitely differentiable functions with bounded derivatives of all orders. Denote by
\mathcal{S}
the space of smooth random variables, that is the space of functions
F
such that there exist an integer
n
and
f\in C^{\infty}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that

F=f(W(h_{1}),\ldots,W(h_{n})).

For a smooth
F
given above, its derivative is defined as a random field on
\mathcal{T}^{2}
and given by

(2.4)

\displaystyle D_{z}F=

\displaystyle\underset{i=1}{\overset{d}{\sum}}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}(W(h_{1}),\ldots,W(h_{n}))h_{i}(z),

and we denote by
\mathcal{D}^{1,2}
the closure of
\mathcal{S}
with respect to the seminorm

(2.5)

\left\|F\right\|_{1,2}^{2}:=\left\|F\right\|_{L^{2}(P)}^{2}+\mathbb{E}[\left\|D_{z}F\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{T}^{2})}^{2}].

In the general case, we can describe the algorithm as follows:

Step 1:
We start in the first row with an orientation point
\mathcal{O}_{1}
. There are two possibilities:

(1)

If
\mathcal{O}_{1}=z_{1,\sigma^{-1}(1)}
, then we choose as integration by
parts variable which leads to a factor
B_{\sigma^{-1}(1),1}
“at this

(2)

If
\mathcal{O}_{1}=(z_{1,\sigma^{-1}(1)},z_{1,\sigma^{-1}(1)+1})
, then
z_{1,\sigma^{-1}(1)}
is the integration by parts
variable and
z_{1,\sigma^{-1}(1)+1}
the substitution variable for
z_{1,\sigma^{-1}(1)}
. Then
z_{1,\sigma^{-1}(1)}
gives rise to the
factor
B_{\sigma^{-1}(1),1}
and
z_{1,\sigma^{-1}(1)+1}
to the factor
B_{\sigma^{-1}(1)+1,1}
. We can then move from
B_{\sigma^{-1}(1),1}
or
B_{\sigma^{-1}(1)+1,1}
in the first row to the next orientation point
\mathcal{O}_{2}
in the second row.

Step 2:
In this step we have 5 possibilities:

(1)

If we start in
B_{\sigma^{-1}(1),1}

and if
\mathcal{O}_{2}=z_{2,\sigma^{-1}(2)}
, then
z_{2,\sigma^{-1}(2)}

is not in the column of
B_{\sigma^{-1}(1),1}
and we can select
z_{2,\sigma^{-1}(2)}
as an integration by parts variable.

(2)

If we begin the
path with
B_{\sigma^{-1}(1)+1,1}
and if
\mathcal{O}_{2}=z_{2,\sigma^{-1}(2)}
, then we distinguish two cases:

•

Case 1:

z_{2,\sigma^{-1}(2)}
is not in the column of
B_{\sigma^{-1}(1)+1,1}
. In this case,
we choose
z_{2,\sigma^{-1}(2)}
as an integration by parts variable (which
corresponds to the next factor
B_{\sigma^{-1}(2),2}
).

•

Case 2:

z_{2,\sigma^{-1}(2)}
belongs to the column of
B_{\sigma^{-1}(1)+1,1}
.
Then, we select the closest variable
z_{2,\sigma^{-1}(2)-k}
to
z_{2,\sigma^{-1}(2)}
on the left, which does not belong to the previous
column (here
k=1
). In this way, we obtain the factor
B_{\sigma^{-1}(2)-k,2}
.

(3)

Let us now start in
B_{\sigma^{-1}(1),1}
in connection
with
\mathcal{O}_{2}=(z_{2,\sigma^{-1}(2)},z_{2,\sigma^{-1}(2)+1})
.
Then,
z_{2,\sigma^{-1}(2)}
is not in the column of
B_{\sigma^{-1}(1),1}

and we can choose this variable as an integration by parts variable, which
gives rise to
B_{\sigma^{-1}(2),2}
.

•

Case 1:
If
z_{2,\sigma^{-1}(2)+1}
is not
in the column of
B_{\sigma^{-1}(1),1}
, then we select
z_{2,\sigma^{-1}(2)+1}
as a substitution variable, which gives rise to the factor
B_{\sigma^{-1}(2)+1,2}
(in the path
(B_{\sigma^{-1}(1),1},B_{\sigma^{-1}(2)+1,2},\ldots)
).

•

Case 2:
However, if
z_{2,\sigma^{-1}(2)+1}
is in the column
of
B_{\sigma^{-1}(1),1}
, then we take the closest variable
z_{2,\sigma^{-1}(2)+1+k}
to
z_{2,\sigma^{-1}(2)+1}
on the right, which is not in
the column of
B_{\sigma^{-1}(1),1}
, as the new substitution variable (

). The latter leads to the factor
B_{\sigma^{-1}(2)+1+k,2}
. In
this case, we get a path
(B_{\sigma^{-1}(1),1},B_{\sigma^{-1}(2)+1+k,2},\ldots)
.

(4)

Then we have to look at the case
B_{\sigma^{-1}(1)+1,1}
in connection with
\mathcal{O}_{2}=\mathcal{(}z_{2,\sigma^{-1}(2)},z_{2,\sigma^{-1}(2)+1})
. Here,

•

Case 1:
if
z_{2,\sigma^{-1}(2)}
is not
in the same column as
B_{\sigma^{-1}(1)+1,1}
, then we choose this

(B_{\sigma^{-1}(1)+1,1},B_{\sigma^{-1}(2),2},\ldots)
.

•

Case 2:
On the other hand, if
z_{2,\sigma^{-1}(2)}
belongs to the same column as
B_{\sigma^{-1}(1)+1,1}
, then we make a shift to the left and choose the variable
z_{2,\sigma^{-1}(2)-k}

, which does not belong to

. This leads to path of the form

.

(5)

The remaining case
with respect to the second row leads either to the path
(B_{\sigma^{-1}(1)+1,1},B_{\sigma^{-1}(2)+1,2},\ldots)

(B_{\sigma^{-1}(1)+1,1},B_{\sigma^{-1}(2)+1+k,2},\ldots)

Step i:
Here we consider two possibilities:

(1)

If we depart here from the specific path of

4.
Proof of the main result

In this section, we give the proof of the main result. More precisely, we consider the following SDE

(4.1)

\displaystyle\mathrm{X}_{s,t}=b(s,t,X_{s,t})\mathrm{d}{s}\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}W_{s,t},\,(s,t)\in\mathcal{T}^{2},X_{s,0}=x=X_{0,t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

in which the drift coefficient
b:\mathcal{T}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
is Borel measurable and bounded.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
b:\mathcal{T}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a smooth function with compact support. Then the corresponding strong solution
X
of (
4.1
) satisfies

(4.2)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big|D_{r,u}X_{s,t}-D_{\bar{r},\bar{u}}X_{s,t}\Big|^{2}\Big]\leq C_{d}(\|b\|_{\infty})(|r-\bar{r}|+|u-\bar{u}|)^{\alpha}

for
0\leq(\bar{r},\bar{u})\preceq(r,u)\preceq(s,t)\preceq(T,T)
,
\alpha=\alpha(s,t)>0
and

(4.3)

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq(r,u)\leq(T,T)}\ E\left[|D_{r,u}X_{s,t}|^{2}\right]\leq C_{d}(\|b\|_{\infty}),

where
C_{d,}:[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
is an increasing, continuous function,
|\cdot|
a matrix-norm on
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
.

Proof.

Using the the chain-rule for the Malliavin derivatives (see
[
31
, Page 144]
), it follows that

\displaystyle D_{r,u}X_{s,t}=

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{d}+\int_{r}^{s}\int_{u}^{t}b^{\prime}(s_{1},t_{1},X_{s_{1},t_{1}})D_{r,u}X_{s_{1},t_{1}}\mathrm{d}s_{1}\mathrm{d}t_{1}

(4.4)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{d}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}r<s_{n}<\ldots<s_{1}<s\\
u<t_{n}<\ldots<t_{1}<t\end{subarray}}b^{\prime}(s_{1},t_{1},X_{s_{1},t_{1}})\dots b^{\prime}(s_{n},t_{n},X_{s_{n},t_{n}})\mathrm{d}s_{1}\mathrm{d}t_{1}\dots\mathrm{d}s_{n}\mathrm{d}t_{n}

\mathbb{P}
-a.e. for all
(r,u)\preceq(s,t)
. Here
\mathcal{I}_{d}
is the
d\times d
identity matrix and
b^{\prime}(s,t,x)=\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}b^{(j)}(s,t,x)\right)_{1\leq i,j\leq d}
is the spatial Jacobian derivative of
b
.
Note that the above representation for the Malliavin derivative can be
obtained by using Picard iteration. Alternatively, one can show that this
representation solves the equation (
4
). Then uniqueness can be
verified by applying a multiparameter version of Grönwall’s Lemma (see
e.g.
[
38
, Lemma 5.1.1]
).

Further, we have that

\displaystyle D_{r,u}X_{s,t}-D_{\bar{r},\bar{u}}X_{s,t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{(\nabla^{(k)}_{\bar{r},r})^{4}}\int_{(\nabla^{(k)}_{\bar{u},t})^{4}}g_{1}(s_{1},t_{1})\ldots g_{4k}(s_{4k},t_{4k})\,\mathrm{d}s_{1}\mathrm{d}t_{1}\ldots\mathrm{d}s_{4k}\mathrm{d}t_{4k}\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\sigma,\gamma}\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{\nabla^{(4k,\sigma)}_{\bar{r},r}}\int_{\nabla^{(4k,\gamma)}_{\bar{u},t}}g_{1}(s_{1},t_{1})\ldots g_{4k}(s_{4k},t_{4k})\,\mathrm{d}s_{1}\mathrm{d}t_{1}\ldots\mathrm{d}s_{4k}\mathrm{d}t_{4k}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\sigma,\gamma}\int_{\nabla^{(4k,\sigma)}_{\bar{r},r}}\int_{\nabla^{(4k,\gamma)}_{\bar{u},t}}|\mathbb{E}[g_{1}(s_{1},t_{1})\ldots g_{4k}(s_{4k},t_{4k})]|\,\mathrm{d}s_{1}\mathrm{d}t_{1}\ldots\mathrm{d}s_{4k}\mathrm{d}t_{4k}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{4^{9k}C_{1}^{4k}\|b\|^{4k}_{\infty}(r-\bar{r})^{2k}(t-\bar{u})^{2k}}{\Gamma\left(\frac{4k+1}{2}\right)^{2}},

since the number of elements in
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{4k}
does not exceed
2^{9k}
. As a consequence,

\displaystyle I_{1}\leq\Big(\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{2^{5k}d^{2k}C_{1}^{k}\|b\|^{k}_{\infty}(r-\bar{r})^{(k-1)/2}(t-\bar{u})^{k/2}}{\Gamma\left(\frac{4k+1}{2}\right)^{1/2}}\Big)^{2}|r-\bar{r}|=C^{(1)}_{d}(\|b\|_{\infty})|r-\bar{r}|.

Similarly, we have

\displaystyle I_{2}\leq\Big(\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{2^{5k}d^{2k}C_{1}^{k}\|b\|^{k}_{\infty}(s-\bar{r})^{k/2}(u-\bar{u})^{(k-1)/2}}{\Gamma\left(\frac{4k+1}{2}\right)^{1/2}}\Big)^{2}|u-\bar{u}|=C^{(2)}_{d}(\|b\|_{\infty})|u-\bar{u}|,

\displaystyle I_{5}\leq

\displaystyle\Big(\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{2^{5k}d^{2k}C_{1}^{k}\|b\|^{k}_{\infty}(r-\bar{r})^{(k-1)/2}(u-\bar{u})^{(k-1)/2}}{\Gamma\left(\frac{8k+1}{2}\right)^{1/4}}\Big)^{2}|r-\bar{r}|\times|u-\bar{u}|=C^{(5)}_{d}(\|b\|_{\infty})|r-\bar{r}|\times|u-\bar{u}|

and

\displaystyle I_{6}\leq

\displaystyle\Big(1+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{2^{5k}d^{2k}C_{1}^{k}\|b\|^{k}_{\infty}(s-r)^{k/2}(t-u)^{k/2}}{\Gamma\left(\frac{8k+1}{2}\right)^{1/4}}\Big)^{2}=C^{(6)}_{d}(\|b\|_{\infty})

On the other hand
\Xi^{(k+n)}_{\bar{u},u,t}\subset\nabla_{\bar{u},t}^{(k+n)}
gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(\int_{\Delta^{(k+n)}_{\bar{r},r,s}}\int_{\Xi^{(k+n)}_{\bar{u},u,t}}\frac{\partial b^{(l_{1})}_{s_{1},t_{1}}}{\partial x_{i}}(W_{s_{1},t_{1}})\frac{\partial b^{(l_{2})}_{s_{2},t_{2}}}{\partial x_{l_{1}}}(W_{s_{2},t_{2}})\ldots\frac{\partial b^{(j)}_{s_{k+n},t_{k+n}}}{\partial x_{l_{k+n-1}}}(W_{s_{k+n},t_{k+n}})\,\mathrm{d}s_{1}\mathrm{d}t_{1}\ldots\mathrm{d}s_{k+n}\mathrm{d}t_{k+n}\Big)^{4}\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{(\Delta^{(k+n)}_{\bar{r},r,s})^{4}}\int_{(\Xi^{(k+n)}_{\bar{u},u,t})^{4}}g_{1}(s_{1},t_{1})\ldots g_{4(k+n)}(s_{4(k+n)},t_{4(k+n)})\,\mathrm{d}s_{1}\mathrm{d}t_{1}\ldots\mathrm{d}s_{4(k+n)}\mathrm{d}t_{4(k+n)}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{(\Delta^{(k+n)}_{\bar{r},r,s})^{4}}\int_{(\Xi^{(k+n)}_{\bar{u},u,t})^{4}}|\mathbb{E}[g_{1}(s_{1},t_{1})\ldots g_{4(k+n)}(s_{4(k+n)},t_{4(k+n)})]|\,\mathrm{d}s_{1}\mathrm{d}t_{1}\ldots\mathrm{d}s_{4(k+n)}\mathrm{d}t_{4(k+n)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{(\Delta^{(k+n)}_{\bar{r},r,s})^{4}}\int_{(\nabla^{(k+n)}_{\bar{u},t})^{4}}|\mathbb{E}[g_{1}(s_{1},t_{1})\ldots g_{4(k+n)}(s_{4(k+n)},t_{4(k+n)})]|\,\mathrm{d}s_{1}\mathrm{d}t_{1}\ldots\mathrm{d}s_{4(k+n)}\mathrm{d}t_{4(k+n)},

where
g_{\ell}\in\Big\{\frac{\partial b^{(j)}_{\cdot,\cdot}}{\partial x_{i}}(W_{\cdot,\cdot}),\,1\leq i,j\leq d\Big\}
for all
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,4(k+n)\}
. Hence, as

\{\Delta^{(4(k+n),\pi,\rho)}_{\bar{r},r,s}\}_{(\pi,\rho)\in\widehat{\mathcal{P}}^{\ast}_{4k}\times\widehat{\mathcal{P}}^{\ast\ast}_{4n}}
is a partition of
(\Delta^{(k+n)}_{\bar{r},r,s})^{4}
(see Lemma
3.4
) and
\{\nabla^{(4(k+n),\sigma)}_{\bar{u},t}\}_{\sigma\in\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{4(k+n)}}
is a partition of
(\nabla^{(k+n)}_{\bar{u},t})^{4}
, we deduce from (
3.3
) (in Corollary
3.2
), that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(\int_{\Delta^{(k+n)}_{\bar{r},r,s}}\int_{\Xi^{(k+n)}_{\bar{u},u,t}}\frac{\partial b^{(l_{1})}_{s_{1},t_{1}}}{\partial x_{i}}(W_{s_{1},t_{1}})\frac{\partial b^{(l_{2})}_{s_{2},t_{2}}}{\partial x_{l_{1}}}(W_{s_{2},t_{2}})\ldots\frac{\partial b^{(j)}_{s_{k+n},t_{k+n}}}{\partial x_{l_{k+n-1}}}(W_{s_{k+n},t_{k+n}})\,\mathrm{d}s_{1}\mathrm{d}t_{1}\ldots\mathrm{d}s_{k+n}\mathrm{d}t_{k+n}\Big)^{4}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{(\Delta^{(k+n)}_{\bar{r},r,s})^{4}}\int_{(\nabla^{(k+n)}_{\bar{u},t})^{4}}|\mathbb{E}[g_{1}(s_{1},t_{1})\ldots g_{4(k+n)}(s_{4(k+n)},t_{4(k+n)})]|\,\mathrm{d}s_{1}\mathrm{d}t_{1}\ldots\mathrm{d}s_{4(k+n)}\mathrm{d}t_{4(k+n)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\pi,\rho,\sigma}\int_{\Delta^{(4(k+n),\pi,\rho)}_{\bar{r},r,s}}\int_{\nabla^{(4(k+n),\sigma)}_{\bar{u},t}}|\mathbb{E}[g_{1}(s_{1},t_{1})\ldots g_{4(k+n)}(s_{4(k+n)},t_{4(k+n)})]|\,\mathrm{d}s_{1}\mathrm{d}t_{1}\ldots\mathrm{d}s_{4(k+n)}\mathrm{d}t_{4(k+n)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{4^{9(k+n)}C_{1}^{4(k+n)}\|b\|^{4(k+n)}_{\infty}(r-\bar{r})^{2n}(s-r)^{2k}(t-\bar{u})^{2(k+n)}}{\Gamma\left(\frac{4n+1}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{4k+1}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{4(k+n)+1}{2}\right)},

since the number of elements in
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}^{\ast}_{4k}
(respectively
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}^{\ast}_{4n}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{4(k+n)}
) does not exceed
2^{9k}
(respectively
2^{9n}
and
2^{9(k+n)}
). Thus,

\displaystyle I_{3}\leq\Big(\sum\limits_{k,n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(2d)^{5(k+n)}C_{1}^{k+n}\|b\|^{k+n}_{\infty}(r-\bar{r})^{(n-1)/2}(s-r)^{k/2}(t-\bar{u})^{(k+n)/2}}{\Gamma\Big(\frac{4n+1}{2}\Big)^{1/4}\Gamma\Big(\frac{4k+1}{2}\Big)^{1/4}\Gamma\Big(\frac{4(k+n)+1}{2}\Big)^{1/4}}\Big)^{2}|r-\bar{r}|=C^{(3)}_{d}(\|b\|_{\infty})|r-\bar{r}|.

Similarly, one may show that

\displaystyle I_{4}\leq\Big(\sum\limits_{k,n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(2d)^{5(k+n)}C_{1}^{k+n}\|b\|^{k+n}_{\infty}(s-\bar{r})^{(k+n)/2}(t-u)^{k/2}(u-\bar{u})^{(n-1)/2}}{\Gamma\Big(\frac{4n+1}{2}\Big)^{1/4}\Gamma\Big(\frac{4k+1}{2}\Big)^{1/4}\Gamma\Big(\frac{4(k+n)+1}{2}\Big)^{1/4}}\Big)^{2}|u-\bar{u}|=C^{(4)}_{d}(\|b\|_{\infty})|u-\bar{u}|.

This ends the proof of (
4.2
). The proof of (

) follows the same lines.

Lemma 4.2
.

Assume that the vector field
b
in the SDE (
1.2
) belongs
to
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{T}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})

The proof is very similar to that of Lemma

\frac{\partial}{\partial x}X_{s,t}^{x}=\mathcal{I}_{d\times d}+\int_{0}^{s}\int_{0}^{t}b^{\prime}(r,l,X_{r,l}^{x})\frac{\partial}{\partial x}X_{r,l}^{x}\mathrm{d}l\mathrm{d}r.

Then, using Picard iteration, we find the representation

\frac{\partial}{\partial x}X_{s,t}^{x}=\mathcal{I}_{d\times d}+\sum_{n\geq 1}\int_{\begin{subarray}0<r_{n}<\cdots<r_{1}<s\\
0<l_{n}<\cdots<l_{1}<t\end{subarray}}b^{\prime}(r_{n},l_{n},X_{r_{n},l_{n}}^{x})\ldots b^{\prime}(r_{1},l_{1},X_{r_{1},l_{1}}^{x})\mathrm{d}l_{n}\mathrm{d}r_{n}\cdots\mathrm{d}l_{1}\mathrm{d}r_{1}

a.e. for all
s,t
. If we replace the Malliavin derivative
D_{u,v}X_{s,t}^{x,n}
by the derivative
\frac{\partial}{\partial x}X_{s,t}^{x}
in the proof of Lemma
4.1
, then we obtain the result for
p=2
. The proof for
p>2
rests on the repeated application of Lemmas
3.3
and
3.4
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
b\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{T}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Assume a sequence of functions
\left\{b_{n}\right\}_{n\geq 1}\subset C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{T}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})

b_{n}(t,x)\underset{n\longrightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}b(t,x)\text{ }(t,x)\text{-a.e.}

\sup_{n\geq 1}\left\|b_{n}\right\|_{\infty}<\infty\text{.}

Let
X_{\cdot}^{x,n}
be the strong solution of the SDE associated with the
vector field
b_{n}
for

X_{\cdot}^{x}
is a weak

continuous functions
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}

\varphi(X_{t_{1},t_{2}}^{x,n})\underset{n\longrightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi(X_{t_{1},t_{2}}^{x})\right.\left|\mathcal{F}_{t_{1},t_{2}}\right]

weakly in

.

Proof.

Recall that the Doleans-Dade exponential for square integrable adapted
processes
Y_{\cdot}
on the plane is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\int_{\left[0,T\right]\times\left[0,u\right]}\left\langle Y_{s_{1},s_{2}},\mathrm{d}W_{s_{1},s_{2}}\right\rangle)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp(\int_{\left[0,T\right]\times\left[0,u\right]}\left\langle Y_{s_{1},s_{2}},\mathrm{d}W_{s_{1},s_{2}}\right\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{u}\left\|Y_{s_{1},s_{2}}\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}s_{1}\mathrm{d}s_{2}).

See Appendix. Since

\sup_{n\geq 1}\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big\|\int_{\mathcal{T}^{2}}b_{n}(s_{1},s_{2},x+W_{s_{1},s_{2}})\mathrm{d}W_{s_{1},s_{2}}\Big\|^{p}\Big]<\infty

\int_{\left[0,T\right]\times\left[0,u\right]}b_{n}(s_{1},s_{2},x+W_{s_{1},s_{2}})\mathrm{d}W_{s_{1},s_{2}}\underset{n\longrightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}\int_{\left[0,T\right]\times\left[0,u\right]}b(s_{1},s_{2},x+W_{s_{1},s_{2}})\mathrm{d}W_{s_{1},s_{2}}

in
L^{p}(\Omega)

.

\displaystyle\Pi_{t_{1},t_{2}}:=\Big\{\exp\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{\left[0,u_{1}\right]\times\left[0,u_{2}\right]}f_{i}(s_{1},s_{2})\mathrm{d}W_{s_{1},s_{2}}^{j}\Big):0\leq u_{i}\leq t_{i},i=1,2,f_{j}\in L^{\infty}(\left[0,t_{1}\right]\times\left[0,t_{2}\right]),j=1,\ldots,d\Big\}

\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi(X_{t_{1},t_{2}}^{x,n})\xi\right]\underset{n\longrightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi(X_{t_{1},t_{2}}^{x})\right.\left|\mathcal{F}_{t_{1},t_{2}}\right]\xi\right]

\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}_{n}=\mathcal{E}(\int_{\mathcal{T}^{2}}\left\langle b_{n}(s_{1},s_{2},X_{s_{1},s_{2}}^{x,n}),\mathrm{d}W_{s_{1},s_{2}}\right\rangle)\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P},\,\,\,n\geq 1

We have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbb{E}\Big[\varphi(X_{t_{1},t_{2}}^{x})\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{1},t_{2}}\Big]\exp(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{\left[0,u_{1}\right]\times\left[0,u_{2}\right]}f_{j}(s_{1},s_{2})\mathrm{d}W_{s_{1},s_{2}}^{j})\Big]

\displaystyle E\Big[\mathbb{E}\Big[\varphi(X_{t_{1},t_{2}}^{x})\exp(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{\left[0,u_{1}\right]\times\left[0,u_{2}\right]}f_{j}(s_{1},s_{2})\mathrm{d}W_{s_{1},s_{2}}^{j})\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{1},t_{2}}\Big]\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\varphi(X_{t_{1},t_{2}}^{x})\exp(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{\left[0,u_{1}\right]\times\left[0,u_{2}\right]}f_{j}(s_{1},s_{2})\mathrm{d}W_{s_{1},s_{2}}^{j})\Big].

Since

for all

\displaystyle\Big|\mathbb{E}\Big[\varphi(X_{t_{1},t_{2}}^{x,n})\exp(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{\left[0,u_{1}\right]\times\left[0,u_{2}\right]}f_{j}(s_{1},s_{2})\mathrm{d}W_{s_{1},s_{2}}^{j})\Big]-\mathbb{E}\Big[\varphi(X_{t_{1},t_{2}}^{x})\exp\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{\left[0,u_{1}\right]\times\left[0,u_{2}\right]}f_{j}(s_{1},s_{2})\mathrm{d}W_{s_{1},s_{2}}^{j}\Big)\Big]\Big|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\times\mathcal{E}(\int_{\mathcal{T}^{2}}\left\langle b_{n}(s_{1},s_{2},x+W_{s_{1},s_{2}}),\mathrm{d}W_{s_{1},s_{2}}\right\rangle)\Big]

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}\Big[\varphi(x+W_{t_{1},t_{2}})\exp\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{\left[0,u_{1}\right]\times\left[0,u_{2}\right]}f_{j}(s_{1},s_{2})\mathrm{d}W_{s_{1},s_{2}}^{,j}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{u_{1}}\int_{0}^{u_{2}}f_{j}(s_{1},s_{2})b^{j}(s_{1},s_{2},x+W_{s_{1},s_{2}})\mathrm{d}s_{1}\mathrm{d}s_{2}\Big)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal{T}^{2}}\left\|b_{n}(s_{1},s_{2},x+W_{s_{1},s_{2}})\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}s_{1}\mathrm{d}s_{2}\Big|^{4}\Big]^{1/4}

and where

is a constant. It follows from the martingale property
of the Doleans-Dade exponential and the bounded assumption of the functions
(see Appendix) that
\ \sup_{n\geq 1}I_{1}^{n}<\infty
. Further, using also

, which gives the result.

Proof of Theorem
2.11
.

We use the previous results to prove

Step 2:
Let
b_{n}:\mathcal{T}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d},n\geq 1
a sequence of
compactly supported smooth vector fields, which approximates
b\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{T}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})

) in the sense of Lemma

. Denote by
X_{\cdot,\cdot}^{x,n},n\geq 1

) associated with the vector fields
b_{n},n\geq 1

(s,t)\in\mathcal{T}^{2}

that
there exists an increasing continuous function
C_{d}:\left[0,\infty\right)\longrightarrow\left[0,\infty\right)

n

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq r\leq s\\
0\leq u\leq t\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big\|D_{r,u}X_{s,t}^{x,n}\Big\|^{2}\Big]\leq C_{d}\Big(\sup_{n\geq 1}\left\|b_{n}\right\|_{\infty}\Big)

as well as

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|D_{r,u}X_{s,t}^{x,n}-D_{\bar{r},\bar{u}}X_{s,t}^{x,n}\right\|^{2}\right]\leq C_{d}\Big(\sup_{n\geq 1}\left\|b_{n}\right\|_{\infty}\Big)(\left|r-\bar{r}\right|+\left|u-\bar{u}\right|)^{\alpha}

for all

and for some

. The latter in connection with the compactness criterion of Theorem
A.1
entails that there exists a subsequence

and some

such that

X_{s,t}^{x,n_{k}}\underset{k\longrightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}Y_{s,t}^{x}\text{ in }L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})\text{.}

Step 3:
Let
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a bounded
continuous function. Then, by selecting an appropriate subsequence with
respect to

, we obtain from Lemma

\varphi(Y_{s,t}^{x})=\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi(X_{s,t}^{x})\right.\left|\mathcal{F}_{s,t}\right]\text{ }a.e.,

where

is a unique weak solution to the SDE (

) and
\mathcal{F}=\left\{\mathcal{F}_{s,t}\right\}_{0\leq s,t\leq}
is the (completed) filtration generated by the driving noise.
Using an approximation argument (with respect to
\varphi
) combined with
dominated convergence, we find that

Y_{s,t}^{x}=\mathbb{E}\left[X_{s,t}^{x}\right.\left|\mathcal{F}_{s,t}\right]\text{ }a.e.

So we see (for the whole sequence) that

X_{s,t}^{x,n}\underset{n\longrightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}\text{ }\mathbb{E}\left[X_{s,t}^{x}\right.\left|\mathcal{F}_{s,t}\right]\text{ in }L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})\text{.}

Hence, for an arbitrary bounded continuous function

we get the following ”transformation

\varphi(\mathbb{E}\left[X_{s,t}^{x}\right.\left|\mathcal{F}_{s,t}\right])=\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi(X_{s,t}^{x})\right.\left|\mathcal{F}_{s,t}\right]\text{ }a.e.

Using once again an approximation argument, we find that

\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\pi_{i}(X_{s,t}^{x})\right.\left|\mathcal{F}_{s,t}\right]\right)^{2}=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\pi_{i}(X_{s,t}^{x})\right)^{2}\right.\left|\mathcal{F}_{s,t}\right],\text{ }a.e.,i=1,\ldots,d

for the projections

,

\mathbb{E}\left[X_{s,t}^{x}\right.\left|\mathcal{F}_{s,t}\right]=X_{s,t}^{x}\text{ }a.e.

Therefore, the weak solution

must be a strong one. Further, we obtain strong uniqueness of solutions by using Girsanov‘s theorem in connection with the
\mathcal{S}-
transform (or alternatively the Wiener transform) in the case of a Wiener sheet. See for example a very similar argument in
[

]
in the Wiener noise case. It also follows from

that for all
(s,t)\in\left[0,1\right]^{2}

X_{s,t}^{x}
is Malliavin differentiable.

The proof of (local) Sobolev differentiability of the solution with respect
to the initial value follows from the estimate in Lemma
4.2
.
See e.g.
[

]
.

Remark 4.4
.

In the proof of Theorem

we constructed a unique strong solution

with respect to a certain probability space

. However, strong solutions can be obtained on any other probability space
by using the following argument: We know that

\displaystyle s_{(i+1)k}<\ldots<s_{1+ik}\,\text{ for all }i\in\{0,1,\ldots,m-1\}
