Score-based Change Point Detection via Tracking the Best of Infinitely Many Experts
Anna Markovich, Nikita Puchkin

Abstract
We propose an algorithm for nonparametric online change point detection based on sequential score function estimation and the tracking the best expert approach. The core of the procedure is a version of the fixed share forecaster tailored to the case of infinite number of experts and quadratic loss functions. The algorithm shows promising results in numerical experiments on artificial and real-world data sets. Its performance is supported by rigorous high-probability bounds describing behaviour of the test statistic in the pre-change and post-change regimes.

Introduction

Let
\mathbf{X}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{X}_{T}
be a sequence of i.i.d. random vectors from a Euclidean space
{\mathcal{X}}
such that
\mathbf{X}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{X}_{\tau^{*}}
have a probability density
\mathsf{p}
(with respect to the Lebesgue measure) while
\mathbf{X}_{\tau^{*}+1},\dots,\mathbf{X}_{T}
are drawn from a density
\mathsf{q}\neq\mathsf{p}
. The parameter
\tau^{*}\in\{1,\dots,T-1\}
as well as the densities
\mathsf{p}
and
\mathsf{q}
are unknown and, based on the successively arriving observations, our goal is to determine the moment of the distribution change as soon as possible. This problem referred to as online change point detection has a long history going back to classical works of
Page (1954)
;
Page (1955)
,
Shiryaev (1961)
;
Shiryaev (1963)
and
Roberts (1966)
but still continues its extensive development. Researchers thoroughly examined various setups. The most popular one is the problem of mean shift detection
(
Pein et al., 2017
;
Eichinger and Kirch, 2018
;
Enikeeva and Harchaoui, 2019
;
Wang et al., 2020
;
Yu et al., 2023
;
Yu et al., 2022
;
Rinaldo et al., 2021
;
Chen et al., 2022
;
Sun et al., 2022
)
, that is,
\mathsf{q}(\textbf{{x}})=\mathsf{p}(\textbf{{x}}-\boldsymbol{\mu})
for some vector
\boldsymbol{\mu}
. In addition, it is usually assumed that both the densities
\mathsf{p}
and
\mathsf{q}
are Gaussian or at least have sub-Gaussian tails. A natural extension of the mean shift detection is the framework of parametric change point detection
(
Cao et al., 2018
;
Maillard, 2019
;
Junzhuo Chen and Xie, 2020
;
Dette and Gösmann, 2020
;
Yu et al., 2023
;
Chaudhuri et al., 2021
;
Chaudhuri and Fellouris, 2022
;
Corradin et al., 2022
;
Sun et al., 2022
;
Titsias et al., 2022
;
Lee et al., 2023
;
Xie et al., 2023
;
Wu et al., 2023
;
Wang and Xie, 2024
)
, where the authors suppose that
\mathsf{p}
and
\mathsf{q}
belong to a fixed family of distributions induced by a finite-dimensional parameter. In this case, one has to detect the moment
\tau^{*}
when the value of the corresponding parameter changes. We would like to note that, in the literature on parametric change point detection, the authors usually impose strong modeling assumptions on the underlying densities
\mathsf{p}
and
\mathsf{q}
. This significantly affects the practical use of such procedures. In the present paper, we are interested in a more general nonparametric change point setup
(
Hero, 2006
;
Harchaoui et al., 2008
;
Liu et al., 2013
;
Xie et al., 2013
;
Zou et al., 2014
;
Li et al., 2015
;
Biau et al., 2016
;
Garreau and Arlot, 2018
;
Arlot et al., 2019
;
Kurt et al., 2021
;
Xie and Xie, 2021
;
Madrid Padilla et al., 2022
;
Shin et al., 2022
;
Ferrari et al., 2023
;
Puchkin and Shcherbakova, 2023
)
. We do not make any presumptions on how
\mathsf{q}
differs from
\mathsf{p}
. For our purposes, it is enough to suppose that the scores
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\log\mathsf{p}
and
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\log\mathsf{q}
corresponding to the pre-change and post-change distributions are smooth and can be approximated well by an appropriate system of functions.
Finally, it is worth mentioning that we are interested in sequential change point detection. This means that we focus our attention on rapid detection of change emergence. There is a vast of literature devoted to localization and estimation of
\tau^{*}
from a retrospectively observed time series
(
Dümbgen and Spokoiny, 2001
;
Zou et al., 2014
;
Matteson and James, 2014
;
Dalang and Shiryaev, 2015
;
Biau et al., 2016
;
Korkas and Fryzlewicz, 2017
;
Garreau and Arlot, 2018
;
Arlot et al., 2019
;
Madrid Padilla et al., 2021
;
Corradin et al., 2022
;
Londschien et al., 2023
)
. This problem called offline change point detection goes beyond the scope of the present paper.

Let us briefly describe the idea of our algorithm. We consider the problem of change point detection through the lens of sequential prediction with expert advice and online convex optimization. In
(
Cao et al., 2018
;
Goldman et al., 2023
)
, the authors have already designed the quickest detection algorithms based on online mirror descent and follow-the-approximate-leader strategy, respectively, but our approach is different. We rely on the standard parametric modelling to approximate the unknown densities
\mathsf{p}
and
\mathsf{q}
. For this purpose, we use a reference class of densities
{\mathcal{P}}=\{\mathsf{p}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}:{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}

with
\mathsf{p}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
of the form

\log\mathsf{p}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\textbf{{x}})=\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\textbf{{x}})^{\top}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\Phi({\boldsymbol{\theta}}),\quad\text{where}\quad\Phi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\log\int\limits_{{\mathcal{X}}}e^{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\textbf{{x}})}\,\mathrm{d}\textbf{{x}}.

Here
\boldsymbol{\Psi}:\textbf{{x}}\mapsto\big(\psi_{1}(\textbf{{x}}),\dots,\psi_{d}(\textbf{{x}})\big)^{\top}
is a fixed mapping. From now on, we can apply online prediction algorithms to determine the best parametric fit for the pre-change and post-change densities
\mathsf{p}
and
\mathsf{q}
. In
(
Cao et al., 2018
)
, the authors measured the quality of forecasts with the negative logarithmic loss. As a consequence, they had to calculate
\Phi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
for various values of
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
on each iteration. Unfortunately, numerical computation of an integral over a high-dimensional space is extremely time consuming. Hence, the algorithm of
Cao et al. (2018)
is useful only when
\Phi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
admits a closed-form expression. This drastically narrows down the practical use of their procedure. To avoid such issues, we measure the closeness of
\mathsf{p}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
to the target densities
\mathsf{p}
and
\mathsf{q}
with the Fisher divergence (see eq. (
3
) for the definition). Given
\mathbf{X}_{t}
, it is straightforward to construct an unbiased estimate
\ell_{t}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
of

\mathsf{F}(\mathsf{p},\mathsf{p}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}\sim\mathsf{p}}\left\|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\log\mathsf{p}(\mathbf{X})\right\|^{2}\quad\text{or}\quad\mathsf{F}(\mathsf{q},\mathsf{p}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}\sim\mathsf{q}}\left\|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\log\mathsf{q}(\mathbf{X})\right\|^{2}

using Green’s first identity (see Section
3
for the details). This estimate is then used for construction of a loss function on the
t
-th round. As long as
t\leqslant\tau^{*}
the problem of online prediction can be easily solved using, for instance, the exponentially weighted average forecaster with Gaussian prior (see Section
2.1
). This algorithm will have a small regret compared to the best static expert
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1:\tau^{*}}^{\circ}
. However, when
t>\tau^{*}
, the best expert
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\tau^{*}+1:T}^{\circ}
on the segment
[\tau^{*}+1,T]
will differ from
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1:\tau^{*}}^{\circ}
. This suggests us
to use online forecasters, which are able to compete with combinations of experts. For this reason, we develop a version of the fixed share algorithm
(
Herbster and Warmuth, 1998
)
for infinite number of experts and construct the test statistic
\widehat{S}_{t}=\widehat{L}_{1:t}^{EW}-\widehat{L}_{1:t}^{FS}
, where
\widehat{L}_{1:t}^{EW}
and
\widehat{L}_{1:t}^{FS}
stand for cumulative losses of the exponentially weighted average and the fixed share forecasters, respectively. If
t\leqslant\tau^{*}
, then the standard exponential weighting performs nearly as well as the fixed share predictor and
\widehat{S}_{t}
stays small. The situation changes when
t
starts exceeding
\tau^{*}
. In this case
\widehat{S}_{t}
rapidly grows, because of the ability of fixed share to adapt to changes in the underlying distribution instantly. We would like to mention that similar ideas were used in
(
Gokcesu and Kozat, 2018
;
Yamanishi and Fukushima, 2018
)
in the context of anomaly detection.
Gokcesu and Kozat (2018)
use the idea of tracking the best expert and derives bounds on the dynamic regret. However, the algorithm of
Gokcesu and Kozat (2018)
has the same limitations as the one of
Cao et al. (2018)
and is applicable to parametric setups only. Besides,
Gokcesu and Kozat (2018)
does not discuss how the regret bounds are related to type-I and type-II errors of their procedure. In
(
Yamanishi and Fukushima, 2018
)
, the authors consider possible changes in the reference class
{\mathcal{P}}
rather than in switching of the best static predictor.

Contribution.
The main contribution of the paper can be summarized as follows.

•

We propose an algorithm for nonparametric online change point detection. It is based on a version of the fixed share forecaster tailored for the problem of online prediction with expert advice with infinite number of static experts and quadratic losses
\ell_{t}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
of the form

\ell_{t}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\frac{1}{2}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}A_{t}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\mathbf{b}_{t}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\quad\text{where $A_{t}\succeq O_{d}$, $\mathbf{b}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ for all $t\in\{1,\dots,T\}$}.

(1)

•

We carry out theoretical study of the algorithm and derive non-asymptotic high-probability bounds on its test statistic in the pre-change and post-change regimes under realistic assumptions.

•

We illustrate algorithm’s performance with numerical experiments on both artificial and real-world data sets.

Paper structure.
The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we briefly introduce the problem of online prediction with expert advice, discuss the exponentially weighted average forecaster and present a version of the fixed share algorithm. In Section
3
, we suggest an online change-point detection algorithm and elaborate on its theoretical properties. We also illustrate its performance with numerical experiments in Section
4
. Proofs of the theoretical results and additional numerical experiments are moved to Appendix.

2
Online Learning with Quadratic Loss

In the problem of prediction with expert advice, a learner aims to predict a sequence of outcomes based on a finite or infinite set
\Theta
of reference forecasts. In the present paper, we focus on a bit more specific setup than described in the book
(
Cesa-Bianchi and Lugosi, 2006
, Section 2)
. To be more precise, we assume that
\Theta=\mathbb{R}^{d}
for some
d\in\mathbb{N}
, that is, each expert is indexed with a
d
-dimensional vector. Moreover, we suppose that, for any
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the expert
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
makes a prediction
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
on each round. In other words, we deal with the case of constant experts. We would like to emphasize that the choice
\Theta=\mathbb{R}^{d}
allows us to handle scenarios of abrupt changes, when the densities
\mathsf{p}
and
\mathsf{q}
(and, hence, their corresponding approximations from the parametric class
{\mathcal{P}}
) are very different.
The game proceeds as follows. An adversary generates loss functions
\ell_{t}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
1\leqslant t\leqslant T
, but does not reveal them to the learner. On a round
t\in\{1,\dots,T\}
, the forecaster makes his prediction
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
based on experts’ performance on the previous rounds
1,\dots,t-1
. After that, the adversary discloses
\ell_{t}
and the learner suffers the loss
\ell_{t}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})
. This is the so-called
oblivious opponent
setup, which is usual for analysis of the exponential weighting and fixed share algorithms. The performance after
T
rounds is measured by the excess cumulative loss compared to the best expert, also referred to as
regret
:

R_{1:T}=\widehat{L}_{1:T}-\inf\limits_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}L_{1:T}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{t}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})-\inf\limits_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{t}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}).

(4)

In what follows, we assume that, for each
t\in\{1,\dots,T\}
, the loss function
\ell_{t}
is quadratic (
1
).

2.1
Exponentially Weighted Average Forecaster

Exponential weighting is one of the most popular strategies for prediction of individual sequences. Let
\pi
be a prior distribution over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let us recall that, for any
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
t\in\{1,\dots,T\}
, we denote the cumulative loss of the expert
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
after
t
rounds by

L_{1:t}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{t}\ell_{j}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}).

We also adopt the notation
L_{1:0}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})=0
for any
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Given a non-increasing sequence
\{\eta_{t}:1\leqslant t\leqslant T\}
of positive numbers, on the round
t
, the exponentially weighted forecaster makes a prediction
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{}_{t}^{EW}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1:t-1}(\eta_{t})
with
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1:t-1}(\eta_{t})
given by (
2
).
In other words,
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{}_{t}^{EW}
is the mean with respect to the posterior measure

\frac{e^{-\eta_{t}L_{1:t-1}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}\pi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{Z_{1:t-1}(\eta_{t})}.

(5)

When the loss functions
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{T}
are quadratic, the standard choice for the prior
\pi
is the Gaussian distribution
{\mathcal{N}}(\mathbf{0},\lambda^{-1}I_{d})
. The reason is that the exponentiallly weighted forecast
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{}_{t}^{EW}
can be computed explicitly. We provide the corresponding lemma below.

Lemma 2.1
.

Assume that the loss function
\ell_{t}
is of the form (
1
) for any
t\in\{1,\dots,T\}
. For any
\eta>0
, let
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{s:t}(\eta)
be the exponentially weighted average (
2
) with the Gaussian prior

\pi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\left(\frac{\lambda}{2\pi}\right)^{d/2}e^{-\lambda\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|^{2}/2}.

(6)

Then it holds that

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{s:t}(\eta)=\left(\sum\limits_{j=s}^{t}A_{j}+\frac{\lambda}{\eta}I_{d}\right)^{-1}\sum\limits_{j=s}^{t}\mathbf{b}_{j}.

(7)

The proof of Lemma
2.1
is straightforward. However, we provide it in Section
B.1
to make the paper self-contained. The expression (
7
) helps to formulate the exponential weighting procedure in a simple and closed form.

2.2
Fixed Share Forecaster

In the standard online learning framework, the performance of a forecaster is measured in terms of the regret (
4
) with respect to the best expert. In
(
Auer and Warmuth, 1998
;
Herbster and Warmuth, 1998
)
, the authors compared the cumulative loss
\widehat{L}_{1:T}
of the forecaster with the one of a combination of experts. For any
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{T})
stand for a
compound expert
, that is, a forecaster, which follows the prediction of the expert
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}
on the round
t
. It is straightforward to observe that the cumulative loss of the compound expert
({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{T})
after
t
rounds is equal to

{\mathcal{L}}_{t}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{T})=\sum\limits_{s=1}^{t}\ell_{s}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{s}).

Of course, it would be unfair to compare the forecaster with all compound experts. For this reason,
Auer and Warmuth (1998)
and
Herbster and Warmuth (1998)
introduced the notion of size of a compound expert defined as

\mathrm{size}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{T})=\sum\limits_{t=2}^{T}\mathbbm{1}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}\neq{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t-1})

and restricted their attention on
dynamic
(also called switching or shifting) regret with respect to the best compound expert of size at most
m
:

{\mathcal{R}}_{1:T}=\widehat{L}_{1:T}-\inf\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{T}):\\
\mathrm{size}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{T})\leqslant m\end{subarray}}{\mathcal{L}}_{T}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{T}).

Here
m\leqslant T
is a predefined parameter. Obviously, if
m=0
, then the switching regret
{\mathcal{R}}_{1:T}
coincides with
R_{1:T}
. The ambitious problem of tracking the best expert gained much attention in the last three decades starting from the pioneering works of
Willems (1996)
;
Willems and Krom (1997)
;
Herbster and Warmuth (1998)
;
Shamir and Merhav (1999)
;
Vovk (1999)
. While
Willems (1996)
;
Willems and Krom (1997)
;
Shamir and Merhav (1999)
were bothered with a particular prediction problem with logarithmic loss,
Herbster and Warmuth (1998)
designed a universal algorithm predicting nearly as well as the best compound expert of size
m
under quite general assumptions on the loss function. They suggested the fixed share forecaster, which is nothing but the exponential weighting in the space of all compound experts with a special prior. Their idea was simple and elegant and helped to overcome computational issues arising during the brute force implementation of the exponentially weighted averaging, which requires a lot of resources even in the case of finite set of experts (see, for instance,
Cesa-Bianchi and Lugosi
,
2006
, Section 5.2
). The main advantage of the fixed share is that, despite the aggregation of all possible compound experts, it remains computationally efficient due to the apposite prior. The algorithm became very popular with the research community, and its properties (of both randomized and deterministic versions) were then intensively studied in numerous subsequent works (for example,
Vovk, 1999
;
Bousquet and Warmuth, 2002
;
György et al., 2005
;
György et al., 2008
;
Cesa-Bianchi et al., 2012
;
György et al., 2012
;
Adamskiy et al., 2016
to name a few). In
(
Bousquet and Warmuth, 2002
;
Adamskiy et al., 2016
)
, the authors went further and examined general schemes for construction of online learning algorithms with low switching regret. Unfortunately, the algorithm of
Herbster and Warmuth (1998)
(as well as many follow-up papers) deals with the case of finite number of experts.
One of the ways to overcome this limit is to consider setups with a growing pool of experts. For instance, in
(
Hazan and Seshadhri, 2007
;
Hazan and Seshadhri, 2009
;
Mourtada and Maillard, 2017
)
, the authors propose meta-algorithms for aggregation of an increasing number of predictions at each step while maintaining strong dynamic regret bounds. These methods can also be adapted for construction of change point detection procedures in the same spirit as we describe in Section
3
. In our approach, however, we use a slightly different strategy. We develop a version of the fixed share forecaster tailored for the specific setup to ensure that its cumulative loss is as close as possible to that of the exponentially weighted average forecaster in the pre-change regime. The advantage of this strategy becomes evident in Section
4
, where our algorithm outperforms a procedure based on the Follow-the-Leading-History (FLH) meta-algorithm
(
Hazan and Seshadhri, 2007
, Section 3)
.

2.2.1
Fixed Share Forecaster for the Infinite Class of Experts

Following the general idea of
Herbster and Warmuth (1998)
, we define the fixed share forecaster as exponentially weighted predictor with a special prior
\rho
over the set of compound experts
({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{T})
. We suggest using

\rho({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{T})=\pi({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1})\prod\limits_{t=2}^{T}\mathsf{f}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t-1}),\;\text{where}\;\mathsf{f}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t-1})=\alpha\pi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})+(1-\alpha)\delta({\boldsymbol{\theta}}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t-1}).

(8)

Here and further in the paper,
\delta
denotes the Dirac delta function. As before,
\pi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
stands for the probability density function of the Gaussian distribution
{\mathcal{N}}(\mathbf{0},\lambda^{-1}I_{d})
,
\lambda>0
, as defined in (
6
).
Given a non-increasing sequence
\{\eta_{t}:1\leqslant t\leqslant T\}
of positive numbers, the fixed share forecaster makes a prediction
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{}_{t}^{FS}=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}(\eta_{t})
on the round
t
, where, for any
\eta>0
and
t\in\{1,\dots,T\}
,

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}(\eta)=\frac{\int{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}e^{-\eta{\mathcal{L}}_{t-1}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{T})}\rho({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{T})\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1}\dots\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{T}}{\int e^{-\eta{\mathcal{L}}_{t-1}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{T})}\rho({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{T})\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1}\dots\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{T}}.

(9)

Straightforward computation of the integrals in (
9
) requires a lot of efforts even in the one-dimensional case. Fortunately, there is a much simpler way to calculate
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}(\eta)
in a recursive manner.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let us fix any
\eta>0
. For any
s,t\in\{1,\dots,T\}
,
s\leqslant t
, let
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{s:t}(\eta)
and
Z_{s:t}(\eta)
be as defined in (
2
). Then
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1}(\eta)=\mathbf{0}
,
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{2}(\eta)=(1-\alpha)\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1:1}(\eta)
, and for any
3\leqslant t\leqslant T
the forecast
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}(\eta)
obeys the recurrent relation

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}(\eta)=\frac{1-\alpha}{V_{t-1}(\eta)}

\displaystyle\Bigg((1-\alpha)^{t-2}\,Z_{1:t-1}(\eta)\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1:t-1}(\eta)

\displaystyle\quad+\alpha\sum\limits_{s=0}^{t-3}(1-\alpha)^{s}\,V_{t-2-s}(\eta)\,Z_{t-1-s:t-1}(\eta)\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t-1-s:t-1}(\eta)\Bigg),

(10)

where
V_{1}(\eta)=Z_{1:1}(\eta)
and

V_{t}(\eta)=(1-\alpha)^{t-1}\,Z_{1:t}(\eta)+\alpha\sum\limits_{s=0}^{t-2}(1-\alpha)^{s}\,V_{t-1-s}(\eta)\,Z_{t-s:t}(\eta)\quad\text{for any $2\leqslant t\leqslant T$.}

(11)

The proof of Lemma
2.2
is moved to Section
B.2
. The expressions (
11
) and (
10
) resemble of the result of
György et al. (2008)
(Lemma 1, see also
Cesa-Bianchi and Lugosi
,
2006
, Lemma 5.4
). Finally, for any
s,t\in\{1,\dots,T\}
,
s\leqslant t
,
Z_{s:t}(\eta)
can be computed explicitly.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let us fix any positive numbers
\eta
and
\lambda
. For any
s,t\in\{1,\dots,T\}
,
s\leqslant t
, let
Z_{s:t}(\eta)
be as defined in (
2
) with the prior
\pi
, given by (
6
). Then it holds that

3
Application to Change Point Detection

The exponentially weighted average and the fixed share forecasters can be applied to online change point detection.
Let us elaborate on the idea briefly described in the introduction. Let us recall that we observe a sequence of independent random vectors
\mathbf{X}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{X}_{T}
such that
\mathbf{X}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{X}_{\tau^{*}}\sim\mathsf{p}
and
\mathbf{X}_{\tau^{*}+1},\dots,\mathbf{X}_{T}\sim\mathsf{q}
, where
\mathsf{p}
and
\mathsf{q}
are unknown probability densities on a Euclidean space
{\mathcal{X}}
. We approximate
\mathsf{p}
and
\mathsf{q}
using a parametric reference class of densities
{\mathcal{P}}=\{\mathsf{p}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}:{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
with
\mathsf{p}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
of the form

\log\mathsf{p}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\textbf{{x}})=\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\textbf{{x}})^{\top}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\Phi({\boldsymbol{\theta}}),\quad\text{where}\quad\Phi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\log\int\limits_{{\mathcal{X}}}e^{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\textbf{{x}})}\,\mathrm{d}\textbf{{x}}

and
\boldsymbol{\Psi}:\textbf{{x}}\mapsto\big(\psi_{1}(\textbf{{x}}),\dots,\psi_{d}(\textbf{{x}})\big)^{\top}
is a fixed mapping. We consider the problem of change point detection through the lens of online learning. To avoid time-consuming computations of
\Phi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
, we measure the closeness of
\mathsf{p}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
to the target densities
\mathsf{p}
and
\mathsf{q}
with the Fisher divergence defined as

\displaystyle\mathsf{F}(\mathsf{p},\mathsf{p}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int\limits_{{\mathcal{X}}}\left\|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\log\mathsf{p}(\textbf{{x}})-\boldsymbol{\nabla}\log\mathsf{p}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\textbf{{x}})\right\|^{2}\mathsf{p}(\textbf{{x}})\,\mathrm{d}\textbf{{x}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}\sim\mathsf{p}}\left\|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\log\mathsf{p}(\mathbf{X})-\nabla\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\mathbf{X})^{\top}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right\|^{2}.

Using Green’s first identity, we can rewrite the right-hand side in the following form:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}\sim\mathsf{p}}\left\|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\log\mathsf{p}(\mathbf{X})-\nabla\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\mathbf{X})^{\top}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right\|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}\sim\mathsf{p}}\left(\left\|\nabla\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\mathbf{X})^{\top}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right\|^{2}+2\Delta\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\mathbf{X})^{\top}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}+\left\|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\log\mathsf{p}(\mathbf{X})\right\|^{2}\right).

Similarly, it holds that

\mathsf{F}(\mathsf{q},\mathsf{p}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}\sim\mathsf{q}}\left\|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\log\mathsf{q}(\mathbf{X})\right\|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}\sim\mathsf{q}}\left(\left\|\nabla\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\mathbf{X})^{\top}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right\|^{2}+2\Delta\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\mathbf{X})^{\top}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right).

For any
t\in\{1,\dots,T\}
, let us consider

\ell_{t}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\frac{1}{2}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}A_{t}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\mathbf{b}_{t}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\quad\text{where}\quad A_{t}=\nabla\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\mathbf{X}_{t})\nabla\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\mathbf{X}_{t})^{\top}+\gamma I_{d}\text{ and }\mathbf{b}_{t}=-\Delta\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\mathbf{X}_{t}).

(13)

Here
\Delta
stands for the Laplacian operator applied to the vector-valued function
\boldsymbol{\Psi}
in a componentwise fashion. Regardless the distribution of
\mathbf{X}_{t}
, the loss
\ell_{t}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
is an unbiased estimate of

\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}_{t}}\left(\left\|\nabla\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\mathbf{X}_{t})^{\top}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right\|^{2}+2\Delta\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\mathbf{X}_{t})^{\top}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right).

We play the game of online prediction with expert advice with the loss functions (
13
) and compare the cumulative losses suffered by the exponentially weighted average and the fixed share forecasters. If
t\leqslant\tau^{*}
, then
\widehat{L}_{1:t}^{EW}
and
\widehat{L}_{1:t}^{FS}
will not differ too much. However, the situation dramatically changes when
t
exceeds
\tau^{*}
. While the exponentially weighted average sticks to the expert
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1:\tau^{*}}^{\circ}\in\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}L_{1:\tau^{*}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
successfully performed during the first
\tau^{*}
rounds, the fixed share algorithm rapidly switches to a new expert with a small post-change cumulative loss
L_{\tau^{*}+1:t}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
. For this reason, the difference between
\widehat{L}_{1:t}^{EW}
and
\widehat{L}_{1:t}^{FS}
starts growing right after
\tau^{*}
. When it exceeds a predefined threshold, we report a change point occurrence.
We provide a pseudocode of the described online change point detection algorithm below.

\eta_{1}\geqslant\dots\geqslant\eta_{T}

operations (vector sums), which is dominated by the

\ell_{t}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{}_{t}^{EW})

operations per
t

Numerical Experiments

We conduct an empirical study to evaluate the performance of Algorithm
3.1
. Computational details concerning the exponentially weighted average forecaster and the fixed share forecaster, as well as threshold tuning procedure and the experiments on artificially generated Gaussian sequences and two real-world time series are presented in Appendix

.
In this section we discuss the change point detection procedures chosen for comparison and demonstrate the results on a human activity detection data set.

For Algorithm
3.1
during all of the trials we use
\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\textbf{{x}})=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d},x_{1}^{2},\dots,x_{d}^{2})^{\top}
. Concerning the parameter tuning, the regularization constants
\lambda>0,\gamma>0
, the shifting constant
\alpha\in[0,1]
and the learning rate
\eta_{t}
are chosen empirically. To evaluate the procedure as objectively as possible, we control the rate of false alarms, the number of undetected change points and track the average detection delay.

We compare Algorithm
3.1
with four non-parametric online change point detection procedures. The first one is based on the Follow-the-Leading-History (FLH) approach presented in
Hazan and Seshadhri (2007, Section 3)
. As discussed before, FLH aggregates a growing set of online prediction algorithms, seen as experts, and tracks the best one using a scheme adapted from
Herbster and Warmuth (1998)
. In the experiments we consider Algorithm
3.1
, where we calculate the FLH prediction instead of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{}_{t}^{FS}
and denote this competitor as FLH. Hyperparameters
\lambda,\gamma,\eta
and
\alpha
throughout the experimental study were tuned independently for Algorithm
3.1
and FLH. We would like to emphasize that
Hazan and Seshadhri
have developed an advanced implementation of FLH, which does not take into account the predictions of all experts, instead operating on subsets of diverse experts where members within each subset are similar. However, to ensure a fair comparison with our approach based on computational effort, we consider the basic method to be more appropriate.

This study also considers other several powerful methods. The Kullback–Leibler importance estimation procedure (KLIEP), proposed in
Sugiyama et al. (2008)
, focuses on direct importance estimation, that is, the ratio of the pre- and post-change density functions, without explicitly estimating neither of them. The method uses Gaussian kernels as basis functions for modeling the importance. Another non-parametric approach, covered in
Li et al. (2015)
,
uses a kernel-based estimate of the squared maximum mean discrepancy between the pre- and post-change distributions as the test statistic for online change point detection. Both KLIEP and the kernel change point detector with M-statistic require maintaining a sliding window to test each timestamp as a potential change point. Too large or too small window size may lead to a longer detection delay, as the performance of the algorithms strongly depends on the amount of data being processed. Both the kernel bandwidth and window size are adjustable hyperparameters. Additionally, we compare Algorithm
3.1
to the fast change point detection procedure based on contrastive approach (FALCON), described in
Goldman et al. (2023)
. The main idea behind the method is to maximize the cross-entropy between the pre- and post-change distributions using a discriminator function that depends on the log ratio of the corresponding densities. As we mentioned earlier, FALCON uses ideas from online convex optimization to reduce computational complexity.

The experiments conducted on Gaussian sequences were designed to assess the effectiveness of the procedure in identifying alterations in the mean and variance of the data distribution. To evaluate the performance of the method thoroughly, we implemented and tested several distinct experimental setups. They enabled us to validate the procedure and fine-tune its parameters, thereby enhancing our understanding of its capabilities and limitations in detecting changes in statistical properties.
Settings of the experiments are listed below.

Example 1: mean shift detection in a univariate sequence.
We generated a series of
T=300
i.i.d. observations of Gaussian random variable. First
\tau=150
samples were drawn from
{\mathcal{N}}(0,\sigma^{2})
with
\sigma=0.2
, the rest were derived from the same distribution, shifted in the expectation by 2 standard deviations:
{\mathcal{N}}(\mu,\sigma^{2}),\;\mu=2\sigma
.

Example 2: variance shift detection in a univariate sequence.
The sequence of i.i.d. observations of length
T=300
came from Gaussian distribution. The first
150
samples were from
{\mathcal{N}}(0,\sigma_{1})
with
\sigma_{1}=0.1
and the last
150
from the scaled distribution
{\mathcal{N}}(0,\sigma_{2}),\;\sigma_{2}=0.3
.

Example 3: mean shift detection in a multivariate sequence.
With data dimension set to
d=3
, we took
T=300
i.i.d. observations of Gaussian vector with non-correlated components. Half of the series was taken from
{\mathcal{N}}(\mathbf{0},\Sigma)
with
\Sigma=\text{diag}(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3})
,
\sigma_{1}=0.1
,
\sigma_{2}=0.2
,
\sigma_{3}=0.3
, the rest were drawn from the shifted in mean distribution
{\mathcal{N}}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\Sigma)
,
\boldsymbol{\mu}=3(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3})^{\top}
.

Example 4: variance shift detection in a multivariate sequence.
As before, the data consists of
300
i.i.d. observations of 3d Gaussian vector with non-correlated components. First
\tau=150
values were drawn from
{\mathcal{N}}(\mathbf{0},\Sigma)
with
\Sigma=\text{diag}(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3})
,
\sigma_{1}=0.1
,
\sigma_{2}=0.2
,
\sigma_{3}=0.3
, the rest were taken
from the same distribution only with standard deviation scaled by 3 component-wise:
{\mathcal{N}}(\mathbf{0},9\Sigma)
.

For our artificial experiments we used a threshold tuning strategy as described in
[
Puchkin and Shcherbakova, 2023
]
. We generated
T=150
i.i.d. samples from the corresponding initial distribution J times and ran the algorithm on each sequence to
obtain the threshold value
\mathfrak{z}=\underset{1\leqslant j\leqslant J}{\text{max}}\underset{1\leqslant t\leqslant T}{\text{max}}\widehat{S}_{t}^{(j)}
. This approach ensures that the running length of Algorithm
3.1
does not exceed
T=150
. If we run the procedure in a stationary regime and obtain the corresponding test statistics
\{\widehat{S}_{t}\}^{T}_{t=1}
, the choice of
\mathfrak{z}
guarantees

\mathbb{P}\left(\underset{1\leqslant t\leqslant T}{\text{max}}\widehat{S}_{t}>\mathfrak{z}\right)=\frac{1}{J+1},

provided that there are no change points.

For each example we present thresholds and the values of hyperparameters for the competing algorithms in Table
2
.
The hyperparameters were tuned to keep the false alarm rate at zero while still detecting all of the change points as quickly as possible.

A.4
Room Occupancy Detection

We applied Algorithm
3.1
to detect changes in the room occupancy based on temperature, humidity, light, and the
CO_{2}
level. A four-dimensional time series was obtained from the UCI repository
[
Bache and Lichman, 2013
]
. The data was preprocessed in three sequential steps. First, we selected every 16th observation to reduce the length of the time series. Next, to convert a non-stationary series into a stationary one, we took the first-order difference and normalized the series by the last of the adjacent values. Additionally, we log-transformed the sequence and scaled the coordinates using the stationary parts of the time series to ensure a similar range of the data. Ultimately, the time series comprised approximately 500 data points, nine of which were labeled as change points. The change point annotation description can be found in
[
van den Burg and Williams, 2022
, Section Annotation Collection]
. The time series is displayed in Figure
4
.

Figure 4:
Room occupancy data set after preprocessing. The dotted lines correspond to the annotated change points. Validation and test parts of the series are depicted in darker and lighter colors respectively.

The algorithm application pipeline differed from the preceding experiments.
Since there were several manual annotations of the change points, it is possible that some of them might not have been detected accurately, leading to incorrect change point identification in the methods’ performance. To avoid false alarms caused by inaccurate annotations, we have introduced the parameter
\text{min\_diff}=10
. This parameter controls the distance between a detected change point and an annotated one. A false alarm is not triggered if a change point is detected before the annotated one but the distance is less than min_diff. In this case, we assume that the annotated change point was detected with zero delay.

Proofs of the results from Section

B.1
Proof of Lemma
2.1

It holds that

\displaystyle e^{-\eta L_{s:t}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}\pi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})

\displaystyle\;\propto\;\exp\left\{-\frac{\eta}{2}\sum\limits_{j=s}^{t}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}A_{j}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\mathbf{b}_{j}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{\theta}})-\frac{\lambda\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|^{2}}{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{-\frac{\eta}{2}\;{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}\left(\sum\limits_{j=s}^{t}A_{j}+\frac{\lambda}{\eta}I_{d}\right){\boldsymbol{\theta}}+{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}\left(\sum\limits_{j=s}^{t}\mathbf{b}_{j}\right)\right\}.

Using the representation

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}\left(\sum\limits_{j=s}^{t}A_{j}+\frac{\lambda}{\eta}I_{d}\right){\boldsymbol{\theta}}-2{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}\left(\sum\limits_{j=s}^{t}\mathbf{b}_{j}\right)

\displaystyle=\left\|\left(\sum\limits_{j=s}^{t}A_{j}+\frac{\lambda}{\eta}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\left(\sum\limits_{j=s}^{t}A_{j}+\frac{\lambda}{\eta}I_{d}\right)^{-1/2}\sum\limits_{j=s}^{t}\mathbf{b}_{j}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad-\left\|\left(\sum\limits_{j=s}^{t}A_{j}+\frac{\lambda}{\eta}I_{d}\right)^{-1/2}\sum\limits_{j=s}^{t}\mathbf{b}_{j}\right\|^{2},

we obtain that

\displaystyle e^{-\eta L_{s:t}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}\pi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})

\displaystyle\propto\exp\left\{-\frac{\eta}{2}\;\left\|\left(\sum\limits_{j=s}^{t}A_{j}+\frac{\lambda}{\eta}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\left(\sum\limits_{j=s}^{t}A_{j}+\frac{\lambda}{\eta}I_{d}\right)^{-1/2}\sum\limits_{j=s}^{t}\mathbf{b}_{j}\right\|^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle\propto\exp\left\{-\frac{\eta}{2}\;\left\|\left(\sum\limits_{j=s}^{t}A_{j}+\frac{\lambda}{\eta}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}\left[{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\left(\sum\limits_{j=s}^{t}A_{j}+\frac{\lambda}{\eta}I_{d}\right)^{-1}\sum\limits_{j=s}^{t}\mathbf{b}_{j}\right]\right\|^{2}\right\}.

Hence, the posterior measure

\frac{1}{Z_{s:t}}e^{-\eta L_{s:t}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}\pi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})

coincides with

{\mathcal{N}}\left(\left(\sum\limits_{j=s}^{t}A_{j}+\frac{\lambda}{\eta}I_{d}\right)^{-1}\sum\limits_{j=s}^{t}\mathbf{b}_{j},\frac{1}{\eta}\left(\sum\limits_{j=s}^{t}A_{j}+\frac{\lambda}{\eta}I_{d}\right)^{-1}\right),

and the prediction of the exponentially weighted forecaster is equal to

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}=\left(\sum\limits_{j=s}^{t}A_{j}+\frac{\lambda}{\eta}I_{d}\right)^{-1}\sum\limits_{j=s}^{t}\mathbf{b}_{j}.

\blacksquare

B.2
Proof of Lemma
2.2

Step 1: definition of
V_{t}(\eta)
and preliminaries.

Before we move to the proof of (
10
) and (
11
), let us elaborate on the sequence
V_{1}(\eta),\dots,V_{T}(\eta)
.
For any
t\in\mathbb{N}
, we define
V_{t}(\eta)
through a weight function
w_{t-1}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},\eta)
:

V_{t}(\eta)=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}v_{t}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},\eta)\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\quad\text{where}\quad v_{t}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},\eta)=w_{t-1}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},\eta)e^{-\eta\ell_{t}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}.

(20)

In its turn, the weight function is given by

w_{0}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},\eta)=\pi({\boldsymbol{\theta}}),\quad w_{t}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},\eta)=(1-\alpha)v_{t}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},\eta)+\alpha\pi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})V_{t}(\eta),\quad t\in\{1,\dots,T\}.

(21)

The function
w_{t-1}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},\eta)
plays a key role in the proof of Lemma
2.2
, because of the following identity.

Proposition B.1
.

For any
t\in\{1,\dots,T\}
and any
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\int\exp\big\{-\eta{\mathcal{L}}_{t-1}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t-1},{\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t+1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{T})\big\}

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\rho({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t-1},{\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t+1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{T})\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1}\dots\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t-1}\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t+1}\dots\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{T}=w_{t-1}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},\eta).

The proof of Proposition
B.1
is deferred to Section
B.4
. It is similar to
Cesa-Bianchi and Lugosi [2006, Theorem 5.1]
and allows us to simplify the formula (
9
) significantly. Indeed, the proposition yields that

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}(\eta)=\frac{1}{W_{t-1}(\eta)}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}w_{t-1}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t},\eta)\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t},\quad\text{where}\quad W_{t-1}(\eta)=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}w_{t-1}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t},\eta)\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}.

(22)

The last expression can be simplified even further. Since
w_{t}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},\eta)=(1-\alpha)v_{t}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},\eta)+\alpha\pi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})V_{t}(\eta)
, we have

\displaystyle W_{t-1}(\eta)=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}w_{t-1}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},\eta)\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha)\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}v_{t-1}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},\eta)\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}+\alpha V_{t-1}(\eta)\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\pi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha)V_{t-1}(\eta)+\alpha V_{t-1}(\eta)=V_{t-1}(\eta)

(23)

and

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}w_{t-1}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},\eta)\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha)\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}v_{t-1}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},\eta)\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}+\alpha V_{t-1}(\eta)\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\pi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha)\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}v_{t-1}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},\eta)\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}.

Hence, it holds that

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}(\eta)=\frac{1-\alpha}{V_{t-1}(\eta)}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}v_{t-1}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},\eta)\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\quad\text{for any $t\geqslant 2$.}

(24)

Step 2: recurrence relation for
V_{t}(\eta)
.
The proof relies on (
21
) and (
20
), yielding the recurrence relation

v_{t}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},\eta)=(1-\alpha)v_{t-1}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},\eta)+\alpha V_{t-1}(\eta)\pi({\boldsymbol{\theta}}).

(25)

Applying (
25
), we obtain that

\displaystyle V_{t}(\eta)=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}v_{t}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},\eta)\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big[(1-\alpha)v_{t-1}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},\eta)+\alpha V_{t-1}(\eta)\pi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\big]e^{-\eta\ell_{t}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}

\displaystyle=\alpha V_{t-1}(\eta)\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-\eta\ell_{t}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}\pi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}+(1-\alpha)\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}v_{t-1}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},\eta)e^{-\eta\ell_{t}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}\pi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}

\displaystyle=\alpha V_{t-1}(\eta)Z_{t:t}(\eta)+(1-\alpha)\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}v_{t-1}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},\eta)e^{-\eta\ell_{t}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}\pi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\theta}},

where the last line follows from the definition of
Z_{t:t}(\eta)
(see eq.
2
).
Similarly, it holds that

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}v_{t-1}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},\eta)e^{-\eta\ell_{t}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}\pi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big[(1-\alpha)v_{t-2}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},\eta)+\alpha V_{t-2}(\eta)\pi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\big]e^{-\eta\ell_{t}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})-\eta\ell_{t-1}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}

\displaystyle=\alpha V_{t-2}(\eta)Z_{t-1:t}(\eta)+(1-\alpha)\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}v_{t-2}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},\eta)e^{-\eta\ell_{t}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})-\eta\ell_{t-1}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}\pi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\theta}},

and, hence,

\displaystyle V_{t}(\eta)

\displaystyle=\alpha V_{t-1}(\eta)Z_{t:t}(\eta)+\alpha(1-\alpha)V_{t-2}(\eta)Z_{t-1:t}(\eta)

\displaystyle\quad+(1-\alpha)^{2}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}v_{t-2}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},\eta)e^{-\eta\ell_{t}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})-\eta\ell_{t-1}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}\pi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}.

Repeating the same argument
(t-2)
times, we get that

V_{t}(\eta)=(1-\alpha)^{t-1}\,Z_{1:t}(\eta)+\alpha\sum\limits_{s=0}^{t-2}(1-\alpha)^{s}\,V_{t-1-s}(\eta)\,Z_{t-s:t}(\eta).

Thus, it only remains to prove (
10
).

Step 2: recurrence relation for
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}(\eta)
.
Let us introduce

\mathbf{Y}_{s:t}(\eta)=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}e^{-\eta L_{s:t}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}\pi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\quad 1\leqslant s\leqslant t\leqslant T.

(26)

Note that, due to (
2
),
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{s:t}(\eta)=\mathbf{Y}_{s:t}(\eta)/Z_{s:t}(\eta)
for any
1\leqslant s\leqslant t\leqslant T
.
The equalities
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1}(\eta)=\mathbf{0}
and

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{2}(\eta)=(1-\alpha)\mathbf{Y}_{1:1}(\eta)/V_{1}(\eta)=(1-\alpha)\mathbf{Y}_{1:1}(\eta)/Z_{1:1}(\eta)=(1-\alpha)\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1:1}(\eta)

follow directly from (
22
), (
24
), and (
11
). Hence, to finish the proof, it is enough to show that, for any
t\geqslant 3
,

\sum\limits_{t=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}[\xi^{2}_{t}\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{t-1}]\leqslant 2B\left(3eR^{2}\log(8B)+4B\right)\sum\limits_{t=1}^{s}\left\|\overline{A}_{t}^{1/2}(\widecheck{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{t}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}^{\star})\right\|^{2}.

\displaystyle\max_{1\leqslant t\leqslant s}\left\|\xi_{t}\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{t-1}\right\|_{\psi_{1}}\leqslant 4BR(1+R).

claiming that with probability at least

Note that, since
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}^{\star}
minimizes the expected loss
\overline{\ell}_{t}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})={\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}\overline{A}_{t}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}/2-\mathbf{b}_{t}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}

Using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we obtain that

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{t=1}^{s}\xi_{t}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\upsigma^{2}}{8B}\left(3eR^{2}\log(8B)+4B\right)^{-1}+4B\left(3eR^{2}\log(8B)+4B\right)\log(1/\delta)

\displaystyle\quad+12BRz(1+R)\log(1/\delta)

\displaystyle\quad+12BRz(1+R)\log(1/\delta)

Lemma C.2
.

Under Assumption
3.2
, for any
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\ell_{t}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})-\ell_{t}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}^{\star})\right)^{2}\leqslant B\left\|\overline{A}_{t}^{1/2}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}^{\star})\right\|^{2}\left(3e\left\|\overline{A}_{t}^{1/2}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}+{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}^{\star})\right\|^{2}\log(8B)+8B\right).

Proof.

According to the definition of
\ell_{t}
and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\ell_{t}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})-\ell_{t}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}^{\star})\right)^{2}\leqslant\mathbb{E}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}A_{t}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}^{\star\top}A_{t}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}^{\star})^{2}+2\mathbb{E}\left(\mathbf{b}_{t}^{\top}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}^{\star})\right)^{2}.

In the rest of the proof, we are going show that

\mathbb{E}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}A_{t}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}^{\star\top}A_{t}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}^{\star})^{2}\leqslant 3eB\log(8B)\left\|\overline{A}_{t}^{1/2}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}+{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}^{\star})\right\|^{2}\left\|\overline{A}_{t}^{1/2}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}^{\star})\right\|^{2}

(34)

and

(35)

Step 1: bound on
\mathbb{E}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}A_{t}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}^{\star\top}A_{t}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}^{\star})^{2}
.

We start with the proof of (
34
).
Let us fix an arbitrary
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\displaystyle\left({\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}A_{t}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}^{\star\top}A_{t}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}^{\star}\right)^{2}\leqslant\left\|A_{t}^{1/2}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}+{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}^{\star})\right\|^{2}\left\|A_{t}^{1/2}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}^{\star})\right\|^{2}

(37)

E.6

3.2

and (
34
) follows.

Step 2: bound on
\mathbb{E}\big(\mathbf{b}_{t}^{\top}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}^{\star})\big)^{2}
.

It remains to check (
35
). Its proof is straightforward. Recalling the definition of
v
(see eq.
36
) and applying Lemma
E.4
, we obtain that

3.2

\smash{4B^{2}\left\|\overline{A}_{t}^{1/2}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}^{\star})\right\|^{2}}
. Hence, (
35
) holds. Taking together (
34
) and (
35

.
Recalling the definition of the exponentially weighed average forecaster (see eq.
7
) and using the fact that
A_{j}\succeq\gamma I_{d}
for all
j\in\{1,\dots,T\}
(see eq. (
13
)), we obtain that

\left\|\overline{A}_{t}^{1/2}\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{s:t-1}(\eta)\right\|\leqslant\left\|\overline{A}_{t}^{1/2}\left(\sum\limits_{j=s}^{t-1}A_{j}+\frac{\lambda}{\eta}I_{d}\right)^{-1}\right\|\,\left\|\sum\limits_{j=s}^{t-1}\mathbf{b}_{j}\right\|\leqslant\frac{\|\overline{A}_{t}\|^{1/2}}{(t-s)\gamma+\lambda/\eta}\left\|\sum\limits_{j=s}^{t-1}\mathbf{b}_{j}\right\|.

According to Lemma
C.6
, with probability at least
(1-\delta)
it holds that

\displaystyle\left\|\overline{A}_{t}^{1/2}\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{s:t-1}(\eta)\right\|

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{4B\|\overline{A}_{t}\|}{\gamma(t-s)+\lambda/\eta}\left(1\vee\sqrt{\frac{\|\overline{A}_{1}\|}{\|\overline{A}_{t}\|}}\;\right)\cdot\left(d\log 6+(t-s)\frac{\log 2}{2}+\log\frac{2}{\delta}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant 4B\|\overline{A}_{t}\|^{1/2}\sqrt{\|\overline{A}_{t}\|\vee\|\overline{A}_{1}\|}\cdot\left(\frac{\log 2}{2\gamma}\vee\frac{d\log 6+\log(2\sqrt{2}/\delta)}{\gamma+\lambda/\eta}\right).

(41)

Combining the inequalities (
40
) and (
41
) and using the union bound, we conclude that with probability at least
(1-2\delta)

\displaystyle\left\|A_{t}^{1/2}\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{s:t-1}(\eta)\right\|\leqslant 2\sqrt{B}\big(d\log 6+\log(2/\delta)\big)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\cdot 4B\|\overline{A}_{t}\|^{1/2}\sqrt{\|\overline{A}_{t}\|\vee\|A_{1}\|}

\displaystyle\cdot\left(\frac{\log 2}{2\gamma}\vee\frac{d\log 6+\log(2\sqrt{2}/\delta)}{\gamma+\lambda/\eta}\right).

∎

Appendix D
Regret bounds

In this section, we present regret bounds for the exponentially weighted average and the fixed share forecasters. The results are valid for arbitrary
A_{t}\succeq O_{d}
and
\mathbf{b}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
(not necessarily related to
\nabla\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\mathbf{X}_{t})
and
\Delta\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\mathbf{X}_{t})
).

D.1
Regret Bounds for Exponentially Weighted Average Forecaster

We start with auxiliary results on the regret of the exponentially weighted average forecaster with Gaussian prior. They will be helpful in the analysis of the fixed share algorithm. Our approach relies on the mixability argument of
Vovk [2001]
and has an important advantage over the online convex optimization techniques
[
Hazan et al., 2007
,
van der Hoeven et al., 2018
]
. In
Hazan et al., 2007
,
van der Hoeven et al., 2018
, the authors require the parameter space to be bounded in order to exploit exp-concavity of the quadratic loss on a compact set. In addition,
Hazan, Agarwal and Kale [2007]
compare the cumulative loss of the exponential weighting with the best expert over a bounded region. In contrast, we are interested in the case of unbounded domain.

We proceed with regret analysis of the exponentially weighted forecaster. It is easy to observe that

\ell_{t}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\frac{1}{2}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}A_{t}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\mathbf{b}_{t}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\frac{1}{2}\left\|A_{t}^{1/2}\left({\boldsymbol{\theta}}-A_{t}^{\dagger}\mathbf{b}_{t}\right)\right\|^{2}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}\mathbf{b}_{t}^{\perp},

where
\mathbf{b}_{t}^{\perp}
is the projection of
\mathbf{b}_{t}
onto
\mathrm{Im}(A_{t})^{\perp}
.
In other words,
\ell_{t}
consists of mixable and linear parts. It is known that worst-case regret bounds for mixable and linear losses are different. If
\mathbf{b}_{1}^{\perp},\dots,\mathbf{b}_{T}^{\perp}
are negligible, we can expect the regret

R_{1:T}^{EW}=\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{t}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}^{EW})-\inf\limits_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{t}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})

to be of order
O(\log T)
. Otherwise,
R_{1:T}^{EW}
will be as large as
O(\sqrt{T})
provided that the parameters are tuned properly. Our analysis based on the following technical lemma captures both favourable and worst-case scenarios.

Lemma D.1
.

Consider a quadratic loss function

\ell(\boldsymbol{\upsilon})=\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\upsilon}^{\top}A\boldsymbol{\upsilon}-\mathbf{b}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\upsilon}

with arbitrary symmetric positive semidefinite matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\Omega^{-1})
be a Gaussian random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then, for any
\eta>0
satisfying the inequality

\left\|A^{1/2}\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}-A^{\dagger}\mathbf{b}\right)\right\|^{2}\leqslant\frac{1}{2\eta},

(42)

it holds that

\exp\left\{-\eta\ell(\boldsymbol{\mu})+\eta^{2}\|\Omega^{-1/2}\,\mathbf{b}^{\perp}\|^{2}\right\}\geqslant\mathbb{E}\exp\big\{-\eta\ell({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\big\},

where
\mathbf{b}^{\perp}
is the projection of
\mathbf{b}
onto the orthogonal complement of
\mathrm{Im}(A)
.

We postpone the proof of Lemma
D.1
to Section
D.3.1
.
Let us recall that the exponentially weighted average
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{}_{t}^{EW}
is the mean of the posterior measure (
5
), which is Gaussian in our case. Using Lemma
D.1
and the argument of
Vovk [2001]
, we derive the following bound on
R_{1:T}^{EW}
.

Theorem D.2
.

Assume that the parameters
\lambda>0
and
\eta_{1}\geqslant\dots\geqslant\eta_{T}>0
are chosen in such a way that

\left\|A_{t}^{1/2}\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1:t-1}(\eta_{t})-A_{t}^{\dagger}\mathbf{b}_{t}\right)\right\|^{2}\leqslant\frac{1}{2\eta_{t}}\quad\text{for all $t\in\{1,\dots,T\}$,}

(43)

where
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1:t-1}
is defined in (
7
). For any
t\in\{1,\dots,T\}
, let
\mathbf{b}_{t}^{\perp}
stand for the projection of
\mathbf{b}_{t}
onto
\mathrm{Im}(A_{t})^{\perp}
and denote

\Omega_{t}=\lambda I_{d}+\eta_{t}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{t-1}A_{j}.

Then the regret
R_{1:T}^{EW}
of the exponentially weighted average forecaster satisfies the inequality

\displaystyle R_{1:T}^{EW}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\lambda\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1:T}^{\circ}\|^{2}}{2\eta_{T}}+\frac{1}{2\eta_{T}}\log\det\left(I_{d}+\frac{\eta_{T}}{\lambda}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}A_{t}\right)+\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\eta_{t}\|\Omega_{t}^{-1/2}\,\mathbf{b}_{t}^{\perp}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\lambda\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1:T}^{\circ}\|^{2}}{2\eta_{T}}+\frac{1}{2\eta_{T}}\log\det\left(I_{d}+\frac{\eta_{T}}{\lambda}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}A_{t}\right)+\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\eta_{t}\|\mathbf{b}_{t}^{\perp}\|^{2}}{\lambda},

where
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1:T}^{\circ}\in\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}\limits_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}L_{1:T}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
.

The proof of Theorem
D.2
is moved to Section
D.3.2
. Despite its simplicity, the result of Theorem
D.2
does not follow from the existing literature. In
[
Vovk, 2001
]
, the author studies a more specific loss
\ell_{t}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})=(\textbf{{x}}_{t}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-y_{t})^{2}
, which is a particular case of (
1
). Many other papers restrict their attention on bounded domains, such as
[
Hazan et al., 2007
,
van Erven et al., 2021
]
. In the case of quadratic loss with
\mathbf{b}_{t}^{\perp}=\mathbf{0}
, a finite domain size yields exp-concavity of the loss
\ell_{t}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
, which is also necessary in
[
Hazan et al., 2007
]
for the analysis. In
[
van der Hoeven et al., 2018
, Theorem 5]
, the authors show that, under minimal assumptions, the excess loss of the exponentially weighted average forecaster with the fixed learning rate
\eta
satisfies the inequality

\widehat{L}_{1:T}^{EW}-L_{1:T}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\leqslant\frac{1}{2\eta\lambda}\left\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1:t-1}(\eta_{t})-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right\|^{2}+\frac{\eta}{2}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\big(A_{t}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1:t-1}(\eta_{t})-\mathbf{b}_{t}\big)^{\top}\Omega_{t+1}^{-1}\big(A_{t}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1:t-1}(\eta_{t})-\mathbf{b}_{t}\big),

where
\Omega_{1},\dots,\Omega_{T+1}
are the same as in Theorem
D.2
. However,
van der Hoeven, van Erven and Kotłowski [2018]
impose additional assumptions (in particular, bounded domain and exp-concavity of the loss) to derive explicit dependence on
T
from the general regret bound.
As we announced, if
\mathbf{b}_{t}^{\perp}=\mathbf{0}
for all
t
from
1
to
T
, then the choice
\eta_{T}\gtrsim 1
,
\lambda=1
leads to logarithmic regret bounds. However, the relation
\mathbf{b}_{t}^{\perp}\neq\mathbf{0}
does not necessarily yield that
R^{EW}_{1:T}
is of order
\sqrt{T}
. In favourable situations (for instance, if
A_{1},\dots,A_{T}
are i.i.d. random matrices with non-degenerate expectation
\overline{A}=\mathbb{E}A_{1}\succ O_{d}
and
\mathbf{b}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{b}_{T}
are bounded almost surely) we can still have
\|\Omega_{t}^{-1/2}\,\mathbf{b}_{t}^{\perp}\|^{2}\lesssim 1/t
and
R^{EW}_{1:T}\lesssim\log T
.

We move to main theoretical results of the paper, the regret bound for Algorithm

presented in Theorem

. The main ingredient of its proof is the following technical result, establishing a mixability-type property of the fixed share forecaster.

is defined in (

\exp\left\{-\eta\ell_{t}\big(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}(\eta)\big)\right\}\geqslant\frac{V_{t}(\eta)}{V_{t-1}(\eta)}=\frac{W_{t}(\eta)}{W_{t-1}(\eta)},

) and (
10
).

We provide the proof of Lemma

in Section
D.3.3
. With this lemma at hand, the proof of the next regret bound is almost straightforward.

Theorem D.4
.

Assume that the outcomes
\{(A_{t},\mathbf{b}_{t}):1\leqslant t\leqslant T\}
and the learning rates
\eta_{1}\geqslant\eta_{2}\geqslant\dots\geqslant\eta_{T}
are such that
\mathbf{b}_{t}\in\mathrm{Im}(A_{t})
for all
t\in\{1,\dots,T\}
and

\left\|A_{t}^{1/2}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{s:t-1}(\eta_{t})\right\|^{2}\vee\left\|(A_{t}^{\dagger})^{-1/2}\mathbf{b}_{t}\right\|^{2}\leqslant\frac{1}{4\eta_{t}}\quad\text{for all $1\leqslant s<t\leqslant T$.}

Fix an arbitrary

and

.
Then the adaptive regret of the fixed share forecaster with respect to the best compound expert of size

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}_{1:T}^{FS}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{(m-1)\log(1/\alpha)}{\eta_{T}}+\frac{(T-m)\log\big(1/(1-\alpha)\big)}{\eta_{T}}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum\limits_{k=0}^{m-1}\left[\frac{\lambda\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\tau_{k}+1:\tau_{k+1}}^{\circ}\|^{2}}{2\eta_{T}}+\frac{1}{2\eta_{T}}\log\det\left(I_{d}+\frac{\eta_{T}}{\lambda}\sum\limits_{j=\tau_{k}+1}^{\tau_{k+1}}A_{j}\right)\right],

The proof of Theorem
D.4
is moved to Section
D.3.4
. Let us note that the fixed share forecaster enjoys the logarithmic dynamic regret
{\mathcal{R}}_{1:T}^{FS}={\mathcal{O}}(m\log(T/m))
provided that
\eta_{1}\geqslant\ldots\geqslant\eta_{T}\gtrsim 1
,
\lambda=1
and
\alpha=m/T
. This agrees with the result of
Herbster and Warmuth [1998]
(Corollary 1) who derived a similar bound for the case of a finite number of experts and mixable losses and with Theorem 3.1 of
Hazan and Seshadhri [2007]
implying a
{\mathcal{O}}(m\log T)
bound on the shifting regret of the follow-the-leading-history algorithm (FLH1) in the case of exp-concave losses and infinite number of experts.

Besides
[
Hazan and Seshadhri, 2007
]
, the problem of tracking the best out of infinite number of experts was studied in
[
Herbster and Warmuth, 2001
,
Cavallanti et al., 2007
,
Hazan and Seshadhri, 2007
,
Kozat and Singer, 2008
,
Hazan and Seshadhri, 2009
]
. The paper
[
Cavallanti et al., 2007
]
is not really relevant to the present work, because
Cavallanti et al. [2007]

consider binary loss functions. The switching regret bound in
[
Kozat and Singer, 2008
, Theorem 1]
, where the authors study the online linear regression problem, coincides with the one in Theorem
D.4
if we put
A_{t}=\textbf{{x}}_{t}\textbf{{x}}_{t}^{\top}
and
\mathbf{b}_{t}=y_{t}\textbf{{x}}_{t}
,
t\in\{1,\dots,T\}
. However, the conditions of
[
Kozat and Singer, 2008
, Theorem 1]
are milder than ours. It is not surprising, such a phenomenon was already discussed in
[
Vovk, 2001
, Section 3.4]
. The reason is that in the online linear regression the learner has an access to the feature vector
\textbf{{x}}_{t}
before he makes the prediction. Hence, he can use the nonlinear Vovk-Azoury-Warmuth forecaster
[
Vovk, 2001
,
Azoury and Warmuth, 2001
]
getting an advantage over standard exponential weighting with Gaussian prior. In contrast to
[
Cavallanti et al., 2007
,
Kozat and Singer, 2008
]
,
Herbster and Warmuth [2001]
consider general loss functions. Their approach to regret analysis is based on the properties of Bregman divergences and generalized projections. Unfortunately, the oracle inequality in
[
Herbster and Warmuth, 2001
]
for the cumulative loss of the learner is not sharp and can lead to extremely poor guarantees in the worst-case scenario. Finally, we have already compared our dynamic regret bounds with the guarantees of
Hazan and Seshadhri [2007]
,
Hazan and Seshadhri [2009]
in the previous paragraph.
In conclusion, we would like to emphasize that
their analysis relies on exp-concavity of the loss functions. Clearly, the quadratic loss (
1
) is not exp-concave on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Proof of Lemma

The proof of Lemma
D.1
relies on the following auxiliary result derived
in Appendix

below.

Lemma D.5
.

Consider a quadratic loss function

\ell(\boldsymbol{\upsilon})=\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\upsilon}^{\top}A\boldsymbol{\upsilon}-\mathbf{b}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\upsilon}

with arbitrary symmetric positive semidefinite matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\Omega^{-1})
be a Gaussian random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then, for any
\eta>0
, satisfying the inequality

\eta\left\|(\Omega+\eta A)^{-1/2}(A\boldsymbol{\mu}-\mathbf{b})\right\|^{2}\leqslant\left\|A^{1/2}(\Omega+\eta A)^{-1}A^{1/2}\right\|,

(46)

it holds that

e^{-\eta\ell(\boldsymbol{\mu})}\geqslant\mathbb{E}e^{-\eta\ell({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}.

Let us represent
\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{b}^{\parallel}+\mathbf{b}^{\perp}
, where
\mathbf{b}^{\parallel}\in\mathrm{Im}(A)
and
\mathbf{b}^{\perp}\in\mathrm{Im}(A)^{\perp}
and apply Lemma
D.5
to the loss

\widetilde{\ell}(\boldsymbol{\upsilon})=\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\upsilon}^{\top}A\boldsymbol{\upsilon}-\boldsymbol{\upsilon}^{\top}\mathbf{b}^{\parallel}.

Note that the condition (
42
) yields that

\displaystyle 2\eta\left\|(\Omega+\eta A)^{-1/2}(A\boldsymbol{\mu}-\mathbf{b}^{\parallel})\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=2\eta\left\|(\Omega+\eta A)^{-1/2}A(\boldsymbol{\mu}-A^{\dagger}\mathbf{b}^{\parallel})\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\eta\left\|A^{1/2}(\Omega+\eta A)^{-1}A^{1/2}\right\|\left\|A^{1/2}(\boldsymbol{\mu}-A^{\dagger}\mathbf{b}^{\parallel})\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left\|A^{1/2}(\Omega+\eta A)^{-1}A^{1/2}\right\|.

Hence, the assumptions of Lemma
D.5
are satisfied, and we obtain that

e^{-2\eta\widetilde{\ell}(\boldsymbol{\mu})}\geqslant\mathbb{E}e^{-2\eta\widetilde{\ell}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}.

Then, due to the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\exp\left\{-\eta\ell({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\exp\left\{-\eta\widetilde{\ell}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})+\eta\,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}\mathbf{b}^{\perp}\right\}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\exp\left\{-2\eta\,\widetilde{\ell}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\right\}}\cdot\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\exp\left\{2\eta\,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}\mathbf{b}^{\perp}\right\}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sqrt{\exp\left\{-2\eta\,\widetilde{\ell}(\boldsymbol{\mu})\right\}}\cdot\sqrt{\exp\left\{2\eta\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{b}^{\perp}+2\eta^{2}\|\Omega^{-1/2}\,\mathbf{b}^{\perp}\|^{2}\right\}}

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{-\eta\widetilde{\ell}(\boldsymbol{\mu})+\eta\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{b}^{\perp}+\eta^{2}\|\Omega^{-1/2}\,\mathbf{b}^{\perp}\|^{2}\right\}.

Taking into account that

\widetilde{\ell}(\boldsymbol{\mu})-\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{b}^{\perp}=\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}A\boldsymbol{\mu}-\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{b}^{\parallel}-\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{b}^{\perp}=\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}A\boldsymbol{\mu}-\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{b}=\ell(\boldsymbol{\mu}),

we obtain the desired bound:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\exp\big\{-\eta\ell({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\big\}

\displaystyle\leqslant\exp\left\{-\eta\widetilde{\ell}(\boldsymbol{\mu})+\eta\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{b}^{\perp}+\eta^{2}\|\Omega^{-1/2}\,\mathbf{b}^{\perp}\|^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{-\eta\ell(\boldsymbol{\mu})+\eta^{2}\|\Omega^{-1/2}\,\mathbf{b}^{\perp}\|^{2}\right\}.

\blacksquare

Note that the exponentially weighted average

Substituting

\displaystyle R_{1:T}^{EW}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum\limits_{k=0}^{m-1}\left[\frac{\lambda\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\tau_{k}+1:\tau_{k+1}}^{\circ}\|^{2}}{2\eta}+\frac{1}{2\eta}\log\det\left(I_{d}+\frac{\eta}{\lambda}\sum\limits_{j=\tau_{k}+1}^{\tau_{k+1}}A_{j}\right)\right].

\blacksquare

Lemma E.1
.

With the notations of Lemma
D.5
, it holds that

\displaystyle\left\|(\eta A+\Omega)^{-1/2}(\eta\mathbf{b}+\Omega\boldsymbol{\mu})\right\|^{2}-\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\Omega\boldsymbol{\mu}+\eta\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}A\boldsymbol{\mu}-2\eta\mathbf{b}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}

\displaystyle=\eta^{2}\left\|(\Omega+\eta A)^{-1/2}(A\boldsymbol{\mu}-\mathbf{b})\right\|^{2}.

(53)

Proof.

We start with rewriting the left-hand side of (
53
) in the following form:

\displaystyle\left\|(\eta A+\Omega)^{-1/2}(\eta\mathbf{b}+\Omega\boldsymbol{\mu})\right\|^{2}-\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\Omega\boldsymbol{\mu}+\eta\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}A\boldsymbol{\mu}-2\eta\mathbf{b}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\left(\Omega(\eta A+\Omega)^{-1}\Omega-\Omega+\eta A\right)\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}-2\eta\mathbf{b}^{\top}\left(I_{d}-(\eta A+\Omega)^{-1}\Omega\right)\boldsymbol{\mu}+\eta^{2}\mathbf{b}^{\top}(\eta A+\Omega)^{-1}\mathbf{b}.

The identity (
53
) simply follows from Lemma
E.2
below.
Applying it to
\Omega(\eta A+\Omega)^{-1}\Omega-\Omega+\eta A
and to
I_{d}-(\eta A+\Omega)^{-1}\Omega
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\Omega(\eta A+\Omega)^{-1}\Omega-\Omega+\eta A

\displaystyle=\Omega\left((\eta A+\Omega)^{-1}-\Omega^{-1}\right)\Omega+\eta A=\eta A-\eta\Omega(\Omega+\eta A)^{-1}A

\displaystyle=\Omega\left(\Omega^{-1}-(\Omega+\eta A)^{-1}\right)\eta A=\eta^{2}A(\Omega+\eta A)^{-1}A

and

\displaystyle I_{d}-(\eta A+\Omega)^{-1}\Omega=\Omega\left(\eta\Omega^{-1}A(\Omega+\eta A)^{-1}\right)=\eta A(\Omega+\eta A)^{-1}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\left(\Omega(\eta A+\Omega)^{-1}\Omega-\Omega+\eta A\right)\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}-2\eta\mathbf{b}^{\top}\left(I_{d}-(\eta A+\Omega)^{-1}\Omega\right)\boldsymbol{\mu}+\eta^{2}\mathbf{b}^{\top}(\eta A+\Omega)^{-1}\mathbf{b}

\displaystyle=\eta^{2}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}A(\Omega+\eta A)^{-1}A\boldsymbol{\mu}-2\eta^{2}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}A(\Omega+\eta A)^{-1}\mathbf{b}+\eta^{2}\mathbf{b}^{\top}(\eta A+\Omega)^{-1}\mathbf{b}

\displaystyle=\eta^{2}\left\|(\Omega+\eta A)^{-1/2}(A\boldsymbol{\mu}-\mathbf{b})\right\|^{2}.

∎

Lemma E.2
.

Let
\Omega
and
B
be symmetric positive semidefinite matrices of size
(d\times d)
, such that
\det(\Omega)\neq 0
. Then it holds that

\Omega^{-1}-(\Omega+B)^{-1}=(\Omega+B)^{-1}B\Omega^{-1}=\Omega^{-1}B(\Omega+B)^{-1}.

Proof.

Denote the difference
\Omega^{-1}-(\Omega+B)^{-1}
by
M
. Then it holds that

M(\Omega+B)=\Omega^{-1}(\Omega+B)-I_{d}=\Omega^{-1}B.

Thus,
M=\Omega^{-1}B(\Omega+B)^{-1}
. Similarly, one has

(\Omega+B)M=(\Omega+B)\Omega^{-1}-I_{d}=B\Omega^{-1}

and, hence,
M=(\Omega+B)^{-1}B\Omega^{-1}
.

∎

Lemma E.3
.

Let
\Omega
and
B
be symmetric positive semidefinite matrices of size
(d\times d)
, such that
\det(\Omega)\neq 0
. Then it holds that

\left\|B^{1/2}(\Omega+B)^{-1}B^{1/2}\right\|\leqslant\log\det\left(I_{d}+\Omega^{-1/2}B\Omega^{-1/2}\right).

Proof.

The proof is based on an observation that the operator norm of
B^{1/2}(\Omega+B)^{-1}B^{1/2}
can be expressed through the eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}(\Omega^{-1/2}B\Omega^{-1/2}),\dots
,
\lambda_{d}(\Omega^{-1/2}B\Omega^{-1/2})
of the matrix
\Omega^{-1/2}B\Omega^{-1/2}
as follows:

\displaystyle\left\|B^{1/2}(\Omega+B)^{-1}B^{1/2}\right\|

\displaystyle=\max\limits_{1\leqslant j\leqslant d}\left\{\frac{\lambda_{j}(\Omega^{-1/2}B\Omega^{-1/2})}{1+\lambda_{j}(\Omega^{-1/2}B\Omega^{-1/2})}\right\}

\displaystyle\leqslant\max\limits_{1\leqslant j\leqslant d}\log\left(1+\lambda_{j}(\Omega^{-1/2}B\Omega^{-1/2})\right).

Here the last line follows from the inequality
x/(1+x)\leqslant\log(1+x)
, which holds for all non-negative
x
.
Since
\log\left(1+\lambda_{j}(\Omega^{-1/2}B\Omega^{-1/2})\right)\geqslant 0
for all
j\in\{1,\dots,d\}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\max\limits_{1\leqslant j\leqslant d}\log\left(1+\lambda_{j}(\Omega^{-1/2}B\Omega^{-1/2})\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum\limits_{1\leqslant j\leqslant d}\log\left(1+\lambda_{j}(\Omega^{-1/2}B\Omega^{-1/2})\right)

\displaystyle=\log\det\left(I_{d}+\Omega^{-1/2}B\Omega^{-1/2}\right).

Hence,

\left\|B^{1/2}(\Omega+B)^{-1}B^{1/2}\right\|\leqslant\log\det\left(I_{d}+\Omega^{-1/2}B\Omega^{-1/2}\right).

∎

Lemma E.6
.

Let
\xi
and
\eta
be non-negative random variables with finite
\psi_{1}
-norms. Then it holds that

\mathbb{E}\xi\eta\leqslant 3e\,\mathbb{E}\xi\,\|\eta\|_{\psi_{1}}\log\frac{4\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\xi^{2}}}{\mathbb{E}\xi}\leqslant 3e\,\mathbb{E}\xi\,\|\eta\|_{\psi_{1}}\log\frac{8\|\xi\|_{\psi_{1}}}{\mathbb{E}\xi}

Proof.

Let us take

\varepsilon=\left(\log\frac{4\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\xi^{2}}}{\mathbb{E}\xi}\right)^{-1}\in(0,1).

Applying Hölder’s inequality, we obtain that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\xi\eta=\mathbb{E}\left(\xi^{1-\varepsilon}\cdot\xi^{\varepsilon}\eta\right)\leqslant\big(\mathbb{E}\xi\big)^{1-\varepsilon}\big(\mathbb{E}\,\xi\eta^{1/\varepsilon}\big)^{\varepsilon}.

Due to the Cauchy-Schwarz bound and Lemma

\displaystyle\big(\mathbb{E}\xi\big)^{1-\varepsilon}\big(\mathbb{E}\,\xi\eta^{1/\varepsilon}\big)^{\varepsilon}\leqslant\big(\mathbb{E}\xi\big)^{1-\varepsilon}\big(\mathbb{E}\xi^{2}\big)^{\varepsilon/2}\big(\mathbb{E}\eta^{2/\varepsilon}\big)^{\varepsilon/2}\leqslant\mathbb{E}\xi\left(\frac{\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\xi^{2}}}{\mathbb{E}\xi}\right)^{\varepsilon}\cdot 2^{\varepsilon/2}\left(1+\frac{2}{\varepsilon}\right)\|\eta\|_{\psi_{1}}.
