Fully commutative elements and spherical nilpotent orbits
Jacopo Gandini

Abstract
. Let 𝔤 \mathfrak{g} be a complex simple Lie algebra, with fixed Borel subalgebra 𝔟 ⊂ 𝔤 \mathfrak{b}\subset\mathfrak{g} and with Weyl group W W . Expanding on previous work of Fan and Stembridge in the simply laced case, this note aims to study the fully commutative elements of W W , and their connections with the spherical nilpotent orbits in 𝔤 \mathfrak{g} . If 𝔤 \mathfrak{g} is not of type G 2 G_{2} , it is shown that an element w ∈ W w\in W is fully commutative if and only if the subalgebra of 𝔟 \mathfrak{b} determined by the inversions of w w lies in the closure of a spherical nilpotent orbit. A similar characterization is also given for the ad-nilpotent ideals of 𝔟 \mathfrak{b} , which are parametrized by suitable elements in the affine Weyl group of 𝔤 \mathfrak{g} thanks to the work of Cellini and Papi.



1.
Introduction

Let
G
be a complex algebraic group, simple and adjoint with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
. Let
T\subset G
be a maximal torus with character lattice
\mathcal{X}(T)
and Lie algebra
\mathfrak{t}
, and let
\Phi\subset\mathcal{X}(T)
be the associated root system, with Weyl group
W=N_{G}(T)/T
. Let also
B\subset G
be a Borel subgroup containing
T
,
\mathfrak{b}\subset\mathfrak{g}
the corresponding Lie algebra, whose nilradical is denoted by
\mathfrak{n}
, and
\Delta\subset\Phi
the associated base. We denote the associated set of positive (resp. negative) roots by
\Phi^{+}
(resp.
\Phi^{-}
). If
\alpha\in\Phi
, we denote by
s_{\alpha}\in W
the corresponding reflection, and by
\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}\subset\mathfrak{g}
the corresponding root space.
If
\alpha\in\Delta
, then the reflection
s_{\alpha}\in W
is called simple. If
\alpha,\beta\in\Delta
, then we denote by
m(s_{\alpha},s_{\beta})
the order of the product
s_{\alpha}s_{\beta}
.

Given
w\in W
, recall that

\bullet

w
is
commutative
if no reduced expression for
w
contains a substring of the shape
s_{\alpha}s_{\beta}s_{\alpha}
, where
\alpha,\beta\in\Delta
satisfy
||\alpha||\leqslant||\beta||
.

\bullet

w
is
fully commutative
if no reduced expression for
w
contains a substring of the shape
s_{\alpha}s_{\beta}s_{\alpha}s_{\beta}\ldots
of length
m(s_{\alpha},s_{\beta})
, where
s_{\alpha}
and
s_{\beta}
are non-commuting simple reflections.

The sets of the commutative and of the fully commutative elements in
W
will be respectively denoted by
W_{c}
and
W_{\!f\!c}
.

Commutative and fully commutative elements were introduced around the same time respectively by Fan
[
6
]
and by Stembridge
[
19
]
in the context of Coxeter groups. It follows from the definition that commutative elements are also fully commutative.
In the simply laced case the two notions are equivalent: indeed in this case all the roots have the same length, and
m(s_{\alpha},s_{\beta})\leqslant 3
for all
\alpha,\beta\in\Delta
. On the other hand, in the non-simply laced case fully commutative elements need not to be commutative. Consider for example the root system of type
B_{2}
, with simple roots
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}
such that
||\alpha_{1}||>||\alpha_{2}||
: then
m(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})=4
, therefore
s_{\alpha_{1}}s_{\alpha_{2}}s_{\alpha_{1}}
is fully commutative but not commutative.

Let
G
act on
\mathfrak{g}
via the adjoint action, denoted by
(g,x)\mapsto g.x
, and recall that
x\in\mathfrak{g}
is called nilpotent if
\ad(x)\in\mathrm{End}(\mathfrak{g})
is nilpotent. Following a construction of Steinberg, every Weyl group element defines a nilpotent orbit in
\mathfrak{g}
(that is, the
G
-orbit of a nilpotent element in
\mathfrak{g}
). In the simply-laced case, a connection between commutative elements in
W
and nilpotent orbits in
\mathfrak{g}
was pointed out by Fan and Stembridge
[
7
]
in terms of such construction: the (fully) commutative elements are precisely those elements which correspond to particular nilpotent orbits, called
spherical
. The main goal of this note is to extend such a connection to the general case.

Let
w\in W
, and denote by

\Phi(w)=\{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}\;|\;w(\alpha)\in\Phi^{-}\}

the corresponding set of inversions. We can attach to
w
a
T
-stable subspace of
\mathfrak{n}
by setting

\mathfrak{a}_{w}=\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Phi(w)}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}=\mathfrak{n}\;\cap\;(w^{-1}w_{0}\,\mathfrak{n})

where
w_{0}\in W
denotes the longest element.

As pointed out by Fan
[
6
, Section 7]
(see also
[
7
]
for the case of arbitrary simply laced Coxeter groups), the commutativity of
w
is equivalent to the fact that
\mathfrak{a}_{w}
is an abelian subalgebra of
\mathfrak{n}
, namely that
\Phi(w)
satisfies the following property:

\alpha,\beta\in\Phi(w)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\alpha+\beta\not\in\Phi(w)

More generally, a characterization of the full commutativity of
w
in terms of the inversion set
\Phi(w)
was given by Cellini and Papi
[
4
]
in the case of arbitrary Coxeter groups (see also Theorem
2.5
, where such characterization will be recalled).

Let
\mathcal{N}=G.\mathfrak{n}
be the subvariety of nilpotent elements in
\mathfrak{g}
. Since
\mathfrak{a}_{w}
is irreducible and since the number of
G
-orbits in
\mathcal{N}
is finite, there exists a unique
G
-orbit
\mathcal{O}_{w}\subset\mathcal{N}
which intersects
\mathfrak{a}_{w}
in a dense open subset. Notice that, up to multiplication by
w_{0}
, the map

\varphi:W\longrightarrow\mathcal{N}/G\qquad\qquad w\longmapsto\mathcal{O}_{w}

is the Steinberg map
\mathrm{St}:W\longrightarrow\mathcal{N}/G
of
[
10
, Section 1.8]
. Namely, we have

\mathrm{St}(w)=\mathcal{O}_{w_{0}w^{-1}}=\mathcal{O}_{ww_{0}}=\varphi(ww_{0}).

In particular, up to multiplication by
w_{0}
, the fibers of
\varphi
are the two-sided Steinberg cells of
W
. As a consequence of a result of Steinberg
[
18
, Theorem 3.5]
, it follows that
\mathrm{St}
is a surjective map. Therefore
\varphi
is a surjective map as well.

Recall that a nilpotent
G
-orbit is called
spherical
if it contains an open
B
-orbit. The sphericality of the orbit of an element
x\in\mathcal{N}
is nicely characterized with the notion of
height
, defined as

\height(x)=\max\{n\in\mathbb{N}\;|\;\ad(x)^{n}\neq 0\}.

Indeed, by a theorem of Panyushev
[
13
, Theorem 3.1]
we have the equivalence

G.x\text{ is spherical}\Longleftrightarrow\height(x)\leqslant 3

We will prove the following characterization, which is the first main result of the paper.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
w\in W
.

i)

If
\Phi
is simply laced or of type
G_{2}
, then
w\in W_{c}
if and only if
\mathcal{O}_{w}
is spherical.

ii)

If
\Phi
is doubly laced, then
w\in W_{\!f\!c}
if and only if
\mathcal{O}_{w}
is spherical.

When
\Phi
is simply laced, the previous theorem is due to Fan and Stembridge
[
7
]
. It seems that after that paper the general situation was never explored. The present note wants to fill such a lack.

As an application of the previous theorem, we get the following corollary (the case of type
G_{2}
is treated on its own).

Corollary 1.2
.

The subset of the fully commutative elements
W_{\!f\!c}\subset W
is a union of
w_{0}
-traslates of two-sided Steinberg cells.

Notice that in general the subset of the commutative elements
W_{\!c}\subset W
is not a union of
w_{0}
-translates of two-sided Steinberg cells. This is indeed true in simply laced type because
W_{\!c}=W_{\!f\!c}
, and is true in type
G_{2}
as well, but it fails in the doubly laced case.

Given a subspace
\mathfrak{a}\subset\mathfrak{n}
, we will say that
\mathfrak{a}
is a
spherical subspace
if every
G
-orbit intersecting
\mathfrak{a}
is spherical, namely if
\height(x)\leqslant 3
for all
x\in\mathfrak{a}
. Notice that the spherical locus

\mathcal{N}_{3}=\{x\in\mathcal{N}\;|\;\height(x)\leqslant 3\}

is a closed subvariety of
\mathcal{N}
, which is indeed irreducible in all cases (see
[
14
, Section 6.1]
). This implies that, if
w\in W
, then
\mathfrak{a}_{w}
is a spherical subspace of
\mathfrak{n}
if and only if
\mathcal{O}_{w}
is a spherical orbit, if and only if
\overline{G.\mathfrak{a}_{w}}
is the closure of a spherical nilpotent orbit. Therefore Theorem
1.1
characterizes the sphericality of
\mathfrak{a}_{w}
in terms of the commutativity and full commutativity of
w
.

We will also consider some kind of variation of Theorem
1.1
, allowing to characterize the sphericality of an ad-nilpotent ideal of
\mathfrak{b}
in terms of the commutativity and full commutativity of a suitable element in the affine Weyl group
\widehat{W}
.

Following Cellini and Papi
[
3
]
, we will say that an ideal
\mathfrak{a}\subset\mathfrak{b}
is
\ad
-nilpotent
if it is contained in
\mathfrak{n}
. Notice that in this case
G.\mathfrak{a}
is automatically closed, because
\mathfrak{a}
is
B
-stable and the map
G\times^{B}\mathfrak{a}\rightarrow\mathfrak{g}
defined by the
G
-action is proper.

Generalizing Peterson’s classification
[
12
]
of the abelian ideals of
\mathfrak{b}
, the ad-nilpotent ideals of
\mathfrak{b}
were classified in
[
3
]
by attaching to every such an ideal
\mathfrak{a}
a suitable element
w_{\mathfrak{a}}\in\widehat{W}
(see Section
2.2
, where the construction will be recalled).
On the other hand, the ad-
nilpotent spherical ideals
(that is, the spherical subspaces of
\mathfrak{n}
which are also ideals in
\mathfrak{b}
) were classified by Panyushev and Röhrle
[
15
]
,
[
16
]
. In these papers, they show that an abelian ideal of
\mathfrak{b}
is necessarily spherical (and ad-nilpotent), and that if
\Phi
is simply laced the converse is also true. Moreover, they classify the maximal ad-nilpotent spherical ideals for all complex simple Lie algebras.

The definition of commutative and fully commutative element given in the finite case go through in the affine case. We will prove the following theorem, which is the second main result of the paper.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\mathfrak{a}\subset\mathfrak{b}
be an ad-nilpotent ideal and let
w_{\mathfrak{a}}\in\widehat{W}
be the corresponding element.

i)

If
\Phi
is simply laced or of type
G_{2}
, then
\mathfrak{a}
is spherical if and only if
w_{\mathfrak{a}}
is commutative.

ii)

If
\Phi
is doubly laced, then
\mathfrak{a}
is spherical if and only if
w_{\mathfrak{a}}
is fully commutative.

Again, in the simply laced case the previous theorem is well known. Indeed, by the mentioned results of Panyushev and Röhrle
[
15
]

[
16
]
, in this case an ad-nilpotent ideal of
\mathfrak{b}
is spherical if and only if it is abelian, and by Peterson’s classification
[
12
]
the abelian ideals
\mathfrak{a}\subset\mathfrak{b}
are classified by the corresponding commutative elements
w_{\mathfrak{a}}\in\widehat{W}
. On the other hand, in the non-simply laced case the previous statement is missing in the literature.

We now discuss the organization of the paper. Sections
2
and
3
deal with the case not of type
G_{2}
, whereas the latter is treated separately in Section
4
.
More precisely, using the characterization of the fully commutative elements of Cellini and Papi
[
4
]
, in Section
2
we will reduce both Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.3
to another characterization of the sphericality of a subspace
\mathfrak{a}\subset\mathfrak{n}
, which is either defined by the inversions of a Weyl group element or is an ideal in
\mathfrak{b}
(see Theorem
2.3
). Section
3
is devoted to the proof of Theorem
2.3
.

2.
Fully commutative elements and spherical nilpotent orbits

Let
\Psi\subset\Phi^{+}
. Recall that
\Psi
is called
closed
if every root which is a sum of two elements in
\Psi
is also in
\Psi
, whereas it is called
convex
if every root which is a linear combination of two elements in
\Psi
with positive coefficients is also in
\Psi
. If both
\Psi
and
\Phi^{+}\smallsetminus\Psi
are closed (resp. convex), then
\Psi
is called
biclosed
(resp.
biconvex
).

Remark 2.1
.

If
\Psi\subset\Phi^{+}
, then the following are equivalent:

i)

\Psi=\Phi(w)
for some
w\in W
;

ii)

\Psi
is biconvex;

iii)

\Psi
is biclosed.

The implication iii)
\Rightarrow
i) is well known (see
[
2
, Exercise 16, Chap. VI, §1]
), whereas the other implications are clear. Notice that the equivalence i)
\Leftrightarrow
ii) holds more generally for the root system of any finitely generated Coxeter group (see
[
17
, Section 8]
), provided
\Psi
is finite.

Following Panyushev, we will say that
\Psi\subset\Phi^{+}
is a
combinatorial ideal
if it is closed under addition of arbitrary positive roots, namely if the following holds

\alpha\in\Psi,\,\beta\in\Phi^{+},\,\alpha+\beta\in\Phi^{+}\Longrightarrow\alpha+\beta\in\Psi

To any subset
\Psi\subset\Phi^{+}
we attach a
T
-stable subspace of
\mathfrak{n}
, defined as follows

\mathfrak{a}_{\Psi}=\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Psi}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}.

Keeping the notation used in the introduction, if
\Psi=\Phi(w)
we will also write
\mathfrak{a}_{w}
instead of
\mathfrak{a}_{\Phi(w)}
.

Remark 2.2
.

Given
\Psi\subset\Phi^{+}
, it follows immediately from the definitions that

i)

\mathfrak{a}_{\Psi}
is a subalgebra of
\mathfrak{n}
if and only if
\Psi
is a closed subset of
\Phi^{+}
,

ii)

\mathfrak{a}_{\Psi}
is an ideal of
\mathfrak{b}
if and only if
\Psi
is a combinatorial ideal in
\Phi^{+}
.

The formalism of biconvex subsets of roots and combinatorial ideals allows to treat with the same language the two problems investigated in the paper.
When
\Phi
is not of type
G_{2}
, both Theorems
1.1
and
1.3
will be easy consequences of the following theorem, thanks to the characterization of Cellini and Papi
[
4
]
of the fully commutative elements of
\widehat{W}
(see Theorem
2.5
).

Denote by
(.,.)
the
W
-invariant scalar product on
\langle\Phi\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}
induced by the Killing form on
\mathfrak{g}
. For
\alpha,\beta\in\Phi
, set as usual
\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle=\tfrac{2(\alpha,\beta)}{(\beta,\beta)}
.

Theorem 2.3
.

Suppose that
\Phi
is not of type
G_{2}
, and let
\Psi\subset\Phi^{+}
be either a combinatorial ideal or a biconvex subset. Then
\mathfrak{a}_{\Psi}
is a spherical subspace of
\mathfrak{n}
if and only
\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle\geqslant 0
for all
\alpha,\beta\in\Psi
.

As for Theorems
1.1
and
1.3
, when
\Phi
is simply laced the previous theorem is well known. The case of biconvex subsets in simply laced type was considered by Fan and Stembridge
[
7
]
. The case of the combinatorial ideals in simply laced type can also be reduced to
[
7
]
, thanks to the mentioned result of Panyushev and Röhrle
[
15
]

[
16
]
that an ideal of
\mathfrak{b}
is spherical if and only if it is abelian, and that following Peterson’s classification the abelian ideals of
\mathfrak{b}
can be defined in terms of the inversions of a commutative element in
\widehat{W}
(see
[
12
]
).

When
\Psi\subset\Phi^{+}
is a combinatorial ideal in non-simply laced type, the previous statement seems to be missing in the literature; however it is implicit in the classification of the maximal ad-nilpotent spherical ideals of
\mathfrak{b}
of Panyushev and Röhrle
[
16
]
.
It is indeed an easy remark to see that, if
\mathfrak{a}_{\Psi}
is spherical, then
\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle\geqslant 0
for all
\alpha,\beta\in\Psi
(see Lemma
3.1
). On the other hand, if
\mathfrak{g}
is doubly-laced, in order to classify the maximal ad-nilpotent spherical ideals of
\mathfrak{b}
, in
[
16
]
the authors first classify the maximal combinatorial ideals
\Psi\subset\Phi^{+}
such that
\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle\geqslant 0
for all
\alpha,\beta\in\Psi
, and then show that the associated ideals are the maximal ad-nilpotent spherical ideals of
\mathfrak{b}
.

The proof of Theorem
2.3
will be given in Section
3
. It is basically an extension of the proof of Fan and Stembridge
[
7
]
from the simply laced case to the non-simply laced case. The lines of the proof are essentially the same, however technical issues make the proof in the general case definitely more involved (see Lemma
3.4
, which constitutes the technical core of the proof). The case of the combinatorial ideals can be treated essentially in a uniform way together with the case of the biconvex subsets, only adding a few extra lines.

In the following subsections we explain how to deduce Theorems
1.1
and
1.3
from Theorem
2.3
provided
\Phi
is not of type
G_{2}
(see Theorems
2.8
and
2.11
, respectively). The case of type
G_{2}
will be discussed on its own in Section
4
.

2.1.
Fully commutative elements in the affine Weyl group.

Let
\widehat{\Phi}=\Phi\pm\mathbb{Z}\delta
be the affine
root system defined by
\Phi
, where
\delta
denotes the fundamental imaginary root (see
[
11
]
).
Let
\widehat{\Phi}^{+}\subset\widehat{\Phi}
be the positive subsystem containing
\Phi^{+}
and all the roots of the shape
\alpha+n\delta
with
\alpha\in\Phi
and
n>0
, and let
\widehat{\Delta}\supset\Delta
be the corresponding base of
\widehat{\Phi}
. We also set
\widehat{\Phi}^{-}=-\widehat{\Phi}^{+}
.

Let
\widehat{W}
be the Weyl group of
\widehat{\Phi}
. For
\alpha\in\widehat{\Delta}
denote by
s_{\alpha}\in\widehat{W}
the corresponding simple reflection. Extend the
W
-invariant scalar product
(.,.)
on
\langle\Phi\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}
to a
\widehat{W}
-invariant symmetric bilinear form on
\langle\widehat{\Phi}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}
, with kernel generated by
\delta
. If
\alpha,\beta\in\widehat{\Phi}\smallsetminus\mathbb{Z}\delta
are real roots, set as usual
\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle=2\tfrac{(\alpha,\beta)}{(\beta,\beta)}
.

Given
w\in\widehat{W}
, the set of inversions of
w
is defined as

\widehat{\Phi}(w)=\{\alpha\in\widehat{\Phi}^{+}\;|\;w(\alpha)\in\widehat{\Phi}^{-}\}.

The notion of biconvex subset in
\widehat{\Phi}^{+}
extends verbatim to the affine context, and still characterizes the inversion sets of elements in
\widehat{W}
(see Remark
2.1
).

The notions of commutative and fully commutative elements also extend verbatim to
\widehat{W}
. We now recall how these elements are characterized in terms of their sets of inversions.

The commutative elements were characterized by Fan and Stembridge
[
7
]
,
[
20
]
.

Theorem 2.4

(
[
7
, Theorem 2.4]
,
[
20
, Theorem 5.3]
)
.

Let
w\in\widehat{W}
, then
w
is commutative if and only if
\alpha+\beta\not\in\widehat{\Phi}
for all
\alpha,\beta\in\widehat{\Phi}(w)
.
If moreover
\Phi
is simply laced, then
w
is commutative if and only if
\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle\geqslant 0
for all
\alpha,\beta\in\widehat{\Phi}(w)
.

The fully commutative elements were characterized by Cellini and Papi
[
4
]
. Recall that a subsystem
\Psi\subset\widehat{\Phi}
is
parabolic
if
\Psi=\langle\Psi\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}\cap\widehat{\Phi}
, namely if
\Psi
is obtained by intersecting
\widehat{\Phi}
with a linear subspace of
\langle\widehat{\Phi}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}
. Every parabolic subsystem is generated by a subset of a suitable base of
\widehat{\Phi}
, and conversely. A
parabolic positive subsystem
of
\widehat{\Phi}
is a positive system of a parabolic subsystem.

Theorem 2.5

(
[
4
, Theorem 2]
)
.

Let
w\in\widehat{W}
, then
w
is fully commutative if and only if
\widehat{\Phi}(w)
does not contain any irreducible parabolic positive subsystem of rank 2.

Thus we obtain the following corollary, which generalizes the last statement of Theorem
2.4
.

Corollary 2.6
.

Suppose that
\Phi
is not of type
G_{2}
and let
w\in\widehat{W}
. Then
w
is fully commutative if and only if
\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle\geqslant 0
for all
\alpha,\beta\in\widehat{\Phi}(w)
.

Proof.

If
\Phi
is of type
A_{1}
the claim is easily shown directly, so we assume that
\Phi
has rank at least 2. Then every parabolic subsystem
\Psi\subset\widehat{\Phi}
of rank 2 is of finite type. If moreover
\Phi
is not of type
G_{2}
, then the Dynkin diagram of
\widehat{\Phi}
contains at most double bonds: thus the parabolic subsystems of
\widehat{\Phi}
of rank 2 can only be of type
A_{2}
or
B_{2}
. On the other hand, if
\Psi
is of type
A_{2}
or
B_{2}
, then two non-proportional elements
\alpha,\beta\in\Psi
form a base of
\Psi
if and only if
\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle<0
. Therefore the claim follows from Theorem
2.5
.
∎

Remark 2.7
.

The statement of Corollary
2.6
fails when
\Phi
is of type
G_{2}
. Indeed in this case the fully commutative elements in
W
are those of length at most
5
, whereas those which satisfy the condition of Corollary
2.6
are those with length at most
4
.

Going back to the case of the finite Weyl group, we get the validity of Theorem
1.1
when
\Phi
is not of type
G_{2}
.

Theorem 2.8
.

Suppose that
\Phi
is not of type
G_{2}
, and let
w\in W
. Then
\mathfrak{a}_{w}
is spherical if and only if
w
is fully commutative.

Proof.

Let
w\in W
, regarded inside
\widehat{W}
via the inclusion
\Delta\subset\widehat{\Delta}
. Then
\widehat{\Phi}(w)=\Phi(w)
, thus by Corollary
2.6
we have

w\in W_{\!f\!c}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle\geqslant 0\quad\forall\alpha,\beta\in\Phi(w).

On the other hand
\Phi(w)\subset\Phi^{+}
is a biconvex subset, therefore the claim follows from Theorem
2.3
.
∎

2.2.
Fully commutative elements in
\widehat{W}
and spherical ideals of
\mathfrak{b}

In order to explain the case of the spherical ideals, we first need to recall how to attach to any ad-nilpotent ideal of
\mathfrak{b}
an element in
\widehat{W}
. The construction is due to Cellini and Papi
[
4
]
, and is inspired by Peterson’s classification of the abelian ideals of
\mathfrak{b}
(see
[
12
]
).

Given an ad-nilpotent ideal
\mathfrak{a}\subset\mathfrak{b}
we denote by
\Psi_{\mathfrak{a}}\subset\Phi^{+}
the corresponding combinatorial ideal, namely the set of positive roots defined by the equality
\mathfrak{a}=\mathfrak{a}_{\Psi_{\mathfrak{a}}}
.

Set
\Psi^{(1)}_{\mathfrak{a}}=\Psi_{\mathfrak{a}}
. For all integer
k>1
, define inductively

\Psi^{(k)}_{\mathfrak{a}}=(\Psi^{(k-1)}_{\mathfrak{a}}+\Psi_{\mathfrak{a}})\cap\Phi^{+}.

Since
\mathfrak{a}
is an ideal of
\mathfrak{b}
, we have
\Psi^{(k)}_{\mathfrak{a}}\subset\Psi_{\mathfrak{a}}
for all
k>0
. Define now a set of positive roots
\widehat{\Psi}_{\mathfrak{a}}\subset\widehat{\Phi}^{+}
, setting

\widehat{\Psi}_{\mathfrak{a}}=\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\{k\delta-\alpha\;|\;\alpha\in\Psi^{(k)}_{\mathfrak{a}}\}.

This is indeed a finite biconvex set of positive roots, which yields the following theorem.

Theorem 2.9

(
[
4
, Theorem 2.6]
)
.

If
\mathfrak{a}\subset\mathfrak{b}
is an ad-nilpotent ideal, then there exists
w_{\mathfrak{a}}\in\widehat{W}
such that
\widehat{\Psi}_{\mathfrak{a}}=\widehat{\Phi}(w_{\mathfrak{a}})
.

If
\mathfrak{a}
is abelian, then by definition we have

\Psi_{\mathfrak{a}}^{(k)}=\emptyset
for all
k>1
.
If instead
\mathfrak{a}
is spherical, then we have

\Psi_{\mathfrak{a}}^{(k)}=\emptyset
for all
k>2
(see
[
16
, Proposition 4.1]
).

Remark 2.10
.

Notice that
\mathfrak{a}
is abelian if and only if
w_{\mathfrak{a}}
is commutative. Since
\langle m\delta-\alpha,n\delta-\beta\rangle=\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle
, it holds moreover

\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle\geqslant 0\quad\forall\alpha,\beta\in\Psi_{\mathfrak{a}}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle\geqslant 0\quad\forall\alpha,\beta\in\widehat{\Psi}_{\mathfrak{a}}.

We can now deduce the validity of Theorem
1.3
when
\Phi
is not of type
G_{2}
.

Theorem 2.11
.

Suppose that
\Phi
is not of type
G_{2}
, and let
\mathfrak{a}\subset\mathfrak{b}
be an ad-nilpotent ideal. Then
\mathfrak{a}
is spherical if and only if
w_{\mathfrak{a}}
is fully commutative.

Proof.

By construction we have
\widehat{\Phi}(w_{\mathfrak{a}})=\widehat{\Psi}_{\mathfrak{a}}
. Thus by Corollary
2.6
together with Remark
2.10
we get

w_{\mathfrak{a}}\in\widehat{W}_{\!f\!c}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle\geqslant 0\quad\forall\alpha,\beta\in\Psi_{\mathfrak{a}}.

On the other hand
\Psi_{\mathfrak{a}}\subset\Phi^{+}
is a combinatorial ideal, therefore the claim follows from Theorem
2.3
.
∎

3.
Proof of Theorem
2.3

Before going into the proof of Theorem
2.3
we fix some further notation. We denote by
\theta
the highest root in
\Phi
. We also denote by
\theta_{s}
the highest short root if
\Phi
is non-simply laced, and we set
\theta_{s}=\theta
when
\Phi
is simply laced. For all
\alpha\in\Phi
, we fix a non-zero element
e_{\alpha}\in\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
, and we denote by
U_{\alpha}\subset G
the root subgroup with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
. In particular
U_{\alpha}
is the image of a one parameter subgroup
u_{\alpha}:\mathbb{C}\rightarrow G
, whose action on
\mathfrak{g}
is obtained by exponentiating the adjoint action of
e_{\alpha}
:

u_{\alpha}(\xi).x=x+\sum_{k>0}\frac{\xi^{k}}{k!}\,\ad(e_{\alpha})^{k}(x).

Finally, if
x=\sum_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}}c_{\alpha}e_{\alpha}
is an element in
\mathfrak{n}
, we define the
support
of
x
as

\supp(x)=\{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}\;|\;c_{\alpha}\neq 0\}.

From now on, we will assume throughout this section that
\Phi
is an irreducible root system not of type
G_{2}
. The necessity of the condition of Theorem
2.3
is well known (see e.g.
[
7
]
,
[
16
]
). We recall a proof in the following lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\alpha,\beta\in\Phi^{+}
be such that
\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle<0
. Then the
G
-orbit of
e_{\alpha}+e_{\beta}
is not spherical.

Proof.

Denote
x=e_{\alpha}+e_{\beta}
and let
\mathfrak{s}\subset\mathfrak{g}
be the subalgebra generated by the root spaces
\mathfrak{g}_{\pm\alpha}
,
\mathfrak{g}_{\pm\beta}
. Then
\mathfrak{s}
is a simple Lie algebra of type
A_{2}
or
C_{2}
, and
x
is a principal nilpotent element in
\mathfrak{s}
. A direct computation shows that
(\ad x_{|\mathfrak{s}})^{4}\neq 0
. Thus
(\ad x)^{4}\neq 0
, and
G.x
cannot be spherical by Panyushev’s characterization.
∎

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
\Psi\subset\Phi^{+}
and suppose that
\mathfrak{a}_{\Psi}
is a spherical subspace. Then
\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle\geqslant 0
for all
\alpha,\beta\in\Psi
.

We now consider the other implication of Theorem
2.3
.

If
x\in\mathfrak{a}_{\Psi}
for some
\Psi\subset\Phi^{+}
, then
(\ad x)^{4}
breaks into a sum of monomials of the shape

\ad(x_{\gamma_{1}})\ad(x_{\gamma_{2}})\ad(x_{\gamma_{3}})\ad(x_{\gamma_{4}})

with
\gamma_{i}\in\Psi
and
x_{\gamma_{i}}\in\mathfrak{g}_{\gamma_{i}}
for every
i=1,2,3,4
. In particular, if one of such monomials is non-zero, there must exist
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3},\gamma_{4}\in\Psi
and
\alpha,\beta\in\Phi\cup\{0\}
such that

\alpha=\beta+\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}+\gamma_{3}+\gamma_{4}.

This leads us to consider similar configurations of roots.

Remark 3.3
.

Let
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3},\gamma_{4}\in\Phi^{+}
be (not necessarily distinct) such that
\langle\gamma_{i},\gamma_{j}\rangle\geqslant 0
for all
i,j
, and let
\alpha,\beta\in\Phi\cup\{0\}
be such that

\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}+\gamma_{3}+\gamma_{4}=\alpha-\beta.

Notice that
\alpha\in\Phi\smallsetminus\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3},\gamma_{4}\}
. If indeed
\alpha=0
, then we get a contradiction because

||\beta||^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{4}||\gamma_{i}||^{2}+2\sum_{i<j}(\gamma_{i},\gamma_{j})\geqslant\sum_{i=1}^{4}||\gamma_{i}||^{2}\geqslant 4||\theta_{s}||^{2}.

Similarly, if
\alpha\in\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3},\gamma_{4}\}
, then
||\beta||^{2}\geqslant 3||\theta_{s}||^{2}
, which is absurd because
\Phi
is not of type
G_{2}
. Analogously, we also have
-\beta\in\Phi\smallsetminus\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3},\gamma_{4}\}
.

Notice also that
\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3},\gamma_{4}\}
has at least cardinality 2. Indeed every root string in
\Phi
has length less than 4.

We say that a subset
\Gamma\subset\Phi
is
orthogonal
if
\langle\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\rangle=0
for all
\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\in\Gamma
with
\gamma\neq\gamma^{\prime}
. The following lemma is the technical core of the proof.

Lemma 3.4
.

Suppose that
\Phi
is not of type
G_{2}
. Let
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3},\gamma_{4}\in\Phi^{+}
be (not necessarily distinct) such that
\langle\gamma_{i},\gamma_{j}\rangle\geqslant 0
for all
i,j
, and denote
\Gamma=\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3},\gamma_{4}\}
. Suppose that
\Gamma
is not orthogonal, and that

(1)

\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}+\gamma_{3}+\gamma_{4}=\alpha-\beta

for some
\alpha,\beta\in\Phi
. Then
\Phi
is doubly laced, and
\alpha=-\beta
is a long root. Moreover there is at most one index
i_{0}
such that
\gamma_{i_{0}}
is a long root, and
\langle\gamma_{i_{0}},\gamma_{j}\rangle=0
for all
j\neq i_{0}
.

Proof.

We split the proof into several remarks and claims.

Notice that everything we will prove for
\alpha
will be true for
-\beta
as well, hence for
\beta
with the obvious modifications. Indeed
\alpha-\beta=(-\beta)-(-\alpha)
, thus it is enough to replace
\alpha
with
-\beta
, and
\beta
with
-\alpha
.

Remark 1
.

For all
\gamma\in\Gamma
, it holds
\langle\alpha,\gamma\rangle\geqslant 0
and
\langle\beta,\gamma\rangle\leqslant 0
.

We only consider the first statement, the other one is similar. By (
1
) we have

\langle\alpha,\gamma\rangle\geqslant\langle\beta,\gamma\rangle+\langle\gamma,\gamma\rangle=\langle\beta,\gamma\rangle+2.

Thus we conclude because
\Phi
is not of type
G_{2}
.

\bigtriangleup

Remark 2
.

It holds
\sum_{i=1}^{4}\langle\gamma_{i},\alpha\rangle\leqslant 4
, with strict inequality if
\alpha
is long and
\alpha\neq-\beta
.

By (
1
) we have

\sum_{i=1}^{4}\langle\gamma_{i},\alpha\rangle=\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle-\langle\beta,\alpha\rangle\leqslant 2-\langle\beta,\alpha\rangle.

Thus we conclude because
\Phi
is not of type
G_{2}
.

\bigtriangleup

Claim 3
.

\Phi
is doubly laced.

By (
1
) we have

(2)

\frac{||\beta||^{2}}{||\theta_{s}||^{2}}=\frac{||\alpha||^{2}}{||\theta_{s}||^{2}}+\sum_{i<j}\frac{||\gamma_{j}||^{2}}{||\theta_{s}||^{2}}\langle\gamma_{i},\gamma_{j}\rangle+\sum_{i=1}^{4}\frac{||\gamma_{i}||^{2}}{||\theta_{s}||^{2}}(1-\langle\alpha,\gamma_{i}\rangle)

Then the middle term on the right hand side is positive by the assumption on
\Gamma
. On the other hand
\alpha\not\in\Gamma
by Remark
3.3
, therefore if
\Phi
is simply laced the last term of the equation is also non-negative, yielding
||\alpha||<||\beta||
, a contradiction.

\bigtriangleup

Since
\Phi
is doubly laced, we also get the following formula for the last term appearing in (
2
), which will be useful later:

(3)

\frac{||\gamma_{i}||^{2}}{||\theta_{s}||^{2}}(1-\langle\alpha,\gamma_{i}\rangle)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}2&\text{if $\gamma_{i}$ is long and $\langle\alpha,\gamma_{i}\rangle=0$}\\
1&\text{if $\gamma_{i}$ is short and $\langle\alpha,\gamma_{i}\rangle=0$}\\
0&\text{if $\langle\alpha,\gamma_{i}\rangle=1$}\\
-1&\text{if $\langle\alpha,\gamma_{i}\rangle=2$}\end{array}\right.

Claim 4
.

Both
\alpha
and
\beta
are long roots.

We show that
\alpha
is long, the case of
\beta
being similar.

Suppose that
\alpha
is short. Since
\alpha\not\in\Gamma
by Remark
3.3
, it follows that
\langle\alpha,\gamma_{i}\rangle\leqslant 1
for all
i
, thus all the terms appearing in (
2
) are non-negative. On the other hand, by the assumption on
\Gamma
, the second term is positive. Therefore
||\beta||>||\alpha||
, namely
\beta
is long and
||\beta||^{2}=2||\alpha||^{2}
because
\Phi
is not of type
G_{2}
. Moreover, we see that the third term on the right hand side of (
2
) must be 0, namely it must be
\langle\alpha,\gamma_{i}\rangle=1
for all
i
. Thus
\langle\gamma_{i},\alpha\rangle\geqslant 1
for all
i
, hence
\langle\gamma_{i},\alpha\rangle=1
for all
i
, thanks to Remark
2
. In particular,
\Gamma
contains only short roots.

For every
i=1,\ldots,4
, it follows from (
1
) that

\langle\beta,\gamma_{i}\rangle=\langle\alpha,\gamma_{i}\rangle-\sum_{j=1}^{4}\langle\gamma_{j},\gamma_{i}\rangle=-1-\sum_{j\neq i}\langle\gamma_{j},\gamma_{i}\rangle<0

Since
\beta
is long and all the
\gamma_{i}
are short, it follows that
\langle\beta,\gamma_{i}\rangle=-2
for all
i
. Thus for every
i
there is a (unique)
j\neq i
such that
\langle\gamma_{i},\gamma_{j}\rangle\neq 0
. Summing both over
i
and
j
, it follows that

\sum_{i<j}\langle\gamma_{i},\gamma_{j}\rangle\geqslant 2

Thus by (
2
) it follows that
||\beta||^{2}\geqslant 3||\theta_{s}||^{2}
, which is absurd because
\Phi
is not of type
G_{2}
.

\bigtriangleup

Remark 5
.

If
\gamma_{i}
is long, then
\langle\gamma_{i},\gamma_{j}\rangle=0
for all
j\neq i
.

Indeed by (
1
) we have
0\leqslant\sum_{j\neq i}\langle\gamma_{j},\gamma_{i}\rangle=\langle\alpha,\gamma_{i}\rangle-\langle\beta,\gamma_{i}\rangle-2
. On the other hand
\alpha,-\beta\not\in\Gamma
by Remark
3.3
, therefore the latter is non-positive by Remark
1
.

\bigtriangleup

Claim 6
.

There is at most one index
i
such that
\gamma_{i}
is long.

If there are three long roots among the
\gamma_{i}
, then
\Gamma
is necessarily orthogonal by Remark
5
, a contradiction. Suppose that there are exactly two long roots, say
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
. Since
\Gamma
is not orthogonal, by Remark
5
the short roots
\gamma_{3}
and
\gamma_{4}
must be different and non-orthogonal: hence it must be
\langle\gamma_{3},\gamma_{4}\rangle=1
. On the other hand
\Phi
is doubly laced by Claim
3
, and both
\alpha
and
\beta
are long roots by Claim
4
. Thus
\langle\alpha,\gamma_{4}\rangle
and
\langle\beta,\gamma_{4}\rangle
are both even integers. But this yields a contradiction with (
1
), indeed

\langle\alpha,\gamma_{4}\rangle-\langle\beta,\gamma_{4}\rangle=\langle\gamma_{3},\gamma_{4}\rangle+2=3

thanks to Remark
5
.
\bigtriangleup

Remark 7
.

There is at most one index
i
such that
\langle\alpha,\gamma_{i}\rangle=0
. In particular,
\sum_{i=1}^{4}\langle\gamma_{i},\alpha\rangle\geqslant 3
, with equality if and only if such an index exists.

Suppose that
\langle\alpha,\gamma_{1}\rangle=\langle\alpha,\gamma_{2}\rangle=0
. Recall that
\alpha
and
\beta
are both long by Claim
4
. Using the assumption on
\Gamma
, the equalities (
2
) and (
3
) imply that

0>\sum_{i=1}^{4}\frac{||\gamma_{i}||^{2}}{||\theta_{s}||^{2}}(1-\langle\alpha,\gamma_{i}\rangle)\geqslant 2+\sum_{i=3}^{4}\frac{||\gamma_{i}||^{2}}{||\theta_{s}||^{2}}(1-\langle\alpha,\gamma_{i}\rangle)\geqslant 0,

a contradiction. Since
\alpha
is long, the last claim follows as well.

\bigtriangleup

Claim 8
.

Suppose that
\sum_{i=1}^{4}\langle\gamma_{i},\alpha\rangle=3
. Then we can enumerate
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3},\gamma_{4}
in such a way that

\displaystyle\langle\alpha,\gamma_{1}\rangle=\langle\gamma_{2},\gamma_{1}\rangle=\langle\gamma_{3},\gamma_{1}\rangle=\langle\gamma_{4},\gamma_{1}\rangle=0,

\displaystyle-\langle\beta,\gamma_{1}\rangle=\langle\alpha,\gamma_{2}\rangle=\langle\alpha,\gamma_{3}\rangle=\langle\alpha,\gamma_{4}\rangle=2

In particular,
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3},\gamma_{4}
are all short roots.

By Remark
7
, there is a unique index
i
such that
\langle\gamma_{i},\alpha\rangle=0
. Assume
\langle\gamma_{1},\alpha\rangle=0
.

By Claim
4
both
\alpha
and
\beta
are long. Therefore, by the assumption on
\Gamma
, equation (
2
) yields

0>\sum_{i=1}^{4}\frac{||\gamma_{i}||^{2}}{||\theta_{s}||^{2}}(1-\langle\alpha,\gamma_{i}\rangle)\geqslant 1+\sum_{i=2}^{4}\frac{||\gamma_{i}||^{2}}{||\theta_{s}||^{2}}(1-\langle\alpha,\gamma_{i}\rangle)

By (
3
), every term in the last sum is greater than or equal to
-1
. Thus at least two of its summands are negative, say
\langle\alpha,\gamma_{2}\rangle=\langle\alpha,\gamma_{3}\rangle=2
. In particular, being
\alpha\not\in\Gamma
by Remark
3.3
, it follows that
\gamma_{2}
and
\gamma_{3}
are short roots.

On the other hand
\langle\alpha,\gamma_{1}\rangle=0
, thus by (
1
) we get

\langle\beta,\gamma_{1}\rangle=-\sum_{i\geqslant 1}\langle\gamma_{i},\gamma_{1}\rangle=-2-\sum_{i>1}\langle\gamma_{i},\gamma_{1}\rangle\leqslant-2

Therefore
\langle\beta,\gamma_{1}\rangle=-2
and
\langle\gamma_{i},\gamma_{1}\rangle=0
for all
i>1
. In particular, being
-\beta\not\in\Gamma
by Remark
3.3
, it follows that
\gamma_{1}
is also a short root.

It only remains to show that
\langle\alpha,\gamma_{4}\rangle=2
. We already know
\langle\alpha,\gamma_{4}\rangle>0
, suppose
\langle\alpha,\gamma_{4}\rangle=1
. Since
\alpha
is long, it follows that
\gamma_{4}
is long as well. Thus by Remark
5
we get
\langle\gamma_{i},\gamma_{4}\rangle=0
for all
i<4
. By equation (
2
) it follows then

\langle\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3}\rangle=-\sum_{i=1}^{4}\frac{||\gamma_{i}||^{2}}{||\theta_{s}||^{2}}(1-\langle\alpha,\gamma_{i}\rangle),

and computing the right hand side we get
\langle\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3}\rangle=1
. Thus by (
1
) we get

\langle\beta,\gamma_{3}\rangle=\langle\alpha,\gamma_{3}\rangle-\langle\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3}\rangle-\langle\gamma_{3},\gamma_{3}\rangle=-1

On the other hand
\beta
is long and
\gamma_{3}
is short, thus
\langle\beta,\gamma_{3}\rangle
must be even. This yields a contradiction, and we conclude that
\langle\alpha,\gamma_{4}\rangle=2
.

\bigtriangleup

Conclusion of the proof.

Recall that
\alpha
is a long root by Claim
4
. In order to prove that
\beta=-\alpha
, by Remark
2
it is therefore enough to show that
\sum_{i=1}^{4}\langle\gamma_{i},\alpha\rangle=4
.

Suppose that this is not the case. Then by Remark
7
we have
\sum_{i=1}^{4}\langle\gamma_{i},\alpha\rangle=3
. Thus by Claim
8
equality (
2
) reduces to

(4)

\langle\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3}\rangle+\langle\gamma_{2},\gamma_{4}\rangle+\langle\gamma_{3},\gamma_{4}\rangle=2

By Claim
8
and equation (
1
), evaluating
\langle\beta,\gamma_{i}\rangle
for
i>1
yields the system

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\langle\beta,\gamma_{2}\rangle+\langle\gamma_{3},\gamma_{2}\rangle+\langle\gamma_{4},\gamma_{2}\rangle=0\\
\langle\beta,\gamma_{3}\rangle+\langle\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3}\rangle+\langle\gamma_{4},\gamma_{3}\rangle=0\\
\langle\beta,\gamma_{4}\rangle+\langle\gamma_{2},\gamma_{4}\rangle+\langle\gamma_{3},\gamma_{4}\rangle=0\end{array}\right.

Notice that
\langle\gamma_{i},\gamma_{j}\rangle=\langle\gamma_{j},\gamma_{i}\rangle
for all
i,j
: indeed the
\gamma_{i}
all have the same length by Claim
8
. Moreover
\beta
is long and all the
\gamma_{i}
are short, thus
\langle\beta,\gamma_{i}\rangle
is either
0
or
-2
for all
i
.

If
\langle\gamma_{i},\gamma_{j}\rangle=1
for some
i,j
, then we get
\langle\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3}\rangle=\langle\gamma_{2},\gamma_{4}\rangle=\langle\gamma_{3},\gamma_{4}\rangle=1
,
contradicting (
4
). Thus the pairings
\langle\gamma_{i},\gamma_{j}\rangle
can only take values 0 or 2.
On the other hand, since all the
\gamma_{i}
have the same length,
\langle\gamma_{i},\gamma_{j}\rangle=2
if and only if
\gamma_{i}=\gamma_{j}
. It follows that
\Gamma
is an orthogonal set, a contradiction.
∎

Example 3.5
.

Examples of roots as in Lemma
3.4
are the followings.

i)

Suppose that
\Phi
is of type
B_{n}
, represented in the usual way in terms of an orthonormal basis
\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n}
of the vector space
\langle\Phi\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}
. Take as a set of positive roots

\Phi^{+}=\{\varepsilon_{i}\;|\;i=1,\ldots,n\}\cup\{\varepsilon_{i}\pm\varepsilon_{j}\;|\;1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n\}.

Then we can take
\gamma_{1}=\gamma_{2}=\varepsilon_{i}
and
\gamma_{3}=\gamma_{4}=\varepsilon_{j}
, whenever
i\neq j
.

ii)

Suppose that
\Phi
is of type
C_{n}
, represented in the usual way in terms of an orthonormal basis
\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n}
of the vector space
\langle\Phi\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}
. Take as a set of positive roots

\Phi^{+}=\{\varepsilon_{i}\pm\varepsilon_{j}\;|\;1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n\}\cup\{2\varepsilon_{i}\;|\;i=1,\ldots,n\}.

Then we can take
\gamma_{1}=\varepsilon_{i}+\varepsilon_{j}
,
\gamma_{2}=\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{j}
,
\gamma_{3}=\varepsilon_{i}+\varepsilon_{k}
,
\gamma_{4}=\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{k}
, whenever
i\neq j
and
i\neq k
.

iii)

When
\Phi
is of type
F_{4}
, it is indeed possibile to have a configuration of roots in the previous lemma with a long root inside
\Gamma
. Let
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3},\alpha_{4}
be the simple roots of
\Phi
, enumerated as in
[
2
]
. Then we can take

\bullet

\gamma_{1}=\alpha_{1}
,

\bullet

\gamma_{2}=\alpha_{1}+2\alpha_{2}+2\alpha_{3}+\alpha_{4}
,

\bullet

\gamma_{3}=\alpha_{1}+2\alpha_{2}+3\alpha_{3}+\alpha_{4}
,

\bullet

\gamma_{4}=\alpha_{1}+2\alpha_{2}+3\alpha_{3}+2\alpha_{4}
,

\bullet

\alpha=2\alpha_{1}+3\alpha_{2}+4\alpha_{3}+2\alpha_{4}
.

Before proving the second implication of Theorem
2.3
, we consider the case of a subspace
\mathfrak{a}_{\Gamma}\subset\mathfrak{n}
, where
\Gamma
either is an orthogonal set of roots, or satisfies the assumptions of Lemma
3.4
.

We first consider the case of an orthogonal set
\Gamma\subset\Phi^{+}
, and show that, if it occurs inside a biconvex subset or in a combinatorial ideal of
\Phi^{+}
which satisfies the condition of Theorem
2.3
, then
\mathfrak{a}_{\Gamma}
is a spherical subspace of
\mathfrak{n}
.

If
\Gamma\subset\Phi^{+}
, let
\Phi_{\Gamma}=\langle\Gamma\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}\cap\Phi
be the parabolic subsystem of
\Phi
generated by
\Gamma
.

Remark 3.6
.

Subsets of orthogonal roots
\Gamma\subset\Phi
giving rise to non-spherical nilpotent orbits were studied in
[
8
]
,
[
9
]
, and classified in
[
5
, Proposition 3.7]
. In particular, if
\Phi
is not of type
G_{2}
and
G.x_{\Gamma}
is not a spherical orbit, we only have the following possibilities:

(
D_{4}
)

There are four pairwise distinct roots
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3},\gamma_{4}
in
\Gamma
such that

\tfrac{1}{2}(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}+\gamma_{3}+\gamma_{4})\in\Phi.

If
\Gamma_{0}=\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3},\gamma_{4}\}
, then
\Phi_{\Gamma_{0}}
is a root system of type
D_{4}
.

(
B\!F_{4}
)

There are four pairwise distinct long roots
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3},\gamma_{4}
in
\Gamma
such that

\tfrac{1}{2}(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})\in\Phi\qquad\text{and}\qquad\tfrac{1}{2}(\gamma_{3}+\gamma_{4})\in\Phi.

If
\Gamma_{0}=\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3},\gamma_{4}\}
, then
\Phi_{\Gamma_{0}}
is a root system of type
B_{4}
or
F_{4}
.

(
B_{3}
)

There are two distinct long roots
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
and a short root
\gamma_{3}
in
\Gamma
such that

\tfrac{1}{2}(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}+2\gamma_{3})\in\Phi.

If
\Gamma_{0}=\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3}\}
, then
\Phi_{\Gamma_{0}}
is a root system of type
B_{3}
.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\Psi\subset\Phi^{+}
be either a combinatorial ideal or a biconvex subset, and suppose that
\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle\geqslant 0
for all
\alpha,\beta\in\Psi
. Let
\Gamma\subset\Psi
be an orthogonal subset, then
\mathfrak{a}_{\Gamma}
is a spherical subspace of
\mathfrak{n}
.

Proof.

Arguing by contradiction, we show that if
\Gamma
contains a subset
\Gamma_{0}
as in cases (
D_{4}
), (
B\!F_{4}
), (
B_{3}
) of Remark
3.6
, then
\Psi
cannot satisfy the assumptions of the lemma.

(
D_{4}
)

Notice that
\{\gamma_{1},-\tfrac{1}{2}(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}+\gamma_{3}+\gamma_{4}),\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3}\}
is a base for
\Phi_{\Gamma_{0}}
. Since it is a root system of type
D_{4}
, it follows that

\Phi_{\Gamma_{0}}=\{\pm\gamma_{1},\pm\gamma_{2},\pm\gamma_{3},\pm\gamma_{4}\}\cup\{\tfrac{1}{2}(\pm\gamma_{1}\pm\gamma_{2}\pm\gamma_{3}\pm\gamma_{4})\}

Since
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3},\gamma_{4}
are all positive, notice that
\Phi_{\Gamma_{0}}\cap\Phi^{+}
must contain at least three elements of the shape

\tfrac{1}{2}(\pm\gamma_{1}\pm\gamma_{2}\pm\gamma_{3}\pm\gamma_{4})

having two coefficients equal to 1 and two coefficients equal to
-1
.
Therefore, up to reordering the
\gamma_{i}
’s, we can assume that

\beta:=\tfrac{1}{2}(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}-\gamma_{3}-\gamma_{4})\qquad\qquad\beta^{\prime}:=\tfrac{1}{2}(\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}-\gamma_{3}+\gamma_{4})

are both positive roots. Notice that
\beta,\beta^{\prime}\in\Phi^{+}\smallsetminus\Psi
by the assumption on
\Psi
: indeed
\langle\beta,\gamma_{3}\rangle=\langle\beta^{\prime},\gamma_{3}\rangle=-1
. Similarly
\beta+\gamma_{3}\in\Phi^{+}\smallsetminus\Psi
, because
\langle\beta+\gamma_{3},\gamma_{4}\rangle=-1
. Therefore
\Psi
cannot be a combinatorial ideal. On the other hand
\gamma_{1}=(\beta+\gamma_{3})+\beta^{\prime}
, therefore
\Psi
cannot be a biconvex subset either.

(
B\!F_{4}
)

Notice that the long roots of a root system of type
B_{4}
or
F_{4}
form a root system of type
D_{4}
. In particular, the set of the long roots
\Phi^{\ell}_{\Gamma_{0}}
in
\Phi_{\Gamma_{0}}
is a root system of type
D_{4}
.

Recall that two roots
\alpha,\beta
are called
strongly orthogonal
if
\alpha\pm\beta\not\in\Phi
. The following properties are easily shown:

i)

In types
B_{4}
and
F_{4}
, two orthogonal short roots are never strongly orthogonal.

ii)

In type
F_{4}
, the half sum of two orthogonal long roots is always a root.

As in case (
D_{4}
), it follows that

\Phi^{\ell}_{\Gamma_{0}}=\{\pm\gamma_{1},\pm\gamma_{2},\pm\gamma_{3},\pm\gamma_{4}\}\cup\{\tfrac{1}{2}(\pm\gamma_{1}\pm\gamma_{2}\pm\gamma_{3}\pm\gamma_{4})\}

Thus we can conclude as in the previous case.

(
B_{3}
)

Notice that
\{\gamma_{1},-\tfrac{1}{2}(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}+2\gamma_{3}),\gamma_{3}\}
is a base for
\Phi_{\Gamma_{0}}
. Since it is a root system of type
B_{3}
, it follows that

\Phi_{\Gamma_{0}}=\{\pm\gamma_{1},\pm\gamma_{2},\pm\gamma_{3}\}\cup\{\tfrac{1}{2}(\pm\gamma_{1}\pm\gamma_{2})\}\cup\{\tfrac{1}{2}(\pm\gamma_{1}\pm\gamma_{2}\pm 2\gamma_{3})\}

Up to switching
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
, we can assume that

\beta=\tfrac{1}{2}(\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2})

is a positive root. Notice that
\beta\in\Phi^{+}\smallsetminus\Psi
, and that
\beta+\gamma_{3}\in\Phi^{+}\smallsetminus\Psi
as well: indeed
\langle\beta,\gamma_{2}\rangle=\langle\beta+\gamma_{3},\gamma_{2}\rangle=-1
. Thus by the assumption
\Psi
cannot be a combinatorial ideal. On the other hand, if
\Psi
is a biconvex subset, consider the roots

\beta^{\prime}:=\tfrac{1}{2}(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}-2\gamma_{3})\qquad\qquad\beta^{\prime\prime}=\tfrac{1}{2}(-\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}+2\gamma_{3}).

Notice that either
\beta^{\prime}\in\Phi^{+}
or
\beta^{\prime\prime}\in\Phi^{+}
, and that none of them can be in
\Psi
since they assume negative values respectively against
\gamma_{3}
and
\gamma_{1}
. On the other hand
\gamma_{1}=(\beta+\gamma_{3})+\beta^{\prime}
and
\gamma_{3}=\beta+\beta^{\prime\prime}
, thus the biconvexity of
\Psi
is contradicted in both cases.
∎

We now consider the non-orthogonal cases arising from Lemma
3.4
. Let
\Gamma=\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3},\gamma_{4}\}
be a set of positive roots satisfying the hypotheses of Lemma
3.4
, and suppose that
\Gamma
is contained in a combinatorial ideal or in a biconvex subset satisfying the condition of Theorem
2.3
. In order to prove that
\mathfrak{a}_{\Gamma}
is spherical, we first show that
\Gamma
does not contain any long root.

Lemma 3.8
.

Suppose that
\Phi
is doubly laced, and let
\Psi\subset\Phi^{+}
be either a combinatorial ideal or a biconvex subset such that such that
\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle\geqslant 0
for all
\alpha,\beta\in\Psi
. Let
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3},\gamma_{4}\in\Psi
(not necessarily distinct) be such that
\tfrac{1}{2}(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}+\gamma_{3}+\gamma_{4})\in\Phi
, and suppose that
\Gamma=\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3},\gamma_{4}\}
is not orthogonal. Then
\Gamma
does not contain any long root.

Proof.

Denote
\alpha=\tfrac{1}{2}(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}+\gamma_{3}+\gamma_{4})
. By Lemma
3.4
, we know that
\alpha
is a long root not in
\Gamma
. Therefore the equality

4=\langle 2\alpha,\alpha\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{4}\langle\gamma_{i},\alpha\rangle

implies that
\langle\gamma_{i},\alpha\rangle=1
for all
i
.

Suppose that
\gamma_{1}
is a long root. Then Lemma
3.4
shows that
\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3},\gamma_{4}
are all short roots, and that
\langle\gamma_{1},\gamma_{i}\rangle=0
for all
i>1
. Since
\langle\gamma_{i},\alpha\rangle=1
, for every
i=2,3,4
it follows that
\langle\alpha,\gamma_{i}\rangle=2
.

Notice that
\langle\gamma_{i},\gamma_{j}\rangle=1
whenever
i,j>1
and
i\neq j
. Indeed

4=\langle 2\alpha,\gamma_{i}\rangle=2+\sum_{j\in\{2,3,4\}\smallsetminus\{i\}}\langle\gamma_{j},\gamma_{i}\rangle

for every
i=2,3,4
. In particular, up to reordering we may assume that
\gamma_{i}-\gamma_{j}\in\Phi^{+}
whenever
2\leqslant i<j\leqslant 4
.

Consider now the roots

\beta_{1}=-\gamma_{1},\qquad\beta_{2}=\alpha-2\gamma_{2},\qquad\beta_{3}=\gamma_{2},\qquad\beta_{4}=-\gamma_{3}

It follows from the preceding remarks that
\big(\langle\beta_{i},\beta_{j}\rangle\big)_{i,j}
is a Cartan matrix of type
F_{4}
. Since two short (resp. long) roots in a root system of type
B
(resp.
C
) are always orthogonal, it follows that
\Phi
is itself of type
F_{4}
, and that
\{\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\beta_{3},\beta_{4}\}
is a base for
\Phi
. In particular, it follows that

\displaystyle\gamma_{2}-\gamma_{3}+\gamma_{4}=\beta_{1}+2\beta_{2}+4\beta_{3}+2\beta_{4}

\displaystyle\gamma_{2}+\gamma_{3}-\gamma_{4}=-\beta_{1}-2\beta_{2}-2\beta_{3}-2\beta_{4}

are both in
\Phi
, hence in
\Phi^{+}
because
\gamma_{2}-\gamma_{3}
and
\gamma_{3}-\gamma_{4}
are both positive.

Denote
\Gamma_{1}=\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}-\gamma_{3}+\gamma_{4},\gamma_{3}\}
and

\Gamma_{2}=\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}+\gamma_{3}-\gamma_{4},\gamma_{4}\}
. Then
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
are two orthogonal sets of roots as in Case (
B_{3}
) of Remark
3.6
, thus by Lemma
3.7
they cannot occur as subsets of
\Psi
. It follows that
\gamma_{2}-\gamma_{3}+\gamma_{4}\in\Phi^{+}\smallsetminus\Psi
and
\gamma_{2}+\gamma_{3}-\gamma_{4}\in\Phi^{+}\smallsetminus\Psi
.

It follows that
\Psi
cannot be a biconvex subset: indeed

2\gamma_{2}=(\gamma_{2}-\gamma_{3}+\gamma_{4})+(\gamma_{2}+\gamma_{3}-\gamma_{4}),

therefore
\gamma_{2}\not\in\Psi
, a contradiction.

Similarly, it follows that
\Psi
cannot be a combinatorial ideal either: indeed

\gamma_{2}+\gamma_{3}-\gamma_{4}=\gamma_{2}+(\gamma_{3}-\gamma_{4}),

therefore
\gamma_{2}+\gamma_{3}-\gamma_{4}\in\Psi
, a contradiction.
∎

Let now
\Psi\subset\Phi^{+}
be a combinatorial ideal or a biconvex subset such that
\langle\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\rangle\geqslant 0
for all
\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\in\Psi
. Suppose that
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3},\gamma_{4}\in\Psi
satisfy an equality of the shape

\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}+\gamma_{3}+\gamma_{4}=\alpha-\beta

for some
\alpha,\beta\in\Phi\cup\{0\}
, and
denote
\Gamma=\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3},\gamma_{4}\}
. Then by Lemma
3.4
and Lemma
3.8
either
\Gamma
is orthogonal, or
\Phi
is doubly laced and
\Gamma
does not contain any long root. If
\Gamma
is orthogonal, then
\mathfrak{a}_{\Gamma}
is a spherical subspace of
\mathfrak{n}
by Lemma
3.7
. The following lemma shows that
\mathfrak{a}_{\Gamma}
is spherical in the second situation as well.

Lemma 3.9
.

Suppose that
\Phi
is doubly laced. Let
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3},\gamma_{4}\in\Phi^{+}
be (not necessarily distinct) short roots such that
\langle\gamma_{i},\gamma_{j}\rangle\geqslant 0
for all
i,j
. Assume that
\Gamma=\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3},\gamma_{4}\}
is not orthogonal, and that
\tfrac{1}{2}(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}+\gamma_{3}+\gamma_{4})\in\Phi
. Then, up to reordering,
\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}=\gamma_{3}+\gamma_{4}
. Moreover,
\mathfrak{a}_{\Gamma}
is a spherical subspace of
\mathfrak{n}
.

Proof.

Denote
\alpha=\tfrac{1}{2}(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}+\gamma_{3}+\gamma_{4})
. Notice that
\sum_{i=1}^{4}\langle\gamma_{i},\alpha\rangle=\langle 2\alpha,\alpha\rangle=4
. By Lemma
3.4
it follows that
\alpha
is a long root, hence
\langle\gamma_{i},\alpha\rangle=1
and
\langle\alpha,\gamma_{i}\rangle=2
for all
i=1,2,3,4
. Notice that

4=\langle 2\alpha,\gamma_{i}\rangle=2+\sum_{j=2}^{4}\langle\gamma_{j},\gamma_{i}\rangle.

Since
\langle\gamma_{j},\gamma_{i}\rangle\in\{0,1\}
for all
i\neq j
, we see that for every
i
there is precisely one index
j
such that
\langle\gamma_{j},\gamma_{i}\rangle=0
.

Up to reordering we may assume that
\langle\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\rangle=\langle\gamma_{3},\gamma_{4}\rangle=0
. Notice that
\alpha-\gamma_{1}\in\Phi
, and that
\langle\alpha-\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\rangle=2
: thus
\alpha-\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}\in\Phi\cup\{0\}
. On the other hand
\alpha-\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}
is orthogonal with
\alpha
, with
\gamma_{1}
, and with
\gamma_{2}
. Thereofore it must be
\alpha-\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}=0
, namely
\alpha=\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}=\gamma_{3}+\gamma_{4}
.

We now show that
\height(x)=2
for all
x\in\mathfrak{a}_{\Gamma}
, which implies the claim by Panyushev’s criterion. Since
\Phi
is not of type
G_{2}
, this is true if
x\in\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
for some
\alpha\in\Gamma
.

Since
\langle\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\rangle=\langle\gamma_{3},\gamma_{4}\rangle=0
, it follows that
\gamma_{2}-\gamma_{1}=s_{\gamma_{1}}(\alpha)
and
\gamma_{4}-\gamma_{3}=s_{\gamma_{3}}(\alpha)
are both in
\Phi
. Thus up to reordering we may assume that
\gamma_{2}-\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{4}-\gamma_{3}
are both in
\Phi^{+}
. Denote
\Gamma_{1}=\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\}
and
\Gamma_{2}=\{\gamma_{3},\gamma_{4}\}
.

Notice that
\height(x)=2
for all
x\in\mathfrak{a}_{\Gamma_{1}}
. Let indeed
x_{\gamma_{1}}\in\mathfrak{g}_{\gamma_{1}}
and
x_{\gamma_{2}}\in\mathfrak{g}_{\gamma_{2}}
be non-zero, and consider the action of the one parameter subgroup
u_{\gamma_{2}-\gamma_{1}}(\xi)
on
x_{\gamma_{1}}+x_{\gamma_{2}}
. Being
2\gamma_{2}-\gamma_{1}\not\in\Phi
, we can find a value of the parameter
\xi_{0}
such that

u_{\gamma_{2}-\gamma_{1}}(\xi_{0}).(x_{\gamma_{1}}+x_{\gamma_{2}})=x_{\gamma_{1}},

hence
\height(x_{\gamma_{1}}+x_{\gamma_{2}})=\height(x_{\gamma_{1}})=2
. Similarly, we have
\height(x)=2
for all
x\in\mathfrak{a}_{\Gamma_{2}}
.

Let now
x\in\mathfrak{a}_{\Gamma}
, and suppose that
x\not\in\mathfrak{a}_{\Gamma_{1}}
and
x\not\in\mathfrak{a}_{\Gamma_{2}}
. Since
\Gamma
is not orthogonal, it must be
\Gamma_{1}\neq\Gamma_{2}
. If
i\in\{1,2,3,4\}
, notice that
\gamma_{i}+\gamma_{2}-\gamma_{1}\in\Phi
if and only if
i=1
: indeed
\gamma_{2}-\gamma_{1}
is a long root, orthogonal both with
\gamma_{3}
and
\gamma_{4}
. Similarly
\gamma_{i}+\gamma_{4}-\gamma_{3}\in\Phi
if and only if
i=3
. If
x\in\mathfrak{a}_{\Gamma}
, it follows that acting with
U_{\gamma_{2}-\gamma_{1}}
and
U_{\gamma_{4}-\gamma_{3}}
we may assume that
\supp(x)=\{\gamma_{i},\gamma_{j}\}
with
\gamma_{i}\in\Gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{j}\in\Gamma_{2}
.

Write
x=x_{\gamma_{i}}+x_{\gamma_{j}}
with
x_{\gamma_{i}}\in\mathfrak{g}_{\gamma_{i}}
and
x_{\gamma_{j}}\in\mathfrak{g}_{\gamma_{j}}
. Then
\langle\gamma_{i},\gamma_{j}\rangle=1
, and
2\gamma_{i}-\gamma_{j}\not\in\Phi
. Therefore, acting with the one parameter subgroup
u_{\gamma_{i}-\gamma_{j}}(\xi)
, for a particular value of the parameter
\xi_{0}
we get

u_{\gamma_{i}-\gamma_{j}}(\xi_{0}).(x_{\gamma_{i}}+x_{\gamma_{j}})=x_{\gamma_{j}}.

Therefore
\height(x)=\height(x_{\gamma_{j}})=2
.
∎

We can now show that the condition in Theorem
2.3
is also sufficient.

Proposition 3.10
.

Let
\Psi\subset\Phi^{+}
be either a combinatorial ideal or a biconvex subset, and suppose that
\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle\geqslant 0
for all
\alpha,\beta\in\Psi
. Then
\mathfrak{a}_{\Psi}
is a spherical subspace.

Proof.

Let
x\in\mathfrak{a}_{\Psi}
. Let
\mathcal{S}\subset\Psi
be the support of
x
, and write
x=\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{S}}x_{\gamma}
with
x_{\gamma}\in\mathfrak{g}_{\gamma}
. Then

(5)

(\ad x)^{4}=\sum_{\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{4}\in\mathcal{S}}\ad(x_{\gamma_{1}})\ad(x_{\gamma_{2}})\ad(x_{\gamma_{3}})\ad(x_{\gamma_{4}})

If a monomial

\ad(x_{\gamma_{1}})\ad(x_{\gamma_{2}})\ad(x_{\gamma_{3}})\ad(x_{\gamma_{4}})

in the previous sum is non-zero, then there must exist
\alpha,\beta\in\Phi\cup\{0\}
such that

\alpha=\beta+\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}+\gamma_{3}+\gamma_{4}.

Notice that
\alpha,\beta
are necessarily in
\Phi
, thanks to Remark
3.3
.

If
\Gamma\subset\mathcal{S}
is a subset of cardinality
k\leqslant 4
, let
\mathrm{Full}_{4}(\Gamma)
be the set of all surjective sequences
(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3},\gamma_{4})
of four elements in
\Gamma
, and set

p_{\Gamma}=\sum_{(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3},\gamma_{4})\in\mathrm{Full}_{4}(\Gamma)}\;\ad(x_{\gamma_{1}})\ad(x_{\gamma_{2}})\ad(x_{\gamma_{3}})\ad(x_{\gamma_{4}})

If
\alpha,\beta\in\Phi
, let
\mathrm{P}_{4}^{+}(\mathcal{S};\alpha,\beta)
be the set of all
\Gamma\subset\mathcal{S}
of cardinality
k\leqslant 4
such that

\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}+\gamma_{3}+\gamma_{4}=\alpha-\beta

for some sequence
(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3},\gamma_{4})\in\mathrm{Full}_{4}(\Gamma)
.

Denote
\mathrm{P}_{4}^{+}(\mathcal{S})=\bigcup_{\alpha,\beta\in\Phi}\mathrm{P}_{4}^{+}(\mathcal{S};\alpha,\beta)
. By definition, every non-zero monomial occurring in (
5
) appears as a summand of
p_{\Gamma}
for a unique
\Gamma\in\mathrm{P}_{4}^{+}(\mathcal{S})
. Therefore

(\ad x)^{4}=\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathrm{P}_{4}^{+}(\mathcal{S})}\;p_{\Gamma}

We show that
p_{\Gamma}=0
for all
\Gamma\in\mathrm{P}_{4}^{+}(\mathcal{S})
, which implies the statement thanks to Panyushev’s characterization.

Let
\Gamma\in\mathrm{P}_{4}^{+}(\mathcal{S})
, and let
\alpha,\beta\in\Phi
with
\Gamma\in\mathrm{P}_{4}^{+}(\mathcal{S};\alpha,\beta)
. Then Lemma
3.4
together with Lemma
3.8
show that either
\Gamma
is an orthogonal set of roots, or
\Phi
is doubly laced,
\beta=-\alpha
and
\Gamma
does not contain any long root. Moreover,
it follows from Lemma
3.7
and Lemma
3.9
that
\mathfrak{a}_{\Gamma}
is a spherical subspace of
\mathfrak{n}
.

If
\Gamma^{\prime}\subseteq\Gamma
, set
x_{\Gamma^{\prime}}=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma^{\prime}}x_{\gamma}
. Then

0=(\ad x_{\Gamma^{\prime}})^{4}=\sum_{\Gamma^{\prime\prime}\subseteq\Gamma^{\prime}}p_{\Gamma^{\prime\prime}}

Arguing inductively on the cardinality of
\Gamma^{\prime}
, we see that
p_{\Gamma^{\prime}}=0
for all
\Gamma^{\prime}\subseteq\Gamma
.
∎

4.
The case of type
G_{2}
.

We now discuss the validity of Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.3
when
\Phi
is of type
G_{2}
. In particular, keeping the notation already introduced, we will see that the following holds.

Proposition 4.1
.

Suppose that
\Phi
is of type
G_{2}
.

i)

Let
w\in W
. Then
\mathfrak{a}_{w}
is spherical if and only if
w
is a commutative element.

ii)

Let
\mathfrak{a}\subset\mathfrak{b}
an ad-nilpotent ideal. Then
\mathfrak{a}
is spherical if and only if
w_{\mathfrak{a}}\in\widehat{W}
is a commutative element.

Concerning the second statement, it was already pointed out in
[
16
]
that in type
G_{2}
an ad-nilpotent ideal
\mathfrak{a}\subset\mathfrak{b}
is spherical if and only if it is abelian, which means that
w_{\mathfrak{a}}
is commutative.

Enumerate the base
\Delta=\{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\}
so that
||\alpha_{1}||<||\alpha_{2}||
, and denote
s_{1}=s_{\alpha_{1}}
and
s_{2}=s_{\alpha_{2}}
. We also denote by
\ell:W\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
the length function defined by
\Delta
, and by
\leqslant
the Bruhat order on
W
. Let moreover
\preceq_{L}
denote the left weak order on
W
(see
[
1
, Section 3.1]
), defined by

v\preceq_{L}w\Longleftrightarrow\Phi(v)\subseteq\Phi(w).

For
\alpha,\beta\in\Phi
, let
N_{\alpha,\beta}
be the structure constants of
\mathfrak{g}
, defined by the equalities

[e_{\alpha},e_{\beta}]=N_{\alpha,\beta}\;e_{\alpha+\beta}\qquad\qquad\text{ if }\alpha,\beta,\alpha+\beta\in\Phi.

Since the characteristic of the field is zero, we have
N_{\alpha,\beta}\neq 0
whenever
\alpha+\beta\in\Phi
.

Before proving the proposition, we first consider some nilpotent orbits in
\mathfrak{g}
which are not spherical.

Remark 4.2
.

i)

Suppose that
\alpha,\beta\in\Phi
are orthogonal roots, then
G.(e_{\alpha}+e_{\beta})
is not spherical. Indeed in this case
\alpha
and
\beta
are strongly orthogonal, and
\height(e_{\alpha}+e_{\beta})=4
(see e.g.
[
9
]
). Thus
G.(e_{\alpha}+e_{\beta})
is not spherical by Panyushev’s characterization.

ii)

Suppose that
\alpha=\alpha_{1}
and
\beta=2\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}
, then
G.(e_{\alpha}+e_{\beta})
is not spherical. Denote indeed
\gamma=\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}
, then

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad u_{\gamma}(\xi).

\displaystyle(e_{\alpha}+e_{\beta})=e_{\alpha}+e_{\beta}+\xi[e_{\gamma},e_{\alpha}+e_{\beta}]+\tfrac{1}{2}\xi^{2}[e_{\gamma},[e_{\gamma},e_{\alpha}]]=

\displaystyle=e_{\alpha_{1}}+(1+N_{\gamma,\alpha}\;\xi)\;e_{2\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}}+\tfrac{1}{2}N_{\gamma,\beta}\;\xi\;(2+N_{\gamma,\alpha}\;\xi)\;e_{3\alpha_{1}+2\alpha_{2}}

By choosing properly the value of the parameter
\xi
, it follows that

e_{\alpha_{1}}+e_{3\alpha_{1}+2\alpha_{2}}\in G.(e_{\alpha}+e_{\beta})

On the other hand
\alpha_{1}
and
3\alpha_{1}+2\alpha_{2}
are orthogonal, thus the claim follows by case i).

iii)

Suppose that
\alpha=\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}
and
\beta=2\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}
, then
G.(e_{\alpha}+e_{\beta})
is not spherical. Indeed
s_{\alpha_{2}}(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})=\alpha_{1}
and
s_{\alpha_{2}}(2\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})=2\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}
. Thus
e_{\alpha_{1}}+e_{2\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}}\in G.(e_{\alpha}+e_{\beta})
, and we conclude thanks to the case ii).

We can now analyze the variuos cases beyond Proposition
4.1
.

Proof of Proposition
4.1
.

Notice that
\mathfrak{b}
contains a unique maximal abelian ideal
\mathfrak{a}_{0}
, whose corresponding set of roots is

\Psi_{0}=\{2\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2},\;3\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2},\;3\alpha_{1}+2\alpha_{2}\}.

i) We analyze the sphericality of the various subspaces
\mathfrak{a}_{w}
with
w\in W
.
Notice that
w
is fully commutative if and only if
\ell(w)\leqslant 5
, whereas it is commutative if and only if
w\leqslant s_{2}s_{1}s_{2}
. In particular, if
w
is not commutative, then either
s_{1}s_{2}s_{1}\preceq_{L}w
or
s_{1}s_{2}s_{1}s_{2}\preceq_{L}w
.

•

Suppose that
w
is commutative, namely
w\leqslant s_{2}s_{1}s_{2}
. Then
\Phi(w)
is conjugated to a subset of
\Psi_{0}
, thus
\mathfrak{a}_{w}
is a spherical subspace.

•

Suppose that
s_{1}s_{2}s_{1}\preceq_{L}w
. Then
\Phi(w)
contains both
\alpha_{1}
and
2\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}
. By Remark
4.2
ii), the element
e_{\alpha_{1}}+e_{2\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}}
does not belong to a spherical orbit. Therefore
\mathfrak{a}_{w}
is not a spherical subspace.

•

Suppose that
s_{1}s_{2}s_{1}s_{2}\preceq_{L}w
. Then
\Phi(w)
contains both
\alpha_{2}
and
2\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}
, which is a pair of orthogonal roots. By Remark
4.2
i), the element
e_{\alpha_{2}}+e_{2\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}}
does not belong to a spherical orbit, thus
\mathfrak{a}_{w}
is not a spherical subspace.

ii) By the definition of
w_{\mathfrak{a}}
, an ad-nilpotent ideal
\mathfrak{a}\subset\mathfrak{b}
is abelian if and only if
w_{\mathfrak{a}}
is commutative. Therefore it is enough to show that every
\ad
-nilpotent ideal
\mathfrak{a}\subset\mathfrak{b}
which is not contained in
\mathfrak{a}_{0}
is not spherical. On the other hand, if
\mathfrak{a}\not\subset\mathfrak{a}_{0}
, then we necessarily have
\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}\in\Psi_{\mathfrak{a}}
: thus
2\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}\in\Psi_{\mathfrak{a}}
as well, and we conclude by Remark
4.2
iii).
∎
