Abstract
. Let X = ( X 1 , X 2 ) X=(X_{1},X_{2}) be a 2-dimensional random variable and X ⁡ ( n ) , n ∈ ℕ X(n),n\in\mathbb{N} , a sequence of i.i.d. copies of X X . The associated random walk is S ⁡ ( n ) = X ⁡ ( 1 ) + ⋯ + X ⁡ ( n ) S(n)=X(1)+\cdots+X(n) . The corresponding absorbed-reflected walk W ⁡ ( n ) , n ∈ ℕ W(n),n\in\mathbb{N} , in the first quadrant is given by W ⁡ ( 0 ) = x ∈ ℝ + 2 W(0)=x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2} and W ⁡ ( n ) = max ⁡ { 0 , W ⁡ ( n − 1 ) − X ⁡ ( n ) } W(n)=\max\{0,W(n-1)-X(n)\} , where the maximum is taken coordinate-wise. This is often called the Lindley process and models the waiting times in a two-server queue. We characterize recurrence of this process, assuming suitable, rather mild moment conditions on X X . It turns out that this is directly related with the tail asymptotics of the exit time of the random walk x + S ⁡ ( n ) x+S(n) from the quadrant, so that the main part of this paper is devoted to an analysis of that exit time in relation with the drift vector, i.e., the expectation of X X .

1.
Introduction

The waiting times in a single server queue are modeled as a Markov chain
W(n)=W^{x}(n)
,
n\geq 0
,
on the non-negative half-axis
\mathbb{R}_{+}\,
. Here,
x\geq 0
is the initial value,
W(0)=x
, and

W(n)=\max\{0,W(n-1)-X(n)\}
, where the
X(n)
,
n\geq 1
, are i.i.d. real random variables.
We think of this process as an absorbing-reflecting random walk on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Namely, setting

S(n)=X(1)+\dots+X(n)
, the process evolves as the random walk
x-S(n)
as long as it stays
non-negative. Only when it attempts to cross
0
and become negative, the new value is
reset to
0
before continuing. This is often called the
Lindley process,

see
Lindley

[
24
]
. There is an extensive literature on this Markov chain, such as
the seminal paper by
Kendall

[
19
]
plus the references therein,
and the monographs by
Feller

[
12
]
,

Borovkov

[
6
]
and
Asmussen

[
1
]
.

In the present work, we are interested in the multi-dimensional case, and more precisely, the 2-dimensional
one, where the
X(n)=\bigl(X_{1}(n),X_{2}(n)\bigr)
are i.i.d. copies of a 2-dimensional
random variable
X=(X_{1}\,,X_{2})
, the starting point
x
lies in the non-negative quadrant
of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and the maximum in
W(n)=W^{x}(n)=\max\{0,W(n-1)-X(n)\}
is taken coordinate-wise.
There are various types of
multi-dimensional queueing processes. A first rigorous study is due to

Kiefer and Wolfowitz

[
20
]
. In that reference, there are several servers and only one queue, while our process corresponds to having as many queues as there are servers.
Kingman

[
21
]
,

Flatto and McKean

[
13
]
and others studied the model with two servers and two queues,
were arriving customers choose the shorter queue.
Greenwood and Shaked

[
15
]
touch the
model where the process restarts from the origin whenever just one of the coordinates
becomes negative. Again, this is different from our situation, for which there are not many
references.
Regarding recurrence of the 2-dimensional Lindley process, see e.g. the recent note of
Cygan and Kloas

[
8
]
.

Another viewpoint is to consider the 2-dimensional absorbing-reflecting random walk
W^{x}(n)
as a
stochastic dynamical system (SDS) evolving in
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}\,
, as in
the work of
Leguesdron

[
23
]
,
Peigné

[
25
]
,
Benda

[
3
]
,
[
4
]
,

[
5
]
.
The viewpoint of the last references
– inspired by important work of
Babillot, Bougerol and Elie

[
2
]
–
has been to exploit the fact that
this SDS is obtained by iterating a sequence of i.i.d. random contractions of

\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}
, and that it is

locally contractive.
See
Peigné and Woess

[
26
]
,
[
27
]

for the precise definition, an outline of relevant parts of Benda’s (not easily accessible) work, and
further results concerning conservativity, ergodicity, and invariant measures.

Indeed, when the one-dimensional marginal processes are recurrent – a well-understood
situation, and the one we are interested in here – then our SDS is
strongly contractive,

that is

|W_{n}^{x}-W_{n}^{y}|\to 0\quad\text{almost surely, for all }\;x,y\in(0\,,\,\infty)^{2}\,.

This implies that the process is either
transient,
that is,
W^{x}(n)\to\infty

almost surely, or else it is recurrent in the following sense: there is a closed, non-empty

limit set

\mathsf{L}\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}
such that for any open set
U
which intersects
\mathsf{L}

and any starting point
x
, we have

(1.1)

\mathbb{P}[W^{x}(n)\in U\;\text{for infinitely many}\;n]=1.

A main goal of this paper is to understand when the 2-dimensional Lindley process
is recurrent.

(1.2) Basic Assumption.

Throughout this paper, we assume that

(i)

\;X
is not constrained to a hyperplane, and that

(ii)

\;\mathbb{P}[X\in(0\,,\,\infty)^{2}]>0\,.

This is a quite natural assumption on the support of the distribution of
X=(X_{1}\,,X_{2})
;
see the discussion in §
4
.
Based on this assumption and the preceding observations, the following is our main result concerning
the two-dimensional Lindley process, with quite general moment assumptions for the
random variable
X
which models the increments, and its positive and
negative parts
X_{i}^{+}=\max\{X_{i}\,,0\}
and
X_{i}^{-}=\max\{-X_{i}\,,0\}
,
i=1,2
.

(1.3) Theorem.

(a)
If for at least one
i\in\{1,2\}
,

\mathsf{E}(X_{i}^{+})<\mathsf{E}(X_{i}^{-})\leq\infty\,,

then
W(n)
is transient.

(b)
If for both
i\in\{1,2\}
,

\mathsf{E}(X_{i}^{-})<\mathsf{E}(X_{i}^{+})<\infty\,,

then
W(n)
is positive recurrent.

(c)
If for both
i\in\{1,2\}
,

\mathsf{E}(X_{i})=0\and\mathsf{E}\bigl(|X_{i}|^{\max\{2+\delta\,,\,\pi/\arccos(-\rho)\}}\bigr)<\infty\,,

where
\delta>0
(arbitrary) and
\rho=\rho(X_{1}\,,X_{2})
is the correlation coefficient of

X_{1}
and
X_{2}\,
, then
W(n)
is null recurrent if
\rho\geq 0
, and transient,
otherwise.

(d)
If for some
\delta>0
, up to a possible exchange of the two coordinates,

\mathsf{E}(|X_{i}|^{2+\delta})<\infty\quad\text{for }\;i=1,2,\quad\mathsf{E}(X_{1})=0,\quad\mathsf{E}(X_{2})>0,\and\mathsf{E}((X_{2}^{-})^{3+\delta})<\infty\,,

then
W(n)
is null recurrent.

The two interesting cases are (c) and (d). In order to prove the theorem, we use the
following.

(1.4) Definition.

For
x\in(0\,,\,\infty)^{2}\,
, the exit time of the random walk from the positive
quadrant is

\tau_{x}=\inf\{n\geq 1:x+S(n)\notin(0\,,\,\infty)^{2}\}\,.

In cases (c) and (d),
\tau_{x}
is almost surely finite.
We have the following relation of the Lindley process with these exit times.

(1.5) Lemma.

For
x=(x_{1}\,,x_{2})\in(0\,,\,\infty)^{2}\,
,

\mathbb{P}\bigl[W^{0}(n)\in[0,x_{1})\times[0,x_{2})\bigr]=\mathbb{P}[\tau_{x}>n].

Thus, we are led to another topic of its own big interest, currently the object
of very active work: the tail asymptotics of exit times of random walks from
cones, in our case, the positive quadrant. Our main source is the profound paper of

Denisov and Wachtel

[
9
]
on exit times from cones for centered
random walks. As we shall see, this leads to statement (c) of Theorem

1.3
. Our main focus is on the case when one coordinate is centered and
the second one has positive drift. Exit times from cones for random walks with
drift were considered by
Duraj

[
11
]
and
Garbit and Raschel

[
14
]
,
who however do not provide the tail asymptotics which we need. The central body of the
present paper concerns that case. We summarize.

(1.6) Theorem.

Under the same moment conditions as in
Theorem
1.3
, we have the following for any

x\in(0\,,\,\infty)^{2}\,
, as
n\to\infty
.

In case
(a)
,  if
\;\mathsf{E}(X_{i})<0\;
and
r\geq 1

then
\;\mathsf{E}\bigl((X_{i}^{+})^{r}\bigr)<\infty\;

implies
\;\mathsf{E}(\tau_{x}^{r})<\infty\,
, whence
\mathbb{P}[\tau_{x}>n]=\mathfrak{o}(n^{-r})
.

In case
(b)
,
\quad\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}[\tau_{x}>n]=\mathbb{P}[\tau_{x}=\infty]>0

In case
(c)
,
\quad\mathbb{P}[\tau_{x}>n]\sim v(x)\,n^{-1/p}\,,\quad\text{where}\quad p=2\arccos(-\rho)/\pi\,.

In case
(d)
,
\quad\mathbb{P}[\tau_{x}>n]\sim\kappa\,h(x)\,n^{-1/2}\,.

In (c) and (d),
v
and
h
are positive harmonic functions for the respective
random walk
x+S(n)

killed when exiting
(0\,,\,\infty)^{2}\,
, and
\kappa>0
.

(The reader will see below why we prefer not to incorporate the constant

\kappa
into

.)

These are the main results of the present paper. The main body of the work consists
in the proof of Theorem
1.6
(d), preceded by (a)–(c).

2.
Dimension one: exit times from the half-line

In this section only,
X
is a one-dimensional random variable,
X(n)
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
are i.i.d. copies of
X
, and
S(n)=X(1)+\dots+X(n)
(with
S(0)=0
).
For
x\in(0\,,\,\infty)
,

(2.1)

\tau_{x}=\inf\{n\in\mathbb{N}:x+S(n)\leq 0\}.

We collect several facts concerning
\tau_{x}
and related random variables, such as

(2.2)

\underline{M\!}\,(n)=\min\{S(k):0\leq k\leq n\}\and\overline{\!M}(n)=\max\{S(k):0\leq k\leq n\}.

For the following, see the monograph by
Gut

[
16
, Thm. 3.1]
.

(2.3) Lemma.

If
X
is integrable and
\mathsf{E}(X)<0
then
\mathsf{E}(\tau_{x})<\infty\,
. In addition, for any
r\geq 1
,

\mathsf{E}\bigl((X^{+})^{r}\bigr)<\infty\iff\mathsf{E}(\tau_{x}^{r})<\infty\,.

For the following,
[
16
]
is again a good source, as well as the very nice
exposition by
Janson

[
18
]
.

(2.4) Lemma.

If
X
is integrable and
\mathsf{E}(X)>0
then

\mathbb{P}[\tau_{x}=\infty]>0\and\underline{M\!}\,=\inf\{S(n):n\geq 0\}>-\infty\quad\text{almost surely,}

so that
\;T_{\min}=\inf\{n\geq 0:S(n)=\underline{M\!}\,\}<\infty\;
almost surely.
Furthermore, for any
r>0
,

\mathsf{E}\bigl((X^{-})^{r+1}\bigr)<\infty\iff\mathsf{E}\bigl(|\underline{M\!}\,|^{r}\bigr)<\infty\iff\mathsf{E}(\tau_{x}^{r}\,;\,\tau_{x}<\infty)<\infty\iff\mathsf{E}\bigl(T_{\min}^{r}\bigr)<\infty\,.

For the remainder of this section, we assume that
X
is centered.

(2.5) Proposition.

Suppose that
\mathsf{E}(X^{2})<\infty
and
\mathsf{E}(X)=0
. Then the
function

h_{1}(x)=x-g_{1}(x)\,,\quad\text{where}\quad g_{1}(x)=\mathsf{E}\bigl(x+S(\tau_{x})\,;\,\tau_{x}<\infty\bigr)\,,\quad x>0,

is finite and harmonic for the random walk killed when exiting
(0\,,\,\infty)
,
that is,

h_{1}(x)=\mathsf{E}\Bigl(h_{1}\bigl(x+S(1)\bigr)\,;\,\tau_{x}>1\Bigr).

There is
d>0
such that

(2.6)

h_{1}(x)\geq\max\{x,d\}\and\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{h_{1}(x)}{x}=1.

Proof.

As
\mathsf{E}(X^{2})<\infty\,
, finiteness of

g_{1}(x)
follows from classical results, see e.g.
[
12
]
. Note that
g_{1}(x)\leq 0
,
whence
h_{1}(x_{1})\geq x_{1}\,
. Since
X
is non-degenerate and centered, there is
c>0
such
that
\mathbb{P}[X\leq-2c]\geq 1/3
. Thus, if
0<x\leq c
then
\mathbb{P}[x+S(1)\leq c]\geq 1/3
,
whence
-g_{1}(x)\geq 1/3
. This proves the first part of (
2.6
).

Harmonicity is easily proved: Since
\mathsf{E}(X)=0
,

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle=\int_{y>-x}(x+y)\,\mathbb{P}[X=dy]+\int_{y\leq-x}(x+y)\,\mathbb{P}[X=dy]

\displaystyle=\mathsf{E}\bigl(x+S(1)\,;\,\tau_{x}>1\bigr)+\int_{y\leq-x}(x+y)\,\mathbb{P}[X=dy].

On the other hand, decomposing with respect to the first step of the walk,

\displaystyle g_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{y>-x}\mathsf{E}\bigl(x+y+S(\tau_{x+y})\,;\,\tau_{x+y}<\infty\bigr)\,\mathbb{P}[X=dy]+\int_{y\leq-x}(x+y)\,\mathbb{P}[X=dy]

\displaystyle=\mathsf{E}\bigl(g_{1}\bigl(x+S(1)\bigr)\,;\,\tau_{x}>1\bigr)+\int_{y\leq-x}(x+y)\,\mathbb{P}[X=dy]\,.

Taking the difference, we get harmonicity of
h_{1}
as required.

Finally,
a proof by D. Denisov and V. Wachtel that
h_{1}(x)/x\to 1
as
x\to\infty

was comunicated to us by V. Wachtel. It is provided in the Appendix.
∎

In the next section, we shall need an additional estimate related to the function
g_{1}(x)
.
Let
(\ell_{k})_{k\geq 0}
be the strictly decreasing ladder indices of
\bigl(S(n)\bigr)_{n\geq 0}\,
.
That is,
\ell_{0}=0
, and
\ell_{k}=\min\{n:S(n)<S(\ell_{k-1})\}
.
Then the increments
S(\ell_{n})-S(\ell_{n-1})
are i.i.d. and integrable, because

\mathsf{E}(X^{2})<\infty
. Also,
\tau_{x}=S(\ell_{k})
for some random
k
depending on
x>0
.

(2.7) Lemma.

For each
m>0
there is a constant
U(m)>0
such that

\mathbb{P}[x+S_{1}(\tau_{x})<-t]\leq U(m)\,\mathbb{P}[S({\ell_{1}})<-t]\quad\text{for all }\;t>0\,,\;x\in(0,m]\,.

Proof.

For
x>0
, let

U(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\bigl[S(\ell_{k})\in(-x\,,\,0]\,\bigr]\,.

This is the potential kernel of
\bigl(S(\ell_{k})\bigr)_{k\geq 0}\,
. It is finite, since

S(\ell_{k})
is a sum of i.i.d. random variables tending almost surely to
-\infty\,
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[x+S_{1}(\tau_{x})<-t]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\bigl[\tau_{x}=\ell_{k}\,,\;x+S(\ell_{k})<-t\bigr]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\bigl[x+S(\ell_{k-1})>0\,,\;S(\ell_{k})-S(\ell_{k-1})<-t\bigr]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\bigl[S(\ell_{k-1})\in(-x\,,\,0]\,\bigr]\times\mathbb{P}[S(\ell_{1})<-t\bigr]=U(x)\,\mathbb{P}[S(\ell_{1})<-t\bigr]\,.

Since
U(x)\leq U(m)
for
x\leq m
, this concludes the proof.
∎

We shall also need the following Lemma.
The first part is in principle known; the second part is adapted from

Pham

[
28
, Lemma 4.5]
.
For the sake of completeness we provide a proof.

(2.8) Lemma.

Suppose that
\mathsf{E}(X^{2})<\infty
and
\mathsf{E}(X)=0
. Then for any
p>2
there is a
constant
c_{p}>0
such that when
\mathsf{E}(|X|^{p})<\infty\,
, for each
t>0
and
n\in\mathbb{N}

\mathsf{E}\bigl(|S(n)|^{p}\bigr)\leq c_{p}\,n^{p/2}\,\mathsf{E}\bigl(|X|^{p}\bigr)\and\mathbb{P}[\,\overline{\!M}(n)>t]\leq c_{p}\,\mathsf{E}\bigl(|X|^{p}\bigr)\,n^{p/2}\,t^{-p}\,.

Furthermore, for any
\alpha>0
,

\mathsf{E}\bigl(n^{1/2}+\overline{\!M}(n)\,;\,\overline{\!M}(n)>n^{1/2+\alpha}\bigr)\leq c_{p}\,\mathsf{E}\bigl(|X|^{p}\bigr)\,\frac{2p-1}{p-1}\,n^{1/2-(p-1)\alpha}\,.

Proof.

Since
\bigl(S(n)\bigr)
is a martingale, Doob’s maximal inequality implies

\mathbb{P}[\,\overline{\!M}(n)>t]\leq\mathsf{E}\bigl(|S(n)|^{p}\bigr)\,t^{-p}\,.

By a well-known inequality which one finds e.g. in

Burkholder

[
7
, Proof of Thm. 3.2]
,

\mathsf{E}\bigl(|S(n)|^{p}\bigr)\leq c_{p}\,\mathsf{E}\biggl(\Bigl(\sum_{k=1}^{n}X(k)^{2}\Bigr)^{p/2}\biggr).

Now Hölder’s inequality concludes the proof of the first two inequalities.
For the third inequality,

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}\bigl(n^{1/2}+\overline{\!M}(n)

\displaystyle;\,\overline{\!M}(n)>n^{1/2+\alpha}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\bigl(n^{1/2}+n^{1/2+\alpha}\bigr)\,\mathbb{P}\bigl[\,\overline{\!M}(n)>n^{1/2+\alpha}\bigr]+\mathsf{E}\Bigl(\bigl(\,\overline{\!M}(n)-n^{1/2+\alpha}\bigr)^{+}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq 2n^{1/2+\alpha}\,\mathbb{P}\bigl[\,\overline{\!M}(n)>n^{1/2+\alpha}\bigr]+\int_{n^{1/2+\alpha}}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\bigl[\,\overline{\!M}(n)>t\bigr]\,dt

\displaystyle\leq 2\,c_{p}\,\mathsf{E}\bigl(|X|^{p}\bigr)\,n^{1/2-(p-1)\alpha}+c_{p}\,\mathsf{E}\bigl(|X|^{p}\bigr)\int_{n^{1/2+\alpha}}^{\infty}n^{p/2}\,t^{-p}\,dt

\displaystyle=c_{p}\,\mathsf{E}\bigl(|X|^{p}\bigr)\,\frac{2p-1}{p-1}\,n^{1/2-(p-1)\alpha}\,,

as proposed.
∎

(a).

It is well known that if

\;\mathsf{E}\bigl((X_{i}^{+})^{r}\bigr)<\infty\;

if and only if
\;\mathsf{E}(\tau_{x_{i}}^{r})<\infty\,

Positive drift in both coordinates

Proof of Theorem
1.6
(b).

We use Lemma
2.4

and set
\underline{M\!}\,_{i}=\inf\{S_{i}(n):n\geq 0\}
. These random variables are almost
surely finite. Therefore, for every

\mathbb{P}[\tau_{x}>n]=\mathbb{P}[\tau_{x_{1}}>n\,,\,\tau_{x_{2}}>n]\geq\mathbb{P}[\underline{M\!}\,_{1}<-x_{1}\,,\,\underline{M\!}\,_{2}<-x_{2}]\to 1\,,\quad\text{as }\;x_{1}\,,x_{2}\to\infty.

Thus, there is

. If

The centered case

Proof of Theorem
1.6
(c).

We assume that both coordinates
of
X
have finite moment of order
\max\{2+\delta\,,\,\pi/\arccos(-\rho)\}

and are centered, with some
\delta>0
and
\rho=\rho(X_{1}\,,X_{2})\in(-1\,,\,1)
.

The result is in principle stated in
[
9
, Example 2]
. For the sake of
completeness, we give a few hints.
Let
\mathbb{K}_{\alpha}
be a standard closed cone in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
: the sides
of the cone are two half-lines issuing from the origin, which is the cone’s vertex. The opening
angle of the cone is
\alpha\in(0\,,\,\pi)
. On
\mathbb{K}_{\alpha}\,
, the paper
[
9
]
considers
a random walk
\tilde{x}+\widetilde{S}(n)
with
\tilde{x}\in\mathbb{K}_{\alpha}^{o}
and

\widetilde{S}(n)=\widetilde{X}(1)+\dots+\widetilde{X}(n)
, where the
\widetilde{X}(n)
are i.i.d. copies
of
\widetilde{X}=(\widetilde{X}_{1}\,,\widetilde{X}_{2})
. The assumptions are that
the coordinates of
\widetilde{X}
have finite moment of order

\max\{2+\delta\,,\,\pi/\alpha\}
, are centered, with
\mathsf{E}(\widetilde{X}_{i}^{2})=1
and

\rho(\widetilde{X}_{1}\,,\widetilde{X}_{2})=\mathsf{E}(\widetilde{X}_{1}\widetilde{X}_{2})=0
. For the associated exit time

\widetilde{\tau}_{\tilde{x}}\,
, the main results of
[
9
]
yield that

\mathbb{P}[\widetilde{\tau}_{\tilde{x}}>n]\sim V(\tilde{x})\,n^{-1/p}\,,\quad\text{where}\quad p=2\alpha/\pi\,.

Thus, the method is to decorrelate
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
and thereby to pass from
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}
to
a possibly modified cone. This can for example be achieved by the matrix transformation

\begin{pmatrix}\widetilde{X}_{1}\\

and
thus also
W_{1}^{x_{1}}(n)\to\infty
almost surely for every
x_{1}\geq 0\,
:
the first marginal process is transient,
whence also the 2-dimensional process is transient.
∎

Zero drift and negative correlation

We now consider the case when both marginals have zero drift, but the
correlation is negative.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
(c), transient case.

Under the moment conditions of Theorem
1.3
(c), when

\rho(X_{1}\,,X_{2})<0
then the combination of Lemma
1.5
with
Theorem
1.6
(c) shows that

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}[W^{0}(n)\in[0\,,\,x_{1})\times[0\,,\,x_{2})]<\infty\quad\text{for all }\;x_{1}\,,\,x_{2}>0.

This is the expected number of visits of the process to each of those rectangles.
Therefore, with probability 1, each rectangle is visited only finitely often, so that

|W^{0}(n)|\to\infty\,
and the process is transient.
∎

Recurrence

We now turn our attention the the remaining cases, that is, statements (b) and (d)
of Theorem
1.3
, as well as (c) with
\rho(X_{1}\,,X_{2})\geq 0
.

Conclusion of the proof of Theorem
1.3
.

In all of those remaining cases, we have by Theorem
1.6

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\bigl[W^{0}(n)\in[0\,,\,x_{1})\times[0\,,\,x_{2})\bigr]=\infty\quad\text{for all }\;x_{1}\,,\,x_{2}>0.

In the case when the distribution of
X=(X_{1}\,,X_{2})
is non-lattice,
this alone does not guarantee that the process is topologically recurrent.
But thanks to Assumption
1.2
(ii), there is
x=(x_{1}\,,x_{2})\in(0\,,\,\infty)^{2}

such that for each
n
,

\mathbb{P}\bigl[X(n+1)\in[x_{1}\,,\,\infty)\times[x_{2}\,,\infty)\bigr]=\mathbb{P}\bigl[X\in[x_{1}\,,\,\infty)\times[x_{2}\,,\infty)\bigr]>0\,.

But if
W^{0}(n)\in[0\,,\,x_{1})\times[0\,,\,x_{2})
and

X(n+1)\in[x_{1}\,,\,\infty)\times[x_{2}\,,\infty)
then
W^{0}(n+1)=0
.
Therefore, for this choice of
(x_{1}\,,x_{2})
, and since
W^{0}(n)
and
X(n+1)

are independent,

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}[W^{0}(n+1)=0]

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\bigl[W^{0}(n)\in[0\,,\,x_{1})\!\times\![0\,,\,x_{2})\,,\;X(n+1)\in[x_{1}\,,\,\infty)\!\times\![x_{2}\,,\infty)\bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\bigl[X\in[x_{1}\,,\,\infty)\!\times\![x_{2}\,,\infty)\bigr]\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\bigl[W^{0}(n)\in[0\,,\,x_{1})\!\times\![0\,,\,x_{2})\bigr]=\infty.

Since
0
is a single state of our Markov process, this implies via basic Markov chain
theory that it is a recurrent state:

\mathbb{P}[W^{0}(n)=0\;\text{ infinitely often}\,]=1\,.

Now let us look at this under the viewpoint of stochastic
dynamical systems (SDS): the random mappings
F_{k}(x)=\max\{0,x-X(k)\}

are elements of the semigroup
\mathfrak{L}
of contractions of
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}

with Liptschitz constants
\leq 1
. We have

W^{x}(n)=F_{n}\circ\dots\circ F_{1}(x)\,.

In particular,
|W^{x}(n)-W^{y}(n)|
is decreasing in
n
, whence

|W^{x}(n)-W^{0}(n)|\leq|x|
. Consequently, our SDS is non-transient:

\mathbb{P}\bigl[|W^{x}(n)|\to\infty\bigr]=0\quad\text{for every }\;x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}\,.

\mathfrak{L}
carries the topology of uniform convergence on compact
sets. Denote by
\widetilde{\mu}
the (common) distribution of the
i.i.d. random mappings
F_{n}\,
. Then the above arguments which led to recurrence of
the state
0
entail
that the constant mapping
x\mapsto 0
can be approximated in
\mathfrak{L}

by a sequence
f_{n}\circ\dots\circ f_{1}\,
,
n\in\mathbb{N}\,
, where each function

f_{k}
is in the support of
\widetilde{\mu}
.
At this point, we can invoke a result going back to
[
23
]
, developped further
in
[
3
]
and
[
26
]
; see
Kloas and Woess

[
22
, Prop. 2.5]
for
a compact formulation. It implies the existence of a limit set
\mathsf{L}
and recurrence
as stated in the Introduction in (
1.1
) and the preceding paragraph.
∎

Discussion

Invariant measures.
In the cases where the two-dimensional Lindley process is recurrent,
it follows from
[
26
]
that it has a unique invariant measure
\nu
up to constant factors.
It is supported on the limit set
\mathsf{L}
, and as a starting measure for the process, it makes
the time shift ergodic. For details, see
[
26
, Thm. 2.13]
.
In particular, its marginals
\nu_{1}
and
\nu_{2}
are the unique invariant measures for the
respective marginal processes. It is well understood that the invariant measure for
a recurrent one-dimensional Lindley process has finite total mass when the increment has
positive expectation. (Recall that we
substract
the increment.) Also, that measure has
infinite total mass in the drift-free case. This is the reason why for the two-dimensional process,

\nu
has finite total mass (positive recurrence)
in Theorem
1.3
(b), while it has infinite total mass (null recurrence)
in Theorem
1.3
(c) and (d).

The roles of Assumption
1.2
(ii) and of the condition

\rho(X_{1}\,,X_{2})\geq 0
.
Consider the typical example of a clerk at a counter serving
a queue of customers. In that case, the waiting time of the
n^{\text{th}}
customer
is modelled by a one-dimensional Lindley process, and recurrence means that the process
returns to state
0
with probability one - the queue will be empty and the clerk
at the counter will be able to have a break. In the two-dimensional case, we have two
counters and two queues, and in general we assume that the clerks do not operate
independently. Then both Assumption
1.2
(ii) and the non-negative correlation
can be interpreted in the sense that the two clerks are co-operative, which will give
them the possibility to have a joint break.

Assumption
1.2
(ii) was used in the proof of Proposition
3.2

in order to show that
h>0
strictly on all of
(0\,,\,\infty)^{2}
. Without it, our proof does not
allow us to go beyond the statement that
for every
b_{1}>0
(possibly small) there is

b_{2}>0
(possibly large) such that
h>0
on

(b_{1}\,,\,\infty)\times(b_{2}\,,\,\infty)
.

In the proof of Theorem
1.3
(d) this would lead to the restriction that
we only get

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\bigl[W^{0}(n)\in[0\,,\,x_{1})\times[0\,,\,x_{2})\bigr]=\infty

for sufficiently large
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
.

Now, in the case when the process is discrete (in which case we assume without loss
of generality that the increments are in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
and the process evolves within

\mathbb{N}_{0}^{2}
), this does imply recurrence in the sense that the process visits each point
of the (integer) limit set infinitely often with probability one. However, the origin is
then not necessarily part of the limit set: the two clerks will not be able to have
a joint break.

On the other hand, when the process is non-discrete then it is by no means clear that divergence
of the above series implies topological recurrence – here, recurrence was deduced because
on the basis of Assumption
1.2
(ii) we could show that

\sum_{n}\mathbb{P}\bigl[W^{0}(n)=0]=\infty
.

The limit set.

\mathsf{L}
depends on the support of the distribution of the increments.
In our case, most importantly, it contains the origin.
It appears quite hard to determine the full limit set
explicitly, compare with
[
22
]
.

Moment assumptions.

In theorems
1.3
(d) and
1.6
(d), we have assumed among other
that
\mathsf{E}((X_{2}^{-})^{3+\delta})<\infty\,
. This is used in the proof of Claim 1 in Proposition

3.2
. We believe that a finite moment of order

2+\delta
should suffice, but so far we have not succeeded in proving this.

Higher dimensions.
We can consider the same issues on
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
for any
d\geq 2
.
The model random variable for the increments is then
X=(X_{1}\,,\dots,X_{d})
, and the definition
of
W(n)
remains the same, as well as Assumption
1.2
with the obvious adaptation.

If there is one coordinate with
\mathsf{E}(X_{i}^{+})<\mathsf{E}(X_{i}^{-})\leq\infty\,

then
W(n)
is transient, while if all coordinates satisfy

\mathsf{E}(X_{i}^{-})<\mathsf{E}(X_{i}^{+})<\infty\,
, then
W(n)
is positive recurrent.

Also, if one coordinate is centered and all others have positive expectiation, then
under the same moment conditions as in Theorem
1.3
(d) one gets null recurrence;
the proof remains practically the same.

For recurrence, one cannot have more than two centered coordinates. So the main case still
to be studied is the analogue of Theorem
1.3
(d) in the case when there are two
centered coordinates and all the other coordinates have positive expectation.
There are several substantial additional issues to be tackled in this situation, which
we reserve for future work.

Remarks on reflected random walk

A model which is very similar to the queueing process is reflected random walk.
In dimension 1, with starting point
x>0
, both processes evolve like a
random walk
x-S(n)
as long as it stays non-negative. When the walk enters the
negative half-axis, the value is reset to zero for the queueing process
W^{x}(n)
, while
for reflected random walk
R(n)=R^{x}(n)
, the sign is changed. In arbitrary dimension, this
means that reflected random walk is given by

R(0)=x\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\,,\quad R(n)=|R(n-1)-X(n)|\,,

where (attention!) the absolute value is taken coordinate-wise.
The one-dimensional model is very well studied; instead of re-displaying all references,
we refer to
[
26
]
, as well as to
[
25
]
and
[
22
]
, where the recurrence
of the multidimensional
reflected random walk is studied in the case where
\mathsf{E}(X_{i})>0
for all coordinates.

In general, it is easy to see that transience of the multi-dimensional queueing process
implies transience of the corresponding reflected random walk. In other words, the recurrence
issue is somewhat harder for reflected random walk. In the following case, the two processes
are recurrent, resp. transient simultaneously.

(4.1) Lemma.

If there is
\Delta>0
such that
X_{i}\geq-\Delta
almost surely for
i=1,\dots,d

then for the two processes starting at the same point,

R_{i}(n)\leq X_{i}(n)+\Delta\quad\text{almost surely for $i=1,\dots,d$ and all $n$.}

The proof is straightforward. Thus, when the multidimensional Lindley process
visits the origin infinitely often with probability
1
, then the corresponding
multidimensional reflected random walk visits the set
[0\,,\,\Delta]^{d}

infinitely often with probability
1
. By the contractivity properties of

\bigl(R^{x}(n)\bigr)
, this implies that the reflected random walk is topologically
recurrent on its limit set, compare with
[
26
]
and
[
22
]
.

In dimension 2, this applies, in particular, to the situation of Theorem
1.3
.

5.
Appendix: a proof due to D. Denisov and V. Wachtel

In this subsection, the proof of Proposition
2.5
is completed. That is,
we present the elaboration of an argument of Denisov and Wachtel which proves

(
2.6
)

\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{h_{1}(x)}{x}=1.

Given the centered real random variable
X
with
\mathsf{E}(X^{2})<\infty\,
,
we consider the following functions
a,b,m:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
associated with
the negative part
X^{-}
of
X
.

\displaystyle a(x)

\displaystyle=-\mathsf{E}(x+X\,;\,x+X\leq 0)=\int_{x}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}[X\leq-y]\,dy\,,

\displaystyle b(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{x}^{\infty}a(y)\,dy\,,\quad\text{and}\quad m(x)=\int_{0}^{x}b(y)\,dy\,,\quad x\geq 0.

Similarly, we set
\displaystyle\bar{a}(x)=\int_{x}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}[X>y]\,dy.

One easily verifies that
\;\displaystyle 2b(x)=\mathsf{E}\bigl((\max\{X^{-}-x,0\})^{2}\bigr)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}(x+y)^{2}dF(y)\to 0
as
x\to\infty\,
,
whence also

(5.1)

\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{m(x)}{x}=0.

(5.2) Proposition.

There are positive constants
R
(sufficiently large) and
A

such that the function

V(x)=x+A\,m(x)+R\,,\quad x\geq 0\,,

is superharmonic for the one-dimensional random walk
S(n)
killed when exiting
\mathbb{R}_{+}\,
, that is,

\mathsf{E}\Bigl(V\bigl(x+S(1)\bigr)\,;\,\tau_{x}>1\Bigr)\leq V(x)\quad\text{for all }\;x\geq 0.

Let us first show how this implies the desired result.

Proof of (
2.6
).

Proposition
5.2
implies that for all
x\geq 0
and
n\in\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle V(x)

\displaystyle\geq\mathsf{E}\Bigl(V\bigl(x+S(n)\bigr)\,;\,\tau_{x}>n\Bigr)

\displaystyle\geq\mathsf{E}\bigl(x+S(n)\,;\,\tau_{x}>n\bigr)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{E}\bigl(x+S(\min\{n,\tau_{x}\})\,;\,\tau_{x}>n\bigr)

\displaystyle=x-\mathsf{E}\bigl(x+S(\tau_{x})\,;\,\tau_{x}\leq n\bigr).

The last identity holds by the martingale property, since
S(n)

is a centered random walk. We infer that

-\mathsf{E}\bigl(x+S(\tau_{x})\,;\,\tau_{x}\leq n\bigr)\leq V(x)-x=A\,m(x)+R

We can let
n\to\infty\,
, and by monotone convergence

0\leq-\mathsf{E}\bigl(x+S(\tau_{x})\,;\,\tau_{x}<\infty\bigr)\leq A\,m(x)+R

By (
5.1
),

\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{1}{x}\,\mathsf{E}\bigl(x+S(\tau_{x})\,;\,\tau_{x}<\infty\bigr)=0\,.

Since
h_{1}(x)=x-\mathsf{E}\bigl(x+S(\tau_{x})\,;\,\tau_{x}<\infty\bigr)
, the result
follows.
∎

Proof of Proposition
5.2
.

We want to show that

\Delta(x)=\mathsf{E}\Bigl(V\bigl(x+S(1)\bigr)\,;\,\tau_{x}>1\Bigr)-V(x)\leq 0\quad\text{for }\;x\geq 0.

We write
F(x)=\mathbb{P}[X\leq x]
and
\overline{F}(x)=1-F(x)
. Using that
x=\mathsf{E}(x+X)
,

\displaystyle\Delta(x)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{E}\bigl(x+X+A\,m(x+X)+R\,;\,X>-x\bigr)-x-A\,m(x)-R

\displaystyle=-\mathsf{E}(x+X\,;\,X\leq-x\bigr)-R\,F(-x)-A\,m(x)\,F(-x)

\displaystyle+A\,\,\mathsf{E}\bigl(m(x+X)-m(x)\,;\,X>-x\bigr)

\displaystyle=a(x)-R\,F(-x)-A\,m(x)\,F(-x)+A\int_{-x}^{\infty}\bigl(m(x+y)-m(x)\bigr)\,dF(y).

We decompose the last integral and integrate twice by parts, recalling that

m^{\prime}(x)=b(x),b^{\prime}(x)=-a(x)
,
a^{\prime}(x)=-F(-x)
and
\bar{a}^{\prime}(x)=-\overline{F}(x)\,
:

\displaystyle\int_{-x}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\bigl(m(x+y)-m(x)\bigr)\,dF(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{-x}^{0}\bigl(m(x+y)-m(x)\bigr)\,dF(y)-\int_{0}^{\infty}\bigl(m(x+y)-m(x)\bigr)\,d\overline{F}(y)

\displaystyle=m(x)\,F(-x)-\int_{-x}^{0}b(x+y)F(y)\,dy+\int_{0}^{\infty}b(x+y)\overline{F}(y)\,dy

\displaystyle=m(x)\,F(-x)+\int_{0}^{x}b(x-y)a^{\prime}(y)\,dy-\int_{0}^{\infty}b(x+y)\overline{a}^{\prime}(y)\,dy

\displaystyle=m(x)\,F(-x)+b(0)a(x)-b(x)a(0)-\int_{0}^{x}a(x-y)a(y)\,dy

\displaystyle+b(x)\bar{a}(0)-\int_{0}^{\infty}a(x+y)\bar{a}(y)\,dy\,.

We observe that
b(x)\bar{a}(0)-b(x)a(0)=b(x)\bigl(\mathsf{E}(X^{+})-\mathsf{E}(X^{-})\bigr)=0
,
and recall that
b(0)=\mathsf{E}\bigl((X^{-})^{2}\bigr)\big/2
.
Combining these computations,

\displaystyle\Delta(x)

\displaystyle=a(x)-R\,F(-x)+A\,\frac{\mathsf{E}\bigl((X^{-})^{2}\bigr)}{2}\,a(x)-A\!\int_{0}^{x}\!\!\!\!a(x-y)a(y)\,dy-A\!\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!\!a(x+y)\bar{a}(y)\,dy

\displaystyle\leq a(x)-R\,F(-x)+A\,\frac{\mathsf{E}\bigl((X^{-})^{2}\bigr)}{2}\,a(x)-A\!\int_{0}^{x}\!\!\!\!a(x-y)a(y)\,dy\,.

Using that
a(x)
is monotone decreasing,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{x}\!\!a(x-y)a(y)\,dy

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{x/2}\!\!a(x-y)a(y)\,dy

\displaystyle\geq 2a(x)\bigl(b(0)-b(x/2)\bigr)=a(x)\mathsf{E}\bigl((X^{-})^{2}\bigr)-2a(x)b(x/2).

We now choose
A=4\big/\mathsf{E}\bigl((X^{-})^{2}\bigr)
. Then we get

\Delta(x)\leq-R\,F(-x)+a(x)\bigl(2A\,b(x/2)-1\bigr)\leq-R\,F(-x)+3a(x).

Since
b(x)\to 0
as
x\to\infty
, there is
x_{0}>0
such that

2A\,b(x_{0}/2)-1=0
and hence
\Delta(x)\leq 0
for all
x\geq x_{0}\,.

Now suppose first that
F(-x_{0})>0
. Then we choose
R=3\mathsf{E}(X^{-})/F(-x_{0})
,
and for
x\leq x_{0}
, we have
\Delta(x)\leq-R\,F(-x_{0})+3a(0)=0
.

Finally suppose that
F(-x_{0})=0
, that is,
\mathbb{P}[X>-x_{0}]=1
. Then also
a(x_{0})=0
.
This time, we choose
R=3x_{0}\,
. For
0\leq x<x_{0}
, there is
\xi\in(x\,,\,x_{0})

such that
a(x)=a(x_{0})-(x_{0}-x)a^{\prime}(\xi)=(x_{0}-x)F(-\xi)
. Then

\Delta(x)\leq 3a(x)-R\,F(-x)=3(x_{0}-x)\bigl(F(-\xi)-F(-x)\bigr)-3xF(-x)\leq 0.

This concludes the proof.
∎
