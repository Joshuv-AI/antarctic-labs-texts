The planted XY model: thermodynamics and inference
Siyu Chen, Guanhao Huang, Giovanni Piccioli, Lenka Zdeborová

Abstract
In this paper we study a fully connected planted spin glass named the planted XY model. Motivation for studying this system comes both from the spin glass field and the one of statistical inference where it models the angular synchronization problem. We derive the replica symmetric (RS) phase diagram in the temperature, ferromagnetic bias plane using the approximate message passing (AMP) algorithm and its state evolution (SE). While the RS predictions are exact on the Nishimori line (i.e. when the temperature is matched to the ferromagnetic bias), they become inaccurate when the parameters are mismatched, giving rise to a spin glass phase where AMP is not able to converge. To overcome the defects of the RS approximation we carry out a one-step replica symmetry breaking (1RSB) analysis based on the approximate survey propagation (ASP) algorithm. Exploiting the state evolution of ASP, we count the number of metastable states in the measure, derive the 1RSB free entropy and find the behavior of the Parisi parameter throughout the spin glass phase.



I
Introduction

In statistical physics, XY models describe a system in which the spins are unit norm vectors living in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, or equivalently unit norm complex numbers
x_{i}=e^{i\theta_{i}}
. They are also characterized by an Hamiltonian which is invariant under global phase shifts, thus displaying a symmetry identifiable with
U(1)
.
Early studies have focused on ferromagnetic lattice models with short range interactions, described by the Hamiltonian
H_{XY}(\theta)=J\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}\cos(\theta_{i}-\theta_{j})
. This line of work culminated with the discovery of the Kosterlitz-Thouless transition
Kosterlitz and Thouless 1973
.
Disordered models have also received attention
Sherrington 1983
. The most studied way of introducing disorder is through Gaussian couplings, giving a Hamiltonian
H_{DXY}(\theta)=\sum_{i<j}J_{ij}\cos(\theta_{i}-\theta_{j})

Sherrington 1983
. The model studied in this paper is a different version of disordered XY model motivated by the statistical inference problem of angular synchronization
Singer 2011
, which consists of retrieving a vector of angles
\theta_{i}\in[0,2\pi]
from measurements of their offsets
\theta_{i}-\theta_{j}
. The full definition is given in section
II
. This problem arises in many applications, for example time synchronization in distributed networks
Karp et al. 2003
Giridhar and Kumar 2006
, alignment in signal processing
Bandeira et al. 2014
, computer vision
Agrawal et al. 2006
and optics
Rubinstein and Wolansky 2001
. Angular synchronization was first introduced in
Singer 2011
and solved using spectral algorithms and semidefinite relaxation techniques
Bandeira et al. 2017
. In
Javanmard et al. 2016
, the authors found the replica symmetric solution via a replica and cavity computation. From the algorithmic point of view, a version of approximate message passing (AMP)
Donoho et al. 2009
was developed for a general class of problems including the angular synchronization
Perry et al. 2018
.
Formulating the angular synchronization problem in the language of XY Hamiltonians, see eq. (
4
), one obtains a planted version of the XY model. In planted systems, the couplings between spins depend on a special configuration
\theta^{\star}
, and the Gibbs measure is nothing but the conditional distribution of
\theta^{\star}
given the couplings
Zdeborová and Krzakala 2016
. The inference problem then corresponds to recovery of
\theta^{\star}
from the couplings.
Another variant of the XY model studied in the literature which admits a planted interpretation is the Gauge glass, an XY spin glass with Hamiltonian
H_{GG}=J\sum_{i,j\in E}\cos(\theta_{i}-\theta_{j}-\phi_{ij})
, where
E
is the set of edge interactions. The randomness is contained in the angles
\{\phi_{ij}\}
and possibly in
E
. This model has been first studied in the random setting with
\phi
drawn i.i.d. from a uniform distribution in
Ebner and Stroud 1985
and later generalized to the planted case, where
\{\phi_{ij}\}
are drawn from a zero mean von Mises distribution
Ozeki and Nishimori 1993
. Previous works on the gauge glass have mainly studied the model on the Nishimori line
Nishimori 2001

Iba 1999
i.e. a line in the temperature-coupling diagram, where calculations greatly simplify. This model was further studied in its discretized version in
Lupo and Ricci-Tersenghi 2017
for a mixture distribution that interpolates between ferromagnetic and uniformly distributed couplings and with interactions on a sparse random graph. Finally, the short range gauge glass model has also been extended to the quantum setting in
Morita et al. 2006
and has further physical relevance
Song and Zhang 2021
.
The model considered in this work, Hamiltonian (
4
), is closely related to
H_{GG}
. Our choice fell on model defined by eq. (
4
) because of the connection to angular synchronization.
Thanks to the invariance of the Hamiltonian under a joint transformation of the couplings and the spins, we are able to map the partial recovery transition in the inference case, into an order-disorder transition.
Moreover, for appropriate choices of the parameters (specifically for
\lambda<1
, defined below), the model has a fully random behavior, thus connecting with the literature on spin glasses.

Our work draws a bridge between the angular synchronization and the studies of disordered XY models in the statistical physics literature. We consider the fully connected disordered model associated with angular synchronization, and we investigate the properties of the Gibbs measure given by the posterior. In the inference case, one is usually interested in characterizing the error in the retrieval of the signal. Instead, we focus on the phase diagram spanned by the inverse temperature and the ferromagnetic bias. Specifically, we concentrate on the region outside the Nishimori line, which is not studied in related models
Ozeki and Nishimori 1993
.
Furthermore, we go beyond the RS analysis of
Perry et al. 2018
Javanmard et al. 2016
and perform a one step replica symmetry breaking study. Our theoretical analysis is complemented by algorithms which provide an instrumental way to study single instances of the model. We use this as an opportunity to characterize the behaviour of AMP, and its 1RSB version Approximate Survey Propagation
Antenucci et al. 2019
. The respective state evolutions give us the RS and 1RSB phase diagrams.
The paper will maintain a schizophrenic attitude, aiming to connect with both the physical XY Hamiltonian and the inference problem that motivated it.

The rest of the article is structured as follows: in section
II
we introduce the model from the inference point of view and we show the equivalence between the inference and the order/disorder formulation. In section
III
we study the RS phase diagram, with special attention to the outside of the Nishimori line. In section
IV
we perform a 1RSB analysis, using the ASP algorithm and its state evolution. Section
V
is dedicated to discussion and conclusions.

II
Definition of the model

We start by introducing the models in the language of statistical inference, where it is known as
U(1)
synchronization, an instance of angular synchronization
Singer 2011
;
Bandeira et al. 2017
;
Perry et al. 2018
.
The problem consists of retrieving a complex signal
x^{\star}=(x^{\star}_{1},x^{\star}_{2},\dots,x^{\star}_{N})
, where each
x^{\star}_{i}
is uniformly distributed on the unit circle, independently of other coordinates. In other words,
x^{\star}_{i}=e^{i\theta_{i}^{\star}}
with
\theta_{i}^{\star}\sim\text{Uniform}([0,2\pi])\;i.i.d.
. We will refer to
x^{\star}
as the ground truth or the planted signal.
A set of
N^{2}
complex measurements
\{Y_{ij}\}_{1\leq i,j\leq N}
is produced according to the rule

Y_{ij}=\sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{N}}x^{\star}_{i}\overline{x^{\star}_{j}}+W_{ij},

(1)

where
W
is a Hermitian matrix (i.e.
W_{ji}=\overline{W}_{ij}
) whose elements above the diagonal are all independent and distributed as
W_{ij}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1/2)+i\mathcal{N}(0,1/2)
. We also set
Y_{ii}=0,\;\forall i\in[N]
.
The parameter
\lambda
plays the role of the signal to noise ratio, while the scaling
1/\sqrt{N}
ensures that the problem of recovering
x^{\star}
is neither trivial (very large signal-to-noise ratios) nor impossible (very small signal-to-noise ratios)
Lesieur et al. 2017
. The goal of the inference problem is to recover
x^{\star}
from the knowledge of
Y_{ij}
.

We can write the posterior probability of
x
given
Y
. In doing so, we assume that the parameter
\lambda
is unknown, hence we study the family of probability measures with varying parameter
\hat{\lambda}
possibly different from
\lambda
. We stress that
Y
is always generated using
\lambda
. When
\lambda=\hat{\lambda}
computing the marginals of the posterior leads to Bayes-optimal inference; in the statistical physics language we say that the Nishimori condition is met. The consequences of this condition are extensively studied in
Zdeborová and Krzakala 2016
.
We first write the likelihood

\displaystyle P(Y|x)=\prod_{i<j}\frac{1}{\pi}\exp\left[-\left|Y_{ij}-\sqrt{\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}}x_{i}\overline{x}_{j}\right|^{2}\right].

(2)

Then, by applying Bayes theorem, we get the posterior measure

\displaystyle P(x|Y)=\frac{P(Y|x)P(x)}{P(Y)}\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi^{\binom{N}{2}}(2\pi)^{N}P(Y)}\exp\left[-\sum_{i<j}\left|Y_{ij}-\sqrt{\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}}x_{i}\overline{x}_{j}\right|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z(Y,\hat{\lambda})}\exp\left[2\sqrt{\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}}\sum_{i<j}\operatorname{Re}(Y_{ij}\overline{x_{i}}x_{j})\right].

(3)

In order to obtain the final expression, the prior as well as the terms in the expression of the likelihood that do not depend on
x
, were absorbed into the normalization.
To reconnect with the statistical physics setting, we write the posterior in terms of a Hamiltonian

\displaystyle P(x|Y)=\frac{1}{Z(Y,\hat{\lambda})}e^{H(x,Y,\hat{\lambda})},

(4)

\displaystyle H(x,Y,\hat{\lambda})=2\sqrt{\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}}\sum_{i<j}\operatorname{Re}(Y_{ij}\overline{x_{i}}x_{j}).

(5)

To highlight the connection with the gauge glass model
H_{GG}=J\sum_{i,j\in E}\cos(\theta_{i}-\theta_{j}-\phi_{ij})
, the Hamiltonian can also be written as
H=2\sqrt{\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}}\sum_{i<j}|Y_{ij}|\cos\left(\theta_{i}-\theta_{j}-\phi_{ij}\right)
, where
Y_{ij}=|Y_{ij}|e^{i\phi_{ij}}
and
x_{i}=e^{i\theta_{i}}
.
The model defined above is characterized by two main symmetries. The first being the
U(1)
symmetry, from which the model takes its name. It consists of the invariance of the Hamiltonian under a global phase shift, that is
H(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})=H(e^{i\phi}x_{1},\dots,e^{i\phi}x_{N})
for any
\phi\in[0,2\pi]
. As a consequence, we are able to recover the planted signal only up to a global phase.
The second symmetry is more subtle but allows us to transform the planted model into an ordered one. This feature is not unique to
U(1)
synchronization: for example, the planted SK model enjoys the same property, and can be transformed into a ferromagnetic model where the ferromagnetic bias is proportional to the signal to noise ratio in the original problem
Zdeborová and Krzakala 2016
. The
U(1)
synchronization Hamiltonian is invariant following simultaneous transformation of
x
and
Y
. Given an arbitrary vector
z=(z_{1},\dots,z_{N})=(e^{i\phi_{i}},\dots,e^{i\phi_{N}})
, we transform

\displaystyle x_{i}^{\prime}=x_{i}\overline{z_{i}}=e^{i(\theta_{i}-\phi_{i})},

(6)

\displaystyle Y^{\prime}_{ij}=Y_{ij}\overline{z_{i}}z_{j}.

(7)

To obtain a ferromagnetic model in the variables
x^{\prime}
, we pick
z_{i}=x^{\star}_{i}
. The planted configuration is then transformed into an ordered one
x^{\star\prime}_{i}=x^{\star}_{i}\overline{x^{\star}}_{i}=1
. For large
\lambda
, configurations
x
sampled from the measure will align with
x^{\star}
. Thus,
x^{\prime}
will align with
(1,\dots,1)
(always up to a global phase shift).

Thanks to this symmetry we can study without loss of generality the problem with
x^{\star}=(1,1,\dots,1)
and our results will extend to the case where
x^{\star}
is sampled uniformly over the unit circle.
Therefore, without loss of generality, we can restrict our analysis to the problem with measure (
4
) and random couplings

Y_{ij}=\sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{N}}+W_{ij},

(8)

where
W
has the same distribution as in (
1
).
We name this particular instance the
planted XY model
. The parameter
\lambda
plays the role of a ferromagnetic bias, while the parameter
\hat{\lambda}
is an inverse temperature.

III
The RS phase diagram

In this section, we derive the RS phase diagram of the planted XY model. This is the phase diagram under the approximation that the Gibbs measure can be represented as a Bethe measure. To obtain the phase diagram we use AMP, a generalization of the Thouless-Anderson-Palmer equations
Thouless et al. 1977
;
Donoho et al. 2009
, and its state evolution, which is equivalent to the replica symmetric solution.
Our analysis also provides a case to characterize the strengths and limitations of AMP.
AMP is a general purpose iterative message passing algorithm which outputs an approximation to the marginals of the desired probability distribution: in our case the posterior
P(x|Y)
.
While we expect AMP to give exact results on the Nishimori line (
\lambda=\hat{\lambda}
), in the case of mismatched parameters (
\lambda\neq\hat{\lambda}
), it can be inaccurate and fail to converge due to the emergence of replica symmetry breaking (RSB).
The derivation of AMP and its state evolution from Belief propagation are presented in appendix
B
. Here, we present the final AMP equations

\displaystyle h_{i}^{(t)}=\sqrt{\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}}\sum_{k\neq i}Y_{ik}\hat{x}_{k}^{(t)}-\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}\hat{x}_{i}^{(t-1)}\sum_{k\neq i}|Y_{ik}|^{2}\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial h}\left(h_{k}^{(t-1)}\right)

(9)

\displaystyle\hat{x}_{i}^{(t+1)}=\eta\left(h_{i}^{(t)}\right),\qquad\eta(h)=\frac{h}{|h|}\frac{I_{1}(|2h|)}{I_{0}(|2h|)},

(10)

where
I_{k}
is the modified Bessel function of the first kind of order
k
and
\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial h}(h)=1-|\eta(h)|^{2}
.

\hat{x}_{i}
is the estimator of the mean of the marginal; that is,
\hat{x}_{i}
estimates
\EX_{x|Y}[x_{i}]
.
One of the elements which distinguishes AMP from other iterative algorithms is the ability (in the
N\to\infty
limit) to track its dynamics through the state evolution equations. In particular, we have closed evolution equations for the two observables

\displaystyle m=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{x}_{i}\qquad q=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\hat{x}_{i}|^{2},

(11)

representing respectively the alignment of the marginals with the planted configuration and how concentrated each marginal is. When doing inference, one is interested in the mean square error (MSE) of the estimator. The MSE can be expressed as
\text{MSE}=1+q-2m
. In the language of statistical physics,
m
is the order parameter that represents how biased the system is towards the ordered state. The SE equations read:

\displaystyle m^{(t+1)}=\EX_{z}\left[\eta\left(h^{(t)}\right)\right]

(12)

\displaystyle q^{(t+1)}=\EX_{z}\left[\left|\eta\left(h^{(t)}\right)\right|^{2}\right],\quad\text{with}

(13)

\displaystyle h^{(t)}=\sqrt{\lambda\hat{\lambda}}m^{(t)}+\sqrt{\hat{\lambda}q^{(t)}/2}z,

(14)

where
z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)+i\mathcal{N}(0,1)
is a complex normal random variable.
Last, we’re also able to compute the Bethe approximation to the free entropy
f=\frac{1}{N}\log Z
. The Bethe free entropy is derived in Appendix
G
, its expression is

\displaystyle\Phi_{\text{RS}}(\hat{\lambda},\lambda)=-\sqrt{\lambda\hat{\lambda}}m^{2}+\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{2}q^{2}-\hat{\lambda}q+

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}_{z}\log[I_{0}(2|\sqrt{\lambda\hat{\lambda}}m+\sqrt{\hat{\lambda}q/2}z|)],

(15)

where
z
is defined as above and
m,q
are determined by iterating SE until convergence.
In appendix
G
, we also show that the stationary points of
\Phi_{\text{RS}}
with respect to
\lambda,\hat{\lambda}
are the fixed points of the SE equations. This confers another interpretation of the SE equations, the one of an iterative method to find the stationary points of the free entropy.
We perform extensive numerical experiments with the goal of studying our model through the lenses of SE and AMP.

III.1
On the Nishimori line

We start by restricting ourselves to the Nishimori line
\lambda=\hat{\lambda}
. For a large class of models, including ours, it was proven that the RS solution is exact in the large size limit
Miolane 2017
. From the inference point of view, this corresponds to the case where we know how the data
Y
is generated, and we can perform Bayes Optimal inference, in the sense that
\hat{\lambda}
matches
\lambda
. As a consequence of this fact, one can establish the relation
m=q

Iba 1999

Zdeborová and Krzakala 2016
. AMP’s analysis on the Nishimori line has been partially conducted in
Perry et al. 2018
for a class of models that includes ours. Moreover, a free entropy equivalent to ours has been obtained in
Perry et al. 2018
and in
Javanmard et al. 2016
via the replica method and proven to be correct in a more general setting in
Miolane 2017
.

Figure
1
illustrates the behavior of SE and AMP on the Nishimori line. On the left panel, the converged values of
m
and
q
, from both SE and AMP are plotted as a function of
\lambda
. First we observe the agreement between AMP and SE, i.e. SE’s fixed points have the same
m,q
as the points to which AMP converges. Next we see that, as one would expect,
m=q
at convergence. In the center panel, SE is run from initial conditions
m^{t=0}=0,q^{t=0}=10^{-2}
, corresponding AMP initialized randomly near
0
(in principle one would like to use
m^{t=0}=0,q^{t=0}=0
but numerical errors arise when initializing with too small
q
), and
m^{t=0}=1,q^{t=0}=1
(i.e. initializing
\hat{x}^{t=0}=(1,1,\dots,1)
in AMP), known as informed initialization. The two iterations converge to the same value of
m
, showing that the SE fixed point is unique in
m
. The right panel provides a free entropy interpretation of this phenomenon. The
m
dependent free entropy clearly has only one maximum, hence SE inevitably lands on it. In other models with multimodal free energies
Lesieur et al. 2017
, the local maximum to which SE (and hence AMP) converges might not be the absolute one.
Being the RS free entropy
\Phi_{\text{RS}}
exact on the Nishimori line, the uniqueness allows us to conclude that AMP computes the true marginals when
N\to\infty
.
Beside AMP’s behavior, Fig.
1
shows that
m
undergoes a second order phase transition at
\lambda=1
: for
\lambda<1
we say the model is in the paramagnetic phase, because
m=0
and there is no correlation with the planted signal. Instead for
\lambda>1
the system develops a ferromagnetic order.

III.2
Contiguity to the random XY model

One important consequence of the previous analysis is the existence of a phase where the effect of the planted signal disappears. When
\lambda<1
, the signal is completely washed out by the noise, and the data
Y
is indistinguishable from random noise; in other words, it is as if
Y=W
. This in turn implies that for
\lambda<1
, all high-probability quantities are independent of
\lambda
(this is referred to as contiguity of probability distributions in the statistics literature
Mossel et al. 2015
).
To put it differently, whenever
\lambda<1
, the planted nature of our model is lost and we look at a spin glass with purely random couplings. In the rest of the article, we will refer to the
\lambda<1
case as the fully random phase.

III.3
Replica symmetric phase diagram

Moving to the general case where
\hat{\lambda}
is possibly different from
\lambda
, we aim to explore the full phase diagram painted by SE. We begin by verifying again the agreement between the fixed points of AMP and SE, also during the dynamics.
In Fig.
2
, AMP is initialized from a random configuration
x^{t=0}
with entries of unit norm (thus
q^{t=0}=1
and
m^{t=0}=0
). It’s evident that SE accurately tracks AMP, apart for some finite size effects.

N=1000
) and SE, for
(a)
on and
(b)
outside the Nishimori line. The red line represents the behavior of SE while each of the 40 gray lines is an independent AMP run. State evolution tracks accurately both
m
and

In Fig.
3
, we plot the full phase diagram in the
\hat{\lambda},\lambda
plane. We can identify several phases:

•

RS unstable phase
The yellow area in the right panel is the region where AMP does not converge. The non-convergence of AMP is synonym of the replica symmetric solution being unstable and the RSB being required to correctly model the measure. The equivalence between the convergence of AMP and the stability of the RS phase is further discussed in section
IV.2
.

•

Paramagnetic phase
corresponding to
m=0,\;q=0
. The boundary of this region can be found analytically (see Appendix
C
) and is given by the curve
\lambda=\min(1,\hat{\lambda}^{-1})
. Throughout the paramagnetic phase, AMP will output the non-informative estimator
\hat{x}=(0,0,\dots,0)
. This corresponds to the estimated marginals being uniform on the circle.

•

Ferromagnetic phase
defined the intersection of the region where
m>0,\;q>0
and the RS stable phase. The marginals produced by AMP are partially aligned with the planted state (or partially ordered). Moreover the RS solution correctly describes the structure of the Gibbs measure.

•

Mixed phase
where
m>0,\;q>0
but the RS solution is unstable. This region corresponds to the slice between the ferromagnetic and spin glass phase.

•

Spin glass phase
where
m=0,\;q>0
. In the spin glass phase, AMP’s marginals are polarized towards a random value which is uncorrelated with the planted configuration. In this phase, AMP also encounters convergence problems, and the RS solution is unstable.
In Appendix
C
we show that the upper boundary of the spin glass phase, dividing the
m=0
from the
m>0
region, can also be expressed analytically in an implicit form. Notice that since we are in the RS unstable phase, the RS prediction for this boundary is not reliable, thus RSB is needed to evaluate the correct boundary between the spin glass and the mixed phase.

More quantitative information about the values of
q
and
m
is found in Fig.
4
. In this figure, the top row represents the phase diagram obtained via AMP, while the bottom row was obtained from SE. First, we notice that across all transition lines, both
m
and
q
are continuous.
Looking at the panels showing

, we see that
q
is always increasing in
\hat{\lambda}
. This can be explained by interpreting
\hat{\lambda}^{1/2}
as an inverse temperature, then it’s clear that spins should be more and more polarized with decreasing temperature. From a mathematical perspective according to (
9
),
\hat{\lambda}^{1/2}
controls the norm of

and hence that of
\hat{x}
.

III.4
Convergence of AMP

It is known that on the Nishimori line the RS ansatz is exact
Zdeborová and Krzakala 2016
;
Miolane 2017
, hence AMP estimates the marginals of
P(x|Y)
exactly in the large size limit. The same cannot be said about the rest of the phase diagram. Therefore we need to distinguish between the true behavior of the model and that of the algorithm. For example, AMP’s shortcomings are evident when the iterations fail to converge. In the left panel of Fig.
5
, we plot the convergence time of the algorithm across the phase diagram. For small enough
\lambda
, when
\hat{\lambda}
is increased, we always encounter a phase in which AMP does not converge. AMP’s convergence has important links to RS stability. In the Sherrington Kirkpatrick (SK) model, it was proved in
Bolthausen 2014
that the RS stability line delimits the region where AMP converges. This property is general and also in our case, the 1RSB analysis will confirm that convergence of AMP and stability of RS coincide. We can thus state that AMP converges
iff
the RS solution is stable.
Analytically, we study AMP’s convergence by looking at stability under a random perturbation
h^{(t)}\mapsto h^{(t)}+\delta h^{(t)}
, with
\delta h^{(t)}_{k}=\epsilon e^{i\phi_{k}^{(t)}}
, with
\phi_{k}^{(t)}\sim\text{Unif}([0,2\pi])
. By propagating the perturbation through the AMP equations (more details are provided in Appendix
D
), we obtain that the perturbation norm grows according to the law
\EX_{\delta h}\left[\left\lVert\delta h^{(t+1)}\right\rVert^{2}\right]=(1-c_{\text{AMP}}^{(t)})\EX_{\delta h}\left[\left\lVert\delta h^{(t)}\right\rVert^{2}\right]
, with

\displaystyle c_{\text{AMP}}^{(t)}=1-\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k}|Y_{ik}|^{2}\right]

(16)

\displaystyle\times\left[\eta_{r}(|h_{i}^{(t)}|)^{2}+\left(\eta_{r}(|h_{i}^{(t)}|)+|h_{i}^{(t)}|\eta_{r}^{\prime}(|h_{i}^{(t)}|)\right)^{2}\right]

(17)

where the expectation is with respect to the randomness in the perturbation and
\eta_{r}(r)=\frac{1}{r}\frac{I_{1}(2r)}{I_{0}(2r)}
.
AMP will converge if the norm of the perturbation decreases in time (
c_{\text{AMP}}>0
), and will oscillate otherwise. This quantity can also be tracked using state evolution

\displaystyle c^{(t)}_{\text{SE}}=1-\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{2}\EX_{z}\left[\eta_{r}(|h|)^{2}+\left(\eta_{r}(|h|)+|h|\eta_{r}^{\prime}(|h|)\right)^{2}\right]

(18)

IV
1RSB analysis

To overcome the shortcomings of the RS approach and AMP, we must carry out a more refined analysis which takes into account replica symmetry breaking. Methods such as BP or AMP basically ‘fit’ the Gibbs measure onto a Bethe measure
Zdeborová and Krzakala 2016
. When this ansatz turns out to be correct, we say the model is in a replica symmetric phase and AMP converges, giving an accurate estimate of the marginals. Otherwise, the Gibbs measure
\mu
can break into a multitude of Bethe measures which we index by
\alpha
, i.e.
\mu=\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{\alpha}Z_{\alpha}\mu_{\alpha}

Krzakała et al. 2007
Coja-Oghlan and Perkins 2019
. The total partition function is then
Z=\sum_{\alpha}Z_{\alpha}
, where
Z_{\alpha}
is the partition function of a single Bethe state (computable by exponentiating the
f_{\text{Bethe}}
of the single state).
Message passing algorithms will exhibit a multitude of fixed points, each corresponding to one of the states.
Replica symmetry breaking allows us to account for this structure of the Gibbs measure.

In the first step of the construction, called 1-step RSB (1RSB), we postulate the existence of a function
\Sigma(f)
, called the complexity function
Bray and Moore 1980
, with the property that the number of states with a free entropy
f_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{N}\log Z_{\alpha}
close to a value of
f
is, at the leading order,
e^{N\Sigma(f)}
. The best approximation to the free entropy of the system, at the 1RSB level then reads
Zamponi 2010

\displaystyle\tilde{\Phi}_{\text{1RSB}}=\frac{1}{N}\log\sum_{\alpha}e^{Nf_{\alpha}}=\frac{1}{N}\log\int df\,e^{N(\Sigma(f)+f)}

\displaystyle=\sup_{f:\Sigma(f)\geq 0}\left[\Sigma(f)+f\right]=\Sigma(\tilde{f}^{*})+\tilde{f}^{*}

(20)

Here,
\tilde{f}^{*}
is the free entropy of each of the equilibrium states and it’s determined by the condition

\tilde{f}^{*}=\argmax_{f:\Sigma(f)>0}\left(\Sigma(f)+f\right)=\begin{cases}f:\Sigma^{\prime}(f)=-1&\text{if $\Sigma(f)>0$}\\
f_{0}&\text{otherwise }\end{cases}

(21)

with
f_{0}
the largest root of the equation
\Sigma(f)=0
.
We will call
\tilde{\Phi}_{\text{1RSB}}
the
true
1RSB free entropy.
By introducing the positivity constraint on
\Sigma
in (
21
), we are discarding the unphysical solutions with
\Sigma(f)<0
. The negative complexity would in fact mean that there is an exponentially (in
N
) small probability of finding a state with the given free entropy.

From ASP we will obtain the related quantity, which we call
replicated
free entropy

\Phi_{\text{1RSB}}(s)=\sup_{f}\left[\Sigma(f)+sf\right]=\Sigma(f^{*}(s))+sf^{*}(s)

(22)

where
f^{*}
satisfies
\Sigma^{\prime}(f^{*})=-s
.
Notice that from (
22
) we have the characterization
f^{*}(s)=\frac{\partial\Phi_{\text{1RSB}}}{\partial s}
.
We also remark that we can access
\Sigma(f)
by computing it parametrically in
s
.

\Sigma(f^{*}(s))=\Phi_{\text{1RSB}}(s)-sf^{*}(s)

(23)

The next goal is to find
\tilde{\Phi}_{\text{1RSB}}
, starting from the newly found
\Sigma
and
f^{*}
. One difference between
\Phi_{\text{1RSB}}
and
\tilde{\Phi}_{\text{1RSB}}
is the relaxation of the positivity constraint on
\Sigma
. This implies that naively setting
s=1
might not give
\tilde{\Phi}_{\text{1RSB}}
. Instead we obtain
\tilde{\Phi}_{\text{1RSB}}=\Sigma(f^{*}(s_{\star}))+f^{*}(s_{\star})
for a well chosen
s_{\star}

s_{\star}=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if $\Sigma(f^{*}(1))\geq 0$}\\
-\Sigma^{\prime}(f_{0})&\text{ if $\Sigma(f^{*}(1))<0$ }\end{cases}

(24)

and justify this choice in Appendix
E
.
Basically,
s_{\star}
is the value of
s
for which the replicated free entropy best approximates the Gibbs measure
Mezard and Montanari 2009
.

IV.1
Approximate survey propagation

In this section, we derive the ASP algorithm. Survey propagation (SP) is a message passing algorithm developed originally in the context of random constraint satisfaction problems
Mézard et al. 2002
. The approach has then been extended to several other inference and optimization problems
Lucibello et al. 2019
Mezard and Montanari 2009
. ASP, through its state evolution, also allows us to compute the 1RSB replicated free entropy exactly in the
N\to\infty
limit.
Appendix
F
provides the details of the derivation, here we only go over the key steps.

In this work we follow the derivation of ASP introduced in
Antenucci et al. 2019
.
We introduce a replicated system composed of
s
independent replicas. We indicate with
x_{i}=(x_{i}^{(1)},\dots,x_{i}^{(s)})
, the replicated variables.
First we write BP for the replicated system
\{x^{(a)}\}_{a=1}^{s}
:

\displaystyle{m}_{{i}\rightarrow{}{ij}}(x_{i})=\frac{1}{Z_{i\to ij}}\prod_{k\neq i,j}{m}_{{ki}\rightarrow{}{i}}(x_{i}),

(25)

\displaystyle{m}_{{ij}\rightarrow{}{i}}(x_{i})=\frac{1}{Z_{ij\to i}}\int dx_{j}{m}_{{j}\rightarrow{}{ij}}(x_{j})

\displaystyle\times\exp\left[2\sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{N}}\sum_{a=1}^{s}\mathrm{Re}\left(Y_{ij}\overline{x}^{(a)}_{i}x^{(a)}_{j}\right)\right].

(26)

Then we relax BP by parametrizing the messages with their means and covariances

\displaystyle\langle x_{i}^{(a)}\rangle=\hat{x}_{i\rightarrow ij}

(27)

\displaystyle\langle x_{i}^{(a)}\overline{x}_{i}^{(b)}\rangle=\begin{cases}\left|\hat{x}_{i\rightarrow ij}\right|^{2}+\Delta_{i\rightarrow ij}&if $a\neq b$\\
1&if $a=b$.\end{cases}

(28)

with
\langle\cdot\rangle
being the average with respect to
m_{i\rightarrow ij}
.

\Delta
is a measure of how coupled the replicas are: when
\Delta=0
the replicas are independent and we recover the BP equations for
s
independent replicas. The crucial assumption of the 1RSB approximation is that all pairs of replicas
(a,b):\;a\neq b
have the same
\Delta
.
\hat{x}
instead plays the same role as in AMP.
Finally, we remove the dependence of the messages on the target node and correct for it by introducing the Onsager term. Once again, this is only possible thanks to the fully connected nature of the model.
After accomplishing these steps, we arrive to the ASP equations

\displaystyle{T}_{i}^{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}Y_{ik}{\hat{x}}_{k}^{t}-\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}{\hat{x}}_{i}^{t-1}\sum_{k}|Y_{ik}|^{2}\frac{d{\hat{x}}_{k}}{dT_{k}}

(29)

\displaystyle{V}_{i}^{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}|Y_{ik}|^{2}{\Delta}_{k}^{t}

(30)

\displaystyle{\hat{x}}_{i}^{t+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\int_{\mathbb{C}}d{h}\,e^{-{V}_{i}^{t}|h|^{2}}\left[I_{0}(2|T_{i}+V_{i}h|)\right]^{s-1}\left[\frac{T_{i}+V_{i}h}{|T_{i}+V_{i}h|}I_{1}(2|T_{i}+V_{i}h|)\right]}{\int_{\mathbb{C}}d{h}\,e^{-{V}_{i}^{t}|h|^{2}}\left[I_{0}(2|T_{i}+V_{i}h|)\right]^{s}}

(31)

\displaystyle{\Delta}_{i}^{t+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\int_{\mathbb{C}}d{h}\,e^{-{V}_{i}^{t}|h|^{2}}\left[I_{0}(2|T_{i}+V_{i}h|)\right]^{s-2}\left[I_{1}(2|T_{i}+V_{i}h|)\right]^{2}}{\int_{\mathbb{C}}d{h}\,e^{-{V}_{i}^{t}|h|^{2}}\left[I_{0}(2|T_{i}+V_{i}h|)\right]^{s}}-|{\hat{x}}_{i}^{t+1}|^{2},

(32)

with
\frac{d{\hat{x}}_{k}}{dT_{k}}
, computed numerically via finite differences.
It can be shown that by setting
s=1
, one recovers AMP.
The ASP equations are equipped with their SE, which allows us to track the scalar quantities
m
,
q
and
\Delta\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}\Delta_{i}^{t}
. SE reads

\displaystyle T^{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\hat{\lambda}\lambda}m^{t}+\sqrt{\hat{\lambda}q^{t}/2}z

(33)

\displaystyle V^{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{\lambda}\Delta^{t}

(34)

\displaystyle m^{t+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{z}\left[\hat{x}(T^{t},V^{t})\right]

(35)

\displaystyle q^{t+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{z}\left[|\hat{x}(T^{t},V^{t})|^{2}\right]

(36)

\displaystyle\Delta^{t+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{z}\left[\Delta(T^{t},V^{t},q^{t})\right].

(37)

The functions
\hat{x}(T,V)
and
\Delta(T,V,q)
are the same as in (
29
), but without indices and with
|\hat{x}_{i}|^{2}
replaced by
q
.
Finally, in (
38
) and (
39
) we compute respectively the 1RSB replicated free entropy for ASP and the corresponding free entropy of the states selected by
s
.

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathrm{1RSB}}(\lambda,\hat{\lambda},s)=-s\sqrt{\hat{\lambda}\lambda}m^{2}+\frac{s^{2}\hat{\lambda}}{2}q^{2}-s\hat{\lambda}(q+\Delta)-\frac{s(s-1)\hat{\lambda}}{2}(\Delta+q)^{2}

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}_{z}\left[\log\left(\frac{\hat{\lambda}\Delta}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{C}}dh\,\exp(-\hat{\lambda}\Delta|h|^{2})[I_{0}(2|T+\hat{\lambda}\Delta h|)]^{s}\right)\right]

(38)

\displaystyle f^{*}(s,\lambda,\hat{\lambda})=\frac{\partial\Phi_{\mathrm{\text{1RSB}}}(s,\lambda,\hat{\lambda})}{\partial s}=-\sqrt{\lambda\hat{\lambda}}m^{2}+s\lambda q^{2}-\lambda(q+\Delta)-\frac{(2s-1)\lambda}{2}(\Delta+q)^{2}

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}_{z}\left[\frac{\int_{\mathbb{C}}dh\exp(-\lambda\Delta|h|^{2})\log[I_{0}(2|T+\lambda\Delta h|)][I_{0}(2|T+\lambda\Delta h|)]^{s}}{\int_{\mathbb{C}}dh\exp(-\lambda\Delta|h|^{2})[I_{0}(2|T+\lambda\Delta h|)]^{s}}\right]

(39)

The derivations are found in Appendix
G
.
In both the free entropy and SE,
z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)+i\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. Analogously to AMP, the stationary points of the replicated free entropy
\Phi_{\text{1RSB}}
with respect to
m,q,\Delta
are fixed points of the ASP equations; a derivation of this fact is provided in Appendix
G
. By manipulating the equations, it can be shown that there are two ways to recover the RS solution: either by setting
s=1
or by having
\Delta=0
.

V
Discussion

In this work, we studied the planted XY model defined by (
4
) and (
8
). Our model enjoys multiple connections, both with spin glasses and inference problems. In the inference setting, it corresponds to the angular synchronization problem, instead from the statistical physics point of view, it can be seen as an XY, mean field spin glass with a ferromagnetic bias. The two problems are related by a change of variables.
We first obtain the RS phase diagram where we recognize several regions, a paramagnetic phase where the spins are each uniformly distributed on the circle, a ferromagnetic phase where a partial global order arises (spins approximately point in the same direction) but the replica symmetric solution is stable, a mixed phase where replica symmetry is not stable and magnetization is positive, and a spin glass phase in which each spin is partially frozen in a random direction.

To mitigate the instability of the RS solution, we resort to the ASP algorithm. ASP is the 1RSB version of AMP, allowing to model the existence of multiple states in the Gibbs measure, each state corresponding to a fixed point of AMP. In the 1RSB formalism, we obtain a better estimate of the free entropy, and we can also count the number of states with each free entropy through the complexity function
\Sigma(f)
. All the estimates are obtained through the state evolution of ASP.

One question remains open: Is the 1RSB approach exact or are further levels of RSB required? There are two failure modes of 1RSB: either several 1RSB states form a cluster together, or each 1RSB state breaks into a multitude of smaller states
Montanari et al. 2004
Mezard and Montanari 2009
Rivoire et al. 2004
. Studying ASP’s convergence allows us to detect the first kind of instability. We can then conclude that the 1RSB ansatz is incorrect in at least part of the phase diagram (i.e. where ASP does not converge or equivalently where
c_{\text{ASP}}<0
). In this phase likely the Full-RSB ansatz would be needed to provide the exact solution.
In the region where ASP converges and
c_{\text{ASP}}>0
one would need to evaluate the 2RSB solution to conclusively decide about the exactness of the 1RSB, this is left for future work. Interestingly in this respect the XY model behaves differently from SK where ASP never converges throughout the phase diagram
Antenucci et al. 2019
and the FRSB solution describes the entirety of the RSB phase. Should the 1RSB solution be exact, the XY model would represent a case of a system where continuous RSB (i.e.
\Delta
is continuous at the RS instability threshold) coexists with a 1RSB phase.

Appendix A
Circular distributions

In this appendix we recall some basic facts about probability measures on the unit circle, and their connection to our setting. In the following we will always assume that
x=e^{i\theta}
is a complex variable of unit modulus.
Let
x\sim P(x)
, then we define the raw moments of
x
as

m_{n}=\oint_{\Gamma}dxe^{inx}P(x).

(40)

In analogy with the linear case one can define the circular mean and variance respectively as
m_{1}
and
1-|m_{1}|
.
We shall explore a family of circular distributions that appear in the analysis of the planted XY model.

Suppose
x
belongs to the following family of probability measures spanned by the complex parameter
h=|h|e^{i\phi}
.

P(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi I_{0}(|h|)}e^{\operatorname{Re}(x\overline{h})},

(41)

where
I_{k}
is the modified Bessel function of the first kind of order
k
.
The real part can now be written as
\operatorname{Re}(x\overline{h})=|h|\cos(\theta-\phi)
, thus yielding a Von Mises distribution
Jammalamadaka and Sengupta 2001
for the variable
\theta
:

P(\theta)=\frac{1}{2\pi I_{0}(|h|)}e^{|h|\cos(\theta-\phi)}.

(42)

The moments of
P
are

m_{n}=\EX_{P}[x^{n}]=\frac{I_{n}(|h|)}{I_{0}(|h|)}

(43)

In fact

\displaystyle 2\pi I_{0}(|h|)m_{n}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{in\theta}e^{|h|cos(\theta)}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left[\cos(n\theta)+i\sin(n\theta)\right]e^{|h|cos(\theta)}

(44)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\cos(n\theta)e^{|h|cos(\theta)}=2\pi I_{n}(|h|).

The last equality follows from the definition of
I_{n}
.

Appendix B
AMP and state evolution derivation

We shall now briefly recall the derivation of the AMP algorithm. While there exist several ways to do so, we choose an approach similar to
Lesieur et al. 2017
based on manipulating the Belief Propagation equations.
Belief propagation for the planted XY model reads

\displaystyle m_{i\rightarrow ij}(x_{i})=\frac{1}{Z_{i\rightarrow ij}}\prod_{k\neq i,j}m_{ki\rightarrow i}(x_{i})

(45)

\displaystyle m_{ij\rightarrow j}(x_{j})=\frac{1}{Z_{ij\rightarrow j}}\oint dx_{i}\exp\left(2\sqrt{\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}}\operatorname{Re}(Y_{ij}\overline{x_{i}}x_{j})\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times m_{i\rightarrow ij}(x_{i}),

(46)

where all the integrals are on the unit circle in the complex plane.
This is a set of
N^{2}
functional equations: it would be impossible to use them in practice on a computer. The first step to obtain AMP consists of relaxing BP: this means finding a parametric form of the messages under which the BP equations can be closed.
We first expand the exponent in (
B
) equation:

\displaystyle m_{ij\rightarrow j}(x_{j})=\frac{1}{Z_{ij\rightarrow j}}\oint dx_{i}\left[1+2\sqrt{\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}}Re(Y_{ij}\overline{x_{i}}x_{j})+2\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}\operatorname{Re}(Y_{ij}\overline{x_{i}}x_{j})^{2}+O(N^{-3/2})\right]m_{i\rightarrow ij}(x_{i})=

(47)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{ij\rightarrow j}}\oint dx_{i}\left[1+2\sqrt{\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}}\operatorname{Re}(Y_{ij}\overline{x_{i}}x_{j})+\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}\operatorname{Re}(Y_{ij}^{2}\overline{x_{i}}^{2}x_{j}^{2})+\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}|Y_{ij}|^{2}+O(N^{-3/2})\right]m_{i\rightarrow ij}(x_{i})=

(48)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{ij\rightarrow j}}\left[1+2\sqrt{\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}}\operatorname{Re}(Y_{ij}\overline{\hat{x}}_{i\rightarrow ij}x_{j})+O(N^{-1})\right]=\frac{1}{Z_{ij\rightarrow j}}\exp\left[2\sqrt{\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}}\operatorname{Re}(Y_{ij}\overline{\hat{x}}_{i\rightarrow ij}x_{j})\right]+O(N^{-1}).

(49)

From the first to the second line we used that
\operatorname{Re}(z)^{2}=\frac{1}{2}(\operatorname{Re}(z^{2})+|z|^{2})
. Then from the second to the third line we dropped the
O(N^{-1})
terms since these are subdominant in the
N\to\infty
limit and we performed the average introducing
\hat{x}_{i\rightarrow ij}=\EX_{m_{i\rightarrow ij}}[x_{i}]
.
The normalization constant can be computed
Z_{ij\rightarrow j}=2\pi I_{0}\left(2\sqrt{\hat{\lambda}/N}|Y_{ij}\hat{x}_{i\rightarrow ij}|\right)

By substituting the last expression in (
45
) we get

\displaystyle m_{i\rightarrow ij}(x_{i})=\frac{1}{Z_{i\rightarrow ij}}\exp\left[2\sqrt{\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}}\operatorname{Re}\left(x_{i}\sum_{k\neq i,j}Y_{ki}\overline{\hat{x}}_{k\rightarrow ki}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi I_{0}(h_{ij\rightarrow j})}\exp\left[2\operatorname{Re}\left(x_{i}\overline{h}_{ij\rightarrow j}\right)\right],\quad

(50)

Where in the last line we defined
h_{ij\rightarrow j}=\sqrt{\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}}\sum_{k\neq i,j}Y_{ik}\hat{x}_{k\rightarrow ki}
.
We’ve finally arrived to the relaxed BP-equations:

\displaystyle h^{(t)}_{ij\rightarrow j}=\sqrt{\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}}\sum_{k\neq i,j}Y_{ik}\hat{x}^{(t)}_{k\rightarrow ki}

(51)

\displaystyle\hat{x}_{i\rightarrow ij}^{(t+1)}=\EX_{m^{(t)}_{i\rightarrow ij}}[x_{i}]=\eta(h^{(t)}_{ij\rightarrow j}),\qquad\eta(h)=\frac{h}{|h|}\frac{I_{1}(|2h|)}{I_{0}(|2h|)}

(52)

For the computation of
\EX_{m_{i\rightarrow ij}}[x_{i}]
we defer to appendix
A
.
To complete the derivation of AMP we now remove the dependence of the target variable by expanding the relaxed-BP equations.
First define the single site fields as

h_{i}=h_{ij\rightarrow j}+\sqrt{\hat{\lambda}/N}Y_{ij}\hat{x}_{j\rightarrow ji}=\sqrt{\hat{\lambda}/N}\sum_{k\neq i}Y_{ik}\hat{x}_{k\rightarrow ki}

(53)

Similarly we introduce the variables
\hat{x}
, which, at convergence, represent AMP’s approximation to the system’s marginals.

\hat{x}_{i}^{(t+1)}=\eta(h_{i}^{(t)})

(54)

the goal is to replace variables indexed on edges with the new variables indexed on vertices: to do so we have to keep track of the error

\displaystyle\hat{x}^{(t)}_{k\rightarrow ki}-\hat{x}_{k}^{(t)}=\eta(h^{(t-1)}_{ki\rightarrow i})-\eta(h^{(t-1)}_{k})

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial h}(h_{k}^{(t-1)})\sqrt{\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}}Y_{ki}\hat{x}^{(t-1)}_{i\rightarrow ik}+O(N^{-1})

(55)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial h}(h_{k}^{(t-1)})\sqrt{\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}}Y_{ki}\hat{x}_{i}^{(t-1)}+O(N^{-1})

For a detailed explanation of the form of
\partial\eta/\partial h
see the paragraph
B.0.1
.
Plugging this into
h_{i}^{(t)}
we have

\displaystyle h_{i}^{(t)}=2\sqrt{\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}}\sum_{k\neq i}Y_{ik}\hat{x}_{k\rightarrow ki}^{(t)}\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}}\sum_{k\neq i}Y_{ik}\left(\hat{x}_{k}^{(t)}-\sqrt{\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}}Y_{ki}\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial h}(h_{k}^{(t-1)})\hat{x}_{i}^{(t-1)}\right)

(56)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}}\sum_{k\neq i}Y_{ik}\hat{x}_{k}^{(t)}-\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}\hat{x}_{i}^{(t-1)}\sum_{k\neq i}|Y_{ik}|^{2}\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial h}(h_{k}^{(t-1)})

Equation (
54
) together with (
B
) constitute the AMP algorithm.

B.0.1
Derivation of the Onsager term

In this section we derive the form of
\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial h}
.
Notice that
\eta
is not an analytic function, so its derivative cannot be expressed as a complex number, instead it takes the form of a
2\times 2
Jacobian. Writing
\eta(h)=\eta(x+iy)=u(x,y)+iv(x,y)
, the directional derivative of
\eta
along
z
is
\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial z}=\operatorname{Re}(z)\left[\partial_{x}u(x,y)+i\partial xv(x,y)\right]+\operatorname{Im}(z)\left[\partial_{u}u(x,y)+i\partial_{y}v(x,y)\right]
.
Decomposing
z
along the directions respectively orthogonal and parallel to
h
, and with the notation
r=|h|
we obtain the following alternative expression

\displaystyle\eta(h+z)-\eta(h)=\left[z-\operatorname{Re}\left(\frac{\overline{h}}{|h|}z\right)\frac{h}{|h|}\right]\eta_{r}(r)

\displaystyle+\operatorname{Re}\left(\frac{\overline{h}}{|h|}z\right)\frac{h}{|h|}\partial_{r}(r\eta_{r}(r))+O(|z|^{2})

(57)

where we have defined
\eta_{r}(r)=\frac{1}{r}\frac{I_{1}(2r)}{I_{0}(2r)}
.
In the computation of the Onsager term we have

\displaystyle h_{i}^{(t)}=\sqrt{\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}}\sum_{k\neq i}Y_{ik}\eta(h_{k}^{(t-1)})-\sqrt{\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}}\sum_{k\neq i}Y_{ik}\left[\eta(h_{k}^{(t-1)})\vphantom{\sqrt{\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}}}\right.

\displaystyle\left.-\eta\left(h_{k}^{(t-1)}-\sqrt{\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}}\overline{Y}_{ik}\hat{x}_{i}^{(t-1)}\right)\right]

(58)

Let us fix
k
in the second summation and look at one term: to lighten the notation rename
h_{k}^{(t-1)}\mapsto h,\;Y_{ik}\mapsto Y,\;\hat{x}_{i}^{(t-1)}\mapsto x
.
Applying (
57
) we have

\displaystyle Y\left[\eta(h)-\eta\left(h-\sqrt{\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}}\overline{Y}x\right)\right]=\sqrt{\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}}Y\left[\overline{Y}x-\operatorname{Re}\left(\frac{\overline{h}}{|h|}\overline{Y}x\right)\frac{h}{|h|}\right]\eta_{r}(r)+\sqrt{\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}}Y\operatorname{Re}\left(\frac{\overline{h}}{|h|}\overline{Y}x\right)\frac{h}{|h|}\partial_{r}(r\eta_{r}(r))+O(1/N)=

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}}\left\{|Y|^{2}x+Y\frac{h}{|h|}\operatorname{Re}\left(\frac{\overline{h}}{|h|}\overline{Y}x\right)\left[\partial_{r}(r\eta_{r}(r))-\eta_{r}(r)\right]\right\}+O(1/N)=

(59)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}}\left\{\frac{1}{2}x|Y|^{2}\left(\partial_{r}(r\eta_{r}(r))+\eta_{r}(r)\right)+\frac{Y^{2}h^{2}\overline{x}}{2|h|^{2}}\left[\partial_{r}(r\eta_{r}(r))-\eta_{r}(r)\right]\right\}+O(1/N),

where in the last step we expanded
\operatorname{Re}(z)=\frac{1}{2}(z+\overline{z})
. Substituting this back into (
58
) and summing over
k
, one sees that the second term is negligible because
Y^{2}
is a zero mean random variable. Moreover the by the properties of the Bessel functions we have the identity
\frac{1}{2}\left(\partial_{r}(r\eta_{r}(r))+\eta_{r}(r)\right)=1-|\eta(r)|^{2}
. To conclude (
B
) holds with

\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial h}(h_{k}^{(t-1)})=1-|\eta(h_{k}^{(t-1)})|^{2}

(60)

B.1
State evolution heuristic derivation

One of the elements which distinguishes AMP from other iterative algorithms is the ability (in the
N\to\infty
limit ) to track its dynamics through the state evolution equations. In particular we will derive closed equations for the two observables

\displaystyle m=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{x}_{i}\qquad q=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\hat{x}_{i}|^{2},

(61)

representing respectively the alignment of the marginals with the planted configuration and how concentrated each marginal is.
We start by deriving an iterative equation for
m^{(t)}
.

\displaystyle m^{(t+1)}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\eta\left(\sqrt{\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}Y_{ik}\hat{x}_{k}^{(t)}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\eta\left(\frac{\sqrt{\lambda\hat{\lambda}}}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\hat{x}_{k}+\sqrt{\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}W_{ik}\hat{x}_{k}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\eta\left(\sqrt{\lambda\hat{\lambda}}m^{(t)}+\sqrt{\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{2}q^{(t)}}z_{i}\right)

(62)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{C}}dz\;e^{-\frac{1}{2}|z|^{2}}\eta\left(\sqrt{\lambda\hat{\lambda}}m^{(t)}+\sqrt{\hat{\lambda}q^{(t)}/2}\;z\right)

In the second passage we defined
z_{i}=\sqrt{\frac{2N}{q^{(t)}}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}W_{ik}\hat{x}_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)+i\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, and in the end we replaced the sum over
i
with an integral, since
N\to\infty
and
z_{i}
s are assumed to be independent.
The whole derivation revolves around the assumption of independence between
\hat{x}^{(t)}
and
W
. This is of course not the case, because
\hat{x}
will depend on
W
through previous iterations, however the Onsager term in the iterations re-establishes asymptotic independence as explained in
Donoho et al. 2009
Bayati and Montanari 2011
.
Similarly for
q
we have

\displaystyle q^{(t+1)}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left|\eta\left(\sqrt{\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}Y_{ik}\hat{x}_{k}^{(t)}\right)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{C}}dz\;e^{-\frac{1}{2}|z|^{2}}\left|\eta\left(\sqrt{\lambda\hat{\lambda}}m^{(t)}+\sqrt{\hat{\lambda}q^{(t)}/2}\;z\right)\right|^{2}.

(63)

B.2
Simplification of state evolution

We can further simplify SE equations. The use of this is to reduce the number of integrals to be done numerically.
We start by simplifying
f_{m}
:

\displaystyle f_{m}(m,q)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{C}}dz\;e^{-\frac{1}{2}|z|^{2}}\eta\left(\sqrt{\lambda\hat{\lambda}}m+\sqrt{\frac{\hat{\lambda}q}{2}}z\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int dx\,dy\;e^{-\frac{1}{2}(x^{2}+y^{2})}\frac{a(x)}{\sqrt{a(x)^{2}+\hat{\lambda}qy^{2}/2}}

\displaystyle\times\tilde{\eta}\left(\sqrt{a(x)^{2}+\hat{\lambda}qy^{2}/2}\right)\quad

(64)

where we split
z=x+iy
and we introduced
a(x)=\sqrt{\lambda\hat{\lambda}}m+\sqrt{\hat{\lambda}q/2}x
. In principle
f_{m}
could be complex however the imaginary part is zero.
By changing variables according to
x^{\prime}=a(x),\;y^{\prime}=\sqrt{\hat{\lambda}q/2}y
we get

\displaystyle f_{m}(m,q)=\frac{1}{\pi\hat{\lambda}q}\int dxdy\;\exp\left[-\frac{1}{\hat{\lambda}q}(y^{2}+(x-\sqrt{\lambda\hat{\lambda}}m)^{2})\right]

\displaystyle\times\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}}\tilde{\eta}\left(\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}\right)=\frac{2}{\pi\hat{\lambda}q}e^{-\frac{\lambda m^{2}}{q}}\qquad

(65)

\displaystyle\times\int_{0}^{\infty}dr\int_{-r}^{r}dx\;\exp\left[-\frac{r^{2}}{\hat{\lambda}q}+2\frac{m}{q}\sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{\hat{\lambda}}}x\right]\frac{x}{\sqrt{r^{2}-x^{2}}}\tilde{\eta}\left(r\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\hat{\lambda}q}e^{-\frac{\lambda m^{2}}{q}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dr\;r\exp\left[-\frac{r^{2}}{\hat{\lambda}q}\right]I_{1}\left(2\frac{m}{q}\sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{\hat{\lambda}}}r\right)\tilde{\eta}\left(r\right)

where in the second line we changed variables to
x,r=\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}
and in the last passage we used that
\int_{-r}^{r}dx\,e^{ax}\frac{x}{\sqrt{r^{2}-x^{2}}}=\pi rI_{1}(ar)
.
An analogous procedure also yields a simplified equation for
q
:

\displaystyle f_{q}(m,q)=\frac{2}{\hat{\lambda}q}e^{-\frac{\lambda m^{2}}{q}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dr\;r\exp\left[-\frac{r^{2}}{\hat{\lambda}q}\right]

(66)

\displaystyle\times I_{0}\left(2\frac{m}{q}\sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{\hat{\lambda}}}r\right)\tilde{\eta}\left(r\right)^{2}.

(67)

Appendix C
Fixed point analysis

We write SE equations in vectorial form as

x^{t+1}=(m^{t+1},q^{t+1})=f(x^{t})=(f_{m}(m^{t},q^{t}),f_{q}(m^{t},q^{t})).

(68)

We say
\tilde{x}
is a fixed point if
f(\tilde{x})=\tilde{x}
.
Let
\tilde{x}
be a fixed point and denote
\Delta=x-\tilde{x}
. Then at the linear order in
f
Delta will evolve as

\Delta^{t+1}_{i}=\sum_{j}\frac{\partial f_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}(\tilde{x})\Delta^{t}_{j}=(\left[J_{f}(\tilde{x})\right]^{t+1}\Delta^{0})_{i}

(69)

In order to characterize the behavior of
\Delta
(and hence study the stability of
\tilde{x}
) we must then look at the Jacobian. In the following we study this Jacobian for multiple fixed points.

C.1
m=0,\;q=0

We first evaluate the stability with respect to
m
.

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f_{m}}{\partial m}(m,q=0)\bigg|_{m=0}=\frac{\partial}{\partial m}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{C}}dz\;e^{-\frac{1}{2}|z|^{2}}

\displaystyle\times\eta\left(\sqrt{\lambda\hat{\lambda}}m\right)\bigg|_{m=0}=\sqrt{\hat{\lambda}\lambda}\eta^{\prime}(0)=\sqrt{\hat{\lambda}\lambda}.

(70)

We now examine the stability with respect to
q
. For this purpose define
\tilde{\eta}=|\eta|
.

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f_{q}}{\partial q}(q,m=0)\bigg|_{q=0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial q}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int dz\;e^{-\frac{|z|^{2}}{2}}\times\tilde{\eta}\left(\sqrt{\hat{\lambda}q/2}\;z\right)^{2}\bigg|_{q=0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial q}\int_{0}^{\infty}dr\;re^{-\frac{r^{2}}{2}}\tilde{\eta}\left(\sqrt{\hat{\lambda}q/2}\;r\right)^{2}\bigg|_{q=0}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{8q}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dr\;r^{2}e^{-\frac{r^{2}}{2}}2\tilde{\eta}\left(\sqrt{\hat{\lambda}q/2}\;r\right)

\displaystyle\times\tilde{\eta}^{\prime}\left(\sqrt{\hat{\lambda}q/2}\;r\right)\bigg|_{q=0}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{2q}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dr\;r^{3}e^{-\frac{r^{2}}{2}}\tilde{\eta}^{\prime}\left(0\right)^{2}\sqrt{\hat{\lambda}q/2}\bigg|_{q=0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{4}\int_{0}^{\infty}dr\;r^{3}e^{-\frac{r^{2}}{2}}=\hat{\lambda}

(71)

In the second to last passage we use the fact that
q\to 0
to expand
\tilde{\eta}
to the first order. Also remember that
\tilde{\eta}^{\prime}(0)=1
.

So the
m=q=0
fixed point is stable if
\hat{\lambda}<1
and
\hat{\lambda}\lambda<1
. This region is delimited by the curve
\lambda=\min(1,\hat{\lambda}^{-1})
shown in Fig.
3
.

C.2
m=0

In the spin glass phase we expect that
m=0
while
q>0
. To find the boundary with the phase where both
m
and
q
are positive we need to compute the stability of
m=0
for the value of
q
given by the converged state evolution.

Starting from (
B.2
) we compute
\partial f_{m}/\partial m
for general
q
.

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f_{m}}{\partial m}=-2\lambda\frac{m}{q}f_{m}(m,q)+\frac{2}{q^{2}\hat{\lambda}}\sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{\hat{\lambda}}}e^{-\frac{\lambda m^{2}}{q}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dr\;r^{2}\tilde{\eta}(r)

\displaystyle\times e^{-\frac{r^{2}}{\hat{\lambda}q}}\left[I_{0}\left(2\frac{m}{q}\sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{\hat{\lambda}}}r\right)+I_{2}\left(2\frac{m}{q}\sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{\hat{\lambda}}}r\right)\right]

(72)

By setting
m=0
one obtains

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f_{m}}{\partial m}\bigg|_{m=0}=\frac{2\sqrt{\lambda}}{q^{2}\hat{\lambda}^{3/2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dr\;r^{2}\tilde{\eta}(r)e^{-\frac{r^{2}}{\hat{\lambda}q}}

(73)

Appendix D
Convergence of AMP and ASP

In this appendix we study the algorithm convergence criteria for AMP and ASP given parameters
(\lambda,\hat{\lambda},s)
. First, we examine the case for AMP. We introduce some quantities that will be required for the analysis. For convenience we will sometimes treat complex numbers and functions as vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
z=(\operatorname{Re}(z),\operatorname{Im}(z))
. Accordingly we will represent
\eta(z)=\frac{z}{|z|}\frac{I_{1}(2|z|)}{I_{0}(2|z|)}
as

\eta(z)=(x\eta_{r}(r),y\eta_{r}(r))
, with
z=x+iy
,
r=\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}
and
\eta_{r}(r)=\frac{1}{r}\frac{I_{1}(2r)}{I_{0}(2r)}
. Since
\eta
is not an analytic function, its derivative cannot be expressed a a single complex number, but the whole
2\times 2
Jacobian
J\eta
is required.
We find

J\eta(x,y)=\begin{bmatrix}\eta_{r}(r)+\frac{x^{2}}{r}\eta_{r}^{\prime}(r)&\frac{xy}{r}\eta_{r}^{\prime}(r)\\
\frac{xy}{r}\eta_{r}^{\prime}(r)&\eta_{r}(r)+\frac{x^{2}}{r}\eta_{r}^{\prime}(r)\\
\end{bmatrix}

(74)

Finally we will need the following fact: let
M=\{M_{ij}\}_{i,j=1}^{2}
be a
2\times 2
matrix and
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{2}
be the euclidean norm in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Then

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\theta\left\lVert M\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta\\
\sin\theta\end{bmatrix}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(M_{11}+M_{12}+M_{21}+M_{22}\right)

(75)

We perturb the vector
h^{(t)}
with an infinitesimal vector
\delta h^{(t)}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
, where coordinates are i.i.d. uniformly distributed on a circle of radius
\epsilon
(i.e.
\delta h^{(t)}_{k}=\epsilon e^{i\phi_{k}^{(t)}},\;\;\phi_{k}^{(t)}\sim\text{Unif}([0,2\pi])
). Let
\tilde{h}^{(t)}=h^{(t)}+\delta h^{(t)}
be the perturbed vector.

If the perturbation grows in time then AMP will not converge since every fixed point would be repulsive.
Define the norm of the perturbation to be
\left\lVert\delta h\right\rVert^{2}\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}|\delta h_{k}|^{2}
. In this way we have
\left\lVert\delta h^{(t)}\right\rVert^{2}=\epsilon^{2}
.
Using (
9
), (
10
) we get

\displaystyle\delta h^{(t+1)}_{i}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}}\sum_{k}Y_{ik}\left[\eta(h^{(t)}_{k}+\delta h^{(t)}_{k})-\eta(h^{(t)}_{k})\right]+\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}\hat{x}_{i}^{(t-1)}\sum_{k}|Y_{ik}|^{2}\left[\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial h}(h_{k}^{(t-1)}+\delta h^{(t-1)}_{k})-\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial h}(h_{k}^{(t-1)})\right]=

(76)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}}\sum_{k}Y_{ik}J\eta(h^{(t)}_{k})\delta h^{(t)}_{k}+\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}\hat{x}_{i}^{(t-1)}\sum_{k}|Y_{ik}|^{2}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(\frac{\tilde{\partial\eta}}{\partial h}(r)\right)\operatorname{Re}\left(\frac{\overline{h}_{k}^{(t-1)}}{|h_{k}^{(t-1)}|}\delta h^{(t)}_{k}\right)\right]=

(77)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}}\sum_{k}Y_{ik}J\eta(h^{(t)}_{k})\delta h^{(t)}_{k}+O(N^{-1/2}).

(78)

In the second passage we introduced the notation
r=|h_{k}^{(t-1)}|
and the function
\frac{\tilde{\partial\eta}}{\partial h}(|h|)\coloneqq\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial h}(h)
. Moreover we used the fact that
|h+\delta h|-|h|=\operatorname{Re}(\frac{\overline{h}}{|h|}\delta h)+O(|\delta h|^{2})
. In the last passage we exploited the fact that
\operatorname{Re}(\frac{\overline{h}}{|h|}\delta h)
has zero average with respect to the randomness in
\delta h
, thus the Onsager term will give a
N^{-1/2}
contribution, which can be neglected. We now compute the norm of the perturbation at time
t+1

\displaystyle\left\lVert\delta h^{(t+1)}\right\rVert^{2}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}\left|\sum_{k}Y_{ik}J\eta(h^{(t)}_{k})\delta h^{(t)}_{k}\right|^{2}+\frac{c_{1}}{N}

(79)

\displaystyle=\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}\sum_{k}\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}|Y_{ik}|^{2}\right]|J\eta(h^{(t)}_{k})\delta h^{(t)}_{k}|^{2}+\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}\sum_{k,l:k\neq l}\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}Y_{ik}\overline{Y}_{il}\right]J\eta(h^{(t)}_{k})\overline{J\eta(h^{(t)}_{l})}\delta h^{(t)}_{k}\overline{\delta h^{(t)}_{l}}+\frac{c_{1}}{N}

(80)

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{=}\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}\sum_{k}|J\eta(h^{(t)}_{k})\delta h^{(t)}_{k}|^{2}+\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}\sum_{k,l:k\neq l}\left(\frac{A_{kl}}{\sqrt{N}}\right)J\eta(h^{(t)}_{k})\overline{J\eta(h^{(t)}_{l})}\delta h^{(t)}_{k}\overline{\delta h^{(t)}_{l}}+\frac{c_{2}}{\sqrt{N}}\overset{(b)}{=}\hat{\lambda}\int dh\,\int d\delta h\,P_{\text{emp}}(h,\delta h)|J\eta(h)\delta h|^{2}

(81)

\displaystyle+\hat{\lambda}\sqrt{N}\int dh_{1}\,dh_{2}\,d\delta h_{1}\,d\delta h_{2}\,dA\,P^{(2)}_{\text{emp}}(h_{1},h_{2},\delta h_{1},\delta h_{2},A)\,A\,J\eta(h_{1})\,\overline{J\eta(h_{2})}\,\delta h_{1}\,\overline{\delta h_{2}}+\frac{c_{2}}{\sqrt{N}}

We pick

Appendix F
Derivation of Approximate Survey Propagation and its state evolution

We derive the ASP algorithm as follows. First, we start from the Belief Propagation (BP) equations for a replicated model consisting of
s
independent replicas. We then put forward an ansatz for the messages, parametrizing them by their means and covariances. Propagating the ansatz through the BP equations, we are able to obtain a closed set of equations for the parameters. This procedure is general and applicable to other statistical physics models with a Boltzmann probability measure, but we will stick to the explicit form of Hamiltonian for our problem for clarity.

The BP equations are

\displaystyle{m}_{{i}\rightarrow{}{ij}}(\vec{x}_{i})=\frac{1}{Z_{i\to ij}}\prod_{a=1}^{s}P_{X}\left(x_{i}^{(a)}\right)\prod_{k\neq j}{m}_{{ki}\rightarrow{}{i}}(\vec{x}_{i})

(91)

\displaystyle{m}_{{ij}\rightarrow{}{i}}(\vec{x}_{i})=\frac{1}{Z_{ij\to i}}\int d\vec{x}_{j}{m}_{{j}\rightarrow{}{ij}}(\vec{x}_{j})

\displaystyle\times\exp\left[\sum_{a=1}^{s}2\sqrt{\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}}\mathrm{Re}\left(Y_{ij}\overline{x}^{(a)}_{i}x^{(a)}_{j}\right)\right],

(92)

where
\vec{x}_{i}=\left(x_{i}^{(1)},x_{i}^{(2)},\dots,x_{i}^{(s)}\right)
refers to the variable
x_{i}
in different replicas, and
P_{X}\left(x_{i}^{(a)}\right)=\delta(|x_{i}^{(a)}|-1)/2\pi
is the prior distribution.

The ansatz for the messages is a Gaussian distribution parametrized by the covariance of variables within and among replicas

\displaystyle{m}_{{j}\rightarrow{}{ij}}(\vec{x}_{j})=\frac{1}{Z_{j\to ij}}\int dh\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\frac{|h-{\hat{x}}_{{j}\rightarrow{}{ij}}|^{2}}{\Delta_{j\to ij}^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\times\prod_{a=1}^{s}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}|h-x_{j}^{(a)}|^{2}\right].

(93)

The ansatz produces the following correlation functions:

\displaystyle\langle x_{j}^{(a)}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\hat{x}}_{{j}\rightarrow{}{ij}}

(94)

\displaystyle\langle x_{j}^{(a)}\overline{x}_{j}^{(b)}\rangle_{a\neq b}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\Delta}_{{j}\rightarrow{}{ij}}+|{\hat{x}}_{{j}\rightarrow{}{ij}}|^{2}

(95)

\displaystyle\langle x_{j}^{(a)}\overline{x}_{j}^{(a)}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1\quad\text{by the unit norm constraint}

(96)

where

93

93

\displaystyle\exp\left[\sum_{a=1}^{s}2\sqrt{\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}}\mathrm{Re}\left(Y_{ij}\overline{x}^{(a)}_{i}x^{(a)}_{j}\right)\right]

(97)

\displaystyle=1+2\sqrt{\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}}\sum_{a=1}^{s}\mathrm{Re}\left(Y_{ij}\overline{x}_{i}^{(a)}x_{j}^{(a)}\right)

(98)

\displaystyle+\frac{\lambda}{N}|Y_{ij}|^{2}\sum_{a,b}^{s}\overline{x}_{i}^{(a)}x_{i}^{(b)}x_{j}^{(a)}\overline{x}_{j}^{(b)}+O(N^{-3/2}),

(99)

and express

in terms of the estimator

and covariance

. In the following computations the symbol
\doteq

\displaystyle{m}_{{ij}\rightarrow{}{i}}(\vec{x}_{i})=\frac{1}{Z^{\prime\prime}_{ij\to i}}\int d\vec{x}_{j}{m}_{{j}\rightarrow{}{ij}}(\vec{x}_{j})\exp\left[\sum_{a=1}^{s}2\sqrt{\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}}\mathrm{Re}\left(Y_{ij}\overline{x}^{(a)}_{i}x^{(a)}_{j}\right)\right]\doteq

(100)

\displaystyle\doteq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Z^{\prime}_{ij\to i}}\left\{1+2\sqrt{\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}}\mathrm{Re}\left(Y_{ij}{\hat{x}}_{{j}\rightarrow{}{ij}}\sum_{a=1}^{s}\overline{x}_{i}^{(a)}\right)+\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}|Y_{ij}|^{2}({\Delta}_{{j}\rightarrow{}{ij}}+|\hat{x}_{j\to ij}|^{2})\left|\sum_{a=1}^{s}x_{i}^{(a)}\right|^{2}+\right.

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}s\left(1-{\Delta}_{{j}\rightarrow{}{ij}}-|\hat{x}_{j\to ij}|^{2}\right)-\left.\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}\operatorname{Re}\left(Y_{ij}^{2}\hat{x}_{j\to ij}^{2}\left(\sum_{a=1}^{s}\overline{x}_{i}^{(a)}\right)^{2}\right)\right\}\doteq

\displaystyle\doteq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Z_{ij\to i}}\exp\left[2\sqrt{\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}}\mathrm{Re}\left(Y_{ij}{\hat{x}}_{{j}\rightarrow{}{ij}}\sum_{a=1}^{s}\overline{x}_{i}^{(a)}\right)-\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}\operatorname{Re}\left[Y_{ij}^{2}\hat{x}_{j\to ij}^{2}\right]\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}|Y_{ij}|^{2}{\Delta}_{{j}\rightarrow{}{ij}}\left|\sum_{a=1}^{s}x_{i}^{(a)}\right|^{2}\right].

(101)

Where all constants not depending on

\displaystyle\times\exp\left[2\sqrt{\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{N}}\operatorname{Re}\left(\left(\sum_{k\neq i,j}Y_{ik}\hat{x}_{k\to ik}\right)\sum_{a=1}^{s}\overline{x}_{i}^{(a)}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Z_{i\to ij}}\int_{\mathbb{C}}d{h}\,e^{-{V}_{{i}\rightarrow{}{ij}}|h|^{2}}

\displaystyle\langle x_{i}^{(a)}\rangle

\displaystyle{\hat{x}}_{{i}\rightarrow{}{ij}}\doteq\frac{\int_{\mathbb{C}}d{h}\,e^{-{V}_{{i}\rightarrow{}{ij}}|h|^{2}}\left[\int d{x}_{i}P_{\mathrm{p}}(x_{i},h,{T}_{{i}\rightarrow{}{ij}},{V}_{{i}\rightarrow{}{ij}})\right]^{s-1}\int d{x}_{i}x_{i}P_{\mathrm{p}}(x_{i},h,{T}_{{i}\rightarrow{}{ij}},{V}_{{i}\rightarrow{}{ij}})}{\int_{\mathbb{C}}d{h}\,e^{-{V}_{{i}\rightarrow{}{ij}}|h|^{2}}\left[\int d{x}_{i}P_{\mathrm{p}}(x_{i},h,{T}_{{i}\rightarrow{}{ij}},{V}_{{i}\rightarrow{}{ij}})\right]^{s}}

H.1
Interpolating integral functions

We encounter a lot of integral functions in the updating steps, especially in the ASP case where the integral dimension is larger compared to AMP. Therefore, we use numerical interpolation of integrals Eq.(
119
,
120
) to speed up both the ASP algorithm and state evolution. After try and error, the interpolation (linear) on grid
(s,\log_{10}(T),\log_{10}(V))
that we are working with is:

•

\log_{10}(T)
in range
[-7,2]
with 800 evenly spaced points,

•

\log_{10}(V)
in range
[-7,2]
with 800 evenly spaced points,

•

s
in range
[0,1]
with any grid configuration that one needs, and the grid extensively used to generate plots shown in the manuscript is chosen in the range
[0,1]
with evenly spaced points.

Such interpolation grid was shown to give SE results with precision on the order of
{10}^{-5}\text{\,}
. The most important parameters here are the resolutions of
T
and
V
, and also their range that should cover all the parameter space of
T
and
V
during any iteration run. Such a precision is the best we can do with reasonable file size and computation speed. The achieved precision is good enough for retrieving most of the quantities we study in this manuscript, but generally not enough to resolve complexity functions at the level of
{10}^{-8}\text{\,}
.

H.2
Resolving complexity functions

Because the complexity functions encountered in the manuscript can be as small as on the order of
{10}^{-8}\text{\,}
, the interpolation setting mentioned in
H.1
is not enough to resolve them. To obtain enough numerical precision to resolve complexity functions, we use the following strategies:

All the derivative operations are done analytically so only numerical integration is performed to ensure enough precision. The evaluations of Eq.(
141
,
142
) are not done through interpolation, since they only need to be evaluated once, and it’s not possible to achieve reasonable precision beyond
{10}^{-5}\text{\,}
with interpolation methods. Instead, they are numerically integrated directly using quadrature methods, with precision beyond
{10}^{-8}\text{\,}
. To obtain free energy fixed points with enough precision, we first run SE with interpolated function until it converges at the error level of
{10}^{-5}\text{\,}
due to its fast computation speed. Then we run SE with direct integration using quadrature method for 100 more steps (much slower but can finish in a reasonable amount of time, targeting a precision of fixed points on the level of
{10}^{-8}\text{\,}
), reducing the error further and approach closer to the true fixed points. The comparison with the AMP result indicates that this SE run method using a combination of interpolated functions, and numerical integration of Eq.(
141
,
142
,
119
,
120
) using quadrature methods, can generally achieved
{10}^{-8}\text{\,}
numerical precision on the results shown in Fig. 7 and 8, correctly revealing the complexity functions on the order of
{10}^{-8}\text{\,}
.
