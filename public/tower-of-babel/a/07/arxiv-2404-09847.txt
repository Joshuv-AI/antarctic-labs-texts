Statistical learning for constrained functional parameters in infinite-dimensional models with applications in fair machine learning
Razieh Nabi, Nima S. Hejazi, Mark J. van der Laan, David Benkeser

Abstract
Constrained learning has become increasingly important, especially in the realm of algorithmic fairness and machine learning. In these settings, predictive models are developed specifically to satisfy pre-defined notions of fairness. Here, we study the general problem of constrained statistical machine learning through a statistical functional lens. We consider learning a function-valued parameter of interest under the constraint that one or several pre-specified real-valued functional parameters equal zero or are otherwise bounded. We characterize the constrained functional parameter as the minimizer of a penalized risk criterion using a Lagrange multiplier formulation. We show that closed-form solutions for the optimal constrained parameter are often available, providing insight into mechanisms that drive fairness in predictive models. Our results also suggest natural estimators of the constrained parameter that can be constructed by combining estimates of unconstrained parameters of the data generating distribution. Thus, our estimation procedure for constructing fair machine learning algorithms can be applied in conjunction with any statistical learning approach and off-the-shelf software. We demonstrate the generality of our method by explicitly considering a number of examples of statistical fairness constraints and implementing the approach using several popular learning approaches.



Keywords:
Constrained learning, Algorithmic fairness, Artificial intelligence, Machine learning, Counterfactual fairness, Causal inference

1
Introduction

Statistical machine learning algorithms have gained widespread use in automating decision-making processes across various domains. The potential benefits of these systems are substantial, with the ability to enhance economic productivity, policy efficiency, and human health. However, there exists a significant risk that these algorithms may perpetuate biases and widen existing societal disparities. Thus, it is crucial to ensure that machine learning models are explicitly designed to exhibit
fair
decision-making. Recent years have seen a surge of interest in incorporating constraints for predictive and decision support systems to address these issues. The notion of
algorithmic fairness
focuses on providing a technical understanding of such issues within their relevant social contexts, with the overarching aim of mitigating unfair treatment of individuals based on sensitive characteristics (e.g., gender and/or race/ethnicity).
Many definitions of
fairness
are available in the literature
(
Mitchell et al. 2021
;
Plecko and Bareinboim 2022
;
Barocas et al. 2023
)
; however, there is no consensus on a universal criterion for fair statistical learning. Indeed, there has been debate about the relevance of the various criteria
(
Prince and Schwarcz 2019
;
Kilbertus et al. 2020
)
and an increasing recognition that it is often impossible to satisfy multiple fairness criteria simultaneously
(
Kleinberg et al. 2017
;
Corbett-Davies and Goel 2018
;
Friedler et al. 2021
)
.

Irrespective of the fairness criteria adopted, the objective of algorithmic fairness in practice involves using data to create a prediction system that adheres to a user-selected fairness constraint. The literature on constrained learning for fairness can broadly be categorized into three primary categories based on the stage of intervention to ensure fairness:
(i)
pre-processing
methods, which adjust the input data to reduce dependencies between sensitive attributes and class labels while preserving the information content between the class labels and other variables
(
Kamiran and Calders 2012
;
Zemel et al. 2013
;
Calmon et al. 2017
)
;
(ii)
in-processing
methods, which involve incorporating fairness constraints or regularization terms directly into the optimization problem
(
Zafar et al. 2019
)
, for example using constrained/regularized empirical risk minimization
(
Donini et al. 2018
)
;
and (iii)
post-processing
methods, which modify the outputs of the model to achieve fairness without changing the training process
(
Hardt et al. 2016
;
Woodworth et al. 2017
)
.
Alternative approaches include
meta algorithms

(
Agarwal et al. 2019
)
and
Bayesian inference

(
Gardner et al. 2014
;
Dimitrakakis et al. 2019
;
Chiappa and Isaac 2019
;
Foulds et al. 2020
;
Perrone et al. 2021
)
.

The methods described are often developed with particular statistical models and defined constraints in mind, and the focus in the literature has been predominantly on classification problems. This specificity renders them less adaptable for straightforward use with a wide range of machine learning models and training algorithms along with a wide range of constraints, thereby restricting their practical utility in diverse real-world scenarios.

In this study, we do not focus on selecting a particular constraint, but instead propose a flexible framework for generating optimal prediction functions under a broad array of constraints. Our approach casts the constrained learning problem as an estimation problem for a constrained functional parameter in an infinite-dimensional statistical model. We leverage a Lagrange-type penalty to characterize the true, population-level form of the constrained functional parameter. For several fairness notions, the characterization of the constrained functional parameter exists in a closed form, involving unconstrained functional parameters. Hence, we obtain a representation of the optimal
fair
prediction function in terms of the optimal
unfair
prediction function and other parameters of the data generating distribution. Consequently, estimation of the fair prediction function is naturally enabled through estimation of unconstrained parameters of the data generating process. Our aim is devising an estimation framework for acquiring fair prediction functions that can be integrated with any standard statistical learning framework compatible with off-the-shelf implementations. Related yet distinct work on this topic was performed by
Chamon and Ribeiro 2020
;
Chamon et al. 2022
who consider the empirical Lagrangian duality problem in this context.

The major contributions of this work can be described as follows:
(i)
A general framework for characterizing solutions to constrained optimization problems.
The framework we develop is based on general results in functional analysis, which allows the results to be applied across a broad class of constrained learning problems.
(ii)
Closed-form characterizations of solutions to several important constraints in fair learning contexts.
We apply our general methodology to various fairness criteria, encompassing both associational and counterfactual notions of fairness, resulting in closed-form solutions for a wide class of problems. Explicitly characterizing the optimal approach to fair learning in these contexts allows us to directly compare the optimal
unfair
approach to prediction with the optimal
fair
approach, thereby providing insights into mechanisms that result in unfair predictions and how to remedy them in estimation. Additionally, we elaborate on a broad category of constraints for which our approach readily offers closed-form solutions, particularly when the risk assessment is based on mean squared error or cross-entropy.
(iii)
Approach to fair learning that is model agnostic.
Unlike previous methods that were closely linked to specific learning frameworks like parametric or empirical likelihood-based methods
(
Nabi et al. 2022
)
or other prior work mentioned earlier, our approach conceptualizes fair learning as the estimation of unconstrained functional parameters of the data generating distribution. This allows any appropriate learning approaches to be applied towards making fair predictions, without any non-standard implementations of existing software.

2
Statistical estimation problem

Unconstrained functional parameter.
Throughout, we assume access to
O_{1},\ldots,O_{n}
,
n
independent copies of the random variable
O
, where each observation
O
is drawn from some distribution
P_{0}
(
O\sim P_{0}
) that is a member of a large, possibly unrestricted (infinite-dimensional), statistical model
\mathcal{M}
(
P_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
). In all of our examples, we let
\mathcal{M}
be the nonparametric model, the unrestricted set of all potential probability distributions compatible with
O
; however, our approach generalizes equally well to other infinite-dimensional semiparametric models. We often use
P
to denote an arbitrary distribution in
\mathcal{M}
.

Let
\Psi:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow{\bf\Psi}
be the
unconstrained
function-valued parameter of interest. This parameter maps a given distribution
P\in\mathcal{M}
of
O
to a parameter space
{\bf\Psi}
. We assume that
\Psi(P)
can be defined as the minimizer of an expectation of a loss function:

\displaystyle\Psi(P)=\argmin_{\psi\in{\bf\Psi}}\ \int L(\psi)(o)\ dP(o)\ .

(1)

We introduce the shorthand notation
R_{P}(\psi)=\int L(\psi)(o)\ dP(o)
to describe the
risk
of a given
\psi\in{\bf\Psi}
under sampling from
P
. We will adopt the notational convention that
\psi=\Psi(P)
for an arbitrary distribution in
\mathcal{M}
, while
\psi_{0}=\Psi(P_{0})
.

Constrained functional parameter.
We assume that the desired constraint can be characterized by fixing the value of a functional of the data generating distribution. Specifically, given
P\in\mathcal{M}
, we write the constraint as
\Theta_{P}:{\bf\Psi}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and without loss of generality assume a constraint of the form
\Theta_{P}(\psi)=0
. We implicitly assume that the set of feasible
\psi
values in
\bf\Psi
that meet the constraint equation is non-empty, underscoring the relevance of the constraint in the context of the problem at hand.

We define a
constrained target parameter
as any mapping
\Psi^{*}:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow{\bf\Psi}
, that satisfies:

\displaystyle\Psi^{*}(P)=\argmin_{\psi\in{\bf\Psi},\ \Theta_{P}(\psi)=0}\ R_{P}(\psi)\ .

(2)

We adopt the notational convention that
\psi^{*}=\Psi^{*}(P)
for an arbitrary distribution in
\mathcal{M}
, while
\psi^{*}_{0}=\Psi^{*}(P_{0})
. Our aim is to develop a framework for characterizing
\psi^{*}_{0}
and developing estimators thereof.
We first consider a scenario with a single-dimensional equality constraint and subsequently describe a straightforward extension to include an inequality constraint. Further extensions of our framework to accommodate multiple equality and inequality constraints are detailed in the supplementary materials.

In the main body, we also make the simplifying assumption that
\psi_{0}
represents a conditional probability or conditional mean, which is common in many statistical learning problems. Thus, we assume that
\psi_{0}^{*}
is a function of a vector of predictors
Z
that are used to predict an outcome
Y
. We use
P_{0,Z}
to denote the probability distribution of
Z
implied by
P_{0}
. Explicit considerations for other functional parameters such as conditional densities are considered in Supplemental Section
G.1.2
.

3
Methods

3.1
Theoretical foundations

For any
\psi\in{\bf\Psi}
, we introduce the idea of a
path through

\psi
that has a particular
direction

h
, where
h
is a function of
Z
. The
path
is denoted by
\{\psi_{\delta,h}:\delta\in\mathbb{R}\}\subset{\bf\Psi}
and is indexed by the univariate parameter
\delta
. We say that this path is
through

\psi
if
\psi_{\delta,h}|_{\delta=0}=\psi
. The
direction
of the path,
h
, is defined as
\frac{d}{d\delta}\psi_{\delta,h}|_{\delta=0}
. We let the directions of the paths vary over a set
\mathcal{H}_{P}(\psi)
chosen such that it captures all possible local directions within the parameter space
{\bf\Psi}
in which one can move away from
\psi
. We embed
\mathcal{H}_{P}(\psi)
in a Hilbert space
\hbox{${\rm I\kern-1.99997ptH}$}_{P}(\psi)
endowed with a covariance inner product of the form
\langle f,g\rangle=\int f(z)g(z)dP_{Z}(z)
. In our examples, we take
\hbox{${\rm I\kern-1.99997ptH}$}_{P}(\psi)=L^{2}(P_{Z})
, the space of all bounded functions
h
of
Z
such that
\int h(z)^{2}dP_{Z}(z)<\infty
.
Let
\mathbb{T}_{P}(\psi)\subseteq\hbox{${\rm I\kern-1.99997ptH}$}_{P}(\psi)
be the closure in
\hbox{${\rm I\kern-1.99997ptH}$}_{P}(\psi)
of the linear span of
\mathcal{H}_{P}(\psi)
.

By embedding
\mathcal{H}_{P}(\psi)
in
\hbox{${\rm I\kern-1.99997ptH}$}_{P}(\psi)
, we can derive an inner-product characterization of
bounded linear functionals
on
\mathbb{T}_{P}(\psi)
of the form
f_{P}(\psi_{\delta,\cdot}):\mathbb{T}_{P}(\psi)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. We recall that the function
h\mapsto f_{P}(\psi_{\delta,h})
for
h\in\mathbb{T}_{P}(\psi)
, is called
linear
on
\mathbb{T}_{P}(\psi)
if it is such that
f_{P}(\psi_{\delta,\alpha h_{1}+\beta h_{2}})=\alpha f_{P}(\psi_{\delta,h_{1}})+\beta f_{P}(\psi_{\delta,h_{2}})
for all
h_{1},h_{2}\in\mathbb{T}_{P}(\psi)
and for all
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}.
Further, we say that the function is
bounded
if there exists an
M>0
such that
|f_{P}(\psi_{\delta,h})|\leq M\langle h,h\rangle^{1/2}
for all
h\in\mathbb{T}_{P}(\psi)
. Finally, we recall that a linear functional on a Hilbert space is bounded if and only if it is continuous.

By the Reisz representation theorem, any bounded linear functional on
\mathbb{T}_{P}(\psi)
will have an inner product representation
f_{P}(\psi_{\delta,h})=\langle h,D_{P}(\psi)\rangle
for some unique element
D_{P}(\psi)\in\mathbb{T}_{P}(\psi)
. The object
D_{P}(\psi)
is sometimes referred to as the
canonical gradient
of the functional.

3.2
Assumptions

In developing our methodology, we consider derivatives of the risk
R_{P}(\psi)
and the constraint

\Theta_{P}(\psi)
along paths
\{\psi_{\delta,h}:\delta\in\mathbb{R}\}
for
h\in\mathbb{T}_{P}(\psi)
. We can view these derivatives (at
\delta=0
) as a functional
f_{P}
on
\mathbb{T}_{P}(\psi)
of the form
h\mapsto\frac{d}{d\delta}f_{P}(\psi_{\delta,h})|_{\delta=0}
.

Our key assumption is that the pathwise derivatives of
R_{P}
and
\Theta_{P}
represent bounded (or equivalently, continuous) linear functionals on
\mathbb{T}_{P}(\psi)

(
van der Laan 1995
)
. Specifically, we assume for every
h\in\mathbb{T}_{P}(\psi)
and for every univariate path through
\psi
with direction
h
,

\displaystyle h\mapsto\frac{d}{d\delta}R_{P}(\psi_{\delta,h})\bigg|_{\delta=0}\ \mbox{ and }\ h\mapsto\frac{d}{d\delta}\Theta_{P}(\psi_{\delta,h})\bigg|_{\delta=0}

(A1)

\displaystyle\mbox{are both bounded linear
functionals on $\mathbb{T}_{P}(\psi)$}\ .

Under (
A1
), the pathwise derivatives will have an inner-product representation,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\delta}R_{P}(\psi_{\delta,h})\bigg|_{\delta=0}=\langle D_{R,P}(\psi),h\rangle\quad\mbox{and}\quad\frac{d}{d\delta}\Theta_{P}(\psi_{\delta,h})\bigg|_{\delta=0}=\langle D_{\Theta,P}(\psi),h\rangle\ ,

for some unique elements
D_{R,P}
and
D_{\Theta,P}
of the tangent space
\mathbb{T}_{P}(\psi)
. See Supplemental Section
B.1
for a remark on pathwise differentiability and links to non/semiparametric efficiency theory. We will also assume the gradient
D_{\Theta,P}
of
\Theta_{P}
is non-degenerate at a non-trivial solution
\psi_{0}^{*}
, i.e., for a
\psi_{0}^{*}\neq\psi_{0}
, we have that

\displaystyle P_{0}\{D_{\Theta,P}(\psi_{0}^{*})(O)=0\}=0\ .

(A2)

Assumption (
A2
) is sufficient but not necessary for our developments and corresponds to the simplest form of the linear independence constraint qualification in our setting
(
Gould and Tolle 1971
;
Sundaram 1996
;
Boltyanski et al. 1998
)
.

3.3
Defining and characterizing constraint-specific path

We first show that under (
A1
) the constrained functional parameter
\Psi^{*}(P)
, defined via (
2
), can be characterized using Lagrange multipliers; see Supplemental Section
B.2
for a proof.

Lemma 1
.

Consider a vector-valued parameter
\Phi(P):\mathcal{M}\rightarrow{\bf\Psi}\times\mathbb{R}
, defined as

\displaystyle\Phi(P)=\argmin_{\psi\in{\bf\Psi},\ \lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\ R_{P}(\psi)+\lambda\Theta_{P}(\psi)\ .

(3)

We note that
\Phi
maps a given distribution
P
into a vector-valued parameter
(\tilde{\Psi}(P),\Lambda(P))
, where
\tilde{\Psi}(P)\in{\bf\Psi}
and
\Lambda(P)\in\mathbb{R}
. Under (
A1
) and (
A2
), for each
P\in\mathcal{M}
,
\Psi^{*}(P)=\tilde{\Psi}(P)
.

For a given
\lambda
, we define
\psi_{0,\lambda}=\argmin_{\psi\in{\bf\Psi}}R_{P_{0}}(\psi)+\lambda\Theta_{P_{0}}(\psi)
as the minimizer of penalized risk under sampling from
P_{0}
. Lemma
1
establishes that
\psi^{*}_{0}=\psi_{0,\lambda_{0}}
, where
\lambda_{0}=\Lambda(P_{0})
. We note that
\{\psi_{0,\lambda}:\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\}\subset{\bf\Psi}
represents a
path
through
\psi_{0}
. We refer to this path as the
constraint-specific path
, noting that both
\psi_{0}
and
\psi_{0}^{*}
are part of this path. We note that for each
\psi_{0,\lambda}
along the constraint-specific path, we have that for
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,
\psi_{0,\lambda}
minimizes the criterion
\psi\mapsto R_{P_{0}}(\psi)+\lambda\Theta_{P_{0}}(\psi)
.
Thus, a derivative of this criterion along a path
\{\psi_{0,\lambda,\delta}:\delta\in\mathbb{R}\}
through
\psi_{0,\lambda}
at
\delta=0
equals zero and for every
h\in\mathbb{T}_{P_{0}}(\psi)
,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{d}{d\delta}R_{P_{0}}(\psi_{0,\lambda,\delta,h})\right|_{\delta=0}+\lambda\left.\frac{d}{d\delta}\Theta_{P_{0}}(\psi_{0,\lambda,\delta,h})\right|_{\delta=0}

\displaystyle=\langle D_{R,P_{0}}(\psi_{0,\lambda}),h\rangle+\lambda\big\langle D_{\Theta,P_{0}}(\psi_{0,\lambda}),h\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle D_{R,P_{0}}(\psi_{0,\lambda})+\lambda D_{\Theta,P_{0}}(\psi_{0,\lambda}),h\rangle\ ,

where the second line follows from (
A1
) and the last line follows because
\mathbb{T}_{P_{0}}(\psi)
is a Hilbert space, which is by definition an inner product space. Because inner products are non-degenerate, the constraint-specific path
\{\psi_{0,\lambda}:\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\}
must satisfy

\displaystyle D_{R,P_{0}}(\psi_{0,\lambda})(o)+\lambda D_{\Theta,P_{0}}(\psi_{0,\lambda})(o)=0\ ,\ \text{for all }\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\text{ and }o\text{ in a support of }P_{0}\ .

(4)

Thus, (
4
) along with the constraint equation
\Theta_{P_{0}}(\psi_{0,\lambda_{0}})=0
provide two equations with which we can attempt to solve for
\psi_{0,\lambda}
and
\lambda_{0}
. We show below that closed-form solutions for
\psi_{0,\lambda}
are available for many risk criteria and constraints. These expressions are ultimately expressed in terms of parameters of
P_{0}
, thereby suggesting natural estimators; see Section
3.4
. Closed-form solutions for
\lambda_{0}
are also often available. However, even in settings where no closed-form solution for
\lambda_{0}
can be found, we show that we are often able to propose practical approaches to estimation via grid search procedures.

When a closed-form solution for
\psi_{0,\lambda}
is not available, it is useful to develop an alternative characterization of the constraint-specific path that allows for a recursive estimation strategy to be used. To that end we define
D_{0,R+\lambda\Theta}(\psi)=D_{R,P_{0}}(\psi)+\lambda D_{\Theta,P_{0}}(\psi)
. Given (
4
), we know that for a given datum
o
,
D_{0,R+\lambda\Theta}(\psi_{0,\lambda})(o)=0
for all
\lambda
, and thus
\frac{d}{d\lambda}D_{0,R+\lambda\Theta}(\psi_{0,\lambda})(o)=0
. The chain rule implies

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{d\psi_{0,\lambda}}D_{R,P_{0}}(\psi_{0,\lambda})\times\frac{d}{d\lambda}\psi_{0,\lambda}+\lambda\times\frac{d}{d\psi_{0,\lambda}}D_{\Theta,P_{0}}(\psi_{0,\lambda})\times\frac{d}{d\lambda}\psi_{0,\lambda}+D_{\Theta,P_{0}}(\psi_{0,\lambda})

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{d\psi_{0,\lambda}}D_{0,R+\lambda\Theta}(\psi_{0,\lambda})\times\frac{d}{d\lambda}\psi_{0,\lambda}+D_{\Theta,P_{0}}(\psi_{0,\lambda})\ .

(5)

We define
\dot{D}_{0,R+\lambda\Theta}(\psi)=\frac{d}{d\psi}D_{0,R+\lambda\Theta}(\psi)
. Assuming that
\dot{D}_{0,R+\lambda\Theta}(\psi)
is invertible, (
5
) implies

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\lambda}\psi_{0,\lambda}=-\big\{\dot{D}_{0,R+\lambda\Theta}(\psi_{0,\lambda})\big\}^{-1}D_{\Theta,P_{0}}(\psi_{0,\lambda})\ .

(6)

We will see in Section
3.4
below that this representation allows for recursively constructed estimators of
\psi_{0,\lambda}
, while
\lambda_{0}
can be determined via grid search, as mentioned above.

We summarize our results on the constraint-specific path in the following theorem.

Theorem 1
.

Let
D_{R,P_{0}}(\psi)
and
D_{\Theta,P_{0}}(\psi)\in\mathbb{T}_{P_{0}}(\psi)
be the canonical gradients of pathwise derivatives of the risk function,
\left.\frac{d}{d\delta}R_{P_{0}}(\psi_{\delta,h})\right|_{\delta=0}
, and the constraint,
\left.\frac{d}{d\delta}\Theta_{P_{0}}(\psi_{\delta,h})\right|_{\delta=0}
, respectively.
Let
\{\psi_{0,\lambda}=\argmin_{\psi\in{\bf\Psi}}R_{P_{0}}(\psi)+\lambda\Theta_{P_{0}}(\psi):\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\}
define a constraint-specific path through
\psi_{0}
at
\lambda=0
. Under (
A1
) and (
A2
), the following two statements are true:

(i)

The constraint-specific path satisfies:

\displaystyle D_{R,P_{0}}(\psi_{0,\lambda})+\lambda D_{\Theta,P_{0}}(\psi_{0,\lambda})=0\ ,\ \ \forall\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\ .

(7)

For a given
\lambda
, the above characterizes a solution to the minimization problem

\argmin_{\psi\in\bm{\Psi}}R_{P}(\psi)+\lambda\Theta_{P}(\psi)
as a function of
P_{0}
and
\lambda
.

(ii)

Let
\dot{D}_{0,R+\lambda\Theta}(\psi)=\frac{d}{d\psi}[D_{R,P_{0}}(\psi)+\lambda D_{\Theta,P_{0}}(\psi)]
and assume it is invertible. Then, the following is an equivalent characterization of the constraint-specific path:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\lambda}\psi_{0,\lambda}=-\big\{\dot{D}_{0,R+\lambda\Theta}(\psi_{0,\lambda})\big\}^{-1}D_{\Theta,P_{0}}(\psi_{0,\lambda})\ .

(8)

The constraint-specific path can be considered as establishing a “first-order” condition for solutions to the constrained optimization problem. Additional “second-order” conditions are required to establish whether the solution constitutes a minimum. Discussions regarding these conditions are deferred to Supplemental Section
C
.

3.4
Estimators of constrained functional parameter

Suppose we can solve (
7
) for
\psi_{0,\lambda}
and that the solution can be expressed as a functional of certain key parameters of
P_{0}
. That is, we can often equivalently index gradients
D_{R,P_{0}}
and
D_{\Theta,P_{0}}
in terms of certain nuisance parameters, say
\eta_{0}=\eta(P_{0})
. This vector of nuisance parameters could contain several real- or function-valued parameters of
P_{0}
. In all our examples below, perhaps unsurprisingly, these nuisance parameters include
\psi_{0}
, the unconstrained minimizer of
R_{P_{0}}
. Beyond that, the nuisance parameters that appear in the solutions described below are unique to the specific problem. However, it can generally be stated that for each problem, we obtain a solution for (
7
) expressed in the form of
\psi_{0,\lambda}=r(\eta_{0},\lambda)
for some mapping
r
. This suggests a natural plug-in estimator of any
\psi_{0,\lambda}
on the constraint-specific path. Let
\psi_{n,\lambda}=r(\eta_{n},\lambda)
, where
\eta_{n}
is an estimate of
\eta_{0}
. In some scenarios, there is also an explicit solution for
\lambda_{0}=\ell(\eta_{0})
for some mapping
\ell
. This again suggests a natural plug-in estimator
\lambda_{n}=\ell(\eta_{n})
. Thus, an estimate of
\psi_{0,\lambda_{0}}
is given by
\psi_{n,\lambda_{n}}
. If no explicit solution for
\lambda_{0}
is available then it is possible to use empirical minimization to estimate
\lambda_{0}
. Let
\Theta_{n}(\psi)
denote an estimate of
\Theta_{P_{0}}(\psi)
for any
\psi\in{\bf\Psi}
and define
\lambda_{n}=\mbox{argmin}_{\lambda\in[\delta_{1},\delta_{2}]}|\Theta_{n}(\psi_{n,\lambda})|
, which can be computed using a grid search. See Section
4
for an example, including details of how to appropriately choose
\delta_{1}
and
\delta_{2}
.

On the other hand, if (
7
) does not yield a closed form solution for
\psi_{0,\lambda}
then we may instead leverage (
8
) to recursively build estimators. With a slight abuse of notation, we define
r(\eta_{0},\lambda)=-\big\{\dot{D}_{0,R+\lambda\Theta}(\psi_{0,\lambda})\big\}^{-1}D_{\Theta,P_{0}}(\psi_{0,\lambda})
, the right-hand side of (
8
). We can now define a procedure that begins with an estimate
\psi_{n}
of
\psi_{0}
, the unconstrained minimizer. For a numerically small step size
d\nu
we can compute
\psi_{n,d\nu}
as
\psi_{n}+r(\eta_{n},0)d\nu
. This procedure proceeds where at the
k
-th step, we define
\lambda_{k}=\lambda_{k-1}+d\nu
and compute
\psi_{n,\lambda_{k}}=\psi_{0,\lambda_{k-1}}+r(\eta_{n},\lambda_{k-1})d\nu
. Since we can compute an estimate of
\psi_{n,\lambda}
for any
\lambda
, it is possible to utilize empirical minimization as above to generate an estimate of
\lambda_{0}
.

3.5
Extensions to inequality constraint

In certain contexts, instead of satisfying an equality constraint of the form
\Theta_{P}(\psi)=0
, it may be sufficient to bound the constraint.
Without loss of generality, we explore an inequality constraint formulated as
\Omega_{P}(\psi)\leq 0
. The objective is to identify the optimal functional parameter,
\Psi^{*}(P)
, defined as
\Psi^{*}(P)=\argmin_{\psi\in{\bf\Psi},\Omega_{P}(\psi)\leq 0}\ R_{P}(\psi)
. Analogous to (
A1
), we assume that for any
h\in\mathbb{T}_{P}(\psi)
,

h\mapsto\frac{d}{d\delta}\Omega_{P}(\psi_{\delta,h})\Big|_{\delta=0}
is a bounded linear functional on
\mathbb{T}_{P}(\psi)
.
The gradient of this mapping is denoted by
D_{\Omega,P}(\psi)
and we again assume this gradient is non-degenerate,
D_{\Omega,P}(\psi_{0}^{*})(o)\neq 0
for all
o
in the support of
O
.

Consider the solution
\psi_{0,\mu}=\argmin_{\psi\in{\bf\Psi}}R_{P_{0}}(\psi)+\mu\Omega_{P_{0}}(\psi)
, where
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}
. Similar to the equality constraint,
\{\psi_{0,\mu}:\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}\}
defines a constraint-specific path through
\psi_{0}
at
\mu=0.
Drawing from the rationale in the proof of Lemma
1
, characterizing a solution
\psi_{0,\mu}
for a particular
\mu
value allows us to find the optimal solution via the so-called
complementary slackness
condition,
\mu\Omega_{P_{0}}(\psi_{0,\mu})=0
. As in Theorem
1
, the path
\{\psi_{0,\mu}:\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}\}
satisfies

\displaystyle D_{R,P_{0}}(\psi_{0,\mu})+\mu D_{\Omega,P_{0}}(\psi_{0,\mu})=0\ ,\quad\text{ for all }\ \mu\in\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}\ ,

(9)

and
\frac{d}{d\mu}\psi_{0,\mu}=-\{\dot{D}_{0,R+\mu\Omega}(\psi_{0,\mu})\}^{-1}D_{\Omega,P_{0}}(\psi_{0,\mu})
, where
\dot{D}_{0,R+\mu\Omega}(\psi)=\frac{d}{d\psi}D_{0,R+\mu\Omega}(\psi)
, where we define
D_{0,R+\mu\Omega}=D_{R,P_{0}}(\psi)+\mu D_{\Omega,P_{0}}(\psi)
.

Estimating
\psi_{0,\mu_{0}}
follows a similar process to estimating
\psi_{0,\lambda_{0}}
as discussed in Section
3.4
; however, there is now an initial step to verify the complementary slackness condition. We first check whether the estimate
\psi_{n,\mu=0}
of the unconstrained parameter
\psi_{0}
satisfies
\Omega_{n}(\psi_{n,\mu=0})\leq 0
, If so, then we set
\mu_{n}=0
and
\psi_{n,\mu_{n}}=\psi_{n}
, i.e., our procedure returns the unconstrained parameter estimate. If the estimate of the unconstrained parameter does not satisfy the estimated constraint,
\Omega_{n}(\psi_{n,\mu=0})\leq 0
, then we can use empirical minimization of the constraint to find
\mu_{n}=\mbox{arg min}_{\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}}|\Omega_{n}(\psi_{n,\mu_{n}})|
.

We discuss how to generalize our methodology to the setting with multiple equality and inequality constraints in Supplementary Section
D
.

4
Examples

To illustrate our proposed method for the nonparametric estimation of constrained functional parameters, we present examples for four specific fairness constraints—average total effect, natural direct effect, equalized risk among cases, and overall equalized risk—and for two common risk criteria—mean squared error and cross-entropy. For both risk criteria the unconstrained minimizer of the risk is a conditional mean (or conditional probability for the binary
Y
case). In all examples, we assume
\mathcal{M}
is a nonparametric model and that
X
represents a binary sensitive characteristic.

Each example begins with defining the canonical gradients needed to define the constraint-specific path, as detailed in (
7
). We then propose an estimator of the optimal constrained parameter based on the representation of these paths. Explicit derivations, analysis of second-order conditions, and a study of the large-sample properties of the proposed estimator are included for each example in the supplementary materials.

4.1
Fairness constraint: average total effect

Suppose
O=(W,X,Y)\sim P_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
. We are interested in
\psi\in\bm{\Psi}
that maps from the support of
(X,W)
to either the reals (for mean squared error) or the unit interval (for cross-entropy). We consider the constraint
\Theta_{P_{0}}(\psi)=\int\{\psi(1,w)-\psi(0,w)\}dP_{0,W}(w)
, where
P_{0,W}(w)=P_{0}(W\leq w)
denotes the marginal cumulative distribution function for
W
implied by
P_{0}
. The constraint
\Theta_{P_{0}}(\psi_{0})
defines the average causal effect of
X
on
Y
, assuming
W
includes the set of all confounders between
X
and
Y
.

Risk criterion: mean squared error.

Let
L(\psi)(o)=\{y-\psi(x,w)\}^{2}
be squared-error loss. We can show (see Supplement Section
E.1.1
) that the relevant gradients are

\displaystyle D_{R,P_{0}}(\psi)(o)

\displaystyle=2\{\psi(x,w)-\psi_{0}(x,w)\}\ ,

(10)

\displaystyle D_{\Theta,P_{0}}(\psi)(o)

\displaystyle=\frac{2x-1}{\pi_{0}(x\mid w)}\ ,

(11)

where
\pi_{0}(x\mid w)=P_{0}(X=x\mid W=w)
. Condition (
7
) yields a closed form solution for the constraint-specific path
\psi_{0,\lambda}(x,w)=\psi_{0}(x,w)-\lambda(2x-1)/\{2\pi_{0}(x\mid w)\}.
This equation combined with
\Theta_{P_{0}}(\psi_{0,\lambda})=0,
yields a closed-form solution for
\lambda_{0}
,

\displaystyle\lambda_{0}=2\frac{\int\left\{\psi_{0}(1,w)-\psi_{0}(0,w)\right\}dP_{0,W}(w)}{\displaystyle\int\left\{\pi_{0}(1\mid w)^{-1}+\pi_{0}(0\mid w)^{-1}\right\}dP_{0,W}(w)}\ .

(12)

To generate estimates of
\psi_{0,\lambda_{0}}
, we note that the key nuisance parameters indexing the gradients and solution for
\lambda_{0}
are
\eta_{0}=(\psi_{0},\pi_{0},P_{0,W})
. The parameters
\psi_{0}
and
\pi_{0}
can be estimated using any appropriate statistical learning technique for conditional means/probabilities, while a natural choice for
P_{0,W}
is its empirical analog. Thus, our proposed estimator is
\psi_{n,\lambda_{n}}(x,w)=\psi_{n}(x,w)-\lambda_{n}(2x-1)/\{2\pi_{n}(x\mid w)\},

where

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}=2\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\{\psi_{n}(1,W_{i})-\psi_{n}(0,W_{i})\right\}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\{\pi_{n}(1\mid W_{i})^{-1}+\pi_{n}(0\mid W_{i})^{-1}\right\}}\ .

For details on the analysis of second-order conditions for
\psi_{0,\lambda_{0}}
and the stochastic behavior of
\psi_{n,\lambda_{n}}
see Supplemental Sections
E.2.1
and
E.3.1
.

Risk criterion: cross-entropy.

Assume
Y
is binary, and let
L(\psi)(o)=-y\log\psi(x,w)-(1-y)\log\{1-\psi(x,w)\}
be the negative log-likelihood loss. The gradient of the constraint is again given by (
11
), while the gradient of the risk is (see Supplemental Section
E.1.2
)

\displaystyle D_{R,P_{0}}(\psi)(o)=\displaystyle\frac{1-\psi_{0}(x,w)}{1-\psi(x,w)}-\frac{\psi_{0}(x,w)}{\psi(x,w)}\ .

(13)

Condition (
7
) implies elements of the constraint-specific path satisfy
\lambda C_{0}(x,w)\psi^{2}_{0,\lambda}(x,w)-\left\{1+\lambda C_{0}(x,w)\right\}\psi_{0,\lambda}(x,w)+\psi_{0}(x,w)=0.

In Supplemental Section
E.2.2
, we show that this equation is guaranteed to have exactly one solution that falls in the unit interval

\displaystyle\psi_{0,\lambda}=\frac{(\lambda+1/C_{0})-C_{0}\pi_{0}\left[\left\{\lambda+1/C_{0}\right\}^{2}-4\lambda\psi_{0}/C_{0}\right]^{1/2}}{2\lambda}\ ,

(14)

where
C_{0}(x,w)=(2x-1)/\pi_{0}(x\mid w)
. In this instance, a closed-form expression for
\lambda_{0}
is not readily available. Thus, we propose to build an estimator by generating estimates
\eta_{n}=(\psi_{n},\pi_{n})
of
\eta_{0}=(\psi_{0},\pi_{0})
. For a given
\lambda
, we propose to estimate
\psi_{0,\lambda}
using a plug-in estimate
\psi_{n,\lambda}
based on (
14
), where
\psi_{0}
and
\pi_{0}
are replaced with estimates
\psi_{n}
and
\pi_{n}
, respectively. An estimate of
\lambda
can then be obtained as
\lambda_{n}=\mbox{arg min}_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\{\psi_{n,\lambda}(1,W_{i})-\psi_{n,\lambda}(0,W_{i})\}\right|,

where the minimization can be solved via grid search.

The analysis of second-order conditions for
\psi_{0,\lambda_{0}}
and the stochastic behavior of
\psi_{n,\lambda_{n}}
are provided in Supplemental Sections
E.2.2
and
E.3.2
.

4.2
Fairness constraint: natural direct effect

Suppose
O=(W,X,M,Y)\sim P_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
, where
M
is a set of mediators of the effect of
X
on
Y
. For simplicity, we assume
M
is a single, binary variable; our results generalize naturally to multivariate and real-valued
M
. Let
\gamma_{0}(m\mid x,w)=P_{0}(M=m\mid X=x,W=w)
denote the conditional probability distribution of
M
. We are interested in mappings
\psi
on the support of
(X,M,W)
and in the constraint
\Theta_{P_{0}}(\psi)=\int\sum_{m=0}^{1}\left\{\psi(1,m,w)-\psi(0,m,w)\right\}\gamma_{0}(m\mid 0,w)\ dP_{0,W}(w).
Under assumptions,
\Theta_{P_{0}}(\psi)
identifies the so-called
natural direct effect
of
X
on
Y
. A function
\psi
that satisfies
\Theta_{P_{0}}(\psi)=0
then satisfies the
counterfactual path-specific effect
notion of fairness proposed by
Nabi and Shpitser 2018
, where the direct effect is selected as the impermissible path-specific effect.

Risk criterion: mean squared error.

Let
L(\psi)(o)=\{y-\psi(x,m,w)\}^{2}
be squared-error loss for this setting. The gradients of the risk and constraint can be written as (see Supplemental Section
F.1.1
)
D_{R,P_{0}}(\psi)(o)=2\{\psi(x,m,w)-\psi_{0}(x,m,w)\}
and

\displaystyle D_{\Theta,P_{0}}(\psi)(o)

\displaystyle=\frac{2x-1}{\pi_{0}(x\mid w)}\displaystyle\frac{\gamma_{0}(m\mid 0,w)}{\gamma_{0}(m\mid x,w)}\ .

(15)

Equation (
7
) yields a closed form solution for
\psi_{0,\lambda}
,

\displaystyle\psi_{0,\lambda}(x,m,w)=\psi_{0}(x,m,w)-\lambda\ \frac{2x-1}{2\pi_{0}(x\mid w)}\displaystyle\frac{\gamma_{0}(m\mid 0,w)}{\gamma_{0}(m\mid x,w)}\ .

(16)

A closed-form solution for
\lambda_{0}
is also available,

\displaystyle\lambda_{0}

\displaystyle=2\frac{\int\sum_{m=0}^{1}\left\{\psi_{0}(1,m,w)-\psi_{0}(0,m,w)\right\}\gamma_{0}(m\mid 0,w)\ dP_{0,W}(w)}{\int\sum_{m=0}^{1}\left\{\frac{1}{\pi_{0}(1\mid w)}\ \frac{\gamma_{0}(m\mid 0,w)}{\gamma_{0}(m\mid 1,w)}+{\frac{1}{\pi_{0}(0\mid w)}}\right\}\gamma_{0}(m\mid 0,w)dP_{0,W}(w)}\ .

The above gradients and the solution for
\lambda_{0}
are indexed by nuisance parameters
\eta_{0}=(\psi_{0},\pi_{0},\gamma_{0},P_{0,W})
. Using the empirical distribution for
P_{0,W}
and estimates
\psi_{n},\pi_{n},\gamma_{n}
, obtained via any appropriate statistical learning technique for conditional means/probabilities, we propose the following estimator

\psi_{n,\lambda_{n}}(x,m,w)=\psi_{n}(x,m,w)-\lambda_{n}\frac{2x-1}{2\pi_{n}(x\mid w)}\frac{\gamma_{n}(m\mid 0,w)}{\gamma_{n}(m\mid x,w)}\ ,

where

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}=2\ \frac{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\sum\limits_{m=0}^{1}\left[\{\psi_{n}(1,m,W_{i})-\psi_{n}(0,m,W_{i})\}\gamma_{n}(m\mid 0,W_{i})\right]}{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\sum\limits_{m=0}^{1}\left[\frac{1}{\pi_{n}(1\mid W_{i})}\frac{\gamma_{n}(m\mid 0,W_{i})^{2}}{\gamma_{n}(m\mid 1,W_{i})}+\frac{\gamma_{n}(m\mid 0,W_{i})}{\pi_{n}(0\mid W_{i})}\right]}\ .

The analysis of second-order conditions for
\psi_{0,\lambda_{0}}
and the stochastic behavior of
\psi_{n,\lambda_{n}}
are provided in Supplemental Sections
F.2.1
and
F.3.1
.

Risk criterion: cross-entropy.

Assume
Y
is binary, and let
L(\psi)(o)=-y\log\psi(x,m,w)-(1-y)\log\{1-\psi(x,m,w)\}
be the negative log-likelihood loss. The gradient of the constraint is given by (
15
), while the gradient of the risk is (see Supplemental Section
F.1.2
)
D_{R,P_{0}}(\psi)(o)=\{1-\psi_{0}(x,m,w)\}/\{1-\psi(x,m,w)\}-\psi_{0}(x,m,w)/\psi(x,m,w).

Condition (
7
) implies a closed form solution for the constraint-specific path

\displaystyle\lambda C_{0}(x,m,w)\psi^{2}_{0,\lambda}(x,m,w)-\left\{1+\lambda C_{0}(x,m,w)\right\}\psi_{0,\lambda}(x,m,w)+\psi_{0}(x,m,w)=0\ .

(17)

In Supplemental Section
F.2.2
, we show that (
17
) yields the following unique solution for
\psi_{0,\lambda}(x,m,w)
in the unit interval

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda+C_{0}(x,m,w)^{-1}-(2x-1)\left[\left\{\lambda+C_{0}(x,m,w)^{-1}\right\}^{2}-4\lambda\psi_{0}C_{0}(x,m,w)^{-1}\right]^{1/2}}{2\lambda}\ ,

(18)

where
C_{0}(x,m,w)=\{(2x-1)/\pi_{0}(x\mid w)\}\gamma_{0}(m\mid 0,w)/\gamma_{0}(m\mid x,w)
. A closed-form expression for
\lambda_{0}
is not readily available for this example. Instead, we propose to estimate
\psi_{0,\lambda}
by first generating estimates
\eta_{n}=(\psi_{n},\pi_{n},\gamma_{n})
of the nuisance parameter
\eta_{0}=(\psi_{0},\pi_{0},\gamma_{0})
. An estimate of
\lambda_{0}
can then be obtained as
\lambda_{n}=\underset{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}{\mbox{arg min}}|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{m=0}^{1}\{\psi_{n,\lambda}(1,m,W_{i})-\psi_{n,\lambda}(0,m,W_{i})\}\ \gamma_{n}(m\mid 0,W_{i})|,

where the minimization can be solved via grid search.

The analysis of second-order conditions for
\psi_{0,\lambda_{0}}
and the stochastic behavior of
\psi_{n,\lambda_{n}}
are provided in Supplemental Sections
F.2.2
and
F.3.2
.

4.3
Fairness constraint: equalized risk among the cases

Suppose
O=(W,X,Y)\sim P_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
. We are interested in the mappings
\psi
on the support of
(X,W)
. Let
\Theta_{P}(\psi)=\mathbb{E}_{P}\{L(\psi)(O)\mid X=1,Y=1\}-\mathbb{E}_{P}\{L(\psi)(O)\mid X=0,Y=1\}
, where
L(\psi)
is the negative log-likelihood loss defined above. We term this the
equalized risk in the cases
constraint, which describes the difference in risks between the two groups defined by sensitive characteristic
X
amongst the “cases” with
Y=1
. Note that this constraint is similar to yet distinct from the
equal opportunity
criterion in
Hardt et al. 2016
.

The gradient of the risk is given by (
13
). We can show (see Supplemental Section
G.1.1
) that the gradient of the constraint is
D_{\Theta,P_{0}}(\psi)(o)=-(2x-1)/p_{0,1}(x)\{\psi_{0}(x,w)/\psi(x,w)\}
,
where
p_{0,1}(x)=P_{0}(X=x,Y=1)
. Condition (
7
) implies a closed form solution for the constraint-specific path

\displaystyle\psi_{0,\lambda}(x,w)=\psi_{0}(x,w)\ \frac{1+\lambda\ C_{0}(x)}{1+\lambda\ C_{0}(x)\ \psi_{0}(x,w)}\ ,

(19)

where
C_{0}(x)=(2x-1)/p_{0,1}(x)
. A closed-form expression for
\lambda_{0}
is not readily available. Thus, for estimation of
\psi_{0,\lambda_{0}}
, we first generate estimates
\psi_{n}
of
\psi_{0}
. We then compute a parsimonious estimate of
p_{0,1}(x)
by setting
p_{n,1}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\{I(X_{i}=x)\psi_{n}(X_{i},W_{i})\}/\sum_{i=1}^{n}I(X_{i}=x)
. An estimate of
\lambda_{0}
can then be obtained via empirical minimization of the constraint,

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}=\underset{-p_{n,1}(1)<\lambda<p_{n,1}(0)}{\mbox{arg min}}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}C_{n}(X_{i})\log\psi_{n,\lambda}(X_{i},W_{i})\right|\ ,

(20)

where
C_{n}(x)=(2x-1)/p_{n,1}(x)
and
\psi_{n,\lambda}=\psi_{n}(1+\lambda C_{n})/(1+\lambda C_{n}\psi_{n})
. The restriction on the range of
\lambda
in (
20
) is suggested by the analysis of second-order conditions (see Supplementary Section
G.1.3
) and the computation of
\lambda_{n}
can be implemented via grid search. An analysis of the stochastic behavior of
\psi_{n,\lambda_{n}}
is provided in Supplemental Section
G.1.4
.

The constraint
\Theta_{P}(\psi)=\mathbb{E}_{P}\{L(\psi)(O)\mid X=1\}-\mathbb{E}_{P}\{L(\psi)(O)\mid X=0\}
defines the
overall
equalized risk, which could be of interest in fairness applications. For an in-depth discussion on this constraint, see Supplemental Sections
G.2
and
G.3
.

4.4
Fairness constraints: weighted prediction constraints

Let
\psi(Z)
, with
Z=O\setminus Y
, be the unconstrained functional parameter. We observe that numerous fairness constraints on are of the form

\displaystyle\Theta_{P}(\psi)=\mathbb{E}_{P}\{\kappa_{P}(Z)\zeta_{P}(\psi)(Z)\}\ ,

(21)

where
\kappa_{P}
is a function of
Z
that does not depend on
\psi
, and
\zeta_{P}(\psi)
is a continuous, differentiable function in
\psi
.
We let
\kappa_{0}=\kappa_{P_{0}}
and
\zeta_{0}=\zeta_{P_{0}}
.

We note that
\kappa_{P}
acts as a weighting function that can assume negative values, and our interest lies in minimizing predictive risk, subject to a constraint on the weighted average of
\zeta_{P}
. This class of constraints generalizes many of the examples in Section
4
, but also encompasses other counterfactual parameters pertinent to fairness, including unfair path-specific effects
(
Nabi and Shpitser 2018
)
and other fairness criteria discussed in
Mishler and Kennedy 2022
. We find for this general class of constraints, our methods provide closed-form solutions for both the mean squared error and cross-entropy risk criteria.

Local deviations from
\psi\in\mathbf{\Psi}
can be characterized via a set of paths
\{\psi_{\delta,h}:h\in L^{2}(P_{Z})\}
where
L^{2}(P_{Z})
is the space of real-valued functions on the support of
Z
implied by
P
with finite second moment. Thus, taking
\mathbb{T}_{P}(\psi)=L^{2}(P_{Z})
the gradient of
\Theta_{P_{0}}
is

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d}{d\delta}\Theta_{P_{0}}(\psi_{\delta,h})\right|_{\delta=0}=\int\kappa_{0}(z)\dot{\zeta}_{0}(z)h(z)dP_{0,Z}(z)\ .

Thus,
D_{\Theta,P_{0}}(\psi)=\kappa_{0}\dot{\zeta}_{0}
, where
\dot{\zeta}_{0}={d\zeta_{0}}/{d\psi}
. Specific forms of
\kappa_{0}
and
\dot{\zeta}_{0}
for each problem are detailed in Supplement Section
H.1
. This general analysis provides insight into (i) when we can expect there to be closed formed solutions for
\lambda_{0}
and (ii) when we can expect more challenging optimization problems as in the equalized risk example above.

Under mean squared error risk, when
\zeta(\psi)=\psi
, Condition (
7
) and
\Theta_{P_{0}}(\psi_{0,\lambda_{0}})=0
together imply
\psi_{0}^{*}=\psi_{0}-\lambda_{0}\kappa_{0}/2
, where
\lambda_{0}=2\Theta_{P_{0}}(\psi_{0})/\int\kappa_{0}(z)^{2}dP_{0}(z)
.
Under cross-entropy risk when
\zeta(\psi)=\psi
, Condition (
7
) implies that
\psi_{0}^{*}
will satisfy the quadratic equation,
\lambda_{0}\kappa_{0}\psi_{0}^{*2}-\left\{1+\lambda_{0}\kappa_{0}\right\}\psi_{0}^{*}+\psi_{0}=0
.
This equation has root
\psi_{0}^{*}(z)=(\lambda_{0}+\kappa_{0}^{-1}(z)-(2x-1)[\{\lambda_{0}+\kappa_{0}^{-1}(z)\}^{2}-4\lambda_{0}\kappa_{0}^{-1}(z)\psi_{0}(z)]^{1/2})/(2\lambda_{0})
.

5
Simulations

We evaluated the asymptotic performance of our estimators in several settings. For each bound on the constraint, we study (i) the convergence of the risk of our estimator
R_{P_{0}}(\psi_{n,\lambda_{n}})
to the optimal constrained risk
R_{P_{0}}(\psi_{0,\lambda_{0}})
and (ii) that the true fairness constraint under
\psi_{n,\lambda_{n}}
,
\Theta_{P_{0}}(\psi_{n,\lambda_{n}})
, converges appropriately. In all simulations, we evaluated our proposed estimators when built based on both equality and inequality constraints. Part of our results are relegated to Supplemental Section
I
.

For each setting, we generated 1000 simulated data sets of sample sizes
n
= 100, 200, 400, 800, 1600 and used an independent test set of 1
e
6 observations to numerically approximate the value of the true risk and the true constraint.

Our first set of simulations considered a setting where the true underlying functional parameters fall into known finite-dimensional parametric models. We considered two data generating mechanisms. Both data generating mechanisms shared the same joint distribution of
(X,W)
. The vector
W
consisted of six components
W_{1},\dots,W_{6}
drawn from the following distribution:
W_{1}\sim\mbox{Bern}(1/4),W_{2}\mid W_{1}\sim\mbox{Bern}(\mbox{expit}(W_{1}))
, where
expit
is the inverse logit function,
W_{3}\mid W_{1},W_{2}\sim\mbox{Bern}(\mbox{expit}(-W_{2}+W_{1}))
,
W_{4}\sim\mbox{Norm}(0,1)
,
W_{5}\sim\mbox{Unif}(0,1)
,
W_{6}\sim\mbox{Exp}(1)
. Given
W
,
X
was generated from a
\mbox{Bern}(\mbox{expit}(W_{1}-W_{2}/3-W_{6}/10))
. We then considered separate means of generating (i) a continuous outcome to study the ATE and NDE constraints in Sections
4.1
and
4.2
and (ii) a binary outcome
Y
to study the equal risk in the cases constraint in Section
4.3
.

5.1
Average treatment effect and natural direct effect

In this setting, we simulated a binary mediator
M
such that
\gamma_{0}(1\mid x,w)=\mbox{expit}(-1-x-w_{1}+w_{2}/2-w_{5}/2)
and simulated the outcome
Y
from a Normal distribution with mean given
(X,W,M)
equal to
-X+2M+2W_{1}-W_{3}-W_{4}+2W_{5}
and variance equal to 4. We separately considered the prediction problem of minimizing mean squared error of constrained predictions of
Y
given
(X,W)
-only under the ATE constraint (Section
4.1
) and given
(X,W,M)
under the NDE constraint (Section
4.2
). Note that the conditional distribution of
Y
implies that linear regression can be used to correctly model both the conditional mean given
(X,W)
(i.e.,
\psi_{0}
for the ATE constraint problem), as well as the conditional mean given
(X,W,M)
(i.e.,
\psi_{0}
for the NDE constraint problem). Both these problems also require estimates of
\pi_{0}
, which was estimated using maximum likelihood based on a correctly-specified logistic regression model.

Our proposed estimator performed as expected in terms of achieving optimal risk and controlling the constraint. Figure
1
shows results for the ATE constraint. The top left panel indicates that our proposed approach nears the optimal risk as sample size increases, while still controlling the constraint with high probability (top right panel). When we relax the constraint bound to only enforce that the ATE is less than 1/2 (bottom right), we find that (i) as expected, the optimal value of the risk (indicated by the dashed line) lowers and our estimator again achieves this optimal risk as sample size increases. Finally, we observed that for each fixed sample size, our estimator appropriately respects the nature of inequality constraints when the bound on the constraint is set larger than the value of
\Theta_{P_{0}}(\psi_{0})
. Results displaying the distribution of the constraint for sample size
n=800
are shown in the bottom right panel. We find that once the bound on the constraint is larger than the true ATE (around -1.27), our procedure shifts to using
\psi_{n}
, the unconstrained estimate of
\psi_{0}
. Accordingly, the true value of the constraint for the estimate levels off at
\Theta_{P_{0}}(\psi_{0})
.

Discussion

In this work, we have developed novel methodologies for optimally estimating constrained functional parameters. The methodology is applicable across a broad spectrum of constraints and statistical models, making them potentially beneficial in other contexts, such as density estimation with moment constraints in classical statistics.

Our primary class of constraints was motivated by fairness in machine learning. In this context, a notable strength of our framework is that we can often obtain closed-form solutions that express the optimal approach for fair prediction in terms of interpretable parameters of
P_{0}
. This is important for several reasons. First, it enables practitioners to integrate background scientific knowledge into the estimation process of these interpretable parameters, potentially leading to improved predictive performance. This approach stands in contrast to methods that aim to estimate the constrained parameter directly, which may not effectively leverage such background knowledge, possibly resulting in sub-optimal predictive performance. Furthermore, having closed-form solutions for optimal fair prediction facilitates an insight into the dynamics that result in unfair predictions, and outlines strategies for their mitigation. This insight could be particularly valuable for the development and maintenance of online monitoring systems for machine learning models in practice, ensuring they remain fair under evolving data distributions. Taking the average treatment effect as an example, our results suggest that, while a population shift in
\psi_{0}
and/or
\pi_{0}
suggest a sub-optimal predictive model, a population shift in
P_{0,W}
would imply that a predictive algorithm that was previously deemed “fair” may no longer yield fair predictions under the shifted distribution.

It is of interest in future work to explore the development and empirical comparison of a comprehensive suite of candidate estimators for the constrained functional parameter, differentiated by their estimation methodologies and optimization techniques. This diversity allows for tailored approaches to specific fairness constraints and contexts, enhancing the flexibility and robustness of fair prediction models. Implementing cross-validation schemes for selection among these candidate estimators could further refine the estimation process, ensuring the selection of the most effective model based on empirical performance. Additionally, exploring double robust estimation methods for the constraints could provide an extra layer of reliability and accuracy in scenarios where traditional estimation techniques might be ineffective.

B.1
Pathwise differentiability and links to efficiency theory

Assumption (
A1
) is similar to the assumption of
pathwise differentiability
that is commonly invoked in nonparametric and semiparametric efficiency theory (see e.g.,
van der Laan 1995
, Definition 1.6). In these applications, it is common to consider paths
\{P_{\delta,h}:\delta\in\mathbb{R}\}
through
P\in\mathcal{M}
where
h\in L_{0}^{2}(P)
, the Hilbert space of real-valued functions defined on the support of
P
with mean zero and finite second moment endowed with inner product
\langle f,g\rangle=\int f(o)g(o)dP(o)
. In these settings pathwise differentiability of a given parameter
\Gamma:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is typically defined by the existence of a continuous linear map
\dot{\Gamma}_{P}:L_{0}^{2}(P)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
that can be used to represent the pathwise derivative, i.e., we say
\Gamma
is pathwise differentiable if
\frac{d}{d\delta}\Gamma(P_{\delta,h})|_{\delta=0}=\dot{\Gamma}_{P}(h)
.

The spirit of assumption (
A1
) is the same as this classic definition of pathwise differentiability: the assumption requires functionals that are
smooth enough
for their derivatives to be well behaved. However, the specific details in terms of constructing the respective Hilbert spaces differ across the two settings. For example, we consider paths through
\psi
, a functional of
P
rather than through
P
itself. Our notation indexing vector spaces by
P
, e.g.,
\hbox{${\rm I\kern-1.99997ptH}$}_{P}(\psi)
, appropriately reflects this distinction. In spite of the subtle distinctions, it should still be considered appropriate to state that assumption (
A1
) is one requiring pathwise differentiability of
R_{P}
and
\Theta_{P}
.

Appendix C
Second-order conditions

Assume
\Theta_{P}(\psi)=0
defines a non-empty feasible region in the parameter space
\bf\Psi
. Given Assumption (
A1
), let
\psi^{*}_{0}=\psi_{0,\lambda_{0}}
denote the functional parameter which fulfills the “first-order” condition as outlined in (
7
), with
\lambda_{0}
selected to ensure
\Theta_{P_{0}}(\psi_{0,\lambda_{0}})=0.
Typically,
\psi^{*}_{0}
is recognized as an extremum point, which could be either a minimum or a maximum. To determine if
\psi^{*}_{0}
represents a minimum, the following arguments on second-order conditions are considered.

Assume
X
represents a sensitive characteristic with
K
categories, and let
Z=O\setminus X
. We can decompose the functional parameter
\psi(O)
into
K
distinct components, where each component corresponds to units with a specific level of
X
. For
i=0,\ldots,K-1
, we denote the component by
\psi^{i}(Z)
. Similarly, we can partition
\psi^{*}_{0}(Z)
into
\psi^{0,*}_{0}(Z),\ldots,\psi^{K-1,*}_{0}(Z)
. For a given data point
z
, our objective is to check whether the vector
(\psi^{0,*}_{0}(z),\ldots,\psi^{K-1,*}_{0}(z))
is a minimum point. For this objective, we can examine the sufficient second-order conditions which involve constructing the bordered Hessian matrix; i.e., a modified version of the Hessian that incorporates the constraints of the problem via the Lagrange multiplier(s). For a given
\psi^{i}\in\Psi(i=0,\ldots,K-1)
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, let

•

\mathcal{L}_{P}(\psi^{i},\lambda)\coloneqq R_{P}(\psi)+\lambda\Theta_{P}(\psi)\big|_{X=i}
define the Lagrangian function evaluated at
X=i
,

•

\dot{\mathcal{L}}_{P}(\psi^{i},\lambda)=\frac{\partial}{\partial\delta}\mathcal{L}_{P}(\psi_{\delta,h},\lambda)\big|_{\delta=0,X=i}
define the the canonical function evaluated at
X=i
. In other words,
\dot{\mathcal{L}}_{P}(\psi^{i},\lambda)\coloneqq D_{R,P}(\psi^{i})+\lambda D_{\Theta,P}(\psi^{i})
, where
D_{R,P}(\psi^{i})=D_{R,P}(\psi)\big|_{X=i}
.

•

\ddot{\mathcal{L}}_{P}(\psi^{i},\lambda)=\frac{\partial}{\partial\delta}\dot{\mathcal{L}}_{P}(\psi_{\delta,h},\lambda)\big|_{\delta=0,X=i}
define the Hessian function evaluated at
X=i
. In other words,
\ddot{\mathcal{L}}_{P}(\psi^{i},\lambda)\coloneqq\dot{D}_{R,P}(\psi^{i})+\lambda\dot{D}_{\Theta,P}(\psi^{i})
, where
\dot{D}_{R,P}(\psi^{i})=\frac{\partial}{\partial\delta}D_{R,P}(\psi^{i}_{\delta,h})\big|_{\delta=0}
.

For a given
\psi
and
\lambda
, the bordered Hessian evaluated at data point
z
is a
(K+d)\times(K+d)
matrix, denoted by
\bar{H}_{z}(\psi,\lambda)
and defined as follows:

\displaystyle\bar{H}_{z}(\psi(z),\lambda)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}0&\bar{D}_{\Theta,P}(\psi)(z)\\
\\
\bar{D}^{T}_{\Theta,P}(\psi)(z)&\bar{\ddot{\mathcal{L}}}_{P}(\psi(z),\lambda)\end{bmatrix}\ ,

(25)

where
\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\lambda^{2}}\mathcal{L}_{P}(\psi,\lambda)=0
,
\bar{D}_{\Theta,P}(\psi)
denotes a vector of length
K
with the
j
-th element being
D_{\Theta,P}(\psi^{j})
, and
\bar{\ddot{\mathcal{L}}}_{P}(\psi,\lambda)
denotes a
K\times K
matrix with the
ij
-th element (
i\not=j
) being
\frac{\partial}{\partial\delta}\Big\{\frac{\partial}{\partial\delta}\mathcal{L}_{P}(\psi_{\delta,h})\big|_{X=i}\Big\}\Big|_{\delta=0,X=j}
, and the
i
-th element on the diagonal being
\ddot{\mathcal{L}}_{P}(\psi^{i},\lambda)
.

To determine whether
\psi^{*}_{0}\equiv\psi_{0,\lambda_{0}}
is a minimizer, we then need to examine the leading principal minors of the bordered Hessian evaluated at
(\psi^{0,*}_{0},\ldots,\psi^{K-1,*}_{0})
. For simplicity, we consider the sufficient conditions required for our provided examples in Section
4
, where
K=2
. In these scenarios, the bordered Hessian in (
25
) simplifies to:

\displaystyle\bar{H}_{z}(\psi^{0}(z),\psi^{1}(z),\lambda)=\begin{bmatrix}0&D_{\Theta,P}(\psi^{0})(z)&D_{\Theta,P}(\psi^{1})(z)\\
\\
D_{\Theta,P}(\psi^{0})(z)&\ddot{\mathcal{L}}_{P}(\psi^{0}(z),\lambda)&0\\
\\
D_{\Theta,P}(\psi^{1})(z)&0&\ddot{\mathcal{L}}_{P}(\psi^{1}(z),\lambda)\end{bmatrix}\ .

(26)

Then to have a minimum, it suffices that the determinant of the
3\times 3
bordered Hessian, evaluated at
(\psi^{0,*}_{0},\psi^{1,*}_{0})
be negative. That is:

\displaystyle-D^{2}_{\Theta,P}(\psi^{1,*}_{0})\times\ddot{\mathcal{L}}_{P}(\psi^{0,*}_{0},\lambda)-D^{2}_{\Theta,P}(\psi^{0,*}_{0})\times\ddot{\mathcal{L}}_{P}(\psi^{1,*}_{0},\lambda)<0\ .

(27)

In order for (
27
) to hold, it is sufficient for the Hessian function
\ddot{\mathcal{L}}_{P}(\psi^{x},\lambda)
to be positive. This positivity can be linked to the convexity of both the risk and constraint functions, as well as the sign of the Lagrange multiplier. A risk function
R_{P}(\psi)
is termed convex if, for any
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}\in{\bf\Psi}
and for any
\alpha
within the closed unit interval,
R_{P}(\alpha\psi_{1}+(1-\alpha)\psi_{2})\leq\alpha R_{P}(\psi_{1})+(1-\alpha)R_{P}(\psi_{2})
. The risk function is considered strictly convex when this inequality is strict. Convexity of the constraint is defined in a similar manner. When the parameter space
\bf\Psi
, the risk function
R_{P}(\psi)
, and the constraint
\Theta_{P}(\psi)
are convex, and with a positive
\lambda
, it is assured that the extremum point will be a minimum.

Appendix D
General multi-dimensional constraints

Suppose there are
d

\Theta^{(j)}_{P}(\psi)=0
for
j=1,\ldots,d
, along with
m

\Omega^{(i)}_{P}(\psi)\leq 0,\ i=1,\ldots,m
. Let
\Theta_{P}(\psi)
collect all the equality constraints and
\Omega_{P}(\psi)
for all the inequality constraints. The task is then to determine the optimal functional parameter,
\Psi^{*}(P)
, defined by minimizing
R_{P}(\psi)
over
\psi
in the parameter space
{\bf\Psi}
, subject to these equality and inequality constraints:

\displaystyle\Psi^{*}(P)=\argmin_{\psi\in{\bf\Psi}}\ R_{P}(\psi)

(28)

\displaystyle\text{subject to:}

\displaystyle\Theta^{(j)}_{P}(\psi)=0,\ j=1,\ldots,d\ ,\ \text{ and }\ \Omega^{(i)}_{P}(\psi)\leq 0,\ i=1,\ldots,m\ .

To extend our method from the one-dimensional constraint to accommodate multi-dimensional constraints, we assume that the element-wise derivatives of the constraints along paths
\{\psi_{\delta,h}:\delta\in\mathbb{R}\}
for
h\in\mathbb{T}_{P}(\psi)
satisfy that

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pth\mapsto\frac{d}{d\delta}\Theta^{(j)}_{P}(\psi_{\delta,h})\Big|_{\delta=0}\quad\text{and}\quad h\mapsto\frac{d}{d\delta}\Omega^{(i)}_{P}(\psi_{\delta,h})\Big|_{\delta=0}\ ,

(S-A5)

are bounded linear functionals on
\mathbb{T}_{P}(\psi)
for
j=1,\ldots,d
and
i=1,\ldots,m
.

For each equality constraint
\Theta^{(j)}_{P}
and inequality constraint
\Omega^{(i)}_{P}
, we define the canonical gradient of their respective pathwise derivatives at
\delta=0
as
D^{(j)}_{\Theta,P}(\psi)
and
D^{(i)}_{\Omega,P}(\psi)
. These gradients are aggregated into vector valued functions
D_{\Theta,P}(\psi)
and
D_{\Omega,P}(\psi)
for the equality and inequality constraints, respectively. We also assume the gradients
D_{\Omega,P}
and
D_{\Theta,P}
are non-degenerate at a solution
\psi_{0}^{*}
. That is

\displaystyle D^{(j)}_{\Theta,P}(\psi_{0}^{*})(o)\ \text{ and }D^{(i)}_{\Omega,P}(\psi_{0}^{*})(o)\quad\text{are linearly independent}\ ,

(S-A6)

for all
j=1,\ldots,d
and
i=1,\ldots,m
, and for all
o
in the support of
O
. This the general form of the linear independence constraint qualification for this problem
[
Boltyanski et al. 1998
,
Sundaram 1996
,
Gould and Tolle 1971
]
.

Consider the solution
\psi_{0,\lambda,\mu}=\argmin_{\psi\in{\bf\Psi}}R_{P_{0}}(\psi)+\lambda^{\top}\Theta_{P_{0}}(\psi)+\mu^{\top}\Omega_{P_{0}}(\psi)
, where
\Theta_{P_{0}}(\psi)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\Omega_{P_{0}}(\psi)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\mu\in(\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0})^{m}
. Similar to the one-dimensional constraint,
\{\psi_{0,\lambda,\mu}:\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\mu\in(\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0})^{m}\}
defines a constraint-specific path through
\psi_{0}
at
\lambda=[0]^{d},\mu=[0]^{m}.

Drawing from the rationale in the proof of Lemma
1
, finding the solution
\psi_{0,\lambda,\mu}
for particular
\lambda
and
\mu
values allows us to find the optimal solution
\psi^{*}_{0}\equiv\Psi^{*}(P_{0})
in (
28
): it is the solution
\psi_{0,\lambda_{0},\mu_{0}}
where
\lambda_{0}
and
\mu_{0}
are determined by the conditions
\Theta_{P_{0}}(\psi_{0,\lambda,\mu})=[0]^{d}
and
\mu\Omega_{P_{0}}(\psi_{0,\lambda,\mu})=[0]^{m}
, where
\mu\Omega_{P_{0}}(\psi_{0,\lambda,\mu})
denotes pointwise multiplication of the elements of the vectors. This latter condition is often referred to in non-linear optimization as
complementary slackness
conditions. Following similar logic preceding Theorem
1
, the path
\{\psi_{0,\lambda,\mu}:\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\mu\in(\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0})^{m}\}
must satisfy

\displaystyle D_{R,P_{0}}(\psi_{0,\lambda,\mu})+\lambda^{\top}D_{\Theta,P_{0}}(\psi_{0,\lambda,\mu})+\mu^{\top}D_{\Omega,P_{0}}(\psi_{0,\lambda,\mu})=0\ ,

(29)

for
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\ \mu\in(\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0})^{m}
. The above characterizes
\psi_{0,\lambda,\mu}
as the solution.

When a closed-form solution for
\psi_{0,\lambda,\mu}
, as described via (

D.1
Example: Equalized risk in the cases and controls

Suppose
O=(W,X,Y)\sim P_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
. We are interested in the mappings
\psi
on the support of
(X,W)
. Let
\Theta_{P_{0}}(\psi)=(\Theta_{P_{0},1}(\psi),\Theta_{P_{0},0}(\psi))

and

is the negative log-likelihood loss.
\Theta_{P_{0}}(\psi)
encodes a two-dimensional constraint corresponding to the equalized risk in both cases (
Y=1
) and controls (
Y=0

The canonical gradient of the cross-entropy risk is given by (
13

G.1.1

\Theta_{P_{0},y}(\psi)
is

-(2x-1)/P_{0}(x,y)\{\psi_{0}(x,w)/\psi(x,w)\}
. Condition (
29

\displaystyle\frac{\psi_{0,\lambda}(y\mid x,w)-\psi_{0}(y\mid x,w)}{\psi_{0,\lambda}(y\mid x,w)}-\ \lambda^{\top}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}C_{0}(x,1)\ \displaystyle\frac{\psi_{0}(1\mid x,w)}{\psi_{0,\lambda}(1\mid x,w)}(\mathbb{I}(Y=1)-\psi_{0,\lambda}(1\mid x,w))\\

where

and
\lambda^{\top}=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
. A closed-form solution for

is obtained as follows

\displaystyle\psi_{0,\lambda}(y\mid x,w)=\psi_{0}(y\mid x,w)\frac{1+\lambda_{1}C_{0}(x,1)y+\lambda_{2}C_{0}(x,0)(1-y)}{1+\lambda_{1}C_{0}(x,1)\psi_{0}(1\mid x,w)+\lambda_{2}C_{0}(x,0)\psi_{0}(0\mid x,w)}\ .

To determine
\psi_{0,\lambda_{0}}
, given a solution for

it suffices to find

that satisfies
\Theta_{P_{0},1}(\psi_{0,\lambda_{0}})=0
and
\Theta_{P_{0},0}(\psi_{0,\lambda_{0}})=0

D.2
Estimation via (
29
)

Assume there exists a closed-form solution for (
29
) that writes as
\psi_{0,\lambda,\mu}=r(\eta_{0},\lambda,\mu)
for some mapping
r
, where
\eta_{0}=\eta(P_{0})
denote the collection of nuisance parameters indexing the gradients
D_{R,P_{0}},D_{\Theta,P_{0}},
and
D_{\Omega,P_{0}}
. The plug-in estimator
\psi_{n,\lambda,\mu}=r(\eta_{n},\lambda,\mu)
can be used to estimate any
\psi_{0,\lambda,\mu}
on the constraint-specific path. A plug-in estimate of
\psi_{0,\lambda_{0},\mu_{0}}
is given by
\psi_{n,\lambda_{n},\mu_{n}}
, where
\lambda_{n}
and
\mu_{n}
are obtained either through a simple plug-in of their corresponding closed-form solutions or empirical minimizations solved via a (d+m)-dimensional grid search or other more sophisticated minimization approaches.

We describe the grid search procedure in the following. Let
\Theta^{(j)}_{n}(\psi)
and
\Omega^{(i)}_{n}(\psi)
denote estimates of
\Theta^{(j)}_{P_{0}}(\psi)
and
\Omega^{(i)}_{P_{0}}(\psi)
, respectively. Before proceeding with the grid search, we first evaluate the complementary slackness condition outlined below. This involves initializing
\mu_{n}=[0]^{m}
and searching for
\lambda^{*}_{n}
that satisfies
\Theta_{n}(\psi_{n,\lambda^{*}_{n},\mu_{n}=0})=[0]^{d}
and
\Omega_{n}(\psi_{n,\lambda^{*}_{n},\mu_{n}=0})\leq[0]^{m}
. If these conditions are met, the process terminates early; otherwise, the search continues in an iterative exclusion and search process described below:

•

Begin by excluding one element at a time from the vector
\mu_{n}
, specifically
\mu_{i,n}
, while maintaining the rest at zero. We use
\mu_{\preceq i,n}
to represent the set of elements in
\mu_{n}
that have been excluded up to and including
\mu_{i,n}
. Conversely,
\mu_{\succ i,n}
denotes the elements that have not been excluded.

•

For each specific configuration identified by
\mu_{i,n}
, we aim to find the vectors
\lambda^{*}_{n}
and
\mu^{*}_{\preceq i,n}
that satisfy the condition:
\Omega^{(\succ i)}_{n}(\psi_{n,\lambda^{*}_{n},\mu^{*}_{\preceq i,n}})\leq[0]^{|\succ i|}
, and

\displaystyle\lambda^{*}_{n}=\arg\min_{\lambda_{n}\in[-\delta,\delta]^{d}}\Theta_{n}(\psi_{n,\lambda_{n},\mu_{\preceq i,n},\mu_{\succ i,n}=0})\ ,

\displaystyle\mu^{*}_{\preceq i,n}=\arg\min_{\mu_{s}\in(0,\delta]}\Omega^{(\preceq i)}_{n}(\psi_{n,\lambda_{n},\mu_{\preceq i,n},\mu_{\succ i,n}=0})=[0]^{|\preceq i|}\ .

Here,
|\preceq i|
and
|\succ i|
represent the number of elements excluded and included, respectively, in
\mu_{n}
relative to
\mu_{i,n}
. The functions
\Omega^{(\preceq i)}_{n}
and
\Omega^{(\succ i)}_{n}
aggregate the constraints from
\Omega_{n}
for the elements in
\mu_{\preceq i,n}
and
\mu_{\succ i,n}
, respectively.

•

The search terminates if such vectors are identified; otherwise, it proceeds by excluding an additional element from
\mu
and repeating the evaluation.

If the procedure has not terminated after exploring all possible combinations of exclusions from
\mu_{n}=[0]^{m}
, we seek to find the optimal estimates of
\lambda^{*}_{n}
and
\mu^{*}_{n}
, such that

\displaystyle\lambda^{*}_{n},\mu^{*}_{n}=\argmin_{\lambda\in[-\delta,\delta]^{d},\mu\in(0,\delta]^{m}}|\Theta_{n}(\psi_{n,\lambda,\mu})|+|\Omega_{n}(\psi_{n,\lambda,\mu})|\ .

D.3
Recursive estimation via (
30
)

Contrary to the one-dimensional constraint scenario, the construction of a recursive estimator for
\psi_{0,\lambda,\mu}
via (
30
) is more involved. This complexity arises from the necessity to perform computations over a (d+m)-dimensional grid to determine
\psi_{0,\lambda,\mu}
across all values of
\lambda
and
\mu
.

As an example, suppose we have two equality constraints. Then a recursive solution for
\psi_{0,\lambda}=\psi_{0,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}}
using (
30
) requires a 2-dimensional grid search and a quantification of
\psi_{0,\lambda_{1}+d\nu,\lambda_{2}}
,
\psi_{0,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}+d\nu}
, and
\psi_{0,\lambda_{1}+d\nu,\lambda_{2}+d\nu}
. Let
r_{j}(\eta_{0},\lambda)=-\big\{\dot{D}_{0,R+\lambda^{\top}\Theta}(\psi_{0,\lambda})\big\}^{-1}D^{(j)}_{\Theta,P_{0}}(\psi_{0,\lambda})
, for
j=1,2
. The characterization in (
30
) describes
\frac{d}{d\lambda_{1}}\psi_{0,\lambda}
as function
r_{1}(\eta_{0},\lambda)
and
\frac{d}{d\lambda_{2}}\psi_{0,\lambda}
as function
r_{2}(\eta_{0},\lambda)
. It also implies
\frac{d^{2}}{d\lambda_{1}d\lambda_{2}}\psi_{0,\lambda}=\frac{d}{d\lambda_{2}}r_{1}(\eta_{0},\lambda)
, which can be represented as

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}}{d\lambda_{1}d\lambda_{2}}\psi_{0,\lambda}

\displaystyle=\left\{\frac{d}{d\psi_{0,\lambda}}r_{1}(\eta_{0},\lambda)\right\}\times\left\{\frac{d}{d\lambda_{2}}\psi_{0,\lambda}\right\}=\left\{\frac{d}{d\psi_{0,\lambda}}r_{1}(\eta_{0},\lambda)\right\}\times r_{2}(\eta_{0},\lambda)\equiv r_{12}(\eta_{0},\lambda)\ .

Equivalently,
r_{12}(\eta_{0},\lambda)
can be defined as
\left\{\frac{d}{d\psi_{0,\lambda}}r_{2}(\eta_{0},\lambda)\right\}\times r_{1}(\eta_{0},\lambda)
.
We have now succeeded in expressing the two first-order derivatives of
\psi_{0,\lambda}
w.r.t.
\lambda_{1}
,
\lambda_{2}
and the second-order derivative w.r.t. both
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
. Using numerical derivatives, we can derive the following pieces required for the recursive estimation of
\psi_{0,\lambda}
for all
\lambda
in an arbitrary
d\nu
-grid:

\displaystyle\psi_{0,\lambda_{1}+d\nu,\lambda_{2}}

\displaystyle=\psi_{0,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}}+d\nu\times r_{1}(\eta_{0},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}),

\displaystyle\psi_{0,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}+d\nu}

\displaystyle=\psi_{0,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}}+d\nu\times r_{2}(\eta_{0},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}),

\displaystyle\psi_{0,\lambda_{1}+d\nu,\lambda_{2}+d\nu}

\displaystyle=\psi_{0,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}}+d\nu\times r_{1}(\eta_{0},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})+d\nu\times r_{2}(\eta_{0},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})+d\nu^{2}\times r_{12}(\eta_{0},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\ .

The above set of differential equations can be used to
recursively
estimate
\psi_{0,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}}
.
We start with
\psi_{n}
, an estimate of the unconstrained minimizer
\psi_{0}
. For a numerically small step size
d\nu
, we can compute
\psi_{n,d\nu,0}=\psi_{n}+r_{1}(\eta_{n},0,0)d\nu
,
\psi_{n,0,d\nu}=\psi_{n}+r_{2}(\eta_{n},0,0)d\nu
, and
\psi_{n,d\nu,d\nu}=\psi_{n}+r_{1}(\eta_{n},0,0)d\nu+r_{2}(\eta_{n},0,0)d\nu+r_{12}(\eta_{n},0,0)d\nu^{2}
. This procedure proceeds where at the
k
-th step, we define
\lambda_{j,k}=\lambda_{j,k-1}+d\nu
, for
j=1,2
, and compute
\psi_{n,\lambda_{1,k},\lambda_{2,k-1}}=\psi_{0,\lambda_{1,k-1},\lambda_{2,k-1}}+r_{1}(\eta_{n},\lambda_{1,k-1},\lambda_{2,k-1})d\nu
,
\psi_{n,\lambda_{1,k-1},\lambda_{2,k}}=\psi_{0,\lambda_{1,k-1},\lambda_{2,k-1}}+r_{2}(\eta_{n},\lambda_{1,k-1},\lambda_{2,k-1})d\nu
, and
\psi_{n,\lambda_{1,k},\lambda_{2,k}}
as

\displaystyle\psi_{n,\lambda_{1,k-1},\lambda_{2,k-1}}+r_{1}(\eta_{n},\lambda_{1,k-1},\lambda_{2,k-1})d\nu+r_{2}(\eta_{n},\lambda_{1,k-1},\lambda_{2,k-1})d\nu+r_{12}(\eta_{n},\lambda_{1,k-1},\lambda_{2,k-1})d\nu^{2}.

Since we can compute an estimate of
\psi_{n,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}}
for any
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
, it is possible to utilize empirical minimization as above to generate an estimate of
\lambda_{0}=(\lambda_{1,0},\lambda_{2,0})
.

Given the canonical gradients

Prior to examining the second-order conditions for

\displaystyle\lambda C_{0}(x,w)\psi^{2}_{0,\lambda}(x,w)-\{1+\lambda C_{0}(x,w)\}\psi_{0,\lambda}(x,w)+\psi_{0}(x,w)=0\ .

[
Existence of a valid solution.

We assert that a solution for

\frac{1+\lambda C_{0}(x,w)\pm\{1+\lambda^{2}C_{0}^{2}(x,w)+2\lambda C_{0}(x,w)-4\lambda C_{0}(x,w)\psi_{0}(x,w)\}^{1/2}}{2\lambda C_{0}(x,w)}\ .

G.1.2
Equalized cross-entropy for conditional probability mass functions

In the example discussed above, we explored paths through
\psi
when
\psi
was a conditional mean. We can also consider paths through conditional probability mass function
P(Y\mid X,W)
. We ultimately arrive at the exact same characterization for the constraint-specific path. However, the details are worth explicating as this reveals additional insight into the choice of Hilbert space where paths are embedded.

Let
\psi_{0}(y\mid x,w)=P_{0}(Y=y\mid X=x,W=w)
be the unconstrained parameter defined as a conditional binary probability mass function. Note that
\psi_{0}=\argmin_{\psi\in{\bf\Psi}}P_{0}L(\psi)
, where
L(\psi)(o)=-\log\psi(y\mid x,w)
is the negative log-likelihood loss. The path
\{\psi_{\delta,h}(o)=\psi(o)\{1+\delta h(o)\}:\delta\in\mathbb{R}\}
with direction
\frac{d}{d\delta}\psi_{\delta,h}|_{\delta=0}=h(o)\psi(o)
is through
\psi
at
\delta=0
. For this path to be within the parameter space
\bf\Psi
,
h(o)
must be mean zero under
\psi
,
\sum_{y=0}^{1}h(o)\ \psi(y\mid x,w)=0
for each
(x,w)
. Thus, exploring all possible local directions at
\psi
within the parameter space
\bf\Psi
would require that
h(o)\in L^{2}_{0}(P_{\psi,0}),
the space of bounded real-valued functions of
O
with mean zero and finite second moment under the distribution
P_{\psi,0}=\psi(y\mid x,w)P_{0,X,W}
. Thus,
\mathbb{T}_{P}(\psi)
can be defined as
L^{2}_{0}(\psi)
, a subspace of
L^{2}_{0}(P_{\psi,0})
containing real-valued functions of
O
defined on the support of
\psi
with conditional mean zero given
(X,W)
under
\psi
. We define the inner product on
L^{2}_{0}(P_{\psi,0})
as
\langle f,g\rangle=\int f(o)g(o)dP_{\psi,0}(o)
.

To derive the canonical gradients
D_{R,P_{0}}(\psi)
and
D_{\Theta,P_{0}}(\psi)
, we note that for any
h\in\mathbb{T}_{P}(\psi)
, we have

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d}{d\delta}P_{0}L(\psi_{\delta,h})\right|_{\delta=0}

\displaystyle=-P_{0}\big(h(O)\big)=-\int h(o)\ dP_{0}(o)

\displaystyle=-\int h(o)\times\frac{\psi_{0}(y\mid x,w)}{\psi(y\mid x,w)}\ dP_{\psi,0}(o)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\psi(y\mid x,w)-\psi_{0}(y\mid x,w)}{\psi(y\mid x,w)}\ h(o)\ dP_{\psi,0}(o)\ .

The last equality holds since
\int h(o)dP_{\psi,0}(o)=0
. Thus,
D_{R,P_{0}}(\psi)=(\psi-\psi_{0})/\psi
. We also have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\delta}

\displaystyle\Theta_{P_{0}}(\psi_{\delta,h})\Big|_{\delta=0}

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{d}{d\delta}\Big\{\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}(L(\psi_{\delta,h})\mid X=1,Y=1)-\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}(L(\psi_{\delta,h})\mid X=0,Y=1)\Big\}\right|_{\delta=0}

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}(h(O)\mid X=1,Y=1)+\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}(h(O)\mid X=0,Y=1)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}\bigg[\Big\{\frac{\mathbb{I}(X=0,Y=1)}{P_{0}(X=0,Y=1)}-\frac{\mathbb{I}(X=1,Y=1)}{P_{0}(X=1,Y=1)}\Big\}h(O)\bigg]

\displaystyle=\int-\frac{y(2x-1)}{P_{0}(x,y)}\ \frac{\psi_{0}(y\mid x,w)}{\psi(y\mid x,w)}\ h(o)\ dP_{\psi,0}(o)

\displaystyle=\int-\frac{2x-1}{P_{0}(x,Y=1)}\ \frac{\psi_{0}(1\mid x,w)}{\psi(1\mid x,w)}\ (y-\psi(1\mid x,w))\ h(o)\ dP_{\psi,0}(o)\ .

The last equality holds since

\sum_{y=0}^{1}\frac{y(2x-1)}{P_{0}(X=x,Y=y)}\ \frac{\psi_{0}(y\mid x,w)}{\psi(y\mid x,w)}\ \psi(y\mid x,w)=\frac{2x-1}{p_{0,1}(x)}\psi_{0}(1\mid x,w)\ .

Thus,

D_{\Theta,P_{0}}(\psi)(o)=-C_{0}(x)\displaystyle\frac{\psi_{0}(1\mid x,w)}{\psi(1\mid x,w)}\ \{y-\psi(1\mid x,w)\}\ .

Condition (
7

\displaystyle\psi_{0,\lambda}(y\mid x,w)=\psi_{0}(y\mid x,w)\ \frac{1+\lambda\ y\ C_{0}(x)}{1+\lambda\ C_{0}(x)\ \psi_{0}(1\mid x,w)}\ ,

which agrees with the characterization of the constraint-specific path in (
19

(37)

\displaystyle\int\left[\frac{\pi_{0}(1\mid w)}{p_{0}(1)}\left\{\psi_{0}(1,w)-\psi(1,w)\right\}^{2}-\frac{\pi_{0}(0\mid w)}{p_{0}(0)}\left\{\psi_{0}(0,w)-\psi(0,w)\right\}^{2}\right]dP(w)

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt=\int\left\{\frac{\pi_{0}(0\mid w)}{p_{0}(0)}\sigma^{2}_{0}(0,w)-\frac{\pi_{0}(1\mid w)}{p_{0}(1)}\sigma^{2}_{0}(1,w)\right\}dP(w)\ ,

(38)

where
\sigma^{2}_{0}(x,w)
denotes the conditional variance of
Y
given
X=x,W=w
. This system of equations can be solved by considering the two cases defined by whether the right hand side of (
38
) is positive versus negative.

If the right-hand side of (
38
) is positive, then the system of equations implies an optimal solution can be achieved by setting
\psi(0,w)=\psi_{0}(0,w)
. This in turn implies that
\lambda_{0}=-p_{0}(1)
and that
\psi_{0}^{*}(1,w)
is any root in
\psi
of the equation

\displaystyle\int\left(\frac{\pi_{0}(1\mid w)}{p_{0}(1)}\left[\left\{\psi_{0}(1,w)-\psi(1,w)\right\}^{2}+\sigma_{0}(1,w)\right]-\frac{\pi_{0}(0\mid w)}{p_{0}(0)}\sigma^{2}_{0}(0,w)\right)dP(w)\ .

(39)

There will in general be infinitely many such roots. However, we can show that
R_{P_{0}}(\psi)=\mathbb{E}[\{1+\lambda_{0}C_{0}(X)\}\{Y-\psi_{0}(X,W)\}]^{2}
, which does not depend on
\psi(1,\cdot)
.
Thus, any root in the above equation will have the same penalized risk and therefore represents a valid solution to the optimization problem. A closed form expression for a solution
\psi_{0}^{*}(1,w)
can be obtained by setting the integrand of (
39
) to 0 and solving,

\displaystyle\psi_{0}^{*}(1,w)=\psi_{0}(1,w)+\left\{\frac{p_{0}(1)}{p_{0}(0)}\frac{\pi_{0}(0\mid w)}{\pi_{0}(1\mid w)}\sigma^{2}_{0}(0,w)-\sigma^{2}_{0}(1,w)\right\}^{1/2}\ .

If the right-hand side of (
38
) is negative, the above arguments can be made almost identically. Such an analysis concludes that
\lambda_{0}=p_{0}(0)
,
\psi_{0}^{*}(1,w)=\psi_{0}(1,w)
, and one solution for
\psi_{0}^{*}(0,w)
is

\displaystyle\psi_{0}^{*}(0,w)=\psi_{0}(0,w)+\left\{\frac{p_{0}(0)}{p_{0}(1)}\frac{\pi_{0}(1\mid w)}{\pi_{0}(0\mid w)}\sigma^{2}_{0}(1,w)-\sigma^{2}_{0}(0,w)\right\}^{1/2}\ .

Thus to generate estimates of
\psi_{0}^{*}
, we could obtain estimates of
\eta_{n}=(\psi_{n},\pi_{n},p_{n},\sigma^{2}_{n})
of
\eta_{0}=(\psi_{0},\pi_{0},p_{0},\sigma^{2}_{0})
. Here
\psi_{n}
and
\pi_{n}
can be obtained via any form of unconstrained learning,
p_{n}(1)=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
and
p_{n}(0)=1-p_{n}(1)
. The estimate
\sigma^{2}_{n}
can be obtained via any approach appropriate for conditional variance estimation. We define the plug-in estimator of the constraint for a particular
\psi
as

\Theta_{n}(\psi)=\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\frac{2X_{i}-1}{p_{n}(X_{i})}L(\psi)(O_{i})\ .

Next, we select a threshold
\epsilon
and check whether
|\Theta_{n}(\psi_{n})|<\epsilon
. If so, we report
\psi_{n}^{*}=\psi_{n}
. Otherwise, we compute the sign of

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\{\frac{\pi_{n}(0\mid W_{i})}{p_{n}(0)}\sigma^{2}_{n}(Y\mid 0,W_{i})-\frac{\pi_{n}(1\mid W_{i})}{p_{n}(1)}\sigma^{2}_{n}(Y\mid 1,W_{i})\right\}\ .

If positive, then we set
\psi_{n}^{*}(0,w)=\psi_{n}(0,w)
, and

\displaystyle\psi_{n}^{*}(1,w)=\psi_{n}(1,w)+\left[\frac{p_{n}(1)}{p_{n}(0)}\frac{\pi_{n}(0\mid w)}{\pi_{n}(1\mid w)}\sigma^{2}_{n}(Y\mid 0,w)-\sigma^{2}_{n}(Y\mid 1,w)\right]^{1/2}\ .

If negative, then we set
\psi_{n}^{*}(1,w)=\psi_{n}(1,w)
, and

\displaystyle\psi_{n}^{*}(0,w)=\psi_{n}(0,w)+\left[\frac{p_{n}(0)}{p_{n}(1)}\frac{\pi_{n}(1\mid w)}{\pi_{n}(0\mid w)}\sigma^{2}_{n}(Y\mid 1,w)-\sigma^{2}_{n}(Y\mid 0,w)\right]^{1/2}\ .

G.2.1
Equalized MSE risk: Second-order conditions

Given the canonical gradients
D_{R,P_{0}}(\psi)
and
D_{\Theta,P_{0}}(\psi)
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{P_{0}}(\psi,\lambda)

\displaystyle=\int\{\psi_{0}(x,w)-\psi(x,w)\}^{2}\{1+\lambda C_{0}(x)\}dP_{0}(x,w)\ ,

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{L}}_{P_{0}}(\psi,\lambda)

\displaystyle=-2\{\psi_{0}(x,w)-\psi(x,w)\}\{1+\lambda C_{0}(x)\}\ ,

\displaystyle\ddot{\mathcal{L}}_{P_{0}}(\psi,\lambda)

\displaystyle=2\ .

For any given
\psi=(\psi^{1},\psi^{0})\in{\bf\Psi}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, the criterion (
27
) simplifies to:

\displaystyle-D^{2}_{\Theta,P}(\psi^{1})\times\ddot{\mathcal{L}}_{P}(\psi^{0},\lambda)-D^{2}_{\Theta,P}(\psi^{0})\times\ddot{\mathcal{L}}_{P}(\psi^{1},\lambda)

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt=-2\frac{(\psi^{1}-\psi_{0}^{1})^{2}}{p_{0}(x)^{2}}-2\frac{(\psi^{0}-\psi_{0}^{0})^{2}}{p_{0}(0)^{2}}<0\ .

This concludes that
\psi_{0,\lambda_{0}}
is the optimal minimizer of the penalized MSE risk.

G.3
Equalized cross-entropy risk

Suppose
O=(W,X,Y)\sim P_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
. We are interested in the mappings
\psi
on the support of
(X,W)
. We consider the cross-entropy risk and let the constraint be the equalized risk,
\Theta_{P}(\psi)=\mathbb{E}_{P}\{L(\psi)(O)\mid X=1\}-\mathbb{E}_{P}\{L(\psi)(O)\mid X=0\}
. Let
C_{0}(x)=(2x-1)/p_{0}(x)
.

Recall that in section
E.1.2
we showed
D_{R,P_{0}}(\psi)=(1-\psi_{0})/(1-\psi)-\psi_{0}/\psi
. We can derive the gradient for the constraint as follows. For any
h\in\mathbb{T}_{P}(\psi)
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\delta}

\displaystyle\Theta_{P_{0}}(\psi_{\delta,h})\Big|_{\delta=0}

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{d}{d\delta}\Big\{\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}(L(\psi_{\delta,h})\mid X=1)-\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}(L(\psi_{\delta,h})\mid X=0)\Big\}\right|_{\delta=0}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}\bigg[\Big\{\frac{\mathbb{I}(X=1)}{P_{0}(X=1)}-\frac{\mathbb{I}(X=0)}{P_{0}(X=0)}\Big\}\Big\{\frac{1-Y}{1-\psi(X,W)}-\frac{Y}{\psi(X,W)}\Big\}h(O)\bigg]

\displaystyle=\int\frac{2x-1}{P_{0}(x)}\Big\{\frac{1-\psi_{0}(x,w)}{1-\psi(x,w)}-\frac{\psi_{0}(x,w)}{\psi(x,w)}\Big\}\ h(o)\ dP_{0}(x,w)\ .

Thus,
D_{\Theta,P_{0}}(\psi)(o)=\displaystyle C_{0}(x)\Big\{\frac{1-\psi_{0}(x,w)}{1-\psi(x,w)}-\frac{\psi_{0}(x,w)}{\psi(x,w)}\Big\}
. Condition (
7
) implies

\displaystyle\{1+\lambda C_{0}(x)\}\left\{\frac{1-\psi_{0}(x,w)}{1-\psi_{0,\lambda}(x,w)}-\frac{\psi_{0}(x,w)}{\psi_{0,\lambda}(x,w)}\right\}=0\ .

Along with the constraint equation, the above implies:

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt\left\{1+\frac{\lambda}{p_{0}(x)}\right\}\left\{\frac{1-\psi_{0}(1,w)}{1-\psi_{0,\lambda}(1,w)}-\frac{\psi_{0}(1,w)}{\psi_{0,\lambda}(1,w)}\right\}=0\ ,

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt\left\{1-\frac{\lambda}{p_{0}(0)}\right\}\left\{\frac{1-\psi_{0}(0,w)}{1-\psi_{0,\lambda}(0,w)}-\frac{\psi_{0}(0,w)}{\psi_{0,\lambda}(0,w)}\right\}=0\ ,

(40)

\displaystyle\int\frac{\pi_{0}(1\mid w)}{p_{0}(x)}\left\{-\psi_{0}(1,w)\log\psi(1,w)-(1-\psi_{0}(1,w))\log(1-\psi(1,w))\right\}dP(w)

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt=\int\frac{\pi_{0}(0\mid w)}{p_{0}(0)}\left\{-\psi_{0}(0,w)\log\psi(0,w)-(1-\psi_{0}(0,w))\log(1-\psi(0,w))\right\}dP(w)\ .

The above system of equations yield two solutions:

1.

\psi(0,w)=\psi_{0}(0,w),
with
\lambda_{0}=-p_{0}(x)
and
\psi(1,w)
chosen such that

\displaystyle\int\frac{\pi_{0}(1\mid w)}{p_{0}(x)}\left\{-\psi_{0}(1,w)\log\psi(1,w)-(1-\psi_{0}(1,w))\log(1-\psi(1,w))\right\}dP(w)

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt=\int\frac{\pi_{0}(0\mid w)}{p_{0}(0)}\left\{-\psi_{0}(0,w)\log\psi_{0}(0,w)-(1-\psi_{0}(0,w))\log(1-\psi_{0}(0,w))\right\}dP(w)\ ,

2.

\psi(1,w)=\psi_{0}(1,w),
with
\lambda_{0}=p_{0}(0)
and
\psi(0,w)
chosen such that

\displaystyle\int\frac{\pi_{0}(0\mid w)}{p_{0}(0)}\left\{-\psi_{0}(0,w)\log\psi(0,w)-(1-\psi_{0}(0,w))\log(1-\psi(0,w))\right\}dP(w)

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt=\int\frac{\pi_{0}(1\mid w)}{p_{0}(x)}\left\{-\psi_{0}(1,w)\log\psi_{0}(1,w)-(1-\psi_{0}(1,w))\log(1-\psi_{0}(1,w))\right\}dP(w)\ .

Note that since the penalized risk at
\lambda_{0}

\displaystyle R_{P_{0}}(\psi)+\lambda_{0}\Theta_{P_{0}}(\psi)

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt=\mathbb{E}[(1+\lambda_{0}C_{0}(X))(-\psi_{0}(X,W)\log\psi(X,W)-(1-\psi_{0}(X,W))\log(1-\psi(X,W))]

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt=\mathbb{E}\bigg[(1+\lambda_{0}C_{0}(X))\Big\{-\psi_{0}(X,W)\log\frac{\psi(X,W)}{\psi_{0}(X,W)}-(1-\psi_{0}(X,W))\log\frac{1-\psi(X,W)}{1-\psi_{0}(X,W)}\Big\}\bigg]

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+\mathbb{E}\bigg[(1+\lambda_{0}C_{0}(X))\Big\{-\psi_{0}(X,W)\log\psi_{0}(X,W)-(1-\psi_{0}(X,W))\log(1-\psi_{0}(X,W))\Big\}\bigg]

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt=\mathbb{E}\bigg[(1+\lambda_{0}C_{0}(X))\Big\{-\psi_{0}(X,W)\log\psi_{0}(X,W)-(1-\psi_{0}(X,W))\log(1-\psi_{0}(X,W))\Big\}\bigg]\ .

does not depend on
\psi(x,w)
. Thus, any
\psi(x,w)
, as long as it satisfies (
40
), should be a minimizer to the penalized risk.

We now investigate the second-order conditions. Given the canonical gradients
D_{R,P_{0}}(\psi)
and
D_{\Theta,P_{0}}(\psi)
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{P_{0}}(\psi,\lambda)

\displaystyle=\int\left\{-\psi_{0}(x,w)\log\psi(x,w)-(1-\psi_{0}(x,w))\log(1-\psi(x,w))\right\}

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt\hskip 56.9055pt\times(1+\lambda C_{0}(x))\ dP_{0}(x,w)\ ,

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{L}}_{P_{0}}(\psi,\lambda)

\displaystyle=\left\{\frac{1-\psi_{0}(x,w)}{1-\psi(x,w)}-\frac{\psi_{0}(x,w)}{\psi(x,w)}\right\}(1+\lambda C_{0}(x))\ ,

\displaystyle\ddot{\mathcal{L}}_{P_{0}}(\psi,\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\psi_{0}(x,w)}{(1-\psi(x,w))^{2}}+\frac{\psi_{0}(x,w)}{\psi(x,w)^{2}}\ .

For any given
\psi=(\psi^{1},\psi^{0})\in{\bf\Psi}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, the criterion (
27
) would always be satisfied. This concludes that
\psi_{0,\lambda_{0}}
is the optimal minimizer of the penalized cross-entropy risk.

Appendix H
Fairness constraint: general form

H.1
General form of specific examples

For the average treatment effect, we have
Z=(X,W)
, and

\displaystyle\Theta_{P}(\psi)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\displaystyle\frac{2X-1}{\pi_{0}(X\mid W)}\psi(X,W)\right]\ ,\ \kappa_{0}(Z)=\frac{2X-1}{\pi_{0}(X\mid W)}\ ,\ \mbox{and}\ \zeta_{0}(\psi)=\psi\ .

For the natural direct effect, we have
Z=(X,M,W)
, and

\displaystyle\Theta_{P}(\psi)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\displaystyle\frac{2X-1}{\pi_{0}(X\mid W)}\frac{\gamma_{0}(M\mid 0,W)}{\gamma_{0}(M\mid X,W)}\psi(X,M,W)\ \right]\ ,

\displaystyle\kappa_{0}(Z)=\frac{2X-1}{\pi_{0}(X\mid W)}\frac{\gamma_{0}(M\mid 0,W)}{\gamma_{0}(M\mid X,W)}\ ,\ \mbox{and}\ \zeta_{0}(\psi)=\psi\ .

For the equalized risk in the cases, we have
Z=(X,W)
and

\displaystyle\Theta_{P}(\psi)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[-\frac{(2X-1)\psi_{0}(X,W)}{p_{0,1}(X)}\log\{\psi(X,W)\}\right]\ ,

\displaystyle\kappa_{0}(Z)=-\frac{(2X-1)\psi_{0}(X,W)}{p_{0,1}(X)}\ ,\ \mbox{and}\ \zeta_{0}(\psi)=\log(\psi)\ .

For equalized mean-squared error, we have
Z=(X,W)
and

\displaystyle\Theta_{P}(\psi)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[-\frac{2X-1}{p_{0}(X)}\left[\sigma^{2}_{0}(X,W)+\{\psi_{0}(X,W)-\psi(X,W)\}^{2}\right]\right]\ ,

\displaystyle\kappa_{0}(Z)=-\frac{2X-1}{p_{0}(X)}\ ,\ \mbox{and}\ \zeta_{0}(\psi)=\sigma_{0}^{2}+\{\psi_{0}-\psi\}^{2}\ ,

where
\sigma^{2}_{0}(X,W)
is the conditional variance of
Y
given
X
and
W
.

For equalized cross-entropy, we have
Z=(X,W)
and

\displaystyle\Theta_{P}(\psi)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\frac{1-2X}{p_{0}(X)}\left[\psi_{0}(X,W)\log\psi(X,W)+\{1-\psi_{0}(X,W)\}\log\{1-\psi(X,W)\}\right]\right]\ ,

\displaystyle\hskip 46.94687pt\kappa_{0}(Z)=-\frac{2X-1}{p_{0}(X)}\ ,\ \mbox{and}\ \zeta_{0}(\psi)=\psi_{0}\log\psi+(1-\psi_{0})\log(1-\psi)\ .

H.2
Observations on the general form

We note that for many causal inference-related parameters, we might expect that
\zeta_{P}(\psi)=\psi
due to the fact that the identifying parameter for many causal effect parameters can be written in an inverse probabilitiy weighted form. This is advantageous for our approach since in this case the gradient of the constraint is simply
\kappa_{0}
, which implies that closed-form solutions will be readily available under both mean squared-error and cross-entropy.

Another observation on this general form helps explain the non-uniqueness of the solution for the equalized risk and anticipate other settings where this non-uniqueness may arise. In particular, we recall that in the equalized risk examples, we have that
\dot{\zeta}(\psi)=D_{R,P_{0}}(\psi)
. More generally, we can imagine a situation where
\dot{\zeta}(\psi)=aD_{R,P_{0}}(\psi)
for some real-valued constant
a
. In this case, equation (
7
) implies that
D_{R,P_{0}}(\psi)(1+\lambda a\kappa_{0})=0
. This equation can be trivially solved for all
\lambda
by letting
\psi=\psi_{0}
, since
\psi_{0}
is a minimizer of the unconstrained risk
R_{P_{0}}
, which in turn implies that
D_{R,P_{0}}(\psi_{0})=0
. Thus, deriving a valid solution to the Lagrangian system of equations necessarily requires explicit consideration for the trivial case that
\psi_{0}
satisfies
\Theta_{P_{0}}(\psi_{0})=0
. In our other specific examples, solving (
7
) yielded a closed for solution for
\psi_{0,\lambda}
that was equal to
\psi_{0}
only if
\lambda=0
.

The lack of uniqueness in the equalized risk setting can also be explained by the fact that if
\dot{\zeta}(\psi)=aD_{R,P_{0}}(\psi)
, then the gradients of the constraint and the risk are proportional. Thus, we can infer that the risk and the constraint have the same level sets, since gradients are orthogonal to the level set. The implication is that
any

\psi
that satisfies the constraint will necessarily have locally minimal risk in
\bm{\Psi}
. This provides a geometric view of the reasoning behind the existence of multiple solutions in this problem.

I.1
Results under misspecification of functional parameters

While efficient estimators of
\Theta_{P_{0}}(\psi_{0})
generally enjoy double-robustness properties, we recall that our estimator
\psi_{n,\lambda_{n}}
of
\psi_{0,\lambda_{0}}
does not. Thus, we repeated the ATE and NDE simulations to explore the impact of misspecification of the model for one of the functional parameters required on our procedure. To this end, we added non-linear and interaction terms to the regression formulas used to generate the mediator
M
and/or the outcome
Y
. However, in the estimation stage, these non-linear terms and interactions were not properly modeled and instead models with only main terms were used.

Specifically, we used the same joint distribution of
W
as described in the main body. In our “base scenario”, we generated
X
from a conditional Bernoulli distribution with
\pi_{0}(1\mid W)=\mbox{expit}(W_{1}-W_{3}/2+W_{6}/10)

(M,X,W)
was
-2X-M+2W_{1}-W_{3}-W_{4}+2W_{5}

\pi_{0}

\gamma_{0}

I.2
Data generating mechanism for high-dimensional simulation

For the simulation setting described in Section 6.3, we simulated
W
as a
p
-dimensional vector of standard Normal covariates for
p=10,50,100
. We set
\pi_{0}(1\mid W)=\mbox{expit}(W_{1}-W_{2}/2+W_{3}/3-W_{4}/4+W_{5}/5)
,
\gamma_{0}(1\mid X,W)=\mbox{expit}(-X+W_{1}-W_{2}/2+W_{3}/3-4*W_{4}/4+W_{5}/5)
. The outcome
Y
was generated from a conditional Normal distribution with mean given
(X,M,W)
of
X+M+W_{1}-W_{2}/2+W_{3}/3-W_{4}/4+W_{5}/5
and variance equal to 4. In this setting, the true value of the ATE is about 0.813 and the true value of the NDE is 1.

Each relevant functional parameter was estimated using LASSO where 10-fold cross-validation was used to select the LASSO penalization parameter that minimized mean squared error (the default behavior in standard LASSO software packages).

I.3
Details for highly adaptive LASSO simulation

In this simulation, we simulated a univariate covariate
W
from a Unif(
-\pi,\pi
) distribution. We set
\pi_{0}(1\mid W)=\mbox{expit}\{\mbox{sin}(\beta W)\}
. We considered only the ATE constraint and so did not simulate a mediator. The outcome
Y
was generated from a conditional Normal distribution with mean given
(X,W)
given by
X+X\mbox{sin}(\beta W)+(1-X)\mbox{cos}(\beta W)
and unit variance. Note that
\beta
controls the variation norm of
\pi_{0}(1\mid W)
and of
\psi_{0}
, with larger values of
\beta
implying larger variation in the underlying function. We ran simulations setting
\beta=1,10
to compare performance under low and high variation norms respectively.
