On Non- and Weakly-Informative Priors for the Conway-Maxwell-Poisson (COM-Poisson) Distribution
Mark J. Meyer, Amia Graye, Kimberly F. Sellers

Abstract
Previous Bayesian evaluations of the Conway-Maxwell-Poisson (COM-Poisson) distribution have little discussion of non- and weakly-informative priors for the model. While only considering priors with such limited information restricts potential analyses, these priors serve an important first step in the modeling process and are useful when performing sensitivity analyses. We develop and derive several weakly- and non-informative priors using both the established conjugate prior and Jeffreys’ prior. Our evaluation of each prior involves an empirical study under varying dispersion types and sample sizes. In general, we find the weakly informative priors tend to perform better than the non-informative priors. We also consider several data examples for illustration and provide code for implementation of each resulting posterior.

Introduction

The Conway-Maxwell-Poisson (COM-Poisson) distribution is a two-parameter flexible model derived by
Conway and Maxwell 1962
that can describe the variation of count data. While several parametrizations of the COM-Poisson distribution have been proposed (see
Sellers 2023
for details), this work assumes the CMP parametrization as described below. Under the CMP parametrization, this distribution has a probability mass function of the form

\displaystyle P(X=x)=\frac{\lambda^{x}}{(x!)^{\nu}Z(\lambda,\nu)},\hskip 36.135ptx=0,1,2,\ldots,

(1)

for a random variable
X
, where
\lambda=E(X^{\nu})>0
is a generalized form of the Poisson rate parameter,
\nu\geq 0
is a dispersion parameter, and
Z(\lambda,\nu)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^{j}}{(j!)^{\nu}}
is the normalizing function ensuring that basic probability axioms hold. The
dispersion parameter
\nu\geq 0
is such that
\nu=1
denotes equi-dispersion, and
\nu>(<)1
signifies under-dispersion (over-dispersion) relative to the Poisson model. Given a sample of observations
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
from a CMP(
\lambda,\nu
) distribution, the likelihood and log-likelihood functions are

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}(\lambda,\nu;{\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\lambda^{x_{i}}}{(x_{i}!)^{\nu}Z(\lambda,\nu)}=\frac{\lambda^{\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}}}{[Z(\lambda,\nu)]^{n}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}!\right)^{\nu}}

(2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda^{\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}}e^{-\nu\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ln(x_{i}!)}\left[Z(\lambda,\nu)\right]^{-n},\text{ and}

\displaystyle\ell(\lambda,\nu;{\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\ln\lambda-\nu\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ln(x_{i}!)-n\ln Z(\lambda,\nu)

(3)

where
{\mathcal{L}}(\cdot)
and
\ell(\cdot)
respectively denote the likelihood and log-likelihood. The CMP distribution is a flexible model that contains three well-known distributions. Two special case distributions are the Poisson distribution with rate parameter
\lambda

Some of the regression-based work implements weakly-informative priors for parameters in the systematic component using log-normal priors with large variances to induce flatter priors with some mass accumulating near zero. To our knowledge, however, no previous work examines the operating characteristics for non- or weakly-informative joints priors on the base CMP model whose likelihood is described in Equation (
2
).

Non-informative priors can take on several forms. Priors that are flat and thereby impart uniform mass over the support, for example, can be considered non-informative. Priors that impart little additional information about the data can also be considered non-informative. The search for truly non-informative priors may, as some have argued, be misguided; most practitioners are not modeling in complete ignorance, and so weakly-informative priors are usually viewed as a better starting point
(
Gelman et al. 2013
)
. Weakly-informative priors impart some additional, but limited information. This could take the form of a distribution with small, centralized mass or simply a truncated distribution, restricting the parameter’s support to a more plausible range. Both non- and weakly-informative priors serve an important role in the modeling process through sensitivity analyses. Thus, it is important to consider such priors for a range of probability models.

2.3

5

2
CMP Prior Specification

Kadane et al. 2006
observe that the COM-Poisson distribution is a member of the exponential family, hence it is guaranteed to have a conjugate prior. The form of that prior is

\displaystyle\pi(\lambda,\nu)\propto\lambda^{a-1}e^{-\nu b}\left[Z(\lambda,\nu)\right]^{-c},

(4)

which clearly has the same functional form of the CMP likelihood presented in Equation (
2
). When normalized, the prior is a proper density for
a,b,c>0
, under the condition that

\displaystyle\frac{b}{c}>\ln\left(\left\lfloor\frac{a}{c}\right\rfloor!\right)+\left(\frac{a}{c}-\left\lfloor\frac{a}{c}\right\rfloor\right)\ln\left(\left\lfloor\frac{a}{c}\right\rfloor+1\right),

(5)

where
\lfloor\cdot\rfloor
denotes the floor function
(
Kadane et al. 2006
)
. Conjugate priors are a natural starting place for generating informative priors via the interpretation of the prior parameters as “additional data.” This prior results in a posterior of the form

\displaystyle p(\lambda,\nu|x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\propto\lambda^{a+\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}-1}e^{-\nu\left[b+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ln(x_{i}!)\right]}\left[Z(\lambda,\nu)\right]^{-(c+n)},

which is clearly of the same class of densities as
\pi(\lambda,\nu)
, and therefore
\pi(\lambda,\nu)
is conjugate. A close examination of this posterior reveals how to interpret the prior parameters as additional data. We observe that the prior parameter
a
can be interpreted as an additional data point
x^{\prime}
or additional sum of data points
x_{j}^{\prime}
for
j=1,\ldots,m
. The parameter
c
has a similarly straightforward interpretation as the additional number of data points used to construct the prior, i.e. the value
m
. The final parameter,
b
, is harder to interpret but is the additional sum of the (natural) log of the factorial of the additional data points
x_{j}^{\prime}
for
j=1,\ldots,m
.

A fully “additional data” based approach, whereby the prior terms are selected to represent observable data values, to selecting a weakly-informative prior has its limits with this prior. Suppose we wish to construct a prior based off of a single hypothetical subject to limit the imparted information on the prior sample size,
m
. Then
c=1
. If
c=1
, however, then for no value of
x^{\prime}
does Equation (
5
) hold since the condition reduces to
\ln(x^{\prime}!)>\ln(x^{\prime}!)
when substituting
x^{\prime}
for
a
,
\ln(x^{\prime})
for
b
. Still, the conjugate prior is a good starting point for selecting weakly informative priors and can provide insight on selecting a non-informative prior as well. The advantage of using the conjugate prior as a guide is that, if the parameters are selected accordingly, the prior will be proper. Below, we construct our priors working first from the conjugate prior to identify weakly-informative priors (Section
2.1
), including a prior with values that are realistic and not constructed, and moving increasingly non-informative (Section
2.2
) ending with a derivation of Jeffreys’ prior (Section
2.3
).

2.1
Weakly-informative Priors

When considering a weakly-informative prior based on Equation (
4
), a natural first choice is to let
a=b=c=1
; such a combination satisfies the constraint in Equation (
5
). Under the “additional data” interpretation,
a=1
implies an additional data point
x^{\prime}=1
and thus
n=1
. Such an interpretation subsequently deteriorates, however, because
x^{\prime}=1
implies that
b=\ln(x^{\prime}!)=0\neq 1
. Since the prior parameters are chosen by the analyst(s), we can note that setting
b=1
is akin to letting
\ln(x^{\prime}!)=1
under the “additional data” interpretation. Provided
n
,
\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}
, and
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ln(x_{i}!)
are not too small,
a=b=c=1
can be viewed as adding a limited amount of additional data and therefore a limited amount of information. The first graph in the bottom row of Figure
1
displays a heat map of this prior which peaks at the origin and dies off relatively quickly as
\lambda
and
\nu
increase.

Figure 1
:
Conjugate prior heat maps under increasing choices of
a,b,
and
c
.

One could consider setting
a,b,
and
c
to something arbitrarily small, e.g.
a=b=c=0.1
or
0.01
. These values will again satisfy the constraint in Equation (
5
). In fact, if
a=b=c
, the constraint becomes

\displaystyle 1>\log\left(\left\lfloor 1\right\rfloor\right)+\left(1-\left\lfloor 1\right\rfloor\right)\log\left(\left\lfloor 1\right\rfloor+1\right)=0,

which is always true. As before, setting
a,b,
and
c
to something small but greater than zero ensures the prior is proper while, ideally, limiting the amount of imparted information. The resulting prior, under either specification, amasses density for near
\lambda
near zero. This can be seen in the heat maps of the surfaces in the top row of Figure
1
. As
a,b,
and
c
near zero, the conjugate prior places increasingly larger amounts of weight on small values of
\lambda
. The priors, however, produce flatter surfaces as
\lambda
increases than in the
a=b=c=1
case.

Priors constructed in this fashion, where
a=b=c
, are just that: constructed. It is impossible to have a hypothetical subject satisfy both this constraint and the constraint in Equation (
5
). While it is not feasible to fully consider and discuss the numerous ways by which one can construct a weakly-informative yet realistic data-based prior, we offer one such prior for illustrative purposes. As noted above, one cannot add just one hypothetical subject and expect a proper prior. Thus, we consider adding two hypothetical subjects. To limit their contribution, our hypothetical vector of data is
\textbf{x}^{\prime}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}2&0\end{array}\right]
. Then,
x_{1}^{\prime}=2
and
x_{2}^{\prime}=0
. A conjugate prior based on this vector implies that
a=2
,
b=\log(2)
, and
c=2
. It is straightforward to confirm that these values satisfy the condition in Equation (
5
), thus the prior is proper. Provided the sample size
n
is not too small, these values should represent only limited additional data contributions to the likelihood. Note that
\textbf{x}^{\prime}
is the minimal amount of data we can base a prior on while ensuring it is proper: an
m
smaller than 2 results in an improper prior while setting
\textbf{x}^{\prime}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\end{array}\right]
returns a value of
b=0
, again giving an improper prior. Improper priors are not detrimental, however they do require additional care. Sections
2.2
and
2.3
consider two such priors.

2.2
Conjugate-based Non-informative Prior

We may also consider the behavior of
\pi(\lambda,\nu)
from Equation (
4
) in the limit as
a,b,
and
c
tend toward zero. This limiting process is mimicked in our weakly-informative prior when taking values of
a,b,
and
c
to be equal and set to something small. From Figure
1
, we observe graphically that, as
a,b,
and
c
get smaller, the density accumulates near
\lambda
close to zero but is relatively diffuse over the
\nu
dimension. Examining the limit will confirm what we see graphically. Beginning from the conjugate prior in Equation (
4
), we take the limit as
(a,b,c)\rightarrow(0,0,0)
:

\displaystyle\lim_{(a,b,c)\rightarrow(0,0,0)}\lambda^{a-1}e^{-\nu b}\left[Z(\lambda,\nu)\right]^{-c}=\lambda^{-1},

which is easily seen by observing the limit of the respective components. Noting the relationship between the COM-Poisson and the Poisson distributions,
\pi(\lambda,\nu)\propto\lambda^{-1}
is an intuitive noninformative prior since, under the Poisson case (i.e. when
\nu=1
), the Jeffreys’ prior on
\lambda
is the same; see Table
1
. The prior
\pi(\lambda,\nu)\propto\lambda^{-1}
is not proper but does give a flat joint prior on
\lambda,\nu
and is noninformative in the sense that it is a constant. We refer to this as the flat prior although its graph it similar to that of the priors with
a=b=c=0.1
and
a=b=c=0.01
.

Conjecture:

Using the flat prior with the CMP likelihood in Equation (
2
) results in a proper posterior density.

Proof:

Letting
S_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}
and
S_{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ln(x_{i}!)
, the posterior under the flat prior is

\displaystyle p(\lambda,\nu|x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\propto\lambda^{S_{1}-1}e^{-\nu S_{2}}\left[Z(\lambda,\nu)\right]^{-n}

which is recognizable as the kernel of the conjugate distribution. Integrating the unnormalized posterior, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\lambda}\int_{\nu}p(\lambda,\nu|x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})d\lambda d\nu=\int_{\lambda}\int_{\nu}\lambda^{S_{1}-1}e^{-\nu S_{2}}\left[Z(\lambda,\nu)\right]^{-n}d\lambda d\nu=\kappa(S_{1},S_{2},n),

where
\kappa(\cdot)
is the integrating constant for the conjugate distribution. This constant is finite when
S_{1},S_{2},
and
n
are all greater than zero and when
S_{1},S_{2},
and
n
satisfy the condition in Equation (
5
). The former will be true when at least one observed data point from the sample,
x_{i}
, is greater than one (i.e.
x_{i}>1
for at least one
i=1,\ldots,n
). The latter holds if

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ln(x_{i}!)>\ln\left(\left\lfloor\bar{x}\right\rfloor\right)+\left(\bar{x}-\left\lfloor\bar{x}\right\rfloor\right)\ln\left(\left\lfloor\bar{x}\right\rfloor+1\right).

Despite the prior being improper, we can obtain a proper posterior under the conditions described above making the flat prior suitable for use. The flat prior is non-informative in the sense that it is a constant across the support of the parameter space. That is, it does not accumulate mass at any one point. Other non-informative priors may exist. To obtain one more rigorously, we now derive Jeffreys’ prior for the CMP likelihood.

2.3
Jeffreys’ Prior

Jeffreys’ prior guarantees the non-informativeness of the prior but often results in improper priors
(
Jeffreys 1946
;
Gelman et al. 2013
)
. The Jeffreys’ prior associated with the general CMP likelihood likewise simplifies to the respective Jeffreys’ priors constructed in association with their corresponding CMP special cases discussed in Section
1
; Table
1
contains
the corresponding priors for each CMP special case. The special cases reduce the CMP likelihood to a univariate model but, when such cases cannot be assumed, the likelihood becomes bivariate with respect to the parameters. In the multivariate case, the prior is constructed using the information matrix. The CMP information matrix
\mathcal{I}(\lambda,\nu)
has a complicated form that depends on partial derivatives of the CMP normalizing constant
Z(\lambda,\nu)
, which is an infinite sum. We express
\mathcal{I}(\lambda,\nu)
as succinctly as possible with

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\lambda,\nu)=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{n}{\lambda^{2}}E(X)+n\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}\frac{E(X)}{\lambda}&\frac{n}{\lambda}\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu}E(X)\\
\frac{n}{\lambda}\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu}E(X)&n\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\nu^{2}}\ln\left\{Z(\lambda,\nu)\right\}\\
\end{array}\right],

where
E(X)=\lambda\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}\ln\left\{Z(\lambda,\nu)\right\}
.

The Jeffreys’ prior is proportional to the square root of the determinant of
\mathcal{I}(\lambda,\nu)
. Let
J(\lambda,\nu)
denote the Jeffreys’ prior for the general CMP likelihood. The prior is then

\displaystyle J(\lambda,\nu)

\displaystyle\propto\sqrt{\left[\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}E(X)+\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}\frac{E(X)}{\lambda}\right]\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\nu^{2}}\ln\left\{Z(\lambda,\nu)\right\}-\left[\frac{1}{\lambda}\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu}E(X)\right]^{2}}.

(6)

Given the CMP likelihood from Equation (
2
), the resulting posterior when using the Jeffreys’ prior is

\displaystyle P(\lambda,\nu|X)

\displaystyle\propto\frac{\lambda^{\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}}}{[Z(\lambda,\nu)]^{n}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}!\right)^{\nu}}

\displaystyle\times\sqrt{\left[\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}E(X)+\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}\frac{E(X)}{\lambda}\right]\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\nu^{2}}\ln\left\{Z(\lambda,\nu)\right\}-\left[\frac{1}{\lambda}\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu}E(X)\right]^{2}}.

(7)

Unlike the other priors we have discussed, the use of
J(\lambda,\nu)
leads to a posterior density that is not recognizable. The likelihood kernel resembles the conjugate prior, hence this posterior is proper under the conditions that (1)
\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}>1
, (2)
n>0
, and (3)
\nu>0
. We must further consider the prior itself; the posterior will be proper if (4)
J(\lambda,\nu)<\infty
, and (5)
\left[\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}E(X)+\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}\frac{E(X)}{\lambda}\right]\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\nu^{2}}\ln\left\{Z(\lambda,\nu)\right\}>\left[\frac{1}{\lambda}\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu}E(X)\right]^{2}
. Given the nature of the sums and derivatives in the
J(\lambda,\nu)
, the latter two conditions are difficult to evaluate. The prior in Equation (
6
) bares little resemblance to the priors in Table
1
. This is because the prior in Equation (
6
) is a joint prior and the support of
\lambda
under the special cases can depend upon the values of
\nu
; see Table
1
.

3
Implementation using
Stan

The conjugate prior density, while recognizable as such, is a non-standard density, as is the resulting posterior when using Jeffreys’ prior. Thus, regardless of prior specification, all resulting posterior densities are non-standard and require additional steps to obtain samples. The intractable nature of the likelihood makes sampling a challenge. As
Benson and Friel 2021
note, the posterior of the CMP regression model is actually doubly-intractable due to the likelihood’s normalizing constant and the fact that the posterior cannot be normalized. To handle this, previous authors consider a range of techniques.
Kadane et al. 2006
treat
\nu
as a nuisance parameter and describe steps to sample from the conditional distribution of
\lambda
given
\nu
under different scenarios, e.g. when
c>a
in the conjugate prior or when
\nu<1
. The sampler for the conditional distribution relies on a rejection algorithm with proposals for
\lambda
drawn from either an
F
distribution or a Gamma, depending on the setting. In the regression context,
Chanialidis et al. 2018
employ an exchange algorithm with two steps aimed at reducing the correlation between successive samples of the coefficients from both the mean and dispersion model.
Benson and Friel 2021
improve on the efficiency of the exchange algorithm described by
Chanialidis et al. 2018
using an enveloping algorithm akin to an adaptive rejection sampler
(
Gilks and Wild 2018
)
. For more details on exchange algorithms, see
Møller et al. 2006
. Others have employed WinBUGs
(
Lord and Guikema 2012
)
and the Metropolis-Hastings algorithm
(
Huang and Kim 2021
)
to obtain samples from CMP-based models.

With respect to the previous authors, our goal is not to develop a new computational technique for sampling from the CMP but to draw samples from the intractable posteriors we describe in Section
2
as simply as possible. To achieve this, we develop
Stan
code to generate posterior samples through the
R
-to-
Stan
interface
RStan
package
(
Stan Development Team 2023a
)
.
Stan
implements a number of algorithms for performing Bayesian inference including the No U-Turn Sampler
(
Hoffman and Gelman 2014
)
and Hamiltonian Monte Carlo
(
Neal 1996
)
. We leverage the ability to build user-defined posteriors in
Stan
to obtain samples from all of the models in Section
2
, including the Jeffreys’ prior-based model, using the No U-Turn Sampler.

4
Empirical Study

Our empirical evaluation of each prior considers three dispersion settings: equi-, over, and under-dispersion. For the equi-dispersed setting, we let the true value be
\lambda=4
and
\nu=1
. The over-dispersed setting has true values of
\lambda=3
and
\nu=0.5
while the under-dispersed setting sets
\lambda=3
and
\nu=2
. For each setting, we consider sample sizes of
n=25,75
, and 125. Per combination of dispersion and
n
, we generate 100 simulated datasets for evaluation to compare six posterior models. Four of the six models use a conjugate prior-based weakly informative prior:
a=b=c=1
,
a=2,b=\log(2),c=2
,
a=b=c=0.1
, and

5
Data Illustrations

To illustrate the use of the best performing prior (i.e. the weakly informative prior where
a=b=c=1
), we present analyses of four different datasets. These illustrations include two over-dispersed datasets and two under-dispersed datasets. The sample sizes range from small (32) to medium (117 and 173) to very large (57,459). They come from a range of substantive fields illustrating the applicability of both the CMP model in general and the use of a weakly-informative prior for an initial analysis. Table
4
displays the posterior estimates and 95% credible intervals for both parameters estimated from each data set. For each model, we draw 4,000 samples each from four separate chains, retaining the last 2,000 for a total of 8,000 retained posterior samples. We monitor convergence across the four chains using the potential scale reduction factor,
\hat{R}

(
Gelman and Rubin 1992
)
. Based on the results of our empirical study in Section
4
, we present the analysis of each data set using the weakly-informative prior with
a=b=c=1
. Using this prior resulted in the most consistent coverage probabilities across all dispersion and sample size settings. All datasets and the scripts used to fit each model are available online at
https://github.com/markjmeyer/CMP
.

5.1
Textile Faults

The smallest dataset concerns the number of faults (i.e. breaks) observed during the manufacturing of rolls of fabric. This dataset appears in
Hinde 1982
as well as in
Gelman et al. 2004
. In total, 32 rolls of fabric were assessed with the number of faults per roll ranging from one to 28. The posterior median for the dispersion parameter
\nu

0.239
[95% CrI
(0.095,0.417)

\lambda
is
1.580
[95% CrI
(1.122,2.381)

\hat{R}
for the posteriors of
\lambda
and
\nu
were 1.003, suggesting convergence of the posterior samples.

5.2
Crab Satellites

Agresti 2013
describes a study of the mating patterns of horseshoe crabs on an island in the Gulf of Mexico, originally published by
Brockmann 1996
. Horseshoe crabs mate in pairs with the female crab arriving on shore with the male attached. Unattached males may group around a mating pair in clusters known as satellites and attempt to fertilize the eggs as well. The study examined different characteristics that contribute to the number of satellites near a female horseshoe crab’s nest, including the female crab’s color, spine condition, weight, and carapace width. In total, there are 173 female crabs available for study. Our analysis focuses simply on the count of satellites. The number of satellites per female crab ranges from zero to 15. The estimated value of dispersion parameter
\nu
is
0.032
[95% CrI
(0.001,0.107)
] which suggests overdispersion; the estimate of
\lambda
is
0.783
[95% CrI
(0.726,0.886)
]. The chains were judged to have converged with
\hat{R}
values of 1.006 for both the posteriors of
\lambda
and
\nu

6
Discussion

Non-informative priors serve an important role in the modeling process as one of several models researchers can consider in conjunction with weakly- and even fully-informative priors. Only working with non-informative priors or striving to find the perfect non-informative prior is not a good modeling strategy. Priors that appear non-informative under one parameterization may be highly informative under other parameterizations
Gelman et al. 2013
. The purpose of this work is to identify and evaluate several weakly- and non-informative priors so that interested researchers can use them as a first step in their Bayesian CMP models. Additional priors, with increasing amounts of information, would need to be considered to round out any analysis but the priors we examine here provide a good starting point.

Previous Bayesian evaluations of the CMP have not examined the weakly- and non-informative cases for the base model.
Kadane et al. 2006
provide a roadmap via their conjugate prior for considering an informative prior and we use this prior to elicit both non- and weakly-informative priors. We find that the weakly informative priors as well as the flat prior derived from the conjugate prior all perform similarly in terms of MSE in our empirical study. When the sample size is modest to large, all of these priors attain nominal or close-to-nominal coverage. However, for smaller sample sizes, the prior with
a=b=c=1
performs the best. In our data illustrations, this prior results in posteriors that are well-behaved and generates results that are consistent with previous analyses, particularly in terms of the dispersion parameter
\nu
. We recommend this prior as a first choice when considering weakly- and non-informative priors for the CMP.

The Jeffreys’ prior
(
Jeffreys 1946
;
Gelman et al. 2013
)
can also be used to find priors that are non-informative. In the multidimensional case in general, the Jefferys’ prior is less straightforward to construct and implement. This is the case for the CMP setting as can be seen from Equations (
6
) and (
7
). In fact, in simulation, this model regularly results in divergent chains in the sampler; see the Supplementary Material for more details. It is difficult to ensure that the resulting posterior is proper, particularly for the under- and equi-dispersed cases. While the Jeffreys’ prior does perform well in the over-dispersed setting, we caution against the use of this prior for the CMP.
