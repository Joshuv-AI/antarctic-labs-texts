DENSITY OF p p -ADIC POLYNOMIALS GENERATING EXTENSIONS WITH FIXED SPLITTING TYPE
John Yin 1

Abstract
. We prove that the density of polynomials P ⁡ ( x ) = ∑ i = 0 n a n ​ x n P(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{n}x^{n} over a local field K K generating an étale extension with specified splitting type is a rational function in terms of the size of the residue field of K K in the case where the splitting type is tame. Moreover, we give a computable recursive formula for these densities and compute the asymptotics of this density as the size of the residue field tends to infinity.



1.
Introduction

In
[
1
]
, the authors conjectured that the probability that a random
p
-adic polynomial
P(x)=c_{n}x^{n}+c_{n-1}x^{n-1}\dots+c_{0}
with coefficients in
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
generates an étale extension such that
p
splits in a specified way (e.g.
{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}[x]/f
is a totally ramified extension, unramified extension, a product of two extensions with specified residue degrees) is a rational function
\rho
of
p
, and moreover satisfies the functional equation
\rho(p)=\rho(1/p)
. We will prove the rationality of
\rho
in the tamely ramified case. As we shall see, our methods are more general and we will prove rationality of the density of polynomials satisfying many other conditions. Moreover, we will give a recursive method to calculate these densities.

We parametrize polynomials
P(x)
of degree at most
n
as above by
\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{n+1}
with the Haar measure normalized so that the total measure of
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}^{n+1}
is
1
. Now given an étale extension
L
, it is isomorphic to a product of field extensions
L_{i}
, so that
L=\prod_{i=1}^{m}L_{i}
. We define the splitting type of
L
to be the multiset of pairs

\sigma=\{(e(L_{1}),f(L_{1})),(e(L_{2}),f(L_{2})),\dots,(e(L_{n}),f(L_{n}))\}.

This is written as
(f(L_{1})^{e(L_{1})},\dots,f(L_{m})^{e(L_{m})})
in
[
1
]
, and we will follow their notation. Fixing such a splitting type
\sigma
, let
U_{\sigma}(p)\subset\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{n+1}
be the
p
-adic open subset of polynomials with splitting type
\sigma
and
\rho(n,\sigma;p)=\mu_{\mathrm{Haar}}(U_{\sigma}(p))
. As a few sample examples,
[
1
]
computes

(1.1)

\displaystyle\rho(2,(1^{1}1^{1});p)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\rho(2,(2^{1});p)

\displaystyle=\frac{p^{2}-p+1}{2(p^{2}+p+1)}

(1.2)

\displaystyle\rho(3,(1^{1}1^{1}1^{1});p)

\displaystyle=\frac{p^{4}+2p^{2}+1}{6(p^{4}+p^{3}+p^{2}+p+1)}

\displaystyle\rho(3,(1^{1}2^{1});p)

\displaystyle=\frac{p^{4}+1}{2(p^{4}+p^{3}+p^{2}+p+1)}.

Similarly, let
\alpha(n,\sigma;p)
and
\beta(n,\sigma;p)
be defined to be the measure of
\{P\in U_{\sigma(p)}:P\text{ is monic}\}
and
\{P\in U_{\sigma(p)}:P\text{ is monic and }P\equiv x^{n}\mod p\}
, respectively. Based on numerical evidence and some of their results,
[
1
, Conjecture 1.2]
states the following conjecture.

Conjecture 1.1
.

The densities
\rho(n,\sigma;p)
,
\alpha(n,\sigma;p)
, and
\beta(n,\sigma;p)
are rational functions in
p
and satisfy

\rho(n,\sigma;p^{-1})=\rho(n,\sigma;p).

\alpha(n,\sigma;p^{-1})=\beta(n,\sigma;p).

In this paper, we will prove the rationality part of this conjecture for tame extensions (
3.1
).

1.1.
Overview of proof

One main ingredient is
3.4
, proven in
[
3
]
, which makes use of the
p
-adic Kac-Rice formula to compute the average number of roots of a random polynomial in a fixed open subset of
{\mathcal{O}}_{L}
for a given étale extension
L
. To use this formula, it turns out one needs to calculate the density of elements
\alpha\in{\mathcal{O}}_{L}
whose minimal polynomial
P_{\alpha/{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}}
over
{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}
has a specified valuation of polynomial discriminant
\Delta_{P_{\alpha/{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}}}
. That is, we need to calculate the density
\mu_{L}(\{\alpha\in{\mathcal{O}}_{L}:v_{p}(\Delta_{P_{\alpha/{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}}})=c\})
for every
c
, where
\mu_{L}
is the normalized Haar measure on
{\mathcal{O}}_{L}
. Calculating this density and summing over the étale extensions
L
is the main technical part of this paper, which we will explain below. With this we obtain the average number of roots of a random polynomial in extensions with prescribed splitting behavior. Finally, a couple of transformations are done in Sections
3
and
6
to get from this to the density of random polynomials generating an extension with prescribed splitting behavior, which concludes the proof.

As an example of the process involved in the calculation of
\mu_{L}(\{\alpha\in{\mathcal{O}}_{L}:v_{p}(\Delta_{P_{\alpha/{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}}})=c\})
, we fix a square root
p^{1/2}
of
p
and take
L
to be
{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}[\zeta_{p^{3}-1},p^{1/2}]
, which is an extension of
{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}
of ramification index
2
and inertia degree
3
. Also let
{\mathfrak{m}}_{L}
be the maximal ideal in
{\mathcal{O}}_{L}
. Elements of
\alpha\in{\mathcal{O}}_{L}
have a Teichmüller expansion of the form
\alpha=\sum a_{i}p^{i/2}
, where
a_{i}
is either a power of a
(p^{3}-1)
-th root of unity or
0
. The
6
Galois conjugates of
\alpha
are of the form
\sum a_{i}^{p^{j}}(-1)^{ib}p^{i/2}
where
j\in\{0,1,2\}
and
b\in\{0,1\}
. With this expression of the Galois conjugates, one can recursively calculate the valuation of the discriminant of the minimal polynomial by using the definition
\Delta_{P_{\alpha/{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}}}=\prod_{\alpha^{\prime}\neq\alpha^{\prime\prime}\text{ Galois conjugates of }\alpha}(\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha^{\prime\prime})
. Here we use the convention that in the case our polynomial is linear, the product is empty and our discriminant is
1
. This recursion is somewhat combinatorially complicated, but we will work out a few steps in the recursion for this
L
to give the reader an idea of the process.

The context of the following paragraphs is that we wish to calculate the
p
-adic valuation of the product of pairwise differences between Galois conjugates of
\alpha
by analyzing its Teichmüller expansion
\alpha=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}a_{i}p^{i/2}
. We start out with considering the lowest degree coefficient
a_{0}
. There are two cases. If
a_{0}\in{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}
, which happens for exactly
p
choices of
a_{0}
, then the coefficient of
p^{0/2}
in the Teichmüller expansion of the Galois conjugates of
\alpha
must all be
a_{0}
, and hence for these elements,
v_{p}(P_{\alpha/{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}})=v_{p}(P_{\alpha-a_{0}})
, where
\alpha-a_{0}\in{\mathfrak{m}}_{L}
. So, we just found that

\mu_{L}(\{\alpha\in{\mathcal{O}}_{L}:a_{0}\in{\mathbb{Q}}_{p},v_{p}(\Delta_{P_{\alpha/{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}}})=c\})=p\mu_{L}(\{\alpha\in{\mathfrak{m}}_{L}:v_{p}(\Delta_{P_{\alpha/{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}}})=c\}).

We now do the same in the other case. If
a_{0}\notin{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}
, which happens for
p^{3}-p
choices of
a_{0}
, then the coefficients of
p^{0/2}
in the Teichmüller expansion of Galois conjugates of
\alpha
are precisely the Galois conjugates of
a_{0}
, of which there will be
3
. In fact, it turns out that the
6
Galois conjugates are neatly partitioned into
3
sets of
2
Galois conjugates, with each set containing the three Galois conjugates with a fixed
p^{0/2}
coefficient. Now,
v_{p}(a_{0}^{p^{j^{\prime}}}-a_{0}^{p^{j}})=0
for distinct
j,j^{\prime}\in\{0,1,2\}
since
\alpha
is a
p^{3}-1
-th root of unity. In other words, for Galois conjugates
\alpha^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime\prime}
in different partitions (i.e. with different
p^{0/2}
coefficients), we have
v_{p}(\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha^{\prime\prime})=0
. So, in the context of finding the valuation of the product of pairwise differences of Galois conjugates of
\alpha
, all that’s left is to calculate the valuation of the product of
\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha^{\prime\prime}
, as
\alpha^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime\prime}
vary over pairs of Galois conjugates of
\alpha
with the same
p^{0/2}
-th coefficient. There are
3
such pairs, one coming from each element of the partition above. Now, note that the Galois group acts transitively on the partitions themselves. In other words, the valuation of the difference between each pair are the same. So, it suffices to study one set in the partition, say the set
\{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\}
whose coefficient of
p^{0/2}
is
a_{0}
. Now we note that
\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}
are precisely the Galois conjugates of
\alpha
over
{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}[a_{0}]
. Moreover,
\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}=(\alpha-a_{0})-(\alpha^{\prime}-a_{0})
, and
\alpha-a_{0}
lies in
{\mathfrak{m}}_{L}
. Thus, also we obtain

\mu_{L}(\{\alpha\in{\mathcal{O}}_{L}:a_{0}\notin{\mathbb{Q}}_{p},v_{p}(\Delta_{P_{\alpha/{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}}})=c\})=(p^{3}-p)\mu_{L}(\{\alpha\in{\mathfrak{m}}_{L}:v_{p}(\Delta_{P_{\alpha/{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}[a_{0}]}})=c\}).

The above two paragraphs conclude the first step of the recursion. The combination of the two cases above reduced the calculation of
\mu_{L}(\{\alpha\in{\mathcal{O}}_{L}:v_{p}(\Delta_{P_{\alpha/{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}}})=c\})
to the calculation of multiple densities of the form
\mu_{L}(\{\alpha\in{\mathfrak{m}}_{L}:v_{p}(\Delta_{P_{\alpha/K}})=c^{\prime}\})
, for some extension
K
of
{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}
and integer
c^{\prime}\leq c
. As we shall see, this pattern continues. Although in the above cases,
c^{\prime}
happened to equal
c
, we do one more step in the recursion to see that
c^{\prime}
can be less than
c
. We continue from the first case, where we ended up wanting to compute
\mu_{L}(\{\alpha\in{\mathfrak{m}}_{L}:v_{p}(\Delta_{P_{\alpha/{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}}})=c\})
. Elements in
\alpha\in{\mathfrak{m}}_{L}
have Teichmüller expansion of the form
\sum_{i\geq 1}a_{i}p^{i/2}
. We again focus on the lowest degree term
a_{1}p^{1/2}
. Now there are three cases. The first case is if
a_{1}=0
, then we reduce to the calculation of
\{\alpha\in{\mathfrak{m}}_{L}^{2}:a_{1}=0,v_{p}(\Delta_{P_{\alpha/{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}}})=c\}
. The second case is if
a_{1}\in{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}\setminus 0
, which occurs for a proportion of
p-1
of all elements in
{\mathfrak{m}}_{L}
, then the Galois conjugates are separated into
2
partitions, each with
3
conjugates with a common leading term in the Teichmüller expansion
\sum_{i\geq 1}a_{i}p^{i/2}
is
a_{1}p^{1/2}
or
-a_{1}p^{1/2}
. Now for conjugates
\alpha^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime\prime}
belonging to two different partitions,
v_{p}(\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha^{\prime\prime})=v_{p}(2a_{1}p^{1/2})=1/2
, assuming
p\neq 2
. This is one example of where the tameness assumption comes into play. There are
2\cdot 3\cdot 3=18
such ordered pairs, giving a contribution of
18/2
to the
p
-adic valuation of the discriminant. Just as we did in the previous paragraph, we can now focus on product of pairwise differences of Galois conjugates
\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}
in one partition, say the one with leading term
a_{1}p^{1/2}
. Moreover, for Galois conjugates in this partition,
\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}=(\alpha-a_{1}p^{1/2})-(\alpha^{\prime}-a_{1}p^{1/2})
, and
\alpha-a_{1}p^{1/2}
lies in
{\mathfrak{m}}_{L}^{2}
. Now there are two partitions, with both of the product of the pairwise differences of elements in each partition giving the same contribution to the
p
-adic valuation of the discriminant. Thus, we have that

\mu_{L}(\{\alpha\in{\mathfrak{m}}_{L}:a_{1}\in{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}\setminus\{0\},v_{p}(\Delta_{P_{\alpha/{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}}})=c\})=(p-1)\mu_{L}(\{\alpha\in{\mathfrak{m}}_{L}^{2}:v_{p}(\Delta_{P_{\alpha/{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}[a_{1}p^{1/2}]}})=(c-9)/2\}).

Finally, we will not work out in full detail the third case, but one can show with basic theory of local fields that if
a_{1}\notin{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}
, then
a_{1}p^{1/2}
will have
6
conjugates, and in particular generate
L
itself. So, the pairwise difference of Galois conjugates of
\alpha
would all have
p
-adic valuation of
1/2
, and we would actually obtain

\mu_{L}(\{\alpha\in{\mathfrak{m}}_{L}:a_{1}\notin{\mathbb{Q}}_{p},v_{p}(\Delta_{P_{\alpha/{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}}})=c\})=(p^{3}-p)\mu_{L}(\{\alpha\in{\mathfrak{m}}_{L}^{2}:v_{p}(\Delta_{P_{\alpha/L}})=(c-15)/6\}).

This concludes another step of the recursion. Note that this point, we have reached a base case. Here we use the convention that the discriminant of a linear polynomial is
1
, and so
\mu_{L}(\{\alpha\in{\mathfrak{m}}_{L}^{2}:v_{p}(\Delta_{P_{\alpha/L}})=(c-15)/6\})
is
p^{-6}
if
(c-15)/6=0
, and
0
otherwise.

More generally, given an integer
i
, a local field
K
, and an integer
c
, and
L
an extension of
K
, our recursion reduces the calculation of
\mu_{L}(\{\alpha\in{\mathfrak{m}}_{L}^{i}:v_{p}(\Delta_{P_{\alpha/K}})=c\})
to
\mu_{L}(\{\alpha\in{\mathfrak{m}}_{L}^{i+1}:v_{p}(\Delta_{P_{\alpha/F}})=c^{\prime}\})
for some extension
F/K
contained in
L
and
c^{\prime}
an integer at most
c
. Now consider the poset

{\mathcal{P}}=\{(i,c,F):i\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0},c\in{\mathbb{Z}},K\subset F\subset L\}

with ordering given by
(i^{\prime},c^{\prime},F^{\prime})\leq(i,c,F)
if
i^{\prime}\geq i
,
c^{\prime}\leq c
, and
F^{\prime}\supset F
. Associating the set
\{\alpha\in{\mathfrak{m}}_{L}^{i}:v_{p}(\Delta_{P_{\alpha/F}})=c\}
to each element of
(i,c,F)
of
{\mathcal{P}}
, we see that the recursive step outlined above is simply the process of moving down on this partial order. Now, the only base cases we know is that we can find the measure of
\{\alpha\in{\mathfrak{m}}_{L}^{i}:v_{p}(\Delta_{P_{\alpha/F}})=c\}
when
F=L
, or when
c<0
since nothing in
{\mathcal{O}}_{L}
can have discriminant with negative valuation. Let
BC=\{(i,c,F):F=L\text{ or }c<0\}\subset{\mathcal{P}}
represent these base cases. Now, if
{\mathcal{P}}
were bounded from below by
BC
, then this recursive step would be enough for us to calculate all
\mu_{L}(\{\alpha\in{\mathfrak{m}}_{L}^{i}:v_{p}(\Delta_{P_{\alpha/K}})=c\})
from our base cases, but it’s not. Indeed, as we saw from the above paragraph, in the first case where
a_{1}=0
, we merely reduced calculating
\mu_{L}(\{\alpha\in{\mathfrak{m}}_{L}:v_{p}(\Delta_{P_{\alpha/{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}}})=c\})
to calculating
\mu_{L}(\{\alpha\in{\mathfrak{m}}_{L}^{2}:v_{p}(\Delta_{P_{\alpha/{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}}})=c\})
. Actually, our recursive step will just keep pushing this along, and move from
(i,c,{\mathbb{Q}}_{p})
to
(i+1,c,{\mathbb{Q}}_{p})
to
(i+2,c,{\mathbb{Q}}_{p})
, and so on. So, we need a further input to close off the recursion. The trick here is simply to notice that

(1.3)

\mu_{L}(\{\alpha\in{\mathfrak{m}}_{L}^{2}:v_{p}(\Delta_{P_{\alpha/{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}}})=c\})=p^{-6}\mu_{L}(\{\alpha\in{\mathfrak{m}}_{L}^{2}:v_{p}(\Delta_{P_{\alpha/{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}}})=c-30\})

where we have used the fact that dividing by
p
changes the
\mu_{L}
measure of a set by a factor of
p^{[L:{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}]}
, and also

\prod_{\alpha^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime\prime}\text{ conjugates of }\alpha}(\alpha^{\prime}/p-\alpha^{\prime\prime}/p)=p^{-30}\prod_{\alpha^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime\prime}\text{ conjugates of }\alpha}(\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha^{\prime\prime}).

In terms of
{\mathcal{P}}
, we are essentially establishing an equivalence relation
(i+e(L/F),c,F)\sim(i,c-\deg(L/F)(\deg(L/F)-1)i/e,{\mathbb{Q}}_{p})
. Now the set
{\mathcal{P}}/\sim
carries the induced partial order, which is bounded below by base cases
BC/\sim
.

In summary, calculating
\{\alpha\in{\mathfrak{m}}_{L}^{i}:v_{p}(\Delta_{P_{\alpha/K}})=c\}
is done with the following four steps.

(1)

Take a general
\alpha\in{\mathfrak{m}}_{L}^{i}
and consider the lowest term
a_{i}p^{i/e(L)}
in the Teichmüller expansion.

(2)

For each choice of
a_{i}
in the Teichmüller expansion, we get a partition of the Galois conjugates of
\alpha
, with each set in the partition containing Galois conjugates with the same leading term.

(3)

Using the partition, reduce the calculation of terms like
\mu(\{\alpha\in{\mathfrak{m}}_{L}^{i}:v_{p}(\Delta_{P_{\alpha/K}})=c\})
to the calculation of terms like
\mu(\{\alpha\in{\mathfrak{m}}_{L}^{i+1}:v_{p}(\Delta_{P_{\alpha/F}})=c^{\prime}\})

(4)

When
i
gets large use the trick similar to Equation
1.3
to reduce the calculation
\{\alpha\in{\mathfrak{m}}_{L}^{i}:v_{p}(\Delta_{P_{\alpha/K}})=c\}
to
\{\alpha\in{\mathfrak{m}}_{L}^{i-e}:v_{p}(\Delta_{P_{\alpha/K}})=c^{\prime}\}
where
c^{\prime}<c
when
i
gets large.

(5)

Repeat the above steps until we reach a base case.

The main idea and novelty of this paper is succinctly summarized above and there are little additional conceptual difficulties. Much of the paper is dedicated to the many technical and combinatorial details in these calculations, including the following. Most of the notational baggage comes from these technicalities.

•

We need to enumerate all extensions of a given splitting type. Fortunately, this is not terrible for tame extensions, but it still takes some bookkeeping.

•

There are extensions like
L={\mathbb{Q}}_{p}[\zeta_{p^{3}-1},(\zeta_{p^{3}-1}p)^{1/4}]
. So, Teichmüller expansions of elements in
{\mathcal{O}}_{L}
are of the form
\sum a_{i}(\zeta_{p^{3}-1}p)^{1/4})^{i}\in{\mathcal{O}}_{L}
. Now although we can still express the Galois conjugates of
\sum a_{i}(\zeta_{p^{3}-1}p)^{1/4})^{i}\in{\mathcal{O}}_{L}
, and find its Galois conjugates fairly easily, there are combinatorics involved in determining the partitions of Galois conjugates arising from a given
a_{i}
, and determining the number of
a_{i}
giving a specified partition.

•

L
can be a product of field extensions rather than a single field extension.

•

We need to sum over
all
extensions
L
with a given splitting type. We only considered one
L
above.

We remark that without summing over all extensions
L
with a given splitting type, we get only get “quasi-rationality” for
\rho
, where quasi-rationality means that as a function of
p
, it is rational in arithmetic progressions with a fixed modulus. Although we do not calculate it out in this paper, following through with the same methods outlined above, we would see that the proportion of polynomials in
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}[x]
of degree
9
generating
{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}[\zeta_{p^{3}-1},p^{1/3}]
is a quasi-rational function in terms of
p
, so that its restriction to

is a rational function, and its restriction to
p\equiv 1,2\mod 3
is another rational function.

Previous work

Densities of random polynomial with fixed splitting data

The study of statistics of roots of random polynomials dates back to the work of Bloch and Pölya who proved asymptotic bounds on the number of expected roots of polynomials with coefficients in
\{-1,0,1\}
. Then, Kac gives an exact formula for the expected number of roots of polynomials with Gaussian distributed coefficients. For a survey and related results, there is
[
4
]
and
[
10
]
.

The questions surrounding the expected number of roots of random polynomials over
p
-adic fields were studied in
[
7
]
,
[
2
]
, and
[
9
]
. Following, there is the paper
[
1
]
(the conjecture in which is precisely the one we attack in this paper), which shows that the density of
p
-adic polynomials with fixed number of roots is a rational function in
p
, and satisfies the functional equation sending
p
to
p^{-1}
. There is also the aforementioned paper
[
3
]
, from which we obtain a crucial formula for finding the average number of roots of a random polynomial in
{\mathcal{O}}_{L}
for a fixed extension
L
.

Notation/Convention

We adopt a similar notation as in
[
3
]
. We fix a local field
K
and an algebraic closure
\bar{K}
. We equip
\bar{K}
with the
p
-adic norm normalized by
||p||=p^{-[K:{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}]}
. Let
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
be the discrete valuation ring of
K
. Let
{\mathfrak{m}}_{K}
be the maximal ideal of
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
. Let
\lambda_{K}
be the associated Haar measure on
K
. Let
e_{\base}
be the ramification index of
K
, and
f_{\base}
be the inertia degree of
K
. Similarly, for an étale extension
L=\prod_{i}L_{i}
, where

are field extensions of

, we denote
{\mathcal{O}}_{L}
to be

and

to be

.

\Omega_{d}
be the space of polynomials of degree at most
d
with coefficients in
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
, which is set-theoretically identified with
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}^{d+1}
. With this identification, we have a probability measure
\mu_{r}
on

associated to
\lambda_{K}^{\otimes d+1}
on
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}^{d+1}
. When we refer to a random degree
d
polynomial, we mean it in this sense. Also, for an étale extension

, and a polynomial

, we call the finite set
\Hom(K[x]/f,E)
the roots of

in
E
. Furthermore, if
\varphi\in\Hom(K[x]/f,E)
is surjective, we say
\varphi
is “new” in
E
. Note that this definition makes sense even for
f
such that
\deg(f)>\dim_{K}E
, but we will only concern ourselves with the case where
\deg(f)=\dim_{K}E
.

Fix a positive integer
d
, and integers
e_{1},\dots,e_{m}
and
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}
such that
d=\sum_{i}e_{i}f_{i}
. Let
\sigma
be the multiset of pairs
\{(e_{1},f_{1}),\dots,(e_{m},f_{m})\}
, which we will write as
(e_{1}^{f_{1}},\dots,e_{m}^{f_{m}})
. We call such symbols splitting types. Let
L
be an étale extension of
K
. We say
\pi_{K}
has splitting type
\sigma
in
L
if
\pi_{K}
factors as
\prod_{i}\pi_{i}^{e_{i}}
in
{\mathcal{O}}_{L}
, where
\pi_{i}
are prime ideals of
{\mathcal{O}}_{L}
having residue degree
f_{i}
. For any field
L
, we define
e(L)
to be the ramification index of
L
over
{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}
, where
p
is the characteristic of the residue field of
L
, and
f(L)
to be the inertia degree of
L
. Similarly, we define
e(L/K)=e(L)/e(K)
for any extension
L/K
to be the relative ramification index of
L
over
K
, and similarly for
f(L/K)
. Finally, let
[m]
denote the set
\{1,\dots,m\}
.

Let
q
be the size of the residue field of
K
. Fix a field extension
L/K
of inertia degree
f
. Let
\pi_{L}
be a uniformizer of
L
. Then, we may write elements of
L
uniquely as
\sum_{i=-\infty}^{\infty}a_{i}\pi_{L}^{i}
, where
a_{i}\in L
is either a
q^{f}-1
-th root of unity or
0
, and there is some cutoff
r\in{\mathbb{Z}}
so that
a_{i}=0
for
i<r
. This representation of an element in
L
is called a Teichmüller expansion.

3.
Main Results

For a splitting type
\sigma=(e_{1}^{f_{1}},\dots,e_{m}^{f_{m}})
, let
\rho(\sigma,K)
denote the density of polynomials
P\in{\mathcal{O}}_{K}[x]
of degree
d=\sum_{i=1}^{m}e_{i}f_{i}
such that
P
generates an étale extension
L
over
K
and
\pi_{K}
has splitting type
\sigma
in
L
. Let
\alpha(\sigma,K)
(resp.
\beta(\sigma,K)
) be the density of such polynomials above with the added condition that
P
is monic (resp.
P
is monic and
P(x)\mod\pi_{K}=x^{n}
. Our main result is the following.

Theorem 3.1
.

Fix a splitting type
\sigma=(e_{1}^{f_{1}},\dots,e_{m}^{f_{m}})
.

(1)

If
K,K^{\prime}
are two local fields with residue characteristic
p
such that
\gcd(p,e_{i})=1
for all
i\in[m]
, such that
e(K)=e(K^{\prime})
and
f(K)=f(K^{\prime})
, then
\rho(\sigma,K)=\rho(\sigma,K^{\prime})
. In particular, we may replace the notation
\rho(\sigma,K)
with
\rho(\sigma,e,f;p)
when
\gcd(p,e_{i})=1
for all
i\in[m]
. Similarly,
\alpha(\sigma,K)
and
\beta(\sigma,K)
may be replaced with
\alpha(\sigma,e,f;p)
and
\beta(\sigma,e,f;p)
.

(2)

Fix
e
and
f
as well. Then, for primes
p
such that
\gcd(p,e_{i})=1
for all
i\in[m]
,
\rho(\sigma,e,f;p)
,
\alpha(\sigma,e,f;p)
, and
\beta(\sigma,e,f;p)
are rational functions of
q=p^{f}
.

We note that it is conjectured in
[
1
, Conjecture 1.2]
that part 2 of this theorem also holds for
p
not satisfying the gcd condition, but we will not prove that in this paper.

We also have an asymptotic for the large
q
limit of
\rho
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
q=p^{f}
. We have

\rho(\sigma,e,f;p)\sim\frac{1}{\perm(\sigma)\prod_{i=1}^{m}f_{i}q^{\sum_{i=1}^{m}(e_{i}-1)f_{i}}}

as
q\to\infty
.

More precisely, if one takes
p
to infinity,
f
to infinity, or both,

\lim_{p^{f}\to\infty}\rho(\sigma,e,f;p)\perm(\sigma)\prod_{i=1}^{m}f_{i}q^{\sum_{i=1}^{m}(e_{i}-1)f_{i}}

approaches
1
. Indeed, this agrees with the calculations
1.1
done in
[
1
]
.

We now launch into the preliminaries of the proof of these theorems. The goal for the rest of this section to transform the problem of finding
\rho(\sigma,K)
to the problem of finding measures of sets of the form
\mu_{L}(\{\alpha\in{\mathfrak{m}}_{L}^{i}:v_{p}(\Delta_{P_{\alpha/{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}}})=c\})
. Let
L/K
be an étale extension of degree
d
. For
U\subset L
an open subset, let
Z_{U,L}^{\new}:\Omega_{d}\to{\mathbb{Z}}
be a random variable sending
P
to the number of new roots of
P
in
U
. With this notation, we have that

Lemma 3.3
.

Fix a local field
K
and a splitting type
\sigma=(e_{1}^{f_{1}},\dots,e_{m}^{f_{m}})
. For
L=\prod_{i=1}^{m}L_{i}
, and
I\subset[m]
let
U_{L,I}=\prod_{i\in I}{\mathfrak{m}}_{L_{i}}\times\prod_{i\in[m]\setminus I}{\mathcal{O}}_{L_{i}}
. Then,

(1)

\rho(\sigma,K)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\textup{isom classes of }L,\\
\pi_{K}\textup{ has splitting type }\sigma\text{ in }L\end{subarray}}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{L,L}^{\new}]}{\Aut(L)}.

(2)

\alpha(\sigma,K)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\textup{isom classes of }L,\\
\pi_{K}\textup{ has splitting type }\sigma\text{ in }L\end{subarray}}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{{\mathcal{O}}_{L},L}^{\new}]}{\Aut(L)}.

(3)

\beta(\sigma,K)=\sum_{I\subsetneq[m]}(-1)^{|I|+1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\textup{isom classes of }L,\\
\pi_{K}\textup{ has splitting type }\sigma\text{ in }L\end{subarray}}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{U_{L,I},L}^{\new}]}{\Aut(L)}.

Proof.

We first partition degree
d
polynomials
P
by the isomorphism classes of étale extensions that they generate. Thus

\rho(\sigma,K)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{isom classes of }L,\\
\pi_{K}\text{ has splitting type }\sigma\text{ in }L\end{subarray}}\rho(L,K),

where
\rho(L,K)
is the proportion of degree
d
polynomials
P
over
K
such that
K[x]/P\cong L
. In other words, this is the proportion of polynomials with a new root in
L
. Now, for any such
P
, it has
\Aut(L)
roots in
L
. Thus,
\Aut(L)\rho(L,K)
is precisely the average number of new roots of a random degree
d
polynomial in
L
. Hence,

\Aut(L)\rho(L,K)={\mathbb{E}}[Z_{L,L}^{\new}].

The formula for
\alpha
follow after noting that monic polynomials are precisely the ones for which all roots lie in the ring of integers of the associated étale extension. Now, monic polynomials
P(x)
that is
x^{n}\mod\pi_{K}
are precisely the ones for which all roots lie in
{\mathcal{O}}_{L}
, with at least one root lies in
{\mathfrak{m}}_{L}
. Thus, we obtain the formula for
\beta
after applying inclusion exclusion.
∎

In the following theorem
[
3
, Theorem 4.8]
, Caruso gives a formula for
{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{U,L}]
, for open subsets
U\subset{\mathcal{O}}_{L}
. Let
w(d)=w_{f(K)}(d)=\frac{q^{d+1}-q^{d}}{q^{d+1}-1}
.

Theorem 3.4

(Theorem 4.5.5
[
3
]
)
.

Let
K
be a local field, and
L
be an étale extension of degree
d
. Let
U\subset{\mathcal{O}}_{L}
be any open set. Then, the average number of new roots of a random degree
d
polynomial over
K
in
L
is

{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{U,L}^{\new}]=||D_{L}||w(d)\int_{\alpha\in U}\frac{1}{[{\mathcal{O}}_{L}:{\mathcal{O}}_{K}[\alpha]]}d\lambda_{L}.

Thus, we have turned a statement about polynomials over
K
to a statement about elements in étale extensions of
K
. We can apply the above formula directly to calculate
{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{{\mathcal{O}}_{L},L}^{\new}]
and
{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{U_{L},L}^{\new}]
and thus obtain
\alpha
and
\beta
. However, since we require
U
to be a subset of
{\mathcal{O}}_{L}
in the above formula, we see that we cannot use this to calculate
{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{L,L}]
and obtain
\rho
directly. However, we will prove the following in Section
6
.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
K
be a local field and let
q=p^{f(K)}
. Let
L
be an étale extension of degree
d
. So,
L=\prod_{i=1}^{m}L_{i}
, where
L_{i}
are field extensions of
K
. For any
A\subset[m]
, denote
L_{A}=\prod_{i\in A}L_{i}
and
A^{c}=[m]\setminus A
. Then,

{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{L,L}^{\new}]=\sum_{A\subset[m]}\frac{w([L:K])}{w([L_{A}:K])w([L_{A^{c}}:K])}{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{{\mathcal{O}}_{L_{A}},L_{A}}^{\new}]{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{{\mathfrak{m}}_{L_{A^{c}}},L_{A^{c}}}^{\new}],

where we make the convention that if
A
is empty,
{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{{\mathcal{O}}_{L_{A}},L_{A}}^{\new}]={\mathbb{E}}[Z_{{\mathfrak{m}}_{L_{A}},L_{A}}^{\new}]=1
and
[L_{A}:K]=0
(so that
w([L_{A}:K])=1
).

We are concerned mainly with tamely ramified extensions. Thus, with Theorem
3.5
and the following characterization of tamely ramified extensions, we can transform our sum

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{isom classes of }L,\\
\pi_{K}\text{ has splitting type }\sigma\text{ in }L\end{subarray}}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{L,L}^{\new}]}{\Aut(L)},

which involves terms of the form
{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{L,L}^{\new}]
, to something that involves only terms of the form
{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{{\mathcal{O}}_{L},L}]
and
{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{{\mathfrak{m}}_{L},L}]
, which we should be able to calculate using Caruso’s formula (
3.4
). The theorem below is given in
[
8
, Chapter 16]
.

Theorem 3.6
.

Fix a uniformizer
\pi_{K}\in K
and a positive integer
n
coprime to
p
. Then, there are
n
tamely and totally ramified extensions of degree
n
of
K
in
\bar{K}
. These extensions may be split into
\gcd(q-1,n)
isomorphism classes, each class isomorphic to
K[(\zeta_{q-1}^{j}\pi_{K})^{1/n}]
, for some
0\leq j<\gcd(q-1,n)
.

Since every tamely ramified extension is an unramified extension followed by a tamely and totally ramified extension, we see that every tamely ramified extension
L
with ramification index
e_{0}
and inertia degree
f_{0}
over
K
is of the form
K[\zeta_{q^{f_{0}}-1},(\zeta_{q^{f_{0}}-1}^{j}\pi_{K})^{1/e_{0}}]
for some
0\leq j<\gcd(q^{f_{0})}-1,e_{0})
. We will now denote this field as
L_{j}
. Thus, extensions
L/K
with splitting type
\sigma
are of the form
\prod_{i}L_{j_{i}}
, where
0\leq j_{i}\leq\gcd(q^{f_{i}}-1,e_{i})
. This also shows that
|\Aut(L_{j_{i}}/K)|=f_{i}\gcd(q^{f_{i}}-1,e_{i})
.

Let

\perm(\sigma)=\prod_{e,f}(\text{occurrences of }e^{f}\text{ in }\sigma)!.

Now we are ready to transform our formula in terms of
{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{L,L}]
, to a formula in terms
{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{{\mathcal{O}}_{L},L}]
and
{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{{\mathfrak{m}}_{L},L}]
. With Lemma
3.3
, Theorem
3.5
, and Theorem
3.6
, we have

Corollary 3.7
.

Let
\sigma=(e_{1}^{f_{1}},\dots,e_{m}^{f_{m}})
be a splitting type with
p\nmid e_{i}
. Let
d=\sum e_{i}f_{i}
. Then,

\rho(\sigma,K)=\frac{w(d)\sum_{A\subset[m]}N_{1,A}^{\prime}N_{2,A}^{\prime}}{\perm(\sigma)}

where

N_{1,A}^{\prime}=\frac{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}L_{A}=\prod_{i\in A}L_{j_{i}}\\
j_{i}\in[\gcd(q^{f_{i}}-1,e_{i})]\end{subarray}}{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{{\mathcal{O}}_{L_{A}},L_{A}}^{\new}]}{\prod_{i\in A}f_{i}\gcd(q^{f_{i}}-1,e_{i})w([L_{A}:K])}

and

N_{2,A}^{\prime}=\frac{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}L_{A^{c}}=\prod_{i\notin A}L_{j_{i}}\\
j_{i}\in[\gcd(q^{f_{i}}-1,e_{i})]\end{subarray}}{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{{\mathfrak{m}}_{L_{A^{c}}},L_{A^{c}}}^{\new}]}{\prod_{i\notin A}f_{i}\gcd(q^{f_{i}}-1,e_{i})w([L_{A^{c}}:K])}.

Proof.

We first have by Lemma
3.3
that

\rho(\sigma,K)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{isom classes of }L,\\
\pi_{K}\text{ has splitting type }\sigma\text{ in }L\end{subarray}}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{L,L}^{\new}]}{\Aut(L)}.

Now as
j_{i}
ranges in
[\gcd(q^{f_{i}}-1),e_{i})]
,
L=\prod_{i}L_{j_{i}}
ranges among étale extensions of splitting type
\sigma
over
K
. However, this will hit the same extension
\perm(\sigma)\left|\frac{\Aut(L)}{\prod_{i}\Aut(L_{j_{i}})}\right|^{-1}
times. Thus,

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{isom classes of }L,\\
\pi_{K}\text{ has splitting type }\sigma\text{ in }L\end{subarray}}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{L,L}^{\new}]}{\Aut(L)}

\displaystyle=\perm(\sigma)^{-1}\prod_{i}|\Aut(L_{j_{i}})|^{-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}L=\prod_{i}L_{j_{i}}\\
j_{i}\in[\gcd(q^{f_{i}}-1,e_{i})]\end{subarray}}{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{L,L}^{\new}]

\displaystyle=\perm(\sigma)^{-1}\prod_{i}\frac{1}{f_{i}\gcd(q^{f_{i}}-1,e_{i})}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}L=\prod_{i}L_{j_{i}}\\
j_{i}\in[\gcd(q^{f_{i}}-1,e_{i})]\end{subarray}}{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{L,L}^{\new}]

Now, by Theorem
3.5
,

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}L=\prod_{i}L_{j_{i}}\\
j_{i}\in[\gcd(q^{f_{i}}-1,e_{i})]\end{subarray}}{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{L,L}^{\new}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}L=\prod_{i}L_{j_{i}}\\
j_{i}\in[\gcd(q^{f_{i}}-1,e_{i})]\end{subarray}}\sum_{A\subset[m]}\frac{w(d)}{w([L_{A}:K])w([L_{A^{c}}:K])}{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{{\mathcal{O}}_{L_{A}},L_{A}}^{\new}]{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{{\mathfrak{m}}_{L_{A^{c}}},L_{A^{c}}}^{\new}]

\displaystyle=w(d)\sum_{A\subset[m]}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}L=\prod_{i}L_{j_{i}}\\
j_{i}\in[\gcd(q^{f_{i}}-1,e_{i})]\end{subarray}}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{{\mathcal{O}}_{L_{A}},L_{A}}^{\new}]}{w([L_{A}:K])}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{{\mathfrak{m}}_{L_{A^{c}}},L_{A^{c}}}^{\new}]}{w([L_{A^{c}}:K])}

where
L_{A}=\prod_{i\in A}L_{j_{i}}
and
L_{A^{c}}=\prod_{i\in A^{c}}L_{j_{i}}
. Thus, factoring the inner sum into a product, we get

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}L=\prod_{i}L_{j_{i}}\\
j_{i}\in[\gcd(q^{f_{i}}-1,e_{i})]\end{subarray}}{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{L,L}^{\new}]=w(d)\sum_{A\subset[m]}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}L_{A}=\prod_{i\in A}L_{j_{i}}\\
j_{i}\in[\gcd(q^{f_{i}}-1,e_{i})]\end{subarray}}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{{\mathcal{O}}_{L_{A}},L_{A}}^{\new}]}{w([L_{A}:K])}\right)\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}L_{A^{c}}=\prod_{i\in A^{c}}L_{j_{i}}\\
j_{i}\in[\gcd(q^{f_{i}}-1,e_{i})]\end{subarray}}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{{\mathfrak{m}}_{L_{A^{c}}},L_{A^{c}}}^{\new}]}{w([L_{A^{c}}:K])}\right).

∎

Although the formula in Corollary

seems complicated, one just needs to note that since by definition

is a rational function of

, Theorem

\frac{1}{\prod_{i\in A}f_{i}\gcd(q^{f_{i}}-1,e_{i})}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}L_{A}=\prod_{i\in A}L_{j_{i}}\\

and

\frac{1}{\prod_{i\in A^{c}}f_{i}\gcd(q^{f_{i}}-1,e_{i})}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}L_{A^{c}}=\prod_{i\in A^{c}}L_{j_{i}}\\
j_{i}\in[\gcd(q^{f_{i}}-1,e_{i})]\end{subarray}}{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{{\mathfrak{m}}_{L_{A^{c}}},L_{A^{c}}}^{\new}]

are rational functions in terms of

, and that these expressions depends on

through

and

. Similarly, following the same process for

and

\prod_{i}\frac{1}{f_{i}\gcd(q^{f_{i}}-1,e_{i})}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}L=\prod_{i}L_{j_{i}}\\

and

(

defined as in Lemma

) are rational functions in terms of

, and that these expressions depends on

through

and

\frac{1}{\prod_{i=1}^{m}f_{i}\gcd(q^{f_{i}}-1,e_{i})}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{i}\in[\gcd(q^{f_{i}}-1,e_{i})]\end{subarray}}{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{\prod_{i=1}^{m}{\mathfrak{m}}_{L_{i}}^{b_{i}},\prod_{i}L_{j_{i}}}^{\new}]

and nonnegative integers

Note that combining Theorem

gives us the formula

(3.1)

\rho(\sigma,K)=\frac{w(d)\sum_{A\subset[m]}N_{1,A}N_{2,A}}{\perm(\sigma)q^{\sum_{i=1}^{m}(e_{i}-1)f_{i}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}f_{i}},

Finally, the integral in Caruso’s formula (

Lemma 3.8
.

Let

\Delta_{P}

\pi_{K}^{2\log_{q}[{\mathcal{O}}_{L}:{\mathcal{O}}_{K}[x]/P]}\Delta_{L}=\Delta_{P}

Thus, combining Theorem

\frac{q^{\frac{\sum(e_{i}-1)f_{i}}{2}}}{\prod_{i=1}^{m}\gcd(q^{f_{i}}-1,e_{i})}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}L=\prod_{i}L_{j_{i}}\\

4.
Generating Functions and Recurrence Relations

First, we will make a switch from relative notation to absolute notation. So, let
\{L_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
be field extensions of
K
. We define
e_{i},f_{i}
to be the
absolute
ramification index and inertia degree of

. Now, for nonnegative integers

, we define

a(\{L_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m},K,c,\{b_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}):=\mu(\{\alpha\in\prod_{i\in[m]}{\mathfrak{m}}_{L_{i}}^{b_{i}}:v_{p}(\Delta_{P_{\alpha/K}})=c\}).

Also, fix two positive integers
e_{\base}
,

, representing the ramification index and inertia degree of our base extension, which we will denote by
K
. Then, let
\sigma=(e_{1}^{f_{1}},\dots,e_{m}^{f_{m}})
be a tame splitting type, so that

for any

and

. Then, we define

a(\sigma,e_{\base},f_{\base},c,\{b_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m},p)=\frac{1}{\prod_{i\in[m]}\gcd(p^{f_{i}}-1,e_{i,\rel})}\sum_{j_{i}\in[\gcd(p^{f_{i}}-1,e_{i,\rel})]}a(\{L_{j_{i}}\},K,c,\{b_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m})

where

is any

-adic field with

and

. As the notation suggests, the value of this expression will be shown to only depend on

through

and

. We will often omit the dependence of

.

Example 4.1
.

As a base case, we can see that from the definition,

a((e^{f}),e,f,c,\{b\})=a(\{K\},K,c,\{b\})=\begin{cases}p^{-bf}&c=0\\
0&c\neq 0\end{cases},

for any
j
since the discriminants of all degree
1
polynomials are all
1=p^{0}
.

Our main proposition of this section, as well as the main technical part of our paper is

Proposition 4.2
.

Fix a tamely ramified splitting type
\sigma=(e_{1}^{f_{1}},\dots,e_{m}^{f_{m}})
. Then, the definition of

a(\sigma,e_{\base},f_{\base},c,\{b_{i}\})

doesn’t depend on the choice of
K
(as long as
e(K)=e_{\base}
and
f(K)=f_{\base}
). Moreover, we have the following recurrence.

a(\sigma,e_{\base},f_{\base},c,\{b_{i}\})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\textup{partitions }\{E_{l}\}_{l=1}^{k}\text{ of }[m]\\
\{n_{O,l}\}_{l=1}^{k}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 1}^{k}\end{subarray}}P(\sigma,\{E_{l}\},\{b_{i}\},\{n_{O,l}\},p)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{c_{l}\}_{l=1}^{k}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{k}\\
\sum n_{O,l}c_{l}=r(c)\end{subarray}}\prod_{l}a(\sigma_{l},e(K)h(\beta,n_{O,l}),f(K)\frac{n_{O,l}}{h(\beta,n_{O,l})},c_{l},\{b_{i}+{\mathbb{1}}_{I}(i)\}_{i\in E_{l}}).

Where
{\mathbb{1}}_{I}(i)
denotes the indicator function of
I
and
\sigma_{l}=(e_{i}^{f_{i}})_{i\in E_{l}}
.

The notation used in this proposition will be introduced shortly. The main idea of this proposition is that we are expressing
a(\sigma,e_{\base},f_{\base},c,\{b_{i}\})
in terms of

a(\sigma_{l},e(K)h(\beta,n_{O,l}),f(K)\frac{n_{O,l}}{h(\beta,n_{O,l})},c_{l},\{b_{i}+{\mathbb{1}}_{I}(i)\}_{i\in E_{l}}).

After introducing the appropriate notations, it will be apparent that this seemingly complicated term satisfies the four conditions:

(1)

\sigma_{l}\subset\sigma
,

(2)

h(\beta,n_{O,l})\geq 1
,

(3)

\frac{n_{O,l}}{h(\beta,n_{O,l})}\geq 1
,

(4)

and
\min_{i}\{b_{i}+{\mathbb{1}}_{I}(i)\}_{i\in E_{l}}>\min_{i}\{b_{i}\}
.

By induction on degree, we have theoretically already calculated the term

a(\sigma_{l},e(K)h(\beta,n_{O,l}),f(K)\frac{n_{O,l}}{h(\beta,n_{O,l})},c_{l},\{b_{i}+{\mathbb{1}}_{I}(i)\}_{i\in E_{l}})

whenever one of the first three conditions is satisfied strictly. Thus, this proposition effectively reduces the calculation of
a(\sigma,e_{\base},f_{\base},c,\{b_{i}\})
to
a(\sigma,e_{\base},f_{\base},c,\{b_{i}+{\mathbb{1}}_{I}(i)\})
.

Now we introduce the relevant terms in the proposition. Fix a tamely ramified splitting type
(e_{1}^{f_{1}},\dots,e_{m}^{f_{m}})
. Let
d_{i}=e_{i,\rel}f_{i,\rel}
, and so
d=\sum_{i}d_{i}
. Also, let
\beta=\beta(\{e_{i}\},e_{\base},\{b_{i}\})=\min\frac{b_{i}}{e_{i,\rel}}
. In other words, for any choices of
i\in[m]
and
j_{i}\in[\gcd(p^{f_{i}}-1,e_{i,\rel})]
,
\beta
is the smallest possible valuation of any element in
{\mathfrak{m}}_{L_{j_{i}}}^{b_{i}}
. We will often omit the dependence of
\beta
on
\{e_{i}\},e_{\base},
and
\{b_{i}\}
, when these inputs are clear from context. Let
I=I(\{e_{i}\},e_{\base},\{b_{i}\})=\{i\in[m]:\frac{b_{i}}{e_{i,\rel}}=\beta
. Let
\denom(\beta)
denote the denominator of
\beta
in its lowest form. Note that if
\frac{a}{b}=\beta
, then
\denom(\beta)=\frac{b}{\gcd(a,b)}
. Let
T_{f_{i},p}
denote the Teichmüller representatives (aka
p^{f_{i}}-1
roots of unity and
0
).

We make a few more definitions which are more ad hoc, and so the reasons that we have defined these terms as such may be clearer if one reads the proof of Proposition
4.2
, and refer to these definitions as needed. Fix a splitting type
\sigma=(e_{1}^{f_{1}},\dots,e_{m}^{f_{m}})
, a partition

of

, an integer

associated to each element
E_{l}
of the partition, and an integer
b_{i}
for each
i\in[m]
. Let

and

h(\beta,n_{O,l})=\begin{cases}\denom(\beta)&n_{O,l}\neq 1\\
1&n_{O,l}=1\end{cases}.

Now consider the set

\prod_{i=1}^{m}[\gcd(p^{f_{i}}-1,e_{i,\rel}]\times\prod_{i\in I}T_{f_{i},p}.

We consider three conditions on elements
(\{j_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m},\{a_{i,b_{i}}\}_{i\in I})
in this set:

(1)

For all

,
i_{1},i_{2}
belong to the same

if and only if

are Galois conjugates.

(2)

If

n_{O}(a_{i,b_{i}}\pi_{L_{j_{i}}}^{b_{i}})=n_{O,l}
.

(3)

If

, then there is some

containing all of

. Moreover, for any

,
a_{i,b_{i}}=0
.

Let

be
\frac{1}{\prod_{i\in[m]}\gcd(p^{f_{i}}-1,e_{i,\rel})}
times the number of elements in

satisfying these conditions. Note that if there is such an

.

As mentioned after Proposition

We have the relation

4.1.
Generating function

We can express the above recurrences nicely with a generating function. Let

G(\sigma,e_{\base},f_{\base},\{b_{i}\},p)(t)=\sum_{c=0}^{\infty}a(\sigma,e_{\base},f_{\base},c,\{b_{i}\},p)t^{c}.

Similar to our notation for
a
, we will often omit the dependence of
G
on
p
.

As an analogous example to Example
4.1
, we have

Example 4.5
.

G((e_{\base}^{f_{\base}}),e_{\base},f_{\base},\{b\})=p^{-bf}
.

This will be our base case, and we will inductively calculate
G(\sigma,e_{\base},f_{\base},\{b_{i}\})(t)
using Proposition
4.2
and Lemma
4.4
.

To simplify our equations, define

\inc^{1}(\{b_{i}\})=\inc(\sigma,e_{\base},f_{\base},\{b_{i}\})=\{b_{i}+{\mathbb{1}}_{I}(i)\},

where we recall,
I
consists of
i
so that

is minimum among such terms. Let
\inc^{n}(\{b_{i}\})=\inc(\inc^{n-1}(\{b_{i}\}))
. Define

\displaystyle H(\sigma,e_{\base},f_{\base},\{b_{i}\})

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{partitions }\{E_{l}\}_{l=1}^{k}\text{ of }[m]\\
\{n_{O,l}\}_{l=1}^{k}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 1}^{k}\\
(\{E_{l}\},\{n_{O,l}\})\neq(\{[m]\},\{1\})\end{subarray}}P(\sigma,\{E_{l}\},\{b_{i}\},\{n_{O,l}\},p)\right)

\displaystyle\times t^{\frac{1}{e_{\base}}(d(d-1)-\sum_{l}\sum_{i\in E_{l}}d_{i}(\sum_{i\in E_{l}}(d_{i}/n_{O,l})-1))\beta}

\displaystyle\times\prod_{l=1}^{k}G(\sigma_{l},e(K)h(\beta,n_{O,l}),f(K)\frac{n_{O,l}}{h(\beta,n_{O,l})},\{b_{i}+{\mathbb{1}}_{I}(i)\}_{i\in E_{l}})(t^{n_{O,l}})

From now on, we will often abbreviate

, and similarly for

. With these notations, we get the following as a reformulation of Proposition

Corollary 4.6
.

G(\{b_{i}\})(t)=P(\sigma,\{[m]\},\{b_{i}\},\{1\},p)G(\inc(\{b_{i}\}))(t)+H(\{b_{i}\})(t)

Also, as a reformulation of Lemma

Corollary 4.7

G(\{b_{i}+e_{i,\rel}\})(t)=p^{-f_{\base}d}t^{\frac{d(d-1)}{e_{\base}}}G(\{b_{i}\})(t)

Next, we will use these two reformulations to inductively calculate
(\sigma,e_{\base},f_{\base},\{b_{i}\})

and let

be the smallest

so that
\inc^{n}(\{b_{i}\})=\{k_{lim}e_{i,\rel}\}
. Also, let

be the

so that

. Then, we have the following formula.

Proposition 4.8
.

G(\{b_{i}\})(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{n_{\{b_{i}\}}-1}H(\inc^{n}(\{b_{i}\}))(t)+\left(p^{-f_{\base}d}t^{\frac{d(d-1)}{e_{\base}}}\right)^{k_{lim}}G(\{0\}_{i=1}^{m})(t),

where

Proof.

First, we use Corollary
4.6
repeatedly and get that

k_{lim}

\sum_{i\in E_{l}}(\frac{e_{i,\rel}}{h(\beta,n_{O,l})}-1)f_{i,\rel}.

Thus, we must show that

\sum(e_{i,\rel}-1)f_{i,\rel}-\sum_{l}\sum_{i\in E_{l}}d_{i}(d-\sum_{i\in E_{l}}(d_{i}/n_{O,l}))\beta+\sum_{l}\sum_{i\in E_{l}}(\frac{e_{i,\rel}}{h(\beta,n_{O,l})}-1)f_{i,\rel}

is even. We have a series of equalities modulo
2
.

\sum(e_{i,\rel}-1)f_{i,\rel}-\sum_{l}\sum_{i\in E_{l}}d_{i}(d-\sum_{i\in E_{l}}(d_{i}/n_{O,l}))\beta+\sum_{l}\sum_{i\in E_{l}}(\frac{e_{i,\rel}}{h(\beta,n_{O,l})}-1)f_{i,\rel}

=\sum_{l}\sum_{i\in E_{l}}d_{i}(d-\sum_{i\in E_{l}}(d_{i}/n_{O,l}))\beta+\sum_{l}\sum_{i\in E_{l}}(\frac{e_{i,\rel}}{h(\beta,n_{O,l})}-e_{i,\rel})f_{i,\rel}

=\sum_{l}\sum_{i\in E_{l}}f_{i,\rel}(e_{i,\rel}(d-\sum_{i\in E_{l}}(d_{i}/n_{O,l}))\beta+(\frac{e_{i,\rel}}{h(\beta,n_{O,l})}-e_{i,\rel})).

First of all, modulo
2
, we may remove all
i
such that
f_{i,\rel}
is even. So, we reduce to the case where
f_{i,\rel}
are all odd.

In the case where
f_{i,\rel}
are all odd, we have that
d_{i}\equiv e_{i,\rel}\mod 2
. So, we may rewrite our sum as

\sum_{l}\sum_{i\in E_{l}}e_{i,\rel}(\sum_{i}e_{i,\rel}-\sum_{i\in E_{l}}(d_{i}/n_{O,l}))\beta+(\frac{e_{i,\rel}}{h(\beta,n_{O,l})}-e_{i,\rel})).

Now for
n_{O,l}\neq 1
, it is actually the degree of a subextension
K^{\prime}/K
of some
L_{j_{i}}
, where
K^{\prime}
is of the form
K^{\prime}=K[a_{i,b_{i}}\pi_{L_{j_{i}}}^{b_{i}}]
. With this notation,
\denom(\beta)=e(K^{\prime}/K)
, and
n_{O,l}=\denom(\beta)f(K^{\prime}/K)
. Now note that since we’re in the case where
f_{i,\rel}
are all odd, modulo
2
, we can assume
n_{O,l}=\denom(\beta)
. Now suppose there are no
l
such that
n_{O,l}=1
. Then, we may rewrite our sum again as

\sum_{l}\sum_{i\in E_{l}}(e_{i,\rel}(\sum_{i}e_{i,\rel}-\sum_{i\in E_{l}}\frac{e_{i,\rel}}{\denom(\beta)})\beta+(\frac{e_{i,\rel}}{\denom(\beta)}-e_{i,\rel})).

Now let
\num(\beta)
denote the numerator of
\beta
in the lowest form. Then, we get

\num(\beta)\sum_{l}\sum_{i\in E_{l}}\frac{e_{i,\rel}}{\denom(\beta)}(\sum_{i}e_{i,\rel}-\sum_{i\in E_{l}}\frac{e_{i,\rel}}{\denom(\beta)})+\sum_{i}(\frac{e_{i,\rel}}{\denom(\beta)}-e_{i,\rel}).

Multiplying the first term out, we get

\num(\beta)\left(\sum_{i}\frac{e_{i,\rel}}{\denom(\beta)}\sum_{i}e_{i,\rel}-\sum_{l}\left(\sum_{i\in E_{l}}\frac{e_{i,\rel}}{\denom(\beta)}\right)^{2}\right)+\sum_{i}(\frac{e_{i,\rel}}{\denom(\beta)}-e_{i,\rel}).

But modulo
2
,
x^{2}=x
, and so our expression simplifies to

\num(\beta)\left(\sum_{i}\frac{e_{i,\rel}}{\denom(\beta)}(\sum_{i}e_{i,\rel}-1)\right)+\sum_{i}(\frac{e_{i,\rel}}{\denom(\beta)}-e_{i,\rel}).

Now we have two cases. If
\denom(\beta)
were odd, then we immediately see that both terms are
0\mod 2
. If
\denom(\beta)
were even, then
\num(\beta)
must be odd. In this case, our expression simplifies to

\sum_{i}e_{i,\rel}(\sum_{i}\frac{e_{i,\rel}}{\denom(\beta)}-1).

But we know
\denom(\beta)|e_{i,\rel}
for each
i
, and so this term is even as well.

Now we are left with the case where there is some
l
with
n_{O,l}=1
, say
n_{O,1}
. All of the above reasoning still applies, and the term

\sum_{i\in E_{l}}e_{i,\rel}(\sum_{i}e_{i,\rel}-\sum_{i\in E_{l}}(d_{i}/n_{O,l}))\beta+(\frac{e_{i,\rel}}{h(\beta,n_{O,l})}-e_{i,\rel})),

where
l=1
simplifies to

\sum_{i\in E_{1}}e_{i,\rel}\sum_{i\notin E_{1}}e_{i,\rel}\beta.

Thus, we wish to show that

\sum_{i\in E_{1}}e_{i,\rel}\sum_{i\notin E_{1}}e_{i,\rel}\beta+\num(\beta)\left(\sum_{i\notin E_{1}}\frac{e_{i,\rel}}{\denom(\beta)}(\sum_{i}e_{i,\rel}-1)\right)+\sum_{i\notin E_{1}}(\frac{e_{i,\rel}}{\denom(\beta)}-e_{i,\rel})

is even. This expression is equivalent to the following

\num(\beta)\left(\sum_{i\notin E_{1}}\frac{e_{i,\rel}}{\denom(\beta)}(\sum_{i}e_{i,\rel}-1+\sum_{i\in E_{1}}e_{i,\rel})\right)+\sum_{i\notin E_{1}}(\frac{e_{i,\rel}}{\denom(\beta)}-e_{i,\rel})

=\num(\beta)\left(\sum_{i\notin E_{1}}\frac{e_{i,\rel}}{\denom(\beta)}(\sum_{i\notin E_{1}}e_{i,\rel}-1)\right)+\sum_{i\notin E_{1}}(\frac{e_{i,\rel}}{\denom(\beta)}-e_{i,\rel}).

As before, we separate into cases where
\denom(\beta)
is odd and
\denom(\beta)
is even, and see that in both cases, this expression is even.
∎

Proof of Theorem

First, we setup and recall some notation. For

Let

to be the proportion of degree
[L:K]

\Omega_{[L:K]}

\rho(L,K)=\sum_{A\subset[m]}\frac{1}{|\Aut(A)|}\rho(\prod_{i\in A}{\mathcal{O}}_{L_{i}}\times\prod_{i\notin A}L_{i}\setminus{\mathcal{O}}_{L_{i}},K)

where

\left|\frac{\Aut(L)}{\Aut(L_{A})\Aut(L_{A^{c}})}\right|

\Aut(L_{A})\times\Aut(L_{A^{c}})

3.3

\rho(U,K)=\frac{{\mathbb{E}}[Z^{\new}_{U,L}]}{\Aut(L)}

\rho(\prod_{i\in A}{\mathcal{O}}_{L_{i}}\prod_{i\notin A}L_{i}\setminus{\mathcal{O}}_{L_{i}},K)=\frac{w([L:K])}{w([L_{A}:K])w([L_{A^{c}}:K])}\rho({\mathcal{O}}_{L_{A}},L_{A})\rho({\mathfrak{m}}_{L_{A^{c}}},L_{A^{c}}).

The central idea to this equivalence is given in
[
1

. In the case where

Lemma 6.1

(Bhargava, Cremona, Fisher, Gajović)
.

Let
a\geq b\geq 0
. The multiplication map

\psi:{\mathcal{O}}_{K}[x]_{b}^{1}\times{\mathcal{O}}_{K}[x]_{a-b}^{2}\to\{P\in\Omega_{a}:\bar{P}\in\kappa_{K}[x]^{1}_{b}\}

is a measure preserving bijection.

Note that the authors proved this over the
p
-adics, but it extends to all extensions as well. Applying this lemma, we get that

\{P_{1}\in{\mathcal{O}}_{K}[x]_{[L_{A}:K]}^{1}:P_{1}\text{ has a new root in }{\mathcal{O}}_{L_{A}}\}\times\{P_{2}\in{\mathcal{O}}_{K}[x]_{[L_{A^{c}}:K]}^{2}:P_{2}\text{ has a new root in }\prod_{i\notin A}L_{i}\setminus{\mathcal{O}}_{L_{i}}\},

which is a subset of
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}[x]_{[L_{A}:K]}^{1}\times{\mathcal{O}}_{K}[x]_{[L_{A^{c}}:K]}^{2}
, has the same measure as

\{P\in\Omega_{[L:K]}:\bar{P}\in\kappa_{K}[x]^{1}_{b},P\text{ has a new root in }{\mathcal{O}}_{L_{A}}\times\prod_{i\notin A}L_{i}\setminus{\mathcal{O}}_{L_{i}}\},

which is a subset of
\{P\in\Omega_{[L:K]}:\bar{P}\in\kappa_{K}[x]^{1}_{[L_{A}:K]}\}.

Thus, it suffices for us to show the following

(1)

\rho({\mathcal{O}}_{L_{A}},K)=w([L_{A}:K])\mu_{[L_{A}:K]}^{1}(\{P_{1}\in{\mathcal{O}}_{K}[x]_{[L_{A}:K]}^{1}:P_{1}\text{ has a new root in }{\mathcal{O}}_{L_{A}}\}),

(2)

\rho({\mathfrak{m}}_{L_{A^{c}}},L_{A^{c}})=qw([L_{A^{c}}:K]))\mu_{[L_{A^{c}}:K]}(\{P_{2}\in{\mathcal{O}}_{K}[x]_{[L_{A^{c}}:K]}^{2}:P_{2}\text{ has a new root in }\prod_{i\notin A}L_{i}\setminus{\mathcal{O}}_{L_{i}}\}),

(3)

and

\rho(\prod_{i\in A}{\mathcal{O}}_{L_{i}}\prod_{i\notin A}L_{i}\setminus{\mathcal{O}}_{L_{i}},K)

=qw([L:K])\mu_{[L:K]}(\{P\in\Omega_{[L:K]}:\bar{P}\in\kappa_{K}[x]^{1}_{b},P\text{ has a new root in }{\mathcal{O}}_{L_{A}}\times\prod_{i\notin A}L_{i}\setminus{\mathcal{O}}_{L_{i}}\}).

We have a surjective map

\varphi:\{\sum_{i=0}^{[L_{A}:K]}a_{i}x^{i}\in\Omega_{[L_{A}:K]}:v_{p}(a_{[L_{A}:K]})\leq v_{p}(a_{i}),i=0,\dots,[L_{A}:K]-1\}\to{\mathcal{O}}_{K}[x]_{[L_{A}:K]}^{1}

via dividing by the coefficient of
x^{[L_{A}:K]}
. One can check on basic opens that the pushforward of
\mu_{[L_{A}:K]}
via this map is precisely
w([L_{A}:K])\mu_{[L_{A}:K]}^{1}
. Now,

\varphi^{-1}(\{P_{1}\in{\mathcal{O}}_{K}[x]_{[L_{A}:K]}^{1}:P_{1}\text{ has a new root in }{\mathcal{O}}_{L_{A}}\}),

is precisely the set of which
\rho({\mathcal{O}}_{L_{A}},K)
is the measure. Hence, we have our first item.

The second and third item are similar, and so we will just prove the second item. Let

S_{\beta}=\{P_{2}\in{\mathcal{O}}_{K}[x]_{[L_{A^{c}}:K]}:P_{2}\text{ has a new root in }\prod_{i\notin A}L_{i}\setminus{\mathcal{O}}_{L_{i}},\bar{P}_{2}=\beta\},

so that

is our set on the RHS of item 2. Then, we see by multiplying by units, that
\mu_{[L_{A}^{c}:K]}(S_{1})=\mu_{[L_{A}^{c}:K]}(S_{\beta})
for any

. Now let

Then,

(1-q^{-([L_{A^{c}}:K]+1)})\rho(\prod_{i\notin A}L_{i}\setminus{\mathcal{O}}_{L_{i}},K)=(q-1)\mu_{[L_{A^{c}}:K]}(S_{1}).

Thus, we get

\rho(\prod_{i\notin A}L_{i}\setminus{\mathcal{O}}_{L_{i}},K)=qw([L_{A^{c}}:K])\mu_{[L_{A^{c}}:K]}(S_{1}),

Lemma 7.2

q
limit asymptotics for

G(\sigma,e_{\base},f_{\base},\{0\}_{i=1}^{m},p)(q^{-e_{\base}/2}).

We will then give a bound on the size of

G(\sigma,e_{\base},f_{\base},\{1\}_{i=1}^{m},p)(q^{-e_{\base}/2}).

It turns out that these facts will suffice to calculate the asymptotics of
\rho
.

Now we start with our first step. We need to find the minimal
c
so that
a(\sigma,e_{\base},f_{\base},c,\{0\}_{i=1}^{m},p)
is nonzero. Fix a base field
K
with
e(K/{\mathbb{Q}}_{p})=e_{\base}
and
f(K/{\mathbb{Q}}_{p})=f_{\base}
. Then,
a(\sigma,e_{\base},f_{\base},c,\{0\}_{i=1}^{m},p)
is nonzero if and only if there is some element
(\alpha_{i})
generating an extension of
K
with splitting type
\sigma
such that
v_{p}(\Delta_{P_{(\alpha_{i})}/K})=c
.

Lemma 8.1
.

The smallest
c
such that there is some element
(\alpha_{i})
generating an extension of
K
with splitting type
\sigma
such that
v_{p}(\Delta_{P_{(\alpha_{i})}/K})=c
is

c_{0}:=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{1}{e_{\base}}f_{i,\rel}(e_{i,\rel}-1).

Moreover, the elements
(\alpha_{i})
that achieve this are precisely the ones that satisfy

(1)

For all
i
, the first term in the Teichmüller expansion of
\alpha_{i}
are primitive
p^{f_{i}}-1
roots of unity, and these roots of unity belong in different Galois orbits over
K
as
i
varies.

(2)

For all
i
, the second term in the Teichmüller expansion of
\alpha_{i}
is nonzero.

Proof.

We approach this problem by essentially following the recursive steps as in Proposition
4.2
. We will prove lower bounds on the valuation of discriminant of a minimal polynomial generating an extension with appropriate splitting type, and see along the way that these bounds are achieved.

We have that (here we use the same notation as Section
4
)

\Delta_{P_{(\alpha_{i})}/K}=\prod_{(i,k)\neq(i^{\prime},k^{\prime}),k,k^{\prime}\in[d_{i}]}(\alpha_{i,k}-\alpha_{i^{\prime},k^{\prime}}),

where
\alpha_{i,k}
are Galois conjugates of
\alpha_{i}
over
K
. So, we wish to maximize distances between Galois conjugates. Now we use equation
4.1
(and its associated notations) and try to minimize distances in each of the terms. So, we have two cases:
(i,k)\sim(i^{\prime},k^{\prime})
and
(i,k)\not\sim(i^{\prime},k^{\prime})
. In the first case, where
(i,k)\sim(i^{\prime},k^{\prime})
, we have the trivial bound that

v_{p}(\alpha_{i,k}-\alpha_{i^{\prime},k^{\prime}})\geq 0.

In the second case, where
(i,k)\sim(i^{\prime},k^{\prime})
, we have that
\alpha_{i,k}
and
\alpha_{i^{\prime},k^{\prime}}
share a common lowest order term
\gamma
(in terms of the Teichmüller expansion). Thus,

v_{p}(\alpha_{i,k}-\alpha_{i^{\prime},k^{\prime}})=v_{p}((\alpha_{i,k}-\gamma)-(\alpha_{i^{\prime},k^{\prime}}-\gamma))\geq\min(v_{p}(\alpha_{i,k}-\gamma_{i,k}),v_{p}(\alpha_{i^{\prime},k^{\prime}}-\gamma_{i,k}))

by definition of a valuation. Now
\alpha_{i,k}-\gamma
and
\alpha_{i^{\prime},k^{\prime}}-\gamma
no longer have valuation
0
, and since they belong in extensions with ramification indices
e_{i}
and
e_{i^{\prime}}
, respectively, we have that
v_{p}(\alpha_{i,k}-\gamma)\geq\frac{1}{e_{i}}
and
v_{p}(\alpha_{i^{\prime},k^{\prime}}-\gamma)\geq\frac{1}{e_{i^{\prime}}}
. Thus, we obtain the lower bound that

v_{p}(\alpha_{i,k}-\alpha_{i^{\prime},k^{\prime}})\geq\min(\frac{1}{e_{i}},\frac{1}{e_{i^{\prime}}}).

We may compactly write these bounds as

v_{p}(\alpha_{i,k}-\alpha_{i^{\prime},k^{\prime}})\geq\begin{cases}0&\alpha_{i,k},\alpha_{i^{\prime},k^{\prime}}\text{ have different lowest order term Galois orbits}\\
\min(\frac{1}{e_{i}},\frac{1}{e_{i^{\prime}}})&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}

With these lower bounds, we see that in order to minimize the valuation of the discriminant, we would like every pair
(i,k)\neq(i^{\prime},k^{\prime})
to satisfy that
v_{p}(\alpha_{i,k}-\alpha_{i^{\prime},k^{\prime}})=0
. In other words, we want
\alpha_{i,k}
and
\alpha_{i^{\prime},k^{\prime}}
to be have valuation
0
and also have different lowest order terms in the Teichmüller expansion. This is certainly possible if
i\neq i^{\prime}
(granted
q
is large enough). Indeed for any choice
(\alpha_{i})
generating an extension
\prod_{i=1}^{m}L_{i}
with the appropriate splitting, we can adjust the valuation-
0
terms of the Teichmüller expansion of
\alpha_{i}
in terms of
\pi_{L_{i}}
in order to make sure that
v_{p}(\alpha_{i,k}-\alpha_{i^{\prime},k^{\prime}})=0
for
i\neq i^{\prime}
(we can do this by choosing appropriate roots of unity belonging to different Galois orbits over
K
). Thus, the problem reduces down to the case of field extensions. That is, given two numbers
e_{abs}
and
f_{abs}
, we ask for the smallest valuation possible of
\Delta_{P_{\alpha}/K}
, given that
\alpha
generates an extension
L/K
of ramification index
e(L/{\mathbb{Q}}_{p})=e_{abs}
and inertia degree
f(L/{\mathbb{Q}}_{p})=f_{abs}
.

We now focus on the field case. Fix an extension
L/K
with
e(L/{\mathbb{Q}}_{p})=e_{abs}
and
f(L/{\mathbb{Q}}_{p})=f_{abs}
. Define
e_{\rel}=e_{abs}/e_{\base}
and
f_{\rel}=f_{abs}/f_{\base}
. Take any
\alpha\in L
. Write it as
a_{0}+\delta
, where
a_{0}\in T_{f_{abs}}
and
\delta\in{\mathfrak{m}}_{L}
. That is, we are extracting the first coefficient of its Teichmüller expansion. Now let
n_{O}(a_{0})
be the number of conjugates of
a_{0}
, and so
n_{O}(a_{0})|f_{\rel}
. Then, by Equation
4.1
and the bound above, we have that

v_{p}(\Delta_{\alpha})\geq\frac{1}{e_{abs}}n_{O}(a_{0})(d/n_{O}(a_{0}))(d/n_{O}(a_{0})-1)

(here
d=e_{\rel}f_{\rel}
). Thus, in order to minimize
v_{p}(\Delta_{\alpha})
, we must choose
a_{0}
so that
n_{O}(a_{0})
is as large as possible, and so we should take
a_{0}
to be a primitive root of unity of order
q^{f_{\rel}}-1
, in which case
n_{O}(a_{0})=f_{\rel}
. With this choice, we have that

v_{p}(\Delta_{\alpha})\geq\frac{1}{e_{abs}}f_{\rel}e_{\rel}(e_{\rel}-1).

Now
\delta
generates
L
over
K[a_{0}]
as a totally ramified extension with relative ramification index
e_{\rel}
. Denote
\{\delta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{e_{\rel}}
to be the Galois conjugates of
\delta
. Then, the lower bound
\frac{1}{e_{abs}}f_{\rel}e_{\rel}(e_{\rel}-1)
will only be achieved if
v_{p}(\delta_{i}-\delta_{j})=\frac{1}{e_{abs}}
, for all
i\neq j
. This happens if and only if
\delta_{i}\notin{\mathfrak{m}}_{L}^{2}
, which gives us the second condition.
∎

Thus, at this point, we know that
G(\sigma_{A},e_{\base},f_{\base},\{0\}_{i\in A})(t)
is of the form

a(\sigma,e_{\base},f_{\base},c_{0},\{0\})t^{c_{0}}+H.O.T.,

We want to find large
q
limit when we evaluate
G
at
t=q^{-e_{\base}/2}
. Now, if we evaluate
G
at
t=1
, we obtain the sum of the coefficients
a(\sigma,e_{\base},f_{\base},c,\{0\}_{i=1}^{m},p)
, which by definition is

\frac{1}{\prod_{i\in[m]}\gcd(p^{f_{i}}-1,e_{i,\rel})}\sum_{j_{i}\in[\gcd(p^{f_{i}}-1,e_{i,\rel})]}a(\{L_{j_{i}}\},K,c,\{0\}_{i=1}^{m}).

Now summing this over
c
, we get

\sum_{c}\frac{1}{\prod_{i\in[m]}\gcd(p^{f_{i}}-1,e_{i,\rel})}\sum_{j_{i}\in[\gcd(p^{f_{i}}-1,e_{i,\rel})]}a(\{L_{j_{i}}\},K,c,\{0\}_{i=1}^{m})

=\frac{1}{\prod_{i\in[m]}\gcd(p^{f_{i}}-1,e_{i,\rel})}\sum_{j_{i}\in[\gcd(p^{f_{i}}-1,e_{i,\rel})]}\sum_{c}a(\{L_{j_{i}}\},K,c,\{0\}_{i=1}^{m})

(8.2)

=\frac{1}{\prod_{i\in[m]}\gcd(p^{f_{i}}-1,e_{i,\rel})}\sum_{j_{i}\in[\gcd(p^{f_{i}}-1,e_{i,\rel})]}1=1.

Thus, if we can show that
\lim_{q\to\infty}a(\sigma,e_{\base},f_{\base},c_{0},\{0\}_{i=1}^{m},p)=1
, then the large
q
limit of
G(\sigma,e_{\base},f_{\base},\{0\}_{i\in A})(q^{-e_{\base}/2})
is precisely the large
q
limit of
q^{-c_{0}e_{\base}/2}
. This is the second step.

Thus, we now aim to show that
\lim_{q\to\infty}a(\sigma,e_{\base},f_{\base},c_{0},\{0\}_{i=1}^{m},p)=1
. This purely comes down to following the appropriate recursive branch (the one associated to algebraic numbers over
K
satisfying the two conditions in Lemma
8.1
) in proposition
4.2
. First, we get a large
q
limit asymptotic for
P(\sigma,\{E_{l}\},\{b_{i}\},\{n_{O,l}\},p)
. We do this by using Theorem
7.3
. We also follow the notations in the theorem.

Corollary 8.2
.

P(\sigma,\{E_{l}\},\{b_{i}\},\{n_{O,l}\},p)=0
unless all of the following are satisfied:

(1)

For all
l
such that
n_{O,l}\neq 1
,
\denom(\beta)|n_{O,l}
.

(2)

For all
i,l
such that
i\in E_{l}
and
n_{O,l}\neq 1
,
\frac{n_{O,l}}{\denom(\beta)}|f_{i,\rel}
.

(3)

If
\denom(\beta)\neq 1
, then there is at most one
l_{0}
so that
n_{O,l_{0}}=1
.

(4)

If
I\neq[m]
, then there is some
l_{0}
, such that
E_{l_{0}}
contains all of
[m]\setminus I
.

If all of the above conditions are satisfied, let
S
be the set of
k
for which there is some
l
such that
n_{O,l}=k
. Also, let
F_{k}=\{i:i\in E_{l},n_{O,l}=k\}
be the set of all indices
i
such that
i
is in a partition
E_{l}
with
n_{O,l}=k
. Then,

P(\sigma,\{E_{l}\},\{b_{i}\},\{n_{O,l}\},p)\sim\prod_{k\in S}N_{k}^{\prime},

where for
k\neq 1

N_{k}^{\prime}=\left(\frac{k}{\denom(\beta)}\right)^{\#F_{k}}\left(\frac{p^{f_{\base}k/\denom(\beta)}}{k/\denom(\beta)}\right)^{\#\{l,n_{O,l}=k\}}

and

N_{1}^{\prime}=\begin{cases}1&\denom(\beta)\neq 1\\
q^{\#\{l,n_{O,l}=1\}}&\denom(\beta)=1,I=[m]\\
q^{\#\{l,n_{O,l}=1\}-1}&\denom(\beta)=1,I\neq[m]\end{cases}

Proof.

We first start with the large
q
limit asymptotic of
P(f_{\base},k^{\prime},p)
. Following its definition, this is
P(f_{\base},k^{\prime},p)\sim\frac{p^{f_{\base}k^{\prime}}}{k^{\prime}}=\frac{q^{k^{\prime}}}{k^{\prime}}
. Next, we use Theorem
7.3
. By definition of
N_{k}
, we have that for
k\neq 1
,

N_{k}\sim\left(\frac{k}{\denom(\beta)}\right)^{\#F_{k}}\left(\frac{q^{k/\denom(\beta)}}{k/\denom(\beta)}\right)^{\#\{l,n_{O,l}=k\}}.

We also have

N_{1}\sim\begin{cases}1&\denom(\beta)\neq 1\\
q^{\#\{l,n_{O,l}=1\}}&\denom(\beta)=1,I=[m]\\
q^{\#\{l,n_{O,l}=1\}-1}&\denom(\beta)=1,I\neq[m]\end{cases}.

∎

Lemma 8.3
.

\lim_{q\to\infty}a(\sigma,e_{\base},f_{\base},c_{0},\{0\}_{i=1}^{m},p)=1
.

Proof.

We follow the recursion in Proposition
4.2
, which expresses
a(\sigma,e_{\base},f_{\base},c_{0},\{0\}_{i=1}^{m},p)
as a sum. Now, by condition 1 in Lemma
8.1
, the only nonzero term in this sum comes from the partition
\{E_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
of
[m]
consist of singleton sets with
E_{i}=\{i\}
,
n_{O,i}=f_{i,\rel}
, and
c_{i}=\frac{1}{e_{\base}}(e_{i,\rel}-1)
. Thus,

a(\sigma,e_{\base},f_{\base},c_{0},\{0\}_{i=1}^{m},p)=P(\sigma,\{E_{i}\},\{0\},\{n_{O,i}\},p)\prod_{i}a((e_{i}^{f_{i}}),e_{\base},f_{i},\frac{1}{e_{\base}}(e_{i,\rel}-1),1).

By Corollary
8.2
,

P(\sigma,\{E_{i}\},\{0\},\{n_{O,i}\},p)\sim\prod_{i}q^{f_{i,\rel}}.

So, it remains to find

a((e_{i}^{f_{i}}),e_{\base},f_{i},\frac{1}{e_{\base}}(e_{i,\rel}-1),1),

to which we will again apply same reasoning as before, except this time we apply condition 2 in Lemma
8.1
. This will yield that

a((e_{i}^{f_{i}}),e_{\base},f_{i},\frac{1}{e_{\base}}(e_{i,\rel}-1),1)\sim q^{-f_{i,\rel}}.

Thus, we obtain that
a(\sigma,e_{\base},f_{\base},c_{0},\{0\}_{i=1}^{m},p)\sim 1
, as desired.
∎

Finally, we obtain our desired asymptotic for
G
.

Proposition 8.4
.

G(\sigma,e_{\base},f_{\base},\{0\}_{i=1}^{m},p)(q^{-e_{\base}/2})\sim q^{-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}f_{i,\rel}\frac{e_{i,\rel}-1}{2}}

Proof.

The RHS is the expansion of
q^{-c_{0}e_{\base}/2}
, after replacing
c_{0}
by its definition in Lemma
8.1
.
∎

Now to proceed to computing an asymptotic for
\rho
, we need the following trivial bound.

Lemma 8.5
.

Let
e_{min}=\min_{i}(e_{i,\rel})
. Then,

G(\sigma,e_{\base},f_{\base},\{1\}_{i=1}^{m},p)(q^{-e_{\base}/2})=o(\sum_{i=1}^{m}f_{i,\rel}(e_{i,\rel}-1)).

Proof.

First we find the smallest exponent of
t
in
G(\sigma,e_{\base},f_{\base},\{1\}_{i=1}^{m},p)(t)
.
\frac{1}{e_{i}}d_{i}(d_{i}-1)
is the minimal valuation of the discriminant of a minimal polynomial of an element
\alpha_{i}
in
{\mathfrak{m}}_{L_{i}}
, since there are
d_{i}(d_{i}-1)
choices of pairs of Galois conjugates, and for each pair
\delta,\gamma
of such Galois conjugates,
v_{p}(\delta-\gamma)\geq\frac{1}{e_{i}}
. Now,
v_{p}(\Delta_{P_{(\alpha_{i})}/K})
takes substantially more into account, and we should also consider the contribution to the discriminant coming from differences of a Galois conjugates of
\alpha_{i}
and a Galois conjugate of
\alpha_{j}
, where
i\neq j
. However, for the sake of an upper bound, we may ignore this. Thus, the smallest exponent of
t
is at least
\sum_{i}\frac{1}{e_{i}}d_{i}(d_{i}-1)
. Next, as in Equation
8.2
, the sum of all coefficients of
G(\sigma,e_{\base},f_{\base},\{1\}_{i=1}^{m},p)
gives
\prod_{i=1}^{m}q^{-f_{i,\rel}}
. Thus, we obtain that

\displaystyle G(\sigma,e_{\base},f_{\base},\{1\}_{i=1}^{m},p)(q^{-e_{\base}/2})

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{i=1}^{m}q^{-f_{i,\rel}}\cdot(q^{-e_{\base}/2})^{\sum_{i}\frac{1}{e_{i}}d_{i}(d_{i}-1)}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{m}q^{-f_{i,\rel}}\cdot q^{-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{1}{e_{i,\rel}}d_{i}(d_{i}-1)}.

Since
\sum_{i=1}^{m}f_{i,\rel}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{1}{e_{i,\rel}}d_{i}(d_{i}-1)>\sum_{i=1}^{m}f_{i,\rel}(e_{i,\rel}-1)
, for any choice of
e_{i}
and
f_{i}
, we obtain our desired bound.
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
3.2
.

Proof of theorem
3.2
.

We have by Equation
8.1

\rho(\sigma,e_{\base},f_{\base};p)=\frac{w(d)\sum_{A\subset[m]}G(A,0)(q^{-e_{\base}/2})G(A^{c},1)(q^{-e_{\base}/2})}{\perm(\sigma)q^{\sum_{i=1}^{m}(e_{i,\rel}-1)f_{i,\rel}/2}\prod_{i=1}^{m}f_{i,\rel}}.

Now by Proposition
8.4
and Lemma
8.5
, most terms in this sum drop out, except

G([m],0)(q^{-e_{\base}/2})\sim q^{-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}f_{i,\rel}(e_{i,\rel}-1)}.

Also, by definition,
w(d)=\frac{q^{d+1}-q^{d}}{q^{d+1}-1}\sim 1
. Thus, we have that

\rho(\sigma,e_{\base},f_{\base};p)\sim\frac{1}{\perm(\sigma)\prod_{i=1}^{m}f_{i,\rel}q^{\sum_{i=1}^{m}(e_{i,\rel}-1)f_{i,\rel}}}=\frac{1}{\perm(\sigma)\prod_{i=1}^{m}f_{i,\rel}q^{\sum_{i=1}^{m}(e_{i,\rel}-1)f_{i,\rel}}}.

∎
