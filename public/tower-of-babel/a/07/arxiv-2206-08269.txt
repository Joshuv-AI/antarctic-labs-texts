Learning with little mixing
Ingvar Ziemann, Stephen Tu

Abstract
We study square loss in a realizable time-series framework with martingale difference noise. Our main result is a fast rate excess risk bound which shows that whenever a trajectory hypercontractivity condition holds, the risk of the least-squares estimator on dependent data matches the iid rate order-wise after a burn-in time. In comparison, many existing results in learning from dependent data have rates where the effective sample size is deflated by a factor of the mixing-time of the underlying process, even after the burn-in time. Furthermore, our results allow the covariate process to exhibit long range correlations which are substantially weaker than geometric ergodicity. We call this phenomenon learning with little mixing , and present several examples for when it occurs: bounded function classes for which the L 2 L^{2} and L 2 + ε L^{2+\varepsilon} norms are equivalent, ergodic finite state Markov chains, various parametric models, and a broad family of infinite dimensional ℓ 2 ​ ( ℕ ) \ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}) ellipsoids. By instantiating our main result to system identification of nonlinear dynamics with generalized linear model transitions, we obtain a nearly minimax optimal excess risk bound after only a polynomial burn-in time.

Introduction

Consider regression in the context of the time-series model:

\displaystyle Y_{t}=f_{\star}(X_{t})+W_{t},\qquad t=0,1,2,\dots.

(1.1)

Such models are ubiquitous in applications of machine learning, signal processing, econometrics, and control theory. In our setup, the learner is given access to

T\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
pairs
\{(X_{t},Y_{t})\}_{t=0}^{T-1}
drawn from the model (
1.1
), and is asked to output a hypothesis
\widehat{f}
from a hypothesis class
\mathscr{F}

which best approximates the (realizable) regression function
f_{\star}\in\mathscr{F}
in terms of square loss.

In this work, we study the least-squares estimator (LSE). This procedure minimizes the empirical risk associated to the square loss over the class
\mathscr{F}
. When each pair of observations
(X_{t},Y_{t})
is drawn iid from some fixed distribution,
this procedure is minimax optimal over a
broad set of hypothesis classes
(
Tsybakov 2009
;
Lecué and Mendelson 2013
;
Mendelson 2014
;
Wainwright 2019
)
. However,
much less is known about the optimal rate of convergence
for the general time-series model (
1.1
),
as correlations across time in the covariates
\{X_{t}\}
complicate the analysis.

With this in mind, we seek to extend our understanding of the minimax optimality of the LSE for the time-series model (
1.1
). We show that for a broad class of function
spaces and covariate processes,
the effects of data dependency across time enter the LSE excess risk only
as a higher order term, whereas the leading term in the excess risk remains order-wise identical
to that in the iid setting. Hence, after a sufficiently long,
but finite
burn-in time
, the LSE’s excess risk scales as if all
T
samples are independent.
This behavior applies to processes that
exhibit correlations which decay slower than
geometrically. We refer to this double phenomenon,
where the mixing-time only enters as a burn-in time, and
where the mixing requirement is mild, as

learning with little mixing
.

Our result stands in contrast to a long line of work
on learning from dependent data (see e.g.,
(
Yu 1994
;
Modha and Masry 1996
;
Mohri and Rostamizadeh 2008
;
Steinwart and Christmann 2009
;
Zou et al. 2009
;
Agarwal and Duchi 2012
;
Duchi et al. 2012
;
Kuznetsov and Mohri 2017
;
Roy et al. 2021
;
Sancetta 2021
)
and the references within),
where the blocking technique
(
Yu 1994
)
is used to create independence
amongst the dependent covariates, so that tools to analyze
independent learning can be applied.
While these aforementioned works differ in their specific setups,
the main commonality is that the resulting dependent data rates mimic
the corresponding independent rates, but with the caveat that the
sample size is replaced by an “effective” sample size that is
decreased
in some way by the mixing-time, even after any necessary burn-in time. Interestingly,
the results of
Ziemann et al. 2022
studying the LSE
on the model (
1.1
)
also suffer from such sample degradation, but do not rely on the blocking technique.

The model (
1.1
) captures learning dynamical systems
by setting
Y_{t}=X_{t+1}
, so that the regression function

f_{\star}
describes the dynamics of the state variable
X_{t}
.
Recent progress in system identification
shows that the lack of ergodicity does not necessarily degrade
learning rates.
Indeed, when the states evolve as a
linear dynamical system (i.e., the function
f_{\star}
is linear),
learning rates are not deflated by any mixing times, and match
existing rates for iid linear regression
(
Simchowitz et al. 2018
;
Faradonbeh et al. 2018
;
Sarkar and Rakhlin 2019
;
Tu et al. 2022
)
.

Kowshik et al. 2021
;
Gao and Raskutti 2021
extend results of this flavor to parameter
recovery of dynamics driven by a generalized linear model.
The extent to which this phenomenon—less ergodicity not impeding learning—generalizes beyond linear and generalized linear models is a
key motivation for our work.

Contributions

We consider the realizable setting, where
f_{\star}
is assumed to
be contained in a known function space
\mathscr{F}
.
Our results rest on two assumptions regarding both the covariate process

\{X_{t}\}
and the function space
\mathscr{F}
.
The first assumption posits that
the process
\{X_{t}\}
exhibits some mild form of ergodicity
(that is significantly weaker than the typical geometric
ergodicity assumption). The second assumption is a hypercontractivity condition that holds uniformly in
\mathscr{F}

along the trajectory
\{X_{t}\}
, extending contractivity
assumptions for iid learning
(
Mendelson 2014
)

to dependent processes.

Informally, our main result (
Theorem

4.1
, presented in
Section

4
),
shows that under these two assumptions,
letting
\textnormal{comp}(\mathscr{F})
denote some (inverse) measure of complexity of
\mathscr{F}
,
the LSE
\widehat{f}
satisfies:

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\|\widehat{f}-f_{\star}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\lesssim\left(\frac{\textnormal{dimensional factors}\times\sigma_{W}^{2}}{T}\right)^{\textnormal{comp}(\mathscr{F})}+\textnormal{higher order $o(1/T^{\textnormal{comp}(\mathscr{F})})$ terms}.

(1.2)

The first term in (
1.2
) matches
existing LSE risk bounds for iid learning order-wise, and most importantly,
does not include any dependence on the mixing-time of the process.
Indeed, all mixing-time dependencies enter only in the higher order term.
Since this term scales as
o(1/T^{\textnormal{comp}(\mathscr{F})})
,
it becomes negligible after a finite burn-in time.
This captures the crux of our results:
on a broad class of problems, given enough data,
the LSE applied to time-series model (
1.1
) behaves as if all samples are independent.

Section

6
provides several examples for which the trajectory
hypercontractivity assumption holds.
When the covariate process
\{X_{t}\}

is generated by a finite-state irreducible and aperiodic Markov chain,
then any function class
\mathscr{F}
satisfies the requisite condition.
More broadly, the condition is satisfied for any bounded function classes for which the
L^{2}
and
L^{2+\varepsilon}

norms (along trajectories)
are equivalent. Next, we show that many infinite dimensional function
spaces based on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})
ellipsoids satisfy our hypercontractivity condition, demonstrating that
our results are not inherently limited to finite-dimensional
hypothesis classes.

To demonstrate the broad applicability of our framework,
Section

7
instantiates our main result on two
system identification problems that have received recent attention
in the literature: linear dynamical systems (LDS),
and systems with generalized linear model (GLM) transitions.
For stable LDS, after a polynomial burn-in time, we recover an excess risk
bound that matches the iid rate. A more general form of this result was recently established by
Tu et al. 2022
.
For stable GLMs, also after a polynomial burn-in time,
we obtain the first excess risk bound for this problem
which matches the iid rate, up to logarithmic factors in various
problem constants including the mixing-time.
In both of these settings, our excess risk bounds also yield
nearly optimal rates for parameter recovery, matching
known results for LDS
(
Simchowitz et al. 2018
)
and
GLMs
(
Kowshik et al. 2021
)
in the stable case.
In
Section

8
, we show experimentally,
using the stable GLM model, that
the trends predicted by our theory are indeed realized
in practice.

2
Related work

While regression is a fundamental problem studied across many
disciplines, our work draws its main inspiration from
Mendelson 2014
and
Simchowitz et al. 2018
.

Mendelson 2014
shows that for nonparametric iid regression, only minimal assumptions are required for one-sided isometry, and thus learning.
Simchowitz et al. 2018
build on this intuition and provide mixing-free rates for linear regression over trajectories generated by a linear dynamical system.
We continue this trend, by leveraging one-sided isometry to show that mixing only enters as a higher order term in the rates of the nonparametric LSE. More broadly, the technical developments we follow synthesize techniques from two lines of work: nonparametric regression with iid data, and learning from dependent data.

Nonparametric regression with iid data

Beyond the seminal work of
Mendelson 2014
, the works
(
Audibert and Catoni 2011
;
Lecué and Mendelson 2013
;
Mendelson 2017
)
all study iid regression with square loss under various moment equivalence conditions.
In addition to moment equivalence, we build on
the notion of offset Rademacher complexity defined by
Liang et al. 2015
in the context of iid regression. Indeed, we show that a martingale analogue of the offset complexity (described in
Ziemann et al. 2022
) characterizes the LSE rate in (
1.1
).

Learning from dependent data

As discussed previously, many existing results for learning
from dependent data reduce the problem to independent
learning via the blocking technique
(
Yu 1994
)
,
at the expense of sample complexity deflation by the mixing-time.

Nagaraj et al. 2020
prove a lower bound for linear regression stating that in a worst case agnostic model,
this deflation is unavoidable.
Moreover, if the linear regression problem is realizable,

Nagaraj et al. 2020
provide upper and lower bounds showing
that the mixing-time only affects
the burn-in time, but not the final risk. We note that their upper bound is
an algorithmic result that holds only for a
specific modification of SGD.
Our work can be interpreted as
an upper bound in the more general
nonparametric setting,
where we put forth sufficient conditions to
recover the iid rate after a burn-in time. Our result is algorithm agnostic and directly
applies to the empirical risk minimizer.

Ziemann et al. 2022
also study the model (
1.1
), and provide an information-theoretic analysis of the nonparametric LSE.
However, their approach fundamentally reduces to showing two-sided
concentration—something our work evades—and therefore their bounds incur worst case dependency on the mixing-time.

Roy et al. 2021
extend the results from
Mendelson 2014
to the dependent data setting.
While following Mendelson’s argument
allows their results to handle
non-realizability and heavy-tailed noise,
their proof ultimately still
relies on
two-sided concentration for both the
“version space” and the “noise interaction”. Hence,
their rates end up
degrading for slower mixing processes.
We note that this is actually expected in the non-realizable setting
in light of the lower bounds in
Nagaraj et al. 2020
.

The measure of dependencies we use
for the process
\{X_{t}\}
is due to
Samson 2000
.
Recently,
Dagan et al. 2019
use a similar measure
to study learning when the covariates have no obvious
sequential ordering (e.g., a graph structure or Ising model).
However, our results are not directly comparable, other than
noting that their risk bounds
degrade as the measure of correlation increases.

Results in linear system identification show that
lack of ergodicity does not degrade parameter recovery rates
(
Simchowitz et al. 2018
;
Faradonbeh et al. 2018
;
Rantzer 2018
;
Sarkar and Rakhlin 2019
;
Jedra and Proutiere 2020
;
Tu et al. 2022
)
.
Beyond linear system identification,

Sattar and Oymak 2020
;
Foster et al. 2020
;
Kowshik et al. 2021
;
Gao and Raskutti 2021

prove parameter recovery bounds for dynamical systems
driven by a generalized linear model (GLM) transition. Most relevant
are
Kowshik et al. 2021
and
Gao and Raskutti 2021
, who again show that the lack of
ergodicity does not hamper rates. Indeed,
Gao and Raskutti 2021
even manage to do so in
a semiparametric setting with an unknown link function.
As mentioned previously, our main result instantiated to these problems
in the stable case matches existing excess risk and parameter recovery
bounds for linear system identification, and actually provides
the sharpest known excess risk bound for the GLM setting
(when the link function is known).
A more detailed comparison
to existing LDS results is given in
Section

7.1
,
and to existing GLM results in
Section

H.1
.

3
Problem formulation

The time-series (
1.1
) evolves on two subsets of Euclidean space,
\mathsf{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathsf{X}}}
and
\mathsf{Y}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathsf{Y}}}
, with
X_{t}\in\mathsf{X}
and
Y_{t},W_{t}\in\mathsf{Y}
.
Expectation (resp. probability) with respect to all the randomness of the underlying
probability space is denoted by
\mathbf{E}
(resp.
\mathbf{P}
).
The Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is denoted
\|\cdot\|_{2}
,
and the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is denoted
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.
For a matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}}
,

\|M\|_{\mathsf{op}}
denotes the largest singular value,

\sigma_{\min}(M)
the smallest non-zero singular value,
and
\mathrm{cond}(M)=\|M\|_{\mathsf{op}}/\sigma_{\min}(M)
the condition
number. When the matrix
M
is symmetric,
\lambda_{\min}(M)
will be used
to denote its minimum eigenvalue.

We assume there exists a filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}
such that (a)
\{W_{t}\}
is a square integrable martingale difference sequence (MDS) with respect to this filtration, and (b)
\{X_{t}\}
is adapted to
\{\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\}
. Further tail conditions on this MDS will be imposed as necessary later on.

Let
\mathscr{F}
be a hypothesis space of functions mapping

\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathsf{X}}}
to
\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathsf{Y}}}
. We assume that the true regression function is an element of
\mathscr{F}

(i.e.,
f_{\star}\in\mathscr{F}
), and that
\mathscr{F}
is known to the learner.
Given two compatible function spaces
\mathscr{F}_{1},\mathscr{F}_{2}
, let

\mathscr{F}_{1}-\mathscr{F}_{2}\triangleq\{f_{1}-f_{2}\mid f_{1}\in\mathscr{F}_{1},f_{2}\in\mathscr{F}_{2}\}
. A key quantity in our analysis is the shifted function class

\mathscr{F}_{\star}\triangleq\mathscr{F}-\{f_{\star}\}
. Our results will be stated under
the assumption that
\mathscr{F}_{\star}
is
star-shaped
,
1
1

1

A function class
\mathscr{F}
is star-shaped if for any
\alpha\in[0,1]
,
f\in\mathscr{F}
implies
\alpha f\in\mathscr{F}
.

although we will see that this is not too restrictive.
For any function
f:\mathsf{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathsf{Y}}}
, we define

\lVert f\rVert_{\infty}\triangleq\sup_{x\in\mathsf{X}}\lVert f\rVert_{2}
.
A function
f
is
B
-bounded if
\|f\|_{\infty}\leq B
. Similarly, a hypothesis class is
B
-bounded if each of its elements is
B
-bounded.
For a bounded class
\mathscr{F}
and
resolution
\varepsilon>0
, the quantity

\mathcal{N}_{\infty}(\mathscr{F},\varepsilon)
denotes the size of the minimal

\varepsilon
-cover of
\mathscr{F}
(contained in
\mathscr{F}
) in the
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\infty}
-norm.

We fix a
T\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
, indicating the number of labeled observations
\{(X_{t},Y_{t})\}_{t=0}^{T-1}
from the
time-series (
1.1
) that are available to the learner.
The joint distribution of
X_{0:T-1}\triangleq(X_{0},\dots,X_{T-1})

is denoted
\mathsf{P}_{X}
. For
p\geq 1
, we endow
\mathscr{F}-\mathscr{F}
with
L^{p}(\mathsf{P}_{X})
norms:
\|f-g\|_{L^{p}}^{p}\triangleq\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbf{E}\|f(X_{t})-g(X_{t})\|_{2}^{p}
, where expectation is taken with respect to
\mathsf{P}_{X}
.
We will mostly be interested in
L^{2}(\mathsf{P}_{X})
, hereafter often just referred to as
L^{2}
. This is the
L^{2}
space associated to the law of the uniform mixture over
X_{0:T-1}
and thus, for iid data, coincides with the standard
L^{2}
space often considered in iid regression.
For a radius
r>0
, we let

B(r)
denote the closed ball of
\mathscr{F}_{\star}
with radius
r
in
L^{2}
,
and we let
\partial B(r)
denote its boundary:

\displaystyle B(r)\triangleq\left\{f\in\mathscr{F}_{\star}\,\bigg|\,\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbf{E}\lVert f(X_{t})\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq r^{2}\right\},\>\>\partial B(r)\triangleq\left\{f\in\mathscr{F}_{\star}\,\bigg|\,\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbf{E}\lVert f(X_{t})\rVert_{2}^{2}=r^{2}\right\}.

The learning task is to produce an estimate
\widehat{f}
of
f_{\star}
, which renders the excess risk
\|\widehat{f}-f_{\star}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
as small as possible. We emphasize that
\|\widehat{f}-f_{\star}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbf{E}_{\tilde{X}_{0:T-1}}\|\widehat{f}(\tilde{X}_{t})-f_{\star}(\tilde{X}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}
where
\tilde{X}_{0:T-1}
is a fresh, statistically independent, sample with the same law
\mathsf{P}_{X}
as
X_{0:T-1}
. Namely,
\|\widehat{f}-f_{\star}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
is a random quantity, still depending on the internal randomness of the learner
and that of the sample
X_{0:T-1}
used to generate
\widehat{f}
. We
study the performance of the least-squares estimator (LSE) defined as

\widehat{f}\in\argmin_{f\in\mathscr{F}}\left\{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\|Y_{t}-f(X_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\right\}
,
and measure the excess risk

\mathbf{E}\lVert\widehat{f}-f_{\star}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}
.

4.1
Hypercontractivity and the dependency matrix

Hypercontractivity

We first
state our main trajectory hypercontractivity condition,
which we will use to establish lower isometry.
The following definition is heavily inspired by recent work on learning without concentration
(
Mendelson 2014
;
Mendelson 2017
)
.

Definition 4.1

(Trajectory
(C,\alpha)
-hypercontractivity)
.

Fix constants
C>0
and
\alpha\in[1,2]
. We say that the tuple
(\mathscr{F},\mathsf{P}_{X})
satisfies the
trajectory
(C,\alpha)
-hypercontractivity
condition if

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\|f(X_{t})\|^{4}_{2}\right]\leq C\left(\mathbf{E}\left[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\|f(X_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\right]\right)^{\alpha}\textnormal{for all $f\in\mathscr{F}$}.

(4.1)

Here, the expectation is with respect to
\mathsf{P}_{X}
, the joint law of
X_{0:T-1}
.

Condition (
4.1
)
interpolates between boundedness and small-ball behavior. Indeed, if the class
\mathscr{F}
is
B
-bounded, then it satisfies trajectory
(B^{2},1)
-hypercontractivity trivially.
On the other hand, for
\alpha=2
, (
4.1
) asks that

\|f\|_{L^{4}}\leq C^{1/4}\|f\|_{L^{2}}
for trajectory-wise
L^{p}
-norms; by the Paley-Zygmund inequality, this
implies that
a small-ball condition holds. Moreover, if for some
\varepsilon\in(0,2)
, the trajectory-wise
L^{2}
and
L^{2+\varepsilon}
norms are equivalent on
\mathscr{F}
, then
Proposition

6.2

(
Section

6
) shows that the condition holds for a nontrivial
\alpha=1+\varepsilon/2\in(1,2)
.
More examples are given in
Section

6
.

Our main results assume that
(\mathscr{F}_{\star},\mathsf{P}_{X})

(or a particular subset of
\mathscr{F}_{\star}
) satisfies the trajectory

(C,\alpha)
-hypercontractivity condition with

\alpha>1
, which
we refer to as the
hypercontractive
regime.
The condition
\alpha>1
is required in our analysis
for the lower order excess risk term
to not depend on the mixing-time.
Our results instantiated for the
\alpha=1
case
directly correspond to existing work by
Ziemann et al. 2022
, and
exhibit a lower order term that depends on the mixing-time.

Ergodicity via the dependency matrix

We now state the main definition we use to measure
the stochastic dependency of a process.
Recall that for two measures
\mu,\nu
on the same measurable space
with
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{A}
,
the total-variation norm is defined as
\|\mu-\nu\|_{\mathsf{TV}}\triangleq\sup_{A\in\mathcal{A}}|\mu(A)-\nu(A)|
.

Definition 4.2

(Dependency matrix,
Samson 2000
)
.

The
dependency matrix

of a process
\{Z_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T-1}
with distribution
\mathsf{P}_{Z}
is the (upper-triangular) matrix
\Gamma_{\mathsf{dep}}(\mathsf{P}_{Z})=\{\Gamma_{ij}\}_{i,j=0}^{T-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times T}
defined as follows.
Let
\mathcal{Z}_{0:i}
denote the
\sigma
-algebra
generated by
\{Z_{t}\}_{t=0}^{i}
.
For indices
i<j
, let

\displaystyle\Gamma_{ij}=\sqrt{2\sup_{A\in\mathcal{Z}_{0:i}}\|\mathsf{P}_{Z_{j:T-1}}(\cdot\mid A)-\mathsf{P}_{Z_{j:T-1}}\|_{\mathsf{TV}}}.

(4.2)

For the remaining indices
i\geq j
, let

\Gamma_{ii}=1
and
\Gamma_{ij}=0
when
i>j
(below the diagonal).

Given the dependency matrix from
Definition

4.2
,
we measure the dependency of
the process
\mathsf{P}_{X}
by the quantity

\|\Gamma_{\mathsf{dep}}(\mathsf{P}_{X})\|_{\mathsf{op}}
. Notice that this quantity always satisfies

1\leq\|\Gamma_{\mathsf{dep}}(\mathsf{P}_{X})\|_{\mathsf{op}}\lesssim T
.
The lower bound indicates that the process
\mathsf{P}_{X}
is independent
across time. The upper bound indicates that the process is fully
dependent, e.g.,
X_{t+1}=X_{t}
for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
.

Our results apply to cases where
\|\Gamma_{\mathsf{dep}}(\mathsf{P}_{X})\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}

grows sub-linearly in
T
– the exact requirement depends on the specific function class
\mathscr{F}
.
If the process
\{X_{t}\}
is geometrically
\phi
-mixing, then
\|\Gamma_{\mathsf{dep}}(\mathsf{P}_{X})\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}

is upper bounded by a constant that depends on the
mixing-time of the process, and is
independent of
T

(
Samson 2000
, Section 2)
. Other examples, such as processes satisfying Doeblin’s condition
(
Meyn and Tweedie 1993
)
, are given in
Samson 2000
. When
\{X_{t}\}
is a stationary time-homogenous Markov chain
with invariant distribution
\pi
, the coefficients
\Gamma_{ij}
simplify to

\Gamma_{ij}^{2}=2\sup_{A\in\mathcal{X}_{\infty}}\|\mathsf{P}_{X_{j-i}}(\cdot\mid A)-\pi\|_{\mathsf{TV}}

for indices
j>i
,
where
\mathcal{X}_{\infty}
is the
\sigma
-algebra generated
by
X_{\infty}\sim\pi
(cf.
Proposition

D.1
).
Hence, the requirement

\|\Gamma_{\mathsf{dep}}(\mathsf{P}_{X})\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}\lesssim T^{\beta}
for
\beta\in(0,1)

then corresponds to

\sup_{A\in\mathcal{X}_{\infty}}\|\mathsf{P}_{X_{t}}(\cdot\mid A)-\pi\|_{\mathsf{TV}}\lesssim 1/t^{1-\beta}
for
t\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
.

Jarner and Roberts 2002
give various examples and conditions to check polynomial convergence rates for Markov chains. We also provide further means to verify
\|\Gamma_{\mathsf{dep}}(\mathsf{P}_{X})\|_{\mathsf{op}}=O(1)
in
Appendix

D
and
Appendix

E
.

4.2
Learning with little mixing

A key quantity appearing in our bounds is
a martingale variant of the notion of Gaussian complexity.

Definition 4.3

(Martingale offset complexity, cf.
Liang et al. 2015
,
Ziemann et al. 2022
)
.

For the regression problem (
1.1
), the martingale offset complexity of a function space
\mathscr{F}
is given by:

\displaystyle\mathsf{M}_{T}(\mathscr{F})\triangleq\sup_{f\in\mathscr{F}}\left\{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}4\langle W_{t},f(X_{t})\rangle-\|f(X_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\right\}.

(4.3)

Recall that
\mathscr{F}_{\star}=\mathscr{F}-\{f_{\star}\}
is the centered
function class
and
\partial B(r)=\{f\in\mathscr{F}_{\star}\mid\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}}=r\}
is the boundary of the
L^{2}
ball
B(r)
.
The following theorem is the main result of this paper.

Theorem 4.1
.

Fix
B>0
,

C:(0,B]\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
,

\alpha\in[1,2]
, and

r\in(0,B]
.
Suppose that
\mathscr{F}_{\star}
is star-shaped and
B
-bounded.
Let
\mathscr{F}_{r}\subset\mathscr{F}_{\star}
be a
r/\sqrt{8}

5
Proof techniques

In this section, we highlight the main ideas behind the proof of
Theorem

4.1
. The full details
can be found in the appendix.
We start with a key insight from
Mendelson 2014
: establishing a one-sided inequality between the empirical versus
true risk is substantially easier than the corresponding two-sided inequality.
Recall that the closed ball of radius
r
is

B(r)=\left\{f\in\mathscr{F}_{\star}\,\Big|\,\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbf{E}\|f(X_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\leq r^{2}\right\}
.
We identify conditions which depend mildly on
\|\Gamma_{\mathsf{dep}}(\mathsf{P}_{X})\|_{\mathsf{op}}
, so that with high probability we have:

\forall f\in\mathscr{F}_{\star}\setminus B(r),\>\>\mathbf{E}\left[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\lVert f(X_{t})\rVert_{2}^{2}\right]\lesssim\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\lVert f(X_{t})\rVert_{2}^{2}.

(5.1)

Once this
lower isometry
condition (
5.1
) holds, we bound the empirical excess risk, the RHS of inequality (
5.1
), by a version of the basic inequality of least squares
(
Liang et al. 2015
;
Ziemann et al. 2022
)
. This leads to an upper bound of the empirical excess risk by the
martingale complexity term (
Definition

4.3
).
As our innovation mainly lies in establishing the lower isometry
condition (
5.1
), we focus on this component for the remainder of the proof outline.

Lower isometry

The key tool we use is
the following exponential inequality, which
controls the lower tail of sums of non-negative
dependent random variables via the dependency matrix
\Gamma_{\mathsf{dep}}(\mathsf{P}_{X})
.

Theorem 5.1

(
Samson 2000
)
.

Let
g:\mathsf{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be non-negative. Then for any
\lambda>0

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\exp\left(-\lambda\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}g(X_{t})\right)\leq\exp\left(-\lambda\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbf{E}g(X_{t})+\frac{\lambda^{2}\|\Gamma_{\mathsf{dep}}(\mathsf{P}_{X})\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbf{E}g^{2}(X_{t})}{2}\right).

(5.2)

We note that

Samson 2000
actually proves a
much stronger statement than
Theorem

5.1
(a Talagrand-style
uniform concentration inequality),
from which
Theorem

5.1
is a byproduct.
With
Theorem

5.1
in hand,
the following

allows us to relate
hypercontractivity to lower isometry.

Proposition 5.1
.

Fix
C>0
and
\alpha\in[1,2]
.
Let

be a non-negative function satisfying

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}g^{2}(X_{t})\right]\leq C\left(\mathbf{E}\left[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}g(X_{t})\right]\right)^{\alpha}.

(5.3)

Then we have:

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left(\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}g(X_{t})\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbf{E}g(X_{t})\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{T}{8C\|\Gamma_{\mathsf{dep}}(\mathsf{P}_{X})\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbf{E}g(X_{t})\right)^{2-\alpha}\right).

Now fix an
f\in\mathscr{F}_{\star}\setminus B(r)
,
and put
g(x)=\lVert f(x)\rVert_{2}^{2}
.
Substituting

yields the trajectory hypercontractivity condition (

)
in

.
Thus,

.
It turns out that it suffices to instead cover the boundary

is star-shaped.
Carrying out these details leads

Theorem 5.2
.

Fix constants
\alpha\in[1,2]
and

. Let
\mathscr{F}_{\star}
be star-shaped, and suppose that there exists a

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left(\exists f\in\mathscr{F}_{\star}\setminus B(r)\>\Bigg|\>\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\|f(X_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\leq\mathbf{E}\frac{1}{8T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\|f(X_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\right)\leq|\mathscr{F}_{r}|\exp\left(\frac{-Tr^{4-2\alpha}}{8C\|\Gamma_{\mathsf{dep}}(\mathsf{P}_{X})\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}}\right).

5.1
Handling unbounded trajectories

Our main result
Theorem

4.1
requires boundedness
of both the hypothesis class
\mathscr{F}
and the covariate
process
\{X_{t}\}
to hold. However, when this does not hold,

Theorem

4.1
can often still be applied via a
careful truncation argument.
In this section, we outline the key ideas of this
argument, with the full details given in
Appendix

E
.

For concreteness, let us consider a Markovian process driven by Gaussian noise.
Let
\{W_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
and
\{W^{\prime}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
be sequences of iid
N(0,I)
vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathsf{X}}}
.
Fix a dynamics function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathsf{X}}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathsf{X}}}

and truncation radius
R>0
. Define the truncated noise process
\{\bar{W}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
as
\bar{W}_{t}\triangleq W^{\prime}_{t}\mathbf{1}\{\lVert W^{\prime}_{t}\rVert_{2}\leq R\}
, and
denote the original process and its truncated process by:

\displaystyle X_{t+1}

\displaystyle=f(X_{t})+HW_{t},\>\>X_{0}=HW_{0},

(original process)

(5.4a)

\displaystyle\bar{X}_{t+1}

\displaystyle=f(\bar{X}_{t})+H\bar{W}_{t},\>\>\bar{X}_{0}=H\bar{W}_{0}.

(truncated process)

(5.4b)

Setting
R
appropriately, it is clear that
the original process (
5.4a
)
coincides with the truncated process (
5.4b
)
with high probability by standard Gaussian concentration inequalities.
Furthermore, the truncated noise process
\{H\bar{W}_{t}\}
remains a martingale difference sequence due to the symmetry of the truncation.
Additionally, since
\{H\bar{W}_{t}\}
is bounded,
if
f
is appropriately Lypaunov stable then the process
\{\bar{X}_{t}\}
becomes bounded.
In turn any class
\mathscr{F}
containing continuous functions is bounded as well on (

).
Hence, the LSE
\hat{f}
on (
5.4a
)
can be controlled by the LSE
\bar{f}
on (
5.4b
).

So far, this is a straightforward reduction. However, a subtle point arises in applying

4.1
to the LSE

on (
5.4b
): the dependency
matrix
\Gamma_{\mathsf{dep}}
now involves the truncated process (
5.4b
)
instead of the original process (
5.4a
).
This is actually
necessary
for
this strategy to work, as the supremum in the dependency matrix coefficients (
4.2
)
is now over the truncated process
\{\bar{X}_{t}\}
, instead of the original process
\{X_{t}\}
which is unbounded.
However, there is a trade-off, as bounding the coefficients
for
\{\bar{X}_{t}\}
is generally more complex
than for
\{X_{t}\}
.
4
4

4

The clearest
example of this is when the dynamics function

is linear:
in this case,
\{X_{t}\}
is jointly Gaussian (and hence (

) can
be bounded by closed-form expressions), whereas
\{\bar{X}_{t}\}
is not due
to the truncation operator.

Nevertheless, a coupling argument
allows us to switch back to bounding the dependency matrix coefficients for
\{X_{t}\}
, but crucially keep the supremum over the truncated

substantially broadens the scope of

4.1
without any modification to the proof.

Examples of trajectory hypercontractivity

In this section, we detail a few examples of
trajectory hypercontractivity.
Let us begin by considering the simplest possible example: a finite hypothesis class. Let
|\mathscr{F}|<\infty
. Define for any fixed
f\in\mathscr{F}_{\star}
the
constant

c_{f}\triangleq\mathbf{E}\left[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\|f(X_{t})\|^{4}_{2}\right]/\left(\mathbf{E}\left[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\|f(X_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\right]\right)^{2}
,
where the ratio
0/0
is taken to be
1
.
Then the class
\mathscr{F}_{\star}
is trajectory
(\max_{f\in\mathscr{F}_{\star}}c_{f},2)
-hypercontractive.

Similarly, processes evolving on a finite state space can
also be verified to be hypercontractive.

Proposition 6.1
.

Fix a
\underline{\mu}>0
.
Let
\{\mu_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T-1}
denote the marginal distributions of
\mathsf{P}_{X}
.
Suppose that the
\mu_{t}
’s all share a common support
of a finite set of atoms

\{\psi_{1},\dots,\psi_{K}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathsf{X}}}
,
and that
\min_{0\leq t\leq T-1}\min_{1\leq k\leq K}\mu_{t}(\psi_{k})\geq\underline{\mu}
.
For any class of functions
\mathscr{F}
mapping

\{\psi_{1},\dots,\psi_{K}\}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathsf{Y}}}
, we have that

\mathscr{F}
satisfies the trajectory
(1/\underline{\mu},2)
-hypercontractivity condition.

We remark that when

\mathsf{P}_{X}
is an aperiodic and irreducible Markov chain over
a finite state space, the condition
\underline{\mu}>0
is always
valid even as
T\to\infty

(
Levin and Peres 2008
)
.
In this case, our findings are related to
Wolfer and Kontorovich 2021
, who show that in the high accuracy regime (i.e., after a burn-in time), the minimax rate of estimating the transition probabilities of such a chain is not affected by the mixing time (in their case the pseudo-spectral gap).

The examples considered thus far rely on the fact that under a certain degree of finiteness, the fourth and second moment can be made uniformly equivalent. The next proposition relaxes this assumption.
Namely, if for some
\varepsilon\in(0,2]
the
L^{2}
and
L^{2+\varepsilon}
norms are equivalent on a bounded class
\mathscr{F}
, this class then satisfies a nontrivial hypercontractivity constant,
\alpha>1
(cf.
Mendelson 2017
).

Proposition 6.2
.

Fix
\varepsilon\in(0,2]
and
c>0
. Suppose that
\mathscr{F}
is
B
-bounded and that
\|f\|_{L^{2+\varepsilon}}\leq c\|f\|_{L^{2}}
for all
f\in\mathscr{F}
. Then
\mathscr{F}
satisfies the trajectory
(B^{2-\varepsilon}c^{2+\varepsilon},1+\varepsilon/2)
-hypercontractivity condition.

Next, we show that for processes
\{X_{t}\}
which converge fast enough to a stationary distribution, it suffices to verify the hypercontractivity condition
only over the stationary distribution.
This mimics existing results in iid learning,
where hypercontractivity is assumed over the covariate distribution
(
Liang et al. 2015
;
Mendelson 2014
)
.
We first recall the definition of the
\chi^{2}
divergence between two measures.
Let
\mu
and
\nu
be two measures over the same probability space,
and suppose that
\mu
is absolutely continuous w.r.t.
\nu
.
The
\chi^{2}(\mu,\nu)
divergence is defined as

\chi^{2}(\mu,\nu)\triangleq\mathbf{E}_{\nu}\left[\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\nu}-1\right)^{2}\right]
.

Proposition 6.3
.

Fix positive
r
,
C_{\chi^{2}}
,
C_{\mathsf{TV}}
, and
C_{8\to 2}
.
Suppose that the process
\{X_{t}\}
has
a stationary distribution
\pi
.
Let
\{\mu_{t}\}
denote the marginal distributions of

\{X_{t}\}
, and suppose that the marginals
\{\mu_{t}\}
are absolutely continuous w.r.t.
\pi
.
Assume the process is ergodic in the sense that:

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\chi^{2}(\mu_{t},\pi)\leq C_{\chi^{2}},\>\>\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\|\mu_{t}-\pi\|_{\mathsf{TV}}\leq C_{\mathsf{TV}}r^{2}.

(6.1)

Suppose also that for all
f\in\mathscr{F}_{\star}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{\pi}\lVert f(X)\rVert_{2}^{8}\leq C_{8\rightarrow 2}(\mathbf{E}_{\pi}\lVert f(X)\rVert_{2}^{2})^{4}.

(6.2)

The set
\partial B(r)

satisfies

(C,2)
-trajectory hypercontractivity
with

Let us discuss the ergodicity conditions in

The condition
\sup_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\chi^{2}(\mu_{t},\pi)<\infty

To illustrate this point, suppose that
\{X_{t}\}
are regularly spaced samples in time
from the Itô stochastic differential equation:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}Z_{t}=f(Z_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{d}B_{t},

where
(B_{t})
is standard Brownian motion in
\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathsf{X}}}
.
Assume the process
(Z_{t})
admits a stationary distribution
\pi
, and
let
\rho_{t}
denote the measure of
Z_{t}
at time
t
.
A standard calculation
(
Bakry et al. 2014
, Theorem 4.2.5)
shows that
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\chi^{2}(\rho_{t},\pi)=-2\mathbf{E}_{\pi}\left\lVert\nabla\left(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\pi}\right)\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\leq 0
, and hence
\sup_{t\geq 0}\chi^{2}(\rho_{t},\pi)\leq\chi^{2}(\rho_{0},\pi)
.
Thus, as long as the initial measure
\rho_{0}
has finite divergence with
\pi
, then
this condition holds.
One caveat is that
\chi^{2}(\rho_{0},\pi)
can scale as

e^{d_{\mathsf{X}}}
, resulting in a hypercontractivity constant that scales
exponentially in dimension. This however only affects the burn-in time and not the final rate.

The second condition in (
6.1
) is

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\|\mu_{t}-\pi\|_{\mathsf{TV}}\lesssim r^{2}
.
A typical setting is

r^{2}\asymp 1/T^{\beta}
for some

\beta\in(0,1]
, where
\beta
is dictated by the
function class
\mathscr{F}
. Hence, this requirement reads:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\|\mu_{t}-\pi\|_{\mathsf{TV}}\lesssim\frac{1}{T^{\beta}}\Longleftrightarrow\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\|\mu_{t}-\pi\|_{\mathsf{TV}}\lesssim T^{1-\beta}.

Therefore, the setting of
\beta
determines the level of ergodicty
required. For example, if
\beta=1
(which corresponds to the parametric function case), then this condition necessitates
geometric ergodicity, since it requires that

\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\|\mu_{t}-\pi\|_{\mathsf{TV}}=O(1)
.
On the other hand, suppose that
\beta\in(0,1)
.
Then this condition is satisfied if

\|\mu_{t}-\pi\|_{\mathsf{TV}}\lesssim 1/t^{\beta}
, allowing for slower mixing rates.

6.1
Ellipsoids in
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})

Given that equivalence of norms is typically a finite-dimensional phenomenon, one may wonder whether examples of
hypercontractivity exist in an infinite-dimensional setting.
Here we show that such examples are actually rather abundant. The key is that hypercontractivity need only be satisfied on an
\varepsilon
-cover of
\mathscr{F}_{\star}
.
As discussed above, every finite hypothesis class (and thus every finite cover) is automatically
(C,2)
-hypercontractive for some
C>0
. The issue is to ensure that this constant does not grow too fast as one refines the cover. The next result shows that the growth can be controlled for
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})
ellipsoids of orthogonal expansions. By Mercer’s theorem, these ellipsoids correspond to unit balls in reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces
(
Wainwright 2019
, Corollary 12.26)
.

Proposition 6.4
.

Fix positive constants
\beta
,
B
,
K
, and
q
. Fix a base measure
\lambda
on
\mathsf{X}
and suppose that
\{\phi_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}}
is an orthonormal system in
L^{2}(\lambda)
satisfying
\|\phi_{n}\|_{\infty}\leq Bn^{q},\,\forall n\in\mathbb{N}
. Suppose
\mu_{j}\leq e^{-2\beta j}
and define the ellipsoid:

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}\triangleq\left\{f=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\theta_{j}\phi_{j}\Bigg|\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{\theta_{j}^{2}}{\mu_{j}}\leq 1\right\}.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
, and let
m_{\varepsilon}
denote the smallest positive integer solution to
m\geq\frac{2}{\beta}\left|\log\left(\frac{8B}{\beta\varepsilon}\right)\right|
subject to
\frac{m}{\log m}\geq\frac{q}{\beta}
.
Let
P\subset\mathscr{P}
be an arbitrary subset.
There exists an
\varepsilon
-cover
P_{\varepsilon}
of
P
in the
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\infty}
-norm satisfying:

\displaystyle\log{|P_{\varepsilon}|}\leq m_{\varepsilon}\log\left(1+\frac{8Bm^{q}_{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon}\right).

Furthermore, let
\{\mu_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T-1}
be the marginal distributions of
\mathsf{P}_{X}
, and suppose that
the
\mu_{t}
’s and
\lambda
are all mutually absolutely continuous. Assume that

\max_{0\leq t\leq T-1}\max\left\{\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}}{\mathrm{d}\lambda},\frac{\mathrm{d}\lambda}{\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}}\right\}\leq K
.
Then, as long as
\varepsilon\leq\inf_{f\in P}\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathsf{P}_{X})}
,
then

(P_{\varepsilon},\mathsf{P}_{X})
is trajectory
(C_{\varepsilon},2)
-hypercontractive with

C_{\varepsilon}=(1+7K^{3}B^{4}m_{\varepsilon}^{4q+2})
.

Proposition

6.4
states that
when
\mathscr{F}_{\star}\subseteq\mathscr{P}
, then

(\partial B(r),\mathsf{P}_{X})
is
(C(r),2)
-hypercontractive where
C(r)=C_{r}
only grows
poly-logarithmically
in
1/r
.
In particular, this example verifies the assumptions of
Corollary

4.2
.

7
System identification in parametric classes

To demonstrate the sharpness of
our main result, we instantiate
Theorem

4.1
on two
parametric system identification problems which have received recent
attention in the literature:
linear dynamical systems (LDS) and generalized linear model (GLM)
dynamics.

7.1
Linear dynamical systems

Consider the setting where the process
\{X_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
is
described by a linear dynamical system:

\displaystyle X_{t+1}=A_{\star}X_{t}+HV_{t},\>\>X_{0}=HV_{0},\>\>V_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathsf{V}}},\>\>V_{t}\sim N(0,I),\>\>V_{t}\perp V_{t^{\prime}}\>\forall\,t\neq t^{\prime}.

(7.1)

In this setting, the system identification problem is to
recover the dynamics matrix
A_{\star}
from

\{X_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T-1}
evolving according to (
7.1
).
We derive rates for recovering
A_{\star}
by first deriving an
excess risk bound on the least-squares estimator via
Theorem

4.1
, and then
converting the risk bound to a parameter error bound.
Since
Theorem

4.1
relies on the process being ergodic, we consider the case when
A_{\star}
is stable.
We start by stating a few standard definitions.

Definition 7.1
.

Fix a
k\in\{1,\dots,d_{\mathsf{X}}\}
.
The pair
(A,H)
is
k
-step controllable
if

\displaystyle\rank\left(\begin{bmatrix}H&AH&A^{2}H&\dots&A^{k-1}H\end{bmatrix}\right)=d_{\mathsf{X}}.

For
t\in\mathbb{N}
, let the
t
-step
controllability gramian
be defined as
\Gamma_{t}\triangleq\sum_{k=0}^{t}A^{k}HH^{\mathsf{T}}(A^{k})^{\mathsf{T}}
.
Since the noise in (
7.1
)
serves as the “control” in this setting, the
controllability gramian also coincides with the covariance at time
t
, i.e.,
\mathbf{E}[X_{t}X_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}]=\Gamma_{t}
.

Definition 7.2
.

Fix a
\tau\geq 1
and
\rho\in(0,1)
.
A matrix
A
is called
(\tau,\rho)
-stable

if for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
we have
\|A^{k}\|_{\mathsf{op}}\leq\tau\rho^{k}
.

With these definitions in place, we now state our result for
linear dynamical system.

Theorem 7.1
.

Suppose that the matrix
A_{\star}
in (
7.1
) is
(\tau,\rho)
-stable (cf.
Definition

7.2
), and that the pair

(A_{\star},H)
is
\kappa
-step controllable (cf.
Definition

7.1
).
Suppose also that
\lVert A_{\star}\rVert_{F}\leq B

for some
B\geq 1
.
Consider the linear hypothesis class and true regression function:

\displaystyle\mathscr{F}\triangleq\{f(x)=Ax\mid A\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathsf{X}}\times d_{\mathsf{X}}},\>\>\|A\|_{F}\leq B\},\>\>f_{\star}(x)=A_{\star}x.

(7.2)

Suppose that model (
1.1
)
follows the process described in (
7.1
)
with
Y_{t}=X_{t+1}
.
There exists
T_{0}
such that the LSE with hypothesis class
\mathscr{F}
achieves for all
T\geq T_{0}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\|\widehat{f}-f_{\star}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq 8\mathbf{E}\mathsf{M}_{T}(\mathscr{F}_{\star})+\frac{4\|H\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}d_{\mathsf{X}}^{2}}{T}.

(7.3)

Furthermore, for a universal positive constant
c_{0}
,
T_{0}
may be chosen as:

\displaystyle T_{0}=c_{0}\frac{\tau^{4}\|H\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{4}d_{\mathsf{X}}^{2}}{(1-\rho)^{2}\lambda_{\min}(\Gamma_{\kappa-1})^{2}}\left[\kappa^{2}\vee\frac{1}{(1-\rho)^{2}}\right]\mathrm{polylog}\left(B,d_{\mathsf{X}},\tau,\|H\|_{\mathsf{op}},\frac{1}{\lambda_{\min}(\Gamma_{\kappa-1})},\frac{1}{1-\rho},\right).

(7.4)

We first discuss the rate (
7.3
) prescribed by

Theorem

7.1
.
A simple computation shows that the martingale complexity
\mathbf{E}\mathsf{M}_{T}(\mathscr{F}_{\star})
can be upper bounded by

1/T
times the self-normalized martingale term which typically
appears in the analysis of least-squares
(
Abbasi-Yadkori et al. 2011
)
.
Specifically, when the empirical covariance matrix
\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}X_{t}X_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}
is invertible:

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\mathsf{M}_{T}(\mathscr{F}_{\star})\leq\frac{4}{T}\mathbf{E}\left\lVert\left(\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}X_{t}X_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}\right)^{-1/2}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}X_{t}V_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}H^{\mathsf{T}}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}.

A sharp analysis of this self-normalized martingale term
(
Tu et al. 2022
, Lemma 4.1)
shows that
\mathbf{E}\mathsf{M}_{T}(\mathscr{F}_{\star})\lesssim\frac{\|H\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}d_{\mathsf{X}}^{2}}{T}
, and hence (
7.3
) yields the minimax optimal rate
up to constant factors after a polynomial burn-in time.
5
5

5

While the burn-in time is polynomial in the problem constants listed in (
7.4
),
Tsiamis and Pappas 2021

show that these constants (specifically
1/\lambda_{\min}(\Gamma_{\kappa-1})
) can scale
exponentially in
\kappa
, the controllability index of the system.

This is unlike the chaining bound (
4.5
)
which yields extra logarithmic factors
(see e.g.
Ziemann et al. 2022
, Lemma 4)
.
Note that the burn-in time of
\tilde{O}(d_{\mathsf{X}}^{2})

given by our result is sub-optimal by a factor of
d_{\mathsf{X}}
. This extra factor
comes from the union bound over a Frobenius norm ball of

d_{\mathsf{X}}\times d_{\mathsf{X}}
matrices in
Theorem

4.1
.

To convert (
7.3
)
into a parameter recovery bound, we simply lower bound the excess risk:

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\lVert\hat{f}-f_{\star}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\geq\mathbf{E}\lVert\hat{A}-A_{\star}\rVert_{F}^{2}\lambda_{\min}(\bar{\Gamma}_{T})\Longrightarrow\mathbf{E}\lVert\hat{A}-A_{\star}\rVert_{F}^{2}\lesssim\frac{\|H\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}d_{\mathsf{X}}^{2}}{T\lambda_{\min}(\bar{\Gamma}_{T})},

(7.5)

where
\bar{\Gamma}_{T}\triangleq\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbf{E}[X_{t}X_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}]
is the average covariance matrix.
The rate (
7.5
) recovers, after the polynomial burn-in time, existing results
(
Simchowitz et al. 2018
;
Sarkar and Rakhlin 2019
;
Jedra and Proutiere 2020
;
Tu et al. 2022
)
for stable systems, with a few caveats.
First, most of the existing
results are given in operator instead of Frobenius norm.
We ignore this issue, since the only difference is the extra
unavoidable factor of
d_{\mathsf{X}}
in the rate for the Frobenius norm
compared to the operator norm rate.
Second,
since
Theorem

4.1
ultimately relies on some degree of ergodicity for the covariate process
\{X_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
, we cannot handle the marginally stable case (where
A_{\star}
is allowed to have spectral radius equal to one) as in
Simchowitz et al. 2018
;
Sarkar and Rakhlin 2019
;
Tu et al. 2022
,
nor the unstable case as in
Faradonbeh et al. 2018
;
Sarkar and Rakhlin 2019
.

We conclude with a short discussion on the proof of
Theorem

7.1
.
As the LDS process (
7.1
) is unbounded, we
use the truncation argument outlined in
Section

5.1

so that
Theorem

4.1
still applies.
Furthermore, since the process (
7.1
) is jointly Gaussian,
the dependency matrix coefficients are simple to bound, resulting
in polynomial rates (
7.4
) for the burn-in time.
A much wider variety of non-Gaussian noise distributions
can be handled via ergodic theory for Markov chains
(see e.g.
Meyn and Tweedie 1993
, Chapter 15)
. While these results typically do not
offer explicit expressions for the mixing coefficients,
both
Douc et al. 2004
and
Hairer and Mattingly 2011

provide a path forward for deriving explicit bounds.
We however omit these calculations in the interest of simplicity.

7.2
Generalized linear models

We next consider the following non-linear dynamical system:

\displaystyle X_{t+1}=\sigma(A_{\star}X_{t})+HV_{t},\>\>X_{0}=HV_{0},\>\>V_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathsf{X}}},\>\>V_{t}\sim N(0,I),\>\>V_{t}\perp V_{t^{\prime}}\>\forall\,t\neq t^{\prime}.

(7.6)

Here,
A_{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathsf{X}}\times d_{\mathsf{X}}}
is the dynamics matrix and

\sigma:\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathsf{X}}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathsf{X}}}
is a coordinate wise link function.
The notation
\sigma
will also be overloaded to refer to the individual coordinate function mapping
\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
.
We study the system identification problem where the link function
\sigma
is assumed to be known, but the dynamics matrix
A_{\star}

is unknown and to be recovered from
\{X_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T-1}
.
We will apply
Theorem

4.1
to derive a nearly optimal excess risk bound for the LSE on this problem in the stable case.

We start by stating a few assumptions that are again standard in the literature.

Assumption 7.1
.

Suppose that
A_{\star}
,
H
, and
\sigma
from the GLM process (
7.6
) satisfy:

1.

(One-step controllability). The matrix
H\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathsf{X}}\times d_{\mathsf{X}}}
is full rank.

2.

(Link function regularity). The link function
\sigma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}

is
1
-Lipschitz, satisfies
\phi(0)=0
,
and there exists a
\zeta\in(0,1]
such that

|\sigma(x)-\sigma(y)|\geq\zeta|x-y|
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
.

3.

(Lyapunov stability). There exists a positive definite diagonal matrix
P_{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathsf{X}}\times d_{\mathsf{X}}}
satisfying
P_{\star}\succcurlyeq I
and a
\rho\in(0,1)
such that
A_{\star}^{\mathsf{T}}P_{\star}A_{\star}\preccurlyeq\rho P_{\star}
.

Several remarks on
7.1
are in order.
First, the rank condition on
H
ensures that the noise process
\{HV_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
is non-degenerate. Viewing (
7.6
)
as a control system mapping

\{V_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}\mapsto\{X_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
, this condition ensures that this system is one-step controllable.
Next, the link function assumption is standard in the literature (see e.g.
Sattar and Oymak 2020
;
Foster et al. 2020
;
Kowshik et al. 2021
).
The expansiveness condition
|\sigma(x)-\sigma(y)|\geq\zeta|x-y|
ensures that the link function is increasing at a uniform
rate.
For efficient parameter recovery, some extra assumption other than
Lipschitzness and monotonicity is needed
(
Kowshik et al. 2021
, Theorem 4)
, and expansiveness yields a sufficient
condition. However, for excess risk, it is unclear if any extra requirements are necessary. We leave resolving this issue
to future work.
Finally, the Lyapunov stability condition is due to

Foster et al. 2020
, and yields a certificate
for global exponential stability (GES) to the origin.
It is weaker than requiring that
\|A_{\star}\|_{\mathsf{op}}<1
, which amounts to taking
P_{\star}=I
.
The assumption
P_{\star}\succcurlyeq I
is without loss of generality by rescaling
P_{\star}
.

With our assumptions in place, we are ready to state our main result concerning the excess risk for the LSE applied to
the process (
7.6
).

Theorem 7.2
.

Suppose the model (
1.1
) follows the process
described in (
7.6
) with
Y_{t}=X_{t+1}
.
Assume that the process (
7.6
) satisfies

7.1
. Fix a
B\geq 1
, and suppose that

\lVert A_{\star}\rVert_{F}\leq B
.
Consider the hypothesis class and true regression function:

\displaystyle\mathscr{F}\triangleq\{f(x)=\sigma(Ax)\mid A\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathsf{X}}\times d_{\mathsf{X}}},\>\>\|A\|_{F}\leq B\},\>\>f_{\star}(x)=\sigma(A_{\star}x).

(7.7)

There exists a
T_{0}

and a universal positive constant
c_{0}

such that the LSE with hypothesis class
\mathscr{F}
achieves for all
T\geq T_{0}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\|\widehat{f}-f_{\star}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq c_{0}\frac{\|H\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}d_{\mathsf{X}}^{2}}{T}\log\left(\max\left\{T,B,d_{\mathsf{X}},\|P_{\star}\|_{\mathsf{op}},\|H\|_{\mathsf{op}},\frac{1}{1-\rho}\right\}\right).

(7.8)

Furthermore, for a universal constant
c_{1}>0
, we may choose
T_{0}
as:

\displaystyle T_{0}=c_{1}\max\frac{\|P_{\star}\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}\mathrm{cond}(H)^{4}d_{\mathsf{X}}^{4}}{\zeta^{4}(1-\rho)^{6}}\mathrm{polylog}\left(B,d_{\mathsf{X}},\|P_{\star}\|_{\mathsf{op}},\mathrm{cond}(H),\frac{1}{\zeta},\frac{1}{1-\rho}\right).

(7.9)

Theorem

7.2
states that after a polynomial burn-in time
(which scales quite sub-optimally as
\tilde{O}(d_{\mathsf{X}}^{4})

in the dimension), the excess risk
scales as the minimax rate
\|H\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}d_{\mathsf{X}}^{2}/T
times a logarithmic factor
of various problem constants.
To the best of our knowledge, this is the sharpest excess risk bound for this problem
in the literature and is nearly minimax optimal.
As noted previously, the logarithmic factor enters via the chaining inequality (
4.5
) when bounding the martingale offset complexity.
We leave to future work
a more refined analysis that removes this logarithmic dependence,
and also improves the polynomial dependence of
T_{0}
on
d_{\mathsf{X}}
.
The extra
d_{\mathsf{X}}^{2}
factor in (
7.9
)
over the LDS burn-in time (
7.4
) comes from our
analysis of the trajectory hypercontractivity constant
for this problem, and should be removable.

Much like in (
7.5
), we can use the
link function expansiveness in
7.1
to
convert the excess risk bound
(
7.8
) to a parameter recovery rate:

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\lVert\hat{A}-A_{\star}\rVert_{F}^{2}\leq\tilde{O}(1)\frac{\|H\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}d_{\mathsf{X}}^{2}}{\zeta^{2}T\lambda_{\min}(\bar{\Gamma}_{T})},

(7.10)

where again
\bar{\Gamma}_{T}\triangleq\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbf{E}[X_{t}X_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}]
is the average covariance matrix of the GLM process (
7.6
).
Note that the one-step controllability assumption in
7.1
ensures
that the covariance matrix
\mathbf{E}[X_{t}X_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}]\succcurlyeq HH^{\mathsf{T}}
is invertible for every
t\in\mathbb{N}
.
A detailed comparison of the excess risk rate (
7.8
) and parameter recovery rate (
7.10
)
with existing bounds in the literature is given in

Section

H.1
.

Let us briefly discuss the proof of
Theorem

7.2
.
As in the LDS case, we use the truncation argument
described in
Section

5.1
that allows us
apply
Theorem

4.1
while still
using bounds on the dependency matrix coefficients of the original unbounded process
(
7.6
).
However, an additional complication arises compared to the LDS case, as the covariates are not jointly Gaussian due to the presence
of the link function.
While at this point we could appeal to ergodic theory,
we instead develop an alternative approach that still allows us to
compute explicit constants.
Building on the work of
Chae and Walker 2020
, we use the smoothness of the Gaussian transition kernel to
upper bound the TV distance by the
1
-Wasserstein distance.
This argument is where our analysis crucially relies on
the non-degeneracy of
H
in
7.1
,
as the transition kernel corresponding
to multiple steps of (
7.6
) is no longer Gaussian.
The
1
-Wasserstein distance is then controlled by using the
incremental stability

(
Tran et al. 2016
)
properties of
the deterministic dynamics
x_{+}=\sigma(A_{\star}x)
.
Since this technique only depends on the GLM dynamics through incremental stability, it is of independent interest as it
applies much more broadly.

8
Numerical experiments

We conduct a simple numerical simulation to illustrate
the phenomenon of learning with little mixing empirically.
We consider system identification of the GLM dynamics described
in
Section

7.2
.

We first describe how the covariate process
\{X_{t}\}
is generated.
We set
d_{\mathsf{X}}=25
.
The true dynamic matrix
A_{\star}
is
randomly sampled from the distribution described in
Section 7 of
Kowshik et al. 2021
.
Specifically,
A_{\star}=U\Sigma U^{\mathsf{T}}
, where

U
is uniform from the Haar measure on the space of
orthonormal
d_{\mathsf{X}}\times d_{\mathsf{X}}
matrices,
and
\Sigma=\textnormal{diag}(\underbrace{\rho,\dots,\rho}_{\lfloor d_{\mathsf{X}}/2\rfloor\textrm{ times}},\rho/3,\dots,\rho/3)
.
We vary
\rho\in\{0.9,0.99\}
for this experiment.
Next, we set the activation function

\sigma
to be the LeakyReLU with slope
0.5
, i.e.,

\sigma(x)=0.5x\mathbf{1}\{x<0\}+x\mathbf{1}\{x\geq 0\}
.
Observe that these dynamics satisfy

7.1
with
\zeta=0.5
, and
where the Lyapunov matrix
P
can be taken
to be identity, since
\|A_{\star}\|_{\mathsf{op}}=\rho<1
.
Next, we generate
X_{0}\sim N(0,I_{d_{\mathsf{X}}})
,
and
X_{t+1}=\sigma(A_{\star}X_{t})+W_{t}
with
W_{t}\sim N(0,0.01I_{d_{\mathsf{X}}})
and
W_{t}\perp W_{t^{\prime}}
for
t\neq t^{\prime}
.
From this trajectory, the labelled dataset is

\{(X_{t},Y_{t})\}_{t=0}^{T-1}
with

Y_{t}=X_{t+1}
.

To study the effects of the correlation
from a single trajectory
\{X_{t}\}

for learning, we consider the following

independent baseline

motivated by the Ind-Seq-LS baseline
described in
Tu et al. 2022
.
Let
\mu_{t}
denote the marginal distribution of
X_{t}
.
We sample
\bar{X}_{t}\sim\mu_{t}
independently across time,
and sample
\bar{Y_{t}}\mid\bar{X}_{t}
from the conditional distribution

N(\sigma(A_{\star}\bar{X}_{t}),0.01I_{d_{\mathsf{X}}})
;
the labelled dataset is
\{(\bar{X}_{t},\bar{Y}_{t})\}_{t=0}^{T-1}
.
This ensures that the
L^{2}
risk of a fixed hypothesis

f(x)=\sigma(Ax)
is the same under both the independent
baseline and the single trajectory distribution, so that our
experiment singles out the effect of learning
from correlated data.
In practice, each
\bar{X}_{t}
is sampled from a new
independent rollout up to time
t
.

Given a dataset
\{(X_{t},Y_{t})\}_{t=0}^{T-1}
, we
search for the empirical risk minimizer (ERM) of the loss

\displaystyle\hat{A}=\argmin_{A\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathsf{X}}\times d_{\mathsf{X}}}}\left\{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\lVert\sigma(AX_{t})-Y_{t}\rVert_{2}^{2}\right\}

(8.1)

by running
scipy.optimize.minimize
with the

L-BFGS-B
method, using the default linesearch
and termination criteria options.
To calculate the
L^{2}
excess risk

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbf{E}\lVert\sigma(\hat{A}X_{t})-\sigma(A_{\star}X_{t})\rVert_{2}^{2}
of a hypothesis
\hat{A}
,
we draw
1000
new trajectories
and average the excess risk over these trajectories.
The experimental code is implemented with

jax

(

9
Conclusion

We developed a framework for showing
when the mixing-time of the covariates plays a relatively small role in the rate of convergence of the least-squares estimator.
In many situations, after a finite burn-in time, this learning procedure exhibits an excess risk that scales as if all the samples were independent (
Theorem

4.1
).
As a byproduct of our framework, by instantiating our results to system
identification for dynamics with generalized linear model transitions (
Section

7.2
),
we derived the sharpest known excess risk rate for this problem; our rates are nearly minimax optimal after only a polynomial burn-in time.

To arrive at
Theorem

4.1
, we leveraged insights from
Mendelson 2014
via a one-sided concentration inequality (
Theorem

5.2
). As mentioned in
Section

4.1
,
hypercontractivity is closely related to the small-ball condition
(
Mendelson 2014
)
. Such conditions can be understood as quantitative identifiability conditions by providing control of the “version space” (cf.
Mendelson 2014
). Given that identifiability conditions also play a key role in linear system identification—a setting in which a similar phenomenon as studied here had already been reported—this suggests an interesting direction for future work: are such conditions actually necessary for learning with little mixing?

We will introduce the following shorthand.

Before we proceed, we recall the coupling representation of

\displaystyle\|\mu-\nu\|_{\mathsf{TV}}=\inf\{\mathbf{P}(X\neq Y)\mid(X,Y)\text{ is a coupling of }(\mu,\nu)\}.

(D.1)

Proposition D.1
.

Suppose that

is a Markov chain. For any integers

\displaystyle\|\mathsf{P}_{Z_{j:k}}(\cdot\mid Z_{i}=z)-\mathsf{P}_{Z_{j:k}}\|_{\mathsf{TV}}=\|\mathsf{P}_{Z_{j}}(\cdot\mid Z_{i}=z)-\mathsf{P}_{Z_{j}}\|_{\mathsf{TV}}.

Proof.

Let us first prove the upper bound.
Let
(Z_{j},Z^{\prime}_{j})
be a coupling of

(\mathsf{P}_{Z_{j}}(\cdot\mid Z_{i}=z),\mathsf{P}_{Z_{j}})
.
We can construct a coupling

(\bar{Z}_{j:k},\bar{Z}^{\prime}_{j:k})

of
(\mathsf{P}_{Z_{j:k}}(\cdot\mid Z_{i}=z),\mathsf{P}_{Z_{j:k}})

by first setting

,

\bar{Z}^{\prime}_{j}=Z^{\prime}_{j}
, and then evolving the chains
onward via the following process.
If
\bar{Z}_{j}=\bar{Z}^{\prime}_{j}
, we evolve

\bar{Z}^{\prime}_{j+1:k}=\bar{Z}_{j+1:k}
. Otherwise if

, then
we evolve both chains separately.
Observe that
\bar{Z}_{j:k}\neq\bar{Z}^{\prime}_{j:k}

iff

.
Hence

\|\mathsf{P}_{Z_{j:k}}(\cdot\mid Z_{i}=x)-\mathsf{P}_{Z_{j:k}}\|_{\mathsf{TV}}\leq\mathbf{P}(Z_{j}\neq Z^{\prime}_{j})
.
Since the coupling

is arbitrary, taking

D.1
).

We now turn to the lower bound. Let

(Z_{j:k},Z^{\prime}_{j:k})
be a coupling
of
(\mathsf{P}_{Z_{j:k}}(\cdot\mid Z_{i}=z),\mathsf{P}_{Z_{j:k}})
.
Since projection
(Z_{j},Z^{\prime}_{j})
is a coupling

\displaystyle\|\mathsf{P}_{Z_{j}}(\cdot\mid Z_{i}=x)-\mathsf{P}_{Z_{j}}\|_{\mathsf{TV}}\leq\mathbf{P}(Z_{j}\neq Z^{\prime}_{j})\leq\mathbf{P}(Z_{j:k}\neq Z^{\prime}_{j:k}).

Proposition D.2
.

Let
M,N
be two size conforming matrices
with all non-negative entries. Suppose that

M\leq N
, where the inequality holds elementwise.
Then,
\|M\|_{\mathsf{op}}\leq\|N\|_{\mathsf{op}}
.

Proof.

Let
Q
be a matrix with non-negative entries,
and let
q_{i}
denote the rows of
Q
.
The variational form of the operator norm states that
\|Q\|_{\mathsf{op}}=\sup_{\lVert v\rVert_{2}\leq 1}\lVert Qv\rVert_{2}=\sup_{\lVert v\rVert_{2}\leq 1}\sqrt{\sum_{i}\langle q_{i},v\rangle^{2}}
.
Since each

only has non-negative entries,
the supremum must be attained by a vector

with non-negative entries, otherwise flipping the sign of the negative entries in

,
and never decrease the value.

Now let

denote the rows of

, and let
v
be a vector with non-negative entries.
Since

(elementwise), it is clear that
\langle m_{i},v\rangle^{2}\leq\langle n_{i},v\rangle^{2}
.
Hence the claim follows.
∎

Proposition D.3
.

Let

Proposition E.3

8

8

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\exp(\lambda\langle u,\bar{W}\rangle)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}_{\bar{W}}\mathbf{E}_{\varepsilon}\exp(\lambda\varepsilon\langle u,\bar{W}\rangle)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{E}_{\bar{W}}\exp(\lambda^{2}\langle u,\bar{W}\rangle^{2}/2)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\exp(\lambda\langle u,\bar{W}\rangle)

Proposition E.6
.

Let

Fix a

We have that:

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[(\bar{W}_{1:k}^{\mathsf{T}}M\bar{W}_{1:k})^{2}]\leq\mathbf{E}[(W_{1:k}^{\mathsf{T}}MW_{1:k})^{2}].

Let

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[(\bar{W}_{1:k}^{\mathsf{T}}M\bar{W}_{1:k})^{2}]

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[(\bar{W}_{1:k}^{\mathsf{T}}M\bar{W}_{1:k})^{2}]

We conclude this section with a result that will be useful for analyzing
the mixing properties of the Gaussian process (
E.1a
), when the dynamics function
f
is nonlinear.
First, recall the definition of the
1
-Wasserstein distance:

\displaystyle W_{1}(\mu,\nu)\triangleq\inf\{\mathbf{E}\lVert X-Y\rVert_{2}\mid(X,Y)\text{ is a coupling of }(\mu,\nu)\}.

(E.3)

The following result uses the smoothness of the Gaussian transition kernel to upper bound the TV norm via the

1
-Wasserstein distance.
This result is inspired by the work of
Chae and Walker 2020
.

Lemma E.1
.

Let
X_{0},Y_{0}
be random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
,
and let
f:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an
L
-Lipschitz function.
Suppose that
X_{0},Y_{0}
are both absolutely continuous w.r.t. the
Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{p}
.
Let
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be positive definite,
and let
X_{1},Y_{1}
be random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
defined
conditionally:
X_{1}\mid X_{0}=N(f(X_{0}),\Sigma)

and
Y_{1}\mid Y_{0}=N(f(Y_{0}),\Sigma)
.
Then:

\displaystyle\|\mathsf{P}_{X_{1}}-\mathsf{P}_{Y_{1}}\|_{\mathsf{TV}}\leq\frac{L\sqrt{\tr(\Sigma^{-1})}}{2}W_{1}(\mathsf{P}_{X_{0}},\mathsf{P}_{Y_{0}}).

Proof.

Since
X_{0},Y_{0}
are absolutely continuous, the Radon-Nikodym theorem ensures that there exists
densities
p_{0},q_{0}
for
X_{0},Y_{0}
, respectively.
Let
\phi
denote the density of
the
N(0,\Sigma)
distribution.
Let
p_{1},q_{1}
denote the densities of
X_{1},Y_{1}
, respectively.
We have the following convolution expressions:

\displaystyle p_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=\int\phi(x-f(x_{0}))p_{0}(x_{0})\,\mathrm{d}x_{0}=\int\phi(x-f(X_{0}))\,\mathrm{d}X_{0},

\displaystyle q_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=\int\phi(x-f(x_{0}))q_{0}(x_{0})\,\mathrm{d}x_{0}=\int\phi(x-f(Y_{0}))\,\mathrm{d}Y_{0}.

Now, let
\pi
be a coupling of
(X_{0},Y_{0})
.
We can equivalently write
p_{1},q_{1}
as a convolution over
\pi
:

\displaystyle p_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=\int\phi(x-f(X_{0}))\,\mathrm{d}\pi(X_{0},Y_{0}),

\displaystyle q_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=\int\phi(x-f(Y_{0}))\,\mathrm{d}\pi(X_{0},Y_{0}).

Hence:

\displaystyle(p_{1}-q_{1})(x)

\displaystyle=\int[\phi(x-f(X_{0}))-\phi(x-f(Y_{0}))]\,\mathrm{d}\pi(X_{0},Y_{0})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}_{\pi(X_{0},Y_{0})}[\phi(x-f(X_{0}))-\phi(x-f(Y_{0}))].

Now by the
L^{1}
representation of total-variation norm
[see e.g.
Tsybakov 2009
, Lemma 2.1]
:

\displaystyle\|\mathsf{P}_{X_{1}}-\mathsf{P}_{Y_{1}}\|_{\mathsf{TV}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int|p_{1}(x)-q_{1}(x)|\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int|\mathbf{E}_{\pi(X_{0},Y_{0})}[\phi(x-f(X_{0}))-\phi(x-f(Y_{0}))]|\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leq}}\frac{1}{2}\int\mathbf{E}_{\pi(X_{0},Y_{0})}[|\phi(x-f(X_{0}))-\phi(x-f(Y_{0}))|]\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{E}_{\pi(X_{0},Y_{0})}\left[\int|\phi(x-f(X_{0}))-\phi(x-f(Y_{0}))|\,\mathrm{d}x\right].

The inequality (a) is Jensen’s inequality,
and the equality (b) is Tonelli’s theorem
since the integrand is non-negative.
We now focus on the inner integral
inside the expectation over
\pi
.
By the mean-value theorem, since
\phi
is continuously differentiable, fixing

x,X_{0},Y_{0}
:

\displaystyle|\phi(x-f(X_{0}))-\phi(x-f(Y_{0}))|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{0}^{1}\nabla\phi((1-s)(x-f(Y_{0}))+s(x-f(X_{0})))^{\mathsf{T}}(f(Y_{0})-f(X_{0}))\,\mathrm{d}s\right|

\displaystyle\leq\lVert f(Y_{0})-f(X_{0})\rVert_{2}\int_{0}^{1}\lVert\nabla\phi(x-(sf(X_{0})-(1-s)f(Y_{0}))\rVert_{2}\,\mathrm{d}s.

Hence, by another application of Tonelli’s theorem:

\displaystyle\int|\phi(x-f(X_{0}))-\phi(x-f(Y_{0}))|\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\lVert f(X_{0})-f(Y_{0})\rVert_{2}\int\int_{0}^{1}\lVert\nabla\phi(x-(sf(X_{0})-(1-s)f(Y_{0}))\rVert_{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\lVert f(X_{0})-f(Y_{0})\rVert_{2}\int_{0}^{1}\int\lVert\nabla\phi(x-(sf(X_{0})-(1-s)f(Y_{0}))\rVert_{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq\lVert f(X_{0})-f(Y_{0})\rVert_{2}\sqrt{\tr(\Sigma^{-1})}.

The last inequality follows from the following computation.
Observe that
\nabla\phi(x)=-\Sigma^{-1}x\phi(x)

and define
\mu=sf(X_{0})-(1-s)f(Y_{0})
.
Since
\mu
does not depend on
x
, by the translation invariance
of the Lebesgue integral:

\displaystyle\int\lVert\nabla\phi(x-(sf(X_{0})-(1-s)f(Y_{0}))\rVert_{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int\lVert\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)\rVert_{2}\phi(x-\mu)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int\lVert\Sigma^{-1}x\rVert_{2}\phi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}_{x\sim N(0,\Sigma^{-1})}[\lVert x\rVert_{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\tr(\Sigma^{-1})}.

The last inequality above is another application of Jensen’s inequality.
Therefore, combining the inequalities thus far,
and using the
L
-Lipschitz property of
f
:

\displaystyle\|\mathsf{P}_{X_{1}}-\mathsf{P}_{Y_{1}}\|_{\mathsf{TV}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sqrt{\tr(\Sigma^{-1})}}{2}\mathbf{E}_{\pi(X_{0},Y_{0})}\left[\lVert f(X_{0})-f(Y_{0})\rVert_{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L\sqrt{\tr(\Sigma^{-1})}}{2}\mathbf{E}_{\pi(X_{0},Y_{0})}[\lVert X_{0}-Y_{0}\rVert_{2}].

Since the coupling
\pi
of
(X_{0},Y_{0})
was arbitrary, the result now follows by taking the infimum of the right hand side
over all valid couplings.
∎

Here we show how to recover the results for mixing systems from

-hypercontractivity is automatic by

-boundedness.

Corollary F.1
.

Suppose that
\mathscr{F}_{\star}
is star-shaped and

The first two terms in inequality (

. Note that, without further control of the moments of

Proof of Corollary
F.1

Fix

\displaystyle B^{2}\mathcal{N}_{\infty}(\mathscr{F}_{\star},r/\sqrt{8})\exp\left(\frac{-Tr^{2}}{8B^{2}\|\Gamma_{\mathsf{dep}}(\mathsf{P}_{X})\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}}\right)

and the assumption

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\Gamma_{t}\succcurlyeq\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=\lfloor T/2\rfloor}^{T-1}\Gamma_{t}\succcurlyeq\frac{T-\lfloor T/2\rfloor}{T}\Gamma_{\lfloor T/2\rfloor}\succcurlyeq\frac{1}{3}\Gamma_{\lfloor T/2\rfloor}.

(G.5)

Since

\Gamma_{\lfloor T/2\rfloor}\succcurlyeq\Gamma_{\kappa-1}
holds, and therefore

\Gamma_{\lfloor T/2\rfloor}
is invertible since
(A_{\star},H)

\kappa
-step controllable.
Therefore:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbf{E}\lVert M\bar{X}_{t}\rVert_{2}^{4}

\displaystyle\leq 3\tr(M^{\mathsf{T}}M\Gamma_{T-1})^{2}

\displaystyle=3\tr(M\Gamma_{\lfloor T/2\rfloor}^{1/2}\Gamma_{\lfloor T/2\rfloor}^{-1/2}\Gamma_{T-1}\Gamma_{\lfloor T/2\rfloor}^{-1/2}\Gamma_{\lfloor T/2\rfloor}^{1/2}M^{\mathsf{T}})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 3\|\Gamma_{\lfloor T/2\rfloor}^{-1}\Gamma_{T-1}\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}\tr(M^{\mathsf{T}}M\Gamma_{\lfloor T/2\rfloor})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 27\|\Gamma_{\lfloor T/2\rfloor}^{-1}\Gamma_{T-1}\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}\tr\left(M^{\mathsf{T}}M\cdot\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\Gamma_{t}\right)^{2}

using (
G.5
)

\displaystyle=27\|\Gamma_{\lfloor T/2\rfloor}^{-1}\Gamma_{T-1}\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbf{E}\lVert MX_{t}\rVert_{2}^{2}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 108\|\Gamma_{\lfloor T/2\rfloor}^{-1}\Gamma_{T-1}\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbf{E}\lVert M\bar{X}_{t}\rVert_{2}^{2}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\text{using \lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{prop:trunc_gauss_approximate_isotropic}}.

Since the matrix

is arbitrary, the claim follows
using the following bound for

\displaystyle\|\Gamma_{\lfloor T/2\rfloor}^{-1}\Gamma_{T-1}\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}\leq\frac{\tau^{4}\|H\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{4}}{(1-\rho^{2})^{2}\mu^{2}}\leq\frac{\tau^{4}\|H\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{4}}{(1-\rho)^{2}\mu^{2}}.

∎

Bounding the dependency matrix for truncated LDS

We control

Proposition G.2
.

Consider the process
\{\bar{X}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
from (

denote the
joint distribution of
\{\bar{X}_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T-1}

We have that:

\displaystyle\|\Gamma_{\mathsf{dep}}(\mathsf{P}_{\bar{X}})\|_{\mathsf{op}}\leq 5\kappa+\frac{22}{1-\rho}\log\left(\frac{\tau^{2}}{4\mu}\left[B_{\bar{X}}^{2}+\frac{d_{\mathsf{X}}\|H\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}}{1-\rho}\right]\right).

Proof.

We first construct an almost sure bound on the process

\{\bar{X}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
. Indeed, for any

,
using the
(\tau,\rho)
-stability of

:

\displaystyle\lVert\bar{X}_{t}\rVert_{2}\leq\frac{\|H\|_{\mathsf{op}}\tau R}{1-\rho}=\frac{\|H\|_{\mathsf{op}}\tau(\sqrt{d_{\mathsf{X}}}+\sqrt{2(1+\beta)\log{T}})}{1-\rho}=B_{\bar{X}}.

Also, by
(\tau,\rho)
-stability, we have for any indices

\displaystyle\|\Gamma_{s}-\Gamma_{t}\|_{\mathsf{op}}=\left\|\sum_{k=s+1}^{t}A^{k}HH^{\mathsf{T}}(A^{k})^{\mathsf{T}}\right\|_{\mathsf{op}}\leq\|H\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}\tau^{2}\sum_{k=s+1}^{t}\rho^{2k}\leq\frac{\|H\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}\tau^{2}}{1-\rho^{2}}\rho^{2(s+1)}.

(G.6)

The marginal and conditional distributions of
\{X_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
are easily characterized.
We have that
X_{t}\sim N(0,\Gamma_{t})
.
Furthermore,
X_{t}\mid X_{0}=x
for

is distributed as
N(A^{t}x,\Gamma_{t-1})

So now for any

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}_{X_{t+k}}(\cdot\mid X_{t}=x)=N(A^{k}x,\Gamma_{k-1}),\>\>\mathsf{P}_{X_{t+k}}=N(0,\Gamma_{t+k}).

Now suppose

The matrices
\Gamma_{k-1}
and
\Gamma_{t+k}

both be invertible, so the two distributions are mutually absolutely continuous.
We can then use the closed-form expression for the KL-divergence between two multivariate Gaussians:

\displaystyle\mathsf{KL}(N(A^{k}x,\Gamma_{k-1}),N(0,\Gamma_{t+k}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[\tr(\Gamma_{t+k}^{-1}\Gamma_{k-1})+x^{\mathsf{T}}(A^{k})^{\mathsf{T}}\Gamma_{t+k}^{-1}A^{k}x-d_{\mathsf{X}}+\log\det(\Gamma_{t+k}\Gamma_{k-1}^{-1})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}x^{\mathsf{T}}(A^{k})^{\mathsf{T}}\Gamma_{t+k}^{-1}A^{k}x+\frac{d_{\mathsf{X}}}{2}\log\|\Gamma_{t+k}\Gamma_{k-1}^{-1}\|_{\mathsf{op}}

\displaystyle\text{since }\Gamma_{k-1}\preccurlyeq\Gamma_{t+k}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\tau^{2}\rho^{2k}\lVert x\rVert_{2}^{2}}{2\mu}+\frac{d_{\mathsf{X}}}{2}\log\left(1+\frac{\|\Gamma_{t+k}-\Gamma_{k-1}\|_{\mathsf{op}}}{\mu}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\tau^{2}\rho^{2k}\lVert x\rVert_{2}^{2}}{2\mu}+\frac{d_{\mathsf{X}}}{2}\log\left(1+\frac{\|H\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}\tau^{2}}{(1-\rho^{2})\mu}\rho^{2k}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left[\frac{\tau^{2}\lVert x\rVert_{2}^{2}}{2\mu}+\frac{d_{\mathsf{X}}\|H\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}\tau^{2}}{2(1-\rho^{2})\mu}\right]\rho^{2k}

\displaystyle\log(1+x)\leq x\,\forall x\geq 0.

Hence by Pinsker’s inequality
[see e.g.
Tsybakov 2009
, Lemma 2.5]
, whenever

\displaystyle\|\mathsf{P}_{X_{t+k}}(\cdot\mid X_{t}=x)-\mathsf{P}_{X_{t+k}}\|_{\mathsf{TV}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathsf{KL}(N(A^{k}x,\Gamma_{k-1}),N(0,\Gamma_{t+k}))/2}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{\tau^{2}\lVert x\rVert_{2}^{2}}{4\mu}+\frac{d_{\mathsf{X}}\|H\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}\tau^{2}}{4(1-\rho^{2})\mu}}\rho^{k}.

By
Proposition

E.2
(which we can invoke since we constrained
\beta\geq 2
), for any
\ell\in\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle\|\Gamma_{\mathsf{dep}}(\mathsf{P}_{\bar{X}})\|_{\mathsf{op}}

\displaystyle\leq 3+\sqrt{2}\sum_{k=1}^{T-1}\max_{t=0,\dots,T-1-k}\esssup_{x\in\bar{\mathsf{X}}_{t}}\sqrt{\|\mathsf{P}_{X_{t+k}}(\cdot\mid X_{t}=x)-\mathsf{P}_{X_{t+k}}\|_{\mathsf{TV}}}

\displaystyle\leq 3+\sqrt{2}(\kappa-1+\ell)+\sum_{k=\kappa+\ell}^{T-1}\left[\frac{\tau^{2}B_{\bar{X}}^{2}}{4\mu}+\frac{d_{\mathsf{X}}\|H\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}\tau^{2}}{4(1-\rho^{2})\mu}\right]^{1/4}\rho^{k/2}

\displaystyle\leq 5(\kappa+\ell)+\left[\frac{\tau^{2}B_{\bar{X}}^{2}}{4\mu}+\frac{d_{\mathsf{X}}\|H\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}\tau^{2}}{4(1-\rho^{2})\mu}\right]^{1/4}\frac{\rho^{(\kappa+\ell)/2}}{1-\rho^{1/2}}.

Now, define
\psi\triangleq\frac{\tau^{2}B_{\bar{X}}^{2}}{4\mu}+\frac{d_{\mathsf{X}}\|H\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}\tau^{2}}{4(1-\rho^{2})\mu}
.
We choose

so

With this choice of

and the observation that

\displaystyle\|\Gamma_{\mathsf{dep}}(\mathsf{P}_{\bar{X}})\|_{\mathsf{op}}\leq 5\kappa+\frac{11\log{\psi}}{4(1-\rho^{1/2})}\leq 5\kappa+\frac{22\log{\psi}}{1-\rho}.

The claim now follows.

Appendix H
General linearized model dynamics

H.1
Comparison to existing results

We first compare the results of
Theorem

7.2
to
the existing bounds from
Sattar and Oymak 2020
,
Foster et al. 2020
,
Kowshik et al. 2021
.
Before doing so, we note that these existing results bound the loss
of specific gradient based algorithms.
On the other hand,
Theorem

7.2

directly applies to the empirical risk minimizer of the square loss.
In general, the LSE optimization problem specialized to this setting is non-convex due to the composition of the square loss with the link function
\sigma
.
However, we believe it should be possible to show that the quasi-Newton method described
in
Kowshik et al. 2021
can be used to optimize the empirical
risk to precision of order
\|H\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}d_{\mathsf{X}}^{2}/T
, in which case a
simple modification of
Theorem

4.1
combined with the current analysis
in
Theorem

7.2
would apply to bound the
excess risk of the final iterate of this algorithm.
This is left to future work.

For our comparison, we will ignore all logarithmic factors, and assume any necessary
burn-in times, remarking that the existing results all
prescribe sharper burn-in times than
Theorem

7.2
.
First, we compare with
Sattar and Oymak 2020
.
In doing so, we will assume that
H=(1+\sigma)I
for some
\sigma>0
,
since this is the setting they study.
When
H
is diagonal,
(
7.10
) is actually invariant to the noise scale
\sigma

which is the correct behavior:

\mathbf{E}\lVert\widehat{A}-A_{\star}\rVert_{F}^{2}\leq\tilde{O}(1)\frac{d_{\mathsf{X}}^{2}}{\zeta^{2}T}
.
On the other hand,
Sattar and Oymak 2020
gives a high probability bound of

\lVert\widehat{A}-A_{\star}\rVert_{F}^{2}\leq\tilde{O}(1)\frac{\sigma^{2}d_{\mathsf{X}}^{2}}{\zeta^{4}(1-\rho)^{3}T}
. Thus, (
7.10
) improves on this rate by not only a factor of
1/\zeta^{2}
, but also in moving the
1/(1-\rho)
dependence into the log.
We note that their result seems to improve as
\sigma\to 0
, but
the probability of success also tends to
0
as
\sigma\to 0
.

Next, we turn our attention to
Foster et al. 2020
.
This result actually gives both in-sample excess risk and parameter recovery bounds.
For simplicity, we only compare to the parameter recovery bounds, as this is their sharper result. Their result yields a high probability bound that

\lVert\widehat{A}-A_{\star}\rVert_{F}^{2}\leq\tilde{O}(1)\frac{\|H\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}\|P_{\star}\|_{\mathsf{op}}d_{\mathsf{X}}^{2}}{\zeta^{4}(1-\rho)T}
. Again, we see (
7.10
) improve this rate by a factor of
1/\zeta^{2}
, and moves the dependence on
\|P_{\star}\|_{\mathsf{op}}
and

1/(1-\rho)
into the logarithm.
We note again, this rate seems to improve as
\|H\|_{\mathsf{op}}\to 0
, but the number of iterations
m
of GLMtron needed tends to
\infty
as

\|H\|_{\mathsf{op}}\to 0
.
We conclude by noting that the rate of
Foster et al. 2020
does not
have any burn-in times.

Finally, we compare to
Kowshik et al. 2021
. We will assume that

H=\sigma I
again, as this is the setting of their work.
Their parameter recovery bound states that with high probability,

\lVert\widehat{A}-A_{\star}\rVert_{F}^{2}\leq\tilde{O}(1)\frac{\sigma^{2}d_{\mathsf{X}}^{2}}{\zeta^{2}T}
, which matches (
7.10
) up to the log factors. As noted previously, their logarithmic dependencies are sharper than ours. Furthermore, their result can also handle
the unstable regime when
\rho\leq 1+O(1/T)
, which ours cannot.
However, we note that
Theorem

7.2
also bounds
L^{2}
excess risk
with logarithmic dependence on
1/(1-\rho)
, which is not an immediate consequence of
parameter error bounds. Indeed, a naïve upper bound using the
1
-Lipschitz property of the link function yields:

\lVert\widehat{f}-f_{\star}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\|\widehat{A}-A_{\star}\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbf{E}\lVert X_{t}\rVert_{2}^{2}\lesssim\|\widehat{A}-A_{\star}\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}\frac{1}{(1-\rho)^{2}}
.
Hence, even if the parameter error only depends logarithmically on
1/(1-\rho)
,
it does not immediately translate over to excess risk.

Proposition

E.5

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1+\rho^{2}}{2}\mathbf{E}\lVert X_{t}\rVert_{P_{\star}}^{4}+\frac{3\cdot 24^{3}}{(1-\rho^{2})^{3}}\tr(H^{\mathsf{T}}P_{\star}H)^{2}.

Unrolling this recursion yields:

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\lVert X_{t}\rVert_{P_{\star}}^{4}\leq\frac{6\cdot 24^{3}}{(1-\rho^{2})^{4}}\tr(H^{\mathsf{T}}P_{\star}H)^{2}.

We now handle the case when
\rho\in[0,1/2)
.
Setting
\varepsilon=2^{1/3}-1
and starting from (
H.3
):

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\lVert X_{t}\rVert_{P_{\star}}^{4}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{E}\lVert X_{t}\rVert_{P_{\star}}^{4}+125\mathbf{E}\lVert HV_{t}\rVert_{P_{\star}}^{4}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{E}\lVert X_{t}\rVert_{P_{\star}}^{4}+375\tr(H^{\mathsf{T}}P_{\star}H)^{2}.

Unrolling this recursion yields:

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\lVert X_{t}\rVert^{4}_{P_{\star}}\leq 750\tr(H^{\mathsf{T}}P_{\star}H)^{2}.

The claim now follows by taking the maximum of these two bounds
and using the inequalities

\tr(H^{\mathsf{T}}P_{\star}H)\leq\|H\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}\|P_{\star}\|_{\mathsf{op}}d_{\mathsf{X}}
and

1-\rho^{2}\geq 1-\rho
.
∎

This proof proceeds quite similarly to the linear dynamical
systems proof given in
Appendix

G
.
We start again by defining the truncated GLM dynamics:

\displaystyle\bar{X}_{t+1}=\sigma(A_{\star}\bar{X}_{t})+H\bar{V}_{t},\>\>\bar{X}_{0}=H\bar{V}_{0},\>\>\bar{V}_{t}=V_{t}\mathbf{1}\{\lVert V_{t}\rVert_{2}\leq R\}.

(H.4)

We set
R=\sqrt{d_{\mathsf{X}}}+\sqrt{2(1+\beta)\log{T}}
where
\beta\geq 2
is a free parameter.
Define the event
\mathcal{E}
as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}:=\left\{\max_{0\leq t\leq T-1}\lVert V_{t}\rVert_{2}\leq R\right\}.

(H.5)

Note that by the setting of
R
, we have
\mathbf{P}(\mathcal{E}^{c})\leq 1/T^{\beta}
using standard Gaussian concentration results plus a union bound. Furthermore on
\mathcal{E}
, the original GLM process
driven by Gaussian noise (
7.6
)
coincides with the truncated process (
H.4
).
Let
\widehat{f}
denote the LSE on the original process
(
H.4
), and
let
\bar{f}
denote the LSE on the truncated process (
H.4
).
Hence:

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\lVert\widehat{f}-f_{\star}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}\lVert\widehat{f}-f_{\star}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\mathbf{1}\{\mathcal{E}\}+\mathbf{E}\lVert\widehat{f}-f_{\star}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\mathbf{1}\{\mathcal{E}^{c}\}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{E}\lVert\bar{f}-f_{\star}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\mathbf{E}\lVert\widehat{f}-f_{\star}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\mathbf{1}\{\mathcal{E}^{c}\}.

(H.6)

Let us now control the error term

\mathbf{E}\lVert\widehat{f}-f_{\star}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\mathbf{1}\{\mathcal{E}^{c}\}
.
Write
\widehat{f}(x)=\sigma(\widehat{A}x)
, and put
\widehat{\Delta}=\widehat{A}-A_{\star}
.
We have:

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\lVert\widehat{f}-f_{\star}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\mathbf{1}\{\mathcal{E}^{c}\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbf{E}\lVert\sigma(\widehat{A}X_{t})-\sigma(A_{\star}X_{t})\rVert_{2}^{2}\mathbf{1}\{\mathcal{E}^{c}\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leq}}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbf{E}\lVert\widehat{\Delta}X_{t}\rVert_{2}^{2}\mathbf{1}\{\mathcal{E}^{c}\}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\leq}}\frac{4B^{2}}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbf{E}\lVert X_{t}\rVert_{2}^{2}\mathbf{1}\{\mathcal{E}^{c}\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(c)}}{{\leq}}\frac{4B^{2}}{T^{1+\beta/2}}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\sqrt{\mathbf{E}\lVert X_{t}\rVert_{2}^{4}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(d)}}{{\leq}}\frac{4B^{2}B_{X}^{2}}{T^{\beta/2}}.

(H.7)

Here, (a) follows since
\sigma
is
1
-Lipschitz,
(b) uses the definition of
\mathscr{F}
in (
7.7
), (c) follows by
Cauchy-Schwarz, and (d) uses
Proposition

H.1
.

The remainder of the proof is to bound the
LSE error
\mathbf{E}\lVert\bar{f}-f_{\star}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}
.
First, we establish an almost sure bound on

\{\bar{X}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
.

Proposition H.2
.

Consider the truncated GLM process (
H.4
).
Under
7.1
, the process
\{\bar{X}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
satisfies:

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\lVert\bar{X}_{t}\rVert_{P_{\star}}\leq\frac{2\|P_{\star}\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{1/2}\|H\|_{\mathsf{op}}(\sqrt{d_{\mathsf{X}}}+\sqrt{2(1+\beta)\log{T}})}{1-\rho}\triangleq B_{\bar{X}}.

(H.8)

Proof.

By triangle inequality and (
H.1
):

\displaystyle\lVert\bar{X}_{t+1}\rVert_{P_{\star}}

\displaystyle=\lVert\sigma(A_{\star}\bar{X}_{t})+H\bar{V}_{t}\rVert_{P_{\star}}\leq\lVert\sigma(A_{\star}\bar{X}_{t})\rVert_{P_{\star}}+\lVert H\bar{V}_{t}\rVert_{P_{\star}}

\displaystyle\leq\rho^{1/2}\lVert\bar{X}_{t}\rVert_{P_{\star}}+\lVert H\bar{V}_{t}\rVert_{P_{\star}}\leq\rho^{1/2}\lVert\bar{X}_{t}\rVert_{P_{\star}}+\|P_{\star}^{1/2}H\|_{\mathsf{op}}R.

Unrolling this recursion,
and using the fact that
\inf_{x\in[0,1]}\frac{1-\sqrt{x}}{1-x}=1/2
yields the result.
∎

We next establish uniform bounds for the covariance matrices
of the truncated process.

Proposition H.3
.

Suppose
T\geq 4
.
Consider the truncated GLM process (
H.4
),
and let the covariance matrices for the process
\{\bar{X}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
be denoted as
\bar{\Gamma}_{t}\triangleq\mathbf{E}[\bar{X}_{t}\bar{X}_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}]
.
Under
7.1
:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}HH^{\mathsf{T}}\preccurlyeq\bar{\Gamma}_{t}\preccurlyeq B_{\bar{X}}^{2}\cdot I.

Proof.

The upper bound is immediate from
Proposition

H.2
,
since
\mathbf{E}[\bar{X}_{t}\bar{X}_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}]\preccurlyeq\mathbf{E}[\lVert\bar{X}_{t}\rVert_{2}^{2}]I\preccurlyeq B_{\bar{X}}^{2}I
.
For the lower bound, it is immediate when
t=0

using
Proposition

E.4
.
On the other hand, for
t\geq 1
, since
\bar{V}_{t}
is zero-mean:

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[\bar{X}_{t}\bar{X}_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}[(\sigma(A_{\star}\bar{X}_{t-1})+H\bar{V}_{t-1})(\sigma(A_{\star}\bar{X}_{t-1})+H\bar{V}_{t-1})^{\mathsf{T}}]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}[\sigma(A_{\star}\bar{X}_{t-1})\sigma(A_{\star}\bar{X}_{t-1})^{\mathsf{T}}]+\mathbf{E}[H\bar{V}_{t-1}\bar{V}_{t-1}^{\mathsf{T}}H^{\mathsf{T}}]\succcurlyeq\mathbf{E}[H\bar{V}_{t-1}\bar{V}_{t-1}^{\mathsf{T}}H^{\mathsf{T}}]\succcurlyeq\frac{1}{2}HH^{\mathsf{T}}.

The last inequality again holds from
Proposition

E.4
.
∎

H.2.1
Trajectory hypercontractivity for truncated GLM

For our purposes, the link function assumption in

7.1
ensures the following
approximate isometry inequality which holds for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathsf{X}}}
and all
matrices
A,A^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathsf{X}}\times d_{\mathsf{X}}}
:

\displaystyle\zeta^{2}\lVert Ax-A^{\prime}x\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\lVert\sigma(Ax)-\sigma(A^{\prime}x)\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\lVert Ax-A^{\prime}x\rVert_{2}^{2}.

(H.9)

This inequality is needed to establish trajectory hypercontractivity for
\mathscr{F}_{\star}
.

Proposition H.4
.

Suppose that
T\geq 4
.
Fix any matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathsf{X}}\times d_{\mathsf{X}}}
.
Under
7.1
, the truncated process (
H.4
) satisfies:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbf{E}\lVert\sigma(A\bar{X}_{t})-\sigma(A_{\star}\bar{X}_{t})\rVert_{2}^{4}\leq\frac{4B_{\bar{X}}^{4}}{\sigma_{\min}(H)^{4}\zeta^{4}}\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbf{E}\lVert\sigma(A\bar{X}_{t})-\sigma(A_{\star}\bar{X}_{t})\rVert_{2}^{2}\right)^{2}.

(H.10)

Hence, the function class
\mathscr{F}_{\star}
with
\mathscr{F}
defined in
(
7.7
) satisfies the
(C_{\mathsf{GLM}},2)
-trajectory
hypercontractivity condition
with
C_{\mathsf{GLM}}=\frac{4B_{\bar{X}}^{4}}{\sigma_{\min}(H)^{4}\zeta^{4}}
.

Proof.

Put
\Delta\triangleq A-A_{\star}
and
M\triangleq\Delta^{\mathsf{T}}\Delta
.
We have:

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\lVert\Delta\bar{X}_{t}\rVert_{2}^{4}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}[\bar{X}_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}M\bar{X}_{t}\bar{X}_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}M\bar{X}_{t}]

\displaystyle\leq B_{\bar{X}}^{2}\tr(M^{2}\bar{\Gamma}_{t})

using
Proposition

H.2

\displaystyle\leq B_{\bar{X}}^{2}\|M\|_{\mathsf{op}}\tr(M\bar{\Gamma}_{t})

Hölder’s inequality

\displaystyle\leq B_{\bar{X}}^{2}\tr(M)\tr(M\bar{\Gamma}_{t})

since
M
is positive semidefinite

\displaystyle\leq B_{\bar{X}}^{4}\tr(M)^{2}

using
Proposition

H.3

\displaystyle\leq\frac{B_{\bar{X}}^{4}}{\lambda_{\min}(HH^{\mathsf{T}})^{2}}\tr(MHH^{\mathsf{T}})^{2}.

On the other hand, by
Proposition

H.3
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\lVert\Delta\bar{X}_{t}\rVert_{2}^{2}=\tr(M\bar{\Gamma}_{t})\geq\frac{1}{2}\tr(MHH^{\mathsf{T}}).

Combining these bounds yields:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbf{E}\lVert\Delta\bar{X}_{t}\rVert_{2}^{4}\leq\frac{B_{\bar{X}}^{4}}{\lambda_{\min}(HH^{\mathsf{T}})^{2}}\tr(MHH^{\mathsf{T}})^{2}\leq\frac{4B_{\bar{X}}^{4}}{\lambda_{\min}(HH^{\mathsf{T}})^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbf{E}\lVert\Delta\bar{X}_{t}\rVert_{2}^{2}\right)^{2}.

The claim now follows via the approximate isometry inequality (
H.9
).
∎

H.2.2
Bounding the dependency matrix for truncated GLM

We will use the result in
Lemma

E.1

to bound the total-variation distance by the
1
-Wasserstein distance.
This is where the non-degenerate noise assumption in
7.1

is necessary.

The starting point is the observation that the diagonal Lyapunov function
in
7.1
actually yields

incremental stability

[
Tran et al. 2016
]
in addition to Lyapunov stability. In particular, let
\{a_{i}\}
denote the rows of
A_{\star}
.
For any
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathsf{X}}}
:

\displaystyle\lVert\sigma(A_{\star}x)-\sigma(A_{\star}x^{\prime})\rVert^{2}_{P_{\star}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\mathsf{X}}}(P_{\star})_{ii}(\sigma(\langle a_{i},x\rangle)-\sigma(\langle a_{i},x^{\prime}\rangle))^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\mathsf{X}}}(P_{\star})_{ii}(\langle a_{i},x\rangle-\langle a_{i},x^{\prime}\rangle)^{2}

\displaystyle=(x-x^{\prime})^{\mathsf{T}}A_{\star}^{\mathsf{T}}P_{\star}A_{\star}(x-x^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq\rho\lVert x-x^{\prime}\rVert_{P_{\star}}^{2}.

(H.11)

This incremental stability property allows us to control the dependency matrix as follows.

Proposition H.5
.

Consider the truncated GLM process
\{\bar{X}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}

from (
H.4
).
Let
\mathsf{P}_{\bar{X}}
denote the joint distribution of
\{\bar{X}_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T-1}
.
Under
7.1
and when
B\geq 1
,
we have that:

\displaystyle\|\Gamma_{\mathsf{dep}}(\mathsf{P}_{\bar{X}})\|_{\mathsf{op}}\leq\frac{22}{1-\rho}\log\left(\frac{B\sqrt{d_{\mathsf{X}}}(B_{\bar{X}}+B_{X})}{2\sigma_{\min}(H)}\right).

Proof.

Let
\{X_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
denote the original GLM dynamics from (
7.6
).
Fix indices
t\geq 0
and
k\geq 1
.
We construct a coupling of
(\mathsf{P}_{X_{t+k}}(\cdot\mid X_{t}=x),\mathsf{P}_{X_{t+k}})
as follows.
Let
\{V_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
be iid
N(0,I)
.
Let
\{Z_{s}\}_{s\geq t}
be the process such that

Z_{t}=x
, and follows the GLM dynamics (
7.6
) using the noise
\{V_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}

(we do not bother defining
Z_{t^{\prime}}
for
t^{\prime}<t
since we do not need it).
Similarly, let
\{Z^{\prime}_{s}\}_{s\geq 0}
be the process following the GLM dynamics (
7.6
)
using the same noise
\{V_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
.
Now we have:

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\lVert Z_{t+k}-Z^{\prime}_{t+k}\rVert_{P_{\star}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}\lVert\sigma(A_{\star}Z_{t+k-1})-\sigma(A_{\star}Z^{\prime}_{t+k-1})\rVert_{P_{\star}}

\displaystyle\leq\rho^{1/2}\mathbf{E}\lVert Z_{t+k-1}-Z^{\prime}_{t+k-1}\rVert_{P_{\star}}

\displaystyle\text{using \lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{eq:glm_incremental_stability}}.

We now unroll this recursion down to
t
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\lVert Z_{t+k}-Z^{\prime}_{t+k}\rVert_{P_{\star}}\leq\rho^{k/2}\mathbf{E}\lVert Z_{t}-Z^{\prime}_{t}\rVert_{P_{\star}}=\rho^{k/2}\mathbf{E}\lVert x-Z^{\prime}_{t}\rVert_{P_{\star}}.

Since
P_{\star}\succcurlyeq I
, this shows that:

\displaystyle W_{1}(\mathsf{P}_{X_{t+k}}(\cdot\mid X_{t}=x),\mathsf{P}_{X_{t+k}})\leq\rho^{k/2}(\lVert x\rVert_{P_{\star}}+\mathbf{E}\lVert X_{t}\rVert_{P_{\star}})\leq\rho^{k/2}(\lVert x\rVert_{P_{\star}}+B_{X}),

where the last inequality follows from
Proposition

H.1
and Jensen’s inequality.

Next, it is easy to see the map
x\mapsto\sigma(A_{\star}x)

is
\|A\|_{\mathsf{op}}
-Lipschitz.
Furthermore, since
H
is full rank
by
7.1
, then for any
t
and
k\geq 1
both

\mathsf{P}_{t}
and

\mathsf{P}_{X_{t+k}}(\cdot\mid X_{t}=x)

are absolutely continuous
w.r.t. the Lebesgue measure in
\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathsf{X}}}
.
Using
Lemma

E.1
, we have for any

k\geq 2
:

\displaystyle\|\mathsf{P}_{X_{t+k}}(\cdot\mid X_{t}=x)-\mathsf{P}_{X_{t+k}}\|_{\mathsf{TV}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|A_{\star}\|_{\mathsf{op}}\sqrt{\tr((HH^{\mathsf{T}})^{-1})}}{2}W_{1}(\mathsf{P}_{X_{t+k-1}}(\cdot\mid X_{t}=x),\mathsf{P}_{X_{t+k-1}})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|A_{\star}\|_{\mathsf{op}}\sqrt{\tr((HH^{\mathsf{T}})^{-1})}}{2}\rho^{(k-1)/2}(\lVert x\rVert_{P_{\star}}+B_{X}).

Using
Proposition

E.2
to bound
\|\Gamma_{\mathsf{dep}}(\mathsf{P}_{\bar{X}})\|_{\mathsf{op}}
(which is valid because we constrained
\beta\geq 2
),
and
Proposition

H.2
to bound
x\in\bar{\mathsf{X}}_{t}
,
for any
\ell\geq 1
:

\displaystyle\|\Gamma_{\mathsf{dep}}(\mathsf{P}_{\bar{X}})\|_{\mathsf{op}}

\displaystyle\leq 3+\sqrt{2}\sum_{k=1}^{T-1}\max_{t=0,\dots,T-1-k}\esssup_{x\in\bar{\mathsf{X}}_{t}}\sqrt{\|\mathsf{P}_{X_{t+k}}(\cdot\mid X_{t}=x)-\mathsf{P}_{X_{t+k}}\|_{\mathsf{TV}}}

\displaystyle\leq 3+\sqrt{2}\ell+\left[\frac{\|A_{\star}\|_{\mathsf{op}}\sqrt{\tr((HH^{\mathsf{T}})^{-1})}(B_{\bar{X}}+B_{X})}{2}\right]^{1/2}\sum_{k=\ell+1}^{T-1}\rho^{(k-1)/4}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leq}}5\ell+\left[\frac{B\sqrt{d_{\mathsf{X}}}(B_{\bar{X}}+B_{X})}{2\sigma_{\min}(H)}\right]^{1/2}\frac{\rho^{\ell/4}}{1-\rho^{1/4}}.

Above, (a) uses the bounds
\|A_{\star}\|_{\mathsf{op}}\leq B
and

\tr(HH^{-1})\leq d_{\mathsf{X}}/\sigma_{\min}(H)^{2}
.
Now put
\psi\triangleq\frac{B\sqrt{d_{\mathsf{X}}}(B_{\bar{X}}+B_{X})}{2\sigma_{\min}(H)}
.
We choose
\ell=\left\lceil\frac{\log(\sqrt{\psi})}{1-\rho^{1/4}}\right\rceil
so that

\rho^{\ell/4}\leq 1/\sqrt{\psi}
.
This yields:

\displaystyle\|\Gamma_{\mathsf{dep}}(\mathsf{P}_{\bar{X}})\|_{\mathsf{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{11\log{\psi}}{2(1-\rho^{1/4})}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leq}}\frac{22\log{\psi}}{1-\rho}=\frac{22}{1-\rho}\log\left(\frac{B\sqrt{d_{\mathsf{X}}}(B_{\bar{X}}+B_{X})}{2\sigma_{\min}(H)}\right).

Above, (a) follows from

\inf_{x\in[0,1]}\frac{1-x^{1/4}}{1-x}=1/4
.
∎

H.2.3
Finishing the proof of
Theorem

7.2

Below, we let
c_{i}
be universal positive constants
that we do not track precisely.

For any
\varepsilon>0

\varepsilon
-covering of
\mathscr{F}_{\star}\setminus B(r)
, with
\mathscr{F}_{\star}
the offset class of
\mathscr{F}

from (

).

\delta

from (

),

\displaystyle B_{\bar{X}}\leq\frac{c_{0}\|P_{\star}\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{1/2}\|H\|_{\mathsf{op}}(\sqrt{d_{\mathsf{X}}}+\sqrt{(1+\beta)\log{T}})}{1-\rho}.

\displaystyle\lVert(\sigma(A_{i}x)-\sigma(A_{\star}x))-(\sigma(Ax)-\sigma(A_{\star}x))\rVert_{2}

[see e.g.
Vershynin 2018

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\infty}(\mathscr{F}_{\star}\setminus B(r),\varepsilon)\leq\mathcal{N}_{\infty}(\mathscr{F}_{\star},\varepsilon/2)\leq\left(1+\frac{4BB_{\bar{X}}}{\varepsilon}\right)^{d_{\mathsf{X}}^{2}}.

Next, by
Proposition

H.4
,
(\mathscr{F}_{\star},\mathsf{P}_{\bar{X}})
is
(C_{\mathsf{GLM}},2)
-hypercontractive for all
T\geq 4
,
where

\displaystyle C_{\mathsf{GLM}}\leq\frac{4B_{\bar{X}}^{4}}{\sigma_{\min}(H)^{4}\zeta^{4}}\leq\frac{c_{1}\|P_{\star}\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}\mathrm{cond}(H)^{4}(d_{\mathsf{X}}^{2}+((1+\beta)\log{T})^{2})}{\zeta^{4}(1-\rho)^{4}}.

Furthermore, by
Proposition

H.5
:

\displaystyle\|\Gamma_{\mathsf{dep}}(\mathsf{P}_{\bar{X}})\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}\leq\frac{c_{2}}{(1-\rho)^{2}}\log^{2}\left(\frac{B\sqrt{d_{\mathsf{X}}}(B_{\bar{X}}+B_{X})}{2\sigma_{\min}(H)}\right)\triangleq\gamma^{2}.

\mathscr{F}_{\star}
is
2BB_{\bar{X}}
-bounded on (

).
Invoking
Theorem

4.1
, for every

:

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\|\bar{f}-f_{\star}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq 8\mathbf{E}\bar{\mathsf{M}}_{T}(\mathscr{F}_{\star})+r^{2}+4B^{2}B_{\bar{X}}^{2}\left(1+\frac{4\sqrt{8}BB_{\bar{X}}}{r}\right)^{d_{\mathsf{X}}^{2}}\exp\left(\frac{-T}{8C_{\mathsf{GLM}}\gamma^{2}}\right).

(H.12)

Here, the notation
\mathbf{E}\bar{\mathsf{M}}_{T}(\mathscr{F}_{\star})

\mathsf{P}_{\bar{X}}
and
not
the original process
\mathsf{P}_{X}
.
We now set

,
and compute a

It suffices to take (assuming

\displaystyle T_{0}\geq c_{4}\frac{\|P_{\star}\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}\mathrm{cond}(H)^{4}d_{\mathsf{X}}^{4}}{\zeta^{4}(1-\rho)^{6}}\mathrm{polylog}\left(B,d_{\mathsf{X}},\|P_{\star}\|_{\mathsf{op}},\mathrm{cond}(H),\frac{1}{\zeta},\frac{1}{1-\rho}\right).

(H.13)

Again, we do not attempt to compute the exact power of the

Next, from (

.
Thus if we further constrain

then

.
Note that setting

suffices.

To finish the proof, it remains to bound
\mathbf{E}\bar{\mathsf{M}}_{T}(\mathscr{F}_{\star})
.
Now, unlike the linear dynamical systems case,

.
Hence, we will bound it via the chaining bound (

is
a sub-Gaussian MDS. The MDS part is clear since

for

, and
\bar{V}_{t}
is zero-mean.
Furthermore,
Proposition

E.3

yields that
H\bar{V}_{t}
is a
4\|H\|_{\mathsf{op}}^{2}
-sub-Gaussian random vector. To finish the proof we need to bound the martingale offset complexity.

Bounding the Martingale Offset Complexity

We will use the chaining bound (
4.5
) in particular. Thus, we need to bound the covering number of
\mathscr{F}_{\star}
. Define

\displaystyle\mathbb{M}_{d_{\mathsf{X}},d_{\mathsf{X}}}(B)=\{A\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathsf{X}}\times d_{\mathsf{X}}}\mid\|A\|_{F}\leq B\}.

By a standard volumetric argument we have that
\log\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{M}_{d_{\mathsf{X}},d_{\mathsf{X}}},\|\cdot\|_{F},\varepsilon)\leq d_{\mathsf{X}}^{2}\log(1+2B/\varepsilon)
. Let now
\{A_{1},\dots,A_{N}\}
be an optimal
\varepsilon
-cover of
\mathbb{M}_{d_{\mathsf{X}},d_{\mathsf{X}}}
. Then for every
A\in\mathbb{M}_{d_{\mathsf{X}},d_{\mathsf{X}}}
we can find
A_{i}\in\{A_{1},\dots A_{N}\}
such that

\displaystyle\|\sigma(A\>\cdot\>)-\sigma(A_{i}\>\cdot\>)\|_{\infty}\leq\sup_{x\in\mathsf{X}}\|(A-A_{i})x\|_{2}\leq\|A-A_{i}\|_{F}\sup_{x\in\mathsf{X}}\|x\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon B_{\bar{X}}

where we used the estimate
B_{\bar{X}}
of (
H.8
) and the fact that
\sigma
is
1
-Lipschitz.

Hence any
\varepsilon
-covering of
\mathbb{M}_{d_{\mathsf{X}},d_{\mathsf{X}}}(B)
induces a
B_{\bar{X}}\varepsilon
-covering of
\mathscr{F}_{\star}
and we have established the upper bound

\displaystyle\log\mathcal{N}(\mathscr{F}_{\star},\|\cdot\|_{\infty},\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\leq\log\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{M}_{d_{\mathsf{X}},d_{\mathsf{X}}},\|\cdot\|_{F},\varepsilon/B_{\bar{X}}B)

(H.14)

\displaystyle\leq d_{\mathsf{X}}^{2}\log(1+2B_{\bar{X}}B/\varepsilon).

Since the truncated process
H\bar{V}_{t}
is
4\|H\|_{\mathsf{op}}
sub-Gaussian, we may invoke (
4.5
) with
\delta=\gamma
. For any
\gamma>0
we have for some constant
c^{\prime}>0
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\bar{\mathsf{M}}_{T}(\mathscr{F}_{\star})

\displaystyle\leq c^{\prime}\left(\frac{\|H\|_{\mathsf{op}}\log\mathcal{N}_{\infty}(\mathscr{F}_{\star},\gamma)}{T}+\gamma\sqrt{d_{\mathsf{X}}\|H\|_{\mathsf{op}}}+\gamma^{2}\right)

(H.15)

\displaystyle\leq c^{\prime}\left(\frac{\|H\|_{\mathsf{op}}d_{\mathsf{X}}^{2}\log(1+2B_{\bar{X}}B/\gamma)}{T}+\gamma\sqrt{d_{\mathsf{X}}\|H\|_{\mathsf{op}}}+\gamma^{2}\right)

where we substituted the estimate (
H.14
) into (
4.5
). To arrive at the desired conclusion (
7.8
), it suffices to take
\gamma^{2}=\frac{d_{\mathsf{X}}\|H\|_{\mathsf{op}}}{T}
and impose
T\geq T_{0}
.
