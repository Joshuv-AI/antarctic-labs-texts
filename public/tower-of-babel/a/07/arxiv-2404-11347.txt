Isotropic surfaces and moment map flow Thanks: Supported by IRL CRM-CNRS 3457, UQAM, CIRGET, UMR6629, ANR-21-CE40-0017, CHL 11-LABX-0020-01
François Jauberteau, Yann Rollin

Abstract
. We consider the moduli space of isotropic maps from a closed surface Σ \Sigma to a symplectic affine space and construct a Kähler moment map geometry, on a space of differential forms on Σ \Sigma , such that the isotropic maps correspond to certain zeroes of the moment map. The moment map geometry induces a modified moment map flow, whose fixed point set correspond to isotropic maps. This construction can be adapted to the polyhedral setting. In particular, we prove that the polyhedral modified moment map flow induces a strong deformation retraction from the space of polyhedral maps onto the space of polyhedral isotropic maps.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Motivations

Symplectic geometry
is the natural mathematical framework for

Hamiltonian mechanics
.
Gromov showed that certain symplectic properties
are
flexible
,
thanks to
convex integration
,
resulting in
h-principle
theorems
[
8
]
, whereas others are
rigid
, due to
the existence of
pseudoholomorphic curves

[
9
]
.
In particular the Gromov-Lees theorem
[
9
,
13
,
5
]
establishes an h-principle for the
problem of
isotropic immersions
in a symplectic manifold.
As a corollary, it is possible to approximate every immersed submanifold of an
affine symplectic space by isotropic immersed submanifolds, with respect to
the

C^{0}
topology.

Our research started as we were
conducting some numerical experiments for
Lagrangian surfaces and their
mean curvature flow
; numerical
simulations naturally involve piecewise linear geometry and
we quickly realized that
the only explicit examples of closed piecewise linear Lagrangian
were the polygons in
{\mathbb{C}}
and some polygonal versions of the Clifford
torus in
{\mathbb{C}}^{m}
.
Assessing the state of the art, we noticed
that very little is known about piecewise
linear symplectic geometry.
Then, we
turned to the existence problem for piecewise
linear isotropic submanifolds in affine symplectic spaces
[
11
,
17
]

and obtained the following approximation theorem:

Theorem 1.1.1

(
[
17
]
)
.

Let
f:\Sigma\looparrowright V
be a smooth isotropic immersion, where

\Sigma
is a surface diffeomorphic to a quotient torus, and

(V,\omega_{V})
is a symplectic affine
space. Then, there exists a family of piecewise linear maps

f_{N}:\Sigma\to V
for
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
, with the following
properties:

(1)

f_{N}:\Sigma\looparrowright V
is a topological
immersion;

(2)

f_{N}
converges toward
f
in
C^{1}
-norm.

As a corollary, every smoothly immersed torus in
V
can be approximated in

C^{0}
-norm by isotropic piecewise linear immersed tori.

Surprisingly, the original proof of Theorem
1.1.1
relies on an

infinite dimensional Kähler moment map
geometry
and the
fixed point principle

developed in
[
11
]
.
However, an alternate proof using only on
soft techniques

was given subsequently by
Etourneau
[
6
]
, with significant technical simplifications.

Several
authors have been considering problems of piecewise linear symplectic
geometry,
including Gratza
[
7
]
and Bertelson-Distexhe
[
3
]
.
Panov also introduced
a notion of
polyhedral Kähler
geometry in
[
16
]
.
The space of piecewise linear symplectic maps of a
4
-torus is also
shown to be related to a
hyperKähler moment map
geometry
in
[
18
]
.
It seems that classical results of smooth symplectic differential
geometry are curiously challenging to generalize to the piecewise linear setting.
Perhaps this is a manifestation of symplectic rigidity ?
To show how much has to be accomplished in the field, here is a short list of challenging open questions:

(1)

The
local Darboux theorem
, well known in the smooth setting,
turns out to be a conjecture for piecewise linear symplectic
manifolds of dimension at least
4
(cf.
[
3
]
for a definition of piecewise
linear symplectic manifolds).

(2)

The
deformation theory of Lagrangian submanifolds
is well described
in the smooth setting, thanks to the Lagrangian neighborhood
theorem
[
14
]
.
However, there is no analogue of such a deformation theory
in the case of
piecewise linear Lagrangian submanifolds of a symplectic
affine space. This motivates the work and partial answers
of
[
11
,
17
]
.

(3)

The
local structure
of the infinite dimensional Lie group of symplectic diffeomorphisms
is well
understood
[
2
]
. However, very little is known about
the local (and global) structure of the space of piecewise linear
symplectic maps, even in the case of a
4
-dimensional torus
[
18
]
.

The above questions seem hard settle for an obvious reason: the Moser’s
trick generally fails in the piecewise linear setting, although it may be
be used with significant efforts, in the case of piecewise linear volume
forms for instance
[
3
]
for instance. All these complications lead us
to speculate whether
some
exotica
arise in the context of piecewise linear symplectic
geometry.

A Kähler moment map geometry inspired by Donaldson
[
4
]
was introduced
in
[
11
]
to construct the approximations
f_{N}
of Theorem
1.1.1
.
The construction starts from a Kähler surface

(\Sigma,g_{\Sigma},J_{\Sigma},\omega_{\Sigma})
and a Hermitian affine space
V

with symplectic form
\omega_{V}
.
These structures induce a

formal
Kähler structure
({\mathscr{M}},{\mathcal{G}},{\mathcal{J}},\Omega)
with Kähler form

\Omega
, on the moduli
space
{\mathscr{M}}=C^{\infty}(\Sigma,V)
.
The group of Hamiltonian diffeomorphisms
\mathrm{Ham}(\Sigma,\omega_{\Sigma})

acts by precomposition on the moduli space space
{\mathscr{M}}
and preserves the formal Kähler structure.
In fact the action of
\mathrm{Ham}(\Sigma,\omega_{\Sigma})
on
{\mathscr{M}}
is formally Hamiltonian,
with moment map given by

\mu^{D}(f)=-\frac{f^{*}\omega_{V}}{\omega_{\Sigma}},

understood as an element of the Lie algebra of

\mathrm{Ham}(\Sigma,\omega_{\Sigma})
, identified to the space of smooth function

C_{0}^{\infty}(\Sigma,{\mathbb{R}})
orthogonal to constants.
Thus, zeroes of the moment map
\mu^{D}
correspond to
isotropic maps
f\in{\mathscr{M}}
. A detailed presentation of this moment map
geometry is given
in
[
11
]
.

A finite dimensional approximation
\phi_{N}
of the energy functional
\phi
of the moment
map
\mu^{D}
is considered in
[
11
]
. The downward gradient flow of

\phi_{N}
provides a flow of piecewise linear surfaces.
A numerical version of the flow and numerical
experiments carried out in
[
11
]
provided some effective examples of piecewise
linear Lagrangian tori in
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
. Overall, this flow
of piecewise linear surfaces has been interesting from an
experimental perspective. However, it is not satisfactory from a
mathematical point of view, for several reasons:
we could not prove that the flow is convergent, although it seems well
behaved numerically. Furthermore, the flow does not come from
a finite dimensional moment map picture and its
geometrical interpretation is somewhat unclear.

As a response to these objections,
a new moment map geometry and its
corresponding flow are introduced in this paper. They do
not have any of the above issues: the new moment map geometry can be
immediately adapted to the polyhedral setting and much stronger mathematical results
are obtained. In
particular, Theorem
E
is a Duistermaat type theorem,
which shows that the flow has the nicest
possible behavior.

1.2.
Statement of results

Let
V
be a Hermitian affine space, with underlying vector space

\vec{V}
and
and
symplectic form

\omega_{V}
.
We consider a
smooth closed oriented surface

\Sigma
, endowed with
a
Riemannian
metric

g_{\Sigma}
, a
compatible almost complex structure

J_{\Sigma}

and a
corresponding
Kähler form

\omega_{\Sigma}=g_{\Sigma}(J_{\Sigma},\cdot,\cdot)
.

The
moduli space of smooth map

f:\Sigma\to V
is denoted
{\mathscr{M}}
.
Formally
{\mathscr{M}}
is an affine space with
\Omega^{0}(\Sigma,\vec{V})
as the
underlying vector space.
The
differential
of a smooth map
f\in{\mathscr{M}}
defines an exact

\vec{V}
-valued
1
-form
df
and we have a linear differential operator

d:{\mathscr{M}}\to{\mathscr{F}}=\Omega^{1}(\Sigma,\vec{V}).

The image of
{\mathscr{M}}
by
d
is the space of exact
1
-forms denoted

{\mathscr{F}}_{0}
.
We show that the moduli space
{\mathscr{F}}
carries a natural Euclidean
L^{2}
-inner product

{\mathcal{G}}
, defined by Formula (
2.3
),
and a compatible almost complex structure

{\mathcal{J}}
, defined by Formula (
2.5
). The corresponding Kähler form is
denoted
\Omega={\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{J}}\cdot,\cdot)
.

Formula (
2.6
) defines an action of the gauge group

{\mathbb{T}}^{\mathbb{C}}=C^{\infty}(\Sigma,{\mathbb{C}}^{*})
on
{\mathscr{F}}
. The action of the
real subgroup
{\mathbb{T}}=C^{\infty}(\Sigma,{\mathbb{R}})

is merely the action by complex multiplication on
\vec{V}
-valued differential

1
-forms and we have the following result:

Theorem A
.

Let
\Sigma
be a smooth closed surface endowed with a Kähler
structure
(\Sigma,g_{\Sigma},J_{\Sigma},\omega_{\Sigma})
and

V
, a
Hermitian affine space.

The moduli space
{\mathscr{F}}=\Omega^{1}(\Sigma,\vec{V})
carries a natural
Kähler structure
({\mathscr{F}},{\mathcal{G}},{\mathcal{J}},\Omega)
and an action of the
gauge group
{\mathbb{T}}^{\mathbb{C}}=C^{\infty}(\Sigma,{\mathbb{C}}^{*})
.

The almost complex structure
{\mathcal{J}}
is invariant under the action
of
{\mathbb{T}}^{\mathbb{C}}
. The action of
{\mathbb{T}}^{\mathbb{C}}
is the complexification
of the
{\mathbb{T}}
-action, where
{\mathbb{T}}=C^{\infty}(\Sigma,S^{1})
is the real
subgroup.

The
{\mathbb{T}}
-action preserves the Kähler structure

({\mathscr{F}},{\mathcal{J}},{\mathcal{G}},\Omega)
and is Hamiltonian.
The map
\mu:{\mathscr{F}}\to\mathfrak{t}
, where
\mathfrak{t}
is the Lie algebra of

{\mathbb{T}}
, given by

\mu(F)=-\frac{F^{*}\omega_{V}}{\omega_{\Sigma}},

is a moment map. In other words,
\mu
is
{\mathbb{T}}
-invariant and, for every
\zeta\in\mathfrak{t}
,

D\langle\!\langle\mu,\zeta\rangle\!\rangle=-\iota_{X_{\zeta}}\Omega,

where
X_{\zeta}
is the vector field on
{\mathscr{F}}
defined by the infinitesimal action of
\zeta

on
{\mathscr{F}}
.

Corollary B
.

With the assumptions of Theorem
A
,
a smooth map
f:\Sigma\to V
is isotropic if, and only if,

\mu(df)=0.

Remark 1.2.1
.

In the finite dimensional setting, the famous Kempf-Ness theorem relates
the existence of zeroes of a Kähler moment
map in a complexified orbit with an algebro-geometric notion of
stability
[
15
]
.
In view Theorem
A
and Corollary
B
, it is
tempting to try to extend this theory in our case:
the existence of isotropic maps should be related to some kind of algebraic
condition. The answer turns out to be somewhat trivial for the
total moduli space
{\mathscr{F}}
, as discussed at §
2.12
. However,
we are mainly interested in the zeroes of the moment map that
belong
to the subspace
{\mathscr{F}}_{0}\subset{\mathscr{F}}
, as they are related to
isotropic maps by Corollary
B
. Unfortunately,
{\mathscr{F}}_{0}
is not invariant under the
gauge group action and it is not clear how to adapt the classical
theory from this point.

We consider the
energy functional
of the moment map

\phi:{\mathscr{F}}\to{\mathbb{R}}

given by

\phi(F)=\frac{1}{2}\|\mu(F)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}.

By construction,
\phi
is non negative and the vanishing locus of
\phi
agrees with the vanishing
locus of the moment map. Our idea is to interpret the functional
\phi

as a
Morse-Bott
function on the moduli
space
{\mathscr{F}}
, in the spirit of Atiyah-Bott
[
1
]
, who considered the case of the Yang-Mills functional.
The expectation is that the flow is going to produce a
strong
deformation retraction
of
{\mathscr{M}}
onto the
space of isotropic maps.
However, it is not clear whether
\phi
satisfies the Morse-Bott condition
in any reasonable sense.
Another difficulty is that the subspace
{\mathscr{F}}_{0}
is not gauge invariant
and may not be preserved by the usual Morse-Bott gradient flow.
We get around this issue by defining the
modified moment map flow
, as the downward gradient flow of the
restricted functional
\phi:{\mathscr{F}}_{0}\to{\mathbb{R}}
. More precisely, the modified
moment map flow along
{\mathscr{F}}_{0}
is given by the evolution equation

\boxed{\frac{\partial F}{\partial t}=-\nabla^{\circ}\phi(F),}

where
F\in{\mathscr{F}}_{0}
and
\nabla^{\circ}\phi
is the gradient of the
restricted functional.
Our first theorem shows that the flow is geometrically relevant and is a
well posed problem from an analytical point of view:

Theorem C
.

The fixed point locus of the modified moment map flow on
{\mathscr{F}}_{0}
, in other
words the critical set of
\phi:{\mathscr{F}}_{0}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, agrees with the vanishing
locus of
\phi:{\mathscr{F}}_{0}\to{\mathbb{R}}
.
Furthermore,

(1)

the modified moment maps flow has the short time
existence property and

(2)

the
L^{2}
-norm is non increasing along the flow.

Remarks 1.2.2
.

(1)

The definition of the
short time existence property
involves
the use of
Hölder completions of the moduli space
{\mathscr{F}}
. A technical
version of the short time existence is given at
Theorem
3.1.3
.

(2)

Unfortunately, the modified moment map flow does not seem to have any nice
regularizing properties, like parabolic flows for instance.
Consequently, the long time existence of the flow
is unclear.

(3)

In the case where
\dim_{\mathbb{R}}V=4
, we show at Corollary
3.2.3

that
\phi:{\mathscr{F}}_{0}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a Morse-Bott
function, in a neighborhood of every

monomorphism
(cf.
Definition
2.10.2
) of its vanishing locus.

The purpose of §
4
is to extend all the above
constructions to the
polyhedral
setting.
The concepts needed for stating the results are quickly introduced below
and the reader may refer directly to §
4
for the exact definitions.

A

polyhedral surface

\Sigma

is a topological surface endowed with a triangulation
{\mathscr{T}}
and a
polyhedral
metric

g_{\Sigma}
. An
orientation
of
\Sigma
induces a
polyhedral Kähler
form

\omega_{\Sigma}
and a
polyhedral almost complex structure

J_{\Sigma}
adapted to
g_{\Sigma}
.

A
polyhedral map
with respect to a triangulation
{\mathscr{T}}
is a continuous map
f:\Sigma\to V
such that the
restriction of
f
to
every simplex of the triangulation is an
affine map
.
The moduli space of polyhedral maps
f:\Sigma\to V
is denoted

{\mathscr{M}}({\mathscr{T}})
.

A polyhedral map is generally not differentiable. However, the
restriction of a polyhedral map to any simplex of the triangulation has a
well defined tangent map.
Accordingly, a polyhedral map
f:\Sigma\to V
is called a
polyhedral isotropic map
, if the pullback of
\omega_{V}
by

f
, restricted to every simplex of the triangulation, vanishes
identically.

The existence of polyhedral isotropic
immersions
in
{\mathscr{M}}({\mathscr{T}})
is an open question.
The proof of Theorem
1.1.1
proceeds by introducing a
particular
sequence of triangulations
{\mathscr{T}}_{N}
, in the case of the
2
-torus
\Sigma
,
with a large number of simplices
of order
{\mathscr{O}}(N^{2})
and stepsize of order
{\mathscr{O}}(N^{-1})
. The piecewise
linear approximations
f_{N}
are in fact polyhedral isotropic maps that
belong to

{\mathscr{M}}({\mathscr{T}}_{N})
.
However, the topology of the space of
polyhedral isotropic immersions in
{\mathscr{M}}({\mathscr{T}})
remains completely
mysterious for a fixed triangulation
{\mathscr{T}}
of
\Sigma
.

Returning to the general case of a oriented polyhedral surface
\Sigma
, we
pursue the analogy with the smooth setting.
The space vector space
{\mathscr{F}}({\mathscr{T}})
is the space of families

F=(F_{\sigma})_{{\mathscr{K}}_{2}}
, where
\sigma
belongs to the set of facets

{\mathscr{K}}_{2}
of the
triangulation
{\mathscr{T}}
and
F_{\sigma}
is a constant
\vec{V}
-valued
1
-form on

\sigma
.
Because the restriction of a polyhedral map to every
simplex of the triangulation is differentiable, there is a natural
differential map

d:{\mathscr{M}}({\mathscr{T}})\to{\mathscr{F}}({\mathscr{T}})

and its image is denoted
{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}})
.

We also define the group
{\mathbb{T}}^{\mathbb{C}}({\mathscr{T}})
as the space of families

\lambda=(\lambda_{\sigma})_{\sigma\in{\mathscr{K}}_{2}}
, where

\lambda_{\sigma}:\sigma\to{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
is a constant function. The real
subgroup of families
\lambda
with
\lambda_{\sigma}\in S^{1}
is denoted
{\mathbb{T}}({\mathscr{T}})
.
The group
{\mathbb{T}}^{\mathbb{C}}({\mathscr{T}})
acts on

{\mathscr{F}}({\mathscr{T}})
by Formula (
4.2
).
As in the smooth case, we construct a Euclidean inner product
{\mathcal{G}}
, a
compatible almost complex structure
{\mathcal{J}}
and a corresponding Kähler form

\Omega
on
{\mathscr{F}}({\mathscr{T}})
.
Thus we have a Kähler structure
({\mathscr{F}}({\mathscr{T}}),{\mathcal{G}},{\mathcal{J}},\Omega)
with an
action of
{\mathbb{T}}^{\mathbb{C}}({\mathscr{T}})
and we can state our next result:

Theorem D
.

Theorem
A
and Corollary
B
hold in the polyhedral setting.

As in the smooth setting, we define an energy of the moment map

\phi:{\mathscr{F}}({\mathscr{T}})\to{\mathbb{R}}
by
\phi(F)=\frac{1}{2}\|\mu(F)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}
.
The downward gradient of the functional
\phi
restricted to

{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}})
is called the
polyhedral modified moment map flow
.

The moduli spaces
{\mathscr{M}}({\mathscr{T}})
and
{\mathscr{F}}({\mathscr{T}})
are finite dimensional
and much stronger result than
Theorem
C
are
expected, as the polyhedral moment map flow is an ordinary
differential equation. This is indeed the case, and we obtain the following Duistermaat type theorem:

Theorem E
.

For
F\in{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}})
,
the polyhedral modified moment map flow
admits a unique solution
F_{t}\in{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}})
, defined for

t\in[0,+\infty)
, such that
F_{0}=F
.
Furthermore,
F_{t}
admits a limit
F_{\infty}\in{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}})
as
t
goes to

+\infty
, with the property that
\phi(F_{\infty})=0
.

The extended flow

\Theta:[0,+\infty]\times{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}})\to{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}})

defined by
\Theta(t,F)=F_{t}
for
t\in[0,+\infty)
and

\Theta(+\infty,F)=F_{\infty}
has the following properties:

(1)

The fixed point locus of the flow
\Theta
is the
vanishing set of
\phi:{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}})\to{\mathbb{R}}
.

(2)

The flow defines a strong deformation
retraction of
{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}})
onto the vanishing locus of

\phi:{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}})\to{\mathbb{R}}
.

(3)

There are non trivial flow lines converging toward
0
. More
precisely, there exists
F\in{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}})\setminus 0
, such that

\Theta(+\infty,F)=0
.

(4)

The flow
\Theta
has exponential convergence rate in a
neighborhood of every regular point of the vanishing locus of

\phi:{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}})\to{\mathbb{R}}
.

The flow
\Theta
on
{\mathscr{F}}_{0}
can be lifted as a flow
\hat{\Theta}
on

{\mathscr{M}}({\mathscr{T}})
and we have the following corollary:

Corollary F
.

There exists a unique map

\hat{\Theta}:[0,+\infty]\times{\mathscr{M}}({\mathscr{T}})\to{\mathscr{M}}({\mathscr{T}})

such that

(1)

d\circ\hat{\Theta}_{t}=\Theta_{t}\circ d
and

(2)

\hat{\Theta}_{0}=\mathrm{id}
on
{\mathscr{M}}({\mathscr{T}})
,

where we used the notation
\hat{\Theta}_{t}=\hat{\Theta}(t,\cdot)
.
The map
\hat{\Theta}
is the flow of the evolution equation
given by Formula (
4.4
) and defines a
strong deformation
retraction of
{\mathscr{M}}({\mathscr{T}})
onto the subspace of
polyhedral isotropic maps.

The existence of regular points (cf. Definition
4.10.1
) of the vanishing set of

\phi:{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}})\to{\mathbb{R}}
is not well understood in comparison to the smooth
setting (cf. §
3.2
). However, we show that the
approximating scheme of
[
11
,
17
]
provides examples of regular
points:

Theorem G
.

In the case where
\Sigma
is a
2
-torus and

f:\Sigma\looparrowright V
is a smooth isotropic immersion,
let
f_{N}\in{\mathscr{M}}({\mathscr{T}}_{N})
be the Jauberteau-Rollin-Tapie isotropic polyhedral
immersions
of Theorem
1.1.1
approximating
f
.
Then
F_{N}=df_{N}\in{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}}_{N})

is a regular point of the vanishing
locus of
\phi:{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}}_{N})\to{\mathbb{R}}
for every sufficiently large
N
.

1.3.
Open problems and future research

In view of the approximation scheme given by Theorem
1.1.1
, it
seems sensible to expect that some type Gromov-Lees
theorem should apply to the polyhedral setting, provided some flexibility
for the choice of triangulation
{\mathscr{T}}
of
\Sigma
.
If this is indeed the case, we expect the following consequence:

Conjecture H
.

Let
\Sigma
be a surface endowed with a triangulation

{\mathscr{T}}
. Then, up to passing to a subdivision of
{\mathscr{T}}
,
the space of polyhedral isotropic immersions in
{\mathscr{M}}({\mathscr{T}})
is
homotopically equivalent to the space of smooth isotropic
immersions of
\Sigma
in
V
.

For triangulation with lower complexity, an adapted Morse-Bott cohomology
theory for

\phi:{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}})\to{\mathbb{R}}

should lead to topological invariants for
the space of non constant polyhedral isotropic maps.
Theorem
E
shows that the polyhedral modified moment map flow
is extremely well behaved, which is a strong incentive to develop a
Morse-Bott theory, with a renormalized flow briefly discussed at
§
4.11
.

A numerical version
of the polyhedral modified moment map flow is currently being developed
for testing purposes of the Morse-Bott theory and for producing effective
examples of polyhedral isotropic immersed surfaces.
We sketch the relevant mathematical ingredients of a computer program
that produces approximate solutions of the flow at
§
4.12
. The code is to be released very soon
[
10
]
.

2.
Kähler moment map

This section is devoted to the description of an infinite dimensional
moment map geometry, which provides an interpretation of smooth isotropic maps
as some particular zeroes of a moment map.

2.1.
Target space

Let
V
be an
affine space
and
\vec{V}
, its underlying vector space.
We assume that
\vec{V}
is a
complex vector space
, of complex dimension

m\geq 2
, endowed with
a
Hermitian inner product

h_{V}
, anti-
{\mathbb{C}}
-linear in the
first variable.
For every
v_{1},v_{2}\in\vec{V}
, the decomposition

h_{V}(v_{1},v_{2})=g_{V}(v_{1},v_{2})+i\omega_{V}(v_{1},v_{2}),

into the real and imaginary parts of
h_{V}
provides a
Euclidean inner
product

g_{V}
and a
symplectic form

\omega_{V}
.
Multiplication by
i
defines a linear endomorphism

i:\vec{V}\to\vec{V}
, also called an

almost complex structure
.
By definition the almost complex structure
i
is
compatible

with
g_{V}
and
\omega_{V}
,
in the sense
that

g_{V}(iv_{1},iv_{2})=g_{V}(v_{2},v_{2})\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\omega_{V}(v_{1},v_{2})=g_{V}(iv_{1},v_{2})

for every
v_{1},v_{2}\in\vec{V}
.

2.2.
Source space

Let
\Sigma
be a smooth closed and oriented surface, endowed with
a Riemannian metric
g_{\Sigma}
. We also assume that
\Sigma
is connected
in all this paper, for simplicity of notations.
We denote by
\omega_{\Sigma}
the
volume
form
of
g_{\Sigma}
compatible with the orientation.
The corresponding
almost complex
structure

J_{\Sigma}\in\mathrm{End}(T\Sigma)
is defined as a fiberwise rotation of

T\Sigma\to\Sigma
with angle

+\frac{\pi}{2}
according to the orientation.
By construction,
J_{\Sigma}
is compatible with
g_{\Sigma}
and

\omega_{\Sigma}
is the associated
Kähler form
, in the sense that

g_{\Sigma}(J_{\Sigma}\eta_{1},J_{\Sigma}\eta_{2})=g_{\Sigma}(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\omega_{\Sigma}(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})=g_{\Sigma}(J_{\Sigma}\eta_{1},\eta_{2}),

for every

x\in\Sigma
and
\eta_{1},\eta_{2}\in T_{x}\Sigma
.
In conclusion,
\Sigma

(\Sigma,g_{\Sigma},J_{\Sigma},\omega_{\Sigma}).

2.3.
Fiberwise structures on tensor bundles

The bundle
T^{*}\Sigma\to\Sigma
is identified to
T\Sigma\to\Sigma
using
the duality induced by the Riemannian metric
g_{\Sigma}
.
Thus
T^{*}\Sigma\to\Sigma
and all the tensor bundles
are endowed with an induced fiberwise Euclidean inner
product
denoted
g_{\Sigma}
as well.

The structure of complex vector space on
\vec{V}
induces a canonical structure of
complex vector bundle on

\Lambda^{n}\Sigma\otimes\vec{V}\to\Sigma
, where the tensor product is taken with respect to
{\mathbb{R}}
.
Furthermore,
h_{V}
and
g_{\Sigma}
induce a fiberwise
Hermitian inner product on
\Lambda^{n}\Sigma\otimes\vec{V}\to\Sigma
, denoted

h
, and
defined by the following property: for every
x\in\Sigma
,
\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\in\Lambda^{n}_{x}\Sigma
and
v_{1},v_{2}\in\vec{V}
, then

h(\beta_{1}\otimes v_{1},\beta_{2}\otimes v_{2})=g_{\Sigma}(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})h_{V}(v_{1},v_{2}).

The complex structure
J_{\Sigma}
acts on
\Lambda^{n}\Sigma\to\Sigma
, by
composition on the right: for every
x\in\Sigma
,
\beta\in\Lambda_{x}^{n}\Sigma
and
\eta_{1},\cdots,\eta_{n}\in T_{x}\Sigma
, we put

(\beta\circ J_{\Sigma})(\eta_{1},\cdots,\eta_{n})=\beta(J_{\Sigma}\eta_{1},\cdots,J_{\Sigma}\eta_{n}).

This action is isometric, in the sense that for every

\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\in\Lambda_{x}^{n}\Sigma
, we have

g_{\Sigma}(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})=g_{\Sigma}(\beta_{1}\circ J_{\Sigma},\beta_{2}\circ J_{\Sigma})

The action of
J_{\Sigma}
extends canonically to

\Lambda^{n}\Sigma\otimes\vec{V}\to\Sigma
and
the Hermitian product
h
is
J_{\Sigma}
-invariant, in the
sense that, for every
\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\in\Lambda^{n}_{x}\Sigma\otimes\vec{V}
, we
have

h(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})=h(\beta_{1}\circ J_{\Sigma},\beta_{2}\circ J_{\Sigma}).

We consider the Riemannian metric
g
given by the real part of
h
.
Then
J_{\Sigma}
and i act isometrically on

\Lambda^{n}\Sigma\otimes\vec{V}\to\Sigma
,
in the sense that

g(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})=g(\beta_{1}\circ J_{\Sigma},\beta_{2}\circ J_{\Sigma}),\quad g(i\beta_{1},i\beta_{2})=g(\beta_{1},\beta_{2}).

We define a fiberwise almost complex structure on
T^{*}\Sigma\otimes\vec{V}\to\Sigma
, by the
formula

(2.1)

J\cdot F=-F\circ J_{\Sigma}.

By definition, for every
F_{1},F_{2}\in T^{*}_{x}\Sigma\otimes\vec{V}
, we have

g(iF_{1},iF_{2})=g(J\cdot F_{1},J\cdot F_{2})=g(F_{1},F_{2}).

In particular, the formula

(2.2)

\omega(F_{1},F_{2})=g(J\cdot F_{1},F_{2})

defines a fiberwise symplectic form on the bundle
T^{*}\Sigma\otimes\vec{V}\to\Sigma
.

2.4.
Moduli spaces and differentials

We consider the moduli space of smooth
\vec{V}
-valued differential
1
-forms

{\mathscr{F}}=\Omega^{1}(\Sigma,\vec{V})

and the moduli space of smooth maps

{\mathscr{M}}=C^{\infty}(\Sigma,V).

Notice that

{\mathscr{M}}
is an affine space with
\Omega^{0}(\Sigma,\vec{V})
as the underlying
vector space.
For every smooth map,
f:M\to V
, the tangent map
f_{*}:T\Sigma\to TV=V\times\vec{V}
can
be regarded as a differential

df:T\Sigma\to\vec{V}

given by
df=\pi_{2}\circ f_{*}
, where
\pi_{2}:TV\to\vec{V}
is the second
canonical projection. Hence we have a differential operator between moduli spaces

{\mathscr{M}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\mathscr{F}}.

The image of
d
is the subspace of
exact

\vec{V}
-valued differential

{\mathscr{F}}_{0}\subset{\mathscr{F}}
.
Furthermore,

is injective up to translations by
constant map in
\Omega^{0}(\Sigma,\vec{V})
, identified to
\vec{V}
.
Thus,

induces a bijection

{\mathscr{M}}/\vec{V}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\mathscr{F}}_{0}.

2.5.
Euclidean structure and Hodge theory

The fiberwise Euclidean inner product
g
on the vector bundle
\Lambda^{n}\Sigma\otimes\vec{V}\to\Sigma

induces an
L^{2}
-Euclidean inner product on
\Omega^{n}(\Sigma,\vec{V})
, given by

(2.3)

{\mathcal{G}}(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})=\int_{\Sigma}g(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})\;\omega_{\Sigma},\quad\mbox{for
$\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\in\Omega^{n}(\Sigma,\vec{V})$.}

For simplicity, we will often use the notations

\langle\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\rangle=g(\beta_{1},\beta_{2}),\quad\langle\!\langle\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\rangle\!\rangle={\mathcal{G}}(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})

and

|\beta|=\sqrt{g(\beta,\beta)},\quad\|\beta\|_{L^{2}}=\sqrt{{\mathcal{G}}(\beta,\beta)}.

The formal adjoint
d^{\star}
of
d
is defined by the property

{\mathcal{G}}(\beta_{1},d\beta_{2})={\mathcal{G}}(d^{\star}\beta_{1},\beta_{2})\quad\mbox{for
$\beta_{1}\in\Omega^{n+1}(\Sigma,\vec{V})$
and $\beta_{2}\in\Omega^{n}(\Sigma,\vec{V})$}

and the
Laplace operator

\Delta
is given by the formula

\Delta=dd^{\star}+d^{\star}d.

The classical
Hodge theory
extends to
\vec{V}
-valued differential
forms.
In particular, we obtain a
{\mathcal{G}}
-orthogonal projection onto
the space of
\vec{V}
-valued exact
1
-forms denoted

\Pi:{\mathscr{F}}\to{\mathscr{F}}_{0}.

Hölder spaces
are better suited for elliptic operators, as the Laplace operator
\Delta
.
Recall that the Riemannian metric
g_{\Sigma}
and the fiberwise inner product
g

induce a
C^{k,\nu}
-
Hölder norm
for tensor fields over
\Sigma
,
denoted
\|\cdot\|_{k,\nu}
,
where
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
is the number of derivatives and
\nu\in(0,1)
is the Hölder
regularity exponent for the
k
-th derivative (cf.
[
12
]
for an
explicit definition).

Hölder norms define Hölder completed spaces. In particular, we
denote
by
{\mathscr{F}}^{k,\nu}
and
{\mathscr{M}}^{k,\nu}
the completion of
{\mathscr{F}}
and

{\mathscr{M}}
.
For every
F\in{\mathscr{F}}^{k,\nu}
with
k\geq 0
, the
Hodge
decomposition
theorem
states that
F
admits a
{\mathcal{G}}
-orthogonal decomposition

F=F_{h}+\Delta G,

where
G\in{\mathscr{F}}^{k+2,\nu}
and
F_{h}
is a smooth harmonic form.
In particular, we can define an orthogonal
Hodge projection

\Pi:{\mathscr{F}}^{k,\nu}\to{\mathscr{F}}^{k,\nu}_{0}
by

\Pi(F)=dd^{\star}G

of
F
onto its exact component.
By Proposition
2.5.1
, the projector
\Pi
is continuous with
respect to Hölder topology. This result, which is an immediate consequence of Hodge
theory, is a crucial argument for the proof of
Theorem
C
, via the Cauchy-Lipschitz theorem (cf.
Theorem
3.1.3
). We provide a proof of the proposition for the sake of
self-containedness.

Proposition 2.5.1
.

For
k\geq 0
and
\nu\in(0,1)
, the orthogonal
projection
\Pi:{\mathscr{F}}^{k,\nu}\to{\mathscr{F}}_{0}^{k,\nu}
onto the
exact component of a differential form is a continuous linear map with respect to the

C^{k,\nu}
-norm.
In other words, there exists a real constant
c>0
such that

\|\Pi(F)\|_{{k,\nu}}\leq c\|F\|_{{k,\nu}},\quad\mbox{for every $F\in{\mathscr{F}}^{k,\nu}$.}

Proof.

For
F\in{\mathscr{F}}^{k,\nu}
, the Hodge decomposition theorem shows that

(2.4)

F=F_{h}+\Delta G,

where
G\in{\mathscr{F}}^{k+2,\nu}
is a
1
-form orthogonal to harmonic forms and
F_{h}
is the
harmonic part of
F
.

A
C^{k,\nu}
-estimate for
F

provides a control on the
C^{k-2,\nu}
-norm of
\Delta F
.
By Formula (
2.4
), we have
\Delta F=\Delta^{2}G
, since
F_{h}
is harmonic.
Hence the
C^{k,\nu}
-norm of

F
control the
C^{k-2,\nu}
-norm of
\Delta^{2}G
. The operator

\Delta
is selfajoint, hence
\Delta G

is orthogonal to the kernel of
\Delta
. Then, elliptic Schauder estimates
provide a control on the
C^{k,\nu}
-norm of
\Delta G
. Since
G
was chosen
orthogonal to harmonic forms, we deduce a
C^{k+2,\alpha}
control
on
G
by the Schauder estimates.
In conclusion, there exists a universal constant
c_{1}>0
, such that

\|G\|_{{k+2,\nu}}\leq c_{1}\|F\|_{{k,\nu}}.

Finally, the
C^{k+2,\nu}
-norm of
G
controls the

C^{k,\nu}
-norm of
dd^{\star}G
and we
conclude that there exists a universal constant
c>0
that
satisfies the proposition.
∎

2.6.
Kähler structures on the moduli space

The space of differential forms
\Omega^{n}(\Sigma,\vec{V})
has a structure
of module over
C^{\infty}(\Sigma,{\mathbb{C}})
, acting by complex multiplication on

\vec{V}
-valued forms. More precisely

(\lambda F)_{x}=\lambda(x)F_{x}

for every
\lambda\in C^{\infty}(\Sigma,{\mathbb{C}})
,
F\in\Omega^{n}(\Sigma,\vec{V})
and

x\in\Sigma
.

The space of differential
1
-forms
{\mathscr{F}}
admits an alternate almost
complex structure

{\mathcal{J}}:{\mathscr{F}}\to{\mathscr{F}},

defined by

(2.5)

({\mathcal{J}}\cdot F)_{x}\cdot\eta=(JF_{x})\cdot\eta=-F_{x}\circ J_{\Sigma}\cdot\eta,

where
J
is defined by Formula (
2.1
),
for every
x\in\Sigma
and
\eta\in T_{x}\Sigma
.
By construction, the almost complex structure
{\mathcal{J}}
is compatible with the
Eulidean
L^{2}
-inner product
{\mathcal{G}}
on
{\mathscr{F}}
. The corresponding
Kähler form
\Omega
deduced from
{\mathcal{G}}
and
{\mathcal{J}}
is given by

\Omega(\dot{F}_{1},\dot{F}_{2})={\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{J}}\dot{F}_{1},F_{2})

for every
\dot{F}_{1},\dot{F}_{2}\in{\mathscr{F}}
.
Equivalently

\Omega(\dot{F}_{1},\dot{F}_{2})=\int_{\Sigma}\omega(\dot{F}_{1},\dot{F}_{2})\omega_{\Sigma},

where
\omega(\dot{F}_{1},\dot{F}_{2})=g(J\cdot\dot{F}_{1},\dot{F}_{2})
.

In conclusion, we have a natural Kähler
structure

({\mathscr{F}},{\mathcal{G}},{\mathcal{J}},\Omega)

on the moduli space
{\mathscr{F}}
.

2.7.
An involution

The complex vector space
{\mathscr{F}}

admits several almost complex
structures: the almost complex structure
i:{\mathscr{F}}\to{\mathscr{F}}
, corresponding
to
the multiplication by
i
and the almost complex structure
{\mathcal{J}}
described above.
By construction, the two almost complex structure
i
and
{\mathcal{J}}
commute.
Therefore, the endomorphism

{\mathscr{R}}:{\mathscr{F}}\to{\mathscr{F}}

defined by

{\mathscr{R}}F=i{\mathcal{J}}\cdot F

is a
linear isometric involution
of
{\mathscr{F}}
, which commutes with
i

and
{\mathcal{J}}
. We obtain a
{\mathcal{G}}
-orthogonal decomposition

{\mathscr{F}}={\mathscr{F}}^{+}\oplus{\mathscr{F}}^{-}

where
{\mathscr{F}}^{\pm}
are the eigenspaces associated to the eigenvalues
\pm 1
of

{\mathscr{R}}
. More explicitely, we have

{\mathscr{F}}^{+}=\{F\in{\mathscr{F}},F\circ J_{\Sigma}=iF\}\quad\mbox{ and }

{\mathscr{F}}^{-}=\{F\in{\mathscr{F}},F\circ J_{\Sigma}=-iF\}.

In other words,
{\mathscr{F}}^{+}
(resp.
{\mathscr{F}}^{-}
) is the
subspace of
complex (resp. anti-complex) morphisms
F:T\Sigma\to\vec{V}
.
Hence, every
F\in{\mathscr{F}}
admits a unique orthogonal decomposition

F=F^{+}+F^{-},

where
F^{\pm}\in{\mathscr{F}}^{\pm}
. By definition, we have

{\mathscr{R}}F=F^{+}-F^{-}.

2.8.
Gauge group action

We consider the infinite dimensional complex Lie group

{\mathbb{T}}^{\mathbb{C}}=C^{\infty}(\Sigma,{\mathbb{C}}^{*}),

with trivial Lie algebra

\mathfrak{t}^{\mathbb{C}}=C^{\infty}(\Sigma,{\mathbb{C}}).

We define an action of
{\mathbb{T}}^{\mathbb{C}}
on
{\mathscr{F}}
as follows:
given
\lambda\in{\mathbb{T}}^{\mathbb{C}}
and

F\in{\mathscr{F}}
,
we put

(2.6)

\lambda\cdot F=\bar{\lambda}^{-1}F^{+}+\lambda F^{-},

where
F=F^{+}+F^{-}
according to the splitting
{\mathscr{F}}={\mathscr{F}}^{+}\oplus{\mathscr{F}}^{-}

and
\bar{\lambda}
denotes the complex conjugate.
By construction the action of
{\mathbb{T}}^{\mathbb{C}}
preserves the almost complex
structure
{\mathcal{J}}
for the following obvious reason:

Lemma 2.8.1
.

Each
subspace
{\mathscr{F}}^{\pm}
is
{\mathcal{J}}
invariant and the action of
{\mathbb{T}}^{\mathbb{C}}
on
{\mathscr{F}}
is
{\mathcal{J}}
-linear.

The group
{\mathbb{T}}^{\mathbb{C}}
contains the real subgroup

{\mathbb{T}}=C^{\infty}(\Sigma,S^{1}),

where
S^{1}\subset{\mathbb{C}}
is the unit circle.
If
\lambda\in{\mathbb{T}}
, then

\bar{\lambda}^{-1}=\lambda
, and group action is merely given by complex
multiplication:

\lambda\cdot F=\lambda F,\quad\mbox{ for every }\lambda\in{\mathbb{T}}.

2.9.
Infinitesimal gauge group action

For
\zeta\in\mathfrak{t}^{\mathbb{C}}=C^{\infty}(\Sigma,{\mathbb{C}})
we define an exponential map

\exp:\mathfrak{t}^{\mathbb{C}}\to{\mathbb{T}}^{\mathbb{C}},

by

\exp(\zeta)=e^{i\zeta},

so that the space of real valued functions
\mathfrak{t}=C^{\infty}(\Sigma,{\mathbb{R}})
is identified to the Lie
algebra of
{\mathbb{T}}
.
As usual, the infinitesimal action of
\zeta\in\mathfrak{t}^{\mathbb{C}}
is the vector
field
X_{\zeta}
on the moduli space
{\mathscr{F}}
, defined by

X_{\zeta}(F)=\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0}\exp(t\zeta)\cdot F.

Formula (
2.6
) shows that

(2.7)

X_{\zeta}(F)=i\bar{\zeta}F^{+}+i\zeta F^{-}.

In particular, if
\zeta\in\mathfrak{t}
, we have

(2.8)

X_{\zeta}(F)=i\zeta F.

If, on the contrary,
\zeta\in i\mathfrak{t}
is a purely imaginary function, we have

(2.9)

X_{\zeta}(F)=-i\zeta{\mathscr{R}}F.

By (
2.8
) and (
2.9
), the
infinitesimal action of
\zeta\in\mathfrak{t}
satisfies

{\mathcal{J}}\cdot X_{\zeta}(F)={\mathcal{J}}\cdot i\zeta F=\zeta{\mathscr{R}}F=-i(i\zeta){\mathscr{R}}F=X_{i\zeta}(F),

and we deduce the following result:

Lemma 2.9.1
.

The action of
{\mathbb{T}}^{\mathbb{C}}
on
{\mathscr{F}}
is
the
{\mathcal{J}}
-complexification of the action of
{\mathbb{T}}
.

In addition, the gauge group
{\mathbb{T}}
acts isometrically on
{\mathscr{F}}
. Indeed for every

\lambda\in{\mathbb{T}}
and
F\in{\mathscr{F}}
,

\|\lambda\cdot F\|^{2}_{L^{2}}=\|\lambda F\|^{2}_{L^{2}}=\|F\|^{2}_{L^{2}}.

In conclusion, we have the following result:

Proposition 2.9.2
.

The action
of
{\mathbb{T}}
on
{\mathscr{F}}
is linear and preserves the Kähler structure

({\mathscr{F}},{\mathcal{G}},{\mathcal{J}},\Omega)
.

2.10.
Symplectic density

The map

(2.10)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mu:{\mathscr{F}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathfrak{t}=C^{\infty}(\Sigma,{\mathbb{R}})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces-\frac{1}{2}g(F,{\mathscr{R}}F).}

can be interpreted
as a
symplectic density
, according to the following lemma:

Lemma 2.10.1
.

The following formulas hold

(2.11)

\mu(F)=-\frac{1}{2}\omega(iF,F)=-\frac{1}{2}(|F^{+}|^{2}-|F^{-}|^{2})=-\frac{F^{*}\omega_{V}}{\omega_{\Sigma}},

for every
F\in{\mathscr{F}}
, where the pullback is defined by

(F^{*}\omega_{V})(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})=\omega_{V}(F(\eta_{1}),F(\eta_{2}))
, for every
x\in\Sigma
and

\eta_{1},\eta_{2}\in T_{x}\Sigma
.

Proof.

Using the fact that
-\frac{1}{2}g({\mathscr{R}}F,F)=-\frac{1}{2}g(i{\mathcal{J}}F,F)=-\frac{1}{2}g({\mathcal{J}}iF,F)=-\frac{1}{2}\omega(iF,F)
we deduce the first identity.

Using the decomposition
F=F^{+}+F^{-}
, we write

g({\mathscr{R}}F,F)=g(F^{+}-F^{-},F^{+}+F^{-})
. Using the fact the
F^{+}

and
F^{-}
are pointwise
g
-orthogonal, we deduce that
g({\mathscr{R}}F,F)=|F^{+}|^{2}-|F^{-}|^{2}
,
which proves the second identity.

Let
x\in\Sigma
and
(e_{1},e_{2})
be a
g_{\Sigma}
-orthonormal oriented basis of

T_{x}\Sigma
. In particular
\omega_{\Sigma}(e_{1},e_{2})=1
and

J_{\Sigma}e_{1}=e_{2}
.
Hence
({\mathcal{J}}\cdot F)(e_{1})=-F(J_{\Sigma}e_{1})=-F(e_{2})
. Similarly

({\mathcal{J}}\cdot F)(e_{2})=F(e_{1})
. Hence
{\mathscr{R}}F(e_{1})=-iF(e_{2})

and
{\mathscr{R}}F(e_{2})=iF(e_{1})
. By definition

\displaystyle\mu(F)(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}g({\mathscr{R}}F,F)(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}g_{V}({\mathscr{R}}F(e_{1}),F(e_{1}))-\frac{1}{2}g_{V}({\mathscr{R}}F(e_{2}),F(e_{2}))

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}(-g_{V}(iF(e_{2}),F(e_{1}))+g_{V}(iF(e_{1}),F(e_{2})))

\displaystyle=-\omega_{V}(F(e_{1}),F(e_{2}))

\displaystyle=-\frac{(F^{*}\omega_{V})(e_{1},e_{2})}{\omega_{\Sigma}(e_{1},e_{2})},

which proves the last identity of the lemma.
∎

We introduce some h-principle terminology before stating an immediate
corollary below.

Definition 2.10.2
.

A differential form
{\mathscr{F}}
is called isotropic if
F:T\Sigma\to\vec{V}
maps
every tangent space to an isotropic subspace of
V
or,
equivalently, if
F^{*}\omega_{V}=0
. If the restriction of

F:T\Sigma\to\vec{V}
to every tangent space is injective, we say
that
F
is a monomorphism.

Corollary 2.10.3
.

Let
F
be an element of
{\mathscr{F}}
.
The following properties are equivalent:

(1)

F\in{\mathscr{F}}
is isotropic

(2)

\mu(F)=0\in\mathfrak{t}
.

(3)

|F^{+}|=|F^{-}|
identically on
\Sigma
.

If
F\in{\mathscr{F}}_{0}
, we deduce the following interpretation for isotropic
maps:

Corollary 2.10.4
.

For every map
f\in{\mathscr{M}}
, the following properties are equivalent:

(1)

f
is an isotropic map.

(2)

F=df\in{\mathscr{F}}_{0}
is isotropic.

(3)

The map
f
satisfies the equation
\mu(df)=0
.

In particular, the space of isotropic maps modulo
\vec{V}
agrees with
the zeroes of
\mu
in
{\mathscr{F}}_{0}
via the bijection

.

2.11.

.

{\mathbb{T}}

The proof of the theorem starts with a computation:

For every

By bilinearity,
2D\mu|_{F}\cdot F=-g({\mathscr{R}}F,\dot{F})-g(F,{\mathscr{R}}\dot{F})
and he lemma
follows from the fact that

-selfadjoint.
∎

2.12.
Stability and isotropic maps

The Kempf-Ness theory, for Kähler moment map geometry, relates the symplectic
reduction with geometric invariant theory. This point of
view seems appealing in our case, where
{\mathscr{F}}
is acted on by the complex
gauge group
{\mathbb{T}}^{\mathbb{C}}
and the
\mu
is a moment map for
{\mathbb{T}}
.
The question of existence of a zero of the moment map in a

{\mathbb{T}}^{\mathbb{C}}
-orbit
is rather trivial:
for simplicity,
we define the
{\mathbb{T}}^{\mathbb{C}}
-invariant subspace of
generic
differentials

{\mathscr{F}}_{gen}
as the subspace of nowhere vanishing differential forms.
Then we have the following result

Lemma 2.12.1
.

For every
F\in{\mathscr{F}}_{gen}
, the following
properties are equivalent:

(1)

F^{+}\in{\mathscr{F}}_{gen}
and
F^{-}\in{\mathscr{F}}_{gen}
.

(2)

There exists
\lambda\in{\mathbb{T}}^{\mathbb{C}}
such that

\mu(\lambda\cdot F)=0
.

In particular, the orbit of
F\in{\mathscr{F}}^{\pm}\setminus 0
does not
contain any zero of the moment map.

Proof.

For
\lambda:\Sigma\to{\mathbb{R}}\setminus 0
we have

\mu(\lambda\cdot F)=-\frac{1}{2}(\lambda^{-2}|F^{+}|^{2}-\lambda^{2}|F^{-}|^{2})

and the lemma is obvious.
∎

However, we are mostly interested in the zeroes of the moment map in

{\mathscr{F}}_{0}
, as they are differentials of isotropic maps by

2.13.
Energy of the moment map

We consider the energy of the moment map

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phi:{\mathscr{F}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathbb{R}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phi(F)=\frac{1}{2}\|\mu(F)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}}

Obviously,

We prove various formulas about the differential and the gradient of the

Proposition 2.13.1

For every

and the gradient of the functional

\nabla\phi(F)=-\mu(F){\mathscr{R}}F.

or

(2.13)

\nabla\phi=-{\mathcal{J}}Z,

where

is the vector field on
{\mathscr{F}}
defined by

Proof.

By definition of

\left.D\phi\right|_{F}\cdot\dot{F}=\langle\!\langle\mu(F),D\mu|_{F}\cdot\dot{F}\rangle\!\rangle.

By Lemma

, we have

\left.D\phi\right|_{F}\cdot\dot{F}=-\langle\!\langle\mu(F),g({\mathscr{R}}F,\dot{F})\rangle\!\rangle.

In particular, for

D\phi|F\cdot F=-\langle\!\langle\mu(F),g({\mathscr{R}}F,F)\rangle\!\rangle=2\langle\!\langle\mu(F),\mu(F)\rangle\!\rangle=2\|\mu(F)\|^{2}=4\phi(F)

Now

\displaystyle D\phi|_{F}\cdot\dot{F}

\displaystyle=\langle\!\langle\mu(F),g({\mathscr{R}}F,\dot{F})\rangle\!\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{\Sigma}\mu(F)g({\mathscr{R}}F,\dot{F})\omega_{\Sigma}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Sigma}g(\mu(F){\mathscr{R}}F,\dot{F})\omega_{\Sigma}

\displaystyle=\langle\!\langle\mu(F){\mathscr{R}}F,\dot{F}\rangle\!\rangle

and we deduce that

In particular
{\mathcal{J}}\nabla\phi(F)=-\mu(F){\mathcal{J}}{\mathscr{R}}F=\mu(F)iF=X_{\mu(F)}(F)
and we conclude that

\nabla\phi=-{\mathcal{J}}Z

where

is the vector field on
{\mathscr{F}}
defined by

.
∎

Corollary 2.13.2
.

The following properties are equivalent for
F\in{\mathscr{F}}_{0}

(1)

F
is a zero of
\phi:{\mathscr{F}}_{0}\to{\mathbb{R}}
.

(2)

F
is a zero of
\mu
in
{\mathscr{F}}_{0}
.

(3)

F
is isotropic.

(4)

is a critical point of
\phi:{\mathscr{F}}_{0}\to{\mathbb{R}}
.

Proof.

We know that

and

.
If

is a zero of

3.
The modified moment map flow

The subspace of exact differentials
{\mathscr{F}}_{0}\subset{\mathscr{F}}

is not stable under the
{\mathbb{T}}
-action.
Hence, the gradient
\nabla\phi
is generally not tangent to

{\mathscr{F}}_{0}
.
The restriction of the functional
\phi:{\mathscr{F}}_{0}\to{\mathbb{R}}

admits a gradient vector field
\nabla^{\circ}\phi
related to

\nabla\phi
by the formula

\nabla^{o}\phi(F)=\Pi(\nabla\phi(F)).

We define the
modified moment map flow
by the evolution equation

(3.1)

\frac{\partial F}{\partial t}=-\nabla^{o}\phi(F)

along
{\mathscr{F}}_{0}
and, more generally, along the Hölder completion

{\mathscr{F}}_{0}^{k,\nu}
.

3.1.
Basic properties of the flow

An interesting feature of the flow is the following decay
property:

Theorem 3.1.1
.

Let
F_{t}
be a solution of the modified moment map
flow, for
t
in some interval of
I
. Then

(3.2)

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\|F_{t}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}=-8\phi(F_{t}).

In particular
t\mapsto\|F_{t}\|_{L^{2}}
is non increasing along
I
.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\|F_{t}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\langle\!\langle\frac{\partial F_{t}}{\partial t},F_{t}\rangle\!\rangle

\displaystyle=-\langle\!\langle\nabla^{o}\phi(F_{t}),F_{t}\rangle\!\rangle

\displaystyle=-\langle\!\langle\nabla\phi(F_{t}),F_{t}\rangle\!\rangle

\displaystyle=-4\phi(F_{t}).

∎

Another important essential property is that the stationary points of the modified
moment map flow are exactly the zeroes of
\phi
.

Proposition 3.1.2
.

The fixed point set of the modified moment map flow agrees with the
vanishing set of
\phi:{\mathscr{F}}_{0}\to{\mathbb{R}}
.

Proof.

This is an immediate consequence of Corollary
2.13.2
.
∎

The modified moment map flow has the short time existence property
according to the following theorem:

Theorem 3.1.3
.

For every
k\geq 0
,

\nu\in(0,1)
and
F\in{\mathscr{F}}_{0}^{k,\nu}
, there exists

\varepsilon>0
and a unique

,
defined for

such that
F_{0}=F
.

Proof.

The map

is polynomial of order
3
in the
coefficients of
F
by Proposition
2.13.1
.
In particular, it is locally Lipschitz with respect to
the

-norm.
By Proposition
2.5.1
, the Hodge projector

is
continuous for the

∎

Proof of Theorem
C

The result is just a restatement of Theorem

,
Proposition

and

3.1.1

∎

3.2.
Energy as a Morse-Bott function

By Proposition
2.13.1
, we have

D\phi|_{F}\cdot\dot{F}_{1}=-\langle\!\langle\mu(F),g({\mathscr{R}}F,\dot{F}_{1})\rangle\!\rangle

for every
F,\dot{F}_{1}\in{\mathscr{F}}
and
second variation of
\phi
with respect to some
\dot{F}_{2}\in{\mathscr{F}}
is given by

D^{2}\phi|_{F}\cdot(\dot{F}_{1},\dot{F}_{2})=\langle\!\langle D\mu|_{F}\cdot\dot{F}_{1},D\mu|_{F}\cdot\dot{F}_{2}\rangle\!\rangle-\langle\!\langle\mu(F),g({\mathscr{R}}\dot{F}_{2},\dot{F}_{1})\rangle\!\rangle.

In particular, if
\mu(F)=0
, we have

(3.3)

D^{2}\phi|_{F}\cdot(\dot{F}_{1},\dot{F}_{2})=\langle\!\langle D\mu|_{F}\cdot\dot{F}_{1},D\mu|_{F}\cdot\dot{F}_{2}\rangle\!\rangle

which proves the following lemma:

Lemma 3.2.1
.

Let
F\in{\mathscr{F}}
be an isotropic differential. Then,

is a non negative bilinear
form. Furthermore, the kernel of
D^{2}\phi|_{F}
is given
by the kernel of the linear map
\ker D\phi|_{F}
.

According to the above lemma, the functional

seems to behave like a
Morse-Bott function. The rest of this section is devoted to proving that
this is indeed the case, in a neighborhood
of a monomorphism.

Let
s_{0}
be the
0

.
The map

defines a local diffeomorphism between a neighborhood of

and a neighborhood of

in

It is a well known fact that

is endowed with a canonical

, where

is the

on

.
Furthermore, a map

,
sufficiently close to

in

for some closed differential
1
-form

[

]
.

It follows that the subspace of isotropic maps is a submanifold of

in a neighborhood of

. Furthermore, the

to the submanifold

\mathrm{Diff}_{k,\nu}(\Sigma)\times\Omega^{1,c}_{k,\nu}(\Sigma)
in a neighborhood of

, where

is the subspace of closed forms.

We now specialize to the particular case where

has real dimension

Let

of

By the Lagrangian neighborhood theorem

and a neighborhood

of

in

Every map

C^{1}

Furthemore,

We put

and deduce the following result:

Theorem 3.2.2
.

We assume that

and let

For

4.
Polyhedral isotropic surfaces

In this section, we adapt all the smooth constructions of §
2
and
§
3
to the
polyhedral
setting
.

4.1.
Definition of polyhedral surfaces

A
triangulation
of a surface
\Sigma
is a triple

{\mathscr{T}}=(\Sigma,{\mathscr{K}},\ell)
, where:

(1)

\Sigma
is a topological surface,

(2)

{\mathscr{K}}
is a locally finite
simplicial complex
, contained in some ambient affine
space
A
and

(3)

\ell:|{\mathscr{K}}|\to\Sigma
is a homeomorphism, where
|{\mathscr{K}}|
is the
topological space associated to the simplicial complex.

Given a triangulation
{\mathscr{T}}
, every simplex
\sigma\in{\mathscr{K}}

defines a subset
\ell(\sigma)\subset\Sigma
, homeomorphic to

\sigma
. It is often convenient to think of
\sigma
as a
domain in
\Sigma
, using the homeomorphism
\ell
and we will take
the liberty to drop
the reference to
\ell
in our notations.

Every simplex
\sigma
of an affine space
A
spans an affine subspace of

A
, with underlying vector space
denoted
\vec{\sigma}
, also called the
tangent direction of the
simplex

\sigma
.

Let
g_{\Sigma}=(g_{\sigma})_{\sigma\in{\mathscr{K}}}
, be a family,
where
g_{\sigma}
is a Euclidean inner product on
\vec{\sigma}
.
Suppose that for
every
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in{\mathscr{K}}
with

\sigma_{2}\subset\sigma_{1}
, the metric
g_{\sigma_{2}}
agrees with the
restriction of
g_{\sigma_{1}}
to
\vec{\sigma}_{2}
. In such a case, we
say that
g_{\Sigma}

is a
polyhedral metric
on
\Sigma
.

Definition 4.1.1
.

A topological surface
\Sigma
endowed with a triangulation

{\mathscr{T}}=(\Sigma,{\mathscr{K}},\ell)
and a polyhedral metric
g_{\Sigma}

is called a
polyhedral surface
.

The polyhedral metric
g_{\Sigma}
provides a flat Riemannian metric

g_{\sigma}
on each simplex
\sigma\in{\mathscr{K}}
. As in the smooth case
discussed at §
2.3
, the metric
g_{\sigma}

induces a fiberwise Euclidean inner product, denoted
g_{\sigma}
as well,
on all the
tensor bundles
over
the simplex

\sigma
. Together with
g_{V}
, we deduce a Euclidean fiberwise
inner product for the bundles of
\vec{V}
-valued forms on
\sigma
, also
denoted
g_{\sigma}
.
For simplicity of notation, these inner products are sometimes denoted

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and the corresponding norm is denoted
|\cdot|
.

4.2.
Whitney cohomology

The theory of Whitney forms and Whitney cohomology
[
21
]
is a generalisation, in
the piecewise linear setting, of smooth differential forms and de Rham cohomology.
The spaces of smooth differential forms
\Omega^{n}(\sigma)
on a simplex

\sigma\in{\mathscr{K}}
are compatible with pullbacks
in the following sense: for every

\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in{\mathscr{K}}
with

\sigma_{2}\subset\sigma_{1}
and
\beta_{1}\in\Omega^{n}(\sigma_{1})
, the pullback

\beta_{2}
of
\beta_{1}
on
\sigma_{2}
is a
smooth differential form as well.

We denote by
\Omega^{n}(\Sigma,{\mathscr{T}})
the space of families of differential
form

\beta=(\beta_{\sigma})_{\sigma\in{\mathscr{K}}}
, where each

\beta_{\sigma}\in\Omega^{n}(\Sigma)
.
Given
\beta=(\beta_{\sigma})\in\Omega^{n}(\Sigma,{\mathscr{T}})
,
suppose that for every
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in{\mathscr{K}}
, with

\sigma_{2}\subset\sigma_{1}
, the
pull back of
\beta_{\sigma_{1}}
agrees with
\beta_{\sigma_{2}}
. Then we say that

\beta
satifies the
Whitney condition
, or that
\beta
is a
Whitney
form
.
The space of Whitney forms is denoted
\Omega_{w}^{n}(\Sigma,{\mathscr{T}})
.

There is a natural exterior derivative
d:\Omega^{n}(\Sigma,{\mathscr{T}})\to\Omega^{n+1}(\Sigma,{\mathscr{T}})
given by
d\beta=(d\beta_{\sigma})_{\sigma\in{\mathscr{K}}}
. Furthermore, the spaces of Whitney forms are
preserved by
d
and we obtain the Whitney complex

d:\Omega^{n}_{w}(\Sigma,{\mathscr{T}})\to\Omega_{w}^{n+1}(\Sigma,{\mathscr{T}})

which defines the Whitney cohomology
denoted

H^{n}_{w}(\Sigma,{\mathbb{R}},{\mathscr{T}})
. The constructions readily extend to the case of

\vec{V}
-valued differential forms and we obtain the Whitney cohomology
spaces
H^{n}_{w}(\Sigma,\vec{V},{\mathscr{T}})
.
Similarly to the de Rham cohomology, the Whitney cohomology
agrees with the usual cohomology
spaces

H^{n}(\Sigma,{\mathbb{R}})\simeq H^{n}_{w}(\Sigma,{\mathbb{R}},{\mathscr{T}})
and

H^{n}(\Sigma,\vec{V})\simeq H^{n}_{w}(\Sigma,\vec{V},{\mathscr{T}})
. In particular, the
Whitney cohomology
does not depend on the choice of triangulation
{\mathscr{T}}
.

4.3.
Orientation and Kähler structure

An
orientation
of the simplicial complex

{\mathscr{K}}
of a polyhedral surface
\Sigma
is also called an orientation of
\Sigma
.
An
oriented polyhedral surface carries
a canonical area form

\omega_{\Sigma}=(\omega_{\sigma})_{\sigma\in{\mathscr{K}}}\in\Omega^{2}_{w}(\Sigma,{\mathscr{T}})

characterized by

(1)

\omega_{\sigma}
is an exterior
2
-form on
\vec{\sigma}
.

(2)

For every
\sigma\in{\mathscr{K}}_{2}
, the volume form
\omega_{\sigma}
is
compatible with the orientation of the facet and

|\omega_{\sigma}|=1
, with respect to
g_{\sigma}
.

(3)

If
\sigma\in{\mathscr{K}}
is an edge or a vertex then

\omega_{\sigma}=0
.

The combination of
\omega_{\sigma}
and
g_{\sigma}
defines a family
of almost complex structures

J_{\Sigma}=(J_{\sigma})_{\sigma\in{\mathscr{K}}_{2}}
on the facets of
{\mathscr{K}}
, where

J_{\sigma}:\vec{\sigma}\to\vec{\sigma}
is an almost complex structure on

\vec{\sigma}
, with the property that

\omega_{\sigma}=g_{\sigma}(J_{\sigma}\cdot,\cdot)
.

In conclusion, an oriented polyhedral surface
\Sigma
is endowed with
the structures

({\mathscr{T}},g_{\Sigma},J_{\Sigma},\omega_{\Sigma})
, referred to as a
polyhedral
Kähler structure
, a notion introduced by
Dmitri Panov in
[
16
]
, where he investigates the
4
dimensional case.

Remark 4.3.1
.

A polyhedral Kähler surface
\Sigma
as above admits a smooth structure

[
16
,
19
,
20
]

such that

(1)

g_{\Sigma}
and
\omega_{\Sigma}
are smooth away from the
vertices of the triangulation.

(2)

The metric may have conical singularities at the vertices.

(3)

The almost complex structure
J_{\Sigma}
is smooth on

\Sigma
.

In presence of conical singularities, the map
\ell:\sigma\to\Sigma
is not smooth at the
vertices. In particular, the smooth differential forms on
\Sigma
do not
necessarily induce smooth families of Whitney forms in

\Omega^{n}_{w}(\Sigma,{\mathscr{T}})
.

4.4.
Polyhedral maps and differentials

We continue our constructions and analogies with the smooth setting,
when
\Sigma
is a closed oriented
polyhedral surface.

A map
f:\Sigma\to V
, or more generally a map to some affine space,
is called
polyhedral
with respect to
{\mathscr{T}}
if:

(1)

the map
f
is continuous and

(2)

the restriction of
f
to every simplex of the triangulation

{\mathscr{T}}
is an affine map.

The space of polyhedral maps is denoted

{\mathscr{M}}({\mathscr{T}})=\{f:\Sigma\to V,\mbox{ $f$ is ${\mathscr{T}}$-polyhedral}\}.

The restriction of a polyhedral map
f
to every simplex

\sigma\in{\mathscr{T}}
is affine, hence differentiable. However,
f
is generally
not differentiable at every point of
\Sigma
. Nevertheless, we can define
the differential
df
as a family

df=(df|_{\sigma})_{\sigma\in{\mathscr{K}}}.

By construction,
df
is a Whitney form

df\in\Omega^{1}_{w}(\Sigma,\vec{V},{\mathscr{T}})
. The moduli space
{\mathscr{M}}({\mathscr{T}})
is an
affine space with underlying vector space contained in

\Omega^{0}_{w}(\Sigma,{\mathscr{T}},\vec{V})
and it follows that
df
is an

exact
Whitney form.

These observations motivate the introduction of a space of constant
\vec{V}
-valued
differentials, closely related to
\Omega^{1}(\Sigma,\vec{V},{\mathscr{T}})
:

{\mathscr{F}}({\mathscr{T}})=\{(F_{\sigma})_{\sigma\in{\mathscr{K}}_{2}},F_{\sigma}\in\vec{\sigma}^{*}\otimes\vec{V}\},

where
\vec{\sigma}^{*}
is the dual of the tangent direction
\vec{\sigma}
.
An element of
\vec{\sigma}^{*}\otimes\vec{V}
is a linear map

F_{\sigma}:\vec{\sigma}\to\vec{V}
and
this map can be regarded as a constant
\vec{V}
-valued differential
1
-form
on
\sigma
.

However, there are no compatibility conditions a priori along the edges of the
triangulation. We introduce the subspace of
Whitney
forms
which is a slightly different condition since
\sigma\in{\mathscr{K}}_{2}
:
an element
F=(F_{\sigma})_{\sigma\in{\mathscr{K}}_{2}}\in{\mathscr{F}}({\mathscr{T}})
satisfies the
Whitney
condition
if for every
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in{\mathscr{K}}_{2}
with a common edge

\sigma_{3}
, the pullbacks of
F_{\sigma_{1}}
and
F_{\sigma_{2}}
agree along

\sigma_{3}
. In this case,
F
is called a
Whitney form
and we define:

{\mathscr{F}}_{w}({\mathscr{T}})=\{F\in{\mathscr{F}}({\mathscr{T}}),\quad F\mbox{ is a Whitney form}\}.

Thanks to the Whitney condition, a Whitney form
F\in{\mathscr{F}}_{w}({\mathscr{T}})
defines via the
pullback an extended
family
(F_{\sigma})
for
\sigma\in{\mathscr{K}}
which is understood as an element
of
\Omega^{1}_{w}(\Sigma,\vec{V},{\mathscr{T}})
.
Hence, we have a
canonical embedding

{\mathscr{F}}_{w}({\mathscr{T}})\hookrightarrow\Omega^{1}_{w}(\Sigma,\vec{V},{\mathscr{T}}).

The elements
F\in{\mathscr{F}}_{w}({\mathscr{T}})
are families of constant differential
forms along the vertices of
{\mathscr{T}}
, hence they are
closed
. Thus, every
F

defines a Whitney cohomology class denoted
[F]\in H^{1}_{w}(\Sigma,\vec{V})
.
We define the subspace of exact forms in
{\mathscr{F}}_{w}({\mathscr{T}})
by

{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{F}})=\{F\in{\mathscr{F}}_{w}({\mathscr{T}}),\quad[F]=0\}.

By construction, the differential
d
defines a map

d:{\mathscr{M}}({\mathscr{T}})\to{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}}).

Furthermore the map
d
induced an homeomorphism

d:{\mathscr{M}}({\mathscr{T}})/\vec{V}\to{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}}).

where
\vec{V}
acts
by translations on
{\mathscr{M}}({\mathscr{T}})
.

4.5.
Moduli space structure in the polyhedral setting

The space
{\mathscr{F}}({\mathscr{T}})
carries a Kähler structure defined similarly to
the smooth setting. For
F=(F_{\sigma})
and
H=(H_{\sigma})\in{\mathscr{F}}({\mathscr{T}})
,
we put

{\mathcal{G}}(F,H)=\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathscr{K}}_{2}}\int_{\sigma}\langle F_{\sigma},H_{\sigma}\rangle\omega_{\sigma}.

The integrands of the above integrals are constant on each simplex. Hence
each term of the above integral is equal to

\langle F_{\sigma},H_{\sigma}\rangle\mathrm{area}(\sigma)
, where
\mathrm{area}(\sigma)
is the area
of
\sigma
with respect to the Euclidean metric
g_{\sigma}
.
As in the smooth case, we will use the notation
\langle\!\langle F,H\rangle\!\rangle={\mathcal{G}}(F,H)
and

\|F\|_{L^{2}}
for the norm deduced from
{\mathcal{G}}
.

An almost complex structure

{\mathcal{J}}
on
{\mathscr{F}}({\mathscr{T}})
is given by

({\mathcal{J}}F)_{\sigma}=-F_{\sigma}\circ J_{\sigma}.

By construction
{\mathcal{J}}
is compatible with
{\mathcal{G}}
and we obtain a Kähler form

\Omega={\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{J}}\cdot,\cdot).

Finally, we have defined a flat Kähler structure

({\mathscr{F}}({\mathscr{T}}),{\mathcal{G}},{\mathcal{J}},\Omega)

on the moduli space.

As in the smooth case, the almost complex structure
i
acts by
multiplication on
\vec{V}
-valued forms and we obtain a linear involution

{\mathscr{R}}
on
{\mathscr{F}}({\mathscr{T}})
, defined by

{\mathscr{R}}F=i{\mathcal{J}}F.

The involution gives an orthogonal splitting into eigenspaces

{\mathscr{F}}({\mathscr{T}})={\mathscr{F}}^{+}({\mathscr{T}})\oplus{\mathscr{F}}^{-}({\mathscr{T}})

and we introduce the
{\mathcal{G}}
-orthogonal projection onto the subspace of exact
differentials

\Pi:{\mathscr{F}}({\mathscr{T}})\to{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}}).

4.6.
Polyhedral symplectic density

We denote denote
by
\mathfrak{t}^{\mathbb{C}}({\mathscr{T}})
the space of maps

\zeta:{\mathscr{K}}_{2}\to{\mathbb{C}}
. Equivalently, an element
\zeta
can be understood
as a family of constant maps
(\zeta_{\sigma})_{\sigma\in{\mathscr{K}}_{2}}
on each facet

\sigma
of the triangulation.
We also denote by
\mathfrak{t}({\mathscr{T}})
the space of real valued maps

\zeta:{\mathscr{K}}_{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}
and we define

\mu:{\mathscr{F}}({\mathscr{T}})\to\mathfrak{t}({\mathscr{T}})

by the formula

\mu(F)(\sigma)=-\frac{1}{2}\langle({\mathscr{R}}F)_{\sigma},F_{\sigma}\rangle.

We can make sense of the pullback
F^{*}\omega_{V}
as a family of differential

2
-forms along each facet defined by

(F^{*}_{\sigma}\omega_{V})(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})=\omega_{V}(F_{\sigma}\cdot\eta_{1},F_{\sigma}\cdot\eta_{2})

for every
\eta_{1},\eta_{2}\in\vec{\sigma}
, and
\sigma\in{\mathscr{K}}_{2}
.
As in the smooth case, the map
\mu
is a symplectic density, in the sense
of the following lemma:

Lemma 4.6.1
.

For every
F\in{\mathscr{F}}({\mathscr{T}})
, we have the identity

(4.1)

\mu(F)(\sigma)=-\frac{F_{\sigma}^{*}\omega_{V}}{\omega_{\sigma}}

for every facet
\sigma\in{\mathscr{K}}_{2}
.

This motivates the following definition:

Definition 4.6.2
.

A differential
F\in{\mathscr{F}}({\mathscr{T}})
is called isotropic if

F_{\sigma}:\vec{\sigma}\to V
is isotropic for every
\sigma\in{\mathscr{K}}_{2}
.
A polyhedral map
f\in{\mathscr{M}}({\mathscr{T}})
is called isotropic if
the pullback of
\omega_{V}
by
f
vanishes along every simplex of
the triangulation.

In particular, by Lemma (
4.6.1
), we have

Lemma 4.6.3
.

A differential
F\in{\mathscr{F}}({\mathscr{T}})
is isotropic if, and only if,

\mu(F)=0
.
A polyhedral map
f\in{\mathscr{M}}({\mathscr{T}})
is isotropic if, and only if,

F=df
is isotropic.

4.7.
Hamiltonian gauge group action

We define the complex gauge group

{\mathbb{T}}^{\mathbb{C}}({\mathscr{T}})
as the space of non vanishing complex valued functions

\lambda:{\mathscr{K}}_{2}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
.
Alternatively,
\lambda
can be thought of as a family of constant
functions
(\lambda_{\sigma})
along each facet of the triangulation.
The group
{\mathbb{T}}^{\mathbb{C}}({\mathscr{T}})
acts on
{\mathscr{F}}({\mathscr{T}})
via the formula

(4.2)

\lambda\cdot F=\bar{\lambda}^{-1}F^{+}+\lambda F^{-},

where
F^{\pm}
are the component of
F
given by the
splitting
{\mathscr{F}}({\mathscr{T}})={\mathscr{F}}^{+}({\mathscr{T}})\oplus{\mathscr{F}}^{-}({\mathscr{T}})
.
The real subgroup
{\mathbb{T}}({\mathscr{T}})
is the the space of functions

\lambda:{\mathscr{K}}_{2}\to S^{1}
and acts by complex multiplication on

{\mathscr{F}}({\mathscr{T}})
.
The exponential map

\exp:\mathfrak{t}^{\mathbb{C}}({\mathscr{T}})\to{\mathbb{T}}^{\mathbb{C}}({\mathscr{T}})

defined by

\exp\zeta=e^{i\zeta},

shows that
\mathfrak{t}^{\mathbb{C}}({\mathscr{T}})
is the Lie algebra of
{\mathbb{T}}^{\mathbb{C}}({\mathscr{T}})
and
identifies the Lie algebra of the subgroup
\mathfrak{t}({\mathscr{T}})
with
\mathfrak{t}({\mathscr{T}})
.
The infinitesimal action of
\mathfrak{t}^{\mathbb{C}}({\mathscr{T}})
on
{\mathscr{F}}({\mathscr{T}})
is given by

X_{\zeta}(F)=i\bar{\zeta}F^{+}+i\zeta F^{-}.

As in the smooth case, the construction has the following nice properties:

Proposition 4.7.1
.

The
{\mathbb{T}}^{\mathbb{C}}({\mathscr{T}})
-action on
{\mathscr{F}}({\mathscr{T}})
perserves the almost complex
structure
{\mathcal{J}}
. Furthermore, the
{\mathbb{T}}^{\mathbb{C}}({\mathscr{T}})
-action is the

{\mathcal{J}}
-complexification of the
{\mathbb{T}}({\mathscr{T}})
-action.
The
{\mathbb{T}}
-action preserves the metric
{\mathcal{G}}
and the
symplectic form
\Omega
as well.
Furthermore the
{\mathbb{T}}({\mathscr{T}})
-action is Hamiltonian with moment map

\mu:{\mathscr{F}}({\mathscr{T}})\to\mathfrak{t}({\mathscr{T}})
given by
Formula (
4.1
). More
precisely,
\mu
is
{\mathbb{T}}({\mathscr{T}})
-invariant and for every

\zeta\in\mathfrak{t}({\mathscr{T}})
, we have

D\langle\!\langle\mu,\zeta\rangle\!\rangle=-\iota_{X_{\zeta}}\Omega.

Proof.

The proof is formally identical to the smooth setting.
∎

4.8.
Polyhedral modified moment map flow

By analogy with the smooth setting, we consider the energy of the moment
map

\phi:{\mathscr{F}}({\mathscr{T}})\to{\mathbb{R}}

defined by

\phi(F)=\frac{1}{2}\|\mu(F)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}.

By contruction,
\phi
is non negative and its vanishing set is the
subspace of isotropic differentials in
{\mathscr{F}}({\mathscr{T}})
.
We denote by
\nabla\phi
the gradient of
\phi:{\mathscr{F}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
with respect
to the metric
{\mathcal{G}}
. The gradient of the restricted functional

\phi:{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}})\to{\mathbb{R}}
is denoted
\nabla^{\circ}\phi
and satisfies

\nabla^{\circ}\phi(F)=\Pi(\nabla\phi(F))
.
Then we define the polyhedral modified moment map flow by the evolution
equation along
{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}})
:

(4.3)

\frac{\partial F}{\partial t}=-\nabla^{\circ}\phi(F).

As in the smooth case, the formal identities are the same and we can prove:

Proposition 4.8.1
.

The critical points of the restricted functional

\phi:{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}})\to{\mathbb{R}}
, in other words the fixed points of
the modified moment map flow, agree with its vanishing locus.

For every
F\in{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}})
, there exists a
polyhedral map
f\in{\mathscr{M}}({\mathscr{T}})
such that
F=df
, unique up to the action of

\vec{V}
with the property that
f
is isotropic if, and only if,
\phi(F)=0
.

Remark 4.8.2
.

The Second statement of the proposition implies that the modified moment
map flow defines a flow for polyhedral maps as well: we fix a point

x_{0}\in\Sigma
and a point
v_{0}\in V
. For every

F\in{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{F}})
, we define the integral

f=\chi(F)\in{\mathscr{M}}({\mathscr{T}})
as the unique map such that
df=F
and
f(x_{0})=v_{0}
. Any solution
F_{t}
of the modified moment map
flow defines a family
f_{t}=\chi(F_{t})
solution of the evolution
equation

(4.4)

\boxed{\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}=-\chi\circ\nabla^{\circ}\phi(df)}

and vice-versa.
The fixed points of this flow are, by definition, the isotropic polyhedral maps.

Proposition 4.8.3
.

A solution of the polyhedral modified moment map flow

F_{t}\in{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}})
, defined for
t
in some interval
satisfies

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\|F\|^{2}_{L^{2}}=-8\phi(F_{t}).

In particular, the
L^{2}
-norm is non increasing along the interval.

Proof.

The ingredients of the proof are the same as in Theorem
3.1.1
,
for the smooth setting. In the polyhedral context, we have the
identity

(4.5)

D\phi|_{F}\cdot F=4\phi(F)

identical to the one in Proposition
2.13.1
and the rest of the
argument is identical.
∎

Proposition
4.8.3
has the following strong consequence:

Corollary 4.8.4
.

For every
F\in{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}})
, there exists a
unique solution
F_{t}
defined for
t\in[0,+\infty)
and such that

F_{0}=F
.
Furthermore,
\|F_{t}\|_{L^{2}}
is bounded by
\|F\|_{L^{2}}
.

Proof.

The short time existence is immediate Proposition
4.8.3

and classical ODE theory. The decay of the

L^{2}
-norm implies that a solution of the flow cannot blow up in
finite time, which implies the long time existence.
∎

4.9.
A Duistermaat theorem

In fact, the polyhedral modified moment map flow converges and provides a
strong deformation retraction onto the
space of polyhedral isotropic maps.

Theorem 4.9.1
.

For every
F\in{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}})
, the solution
F_{t}
of the modified moment
map flow, defined for
t\in[0,+\infty)
with
F_{0}=F
converges
toward a limit
F_{\infty}
such that
\phi(F_{\infty})=0
.
Thus, we can define an extended flow

\Theta:[0,+\infty]\times{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}})\to{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}})

by
\Theta(t,F)=F_{t}
and
\Theta(+\infty,F)=F_{\infty}
.
Furthermore, the map
\Theta
is a strong deformation retraction onto the vanishing
locus of
\phi:{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}})\to{\mathbb{R}}
.

Proof.

The proof is essentially contained in the analogous result

[
18
, Theorem 7.8.4]
, where a downward gradient flow is also
studied in a finite dimensional situation.
Although the space
{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}})
, the functional
\phi:{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}})\to{\mathbb{R}}
and the
Euclidean metric
{\mathcal{G}}
are completely different in

[
18
]
, they share similar formal properties:

(1)

The vector space is finite dimensional.

(2)

The functional
\phi
is a polynomial
function.

(3)

The functional
\phi
is non negative.

(4)

The critical points of
\phi
agree with its
vanishing set.

(5)

The solutions of the downward gradient flow satisfy the
decay property of Proposition
4.8.3

.

Properties (
1
) and (
5
) imply the long time
existence of the flow proved at Corollary
4.8.4
.
Property (
2
) implies that
\phi
is analytic. In
particular the Łojaziewicz inequality can be applied to
\phi
,
which is crucial to prove the convergence of the flow lines.
The
interested reader may check that only these properties are used in
the proof of
[
18
, Theorem 7.8.4]
and that they imply
the congergence and continuity of the flow.
∎

4.10.
Regular points of the moduli space

We obtain much stronger result for the behavior of the modified moment map flow in the
polyhedral setting, as proved by Theorem
4.9.1
. On the contrary, the
regularity of the space of isotropic maps is much easier to study in the
smooth setting, at least
in a neighborhood of an immersion, as showed at §
3.2
. However,
some partial results can be obtained in the polyhedral case, as we are
going to see in this
section.

Definition 4.10.1
.

A differential form
F\in{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}})
is called regular, if
the differential of
the restricted moment map
\mu:{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}})\to\mathfrak{t}({\mathscr{T}})
has constant
rank in a neighborhood of
F
.

By definition, the vanishing locus of
\phi:{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}})\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a
submanifold of
{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}})
in a neighborhood of a regular point
F
.
Furthermore, the following theorem shows that
\phi
behaves like a
Morse-Bott function on such a neighborhood:

Theorem 4.10.2
.

Let
F\in{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}})
be a regular point of the vanishing locus
of
\phi
. Then, there exists an open neighborhood

U\subset{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}})
of
F
, such
that:

(1)

the vanishing locus of
\phi:U\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a submanifold
of
U
;

(2)

the Hessian of
\phi
is positive definite in
direction transverse to the vanishing locus;

(3)

U
is invariant under the modified moment map flow

\Theta
, which has an exponential convergence rate.

Proof.

The Hessian of
\phi
is calculated
in the smooth
setting at §
3.2
and is given a point
F

such
\phi(F)=0
by Formula (
3.3
).
The same calculations show that the formula holds in the polyhedral
setting as well and obtain

D^{2}\phi|_{F}\cdot(\dot{F}_{1},\dot{F}_{2})=\langle\!\langle D\mu|_{F}\cdot\dot{F}_{1},D\mu|_{F}\cdot\dot{F}_{2}\rangle\!\rangle

a any point
F
of the vanishing locus of

\phi:{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}})\to{\mathbb{R}}
.
If
F
is a regular, the vanishing locus of
\phi:{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}})\to{\mathbb{R}}
is
a submanifold of
{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}})

in a neighborhood of
F
and the above formula shows
that the Hessian of
\phi
is definite positive in transverse
directions to the submanifold.
In conclusion,
\phi
behaves like a Morse-Bott function in a neighborhood
of
F
and its vanishing set is a stable critical component. The statements
of the theorem are classical facts of ODE theory under these assumptions.
∎

Constructing regular points of the moduli
space is not a simple problem. We are going to show that
Theorem
1.1.1
provides a construction of regular points.
In
[
11
]
, a quotient
2
-torus

\Sigma={\mathbb{R}}^{2}/\Gamma
is considered together with
a smooth isotropic immersion
f:\Sigma\looparrowright{\mathbb{R}}
.
Refinements of stepsize
{\mathscr{O}}(N^{-1})
of
the lattice
\Gamma
provide a family of quadrangulations
{\mathcal{Q}}_{N}
of
\Sigma
, with
N^{2}
facets.
The quadrangulations can be completed into triangulations
{\mathscr{T}}_{N}
by replacing each
quadrilateral of
{\mathcal{Q}}_{N}
with a pyramid.
The main construction of
[
11
]
provides a sequence of polyhedral isotropic maps

f_{N}\in{\mathscr{M}}({\mathscr{T}}_{N})
, for every
N
sufficiently large, such that
f_{N}

approximates
f
in
C^{1}
-norm by
[
17
]
.
The construction of
f_{N}
relies on an effective version of the fixed point principle
applied to the family of symplectic densities

\mu_{N}\circ d:{\mathscr{M}}({\mathscr{T}}_{N})\to\mathfrak{t}({\mathscr{T}}_{N}),

where
\mu_{N}:{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}}_{N})\to\mathfrak{t}({\mathscr{T}}_{N})
is the moment map.
The fact that the cohomology class of
\omega_{V}
vanishes implies that

\mu_{N}\circ d
takes values in the subspace of
\zeta\in\mathfrak{t}({\mathscr{T}}_{N})

orthogonal to constant functions. Hence
\mu_{N}\circ d
can never be a
submersion, but it may have maximal rank with corank one.
A careful examination of
the proof in
[
11
]
allows to make the following observation:

Lemma 4.10.3
.

For every
N
sufficiently large, the differential of the map
\mu_{N}\circ d:{\mathscr{M}}({\mathscr{T}}_{N})\to\mathfrak{t}({\mathscr{T}}_{N})
has maximal rank
at
f_{N}
, where
f_{N}
is the Jauberteau-Rollin-Tapie approximation
of
f
. In particular,
F_{N}=df_{N}\in{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}}_{N})
is a regular
point of the vanishing set of
\phi:{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}}_{N})\to{\mathbb{R}}
.

We can now complete the proofs of the main theorems given in the
introduction.

Proof of Theorem
G
.

The result is an immediate consequence of Lemma
4.10.3
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
E
.

The result is a combination of Proposition
4.8.1
, Theorem
4.9.1
and
Theorem
4.10.2
. The statement
(3)
will be proved
at the end of §
4.11
.
∎

Proof of Corollary
F
.

The differential
d:{\mathscr{M}}({\mathscr{T}})\to{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}})
admits a unique right inverse

\chi:{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}})\to{\mathscr{M}}({\mathscr{T}})

defined by the condition that
d\circ\chi=\mathrm{id}
and

f=\chi(F)
satisfies
f(x_{0})=v_{0}
for some
marked points
x_{0}\in\Sigma
and
v_{0}\in V
.
We consider the continuous map

\hat{\Theta}(t,f)=\chi\circ\Theta(t,df)+(f(x_{0})-v_{0}).

By construction

\hat{\Theta}:[0,+\infty]\times{\mathscr{M}}({\mathscr{T}})\to{\mathscr{M}}({\mathscr{T}})

defines a strong deformation retraction of
{\mathscr{M}}({\mathscr{T}})
onto the subspace of isotropic
polyhedral maps. It is easy to see that
\hat{\Theta}
is the flow
of the evolution equation on
{\mathscr{M}}({\mathscr{T}})
defined at
Remark
4.8.2
. In particular
\Theta_{t}\circ d=d\circ\hat{\Theta}_{t}
, which proves the corollary.
∎

4.11.
Topology of the moduli space

The homotopy of the space of smooth isotropic immersions

f:\Sigma\looparrowright V
can be reduced to a matter of algebraic
topology,
thanks to the Gromov-Lees theorem
[
9
,
13
]
: every isotropic immersion
f
defines
an exact differential form
F=df\in\Omega^{1}(\Sigma,\vec{V})
which is by
definition an
isotropic monomorphism.
The Gromov-Lees theorem establishes an h-principle, which shows that the
differential
d:{\mathscr{M}}/\vec{V}\to{\mathscr{F}}_{0}

induces a homotopy equivalence from the space of isotropic immersions to
the space of isotropic monomorphisms. Computing the homotopy of the latter
space turns out to be a basic problem of algebraic topology, related to
the Grassmanian
of isotropic planes in
\vec{V}
.

Unfortunately, the Gromov-Lees theory does not extend immediately to
the polyhedral setting. Thus, most topological properties for
the space of polyhedral isotropic
immersions are open questions although the partial results of

[
11
,
17
,
6
]
lead us to state
Conjecture
H
.

The map
d:{\mathscr{M}}({\mathscr{T}})/\vec{V}\to{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}})
is a homeomorphism, that
identifies the subspace of polyhedral isotropic maps modulo
\vec{V}
with
the space of exact isotropic differentials.
The latter space is a cone in

{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}})
, which implies that it is contractible. Therefore,
the space of polyhedral isotropic maps is homotopically trivial. The same
property holds in the smooth setting, which is why the problem of
immersions is
considered instead. There are several issues here to formulate analogous
questions in the polyhedral setting:

(1)

A polyhedral map

f\in{\mathscr{M}}({\mathscr{T}})
which is a topological immersion define a monomorphism

F=df\in{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}})
. However the converse is generally not true and
some local injectivity condition along the skeleton of the triangulation
should be added to obtain a correspondence.

(2)

The space of monomorphisms in
{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}})
does not seem to be
preserved by the modified moment map flow
\Theta
. From this point, the prospect of
using the flow to
investigate the homotopy type of the space of polyhedral isotropic
immersions seems rather low.

However the space of non constant polyhedral isotropic maps may already contain
some non trivial topology. Furthemore, this subspace modulo
\vec{V}
is
identified to
{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}})\setminus 0
via
d
. Finally,
{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}})\setminus 0

is invariant under the gauge group action and invariant under the modified
moment map flow, for finite time.

Question 4.11.1
.

What is the homotopy type of the space of non constant polyhedral isotropic maps

f\in{\mathscr{M}}({\mathscr{T}})
?

The space of isotropic exact differentials in
{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}})\setminus 0
is
invariant by scaling. In particular, it is homotopically
equivalent to the vanishing set of the restriction
\phi:{\mathbb{S}}({\mathscr{T}})\to{\mathbb{R}}
, where

{\mathbb{S}}({\mathscr{T}})
is the unit sphere in
{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}})
.

The idea to tackle Question
4.11.1
is to interpret
\phi:{\mathbb{S}}({\mathscr{T}})\to{\mathbb{R}}
as a Morse-Bott function
to obtain some dynamical interaction between its critical components. By
definition the critical set of
\phi:{\mathbb{S}}({\mathscr{T}})\to{\mathbb{R}}
contains its
vanishing set, but there may be other critical points,
called the
solitons
. More explicitly, a soliton
F\in{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}})
is
a solution of the equation

\nabla^{\circ}\phi(F)=\kappa F

for some
\kappa\in{\mathbb{R}}
. The constant
\kappa
is determined by
Formula
4.5
and we obtain the soliton equation:

(4.6)

\boxed{\|F\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\nabla^{\circ}\phi(F)=4\phi(F).}

Solitons are by definition the fixed points of the downward gradient flow of the
restricted functional
\phi:{\mathbb{S}}({\mathscr{T}})\to{\mathbb{R}}
defined for
F\in{\mathbb{S}}({\mathscr{T}})
by

(4.7)

\boxed{\frac{\partial F}{\partial t}=4\phi(F)-\nabla^{\circ}\phi(F)}

and called the
renormalized
polyhedral flow.

Many questions are open at this stage for the renormalized flow and the
solitons:

(1)

Is
\phi:{\mathbb{S}}({\mathscr{T}})\to{\mathbb{R}}
a Morse-Bott type function, in
some sense, perhaps up to
the choice a refinement of the triangulation and a generic polyhedral metric ?

(2)

What are the intrinsic geometrical properties of polyhedral maps

f\in{\mathscr{M}}({\mathscr{T}})
such that
F=df
is a soliton ?

(3)

Does the functional
\phi:{\mathbb{S}}({\mathscr{T}})\to{\mathbb{R}}
define a
Morse-Bott cohomology theory ?

We can prove that solitons never reduce to the vanishing locus of
\phi
.

Lemma 4.11.2
.

There exists solitons
G\in{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}})
such that
\phi(G)\neq 0
.

Proof.

Clearly,
\phi:{\mathbb{S}}({\mathscr{T}})\to{\mathbb{R}}
is not the zero map, as it is easy to
construct polyhedral maps
f\in{\mathscr{M}}({\mathscr{T}})
which are not isotropic. In
particular
\phi(df)>0
in this case. We conclude that
\phi
admits a
maximum at some point
G\in{\mathbb{S}}({\mathscr{T}})
with
\phi(G)>0
. If follows that

G
is a critical point of
\phi:{\mathbb{S}}({\mathscr{T}})\to{\mathbb{R}}
, which is to say a soliton. Furthermore,

\phi(G)>0
implies that
G
is not isotropic.
∎

Remark 4.11.3
.

We introduce a family
F_{t}\in{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}})
given by
F_{t}=r(t)G
, where

G\in{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}})
is a soliton such that
\phi(G)>0
and

r(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{8(t-t_{0})\phi(G)}}

is defined on the interval
(t_{0},+\infty)
, for some choice of
t_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
It is readily checked that
F_{t}
is a solution of the polyhedral modified
moment map flow. We notice that
F_{t}
defines a non trivial solution of
flow, with the property that
\displaystyle\lim_{t\to+\infty}F_{t}=0
.

Proof of Theorem
E
, item (3).

The existence of non isotropic solitons in
{\mathbb{S}}({\mathscr{T}})
provides
non trivial solutions of the polyhedral modified moment map flow as
discussed above.
This completes the proof of the theorem.
∎

4.12.
Numerical flow

The polyhedral modified moment map flow
is an ordinary differential evolution equation. A numerical
version of the flow can be implemented on a computer. In this section
we outline all the ingredients for such a computer program.
The code is being developed currently and is going to be released
very soon
[
10
]
.

For simplicity, we specialize to the case where
\Sigma
is
a quotient
2
-torus.
Let
\Gamma
be the lattice

\Gamma=\gamma_{1}{\mathbb{Z}}\oplus\gamma_{2}{\mathbb{Z}}\subset{\mathbb{C}}

where
\gamma_{1}=1

\gamma_{2}=e^{i\frac{\pi}{3}}
. The surface
\Sigma
, defined by

\Sigma={\mathbb{C}}/\Gamma,

is endowed with the flat Kähler structure

(\Sigma,g_{\Sigma},J_{\Sigma},\omega_{\Sigma})
deduced from the
canonical flat Kähler structure of
{\mathbb{C}}
.
The rhombus in
{\mathbb{C}}
with
vertices
0,\gamma_{1},\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}
and
\gamma_{2}
is a fundamental
domain for the action of
\Gamma
.
This rhombus is composed of two equilateral triangles and each triangle has
area
\frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}
. Using the
\Gamma
-action,
we obtain the familiar tiling of
{\mathbb{C}}
by equilateral triangles:

Figure 1.
Triangulation of
{\mathbb{C}}
by equilateral triangles

This tiling is a simplicial decomposition of
{\mathbb{C}}
and can be
regarded as a triangulation
{\mathscr{T}}^{\prime}
of
{\mathbb{C}}
.
Let
N
be a positive integer. We define the lattice

\Gamma_{N}=N^{-1}\Gamma,

understood as the set of vertices of the rescaled triangulation
{\mathscr{T}}^{\prime}_{N}

of
{\mathscr{T}}^{\prime}
, by a factor
N^{-1}
.
By
Definition, the triangulation
{\mathscr{T}}^{\prime}_{N}
is
\Gamma
-invariant. Hence,
{\mathscr{T}}^{\prime}_{N}
defines a triangulation of the quotient

\Sigma
, denoted

{\mathscr{T}}_{N}
, with each triangle of area
\frac{\sqrt{3}}{4N^{2}}
.

We consider the particular space
V={\mathbb{C}}^{2}

endowed with its canonical structure of Hermitian affine space and the
coordinates
(x_{1}+ix_{2},x_{3}+ix_{4})\in V
, where
x_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
The symplectic form
\omega_{V}
is given in coordinates by the formula

\omega_{V}=dx_{1}\wedge dx_{2}+dx_{3}\wedge dx_{4}

and the almost complex structure
i:\vec{V}\to\vec{V}
, given by the
multiplication by
i
satisfies the identities

(4.8)

i\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{2}}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad i\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{3}}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{4}}.

Every
F\in{\mathscr{F}}({\mathscr{T}}_{N})
is given by a collection

F=(F_{\sigma})_{\sigma\in{\mathscr{K}}_{2}}
where
F_{\sigma}\in\vec{\sigma}^{*}\otimes\vec{V}
.
We use the canoncial coordinates
z=u_{1}+iu_{2}\in{\mathbb{C}}
on the universal
cover
{\mathbb{C}}

of
\Sigma
.
By definition we have

(4.9)

-du_{1}\circ J_{\Sigma}=du_{2}\mbox{ and }-du_{2}\circ J_{\Sigma}=-du_{1}

The
du_{i}\otimes\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}
provide a
g_{\sigma}
-orthonormal
basis of
\vec{\sigma}^{*}\otimes\vec{V}
for each facet of the
triangulation
{\mathscr{T}}_{N}
.
Hence,
F_{\sigma}
admits a decomposition

(4.10)

F_{\sigma}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i\leq 2\\
1\leq j\leq 4\end{subarray}}F_{\sigma ij}du_{i}\otimes\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}

where
F_{\sigma ij}\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
In particular, for
F,H\in{\mathscr{F}}({\mathscr{T}}_{N})
, we have

\langle\!\langle F,H\rangle\!\rangle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i\leq 2\\
1\leq j\leq 4\\
\sigma\end{subarray}}F_{\sigma ij}H_{\sigma ij}\frac{\sqrt{3}}{4N^{2}}

where the sum is taken over all the facets
\sigma
of
{\mathscr{T}}_{N}
.

The space
{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}}_{N})
is the subspace of exact

\vec{V}
-valued Whitney forms in
{\mathscr{F}}({\mathscr{T}}_{N})
.
We need to construct an orthonormal basis of this subspace to compute the
matrix of the projector
\Pi:{\mathscr{F}}({\mathscr{T}}_{N})\to{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}}_{N})
.

We consider the affine canonical base of
V
given by the points

e_{0}=0
and
e_{j}=e_{0}+\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}
for
1\leq j\leq 4
.
For each vertex
\sigma_{0}
of
{\mathscr{T}}_{N}
and
1\leq j\leq 4
, we define the polyhedral
function
f^{\sigma_{0}j}:\Sigma\to V

given by

f^{{\sigma_{0}}j}(\sigma_{0}^{\prime})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}e_{j}&\mbox{if $\sigma_{0}=\sigma^{\prime}_{0}$,}\\
e_{0}&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

for every vertex
\sigma_{0}^{\prime}
of
{\mathscr{T}}_{N}
.
The above function extends uniquely as a polyhedral function

f^{\sigma_{0}j}\in{\mathscr{M}}({\mathscr{T}}_{N})

and defines the exact differential

F^{\sigma_{0}j}=df^{\sigma_{0}j}\in{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}}_{N}).

The family
F^{\sigma_{0}j}
for
\sigma_{0}\in{\mathscr{K}}_{0}
and
1\leq j\leq 4
spans
{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}}_{N})
, by definition.
The only relations between the
F^{\sigma j}
are the
4
relations given
by

\sum_{\sigma_{0}}F^{\sigma_{0}j}=0

for
1\leq j\leq 4
,
where the sum is taken over all the vertices of the triangulation

{\mathscr{T}}_{N}
.
We obtain the following lemma:

Lemma 4.12.1
.

For a fixed vertex
\sigma^{\prime}_{0}
of the triangulation
{\mathscr{T}}_{N}
, The family

F^{\sigma_{0}j}\in{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}}_{N}),

where
1\leq j\leq 4

and
\sigma_{0}
belongs to the vertices of
{\mathscr{T}}_{N}
with
\sigma_{0}\neq\sigma^{\prime}_{0}
,
is a basis of
{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}}_{N})
.

However, the family
F^{\sigma_{0}j}
is not orthonormal and we have to use
the Gram-Schmidt algorithm.
The
differential
F^{\sigma_{0}j}
can be computed explicitly,
working on the universal cover
{\mathbb{C}}
of
\Sigma
, and assuming
\sigma_{0}=0

for simplicity. There are six
triangles of
{\mathscr{T}}^{\prime}_{N}
with vertex
\sigma_{0}=0
. We can compute the differential of

f^{\sigma_{0}j}
on each triangle. In particular on
the triangle
\sigma_{1}
with vertices
0
,
\gamma_{1}/N
and

\gamma_{2}/N
we have

(4.11)

F^{\sigma_{0}j}_{\sigma_{1}}=\left(-Ndu_{1}-\frac{N}{\sqrt{6}}du_{2}\right)\otimes\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}.

The differential on all the other triangles with vertex
\sigma_{0}=0
is obtained by rotations of angle

\frac{\pi}{3}
. For every other triangle
\sigma
not in the star of

\sigma_{0}
, we have
F_{\sigma}^{\sigma_{0}j}=0
.
In particular, on the triangle
\sigma_{2}
with vertices
0
,
-\gamma_{1}/N
and

e^{2i\frac{\pi}{3}}/N
, we
have the formula

(4.12)

F^{\sigma_{0}j}_{\sigma_{2}}=\left(Ndu_{1}-\frac{N}{\sqrt{6}}du_{2}\right)\otimes\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}.

Lemma 4.12.2
.

The family of Whitney forms

F^{\sigma_{0}j}\in{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}}_{N})
described above
satisfies the identities

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle F^{\sigma_{0}j},

\displaystyle F^{\sigma_{0}^{\prime}j^{\prime}}\rangle\!\rangle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\frac{7\sqrt{3}}{4}&\mbox{ if $i=i^{\prime}$ and
$\sigma_{0}=\sigma_{0}^{\prime}$,}\\
-\frac{5}{4\sqrt{3}}&\mbox{if $i=i^{\prime}$ and
$\sigma_{0}$ and $\sigma_{0}^{\prime}$ are joined by an
edge,}\\
0&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Proof.

The third case is clear, since the families

(F_{\sigma}^{\sigma_{0}j})

and
(F_{\sigma}^{\sigma_{0}^{\prime}j^{\prime}})
have disjoint supports for
j\neq j^{\prime}
or if
\sigma_{0}
and
\sigma^{\prime}_{0}
do not belong to the same
facet of
{\mathscr{T}}_{N}
.

We assume now that, on the contrary,

j=j^{\prime}
and
\sigma_{0}
and
\sigma^{\prime}_{0}
belong to the same facet.
Then either
\sigma_{0}=\sigma^{\prime}_{0}
or
\sigma_{0}
and
\sigma^{\prime}_{0}
are
the two ends of an edge in
{\mathscr{T}}_{N}
.

In the first case, working on the universal cover and assuming

\sigma_{0}=0
, Formula (
4.11
)
shows that
|F^{\sigma_{0}j}_{\sigma_{1}}|^{2}=\frac{7N^{2}}{6}
. Using the
fact that the area of a facet is
\frac{\sqrt{3}}{4N^{2}}
and that
there are
6
facets in the star of
\sigma_{0}
, we find

\|F^{\sigma_{0}j}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}=\frac{7\sqrt{3}}{4}.

In the second case, working on the universal cover, we may assume that

\sigma_{0}^{\prime}=\frac{\gamma_{1}}{N}
. By Formulas (
4.11
)
and (
4.12
), we deduce that

\langle F^{\sigma_{0}j}_{\sigma_{1}},F^{\sigma_{0}^{\prime}j}_{\sigma_{1}}\rangle=-\frac{5N^{2}}{6}
.
There is a second facet of
{\mathscr{T}}_{N}
in the common support of
F^{\sigma_{0}j}

and
F^{\sigma_{0}^{\prime}j}
. By symmetry, the inner product is the same
on the second facet. Since the area of each triangle is equal to

\frac{\sqrt{3}}{4N^{2}}
, we obtain

\langle\!\langle F^{\sigma_{0}j},F^{\sigma_{0}^{\prime}j}\rangle\!\rangle=-\frac{5N^{2}}{6}\cdot 2\cdot\frac{\sqrt{3}}{4N^{2}}=-\frac{5}{4\sqrt{3}}.

∎

The family
F^{\sigma_{0}j}

provides a basis of
{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}}_{N})
thanks to
Lemma
4.12.1
and
the Gram-Schmidt method gives an algorithm to construct an orthonomal
basis on
{\mathscr{F}}_{0}({\mathscr{T}}_{N})
from this data.
Furthermore, the Gram-Schmidt method is pretty
straightforward regarding computer implementation thanks to the explicit
formulas of
Lemma
4.12.2
.

Eventually,
the matrix of the endomorphism

{\mathscr{R}}:{\mathscr{F}}_{N}({\mathscr{F}}_{N})\to{\mathscr{F}}({\mathscr{T}}_{N})

can be explicitely computed in the coordinates
given by (
4.10
), using Formula (
4.9
) and Formula (
4.8
) as

{\mathscr{R}}F=-iF\circ J_{\Sigma}
, by definition.
Thus, we have an algorithm to compute

\mu(F)=-\frac{1}{2}\langle{\mathscr{R}}F,F\rangle

as well as

\nabla\phi(F)=-\mu(F){\mathscr{R}}F
.
We also have a matrix representation for
\Pi
via Gram-Schmidt and we can compute

\nabla^{\circ}\phi(F)=\Pi(\nabla(F))
.
It is now easy to find approximate solutions of the polyhedral modified
moment map flow (
4.3
) via the Euler method. Similarly we can apply the Euler
approximation method to the polyhedral renormalized flow (
4.7
).
