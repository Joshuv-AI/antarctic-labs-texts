Abstract
. The determinant of a skew-symmetric matrix has a canonical square root given by the Pfaffian. Similarly, the resultant of two reciprocal polynomials of even degree has a canonical square root given by their reciprocant . Computing the reciprocant of two cyclotomic polynomials yields a short and elegant proof of the Law of Quadratic Reciprocity.



1.
Introduction

Let
p
be a prime number and let
a
be an integer not divisible by
p

The
Legendre symbol

\left(\frac{a}{p}\right)

\left(\frac{a}{p}\right)=1
if
a

\left(\frac{a}{p}\right)=-1

According to

a^{(p-1)/2}\equiv 1\pmod{p}
if
\left(\frac{a}{p}\right)=1
and
a^{(p-1)/2}\equiv-1\pmod{p}
if
\left(\frac{a}{p}\right)=-1

The Law of Quadratic Reciprocity, first proved by Gauss, asserts that there is an unexpected relationship between
\left(\frac{p}{q}\right)
and
\left(\frac{q}{p}\right)
when

are distinct odd primes, and a supplement to the law asserts that
\left(\frac{2}{p}\right)

Theorem 1.1

(Law of Quadratic Reciprocity)
.

(a)

If

and

are distinct odd primes then
\left(\frac{p}{q}\right)\left(\frac{q}{p}\right)=(-1)^{\frac{p-1}{2}\frac{q-1}{2}}
.

(b)

If
p

\left(\frac{2}{p}\right)=(-1)^{\frac{p^{2}-1}{8}}
.

There are currently more than 300 known proofs of the Law of Quadratic Reciprocity

The basic approach, via the identity

Our exposition is somewhat novel, in that a central role is played by an expression that we dub the
reciprocant

2.3.
Reciprocants

Over an integral domain, the reciprocant is a canonical square root of the resultant of two reciprocal polynomials.
More precisely:

Proposition 2.3
.

If
R
is an integral domain and
f,g\in R[x]
are monic reciprocal polynomials of even degree, then

\Res(f,g)=\Rec(f,g)^{2},

where
\Rec(f,g):=\Res(f^{\#},g^{\#})\in R
is the reciprocant of
f
and
g
.

Proof.

Let
K
be the fraction field of
R
and let
L
be a splitting field for
f
over
K
.
By Remark
2.2
, we can write
f(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}(x-\alpha_{i})(x-\alpha_{i}^{-1})
with
\alpha_{i}\in L
for all
i
.
In what follows, will apply (RES3) to the natural injective map
\phi:R\to L
.

Let
a_{i}=\alpha_{i}+\alpha_{i}^{-1}
for
i=1,\ldots,m
. We have:

\displaystyle\Res(f^{\#},g^{\#})^{2}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i}g^{\#}(a_{i})\cdot\prod_{i}g^{\#}(a_{i})\qquad\text{(by (RES1), (RES3), and \eqref{eq:product formula for gsharp})}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i}\alpha_{i}^{-m}g(\alpha_{i})\cdot\prod_{i}\alpha_{i}^{m}g(\alpha_{i}^{-1})\qquad\text{(by \eqref{eq:Chebyshev transform})}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i}g(\alpha_{i})\cdot\prod_{i}g(\alpha_{i}^{-1})

\displaystyle=\Res(f,g)\qquad\text{(by (RES1)).}

\blacksquare

We will use the following in our proof of Quadratic Reciprocity:

Proposition 2.4
.

If
g_{1},g_{2},h\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x]
are monic and reciprocal polynomials of even degree and

g_{1}\equiv g_{2}\pmod{n}

\Rec(g_{1},h)\equiv\Rec(g_{2},h)\pmod{n}

and

\Rec(h,g_{1})\equiv\Rec(h,g_{2})\pmod{n}.

A proof, based on property (RES3) of resultants, is given in Appendix B.

3.
Proof of the Law of Quadratic Reciprocity

For
n\geq 1
, define

(4)

g_{n}(x)=\frac{x^{n}-1}{x-1}=x^{n-1}+x^{n-2}+\cdots+x+1\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x].

If
p
is prime, then since
x^{p}-1\equiv(x-1)^{p}\pmod{p}
we have

(5)

g_{p}(x)\equiv(x-1)^{p-1}\pmod{p}.

Proposition 3.1
.

If
m,n
are relatively prime positive integers,
{\rm Res}(g_{m},g_{n})=1
.

Proof.

If
m=n=1
then
\Res(g_{m},g_{n})=\Res(1,1)=1
.
We may therefore suppose without loss of generality that
n>m
.
Note that since
{\rm gcd}(m,n)=1
, at least one of
m
and
n
is odd.

By the division algorithm, we can write
n=mq+r
with
q,r
integers such that
q\geq 0
and
0\leq r<q
.
Since at least one of
m
and
n
is odd, the same is true for
m
and
r
.

Working in the quotient ring
{\mathbb{Z}}[x]/(x^{m}-1)
, we have

\displaystyle x^{n}-1

\displaystyle\equiv(x^{m})^{q}\cdot x^{r}-1

\displaystyle\equiv 1^{q}\cdot x^{r}-1

\displaystyle\equiv x^{r}-1\pmod{x^{m}-1}.

In other words, there is a polynomial
h(x)\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x]
such that

x^{n}-1=(x^{m}-1)h(x)+x^{r}-1.

Dividing both sides by
x-1
gives

g_{n}(x)=g_{m}(x)h(x)+g_{r}(x).

By (RES4) and (RES2), we have

(6)

\Res(g_{m},g_{n})=\Res(g_{m},g_{r})=\Res(g_{r},g_{m}).

Since
{\rm gcd}(m,n)=1
, it follows from (
6
) and the Euclidean algorithm that there is an integer
k\geq 1
such that

\Res(g_{m},g_{n})=\Res(g_{k},g_{1})=\Res(g_{k},1)=1.

\blacksquare

Remark 3.2
.

Conversely, if
{\rm gcd}(m,n)=d>1
then (RES1) and (RES3) (applied to the natural injection
\phi:{\mathbb{Z}}\hookrightarrow{\mathbb{C}}
) imply that
\Res(g_{m},g_{n})=0
, since a primitive
d^{\rm th}
root of unity in
{\mathbb{C}}
is a common root of
g_{m}
and
g_{n}
.

Remark 3.3
.

Here is an alternate proof of Proposition
3.1
which is arguably more conceptual, but somewhat less elementary.
First, observe that if
K
is any field and
\alpha\in K
satisfies both
\alpha^{m}=1
and
\alpha^{n}=1
, with
{\rm gcd}(m,n)=1
, then necessarily
\alpha=1
.
Let
p
be a prime number, let
{\mathbf{F}}_{p}
be the finite field of order
p
, and let
\phi:{\mathbb{Z}}\to{\mathbf{F}}_{p}
be the natural homomorphism.
Applying (RES3) to
\phi
implies, together with (RES1) and the above observation with
K={\mathbf{F}}_{p}
, that
\Res(g_{m},g_{n})\not\equiv 0\pmod{p}
.
Since this holds for all prime numbers
p
, we must have
\Res(g_{m},g_{n})=\pm 1
.
By Proposition
2.3
, we must in fact have
\Res(g_{m},g_{n})=1
.

Assume from now on that
n
is odd.
Since
g_{n}
is a reciprocal polynomial of even degree, it follows from
(
2
) that

(7)

g_{n}^{\#}(2)=g_{n}(1)=n.

Furthermore, for any ring
R
, if
g(x)=(x-1)^{2m}\in R[x]
then, by Lemma
2.1
,

(8)

g^{\#}(x)=(x-2)^{m}.

Remark 3.4
.

By Remark
B.3
, we have
g_{1}^{\#}(x)=1
and
g_{3}^{\#}(x)=x+1
, and

(9)

g_{n}^{\#}(x)=xg_{n-2}^{\#}(x)-g_{n-4}^{\#}(x)

for all odd integers
n\geq 5
.
This implies that the polynomials
g_{n}^{\#}
are related to the
classical
Lucas polynomials

L_{n}(x)
, defined for
n\geq 0
by
L_{0}(x)=2,L_{1}(x)=x
, and
L_{n}(x)=xL_{n-1}(x)+L_{n-2}(x)
, as follows.
For
n\geq 1

H_{n}(x)
by
L_{n}(x)=xH_{n}(x^{2})
. Then
g_{n}^{\#}(x)=H_{n}(x-2)

Proof of the Law of Quadratic Reciprocity.

Let
p,q
be distinct odd primes.

Since
\Res(g_{p},g_{q})=1
by Proposition
3.1
, it follows from Proposition
2.3
that
\Rec(g_{p},g_{q})\in\{\pm 1\}
.
We compute the following congruences modulo
p
:

\displaystyle\Rec(g_{p},g_{q})

\displaystyle\equiv\Rec((x-1)^{p-1},g_{q})\qquad\text{(by \eqref{eq:gpmodp} and Proposition~\ref{prop:rec mod p})}

\displaystyle\equiv\Res((x-2)^{\frac{p-1}{2}},g^{\#}_{q})\qquad\text{(by \eqref{eq:special sharp} and the definition of the reciprocant)}

\displaystyle=g_{q}^{\#}(2)^{\frac{p-1}{2}}\qquad\text{(by (RES1))}

\displaystyle=q^{\frac{p-1}{2}}\qquad\text{(by \eqref{eq:gnsharp2})}

\displaystyle\equiv\left(\frac{q}{p}\right)\qquad\text{(by Euler's criterion).}

Since
\Rec(g_{p},g_{q})
and
\left(\frac{q}{p}\right)
both belong to
\{\pm 1\}
, it follows that

(10)

\Rec(g_{p},g_{q})=\left(\frac{q}{p}\right).

By symmetry, we also have

(11)

\Rec(g_{q},g_{p})=\left(\frac{p}{q}\right).

The Law of Quadratic Reciprocity now follows from (
10
), (
11
), and (RES2).

\blacksquare

4.
The supplementary law

We can use a similar argument to prove the supplementary law characterizing
\left(\frac{2}{p}\right)
when
p
is an odd prime.
Actually, it turns out to be more straightforward to establish a formula for
\left(\frac{-2}{p}\right)
.

By Euler’s criterion, we have

\left(\frac{-2}{p}\right)=\left(\frac{-1}{p}\right)\cdot\left(\frac{2}{p}\right)=(-1)^{\frac{p-1}{2}}\left(\frac{2}{p}\right),

so the supplemental law is equivalent to:

Theorem 4.1
.

If
p
is an odd prime then
\left(\frac{-2}{p}\right)=1
if
p\equiv 1
or
3\pmod{8}
and
\left(\frac{-2}{p}\right)=-1
if
p\equiv 5
or
7\pmod{8}
.

Instead of the polynomial
g_{2}(x)=x+1
, we will use the cyclotomic polynomial
\Phi_{4}(x)=x^{2}+1
. Note that
\Phi_{4}
is a reciprocal polynomial of even degree, with
\Phi_{4}^{\#}(x)=x
.

Proposition 4.2
.

If
n
is an odd positive integer, then
{\rm Rec}(\Phi_{4},g_{n})
is equal to 1 if
n
is 1 or 3 (mod 8) and
-1
if
n
is 5 or 7 (mod 8).

Proof.

We have

\Rec(\Phi_{4},g_{n})=\Res(x,g_{n}^{\#})=g_{n}^{\#}(0),

so it suffices to evaluate
g_{n}^{\#}(0)
.

This is a straightforward but tedious calculation given (
2
), which implies that

g_{n}^{\#}(0)=g_{n}(i)i^{-\frac{n-1}{2}}=\frac{i^{n}-1}{i-1}i^{-\frac{n-1}{2}},

where
i^{2}=-1\in{\mathbb{C}}
.

Alternatively, recall from Remark
3.4
that for
n\geq 5
odd we have

g_{n}^{\#}(x)=xg_{n-2}^{\#}(x)-g_{n-4}^{\#}(x).

From this, a simple inductive argument shows that

g_{n}^{\#}(0)=1
if
n
is 1 or 3 (mod 8) and
-1
if
n
is 5 or 7 (mod 8).

\blacksquare

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

Let
p
be an odd prime. We compute:

\displaystyle\Rec(\Phi_{4},g_{p})

\displaystyle\equiv\Rec(\Phi_{4},(x-1)^{p-1})\qquad\text{(by \eqref{eq:gpmodp} and Proposition~\ref{prop:rec mod p})}

\displaystyle\equiv\Res(x,(x-2)^{\frac{p-1}{2}})\qquad\text{(by \eqref{eq:special sharp} and the definition of the reciprocant)}

\displaystyle=(-2)^{\frac{p-1}{2}}\qquad\text{(by (RES1))}

\displaystyle\equiv\left(\frac{-2}{p}\right)\pmod{p}\qquad\text{(by Euler's criterion).}

Since
\Rec(\Phi_{4},g_{p})
and
\left(\frac{-2}{p}\right)
both belong to
\{\pm 1\}
, we have

\Rec(\Phi_{4},g_{p})=\left(\frac{-2}{p}\right)
,
which implies the desired result via Proposition
4.2
.

\blacksquare

Appendix A
Resultants

Let
R
be a ring and let
f,g\in R[x]
be monic polynomials.
Inspired by an observation of Barnett
[
3
]
, we define the
resultant
of
f
and
g
to be

\Res(f,g):={\rm det}\left(g(C_{f})\right)\in R,

where
C_{f}
is the
companion matrix
of
f(x)=a_{0}+a_{1}x+\cdots+a_{m-1}x^{m-1}+x^{m}
:

C_{f}:=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0&\cdots&0&-a_{0}\\
1&0&0&\cdots&0&-a_{1}\\
0&1&0&\cdots&0&-a_{2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&1&-a_{m-1}\\
\end{pmatrix}

We assume for the rest of this section that
R
is an integral domain with fraction field
K
.
We will use the following two well-known facts from linear algebra:

(LA1)

The characteristic polynomial of
C_{f}
over
K
is
f
(cf.
[
12
, Lemma 8.4]
).

(LA2)

If an
m\times m
matrix
A
over
K
has characteristic polynomial
f
, and if
f
factors over some extension field
L
of
K
as
f(x)=(x-\lambda_{1})\cdots(x-\lambda_{m})
,
then the characteristic polynomial of
g(A)
is
(x-g(\lambda_{1}))\cdots(x-g(\lambda_{m}))
. (
Proof:
By
[
12
, Theorem 14.17]
,
A
is similar to an upper triangular matrix
B
with
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{m}
on the diagonal.
The diagonal entries of
g(B)
are
g(\lambda_{1}),\ldots,g(\lambda_{m})
, and by
[
12
, Theorem 8.12]
the characteristic polynomial of
p(A)
is equal to that of
p(B)
.)

Assume
f
splits into linear factors over
R
as
f(x)=(x-\alpha_{1})\cdots(x-\alpha_{m})
.
Then by (LA1) and (LA2), the characteristic polynomial of
g(C_{f})
over
K
is

(x-g(\alpha_{1}))\cdots(x-g(\alpha_{m})).

It follows that the determinant of
g(C_{f})
is
\prod_{i=1}^{m}g(\alpha_{i})
, which proves (RES1).
Moreover, if
g(x)=(x-\beta_{1})\cdots(x-\beta_{n})
with all
\beta_{j}\in R
then

(12)

\Res(f,g)=\prod_{i,j}(\alpha_{i}-\beta_{j})\in R.

If we view the coefficients of
f
and
g
as indeterminates, the expression
{\rm det}\left(g(C_{f})\right)
is a polynomial of degree
m+n
with integer coefficients in these variables.
In other words, there is a multivariate polynomial
S_{m,n}\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{m-1},y_{0},y_{1},\ldots,y_{n-1}]
such that for every ring
R
and every pair of monic polynomials

f(x)=a_{0}+a_{1}x+\cdots+a_{m-1}x^{m-1}+x^{m}
and
g(x)=b_{0}+b_{1}x+\cdots+b_{n-1}x^{n-1}+x^{n}
in
R[x]
,

{\rm Res}(f,g)=S_{m,n}(a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{m-1},b_{0},b_{1},\ldots,b_{n-1}).

The ‘functoriality’ relation (RES3) follows easily from this observation.

By (RES3) and the fact that
R
is an integral domain, we may replace
R
by a splitting field
L
for
fg
over the fraction field
K
of
R
.
The identity (RES2) then follows immediately from (
12
).

In the same way, we can reduce the proof of (RES4) to the case where
f(x)=(x-\alpha_{1})\cdots(x-\alpha_{m})
with all
\alpha_{i}\in R
.
Using (RES1), we compute:

\displaystyle{\rm Res}(f(x),r(x))

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{m}r(\alpha_{i})

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\left(g(\alpha_{i})-f(\alpha_{i})q(\alpha_{i})\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{m}g(\alpha_{i})

\displaystyle={\rm Res}(f(x),g(x)),

which proves (RES4).

Appendix B
The trace polynomial

Our primary goal in this Appendix is to prove:

Proposition B.1
.

Suppose
R
is a ring and
g\in R[x]
is a reciprocal polynomial of even degree
2m
.
Then there is a unique polynomial
h(x)\in R[x]
of degree
m
such that
g(x)=x^{m}h(x+\frac{1}{x})
.

The following proof was suggested by Darij Grinberg.

Proof.

We first prove the existence of
h(x)
. This will be done by induction on
m
. The base case
m=0
is clear. For the induction step, let
g(x)=a_{0}+a_{1}x+...+a_{2m}x^{2m}
be a reciprocal polynomial of degree
2m
; in particular,
a_{2m}=a_{0}
.
Thus
\tilde{g}(x):=\left(g(x)-a_{0}(1+x^{2})^{m}\right)/x
is a reciprocal polynomial of degree
2(m-1)
. By the inductive hypothesis,
\tilde{g}(x)=x^{m-1}\tilde{h}(x+1/x)
for some polynomial
\tilde{h}(x)
of degree
m-1
.
Setting
h(x)=a_{0}x^{m}+\tilde{h}(x)
yields
g(x)=x^{m}h(x+1/x)
, as desired. This establishes the existence of
h
.

The uniqueness of
h
follows by reversing the existence argument. More formally, we again proceed by induction on
m
.
The base case
m=0
is obvious. For the induction step, note that the equation
g(x)=x^{m}h(x+1/x)
implies that the
x^{m}
-coefficient of
h(x)
must be
a_{0}
.
Let
\tilde{h}(x)=h(x)-a_{0}x^{m}
, which has degree
m-1
, and let
\tilde{g}(x)=\left(g(x)-a_{0}(1+x^{2})^{m}\right)/x
, which is reciprocal of degree
2(m-1)
. Then
\tilde{g}(x)=x^{m-1}\tilde{h}(x+1/x)
, and by the inductive hypothesis

\tilde{h}(x)
is uniquely determined by
\tilde{g}(x)
. It follows that
h
is uniquely determined by
g
.

\blacksquare

g^{\#}
of
g
to be the polynomial
h

Remark B.2
.

The following alternative proof of the existence portion of Proposition
B.1
was
suggested by Franz Lemmermeyer, and provides an explicit recursion which will be useful in the next remark.

Write
g(x)=a_{0}+a_{1}x+\cdots+a_{2m}x^{2m}
with
a_{i}=a_{2m-i}
for all
0\leq i\leq m
and
h(x)=b_{0}+b_{1}x+\cdots+b_{m}x^{m}
.
We wish to prove that we can uniquely solve for the coefficients of
h
in terms of the coefficients of
g
.

In the Laurent polynomial ring
R[x,\frac{1}{x}]
, we have the identity

x^{-m}g(x)=a_{0}(x^{m}+x^{-m})+a_{1}(x^{m-1}+x^{-(m-1)})+\cdots+a_{m-1}(x+x^{-1})+a_{m},

so it suffices to prove the result for the special Laurent polynomials
f_{n}(x):=x^{n}+x^{-n}
for all
n\geq 0
.
In other words, we want to prove that for each
n\geq 0
, there is a polynomial
h_{n}(x)\in R[x]
of degree
n
such that
f_{n}(x)=h_{n}(x+x^{-1})
.

We prove existence of the polynomials
h_{n}(x)
by induction on
n
.
The result is trivial for
n=0,1
, so we may assume that
n\geq 2
and that the result is true for polynomials of degree at most
n-1
.
A simple calculation gives

f_{n}(x)=(x+x^{-1})f_{n-1}(x)-f_{n-2}.

Therefore, if we set
h_{0}(x)=2
,
h_{1}(x)=x
, and

(13)

h_{n}(x)=xh_{n-1}(x)-h_{n-2}(x),

we will have the desired identity
f_{n}(x)=h_{n}(x+x^{-1})
.

Remark B.3
.

For
n\geq 0
, define
g_{2n+1}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{2n}x^{k}
as in (
4
).

Then with
f_{k}(x)
and
h_{k}(x)
as in Remark

we have
x^{-n}g_{2n+1}=1+\sum_{k=1}^{n}f_{k}(x)

g^{\#}_{2n+1}(x)=1+\sum_{k=1}^{n}h_{k}(x)

Since
g_{1}(x)=1
and
g_{3}(x)=1+x+x^{2}
, we have
g_{1}^{\#}(x)=1
and
g_{3}^{\#}(x)=x+1
.
Moreover, since
h_{k}(x)=xh_{k-1}(x)-h_{k-2}(x)
for
k\geq 2
, it follows from (
13
) that for
n\geq 2
,

xg^{\#}_{2n-1}(x)-g^{\#}_{2n-3}(x)=x+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\left(xh_{k}(x)-h_{k-1}(x)\right)-1+h_{0}=1+x+\sum_{k=2}^{n}h_{k}(x)=g^{\#}_{2n+1}.

In other words, for all odd integers

we have

(14)

g_{n}^{\#}(x)=xg_{n-2}^{\#}(x)-g_{n-4}^{\#}(x).

Proof of Lemma
2.1
.

To see that
g(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}(x-\alpha_{i})(x-\alpha_{i}^{-1})
is reciprocal, we compute:

\displaystyle x^{2m}g(\frac{1}{x})

\displaystyle=x^{2m}\prod(\frac{1}{x}-\alpha_{i})(\frac{1}{x}-\frac{1}{\alpha_{i}})

\displaystyle=\prod(1-\alpha_{i}x)(1-\frac{1}{\alpha_{i}}x)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{m}\prod\alpha_{i}(x-\frac{1}{\alpha_{i}})\cdot(-1)^{m}\prod\frac{1}{\alpha_{i}}(x-\alpha_{i})

\displaystyle=\prod(x-\frac{1}{\alpha_{i}})(x-\alpha_{i})

\displaystyle=g(x).

To prove (
3
), the case
m=1
can be handled by a simple computation: setting
\alpha=\alpha_{1}
and
a=\alpha+\alpha^{-1}
, we have
g(x)=(x-\alpha)(x-\alpha^{-1})=x^{2}-ax+1=x(x+\frac{1}{x}-a)
, and thus

g^{\#}(x)=x-a
. The general case follows immediately from the special case
m=1
: if
a_{j}=\alpha_{j}+\alpha_{j}^{-1}
then
g^{\#}(x)=\prod_{j=1}^{m}(x-a_{j})
.

\blacksquare

As mentioned in the text, we define the
reciprocant

\Rec(f,g)
of two reciprocal polynomials of even degree to be
\Res(f^{\#},g^{\#})
.

Proof of Proposition
2.4
.

It suffices, by (RES2), to prove the following statement: if
g_{1},g_{2},h\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x]
are reciprocal of even degree and
n
is a positive integer such that
g_{1}\equiv g_{2}\pmod{n}
, then
\Rec(g_{1},h)\equiv\Rec(g_{2},h)\pmod{n}
.

By Proposition
B.1
, we have
g_{1}^{\#}(x)\equiv g_{2}^{\#}(x)\pmod{n}
. Applying (RES3) to the natural ring homomorphism
\phi:{\mathbb{Z}}\to{\mathbb{Z}}/n{\mathbb{Z}}
shows that
\Res(g^{\#}_{1},h^{\#})\equiv\Res(g^{\#}_{2},h^{\#})\pmod{n}
as desired.

\blacksquare
