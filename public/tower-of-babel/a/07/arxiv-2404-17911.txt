Spectral properties of the gradient operator with nonconstant coefficients
F. Colombo, F. Mantovani, P. Schlosser

Abstract
. In mathematical physics, the gradient operator with nonconstant coefficients encompasses various models, including Fourier’s law for heat propagation and Fick’s first law, that relates the diffusive flux to the gradient of the concentration. Specifically, consider n ≥ 3 n\geq 3 orthogonal unit vectors e 1 , … , e n ∈ ℝ n e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n} , and let Ω ⊆ ℝ n \Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n} be some (in general unbounded) Lipschitz domain. This paper investigates the spectral properties of the gradient operator T = ∑ i = 1 n e i ​ a i ​ ( x ) ​ ∂ ∂ x i T=\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}a_{i}(x)\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}} with nonconstant positive coefficients a i : Ω ¯ → ( 0 , ∞ ) a_{i}:\overline{\Omega}\to(0,\infty) . Under certain regularity and growth conditions on the a i a_{i} , we identify bisectorial or strip-type regions that belong to the S S -resolvent set of T T . Moreover, we obtain suitable estimates of the associated resolvent operator. Our focus lies in the spectral theory on the S S -spectrum, designed to study the operators acting in Clifford modules V V over the Clifford algebra ℝ n \mathbb{R}_{n} , with vector operators being a specific crucial subclass. The spectral properties related to the S S -spectrum of T T are linked to the inversion of the operator Q s ​ ( T ) := T 2 − 2 ​ s 0 ​ T + | s | 2 Q_{s}(T):=T^{2}-2s_{0}T+|s|^{2} , where s ∈ ℝ n + 1 s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1} is a paravector, i.e., it is of the form s = s 0 + s 1 ​ e 1 + ⋯ + s n ​ e n s=s_{0}+s_{1}e_{1}+\dots+s_{n}e_{n} . This spectral problem is substantially different from the complex one, since it allows to associate general boundary conditions to Q s ​ ( T ) Q_{s}(T) , i.e., to the squared operator T 2 T^{2} .

1.
Introduction

Quaternionic and Clifford operators encompass vector operators, which arise in numerous scientific contexts. Contrary to complex holomorphic function theory, there are different possible notions of hyperholomorphicity in the noncommutative setting of
\mathbb{R}_{n}
. Noncommutative spectral theories then rely on various concepts of spectra, based on distinct Cauchy kernels depending on the type of hyperholomorphicity being considered.

The spectral theory, grounded in the notion of
S
-spectrum and associated with slice hyperholomorphicity, initiated its development in 2006, showing that this noncommutative framework is not merely an extension deducible from complex operator theory, especially when dealing with unbounded operators, see
[
30
]
. The identification of the
S
-spectrum emerged through methodologies in hypercomplex analysis, although its roots were initially hinted by quaternionic quantum mechanics, see
[
9
]
. An exhaustive description on the discovery of the
S
-spectrum can be found in the introduction of the book
[
18
]
. The spectral theory on the
S
-spectrum was developed in several papers and it is systematically organized in the books
[
2
,
3
,
17
,
18
,
24
]
, where one can find also the development of the theory of slice hyperholomorphic functions. It is also important to point out that the quaternionic and the Clifford spectral theorems are based on the notion of
S
-spectrum as well, see
[
1
,
20
]
. However, this spectral theory goes beyond the quaternionic and the Clifford setting, as shown in
[
19
]
.

Let us give a short explanation of the differences between complex and
S
-spectral theory. The resolvent operator of a complex linear operator
A
is directly linked to the definition of its spectrum via the invertibility of the operator
\lambda-A
. In particular, if
A
is bounded, the inverse of this operator admits the series representation

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}A^{n}\lambda^{-1-n}=(\lambda-A)^{-1},\qquad|\lambda|>\|A\|.

However, this identity is not true for Clifford operators
T
. In this case there rather holds

(1.1a)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}T^{n}s^{-1-n}=(T^{2}-2s_{0}T+|s|^{2})^{-1}(\overline{s}-T),\qquad|s|>\|T\|,

(1.1b)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}s^{-1-n}T^{n}=(\overline{s}-T)(T^{2}-2s_{0}T+|s|^{2})^{-1},\qquad|s|>\|T\|,

for
s\in\mathbb{R}_{n}
of the form
s=s_{0}+s_{1}e_{1}+\dots+s_{n}e_{n}
. This relation suggests, that in contrast to the complex spectral theory, the definition of the
S
-spectrum is connected to the invertibility of the second order operator

(1.2)

Q_{s}(T):=T^{2}-2s_{0}T+|s|^{2}.

That is, for every
T\in\mathcal{B}(V)
, the
S
-spectrum
and the
S
-resolvent set
of
T
are defined as

\rho_{S}(T):=\Set{s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}}{Q_{s}(T)^{-1}\in\mathcal{B}(V)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sigma_{S}(T):=\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus\rho_{S}(T).

Here
V
is a Clifford module and
\mathcal{B}(V)
the set of all bounded right-linear operators
T:V\to V
. For every
s\in\rho_{S}(T)
, the inverse
Q_{s}(T)^{-1}
is called the
pseudo
S
-resolvent operator
.

A second main difference between complex and
S
-spectral theory, lies in the fact that

\rho_{S}(T)\ni s\mapsto Q_{s}(T)^{-1},

is not a (operator valued) slice hyperholomorphic function. The operator
Q_{s}(T)
is only used for the definition of the
S
-spectrum, while the
left
and the
right
S
-resolvent operators
are motivated by the series representations (
1.1a
) and (
1.1b
)

S_{L}^{-1}(s,T):=Q_{s}(T)^{-1}(\overline{s}-T)\qquad\text{and}\qquad S_{R}^{-1}(s,T):=(\overline{s}-T)Q_{s}(T)^{-1},\qquad s\in\rho_{S}(T).

These resolvent operators now preserve the slice hyperholomorphicity and are used in the definition of the
S
-functional calculus for Clifford operators.

Clifford operators that are sectorial, bi-sectorial or strip-operators, can be defined in analogy to complex operators, but in this case the estimates are associated with the
S
-resolvents

\|S_{L}^{-1}(s,T)\|\leq\frac{C}{|s|}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\|S_{R}^{-1}(s,T)\|\leq\frac{C}{|s|},

where the parameter
s
belongs to suitable sectorial, bi-sectorial or strip-type subsets of
\rho_{S}(T)
.

In this paper we will study the spectral properties of the
first order differential operator

(1.3)

T=\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}a_{i}(x)\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}},\qquad x\in\Omega,

with Dirichlet, Neumann and Robin boundary conditions associated to
Q_{s}(T)
. Here
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
are the imaginary units of the Clifford algebra
\mathbb{R}_{n}
and the coefficients
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
are defined on some minimally smooth domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
in the sense of
[
45
, Section VI.3.3]
, in
n\geq 3
dimensions. The restriction to
n\geq 3
is necessary since the crucial Sobolev inequality in Lemma
2.3
only holds true in those dimensions. For the coefficients of
T
in (
1.3
) we require that
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}:\overline{\Omega}\mapsto(0,\infty)
belong to
C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})
and satisfy the bounds

(1.4a)

\displaystyle m_{a}:=

\displaystyle\min\limits_{i\in\{1,\dots,n\}}\inf\limits_{x\in\Omega}a_{i}(x)>0,

(1.4b)

\displaystyle M_{a}:=

\displaystyle\Big(\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{n}\|a_{i}\|_{\infty}^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}<\infty,

(1.4c)

\displaystyle M_{a}^{\prime}:=

\displaystyle\Big(\sum\nolimits_{i,j=1}^{n}\Big\|a_{j}\frac{\partial a_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}\Big\|^{2}_{L^{n}}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}<\infty,

where
\|\cdot\|_{L^{n}}
is the usual Lebesgue norm of real valued functions and
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
is the supremum norm of continuous functions. The Clifford module in which we will be working is

L^{2}(\Omega)=\Set{f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}_{n}}{\int_{\Omega}|u(x)|^{2}dx<\infty},

that is the space of square integrable functions with values in the Clifford algebra
\mathbb{R}_{n}
.

In this paper we will interpret the invertibility of the operator
Q_{s}(T)
in (
1.2
) in the weak sense. That is, we ask whether the partial differential equation

(1.5)

Q_{s}(T)u(x)=(T^{2}-2s_{0}T+|s|^{2})u(x)=f(x),\qquad x\in\Omega,

equipped with boundary conditions of Dirichlet, Neumann or Robin type, admits a unique weak solution for every
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. In order to derive this weak formulation, we first note that the square of the operator
T
in (
1.3
) is given by

\displaystyle T^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}e_{j}a_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}\Big(e_{i}a_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\Big)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\Big(a_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\Big)+\sum_{i,j=1,j\neq i}^{n}e_{j}e_{i}a_{j}\frac{\partial a_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}+\underbrace{\sum_{i,j=1,j\neq i}^{n}e_{j}e_{i}a_{j}a_{i}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{j}\partial x_{i}}}_{\makebox[0.0pt]{=0}}

(1.6)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\Big(a_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\Big)-\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}\Big(B_{i}-e_{i}a_{i}\frac{\partial a_{i}}{\partial x_{i}}\Big)\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}},

where the third sum in the second line vanishes due to anti-commutation property of the units of the Clifford algebra
e_{j}e_{i}=-e_{i}e_{j}
for every
j\neq i
, and in the last line we used the abbreviation

(1.7)

B_{i}:=\sum_{j=1}^{n}e_{j}a_{j}\frac{\partial a_{i}}{\partial x_{j}},\qquad i\in\{1,\dots,n\}.

Applying this operator to some
u\in H^{2}(\Omega)
, testing with
v\in H^{1}(\Omega)
and using integration by parts with the outer unit normal vector
\vec{\nu}=(\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{n})
of the boundary
\partial\Omega
, gives

\displaystyle\langle T^{2}u,v\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big\langle a_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\Big(a_{i}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}\Big),v\Big\rangle_{L^{2}}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big\langle e_{i}\Big(B_{i}-e_{i}a_{i}\frac{\partial a_{i}}{\partial x_{i}}\Big)\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}},v\Big\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big\langle a_{i}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}},\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}(a_{i}v)\Big\rangle_{L^{2}}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big\langle\nu_{i}a_{i}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}},a_{i}v\Big\rangle_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}

\displaystyle\quad-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big\langle\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}},\Big(B_{i}-e_{i}a_{i}\frac{\partial a_{i}}{\partial x_{i}}\Big)e_{i}v\Big\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big\langle\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}},a_{i}^{2}\frac{\partial v}{\partial x_{i}}-B_{i}e_{i}v\Big\rangle_{L^{2}}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big\langle\nu_{i}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}},a_{i}^{2}v\Big\rangle_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}.

Consequently, the weak formulation of the problem (
1.5
) is connected to the form

(1.8)

q_{s}(u,v):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big\langle\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}},a_{i}^{2}\frac{\partial v}{\partial x_{i}}+(2s_{0}a_{i}-B_{i})e_{i}v\Big\rangle_{L^{2}}+|s|^{2}\langle u,v\rangle_{L^{2}}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big\langle\nu_{i}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}},a_{i}^{2}v\Big\rangle_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}.

The primary objective of this paper is to delve into this spectral problem with Dirichlet, Neumann and Robin-type boundary conditions, establishing bi-sectorial estimates for the solution and hence the
S
-resolvent operator. These findings are an extension of the quaternionic framework explored in prior publications such as
[
8
,
14
,
15
,
21
]
, and in some cases contains results never investigated in previous works.

The structure of the paper is outlined as follows: In Section
2
, we present the main results concerning modules over the Clifford algebra
\mathbb{R}_{n}
. Among those is the Lax-Milgram lemma, which will be crucial for the solvability of the form (
1.8
) in Section
3
and Section
4
. Section
3
focuses on the
S
-spectral problem (
1.5
) with Dirichlet boundary conditions
u=0
on
\partial\Omega
, and similarly, Section
4
investigates Robin-type boundary conditions
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nu_{i}a_{i}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}+bu=0
on
\partial\Omega
. Additional to the unique weak solvability, for both kinds of boundary conditions,
L^{2}
- and
H^{1}
-estimates of the solution are established. Consequently, sectoriality estimates of the resolvent operators can be deduced. For the readability of the paper, an auxiliary maximization problem is put in Section
5
. The final Section
6
contains concluding remarks on the current state of this theory.

2.
Functional setting in Clifford modules over
\mathbb{R}_{n}

In this section we consider for
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
n\geq 3
the
Clifford algebra

\mathbb{R}_{n}
, generated by
n

imaginary units

e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
which satisfy the relations

e_{i}^{2}=-1\qquad\text{and}\qquad e_{i}e_{j}=-e_{j}e_{i},\qquad i\neq j\in\{1,\dots,n\}.

More precisely,
\mathbb{R}_{n}
is given by

(2.1)

\mathbb{R}_{n}:=\Set{\sum\nolimits_{A\in\mathcal{A}}x_{A}e_{A}}{x_{A}\in\mathbb{R},\,A\in\mathcal{A}},

using the index set

\mathcal{A}:=\Set{(i_{1},\dots,i_{r})}{r\in\{0,\dots,n\},\,1\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{r}\leq n},

and the
basis vectors

e_{A}:=e_{i_{1}}\dots e_{i_{r}}
. Note, for
A=\emptyset
the empty product of imaginary units is the real number
e_{\emptyset}:=1
. Moreover, we will consider the set of all
paravectors

\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:=\Set{x_{0}+\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}e_{i}}{x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}}.

For any Clifford number
x\in\mathbb{R}_{n}
, we define

\displaystyle\operatorname{Sc}(x)

\displaystyle:=x_{\emptyset}=x_{0},

(scalar part)

\displaystyle\overline{x}

\displaystyle:=\sum\nolimits_{A\in\mathcal{A}}x_{A}\overline{e_{A}},

(conjugate)

\displaystyle|x|

\displaystyle:=\Big(\sum\nolimits_{A\in\mathcal{A}}|x_{A}|^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}=(\operatorname{Sc}(x\overline{x}))^{\frac{1}{2}}=(\operatorname{Sc}(\overline{x}x))^{\frac{1}{2}},

(norm)

where
\overline{e_{A}}=\overline{e_{i_{r}}}\dots\overline{e_{i_{1}}}
and
\overline{e_{i}}=-e_{i}
. It is now obvious that for any Clifford number
x\in\mathbb{R}_{n}
one can calculate its coefficients
x_{A}
by

(2.2)

\operatorname{Sc}(x\overline{e_{A}})=\operatorname{Sc}\Big(\sum\nolimits_{B\in\mathcal{A}}x_{B}e_{B}\overline{e_{A}}\Big)=\sum\nolimits_{B\in\mathcal{A}}x_{B}\operatorname{Sc}(e_{B}\overline{e_{A}})=x_{A},

where in the last equation we used that

(2.3)

\operatorname{Sc}(e_{B}\overline{e_{A}})=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }B=A,\\
0,&\text{if }B\neq A.\end{cases}

For any real Hilbert space
V_{\mathbb{R}}
with inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}
and norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}=\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}
, we define the
Clifford module

V:=\Set{\sum\nolimits_{A\in\mathcal{A}}v_{A}\otimes e_{A}}{v_{A}\in V_{\mathbb{R}}}.

For any vector
v=\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}v_{A}\otimes e_{A}\in V
and any Clifford number
x=\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}x_{A}e_{A}\in\mathbb{R}_{n}
, we equip this space with a left and a right scalar multiplication

(2.4a)

\displaystyle xv:=

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{A,B\in\mathcal{A}}(x_{B}v_{A})\otimes(e_{B}e_{A}),

(left-multiplication)

(2.4b)

\displaystyle vx:=

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{A,B\in\mathcal{A}}(v_{A}x_{B})\otimes(e_{A}e_{B}).

(right-multiplication)

Moreover, we define the
inner product

(2.5)

\langle v,w\rangle:=\sum\nolimits_{A,B\in\mathcal{A}}\langle v_{A},w_{B}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}\,\overline{e_{A}}e_{B},\qquad v,w\in V,

and the
norm

(2.6)

\|v\|:=\Big(\sum\nolimits_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\|v_{A}\|_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}=\big(\operatorname{Sc}\langle v,v\rangle\big)^{\frac{1}{2}},\qquad v\in V.

The sesquilinear form (
2.5
) is clearly right-linear in the second, and right-antilinear in the first argument, i.e. for every
u,v,w\in V
,
x\in\mathbb{R}_{n}
, there holds

\displaystyle\langle u,v+w\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle u,v\rangle+\langle u,w\rangle,

\displaystyle\langle v,wx\rangle=\langle v,w\rangle x,

\displaystyle\langle v+w,u\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle v,u\rangle+\langle w,u\rangle,

\displaystyle\langle vx,w\rangle=\overline{x}\langle v,w\rangle.

Moreover, there also holds

(2.7)

\langle v,xw\rangle=\langle\overline{x}v,w\rangle.

Next we provide some more basic properties of the inner product (
2.5
) and the norm (
2.6
).

Lemma 2.1
.

For every
v,w\in V
,
x\in\mathbb{R}_{n}
, there holds

i)

\|vx\|\leq 2^{\frac{n}{2}}|x|\|v\|
and
\|xv\|\leq 2^{\frac{n}{2}}|x|\|v\|
,

ii)

\|vx\|=\|xv\|=|x|\|v\|
,  if
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
,

iii)

|\langle v,w\rangle|\leq 2^{\frac{n}{2}}\|v\|\,\|w\|
,

iv)

|\operatorname{Sc}\langle v,w\rangle|\leq\|v\|\,\|w\|
.

Proof.

i)
Analogously to (
2.2
), one can write for every
A\in\mathcal{A}
the component
(vx)_{A}
as

(vx)_{A}=\operatorname{Sc}(vx\overline{e_{A}})=\operatorname{Sc}\bigg(\sum\nolimits_{B,C\in\mathcal{A}}(v_{B}(x\overline{e_{A}})_{C})\otimes(e_{B}e_{C})\bigg)=\sum\nolimits_{B\in\mathcal{A}}(-1)^{\frac{|B|(|B|+1)}{2}}v_{B}(x\overline{e_{A}})_{B},

where in the last equation we used that
e_{B}e_{C}\in\mathbb{R}
if and only if
B=C
. In this form, we can estimate every component of the product as

\displaystyle\|(vx)_{A}\|_{\mathbb{R}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum\nolimits_{B\in\mathcal{A}}\|v_{B}(x\overline{e_{A}})_{B}\|_{\mathbb{R}}=\sum\nolimits_{B\in\mathcal{A}}\|v_{B}\|_{\mathbb{R}}|(x\overline{e_{A}})_{B}|

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\sum\nolimits_{B\in\mathcal{A}}\|v_{B}\|^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big(\sum\nolimits_{B\in\mathcal{A}}|(x\overline{e_{A}})_{B}|^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}=\|v\|\,|x\overline{e_{A}}|=\|v\|\,|x|.

Consequently the norm of the whole product admits the upper bound

\|vx\|^{2}=\sum\nolimits_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\|(vx)_{A}\|_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\leq\sum\nolimits_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\|v\|^{2}|x|^{2}=2^{n}\|v\|^{2}|x|^{2}.

The second inequality
\|xv\|^{2}\leq 2^{n}\|v\|^{2}|x|^{2}
can be proven in the same way.

ii)
If we assume that
x
is a paravector, it clearly satisfies
x\overline{x}=|x|^{2}
, and we have

\|vx\|^{2}=\operatorname{Sc}\langle vx,vx\rangle=\operatorname{Sc}\big(\overline{x}\langle v,v\rangle x\big)=\operatorname{Sc}\big(x\overline{x}\langle v,v\rangle\big)=|x|^{2}\operatorname{Sc}\langle v,v\rangle=|x|^{2}\|v\|^{2}.

For the second identity, we use (
2.7
) and get

\|xv\|^{2}=\operatorname{Sc}\langle xv,xv\rangle=\operatorname{Sc}\langle\overline{x}xv,v\rangle=|x|^{2}\operatorname{Sc}\langle v,v\rangle=|x|^{2}\|v\|^{2}.

iii)
Using the representation (
2.2
) for the Clifford number
\langle v,w\rangle
as well as the already proven results i) and ii), gives

|\langle v,w\rangle|^{2}=\sum\nolimits_{A\in\mathcal{A}}(\langle v,w\rangle_{A})^{2}=\sum\nolimits_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\big(\operatorname{Sc}\langle v,w\,\overline{e_{A}}\rangle\big)^{2}\leq\sum\nolimits_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\|v\|^{2}\|w\,\overline{e_{A}}\|^{2}=2^{n}\|v\|^{2}\|w\|^{2}.

iv)
It follows from (
2.3
) and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality of
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}
, that

\displaystyle|\operatorname{Sc}\langle u,v\rangle|

\displaystyle=\Big|\operatorname{Sc}\sum\nolimits_{A,B\in\mathcal{A}}\langle v_{A},w_{B}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}\overline{e_{A}}e_{B}\Big|=\Big|\sum\nolimits_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\langle v_{A},w_{A}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}\Big|\leq\sum\nolimits_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\|v_{A}\|_{\mathbb{R}}\|w_{A}\|_{\mathbb{R}}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\sum\nolimits_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\|v_{A}\|_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big(\sum\nolimits_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\|w_{A}\|_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}=\|v\|\,\|w\|.\qed

Next, we come to the main result of this first section, the Lax-Milgram lemma for Clifford modules. We will need this result in the later sections to proof unique solvability of the spectral problem for the
S
-spectrum, in particular to detect regions of
S
-resolvent set and related estimates of the
S
-resolvent operator. Although the Lax-Milgram lemma is a well known result in complex functional analysis, for its Clifford algebra version, we cannot provide a precise reference, hence we give a short proof.

Lemma 2.2

(Lax-Milgram lemma)
.

Let
q:V\times V\to\mathbb{R}_{n}
be right-linear in the second and right-antilinear in the first argument. Moreover, we assume that there exists constants
C\geq 0
and
\kappa>0
, such that

\operatorname{Sc}q(v,v)\geq\kappa\|v\|^{2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad|q(v,w)|\leq C\|v\|\|w\|,\qquad v,w\in V.

Then for every right-linear bounded functional
\varphi:V\to\mathbb{R}_{n}
, there exists a unique vector
u_{\varphi}\in V
, satisfying

\|u_{\varphi}\|\leq\frac{1}{\kappa}\|\operatorname{Sc}\varphi\|\qquad\text{and}\qquad q(u_{\varphi},v)=\varphi(v),\qquad\text{for all }v\in V.

Proof.

Note that it is possible to consider
V
together with the inner product
\operatorname{Sc}\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
as a real Hilbert space, by simply restricting the scalar multiplication to real numbers. In this sense, the scalar part
\operatorname{Sc}q(\cdot,\cdot):V\times V\to\mathbb{R}
turns out to be a real bilinear mapping, which satisfies for every
v,w\in V
the estimates

\operatorname{Sc}q(v,v)\geq\kappa\|v\|^{2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad|\operatorname{Sc}q(v,w)|\leq|q(v,w)|\leq C\|v\|\,\|w\|.

Since also
\operatorname{Sc}\varphi:V\to\mathbb{R}
is a linear and bounded functional, the classical Lax-Milgram lemma ensures the existence of a unique
u_{\varphi}\in V
, satisfying

\|u_{\varphi}\|\leq\frac{1}{\kappa}\|\operatorname{Sc}\varphi\|\qquad\text{and}\qquad\operatorname{Sc}q(u_{\varphi},v)=\operatorname{Sc}\varphi(v),\qquad\text{for all }v\in V.

Using now the components
\varphi(v)_{A}=\operatorname{Sc}\varphi(v\overline{e_{A}})
and
q(u_{\varphi},v)_{A}=\operatorname{Sc}q(u_{\varphi},v\overline{e_{A}})
, see (
2.2
), we can extend this representation result to

q(u_{\varphi},v)=\sum\nolimits_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\operatorname{Sc}q(u_{\varphi},v\overline{e_{A}})e_{A}=\sum\nolimits_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\operatorname{Sc}\varphi(v\overline{e_{A}})e_{A}=\varphi(v),\qquad\text{for all }v\in V.\qed

We will end this section with two basic lemmas, which will be crucial throughout the paper, namely the Sobolev and the Poincaré inequality.
In order to state these inequalities we will need for every function
u\in H^{1}(\Omega)
the
Sobolev-seminorm

\|u\|_{D}:=\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big\|\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}\Big\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}.

Lemma 2.3

(Sobolev inequality)
.

Let
n\geq 3
and
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a minimally smooth domain in the sense of
[
45
, Section VI.3.3]
. Then there exists a constant
C_{S}\geq 0
, such that

(2.8a)

\displaystyle\|u\|_{L^{\frac{2n}{n-2}}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{S}\|u\|_{D},\hskip 27.03003ptu\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega),

(2.8b)

\displaystyle\|u\|_{L^{\frac{2n}{n-2}}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{S}\|u\|_{H^{1}},\qquad u\in H^{1}(\Omega).

In (
2.8a
) the constant is explicitly given by
C_{S}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi n(n-2)}}\big(\frac{\Gamma(n)}{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2})}\big)^{\frac{1}{n}}
.

Proof.

The basic Gagliardo-Nirenberg-Sobolev inequality on
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{n}
is

(2.9)

\|u\|_{L^{\frac{2n}{n-2}}}\leq C_{S}\|u\|_{D},\qquad u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

where the optimal constant
C_{S}
is for example calculated in
[
46
]
. If we now consider
u\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
, the zero-extension of
u
is an element in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and the inequality (
2.8a
) follows immediately from (
2.9
) with the same constant. If on the other hand
u\in H^{1}(\Omega)
, we have to use the extension operator
E:H^{1}(\Omega)\to H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
from
[
45
, Section VI.3.1,Theorem 5]
, to get

\|u\|_{L^{\frac{2n}{n-2}}(\Omega)}\leq\|Eu\|_{L^{\frac{2n}{n-2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq C_{S}\|Eu\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq C_{S}\|E\|\|u\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}.\qed

Lemma 2.4

(Poincaré inequality)
.

[
10
, Corollary 9.19 & Remark 21]

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be open and either bounded in one direction or of finite measure. Then there exists a constant
C_{P}>0
, such that

(2.10)

\|u\|_{L^{2}}\leq C_{P}\|u\|_{D},\qquad u\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega).

2-\frac{M_{a}}{C_{P}K_{a}^{2}|s_{0}|}-\frac{C_{P}K_{a}^{2}|s_{0}|}{M_{a}},&\text{if }|s_{0}|\geq\frac{M_{a}}{C_{P}K_{a}^{2}}.\end{cases}

This maximization problem now transforms (
3.24
) into

\operatorname{Sc}q_{s}^{D}(u_{f},u_{f})\geq\|u_{f}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\begin{cases}|s|^{2}-\frac{2M_{a}|s_{0}|}{C_{P}}+\frac{M_{a}^{2}}{C_{P}^{2}K_{a}^{2}},&\text{if }|s_{0}|\leq\frac{M_{a}}{C_{P}K_{a}^{2}},\\
|s|^{2}-K_{a}^{2}s_{0}^{2},&\text{if }|s_{0}|\geq\frac{M_{a}}{C_{P}K_{a}^{2}},\end{cases}=\kappa_{0}(s)\|u_{f}\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

where in the second equality we used the constant
\kappa_{0}(s)
from (
3.17
). Combining this inequality with (
3.12
), then gives the final
L^{2}
-estimate of the solution

(3.25)

\|u_{f}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\frac{\operatorname{Sc}q_{s}^{D}(u_{f},u_{f})}{\kappa_{0}(s)}\leq\frac{\|f\|_{L^{2}}\|u_{f}\|_{L^{2}}}{\kappa_{0}(s)},

which is exactly the
\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}}
-norm estimate in (
3.16
). For the
\|\cdot\|_{D}
-estimate of the solution
u_{f}
, we once more use the coercivity (
3.20
), use the nonnegativity of the second term, and get

\operatorname{Sc}q_{s}^{D}(u_{f},u_{f})\geq\Big(\frac{M_{a}^{2}}{K_{a}^{2}}-\frac{\alpha C_{P}M_{a}|s_{0}|}{\delta}-2(1-\alpha)C_{P}M_{a}|s_{0}|\Big)\|u_{f}\|_{D}^{2}.

\alpha
and

The maximization problem (

) with the values (

\sup\limits_{\alpha\in(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1})}\alpha\Big(2-\frac{\alpha M_{a}|s_{0}|}{C_{P}|s|^{2}}\Big)=\begin{cases}\frac{C_{P}|s|^{2}}{M_{a}|s_{0}|},&\text{if }|s|^{2}\leq\frac{M_{a}|s_{0}|}{C_{P}},\\
2-\frac{M_{a}|s_{0}|}{C_{P}|s|^{2}},&\text{if }|s|^{2}\geq\frac{M_{a}|s_{0}|}{C_{P}}.\end{cases}

This maximization problem allows us to also maximize (
3.10
) with respect to
\alpha

\operatorname{Sc}q_{s}^{D}(u_{f},u_{f})\geq\|u_{f}\|_{D}^{2}\begin{cases}\frac{M_{a}^{2}}{K_{a}^{2}}-2C_{P}M_{a}|s_{0}|+C_{P}^{2}|s|^{2},&\text{if }|s|^{2}\leq\frac{M_{a}|s_{0}|}{C_{P}},\\

where in the second equality we used the constant

5.9
) looks like

0<xy-1\Leftrightarrow y>\begin{cases}\frac{1}{x},&\text{if }x\leq 1,\\
2-x,&\text{if }x\geq 1.\end{cases}

It is true, because for
x\leq 1
both inequalities are the same, and for
x\geq 1
both inequalities are always satisfied.

In the
third step
we prove (
5.2
). The zeros of
p
, if we forget about the constraint
\alpha\in(0,1)
for the moment, are given by

(5.10)

\alpha_{\pm}=y\pm\sqrt{y(x+y-2)}.

Since we assumed that (
5.1
) is satisfied, there exists at least one point
\alpha\in(0,1)
, for which
p(\alpha)>0
. Since moreover
\lim_{\alpha\to\pm\infty}p(\alpha)=-\infty
, the zeros (
5.10
) have to be real valued, i.e.
x+y>2
is satisfied and the interval where
p
is positive is exactly between those zeros,

\Set{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}}{p(\alpha)>0}=(\alpha_{-},\alpha_{+}).

Restricting now again to the interval
\alpha\in(0,1)
, we get

\Set{\alpha\in(0,1)}{p(\alpha)>0}=\big(\max\{0,\alpha_{-}\},\min\{1,\alpha_{+}\}\big).

It is left to check when we have
\alpha_{-}\geq 0
and
\alpha_{+}\leq 1
respectively. The condition
\alpha_{-}\geq 0
is equivalent to

(5.11)

\alpha_{-}\geq 0\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad y\geq\sqrt{y(x+y-2)}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\sqrt{y}\geq\sqrt{x+y-2}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad x\leq 2,

and the condition
\alpha_{+}\leq 1
is equivalent to

\displaystyle\alpha_{+}\leq 1\quad

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad y+\sqrt{y(x+y-2)}\leq 1

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad 1-y\geq 0\quad\text{and}\quad y(x+y-2)\leq(1-y)^{2}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad y\leq 1\quad\text{and}\quad xy\leq 1

(5.12)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad y\leq\frac{1}{x},

where in the last equivalence we used that the combination
y>1
and
y\leq\frac{1}{x}
is not allowed, since in this case we have
1<y\leq\frac{1}{x}
, consequently
x<1
, which is a contradiction to the assumed (
5.9
), see also the shaded region in (
5.5
). The two equivalences (
5.11
) and (
5.12
) now show that
\max\{0,\alpha_{-}\}=\alpha_{0}
and
\min\{1,\alpha_{+}\}=\alpha_{1}
coincide with the values in (
5.3
). Hence (
5.2
) is proven.

In the
fourth step
, we will solve the first maximization problem (
5.5a
), i.e. we will maximize

f(\alpha):=\alpha\Big(2-\frac{\alpha}{y}\Big),\qquad\alpha\in(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1}).

The zero of the derivative

(5.13)

f^{\prime}(\alpha)=2\Big(1-\frac{\alpha}{y}\Big),

is given by
\alpha=y
. We now have to check if this value
y
is contained in the interval
(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1})
of allowed values. First,

(5.14)

y>\begin{cases}y-\sqrt{y(x+y-2)},&\text{if }x\leq 2,\\
0,&\text{if }x\geq 2,\end{cases}=\alpha_{0},\qquad\text{is always satisfied.}

The second condition
y<\alpha_{1}
can be characterized by

(5.15)

\begin{split}y<\alpha_{1}\quad&\Leftrightarrow\quad y<\begin{cases}y+\sqrt{y(x+y-2)},&\text{if }y\leq\frac{1}{x}\\
1,&\text{if }y\geq\frac{1}{x}\end{cases}\\
&\Leftrightarrow\quad\Big(y\leq\frac{1}{x}\Big)\quad\text{or}\quad\Big(y\geq\frac{1}{x}\quad\text{and}\quad y<1\Big)\\
&\Leftrightarrow\quad y<1,\end{split}

where in the last equivalence we used that the combination
y>1
and
y\leq\frac{1}{x}
is not allowed, similar to the argument below equation (
5.12
). Hence, the maximal value of
f
is attained at
\alpha=y
if
y<1
and at the right endpoint
\alpha\to\alpha_{1}=1
if
y\geq 1
. This means, we get

\sup\limits_{\alpha\in(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1})}f(\alpha)=\begin{cases}f(y),&\text{if }y<1,\\
f(1),&\text{if }y\geq 1,\end{cases}=\begin{cases}y,&\text{if }y\leq 1,\\
2-\frac{1}{y},&\text{if }y\geq 1.\end{cases}

In the
fifth step
we first note that for every
\alpha\in(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1})
, there holds

x-2+2\alpha>x-2+2\alpha_{0}=\begin{cases}x-2+2y-2\sqrt{y(x+y-2)},&\text{if }x\leq 2,\\
x-2,&\text{if }x\geq 2,\end{cases}\geq 0,

where the right hand side is nonnegative because
a+b-2\sqrt{ab}\geq 0
, for every
a,b\geq 0
. Moreover, there holds

\delta_{0}(\alpha):=\frac{\alpha}{x-2+2\alpha}<\frac{y}{\alpha}=:\delta_{1}(\alpha),\qquad\alpha\in(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1}),

which is a simple rearrangement of the inequality
(x-2)y+2\alpha y-\alpha^{2}>0
in (
5.2
). Now, we will solve the second maximization problem (
5.5b
), i.e. we want to maximize the function

g(\alpha,\delta):=\alpha\Big(2-\frac{1}{\delta}-\delta\Big),\qquad\alpha\in(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1}),\,\delta\in(\delta_{0}(\alpha),\delta_{1}(\alpha)).

First, we will fix
\alpha\in(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1})
and maximize
g(\alpha,\cdot\,)
with respect to
\delta
. The only positive zero of the partial derivative

\frac{\partial g}{\partial\delta}(\alpha,\delta)=\alpha\Big(\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}-1\Big),

is given by
\delta=1
. The maximum value of
g(\alpha,\cdot\,)
now depends on the location of this zero with respect to the interval
(\delta_{0}(\alpha),\delta_{1}(\alpha))
of allowed
\delta
-values. If
1\leq\delta_{0}(\alpha)
, the maximum is attained the left endpoint
\delta\to\delta_{0}(\alpha)^{+}
, if
1\in(\delta_{0}(\alpha),\delta_{1}(\alpha))
is an allowed value, the maximum is attained at exactly this point
\delta=1
, and if
1\geq\delta_{1}(\alpha)
, the maximum value is attained at the right endpoint
\delta\to\delta_{1}(\alpha)
. I.e., we get

\displaystyle h(\alpha):=\sup\limits_{\delta\in(\delta_{0}(\alpha),\delta_{1}(\alpha))}g(\alpha,\delta)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}g(\alpha,\delta_{0}(\alpha)^{+})&\text{if }1\leq\delta_{0}(\alpha),\\
g(\alpha,\frac{1}{C}),&\text{if }\delta_{0}(\alpha)<1<\delta_{1}(\alpha),\\
g(\alpha,\delta_{1}(\alpha)),&\text{if }1\geq\delta_{1}(\alpha),\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}2-x-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{x-2+2\alpha},&\text{if }\alpha\leq 2-x,\\
0,&\text{if }2-x\leq\alpha\leq y,\\
2\alpha-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{y}-y,&\text{if }\alpha\geq y.\end{cases}

Note, that we were allowed to replace the
<
-inequalities by
\leq
-inequalities in the second case since
h(\alpha)
is continuous at
\alpha=2-x
and
\alpha=y
. Next, we want to maximize this function
h
with respect to the parameter
\alpha
. Therefore, we calculate its derivative

(5.16)

h^{\prime}(\alpha)=\begin{cases}\frac{2\alpha(2-x-\alpha)}{(x-2+2\alpha)^{2}},&\text{if }\alpha\leq 2-x,\\
0,&\text{if }2-x\leq\alpha\leq y,\\
2(1-\frac{\alpha}{y}),&\text{if }\alpha\geq y.\end{cases}

In the first two cases
\alpha\leq 2-x
and
2-x\leq\alpha\leq y
in (
5.16
), we obviously have a nonnegative derivative
h^{\prime}(\alpha)\geq 0
. Only in the third case
\alpha\geq y
, there exists the possible zero
\alpha_{\max}=y
of the derivative. It is already calculated in (
5.14
) and (
5.15
), that

\alpha_{\max}\in(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1})\Leftrightarrow y<1\qquad\text{and}\qquad\alpha_{\max}\geq\alpha_{1}\Leftrightarrow y\geq 1.

Hence, we conclude that for
y\geq 1
the function
h(\alpha)
is monotone increasing on the whole interval
(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1})
, which means that its supremum is attained at the right endpoint
\alpha\to\alpha_{1}=1
. In the case
y<1
on the other hand, the maximal value is attained at the interior point
\alpha=\alpha_{\max}=y
. The solution of the maximization problem is then given by

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{\alpha\in(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1})}h(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}h(1),&\text{if }y\geq 1,\\
h(y),&\text{if }y<1,\end{cases}=\begin{cases}2-x-\frac{1}{x},&\text{if }y\geq 1\;\;\&\;\;x\leq 1,\\
0,&\text{if }y\geq 1\;\;\&\;\;x\geq 1,\\
2-\frac{1}{y}-y,&\text{if }y\geq 1\;\;\&\;\;1\geq y,\\
0,&\text{if }y<1,\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}2-x-\frac{1}{x},&\text{if }x\leq 1,\\
0,&\text{if }x\geq 1.\end{cases}\qed
