Bregman Deviations of Generic Exponential Families
Sayak Ray Chowdhury, Patrick Saux, Odalric-Ambrym Maillard, Aditya Gopalan

Abstract
We revisit the method of mixtures, or Laplace method, to study the concentration phenomenon in generic (possibly multidimensional) exponential families. Using duality properties of the Bregman divergence to construct nonnegative martingales, we establish a generic inequality controlling the deviation between the parameter of the family and a finite sample estimate. This bound is expressed in the local geometry induced by the Bregman pseudo-metric. Moreover, it is time-uniform and involves a quantity extending the classical information gain to exponential families, which we call the Bregman information gain . For the practitioner, we instantiate this novel bound to several classical families, e.g., Gaussian (including with unknown variance or multivariate), Bernoulli, Exponential, Weibull, Pareto, Poisson and Chi-square, yielding explicit forms of the confidence sets and the Bregman information gain. We further compare the resulting confidence bounds to state-of-the-art time-uniform alternatives and show this novel method yields competitive results. Finally, we apply our result to the design of generalized likelihood ratio tests for change detection, capturing new settings such as variance change in Gaussian families.

Introduction

Concentration inequalities are a powerful set of methods in statistical theory with key applications in machine learning. Often in machine learning applications, a learner estimates some quantity solely based on samples from an unknown distribution and would like to know the magnitude of the estimation error. The typical example is that of the mean
\mu
of some real-valued random variable
X
, estimated by its empirical mean built from a sample of
n
independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) observations. We refer the interested reader to the monographs of
Boucheron et al. 2013
;
Raginsky and Sason 2018
;
Zeitouni and Dembo 1998
for standard results and related topics.

In many situations, one may want to further estimate some vector parameter, as in, e.g., linear bandits
(
Abbasi-Yadkori et al. 2011
)
and logistic bandits
(
Faury et al. 2020
)
. A closely related problem is to estimate the parameter of a distribution coming from a parametric family

(
Chowdhury et al. 2021
)
.

Exponential families
are a flexible way to formalize such distributions over a set
\mathcal{X}
by describing densities of the form
p_{\theta}(x)\propto h(x)\exp(\langle\theta,F(x)\rangle)
, for some given feature function
F:\mathcal{X}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{d}
and base function
h
(Section
2
). Most classical distributions fall into this category, e.g., Gaussian (possibly multivariate, with or without known variance), Exponential, Gamma, Chi-square, Weibull, Pareto, Poisson, Bernoulli and Multinomial distributions
(
Amari 2016
)
.

A number of problems in current-day machine learning involve sequential, active data-sampling strategies
(
Cesa-Bianchi and Lugosi 2006
)
. This includes multi-armed bandits, reinforcement learning, active learning and federated learning, to mention a few application domains. Since the decision to sample a novel observation
results from the interaction between the learning algorithm and the environment,
and depends on past observations,
one needs to design concentration inequalities working with a
random
number of observations typically at a random stopping time
(
Durrett 2019
)
. A natural way to handle this difficulty is to derive time-uniform concentration inequalities, producing sequences of confidence sets valid
uniformly
over all number of observations with high probability, as opposed to being valid for a single number of observation.

A popular method in bandit theory is to combine supermartingale techniques with union bound arguments over a geometric time grid, a technique known as time peeling (or stitching) – see
Bubeck 2010
;
Cappé et al. 2013
for early uses in bandits, as well as
Garivier 2013
, or more recently
Maillard 2019b
; see also
Howard et al. 2020
;
Howard et al. 2021
for a recent, complementary survey of the history of this field, and
Kuchibhotla and Zheng 2021
for an extension of Bentkus’ concentration bounds
(
Bentkus 2004
)
for bounded distributions using time peeling.
The method of mixtures, initiated by
Robbins and Pitman 1949
;
Robbins 1970
and popularized in
Peña et al. 2008
is a powerful alternative to peeling for developing time-uniform confidence sets.
It has been applied to sub-Gaussian families in
Abbasi-Yadkori et al. 2011
, leading to a variety of applications
(
Chowdhury and Gopalan 2017
;
Durand et al. 2018
;
Kirschner and Krause 2018
)
. In
Kaufmann and Koolen 2021
, a generalization to handle one-dimensional exponential families is considered, with applications to Gaussian (known variance) and Gamma distributions (known shape). A fairly different ‘capital process’ construction technique has been recently developed for bounded distributions in
Shafer and Vovk 2019
, and popularized further in
Waudby-Smith and Ramdas 2023
.

In this work, we revisit the method of mixtures for parametric exponential families of arbitrary dimension, expressing deviations in the natural (Bregman) divergence of the family.
The setting of exponential families is convenient for integration in a Bayesian setup, thanks to the notion of conjugate prior that enables us to reduce computation of tedious integrals to simple parameter updates. Here, we exploit this property to obtain explicit mixtures of martingales. Exponential families are largely used in modern machine learning, yet concentration tools available to the practitioner are comparatively scarce beyond the Gaussian case. To help close this gap, we obtain both sharp and computationally tractable confidence sets, especially in the small sample regime.

Outline and contributions.

In Section

, we first recall some background material on exponential families and their associated Bregman divergences.

). To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to derive an explicit

technique, and a proof sketch is outlined in Section

2
Exponential Families and Bregman Divergence

In this section, we introduce exponential families and the link between their Kullback-Leibler (KL) and Bregman divergences, as well as useful properties of these divergences.

Exponential families.

We consider an exponential family of distributions
\{p_{\theta}\}_{\theta\in\Theta}
over some set
\mathcal{X}
,
parameterized in some open set
\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, whose density or mass function has the form
p_{\theta}(x)\!=\!h(x)\exp(\langle\theta,F(x)\rangle\!-\!\mathcal{L}(\theta))
. Here,
F\!:\!\mathcal{X}\!\to\!\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the feature function,
h\!:\!\mathcal{X}\!\to\!\mathbb{R}_{+}
is the base function,
and
\mathcal{L}
represents the normalization term (a.k.a. log-partition function, convex w.r.t.
\theta
) given by
\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\log\int_{\mathcal{X}}h(x)\exp(\langle\theta,F(x)\rangle)dx
.
We denote by
\Theta_{\mathcal{D}}=\big\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\mathcal{L}(\theta)<\infty\big\}
the domain of
\mathcal{L}
and by
\Theta_{I}=\big\{\theta\in\Theta_{\mathcal{D}}:\det\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}(\theta)>0\big\}
the set on which its Hessian is invertible. We assume that
\Theta\subset\Theta_{I}
, which is tantamount to assuming that the family is minimal, and ensures we only consider non-degenerate distributions (
\nabla\mathcal{L}
is one-to-one on its domain). We use notations
\mathbb{P}_{\theta},\mathbb{E}_{\theta}
to explicitly refer to the probability and expectation associated to the distribution
p_{\theta}
.

Bregman divergence of an exponential family.

A fundamental property of exponential families is the following form for the KL divergence between two distributions with parameters
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta_{\mathcal{D}}
:

\displaystyle\texttt{KL}(p_{\theta},p_{\theta^{\prime}})=\langle\theta-\theta^{\prime},\mathbb{E}_{\theta}(F(X))\rangle-\mathcal{L}(\theta)+\mathcal{L}(\theta^{\prime})\,.

Here,
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}(F(X))
is called the vector of
expectation parameters
(a.k.a.
dual parameters
), and is equal to

\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)
. Hence, it holds that
\texttt{KL}(p_{\theta},p_{\theta^{\prime}})=\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\prime},\theta)
, where
\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}
is known as the Bregman divergence
(
Bregman 1967
)

with potential function
\mathcal{L}
, defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\prime},\theta)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\mathcal{L}(\theta^{\prime})-\mathcal{L}(\theta)-\langle\theta^{\prime}-\theta,\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)\rangle\,.

Tail and duality properties.

The canonical Bregman divergence of an exponential family enjoys two fundamental properties.
The first one links it to the log-moment generating function of the random variable
F(X)
, which makes Bregman divergences well suited to control the tail behavior of random variables appearing in concentration inequalities. The second one highlights duality properties that enables convenient algebraic manipulations. To this end, for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we define the function
\mathcal{B}_{{\mathcal{L}},\theta}(\lambda)\!=\!\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta\!+\!\lambda,\theta)\!=\!{\mathcal{L}}(\theta\!+\!\lambda)\!-\!{\mathcal{L}}(\theta)\!-\!\langle\lambda,\nabla{\mathcal{L}}(\theta)\rangle
. Also, we introduce the Legendre-Fenchel dual operator
\star
associating a function
G
to its dual
G^{\star}(x)=\sup_{\lambda}\langle\lambda,x\rangle-G(\lambda)
.

Lemma 1

(Properties of Bregman divergences)
.

For all
\theta\!\in\!\Theta_{\mathcal{D}}
and
\lambda\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\theta\!+\!\lambda\in\Theta_{\mathcal{D}}
,

\displaystyle\log\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\left[\exp\left(\langle\lambda,F(X)-\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[F(X)]\rangle\right)\right]=\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L},\theta}(\lambda)\,.

Furthermore, if
\nabla\mathcal{L}
is one-to-one, the following Bregman duality relations hold for any
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta_{\mathcal{D}}:

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\prime},\theta)=\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L},\theta^{\prime}}^{\star}(\nabla{\mathcal{L}}(\theta)-\nabla{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\prime}))=\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}^{\star}}(\nabla{\mathcal{L}}(\theta),\nabla{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\prime})).

More generally, the following holds for any
\alpha\in[0,1]:

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{{\mathcal{L}},\theta^{\prime}}^{\star}\left(\alpha(\nabla{\mathcal{L}}(\theta)-\nabla{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\prime}))\right)=\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}\big(\theta^{\prime},\theta_{\alpha}\big),\,\,\text{where}\,\,\theta_{\alpha}=\nabla{\mathcal{L}}^{-1}\big(\alpha\nabla{\mathcal{L}}(\theta)+(1-\alpha)\nabla{L}(\theta^{\prime})\big).

The second half of this technical lemma is essentially a change of variable formula to move back and forth between two representations of an exponential family: in natural parameters (measured by the Bregman divergence between
\theta^{\prime}
and
\theta
) and in expectation parametrization (measured by the dual Bregman divergence between
\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[F(X)]
and
\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta^{\prime})=\mathbb{E}_{\theta^{\prime}}[F(X)]
). For more background on this, which forms the basis of the
information geometry
field, we refer to
Amari 2016
. This result is at the root of the martingale construction behind Theorem
3
in the next section. For completeness, the proof of this classical lemma is given in Appendix
B
.

3
Time-uniform Bregman Concentration

In this section, we are interested in controlling the deviation
between a parameter
\theta\in\Theta
and its estimate
\theta_{n}
built from
n
observations from distribution
p_{\theta}
. We naturally measure this deviation in terms of the canonical Bregman divergence
of the family. Further, we would like to control this deviation not only for a single sample number
n
,
but
simultaneously for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Namely, we would like to upper bound quantities of the form
\mathbb{P}\left[\exists n\!\in\!\mathbb{N}:\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta,\theta_{n})\geqslant\dots\right]
.
Such a control is very useful in contexts when observations are gathered sequentially (either actively or otherwise), and especially when the number of observations
n
is unknown beforehand. Classical examples include multi-armed bandits
(
Auer et al. 2002
)
, or model-based reinforcement learning
(
Jaksch et al. 2010
)
.

3.1
A generic deviation inequality

In this section, we consider the problem of controlling the Bregman-deviations of a parameter estimate of
\theta
.
To this end, we adapt the method of mixtures (a.k.a. Laplace method) from
(
Peña et al. 2008
)
.
The method is originally designed in the context of Gaussian distributions, where it yields simple closed-form expressions, even though it can be applied more generally.
We state below a generic extension of the method to parametric exponential families and introduce a quantity that measures a form of
information gain
about
\theta
after observing
n
samples, but expressed in terms of the natural Bregman divergence.
For this reason, we call this quantity the
Bregman information gain
.

Definition 2

(Bregman information gain)
.

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\!\sim\!p_{\theta}
be i.i.d. samples generated from
p_{\theta}
, where
\theta\in\Theta\subset\Theta_{I}
, and let
\theta_{0}\!\in\!\Theta
be a reference parameter.
For any constant
c\!>\!0
, the Bregman information gain about
\theta
after observing
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
from
\theta_{0}
is defined as

\displaystyle\gamma_{n,c}(\theta_{0})\!=\!\log\!\left(\!\frac{\int_{\Theta}\exp\big(\!-\!c\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\prime},\theta_{0})\big)d\theta^{\prime}}{\int_{\Theta}\!\exp\!\Big(\!-\!(n\!+\!c)\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\prime},\theta_{n,c}(\theta_{0}))\Big)d\theta^{\prime}}\!\right)\!,

where
\theta_{n,c}(\theta_{0})\!=\!(\nabla\mathcal{L})^{-1}\!\bigg(\!\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{n}F(X_{t})+c\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta_{0})}{n+c}\!\bigg)
denotes a parameter estimate of
\theta
.
1
1

1

\theta_{n,c}(\theta_{0}\!)
is actually a maximum a posteriori estimate under a conjugate prior on
\theta
, depending on the reference point
\theta_{0}
.

Dependence on
\theta_{0}
and example.

The acute reader can note that the considered parameter estimate
\theta_{n,c}(\theta_{0})
and Bregman information gain
\gamma_{n,c}(\theta_{0})
involve a reference parameter
\theta_{0}
.
It makes sense to have such a local reference point since the Bregman divergence is typically linked to metrics with
local
(non-constant) curvature. Hence, the (information) geometry seen from the perspective of different points
\theta_{0}
may be different, unlike in the Gaussian case.
Specifically, for a Gaussian
\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
with known variance
\sigma^{2}
, the Bregman information gain w.r.t. a reference point
\mu_{0}
reads

\displaystyle\gamma_{n,c}^{\mathcal{N}}(\mu_{0})\!=\!\frac{1}{2}\!\log\frac{2\pi\sigma^{2}}{c}\!-\!\frac{1}{2}\!\log\frac{2\pi\sigma^{2}}{n\!+\!c}\!=\!\frac{1}{2}\!\log\frac{n\!+\!c}{c}\,.

It is independent of the reference parameter
\mu_{0}
, which is a consequence of the fact that the Bregman divergence in this case is proportional to the squared Euclidean distance; in other words, Gaussian distributions with known variance exhibit invariant geometry. However, for other exponential families, the geometries are inherently different, and hence Bregman information gain
depend explicitly on local reference points (see Section
3.2
for details).
For reference, the classical Gaussian information gain, i.e., the mutual information between a prior
\mu\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{0},\sigma^{2})
and the average of an i.i.d. sample
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
drawn from
\mathcal{N}(\mu,c\sigma^{2})
is
\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{n\!+\!c}{c}
, which matches the Bregman information gain.

We now present the main result of this paper – a time uniform confidence bound for
\theta
connecting the Bregman divergence geometry of the exponential family with its Bregman information gain.

Theorem 3

(Main result: Laplace method for generic exponential families)
.

Fix any
\delta\in(0,1]
and
n\!\in\!\mathbb{N}
.
Under the hypothesis of Definition
2
, consider the confidence set

\displaystyle\Theta_{n,c}(\delta)=\left\{\theta_{0}\in\Theta:(n+c)\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}\left(\theta_{0},\theta_{n,c}(\theta_{0})\right)\leqslant\log\frac{1}{\delta}+\gamma_{n,c}(\theta_{0})\right\}.

The following time-uniform control holds whenever the Bregman information gain is well-defined:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\left[\exists n\in\mathbb{N}:\theta\notin\Theta_{n,c}(\delta)\right]\leqslant\delta\,.

Note the implicit definition of the confidence set
\Theta_{n,c}(\delta)
. where the parameter of interest
\theta_{0}
appears in both arguments of the Bregman divergence, as well as in the Bregman information gain
\gamma_{n,c}(\theta_{0})
. Because of this,
computing
this confidence set from the equation in Theorem
3
may seen non-trivial at first glance.
However, we show in Section
3.2
how these sets simplify for many classical families, revealing how the computation can be made efficiently.
Moreover, we observe that these confidence sets are actually tighter than those of prior work, and as such are especially well suited to be used when
n
is small (for large
n
, most methods produce essentially equivalent sets).
Note that our concentration bound holds uniformly over all
n
. Equivalently
(
Howard et al. 2020
, Lemma 3)
, it also holds that
\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\left[\theta\notin\Theta_{\tau,c}(\delta)\right]\leqslant\delta
for any random stopping time
\tau
.

Comparison with prior work.

Similar to this work,
Kaufmann and Koolen 2021
extend the method of mixtures technique to derive time-uniform concentration bounds for exponential families. However, their proof technique is fairly different, relying instead on discrete mixtures and stitching, which only works for single parameter families, and involves case-specific calculations that are difficult to generalize beyond Gaussian (with known variance) and Gamma (with known shape). In contrast, our method applies to generic exponential families, including distributions with more than one parameter such as Gaussian when both mean and variance are unknown. Moreover, their discrete prior construction leads to technical constants seemingly unrelated to the exponential family model. Our prior is naturally induced by the exponential family, leveraging key properties of Bregman divergences, yields more intrinsic quantities (Bregman information gain) and perhaps a more elegant and shorter proof. Hence, our results are not only more general, but also of fundamental interest.

Asymptotic behavior.

The asymptotic width of
\Theta_{n,c}(\delta)
depends on the behavior of
\gamma_{n,c}(\theta_{0})
as
n\rightarrow+\infty
. Standard arguments show that
\theta_{n,c}(\theta_{0})\rightarrow\theta
and the Taylor expansion of
\theta^{\prime}\mapsto\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\prime},\theta)
around
\theta^{\prime}=\theta
is
\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\prime},\theta)=\frac{1}{2}(\theta^{\prime}-\theta)^{\top}\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}(\theta)(\theta^{\prime}-\theta)+o\left(\lVert\theta^{\prime}-\theta\rVert^{2}\right)
(note that
\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}(\theta)
is positive definite for
\theta\in\Theta_{I}
). Laplace’s method for integrals (chapter 20 in
Lattimore and Szepesvári 2019
,
Shun and McCullagh 1995
) then gives the following estimate:

\displaystyle\int_{\Theta}\!\exp\!\Big(\!-\!(n\!+\!c)\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\prime},\theta_{n,c}(\theta_{0}))\Big)d\theta^{\prime}\approx\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\!\exp\!\Big(\!-\!\frac{n\!+\!c}{2}(\theta-\theta^{\prime})^{\top}\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}(\theta)(\theta-\theta^{\prime})\Big)d\theta^{\prime}\,,

which is a simple Gaussian integral, and thus
\gamma_{n,c}(\theta_{0})=\frac{d}{2}\log(1\!+\!n/c)+\mathcal{O}(1)
. This asymptotic scaling is worse than the
\log\log n
rate of the law of iterated logarithm.
However, this is a standard feature of the method of mixtures compared to stitching
(
Maillard 2019b
;
Howard et al. 2020
)
, which is compensated by its improved nonasymptotic sharpness, as evidenced in Section
4
.

Dependence on the parameter
c
.

The parameter
c
is usually chosen to be
1
in the Gaussian case. However, it is useful to study its influence over the bounds. We provide in Section
4
a detailed study of this parameter, revealing first that the confidence bounds are not significantly altered over a large range of values, and then explaining how to pick an optimized value of
c
, e.g., for a specific horizon
n=n_{0}
. Note that choosing a variable
c
changing with
n
is not allowed by the theory, as this would break the martingale property used in our proof (see Section
3.3
). Moreover, it would be incompatible with the time-uniform lower bound
\lvert\Theta_{n,c}\left(\delta\right)\rvert=\Omega(\sqrt{\log\log n/n})
provided by the law of iterated logarithm. Indeed, the Bregman information gain with
c_{n}\!\propto\!n
would be asymptotically
\gamma_{n,c_{n}}(\theta_{0})=\frac{d}{2}\log(1\!+\!\mathcal{O}(1))+\mathcal{O}(1)=\mathcal{O}(1)
, leading to
\lvert\Theta_{n,c}\left(\delta\right)\rvert=\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{n})
when
n\rightarrow+\infty
.

Application to bandits.

One can apply the technique developed in proving Theorem
3
to build confidence sets in standard
K
-armed bandit problems. In such problems, we are given
K
distributions
\{p_{\theta_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{K}
from a generic exponential family (e.g., Gaussian) with parameters
\{\theta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{K}
, from which we can draw samples, interpreted as rewards we want to maximize. This setting is standard to analyze regret-optimal bandit algorithms
(
Cappé et al. 2013
;
Korda et al. 2013
;
Baudry et al. 2020
)
.
Specifically, at each time
n
, we choose an arm
i_{n}\in[K]
based on past observations, and draw a sample
X_{n}
from its distribution
p_{\theta_{i_{n}}}
. The samples are then used to update knowledge about the parameters
\{\theta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{K}
by building confidence sets for each of them. Let
N_{i}(n)
denote the number of times that we have chosen action
i
up to time
n
. Also, let
\theta_{i,N_{i}(n),c}(\theta_{0})
and
\gamma_{i,N_{i}(n),c}(\theta_{0})
denote the parameter estimate and Bregman information gain for arm
i
, respectively (similar to Definition
2
with
n
replaced by
N_{i}(n)

). We construct the confidence set for arm
i
at time
n
as

\displaystyle\Theta_{i,n,c}(\delta)=\bigg\{\theta_{0}\in\Theta:(N_{i}(n)+c)\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}\left(\theta_{0},\!\theta_{i,N_{i}(n),c}(\theta_{0})\right)\leqslant\log\frac{1}{\delta}+\gamma_{i,N_{i}(n),c}(\theta_{0})\bigg\}.

Then, similar to Theorem
3
, it holds that the true parameter
\theta_{i}
lies in the set
\Theta_{i,n,c}(\delta)
for all time-steps
n\in\mathbb{N}
with probability at least
1-\delta
. Finally, we take a union bound over
i\in[K]
to obtain confidence sets for all arms (with widths inflated by an additive
\log K
factor). Such construction is standard and is used in
Abbasi-Yadkori et al. 2011
for (sub)-Gaussian families. Possible applications include UCB algorithms for regret minimization
and designing GLR stopping rules for tracking algorithms in pure exploration
(
Garivier and Kaufmann 2016
)
in the context of generic exponential families (see also Section
5
for another application of GLR tests using the parameter estimate
\theta_{n,c}(\theta_{0})
).

Remark 4
.

We provide in Appendix
A.2
a complementary result (Corollary
8
) using a Legendre function
\mathcal{L}_{0}
instead of the regularizing parameter
c
, which eschews the use of a local reference
\theta_{0}
. However, we argue that such a global regularization is actually less convenient to use except for univariate and multivariate Gaussian distributions that anyway exhibit invariant geometry. Furthermore, in Theorem
7
, we prove a more general result that handles the case of a sequence
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
of random variables that are not independent, and having possibly different distributions from each other, which we apply to build confidence sets in linear bandits (see Appendix
F
).

3.2
Specification to classical families

In this section, we specify the result of Theorem
3
to some classical exponential families. Interestingly, the literature on time-uniform concentration bounds outside of random variables that are bounded, gamma with fixed shape or Gaussian with known variance is significantly scarce, even though many more distributions are commonly used in machine learning models. We derive below explicit confidence sets for a range of distributions, which we believe will be of interest for the wider machine learning and statistics community. For instance, consider active learning in bandit problems
(
Carpentier et al. 2011
)
, where one targets upper confidence bounds on the variance (rather than the mean); in the Gaussian case, this can be achieved with Chi-square concentration.
Hao et al. 2019
studies the classical UCB algorithm for bandits under a weaker assumption that sub-Gaussianity, involving the Weibull concentration.
In differential privacy, concentration of Laplace distribution (symmetrized exponential) is often used to study the utility of differentially private mechanisms
(
Dwork et al. 2014
)
. Finally, heavy-tailed distributions such as Pareto have recently been of interest to study risk-averse or corruption in bandit problems
(
Holland and Haress 2021
;
Basu et al. 2022
)
.

We now make explicit the Bregman information gains and confidence sets for some illustrative families (more examples and full derivations are provided in Appendix
C
).

Gaussian (unknown mean and variance).

Let
X\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
. Given samples
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
, we define
S_{n}=\sum_{t=1}^{n}X_{t}
and
\widehat{\mu}_{n}=S_{n}/n
. Further, for
\mu,\sigma\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}
, we define the normalized sum of squares
Z_{n}(\mu,\sigma)=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{n}(X_{t}-\mu)^{2}
. Then, for reference parameters
\mu_{0}\in\mathbb{R},\sigma_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{+},c>0
, the Bregman information gain reads

\displaystyle\gamma_{n,c}^{\mathcal{N}}(\mu_{0},\sigma_{0})=\!\frac{3}{2}\log\left(\frac{n}{n\!+\!c}Z_{n}(\widehat{\mu}_{n},\sigma_{0})+\frac{c}{n\!+\!c}Z_{n}(\mu_{0},\sigma_{0})+c\right)\!+\!f_{n,c}\,,

where
f_{n,c}\!=\!\left(\frac{n+c+1}{2}\right)\log\!\left(n\!+\!c\right)\!-\!\left(\frac{c}{2}\!+\!2\right)\log c\!-\!\frac{n}{2}(1\!+\!\log 2)\!+\!\log\Gamma\!\left(\frac{c+3}{2}\right)\!-\!\log\Gamma\!\left(\frac{n+c+3}{2}\right)
.

Bernoulli.

Let
X\sim\text{Bernoulli}(\mu)
, with unknown mean
\mu\in[0,1]
. Define, for reference parameter
\mu_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
,
c>0
, the estimate
\mu_{n,c}(\mu_{0})\!=\!\frac{S_{n}+c\mu_{0}}{n+c}
. Then, the Bregman information gain is given by

\displaystyle\gamma_{n,c}^{\text{Bernoulli}}(\mu_{0})=c\,\mathbb{H}(\mu_{0})\!-\!(n\!+\!c)\mathbb{H}(\mu_{n,c}(\mu_{0}))+\log\frac{\mathbf{B}(c\mu_{0},c(1-\mu_{0}))}{\mathbf{B}((n+c)\mu_{n,c}(\mu_{0}),(n+c)(1-\mu_{n,c}(\mu_{0}))},

where
\mathbf{B}(\alpha,\beta)\!=\!\int_{0}^{1}u^{\alpha-1}(1\!-\!u)^{\beta-1}du
is the Beta function and
\mathbb{H}(\cdot)
the Bernoulli entropy function.

Exponential.

Let
X\sim\text{Exp}(1/\mu)
, with unknown mean
\mu>0
. For reference parameters
\mu_{0},c>0
, the Bregman information gain
reads

\displaystyle\gamma_{n,c}^{\text{Exp}}(\mu_{0})=\log\left(S_{n}/\mu_{0}+c\right)+\log\frac{\Gamma(c)}{\Gamma(n+c)}+(n+c-1)\log(n+c)-c\log c-n~.

Pareto.

Let
X\sim\text{Pareto}\left(\alpha\right)
, with unknown shape
\alpha>0
. Define
L_{n}\!=\!\sum_{t=1}^{n}\log X_{t}
. Then, for reference parameters
\alpha_{0},c>0
, the Bregman information gain
is given by

\displaystyle\gamma_{n,c}^{\text{Pareto}}(\alpha_{0})\!=\!\log\left(\alpha_{0}L_{n}\!+\!c\right)\!+\!\log\frac{\Gamma(c)}{\Gamma(n+c)}\!+\!(n\!+\!c\!-\!1)\log(n+c)\!-\!c\log c\!-\!n.

Chi-square.

Let
X\!\sim\!\chi^{2}(k)
, where
k\!\in\!\mathbb{N}
is unknown. Define
K_{n}\!=\!\sum_{t=1}^{n}\log\frac{X_{t}}{2}
. For reference points
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N},c>0
, introduce an estimate
k_{n,c}(k_{0})
satisfying
\psi_{0}\!\left(\!\frac{k_{n,c}(k_{0})}{2}\!\right)\!=\!\frac{K_{n}+c\;\psi_{0}\left(\frac{k_{0}}{2}\right)}{n+c}
. In this case, the Bregman information gain is given by

\displaystyle\gamma_{n,c}^{\chi^{2}}(k_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{k_{n,c}(k_{0})}{2}\Big(K_{n}\!+\!c\psi_{0}\Big(\frac{k_{0}}{2}\Big)\Big)\!-\!(n\!+\!c)\log\Gamma\Big(\frac{k_{n,c}(k_{0})}{2}\Big)\!+\!c\log\Gamma\Big(\frac{k_{0}}{2}\Big)\!-\!c\;\frac{k_{0}}{2}\psi_{0}\Big(\frac{k_{0}}{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\!+\!\log\frac{J(c,c\psi_{0}(\frac{k_{0}}{2}))}{J(n\!+\!c,K_{n}\!+\!c\psi_{0}(\frac{k_{0}}{2}))}\,,\,\text{where}\,\,J(a,b)\!:=\!\sum\limits_{k^{\prime}=1}^{\infty}\exp\left(\!-\!a\log\Gamma\left(\!\frac{k^{\prime}}{2}\!\right)\!+\!b\frac{k^{\prime}}{2}\!\right)\!.

The function
J(a,b)
can be estimated using numerical methods (see Section
4
). This paves a way to build high-probability confidence sets for Chi-square distribution which has not been adequately captured in prior work. The sums over
k^{\prime}
above derive from the martingale construction of Theorem
3
with discrete mixture (i.e., w.r.t the counting measure). A continuous (i.e., w.r.t the Lebesgue measure) mixture similar to the other families is also possible, as detailed in the appendix in Remark
9
.

3.3
Proof Sketch: Theorem
3

We now sketch the proof of Theorem
3
, and refer the interested reader to the appendix for details.

Step 1: Martingale construction.

For any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we introduce the quantity

\displaystyle M_{n}^{\lambda}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left(\langle\lambda,n(\mu_{n}\!-\!\mu)\rangle\!-\!n\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L},\theta}(\lambda)\right)\,,

where
\mu_{n}\!=\!\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\!F(X_{t})
and
\mu\!=\!\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[F(X)]
. By Lemma
1
,
M_{n}^{\lambda}
is a martingale such that
\mathbb{E}[M_{n}^{\lambda}]\!=\!1
. We now introduce the distribution
q(\theta|\alpha,\beta)=\exp(\langle\theta,\alpha\rangle-\beta\mathcal{L}(\theta))H(\alpha,\beta)
where
H(\cdot,\cdot)
is the normalization function, and define the mixture martingale
M_{n}=\int M_{n}^{\lambda}q(\theta+\lambda|\alpha,\beta)d\lambda
. Note that
M_{n}
also satisfies
\mathbb{E}[M_{n}]=1
.

Step 2. Choice of parameters and duality properties.

Choosing
\alpha=c\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)
and
\beta=c
yields

\displaystyle M_{n}\!=\!G(\theta,c)\!\int\!\!\exp\!\left(\!\langle\lambda,n(\mu_{n}\!-\!\mu)\rangle\!-\!(n\!+\!c)\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L},\theta}(\lambda)\!\right)d\lambda\,,

where
G(\theta,c)=1/\int\exp\big(-c\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\prime},\theta)\big)d\theta^{\prime}
. Now, we consider the function
\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L},\theta}^{\star}(x)=\max_{\lambda}\langle\lambda,x\rangle-\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L},\theta}(\lambda)
. Note that its maximal point
\lambda^{\star}_{x}
satisfies
x+\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta+\lambda^{\star}_{x})
for every
x
. In particular, for the choice
x=\frac{n}{n+c}(\mu_{n}-\mu)
, we have
\frac{n}{n+c}\mu_{n}+\frac{c}{n+c}\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta+\lambda^{\star}_{x})
,
which justifies introducing the following regularized estimate

\displaystyle\theta_{n,c}(\theta)=(\nabla\mathcal{L})^{-1}\left(\frac{n}{n+c}\mu_{n}+\frac{c}{n+c}\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)\right),

provided that
\nabla\mathcal{L}
is one-to-one in
\Theta
.

Step 3. Martingale rewriting and conclusion.

Note that the duality property also yields
\langle\lambda,x\rangle-\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L},\theta}(\lambda)-\langle\lambda^{\star}_{x},x\rangle+\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L},\theta}(\lambda^{\star}_{x})=\langle\lambda-\lambda^{\star}_{x},\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta+\lambda^{\star}_{x})\rangle+\mathcal{L}(\theta+\lambda^{\star}_{x})-\mathcal{L}(\theta+\lambda)
for each
x
. Using this property, we obtain

\displaystyle M_{n}=\exp\big((n\!+\!c)\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L},\theta}^{\star}(x)\big)\frac{G(\theta,c)}{G(\theta_{n,c}(\theta),n+c)}\,.

After applying Markov’s inequality, we obtain the following inequality:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\bigg[\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L},\theta}^{\star}\bigg(\frac{n}{n\!+\!c}(\mu_{n}-\mu)\bigg)\geqslant\frac{1}{n\!+\!c}\log\frac{G(\theta_{n,c}(\theta),n\!+\!c)}{\delta\,G(\theta,c)}\bigg]\leqslant\delta\,.

Now, we use duality property of Bregman divergence to obtain
\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L},\theta}^{\star}\!\left(\!\frac{n}{n+c}(\mu_{n}\!-\!\mu)\right)\!=\!\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}\big(\theta,\theta_{n,c}(\theta)\big)
, which yields the desired form. Finally, by properties of the martingale
M_{n}
, the deviation bound also holds for any random stopping time
N
, and hence we obtain the time uniform bound by employing a stopping time construction similar to that of
Peña et al. 2008
;
Abbasi-Yadkori et al. 2011
.

5
Application: Generalized Likelihood Ratio Test in Exponential Families

In this section, we apply our result to control the false alarm rate of Generalized Likelihood Ratio (GLR) tests when detecting a change of measure from a sequence of observations.
The GLR test in the exponential family model
\mathcal{E}
is defined, for a threshold
\alpha>0
, as

\displaystyle\tau(\alpha;\mathcal{E})=\min\{t\in\mathbb{N}:\max_{s\in[0,t)}G^{\mathcal{E}}_{1:s:t}\geqslant\alpha\},

where the GLR is defined to be
G^{\mathcal{E}}_{1:s:t}\!=\!\inf_{\theta^{\prime}}\sup_{\theta_{1},\theta_{2}}\log\bigg(\!\frac{\prod_{t^{\prime}=1}^{s}p_{\theta_{1}}(X_{t^{\prime}})\!\prod_{t^{\prime}=s+1}^{t}p_{\theta_{2}}(X_{t^{\prime}})}{\prod_{t^{\prime}=1}^{t}p_{\theta^{\prime}}(X_{t^{\prime}})}\!\bigg)
.

Hereafter, we consider that all the observations
(X_{t^{\prime}})_{t^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}}
come from a distribution with same parameter
\theta\in\Theta
. Then, the false alarm rate of the GLR test can be bounded using

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\theta}(\tau(\alpha;\mathcal{E})<\infty)\!=\!\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\big(\exists(t,s)\in\mathbb{N}^{2},s<t:G^{\mathcal{E}}_{1:s:t}\geqslant\alpha\big).

Observe that solving for the inner supremums in the expression of GLR, we obtain

\displaystyle G^{\mathcal{E}}_{1:s:t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf_{\theta^{\prime}}s\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\prime},\theta_{1:s})+(t-s)\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\prime},\theta_{s+1:t}),

where we introduce parameter estimates
\theta_{1:s}
and
\theta_{s+1:t}
such that
\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta_{1:s})=\frac{1}{s}\sum_{t^{\prime}=1}^{s}F(X_{t^{\prime}})
and
\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta_{s+1:t})=\frac{1}{t-s}\sum_{t^{\prime}=s+1}^{t}F(X_{t^{\prime}})
, respectively.
Hence, the false alarm rate can be controlled as

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\theta}(\tau(\alpha;\mathcal{E})\!<\!\infty)\!\leqslant\!\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\Big(\!\exists s\!\in\!\mathbb{N}\!:\!s\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta,\theta_{1:s})\!\geqslant\!\alpha_{1}\!\Big)\!+\!\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\Big(\!\exists(t,s)\!\in\!\mathbb{N}^{2},\!s\!<\!t\!:\!(t\!-\!s)\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta,\theta_{s+1:t})\!\geqslant\!\alpha_{2}\!\Big)

for appropriate terms
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}
such that
\alpha=\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}
.
The control of the first term
comes naturally from our time-uniform deviation result (Theorem
3
) using a regularized version of the estimate
\theta_{1:s}
.
For the second term, we need to study the concentration of
\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta,\theta_{s+1:t})
uniformly over
s
and
t
.

Doubly time-uniform concentration.

For any
s\!<\!t
,
c>0
and reference parameter
\theta_{0}\in\Theta
, we define the regularized estimate

\theta_{s+1:t,c}(\theta_{0})=(\nabla\mathcal{L})^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{t-s+c}\left(\sum_{t^{\prime}=s+1}^{t}F(X_{t^{\prime}})+c\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta_{0})\right)\right)
built from
(t\!-\!s)
observations
X_{s+1},\ldots,X_{t}
. Similarly, we introduce the corresponding Bregman information gain
\gamma_{s+1:t,c}(\theta_{0})
as in Definition
2
. The following result extends the Laplace method for exponential families (Theorem
3
) to control the Bregman deviation of
\theta_{s+1:t,c}(\theta_{0})
around
\theta_{0}
.

Theorem 5

(Doubly time-uniform concentration)
.

Let
\delta\!\in\!(0,1]
and

g\colon\mathbb{N}\!\rightarrow\!\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
such that
\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}1/g(t)\!\leqslant\!1

(e.g.,
g(t)\!=\!\kappa(1\!+\!t)\log^{2}(1\!+\!t)
where
\kappa=2.10975
).
Then, under the assumptions of Definition
2
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\!\left[\exists t\in\mathbb{N},\exists s\!<\!t:\!(t\!-\!s\!+\!c)\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta,\theta_{s+1:t,c}(\theta))\!\geqslant\!\log\frac{g(t)}{\delta}+\gamma_{s+1:t,c}(\theta)\right]\leqslant\delta\,.

Remark 6
.

Maillard 2019a
proves a doubly uniform concentration inequality for means of (sub)-Gaussian random variables and conjectures that it could be extended to other types of changes, such as changes of variance in a Gaussian family. Theorem
5
can be seen as a generalization of this result to generic exponential families. This naturally comes with additional challenges due to the intrinsic local geometry of exponential families. The proof of this result is deferred to Appendix
E
.

A regularized GLR test.

For a given false alarm (i.e., false positive) probability
\delta\in(0,1]
and parameter
c>0
, we define the regularized GLR test
\tau^{\delta}_{c}(\mathcal{E})
as follows:

\displaystyle\Theta_{s,c}(\delta)=\left\{\theta\in\Theta:(s+c)\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta,\theta_{1:s,c}(\theta))\leqslant\log(1/\delta)+\gamma_{1:s,c}(\theta)\right\}\,,

\displaystyle\Theta_{s+1:t,c}(\delta)=\left\{\theta\in\Theta:(t-s+c)\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta,\theta_{s+1:t,c}(\theta))\leqslant\log(g(t)/\delta)+\gamma_{s+1:t,c}(\theta)\right\}\,,

\displaystyle\tau^{\delta}_{c}(\mathcal{E})=\min\left\{t\in\mathbb{N}:\exists s<t,\Theta_{s,c}(\delta/2)\cap\Theta_{s+1:t,c}(\delta/2)=\emptyset\right\}\,.

Note that, under the measure
\mathbb{P}_{\theta}
,
\theta\in\Theta_{s,c}(\delta/2)
holds with probability at least
1-\delta/2
by time-uniform concentration over
s
(Theorem
3
), and
\theta\in\Theta_{s+1:t,c}(\delta/2)
holds with probability at least
1\!-\!\delta/2
by doubly time-uniform concentration over both
s
and
t
(Theorem
5
). Then, by a union-bound, this test is guaranteed to have a false alarm probability controlled by
\delta
.
The factor
g(t)
is essentially a function growing slightly faster than linearly; other common choices include
g(t)\propto t^{\eta}
for some
\eta>1
, though it leads to a looser bound due to the faster growth.
Furthermore, if one is interested in detecting changes up to a known horizon
T
, one can replace the function
g(t)
by the slightly tighter factor

g(t)=\kappa(1\!+\!t)\log^{2}(1\!+\!t)
with
\kappa\!=\!\sum_{t=1}^{T}1/g(t)

and still guarantee a false alarm rate under
\delta
. In Appendix
E.3
, we report simulations of this GLR test to detect changes of variance in the family of centered Gaussian (Figure
14
), which answers an open question from
Maillard 2019a
.

6
Conclusion

We apply the method of mixture to derive a time-uniform deviation inequality for generic parametric exponential families expressed in terms of their Bregman divergences, highlighting the role of a quantity, the
Bregman information gain
, that is related to the geometry of the family.
We specialize this general result to build confidence sets for classical examples.
Our method compares favorably to the state-of-the art for Gaussian, Bernoulli and exponential distributions, and extends to other families like Chi-square, Pareto and two-parameter Gaussian, where no known time-uniform bound exists. Our method is also general enough to design GLR tests for change detection.
An interesting direction for future work would be to consider the case where the log-partition function (and thus the Bregman divergence) is misspecified or unknown and has to be learned alongside the parameter.

\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[F(X)]=\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)
and
\log\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\left[\exp\langle\lambda,F(X)\rangle\right]=\mathcal{L}(\theta+\lambda)-\mathcal{L}(\theta)
.
Hence, we deduce that

\displaystyle\log\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\left[\exp\langle\lambda,F(X)-\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[F(X)]\rangle\right]=\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L},\theta}(\lambda)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\mathcal{L}(\theta+\lambda)-\mathcal{L}(\theta)-\langle\lambda,\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)\rangle\,.

Now, let
\mu_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}F(X_{t})
and
\mu=\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[F(X)]
. For any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the following quantity

\displaystyle M_{n}^{\lambda}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\bigg(\langle\lambda,n(\mu_{n}-\mu)\rangle-n\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L},\theta}(\lambda)\bigg)\,,

is thus a martingale such that
\mathbb{E}[M_{n}^{\lambda}]=1
.

We now introduce the distribution
q(\theta|\alpha,\beta)=H(\alpha,\beta)\cdot\exp(\langle\theta,\alpha\rangle-\beta\mathcal{L}(\theta))
where
H
is the normalization term. We further introduce the following quantity

\displaystyle M_{n}=\int_{\Lambda_{\theta}}M_{n}^{\lambda}q(\theta+\lambda|\alpha,\beta)d\lambda\,,

where
\Lambda_{\theta}=\{\lambda:\theta+\lambda\in\Theta\}
.
This also satisfies
\mathbb{E}[M_{n}]=1
. Further, we have the rewriting

\displaystyle M_{n}=H(\alpha,\beta)\int_{\Lambda_{\theta}}\exp\bigg(\langle\lambda,n(\mu_{n}-\mu)\rangle-n\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L},\theta}(\lambda)+\langle\lambda+\theta,\alpha\rangle-\beta\mathcal{L}(\theta+\lambda)\bigg)d\lambda\,.

Step 2. Choice of parameters and duality properties.

Considering in particular the choice
\alpha=c\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)
and
\beta=c
, we get

\displaystyle M_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H(c\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta),c)\int_{\Lambda_{\theta}}\exp\bigg(\langle\lambda,n(\mu_{n}-\mu)\rangle-n\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L},\theta}(\lambda)+c\langle\lambda+\theta,\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)\rangle-c\mathcal{L}(\theta+\lambda)\bigg)d\lambda

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle G(\theta,c)\int_{\Lambda_{\theta}}\exp\bigg(\langle\lambda,n(\mu_{n}-\mu)\rangle-(n+c)\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L},\theta}(\lambda)\bigg)d\lambda~,

where we introduce the following quantity

\displaystyle G(\theta,c)=H(c\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta),c)\exp\bigg(c\langle\theta,\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)\rangle-c\mathcal{L}(\theta)\bigg)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\exp\bigg(c\langle\theta,\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)\rangle-c\mathcal{L}(\theta)\bigg)}{\int_{\Theta}\exp\bigg(c\langle\theta^{\prime},\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)\rangle-c\mathcal{L}(\theta^{\prime})\bigg)d\theta^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigg[\int_{\Theta}\exp\big(-c\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\prime},\theta)\big)d\theta^{\prime}\bigg]^{-1}~.

At this point, let us introduce
x=\frac{n}{n+c}(\mu_{n}-\mu)
. We also consider the Legendre-Fenchel dual function
\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L},\theta}^{\star}(x)=\max_{\lambda}\langle\lambda,x\rangle-\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L},\theta}(\lambda)

and denote
\lambda^{\star}_{x}
its maximal point.
Now, we note that
x+\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta+\lambda^{\star}_{x})
and thus

\displaystyle\langle\lambda,x\rangle-\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L},\theta}(\lambda)-\langle\lambda^{\star}_{x},x\rangle+\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L},\theta}(\lambda^{\star}_{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\lambda-\lambda^{\star}_{x},x+\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)\rangle+\mathcal{L}(\theta+\lambda^{\star}_{x})-\mathcal{L}(\theta+\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\lambda-\lambda^{\star}_{x},\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta+\lambda^{\star}_{x})\rangle+\mathcal{L}(\theta+\lambda^{\star}_{x})-\mathcal{L}(\theta+\lambda)\,.

Also, since it holds that
x=\frac{n}{n+c}(\mu_{n}-\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta))
, then the quantity

x+\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta+\lambda^{\star}_{x})

rewrites

\frac{n}{n+c}\mu_{n}+\frac{c}{n+c}\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta+\lambda^{\star}_{x})
,
which justifies to introduce the following regularized parameter estimate

\displaystyle\theta_{n,c}(\theta)=(\nabla\mathcal{L})^{-1}\bigg(\frac{n}{n+c}\mu_{n}+\frac{c}{n+c}\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)\bigg)\,.

Step 3. Martingale rewriting and conclusion.

Using this property, we note that

\displaystyle M_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\big((n\!+\!c)\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L},\theta}^{\star}(x)\big)\cdot G(\theta,c)\!\!\int_{\Lambda_{\theta}}\!\!\exp\!\bigg(\!(n\!+\!c)\Big[\langle\lambda\!-\!\lambda^{\star}_{x},\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta\!+\!\lambda^{\star}_{x})\rangle\!-\!\mathcal{L}(\theta\!+\!\lambda)\!+\!\mathcal{L}(\theta\!+\!\lambda^{\star}_{x})\Big]\!\bigg)d\lambda

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\big((n\!+\!c)\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L},\theta}^{\star}(x)\big)\cdot G(\theta,c)\!\int_{\Lambda_{\theta}}\!\exp\!\bigg(\langle\lambda\!+\!\theta,(n\!+\!c)\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta\!+\!\lambda^{\star}_{x})\rangle\!-\!(n\!+\!c)\mathcal{L}(\theta\!+\!\lambda)\bigg)d\lambda\times

\displaystyle\exp\bigg(\!-(n\!+\!c)\langle\lambda^{\star}_{x}\!+\!\theta,\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta\!+\!\lambda^{\star}_{x})+(n\!+\!c)\mathcal{L}(\theta\!+\!\lambda^{\star}_{x})\rangle\bigg)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\big((n\!+\!c)\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L},\theta}^{\star}(x)\big)\frac{G(\theta,c)}{G(\theta+\lambda^{\star}_{x},n+c)}=\exp\big((n\!+\!c)\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L},\theta}^{\star}(x)\big)\frac{G(\theta,c)}{G(\theta_{n,c}(\theta),n+c)}\,.

We then apply simple Markov inequality, which yields for all constant
c
,

\displaystyle\forall\delta\in(0,1],\quad\mathbb{P}\bigg(\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L},\theta}^{\star}\bigg(\frac{n}{n\!+\!c}(\mu_{n}\!-\!\mu)\bigg)\geqslant\frac{1}{n+c}\log\bigg(\frac{G(\theta_{n,c}(\theta),n+c)}{G(\theta,c)}\frac{1}{\delta}\bigg)\bigg)\leqslant\delta.

To conclude, we use the duality property of the Bregman divergence (Lemma
1
), considering that
\nabla\mathcal{L}
is invertible. Indeed, denoting
\alpha=n/(n+c)
,
and
\theta_{n}=(\nabla\mathcal{L})^{-1}(\mu_{n})
, it comes

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L},\theta}^{\star}\bigg(\alpha(\mu_{n}-\mu)\bigg)=\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}\big(\theta,\theta_{\alpha}\big)\,\qquad\text{where}\,\,\theta_{\alpha}=\nabla\mathcal{L}^{-1}\big(\alpha\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta_{n})+(1-\alpha)\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)\big)=\theta_{n,c}(\theta)\,.

Last, we note that this extends from a single
n
to being time-uniform
thanks to Doob’s maximal inequality for nonnegative supermartingale applied to
M_{n}
(also known as Vile’s inequality
Ville 1939
).

\hfill\square

A.2
Bregman concentration using Legendre function
\mathcal{L}_{0}

In this section, we state a more general result that can handle the case of sequence
(X_{t})_{t\geqslant 1}
of random variables that are not independent, each having possibly different distribution from others.

Theorem 7

(Laplace method for non i.i.d. samples)
.

Suppose
\{\mathcal{H}_{t}\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
is a filtration such that for each
t\geqslant 1
, (i)
X_{t}
is
\mathcal{H}_{t}
-measurable, (ii)
F_{t}
and
h_{t}
are
\mathcal{H}_{t-1}
-measurable and (iii) given
\mathcal{H}_{t-1}
,
X_{t}\sim p_{\theta_{0},t}
where
p_{\theta_{0},t}
belongs to an exponential family with parameter
\theta_{0}
, feature function
F_{t}
and base function
h_{t}
. Let
\mathcal{L}_{t}
be the log-partition function corresponding to
F_{t}
.
For any Legendre (i.e., strictly convex and continuously differentiable) function
\mathcal{L}_{0}:\Theta\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\exp(-\mathcal{L}_{0})
is integrable and any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we introduce the parameter estimate and Bregman information gain

\displaystyle\theta_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}}

\displaystyle=\Big(\sum_{t=0}^{n}\nabla\mathcal{L}_{t}\Big)^{-1}\Big(\sum_{t=1}^{n}F_{t}(X_{t})\Big)\,,

\displaystyle\gamma_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}}

\displaystyle=\log\bigg(\frac{\int_{\Theta}\exp\Big(-\mathcal{L}_{0}(\theta^{\prime})\Big)d\theta^{\prime}}{\int_{\Theta}\exp\Big(-\sum_{t=0}^{n}\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}_{t}}(\theta^{\prime},\theta_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}})\Big)d\theta^{\prime}}\bigg)\,,

and then, for any
\delta\in(0,1]
, the set

\displaystyle\Theta_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}}(\delta)=\left\{\theta:\sum_{t=0}^{n}\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}_{t}}\left(\theta,\theta_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}}\right)-\mathcal{L}_{0}(\theta)\leqslant\log\frac{1}{\delta}+\gamma_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}}\right\}\,.

Then, the following time-uniform concentration holds:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big[\exists n\in\mathbb{N}:\theta_{0}\notin\Theta_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}}(\delta)\big]\leqslant\delta\,.

In contrast to Theorem
3
that involves a local regularization using the true parameter
\theta_{0}\in\Theta
of the family and a constant
c\!>\!0
, here
we make use of a global regularization, in the form of the Legendre function
\mathcal{L}_{0}
. Concretely, the regularized parameter estimate
\theta_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}}
and the Bregman information gain
\gamma_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}}
do not depend on the true parameter
\theta_{0}
, thus making for a more explicit confidence set
\Theta_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}}
. The trade-off here is that the choice of regularizer is limited by the integrability assumption on
\exp(-\mathcal{L}_{0})
, which is critical to build an appropriate prior for the method of mixtures.

The proof of this result follows a similar line of proof as before. However the use of
\mathcal{L}_{0}
instead of
c
induces a few changes that we detail below. In particular, we use a different prior to build the mixture of martingales.

Proof of Theorem
7
:

Step 1. Martingale and mixture martingale construction.

For any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we define

\displaystyle M_{n}^{\lambda}=\exp\left(\sum_{t=1}^{n}\left(\lambda^{\top}\left(F_{t}(X_{t})-\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\left[F_{t}(X_{t})\right]\right)-B_{\mathcal{L}_{t},\theta}(\lambda)\right)\right)~,

where we introduced
B_{\mathcal{L}_{t},\theta}(\lambda)=\mathcal{L}_{t}(\theta+\lambda)-\mathcal{L}_{t}(\theta)-\langle\lambda,\nabla\mathcal{L}_{t}(\theta)\rangle

for convenience.

Note that
M_{n}^{\lambda}>0
and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\left[\exp\left(\lambda^{\top}F_{t}(X_{t})\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\mathcal{L}_{t}(\theta+\lambda)-\mathcal{L}_{t}(\theta)\right)~,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\left[\exp\left(\lambda^{\top}\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[F_{t}(X_{t})\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\lambda^{\top}\mathcal{L}_{t}(\theta)\right)~.

Note that
M_{n}^{\lambda}
is
\mathcal{H}_{n}
-measurable and in fact
\mathbb{E}[M_{n}^{\lambda}|\mathcal{H}_{n-1}]=M_{n-1}^{\lambda}
. Therefore
\{M_{n}^{\lambda}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
is a nonnegative martingale adapted to the filtration
\{\mathcal{H}_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
and actually satisfies
\mathbb{E}\left[M_{n}^{\lambda}\right]=1~
.
For any prior density
q(\theta)
for
\theta
, we now define a mixture of martingales

\displaystyle M_{n}=\int_{\Lambda_{\theta}}M_{n}^{\lambda}q(\theta+\lambda)d\lambda~.

(1)

where
\Lambda_{\theta}=\{\lambda:\theta+\lambda\in\Theta\}
.
Then
M_{n}
is also a martingale and actually satisfies
\mathbb{E}\left[M_{n}\right]=1~
.
Now consider the prior density

\displaystyle q(\theta+\lambda)=c_{0}\cdot\exp\left(-\mathcal{L}_{0}(\theta+\lambda)\right)~,

(2)

where
c_{0}=\frac{1}{\int_{\Theta}\exp\left(-\mathcal{L}_{0}(\theta^{\prime})\right)d\theta^{\prime}}
(which is well-defined since
\exp(-\mathcal{L}_{0})
is integrable over
\Theta
). We then have

\displaystyle M_{n}=c_{0}\int_{\Lambda_{\theta}}\exp\left(\lambda^{\top}S_{n}-\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}_{t},\theta}(\lambda)-\mathcal{L}_{0}(\theta+\lambda)\right)d\lambda~,

where we denote
S_{n}=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\left(F_{t}(X_{t})-\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\left[F_{t}(X_{t})\right]\right)
. Now, from the formula of parameter estimate, we have

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{t=1}^{n}\nabla\mathcal{L}_{t}(\theta_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}})+\nabla\mathcal{L}_{0}(\theta_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}})=\sum_{t=1}^{n}F_{t}(X_{t})~.

(3)

This yields

S_{n}=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\left(\nabla\mathcal{L}_{t}(\theta_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}})-\nabla\mathcal{L}_{t}(\theta)\right)+\nabla\mathcal{L}_{0}(\theta_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}})
.

Step 2. Legendre function and Bregman properties.

We now introduce the function
\mathcal{L}_{1:n}(\theta)=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathcal{L}_{t}(\theta)
. Note that
\mathcal{L}_{1:n}
is a also Legendre function and its associated Bregman divergence satisfies

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}_{1:n}}(\theta^{\prime},\theta)=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\left(\mathcal{L}_{t}(\theta^{\prime})-\mathcal{L}_{t}(\theta)-(\theta^{\prime}-\theta)^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{L}_{t}(\theta)\right)=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}_{t}}(\theta^{\prime},\theta).

In this notation, we can rewrite
\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}_{t},\theta}(\lambda)=\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}_{1:n},\theta}(\lambda)
and
S_{n}=\nabla\mathcal{L}_{n}(\theta_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}})-\nabla\mathcal{L}_{n}(\theta)+\nabla\mathcal{L}_{0}(\theta_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}})
. We then obtain

\displaystyle M_{n}=c_{0}\cdot\exp\left(-\mathcal{L}_{0}(\theta)\right)\int_{\Lambda_{\theta}}\exp\left(\lambda^{\top}x_{n}-\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}_{1:n},\theta}(\lambda)+\lambda^{\top}x_{0}-\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}_{0},\theta}(\lambda)\right)d\lambda~,

(4)

where we have introduced
x_{0}=\nabla\mathcal{L}_{0}(\theta_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}})-\nabla\mathcal{L}_{0}(\theta)
and
x_{n}=\nabla\mathcal{L}_{1:n}(\theta_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}})-\nabla\mathcal{L}_{1:n}(\theta)
.

We now have from the Bregman-duality property recalled in Lemma
1
that

\displaystyle\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\lambda^{\top}x_{n}-\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}_{1:n},\theta}(\lambda)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}_{1:n},\theta}^{\star}(x_{n})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}_{1:n},\theta}^{\star}(\nabla\mathcal{L}_{1:n}(\theta_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}})-\nabla\mathcal{L}_{1:n}(\theta))=\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}_{1:n}}(\theta,\theta_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}})~.

Further, any optimal
\lambda
must satisfy

\displaystyle\nabla\mathcal{L}_{1:n}(\theta+\lambda)-\nabla\mathcal{L}_{1:n}(\theta)=x_{n}\implies\nabla\mathcal{L}_{1:n}(\theta+\lambda)=\nabla\mathcal{L}_{1:n}(\theta_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}})~.

One possible solution is
\lambda^{\star}=\theta_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}}-\theta
. We then have

\displaystyle\lambda^{\top}x_{n}-\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}_{1:n},\theta}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{\top}x_{n}-\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}_{1:n},\theta}(\lambda)+\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}_{1:n}}(\theta,\theta_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}})-\left({\lambda^{\star}}^{\top}x_{n}-\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}_{1:n},\theta}(\lambda^{\star})\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}_{1:n}}(\theta,\theta_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}})+(\lambda-\lambda^{\star})^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{L}_{n}(\theta+\lambda^{\star})+\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}_{1:n},\theta}(\lambda^{\star})-\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}_{1:n},\theta}(\lambda)-(\lambda-\lambda^{\star})^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{L}_{1:n}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}_{1:n}}(\theta,\theta_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}})+(\lambda-\lambda^{\star})^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{L}_{1:n}(\theta+\lambda^{\star})+\mathcal{L}_{1:n}(\theta+\lambda^{\star})-\mathcal{L}_{1:n}(\theta+\lambda)~.

(5)

Similarly, we have

\displaystyle\lambda^{\top}x_{0}-\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}_{0},\theta}(\lambda)=\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}_{0}}(\theta,\theta_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}})+(\lambda-\lambda^{\star})^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{L}_{0}(\theta+\lambda^{\star})+\mathcal{L}_{0}(\theta+\lambda^{\star})-\mathcal{L}_{0}(\theta+\lambda)\,.

(6)

Step 3. Martingale rewriting and conclusion.

Plugin-in (
6
) and (
5
) in (
4
), we now obtain

\displaystyle M_{n}

\displaystyle=c_{0}\exp\Big(\sum_{j\in\{0,1:n\}}\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}_{j}}(\theta,\theta_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}})-\mathcal{L}_{0}(\theta)\Big)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\times\int_{\Lambda_{\theta}}\exp\Big(\sum_{j\in\{0,1:n\}}\left((\lambda-\lambda^{\star})^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{L}_{j}(\theta+\lambda^{\star})+\mathcal{L}_{j}(\theta+\lambda^{\star})-\mathcal{L}_{j}(\theta+\lambda)\right)\Big)d\lambda

\displaystyle=c_{0}\exp\Big(\sum_{j\in\{0,1:n\}}\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}_{j}}(\theta,\theta_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}})-\mathcal{L}_{0}(\theta)\Big)\cdot\exp\Big(-\sum_{j\in\{0,1:n\}}\left((\theta+\lambda^{\star})^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{L}_{j}(\theta+\lambda^{\star})-\mathcal{L}_{j}(\theta+\lambda^{\star})\right)\Big)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\times\int_{\Lambda_{\theta}}\exp\Big(\sum_{j\in\{0,1:n\}}\left((\theta+\lambda)^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{L}_{j}(\theta+\lambda^{\star})-\mathcal{L}_{j}(\theta+\lambda)\right)\Big)d\lambda

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{0}}{c_{n}}\exp\Big(\sum_{j\in\{0,1:n\}}\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}_{j}}(\theta,\theta_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}})-\mathcal{L}_{0}(\theta)\Big)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\times\frac{\int_{\Lambda_{\theta}}\exp\left(\sum_{j\in\{0,1:n\}}\left((\theta+\lambda)^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{L}_{j}(\theta+\lambda^{\star})-\mathcal{L}_{j}(\theta+\lambda)\right)\right)d\lambda}{\int_{\Theta}\exp\left(\sum_{j\in\{0,1:n\}}\left((\theta^{\prime})^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{L}_{j}(\theta+\lambda^{\star})-\mathcal{L}_{j}(\theta^{\prime})\right)\right)d\theta^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{0}}{c_{n}}\exp\Big(\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}_{t}}(\theta,\theta_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}})+\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}_{0}}(\theta,\theta_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}})-\mathcal{L}_{0}(\theta)\Big)~,

where we have introduced

c_{n}=\frac{\exp\left(\sum_{j\in\{0,1:n\}}\left((\theta+\lambda^{\star})^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{L}_{j}(\theta+\lambda^{\star})-\mathcal{L}_{j}(\theta+\lambda^{\star})\right)\right)}{\int_{\Theta}\exp\left(\sum_{j\in\{0,1:n\}}\left((\theta^{\prime})^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{L}_{j}(\theta+\lambda^{\star})-\mathcal{L}_{j}(\theta^{\prime})\right)\right)d\theta^{\prime}}~.

We then obtain the following result by a simple application of Markov’s inequality, which yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}_{t}}(\theta,\theta_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}})+\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}_{0}}(\theta,\theta_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}})-\mathcal{L}_{0}(\theta)\geqslant\log\left(\frac{c_{n}}{c_{0}}\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[M_{n}\geqslant\frac{1}{\delta}\right]\leqslant\delta\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[M_{n}\right]=\delta~.

(7)

Finally, since
\lambda^{\star}=\theta_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}}-\theta
, we have the more explicit form

\displaystyle\frac{c_{n}}{c_{0}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{\Theta}\exp\Big(-\mathcal{L}_{0}(\theta^{\prime})\Big)d\theta^{\prime}}{\int_{\Theta}\exp\Big(-\sum\limits_{j\in\{0,1:n\}}\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}_{j}}(\theta^{\prime},\theta+\lambda^{\star})\Big)d\theta^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{\Theta}\exp\Big(-\mathcal{L}_{0}(\theta^{\prime})\Big)d\theta^{\prime}}{\int_{\Theta}\exp\Big(-\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}_{t}}(\theta^{\prime},\theta_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}})-\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}_{0}}(\theta^{\prime},\theta_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}})\Big)d\theta^{\prime}}~.

We obtain the time-uniform bound by applying Doob’s maximal inequality for nonnegative supermartingale.

\hfill\square

The general result of Theorem
7
specifies straightforwardly to the case when all observations have same law, yielding the following corollary that we state now for completeness.

Corollary 8
.

Let
\mu_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}F(X_{t})
.
For all Legendre function
\mathcal{L}_{0}
such that
\exp(-\mathcal{L}_{0})
is integrable over
\Theta
, let

\displaystyle\theta_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}}\!=\big(\nabla\mathcal{L}\!+\!\frac{1}{n}\nabla\mathcal{L}_{0}\big)^{-1}(\mu_{n})\,\text{ and }\,\gamma_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}}\!=\log\left(\frac{\int_{\Theta}\exp\Big(-\mathcal{L}_{0}(\theta^{\prime})\Big)d\theta^{\prime}}{\int_{\Theta}\exp\Big(-\!n\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}\left(\theta^{\prime},\theta_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}}\right)\!-\!\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}_{0}}\left(\theta^{\prime},\theta_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}}\right)\Big)d\theta^{\prime}}\right)\,.

Then for all
\delta\in(0,1],

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigg(\exists n\in\mathbb{N},\,\,n\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}\left(\theta,\theta_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}}\right)+\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}_{0}}\left(\theta,\theta_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}}\right)\geqslant\log\frac{1}{\delta}+\mathcal{L}_{0}(\theta)+\gamma_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}}\bigg)\leqslant\delta\,.

Choice of Legendre function
\mathcal{L}_{0}

A natural choice for the Legendre regularizer
\mathcal{L}_{0}
is to use the log-partition function of the exponential family at hand. If
\mathcal{L}_{0}=c\mathcal{L}
, where
c>0
is a scaling coefficient, the formula above simplify to

\displaystyle\theta_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}}\!=\frac{\theta_{n}}{1+\frac{c}{n}}\,\text{ and }\,\gamma_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}}\!=\log\left(\frac{\int_{\Theta}\exp\Big(-c\mathcal{L}(\theta^{\prime})\Big)d\theta^{\prime}}{\int_{\Theta}\exp\Big(-\!(n+c)\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}\left(\theta^{\prime},\theta_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}}\right)\Big)d\theta^{\prime}}\right)\,,

where
\theta_{n}=\left(\nabla\mathcal{L}\right)^{-1}(\mu_{n})
is the standard maximum likelihood estimate of the parameter
\theta_{0}
.

In the special case of the one-dimensional Gaussian family
\mathcal{N}(\theta_{0},\sigma^{2})
where the variance
\sigma^{2}
is known, the log-partition function regularizer defined by
\mathcal{L}_{0}(\theta)=\frac{\theta^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}
satisfies the integrability assumption
\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-c\mathcal{L}_{0}(\theta)}d\theta<\infty
with
c>0
. Straightforward calculations show that the resulting confidence set is the same as the one derived from Theorem
3
with local regularization
c
(see Appendix
C.1
). For many other standard families however, this integrability assumption may fail, as in the case of the exponential distributions
\mathcal{E}(-\theta)
, for which
\mathcal{L}(\theta)=-\log(-\theta)
(see Appendix
C.5
).

For other choices of Legendre function, computing the regularized parameter estimate
\theta_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}}
and the information gain
\gamma_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}}
requires inverting the function
\nabla\mathcal{L}+\frac{1}{n}\nabla\mathcal{L}_{0}
and computing a tedious integral, both of which seldom result in closed-form expressions. For these reasons, we recommend as a first step the use of local regularization (Theorem
3
) over Legendre regularizers. In Appendix
F
, we detail an application of Theorem
7
to Gaussian contextual bandits using a quadratic regularizer.

Appendix B
Properties of Bregman Divergences

In this section, we detail the derivation of some useful properties of the Bregman divergence that are used in the proof of the Bregman deviation inequalities.

See
1

Proof of Lemma
1
:

The first equality is immediate, since
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[F(X)]=\nabla{\mathcal{L}}(\theta)
and

\displaystyle\log\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\left[\exp(\langle\lambda,F(X)\rangle)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log\int\exp(\langle\lambda,F(x)\rangle+\langle{\theta},F(x)\rangle-{\mathcal{L}}(\theta))dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}(\lambda+\theta)-{\mathcal{L}}(\theta)\,.

We now turn to the duality formula. Using the definition of each terms, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{{\mathcal{L}},\theta^{\prime}}^{\star}(\nabla{\mathcal{L}}(\theta)-\nabla{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{\lambda}\langle\lambda,\nabla{\mathcal{L}}(\theta)-\nabla{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\prime})\rangle-\Big[{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\prime}+\lambda)-{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\prime})-\langle\lambda,\nabla{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\prime})\rangle\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{\lambda}\langle\lambda,\nabla{\mathcal{L}}(\theta)\rangle-{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\prime}+\lambda)+{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\prime})\,.

An optimal
\lambda
must satisfy
\nabla{\mathcal{L}}(\theta)=\nabla{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\prime}+\lambda)
. Hence, provided that
\nabla{\mathcal{L}}
is invertible, this means
\lambda=\theta-\theta^{\prime}
. Plugin-in this value, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{{\mathcal{L}},\theta^{\prime}}^{\star}(\nabla{\mathcal{L}}(\theta)-\nabla{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\prime}))=\langle\theta-\theta^{\prime},\nabla{\mathcal{L}}(\theta)\rangle-{\mathcal{L}}(\theta)+{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\prime})=\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\prime},\theta)\,.

The remaining equality
\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\prime},\theta)=\mathcal{B}_{{\mathcal{L}}^{\star}}(\nabla{\mathcal{L}}(\theta),\nabla{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\prime}))
is a standard result.
Finally, regarding the generalization, we note that

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{{\mathcal{L}},\theta^{\prime}}^{\star}\Big(\alpha(\nabla{\mathcal{L}}(\theta)-\nabla{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\prime}))\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{\lambda}\langle\lambda,\alpha\nabla{\mathcal{L}}(\theta)+(1-\alpha)\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta^{\prime})\rangle-{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\prime}+\lambda)+{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\prime})\,.

Hence, an optimal
\lambda
must now satisfy
\alpha\nabla{\mathcal{L}}(\theta)+(1-\alpha)\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta^{\prime})=\nabla{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\prime}+\lambda)
, that is
\lambda=\theta_{\alpha}-\theta^{\prime}
. This further yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{{\mathcal{L}},\theta^{\prime}}^{\star}\Big(\alpha(\nabla{\mathcal{L}}(\theta)-\nabla{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\prime}))\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\theta_{\alpha}-\theta^{\prime},\nabla{\mathcal{L}}(\theta_{\alpha})\rangle-{\mathcal{L}}(\theta_{\alpha})+{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\prime})=\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\prime},\theta_{\alpha})\,.

\hfill\square

C.3
Gaussian with unknown mean and variance

We consider
X\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\mu,\sigma^{2}\right)
, where both
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
and
\sigma>0
are unknown. These distributions form a two-dimensional exponential family with parameter
\theta=\begin{pmatrix}\theta_{1}\\
\theta_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\mu}{\sigma^{2}}\\
-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\end{pmatrix}
belonging to the domain
\Theta=\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{*}_{-}
. The corresponding feature and log-partition functions writes
F(x)=\begin{pmatrix}x\\
x^{2}\end{pmatrix}
and
\mathcal{L}(\theta)=-\frac{1}{2}\log(-\theta_{2})-\frac{\theta_{1}^{2}}{4\theta_{2}}
respectively. The gradient and Hessian of
\mathcal{L}
follows from straightforward calculations and write:

\displaystyle\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{\theta_{1}}{2\theta_{2}}\\
-\frac{1}{2\theta_{2}}+\frac{\theta_{1}^{2}}{4\theta_{2}^{2}}\end{pmatrix}\,\,,\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{1}{2\theta_{2}}&\frac{\theta_{1}}{2\theta_{2}^{2}}\\
\frac{\theta_{1}}{2\theta_{2}^{2}}&\frac{1}{2\theta_{2}^{2}}-\frac{\theta_{1}^{2}}{2\theta_{2}^{3}}\end{pmatrix}\,.

In particular,
\text{Tr }\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}(\theta)=-\frac{1}{2\theta_{2}}+\frac{1}{2\theta_{2}^{2}}-\frac{\theta_{1}^{2}}{2\theta_{2}^{3}}>0
and
\det\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}(\theta)=-\frac{1}{4\theta_{2}^{3}}>0
, therefore the symmetric matrix
\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}(\theta)
is positive definite (both its eigenvalues are positive). The inverse of the gradient mapping
\nabla\mathcal{L}\colon\Theta\rightarrow\mathcal{P}
, where the image set is
\mathcal{P}=\left\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2},y>x^{2}\right\}
, can be explicitly computed as:

\displaystyle\left(\nabla\right)^{-1}\mathcal{L}\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\end{pmatrix}=\frac{1}{y-x^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}x\\
-\frac{1}{2}\end{pmatrix}\,.

\theta
and
\theta^{\prime}
can be calculated from the above results and reads:

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\prime},\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{\theta_{2}}{\theta^{\prime}_{2}}+\frac{\theta^{\prime}_{2}}{2\theta_{2}}-\theta^{\prime}_{2}\left(\frac{\theta^{\prime}_{1}}{2\theta^{\prime}_{2}}-\frac{\theta_{1}}{2\theta_{2}}\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}.

-\frac{1}{2}\end{pmatrix}\,.

\theta_{1}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=e^{\frac{c}{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{0}\left(\frac{\theta^{\prime}_{2}}{\theta_{2}}\right)^{\frac{c}{2}}e^{-\frac{c}{2}\frac{\theta^{\prime}_{2}}{\theta_{2}}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left(c\theta^{\prime}_{2}\left(\frac{\theta^{\prime}_{1}}{2\theta^{\prime}_{2}}-\frac{\theta_{1}}{2\theta_{2}}\right)^{2}\right)d\theta_{1}^{\prime}d\theta_{2}^{\prime}\,.

Let
m=\frac{\theta_{1}}{2\theta_{2}}
and
s=\frac{1}{\sqrt{-2c\theta^{\prime}_{2}}}
. The integral w.r.t
\theta^{\prime}_{1}
can be rewritten using the change of variable

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left(c\theta^{\prime}_{2}\left(\frac{\theta^{\prime}_{1}}{2\theta^{\prime}_{2}}-\frac{\theta_{1}}{2\theta_{2}}\right)^{2}\right)d\theta_{1}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=-2\theta_{2}^{\prime}\sqrt{2\pi}s\underbrace{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}s}e^{-\frac{\left(y-m\right)^{2}}{2s^{2}}}dy}_{=1}\,.

Therefore, the above calculation simplifies to:

\displaystyle\int_{\Theta}\exp\left(-c\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\prime},\theta)\right)d\theta^{\prime}

\displaystyle=2\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{c}}e^{\frac{c}{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{0}\left(\frac{\theta^{\prime}_{2}}{\theta_{2}}\right)^{\frac{c}{2}}e^{-\frac{c}{2}\frac{\theta^{\prime}_{2}}{\theta_{2}}}\sqrt{-\theta_{2}^{\prime}}d\theta_{2}^{\prime}\,,

which after a linear change of variable on
\theta^{\prime}_{2}
can be related to the Gamma function as follows:

\displaystyle\int_{\Theta}\exp\left(-c\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\prime},\theta)\right)d\theta^{\prime}

\displaystyle=2\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{c}}\left(\frac{2}{c}\right)^{\frac{c+1}{2}+1}\Gamma\left(\frac{c+1}{2}+1\right)\left(-\theta_{2}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}\,.

The Bregman information gain is thus:

\displaystyle\gamma_{n,c}(\theta)

\displaystyle=-\left(\frac{1\!+\!\log 2}{2}\right)n-\left(\frac{c}{2}+2\right)\log c+\left(\frac{n\!+\!c}{2}+2\right)\log(n\!+\!c)

\displaystyle\quad+\log\Gamma\left(\frac{c\!+\!3}{2}\right)-\log\Gamma\left(\frac{n\!+\!c\!+\!3}{2}\right)+\frac{3}{2}\log\frac{\theta_{2}}{\theta_{n,c}(\theta)_{2}}\,,

shows that w.p.
\geqslant 1-\delta
,
\forall n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle-\frac{n\!+\!c\!+\!3}{2}\log\left(\frac{\theta_{2}}{\theta_{n,c}(\theta)_{2}}\right)-\theta_{2}(n\!+\!c)V_{n,c}-\frac{n\theta_{1}^{2}}{4\theta_{2}}-\frac{\theta_{2}}{n\!+\!c}S_{n}^{2}-\theta_{1}S_{n}

\displaystyle\quad\leqslant\log\frac{1}{\delta}-\frac{n}{2}\log 2-\left(\frac{c}{2}\!+\!2\right)\log c+\left(\frac{n\!+\!c}{2}\!+\!2\right)\log\left(n\!+\!c\right)+\log\Gamma\left(\frac{c\!+\!3}{2}\right)-\log\Gamma\left(\frac{n\!+\!c\!+\!3}{2}\right)\,.

After expanding the ratio
\frac{\theta_{2}}{\theta_{n,c}(\theta)_{2}}
and substituting the natural parametrization in terms of
\mu
and
\sigma
, we finally obtain that w.p.
\geqslant 1-\delta
,
\forall n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle-\frac{n\!+\!c\!+\!3}{2}\log\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}V_{n,c}+\frac{c}{n\!+\!c}+\frac{nc}{(n\!+\!c)^{2}}\frac{\mu^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}-\frac{2c}{(n\!+\!c)^{2}}\frac{\mu}{\sigma^{2}}S_{n}\right)+\frac{n\!+\!c}{2\sigma^{2}}V_{n,c}+\frac{n\mu^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}+\frac{1}{2(n\!+\!c)\sigma^{2}}S_{n}^{2}-\frac{\mu}{\sigma^{2}}S_{n}

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant\log\frac{1}{\delta}-\frac{n}{2}\log 2-\left(\frac{c}{2}\!+\!2\right)\log c+\left(\frac{n\!+\!c}{2}\!+\!2\right)\log\left(n\!+\!c\right)+\log\Gamma\left(\frac{c\!+\!3}{2}\right)-\log\Gamma\left(\frac{n\!+\!c\!+\!3}{2}\right)\,.

To simplify this formula, we introduce the standardized sum of squares
Z_{n}(m,s)=\sum\limits_{t=1}^{n}\left(\frac{X_{t}-m}{s}\right)^{2}
for
(m,s)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}

\widehat{\mu}_{n}=S_{n}/n

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}Z_{n}(\mu,\sigma)-\frac{n+c+3}{2}\log\left(\frac{n}{n\!+\!c}Z_{n}(\widehat{\mu}_{n},\sigma)+\frac{c}{n\!+\!c}Z_{n}(\mu,\sigma)+c\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\log\frac{1}{\delta}-\frac{n}{2}\log 2-\left(\frac{c}{2}\!+\!2\right)\log c+\frac{1}{2}\log\left(n\!+\!c\right)+\log\Gamma\left(\frac{c\!+\!3}{2}\right)-\log\Gamma\left(\frac{n\!+\!c\!+\!3}{2}\right)\,.

C.4
Bernoulli

We consider
X\sim\text{Bernoulli}(\mu)
. This corresponds to an exponential family model, with parameter
\theta=\log\frac{\mu}{1-\mu}
, feature function
F(x)=x
and log-partition function
\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\log(1+\exp(\theta))
. The Bregman divergence between two parameters
\theta^{\prime}
and
\theta
associated with
\mathcal{L}
is given by
\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\prime},\theta)=\texttt{kl}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})=\mu\log\frac{\mu}{\mu^{\prime}}+(1-\mu)\log\frac{1-\mu}{1-\mu^{\prime}}
.
We further have
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(\theta)=\frac{\exp(\theta)}{1+\exp(\theta)}=\mu
and
\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime}(\theta)=\frac{\exp(\theta)}{(1+\exp(\theta))^{2}}=\mu(1-\mu)
. Therefore
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
is invertible and we have the expression
\theta_{n,c}(\theta)=(\mathcal{L}^{\prime})^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{n+c}\sum_{t=1}^{n}X_{t}+\frac{c}{n+c}\frac{\exp(\theta)}{1+\exp(\theta)}\right)
. Then, denoting
S_{n}=\sum_{t=1}^{n}X_{t}
, we get

\displaystyle\mu_{n,c}(\mu):=\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(\theta_{n,c}(\theta))=\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{n}X_{t}+c\mu}{n+c}=\frac{S_{n}+c\mu}{n+c}~.

Therefore, the Bregman deviation specifies to the following closed-form formula

\displaystyle(n+c)\cdot\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta,\theta_{n,c}(\theta))

\displaystyle=(n+c)\cdot\texttt{kl}(\mu_{n,c}(\mu),\mu)

\displaystyle=(S_{n}+c\mu)\log\frac{\mu_{n,c}(\mu)}{\mu}+\left(n-S_{n}+c(1-\mu)\right)\log\frac{1-\mu_{n,c}(\mu)}{1-\mu}~.

Now, we observe that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\exp\big(-\!c\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\prime},\theta)\big)d\theta^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{\text{B}(c\mu,c(1-\mu))}{\mu^{c\mu}(1-\mu)^{c(1-\mu)}}~,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\exp\big(-\!(n+c)\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\prime},\theta_{n,c}(\theta))\big)d\theta^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{\text{B}((n+c)\mu_{n,c}(\mu),(n+c)(1-\mu_{n,c}(\mu)))}{\mu_{n,c}(\mu)^{(n+c)\mu_{n,c}(\mu)}(1-\mu_{n,c}(\mu))^{(n+c)(1-\mu_{n,c}(\mu))}}~.

Therefore, we deduce that the Bregman information gain rewrites

\displaystyle\gamma_{n,c}(\mu)=

\displaystyle(S_{n}+c\mu)\log\mu_{n,c}(\mu)+\left(n-S_{n}+c(1-\mu)\right)\log(1-\mu_{n,c}(\mu))-c\mu\log\mu-c(1-\mu)\log(1-\mu)

\displaystyle+\log\frac{\Gamma(c\mu)\Gamma(c(1-\mu))}{\Gamma(S_{n}+c\mu)\Gamma(n-S_{n}+c(1-\mu))}+\log\frac{\Gamma(n+c)}{\Gamma(c)}~.

Combining the above and using Theorem
3
, we obtain that w.p.
\geqslant 1-\delta
,
\forall n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle S_{n}\log\frac{1}{\mu}+(n-S_{n})\log\frac{1}{1-\mu}+\log\frac{\Gamma(S_{n}+c\mu)\Gamma(n-S_{n}+c(1-\mu))}{\Gamma(c\mu)\Gamma(c(1-\mu))}\leqslant\log\frac{1}{\delta}+\log\frac{\Gamma(n+c)}{\Gamma(c)}~.

\mathcal{L}^{\prime}

\displaystyle k\lambda_{n,c}(\lambda):=\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(\theta_{n,c}(\theta))=\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{n}X_{t}+ck\lambda}{n+c}

Therefore, the Bregman divergence takes the following form

\displaystyle(n+c)\cdot\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta,\theta_{n,c}(\theta))

\displaystyle=(n+c)\cdot\texttt{KL}(p_{\lambda_{n,c}(\lambda)},p_{\lambda})

\displaystyle=k(n+c)\left(\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{n}X_{t}+ck\lambda}{(n+c)k\lambda}\right)-k(n+c)\log\left(\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{n}X_{t}+ck\lambda}{(n+c)k\lambda}\right)-k(n+c)~.

Now, we observe that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\exp\big(-\!c\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\prime},\theta)\big)d\theta^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(ck)}{\lambda}\left(\frac{e}{ck}\right)^{ck},

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\exp\big(-\!(n+c)\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\prime},\theta_{n,c}(\theta))\big)d\theta^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma((n+c)k)}{\lambda_{n,c}(\lambda)}\left(\frac{e}{(n+c)k}\right)^{(n+c)k}~.

Therefore, the Bregman information gain writes explicitly as follows

\displaystyle\gamma_{n,c}(\theta)=\log\left(\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{n}X_{t}+ck\lambda}{(n+c)k\lambda}\right)+\log\left(\Gamma(ck)\left(\frac{e}{ck}\right)^{ck}\right)-\log\left(\Gamma((n+c)k)\left(\frac{e}{(n+c)k}\right)^{(n+c)k}\right).

Using the above with Theorem
3
, we obtain w.p.
\geqslant 1-\delta
,
\forall n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle k(n+c)\left(\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{n}X_{t}+ck\lambda}{(n+c)k\lambda}\right)-(k(n+c)+1)\log\left(\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{n}X_{t}+ck\lambda}{(n+c)k\lambda}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\log\frac{1}{\delta}+\log\frac{\Gamma(ck)}{\Gamma((n+c)k)}+(n+c)k\log((n+c)k)+ck-ck\log ck.

Weibull with fixed shape

We consider
X\sim\text{Weibull}(\lambda,k)
with fixed shape
k>0
and unknown scale
\lambda>0
.
3
3

3

Note that
\text{Weibull}(\lambda,k)

\text{Exp}(1/\lambda)
.
The distribution of

\theta=-\frac{1}{\lambda^{k}}

\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\log(\lambda^{k})=\log(-\frac{1}{\theta})

\theta^{\prime}
and
\theta

\mathcal{L}

\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\prime},\theta)=\texttt{KL}(P_{\lambda},P_{\lambda^{\prime}})=\left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda^{\prime}}\right)^{k}-1-\log\left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda^{\prime}}\right)^{k}

Note that
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(\theta)=-\frac{1}{\theta}=\lambda^{k}
and
\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime}(\theta)=\frac{1}{\theta^{2}}=\lambda^{2k}

\mathcal{L}^{\prime}

\displaystyle(\lambda_{n,c}(\lambda))^{k}:=\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(\theta_{n,c}(\theta))=\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{n}X_{t}^{k}+c\lambda^{k}}{n+c}.

Therefore, the Bregman divergence takes the following form

\displaystyle(n+c)\cdot\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta,\theta_{n,c}(\theta))

\displaystyle=(n+c)\cdot\texttt{KL}(p_{\lambda_{n,c}(\lambda)},p_{\lambda})

\displaystyle=(n+c)\left(\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{n}X_{t}^{k}+c\lambda^{k}}{(n+c)\lambda^{k}}\right)-(n+c)\log\left(\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{n}X_{t}^{k}+c\lambda^{k}}{(n+c)\lambda^{k}}\right)-(n+c)~.

Now, we observe that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\exp\big(-\!c\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\prime},\theta)\big)d\theta^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(n+c)}{(\lambda_{n,c}(\lambda))^{k}}\left(\frac{e}{n+c}\right)^{n+c}~.

Therefore, the Bregman information gain writes explicitly as follows

\displaystyle\gamma_{n,c}(\theta)=\log\left(\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{n}X_{t}^{k}+c\lambda^{k}}{(n+c)\lambda^{k}}\right)+\log\left(\Gamma(c)\left(\frac{e}{c}\right)^{c}\right)-\log\left(\Gamma(n+c)\left(\frac{e}{n+c}\right)^{n+c}\right).

Using the above with Theorem
3

\geqslant 1-\delta

\displaystyle\leqslant\log\frac{1}{\delta}+\log\frac{\Gamma(c)}{\Gamma(n+c)}+(n+c)\log(n+c)+c-c\log c.

Pareto with fixed scale

is unknown.

\mathcal{L}(\theta)=-\log\left(-1\!-\!\theta\right)

\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(\theta)=-\frac{1}{1+\theta}

C.9
Chi-square

We finally consider
X\sim\chi^{2}(k)
or, equivalently,
X\sim\texttt{Gamma}\left(\frac{k}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right)
, i.e.,
p_{k}(x)=\frac{(\frac{1}{2})^{\frac{k}{2}}}{\Gamma\left(\frac{k}{2}\right)}x^{\frac{k}{2}-1}e^{-\frac{x}{2}}
,
x\geqslant 0
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
(or
k\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\setminus\{0\}
if one considers
Gamma
distributions).
This corresponds to an exponential family model with parameter
\theta=\frac{k}{2}-1
, feature function
F(x)=\log x
and log-partition function
\mathcal{L}(\theta)=(\theta+1)\log 2+\log\Gamma(\theta+1)
. The Bregman divergence between two parameters
\theta^{\prime}
and
\theta
associated with
\mathcal{L}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\prime},\theta)=\texttt{KL}(P_{k},P_{k^{\prime}})=\frac{1}{2}(k-k^{\prime})\psi_{0}(k/2)-\log\frac{\Gamma(k/2)}{\Gamma(k^{\prime}/2)}~,

where
\psi_{0}(x)=\frac{d}{dx}\log\Gamma(x)
denotes the digamma function.

We further have
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(\theta)=\log 2+\psi_{0}(\theta+1)
and
\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime}(\theta)=\psi_{1}(\theta+1)
, where
\psi_{1}(x)=\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}\log\Gamma(x)
denotes the trigamma function. Therefore
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
is invertible, and the parameter estimate is given by

\displaystyle\log 2+\psi_{0}(\theta_{n,c}(\theta)+1)=\frac{1}{n+c}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\log X_{t}+\frac{c}{n+c}(\log 2+\psi_{0}(\theta+1))~,

yielding

\displaystyle k_{n,c}(k):=2(1+\theta_{n,c}(\theta))

\displaystyle=2\psi_{0}^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{n+c}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\log X_{t}+\frac{c}{n+c}\psi_{0}(k/2)-\frac{n}{n+c}\log 2\right)

\displaystyle=2\psi_{0}^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{n+c}K_{n}+\frac{c}{n+c}\psi_{0}(k/2)\right)\,,

where
K_{n}=\sum\limits_{t=1}^{n}\log\frac{X_{t}}{2}
.
Therefore, the Bregman divergence rewrites as follows

\displaystyle(n+c)\cdot\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta,\theta_{n,c}(\theta))

\displaystyle=(n+c)\cdot\texttt{KL}(P_{k_{n,c}(k)},P_{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(k_{n,c}(k)-k)\left(K_{n}+c\;\psi_{0}\left(\frac{k}{2}\right)\right)-(n+c)\log\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{k_{n,c}(k)}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{k}{2}\right)}~.

Now, we see that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\exp\big(\!-\!c\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\prime},\theta)\big)d\theta^{\prime}\!

\displaystyle=\!\int\frac{1}{2}\exp\left(\!-\frac{c}{2}(k-k^{\prime})\psi_{0}(k/2)\right)\left(\frac{\Gamma(k/2)}{\Gamma(k^{\prime}/2)}\right)^{c}dk^{\prime}~,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\exp\big(\!-\!(n+c)\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\prime},\theta_{n,c}(\theta))\big)d\theta^{\prime}\!

\displaystyle=\!\int\frac{1}{2}\exp\left(\!-\frac{n\!+\!c}{2}(k_{n,c}(k)-k^{\prime})\psi_{0}(k_{n,c}(k)/2)\right)\left(\frac{\Gamma(k_{n,c}(k)/2)}{\Gamma(k^{\prime}/2)}\right)^{n\!+\!c}\!dk^{\prime}~.

Therefore, the Bregman information gain writes

\displaystyle\gamma_{n,c}(\theta)

\displaystyle=c\log\Gamma\left(\frac{k}{2}\right)-(n+c)\log\Gamma\left(\frac{k_{n,c}(k)}{2}\right)-c\;\frac{k}{2}\psi_{0}\left(\frac{k}{2}\right)+(n+c)\frac{k_{n,c}(k)}{2}\psi_{0}\left(\frac{k_{n,c}(k)}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\log\frac{\int\left(\Gamma\left(k^{\prime}/2\right)\right)^{-c}\exp\left(c\;\frac{k^{\prime}}{2}\psi_{0}\left(\frac{k}{2}\right)\right)dk^{\prime}}{\int\left(\Gamma\left(k^{\prime}/2\right)\right)^{-(n+c)}\exp\left((n+c)\frac{k^{\prime}}{2}\psi_{0}\left(\frac{k_{n,c}(k)}{2}\right)\right)dk^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=c\log\Gamma\left(\frac{k}{2}\right)-(n+c)\log\Gamma\left(\frac{k_{n,c}(k)}{2}\right)-c\;\frac{k}{2}\psi_{0}\left(\frac{k}{2}\right)+\frac{k_{n,c}(k)}{2}\left(K_{n}+c\psi_{0}\left(\frac{k}{2}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\log\frac{\int\left(\Gamma\left(k^{\prime}/2\right)\right)^{-c}\exp\left(c\;\frac{k^{\prime}}{2}\psi_{0}\left(\frac{k}{2}\right)\right)dk^{\prime}}{\int\left(\Gamma\left(k^{\prime}/2\right)\right)^{-(n+c)}\exp\left(\frac{k^{\prime}}{2}\left(K_{n}+c\psi_{0}\left(\frac{k}{2}\right)\right)\right)dk^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=c\log\Gamma\left(\frac{k}{2}\right)-(n+c)\log\Gamma\left(\frac{k_{n,c}(k)}{2}\right)-c\;\frac{k}{2}\psi_{0}\left(\frac{k}{2}\right)+\frac{k_{n,c}(k)}{2}\left(K_{n}+c\psi_{0}\left(\frac{k}{2}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\log J\left(c,c\psi_{0}\left(\frac{k}{2}\right)\right)-\log J\left(n\!+\!c,K_{n}\!+\!c\psi_{0}\left(\frac{k}{2}\right)\right)\,,

where we define the auxiliary function
J(a,b)=\int\exp\left(-a\log\Gamma\left(\frac{k^{\prime}}{2}\right)+b\frac{k^{\prime}}{2}\right)dk^{\prime}
. Combining the above with Theorem

Remark 10
.

The ratio of integrals (or infinite sums) in (
12
) can be efficiently implemented using a simple integration scheme (or by truncation). Indeed, for a given
k_{\max}\in\mathbb{N}
,
b\ll 1
and
B\gg 1
, we define
x_{k^{\prime}}=b+\frac{k^{\prime}}{k_{\max}}\left(B-b\right)
for
k^{\prime}=0,\dots,k_{\max}
, so that

\displaystyle\log\frac{\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left(\Gamma\left(\frac{k^{\prime}}{2}\right)\right)^{-c}\exp\left(\frac{k^{\prime}}{2}c\psi_{0}\left(\frac{k}{2}\right)\right)dk^{\prime}}{\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left(\Gamma\left(\frac{k^{\prime}}{2}\right)\right)^{-(n+c)}\exp\left(\frac{k^{\prime}}{2}\left(c\psi_{0}\left(\frac{k}{2}\right)+\sum_{t=1}^{n}\log\frac{X_{t}}{2}\right)\right)dk^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\approx\underset{k^{\prime}=1,\dots,k_{\max}}{\emph{logsumexp}}\left(-c\log\Gamma\left(\frac{x_{k^{\prime}}}{2}\right)+\frac{cx_{k^{\prime}}}{2}\psi_{0}\left(\frac{k}{2}\right)+\log\left(x_{k^{\prime}}-x_{k^{\prime}-1}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\quad-\underset{k^{\prime}=1,\dots,k_{\max}}{\emph{logsumexp}}\left(-\left(n+c\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{x_{k^{\prime}}}{2}\right)+\frac{x_{k^{\prime}}}{2}\left(c\psi_{0}\left(\frac{k}{2}+\sum_{t=1}^{n}\log\frac{X_{t}}{2}\right)\right)+\log\left(x_{k^{\prime}}-x_{k^{\prime}-1}\right)\right).

Similarly, we have

\displaystyle\log\frac{\sum\limits_{k^{\prime}=1}^{+\infty}\left(\Gamma\left(\frac{k^{\prime}}{2}\right)\right)^{-c}\exp\left(\frac{k^{\prime}}{2}c\psi_{0}\left(\frac{k}{2}\right)\right)}{\sum\limits_{k^{\prime}=1}^{+\infty}\left(\Gamma\left(\frac{k^{\prime}}{2}\right)\right)^{-(n+c)}\exp\left(\frac{k^{\prime}}{2}\left(c\psi_{0}\left(\frac{k}{2}\right)+\sum_{t=1}^{n}\log\frac{X_{t}}{2}\right)\right)}

\displaystyle\approx\underset{k^{\prime}=1,\dots,k_{\max}}{\emph{logsumexp}}\left(-c\log\Gamma\left(\frac{k^{\prime}}{2}\right)+\frac{k^{\prime}}{2}c\psi_{0}\left(\frac{k}{2}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\quad-\underset{k^{\prime}=1,\dots,k_{\max}}{\emph{logsumexp}}\left(-\left(n+c\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{k^{\prime}}{2}\right)+\frac{k^{\prime}}{2}\left(c\psi_{0}\left(\frac{k}{2}+\sum_{t=1}^{n}\log\frac{X_{t}}{2}\right)\right)\right),

The final steps correspond to the right-rectangular scheme over
(b,B)
with
k_{\max}
steps and the truncation to the first
k_{\max}
terms respectively. Note the use of
\underset{k^{\prime}=1,\dots,k_{\max}}{\emph{logsumexp}}(z)=\log\sum\limits_{k^{\prime}=1}^{k_{\max}}\exp\left(z_{k^{\prime}}\right)
for
z\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{\max}}
, which is efficiently implemented in many libraries for scientific computing and better handles summation of large numbers. Empirically, we found that
\log b=-10,\log B=10
and
k_{\max}=2000
provided sufficient accuracy and that using finer approximation schemes did not significantly impact the numerical results.

Gaussian with unknown mean and variance

Figure 7
:
Example of time-uniform, joint confidence sets around

We consider the two-dimensional family
\left\{\mathcal{N}\left(\mu,\sigma^{2}\right),\left(\mu,\sigma\right)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{*}_{+}\right\}
. To the best of our knowledge, there does not exist time-uniform joint confidence sets for
\left(\mu,\sigma\right)
prior to this work. In order to compare ourselves against some baseline, we derive a crude one based on Chi-square quantiles and a union bound.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N},\delta\in(0,1)
,
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
i.i.d samples from
\mathcal{N}(\mu_{0},\sigma_{0}^{2})
. The standardized sum of squares

Z_{t}(\mu_{0},\sigma_{0})=\sum\limits_{s=1}^{t}\left(\frac{X_{t}-\mu_{0}}{\sigma_{0}}\right)^{2}

follows a
\chi^{2}(t)
distribution, therefore, denoting by
q_{\chi^{2}(t)}
the corresponding quantile function, we have:

\mathbb{P}\left(q_{\chi^{2}(t)}\left(\frac{\delta}{2}\right)\leqslant Z_{t}(\mu_{0},\sigma_{0})\leqslant q_{\chi^{2}(t)}\left(1-\frac{\delta}{2}\right)\right)\geqslant 1-\delta\,.

By a union bound argument, the intersection of
n
such events with confidence
\frac{\delta}{n}
, i.e

\Theta^{Z}_{t,n}(\delta)=\bigcap\limits_{s=1}^{t}\left\{(\mu,\sigma)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}\colon\ q_{\chi^{2}(s)}\left(\frac{\delta}{2n}\right)\leqslant Z_{s}(\mu,\sigma)\leqslant q_{\chi^{2}(s)}\left(1-\frac{\delta}{2n}\right)\right\}\,

describes a sequence of confidence sets at level
\delta
that hold uniformly over
t\in\{1,\dots,n\}
.

We report our confidence sets (cf. Equation
2
) and the above on Figure
7
. The most striking drawback of
\Theta^{Z}_{t,n}(\delta)
is that it is not convex nor even bounded; in particular, projecting onto the axes of
\mu
and
\sigma
only provides trivial confidence sets,
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
respectively, rendering this result vacuous. To better grasp this phenomenon, let us informally consider
\mu=\alpha+\mu_{0}
and
\sigma=\alpha
for some
\alpha>0
. We have:

\displaystyle Z_{n}(\mu,\sigma)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{t=1}^{n}\left(\frac{X_{t}-\mu_{0}}{\alpha}-1\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{n}\left(X_{t}-\mu_{0}\right)^{2}}_{\approx\frac{\sigma_{0}^{2}}{\alpha^{2}}n}-\underbrace{\frac{2}{\alpha}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{n}\left(X_{t}-\mu_{0}\right)}_{\approx\frac{2}{\alpha}\sqrt{n}}+n\,.

Hence, for
\alpha\rightarrow+\infty
,
Z_{n}(\mu_{0}+\alpha,\alpha)\approx n
. On the other hand, Lemma 1 in
Laurent and Massart 2000
shows that
q_{\chi^{2}(n)}\left(1-\delta/2n\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(n+\sqrt{n\log n}\right)
and
q_{\chi^{2}(s)}\left(\delta/2n\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(n\right)
. Therefore, even with increasing the sample size
n
, there exists arbitrary large
\alpha
such that
(\mu_{0}+\alpha,\alpha)
may belong to
\Theta^{Z}_{t,n}(\delta)
.

By contrast, our Bregman confidence sets are convex and bounded, which we interpret as the result of exploiting the true geometry of the two-dimensional Gaussian family. In addition to the unboundedness,
\Theta^{Z}_{t,n}(\delta)
is built using a crude union bound, which is not anytime (depends on the terminal time
n
) and rather loose.

D.2
Bernoulli

We consider the Bernoulli distribution
\text{Bernoulli}(\mu)
for some unknown
\mu\in[0,1]
. Confidence bounds are displayed in Figure

for
\mu\in\{0.2,0.5,0.8\}

Chernoff-Laplace method for sub-Gaussian distributions

We recall that
B(\mu)
being supported in
[0,1]
, it is
1/2
-sub-Gaussian (Hoeffding lemma,
Hoeffding 1963
). The method of mixtures for exponential martingales
(
Peña et al. 2008
)
shows that
\mathbb{P}\left(\forall n\in\mathbb{N},\mu\in\Theta^{\text{Laplace}}_{n}(\delta)\right)\geqslant 1-\delta
, with

\Theta^{\text{Laplace}}_{n}(\delta)=\left[\widehat{\mu}_{n}-\sqrt{\left(1+\frac{1}{n}\right)\frac{\log\left(2\sqrt{1+n}/\delta\right)}{2n}},\widehat{\mu}_{n}+\sqrt{\left(1+\frac{1}{n}\right)\frac{\log\left(2\sqrt{1+n}/\delta\right)}{2n}}\right].

Note that a sharper sub-Gaussian control holds for
B(\mu)
, see
Kearns and Saul 1998
,
Berend and Kontorovich 2013
. However, the optimal sub-Gaussian parameter they suggest (
\frac{1/2-\mu}{\log(1/\mu-1)}
instead of
1/2
) is a function of the unknown
\mu
.

Bentkus concentration with geometric time-peeling

Kuchibhotla and Zheng 2021
show that
\mathbb{P}\left(\forall n\in\mathbb{N},\mu\in\Theta^{\text{Bentkus}}_{n}(\delta)\right)\geqslant 1-\delta
with

\Theta^{\text{Bentkus}}_{n}(\delta)=\left[\widehat{\mu}_{n}-\frac{1}{n}q\left(\frac{\delta}{2h(k_{n})};c_{n},1/2,1\right),\widehat{\mu}_{n}+\frac{1}{n}q\left(\frac{\delta}{2h(k_{n})};c_{n},1/2,1\right)\right].

Here
q(\delta^{\prime};N,A,B)
is the Bentkus bound, where
\delta^{\prime}
is the confidence level,
N
is the sample size,
B
is the
almost sure
upper bound of the random variables and
A^{2}
is the variance upper bound. The function
h(k)
is defined as
h(k)=\zeta(1.1)(k+1)^{1.1}
, where
\zeta(\cdot)
is the Riemann-zeta function. The integer
k_{n}
and the real
c_{n}
are defined as
k_{n}=\min\{k\in\mathbb{N}:\lceil\eta^{k}\rceil\leqslant n\leqslant\lfloor\eta^{k+1}\rfloor\}
and
c_{n}=\lfloor\eta^{k_{n}+1}\rfloor
, with
\eta=1.1
. The referenced theorem is stated with an empirical overestimate of the standard deviation instead of the fixed bound
1/2
, which would lead to replace the
\delta/2
present in the confidence set with
\delta/3
by union bound; we found this step to be of negligible, and even slightly detrimental impact, in the case of Bernoulli distributions.

Hedged Capital martingale method

Waudby-Smith and Ramdas 2023
present a nonnegative martingale construction from observations
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
bounded in
[0,1]
. Following the recommendations of the authors, we define the following quantities:

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{n}(m)=\max(\mathcal{K}_{n}^{+},\mathcal{K}_{n}^{-}(m)),

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{n}^{\pm}(m)=\prod_{k=1}^{n}\left(1\pm\lambda_{k}^{\pm}(m)\left(X_{k}-m\right)\right),

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}^{+}(m)=\min\left(\lvert\lambda_{k}\rvert,\frac{1/2}{m}\right),\lambda_{k}^{-}(m)=\min\left(\lvert\lambda_{k}\rvert,\frac{1/2}{1-m}\right),

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}=\sqrt{\frac{2\log(2/\delta)}{\widehat{\sigma}_{k-1}^{2}k\log(k+1)}},

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}_{k}^{2}=\frac{1/4+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(X_{i}-\widehat{\mu}_{i}\right)^{2}}{k+1},

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}_{k}=\frac{1/2+\sum_{i=1}^{k}X_{i}}{k+1}.

Then, it holds that
\mathbb{P}\left(\forall n\in\mathbb{N},\mu\in\Theta^{\text{HC}}_{n}(\delta)\right)\geqslant 1-\delta
, where

\Theta^{\text{HC}}_{n}(\delta)=\left\{m\in[0,1]:\mathcal{K}_{n}(m)<1/\delta\right\}
.

D.6
Key Observations

On the studied examples, confidence intervals based on time-uniform Bregman concentration are either comparable with state-of-the art methods for the corresponding setting or result in sharper bounds, especially for small sample sizes (lower bounds for Bernoulli compared to Hedged Capital, upper bound for Exponential compared to Kaufmann-Koolen). In particular, due to the formulation in terms of Bregman divergence, our intervals are naturally asymmetric when the underlying distribution is, and respect the support constraints (for instance Bernoulli Bregman bounds are in
[0,1]
without the need for clipping). Moreover, we also provide bounds in novel settings for which, to the best of our knowledge, time-uniform confidence sets are lacking (Chi-square, Poisson, Weibull, Pareto, mean-variance for Gaussian).

Appendix E
Application: Generalized Likelihood Ratio Test in Exponential Families

In this section, we apply our result to revisit GLR tests in exponential families.
The Generalized Likelihood Ratio (GLR) in the exponential family model
\mathcal{E}
writes a follows

\displaystyle G^{\mathcal{E}}_{1:s:t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log\bigg(\frac{\sup_{\theta_{1},\theta_{2}}\prod_{t^{\prime}=1}^{s}p_{\theta_{1}}(X_{t^{\prime}})\prod_{t^{\prime}=s+1}^{t}p_{\theta_{2}}(X_{t^{\prime}})}{\sup_{\theta}\prod_{t^{\prime}=1}^{t}p_{\theta}(X_{t^{\prime}})}\bigg)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta_{1},\theta_{2}}\sum_{t^{\prime}=1}^{s}\log p_{\theta_{1}}(X_{t^{\prime}})+\sum_{t^{\prime}=s+1}^{t}\log p_{\theta_{2}}(X_{t^{\prime}})-\sup_{\theta}\sum_{t^{\prime}=1}^{t}\log p_{\theta}(X_{t^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s\mathcal{L}^{\star}(F_{1:s})+(t-s)\mathcal{L}^{\star}(F_{s+1:t})-t\mathcal{L}^{\star}(F_{1:t})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s(\mathcal{L}^{\star}(F_{1:s})-\mathcal{L}^{\star}(F_{1:t}))+(t-s)(\mathcal{L}^{\star}(F_{s+1:t})-\mathcal{L}^{\star}(F_{1:t}))\,,

where
F_{s+1:t}=\frac{1}{t-s}\sum_{t^{\prime}=s+1}^{t}F(X_{t^{\prime}})
, and it makes sense to introduce
\theta_{s+1:t}
such that
\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta_{s+1:t})=F_{s+1:t}
.
The GLR test is then defined, for a threshold
\alpha
as

\displaystyle\tau(\alpha;\mathcal{E})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min\{t\in\mathbb{N}:\max_{s\in[0,t)}G^{\mathcal{E}}_{1:s:t}\geqslant\alpha\}

An alternative formulation of the GLR shows that

\displaystyle G^{\mathcal{E}}_{1:s:t}

\displaystyle=\inf_{\theta}\sup_{\theta_{1},\theta_{2}}\log\bigg(\frac{\prod_{t^{\prime}=1}^{s}p_{\theta_{1}}(X_{t^{\prime}})\prod_{t^{\prime}=s+1}^{t}p_{\theta_{2}}(X_{t^{\prime}})}{\prod_{t^{\prime}=1}^{t}p_{\theta}(X_{t^{\prime}})}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\inf_{\theta}\sup_{\theta_{1},\theta_{2}}s\Big(\langle\theta_{1},F_{1:s}\rangle-\mathcal{L}(\theta_{1})-\langle\theta,F_{1:t}\rangle+\mathcal{L}(\theta)\Big)

\displaystyle\quad+(t-s)\Big(\langle\theta_{2},F_{s+1:t}\rangle-\mathcal{L}(\theta_{2})-\langle\theta,F_{1:t}\rangle+\mathcal{L}(\theta)\Big)\,.

The first supremum is obtained for
\theta_{1}=[\nabla\mathcal{L}]^{-1}(F_{1:s})
, with value equal to

\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta,\theta_{1})+\langle\theta-\theta_{1},\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta_{1})\rangle+\langle\theta_{1},F_{1:s}\rangle+\langle\theta_{1},F_{1_{t}}\rangle
, that is
\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta,\theta_{1:s})+\langle\theta,F_{1:s}-F_{1:t}\rangle
.
Likewise, the the second supremum is obtained for

\theta_{2}=[\nabla\mathcal{L}]^{-1}(F_{s+1:t})
, with value equal to

\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta,\theta_{s+1:t})+\langle\theta,F_{s+1:t}-F_{1:t}\rangle
.
Combining these two results, and remarking that
s(F_{1:s}-F_{1:t})+(t-s)(F_{s+1:t}-F_{1:t})=0
, we obtain that

\displaystyle G^{\mathcal{E}}_{1:s:t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf_{\theta}s\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta,\theta_{1:s})+(t-s)\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta,\theta_{s+1:t})

(15)

We further note that a solution to this optimization problem
is obtained for
\theta
such that

s(\nabla L(\theta)-\nabla L(\theta_{1:s}))+(t-s)(\nabla L(\theta)-\nabla L(\theta_{s+1:t}))=0
. Reorganizing the terms, this entails that

\nabla L(\theta)=\frac{1}{t}(sF_{1:s}+(t-s)F_{s+1:t})=F_{1:t}
,
and thus (without surprise)
\theta=\theta_{1:t}
.

Written in the form (
15
), the GLR satisfies for any
\theta^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle G^{\mathcal{E}}_{1:s:t}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle s\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\prime},\theta_{1:s})+(t-s)\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta^{\prime},\theta_{s+1:t})\,.

In particular, the false alarm rate of the GLR test, when all observations are generated from parameter
\theta
, can be bounded using

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\theta}(\tau(\alpha;\mathcal{E})<\infty)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\big(\exists(t,s)\in\mathbb{N}^{2},s<t:G^{\mathcal{E}}_{1:s:t}\geqslant\alpha\big)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\Big(\exists(t,s)\in\mathbb{N}^{2},s<t:s\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta,\theta_{1:s})+(t-s)\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta,\theta_{s+1:t})\geqslant\alpha\Big)\,.

At this point, provided that for appropriate terms
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\Big(\exists s\in\mathbb{N}:s\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta,\theta_{1:s})\geqslant\alpha_{2}\Big)\leqslant\frac{\delta}{2}\,,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\Big(\exists(t,s)\in\mathbb{N}^{2},s<t:(t-s)\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta,\theta_{s+1:t})\geqslant\alpha_{1}\Big)\leqslant\frac{\delta}{2}\,.

we deduce by a simple union bound argument, and for
\alpha=\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}
that
\mathbb{P}_{\theta}(\tau(\alpha:\mathcal{E})<\infty)\leqslant\delta
.

A regularized GLR test

Our concentration result shows that such a controlled can be obtained for a penalized version of the maximum likelihood parameter estimates
\theta_{1:s},\theta_{s+1,t},\theta_{1,t}
. Namely, let us consider the set

\displaystyle\Theta_{s,c}(\delta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigg\{\theta\in\Theta:\exists s\in\mathbb{N}:s\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta,\theta_{1:s,c}(\theta))\geqslant\alpha_{1}\bigg\}\,,

\displaystyle\Theta_{s+1:t,c}(\delta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigg\{\theta\in\Theta:\exists(t,s)\in\mathbb{N}^{2},s<t:(t-s)\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta,\theta_{s+1:t,c}(\theta))\geqslant\alpha_{2}\bigg\}\,.

Provided that
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
are defined to ensure that these are high-probability confidence sets for the parameter
\theta_{0}
, then they should have non-empty intersections under
\mathbb{P}_{\theta}
. This motivates the following definition of the regularized GLR-test:

\displaystyle\tau^{\delta}_{c}(\mathcal{E})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min\left\{t\in\mathbb{N}:\exists s\!<\!t,\Theta_{s,c}(\delta/2)\cap\Theta_{s+1:t,c}(\delta/2)=\emptyset\right\}\,.

The appropriate quantity for
\alpha_{1}
is immediately obtained by Theorem
3
as
\frac{s}{s+c}(\log(1/\delta)+\gamma_{1:s,c}(\theta))
.
Now for
\alpha_{2}
, we need to study doubly-time uniform concentration inequalities over both
s
and
t
.

E.1
Doubly-time uniform concentration inequalities for exponential families

See
5

Proof of Theorem
5
:

We consider that all the
(X_{t^{\prime}})_{t^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}}
come from a distribution with same parameter
\theta
.
Let
t\in\mathbb{N}
and
s\in\{0,\dots,t-1\}
.
We introduce the scan-mean
\mu_{s+1:t}=\frac{1}{t-s}\sum_{t^{\prime}=s+1}^{t}F(X_{t^{\prime}})
and its mean
\mu=\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)
. We define as in the proof of Theorem
3
,
for each
\lambda
the martingale

\displaystyle M_{s+1:t}^{\lambda}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\bigg(\langle\lambda,(t-s)(\mu_{s+1:t}-\mu)\rangle-(t-s)\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L},\theta}(\lambda)\bigg)\,.

Then, applying the method of mixture and replacing each term with its scan version from
s+1
to
t
, we introduce the quantity

\displaystyle M_{s+1,t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\big((t\!-\!s\!+\!c)\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L},\theta}^{\star}(x)\big)\frac{G(\theta,c)}{G(\theta_{s+1:t,c}(\theta_{0}),t-s+c)}

where
x=\frac{t\!-\!s}{t\!-\!s\!+\!c}(\mu_{s+1:t}-\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta))
and
\theta_{s+1:t,c}(\theta)
is the regularized estimate from the scan samples

\displaystyle\theta_{s+1:t,c}(\theta)=(\nabla\mathcal{L})^{-1}\bigg(\frac{\sum_{t^{\prime}=s+1}^{t}F(X_{t^{\prime}})+c\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)}{t\!-\!s\!+\!c}\bigg)\,.

Remarking that
\mathbb{E}[M_{t,t}]\leqslant\mathbb{E}[M_{t+1,t}]=1
and that
(M_{s+1,t})_{s}
is a nonnegative supermartingale, we can now control its doubly-time uniform deviations following the proof of
Maillard 2019b
[Th.3.2, p.58].
More precisely, for any non-decreasing function
g
, it can be shown that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\big[\exists t,\exists s<t:M_{s+1,t}\geqslant\frac{g(t)}{\delta}\big]\leqslant\delta\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\Big[\max_{t}\max_{s<t}\frac{M_{s+1:t}}{g(t)}\Big]\leqslant\delta\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{g(t)}\,.

Rewriting the terms thanks to the duality formulas, yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\bigg[\exists t,\exists s<t:(t\!-\!s\!-\!c)\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta,\theta_{s+1:t,c}(\theta))\geqslant\log\Big(\frac{g(t)}{\delta}\Big)+\gamma_{s+1:t,c}(\theta)\bigg]\leqslant\delta\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{g(t)}\,.

This suggests to set
\alpha_{2}=\frac{t\!-\!s}{t\!-\!s\!-\!c}\left(\log\left(\frac{g(t)}{\delta}\right)+\gamma_{s+1:t,c}(\theta)\right)
in the definition of the regularized GLR test. Putting all terms together, this leads to the definition of the following regularized GLR test, for a given false-detection error probability
\delta\in[0,1]
and regularization parameter
c
,

\displaystyle\Theta_{s,c}(\delta)=\left\{\theta\in\Theta:(s\!+\!c)\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta,\theta_{1:s,c}(\theta))\leqslant\log(1/\delta)+\gamma_{1:s,c}(\theta)\right\}\,,

\displaystyle\Theta_{s+1:t,c}(\delta)=\left\{\theta\in\Theta:(t\!-\!s+\!c)\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}(\theta,\theta_{s+1:t,c}(\theta))\leqslant\log(g(t)/\delta)+\gamma_{s+1:t,c}(\theta)\right\}\,,

\displaystyle\tau^{\delta}_{c}(\mathcal{E})=\min\left\{t\in\mathbb{N}:\exists s\!<\!t,\Theta_{s,c}(\delta/2)\cap\Theta_{s+1:t,c}(\delta/2)=\emptyset\right\}\,.

The factor
g(t)
that inflates the width of the confidence set can be tuned to satisfy
\sum_{t=1}^{+\infty}1/g(t)\leqslant 1
, thus controlling the above deviation probability by at most
\delta
(ideally,
\sum_{t=1}^{+\infty}1/g(t)
should be close to
1
to avoid overinflating the confidence width). A natural choice for this is
g(t)=\kappa(1\!+\!t)\log^{2}(1\!+\!t)
where
\kappa=2.10974\geqslant\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(1\!+\!t)\log^{2}(1\!+\!t)}
(for completeness, we derive in the next section an elementary way to computing suitable values for
\kappa
). By construction, this test is guaranteed to have a false-detection probability controlled by
\delta
(that is,
\mathbb{P}_{\theta}(\tau^{\delta}_{c}(\mathcal{E})<\infty)\leqslant\delta
).

Doubly time-uniform control of supermartingale sequences

For completeness, we reproduce below the derivation from
Maillard 2019b
[Th.3.2, p.58], applied to our setup.
First, let us note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigg(\exists t\!\in\!\mathbb{N}_{\star},\exists s\!\in\![0,t-1],\,\,M_{s+1:t}\geqslant\frac{g(t)}{\delta}\bigg)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigg(\max_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{\star}}\max_{s\in[0,t-1]}\frac{M_{s+1:t}}{g(t)}\geqslant\frac{1}{\delta}\bigg)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\delta\mathbb{E}\bigg[\max_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{\star}}\max_{s\in[0,t-1]}\frac{M_{s+1:t}}{g(t)}\bigg]\,.

Let us also denote
\tau
the random stopping time corresponding to the first occurrence
t
of the event
\max_{s\in[0,t-1]}\frac{M_{s+1:t}}{g(t)}\geqslant\frac{1}{\delta}
.
It is convenient to introduce the quantity

\overline{M}_{t}=\frac{\sum_{s\in\{0,\dots,t-1\}}M_{s+1,t}}{g(t)}
for each
t\in\mathbb{N}_{\star}
. Since each
M_{s+1,t}
and
g(t)
is nonnegative, we first get that for every random stopping time
\tau\in\mathbb{N}_{\star}
, the following inequality holds:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{\max_{s<\tau}M_{s+1,\tau}}{g(\tau)}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\overline{M}_{\tau}\bigg]=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\overline{M}_{1}+\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}(\overline{M}_{t+1}-\overline{M}_{t})\mathbb{I}\{\tau>t\}\bigg]\,.

Furthermore, we note that, conveniently

\displaystyle\overline{M}_{t+1}-\overline{M}_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{M_{t+1,t+1}}{g(t+1)}+\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}\bigg(\frac{M_{s+1,t+1}}{g(t+1)}-\frac{M_{s+1,t}}{g(t)}\bigg)\,.

Next, by assumption, we note that
\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}[M_{s+1,t+1}|\mathcal{F}_{t}]\leqslant M_{s+1,t}}
.
Thus, since
\mathbb{I}\{\tau>t\}\in\mathcal{F}_{t}
, we deduce that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{\max_{s<\tau}M_{s+1,\tau}}{g(\tau)}\bigg]

\displaystyle\quad\leqslant\mathbb{E}\big[\overline{M}_{1}\big]+\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}[M_{t+1,t+1}]}{g(t+1)}+\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\sum_{s<t}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\bigg(\frac{1}{g(t+1)}-\frac{1}{g(t)}\bigg)M_{s+1,t}\mathbb{I}\{\tau>t\}\bigg]

\displaystyle\quad=\mathbb{E}\big[\overline{M}_{1}\big]+\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}[M_{t+1,t+1}]}{g(t+1)}+\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\sum_{s<t}\bigg(\frac{1}{g(t+1)}-\frac{1}{g(t)}\bigg)\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\bigg[M_{s+1,t}\mathbb{I}\{\tau>t\}\bigg]}_{\geqslant 0}\,.

Hence, the assumption that
g
is non-decreasing ensures that the last sum is upper bounded by
0
. Since on the other hand
\mathbb{E}[M_{t+1,t+1}]\leqslant 1
holds for all
t
(and thus
\mathbb{E}\big[\overline{M}_{1}\big]\leqslant 1/g(1)
), we deduce that the following inequality holds:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{\max_{s<\tau}M_{s+1,\tau}}{g(\tau)}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\frac{1}{g(1)}+\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{g(t+1)}=\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{g(t)}\leqslant 1\,.

\hfill\square

E.2
Computing the inflating factor
g(t)

The right-hand side of the doubly time-uniform confidence set of Theorem
5
involves a
\log g(t)/\delta
term instead of
\log 1/\delta
as in Theorem
3
, which is a by-product of the union-like argument used in the proof. Ideally,
g(t)
should grow as slow as possible with
t
to avoid unnecessary looseness in the confidence bound. That is however limited by the constraint
\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}1/g(t)\leqslant 1
, which prohibits the use of a linearly growing
g(t)
(since
\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}1/t
diverges). The choice
g(t)=\kappa(1\!+\!t)\log^{1\!+\!\eta}(1\!+\!t)
for some
\eta>0
and
\kappa>0
guarantees that the series of inverses converges, although its limit is not available in closed-form. However, it is sufficient to set
\kappa
to an upper bound on
\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(1\!+\!t)\log^{1\!+\!\eta}(1\!+\!t)}
. The following elementary lemma explains how to compute a tight value for
\kappa
.

Lemma 12
.

Let
f\colon t\in\mathbb{N}\mapsto(1\!+\!t)\log^{1\!+\!\eta}(1\!+\!t)
and define
S_{p}=\sum_{t=1}^{p}1/f(t)
for
p\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{+\infty\}
. Then
S_{\infty}\leqslant S_{p}+\frac{1}{\eta\log(1+p)^{\eta}}
.

Proof of Lemma
12
:

The function
f
is nondecreasing and positive, therefore for
t\in\mathbb{N}
and
x\in[t,t+1)
, it holds that
1/f(t+1)\leqslant 1/f(x)
. Integrating both terms as functions of
x
over the interval
[t,t+1)
yields
1/f(t+1)\leqslant\int_{t}^{t+1}1/f(x)dx
. Summing starting at
t=p
, we further get the following sum-integral comparison:

S_{\infty}-S_{p}\leqslant\int_{p}^{\infty}\frac{dx}{f(x)}\,.

Finally, note that
\frac{d}{dx}\frac{1}{\eta\log^{\eta}(1+x)}=-\frac{1}{f(x)}
, and thus
\int_{p}^{\infty}\frac{dx}{f(x)}=\frac{1}{\eta\log(1+p)^{\eta}}
.

\hfill\square

This lemma shows that
\kappa=S_{p}+1/\log(1\!+\!p)
for some
p\geqslant 1
(
\eta=1
) is a valid choice for the definition of the inflating factor
g(t)
, and is straightforward to compute numerically. For
p=100
, we obtain
\kappa\lesssim 2.10974
and increasing
p
only changes further digits.

E.3
Experiments on change point detection for Gaussian with unknown variance

We illustrate the change point GLR test above in a numerical experiment as follows. We consider the one-dimensional exponential family of centered Gaussian distributions with unknown variance
\mathcal{E}=\left\{\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}),\sigma>0\right\}
. A sequence of independent random variables
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
is drawn from
X_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{0}^{2})
if
t\leqslant t^{*}
and from
X_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{1}^{2})
if
t>t^{*}
, where
t^{*}=50
and
\sigma_{0}=1
and
\sigma_{1}\in[1,2,3,4]
. Of note, this setting corresponds to an open question in
Maillard 2019a
, which studies GLR tests in sub-Gaussian families, which are adapted to the detection of changing means but not variances. Detection times are reported as
\min(\tau^{\delta}_{c}(\mathcal{E}),T)
with
T=100
, and
\tau^{\delta}_{c}(\mathcal{E})>T
is interpreted as no change being detected. For the doubly time-uniform confidence set in the definition of the regularized GLR test, we use the factor
g(t)=\kappa(1\!+\!t)\log^{2}(1\!+\!t)
with
\kappa=2.10974
.

A practical motivation for this setting is, for instance, the design of maintenance models for equipment: with time, a component of a physical system (mechanical component, measuring instrument…) starts to wear off and while it is still functioning (same output on average), it is more imprecise or less stable (higher output variance); the goal of the maintenance agent is to detect as early as possible such changes of regime in order to replace the failing component before it breaks completely.

In Figure
14
, we report the histograms of detection times across
1000
independent simulations for increasingly abrupt changes of variance (
\sigma_{1}\in\{1,2,3,4\}
). As expected, the distribution of detection times shifts closer to the actual change point
t^{*}
when
\sigma_{1}
increases, i.e., more obvious changes are detected earlier. This is confirmed in Figure
15
, where we report the median and interquartile range of detection times for
\sigma_{1}\in[1,4]
.

We empirically validate in Figure
14(a)
the control of the false positive probability by at most
\delta=5\%
. However, the actual false positive rate appears much lower (
0.2\%
), which is a consequence of the looseness of the union bound involving the factor
g(t)
in the doubly time-uniform confidence set. Following
Maillard 2019a
, a sharper approach would involve a direct concentration result on the pair
\left(\theta_{s,c},\theta_{s+1:t,c}\right)
(i.e., in the terminology of
Maillard 2019a
, a joint bound, as opposed to the current disjoint one). This is a nontrivial result which we leave for future work. Finally, note that the high false negative rate of Figure
14(b)
(27.6%) is an artifact of thresholding the detection time at
T=100
; increasing
T
would enable later detections, thus reducing the number of observed false negatives, at the cost of increasing the average detection delay (as well as the computational burden of the experiment).

Appendix F
Application: Linear Contextual Bandits

In this section, we apply Theorem
7
to build confidence sets in the well-known linear bandit setting.
We consider the setting of linear contextual bandits
(
Auer et al. 2002
;
Abbasi-Yadkori et al. 2011
)
, but with possibly arm-dependent noise variance (heteroscedastic noise). An algorithm for this problem chooses, at each round
t\in\mathbb{N}
, an action or arm
A_{t}\!\in\!\mathcal{A}
, and subsequently observes a reward
X_{t}=\theta^{\top}\varphi(A_{t})+\varepsilon_{t}
, where
\varphi\!:\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a (fixed) map from actions to their context vectors,
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a vector of weights unknown to the algorithm and given the action
A_{t}
,
\varepsilon_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{A_{t}}^{2}I)
is a Gaussian noise with known variance
\sigma^{2}_{A_{t}}
. The goal is to select actions to maximize the expected cumulative reward
\sum_{t}\theta^{\top}\varphi(A_{t})
. Any rational agent would choose the action
A_{t}
causally depending upon the history of arms and reward sequences available before the start of round
t
. Naturally, for each
n\!\in\!\mathbb{N}
, it boils down to controlling the deviation between the unknown parameter
\theta
and a suitable estimate
\theta_{n}
built from
n
observations
(A_{t},X_{t})_{t\leqslant n}
.

Under this observation model, the feature function and the log-partition function are given by
F_{t}(x)=\frac{x}{\sigma_{A_{t}}^{2}}\varphi(A_{t})
and
\mathcal{L}_{t}(\theta)=\frac{1}{2\sigma_{A_{t}}^{2}}\|\theta\|^{2}_{\varphi(A_{t})\varphi(A_{t})^{\top}}
. Let us define the matrix

\displaystyle V_{n}=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\sigma_{A_{t}}^{2}}\varphi(A_{t})\varphi(A_{t})^{\top}\,,

and introduce the Legendre function
\mathcal{L}_{0}(\theta)=\frac{1}{2}\|\theta\|^{2}_{V_{0}}
, where
V_{0}
is some fixed positive definite matrix. Then, the parameter estimate and the Bregman information gain take the form

\displaystyle\theta_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}}=\left(V_{n}+V_{0}\right)^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\frac{X_{t}}{\sigma_{A_{t}}^{2}}\varphi(A_{t})~,\quad\gamma_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}}=\log\frac{\det(V_{0}+V_{n})^{1/2}}{\det(V_{0})^{1/2}}~.

Now, defining
\mathcal{H}_{t-1}:=\{A_{1},X_{1},\ldots,A_{t-1},X_{t-1},A_{t}\}
to be the set of all information available before observing
X_{t}
, one can see that the confidence set
\Theta_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}}(\delta)
given by Theorem
7
is the set of all
\theta\in\Theta
satisfying

\displaystyle\|\theta-\theta_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}}\|^{2}_{V_{n}+V_{0}}\leqslant\|\theta\|^{2}_{V_{0}}+2\log\frac{\det(V_{0}+V_{n})^{1/2}}{\delta\det(V_{0})^{1/2}}.

Comparison with Existing Results

Under the assumption that the noise
\varepsilon_{t}
is
\sigma_{A_{t}}
-sub-Gaussian conditioned on
\mathcal{H}_{t-1}
,
Abbasi-Yadkori et al. 2011
;
Kirschner and Krause 2018
obtain a high-probability confidence set
\widetilde{\Theta}_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}}(\delta)
, which is the set of all
\theta\in\Theta
satisfying

\displaystyle\|\theta-\theta_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}}\|_{V_{n}+V_{0}}\leqslant\|\theta\|_{V_{0}}+\sqrt{2\log\frac{\det(V_{0}+V_{n})^{1/2}}{\delta\det(V_{0})^{1/2}}}.

Interestingly, since
\sqrt{a+b}\leqslant\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{b}
for positive
a,b
, it is then clear that
\Theta_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}}(\delta)\subset\widetilde{\Theta}_{n,\mathcal{L}_{0}}(\delta)
. In particular, as a side result of our approach, we obtain a tighter confidence set compared to them.
