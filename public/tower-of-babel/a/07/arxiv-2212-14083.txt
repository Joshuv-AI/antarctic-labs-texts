Determining the primes of bad reduction of CM curves of genus 3 3
Sorina Ionica, Pınar Kılıçer, Kristin Lauter, Elisa Lorenzo García, Adelina Mânzăţeanu, Christelle Vincent

Abstract
. In this paper we introduce a new problem called the Isogenous Embedding Problem (IEP). The existence of solutions to this problem is related to the primes of bad reduction of CM curves of genus 3 3 and we can detect potentially good reduction in absence of solutions. We propose an algorithm for computing the solutions to the IEP and run the algorithm through different families of curves. We were able to prove the reduction type of some particular curves at certain primes that were open cases in [ 29 ] .



1.
Introduction

Let
\mathcal{O}
be an order in an imaginary quadratic field
K
. The
class polynomial
associated to
\mathcal{O}
is defined to be

H_{\mathcal{O}}(t)=\prod_{\{E/\mathbb{C}\text{ has CM by }\mathcal{O}\}/\simeq}(t-j(E)),

that is, the polynomial whose roots are the
j
-invariants of elliptic curves
E
with complex multiplication by
\mathcal{O}
. It is an irreducible polynomial with integer coefficients. Its roots generate a class field of
K
and are used for constructing elliptic curves with a given number of points, which are useful in cryptography
[
7
,
3
,
13
]
. When
\mathcal{O}=\mathcal{O}_{K}
is the maximal order of
K
, the class polynomial
H_{\mathcal{O}_{K}}(t)
is called the

Hilbert class polynomial
and its roots generate the Hilbert class field of
K
.

In order to compute the class polynomial
H_{\mathcal{O}}(t)
, we begin by computing the set of fundamental
\tau\in\mathbb{H}
that correspond to elliptic curves with complex multiplication (CM) by
\mathcal{O}
. These values
\tau\in\mathbb{H}
can be obtained by computing the root with positive imaginary part of each primitive quadratic form of discriminant
\text{disc}(\mathcal{O})
up to equivalence. The elliptic curve corresponding to
\tau
is obtained by evaluating the
j
-invariant as a modular function at
\tau
using its
q
-expansion, which gives an approximate value for the
j
-invariants, and hence the roots of the class polynomial. Since the coefficients of
H_{\mathcal{O}}(t)
are integers, by evaluating the
j
-invariants to enough precision, we can compute the exact values of the coefficients
[
5
,
8
,
41
]
.

This construction, known as the
CM Method
, can be generalized to curves of genus
g
with CM by an order
\mathcal{O}
in a CM field
K
of degree
2g
, that is, a totally imaginary quadratic extension of a totally real number field of degree
g
. The theory of Shimura and Taniyama
[
38
]
tells us how to compute the
Riemann matrix

\tau\in\mathbb{H}_{g}
for each Jacobian with CM by
\mathcal{O}
up to isomorphism. We then need curve invariants which are analogous to the
j
-invariant. These invariants need to be absolute (quotients of equal weight invariants having a power of the discriminant in the denominator so that they are always defined) and modular (given by Siegel modular functions so that we can approximate their values at a given
\tau
). We call these invariants
good invariants
. Given such invariants (see
[
40
, Section 2]
for
g=2
;
[
34
, Section 5]
and
[
30
]
for
g=3
), we may define class polynomials and compute class fields of
K
in a similar fashion. However, in contrast to the dimension
1
case, the coefficients of these polynomials are not integers, but rational numbers. Therefore, efficient computational methods for numerically approximating these polynomials and proving their complexity rely on explicit bounds for the denominators of class polynomials.

f\in\mathbb{Q}[X]
we mean the smallest
D\in\mathbb{Z}

.

For
g=2
, the denominators of class polynomials are products of powers of primes of geometric bad reduction of the corresponding curves. Thanks to the work of Bruinier, Goren, Lauter, Viray, and Yang
[
9
,
14
,
15
,
27
]
, there are explicit formulas for computing these denominators. As a consequence, a bound on the runtime of the algorithm computing the class polynomials in terms of the discriminant of the field
K
is given in
[
40
]
.

In higher genus, several families of curves with CM have been computed: for genus 3 hyperelliptic curves
[
2
,
44
]
; Picard curves
[
1
,
24
,
25
,
26
]
; plane quartics
[
22
]
; superelliptic curves
[
39
]
. Each of these papers lists examples of CM curves, but for most of these examples only a heuristic argument is given that the curves have CM by the correct order. While some of them are proven correct by running an algorithm computing the endomorphism of the Jacobian
[
11
]
, obtaining bounds on the denominators of class polynomials would allow to prove the correctness of the output of these algorithms in general.

In this paper, we turn our attention to the case of genus 3.
First, we note genus 3 curves are either plane quartics or hyperelliptic curves. When all curves with CM by an order in a sextic CM field are plane quartics, or hyperelliptic curves, then one may define class polynomials over
\mathbb{Q}
as in genus 2. For some rare sextic CM fields, called mixed in
[
4
]
, both hyperelliptic curves and plane quartics with CM by that field exist. To work around the problem of handling two different sets of invariants for these curves, one may define class polynomials whose roots are invariants of CM points in the Galois orbit of a certain Galois group
[
12
,
4
]
. CM points on these orbits
are either Jacobians of hyperelliptic curves or of plane quartics and a single set of invariants may be used. To mark the distinction, we will call the polynomials defined in this way
hyperelliptic class polynomials
and
plane quartic class polynomials
.

The primes appearing in the discriminant of a plane quartic, and consequently in the denominators of plane quartic class polynomials,
are either primes of geometric bad reduction or of hyperelliptic reduction of the curve
[
29
]
. It is conjectured in
[
22
, Section 4]
that the primes of hyperelliptic reduction cannot be bounded in terms of the discriminant of the order, which suggests that the CM method used for
g=1,2
cannot be directly generalized to plane quartics. The case of genus 3 hyperelliptic curves with CM is much simpler, since all primes appearing in the denominators are primes of bad reduction
[
18
]
.
In
[
6
]
and
[
23
]
, the authors formulate for genus 3 a problem analogous to the embedding problem studied by Goren and Lauter for genus 2. This allows to establish bounds on the primes of bad reduction, but these bounds are unfortunately too large to be practical.

In this paper, we work towards giving a better bound.
Indeed, we give new better bounds in some particular cases (see Section
5.3
). In the general case, our main contribution is of a slightly different flavour: we give an algorithm which given a sextic CM field outputs a precise short list of primes of bad reduction for the curves with CM by this field (see Section
5.6
).
One of the consequences of these results is that we are able to answer the question about the reduction type of certain plane quartics studied in
[
22
,
29
]
(see Proposition
6.1
).

Outline

In Section
2
, we introduce the objects that allow us to detect bad reduction: the solutions to the REP (Reduction Embedding Problem) and to the IEP (Isogenous Embedding Problem). Those embedding problems are generalizations to the

in genus 2 as defined in
[

. In Section

We run the algorithm for some sextic CM fields.

.

1.2.
Notation and assumptions

Throughout the paper,
K
is a sextic CM field, that is, a totally imaginary quadratic extension of a totally real cubic field
K_{+}
. Thus,
K_{+}={\mathbb{Q}}(\mu)
and
K=K_{+}(\eta)
, where
\eta
is a totally imaginary element in
K
and
\mu=-\eta^{2}
. We denote the maximal orders of
K
and
K_{+}
by
\mathcal{O}_{K}
and
\mathcal{O}_{K_{+}}
, respectively. A generic order of
K
, i.e., a
{\mathbb{Z}}
-module of rank
6
in
\mathcal{O}_{K}
, is denoted by
\mathcal{O}
and we denote
\mathcal{O}_{+}:=\mathcal{O}\cap K_{+}
.

By a
curve
, we mean a smooth, projective,
geometrically irreducible curve. We denote by
X
a curve of genus 3 with CM by
{\mathcal{O}}
and with absolutely simple Jacobian
J:=J(X)
, and by
M
a number field over which
X
and its stable model are defined.

We say that a curve
X
of genus 3 defined over a
field
M
has
complex multiplication
(CM) by
{\mathcal{O}}\subset K

if there exists an embedding

\iota:{\mathcal{O}}\hookrightarrow\End(J_{\overline{M}})
such that
\iota^{-1}(\End(J_{\overline{M}}))={\mathcal{O}}
,
where
J
is the Jacobian of
X
. A
CM type

\Phi
of
K
is
a set of
3
non-conjugate complex embeddings

K\hookrightarrow{\mathbb{C}}
. Given a Jacobian
J/M
with CM by
K
, then there is a unique CM type
\Phi
of
K
such that the analytic representation of
\End_{0}(J_{\overline{M}})
is equivalent to the direct sum representation
\oplus_{\phi\in\Phi}\phi
.
We say that a CM type is
primitive
if its restriction to any strict CM
subfield of
K
is not a CM type. The CM type corresponding to
J

is
primitive
if and only if
J
is
absolutely
simple.

We denote the endomorphism ring of an elliptic curve
E
by
\mathcal{R}
and we define
{\mathcal{B}}:=\mathcal{R}\otimes{\mathbb{Q}}
. In this paper, an elliptic curve is always defined over a finite field
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
, where
q=p^{r}
and
p
is a prime number, so the endomorphism algebra
{\mathcal{B}}
is either an indefinite quaternion algebra or an imaginary quadratic field.
If
E
is supersingular, then
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{B}_{p,\infty}
is the indefinite quaternion algebra only ramified at
p
and
\infty
, and
\mathcal{R}
is a maximal order in
\mathcal{B}
.

2.
Bad reduction and the embedding problem

In this section, we define two different embedding problems and we explain their connection to the primes of (geometric) bad reduction for curves of genus 3 with CM. Recall that the embedding problem for genus
2
introduced in
[
14
, Section 3]
is the main ingredient in
[
14
,
27
]
for determining and controlling the primes and their exponents in the denominators of the coefficients of the Igusa Class Polynomials.

First, we review some results from
[
6
]
and
[
23
]
. We begin with enumerating all reduction types of CM curves of genus 3 modulo a prime ideal of the ring of integers of its field of definition.

Proposition 2.1
.

[
6
, Corollary 4.3]

Let
X
be a genus 3 curve with CM by an order
\mathcal{O}
in a sextic CM field
K
. Let
\mathfrak{p}
be a prime ideal of
\mathcal{O}_{M}
, where
M
is a number field over which
X
and its stable model are defined. Then, one of the following statements holds for the special fiber
X_{\mathfrak{p}}
of the stable reduction of
X
:

(1)

(good reduction)
X_{\mathfrak{p}}
is a smooth curve of genus 3,

(2)

X_{\mathfrak{p}}
has three irreducible components of genus 1,

(3)

X_{\mathfrak{p}}
has an irreducible component of genus 1 and one of genus 2.

If
X
and
\mathfrak{p}
are such that one of the two last cases of Proposition
2.1
holds, then we say that
X
has
bad reduction
at
\mathfrak{p}
. The reader should be aware that what we call bad reduction in this paper is usually called
geometric bad reduction
.

The following result provides a motivation for the first embedding problem that we consider, namely the Reduction Embedding Problem.

Proposition 2.2
.

[
6
, Theorem 4.5 and Proposition 4.8]

Let
X
be a curve of genus
3
defined over a number field
L
with CM by an order
{\mathcal{O}}
in a sextic CM field
K
. Suppose that the Jacobian
J
of
X
is absolutely simple. Let
M
be a finite extension of
L
such that
J
has good reduction everywhere and
X
has the stable model over
\mathcal{O}_{M}
. Let
\mathfrak{p}
be a prime ideal in
\mathcal{O}_{M}
lying above
p\subset{\mathbb{Z}}
.

If
X
has bad reduction modulo
\mathfrak{p}
, then there exists an elliptic curve
E
defined over
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
, a principally polarized abelian surface
A
defined over
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
with a polarization
\lambda_{A}
, and a ring embedding

\iota_{0}:\,\mathcal{O}\hookrightarrow\End(E\times A)

such that the Rosati involution coming from the polarization
1\times\lambda_{A}
induces complex conjugation on
{\mathcal{O}}
. Moreover, in this situation,
A\sim E^{2}
and the embedding can be taken to be optimal, i.e. with
[\iota_{0}^{-1}(\operatorname{End}(E\times A)\cap(\iota_{0}(\mathcal{O})\otimes\mathbb{Q})):\mathcal{O}]
a power of
p
.

Proof.

The proof of this proposition is given in
[
6
]
, but we sketch the argument here to set up the notation. Since the Jacobian
J
of
X/L
has complex multiplication, by Serre–Tate
[
37
]
, there exists an extension
M
of
L
such that
J
has potentially good reduction everywhere. Let
\mathfrak{p}\mid p
be a prime in
\mathcal{O}_{M}
such that
X
has bad reduction modulo
\mathfrak{p}
whereas
J
has good reduction modulo
\mathfrak{p}
. Since
X
has bad reduction, the reduction
J_{\mathfrak{p}}
is decomposable.

By Proposition
2.1
, there are two possibilities for how
J_{\mathfrak{p}}
decomposes; it is either a product of an elliptic curve and an abelian surface or a product of three elliptic curves. In both cases, one may assume that
J_{\mathfrak{p}}\cong E\times A
as principally polarized abelian varieties, where
E
is an elliptic curve and
A
is an abelian surface with a fixed principal polarization
\lambda_{A}
. We note that this includes the case where
A\cong E_{1}\times E_{2}
with the product polarization.

The embedding
\End(J)\hookrightarrow\End(J_{\mathfrak{p}})
gives an embedding
\mathcal{O}\hookrightarrow\End(E\times A)
and
[
6
, Proposition 4.8]
implies the condition on the Rosati involution. Moreover, it follows from
[
6
, Theorem 4.5]
that
A\sim E^{2}
and from
[
15
, Corollary 6.1.2]
that the embedding is optimal.

∎

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
X
be a curve of genus
3
defined over a number field
L
with CM by an order
{\mathcal{O}}
in a sextic CM field
K
. Suppose that the Jacobian
J
of
X
is absolutely simple.
Let
M
be a finite extension of
L
such that the
J
has good reduction everywhere and
X
has a stable model defined over
\mathcal{O}_{M}
.
Let
\mathfrak{p}\nmid 2
be a prime ideal of
\mathcal{O}_{M}
such that
X
has bad reduction at
\mathfrak{p}
. Then
J_{\mathfrak{p}}\sim E^{3}
, where
E
is a supersingular elliptic curve.

Proof.

By Proposition
2.2
, we have
J_{\mathfrak{p}}\sim E^{3}
where
E
is an elliptic curve. Assume that
E
is ordinary. Then,
E
has CM by an order of an imaginary quadratic field
k:={\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{D})
, where
D
is the fundamental discriminant.
By
[
20
, Theorem 2]
, there are ordinary elliptic curves
E_{i}
with CM by
k
such that
E_{i}
is isogenous to
E
and
J_{\mathfrak{p}}\simeq E_{1}\times E_{2}\times E_{3}
. Thus, we have
\text{End}(J_{\mathfrak{p}})\otimes{\mathbb{Q}}\simeq\mathcal{M}_{3\times 3}(k)
and, by
[
10
, Theorem 1.3.1.1]
, the field
k
is isomorphic to the center of
{M}_{3\times 3}(k)
hence isomorphic to a subfield in
K
. Therefore, if
K
does not contain any imaginary quadratic field, then
E
is not ordinary and we get a contradiction. This has already been proven in
[
6
, Proposition 4.6]
.

Now suppose that
K
contains an imaginary quadratic field. Then, the imaginary quadratic field of
K
is isomorphic to
k
. We identify
k
and the imaginary quadratic subfield in
K
. Write
\End(E_{i})=\langle 1,f_{i}\omega\rangle
, where
\omega=\frac{D+\sqrt{D}}{2}
and
f_{i}
is the conductor of
\End(E_{i})
in the maximal order of
k
. Let
f=\operatorname{lcm}(f_{1},f_{2},f_{3})
. Since

is embedded diagonally in
\mathcal{M}_{3\times 3}(k)
, if

, this contradicts the primitivity of

, by
[

. If

and

, then
p\mid f_{i}D
for some

. Write the Frobenius element
\pi_{E_{i}}=a+bf_{i}\omega
. Then,
N(\pi_{E_{i}})=abf_{i}D+a^{2}+b^{2}f_{i}^{2}\frac{D(D-1)}{4}
is a power of

, so

. Hence,
p\mid\Tr(\pi_{E_{i}})=2a+bf_{i}D
, which implies that
E_{i}
is not ordinary. This is a contradiction, so

is supersingular.

∎

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
X
be a curve of genus 3 with CM by the maximal order of a sextic CM field
K
. Let
M
be a number field such that the Jacobian
J
of

has good reduction at every prime of

. Let
{\mathfrak{p}}_{M}\subset\mathcal{O}_{M}
and let

If

p\mathcal{O}_{K}={\mathfrak{p}}_{1}\overline{{\mathfrak{p}}_{1}}{\mathfrak{p}}_{2}\overline{{\mathfrak{p}}_{2}}\quad\text{or}\quad{\mathfrak{p}}_{1}\overline{{\mathfrak{p}}_{1}}{\mathfrak{p}}_{2}{\mathfrak{p}}_{3}\quad\text{or}\quad{\mathfrak{p}}_{1}\overline{{\mathfrak{p}}_{1}}{\mathfrak{p}}_{2}\overline{{\mathfrak{p}}_{2}}{\mathfrak{p}}_{3}\quad\text{or}\quad{\mathfrak{p}}_{1}\overline{{\mathfrak{p}}_{1}}{\mathfrak{p}}_{2}\overline{{\mathfrak{p}}_{2}}{\mathfrak{p}}_{3}\overline{{\mathfrak{p}}_{3}}

. Moreover, if

is a Galois extension, then

and

{\mathfrak{p}}^{3}\overline{{\mathfrak{p}}}^{3}

.

Proof.

Zaytsev
[

proves that the

is different than

.

We now state our first embedding problem for genus 3 CM curves which is a generalization of the embedding problem for genus 2 CM curves given in
[
14
]
.

Problem 2.6
.

(The Reduction Embedding Problem)
Given an order
\mathcal{O}
in a sextic CM field
K
and a prime number
p
, the Reduction Embedding Problem (REP) is to find a supersingular elliptic curve
E
and a principally polarized abelian surface
(A,\lambda_{A})
both defined over
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
, and a ring embedding

\iota_{0}\colon\mathcal{O}\hookrightarrow\End(E\times A)

such that the Rosati involution coming from the product polarization
1\times\lambda_{A}
induces complex conjugation on
{\mathcal{O}}
. We call the tuple
(E\times A,1\times\lambda_{A},\iota_{0})
a solution to the REP for
(\mathcal{O},p)
.

The following corollary is a direct consequence of Proposition
2.2
.

Corollary 2.7
.

With the notation and the assumptions of Proposition
2.2
, a genus
3
curve with CM by
\mathcal{O}\subset K
and a prime
\mathfrak{p}\mid p
, with
p\neq 2
, of bad reduction for
X
produce a solution to the REP.

We will discuss the converse of the corollary in Section
2.1
.

The ideas for the proof of the following proposition are given in
[
23
, Section 3]
, but we reproduce it here for completeness, using our terminology.

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
X
be a curve of genus
3
defined over a number field
L
with CM by an order
{\mathcal{O}}
in a sextic CM field
K
. Suppose that the Jacobian
J
of
X
is absolutely simple. Let
M
be a finite extension of
L
such that
J
has good reduction everywhere and
X
has a stable model over
\mathcal{O}_{M}
.
If

has bad reduction modulo a prime ideal
\mathfrak{p}\subset\mathcal{O}_{M}
, with
\mathfrak{p}\nmid 2
, then there exists a supersingular elliptic curve
E
defined over
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
with endomorphism ring
\mathcal{R}
,
a polarization
\lambda=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
\beta^{\vee}&\gamma\end{pmatrix}
on
E^{2}
where
\alpha,\gamma\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}
,
\beta\in{\mathcal{R}}
,

and a ring embedding

\mathcal{O}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{M}_{3\times 3}(\mathcal{R}/n)

such that the Rosati involution coming from the polarization

induces complex conjugation on
{\mathcal{O}}
and the entries of the first row are in
\mathcal{R}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
X
has bad reduction modulo
\mathfrak{p}\subset\mathcal{O}_{M}
. Then, by Proposition

and Corollary

,
(X,\mathfrak{p})
has a solution to the REP. It is proven in Propositions

where

is a supersingular elliptic curve over
\overline{{\mathbb{F}}}_{p}
. Let

be a fixed isogeny. Then by
[

, Lemma 4.3]
, the polarization
\lambda

\lambda_{A}
on

is as given in the statement, and for

, there is an isogeny
\tilde{s}\colon A\rightarrow E^{2}
be an isogeny such that

. Then, by sending

to
\frac{1}{n}(n\times\tilde{s})\varphi(1\times s)\in\End(E^{3})\otimes{\mathbb{Q}}=\mathcal{M}_{3\times 3}(\mathcal{R}/n)
, we embed

into
\mathcal{M}_{3\times 3}(\mathcal{R}/n)
. Hence, we obtain an embedding

.

It is straightforward to check the Rosati involution condition. The last condition follows from
[

.
∎

Problem 2.10
.

(The Isogenous Embedding Problem)
Given an order
\mathcal{O}
in a sextic CM field
K
and a prime number
p
, the Isogenous Embedding Problem (IEP) is to find a supersingular elliptic curve
E
over
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
with
\End(E)\cong\mathcal{R}
,
a polarization
\lambda=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
\beta^{\vee}&\gamma\end{pmatrix}
on

where
\alpha,\gamma\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}
,
\beta\in{\mathcal{R}}
,

, and a ring embedding

\iota:\mathcal{O}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}_{3\times 3}(\mathcal{R}/n)

such that the Rosati involution coming from the polarization

induces complex conjugation on
{\mathcal{O}}
and the entries of the first row are in
\mathcal{R}
. We call the tuple
(E^{3},1\times\lambda,\iota:\mathcal{O}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}_{3\times 3}(\mathcal{R}/n))

.

Corollary 2.11
.

With the notation and the assumptions of Proposition
2.8
, a genus
3
curve with CM by
\mathcal{O}\subset K
and a prime
\mathfrak{p}\mid p
, with

, of bad reduction produce a solution to the IEP.

We saw that

implies the existence of a solution to the REP and that implies the existence of a solution to the IEP. In order to compute primes of bad reduction for a genus 3 curve with CM by a sextic order
\mathcal{O}
, we compute solutions to the IEP. This will provide a bound for the primes of bad reduction.

2.1.
From a solution to REP to bad reduction

In genus
2
, a solution to the embedding problem produces a curve with bad reduction
[
14
, Theorem 4.2.1]
. Concretely, suppose that there exists an embedding
{\mathcal{O}}\hookrightarrow\End(E^{2})\otimes{\mathbb{Q}}
, where
{\mathcal{O}}
is the ring of integers of a non-biquadratic quartic CM field
K
and
E
is defined over
\overline{{\mathbb{F}}}_{p}
, such that the Rosati involution coming from the product polarization induces complex conjugation on
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
.
Recall that a CM type of a CM field
K
of degree
4
is primitive if and only if
K
does not contain any imaginary quadratic field. Then, there exists a curve
X
of genus 2 over a number field
L
with CM by
{\mathcal{O}}
, whose endomorphisms and the stable model are defined over
{\mathcal{O}}_{L}
, and a prime
{\mathfrak{p}}\subset{\mathcal{O}}_{L}
such that
X
has bad reduction modulo
{\mathfrak{p}}
. In genus 3, bad reduction produces a solution to the REP by Proposition
2.2
. However, this strategy cannot be fully used in genus 3 to prove the converse.

Proposition 2.12
.

Let
K
be a sextic CM field not containing any imaginary quadratic field and
\mathcal{O}\subseteq K
be an order.
Let
(E\times A,1\times\lambda_{A},\iota_{0}:\mathcal{O}\rightarrow\End(E\times A))
be a solution to the REP, then there exists a genus 3 curve with CM by
\mathcal{O}
over a number field having bad reduction at a prime
\mathfrak{p}
dividing
p
.

Proof.

From a solution to the REP there exists (at least) a lift to characteristic zero by
[
14
, Lemma 4.4.1]
. By the Lefschetz principle, this lift can be realized over
\mathbb{C}
and, by the theory of complex multiplication, it is actually defined over a number field. This produces a principally polarized absolutely simple abelian threefold and hence a Jacobian. The corresponding curve has bad reduction at
p
and it produces the REP solution.
∎

When
K
contains an imaginary quadratic field, it admits both primitive and non-primitive CM-types so we cannot conclude that the CM type of the lift is primitive.
Even more, in the case that the CM type is non-primitive we cannot deduce that the polarization of the lift is indecomposable hence we cannot conclude that the lift is a Jacobian.

Another issue that deserves more study is the fact that the lift may not be unique, this is related to the generalization of Gross-Zagier formula on singular moduli measuring for which primes different CM curves have isomorphic reduction, see
[
16
,
28
]
.

5.1.
Bounds for

Using (

Since

Lemma 5.1
.

We have
0<x<\sqrt{A^{2}-2B}
,
0<a\leq\frac{A^{2}-2B-x^{2}}{2}
, and
\gamma\leq a\left(A^{2}-2B-x^{2}-2a\right)

Recall from the text below (
3.3
) that we have
\gamma,a,n>0
.
We first show that

. Suppose

. By (
3.9
), we have

. Thus,
\frac{d}{n}>0
. Then,

. Moreover, from (

By combining (

The bounds in this section are used in

3
. We first remark the following.

.

In this section we will present bounds for the primes of bad reduction for genus 3 curves with CM by an order

(i)

contains

or

(ii)

If

with

and

(iii)

If

where

for a totally positive real

such that

. We take

.

The bounds obtained in this section are not only asymptotically better than the bounds given in
[
6
,
23
]
, but Theorem
5.7
gives also a very short list of primes for case (i). Notice that

in this paper corresponds to what is called

in

]

.
Checking that our bound for case (ii) is (actually much) better is more subtle: in this case, the roots of the sextic polynomial are of the form
\pm\alpha_{1}\sqrt{d}
,
\pm\alpha_{2}\sqrt{d}
and
\pm\alpha_{3}\sqrt{d}
with

, so the bound in
[

]
is

and ours for the same polynomial is

, but in our setting we can directly work with the cubic polynomial defining the

Theorem 5.7

(Case (i))
.

K
be a sextic CM field. Let
\mathcal{O}
be an order in a sextic CM field containing
\zeta_{4}
.

be a genus-3 curve such that its Jacobian is absolutely simple and has CM by

. Let

(lying above rational prime

.
Then we have

, where
n
is a positive integer such that
[n]\ker(s)=0
in Lemma
3.1
. In particular, we have
p\leq(A^{2}-2B)^{3}/2
or
p\mid 2n
.

By Lemma
5.1
, we obtain

n=a\gamma-b^{2}\leq a^{2}\left(A^{2}-2B-x^{2}-2a\right)\leq\frac{\left(A^{2}-2B-x^{2}\right)^{3}}{2}.

Since,

By Corollary
3.14

\iota(\sqrt{-1})
is a diagonal matrix. Then, by Proposition
3.12

. In this case

Theorem 5.10

(Case (ii))
.

Let

where where

is square-free. Let

be a genus-3 curve such that its Jacobian is absolutely simple and has CM by an order

in

{\mathcal{O}}\cap k

Let

(lying above a rational prime

Proof.

By Proposition
2.2
, bad reduction gives a solution to the REP. This gives a solution to the IEP given by
\iota(\mu)=\Lambda_{1}
and

\iota(f\sqrt{D})=\Lambda_{3}
.
The entries of

are rationals and the entries of

\N(d_{3})\leq-\gamma f^{2}D

Therefore, if

5.9
, the quaternions
d_{i}
commute. This implies that
\iota(f\sqrt{D})=\text{diag}(f\sqrt{D},f\sqrt{D},f\sqrt{D})
. Hence, by Proposition
3.12
, we have

. As in Theorem

, we get
p\leq fD\left(A^{2}-2B\right)^{3}/4
. If

, then commutativity of

’s gives a contradiction. Therefore, we obtain that

Following the idea in

Let
