The Ghanaian NLP Landscape: A First Look
Sheriff Issaka, Zhaoyi Zhang, Mihir Heda, Yinka Ajibola

Abstract
Despite comprising one-third of global languages, African languages are critically underrepresented in Artificial Intelligence (AI), threatening linguistic diversity and cultural heritage. Ghanaian languages, in particular, face an alarming decline, with documented extinction and several at risk. This study pioneers a comprehensive survey of Natural Language Processing (NLP) research focused on Ghanaian languages, identifying methodologies, datasets, and techniques employed. Additionally, we create a detailed roadmap outlining challenges, best practices, and future directions, aiming to improve accessibility for researchers. This work serves as a foundational resource for Ghanaian NLP research and underscores the critical need for integrating global linguistic diversity into AI development.



1
Introduction

Underrepresented yet abundant, African languages make up a third of the world’s languages, with over 2,000 distinct tongues. Despite their cultural significance, symbolic meaning, diversity, and history, these languages remain conspicuously underrepresented in Artificial Intelligence (AI). This paucity culminates macro socioeconomic factors, historical events such as colonization and globalization, and local factors such as military conquest and misguided government policies
Childs 2020
. Consequently, a staggering number of African languages – especially those spoken by small speech communities – face endangerment, with up to 10 percent projected to go extinct within a century
UNESCO 2010
;
Childs 2020
. The magnitude of this issue is best illustrated by Batibo’s comprehensive survey of language situations in African countries
Batibo 2005
, which identified 509 African languages as highly endangered, extinct, or on the brink of extinction. Alarmingly, this number may be an underestimation given the lack of complete socio-linguistic information for most African languages and the potential for language shifts to accelerate the extinction rate, putting even more languages at risk of disappearing by the end of the 21st century.

Of the 84 established languages in Ghana, one is extinct, and eight others are either in trouble or dying
Eberhard et al. 2024
. This dire situation underscores the urgent need for action to preserve these languages before they are lost forever. Fortunately, the digital age offers a unique opportunity to combat this decline. By harnessing advanced Natural Language Processing (NLP) techniques, especially Machine Translation (MT), researchers have contributed to preserving and digitizing some Ghanaian languages, ensuring their perpetuity and enhancing their representation in AI systems. However, the current state of research in this domain remains fragmented and limited in scope, with most Ghanaian languages still lacking any digitization or research efforts.

While it is widely acknowledged within the NLP community that African languages, and by extension, Ghanaian languages, face a scarcity of resources and research attention, a comprehensive assessment of this imbalance is conspicuously absent. Previous surveys, such as
Magueresse et al. 2020
;
Haddow et al. 2022
, have offered only high-level insights into the state of African language research in NLP, lacking the granularity needed for region-specific evaluations. This lack of detailed analysis has led to inconsistencies in the reported state of the field and challenges for researchers in identifying the most pressing needs and opportunities for impact. For example, the surveys fail to capture the specific challenges faced by researchers working on Ghanaian languages, such as the lack of standardized orthography and the difficulty in collecting and annotating low-resource language (LRL) data.

In contrast, this study adopts a novel region-specific approach to meticulously examine Ghana’s linguistic landscape. This research endeavors to achieve four primary objectives: (1) provide the first extensive survey of NLP research specific to Ghanaian languages, thereby creating a comprehensive overview of the current state of research in this domain; (2) scrutinize and compare models, metrics, datasets, and techniques utilized in prior studies, aiming to identify strengths and areas for improvement; (3) outline a roadmap of challenges, best practices, and recommendations for future research, aimed at fostering a robust and effective research framework; and (4) contribute to the sparse body of knowledge of LRLs, thereby enriching the broader academic discourse, providing new insight, and paving the way for more inclusive linguistic studies.

3
Ghanaian Languages

Ghana, a West African nation with a population exceeding 33 million, boasts a rich linguistic landscape. While English, a legacy of British colonial rule, is the official language, the country is home to 73 living indigenous languages
Eberhard et al. 2024
. Among these, Asante, Ewe, Fante, Boron, Dagomba, Dangme, Dagarte, Kokomba, Akyem, and Ga stand out as the most widely spoken (Table
1
)
Ghana Statistical Service 2023
.

Akan, consisting of distinct dialects, is the most widely spoken language, with approximately 44% of Ghana’s population being native speakers
Osam 2003
. These dialects can be divided into two main groups: Fante and Twi. The Fante group has several sub-dialects, including Gomua, Ekumfi, Nkusukum, Iguae, Breman, and sometimes Agona. On the other hand, the Twi group consists of two main dialects: Akuapem and Asante. Both dialects are mutually intelligible, used for instructional purposes, and spoken by predominantly southern communities in Ghana. Although Asante Twi enjoys wider usage, Akuapem Twi has historically been recognized as the written literary standard
Schachter and Fromkin 1968
. The two dialects share significant phonological similarities and structural variations, such as morphological redundancies and adherence to the same vowel harmony rules
Schachter and Fromkin 1968
.

Government policies have played a pivotal role in shaping language use in Ghana. The current language policy mandates using the child’s first language (L1) as the medium of instruction from kindergarten through Grade 3, with English taking over as the L1 thereafter
USAID 2020
. However, these L1 indigenous languages are limited to 11 government-sponsored languages: Akuapem Twi, Asanti Twi, Fante, Nzema, Dagaare, Dagbane, Ewe, Dangme, Ga, Gonja, and Kasem. Studies have revealed a relatively low implementation of the mother tongue policy
Awedoba 2009
;
Adika 2012
;
Davis and Agbenyega 2012
, with critics suggesting that the limited selection of languages and the lack of textbooks and qualified teachers often force educators to resort to English as the medium of instruction
Akpanglo-Nartey and Akpanglo-Nartey 2012
;
Owu-Ewie 2017
.

The unintended consequences of Ghana’s language policies have been far-reaching, with certain indigenous languages, such as Akan, gaining prominence at the expense of others. This overshadowing effect has posed a significant threat to the preservation and vitality of less widely spoken languages, rivaling the impact of English as the official language. The media landscape in Ghana has further exacerbated this issue, with Akan, particularly the Twi dialect, dominating 90% of non-English television programming
Akpanglo-Nartey and Akpanglo-Nartey 2012
. This disproportionate representation has not only limited the exposure of other indigenous languages but has also contributed to their marginalization and potential endangerment, highlighting the urgent need for more inclusive and equitable language policies and practices in Ghana.

The combination of these language policies and the dwindling number of fluent elderly speakers in indigenous communities has compelled language enthusiasts to explore alternative ways to engage with these languages. The growing interest in technological solutions to preserve and revitalize endangered African languages stems from the desire to safeguard the vast knowledge and culture embedded within them
Galla 2016
.

4
NLP and MT in LRLs Revitalization

NLP and MT have emerged as the primary technological approaches for revitalizing African languages. NLP, an interdisciplinary field that combines Linguistics, Computer Science, and AI, enables computers to understand and process human language. MT, a subfield of NLP, focuses on translating text or speech from one language to another. The development of MT systems has evolved through four main paradigms: Rule-Based MT (RBMT), Statistical MT (SMT), Hybrid MT (HMT), and Neural MT (NMT).

RBMT relies on grammatical rules and extensive linguistic knowledge, resulting in poor translation quality and requiring significant manual post-editing
Wang et al. 2022a
;
Wang et al. 2022b
. SMT, which uses statistical models to generate translations based on bilingual text analysis
Brown et al. 1990
, faces challenges such as lack of context consideration and reordering issues for distant language pairs
Wang et al. 2022b
. HMT combines SMT and RBMT, leveraging translation memory to improve translation quality
Hunsicker et al. 2012
;
Costa-jussà and Fonollosa 2015
.

NMT, the current state-of-the-art approach, employs Neural Networks to enhance translation accuracy and speed, enabling training and scaling to new languages
Maazouzi et al. 2017
;
Swathi and Jayashree 2020
;
Mohamed et al. 2021
;
Ranathunga et al. 2023
. Recent advancements in Large Language Models (LLMs) have further revolutionized the field, with models like GPT-4
OpenAI et al. 2024
and PaLM
Chowdhery et al. 2022
demonstrating great performance across a wide range of NLP tasks, including MT.

4.1
Pre-training and Fine-tuning

The development of powerful pre-trained language models, such as BERT
Devlin et al. 2019
, GPT
Radford et al. 2018
;
OpenAI et al. 2024
, and T5
Raffel et al. 2020
, has transformed the approach to NLP tasks. These models enable researchers to fine-tune pre-trained models for specific applications rather than building new models from scratch for each task. Pre-training has significantly improved the accuracy of NLP models for various tasks, including language translation, sentiment analysis, and text classification
Doddapaneni et al. 2021
;
Sun et al. 2022
;
Wang et al. 2022b
. This approach has also facilitated the development of NLP applications for LRLs, which often lack sufficient data for training models from scratch. As the technology continues to advance, pre-trained models are expected to become even more powerful, enabling more accurate and nuanced language analysis
Qiu et al. 2020
;
Han et al. 2021
;
Chowdhery et al. 2022
.

4.2
State of NLP in Ghanaian Languages

Like many other African languages, all Ghanaian languages are considered LRLs. LRLs are characterized by limited research attention, minimal digitization, and a lack of privileged status in terms of teaching and resources
Magueresse et al. 2020
;
Afram et al. 2022
. The striking disparity between the linguistic diversity of African languages and their inadequate representation in NLP can be attributed to factors such as the opaqueness of resources and the linguistic complexity of these languages
Orife et al. 2020
.

Despite ongoing efforts to document and digitize Ghanaian languages, particularly Twi, most available datasets are either religious texts or extremely limited in size. Twi, like all indigenous Ghanaian languages, is primarily used colloquially in unofficial settings, resulting in a scarcity of digitized resources despite having millions of native speakers. Consequently, making it a LRL.

The low-resource nature of all Ghanaian languages has far-reaching negative consequences in social, cultural, and financial domains. The lack of resources hinders the global Ghanaian diaspora from learning their languages and threatens the electronic preservation of these languages and their associated cultures
Azunre et al. 2021b
. Additionally, service providers and health workers face communication obstacles when reaching remote areas. The absence of advanced NLP technologies, such as text summarization and classification, also poses threats to cybersecurity solutions that could be deployed to protect critical social and cultural infrastructure
Azunre et al. 2021b
. Moreover, the scarcity of large, clean, and reliable datasets creates a negative feedback loop, hindering the advancement of NLP in the region
Siminyu et al. 2021
.

5
Survey Methodology

This study employs a systematic review methodology to assess the state of NLP research for Ghanaian languages. The survey involves a rigorous protocol for identifying, screening, and analyzing relevant papers from online databases.

5.1
Search Strategy and Data Sources

To identify relevant publications, we searched four major online databases: Google Scholar, Scopus, Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE) Xplore, and Technology Collection. The search terms used were carefully selected to capture the core concepts of this study: NLP, MT, Ghana, Twi, and Language. These terms were combined using boolean operators to ensure comprehensive coverage of the research domain.

5.2
Screening and Eligibility Criteria

Individual researchers screened the initial search results to determine their relevance to the study. Papers focused on NLP or MT applications for Ghanaian languages were considered eligible. The screening process involved examining each paper’s title, abstract, and keywords to assess its fit with the study’s objectives.

5.3
Data Extraction and Synthesis

We extracted key information for papers that met the eligibility criteria, such as the research objectives, methodologies employed, datasets used, and significant findings. The data was synthesized to identify trends, challenges, and opportunities.

6.3
Model Evaluation Metrics

Evaluating the performance of MT models is crucial for assessing their effectiveness and guiding future research. While human evaluation is the gold standard, it is time-consuming and difficult to quantify. To address these challenges, various automatic evaluation metrics have been developed. The reviewed papers primarily employed three metrics: Bilingual Evaluation Understudy (BLEU)
Papineni et al. 2002
, Translation Edit Rate (TER)
Snover et al. 2006
, and Character n-gram F-score (CHRF)
Popović 2015
.

BLEU is a widely used metric that calculates the similarity between candidate and reference translations by computing the precision of n-grams. It is computationally inexpensive, language-independent, and has shown a high correlation with human evaluation.

Conversely, TER measures the minimum number of edits required to transform the candidate translation into the reference, normalized by the average reference length. It involves finding a set of shifts to reduce the number of edits and then computing the minimum edits between the shifted hypothesis and the reference.

CHRF calculates an F-1 score over character n-grams, providing a more nuanced translation quality assessment. It has demonstrated a superior correlation with human evaluations compared to BLEU and TER
Popović 2015
.

7
Discussion and Roadmap

This section discusses the modeling and data collection approaches observed in the reviewed papers, comparing and contrasting their strategies and providing recommendations for future work.

7.1
Model Selection

Transformer and Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) architectures dominate NLP for Ghanaian languages. The Transformer’s multi-head attention mechanism enables parallel processing and state-of-the-art performance across a wide range of applications
Vaswani et al. 2017
. However, training Transformers on limited data can be challenging, especially for LRLs that often lack extensive labeled datasets. Also, transfer learning from high-resource languages may not always capture the linguistic nuances and cultural context of LRLs
Salman et al. 2022
;
Choenni et al. 2023
. Moreover, Transformers require significant computational resources, which can be a limiting factor for LRL researchers
Tay et al. 2022
.

RNNs, including LSTMs, excel at modeling sequence data and retaining context over extended periods. LSTMs address the exploding or vanishing gradient problem
Greff et al. 2017
. However, sequential processing limits parallel processing and increases memory usage, particularly for long sequences
Le et al. 2015
;
Greff et al. 2017
.

Given these constraints, the research community must develop specialized model architectures for LRLs, starting with simpler yet robust pre-trained models. Until then, despite their challenges, we advocate for adopting Transformers due to their performance and ability to transfer knowledge across a broad range of NLP tasks.

7.2
Model Evaluation

Ensuring a robust evaluation process for NLP models in LRLs requires a combination of automatic metrics and human evaluation. Automatic metrics, such as BLEU, TER, and CHRF, provide insights into translation quality aspects like fluency, adequacy, and semantic similarity but should be interpreted cautiously due to the scarcity of high-quality reference translations in LRLs. To address this, we recommend complementing automatic metrics with human evaluation by native speakers and linguists, who can identify subtle errors, biases, and cultural nuances that automatic metrics may overlook. To mitigate the challenges associated with human evaluation, such as financial and time investments and potential annotator biases, we propose the following strategies:
1. Assemble a diverse team of human annotators to minimize dataset biases.
2. Complement human evaluation with crowdsourcing methods to gather feedback from a larger and more diverse group of stakeholders.
3. Develop clear guidelines and quality control mechanisms for human evaluation to ensure consistency and reduce individual biases.
4. Implement a two-stage evaluation process that first uses automatic metrics to identify low-quality translations and potential issues, followed by human annotators refining the translations and providing detailed feedback on quality and cultural appropriateness.
5. Continuously monitor and update the evaluation process based on feedback and advancements in the field.

7.3
Data Collection

The results section demonstrates several data collection approaches for NLP modeling for Ghanaian languages, each with merits and drawbacks.

7.3.1
Religious Texts

Religious texts, particularly the Bible, are our research findings’ most prevalent data source. However, their ancient origins may render some content outdated or misaligned with contemporary perspectives. Historical texts also carry inherent biases, especially against marginalized groups
McCullagh 2000
;
Dev et al. 2022
.

When using religious corpora in LRL research, we recommend the following:

1. Familiarize oneself with the corpus’s historical, cultural, and linguistic nuances to minimize misinterpretation or over-generalization.
2. Augment the religious corpus with contemporary texts or other religious writings to ensure a more holistic and less biased dataset.
3. Implement bias detection and mitigation techniques to avoid propagating historical prejudices.
4. Engage with theologians, linguists, and community representatives for insights and validation of interpretations.
5. Document and communicate the sources, processes, and potential limitations when using large volumes of religious texts.
6. Regularly validate models against real-world scenarios and contemporary language use and establish a mechanism for continuous feedback from users and experts.

7.3.2
Crowdsourcing and Web Scraping

Crowdsourcing and web scraping require more effort than religious corpus collection but offer the potential for a more diverse dataset and increased efficiency. However, assessing data quality and vetting the competence of participants or online resources can be challenging. Additionally, there are logistical and legal issues, such as copyright, terms of use, and ethical concerns when collecting personal or sensitive information without consent.

To mitigate these challenges, we recommend the following:

1. Utilize robust preprocessing techniques, extensive data validation, and comprehensive documentation of data sources.
2. Uphold ethical considerations, including obtaining appropriate consent and ensuring compliance with copyright and terms of use agreements.
3. Implement advanced data-cleaning methodologies for handling missing or conflicting information.
4. Employ strategies for bias detection and mitigation and conduct sensitivity analyses to assess the impact of external motivations or biases among contributors.

7.3.3
Open-Source Data

Open-source data is popular among Ghanaian NLP researchers due to its free availability, accessibility, and the usual presence of parallel corpora. However, LRLs are often underrepresented in open-source datasets, and data quality and consistency may vary.

When working with open-source data for LRLs, we recommend the following:

1. Leverage freely available data to reduce costs and save time, especially if it is already in a parallel corpus format.
2. Foster community collaboration to enhance resource development and ensure ethical usage.
3. Customize and adapt open-source data to suit the specific needs of the target language.
4. Carefully assess data quality, harmonize inconsistent formats, and respect copyright and licensing terms.

7.4
Future Directions

The field of NLP for Ghanaian languages presents numerous opportunities for future research and development. Some promising directions include:

1. Developing specialized pre-training techniques and architectures tailored to the unique characteristics of Ghanaian languages, such as their morphological complexity and tonal systems.
2. Exploring multilingual and cross-lingual approaches to leverage resources from related languages and facilitate transfer learning.
3. Investing in creating high-quality, diverse datasets that cover a wide range of domains and genres, ensuring better representation of contemporary language use.
4. Designing and implementing language-specific evaluation metrics and benchmarks to accurately assess the performance of NLP models for Ghanaian languages.
5. Collaborating with local communities, linguists, and domain experts to incorporate cultural knowledge and ensure NLP technologies’ ethical development and deployment.
6. Investigating the potential of low-resource techniques, such as few-shot learning and unsupervised methods, to mitigate the scarcity of labeled data. LLMs have shown success in few-shot learning, suggesting their potential to power NLP applications for LRLs.
7. Developing user-friendly tools and platforms to facilitate the adoption and use of NLP technologies by non-expert users, such as educators, content creators, and translators.
8. Investigating the societal implications of deploying LLMs for Ghanaian languages, including accessibility, equity, and cultural appropriateness.

8
Conclusion

This study presents the first large-scale, region-specific survey of NLP research dedicated to Ghanaian languages. By analyzing 12 key publications, we reveal the stark underrepresentation of Ghanaian languages (and LRLs in general) within NLP research. Our findings highlight critical challenges and opportunities, paving the way for a more inclusive future in the field. The concrete recommendations outlined in this study, spanning data collection to model evaluation, can significantly boost future research efforts for LRLs. Implementing these recommendations can unlock the vast potential of LRLs, preserve cultural heritage, and empower speakers in the digital age. This comprehensive survey paves the way for more inclusive AI and NLP research, fostering the development of robust, efficient, and culturally sensitive technologies for all languages.
