Radial projection theorems in finite spaces
Ben Lund and Thang Pham and Vu Thi Huong Thu

Abstract
. Motivated by recent results on radial projections and applications to the celebrated Falconer distance problem, we study radial projections in the setting of finite fields. More precisely, we extend results due to Mattila and Orponen (2016), Orponen (2018), and Liu (2020) to finite spaces. In some cases, our results are stronger than the corresponding results in the continuous setting. In particular, we solve the finite field analog of a conjecture due to Liu and Orponen on the exceptional set of radial projections of a set of dimension between d − 2 d-2 and d − 1 d-1 .



1.
Introduction

For any point
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the radial projection
\pi^{y}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{y\}\rightarrow S^{d-1}
is

\pi^{y}(x)=\frac{x-y}{|x-y|}.

For
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the radial projection of
E
from
y
is

\pi^{y}(E)=\{\pi^{y}(x):x\in E\setminus\{y\}\}.

There are many results and open problems related to the general question: What can be said about the radial projections
\{\pi^{y}(E):y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
, given a bound on the Hausdorff dimension
\dim_{H}(E)
of
E
?

Here we consider finite analogs to various results on this general question.
Over a general field
\mathbb{F}
, for a given point
y\in\mathbb{F}^{d}
, the radial projection
\pi^{y}
from
y
maps a point
x\neq y
to the line that contains
x
and
y
.
Usually we work over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, and in this setting the standard analog to
\dim_{H}(E)
is
\log_{q}(|E|)
.

One reason to be interested in finite analogs is that working in the finite field model can help to understand the behavior of the corresponding problems in the continuous setting.
A second reason that we are interested in radial projections of point sets is because of a connection to the Falconer distance problem.
Recent work on the Falconer distance problem
[
6
,
7
]
relies on a radial projection theorem due to Orponen
[
21
]
.
We hope that a better understanding of radial projections of finite point sets may also lead to progress on finite versions of the Falconer distance problem - see the last paragraph for a more detailed discussion.

Mattila and Orponen
[
16
]
proved that for any Borel set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, if
\dim_{H}(E)>d-1
, then

(1)

\dim_{H}\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\colon\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\pi^{y}(E))=0\}\leq 2(d-1)-\dim_{H}(E),

where
\mathcal{H}^{d}
denotes the
d
-dimensional Hausdorff measure.
Orponen showed
[
21
]
that this is sharp in the sense that for any
\alpha\in(d-1,d]
, there exists a Borel set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
of Hausdorff dimension
\alpha
such that
\dim_{H}\{y:\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\pi^{y}(E))=0\}=2(d-1)-\alpha
.

Our first result is an analogous bound for sets in finite space.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
E\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
and let
M
be a positive integer, with
|E|\geq 6q^{d-1}
and
M\leq 4^{-1}q^{d-1}
.
Then,

(2)

\#\{y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}\colon|\pi^{y}(E)|\leq M\}<12q^{d-1}M|E|^{-1}.

This is stronger than the most natural finite analog to (
1
) in the case that
M
is much smaller than
q^{d-1}
, and suggests the following strengthening of (
1
).

Conjecture 1.2
.

Let
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a Borel set with
\dim_{H}(E)>d-1
, and let
s\leq d-1
be a real number.
Then,

\dim_{H}\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\dim_{H}\pi^{y}(E)<s\}\leq d-1+s-\dim_{H}(E).

Liu
[
13
]
proved that for any Borel set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\dim_{H}(E)\leq d-1
,

(3)

\dim_{H}\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\colon\dim_{H}\pi^{y}(E)<\dim_{H}(E)\}\leq\min(2(d-1)-\dim_{H}(E),\dim_{H}(E)+1).

Liu and Orponen
[
13
]
further suggested the following, stronger conjecture.

Conjecture 1.3
.

Let
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a Borel set with
\dim_{H}(E)\in(k-1,k]
for
k\in\{0,1,\ldots,d-1\}
. Then

\dim_{H}\left\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\colon\dim_{H}\pi^{y}(E)<\dim_{H}(E)\right\}\leq k.

We prove the natural finite field analog to Conjecture
1.3
for
|E|\geq q^{d-2}
.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
E\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
with
|E|\leq 100^{-1}q^{d-1}
.
Then,

(4)

\#\{y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}:|\pi^{y}(E)|\leq 10^{-1}|E|\}<8q^{d-1}.

For
|E|<q^{d-2}
, we prove the following bound, which is slightly stronger than the natural analog to (
3
) over the corresponding range.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
E\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
and let
1<C<|E|
.
Then,

(5)

\#\{y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}:|\pi^{y}(E)|<C^{-1}|E|\}<(C-1)^{-1}q|E|.

We do not believe that Theorem
1.5
is sharp, and offer the following conjecture, which is a natural finite analog to Conjecture
1.3
.

Conjecture 1.6
.

Let
E\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
with
q^{k-1}<|E|\leq q^{k}
for
k\in\{1,\ldots,d-1\}
.
Then,

\#\{y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}:|\pi^{y}(E)|<10^{-1}|E|\}\leq 10q^{k}.

If true, then Conjectures
1.3
and
1.6
would be best possible, up to constant factors.
Indeed, if
E
is contained in a translate of a
k
-dimensional subspace
F
, then
\dim_{H}\pi^{y}(E)\leq k-1
for every
y\in F
.
However, in some cases it is possible to obtain stronger conclusions by assuming that
E
is not nearly contained in a translate of a low-dimensional subspace.
For example, Orponen showed that, if
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a Borel set such that
\dim_{H}(E\setminus\ell)=\dim_{H}(E)
for each line
\ell
, then

(6)

\dim_{H}\left\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\colon\dim_{H}\pi^{y}(E)<\frac{\dim_{H}(E)}{2}\right\}=0.

As a consequence of this result, when
\dim_{H}(E)>0
and
\dim_{H}(E\setminus\ell)=\dim_{H}(E)
for each line
\ell
, there exists
y\in E
such that
\dim_{H}\pi^{y}(E)>\frac{\dim_{H}(E)}{2}
.
Several improvements have been obtained in
[
14
,
26
,
27
]
.
The most recent, due to Shmerkin and Wang
[
27
]
, states that for a fixed
k\in\{1,\ldots,d-1\}
,
d\geq 2
, and a Borel set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\dim_{H}(E)=s\in(k-1/k-\eta(d),k]
, where
\eta(d)
is a small positive constant satisfying
\eta(1)=0
, then

(7)

\sup_{y\in E}\dim_{H}\pi^{y}(E)\geq k-1+\phi(s-k+1),~~\phi(u)=\frac{u}{2}+\frac{u^{2}}{2(2+\sqrt{u^{2}+4})},

under the condition that
E
is not contained in any
k
-plane. Hence, with
d=2
and
k=1
, one has there exists
y\in E
such that

(8)

\dim_{H}\pi^{y}(E)\geq\phi(s)>s/2.

The following finite analog to (
6
) has a very simple proof, using only the fact that there is a unique line through any pair of points.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
\mathbb{F}
be an arbitrary field, and let
E\subset\mathbb{F}^{d}
be a finite set of points such that no line contains more than
(3/4)|E|
points of
E
.
Then,

(9)

\#\{y\in\mathbb{F}^{d}:|\pi^{y}(E)|<2^{-1}|E|^{1/2}\}\leq 1.

In a general setting, we have the next theorem.

Theorem 1.8
.

Let
\mathbb{F}
be an arbitrary field and
E
be a set in
\mathbb{F}^{d}
such that no line contains more than
|E|/2
points of
E
. For
M<|E|/4
, we have

\#\{y\in\mathbb{F}^{d}:|\pi^{y}(E)|<M\}<4M^{2}.

This theorem is essentially sharp up to constant factors. For example, suppose that
\mathbb{F}_{q}
has a subfield of order
p
, and that
E\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}
is the set of points in a subplane isomorphic to
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{2}
.
Then,
|\pi^{y}(E)|=p+1>|E|^{1/2}
for every point
y\in E
.

We can reasonably hope to improve Theorem
1.8
under the additional assumption that
\mathbb{F}
does not have a subfield of suitable size.
We obtain some such improvements using incidence bounds, in particular the Szemerédi-Trotter theorem in the real plane
[
29
]
and an analog proved by Stevens and de Zeeuw for planes over fields of finite characteristic
[
28
]
.

Theorem 1.9
.

There exist constants
0<c_{1},c_{2}<1
such that the following holds.
For any finite
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
|\ell\cap E|<c_{1}|E|
for each line
\ell
and
M\leq c_{2}|E|
, we have

\#\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:|\pi^{y}(E)|<M\}<O(M^{2}|E|^{-1}).

One corollary to Theorem
1.9
(in the spirit of (
8
)) is that, for any finite set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
with no more than
c_{1}|E|
points contained in any line, there is a point
y\in E
such that
|\pi^{y}(E)|=O(|E|)
.

Theorem 1.10
.

There exists a constant
0<c<1
such that the following holds.
Let
p
be prime.
For any set
E\subset\mathbb{F}_{p}^{2}
with
|E|\ll p^{8/5}
and
|\ell\cap E|<c|E|
for each line
\ell
and
M\leq c^{11/7}|E|^{4/7}
, we have

\#\{y\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{2}:|\pi^{y}(E)|<M\}<O(M^{11/4}|E|^{-1}).

We conjecture that the conclusion of Theorem
1.9
holds for sets
E\subset\mathbb{F}_{p}^{2}
with
|E|\ll p
and no more than
c|E|
points in any line.

The Falconer distance problem:
Another motivation of this work that we want to emphasize here is a connection between this topic and the Falconer distance problem, which is one of central problems in Geometric Measure Theory. Given a compact set
E
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the Falconer distance conjecture states that if the Hausdorff dimension of
E
is greater than
d/2
, then its distance set has positive Lebesgue measure. The recent breakthrough of Guth, Iosevich, Ou, and Wang
[
7
]
says that in two dimensions the conclusion holds when
\dim_{H}(E)>5/4
. In higher dimensions, the best current dimensional thresholds are as follows:

•

d=3
, Du, Guth, Ou, Wang, Wilson, and Zhang
[
4
]
(2017):
\frac{3}{2}+\frac{3}{10}

•

d\geq 4
even, Du, Iosevich, Ou, Wang, and Zhang
[
6
]
(2020):
\frac{d}{2}+\frac{1}{4}

•

d\geq 5
odd, Du and Zhang
[
5
]
(2018):
\frac{d}{2}+\frac{d}{4d-2}

The finite field variant of this problem is known as the Erdős-Falconer distance problem, which asks for the smallest exponent
\alpha
such that for any
E\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
, if
|E|\geq q^{\alpha}
, then the distance set covers a positive proportion of all distances. In 2005, Iosevich and Rudnev
[
11
]
showed that if
|E|\gg q^{\frac{d+1}{2}}
,
E
determines a positive proportion of all distances. Unlike the continuous version, the exponent
\frac{d+1}{2}
has been proved to be sharp in
[
10
]
except for even dimensions or dimensions
d\equiv 3\mod 4
with
q\equiv 3\mod 4
.
For these cases, the conjectured exponent is
d/2
, which is in line with the Falconer distance conjecture. Compared to the continuous version, only a few improvements has been able to make during the last 15 years. In particular, Chapman, Erdogan, Hart, Iosevich, and Koh
[
3
]
proved the exponent
\frac{4}{3}
over arbitrary finite fields, which was recently improved to
\frac{5}{4}
over prime fields by Murphy, Petridis, Pham, Rudnev, and Stevens
[
17
]
. However, in higher even dimensions, we know nothing except the exponent
(d+1)/2
. Since one of the key steps in the papers
[
6
,
7
]
is a radial projection theorem due to Orponen
[
20
]
, if one wishes to adapt the methods from
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
, then a full understanding about the radial projection over finite fields is needed. Thus, the results in this paper provide some new progress in this direction.

An update on Conjectures
1.2
and
1.3
:
In a very recent paper
[
22
]
, Orponen and Shmerkin proved that Conjecture
1.2
and Conjecture
1.3
hold in two dimensions. We refer the reader to their paper for more details and discussions.

2.
Notation and definitions

Here we fix notation and definitions that we use in several proofs.

For
E\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
and an integer
M>0
, let

T:=\{y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}:|\pi^{y}(E|\leq M\}.

For any line
\ell
, let
e(\ell)=|\ell\cap E|
, let
t(\ell)=|\ell\cap T|
, and let
\ell(x)
be the indicator function for
x\in\ell
.
Let
L
be the set of lines that each contain at least one point of
T
and at least one point of
E
, and let
G
be the set of all lines in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
. We also use the notation
\binom{d}{1}_{q}
, which is defined by

\binom{d}{1}_{q}:=\frac{q^{d}-1}{q-1}.

Note that
\binom{d}{1}
is the number of lines in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
that are incident to a specified point.

3.
Proofs of Theorem
1.5
, Theorem
1.7
, and Theorem
1.8

The proofs in this section use the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality together with the fact that there is a unique line through each pair of distinct points.

The following lemma immediately implies Theorem
1.5
, and plays an important role in the proof of Theorem
1.8
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
E\subset\mathbb{F}^{d}
be a finite set of points, let
1<C<|E|
, let
k
be a positive integer, and let
T
be a set of points such that

•

for each point
y\in T
, we have
|\pi^{y}(E)|<|E|C^{-1}
, and

•

for any line
\ell
, we have
|\ell\cap T|<k
.

Then,

|T|\leq(C-1)^{-1}k|E|.

Proof.

The strategy is to find upper and lower bounds on the number of triples in

R:=\{(y,x_{1},x_{2})\in T\times E\times E:y,x_{1},x_{2}\text{ are co-linear and }y\notin\{x_{1},x_{2}\}\}.

Denote by
M=|E|C^{-1}
the assumed upper bound on
\pi^{y}(E)
for
y\in T
.
To obtain a lower bound on
|R|
, we bound the number of triples in
R
that contain a fixed
y\in T
:

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell:y\in\ell\end{subarray}}(e(\ell)-\mathbf{1}_{y\in E})^{2}\geq M^{-1}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell:y\in\ell\end{subarray}}(e(\ell)-\mathbf{1}_{y\in E})\right)^{2}\geq M^{-1}(|E|-M\cdot\mathbf{1}_{y\in E})^{2}.

Summing over all
y\in T
:

|R|=\sum_{y\in T}\sum_{\ell:y\in\ell}(e(\ell)-\mathbf{1}_{y\in E})^{2}\geq\sum_{y\in T}M^{-1}(|E|-M\cdot\mathbf{1}_{y\in E})^{2}=C|T||E|-2|E||T\cap E|+M|T\cap E|.

The upper bound relies on the observation that each pair of distinct points in
E
belongs to at most
k
triples in
R
:

\displaystyle|R|

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in E}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in T\\
x\neq y\end{subarray}}1+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{1},x_{2}\in E\\
x_{1}\neq x_{2}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in T\\
y\notin\{x_{1},x_{2}\}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\ell\in G}\ell(x_{1})\ell(x_{2})\ell(y)

\displaystyle\leq|T|\,|E|-|E\cap T|+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{1},x_{2}\in E\\
x_{1}\neq x_{2}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\ell\in G}\ell(x_{1})\ell(x_{2})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in T\\
y\notin\{x_{1},x_{2}\}\end{subarray}}\ell(y)

\displaystyle=|T|\,|E|-|E\cap T|+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{1},x_{2}\in E\\
x_{1}\neq x_{2}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\ell\in G}\ell(x_{1})\ell(x_{2})(t(\ell)-\mathbf{1}_{x_{1}\in T}-\mathbf{1}_{x_{2}\in T})

\displaystyle\leq|T|\,|E|-|E\cap T|+k|E|^{2}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{1},x_{2}\in E\\
x_{1}\neq x_{2}\end{subarray}}(\mathbf{1}_{x_{1}\in T}+\mathbf{1}_{x_{2}\in T})

\displaystyle=|T|\,|E|-|E\cap T|+k|E|^{2}-2(|E\cap T|(|E\cap T|-1)+(|E|-|E\cap T|)|E\cap T|)

\displaystyle=|T|\,|E|+k|E|^{2}-2|E|\,|E\cap T|+|E\cap T|.

Combining the lower and upper bounds, we get

\displaystyle C|T|\,|E|-2|E|\,|T\cap E|+M|T\cap E|

\displaystyle\leq|T|\,|E|+k|E|^{2}-2|E|\,|E\cap T|+|E\cap T|,

hence
|T|<(C-1)^{-1}k|E|
.
∎

As stated above, Lemma
3.1
immediately implies theorem
1.5
.

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

At most
q
points of
T
are contained in any line in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
.
Hence, Theorem
1.5
follows from the case
k=q
of Lemma
3.1
.
∎

Next we prove Theorem
1.8
.
Lemma
3.1
gives a bound on
|T|
when not too many points of
T
are on any single line.
The next lemma bounds the number of elements of
T
that can be contained in a single line.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\mathbb{F}
be a field, and let
E\subset\mathbb{F}^{d}
be a finite set of points.
Let
\ell
be a line such that
\ell\cap E=\emptyset
.
Then, for
M<|E|/2
,

|\ell\cap\{y\in\mathbb{F}^{d}:|\pi^{y}(E)|\leq M\}|\leq 2M.

Proof.

Let
T
be the set of
y\in\mathbb{F}^{d}
such that
|\pi^{y}(E)|\leq M
and
L
be the set of lines that contain at least one point of
T
and at least one point of
E
. Let

R:=\{(y,x_{1},x_{2})\in(T\cap\ell)\times E\times E:y\neq x_{1}\neq x_{2}\text{ and }y,x_{1},x_{2}\text{ are co-linear}\}.

Since each ordered pair
(x_{1},x_{2})\in E^{2}
of distinct points in
E
determines a line that intersects
\ell
in at most one point, we have

(10)

|R|\leq|E|(|E|-1)<|E|^{2}.

On the other hand, we have

\displaystyle|R|

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in T\cap\ell}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell^{\prime}\in L\\
y\in\ell^{\prime}\end{subarray}}e(\ell^{\prime})(e(\ell^{\prime})-1)

\displaystyle\geq|T\cap\ell|M^{-1}|E|^{2}-|T\cap\ell|\,|E|=|T\cap\ell||E|^{2}M^{-1}(1-M|E|^{-1})

(11)

\displaystyle\geq 2^{-1}|T\cap\ell|\,|E|^{2}M^{-1}.

Here, we use the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the fact that each point of
T
is incident to at most
M
lines of
L
.

The result follows directly from (
10
) and (
11
).
∎

Theorem
1.8
follows easily from Lemmas
3.1
and
3.2
.

Proof of Theorem
1.8
.

Let
\ell
be a line.
Since there are at least
|E|/2
points of
E
that do not lie on
\ell
and
M<|E|/4
, Lemma
3.2
implies that there are at most
2M
points of
T
contained in
\ell
.
Consequently, Lemma
3.1
applied with
C=|E|M^{-1}
and
k=2M
implies that

|T|\leq(|E|M^{-1}-1)^{-1}2M|E|<4M^{2}.

∎

3.1.
Proof of Theorem
1.7

As promised in the introduction, the proof of Theorem
1.7
is very simple.

Proof of Theorem
1.7
.

Let
T
be the set of points such that
|\pi^{y}(E)|<|E|^{1/2}
, and let
M=2^{-1}|E|^{1/2}-1
.
Let
L
be the set of lines that contain at least one point of
T
and at least one point of
E
.

Suppose, for contradiction, that
y,z\in T
with
y\neq z
.
Let
L_{y},L_{z}\subset L
be those lines of
L
incident to
y
and
z
, respectively.
Let
\ell_{M}
be the line that contains both
y
and
z
.
Let
\ell\in L_{y}\setminus\{\ell_{M}\}
.
Then each point of
E\cap\ell
is on a different line of
L_{z}
, hence
|\ell\cap E|\leq M
.
Since
|L_{y}\setminus\{\ell_{M}\}|\leq M
, we have that
\left|\bigcup_{\ell\in L_{y}\setminus\{\ell_{M}\}}(L_{y}\cap E)\right|\leq M^{2}
.
Since
M^{2}<(1/4)|E|
, it must be the case that
|\ell_{M}\cap E|>(3/4)|E|
, contradicting our assumption.
∎

4.
Proofs of Theorems
1.1
and
1.4

The proofs in this section use the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality together with several combinatorial properties of points and lines in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
.
In particular, each point is incident to
\binom{d}{1}_{q}
lines, each line is incident to
q
points, and
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
has
q^{d}
points in total.

The proofs of Theorems
1.1
and
1.4
are similar.
The basic plan of both proofs is to give upper and lower bounds on
\sum_{\ell\in L}e(\ell)t(\ell)
.

We use the same simple lower bound for both proofs.
Each pair
(x,y)\in E\times T
is contained in exactly one line of
L
if
x\neq y
, and at least one line of
L
if
x=y
.
Hence,

(12)

\sum_{\ell\in L}e(\ell)t(\ell)\geq|E|\,|T|.

The upper bounds on
\sum_{\ell\in L}e(\ell)t(\ell)
used in the two proofs of this section rely on different arguments, but both are inspired by work on incidence bounds for large sets in finite space.
These bounds were first investigated by Haemmers
[
9
]
, and have had many recent applications to problems in arithmetic combinatorics and Erdős-type questions over finite vector spaces
[
1
,
2
,
19
,
23
,
24
]
.

The following lemma and its proof is nearly identical to Lemma 1 in
[
18
]
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
E\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
.
Then,

(13)

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell\in G}e(\ell)^{2}=\binom{d}{1}_{q}|E|+|E|(|E|-1),\text{ and}

(14)

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell\in G}(e(\ell)-|E|q^{1-d})^{2}\leq\binom{d}{1}_{q}|E|.

Proof.

The following calculation yields (
13
):

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell\in G}e(\ell)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell\in G}\sum_{(x,x^{\prime})\in E^{2}}\ell(x)\ell(x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell}\sum_{x\in E}\ell(x)+\sum_{\ell}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x,x^{\prime})\in E^{2}:\\
x\neq x^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\ell(x)\ell(x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\binom{d}{1}_{q}|E|+|E|(|E|-1).

In the third line, we use the facts that each point is incident to exactly
\binom{d}{1}_{q}
lines, and each pair of distinct points is contained in exactly one line.

We now show that (

). Indeed,

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell\in G}(e(\ell)-|E|q^{-(d-1)})^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell\in G}e(\ell)^{2}-2|E|q^{-(d-1)}\sum_{\ell\in G}e(\ell)+|G||E|^{2}q^{-2(d-1)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell\in G}e(\ell)^{2}-q^{-(d-1)}\binom{d}{1}_{q}|E|^{2}

\displaystyle<\binom{d}{1}_{q}|E|.

In the second line, we use the facts that each point is incident to exactly
\binom{d}{1}_{q}
lines, and that
|G|=q^{d-1}\binom{d}{1}_{q}
.
∎

5.1.
Over the reals

To proceed, we need to recall the following celebrated Szemerédi-Trotter theorem and one of its consequence on the number of
k
-rich lines.

Theorem 5.1

(
[
29
]
)
.

Let
E
be a set of points and
L
be a set of lines in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Then,

(25)

\sum_{\ell\in L}e(\ell)=O(|E|^{2/3}|L|^{2/3}+|E|+|L|).

Let
L_{\geq k}
be the set of
k
-rich lines, i.e. lines with at least
k
points from
E

(26)

|L_{\geq k}|=O(|E|^{2}k^{-3}+|E|k^{-1}).

The following is a standard consequence, we include a proof for the reader’s convenience.

Lemma 5.2
.

There exist positive constants
c_{1},c_{2}
such that, for any set
E
of points in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
,

(27)

\sum_{c_{1}\leq k\leq c_{2}|E|}k^{2}|L_{=k}|<10^{-1}|E|^{2}.

Proof.

\displaystyle\sum_{c_{1}\leq k\leq c_{2}|E|}k^{2}|L_{=k}|

\displaystyle=\sum_{c_{1}\leq k\leq c_{2}|E|}k^{2}(|L_{\geq k}|-|L_{\geq k+1}|)

\displaystyle=\sum_{c_{1}\leq k\leq c_{2}|E|}k^{2}|L_{\geq k}|-\sum_{c_{1}+1\leq k\leq c_{2}|E|+1}(k-1)^{2}|L_{\geq k}|

\displaystyle=\sum_{c_{1}\leq k\leq c_{2}|E|}(k^{2}-(k-1)^{2})|L_{=k}|+c_{1}^{2}|L_{=c_{1}}|-(c_{2}|E|)^{2}|L_{=c_{2}|E|+1}|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{c_{1}\leq k\leq c_{2}|E|-1}kO(|E|^{2}k^{-3}+|E|k^{-1})+O(c_{1}^{-1}|E|^{2})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{c_{1}\leq k}O(|E|^{2}k^{-2})+\sum_{k\leq c_{2}|E|}O(|E|)+O(c_{1}^{-1}|E|^{2})

\displaystyle\leq O(c_{1}^{-1}|E|^{2})+O(c_{2}|E|^{2}).

No matter what the constants hidden in the
O
-notation are, there are choices for
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
so that the right side is bounded by
10^{-1}|E|^{2}
, as claimed.
∎

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
be as in Lemma
5.2
.
There exists a constant
0<c_{3}<1
such that the following holds.
For any finite

such that

for each line
\ell

\#\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\pi^{y}(E)<M\}<O(M^{2}E^{-1}).

Proof.

As before, we proceed by bounding the sum
\sum_{\ell\in L}e(\ell)t(\ell)
from above and below.
We use the same lower bound
\sum_{\ell\in L}e(\ell)t(\ell)\geq|E|\,|T|
as before.
For the upper bound, we partition the sum as follows:

(28)

\sum_{\ell\in L}e(\ell)t(\ell)\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\in L:\\
e(\ell)<c_{1}\end{subarray}}e(\ell)t(\ell)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\in L:\\

e(\ell)\geq c_{1},t(\ell)=1\end{subarray}}e(\ell)t(\ell).

This is easy, namely,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\in L:\\

y\in\ell\end{subarray}}e(\ell)<c_{1}|M|\,|T|\leq c_{1}c_{3}|E|\,|T|.

Bounding the second term:
By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

(29)

\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\in L:\\
e(\ell)\geq c_{1},t(\ell)>1\end{subarray}}e(\ell)t(\ell)\right)^{2}\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\in L:\\
e(\ell)\geq c_{1},t(\ell)>1\end{subarray}}e(\ell)^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\in L:\\
e(\ell)\geq c_{1},t(\ell)>1\end{subarray}}t(\ell)^{2}.

Lemma
5.2
bounds the first sum on the right side by

To bound the second sum, we use the observation that each point of
T

T
lines
\ell
