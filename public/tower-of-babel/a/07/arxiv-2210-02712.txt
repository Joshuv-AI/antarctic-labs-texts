Dispersive decay bound of small data solutions to Kawahara equation in a finite time scale
Jongwon Lee

Abstract
. In this article, we prove that small localized data yield solutions to Kawahara type equations which have linear dispersive decay on a finite time, depending on the size of the initial data. We use the similar method used to derive the dispersive decay bound of the solutions to the KdV equation, with some steps being simpler. This result is expected to be the first result of the small data global bounds of the fifth-order dispersive equations with quadratic nonlinearity.



Keywords.
Dispersive decay bound; Finite time scale; Kawahara equation; Modified Kawahara equation

1.
Introduction

A global behavior of a small data solution to a nonlinear dispersive equation has drawn attentions of many authors. It might result in the finite or infinite time linear decay bound, or the linear or modified scattering. The goal of this paper is to find a dispersive decay bound of a small data solution to the Kawahara equation:

(KW)

u_{t}-u_{5x}=uu_{x}.

We also consider the case of the modified Kawahara equation

(mKW)

u_{t}-u_{5x}=cu^{2}u_{x},

with
c=\pm 1,
which is the special case of the fifth-order KdV equation

(5KdV)

u_{t}-u_{5x}=c_{1}uu_{3x}+c_{2}u_{x}u_{2x}+c_{3}u^{2}u_{x}.

The equation (
KW
) arises from the study of capillary waves on a shallow layer and magneto-sound propagation in
plasmas; see
[
19
]
.

The local and global Cauchy problems of the fifth-order nonlinear dispersive equations such as (
KW
), (
mKW
), (
5KdV
) have been extensively studied. Kenig, Ponce, and Vega
[
21
,
22
]
proved the local well-posedness (LWP) of the general class of dispersive equations

\partial_{t}u+\partial_{x}^{2j+1}u+P(u,\partial_{x}u,\cdots,\partial_{x}^{2j}u)=0

where
P
is a polynomial without constant or linear terms, on the space
H^{s}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{2}(|x|^{m}dx)
with
s,m
being a sufficiently large positive integer. Kwon
[
24
]
proved LWP of (
5KdV
) in
H^{s}
with
s>5/2
without the weighted
L^{2}
structure. Kenig and Pilod
[
20
]
proved the global well-posedness (GWP) of the equations (
5KdV
) with
c_{2}=2c_{1}
and (
KW
) in
H^{s}
with
s\geq 2.
Very recently, Bringmann et al.
[
1
]
proved GWP in
H^{-1}(\mathbb{R})
of (
5KdV
) with
c_{1}=10,\;c_{2}=20,\;c_{3}=30
.

When it comes to (
KW
), Cui, Deng, and Tao
[
5
]
proved LWP in
H^{s}
with
s>-1
and GWP in
L^{2}
. This result is later refined by Wang, Cui, and Deng
[
29
]
down to the regularity
s\geq-\frac{7}{5}
for LWP and
s>-\frac{1}{2}
for GWP. Chen et al.
[
4
]
proved LWP of both (
KW
) and (
mKW
), with the regularity being
s>-\frac{7}{4}
for (
KW
) and
s\geq-\frac{1}{4}
for (
mKW
). Chen and Guo
[
3
]
extended the LWP result to
s\geq-\frac{7}{4}
and also proved GWP for the same regularity. Kato
[
18
]
proved LWP for
s\geq-2
and GWP for
s\geq-\frac{38}{21}.
He also proved that the LWP threshold is optimal by proving ill-posedness for
s<-2.

Also, the small data global analysis of nonlinear dispersive equations has been spotlighted for a long time. For instance, Deift and Zhou
[
6
]
proved the small data asymptotics of mKdV first, taking advantage of the complete integrability of the equation. Hayashi and Naumkin
[
11
,
12
,
13
]
proved the similar result without using the complete integrability, which helped them extend the result for nonlinearities with time-dependent coefficients. However, their results required an initial data to have zero mean.
[
2
]
introduces some classical scattering results for defocusing NLS, together with asymptotic completeness. It also mentions that the one-dimensional cubic NLS

iu_{t}+u_{xx}=\lambda|u|^{2}u,\hskip 10.00002pt\lambda=\pm 1

does not show a linear scattering. Rather, the modified scattering results for (
1
) and similar types of equations are proved by many authors with different settings. Ifrim and Tataru
[
16
]
proved the global dynamics of (
1
) by testing the wave packets. Adopting this idea of Ifrim and Tataru, Harrop-Griffiths
[
10
]
proved the modified scattering and asymptotic completeness results for the mKdV equation. The work of Harrop-Griffiths is meaningful in that it requires neither the integrability of the equation, nor the zero mean of the initial datum. Okamoto
[
27
]
later proved the global behavior of solutions to the fifth-order mKdV type equation, without proving asymptotic completeness, using the same idea as above. Okamoto later proved
[
26
]
the asymptotic behavior of solutions to the higher order KdV-type equation with critical nonliearity. Here the nonlinearity is said to be critical in the sense that the small data solution shows the modified scattering behavior at that order of nonlinearity.

Unlike the mKdV-type equations, the global dynamics of KdV-type equations have not been studied so much. The main difficulty of the global analysis of KdV-type equations is that the nonlinearities are less perturbative than those of the equations on the mKdV hierarchy; the nonlinearities of mKdV-type equations are cubic, whereas those of KdV-type equations are quadratic. The first result regarding the nonlinear dispersive equation with a quadratic nonlinearity is due to
[
17
]
, which discovered that the solution to the Benjamin-Ono equation

u_{t}+Hu_{xx}=uu_{x}

has linear dispersive decay bound in an almost global time scale
|t|\sim e^{\frac{c}{\epsilon}}
where
\epsilon
is the size of an initial datum. A global result for KdV equation is discovered fairly recently, by Ifrim et al.,
[
15
]
which stated that the linear dispersive decay bound may break down beyond the quartic time scale
|t|\sim\epsilon^{-3}
where
\epsilon
is the size of an initial datum. They proved that the result is optimal, i.e. the time scale cannot be extended further, using the inverse scattering theory. No other meaningful results are known about the global dynamics of KdV-type equations. The main goal of this paper is to show the first result regarding the global behavior of the fifth-order dispersive equations with quadratic nonlinearities, whose byproduct is the small data global behavior of (
mKW
).

1.1.
Main result

First we state the main result of this paper:

Theorem 1.1
.

Consider the Cauchy problem

\begin{cases}u_{t}-u_{5x}=\pm u^{m}u_{x},\\
u(0,x)=u_{0}(x),\end{cases}\hskip 10.00002ptm=1,2.

Suppose that the initial datum
u_{0}
satisfies

\|u_{0}\|_{\dot{B}^{-\frac{1}{2}}_{2,\infty}}+\|xu_{0}\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\leq\epsilon\ll 1,

then we have the following pointwise bound

|\partial_{x}^{k}u(t,x)|\lesssim\epsilon t^{-\frac{1}{8}-\frac{k}{4}}\langle x\rangle^{-\frac{3}{8}+\frac{k}{4}},\hskip 10.00002ptk=0,1,2,3

provided
|t|\ll\epsilon^{-\frac{5}{5-2m}}.

Moreover, in the elliptic region
\{x\gg t^{\frac{1}{5}}\},
we have a better bound

|\partial_{x}^{k}u(t,x)|\lesssim\epsilon t^{-\frac{k}{4}}\langle x\rangle^{-1+\frac{k}{4}}\log(t^{-\frac{1}{5}}\langle x\rangle),\hskip 10.00002ptk=0,1,2,3

when
|t|\ll\epsilon^{-\frac{5}{5-2m}}.

Remark 1.2
.

Note that the case
m=1
corresponds to (
KW
), and
m=2
to (
mKW
).

Remark 1.3
.

The time scale in (
KW
) is worse than that in the KdV equation, which is even worse than that in the Benjamin-Ono equation. This can be deduced heuristically as follows: The group velocity of the fifth-order linear equation is given as
\omega(\xi)=-5\xi^{4}
, so the high frequency part of the solution travels much faster towards to the negative
x
-axis than in the second or third-order equations. However, the possible emergence of nonlinear “bulk”, such as solitons and dispersive shocks, inturrupts such linear dispersive decay. The faster the linear part travels, the earlier the nonlinear bulk emerges.

The time scale in (
mKW
) is better than that of the KdV and (
KW
). This is because the nonlinearity becomes cubic, making it more perturbative. However, such time scale is worse than that of the mKdV equation, as the mKdV equation has modified scattering and global dispersive decay bound; see
[
10
]
. Such discrepancy arises from the same heuristics used in the comparision between (
KW
), KdV, and Benjamin-Ono equations. One may also compare this result with
[
27
]
or
[
26
]
, in which case the nonlinearities become more perturbative than (
mKW
).

Remark 1.4
.

The two norms used in the main result are inspired from
[
15
]
. As seen in the later analysis, the
\dot{B}^{-\frac{1}{2}}_{2,\infty}
norm helps properly control the low frequency, and the growth of the high frequency norms is slow enough that it does not disrupt our analysis. The
\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}
norm exactly does the opposite. It does the control of the high frequency, and guarantees the mild growth of the low frequency part.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Definitions and Notations

Let
1\leq p<\infty.

L^{p}(X)
be the set of measurable function on
X
with the norm
\|f\|_{L^{p}(X)}=\left(\int_{X}|f|^{p}d\mu\right)^{1/p}
on the measure space
(X,\mu).

If
p=\infty,
then
\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(X)}=\inf\{M>0:|f(x)|\leq M\;\;\text{a.e.}\}
. If
X=\mathbb{R}
and
\mu
is the Lebesgue measure, then we write
L^{p}(X)=L^{p}
for simplicity, in either case
p<\infty
or
p=\infty.

We use the time-dependent Japanese bracket
\langle x\rangle=(x^{2}+t^{\frac{2}{5}})^{\frac{1}{2}}.
In some of the following sections, we adapt the time scale to
t=1
by a proper scaling, and in this case
\langle x\rangle=(x^{2}+1)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

We define the spatial Fourier transform of
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
, the space of all Schwarz functions on
\mathbb{R}
, by

\hat{f}(\xi)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)e^{-ix\xi}dx,

and its inverse transform by

{f}^{\vee}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(\xi)e^{ix\xi}d\xi.

Both transforms can be extended to
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
, the space of tempered distributions, by duality.

Let
u_{kx}(x):=\partial_{x}^{k}u(x)=\left((i\xi)^{k}\hat{u}(\xi)\right)^{\vee}
be the
k
-th order partial derivative of
u
with respect to the spatial variable
x
. Also let
|D|^{s}
be the homogeneous fractional derivative of order
s\in\mathbb{R}
whose symbol is
|\xi|^{s}
. Note that
|D|^{s}=H\partial_{x}
, where
H
is the Hilbert transform defined by the Fourier symbol
-i\mathrm{sgn}(\xi).

Let
A,B
be two nonnegative quantities. If there exists
C>0
such that
A\leq CB,
then we denote by
A\lesssim B.
If
A
need not be nonnegative and
|A|\lesssim B,
then we denote
A=O(B).
If
A\lesssim B
and
B\lesssim A,
then we denote
A\sim B.
If the implied constant
C
depends on some parameters
a,b,c,\cdots,
then we denote
A\lesssim_{a,b,c,\cdots}B,

A=O_{a,b,c,\cdots}(B),

A\sim_{a,b,c,\cdots}B
respectively.

Let
\Psi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
with
\psi=\widehat{\Psi}
is even, supported on
[-2,2]
, and
\psi=1
on
[-1,1].
Also let
\psi_{\leq N}(\xi)=\psi(\frac{\xi}{N})
and
\Psi_{\leq N}=(\psi_{\leq N})^{\vee}
for
N>0.
Then define the Littlewood-Paley projection of
f\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
by
P_{\leq N}f(x)=(\Psi_{\leq N}\ast f)(x),

P_{N}=P_{\leq N}-P_{\leq N/2}.
Also define
P_{<N}:=P_{\leq N}-P_{N}
,
P_{\geq N}=1-P_{<N},

P_{>N}=1-P_{\leq N}.
By a slight abuse of notation, we denote a Fourier multiplier whose symbol is supported on
\{|\xi|\sim N\}
by
P_{N}.
In particular, if
N=2^{j}\in 2^{\mathbb{Z}},
then we denote
P_{N}:=P_{j}
and similarly for
P_{\leq N},

P_{<N},

P_{>N},

P_{\geq N}.

Now define Sobolev and Besov spaces. For
s\in\mathbb{R}
and
1<p<\infty
, the inhomogeneous Sobolev space
W^{s,p}
and its homogeneous counterpart
\dot{W}^{s,p}
are defined as the subspace of
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
under the norm

\|f\|_{W^{s,p}}=\left\|\left((1+\xi^{2})^{s/2}\hat{f}\right)^{\vee}\right\|_{L^{p}},\hskip 10.00002pt\|f\|_{\dot{W}^{s,p}}=\left\|\left(|\xi|^{s}\hat{f}\right)^{\vee}\right\|_{L^{p}},

respectively. Recall the Littlewood-Paley inequality

\|f\|_{W^{s,p}}\sim_{s,p}\|P_{\leq 0}f\|_{L^{p}}+\bigg\|\big(\sum_{j\geq 1}2^{2js}|P_{j}f|^{2}\big)^{1/2}\bigg\|_{L^{p}}

and its homogeneous counterpart

\|f\|_{\dot{W}^{s,p}}\sim_{s,p}\bigg\|\big(\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}2^{2js}|P_{j}f|^{2}\big)^{1/2}\bigg\|_{L^{p}}.

Motivated by the above Littlewood-Paley inequalities, we define the inhomogeneous and homogeneous Besov spaces, denoted as
B_{s,q}^{p}
and
\dot{B}_{s,q}^{p}
, respectively, is defined as the subspace of
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
under the norm

\|f\|_{B_{p,q}^{s}}=\big(\|P_{\leq 1}f\|_{L^{p}}^{q}+\sum_{j\geq 1}2^{qjs}\|P_{j}f\|_{L^{p}}^{q}\big)^{1/q},

\|f\|_{\dot{B}_{p,q}^{s}}=\big(\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}2^{qjs}\|P_{j}f\|_{L^{p}}^{q}\big)^{1/q},

with
1\leq q<\infty
and the natural modification for
q=\infty.

2.2.
Useful theorems

In this subsection, some of the theorems are presented for the later use. I omit the proofs here, since the proofs can be found in many literatures.

Theorem 2.1

(Bernstein’s inequality)
.

Let
1\leq p\leq q\leq\infty
and
s\in\mathbb{R}.
Then one has

(2.1)

\displaystyle\|P_{N}f\|_{L^{q}}\lesssim_{p,q}N^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}}\|P_{N}f\|_{L^{p}},

(2.2)

\displaystyle\||D|^{s}P_{N}f\|_{L^{p}}\lesssim N^{s}\|P_{N}f\|_{L^{p}}.

(2.1) still holds when
P_{N}
is replaced by
P_{\leq N}.
Also, if
s>0,
then (2.2) holds when
P_{N}
is replaced by
P_{\leq N},
and if
s<0,
then (2.2) holds when
P_{N}
is replaced by
P_{\geq N}.

In particular, a notable case of Bernstein’s inequality is (2.1) with
q=\infty
and
p=2
, namely
\|P_{N}f\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim N^{\frac{1}{2}}\|P_{N}f\|_{L^{2}}.

Also we have the Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequality, a refined version of the Sobolev embedding:

Theorem 2.2

(Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequality)
.

Let
1<p<q\leq\infty
and
s>0
be such that

\frac{1}{q}=\frac{1}{p}-\frac{\theta s}{d}

for some
0<\theta<1.
Then for any
u\in W^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we have

\|u\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\lesssim_{d,p,q,s}\|u\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{1-\theta}\|u\|_{\dot{W}^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{\theta}.

For the proof, see for example
[
28
]
.

Throughout the paper, a special case of Theorem 2.2, namely

\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{1/2}\|u\|_{\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R})}^{1/2},

will be used frequently.

Let
M
be the one-dimensional Hardy-Littlewood maximal operator defined as

Mf(x)=\sup_{r>0}\frac{1}{2r}\int_{x-r}^{x+r}|f(y)|dy.

Then we have following pointwise bound by
[
9
]
:

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
f
be a function whose Fourier support is in the annulus
|\xi|\sim R.
Then for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
s\in\mathbb{R},

\left||D|^{s}f(x)\right|\lesssim_{s}R^{s}Mf(x).

Moreover, if
f
is not necessarily frequency-localized, then

\left||D|^{s}P_{N}f(x)\right|\lesssim N^{s}Mf(x).

In both cases, if
s
is an integer, then replacing
|D|
by
\partial_{x}
is acceptable. Also, if
s\geq 0
, then the annulus
|\xi|\sim R
can be replaced by the disk
|\xi|\lesssim R.

Remark 2.4
.

The above lemma implies that a polynomially decaying pointwise bound is stable under the frequency restriction, since if
g_{s}(x)=\langle x\rangle^{-s}
for
s\geq 0,
then
Mg_{s}(x)\lesssim_{s}g_{s}(x).
Thus, we can apply the bootstrap bounds of
u
appearing in the later sections freely to the frequency-localized pieces of
u
.

Lemma 2.5

(Interpolating pointwise bounds)
.

Let
0<s<1
. Then one has

\left||D|^{s}u(x)\right|\lesssim(Mu(x))^{1-s}(M\partial_{x}u(x))^{s}.

Proof.

This is a special case of the theorem in Section 12.3.2 of
[
25
]
.
∎

Note that, combining Lemma 2.5 and Remark 2.4, if
|u(x)|\leq A\langle x\rangle^{-\alpha}
and
|\partial_{x}u(x)|\leq\langle x\rangle^{-\beta}
for some
\alpha,\beta\geq 0
and
A,B>0
, then
||D|^{\frac{1}{2}}u(x)|\lesssim\sqrt{AB}\langle x\rangle^{-\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}}
. This kind of estimate will be used in the proof of Proposition 4.1.

3.
Linear Analysis

First we consider the decay estimate of the solution of linear fifth-order KdV equation

(Lin)

u_{t}-u_{5x}=0.

We introduce the time-dependent linear operator
L(t):=x+5t\partial_{x}^{4},
so that if
u
solves (
Lin
) with initial datum
u_{0}
then
Lu
also solves the same equation with initial datum
xu_{0}.

Now we state the basic decay bound for the solution of (
Lin
), which is given as below:

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
u
be a solution of (
Lin
) with initial datum
u_{0}\in L^{1}
. Then
u
satisfies the uniform decay bound

\|u(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim t^{-1/5}\|u_{0}\|_{L^{1}}.

Proof.

Solving the equation via spatial Fourier transform gives

u(t,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{i\xi^{5}t+i\xi(x-y)}u_{0}(y)dyd\xi.

We change coordinates
\xi\mapsto t^{-1/5}\eta
so that

u(t,x)=t^{-1/5}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{i\eta^{5}+i\eta t^{-1/5}(x-y)}u_{0}(y)dyd\eta=t^{-1/5}A(t^{-1/5}x)\ast u_{0}(x),

where
A(x)
is an oscillatory integral

A(x):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{i\eta^{5}+i\eta x}d\eta.

Then
A
is bounded by standard stationary phase argument. Now the result follows from Young’s inequality.
∎

To obtain a more refined version of the above estimate, we need the following lemma due to
[
7
]
and
[
8
]
:

Lemma 3.2
.

A(x)
in the proof of Proposition 3.1 satisfy the following asymptotic bound:

A(x)\lesssim\langle x\rangle^{-3/8}e^{-Cx_{+}^{5/4}},

where
C
is an appropriate positive constant.

From the above lemma, it follows that solutions with initial datum
u_{0}
satisfying
\|u_{0}\|_{L^{1}}\leq 1
and
\mathrm{supp}u_{0}
bounded has the following decay bound for
t\gtrsim 1:

|u(t,x)|\lesssim t^{-1/8}\langle x\rangle^{-3/8}e^{-Cx_{+}^{5/4}t^{-1/4}},\hskip 10.00002pt|u_{x}(t,x)|\lesssim t^{-3/8}\langle x\rangle^{-1/8}e^{-C^{\prime}x_{+}^{5/4}t^{-1/4}}

Now we aim to relax the compact support assumption to a decay estimate, in the similar way with
[
15
]
. Our goal of this section is to prove the following proposition:

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
t>0
. Assume that a function
u
satisfies:

(3.1)

\|u\|_{\dot{B}_{2,\infty}^{-1/2}}+\|L(t)u\|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}}\leq 1.

Then it also satisfies the bound

(3.2)

t^{\frac{1}{8}+\frac{k}{4}}\langle x\rangle^{\frac{3}{8}-\frac{k}{4}}|\partial_{x}^{k}u|\lesssim 1

for
k=0,1,2,3.
Moreover, in the elliptic region
E=\{x\gg t^{\frac{1}{5}}\}
we have the better bound

(3.3)

t^{\frac{k}{4}}\langle x\rangle^{1-\frac{k}{4}}|\partial_{x}^{k}u(x)|\lesssim\log(t^{-\frac{1}{5}}\langle x\rangle).

This is an analogous result of the Lemma 2.2 in
[
15
]
. The only difference with it is the linear operator
L(t),
where
L(t)=x-3t\partial_{x}^{2}
in
[
15
]
and
L(t)=x+5t\partial_{x}^{4}
here.
The steps of the proof will basically follow the idea of
[
15
]
. First we may rescale to
t=1
since
\|u\|_{\dot{B}_{2,\infty}^{-1/2}}+\|L(t)u\|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}}
is invariant under the scaling
u(\lambda^{5},x)\mapsto\lambda u(\lambda^{5},\lambda x).
Also we will split the real line into the self-similar region
S=\{|x|\lesssim 1\}
(would be
=\{|x|\lesssim t^{1/5}\}
in general), the elliptic region
E=\{x\gg 1\}
(would be
=\{x\gg t^{1/5}\}
in general), and the hyperbolic region
H=\{-x\gg 1\}.
Also we split the elliptic and hyperbolic regions into dyadic components, namely
A_{R}=\{\langle x\rangle\sim R\gg 1\}
and
A_{1}=\{\langle x\rangle\lesssim 1\}=S
,
A_{R}^{H}=A_{R}\cap H
,
A_{R}^{E}=A_{R}\cap E
for
R\gg 1.
Therefore the proof is reduced to showing that

(3.4)

\|\partial_{x}^{k}u\|_{L^{\infty}(A_{R})}\lesssim R^{-\frac{3}{8}+\frac{k}{4}}

for each
R\geq 1
and
k=0,1,2,3
(and the corresponding counterpart for the elliptic region).

3.1.
Localized bounds of a linear solution

Now we present the following low frequency bound, which is the same as in
[
15
]
:

Lemma 3.4
.

If (
3.1
) holds, we have

(3.5)

\|Lu\|_{L^{2}(A_{R})}\lesssim R^{1/2}.

Remark 3.5
.

The reason why we call (
3.5
) as a low frequency bound is that the suppressed low frequency factors in
\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}
norm become dominant in the
L^{2}
norm. Such low frequency bound turns out to be worse than the high frequency bound, but it is still acceptable thanks to the good low frequency bound of
u
due to the
\dot{B}^{-\frac{1}{2}}_{2,\infty}
norm.

Proof.

The proof is almost identical to the case of
[
15
]
, but I present the details here to clarify the ideas.

First we split
u
at some frequency cut-off
M
, where
M
is to be chosen later:

u=u_{<M}+u_{\geq M}.

Then the Besov bound from (
3.1
) tells that
\|u_{M}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim M^{1/2}.
Hence

\|u_{<M}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\leq\sum_{N<M}\|u_{N}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim\sum_{N<M}N^{1/2}\sim M^{1/2}

where the sum runs over all dyadic numbers
<M.
Therefore one has

(3.6)

\|Lu_{<M}\|_{L^{2}(A_{R})}\leq\|xu_{<M}\|_{L^{2}(A_{R})}+\|\partial_{x}^{4}u_{<M}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim RM^{1/2}+M^{9/2}.

On the other hand,

Lu_{\geq M}=P_{\geq M}Lu+[L,P_{\geq M}]u=P_{\geq M}Lu+[x,P_{\geq M}]u,

where the last equality follows since
[\partial_{x}^{4},P_{\geq M}]=0
(note that any two Fourier multipliers commute). Now we can estimate

\|P_{\geq M}Lu\|_{L^{2}(A_{R})}\leq\|(1-\psi(\xi/M))\widehat{Lu}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim M^{-1/2}\|Lu\|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R})}\leq M^{-1/2},

with
\psi
being a bump function supported in
|\xi|\lesssim 1.
Also we have

\|[x,P_{\geq M}]u\|_{L^{2}(A_{R})}\leq\|M^{-1}\psi^{\prime}(\xi/M)\hat{u}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim M^{-1/2}

by (
3.1
). This gives

(3.7)

\|Lu_{\geq M}\|_{L^{2}(A_{R})}\lesssim M^{-1/2}.

Now we are going to optimize the bound, compromising between (
3.6
) and (
3.7
) by choosing the appropriate
M
. Note that the bound in (
3.6
) becomes smaller if
M
is small and the same holds for the bound in (
3.7
) if
M
is large. Hence the bound is optimized when the two bounds are comparable. If
RM^{1/2}\sim M^{-1/2},
then
M\sim R^{-1},
and the resulting bound becomes
R^{1/2}.
Also if
M^{9/2}\sim M^{-1/2}
, then
M\sim 1,
so the resulting bound becomes
R
, and the former is better, so we choose
M=R^{-1}.
Hence (
3.5
) follows.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

The above argument works in both cases
R=1
and
R>1.

Lemma 3.7
.

If (
3.1
) holds, we have

(3.8)

\|\partial_{x}^{k}u\|_{L^{2}(A_{R})}\lesssim R^{\frac{1}{8}+\frac{k}{4}},\hskip 10.00002ptk=0,1,2,3,4.

Remark 3.8
.

In (
3.8
), one more derivative corresponds to the additional
R^{\frac{1}{4}}
factor. This can be deduced heuristically from the fact that the fifth-order Airy function

A(t,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx+i\xi^{5}t}d\xi

is concentrated near
(|x|/t)^{\frac{1}{4}}
with
x<0,
as the principle of stationary phase implies.

Proof.

Again we split
u=u_{<M}+\sum_{\lambda\geq M}u_{\lambda}
and choose
M
later as before. The bound on
u_{<M}
follows by:

(3.9)

\|\partial_{x}^{k}u_{<M}\|_{L^{2}(A_{R})}\lesssim M^{k}\|u_{<M}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim M^{k+\frac{1}{2}}

where the last inequality follows from (
3.1
). Now consider the high frequency bound. The bound of
Lu_{\lambda}
can be computed in the same way with
Lu_{\geq M}
in Lemma 3.4, and the bound of
u_{\lambda}
directly follows from (
3.1
) so that

(3.10a)

\|Lu_{\lambda}\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim\lambda^{-1/2},

(3.10b)

\|u_{\lambda}\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim\lambda^{1/2},

(3.10c)

\|u_{\lambda,x}\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim\lambda^{3/2}.

Now integrating by parts gives

\displaystyle\int\chi_{R}u_{\lambda,2x}^{2}dx=\frac{1}{5}\int\chi_{R}u_{\lambda}Lu_{\lambda}-\frac{1}{5}\int\chi_{R}xu_{\lambda}^{2}+\int(\chi_{R}^{\prime\prime}u_{\lambda,2x}+2\chi_{R}^{\prime}u_{\lambda,3x})u_{\lambda}.

The first integral can be estimated via Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, which yields the bound 1 by (
3.10a
) and (
3.10b
), and the bound of the second integral follows directly from (
3.10b
), which yields
R\lambda.
To bound the third integral, we integrate by parts again to obtain:

\displaystyle\int(\chi_{R}^{\prime\prime}u_{\lambda,2x}+2\chi_{R}^{\prime}u_{\lambda,3x})u_{\lambda}=-\frac{1}{2}\int\chi_{R}^{(4)}u_{\lambda}^{2}+2\int\chi_{R}^{\prime\prime}u_{\lambda,x}^{2}.

Hence the third integral is bounded by
R^{-4}\lambda+R^{-2}\lambda^{3}
due to (
3.10b
), (
3.10c
), and the fact
\|\partial_{x}^{k}\chi_{R}\|_{L^{\infty}}=O_{k,\chi}(R^{-k}).
This gives the preliminary bound

(3.11)

\|u_{\lambda,2x}\|_{L^{2}(A_{R})}\lesssim 1+R^{1/2}\lambda^{1/2}+R^{-1}\lambda^{3/2}:=N.

Repeating the similar steps for the integral
\int\chi_{R}u_{\lambda,3x}^{2}dx
one gets the similar bound:

(3.12)

\|u_{\lambda,3x}\|_{L^{2}(A_{R})}\lesssim R^{-1}N+\lambda^{1/4}N^{1/2}+R^{1/2}\lambda^{1/4}N^{1/2}:=N^{\prime}.

Now we are going to refine (
3.10b
) and (
3.10c
) using (
3.11
) and (
3.12
) so that the bound becomes summable with respect to
\lambda.
To do so we decompose

\chi_{R}u_{\lambda}=\partial_{x,\lambda}^{-2}(\chi_{R}u_{\lambda,2x})-[\partial_{x,\lambda}^{-2},\chi_{R}]u_{\lambda,2x},

where the antiderivative
\partial_{x,\lambda}^{-2}
is localized at frequency
\lambda,
namely having the symbol
-\xi^{-2}\varphi(\xi/\lambda)
with
\varphi
being a bump function supported on the annulus
|\xi|\sim 1.
Let
\psi
be the Fourier inversion of
-\xi^{-2}\varphi(\xi),
so that
\psi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}).
Then the inversion of
-\xi^{-2}\varphi(\xi/\lambda)
becomes
\lambda^{-1}\psi(\lambda x).
Then one has

\displaystyle\|[\partial_{x,\lambda}^{-2},\chi_{R}]u_{\lambda,2x}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{-1}\left\|\int\psi(\lambda(x-y))(\chi_{R}(y)-\chi_{R}(x))u_{\lambda,2x}(y)dy\right\|_{L^{2}_{x}(\mathbb{R})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\lambda^{-1}\||\psi(\lambda\cdot)|\ast|u_{\lambda,2x}|\|_{L^{2}_{x}}\leq\lambda^{-2}\|\psi\|_{L_{x}^{1}}\|u_{\lambda,2x}\|_{L_{x}^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\lambda^{-2}N.

Also

\|\partial_{x,\lambda}^{-2}(\chi_{R}u_{\lambda,2x})\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim\lambda^{-2}\|u_{\lambda,2x}\|_{L^{2}(A_{R})}\lesssim\lambda^{-2}N.

This yields a local bound for
u_{\lambda}
:

(3.13)

\|u_{\lambda}\|_{L^{2}(A_{R})}\lesssim\lambda^{-2}N.

Now summing up (
3.13
) on the frequency range
\lambda\geq M
gives

(3.14)

\|u_{\geq M}\|_{L^{2}(A_{R})}\lesssim M^{-2}+R^{1/2}M^{-3/2}+R^{-1}M^{-1/2}.

Repeating the same argument above to the decomposition

(3.15)

\chi_{R}u_{\lambda,x}=\partial^{-2}_{x,\lambda}(\chi_{R}u_{\lambda,3x})-[\partial^{-2}_{x,\lambda},\chi_{R}]u_{\lambda,3x}

we also get the refined local bound for
u_{\lambda,x}
:

(3.16)

\|u_{\lambda,x}\|_{L^{2}(A_{R})}\lesssim\lambda^{-2}N^{\prime},

so that

(3.17)

\|\partial_{x}u_{\geq M}\|_{L^{2}(A_{R})}\lesssim R^{-1}M^{-2}+R^{-2}M^{-\frac{1}{2}}+R^{\frac{1}{2}}M^{-\frac{7}{4}}+R^{\frac{3}{4}}M^{-\frac{3}{2}}+M^{-1}.

Now we want to optimize the bounds (
3.9
), (
3.14
), (
3.17
) by making them comparable. It turns out that
M=R^{1/4}
is the case with the best bound, so that
M^{-2}N\sim R^{1/8}.
This gives

(3.18a)

\|u\|_{L^{2}(A_{R})}\lesssim R^{1/8},

(3.18b)

\hskip 10.00002pt\|u_{x}\|_{L^{2}(A_{R})}\lesssim R^{3/8},

(3.18c)

\hskip 10.00002pt\|u_{2x}\|_{L^{2}(A_{R})}\lesssim R^{5/8}.

Here the bound (
3.18c
) follows from integration by parts below with (
3.5
), (
3.18a
), and (
3.18b
):

\displaystyle\int\chi_{R}u_{2x}^{2}dx=\frac{1}{5}\int\chi_{R}uLu-\frac{1}{5}\int\chi_{R}xu^{2}+\int(\chi_{R}^{\prime\prime}u_{2x}+2\chi_{R}^{\prime}u_{3x})u.

Using the bound (
3.5
) and (
3.18a
) easily gives that

(3.19)

\|u_{4x}\|_{L^{2}(A_{R})}\lesssim R^{9/8}.

Finally, it remains to find the bound of
\|u_{3x}\|_{L^{2}(A_{R})}.
This can be done easily by integration by parts:

\int\chi_{R}u_{3x}^{2}=-\int\chi_{R}^{\prime}u_{3x}u_{2x}-\int\chi_{R}u_{4x}u_{2x}=\frac{1}{2}\int\chi_{R}^{\prime\prime}u_{2x}^{2}-\int\chi_{R}u_{4x}u_{2x}

so the bound follows from (
3.18c
) and (
3.19
).
∎

3.2.
Pointwise estimation on each region

Let
v:=\chi_{R}u.
For notational convenience, we are going to restrict the norm to the region
A_{R},
so we will write
L^{2}(A_{R})
simply as
L^{2}.
Then
v
solves an equation of the form

(x+5\partial_{x}^{4})v=f

in
A_{R},
where we control

(3.20)

\|\partial_{x}^{k}v\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim R^{\frac{1}{8}+\frac{k}{4}},\hskip 10.00002pt0\leq k\leq 4

by (
3.8
), and

(3.21)

\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}}\lesssim 1,\hskip 10.00002pt\|f\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim R^{\frac{1}{2}}

which follows by (
3.1
), (
3.5
).

Now we turn to the pointwise estimate in each region. In the case of self-similar region, the result follows directly from Sobolev embedding (note that in this region just
\lesssim 1
bound is enough, since
R=1
). Hence we concentrate on elliptic and hyperbolic regions.

First we see the elliptic region, namely
x\sim R,
which seems to be easier to deal with than the hyperbolic region. Split
f=f_{lo}+f_{hi},
where the frequency scale is
R^{1/4}.
Here the operator
L=x+5\partial_{x}^{4}
is elliptic, so the leading part will then be
x^{-1}f_{lo}.
Then let
v_{1}:=v-x^{-1}f_{lo}
be the remainder, which solves

Lv_{1}=f_{1}:=f_{hi}-5\partial_{x}^{4}(x^{-1}f_{lo}).

By (2) one has

\|f_{hi}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim R^{-1/8}\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}}\lesssim R^{-1/8},

\displaystyle\|\partial_{x}^{4}(x^{-1}f_{lo})\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim

\displaystyle R^{-5}\|f_{lo}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}+R^{-4}\|\partial_{x}f_{lo}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}+R^{-3}\|\partial_{x}^{2}f_{lo}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle R^{-2}\|\partial_{x}^{3}f_{lo}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}+R^{-1}\|\partial_{x}^{4}f_{lo}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle R^{-9/2}+R^{-31/8}+R^{-21/8}+R^{-11/8}+R^{-1/8}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle R^{-1/8},

where for
k\geq 1
,

\|\partial_{x}^{k}f_{lo}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim R^{\frac{k}{4}-\frac{1}{8}}\|f_{lo}\|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}}\lesssim R^{\frac{k}{4}-\frac{1}{8}},

which yield

\|f_{1}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim R^{\frac{k}{4}-\frac{1}{8}}.

Hence we may integrate by parts in the following identity

\int_{\mathbb{R}}v_{1}Lv_{1}dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f_{1}v_{1}dx

and arrive at

\int_{\mathbb{R}}x|v_{1}|^{2}dx+5\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\partial_{x}^{2}v_{1}|^{2}dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f_{1}v_{1}dx.

Then by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

R\|v_{1}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}+5\|\partial_{x}^{2}v_{1}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}\lesssim\|f_{1}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\|v_{1}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\leq\delta R\|v_{1}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}+\frac{4}{\delta R}\|f_{1}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}

for any
\delta>0,
so letting
\delta>0
sufficiently small gives

R\|v_{1}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}+5\|\partial_{x}^{2}v_{1}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}\lesssim R^{-1}\|f_{1}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}.

Thus, using the bound on
f_{1},
we arrive at

\|\partial_{x}^{k}v_{1}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim R^{-\frac{9}{8}+\frac{k}{4}},\hskip 10.00002ptk=0,2,

and using the
v_{1}
equation shows that this bound also holds for
k=4,
and by interpolation it still holds for
k=1,3.
To sum up, we have

\|\partial_{x}^{k}v_{1}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim R^{-\frac{9}{8}+\frac{k}{4}},\hskip 10.00002ptk=0,1,2,3,4.

Finally, by Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequality, one has

|\partial_{x}^{k}v_{1}|\lesssim R^{-1+\frac{k}{4}},\hskip 10.00002ptk=0,1,2,3,

which is exactly as needed.

Now we need to get the bound of
x^{-1}f_{lo}.
Proceeding as above, one has

\|\partial_{x}^{k}(x^{-1}f_{lo})\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim R^{-\frac{9}{8}+\frac{k}{4}}

for
k=1,2,3,4.
Hence again Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequality gives

\|x^{-1}f_{lo}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim R^{-1+\frac{k}{4}},\hskip 10.00002ptk=1,2,3.

For
k=0
, we decompose
f_{lo}
further as

f_{lo}=f_{<R^{-1}}+\sum_{R^{-1}\leq\lambda\leq R^{1/4}}f_{\lambda}.

Then Bernstein’s inequality and Littlewood-Paley characterization of Sobolev norms give

\displaystyle\|f_{<R^{-1}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}

\displaystyle\lesssim R^{-1/2}\|f_{<R^{-1}}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim 1,

\displaystyle\sum_{R^{-1}\leq\lambda\leq R^{1/4}}\|f_{\lambda}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{R^{-1}\leq\lambda\leq R^{1/4}}\lambda^{1/2}\|f_{\lambda}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}

\displaystyle\lesssim(\log R)^{1/2}\Big(\sum_{R^{-1}\leq\lambda\leq R^{1/4}}\lambda\|f_{\lambda}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\lesssim(\log R)^{1/2}\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{1/2}}\lesssim(\log R)^{1/2}.

where the inequality used in the penultimate line is just an elementary inequality

\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}\leq(n\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}^{2})^{1/2}.

Hence one has

(3.22)

\|x^{-1}f_{lo}\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim R^{-1}(\log R)^{1/2},

which is exactly needed.

Finally we turn to the hyperbolic region. As in the third-order case,
f
might be worse at lower frequency (below
R^{\frac{1}{4}}
) than higher frequencies, due to the weight
|\xi|^{1/2}.
Hence we proceed by splitting
f
to low and high frequencies:

f=\widetilde{\chi}_{R}f_{\leq R^{1/4}}+\widetilde{\chi}_{R}f_{>R^{1/4}}:=f_{lo}+f_{hi},

where
\tilde{\chi}_{R}
is smooth, supported on
|x|\sim R,
and
\tilde{\chi}_{R}\chi_{R}=\chi_{R}
.

Then we may use an energy estimate,

\frac{d}{dx}(-x|v|^{2}+5|v_{xx}|^{2}-10v_{x}v_{3x}+2f_{lo}v)=-|v|^{2}-2f_{hi}v_{x}+2f_{lo,x}v\leq-2fv_{x}+2f_{lo,x},

and integrating both sides gives

(3.23)

-x|v|^{2}+5|v_{xx}|^{2}-10v_{x}v_{3x}\lesssim\|f_{hi}\|_{L^{2}}\|v_{x}\|_{L^{2}}+\|f_{lo,x}\|_{L^{2}}\|v\|_{L^{2}}+|f_{lo}v|

whenever
x\in A_{R}^{H}
, so the term
|f_{lo}v|
can be controlled via (
3.22
) and the Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequality

\|v\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim\|v\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|v_{x}\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\lesssim R^{\frac{1}{4}},

and the other terms can be bounded by (
3.20
) and (
3.21
). This finally gives

(3.24)

-x|v|^{2}+5|v_{xx}|^{2}-10v_{x}v_{3x}\lesssim R^{\frac{1}{4}}(\log R)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Now we have a task to deal with the extra
v_{x}v_{3x}
term, which was absent in the third-order case. The problem is that, we should control the pointwise bound of
v_{x}v_{3x}
, either.

Now try a similar estimate as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}

\displaystyle(-x|v_{x}|^{2}+5|v_{3x}|^{2}+2xvv_{xx}+2f_{lo}v_{2x})\leq 2vv_{xx}+2f_{hi}v_{3x}-2f_{lo,x}v_{2x}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle|x|^{-\frac{1}{2}}(-x|v|^{2}+5|v_{xx}|^{2})+|f_{hi}v_{3x}|+|f_{lo,x}v_{2x}|

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle R^{-\frac{1}{2}}(R^{\frac{1}{4}}(\log R)^{\frac{1}{2}}+10v_{x}v_{3x})+|f_{hi}v_{3x}|+|f_{lo,x}v_{2x}|,

and integrating both sides, together with Cauchy-Schwarz inequality gives

\displaystyle-x|v_{x}|^{2}+5|v_{3x}|^{2}+2xvv_{xx}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle R^{\frac{3}{4}}(\log R)^{\frac{1}{2}}+R^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|v_{x}\|_{L^{2}}\cdot\|v_{3x}\|_{L^{2}}+\|f_{hi}\|_{L^{2}}\|v_{3x}\|_{L^{2}}+\|f_{lo,x}\|_{L^{2}}\|v_{2x}\|_{L^{2}}+|f_{lo}v_{2x}|

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle R^{\frac{3}{4}}(\log R)^{\frac{1}{2}}+|f_{lo}v_{2x}|

for
x\in A_{R}^{H}
. Here the term
|f_{lo}v_{2x}|
can be treated in the same way as above, so one has

(3.25)

-x|v_{x}|^{2}+5|v_{3x}|^{2}+2xvv_{xx}\lesssim R^{\frac{3}{4}}(\log R)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Now we obtained the system of two inequalities:

(3.26a)

-x|v|^{2}+5|v_{xx}|^{2}-10v_{x}v_{3x}\leq CR^{\frac{1}{4}}(\log R)^{\frac{1}{2}},

(3.26b)

-x|v_{x}|^{2}+5|v_{3x}|^{2}+2xvv_{xx}\leq CR^{\frac{3}{4}}(\log R)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

From (
3.26a
) and (
3.26b
) we have

\displaystyle-x|v|^{2}+5|v_{xx}|^{2}\leq

\displaystyle CR^{\frac{1}{4}}(\log R)^{\frac{1}{2}}+10v_{x}v_{3x}\leq CR^{\frac{1}{4}}(\log R)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\sqrt{\frac{5}{-x}}(-xv_{x}^{2}+5v_{3x}^{2})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle CR^{\frac{1}{4}}(\log R)^{\frac{1}{2}}+2\sqrt{-5x}vv_{2x},

so that

(3.27)

|\sqrt{-x}v-\sqrt{5}v_{2x}|\lesssim R^{\frac{1}{8}}(\log R)^{\frac{1}{4}}

and in the same way one can show

(3.28)

|\sqrt{-x}v_{x}-\sqrt{5}v_{3x}|\lesssim R^{\frac{3}{8}}(\log R)^{\frac{1}{4}}.

Now it remains to show that

(3.29)

\|\partial_{x}^{k}v\|_{L^{\infty}(A_{R})}\lesssim R^{-\frac{3}{8}+\frac{k}{4}}(\log R)^{\frac{1}{4}},\hskip 10.00002ptk=0,1,2,3.

To deduce a contradiction, suppose that
\|v\|_{L^{\infty}(A_{R})}\not\lesssim R^{-\frac{3}{8}}(\log R)^{\frac{1}{4}}
. Then there exists a sequence
\{R_{n}\}\uparrow\infty
as
n\to\infty
such that
\|v\|_{L^{\infty}(A_{R_{n}})}\geq 2^{n}R_{n}^{-\frac{3}{8}}(\log R_{n})^{\frac{1}{4}}.
Then by (
3.27
) it turns out that
\|v_{2x}\|_{L^{\infty}(A_{R_{n}})}\gtrsim 2^{n}R_{n}^{\frac{1}{8}}(\log R_{n})^{\frac{1}{4}}
whenever
n
is large enough. Also by (
3.26a
) and (
3.28
) one may show that
\|v_{x}\|_{L^{\infty}(A_{R_{n}})}\gtrsim 2^{n}R_{n}^{-\frac{1}{8}}(\log R_{n})^{\frac{1}{4}}
and
\|v_{3x}\|_{L^{\infty}(A_{R_{n}})}\gtrsim 2^{n}R_{n}^{\frac{3}{8}}(\log R_{n})^{\frac{1}{4}}
again for
n
large enough. Now define the set
B_{n}:=\{x:|v(x)|\geq 2^{n-1}R_{n}^{-\frac{3}{8}}(\log R_{n})^{\frac{1}{4}}\}.
Here
v
is assumed to be differentiable sufficiently many times, so by taking a connected component we may assume that
B_{n}=[a_{n},b_{n}]
, where
a_{n}
and
b_{n}
are the first left and right points where
v(a_{n})=v(b_{n})=2^{n-1}R_{n}^{-\frac{3}{8}}(\log R_{n})^{\frac{1}{4}}.
Note that
-\infty<a_{n}
and
b_{n}<\infty
because
v
is compactly supported.

Now observe by (
3.26a
) one has

(3.30)

v_{x}v_{3x}\geq\frac{1}{10}\left(-x|v|^{2}+5|v_{2x}|^{2}-CR_{n}^{\frac{1}{4}}(\log R_{n})^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)\gtrsim 2^{2(n-1)}R_{n}^{\frac{1}{4}}(\log R_{n})^{\frac{1}{2}}

whenever
x\in B_{n}
with
n
large enough.
Similarly, by (
3.26b
) one also has

(3.31)

\begin{split}vv_{2x}&\geq\frac{1}{-2x}\left(-x|v_{x}|^{2}+5|v_{3x}|^{2}-CR_{n}^{\frac{3}{4}}(\log R_{n})^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)\\
&\geq\frac{1}{-2x}\left(2\sqrt{-5x}v_{x}v_{3x}-CR_{n}^{\frac{3}{4}}(\log R_{n})^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)\gtrsim 2^{2(n-1)}R_{n}^{-\frac{1}{4}}(\log R_{n})^{\frac{1}{2}}\end{split}

under the same assumption above. (
3.31
) shows that for sufficiently large
n
,
v
and
v_{2x}
always have the same sign on
B_{n}
, and the similar statement holds for
v_{x}
and
v_{3x}.

First we may assume that
v>0
on
B_{n}
(otherwise we may replace
v
by
-v
). Then
v\geq 2^{n-1}R_{n}^{-\frac{3}{8}}(\log R_{n})^{\frac{1}{4}}
and it is strictly convex on
B_{n}
since
v_{2x}>0
. Also by definition
v(a_{n})=v(b_{n})=2^{n-1}R_{n}^{-\frac{3}{8}}(\log R_{n})^{\frac{1}{4}}
. However, this is a contradiction because strict convexity means
v(ta_{n}+(1-t)b_{n})<tv(a_{n})+(1-t)v(b_{n})=2^{n-1}R_{n}^{-\frac{3}{8}}(\log R_{n})^{\frac{1}{4}}
on
B_{n}.
Hence it follows that
\|v\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim R^{-\frac{3}{8}}(\log R)^{\frac{1}{4}}
. This proves (
3.29
), and plugging (
3.29
) into the right hand side of the inequality (
3.23
) gives

(3.32a)

-x|v|^{2}+5|v_{xx}|^{2}-10v_{x}v_{3x}\lesssim R^{\frac{1}{4}},

(3.32b)

-x|v_{x}|^{2}+5|v_{3x}|^{2}+2xvv_{xx}\lesssim R^{\frac{3}{4}}.

and repeating the same argument removes the logarithm from (
3.29
), and the Proposition 3.3 is proved.

Remark 3.9
.

(1)

The overview of the linear analysis is similar to that of
[
15
]
. However, since we have to deal with the higher order equation, there are some additional steps which was not in
[
15
]
. More specifically, in (
3.26a
) and (
3.26b
) we had to control the cross terms which did not appear in
[
15
]
. This is one of the main obstacles we have to pay attention, since such cross terms may become more and more complicated as the order of the linear equation becomes higher, making the linear analysis more and more difficult.

(2)

A notable advantage of the argument is that it does not depend on the fact that
u
is a solution of (
Lin
). Thus, as one can see in the last section, the same argument can be applied to the nonlinear case after a slight modification.

4.
Outline of the nonlinear analysis

Let
u
be a solution to (
KW
) with the smallness assumption of the initial datum

(4.1)

\|u_{0}\|_{\dot{B}_{2,\infty}^{-\frac{1}{2}}}+\|xu_{0}\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\leq\epsilon\ll 1.

Motivated from Proposition 3.3, we are going to make bootstrap assumptions

(4.2)

|u_{kx}(x,t)|\leq M\epsilon t^{-\frac{1}{8}-\frac{k}{4}}\langle x\rangle^{\frac{k}{4}-\frac{3}{8}},\hskip 10.00002ptk=0,1,2,3,

where
M
is a large universal constant, independent of
\epsilon,
to be chosen later, and
\epsilon\ll 1
is small depending on
M
.
The overall process for nonlinear estimates is similar with that in
[
15
]
, sometimes being simpler. More precisely, the process may include energy estimates for both nonlinear and linearized equations, uniform Sobolev bounds for
L^{NL}u
for some nonlinear operator
L^{NL},
and finally the nonlinear dispersive bounds.

Let us explain the overall strategies of this paper. Mainly we focus on the case of (
KW
), since the case of (

\displaystyle M\epsilon t^{-\frac{3}{10}}\cdot t^{\frac{2}{5}}\|\partial_{x}^{-1}u_{hi}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|H|D|^{-\frac{1}{2}}y_{hi}\|_{L^{2}}+M\epsilon t^{-\frac{1}{5}}\cdot t^{\frac{3}{10}}\|\partial_{x}^{-1}u_{hi}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|H|D|^{-\frac{1}{2}}y_{hi}\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle M\epsilon t^{-\frac{2}{5}}\|y\|_{L^{2}}.

Hence we obtain the bound
M\epsilon t^{-\frac{2}{5}}\|y\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
. The other term
\int|D|^{-\frac{1}{2}}y_{hi}B(\partial_{x}^{-1}u_{hi},H(u|D|^{\frac{1}{2}}y)_{hi})
can be treated similarly after decomposing
(u|D|^{\frac{1}{2}}y)_{hi}=\partial_{x}(uH|D|^{-\frac{1}{2}}y)_{hi}-(u_{x}H|D|^{-\frac{1}{2}}y)_{hi}
. One may handle the former term by noting that the bilinear map
(u,v)\mapsto B(u,v_{x})
has symbol
\frac{i\eta}{\xi^{2}+\xi\eta+\eta^{2}}
, so that
\|B(u,v_{x})\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim t^{\frac{1}{5}}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}\|v\|_{L^{2}},
provided
u
and
v
are frequency supported at the scale
\gtrsim t^{-\frac{1}{5}}.

Hence, we conclude that, if we let the corrected energy
E[y]=\frac{1}{2}\int y^{2}+E^{\prime}[y],
then

\frac{d}{dt}E[y]\lesssim M\epsilon t^{-\frac{2}{5}}\|y\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\sim M\epsilon t^{-\frac{2}{5}}E[y].

Hence Gronwall’s inequality gives

E[y]\leq e^{CM\epsilon t^{\frac{3}{5}}}E[y_{0}]\lesssim E[y_{0}]

in the time scale
t\ll_{M}\epsilon^{-\frac{5}{3}}.
This completes the proof of Proposition 4.3.

6.
Sobolev bound of
L^{NL}u

In this section, we are going to prove Proposition 4.4. Unlike the
[
15
]
, we do not have to dive into a complicated normal form analysis, since we have a better bootstrap bound for
u
. Namely, we already proved that
\|u^{2}\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\lesssim M^{2}\epsilon^{2}t^{-\frac{2}{5}}
in the Proposition 4.1. Then it remains to see that the
\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}
bound of the inhomogeneous term is perturbative enough that the result of Proposition 4.3 is still valid to
L^{NL}u.
Of course, the answer is affirmative, which is merely the Proposition 4.4.

Let
z:=|D|^{\frac{1}{2}}L^{NL}u.
Then
z
solves the equation

z_{t}-z_{5x}=-|D|^{\frac{1}{2}}(uH|D|^{\frac{1}{2}}z-\frac{3}{2}u^{2}).

Hence one has:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}E[z]=

\displaystyle-\frac{3}{2}\int z|D|^{\frac{1}{2}}(u^{2})-\frac{3}{10}\int(u^{2})_{hi}B(\partial_{x}^{-1}u_{hi},H|D|^{-\frac{1}{2}}z_{hi})

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\frac{3}{10}\int|D|^{-\frac{1}{2}}z_{hi}B(\partial_{x}^{-1}u_{hi},H(u^{2})_{hi})+O(M\epsilon t^{-\frac{2}{5}})\|z\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

where
E[\cdot]
is the corrected energy defined in the Section 5. Here most of the steps are identical to the Section 5, so it only remains to estimate the integrals including the inhomogeneous term. Namely,

\displaystyle\left|\int z|D|^{\frac{1}{2}}(u^{2})\right|\lesssim M^{2}\epsilon^{2}t^{-\frac{2}{5}}\|z\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim M^{2}\epsilon t^{-\frac{2}{5}}\|z\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+M^{2}\epsilon^{3}t^{-\frac{2}{5}}\lesssim M^{2}\epsilon t^{-\frac{2}{5}}\|z\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\epsilon^{2}t^{-1},

\displaystyle\left|\int(u^{2})_{hi}B(\partial_{x}^{-1}u_{hi},H|D|^{-\frac{1}{2}}z_{hi})\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\|u^{2}\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}}t^{\frac{2}{5}}\|\partial_{x}^{-1}u_{hi}\|_{L^{\infty}}\||D|^{-\frac{1}{2}}z_{hi}\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim M^{3}\epsilon^{3}t^{\frac{1}{10}}\|z\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim M^{2}\epsilon t^{-\frac{2}{5}}\|z\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+M^{4}\epsilon^{5}t^{\frac{3}{5}}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle M^{2}\epsilon t^{-\frac{2}{5}}\|z\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\epsilon^{2}t^{-\frac{6}{5}},

and the rest terms can be estimated similarly.

To sum up, one obtains the bound

\frac{d}{dt}E[z]\lesssim M^{2}\epsilon t^{-\frac{2}{5}}\|z\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\epsilon^{2}t^{-1}.

Hence Grönwall’s inequality gives

\|z\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\lesssim\epsilon^{2}e^{CM^{2}\epsilon t^{\frac{3}{5}}}\lesssim\epsilon^{2}

in the time scale
|t|\ll\epsilon^{-\frac{5}{3}}.
This completes the proof of Proposition 4.4.

Appendix: The case of modified Kawahara equation

In this appendix, we sketch the proof of the case
m=2
, namely (

First, when it comes to the Subsection 4.1, one may rescale the equation

to

x\tilde{u}+5\tilde{u}_{4x}+\frac{5}{3}\tilde{u}^{3}=\tilde{f}

by letting
\tilde{u}(x)=t^{\frac{2}{5}}u(t,xt^{\frac{1}{5}})
and
\tilde{f}(x)=t^{\frac{1}{5}}f(t,xt^{\frac{1}{5}})
. Also the localized equation becomes

(x+5\partial_{x}^{4})v+\frac{5}{3}u^{2}v=g

with

and

satisfying the same bounds as in the Subsection 4.1. In these settings, all the same steps work properly.

Moreover, one may easily see that the argument in the Section 5 does work to prove Proposition 4.1 in the case of (
mKW
), under the time bound assumption
M^{\frac{3}{2}}\epsilon t^{\frac{1}{5}}\leq 1.
Here is the step where the time scale restriction
|t|\ll\epsilon^{-5}
is needed.

Secondly, as mentioned in the remark at the end of the Section 5, one may prove the same bound from Proposition 4.3 with the same argument. Here we have a subtly different setting, and I state here for convenience.

The equation (
mKW
) enjoys the scaling symmetry
u(t,x)\mapsto\lambda^{2}u(\lambda^{5}t,\lambda x)
, so the scaling vector field is given as
\mathcal{S}=5t\partial_{t}+x\partial_{x}+2,
and one may see that if
u

mKW

\Lambda u:=\partial_{x}^{-1}\mathcal{S}u=xu+5tu_{4x}+\frac{5}{3}tu^{3}+\partial_{x}^{-1}u

w_{t}-w_{5x}=u^{2}w_{x}.

Now the rest of the arguments are the same as in the Section 5.

The Section 6 is again similar. In this case
L^{NL}u=xu+5tu_{4x}+\frac{5}{3}tu^{3}=\Lambda u-\partial_{x}^{-1}u

8.
Conclusion

We have discussed how long does a small data solution to Kawahara and modified Kawahara equation shows a linear dispersive decay bound. The result shows the tendency of change of the time scale where the linear dispersive decay bound holds depending on the linear and nonlinear part of the equation. More specifically, for the Kawahara equation, which has the same nonlinearity as KdV equation and a higher order of the linear part, the time scale of linear dispersive decay bound is possibly shorter than that of KdV equation. On the other hand, for the modified Kawahara equation, which has a higher order nonlinearity than KdV and Kawahara equations, has a longer time scale of such decay bound. However, unlike the KdV equation, we still do not know whether the time bounds found in this article is optimal due to the non-integrability of the equations, which should be found in a future work.
