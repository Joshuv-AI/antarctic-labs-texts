Boris Adamczewski, Colin Faverjon

Abstract
. We develop a theory of linear Mahler systems in several variables from the perspective of transcendence and algebraic independence, which also includes the possibility of dealing with several systems associated with sufficiently independent matrix transformations. Our main results go far beyond the existing literature, also surpassing those of two unpublished preprints the authors made available on the arXiv in 2018. The main new feature is that they apply now without any restriction on the matrices defining the corresponding Mahler systems. As a consequence, we settle several problems concerning expansions of numbers in multiplicatively independent bases. For instance, we prove that no irrational real number can be automatic in two multiplicatively independent integer bases, and we give a new proof and a broad algebraic generalization of Cobham’s theorem in automata theory. We also provide a new proof and a multivariate generalization of Nishioka’s theorem, a landmark result in Mahler’s method.

2.
Mahler’s method in several variables

Let
n\geq 1
be an integer and
{\boldsymbol{z}}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})
be a
n
-tuple of indeterminates.
We let
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}\{{\boldsymbol{z}}\}
denote the ring of
n
variables convergent power
series with algebraic coefficients,
and we set
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}^{\star}:=\overline{\mathbb{Q}}\setminus\{0\}
. Given a field extension
\mathbb{L}
of a field

\mathbb{K}
, and

a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}
in
\mathbb{L}
,
we let
{\rm tr.deg}_{\mathbb{K}}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})
denote the transcendence degree of
\mathbb{K}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})
over

\mathbb{K}
.

Given

f_{1}({\boldsymbol{z}}),\ldots,f_{m}({\boldsymbol{z}})\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}\{{\boldsymbol{z}}\}
related by a system
of the form
(
2.2
) (see below) and
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}^{\star})^{n}
a point at which these functions are well-defined,
Mahler’s method aims at transferring results about the absence of algebraic (resp., linear) relations between

f_{1}({\boldsymbol{z}}),\ldots,f_{m}({\boldsymbol{z}})
over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}({\boldsymbol{z}})
to the absence of algebraic
(resp., linear) relations over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
between the complex numbers

f_{1}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}),\ldots,f_{m}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}})
. In particular, a reoccurring theme consists in establishing the equality

(2.1)

{\rm tr.deg}_{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}(f_{1}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}),\ldots,f_{m}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}))={\rm tr.deg}_{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}({\boldsymbol{z}})}(f_{1}({\boldsymbol{z}}),\ldots,f_{m}({\boldsymbol{z}}))\,,

under some reasonable assumptions on
A({\boldsymbol{z}})
,
T
, and
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
.
This problem goes back to the pioneering work of
Mahler
[
40
,
41
,
42
]
at the end of the 1920s.

2.1.
Mahler’s transformations and linear Mahler systems

Let
T=(t_{i,j})_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
be an
n\times n
matrix with
nonnegative integer coefficients.
We set

T{\boldsymbol{z}}=(z_{1}^{t_{1,1}}z_{2}^{t_{1,2}}\cdots z_{n}^{t_{1,n}},\ldots,z_{1}^{t_{n,1}}z_{2}^{t_{n,2}}\cdots z_{n}^{t_{n,n}})\,,

and we let also
T
act on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
in a similar way.

Definition 2.1
.

A
linear
T
-Mahler system
, or simply a
Mahler system
, is a system
of functional equations of the form

(2.2)

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}f_{1}(T{\boldsymbol{z}})\\
\vdots\\
f_{m}(T{\boldsymbol{z}})\end{array}\right)=A({\boldsymbol{z}})\left(\begin{array}[]{c}f_{1}({\boldsymbol{z}})\\
\vdots\\
f_{m}({\boldsymbol{z}})\end{array}\right)\,,

where
A({\boldsymbol{z}})\in{\rm GL}_{m}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}({\boldsymbol{z}}))
.
A
Mahler function

f({\boldsymbol{z}})\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}\{{\boldsymbol{z}}\}
is a coordinate of a vector representing a solution to
a linear Mahler system.

Definition 2.2
.

A point
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}^{\star})^{n}
is said to be
regular
with respect to the Mahler system
(
2.2
) if the matrix
A({\boldsymbol{z}})
is well-defined and invertible at
T^{k}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
for all nonnegative integers
k
.

Warning

Independently of the choice of the Mahler system (
2.2
), there are some unavoidable
restrictions that one has to impose on the matrix transformation
T
and on the algebraic point
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
.
When these conditions are fulfilled, the pair
(T,{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})
is said to be
admissible
. We postpone the definition of an admissible pair to Section
4
, but let us just already say that, in this respect, our results are as general as possible.
With this formalism, all our results are concerned with values at some algebraic point

{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
of some Mahler functions
f_{1}({\boldsymbol{z}}),\ldots,f_{m}({\boldsymbol{z}})
related by a system
of the form (
2.2
) under the
assumption that:

(a)

the pair
(T,{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})
is admissible,

(b)

{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
is regular with respect to (
2.2
).

As discussed in
[
1
]
, these conditions are typical in Mahler’s method.

2.2.
The lifting theorem

As a first contribution, we prove the following result.
Let us recall that a field extension
\mathbb{L}
of a field
\mathbb{K}
is said to be
regular

3

3

if
\mathbb{K}
is algebraically closed in
\mathbb{L}
and
\mathbb{L}
is separable over
\mathbb{K}
.
If
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}

\overline{\mathbb{Q}({\boldsymbol{z}})}_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}
denote the algebraic closure of
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}({\boldsymbol{z}})
in
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}\{{\boldsymbol{z}}-{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\}

4

The ring
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}\{{\boldsymbol{z}}-{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\}
is the ring of convergent power series in

Theorem 2.3

(Lifting)
.

Let
f_{1}({\boldsymbol{z}}),\ldots,f_{m}({\boldsymbol{z}})\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}\{{\boldsymbol{z}}\}
be related by a system of functional equations of the form
(
2.2
).
Let us assume that
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}^{\star})^{n}
is a regular point with respect to (
2.2
) and
that the pair
(T,{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})
is admissible. Then for every homogeneous polynomial

P\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}]
such that

P(f_{1}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}),\ldots,f_{m}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}))=0\,,

there exists a homogeneous polynomial
Q\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}({\boldsymbol{z}})}_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}]

such that

\displaystyle Q({\boldsymbol{z}},f_{1}({\boldsymbol{z}}),\ldots,f_{m}({\boldsymbol{z}}))=0

and

\displaystyle Q({\boldsymbol{\alpha}},X_{1},\ldots,X_{m})=P(X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}).

Furthermore, if
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}({\boldsymbol{z}})(f_{1}({\boldsymbol{z}}),\ldots,f_{m}({\boldsymbol{z}}))
is a regular extension of
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}({\boldsymbol{z}})
, then there exists such a polynomial
Q
in
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[{\boldsymbol{z}},X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}]
.

Theorem
2.3
is the first that applies to
all

linear Mahler systems in several variables, that is, without any restriction on the matrix
A({\boldsymbol{z}})
. Furthermore, the quantitative Equality (
2.1
) is replaced by a qualitative statement:
any algebraic relation over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
between the values
f_{1}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}),\ldots,f_{m}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}})
can be lifted to
a similar algebraic relation over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}({\boldsymbol{z}})}
between the functions
f_{1}({\boldsymbol{z}}),\ldots,f_{m}({\boldsymbol{z}})
.
Such a qualitative refinement is a key for applications.

Remark 2.4
.

Theorems
2.3
also applies to nonhomogeneous polynomials, for we can always
turn an inhomogeneous relation into an homogeneous one by adding the constant function

f_{0}\equiv 1

by

\left(\begin{array}[]{c|ccc}1&&0&\\
\hline\cr\\
0&&A({\boldsymbol{z}})&\\
&&&\end{array}\right)\,.

As a corollary of the lifting theorem, we deduce the following result.

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
f_{1}({\boldsymbol{z}}),\ldots,f_{m}({\boldsymbol{z}})\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}\{{\boldsymbol{z}}\}
be related by a system of the form
(

Let us assume furthermore that

is a regular point with respect to (

)

that the pair

(2.3)

{\rm tr.deg}_{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}(f_{1}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}),\ldots,f_{m}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}))={\rm tr.deg}_{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}({\boldsymbol{z}})}(f_{1}({\boldsymbol{z}}),\ldots,f_{m}({\boldsymbol{z}}))\,.

Now, let us compare Theorem

The case

The case

Unfortunately, the method used for proving Nishioka’s theorem hardly generalizes to higher dimension.
In 1982, Loxton and van der Poorten
[
39
]

published a paper claiming that

(2.5)

{\rm tr.deg}_{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}(f_{1}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}),\ldots,f_{m}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}))={\rm tr.deg}_{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}({\boldsymbol{z}})}(f_{1}({\boldsymbol{z}}),\ldots,f_{m}({\boldsymbol{z}}))\,

when the matrix

is well-defined and nonsingular, the pair

This is reported, for instance, by Nishioka in

]

In the end, Mahler’s method in several variables has been applied successfully only for the two following

In 1977, Kubota
[

]
proved that Equality (

) holds
true when the matrix

is

almost diagonal
, that is, when

satisfy a system of equations of the form

(2.6)

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}1\\
f_{1}(T{\boldsymbol{z}})\\
\vdots\\
f_{m}(T{\boldsymbol{z}})\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|ccc}1&0\cdots&&0\\
\hline\cr b_{1}({\boldsymbol{z}})&a_{1}({\boldsymbol{z}})&&\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\\
b_{m}({\boldsymbol{z}})&&&a_{m}({\boldsymbol{z}})\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}1\\
f_{1}({\boldsymbol{z}})\\
\vdots\\
f_{m}({\boldsymbol{z}})\end{array}\right)\,

where

, and

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}1\\

\vdots\\

f_{1}({\boldsymbol{z}})\\
\vdots\\
f_{m}({\boldsymbol{z}})\end{array}\right)\,

, and

The proof of these results (and also of the failed attempt by

2.3.
The two purity theorems

According to the lifting theorem, the study of the algebraic relations between the values of Mahler functions
related by a system of equations of the form
(
2.2
)
can be reduced to the easier study of the algebraic relations between the functions themselves.
However, easier
does not necessarily mean
easy
, and, so far, only the linear relations between

M
-functions have been fully understood
[
8
,
9
]
.
Our second main result is of a different nature. It states that, when evaluated at sufficiently independent
algebraic points, Mahler functions associated with transformations
having the same spectral radius
always
behave independently.
The main feature of this result is that there is no need to check any kind of independence
between the Mahler functions under consideration.

To state this result properly, we first need some notation.
Let us consider several tuples of complex numbers

\mathcal{E}_{1}=(\zeta_{1,1},\ldots,\zeta_{1,s_{1}}),\ldots,\mathcal{E}_{r}=(\zeta_{r,1},\ldots,\zeta_{r,s_{r}})\,.

With every
i
,
1\leq i\leq r
, we associate
a vector of indeterminates
\boldsymbol{X}_{i}=(X_{i,1},\ldots,X_{i,s_{i}})
, and we let

{\rm Alg}_{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}(\mathcal{E}_{i}):=\left\{P(\boldsymbol{X}_{i})\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[\boldsymbol{X}_{i}]:P(\zeta_{i,1},\ldots,\zeta_{i,s_{i}})=0\right\}\,

denote the ideal of algebraic relations over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
between the coordinates of
\mathcal{E}_{i}
.
We also consider the tuple
\mathcal{E}=(\zeta_{1,1},\ldots,\zeta_{r,s_{r}})
obtained by concatenation of the tuples
\mathcal{E}_{i}
, and we set
\boldsymbol{X}:=(\boldsymbol{X}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{X}_{r})
and

{\rm Alg}_{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}(\mathcal{E}):=\left\{P(\boldsymbol{X})\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[\boldsymbol{X}]:P(\zeta_{1,1},\ldots,\zeta_{r,s_{r}})=0\right\}\,.

We say that
P\in{\rm Alg}_{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}(\mathcal{E})

is a
pure algebraic relation
with respect to
\mathcal{E}_{i}
if
it belongs to the extended ideal

2.4.
Main new ingredients

As already mentioned, all previous results concerning the transcendence theory of linear Mahler systems
in several variables are very much inspired by the early
work of Mahler
[
42
]
.
We also start with the same initial strategy, but we add a number of fundamental new ingredients, including
Hilbert’s Nullstellensatz, tools from ergodic Ramsey theory, and a new vanishing
theorem.

In all previous works, a crucial step consists in expressing the coordinates of the
iterated matrix

A_{k}({\boldsymbol{z}}):=A({\boldsymbol{z}})A(T{\boldsymbol{z}})\cdots A(T^{k-1}{\boldsymbol{z}})
associated with the Mahler system (
2.2
)
in terms of linear combination some convergent power series of the form
g_{i}(T^{k}{\boldsymbol{z}})
,
possibly twisted by some multivariate
exponential polynomials.
This is really of great importance for one can then apply some vanishing theorems to the power series
g_{i}({\boldsymbol{z}})
.
This step has gradually become more difficult in the aforementioned works,
as the matrices under consideration have taken a more general form.
Its complexity culminated in
[
10
]
.
Unfortunately, one cannot expect to find this kind of expression
when
A({\boldsymbol{z}})
is not regular singular in the sense of
[
10
, Definition 1.1]
.
Hence this strategy suffers from an intrinsic limitation, which prevents from dealing with
arbitrary Mahler systems. We overcome this main deficiency
by defining the so-called
relation matrices
in Section
7
. Their existence
and main properties
are obtained by means of Hibert’s Nullstellensatz and the notion of piecewise syndetic set.
Introducing these matrices is a cornerstone of the present work and the main novelty with respect to
our two unpublished preprints

[
10
,
11
]
.

In order to apply our results to transformations
T
and points
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
that are as general
as possible, it is of great importance to prove suitable
vanishing theorems
.
That is, results that guarantee the nonvanishing of arbitrary multivariate analytic functions at special sets of points
(typically, certain subsets of
\{T^{k}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\,k\geq 0\}
).
In the case of a single transformation, Masser
[
44
]
solved this problem
in a rather definitive way.
We note that Masser’s vanishing theorem (in fact a refinement using the notion of piecewise syndetic set) is already strong enough to prove Theorems
2.3
and
2.6
.
In fact, we only need the identity theorem for reproving Nishioka’s theorem and Philippon’s lifting theorem.
Unfortunately, Masser’s vanishing theorem is not suited to
deal with Mahler systems associated with independent transformations.
First results towards this goal were proved
by Nishioka
[
50
]
and, again, by Masser
[
45
]
. Unfortunately,
they remain too restricted for proving Theorem
2.8
.
In 2005, Corvaja and Zannier
[
22
, Theorem 3]
deduced from the subspace theorem a general result
concerning the vanishing at
\mathcal{S}
-units of analytic multivariate power series with algebraic coefficients.
They already noticed that it could be relevant for Mahler’s method.
Using the flexibility of their result and the notion of piecewise syndetic set, we cook up
in Section
5
our own vanishing theorem,
which is specifically shaped for our purpose.

2.5.
Relevance of Mahler’s formalism

To end this section, we recall two major advantages that this multivariate
formalism offers.

First, adding variables makes it possible to deal with values of
M
-functions at

different algebraic points
.
Let us give a basic example. With the function
\mathfrak{f}(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}z^{2^{n}}

we can associate
the two variables linear

-Mahler system

(2.9)

\begin{pmatrix}1\\
\mathfrak{f}(z_{1}^{2})\\
\mathfrak{f}(z_{2}^{2})\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
-z_{1}&1&0\\
-z_{2}&0&1\\
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
\mathfrak{f}(z_{1})\\
\mathfrak{f}(z_{2})\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad\mbox{where }\;T=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\

\end{pmatrix}\,.

By Theorem

The second advantage of Mahler’s multivariate formalism comes from the possibility of dealing with

Mahler’s favorite example is the family of the Hecke-Mahler functions

5

4.
Admissibility conditions

A well-known feature of Mahler’s method is that,
independently of the choice of the matrix
A({\boldsymbol{z}})
defining the system (
2.2
),
some unavoidable restrictions on the transformation
T
and
on the point
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
are required.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
T
be an
n\times n
matrix with nonnegative integer coefficients and
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}^{\star})^{n}
.
The pair
(T,{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})
is said to be
admissible
if there exist two real numbers
\rho>1
and

c>0
such that the following three conditions hold.

(a)

The coefficients of the matrix
T^{k}
belong to
\mathcal{O}(\rho^{k})
.

(b)

Set
T^{k}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}:=(\alpha_{1}^{(k)},\ldots,\alpha_{n}^{(k)})
.
Then

\log|\alpha_{i}^{(k)}|\leq-c\rho^{k}
,
for every integer
i
,
1\leq i\leq n
, and all sufficiently large integers
k
.

(c)

If
f({{\boldsymbol{z}}})\in\mathbb{C}\{{{\boldsymbol{z}}}\}
is nonzero, then there
are infinitely many integers
k
such that
f(T^{k}\boldsymbol{\alpha})\not=0
.

The strength of our results strongly depends on our ability to
provide simple and natural conditions
that imply
Conditions (a), (b), and (c). The latter are in fact necessary
to apply Mahler’s method (see
[
40
]
).
Though they appear naturally in proofs,
it is not that easy, at first glance, to see how to check them.
We provide here a simple characterization of matrices and algebraic points
satisfying these conditions, gathering results of Kubota
[
31
]
,
Loxton and van der Poorten

[
36
,
37
]
, and mainly Masser
[
44
]
.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
T
be an
n\times n
matrix with nonnegative integer coefficients and with spectral radius
\rho(T)
.
We say that
T
belongs to the class
\mathcal{M}
if it satisfies the following three conditions.

(i)

It is nonsingular.

(ii)

None of its eigenvalues are roots of unity.

(iii)

There exists an eigenvector with positive coordinates associated with the eigenvalue
\rho(T)
.

In particular, if
T
belongs to the class
\mathcal{M}
, then
\rho(T)>1
.

Remark 4.3
.

Let us consider
r
Mahler systems associated with transformations
T_{1},\ldots,T_{r}
in
\mathcal{M}
, all
having the same spectral radius
\rho
. Then the direct sum
T_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus T_{r}

also belongs to the class
\mathcal{M}
. More generally, given
r
Mahler
systems, associated with transformation matrices
T_{1},\ldots,T_{r}\in\mathcal{M}
with pairwise multiplicatively dependent spectral radius, it is possible to gather them into a larger Mahler system whose transformation matrix
also belongs to the class
\mathcal{M}
, and then to apply Theorem
2.3
.

Given a one-variable Mahler system associated with a matrix
A(z)
, we can
consider the same
system twice but with different variables.
That is, the system associated with the matrix

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}A(z_{1})&0\\
0&A(z_{2})\end{array}\right)\,.

This shows that some kind of minimal independence between the coordinates of the point

{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})

is required in order to apply Mahler’s method. Typically, we cannot consider a point
of the form
(\alpha,\alpha)
in that case.

Definition 4.4
.

A point
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}^{\star})^{n}
is said to be
T
-independent

if there is no nonzero
n
-tuple of integers
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
for which
(T^{k}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})^{\boldsymbol{\mu}}=1
for all
k

in an arithmetic progression.

Remark 4.5
.

According to Definition
4.4
,
Condition (ii) of Theorem
2.6
is equivalent to the fact that
the point
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}:=({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{r})
is
T
-independent with respect to the
direct sum
T=T_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus T_{r}
.

With these definitions, we have the following characterization
of admissibility, which makes our main results very convenient to apply.

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
T
be an
n\times n
matrix with nonnegative integer coefficients and
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}^{\star})^{n}
.
Then the pair
(T,{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})
is admissible if and only if

T
belongs to the class
\mathcal{M}
,
\lim_{k\to\infty}T^{k}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=\boldsymbol{0}
, and
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
is
T
-independent.

Remark 4.7
.

There is no difficulty in checking whether or not a matrix belongs to the class
\mathcal{M}
.
Furthermore, if
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
are multiplicatively independent
complex numbers,
then
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})
is
a fortiori

T
-independent.

Proof of Theorem
4.6
.

We first prove the reverse direction.
Let us assume that
T
belongs to
\mathcal{M}
,
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}^{\star})^{n}
is
T
-independent, and
that
\lim_{k\to\infty}T^{k}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=0
. Then there exists
k_{0}
such
that
\|T^{k_{0}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\|<1
. We observe that if the pair
(T,T^{k_{0}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})
is admissible,
then so is the pair
(T,{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})
. Thus, we can assume without any loss of generality that

\|{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\|<1.
We let
{\boldsymbol{x}}

denote the transpose of the vector

\left(-\log|\alpha_{1}|,\ldots,-\log|\alpha_{n}|\right)\,,

whose coordinates are all positive. By assumption,
T
has a positive eigenvector associated with the eigenvalue
\rho(T)
.
Let us choose such an eigenvector
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
whose coordinates are all smaller than those of
{\boldsymbol{x}}
.
We have

-\log\|T^{k}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\|=\|T^{k}({\boldsymbol{x}})\|=\|T^{k}({\boldsymbol{\mu}})+T^{k}({\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{\mu}})\|>\|T^{k}({\boldsymbol{\mu}})\|=\rho(T)^{k}\|{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\|\,,

for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, because
T^{k}({\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{\mu}})
has positive coordinates. Condition (b) is thus satisfied with
\rho=\rho(T)
. Following Loxton and van der Poorten
[
36
]
, Condition (a) is satisfied for
the matrix
T
belongs to
\mathcal{M}
.
Finally, Masser vanishing theorem
[
44
]
implies that Condition (c) holds since
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
is
T
-independent. Hence, the
pair
(T,{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})
is admissible.

Now, we prove the forward direction. Let
(T,{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})
be an admissible pair. We first note that Condition (b)
implies that
\lim_{k\to\infty}T^{k}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=0
.

Replacing
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
by
T^{k}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
for some
k
if
necessary, we can thus assume without any loss of generality that
\|{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\|<1
. By
[
31
]
,
Condition (c)
implies that the matrix
T
is nonsingular and that none of its eigenvalues is a root of unity. Hence
its spectral radius
\rho(T)
is larger than
1
.
Let us prove that there exists an eigenvector with positive coordinates associated with the eigenvalue

\rho(T)
.
Let
\rho
be as in Definition
4.1
. We first infer from Conditions (a) and (b) that

\rho=\rho(T)
. Since the coefficients of
T
are nonnegative integers, for every eigenvalue
\rho^{\prime}

with

, there is a root of unity
\mu

\rho^{\prime}=\mu\rho
(see, for instance,
[
28

). Replacing
T
by some power if necessary, we can assume
that
\rho
is larger than every other eigenvalue of
T
.
Let
E_{\rho}
denote the eigenspace associated with
\rho
. Condition (a) implies that the characteristic space associated with
\rho
is equal to
E_{\rho}
, since otherwise the sequence
T^{k}/\rho^{k}
would not be bounded. Hence
E_{\rho}
has a
T
-invariant complement, say
E_{\rho}^{\perp}
.
From Condition (b), we infer the existence of a positive real number
\gamma
such that every coordinate of
T^{k}({\boldsymbol{x}})
is larger than
\gamma\rho^{k}
. Set

{\boldsymbol{x}}=\boldsymbol{e}+\boldsymbol{e}^{\perp},\,\text{ where }\boldsymbol{e}\in E_{\rho}\text{ and }\boldsymbol{e}^{\perp}\in E_{\rho}^{\perp}\,.

Suppose that there is no vector in
E_{\rho}
with positive coordinates. Then, for some
j
, the
j
th
coordinate of
\boldsymbol{e}
is nonpositive. Since
T^{k}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\rho^{k}\boldsymbol{e}+T^{k}(\boldsymbol{e}^{\perp})
,
we deduce that the
j
th coordinate of
T^{k}(\boldsymbol{e}^{\perp})
is larger than
\gamma\rho^{k}
.
Since all eigenvalues of
T
on
E_{\rho}^{\perp}
are smaller than
\rho
, we obtain a contradiction.
This shows that
T
belongs to the class
\mathcal{M}
.

Finally,
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
is
T
-independent. Indeed,
otherwise there would exist two tuples of nonnegative integers
(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n})
and
(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})
, not both zero, such that
P(T^{k}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})=0
for infinitely many
k
, where
P({\boldsymbol{z}})=z_{1}^{s_{1}}\cdots z_{n}^{s_{n}}
-
z_{1}^{t_{1}}\cdots z_{n}^{t_{n}}
, providing a contradiction with Condition (c).
∎

5.
A new vanishing theorem

As already mentioned, it is of great importance to find natural conditions that ensure
nonvanishing properties similar to Condition (c) in Definition
4.1
.
Of course, our goal is to obtain a
vanishing theorem

that can be applied to transformation matrices and points which are as general as possible.
Our contribution to this problem is Theorem
5.4
.

In the framework of Mahler’s method, several vanishing theorems have been formulated
by saying that a nonzero multivariate power series cannot vanish
at all points in some well-structured large sets. The latter are obtained by iteration of the transformation
matrix and usually involve arithmetic progressions. In order to prove our main theorems, we need
to replace these
well-structured sets
by sets which remain large but offer much more flexibility.
We use the notion of piecewise syndetic set, which is classical in Ramsey theory,
especially in its ergodic counterpart. As we just said, it can be though of as a notion of largeness
for subsets of
\mathbb{N}
. Furthermore, Brown’s lemma (see (ii) in Lemma
5.2
)
shows that such sets are partition regular, and thus much more robust in terms of partitions
than arithmetic progressions.

Definition 5.1
.

A set
{\mathcal{Z}}\subset\mathbb{N}
is said to be
piecewise syndetic
if there exists a natural number

B\geq 1
such that for any given integer
C\geq 2
there exist
l_{1}<\cdots<l_{C}
in
{\mathcal{Z}}

such that

l_{i+1}-l_{i}\leq B,\qquad 1\leq i<C\,.

In this case, we say that
B
is a
bound
for
{\mathcal{Z}}
. A set
{\mathcal{Z}}\subset\mathbb{N}
is said to be

negligible
if it is not piecewise syndetic, while it is said to be
full
if its complement is negligible.

Let us recall that a subset of
\mathbb{N}
is said to be
syndetic
, or sometimes

relatively dense
, if it has bounded gaps. A subset of
\mathbb{N}
is said to be
thick

if it contains arbitrarily long intervals. Thus piecewise syndetic sets are those that
can be obtained as the intersection of a syndetic set and a thick set. In the rest of this section,
as well as all along Section
7
, we will use heavily the following results.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
{\mathcal{Z}}\subset\mathbb{N}
be a piecewise syndetic set with bound
B
.
Then the following properties hold.

(i)

If
{\mathcal{Z}}\subset{\mathcal{Z}}^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{N}
, then
{\mathcal{Z}}^{\prime}
is also piecewise syndetic.

(ii)

If
{\mathcal{Z}}\subset\cup_{i=1}^{s}{\mathcal{Z}}_{i}
, then at least one of the sets
{\mathcal{Z}}_{i}

is piecewise syndetic.

(iii)

Let
l_{0}
be a natural number. The set

{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}:=\left\{l\in\mathcal{Z}:(l+\{l_{0},\ldots,l_{0}+B\})\cap{\mathcal{Z}}\neq\emptyset\right\}

is piecewise syndetic.

(iv)

The set
{\mathcal{Z}}
contains arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions.

Proof.

The point (i) immediately follows from the definition, while points (ii) and (iv) correspond to
classical results respectively known as Brown’s lemma (see
[
18
]
) and Szemerédi’s
theorem
[
59
]
. Let us prove (iii). Let
l_{0}
and
C
be two natural numbers and let
a

be the smallest integer such that
aB\geq l_{0}
.
Since
{\mathcal{Z}}
is piecewise syndetic, there exists a sequence
l_{1}<l_{2}<\cdots<l_{C+aB}

of integers in
{\mathcal{Z}}
such that
l_{i+1}-l_{i}<B
. Let
i\in\{1,\ldots,C\}
.
Then,
l_{i}+l_{0}\leq l_{i}+aB\leq l_{i+aB}
.
There thus exists an integer
j\leq aB
such that
l_{i}+l_{0}\leq l_{i+j}\leq l_{i}+l_{0}+B
.
Hence

l_{i}\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}
. Thus,
l_{1},\ldots,l_{C}

all belong to the set
{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}
, which proves that this set is piecewise syndetic.
∎

Part of Lemma
5.2
can be naturally rephrased as follows.

Lemma 5.3
.

The following properties hold.

(i)

A finite union of negligible sets is negligible.

(ii)

A finite intersection of full sets is full.

(iii)

If
{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}
is full and
{\mathcal{Z}}_{2}
is negligible, then
{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}\setminus{\mathcal{Z}}_{2}
is full.

Proof.

Point (i) follows directly from Point (ii) of Lemma
5.2
. Let
{\mathcal{Z}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathcal{Z}}_{r}
be full sets.
By (i), we obtain that
(\cap{\mathcal{Z}}_{i})^{c}=\cup({\mathcal{Z}}_{i}^{c})
is negligible, which proves (ii).
Let us prove (iii). By assumption,
{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}^{c}
is negligible. By (i), the set
{\mathcal{Z}}_{2}\cup{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}^{c}
is also negligible. Since
{\mathcal{Z}}_{2}\cup{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}^{c}=({\mathcal{Z}}_{1}\setminus{\mathcal{Z}}_{2})^{c}
, we obtain that

{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}\setminus{\mathcal{Z}}_{2}
is full, as wanted.
∎

We are now ready to state our vanishing theorem.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
T_{1},\ldots,T_{r}
be matrices in the class
\mathcal{M}
whose spectral radii
\rho(T_{1}),\ldots,\rho(T_{r})

are pairwise multiplicatively independent.
Let
n_{i}
denote the size of the matrix
T_{i}
and set
N:=\sum_{i=1}^{r}n_{i}
.
Set

(5.1)

\Theta:=\left(\frac{1}{\log\rho(T_{1})},\ldots,\frac{1}{\log\rho(T_{r})}\right)\,.

For every
l\in\mathbb{N}
, we let

{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}:=(k_{l,1},\ldots,k_{l,r})
denote a
r
-tuple of positive integers. Let us assume that

(5.2)

\|{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}-l\Theta\|=\mathcal{O}(1)\,.

Let
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}:=({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{r})\in(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}^{\star})^{N}
be
such that the pair
(T_{i},{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i})
is admissible for every
i
, and
let

g({\boldsymbol{z}})\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}\{{\boldsymbol{z}}\}
be nonzero. Then the set

\left\{l\in\mathbb{N}:g(T_{1}^{k_{l,1}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{1},\ldots,T_{r}^{k_{l,r}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{r})=0\right\}

is negligible.

Applying Mahler’s method to several Mahler systems requires some uniform speed of
convergence to the origin for the orbits of each algebraic point
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i}
under the matrix
transformations
T_{i}
. As noticed by van der Poorten
[
55
]
, one way to overcome
this difficulty is to iterate each transformation
T_{i}

k_{i}
-times, and to choose the iteration vector

{\boldsymbol{k}}=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{r})
so that asymptotically the matrices
T_{i}^{k_{i}}
have essentially the same radius of convergence. As explained by Lemma
5.5
,
this forces us to consider only iteration
vectors
{\boldsymbol{k}}
that remain at bounded distance from the real line
\mathbb{R}\Theta
, where
\Theta

is defined by (
5.1
). This explains why the assumption (
5.2
)
is natural in this framework. In the rest of this section, we set

T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}:=T_{1}^{k_{1}}\oplus\cdots\oplus T_{r}^{k_{r}}\;\mbox{ and }\;T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}:=(T_{1}^{k_{1}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{1},\ldots,T_{r}^{k_{r}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{r}).

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
T_{1},\ldots,T_{r}
,
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{r}
be as in Theorem
5.4

and let
({\boldsymbol{k}}_{l})_{l\in\mathbb{N}}
be an arbitrary sequence with values in
\mathbb{N}^{r}
.
Then the following properties are equivalent.

•

There exist a real numbers
\rho>1
and
c>0
such that

\left\|T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}\right\|=\mathcal{O}(\rho^{l})\,\mbox{ and }\log\left\|T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\right\|\leq-c\rho^{l}\,\mbox{ for all large enough $l$.}

•

There exists a real number
\lambda
such that
\|{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}-\lambda l\Theta\|=\mathcal{O}(1)
,
where
\Theta
is defined as in (
5.1
).
In that case, one has
\rho=e^{\lambda}
. In particular, if
\lambda=1
, then
\rho=e
.

Proof.

Let us assume that there exists a real number
\lambda
such that

\|{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}-\lambda l\Theta\|=\mathcal{O}(1)
.
There thus exists a positive real number
B
, such that, for every
l\in\mathbb{N}
and
i
,
1\leq i\leq r
, we have

k_{i,l}=\lambda l/\log(\rho(T_{i}))+\epsilon(i,l)
, for some real number
\epsilon(i,l)
with

|\epsilon(i,l)|\leq B
.
By Conditions (a) and (b) in Definition

4.1
, we have, on the one hand, that

\left\|T_{i}^{k_{i,l}}\right\|=\mathcal{O}\left(\rho(T_{i})^{k_{i,l}}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\rho(T_{i})^{\lambda l/\log(\rho(T_{i}))}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(e^{\lambda l}\right)\,,

while, on the other hand,

\log\left\|T_{i}^{k_{i,l}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i}\right\|\leq-c_{i}\rho(T_{i})^{k_{i,l}}\leq-c^{\prime}_{i}e^{\lambda l}\,,

for all large enough
l
, where
c_{i}
and
c^{\prime}_{i}
are positive real numbers. Setting
c:=\min_{i}\{c^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,c^{\prime}_{r}\}
,
we obtain the desired estimate.

Now, let us assume that

\left\|T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}\right\|=\mathcal{O}(\rho^{l})\,\mbox{ and }\log\left\|T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\right\|\leq-c\rho^{l}\,\mbox{ for all large enough $l$.}

Set
\lambda:=\log(\rho)
and let
i
be an integer with
1\leq i\leq r
.
Since the pair
(T_{i},{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i})
satisfies Conditions (a) and (b) of Definition
4.1
,
we have

\left\|T_{i}^{k_{i,l}}\right\|=\mathcal{O}\left(\rho(T_{i})^{k_{i,l}}\right)\,\mbox{ and }\log\left\|T_{i}^{k_{i,l}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i}\right\|\leq-c_{i}\rho(T_{i})^{k_{i,l}}\,,

for some positive real number
c_{i}
and all large enough
l
. There thus exist two positive real number
\kappa_{i}
and
\gamma_{i}

such that

\kappa_{i}e^{\lambda l}\leq\rho(T_{i})^{k_{i,l}}\leq\gamma_{i}e^{\lambda l}\,,

for all
l\in\mathbb{N}
. Taking the logarithm, it follows that

\log(\kappa_{i})+\lambda l\leq\log(\rho(T_{i}))k_{i,l}\leq\log(\gamma_{i})+\lambda l\,.

Dividing by
\log(\rho(T_{i}))
, we see that
k_{i,l}
remains at bounded distance from

\lambda l/\log(\rho(T_{i}))
. This ends the proof.
∎

Before proving Theorem
5.4
, we
need the two following auxiliary results.
The proof of Theorem
5.4
is based on a vanishing theorem due to
Corvaja and Zannier
[
22
, Theorem 3]
. The latter states that if the set of zeros of a multivariate
analytic function with algebraic coefficients contains an infinite sequence of
\mathcal{S}
-unit points whose height does not grow too fast, then these points all belong to
a finite number of translates of tori. The goal of the following two lemmas is to show that most points of the form
T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
,
l\in\mathbb{N}
, avoid these tori.

Lemma 5.6
.

We continue with the assumptions of Theorem
5.4
.
Then,
for every nonzero integer
N
-tuple
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
, the set

{\mathcal{Z}}:=\left\{l\in\mathbb{N}:(T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})^{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}=1\right\}\,

is negligible.

Proof.

We argue by contradiction, assuming that
{\mathcal{Z}}
is piecewise syndetic.
For every pair of nonnegative integers
(l,m)
, with
m>0
, we define the
r
-tuple of natural numbers

{\boldsymbol{e}}:={\boldsymbol{e}}(l,m)
by

(5.3)

{\boldsymbol{e}}={\boldsymbol{k}}_{l+m}-{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}\,

and we set
\mathcal{E}:=\{{\boldsymbol{e}}(l,m):\ l,\,m\in\mathbb{N}\}
.
Since by (
5.2
) we have
{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}=l\Theta+\mathcal{O}(1)
, we obtain that

(5.4)

{\boldsymbol{e}}(l,m)=m\Theta+\mathcal{O}(1)\,,

which shows that
\mathcal{E}
is infinite. However, given any positive integer
m_{0}
, the set

\{{\boldsymbol{e}}(l,m_{0}):\ l\in\mathbb{N}\}
is finite.

Let us remark that given any pair

(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})
of nonzero complex numbers that are not roots of unity, and any pair of natural numbers

(i,j)
,
1\leq i<j\leq r
, the set

\mathcal{E}_{1}:=\left\{{\boldsymbol{e}}=(e_{1},\ldots,e_{r})\in\mathcal{E}:\beta_{1}^{e_{i}}=\beta_{2}^{e_{j}}\right\}

is finite.
Indeed, the set of natural numbers
u
such that there exists a natural number
v
for which

\beta_{1}^{u}=\beta_{2}^{v}
is an ideal of
\mathbb{Z}
. Let
u_{0}\geq 0
be a generator of this ideal and let

v_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
\beta_{1}^{u_{0}}=\beta_{2}^{v_{0}}
. For every
(e_{1},\ldots,e_{r})\in\mathcal{E}_{1}
,
there exists an integer
a
such that
e_{i}=au_{0}
.
We obtain that

\beta_{2}^{e_{j}}=\beta_{1}^{e_{i}}=\beta_{1}^{au_{0}}=\beta_{2}^{av_{0}}\,.

Since
\beta_{2}
is nonzero and is not a root of unity, we have
e_{j}=av_{0}
.
Hence
e_{i}/e_{j}=u_{0}/v_{0}\in\mathbb{Q}
. Since

e_{i}=k_{i,l+m}-k_{i,l}
and
e_{j}=k_{j,l+m}-k_{j,l}
, we get that

\frac{k_{j,l+m}-k_{j,l}}{k_{i,l+m}-k_{i,l}}=\frac{u_{0}}{v_{0}}\in\mathbb{Q}\,.

Now let us assume by contradiction that
\mathcal{E}_{1}
is infinite. Then, there exist arbitrarily large integers

m
with this property.
Letting
m
tends to infinity, we deduce from (
5.4
) that
the ratio
\log\rho(T_{i})/\log\rho(T_{j})
is rational. This provides a contradiction
since by assumption
\rho(T_{i})
and
\rho(T_{j})
are multiplicatively independent.
Hence
\mathcal{E}_{1}
is finite.

Let us recall that, by assumption, none of the eigenvalues of the matrices
T_{i}

is equal to zero or to a root of unity. The previous reasoning shows that
there exists a positive integer
m_{0}
such that, for every
m\geq m_{0}
, every
l\in\mathbb{N}
,
every eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}
of
T_{i}
, and every eigenvalue
\lambda_{j}
of
T_{j}
,
i\neq j
,
we have

(5.5)

\lambda_{i}^{e_{i}}\neq\lambda_{j}^{e_{j}}\,,

where
{\boldsymbol{e}}={\boldsymbol{e}}(l,m)=(e_{1},\ldots,e_{r})
.
For such a vector
{\boldsymbol{e}}
, set

T_{\boldsymbol{e}}:=T_{1}^{e_{1}}\oplus\cdots\oplus T_{r}^{e_{r}}\,.

Given a vector space
V\subset\mathbb{C}^{N}
, we have

V\subset\bigoplus_{i=1}^{r}\iota_{i}\circ\pi_{i}(V)\,,

where we let
\pi_{i}:\mathbb{C}^{N}=\mathbb{C}^{n_{1}+\cdots+n_{r}}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{n_{i}}

denote the projection defined by
\pi_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{r})={\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}
and
\iota_{i}:\mathbb{C}^{n_{i}}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{N}
be such that
\pi_{i}\circ\iota_{i}
is the identity on
\mathbb{C}^{n_{i}}
.
By (
5.5
), if
V
is invariant under
T_{\boldsymbol{e}}
, then

(5.6)

V=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{r}\iota_{i}\circ\pi_{i}(V)\,.

We are now ready to proceed with the proof of the lemma.
Let us consider the column vector
{\boldsymbol{x}}
whose transpose is the vector

(\log\alpha_{1,1},\log\alpha_{1,2},\ldots,\log\alpha_{1,n_{1}},\log\alpha_{2,1},\ldots,\log\alpha_{r,n_{r}})\ ,

where
\log
stands for a suitable determination of the logarithm (that is, the corresponding branch
cut avoids all the coordinates of the points
T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
,
l\in\mathbb{N}
).
By assumption, we have

\langle{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\,,\,T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}({\boldsymbol{x}})\rangle=0\,

for all
l\in{\mathcal{Z}}
, where we let
\langle\,,\rangle
denote the usual scalar product.
Let
U
denote the orthogonal complement to the vector
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
in
\mathbb{C}^{N}
.
This is a proper subspace of
\mathbb{C}^{N}
defined over
\mathbb{Q}
, which contains all vectors

T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}({\boldsymbol{x}})
,
l\in{\mathcal{Z}}
.
Given
{\mathcal{Z}}^{\prime}\subset{\mathcal{Z}}
, we let
U({\mathcal{Z}}^{\prime})
denote the smallest vector subspace of
\mathbb{C}^{N}
defined over
\mathbb{Q}

and containing all

T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}({\boldsymbol{x}})
,
l\in{\mathcal{Z}}^{\prime}
.
It follows that
U({\mathcal{Z}})\subset U
. Furthermore, if
{\mathcal{Z}}^{\prime\prime}\subset{\mathcal{Z}}^{\prime}
, then
U({\mathcal{Z}}^{\prime\prime})\subset U({\mathcal{Z}}^{\prime})
.
The subspace
U({\mathcal{Z}})
having finite dimension, there exists a subset
{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}\subset{\mathcal{Z}}

that is piecewise syndetic,
and such that for all piecewise syndetic set
{\mathcal{Z}}^{\prime}\subset{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}
, one has

U({\mathcal{Z}}^{\prime})=U({\mathcal{Z}}_{1})
.
Let
B
denote a bound for
{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}
and set

\mathcal{E}_{0}:=\{{\boldsymbol{e}}(l,m):m\in[m_{0},m_{0}+B],l\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{1},l+m\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}\}\,,

where
m_{0}
is defined as in the first part of the proof (just before (
5.5
)).
This is a finite set.
Let

{\mathcal{Z}}_{2}:=\{l\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}:\exists m\in[m_{0},m_{0}+B]\mbox{ such that }l+m\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}\}\,.

By Lemma
5.2
, the set
{\mathcal{Z}}_{2}
is piecewise syndetic.
Now, given
{\boldsymbol{e}}={\boldsymbol{e}}(l,m)\in\mathcal{E}_{0}
, we set

{\mathcal{Z}}_{\boldsymbol{e}}:=\{l\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{2}:T_{{\boldsymbol{e}}}(T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}({\boldsymbol{x}}))=T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l+m}}({\boldsymbol{x}})\in U({\mathcal{Z}}_{1})\}\,.

If
l\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{2}
, then there exists
m\in[m_{0},m_{0}+b]
such that
l\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{{\boldsymbol{e}}}
for some
{\boldsymbol{e}}={\boldsymbol{e}}(l,m)
.
Hence,
{\mathcal{Z}}_{2}\subset\cup_{{\boldsymbol{e}}\in\mathcal{E}_{0}}{\mathcal{Z}}_{\boldsymbol{e}}
. Since
{\mathcal{Z}}_{2}
is piecewise syndetic, Lemma
5.2

ensures the existence of
{\boldsymbol{e}}\in\mathcal{E}_{0}
such that
{\mathcal{Z}}_{{\boldsymbol{e}}}
is piecewise syndetic. Furthermore,
{\mathcal{Z}}_{{\boldsymbol{e}}}\subset{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}
.
Thus we obtain that

U({\mathcal{Z}}_{{\boldsymbol{e}}})=U({\mathcal{Z}}_{1})\,.

Hence, the vector space
U({\mathcal{Z}}_{1})
is invariant under
T_{{\boldsymbol{e}}}
. Indeed, if
T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}({\boldsymbol{x}})\in U({\mathcal{Z}}_{1})=U({\mathcal{Z}}_{{\boldsymbol{e}}})
, then

T_{{\boldsymbol{e}}}(T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}({\boldsymbol{x}}))\in U({\mathcal{Z}}_{1})
.
By (
5.6
), there is a decomposition of the form

U({\mathcal{Z}}_{1})=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{r}\iota_{i}(U_{i})\,,

where, for every
i
,
U_{i}=\pi_{i}(U({\mathcal{Z}}_{1}))\subset\mathbb{C}^{n_{i}}
is a
T_{i}^{e_{i}}
-invariant vector space
defined over
\mathbb{Q}
.
Since
U({\mathcal{Z}}_{1})
is a proper subspace of
\mathbb{C}^{N}
, there exists
i
,
1\leq i\leq r
, such that

U_{i}
is a proper subspace of
\mathbb{C}^{n_{i}}
. This vector space being defined over
\mathbb{Q}
, it has a
nonzero vector

{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n_{i}}
in its orthogonal complement. We thus have

(5.7)

\langle{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{0}\,,\,T_{i}^{e_{i}k_{i,l}}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})\rangle=0\,,

for all
l\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}
, where
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}:=\pi_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})
.
The set
{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}
being piecewise syndetic, we infer from the definition of the sequence
{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}

that the set
{\mathcal{Z}}_{2}:=\{k_{i,l}:l\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}\}

is also piecewise syndetic.
By property (iv) of Lemma
5.2
, it contains arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions.
Let us consider an arithmetic progression of length
n_{i}
in
{\mathcal{Z}}_{2}
, say

a,a+b,a+2b,\ldots,a+(n_{i}-1)b\,,

where
a,b\in\mathbb{N}
.
Let us also consider the sequence of vector spaces

V_{0}\subset\cdots\subset V_{n_{i}-1}\subset{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{0}^{\perp}

defined by

V_{j}={\rm Vect}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left\{T_{i}^{e_{i}a}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}),\ldots,T_{i}^{e_{i}(a+jb)}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})\right\}\,.

Since
\dim V_{n_{i}-1}<n_{i}
, there exists
j_{0}
such that

V_{j_{0}}=V_{j_{0}+1}
. The vector space
V_{j_{0}}
is then invariant under
T^{e_{i}b}

and we get that

\langle{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{0}\,,\,T^{e_{i}(a+kb)}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})\rangle=0
,
or equivalently that

\left(T_{i}^{e_{i}(a+kb)}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i}\right)^{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{0}}=1\,,\;\mbox{for all }k\in\mathbb{N}\,.

Hence
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i}
is not
T_{i}
-independent.
By Theorem
4.6
, this provides a contradiction with the assumption
that the pair
(T_{i},{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i})
is admissible.
∎

Lemma 5.7
.

We continue with the notation of Theorem
5.4
.
Let
\gamma\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}^{\star}
and
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
be a nonzero integer
N
-tuple.
Then the set

{\mathcal{Z}}:=\left\{l\in\mathbb{N}:\left(T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\right)^{\boldsymbol{\mu}}=\gamma\right\}

is negligible.

Proof.

Let us assume by contradiction that
{\mathcal{Z}}
is piecewise syndetic and let
B
be a bound for
{\mathcal{Z}}
.
Set

For every

Lemma
5.2
implies that
{\mathcal{Z}}^{\prime}
is piecewise syndetic.
For

, there exists
{\boldsymbol{e}}=e(l,m)\in\mathcal{E}
such that

and

.
Thus,

\left(T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\right)^{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}-{\boldsymbol{\mu}}T_{\boldsymbol{e}}}=\gamma/\gamma=1\,

and we get that

. Lemma
5.2
ensures the existence of

{\boldsymbol{e}}\in\mathcal{E}

is piecewise syndetic.
By Lemma

contradicts the assumption that none of the eigenvalues of

is a root of unity.
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
5.4
.

Proof of Theorem
5.4
.

Set

{\mathcal{Z}}:=\left\{l\in\mathbb{N}:g(T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})=0\right\}\,.

The assumption that the pairs
(T_{i},{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i})
are admissible allows us to apply
[
22
, Theorem 3 ]

to the sequence of points
(T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})_{l\in\mathbb{N}}
.
In order to apply the result of Corvaja and Zannier, we need to prove that the following three conditions are satisfied.

(i)

There exists a finite set of places
\mathcal{S}
such that the algebraic points
T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
are
\mathcal{S}
-units.

(ii)

The sequence
(T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})_{l\in\mathbb{N}}
tends to
0
.

(iii)

One has
\log H(T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})=\mathcal{O}(-\log\|T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\|)
, where we let
H
denote
the absolute Weil height (see Section
3
).

Condition (i) is easy to check. Indeed, any finite number of nonzero algebraic numbers are
\mathcal{S}
-units
for some
\mathcal{S}
.
The coordinates of the vector
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
are thus
\mathcal{S}
-units for some fixed
\mathcal{S}
, and it follows directly that
all
T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
are
\mathcal{S}
-units too.
Since by assumption the pairs
(T_{i},{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i})
are admissible, Theorem
4.6
implies that
the sequence
(T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})_{l\in\mathbb{N}}
tends to
0
, and
thus (ii) is satisfied.
Next we check that (iii) holds. We infer from Lemma
5.5

that

\|T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}\|=\mathcal{O}(e^{l})\ \mbox{ and }\ \log\|T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\|\leq-ce^{l}\,,

for some positive real number
c
and all sufficiently large integers

. On the other hand, we have

\log H(T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})=\mathcal{O}(\|T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}\|)
. It thus follows that

\log H(T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})=\mathcal{O}(-\log\|T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\|)\,,

which shows that Condition (iii) is satisfied.

Applying
[
22
, Theorem 3]
to the sequence of algebraic points

(T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})_{l\in\mathbb{N}}

and to the function
g({\boldsymbol{z}})
,
we obtain the existence of a finite number of
N
-tuples
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{s}
and of algebraic numbers

\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{s}
,
such that

{\mathcal{Z}}\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{s}{\mathcal{Z}}_{i}

where

{\mathcal{Z}}_{i}:=\left\{l\in\mathbb{N}:\left(T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\right)^{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{i}}=\gamma_{i}\right\}\,.

By Lemma
5.7
, the sets
{\mathcal{Z}}_{i}
are all negligible. It thus follows from Lemma
5.3

that

is also negligible, which proves the theorem.
∎

6

6

Since
{\mathcal{Z}}
is not negligible, Theorem
5.4
implies that

\det C({\boldsymbol{z}})=0
. Hence
\mathcal{I}(\delta_{1})
is a strict subspace of
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}({\boldsymbol{z}})[\boldsymbol{Y}]_{\delta_{1}}
,
say of dimension
d<h
.
There thus exist polynomials
b_{i,j}(z)\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[{\boldsymbol{z}}]
,
1\leq i\leq h-d
,
1\leq j\leq h
, such that
for all
p_{1}({\boldsymbol{z}}),\ldots,p_{h}({\boldsymbol{z}})\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}({\boldsymbol{z}})
:

(7.7)

\sum_{j=1}^{h}p_{j}({\boldsymbol{z}})\boldsymbol{Y}^{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{j}}\in\mathcal{I}(\delta_{1})\Leftrightarrow\sum_{j=1}^{h}b_{i,j}({\boldsymbol{z}})p_{j}({\boldsymbol{z}})=0\;\;\;\;\forall i,\;1\leq i\leq h-d\,.

Since
d+(h-d)=h
, these equations are linearly independent.
Now, let us consider a polynomial

P:=\sum_{j=1}^{h}p_{j}({\boldsymbol{z}})\boldsymbol{Y}^{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{j}}\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[\boldsymbol{Y},{\boldsymbol{z}}]_{\delta_{1},\delta_{2}}
and set

p_{j}({\boldsymbol{z}})=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\,:\,|{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}|\leq\delta_{2}}p_{j,{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\ \text{ and }\ b_{i,j}({\boldsymbol{z}})=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}\,:\,|{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}|\leq\delta_{1}^{\prime}}b_{i,j,{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}}{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}\,,

where
\delta_{1}^{\prime}
is a nonnegative integer depending only on
\delta_{1}
, and where the numbers

p_{j,{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}
and
b_{i,j,{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}}
are algebraic for all quadruples
(i,j,{\boldsymbol{\lambda}},{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})
.
By (
7.7
),
P
belongs to
\mathcal{I}(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})
if and only if

\sum_{j=1}^{h}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}|\leq\delta_{2},|{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}|\leq\delta_{1}^{\prime}\\
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}+{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}={\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\end{subarray}}b_{i,j,{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}}p_{j,{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}=0\,,\;\;\;\;\forall({\boldsymbol{\gamma}},i)\,.

If we fix the vector
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
, then, as soon as every coordinate of

{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
is larger than
\delta_{1}^{\prime}
and that
|{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}|\leq\delta_{2}
,
the number of linearly independent equations does not
depend on our choice for
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
.
Thus,
the total number of linearly independent equations defining the vector space

\mathcal{I}(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})
is equivalent to

c_{1}(\delta_{1})\delta_{2}^{N}\,,

when
\delta_{2}
tends to infinity, where
c_{1}(\delta_{1})
is a positive real number depending
only on
\delta_{1}
. This number is precisely the dimension
of the vector space
\mathcal{I}^{\perp}(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})
, which ends the proof.
∎

Lemma 7.2
.

For every pair of nonnegative integers
(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})
, one has

\dim\mathcal{I}^{\perp}(2\delta_{1},\delta_{2})\leq 2^{t^{2}}\dim\mathcal{I}^{\perp}(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})\,.

Proof.

Let
P(\boldsymbol{Y},{\boldsymbol{z}})\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[\boldsymbol{Y},{\boldsymbol{z}}]_{2\delta_{1},\delta_{2}}
. Such a polynomial has a decomposition of the form

(7.8)

P(\boldsymbol{Y},{\boldsymbol{z}})=\sum_{\ell=1}^{t^{2}}e_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{Y})^{\delta_{1}}P_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{Y},{\boldsymbol{z}})\,,

where we let
e_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{Y})
denote the
2^{t^{2}}
monomials of degree at most one in the indeterminates

y_{i,j}
, and where each
P_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{Y},{\boldsymbol{z}})
belongs to
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[\boldsymbol{Y},{\boldsymbol{z}}]_{\delta_{1},\delta_{2}}
. If, in such a decomposition,
every polynomial
P_{\ell}
belongs to
\mathcal{I}(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})
, then
P
belongs to
\mathcal{I}(2\delta_{1},\delta_{2})
.
The decomposition (
7.8
) naturally defines a surjective linear map from

\left(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[\boldsymbol{Y},{\boldsymbol{z}}]_{\delta_{1},\delta_{2}}/\mathcal{I}(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})\right)^{2^{t^{2}}}
to
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[\boldsymbol{Y},{\boldsymbol{z}}]_{2\delta_{1},\delta_{2}}/\mathcal{I}(2\delta_{1},\delta_{2})
.
It follows that

\dim_{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\mathcal{I}^{\perp}(2\delta_{1},\delta_{2})\leq 2^{t^{2}}\dim_{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\mathcal{I}^{\perp}(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})\,,

as wanted.
∎

7.2.
Nullstellensatz and relation matrices

In this section, we show how Hilbert’s Nullstellensatz allows us to exhibit a matrix
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
,
called a
relation matrix
, whose coordinates are all algebraic over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}({\boldsymbol{z}})
,
and which encodes the algebraic relations over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}({\boldsymbol{z}})

of degree at most
d_{i}
in each variables between the functions

f_{1,1}({\boldsymbol{z}}),\ldots,f_{r,m_{r}}({\boldsymbol{z}})
. These relation matrices are the cornerstone of the proof
of Theorem

6.2
.

We first prove the following lemma.

Lemma 7.3
.

The set
\mathcal{I}
is a radical ideal of
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}({\boldsymbol{z}})[\boldsymbol{Y}]
.

Proof.

Checking that
\mathcal{I}
is an ideal of
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}({\boldsymbol{z}})[\boldsymbol{Y}]
is not difficult. If
P_{1},P_{2}\in\mathcal{I}
, and if

{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}
(resp.
{\mathcal{Z}}_{2}
) are full sets of nonnegative integers
l
for which

P_{1}({\boldsymbol{R}}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}),T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})=0
(resp.
P_{2}({\boldsymbol{R}}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}),T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})=0
),
then
P_{1}+P_{2}
vanishes at the points
({\boldsymbol{R}}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}),T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})
for all

l\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}\cap{\mathcal{Z}}_{2}
. By Lemma
5.3
, this set is full. Hence
P_{1}+P_{2}\in\mathcal{I}
.

Now let
P_{1}\in\mathcal{I}
and
P_{2}\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}({\boldsymbol{z}})[\boldsymbol{Y}]
. On the one hand,
P_{1}({\boldsymbol{R}}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}),T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})=0

for all
l
in a full set
{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}
, while, on the other hand, Theorem
5.4
ensures that

P_{2}(\boldsymbol{Y},{\boldsymbol{z}})
is well-defined at
({\boldsymbol{R}}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}),T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})
for all nonnegative integers

l

outside a negligible set
{\mathcal{Z}}_{2}
. We deduce that

P_{1}({\boldsymbol{R}}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}),T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})P_{2}({\boldsymbol{R}}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}),T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})=0

for all
l\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}\setminus{\mathcal{Z}}_{2}
. By Lemma
5.3
, this is a full set. Hence
P_{1}P_{2}\in\mathcal{I}
.

Let
P\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}({\boldsymbol{z}})[\boldsymbol{Y}]
be such that
P^{r}\in\mathcal{I}
for some
r
. If
l
is a nonnegative integer such that
P({\boldsymbol{R}}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}),T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})^{r}=0
, then
P({\boldsymbol{R}}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}),T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})=0
.
Hence
P\in\mathcal{I}
and

\mathcal{I}
is a radical ideal.
∎

Let
\mathbb{K}
denote an algebraic closure of
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}({\boldsymbol{z}})
.

Lemma 7.4
.

There exists a matrix
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\in{\rm GL}_{t}(\mathbb{K})
such that

P({\boldsymbol{\phi}},{\boldsymbol{z}})=0\,,

for all polynomials
P\in\mathcal{I}
.

Proof.

Let us consider the affine algebraic set
\mathcal{V}
associated with the radical ideal
\mathcal{I}
. That is,

{\mathcal{V}}:=\{{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\in\mathcal{M}_{t}(\mathbb{K})\ :\ P({\boldsymbol{\phi}},{\boldsymbol{z}})=0\,,\;\forall P\in\mathcal{I}\}\,.

According to the weak form of Hilbert’s Nullstellensatz (see, for instance,
[
33
, Theorem 1.4, p. 379]
),

\mathcal{V}
is nonempty as soon as

\mathcal{I}
is a proper ideal of
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}({\boldsymbol{z}})[\boldsymbol{Y}]
. But the definition of
\mathcal{I}
clearly implies that nonzero constant polynomials do not belong to
\mathcal{I}
. Hence
\mathcal{V}
is nonempty.

Now, let us assume by contradiction that
\det{\boldsymbol{\phi}}=0
for all
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
in
\mathcal{V}
.
By Hilbert’s Nullstellensatz (see, for instance,
[
33
, Theorem 1.5, p. 380]
),
the polynomial
\det\boldsymbol{Y}
belongs to the radical of the ideal

\mathcal{I}
. Hence
\det\boldsymbol{Y}\in\mathcal{I}
for
\mathcal{I}
is radical. Thus,
\det{\boldsymbol{R}}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}})=0

for all
l
in a full set. This provides a contradiction since
{\boldsymbol{R}}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}})
is invertible
for all
l
in
\mathbb{N}
. We thus deduce that there exists an invertible matrix
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}

in
\mathcal{V}
, as wanted.
∎

Definition 7.5
.

A matrix
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\in{\rm GL}_{t}(\mathbb{K})
satisfying the property of Lemma

7.4
is called a
relation matrix
.

The next lemma plays a central role in the proof of Theorem
6.2
.

Lemma 7.6
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\in{\rm GL}_{t}(\mathbb{K})

be a relation matrix.
Then

P\left({\boldsymbol{\phi}}{\boldsymbol{R}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}({\boldsymbol{z}}),T_{\boldsymbol{k}}{\boldsymbol{z}}\right)=0\,,

for all
P\in\mathcal{I}
and all
{\boldsymbol{k}}\in V_{+}^{\perp}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{A}
denote the subring of
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}({\boldsymbol{z}})
formed by all rational functions with no pole
at the points
T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
,
{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
. The set
\mathcal{S}
of polynomials
P\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[{\boldsymbol{z}}]

that does not vanish at any of the points
T_{\boldsymbol{k}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
,
{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
, being multiplicatively closed,
the ring
\mathcal{A}
is the localization of
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[{\boldsymbol{z}}]
at
\mathcal{S}
,
i.e.
,
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{S}^{-1}\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[{\boldsymbol{z}}]
.
It follows that
\mathcal{A}
is a
Noetherian ring (see, for instance,
[
33
, Proposition 1.6, p. 415]
).
Since by assumption each of the points
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{r}
is regular with respect to the
corresponding Mahler system (
6.1.

i

), the coefficients of the matrices

{\boldsymbol{R}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}({\boldsymbol{z}})
,
{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
, belong to the ring
\mathcal{A}
.
With any piecewise syndetic set
{\mathcal{Z}}\subset\mathbb{N}
, we associate the set

\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Z}}:=\left\{P\in\mathcal{A}[\boldsymbol{Y}]\,:\,P({\boldsymbol{R}}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}),T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})=0\,,\;\forall l\in{\mathcal{Z}}\right\}\,.

The proof is divided into the following seven simple results, namely Facts
1
to
7
.

Fact 1
.

The set
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Z}}
is an ideal of
\mathcal{A}[\boldsymbol{Y}]
.

Let
P_{1},P_{2}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Z}}
. Then
P_{1}+P_{2}
vanishes at
({\boldsymbol{R}}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}),T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})

for all
l\in{\mathcal{Z}}
. Hence
P_{1}+P_{2}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Z}}
. Let
P_{1}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Z}}
and
P_{2}\in\mathcal{A}[\boldsymbol{Y}]
.
Then
P_{1}({\boldsymbol{R}}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}),T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})=0
for all
l\in{\mathcal{Z}}
. On the other hand,
by definition of
\mathcal{A}
,

P_{2}(\boldsymbol{Y},{\boldsymbol{z}})
has no pole at
({\boldsymbol{R}}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}),T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})
,
l\in\mathbb{N}
. It follows that

P_{1}P_{2}
vanishes at
({\boldsymbol{R}}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}),T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})
for all
l\in{\mathcal{Z}}
. Hence
P_{1}P_{2}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Z}}
, which proves Fact
1
.

If
{\mathcal{Z}}^{\prime}
is a piecewise syndetic set such that
{\mathcal{Z}}^{\prime}\subset{\mathcal{Z}}\subset\mathbb{N}
, one has

\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Z}}\subset\mathcal{I}_{{\mathcal{Z}}^{\prime}}
. Since
\mathcal{A}
is Noetherian,
\mathcal{A}[\boldsymbol{Y}]
is Noetherian too
and any increasing sequence of ideals is stationary.
Thus, for every piecewise syndetic set
{\mathcal{Z}}\subset\mathbb{N}
, there exists a
piecewise syndetic set
{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}\subset{\mathcal{Z}}
such that
\mathcal{I}_{{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}}=\mathcal{I}_{{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}}
for all piecewise syndetic sets

{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}\subset{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}
. For Facts
2
to
7
, we fix such a pair of sets

({\mathcal{Z}},{\mathcal{Z}}_{0})
.

Fact 2
.

There exist infinitely many
r
-tuples
{\boldsymbol{k}}\in V_{+}^{\perp}
such that

(7.9)

{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}({\boldsymbol{k}}):=\{l\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}\ :\ \exists l^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{0},\;{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l^{\prime}}={\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}+{\boldsymbol{k}}\}

is a piecewise syndetic set.

Let
B
be a bound for
{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}
and
e\geq 0
be an integer. By Lemma
5.2
,
the set

{\mathcal{Z}}_{e}:=\{l\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}\ :\ \exists l^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{0},e\leq l^{\prime}-l\leq e+B\}

is piecewise syndetic. By (
6.6
), there are only finitely many differences
{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l^{\prime}}-{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}

for which
e\leq l^{\prime}-l\leq e+B
. Furthermore, by Lemma
6.3
, such differences all belong to
V_{+}^{\perp}
.
Let
\mathcal{K}_{e}\subset V_{+}^{\perp}
denote the finite set formed by these differences. Then

{\mathcal{Z}}_{e}\subset\cup_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathcal{K}_{e}}{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}({\boldsymbol{k}})\,.

By Lemma
5.2
, at least one of the sets
{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}({\boldsymbol{k}})
,
{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathcal{K}_{e}
, is piecewise syndetic.
Letting
e
tend to infinity, this proves Fact
2
.

Now, let

\mathcal{K}:=\{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in V_{+}^{\perp}:\ {\mathcal{Z}}_{0}({\boldsymbol{k}})\,\mbox{ is piecewise syndetic}\}\,

denote this infinite set.

Fact 3
.

The
\mathbb{Z}
-module generated by
\mathcal{K}
in
\mathbb{Z}^{r}
is equal to
V^{\perp}
.

Let
W
denote the
\mathbb{Z}
-module generated by
\mathcal{K}
in
\mathbb{Z}^{r}
. It is enough to show that
W^{\perp}
, its orthogonal complement in
\mathbb{Z}^{r}
, is equal to
V
. Since
W\subset V^{\perp}
, we have

V\subset W^{\perp}
. Let us prove the reverse inclusion. Let
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\in W^{\perp}
. Then
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
is orthogonal to all
{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathcal{K}
.
By construction,
\mathcal{K}
remains at bounded distance from
\mathbb{R}\Theta
.
Renormalizing and taking the limit for large vectors
{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathcal{K}
,
we get that
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
is orthogonal to
\Theta
. Hence,
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\in V
.
This proves Fact
3
.

Given
{\boldsymbol{k}}\in V_{+}^{\perp}
, we define an action from the monoid
V_{+}^{\perp}
to
\mathcal{A}[\boldsymbol{Y}]
by:

\sigma_{\boldsymbol{k}}:\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathcal{A}[\boldsymbol{Y}]&\rightarrow&\mathcal{A}[\boldsymbol{Y}]\\
P(\boldsymbol{Y},{\boldsymbol{z}})&\mapsto&P(\boldsymbol{Y}{\boldsymbol{R}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}({\boldsymbol{z}}),T_{\boldsymbol{k}}{\boldsymbol{z}})\,.\end{array}\right.

Note that the map
\sigma_{\boldsymbol{k}}
is well-defined. Indeed, we already observed that the coordinates of

{\boldsymbol{R}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}({\boldsymbol{z}})
belong
to the ring
\mathcal{A}
for all
{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
.

Fact 4
.

For all
{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathcal{K}
,
\sigma_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\mathcal{I}_{{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}})\subset\mathcal{I}_{{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}}
.

Let
P\in\mathcal{I}_{{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}}
,
{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathcal{K}
, and
l\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}({\boldsymbol{k}})
. Let
l^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}
be such that

{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l^{\prime}}={\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\sigma_{\boldsymbol{k}}(P)({\boldsymbol{R}}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}),T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P({\boldsymbol{R}}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}){\boldsymbol{R}}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}(T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}),T_{\boldsymbol{k}}T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P({\boldsymbol{R}}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}+{\boldsymbol{k}}}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}),T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P({\boldsymbol{R}}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l^{\prime}}}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}),T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l^{\prime}}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0\,.

Thus,
\sigma_{\boldsymbol{k}}(P)
belongs to the ideal
\mathcal{I}_{{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}({\boldsymbol{k}})}
, which is equal to
\mathcal{I}_{{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}}
by minimality.
This proves Fact
4
.

Fact 5
.

Let
P\in\mathcal{I}_{{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}}
be such that
P=\sigma_{\boldsymbol{k}}(Q)
for some
Q\in\mathcal{A}[\boldsymbol{Y}]
and
{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathcal{K}
.
Then
Q\in\mathcal{I}_{{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}}
.

Since
{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}({\boldsymbol{k}})
is piecewise syndetic, we infer from (
6.6
) that the set

{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}({\boldsymbol{k}})^{-1}:=\{l^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}\ :\ \exists l\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{0},\,{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l^{\prime}}={\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}+{\boldsymbol{k}}\}

is also piecewise syndetic. By minimality, we obtain
\mathcal{I}_{{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}({\boldsymbol{k}})^{-1}}=\mathcal{I}_{{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}}
.
Let
l^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}({\boldsymbol{k}})^{-1}
and let
l\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}
be such that
{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l^{\prime}}:={\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}+{\boldsymbol{k}}
.
Then

\displaystyle Q({\boldsymbol{R}}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l^{\prime}}}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}),T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l^{\prime}}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Q({\boldsymbol{R}}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}+{\boldsymbol{k}}}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}),T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Q({\boldsymbol{R}}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}){\boldsymbol{R}}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}(T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}),T_{\boldsymbol{k}}T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma_{\boldsymbol{k}}(Q)({\boldsymbol{R}}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}),T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P({\boldsymbol{R}}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}),T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0\,.

Thus,
Q\in\mathcal{I}_{{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}({\boldsymbol{k}})^{-1}}=\mathcal{I}_{{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}}
.
This
proves Fact
5
.

Fact 6
.

For all
{\boldsymbol{k}}\in V_{+}^{\perp}
,
\sigma_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\mathcal{I}_{{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}})\subset\mathcal{I}_{{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}}
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{k}}\in V_{+}^{\perp}
and
P\in\mathcal{I}_{{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}}
.
By Fact
3
, there is a decomposition of the form

{\boldsymbol{k}}:={\boldsymbol{a}}_{1}+\cdots+{\boldsymbol{a}}_{u}-{\boldsymbol{a}}_{u+1}-\cdots-{\boldsymbol{a}}_{v}

with
{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{a}}_{v}\in\mathcal{K}
.
Using recursively Fact
4
with
\sigma_{{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1}},\ldots,\sigma_{{\boldsymbol{a}}_{u}}
,
we deduce that

\sigma_{{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1}+\cdots+{\boldsymbol{a}}_{u}}(P)\in\mathcal{I}_{{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}}
.
On the other hand, we have

\sigma_{{\boldsymbol{a}}_{u+1}+\cdots+{\boldsymbol{a}}_{v}}(\sigma_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}(P))=\sigma_{{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1}+\cdots+{\boldsymbol{a}}_{u}}(P)\in\mathcal{I}_{{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}}\,.

Using recursively Fact
5
with
\sigma_{{\boldsymbol{a}}_{u+1}},\ldots,\sigma_{{\boldsymbol{a}}_{v}}
,
we obtain that
\sigma_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}(P)\in\mathcal{I}_{{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}}
.
This proves Fact
6
.

Fact 7
.

One has
\mathcal{I}_{{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}}\subset\mathcal{I}
.

Let
l_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
denote the smallest element in
{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}
.
Let
P\in\mathcal{I}_{{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}}
and let
l\geq l_{0}
be an integer.
By Lemma
6.3
,

{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}-{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l_{0}}\in V_{+}^{\perp}
.
Set
Q:=\sigma_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}-{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l_{0}}}(P)
.
By Fact
6
, we obtain that
Q\in\mathcal{I}_{{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}}
.
Since
l_{0}\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}
, we have

P({\boldsymbol{R}}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}),T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})=Q({\boldsymbol{R}}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l_{0}}}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}),T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l_{0}}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})=0\,.

Hence
P
vanishes at
({\boldsymbol{R}}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}),T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})
,
for all
l\geq l_{0}
.
This proves Fact
7
.

We are now ready to conclude the proof of Lemma
7.6
.
Let
P\in\mathcal{I}
,
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\in{\rm GL}_{t}(\mathbb{K})
be a relation
matrix, and
{\boldsymbol{k}}\in V_{+}^{\perp}
. Let
b({\boldsymbol{z}})\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[{\boldsymbol{z}}]
be a nonzero polynomial such that

b({\boldsymbol{z}})P(\boldsymbol{Y},{\boldsymbol{z}})\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[{\boldsymbol{z}},\boldsymbol{Y}]
. In particular,
b({\boldsymbol{z}})P(\boldsymbol{Y},{\boldsymbol{z}})\in\mathcal{I}\cap\mathcal{A}[\boldsymbol{Y}]
. Let
{\mathcal{Z}}
be the set of
integers
l\geq 0
for which
b(T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})P({\boldsymbol{\phi}}{\boldsymbol{R}}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}),T_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})=0
.
Since
bP\in\mathcal{I}
,
{\mathcal{Z}}
is full and hence piecewise syndetic. There thus exists a piecewise syndetic set

{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}\subset{\mathcal{Z}}
satisfying Facts
2
to
7
. By definition,

b({\boldsymbol{z}})P(\boldsymbol{Y},{\boldsymbol{z}})\in\mathcal{I}_{{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}}
. By Fact
6
, we also have

\sigma_{\boldsymbol{k}}(bP)\in\mathcal{I}_{{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}}
, for all
{\boldsymbol{k}}\in V_{+}^{\perp}
. By Fact
7
, we deduce that

\sigma_{\boldsymbol{k}}(bP)\in\mathcal{I}
. Then, we infer from Lemma
7.4
that

b(T_{\boldsymbol{k}}{\boldsymbol{z}})P({\boldsymbol{\phi}}{\boldsymbol{R}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}({\boldsymbol{z}}),T_{\boldsymbol{k}}{\boldsymbol{z}})=\sigma_{\boldsymbol{k}}(bP)({\boldsymbol{\phi}},{\boldsymbol{z}})=0\,.

Since
T_{\boldsymbol{k}}
is nonsingular and
b({\boldsymbol{z}})\not=0
, we obtain

P({\boldsymbol{\phi}}{\boldsymbol{R}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}({\boldsymbol{z}}),T_{\boldsymbol{k}}{\boldsymbol{z}})=0
.
∎

7.3.
Analyticity of relation matrices

Let
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
be a relation matrix.
All coordinates of
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
being algebraic over

Lemma 8.3

Let us first briefly describe the general strategy we use for proving this key lemma.
The scheme of the proof is classical and takes its source in the early work of Mahler

It was gradually improved and refined by Kubota

[

Assuming by contradiction that

Appendix A
Representing numbers in independent bases

In this appendix, we show how our main results apply to certain problems concerning expansions
of numbers in multiplicatively independent bases. In particular, we state and prove
Conjectures
A.2
,
A.3
, and Corollary
A.4
,
which were our initial goal.
All these results are deduced from Theorem
1.1
.

A.1.
The dynamical point of view: Furstenberg’s conjecture

In the late 1960s, Furstenberg
[
26
,
27
]

suggested a series of conjectures whose aim is to capture
the heuristic which has been alluded to only in very vague terms at the beginning of this paper.
These conjectures, which became famous, take place in a dynamical setting.
This does not come as a great surprise for there is a well-known dictionary
transferring combinatorial properties of
the expansion of a real number
x
in an integer base
q
in terms of
dynamical properties of the orbit of
\{x\}
under the map
T_{q}
defined on

\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
by
x\mapsto qx
.
We let
\mathcal{O}_{q}(x)
denote the forward orbit of
x
under
T_{q}
, that is,

\mathcal{O}_{q}(x):=\left\{x,T_{q}(x),T^{2}_{q}(x),\ldots\right\}\,.

If
X\subset\mathbb{R}
, we let
\dim_{H}(X)
denote the Hausdorff dimension of
X

and
\overline{X}
its closure.
One of Furstenberg’s conjecture
[
27
]
reads as follows.

Conjecture A.1

(Furstenberg)
.

Let
p
and
q
be two multiplicatively independent natural numbers, and
let
x\in[0,1)
be a real number. Then

\dim_{H}\overline{\mathcal{O}_{p}(x)}+\dim_{H}\overline{\mathcal{O}_{q}(x)}\geq 1\,,

unless
x
is rational.

This conjecture has wonderful consequences about expansions of both real and natural numbers.
It beautifully expresses the expected balance between the complexity of expansions of an irrational real number
in two multiplicatively independent bases:

If
x
has a simple expansion in base
p
, then it should have a complex expansion in base
q
.

It is easy to see that Conjecture
A.1
holds true generically. Indeed,
endowed with the Haar measure, the topological dynamical system

(T_{q},\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})
becomes ergodic,
and it follows from the ergodic theorem that

\dim_{H}\overline{\mathcal{O}_{p}(x)}=\dim_{H}\overline{\mathcal{O}_{q}(x)}=1\,,

for almost all real numbers
x
in
[0,1)
.
In fact, all the strength of Conjecture
A.1
takes shape when
x
has a
simple expansion in one of the two bases.
Defining the entropy of
x
with respect to the base
q

as the topological entropy of the
dynamical system
(T_{q},\overline{\mathcal{O}_{q}(x)})
,
Conjecture
A.1
implies that if
x
has zero entropy in base
p
, then it has a dense orbit under

T_{q}
.

Let us illustrate this with a concrete example. The binary Thue-Morse number
\tau
is defined as follows.
Its
n
th binary digit is equal to
0
if the sum of digits in the binary expansion of
n
is even,
and to
1
otherwise. It is somewhat puzzling that its decimal expansion

\langle\tau\rangle_{10}=0.412\,454\,033\,640\,107\,597\,783\,361\,368\,258\,455\,283\,089\cdots

seems unpredictable, while its binary expansion

\langle\tau\rangle_{2}=0.011\,010\,011\,001\,011\,010\,010\,110\,011\,010\,011\,001\,011\cdots\,

is, by definition, so regular.
This intriguing phenomenon would be nicely explained
by Conjecture
A.1
.
Indeed, since
\tau
has zero entropy in base
2
,
it should have a dense orbit under
T_{10}
, meaning that
all blocks of digits should occur in its decimal expansion.

Other astonishing consequences of Conjecture
A.1
concern expansions of natural numbers.
For instance, using an elementary construction, Furstenberg shows in
[
27
]
how to deduce from
Conjecture
A.1
that any finite block of digits
occurs in the decimal expansion of
2^{n}
, as soon as
n
is large enough.
Note that, in the same vein, a conjecture of Erdös claims that the digit
2
occurs in the ternary
expansion of
2^{n}
for all
n>8
(see, for instance,
[
32
]
).

Recently, Shmerkin
[
58
]
and Wu
[
61
]
proved that
the set of exceptions to Conjecture
A.1
has Hausdorff dimension zero.
Unfortunately, this remarkable results does not tell us anything about expansions of real numbers
with zero entropy in some base. Indeed,
the set of all such real numbers
has Hausdorff dimension zero
[
46
]
. Though the works of Shmerkin and Wu mark
significant progress towards Conjecture
A.1
, the latter remains far out of the reach of current methods.
Even worse, we are afraid that their result could be essentially the best dynamical methods
have to say about this conjecture.

A.2.
The computational point of view: from finite automata to Mahler’s method

From a computational point of view, there is another relevant notion of
simple number, namely the notion of
automatic real number
(see
[
12
, Chap. 13]
).
While computable numbers can be generated by general Turing machines,
automatic numbers are those whose expansion in some base
can be generated by a finite automaton. Broadly speaking, a finite automaton is a Turing machine
without any memory tape, all its memory being stored in the finite state control.
This severe restriction justifies that these numbers are considered as especially simple.
For example, the Thue-Morse number
\tau
is automatic in base
2
.
We refer the reader to
[
5
]
and the references therein for a discussion on these different models
of computation. In this new framework, our general heuristic naturally leads to the following conjecture.

Conjecture A.2
.

Let
p
and
q
be two multiplicatively independent natural
numbers. A real number cannot be automatic in both bases
p
and
q
, unless it is rational.

This conjecture turns out to be a very special case of Conjecture
A.1
for
being automatic in some base implies having zero entropy in that base.
Nevertheless, Conjecture
A.2
remains quite challenging since,
to date, not a single real number has been proved to be at once automatic in some base and
not automatic in another one. We also mention that a weaker version of Conjecture
A.2

appears as Open Problems 7 in
[
12
, Chap. 13]
.

With a more Diophantine flavor, Conjecture

A.2
can be strongly strengthened as follows.

Conjecture A.3
.

Let
r\geq 1
be an integer. Let
b_{1},\ldots,b_{r}
be multiplicatively independent positive integers,
and, for every
i
,
1\leq i\leq r
,
let
\xi_{i}
be a real number that is automatic in base
b_{i}
.
Then the numbers
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{r}
are algebraically independent over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
, unless one of them is rational.

Conjecture
A.3
is not implied by Furstenberg’s conjecture. The former
does not only imply that the
Thue-Morse number
\tau
cannot be automatic in base
10
, but also
that this is the case for any number obtained from
\tau
by using algebraic numbers and algebraic
operations
(addition, multiplication, division, taking
n
th roots…).
The case
r=1
was a long-standing conjecture first proved by Bugeaud and the first author

[
4
]
by means of the subspace theorem.
See also
[
8
,
53
]
for a recent different proof based on Mahler’s method.
So far, Conjecture
A.3
has only be settled in that particular case.

A.2.1.
Connection with Mahler’s method and Theorem

In 1968, Cobham
[
20
]
first noticed the following fundamental connection
between automatic sequences and
M

If the sequence
(a_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is

-automatic, then the generating function

is a
q
-Mahler function
.
In turn, problems about transcendence and algebraic independence of
automatic real numbers can be translated and extended to problems concerning
transcendence and algebraic independence of

-functions at algebraic points.
In particular, Conjecture

and hence Conjecture

, easily follow from Part (i) of Theorem

Proof of Conjecture

Replacing

By assumption, the number
\xi_{i}
is automatic in base

This means that, for some integer

, there exists a

-automatic sequence

with values in

, where

f_{i}(z):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{i,n}z^{n}\in\mathbb{Q}\{z\}
. A discussed in
[
20
]
, the fact that

is

-automatic implies that

is a
q_{i}
-Mahler function.
Now, let us assume that

are all irrational. By
[
8
, Corollaire 1.8]

we obtain that these numbers are all transcendental.
Since by assumption the numbers

are multiplicatively independent,

implies that the numbers

∎

As with Furstenberg’s conjecture, Theorem
1.1

has also valuable consequences about expansions of natural numbers.
Let us first recall that a set

is

when written in base

can be recognized by a finite automaton (See, for instance,
[

In this framework, there is a famous theorem by Cobham
[

that can be stated as follows.

is both

where

and

is a periodic set, meaning that

is the union of a finite set and finitely many arithmetic progressions.

Cobham’s theorem can be rephrased in terms of power series.
Indeed, it is equivalent to the fact that, given any aperiodic

\mathcal{E}_{p}
and any

q

In 1987, Loxton and van der Poorten
[

conjectured the following generalization:

cannot be both

This conjecture was first proved by Bell and the first author in
[

a different proof was given by Schäfke and Singer
[

. Very recently, the authors of

[
7

even proved a stronger result also conjectured by Loxton and van der Poorten
[

]
:
a

, unless one of them is rational.
This result refines Cobham’s theorem by expressing, in algebraic terms, the discrepancy between
aperiodic automatic sets associated with
multiplicatively independent input bases. The proof given in
[
7

is based on a
suitable parametrized Galois theory associated with linear Mahler equations and follows the strategy
initiated in

[
6
]
.

Part (ii) of Theorem
