Abstract
This paper investigates the performance of a likelihood ratio test in combination with a polynomial subspace projection approach to detect weak transient signals in broadband array data. Based on previous empirical evidence that a likelihood ratio test is advantageously applied in a lower-dimensional subspace, we present analysis that highlights how the polynomial subspace projection whitens a crucial part of the signals, enabling a detector to operate with a shortened temporal window. This reduction in temporal correlation, together with a spatial compaction of the data, also leads to both computational and numerical advantages over a likelihood ratio test that is directly applied to the array data. The results of our analysis are illustrated by examples and simulations.

For the weak transient signal detection

II
Space-Time Covariance and Polynomial Subspace Approach

II-A

Signal Model and Space-Time Covariance

To expand on the signal model of Sec.
I
in
Fig.
1
, we assume that in the stationary case,
contributions by
L
sources are received by
M>L
sensors via a
convolutive mixing matrix
{\mathbf{H}}[n]\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times L}
.
The sources, gathered in a signal vector
{\mathbf{u}}[n]\in\mathbb{C}^{L}
, are mutually independent, temporally uncorrelated,
with zero mean and unit variance signals
u_{\ell}[n]
,

\ell=1,\dotsc,L
, such that
{\mathbf{u}}[n]=[u_{1}[n],\dotsc,u_{L}[n]]^{\mathrm{T}}\sim\mathcal{CN}({\underline{0}},{\mathbf{I}}_{L})
.
Any specific source power spectral densities are captured in the model
of Fig.
1
via the convolutive mixing matrix

{\mathbf{H}}[n]
, which also operates as an innovation
filter
[
26
]
. The data received from the sources is
corrupted by additive complex Gaussian noise
{\mathbf{v}}[n]\sim\mathcal{CN}({\underline{0}},\sigma^{2}_{v}{\mathbf{I}}_{M})
. A transient signal
potentially illuminates the array via a vector of filters

{\mathbf{h}}_{\mathrm{t}}[n]\in\mathbb{C}^{M}
to another uncorrelated signal

u_{L+1}[n]\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,\sigma^{2}_{t})
, whose presence we would like
to detect.

For the space-time covariance, based on the expectation
{\mathcal{E}}\!\left\{\cdot\right\}

and Hermitian transpose operator
\{\cdot\}^{\mathrm{H}}
, we have

{\mathbf{R}}[\tau]={\mathcal{E}}\!\left\{{\mathbf{x}}[n]{\mathbf{x}}^{\mathrm{H}}[n]\right\}
of the
measurement vector
{\mathbf{x}}[n]
. We first consider the absence of
the transient signal, i.e.
u_{L+1}[n]=0
. In this case, for the
cross-spectral density (CSD)
{\boldsymbol{R}}(z)=\sum_{\tau}{\mathbf{R}}[\tau]z^{-\tau}
, or short
{\mathbf{R}}[\tau]\mbox{$\;\circ\hskip-0.5pt\rule[2.0pt]{10.0pt}{0.5pt}\!\bullet\;$}{\boldsymbol{R}}(z)
, we obtain

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{R}}(z)={\boldsymbol{H}}(z){\boldsymbol{H}}^{\mathrm{P}}\!(z)+\sigma^{2}_{v}{\mathbf{I}}_{M}\;.

(1)

In (
1
),
{\boldsymbol{H}}(z)\mbox{$\;\bullet\!\rule[2.0pt]{10.0pt}{0.5pt}\hskip-0.5pt\circ\;$}{\mathbf{H}}[n]

represents a matrix of transfer functions. The parahermitian operator

\{\cdot\}^{\mathrm{P}}\!
implies a Hermitian transposition and time reversal,
such that
{\boldsymbol{H}}^{\mathrm{P}}\!(z)=\{{\boldsymbol{H}}(1/z^{\ast})\}^{\mathrm{H}}

[
27
]
. Hence,

{\boldsymbol{R}}(z)
is a parahermitian matrix that satisfies

{\boldsymbol{R}}^{\mathrm{P}}\!(z)={\boldsymbol{R}}(z)
.

If
u_{L+1}[n]\neq 0
, then this signal’s contribution to the overall
CSD matrix is

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(z)={\boldsymbol{h}}_{\mathrm{t}}(z){\boldsymbol{h}}_{\mathrm{t}}^{\mathrm{P}}\!(z)\;,

(2)

with
{\boldsymbol{h}}_{\mathrm{t}}(z)\mbox{$\;\bullet\!\rule[2.0pt]{10.0pt}{0.5pt}\hskip-0.5pt\circ\;$}{\boldsymbol{h}}[n]
. The overall CSD of
the measurement vector
{\mathbf{x}}[n]
thus is
{\boldsymbol{R}}(z)+{\boldsymbol{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(z)
, whereby
{\boldsymbol{R}}_{\mathrm{t}}(z)
is a rank one
contribution.

Analytic Eigenvalue Decomposition

Given the signal model in Fig.

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{R}}(z)

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{Q}}(z){\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}(z){\boldsymbol{Q}}^{\mathrm{P}}\!(z)\;.

In (

{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}(z)=\mbox{diag}\!\left\{\lambda_{1}(z),\dotsc,\lambda_{M}(z)\right\}
is a diagonal parahermitian
matrix holding the analytic eigenvalues
\lambda_{m}(z)
,
m=1,\dotsc,M

of
{\boldsymbol{R}}(z)
. Their corresponding eigenvectors form the columns
of
{\boldsymbol{Q}}(z)
, which is also analytic in

and satisfies
paraunitarity, such that
{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{\mathrm{P}}\!(z){\boldsymbol{Q}}(z)={\boldsymbol{Q}}(z){\boldsymbol{Q}}^{\mathrm{P}}\!(z)={\mathbf{I}}_{M}
,and therefore

Analyticity of the factors in (

as this property permits to approximate

closely by polynomials of sufficient order by shift and truncation

33
,
34

Based on (

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}(z)

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{H}(z)+\sigma^{2}_{v}{\mathbf{I}}_{L}&\\
&\sigma^{2}_{v}{\mathbf{I}}_{M-L}\end{array}\right]\;,

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{Q}}(z)

\displaystyle=[{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{\parallel}(z),\;\;{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{\perp}(z)]\;,

(6)

where

In practice, analytic EVD algorithms

,

,

,

,

,

,

II-C

Subspace Projection

With the partitioning of the analytic SVD factors in (
II-B
)
and (
6
),
{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{\perp}(z)\mbox{$\;\bullet\!\rule[2.0pt]{10.0pt}{0.5pt}\hskip-0.5pt\circ\;$}{\mathbf{Q}}_{\perp}[n]

can be used to project the measurement data

into the

\displaystyle{\mathbf{s}}[n]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\nu}{\mathbf{Q}}^{\mathrm{H}}_{\perp}[-\nu]{\mathbf{x}}[n-\nu]\;,

where
{\mathbf{s}}[n]\in\mathbb{C}^{M-L}
is the projected data, as
shown in Fig.

. In the ideal case, this new signal

is now free of any contributions from the

]

[

]

or noise

]

Such polynomial subspace decompositions have also been applied, for

[

III
Likelihood Ratio Test

We now follow the approach in
[
7
]
, where a likelihood
ratio approach can be applied to either the measurement data

{\mathbf{x}}[n]
or to its subspace projection
{\mathbf{s}}[n]
. We
first briefly comment on the LRT formulation before we focus on the
two application cases.

III-A

Likelihood Ratio Test

For a general exploration of the likelihood ratio test, we utilise a
test variable
{\mathbf{y}}_{n}\in\mathbb{C}^{K}
, which can later be
constructed from measurement vectors
{\mathbf{x}}[n]
or syndrome
vectors
{\mathbf{s}}[n]
, including a concatenation of temporal
snapshots. The dimension
K
will therefore depend on this choice.
We assume that
{\mathbf{y}}_{n}
can consist of
{\mathbf{y}}_{0,n}\in\mathbb{C}^{K}
, which is the stationary noise, and

{\mathbf{y}}_{1,n}\in\mathbb{C}^{K}
, representing the transient
component. Both signals are assumed to be zero mean complex Gaussian
with
{\mathbf{y}}_{0,n}\sim\mathcal{CN}({\underline{0}},{\mathbf{R}}_{0})
and

{\mathbf{y}}_{1,n}\sim\mathcal{CN}({\underline{0}},{\mathbf{R}}_{1})
. The aim
is thus to distinguish between the two hypotheses

\displaystyle H_{0}:

\displaystyle\quad{\mathbf{y}}_{n}={\mathbf{y}}_{0,n}\;,

\displaystyle H_{1}:

\displaystyle\quad{\mathbf{y}}_{n}={\mathbf{y}}_{0,n}+{\mathbf{y}}_{1,n}\;.

In our context,
{\mathbf{y}}_{0,n}
holds the contribution from the
L

stationary sources and additive noise, while
{\mathbf{y}}_{1,n}

comprises of components due to the transient signal. The probability
density functions for
{\mathbf{y}}_{n}
, under the two hypotheses are
then given by

\displaystyle p({\mathbf{y}}_{n}|H_{0})

\displaystyle=(2\pi|{\mathbf{R}}_{0}|)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{2}{\mathbf{y}}^{\mathrm{H}}_{n}{\mathbf{R}}_{0}^{-1}{\mathbf{y}}_{n}}\;,

(8)

\displaystyle p({\mathbf{y}}_{n}|H_{1})

\displaystyle=(2\pi|{\mathbf{R}}_{0}+{\mathbf{R}}_{1}|)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{2}{\mathbf{y}}^{\mathrm{H}}_{n}({\mathbf{R}}_{0}+{\mathbf{R}}_{1})^{-1}{\mathbf{y}}_{n}}\;,

(9)

where the determinant of a matrix
{\mathbf{X}}
is denoted as

|{\mathbf{X}}|
.

For the likelihood ratio
L({\mathbf{y}}_{n})
, we have

\displaystyle L({\mathbf{y}}_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{p({\mathbf{y}}_{n}|H_{0})}{p({\mathbf{y}}_{n}|H_{1})}=\frac{|{\mathbf{R}}_{0}+{\mathbf{R}}_{1}|^{\frac{1}{2}}}{|{\mathbf{R}}_{0}|^{\frac{1}{2}}}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{2}{\mathbf{y}}^{\mathrm{H}}_{n}{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{y}}_{n}}\;,

(10)

where for brevity

\displaystyle{\mathbf{A}}={\mathbf{R}}^{-1}_{0}-({\mathbf{R}}_{0}+{\mathbf{R}}_{1})^{-1}\;.

(11)

With its EVD
{\mathbf{A}}={\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{\Lambda}}{\mathbf{Q}}^{\mathrm{H}}
, the likelihood ratio can be further expressed
as

\displaystyle L({\mathbf{y}}_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{|{\mathbf{R}}_{0}+{\mathbf{R}}_{1}|^{\frac{1}{2}}}{|{\mathbf{R}}_{0}|^{\frac{1}{2}}}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{2}\|{\mathbf{\Lambda}}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\mathbf{Q}}^{\mathrm{H}}{\mathbf{y}}_{n}\|^{2}_{2}}\;.

(12)

In order to accept or reject the hypothesis, we now need to find a
threshold
c
such that

\displaystyle L({\mathbf{y}}_{n})\overset{H_{0}}{\underset{H_{1}}{\lessgtr}}c\;.

(13)

Rearranging leads to

\displaystyle\|{\mathbf{\Lambda}}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\mathbf{Q}}^{\mathrm{H}}{\mathbf{y}}_{n}\|\overset{H_{0}}{\underset{H_{1}}{\lessgtr}}2\ln\left\{\frac{|{\mathbf{R}}_{0}|^{\frac{1}{2}}}{|{\mathbf{R}}_{0}+{\mathbf{R}}_{1}|^{\frac{1}{2}}}c\right\}=c^{\prime}\;,

(14)

where
c^{\prime}
is a modified threshold. With the term

\|{\mathbf{\Lambda}}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\mathbf{Q}}^{\mathrm{H}}{\mathbf{y}}_{n}\|
, we have
defined the test statistic for the likelihood ratio test.

III-B

Likelihood Ratio Test on Measurements

Applying the LRT directly to the measurement data in principle
exploits all information that we can possibly draw from it. For the
purpose of temporal averaging the test outcome, for the LRT variable

{\mathbf{y}}_{n}
we utilise a concatenation of
T
snapshots of

{\mathbf{x}}[n]
,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{y}}^{\mathrm{H}}_{n}=[{\mathbf{x}}^{\mathrm{H}}_{n},{\mathbf{x}}^{\mathrm{H}}_{n-1},\dotsc,{\mathbf{x}}^{\mathrm{H}}_{n-T+1}]\;,

(15)

such that

For the covariance matrices in (

), we introduce
extra subscripts to denote their reference to the measurement vector
via
{\mathbf{R}}_{{\mathbf{x}},0}
and
{\mathbf{R}}_{{\mathbf{x}},1}
. For
the covariance covering the hypothesis

, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbf{R}}_{{\mathbf{x}},0}

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\mathbf{R}}[0]&\ldots&{\mathbf{R}}[T-1]\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
{\mathbf{R}}[1-T]&\ldots&{\mathbf{R}}[0]\end{array}\right]\;,

where (
1
) defines
{\mathbf{R}}[\tau]\mbox{$\;\circ\hskip-0.5pt\rule[2.0pt]{10.0pt}{0.5pt}\!\bullet\;$}{\boldsymbol{R}}(z)
. Similarly,
{\mathbf{R}}_{{\mathbf{x}},1}
can be
constructed from the lag components of

in
(

). Both covariance matrices

III-C

Likelihood Ratio Test on Subspace Data

In order to apply the likelihood ratio test to
T
successive
samples of the projected data vector
{\mathbf{s}}[n]
, we define
analogously to (

\displaystyle{\mathbf{y}}^{\mathrm{H}}_{n}=[{\mathbf{s}}^{\mathrm{H}}_{n},{\mathbf{s}}^{\mathrm{H}}_{n-1},\dotsc,{\mathbf{s}}^{\mathrm{H}}_{n-T+1}]\;,

where now

For its space-time covariance

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{R}}^{\prime}(z)

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{Q}}^{\mathrm{P}}\!_{\perp}(z){\boldsymbol{R}}(z){\boldsymbol{Q}}_{\perp}(z)\;,

(

). Analogously to (

), we can obtain a

{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{t}}[n]\mbox{$\;\circ\hskip-0.5pt\rule[2.0pt]{10.0pt}{0.5pt}\!\bullet\;$}{\boldsymbol{R}}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{t}}(z)

Overall, in the subspace-based case we now have covariance matrices

III-D

Application and Generalised LRT

The application of the above LRT test to data typically assumes that
the covariance matrices under the two hypothesis are known —

{\mathbf{R}}_{0}
for
H_{0}
, and the composite
({\mathbf{R}}_{0}+{\mathbf{R}}_{1})
for
H_{1}
— independent of whether these are derived
from the measurements or the subspace-projected data. The covariance
matrices represent the exact ensemble statistics, i.e. the source
model of Fig.
1
is known a priori. In practise, where
only finite data is available to estimate the statistics, an estimated
space-time covariance
\hat{{\boldsymbol{R}}}(z)
will be subject to
estimation errors that depend on both the sample size of the data, as
well as on the ground truth
{\boldsymbol{R}}(z)

[
41
,
42
,
43
,
44
]
, thus resulting in estimates

\hat{{\mathbf{R}}}_{0}
and
(\hat{{\mathbf{R}}}_{0}+\hat{{\mathbf{R}}}_{1})
.
This reliance on potentially inaccurate estimates turns the LRT into a
generalised likelihood ratio test (GLRT).

Recall that the aim of this paper is to explore the optimality of the
test. For our application outlined in Sec.
I
, we have an
estimate
\hat{{\mathbf{R}}}_{0}
, but we are not able to measure

\hat{{\mathbf{R}}}_{1}
, neither by itself or in combination with

\hat{{\mathbf{R}}}_{0}
, ahead of performing a change point
detection. Knowing what is optimally achievable given either

{\mathbf{R}}_{i}
or
\hat{{\mathbf{R}}}_{i}
provide a useful benchmark
for
[
11
]
and applications such as
[
13
,
12
]
.

IV
Analysis of Subspace-Based LRT

In this section we explore properties of the covariance matrices

{\mathbf{R}}_{{\mathbf{x}},i}
,
i=0,1
, for the measurements and of

{\mathbf{R}}_{{\mathbf{s}},i}
,
i=0,1
, for the projected data, that
feed into the LRT discussed in Sec.

.

IV-A

Consideration of Temporal Correlation

We first focus on the hypothesis
H_{0}
, where only
L
stationary
signals are present. In this case, if the mixing system

{\boldsymbol{H}}(z)
consists of FIR filters of length
(J+1)
, i.e. it is a
polynomial matrix of order
J
, then
{\boldsymbol{R}}(z)
is a Laurent
polynomial matrix of order
2J
. Generally, this matrix will be dense
in the sense that generally all its coefficients
{\mathbf{R}}[\tau]

for
|\tau|\leq J
will have non-zero elements. As a result, for the
consideration of a temporal window
T<J
in the LRT,

{\mathbf{R}}_{{\mathbf{x}},0}
will be a dense matrix. Only for the case

T>J
will we start to see zero corner blocks to appear.

Inspecting the projected data under hypothesis
H_{0}
, we have

{\boldsymbol{R}}^{\prime}(z)
given by (

IV-B

Consideration of Covariance Matrix Conditioning

We now want to assess how the computation of the inverses for

{\mathbf{R}}_{0}
and
({\mathbf{R}}_{0}+{\mathbf{R}}_{1})
are affected by
whether we operate the LRT on the measurements or the projected data.
We assess this via the condition number of a matrix
[
67
]
,
which assesses the gain w.r.t. any random perturbations —
e.g. through estimation errors
[
42
]
— in the inversion
process.

For the measurement case, the condition number of

{\mathbf{R}}_{{\mathbf{x}},0}
can be related to the eigenvalue
spread
[
17
]
of the signals, which assesses the ratio
between the minimum and maximum spectral value and here additionally
has a spatial component. For the maximum eigenvalue, we have

\displaystyle\max\{\lambda_{{\mathbf{x}},0}\}

\displaystyle=\max_{\Omega,m}\lambda\{{\boldsymbol{R}}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}\Omega})\}

(28)

\displaystyle\approx\max_{\Omega,m}\lambda\{{\boldsymbol{H}}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}\Omega}){\boldsymbol{H}}^{\mathrm{P}}\!(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}\Omega})\}>\sigma^{2}_{s}\;,

(29)

where
\lambda\{{\boldsymbol{R}}(z)\}
returns the analytic eigenvalues of
the parahermitian matrix
{\boldsymbol{R}}(z)

[
28
]
, and

\sigma^{2}_{s}
is the maximum power of a stationary source in a measurement

x_{m}[n]
, assuming for the SNR
\sigma^{2}_{s}/\sigma^{2}_{v}\gg 1
. For
the smallest eigenvalue, the noise floor in the noise-only subspace
will be given by
\sigma^{2}_{v}
, such that we obtain

\displaystyle\gamma_{{\mathbf{x}},0}>\sigma^{2}_{s}/\sigma^{2}_{v}

(30)

for the condition number under
H_{0}
. Under
H_{1}
, the maximum
eigenvalue is still due to a strong stationary component, while the
minimum possible eigenvalue remains given by the noise floor. Hence,
we have approximately
\gamma_{{\mathbf{x}},1}=\gamma_{{\mathbf{x}},0}
.

For the case of projected data, ideally the stationary signals are no
longer present. In this case, the maximum eigenvalue is given by the

\displaystyle\max\{\lambda_{{\mathbf{s}},0}\}

\displaystyle=\max_{\Omega,m}\lambda\{{\boldsymbol{R}}^{\prime}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}\Omega})\}

(31)

\displaystyle=\max_{\Omega,m}\lambda\{{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\mathrm{t}}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}\Omega}){\boldsymbol{H}}^{\mathrm{P}}\!_{\mathrm{t}}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}\Omega})+\sigma^{2}_{v}{\mathbf{I}}_{T(M-L)}\}

\displaystyle>\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{t}}+\sigma^{2}_{v}\;,

(32)

where
\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{t}}
represents the maximum power of the transient
signal in any of the measurement signals
x_{m}[n]
. Note that this
power is not increased when passing through
{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{\mathrm{P}}\!(z)
, since
this matrix completes to a paraunitary and therefore energy-preserving
system. Since the smallest eigenvalue is still limited by the noise
floor, we therefore have
\gamma_{{\mathbf{s}},0}>1
and

\displaystyle\gamma_{{\mathbf{s}},1}>\frac{\sigma^{2}_{t}+\sigma^{2}_{v}}{\sigma^{2}_{v}}

(33)

for the condition number under hypotheses
H_{0}
and
H_{1}
. Note that
in the case that the transient signal is significantly weaker than the
stationary signals, the covariance matrices for the projected data
have a much lower bound for the condition number and generally will be
much better conditioned than their equivalent quantities based on the
measurement data.

VI
Discussion and Conclusions

To detect a weak broadband transient signal, we have investigated the
application of a likelihood ratio test to polynomial
subspace-projected data rather than directly to the measurements.
While for the stronger transient signals, the latter approach is
optimal as it exploits all available information about the transient
signal, the restriction to the noise-only subspace in the case of
weaker transient signals had been shown to be beneficial. The subspace
project approach ideally suppresses any strong stationary source
components. This firstly removes any strong temporal correlations,
which hinder the test, and secondly also significantly reduce the
condition number of the covariance matrices that the LRT requires to
be inverted, hence providing. As a result, the subspace-projected LRT
operates on matrices that are decreased in both their dimension and
condition number. This effect is the more pronouced the stronger the
temporal correlations of the measured signals are and the weaker the
transient signal is compared to the stationary sources.
