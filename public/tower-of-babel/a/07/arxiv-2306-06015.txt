Normalized solutions to Schrödinger equations in the strongly sublinear regime
Jarosław Mederski, Jacopo Schino

Abstract
. The behaviour of g g at the origin is allowed to be strongly sublinear, i.e., lim s → 0 g ⁡ ( s ) / s = − ∞ \lim_{s\to 0}g(s)/s=-\infty , which includes the case g ⁡ ( s ) = α ​ s ​ ln ⁡ s 2 + μ ​ | s | p − 2 ​ s g(s)=\alpha s\ln s^{2}+\mu|s|^{p-2}s with α > 0 \alpha>0 and μ ∈ ℝ \mu\in\mathbb{R} , 2 < p ≤ 2 ∗ 2<p\leq 2^{*} properly chosen. Introduction

We look for solutions
(u,\lambda)\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\times\mathbb{R}
to the following equation

(1.1)

-\Delta u+\lambda u=g(u)\quad\mathrm{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{N},\ N\geq 2

paired with the constraint

(1.2)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|u|^{2}\,dx=\rho^{2},

where
\rho>0
is fixed and
g
satisfies suitable assumptions (see (g0)–(g4) below). Equations like (
1.1
) arise when one looks for standing-wave solutions to the evolution equation

(1.3)

\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}\Psi+\Delta\Psi+g(\Psi)=0,

i.e.,
\Psi(t,x)=e^{\mathrm{i}\lambda t}u(x)
. The condition (
1.2
), instead, is motivated by the property that, formally, the quantity

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\Psi(t,x)|^{2}\,dx

is conserved in time. Moreover, the
L^{2}
-norm squared of a solution to (
1.3
) has a precise physical meaning in the contexts which this equation is derived from: it represents, e.g., the power supply in nonlinear optics and the total number of atoms in the Bose–Einstein condensation.
The model for
g
that inspires this paper is

(1.4)

g(s)=s\ln s^{2},

which was introduced in
[
4
]
to obtain the
separability of non-interacting subsystems
; observe that, in this case,

\lim_{s\to 0}\frac{g(s)}{s}=-\infty,

which is known in the literature as the
strongly sublinear
(or
infinite-mass
) regime. After that, both (
1.1
) – possibly with an external potential – and (
1.3
), in both cases with
g
as in (
1.4
), raised great interest: we mention
[
13
,
31
,
33
,
34
,
35
]
for the former context and
[
8
,
9
,
11
,
14
]
(see also
[
10
, Section 9.3]
) for the latter one. The common feature of most of the articles above is that the authors can work in the subspace of
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})

{\mathcal{H}}:=\left\{u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N}):\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}u^{2}|\ln u^{2}|\,dx<\infty\right\},

where the energy functional (see (
1.7
) below) is of class
{\mathcal{C}}^{1}
(we mention that, instead, the approach of
[
13
]
is based on critical point theory for lower semi-continuous functionals, while that of
[
14
]
on a truncation argument). Of course, this strategy is impossible with generic nonlinearities as in this paper; speaking of which, generic nonlinear terms were recently considered in
[
15
,
24
]
.

Concerning normalized solutions, i.e., in the presence of (
1.2
), to the best of our knowledge, they are only treated in two very recent pieces of work: in
[
30
]
, with

(1.5)

g(s)=\alpha s\ln s^{2}+\mu|s|^{p-2}s,

where
\alpha,\mu\in\mathbb{R}
and
2<p\leq 2^{*}
, and in
[
37
]
, which mainly concerns systems of two equations. It is worth mentioning that, when
g
is as in (
1.4
), if
(v,0)
solves (
1.1
) and
v\neq 0
, then
(u,\lambda)
given by

u=\frac{\rho}{|v|_{2}}v,\quad\lambda=2\ln\left(\frac{\rho}{|v|_{2}}\right)

is a solution to (
1.1
)–(
1.2
). Nonetheless, the approach of
[
30
,
37
]
still makes use of
{\mathcal{H}}
. In particular, to recover compactness, the authors consider the subspace of radially symmetric functions as in
[
9
,
11
]
, which embeds compactly into
L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
for every
q\in[2,2^{*})
; this is different from the classical result concerning the subspace of radial functions in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, which embeds compactly into
L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
only for
q\in(2,2^{*})
. The additional compact embedding into
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
proves to be extremely useful owing to the constraint (
1.2
); in particular, this is what allows the authors to work with every
\rho>0
when
2<p<2+4/N
, where
p
is the same as in (
1.5
). Once again, here we cannot utilize the compact embedding into
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
because
g
need not be given by (
1.5
).

The function
g
in (
1.1
) satisfies the following assumptions. Define
G(s)=\int_{0}^{s}g(t)\,dt
and

(1.6)

G_{+}(s)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\int_{0}^{s}\max\{g(t),0\}\,dt&\text{if }s\geq 0,\\
\displaystyle\int_{s}^{0}\max\{-g(t),0\}\,dt&\text{if }s<0.\end{cases}

(g0)

g\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is continuous and
g(0)=0
.

(g1)

\lim_{s\to 0}G_{+}(s)/|s|^{2}=0
and
\limsup_{s\to 0}g_{+}(s)/s<\infty
, where
g_{+}=G_{+}^{\prime}
.

(g2)

If
N\geq 3
, then
\limsup_{|s|\to\infty}|g(s)|/|s|^{2^{*}-1}<\infty
, where
2^{*}=\frac{2N}{N-2}
.

If
N=2
, then
\lim_{|s|\to\infty}g(s)/e^{\alpha s^{2}}=0
for all
\alpha>4\pi
.

(g3)

\lim_{|s|\to\infty}G_{+}(s)/|s|^{2+4/N}=0
.

(g4)

There exists
\xi_{0}\neq 0
such that
G(\xi_{0})>0
.

The assumptions (g0), (g2), and (g4) are classical in the context of elliptic problems (cf.
[
2
,
3
]
), while (g1) is a weaker version of what is normally assumed in the presence of
L^{2}
-constraints, i.e.,
\lim_{s\to 0}g(s)/s=0
; (g3) indicates that the growth of
G_{+}
is mass-subcritical at infinity. With these assumptions, we can deal with nonlinearities that are much more general than (
1.5
); for example, we can consider functions
g
that behave like
-|s|^{\omega-1}s
or
|s|^{\omega-1}s\ln s^{2}
for
|s|
small, with
0<\omega<1
. We can also cover the case when
g
goes to
0
more slowly than any power, e.g., when
g
behaves like
1/\ln s^{2}
for
|s|
small.

Let us define

{\mathcal{D}}:=\left\{u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N}):\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|u|^{2}\,dx\leq\rho^{2}\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathcal{S}}:=\left\{u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N}):\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|u|^{2}\,dx=\rho^{2}\right\}.

Sometimes, we will write explicitly
{\mathcal{D}}(\rho)
and
{\mathcal{S}}(\rho)
. Let also
J\colon H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
be the energy functional associated with (
1.1
)–(
1.2
), i.e.,

(1.7)

J(u)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\nabla u|^{2}\,dx-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}G(u)\,dx.

Our first result is the existence of a solution to (
1.1
)–(
1.2
), with additional properties. We point out that by
solution
to (
1.1
) we mean a pair
(u,\lambda)\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\times\mathbb{R}
such that
J^{\prime}(u)v=-\lambda uv
for every
v\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
.

Theorem 1.1
.

If
g
satisfies (g0)–(g4), then there exists
\bar{\rho}>0
such that for every
\rho>\bar{\rho}
there exist
\lambda>0
and
u\in{\mathcal{S}}
such that
J(u)=\min_{{\mathcal{D}}}J<0
and
(u,\lambda)
is a solution to (
1.1
)–(
1.2
). Moreover,
u
has constant sign and is, up to a translation, radial and radially monotonic.

Remark 1.2
.

Of course, (g0)–(g4) include the classical case
\lim_{s\to 0}g(s)/s=0
, which implies that
J
is of class
{\mathcal{C}}^{1}
in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. In this case, with the notations of Theorem
1.1
,
u
is actually a critical point of
J|_{\mathcal{S}}
. In addition, if
\lim_{s\to 0}G(s)/|s|^{2+4/N}=\infty
(i.e., in the mass-subcritical regime), then
\bar{\rho}=0
in Theorem
1.1
(see, e.g.,
[
28
]
for more details). An important example in this context is given by nonlinear media with
saturation

[
18
]
, where
g(s)=s^{3}/(1+s^{2})
.

Remark 1.3
.

Under the assumptions (g0)–(g4), if there exists a solution
(u,\lambda)\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\times\mathbb{R}
to (
1.1
)–(
1.2
) with
J(u)\leq 0
, then
\lambda>0
. As a matter of fact,
J(u)\leq 0
implies that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}G(u)\,dx
is finite, thus from
[
24
, Proposition 3.1]

(u,\lambda)
satisfies the Pohožaev identity
(N-2)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\nabla u|^{2}\,dx=2N\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}G(u)-\frac{1}{2}\lambda|u|^{2}\,dx
and, consequently,
0\geq J(u)=\frac{1}{N}|\nabla u|_{2}^{2}-\frac{\lambda}{2}\rho^{2}
.

Our next result concerns the limit of the ground state energy map
\rho\mapsto\inf_{{\mathcal{D}}(\rho)}J
as
\rho\to\infty
.

Proposition 1.4
.

If
g
satisfies (g0)–(g4), then

\lim_{\rho\to\infty}\inf_{{\mathcal{D}}(\rho)}J=-\infty.

Moreover, we obtain the relative compactness in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
of minimizing sequences for
\inf_{{\mathcal{D}}(\rho)}J
up to translations. Here and in the sequel, with a small abuse of notations, if
(u_{n})_{n}
is a sequence in
X
, we write
u_{n}\in X
instead of
(u_{n})_{n}\subset X
.

Theorem 1.5
.

If (g0)–(g4) hold, then there exists
\bar{\rho}>0
such that for every
\rho>\bar{\rho}
, if
0<\rho_{n}\to\rho
and
u_{n}\in{\mathcal{D}}(\rho_{n})
is such that
J(u_{n})\to\inf_{{\mathcal{D}}(\rho)}J
, then
u_{n}
is relatively compact in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
up to translations.

It is well known that such a relative compactness is an important step toward the orbital stability of solutions to (
1.1
)–(
1.2
). Unfortunately, we currently do not have the necessary tools to investigate the dynamics of (
1.3
) in this general setting, hence this issue is postponed to future work. Moreover, unlike the classical approach when
J
is of class
{\mathcal{C}}^{1}
, where the existence of a constrained minimizer for
J
and, consequently, a solution to (
1.1
)–(
1.2
) is a by-product of the aforementioned relative compactness, here we prove first the existence of a solution to (
1.1
)–(
1.2
) and then use this fact to prove the compactness result.

When
g
is as in (
1.5
), we additionally have the following non-existence result.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let

be given by (

) with

,

, and

(

).

(i) If

, or

and

.

(u,\lambda)\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\times[0,\infty)

Remark 1.7
.

In Theorem
1.1
, we look for (ground state) solutions with sufficiently large
\rho
because this implies that the energy is negative, which is used, in turn, to ensure such an existence. Then, as we pointed out in Remark
1.3
, a consequence is that
\lambda>0
. On the other hand, in the context of Theorem
1.6
(i) and
\mu>0
, ground state solutions still exist for all
\rho>0
as in Corollary
1.9
below, but with no information about the Lagrange multiplier or the energy.

We follow the approach of
[
24
]
and consider a family of approximating problems. Note that the approximations are different from those in
[
15
]
, where the nonlinearity is modified in a neighbourhood of infinity, and are more similar to those in
[
14
]
, although not the same as the nonlinearity therein is of the form (
1.4
).

Let
g_{-}(s):=g_{+}(s)-g(s)
and
G_{-}(s):=G_{+}(s)-G(s)\geq 0
for
s\in\mathbb{R}
.
In view of (g1) and (g3),
G_{+}(u)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
for
u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
; however,

G_{-}(u)
may not be integrable
unless
G_{-}(s)\lesssim|s|^{2}
for small
|s|
. Moreover, for any
u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
,
J
is differentiable at
u
along functions in
{\mathcal{C}}_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, but it is not of class
{\mathcal{C}}^{1}
in general. In order to overcome this problem, for every
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
let us take an even function
\varphi_{\varepsilon}\colon\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
such that
\varphi_{\varepsilon}(s)=|s|/\varepsilon
for
|s|\leq\varepsilon
,
\varphi_{\varepsilon}(s)=1
for
|s|\geq\varepsilon
. We introduce a new functional,

(1.8)

J_{\varepsilon}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\nabla u|^{2}\,dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}G_{-}^{\varepsilon}(u)\,dx-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}G_{+}(u)\,dx,

such that
G_{-}^{\varepsilon}(s)=\int_{0}^{s}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(t)g_{-}(t)\,dt
,
s\in\mathbb{R}
,
and now observe that
G_{-}^{\varepsilon}(s)\leq c_{\varepsilon}|s|^{2}
for every
|s|\leq 1
and some constant
c_{\varepsilon}>0
depending only on
\varepsilon>0
.
Hence, for
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,
J_{\varepsilon}
is of class
{\mathcal{C}}^{1}
on
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
.

The idea of considering the
L^{2}
-disc
{\mathcal{D}}
instead of the
L^{2}
-sphere
{\mathcal{S}}
was introduced in
[
5
]
in a context where the nonlinear term is mass-supercritical and Sobolev-subcritical at infinity (i.e.,
\lim_{|s|\to\infty}G(s)/|s|^{2+4/N}=\infty
,
\lim_{|s|\to\infty}G(s)/|s|^{2^{*}}=0
) and mass-critical or -supercritical at the origin (i.e.,
\limsup_{s\to 0}G(s)/|s|^{2+4/N}<\infty
); later on, it was exploited again in
[
25
]
, in a similar context that allows systems with Sobolev-critical nonlinearities, in
[
28
]
, for equations and systems of equations in the mass-critical or -subcritical setting, and in
[
6
]
, where polyharmonic operators and Hardy-type potentials are considered. One of the reasons for this choice was that the embedding
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
is not compact, even if considering radial functions or with symmetries as in
[
20
]
, which, in turn, causes that the limit point of a weakly convergent sequence in
{\mathcal{S}}
need not belong to
{\mathcal{S}}
; on the contrary, limit points of weakly convergent sequences in
{\mathcal{D}}
stay in
{\mathcal{D}}
, and the additional information that such a weak limit point belongs to the set one is working with proves to be useful. In this paper, this strategy is fundamental: it allows the critical point found in Theorem
2.1
below – the existence result for the perturbed problem – to be a minimizer of the corresponding energy functional over the whole set
{\mathcal{D}}
, and this fact plays an important role in the proof of Theorem
1.1
because the “candidate minimizer” of
J|_{\mathcal{D}}
might not, initially, belong to
{\mathcal{S}}
.

Now, we turn to the problem of finding multiple solutions (in fact, infinitely many) to (
1.1
)–(
1.2
). In this case, we need to change both the assumptions about
g
and our approach. The idea is to build a subspace of
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
in the spirit of
{\mathcal{H}}
, but with a more general setting. The issues with using the previous approach to obtain an infinite sequence of solutions are mainly two. First, even at the level of the perturbed functional, one can obtain an arbitrary large number of solutions if
\rho
is sufficiently large, but it is unclear whether infinitely many solutions exist; this is caused by the lack of compact embedding into
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, which in turn raises the need for each of the usual minimax values (cf. Theorem
4.9
below) to be negative, for which we need
\rho
to be large (and how large depends differently on each minimax level). Second, fixed a family (depending on
\varepsilon
) of critical values of the perturbed functional obtained in the way we have just briefly mentioned, and considering the corresponding family of critical points, it is not clear whether this family converges to a critical point of the unperturbed functional, unlike what happens in the Proof of Theorem
1.1
, where we can exploit the information that the critical levels are minima.

We assume that the right-hand side in (

) is given by

(1.9)

together with the following assumptions, where

,

,

, and

.

(A)

is an

-function that satisfies the

(f0)

is continuous and odd.

(f1)

\lim_{s\to 0}f_{+}(s)/s=0
.

(f2)

If

, then

;

If

.

Observe that (f3) implies

\eta:=\limsup_{|s|\to\infty}\frac{F_{+}(s)}{|s|^{2+4/N}}<\infty.

Let us define

W:=\left\{u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N}):A(u)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\right\}

and, for a subgroup

If

, with the purpose of finding non-radial solutions (cf.

), fix

and let

for

(

is removed form the definition of

For

, let us denote by

|u|_{p}\leq C_{N,p}|\nabla u|_{2}^{N(1/2-1/p)}|u|_{2}^{1-N(1/2-1/p)}\quad\forall\,u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N}).

that satisfies (f0)–(f3). The price to pay for this different approach is that we need (
1.9
) and (A) to construct a proper functional setting (i.e., an Orlicz one); this prevents us from dealing, e.g., with terms that behave like
1/\ln s^{2}
near the origin, which instead are allowed by the use of the approximating problems to find least-energy solutions. On the other hand, we can weaken the assumptions about
f
, the “remaining” part of the nonlinearity: it can be controlled by
a
instead of the usual conditions with an
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
-setting (in particular, we can deal with Sobolev-supercritical nonlinearities), we do not need
F>A
anywhere,
f_{+}
can have a mass-critical growth at infinity (provided
\rho
is small), and there are no restrictions on
\rho
if
f_{+}
has a mass-subcritical growth at infinity; none of these scenarios can be handled with the previous approach. Except for
[
30
, Theorems 1.2 and 1.3]
, where the nonlinear term has the very specific form (
1.5
), Theorem
1.8
seems to be the first multiplicity result when normalized solutions are sought in the strongly sublinear regime, and also the first time at all that more than two solutions are obtained.

The paper is structured al follows: in Section
2
, we obtain least-energy solutions to the perturbed problem; in Section
3
, we prove Theorems
1.1
,
1.5
,
1.6
, and Proposition
1.4
; in Section
4
, we obtain infinitely many solutions to (
1.1
)–(
1.2
).

2.
Solutions to the perturbed problem

Recall from (
1.8
) the definition of
J_{\varepsilon}\colon H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\to\mathbb{R}
. The main purpose of this section is to prove the following result, where we denote
c_{\varepsilon}(\rho):=\inf_{{\mathcal{D}}(\rho)}J_{\varepsilon}
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
g
satisfy (g0)–(g4). Then for every
\beta>0
there exists
\bar{\rho}>0
such that for every
\rho>\bar{\rho}
and
\varepsilon>0
there exist
\lambda_{\varepsilon}>0
and
u_{\varepsilon}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
such that
J_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})=c_{\varepsilon}(\rho)<-\beta
and

(2.1)

\begin{cases}-\Delta u_{\varepsilon}+\lambda_{\varepsilon}u_{\varepsilon}=g_{+}(u_{\varepsilon})-\varphi_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})g_{-}(u_{\varepsilon})\quad\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{N}\\
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|u_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\,dx=\rho^{2}.\end{cases}

Moreover, every such
u_{\varepsilon}
has constant sign and, up to a translation, is radial and radially monotonic.

We begin by proving a series of lemmas.

Lemma 2.2
.

If
g
satisfies (g0)–(g3),
then
J_{\varepsilon}|_{\mathcal{D}}
is coercive and bounded below.

Proof.

From (g1) and (g3), for every
\delta>0
there exists
C_{\delta}>0
such that for every
s\in\mathbb{R}

G_{+}(s)\leq C_{\delta}|s|^{2}+\delta|s|^{2+4/N}.

Consequently, from (
1.11
), for every
u\in{\mathcal{D}}
there holds

\begin{split}J_{\varepsilon}(u)&\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u|^{2}-G_{+}(u)\,dx\geq\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u|_{2}^{2}-C_{\delta}|u|_{2}^{2}-\delta|u|_{2+4/N}^{2+4/N}\\
&\geq\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u|_{2}^{2}-C_{\delta}\rho^{2}-\delta C_{N,2+4/N}^{2+4/N}\rho^{4/N}|\nabla u|_{2}^{2}.\end{split}

Taking
\delta<\left(2C_{N,2+4/N}^{2+4/N}\rho^{4/N}\right)^{-1}
we conclude.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

If
g
satisfies (g0)–(g2) and (g4), then for every
\beta>0
there exists
\bar{\rho}>0
such that

c_{\varepsilon}(\rho)<-\beta

for every
\rho>\bar{\rho}
and every
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
.

Proof.

Arguing as in
[
2
, Proof of Theorem 2]
and observing that
G^{\varepsilon}:=G_{+}-G_{-}^{\varepsilon}\geq G
, there exists
u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
such that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}G^{\varepsilon}(u)\,dx\geq 1
for every
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. Define
u_{\rho}:=u(|u|_{2}^{2/N}\rho^{-2/N}\cdot)\in{\mathcal{S}}(\rho)
. There holds

\begin{split}J_{\varepsilon}(u_{\rho})&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u_{\rho}|^{2}-G^{\varepsilon}(u_{\rho})\,dx=\frac{|\nabla u|_{2}^{2}}{2|u|_{2}^{2(N-2)}}\rho^{2-4/N}-\frac{1}{|u|_{2}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}G^{\varepsilon}(u)\,dx\rho^{2}\\
&\leq\frac{|\nabla u|_{2}^{2}}{2|u|_{2}^{2(N-2)}}\rho^{2-4/N}-\frac{1}{|u|_{2}^{2}}\rho^{2}\to-\infty\end{split}

as
\rho\to\infty
, whence the statement.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

If (g0)–(g3) hold, then for every
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}>0
there holds
c_{\varepsilon}(\sqrt{\rho_{1}^{2}+\rho_{2}^{2}})\leq c_{\varepsilon}(\rho_{1})+c_{\varepsilon}(\rho_{2})
.

Proof.

Fix
\delta>0
. From the definition of
c_{\varepsilon}
, which is finite due to Lemma
2.2
, there exist
u_{1},u_{2}\in{\mathcal{C}}_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
such that
|u_{i}|_{2}\leq\rho_{i}
and
J_{\varepsilon}(u_{i})\leq c_{\varepsilon}(\rho_{i})+\delta
,
i=1,2
. In virtue of the translation invariance, we can assume that
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
have disjoint supports. Then
|u_{1}+u_{2}|_{2}^{2}=|u_{1}|_{2}^{2}+|u_{2}|_{2}^{2}\leq\rho_{1}^{2}+\rho_{2}^{2}
and so

c_{\varepsilon}\left(\sqrt{\rho_{1}^{2}+\rho_{2}^{2}}\right)\leq J_{\varepsilon}(u_{1}+u_{2})\leq c_{\varepsilon}(\rho_{1})+c_{\varepsilon}(\rho_{2})+2\delta.\qed

The following lemma is inspired from
[
29
, Lemma 3.2 (ii)]
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Assume (g0)–(g3) hold and let
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}>0
. If there exist
u_{i}\in{\mathcal{D}}(\rho_{i})
such that
J_{\varepsilon}(u_{i})=c_{\varepsilon}(\rho_{i})
,
i=1,2
, and
(u_{1},u_{2})\neq(0,0)
, then
c_{\varepsilon}(\sqrt{\rho_{1}^{2}+\rho_{2}^{2}})<c_{\varepsilon}(\rho_{1})+c_{\varepsilon}(\rho_{2})
.

Proof.

For every
s\geq 1
and
i=1,2
there holds

c_{\varepsilon}(\sqrt{s}\rho_{i})\leq J_{\varepsilon}\bigl(u_{i}(s^{-1/N}\cdot)\bigr)=s\left(\frac{1}{2s^{2/N}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\nabla u_{i}|^{2}\,dx-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}G^{\varepsilon}(u_{i})\,dx\right)\leq sJ_{\varepsilon}(u_{i})=sc_{\varepsilon}(\rho_{i}).

Moreover, if
s>1
and
u_{i}\neq 0
, then
c_{\varepsilon}(\sqrt{s}\rho_{i})<sc_{\varepsilon}(\rho_{i})
. Let us assume, without loss of generality, that
u_{1}\neq 0
. If
\rho_{1}\geq\rho_{2}
, then

c_{\varepsilon}\left(\sqrt{\rho_{1}^{2}+\rho_{2}^{2}}\right)<\frac{\rho_{1}^{2}+\rho_{2}^{2}}{\rho_{1}^{2}}c_{\varepsilon}(\rho_{1})=c_{\varepsilon}(\rho_{1})+\frac{\rho_{2}^{2}}{\rho_{1}^{2}}c_{\varepsilon}(\rho_{1})\leq c_{\varepsilon}(\rho_{1})+c_{\varepsilon}(\rho_{2}).

If
\rho_{1}<\rho_{2}
, then

c_{\varepsilon}\left(\sqrt{\rho_{1}^{2}+\rho_{2}^{2}}\right)\leq\frac{\rho_{1}^{2}+\rho_{2}^{2}}{\rho_{2}^{2}}c_{\varepsilon}(\rho_{2})=\frac{\rho_{1}^{2}}{\rho_{2}^{2}}c_{\varepsilon}(\rho_{2})+c_{\varepsilon}(\rho_{2})<c_{\varepsilon}(\rho_{1})+c_{\varepsilon}(\rho_{2}).\qed

Remark 2.6
.

(a) Lemmas
2.4
and
2.5
still hold if
J_{\varepsilon}
is replaced with
J
.

(b) As proved in
[
28
, Lemma 2.5 (ii)]
, Lemma
2.5
holds under the weaker assumption that
J_{\varepsilon}(u_{1})=c_{\varepsilon}(\rho_{1})
or
J_{\varepsilon}(u_{2})=c_{\varepsilon}(\rho_{2})
.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
g
satisfy (g0)–(g4). Then, for every
\beta>0
, there exists
\bar{\rho}>0
such that for every
\rho>\bar{\rho}
and
\varepsilon>0
we have
c_{\varepsilon}(\rho)<-\beta
and, if
0<\rho_{n}\to\rho
and
u_{n}\in{\mathcal{D}}(\rho_{n})
is such that
J_{\varepsilon}(u_{n})\to c_{\varepsilon}(\rho)
, then there exists
u\in{\mathcal{D}}(\rho)\setminus\{0\}
such that, up to translations,
u_{n}\to u
in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
,
2<p<2^{*}
.

Proof.

Let
\bar{\rho}>0
be determined by Lemma
2.3
and consider
u_{n}
and
\rho_{n}
as in the statement. Then
u_{n}
is bounded in view of Lemma
2.2
. If

\lim_{n}\max_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\int_{B_{1}(y)}|u_{n}|^{2}\,dx=0,

then
\lim_{n}|u_{n}|_{p}=0
for every
2<p<2^{*}
due to Lions’ lemma
[
21
, Lemma I.1 in Part 2]
, hence, taking into account (g1) and (g3),
\lim_{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}G_{+}(u_{n})\,dx=0
and, consequently,
c_{\varepsilon}(\rho)=\lim_{n}J_{\varepsilon}(u_{n})\geq 0
, a contradiction with Lemma
2.3
. Therefore, there exist
y_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
u\in{\mathcal{D}}(\rho)\setminus\{0\}
such that, up to a subsequence,
u_{n}(\cdot-y_{n})\rightharpoonup u
in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
and
u_{n}(\cdot-y_{n})\to u
a.e. in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Let us denote
v_{n}(x):=u_{n}(x-y_{n})-u(x)
. In virtue of the Brezis–Lieb lemma
[
7
, Theorem 2]
,

\lim_{n}J_{\varepsilon}(u_{n})-J_{\varepsilon}(v_{n})=J_{\varepsilon}(u).

We want to prove that

\lim_{n}\max_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\int_{B_{1}(y)}|v_{n}|^{2}\,dx=0,

which, as before, will yield the statement of the Lemma. If this is not the case, then, as before, there exist
z_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
v\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\setminus\{0\}
such that, denoting
w_{n}(x):=v_{n}(x-z_{n})-v(x)
, we have
w_{n}\rightharpoonup 0
in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
,
w_{n}\to 0
a.e. in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, and

\lim_{n}J_{\varepsilon}(v_{n})-J_{\varepsilon}(w_{n})=J_{\varepsilon}(v).

Observe that, again owing to the Brezis–Lieb lemma,

\lim_{n}|u_{n}|_{2}^{2}-|w_{n}|_{2}^{2}=\lim_{n}|u_{n}|_{2}^{2}-|v_{n}|_{2}^{2}+|v_{n}|_{2}^{2}-|w_{n}|_{2}^{2}=|u|_{2}^{2}+|v|_{2}^{2},

whence, denoting
\beta:=|u|_{2}>0
and
\gamma:=|v|_{2}>0
, there holds

\rho^{2}-\beta^{2}-\gamma^{2}\geq\limsup_{n}|u_{n}|_{2}^{2}-\beta^{2}-\gamma^{2}=\limsup_{n}|w_{n}|_{2}^{2}=:\delta^{2}\geq 0.

If
\delta>0
, then let us set
\tilde{w}_{n}:=\frac{\delta}{|w_{n}|_{2}}w_{n}\in{\mathcal{S}}(\delta)
. Via explicit computations (cf., e.g.,
[
29
, Lemma 2.4]
), we have
\lim_{n}J_{\varepsilon}(w_{n})-J_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{w}_{n})=0
, hence, together with Lemma
2.4
and since
c_{\varepsilon}
is non-increasing,

(2.2)

\begin{split}c_{\varepsilon}(\rho)&=\lim_{n}J_{\varepsilon}(u_{n})=J_{\varepsilon}(u)+J_{\varepsilon}(v)+\lim_{n}J_{\varepsilon}(w_{n})=J_{\varepsilon}(u)+J_{\varepsilon}(v)+\lim_{n}J_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{w}_{n})\\
&\geq c_{\varepsilon}(\beta)+c_{\varepsilon}(\gamma)+c_{\varepsilon}(\delta)\geq c_{\varepsilon}(\sqrt{\beta^{2}+\gamma^{2}+\delta^{2}})\geq c_{\varepsilon}(\rho).\end{split}

Thus all the inequalities in (
2.2
) are in fact equalities and, in particular,
J_{\varepsilon}(u)=c_{\varepsilon}(\beta)
and
J_{\varepsilon}(v)=c_{\varepsilon}(\gamma)
. Therefore Lemma
2.5
yields
c_{\varepsilon}(\beta)+c_{\varepsilon}(\gamma)+c_{\varepsilon}(\delta)>c_{\varepsilon}(\sqrt{\beta^{2}+\gamma^{2}+\delta^{2}})
, which contradicts (
2.2
).
If
\delta=0
, then
w_{n}\to 0
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, which, together with (
1.11
), implies
\lim_{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}G_{+}(w_{n})\,dx=0
, whence
\liminf_{n}J_{\varepsilon}(w_{n})\geq 0
. Then (
2.2
) becomes

c_{\varepsilon}(\rho)=\lim_{n}J_{\varepsilon}(u_{n})=J_{\varepsilon}(u)+J_{\varepsilon}(v)+\lim_{n}J_{\varepsilon}(w_{n})\geq c_{\varepsilon}(\beta)+c_{\varepsilon}(\gamma)\geq c_{\varepsilon}(\sqrt{\beta^{2}+\gamma^{2}})=c_{\varepsilon}(\rho)

and we reach a contradiction as before.
∎

Let us recall that every solution
(u_{\varepsilon},\lambda_{\varepsilon})
to the differential equation in (
2.1
) satisfies the Pohožaev identity

(2.3)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(N-2)|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}|^{2}+N\lambda_{\varepsilon}|u_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\,dx=2N\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}G_{+}(u_{\varepsilon})-G_{-}^{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})\,dx.

Proof of Theorem
2.1
.

Let
\bar{\rho}>0
be determined by Lemma
2.3
and consider a minimizing sequence
u_{n}\in{\mathcal{D}}(\rho)
for
c_{\varepsilon}(\rho)
. Then, in virtue of Lemmas
2.2
and
2.7
, there exists
u_{\varepsilon}\in{\mathcal{D}}(\rho)\setminus\{0\}
such that, up to subsequences and translations,
u_{n}\rightharpoonup u_{\varepsilon}
in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
and
u_{n}\to u_{\varepsilon}
in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
for every
2<p<2^{*}
and a.e. in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. From (g1) and (g3) we have
\lim_{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}G_{+}(u_{n})\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}G_{+}(u_{\varepsilon})\,dx
, therefore, using Fatou’s Lemma,
c_{\varepsilon}(\rho)\leq J_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})\leq\lim_{n}J_{\varepsilon}(u_{n})=c_{\varepsilon}(\rho)<-\beta
. In particular, there exists
\lambda_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

-\Delta u_{\varepsilon}+\lambda_{\varepsilon}u_{\varepsilon}=g^{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})\quad\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{N}.

Note that
\lambda_{\varepsilon}=0
if
u_{\varepsilon}
is an interior point of
{\mathcal{D}}(\rho)
, i.e.,
|u_{\varepsilon}|_{2}<\rho
. If
\lambda_{\varepsilon}\leq 0
, then (
2.3
) yields
0>J_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})\geq|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}|_{2}^{2}/N\geq 0
, a contradiction, therefore
\lambda_{\varepsilon}>0
and
u_{\varepsilon}\in{\mathcal{S}}(\rho)
. The last statement follows from
[
16
, Theorem 1.4]
(see also
[
22
]
).
∎

Remark 2.8
.

The proof of Theorem
2.1
shows that every minimizing sequence for
c_{\varepsilon}(\rho)
is relatively compact in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
up to a translation. As a matter of fact, from
\lim_{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}G_{+}(u_{n})\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}G_{+}(u_{\varepsilon})\,dx
and
\lim_{n}J_{\varepsilon}(u_{n})=J_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})
we get
\lim_{n}|\nabla u_{n}|_{2}=|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}|_{2}
. Moreover,

\rho=|u_{\varepsilon}|_{2}\leq\liminf_{n}|u_{n}|_{2}\leq\limsup_{n}|u_{n}|_{2}\leq\rho,

i.e.,
\lim_{n}|u_{n}|_{2}=|u_{\varepsilon}|_{2}
. Recalling that
u_{n}\rightharpoonup u_{\varepsilon}
in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, we obtain
u_{n}\to u_{\varepsilon}
in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
.

3.
Solutions to (
1.1
) and related properties

Throughout this section, it is assumed that (g0)–(g4) hold. We consider the family of functions and positive numbers
(u_{\varepsilon},\lambda_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(0,1)}
given by Theorem
2.1
.

Lemma 3.1
.

The quantities
|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}|_{2}
and
\lambda_{\varepsilon}
are bounded.

Proof.

From (g0), (g1), and (g3), for every
\delta>0
there exists
C_{\delta}>0
such that for every
s\in\mathbb{R}

G_{+}(s)\leq C_{\delta}|s|^{2}+\delta|s|^{2+4/N}.

Let us first consider the case
N\geq 3
. Since
\lambda_{\varepsilon}>0
, from (
2.3
) we obtain

\frac{1}{2^{*}}|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}|_{2}^{2}<\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}G_{+}(u_{\varepsilon})\,dx\leq C_{\delta}|u_{\varepsilon}|_{2}^{2}+\delta|u_{\varepsilon}|_{2+4/N}^{2+4/N}\leq C_{\delta}\rho^{2}+\delta C_{N,2+4/N}^{2+4/N}\rho^{4/N}|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}|_{2}^{2}.

Taking
\delta<\left(2^{*}C_{N,2+4/N}^{2+4/N}\rho^{4/N}\right)^{-1}
, we obtain the boundedness of
|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}|_{2}
. In addition, this and again (
2.3
) yield

(3.1)

0<\frac{2^{*}}{2}\lambda_{\varepsilon}\rho^{2}<\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}|^{2}+\frac{2^{*}}{2}\lambda_{\varepsilon}|u_{\varepsilon}|^{2}+2^{*}G_{-}^{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})\,dx=2^{*}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}G_{+}(u_{\varepsilon})\,dx\leq C<\infty

and
\lambda_{\varepsilon}
is bounded as well. Now, let us consider the case
N=2
. From (
2.3
) we get

(3.2)

\rho^{2}\lambda_{\varepsilon}\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\lambda_{\varepsilon}|u_{\varepsilon}|^{2}+2G_{-}^{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})\,dx=2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}G_{+}(u_{\varepsilon})\,dx\leq C_{\delta}\rho^{2}+\delta C_{N,2+4/N}^{2+4/N}\rho^{4/N}|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}|_{2}^{2}.

Moreover, again using (
2.3
) we obtain

-\beta>J_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})=\frac{1}{2}\left(|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}|_{2}^{2}-\rho^{2}\lambda_{\varepsilon}\right),

which, together with (
3.2
), implies

\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}|_{2}^{2}\leq C_{\delta}\rho^{2}-\beta+\delta C_{N,2+4/N}^{2+4/N}\rho^{4/N}|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}|_{2}^{2}.

Taking
\delta<\left(2C_{N,2+4/N}^{2+4/N}\rho^{4/N}\right)^{-1}
, we obtain the boundedness of
|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}|_{2}
, hence that of
\lambda_{\varepsilon}
follows from (
3.2
).
∎

Note that for every
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and every
u\in{\mathcal{D}}
there holds
J_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})\leq J_{\varepsilon}(u)\leq J(u)
, whence
J_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})\leq\inf_{{\mathcal{D}}(\rho)}J=:c(\rho)
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

From Lemma
3.1
there exist
u\in{\mathcal{D}}
and
\lambda\geq 0
such that, up to a subsequence,
u_{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup u
in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
and
\lambda_{\varepsilon}\to\lambda
as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
. Arguing as in
[
24
, Proof of Theorem 1.1]
we obtain that
\varphi_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})g_{-}(u_{\varepsilon})v\to g_{-}(u)v
a.e. for every
v\in{\mathcal{C}}_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
and that
g_{+}(u_{\varepsilon})v
and
\varphi_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})g_{-}(u_{\varepsilon})v
are uniformly integrable (and tight).
We deduce that for every
v\in{\mathcal{C}}_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})

-\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}uv\,dx\leftarrow J_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(u_{\varepsilon})v\to J^{\prime}(u)v,

i.e.,

-\Delta u+\lambda u=g(u)\quad\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{N}.

Moreover, (
3.1
) (when
N\geq 3
) or (
3.2
) (when
N=2
) yields

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}G_{-}^{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})\,dx\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}G_{+}(u_{\varepsilon})\,dx\leq C

hence, in view of Fatou’s lemma,
G_{-}(u)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. In particular, from
[
24
, Proposition 3.1]
,
(u,\lambda)
satisfies the Pohožaev identity

(3.3)

(N-2)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\nabla u|^{2}\,dx=2N\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}G(u)-\frac{1}{2}\lambda|u|^{2}\,dx.

We can assume that
|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}|_{2}
and
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}G_{-}^{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})\,dx
are convergent. Since
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}G_{+}(u_{\varepsilon})\,dx\to\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}G_{+}(u)
(remember that each
u_{\varepsilon}
is radially symmetric), we have
J(u)\leq\lim_{\varepsilon}J_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})<0
from Lemma
2.3
; in particular,
u\neq 0
. If
\lambda=0
, then from (
3.3
) we have
J(u)=|\nabla u|_{2}^{2}/N>0
, therefore
\lambda>0
. We prove that
u_{\varepsilon}\to u
in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. Since
\lambda>0
, from (
3.3
) there follows

\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(N-2)|\nabla u|^{2}+N\lambda|u|^{2}+2NG_{-}(u)\,dx&\leq\lim_{\varepsilon}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(N-2)|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}|^{2}+N\lambda|u_{\varepsilon}|^{2}+2NG^{\varepsilon}_{-}(u_{\varepsilon})\,dx\\
&=2N\lim_{\varepsilon}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}G_{+}(u_{\varepsilon})\,dx=2N\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}G_{+}(u)\,dx\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(N-2)|\nabla u|^{2}+N\lambda|u|^{2}+2NG_{-}(u)\,dx,\end{split}

which yields
|u_{\varepsilon}|_{2}\to|u|_{2}
;
in particular,
u\in{\mathcal{S}}
. Furthermore, in a similar fashion we obtain
J(u)\leq\lim_{\varepsilon}J_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})\leq c(\rho)\leq J(u)
, which shows that
|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}|_{2}\to|\nabla u|_{2}
and
J(u)=c(\rho)
. Since (
2.1
) is translation-invariant, we can assume that every
u_{\varepsilon}
is radial, hence so is
u
. Moreover, because
u\neq 0
, there exists
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that
u(x)\neq 0
and so
u_{\varepsilon}(x)
has the same sign as
u(x)
for every
\varepsilon
sufficiently small; since every
u_{\varepsilon}
has constant sign, it has everywhere the same sign as
u(x)
, hence
u
has constant sign too. Finally, if there exist
x,y,z\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that
|x|<|y|<|z|
and either
u(y)<\min\{u(x),u(z)\}
or
u(y)>\max\{u(x),u(z)\}
, then arguing as before we obtain a contradiction.
∎

.

Let

,
2<p<2^{*}
. Fix

. Arguing (by contradiction) as in the proof of Lemma

, we find
\beta,\gamma>0
,

,

,

, and
v_{n},w_{n}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
bounded, none of which depending on
\varepsilon
, such that
\rho^{2}-\beta^{2}-\gamma^{2}\geq\delta^{2}
and
\lim_{n}J_{\varepsilon}(u_{n})-J_{\varepsilon}(w_{n})=J_{\varepsilon}(u)+J_{\varepsilon}(v)
, where
v_{n}(x)=u_{n}(x-y_{n})-u(x)
and
w_{n}(x)=v_{n}(x-z_{n})-v(x)
. If
\delta>0
, then
\tilde{w}_{n}:=\frac{\delta}{|w_{n}|_{2}}w_{n}\in{\mathcal{S}}(\delta)
and, again from
[
29
, Lemma 2.4]
,

\begin{split}c_{\varepsilon}(\rho)&=\lim_{n}J_{\varepsilon}(u_{n})=J_{\varepsilon}(u)+J_{\varepsilon}(v)+\lim_{n}J_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{w}_{n})\geq c_{\varepsilon}(\beta)+c_{\varepsilon}(\gamma)+c_{\varepsilon}(\delta)\\
&\geq c_{\varepsilon}(\sqrt{\beta^{2}+\gamma^{2}+\delta^{2}})\geq c_{\varepsilon}(\rho).\end{split}

Letting
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
, observing that
J_{\varepsilon}(u)\to J(u)
and
J_{\varepsilon}(v)\to J(v)
from the monotone convergence theorem, and using Remark
3.2
(i), we obtain

\begin{split}c(\rho)&\geq J(u)+J(v)+\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\lim_{n}J_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{w}_{n})\geq\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}c_{\varepsilon}(\beta)+\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}c_{\varepsilon}(\gamma)+\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}c_{\varepsilon}(\delta)\\
&\geq\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}c_{\varepsilon}(\sqrt{\beta^{2}+\gamma^{2}+\delta^{2}})\geq c(\rho).\end{split}

In particular,
c_{\varepsilon}(\beta)\to J(u)
and
c_{\varepsilon}(\gamma)\to J(v)
as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
, therefore

c(\beta)\leq J(u)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}c_{\varepsilon}(\beta)\leq c(\beta),\quad c(\gamma)\leq J(v)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}c_{\varepsilon}(\gamma)\leq c(\gamma),

and so, from Lemma
2.4
and Remark
2.6
(a),

\begin{split}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}c_{\varepsilon}(\sqrt{\beta^{2}+\gamma^{2}+\delta^{2}})&\leq\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}c_{\varepsilon}(\sqrt{\beta^{2}+\gamma^{2}})+\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}c_{\varepsilon}(\delta)\leq c(\sqrt{\beta^{2}+\gamma^{2}})+\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}c_{\varepsilon}(\delta)\\
&<c(\beta)+c(\gamma)+\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}c_{\varepsilon}(\delta)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}c_{\varepsilon}(\sqrt{\beta^{2}+\gamma^{2}+\delta^{2}}),\end{split}

a contradiction. If
\delta=0
, then, again as in the proof of Lemma
2.7
,
\liminf_{n}J_{\varepsilon}(w_{n})\geq 0
and we get

c_{\varepsilon}(\rho)=\lim_{n}J_{\varepsilon}(u_{n})=J_{\varepsilon}(u)+J_{\varepsilon}(v)+\lim_{n}J_{\varepsilon}(w_{n})\geq c_{\varepsilon}(\beta)+c_{\varepsilon}(\gamma)\geq c_{\varepsilon}(\sqrt{\beta^{2}+\gamma^{2}})\geq c_{\varepsilon}(\rho),

thus we obtain a contradiction arguing in a similar (and simpler) way as before. Having proved that
u_{n}
is relatively compact up to translations in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
,
2<p<2^{*}
, now we prove that the same convergence holds in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. Recalling that
u_{n}
is bounded, there exists
u\in{\mathcal{D}}(\rho)
such that, up to translations and subsequences,
u_{n}\rightharpoonup u
in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
and
u_{n}\to u
in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
,
2<p<2^{*}
, whence

c(\rho)\leq J(u)\leq\lim_{n}J(u_{n})=c(\rho)

and so
|\nabla u_{n}|_{2}^{2}\to|\nabla u|_{2}^{2}
. Moreover,
J(u)=c(\rho)<0
, which implies, in particular, that
G(u)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. Assume by contradiction that
|u|_{2}<\rho
; then, for every
v\in{\mathcal{C}}_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
and every
t\in\mathbb{R}
sufficiently small,
|u+tv|_{2}<\rho
. Next, we check that
J
is differentiable at
u
along every
v\in{\mathcal{C}}_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. Of course, this only needs to be checked for the functional
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}G(\cdot)\,dx
. Let
t\neq 0
: from (g0) and (g2),
\frac{1}{t}[G(u+tv)-G(u)]=\int_{0}^{1}g(u+stv)\,ds\,v
and the family
\{\int_{0}^{1}g(u+stv)\,ds\,v\}_{t}
is uniformly integrable (and tight), thus the claim holds true. There follows that
J^{\prime}(u)[v]=0
for every
v\in{\mathcal{C}}_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, i.e.,
-\Delta u=g(u)
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Then, from
[
24
, Proposition 3.1]
,
u
satisfies (
3.3
) with
\lambda=0
, whence
J(u)=|\nabla u|_{2}^{2}/N>0
, which is impossible.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.6
.

Since
G(s)=\alpha/2(\ln s^{2}-1)s^{2}+\mu/p|s|^{p}
, it is clear that
(g0)–(g3)
hold. We want to prove that
(g4)
is satisfied if and only if
\mu>-\frac{\alpha p}{p-2}e^{-p/2}
. If
\mu\geq 0
, then clearly
(g4)
holds, so let us consider the case
\mu<0
.
Let
s>0
. Of course,
G(s)>0
if and only if

\widetilde{G}(s):=\frac{\alpha}{2}\left(\ln s^{2}-1\right)+\frac{\mu}{p}s^{p-2}>0.

Since

\widetilde{G}^{\prime}(s)=\frac{\alpha}{s}+\mu\frac{p-2}{p}s^{p-3},

we have

\max\widetilde{G}=\frac{\alpha}{2}\left(\ln\left(\frac{\alpha p}{\mu(2-p)}\right)^{2/(p-2)}-1\right)-\frac{\alpha}{p-2}

and the claim holds because
(g4)
is equivalent to
\max\widetilde{G}>0
. Point (i) is then a consequence of Theorem
1.1
. Concerning point (ii), observe that
\max G=0
if and only if
\mu=-\frac{\alpha p}{p-2}e^{-p/2}
. Since, from
[
24
, Proposition 3.1]
, every solution to (
1.1
) satisfies the Pohožaev identity (
3.3
), the statement easily holds true if
\lambda>0
or
N\geq 3
or
\mu<-\frac{\alpha p}{p-2}e^{-p/2}
. Now let us consider the case
\lambda=0
,
N=2
, and
\mu=-\frac{\alpha p}{p-2}e^{-p/2}
and assume by contradiction that such a
u
exists. Since (
3.3
) reads
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}G(u)\,dx=0
,
G\leq 0
, and
u
is continuous (se, e.g.,
[
32
, p. 271]
), necessarily
u\equiv 0
or
u\equiv\pm\bar{s}
, where
\bar{s}
is the unique
s>0
such that
G(s)=0
, which contradicts
|u|_{2}=\rho\in(0,\infty)
.
∎

4.
Multiple solutions

4.1.
Functional setting

Let us recall from
[
27
]
that a function
A\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is called an
N-function
if and only if it is nonnegative, even, convex, and satisfies

\lim_{s\to 0}\frac{A(s)}{s}=\lim_{s\to\infty}\frac{A(s)}{s}=\infty.

It is said to satisfy the
\Delta_{2}
condition
globally if and only if there exists
K>0
such that

A(2s)\leq KA(s)\quad\text{for all }s\in\mathbb{R}.

It is said to satisfy the
\nabla_{2}
condition
globally if and only if there exist
\ell>1
such that

2\ell A(s)\leq A(\ell s)\quad\text{for all }s\in\mathbb{R}.

Equivalently,
A
satisfies the
\Delta_{2}
(respectively,
\nabla_{2}
) condition globally if and only if there exists
C>1
such that

(4.1)

CA(s)\geq sA^{\prime}(s)\;\text{(respectively, $sA^{\prime}(s)\geq CA(s)$)}\quad\text{for all }s\in\mathbb{R}.

From now on,
A
shall denote the same function as in (A). We can define the
Orlicz space
associated with
A
as

V:=\left\{u\in L^{1}_{\textup{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{N}):A(u)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\right\}.

If we define the norm

\|u\|_{V}:=\inf\left\{\kappa>0:\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}A(u/\kappa)\,dx\leq 1\right\},

then
(V,\|\cdot\|_{V})
is a reflexive Banach space (cf.
[
27
, Theorem IV.I.10]
).
Moreover, the following holds true.

Lemma 4.1
.

(i) Let
u_{n},u\in V
. Then
\lim_{n}\|u_{n}-u\|_{V}=0
if and only if
\lim_{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}A(u_{n}-u)\,dx=0
.

(ii) Let
X\subset V
. Then
X
is bounded if and only if

\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}A(u)\,dx:u\in X\right\}

is bounded.

(iii) Let
u_{n},u\in V
. If
u_{n}\to u
a.e. and
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}A(u_{n})\,dx\to\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}A(u)\,dx
, then
\|u_{n}-u\|_{V}\to 0
.

Proof.

The first two points follow from
[
27
, Theorem III.IV.12 and Corollary III.IV.15]
respectively, while the third one is a consequence of the previous two and
[
7
, Theorem 2, Example (b)]
.
∎

Note that
W=H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap V
. Since
(V,\|\cdot\|_{V})
is a reflexive Banach space, so is
(W,\|\cdot\|_{W})
, with

\|u\|_{W}^{2}:=|\nabla u|_{2}^{2}+|u|_{2}^{2}+\|u\|_{V}^{2}.

We obtain the following variant of Lions’ lemma.

Lemma 4.2
.

Suppose that
u_{n}\in W
is bounded and for some
r>0

(4.2)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\int_{B(y,r)}|u_{n}|^{2}\,dx=0.

Then
u_{n}\to 0
in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
for every
p\in[2,2^{*})
.

Proof.

In view of Lions’ lemma
[
21
, Lemma I.1 in Part 2]
,
u_{n}\to 0
in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
for every
p\in(2,2^{*})
. We prove the convergence in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
.
Take any
p\in(2,2^{*})
and
\varepsilon>0
. We find
\delta>0
such that

\begin{split}|s|^{2}&\leq\varepsilon A(s)\quad\hbox{ if }|s|\in[0,\delta],\\
|s|^{2}&\leq\delta^{2-p}|s|^{p}\quad\hbox{ if }|s|>\delta,\end{split}

Hence, we get

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|u_{n}|^{2}\,dx\leq\varepsilon\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}A(u_{n})\,dx+\delta^{2-p}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|u_{n}|^{p}\,dx=\varepsilon\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}A(u_{n})\,dx.

In view of Lemma
4.1
(ii), we conclude by letting
\varepsilon\to 0
.
∎

Let
{\mathcal{O}}
be any subgroup
{\mathcal{O}}(N)
such that
\mathbb{R}^{N}
is compatible with
{\mathcal{O}}

(cf.
[
20
]
), i.e.,

\lim_{|y|\to\infty}m(y,r)=\infty
for some
r>0
,
where, for
y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,

m(y,r):=\sup\big\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\hbox{there exist }g_{1},\dots,g_{n}\in{\mathcal{O}}\hbox{ such that }B(g_{i}y,r)\cap B(g_{j}y,r)=\emptyset\hbox{ for }i\neq j\big\}.

In view of
[
20
]
,
H^{1}_{{\mathcal{O}}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
embeds compactly into
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
for
2<p<2^{*}
. As for the subspace
W_{\mathcal{O}}:=W\cap H^{1}_{{\mathcal{O}}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, we obtain the compact embedding also in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
.

Corollary 4.3
.

Suppose that
u_{n}\in W_{{\mathcal{O}}}
is bounded and
u_{n}\to 0
in
L^{2}_{\textup{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. Then
u_{n}\to 0
in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
for every
p\in[2,2^{*})
.

Proof.

Suppose that

(4.3)

\int_{B(y_{n},1)}|u_{n}|^{2}\,dx\geq c>0

for some sequence
y_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and a constant
c
.
Observe that in the family
\{B(hy_{n},1)\}_{h\in{\mathcal{O}}}
we find an increasing number of disjoint balls provided that
|y_{n}|\to\infty
. Since
u_{n}
is bounded in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
and invariant with respect to
{\mathcal{O}}
, by (
4.3
)
(y_{n})
must be bounded. Then for sufficiently large
r
one obtains

\int_{B(0,r)}|u_{n}|^{2}\,dx\geq\int_{B(y_{n},1)}|u_{n}|^{2}\,dx\geq c>0,

and we get a contradiction with the convergence of
u_{n}
in
L^{2}_{\textup{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. Therefore by Lemma
4.2
we conclude.
∎

Finally, we have the following result.

Proposition 4.4
.

If (A) and (f0)–(f3) hold, then the functional
J|_{W}\colon W\to\mathbb{R}
is of class
{\mathcal{C}}^{1}
and, for every
u\in W\cap{\mathcal{S}}
,
J|_{W\cap{\mathcal{S}}}^{\prime}(u)=0
if and only if there exists
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
such that
(u,\lambda)
is a solution to (
1.1
)–(
1.2
).

Proof.

Let
B\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be the complementary
N
-function of
A
. From
[
27
, Theorem II.V.8]
, it satisfies the
\Delta_{2}
and
\nabla_{2}
conditions globally because
A
does. Let us recall (cf.
[
27
, Definition III.IV.2, Corollary III.IV.5, and Corollary IV.II.9]
) that
V^{\prime}
, the dual space of
V
, is isomorphic to the Orlicz space associated with
B
. From
[
27
, Theorem I.III.3]
,

B\bigl(a(s)\bigr)=sa(s)-A(s)\leq(C-1)A(s),

where
C>1
is the constant given in the characterization of the
\Delta_{2}
condition – i.e., (
4.1
). Then, for every
u,v\in V
, we have

\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}a(u)v\,dx\right|\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|a(u)||v|\,dx\lesssim\|a(u)\|_{V^{\prime}}\|v\|_{V}.

Now we can use the same argument as in
[
12
, Lemma 2.1]
to obtain that the functionals
u\mapsto\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}A(u)\,dx
and
u\mapsto\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}F(u)\,dx
belong to
{\mathcal{C}}^{1}(W)
. The remaining part is obvious.
∎

4.2.
Proof of Theorem
1.8

We recall the definitions
F_{-}:=F_{+}-F
and
f_{-}:=F_{-}^{\prime}
.

Lemma 4.5
.

If (A), (f0)–(f3), and (
1.12
) hold, then
J|_{W\cap{\mathcal{S}}}
is coercive and bounded below.

Proof.

For every
\delta>0
, there exists
C_{\delta}>0
such that for every
s\in\mathbb{R}

F_{+}(s)\leq C_{\delta}|s|^{2}+(\eta+\delta)|s|^{2+4/N}.

For every
u\in W\cap{\mathcal{S}}
there holds

\begin{split}J(u)&\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u|^{2}+A(u)-F_{+}(u)\,dx\\
&\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u|^{2}+A(u)-C_{\delta}|u|^{2}-(\eta+\delta)|u|^{2+4/N}\,dx\\
&\geq\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u|_{2}^{2}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}A(u)\,dx-C_{\delta}\rho^{2}-(\eta+\delta)C_{N,2+4/N}^{2+4/N}\rho^{4/N}|\nabla u|_{2}^{2}.\end{split}

We conclude by Lemma
4.1
(ii) and taking
\delta
so small that
2(\eta+\delta)C_{N,2+4/N}^{2+4/N}\rho^{4/N}<1
.
∎

Remark 4.6
.

Setting
f_{\textup{p}}:=\max\{f,0\}
and
f_{\textup{n}}:=\max\{-f,0\}
, we have that
f_{-}(s)=f_{\textup{n}}(s)
if
s\geq 0
and
f_{-}(s)=-f_{\textup{p}}(s)
if
s<0
. In particular,
f_{-}(s)s\geq 0
for every
s\in\mathbb{R}
.

Lemma 4.7
.

If (A) and (f0)–(f3) hold, then
J|_{W_{{\mathcal{O}}}}\cap{\mathcal{S}}
satisfies the Palais–Smale condition.

Proof.

Let
u_{n}\in W_{{\mathcal{O}}}\cap{\mathcal{S}}
such that
J(u_{n})
is bounded and
J|_{W_{{\mathcal{O}}}\cap{\mathcal{S}}}^{\prime}(u_{n})\to 0
. From Lemma
4.5
,
u_{n}
is bounded in
W
, therefore there exists
u\in W_{{\mathcal{O}}}
such that
u_{n}\rightharpoonup u
in
W_{\mathcal{O}}
up to a subsequence. Then, from Corollary
4.3
,
u_{n}\to u
in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
for every
p\in[2,2^{*})
; in particular,
u\in{\mathcal{S}}
. Up to a second subsequence, we can assume that
u_{n}\to u
a.e. in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Additionally, from
[
3
, Lemma 3]
, there exist
\lambda_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

(4.4)

-\Delta u_{n}+\lambda_{n}u_{n}-g(u_{n})u_{n}\to 0\quad\text{in }W_{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime},

where
W_{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime}
is the dual space of
W_{\mathcal{O}}
. Testing (
4.4
) with
u_{n}
, we obtain that
\lambda_{n}
is bounded as well, hence there exists
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\lambda_{n}\to\lambda
up to a subsequence, and
(u,\lambda)
is a solution to (
1.1
).
Finally, from the Nehari identity and the fact that
\lambda_{n}\to\lambda
, we obtain

\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\nabla u|^{2}+a(u)u+f_{-}(u)u\,dx&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}f_{+}(u)u-\lambda|u|^{2}\,dx=\lim_{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}f_{+}(u_{n})u_{n}-\lambda|u_{n}|^{2}\,dx\\
&=\lim_{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\nabla u_{n}|^{2}+a(u_{n})u_{n}+f_{-}(u_{n})u_{n}\,dx,\end{split}

which, together with Remark
4.6
, implies that
|\nabla u_{n}|_{2}^{2}\to|\nabla u|_{2}^{2}
and
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}a(u_{n})u_{n}\,dx\to\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}a(u)u\,dx
. It remains to prove that
\|u_{n}-u\|_{V}\to 0
. In virtue of Lemma
4.1
(iii), it suffices to prove that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}A(u_{n})\,dx\to\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}A(u)\,dx
. Additionally, since
A
satisfies the
\nabla_{2}
condition globally, this occurs if the sequence
a(u_{n})u_{n}
is bounded above by an integrable function, which holds true from (A) and
[
7
, Example (b)]
.
∎

Lemma 4.8
.

For every
k\geq 1
there exist
\pi_{k}\colon\mathbb{S}^{k-1}\to W_{{\mathcal{O}}(N)}\cap{\mathcal{S}}
and
\tilde{\pi}_{k}\colon\mathbb{S}^{k-1}\to{\mathcal{X}}\cap{\mathcal{S}}
odd and continuous.

Proof.

The existence of
\pi_{k}
was proved in
[
3
, Theorem 10, Lemma 8]
(with the notations therein, note that we can take any number different from
0
instead of
\zeta
because we do not need
V(u)\geq 1
). Then, following
[
17
, Proof of Lemma 3.4]
and
[
23
, Remark 4.2]
, we take
\phi\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
odd such that
0\leq\phi\leq 1
and
\phi(t)=1
for all
t\geq 1
, and define for
\sigma\in\mathbb{S}^{k-1}

\tilde{\pi}_{k}(\sigma)(x):=\pi_{k}(\sigma)(x)\phi(|x_{1}|-|x_{2}|)\quad\text{for all }x=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{M}\times\mathbb{R}^{M}\times\mathbb{R}^{N-2M}.\qed

We make use of the following abstract theorem, where
{\mathcal{G}}
stands for the Krasnoselsky genus
[
32
, Chapter 5]
.

Theorem 4.9
.

Let
E
be a Banach space,
H\supset E
a Hilbert space with scalar product
(\cdot|\cdot)
,
R>0
,
{\mathcal{M}}:=\left\{u\in E:(u|u)=R\right\}
, and
I\in{\mathcal{C}}^{1}(E)
even such that
I|_{\mathcal{M}}
is bounded below. For every
k\geq 1
, define

c_{k}:=\inf_{A\in\Gamma_{k}}\sup_{u\in A}I(u),\quad\Gamma_{k}:=\left\{A\subset{\mathcal{M}}:A=-A=\overline{A}\text{ and }{\mathcal{G}}(A)\geq k\right\}.

Then, for every
k\geq 1
,
-\infty<c_{1}\leq\dots\leq c_{k}\leq c_{k+1}\leq\dots
and the following holds: if there exist
k\geq 1
and
h\geq 0
such that
c_{k}=\dots=c_{k+h}<\infty
and
I|_{\mathcal{M}}
satisfies the Palais–Smale condition at the level
c_{k}
, then

{\mathcal{G}}\left(\left\{u\in{\mathcal{M}}:I(u)=c_{k}\text{ and }I|_{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}(u)=0\right\}\right)\geq h+1

(in particular, taking
h=0
, every
c_{k}
is a critical value of
I|_{\mathcal{M}}
).

Proof.

This theorem is basically (part of)
[
17
, Theorem 2.1]
(see also
[
26
]
), so we omit the proof. The only difference is that the values
c_{k}
are critical regardless of their sign, which is a consequence of
I|_{\mathcal{M}}
satisfying the Palais–Smale condition at any level.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.8
.

Let us set
E=W_{{\mathcal{O}}(N)}
(respectively,
E={\mathcal{X}}
),

,
R=\rho^{2}
,
{\mathcal{M}}={\mathcal{S}}\cap E
, and

. From Lemmas
4.5
and
4.7
,

is bounded below and satisfies the Palais–Smale condition; moreover, from Lemma

,
\pi_{k}(\mathbb{S}^{k-1})\in\Gamma_{k}
(respectively,
\tilde{\pi}_{k}(\mathbb{S}^{k-1})\in\Gamma_{k}
) for every

, so the numbers

are finite. Applying Theorem

, we conclude the part about the existence of infinitely many solutions. Concerning the existence of a least-energy solution, we consider a sequence

such that

. From Ekeland’s variational principle, we can assume that

)–(
