Abstract
. We introduce a general technique to construct Lagrangian torus fibrations in degenerations of Kähler manifolds. We show that such torus fibrations naturally occur at the boundary of the A’Campo space . This space extends a degeneration over a punctured disc to a hybrid space involving its real oriented blowup and the dual complex of the special fiber; equipped with an exact modification of a given Kähler form which becomes fiberwise symplectic at the boundary. Therefore, it allows to move Lagrangian tori from the “radius zero” fibers at the boundary to the nearby fibers of the original generation using the symplectic connection. As a consequence, for any maximal Calabi–Yau degeneration we prove that a generic region of each nearby fiber admits a Lagrangian torus fibration with asymptotic properties expected from the (conjectural) Strominger–Yau–Zaslow fibration.

Introduction

The Strominger–Yau–Zaslow conjecture
[
SYZ96
]
interprets mirror symmetry for Calabi–Yau manifolds in terms of conjectural fibrations by special Lagrangian tori: for a mirror pair, these fibrations are expected to become dual when one takes large volume limit on one side, and large complex structure limit on the other, see
[
AB+09
, Conjecture 1.5]
. The latter limit gives rise to a maximal Calabi–Yau degeneration. In this setting, explicit conjectures concerning existence and geometric properties of special Lagrangian torus fibrations were formulated by Kontsevich and Soibelman in
[
KS01
, Conjectures 1, 2]
. In this article, we prove their asymptotic versions. In order to state our main results, we briefly recall the relevant definitions; for more details see Section
2
or e.g.
[
AB+09
,
Gro13
,
Li22b
]
.

A
maximal Calabi–Yau degeneration
is a complex projective morphism
f^{\circ}:X^{\circ}\longrightarrow\mathbb{D}^{*}
, where
\mathbb{D}^{*}
is a punctured disc, such that the fibers
X_{z}\coloneqq(f^{\circ})^{-1}(z)
are Calabi–Yau
n
-folds, and the monodromy has Jordan blocks of maximal size
n+1
. Applying the semistable reduction theorem
[
KK+73
]
after a finite base change we can extend
f^{\circ}
to a model
f\colon X\longrightarrow\mathbb{D}
such that
X
is smooth, the central fiber
D=\sum_{i}D_{i}
is a reduced snc divisor, and
f|_{X\setminus D}=f^{\circ}
. Since each fiber of
f^{\circ}
is Calabi–Yau, there exists a holomorphic section
\Omega\in\Gamma(X,K_{X/\mathbb{D}})
with zeros only at the central fiber. Multiplying
\Omega
by a holomorphic function on the base
\mathbb{D}^{*}
, we can assume that the divisor of
\Omega
is
\sum_{i}a_{i}D_{i}
, with
\mathrm{min}_{i}a_{i}=0
. We say that a component
D_{i}
is
essential
if
a_{i}=0
. The
essential skeleton

\Delta_{\mathcal{S}}
of
f^{\circ}
is a subcomplex of the dual complex of
D
spanned by vertices corresponding to essential divisors.

Let
t\coloneqq-1/\log|f|
. It is known, cf.
[
KS01
, §3]
or Section
7.2
, that
\mathrm{vol}^{\Omega}_{\mathrm{new}}\coloneqq t^{n}(\frac{\imath}{2})^{n}(-1)^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}\Omega\wedge\overline{\Omega}
restricts to a volume form on each
X_{z}
such that the limit of the volume of
X_{z}
is finite as
z\rightarrow 0
. Let
\omega_{X}
be any Kähler form in
X
. Yau’s proof of the Calabi conjecture
[

]
implies that there exists a family of potentials
\phi_{z}^{\textnormal{CY}}\colon X_{z}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
such that the Calabi–Yau metric
\omega_{X_{z}}^{\textnormal{CY}}\coloneqq\omega_{X}|_{X_{z}}+dd^{c}\phi_{z}^{\textnormal{CY}}
is
Ricci flat
for all
z
. Ricci flatness is equivalent to the existence of a function
c\colon\mathbb{D}^{*}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
such that

(1)

\tfrac{1}{n!}(\omega_{X_{z}}^{\textnormal{CY}})^{n}=c(z)\cdot\textnormal{vol}^{\Omega}_{\mathrm{new}}|_{X_{z}}.

The rescaling in the above definition of
\textnormal{vol}^{\Omega}_{\mathrm{new}}
ensures that
\lim_{z\rightarrow 0}c(z)
exists and is nonzero.

Motivated by Mirror Symmetry, Strominger, Yau and Zaslow

predicted that Calabi–Yau

-Lagrangian fibrations by tori which are special with respect to

[

,

]

[

,
Rua07
,
CBM09
,
GTZ13
,
RZ21
,
EM21
,
MPS23
]

This article is mostly inspired by the formulation due to Kontsevich and Soibelman
[
KS01
, §3]
. In short, the main aspects of their prediction are as follows; for details see Conjectures 1 and 2 loc. cit.

1.

Gromov–Hausdorff limit
.  On each fiber
X_{z}
, consider the metric induced by the Calabi–Yau form
\omega_{X_{z}}^{\textnormal{CY}}

X_{z}
is independent of

as

is a metric space
\Delta

n

whose complement in

has a potential satisfying the real Monge-Ampère equation, i.e. it is locally given by a matrix
\operatorname{Hess}(K)
for some function

\det\operatorname{Hess}(K)
is constant.

2.

Special Lagrangian fibration
.  There is
\delta>0
such that for any

, there is a subset
X^{\textnormal{sm}}_{z}\subseteq X_{z}
and a fibration

equipped with the rescaled Calabi–Yau metric as in

It is expected that as

[

Moreover the Gromov–Hausdorff limit

The main novelty of this article is a general technique that allows to construct fibrations by Lagrangian tori in Kähler degenerations, where fibers do not need to be Calabi–Yau nor compact. Theorems

1.2

(a)
Put

3.4.
The rounded dual complex

The

For every nonempty subset

(\pi_{\log},(\widehat{v}_{i})_{i\in I})\colon A_{I}^{\circ}\longrightarrow(X_{I}^{\circ})_{\log}\times\widetilde{\Delta}_{I}

We identify

as in Section

. We put

with its preimage in

4.1.

Recall that by Proposition

J
-compatible, i.e. we have an equality
\omega^{\sharp}(J\,\cdot\,,J\,\cdot\,)=\omega^{\sharp}(\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,)
on the tangent space to the fiber
W_{z}
for all
z\in\mathbb{D}_{\delta}^{*}
.

Proof.

Clearly, it is enough to prove the first assertion. If
D
has only one component
D_{1}
then
\widehat{s}_{1}=-m_{1}t^{-1}
is fiberwise constant, hence
\lambda^{\sharp}=-\widehat{v}_{1}\cdot d^{c}\widehat{s}_{1}
is fiberwise zero. Therefore, we can assume that
D
has at least two components. Fix
i\in\{1,\dots,N\}
.

By definition (
17
), the function
\widehat{v}_{i}\colon W_{X}\setminus D\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
is equal to the composition
\check{v}_{i}\circ(\widehat{s}_{i},t)
, where

\check{v}_{i}\colon T\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}\quad\mbox{is defined as}\quad\check{v}_{i}(\check{s},\check{t})=-(m_{i}\check{s})^{-1}-\eta(-m_{i}\cdot\check{s}\check{t}),

and
T\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
is the image of the map
(\widehat{s}_{i},t)\colon W_{X}\setminus D\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

We claim that
T
is a union of some horizontal segments (or half-lines), ending on the vertical line
\check{s}=-1
. By Lemma
3.1
(d)
we have
\widehat{s}_{i}\leqslant-1
, and the equality
\widehat{s}_{i}=-1
holds on
X\setminus R_{i}
. Since
D
has at least two components, the set
X\setminus D_{i}
contains a nonempty set
R_{j}^{\circ}
for some
j\neq i
, which meets each fiber by property
(d)
of the atlas used to define the functions
\widehat{r}_{i}
. Thus the equality
\widehat{s}_{i}=-1
is attained somewhere on each fiber, and the claim follows since each fiber is connected.

Therefore, we can define a smooth function
\check{\phi}_{i}\colon T\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
by the formula
\check{\phi}_{i}(\check{s},\check{t})=\int_{\check{s}}^{-1}\check{v}_{i}(s,\check{t})\,ds
. Fundamental theorem of calculus gives
\frac{\partial}{\partial\check{s}}\check{\phi}_{i}=-\check{v}_{i}
. We define a smooth function
\phi_{i}\colon W_{X}\setminus D\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
as
\phi_{i}\coloneqq\check{\phi}_{i}\circ(\widehat{s}_{i},t)
. Since
\frac{\partial}{\partial\check{s}}\check{\phi}_{i}=-\check{v}_{i}
, we have a fiberwise equality
d\phi_{i}=-\check{v}_{i}\circ(\widehat{s}_{i},t)\,d\widehat{s}_{i}=-\widehat{v}_{i}\,d\widehat{s}_{i}
, so
d^{c}\phi_{i}=-\widehat{v}_{i}\,d^{c}\widehat{s}_{i}=\widehat{v}_{i}\widehat{\alpha}_{i}
by definition (
17
) of
\widehat{\alpha}_{i}
. Eventually, we put
\phi=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\phi_{i}
, so we have a fiberwise equality
d^{c}\phi=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\widehat{v}_{i}\widehat{\alpha}_{i}=\lambda^{\sharp}
, as needed.

∎

4.2.
The
“semi-flat form”

\omega^{\flat}

As in Section
2.1
, we fix any subset
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\{1,\dots,N\}
: in the setting of Calabi–Yau degenerations, it will correspond to essential divisors. Now, we define smooth forms on
A
:

(21)

\lambda^{\flat}=-\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}m_{i}\,\widehat{w}_{i}\cdot\widehat{\alpha}_{i},\quad\omega^{\flat}=d\lambda^{\flat}.

Lemma 4.2
.

Put
\phi^{\flat}=-\frac{1}{2t}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\widehat{w}_{i}^{2}
. Then the form
d^{c}\phi^{\flat}
extends to a smooth form on
A
, and we have an equality
\lambda^{\flat}=d^{c}\phi^{\flat}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\widehat{w}_{i}^{2}\cdot d\theta
. In particular, we have a fiberwise equality
\lambda^{\flat}=d^{c}\phi^{\flat}
, so the form
\omega^{\flat}
is fiberwise
J
-compatible.

Proof.

Using identities
\widehat{w}_{i}=-m_{i}\widehat{s}_{i}t
and
-d^{c}\widehat{s}_{i}=\widehat{\alpha}_{i}
from (
17
), we get

d^{c}\widehat{w}_{i}=-m_{i}\,d^{c}(\widehat{s}_{i}t)=m_{i}t\,\widehat{\alpha}_{i}-m_{i}\widehat{s}_{i}d^{c}t=m_{i}t\,\widehat{\alpha}_{i}+\tfrac{1}{t}\widehat{w}_{i}\,d^{c}t.

Put
\phi_{i}^{\flat}=-\tfrac{1}{2t}\widehat{w}_{i}^{2}
, so
\phi^{\flat}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\phi_{i}^{\flat}
. The above equality gives

d^{c}\phi_{i}^{\flat}=-\tfrac{1}{t}\widehat{w}_{i}\,d^{c}\widehat{w}_{i}+\tfrac{1}{2t^{2}}\widehat{w}_{i}^{2}\,d^{c}t=-m_{i}\widehat{w}_{i}\widehat{\alpha}_{i}-\tfrac{1}{2t^{2}}\widehat{w}_{i}^{2}\,d^{c}t.

Moreover, we have an equality

d^{c}t=-t^{2}d\theta.

Indeed, putting
s=\log|f|
we see that
d^{c}s=-d\theta
and
t=-s^{-1}
, so
d^{c}t=-d^{c}s^{-1}=s^{-2}d^{c}s=-t^{2}d\theta
, as claimed. Substituting this to the previous equality, we conclude that

d^{c}\phi_{i}^{\flat}=-m_{i}\,\widehat{w}_{i}\alpha_{i}+\tfrac{1}{2}\widehat{w}_{i}^{2}\,d\theta.

By Proposition
3.3
(b)
,
(c)
, the functions
\theta
,
\widehat{w}_{i}
and the form
\widehat{\alpha}_{i}
are smooth in
A
, hence so is
d^{c}\phi_{i}^{\flat}
. The result follows after taking a sum over all
i\in\mathcal{S}
.

∎

5.1.
Ivy-like manifolds

An
open graph
is a topological space
C
obtained as a quotient of a finite disjoint union of closed and semi-closed intervals, by some equivalence relation identifying only some endpoints of different intervals. The images of those endpoints are called
vertices
of
C
, a
degree
of such vertex is the number of intervals containing it. The set of vertices of degree at least
3
is called a
ramification locus
and denoted
\operatorname{Ram}(C)
; similarly, the set of vertices of degree
1
is called the
boundary
of
C
, and denoted by
\partial C
. Clearly,
C\setminus\operatorname{Ram}(C)
is a
1
-dimensional manifold with boundary
\partial C
. Since we are only interested in
C
as a topological space, we can and do assume that no vertex has degree
2
.

An
ivy
is an open graph
C
together with a proper continuous map
p\colon C\longrightarrow(0,\infty)
whose restriction to each interval constituting
C
is a homeomorphism onto its image. Note that the restriction of
p
to
C\setminus(\operatorname{Ram}(C)\sqcup\partial C)
is a local homeomorphism. We choose a smooth structure on
C\setminus\operatorname{Ram}(C)
such that the restriction
p|_{C\setminus\operatorname{Ram}(C)}
becomes a local diffeomorphism onto its image.

We now return to abstract definitions. An
ivy-like chart
(
with boundary and corners
) is a homeomorphism
\varphi\colon V\longrightarrow U\times C
from a topological space
V
to a product
U\times C
of a smooth manifold
U
(with boundary and corners) and an ivy
p\colon C\longrightarrow(0,\infty)
. The
ramification locus
of such chart is
\operatorname{Ram}(V)\coloneqq\varphi^{-1}(U\times\operatorname{Ram}(C))
, and
outer boundary
is
\partial_{\textnormal{out}}V\coloneqq\varphi^{-1}(U\times\partial C)
. Note that
V\setminus\operatorname{Ram}(V)
inherits a structure of a smooth manifold with boundary and corners from
U\times(C\setminus\operatorname{Ram}(C))
.

We say that a continuous, surjective map
\psi\colon N\longrightarrow V
from a smooth manifold (with boundary and corners) to an ivy-like chart
V
as above is an
ivy-like submersion
if it locally comes from a Stein factorization, i.e. the following holds. There is an open covering
N=\bigcup_{i}N_{i}
and for each
i
, there is a smooth manifold
N_{i}^{\prime}
(with boundary and corners), a Riemann surface
M_{i}
, and a diffeomorphism
\tau_{i}\colon N_{i}^{\prime}\times M_{i}\longrightarrow N_{i}
so that the composition
\varphi|_{\psi(N_{i})}\circ\psi|_{N_{i}}\circ\tau_{i}
is of the form
(\varphi^{\prime},\overline{h}_{i})
, where
\varphi^{\prime}\colon N_{i}^{\prime}\longrightarrow U
is a submersion onto its image, and
\overline{h}_{i}
is the Stein factorization of some Morse function
h_{i}\colon M_{i}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
, see Example
5.1
. Clearly, an ivy-like submersion restricts to a usual submersion over
V\setminus\operatorname{Ram}(V)
.

It will be convenient to call a
usual chart
a homeomorphism
\varphi\colon V\longrightarrow U
for some smooth manifold
U
(with boundary and corners). In this case we put
\operatorname{Ram}(V)=\partial_{\textnormal{out}}V=\emptyset
.

An
ivy-like manifold
(with boundary and corners) is a Hausdorff topological space
E
together with an open covering
E=\bigcup_{i}V_{i}
, such that each
V_{i}
is a domain of a usual or ivy-like chart
\varphi_{i}
; for every
i\neq j
we have
\operatorname{Ram}(V_{i})\cap\operatorname{Ram}(V_{j})=\emptyset
; and the transition maps
\varphi_{j}|_{V_{i}\cap V_{j}}\circ\varphi_{i}^{-1}|_{\varphi_{i}(V_{i}\cap V_{j})}
are smooth, as maps between usual manifolds (with boundary and corners). We define its ramification locus
\operatorname{Ram}(E)
and outer boundary
\partial_{\textnormal{out}}E
as the union of, respectively, all ramification loci and outer boundaries of the charts. A collection
\{(V_{i},\varphi_{i})\}
is called an
ivy-like atlas
. Note that the subspace
E\setminus\operatorname{Ram}(E)
is a manifold (with boundary and corners) in the usual sense; and
\partial_{\textnormal{out}}E\subseteq\partial E
.

A continuous, surjective map
\psi\colon N\longrightarrow E
from a smooth manifold
N
with boundary and corners to an ivy-like manifold
E
as above is an
ivy-like submersion
if for every
i
, the composition
\varphi_{i}\circ\psi|_{\psi^{-1}(V_{i})}
is a (usual, or ivy-like) submersion. In particular,
\psi
restricts to a usual submersion over
E\setminus\operatorname{Ram}(E)
.

Definition 5.2

(Ivy-like Lagrangian torus fibrations)
.

Let
(Y,\omega)
be a symplectic manifold of dimension
2n
, and let
\iota\colon L\longrightarrow Y
be an embedding (or an immersion). We say that its image
\iota(L)
is an (
immersed
)
isotropic submanifold
if
\iota^{*}\omega=0
. It is (
immersed
)
Lagrangian
if additionally
\dim L=n
.

Let
E
be an ivy-like manifold (with boundary and corners). We say that a map
{l}\colon Y\longrightarrow E
is an
ivy-like Lagrangian torus fibration
if it is an ivy-like submersion and for every
p\in E
, and every connected component
T
of the fiber
{l}^{-1}(p)
the following hold.

(a)

If
p\in E\setminus(\operatorname{Ram}E\cup\partial_{\textnormal{out}}E)
then
T
is Lagrangian torus
(\mathbb{S}^{1})^{n}
.

(b)

If
p\in\operatorname{Ram}E
then
T
is an immersed Lagrangian torus
(\mathbb{S}^{1})^{n}
.

(c)

if
p\in\partial_{\textnormal{out}}E
then
T
is an isotropic torus
(\mathbb{S}^{1})^{n-1}
.

We say that
{l}
is a
Lagrangian torus fibration
if
\operatorname{Ram}E\cup\partial_{\textnormal{out}}E=\emptyset
. In this case,
E
is a manifold (with boundary and corners), the map
{l}
is a usual submersion and all fibers of
{l}
are Lagrangian tori.

. Eventually, we glue to

a usual chart

and an ivy-like chart

.

It remains to prove claim (
27
). Clearly, the function
p
is smooth on the interior of
A_{J}^{\circ}
, where it equals the coordinate
v_{1}
; and on

, where it is a pullback of a smooth function from

. It remains to prove that all derivatives of

approach

as we approach

from

, i.e. as
v_{1}\searrow 0
.

Recall that
z=\lambda(z_{1})\cdot z_{1}
for some non-vanishing, holomorphic function
\lambda
such that
\lambda(0)=1
. Moreover, on
A_{I}^{\circ}
we have
v_{1}=t_{1}=\frac{-1}{m_{1}\log|z_{1}|}
, so
p=\frac{-1}{m_{1}\log|z|}=\frac{-1}{m_{1}\log|\lambda(z_{1})|+m_{1}\log|z_{1}|}=\frac{v_{1}}{1-v_{1}m_{1}\log|\lambda(z_{1})|}
. Thus
p-v_{1}=\frac{v_{1}^{2}m_{1}\log|\lambda(z_{1})|}{1-v_{1}m_{1}\log|\lambda(z_{1})|}
. By Leibniz rule, it is enough to show that
\log|\lambda(z_{1})|
and all its derivatives approach

as
v_{1}\searrow 0
. By assumption we have
\log|\lambda(0)|=\log 1=0
, so by chain rule it remains to show that all derivatives of
z_{1}
vanish as
v_{1}\searrow 0
. Since
v_{1}=\frac{-1}{m_{1}\log|z_{1}|}
on
A_{I}^{\circ}
, we have
z_{1}=\exp(-m_{1}^{-1}v_{1}^{-1}+2\pi\imath\cdot\theta_{1})
, where
\theta_{1}
is a smooth coordinate on

. Thus
dz_{1}=\exp(-m_{1}^{-1}v_{1}^{-1})\cdot\exp(2\pi\imath\cdot\theta_{1})\cdot(m_{1}^{-1}v_{1}^{-2}\,dv_{1}+2\pi\imath\,d\theta_{1})
. The result follows since all derivatives of the one-variable function

vanish at
0
.

Remark 5.4
.

If for some submaximal face
\Delta_{I}
of
\Delta_{\mathcal{S}}
we have

, then we have a canonical choice of the Morse function

, where

of

, when we take for

Remark 5.8

(Submaximal Calabi–Yau degenerations)

Our results are most powerful in case

.

6.2.
The associated metrics

Motivated by
[
KS01
, Conjectures 1 and 2]
, we study the behavior of Kähler metrics

on
X_{z}
as

. For maximal Calabi–Yau degenerations,
[

, Conjecture 1]
expects that the fiberwise Ricci-flat metric should converge, after appropriate re-scaling, to one satisfying a real Monge–Ampére equation. In our setting, an example of such metric is given by
\omega^{\flat}
, so looking at formula (
3
), it is reasonable to expect such limiting behavior as

. This motivates considering a family of Kähler metrics parametrized both by

and

.

Before we state our result in Proposition

, we need some preparations. We denote by

the vertical tangent bundle to the A’Campo space, i.e. the kernel of the map

.

Recall that we identify

with its image

; in particular we have there the standard complex structure

induced from

. We study the following sections of

\begin{split}g^{\sharp}=\omega^{\sharp}(\,\cdot\,,J\,\cdot\,),\ g^{\flat}=\omega^{\flat}(\,\cdot\,,J\,\cdot\,),\ g_{q}=\omega_{q}^{\varepsilon}(\,\cdot\,,J\,\cdot\,)\\
\mbox{and}\quad g^{\sharp}_{\mathrm{new}}=t\cdot g^{\sharp},\ g^{\flat}_{\mathrm{new}}=t\cdot g^{\flat},\ g_{q,\mathrm{new}}=t\cdot g_{q}.\end{split}

Since

and

are fiberwise

6.3.
Admissible paths

We have set up a bi-parametric approach to study the degeneration of Kähler manifolds
X_{z}^{q}
, where the first parameter
z
determines the fiber
X_{z}
of the degeneration, and the second one,
q
, determines the Kähler form
\omega_{q}^{\varepsilon}
within the family (
3
). To prove our main results, we choose some paths in this bi-parametric model. First, we distinguish
admissible paths
as those used in Theorem
1.1
(c)
and Theorem
1.2
\theenumthmii
,
\theenumthmii
, that is, we pose the following definition.

Definition 6.7

(see Figure
1(a)
)
.

A smooth path
\gamma=(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})\colon[0,\rho)\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}_{\log}\times[0,1]
is
admissible
if
|\gamma_{1}(0)|=\gamma_{2}(0)=0
and
|\gamma_{1}(\tau)|>0
,
\gamma_{2}(\tau)>0
for
\tau>0
.

Let
(r,\theta)
be coordinates on
\mathbb{C}_{\log}=[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
. In our setting, it is natural to replace

by
t=e^{-\frac{1}{|f|}}
, see formula (
11
): indeed, both the volume
\textnormal{vol}^{\Omega}_{\mathrm{new}}
and the metrics

, defined to give a finite nonzero limits as

, required a rescaling by a power of

, see Sections
1
and
6.2
. So, we endow
\mathbb{C}_{\log}
with another smooth coordinate system, not compatible with
(r,\theta)
, given by
(t,\theta)=(e^{-\frac{1}{r}},\theta)
.

For any admissible path
\gamma\colon[0,\rho)\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}_{\log}\times[0,1]
, the restriction
\gamma|_{[0,\rho^{\prime})}
for some
\rho^{\prime}>0
can be parametrized, in the above coordinates
(t,\theta)
of
\mathbb{C}_{\log}
, as
\gamma(h)=(h,\theta(h),q(h))
, where
\theta\colon[0,\rho^{\prime})\longrightarrow\mathbb{S}^{1}
and
q\colon[0,\rho^{\prime})\longrightarrow[0,1]
are continuous functions whose restrictions to
(0,\rho^{\prime})
are smooth. Such a parametrization will be called
normal
. We can now distinguish paths used in Theorem
1.2
\theenumthmii
.

Definition 6.8
.

Let
\gamma(h)=(h,\theta(h),q(h))
be a normal parametrization of an admissible path
\gamma
. We say that
\gamma
is

if the function

is
\mathcal{C}^{2}
and
q^{\prime}(0)=0
.

Example 6.9

(see Figure
1(b)
)
.

Let
\gamma=(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})\colon[0,\rho)\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}_{\log}\times[0,1]
be an admissible path such that for all

we have
|\gamma_{1}(\tau)|=\tau
, where the absolute value refers to the natural coordinates
(r,\theta)
on
\mathbb{C}_{\log}
, see Theorem
1.2
\theenumthmii
. We claim that

is tame. Indeed, the normal parametrization of

is
(h,\theta(h),q(h))
, where

is the argument of
\gamma_{1}(e^{-\frac{1}{h}})
, and

. Now

is smooth since
\gamma_{2}
is; and
q^{\prime}(h)=-h^{-2}e^{-\frac{1}{h}}\gamma_{2}(e^{-\frac{1}{h}})\rightarrow 0
as

, as needed.

.

Let

6.4.
The Gromov–Hausdorff limit

With the above notation, we have the following consequence of Lemma
6.5
.

Lemma 6.11
.

Let
\gamma
be an admissible path. Then for every

we have

g^{\gamma}_{h,\mathrm{new}}=(1-q(h))\cdot\varepsilon\cdot\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}(d\widehat{w}_{i})^{2}+\widetilde{g}_{h},

where
\widetilde{g}_{0}=0
, and for

g_{0,\mathrm{new}}^{\gamma}=\varepsilon\cdot\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}(d\widehat{w}_{i})^{2}

Proof.

Let

be the Kähler metric on

. Recall that
\omega_{h}^{\gamma}=\omega_{q(h)}^{\varepsilon}
, so formula (

g^{\gamma}_{h,\mathrm{new}}=h\cdot\pi^{*}g_{X}+q(h)\cdot\varepsilon\cdot g^{\sharp}_{\mathrm{new}}+(1-q(h))\cdot\varepsilon\cdot g^{\flat}_{\mathrm{new}}.

Substituting the second equality of (

. Since the path

is admissible, we have

, as claimed.

∎

We can now prove the main result of this section.
For a definition and basic properties of the Gromov–Hausdorff convergence we refer to
[

Proposition 6.12
.

Let
\gamma
be an admissible path. For

, we equip the fiber

with a Riemannian metric
\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\cdot g^{\gamma}_{h,\mathrm{new}}

\lim_{h\rightarrow 0}(X_{h}^{\gamma},X_{h}^{\gamma}\setminus(X_{h}^{\gamma})_{\mathcal{S}}^{\textnormal{sm}})=(\Delta_{\mathcal{S}},\Delta_{\mathcal{S}}^{\geqslant 2}),

where
\Delta_{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq\Delta_{D}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
is the essential skeleton, see Section

, equipped with the standard metric of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
; and
\Delta_{\mathcal{S}}^{\geqslant 2}\subseteq\Delta_{\mathcal{S}}
is the union of faces of
\Delta_{\mathcal{S}}
of codimension at least

.

Proof.

For
i\in\{1,\dots,N\}
put
\widehat{w}_{i}^{\prime}=\widehat{w}_{i}
if

and
\widehat{w}_{i}^{\prime}=0
otherwise, and consider a smooth map
\mu=(\widehat{w}_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\widehat{w}_{N}^{\prime})\colon A\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}
. It is easy to see that

\mu(A_{I}^{\circ})=\Delta_{I}
for every face
\Delta_{I}
of
\Delta_{\mathcal{S}}
, cf. Lemma
3.5
or
[
FdBP22
, Lemma 3.10]
, so

\mu(X_{0}^{\gamma})=\Delta_{\mathcal{S}}
and
\mu(X_{0}^{\gamma}\setminus(X_{0}^{\gamma})_{\mathcal{S}}^{\textnormal{sm}})=\Delta_{\mathcal{S}}^{\geqslant 2}

For any

gives maps of compact metric spaces

Let

,

be the distance functions associated to the metric

and to the euclidean metric on

, respectively. Lemma

as

. Since

as

and

, see

, Definition 7.1.4]

as

.

implies that for every

such that for every

is at distance at most

. This means that

is an

[

, Definition 1.6.1]

. Shrinking

be a Calabi–Yau manifold, with a holomorphic volume form
\Omega_{Y}
and a Ricci-flat Kähler form
\omega_{\textnormal{CY}}
, see equation (
5

special of phase
\varpi\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
if
\operatorname{Im}(\varpi\cdot\Omega_{Y})|_{L}=0

\operatorname{Re}(\varpi\cdot\Omega_{Y})
, see
[

in particular they minimize volume in their homology class, see Proposition 7.1 loc. cit. The specialty condition makes the geometry of Lagrangians much more rigid, see
[

, §6.1]
; and indeed the Lagrangian tori in SYZ conjecture are expected to be special, see paragraph
(2)
in the introduction. In this section, we prove that for our tori specialty holds asymptotically, in the generic region, as claimed in Theorem
1.2
\theenumthmii
.

To give a precise statement in Proposition

below, we introduce some notation. Fix an admissible path
\gamma
, see Definition

, and a point

over

does not vanish on

, so at each point it is a nonzero complex multiple of a volume form on

. This allows us to pose the following definition.

Definition 7.6
.

Let
\textnormal{vol}_{h}
be a volume form on
L_{h}
. Let
c_{h}\colon L_{h}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{*}
be the smooth function satisfying

\textnormal{vol}_{h}=c_{h}\cdot\Omega|_{L_{h}}.

We define

as
\varpi_{h}\coloneqq\frac{c_{h}}{|c_{h}|}\colon L_{h}\longrightarrow\mathbb{S}^{1}
.

Clearly, this definition does not depend on the choice of the volume form

. We let

be the union of fibers

for all

, so the fiberwise phase map yields a smooth map
\varpi\colon L_{+}\longrightarrow\mathbb{S}^{1}

In the generic region

Lemma 7.10
.

Let

(b)

(c)

Let

\nu_{t}^{0}

(a)

(b)

(c)
Since the vector field

Proof of Corollary
7.8
.

We follow closely the argument of
[
HL82
, §III.1]
, proving that the special Lagrangians of phase
\varpi_{0}
are
\operatorname{Re}(\varpi_{0}\Omega)
-calibrated, hence volume minimizing. The only difference in our setting is that some bounds hold only up to asymptotically small errors.

We work in
(X_{h}^{\gamma})^{\textnormal{gen}}_{I}
for a fixed maximal essential face
\Delta_{I}
. Dividing the chosen holomorphic form
\Theta
by the constant (
42
), we can assume that the value of the phase map
\varpi
on
L_{0}
is
1
. Lemma
7.5
gives

(46)

\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{c_{+}}}\cdot\textnormal{vol}^{g}_{\mathrm{new}}|_{L_{h}}=\operatorname{Re}\Omega_{\mathrm{new}}|_{L_{h}}+\beta,\quad\mbox{where }\beta\rightarrow 0\mbox{ as }h\rightarrow 0.

Now, we argue as in
[
HL82
, Lemma 1.9]
. Fix
h>0
,
x\in L_{h}
, and let
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
be an orthonormal basis of
T_{x}L_{h}
. Let
H
be another
n
-dimensional subspace of
T_{x}X_{h}^{\gamma}
, let
\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{n}
be an orthonormal basis of
H
, and let
M
be a linear automorphism of
T_{x}X_{h}^{\gamma}
defined by
Me_{i}=\varepsilon_{i}
,
MJe_{i}=J\varepsilon_{i}
. Note that
M
is
\mathbb{C}
-linear, so we can compute both the complex and real determinant of
M
, and they are related by
|\det_{\mathbb{C}}M|^{2}=\det_{\mathbb{R}}M
. Using Lemma
7.5
, we compute

(\operatorname{Re}\Omega_{\mathrm{new}}(\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{n}))^{2}\leqslant|\Omega_{\mathrm{new}}(\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{n})|^{2}=|\det\nolimits_{\mathbb{C}}M|^{2}\cdot|\Omega_{\mathrm{new}}(e_{1},\dots,e_{n})|^{2}=\det\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}}M\cdot c_{+}^{-1}\leqslant c_{+}^{-1}

by Hadamard inequality and the fact that the vectors
\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{n}
have length one. Thus on
n
-dimensional subspaces of
T_{x}X_{h}^{\gamma}
we have an inequality

(47)

\operatorname{Re}\Omega_{\mathrm{new}}\leqslant\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{c_{+}}}\cdot\textnormal{vol}^{g}_{\mathrm{new}}.

On

, the above inequality becomes the
“asymptotic”
equality (
46
), cf.
[
HL82
, Theorem 1.10]
.

Now fix
\Gamma\in[L_{h}]
. The form
\Omega
, hence
\operatorname{Re}\Omega
is closed. Its rescaled version
\operatorname{Re}\Omega_{\mathrm{new}}
is therefore fiberwise closed, so Stokes theorem gives
\int_{L_{h}}\operatorname{Re}\Omega_{\mathrm{new}}=\int_{\Gamma}\operatorname{Re}\Omega_{\mathrm{new}}
. We conclude that

\int_{L_{h}}\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{c_{+}}}\cdot\textnormal{vol}^{g}_{\mathrm{new}}\overset{\mbox{\tiny{\eqref{eq:ca2}}}}{=}\int_{L_{h}}\operatorname{Re}\Omega_{\mathrm{new}}+\int_{L_{h}}\beta=\int_{\Gamma}\operatorname{Re}\Omega_{\mathrm{new}}+\int_{L_{h}}\beta\overset{\mbox{\tiny{\eqref{eq:calibration}}}}{\leqslant}\int_{\Gamma}\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{c_{+}}}\cdot\textnormal{vol}^{g}_{\mathrm{new}}+\int_{L_{h}}\beta.

The required convergence (

) follows since
\lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\beta=0
and
\lim_{h\rightarrow 0}c_{+}
is a positive constant.
