Spline-Based Adaptive Cancellation of Even-Order Intermodulation Distortions in LTE-A/5G RF Transceivers
Thomas Paireder, Christian Motz, and Mario Huemer

Abstract
Radio frequency transceivers operating in in-band full-duplex or frequency-division duplex mode experience strong transmitter leakage. Combined with receiver nonlinearities, this causes intermodulation products in the baseband, possibly with higher power than the desired receive signal. In order to restore the receiver signal-to-noise ratio in such scenarios, we propose two novel digital self-interference cancellation approaches based on spline interpolation. Both employ a Wiener structure, thereby matching the baseband model of the intermodulation effect. Unlike most state-of-the-art spline-based adaptive learning schemes, the proposed concept allows for complex-valued in- and output signals. The optimization of the model parameters is based on the stochastic gradient descent concept, where the convergence is supported by an appropriate step-size normalization. Additionally, we provide a gain control scheme and enable pipelining in order to facilitate a hardware implementation. An optional input transform improves the performance consistency for correlated sequences. In a realistic interference scenario, the proposed algorithms clearly outperform a state-of-the-art least mean squares variant with comparable complexity, which is specifically tailored to second-order intermodulation distortions. The high flexibility of the spline interpolation allows the spline-based Wiener models to match the performance of the kernel recursive least squares algorithm at less than 0.6 % of the arithmetic operations.



I
Introduction

Power efficiency is a key aspect for radio frequency transceivers in mobile communications equipment in order to ensure sufficient battery life at high data rates. Combined with other metrics, such as cost or area usage, this might lead to a decrease in receiver linearity. Given the low powers of the wanted receive signal at the input, these design trade-offs usually do not cause relevant distortions. However, in in-band full-duplex and frequency-division duplex operation, a strong transmit leakage in the receiver is unavoidable, leading to a severe deterioration of the receive signal. When using the predominant direct-conversion receiver architecture, especially even-order intermodulation distortions are a major issue, since they fall directly into the receive baseband independent of the transmit carrier frequency
[
1
]
.

In the Long-Term Evolution Advanced (LTE-A) and 5G New Radio standards, an important transmission mode is frequency-division duplex. The resulting separation of the transmit and receive carriers enables the usage of band-selection filters, in communication transceivers these are typically duplexers. These components provide a limited suppression of the transmitter-to-receiver leakage of about
5055\text{\,}\mathrm{dB}

[

27\text{\,}\mathrm{dBm}
at the output of the power amplifier, the spectrally shaped leakage still has considerable power at the duplexer receive port, causing intermodulation distortion. Besides design changes in the analog front-end, several baseband mitigation techniques exist to attenuate the intermodulation distortion interference
[
3
]
. In this work, we focus on fully digital self-interference cancellation for frequency-division duplex transceivers, where the interference is replicated based on the known transmit data. While increasing the computational burden in the digital baseband compared to mixed-signal solutions, digital self-interference mitigation approaches do not require any changes to the analog front-end and scale well with smaller technology nodes. Most published approaches target second-order intermodulation products, both, with

The generation of higher-order intermodulation distortion is best represented by Wiener models, but the required nonlinear estimation is often assumed to be unsuitable for real-time implementation on devices with limited computational resources. With the development of spline adaptive filters
[

, low-complex adaptive Wiener models for a wide range of nonlinearities are available. However, most of them target real-valued functions and, thus, are not applicable to the complex-valued intermodulation distortion cancellation problem. Two exceptions featuring spline interpolation with complex control points are presented in
[
22
,
23

In this work, we introduce two novel Wiener spline adaptive filters, comprised of a complex-valued linear system, a fixed internal nonlinearity and a real- or complex-valued spline output. The latter is achieved by combining two real-valued spline functions, where one models the real and one the imaginary part of the output. Both proposed spline adaptive filter variants allow for a filtered output, which, for example, can cover a delayed update as it is caused by a pipelined hardware implementation. For the first time, internal clipping is avoided by means of an optimization constraint, that controls the norm of the linear filter weights. Additionally, an alternative limiter for the weight norm with lower complexity is proposed. The stochastic gradient descent update used for learning is improved by means of an appropriate novel step-size normalization. Optionally, the learning is augmented with the well-known transform-domain concept to improve the performance consistency of the algorithms.

III
Basics of Spline Interpolation

In this work, we introduce two adaptive algorithms for simultaneous digital cancellation of multiple even-order intermodulation distortion products, which both rely on spline interpolation to replicate the nonlinear function present in
y_{\text{BB}}^{\text{IMD}}[n]

IV

III-A

B-Splines

In many applications, like numerical simulations or computer graphics, it is desired to approximate general nonlinear functions by simpler expressions in order to enable efficient evaluation and easier analysis. Moreover, discrete data series frequently have to be interpolated to obtain intermediate values or to enable analytic manipulation. A straight-forward approximation method for both types of applications is the use of a single polynomial over the whole domain of the target function. While Weierstrass’ theorem states that this is generally possible to any desired accuracy, without further precautions oscillations occur (Runge’s phenomenon)

Since the

are non-zero only in the interval

\sum_{i=0}^{Q_{\text{sp}}-1}B_{Q_{\text{sp}},m-i}(x)=1,\qquad x_{m}\leq x<x_{m+1}.

(19)

In order to simplify the evaluation of a uniform spline function, we replace the global input

by the normalized input
{\nu\in[0,1)}
relative to the lower limit of the interval

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle=\nu\Delta x+\iota\Delta x+x_{0}

(20)

\displaystyle\nu

\displaystyle=\frac{x}{\Delta x}-\left\lfloor\frac{x}{\Delta x}\right\rfloor,\quad\iota=\left\lfloor\frac{x-x_{0}}{\Delta x}\right\rfloor.

(21)

This allows to rewrite

into a matrix-vector product, as exemplarily shown for

\displaystyle S(\nu,\iota)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{2}q_{\iota-i}\,B_{3,\iota-i}(\nu\Delta x+\iota\Delta x+x_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{q_{\iota-2}}{2\left(\Delta x\right)^{2}}\left(\Delta x\right)^{2}(1-\nu)^{2}

\displaystyle\phantom{{}={}}+\frac{q_{\iota-1}}{2\left(\Delta x\right)^{2}}\left(\Delta x\right)^{2}\left((\nu+1)(1-\nu)+(2-\nu)\nu\right)

\displaystyle\phantom{{}={}}+\frac{q_{\iota}}{2\left(\Delta x\right)^{2}}\left(\Delta x\right)^{2}\nu^{2}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\nu^{2}&\nu&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{2}&-1&\frac{1}{2}\\
-1&1&0\\
\frac{1}{2}&\frac{1}{2}&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}q_{\iota-2}\\
q_{\iota-1}\\
q_{\iota}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\bm{\upnu}}_{3}^{\mathsf{T}}\,\mathbf{B}_{3}^{\text{sp}}\,\mathbf{q}_{3,\iota}.

(22)

For arbitrary spline order, the input vector
\mathbf{\bm{\upnu}}_{Q_{\text{sp}}}
and the control point vector
\mathbf{q}_{Q_{\text{sp}},\iota}
are

\displaystyle\mathbf{\bm{\upnu}}_{Q_{\text{sp}}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\nu^{Q_{\text{sp}}-1},\nu^{Q_{\text{sp}}-2},\ldots,1\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}

(23)

\displaystyle\mathbf{q}_{Q_{\text{sp}},\iota}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}q_{\iota-Q_{\text{sp}}+1},q_{\iota-Q_{\text{sp}}+2},\ldots,q_{\iota}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}.

(24)

While the formulation (

\mathbf{B}_{Q_{\text{sp}}}^{\text{B}}

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{1}^{\text{B}}\coloneqq 1,

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{2}^{\text{B}}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}-1&1\\

(25)

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{3}^{\text{B}}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{2}&-1&\frac{1}{2}\\
-1&1&0\\
\frac{1}{2}&\frac{1}{2}&0\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{4}^{\text{B}}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}-\frac{1}{6}&\frac{1}{2}&-\frac{1}{2}&\frac{1}{6}\\
\frac{1}{2}&-1&\frac{1}{2}&0\\
-\frac{1}{2}&0&\frac{1}{2}&0\\
\frac{1}{6}&\frac{2}{3}&\frac{1}{6}&0\end{bmatrix}.

(26)

The normalization of the basis functions, shown in (

), is directly resembled in the matrices
\mathbf{B}_{Q_{\text{sp}}}
since we have

\mathbf{\bm{\upnu}}_{Q_{\text{sp}}}^{\mathsf{T}}\,\mathbf{B}_{Q_{\text{sp}}}^{\text{sp}}\,\mathbf{1}_{Q_{\text{sp}}\times 1}=\mathbf{\bm{\upnu}}_{Q_{\text{sp}}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{e}_{Q_{\text{sp}}}=1,

(27)

where
{\mathbf{q}_{Q_{\text{sp}},\iota}=\mathbf{1}_{Q_{\text{sp}}\times 1}}
ensures equal weighting of the base functions,
\mathbf{1}_{K\times 1}

III-B

Catmull-Rom Splines

As a trade-off between approximation accuracy and the risk of overfitting, cubic splines are preferred in many applications. Among all cubic splines, B-splines offer the highest degree of smoothness, but they do not exactly interpolate its control points. Conversely, this means that obtaining the control points of a well approximating spline curve based on a given set of function points necessitates to solve a system of equations. In adaptive learning, however, a simple way to initialize the control points to a given function is beneficial. Catmull-Rom (CR) splines feature the desired interpolation property at the cost of lower smoothness, which is only
C^{1}
compared to
C^{2}
for cubic B-splines
[
31
]
. The basis matrix for CR-splines is given by

\mathbf{B}^{\text{CR}}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}-\frac{1}{2}&\frac{3}{2}&-\frac{3}{2}&\frac{1}{2}\\
1&-\frac{5}{2}&2&-\frac{1}{2}\\
-\frac{1}{2}&0&\frac{1}{2}&0\\
0&1&0&0\end{bmatrix}.

(28)

Note that CR-splines are limited to order 4.

IV
Spline-Based Cancellation of Even-Order IMD Self-Interference

An adaptive algorithm used for simultaneous digital cancellation of multiple even-order intermodulation distortion products has to estimate the leakage path
\tilde{h}_{\text{BB}}^{\text{TxL}}[n]
and the coefficients of the receiver nonlinearity
\gamma_{k}
. We aim to solve this problem by means of a linear adaptive filter followed by a nonlinear adaptive function based on splines. Hence, in the following we present two novel complex Wiener spline adaptive filter algorithms, which are tailored to the intermodulation distortion effect. Besides the basic algorithm, we cover implementation-related aspects, such as pipelining, and use a constraint optimization technique to avoid internal clipping, an inherent issue of spline adaptive filters.

IV-A

Wiener-SAF with Complex Input, Internal Fixed Nonlinearity and Fixed Output Filter

IV-A
1
Basic Algorithm

Block diagram of adaptive nonlinear DSIM structure with real output and subsequent single-tap scaling for Q-path.

We propose a Wiener model as shown in the block diagram in Fig.

, which employs a fixed nonlinear function
{\zeta:\mathbb{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}}
that transforms the complex filter output
s[n]
into a real quantity

{\zeta(s)=|s|^{2}}

{\zeta(s)=|s|}
could be used, which features an advantageous amplitude distribution. The signal

is the input of the spline function, which is capable of approximating a wide range of nonlinearities. Obviously, this includes the intermodulation distortion products of the form
|.|^{2k}
and any linear combination of these terms. The latter case matches the interference

. In the following, we refer to the whole structure as complex-input Wiener SAF (CI-WSAF). Its output
{\hat{y}_{\text{saf}}[n]\in\mathbb{R}}
is given by

\displaystyle\hat{y}_{\text{saf}}[n]

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\left(\mathbf{\bm{\upnu}}_{Q_{\text{sp}}}[n]^{\mathsf{T}}\,\mathbf{B}_{Q_{\text{sp}}}^{\text{sp}}\,\mathbf{q}_{Q_{\text{sp}},\iota[n]}[n\!-\!1]\right)}_{\varphi[n]}{}*h_{\text{out}}[n]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{Q_{\text{out}}-1}\varphi[n\!-\!k]\,h_{\text{out}}[k]

(29)

using the definitions

\displaystyle r[n]

\displaystyle=\zeta(s[n]),\quad s[n]=\mathbf{w}[n\!-\!1]^{\mathsf{T}}\,\mathbf{x}[n]

(30)

\displaystyle\nu[n]

\displaystyle=\frac{r[n]}{\Delta r}-\left\lfloor\frac{r[n]}{\Delta r}\right\rfloor,\quad\iota[n]=\left\lfloor\frac{r[n]-r_{0}}{\Delta r}\right\rfloor.

(31)

\hat{y}_{\text{saf}}[n]
is an approximation of the (noisy) desired sequence
y[n]
, which is

in case of intermodulation distortion cancellation. The corresponding input

of the algorithm is the known transmit baseband stream
x_{\text{BB}}[n]
. The real impulse response

allows to apply a known filter to the output of the spline function. Depending on the setup, this filter could be a channel-select filter or a pure delay. The latter is important to model pipelining stages in the output computation, which help to achieve the desired operating frequency. The length of the adaptive filter
\mathbf{w}[n]
is
Q_{\text{lin}}
, the length of the output filter

is
Q_{\text{out}}
and the spline order is
Q_{\text{sp}}
. We use uniform knots with a spacing of
\Delta r
and a lower limit of
r_{0}
. When calculating
\iota[n]
, it is important to realize that its value must be in the range
[Q_{\text{sp}}-1,N_{\text{sp}}-1]
, where

is the number of control points. For any values outside of this range, the correct spline output cannot be calculated due to an index underrun or overflow in
\mathbf{q}_{Q_{\text{sp}},\iota}
. The vectors
\mathbf{\bm{\upnu}}_{Q_{\text{sp}}}[n]
,
\mathbf{q}_{Q_{\text{sp}},\iota[m]}[n]
,
\mathbf{w}[n]
and
\mathbf{x}[n]
are given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{\bm{\upnu}}_{Q_{\text{sp}}}[n]

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\left(\nu[n]\right)^{Q_{\text{sp}}-1},\left(\nu[n]\right)^{Q_{\text{sp}}-2},\ldots,1\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}

(32)

\displaystyle\mathbf{q}_{Q_{\text{sp}},\iota[m]}[n]

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}q_{\iota[m]-Q_{\text{sp}}+1}[n],q_{\iota[m]-Q_{\text{sp}}+2}[n],\ldots,q_{\iota[m]}[n]\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}

(33)

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}[n]

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}w_{0}[n],w_{1}[n],\ldots,w_{Q_{\text{lin}}-1}[n]\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}

(34)

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}[n]

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}x[n],x[n\!-\!1],\ldots,x[n\!-\!Q_{\text{lin}}\!+\!1]\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}.

(35)

Note the difference in the time indices between the segment index
\iota[m]
and the control points
q_{\iota}[n]
. The

and thus also

are real-valued. For the following derivations, we also define the vector
{\mathbf{q}[n]=[q_{0}[n],q_{1}[n],\ldots,q_{N_{\text{sp}}-1}[n]]^{\mathsf{T}}}
comprising all control points, and use the indexing scheme
{q_{\iota}[n]=[\mathbf{q}[n]]_{\iota}}
.

We propose to estimate the parameters
\mathbf{w}
and
\mathbf{q}
by means of a stochastic gradient descent method. The corresponding cost function is the instantaneous squared estimation error, augmented by an additional penalty using the
p
-norm of the weights:

\displaystyle J[n]

\displaystyle=\left|e[n]\right|^{2}+\epsilon\,\underbrace{\left(\left\lVert\mathbf{w}[n\!-\!1]\right\rVert_{p}^{p}-\rho_{w}\right)^{2}}_{c_{\mathbf{w}}[n]}

(36)

\displaystyle e[n]

\displaystyle=y[n]-\hat{y}_{\text{saf}}[n].

(37)

\rho_{w}
is the target value for
\left\lVert\mathbf{w}[n]\right\rVert_{p}^{p}
and
\epsilon
is a weighting factor. The norm constrained stochastic gradient descent concept is known from sparse estimation, where, for instance, an approximation of the
\ell_{0}
norm is employed
[
32
]
. In the spline adaptive filter application, the goal is to avoid any gain ambiguity between the weights and the control points on the one hand and to ensure a limited number range at the input of the spline function on the other hand. This avoids saturation of the signal
r[n]
, which could lead to instability of the algorithm. From finite impulse response filter implementations in fixed-point arithmetic it is known that the
\ell_{1}
norm of the weights poses a conservative bound on the output range for a given input number format
[
33
]
. A computationally less expensive approximation would be the
\ell_{2}
norm, where it holds that
{\left\lVert\mathbf{w}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\left\lVert\mathbf{w}\right\rVert_{1}}
. Thus, the
\ell_{2}
norm is less restrictive and does not guarantee that overflows are eliminated completely. In order to minimize the cost function, we require the gradients of
J[n]
with respect to the parameters.

Since the differentiation is not directly possible due to the time-delays caused by the convolution with
h_{\text{out}}[n]
, we introduce approximations, where the targeted parameter is assumed to be time-independent. Consequently, the approximated gradient with respect to
\mathbf{q}
is

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{\mathbf{q}}[n]^{\mathsf{T}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial J[n]}{\partial e[n]}\frac{\partial e[n]|_{\mathbf{q}[.]=\mathbf{q}}}{\partial\mathbf{q}}

\displaystyle=2\,e[n]\,\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{e,\mathbf{q}}[n]^{\mathsf{T}}.

(38)

Throughout this work, any derivative of the form
\partial f(\mathbf{x})/\partial\mathbf{x}
shall be a row vector when
\mathbf{x}
is a column vector
[
34
]
. This allows for a straight-forward application of the chain rule. When evaluating the partial derivative
\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{e,\mathbf{q}}[n]
we yield

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{e,\mathbf{q}}[n]

\displaystyle=-\sum_{k=0}^{Q_{\text{out}}-1}h_{\text{out}}[k]\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0}_{(\iota[n-k]-Q_{\text{sp}}+1)\times 1}\\
(\mathbf{B}_{Q_{\text{sp}}}^{\text{sp}})^{\mathsf{T}}\,\mathbf{\bm{\upnu}}_{Q_{\text{sp}}}[n\!-\!k]\\
\mathbf{0}_{(N_{\text{sp}}-\iota[n-k]-1)\times 1}\end{bmatrix},

(39)

where the all-zero vectors
\mathbf{0}_{K\times 1}
are used to appropriately place the derivative of the spline output depending on the interval index at the time step
{n\!-\!k}
. Since the evaluation of (
39

Q_{\text{out}}
, a possible approximation is to neglect small

or to replace the filter with its gain and group delay. The approximated gradient with respect to
\mathbf{w}
is obtained in a similar manner

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{\mathbf{w}}[n]^{\mathsf{T}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial J[n]}{\partial e[n]}\frac{\partial e[n]|_{\mathbf{w}[.]=\mathbf{w}}}{\partial\mathbf{w}^{*}}+\frac{\partial J[n]}{\partial c_{\mathbf{w}}[n]}\frac{c_{\mathbf{w}}[n]}{\partial\mathbf{w}[n\!-\!1]^{*}}

\displaystyle=2\,e[n]\,\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{e,\mathbf{w}}[n]^{\mathsf{T}}+\epsilon\,\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{c,\mathbf{w}}[n]^{\mathsf{T}}

(40)

using the
\mathbb{CR}
(or Wirtinger) calculus
[
35
]
, which conveniently guarantees the correct direction of the gradient for both, the real and the imaginary part of
\mathbf{w}
. The substitution of
\mathbf{w}[.]
shall affect only the spline input
\nu[.]
, but not the spline segment indices
\iota[.]
. We assume that
\zeta(s,s^{*})
and

fulfill Brandwood’s analyticity condition
[
36
]
, thereby simplifying the
\mathbb{CR}
derivatives. The term
\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{e,\mathbf{w}}[n]
evaluates to

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{e,\mathbf{w}}[n]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{Q_{\text{out}}-1}\frac{\partial e[n]}{\partial\mathbf{\bm{\upnu}}_{Q_{\text{sp}}}[n\!-\!k]}\frac{\partial\mathbf{\bm{\upnu}}_{Q_{\text{sp}}}[n\!-\!k]}{\partial\nu[n\!-\!k]}

\displaystyle\phantom{{}={}}\hskip 40.00006pt\frac{\partial\nu[n\!-\!k]}{\partial r[n\!-\!k]}\frac{\partial r[n\!-\!k]}{\partial s[n\!-\!k]^{*}}\frac{\partial s[n\!-\!k]^{*}|_{\mathbf{w}[.]=\mathbf{w}}}{\partial\mathbf{w}^{\mathsf{H}}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\Delta r}\sum_{k=0}^{Q_{\text{out}}-1}h_{\text{out}}[k]\,\mathbf{\bm{\upnu}}_{Q_{\text{sp}}}^{\prime}[n\!-\!k]^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{B}_{Q_{\text{sp}}}^{\text{sp}}\mathbf{q}_{Q_{\text{sp}},\iota[n-k]}[n\!-\!1\!-\!k]

\displaystyle\phantom{{}={}}\hskip 55.00008pt\zeta^{\prime}(s[n\!-\!k])^{*}\,\mathbf{x}[n\!-\!k]^{*}

(41)

with
{\zeta^{\prime}(s)=\partial\zeta(s)/\partial s}
and

\displaystyle\mathbf{\bm{\upnu}}_{Q_{\text{sp}}}^{\prime}[n]=

\displaystyle\hskip 25.00003pt\begin{bmatrix}\left(Q_{\text{sp}}\!-\!1\right)(\nu[n])^{Q_{\text{sp}}-2},\left(Q_{\text{sp}}\!-\!2\right)(\nu[n])^{Q_{\text{sp}}-3},\ldots,0\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}.

(42)

When using
{\zeta(s)=|s|^{2}}
, the

{\zeta^{\prime}(s)=s^{*}}
. Within one spline segment, the index

\mathbf{w}
due to the floor function. Again, small values of

could be neglected to reduce the complexity of evaluating
\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{e,\mathbf{w}}[n]

\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{c,\mathbf{w}}[n]
depends on the chosen norm

\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{c,\mathbf{w}}[n]=\begin{cases}\left(\left\lVert\mathbf{w}[n\!-\!1]\right\rVert_{1}-\rho_{w}\right)&p=1\\
\hskip 50.00008pt\left[\frac{w_{k}[n\!-\!1]}{\left|w_{k}[n\!-\!1]\right|}\right]_{k\,=\,0,\ldots,Q_{\text{lin}}-1}&\\
2\left(\left\lVert\mathbf{w}[n\!-\!1]\right\rVert_{2}^{2}-\rho_{w}\right)\mathbf{w}[n\!-\!1]&p=2\end{cases}

(43)

with
{w_{k}=[\mathbf{w}]_{k}}

{w_{k}/\left|w_{k}\right|=e^{j\arg(w_{k})}}

\displaystyle\mathbf{q}[n]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{q}[n\!-\!1]-2\,\tau\,\mu[n]\,e[n]\,\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{e,\mathbf{q}}[n]

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}[n]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{w}[n\!-\!1]-\mu[n]\left(2\,e[n]\,\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{e,\mathbf{w}}[n]+\epsilon\,\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{c,\mathbf{w}}[n]\right)

The standard stochastic gradient descent approach is difficult to tune, since the effective learning rate depends on the dynamics of the involved signals. Thus, commonly a normalized variant is used. One method to derive the normalization is to consider a partial Taylor series expansion of the error signal
[
37

20
]

\displaystyle e[

\displaystyle n\!+\!1]

to

\displaystyle\left|e[n]\right|

\displaystyle\geq\left|e[n]\right|\left|1-2\,\tau\,\mu[n]\left\lVert\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{e,\mathbf{q}}[n]\right\rVert_{2}^{2}-4\,\mu[n]\left\lVert\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{e,\mathbf{w}}[n]\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right|

Complex Output

In the intermodulation distortion problem, a complex-valued interference signal

has to be replicated, but the CI-WSAF only provides a real-valued output. When we assume nonlinearity coefficients of the form
{\gamma_{k}=\gamma_{k,\text{I}}\,(1+j\delta_{\text{Q}})}

{\gamma_{k,\text{I}},\delta_{\text{Q}}\in\mathbb{R}}
, then the imaginary part of

is just a scaled copy of the real part. The coupling
\delta_{\text{Q}}
might be estimated by using a single-tap normalized least mean squares (N-LMS)
or a weighted least squares algorithm, following a cascaded scheme as illustrated in Fig.

\hat{y}_{\text{BB}}^{\text{IMD}}[n]=\hat{y}_{\text{saf}}[n]+jw_{\text{cpl}}[n]\,\hat{y}_{\text{saf}}[n]

with the weight calculations

(51)

for the 1-tap normalized LMS using
{e_{\text{Q}}[n]=y_{\text{Q}}[n]-w_{\text{cpl}}[n]\,\hat{y}_{\text{saf}}[n]}
or

\displaystyle w_{\text{cpl}}[n]

\displaystyle=\frac{r_{y\hat{y}}[n]}{r_{\hat{y}\hat{y}}[n]}

(52)

\displaystyle r_{y\hat{y}}[n]

\displaystyle=r_{y\hat{y}}[n\!-\!1]+\lambda_{\text{cpl}}\,y_{\text{Q}}[n]\,\hat{y}_{\text{saf}}[n],

\displaystyle r_{y\hat{y}}[-1]=0

(53)

\displaystyle r_{\hat{y}\hat{y}}[n]

\displaystyle r_{\hat{y}\hat{y}}[-1]=0

(54)

Transform-Domain Concept

Furthermore, it is well-known that stochastic gradient descent algorithms suffer from slow convergence in case of correlated input signals
[
38
]
. This property is particularly critical if the reference signal is a Long-Term Evolution/New Radio sequence with narrow allocation, a common case for uplink. In
[
39
]
, it has been shown that the transform-domain concept provides an appropriate mitigation if a precomputed power normalization is available. For the CI-WSAF, this approach is applied by replacing the input vector
\mathbf{x}[n]
with the transformed vector
\mathbf{v}[n]
:

\mathbf{v}[n]=\mathbf{P}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{Q_{\text{lin}}}\mathbf{x}[n],\quad[\mathbf{P}]_{k,l}=\delta_{k,l}\left[\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{Q_{\text{lin}}}\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{Q_{\text{lin}}}^{\mathsf{T}}\right]_{k,l}.

(55)

{\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{Q_{\text{lin}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{Q_{\text{lin}}\times Q_{\text{lin}}}}
is the discrete cosine transform matrix and
\delta_{k,l}
is the Kronecker delta.
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}
is the auto-covariance matrix of the input vector
\mathbf{x}[n]
, which is used to compute the power normalization
\mathbf{P}^{-\frac{1}{2}}
. When calculating
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}
, it is important to take any resampling of
x[n]
into account. Besides operating on a different input, the CI-WSAF algorithm in the transform-domain variant stays unaltered.

5
Weight Norm Limiting

Last, in the iterative optimization of
\mathbf{w}[n]
, the norm correction
{\epsilon\,\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{c,\mathbf{w}}[n]}
has to be applied in every iteration. In order to relax the requirements on a hardware implementation, we propose an alternative heuristic rescaling of the weights

\mathbf{w}_{\text{lim}}[n]=\begin{cases}\mathbf{w}[n]&\left\lVert\mathbf{w}[n\!-\!1]\right\rVert_{p}^{p}<\rho_{w}\\
\frac{\mathbf{w}[n]}{2}&\text{else}\end{cases},

(56)

where the decision can be computed in parallel to the update step and the potential rescaling is a simple shift operation. Obviously, this scaling has to be compensated by the spline control points
\mathbf{q}
in order to maintain the output signal level. According to simulations, the iterative adaptation of
\mathbf{q}
is capable of this task if the rescaling is applied seldomly (cf. Fig.
7
).

IV-B

Extension to Complex Control Points

Fig. 3:
Block diagram of adaptive nonlinear DSIM structure with complex output.

The CI-WSAF presented above operates on complex input data and uses complex filter weights, but in its basic form has a real-valued output. Still, a complex interference can be canceled by means of an additional single-tap adaptive filter, under the assumption that the interference in the Q-path is a scaled version of the I-path. When considering the intermodulation distortion baseband model (
10
), this constraint is not fulfilled in general. Therefore, we extend the algorithm by including separate spline functions for the I- and the Q-path.

IV-B
1
Basic Algorithm

The structure of this algorithm is illustrated in Fig.
3
. The output of the so-called complex-input-output Wiener SAF (CIO-WSAF) is given by

\displaystyle\hat{y}_{\text{saf}}[n]

\displaystyle=\Big(\mathbf{\bm{\upnu}}_{Q_{\text{sp}}}[n]^{\mathsf{T}}\,\mathbf{B}_{Q_{\text{sp}}}^{\text{sp}}

\displaystyle\hskip 25.00003pt\left(\mathbf{q}_{\text{I},Q_{\text{sp}},\iota[n]}[n\!-\!1]+j\mathbf{q}_{\text{Q},Q_{\text{sp}},\iota[n]}[n\!-\!1]\right)\Big)*h_{\text{out}}[n]

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\left(\mathbf{\bm{\upnu}}_{Q_{\text{sp}}}[n]^{\mathsf{T}}\,\mathbf{B}_{Q_{\text{sp}}}^{\text{sp}}\,\mathbf{q}_{Q_{\text{sp}},\iota[n]}[n\!-\!1]\right)}_{\varphi[n]}{}*h_{\text{out}}[n],

(57)

where
{\mathbf{q}[n]\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{\text{sp}}}}
and
{\hat{y}_{\text{saf}}[n]\in\mathbb{C}}
. Despite this change, all signal definitions and the stochastic gradient descent cost function, (
30
)–(
37
), remain valid. In the intermodulation distortion problem, the output of the CIO-WSAF can be directly used for interference cancellation, i. e.
{\hat{y}_{\text{BB}}^{\text{IMD}}[n]=\hat{y}_{\text{saf}}[n]}
. The approximate gradient of the cost function with respect to the control points now also requires the Wirtinger calculus. Applying the corresponding chain rule leads to:

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{\mathbf{q}}[n]

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial J[n]}{\partial e[n]}\frac{\partial e[n]|_{\mathbf{q}[.]=\mathbf{q}}}{\partial\mathbf{q}^{\mathsf{H}}}+\frac{\partial J[n]}{\partial e[n]^{*}}\frac{\partial e[n]^{*}|_{\mathbf{q}^{*}[.]=\mathbf{q}^{*}}}{\partial\mathbf{q}^{\mathsf{H}}}

\displaystyle=-e[n]\sum_{k=0}^{Q_{\text{out}}-1}h_{\text{out}}[k]\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0}_{(\iota[n-k]-Q_{\text{sp}}+1)\times 1}\\
(\mathbf{B}_{Q_{\text{sp}}}^{\text{sp}})^{\mathsf{T}}\,\mathbf{\bm{\upnu}}_{Q_{\text{sp}}}[n\!-\!k]\\
\mathbf{0}_{(N_{\text{sp}}-\iota[n-k]-1)\times 1}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=e[n]\,\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{e,\mathbf{q}}[n].

(58)

Due to the real spline input, this result can be interpreted as an independent optimization of the nonlinearities in the I- and the Q-path. The same structure of the chain rule also occurs in case of the approximate gradient with respect to the filter weights:

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{\mathbf{w}}[n]^{\mathsf{T}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial J[n]}{\partial e[n]}\frac{\partial e[n]|_{\mathbf{w}[.]=\mathbf{w}}}{\partial\mathbf{w}^{*}}+\frac{\partial J[n]}{\partial e[n]^{*}}\frac{\partial e[n]^{*}|_{\mathbf{w}[.]=\mathbf{w}}}{\partial\mathbf{w}^{*}}

\displaystyle\phantom{{}={}}+\frac{\partial J[n]}{\partial c_{\mathbf{w}}[n]}\frac{c_{\mathbf{w}}[n]}{\partial\mathbf{w}[n\!-\!1]^{*}}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{J,e,\mathbf{w}}[n]^{\mathsf{T}}+\epsilon\,\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{c,\mathbf{w}}[n]^{\mathsf{T}}.

(59)

The notation
\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{J,e,\mathbf{w}}[n]
indicates that no separation between the error
e[n]
and the partial derivative of
e[n]
is possible anymore, which is a major difference to the real case. The gradient
\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{J,e,\mathbf{w}}[n]
is given by

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{J,e,\mathbf{w}}[n]

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial J[n]}{\partial e[n]}\sum_{k=0}^{Q_{\text{out}}-1}\frac{\partial e[n]}{\partial\mathbf{\bm{\upnu}}_{Q_{\text{sp}}}[n\!-\!k]}\frac{\partial\mathbf{\bm{\upnu}}_{Q_{\text{sp}}}[n\!-\!k]}{\partial\nu[n\!-\!k]}

\displaystyle\hskip 70.0001pt\frac{\partial\nu[n\!-\!k]}{\partial r[n\!-\!k]}\frac{\partial r[n\!-\!k]}{\partial s[n\!-\!k]^{*}}\frac{\partial s[n\!-\!k]^{*}|_{\mathbf{w}[.]=\mathbf{w}}}{\partial\mathbf{w}^{\mathsf{H}}}

\displaystyle\phantom{{}={}}+\frac{\partial J[n]}{\partial e[n]^{*}}\sum_{k=0}^{Q_{\text{out}}-1}\frac{\partial e[n]^{*}}{\partial\mathbf{\bm{\upnu}}_{Q_{\text{sp}}}[n\!-\!k]}\frac{\partial\mathbf{\bm{\upnu}}_{Q_{\text{sp}}}[n\!-\!k]}{\partial\nu[n\!-\!k]}

\displaystyle\hskip 85.00012pt\frac{\partial\nu[n\!-\!k]}{\partial r[n\!-\!k]}\frac{\partial r[n\!-\!k]}{\partial s[n\!-\!k]^{*}}\frac{\partial s[n\!-\!k]^{*}|_{\mathbf{w}[.]=\mathbf{w}}}{\partial\mathbf{w}^{\mathsf{H}}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{\Delta r}\sum_{k=0}^{Q_{\text{out}}-1}h_{\text{out}}[k]\,\mathbf{\bm{\upnu}}_{Q_{\text{sp}}}^{\prime}[n\!-\!k]^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{B}_{Q_{\text{sp}}}^{\text{sp}}

\displaystyle\hskip 33.00008pt\Re\!\left\{e[n]^{*}\,\mathbf{q}_{Q_{\text{sp}},\iota[n-k]}[n\!-\!1\!-\!k]\right\}\,\zeta^{\prime}(s[n\!-\!k])^{*}\,\mathbf{x}[n\!-\!k]^{*}.

(60)

This form is obtained by factoring out all common terms and using the identity
{a+a^{*}=2\,\Re\{a\}}
. The gradient of the norm constraint (
43
), as well as the limiter (
56
), remain unaffected by the complex control points and can be directly reused. Combining all results, the update equations of the CIO-WSAF are

\displaystyle\mathbf{q}[n]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{q}[n\!-\!1]-\tau\,\mu[n]\,e[n]\,\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{e,\mathbf{q}}[n]

(61)

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}[n]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{w}[n\!-\!1]-\mu[n]\left(\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{J,e,\mathbf{w}}[n]+\epsilon\,\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{c,\mathbf{w}}[n]\right).

(62)

Step-Size Normalization

Similar to the real case, the adaptation performance of the algorithm is improved by calculating a partial linear Taylor approximation of the error signal and choosing the step-size such that the error decreases with each iteration. In order to simplify the corresponding inequality for
\mu[n]
, we replace the convolution with
h_{\text{out}}[n]
by the group delay
k_{\text{g}}
and the passband gain
h_{\text{g}}
of the output filter.

\displaystyle e[n\!+\!1]

\displaystyle\approx e[n]+\frac{\partial e[n]|_{\mathbf{q}[.]=\mathbf{q}}}{\partial\mathbf{q}}\Delta\mathbf{q}[n]

\displaystyle\phantom{{}\approx{}}+\frac{\partial e[n]|_{\mathbf{w}[.]=\mathbf{w};\,h_{\text{g}}}}{\partial\mathbf{w}}\Delta\mathbf{w}[n]+\frac{\partial e[n]|_{\mathbf{w}^{*}[.]=\mathbf{w}^{*};\,h_{\text{g}}}}{\partial\mathbf{w}^{*}}\Delta\mathbf{w}[n]^{*}

\displaystyle\approx e[n]-\tau\,\mu[n]\,e[n]\left\lVert\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{e,\mathbf{q}}[n]\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\phantom{{}\approx{}}-\mu[n]\,\frac{4}{(\Delta r)^{2}}\,h_{\text{g}}^{2}\,\mathbf{\bm{\upnu}}_{Q_{\text{sp}}}^{\prime}[n\!-\!k_{\text{g}}]^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{B}_{Q_{\text{sp}}}^{\text{sp}}\mathbf{q}_{Q_{\text{sp}},\iota[n-k_{\text{g}}]}[n\!-\!1\!-\!k_{\text{g}}]

\displaystyle\phantom{{}\approx{}-{}}\mathbf{\bm{\upnu}}_{Q_{\text{sp}}}^{\prime}[n\!-\!k_{\text{g}}]^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{B}_{Q_{\text{sp}}}^{\text{sp}}\Re\!\left\{e[n]^{*}\,\mathbf{q}_{Q_{\text{sp}},\iota[n-k_{\text{g}}]}[n\!-\!1\!-\!k_{\text{g}}]\right\}

\displaystyle\phantom{{}\approx{}-{}}\left|\zeta^{\prime}(s[n\!-\!k_{\text{g}}])\right|^{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}[n\!-\!k_{\text{g}}]\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

(63)

In the classical derivation of the step-size bound, it is assumed that
{\left|e[n\!+\!1]\right|\leq\left|e[n]\right|}
. Since no closed-form solution of this inequality for
\mu[n]
independent of
e[n]
is possible, we instead place constraints in the real and imaginary part of
e[n]
separately:

\displaystyle\left|e_{\text{I}}[n]\right|

\displaystyle\geq\left|e_{\text{I}}[n\!+\!1]\right|

\displaystyle\geq\bigg|e_{\text{I}}[n]-\tau\,\mu[n]\,e_{\text{I}}[n]\left\lVert\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{e,\mathbf{q}}[n]\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\phantom{{}\geq{}}-\mu[n]\,e_{\text{I}}[n]\,\frac{4}{(\Delta r)^{2}}\,h_{\text{g}}^{2}\,\left|\zeta^{\prime}(s[n\!-\!k_{\text{g}}])\right|^{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}[n\!-\!k_{\text{g}}]\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\phantom{{}\geq{}-{}}\left(\mathbf{\bm{\upnu}}_{Q_{\text{sp}}}^{\prime}[n\!-\!k_{\text{g}}]^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{B}_{Q_{\text{sp}}}^{\text{sp}}\mathbf{q}_{\text{I},Q_{\text{sp}},\iota[n-k_{\text{g}}]}[n\!-\!1\!-\!k_{\text{g}}]\right)^{2}\bigg|

\displaystyle\geq\left|e_{\text{I}}[n]\right|\left|1-\mu[n]\,b_{\mu,\text{I}}[n]\right|

(64)

\displaystyle\left|e_{\text{Q}}[n]\right|

\displaystyle\geq\left|e_{\text{Q}}[n\!+\!1]\right|

\displaystyle\geq\bigg|e_{\text{Q}}[n]-\tau\,\mu[n]\,e_{\text{Q}}[n]\left\lVert\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{e,\mathbf{q}}[n]\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\phantom{{}\geq{}}-\mu[n]\,e_{\text{Q}}[n]\,\frac{4}{(\Delta r)^{2}}\,h_{\text{g}}^{2}\,\left|\zeta^{\prime}(s[n\!-\!k_{\text{g}}])\right|^{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}[n\!-\!k_{\text{g}}]\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\phantom{{}\geq{}-{}}\left(\mathbf{\bm{\upnu}}_{Q_{\text{sp}}}^{\prime}[n\!-\!k_{\text{g}}]^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{B}_{Q_{\text{sp}}}^{\text{sp}}\mathbf{q}_{\text{Q},Q_{\text{sp}},\iota[n-k_{\text{g}}]}[n\!-\!1\!-\!k_{\text{g}}]\right)^{2}\bigg|

\displaystyle\geq\left|e_{\text{Q}}[n]\right|\left|1-\mu[n]\,b_{\mu,\text{Q}}[n]\right|.

(65)

Here, we used the definitions

\displaystyle b_{\mu,\text{I}}[n]

\displaystyle\hskip 5.0pt=\tau\left\lVert\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{e,\mathbf{q}}[n]\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\frac{4}{(\Delta r)^{2}}\,h_{\text{g}}^{2}\,\left|\zeta^{\prime}(s[n\!-\!k_{\text{g}}])\right|^{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}[n\!-\!k_{\text{g}}]\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 30.00005pt\left(\mathbf{\bm{\upnu}}_{Q_{\text{sp}}}^{\prime}[n\!-\!k_{\text{g}}]^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{B}_{Q_{\text{sp}}}^{\text{sp}}\mathbf{q}_{\text{I},Q_{\text{sp}},\iota[n-k_{\text{g}}]}[n\!-\!1\!-\!k_{\text{g}}]\right)^{2}

(66)

\displaystyle b_{\mu,\text{Q}}[n]

\displaystyle\hskip 5.0pt=\tau\left\lVert\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{e,\mathbf{q}}[n]\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\frac{4}{(\Delta r)^{2}}\,h_{\text{g}}^{2}\,\left|\zeta^{\prime}(s[n\!-\!k_{\text{g}}])\right|^{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}[n\!-\!k_{\text{g}}]\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 30.00005pt\left(\mathbf{\bm{\upnu}}_{Q_{\text{sp}}}^{\prime}[n\!-\!k_{\text{g}}]^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{B}_{Q_{\text{sp}}}^{\text{sp}}\mathbf{q}_{\text{Q},Q_{\text{sp}},\iota[n-k_{\text{g}}]}[n\!-\!1\!-\!k_{\text{g}}]\right)^{2}.

(67)

In the inequality for
e_{\text{I}}[n]
, we neglect
e_{\text{Q}}[n]
and vice versa. The solutions of both inequalities are of the form
{0\leq\mu[n]\leq 2/{b_{\mu,\text{I/Q}}}[n]}
. Since we did not include any coupling between
e_{\text{I}}[n]
and
e_{\text{Q}}[n]
, the final upper bound for
\mu[n]
is chosen conservatively as
{\mu_{\text{max}}[n]=2/(b_{\mu,\text{I}}[n]+b_{\mu,\text{Q}}[n]+\xi)}
. This value is guaranteed to be smaller than or equal to
2/{b_{\mu,\text{I/Q}}[n]}
since the
b_{\mu,\text{I/Q}}[n]
are non-negative. Following this approach, we obtain the final step-size normalization by choosing

\displaystyle\mu[n]

\displaystyle=\mu\,\mu_{\text{max}}[n]=\mu\,\frac{2}{b_{\mu,\text{I}}[n]+b_{\mu,\text{Q}}[n]+\xi}

\displaystyle=\mu\,\bigg(2\frac{h_{\text{g}}^{2}}{(\Delta r)^{2}}\left|\zeta^{\prime}(s[n\!-\!k_{\text{g}}])\right|^{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}[n\!-\!k_{\text{g}}]\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\phantom{{}=\mu\,\bigg(}\left|\mathbf{\bm{\upnu}}_{Q_{\text{sp}}}^{\prime}[n\!-\!k_{\text{g}}]^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{B}_{Q_{\text{sp}}}^{\text{sp}}\mathbf{q}_{Q_{\text{sp}},\iota[n-k_{\text{g}}]}[n\!-\!1\!-\!k_{\text{g}}]\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\phantom{{}=\mu\,\bigg(}+\tau\left\lVert\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{e,\mathbf{q}}[n]\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\xi\bigg)^{-1}

(68)

with the constant step-size
{0\leq\mu\leq 1}
. In order to improve the adaptation rate and the performance consistency of the CIO-WSAF, the transform-domain concept (
55

[

\mathcal{D}
of relevant input vectors, combined with a set of complex weights. In stationary scenarios, the dictionary size

V
Performance Evaluation

We conclude our investigations on spline-based digital self-interference mitigation algorithms with performance simulations on a realistic intermodulation distortion interference scenario. Besides the steady-state cancellation with and without output delays, we also show the effect of the norm constraint on the filter weights.

V-A

Setup

Fig. 4:
Measurement setup for characterization of duplexer and Rx nonlinearity. Unused ports are terminated.

The interference model used in the following is based on measurements on an integrated complementary metal–oxide–semiconductor transceiver using the setup outlined in Fig.
4
. The transmit signal is generated using a vector signal generator and optionally amplified by a discrete power amplifier. A discrete duplexing filter provides the Tx-Rx isolation in the analog front-end. The signal after the duplexer is captured by a vector signal analyzer in order to obtain the actual input signal of the receive chain. Simultaneously, the signal is passed to an external low-noise amplifier with a gain of
15\text{\,}\mathrm{dB}
. Unlike the internal low-noise amplifier of the subsequent radio frequency transceiver, the external low-noise amplifier is assumed to be sufficiently linear to not contribute to the intermodulation distortion. On the one hand, this setup enables a characterization of multiple duplexers for different Long-Term Evolution bands. On the other hand, it allows to extract the intermodulation distortion components from the receive baseband sequence. The intermodulation distortion interference power was measured for various leakage power levels
P(y_{\text{BB}}^{\text{TxL}})
at the chip input, leading to the nonlinearity coefficients
\gamma_{k}
as used in (
12
).

(a)

(b)

(c)

Input-referred coefficients of intermodulation products in radio frequency receiver: (a) IMD2, (b) IMD4 and (c) IMD6.

Fig.
5
depicts the real and imaginary parts of the input-referred coefficients
{\check{\gamma}_{k}=\gamma_{k}/A_{\text{lin}}}
, which differ considerably. The values are not constant, since for low input power levels, the intermodulation distortion products are below the noise floor, thereby increasing the error of the fit. Additionally, for high input powers, compression effects occur that are not covered by the intermodulation distortion modeling provided in Section
II
.

In addition to the receiver nonlinearity, we take the transmit distortions caused by the power amplifier into account. This is achieved by replacing the ideal transmit radio frequency model (
1
) by

x_{\text{RF}}(t)=\Re\left\{g_{\text{BB}}^{\text{PA}}(x_{\text{BB}}(t))\,e^{j2\pi f_{\text{Tx}}t}\right\}

(69)

with the equivalent baseband transfer characteristic
g_{\text{BB}}^{\text{PA}}(.)
of the power amplifier. With this model, we assume that the power of any harmonic emissions caused by the power amplifier is much lower than the in-band signal power. For
g_{\text{BB}}^{\text{PA}}(.)
, we choose the well-known Rapp model, which is a memoryless behavioral model for solid-state amplifiers
[
44
,
45
]

VI
Conclusion

We presented two novel adaptive learning schemes based on spline interpolation that allow for low-complex digital cancellation of transceiver self-interference caused by higher-order intermodulation distortion. Based on a comprehensive modeling of the receiver nonlinearities, we derived the baseband interference model, allowing to extract a Wiener structure suitable for online estimation. We proposed several extensions to the spline-based algorithm and precisely assessed the computational complexity of all modules. The interference cancellation performance was evaluated in practically relevant intermodulation distortion scenarios, which were based on measured receiver parameters. Compared to a general nonlinear estimation approach, our algorithms showed similar accuracy at a fraction of the computational costs.
