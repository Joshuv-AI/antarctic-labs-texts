Thermodynamically consistent modelling of viscoelastic solids under finite strain
Mario Kunzemann, Leonhard K. Doppelbauer, Rene Preuer, Astrid Pechstein

Abstract
The present article is concerned with modelling the viscoelastic behavior of Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) in large-strain regime. Starting from the basic principles of thermodynamics, an incremental variational formulation is derived. Within this model, the free energy density and dissipation function determine elastic and viscous properties of the solid. The main contribution of this paper is the estimation of the parameters in the proposed phenomenological model from measurements conducted on PDMS samples. This non-linear material model simplifies to a Prony-series representation in frequency domain in case of small deformations. The coefficients of this Prony-series are detected from dynamical temperature mechanical analysis measurements. Time-temperature superposition allows to combine measurements at different temperatures, such that a sufficiently large frequency range is available for subsequent fitting of Prony-parameters. A set of material parameters is thus provided. The incremental variational formulation directly lends itself to finite element discretization, where an efficient and stable choice of elements is proposed for radially symmetric problems. This formulation allows to verify the proposed model against experimental data gained in ball-drop experiments.

Introduction

Silicone-based polymers, like Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS), are known for their high flexibility and excellent damping properties. Thermal and chemical stability, corrosion resistance and optical transparency add to the features of these materials.
Not least their easy accessiblity at low cost and simple generation of samples allow for numerous applications in science and engineering.
We cite the recent review papers by
[
1
]
and
[
30
]
, both of which provide a great overview over the properties and applications of PDMS.

Typical applications of PDMS include, for example, microfluidics, where channels are required for filtering, separating and mixing fluids (cf.
[
3
]
), the insulation of electronic components, or coating of sensitive materials to protect them from external effects (cf.
[
10
]
). In these applications, the damping nature of PDMS is of high advantage. An increase of the damping behavior could be realized through micro-structure design of sponges in
[
31
]
. Treatment of surface layers was used to generate custom wrinkling patterns in specimen in
[
15
]
.

The reliable realization of many of these applications requires accurate and efficient simulation techniques to predict the behavior of the system under consideration. Due to the high flexibility of PDMS, such a model must be fit to represent large deformations, and the damping properties have to be characterized properly.

Although frequently used in biomechanical or medical applications, the mechanical characterization of PDMS is not yet sufficiently accurate. Concerning the hyperelastic behavior,
[
26
]
proposed a simple shear strain test to obtain material parameters for Mooney-Rivlin type materials. They proposed to use a variant to the classical Mooney-Rivlin material law to better fit their measurements. Later,
[
5
]
fitted different hyperelastic material laws, including Mooney-Rivlin, Ogden and Yeoh models. The influence of surface patterns on the elastic response was analyzed by
[
18
]
. Characterizations of the damping properties are scarce,
[
20
]
experimentally detected Prony-parameters from punch tests and a specially developed dynamical mechanical analysis (DMA); however, the frequency range of this DMA is limited and the influence of temperature is not treated.
[
7
]
measured storage and loss modulus at a single frequency of
1
Hz for a range of temperatures and several PDMS polymers. In their technical note,
[
22
]
treat the applicability of time-temperature-superposition to Sylgard 184.

We propose an approach that ensures thermodynamic consistency when dealing with large deformations in viscoelastic solids.
The ensuing incremental formulation is based on the Clausius-Duhem inequality and is defined not only through hyperelastic energy densities, but also through a dissipation function to model viscoelastic losses.
In
[
17
]
, a similar characterization was extended to include electromechanical coupling due to dielectric effects.
To determine the material parameters used in these functions, a specimen of Sylgard 184 is subjected to dynamical temperature mechanical analysis (DTMA) in a limited range of frequencies. Through time-temperature superposition, a sufficiently large range of frequencies can be covered. From this data, a set of material parameters for a Prony-series expansion is found through a non-negative, non-linear least squares fit, similar to
[
16
]
. These Prony-parameter directly correspond to the parameters of the proposed non-linear model.

In order to verify the proposed constitutive model, a finite element model of a ball-drop experiment is set up. The incremental variational formulation can directly be transferred to the context of finite element simulation. A classical stable pair of displacement-pressure elements is proposed to account for the incompressible nature of PDMS. Additional degrees of freedom for viscous strains satisfy the incompressibility condition exactly for radially symmetric problems. Time integration using the Newmark-
\beta
scheme in variational context avoids any numerical damping.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: After the introduction, the constitutive model based on a hyperelastic potential, viscous contributions and a dissipation function is discussed. Next, the correspondence of this non-linear model to a Prony-series expansion is discussed. The estimation of Prony-parameters from DTMA measurements is conducted using time-temperature superposition as well as non-negative least squares approximation. An incremental variational formulation, which replaces D’Alembert’s principle in dissipative processes, is derived and shown to be equivalent to classical viscoelastic models. This directly leads to the definition of a finite element model for radially symmetric problems in the following section. Finally, computational and experimental results are compared for a ball-drop experiment.

2.2
Constitutive equations

In the following, a thermodynamically consistent model for a viscoelastic material shall be developed.
To this end, the free energy density
\Psi
shall be defined for independent Cauchy-Green tensor
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}
or deformation gradient
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}
.
The viscoelastic Cauchy-Green tensors
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}
will serve as internal variables, and pressure
p
will act as a Lagrangian multiplier for the incompressibility constraint.

The free energy is assumed to be additively composed of several independent potentials, compare
[
2
]
. We consider a decomposition into a hyperelastic potential
\Psi_{\infty}
and a viscoelastic energy densities
\Psi_{v,i}
, which shall be defined in the following,

\Psi(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i},p)=\Psi_{\infty}({\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}},p)+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Psi_{v,i}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}).

(6)

For the formulation of the constitutive equations we use the framework of the Clausius-Duhem inequality.
This inequality states essentially that the dissipation
\mathcal{D}
is non-negative (cf.
[
9
]
), which can be written as,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{T}}:\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}-\dot{\Psi}\geq 0.

(7)

Above,
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{T}}
is the second Piola-Kirchhoff stress. Computing the rate
\dot{\Psi}
explicitly, equation (
7
) becomes,

\mathcal{D}=-\left(\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{T}}\right):\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}}:\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}_{v,i}-\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial p}\dot{p}\geq 0.

(8)

Equation (
8
) implies the classical constitutive equations for the second Piola-Kirchhoff stress
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{T}}
as,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathrm{T}}=2\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}.

(9)

Moreover, pressure
p
acting as a Lagrangian multiplier ensures the condition of incompressibility as
\partial\Psi/\partial p=0
.
The reduced dissipation inequality states that the negative inner product of
generalized driving forces

\;\partial\Psi/\partial\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}
and viscous strain rates
\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}_{v,i}
is non-negative,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}}:\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}_{v,i}\geq 0.

(10)

Free energy

Let us collect specific representations for the energy densities constituting the augmented free energy
\Psi
.
The elastic part of the free energy
\Psi_{\infty}
will be governed by an incompressible Neo-Hookean hyperelastic potential. We use
\mu_{\infty}=G_{\infty}
for the long-term shear modulus,

{\Psi}_{\infty}({\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}},p)=\frac{\mu_{\infty}}{2}\left(\operatorname{tr}{\mathbf{C}}-3\right)-\mu_{\infty}\log J+p\log J.

(11)

Pressure
p
serves as a Lagrangian multiplier to ensure the incompressibility constraint,

\frac{\partial\Psi_{\infty}}{\partial p}=\log J=0,

such that
J\equiv 1
.

Concerning the
i^{th}
multiplicative decomposition, a similar incompressible Neo-Hookean hyperelastic potential models the elastic energy for
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}_{e,i}
with shear modulus
\mu_{v,i}
.
As
\operatorname{tr}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}_{e,i}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}:{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}_{v,i}^{-1}
, we use the representation,

\Psi_{v,i}({\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}},\mathbf{C}_{{v},i}})=\frac{\mu_{v,i}}{2}\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}:\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}^{-1}-3\right).

(12)

Dissipation

Viscoelastic evolution is defined implicitly through a
dissipation function

\Phi
such that,

\mathcal{D}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}_{v,i}}:\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}_{v,i}.

(13)

As for the free energy densities, we also assume the dissipation function to be composed additively from contributions due to the
N
internal variables
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}
. Each of the terms is governed by a viscosity parameter
\eta_{i}
. To be specific, we use,

\displaystyle\Phi=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Phi_{i}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i},\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}_{v,i}),

(14)

with,

\displaystyle\Phi_{i}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i},\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}_{v,i})=\frac{1}{12}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\eta_{i}({\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}_{v,i}^{-1}\cdot\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}_{v,i}):({\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}_{v,i}^{-1}\cdot\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}_{v,i}).

(15)

To analyze the implications of free energy
\Psi
and dissipation function
\Phi
on the evolution of
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}
, an incremental variational formulation based on these quantities is posed later. There, we see that classical viscoelastic material models are reproduced for the above choices. Additionally, the incremental variational formulation can directly be transferred to a finite element discretization, which we do for the special case of rotationally symmetric problems.

3
Temperature-time shifting and parameter fitting of measured data

3.1
Linear viscoelasticity

The viscoelastic parameters such as the long-term modulus
\mu_{\infty}
and the viscous properties
\mu_{i}
and
\eta_{i}
of our non-linear constitutive model
are characterized from small-strain measurements, such that an identification using the linear Wiechert model is valid.
Within this model, each spring is characterized through its elastic modulus
E_{i}
. The long-term modulus of the system equals
E_{\infty}
, i.e. the modulus of the single spring. Its instantaneous modulus
E_{0}
can be found as,

\displaystyle E_{0}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}E_{i}+E_{\infty},

(16)

for a thorough discussion we refer to
[
13
]
.
For incompressible materials with a Poisson ratio of
\nu=0.5
, shear moduli
\mu_{i}
and Young’s moduli
E_{i}
are connected via,

\displaystyle\mu_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{E_{i}}{1+2\nu}=\frac{E_{i}}{3},

\displaystyle i\in\{1,\dots N,\infty\}.

(17)

Additionally,
\tau_{i}
defines the relaxation time of the
i^{\text{th}}
Maxwell element by,

\displaystyle\eta_{i}=\tau_{i}E_{i}.

(18)

At time
t>0
, the relaxation modulus is found as, see
[
4
]
,

\displaystyle E(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}E_{i}e^{-t/\tau_{i}}+E_{\infty}.

(19)

Representation (
19
) is often referred to as Prony-series. After Fourier transformation to frequency domain, one obtains the more common series expansion,

\displaystyle E^{*}(\omega)=\underbrace{E_{\infty}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{E_{i}\tau_{i}^{2}\omega^{2}}{1+\tau_{i}^{2}\omega^{2}}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}E^{\prime}(\omega)\end{subarray}}+j\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{E_{i}\tau_{i}\omega}{1+\tau_{i}^{2}\omega^{2}}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}E^{\prime\prime}(\omega)\end{subarray}},

(20)

where
E^{*}(\omega)
is termed the complex modulus of the material.
In this context, the real part
\Re(E^{*}(\omega))
is called storage modulus
E^{\prime}(\omega)
and the imaginary part
\Im(E^{*}(\omega))
is known as the loss modulus
E^{\prime\prime}(\omega)
, see
[
28
]
.

Furthermore we define
e_{i}=E_{i}/E_{0}
with the instantaneous modulus
E_{0}
as defined in (
16
). Following our model, the material is now fully characterized when estimating the
2N
parameters
e_{i}
and
\tau_{i}
as well as the long-term modulus
E_{\infty}
. We often refer to
N
as the
order
of the Prony-series.

3.3
Time-temperature superposition

Concerning time-temperature superposition, Sylgard 184 is treated as a thermorheologically complex material, cf.
[
14
]
.
In the modeled area, the cross-linked material is well above the glass transition temperature
T_{g}
and the melting temperature
T_{m}
, cf.
[
8
]
. This results in a melt-like mobility, which can be described by means of thermorheologically complex modeling.
The selected approach combines the classic TTSP for solid polymers (complex Young’s modulus) and the TTSP for polymer melts (complex viscosity).
Thus, horizontal frequency shifts as well as vertical shifts in the modulus are applied.
In
[
29
]
, the following general form of time-temperature shifts was identified as,

\displaystyle E_{T}^{*}(\omega)=b_{T}E_{T_{0}}^{*}(a_{T}\omega).

(21)

Shifting parameters
a_{T}
and
b_{T}
allow to transfer the material’s modulus from reference temperature
T_{0}
to any other temperature
T
, at least within a certain temperature range.
A simple approach for shifting the modulus is given by,

\displaystyle b_{T}=\frac{T_{0}\rho_{0}}{T\rho},

(22)

where
\rho
and
\rho_{0}
are the mass density of the polymer at
T
and
T_{0}
.
Since we assume no relevant density differences within the sampled temperature range of
0\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}
to
40\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}
, this density ratio is neglected in the following.

For the horizontal frequency shift a large variety of models can be found. Probably the one most commonly used is the Williams-Landel-Ferry model (WLF).
However, a main criterion for this is free-volume availability, which is despite the amorphous morphology at ambient temperature limited by the intermolecular bonds created in cross linking. As shown in
[
21
]
, the WLF model can be used very well for a temperature range of
T_{g}<T<T_{g}+$100\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}$
.

[
6
]
and also
[
19
]
found that the glass transition temperature of Sylgard 184 is in the range of
-150\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}

<T_{g}<$-120\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}$
and the melting temperature
T_{m}
lies between
-50\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}
and
-70\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}
. This implies that the WLF model is not applicable to Sylgard 184 at ambient temperatures.

[
21
]
states that in this relatively high temperature range of
T>T_{g}+$100\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}$
, the so-called Arrhenius equation is applicable,

\displaystyle a_{T}=e^{\alpha\left(\frac{1}{T}-\frac{1}{T_{0}}\right)}.

(23)

The parameter
\alpha=E_{a}/\mathcal{R}
is independent of temperature, but can be computed from the activation energy
E_{0}
and the universal gas constant
\mathcal{R}
. We use the DTMA measurement data to compute
\alpha
, and thereby the activation energy
E_{a}
, using a least-squares method.

In order to estimate
\alpha
, at reference temperature of
T_{0}=$20\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}$
measurements within an extended frequency range were conducted. While it is of course not possible to extend this range towards higher frequencies, measurements for lower frequencies approaching zero could easily be conducted; the results are displayed in Figure
4
.
Then, data sets for temperatures from
20\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}
to
40\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}
were used to determine the unknown shift parameter
\alpha
, as higher temperatures correspond to lower frequencies.
A least squares method is used to find the optimal parameter
\alpha
in order to minimise the squared vertical distance of the respective data curves when shifted by
b_{T}
and
a_{T}
as in (
22
) and (
23
), respectively.
As the shifted frequencies for the data points do no coincide in general,
a linear interpolation of the reference data
E^{*}_{T_{0}}
in between data points is used instead. For
\omega_{i},i=1\dots n_{\omega}
the frequencies at which DTMA data is available, the least-squares problem finally reads,

\displaystyle\sum_{T>T_{0}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\omega}}\left|b_{T}E^{*}_{T_{0}}(a_{T}\omega_{i})-E^{*}_{T}(\omega_{i})\right|^{2}\to\min_{\alpha>0}.

(24)

For the available DTMA data, this procedure results in
\alpha=$7389.124\text{\,}\mathrm{K}$
.
Then, both the storage and loss moduli from all DTMA data temperatures can be shifted according to (
21
) to get a master curve at temperature
T_{0}=$20\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}$
as shown in Figure
4
. These shifted data sets are used in the subsequent detection of Prony-parameters; the additional data in the extended frequency range for temperature
T_{0}
are not included in this process anymore.

3.4
Prony-parameters

Once data in an extended frequency range has been generated using the TTSP, Prony-parameters
E_{\infty}
as well as
e_{i}
and
\tau_{i}
can be detected. To this end, a non-linear least-squares optimization problem for the
2N+1
unknown Prony-parameters needs to be solved.
For the presented data, the non-negative least squares based routine
curve_fit
provided within python’s
scipy
package could be applied successfully.

This function works with complex data sets, and thereby ensures that the parameters determined are accurate for both the storage and loss modulus.
Moreover, range conditions have to be met for the different parameters. A minimal set of these conditions is to seek for

•

a positive long-term modulus
E_{\infty}>0
,

•

positive relaxation times
\tau_{i}>0
,

•

relative viscoelastic moduli
0<e_{i}<1
,

•

satisfying additionally
\sum_{i=1}^{N}e_{i}\leq 1
.

Simple range conditions like the former three items can be included in the parameters passed to the least-squares fitting routine.
The latter condition has to be verified manually, once the parameters are computed. As one often observes in non-linear problems, choosing a feasible starting value is of utmost importance.
We observed that setting all values to one worked well when seeking the long-term modulus equivalent to
1\text{\,}\mathrm{MPa}
.

Finally, the order
N
of the Wiechert model has to be chosen in accordance with the available data set.
As a rule of thumb, good approximation is obtained when using one Maxwell element per measured frequency decade.
Since after TTSP, measurements are available for frequencies from
0.1\text{\,}\mathrm{Hz}
up to almost
1000\text{\,}\mathrm{Hz}
(see Figure
4
), fitting Prony-parameters for models of third and fourth order seems appropriate.
The respective results are depicted in Figure

.

4
Incremental minimization principle

Having determined the relevant constitutive parameters, we now seek a finite element formulation for the problem at hand. As a starting point, a variational formulation for viscoelastic solids is derived from an incremental minimization problem. A similar procedure has been presented for modeling various dissipative processes by the group around Miehe, we cite non-exhaustively
[
24
,
25
,
23
]
.
For reasons of simplicity of presentation, we restrict ourselves to the case of a conservative volume force density
{\boldsymbol{f}}
. With
\rho_{0}
the mass density, the kinetic energy density
T
and local power of external forces
\mathcal{P}_{ext}
read,

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\rho_{0}\dot{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\cdot\dot{{\boldsymbol{u}}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{ext}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{f}}\cdot\dot{{\boldsymbol{u}}}.

(25)

Consider a finite time step
[t_{0},t_{0}+\Delta t]
with known initial conditions at time
t_{0}
.
We introduce the
incremental potential
for this time step as,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Pi_{t}^{t+\Delta t}&=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+\Delta t}(\dot{T}+\dot{\Psi}+\Phi-\mathcal{P}_{ext})\,dt\\
&=\underbrace{T_{t_{0}+\Delta t}-T_{t_{0}}}_{\text{kinetic}}+\underbrace{\Psi_{t_{0}+\Delta t}-\Psi_{t_{0}}}_{\text{stored}}+\underbrace{\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+\Delta t}\Phi dt}_{\text{dissipated}}-\underbrace{\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+\Delta t}\mathcal{P}_{ext}\,dt}_{\text{external}}.\end{split}

(26)

The incremental potential
\Pi_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+\Delta t}
is then minimized with respect to all admissible displacements and viscoelastic internal parameters, and maximized with respect to hydrostatic pressure.
For time step size
\Delta t
approaching zero, we arrive at,

\displaystyle\dot{T}+\dot{\Psi}+\Phi-\mathcal{P}_{ext}\to\min_{\dot{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}\min_{\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}_{v,i}}\max_{\dot{p}}.

(27)

We can now rewrite equation (
27
) for the specific choice of free energy
\Psi
and get,

\begin{split}\rho\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}+\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}\colon\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}}\colon\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}_{v,i}+\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial{p}}\dot{p}+\Phi-{\boldsymbol{f}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\dot{u}}}\to\min_{\dot{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}\min_{\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}_{v,i}}\max_{\dot{p}}.\end{split}

(28)

Under common assumptions on convexity and smoothness of the involved energies and dissipation functions, the optimization problem is equivalent to finding critical points as solutions to the corresponding variational equation, also known as D’Alembert’s principle,

\begin{split}&\int_{\mathcal{B}}\rho\ddot{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\cdot\delta{\boldsymbol{u}}\,dV+\int_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}\colon\delta\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}}\colon\delta{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}_{v,i}+\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial{p}}\delta{{p}}\right)\,dV\\
&+\int_{\mathcal{B}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}_{v,i}}\colon\delta\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}\,dV-\int_{\mathcal{B}}{\boldsymbol{f}}\cdot\delta{\boldsymbol{u}}\,dV=0.\end{split}

(29)

For a simplified presentation, we rewrite D’Alembert’s principle in more compact form, although abusing notation concerning the variation of the dissipation function,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\rho\ddot{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\cdot\delta{\boldsymbol{u}}+\delta\Psi+\delta\Phi-{\boldsymbol{f}}\cdot\delta{\boldsymbol{u}}\right)\,dV

\displaystyle=0,

(30)

with,

\displaystyle\delta\Psi:=\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}\colon\delta\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}}\colon\delta{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}_{v,i}+\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial{p}}\delta{{p}},

(32)

and

\displaystyle\delta\Phi:=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}_{v,i}}\colon\delta\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}.

(33)

The variational equation (
29
) implicitly defines the evolution law for
\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}_{v,i}
for arbitrary but fixed index
i\in\{1,\dots N\}
. We show that, for the proposed choice of dissipation function from equation (
15
), the standard model as e.g. proposed by
[
13
]
is re-established.
We collect all terms containing
\delta\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}
from equation (
29
), and obtain,

\int_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\frac{\partial\Psi_{v,i}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}}+\frac{1}{6}\eta_{i}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}^{-1}\cdot\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}_{v,i}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}^{-1}\right)\right)\colon\delta\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}\,dV=0.

(34)

Since
\det\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}=1
, we observe for its rate and variation,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}(\det\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i})=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}^{-1}\colon\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}_{v,i}=0,

(35)

\displaystyle\delta(\det\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i})=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}^{-1}\colon\delta{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}_{v,i}=0.

(36)

Thus, a contribution of the form
\gamma{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}}^{-1}\colon\delta{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}_{v,i}=0
can be added without changing the equation (
34
), which leads to,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{B}}{\left(\frac{\partial\Psi_{v,i}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}}+\gamma\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}^{-1}+\frac{1}{6}\eta_{i}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}^{-1}\cdot\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}_{v,i}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}^{-1}\right)}\colon\delta\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}\,dV={0}.

Here,
\gamma
is an arbitrary constant that still has to be determined in the following.
As
\delta\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}
is otherwise arbitrary, the equality holds in classical sense,

\frac{\partial\Psi_{v,i}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}}+\gamma\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}^{-1}+\frac{1}{6}\eta_{i}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}^{-1}\cdot\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}_{v,i}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}^{-1}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{0}},

(37)

which directly implies,

\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}_{v,i}=-\frac{6}{\eta_{i}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}\cdot\left(\frac{\partial\Psi_{v,i}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}}+\gamma\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}^{-1}\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}.

(38)

Contraction with
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}^{-1}
leads through equation (
35
) to,

\displaystyle 0=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}^{-1}:\left[\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}\cdot\left(\frac{\partial\Psi_{v,i}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}}+\gamma\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}^{-1}\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}\right]=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}}:\left[\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}\cdot\frac{\partial\Psi_{v,i}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}}+\gamma\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}}\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}^{-1}\right].

Since
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}^{-1}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}}
, we can simplify even further,

\displaystyle 0=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}\cdot\frac{\partial\Psi_{v,i}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}}:\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}}+\gamma\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}}:\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}}=-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{M}}_{v,i}:\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}}+3\gamma.

Above, we introduce
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{M}}_{v,i}
as the
i^{\text{th}}
Mandel-type stress tensor,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathrm{M}}_{v,i}=-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}\cdot\frac{\partial\Psi_{v,i}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}}.

(39)

With this, we finally have an expression to determine the unknown parameter
\gamma
,

\displaystyle\gamma=\frac{1}{3}\operatorname{tr}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{M}}_{v,i}.

(40)

We can now substitute
\gamma
back into equation (
38
) and after a few algebraic manipulations we get,

\displaystyle\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}_{v,i}

\displaystyle=\frac{6}{\eta_{i}}\operatorname{dev}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{M}}_{v,i})\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i},

(41)

which is stated in similar form by
[
2
]
for viscoelastic electrostrictive polymers.
To compare the final evolution law to standard theory, we explicitly compute the Mandel-type stresses for the viscoelastic potential
\Psi_{v,i}
from equation (
12
) and obtain,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathrm{M}}_{v,i}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mu_{v,i}}{2}\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}^{-1}\right).

(42)

After combining the two equations equation (
41
) and equation (
42
) we finally obtain the evolution law for
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}
as stated in
[
13
]
,

\displaystyle\frac{2}{3}\eta\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}_{v,i}=2\mu_{v,i}\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}-\frac{1}{3}\operatorname{tr}\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}^{-1}\right)\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}\right).

(43)

5
Finite elements for radially symmetric problems

For radially symmetric problems, a finite element model respecting incompressibility of the viscous strains explicitely can be devised as follows. This setup is designed such that it allows for verification against various measurement setups,
like the ball-drop experiment in the last section to determine rebound resilience.

5.1
Spatial discretization for radially symmetric problems

Let the body
\mathcal{B}
as well as all external forces and kinematic constraints be in accordance with radial symmetry. Without restriction of generality, we consider
{\boldsymbol{e}}_{z}
as axis of rotation. Deformation gradient
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}
and right Cauchy-Green
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}
are defined in the standard way, assuming that the solution is independent of the angular coordinate
\varphi
.

Assume
\mathcal{T}=\{T\}
to be a triangular finite element mesh of the section of
\mathcal{B}
under consideration. Concerning the elastic problem, we propose to use the pair of displacement-pressure elements as proposed by Taylor and Hood (cf.
[
27
]
), where both
{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
p
are represented by continuous basis functions
{\boldsymbol{N}}^{(u)}_{i}
and
N^{(p)}_{i}
respectively, and the approximation order for
{\boldsymbol{u}}
is one higher than for
p
.
Additionally, kinematic boundary conditions
{\boldsymbol{u}}={\boldsymbol{0}}
on the boundary part
\Gamma_{fix}
have to be satisfied. Summing up, finite element approximations for
{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
p
satisfy,

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{u}}

\displaystyle\in\{{\boldsymbol{v}}:{\boldsymbol{v}}|_{T}\in(P^{k}(T))^{2},{\boldsymbol{v}}\text{ cont.},{\boldsymbol{v}}|_{\Gamma_{fix}}={\boldsymbol{0}}\},

(44)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{u}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{u}}q_{i}^{(u)}{\boldsymbol{N}}^{(u)}_{i},

(45)

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle\in\{q:q|_{T}\in P^{k-1}(T),q\text{ cont.}\},

(46)

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{p}}q_{i}^{(p)}N^{(p)}_{i}.

(47)

In addition, it is necessary to provide a finite-dimensional approximation for the viscoelastic internal variables
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}
. Here, it is important that the kinematic constraint of incompressibility is satisfied by construction. For a rotationally symmetric setting, this can be achieved by a non-linear representation of
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}
in terms of the independent viscoelastic strains
s_{i,rr}
,
s_{i,zz}
and
s_{i,rz}
. The
i^{th}
viscoelastic Cauchy-Green tensor respects the axisymmetric structure,

\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}=\begin{bmatrix}2s_{i,rr}+1&0&2s_{i,rz}\\
0&c_{i,\varphi\varphi}&0\\
2s_{i,rz}&0&2s_{i,zz}+1\end{bmatrix}.

(48)

Component
c_{i,\varphi\varphi}
is chosen such that the incompressibility constraint
\det\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i}\equiv 1
is satisfied a-priorily, which implies,

\displaystyle c_{i,\varphi\varphi}=\left((2s_{i,rr}+1)(1+2s_{i,zz})-4s_{i,rz}\right)^{-1}.

(49)

The viscoelastic strains
s_{i,rr},s_{i,zz}
and
s_{i,rz}
are defined element-wise polynomial, without continuity, one polynomial order lower than the displacements,

\displaystyle\begin{split}s_{i,rr},s_{i,zz},s_{i,rz}&\in\{s:s|_{T}\in P^{k-1}(T)\},\\
s_{i,\alpha\beta}&=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}q_{i}^{(s,\alpha\beta)}N^{(s)}_{i}.\end{split}

(50)

5.2
Time integration

Introducing the finite element discretizations equations (
46
), (
47
) and (
50
) in D’Alembert’s principle (
29
) leads to a system of ordinary differential equations for the degree of freedom vector
{\boldsymbol{q}}
. As the physical problem includes accelerations
\ddot{{\boldsymbol{u}}}
and viscoelastic strain rates
\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}_{v,i}
as well as the Lagrangian multiplier
p
, a differential-algebraic system of equations is generated after spatial discretization. It is well known that the Newmark-
\beta
scheme is well-suited to solve differential-algebraic equations of index 2, where the choice of parameter
\beta
further determines its numerical damping properties.

We shortly present the Newmark-
\beta
scheme adapted for the viscoelastic problem at hand. To this end, we assume that velocities
{\boldsymbol{v}}=\dot{{\boldsymbol{u}}}
are approximated by an independent linear combination of displacement basis functions
{\boldsymbol{N}}^{(u)}_{i}
, via additional degrees of freedom
q^{(v)}_{i}
. For any time step
[t_{n},t_{n+1}=t_{n}+\Delta t]
,
the quantities
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{n}
,
{\boldsymbol{v}}_{n}
,
p_{n}
as well as
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}_{v,i,n}
,
\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}_{v,i,n}
(or rather the viscoelastic strains
s_{i,\alpha\beta,n}
and strain rates
\dot{s}_{i,\alpha\beta,n}
) at time
t_{n}
are assumed to be available from the previous step.
According updates
{\boldsymbol{u}}
,
{\boldsymbol{v}}
,
p
,
s_{i,\alpha\beta}
, and
\dot{s}_{i,\alpha\beta}
at time
t_{n+1}=t_{n}+\Delta t
are to be computed, where we omit
n+1
as an index in favor of brevity of presentation.
The Newmark-
\beta
scheme interpreted in terms of the underlying variational equations leads to the coupled non-linear system, where we use
\delta{\boldsymbol{v}}
as the (independent) variation of the velocity
{\boldsymbol{v}}
,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{B}}

\displaystyle\rho\left({\boldsymbol{u}}-{\boldsymbol{u}}_{n}-\Delta t\,{\boldsymbol{v}}_{n}\right)\cdot\delta{\boldsymbol{u}}\,dV+\left(\Delta t\right)^{2}\int_{\mathcal{B}}\beta(\delta\Psi+\delta\Phi-{\boldsymbol{f}}\cdot\delta{\boldsymbol{u}})dV

\displaystyle+\left(\Delta t\right)^{2}\int_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}-\beta\right)(\delta\Psi_{n}+\delta\Phi_{n}-{\boldsymbol{f}}_{n}\cdot\delta{\boldsymbol{u}})\,dV=0,

(51)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{B}}

\displaystyle\rho\left({\boldsymbol{v}}-{\boldsymbol{v}}_{n}\right)\cdot\delta{\boldsymbol{v}}\,dV+\Delta t\int_{\mathcal{B}}\gamma\left(\left.\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial{\boldsymbol{u}}}\right|_{t_{n+1}}\cdot\delta{\boldsymbol{v}}-{\boldsymbol{f}}\cdot\delta{\boldsymbol{v}}\right)dV

\displaystyle+\Delta t\int_{\mathcal{B}}\left(1-\gamma\right)\left(\left.\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial{\boldsymbol{u}}}\right|_{t_{n}}\cdot\delta{\boldsymbol{v}}-{\boldsymbol{f}}_{n}\cdot\delta{\boldsymbol{v}}\right)\,dV=0.

(52)

Note that equation (
51
) does not only implement the Newmark integration scheme for the displacements, but also the incompressibility constraint via
\partial\Psi/\partial p\cdot\delta p=0
and the evolution law for the viscoelastic strains as indicated by the dissipation function. The variation of the dissipation function
\delta\Phi
is to be understood in the same manner as in equation (

7
Conclusion

In the present article, we have introduced a thermodynamically consistent characterization for viscoelastic materials for modeling the behavior of the PDMS Sylgard 184. To determine the model’s parameters, a dynamical temperature mechanical analysis was conducted in a limited frequency range. The time-temperature superposition principle was used successfully to generate an extended frequency response.
For this data, Prony-parameters based on the Wiechert model were identified using a non-negative least squares fitting algorithm.
To verify this constitutive model, a mixed finite element model for rotationally symmetric problems was created.
A ball-drop test setup was designed in order to conduct measurements on the rebound resilience for different ball sizes and drop heights. Data generated in the finite element simulation were then compared to optical tracking data from the experiment.
Our numerical results match the measured data almost perfectly as far as the indentation depth is concerned. Rebound resiliences differ, however, as we observe less dissipation in computations than in experiments.
To analyse these deviations in detail, the effects of friction-less contact and the imposed radial symmetry in computations need to be quantified in the future. Additionally, DTMA measurements in an even larger range of temperature may lead to a better characterization of the viscous behaviour at high frequencies, which are certainly excited in a ball-drop test.
