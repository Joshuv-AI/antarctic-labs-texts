On the convergence of multi-level Hermite-Padé approximants
L.G. González Ricardo, G. López Lagomasino, and S. Medina Peralta

Abstract
. In the present paper we prove a Stieltjes type theorem on the convergence of a sequence of rational functions associated with a mixed type Hermite-Padé approximation problem of a Nikishin system of functions and analyze the ratio asymptotic of the corresponding Hermite-Padé polynomials.



Keywords:
Nikishin system, multiple orthogonal polynomials, Hermite-Padé approximation, ratio asymptotic

AMS classification:
Primary: 42C05; 30E10, Secondary: 41A21

1.
Introduction

In
[
24
]
, a mixed type Hermite-Padé approximation problem was introduced in order to find multipeakon
solutions of the Degasperis-Procesi equation. The same construction is relevant in the study of the two matrix model
[
6
]
and Cauchy biorthogonal polynomials
[
5
]
.
The functions approximated are the Cauchy transforms of a pair of measures forming a Nikishin system. This motivated in
[
20
]
the extension of the approximation problem to the case of Nikishin systems with an arbitrary number of measures and study their convergence properties. Previously, the authors of
[
4
]
extended the two matrix model to an
m-
matrix model and studied a Riemann-Hilbert problem that leads to the mixed type Hermite-Padé approximation problem introduced in
[
20
]
.
Recently, the definition was further extended by V.G. Lysov in
[
21
]
and a number of interesting properties revealed. Before stating our goals, let us briefly present the so called Nikishin systems of measures and Lysov’s definition of multi-level Hermite-Padé approximants.

1.1.
Nikishin systems

Nikishin systems of measures were first introduced by E.M. Nikishin in
[
26
]
. Throughout the years, it has been shown that polynomials which share orthogonality relations with respect to such systems of measures behave asymptotically in a way similar to standard orthogonal polynomials (with respect to a single measure). A brief overview of the subject may be found in
[
18
]
.

In the sequel, we will only consider Borel measures
s
with constant sign, finite moments
c_{n}=\int x^{n}\D s(x)\neq\infty,n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, whose support
\supp s\subset\mathbb{R}
consists of infinitely many points. We will denote by
\Delta
the smallest interval which contains
\supp s
(
\Co(\supp s)=\Delta
). The class of these measures will be denoted by
\mathcal{M}(\Delta)
. Let

\widehat{s}(z)=\int\frac{\D s(x)}{z-x}

denote the Cauchy transform of the measure
s
. Obviously,
\widehat{s}\in\mathcal{H}(\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Delta)
(holomorphic in
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Delta
). We can associate to
\widehat{s}
its formal Laurent expansion at infinity

\widehat{s}(z)\sim\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{c_{j}}{z^{j+1}},\qquad c_{j}=\int x^{j}\D s(x).

Let
\Delta_{\alpha}
,
\Delta_{\beta}
be two intervals contained in the real line such that
\Delta_{\alpha}\cap\Delta_{\beta}
contains at most one common point. Assume that
\sigma_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{M}(\Delta_{\alpha}),\sigma_{\beta}\in\mathcal{M}(\Delta_{\beta})
, and
\widehat{\sigma}_{\beta}\in L_{1}(\sigma_{\alpha})
(integrable with respect to
\sigma_{\alpha}
). With these two measures we construct a third one, denoted
\langle\sigma_{\alpha},\sigma_{\beta}\rangle
, whose differential notation is given by

\D\langle\sigma_{\alpha},\sigma_{\beta}\rangle(x):=\widehat{\sigma}_{\beta}(x)\D\sigma_{\alpha}(x).

When considering consecutive products of measures, for example
\langle\sigma_{\alpha},\sigma_{\beta},\sigma_{\gamma}\rangle:=\langle\sigma_{\alpha},\langle\sigma_{\beta},\sigma_{\gamma}\rangle\rangle
, we implicitly assume not only that
\widehat{\sigma}_{\gamma}\in L_{1}(\sigma_{\beta})
, but also
\langle\sigma_{\beta},\sigma_{\gamma}\widehat{\rangle}\in L_{1}(\sigma_{\alpha})
, where
\langle\sigma_{\beta},\sigma_{\gamma}\widehat{\rangle}
denotes the Cauchy transform of
\langle\sigma_{\beta},\sigma_{\gamma}\rangle
. Notice that this product is neither commutative nor associative.

Definition 1
.

Take a collection
\Delta_{j}
,
j=1,\ldots,m,
of intervals such that

\Delta_{j}\cap\Delta_{j+1},

j=1,\ldots,m-1
contains at most one point.
Let
(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{m})
be a system of measures such that
\Co(\supp\sigma_{j})=\Delta_{j}
,
\sigma_{j}\in\mathcal{M}(\Delta_{j})
,
j=1,\ldots,m
. We say that
(s_{1,1},\ldots,s_{1,m})=\mathcal{N}(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{m})
, where

s_{1,1}=\sigma_{1},\quad s_{1,2}=\langle\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\rangle,\quad\ldots,\quad s_{1,m}=\langle\sigma_{1},\langle\sigma_{2},\ldots,\sigma_{m}\rangle\rangle,

is the Nikishin system of measures generated by
(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{m})
. The vector
(\widehat{s}_{1,1},\ldots,\widehat{s}_{1,m})
is the corresponding Nikishin system of functions.

Notice that any sub-system of
(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{m})
of consecutive measures is also a generator of a Nikishin system. In the sequel, for
1\leq j\leq k\leq m
we write

s_{j,k}:=\langle\sigma_{j},\sigma_{j+1},\ldots,\sigma_{k}\rangle,\qquad s_{k,j}:=\langle\sigma_{k},\sigma_{k-1},\ldots,\sigma_{j}\rangle.

With the system of measures
(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{m})
we can also define the reversed Nikishin system
(s_{m,m},\ldots,s_{m,1})=\mathcal{N}(\sigma_{m},\ldots,\sigma_{1})
.

1.2.
Multilevel Hermite-Padé polynomials

We use the definition given in
[
21
, Problem A]
.

Let
(\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{m})^{*}
be the set of all
m
-dimensional vectors with non-negative integer components, not identically equal to zero. For
\vec{n}=(n_{1},\ldots,n_{m})\in(\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{m})^{*}
we define
|\vec{n}|=n_{1}+\cdots+n_{m}
.

Definition 2
.

Consider the Nikishin system
\mathcal{N}(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{m})
and
\vec{n}=(n_{1},\ldots,n_{m})\in(\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{m})^{*}
. There exist polynomials
a_{\vec{n},0},a_{\vec{n},1},\ldots,a_{\vec{n},m}
, where
\deg a_{\vec{n},j}\leq|\vec{n}|-1,j=0,1,\ldots,m-1,
and
\deg a_{\vec{n},m}\leq|\vec{n}|
, not all identically equal to zero, called multi-level (ML) Hermite-Padé polynomials of
\mathcal{N}(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{m})
with respect to
\vec{n}
, that verify

(1.1)

\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n},j}(z):=\left((-1)^{j}a_{\vec{n},j}+\sum_{k=j+1}^{m}(-1)^{k}a_{\vec{n},k}\widehat{s}_{j+1,k}\right)(z)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{z^{n_{j+1}+1}}\right),\qquad z\to\infty,

where
j=0,\ldots,m-1
(the asymptotic expansion of
\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n},j}
at
\infty
begins with
z^{-n_{j+1}-1}
, or higher). For completeness, set
\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n},m}:=(-1)^{m}a_{\vec{n},m}
.

We warn the reader that with our terminology in
[
21
, Problem A]
the ML Hermite-Padé polynomials were defined with respect to the system
\mathcal{N}(\sigma_{m},\ldots,\sigma_{1})
.

When
m=1
the definition reduces to that of classical Padé approximation, which plays a central role in the solution of the inverse spectral problem for a discrete
string with Dirichlet boundary condition, see
[
3
,
31
]
. When
m=2
and
\vec{n}=(n,0)
definition
2
reduces to the Hermite-Padé approximation problem used in the solution of the inverse spectral problem for the discrete cubic string, see
[
24
]
. For an arbitrary
m
and
\vec{n}=(n,0,\ldots,0)
, one obtains the original definition of ML Hermite-Padé polynomials given in
[
20
]
.
We suspect that the ML Hermite-Padé approximations studied here, or the particular case considered in
[
20
]
, may be connected with the solution of the inverse spectral problem for the discrete
(m+1)-
string.

In this scheme of approximation, the interpolation conditions involve all the Nikishin systems of the “inner levels”, i.e.
\mathcal{N}(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{m})
,
\mathcal{N}(\sigma_{2},\ldots,\sigma_{m})
, …,
\mathcal{N}(\sigma_{m})=(s_{m,m})
. Finding the polynomials
a_{\vec{n},0},a_{\vec{n},1},\ldots,a_{\vec{n},m}
reduces to solving a homogeneous linear system of
|\vec{n}|(m+1)
equations, given by the interpolation conditions, on
|\vec{n}|(m+1)+1
unknowns, corresponding with the coefficients of the polynomials. Consequently, the system of equations has a non trivial solution.

Following K. Mahler
[
22
]
, a multi-index
\vec{n}\in(\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{m})^{*}
is said to be normal if
\deg a_{{\vec{n}},j}=|\vec{n}|-1,j=0,\ldots,m-1,
and
\deg a_{{\vec{n}},m}=|\vec{n}|
(that is, when all the polynomials have maximum degree possible). The system of functions is said to be perfect when all the multi-indices are normal. In
[
21
, Theorem 1.1]
, it was proved that the Nikishin system of functions is perfect for this approximation problem. Normality implies that the vector
(a_{\vec{n},0},\ldots,a_{\vec{n},m})
is uniquely determined up to a multiplicative factor. In the sequel, we normalize this vector so that
a_{\vec{n},m}
has leading coefficient equal to one.

A sequence of multi-indices
\Lambda\subset(\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{m})^{*}
is called a ray sequence when
\lim_{\vec{n}\in\Lambda}n_{j}/|\vec{n}|
exists for all
j=1,\ldots,m
. When the
\Delta_{j}
are bounded non-intersecting intervals, and
\sigma_{j}^{\prime}\neq 0
, a.e. in
\Delta_{j}
,
j=1,\ldots,m,
in
[
21
, Theorem 1.2]
the logarithmic asymptotic of ray sequences of ML polynomials was obtained. Using that result, it was also proved
[
21
, Proposition 1.2]
that

(1.2)

\lim_{\vec{n}\in\Lambda}\frac{a_{\vec{n},j}}{a_{\vec{n},m}}=\widehat{s}_{m,j+1},\qquad j=0,\ldots,m-1

uniformly on each compact subset of
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Delta_{m}
(with geometric rate). This is a Markov type theorem, see
[
23
]
. Notice that the limits belong to the Nikishin system of functions corresponding to
\mathcal{N}(\sigma_{m},\ldots,\sigma_{1})
. It should be said that the proof of these results given in
[
21
]
may be adapted to the case when the measures
\sigma_{j}
are in the much wider class of regular measures (for the definition of a regular measure see
[
30
, Chapter 3]
).

We provide a convergence result such as (
1.2
) in which the intervals
\Delta_{j}
may be unbounded and consecutive intervals can have a common end point. This situation appears in
[
6
]
in relation with the study of the two matrix model.

When the
\Delta_{j}
are bounded non-intersecting intervals, and
\sigma_{j}^{\prime}\neq 0
a.e. in
\Delta_{j}
, we also give a result about the asymptotic of sequences of ratios of polynomials
a_{\vec{n},j}
corresponding to “consecutive” multi-indices which resembles E.A. Rakhmanov’s celebrated theorem on the ratio asymptotic of standard orthogonal polynomials (see
[
27
,
28
,
29
,
25
]
). With the original definition of ML Hermite-Padé polynomials introduced in
[
20
]
the ratio asymptotic was obtained in
[
11
]
.

1.3.
Statement of the main results

A measure
s\in\mathcal{M}(\Delta)
is said to satisfy Carleman’s condition when the sequence of its moments
(c_{n})_{n\geq 0}
verifies

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|c_{n}|^{-1/2n}=\infty.

When
\Delta
is either
\mathbb{R}_{+}
of
\mathbb{R}_{-}
, it is well known, see
[
9
]
, that this condition implies that the moment problem for
(c_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is determinate (that is, there is only one measure with that collection of moments). In turn, if the moment problem for
s
is determinate then the sequence of diagonal Padé approximants converges to
\widehat{s}
on each compact subset of
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Delta
(see T.J. Stieltjes
[
31
]
). We prove the following Carleman-Stieltjes type theorem in the context of ML Hermite-Padé approximants.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\Lambda\subset(\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{m})^{*}
be an infinite sequence of distinct multi-indices for which there exist
\ell\in\{0,\ldots,m-2\}
and a (fixed) non-negative integer
N
such that
n_{j+1}\leq n_{j}+N
for all
\ell+1\leq j\leq m-1
and
\vec{n}\in\Lambda
. Consider the sequence of vector polynomials
(a_{\vec{n},0},\ldots,a_{\vec{n},m})_{\vec{n}\in\Lambda}
associated with
\mathcal{N}(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{m})
. For
j=\ell,\ldots,m-2
the polynomial
a_{\vec{n},j}
has at least
|\vec{n}|-2m-N\frac{m(m+1)}{2}
sign changes in
\mathring{\Delta}_{m}
(the interior of
\Delta_{m}
with the Euclidean topology of
\mathbb{R}
). The polynomials
a_{\vec{n},m-1}
and
a_{\vec{n},m}
have, respectively,
|\vec{n}|-1
and
|\vec{n}|
interlacing simple zeros in
\mathring{\Delta}_{m}
.
Suppose that either the sequence of moments of
\sigma_{m}
satisfies Carleman’s condition or
\Delta_{m-1}
is a bounded interval which does not intersect
\Delta_{m}
; then (
1.2
) holds uniformly on each compact subset of
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Delta_{m}
for
j=\ell,\ldots,m-1
. If
\Lambda\subset(\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{m})^{*}
is an arbitrary infinite sequence of distinct multi-indices and
\sigma_{m}
satisfies Carleman’s condition or,
\Delta_{m-1}
is a bounded interval which does not intersect
\Delta_{m}
and
\lim_{\vec{n}\in\Lambda}(n_{1}+\cdots+n_{m-1})=\infty
, then (
1.2
) takes place for
j=m-1
.

If
\Lambda
is a sequence of distinct multi-indices whose components are decreasing, the (first) condition on
\Lambda
in Theorem
1.1
is verified with
\ell=0
and
N=0
. More precise information regarding the zeros of the polynomials
a_{\vec{n},j},j=0,\ldots,m-2
will be given in Section 2.

Let
\vec{n}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{m}
and
l\in\{1,\ldots,m\}.
Define

\vec{n}^{l}:=\left(n_{1},\ldots,n_{l}+1,\ldots,n_{m}\right),

the multi-index obtained adding
1
to the
l
-th component of
\vec{n}
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Consider the Nikishin system
\mathcal{N}(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{m})
where the
\Delta_{k},k=1,\ldots,m,
are bounded, disjoint intervals, and
\sigma_{k}^{\prime}\neq 0
a.e. in
\Delta_{k}
. Let
\Lambda\subset(\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{m})^{*}
be an infinite sequence of distinct multi-indices for which there exists a non-negative integer
N
such that
n_{j+1}\leq n_{j}+N
for all
1\leq j\leq m-1
and
\vec{n}\in\Lambda
.
Then, for
k=0,\ldots,m

(1.3)

\lim_{\vec{n}\in\Lambda}\frac{a_{\vec{n}^{l},k}(z)}{a_{\vec{n},k}(z)}=\frac{{\psi}^{(l)}_{m}(z)}{({\psi}^{(l)}_{m})^{\prime}(\infty)},

uniformly on each compact subset of
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Delta_{m}
, where
{\psi}^{(l)}_{m}\in\mathcal{H}(\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Delta_{m})
is defined in (
3.6
).

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we prove Theorem
1.1
. In Section
3
we study the asymptotic properties of the forms
\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n},k}
and the polynomials
a_{\vec{n},k}
. In particular, Theorem
1.2
is derived from the more general Theorem
3.4
. In the analysis of the ratio asymptotic an associated Riemann surface comes into play which will be introduced in that section.

2.
Convergence of the ML Hermite-Padé approximants

2.1.
Preliminaries

First, we study the location of the zeros of the forms
\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n},j}
,
j=0,\ldots,m
. For that purpose, we use
[
19
, Lemma 2.1]
which we state here for convenience of the reader.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
(s_{1,1},\ldots,s_{1,m})=\mathcal{N}(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{m})
be given. Assume that there exist polynomials with real coefficients
a_{0},\ldots,a_{m}
and a polynomial
w
with real coefficients whose zeros lie in
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Delta_{1}
such that

\frac{\mathcal{A}(z)}{w(z)}\in\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{C}\setminus\Delta_{1})\quad\textrm{ and }\quad\frac{\mathcal{A}(z)}{w(z)}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{z^{N}}\right),\quad z\rightarrow\infty,

where
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}:=a_{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}\widehat{s}_{1,k}
and
N\geq 1
. Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{1}:=a_{1}+\sum_{k=2}^{m}a_{k}\widehat{s}_{2,k}
. Then,

\frac{\mathcal{A}(z)}{w(z)}=\int\frac{\mathcal{A}_{1}(x)}{z-x}\frac{\D\sigma_{1}(x)}{w(x)}.

If
N\geq 2
, we also have

(2.1)

\int x^{\nu}\mathcal{A}_{1}(x)\frac{\D\sigma_{1}(x)}{w(x)}=0,\qquad\nu=0,1,\ldots,N-2.

In particular,
\mathcal{A}_{1}
has at least
N-1
sign changes in
\mathring{\Delta}_{1}
(the interior of
\Delta_{1}
in
\mathbb{R}
with the usual topology).

Given
\vec{n}=(n_{1}\ldots,n_{m})\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{m}
, set

\displaystyle\eta_{\vec{n},j}

\displaystyle:=n_{1}+\cdots+n_{j}.

Lemma 2.2
.

The form
\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n},0}
has no zero in
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Delta_{1}
. For
j=1,\ldots,m,

\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n},j}
has exactly
\eta_{\vec{n},j}
zeros in
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Delta_{j+1},\,(\Delta_{m+1}=\emptyset)
, they are all simple and lie in
\mathring{\Delta}_{j}
. If
w_{\vec{n},j},j=1,\ldots,m-1,
denotes the monic polynomial whose roots are the simple zeros which
\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n},j}
has in
\mathring{\Delta}_{j}
then

(2.2)

\frac{\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n},j}}{w_{\vec{n},j}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{z^{\eta_{\vec{n},j+1}+1}}\right)\in\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{C}\setminus\Delta_{j+1}),\qquad z\to\infty.

For each
j=0,\ldots,m-1
the order of interpolation at infinity prescribed in (
1.1
) is exact.

Proof.

From (
1.1
) for
j=0
, using Lemma
2.1
with
w\equiv 1
, we obtain

\int x^{\nu}\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n},1}(x)\D\sigma_{1}(x)=0,\qquad\nu=0,1,\ldots,n_{1}-1.

Therefore,
\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n},1}
has at least
n_{1}
sign changes in
\mathring{\Delta}_{1}
.

Let
w_{\vec{n},1}
be a polynomial whose roots lie in
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Delta_{2}
and contain all the points where
\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n},1}
changes sign in
\mathring{\Delta}_{1}
. Then,
\deg w_{\vec{n},1}\geq n_{1}
and taking into account (
1.1
) for
j=1
, we obtain

\frac{\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n},1}(z)}{w_{\vec{n},1}(z)}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{z^{\eta_{\vec{n},2}+1}}\right)\in\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{C}\setminus\Delta_{2}).

Notice that
\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n},1}
and
w_{\vec{n},1}
satisfy the hypothesis of Lemma
2.1
, so

\int x^{\nu}\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n},2}(x)\frac{\D\sigma_{2}(x)}{w_{\vec{n},1}(x)}=0,\qquad\nu=0,1,\ldots,n_{1}+n_{2}-1.

This implies that
\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n},2}
has, at least,
n_{1}+n_{2}
sign changes in
\mathring{\Delta}_{2}
.

Let
w_{\vec{n},2}
be a polynomial whose roots lie in
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Delta_{3}
and contain all the points where
\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n},2}
changes sign in
\mathring{\Delta}_{2}
. Then,
\deg w_{\vec{n},2}\geq n_{1}+n_{2}
and taking into account (
1.1
) for
j=2
, we obtain

\frac{\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n},2}(z)}{w_{\vec{n},2}(z)}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{z^{\eta_{\vec{n},3}+1}}\right)\in\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{C}\setminus\Delta_{3}).

We have deduced analogous conclusions for
\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n},2}
as we had for
\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n},1}
.

We can repeat these arguments inductively and obtain that for each
j=0,\ldots,m-1
there exists a polynomial
w_{\vec{n},j},\deg w_{\vec{n},j}\geq n_{1}+\cdots+n_{j}=\eta_{\vec{n},j}
(
w_{\vec{n},0}\equiv 1
) whose
roots lie in
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Delta_{j+1}
and contain all the points where
\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n},j}
changes sign in
\mathring{\Delta}_{j}
and (
2.2
) takes place.

For
j=m-1
, we get

(2.3)

\frac{a_{\vec{n},m}\widehat{s}_{m,m}-a_{\vec{n},m-1}}{w_{\vec{n},m-1}}(z)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{z^{\eta_{\vec{n},m}+1}}\right)\in\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{C}\setminus\Delta_{m})

and using again Lemma
2.1

\int x^{\nu}a_{\vec{n},m}(x)\frac{\D s_{m,m}(x)}{w_{\vec{n},m-1}(x)}=0,\qquad\nu=0,1,\ldots,|\vec{n}|-1.

This implies that
a_{\vec{n},m}
has at least
|\vec{n}|
sign changes in
\mathring{\Delta}_{m}
. Since
\deg a_{\vec{n},m}\leq|\vec{n}|
we get that
a_{\vec{n},m}
is either identically equal to zero or it has exactly
|\vec{n}|
simple zeros, all in
\mathring{\Delta}_{m}
. The first situation cannot occur since from (
1.1
) it would follow that
(a_{\vec{n},0}\ldots,a_{\vec{n},m})\equiv\vec{0}
. So, only the second statement is possible.

Notice that if
\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n},0}
has a zero in
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Delta_{1}
, or for some
j=1,\ldots,m-1
,
\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n},j}
has more than
\eta_{\vec{n},j}
zeros in
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Delta_{j+1}
, we can get an extra order of interpolation in (
2.3
). This also occurs if for some
j=0,\ldots,m-1
the order of intepolation at
\infty
in (
1.1
) is higher than the one imposed. This entails one more orthogonality relation for
a_{\vec{n},m}
implying that this polynomials is identically equal to zero which is not possible. The statements of the lemma readily follow.
∎

In order to prove Theorem
1.1
, we need relations similar to (
2.3
) for
a_{\vec{n},m}\widehat{s}_{m,j+1}-a_{\vec{n},j}
,
j=0,\ldots,m-1
. For this purpose, some transformations involving reciprocals and ratios of Cauchy transforms of measures will be employed. We introduce them next.

It is well known that for each measure
\sigma\in\mathcal{M}(\Delta)
, where
\Delta\subset\mathbb{R}
is either a closed bounded interval or it is a half-line (that is, it is unbounded only on one side of the real line), there exist a measure
\tau\in\mathcal{M}(\Delta)
and a polynomial
\ell(z)=az+b
,
a=1/|\sigma|,b\in\mathbb{R}
, such that

(2.4)

\frac{1}{\widehat{\sigma}(z)}=\ell(z)+\widehat{\tau}(z),

where
|\sigma|
denotes the total mass of the measure
\sigma
. For more details, in the case of measures with compact support see
[
13
, Appendix]
and
[
30
, Theorem 6.3.5]
, when the measure is supported in a half line see
[
10
, Lemma 2.3]
. If
\sigma
satisfies Carleman’s condition then
\tau
also verifies that condition,
[
19
, Theorem 1.5]
. We call
\tau
the inverse measure of
\sigma
.

Inverse measures appear frequently in our developments, so we will fix a notation to differentiate them. In relation with the measures denoted with
s
they will carry over to them the corresponding sub-indices. The same goes for the polynomials
\ell
. For example:

\frac{1}{\widehat{s}_{j,k}(z)}=\ell_{j,k}(z)+\widehat{\tau}_{j,k}(z).

We also use

\frac{1}{\widehat{\sigma}_{\alpha}(z)}=\ell_{\alpha}(z)+\widehat{\tau}_{\alpha}(z).

On some occasions, we write
\langle\sigma_{\alpha},\sigma_{\beta}\widehat{\rangle}
in place of
\widehat{s}_{\alpha,\beta}
. In
[
10
, Lemma 2.10]

Theorem 2.5

So, the fraction
a_{\vec{n},j}/a_{\vec{n},m}
is the
|\vec{n}|
-th diagonal multipoint Padé approximation of
\widehat{s}_{m,j+1}
. From the theory of diagonal multipoint Padé approximation (or simply using (
2.1
)) we know that
a_{\vec{n},m}
is the
|\vec{n}|-th
monic orthogonal polynomials with respect to the varying measure
\D s_{m,j+1}/w^{*}_{\vec{n},j}
and
a_{\vec{n},j}
is the corresponding polynomial of the second kind whose zeros interlace those of
a_{\vec{n},m}
. We are done.
∎

3.
Ratio asymptotic

Throughout this section
Q_{\vec{n},j}
,
j=1,\ldots,m,
denotes the monic polynomial whose roots coincide with the zeros of
\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n},j}
in
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Delta_{j+1}
(
\Delta_{m+1}=\emptyset
). In Lemma
2.2
, these polynomials were denoted
w_{\vec{n},j}
. From that lemma it follows that
\deg Q_{\vec{n},j}=\eta_{\vec{n},j}=n_{1}+\cdots+n_{j}
, its zeros are simple and lie in
\mathring{\Delta}_{j}
. We will show that these polynomials satisfy full orthogonality relations with respect to certain varying measures. This fact plays an important role in the study of ratio asymptotic.

3.1.
Multi-orthogonality relations

From Lemma
2.1
and (
2.2
) in Lemma
2.2
it readily follows that for
j=0,\ldots,m-1

(3.1)

\frac{\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n},j}(z)}{Q_{\vec{n},j}(z)}=\int\frac{\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n},j+1}(x)}{z-x}\frac{\D\sigma_{j+1}(x)}{Q_{\vec{n},j}(x)},

where
Q_{\vec{n},0}\equiv 1
, and

(3.2)

\int x^{\nu}\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n},j+1}(x)\frac{\D\sigma_{j+1}(x)}{Q_{\vec{n},j}(x)}=0,\qquad\nu=0,1,\ldots\eta_{\vec{n},j+1}-1.

Set

(3.3)

\mathcal{H}_{\vec{n},j}:=\frac{Q_{\vec{n},j+1}\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n},j}}{Q_{\vec{n},j}},\qquad j=0,\ldots,m-1,

where
Q_{\vec{n},0}\equiv Q_{\vec{n},m+1}\equiv 1
. Since
\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n},m}=(-1)^{m}a_{\vec{n},m}
and
a_{\vec{n},m}
is monic, we take
\mathcal{H}_{\vec{n},m}=(-1)^{m}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Consider the Nikishin system
\mathcal{N}(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{m})
. For each fixed
\vec{n}\in(\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{m})^{*}
and
j=0,\ldots,m-1

(3.4)

\int x^{\nu}Q_{\vec{n},j+1}(x)\frac{\mathcal{H}_{\vec{n},j+1}(x)\D\sigma_{j+1}(x)}{Q_{\vec{n},j}(x)Q_{\vec{n},j+2}(x)}=0,\qquad\nu=0,\ldots,\eta_{\vec{n},j+1}-1

and

(3.5)

\mathcal{H}_{\vec{n},j}(z)=\int\frac{Q_{\vec{n},j+1}^{2}(x)}{z-x}\frac{\mathcal{H}_{\vec{n},j+1}(x)\D\sigma_{j+1}(x)}{Q_{\vec{n},j}(x)Q_{\vec{n},j+2}(x)}.

Proof.

Formula (
3.4
) is (
3.2
) rewritten with the new notation. Since
\deg Q_{\vec{n},j+1}=\eta_{\vec{n},j+1}
, (
3.4
) implies that

\int\frac{Q_{\vec{n},j+1}(z)-Q_{\vec{n},j+1}(x)}{z-x}Q_{\vec{n},j+1}(x)\frac{\mathcal{H}_{\vec{n},j+1}(x)\D\sigma_{j+1}(x)}{Q_{\vec{n},j}(x)Q_{\vec{n},j+2}(x)}=0.

Consequently,

Q_{\vec{n},j+1}(z)\int\frac{Q_{\vec{n},j+1}(x)}{z-x}\frac{\mathcal{H}_{\vec{n},j+1}(x)\D\sigma_{j+1}(x)}{Q_{\vec{n},j}(x)Q_{\vec{n},j+2}(x)}=\int\frac{Q_{\vec{n},j+1}^{2}(x)}{z-x}\frac{\mathcal{H}_{\vec{n},j+1}(x)\D\sigma_{j+1}(x)}{Q_{\vec{n},j}(x)Q_{\vec{n},j+2}(x)}.

Taking into account (
3.3
) and (
3.1
) we get

\int\frac{Q_{\vec{n},j+1}(x)}{z-x}\frac{\mathcal{H}_{\vec{n},j+1}(x)\D\sigma_{j+1}(x)}{Q_{\vec{n},j}(x)Q_{\vec{n},j+2}(x)}=\int\frac{\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n},j+1}(x)}{z-x}\frac{\D\sigma_{j+1}(x)}{Q_{\vec{n},j}(x)}=\frac{\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n},j}(z)}{Q_{\vec{n},j}(z)}.

Therefore, (
3.5
) holds.
∎

Given
\vec{n}\in(\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{*})^{*}
and
l\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
, by
\vec{n}^{l}
we denote the multi-index obtained adding
1
to the
l
-th component of
\vec{n}
. In the next lemma, we prove that the zeros of the polynomials
Q_{\vec{n},j}
and
Q_{\vec{n}^{l},j}
interlace. The idea of the proof was borrowed from
[
1
, Theorem 2.1]
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Consider the Nikishin system
\mathcal{N}(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{m})
. For each
\vec{n}\in(\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{m})^{*}
and
j=1,\ldots,m
, the zeros of the forms
\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n},j}
and
\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n}^{l},j}
in
\mathring{\Delta}_{j}
interlace.

Proof.

Fix
\vec{n}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{m}
and
j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
. Let
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
be such that
\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2}\neq 0
. Define the linear form

\mathcal{D}_{\vec{n},j}:=\alpha\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n},j}+\beta\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n}^{l},j}.

Repeating the arguments in the proof of Lemma
2.2
we deduce that the form
\mathcal{D}_{\vec{n},j}
has at least
\eta_{\vec{n},j}
sign changes in
\mathring{\Delta}_{j}
, and at most
\eta_{\vec{n},j}+1
zeros in
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Delta_{j+1}
(
\Delta_{m+1}=\emptyset
). Consequently, all the zeros of
\mathcal{D}_{\vec{n},j}
in
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Delta_{j+1}
are real and simple.

From this assertion, we deduce that the forms
\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n},j}
and
\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n}^{l},j}
cannot have common zeros. If such a point
y
exists, the function

\mathcal{D}_{\vec{n},j}(x)=\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n},j}(x)-\frac{\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n},j}^{\prime}(y)}{\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n}^{l},j}^{\prime}(y)}\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n}^{l},j}(x)

would have a double zero at
y
. But this last statement contradicts what we already know.

Fix
y\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\Delta_{j+1}
, and consider the form

\mathcal{D}_{\vec{n},j,y}(x)=\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n}^{l},j}(y)\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n},j}(x)-\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n},j}(y)\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n}^{l},j}(x).

By construction
\mathcal{D}_{n,j,y}(y)=0
, and thus
\mathcal{D}_{n,j,y}^{\prime}(y)\neq 0
. Take two consecutive zeros
y_{1},y_{2}
of
\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n}^{l},j}
in
\mathbb{R}\setminus\Delta_{j+1}
and suppose that
y_{1}<y_{2}
. The zeros of
\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n}^{l},j}
are simple; therefore,
\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n}^{l},j}^{\prime}(y_{1})\neq 0
and
\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n}^{l},j}^{\prime}(y_{2})\neq 0
. Since
\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n}^{l},j}
and
\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n},j}
have no common zero, we also get that
\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n},j}(y_{1})\neq 0
and
\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n},j}(y_{2})\neq 0
. Thus,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\vec{n},j,y_{1}}^{\prime}(y_{1})

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n},j}(y_{1})\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n}^{l},j}^{\prime}(y_{1})\neq 0,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\vec{n},j,y_{2}}^{\prime}(y_{2})

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n},j}(y_{2})\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n}^{l},j}^{\prime}(y_{2})\neq 0.

However, the function
\mathcal{D}_{\vec{n},j,y}^{\prime}(y)
preserves the same sign all along the interval
[y_{1},y_{2}]
. Notice that
\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n}^{l},j}^{\prime}(y)
changes sign when
y
moves from
y_{1}
to
y_{2}
, so
\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n},j}
must also change sign. By Bolzano’s theorem
\mathcal{A}_{\vec{n},j}
has a zero in
(y_{1},y_{2})
. The proof is complete.
∎

3.2.
The Riemann surface

The ratio asymptotic of the ML multiple orthogonal polynomials is described in terms of the branches of a conformal mapping defined on a Riemann surface associated with the geometry of the problem. In the sequel, we assume that
\Delta_{k}
is a closed bounded interval for all
k=1,\ldots,m
. Let us briefly describe the Riemann surface of interest.

Let
\mathcal{R}
denote the compact Riemann surface

\mathcal{R}=\overline{\bigcup_{k=0}^{m}\mathcal{R}_{k}}

formed by the
m+1
consecutively “glued” sheets

\mathcal{R}_{0}:=\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Delta_{1},\qquad\mathcal{R}_{k}:=\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus(\Delta_{k}\cup\Delta_{k+1}),\quad k=1,\ldots,m,\qquad\mathcal{R}_{m}:=\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Delta_{m},

where the upper and lower banks of the slits of two neighboring sheets are identified. This surface is of genus zero. For this and other notions of Riemann surfaces as well as meromorphic functions defined on them we recommend
[
14
]
.

Let
\pi:\mathcal{R}\longrightarrow\overline{\mathbb{C}}
be the canonical projection from
\mathcal{R}
to
\overline{\mathbb{C}}
and denote by
z^{(k)}
the point on
\mathcal{R}_{k}
satisfying
\pi(z^{(k)})=z
,
z\in\overline{\mathbb{C}}
. For a fixed
l\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
, let
\psi^{(l)}:\mathcal{R}\longrightarrow\overline{\mathbb{C}}
denote a conformal mapping whose divisor consists of one simple zero at the point
\infty^{(0)}\in\mathcal{R}_{0}
and one simple pole at
\infty^{(l)}\in\mathcal{R}_{l}
. This mapping exists and is uniquely determined up to a multiplicative constant. Denote the branches of
\psi^{(l)}
by

(3.6)

\psi_{k}^{(l)}(z):=\psi^{(l)}(z^{(k)}),\qquad k=0,\ldots,m,\qquad z^{(k)}\in\mathcal{R}_{k}.

From the properties of
\psi^{(l)}
, we have

(3.7)

\psi_{0}^{(l)}(z)=C_{1,l}/z+O(1/z^{2}),\,\,\,z\rightarrow\infty,\qquad\psi_{l}^{(l)}(z)=C_{2,l}\,z+O(1),\,\,\,z\rightarrow\infty,

where
C_{1,l}
,
C_{2,l}
are non-zero constants.

It is well known and easy to verify that the function
\prod_{k=0}^{m}\psi_{k}^{(l)}
admits an analytic continuation to the whole extended plane
\overline{\mathbb{C}}
without singularities; therefore, it is constant. Multiplying
\psi^{(l)}
if necessary by a suitable non-zero constant, we may assume that
\psi^{(l)}
satisfies the conditions

\prod_{k=0}^{m}\psi_{k}^{(l)}=C,\qquad|C|=1,\qquad C_{1,l}>0.

Let us show that with this normalization,
C
is either
+1
or
-1
.

Indeed, for a point
z^{(k)}\in\mathcal{R}_{k}
on the Riemann surface we define its conjugate
\overline{z^{(k)}}:=\overline{z}^{(k)}
. Now, let
\overline{\psi}^{(l)}:\mathcal{R}\longrightarrow\overline{\mathbb{C}}
be the function defined by
\overline{\psi}^{(l)}(\zeta):=\overline{\psi^{(l)}(\overline{\zeta})}
. It is easy to verify that
\overline{\psi}^{(l)}
is a conformal mapping of
\mathcal{R}
onto
\overline{\mathbb{C}}
with the same divisor as
\psi^{(l)}
. Therefore, there exists a constant
c
such that
\overline{\psi}^{(l)}=c\psi^{(l)}
. The corresponding branches satisfy the relations

\overline{\psi}_{k}^{(l)}(z)=\overline{\psi_{k}^{(l)}(\overline{z})}=c{\psi}_{k}^{(l)}(z),\qquad k=0,\ldots,m.

Comparing the Laurent expansions at
\infty
of
\overline{\psi_{0}^{(l)}(\overline{z})}
and
c{\psi}_{0}^{(l)}(z)
, using the fact that
C_{1,l}>0
, it follows that
c=1
. Then

{\psi}_{k}^{(l)}(z)=\overline{\psi_{k}^{(l)}(\overline{z})},\qquad k=0,\ldots,m.

This in turn implies that for each
k=0,\ldots,m,
all the coefficients, in particular the leading one, of the Laurent expansion at infinity of
{\psi}_{k}^{(l)}
are real numbers. Obviously,
C
is the product of these leading coefficients. Therefore,
C
is real, and
|C|=1
implies that
C
equals
1
or
-1
as claimed. So, we can assume in the following that

(3.8)

\prod_{k=0}^{m}\psi_{k}^{(l)}\equiv e,\qquad C_{1,l}>0,

where
e
is either
1
or
-1
. It is easy to see that conditions (
3.7
) and (
3.8
) determine
\psi^{(l)}
uniquely.

We will need the following lemma. Its proof can be found in
[
1
, Lemma 4.2]

Lemma 3.3
.

Set

(3.9)

F_{k}^{(l)}:=\prod_{\nu=k}^{m}\psi_{\nu}^{(l)}

where the algebraic functions
\psi_{\nu}^{(l)}
are defined by (
3.6
)-(
3.8
). The collection of functions
F_{k}^{(l)},k=

1,\ldots,m,
is the unique solution of the system of boundary value problems

\displaystyle\text{ 1) }F_{k}^{(l)},1/F_{k}^{(l)}\in\mathcal{H}\left(\mathbb{C}\setminus\Delta_{k}\right)

\displaystyle\text{ 2a) }F_{k}^{(l)}(\infty)>0,\quad k=1,\ldots,l-1

\displaystyle\text{ 2b) }\left(F_{k}^{(l)}\right)^{\prime}(\infty)>0,\quad k=l,\ldots,m

\displaystyle\text{ 3) }\left|F_{k}^{(l)}(x)\right|^{2}\frac{1}{\left|\left(F_{k-1}^{(l)}F_{k+1}^{(l)}\right)(x)\right|}=1,\quad x\in\Delta_{k}

where
F_{0}^{(l)}\equiv F_{m+1}^{(l)}\equiv 1
.

3.3.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

Theorem
1.2
will be derived from Theorem

and Theorem

below which gives the ratio asymptotic of the polynomials

[

for the study of the ratio asymptotic of type

Given an arbitrary function

which has in a neighborhood of infinity a Laurent expansion of the form

and

Theorem 3.4
.

Consider the Nikishin system
\mathcal{N}(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{m})
where the intervals
\Delta_{k}

are bounded and
\sigma_{k}^{\prime}\neq 0
a.e. in
\Delta_{k}

\Lambda\subset(\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{m})^{*}

(3.10)

\lim_{\vec{n}\in\Lambda}\frac{Q_{\vec{n}^{l},k}(z)}{Q_{\vec{n},k}(z)}=\widetilde{F}^{(l)}_{k}(z),

uniformly on each compact subset of
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Delta_{k}
.

Proof of Theorems
3.4
and
1.2
.
From Lemma

and

\lim_{\vec{n}\in\Lambda^{\prime}}\frac{Q_{\vec{n}^{l},k}(z)}{Q_{\vec{n},k}(z)}=G_{k}(z),\qquad k=1,\ldots,m,

First, it is obvious that the functions

3.3

On the other hand, considering the degrees of the polynomials

and

) at infinity are either equal to 1 when

or

,

or

Define the constants

(3.12)

\displaystyle K_{\vec{n},k-1}:=

\displaystyle\left(\int Q_{\vec{n},k}^{2}(x)\frac{|\mathcal{H}_{\vec{n},k}(x)||\D\sigma_{k}(x)|}{|Q_{\vec{n},k-1}(x)Q_{\vec{n},k+1}(x)|}\right)^{-1/2},\quad k=1,\ldots,m,

\displaystyle 1,

Set

(3.13)

q_{\vec{n},k}:=\kappa_{\vec{n},k}Q_{\vec{n},k},\quad h_{\vec{n},k}:=K_{\vec{n},k}^{2}\mathcal{H}_{\vec{n},k},\quad k=1,\ldots,m,\quad h_{\vec{n},0}:=K_{\vec{n},0}^{2}\mathcal{H}_{\vec{n},0}.

), we have

(3.20)

\lim_{\vec{n}\in\Lambda^{\prime}}\frac{|h_{\vec{n}^{l},k(x)}|}{|h_{\vec{n},k}(x)|}\frac{|Q_{\vec{n},k-1}(x)Q_{\vec{n},k+1}(x)|}{|Q_{\vec{n}^{l},k-1}(x)Q_{\vec{n}^{l},k+1}(x)|}=\frac{1}{|G_{k-1}(x)G_{k+1}(x)|},\qquad k=1,\ldots,m,

uniformly on the interval
\Delta_{k}
. The function on the right hand side of the previous expression is different from zero on
\Delta_{k}
.

Fix
k=1,\ldots,m
. We distinguish two cases. If
k=1,\ldots,l-1\,(l\geq 2)
, then
\deg Q_{\vec{n}^{l},k}=\deg Q_{\vec{n},k}=\eta_{\vec{n},k}
. Using (
3.14
) and (
3.20
), the result on relative asymptotic of orthogonal polynomials with respect to varying measures which appears in
[
2
, Theorem 2]
implies that

(3.21)

\lim_{\vec{n}\in\Lambda^{\prime}}\frac{Q_{\vec{n}^{l},k}(z)}{Q_{\vec{n},k}(z)}=G_{k}(z)=\frac{\mathsf{S}_{k}(z)}{\mathsf{S}_{k}(\infty)},\qquad k=1,\ldots,l-1,

where
\mathsf{S}_{k}
is the Szegő function on
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Delta_{k}
with respect to the weight function

|G_{k-1}(z)G_{k+1}(z)|^{-1},\qquad x\in\Delta_{k}.

Consequently,

(3.22)

|\mathsf{S}_{k}(x)|^{2}|G_{k-1}(z)G_{k+1}(z)|^{-1}=1,\qquad x\in\Delta_{k}.

and for
x\in\Delta_{k}

(3.23)

\frac{|G_{k}(x)|^{2}}{|G_{k-1}(x)G_{k+1}(x)|}=\frac{1}{\mathsf{S}^{2}_{k}(\infty)},\qquad k=1,\ldots,l-1.

Now, if
k=l,\ldots,m,
then
\deg Q_{\vec{n}^{l},k}=\deg Q_{\vec{n},k}+1=\eta_{\vec{n},k}+1
.
Let
Q_{\vec{n},k}^{*}
be the
\eta_{\vec{n},k}
-th monic orthogonal polynomial with respect to the varying measure (
3.14
). Take

\frac{Q_{\vec{n}^{l},k}}{Q_{\vec{n},k}}=\frac{Q_{\vec{n}^{l},k}}{Q_{\vec{n},k}^{*}}\frac{Q_{\vec{n},k}^{*}}{Q_{\vec{n},k}}.

For the second factor, reasoning as above, we get

(3.24)

\lim_{\vec{n}\in\Lambda^{\prime}}\frac{Q^{*}_{\vec{n},k}(z)}{Q_{\vec{n},k}(z)}=\frac{\mathsf{S}_{k}(z)}{\mathsf{S}_{k}(\infty)},

where
\mathsf{S}_{k}
is the same Szegő function we had before.
In the first factor, we have the ratio of two monic polynomials of consecutive degrees orthogonal with respect to the same varying measure and with the help of the theorem on ratio asymptotic of orthogonal polynomials with respect to varying measures
[
8
, Theorem 6]
we deduce

(3.25)

\lim_{\vec{n}\in\Lambda^{\prime}}\frac{Q_{\vec{n}^{l},k}}{Q_{\vec{n},k}^{*}}(z)=\frac{\varphi_{k}(z)}{\varphi_{k}^{\prime}(\infty)},

uniformly on compact subsets of
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Delta_{j}
, where
\varphi_{k}
is the conformal representation of
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Delta_{k}
onto the exterior of the unit disc such that
\varphi_{k}(\infty)=\infty
and
\varphi_{k}^{\prime}(\infty)>0
.
Combining (
3.11
) with (
3.25
) and (
3.24
) we have

(3.26)

\lim_{\vec{n}\in\Lambda^{\prime}}\frac{Q_{\vec{n}^{l},k}(z)}{Q_{\vec{n},k}(z)}=G_{k}(z)=\frac{\mathsf{S}_{k}(z)\varphi_{k}(z)}{\mathsf{S}_{k}(\infty)\varphi_{k}^{\prime}(\infty)},\qquad k=l,\ldots,m,

and using (
3.22
) it follows that for
x\in\Delta_{k}
,

(3.27)

\frac{|G_{k}(x)|^{2}}{|G_{k-1}(x)G_{k+1}(x)|}=\frac{1}{(\mathsf{S}_{k}(\infty)\varphi_{k}^{\prime}(\infty))^{2}},\quad k=l,\ldots,m.

Putting together (
3.23
) and (
3.27
) we have proved that the collection of functions
(G_{k})_{k=1}^{m}
satisfies the conditions of Lemma
3.3
, where the right hand side of 3) is
1/w_{k}

(3.28)

w_{k}=\begin{array}[]{ll}(\mathsf{S}_{k}(\infty))^{2},&k=1,\ldots,l-1,\\
(\mathsf{S}_{k}(\infty)\varphi_{k}^{\prime}(\infty))^{2},&k=l,\ldots,m,\end{array}

(instead of
1
).

Let
\widetilde{G}_{k}=c_{k}G_{k},
where
c_{k},k=1,\ldots,m,
are constants chosen appropriately so that

\frac{c_{k}^{2}}{w_{k}c_{k-1}c_{k+1}}=1,\qquad k=1,\ldots,m\qquad(c_{0}=c_{m+1}=1).

Such constants exist. Indeed, taking logarithm we obtain the linear system of equations (in
\ln c_{k}
)

(3.29)

2\ln c_{k}-\ln c_{k-1}-\ln c_{k+1}=\ln w_{k},\qquad k=1,\ldots,m,

which has a solution because the determinant of the system is different from zero. It is easy to verify that the collection of functions
(\widetilde{G}_{k})_{k=1}^{m}
satisfies all the conditions of Lemma
3.3
. Since that system of boundary value problems has only one solution, it follows that

\widetilde{G}_{k}=c_{k}G_{k}={F}_{k}^{(l)},\qquad k=1,\ldots,m.

Now,
G_{k}(\infty)=1
when
k=1,\ldots,l-1\,(l\geq 2)
, and
G_{k}^{\prime}(\infty)=1
when
k=l,\ldots,m
; therefore, taking limit as
z\to\infty
it follows that

(3.30)

c_{k}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{F}_{k}^{(l)}(\infty),&k=1,\ldots,l-1,\\
({F}_{k}^{(l)})^{\prime}(\infty),&k=l,\ldots,m.\end{array}\right.

In any case, we have shown that independent of the subsequence
\Lambda^{\prime}\subset\Lambda
taken such that (
3.11
) takes place the limiting functions are

G_{k}=\widetilde{F}_{k}^{(l)},\qquad k=1,\ldots,m,

and (
3.10
) follows. With this we conclude the proof of Theorem
3.4
.

Since
a_{\vec{n},m}=Q_{\vec{n},m}
for all
\vec{n}
, (
1.3
) is a direct consequence of (
3.10
) and (
3.9
) when
k=m
. Now, fix
k\in\{0,\ldots,m-1\}
and
\varepsilon>0
. Consider the positively oriented closed curve
\Gamma
which surrounds
\Delta_{m}
at distance
\varepsilon
. From (
1.2
) and the argument principle it follows that

\lim_{\vec{n}\in\Lambda}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma}\frac{(a_{\vec{n},k}/a_{\vec{n},m})^{\prime}(\zeta)}{(a_{\vec{n},k}/a_{\vec{n},m})(\zeta)}\D\zeta=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma}\frac{\widehat{s}_{m,k+1}^{\prime}(\zeta)}{\widehat{s}_{m,k+1}(\zeta)}\D\zeta=1

because
\widehat{s}_{m,k+1}
has a simple zero at
\infty
and no other zero or pole in all
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Delta_{m}
. The integrals on the left hand side only take integer values so they must be constantly equal to
1
for all
\vec{n}\in\Lambda
such that
|\vec{n}|
is sufficiently large. Now,
\deg a_{\vec{n},m}=|\vec{n}|
and its zeros lie on
\Delta_{m}
and

with

sufficiently large,

and
a_{\vec{n},k}
has no zeros in the unbounded connected component of
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Gamma

is arbitrary, we also obtain that the zeros of

.

Now, using (
1.2
) and (

) (for
k=m

\lim_{\vec{n}\in\Lambda}\frac{a_{\vec{n}^{l},k}(z)}{a_{\vec{n},k}(z)}=\lim_{\vec{n}\in\Lambda}\frac{a_{\vec{n}^{l},k}(z)}{a_{\vec{n}^{l},m}(z)}\frac{a_{\vec{n},m}(z)}{a_{\vec{n},k}(z)}\frac{a_{\vec{n}^{l},m}(z)}{a_{\vec{n},m}(z)}=\frac{{\psi}^{(l)}_{m}(z)}{({\psi}^{(l)}_{m})^{\prime}(\infty)},

\displaystyle\lim_{\vec{n}\in\Lambda}\frac{\kappa_{\vec{n}^{l},k}}{\kappa_{\vec{n},k}}=
