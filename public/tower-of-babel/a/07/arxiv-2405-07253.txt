Sharp estimates for the Cramér transform of log-concave measures and geometric applications
Silouanos Brazitikos, Giorgos Chasapis

Abstract
. We establish a new comparison between the Legendre transform of the cumulant generating function and the half-space depth of an arbitrary log-concave probability distribution on the real line, that carries on to the multidimensional setting. Combined with sharp estimates for the Cramér transform of rotationally invariant measures, we are led to some new phase-transition type results for the asymptotics of the expected measure of random polytopes. As a byproduct of our analysis, we address a question on the sharp exponential separability constant for log-concave distributions, in the symmetric case.

Primary 60D05; Secondary 60E15, 52A22, 52A23.

Key words. log-concave probability measures, Cramér transform,half-space depth, random polytopes, convex bodies

1.
Introduction

In the present work we are concerned with a study of the Cramér transform of log-concave probability distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, particularly in connection with the so-called notion of half-space depth and aiming towards an improved understanding of asymptotic geometric properties of random structures in high-dimensional spaces.

Given a probability measure
\mu
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, Tukey’s half-space depth is defined
for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
by

q_{\mu}(x)=\inf\{\mu(H):H\in{\mathcal{H}}(x)\},

where
{\mathcal{H}}(x)
is the set of all closed half-spaces
H
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
containing
x
. Such a functional can be considered as a measure of centrality or outlyingness for multivariate data points, quantifying the “depth” of a point
x
with respect to a set of data by considering the fraction of half-spaces containing
x
. The first work in statistics where some form of the half-space depth appears
is an article of Hodges
[
38
]
from 1955. Tukey introduced the half-space depth for data sets in
[
54
]
as a
tool that enables efficient visualization of random samples in the plane. The term “depth” also comes
from Tukey’s article. A formal definition of the half-space depth was given by
Donoho and Gasko in
[
24
]
(see also
[
53
]
). Quite expectedly, depth functionals emerge also in a more geometric context as measures of symmetry or moduli for the approximation of convex bodies by polytopes. We refer the reader to the survey article of Nagy, Schütt and Werner

[
47
]
for an extensive treatise on data depth functionals, their connections to convex geometry and more, as well as many references.

By the Cramér transform of a probability distribution
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
on the other hand, we refer to the functional defined by

\Lambda^{\ast}_{\mu}(x)=\sup_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\langle x,\xi\rangle-\log\mathbb{E}_{\mu}e^{\langle X,\xi\rangle}\right),

that is, the Legendre transform of the cumulant generating function (or Log-Laplace transform) of
\mu
. The terminology stems from the pivotal role that this quantity plays in Cramér’s theorem within the theory of Large Deviations
[
22
]
. In the context of Asymptotic Geometric Analysis, the cumulant generating function arises naturally as a central object in the study of high-dimensional convex bodies and log-concave measures (see for example
[
42
,
28
,
44
]
). Interestingly, in a framework closer to mathematical optimisation, Bubeck and Eldan
[
15
]
(see also
[
21
]
) proved that the Cramér transform of the uniform probability distribution on a convex body in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an
n
-self-concordant barrier, giving the first universal barrier for convex bodies with optimal self-concordance parameter (for more on barrier functions, interior point methods and the theory of convex optimisation, we point the interested reader to
[
48
]
).

These two quantities are intimately connected; since

q_{\mu}(x)=\inf_{\xi\in S^{n-1}}\mathbb{P}_{\mu}(\langle X,\xi\rangle\geqslant\langle x,\xi\rangle),

we have, using Markov’s inequality, that

q_{\mu}(x)\leqslant\exp(-(\langle x,\xi\rangle-\log\mathbb{E}_{\mu}e^{\langle X,\xi\rangle}))

for every
\xi\in S^{n-1}
. Taking infimum over
\xi
, it follows that the inequality
q_{\mu}(x)\leqslant e^{-\Lambda_{\mu}^{\ast}(x)}
, or equivalenty,

(1)

\Lambda_{\mu}^{\ast}(x)\leqslant\log\frac{1}{q_{\mu}(x)}

holds for every
x
and any probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Exploring the precision of this upper estimate has been crucial in many instances for problems regarding the geometry of high-dimensional random sets. It was probably first noted by Dyer, Füredi and McDiarmid in
[
26
]
that, for sufficiently large
n
, (
1
) can be essentially reversed when one takes the measure
\mu
to be the uniform probability distribution on the vertices (or interior) of the
n
-dimensional cube
[-1,1]^{n}
, an observation that came to be crucial also in the work of Bárány and Pór
[
3
]
on the expected facet number of 0/1 polytopes. Subsequently, Gatzouras and Giannopoulos
[
32
]
(see also
[
31
]
, as well as
[
33
]
,
[
34
]
) carefully analysed the argument of
[
26
]
to obtain a similar result for more general even product probability measures
\mu_{n}=\mu^{\otimes n}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with compact support that satisfy a certain regularity assumption, namely that

(2)

\lim_{x\to x^{\ast}}\frac{-\ln\mu([x,\infty))}{\Lambda_{\mu}^{\ast}(x)}=1,

where
x^{\ast}
is the right endpoint of the support of
\mu
(some instances of the same behaviour for product measures with no compact support were recently studied in
[
49
]
).

Quite recently, a reasonable reversal of (
1
) was established in
[
13
]
in a fairly general setting; the inequality

(3)

\Lambda^{\ast}_{\mu}(x)\geqslant\log\frac{1}{q_{\mu}(x)}-5\sqrt{n}

is true for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
if
\mu
is the uniform probability distribution on any centered convex body of volume 1 in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(as is explained in
[
13
]
, the
O(\sqrt{n})
-error term is fairly good for the sake of the applications considered by the authors). One of our main results is the following lower estimate on
\Lambda_{\mu}^{\ast}
for any log-concave probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\mu
be a log-concave probability on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,

(4)

\Lambda_{\mu}^{\ast}(x)\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)\log\frac{1}{q_{\mu}(x)}+\log\frac{\varepsilon}{2^{1-\varepsilon}}

holds for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Optimising over
\varepsilon>0
, we can see that the right hand side of (
4
) is maximised when
\varepsilon=(-\log(2q_{\mu}(x)))^{-1}
. This leads to the lower estimate

\Lambda^{\ast}_{\mu}(x)\geqslant\log\frac{1}{2q_{\mu}(x)\log\frac{1}{2q_{\mu}(x)}}-1,

for every
x
with
q_{\mu}(x)<1/(2e)
.

A key ingredient for the proof of Theorem
1.1
is a sharp inequality for the negative moments of
q_{\mu}(X)
, for any real random variable
X
with distribution
\mu
(see Proposition
3.2
). Then, fundamental convexity considerations lead us to the 1-dimensional version of (
4
), which can be extended to higher dimensions thanks to the log-concavity of marginal distributions of
\mu
.

A similar argument to that used for the proof of Theorem
1.1
lets us provide a characterisation of the measures
\mu
on the real line that satisfy (
2
), under no symmetry or compactness assumption.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\mu
be probability measure on
\mathbb{R}
and let
x^{\ast}\in(-\infty,+\infty]
denote the right endpoint of
\mathrm{supp}(\mu)
. Then,

\lim_{x\to x^{\ast}}\frac{-\log\mu([x,\infty))}{\Lambda_{\mu}^{\ast}(x)}=1

holds if and only if there exists a convex function
V(x)
such that
\lim_{x\to x^{\ast}}\frac{-\log\mu([x,\infty))}{V(x)}=1
.

Next, we focus on radially symmetric probability distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We determine the radially symmetric distributions that maximise/minimise the Cramér transform functional pointwise in the log-concave setting under an isotropicity assumption, and establish sharp bounds for the order of growth of
\mathbb{E}\Lambda^{\ast}_{\mu}
and
\mathbb{E}e^{-\Lambda^{\ast}_{\mu}}
for certain classes of rotationally invariant log-concave probability measures. Our methods combine an analysis of the cumulant generating function of the marginals of the spherical distribution, majorisation-type arguments and log-concavity considerations that let in for a fruitful comparison of the underlying densities. One particular outcome of our results is the following.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
X=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
be a random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with independent and symmetric log-concave components. Then
\mathbb{E}e^{-\Lambda_{X}^{\ast}}\leqslant c_{\mathrm{exp}}^{n}
for
c_{\mathrm{exp}}=\mathbb{E}e^{-\Lambda_{Y}^{\ast}}
, where
Y
is a standard double exponential random variable, i.e. with density
\frac{1}{2}e^{-\frac{|x|}{2}}
.

Theorem
1.3
provides a partial positive answer, in the symmetric case, to a question raised in
[
36
]
on the exponential one-shot separability constant of log-concave distributions. In connection with stochastic separability and applications to machine learning and
error-correction mechanisms in artificial intelligence systems, the authors in
[
36
]
considered the quantity
\mathbb{E}q_{\mu}
as a “measure of separability” of the distribution
\mu
, and have pursued the understanding of cases of distributions for which
\mathbb{E}q_{\mu}
decreases exponentially fast with the dimension (the definition of “one-shot separability” is stated in
[
36
]
only for product distributions, but one could as well consider the general case). It is suggested in the same work that the constant
c_{\mathrm{exp}}
above provides the sharp upper bound in general, for log-concave product distributions. We actually conjecture that the result of Theorem
1.3
provides the correct upper bound even in the high-dimensional case (for not-necessarily product measures), at least in the rotationally-invariant setting. Relative to the case of product distributions, the general upper bound
1-2\cdot 10^{-5}
for
\mathbb{E}e^{-\Lambda^{\ast}_{\mu}}
, where
\mu
is any log-concave probability measure on
\mathbb{R}
, has been announced in
[
36
, Proposition 3]
. However, the referenced manuscript has not yet come to our attention. Theorem
1.1
lets us also provide an improved, although still suboptimal, general estimate.

Proposition 1.4
.

For every log-concave random variable
X
we have
\mathbb{E}e^{-\Lambda_{X}^{\ast}(X)}\leqslant 1-(4e)^{-1}
.

The proof of Proposition
1.4
is provided in Section
5.1
.

The starting point and main motivation for the results in this work have been questions on the geometry of high-dimensional random sets, in particular phase transition-type phenomena for the asymptotics of the expected measure of randomly generated convex hulls in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Following a long line of research
[
26
,
32
,
51
,
7
,
49
]
that we briefly recall in the next section, as well as the recent treatment of the problem in the general setting of log-concave probability measures
[
13
]
, we are led to a natural conjecture that we are now able to support in some new cases, including that of general product probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and several rotationally invariant distributions in high-dimensions.

The rest of the paper is organised as follows: In Section
2
we have gathered some preliminaries and further develop the background related to our considered applications. The proofs of Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
are presented in Section
3
. Results regarding the Crámer transform of rotationally invariant distributions are collected in Section
4
. Finally, in Section
5
we record some implications of our results in various settings for the expected measure-threshold problem.

Throughout the text we will use standard big-O notation. In particular,
f(x)=\omega(g(x))
stands for
\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)/g(x)=+\infty
and
f(x)\sim g(x)
stands for
\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)/g(x)=1
.

2.
Preliminaries and Background

2.1.
Log-concave probability measures

We denote by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
the standard Euclidean inner product in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and by
|\cdot|
the Euclidean norm in the respective dimension. The
n
-dimensional closed Euclidean unit ball is denoted by
B_{2}^{n}
and
S^{n-1}
is its boundary, the unit sphere. It is known that
S^{n-1}
is equipped with a unique rotationally invariant probability measure, which we usually denote by
\sigma
(surpassing explicit reference to the dimension
n
). The
n
-dimensional Lebesgue measure of a measurable set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
will be denoted by
\vol_{n}(A)
.

We say that a subset
K
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a convex body if it is compact and convex, with a nonempty interior. A convex body
K
is (centrally) symmetric if
K=-K
and centered if its barycenter
{\rm bar}(K)=\vol_{n}(K)^{-1}\int_{K}x\,dx
is the origin. A Borel measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called log-concave if
\mu((1-\lambda)A+\lambda B)\geqslant\mu(A)^{1-\lambda}\mu(B)^{\lambda}
for any pair of measurable
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\lambda\in(0,1)
. We say that a random vector
X
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is log-concave if it is distributed according to a log-concave probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. It is known (see
[
10
]
) that any log-concave probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
which is not concentrated on a hyperplane (that is,
\mu(H)<1
for every hyperplane
H
) has a log-concave density
f_{\mu}
. We will mainly focus on this non-degenerate setting. A standard example of a log-concave probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is that of the uniform measure on a convex body
K
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, which we might denote by
\mu_{K}
. Clearly then
f_{\mu_{K}}=\vol_{n}(K)^{-1}\mathds{1}_{K}
(which is log-concave, by the convexity of
K
). Another common example is the
n
-dimensional Gaussian measure, which we denote by
\gamma_{n}
and has density
f(x)=(2\pi)^{-\frac{n}{2}}e^{-\frac{|x|^{2}}{2}}
.

We say that a convex body
K
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is isotropic if it is centered,
\vol_{n}(K)=1
, and there is a constant
L_{K}>0
such that

\left(\int_{K}\langle x,\theta\rangle^{2}\,dx\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}=L_{K}

for every
\theta\in S^{n-1}
. More generally, we say that a log-concave probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is isotropic if

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\langle x,\theta\rangle\,d\mu(x)=0\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\langle x,\theta\rangle^{2}\,d\mu(x)=1,

both hold for all
\theta\in S^{n-1}
. Note that, under this normalisation, a convex body
K
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is isotropic if and only if the probability measure with density
f(x)=L_{K}^{n}\mathds{1}_{L_{K}^{-1}K}
is isotropic. It is known that every log-concave measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
can be pushed forward to an isotropic measure by an affine transformation. The isotropic constant of a log-concave probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with density
f_{\mu}
is defined by

L_{\mu}:=\left(\mathrm{det}(\mathrm{Cov}(\mu))\|f_{\mu}\|_{\infty}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2n}},

where
\mathrm{Cov}(\mu)
is the covariance matrix of
\mu
. Note that if
\mu
is isotropic then
\mathrm{Cov}(\mu)
is the identity matrix. The celebrated isotropic constant conjecture (equivalent to the hyperplane conjecture posed by Bourgain
[
11
]
) asking if there exists an absolute constant
C>0
such that
L_{n}:=\sup L_{\mu}\leqslant C
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
(where the supremum is taken over all isotropic log-concave probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
), had been a central open problem for more than 30 years in the field of Asymptotic Geometric Analysis. It was not only until very recently (while the present paper was undergoing the peer review process) that this question was settled to the affirmative by Klartag-Lehec
[
43
]
, following a crucial observation by Guan
[
37
]
after several other recent breakthrough advances. We refer the interested reader to
[
14
]
for details on the rich history of this problem, its connection with other major open questions in the field, as well as an extensive study of the theory of log-concave measures.

2.2.
Threshold for the expected measure of random sets

For any dimension
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
N>n
and
X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}
be independent random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with a common distribution
\mu_{n}
. We denote by
K_{N}:=\conv\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}\}
, the convex hull of
X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}
. Our main object of study is the behaviour of the sequence
a_{n}=\mathbb{E}\mu_{n}(K_{N})
when
n
tends to infinity. Here, expectation is taken with repsect to the product distribution
\mu_{n}^{\otimes N}
(in a more general setting, one could even take the points
X_{i}
distributed according to different measures
\mu_{n}^{i}
). Note that the quantity
\mathbb{E}\mu_{n}(K_{N})
is invariant under affine transformations of
\mu_{n}
, so we may in general assume that
\mu_{n}
is centered.

We will see that usually the asymptotics of
a_{n}
will depend on the magnitude of the number of random points
N
chosen, with respect to the dimension
n
. To make the notion of a “sharp threshold” precise, we rely on the following definitions, essentially first considered in
[
13
]
. Given
\delta\in(0,1)
and a probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we let

\varrho_{1}(\mu,\delta):=\sup\{r>0:\mathbb{E}\mu(K_{N})\leqslant\delta,\hbox{ for every }N\leqslant\exp(r)\},

and

\varrho_{2}(\mu,\delta):=\inf\{r>0:\mathbb{E}\mu(K_{N})\geqslant 1-\delta,\hbox{ for every }N\geqslant\exp(r)\}.

Let also

\varrho(\mu,\delta):=\varrho_{2}(\mu,\delta)-\varrho_{1}(\mu,\delta).

With this notation at hand, we give the following definition.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
(\mu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of probability measures
\mu_{n}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
(\mathcal{T}_{n})_{n}
a sequence of positive numbers. We say that
(\mu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
exhibits a sharp threshold around
\mathcal{T}_{n}
if for every
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
there is
n_{0}=n_{0}(\varepsilon)\in\mathbb{N}
such that for every
n\geqslant n_{0}
,

\varrho_{1}(\mu_{n},\delta_{n})\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)\mathcal{T}_{n}\qquad\hbox{ and }\qquad\varrho_{2}(\mu_{n},\delta_{n})\leqslant(1+\varepsilon)\mathcal{T}_{n},

for some sequence
(\delta_{n})
with
\lim_{n\to\infty}\delta_{n}=0
.

Clearly, if
(\mu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
exhibits a sharp threshold around
\mathcal{T}_{n}
under the above definition, then it is the case that for every fixed
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, any choice
N\leqslant e^{(1-\varepsilon)\mathcal{T}_{n}}
will imply that
\mathbb{E}\mu_{n}(K_{N})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
and respectively
\mathbb{E}\mu_{n}(K_{N})\to 1
as
n\to\infty
if
N\geqslant e^{(1+\varepsilon)\mathcal{T}_{n}}
. That is, the expected measure of
K_{N}
exhibits an asymptotic phase transition from 0 to 1 around
N=e^{\mathcal{T}_{n}}
, with a “threshold window” of the order of
\mathcal{T}_{n}^{-1}\varrho(\mu_{n},\delta_{n})\leqslant 2\varepsilon
.

Using this terminology, the authors in
[
13
]
put into a common perspective a number of related older results. They also studied extensively the quantities
\varrho_{1}
and
\varrho_{2}
under a log-concavity assumption. In the following subsection we elaborate further on the history of the problem and recall several achievements and natural conjectures.

2.3.
Half-space depth and the Cramér transform

Recall the notion of Tukey’s half-space depth defined for any probability measure
\mu
and point
x
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
by

q_{\mu}(x)=\inf\{\mu(H):H\hbox{ is a closed half-space containing }x\}.

In the sequel, given a random vector
X
distributed according to
\mu
we might use either the notation
q_{\mu}
or
q_{X}
for the half-space depth of
\mu
(or even relax this to
q
, if the underlying distribution is obvious from the context). By definition, in dimension 1 we have
q_{\mu}(x)=\min\{\mu((-\infty,x]),\mu([x,\infty))\}
for every
x\in\mathbb{R}
. In general, in any dimension
n
, note that since any half-space
H
that contains
x
is a set of the form
\{y:\langle y-x,\xi\rangle\geqslant 0\}
for some
\xi\in S^{n-1}
,
q_{\mu}(x)
can be realised as

q_{\mu}(x)=\inf_{\xi\in S^{n-1}}\mathbb{P}_{\mu}(\langle X,\xi\rangle\geqslant\langle x,\xi\rangle).

It is then easy to see that
q_{Y}(ax+b)=q_{X}(x)
for any
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
whenever
Y
is equidistributed to
aX+b
and, as a consequence, if
\mu
is the distribution of
X
and
\nu
is the distribution of
aX+b
then

\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\Phi(q_{\nu}(Y))=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\Phi(q_{\nu}(aX+b))=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\Phi(q_{\mu}(X))

holds for any function
\Phi:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
, that is, the quantity
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\Phi(q_{\mu})
is invariant under affine transformations of
\mu
. Another important property that we will rely on is the fact that
q_{\mu}
respects log-concavity.

Lemma 2.2
.

If
\mu
is a probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
whose marginals have log-concave tails, then the function
q_{\mu}
is log-concave. In particular if
\mu
has a log-concave density in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then
q_{\mu}
is log-concave.

Proof.

Assume that
X
is distributed according to
\mu
. Fix some arbitrary
\xi\in S^{n-1}
. By our hypothesis, for any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\lambda\in(0,1)

\mathbb{P}(\langle X,\xi\rangle\geqslant\langle(1-\lambda)x+\lambda y,\xi\rangle)\geqslant\left(\mathbb{P}(\langle X,\xi\rangle\geqslant\langle x,\xi\rangle)\right)^{1-\lambda}\left(\mathbb{P}(\langle X,\xi\rangle\geqslant\langle y,\xi\rangle)\right)^{\lambda}\geqslant q_{\mu}(x)^{1-\lambda}q_{\mu}(y)^{\lambda}.

Taking infimum over
\xi\in S^{n-1}
concludes the proof.
∎

In a seminal work, Dyer, Füredi and McDiarmid
[
26
]
were the first to show that a threshold-type behaviour like that described in Definition
2.1
holds for the expected volume of random polytopes with independent vertices uniformly distributed in the unit cube
[-1,1]^{n}
; if
X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}
are as such and
K_{N}=\conv\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}\}
, then there is an absolute constant
\kappa>0
such that for every
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,

(5)

\lim_{n\to\infty}2^{-n}\mathbb{E}\vol_{n}(K_{N})=\begin{cases}1,&\hbox{ if }N\geqslant(\kappa+\varepsilon)^{n}\\
0,&\hbox{ if }N\leqslant(\kappa-\varepsilon)^{n}\end{cases}

(a similar result holds also in the case that the random points are chosen uniformly from the vertices of the cube).
The proof in
[
26
]
relies on an ingenious blend of probabilistic and geometric arguments, manifested in the following central lemma.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
K_{N}
be the convex hull of
N
random vectors independently chosen with respect to a common probability distribution
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then for every Borel subset
A
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

(6)

\mathbb{E}\mu(K_{N})\leqslant\mu(A)+N\cdot\sup_{x\in A^{c}}q_{\mu}(x).

If additionally
\mu
assigns zero mass to every hyperplane in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then

(7)

\mathbb{E}\mu(K_{N})\geqslant\mu(A)\left(1-2\binom{N}{n}\left(1-\inf_{x\in A}q_{\mu}(x)\right)^{N-n}\right).

It is noteworthy that the proof of Lemma
2.3
does not rely on particular properties of the underlying distribution
\mu
, turning this into a versatile tool that has been the starting point in most, if not all, subsequent works establishing similar threshold-type phenomena.

The exact value of the constant
\kappa
in (
5
) is
\kappa=2\pi e^{-\gamma-\frac{1}{2}}
, where
\gamma
is Euler’s constant. One can see that this is exactly
\mathbb{E}\Lambda^{\ast}_{U}(U)
, where
U
is uniform in
[-1,1]
. Let us recall some basic facts on the log-Laplace and Cramér transforms of log-concave probability distributions. We denote by
\Lambda_{\mu}
the cumulant generating function of a probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, that is

\Lambda_{\mu}(\xi)=\log\mathbb{E}e^{\langle X,\xi\rangle},

for every
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
X
is distributed according to
\mu
(again, in this case we will feel free to interchange between the notation
\Lambda_{\mu}
and
\Lambda_{X}
). Note that
\Lambda_{\mu}
is a convex function on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(this is a direct consequence of Hölder’s inequality), which is equal to 0 at the origin. Moreover,
\Lambda_{\mu}\geqslant 0
holds if
\mu
is centered, by Jensen’s inequality. The Cramér transform of
\mu
is defined as the Legendre–Fenchel transform of
\Lambda_{\mu}
, that is,

(8)

\Lambda^{\ast}_{\mu}(x)=\sup_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\langle x,\xi\rangle-\Lambda_{\mu}(\xi)\right).

As a Legendre transform,
\Lambda_{\mu}^{\ast}
is a non-negative convex and lower semicontinuous function on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In the case of one-dimensional symmetric distributions, which will be of interest to us,
\Lambda_{\mu}
and
\Lambda_{\mu}^{\ast}
are even functions, which are well defined when
\Lambda_{\mu}
is finite on some neighborhood of 0. The set
\{\Lambda_{\mu}<\infty\}
is in that case a symmetric open interval
(-t^{\ast},t^{\ast})
for some
t^{\ast}\in(0,\infty]
, and
\Lambda_{\mu}
is
C^{\infty}
on
(-t^{\ast},t^{\ast})
. If moreover
\mu
is log-concave and
\mathrm{supp}(\mu)=(-x^{\ast},x^{\ast})
,
x^{\ast}\in(0,\infty]
, then
\Lambda_{\mu}
is differentiable, with
\Lambda^{\prime}_{\mu}
strictly increasing on
(-t^{\ast},t^{\ast})
and onto
(-x^{\ast},x^{\ast})
, in particular

\lim_{t\to\pm t^{\ast}}\Lambda_{\mu}^{\prime}(t)=\pm x^{\ast}

(see
[
49
, Lemma 2.3]
for a proof of these claims). The Legendre transform
\Lambda^{\ast}_{\mu}
on the other hand is
C^{\infty}
on
\mathrm{supp}(\mu)
and satisfies
({\Lambda_{\mu}^{\ast}})^{\prime}=(\Lambda_{\mu}^{\prime})^{-1}
. Moreover, for every
x\in(-x^{\ast},x^{\ast})
,

\Lambda^{\ast}_{\mu}(x)=x\cdot t_{x}-\Lambda_{\mu}(t_{x}),

where
t_{x}=({\Lambda^{\ast}_{\mu}})^{\prime}(x)
(equivalently,
x=\Lambda_{\mu}^{\prime}(t)
). We point the reader to the monograph
[
52
]
for more on the general theory of Legendre duality as well as
[
22
,
23
]
for connections of the Cramér transform to the theory of Large Deviations.

Note finally that if
X
is a random vector with distribution
\mu
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
\nu
is the distribution of
Y=aX+b
, for any real numbers
a,b
, it is straightforward to check that
\Lambda^{\ast}_{\nu}(x)=\Lambda^{\ast}_{\mu}\left(\frac{x-b}{a}\right)
for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. It follows then that for any function
\Phi:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
,

\mathbb{E}_{\nu}(\Phi(\Lambda_{\nu}^{\ast}(Y)))=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}(\Phi(\Lambda_{\nu}^{\ast}(aX+b)))=\mathbb{E}_{X}\Phi(\Lambda_{\mu}^{\ast}(X)),

in other words
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}(\Phi(\Lambda^{\ast}_{\mu}))
is affinely invariant.

From the very definitions, it is easy to verify that for any probability measure
\mu
, the pointwise inequality

(9)

q_{\mu}(x)\leqslant e^{-\Lambda^{\ast}_{\mu}(x)}

holds (this direct consequence of the Markov/Chernoff bound was already explained in the Introduction). This fundamental observation led the authors of
[
26
]
to the “small N” part of (
5
) as follows: If
x
is such that
q_{\mu}(x)>e^{-\alpha}
for some
\alpha>0
, then
\Lambda_{\mu}^{\ast}(x)<\alpha
. Independence however implies that if
\mu
is the uniform probability on the cube, then
\Lambda_{\mu}^{\ast}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Lambda^{\ast}_{U}(x_{j})
, where
U
is uniform in
[-1,1]
, so a choice of
\alpha=(1-\varepsilon/2)n\kappa
, for
\kappa=\mathbb{E}\Lambda^{\ast}_{U}
and
A=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:q(x)>\alpha\}
in Lemma
2.3
implies

\mu(A)\leqslant\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Lambda^{\ast}_{U}(U_{j})<(1-\varepsilon/2)\mathbb{E}\Lambda_{U}^{\ast}\right),

where
U_{1},\ldots,U_{n}
are independent copies of
U
. The latter probability decreases to 0 with
n
, by the Central Limit Theorem, and this will eventually lead us to the upper bound in (
5
), thanks to (
6
). For the other half of the Theorem, a comparison reverse to that of (
9
) would let us argue in a similar fashion when
N
is “large”. Second to Lemma
2.3
, the non-trivial part in the proof of (
5
) was to show that for any
\varepsilon>0
the inequality
q_{\mu}(x)\geqslant e^{-\Lambda_{\mu}^{\ast}(x)-\varepsilon}
also holds when
n
is sufficiently large.

The argument of
[
26
]
was carefully reviewed by Gatzouras and Giannopoulos
[
32
]
, who verified that (

) still holds for any even and compactly supported product probability measure
\mu_{n}=\mu^{\otimes n}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, provided that
\mathbb{E}\Lambda^{\ast}_{\mu}<\infty
and
\mu
satisfies the “
\Lambda^{\ast}
-condition”,

(
*
)

\lim_{x\to x^{\ast}}\frac{-\log\mu([x,\infty))}{\Lambda^{\ast}_{\mu}(x)}=1,

where
\mathrm{supp}(\mu)=[-x^{\ast},x^{\ast}]
. Condition (

∗

‣
2.3
) essentially requires that
q_{\mu}
and
e^{-\Lambda_{\mu}^{\ast}}
tend to decrease at the same rate near the boundary. The authors in
[
32
]
also provided sufficient conditions for a measure to be “admissible” in the above sense. The result of Gatzouras–Giannopoulos has been quite recently generalised further by Pafis
[

, who showed that (

∗

‣
2.3

around

even in cases of several even product measures with non-compact support, an important example demonstrated by densities of the form
c_{p}e^{-|x|^{p}}
,
p\geqslant 1
. After Theorem
1.1
, we can verify that the same behaviour is exhibited by arbitrary log-concave probability measures and moreover determine the sharpness of the threshold window
\varrho
.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
(\lambda_{m})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of log-concave probability measures on
\mathbb{R}
and for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
set
\mu_{n}=\lambda_{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes\lambda_{n}
. For any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\mu_{n}(K_{N})=\begin{cases}0,&\hbox{ if }N\leqslant\exp((1-\varepsilon)\mathbb{E}\Lambda_{\mu_{n}}^{\ast}),\\
1,&\hbox{ if }N\geqslant\exp((1+\varepsilon)\mathbb{E}\Lambda_{\mu_{n}}^{\ast})\end{cases}.

Moreover,
(\mathbb{E}\Lambda^{\ast}_{\mu_{n}})^{-1}\varrho(\mu_{n},\delta)\leqslant C\max\{(\delta n)^{-\frac{1}{2}},\log n/n\}
for every
\delta\in(0,1)
, where
C>0
is an absolute constant.

As it turns out, it is considerably harder for one to successfully treat the case of arbitrary, non-product, distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{n}

or the Gaussian measure on

to the case of general Beta and Beta-prime distributions (see also

Still however it is not clear to us whether a unified argument could let one handle the case of arbitrary rotationally invariant, say log-concave, probability distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Our results allow us to give a partial answer under some further convexity assumptions. In the sequel, we identify any function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to(0,\infty)

with the real function

given by
\tilde{f}(r)=f(re_{1})

Theorem 2.5
.

Suppose that
\mu_{n}
is a rotationally invariant log-concave probability distribution on

, with
\lim_{n\to\infty}(\mathbb{E}\Lambda_{\mu_{n}}^{\ast})^{-1}\varrho({\mu_{n}},\delta)=0
, for any

.

Another example of rotationally invariant distributions on

and

with

. Early works where the beta distribution comes up, primarily in the context of polytopal approximation of convex bodies include

Theorem 2.6
.

Let

In view of all past and recent results mentioned in this section, we are led to believe that in general, in the log-concave setting, there is a natural candidate for the “threshold constant” for the expected measure of random convex sets. We are yet to find a counterexample to the following.

Conjecture 1
.

For any log-concave probability measure
\mu_{n}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the family
\{\mu_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
exhibits a sharp threshold in the sense of Definition
2.1
around
\mathcal{T}_{n}=\mathbb{E}\Lambda^{\ast}_{\mu_{n}}
.

Even though Conjecture
1
still remains elusive even for the case of uniform probability densities on convex bodies, our current results provide some further examples of measures that support it, namely
n
-products of arbitrary one-dimensional log-concave distributions (Theorem

) and a number of rotationally invariant probability distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
including densities proportional to
e^{{-|x|}^{p}}
,

, Gamma-type densities proportional to

,

(Theorem

, see Remark

) and Beta-type densities (Theorem

).

Recently, a systematic study of the problem was carried out in
[
13
]
, under only the assumption of log-concavity of the underlying distribution. The strategy developed in
[
13
]
builds upon the previous work of the same authors
[
12
]
, where very accurate upper and lower bounds are provided for the expected value of the half-space depth

for an arbitrary log-concave probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In particular, it was proved in

that there are absolute constants

such that

(10)

e^{-c_{1}n}\leqslant\mathbb{E}_{\mu}q_{\mu}(X)\leqslant e^{-c_{2}n/L_{\mu}^{2}}

for any log-concave probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where

is the isotropic constant of
\mu
. We remark that the upper bound in (

(11)

\mathbb{E}_{\mu}e^{-\Lambda^{\ast}_{\mu}(X)}\leqslant e^{-c_{2}n/L_{\mu}^{2}},

for any log-concave probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Note also that, by Jensen’s inequality, the latter implies that

(12)

\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\Lambda_{\mu}^{\ast}(X)\geqslant cn/L_{\mu}^{2}.

Given the recent resolution of the isotropic constant conjecture, inequality (

grows at least linearly with the dimension. This is a very useful estimate that we will recall in a number of instances in the sequel. In Section

The problem of obtaining sharp upper bounds for

. This estimate is optimal; one can check that

grows at the same rate with

The approach of

3.
Lower estimate for the Cramér transform under log-concavity

Given two random variables
X
and
Y
, we say that
X
stochastically dominates
Y
(in the first order) if
\mathbb{P}(Y\leqslant x)\leqslant\mathbb{P}(X\leqslant x)
for every
x\in\mathbb{R}
. It is well known that this is equivalent to the condition
\mathbb{E}(f(X))\leqslant\mathbb{E}(f(Y))
for any non-decreasing
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
. Given a random variable
X
, we denote by
F(x)=\mathbb{P}(X\leqslant x)
, the cumulative distribution function of
X
, and
Z(x)=\mathbb{P}(X\geqslant x)
. It is well known that if
X
has a continuous c.d.f.
F
, then the random variable
F(X)
is uniform on
[0,1]
. This can be verified immediately in the case that
F
is strictly increasing, since then

\mathbb{P}(F(X)\leqslant x)=\mathbb{P}(X\leqslant F^{-1}(x))=x=\mathbb{P}(U\leqslant x)

for any
0<x<1
, where
U
is uniform on
[0,1]
(the argument needs to be only slightly modified to capture the general case). In the next lemma, we verify that
F(X)
and
Z(X)
always stochastically dominate the uniform distribution.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
U
be a random variable uniformly distributed on
[0,1]
, and
X
be any random variable. Then the random variables
F(X),Z(X)
stochastically dominate
U
.

Proof.

We need to show that for any
s\in(0,1)
,

\max\{\mathbb{P}(Z(X)\leqslant s),\mathbb{P}(F(X)\leqslant s)\}\leqslant\mathbb{P}(U\leqslant s)=s.

Since
F
is right-continuous and non-decreasing, it has countably many jump discontinuities, say
(x_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
, with
x_{k-1}<x_{k}
for every
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
a_{k}=\lim_{x\to x_{k}^{-}}F(x)
and
b_{k}=F(x_{k})
, so that we always have
a_{k}<b_{k}
. Note also that
\mathbb{P}(F(X)\in[a_{k},b_{k}))=0
for all
k
. Given
s\in(0,1)
, we distinguish two cases: If
s\in[a_{k},b_{k})
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
, then

\mathbb{P}(F(X)\leqslant s)=\mathbb{P}(F(X)<a_{k})+\mathbb{P}(F(X)\in[a_{k},s])=\mathbb{P}(X<x_{k})=a_{k}<s.

If again
b_{k-1}<a_{k}
and
s\in[b_{k-1},a_{k})
, then
F
is continuous on
s
. Letting
m:=\sup\{y:F(y)\leqslant s\}
, we can check that

\mathbb{P}(F(X)\leqslant s)=\mathbb{P}(X\in\{y:F(y)\leqslant s\})=\mathbb{P}(X\leqslant m)=F(m)=s,

by continuity.

The result for
Z
then follows: since
F(X)
stochastically dominates
U
for any
X
,
Z(X)=F(-X)
also stochastically dominates
U
.
∎

We will use Lemma
3.1
to obtain good bounds on the
p
-th moments of
q_{X}(X)
,
p>-1
, for an arbitrary random variable
X
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
X
be any random variable and
p>-1
.

a)
If
p>0
, then
(2^{p}(p+1))^{-1}\leqslant\mathbb{E}(q(X)^{p})\leqslant 1
.

b)
If
p<0
, then
1\leqslant\mathbb{E}(q(X)^{p})\leqslant(2^{p}(p+1))^{-1}
.

Remark 3.3
.

Recall that if
X
is a continuous random variable, then
q(x)=\min\{F(x),1-F(x)\}
. If moreover
F
is continuous, then
F(X)
is uniform on
[0,1]
, so for any
p>-1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(q(X)^{p})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(\min\{F(X),1-F(X)\}^{p})=\mathbb{E}(\min\{U,1-U\}^{p})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}u^{p}\,du+\int_{\frac{1}{2}}^{1}(1-u)^{p}\,du=(2^{p}(p+1))^{-1}.

This, together with the trivial fact
q=1
for any atomic distribution, shows that the inequalities in Proposition
3.2
are sharp.

Proof.

The upper and lower bound in (a) and (b) respectively are due to the trivial inequality
q(x)\leqslant 1
. To prove the lower bound in (a), let
M=2^{-p}
and write

\mathbb{E}(q(X)^{p})=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(q(X)^{p}\geqslant x)\,dx\geqslant\int_{0}^{M}\mathbb{P}(q(X)^{p}\geqslant x)\,dx\geqslant\int_{0}^{M}1-\mathbb{P}(q(X)\leqslant x^{\frac{1}{p}})\,dx.

By Lemma
3.1
,

\mathbb{P}(q(X)\leqslant x^{\frac{1}{p}})\leqslant\mathbb{P}(F(X)\leqslant x^{\frac{1}{p}})+\mathbb{P}(Z(X)\leqslant x^{\frac{1}{p}})\leqslant 2x^{\frac{1}{p}}.

It follows then that

\mathbb{E}(q(X)^{p})\geqslant\int_{0}^{M}1-2x^{\frac{1}{p}}\,dx=M-2\frac{p}{p+1}M^{\frac{p+1}{p}}=\frac{2^{-p}}{p+1}.

Similarly, in the case that
-1<p<0
, we have that

\mathbb{E}(q(X)^{p})\leqslant\int_{0}^{M}1\,dx+\int_{M}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(q(X)^{p}\geqslant x)=M+\int_{M}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(q(X)\leqslant x^{\frac{1}{p}})\,dx,

where again we let
M=2^{-p}
, and using
\mathbb{P}(q(X)\leqslant x^{\frac{1}{p}})\leqslant 2x^{\frac{1}{p}}
this time we get

\mathbb{E}(q(X)^{p})\leqslant M-2\frac{p}{p+1}M^{\frac{p}{p+1}}=\frac{2^{-p}}{p+1},

which is the upper bound in (b).
∎

With the aid of Proposition
3.2
we are in place to prove the following one-dimensional inequality from which Theorem
1.1
will follow. In the statement we consider also the case of discrete measures in
\mathbb{R}
. We say that a discrete probability distribution
\mu
with probability mass function
p:\mathbb{Z}\to(0,\infty)
is log-concave if
p
is log-concave on
\mathbb{Z}
, that is,
p(k)^{2}\geqslant p(k-1)p(k+1)
for any
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. It can be proved that the tail and distribution functions of a log-concave p.m.f. are also log-concave in the above sense (see for example
[
50
, Theorem 2]
for a more general statement), and consequently this is also the case for
q_{\mu}
. For an arbitrary subset
A
of
\mathbb{R}
we let here
\conv(A)
denote the smallest interval containing
A
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
\mu
be a log-concave probability measure on
\mathbb{R}
that is either absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure, or supported on a subset of the integers. For every
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and every
x\in\conv(\mathrm{supp}(\mu))
,

(14)

\Lambda_{\mu}^{\ast}(x)\geq(1-\varepsilon)\log\frac{1}{q_{\mu}(x)}+\log\frac{\varepsilon}{2^{1-\varepsilon}}.

Proof.

To start with the absolutely continuous case, fix
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
x\in\conv(\mathrm{supp}(\mu))
. Since
\mu
is log-concave, the functions
\mu((-\infty,y])
and
\mu([y,\infty))
are both log-concave and so is
q_{\mu}
(recall Lemma
2.2
). Consider the function
g(y)=-(1-\varepsilon)\log q_{\mu}(y)
. By the convexity of
g
, there are
t,b\in\mathbb{R}
such that the tangent line
l(y)=ty+b
of
g
at
x
satisfies
l(x)=g(x)
and
l(y)\leqslant g(y)
for all
y\neq x
. It follows that
\mathbb{E}e^{l(X)}\leqslant\mathbb{E}e^{g(X)}
, which is equivalent to
-\log\mathbb{E}e^{g(X)}\leqslant-\log\mathbb{E}e^{l(X)}=-b-\Lambda(t)
. Since
l(x)=g(x)
, we eventually get that

g(x)-\log\mathbb{E}e^{g(X)}\leqslant xt-\Lambda_{\mu}(t)\leqslant\Lambda_{\mu}^{\ast}(x).

This fact combined with Proposition
3.2
(b) implies the desired inequality.

The argument is almost identical in the discrete setting: If the p.m.f. of
\mu
is log-concave on
\mathbb{Z}
then both
F
and
Z
are log-concave on
\mathbb{Z}
and consequently so is
q_{\mu}
. Now let
g
be the linear extension of
f:=-(1-\varepsilon)\log q_{\mu}
on
\mathbb{R}
(that is,
g(k+x)=f(k)+x(f(k+1)-f(k))
for every
k\in\mathrm{supp}(\mu)
and
x\in[0,1]
). It is easy to see that
g
is a convex function on
\mathbb{R}
(
g
is actually well-defined on the smallest interval containing
\mathrm{supp}\mu
) which is piecewise linear, so it stays above its “tangents”. Moreover,
\mathbb{E}e^{g(X)}=\mathbb{E}q(X)^{\varepsilon-1}
, since
g=f
on
\mathrm{supp}(\mu)
, which lets us conclude the proof applying again the bound in Proposition
3.2
(b).
∎

Remark 3.5
.

As is clear from the proof, equality in (
14
) can be attained for some
x,\varepsilon
if
\mu
has a log-affine density (take for example
x=2\log 2
and
\varepsilon=(\log 2)^{-1}
when
\mu
is the standard exponential distribution).

Following a similar reasoning, we can also prove the characterisation of the condition (
2
).

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
\mu
be a probability measure on
\mathbb{R}
and let
x^{\ast}\in[0,+\infty]
denote the right endpoint of
\mathrm{supp}(\mu)
. Then,

Corollary 3.8

4.1.
Pointwise bounds on the Cramér transform

Our first goal is to provide upper and lower pointwise bounds for the Cramér transform of an arbitrary rotationally invariant distribution on

(16)

Lemma 4.1

Let

Since
X\overset{d}{=}R_{X}\vartheta

Lemma 4.4

Lemma 4.6

Let

which is distributed according to a radially symmetric density

Denote by

\displaystyle m((1-\lambda)x+\lambda y)

Theorem 4.9

For the proof, we recall the notion of (second-order) stochastic dominance. If

\mathbb{E}X=\mathbb{E}Y\qquad\hbox{ and }\int_{x}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(X>t)\,dt\leqslant\int_{x}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(Y>t)\,dt\hbox{ for every }x\in\mathbb{R}.

The following Lemma can be found in
[

[

]

Lemma 4.10
.

Suppose that
X,Y:(\Omega,\mathbb{P})\to\mathbb{R}
are real random variables in

(a)

X\prec Y
if and only if
\mathbb{E}\phi(X)\leqslant\mathbb{E}\phi(Y)
for every convex function

(b)

If
X=c

\mathbb{E}Y
, then

.

(c)

If
\mathbb{E}X=\mathbb{E}Y
and there are

,

, such that the densities

satisfy

on

on

.

It is immediate by (b) above that if

Lemma 4.11

If we write

f(r)=\frac{1}{2}n\omega_{n}r^{\frac{n}{2}-1}f_{X}(\sqrt{r})
and
g(r)=\frac{1}{2}n\omega_{n}(2\pi)^{-\frac{n}{2}}r^{\frac{n}{2}-1}e^{-\frac{r}{2}}
. The constraints

\int_{0}^{\infty}f(r)-g(r)\,dr=0\qquad\hbox{ and }\qquad\int_{0}^{\infty}r(f(r)-g(r))\,dr=0,

imposed by isotropicity, grant us that
f-g
has at least two roots
0<x_{1}<x_{2}
on
(0,\infty)
. To verify that
x_{1},x_{2}
are the only roots on
(0,\infty)
, we examine the sign of
\log f(r)-\log g(r)=\frac{n}{2}\log(2\pi)+\log f_{X}(\sqrt{r})+\frac{r}{2}
. Recall that the function
r\mapsto\log f_{X}(\sqrt{r})
is non-increasing and concave (resp. convex). It follows that it crosses the graph of the affine function
-\frac{n}{2}\log(2\pi)-\frac{r}{2}
at most twice. Moreover, if
r\mapsto\log f_{X}(\sqrt{r})
is concave then the signature of
\log f-\log g
, and therefore
f-g
, must be
-,+,-
. If it is convex, the respective sign changes are
+,-,+
.
∎

The last ingredient we will need for the proof of Theorem
4.9
is a differential inequality for the moment generating function of the one-dimensional marginals of the spherical distribution.

Lemma 4.12
.

Let
\phi(t)=\mathbb{E}e^{t\vartheta_{1}}
. Then
\phi^{\prime}(t)\leqslant t\phi^{\prime\prime}(t)
, for every
t>0
.

Given Lemma
4.12
, we can conclude the proof of Theorem
4.9
.

Proof of Theorem
4.9
.

For every
t>0
let
\phi_{t}(x)=\mathbb{E}e^{t\sqrt{x}\vartheta_{1}}
. Note that
e^{\Lambda_{X_{1}}(t)}=\mathbb{E}\phi_{t}(|X|^{2})
and
e^{\Lambda_{G_{1}}(t)}=\mathbb{E}\phi_{t}(|G|^{2})
, so in view of Lemma
4.10
(a) it is enough to verify that
\phi_{t}
is convex on
(0,\infty)
. Differentiating twice, we see that

\frac{d^{2}\phi_{t}(x)}{dx^{2}}=\frac{t^{2}}{4x}\left(\phi^{\prime\prime}(t\sqrt{x})-\frac{1}{t\sqrt{x}}\phi^{\prime}(t\sqrt{x})\right),

where
\phi(y)=\mathbb{E}e^{y\vartheta_{1}}
. The result follows from Lemma
4.12
.
∎

We conclude with the proof of Lemma
4.12
.

Proof of Lemma
4.12
.

Recall that in (
16
) we have verified that
\phi(x)=\Gamma(n/2)h_{0}(x)
, where for
s=0,1,2,\ldots
we let

h_{s}(x)=\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)^{-\frac{n}{2}+1-s}I_{\frac{n}{2}-1+s}(x).

The modified Bessel function satisfies the recurrence relations

(20)

I_{v}^{\prime}(x)=\frac{1}{2}(I_{v-1}(x)+I_{v+1}(x)),\qquad I_{v-1}(x)=\frac{2v}{x}I_{v}(x)+I_{v+1}(x)

(see for example
[
1
, 9.6.26]
). Differentiating and using (
20
), we can check that
h_{s}^{\prime}(x)=(x/2)h_{s+1}(x)
, for every
s=0,1,2,\ldots
. In particular,

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime\prime}(x)=\Gamma\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)\left(\frac{1}{2}h_{1}(x)+\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)^{2}h_{2}(x)\right)=\frac{1}{x}\phi^{\prime}(x)+\Gamma\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)^{2}h_{2}(x),

from which the wanted claim follows (clearly
h_{2}\geqslant 0
, cf. the series expansion (
17
)).
∎

4.2.
Inequalities for the expectation of
\Lambda^{\ast}_{X}

In this section we provide sharp bounds on the expected value of
\Lambda^{\ast}_{X}
, and in particular the parameter
\mathbb{E}e^{-\Lambda_{X}^{\ast}(X)}
, for rotationally invariant dstributions
X
. We start with the following general upper bound.

Theorem 4.13
.

For every rotationally invariant log-concave random vector
X
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
we have
\mathbb{E}e^{-\Lambda^{\ast}_{X}(X)}\leqslant\mathbb{E}e^{-\Lambda_{\mathcal{E}}^{\ast}(\mathcal{E})}
.

For the proof of Theorem
4.13
we rely on the pointwise comparison established in Proposition
4.3
combined with the “intersecting densities” argument which has turned out to be a really efficient tool towards the proof of extremal inequalities in the log-concave setting (see for example
[
29
]
,
[
19
]
and in particular
[
4
]
, where a comprehensive exposition of the method in a general framework is included).

Proof of Theorem
4.13
.

By the results of the previous section (Proposition
4.3
), we have that that
\Lambda^{\ast}_{X_{1}}(x)\geqslant\Lambda^{\ast}_{\mathcal{E}_{1}}(x)
holds pointwise for any
x>0
, for every isotropic log-concave and spherically symmetric random vector
X
. It follows that for every such
X
,
\mathbb{E}e^{-\Lambda_{X}^{\ast}(X)}\leqslant\mathbb{E}e^{-\Lambda_{\mathcal{E}}^{\ast}(X)}=\mathbb{E}e^{-\Lambda_{\mathcal{E}_{1}}^{\ast}(|X|)}
. It remains to show that
\mathbb{E}e^{-\Lambda_{\mathcal{E}_{1}}^{\ast}(|X|)}\leqslant\mathbb{E}e^{-\Lambda_{\mathcal{E}_{1}}^{\ast}(|{\mathcal{E}}|)}
.
We denote by
f
the density of
X
. Recall that the density of
|X|
is then equal to
n\omega_{n}r^{n-1}f(r)\mathds{1}_{r>0}
.

Lemma 4.14
.

The function
f-f_{\mathcal{E}}
changes sign exactly twice on
(0,\infty)
, and its signature is
-,+,-
.

Proof.

Since
\log f
is concave and decreasing on
(0,\infty)
and
\log f_{\mathcal{E}}(x)=\log c_{n}-x\sqrt{n+1}
is affine, it follows that
\log f-\log f_{\mathcal{E}}
changes sign at most twice on
(0,\infty)
. Since
\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)-f_{\mathcal{E}}(x)\,dx=0
, there must be at least one sign change. If there is only one, say at
x_{1}>0
, then the constraint
\mathbb{E}|X|^{2}=\mathbb{E}|{\mathcal{E}}|^{2}
implies that
\int_{0}^{\infty}(x^{2}-x_{1}^{2})(f(x)-f_{\mathcal{E}}(x))\,dx=0
. It is easy to check however that the integrand does not change its sign on
(0,\infty)
, which leads to a contradiction.

Moreover, since
\log f
and
\log f_{\mathcal{E}}
are both decreasing on
(0,\infty)
, if
f(0)>c_{n}=f_{\mathcal{E}}(0)
then the graphs of
\log f,\log f_{\mathcal{E}}
would cross at most once on
(0,\infty)
, which we have seen is not the case. This shows that
\log f-\log f_{\mathcal{E}}
, and hence
f-f_{\mathcal{E}}
, is negative close to
0
.
∎

Let us assume that
0<x_{1}<x_{2}
are the two distinct sign changes of
f-f_{\mathcal{E}}
on
(0,\infty)
, granted by Lemma
4.14
. Note that, thanks to symmetry and
\mathbb{E}|X|^{2}=\mathbb{E}|{\mathcal{E}}|^{2}
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-\Lambda_{\mathcal{E}}^{\ast}(x)}(f(x)-f_{\mathcal{E}}(x))\,dx=n\omega_{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{n-1}(e^{-\Lambda_{\mathcal{E}_{1}}^{\ast}(x)}-ax^{2}-b)(f(x)-f_{\mathcal{E}}(x))\,dx,

for any
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
. Let
\Psi(x):=e^{-\Lambda_{\mathcal{E}_{1}}^{\ast}(x)}-ax^{2}-b
. We can choose
a,b
such that
\Psi(x_{1})=\Psi(x_{2})=0
. This is because this condition is equivalent to

A\cdot\begin{bmatrix}a\\
b\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}e^{-\Lambda_{\mathcal{E}_{1}}^{\ast}(x_{1})}\\
e^{-\Lambda_{\mathcal{E}_{1}}^{\ast}(x_{2})}\end{bmatrix}

with
A=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}^{2}&1\\
x_{2}^{2}&1\end{bmatrix}
, so that
\mathrm{det}A=x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2}<0
. In particular, we can solve explicitly for
a,b
and get

a=\frac{e^{-\Lambda_{\mathcal{E}_{1}}^{\ast}(x_{1})}-e^{-\Lambda_{\mathcal{E}_{1}}^{\ast}(x_{2})}}{x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2}}\qquad\hbox{ and }\qquad b=\frac{x_{1}^{2}e^{-\Lambda_{\mathcal{E}}^{\ast}(x_{2})}-x_{2}^{2}e^{-\Lambda_{\mathcal{E}}^{\ast}(x_{1})}}{x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2}}.

Notice that
a<0
, due to the monotonicity of
\Lambda^{\ast}
. Moreover, the following Lemma implies that
a>-1/2
.

Lemma 4.15
.

The function
x\mapsto e^{-\Lambda^{\ast}_{\mathcal{E}_{1}}(x)}+\frac{x^{2}}{2}
is nondecreasing on
(0,\infty)
.

Proof.

Letting
\Lambda^{\ast}=\Lambda^{\ast}_{\mathcal{E}_{1}}
and
h(x)=e^{-\Lambda^{\ast}(x)}+x^{2}/2
, we can see that
h^{\prime}(x)=x(1-\psi(x))
for
\psi(x)=\frac{(\Lambda^{\ast})^{\prime}(x)}{x}e^{-\Lambda^{\ast}(x)}
. We can check that
\psi
is decreasing on
(0,\infty)
. This is because

\psi^{\prime}(x)=-\frac{(\Lambda^{\ast})^{\prime}(x)^{2}}{x}e^{-\Lambda^{\ast}(x)}+\frac{e^{-\Lambda^{\ast}(x)}}{x^{2}}((\Lambda^{\ast})^{\prime\prime}(x)x-(\Lambda^{\ast})^{\prime}(x))

and
(\Lambda^{\ast})^{\prime\prime}(x)x-(\Lambda^{\ast})^{\prime}(x)<0
(by Corollary
4.8
, since
f_{\mathcal{E}}\in\mathcal{LC}_{conv}
). Moreover, notice that
\lim_{x\to 0^{+}}\frac{(\Lambda^{\ast})^{\prime}(x)}{x}
equals 1 (recall that
\Lambda^{\ast}
is given by (
19
)), which eventually implies that
\psi(x)\leqslant\lim_{x\to 0^{+}}\psi(x)=1
for all
x>0
, and consequently
h^{\prime}\geqslant 0
on
(0,\infty)
.
∎

The restrictions
a\in(-1/2,0)
are crucial in proving the following.

Lemma 4.16
.

If
-1/2<a<0
, then the function
\Psi^{\prime}
changes sign exactly once on
(0,\infty)
, from negative to positive.

Proof.

Differentiating, we see that
\Psi^{\prime}(x)=-x\psi(x)-2ax
, where
\psi(x)=\frac{(\Lambda^{\ast})^{\prime}(x)}{x}e^{-\Lambda^{\ast}(x)}
(for
\Lambda^{\ast}=\Lambda^{\ast}_{\mathcal{E}_{1}}
). We have explained that
\psi
is decreasing and satisfies
\lim_{x\to 0^{+}}\psi(x)=1
. It follows that
\Psi^{\prime}(x)<0
if
\psi(x)>-2a
, which is true for small enough values of
x
, since
a>-1/2
, and
\Psi^{\prime}(x)>0
if
\psi(x)<-2a
.
∎

We are now in place to complete the proof of Theorem
4.13
: By Lemma
4.16
, we deduce that
\Psi
changes its monotonicity exactly once on
(0,\infty)
, from decreasing to increasing. This however implies that
x_{1},x_{2}
are the only roots of
\Psi
on
(0,\infty)
, which correspond to sign change points. In particular,
\Psi
must be positive on
(0,x_{1})\cup(x_{2},\infty)
and negative on
(x_{1},x_{2})
. By Lemma
4.14
, it follows then that
\Psi(x)(f(x)-f_{\mathcal{E}}(x))<0
everywhere on
(0,\infty)
and, as a consequence,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-\Lambda_{\mathcal{E}}^{\ast}(x)}(f(x)-f_{\mathcal{E}}(x))\,dx=n\omega_{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{n-1}\Psi(x)(f(x)-f_{\mathcal{E}}(x))\,dx\leqslant 0,

which is the desired inequality.
∎

We can also use Lemma
4.14
and an argument similar to the above, to prove the following.

Theorem 4.17
.

Any rotationally invariant log-concave
X
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfies
\mathbb{E}\Lambda^{\ast}_{X}(X)\geqslant\mathbb{E}\Lambda^{\ast}_{\mathcal{E}}({\mathcal{E}})
.

Proof.

The argument is the same as in the proof of Theorem
4.13
. First we argue that

\mathbb{E}\Lambda_{X}^{\ast}(X)=\mathbb{E}\Lambda_{X_{1}}^{\ast}(|X|)\geqslant\mathbb{E}\Lambda_{{\mathcal{E}}_{1}}^{\ast}(|X|),

since
\Lambda_{X_{1}}^{\ast}\geqslant\Lambda_{\mathcal{E}_{1}}^{\ast}
pointwise (by Proposition
4.3
). Again thanks to the constraint
\mathbb{E}|X|^{2}=\mathbb{E}|{\mathcal{E}}|^{2}
we now have

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Lambda_{\mathcal{E}}^{\ast}(x)(f(x)-f_{\mathcal{E}}(x))\,dx=n\omega_{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{n-1}(\Lambda_{\mathcal{E}_{1}}^{\ast}(x)-ax^{2}-b)(f(x)-f_{\mathcal{E}}(x))\,dx

for any
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
. We now consider the function
\Phi(x)=\Lambda^{\ast}_{\mathcal{E}_{1}}(x)-ax^{2}-b
, with
a,b
now chosen so that
\Phi(x_{1})=\Phi(x_{2})=0
(where again
0<x_{1}<x_{2}
are the unique sign changes of
f_{X}-f_{\mathcal{E}}
on
(0,\infty)
, granted by Lemma
4.14
). Notice that now
a=\frac{\Lambda^{\ast}_{\mathcal{E}_{1}}(x_{1})-\Lambda^{\ast}_{\mathcal{E}_{1}}(x_{2})}{x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2}}\in(0,1/2)
. The lower bound here is obvious, since
x_{1}<x_{2}
, and the upper bound follows from the fact that the function
h(x)=\Lambda^{\ast}_{\mathcal{E}_{1}}(x)-x^{2}/2
is non-increasing (since
h^{\prime}(x)=\frac{x\left(1-\sqrt{\frac{4x^{2}}{n+1}+1}\right)}{1+\sqrt{\frac{4x^{2}}{n+1}+1}}
is clearly non-positive).

Like before, we now need to show that
\Phi
has the same sign as
f_{X}-f_{\mathcal{E}}
on each of the intervals
(0,x_{1})
,
(x_{1},x_{2})
and
(x_{2},\infty)
, so it is enough to verify that
\Phi^{\prime}
changes its sign exactly once, from positive to negative. Note that
\Phi^{\prime}(x)=x(\phi(x)-2a)
, where
\phi(x)=(\Lambda^{\ast}_{\mathcal{E}_{1}})^{\prime}(x)/x
. Since
\phi^{\prime}(x)=\frac{(\Lambda_{\mathcal{E}_{1}}^{\ast})^{\prime\prime}(x)x-(\Lambda_{\mathcal{E}_{1}}^{\ast})^{\prime}(x)}{x^{2}}\leqslant 0
(again, by Corollary
4.8
) we get that
\phi
is non-increasing, with
\phi(x)\to 1
as
x\to 0
. Since
a<1/2
, it follows then that
\phi(x)>2a
holds only for
x
sufficiently close to 0, which shows the desired result for
\Phi^{\prime}
.
∎

As a direct corollary of Theorem
4.13
, for the case
n=1
, we get the following bound on the exponential one-shot separability constant of
symmetric
log-concave distributions.

Corollary 4.18
.

Let
X=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
be a random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with independent and symmetric log-concave components. Then
\mathbb{E}e^{-\Lambda^{\ast}_{X}(X)}\leqslant(\mathbb{E}e^{-\Lambda^{\ast}_{Y}(Y)})^{n}
, where
Y
is a standard double exponential (note that
\mathbb{E}e^{-\Lambda^{\ast}_{Y}(Y)}\simeq 0.787...
).

Proof.

The case
n=1
of Theorem
4.13
implies that
\mathbb{E}e^{-\Lambda^{\ast}_{X_{j}}(X_{j})}\leqslant\mathbb{E}e^{-\Lambda^{\ast}_{Y}(Y)}
for every
j=1,\ldots,n
, and by independence we have that
\mathbb{E}e^{-\Lambda^{\ast}_{X}(X)}=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}e^{-\Lambda^{\ast}_{X_{j}}(X_{j})}
.
∎

Given Theorem
4.13
, one could wonder if a more general result than that of Corollary
4.18
holds, namely that the right-hand side bound
(\mathbb{E}e^{-\Lambda^{\ast}_{Y}(Y)})^{n}
is true for all rotationally-invariant (not necessarily product) log-concave distributions
X
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We already know that for every such
X
,
\mathbb{E}e^{-\Lambda^{\ast}_{X}}\leqslant c^{n}
for some absolute constant
c\in(0,1)
(this follows already from (
12
) and the well known fact that
L_{X}
is bounded in the rotationally invariant case). We believe that the sharp value of
c
is that provided by Theorem
4.13
. In particular, numerical evidence leads us to the following conjecture. To stress the dependence on the dimension
n
, we denote here by
X_{n}
the random vector
\mathcal{E}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Conjecture 2
.

The function

n\mapsto\left(\mathbb{E}e^{-\Lambda^{\ast}_{X_{n}}(X_{n})}\right)^{\frac{1}{n}}

is non-increasing.

We close this section by providing improved Gaussian bounds for the parameter
e^{-\Lambda_{X}^{\ast}(X)}
when
f_{X}
lies in either of the classes
\mathcal{LC}_{conc}
or
\mathcal{LC}_{conv}
. The proof of the following theorem employs the same argument as that of Theorem
4.13
.

Theorem 4.19
.

Let
X
be a rotationally invariant log-concave random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and assume that
f_{X}\in\mathcal{LC}_{conv}
. Then
\mathbb{E}e^{-\Lambda_{X}^{\ast}(X)}\geqslant\mathbb{E}e^{-\Lambda_{G}^{\ast}(G)}
. If
f_{X}\in\mathcal{LC}_{conc}
, the inequality is reversed.

Proof.

We can assume that
X
is isotropic. Recall that, by Theorem
4.9
,
e^{-\Lambda^{\ast}_{X_{1}}(x)}\geqslant e^{-\Lambda_{G_{1}}(x)}=e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2}}
for every
x>0
if
f_{X}\in\mathcal{LC}_{conv}
(and the opposite inequality holds if
f_{X}\in\mathcal{LC}_{conc}
). It is then enough to prove that
\mathbb{E}e^{-\frac{|X|^{2}}{2}}\geqslant\mathbb{E}e^{-\frac{|G|^{2}}{2}}
when
f_{X}\in\mathcal{LC}_{conv}
(resp.
\mathbb{E}e^{-\frac{|X|^{2}}{2}}\leqslant\mathbb{E}e^{-\frac{|G|^{2}}{2}}

f_{X}\in\mathcal{LC}_{conc}
). As in the proof of Theorem
4.13
, the wanted result will rely on the comparison of the densities
f:=f_{X}
and
f_{G}
. Note that, after integration in polar coordinates and a change of variables,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}e^{-\frac{|X|^{2}}{2}}-\mathbb{E}e^{-\frac{|G|^{2}}{2}}

\displaystyle=n\omega_{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2}}x^{n-1}(f(x)-f_{G}(x))\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{n\omega_{n}}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{y}{2}}y^{\frac{n}{2}}(f(\sqrt{y})-f_{G}(\sqrt{y}))\,dy.

As in the proof of Lemma
4.14
, the constraint
\mathbb{E}|X|^{2}=\mathbb{E}|G|^{2}
, imposed by isotropicity, will imply that
f(\sqrt{y})-f_{G}(\sqrt{y})
has at least two zeroes on
(0,\infty)
. Moreover
\log f(\sqrt{y})
is nonincreasing and either convex or concave (given that
f_{X}\in\mathcal{LC}_{conv}
or
f_{X}\in\mathcal{LC}_{conc}
, respectively). It follows that it crosses the affine function
\log f_{G}(\sqrt{y})
at most twice, hence
f(\sqrt{y})-f_{G}(\sqrt{y})
must have exactly two sign changes, say at the points
0<y_{1}<y_{2}
, with its sign being
+,-,+
(if
f_{X}\in\mathcal{LC}_{conv}
) or
-,+,-
(if
f_{X}\in\mathcal{LC}_{conc}
).

Next, consider the function
\Phi(y)=e^{-\frac{y}{2}}-ay-b
, where
a,b
are chosen so that
\Phi(y_{1})=\Phi(y_{2})=0
. Namely, we have that

a=\frac{e^{-\frac{y_{1}}{2}}-e^{-\frac{y_{2}}{2}}}{y_{1}-y_{2}}\qquad\hbox{ and }\qquad b=\frac{y_{1}e^{-\frac{y_{1}}{2}}-y_{2}e^{-\frac{y_{2}}{2}}}{y_{1}-y_{2}}.

Note that the fact that
t\mapsto e^{-t}+t
is increasing on
(0,\infty)
implies that
a\in(-1/2,0)
. Differentiating, it is immediate to check that
\Phi
is convex decreasing for
e^{-y/2}>-2a
and increasing for
e^{-y/2}<-2a
. It follows that
\Phi
is first decreasing and then increasing, and since it has at least two zeroes, convexity implies that these are the only two, with its sign being
+,-,+
. Putting everything together, we have verified that
\mathbb{E}e^{-\frac{|X|^{2}}{2}}-\mathbb{E}e^{-\frac{|G|^{2}}{2}}\geqslant 0
if
f_{X}\in\mathcal{LC}_{conv}
(and
\leqslant 0
if
f_{X}\in\mathcal{LC}_{conc}
), which is the wanted statement.
∎

Remark 4.20
.

Thanks to independence, it is easy to verify that

2^{-\frac{n}{2}}
for every rotationally invariant log-concave random vector

such that
f_{X}\in\mathcal{LC}_{conc}
(not necessarily with independent coordinates).

5.
Threshold for the expected measure of random polytopes: new results

Let us briefly review the strategy of
[
13
]
for the expected measure threshold problem. Recall that in general, given
\delta\in(0,1)
and a probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we let

\varrho_{1}(\mu,\delta):=\sup\{r>0:\mathbb{E}\mu(K_{N})\leqslant\delta,\hbox{ for every }N\leqslant\exp(r)\},

and

\varrho_{2}(\mu,\delta):=\inf\{r>0:\mathbb{E}\mu(K_{N})\geqslant 1-\delta,\hbox{ for every }N\geqslant\exp(r)\}.

We also denote

\varrho(\mu,\delta):=\varrho_{2}(\mu,\delta)-\varrho_{1}(\mu,\delta).

Our general goal is to obtain lower and upper bounds for the parameters
\varrho_{1}(\mu,\delta)
and
\varrho_{2}(\mu,\delta)
, respectively. Starting with
\varrho_{1}
, we apply Lemma
2.3
(a) taking
A=A_{\eta}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\Lambda_{\mu}^{\ast}(x)\leqslant(1-\eta)\kappa\}
,
\eta\in(0,1)
, where we denote
\kappa=\mathbb{E}\Lambda_{\mu}^{\ast}
. An application of Chebyshev’s inequality leads us then to the upper bound

(21)

\mu(A)\leqslant\mathbb{P}\left(|\Lambda^{\ast}_{\mu}(X)-\kappa|\geqslant\eta\kappa\right)\leqslant\frac{\mathbb{E}|\Lambda^{\ast}_{\mu}(X)-\kappa|^{2}}{\eta^{2}\kappa^{2}}.

This gives rise to the parameter

(22)

\upbeta(\mu):=\frac{\Var(\Lambda_{\mu}^{\ast})}{(\mathbb{E}\Lambda_{\mu}^{\ast})^{2}}.

With (
21
) as a starting point, the authors in
[
13
]
also employed (
12
) to prove the following result.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\mu
be a centered log-concave probability measure on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.
Assume that
\upbeta(\mu)<1/8
and
8\upbeta(\mu)<\delta<1
. If
n/L_{\mu}^{2}\geqslant c_{2}\ln(2/\delta)\sqrt{\delta/\upbeta(\mu)}

where
L_{\mu}
is the isotropic constant of
\mu
, then

\varrho_{1}(\mu,\delta)\geqslant\left(1-\sqrt{\frac{8\upbeta(\mu)}{\delta}}\right)\mathbb{E}_{\mu}(\Lambda_{\mu}^{*}).

As a second step, relying on (
3
), a matching upper bound for
\varrho_{2}(\mu,\delta)
is provided in
[
13
]
, in the case that the measure is the uniform on a convex body. Using Theorem
1.1
, we can now verify that a similar estimate holds for all log-concave measures.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
\delta>0
and
\mu
be any log-concave probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
128\upbeta(\mu)<\delta<1
. Then we can find some
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\varrho_{2}(\mu,\delta)\leqslant\left(1+\varepsilon(n)\right)\mathbb{E}_{\mu}(\Lambda^{\ast}_{\mu}),

for every
n\geqslant n_{0}
, where
c>0
is an absolute constant, where

\varepsilon(n)=\max\left\{\sqrt{128\upbeta(\mu)/\delta},\frac{8\log\left(e\left(1+\frac{1}{2}\right)\mathbb{E}\Lambda^{*}\right)}{3\,\mathbb{E}\Lambda^{*}}\right\}.

Proof.

We will rely on the lower bound

\mathbb{E}\mu(K_{N})\geqslant\mu(A)\left(1-2\binom{N}{n}\left(1-\inf_{x\in A}q_{\mu}(x)\right)^{N-n}\right),

of Lemma
2.3
, applied for
A=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\Lambda^{\ast}\leqslant\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{8}\right)\mathbb{E}\Lambda^{\ast}\}
, for a proper choice of
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. On the one hand, by Chebyshev’s inequality we have that for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,

\mu\left(\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:|\Lambda^{\ast}(x)-\mathbb{E}\Lambda^{\ast}|\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{8}\mathbb{E}\Lambda^{\ast}\}\right)\leqslant\frac{64\upbeta(\mu)}{\varepsilon^{2}}.

Choosing any
\varepsilon\geq\varepsilon_{0}:=\sqrt{128\upbeta(\mu)/\delta}
, this implies that
\mu(A)\geqslant 1-\delta/2
. What we are left with then, is to show that

(23)

2\binom{N}{n}\left(1-\inf_{x\in A}q_{\mu}(x)\right)^{N-n}\leqslant\frac{\delta}{2}

for all
N\geqslant\exp\left((1+\varepsilon)\mathbb{E}\Lambda^{\ast}\right)
if
n
is large. Recall that, by Corollary
3.8
, we have

\inf_{x\in A}q(x)\geqslant\exp\left(-\frac{1}{1-\eta}\left(\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{8}\right)\mathbb{E}\Lambda^{\ast}-\log\eta\right)\right),

for every
\eta\in(0,1)
. Set
c_{\varepsilon}:=(1+\varepsilon/8)\mathbb{E}\Lambda^{\ast}
and note that this tends to infinity as
n\to\infty
. Fix
\varepsilon\geqslant\varepsilon_{0}
and choose

\eta_{\varepsilon}=\left(\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)\mathbb{E}\Lambda^{*}\right)^{-1}.

Then, the inequality

\frac{c_{\varepsilon}-\log\eta}{1-\eta}\leqslant(1+\varepsilon/2)\mathbb{E}\Lambda^{\ast},

is equivalent to

\varepsilon\geqslant\frac{8}{3\,\mathbb{E}\Lambda^{*}}\log\left(e\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)\mathbb{E}\Lambda^{*}\right).

Since
\varepsilon_{0}\leq 1
, it suffices to find
\varepsilon
, such that

\varepsilon\geqslant\varepsilon_{0}\ \ \text{and}\ \ \varepsilon\geqslant\frac{8}{3\,\mathbb{E}\Lambda^{*}}\log\left(e\left(1+\frac{1}{2}\right)\mathbb{E}\Lambda^{*}\right).

We can satisfy both inequalities by taking
\varepsilon
, the maximum of the two.

Finally, to achieve (
23
), we can assume that
n
is large enough so that
2^{-n}\leqslant\delta/2
. Using this assumption together with
\binom{N}{n}\leqslant(eN/n)^{n}/e
and
\log(1+y)\leqslant y
, we get that

\frac{2}{\delta}\cdot 2\binom{N}{n}\exp\left((N-n)\log(1-\inf_{x\in A}q_{\mu}(x))\right)\leqslant\exp\left(n\left(\log(2xe)-(x-1)e^{-(1+\varepsilon/2)\mathbb{E}\Lambda^{\ast}}\right)\right),

where
x=N/n
. Eventually, the right hand side is smaller than 1 if
e^{(1+\varepsilon/2)\mathbb{E}\Lambda^{\ast}}<\frac{x-1}{\log(2xe)}
, which can be achieved if we choose
N\geqslant\exp((1+\varepsilon)\mathbb{E}\Lambda^{\ast})
(and let
n
grow even larger, if needed).
∎

Remark 5.3
.

Recall that the
\log(\mathbb{E}\Lambda_{\mu}^{\ast})/\mathbb{E}\Lambda_{\mu}^{\ast}
term in the statement of the last Theorem is in general, for any log-concave
\mu
, of the order
O(\log n/n)
. This is due to the general estimates (
12
) and (
13
).

For any log-concave
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
we let
\omega_{\mu}:=-\log q_{\mu}
and consider the parameter

(24)

\uptau(\mu):=\frac{\Var(\omega_{\mu})}{(\mathbb{E}\omega_{\mu})^{2}}.

Another result of
[
13
]
for the case of uniform measures on convex sets that we can now extend to the general log-concave setting is the fact that
\upbeta(\mu)
and
\uptau(\mu)
behave the same way, when the dimension is large. In particular,
\upbeta(\mu)
tends to 0 with the dimension if and only if
\uptau(\mu)
does.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. For any log-concave probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

(25)

(1-\varepsilon)^{2}\uptau(\mu)+\frac{2(1-\varepsilon)\log\frac{\varepsilon}{2^{1-\varepsilon}}}{\mathbb{E}\omega}+\varepsilon^{2}-2\varepsilon\leqslant\upbeta(\mu)\leqslant\\
\leqslant\left(\uptau(\mu)+2\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{2}-\frac{2(1-\varepsilon)\log\frac{\varepsilon}{2^{1-\varepsilon}}}{\mathbb{E}\omega}\right)\left(\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}\left(1-\frac{\log\frac{\varepsilon}{2^{1-\varepsilon}}}{\mathbb{E}\Lambda^{\ast}}\right)\right)^{2}.

Consequently,

(26)

(1+o(1))\uptau(\mu)-O(L_{\mu}^{2}\log n/n)\leqslant\upbeta(\mu)\leqslant\left(\uptau(\mu)+O(L_{\mu}^{2}\log n/n)\right)(1+O(L_{\mu}^{2}\log n/n)).

Moreover,

(27)

\left(1-O\left(L_{\mu}^{2}\log n/n\right)\right)\mathbb{E}\omega_{\mu}\leqslant\mathbb{E}\Lambda^{\ast}_{\mu}\leqslant\mathbb{E}\omega_{\mu}.

Proof.

∎

For the lower bound, by Theorem
1.1
we get that for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,

\mathbb{E}{\Lambda^{\ast}}^{2}\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)^{2}\mathbb{E}\omega^{2}+2(1-\varepsilon)\log\frac{\varepsilon}{2^{1-\varepsilon}}\mathbb{E}\omega

and using also
\mathbb{E}\Lambda^{\ast}\leqslant\mathbb{E}\omega
(by (
1
)) we can write

\displaystyle\upbeta(\mu)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Var(\Lambda^{\ast})}{(\mathbb{E}\omega)^{2}}\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}\omega}{\mathbb{E}\Lambda^{\ast}}\right)^{2}\geqslant\frac{(1-\varepsilon)^{2}\mathbb{E}\omega^{2}+2(1-\varepsilon)\log\frac{\varepsilon}{2^{1-\varepsilon}}\mathbb{E}\omega-(\mathbb{E}\omega)^{2}}{(\mathbb{E}\omega)^{2}}

\displaystyle=(1-\varepsilon)^{2}\uptau(\mu)+\frac{2(1-\varepsilon)\log\frac{\varepsilon}{2^{1-\varepsilon}}}{\mathbb{E}\omega}+\varepsilon^{2}-2\varepsilon.

Since
\mathbb{E}\omega\geqslant\mathbb{E}\Lambda^{\ast}\geqslant cn/L_{\mu}^{2}
we get that for, say
\varepsilon=1/n
,

-\frac{2(1-\varepsilon)\log\frac{\varepsilon}{2^{1-\varepsilon}}}{\mathbb{E}\omega}=O(L_{\mu}^{2}\log n/n),

and the left hand side inequality follows.

For the upper bound, we apply
\mathbb{E}\Lambda^{\ast}\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)\mathbb{E}\omega+\log\frac{\varepsilon}{2^{1-\varepsilon}}
and
\mathbb{E}{\Lambda^{\ast}}^{2}\leqslant\mathbb{E}\omega^{2}
to get that

\displaystyle\upbeta(\mu)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\mathbb{E}\omega^{2}-(1-\varepsilon)^{2}(\mathbb{E}\omega)^{2}-2(1-\varepsilon)\log\frac{\varepsilon}{2^{1-\varepsilon}}\mathbb{E}\omega}{(\mathbb{E}\omega)^{2}}\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}\omega}{\mathbb{E}\Lambda^{\ast}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(\uptau(\mu)+2\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{2}-\frac{2(1-\varepsilon)\log\frac{\varepsilon}{2^{1-\varepsilon}}}{\mathbb{E}\omega}\right)\left(\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}\left(1-\frac{\log\frac{\varepsilon}{2^{1-\varepsilon}}}{\mathbb{E}\Lambda^{\ast}}\right)\right)^{2}.

Choosing
\varepsilon=1/n
we can check that

\left(\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}\left(1-\frac{\log\frac{\varepsilon}{2^{1-\varepsilon}}}{\mathbb{E}\Lambda^{\ast}}\right)\right)=O(1+O(L_{\mu}^{2}\log n/n))

and

-\frac{2(1-\varepsilon)\log\frac{\varepsilon}{2^{1-\varepsilon}}}{\mathbb{E}\omega}\leqslant O(L_{\mu}^{2}\log n/n)

(recalling again that
\mathbb{E}\omega\geqslant\mathbb{E}\Lambda^{\ast}\geqslant cn/L_{\mu}^{2}
). It follows that

\upbeta(\mu)\leqslant\left(\uptau(\mu)+O(L_{\mu}^{2}\log n/n)\right)(1+O(L_{\mu}^{2}\log n/n)),

which concludes the proof of (
26
). Finally, the lower bound in (
27
) follows again from Theorem
1.1
applied for
\varepsilon=1/n
, since

∎

\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)\mathbb{E}\omega_{\mu}-\log\frac{n}{2^{1-\frac{1}{n}}}\geqslant\mathbb{E}\omega_{\mu}\left(1-\frac{1}{n}-\frac{\log(2n)}{\mathbb{E}\omega_{\mu}}\right)\geqslant\mathbb{E}\omega_{\mu}\left(1-\frac{1}{n}-\frac{L_{\mu}^{2}\log(2n)}{cn}\right).

Combining Theorems
5.1
and
5.2
we obtain the following upper bound for the width
\varrho(\mu,\delta)=\varrho_{2}(\mu,\delta)-\varrho_{1}(\mu,\delta)
: There is an absolute constant
C>0
such that

(28)

\varrho(\mu,\delta)\leq C\left(\sqrt{\frac{\upbeta(\mu)}{\delta}}+\varepsilon(n)\right)\mathbb{E}\Lambda^{\ast}_{\mu},

if
n
is large enough, where
\varepsilon(n)
is as in the statement of Theorem
5.2
. This general estimate leads us to new threshold-type results for the asymptotics of the expected measure of random polytopes. We record some consequences in the rest of this section.

5.1.
Product measures: Proof of Theorem
2.4

For the proof of Theorem
2.4
, we will need uniform upper and lower bounds for the variance and expected value of
\Lambda^{\ast}_{X}
in the one-dimensional log-concave setting. The following is another consequence of Theorem
3.4
.

Lemma 5.5
.

There is an absolute constant
c>0
such that
\Var(\Lambda_{X}^{\ast})\leqslant c(\mathbb{E}\Lambda_{X}^{\ast})^{2}
for any continuous log-concave random variable
X
. More precisely,
\Var(\Lambda^{\ast}_{X})<4
and
\mathbb{E}(\Lambda^{\ast}_{X})>0.1484
.

Proof.

We will rely on the fact, stressed again in Remark
3.3
, that for a continuous random variable
X
the random variable
q_{X}(X)
is equidistributed to
\min\{U,1-U\}
, where
U
is uniform on
[0,1]
. Using
q_{X}\leqslant e^{-\Lambda^{\ast}_{X}}
we write

\mathbb{E}(\Lambda^{\ast}_{X}(X)^{2})\leqslant\mathbb{E}(\log^{2}(q_{X}(X)))=2\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}\log^{2}(u)\,du=2+\log^{2}(2)+\log 2\simeq 3.866.

To lower bound
\mathbb{E}\Lambda^{\ast}_{X}
, we will use the inequality (
14
) for the optimal choice of
\varepsilon
. Differentiating, we can see that the right hand side of (
14
) is maximised when
\varepsilon=(-\log(2q(x)))^{-1}
. Let
A=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:q_{X}(x)<1/(2e)\}
. For every
x\in A
then we have that
\varepsilon=(-\log(2q(x)))^{-1}<1
and so (
14
) gives

\Lambda_{X}^{\ast}(x)\geqslant\log\frac{1}{2q_{X}(x)\log\frac{1}{2q_{X}(x)}}-1,\qquad x\in A.

We can now estimate

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Lambda^{\ast}_{X}(X)

\displaystyle\geqslant\int_{A}\Lambda^{\ast}_{X}(x)f_{X}(x)\,dx

\displaystyle\geqslant\int_{A}\left(\log\frac{1}{2q_{X}(x)\log\frac{1}{2q_{X}(x)}}-1\right)f_{X}(x)\,dx=2\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{2e}}\left(\log\frac{1}{2u\log\frac{1}{2u}}-1\right)\,du\geqslant 0.1484,

where the equality is due to the distribution of
q_{X}(X)
, and the Lemma follows.
∎

We now conclude the Proof of Theorem
2.4
: It is essentially enough to show that
\upbeta(\mu_{n})\to 0
with
n
. Due to independence,

\Var_{\mu_{n}}(\Lambda_{\mu_{n}}^{\ast})=\Var_{\mu_{n}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Lambda_{\lambda_{j}}^{\ast}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Var_{\lambda_{j}}(\Lambda_{\lambda_{j}}^{\ast})\leqslant nM,

where, by Lemma
5.5
,
M:=\max_{j}\Var_{\lambda_{j}}(\Lambda^{\ast}_{\lambda_{j}})<4
. Similarly,
\mathbb{E}\Lambda^{\ast}_{\mu_{n}}\geqslant nL
, where
L:=\min_{j}\mathbb{E}\Lambda^{\ast}_{\lambda_{j}}>0.14
. It follows that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\upbeta(\mu_{n})=\frac{\Var(\Lambda^{\ast}_{\mu_{n}})}{(\mathbb{E}\Lambda_{\mu_{n}}^{\ast})^{2}}\leqslant Cn^{-1}

for some absolute constant
C>0
. This estimate, together with (
28
), show that
\varrho(\mu_{n},\delta)\leqslant cn^{\frac{\alpha-1}{2}}
if
\delta\geqslant n^{-\alpha}
,
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
n
is sufficiently large. In particular,
(\mu_{n})
exhibits a sharp threshold around
\mathbb{E}\Lambda_{\mu_{n}}^{\ast}
, in the sense of Definition
2.1
.∎

We conclude this section with the proof of Proposition
1.4
, which relies on the argument used in Lemma
5.5
.

Proof of Proposition
1.4
.

In the proof of Lemma
5.5
we explained that the lower bound

\Lambda_{X}^{\ast}(x)\geqslant\log\frac{1}{2q_{X}(x)\log\frac{1}{2q_{X}(x)}}-1

holds for every
x
in the set
A=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:q_{X}(x)<1/(2e)\}
. We will combine again this with the fact that the random variable
q_{X}(X)
is equidistributed to
\min\{U,1-U\}
, where
U
is uniform on
[0,1]
. Using the trivial bound
e^{-\Lambda_{X}^{\ast}(x)}\leqslant 1
on
A^{c}
, we write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}e^{-\Lambda^{\ast}_{X}}

\displaystyle=\int_{A}e^{-\Lambda^{\ast}_{X}(x)}f_{X}(x)\,dx+\int_{A^{c}}e^{-\Lambda^{\ast}_{X}(x)}f_{X}(x)\,dx

\displaystyle\leqslant e\int_{A}2q_{X}(x)\log\frac{1}{2q_{X}(x)}f_{X}(x)\,dx+\mathbb{P}(q_{X}(X)\geqslant 1/(2e))

\displaystyle=e\cdot 2\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{2e}}2u\log\frac{1}{2u}\,du+\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{2e}\leqslant U\leqslant 1-\frac{1}{2e}\right)=\frac{3}{4e}+1-\frac{1}{e},

which is the promised estimate.
∎

5.2.
Rotationally invariant
\mathcal{LC}_{conv}
-random polytopes: Proof of Theorem
2.5

The proof of Theorem
2.5
will be based on the general estimate (
28
). To control
\upbeta(X)
, we will rely on the Poincaré inequality. We say that a random vector
X
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfies a Poincaré inuality with constant
\kappa>0
if for every locally Lipschitz and square integrable
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
,

(29)

\Var(f(X))\leqslant\frac{1}{\kappa}\mathbb{E}|\nabla f(X)|^{2}.

We know
[
6
]
that if
X
is isotropic and log-concave on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with a radially symmetric distribution, we can take
\kappa^{-1}\leqslant 13
in (
29
).

We can assume without loss of generality that
X
is isotropic. Our starting point is (
28
): There is an absolute constant
C>0
such that for every
\delta\in(0,1)
and sufficiently large
n
we have

\frac{\varrho(X,\delta)}{\mathbb{E}\Lambda^{\ast}_{X}}\leqslant C\sqrt{\frac{\upbeta(X)}{\delta}}\leqslant C^{\prime}\sqrt{\frac{\Var(\Lambda_{X}^{\ast})}{\delta n^{2}}},

since
\mathbb{E}\Lambda_{X}^{\ast}\geqslant cn
(due to (
12
), since we know that
L_{X}
is bounded in the rotational-invariant case, by the main result of
[
6
]
). To upper bound the latter, we use the assumption
f_{X}\in\mathcal{LC}_{conv}
. For any
x>0
let
t=(\Lambda_{X_{1}}^{\ast})^{\prime}(x)
. Then we have
0\leqslant\Lambda_{X_{1}}^{\ast}(x)=xt-\Lambda_{X_{1}}(t)
. By Theorem
4.9
however,
f_{X}\in\mathcal{LC}_{conv}
implies that
\Lambda_{X_{1}}(t)\geqslant\Lambda_{G_{1}}(t)=t^{2}/2
. It follows that
0\leqslant xt-t^{2}/2
, equivalently,
(\Lambda_{X_{1}}^{\ast})^{\prime}(x)\leqslant 2x
. Using this bound we get by the Poincaré inequality that

\displaystyle\Var(\Lambda_{X}^{\ast})\leqslant 13\cdot\mathbb{E}|(\Lambda_{X_{1}}^{\ast})^{\prime}(|X|)|^{2}\leqslant 52\cdot\mathbb{E}|X|^{2}=52n.

It follows then that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\varrho(X,\delta)}{\mathbb{E}\Lambda^{\ast}_{X}}=0
for any
\delta\geqslant n^{-\eta}
,
\eta\in(0,1)
(
\varrho(X,\delta)
is again of the order of
n^{\frac{\eta-1}{2}}
for this choice of
\delta
).

Remark 5.6
.

We can verify that if
X
is such that
f_{X}\in\mathcal{LC}_{conv}
, then
\mathbb{E}\Lambda^{\ast}_{X}
is of the order of
n
. On the one hand, by Lemma
4.6
and Lemma
4.7
it follows that
x\mapsto\Lambda^{\ast}_{X_{1}}(\sqrt{x})
is a concave function. Assuming that
X
is isotropic, it follows that
\mathbb{E}\Lambda^{\ast}_{X_{1}}(R)\leqslant\Lambda^{\ast}_{X_{1}}(\sqrt{n})
and by Theorem
4.9
the latter is at most
\Lambda_{G_{1}}^{\ast}(\sqrt{n})=n/2
. On the other hand, by Theorem
4.17
we have that
\mathbb{E}\Lambda^{\ast}_{X}(X)\geqslant\mathbb{E}\Lambda^{\ast}_{\mathcal{E}}(\mathcal{E})
. Recall that in (
19
) we verified that
\Lambda^{\ast}_{\mathcal{E}_{1}}(x)=\frac{n+1}{2}g(\frac{2x}{\sqrt{n+1}})
, were
g(y)=\sqrt{y^{2}+1}-1+\log(2(\sqrt{y^{2}+1}-1))
. By convexity of the Cramér transform then,

\mathbb{E}\Lambda^{\ast}_{X}\geqslant\mathbb{E}\Lambda_{\mathcal{E}_{1}}^{\ast}(|{\mathcal{E}}|)\geqslant\Lambda_{\mathcal{E}_{1}}^{\ast}(\mathbb{E}|{\mathcal{E}}|)=\Lambda_{\mathcal{E}_{1}}^{\ast}(\frac{n}{\sqrt{n+1}})=\frac{n+1}{2}g(2n).

Since
g
is increasing, we eventually get that every rotationally invariant log-concave random vector
X
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
f_{X}\in\mathcal{LC}_{conv}
satisfies

(\sqrt{2}-1+\log(2(\sqrt{2}-1)))\cdot\frac{n+1}{2}\leqslant\mathbb{E}\Lambda^{\ast}_{X}\leqslant\frac{n}{2}.

Remark 5.7
.

An example of families of measures covered by Theorem
2.5
is the exponential distribution
\mathcal{E}
with density proportional to
e^{-|x|}
, considered in Section
4
, and more generally densities proportional to
e^{-|x|^{p}}
,
p\in(0,2)
or Gamma-type densities proportional to
|x|^{\alpha-1}e^{-|x|}
,
0<\alpha<n
.

5.3.
Beta polytopes: Proof of Theorem
2.6

Recall that given
\beta>-1
,
\mu_{n,\beta}
is the probability distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with density
f_{n,\beta}(x)=c_{n,\beta}(1-|x|^{2})^{\beta}\mathds{1}_{B_{2}^{n}}(x)
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
c_{n,\beta}=\frac{\Gamma\left(\beta+n/2+1\right)}{\pi^{\frac{n}{2}}\Gamma(\beta+1)}
. For the proof of Theorem
2.6
, it is again essentially enough to show that
\upbeta(\mu_{n,\beta})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. Given Theorem
5.4
, it is enough to estimate
\uptau(\mu_{n,\beta})
. We work in a similar fashion as in
[
13
]
; by Lemma 2.2 in
[
7
]
we know that
q_{\mu_{n,\beta}}(x)=(1-x^{2})^{\beta+\frac{n+1}{2}}h(x,n,\beta)
for every
x\in(0,1)
, for a function
h
that satisfies

(30)

\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}\sqrt{\beta+\frac{n}{2}+1}}\leqslant h(x,n,\beta)\leqslant\frac{1}{2x\sqrt{\pi}\sqrt{\beta+\frac{n}{2}}}.

Using these bounds, calculations of the integrals involved lead us to the following Lemma, whose proof we defer for the end of the section. We use here the standard notation
\psi(x)=\frac{\Gamma^{\prime}(x)}{\Gamma(x)}
.

Lemma 5.8
.

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
-1<\beta\leqslant c_{1}n
for some constant
c_{1}>0
,

\mathbb{E}\omega_{\mu_{n,\beta}}=\left(\beta+\frac{n+1}{2}\right)\left(\psi\left(\frac{n}{2}+\beta+1\right)-\psi\left(\beta+1\right)\right)+O(\log(\beta+n))

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\omega_{\mu_{n,\beta}}^{2}=\left(\beta+\frac{n+1}{2}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\left(\left(\psi\left(\frac{n}{2}+\beta+1\right)-\psi(\beta+1)\right)^{2}+\psi^{\prime}(\beta+1)-\psi^{\prime}\left(\frac{n}{2}+\beta+1\right)\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 227.62204pt+O(n\log^{2}(\beta+n)).

Lemma
5.8
grants us that

\mathbb{E}\uptau(\mu_{n,\beta})=O\left(\frac{\psi^{\prime}(\beta+1)-\psi^{\prime}\left(\frac{n}{2}+\beta+1\right)}{\left(\psi\left(\frac{n}{2}+\beta+1\right)-\psi(\beta+1)\right)^{2}}\right).

For simplicity, assume that
n
is an even integer. Then

(31)

\psi\left(\frac{n}{2}+\beta+1\right)-\psi(\beta+1)=-\sum_{k=1}^{n/2}\frac{1}{\beta+k},

and since

\sum_{k=1}^{n/2}\frac{1}{\beta+k}\geqslant\int_{1}^{\frac{n}{2}+1}\frac{1}{\beta+x}\,dx=\log\left(\frac{n}{2}+\beta+1\right)-\log(\beta+1),

it follows that
\mathbb{E}\uptau(\mu_{n,\beta})\leqslant c\psi^{\prime}(\beta+1)\log^{-2}n
for some absolute constant
c>0
, if
n
is big enough. The estimate for the order of growth of
\mathbb{E}\Lambda^{\ast}_{\mu_{n,\beta}}
follows again immediately by Lemma
5.8
, since
\mathbb{E}\Lambda^{\ast}\sim\mathbb{E}\omega
. The upper bound on
\varrho
is a consequence of the latter, since the maximum in the statement of Theorem
5.2
is then attained by the
\sqrt{\upbeta(\mu_{n,\beta})/\delta}
term, combined with Theorem
5.4
and our upper estimate of
\mathbb{E}\uptau(\mu_{n,\beta})
.

It remains to prove Lemma
5.8
.

Proof of Lemma
5.8
.

Since

B(x,y)=\int_{0}^{1}s^{x-1}(1-s)^{y-1}\,ds=\frac{\Gamma(x)\Gamma(y)}{\Gamma(x+y)},

we can see that for any
k,l=0,1,2,\ldots
,

(32)

\int_{0}^{1}s^{x-1}(1-s)^{y-1}\log^{k}(s)\log^{l}(1-s)\,ds=\frac{\partial^{k}}{\partial x^{k}}\frac{\partial^{l}}{\partial y^{l}}B(x,y).

This lets us compute the integrals involved in
\mathbb{E}\omega_{\mu_{n,\beta}}
and
\mathbb{E}\omega^{2}_{\mu_{n,\beta}}
in terms of the derivatives of the Beta function. We start with

\mathbb{E}\omega_{\mu_{n,\beta}}=b_{n,\beta}\int_{0}^{1}s^{\frac{n}{2}-1}(1-s)^{\beta}\log(1/q_{\mu_{n,\beta}}(\sqrt{s}))\,ds,

where here and for the rest of the proof we denote
b_{n,\beta}=(B(n/2,\beta+1))^{-1}
. Recall that

-\log q(\sqrt{s})=-\left(\beta+\frac{n}{2}+1\right)\log(1-s)-\log h(\sqrt{s},n,\beta),

where
h
satisfies (
30
). We compute

(33)

\int_{0}^{1}s^{\frac{n}{2}-1}(1-s)^{\beta}\log(1-s)\,ds=\frac{\partial B(n/2,y)}{\partial y}\Big|_{y=\beta+1}=b_{n,\beta}^{-1}(\psi(\beta+1)-\psi(n/2+\beta+1)),

and

(34)

\int_{0}^{1}s^{\frac{n}{2}-1}(1-s)^{\beta}\log(s)\,ds=\frac{\partial B(x,\beta+1)}{\partial x}\Big|_{x=\frac{n}{2}}=b_{n,\beta}^{-1}(\psi(n/2)-\psi(n/2+\beta+1)).

By the latter and (
30
), we can check that the term
b_{n,\beta}\int_{0}^{1}s^{\frac{n}{2}-1}(1-s)^{\beta}(-\log h(\sqrt{s},n,\beta))\,ds
is lower bounded by
\log(2\sqrt{\pi}\sqrt{\beta+n/2})+\frac{1}{2}(\psi(n/2)-\psi(n/2+\beta+1))
and upper bounded by
\log(2\sqrt{\pi}\sqrt{n/2+\beta+1})
. The assumption
\beta\leqslant c_{1}n
grants us that
\psi(n/2)-\psi(n/2+\beta+1)\to 0
with
n
, and putting things together we eventually get to

\mathbb{E}\omega_{\mu_{n,\beta}}=\left(\beta+\frac{n}{2}+1\right)\left(\psi\left(\frac{n}{2}+\beta+1\right)-\psi(\beta+1)\right)+O(\log(\beta+n/2)).

For the second moment, we will have two consider integration of the terms
\log^{2}(1-s)
,
\log(1-s)\log h(\sqrt{s},n,\beta)
and
\log^{2}h(\sqrt{s},n,\beta)
against the Beta density. We first use the
(k=0,l=2)
-case of (
32
) to arrive at

b_{n,\beta}\int_{0}^{1}s^{\frac{n}{2}-1}(1-s)^{\beta}\log^{2}(1-s)\,ds=(\psi(\beta+1)-\psi(n/2+\beta+1))^{2}+\psi^{\prime}(\beta+1)-\psi^{\prime}(n/2+\beta+1).

For the second term we combine the upper bound
h(\sqrt{s},n,\beta)^{-1}\leqslant 2\sqrt{\pi}\sqrt{\beta+n/2}
with the computation (
33
) to get

b_{n,\beta}\int_{0}^{1}s^{\frac{n}{2}-1}(1-s)^{\beta}\log(1-s)\log h\,ds\leqslant\log(2\sqrt{\pi}\sqrt{\beta+n/2})(\psi(n/2+\beta+1)-\psi(\beta+1)).

Finally, to upper bound the
\log^{2}h
term we check with the aid of (
30
) that

\log^{2}h(\sqrt{s},n,\beta)\leqslant\log^{2}(2\sqrt{\pi}\sqrt{n/2+\beta+1})+\log^{2}(2\sqrt{\pi s}\sqrt{n/2+\beta}),

and using the
(k=2,l=0)
-case of (
32
) we can verify that

\displaystyle b_{n,\beta}\int_{0}^{1}s^{\frac{n}{2}-1}(1-s)^{\beta}\log^{2}h\,ds\leqslant

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt\leqslant\frac{1}{4}\left((\psi(n/2)-\psi(n/2+\beta+1))^{2}+\psi^{\prime}(n/2)-\psi^{\prime}(n/2+\beta+1)\right)+c\log n.

Putting everything together, and checking that the estimates for the last two terms are at most
O(\log^{2}(\beta+n/2))
(recall (
31
)), we eventually get that indeed

\mathbb{E}\omega_{\mu_{n,\beta}}^{2}=\left(\beta+\frac{n+1}{2}\right)^{2}\left(\left(\psi\left(\frac{n}{2}+\beta+1\right)-\psi(\beta+1)\right)^{2}+\psi^{\prime}(\beta+1)-\psi^{\prime}\left(\frac{n}{2}+\beta+1\right)\right),

modulo a
O((\beta+n/2)\log^{2}(\beta+n/2))
-term.
∎
