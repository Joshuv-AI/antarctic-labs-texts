Mean-Biased Processes for Balanced Allocations Some results from the paper were presented at SODA 2022 [ 34 ] .
Dimitrios Los, Thomas Sauerwald, John Sylvester

Abstract
We introduce a new class of balanced allocation processes which bias towards underloaded bins (those with load below the mean load) either by skewing the probability by which a bin is chosen for an allocation ( probability bias ), or alternatively, by adding more balls to an underloaded bin ( weight bias ). A prototypical process satisfying the probability bias condition is Mean-Thinning : At each round, we sample one bin and if it is underloaded, we allocate one ball; otherwise, we allocate one ball to a second bin sample. Versions of this process have been in use since at least 1986. An example of a process, introduced by us, which satisfies the weight bias condition is Twinning : At each round, we only sample one bin. If the bin is underloaded, then we allocate two balls; otherwise, we allocate only one ball. Our main result is that for any process with a probability or weight bias, with high probability the gap between maximum and minimum load is logarithmic in the number of bins. This result holds for any number of allocated balls (heavily loaded case), covers many natural processes that relax the Two-Choice process, and we also prove it is tight for many such processes, including Mean-Thinning and Twinning . Our analysis employs a delicate interplay between linear, quadratic and exponential potential functions. It also hinges on a phenomenon we call “mean quantile stabilization”, which holds in greater generality than our framework and may be of independent interest. Keywords— Balls-into-bins, balanced allocations, potential functions, heavily loaded, gap bounds, maximum load, thinning, two-choices, weighted balls. AMS MSC 2010— 68W20, 68W27, 68W40, 60C05

Introduction

We consider the sequential allocation of
m
balls (jobs or data items) to
n
bins (servers or memory cells), by allowing each ball to choose from a set of randomly sampled bins. The goal is to allocate balls efficiently, while also keeping the load vector balanced. The
balls-into-bins
framework has found numerous applications in hashing, load balancing, routing, but also has connections to more theoretical topics such as randomized rounding or pseudorandomness (we refer to the surveys
[
39
]
and
[
46
]
for more details).

A classical algorithm is the
d
-Choice
process introduced by Azar, Broder, Karlin, Upfal
[
8
]
and Karp, Luby, and Meyer auf der Heide
[
29
]
, where at each round, we sample
d\geqslant 1
bins uniformly at random and then allocate the ball to the least loaded of the
d
sampled bins. It is well-known that for the
One-Choice
process (
d=1
), the
gap
between the maximum and mean load is
\Theta\bigr(\sqrt{(m/n)\cdot\log n}\bigr)
for
m\geqslant n\log n
. In particular, this gap grows significantly as
m/n\rightarrow\infty
, which is called the
heavily loaded case
. For
Two-Choice
(
d=2
), it was proved
[
8
]
that the gap is only
\log_{2}\log n+\mathcal{O}(1)
for
m=n
. This result was generalized by Berenbrink, Czumaj, Steger and Vöcking
[
11
]
who proved that the same guarantee also holds for
m\geqslant n
, in other words, even as
m/n\rightarrow\infty
, the difference between the maximum and mean load remains bounded by a slowly growing function in
n
that is independent of
m
.
This dramatic improvement of
Two-Choice
over
One-Choice
is widely known as the “power of two choices” paradigm.

The importance of this paradigm was recently recognized in the 2020 ACM Paris Kanellakis Theory and Practice award
[
7
]
. However, in some real-world applications the original
Two-Choice
process has been shown to underperform and so variants of
Two-Choice
are being used instead (e.g.
[
40
,
36
,
47
]
). These variants relax the assumptions of
Two-Choice
and can thus be regarded as more
sample-efficient
and
robust
.

As noted in
[
36
]
, in some real-word systems one important shortcoming of
Two-Choice
are its communication requirements:

More importantly, the
[Two-Choice]
algorithm requires communication between dispatchers and processors at the time of job assignment. The communication time is on the critical path, hence contributes to the increase in response time.

An important family of processes,
Two-Thinning
attempts to address this issue. In this process, each ball first samples a bin uniformly at random and if its load is below some threshold, the ball is allocated to that sample. Otherwise, the ball is allocated to a
second
bin sampled uniformly at random, without inspecting its load. These processes have the advantage that they relax communication between the job dispatchers and servers (see
Fig.

1.1
).

was used by Eager, Lazowska and Zahorjan
[

and was analyzed by Iwama and Kawachi
[

]
and Feldheim and Gurel-Gurevich
[

]
in the lightly-loaded case (
m=\mathcal{O}(n)
). In
[
21
]
, the authors proved that for
m=n
, there is a
Two-Thinning
process using a threshold on the number of times a bin has been used as a first sample, which achieves a gap of
\mathcal{O}\big({\scriptstyle\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}}}\big)
. This process achieves a significant improvement over
One-Choice
and also uses a total of
(1+o(1))\cdot n
samples, which is an improvement over
Two-Choice
. Similar threshold processes have been studied in queuing
[
20
]
and discrepancy theory
[
19
]
.
For values of
m
sufficiently larger than
n
, some lower and upper bounds have been established for a more general class of
adaptive

Two-Thinning
processes
[
22
,
30
]
. Here, adaptive means that the choice of the threshold
may
depend on the entire load vector, which is not ideal from a practical perspective. Related to this line of research,
[
30
]
also analyzed a so-called
Quantile
process, which is a version of
Two-Thinning
where a ball is allocated to a second sample only if the first bin has a load which is at least the median load.

In
[
30
]
, an extension of
Two-Thinning
was studied, the
k
-Threshold
processes, where for each ball two bins are sampled uniformly at random and
k
queries of the form “is the load of the bin at least
x
?” are sent to each. Then, the process allocates to the bin that was witnessed to be lesser loaded.

Extensions of
Two-Thinning
where up to
d
bins may be sampled have been studied in
[
30
,
23
,
37
]
. A similar class of adaptive sampling processes (where, depending on the loads of the samples so far, the ball may or may not sample another bin) was analyzed by Czumaj and Stemann
[
17
]
who proved tight results for
m=n
and by Berenbrink, Khodamoradi, Sauerwald and Stauffer
[
13
]
for some specific instances for
m\geqslant n
.

Another example of a process that reduces the number of samples is the
(1+\beta)
-process introduced by Mitzenmacher
[
37
]
, where each ball is allocated using
One-Choice
with probability
1-\beta
and otherwise is allocated using
Two-Choice
. Peres, Talwar and Wieder
[
42
]
proved that for any
\beta\in(0,1]
, the gap is only
\mathcal{O}\big(\frac{\log n}{\beta}\big)
. Hence, only a
small
fraction of
Two-Choice
rounds are enough to inherit the property of
Two-Choice
that the gap is independent of
m
. It was recently shown that this process asymptotically outperforms
Two-Choice
in the presence of outdated information
[
31
]
. The
(1+\beta)
-process has been used to analyze
population protocols

[
2
,
4
]
,
distributed data structures

[
3
,
5
]
and
online carpooling

[
27
]
. Another sample-efficient variant of
Two-Choice
is the
(k,d)\textsc{-Choice}
process allocates to the
k
lightest out of the
d
sampled bins
[
41
]
.

From a more technical perspective, apart from analyzing a large class of natural allocation processes, an important question is to understand how sensitive the gap is to changes in the probability allocation vector
p^{t}
of the process, where
p_{i}^{t}
gives the probability to allocate to the
i
-th heaviest bin in round
t
. To this end,
[
42
]
formulated general conditions on the probability allocation vector, which, when satisfied in all rounds, imply a small gap bound. These were then applied not only to the
(1+\beta)
-process, but also to analyze
graphical balanced allocations
where an edge of a graph is chosen uniformly at random and the ball is allocated to the least loaded of its two endpoints. Other works which study perturbations on the probability allocation vector are: allocations on
hypergraphs

[
26
]
, balls-into-bins with
correlated choices

[
45
]
; or balls-into-bins with
hash functions

[
14
]
.

Our Results

We introduce a general framework that allows us to deduce a small gap independent of
m
for a large family of processes, but also opens the door for the study of novel allocation processes such as
Twinning
(to be defined below). To ensure that an allocation process produces a balanced load vector, we could bias the allocation towards underloaded bins (bins whose load is below the mean) by skewing the probability by which a bin is chosen for an allocation (
probability bias
), or alternatively, we could add more balls to a bin if it is underloaded (
weight bias
). We call both of these processes
Mean-Biased
.

Note that a small bias in the probability allocation vector can be achieved in various ways, for example, by taking a
second
bin sample:
(i)
in each round (
Two-Choice
), or
(ii)
in each round with some probability
\beta
(
(1+\beta)
-process), or
(iii)
in each round dependent on the load of the first sample (
Two-Thinning
). Regarding the weight bias, as pointed out in
[
38
]
, allocating consecutive balls to the same bin is very natural in applications like sticky routing
[
25
]
or load balancing, where balls may often arrive in bursts. To this end, we study
Twinning
which allocates two balls if the bin is underloaded and one ball if it is overloaded.

We now describe two general conditions, which state that the probability allocation vector is biased towards
underloaded
bins, and we allocate fewer balls to
overloaded
bins. Neglecting some technical details (see
Section

3
), for
\delta^{t}\in(0,1)
being the quantile of the mean load in round
t
, meaning there are
\delta^{t}n
overloaded bins, the two conditions are (roughly) as follows:

Condition
\mathcal{P}_{2}
: At each round, the probability of allocating to any fixed underloaded bin is
\frac{1}{n}\cdot(1+\Omega(\delta^{t}))
, while to any fixed overloaded bin is
\frac{1}{n}\cdot(1-\Omega(1-\delta^{t}))
.

Condition
\mathcal{W}_{2}
: At each round, if an underloaded bin is chosen for allocation, allocate (a constant number) more balls than if an overloaded bin is chosen.

As our main result we prove a gap bound of
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
whenever a process satisfies
at least one
of these two
mean-biasing
conditions (in addition to some mild and general conditions). It turns out that
\mathcal{P}_{2}
is satisfied by
Mean-Thinning
(i.e., the
Two-Thinning
process with threshold being the mean load), while
\mathcal{W}_{2}
is satisfied by
Twinning
; thus for both of these processes a gap bound of
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
follows immediately. The result for
Twinning
gives an example of a process with gap
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
, which is also more sample-efficient than
One-Choice
.

The result for
Mean-Thinning
extends to any
\textsc{Relative}\text{-}\textsc{Threshold}(f(n))
process which uses a threshold of
\frac{t}{n}+f(n)
for the
offset function

f(n)\geqslant 0
, via a coupling argument, proving that w.h.p.
\Gap(m)=\mathcal{O}(f(n)+\log n)
.
Finally, the framework also applies to the
(1+\beta)
-process with any constant
\beta\in(0,1)
and a relaxation of
Mean-Thinning
which we call the
(1+\zeta)
-process.

To the best of our knowledge, most of the related work on balls-into-bins focuses on a small number (usually one or two) allocation processes, and analyzes their gap (with
[
42
,
44
]
being exceptions). Here we develop a framework that captures a variety of existing and new processes.

Our analysis, especially of
Twinning
, bears some resemblance to the
weighted balls-into-bins setting

[
43
,
42
,
12
]
, but there, the weights of the balls are drawn from some distribution
before
the bin is sampled and there is a time-invariant probability bias towards lightly loaded bins (e.g., as in
(1+\beta)
-process and
Two-Choice
). However, in our model the weights
depend
on the load of the sampled bin.

The
Twinning
process can also be applied to the graphical balanced allocations on
d
-regular graphs for any
d\geqslant 2
(see
3.5
). More specifically, the
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
bound from the classical setting (corresponding to the complete graph) still applies even for sparse regular graphs, like the cycle where
Two-Choice
is conjectured to have an
\Omega(\operatorname{poly}(n))
gap
[
9
,
6
]
.

An extension of
Twinning
, called
Packing
, was studied by the authors of this paper in
[
35
]
, where an underloaded bin is “filled” until it becomes overloaded, this process also achieves an
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
gap.

Overview of Proof Techniques

Parts of our analysis are based on the use of the exponential potential pioneered in
[
42
]
. However, in
[
42
]
time/load independent conditions on the processes ensure that the potential
drops
in expectation in every round in every round, whereas for our more general conditions there exist load vectors where the potential may
increase
in expectation, even when it is
large
(
B.1
). This precludes any standard application of the approach in
[
42
]
and presents our main challenge. To overcome this challenge we introduce two new ideas/techniques, which since the conference version of this paper
[
34
]
have already found further application
[
32
,
33
]
. The first is a series of quantitative relationships between the linear, quadratic, and exponential potential functions; this helps overcome the issue of bad configurations “blocking” progress. Our second innovation is the phenomenon of “Mean Quantile Stabilization” where we show that any round with
\mathcal{O}(n)
linear potential is followed by a
\Theta(n)
length interval in which for a constant fraction of rounds the ratio of overload/underload bins is bounded away from
0
or
1
; this helps maintain progress once it is “unblocked”.

To see in more detail how these two components come together, we observe that in any round where the process has a
stable
quantile, i.e.,
\delta^{t}\in[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]
for some constant
\varepsilon\in\big(0,\frac{1}{2}\big)
, the exponential potential decreases in expectation; and prove that quantile of the mean load
self-stabilizes
. This is done by first proving that the
quadratic potential
drops as long as the
absolute value potential
is
\Omega(n)
(
6.2
). From this, we can deduce that the absolute value potential has to be
\mathcal{O}(n)
at some point. Then we prove that once this happens, the quantile of the mean load will be stable for sufficiently many rounds (
5.1
). The final ingredient is to prove that in rounds where the mean quantile is stable, the exponential potential drops by a sufficiently large factor in expectation, while in other rounds it increases by a smaller factor. A more detailed overview is given in
Section

4
and the details of the proof in
Sections

5
,
6
and
7
.

Notation and Balanced Allocation Processes

We consider processes that sequentially allocate balls into
n
bins, labeled
[n]:=\{1,\ldots,n\}
. By
x^{t}
we denote the
load vector
of the
n
bins at round
t=0,1,2,\ldots
(the state after
t
allocations have been completed). Initially, we start with the empty load vector
x^{0}:=(0,\ldots,0)
, unless we explicitly state otherwise. In many parts of the analysis, we will assume a labeling of the
n
bins so that their loads at round
t
are ordered non-increasingly, i.e.,

x_{1}^{t}\geqslant x_{2}^{t}\geqslant\cdots\geqslant x_{n}^{t}.

Unlike many of the standard balls-into-bins processes, some of our processes may allocate more than one ball in a single allocation. To this end, we define
W^{t}:=\sum_{i\in[n]}x_{i}^{t}
as the total number of balls allocated in the first
t
allocations (for
Two-Thinning
we allocate one ball per allocation, so
W^{t}=t
, however for
Twinning

W^{t}\geqslant t
).
We will also use
w^{t+1}:=W^{t+1}-W^{t}
to denote the number of balls allocated in the
(t+1)
-th allocation.

We define the
gap
as

\Gap(t):=\max_{i\in[n]}x_{i}^{t}-\frac{W^{t}}{n},

which is the difference between the maximum load and mean load
1

It is common in the literature to focus on this difference, rather than the difference between maximum and minimum load; however, our results for
Mean-Biased
processes apply to both.

at round
t
. When the process
\mathcal{Q}
is not clear from the context, we write
\Gap_{\mathcal{Q}}(t)
for the gap and
x_{\mathcal{Q}}^{t}
for the load vector of the process.
Finally, for any round
t\geqslant 0
we define the
normalized load
of a bin
i\in[n]
as:

y_{i}^{t}:=x_{i}^{t}-\frac{W^{t}}{n}.

Further, we define
\mathfrak{F}^{t}
to be the
filtration
corresponding to the first
t
allocations of the process (so in particular,
\mathfrak{F}^{t}
reveals
x^{t}
). Following
[

]
, let
p^{t}:=p^{t}(\mathfrak{F}^{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the
probability allocation vector
of the process, where for every
i\in[n]
,
p_{i}^{t}
is the probability that
(t+1)
-th allocation is to
i
-th heaviest bin.

Further, let
B_{+}^{t}:=\left\{i\in[n]\colon y_{i}^{t}\geqslant 0\right\}
be the set of
overloaded
bins, and
B_{-}^{t}:=[n]\setminus B_{+}^{t}
be the set of
underloaded
bins. Let
\delta^{t}:=|B_{+}^{t}|/n\in\big\{\frac{1}{n},\ldots,1\big\}
be the
mean quantile
corresponding to the mean load. Let
P_{+}^{t}:=\sum_{i\in B_{+}^{t}}p_{i}^{t}
and
P_{-}^{t}:=\sum_{i\in B_{-}^{t}}p_{i}^{t}
, which are the probabilities of allocating to an overloaded or underloaded bin respectively.

For two vectors
p
and
q
, we say that
p

majorizes

q
if
\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}\geqslant\sum_{i=1}^{k}q_{i},
for all
k\in[n]
. We denote this by
p\succeq q
. Typically we will be considering the majorization ordering between two (partial) probability allocation vectors, or sorted load vectors. For random variables
Y,Z
we say that
Y
is
stochastically smaller
than
Z
if
\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,Y\geqslant x\,\right]\leqslant\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,Z\geqslant x\,\right]
for all real
x
.

For some specific allocation processes considered in this work, we will also analyze the
sample efficiency
defined as
\eta^{m}:=W^{m}/S^{m}
, where
W^{m}
is the total number the balls allocated in the first
m
allocations, and
S^{m}
is the total number of uniform samples taken by the process in the first
m
allocations.

We begin with the definition of the classical
One-Choice
process.

One-Choice
Process:

Iteration:
For each round
t\geqslant 0
, sample one bin
i\in[n]
uniformly at random. Then, update:

x_{i}^{t+1}=x_{i}^{t}+1.

The
One-Choice
process has a
time-independent
probability allocation vector

p:=p^{t}=\left(\frac{1}{n},\ldots,\frac{1}{n}\right),

and it has a sample efficiency of
\eta^{m}=1
.
We proceed with the definition of the
Two-Choice
process
[
8
,
29
]
.

Two-Choice
Process:

Iteration:
For each round
t\geqslant 0
, sample two bins
i_{1},i_{2}\in[n]
, independently and uniformly at random. Let
i\in\{i_{1},i_{2}\}
be such that
x_{i}^{t}=\min\{x_{i_{1}}^{t},x_{i_{2}}^{t}\}
, favoring bins with higher indices in case of a tie. Then, update:

x_{i}^{t+1}=x_{i}^{t}+1.

The
Two-Choice
process also has a time-independent probability allocation vector
p:=p^{t}
, where

p_{i}:=\frac{2i-1}{n^{2}},\quad\text{for any }i\in[n],

and it has a sample efficiency of
\eta^{m}=\frac{1}{2}
.

Mixing
One-Choice
with
Two-Choice
rounds at a rate
\beta
, one obtains the
(1+\beta)
-process
[
42
]
:

(1+\beta)
-Process:

Parameter:
A
mixing factor

\beta\in(0,1]
.

Iteration:
For each round
t\geqslant 0
, with probability
\beta
allocate one ball via the
Two-Choice
process, otherwise allocate one ball via the
One-Choice
process.

The probability allocation vector for the
(1+\beta)
-process is a convex combination of the
One-Choice
and
Two-Choice
probability allocation vectors given by

p_{i}:=(1-\beta)\cdot\frac{1}{n}+\beta\cdot\frac{2i-1}{n^{2}},\quad\text{for any }i\in[n].

The following process has been studied by several authors
[
28
,
21
,
23
]
.

\textsc{Two-Thinning}(f(n,x^{t}))
Process:

Parameter:
A
threshold function

f(n,x^{t})\geqslant 0
.

Iteration:
For each round
t\geqslant 0
, sample two uniform bins
i_{1}
and
i_{2}
independently, and update:

\begin{cases}x_{i_{1}}^{t+1}=x_{i_{1}}^{t}+1&\mbox{if $x_{i_{1}}^{t}<f(n,x^{t})$},\\
x_{i_{2}}^{t+1}=x_{i_{2}}^{t}+1&\mbox{if $x_{i_{1}}^{t}\geqslant f(n,x^{t})$}.\end{cases}

In this work, we focus on processes that have a threshold that is
relative
to the mean load, i.e.,
f(n,x^{t})=\frac{t}{n}+f(n)
. These have the attractive property that they can be implemented just having knowledge of the mean load (instead of the entire load vector). The prototypical such process is
Mean-Thinning
(introduced in
Section

3.3
), which has threshold equal to the mean load, i.e.,
f(n)=0
.

The probability allocation vector for
Mean-Thinning
is given by

p^{t}:=\Bigg(\underbrace{\frac{\delta^{t}}{n},\ldots,\frac{\delta^{t}}{n}}_{\delta^{t}n\text{ bins}},\underbrace{\frac{1+\delta^{t}}{n},\ldots,\frac{1+\delta^{t}}{n}}_{(1-\delta^{t})\cdot n\text{ bins}}\Bigg).

Throughout the paper, we often make use of statements and inequalities which hold only for sufficiently large
n
. For simplicity, we do not state this explicitly.

3
Mean-Biased
Processes and Our Results

In this section, we begin by defining the conditions for
Mean-Biased
processes. Then, we present our main result for
Mean-Biased
processes.

Following this we define some typical
Mean-Biased
processes, including
Twinning
,
Mean-Thinning
and the
(1+\zeta)
-process, and show that they fall under the probability and weight biased subclasses. We summarize our lower bounds and upper bounds for these processes, showing that these are tight, and also consider the sample efficiency of these processes. We also consider the
\textsc{Relative}\text{-}\textsc{Threshold}(f(n))
processes with
f(n)>0
, which although are not
Mean-Biased
processes strictly speaking, we show that our general bound can be extended via a coupling to give tight gap bounds for any
f(n)\geqslant\log n
.

3.1
General
Mean-Biased
Framework and Bounds

First, we will start by defining some conditions on the probability allocation vector and on the weights of the balls allocated to underloaded and overloaded bins. Recall that for any
i\in[n]
,
p_{i}^{t}
is the probability of allocating to the
i
-th heaviest bin at round
t
.
As before,
p^{t}
may depend on the filtration
\mathfrak{F}^{t}
. Recall also that
B_{+}^{t}:=\{i\in[n]:y_{-}^{t}\geqslant 0\}
,
\delta^{t}:=|B_{+}^{t}|/n
, and
B_{-}^{t}:=[n]\setminus B_{+}^{t}
.

Condition
\mathcal{P}_{1}
: There exists an integer constant
k_{1}\geqslant 1
and a constant
k_{2}\in(0,1]
, such that for each round
t\geqslant 0
,

–

the overloaded bins satisfy

\displaystyle\max_{i\in B_{+}^{t}}p_{i}^{t}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{k_{1}}{n}\qquad

\displaystyle\text{and}\qquad\left(p_{1}^{t},\ldots,p_{\delta^{t}n}^{t}\right)

\displaystyle\preceq\left(\frac{1}{n},\ldots,\frac{1}{n}\right),

–

and the underloaded bins satisfy

\displaystyle\min_{i\in B_{-}^{t}}p_{i}^{t}

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{k_{2}}{n}\qquad

\displaystyle\text{and}\qquad\left(p_{n}^{t},\ldots,p_{\delta^{t}n+1}^{t}\right)

\displaystyle\succeq\left(\frac{1}{n},\ldots,\frac{1}{n}\right).

Condition
\mathcal{W}_{1}
: For each round
t\geqslant 0
, when bin
i\in[n]
is chosen for allocation,

–

if
y_{i}^{t}<0
, then allocate
w_{-}
balls to bin
i
,

–

if
y_{i}^{t}\geqslant 0
, then allocate
w_{+}
balls to bin
i
,

where
1\leqslant w_{+}\leqslant w_{-}
are constant integers
2
2

2

By scaling, this can be generalized to constant rational weights.
, i.e., independent of
t
and
n
.

Both conditions
\mathcal{P}_{1}
and
\mathcal{W}_{1}
are natural, but on their own they are not sufficient to establish a good bound on the gap, as the
One-Choice
process satisfies both conditions with
k_{1}:=k_{2}:=1
. Thus, we will require that processes satisfy at least one of the following two stronger versions of
\mathcal{P}_{1}
and
\mathcal{W}_{1}
:

Condition
\mathcal{P}_{2}
: There exists an integer constant
k_{1}\geqslant 1
and constants
k_{2},k_{3},k_{4}\in(0,1]
, such that for each round
t\geqslant 0
,

–

the overloaded bins satisfy

\displaystyle\max_{i\in B_{+}^{t}}p_{i}^{t}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{k_{1}}{n}

\displaystyle\quad\text{ and }\quad\left(p_{1}^{t},\ldots,p_{\delta^{t}n}^{t}\right)

\displaystyle\preceq\left(\frac{1}{n}-\frac{k_{3}\cdot(1-\delta^{t})}{n},\ldots,\frac{1}{n}-\frac{k_{3}\cdot(1-\delta^{t})}{n}\right),

–

and the underloaded bins satisfy

\displaystyle\min_{i\in B_{-}^{t}}p_{i}^{t}

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{k_{2}}{n}

\displaystyle\quad\text{ and }\quad\left(p_{n}^{t},\ldots,p_{\delta^{t}n+1}^{t}\right)\succeq\left(\frac{1}{n}+\frac{k_{4}\cdot\delta^{t}}{n},\ldots,\frac{1}{n}+\frac{k_{4}\cdot\delta^{t}}{n}\right).

Condition
\mathcal{W}_{2}
: This is as Condition
\mathcal{W}_{1}
, but additionally we have the strict inequality:
w_{+}<w_{-}
. Also, we assume that for each round
t\geqslant 0
,

\left(p_{n}^{t},\ldots,p_{\delta^{t}n+1}^{t}\right)\succeq\left(\frac{P_{-}^{t}}{|B_{-}^{t}|},\ldots,\frac{P_{-}^{t}}{|B_{-}^{t}|}\right),

where
P_{-}^{t}:=\sum_{i\in B_{-}^{t}}p_{i}^{t}
is the sum of allocation probabilities over the underloaded bins.

We refer to a balanced allocation process as
Mean-Biased
if it satisfies
{\hyperlink{p2}{\mathcal{P}_{2}}}\cap{\hyperlink{w1}{\mathcal{W}_{1}}}
or
{\hyperlink{p1}{\mathcal{P}_{1}}}\cap{\hyperlink{w2}{\mathcal{W}_{2}}}
, that is it has a probability or weight bias respectively. Our main result proves a logarithmic upper bound on the gap for any
Mean-Biased
process in the heavily loaded case.

Theorem 3.1

(Main Theorem)
.

Consider any
{\hyperlink{p2}{\mathcal{P}_{2}}}\cap{\hyperlink{w1}{\mathcal{W}_{1}}}
-process or
{\hyperlink{p1}{\mathcal{P}_{1}}}\cap{\hyperlink{w2}{\mathcal{W}_{2}}}
-process. Then, there exists a constant
\kappa>0
such that for any round
m\geqslant 0
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\max_{i\in[n]}\left|x_{i}^{m}-\frac{W^{m}}{n}\right|\leqslant\kappa\log n\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-4};

so in particular,
\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Gap(m)\leqslant\kappa\log n\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-4}
.

To prove a matching lower bound we add the following condition:

•

Condition
\mathcal{P}_{3}
: For any
\varepsilon\in\big(0,\frac{1}{2}\big)
there exists a
k_{5}:=k_{5}(\varepsilon)\in(0,1]
such that for each round
t\geqslant 0
with
\delta^{t}\in[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]
, the probability allocation vector
p^{t}
satisfies,

\min_{i\in[n]}p_{i}^{t}\geqslant\frac{k_{5}}{n}.

This condition is satisfied for many natural processes, including
Twinning
,
Mean-Thinning
, and the
(1+\beta)
-process with constant
\beta\in(0,1)
. Assuming condition
\mathcal{P}_{3}
in addition to the
Mean-Biased
conditions allows us to prove a tight lower bound.

Theorem 3.2
.

Consider any
{\hyperlink{p2}{\mathcal{P}_{2}}}\cap{\hyperlink{w1}{\mathcal{W}_{1}}}
-process or
{\hyperlink{p1}{\mathcal{P}_{1}}}\cap{\hyperlink{w2}{\mathcal{W}_{2}}}
-process, which also satisfies condition
\mathcal{P}_{3}
. Then there exists a constant
\kappa>0
such that

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Gap(\kappa n\log n)\geqslant\kappa\log n\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-1/2}.

In the next subsection we give some examples of processes for which our bounds hold, and some processes which are not
Mean-Biased
but are sufficiently close that we can cover them with our bounds. First we discuss the meaning behind the
Mean-Biased
conditions.

The rationale behind condition
\mathcal{P}_{2}
is that we wish to slightly bias the probability allocation vector
p^{t}
towards underloaded bins at each round
t
. However, it is natural to assume that this influence is limited by a process that samples, say, at most two bins independently and uniformly, and then allocates balls to the least loaded of the two. Concretely, if a process takes
two
independent and uniform bin samples at each round, the probability of picking two overloaded bins equals

\bigl(\frac{|B_{+}^{t}|}{n}\bigr)^{2}.

Hence by averaging, for each overloaded bin
i\in B_{+}^{t}

p_{i}^{t}\geqslant\left(\frac{|B_{+}^{t}|}{n}\right)^{2}\cdot\frac{1}{|B_{+}^{t}|}=\frac{|B_{+}^{t}|}{n^{2}}=\frac{\delta^{t}}{n}.

The relaxation of the first constraint in
\mathcal{P}_{2}
by taking a strict convex combination of
\frac{1}{n}
and
\frac{\delta^{t}}{n}

ensures some slack, for instance, it allows the framework to cover the
(1+\zeta)
-process, where we perform a
One-Choice
allocation with some constant probability
\eta\in(0,1]
, and otherwise we perform an allocation following the
Mean-Thinning
process (see
3.10
for details).
Similarly, for any process which takes at most two uniform samples, by averaging for any underloaded bin
i\in B_{-}^{t}

\displaystyle p_{i}^{t}\leqslant\frac{1-\frac{|B_{+}^{t}|^{2}}{n^{2}}}{|B_{-}^{t}|}=\frac{(n-|B_{+}^{t}|)\cdot(n+|B_{+}^{t}|)}{n^{2}|B_{-}^{t}|}=\frac{1}{n}+\frac{|B_{+}^{t}|}{n^{2}}=\frac{1}{n}+\frac{\delta^{t}}{n}.

Finally, we remark that
\mathcal{P}_{2}
resembles the framework of
[
42
, Equation 2]
, where

p^{t}_{i}=p_{i}
is non-decreasing in
i
and
p_{n/3}\leqslant\frac{1-4\varepsilon}{n}
and
p_{2n/3}\geqslant\frac{1+4\varepsilon}{n}
holds for some
0<\varepsilon<1/4
(not necessarily constant). In contrast to that, for constants
k_{3},k_{4}>0
, the conditions in
\mathcal{P}_{2}
are relaxed as they only imply such a bias if
\delta^{t}
is bounded away from
0
and
1
, which may not hold in all rounds.

We note that the requirements on the probability allocation vectors in
\mathcal{P}_{1}
and
\mathcal{P}_{2}
are a relaxation of those in the conference version of this paper
[
34
]
. Previously we assumed that the probability allocation vector is non-decreasing, which implies the current majorization condition. Also more restrictive bounds of
\max_{i\in B_{+}^{t}}p_{i}^{t}\leqslant\frac{1}{n}
and
\min_{i\in B_{-}^{t}}p_{i}^{t}\geqslant\frac{1}{n}
were assumed. As we shall see this relaxation allows our new framework to include the
(1+\beta)
-process with constant
\beta\in(0,1)
(
3.8
) and the
k\textsc{-Relative}\text{-}\textsc{Threshold}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{k})
processes with
f_{k}=0
(
3.9
). Further, the bound on the maximum probability to allocate to any single bin is satisfied by any process that takes
d
uniform and independent samples in each round with
k_{1}:=d
. For instance,
Two-Choice
, the
(1+\beta)
-process and
Two-Thinning
processes, all satisfy this with
k_{1}:=2
.

Lemma 3.8
.

The
(1+\beta)
-process with any constant
\beta\in(0,1)
satisfies
\mathcal{P}_{2}
and
\mathcal{W}_{1}
.

Proof.

The
(1+\beta)
-process trivially satisfies
\mathcal{W}_{1}
with
w_{-},w_{+}:=1
. To verify
\mathcal{P}_{2}
, recall that

p_{i}:=(1-\beta)\cdot\frac{1}{n}+\beta\cdot\frac{2i-1}{n^{2}}.

Hence,
\max_{i\in[n]}p_{i}\leqslant\frac{2}{n}
and
\min_{i\in[n]}p_{i}\geqslant\frac{1-\beta}{n}
, and so
k_{1}:=2
and
k_{2}:=1-\beta
. Now, for the majorization condition, note that the prefix sum is given by

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\delta^{t}n}p_{i}

\displaystyle=\delta^{t}n\cdot(1-\beta)\cdot\frac{1}{n}+\beta\cdot\frac{(\delta^{t}n)^{2}}{n^{2}}=\delta^{t}-\beta\cdot\delta^{t}\cdot(1-\delta^{t}).

This is the same prefix sum as for the uniform vector which has all entries being equal to
\frac{1}{n}-\beta\cdot(1-\delta^{t})
. Since the probability allocation vector of the
(1+\beta)
-process is non-decreasing in
i\in[n]
, it follows that for
k_{3}:=\beta
,

\left(p_{1},\ldots,p_{\delta^{t}n}\right)\preceq\left(\frac{1}{n}-\frac{k_{3}\cdot(1-\delta^{t})}{n},\ldots,\frac{1}{n}-\frac{k_{3}\cdot(1-\delta^{t})}{n}\right).

The argument for the suffix sum is analogous. We have,

\sum_{i=\delta^{t}n+1}^{n}p_{i}=1-\delta^{t}+\beta\cdot\delta^{t}\cdot(1-\delta^{t}).

This is the same suffix sum as for the uniform vector
\frac{1}{n}+\beta\cdot\delta^{t}
. Since the probability allocation vector
p_{i}
is non-decreasing in
i\in[n]
, it follows that with
k_{4}:=\beta
,

\left(p_{n},\ldots,p_{\delta^{t}n+1}\right)\succeq\left(\frac{1}{n}+\frac{k_{4}\cdot\delta^{t}}{n},\ldots,\frac{1}{n}+\frac{k_{4}\cdot\delta^{t}}{n}\right).\qed

f(n)\geqslant 0

In the previous section, we showed that a special case of
k\textsc{-Relative}\text{-}\textsc{Threshold}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{k})
process with
f_{k}=0
is
Mean-Biased
. We now turn our attention to instances with
k=1
but the offset function
f_{1}(n)=f(n)
is arbitrary non-negative. This generalizes
Mean-Thinning
, and for
f(n)>0
the process is not
Mean-Biased
as there are load configurations where the processes may not bias allocations away from all overloaded bins.

\textsc{Relative}\text{-}\textsc{Threshold}(f(n))
Process:

Parameter:
An
offset function

f(n)\geqslant 0
.

Iteration:
For each round
t\geqslant 0
, sample two bins
i_{1}
and
i_{2}
independently and uniformly at random, and update:

\begin{cases}x_{i_{1}}^{t+1}=x_{i_{1}}^{t}+1&\mbox{if $x_{i_{1}}^{t}<\frac{t}{n}+f(n)$},\\
x_{i_{2}}^{t+1}=x_{i_{2}}^{t}+1&\mbox{if $x_{i_{1}}^{t}\geqslant\frac{t}{n}+f(n)$}.\end{cases}

Similarly to
Mean-Thinning
, the
Relative
-
Threshold
processes are appealing, as they require just knowledge (or estimate) of the mean load, which changes only every
n
rounds. For this process we prove the following upper bound on the gap.

Theorem 3.12
.

There exists a constant
\kappa>0
such that for any round
m\geqslant 0
and function
f(n)\geqslant 0
the
\textsc{Relative}\text{-}\textsc{Threshold}(f(n))
process satisfies

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Gap(m)\leqslant\kappa\left(\log n+f(n)\right)\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-4}.

The bound above is tight for any
\textsc{Relative}\text{-}\textsc{Threshold}(f(n))
process with
f(n)\geqslant\log n
.

Lemma 3.13
.

Consider any
\textsc{Relative}\text{-}\textsc{Threshold}(f(n))
process with
f(n)\geqslant\log n
. Then, for
m:=\frac{1}{24}\cdot\frac{n\cdot(f(n))^{2}}{\log n}
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Gap(m)\geqslant\frac{f(n)}{50}\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-1/4}.

For the remaining offset functions
f(n)\in(0,\log n)
, we can apply the general
Two-Thinning
lower bound of
\Omega\big(\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}\big)
derived in
[
30
]
(and in
[
22
]
) to the
\textsc{Relative}\text{-}\textsc{Threshold}(f(n))
process. This means that the upper bound from
3.12
is tight within an
\log\log n
factor. This also highlights the question of determining the behavior of the gap in this regime; we conjecture that the lower bound is tight for some values of
f(n)
.

We prove
3.13
later in
Section

8
, however we now proceed with the proof of
3.12
. We use a coupling argument involving adding
f(n)
“extra balls” to each bin, which allows us to reduce the
\textsc{Relative}\text{-}\textsc{Threshold}(f(n))
process with a non-negative offset function
f(n)
, to
Mean-Thinning
. Thanks to this reduction, proved in
3.15
and
3.14
we deduce
3.12
.

Lemma 3.14
.

Consider the
Mean-Thinning
and
\textsc{Relative}\text{-}\textsc{Threshold}(f(n))
processes for any non-negative
f(n)\geqslant 0
. Let
\Gap_{0}
and
\Gap_{f(n)}
be their gaps respectively. Then,
\Gap_{f(n)}
is stochastically smaller than
\Gap_{0}+f(n)
.

Before proving the lemma, we need the following domination result:

Lemma 3.15
.

Let
\mathcal{R}
be the
\textsc{Two-Thinning}(\frac{t}{n}+f(n))
process where
f(n)
is non-negative, starting with an empty load vector
x_{\mathcal{R}}^{0}=(0,\ldots,0)
. Further, let
\mathcal{Q}
be the
\textsc{Two-Thinning}(\frac{t}{n}+f(n))
process with initial load vector
x_{\mathcal{Q}}=(f(n),\ldots,f(n))
. Then, there is a coupling so that at any round
t\geqslant 0
, it holds that
(x_{\mathcal{R}}^{t})_{i}\leqslant(x_{\mathcal{Q}}^{t})_{i}
, for any bin
i\in[n]
.

Proof of
3.15
.

Let
j_{1}=j_{1}^{t}
and
j_{2}=j_{2}^{t}
be the two bins sampled at round
t\geqslant 0
, which are uniform and independent over
[n]
. We consider a coupling between
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{Q}
, where these random bin samples are identical, and prove inductively that for any
t\geqslant 0
and any
i\in[n]
,

(x_{\mathcal{R}}^{t})_{i}\leqslant(x_{\mathcal{Q}}^{t})_{i}.

The base case
t=0
holds by definition. For the inductive step, we make a case distinction:

Case 1
[
(x_{\mathcal{R}}^{t})_{j_{1}}<t/n+f(n)
]. In this case,
\mathcal{R}
allocates a ball to
j_{1}
. If
(x_{\mathcal{Q}}^{t})_{j_{1}}<t/n+f(n)
, then
\mathcal{Q}
also allocates a ball to
j_{1}
; otherwise, we have
(x_{\mathcal{Q}}^{t})_{j_{1}}\geqslant t/n+f(n)
, and hence
(x_{\mathcal{Q}}^{t})_{j_{1}}>(x_{\mathcal{R}}^{t})_{j_{1}}
, i.e.,
(x_{\mathcal{Q}}^{t})_{j_{1}}\geqslant(x_{\mathcal{R}}^{t})_{j_{1}}+1
. This implies

(x_{\mathcal{Q}}^{t+1})_{j_{1}}=(x_{\mathcal{Q}}^{t})_{j_{1}}\geqslant(x_{\mathcal{R}}^{t})_{j_{1}}+1=(x_{\mathcal{R}}^{t+1})_{j_{1}},

and the inductive step follows from this and the induction hypothesis.

Case 2
[
(x_{\mathcal{R}}^{t})_{j_{1}}\geqslant t/n+f(n)
]. In this case,
\mathcal{R}
allocates a ball to
j_{2}
. By induction hypothesis,
(x_{\mathcal{R}}^{t})_{j_{2}}\leqslant(x_{\mathcal{Q}}^{t})_{j_{2}}
, which implies
\mathcal{Q}
also allocates a ball to
j_{2}
. Thus we have

(x_{\mathcal{Q}}^{t+1})_{j_{2}}=(x_{\mathcal{Q}}^{t})_{j_{2}}+1\geqslant(x_{\mathcal{R}}^{t})_{j_{2}}+1=(x_{\mathcal{R}}^{t+1})_{j_{2}},

and the inductive step is complete.

Since in both cases all other bins remain unchanged the proof is complete.
∎

Lemma 3.16
.

Let
\mathcal{R}
be the
\textsc{Two-Thinning}(\frac{t}{n}+f(n))
process starting with the initial load vector
(x_{\mathcal{R}}^{0})_{1}=\ldots=(x_{\mathcal{R}}^{0})_{n}=f(n)
and
\mathcal{Q}
be the
Mean-Thinning
process with initial load vector
x_{\mathcal{Q}}^{0}=(0,\ldots,0)
. Then, there is a coupling so that
x_{\mathcal{R}}^{t}=x_{\mathcal{Q}}^{t}+f(n)
for any round
t\geqslant 0
.

Proof.

In the execution of the process
\mathcal{R}
, we start the process at round
t=0
from an initial load of
f(n)
balls in each bin. Since the threshold is
t/n+f(n)
, that is the process
\mathcal{R}
does not have a threshold relative to the actual mean load, the effect of adding these balls is to reduce the threshold of
\mathcal{R}
by exactly
f(n)
. Thus,
\mathcal{R}
is operating with a threshold of
t/n+f(n)-f(n)=t/n
, which is equivalent to the
Mean-Thinning
process, i.e.,
\mathcal{Q}
. So, we obtain a coupling such that
x_{\mathcal{R}}^{t}=x_{\mathcal{Q}}^{t}+f(n)
for any round
t\geqslant 0
.
∎

We can now complete the proof of
3.14
.

Proof of
3.14
.

We define the following processes:

•

\mathcal{R}_{1}
: The
\textsc{Two-Thinning}(\frac{t}{n}+f(n))
process (starting from the empty load vector).

•

\mathcal{R}_{2}
: The
\textsc{Two-Thinning}(\frac{t}{n}+f(n))
starting with
(x_{\mathcal{R}_{2}}^{0})_{1}=\ldots=(x_{\mathcal{R}_{2}}^{0})_{n}=f(n)
.

•

\mathcal{R}_{3}
: The
Mean-Thinning
process (starting from the empty load vector).

By
3.15
, there exists a coupling such that
x_{\mathcal{R}_{2}}^{t}
pointwise majorises
x_{\mathcal{R}_{1}}^{t}
for any round
t\geqslant 0
. By
3.16
, there exists a coupling such that
x_{\mathcal{R}_{2}}^{t}=x_{\mathcal{R}_{3}}^{t}+f(n)
. Hence, we deduce that there is a coupling between the three processes such that

\displaystyle\Gap_{f(n)}(t)

\displaystyle=\Gap_{\mathcal{R}_{1}}(t)=\max_{i\in[n]}(x_{\mathcal{R}_{1}}^{t})_{i}-\frac{t}{n}\leqslant\max_{i\in[n]}(x_{\mathcal{R}_{2}}^{t})_{i}-\frac{t}{n}=\max_{i\in[n]}(x_{\mathcal{R}_{3}}^{t})_{i}+f(n)-\frac{t}{n}

\displaystyle=\Gap_{0}(t)+f(n).\qed

4
Overview of the Analysis

In this section, we outline the proof for the
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
upper bound on the gap for
Mean-Biased
processes, i.e., processes satisfying conditions
\mathcal{P}_{1}
and
\mathcal{W}_{1}
and in addition
\mathcal{P}_{2}
or
\mathcal{W}_{2}
. The proof details are given in
Sections

5
,
6
and
7
. Some of the properties we prove are useful for the lower bounds (
Section

8
) and in analyzing the sample-efficiency of the processes (
Section

9
).

Our analysis is based on the interplay between the following potential functions.

•

The
absolute value potential
(also known as the
number of holes

[
11
]
):

\Delta^{t}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|y_{i}^{t}|.

In
5.1
, we prove that when
\Delta^{s}=\mathcal{O}(n)
, then w.h.p., among the next
\Theta(n)
rounds a constant fraction of them are rounds
s
where the quantile of the mean
\delta^{s}\in[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]
, for some constant
\varepsilon\in\big(0,\frac{1}{2}\big)
.

•

The
quadratic potential
:

\Upsilon^{t}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(y_{i}^{t}\right)^{2}.

In
6.2
, we prove that in any round
t
with
\Delta^{t}=\Omega(n)
holds, the potential
\Upsilon^{t}
decreases in expectation over the next round.

•

The
exponential potential
for a constant smoothing parameter
\alpha>0
(specified in (
7.2
)):

\displaystyle\Lambda^{t}:=\Lambda^{t}(\alpha):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\exp\left(\alpha\cdot|y_{i}^{t}|\right)=\sum_{i\in B_{+}^{t}}\exp\left(\alpha y_{i}^{t}\right)+\sum_{i\in B_{-}^{t}}\exp\left(-\alpha(-y_{i}^{t})\right).

(4.1)

In
6.6
, we prove that when
\Lambda^{t}=\Omega(n)
, if
\delta^{t}\in[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]
, then
\Lambda^{t}
decreases in expectation, otherwise it increases by a smaller factor. This potential is similar to the
hyperbolic cosine potential
used in
[
42
]
, but for each bin there is only one term. Our potential is slightly easier to analyze since the bias we study here is based on whether the bin is overloaded or underloaded. Further, unlike the analysis of the
(1+\beta)
-process in
[
42
]
, the potential can increase in expectation over a single round, even when it is large (
B.1
).

•

A “weaker” instance of the
\Lambda
potential function with smoothing parameter
\widetilde{\alpha}=\Theta(1/n)
:

V^{t}:=V^{t}(\widetilde{\alpha}):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\exp\left(\widetilde{\alpha}\cdot|y_{i}^{t}|\right)=\sum_{i\in B_{+}^{t}}\exp\left(\widetilde{\alpha}y_{i}^{t}\right)+\sum_{i\in B_{-}^{t}}\exp\left(-\widetilde{\alpha}(-y_{i}^{t})\right).

In
6.8
, we prove that
V
drops in expectation in every round (regardless of the value of
\delta^{t}
), and so we can establish that
\mathbf{E}[\,V^{t}\,]=\operatorname{poly}(n)
at an arbitrary round
t
. Then, using Markov’s inequality we establish w.h.p. that
\Gap(t)=\mathcal{O}(n\log n)
. Note that this is similar to the case
\beta=\Theta(1/n)
in the
(1+\beta)
-process
[
42
]
. We use this bound on the gap as a starting point for the tighter bound.

Having defined the potential functions, let us now describe in more detail why the potential
\Lambda
may increase in expectation in some rounds, for e.g., the
Mean-Thinning
process.
For this process, there exist bad configurations where
(i)

\Gap(t)
is large and
(ii)
for all
s\in[t,t+\omega(n)]
rounds the quantile of the mean load satisfies
\delta^{s}=1-o(1)
(or
\delta^{s}=o(1)
). When
\delta^{s}
is too close to
1
(or
0
), then the bias away from any
fixed
overloaded (or towards any
fixed
underloaded) bin is too small, and the process allocates balls
almost
uniformly, similarly to
One-Choice
. As a result, the exponential potential may increase for several rounds, until
\delta^{s}
is bounded away from
0
and
1
(see
Fig.

4.3
for an illustration showing experimental results and
B.1
for a concrete example of a bad configuration).

So, instead we start with the weaker exponential potential function
V:=V(\widetilde{\alpha})
where
\widetilde{\alpha}=\Theta(1/n)
. Because
Mean-Biased
processes always have a small bias to allocate away from (or allocate fewer balls to) the maximum load, we are able to prove in
6.8
that when
V
is sufficiently large it decreases in expectation. This allows us to prove that
\mathbf{E}[\,V^{t}\,]=\operatorname{poly}(n)
at an arbitrary round
t
and infer, using Markov’s inequality, that the gap is w.h.p.
\Gap(t)=\mathcal{O}(n\log n)
(
6.8
). Then, our next goal is to show that starting with
\Gap(t_{0})=\mathcal{O}(n\log n)
we reach a round

where
\Lambda^{s}=\mathcal{O}(n)
(the
recovery phase
–
Section

7.3
) and finally show that the gap remains
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
for the remaining rounds
[s,m]
(the
stabilization phase
–
Section

7.4
).

Lemma 4.1

.

Consider any

\delta^{t}
is

\Theta(n)

\Delta^{t_{1}}

\Delta^{t}
and the quadratic potential
\Upsilon^{t}

\Upsilon^{t}

\Delta^{t}
must eventually become linear.

Lemma 4.2

(Restated, page
6.2
)

Consider any
{\hyperlink{p1}{\mathcal{P}_{1}}}\cap{\hyperlink{w1}{\mathcal{W}_{1}}}
-process. Then, for any round

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\Upsilon^{t+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]\leqslant\Upsilon^{t}+\sum_{i\in B_{+}^{t}}2y_{i}^{t}p_{i}^{t}\cdot w_{+}+\sum_{i\in B_{-}^{t}}2y_{i}^{t}p_{i}^{t}\cdot w_{-}+4\cdot(w_{-})^{2}.

Hence for any
{\hyperlink{p2}{\mathcal{P}_{2}}}\cap{\hyperlink{w1}{\mathcal{W}_{1}}}

{\hyperlink{p1}{\mathcal{P}_{1}}}\cap{\hyperlink{w2}{\mathcal{W}_{2}}}
-process, this implies by

that there exist constants

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\Upsilon^{t+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]\leqslant\Upsilon^{t}-\frac{c_{1}}{n}\cdot\Delta^{t}+c_{2}.

). Finally, we prove some deterministic bounds on the quadratic potential.

, we complete the proof for the

gap for
Mean-Biased

5
Mean Quantile Stabilization

In this section we prove that once the absolute value potential is
\mathcal{O}(n)
, then the allocation process will satisfy
\delta^{t}\in[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]
for “many” of the following rounds. The condition on
\delta^{t}
means that a constant fraction of bins are overloaded, and a constant fraction are underloaded. Interestingly, this lemma does not require either of the stronger properties
\mathcal{P}_{2}
or
\mathcal{W}_{2}
to hold.

Theorem 5.1

(Mean Quantile Stabilization)
.

Consider any
{\hyperlink{p1}{\mathcal{P}_{1}}}\cap{\hyperlink{w1}{\mathcal{W}_{1}}}
-process and let
C\geqslant 1
be any integer constant. Then, there exists a constant
\varepsilon:=\varepsilon(C)\in\big(0,\frac{1}{2}\big)
where
(4\varepsilon)^{-1}
is an integer, such that for any round
t_{0}\geqslant 0
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left|\left\{t\in\left[t_{0},t_{0}+\frac{n}{4\varepsilon}\right]\colon\delta^{t}\in[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]\right\}\right|\geqslant\varepsilon n~\Big|~\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\Delta^{t_{0}}\leqslant Cn\,\right]\geqslant 1-e^{-\varepsilon n}.

Proof.

Define
B_{\#}:=\left\{i\in[n]\colon|y_{i}^{t_{0}}|<4C\right\}
to be the bins whose load deviates from the mean
W^{t}/n
by less than
4C
. Then, conditional on the event
\mathcal{C}:=\{\Delta^{t_{0}}\leqslant Cn\}
, we have

Cn\geqslant\Delta^{t_{0}}=\sum_{i\in[n]}|y_{i}^{t_{0}}|>\sum_{i\colon|y_{i}^{t_{0}}|\geqslant 4C}4C=(n-|B_{\#}|)\cdot 4C,

rearranging this gives that conditional on
\mathcal{C}
,

|B_{\#}|>n\cdot\frac{C(4-1)}{4C}=\frac{3n}{4}.

(5.1)

Note that the bins in
B_{\#}
may be underloaded or overloaded.

We now proceed with two claims. Using the fact that
|B_{\#}|
is large, the first claim (
5.2
) proves that there exists an “early” round
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
such that a constant fraction of bins are underloaded. Similarly, the second claim (
5.3
) proves that there exists a “late” round
t\in[t_{2},t_{3}]
with
t_{2}=t_{1}+\Theta(n)
such that a constant fraction of bins are overloaded. Since the set of overloaded bins can only increase by
1
per round, we then finally conclude that for
\Omega(n)
rounds
t\in[t_{1},t_{2}]
, both conditions hold, i.e.,
\delta^{t}\in[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]
. This is made formal by
5.4
.

Claim 5.2
.

For any integer constant
C\geqslant 1
, there exists a constant
\kappa_{1}:=\kappa_{1}(C)>0
such that for
t_{1}:=t_{0}+4C\cdot n+1
we have

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left.\bigcup_{t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]}\left\{\delta^{t}\leqslant 1-\kappa_{1}\right\}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\Delta^{t_{0}}\leqslant Cn\,\right]\geqslant 1-e^{-\kappa_{1}n}.

Proof of Claim.

If
|B_{-}^{t_{0}}\cap B_{\#}|>|B_{\#}|/3
, then, since
|B_{\#}|>3n/4
by (
5.1
), the statement of the claim follows immediately for
t=t_{0}
and
\kappa_{1}=1/4
.
Otherwise, we may assume
|B_{+}^{t_{0}}\cap B_{\#}|\geqslant 2|B_{\#}|/3
, i.e., at least two thirds of the bins in
B_{\#}
are overloaded at round
t_{0}
. Note that the bins in
B_{+}^{t_{0}}\cap B_{\#}
all have loads in the range
[W^{t_{0}}/n,\;W^{t_{0}}/n+4C)
. Thus, since loads are integers (as
w_{+}
and
w_{-}
are integers), there can be at most
4C
different load levels within
B_{+}^{t_{0}}\cap B_{\#}
. Hence, by the pigeonhole principle and (
5.1
), conditional on
\mathcal{C}
, there exists a subset
B_{*}\subseteq B_{+}^{t_{0}}\cap B_{\#}
with

|B_{*}|\geqslant\frac{1}{4C}\cdot|B_{+}^{t_{0}}\cap B_{\#}|\geqslant\frac{1}{4C}\cdot\frac{2}{3}\cdot|B_{\#}|\geqslant\frac{1}{4C}\cdot\frac{2}{3}\cdot\frac{3n}{4}=\frac{n}{8C},

(5.2)

such that all bins
i\in B_{*}
have the same (non-negative) load at round
t_{0}
. Let this common load be denoted by
0\leqslant\ell<4C
.
In the following, let us define the stopping time

\tau:=\min\left\{t\geqslant t_{0}\colon\frac{W^{t}}{n}-\frac{W^{t_{0}}}{n}>\ell\right\},

that is
\tau
is the minimum number of rounds until the average load has dropped by more than
\ell
. By definition, if a bin in
B_{*}
was never chosen for an allocation during any round
[t_{0},\tau)
, it becomes underloaded. Also note that, while
\tau
is a random variable, there is a deterministic upper bound of
T:=t_{0}+n\cdot\ell+1
, since in each round the total load increases by at least
w_{+}\geqslant 1
.
For any round
t\in[t_{0}+1,\tau]
, let
B_{*}^{t}
be the subset of bins in
B_{*}
that are never allocated to during
[t_{0},t)
and let
B_{*}^{t_{0}}:=B_{*}
. Note that since any bin in
B_{*}^{t}
is still overloaded in such a round
t
, the bin is chosen for an allocation with probability at most
k_{1}/n
by condition
\mathcal{P}_{1}
.

For any round
t\in[t_{0},T]
, define

Z^{t}:=\frac{|B_{*}^{t}|}{(1-k_{1}/n)^{t-t_{0}}},

so in particular,
Z^{t_{0}}=|B_{*}^{t_{0}}|=|B_{*}|
.
Then, for any round
t\in[t_{0},\tau)
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,Z^{t+1}\,\mid\,\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1-k_{1}/n)^{t+1-t_{0}}}\cdot\mathbf{E}\left[\,|B_{*}^{t+1}|\,\mid\,\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{(1-k_{1}/n)^{t+1-t_{0}}}\cdot(1-k_{1}/n)|B_{*}^{t}|

\displaystyle=Z^{t},

thus
(Z^{t\wedge\tau})_{t=t_{0}}^{T}
forms a sub-martingale.

We now state a bound that will be used often in the upcoming proof: Since
1-x\geqslant e^{-2x}
for any
0\leqslant x\leqslant 1/2
,
T-t_{0}\leqslant 4C\cdot n+1\leqslant 5Cn
, and
n
is large, for any
t\in[t_{0},T]
we have

(1-k_{1}/n)^{t-t_{0}}\geqslant e^{-(t-t_{0})\cdot 2k_{1}/n}\geqslant e^{-(T-t_{0})\cdot 2k_{1}/n}\geqslant e^{-10k_{1}C}.

(5.3)

We proceed to bound
Z^{t+1}-Z^{t}
. First,
observe that
|B_{*}^{t}|
is non-increasing in
t
. Thus, by (
5.3
) and
|B_{*}^{t}|\leqslant n
,

Z^{t+1}-Z^{t}\leqslant\frac{|B_{*}^{t}|}{(1-k_{1}/n)^{t-t_{0}}}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{1-k_{1}/n}-1\right)\leqslant\frac{n}{e^{-10k_{1}C}}\cdot\frac{2k_{1}}{n}=2k_{1}e^{10k_{1}C}.

Similarly, for a lower bound, note that
|B_{*}^{t}|
can decrease by at most one in each round, since only one bin is allocated to in each round, and therefore

\displaystyle Z^{t+1}-Z^{t}

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{|B_{*}^{t}|-1}{(1-k_{1}/n)^{t+1-t_{0}}}-\frac{|B_{*}^{t}|}{(1-k_{1}/n)^{t-t_{0}}}

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{|B_{*}^{t}|-1}{(1-k_{1}/n)^{t-t_{0}}}-\frac{|B_{*}^{t}|}{(1-k_{1}/n)^{t-t_{0}}}

\displaystyle\geqslant-\frac{1}{(1-k_{1}/n)^{t-t_{0}}}

\displaystyle\geqslant-e^{10k_{1}C}.

Thus
|Z^{t+1}-Z^{t}|\leqslant 2k_{1}e^{10k_{1}C}
. Applying Azuma’s inequality for sub-martingales (
A.4
) on
Z^{T\wedge\tau}=Z^{\tau}
yields

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,Z^{\tau}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\cdot Z^{t_{0}}\,\Big|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}}\,\right]\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{\left(\frac{1}{2}\cdot Z^{t_{0}}\right)^{2}}{2(T-t_{0})\cdot(2k_{1}e^{10k_{1}C})^{2}}\right).

(5.4)

Observe that
\{Z^{\tau}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\cdot Z^{t_{0}}\}=\{|B_{*}^{\tau}|\leqslant(1-k_{1}/n)^{\tau-t_{0}}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\cdot|B_{*}|\}
. Recall that, conditional on
\mathcal{C}:=\{\Delta^{t_{0}}\leqslant Cn\}
, we have
|B_{*}|\geqslant\frac{n}{8C}
by (
5.2
), and
\tau-t_{0}\leqslant 5Cn
. Thus, by (
5.3
) and (
5.4
),

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left.|B_{*}^{\tau}|\leqslant e^{10k_{1}C}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{n}{8C}\;\right|\;\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\;\mathcal{C}\,\right]\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{n}{5\cdot 2^{11}\cdot k_{1}^{2}C^{3}e^{20k_{1}C}}\right).

Since all load levels in
B_{*}
before round
t_{0}
are identical, we conclude that they all become underloaded the first time in the same round
\tau\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
, so, by merging the constants

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left.\bigcup_{t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]}\{|B_{-}^{t}\cap B_{*}|\geqslant\kappa_{1}\cdot n\}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\;\mathcal{C}\,\right]\geqslant 1-e^{-\kappa_{1}n},

where
\kappa_{1}:=(5\cdot 2^{11}\cdot k_{1}^{2}C^{3}e^{20k_{1}C})^{-1}>0
.
∎

Claim 5.3
.

For any integer constant
C\geqslant 1
there exists a constant
\kappa_{2}:=\kappa_{2}(C)>0
such that for
t_{2}:=t_{1}+\big\lceil\frac{n}{w_{+}}\big\rceil
, where
t_{1}:=t_{0}+4Cn+1
, and

t_{3}:=t_{2}+\big\lceil\frac{n}{10w_{-}}\big\rceil
we have

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left.\bigcup_{t\in[t_{2},t_{3}]}\left\{\delta^{t}\geqslant\kappa_{2}\right\}~\right|~\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\Delta^{t_{0}}\leqslant Cn\,\right]\geqslant 1-e^{-\kappa_{2}n}.

Proof of Claim.

If
|B_{+}^{t_{2}}\cap B_{\#}|>|B_{\#}|/6
, the claim follows by (
5.1
) for
t=t_{2}
and
\kappa_{2}=1/8
. So, if we let
B_{*}:=B_{-}^{t_{2}}\cap B_{\#}
, then we can assume, by (
5.1
), that

|B_{*}|\geqslant\frac{5}{6}\cdot|B_{\#}|>\frac{5}{6}\cdot\frac{3n}{4}=\frac{5n}{8}.

(5.5)

Observe that for any bin
i\in B_{*}
, at round
t_{2}
the normalized load satisfies

y_{i}^{t_{2}}\geqslant y_{i}^{t_{0}}-(t_{2}-t_{0})\cdot\frac{w_{-}}{n}>-4C-\left(4Cn+1+\left\lceil\frac{n}{w_{+}}\right\rceil\right)\cdot\frac{w_{-}}{n}>-10C\cdot w_{-},

using that
y_{i}^{t_{0}}>-4C
as
i\in B_{*}\subseteq B_{\#}
,
C\geqslant 1
, and
w_{-}\geqslant w_{+}\geqslant 1
by
\mathcal{W}_{1}
. It follows that if
i
is chosen for an allocation
20C
times during
[t_{2},t_{3}]
while underloaded, it must become overloaded before round
t_{3}
as

y_{i}^{t_{3}}\geqslant y_{i}^{t_{2}}-(t_{3}-t_{2})\frac{w_{-}}{n}+20C\cdot w_{-}\geqslant-10C\cdot w_{-}-2\cdot w_{-}+20C\cdot w_{-}>0.

(5.6)

Observe that, while
i\in B_{*}
is underloaded, it will be chosen for allocation with probability at least
k_{2}/n
in each round by Condition
\mathcal{P}_{1}
. Our aim now will be to couple the allocation process over the interval
[t_{2},t_{3}]
to some sequences of independent random variables. Firstly, denote

B_{*}^{t}:=B_{*}\cap B_{-}^{t}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad s:=\frac{4}{5}\cdot|B_{*}|.

(5.7)

By (
5.5
) we have

s:=\frac{4}{5}\cdot|B_{*}|\geqslant\frac{4}{5}\cdot\frac{5n}{8}=\frac{n}{2}.

(5.8)

Observe that, since
t_{3}=t_{2}+\big\lceil\frac{n}{10w_{-}}\big\rceil
and by (
5.1
), for any time
t\in[t_{2},t_{3}]
we have

|B_{*}^{t}|\geqslant|B_{*}|-\left\lceil\frac{n}{10w_{-}}\right\rceil=s+\frac{1}{5}\cdot|B_{*}|-\left\lceil\frac{n}{10w_{-}}\right\rceil>s+\frac{n}{8}-\left\lceil\frac{n}{10w_{-}}\right\rceil\geqslant s,

(5.9)

thus at least
s
many bins from
B_{*}
remain underloaded during the interval
[t_{2},t_{3}]
. For
t>t_{2}
let
(\nu_{i}^{t})_{i\in[n]}
be given by

\nu_{i}^{t}:=\begin{cases}\left(1-\frac{k_{2}s}{n}\right)^{-1}\cdot p_{i}^{t}&\text{ if }i\in[n]\setminus B_{*}^{t},\\
\left(1-\frac{k_{2}s}{n}\right)^{-1}\cdot\left(p_{i}^{t}-\frac{k_{2}s}{|B_{*}^{t}|n}\right)&\text{ if }i\in B_{*}^{t}.\end{cases}

We first show that
\nu_{i}^{t}\in[0,1]
. As
k_{2}\leqslant 1
we see that
k_{2}s/n\leqslant 4/5
by (
5.7
). Thus, for
i\in[n]\setminus B_{*}^{t}
, we have
0\leqslant p_{i}^{t}\leqslant\nu_{i}^{t}\leqslant 5k_{1}/n<1
. For
i\in B_{*}^{t}
we have
\nu_{i}^{t}\geqslant p_{i}^{t}-\frac{k_{2}s}{|B_{*}^{t}|n}>p_{i}^{t}-\frac{k_{2}}{n}\geqslant 0
, as
|B_{*}^{t}|>s
by (
5.9
) and
B_{*}^{t}\subseteq B_{-}^{t}
. Finally, since
p_{i}^{t}\geqslant k_{2}/n
for all
i\in B_{-}^{t}
and
s<|B_{*}^{t}|\leqslant|B_{-}^{t}|
we have

\nu_{i}<\frac{p_{i}^{t}}{1-k_{2}s/n}\leqslant\frac{1-k_{2}|B_{-}^{t}|/n}{1-k_{2}s/n}<1.

To see that
\nu^{t}
, where
\nu_{i}^{t}\in[0,1]
, is a probability vector, we observe that

\left(1-\frac{k_{2}s}{n}\right)\cdot\sum_{i\in[n]}\nu_{i}^{t}=\sum_{i\in[n]\setminus B_{*}^{t}}p_{i}^{t}+\sum_{i\in B_{*}^{t}}\left(p_{i}^{t}-\frac{k_{2}s}{|B_{*}^{t}|n}\right)=1-\frac{k_{2}s}{n}.

For a set
S\neq\emptyset
, the uniform distribution
\mathsf{Uni}(S)
samples each
s\in S
with probability
1/|S|
.

The first step of the coupling is to sample two independent infinite sequences
(X^{t})_{t>t_{2}}
and
(U^{j})_{j\geqslant 1}
, where
X^{t}\sim\mathsf{Ber}(k_{2}s/n)
i.i.d. for each
t>t_{2}
, and
U^{j}\sim\mathsf{Uni}(B_{*})
i.i.d., for each
j\geqslant 1
.

We can then generate the process as follows: initialize
j^{t_{2}+1}:=1
, and for each round
t>t_{2}
,

•

If
X^{t}=0
, then allocate to a single bin
i\in[n]
sampled according to
\nu^{t}
, and set
j^{t+1}:=j^{t}
.

•

If
X^{t}=1
and
U^{j^{t}}\in B_{*}^{t}
, then allocate to the bin
U^{j^{t}}
, and set
j^{t+1}:=j^{t}+1
.

•

If
X^{t}=1
and
U^{j^{t}}\notin B_{*}^{t}
, then allocate to a bin
R^{t}\sim\mathsf{Uni}(B_{*}^{t})
sampled independently, and set
j^{t+1}:=j^{t}+1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}_{i}^{t}
be the event that bin
i\in[n]
receives the allocation in round
t\in[t_{2}+1,t_{3}]
and observe that, for
i\in B_{*}^{t}
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathcal{A}_{i}^{t}\mid X^{t}=1\,\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,U^{j^{t}}=i\mid U^{j^{t}}\in B_{*}^{t}\,\right]\cdot\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,U^{j^{t}}\in B_{*}^{t}\,\right]+\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,R^{t}=i\,\right]\cdot\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,U^{j^{t}}\notin B_{*}^{t}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|B_{*}^{t}|}\cdot\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,U^{j^{t}}\in B_{*}^{t}\,\right]+\frac{1}{|B_{*}^{t}|}\cdot\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,U^{j^{t}}\notin B_{*}^{t}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|B_{*}^{t}|}.

Thus, to see this gives a valid coupling, note that a ball is allocated to
i\in B_{*}^{t}
with probability

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathcal{A}_{i}^{t}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,X^{t}=0\,\right]\cdot\nu_{i}^{t}+\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,X^{t}=1\,\right]\cdot\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathcal{A}_{i}^{t}\mid X^{t}=1\,\right]

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{k_{2}s}{n}\right)\cdot\frac{p_{i}^{t}-\frac{k_{2}s}{|B_{*}^{t}|n}}{1-k_{2}s/n}+\frac{k_{2}s}{n}\cdot\frac{1}{|B_{*}^{t}|}

\displaystyle=p_{i}^{t}.

The corresponding calculation for
i\in[n]\setminus B_{*}^{t}
is immediate.

Observe that by (
5.8
) we have
k_{2}s/n\geqslant k_{2}/2
. Thus if we define the event

\mathcal{E}_{1}:=\left\{\sum_{t=t_{2}+1}^{t_{3}}X^{t}\geqslant\tau_{1}\right\},\qquad\text{where }\tau_{1}:=\left\lceil\frac{k_{2}(t_{3}-t_{2})}{4}\right\rceil.

Then, by a Chernoff bound, we have

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\neg\mathcal{E}_{1}~\big|~\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\mathcal{C}\,\right]\leqslant\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathsf{Bin}\left(t_{3}-t_{2},\frac{k_{2}}{2}\right)<\tau_{1}\,\right]\leqslant e^{-\frac{k_{2}(t_{3}-t_{2})}{2^{2}\cdot 2\cdot 2}}\leqslant e^{-\frac{k_{2}n}{160w_{-}}}.

(5.10)

For
i\in B_{*}
let
S_{i}:=\sum_{j=1}^{\tau_{1}}\mathbf{1}_{\{U^{j}=i\}}
. Then, since
\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,U^{j}=i\,\right]=1/|B_{*}|\geqslant 1/n
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,S_{i}\geqslant 20C\;\big|\;\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\;\mathcal{C}\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathsf{Bin}\left(\tau_{1},\;\frac{1}{n}\right)\geqslant 20C\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\binom{\tau_{1}}{20C}\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)^{20C}\cdot\left(1-\frac{k_{2}}{n}\right)^{\tau_{1}-20C}

\displaystyle\geqslant\left(\frac{k_{2}}{800Cw_{-}}\right)^{20C}\cdot e^{-1}=:\kappa,

(5.11)

since
\tau_{1}=\left\lceil\frac{k_{2}(t_{3}-t_{2})}{4}\right\rceil=(1+o(1))\cdot\frac{k_{2}n}{40w_{-}}
, where in the last inequality in (
5
) we used
\binom{n}{k}\geqslant(\frac{n}{k})^{k}
, the inequality
1-x\geqslant e^{-2x}
for any
0\leqslant x\leqslant 1/2
, and
n
being large. Define the random variable

Z:=\left|\left\{i\in B_{*}\;:\;S_{i}\geqslant 20C\right\}\right|\quad\text{and the event }\quad\mathcal{E}_{2}:=\left\{Z\geqslant\kappa n/4\right\}.

Observe that by (
5
) and (
5.8
),

\mathbf{E}\left[\,Z\;\big|\;\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\;\mathcal{C}\,\right]=\sum_{i\in B_{*}}\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,S_{i}\geqslant 20C\;\big|\;\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\;\mathcal{C}\,\right]\geqslant|B_{*}|\cdot\kappa\geqslant\frac{\kappa n}{2}.

Notice also that
Z
is generated by the sequence
(U^{j})_{j=1}^{\tau_{1}}
of mutually independent
B_{*}
-valued random variables. Furthermore, changing the value of any single
U^{j}
can cause
Z
to change by at most one. Thus, applying the Method of Bounded Independent Differences (
A.3
), assuming (
5.5
), yields,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\neg\mathcal{E}_{2}\;\big|\;\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\;\mathcal{C}\,\right]=\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,Z<\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{\kappa n}{2}\;\Bigg|\;\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\;\mathcal{C}\,\right]\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{\frac{1}{4}\left(\kappa n/2\right)^{2}}{2\cdot\tau_{1}\cdot 1^{2}}\right)\leqslant\exp\left(-\kappa^{2}n\right).

(5.12)

Our first observation is that, conditional on
\mathcal{E}_{1}\cap\mathcal{E}_{2}
, at least
Z\geqslant\kappa n/4
bins become overloaded at least once at some round in
[t_{2},t_{3}]
. To see this note conditional on
\mathcal{E}_{1}
an allocation is made based on the value of
U^{j}
for each
j\leqslant\tau_{1}
, and conditional on
\mathcal{E}_{2}
there are
Z
many
i\in B_{*}
such that
\sum_{j=1}^{\tau_{1}}\mathbf{1}_{\{U^{j}=i\}}\geqslant 20C
. Finally, from the coupling, observe that whenever
U^{j}=i
, either an allocation is made to
i
, or
i
is already overloaded. Thus, for
Z
many bins at some time during the window
[t_{2},t_{3}]
, either the bin has received at least
20C
allocations (which would make them overloaded by (
5.6
)) or they became overloaded. However, if each bin is overloaded for only one round, and all at different rounds, then it is possible that at no single round in
[t_{2},t_{3}]
do we have
\Omega(n)
overloaded bins. We now explain why this cannot happen.

Note that during the interval
[t_{2},t_{3}]
, as
t_{3}-t_{2}=\big\lceil\frac{n}{10w_{-}}\big\rceil
, there is at most one round
t
where the integer parts of the mean load changes, that is, there is only at most one round
t\in[t_{2},t_{3})
such that
\lfloor W^{t}/n\rfloor<\lfloor W^{t+1}/n\rfloor
. Hence only at the transition from
t
to
t+1
could an overloaded bin become underloaded during the interval
[t_{2},t_{3}]
. Therefore, if
Z\geqslant\kappa n/4
we must have

|B_{+}^{t}\cap B_{*}|\geqslant\kappa n/8\qquad\mbox{ or }\qquad|B_{+}^{t_{3}}\cap B_{*}|\geqslant\kappa n/8.

Hence, conditional on
\mathcal{E}_{1}\cap\mathcal{E}_{2}
,
Z\geqslant\kappa n/4
implies that there exists
t\in[t_{2},t_{3}]
such that
|B_{+}^{t}|\geqslant\kappa n/8
. The claim then follows by choosing
\kappa_{2}:=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\min\{\kappa^{2},\kappa/8,\frac{k_{2}n}{160w_{-}}\}
as

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\neg\mathcal{E}_{1}\cup\neg\mathcal{E}_{2}\;\big|\;\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\;\mathcal{C}\,\right]\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{k_{2}n}{160w_{-}}\right)+\exp\left(-\kappa^{2}\cdot n\right)\leqslant\exp\left(-\kappa_{2}\cdot n\right),

by the union bound over (
5.10
) and (
5.12
).
∎

The first claim implies that, w.p.
1-e^{-\kappa_{1}n}
, that the process reaches a round
s_{1}\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
with at least
\kappa_{1}n
underloaded bins. The second claim shows, w.p.
1-e^{-\kappa_{2}n}
, that the process reaches a round
s_{2}\in[t_{1}+\lceil\frac{n}{w_{+}}\rceil,t_{3}]
with at least
\kappa_{2}n
overloaded bins. By the union bound, both events occur with probability
1-e^{-\kappa_{1}n}-e^{-\kappa_{2}n}
.
Since
\delta^{t}=|B_{+}^{t}|/n
, we have
\delta^{t+1}\leqslant\delta^{t}+\frac{1}{n}
,
and in this case applying
5.4
below with

r_{0}=s_{1},\qquad r_{1}=s_{2},\qquad f(t)=\delta^{t},\quad\text{and}\quad\vartheta:=\min\{\kappa_{2},\kappa_{1},2/3\},

yields

\displaystyle\left|\left\{t\in[t_{0},t_{3}]\colon\delta^{t}\in[\vartheta/2,1-\vartheta/2]\right\}\right|

\displaystyle\geqslant\left|\left\{t\in[s_{1},s_{2}]\colon\delta^{t}\in[\vartheta/2,1-\vartheta/2]\right\}\right|

\displaystyle\geqslant\min\{\vartheta/2\cdot n,s_{2}-s_{1}\}

\displaystyle\geqslant\min\left\{\vartheta/2\cdot n,\frac{n}{w_{+}}\right\}.

Thus, finally, we require an
\varepsilon>0
in the statement of
5.1
which satisfies the following three conditions:
(i):

\varepsilon\leqslant\min\{\vartheta/2,1/w_{+}\}
,
(ii):

\frac{n}{4\varepsilon}\geqslant t_{3}-t_{0}=4Cn+1+\big\lceil\frac{n}{w_{+}}\big\rceil+\big\lceil\frac{n}{10w_{-}}\big\rceil
, and
(iii):

(4\varepsilon)^{-1}
is an integer. Observe that such an
\varepsilon>0
can always be found by making
\varepsilon
sufficiently small, giving the result.
∎

It remains to state and prove the technical lemma used in the proof of
5.1
.

Lemma 5.4
.

For integers
0\leqslant r_{0}\leqslant r_{1}
, and reals
\vartheta\in(0,2/3)
and
\xi>0
, we let
f:[r_{0},r_{1}]\cap\mathbb{N}\rightarrow[0,1]
be a function satisfying

(i)

f(r_{0})\leqslant 1-\vartheta
,

(ii)

f(r_{1})\geqslant\vartheta
,

(iii)

f(t+1)\leqslant f(t)+\xi
for all
t\in[r_{0},r_{1}-1]
.

Then,

\left|\left\{t\in[r_{0},r_{1}]\colon f(t)\in\left[\frac{\vartheta}{2},1-\frac{\vartheta}{2}\right]\right\}\right|\geqslant\min\left\{\frac{\vartheta}{2\xi},r_{1}-r_{0}\right\}.

Proof.

First consider the case where there exists a
t\in[r_{0},r_{1}]
with
f(t)\geqslant 1-\vartheta/2
. Further, let
t
be the first round with that property, hence for any
s\in[r_{0},t]
,
f(s)<1-\vartheta/2
. Further, thanks to the third property,
f(t-x)\geqslant f(t)-x\xi\geqslant 1-\vartheta/2-x\xi
for any
x\geqslant 0
(which also implies
t-r_{0}\geqslant\vartheta/(2\xi)
due to precondition
(i)
), and thus as long as
0\leqslant x\leqslant\vartheta/(2\xi)
,

f(t-x)\geqslant f(t)-x\xi\geqslant 1-\vartheta/2-\vartheta/2\geqslant\vartheta/2,

since
\vartheta\leqslant 2/3
.
Hence for any
s\in[t-\vartheta/(2\xi),t]
,

f(s)\in[\vartheta/2,1-\vartheta/2].

Now consider the case where for all rounds
t\in[r_{0},r_{1}]
we have
f(t)\leqslant 1-\vartheta/2
. Since
f(r_{1})\geqslant\vartheta
, we conclude for any
x\leqslant\vartheta/(2\xi)
,

f(r_{1}-x)\geqslant f(r_{1})-x\xi\geqslant\vartheta-\vartheta/2\geqslant\vartheta/2.

Hence for any
s\in[\max\{r_{0},r_{1}-\vartheta/(2\xi)\},r_{1}]
we have
f(s)\in[\vartheta/2,1-\vartheta/2]
.
∎

6
Potential Function Inequalities

In this section, we derive several inequalities involving potential functions.

In
Section

6.1
, we establish the interplay between the
absolute value
and
quadratic
potential which, later in
Section

7.2
, will be used in combination with
5.1
to establish that in a sufficiently long interval there is a constant fraction of rounds
t
with
\delta^{t}\in[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]
.

In
Section

6.2
, most of the effort goes into establishing a drop in the expectation of the
exponential potential

\Lambda:=\Lambda(\alpha)
for sufficiently small smoothing parameter
\alpha>0
, which depends on the constants defined by the process. One of the main insights for
\Lambda:=\Lambda(\alpha)
with
\alpha=\Theta(1)
is
6.6
, which establishes:
(i)
a
drop
in the expectation of
\Lambda
over one round if the mean quantile satisfies
\delta^{t}\in[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]
, and
(ii)
a
not too large
increase for any quantile. For the potential
V:=V(\widetilde{\alpha})
with
\widetilde{\alpha}=\Theta(1/n)
we establish that it drops in every round. In turn this implies that
\mathbf{E}\left[\,V^{m}\,\right]=\operatorname{poly}(n)
, which implies the weak bound of
\mathcal{O}(n\log n)
on the gap.

On a high level, much of the analysis in
Section

6.2
follows relatively standard estimates and bears resemblance to the one in
[
42
]
. However, the conditions we enforce on processes are more relaxed than those in
[
42
]
and this makes the analysis a lot more challenging. In particular, unlike in
[
42
]
, the conditions imposed on the probability allocation vector
p^{t}
are time/load dependent. That being said, a reader may wish to skip this part (or the proofs).

Finally, in
Section

6.3
, we prove some deterministic bounds on the quadratic potential.

6.1
Interplay between Absolute Value and Quadratic Potentials

In this section, we prove the relation between the absolute value and quadratic potentials. We will use this in
7.3
to deduce that in a sufficiently long interval, there is a constant fraction of rounds with
\delta^{t}\in[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Consider any
{\hyperlink{p2}{\mathcal{P}_{2}}}\cap{\hyperlink{w1}{\mathcal{W}_{1}}}
-process or
{\hyperlink{p1}{\mathcal{P}_{1}}}\cap{\hyperlink{w2}{\mathcal{W}_{2}}}
-process. Then, there exists a constant
c_{1}>0
, such that for any round
t\geqslant 0
,

\sum_{i\in B_{+}^{t}}2y_{i}^{t}p_{i}^{t}\cdot w_{+}+\sum_{i\in B_{-}^{t}}2y_{i}^{t}p_{i}^{t}\cdot w_{-}\leqslant-\Delta^{t}\cdot\frac{c_{1}}{n}.

Proof.

Before we begin, note that
\sum_{i\in B_{+}^{t}}y_{i}^{t}=-\sum_{i\in B_{-}^{t}}y_{i}^{t}=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\Delta^{t}
. We consider two cases based on the conditions satisfied:

Case 1 [
\mathcal{P}_{2}
and
\mathcal{W}_{1}
hold]:
In this case
\mathcal{W}_{1}
implies
w_{-}\geqslant w_{+}\geqslant 1
, and so we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in B_{+}^{t}}2y_{i}^{t}p_{i}^{t}\cdot w_{+}+\sum_{i\in B_{-}^{t}}2y_{i}^{t}p_{i}^{t}\cdot w_{-}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in B_{+}^{t}}2y_{i}^{t}p_{i}^{t}\cdot w_{+}-\sum_{i\in B_{-}^{t}}2|y_{i}^{t}|p_{i}^{t}\cdot w_{-}

\displaystyle\leqslant w_{+}\cdot\left(\sum_{i\in B_{+}^{t}}2y_{i}^{t}p_{i}^{t}-\sum_{i\in B_{-}^{t}}2|y_{i}^{t}|p_{i}^{t}\right).

(6.1)

By
\mathcal{P}_{2}
we have
\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}^{t}\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(\frac{1}{n}-\frac{k_{3}\cdot(1-\delta^{t})}{n}\right)
for all
1\leqslant k\leqslant\delta^{t}n
, and also that
y_{i}^{t}\geqslant 0
and non-increasing for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant\delta^{t}n
(equivalently for all
i\in B_{i}^{t}
). Hence, by
A.1
,

\sum_{i\in B_{+}^{t}}2y_{i}^{t}\cdot p_{i}^{t}\leqslant\sum_{i\in B_{+}^{t}}2y_{i}^{t}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{n}-\frac{k_{3}\cdot(1-\delta^{t})}{n}\right)=\Delta^{t}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{n}-\frac{k_{3}\cdot(1-\delta^{t})}{n}\right).

Similarly,
\sum_{i\in B_{-}^{t}}2|y_{i}^{t}|p_{i}^{t}\geqslant\Delta^{t}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{n}+\frac{k_{4}\cdot\delta^{t}}{n}\right)
, also by
A.1
. Thus, it follows from (
6.1
) that

\sum_{i\in B_{+}^{t}}2y_{i}^{t}p_{i}^{t}\cdot w_{+}+\sum_{i\in B_{-}^{t}}2y_{i}^{t}p_{i}^{t}\cdot w_{-}\leqslant w_{+}\cdot\Delta^{t}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{n}-\frac{k_{3}\cdot(1-\delta^{t})}{n}-\frac{1}{n}-\frac{k_{4}\cdot\delta^{t}}{n}\right)\leqslant-\Delta^{t}\cdot\frac{c_{1}}{n},

where
c_{1}:=w_{+}\cdot\min\{k_{3},k_{4}\}
.

Case 2 [
\mathcal{P}_{1}
and
\mathcal{W}_{2}
hold]:
By applying
A.1
using the majorization conditions in
\mathcal{P}_{1}
,

\sum_{i\in B_{+}^{t}}2y_{i}^{t}p_{i}^{t}\cdot w_{+}+\sum_{i\in B_{-}^{t}}2y_{i}^{t}p_{i}^{t}\cdot w_{-}\leqslant\sum_{i\in B_{+}^{t}}2y_{i}^{t}\cdot\frac{w_{+}}{n}+\sum_{i\in B_{-}^{t}}2y_{i}^{t}\cdot\frac{w_{-}}{n}=\frac{\Delta^{t}}{n}\cdot\left(w_{+}-w_{-}\right)\leqslant-\Delta^{t}\cdot\frac{c_{1}}{n},

for
c_{1}:=w_{-}-w_{+}>0
, since the weights are constants satisfying
w_{-}>w_{+}
.
∎

We can now prove the main result in this section.

Lemma 6.2
.

Consider any
{\hyperlink{p1}{\mathcal{P}_{1}}}\cap{\hyperlink{w1}{\mathcal{W}_{1}}}
-process. Then, for any round
t\geqslant 0
,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\Upsilon^{t+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]\leqslant\Upsilon^{t}+\sum_{i\in B_{+}^{t}}2y_{i}^{t}p_{i}^{t}\cdot w_{+}+\sum_{i\in B_{-}^{t}}2y_{i}^{t}p_{i}^{t}\cdot w_{-}+4\cdot(w_{-})^{2}.

Hence for any
{\hyperlink{p2}{\mathcal{P}_{2}}}\cap{\hyperlink{w1}{\mathcal{W}_{1}}}
-process or
{\hyperlink{p1}{\mathcal{P}_{1}}}\cap{\hyperlink{w2}{\mathcal{W}_{2}}}
-process, this implies by
6.1
that there exist constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that for any round
t\geqslant 0
,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\Upsilon^{t+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]\leqslant\Upsilon^{t}-\frac{c_{1}}{n}\cdot\Delta^{t}+c_{2}.

Proof.

We begin by decomposing the quadratic potential
\Upsilon
over the
n
bins, using
\Upsilon_{i}^{t+1}:=(y_{i}^{t+1})^{2}
:

\Upsilon^{t+1}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Upsilon_{i}^{t+1}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_{i}^{t+1})^{2}.

We shall analyze the aggregate terms for overloaded and underloaded bins. By the
\mathcal{W}_{1}
condition, if the chosen bin is overloaded, we allocate a ball of weight
w_{+}
, otherwise we allocate a ball of weight
w_{-}
. Also, recall that
P_{+}^{t}:=\sum_{i\in B_{+}^{t}}p_{i}^{t}
and
P_{-}^{t}:=\sum_{i\in B_{-}^{t}}p_{i}^{t}
.

Case 1
[Overloaded bins]. Let
i\in[n]
be any overloaded bin, Then,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\Upsilon_{i}^{t+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\underbrace{p_{i}^{t}\cdot\Big(y_{i}^{t}+w_{+}-\frac{w_{+}}{n}\Big)^{2}}_{\text{allocate to $i$}}+\underbrace{(P_{+}^{t}-p_{i}^{t})\cdot\Big(y_{i}^{t}-\frac{w_{+}}{n}\Big)^{2}}_{\text{allocate to $B_{+}^{t}\setminus\{i\}$}}+\underbrace{P_{-}^{t}\cdot\Big(y_{i}^{t}-\frac{w_{-}}{n}\Big)^{2}}_{\text{allocate to $B_{-}^{t}$}}.

Aggregating over all overloaded bins and expanding out the squares,

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in B_{+}^{t}}\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\Upsilon_{i}^{t+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in B_{+}^{t}}\bigg[(y_{i}^{t})^{2}+2y_{i}^{t}\cdot\left(p_{i}^{t}\cdot\Big(w_{+}-\frac{w_{+}}{n}\Big)-(P_{+}^{t}-p_{i}^{t})\cdot\frac{w_{+}}{n}-P_{-}^{t}\cdot\frac{w_{-}}{n}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+p_{i}^{t}\cdot\Big(w_{+}-\frac{w_{+}}{n}\Big)^{2}+(P_{+}^{t}-p_{i}^{t})\cdot\frac{(w_{+})^{2}}{n^{2}}+P_{-}^{t}\cdot\frac{(w_{-})^{2}}{n^{2}}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in B_{+}^{t}}\bigg[(y_{i}^{t})^{2}+2y_{i}^{t}\cdot\Big(p_{i}^{t}\cdot w_{+}-P_{+}^{t}\cdot\frac{w_{+}}{n}-P_{-}^{t}\cdot\frac{w_{-}}{n}\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad+p_{i}^{t}\cdot(w_{+})^{2}-2\cdot p_{i}^{t}\cdot\frac{(w_{+})^{2}}{n}+P_{+}^{t}\cdot\frac{(w_{+})^{2}}{n^{2}}+P_{-}^{t}\cdot\frac{(w_{-})^{2}}{n^{2}}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i\in B_{+}^{t}}\bigg[(y_{i}^{t})^{2}+2y_{i}^{t}\cdot\Big(p_{i}^{t}\cdot w_{+}-P_{+}^{t}\cdot\frac{w_{+}}{n}-P_{-}^{t}\cdot\frac{w_{-}}{n}\Big)\bigg]+2\cdot(w_{-})^{2},

(6.2)

using in the last step that

\sum_{i\in B_{+}^{t}}\bigg[p_{i}^{t}\cdot(w_{+})^{2}+P_{+}^{t}\cdot\frac{(w_{+})^{2}}{n^{2}}+P_{-}^{t}\cdot\frac{(w_{-})^{2}}{n^{2}}\bigg]\leqslant(w_{+})^{2}+P_{+}^{t}\cdot\frac{(w_{+})^{2}}{n}+P_{-}^{t}\cdot\frac{(w_{-})^{2}}{n}\leqslant 2\cdot(w_{-})^{2}.

Case 2
[Underloaded bins]. Let
i\in[n]
be any underloaded bin. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\Upsilon_{i}^{t+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\underbrace{p_{i}^{t}\cdot\Big(y_{i}^{t}+w_{-}-\frac{w_{-}}{n}\Big)^{2}}_{\text{allocate to $i$}}+\underbrace{(P_{-}^{t}-p_{i}^{t})\cdot\Big(y_{i}^{t}-\frac{w_{-}}{n}\Big)^{2}}_{\text{allocate to $i\in B_{-}^{t}\setminus\{i\}$}}+\underbrace{P_{+}^{t}\cdot\Big(y_{i}^{t}-\frac{w_{+}}{n}\Big)^{2}}_{\text{allocate to $i\in B_{+}^{t}$}}.

Aggregating over all underloaded bins and expanding out the squares,

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in B_{-}^{t}}\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\Upsilon_{i}^{t+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in B_{-}^{t}}\bigg[(y_{i}^{t})^{2}+2y_{i}^{t}\cdot\Big(p_{i}^{t}\cdot\Big(w_{-}-\frac{w_{-}}{n}\Big)-(P_{-}^{t}-p_{i}^{t})\cdot\frac{w_{-}}{n}-P_{+}^{t}\cdot\frac{w_{+}}{n}\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad+p_{i}^{t}\cdot\Big(w_{-}-\frac{w_{-}}{n}\Big)^{2}+(P_{-}^{t}-p_{i}^{t})\cdot\frac{(w_{-})^{2}}{n^{2}}+P_{+}^{t}\cdot\frac{(w_{+})^{2}}{n^{2}}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in B_{-}^{t}}\bigg[(y_{i}^{t})^{2}+2y_{i}^{t}\cdot\Big(p_{i}^{t}\cdot w_{-}-P_{-}^{t}\cdot\frac{w_{-}}{n}-P_{+}^{t}\cdot\frac{w_{+}}{n}\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad+p_{i}^{t}\cdot(w_{-})^{2}-2\cdot p_{i}^{t}\cdot\frac{(w_{-})^{2}}{n}+P_{-}^{t}\cdot\frac{(w_{-})^{2}}{n^{2}}+P_{+}^{t}\cdot\frac{(w_{+})^{2}}{n^{2}}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i\in B_{-}^{t}}\bigg[(y_{i}^{t})^{2}+2y_{i}^{t}\cdot\Big(p_{i}^{t}\cdot w_{-}-P_{-}^{t}\cdot\frac{w_{-}}{n}-P_{+}^{t}\cdot\frac{w_{+}}{n}\Big)\bigg]+2\cdot(w_{-})^{2},

(6.3)

using in the last step that

\sum_{i\in B_{-}^{t}}\bigg[p_{i}^{t}\cdot(w_{-})^{2}+P_{-}^{t}\cdot\frac{(w_{-})^{2}}{n^{2}}+P_{+}^{t}\cdot\frac{(w_{+})^{2}}{n^{2}}\bigg]\leqslant(w_{+})^{2}+P_{+}^{t}\cdot\frac{(w_{+})^{2}}{n}+P_{-}^{t}\cdot\frac{(w_{-})^{2}}{n}\leqslant 2\cdot(w_{-})^{2}.

Recall that
\sum_{i\in B_{+}^{t}}y_{i}^{t}=-\sum_{i\in B_{-}^{t}}y_{i}^{t}=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\Delta^{t}
. This leads to cancellation from some terms in the brackets when we combine (
6.1
) and (
6.1
), as follows

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\Upsilon^{t+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in B_{+}^{t}}\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\Upsilon_{i}^{t+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]+\sum_{i\in B_{-}^{t}}\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\Upsilon_{i}^{t+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\Upsilon^{t}+\sum_{i\in B_{+}^{t}}2y_{i}^{t}p_{i}^{t}\cdot w_{+}+\sum_{i\in B_{-}^{t}}2y_{i}^{t}p_{i}^{t}\cdot w_{-}+4\cdot(w_{-})^{2}.\qed

6.2
Exponential Potential

In this section, we consider the exponential potential
\Lambda:=\Lambda(\alpha)
defined in
Eq.

4.1
. Let
\mathcal{G}^{t}:=\mathcal{G}^{t}(\varepsilon)
be the event that
\delta^{t}\in[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]
holds. In
6.4
, we will show that the potential
\Lambda
drops in expectation over one round
t
when
\mathcal{G}^{t}
holds, i.e.,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\Lambda^{t+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t},\mathcal{G}^{t}\,\right]\leqslant\Lambda^{t}\cdot\left(1-\frac{2c_{3}\alpha}{n}\right)+8.

Note that for constant smoothing parameter
\alpha
, the exponential potential cannot decrease in expectation in every round (
B.1
). Therefore, in
6.5
we will show that it does not increase too much in a round when the mean quantile is not good, i.e.,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\Lambda^{t+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t},\neg\mathcal{G}^{t}\,\right]\leqslant\Lambda^{t}\cdot\left(1+\frac{c_{4}\alpha^{2}}{2n}\right)+c_{4}.

In the above expression the decrease factor can be made arbitrarily larger than the increase factor, by choosing
\alpha>0
small enough. Once we prove that there is a constant fraction of rounds with a good quantile (
7.3
), we will use an adjusted version of
\Lambda
(defined in (
7.3
)) to show that overall it decreases in expectation (
7.4
and
7.5
), when the potential is sufficiently large.

For the analysis of
\Lambda
as well as
V
, we consider the labeling of the bins
i\in[n]
used by the allocation process in round
t
so that
x_{i}^{t}
is non-decreasing in
i\in[n]
. We write

\Lambda^{t}(\alpha)=:\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Lambda_{i}^{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{\alpha|y_{i}^{t}|}\quad\Big(\text{and}\quad V^{t}(\widetilde{\alpha})=:\sum_{i=1}^{n}V_{i}^{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{\widetilde{\alpha}|y_{i}^{t}|}\Big),

and handle separately the following three cases of bins based on their load:

•

Case 1
[Robustly Overloaded Bins]. The set of bins
B_{++}^{t}
defined as the set of bins with load
y_{i}^{t}\geqslant\frac{w_{-}}{n}
. These bins are
robustly overloaded
in the sense that they are guaranteed to be overloaded in round
t+1
(i.e., to be in
B_{+}^{t+1}
), since the mean load can increase by at most
\frac{w_{-}}{n}
.

For the exponential potential
\Lambda^{t}
(and
V^{t}
respectively), the expected contribution of a single bin
i\in B_{++}^{t}
in
\Lambda^{t+1}
is given by,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\Lambda_{i}^{t+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]=\Lambda_{i}^{t}\cdot\Big(\underbrace{p_{i}^{t}\cdot e^{\alpha w_{+}-\alpha w_{+}/n}}_{\text{allocate to $i$}}+\underbrace{(P_{+}^{t}-p_{i}^{t})\cdot e^{-\alpha w_{+}/n}}_{\text{allocate to $B_{+}^{t}\setminus\{i\}$}}+\underbrace{P_{-}^{t}\cdot e^{-\alpha w_{-}/n}}_{\text{allocate to $B_{-}^{t}$}}\Big).

(6.4)

•

Case 2
[Robustly Underloaded Bins]. The set of bins
B_{--}^{t}
with load
y_{i}^{t}\leqslant-w_{-}
. These are bins in
B_{-}^{t}
that are guaranteed to be underloaded in round
t+1
(i.e., to be in
B_{-}^{t+1}
), since any bin can be allocated a ball of weight at most
w_{-}
in one round.

For the exponential potential
\Lambda^{t}
(and
V^{t}
respectively), the expected contribution of a single bin
i\in B_{--}^{t}
in
\Lambda^{t+1}
is given by,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\Lambda_{i}^{t+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]=\Lambda_{i}^{t}\cdot\Big(\underbrace{p_{i}^{t}\cdot e^{-\alpha w_{-}+\alpha w_{-}/n}}_{\text{allocate to $i$}}+\underbrace{(P_{-}^{t}-p_{i}^{t})\cdot e^{\alpha w_{-}/n}}_{\text{allocate to $B_{-}^{t}\setminus\{i\}$}}+\underbrace{P_{+}^{t}\cdot e^{\alpha w_{+}/n}}_{\text{allocate to $B_{+}^{t}$}}\Big).

(6.5)

•

Case 3
[Swinging Bins]. The set of bins
B_{+/-}^{t}
with load
y_{i}^{t}\in(-w_{-},\frac{w_{-}}{n})
.

We begin by showing that the aggregated contribution of the swinging bins to the expected change of the potential
\Lambda
is
small
. This will be used in the proofs of Lemmas
6.4
and
6.5
.

Lemma 6.3
.

Consider the potential
\Lambda:=\Lambda(\alpha)
for any
\alpha\in(0,\frac{1}{4w_{-}}]
and any non-negative vector
(\kappa_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
. Then, for any set of swinging bins
S\subseteq B_{+/-}^{t}
and for any round
t\geqslant 0
,

\sum_{i\in S}\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\Lambda_{i}^{t+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]\leqslant\sum_{i\in S}\Lambda_{i}^{t}\cdot(1-\kappa_{i})+2\cdot\sum_{i\in S}\kappa_{i}+3.

Note that this statement says that we can make the
multiplicative factor
arbitrarily small at the expense of increasing the
additive factor
.

Proof.

For each bin
i\in B_{+/-}^{t}
, the two events affecting the contribution of the bin to
\Lambda^{t+1}
are
(i)
“internal” due to a ball being allocated to
i
and
(ii)
“external” due to the change in the mean.

For
(i)
, the chosen bin
i\in B_{+/-}^{t}
can increase by at most
w_{-}
, so the potential value satisfies
\Lambda_{i}^{t+1}\leqslant e^{2\alpha w_{-}}
, since
\alpha\leqslant\frac{1}{4w_{-}}
. For
(ii)
, the maximum change in the mean load is at most
w_{-}/n
, and so
\Lambda_{i}^{t+1}\leqslant\Lambda_{i}^{t}\cdot e^{\alpha w_{-}/n}
. Combining the two contributions and aggregating over all bins in
S
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in S}\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\Lambda_{i}^{t+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i\in S}\Lambda_{i}^{t}\cdot e^{\alpha w_{-}/n}\cdot(1-p_{i}^{t})+\sum_{i\in S}2\cdot p_{i}^{t}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i\in S}\Lambda_{i}^{t}\cdot e^{\alpha w_{-}/n}+e^{2\alpha w_{-}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leqslant}}\sum_{i\in S}\Lambda_{i}^{t}\cdot\Big(1+\frac{2\alpha w_{-}}{n}\Big)+e^{2\alpha w_{-}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\leqslant}}\sum_{i\in S}\Lambda_{i}^{t}+2\alpha w_{-}\cdot e^{\alpha w_{-}}+e^{2\alpha w_{-}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(c)}}{{\leqslant}}\sum_{i\in S}\Lambda_{i}^{t}+3,

using in
(a)
the Taylor estimate
e^{z}\leqslant 1+2z
for any
z<1.2
, for sufficiently large
n
, in
(b)
that
\Lambda_{i}^{t}\leqslant e^{\alpha w_{-}}
for any
i\in B_{+/-}^{t}
and in
(c)
that
2\alpha w_{-}\cdot e^{\alpha w_{-}}+e^{2\alpha w_{-}}\leqslant 3
since
\alpha w_{-}\leqslant\frac{1}{4}
. By adding and subtracting
\sum_{i\in S}\Lambda_{i}^{t}\cdot\kappa_{i}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in S}\mathbf{E}[\,\Lambda_{i}^{t+1}\mid\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,]

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i\in S}\Lambda_{i}^{t}\cdot(1-\kappa_{i})+\sum_{i\in S}\Lambda_{i}^{t}\cdot\kappa_{i}+3\leqslant\sum_{i\in S}\Lambda_{i}^{t}\cdot(1-\kappa_{i})+2\cdot\sum_{i\in S}\kappa_{i}+3,

using that
\Lambda_{i}^{t}\leqslant e^{\alpha w_{-}}\leqslant 2
for
i\in S
.
∎

Next, we show that if the mean quantile
\delta^{t}
of the mean is in
[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]
, then the potential
\Lambda
drops in expectation over one round. The following lemma applies to both
\Lambda
and
V
, as
V
is an instance of
\Lambda
. For
\Lambda:=\Lambda(\alpha)
with
\alpha=\Theta(1)
this lemma applies when
\varepsilon
is constant (
6.6
). For
V:=V(\widetilde{\alpha})
with

6.3
Deterministic Bounds on the Quadratic Potential

The next lemma provides a bound for the quadratic potential in terms of the exponential potential
\Lambda
and holds for an arbitrary process.

Lemma 6.9
.

Consider an arbitrary load vector
x^{t}
and the potentials
\Upsilon^{t}:=\Upsilon^{t}(x^{t})
and
\Lambda^{t}:=\Lambda(\alpha,x^{t})
for any
\alpha\in(0,1]
. Then,

\Upsilon^{t}\leqslant\left(\frac{4}{\alpha}\cdot\log\left(\frac{4}{\alpha}\right)\right)^{2}\cdot\Lambda^{t}.

Proof.

Let
\kappa:=(4/\alpha)\cdot\log(4/\alpha)
. For any
y\geqslant\kappa
, it follows that

e^{\alpha y/2}=e^{\alpha y/4}\cdot e^{\alpha y/4}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\geqslant}}\frac{\alpha y}{4}\cdot e^{\alpha y/4}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\geqslant}}\frac{\alpha y}{4}\cdot\frac{4}{\alpha}\geqslant y,

using in
(a)
that
e^{y}\geqslant y
(for any
y\geqslant 0
) and in
(b)
that
\frac{\alpha y}{4}\geqslant\log(4/\alpha)
(as
y\geqslant\kappa
). Hence for
y\geqslant\kappa
,

e^{\alpha y}=e^{\alpha y/2}\cdot e^{\alpha y/2}\geqslant y\cdot y=y^{2}.

Thus, we conclude

\displaystyle\Upsilon^{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_{i}^{t})^{2}\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{n}\max\left\{\Lambda_{i}^{t},\kappa^{2}\right\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leqslant}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\max\left\{\Lambda_{i}^{t},\kappa^{2}\cdot\Lambda_{i}^{t}\right\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{=}}\kappa^{2}\cdot\Lambda^{t},

using in
(a)
that
\Lambda_{i}^{t}\geqslant 1
for any
i\in[n]
and in
(b)
that
\kappa\geqslant 1
.
∎

The next lemma is very basic but is used in
7.3
, so we prove it for completeness.

Lemma 6.10
.

Consider any
W

1

-process. Then, for any round
t\geqslant 0

\left|\Upsilon^{t+1}-\Upsilon^{t}\right|\leqslant 4w_{-}\cdot\max_{i\in[n]}|y_{i}^{t}|+2w_{-}^{2}.

Proof.

Recall that
w^{t+1}:=W^{t+1}-W^{t}
is the number of balls allocated in the
(t+1)
-th allocation. We will upper bound the change in the quadratic potential using

\left|\Upsilon^{t+1}-\Upsilon^{t}\right|\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\Upsilon_{i}^{t+1}-\Upsilon_{i}^{t}\right|,

where
\Upsilon_{i}^{t}:=(y_{i}^{t})^{2}
and
\Upsilon_{i}^{t+1}:=(y_{i}^{t+1})^{2}
. Now, let
i\in[n]
be an arbitrary bin. We bound
|\Upsilon_{i}^{t+1}-\Upsilon_{i}^{t}|
by considering two cases based on whether bin
i
was allocated to in the
(t+1)
-th allocation or not:

Case 1:
If
i
was allocated to, then
y_{i+1}^{t}=y_{i}^{t}+w^{t+1}\cdot\big(1-\frac{1}{n}\big)
and so

\displaystyle\left|\Upsilon_{i}^{t+1}-\Upsilon_{i}^{t}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\left(y_{i}^{t}+w^{t+1}\cdot\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{n}\Bigr)\right)^{2}-\left(y_{i}^{t}\right)^{2}\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\cdot|y_{i}^{t}|\cdot\left|w^{t+1}\cdot\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{n}\Bigr)\right|+\left|w^{t+1}\cdot\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{n}\Bigr)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\cdot\max_{j\in[n]}|y_{j}^{t}|\cdot w_{-}+w_{-}^{2}.

Case 2:
If
i
was not allocated to, then
y_{i+1}^{t}=y_{i}^{t}-\frac{w^{t+1}}{n}
and so

\displaystyle\left|\Upsilon_{i}^{t+1}-\Upsilon_{i}^{t}\right|=\left|\left(y_{i}^{t}-\frac{w^{t+1}}{n}\right)^{2}-\left(y_{i}^{t}\right)^{2}\right|\leqslant 2\cdot\left|\frac{w^{t+1}}{n}\right|\cdot|y_{i}^{t}|+\left|\frac{w^{t+1}}{n}\right|^{2}\leqslant\frac{2w_{-}}{n}\cdot\max_{j\in[n]}|y_{j}^{t}|+\frac{w_{-}^{2}}{n^{2}},

using that

.

By aggregating over all bins using the two cases above, we get the conclusion.
∎

G_{t_{0}}^{s}:=G_{t_{0}}^{s}(\varepsilon)
be the number of rounds
t\in[t_{0},s]
with
\delta^{t}\in[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]
, and similarly we define
B_{t_{0}}^{s}:=(s-t_{0}+1)-G_{t_{0}}^{s}
.

We now introduce the
adjusted exponential potential function

\widetilde{\Lambda}_{t_{0}}^{s}
which involves the random variables and event above. Let
c_{3}:=c_{3}(\varepsilon)>0
be the constant in
6.4
and
c_{4}:=c_{4}(\varepsilon)>0
the constant in
6.5
. Then we define
\widetilde{\Lambda}_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}}(\alpha,\varepsilon,c_{3},c_{4}):=\Lambda^{t_{0}}(\alpha)
and, for any round
s>t_{0}
,

\widetilde{\Lambda}_{t_{0}}^{s}(\alpha,\varepsilon,c_{3},c_{4}):=\Lambda^{s}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t_{0}}^{s-1}}\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{c_{4}\alpha^{2}}{n}\cdot B_{t_{0}}^{s-1}(\varepsilon)\right)\cdot\exp\left(+\frac{c_{3}\alpha}{n}\cdot G_{t_{0}}^{s-1}(\varepsilon)\right).

(7.3)

We also define
\widetilde{G}_{t_{0}}^{s}:=\widetilde{G}_{t_{0}}^{s}(C)
to be the number of rounds
t\in[t_{0},s]
with
\Delta^{t}\leqslant Cn
.

7.1
The Adjusted Exponential Potential is a Super-Martingale

We now show that the sequence defined in (
7.3
) forms a super-martingale.

Lemma 7.1
.

Consider any
{\hyperlink{p2}{\mathcal{P}_{2}}}\cap{\hyperlink{w1}{\mathcal{W}_{1}}}
-process or
{\hyperlink{p1}{\mathcal{P}_{1}}}\cap{\hyperlink{w2}{\mathcal{W}_{2}}}
-process and an arbitrary round
t_{0}\geqslant 0
. Further, let the potential
\widetilde{\Lambda}_{t_{0}}:=\widetilde{\Lambda}_{t_{0}}(\alpha,\varepsilon,c_{3},c_{4})
for
\alpha>0
as defined in (
7.2
),
\varepsilon>0
as defined in (
7.1
), and
c_{3}:=c_{3}(\varepsilon)>0
and
c_{4}:=c_{4}(\varepsilon)>0
as defined in Lemmas
6.4
and
6.5
respectively. Then, for any round
s\geqslant t_{0}
,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\widetilde{\Lambda}_{t_{0}}^{s+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{s}\,\right]\leqslant\widetilde{\Lambda}_{t_{0}}^{s}.

Proof.

We see that
\mathbf{E}[\,\widetilde{\Lambda}_{t_{0}}^{s+1}\mid\mathfrak{F}^{s}\,]
is given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\Lambda^{s+1}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t_{0}}^{s}}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{s}\,\right]\cdot\exp\bigg(-\frac{c_{4}\alpha^{2}}{n}\cdot B_{t_{0}}^{s}\bigg)\cdot\exp\bigg(\frac{c_{3}\alpha}{n}\cdot G_{t_{0}}^{s}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\Lambda^{s+1}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t_{0}}^{s}}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{s}\,\right]\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{c_{4}\alpha^{2}}{n}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\neg\mathcal{G}^{s}}+\frac{c_{3}\alpha}{n}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{G}^{s}}\right)\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{c_{4}\alpha^{2}}{n}\cdot B_{t_{0}}^{s-1}\right)\cdot\exp\left(\frac{c_{3}\alpha}{n}\cdot G_{t_{0}}^{s-1}\right).

Thus, we see that it suffices to prove that

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\Lambda^{s+1}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t_{0}}^{s}}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{s}\,\right]\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{c_{4}\alpha^{2}}{n}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\neg\mathcal{G}^{s}}+\frac{c_{3}\alpha}{n}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{G}^{s}}\right)\leqslant\Lambda^{s}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t_{0}}^{s-1}}.

(7.4)

To show (
7.4
), we consider two cases based on whether
\mathcal{G}^{s}
holds.

Case 1
[
\mathcal{G}^{s}
holds]. Recall that the event
\mathcal{G}^{s}
means
\delta^{s}\in[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]
holds. Further, we are additionally conditioning on the event
\mathcal{E}_{t_{0}}^{s}
, via the indicator, and so
\Lambda^{t}>cn
holds for any round
t\in[t_{0},s]
. Thus we can use the upper bound from
6.6
to get

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\Lambda^{s+1}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t_{0}}^{s}}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{s},\mathcal{G}^{s}\,\right]\leqslant\Lambda^{s}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t_{0}}^{s-1}}\cdot\left(1-\frac{c_{3}\alpha}{n}\right)\leqslant\Lambda^{s}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t_{0}}^{s-1}}\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{c_{3}\alpha}{n}\right).

Hence, since in this case
\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{G}^{s}}=1
, the left-hand side of (
7.4
) is equal to

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\Lambda^{s+1}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t_{0}}^{s}}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{s},\mathcal{G}^{s}\,\right]\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{c_{4}\alpha^{2}}{n}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\neg\mathcal{G}^{s}}+\frac{c_{3}\alpha}{n}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{G}^{s}}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant\left(\Lambda^{s}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t_{0}}^{s-1}}\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{c_{3}\alpha}{n}\right)\right)\cdot\exp\left(\frac{c_{3}\alpha}{n}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=\Lambda^{s}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t_{0}}^{s-1}}.

Case 2
[
\mathcal{G}^{s}
does not hold]. Similarly, by
6.6

(ii)
.

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\Lambda^{s+1}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t_{0}}^{s}}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{s},\neg\mathcal{G}^{s}\,\right]\leqslant\Lambda^{s}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t_{0}}^{s-1}}\cdot\left(1+\frac{c_{4}\alpha^{2}}{n}\right)\leqslant\Lambda^{s}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t_{0}}^{s-1}}\cdot\exp\left(\frac{c_{4}\alpha^{2}}{n}\right).

Hence, since in this case
\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{G}^{s}}=0
, the left-hand side of (
7.4
) is equal to

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\Lambda^{s+1}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t_{0}}^{s}}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{s},\neg\mathcal{G}^{s}\,\right]\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{c_{4}\alpha^{2}}{n}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\neg\mathcal{G}^{s}}+\frac{c_{3}\alpha}{n}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{G}^{s}}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant\left(\Lambda^{s}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t_{0}}^{s-1}}\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{c_{4}\alpha^{2}}{n}\right)\right)\cdot\exp\left(\frac{c_{4}\alpha^{2}}{n}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=\Lambda^{s}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t_{0}}^{s-1}}.

Since (
7.4
) holds in both cases, we deduce that
(\widetilde{\Lambda}_{t_{0}}^{s})_{s\geqslant t_{0}}
forms a super-martingale.
∎

7.2
Taming the Mean Quantile and Absolute Value Potential

We have seen in the previous section that if we augment the exponential potential
\Lambda^{t}
with some terms involving the random variable
G_{t_{0}}^{s}
, then the resulting potential
\widetilde{\Lambda}^{t}
is a super-martingale. Thus, it will be useful to control
G_{t_{0}}^{s}:=G_{t_{0}}^{s}(\varepsilon)
, which we recall is the number of rounds
t\in[t_{0},s]
with
\delta^{t}\in[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]
. This is done in part by controlling
\widetilde{G}_{t_{0}}^{s}:=\widetilde{G}_{t_{0}}^{s}(C)
, which we recall is the number of rounds
t\in[t_{0},s]
with
\Delta^{t}\leqslant Cn
. In particular, our first lemma shows that for an interval of length at least
\frac{n}{4\varepsilon}
, if we have a constant fraction of round
t
with
\Delta^{t}\leqslant Cn
, then w.h.p. we also have a constant fraction of rounds with
\delta^{t}\in[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]
.

Lemma 7.2
.

Consider any
{\hyperlink{p2}{\mathcal{P}_{2}}}\cap{\hyperlink{w1}{\mathcal{W}_{1}}}
-process or
{\hyperlink{p1}{\mathcal{P}_{1}}}\cap{\hyperlink{w2}{\mathcal{W}_{2}}}
-process and let
\varepsilon>0
as defined in (
7.1
). Then, for any rounds
t_{0}\geqslant 0
and
t_{1}\geqslant t_{0}+\frac{n}{4\varepsilon}
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,G_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}(\varepsilon)\geqslant 4\varepsilon^{2}\cdot\widetilde{G}_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}-\frac{n}{4\varepsilon}}(C)\;\Big|\;\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}}\,\right]\geqslant 1-(t_{1}-t_{0})\cdot e^{-\varepsilon n}.

Proof.

Let
\ell:=\frac{n}{4\varepsilon}
. For any round
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
, define

\mathcal{H}^{t}:=\Bigl\{\Delta^{t}>Cn\Bigr\}\cup\Bigl\{\bigl|\left\{s\in[t,t+\ell]\colon\delta^{s}\in[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]\right\}\bigr|\geqslant\varepsilon n\Bigr\}.

Note that event
\mathcal{H}^{t}
is logically equivalent to the statement:
\Delta^{t}\leqslant Cn
implies

\left|\left\{s\in[t,t+\ell]\colon\delta^{s}\in[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]\right\}\right|\geqslant\varepsilon n.

Then by
5.1
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathcal{H}^{t}\;\Big|\;\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}}\,\right]\geqslant 1-e^{-\varepsilon n},

and so the union bound gives

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left.\bigcap_{t=t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\mathcal{H}^{t}\;\right|\;\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}}\,\right]\geqslant 1-(t_{1}-t_{0})\cdot e^{-\varepsilon n}.

(7.5)

In the following, we will condition on the event
\bigcap_{t=t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\mathcal{H}^{t}
. Next define for any
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}-\ell]
,

g^{t}:=\left\{s\in[t,t+\ell]\colon\delta^{s}\in[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]\right\}.

Then, conditional on
Eq.

7.5
,

\displaystyle G_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}

\displaystyle\geqslant\left|\bigcup_{t=t_{0}}^{t_{1}-\ell}g^{t}\right|\geqslant\frac{\sum_{t=t_{0}}^{t_{1}-\ell}|g^{t}|}{\max_{s\in[t,t+\ell]}\Bigl|\bigr\{t\in[t_{0},t_{1}-\ell]\colon s\in g^{t}\bigr\}\Bigr|}\geqslant\frac{\widetilde{G}_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}-\ell}\cdot\varepsilon\cdot n}{\ell}\geqslant\widetilde{G}_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}-\frac{n}{4\varepsilon}}\cdot 4\varepsilon^{2},

using in the last step that
\ell:=\frac{n}{4\varepsilon}
. This completes the proof.∎

The next lemma establishes the key fact that w.h.p. when
\Upsilon^{t_{0}}\leqslant T
there are many rounds close to
t_{0}
with a good quantile (or the gap becomes too large). We will apply this lemma twice, once in the recovery phase with
T:=n^{3}\log^{4}n
(
7.4
) and once in the stabilization phase with
T:=\Theta(n\log n)
(
7.5
)

Lemma 7.3
.

Consider any
{\hyperlink{p2}{\mathcal{P}_{2}}}\cap{\hyperlink{w1}{\mathcal{W}_{1}}}
-process or
{\hyperlink{p1}{\mathcal{P}_{1}}}\cap{\hyperlink{w2}{\mathcal{W}_{2}}}
-process and the potential
\Lambda:=\Lambda(\alpha)
with
\alpha>0
as defined in (
7.2
) and
\varepsilon>0
as defined in (
7.1
). Further, consider any
T\geqslant\frac{n}{\varepsilon}
, any round
t_{0}\geqslant 0
and let

\tau:=\inf\Big\{t\geqslant t_{0}\colon\max_{i\in[n]}|y_{i}^{t}|>\sqrt{T}\cdot\log^{-2}n\Big\}.

Then, for any round
t_{1}\geqslant 0
satisfying
t_{0}+T\leqslant t_{1}\leqslant t_{0}+T\log^{2}n
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left\{G_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}(\varepsilon)\geqslant\varepsilon^{2}\cdot(t_{1}-t_{0})\right\}\cup\{\tau\leqslant t_{1}\}\;\Big|\;\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\Upsilon^{t_{0}}\leqslant T\,\right]\geqslant 1-(t_{1}-t_{0})\cdot e^{-\varepsilon n}-n^{-\omega(1)}.

Proof.

Let
c_{1},c_{2}>0
be the constants given in
6.2
. We define
Z^{t_{0}}:=\Upsilon^{t_{0}}
and for any round
t>t_{0}

Z^{t}:=\Upsilon^{t}-c_{2}\cdot(t-t_{0})+\frac{c_{1}}{n}\sum_{s=t_{0}}^{t-1}\Delta^{s}.

This sequence forms a super-martingale since by
6.2
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.Z^{t+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\Upsilon^{t+1}-c_{2}\cdot(t-t_{0}+1)+\frac{c_{1}}{n}\sum_{s=t_{0}}^{t}\Delta^{s}~\right|~\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\Upsilon^{t+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]-c_{2}\cdot(t-t_{0}+1)+\frac{c_{1}}{n}\sum_{s=t_{0}}^{t}\Delta^{s}

\displaystyle\leqslant\Upsilon^{t}+c_{2}-\frac{c_{1}}{n}\cdot\Delta^{t}-c_{2}\cdot(t-t_{0}+1)+\frac{c_{1}}{n}\sum_{s=t_{0}}^{t}\Delta^{s}

\displaystyle=\Upsilon^{t}-c_{2}\cdot(t-t_{0})+\frac{c_{1}}{n}\sum_{s=t_{0}}^{t-1}\Delta^{s}=Z^{t}.

Further, consider the stopped random variable

\widetilde{Z}^{t}:=Z^{t\wedge\tau},

which also forms a super-martingale.
To prove concentration of
\widetilde{Z}^{t+1}
, we will now derive an upper bound on
\left|\widetilde{Z}^{t+1}-\widetilde{Z}^{t}\right|
, by considering the following two cases:

Case 1
[
t\geqslant\tau
].
In this case,
\widetilde{Z}^{t+1}=Z^{(t+1)\wedge\tau}=Z^{\tau}
, and similarly,
\widetilde{Z}^{t}=Z^{t\wedge\tau}=Z^{\tau}
, so

\left|\widetilde{Z}^{t+1}-\widetilde{Z}^{t}\right|=0.

Case 2
[
t<\tau
]. In this case, we have that
\max_{i\in[n]}|y_{i}^{t}|\leqslant\sqrt{T}\cdot\log^{-2}n
and so

\displaystyle\left|\widetilde{Z}^{t+1}-\widetilde{Z}^{t}\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\left|\Upsilon^{t+1}-\Upsilon^{t}\right|+c_{2}+\frac{c_{1}}{n}\cdot\Delta^{t}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leqslant}}5w_{-}\cdot\sqrt{T}\cdot\log^{-2}n+c_{2}+c_{1}\sqrt{T}\cdot\log^{-2}n

\displaystyle\leqslant(5w_{-}+2c_{1})\cdot\sqrt{T}\cdot\log^{-2}n,

using in
(a)
that
|\Upsilon^{t+1}-\Upsilon^{t}|\leqslant 4w_{-}\cdot\sqrt{T}\cdot\log^{-2}n+2w_{-}^{2}\leqslant 5w_{-}\cdot\sqrt{T}\cdot\log^{-2}n
by
6.10
, and
\Delta^{t}\leqslant n\cdot\max_{i\in[n]}|y_{i}^{t}|
.

Combining
Case 1
and
Case 2
above, we conclude

\left|\widetilde{Z}^{t+1}-\widetilde{Z}^{t}\right|\leqslant(5w_{-}+2c_{1})\cdot\sqrt{T}\cdot\log^{-2}n.

Using Azuma’s inequality (
A.4
) for super-martingales,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left.\widetilde{Z}^{t_{1}+1}-\widetilde{Z}^{t_{0}}>T\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\Upsilon^{t_{0}}\leqslant T\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{T^{2}}{2\cdot\sum_{t=t_{0}}^{t_{1}}((5w_{-}+2c_{1})\cdot\sqrt{T}\cdot\log^{-2}n)^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\frac{T^{2}}{2\cdot(t_{1}-t_{0})\cdot(5w_{-}+2c_{1})^{2}\cdot T\cdot\log^{-4}n}\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leqslant}}\exp\left(-\frac{\log^{2}n}{2\cdot(5w_{-}+2c_{1})^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{=}}n^{-\omega(1)},

using in
(a)
that
t_{1}\leqslant t_{0}+T\log^{2}n
and in
(b)
that
w_{+}
and
c_{1}
are constants.

For the sake of a contradiction, assume now that at least half of the rounds
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
satisfy
\Delta^{t}\geqslant Cn
, which implies that

\sum_{t=t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\Delta^{t}\geqslant\frac{t_{1}-t_{0}+1}{2}\cdot Cn.

When the event

\left\{\widetilde{Z}^{t_{1}+1}\leqslant\widetilde{Z}^{t_{0}}+T\right\}\cap\{\tau>t_{1}\}

holds, then we have that
\big\{Z^{t_{1}+1}\leqslant Z^{t_{0}}+T\big\}
and so

\Upsilon^{t_{1}+1}-c_{2}\cdot(t_{1}-t_{0}+1)+\frac{c_{1}}{n}\sum_{t=t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\Delta^{t}\leqslant\Upsilon^{t_{0}}+T.

Rearranging the inequality above gives

\displaystyle\Upsilon^{t_{1}+1}

\displaystyle\leqslant\Upsilon^{t_{0}}+T+c_{2}\cdot(t_{1}-t_{0}+1)-\frac{c_{1}}{n}\sum_{t=t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\Delta^{t}

\displaystyle\leqslant\Upsilon^{t_{0}}+T+c_{2}\cdot(t_{1}-t_{0}+1)-\frac{c_{1}}{n}\cdot\frac{t_{1}-t_{0}+1}{2}\cdot Cn

\displaystyle\leqslant\Upsilon^{t_{0}}+T+(t_{1}-t_{0}+1)\cdot\left(c_{2}-\frac{c_{1}}{2}\cdot C\right).

(7.6)

Recall that we start from a round
t_{0}
where
\Upsilon^{t_{0}}\leqslant T
, that
C>\frac{8c_{2}}{c_{1}}
(and
c_{2}>1
) and
t_{1}-t_{0}\geqslant T
. So by (
7.6
) we have

\Upsilon^{t_{1}+1}\leqslant T+T+T\cdot\left(c_{2}-\frac{c_{1}}{2}\cdot C\right)<2T-3T\cdot c_{2}<0,

which is a contradiction. We conclude that when
\{Z^{t_{1}+1}\leqslant Z^{t_{0}}+T\}
, then half of the rounds
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
satisfy
\Delta^{t}\leqslant Cn
, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left.\left\{\widetilde{G}_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\cdot(t_{1}-t_{0})\right\}\cup\{\tau\leqslant t_{1}\}~\right|~\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\Upsilon^{t_{0}}\leqslant T\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-\omega(1)}.

(7.7)

Now,
7.2
gives

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left.G_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\geqslant 4\varepsilon^{2}\cdot\widetilde{G}_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}-\frac{n}{4\varepsilon}}\;\right|\;\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\Upsilon^{t_{0}}\leqslant T\,\right]\geqslant 1-(t_{1}-t_{0})\cdot e^{-\varepsilon n}.

(7.8)

Then, noting that

\widetilde{G}_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}-\frac{n}{4\varepsilon}}\geqslant\widetilde{G}_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}-\frac{n}{4\varepsilon}
and
by taking the union bound of (
7.7
) and (
7.8
) we have,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left.\left\{G_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\geqslant 4\varepsilon^{2}\cdot\Big(\frac{1}{2}\cdot(t_{1}-t_{0})-\frac{n}{4\varepsilon}\Big)\right\}\cup\{\tau\leqslant t_{1}\}\;\right|\;\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\Upsilon^{t_{0}}\leqslant T\,\right]\geqslant 1-(t_{1}-t_{0})\cdot e^{-\varepsilon n}-n^{-\omega(1)}.

Since,
\frac{1}{4}\cdot(t_{1}-t_{0})\geqslant\frac{1}{4}T\geqslant\frac{n}{4\varepsilon}
, we can deduce that

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left.\left\{G_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\geqslant\varepsilon^{2}\cdot(t_{1}-t_{0})\right\}\cup\{\tau\leqslant t_{1}\}\;\right|\;\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\Upsilon^{t_{0}}\leqslant T\,\right]\geqslant 1-(t_{1}-t_{0})\cdot e^{-\varepsilon n}-n^{-\omega(1)}.\qed

7.3
Recovery Phase of the Process

In the following, we start the analysis at round
t_{0}=m-n^{3}\log^{4}n
and using
6.8
, we obtain that w.h.p.
\Gap(t_{0})=\mathcal{O}(n\log n)
and
\Lambda^{t_{0}}\leqslant n\cdot\exp(\mathcal{O}(n\log n))
. Using
7.3
we conclude that w.h.p. for a constant fraction of the rounds
t\in[t_{0},t_{0}+n^{3}\log^{4}n]
, it holds that
\delta^{t}\in[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]
.
Next, using the adjusted exponential potential
\widetilde{\Lambda}
we exploit the drop of the exponential potential function in those rounds to infer that w.h.p. there is a round
s\in[t_{0},t_{0}+n^{3}\log^{4}n]
with
\Lambda^{s}\leqslant cn
(
7.4
). In
Section

7.4
, we will prove that w.h.p. for the remaining rounds in
[s,m]
, which are at most
\mathcal{O}(n^{3}\log^{4}n)
, the potential becomes
\mathcal{O}(n)
once every
\mathcal{O}(n\log n)
rounds (
7.5
).

Lemma 7.4

(Recovery)
.

Consider any
{\hyperlink{p2}{\mathcal{P}_{2}}}\cap{\hyperlink{w1}{\mathcal{W}_{1}}}
-process or
{\hyperlink{p1}{\mathcal{P}_{1}}}\cap{\hyperlink{w2}{\mathcal{W}_{2}}}
-process and the potential
\Lambda:=\Lambda(\alpha)
for
\alpha>0
as defined in (
7.2
). Then, for
c>1
as defined in
6.6
, for any round
m\geqslant n^{3}\log^{4}n
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcup_{s\in[m-n^{3}\log^{4}n,m]}\{\Lambda^{s}\leqslant cn\}\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-5}.

Proof.

Let
T:=n^{3}\log^{4}n
,
t_{0}:=m-T
and
t_{1}:=m
. By
6.8
, there exists a constant
c_{5}>0
, such that

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\max_{i\in[n]}\left|y_{i}^{t_{0}}\right|\leqslant c_{5}n\log n\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-12}.

(7.9)

Note that when
\big\{\max_{i\in[n]}\left|y_{i}^{t_{0}}\right|\leqslant c_{5}n\log n\big\}
holds, then we also have that

\Upsilon^{t_{0}}\leqslant n\cdot(c_{5}n\log n)^{2}\leqslant c_{5}^{2}n^{3}\log^{2}n,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Lambda^{t_{0}}\leqslant n\cdot e^{\alpha c_{5}n\log n}\leqslant e^{2c_{5}n\log n}.

By
7.3
we have for
\tau:=\inf\big\{t\geqslant t_{0}\colon\max_{i\in[n]}|y_{i}^{t}|>\sqrt{T}\cdot\log^{-2}n\big\}
, and using that the event
\big\{\max_{i\in[n]}\left|y_{i}^{t_{0}}\right|\leqslant c_{5}n\log n\big\}
implies that
\{\Upsilon^{t_{0}}\leqslant T\}
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left\{G_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\geqslant\varepsilon^{2}\cdot(t_{1}-t_{0})\right\}\cup\{\tau\leqslant t_{1}\}\;\Big|\;\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\max_{i\in[n]}\left|y_{i}^{t_{0}}\right|\leqslant c_{5}n\log n\,\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\geqslant\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left\{G_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\geqslant\varepsilon^{2}\cdot(t_{1}-t_{0})\right\}\cup\{\tau\leqslant t_{1}\}\;\Big|\;\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\Upsilon^{t_{0}}\leqslant T\,\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\geqslant 1-T\cdot e^{-\varepsilon n}-n^{-\omega(1)}\geqslant 1-n^{-\omega(1)}.

By
6.8

(i)
and the union bound over
t_{1}-t_{0}=n^{3}\log^{4}n\leqslant n^{4}
rounds, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\tau>t_{1}\,\left|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\max_{i\in[n]}\left|y_{i}^{t_{0}}\right|\leqslant c_{5}n\log n\right.\,\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\geqslant\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left.\max_{t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]}\max_{i\in[n]}\left|y_{i}^{t}\right|\leqslant 2c_{5}n\log n\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\max_{i\in[n]}\left|y_{i}^{t_{0}}\right|\leqslant c_{5}n\log n\,\right]\geqslant 1-T\cdot n^{-12}\geqslant 1-n^{-8}.

Hence, taking the union bound for the above two inequalities, we get that

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,G_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\geqslant\varepsilon^{2}\cdot(t_{1}-t_{0})\;\left|\;\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\max_{i\in[n]}\left|y_{i}^{t_{0}}\right|\leqslant c_{5}n\log n\right.\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-\omega(1)}-2n^{-8}.

(7.10)

We will now use the adjusted exponential potential
(\widetilde{\Lambda}_{t_{0}}^{t})_{t\geqslant t_{0}}
(defined in (
7.3
)) to show that
\Lambda
becomes small in at least one round in
[t_{0},t_{1}]
. By
7.1
, it forms a super-martingale, so
\mathbf{E}[\,\widetilde{\Lambda}_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}+1}\mid\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}}\,]\leqslant\widetilde{\Lambda}_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}}=\Lambda^{t_{0}}
. Hence, using Markov’s inequality we get

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left.\widetilde{\Lambda}_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}+1}>\Lambda^{t_{0}}\cdot n^{8}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\max_{i\in[n]}\left|y_{i}^{t_{0}}\right|\leqslant c_{5}n\log n\,\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left.\widetilde{\Lambda}_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}+1}>\Lambda^{t_{0}}\cdot n^{8}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\Lambda^{t_{0}}\leqslant e^{2c_{5}n\log n}\,\right]\leqslant n^{-8}.

(7.11)

Thus, negating and by the definition of
\widetilde{\Lambda}_{t_{0}}^{t}
, we have

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Lambda^{t_{1}+1}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}}\leqslant\Lambda^{t_{0}}\cdot n^{8}\cdot\exp\left(\frac{c_{4}\alpha^{2}}{n}\cdot B_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}-\frac{c_{3}\alpha}{n}\cdot G_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\right)\,\Bigg|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\Lambda^{t_{0}}\leqslant e^{2c_{5}n\log n}\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-8}.

Further, if in addition to the two events
\{\widetilde{\Lambda}_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}+1}\leqslant\Lambda^{t_{0}}\cdot n^{8}\}
and
\{\Lambda^{t_{0}}\leqslant e^{2c_{5}n\log n}\}
, also the event
\{G_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\geqslant\varepsilon^{2}\cdot(t_{1}-t_{0})\}
holds, then

\displaystyle\Lambda^{t_{1}+1}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\Lambda^{t_{0}}\cdot n^{8}\cdot\exp\left(\frac{c_{4}\alpha^{2}}{n}\cdot B_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}-\frac{c_{3}\alpha}{n}\cdot G_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant e^{2c_{5}n\log n}\cdot n^{8}\cdot\exp\left(\frac{c_{4}\alpha^{2}}{n}\cdot(1-\varepsilon^{2})\cdot(t_{1}-t_{0})-\frac{c_{3}\alpha}{n}\cdot\varepsilon^{2}\cdot(t_{1}-t_{0})\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leqslant}}e^{2c_{5}n\log n}\cdot n^{8}\cdot\exp\left(\frac{c_{3}\alpha}{n}\cdot\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\cdot(t_{1}-t_{0})-\frac{c_{3}\alpha}{n}\cdot\varepsilon^{2}\cdot(t_{1}-t_{0})\right)

\displaystyle=e^{2c_{5}n\log n}\cdot n^{8}\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{c_{3}\alpha}{n}\cdot\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\cdot(t_{1}-t_{0})\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{=}}e^{2c_{5}n\log n}\cdot n^{8}\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{c_{3}\alpha}{n}\cdot\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\cdot n^{3}\log^{4}n\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(c)}}{{\leqslant}}1,

using in
(a)
that
\alpha\leqslant\frac{c_{3}\varepsilon^{2}}{2\cdot c_{4}\cdot(1-\varepsilon^{2})}
and
(b)
that
T:=n^{3}\log^{4}n
and in
(c)
that
\alpha,\varepsilon,c_{5}>0
are constants.
Observe that
\{\Lambda^{t_{1}+1}\geqslant n\}
holds deterministically, so we deduce from the above inequality that
\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}}=0
, that is,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\neg\mathcal{E}_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\;\Bigg|\;\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\;\;\;\widetilde{\Lambda}_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}+1}\leqslant\Lambda^{t_{0}}\cdot n^{8},\;\;\;\Lambda^{t_{0}}\leqslant e^{2c_{5}n\log n},\;\;\;G_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\geqslant\varepsilon^{2}\cdot(t_{1}-t_{0})\,\right]\leqslant 1.

Recalling the definition of
\mathcal{E}_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}=\bigcap_{s\in[t_{0},t_{1}]}\{\Lambda^{s}>cn\}
, and taking the union bound over (
7.10
) and (
7.11
) yields

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left.\bigcup_{s\in[t_{0},t_{1}]}\{\Lambda^{s}\leqslant cn\}\;\right|\;\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\max_{i\in[n]}\left|y_{i}^{t_{0}}\right|\leqslant c_{5}n\log n\,\right]\geqslant 1-2n^{-8}-n^{-8}=1-3n^{-8}.

We conclude by combining with (
7.9
),

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcup_{s\in[t_{0},t_{1}]}\{\Lambda^{s}\leqslant cn\}\,\right]\geqslant\left(1-3n^{-8}\right)\cdot\left(1-n^{-12}\right)\geqslant 1-n^{-5}.\qed

7.4
Stabilization Phase of the Process

The next lemma establishes that a small value of the exponential potential function is preserved for some longer time period. We will refer to this property of the exponential potential function of being “trapped” in some region as
stabilization
. More precisely, we prove in the lemma below that if for some round
t_{0}
the exponential potential is not too small, i.e., at most
2cn
, then within the next
\mathcal{O}(n\log n)
rounds, the exponential potential will w.h.p. be smaller than
cn
at least once.

Lemma 7.5

(Stabilization)
.

Consider any
{\hyperlink{p2}{\mathcal{P}_{2}}}\cap{\hyperlink{w1}{\mathcal{W}_{1}}}
-process or
{\hyperlink{p1}{\mathcal{P}_{1}}}\cap{\hyperlink{w2}{\mathcal{W}_{2}}}
-process and the potential
\Lambda:=\Lambda(\alpha)
for
\alpha>0
as defined in (
7.2
). Further, let
\varepsilon>0
be given by (
7.1
),
c:=c(\varepsilon)>1
be as defined in
6.6
, and
c_{s}:=\frac{22}{c_{3}\varepsilon^{2}\alpha}
, for
c_{3}:=c_{3}(\varepsilon)>0
as defined in
6.4
. Then, for any round
t_{0}\geqslant 0
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left.\bigcup_{s\in[t_{0},t_{0}+c_{s}n\log n]}\left\{\Lambda^{s}\leqslant cn\right\}~\right|~\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\Lambda^{t_{0}}\in(cn,2cn]\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-9}.

Proof.

Let
T:=c_{s}n\log n
and
t_{1}:=t_{0}+T
. By
7.3
, we have for
\tau:=\inf\{t\geqslant t_{0}\colon\max_{i\in[n]}|y_{i}^{t}|>\sqrt{T}\cdot\log^{-2}n\}
that

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left\{G_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\geqslant\varepsilon^{2}\cdot(t_{1}-t_{0})\right\}\cup\{\tau\leqslant t_{1}\}\;\Big|\;\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\Upsilon^{t_{0}}\leqslant T\,\right]\geqslant 1-T\cdot e^{-\varepsilon n}-n^{-\omega(1)}\geqslant 1-n^{-12}.

By
6.9
, the event
\big\{\Lambda^{t_{0}}\in(cn,2cn]\big\}
implies that
\big\{\Upsilon^{t_{0}}\leqslant T\big\}
(since
T=\omega(n)
), and hence

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left\{G_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\geqslant\varepsilon^{2}\cdot(t_{1}-t_{0})\right\}\cup\{\tau\leqslant t_{1}\}\;\Big|\;\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\Lambda^{t_{0}}\in(cn,2cn]\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-12}.

(7.12)

By
6.7
with
\kappa:=c_{s}
, as
\sqrt{T}\cdot\log^{-2}n\geqslant\log^{2}n
we have that

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left.\tau>t_{1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\;\Lambda^{t_{0}}\in(cn,2cn]\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left.\max_{t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]}\max_{i\in[n]}\left|y_{i}^{t}\right|\leqslant\log^{2}n\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\;\Lambda^{t_{0}}\in(cn,2cn]\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left.\max_{t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]}\max_{i\in[n]}\left|y_{i}^{t}\right|\leqslant\log^{2}n\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\;\Lambda^{t_{0}}\leqslant n^{2}\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant 1-n^{-12}.

(7.13)

Hence, taking the union bound over (
7.12
) and (
7.13
), we get that

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,G_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\geqslant\varepsilon^{2}\cdot(t_{1}-t_{0})\;\Big|\;\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\Lambda^{t_{0}}\in(cn,2cn]\,\right]\geqslant 1-2n^{-12}.

(7.14)

We will now use the adjusted exponential potential
(\widetilde{\Lambda}_{t_{0}}^{t})_{t\geqslant t_{0}}
(defined in (
7.3
)) to show that
\Lambda
becomes small in at least one round in
[t_{0},t_{1}]
. By
7.1
, it forms a super-martingale, so
\mathbf{E}[\,\widetilde{\Lambda}_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}+1}\mid\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}}\,]\leqslant\widetilde{\Lambda}_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}}=\Lambda^{t_{0}}
. Hence, using Markov’s inequality we get

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left.\widetilde{\Lambda}_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}+1}>\Lambda^{t_{0}}\cdot n^{10}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\Lambda^{t_{0}}\in(cn,2cn]\,\right]\leqslant n^{-10}.

(7.15)

Thus, by the definition of
\widetilde{\Lambda}_{t_{0}}^{t}
, we have

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Lambda^{t_{1}+1}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}}\leqslant\Lambda^{t_{0}}\cdot n^{10}\cdot\exp\left(\frac{c_{4}\alpha^{2}}{n}\cdot B_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}-\frac{c_{3}\alpha}{n}\cdot G_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\right)\,\Bigg|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\Lambda^{t_{0}}\in(cn,2cn]\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-10}.

Further, if in addition to the two events
\big\{\widetilde{\Lambda}_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}+1}\leqslant\Lambda^{t_{0}}\cdot n^{10}\big\}
and
\big\{\Lambda^{t_{0}}\in(cn,2cn]\big\}
, also the event
\{G_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\geqslant\varepsilon^{2}\cdot(t_{1}-t_{0})\}
holds, then

\displaystyle\Lambda^{t_{1}+1}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\Lambda^{t_{0}}\cdot n^{10}\cdot\exp\left(\frac{c_{4}\alpha^{2}}{n}\cdot B_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}-\frac{c_{3}\alpha}{n}\cdot G_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant 2cn^{11}\cdot\exp\left(\frac{c_{4}\alpha^{2}}{n}\cdot(1-\varepsilon^{2})\cdot(t_{1}-t_{0})-\frac{c_{3}\alpha}{n}\cdot\varepsilon^{2}\cdot(t_{1}-t_{0})\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leqslant}}2cn^{11}\cdot\exp\left(\frac{c_{3}\alpha}{n}\cdot\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\cdot(t_{1}-t_{0})-\frac{c_{3}\alpha}{n}\cdot\varepsilon^{2}\cdot(t_{1}-t_{0})\right)

\displaystyle=2cn^{11}\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{c_{3}\alpha}{n}\cdot\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\cdot(t_{1}-t_{0})\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{=}}2cn^{11}\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{c_{3}\alpha}{n}\cdot\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\cdot\frac{22}{c_{3}\varepsilon^{2}\alpha}\cdot n\log n\right)=2c,

using in
(a)
that
\alpha\leqslant\frac{c_{3}\varepsilon^{2}}{2\cdot(1-\varepsilon^{2})\cdot c_{4}}
and
(b)
that
T:=\frac{22}{c_{3}\varepsilon^{2}\alpha}\cdot n\log n
.
Observe that
\{\Lambda^{t_{1}}\geqslant n\}
holds deterministically, so we deduce from the above inequality that
\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}}=0
, that is,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\neg\mathcal{E}_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\;\Bigg|\;\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\;\;\;\widetilde{\Lambda}_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}+1}\leqslant\Lambda^{t_{0}}\cdot n^{10},\;\;\;\Lambda^{t_{0}}\in(cn,2cn],\;\;\;G_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\geqslant\varepsilon^{2}\cdot(t_{1}-t_{0})\,\right]=1.

Recalling the definition of
\mathcal{E}_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}=\bigcap_{s\in[t_{0},t_{1}]}\{\Lambda^{s}\geqslant cn\}
, and taking the union bound over (
7.14
) and (
7.15
) yields

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left.\bigcup_{s\in[t_{0},t_{0}+c_{s}n\log n]}\{\Lambda^{s}\leqslant cn\}\;\right|\;\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\Lambda^{t_{0}}\in(cn,2cn]\,\right]\geqslant 1-2n^{-12}-n^{-10}\geqslant 1-n^{-9},

as claimed.
∎

The next lemma shows that if there is a round with linear (exponential) potential then the gap is at most logarithmic for the next
n^{4}
rounds. This follows by repeatedly applying
7.5
to any contiguous sub-interval of length
\Theta(n\log n)
and showing that it contains a round where
\Lambda
is linear. The result then follows since a linear potential implies a logarithmic gap, and the load of a bin can change by at most
\Theta(\log n)
in
\Theta(n\log n)
rounds.

Lemma 7.6
.

Consider any
{\hyperlink{p2}{\mathcal{P}_{2}}}\cap{\hyperlink{w1}{\mathcal{W}_{1}}}
-process or
{\hyperlink{p1}{\mathcal{P}_{1}}}\cap{\hyperlink{w2}{\mathcal{W}_{2}}}
-process, the potential
\Lambda:=\Lambda(\alpha)
for
\alpha>0
as defined in (
7.2
),
c>0
as defined in
6.6
and
c_{s}>0
as defined in
7.5
. Then, for
\kappa:=\frac{2}{\alpha}+w_{-}\cdot c_{s}
and for any rounds
t_{0}\geqslant 0
and
t_{0}<t_{1}\leqslant t_{0}+n^{4}
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left.\max_{i\in[n]}\lvert y_{i}^{t_{1}}\rvert\leqslant\kappa\cdot\log n\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\Lambda^{t_{0}}\leqslant cn\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-5}.

Proof.

We start by defining the event

\mathcal{M}_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}=\left\{\text{for all }t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]\text{ there exists }s\in[t,t+c_{s}n\log n]\text{ such that }\Lambda^{s}\leqslant cn\right\},

that is, if
\mathcal{M}_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}
holds then we have
\Lambda^{s}<cn
at least once every
c_{s}n\log n
rounds.

Assume now that
\mathcal{M}_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}
holds. Choosing
t=t_{1}
, there exists
s\in[t_{1},t_{1}+c_{s}n\log n]
such that
\Lambda^{s}\leqslant cn
, which in turn implies by definition of
\Lambda
that
\max_{i\in[n]}|y_{i}^{s}|\leqslant\frac{1}{\alpha}\cdot\log(cn)<\frac{2}{\alpha}\cdot\log n
. Clearly, any
y_{i}^{t}
can decrease by at most
w_{-}/n
in each round, and from this it follows that if
\mathcal{M}_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}
holds, then

\max_{i\in[n]}y_{i}^{t_{1}}\leqslant\max_{i\in[n]}|y_{i}^{s}|+w_{-}\cdot c_{s}\log n\leqslant\kappa\cdot\log n,

for
\kappa:=\frac{2}{\alpha}+w_{-}\cdot c_{s}
.

We now turn to lower bounding the minimum load. If
t_{1}\geqslant t_{0}+c_{s}n\log n
and
\mathcal{M}_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}
holds, then choosing
t=t_{1}-c_{s}n\log n
, there exists
s\in[t_{1}-c_{s}n\log n,t_{1}]
such that
\Lambda^{s}\leqslant cn
. (In case
t_{1}-c_{s}n\log n<t_{0}
, then we arrive at the same conclusion by taking
s=t_{0}
and using the condition
\Lambda^{t_{0}}\leqslant cn
,). This in turn implies

\max_{i\in[n]}|y_{i}^{s}|<\frac{2}{\alpha}\cdot\log n.

Hence

\min_{i\in[n]}y_{i}^{t_{1}}\geqslant\min_{i\in[n]}y_{i}^{s}-w_{-}\cdot c_{s}\log n\geqslant-\max_{i\in[n]}|y_{i}^{s}|-w_{-}\cdot c_{s}\log n\geqslant-\kappa\cdot\log n.

Hence,
\mathcal{M}_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}
(conditioned on
\Lambda^{t_{0}}\leqslant cn
) implies that
\max_{i\in[n]}\lvert y_{i}^{t_{1}}\rvert\leqslant\kappa\cdot\log n
. It remains to bound
\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\neg\mathcal{M}_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\mid\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\Lambda^{t_{0}}\leqslant cn\,\right]
.

Note that since we start with
\Lambda^{t_{0}}\leqslant cn
, if for some round
j\geqslant t_{0}
,
\Lambda^{j}>2cn
, there must exist some
s\in[t_{0},j)
, such that
\Lambda^{s}\in(cn,2cn]
,
since for every
t\geqslant 0
it holds
\Lambda^{t+1}\leqslant\Lambda^{t}\cdot e^{\alpha w_{-}}\leqslant 2\Lambda^{t}
and
\alpha\leqslant 1/(2w_{-})
. Let
t_{0}<\tau_{1}<\tau_{2}<\cdots
and
t_{0}=:s_{0}<s_{1}<\cdots
be two interlaced sequences defined recursively for
i\geqslant 1
by

\tau_{i}:=\inf\{\tau>s_{i-1}:\Lambda^{\tau}\in(cn,2cn]\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad s_{i}:=\inf\{s>\tau_{i}:\Lambda^{s}\leqslant cn\}.

Thus we have

t_{0}=s_{0}<\tau_{1}<s_{1}<\tau_{2}<s_{2}<\cdots,

and since
\tau_{i}>\tau_{i-1}
we have
\tau_{t_{1}-t_{0}}\geqslant t_{1}-t_{0}
. Observe that if the event
\cap_{i=1}^{t_{1}-t_{0}}\{s_{i}-\tau_{i}\leqslant c_{s}n\log n\}
holds, then also
\mathcal{M}_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}
holds.

Recall that by
7.5
we have for any
i=1,2,\ldots,t_{1}-t_{0}
and any
\tau=t_{0}+1,\ldots,t_{1}

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left.\bigcup_{t\in[\tau_{i},\tau_{i}+c_{s}n\log n]}\left\{\Lambda^{t}\leqslant cn\right\}~\right|~\mathfrak{F}^{\tau},\;\Lambda^{\tau}\in(cn,2cn],\tau_{i}=\tau\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-9},

and by negating and the definition of
s_{i}
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,s_{i}-\tau_{i}>c_{s}n\log n\,\Big|\,\mathfrak{F}^{\tau},\Lambda^{\tau}\in(cn,2cn],\tau_{i}=\tau\,\right]\leqslant n^{-9}.

Since the above bound holds for any
i
and
\mathfrak{F}^{\tau}
, with
\tau_{i}=\tau
, it follows by the union bound over all
i=1,2,\ldots,t_{1}-t_{0}
, as
t_{1}-t_{0}\leqslant n^{4}
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left.\neg\mathcal{M}_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\Lambda^{t_{0}}\leqslant cn\,\right]\leqslant(t_{1}-t_{0})\cdot n^{-9}\leqslant n^{-5}.\qed

7.5
Completing the Proof of Theorem
3.1

We are now ready to combine the recovery and stabilization phases to complete the proof the main theorem.

Theorem
3.1

(Restated, page
3.1
)
.

Consider any
{\hyperlink{p2}{\mathcal{P}_{2}}}\cap{\hyperlink{w1}{\mathcal{W}_{1}}}
-process or
{\hyperlink{p1}{\mathcal{P}_{1}}}\cap{\hyperlink{w2}{\mathcal{W}_{2}}}
-process. Then, there exists a constant
\kappa>0
such that for any round
m\geqslant 0
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\max_{i\in[n]}\left|x_{i}^{m}-\frac{W^{m}}{n}\right|\leqslant\kappa\log n\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-4};

so in particular,
\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Gap(m)\leqslant\kappa\log n\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-4}
.

Proof.

Consider an arbitrary round
m\geqslant 0
and the potential
\Lambda:=\Lambda(\alpha)
with smoothing parameter
\alpha>0
as defined in (
7.2
) and let
c\geqslant 1
be as defined in
6.6
. If
m<n^{3}\log^{4}n
, then the claim follows by
7.6
with
t_{0}:=0
and
t_{1}:=m
, since
\Lambda^{t_{0}}=n\leqslant cn
.

Otherwise, let
t_{0}:=m-n^{3}\log^{4}n
. Firstly, by
7.4
, we get

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcup_{s\in[t_{0},m]}\left\{\Lambda^{s}\leqslant cn\right\}\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-5}.

Hence for the stopping time
\sigma:=\inf\{s\geqslant t_{0}\colon\Lambda^{s}\leqslant cn\}
we have
\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\sigma\leqslant m\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-5}
.

Secondly, by
7.6
, there exists a constant
\kappa:=\kappa(\alpha)
such that for any round
s\in[t_{0},m]

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left.\max_{i\in[n]}\left|y_{i}^{m}\right|\leqslant\kappa\cdot\log n~\right|~\mathfrak{F}^{s},\Lambda^{s}\leqslant cn\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-5}.

Combining the two inequalities from above,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\max_{i\in[n]}\left|y_{i}^{m}\right|\leqslant\kappa\log n\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\sum_{s=t_{0}}^{m}\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left.\max_{i\in[n]}\left|y_{i}^{m}\right|\leqslant\kappa\log n~\right|~\sigma=s\,\right]\cdot\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\sigma=s\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\sum_{s=t_{0}}^{m}\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left.\max_{i\in[n]}\left|y_{i}^{m}\right|\leqslant\kappa\log n~\right|~\mathfrak{F}^{s},\Lambda^{s}\leqslant cn\,\right]\cdot\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\sigma=s\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\left(1-n^{-5}\right)\cdot\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\sigma\leqslant m\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\left(1-n^{-5}\right)\cdot\left(1-n^{-5}\right)\geqslant 1-n^{-4}.\qed

8
Lower Bounds

In this section, we shall prove lower bounds for
Mean-Biased
processes satisfying condition
\mathcal{P}_{3}
. We now recall the definition of condition
\mathcal{P}_{3}
:

•

Condition
\mathcal{P}_{3}
: for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists a
k_{5}:=k_{5}(\varepsilon)\in(0,1]
such that for each round
t\geqslant 0
with
\delta^{t}\in[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]
, the probability allocation vector
p^{t}
satisfies,

\min_{i\in[n]}p_{i}^{t}\geqslant\frac{k_{5}}{n}.

This condition is satisfied for many natural processes, including
Twinning
,
Mean-Thinning
, and the
(1+\beta)
-process with constant
\beta\in(0,1)
. See Table
1.2
for a concise overview of old and new lower bounds.
One interesting aspect is that our lower bound for
Mean-Biased
processes makes use of the quantile stabilization result from the previous section (
7.3
).

Essentially this condition
\mathcal{P}_{3}
implies that in any round
t
where the mean quantile
\delta^{t}
is in
[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]
, there is at least an
\Omega(1/n)
-probability of allocating to each bin.

We shall now observe that

•

The
Mean-Thinning
process has
p_{i}^{t}\geqslant\frac{\delta^{t}}{n}
, satisfying
\mathcal{P}_{3}
with
k_{5}:=\varepsilon
.

•

The
Twinning
process has
p_{i}^{t}=\frac{1}{n}
, satisfying
\mathcal{P}_{3}
with
k_{5}:=1
.

•

The
(1+\beta)
-process has
p_{i}^{t}=(1-\beta)\cdot\frac{1}{n}+\beta\cdot\frac{2i-1}{n^{2}}>\frac{1-\beta}{n}
, satisfying
\mathcal{P}_{3}
with
k_{5}:=1-\beta
.

Recall that
G_{0}^{m}:=G_{0}^{m}(\varepsilon)
is the number of rounds
t\in[0,m]
with
\delta^{t}\in[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]
. The next lemma shows that w.h.p. a constant fraction of the first
m=\operatorname{poly}(n)
rounds are good.

Lemma 8.1
.

Consider any
{\hyperlink{p2}{\mathcal{P}_{2}}}\cap{\hyperlink{w1}{\mathcal{W}_{1}}}
-process or
{\hyperlink{p1}{\mathcal{P}_{1}}}\cap{\hyperlink{w2}{\mathcal{W}_{2}}}
-process. Let
\varepsilon\in\big(0,\frac{1}{2}\big)
be given by (
7.1
), and
m\in[c_{s}n\log n,n^{3}\log^{4}n]
for
c_{s}:=c_{s}(\varepsilon)>0
as defined in
7.5
. Then,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,G_{0}^{m}(\varepsilon)>\varepsilon^{2}\cdot m\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-1/2}.

Proof.

The proof is analogous to the first steps in the proof of the stabilization lemma (
7.5
).
At round
0
, we have that

\Lambda^{0}=n,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Upsilon^{0}=0.

Let
t_{0}:=0
,
t_{1}:=m
,
T:=m
, and
\tau:=\inf\{t\geqslant 0\colon\max_{i\in[n]}|y_{i}^{t}|>\sqrt{T}\cdot\log^{-2}n\}
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left\{G_{0}^{m}>\varepsilon^{2}\cdot m\right\}\cup\{\tau\leqslant m\}\,\right]\geqslant 1-T\cdot e^{-\varepsilon n}-n^{-\omega(1)}\geqslant 1-n^{-12},

(8.1)

by
7.3
. By
3.1
and the union bound we have

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\tau>m\,\right]\geqslant\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\max_{t\in[0,m]}\max_{i\in[n]}\left|y_{i}^{t}\right|\leqslant\log^{2}n\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-4}\cdot m\geqslant 1-n^{-4/5}.

(8.2)

Hence, taking the union bound over (
8.1
) and (
8.2
), we get that

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,G_{0}^{m}>\varepsilon^{2}\cdot m\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-12}-n^{-4/5}\geqslant 1-n^{-1/2}.\qed

We will use the following lower bound on the
One-Choice
process (see also
[
42
, Section 4]
).

Lemma 8.2

(
[
33
, Lemma A.2]
)
.

Consider the
One-Choice
process for
m=\lambda n\log n
balls where
\lambda\geqslant 1/\log n
.
Then,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\max_{i\in[n]}x_{i}^{m}\geqslant\left(\lambda+\frac{\sqrt{\lambda}}{10}\right)\cdot\log n\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-2}.

We are now ready to restate and prove the main result of this section.

Theorem
3.2

(Restated, page
3.2
)
.

Consider any
{\hyperlink{p2}{\mathcal{P}_{2}}}\cap{\hyperlink{w1}{\mathcal{W}_{1}}}
-process or
{\hyperlink{p1}{\mathcal{P}_{1}}}\cap{\hyperlink{w2}{\mathcal{W}_{2}}}
-process, which also satisfies condition
\mathcal{P}_{3}
. Then there exists a constant
\kappa>0
such that

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Gap(\kappa n\log n)\geqslant\kappa\log n\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-1/2}.

Proof.

We start by defining the constant
k_{5}:=k_{5}(\varepsilon)\in(0,1]
for
\varepsilon\in\big(0,\frac{1}{2}\big)
given by (
7.1
). Further, set
\kappa:=\frac{k_{5}\varepsilon^{2}}{800w_{-}^{2}}>0
, and let
m:=\kappa n\log n
. Define the event
\mathcal{E}_{1}:=\{|\{t\in[0,m]:\delta^{t}\in[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]\}|\geqslant\varepsilon^{2}m\}
and note that
\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\neg\mathcal{E}_{1}\,\right]<n^{-2/3}
by
8.1
.

We will now define a coupling between the allocations of the process and a
One-Choice
process for each round
t\in[0,m]
:

If
\delta^{t}\in[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]
: Sample an independent Bernoulli random variable
X^{t}\sim\mathsf{Ber}(k_{5})
.

•

If
X^{t}=1
, then we allocate the ball to a bin sampled uniformly at random.

•

Otherwise, allocate to the
i
-th heaviest bin with probability
\displaystyle{\frac{p_{i}^{t}-\frac{k_{5}}{n}}{1-k_{5}}}
.

If
\delta^{t}\notin[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]
: allocate to bin
i\in[n]
with probability
p_{i}^{t}
.

Observe that this has the correct marginal distribution, since for any step
t
with
\delta^{t}\in[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]
,

k_{4}\cdot\frac{1}{n}+(1-k_{4})\cdot\frac{p_{i}^{t}-\frac{k_{4}}{n}}{1-k_{4}}=p_{i}^{t}.

Let
(Y^{i})_{i\geqslant 0}
, where
Y^{i}\sim\mathsf{Ber}(k_{5})
, be a sequence of i.i.d. Bernoulli random variables used in the coupling. That is, we pre-sample
(Y^{i})_{i\geqslant 0}
and then at round
t
of the coupling if we have
\delta^{t}\in[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]
for the
i
-th time then we take
X^{t}=Y^{i}
. Define the event
\mathcal{E}_{2}:=\{\sum_{i=1}^{\varepsilon^{2}m}Y^{i}>k_{5}\varepsilon^{2}m/2\}
. Then conditional on
\mathcal{E}_{1}\cap\mathcal{E}_{2}
, at least
(k_{5}\varepsilon^{2}\kappa/2)\cdot n\log n
balls are allocated according to the
One-Choice
process during the
m
steps of the coupling. By a Chernoff bound,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\neg\mathcal{E}_{2}\,\right]=\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathsf{Bin}(\varepsilon^{2}m,k_{5})\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\cdot k_{5}\varepsilon^{2}m\,\right]\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{k_{5}\varepsilon^{2}m}{8}\right)<n^{-2}.

We then sample a
One-Choice
process, with load vector denoted by
\widetilde{x}^{t}
, on
h:=\lambda n\log n
rounds where
\lambda:=\frac{k_{5}\varepsilon^{2}\kappa}{2}
, independently from all other random bits used in the coupling. Define the event
\mathcal{E}_{3}=\{\max_{i\in[n]}\widetilde{x}_{i}^{h}\geqslant\left(c+\frac{\sqrt{c}}{10}\right)\cdot\log n\}
and observe that
\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\neg\mathcal{E}_{3}\,\right]<n^{-2}
by
8.2
.

Now, as
\kappa:=k_{5}\varepsilon^{2}/(800w_{-}^{2})
, and each ball has weight in
[1,w_{-}]
, conditional on
\mathcal{E}_{1}\cap\mathcal{E}_{2}\cap\mathcal{E}_{3}
,

\frac{\Gap(m)}{\log n}\geqslant\frac{k_{5}\varepsilon^{2}\kappa}{2}+\frac{1}{10}\sqrt{\frac{k_{5}\varepsilon^{2}\kappa}{2}}-w_{-}\kappa\geqslant\frac{1}{10}\sqrt{\frac{k_{5}^{2}\varepsilon^{4}}{1600w_{-}^{2}}}-w_{-}\kappa=w_{-}\kappa\geqslant\kappa.

The result then follows since
\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\neg\mathcal{E}_{1}\cup\neg\mathcal{E}_{2}\cup\neg\mathcal{E}_{3}\,\right]\leqslant n^{-2/3}+n^{-2}+n^{-2}<n^{-1/2}
.∎

Hence, we can deduce from the lemma above by recalling that
Mean-Thinning
,
Twinning
and the
(1+\beta)
-process all satisfy
\mathcal{P}_{3}
and the conditions
\mathcal{P}_{1}
and
\mathcal{W}_{2}
, or,
\mathcal{W}_{1}
and
\mathcal{P}_{2}
:

Corollary 8.3
.

Consider any of the
Mean-Thinning
,
Twinning
or the
(1+\beta)
-process with any constant
\beta\in(0,1)
. Then, there exists a constant
\kappa>0
(different for each process) such that

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Gap(\kappa n\log n)\geqslant\kappa\log n\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-1}.

Finally, we prove a tight lower bound for any
\textsc{Relative}\text{-}\textsc{Threshold}(f(n))
process with
f(n)\geqslant\log n
.

Lemma
3.13

(Restated, page
3.13
)
.

Consider any
\textsc{Relative}\text{-}\textsc{Threshold}(f(n))
process with
f(n)\geqslant\log n
. Then, for
m:=\frac{1}{24}\cdot\frac{n\cdot(f(n))^{2}}{\log n}
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Gap(m)\geqslant\frac{f(n)}{50}\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-1/4}.

Proof.

Note that in any round
t\geqslant 0
where
\max_{i\in[n]}y_{i}^{t}<f(n)
, the
\textsc{Relative}\text{-}\textsc{Threshold}(f(n))
process will always accept the first sample, and hence the allocation of this process can be coupled with the allocation of
One-Choice
.

In the following, fix an arbitrary bin
i\in[n]
. Let
(Y^{t})_{1\leqslant t\leqslant m}
be independent
\mathsf{Ber}(1/n)
random variables, and for any
1\leqslant t\leqslant m
, define
Z^{t}:=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\left(Y^{s}-\frac{1}{n}\right)
, Then
\mathbf{E}\left[\,Z^{t}\,\right]=0
, and

\mathbf{Var}\left[\,Z^{t+1}\,\mid\,Z^{t}\,\right]=\frac{1}{n}\cdot\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)\leqslant\frac{1}{n}=:\sigma^{2}.

Also
|Z^{t+1}-Z^{t}|\leqslant 1=:M
holds deterministically. Further, define
\tau:=\min\{t\in\mathbb{N}\colon y_{i}^{t}\geqslant f(n)\}
. Further, since
Z^{t\wedge\tau}
is a martingale, it follows by
A.5
with
\lambda:=f(n)
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,|Z^{m\wedge\tau}|\geqslant f(n)\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\exp\left(-\frac{f(n)^{2}}{2(m\cdot\frac{1}{n}+f(n)/3)}\right)

\displaystyle=2\exp\left(-\frac{f(n)}{2\cdot(f(n)/(24\log(n))+1/3)}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant 2n^{-4/3},

using that
m:=\frac{1}{24}\cdot\frac{n\cdot(f(n))^{2}}{\log n}
and
f(n)\geqslant\log n
. If the event
|Z^{m\wedge\tau}|<f(n)
holds, then this implies that
\max_{1\leqslant t\leqslant m}Z^{t}<f(n)
, and thanks to the coupling,
\max_{1\leqslant t\leqslant m}y_{i}^{t}<f(n)
. Taking the union bound over all
n
bins
i\in[n]
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\max_{i\in[n]}\max_{1\leqslant t\leqslant m}y_{i}^{t}<f(n)\,\right]\geqslant 1-2n^{-1/3}.

Conditional on this, the allocations of
\textsc{Relative}\text{-}\textsc{Threshold}(f(n))
and
One-Choice
can be coupled until round
m
. Now, for
One-Choice
,
8.2
with
\lambda:=(1/24)\cdot f(n)^{2}/(\log n)^{2}
implies,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\max_{i\in[n]}y_{i}^{m}\geqslant\frac{\sqrt{\lambda}}{10}\cdot\log n\,\right]\geqslant\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\max_{i\in[n]}y_{i}^{m}\geqslant\frac{f(n)}{50}\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-2}.

Hence by the union bound, with probability at least
1-2n^{-1/3}-n^{-2}\geqslant 1-n^{-1/4}
the gap of
\textsc{Relative}\text{-}\textsc{Threshold}(f(n))
at round
m
is at least
f(n)/50
.
∎

9
Sample Efficiency

Recall that the
sample efficiency

\eta^{m}=W^{m}/S^{m}
of a process, as the ratio of the weight
W^{m}
of the balls allocated to the number of bin samples
S^{m}
taken in the first
m
rounds. The
One-Choice
process has
\eta^{m}=1
and the
Two-Choice
process has
\eta^{m}=\frac{1}{2}
.

We begin by showing that
Mean-Thinning
is more sample-efficient than
Two-Choice
.

Lemma 9.1

(Restated, page
3.7
)
.

There exists a constant
\rho>0
, such that for any
m\geqslant\rho^{-1}\cdot n\log n
, the
Mean-Thinning
process satisfies

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\eta^{m}\,\right]\geqslant\frac{1}{2}+\rho\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\eta^{m}\geqslant\frac{1}{2}+\rho\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-1/4}.

Proof.

Consider any
m\geqslant c_{s}n\log n
and any interval
[t_{0},t_{1}]
with
t_{1}:=t_{0}+n^{3}\log^{4}n
. By (
7.10
) in the recovery lemma (
7.4
), there exists a constant
\varepsilon\in\big(0,\frac{1}{2}\big)
such that,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,G_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}>\varepsilon^{2}\cdot(t_{1}-t_{0})\;\left|\;\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\max_{i\in[n]}\left|y_{i}^{t_{0}}\right|\leqslant c_{5}n\log n\right.\,\right]\geqslant 1-2n^{-12}.

Hence, using
6.8
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,G_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}>\varepsilon^{2}\cdot(t_{1}-t_{0})\,\right]\geqslant\left(1-2n^{-12}\right)\cdot\left(1-n^{-12}\right)\geqslant 1-3n^{-12}.

Let
S_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}
be the number of samples in the interval
(t_{0},t_{1}]
. Note that
Mean-Thinning
takes only one sample in round
t
if the first sample is an underloaded bin, i.e., with probability
1-\delta^{t}
, and when
\delta^{t}\in[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]
, it follows that this probability is at least
\geqslant\varepsilon
.

Consider the following coupling between the
Mean-Thinning
process and the random variables
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{N}\sim\mathsf{Ber}(\varepsilon)
for
N:=\varepsilon^{2}\cdot(t_{1}-t_{0})
. For any
t\in[t_{0},t_{1})
, having initially
j^{t_{0}}=1
:

If
\delta^{t}\in[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]
and
j^{t}\leqslant N
:

•

If
Z^{j^{t}}=1
, then allocate to an underloaded bin
i\in B_{-}^{t}
with probability
\frac{1}{n\cdot(1-\delta^{t})}
, and set
j^{t+1}:=j^{t}+1
.

•

Otherwise: Allocate to bin
i\in[n]
with probability

\begin{cases}\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}\cdot\frac{\delta^{t}}{n}&\text{if }i\in B_{+}^{t},\\
\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}\cdot\left(\frac{1+\delta^{t}}{n}-\frac{\varepsilon}{n\cdot(1-\delta^{t})}\right)&\text{if }i\in B_{-}^{t},\end{cases}

and set
j^{t+1}:=j^{t}
.

Otherwise: Allocate to bin
i\in[n]
with probability
p_{i}^{t}
.

The marginal distribution of this coupling is correct, since any bin
i\in B_{+}^{t}
is allocated to with probability

(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}\cdot\frac{\delta^{t}}{n}=\frac{\delta^{t}}{n},

whereas any bin
i\in B_{-}^{t}
is allocated to with probability

\varepsilon\cdot\frac{1}{n\cdot(1-\delta^{t})}+(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}\cdot\left(\frac{1+\delta^{t}}{n}-\frac{\varepsilon}{n\cdot(1-\delta^{t})}\right)=\frac{1+\delta^{t}}{n}.

Next observe that, under the above coupling,
Z^{t}=1

implies that the first sample of
Mean-Thinning
is an underloaded bin, and thus only one sample is used. This implies,

S_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\leqslant\sum_{t=1}^{j^{t_{1}}-1}Z^{t}+2\cdot\left(t_{1}-t_{0}-\sum_{t=1}^{j^{t_{1}}-1}Z^{t}\right).

By a Chernoff bound, and since
N=\Omega(n\log n)
, we have that

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\sum_{t=1}^{N}Z^{t}\geqslant\frac{1}{2}N\cdot\varepsilon\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-12}.

By taking the union bound we have that,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left\{G_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}>\varepsilon^{2}\cdot(t_{1}-t_{0})\right\}\cap\left\{\sum_{t=1}^{N}Z^{t}\geqslant\frac{1}{2}N\cdot\varepsilon\right\}\,\right]\geqslant 1-4n^{-12}.

Note that
\left\{G_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}>\varepsilon^{2}\cdot(t_{1}-t_{0})\right\}
implies that
\{j^{t_{1}}=N+1\}
. Therefore, with probability at least
1-4n^{-12}
we have that

\displaystyle S_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\leqslant 1\cdot\left(\sum_{t=1}^{N}Z^{t}\right)+2\cdot\left(t_{1}-t_{0}-\sum_{t=1}^{N}Z^{t}\right)\leqslant(t_{1}-t_{0})\cdot\left(2-\frac{\varepsilon^{3}}{2}\right).

For the first
t_{0}:=\mathrm{rem}(m,n^{3}\log^{4}n)
rounds, we consider two cases. If
t_{0}\leqslant c_{s}n\log n
, then set
\xi:=1
and we shall use the trivial estimate
S_{0}^{t_{0}}\leqslant 2t_{0}
. Otherwise, for
t_{0}\in[c_{s}n\log n,n^{3}\log^{4}n]
, set
\xi:=0
and by
8.1
, we obtain,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,G_{0}^{t_{0}}>\varepsilon^{2}\cdot t_{0}\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-1/2},

and so using the coupling above

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,S_{0}^{t_{0}}\leqslant t_{0}\cdot\left(2-\frac{\varepsilon^{3}}{2}\right)\,\right]\geqslant 1-2n^{-1/2}.

Let
k:=\lfloor\frac{m}{n^{3}\log^{4}n}\rfloor
and define

L^{0}:=\mathbf{1}_{S_{0}^{t_{0}}>t_{0}\cdot\left(2-\frac{\varepsilon^{3}}{2}\right)},\quad\text{ and }\quad L^{j}:=\mathbf{1}_{S_{t_{0}+j\cdot n^{3}\log^{4}n}^{t_{0}+(j+1)\cdot n^{3}\log^{4}n}>n^{3}\log^{4}n\cdot\left(2-\frac{\varepsilon^{3}}{2}\right)},

where
0<j<k
. Then for
L:=\sum_{j=\xi}^{k-1}L^{j}
, we have that
\mathbf{E}\left[\,L\,\right]\leqslant 2n^{-1/2}\cdot k
, and hence by Markov’s inequality,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,L\leqslant 2n^{-1/4}\cdot k\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-1/4}.

When
\{L\leqslant 2n^{-1/4}\cdot k\}
holds, we have that

S^{m}=S_{0}^{m}\leqslant 2\cdot(2n^{-1/4}+\xi\cdot n^{-2})\cdot m+\left(2-\frac{\varepsilon^{3}}{2}\right)\cdot(1-2n^{-1/4}-\xi\cdot n^{-2})\cdot m\leqslant\left(2-\frac{\varepsilon^{3}}{4}\right)\cdot m,

using that
t_{0}/m\leqslant n^{-2}
when
\xi:=1
. Further, we obtain

\eta^{m}=\frac{W^{m}}{S^{m}}\geqslant\frac{m}{\left(2-\frac{\varepsilon^{3}}{4}\right)\cdot m}\geqslant\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon^{3}}{8},

using that
\frac{1}{1-z}\geqslant 1+z
for any
z\in(0,1)
. Finally,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\eta^{m}\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathbf{E}\left[\,\eta^{m}\,\left|\,S_{0}^{m}\leqslant\left(2-\frac{\varepsilon^{3}}{4}\right)\cdot m\right.\,\right]\cdot\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,S_{0}^{m}\leqslant\left(2-\frac{\varepsilon^{3}}{4}\right)\cdot m\,\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\cdot\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,S_{0}^{m}>\left(2-\frac{\varepsilon^{3}}{4}\right)\cdot m\,\right]\geqslant\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon^{3}}{16}.

The statement follows by choosing
\rho:=\min\big\{\frac{\varepsilon^{3}}{16},\frac{1}{c_{s}}\big\}
.
∎

Next, we show that
Twinning
is more sample-efficient than
One-Choice
.

Lemma 9.2

(Restated, page
3.4
)
.

There exists a constant
\rho>0
, such that for any
m\geqslant\rho^{-1}\cdot n\log n
, the
Twinning
process satisfies

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\eta^{m}\,\right]\geqslant 1+\rho\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\eta^{m}\geqslant 1+\rho\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-1/4}.

Proof.

The proof proceeds similarly to the proof of
3.7
, but here we lower bound
W^{m}
instead of upper bounding
S^{m}
. In round
t
,
Twinning
allocates two balls with probability
1-\delta^{t}
, which is
\geqslant\varepsilon
when
\delta^{t}\in[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]
.

Similarly to
3.7
, we define the following coupling between
Twinning
and random variables
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{N}\sim\mathsf{Ber}(\varepsilon)
for
N:=\varepsilon^{2}\cdot(t_{1}-t_{0})
. For any
t\in[t_{0},t_{1})
, having initially
j^{t_{0}}=1
:

If
\delta^{t}\in[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]
and
j^{t}\leqslant N
:

•

If
Z^{j^{t}}=1
, then allocate to an underloaded bin
i\in B_{-}^{t}
with probability
\frac{1}{n\cdot(1-\delta^{t})}
, and set
j^{t+1}:=j^{t}+1
.

•

Otherwise: Allocate to bin
i\in[n]
with probability

\begin{cases}\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}\cdot\frac{1}{n}&\text{if }i\in B_{+}^{t},\\
\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{n}-\frac{\varepsilon}{n\cdot(1-\delta^{t})}\right)&\text{if }i\in B_{-}^{t},\end{cases}

and set
j^{t+1}:=j^{t}
.

Otherwise: Allocate to bin
i\in[n]
with probability
\frac{1}{n}
.

The marginal distribution of this coupling is correct, since any bin
i\in B_{+}^{t}
is allocated to with probability

(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}\cdot\frac{1}{n}=\frac{1}{n},

whereas any bin
i\in B_{-}^{t}
is allocated to with probability

\varepsilon\cdot\frac{1}{n}+(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{n}-\frac{\varepsilon}{n}\right)=\frac{1}{n}.

Next observe that, under the above coupling,
Z^{t}=1
implies that the first sample of
Mean-Thinning
is an underloaded bin, and thus only one sample is used. This implies,

W_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\geqslant 2\cdot\sum_{t=1}^{j^{t_{1}}-1}Z^{t}+1\cdot\left(t_{1}-t_{0}-\sum_{t=1}^{j^{t_{1}}-1}Z^{t}\right).

Proceeding as in
3.7
(with the main difference being that we do not need to apply the inequality
\frac{1}{1-z}\geqslant 1+z
), we obtain

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\eta^{m}\geqslant 1+\frac{\varepsilon^{3}}{4}\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-1/4},

and thus also
\mathbf{E}\left[\,\eta^{m}\,\right]\geqslant 1+\varepsilon^{3}/8.

∎

11
Conclusions

In this work we introduced a framework for analyzing
Mean-Biased
processes in the heavily loaded case. These include processes that bias allocations (of single balls) towards underloaded bins as opposed to overloaded bins, the prototypical process being
Mean-Thinning
. These also include processes that weight-bias allocations to underloaded bins as opposed to overloaded, the prototypical process being
Twinning
. Our analysis which uses a complex interplay between three different types of potential functions implies an
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
upper bound on the gap for any process in the framework. For
Mean-Thinning
and
Twinning
we proved a matching lower bound on the gap and show that the processes are more sample-efficient than
Two-Choice
and
One-Choice
respectively. By a coupling argument, we also obtain tight bounds for
\textsc{Relative}\text{-}\textsc{Threshold}(f(n))
processes with any
f(n)\geqslant\log n
.

There are several directions for future work. One avenue is to derive sub-logarithmic gap bounds.
More specifically, one could investigate
k
-Relative
-
Threshold
processes either with
k\geqslant 2
, or with
k=1
and
f(n)\in(0,\log n)
; we conjecture that for a suitable choice of
f(n)
(being poly-logarithmic in
n
), a gap bound of
\mathcal{O}(\frac{\log n}{\log\log n})
holds. A second direction is to extend the
Mean-Biased
framework to noisy settings, where load information is outdated
[
30
]
, or dynamic settings, where in addition balls could also be removed
[
16
,
10
]
.

Auxiliary Inequalities

In this section we collect some useful inequalities used throughout the paper.

Deterministic Inequalities

The following lemma is similar to
[
24
, Lemma A.1]
.

Lemma A.1
.

Let the sequences
(a_{k})_{k=1}^{n},(b_{k})_{k=1}^{n}
be non-negative and the sequence
(c_{k})_{k=1}^{n}
be non-negative and non-increasing. If
\sum_{k=1}^{i}a_{k}\leqslant\sum_{k=1}^{i}b_{k}
holds for all
i\in[n]
then,

\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}\cdot c_{k}\leqslant\sum_{k=1}^{n}b_{k}\cdot c_{k}.

(A.1)

Proof.

We shall prove (
A.1
) holds by induction on
n\geqslant 1
. The base case
n=1
follows immediately from the fact that
a_{1}\leqslant b_{1}
and
c_{1}\geqslant 0
. Thus we assume
\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}a_{k}\cdot c_{k}\leqslant\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}b_{k}\cdot c_{k}
holds for all sequences
(a_{k})_{k=1}^{n-1},(b_{k})_{k=1}^{n-1}
and
(c_{k})_{k=1}^{n-1}
satisfying the conditions of the lemma.

For the inductive step, suppose we are given sequences
(a_{k})_{k=1}^{n}
,
(b_{k})_{k=1}^{n}
and
(c_{k})_{k=1}^{n}
satisfying the conditions of the lemma. If
c_{2}=0
then, since
(c_{k})_{k=1}^{n}
is non-negative and non-increasing,
c_{k}=0
for all
k\geqslant 2
. Thus as
a_{1}\leqslant b_{1}
and
c_{1}\geqslant 0
by the precondition of the lemma, we conclude

\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}\cdot c_{k}=a_{1}\cdot c_{1}\leqslant b_{1}\cdot c_{1}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}b_{k}\cdot c_{k}.

We now treat the case
c_{2}>0
. Define the non-negative sequences
(a_{k}^{\prime})_{k=1}^{n-1}
and
(b_{k}^{\prime})_{k=1}^{n-1}

as follows:

•

a^{\prime}_{1}=\frac{c_{1}}{c_{2}}\cdot a_{1}+a_{2}
and
a^{\prime}_{k}=a_{k+1}
for
2\leqslant k\leqslant n-1
,

•

b^{\prime}_{1}=\frac{c_{1}}{c_{2}}\cdot b_{1}+b_{2}
and
b^{\prime}_{k}=b_{k+1}
for
2\leqslant k\leqslant n-1
,

Then as the inequalities
c_{1}\geqslant c_{2}
,
a_{1}\leqslant b_{1}
and
\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{k}\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{k}
hold by assumption, we have

\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}a_{k}^{\prime}=\left(\frac{c_{1}}{c_{2}}-1\right)a_{1}+\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}\leqslant\left(\frac{c_{1}}{c_{2}}-1\right)b_{1}+\sum_{k=1}^{n}b_{k}=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}b_{k}^{\prime}.

Thus if we also let
(c_{k}^{\prime})_{k=1}^{n-1}=(c_{k+1})_{k=1}^{n-1}
, which is positive and non-increasing, then

\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}a_{k}^{\prime}\cdot c_{k}^{\prime}\leqslant\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}b_{k}^{\prime}\cdot c_{k}^{\prime},

by the inductive hypothesis. However

\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}a_{k}^{\prime}\cdot c_{k}^{\prime}=\left(\frac{c_{1}}{c_{2}}\cdot a_{1}+a_{2}\right)c_{2}+\sum_{k=2}^{n-1}a_{k+1}\cdot c_{k+1}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}\cdot c_{k},

and likewise
\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}b_{k}^{\prime}\cdot c_{k}^{\prime}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}b_{k}\cdot c_{k}
. The result follows.∎

Claim A.2
.

For any
\varepsilon\in\big(0,\frac{1}{2}\big)
, we have
(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2})/(1-\varepsilon)\geqslant 1+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
.

Proof.

For
\varepsilon\in\big(0,\frac{1}{2}\big)
the following chain of double implications holds

\frac{1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}{1-\varepsilon}\geqslant 1+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad 1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\Big(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\Big)=1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\geqslant 0.\qed

Probabilistic Inequalities

We begin by stating two well-known concentration inequalities.

Lemma A.3

(Method of Bounded Independent Differences
[
18
, Corollary 5.2]
)
.

Let
f
be a function of
N
independent random variables
X^{1},\dots,X^{N}
, where each
X^{i}
takes values in a set
\Omega^{i}
. Assume that for each
i\in[N]
there exists a
c_{i}\geqslant 0
such that

\left|f(x_{1},\dots,x_{i-1},x_{i},x_{i+1},\dots,x_{N})-f(x_{1},\dots,x_{i-1},x_{i}^{\prime},x_{i+1},\dots,x_{N})\right|\leqslant c_{i},

for any
x_{1}\in\Omega_{1},\dots,x_{i-1}\in\Omega_{i-1},x_{i},x_{i}^{\prime}\in\Omega_{i},x_{i+1}\in\Omega_{i+1},\dots,x_{N}\in\Omega_{N}
. Then, for any
\lambda>0
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,f<\mathbf{E}\left[\,f\,\right]-\lambda\,\right]\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{i}^{2}}\right).

Lemma A.4

(Azuma’s Inequality for Super-Martingales
[
18
, Problem 6.5]
)
.

Let
X^{0},\ldots,X^{N}
be a super-martingale with filtration
\mathfrak{F}^{0},\ldots,\mathfrak{F}^{N}
. Assume that for each
i\in[N]
there exists
c_{i}\geqslant 0
such that

\left(\left|X^{i}-X^{i-1}\right|~\big|~\mathfrak{F}^{i-1}\right)\leqslant c_{i}.

Then, for any
\lambda>0
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,X^{N}\geqslant X^{0}+\lambda\,\right]\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{i}^{2}}\right).

Lemma A.5

(
[
15
, Theorems 6.1 & 6.5]
)
.

Let
X^{0},\ldots,X^{N}
be a martingale with filtration
\mathfrak{F}^{0},\ldots,\mathfrak{F}^{N}
. Assume that for each
i\in[n]
,

\left(\left|X^{i}-X^{i-1}\right|~\big|~\mathfrak{F}^{i-1}\right)\leqslant M,

and additionally,

\mathbf{Var}\left[\,X^{i}\mid\mathfrak{F}^{i-1}\,\right]\leqslant\sigma_{i}^{2}.

Then, for any
\lambda>0
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left|X^{N}-\mathbf{E}\left[\,X^{N}\,\right]\right|\geqslant\lambda\,\right]\leqslant 2\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2\cdot(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}^{2}+M\lambda/3)}\right).

Next, we state the following well-known inequality for a sequence of random variables, whose expectations are related through a recurrence inequality.

Lemma A.6
.

Consider any sequence of random variables
(X^{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
for which there exist
a>0
and
b>0
, such that every
i\geqslant 1
,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,X^{i}\mid X^{i-1}\,\right]\leqslant X^{i-1}\cdot a+b.

Then,
(i)
for every
i\geqslant 0
,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,X^{i}\mid X^{0}\,\right]\leqslant X^{0}\cdot a^{i}+b\cdot\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}a^{j}.

Further,
(ii)
for
a\in(0,1)
and for every
i\geqslant 0
,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,X^{i}\mid X^{0}\,\right]\leqslant X^{0}\cdot a^{i}+\frac{b}{1-a}.

Finally,
(iii)
for
a\in(0,1)
and if
X^{0}\leqslant\frac{b}{1-a}
holds, then for every
i\geqslant 0
,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,X^{i}\,\right]\leqslant\frac{b}{1-a}.

Proof.

First statement.
We will prove this claim by induction.
For
i=0
,
\mathbf{E}\left[\,X^{0}\mid X^{0}\,\right]\leqslant X^{0}
. Assuming the induction hypothesis holds for some
i\geqslant 0
, then since
a>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,X^{i+1}\mid X^{0}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}\left[\,\mathbf{E}\left[\,X^{i+1}\mid X^{i}\,\right]\mid X^{0}\,\right]\leqslant\mathbf{E}\left[\,X^{i}\mid X^{0}\,\right]\cdot a+b

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(X^{0}\cdot a^{i}+b\cdot\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}a^{j}\right)\cdot a+b

\displaystyle=X^{0}\cdot a^{i+1}+b\cdot\sum_{j=0}^{i}a^{j}.

Second statement.
The claims follows using that
\sum_{j=0}^{i}a^{j}\leqslant\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}a^{j}=\frac{1}{1-a}
, for any
a\in(0,1)
.

Third statement.
We will prove this claim by induction. For
i=0
, it follows by the assumption. Then, assuming that
\mathbf{E}\left[\,X^{i}\,\right]\leqslant\frac{b}{1-a}
holds for
i\geqslant 0
, then for
i+1

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,X^{i+1}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}\left[\,\mathbf{E}\left[\,X^{i+1}\mid X^{i}\,\right]\,\right]\leqslant\mathbf{E}\left[\,X^{i}\,\right]\cdot a+b\leqslant\frac{b}{1-a}\cdot a+b=\frac{b}{1-a}.\qed

Appendix B
Counterexample for the Exponential Potential Function

In this section, we present a load vector for which the potential function
\Lambda:=\Lambda(\alpha)
for any constant
\alpha\in(0,1)
may increase in expectation over one round, if we do not condition on any “good event”.

In contrast to processes with constant probability bias in every round, such as those studied in
[
42
]
, for the
Mean-Thinning
process, there exists configurations where the potential
\Lambda
for constant
\alpha
increases in expectation over a single round, even when it is
\omega(n)
. This is because in the worst-case, the
Mean-Thinning
process may have a very small
\Theta(1/n^{2})
probability bias to allocate away from overloaded bins, corresponding to the
Towards-Min
process in
[
42
]
.

Claim B.1
.

For any constant
\alpha>0
and for sufficiently large
n:=n(\alpha)>0
consider the (normalized) load configuration at some round
t\geqslant 0
,

y^{t}=\left(n^{2},n,n,\ldots,n,-\frac{n\cdot(2n-3)}{2},-\frac{n\cdot(2n-3)}{2}\right).

Then for the
Mean-Thinning
process,
(i)
the exponential potential
\Lambda:=\Lambda(\alpha)
increases in expectation over the next round, i.e.,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\Lambda^{t+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]\geqslant\Lambda^{t}\cdot\left(1+0.2\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\right),

and
(ii)
the mean quantile satisfies
\delta^{s}\geqslant 1-2/n
for all rounds
s\in[t,t+n^{2})
.

Proof.

First statement.
Consider a labeling of the bins so that the normalized loads are sorted in non-increasing order. We will show that just the expected increase of bin
i=1
with maximum load
y_{1}^{t}=n^{2}
implies an expected increase for the entire potential. The probability of allocating to that bin is
\frac{1-\frac{2}{n}}{n}
, so using the Taylor estimate
e^{z}\geqslant 1+z+0.3z^{2}
for
z\geqslant-1.5
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\Lambda_{1}^{t+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]

\displaystyle=e^{\alpha n^{2}}\cdot e^{-\alpha/n}\cdot\left(1+(e^{\alpha}-1)\cdot\frac{1-\frac{2}{n}}{n}\right)

\displaystyle\geqslant e^{\alpha n^{2}}\cdot\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{n}+0.3\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n^{2}}\right)\cdot\left(1+(\alpha+0.3\alpha^{2})\cdot\frac{1-\frac{2}{n}}{n}\right)

\displaystyle=e^{\alpha n^{2}}\cdot\Big(1-\frac{\alpha}{n}+(\alpha+0.3\alpha^{2})\cdot\frac{1}{n}+o(n^{-1})\Big)

\displaystyle=e^{\alpha n^{2}}\cdot\left(1+0.4\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\right).

To relate this change to the expected change of the rest of the bins, we note that for sufficiently large
n
and since
\alpha
is constant,

\displaystyle 0.2\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\cdot e^{\alpha n^{2}}

\displaystyle\geqslant\left(1+0.2\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\right)\cdot n\cdot e^{\alpha n^{2}}\cdot e^{-\alpha\cdot 3n/2}=\left(1+0.2\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\right)\cdot n\cdot e^{\alpha n(2n-3)/2}

\displaystyle\geqslant\left(1+0.2\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\right)\cdot\left(\sum_{i>1}\Lambda_{i}^{t}\right),

(B.1)

since
\Lambda_{i}^{t}\leqslant e^{\alpha n(2n-3)/2}
for
i>1
. Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\Lambda^{t+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\Lambda_{1}^{t+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]\geqslant e^{\alpha n^{2}}\cdot\left(1+0.4\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\right)

\displaystyle=e^{\alpha n^{2}}\cdot\left(1+0.2\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\right)+0.2\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\cdot e^{\alpha n^{2}}

\displaystyle\!\!\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq:first_term_dominates})}}{{\geqslant}}\Lambda_{1}^{t}\cdot\left(1+0.2\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\right)+\left(\sum_{i>1}\Lambda_{i}^{t}\right)\cdot\left(1+0.2\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\Lambda^{t}\cdot\left(1+0.2\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\right).

Second statement.
Since there are
n-2
overloaded bins with overload at least
n
and these can decrease by at most
1/n
in each round, we have that
\delta^{s}\geqslant 1-2/n
for any
s\in[t,t+n^{2})
.
∎
