Abstract
. We address the question of counting maps between projective spaces such that images of cycles on the source intersect cycles on the target. In this paper we do it by embedding maps into quasimaps that form a projective space of their own. When a quasimap is not a map, it contains freckles (studied earlier) and/or scars, appearing when the complex dimension of the source is greater than one. We consider a lot of examples showing that freckle/scar calculus (using excess intersection theory) works. We also propose the “smooth conjecture” that may lead to computation of the number of maps by an integral over the space of quasimaps.



1.
Introduction

The goal of this paper is to attract attention to an enumerative problem that generalizes a well-known problem of counting rational curves in a toric manifold. The generalization is to higher-dimensional sources. We are doing the first steps in this project. We propose to study this problem starting with quasimaps, where the count is straightforward.
By subtracting the contribution of non-map (“proper quasimap”) solutions we obtain the number of maps. For one-dimensional source, this approach was discussed in
[
17
,
18
]
.

There are two ways to extract extra contributions from the quasimap count:

I.

Study the proper quasimap configurations themselves. We present here a lot of examples.
The proper treatment of these configurations requires Fulton’s excess intersection theory
[
6
]
.
The main formula is:

(1)

\mathrm{QM}=\mathrm{KM}+\mathrm{PQM}

where
\mathrm{KM}
is the number of holomorphic maps (that we call Kontsevich-Manin number
1
1

1

For source of complex dimension one, these numbers are known as Gromov-Witten invariants that were effectively computed by Kontsevich-Manin
[
14
]
.

) we are interested in.
\mathrm{QM}
is the (easily computable by a Bézout-like formula) total number of quasimaps and
\mathrm{PQM}
is the count of proper quasimap configurations. Proper quasimaps include

•

“freckle” configurations (where the evaluation is not defined at a collection of isolated points – “freckles” – in the source) and

•

“scar” configurations (evaluation fails on a cycle of positive dimension on the source) appearing for
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathrm{source})>1
.

We define scars in Section
2.4
(see also Example
2.15
) and study examples with them in Sections
6.5.2
and
6.6
.

PQM numbers range in complexity. It is easy to treat isolated freckle configurations. A bit more work is required to treat what we call
quasi-stable
examples. However, we found non-quasi-stable examples where the full machinery of excess intersection theory (in particular, Segre classes) is needed. We plan to come to this issue in a subsequent publication.

II.

Study integrals of differential forms over the space of quasimaps that are smooth on the locus of actual maps. Here we experimentally observe two things:

(i)

such integrals are convergent,

(ii)

they give correct answers in the simplest cases.

Therefore, we propose the Smooth Conjecture that states that having enough computational power, these numbers could be computed numerically.

As a byproduct, we were looking for a higher analog of quantum multiplication – a generating function for KM numbers with only 0-dimensional cycles on the source. Surprisingly enough, for source dimension greater than one, there is a quantum ring – a Frobenius algebra with free commutative product but nontrivial counit – which does not descend to a deformation of the cohomology ring of the target.

This paper is intended as a self-contained mathematical text motivated by the problem of gauged holomorphic models
[
9
,
16
]
for complex dimension of the source 1 and 2. The relation of numbers that we find here to physics will be explained elsewhere; here we focus on the mathematical side of the problem.

2.2.
Evaluation map

A quasimap
f\colon X\not\to Y

can be evaluated at a point
x\in X\setminus\mathrm{PQL}(f)
i.e., one has an evaluation map

(3)

\mathrm{ev}\colon(\mathrm{QMap}(X,Y)\times X)\backslash\{(f,x)\;|\;x\not\in\mathrm{PQL}(f)\}\;\longrightarrow\;Y.

In particular, evaluation of a quasimap at a point of
\mathrm{PQL}(f)
(e.g. a freckle) is not defined.
Restricted to holomorphic maps (quasimaps with
\mathrm{PQL}(f)=\varnothing

(4)

\mathrm{ev}\colon\mathrm{Map}(X,Y)\times X\rightarrow Y.

3.2.
Important remark: meaningfulness of the problem. Syzygies of holomorphicity equations.

It is widely believed that the problem of holomorphic maps from a higher-dimensional source has virtual dimension equal to
-\infty
and that is why it should not be studied on the same footing as holomorphic maps from 1-dimensional source. However, this argument is not quite correct due to syzygies, as we will outline below.

Let
(X,J^{X})
and
(Y,J^{Y})
be two almost complex manifolds.
A map
f\colon X\to Y
is called
pseudo-holomorphic
if its differential intertwines the two almost complex structures, i.e. if

(21)

J^{Y}\circ f_{*}=f_{*}\circ J^{X}.

Consequently, a pseudo-holomorphic map

intertwines the Nijenhuis tensors of
X
and
Y

(22)

\partial_{\mu}f^{a}\ (N_{J^{X}})^{\mu}_{\bar{\rho}\bar{\sigma}}=(N_{J^{Y}}\circ f)^{a}_{\bar{b}\bar{c}}\ \overline{\partial_{\rho}f^{b}}\ \overline{\partial_{\sigma}f^{\vphantom{b}c}}.

A sufficient condition to satisfy the constraints (
22
) is the integrability of the source and target complex structures
J^{X}
and
J^{Y}
, which we will consider henceforth.

The space of holomorphic maps
X\to Y
between two complex manifolds naively has negative infinite virtual dimension if
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}X\geq 2
.
Indeed, if
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}X=k
and
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}Y=n

are local complex coordinates of
X

(23)

\bar{\partial}f^{a}(z)=0

yield

n

.
Therefore

\dim_{\rm vir}\mathrm{Map}(X,Y)=(\#{\rm variables}-\#{\rm equations})\cdot\#{\rm points}=n(1-k)\cdot\infty

which equals

when
k\geq 2
.
However, in higher dimensions (
k\geq 2
) there exist syzygies (linear relations among the conditions (
23
)) which render the dimension of the space of holomorphic maps finite.

Since the holomorphicity equation (
23
) give point-wise conditions, syzygies can be expressed in terms of integrals.
For

, we call

\int_{X}\sigma_{a}\wedge\bar{\partial}f^{a}=0,

Syzygies in the above sense are thus given by

-closed forms.
If

-exact forms.
This redundancy usually indicates the presence of syzygies among syzygies.

Remark 3.3
.

In principle it is possible to construct the full tower of syzygies by constructing the Koszul-Tate resolution of an appropriate bundle:
Let

be the vector bundle over the space of smooth maps

Equation (

Syzygies (and syzygies for syzygies and so forth) can now be described in terms of Tate generators of the Koszul-Tate resolution of the sheaf of function on the zero-locus of

as the Frobenius algebra
\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{P}^{k},\mathbb{P}^{n}}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\mathbb{C}[[q]][x]
with the standard product and the counit

(34)

\eta(p(x))\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\sum_{d\geq 0}q^{d}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\gamma}\frac{p(x)dx}{x^{\mathsf{n}_{k,n,d}+1}}.

Note that
\eta
acts on monomials as

\eta(x^{j})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}q^{d}&\mathrm{if}\;j=\mathsf{n}_{k,n,d},\\
0&\mathrm{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

Example 3.5
.

For instance, for
k=n=2

Remark 3.6
.

For
k=2
(i.e. for maps
\mathbb{P}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n}
), one can write the counit (

) in terms of the Jacobi theta function
\theta_{10}
(in Mumford’s notation) as follows:

\eta(p(x))=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\gamma}p(x)F(q,x),

where

\displaystyle F(q,x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{d\geq 0}\frac{q^{d}}{x^{(n+1)\frac{(d+2)(d+1)}{2}}}dx

\displaystyle=\mathrm{reg}_{q=0}\left(q^{-1}\sum_{d^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{q^{d^{\prime}}}{x^{(n+1)\frac{d^{\prime}(d^{\prime}+1)}{2}}}dx\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{reg}_{q=0}\Big(x^{\frac{n+1}{8}}q^{-\frac{3}{2}}\underbrace{\sum_{d^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}}q^{d^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2}}x^{-\frac{n+1}{2}(d^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2})^{2}}}_{\theta_{10}(z,\tau)}dx\Big).

Here

stands for the operation of subtracting the negative part of the Laurent expansion in

are related to

by

x^{-\frac{n+1}{2}}=e^{\pi i\tau},\;\;q=e^{2\pi iz}.

Thus, one has

(35)

\eta(p(x))=-\frac{1}{n+1}\mathsf{P}\oint_{\tilde{\gamma}}d\tau\,p(e^{-\frac{2\pi i\tau}{n+1}})e^{-2\pi i\tau(\frac{1}{n+1}+\frac{1}{8})}e^{-3\pi iz}\theta_{10}(z,\tau),

where
\mathsf{P}
is the projection to nonnegative Fourier modes in the variable
z
;
\tilde{\gamma}
is the image of the contour
\gamma
in the plane of the modular parameter
\tau
.

The following is an obvious consequence of (

Corollary 3.7
.

One has

(36)

\mathsf{CQP}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{l})=\eta(p_{1}(x)\cdots p_{l}(x)),

where
\alpha_{i}
are cohomology classes of
\mathbb{P}^{n}
and
p_{i}(x)
are the corresponding polynomials.

Lemma 3.8
.

For
k\geq 2
, the pairing on
\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{P}^{k},\mathbb{P}^{n}}
induced by the counit,
(p_{1},p_{2})=\eta(p_{1}p_{2})
, has zero kernel.

Proof.

Assume that a polynomial

p(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{\delta}p_{i}(x)q^{i}

(where
p_{i}
are polynomials in
x
independent of
q

(,)

\eta(p(x)x^{j})=0
for all

p_{\delta}
is nonzero. Choose
d

•

\mathsf{n}_{k,n,d}\geq\deg p_{\delta}
,

•

\mathsf{n}_{k,n,d}+\deg p_{i}<\mathsf{n}_{k,n,d+1}
for

The fact that it is possible to satisfy the second condition relies on the assumption

– then the gaps between consecutive numbers in the sequence
\{\mathsf{n}_{k,n,d}\}_{d=0,1,2,\ldots}
are growing.

The expression

(37)

\eta(p(x)x^{\mathsf{n}_{k,n,d}-\deg p_{\delta}})

contains the term
q^{\delta+d}\cdot(\mathrm{top\;coefficient\;of}\;p_{\delta})
coming from

which cannot be canceled by anything from
p_{<\delta}(x)
. Thus, (
37

cannot be in the kernel of
(,)
.
∎

In particular, Lemma
3.8
implies that unlike in the case of the usual quantum product (
k=1
),
\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{P}^{k},\mathbb{P}^{n}}
does not have a finite-dimensional quotient isomorphic to the cohomology of the target with a
q
-deformed product.

Considering the higher quantum product as a
2\rightarrow 1
operation
*

x^{j}
with cohomology classes
\omega^{j}
of
\mathbb{P}^{n}

is

if

does not correspond to an element of

(but corresponds to an element of
\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{P}^{k},\mathbb{P}^{n}}

is identified with a subspace of
\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{P}^{k},\mathbb{P}^{n}}
which is not closed under the quantum product.

In the language of QFT (a higher-dimensional A-model localizing to holomorphic maps
X\rightarrow Y
), we expect the higher quantum cohomology ring to describe the OPE algebra of a class of observables: elements of

correspond to the usual evaluation observables, while other elements of

correspond to a new type of observables.
20
20

20

Perhaps, a higher-dimensional counterpart of the “tangency observables” known in the A-model.
In particular, OPEs of evaluation observables can contain the “new” observables.

Remark 3.9
.

The discussion above generalizes straightforwardly to maps

(cf. Remark

so that a map of multi-degree

is counted with weight
q_{1}^{d_{1}}\cdots q_{m}^{d_{m}}

\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{P}^{k_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{P}^{k_{m}},\mathbb{P}^{n}}=\mathbb{C}[[q_{1},\ldots,q_{m}]][x]
with the standard product and the counit

\eta(p(x))=\sum_{d_{1},\ldots,d_{m}\geq 0}q_{1}^{d_{1}}\cdots q_{m}^{d_{m}}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\gamma}\frac{p(x)dx}{x^{\mathsf{n}_{(k_{1},\ldots,k_{m}),n,(d_{1},\ldots,d_{m})}+1}}

with the exponents
\mathsf{n}_{(k_{1},\ldots,k_{m}),n,(d_{1},\ldots,d_{m})}

For instance, for maps

with

4.
Counting quasimaps (“
\mathrm{QM}
numbers”)

4.1.
Formulation of the problem

We now describe the
quasimap counting problem
. Let
X
be a compact complex manifold and
Y=\mathbb{C}^{N}\sslash G
. We denote
\pi\colon\mathbb{C}^{N}\setminus\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\to Y
the quotient map. We want to count quasimaps
f=(\mathcal{P},\underline{f})
subject to certain conditions. Namely, fix a natural number
l
and closed submanifolds
c_{1}^{X},\ldots,c_{l}^{X}\subset X
and
c_{1}^{Y},\ldots,c_{l}^{Y}\subset Y
.
We have that
\overline{\pi^{-1}(c_{i}^{Y})}
is a
G
-space and we can consider the inclusion of fiber bundles

(38)

\iota\colon\mathcal{P}\times_{G}\overline{\pi^{-1}(c_{i}^{Y})}\to\mathcal{P}\times_{G}\mathbb{C}^{N}.

We denote
\widetilde{c_{i}^{Y}}=\mathcal{P}\times_{G}\overline{\pi^{-1}(c_{i}^{Y})}
.

To condense the notation, we denote

(39)

\mathcal{D}=(\{c_{i}^{X}\}_{i=1}^{l},\{c_{i}^{Y}\}_{i=1}^{l}|\mathcal{P})

and call it the
quasimap counting data
.

Enumerative Problem B
.

Given a quasimap counting data (
39
), we consider the set

(40)

\mathrm{QMap}(X,Y,\mathcal{D})\!\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\!\left\{f\in\mathrm{QMap}(X,Y)\colon f=(\mathcal{P},\underline{f}),\;\underline{f}(c_{i}^{X})\cap\widetilde{c_{i}^{Y}}\neq\varnothing\right\}.

If this set is finite we call the quasimap data
stable
and we define the
\mathrm{QM}
number

(41)

\mathrm{QM}(X,Y,\mathcal{D})\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\#\mathrm{QMap}(X,Y,\mathcal{D}).

Remark 4.1
.

Suppose
f\in\mathrm{QMap}(X,Y,\mathcal{P},(c_{i}^{X})_{i=1}^{n},(c_{i}^{Y})_{i=1}^{n})
and
\mathrm{PQL}(f)=\varnothing
. Then
f
defines a holomorphic map
f\colon X\to Y
such that
f(c_{i}^{X})\cap c_{i}^{Y}\neq\varnothing
, whose homotopy class
\delta
is determined by the principal bundle
\mathcal{P}
.

Remark 4.2
.

We can extend the
\mathrm{QM}
number to collections of
cycles

c_{i}^{X}
,
c_{i}^{Y}
by multilinearity.

4.1.1.
Main example

Let us consider the main example of quasimaps
\mathbb{P}^{k}\not\to\mathbb{P}^{n}
. In this case
\mathcal{P}=O(d)
for some
d\geq 1
and we will denote the quasimap data simply by
\mathcal{D}=(\{c_{i}^{X}\}_{i=1}^{l},\{c_{i}^{Y}\}_{i=1}^{l}|d).
Quasimaps
f=(O(d),P^{\alpha}(x))
are given by a collection of
n+1
homogeneous polynomials in
k+1
variables of degree
d
.
We consider target cycles

c^{Y}_{i}\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
such that the closure of the preimage
\overline{\pi^{-1}(c^{Y}_{i})}
is a linear subspace of the same codimension and hence can be written as an intersection of
n_{i}^{Y}=\operatorname{codim}c_{i}^{Y}
hyperplanes
H_{i}^{j}
.
Then, quasimaps
f\in\mathrm{QMap}(\mathbb{P}^{k},\mathbb{P}^{n},\{c_{i}^{X}\}_{i=1}^{l},\{c_{i}^{Y}\}_{i=1}^{l})|d)
are given by solutions to the system of equations

(42)

H_{i}^{j}(P^{\alpha}(x_{i}))=0

subject to the condition
x_{i}\in c_{i}^{X}
. Here the coefficients of these equations are the coefficients of
H_{i}^{j}
, the unknowns are the coefficients of the homogeneous polynomials
P^{\alpha}
and the points
x_{i}
. In particular, the virtual dimension of
\mathrm{QMap}(\mathbb{P}^{k},\mathbb{P}^{n},\{c_{i}^{X}\}_{i=1}^{l},\{c_{i}^{Y}\}_{i=1}^{l})|d)
is given by

(43)

\dim_{\mathrm{vir}}\mathrm{QMap}(\mathbb{P}^{k},\mathbb{P}^{n},(c_{i}^{X})_{i=1}^{l},(c_{i}^{Y})_{i=1}^{l}|d)=\\
=\mathsf{n}_{k,n,d}+\sum_{i=1}^{l}\dim c_{i}^{X}-\operatorname{codim}c_{i}^{Y},

cf. (
28
).

Example 4.3
.

Consider the example
k=1,n=2
and
d\geq 1
. For
i=1,2,3
we let
c_{i}^{X}
be points in
\mathbb{P}^{1}
and
c_{i}^{Y}
be lines in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
. Let
l^{\prime}
be a natural number. For
i=4,\ldots,l^{\prime}+3=\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}l
, we let
c_{i}^{X}=\mathbb{P}^{1}
and
c_{i}^{Y}
be points in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
. Then the virtual dimension of the quasimap space
\mathrm{QMap}(\mathbb{P}^{k},\mathbb{P}^{n},(c_{i}^{X})_{i=1}^{l},(c_{i}^{Y})_{i=1}^{l}|d)
is

(44)

\underbrace{3(d+1)-1}_{n_{1,2,d}}+\underbrace{3\cdot 0+l^{\prime}\cdot 1}_{\sum\dim c_{i}^{X}}-\underbrace{(2l^{\prime}+3)}_{\sum\operatorname{codim}c_{i}^{Y}}=3d+2-l^{\prime}-3=3d-1-l^{\prime}.

We interpret this example as follows: By fixing the images of three points in
\mathbb{P}^{1}
to lie on lines we “gauge fix” the
\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})
action on
\mathbb{P}^{1}
(notice that this reduces the dimension of the quasimap space by
3=\dim\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})
).
To have a valid quasimap count we need to demand that the quasi-map pass through
3d-1
points in the target
Y
. In this way we recover the zero virtual dimension
condition for counting holomorphic maps.

4.2.
Explicit answer from intersection theory

In the main example, we can easily compute the
\mathrm{QM}
number from intersection theory. Namely, we observe that given
H\in(\mathbb{C}^{n+1})^{\vee}
, the map
(P^{\alpha},x)\mapsto H(P^{\alpha}(x))
can be interpreted as a section of the line bundle

(45)

L_{d}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\mathcal{O}(1)\boxtimes\mathcal{O}(d)\to\mathrm{QMap}_{d}(\mathbb{P}^{k},\mathbb{P}^{n})\times\mathbb{P}^{k}.

Equation (
42
) can then be interpreted as the statement that such a section has a zero.
We will use this observation to reformulate the quasimap count as a the count of zeros of a section.
Namely, given a collection of submanifolds
c_{i}^{X}\subset\mathbb{P}^{k},i=1,\ldots,k
, we define the compact complex manifold

(46)

\mathrm{Var}=\mathrm{QMap}_{d}(\mathbb{P}^{k},\mathbb{P}^{n})\times c_{1}^{X}\times\ldots\times c_{l}^{X}.

We have the obvious maps
p_{i}\colon\mathrm{Var}\to\mathrm{QMap}_{d}(\mathbb{P}^{k},\mathbb{P}^{n})\times\mathbb{P}^{k}
given by composing the inclusion
c_{i}^{X}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{k}
and the projection to the
i
th factor. We then have the following

Proposition 4.4
.

Given the quasimap counting data
\mathcal{D}=\left(\{c_{i}^{X}\}_{i=1}^{l},\{c_{i}^{Y}\})_{i=1}^{l}|d\right)
we define the following vector bundle
E
over
\mathrm{Var}

(47)

E\equiv E(\mathcal{D})\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{l}\oplus_{j=1}^{\operatorname{codim}c_{i}^{Y}}p_{i}^{*}L_{d}.

Writing
\overline{\pi^{-1}(c_{i}^{Y})}=\cap_{j=1}^{\operatorname{codim}c_{i}^{Y}}H_{i}^{j}
,
one has a section
\sigma
of
E

given by

\sigma\colon\underbrace{(\underline{f},x_{1},\ldots,x_{l})}_{\in\mathrm{Var}}\mapsto\underbrace{\Big(\Big(H_{i}^{j}(\underline{f}(x_{i}))\Big)_{i=1}^{l}\Big)_{j=1}^{\operatorname{codim}c_{i}^{Y}}}_{\in E_{\underline{f},x_{1},\ldots,x_{l}}}.

Then

(48)

p(\sigma(\mathrm{Var})\cap E_{0})=\mathrm{QMap}(\mathbb{P}^{k},\mathbb{P}^{n},\mathcal{D}),

where
E_{0}
denotes the zero section of
E
and
p
is the projection to the first factor in the r.h.s. of (
46
).

Remark 4.5
.

The base space
\mathrm{Var}
has dimension

\dim\mathrm{Var}=\mathsf{n}_{k,n,d}+\sum_{i}\dim c_{i}^{X},

while the vector bundle
E
has rank

\mathrm{rk}(E)=\sum_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{\operatorname{codim}c_{i}^{Y}}\mathrm{rk}(p_{i}^{*}L_{d})=\sum_{i}\operatorname{codim}c_{i}^{Y}.

The virtual dimension can thus be expressed as

\dim_{\mathrm{vir}}\mathrm{QMap}(\mathbb{P}^{k},\mathbb{P}^{n},\mathcal{D})=\dim\mathrm{Var}-\mathrm{rk}(E).

Observing that the number of zeroes of a generic section of a vector bundle
E
is given by the Euler number
e(E)
of that vector bundle, we obtain the following

Corollary 4.6
.

For generic stable quasimap data
\mathcal{D}
, we have

(49)

\mathrm{QM}(\mathbb{P}^{k},\mathbb{P}^{n},\mathcal{D})=e(E(\mathcal{D})).

The Euler number can be readily computed from the fact that the Euler class
[\tilde{e}]
of a complex vector bundle is its top Chern class and that the Euler class is multiplicative under the Whitney sum of vector bundles

\tilde{e}(E\oplus E^{\prime})=\tilde{e}(E)\wedge\tilde{e}(E^{\prime}).

Let us denote by
H\in\Omega^{2}_{cl}(\mathrm{QMap}_{d}(\mathbb{P}^{k},\mathbb{P}^{n}))

a representative of the class Poincaré dual to a hyperplane in
\mathrm{QMap}_{d}(\mathbb{P}^{k},\mathbb{P}^{n})=\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{n}_{k,n,d}}
and by
h\in\Omega^{2}_{cl}(\mathbb{P}^{k})
– a representative of the class Poincaré dual to a hyperplane in
\mathbb{P}^{k}
.
Then the first Chern class of
L_{d}
is

(50)

c_{1}(L_{d})=H+h.

(We suppress in the notation the pullbacks along the projections from
\mathrm{QMap}_{d}(\mathbb{P}^{k},\mathbb{P}^{n})\times\mathbb{P}^{k}
to the first or second summand.)
In particular, we have the following result.

Corollary 4.7
.

For stable quasimap counting data
\mathcal{D}
, the
\mathrm{QM}
number is given by the following integral

(51)

\mathrm{QM}(\mathbb{P}^{k},\mathbb{P}^{n},\mathcal{D})=\int_{\mathrm{Var}}\tilde{e}(E)=\int_{\mathrm{Var}}\bigwedge_{i=1}^{l}(H+d\,h_{i})^{\operatorname{codim}c_{i}^{Y}}.

Here
h_{i}=p_{i}^{*}h
.

Remark 4.8
.

Sometimes we will work with the space
\widetilde{\mathrm{Var}}=\mathrm{QMap}(X,Y)\times X^{\times l}
instead. Then, for arbitrary representatives
\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{l}
of classes Poincaré dual to
c_{1}^{X},\ldots,c_{l}^{X}
, we have

(52)

\begin{split}\mathrm{QM}(\mathbb{P}^{k},\mathbb{P}^{n},\mathcal{D})&=\int_{\widetilde{\mathrm{Var}}}\pi_{1}^{*}\delta_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\pi_{l}^{*}\delta_{l}\wedge\tilde{e}(E)\\
&=\int_{\widetilde{\mathrm{Var}}}\pi_{1}^{*}\delta_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\pi_{l}^{*}\delta_{l}\wedge\bigwedge_{i=1}^{l}(H+d\,\pi_{i}^{*}h)^{\operatorname{codim}c_{i}^{Y}}.\end{split}

where
\pi_{i}\colon\widetilde{\mathrm{Var}}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{k}
is the projection to the
i
th copy of
\mathbb{P}^{k}
.

4.3.
\mathrm{QM}
numbers as sums of map counts and proper quasimap numbers

Let us consider again the general case of quasimaps
f\colon X\not\to Y
. Imposing stable counting data
\mathcal{D}
on the decomposition

\mathrm{QMap}(X,Y)=\mathrm{Map}(X,Y)\sqcup\mathrm{QMap}^{\mathrm{pr}}(X,Y),

we obtain

(53)

\mathrm{QMap}(X,Y,\mathcal{D})=\mathrm{Map}(X,Y,\mathcal{D})\sqcup\mathrm{QMap}^{\mathrm{pr}}(X,Y,\mathcal{D}).

This immediately implies the following statement:

(54)

\mathrm{QM}(X,Y,\mathcal{D})=\mathrm{KM}(X,Y,\mathcal{D})+\#\mathrm{QMap}^{\mathrm{pr}}(X,Y,\mathcal{D}),

where
\mathrm{KM}(X,Y,\mathcal{D})
is the Kontsevich-Manin number defined in (
20
).

Definition 4.9
.

Given stable quasimap counting data
\mathcal{D}
we denote the number of proper quasimaps in
\mathrm{QMap}(X,Y,\mathcal{D})
by

(55)

\mathrm{PQM}(X,Y,\mathcal{D})\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\#\mathrm{QMap}^{\mathrm{pr}}(X,Y,\mathcal{D}).

We restate (
54
) in this terminology:

Proposition 4.10
.

For
X,Y
as in Section
4.1
and stable quasimap counting data
\mathcal{D}
, the
\mathrm{QM}
number is the sum of the Kontsevich-Manin and the proper quasimap numbers,

(56)

\mathrm{QM}(X,Y,\mathcal{D})=\mathrm{KM}(X,Y,\mathcal{D})+\mathrm{PQM}(X,Y,\mathcal{D}).

5.
Counting quasimaps in non-stable cases via excess intersection theory

5.1.
Quasi-stable case

As we will see in the next section, in many examples the counting data turns out to be
unstable
, i.e. the zero locus of the section
\sigma
has components of positive dimension.
In this case the
\mathrm{QM}
number can be defined by the integral (
51
), i.e.

(57)

\mathrm{QM}(X,Y,\mathcal{D})\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\int_{\mathrm{Var}}\tilde{e}(E).

The right hand side no longer has an interpretation as counting the zeros of the section
\sigma
. However, one can still make sense of the formula

\mathrm{QM}(X,Y,\mathcal{D})=\mathrm{KM}(X,Y,\mathcal{D})+\mathrm{PQM}(X,Y,\mathcal{D})

via
excess intersection theory

[
6
]
(see also the nice introduction in
[
13
, Chapter 8]
). We denote by
{\rm Z}(\sigma)
the zero set of the section
\sigma
restricted to proper quasimaps. Then, suppose that

Z\subseteq{\rm Z}(\sigma)

is a connected component,
possibly of positive dimension.
If
Z
is a smooth submanifold of
\mathrm{Var}
, we can define the
excess bundle
on
Z
by

(58)

B_{Z}=\frac{E\rvert_{Z}}{\sigma_{*}(N_{Z})},

where
N_{Z}
is the normal bundle of
Z
in
\mathrm{Var}
that we identify as a subbundle of
E
via the section
\sigma
. We give a special name to configurations where all components of the zero set are of this form:

Definition 5.1
.

In the situation of the Enumerative Problem
B
, suppose that
\mathrm{KM}(X,Y,\mathcal{D})
is finite and
{\rm Z}(\sigma)
is a union of connected components that are submanifolds defined by transverse intersections,

{\rm Z}(\sigma)=\bigsqcup_{i}Z_{i}.

Then, we call the counting data
\mathcal{D}

quasi-stable
.

For quasi-stable counting data, we have the following result.

Proposition 5.2
.

Suppose the counting data is quasi-stable and denote
Z_{i}
the connected components of
{\rm Z}(\sigma)
. Let
c(B_{Z_{i}})
denote the total Chern class of the bundle
B_{Z_{i}}
. Then

\mathrm{QM}(X,Y,\mathcal{D})=\int_{\mathrm{Var}}\tilde{e}(E)=\mathrm{KM}(X,Y,\mathcal{D})+\sum_{i}\int_{Z_{i}}c(B_{Z_{i}}).

Proof.

For a holomorphic vector bundle
E\to\mathrm{Var}
, the self-intersection class of the zero section is the the Euler class of
\mathrm{Var}
,

[E_{0}]\cdot[E_{0}]=c_{\mathrm{rk}\,E}\frown[\mathrm{Var}]\in A_{0}(\mathrm{Var}),

see e.g.
[
1
, Example 2.9]
. Here
c_{\mathrm{rk}\,E}
denotes the
\mathrm{rk}\,E
Chern class of
E
. Since the image
\sigma(\mathrm{Var})
of a holomorphic section
\sigma\colon\mathrm{Var}\to E
is rationally equivalent to the zero section, the intersection product
[\sigma(\mathrm{Var})]\cdot[E_{0}]=c_{\mathrm{rk}\,E}\frown[\mathrm{Var}]
as well. Then, we have that

(59)

\sigma(\mathrm{Var})\cap E_{0}=\widehat{\mathrm{Map}}(X,Y,\mathcal{D})\sqcup\bigsqcup_{i}Z_{i}.

In formula (
59
),
\widehat{\mathrm{Map}}(X,Y,\mathcal{D})
is the intersection of the zero set of
\sigma
with
\mathrm{Map}_{d}(X,Y)\times\prod_{i}c_{i}^{X}
.
By a Bézout-like theorem, on the one hand the degree of
[\sigma(\mathrm{Var})]\cdot[E_{0}]
is the quasimap number
\mathrm{QM}(X,Y,\mathcal{D})
. On the other hand, the intersection product

6.4.
Quasi-stable examples

In this subsection we consider examples where the naive quasimap count is degenerate, i.e. even though we are considering a balanced configuration of cycles, there are positive-dimensional components in the space of quasimaps satisfying all the equations.

Intuitively, we encounter the following situation: for each pair of source and target cycles
c_{i}^{X},c_{i}^{Y}
we have
\dim c_{i}^{X}
moduli and
\operatorname{codim}c_{i}^{Y}
equations, i.e. by adding such a pair we expect to reduce the dimension of the space of quasimaps by
d_{i}=\operatorname{codim}c_{i}^{Y}-\dim c_{i}^{X}
. For
proper
quasimaps, however, the equations become void when the running points on
c_{i}^{X}
hit the freckle.
If in this way, we lose more
d_{i}
than the codimension of the corresponding freckle stratum, we obtain a family of such quasimaps. For example, for
k=1,n=2
, the freckle stratum has codimension 2 (common zero locus of three polynomials). A running point (sent to a point) has
d=1
, so if we have 3 running points colliding in a freckle, we obtain a family of positive dimension.
