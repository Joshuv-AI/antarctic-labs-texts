Bound State Operators and Wedge-Locality in Integrable Quantum Field Theories
Yoh TANIMOTO †‡

Abstract
We consider scalar two-dimensional quantum field theories with a factorizing S S -matrix which has poles in the physical strip. In our previous work, we introduced the bound state operators and constructed candidate operators for observables in wedges. Under some additional assumptions on the S S -matrix, we show that, in order to obtain their strong commutativity, it is enough to prove the essential self-adjointness of the sum of the left and right bound state operators. This essential self-adjointness is shown up to the two-particle component.

Introduction

The goal of this series of papers
[
6
,
7
,
9
,
35
]
is to construct more interacting two-dimensional integrable quantum field theories in the Haag–Kastler framework. The novelty of the recent progresses is that one first constructs observables localized in infinitely-extended wedge regions, then obtains compactly localized observables in an indirect way. In the preceding paper
[
6
]
, we constructed candidates for wedge-local observables, however, they have subtle domain properties and their strong commutativity remained open. In this paper, we provide a possible way to settle this question, which is a crucial step towards construction of Haag–Kastler nets.

The main strategy was explained in
[
6
]
and we only briefly summarize it. See also
[
21
]
for an overview of the program. The operator-algebraic framework for quantum field theory (QFT), or a Haag–Kastler net
[
16
]
, concerns the collection of algebras of local observables. As in any mathematical framework for relativistic QFT, it is a very difficult problem to construct interacting examples of Haag–Kastler net. For certain integrable models with prescribed
S
-matrix, Schroer proposed
[
31
]
to construct certain wedge-local observables, then obtain compactly localized observables as the intersection of two wedge-algebras. Some constructions of wedge-local algebras have been obtained for a class of
S
-matrices
[
18
,
22
,
34
]
, as well as operator-algebraic constructions or deformations
[
1
,
2
,
5
,
12
,
20
,
33
,
34
]
. The final step to show the existence of local observables has been completed in certain cases, by showing modular nuclearity
[
4
,
19
]
, or by split property
[
34
]
.

In
[
18
]
, Lechner constructed wedge-local fields for scalar factorizing
S
-matrices without poles in the physical strip. We attempted to extend this construction to the cases with poles in
[
6
]
, however, certain spectral properties remained unsolved. More precisely, our candidates for wedge-local fields were a slight modification of the fields of
[
18
]
, but contained a very singular term, the bound state operator, which has been introduced exactly in order to treat the poles. The strong commutativity of these candidates remained unclear. We studied the one-particle component of these operators in detail in
[
35
]
and found convenient self-adjoint extensions. In the present work, we propose a strategy to prove the strong commutativity between the candidate operators.

Self-adjointness of unbounded operators is in general a hard problem. There are several criteria (e.g., see
[
26
]
), but we saw in
[
6
,
35
]
that most of them do not apply to our situation: the reason is that the domain of self-adjointness depends very much on the observables and one cannot find either a single domain or a single dominating operator. We try to solve this problem by a front attack, namely, we should find an appropriate common domain for any pair of wedge-observables and show their strong commutativity. Thanks to the Driessler–Fröhlich theorem, the problem of strong commutativity is reduced to the (essential) self-adjointness of the sum of the left and right bound state operators. We show this self-adjointness up to the two-particle component. It is worth mentioning that, because of all these technicalities, the candidate observable
{\widetilde{\phi}}
is no longer an operator-valued distribution.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we recall our approach to integrable QFT with bound states. Especially, we extend the domain of the bound state operators introduced in
[
6
]
using the convenient extension found in
[
35
]
. Then in Section
3
it is shown that the strong commutativity of wedge-observable candidates follows from the essential self-adjointness of the sum bound state operator. We do this by combining the Driessler–Fröhlich theorem. With this choice of domain, the covariance of the candidate operators also follows, which allows one to proceed to Borchers triples. In Section
4
, we show the essential self-adjointness of the one and two particle components of the sum bound state operator. We also present some observations on
n
-particle components which demonstrates how the poles and zeros in the
S
-matrix affect the analytic properties of the observables.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Wedge-local nets and Borchers triples

Let us summarize the construction of
[
6
]
. Our goal is to construct interacting Haag–Kastler nets in two dimensions. As an intermediate step, we need to construct nets of observables localized in wedges, or wedge-local nets (of von Neumann algebras) for short. This notion is equivalent to that of Borchers triples, which we will use in this work. We refer to
[
6
, Section 2.1]
for an overview of the construction program through Borchers triples.

A (two-dimensional)
Borchers triple

({\mathcal{M}},U,\Omega)
consists of a von Neumann algebra
{\mathcal{M}}
, a unitary representation of the proper orthochronous Poincaré group
{{\mathcal{P}}^{\uparrow}_{+}}
and a vector
\Omega
which satisfy the following conditions (see
[
6
, Section 2.1]
):

•

\Omega
is cyclic and separating for
{\mathcal{M}}
;

•

the restriction of
U
to the translation subgroup
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
has the joint spectrum included in
\overline{V_{+}}=\{(a_{0},a_{1})\colon a_{0}\geq|a_{1}|\}
;

•

{\mathcal{M}}
is covariant with respect to
U
, namely,
\operatorname{Ad}U(g){\mathcal{M}}\subset{\mathcal{M}}
for
g\in{{\mathcal{P}}^{\uparrow}_{+}}
which preserves the standard right-wedge
W_{\mathrm{R}}
.

In
[
6
]
, we constructed for test functions
f
,
g
supported in
W_{\mathrm{L}}
,
W_{\mathrm{R}}
respectively, unbounded operators
{\widetilde{\phi}}(f)
,
{\widetilde{\phi}}^{\prime}(g)
which commute weakly on a dense domain. We also showed that, if
{\widetilde{\phi}}(f)
and
{\widetilde{\phi}}^{\prime}(g)
commute strongly, then
\Omega
is cyclic and separating for
{\mathcal{M}}:=\big\{e^{i{\widetilde{\phi}}^{\prime}(g)}\colon\operatorname{supp}g\big\}^{\prime\prime}
(strong commutativity is important for the separating property). Furthermore, if we can show the covariance of
{\widetilde{\phi}}
with respect to
U
, then the covariance of the Borchers triple follows and the construction is complete. We will investigate strict locality, namely whether this Borchers triple defines a Haag–Kastler net, in a separate paper
[
8
]
(actually, for this purpose the definition of
{\widetilde{\phi}}
must be slightly extended as we will do in Section
2.4
). Therefore, our most urgent problem is the strong commutativity.

We have
{\widetilde{\phi}}(f)=\phi(f)+\chi(f)
, where
\phi(f)
is very well under control, while
\chi(f)
is a new sort of operator and we investigated its one-particle component
\chi_{1}(f)
in detail. We review the construction of these operators below.

2.2
Factorizing
\boldsymbol{S}
-matrix with poles

As an input, we fix a
two-particle scattering function

S
,
which is at first a meromorphic function on
{\mathbb{R}}+i(0,\pi)
, the region we call the
physical strip
,
and later extended to
{\mathbb{C}}
which satisfies the following properties:

(S1)

Unitarity.

S(\theta)^{-1}=\overline{S(\theta)}
for
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

(S2)

Hermitian analyticity.

S(-\theta)=S(\theta)^{-1}
for
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

(S3)

Crossing symmetry.

S
has a meromorphic extension in
{\mathbb{C}}
and satisfies
S(\zeta)=S(\pi i-\zeta)
.

(S4)

Bootstrap equation.

S(\zeta)=S\big(\zeta+\frac{\pi i}{3}\big)S\big(\zeta-\frac{\pi i}{3}\big)
.

(S5)

Positive residue.

S
has a simple pole at
\frac{2\pi i}{3}
and
R:={\underset{\zeta=\frac{2\pi i}{3}}{\operatorname{Res}}S(\zeta)\in i{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}
. Except this and the pole at
\frac{\pi i}{3}
, there is no pole in the physical strip
{\mathbb{R}}+i(0,\pi)
and
S(\zeta)
is bounded in the complement of the union of neighborhoods of these poles in the physical strip.

(S6)

Value at zero.

S(0)=-1
.

(S7)

A bound on the real part.

0\leq\operatorname{Re}S\big(\theta+\frac{2\pi i}{3}\big)

\big({=}\operatorname{Re}S\big(\theta+\frac{\pi i}{3}\big)\big)
.

(S8)

A bound on the modulus.

\big|S\big(\theta+\frac{\pi i}{6}\big)\big|\leq 1
.

(S9)

Regularity.

S
has only finitely many zeros in the physical strip and
there is
\kappa>0
such that
\|S\|_{\kappa}:=\sup\{|S(\zeta)|\colon\zeta\in{\mathbb{R}}+i(-\kappa,\kappa)\}<\infty
.

We showed in
[
6
]
that (S
6
) follows from (S
1
)–(S
5
). It turns out crucial in the consideration of domains of our operators. We also classified all functions which satisfy these conditions
[
6
, Appendix A]
. We show that (S
8
) follows from (S
1
)–(S
5
) in Appendix
A.2
.

(S
7
) and (S
9
) are new requirements. The property (S
7
) had not appeared either in the literature on form factor program or in our previous works
[
6
,
7
]
. Yet, it is necessary in order to apply our methods, for some properties of the sum bound state operators. The last assumption (S
9
) will be necessary also in the proof of modular nuclearity (cf.
[
19
]
), and we use it here already for wedge-observables, in Lemma
E.5
. With the finiteness of zeros in the physical strip, the finiteness of
\|S\|_{\kappa}
is equivalent to the absence of the singular part in
S
(cf.
[
23
, Appendix A]
). From the condition (S
6
), there must be at least one zero on the interval
i(0,\frac{\pi}{3})
, hence
\kappa<\frac{\pi}{3}
, as we saw in
[
6
]
.

We check in Appendix
A
that there are concrete examples of
S
which satisfy all these requirements. In the following, we mark explicitly the points where (S
7
) is needed.

2.3
The wedge-local observables

Some materials here are parallel to those of
[
18
]
. Assume that our model has only a single species of particle with mass
m>0
. We fix a two-particle scattering function
S
, which satisfies the properties explained in Section
2.2
.

Our Hilbert space is as follows. The one-particle space
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
is simply
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
with the Lebesgue measure. We consider
n
-particle space
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}^{\otimes n}
. An element of
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}^{\otimes n}
can be considered as a function in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. On each of these spaces, there is the representation of the symmetric group
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
:

\displaystyle(D_{n}(\tau_{k})\Psi_{n})(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{n}):=S(\theta_{k+1}-\theta_{k})\Psi_{n}(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{k+1},\theta_{k},\dots,\theta_{n}),

where
\tau_{k}
is the transposition of
k
and
k+1
. This indeed extends to a unitary representation of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. We say that
\Psi_{n}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{1}^{\otimes n}
is
S
-symmetric
if
D_{n}(\sigma)\Psi_{n}=\Psi_{n}
for
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. This notion is equivalent to that for each
k

\displaystyle\Psi_{n}(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{n})=S(\theta_{k+1}-\theta_{k})\Psi_{n}(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{k+1},\theta_{k},\dots,\theta_{n}).

We denote the orthogonal projection onto the
S
-symmetric functions by
P_{n}
and the space of
S
-symmetric functions by
{\mathcal{H}}_{n}=P_{n}{\mathcal{H}}_{1}^{\otimes n}
. The physical Fock space is the direct sum

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}=\bigoplus_{n}{\mathcal{H}}_{n},

where
{\mathcal{H}}_{0}={\mathbb{C}}
. Let us also introduce an auxiliary Hilbert space, the unsymmetrized Fock space, which is
{\mathcal{H}}^{\Sigma}=\bigoplus_{n}{\mathcal{H}}_{1}^{\otimes n}
. By putting
P_{S}=\bigoplus_{n}P_{n}
, we have
{\mathcal{H}}=P_{S}{\mathcal{H}}^{\Sigma}
.

For
\psi\in{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
, the unsymmetrized creation and annihilation operators are defined on the algebraic direct sum of
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}^{\otimes n}
by

\displaystyle\big(a^{\dagger}(\psi)\Psi\big)_{n}(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{n})=\sqrt{n}\psi(\theta_{1})\Psi_{n-1}(\theta_{2},\dots,\theta_{n}),\qquad a(\psi)=\big(a^{\dagger}(\psi)\big)^{*}.

By our convention
a^{\dagger}(\psi)
is linear in
\psi
, while
a(\psi)
is antilinear. The
S
-symmetrized creation and annihilation operators are
z^{\dagger}(\psi)=P_{S}a^{\dagger}(\psi)P_{S}
,
z(\psi)=P_{S}a(\psi)P_{S}
.

The one-particle CPT operator
J_{1}
is given by
(J_{1}\psi)(\theta)=\overline{\psi(\theta)}
, and the
n
-particle component
J_{n}
is

\displaystyle(J_{n}\Psi_{n})(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{n})=\overline{\Psi_{n}(\theta_{n},\dots,\theta_{1})}.

It is easy to see that
J_{n}
commutes with
P_{n}
, hence its preserves the subspace
{\mathcal{H}}_{n}
. The full CPT operator is
J=\bigoplus_{n}J_{n}
, where
J_{0}
is the complex conjugation on
{\mathcal{H}}_{0}={\mathbb{C}}
.

For a test function
f
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, we define

\displaystyle f^{\pm}(\theta)=\int dx\,f(x)e^{\pm ip(\theta)\cdot x},\qquad p(\theta)=m\left(\begin{matrix}\cosh\theta\\
\sinh\theta\end{matrix}\right).

As
f
is compactly supported, its Fourier transform decays fast, and so do
f^{\pm}
, therefore, they belong to
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
. On the level of test functions, we define the CPT transformation by
f_{j}(x)=\overline{f(-x)}
.

If
S
had no pole, then Lechner took the field
\phi(f)=z^{\dagger}(f^{+})+z(J_{1}f^{-})
and the reflected field
\phi^{\prime}(g)=z^{\prime\dagger}(g^{+})+z^{\prime}(J_{1}g^{+})
, where
z^{\prime\dagger}(\psi)=Jz^{\dagger}(J_{1}\psi)J
and
z^{\prime}(\psi)=Jz(J_{1}\psi)J
and proved that they were wedge-local
[
18
]
: namely, if
\operatorname{supp}f\subset W_{\mathrm{L}}
and
\operatorname{supp}g\subset W_{\mathrm{R}}
, then
\phi(f)
and
\phi^{\prime}(g)
strongly commute.

Extension to Hardy space vectors

Actually, it is possible and also natural to consider
\phi
,
z^{\dagger}
and
z
as a function of
f^{+}
, and in this sense we can extend them to any
\xi\in{H^{2}({\mathbb{S}}_{0,\pi})}\cap{H^{\infty}({\mathbb{S}}_{0,\pi})}
. For
a\leq 0\leq b
, recall the definition of the Hardy space:

\displaystyle H^{2}({\mathbb{S}}_{a,b})=\left\{\xi\in{\mathcal{H}}_{1}\colon\xi(\theta)\mbox{ has an }L^{2}\mbox{-bounded analytic continuation to }{\mathbb{S}}_{a,b}\right\}.

In other words, a function
\xi\in H^{2}({\mathbb{S}}_{a,b})
is an analytic function on
{\mathbb{S}}_{a,b}
whose
L^{2}
-norms on horizontal lines
\xi(\,\cdot+i\lambda)
are bounded uniformly for
\lambda\in(a,b)
. It is well-known that for
\lambda\to a,b
the limits
\xi(\,\cdot+i\lambda)
exist in the
L^{2}
-sense
[
32
, Corollary III.2.10]
. As
0\in[a,b]
, we can idenfity
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}=L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
as a subspace of
H^{2}({\mathbb{S}}_{a,b})
by considering the value
\xi(\theta)
,
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}
. We denote the
L^{2}
-norm by
\|\xi\|
, while the Hardy space norm is
\|\xi\|_{2}:=\sup\limits_{\lambda\in(a,b)}\|\xi(\,\cdot+i\lambda)\|
.

Similarly,
H^{\infty}({\mathbb{S}}_{a,b})
is the space of bounded analytic functions on
{\mathbb{S}}_{a,b}
and we define the norm
\|\xi\|_{\infty}:=\sup\limits_{\zeta\in{\mathbb{S}}_{a,b}}|\xi(\zeta)|
.

The operators
z^{\dagger}(\xi)
and
z(\xi)
are defined for vectors
\xi\in{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
, hence with the understanding that
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}=L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})\supset{H^{2}({\mathbb{S}}_{0,\pi})}
by taking the boundary value at
\operatorname{Im}\zeta=0
,
z^{\dagger}
and
z
are also defined on
{H^{2}({\mathbb{S}}_{0,\pi})}
. The reality condition
f(a)=\overline{f(a)}
on a test function
f
translates to an element
\xi\in{H^{2}({\mathbb{S}}_{0,\pi})}
as
\xi(\zeta+i\pi)=\overline{\xi(\zeta)}
. It is straightforward to see that the proof of wedge-locality for
S
without pole
[
18
]
works for such
\xi
’s: the Cauchy theorem works for
H^{2}
-functions (see, e.g.,
[
6
, Lemma B.2]
).

As we are interested in cases where
S
has poles, we introduce the bound state operator
\chi(\xi)
(cf. in
[
6
]
we considered it as associated with
f^{+}
, instead of
\xi
). We associate an operator
\chi(\xi)
to
\xi\in{H^{2}({\mathbb{S}}_{0,\pi})}\cap{H^{\infty}({\mathbb{S}}_{0,\pi})}
which satisfies
\overline{\xi(\theta)}=\xi(\theta+\pi i)
by

\displaystyle(\chi_{1}(\xi)\Psi)(\theta):=\sqrt{2\pi|R|}\xi\left(\theta+\frac{\pi i}{3}\right)\Psi\left(\theta-\frac{\pi i}{3}\right),

\displaystyle\chi_{n}(\xi):=nP_{n}\left(\chi_{1}(\xi)\otimes{\mathbbm{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\mathbbm{1}}\right)P_{n},\qquad\chi(\xi):=\bigoplus_{n}\chi_{n}(\xi),

where
\chi_{0}(\xi)=0
and
R=\underset{\zeta=\frac{2\pi i}{3}}{\operatorname{Res}}S(\zeta)
. Of course,
\chi_{1}(\xi)
cannot be defined on arbitrary vectors, as it involves an analytic continuation of
\Psi
. In Section
2.4
, we will specify a domain of self-adjointness of
\chi_{1}(\xi)

and define
\chi_{n}(\xi)
accordingly. It follows that
\chi(\xi)
is symmetric as in
[
6
, Proposition 3.1]
. For
\eta\in{H^{2}({\mathbb{S}}_{-\pi,0})}\cap{H^{\infty}({\mathbb{S}}_{-\pi,0})}
,
\overline{\eta(\theta)}=\eta(\theta-\pi i)
we introduce also

\displaystyle(\chi^{\prime}_{1}(\eta)\Psi)(\theta):=\sqrt{2\pi|R|}\eta\left(\theta-\frac{\pi i}{3}\right)\Psi\left(\theta+\frac{\pi i}{3}\right),

\displaystyle\chi_{n}(\eta):=nP_{n}\left({\mathbbm{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\mathbbm{1}}\otimes\chi^{\prime}_{1}(\eta)\right)P_{n},\qquad\chi^{\prime}(\eta):=\bigoplus_{n}\chi^{\prime}_{n}(\eta).

It holds that
\chi^{\prime}_{1}(\eta)=J_{1}\chi_{1}(J_{1}\eta)J_{1}
,
\chi^{\prime}_{n}(\eta)=J_{n}\chi_{n}(J_{1}\eta)J_{n}
and
\chi^{\prime}(\eta)=J\chi(J_{1}\eta)J
.

Our candidate operators for wedge-observables are
{\widetilde{\phi}}(\xi):=\phi(\xi)+\chi(\xi)
and
{\widetilde{\phi}}^{\prime}(\eta):=\phi^{\prime}(\eta)+\chi^{\prime}(\eta)
. We will show that they weakly commute on the intersection of their domains and propose a way to prove their strong commutativity.

2.4
The bound state operator

We studied the one-particle component
\chi_{1}(\xi)
in detail in
[
35
]
: actually, we studied the domain
{H^{2}({\mathbb{S}}_{-\pi,0})}
rather than
{H^{2}\big({\mathbb{S}}_{-\frac{\pi}{3},0}\big)}

\chi_{1}(\xi)
, when defined on
{H^{2}\big({\mathbb{S}}_{-\frac{\pi}{3},0}\big)}
, does not always have a nice self-adjoint extension and even if it does, its domain varies as

3
Towards strong commutativity

In this Section we show that, in order to construct the Borchers triple associated with
S
with poles, it is enough to prove that
\chi(\xi)+\chi^{\prime}(\eta)
is essentially self-adjoint.

3.1
A general criterion

This Section is technically independent from QFT.

For unbounded operators, strong commutativity is a hard problem. It may fail even for a two self-adjoint operators which commute on a common invariant core
[
25
, Section 10]
,
[
26
, Section VIII.5, Example 1]
. We also exhibit more examples of weakly-commuting but not strongly-commuting operators in Appendix
B
. A sufficient condition which is used very widely in the context of QFT (e.g.,
[
2
,
10
,
15
]
) is the Driessler–Fröhlich theorem
[
11
]
. The theorem says roughly that, if there is a positive self-adjoint operator
T
(“Hamiltonian”) which can nicely bound the weakly commuting symmetric operators
A
,
B
, and their commutators with
T
, then
A
and
B
are actually self-adjoint and commute strongly (see Appendix
C
for the precise statements).

The trouble in our situation is that the physical Hamiltonian is not strong enough to estimate our candidates which contain the bound state operator
\chi(\xi)
: the Hamiltonian is the multiplication operator, while
\chi(\xi)
contains an analytic continuation. We have no idea for any other operator which dominates
\chi(\xi)
“nicely”, and anyway there cannot be a single operator which bounds all
\chi(\xi)
’s with different
\xi
’s, as
\chi_{1}(\xi)
’s have already different domains of self-adjointness. In such a case, the question of strong commutativity is considerably difficult (cf.
[
10
]
).

Yet, the Driessler–Fröhlich theorem can be used to reduce the problem of strong commutativity to self-adjointness of a certain positive operator.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
A
,
B
and
T_{0}
be symmetric operators. Assume that
T:=A+B+T_{0}\geq{\mathbbm{1}}
and is essentially self-adjoint on its natural domain
\operatorname{Dom}(T):=\operatorname{Dom}(A)\cap\operatorname{Dom}(B)\cap\operatorname{Dom}(T_{0})
, and there are
{\mathscr{D}}\subset\operatorname{Dom}(T)
which is a core of
T
and
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}>0
such that

(1)

\|A\psi\|\leq c_{1}\|T\psi\|
and
\|B\psi\|\leq c_{1}\|T\psi\|
for all
\psi\in{\mathscr{D}}
,

(2)

for all
\varphi,\psi\in{\mathscr{D}}
,

•

|\langle T_{0}\psi,A\varphi\rangle-\langle A\psi,T_{0}\varphi\rangle|\leq c_{2}\big\|T^{\frac{1}{2}}\psi\big\|\cdot\big\|T^{\frac{1}{2}}\varphi\big\|
,

•

|\langle T_{0}\psi,B\varphi\rangle-\langle B\psi,T_{0}\varphi\rangle|\leq c_{2}\big\|T^{\frac{1}{2}}\psi\big\|\cdot\big\|T^{\frac{1}{2}}\varphi\big\|
,

(3)

for all
\varphi,\psi\in{\mathscr{D}}
,

•

|\langle T_{0}\psi,A\varphi\rangle-\langle A\psi,T_{0}\varphi\rangle|\leq c_{3}\|\psi\|\cdot\|T\varphi\|
,

•

|\langle T_{0}\psi,B\varphi\rangle-\langle B\psi,T_{0}\varphi\rangle|\leq c_{3}\|\psi\|\cdot\|T\varphi\|
,

(4)

\langle A\psi,B\varphi\rangle=\langle B\psi,A\varphi\rangle
for all

\psi,\varphi\in{\mathscr{D}}
.

Then
A
and
B
are essentially self-adjoint on any core of
T
and they strongly commute.

Proof.

As we have
\langle A\psi,B\varphi\rangle=\langle B\psi,A\varphi\rangle
by assumption and
T=A+B+T_{0}
by definition, it follows that for all
\varphi,\psi\in{\mathscr{D}}

•

|\langle T\psi,A\varphi\rangle-\langle A\psi,T\varphi\rangle|\leq c_{2}\big\|T^{\frac{1}{2}}\psi\big\|\cdot\big\|T^{\frac{1}{2}}\varphi\big\|
,

•

|\langle T\psi,B\varphi\rangle-\langle B\psi,T\varphi\rangle|\leq c_{2}\big\|T^{\frac{1}{2}}\psi\big\|\cdot\big\|T^{\frac{1}{2}}\varphi\big\|
,

•

|\langle T\psi,A\varphi\rangle-\langle A\psi,T\varphi\rangle|\leq c_{3}\|\psi\|\cdot\|T\varphi\|
,

•

|\langle T\psi,B\varphi\rangle-\langle B\psi,T\varphi\rangle|\leq c_{3}\|\psi\|\cdot\|T\varphi\|
.

Hence we can apply Theorem
C.1
, by taking
\overline{T}
as the positive self-adjoint operator.
∎

The point of this proposition is that, instead of using a single “Hamiltonian” for all cases, we take the operator
T=A+B+T_{0}
which depends on
A
and
B
. Of course, as weak commutativity does not imply strong commutativity, the essential self-adjointness hypothesis of
T
and the estimate of
A
,
B
by
T
are crucial.

We have the following case in mind:
A={\widetilde{\phi}}(\xi)
,
B={\widetilde{\phi}}^{\prime}(\eta)
,
T_{0}=c(N+{\mathbbm{1}})
, where

is the number operator on the
S
-symmetric Fock space and

. Furthermore, we will see in Section

that

is a Kato–Rellich perturbation of the operator
\chi(\xi)+\chi^{\prime}(\eta)+c(N+{\mathbbm{1}})
. This last operator preserves each
n

This self-adjointness is far from obvious. By a slight modification, the self-adjointness of a similar operator easily fails (see Appendix

Observations on closure

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
X
,
Y
be closed operators and assume that there is
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that
c\|\Psi\|^{2}\leq\operatorname{Re}\langle X\Psi,Y\Psi\rangle
for
\Psi\in\operatorname{Dom}(X)\cap\operatorname{Dom}(Y)
. Then
X+Y
is closed.

Proof.

Let
\Psi_{n}\in\operatorname{Dom}(X)\cap\operatorname{Dom}(Y)
such that
\Psi_{n}\to\Psi
and
(X+Y)\Psi_{n}\to\Phi
. Then

\displaystyle\|(X+Y)(\Psi_{m}-\Psi_{n})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\|X(\Psi_{m}-\Psi_{n})\|^{2}+\|Y(\Psi_{m}-\Psi_{n})\|^{2}+2\operatorname{Re}\langle X(\Psi_{m}-\Psi_{n}),Y(\Psi_{m}-\Psi_{n})\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad{}\geq\|X(\Psi_{m}-\Psi_{n})\|^{2}+\|Y(\Psi_{m}-\Psi_{n})\|^{2}+2c\|\Psi_{m}-\Psi_{n}\|^{2},

from which it follows that
\{X\Psi_{m}\}
and
\{Y\Psi_{m}\}
are both convergent, and therefore,
\Psi\in\operatorname{Dom}(X)\cap\operatorname{Dom}(Y)
, namely,
X+Y
is closed.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
X
,
Y
be closable operators and assume that there is
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that
c\|\Psi\|^{2}\leq\operatorname{Re}\langle X\Psi,Y\Psi\rangle
for
\Psi\in\operatorname{Dom}(X)\cap\operatorname{Dom}(Y)
. Then
X+Y
is closable and
\overline{X+Y}\subset\overline{X}+\overline{Y}
.

Proof.

For
\Psi\in\operatorname{Dom}(X)\cap\operatorname{Dom}(Y)
, we have
\|(X+Y)\Psi\|^{2}=\|X\Psi\|^{2}+\|Y\Psi\|^{2}+2\operatorname{Re}\langle X\Psi,Y\Psi\rangle
where the last term is bounded below by
c\|\Psi\|^{2}
by assumption. If
\Psi_{n}\to 0
and
(X+Y)\Psi_{n}\to\Phi
, then
\|(X+Y)(\Psi_{n}-\Psi_{m})\|\to 0
and

\displaystyle\lim_{m,n}\,\inf\left(2\operatorname{Re}\langle X(\Psi_{n}-\Psi_{m}),Y(\Psi_{n}-\Psi_{m})\rangle\right)\geq 0.

From this it follows that
\|X(\Psi_{n}-\Psi_{m})\|\to 0
and
\|Y(\Psi_{n}-\Psi_{m})\|\to 0
, namely
X\Psi_{n}
and
Y\Psi_{n}
are separately convergent. As
X
and
Y
are closable, we obtain
X\Psi_{n}\to 0
and
Y\Psi_{n}\to 0
and
\Phi=0
, namely
X+Y
is closable.

To show
\overline{X+Y}\subset\overline{X}+\overline{Y}
, we only have to repeat the argument with
\Psi_{n}\to\Psi
to obtain
\Psi\in\operatorname{Dom}(\overline{X})\cap\operatorname{Dom}(\overline{Y})=\operatorname{Dom}(\overline{X}+\overline{Y})
.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
X
,
Y
be closed operators and assume that there is
c>0
such that
0\leq c^{2}\|\Psi\|^{2}+2c(\langle\Psi,X\Psi\rangle+\langle\Psi,Y\Psi\rangle)+2\operatorname{Re}\langle X\Psi,Y\Psi\rangle
for
\Psi\in\operatorname{Dom}(X)\cap\operatorname{Dom}(Y)
.
Then
X+Y
is closed.

Proof.

Let
\Psi_{n}\in\operatorname{Dom}(X)\cap\operatorname{Dom}(Y)
such that
\Psi_{n}\to\Psi
and
(X+Y+c)\Psi_{n}\to\Phi
. Hence we have

\displaystyle\|(X+Y+c)(\Psi_{m}-\Psi_{n})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\|X(\Psi_{m}-\Psi_{n})\|^{2}+\|Y(\Psi_{m}-\Psi_{n})\|^{2}+2\operatorname{Re}\langle X(\Psi_{m}-\Psi_{n}),Y(\Psi_{m}-\Psi_{n})\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad\quad{}+2c(\langle(\Psi_{m}-\Psi_{n}),X(\Psi_{m}-\Psi_{n})\rangle+\langle(\Psi_{m}-\Psi_{n}),Y(\Psi_{m}-\Psi_{n})\rangle)+c^{2}\|(\Psi_{m}-\Psi_{n})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad{}\geq\|X(\Psi_{m}-\Psi_{n})\|^{2}+\|Y(\Psi_{m}-\Psi_{n})\|^{2},

from which it follows that
\{X\Psi_{m}\}
and
\{Y\Psi_{m}\}
are both convergent, and therefore,
\Psi\in\operatorname{Dom}(X)\cap\operatorname{Dom}(Y)
, namely,
X+Y+c
is closed. This is equivalent to that
X+Y
is closed.
∎

4.1
One-particle components

Now we show that
\chi_{1}(\xi)+\chi^{\prime}_{1}(\eta)
is self-adjoint on the intersection
\operatorname{Dom}(\chi_{1}(\xi))\cap\operatorname{Dom}(\chi^{\prime}_{1}(\eta))
.
A key observation is that
\chi_{1}(\xi)
can be written in the form
X^{*}X
where
X={\Delta_{1}^{\frac{1}{12}}}M_{\xi_{0}(\cdot\,+\frac{2\pi i}{3})}
. It is clear that
X
is densely defined, as
\xi_{0}\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{S}}_{\frac{\pi}{3},\frac{2\pi}{3}})
, and closed as
M_{\xi_{0}(\cdot\,+\frac{2\pi i}{3})}
is bounded. Furthermore, it holds that
X^{*}=M_{\xi_{0}(\cdot\,+\frac{2\pi i}{3})}^{*}{\Delta_{1}^{\frac{1}{12}}}
without closure, since
M_{\xi_{0}(\cdot\,+\frac{2\pi i}{3})}^{*}
is unitary. Similarly, one can write
\chi^{\prime}_{1}(g)=Y^{*}Y
, where
Y={\Delta_{1}^{-\frac{1}{12}}}M_{\eta_{0}(\cdot\,-\frac{2\pi i}{3})}
and
Y^{*}=M_{\eta_{0}(\cdot\,-\frac{2\pi i}{3})}^{*}{\Delta_{1}^{-\frac{1}{12}}}
.

Some ideas in the following lemma are due to Henning Bostelmann.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
X
,
Y
be closed, densely defined operators such that

•

X\operatorname{Dom}(X)\subset\operatorname{Dom}(Y^{*})
and
Y\operatorname{Dom}(Y)\subset\operatorname{Dom}(X^{*})
,

•

Y^{*}X
and
X^{*}Y
are bounded,

•

X+Y
and
X^{*}+Y^{*}
are densely defined,

•

there is a decomposition of
{\mathcal{H}}={\mathcal{H}}_{X}\oplus{\mathcal{H}}_{Y}
such that

–

any
\Psi\in\operatorname{Dom}({X})
is the sum
\Psi=\Psi_{X}\oplus\Psi_{Y}
,
\Psi_{X}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{X}\cap\operatorname{Dom}({X})
and
\Psi_{Y}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{Y}\cap\operatorname{Dom}({X})
,

–

any
\Phi\in\operatorname{Dom}({Y})
is the sum
\Phi=\Phi_{X}\oplus\Phi_{Y}
,
\Phi_{X}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{X}\cap\operatorname{Dom}({Y})
and
\Phi_{Y}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{Y}\cap\operatorname{Dom}({Y})
,

–

{X}
is bounded on
{\mathcal{H}}_{X}
and
{Y}
is bounded on
{\mathcal{H}}_{Y}
.

Then the sum
X^{*}X+Y^{*}Y
is self-adjoint on
\operatorname{Dom}(X^{*}X)\cap\operatorname{Dom}(Y^{*}Y)
.

Proof.

By definition,
\operatorname{Dom}(X+Y)=\operatorname{Dom}(X)\cap\operatorname{Dom}(Y)
. Under the assumption,
X+Y
is closed. Indeed, we have
\langle X\Psi,Y\Psi\rangle=\langle\Psi,X^{*}Y\Psi\rangle
which is bounded by
\|X^{*}Y\|\cdot\|\Psi\|^{2}
and we can apply Lemma
4.1
.

Next, note that
\operatorname{Dom}({X}+{Y})=\operatorname{Dom}({X})\cap\operatorname{Dom}({Y})
is a core of
{X}
. Indeed, any
\Psi\in\operatorname{Dom}({X})
can be decomposed as
\Psi_{X}\oplus\Psi_{Y}
by assumption. On the other hand,
\operatorname{Dom}({Y})
is dense and admits the decomposition
\operatorname{Dom}({Y})=(\operatorname{Dom}({Y})\cap{\mathcal{H}}_{X})\oplus(\operatorname{Dom}({Y})\cap{\mathcal{H}}_{Y})
. As
\operatorname{Dom}({X}+{Y})
is dense by assumption,
\operatorname{Dom}({X}+{Y})\cap{\mathcal{H}}_{X}
is dense in
{\mathcal{H}}_{X}
. But
{X}
is bounded on
{\mathcal{H}}_{X}
, thus
\operatorname{Dom}({X})
includes
{\mathcal{H}}_{X}
. Namely,
\operatorname{Dom}({X})={\mathcal{H}}_{X}\oplus(\operatorname{Dom}({X})\cap{\mathcal{H}}_{Y})
. Similarly, we have
\operatorname{Dom}({Y})=(\operatorname{Dom}({Y})\cap{\mathcal{H}}_{X})\oplus{\mathcal{H}}_{Y}
. Therefore,
\operatorname{Dom}({X}+{Y})=(\operatorname{Dom}({Y})\cap{\mathcal{H}}_{X})\oplus(\operatorname{Dom}({X})\cap{\mathcal{H}}_{Y})
. We saw that
\operatorname{Dom}({X}+{Y})\cap{\mathcal{H}}_{X}=\operatorname{Dom}({Y})\cap{\mathcal{H}}_{X}
is dense in
{\mathcal{H}}_{X}
and
{X}
is bounded here, hence, the closure of
{X}
restricted on
\operatorname{Dom}({X}+{Y})
is
{\mathcal{H}}_{X}\oplus\operatorname{Dom}({X})\cap{\mathcal{H}}_{Y}=\operatorname{Dom}({X})
. Similarly, it holds that
\operatorname{Dom}({X}+{Y})
is a core of
{Y}
.

Next we see that
\operatorname{Dom}((X+Y)^{*})=\operatorname{Dom}(X^{*})\cap\operatorname{Dom}(Y^{*})
. For each vector
\Psi\in\operatorname{Dom}({X}+{Y})=\operatorname{Dom}({X})\cap\operatorname{Dom}({Y})
, we take the decomposition
\Psi=\Psi_{X}\oplus\Psi_{Y}
which is given by the assumption. By the definition of the adjoint operator, a vector
\Phi
is in
\operatorname{Dom}(({X}+{Y})^{*})
if and only if the map
\Psi\mapsto\langle\Phi,({X}+{Y})\Psi\rangle
is continuous for
\Psi\in\operatorname{Dom}({X}+{Y})
(namely, there is
c
such that
|\langle\Phi,({X}+{Y})\Psi\rangle|\leq c\|\Psi\|
). Then we have the following chain of equivalences:

\displaystyle\Phi\in\operatorname{Dom}(({X}+{Y})^{*})

\displaystyle\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\ \Psi\mapsto\langle\Phi,({X}+{Y})\Psi\rangle\textrm{ is continuous for }\Psi\in\operatorname{Dom}({X}+{Y})

\displaystyle\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\ \Psi_{X}\mapsto\langle\Phi,({X}+{Y})\Psi_{X}\rangle\textrm{ and }\Psi_{Y}\mapsto\langle\Phi,({X}+{Y})\Psi_{Y}\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad{}\hphantom{\Longleftrightarrow}{}\ \ \textrm{are continuous for }\Psi_{X}\in\operatorname{Dom}({X}+{Y})\cap{\mathcal{H}}_{X},\Psi_{Y}\in\operatorname{Dom}({X}+{Y})\cap{\mathcal{H}}_{Y}.

\displaystyle\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\ \Psi_{X}\mapsto\langle\Phi,{Y}\Psi_{X}\rangle\textrm{ and }\Psi_{Y}\mapsto\langle\Phi,{X}\Psi_{Y}\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad{}\hphantom{\Longleftrightarrow}{}\ \ \textrm{are continuous for }\Psi_{X}\in\operatorname{Dom}({X}+{Y})\cap{\mathcal{H}}_{X},\Psi_{Y}\in\operatorname{Dom}({X}+{Y})\cap{\mathcal{H}}_{Y}.

\displaystyle\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\ \Psi_{X}\oplus\Psi_{Y}\mapsto\langle\Phi,{Y}(\Psi_{X}\oplus\Psi_{Y})\rangle\textrm{ and }\Psi_{X}\oplus\Psi_{Y}\mapsto\langle\Phi,{X}(\Psi_{X}\oplus\Psi_{Y})\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad{}\hphantom{\Longleftrightarrow}{}\ \ \textrm{are continuous for }\Psi=\Psi_{X}\oplus\Psi_{Y}\in\operatorname{Dom}({X}+{Y}).

We saw that
\operatorname{Dom}({X}+{Y})
is a core both for
{X}
and
{Y}
, thus, the continuity of
\Phi\mapsto\langle\Phi,{X}\Psi\rangle
for
\Psi\in\operatorname{Dom}({X}+{Y})
extends to
\Psi\in\operatorname{Dom}({X})
, which implies that
\Phi\in\operatorname{Dom}(X^{*})
. Analogously, we obtain
\Phi\in\operatorname{Dom}(Y^{*})
. This implies that
({X}+{Y})^{*}=X^{*}+Y^{*}
.

It is well-known
[
26
, Theorem X.25]
that, because
X+Y
is closed, on the domain
\operatorname{Dom}((X+Y)^{*}(X+Y))=\{\Psi\in\operatorname{Dom}(X+Y):(X+Y)\Psi\in\operatorname{Dom}((X+Y)^{*}))\}
the operator
(X+Y)^{*}(X+Y)
is self-adjoint. From the above observations, this domain is actually

\displaystyle\operatorname{Dom}((X+Y)^{*}(X+Y))=\{\Psi\in\operatorname{Dom}(X+Y)\colon(X+Y)\Psi\in\operatorname{Dom}((X+Y)^{*}))\}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\operatorname{Dom}((X+Y)^*(X+Y))}{}=\{\Psi\in\operatorname{Dom}(X+Y)\colon(X+Y)\Psi\in\operatorname{Dom}(X^{*})\cap\operatorname{Dom}(Y^{*})\}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\operatorname{Dom}((X+Y)^*(X+Y))}{}=\{\Psi\in\operatorname{Dom}(X+Y)\colon X\Psi\in\operatorname{Dom}(X^{*}),Y\Psi\in\operatorname{Dom}(Y^{*})\},

where in the last line we used the assumptions that
X\operatorname{Dom}(X)\subset\operatorname{Dom}(Y^{*})
and
Y\operatorname{Dom}(Y)\subset\operatorname{Dom}(X^{*})
.

Now we have
(X+Y)^{*}(X+Y)=X^{*}X+X^{*}Y+Y^{*}X+Y^{*}Y
and this is different from
X^{*}X+Y^{*}Y
only by a bounded operator.
∎

Proposition 4.5
.

The sum of one-particle components
\chi_{1}(\xi)+\chi_{1}^{\prime}(\eta)
is self-adjoint on the domain
\operatorname{Dom}(\chi_{1}(\xi))\cap\operatorname{Dom}(\chi_{1}^{\prime}(\eta))
.

Proof.

This is a straightforward consequence of this Lemma
4.4
.

Indeed, we only check the existence of the decomposition
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}={\mathcal{H}}_{X}\oplus{\mathcal{H}}_{Y}
. We consider
\log\Delta
and its spectral decomposition: we take
{\mathcal{H}}_{X}
as the spectral subspace of
\log\Delta
corresponding to
(-\infty,0]
, while
{\mathcal{H}}_{Y}
corresponds to
(0,\infty)
. Now the required properties are shown as follows.

Let us denote the spectral projections onto these subspaces by
P_{X}
and
P_{Y}
. Let us take an element
\Psi\in\operatorname{Dom}(X)=\operatorname{Dom}\big({\Delta^{\frac{1}{12}}}M_{\xi_{0}(\cdot\,+\frac{2\pi i}{3})}\big)
. This means, by definition,
\xi_{0}(\theta+\frac{2\pi i}{3})\Psi(\theta)
is in
{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\left(-\frac{\pi}{6},0\right)
. In addition,
\Psi(\theta)
alone is
L^{2}
. While it holds that
\Psi=P_{X}\Psi\oplus P_{Y}\Psi
,
P_{X}\Psi
is obviously in the domain of
{\Delta^{\frac{1}{12}}}
, hence also in the domain of
X={\Delta^{\frac{1}{12}}}M_{\xi_{0}(\cdot\,+\frac{2\pi i}{3})}
. This means that
P_{Y}\Psi=\Psi-P_{X}\Psi
is also in the domain of
X
. The restriction of
X
on
{\mathcal{H}}_{X}
is bounded, since there it holds that
X\Psi_{X}=M_{\xi_{0}(\cdot\,+\frac{\pi i}{2})}{\Delta^{\frac{1}{12}}}
, where
\xi_{0}(\theta+\frac{\pi i}{2})
is bounded.

Similarly, the decomposition
{\mathcal{H}}_{X}\oplus{\mathcal{H}}_{Y}
is compatible with
\operatorname{Dom}(Y)
and
Y
is bounded on
{\mathcal{H}}_{Y}
.
∎

4.2

In this Section, under (S
7

4.2.1

\displaystyle\hphantom{\chi_2(\xi)}{}=(M_{\xi_{0}}^{*}\otimes M_{\xi_{0}}^{*})P_{2}\big({\Delta_{1}^{\frac{1}{6}}}\otimes{\mathbbm{1}}\big)P_{2}(M_{\xi_{0}}\otimes M_{\xi_{0}}),

Proposition 4.7
.

P_{2}\big({\Delta_{1}^{\frac{1}{6}}}\otimes{\mathbbm{1}}\big)P_{2}
is essentially self-adjoint. Under
(S
7
)
, it is even self-adjoint.

Proof.

It is well-known that
A^{*}A
is self-adjoint for a closed operator

[

. The operator
\big({\Delta_{1}^{\frac{1}{12}}}\otimes{\mathbbm{1}}\big)P_{2}

. The domain of
\overline{P_{2}\big({\Delta_{1}^{\frac{1}{12}}}\otimes{\mathbbm{1}}\big)}\cdot\big({\Delta_{1}^{\frac{1}{12}}}\otimes{\mathbbm{1}}\big)P_{2}

Proposition 4.9

For simplicity of notation we write

and

\overline{P_{2}(A\otimes{\mathbbm{1}}+{\mathbbm{1}}\otimes B)}\subset\overline{P_{2}(A\otimes{\mathbbm{1}})}+\overline{P_{2}({\mathbbm{1}}\otimes B)}

4.2

c\|\Psi\|^{2}\leq\operatorname{Re}\langle P_{2}(A\otimes{\mathbbm{1}})\Psi,P_{2}({\mathbbm{1}}\otimes B)\Psi\rangle

\displaystyle\langle P_{2}(A\otimes{\mathbbm{1}})\Psi,P_{2}({\mathbbm{1}}\otimes B)\Psi\rangle=\frac{1}{4}\langle({\mathbbm{1}}+D_{2}(\tau_{1}))(A\otimes{\mathbbm{1}})\Psi,({\mathbbm{1}}+D_{2}(\tau_{1}))({\mathbbm{1}}\otimes B)\Psi\rangle

We show that, if

\operatorname{Dom}(A\otimes{\mathbbm{1}})

4.7
(see the third and fourth paragraphs).
Namely, we consider the following formal expression with

integral over the spectral space with respect to
\log A\otimes{\mathbbm{1}}
and
{\mathbbm{1}}\otimes\log A

because

\displaystyle\big(A\otimes{\mathbbm{1}}+({\mathbbm{1}}\otimes A)M_{S(\cdot\,+\frac{\pi i}{6})}F\big)(A\otimes{\mathbbm{1}}+{\mathbbm{1}}\otimes B)\Psi

has the meaning as an

\displaystyle\big\|(A\otimes{\mathbbm{1}})\tilde{\Psi}\big\|^{2}+\big\|({\mathbbm{1}}\otimes A)M_{S(\cdot\,+\frac{\pi i}{6})}F\tilde{\Psi}\big\|^{2}+2\operatorname{Re}\big\langle(A\otimes{\mathbbm{1}})\tilde{\Psi},({\mathbbm{1}}\otimes A)M_{S(\cdot\,+\frac{\pi i}{6})}F\tilde{\Psi}\big\rangle,

where each of these terms can be understood as an integral over the above-mentioned spectral space. If the first term is finite, it means that

is in the domain of

On the spectral space of
\log A

as the multiplication operator by

and

commutes with

and

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\big\langle Q_{N}({\mathbbm{1}}\otimes AB)(A\otimes{\mathbbm{1}})\Psi,M_{S(\cdot\,+\frac{\pi i}{6})}F(A\otimes{\mathbbm{1}}+{\mathbbm{1}}\otimes B)\Psi\big\rangle,

Lemma 4.11
.

Let

and

be symmetric operators on
\operatorname{Dom}(X)
and
\operatorname{Dom}(Y)
, respectively. Assume that

is self-adjoint on
\operatorname{Dom}(X+Y)=\operatorname{Dom}(X)\cap\operatorname{Dom}(Y)
and it holds that
\operatorname{Re}\langle X\Psi,Y\Psi\rangle\geq 0
for
\Psi\in\operatorname{Dom}(X+Y)
. Then

and

are essentially self-adjoint on

.

Proof.

Compute:

\displaystyle\|(X+Y)\Psi\|^{2}=\|X\Psi\|^{2}+\|Y\Psi\|^{2}+2\operatorname{Re}\langle X\Psi,Y\Psi\rangle\geq\max\big\{\|X\Psi\|^{2},\|Y\Psi\|^{2}\big\},

now one can use Wüst’s theorem
[

, Theorem X.14]
to see that

and

are essentially self-adjoint on

Appendix B
Weakly but not strongly commuting operators

Let
A
,
B
be self-adjoint operators with domains
\operatorname{Dom}(A)
,
\operatorname{Dom}(B)
with the dense intersection
\operatorname{Dom}(A)\cap\operatorname{Dom}(B)
. It is well-known that, even if
A
and
B
weakly commute, namely it holds that
\langle A\Psi_{1},B\Psi_{2}\rangle=\langle B\Psi_{1},A\Psi_{2}\rangle
for
\Psi_{k}\in\operatorname{Dom}(A)\cap\operatorname{Dom}(B)
, it does not imply that the spectral projections of
A
and
B
commute.

A classical (counter)example is due to Nelson
[
25
, Section 10]
: one takes a Riemann surface with a nontrivial topology and considers it as a real manifold and the
L^{2}
-space on it. If one chooses two different translations, their generators on the
L^{2}
-space commute weakly (actually as operators on a common core), as the derivations in these directions commute. However, the two translations do not commute, as the Riemann surface
has a nontrivial topology.

The example above shows clearly why two operators weakly commute but not strongly: weak commutation is a local property, while strong commutation is global. The question of strong commutativity is not just technical and, if it fails, there is often a good reason for it. See
[
29
]
,
[
30
, Example 5.5, Exercise 6.16 ]
for some other examples. We present here two more families of counterexamples.

B.1
From canonical commutation relation

This example is essentially due to Faddeev and Volkov
[
14
]
. Consider a CCR pair
X=M_{{\rm id}}
, where
{\rm id}(t)=t
is the identity function and
P=-i\frac{d}{dt}
on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
. Note that
(e^{s_{1}X}\xi)(t)=e^{s_{1}t}\xi(t)
,
(e^{s_{2}P}\xi)(t)=\xi(t-is_{2})
on suitable domains. If we take
s_{2}=\frac{2\pi}{s_{1}}
, then
(e^{s_{1}X}e^{s_{2}P}\xi)(t)=(e^{s_{2}P}e^{s_{1}X}\xi)(t)=e^{s_{1}t}\xi(t-\frac{2\pi}{s_{2}})
. It is not difficult to find a common dense domain of
e^{s_{1}X}
and
e^{s_{2}P}
, and they weakly commute. Yet they do not strongly commute. Indeed we know that
\{e^{s_{1}X},e^{s_{2}P}\}^{\prime\prime}={\mathcal{B}}(L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}))
, while strong commutativity would imply
\{e^{s_{1}X},e^{s_{2}P}\}^{\prime\prime}
to be abelian, which is not true.

B.2
From bound state operators

Take two Blaschke products
f_{1}
,
f_{2}
on
{\mathbb{R}}+i(-\pi,0)
with the symmetry condition
f_{j}(\theta-\pi i)=\overline{f_{j}(\theta)}
. They extend meromorphically to
{\mathbb{C}}
and satisfy
f_{j}(\theta-2\pi i)=f_{j}(\theta)
. Form
A_{1}:=M_{\overline{f_{1}}}\Delta M_{f_{1}}
,
A_{2}:=M_{\overline{f_{2}}}\Delta M_{f_{2}}
, where
(\Delta\xi)(\theta)=\xi(\theta-2\pi i)
. They are manifestly self-adjoint and, by a similar consideration as
[
35
, Section 5.2]
, one can conclude that their domains are determined by the zeros and poles of
f_{j}
. Especially one can see that the domains
\operatorname{Dom}(A_{1}A_{2})
and
\operatorname{Dom}(A_{2}A_{1})
are dense: indeed, there are analytic functions with specified zeros at the poles of
f_{1}
,
f_{2}
in
{\mathbb{R}}+i(-4\pi,0)
. On such a function
\xi
it is clear that
A_{1}A_{2}\xi=A_{2}A_{1}\xi
, because on this domain it holds that
M_{f_{j}}\Delta\xi=\Delta M_{f_{j}}\xi
, as
f_{j}
are
2\pi i
-periodic. Yet,
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
do not strongly commute. Indeed, the intersection of their domains is not a core for any of them if
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
have different zeros or poles, which also follows from the ideas of
[
35
, Section 3]
.

Appendix C
The Driessler–Fröhlich theorem

First let us recall the commutator theorem of Glimm–Jaffe–Nelson. The theorem roughly says the following: if
T
is a positive self-adjoint operator,
A
is a symmetric operator and if
A
and
[T,A]
can be estimated by
T
, then
A
is essentially self-adjoint. Yet, depending on how to make such estimates, there are certain variations in the hypothesis of the theorem.

In one of such variations, one estimates
A
and
[T,A]
as bilinear forms, defined on
\operatorname{Dom}\big(T^{\frac{1}{2}}\big)
and
\operatorname{Dom}\big(T^{\frac{3}{2}}\big)
(or on their cores), respectively
[
26
, Theorem X.36
′
]
. When
T
is a complicated operator, it is difficult to have control over
T^{\frac{1}{2}}
and
T^{\frac{3}{2}}
.
Another variation requires to estimate
A
as an operator, namely
\|A\psi\|\leq c\|T\psi\|
for
\psi\in\operatorname{Dom}(T)
, or for a core of
T
.
For our situation, this latter version has a better chance to apply (see
[
26
, Theorem X.37]
):

Theorem C.1

(the commutator theorem)
.

Let
T
be a self-adjoint operator with
T\geq{\mathbbm{1}}
. Suppose that
A
is a symmetric operator defined on a core
{\mathscr{D}}
of
T
so that

(1)

\|A\psi\|\leq c_{1}\|T\psi\|
for all
\psi\in{\mathscr{D}}
,

(2)

|\langle T\psi,A\psi\rangle-\langle A\psi,T\psi\rangle|\leq c_{2}\|T^{\frac{1}{2}}\psi\|^{2}
for all
\psi\in{\mathscr{D}}
.

Then,
A
is essentially self-adjoint on any core of
T
.

The Driessler–Fröhlich theorem provides a sufficient condition for two symmetric operators
A
,
B
to strongly commute. As the hypothesis, one should have a positive self-adjoint operator
T
which can estimate
A
,
B
,
[T,A]
,
[T,B]
. Again, there can be two variations: estimates either as bilinear forms or operators. The original proof
[
11
, Theorem 3.1]
is written for bilinear forms. In our application, the operator
T
is complicated and we need a variation.

Furthermore, let us note the following subtle point. The original paper assumes certain estimates of
A
,
B
,
[T,A]
,
[T,B]
,
[T,[T,A]]
,
[T,[T,B]]
“on some form core of
T
”, which does not seem to suffice: a form core of
T
might not be a form core of
T^{\frac{3}{2}}
(see also
[
26
, remark after Theorem X.37]
).

Although the essential idea is the same as the original
[
11
]
, we here present the adapted proof for the sake of clarity. Note that the domain of the weak commutativity is also modified: the original proof requires only the weak commutativity on a core of
T^{2}
, while here we need it on a core of
T
. By the same reason as explained in
[
26
, remark after Theorem X.37]
, the results might fail by a tiny change in assumptions, therefore, one should not underestimate the importance of these careful examinations.

Theorem C.2

(Driessler–Fröhlich)
.

Let
T\geq{\mathbbm{1}}
be a positive self-adjoint operator,
A
and
B
symmetric operator on a core
{\mathscr{D}}
of
T
. Assume that there are positive numbers
c_{1}
,
c_{2}
,
c_{3}
such that

(1)

\|A\psi\|\leq c_{1}\|T\psi\|
and
\|B\psi\|\leq c_{1}\|T\psi\|
for all
\psi\in{\mathscr{D}}
,

(2)

for all
\varphi,\psi\in{\mathscr{D}}
,

•

|\langle T\psi,A\varphi\rangle-\langle A\psi,T\varphi\rangle|\leq c_{2}\big\|T^{\frac{1}{2}}\psi\big\|\cdot\big\|T^{\frac{1}{2}}\varphi\big\|
,

•

|\langle T\psi,B\varphi\rangle-\langle B\psi,T\varphi\rangle|\leq c_{2}\big\|T^{\frac{1}{2}}\psi\big\|\cdot\big\|T^{\frac{1}{2}}\varphi\big\|
,

(3)

for all
\varphi,\psi\in{\mathscr{D}}
,

•

|\langle T\psi,A\varphi\rangle-\langle A\psi,T\varphi\rangle|\leq c_{3}\|\psi\|\cdot\|T\varphi\|
,

•

|\langle T\psi,B\varphi\rangle-\langle B\psi,T\varphi\rangle|\leq c_{3}\|\psi\|\cdot\|T\varphi\|
,

(4)

\langle A\psi,B\varphi\rangle=\langle B\psi,A\varphi\rangle
for all
\psi,\varphi\in{\mathscr{D}}
.

Then
A
,
B
are essentially self-adjoint on any core of
T
and they strongly commute.

Proof.

Note that all the assumptions hold on
\operatorname{Dom}(T)
. Indeed, for any pair
\psi,\varphi\in\operatorname{Dom}(T)
, there are
\{\psi_{n}\},\{\varphi_{n}\}\subset{\mathscr{D}}
such that
\psi_{n}\to\psi
,
T\psi_{n}\to T\psi
, therefore,
T^{\frac{1}{2}}\psi_{n}\to T^{\frac{1}{2}}\psi
and
\varphi_{n}\to\varphi
,
T\varphi_{n}\to T\varphi
, therefore,
T^{\frac{1}{2}}\varphi_{n}\to T^{\frac{1}{2}}\varphi
. From this,
A
and
B
can be naturally extended to
\operatorname{Dom}(T)
and all the statements and the weak commutativity hold there.

Now, the claim that
A
and
B
are essentially self-adjoint of any core of
T
follows from the assumptions (
1
), (
2
) and Theorem
C.1
.

Next, we observe that, from assumption (
3
), the commutators
[T,A]
,
[T,B]
are well-defined on
\operatorname{Dom}(T)
: for a fixed
\varphi\in\operatorname{Dom}(T)
,
\langle T\psi,B\varphi\rangle-\langle B\psi,T\varphi\rangle
is an antilinear form in
\psi
and bounded by
c_{3}\|\psi\|\cdot\|T\varphi\|
. By Riesz’ representation theorem, there is a unique vector
\eta
such that
\langle T\psi,B\varphi\rangle-\langle B\psi,T\varphi\rangle=\langle\psi,\eta\rangle
and
\|\eta\|\leq c_{3}\|T\varphi\|
. We denote the correspondence
\varphi\mapsto\eta
by
[T,B]
. It follows by this definition that
\|[T,B]T^{-1}\|\leq c_{3}
.

Let us show next that
(B+z)T^{-1}{\mathscr{D}}
(which is well-defined since
B
is extended to
\operatorname{Dom}(T)
) is in the domain of
T
and
T(B+z)T^{-1}{\mathscr{D}}
is dense in
{\mathcal{H}}
, where
z
is a complex number such that
|\operatorname{Im}z|>c_{3}
. For
\psi\in\operatorname{Dom}(T)
and
\varphi\in T^{-1}{\mathscr{D}}
, the following is meaningful:

\displaystyle\langle T\psi,(B+z)\varphi\rangle=\langle(B+\bar{z})\psi,T\varphi\rangle+(\langle T\psi,B\varphi\rangle-\langle B\psi,T\varphi\rangle).

As
\varphi\in T^{-1}{\mathscr{D}}
,
T\varphi\in{\mathscr{D}}\subset\operatorname{Dom}(T)\subset\operatorname{Dom}(B)

and we have

\displaystyle\langle(B+\bar{z})\psi,T\varphi\rangle=\langle\psi,(B+z)T\varphi\rangle,

which implies that
|\langle(B+\bar{z})\psi,T\varphi\rangle|\leq\|\psi\|\cdot\|(B+z)T\varphi\|
. On the other hand, by assumption (
3
) we have
|\langle T\psi,B\varphi\rangle-\langle B\psi,T\varphi\rangle|\leq c_{3}\|\psi\|\cdot\|T\varphi\|
. Altogether, we have the following estimate

\displaystyle|\langle T\psi,(B+z)\varphi\rangle|\leq\|\psi\|\cdot(\|(B+z)T\varphi\|+c_{3}\|T\varphi\|)

for any
\psi\in\operatorname{Dom}(T)
, and therefore,
(B+z)\varphi\in\operatorname{Dom}(T^{*})=\operatorname{Dom}(T)
, and

\displaystyle\langle\psi,T(B+z)\varphi\rangle=\langle\psi,(B+z)T\varphi\rangle+\langle T\psi,B\varphi\rangle-\langle B\psi,T\varphi\rangle,

or equivalently by using
[T,B]
defined as in the previous paragraph,

\displaystyle T(B+z)\varphi=((B+z)T+[T,B])\varphi=\big((B+z)+[T,B]T^{-1}\big)T\varphi.

Now, let us assume that
\langle\psi,T(B+z)\varphi\rangle=0
for arbitrary
\varphi\in T^{-1}{\mathscr{D}}
. By the above expression of
T(B+z)\varphi
, this is equivalent to

\displaystyle 0=\langle\psi,((B+z)T+[T,B])\varphi\rangle=\big\langle\psi,\big((B+z)+[T,B]T^{-1}\big)\tilde{\varphi}\big\rangle,

where
\tilde{\varphi}\in{\mathscr{D}}
is arbitrary. As
{\mathscr{D}}
is a core of
T
, it is a core of
B
as well. Since
B
is essentially self-adjoint on
{\mathscr{D}}
and
[T,B]T^{-1}
is bounded, it follows that
(B+\bar{z}+([T,B]T^{-1})^{*})\psi=0
. If
|\operatorname{Im}z|>c_{3}
, this implies that
\psi=0
, namely,
T(B+z)\varphi
form a dense subspace.

From this and the assumption that
T\geq{\mathbbm{1}}
, it follows that
(B+z){\mathscr{D}}
is a core of
T

[
26
, Theorem X.26]
, and therefore, a core of
A
. It follows from this that
(A+z_{1})(B+z_{2}){\mathscr{D}}
is dense in
{\mathcal{H}}
for
z_{1}\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus{\mathbb{R}}
,
|\operatorname{Im}z_{2}|>c_{3}
.

Our goal is to show that the resolvents
R_{A}(z_{1})=(A+z_{1})^{-1}
and
R_{B}(z_{2})
commute (for
z_{1}
,
z_{2}
in an open set, which implies the strong commutativity. Let us take
\psi,\varphi\in{\mathscr{D}}
. We compute

\displaystyle\langle(A+\bar{z}_{1})(B+\bar{z}_{2})\psi,R_{A}(z_{1})R_{B}(z_{2})(A+z_{1})(B+z_{2})\varphi\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\langle(A+\bar{z}_{1})\psi,(B+z_{2})\varphi\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\langle(B+\bar{z}_{2})\psi,(A+z_{1})\varphi\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\langle(A+\bar{z}_{1})(B+\bar{z}_{2})\psi,\;R_{B}(z_{2})R_{A}(z_{1})(A+z_{1})(B+z_{2})\varphi\rangle,

where the second equality follows from the weak commutativity. We saw that the sets
(A+\bar{z}_{1})(B+\bar{z}_{2}){\mathscr{D}}
and
(A+z_{1})(B+z_{2}){\mathscr{D}}
are both dense in
{\mathcal{H}}
, which completes the proof.
∎

|f(\theta)|=1
and
a\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Then
a{\Delta_{1}^{\frac{1}{6}}}+M_{f}^{*}{\Delta_{1}^{\frac{1}{6}}}M_{f}
has nontrivial self-adjoint extensions if the function
a+\overline{f(\bar{\zeta}-\frac{\pi i}{3})}f(\zeta)
has zeros in the strip or has a nontrivial singular inner factor.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle M_{a+\overline{f(\cdot)}f(\cdot\,-\frac{\pi i}{3})}{\Delta_{1}^{\frac{1}{6}}}=a{\Delta_{1}^{\frac{1}{6}}}+M_{f}^{*}M_{f(\cdot\,-\frac{\pi i}{3})}{\Delta_{1}^{\frac{1}{6}}}=a{\Delta_{1}^{\frac{1}{6}}}+M_{f}^{*}{\Delta_{1}^{\frac{1}{6}}}M_{f},

because the domain of the right-hand side is that of
{\Delta_{1}^{\frac{1}{6}}}
. And we know what extensions the left-hand side has
[
35
]
. Namely, if the function
a+\overline{f(\bar{\zeta}-\frac{\pi i}{3})}f(\zeta)
has a Blaschke factor which has zeros in
{\mathbb{R}}+i(-\frac{\pi}{3},0)
, or if it has a singular inner factor, then the operator
M_{a+\overline{f(\cdot)}f(\cdot\,-\frac{\pi i}{3})}{\Delta_{1}^{\frac{1}{6}}}
has nontrivial extensions.
∎

As a corollary, one can show that
{\Delta_{1}^{\frac{1}{6}}}\otimes{\mathbbm{1}}+M_{f}\big({\mathbbm{1}}\otimes{\Delta_{1}^{\frac{1}{6}}}\big)M_{f}^{*}
is
not always
self-adjoint, if

, where
\alpha<-\frac{\pi}{3}
. Indeed, each term commutes strongly with
{\Delta_{1}^{\frac{1}{12}}}\otimes{\Delta_{1}^{-\frac{1}{12}}}
. By considering the sum on the spectral subspace with respect to
{\Delta_{1}^{\frac{1}{12}}}\otimes{\Delta_{1}^{-\frac{1}{12}}}
, where it can be regarded as a constant
a_{0}
, the sum can be reduced to an operator of the form
\frac{1}{a_{0}}{\Delta_{1}^{\frac{1}{12}}}\otimes{\Delta_{1}^{\frac{1}{12}}}+a_{0}M_{f}\big({\Delta_{1}^{\frac{1}{12}}}\otimes{\Delta_{1}^{\frac{1}{12}}}\big)M_{f}^{*}
, which is further reduced to
a{\Delta_{1}^{\frac{1}{6}}}+M_{f}^{*}{\Delta_{1}^{\frac{1}{6}}}M_{f}
. We saw that this is not always self-adjoint, depending on
f
and
a
.

For a Hilbert space
{\mathcal{K}}

L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathcal{K}})
denote the space of
{\mathcal{K}}

L^{2}
-functions, and for an open subset

of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

H^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}+i{\mathcal{U}},{\mathcal{K}})
be the Hardy space of
{\mathcal{K}}
-valued functions
\Psi
which have an analytic continuation in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}+i{\mathcal{U}}
such that
\Psi(\,\cdot+i\boldsymbol{\lambda})
,
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in{\mathcal{U}}

L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
and their
L^{2}
-norms are uniformly bounded with respect to
\lambda

Lemma E.1
.

Let
\Psi(\theta)\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathcal{K}})
and
\psi\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
. Then the integral
\int d\theta\,\overline{\psi(\theta)}\Psi(\theta)
defines a vector in
{\mathcal{K}}
.

Proof.

\Psi(\theta)\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathcal{K}})
means that
\|\Psi(\theta)\|
is an
L^{2}
-function, hence
\|\overline{\psi(\theta)}\Psi(\theta)\|
is in
L^{1}({\mathbb{R}})
.
∎

Lemma E.2
.

Let
\{\Psi_{n}\}
be a sequence in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathcal{K}})
and assume that there is an
\psi\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
which satisfies
\|\Psi_{n}(\theta)\|\leq\psi(\theta)

(
almost everywhere
)
. If
\Psi_{n}
is almost everywhere pointwise convergent to
\Psi
, then
\Psi
is again
L^{2}
and
\Psi_{n}\to\Psi
in the
L^{2}
-sense.

Proof.

We have the estimate
\|\Psi_{n}(\theta)\|\leq\psi(\theta)
, therefore, the pointwise limit is again bounded:
\|\Psi(\theta)\|\leq\psi(\theta)
, especially,
\Psi
is
L^{2}
. In addition, we have the estimate
\|\Psi_{n}(\theta)-\Psi_{m}(\theta)\|^{2}\leq 4\psi(\theta)
, thus by Lebesgue’s dominated convergence,

\displaystyle\int d\theta\,\|\Psi_{n}(\theta)-\Psi_{m}(\theta)\|^{2}

converges to
0
. Namely,
\{\Psi_{n}\}
is convergent in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathcal{K}})
.
∎

Lemma E.3
.

Let
{\mathcal{K}}
be a Hilbert space. Consider the operator

on

. By identifying
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})\otimes{\mathcal{K}}\cong L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathcal{K}})
, we have
\operatorname{Dom}(\Delta_{1}\otimes{\mathbbm{1}})=H^{2}({\mathbb{S}}_{-\pi,0},{\mathcal{K}})

(=H^{2}({\mathbb{R}}+i(-\pi,0),{\mathcal{K}}))
.

Proof.

This can be reduced to the case where
{\mathcal{K}}={\mathbb{C}}
(see, e.g.,
[
35
, Proposition A.1]
) and the standard facts about Banach-space valued holomorphic functions (e.g.,
[
28
, Chapter 3, holomorphic functions]
).
∎

Lemma E.4

Let
{\mathcal{K}}

We may assume that

In the latter expression, the first factor

-function in

. Therefore, the integral gives a vector in

and it does not depend on

\Psi(\zeta)
. This notation coincides with
\Psi

We claim that the map

is analytic in norm. First, it is continuous in norm. Indeed, if

, where
\alpha

\displaystyle\int dk\,\hat{\Psi}(k)\big(e^{ik\zeta}-e^{ik\zeta^{\prime}}\big)\big(e^{\frac{\alpha}{3}k}+e^{-\frac{\alpha}{3}k}\big)\cdot\frac{1}{e^{\frac{\alpha}{3}k}+e^{-\frac{\alpha}{3}k}}

. As

-convergent, and the integral is convergent as well. As for analyticity in norm, we can use the Morera theorem. For a closed path

Now, again for a fixed

is an analytic function in one variable

\zeta=0

As

remains analytic in norm. Indeed, by definition of analyticity in norm,
\lim\limits_{\zeta\to 0}\frac{1}{\zeta-0}(\Psi(\zeta)-0)=\lim\limits_{\zeta\to 0}\frac{1}{\zeta}\Psi(\zeta)
exists. Therefore, so does the limit

\zeta f(\zeta)
is analytic by assumption. Especially, the

-valued function
\Psi(\zeta)f(\zeta)

Away from

is bounded by assumption, therefore, as

for

\Psi(\theta+i\lambda)f(\theta)

.
∎
