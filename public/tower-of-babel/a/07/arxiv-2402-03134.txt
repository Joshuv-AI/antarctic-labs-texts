The Patch Topology in Univalent Foundations
Igor Arrieta, Martín H. Escardó, Ayberk Tosun

Abstract
. Stone locales together with continuous maps form a coreflective subcategory of spectral locales and perfect maps. A proof in the internal language of an elementary topos was previously given by the second-named author. This proof can be easily translated to univalent type theory using resizing axioms . In this work, we show how to achieve such a translation without resizing axioms, by working with large and locally small frames with small bases. This requires predicative reformulations of several fundamental concepts of locale theory in predicative HoTT/UF , which we investigate systematically.



1.
Introduction

The category
\mathbf{Stone}
of Stone locales together with continuous maps forms a
coreflective subcategory of the category
\mathbf{Spec}
of spectral locales and

perfect
maps. A proof in the internal
language of an elementary topos was previously constructed by

Escardó, 1999
;
Escardó, 2001
, defining the patch frame as the frame of
Scott continuous nuclei on a given frame.

In the present work, we show that the same construction can be carried out in
predicative and constructive univalent foundations. In the presence of
Voevodsky’s
(
Voevodsky, 2011
)
resizing axioms, it is
straightforward to translate this proof to univalent type theory. At the time of
writing, however, there is no known computational interpretation of the resizing
axioms. Therefore, the question of whether resizing can be avoided in the
construction of the patch locale is of interest.
In such a predicative situation, the usual approach to locale
theory is to work with presentations of locales, known as
formal
topologies

(
Sambin, 1987
;
Coquand et al., 2003
;
Coquand and Tosun, 2021
)
. We show in our work, however, that it is
possible to work with locales directly. This requires a number of
modifications to the proofs and constructions of
Escardó, 1999
;
Escardó, 2001
:

(1)

The major modification is that we work with large and locally small
frames with small bases. The reason for this is that, in the absence of
propositional resizing, there are no non-trivial examples of
frames
(
de Jong and Escardó, 2021
)
. See Section
3

for further discussion.

(2)

The patch frame is defined as the frame of Scott continuous nuclei by

Escardó, 1999
;
Escardó, 2001
. In order to prove that this is indeed a
frame, one starts with the frame of all nuclei, and then exhibits the
Scott continuous nuclei as a subframe. This procedure, however, does not seem
to be possible in the context of our work, as it is not clear whether all nuclei
can be shown to form a frame predicatively; so we construct the frame of
Scott continuous nuclei
directly
, which requires predicative
reformulations of all proofs about
it inherited from the frame of all nuclei.

(3)

In the impredicative setting, any frame has all Heyting
implications, which are needed to construct open nuclei. Again, this does not
seem to be the case in the predicative setting. We show, however, that it is
possible to construct Heyting implications in large and locally small frames
with small bases, by an application of the posetal instance of the Adjoint
Functor Theorem.

(4)

Similar to (
3
), we use the posetal Adjoint Functor Theorem
to define the right adjoint of a frame homomorphism, which we then use to
define the notion of a
perfect map
, namely, a map whose defining frame
homomorphism’s right adjoint is Scott continuous. This notion is used by
Escardó in his impredicative proof

(
Escardó, 1999
;
Escardó, 2001
)
.

For the purposes of our work, a
spectral locale
is a locale in which any
open can be expressed as the join of a family of compact opens. A continuous map
of spectral locales is
spectral
if its defining frame homomorphism
preserves compact opens. A
Stone locale
is one that is compact and in
which any open is expressible as the join of a family of clopens. Every Stone
locale is spectral since the clopens coincide with the compact opens in Stone
locales. The patch frame construction is the right adjoint to the inclusion

\mathbf{Stone}\hookrightarrow\mathbf{Spec}
. The main contribution of our work is the construction of this
right adjoint in the predicative context of univalent type theory. We have also
formalized
(
Tosun, 2023
)
the development of this paper in
the
Agda
proof assistant
(
Norell, 2009
)
as part of the
TypeTopology

library
(
Escardó and contributors, 2023
)
. Our presentation here, however, is self-contained
and can be followed independently of the formalization.

The organization of this paper is as follows. In Section
2
,
we present the type-theoretical context in which we work. In
Section
3
, we introduce our notion of locale
discussed above. In Section
4
, we present our definitions
of spectral and Stone locales that provide a suitable basis for a predicative
development. In Section
5
, we present the posetal instance of the
Adjoint Functor Theorem for the simplified context of locales that is central to
our development. In Section
6
, we define the
meet-semilattice of perfect nuclei as preparation for the complete lattice of
perfect nuclei, which we then construct in Section
7
. Finally in
Section
8
, we prove the desired universal property, namely,
that the patch locale exhibits the category
\mathbf{Stone}
as a coreflective
subcategory of
\mathbf{Spec}
, where we restrict ourselves to locales with small bases.

Finally, we note that a preliminary version of the work that we present here
previously appeared in
(
Tosun and Escardó, 2023
)
. Our work here extends

loc. cit.
in several directions. The presentation that we provide
through Theorem
4.17
and Theorem
4.30
here
involves new results. Furthermore, we provide a complete proof of the universal
property of
\mathsf{Patch}
through a new approach, as given in
Theorem
8.8
and Theorem
8.10
.

2.
Foundations

In this section, we introduce the type-theoretical setting in which we work and
then present the type-theoretical formulations of some of the preliminary
notions that form the basis of our work. Our type-theoretical conventions follow
those of de Jong and Escardó
(
de Jong and Escardó, 2021
;
de Jong, 2023
)

and
The Univalent Foundations Program, 2013
.

We work in Martin-Löf Type Theory with binary sums
(\mathchar 45\relax)+(\mathchar 45\relax)
,
dependent products
\prod
, dependent sums
\sum
, the identity type

(\mathchar 45\relax)=(\mathchar 45\relax)
, and inductive types including the empty type
\mathbf{0}
,
the unit type
\mathbf{1}
, and the type
\mathsf{List}(A)
of lists, or
words
,
over any type
A
. We denote by
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
the first and the second
projections of a
\sum
type. We adhere to the convention of the HoTT
Book
(
The Univalent Foundations Program, 2013
)
of using
(\mathchar 45\relax)\equiv(\mathchar 45\relax)
for
judgemental equality and
(\mathchar 45\relax)=(\mathchar 45\relax)
for the identity type.

We work explicitly with universes, for which we adopt the convention of using
the variables
\mathcal{U},\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W},\ldots
The ground universe is denoted
\mathcal{U}_{0}
and the
successor of a given universe
\mathcal{U}
is denoted
{\mathcal{U}}^{+}
. The least upper
bound of two universes is given by the operator
(\mathchar 45\relax)\sqcup(\mathchar 45\relax)
which is
assumed to be associative, commutative, and idempotent. We do not assume that
the universes are, or are not, cumulative. Furthermore,
{(\mathchar 45\relax)}^{+}
is
assumed to distribute over
(\mathchar 45\relax)\sqcup(\mathchar 45\relax)
. Universes are computed for the
given type formers as follows:

•

Given types
X:\mathcal{U}
and
Y:\mathcal{V}
, the type
X+Y
inhabits universe

\mathcal{U}\sqcup\mathcal{V}
.

•

Given a type
X:\mathcal{U}
and an
X
-indexed family,
Y:X\to\mathcal{V}
, both

\sum_{x:X}Y(x)
and
\prod_{x:X}Y(x)
inhabit the universe
\mathcal{U}\sqcup\mathcal{V}
.

•

Given a type
X:\mathcal{U}
and inhabitants
x,y:X
, the identity type
x=y
inhabits universe
\mathcal{U}
.

•

The type
\mathbb{N}
of natural numbers inhabits
\mathcal{U}_{0}
.

•

The empty type
\mathbf{0}
and the unit type
\mathbf{1}
have copies in
every universe
\mathcal{U}
, which we occasionally make explicit using the
notations
\mathbf{0}_{\mathcal{U}}
and
\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{U}}
.

•

Given a type
A:\mathcal{U}
, the type
\mathsf{List}(A)
inhabits
\mathcal{U}
.

We assume the univalence axiom and therefore function extensionality and
propositional extensionality. We maintain a careful distinction between

structure
and
property
, and reserve logical connectives for

propositional types
i.e. types
A
satisfying
\mathsf{isProp}\left(A\right)\vcentcolon\equiv\prod_{x,y:A}{x=y}
. We denote by
\Omega_{\mathcal{U}}
the type of propositional types in
universe
\mathcal{U}
i.e.
\Omega_{\mathcal{U}}\vcentcolon\equiv\sum_{A:\mathcal{U}}{\mathsf{isProp}\left(A\right)}
. A type

A
is called a
set
if its identity type is always a proposition
i.e.
x=y
is a propositional type, for every
x,y:A
.

We assume the existence of
propositional truncation
, given by a type
former
\left\|\mathchar 45\relax\right\|:\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{U}
and a unit operation
\left|\mathchar 45\relax\right|:A\to\left\|A\right\|
. The existential quantification operator is defined using
propositional truncation as

\exists x:A,B(x)\vcentcolon\equiv\left\|\Sigma x:A,B(x)\right\|
.

When presenting proofs informally, we adopt the following conventions for
avoiding ambiguity between propositional and non-propositional types.

•

For the anonymous inhabitation
\left\|A\right\|
of a type, we say that
A

is inhabited.

•

For truncated
\sum
types, we use the terminologies

there is
and
there exists
.

•

We say
specified inhabitant
of type
A
to contrast it with the
anonymous inhabitation
\left\|A\right\|
. Similarly, we say there is a

specified
or
chosen
element to emphasize that we are using

\sum
instead of
\bigexists
.

•

When we use the phrase
has a
, we take it to mean a

specified
inhabitant. In contrast, we say
has some
when talking
about an unspecified inhabitant. If we want to completely avoid ambiguity,
we prefer to use the more explicit terminology of

has specified
or
has unspecified
.

If a given type
A
is a proposition, it is clear that it is logically
equivalent to its own propositional truncation. The converse, however, is not
always true: there are types that are logically equivalent to their own
propositional truncations, despite not being propositions themselves. Types that
satisfy this more general condition of being logically equivalent to their own
truncations are said to have
split support
, and have previously been
investigated by
Kraus et al., 2017
.

Definition 2.1

(
\mathcal{V}
-small type)
.

A type
A:\mathcal{U}
is called
\mathcal{V}
-small
if it has a copy in universe

\mathcal{V}
. That is to say,
\sum_{B:\mathcal{V}}{A\simeq B}
.

Lemma 2.2
.

The following are equivalent.

(1)

For every type
A:\mathcal{U}
and universe
\mathcal{V}
, the property of being

\mathcal{V}
-small
is a proposition.

(2)

Univalence holds.

We also have the notion of
local smallness
which refers to the equality
type always being small with respect to some universe.

Definition 2.3

(Local
\mathcal{V}
-smallness)
.

A type
X:\mathcal{U}
is called
locally
\mathcal{V}
-small
if the identity
type
x=y
is
\mathcal{V}
-small
for every
x,y:X
.

We have mentioned the
propositional resizing
axiom in
Section
1
. Having formally defined the notion of
\mathcal{V}
-smallness,
we can now give the precise definition of this axiom.

Definition 2.4
.

The
propositional
(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{V})
-resizing axiom
says that any
proposition
P:\Omega_{\mathcal{U}}
is
\mathcal{V}
-small
.
The
global propositional resizing
axiom says that, for any two
universes
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{V}
, the propositional

({\mathcal{U}},{\mathcal{V}})
-resizing
axiom holds.

Definition 2.5

(The set replacement axiom)
.

Let
f:X\to Y
be a function from a type
X:\mathcal{U}
into a set
Y:\mathcal{V}
.
The

says that if
X
is
\mathcal{U}^{\prime}
-small
and the

is locally
\mathcal{W}
-small then
\mathsf{image}(f)
is
(\mathcal{U}^{\prime}\sqcup\mathcal{W})
-small
, where

\mathsf{image}(f)\vcentcolon\equiv\sum_{y:Y}{\bigexists_{x:X}f(x)=y}.

Remark 2.6
.

Notice that we obtain the following as a special case of the set replacement
axiom: given a locally
\mathcal{U}
-small set
X^{\prime}:\mathcal{U}^{+}
, the image of any function

f:X\to X^{\prime}
is
\mathcal{U}
-small whenever the type
X
is
\mathcal{U}
-small.

We now proceed to define, in the presented type-theoretical setting, some

(Family)
.

A

A function

3.
Locales

A
locale
is a notion of space characterized solely by its lattice of
opens. The lattice-theoretic notion abstracting the behaviour of a lattice of
open subsets is a
frame
: a lattice with finite meets and arbitrary joins
in which the binary meets distribute over arbitrary joins.

Our type-theoretic definition of a frame is parameterized by three universes:
(1) for the carrier set, (2) for the order, and (3) for the join-completeness
i.e. the index types of families on which the join operation is defined. We
adopt the convention of using the universe variables
\mathcal{U}
,
\mathcal{V}
, and
\mathcal{W}
for
these respectively.

Definition 3.1

(Frame)
.

A
(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W})
-frame

L
consists of:

•

a set
|L|:\mathcal{U}
,

•

a partial order
(\mathchar 45\relax)\leq(\mathchar 45\relax):|L|\to|L|\to\Omega_{\mathcal{V}}
,

•

a top element
\mathbf{1}:|L|
,

•

an operation
(\mathchar 45\relax)\wedge(\mathchar 45\relax):|L|\to|L|\to|L|
giving the
greatest lower bound
U\wedge V
of any two
U,V:|L|
,

•

an operation
\bigvee(\mathchar 45\relax):\hyperref@@ii[defn:family]{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{Fam}}}_{\mathcal{W}}\left(|L|\right)\to|L|
giving the least upper
bound
\bigvee(I,\alpha)
of any
\mathcal{W}
-family
(I,\alpha)
,

such that binary meets distribute over arbitrary joins, i.e.

U\wedge\bigvee_{i:I}x_{i}=\bigvee_{i:I}U\wedge x_{i}

for every
x:|L|
and
\mathcal{W}
-family
(x_{i})_{i:I}
in
|L|
.

As we have done in the definition above, we adopt the shorthand notation

\bigvee_{i:I}x_{i}
for the join of a family
(x_{i})_{i:I}
.
We also adopt the usual abuse of notation and write
L
instead of
|L|
.

The reader might have noticed that we have not imposed a sethood condition on
the underlying type of a frame in this definition. The reason for this is that
it follows automatically from the antisymmetry condition for partial orders that
the underlying type of a frame is a set.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
L
be a
(\mathcal{U}^{+},\mathcal{V},\mathcal{U})
-frame and let
x,y:L
. We have that
x\leq y
is
\mathcal{U}
-small
if and only if the carrier of
L
is a locally
\mathcal{U}
-small type.

Proof.

Let
x,y:L
. It is a standard fact of lattice theory that
x\leq y\leftrightarrow x\wedge y=x
. (
\Rightarrow
) If the frame is locally small in the sense that
x\leq y
is

\mathcal{U}
-small
for every
x,y:L
, then the identity type
x=y
must
also be
\mathcal{U}
-small
since
x=y
is equivalent to the conjunction
(x\leq y)\wedge(y\leq x)
. (
\Leftarrow
) Conversely, if the carrier of
L
is a locally small
type, then
x\leq y
must be
\mathcal{U}
-small
since it is equivalent to the identity
type
x\wedge y=x
which is
\mathcal{U}
-small
by the local smallness of the
carrier.
∎

We also note that, in the work of de Jong and
Escardó
(
de Jong and Escardó, 2021
;
de Jong and Escardó, 2021
;
de Jong, 2023
)
, the term

\mathcal{V}
-dcpo is used for a directed-complete partially ordered set whose
directed joins are over
\mathcal{V}
-families. This terminology leaves the carrier and
the order implicit as in most cases, the completeness universe is the only one
relevant to the discussion.

We gave a highly general definition of the notion of frame in
Definition
3.1
: all three universes involved in the definition are
permitted to live in separate universes. This generality, however, is never
needed in our work. In fact, we know that locales of a certain form cannot be
constructed predicatively; it follows from a result due to

de Jong and Escardó, 2021
that the existence of a nontrivial small locale is
equivalent to a form of propositional resizing that is known to be independent
of type theory — see
(
de Jong and Escardó, 2021
, Theorem 35)
for details. In short,
the carrier set is naturally forced to be large when taking a predicative
approach to locale theory.

In light of this, we restrict attention to
(\mathcal{U}^{+},\mathcal{U},\mathcal{U})
-frames, which we
refer to as
large and locally small
frames.

Convention 3.3
.

From now on, we fix a
base
universe
\mathcal{U}
, and refer to types that have
isomorphic copies in
\mathcal{U}
as
small types
. In contrast, we refer to types
in
{\mathcal{U}}^{+}
(with no isomorphic copies in
\mathcal{U}
) as
large types
.
Accordingly, we hereafter take frame/locale to mean one whose underlying type is

large and locally small
i.e. a
({\mathcal{U}}^{+},\mathcal{U},\mathcal{U})
-frame/locale.

Definition 3.4

(Frame homomorphism)
.

Let
K
and
L
be two locales.
A function
h:|K|\to|L|
is called a
frame homomorphism
if it
preserves the top element, binary meets, and joins of small families. We denote
by
\mathbf{Frm}
the category of frames and their homomorphisms (over our base universe

\mathcal{U}
).

We adopt the notational conventions of
Mac Lane and Moerdijk, 1994
. A
locale
is a
frame considered in the opposite category denoted
\mathbf{Loc}\vcentcolon\equiv{\mathbf{Frm}}^{\mathsf{op}}
. To
highlight this, we adopt the standard conventions of (1) using the letters
X,Y,Z,\ldots
(or sometimes
A,B,C,\ldots
) for locales and (2) denoting the frame
corresponding to a locale
X
by
\mathcal{O}(X)
. For variables that range over the
frame of opens of a locale
X
, we use the letters
U,V,W,\ldots
We use the
letters
f
and
g
for continuous maps
X\to Y
of locales. A continuous map
f:X\to Y
is given by a frame homomorphism
f^{*}:\mathcal{O}(Y)\rightarrow\mathcal{O}(X)
.

Definition 3.5

(Nucleus)
.

A
nucleus on a locale

X
is an endofunction
j:\mathcal{O}(X)\to\mathcal{O}(X)
that is inflationary, idempotent, and preserves binary meets.

In Section
7
, we will work with inflationary and
binary-meet-preserving functions that are not necessarily idempotent. Such
functions are called
prenuclei
. We also note that, to show a prenucleus

j
to be idempotent, it suffices to show
j(j(U))\leq j(U)
as the other
direction follows from inflationarity. In fact, the notion of a nucleus could be
defined as a prenucleus satisfying the inequality
j(j(U))\leq j(U)
, but we
define it as in Definition
3.5
for the sake of simplicity and
make implicit use of this fact in our proofs of idempotency.

In point-set topology, a
basis
for a space
X
is a collection
\mathcal{B}
of
subsets of
X
such that every open
U\in\mathcal{O}(X)
can be decomposed into a
union of subsets contained in
\mathcal{B}
. These subsets are called the
basic
opens
. In the context of pointfree topology, the notion of a basis is captured
by the lattice-theoretic notion of a
generating lattice
. In

(
Johnstone, 2002
, p. 548)
, for example, a basis for a locale
\mathcal{O}(X)
is
defined as a subset
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{O}(X)
such that every element of
\mathcal{O}(X)
is
expressible as a join of members of
\mathcal{B}
. We now give the formal definition of
this notion in our type-theoretical setting.

Definition 3.6

(Directed family)
.

Let
(x_{i})_{i:I}
be a family on some type
A
that is equipped with a
preorder
(\mathchar 45\relax)\leq(\mathchar 45\relax)
. This family is called
directed
if

(1)

I
is inhabited, and

(2)

for every
i,j:I
, there exists some
k:I
such that
x_{k}
is an
upper bound of
\{x_{i},x_{j}\}
.

Definition 3.7

(Basis of a frame)
.

Let
X
be a locale. A
basis
for
X
is an embedding family

(B_{i})_{i:I}

such that for every
U:\mathcal{O}(X)
, there is a specified directed small
family
(i_{j})_{j:J}
on the basis
I
satisfying

U=\bigvee_{j:J}B_{i_{j}}
.
We often drop the embedding requirement, in which case we speak of

intensional bases
. When we want to emphasize the distinction, we speak
of
extensional bases
when we require the embedding condition.

Notice that the embedding requirement says that the family in consideration does
not have repetitions. However, it will often be convenient to work with families
which might have repetitions. Such repetitions can be removed (constructively)
if necessary, by factoring the family through its image. In the case of a

spectral locale
, for example, there can be different intensional bases but
the extensional basis is unique.

Even though we do not require a basis to be given by a small family, we always
work with small bases in our work.

Remark 3.8
.

In the definition above, we required covering families to be directed. This
requirement is not essential since a basis can always be
directified
by
closing it under finite joins, that is, given a basis
(B_{i})_{i:I}
,
we can define
B^{\uparrow}:\mathsf{List}(I)\to\mathcal{O}(X)
as

B^{\uparrow}(i_{0},\ldots,i_{n-1})\quad\vcentcolon\equiv\quad B_{i_{0}}\vee\cdots\vee B_{i_{n-1}},

which yields an equivalent basis. We nevertheless prefer to include the
directedness requirement in the definition for technical convenience. Finally,
we also note that the directification of a small basis gives a small basis.

Lemma 3.9
.

Given any locale
X
with some small intensional basis
(B_{i})_{i:I}
,
the image of
B:I\to\mathcal{O}(X)
is small.

Proof.

Since being small is a propositional type (by
Lemma
2.2
), we can appeal to the induction
principle of propositional truncation and assume we are given a
specified

intensional small basis
(B_{i})_{i:I}
.
By appealing to the set replacement axiom (Definition
2.5
) as
explained in Remark
2.6
, it just remains to show that the
carrier of
\mathcal{O}(X)
is locally small, which is always the case in a locally
small locale thanks to Lemma
3.2
.
∎

4.
Spectral and Stone Locales

The standard impredicative definition of a spectral locale is as one in which
the compact opens form a basis closed under finite meets (see II.3.2 from

(
Johnstone, 1982
)
). To talk about compactness, we define the
way-below

relation.

Definition 4.1

(Way-below relation)
.

We say that an open
U
of a locale
X
is
way below
an open
V
,
written
U\WayBelow V
,
if for every directed family
(W_{i})_{i:I}
with
V\leq\bigvee_{i:I}W_{i}

there is some
i:I
with
U\leq W_{i}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Given any two opens
U,V:\mathcal{O}(X)
, the type
U\WayBelow V
is a
proposition.

The statement
U\WayBelow V
is thought of as expressing that open
U
is
compact
relative to
open
V
. An open is said to be compact if it is
compact relative to itself:

Definition 4.3

(Compact open of a locale)
.

An open
U:\mathcal{O}(X)
is called
compact
if
U\WayBelow U
.

4.1.
Spectral locales

Definition 4.4

(Compact locale)
.

A locale
X
is called
compact
if the top open
\mathbf{1}_{X}
is

compact
.

We denote by
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{K}}(X)
the type of compact opens of a locale
X
. In
other words, we define

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{K}}(X)\quad\vcentcolon\equiv\quad\sum_{U:\mathcal{O}(X)}{U\ \text{\rm is compact}}.

Since our locales are large and locally small, we have that the type of compact
opens lives in
\mathcal{U}^{+}
i.e. is not
a priori
small. A spectral locale,
also sometimes called
coherent
in the
literature
(
Johnstone, 1982
, II.3.2)
, is defined in impredicative locale
theory as one in which the compact opens (1) are closed under finite meets, and
(2) form a basis. In our formulation of this notion, we capture the same idea
but impose the additional requirement that the type
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{K}}(X)
of
compact opens forms a small type.

Definition 4.5

(Spectral locale)
.

A locale
X
is called
spectral
if it satisfies the following
conditions.

\left({\mathsf{SP{1}}}\right)

It is a
compact locale

(i.e. the empty meet is compact).

\left({\mathsf{SP{2}}}\right)

The meet of two
compact open
s is a
compact open
.

\left({\mathsf{SP{3}}}\right)

For any
U:\mathcal{O}(X)
, there exists a small
directed
family

(K_{i})_{i:I}
, with each
K_{i}

compact
, such that

U=\bigvee_{i:I}K_{i}
.

\left({\mathsf{SP{4}}}\right)

The type
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{K}}(X)
is
small
.

The last two conditions are together equivalent to saying that the compact opens
form a small basis, although this is not immediate (Theorem
4.17
).
Notice that in the impredicative notion of
spectral locale
, everything is
small. One way of understanding the above definition is that we only make the

carrier
large, but everything else, including the basis, remains small.
This is important for a variety of reasons. Without the smallness condition, it
does not seem possible to (1) define Heyting implication, and hence open nuclei,
(2) show that frame homomorphisms have right adjoints, and so define the notion
of perfect frame homomorphism. Additionally, a natural Stone-type duality to
consider is that between small distributive lattices and spectral locales, for
which it becomes absolutely necessary that we work with small bases as in the
above definition. Notice that the same kind of phenomenon occurs when we work
with locally presentable categories, which are large, locally small categories
with a small set of objects that suitably generate all objects by taking
filtered colimits (
Adámek and Rosicky, 1994
;
Johnstone, 2002
).

Lemma 4.6
.

For any locale
X
, the statement that
X
is spectral is a proposition.

Proof.

Immediate from Lemma
4.2
and the fact that
the
\prod
type over a family of propositions is a proposition.
∎

The natural notion of morphism between spectral locales is that of a

spectral map
.

Definition 4.7

(Spectral map)
.

A continuous map
f:X\to Y
of spectral locales
X
and
Y
is called

spectral
if it reflects compact opens, that is, the open
f^{*}(V):\mathcal{O}(X)
is compact of
X
whenever the open
V
is compact.

We denote by
\mathbf{Spec}
the category of spectral locales with spectral maps as the
morphisms. We recall the following useful fact (see e.g.
Escardó, 1999
).

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
X
be a spectral locale and let
U,V:\mathcal{O}(X)
be two
opens.

U\leq V
if and only if
\prod_{K:\mathcal{O}(X)}{K\ \text{\rm is compact}\to K\leq U\to K\leq V}
.

In the context of the definition of spectrality that we gave in
Definition
4.5
, where we have conditions ensuring that
the compact opens form a small basis, it is natural to ask whether the
basis in consideration unique. This is indeed the case, but it is
subtler than one might expect, and is proved in Theorem
4.17
below. We
first need some preparation.

Given that the conditions in Definition
4.5
capture the
idea of the compact opens behaving like a small basis closed under finite meets,
one might wonder if the same notion can be formulated by starting with a small
basis closed under finite meets and requiring it to consist of compact opens.

Definition 4.9

(Intensional spectral basis)
.

An
intensional spectral basis
for a locale
X
is a small
intensional basis
(B_{i})_{i:I}
for the frame
\mathcal{O}(X)

satisfying the following three conditions.

(1)

For every
i:I
, the open
B_{i}
is
compact
.

(2)

There is an index
t:B
such that
B_{t}=\mathbf{1}_{X}
.

(3)

For any two
i,j:I
, there exists some
k:I
such that

B_{k}=B_{i}\wedge B_{j}
.

We say that the basis is
extensional
if
the map
B:I\to\mathcal{O}(X)
is an
embedding
.
When we say
spectral basis
without any qualification,
we mean an extensional spectral basis.

In Remark
3.8
, we mentioned that a basis can always be
directified by closing it under finite joins. The same applies to spectral bases
as well since the join of a finite family of compact opens is compact.

Lemma 4.10
.

Given any locale
X
with an intensional basis
(B_{i})_{i:I}
, every
compact open of
X
falls in the basis, that is, for every

compact open

K
, there exists an index
i:I
such that
B_{i}=K
.

Proof.

Let
K:\mathcal{O}(X)
be a compact open. As
(B_{i})_{i:I}
is an
intensional basis, there must be a specified directed family

(i_{j})_{j:J}
on
I
such that

K=\bigvee_{j:J}B_{i_{j}}
. By the compactness of
K
, there must be some

k:J
such that
K\leq B_{i_{k}}
.
Clearly,
B_{i_{k}}\leq K
is also the case, since
K
is an upper bound of the
family
(B_{i_{j}})_{j:J}
, which means we have that

K=B_{i_{k}}
.
∎

Lemma 4.11
.

Given any locale
X
with an intensional basis
(B_{i})_{i:I}

consisting of compact opens, we have an equivalence of types

\mathsf{image}(B)\simeq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{K}}(X)
.

Lemma 4.12
.

If a locale
X
has an unspecified, intensional, spectral, and small basis,
then
X
is
spectral
.

Proof.

First, notice that the conclusion is a proposition since being spectral is a
proposition (by Lemma
4.6
). This means that we
may appeal to the induction principle of propositional truncation and assume we
have a specified intensional spectral basis
(B_{i})_{i:I}
. We know that

\mathsf{image}(B)\simeq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{K}}(X)
by
Lemma
4.11
, which is to say that the type

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{K}}(X)
is small, by the smallness of
\mathsf{image}(B)

(given by Lemma
3.9
) meaning

(
SP4
)

is satisfied.

The top element is basic and hence compact so

(
SP1
)

holds.
Given two compact opens
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
, they must be basic meaning there exist

k_{1},k_{2}
such that
K_{1}=B_{k_{1}}
and
K_{2}=B_{k_{2}}
. Because the basis is
closed under binary meets there must be some
k_{3}
with
B_{k_{3}}=K_{1}\wedge K_{2}

which means condition

(
SP2
)

also holds.

For

(
SP3
)

, consider an open
U:\mathcal{O}(X)
. We know that
there is a specified a small family
(i_{j})_{j:J}
of indices such that

U=\bigvee_{j:J}B_{i_{j}}
.
The subfamily
(B_{i_{j}})_{j:J}
is then clearly a small directed
family with each
B_{i_{j}}

compact
which is what we needed.
∎

Lemma 4.13
.

If a locale
X
is
spectral
, then it has a specified, extensional, and
spectral small basis.

Proof.

Let
X
be a
spectral
locale. We claim that the inclusion
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{K}}(X)\hookrightarrow\mathcal{O}(X)
, which is formally given by the first projection, is an extensional and spectral small basis. The fact that it is small
is given by

(
SP4
)

. By

(
SP1
)

and

(
SP2
)

, we know that this basis contains
\mathbf{1}_{X}
and is closed
under binary meets. It remains to show that it forms a basis.
To define a covering family for an open
U:\mathcal{O}(X)
, we let the index type be the subtype of compact opens below
U
, which is again small by

(
SP4
)

, and the family is again given by the first projection.
It is clear that
U
is an upper bound of this family so it remains to show
that it is its least upper bound. Consider some
V
that is an upper bound
of this family. By Lemma
4.8
, it suffices to show

K\leq U
implies
K\leq V
, for every compact open
K
. Any such compact open

K\leq U
is below
U
by construction, which implies
K\leq V
since it
is an upper bound.
∎

Definition 4.14

(Extensionalization of an intensional basis)
.

For an intensional basis
(B_{i})_{i:I}
on a locale
X
, its

extensionalization
, is defined by taking the index set to be the

\mathsf{image}(B)
, and the family to be the corestriction of
B
to its image,
which is given by the first projection, and hence is an embedding.

Lemma 4.15
.

If
(B_{i})_{i:I}
is a spectral basis, then so is its extensionalization.

Lemma 4.16
.

From an unspecified intensional spectral basis on a locale
X
we can obtain
a specified extensional spectral basis.

Proof.

Let
X
be a locale with an unspecified intensional spectral basis.
By Lemma
4.12
, we know that it is spectral and therefore
that it has a specified extensional basis by Lemma
4.13
.
∎

Recall that a type
X
is said to have
split support
if
\left\|X\right\|\to X

(
Kraus et al., 2017
)
. With this terminology, the above lemma says that the
type of intensional spectral bases on a locale
X
has split support.

Theorem 4.17
.

The following are logically equivalent for any locale
X
.

(1)

X
is
spectral
.

(2)

X
has an unspecified intensional small spectral basis.

(3)

X
has an unspecified extensional small spectral basis.

(4)

The inclusion
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{K}}(X)\hookrightarrow X
is an extensional spectral basis,
where to an open
U
we assign the directed family of compact opens below it as in the construction of Lemma
4.13
.

(5)

X
has a specified intensional small spectral basis.

(6)

X
has a specified extensional small spectral basis.

Notice that the first four conditions are propositions, but the last two are not in general.

Proof.

We have already established one direction of the logical equivalence
(\ref{it:II})\leftrightarrow(\ref{it:III})
in
Lemma
4.16
, and the other is the unit
of propositional truncation.
Every extensional basis is intensional, and so
(\ref{it:IV})\to(\ref{it:III})
is clear.
The implication
(\ref{it:I})\rightarrow(\ref{it:IV})
is Lemma
4.13
.
By Corollary
4.11
, we know that

\mathsf{image}(B)\simeq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{K}}(X)
for any intensional spectral basis

(B_{i})_{i:I}
. This implies that any extensional spectral basis is equivalent to the
basis
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{K}}(X)\hookrightarrow X
.
Conversely, the inclusion
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{K}}(X)\hookrightarrow X
is always a small
extensional basis, so that we get

(\ref{it:IV})\leftrightarrow(\ref{it:V})
. We also clearly have
(\ref{it:IV})\rightarrow(\ref{it:IIPrime})\rightarrow(\ref{it:II})
, which concludes our proof.
∎

4.2.
Stone locales

Clopenness is central to the notion of Stone locale, similar to the fundamental
role played by the notion of a
compact open
in the definition of a

spectral locale
. To define the clopens, we first define the well-inside
relation.

Definition 4.18

(The well-inside relation)
.

We say that an open
U
of a locale
X
is

well inside
an open
V
, written
U\WellInside V
, if there is an open
W
with

U\wedge W=\mathbf{0}_{X}
and
V\vee W=\mathbf{1}_{X}
.

Definition 4.19

(Clopen)
.

An open
U
is called a
clopen
if it is well inside itself, which
amounts to saying that it has a Boolean complement. We denote by
\mathsf{C}(X)
the subtype of
\mathcal{O}(X)
consisting of clopens.

Before we proceed to the definition of a Stone locale, we record the following
fact about the well inside relation:

Lemma 4.20
.

Given opens
U,V,W:\mathcal{O}(X)
of a locale
X
,

(1)

if
U\WellInside V
and
V\leq W
then
U\WellInside W
; and

(2)

if
U\leq V
and
V\WellInside W
then
U\WellInside W
.

Definition 4.21

(Stone locale)
.

A locale
X
is called
Stone
if it satisfies the following conditions:

\left({\mathsf{ST{1}}}\right)

It is compact.

\left({\mathsf{ST{2}}}\right)

For any
U:\mathcal{O}(X)
, there exists a small
directed
family

(C_{i})_{i:I}
, with each
C_{i}
clopen, such that

U=\bigvee_{i:I}C_{i}
.

\left({\mathsf{ST{3}}}\right)

The type
\mathsf{C}(X)
is small.

Lemma 4.22
.

For any locale
X
, being Stone is a proposition.

Proof.

Being compact is a proposition (Lemma
4.2
) and being
small is a proposition by Lemma
2.2
(assuming
univalence). The condition

(
ST2
)

is a proposition since
the
\prod
-type over a family of propositions is a proposition.
∎

We denote by
\mathbf{Stone}
the category of Stone locales with continuous maps as the
morphisms. The defining frame homomorphism of any continuous map of Stone
locales automatically preserves clopens.

The following two lemmas are needed to prove that the
compact open
s and the
clopens coincide in Stone locales, which we will need later. The proofs are
standard
(
Johnstone, 1982
, Lemma VII.3.5)
. We provide the proof of
Lemma
4.24
for the sake of self-containment,
since it uses our reformulation of the notion of a Stone locale.

Lemma 4.23
.

In any
compact locale

,
U\WellInside V
implies
U\WayBelow V
for any
two opens
U,V
.

Lemma 4.24
.

In any Stone locale,
U\WayBelow V
implies
U\WellInside V
for any
two opens
U,V
.

Proof.

Let
U,V:\mathcal{O}(X)
with
U\WayBelow V
. We know that

V=\bigvee_{i:I}C_{i}
for a family
(C_{i})_{i:I}
consisting of
clopens.
Since
V\leq\bigvee_{i:I}C_{i}
, it must be the case that there is some

k:I
with
U\leq C_{k}
as we know
U\WayBelow V
.
We then have
U\leq C_{k}\WellInside V
which implies
U\WellInside V

by Lemma
4.20
.
∎

Lemma 4.25
.

In any Stone locale, the type
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{K}}(X)\simeq\mathsf{C}(X)
.

Lemma 4.26
.

Every Stone locale
X
is spectral.

A consequence of Corollary
4.25
is that we immediately
get a characterization of Stone locales analogous to the notion of intensional
spectral basis that we gave in Definition
4.9
.

Definition 4.27

(Basis of clopens)
.

An
intensional basis of clopens
for a locale
X
is a small
intensional basis
(B_{i})_{i:I}
for the frame
\mathcal{O}(X)
that
consists of clopens.
We say that the basis is
extensional
if the map

B:I\to\mathcal{O}(X)
is
an
embedding
. When we say
basis of clopens
without any
qualification, we mean an extensional basis of clopens.

Notice that, because clopens are closed under finite joins, the directification
of a basis of clopens is again a basis of clopens. For the next lemma, recall
the notion of extensionalization given in
Definition
4.14
.

Lemma 4.28
.

If
(B_{i})_{i:I}
is a basis of clopens, then so is its extensionalization.

Lemma 4.29
.

If a locale
X
is compact and has an unspecified, intensional, and small
basis of clopens, then it is Stone.

Proof.

Since being Stone is a proposition (by Lemma
4.22
), we can
work with a specified basis
(B_{i})_{i:I}
with each
B_{i}
clopen.
Condition

(
ST2
)

is immediate since any covering family given
by the basis is a subfamily of
\mathsf{C}(X)
. It remains to show
that
\mathsf{C}(X)
is a small type

(
ST3
)

. Let

C:\mathsf{C}(X)
. Since
X
is compact,
C
must be a compact open by
Lemma
4.23
, and hence must fall in
the basis by Lemma
4.10
. The other direction is direct by construction
meaning
\mathsf{C}(X)\simeq\mathsf{image}(B)
, which concludes that

\mathsf{C}(X)
is small by Remark
2.6
.
∎

Theorem 4.30
.

The following are logically equivalent for any locale
X
.

(1)

X
is Stone.

(2)

X
is compact and has an unspecified intensional small basis of clopens.

(3)

X
is compact and has an unspecified extensional small basis of clopens.

(4)

X
is compact and the inclusion
\mathsf{C}(X)\hookrightarrow X
is an extensional
basis of clopens, where to an open

we assign the family of all clopens below it.

(5)

is compact has a specified intensional small basis of clopens.

(6)

X
is compact has a specified extensional small basis of clopens.

Notice that the first four conditions are propositions, but the last two are not in general.

Proof.

We know by Corollary
4.26
and
Corollary
4.25
that every Stone

X
is spectral and has
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{K}}(X)\simeq\mathsf{C}(X)
.
Therefore, by Theorem
4.17
, we know that
\mathsf{C}(X)
is an
extensional basis of clopens, which gives the implication
(

)
\rightarrow
(

).
The implications (
4
)
\rightarrow
(
6
)

\rightarrow
(

)
\rightarrow
(

) are
direct, and the implication (

)
\rightarrow
(

)
is Lemma

. We also clearly have

, which concludes our proof.
∎

5.
Predicative form of the posetal Adjoint Functor Theorem

We start with the definition of the notion of an adjunction in the simplified
context of lattices.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
(X,\leq_{X})
and
(Y,\leq_{Y})
be two preordered sets.
An adjunction between
X
and
Y
consists of a pair of monotonic maps

f:X\to Y
and
g:Y\to X
satisfying

f\dashv g\quad\vcentcolon\equiv\quad\text{$f(x)\leq y\leftrightarrow x\leq g(y)$ for all $x,y:X$}.

In locale theory, it is standard convention to denote by
f_{*}:\mathcal{O}(X)\to\mathcal{O}(Y)
the right adjoint of a frame homomorphism
f^{*}:\mathcal{O}(Y)\to\mathcal{O}(X)
corresponding to a continuous map of locales
f:X\to Y
. The right
adjoint here is known to always exist thanks to a simple application of the
Adjoint Functor Theorem which amounts to the definition:

f_{*}(U)\quad\vcentcolon\equiv\quad\bigvee\{V:\mathcal{O}(Y)\mid f^{*}(V)\leq U\}.

In the predicative setting of type theory, however, it is not clear how the
right adjoint of a frame homomorphism would be defined as the family
\{V:\mathcal{O}(Y)\mid f^{*}(V)\leq U\}
might be too big in general. This gives rise to the
problem that it is not
a priori
clear that its join in
\mathcal{O}(X)

exists. To resolve this problem, we use the assumption of a small basis.The use of a small basis for the posetal Adjoint Functor Theorem in a predicative setting was
independently observed by Tom de Jong (personal communication).

Theorem 5.2

(Posetal Adjoint Functor Theorem)
.

Let
X
and
Y
be two locales and
let
h:\mathcal{O}(Y)\to\mathcal{O}(X)
be a monotone map of frames.
Assume that
Y
has some small basis.
The map
h
has a right adjoint if and only if it preserves the joins of small
families.

Proof.

Let
h:\mathcal{O}(Y)\to\mathcal{O}(X)
be a monotone map of frames
and assume that
Y
has a small basis
(B_{i})_{i:I}
.
The forward direction is easy: suppose
h
has a right adjoint
g:\mathcal{O}(X)\to\mathcal{O}(Y)
and let
(U_{i})_{i:I}
be a family in
\mathcal{O}(Y)
. By the
uniqueness of joins, it is sufficient to show that
h(\bigvee_{i}U_{i})
is the join of
the family
(h(U_{i}))_{i:I}
. It is clearly an upper bound by the fact
that
h
is monotone. Given any other upper bound
V
of

(h(U_{i}))_{i:I}
,
we have that
h(\bigvee_{i}U_{i})\leq V
since
h(\bigvee_{i}U_{i})\leq V\leftrightarrow\left(\bigvee_{i}U_{i}\right)\leq g(V)
meaning it is sufficient to show
U_{i}\leq g(V)
for each
U_{i}
.
Since
U_{i}\leq g(V)
if and only if
h(U_{i})\leq V
, we are done as the latter can be seen to
hold directly from the fact that
V
is an upper bound of the family in
consideration.

For the converse, suppose
h(\bigvee_{i}U_{i})=\bigvee_{i:I}h(U_{i})
for every small
family of opens
(U_{i})_{i:I}
. We define the right adjoint of
h
as

g(U)\quad\vcentcolon\equiv\quad\bigvee\left\{\beta(b)\mid i:I,h(B_{i})\leq U\right\}.

We need to show that
g
is the right adjoint of
h
i.e. that

h(V)\leq U\leftrightarrow V\leq g(U)

for any two
V:\mathcal{O}(Y)
,
U:\mathcal{O}(X)
.

For the
(\rightarrow)
direction, assume
h(V)\leq U
. It must be the case that

V=\bigvee_{j:J}B_{i_{j}}
for
some specified covering family
(i_{j})_{j:J}
.
This means that we just have to show
B_{i_{j}}\leq g(U)
for every
j:J
,
which is the case since
h(B_{i_{j}})\leq h(V)\leq U
.

For the
(\leftarrow)
direction, assume
V\leq g(U)
. This means that we have:

\displaystyle h(V)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\quad h(g(U))

\displaystyle\quad=\quad h\left(\bigvee\left\{B_{i}\mid i:I,h(B_{i})\leq U\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\quad=\quad\bigvee\left\{h(B_{i})\mid i:I,h(B_{i})\leq U\right\}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\quad U

so that
h(V)\leq U
, as required.
∎

Our primary use case for the Adjoint Functor Theorem is the construction of
Heyting implications in locally small frames with small bases.

Definition 5.3

(Heyting implication)
.

Let
X
be a locale with some small basis. Given any open
U:\mathcal{O}(X)
, the map
(\mathchar 45\relax)\wedge U:\mathcal{O}(X)\to\mathcal{O}(X)
preserves joins by
the frame distributivity law. This means, by Theorem
5.2
, that it must
have a right adjoint
U\Rightarrow(\mathchar 45\relax):\mathcal{O}(X)\to\mathcal{O}(X)
. The operation

(\mathchar 45\relax)\Rightarrow(\mathchar 45\relax)
is known as
Heyting implication
.

Definition 5.4
.

By Theorem
5.2
, for any continuous map
f:X\to Y
of locales where

Y
has an unspecified small basis, the frame homomorphism
f^{*}:\mathcal{O}(Y)\to\mathcal{O}(X)
has a right adjoint, denoted by

f_{*}:\mathcal{O}(X)\to\mathcal{O}(Y).

Definition 5.5

(Perfect frame homomorphism)
.

Let
X
and
Y
be two locales and assume that
Y
has an unspecified small
basis. A continuous map
f:X\to Y
is said to be
perfect
if
f_{*}
is
Scott continuous, that is, it preserves small directed suprema.

Let us also record the following fact about perfect maps that we will need
later.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
f:X\to Y
be a perfect map where
Y
is a locale with some small basis.
The frame homomorphism
f^{*}
respects the
way below
relation, that is,
U\WayBelow V
implies
f^{*}(U)\WayBelow f^{*}(V)
, for any two
U,V:\mathcal{O}(Y)
.

A proof of this fact can be found in
(
Escardó, 1999
)
and it works in our
predicative setting.

Lemma 5.7
.

Perfect maps are
spectral
as they preserve the
compact open
s.

In fact, the converse is also true in the case of spectral locales. The proof
given in
(
Escardó, 1999
)
works, once we know that the required adjoints are available, as established above. We include it in order to illustrate this point.

Lemma 5.8
.

Let
X
and
Y
be locales and assume that
Y
has some small basis. A
continuous map
f:X\to Y
is perfect if and only if it is spectral.

Proof.

The forward direction is given by
Corollary
5.7
. For the backward direction,
assume
f:X\to Y
to be a spectral map. We have to show that the right adjoint

f_{*}:\mathcal{O}(X)\to\mathcal{O}(Y)
of its defining frame homomorphism is
Scott continuous. Letting
(U_{i})_{i:I}
be a directed family in

\mathcal{O}(X)
, we show
f_{*}(\bigvee_{i:I}U_{i})=\bigvee_{i:I}f_{*}(U_{i})
. The

\bigvee_{i:I}f_{*}(U_{i})\leq f_{*}(\bigvee_{i:I}U_{i})
direction is easy. For the

f_{*}(\bigvee_{i:I}U_{i})\leq\bigvee_{i:I}f_{*}(U_{i})
direction, we appeal
to Lemma
4.8
. Let
K
be a compact open
with
K\leq f_{*}(\bigvee_{i:I}U_{i})
. By the adjunction
f^{*}\dashv f_{*}
, it must be the
case that
f^{*}(K)\leq\bigvee_{i:I}U_{i}
and since
f^{*}(K)
is compact, by the
spectrality assumption of
f^{*}
, there must exist some
l:I
such that
f^{*}(K)\leq U_{l}
. Again by adjointness,
K\leq f_{*}(U_{l})
which implies
K\leq\bigvee_{i:I}f_{*}(U_{i})
.
∎

6.
Meet-semilattice of Scott continuous nuclei

In this section, we take the first step towards constructing the defining frame
of the patch locale on a spectral locale i.e. the frame of Scott continuous
nuclei. We start by constructing the meet-semilattice of
all
nuclei on a
frame.

Lemma 6.1
.

The type of nuclei on a given frame
\mathcal{O}(X)
forms a meet-semilattice
under the pointwise order.

Proof.

The top nucleus is defined as the constant map with value
\mathbf{1}_{X}
and
the meet of two nuclei as
j\wedge k\vcentcolon\equiv U\mapsto j(U)\wedge k(U)
. It is easy to see that
j\wedge k
is the greatest lower bound of
j
and
k
so it remains to show that
j\wedge k
satisfies the nucleus laws.

Inflationarity can be seen to be satisfied from the inflationarity
of
j
and
k
combined with the fact that
j(U)\wedge k(U)
is the
greatest lower bound of
j(U)
and
k(U)
. To see that meet preservation
holds, let
U,V:\mathcal{O}(X)
; we have:

\displaystyle(j\wedge k)(U\wedge V)

\displaystyle\quad=\quad j(U\wedge V)\wedge k(U\wedge V)

\displaystyle\quad=\quad j(U)\wedge j(V)\wedge k(U)\wedge k(V)

\displaystyle\quad=\quad(j(U)\wedge k(U))\wedge(j(V)\wedge k(V))

\displaystyle\quad=\quad(j\wedge k)(U)\wedge(j\wedge k)(V)

For idempotency, let
U:\mathcal{O}(X)
. We have:

\displaystyle(j\wedge k)((j\wedge k)(U))

\displaystyle\quad=\quad j(j(U)\wedge k(U))\wedge k(j(U)\wedge k(U))

\displaystyle\quad=\quad j(j(U))\wedge j(k(U))\wedge k(j(U))\wedge k(k(U))

\displaystyle\quad\leq\quad j(j(U))\wedge k(k(U))

\displaystyle\quad=\quad j(U)\wedge k(U)

\displaystyle\quad=\quad(j\wedge k)(U)

∎

We now show that this meet-semilattice can be
refined
to the Scott continuous nuclei
(i.e. the
perfect
nuclei).

Lemma 6.2
.

The Scott continuous nuclei on any locale form a meet-semilattice.

Proof.

Let
X
be a locale. The construction is the same as the one from
Lemma
6.1
; the top element is constant map with
value
\mathbf{1}_{X}
, which is trivially Scott continuous so it remains to show
that the meet of two Scott continuous nuclei is Scott continuous. Consider
two Scott continuous nuclei
j
and
k
on
\mathcal{O}(X)
and a directed small
family
(U_{i})_{i:I}
. We then have:

\displaystyle(j\wedge k)\left(\bigvee_{i:I}U_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\equiv\quad j\left(\bigvee_{i:I}U_{i}\right)\wedge k\left(\bigvee_{j:I}U_{j}\right)

\displaystyle\quad=\quad\left(\bigvee_{i:I}j(U_{i})\right)\wedge\left(\bigvee_{j:I}k(U_{j})\right)

\displaystyle[\text{Scott continuity of $j$ and $k$}]

\displaystyle\quad=\quad\bigvee_{(i,j):I\times I}j(U_{i})\wedge k(U_{j})

\displaystyle[\text{distributivity}]

\displaystyle\quad=\quad\bigvee_{i:I}j(U_{i})\wedge k(U_{i})

\displaystyle[\text{\textdagger}]

\displaystyle\quad\equiv\quad\bigvee_{i:I}(j\wedge k)(U_{i})

\displaystyle[\text{meet preservation}]

where, for the (†) step, we use antisymmetry. The
(\geq)
direction is immediate.
For the
(\leq)
direction, we need to show that
\bigvee_{(i,j):I\times I}j(U_{i})\wedge k(U_{j})\leq\bigvee_{i:I}j(U_{i})\wedge k(U_{i})
, for which it suffices to show that
\bigvee_{i:I}j(U_{i})\wedge k(U_{i})
is an upper bound of
\{j(U_{i})\wedge k(U_{j})\}_{(i,j):I\times I}
.
Let
m,n:I
be two indices. As
(U_{i})_{i:I}
is directed, there must
exist some
o
such that
U_{o}
is an upper bound of
\{U_{m},U_{n}\}
. Using the
monotonicity of
j
and
k
, we get
j(U_{m})\wedge k(U_{n})\leq j(U_{o})\wedge k(U_{o})\leq\bigvee_{i:I}j(U_{i})\wedge k(U_{i})
as desired.
∎

7.
Joins in the frame of Scott continuous nuclei

The nontrivial component of the patch frame construction is the join of a family

(k_{i})_{i:I}
of Scott continuous nuclei, as the pointwise join fails to be
idempotent in general, and not inflationary when the family in consideration is
empty. When the family in consideration is directed, however, the pointwise join
is idempotent, and so it is the join in the poset of Scott continuous nuclei.

Lemma 7.1
.

Given a directed family
(k_{i})_{i:I}
of Scott continuous nuclei, their join
is computed pointwise, that is,
\left(\bigvee_{i:I}k_{i}\right)(U)=\bigvee_{i:I}k_{i}(U)
.

Proof.

The argument given in the paragraph preceding

(
Escardó, 1998
, Lemma 3.1.8)
works in our setting.
∎

Regarding arbitrary joins, the situation is more complicated. A construction of
the join, due to
Escardó, 1998
, is based on the idea that, if we
start with a family
(k_{i})_{i:I}
of nuclei, we can consider the family
with index type
\mathsf{List}(I)
of words over
I
, defined by

(i_{0}i_{1}\cdots i_{n-1})\mapsto k_{i_{n-1}}\circ\cdots\circ k_{1}\circ k_{0}.

This family is easily seen to be direced.

To talk about such families of finite compositions over a given family of
(pre)nuclei, we adopt the following notation.

•

\varepsilon
denotes the empty word.

•

Given words
s:\mathsf{List}(I)
and
t:\mathsf{List}(I)
, the concatenation
is denoted by
st
.

To define the join operation, we will use the iterated composition function

k
that we define as follows:

Definition 7.2

(Family of finite compositions)
.

Given a small family
(k_{i})_{i:I}
of nuclei on a given locale
X
,
its
family of finite compositions
is the family defined by

k^{*}(i_{0}\cdots i_{n-1})\quad\vcentcolon\equiv\quad k_{i_{n-1}}\circ\cdots\circ k_{i_{0}}.

By an abuse of notation, we omit the superscript “
*
” when there is no possibility of confusion.

The order of composition is actually not important.
A finite composition
k_{s}
is, in general, not a nucleus. It is, however, always
a prenucleus which we prove below.

Lemma 7.3
.

Given a family
(k_{i})_{i:I}
of nuclei on a locale,

k_{s}
is a prenucleus for every
s:\mathsf{List}(I)
.

Proof.

If
s=\varepsilon
, we are done as it is immediate that the identity function

\mathsf{id}
is a prenucleus. If
s=is^{\prime}
, we need to show that

k_{s^{\prime}}\circ k_{i}
is a prenucleus. For meet preservation, let
U,V:\mathcal{O}(X)
.

\displaystyle(k_{s^{\prime}}\circ k_{i})(U\wedge V)

\displaystyle\quad=\quad k_{s^{\prime}}(k_{i}(U\wedge V))

\displaystyle\quad=\quad k_{s^{\prime}}(k_{i}(U)\wedge k_{i}(V))

\displaystyle[\text{$k_{i}$ is a nucleus}]

\displaystyle\quad=\quad k_{s^{\prime}}(k_{i}(U))\wedge k_{s^{\prime}}(k_{i}(V))

\displaystyle[\text{inductive hypothesis}]

\displaystyle\quad=\quad(k_{s^{\prime}}\circ k_{i})(U)\wedge(k_{s^{\prime}}\circ k_{i})(V)

For inflationarity, consider some
U:\mathcal{O}(X)
. We have that
U\leq k_{i}(U)\leq k_{s^{\prime}}(k_{i}(U))
, by the inflationarity property of
k_{i}
and the
inductive hypothesis.
∎

Lemma 7.4
.

Given a nucleus
j
and a family
(k_{i})_{i:I}
of nuclei on a
locale, if
j
is an upper bound then it is also an upper bound of the
family of finite compositions.

Proof.

Let
j
and
(k_{i})_{i:I}
be, respectively, a nucleus and a
family of nuclei on a locale. Let
s:\mathsf{List}(I)
. We proceed by
induction on
s
. If
s=\varepsilon
, we have that
\mathsf{id}(U)\equiv U\leq j(U)
. If

s=is^{\prime}
, we then have:

\displaystyle k_{s^{\prime}}(k_{i}(U))\quad

\displaystyle\leq\quad k_{s^{\prime}}(j(U))

\displaystyle[\text{monotonicity of $k_{s^{\prime}}$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:star-prenucleus} and monotonicity of prenuclei)}]

\displaystyle\leq\quad j(j(U))

\displaystyle[\text{IH}]

\displaystyle\leq\quad j(U)

\displaystyle[\text{idempotency of $j$}]

∎

Lemma 7.5
.

Given a family
(k_{i})_{i:I}
of Scott continuous nuclei on a locale,
the prenucleus
k_{s}
is Scott continuous, for every
s:\mathsf{List}(I)

Proof.

Any composition of finitely many Scott continuous functions is
Scott continuous.
∎

Lemma 7.6
.

Given a family
(k_{i})_{i:I}
of nuclei on a locale, the family of
finite compositions is
directed
.

Proof.

The family
(k_{s})_{s:\mathsf{List}(I)}
is indeed always inhabited by
the identity nucleus given by
k_{\epsilon}
. The upper bound of nuclei
k_{s}
and

k_{t}
is given by
k_{st}
, which is equal to
k_{t}\circ k_{s}
.
The fact that this is an upper bound of
\{k_{s},k_{t}\}
follows from
monotonicity and inflationarity.
∎

Lemma 7.7
.

Let
j
be a nucleus and
(k_{s})_{s:\mathsf{List}(I)}
a family of
nuclei on a locale
X
. Consider the family of finite compositions over
the family
(j\wedge k_{i})_{i:I}
. Each finite composition

(j\wedge k)^{*}_{s}
is a lower bound of
\{k_{s},j\}
for every

s:\mathsf{List}(I)
.

We are now ready to construct the join operation in the meet-semilattice of
Scott continuous nuclei hence defining the patch frame
\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{Patch}(X))
of the frame of
a locale
X
.

Theorem 7.8

(Join of Scott continuous nuclei)
.

Let
(k_{i})_{i:I}
be a family of Scott continuous nuclei. The join of
K
can be
calculated as

\left(\bigvee_{i:I}k_{i}\right)(U)\vcentcolon\equiv\bigvee_{s:\mathsf{List}(I)}k_{s}(U).

Proof.

It must be checked that this is (1) indeed the join, (2) is a Scott continuous nucleus
i.e. it is inflationary, binary-meet-preserving, idempotent, and
Scott continuous. The inflationarity property is direct. For meet preservation,
consider some
U,V:\mathcal{O}(X)
. We have:

\displaystyle\left(\bigvee_{i:I}k_{i}\right)(U\wedge V)

\displaystyle\quad=\quad\bigvee_{s:\mathsf{List}(I)}k_{s}(U\wedge V)

\displaystyle\quad=\quad\bigvee_{s:\mathsf{List}(I)}k_{s}(U)\wedge k_{s}(V)

\displaystyle[\text{Lemma~\ref{prop:star-sc}}]

\displaystyle\quad=\quad\bigvee_{s,t:\mathsf{List}(I)}k_{s}(U)\wedge k_{t}(V)

\displaystyle[{\dagger}]

\displaystyle\quad=\quad\left(\bigvee_{s:\mathsf{List}(I)}k_{s}(U)\right)\wedge\left(\bigvee_{t:\mathsf{List}(I)}k_{t}(V)\right)

\displaystyle[\text{distributivity}]

\displaystyle\quad=\quad\left(\bigvee_{i:I}k_{i}\right)(U)\wedge\left(\bigvee_{i:I}k_{i}\right)(V)

where step (
{\dagger}
) uses antisymmetry. The

(\leq)
direction is direct whereas for the
(\geq)

direction, we show that
\bigvee_{s:\mathsf{List}(I)}k_{s}(U)\wedge k_{s}(V)
is an upper
bound of the family

(k_{s}(U)\wedge k_{t}(V))_{s,t:\mathsf{List}(I)}.

Consider arbitrary
s,t:\mathsf{List}(I)
. By the directedness of the family of
finite compositions we know that there exists some
u:\mathsf{List}(I)
such that

k_{u}
is an upper bound of
\{k_{s},k_{t}\}
.
We then have

k_{s}(U)\wedge k_{t}(V)\leq k_{u}(U)\wedge k_{u}(V)\leq\bigvee_{s:\mathsf{List}(I)}k_{s}(U)\wedge k_{s}(V).

For idempotency, let
U:\mathcal{O}(X)
. We have that:

\displaystyle\left(\bigvee_{i}k_{i}\right)\left(\left(\bigvee_{i}k_{i}\right)(U)\right)

\displaystyle\quad\equiv\quad\bigvee_{s:\mathsf{List}(I)}k_{s}\left(\bigvee_{t:\mathsf{List}(I)}k_{t}(U)\right)

\displaystyle\quad=\quad\bigvee_{s:\mathsf{List}(I)}\bigvee_{t:\mathsf{List}(I)}k_{s}(k_{t}(U))

\displaystyle[\text{Lemma~\ref{prop:star-sc}}]

\displaystyle\quad\leq\quad\bigvee_{s,t:\mathsf{List}(I)}k_{s}(k_{t}(U))

\displaystyle\quad\leq\quad\bigvee_{s:\mathsf{List}(I)}k_{s}(U)

\displaystyle[{\dagger}]

\displaystyle\quad\equiv\quad\left(\bigvee_{i}k_{i}\right)(U),

where for the step (
{\dagger}
) it suffices to show that

\bigvee_{s:\mathsf{List}(I)}k_{s}(U)
is an upper bound
of the family

(k_{s}(k_{t}(U)))_{s,t:\mathsf{List}(I)}.

The prenucleus
k_{st}
is an upper bound of
k_{s}
and
k_{t}

(as in Lemma
7.6
).
We have that

k_{s}(k_{t}(U))=k_{st}(U)\leq\bigvee_{s:\mathsf{List}(I)}k_{s}(U).

For Scott continuity, let
(U_{j})_{j:J}
be a directed family over

\mathcal{O}(X)
. We then have:

\displaystyle\left(\bigvee_{i:I}k_{i}\right)\left(\bigvee_{j:J}U_{j}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\equiv\quad\bigvee_{s:\mathsf{List}(I)}k_{s}\left(\bigvee_{j:J}U_{j}\right)

\displaystyle\quad=\quad\bigvee_{s:\mathsf{List}(I)}\bigvee_{j:J}k_{s}(U_{j})

\displaystyle[\text{Lemma~\ref{prop:star-sc}}]

\displaystyle\quad=\quad\bigvee_{j:J}\bigvee_{s:\mathsf{List}(I)}k_{s}(U_{j})

\displaystyle[\text{joins commute}]

\displaystyle\quad\equiv\quad\bigvee_{j:J}\left(\bigvee_{i:I}k_{i}\right)(U_{j})

as required.

The fact that
\bigvee_{i}k_{i}
is an upper bound of
(k_{i})_{i:I}
is easy
to verify. To see that it is
the least
upper bound, consider a
Scott continuous nucleus
j
that is an upper bound of
(k_{i})_{i:I}
. Let
U:\mathcal{O}(X)
. We need to show that
(\bigvee_{i}k_{i})(U)\leq j(U)
. We know by
Lemma
7.4
that
j
is an upper bound of the family of
finite compositions, since it is
an upper bound of
(k_{i})_{i:I}
, which is to say

k_{s}(U)\leq j(U)
for every
s:\mathsf{List}(I)
i.e.
j(U)
is an upper bound
of the family
(k_{s}(U))_{s:\mathsf{List}(I)}
.
Since
\left(\bigvee_{i}k_{i}\right)(U)
is the least upper bound of this family by
definition, it follows that it is below
j(U)
.
∎

We use Lemma
7.7
to prove the distributivity law.

Lemma 7.9

(Distributivity)
.

For any Scott continuous nucleus
j
and any family
(k_{i})_{i:I}
of
Scott continuous nuclei, we have the equality:

j\wedge\left(\bigvee_{i:I}k_{i}\right)=\bigvee_{i:I}j\wedge k_{i}.

It follows that the Scott continuous nuclei form a frame.

Definition 7.10

(Patch locale of a spectral locale)
.

The
patch locale
of a spectral locale
X
, written
\mathsf{Patch}(X)
,
is given by the frame of Scott continuous nuclei on
X
.

Notice that the truth value of the relation
j\leq k
between Scott continuous nuclei lives by default in the universe
{\mathcal{U}}^{+}
. However, it has an equivalent copy in the universe
\mathcal{U}
:

Lemma 7.11
.

For any spectral locale
X
and any two nuclei
j,k:\mathsf{Patch}(X)
, we have that

\text{$j\leq k$ if and only if $j(K)\leq k(K)$ for all $K:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{K}}(X)$},

and hence
\mathsf{Patch}(X)
is locally small.

Proof.

The usual pointwise ordering obviously implies the basic ordering so we
address the other direction. Let
j
and
k
be two Scott continuous nuclei on
a
spectral locale

X
and assume
j(K)\leq k(K)
, for all
K:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{K}}(X)
.
We need to show that
j(U)\leq k(U)
for every open
U
so let
U:\mathcal{O}(X)
.
By

(
SP3
)

,
U
can be decomposed as

U=\bigvee_{i:I}K_{i}

for some directed covering family
(K_{i})_{i:I}
consisting of

compact open
s.
We then have

j(\bigvee_{i:I}K_{i})=\bigvee_{i:I}j(K_{i})
by Scott continuity and

\bigvee_{i:I}j(K_{i})\leq\bigvee_{i:I}k(K_{i})

since
j(K_{i})\leq k(K_{i})
for every
i:I
.
Finally, because the quantification is over the small type
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{K}}(X)
, the proposition
“
j(K)\leq k(K)
for all
K:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{K}}(X)
”
is small.
∎

8.
The coreflection property of
\mathsf{Patch}

We prove in this section that our construction of
\mathsf{Patch}
has the desired
universal property: it exhibits
\mathbf{Stone}
as a coreflective subcategory of

\mathbf{Spec}
.
The notions of
closed
and
open
nuclei are crucial for proving
the universal property. We first give the definitions of these. Let
U
be an
open of a locale
X
;

(1)

The
closed nucleus
induced by
U
is the map
V\mapsto U\vee V
;

(2)

The
open nucleus
induced by
U
is the map
V\mapsto U\Rightarrow V
.

We denote the closed nucleus induced by open
U
by
\mathbf{c}(U)
and the
open nucleus induced by
U
by
\mathbf{o}(U)
.

Lemma 8.1
.

For any spectral locale
X
and any monotone map
h:\mathcal{O}(X)\to\mathcal{O}(X)
,
if for every
U:\mathcal{O}(X)
and compact
K:\mathcal{O}(X)
with
K\leq h(U)
,
there is some compact
K^{\prime}\leq U
such that
K\leq h(K^{\prime})
, then
h
is
Scott continuous

Proof.

It suffices to show the relation
h(\bigvee_{i:I}U_{i})\leq\bigvee_{i:I}h(U_{i})
holds. Since
X
is spectral, let
(K_{j})_{j:J}
be a small family of compact opens such that
\bigvee_{j:J}K_{j}=h(\bigvee_{i:I}U_{i})
. For any
j:J
, we have
K_{j}\leq h(\bigvee_{i:I}U_{i})
, so by assumption there is a compact open
K\leq\bigvee_{i:I}U_{i}
such that
K_{j}\leq h(K)
. By compactness of
K
there is an
i:I
such that
K\leq U_{i}
, and so
K_{j}\leq h(K)\leq h(U_{i})\leq\bigvee_{i:I}h(U_{i})
and so we can take
K^{\prime}\vcentcolon\equiv K_{j}
.
∎

Lemma 8.2
.

For any open of any locale, the closed nucleus
\mathbf{c}(U)
is Scott continuous,
whereas the open nucleus
\mathbf{o}(U)
is Scott continuous if
the open
U
is
compact
.

Proof.

The Scott continuity of the closed nucleus is easy to see.
For the open nucleus, let
D
be a
compact
open of a locale. By
Lemma
8.1
, it is sufficient to show that for any open
V

and any
compact
open
C_{1}
with
C_{1}\leq D\Rightarrow V
, there exists some compact

C_{2}\leq D
such that
C_{1}\leq D\Rightarrow C_{2}
. Let
V
and
C_{1}
be two opens with
C_{1}

compact and satisfying
C_{1}\leq D\Rightarrow V
. Pick
C_{2}\vcentcolon\equiv D\wedge C_{1}
. We know that this
is compact by

(
SP2
)

. It remains to check (1)
C_{2}\leq V
and (2)

C_{1}\leq D\Rightarrow C_{2}
, both of which are direct.
∎

In Lemma
8.4
, we prove that the map whose inverse image sends
an open
U
to the closed nucleus
\mathbf{c}(U)
is perfect. Before
Lemma
8.4
, we record an auxiliary result:

Lemma 8.3
.

Let
X
be a spectral locale. The right adjoint
\varepsilon_{*}:\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{Patch}(X))\to\mathcal{O}(X)
to the closed-nucleus formation operation
\mathbf{c}(\mathchar 45\relax)
is
given by
\varepsilon_{*}(j)=j(\mathbf{0})
for every Scott continuous nucleus
j
on
X
.

The proof of Lemma
8.3
can be found in

Escardó, 1999
. It is omitted here as it is unchanged in our
predicative setting.

Lemma 8.4
.

The function
\mathbf{c}(\mathchar 45\relax)
is a perfect frame homomorphism
\mathcal{O}(X)\to\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{Patch}(X))
.

Proof.

We have to show that the right adjoint
\varepsilon_{*}
of
\mathbf{c}(\mathchar 45\relax)
is
Scott continuous. Let
(k_{i})_{i:I}
be a directed family of
Scott continuous nuclei. Thanks to Lemma
8.3
, it suffices to show

\left(\bigvee_{i:I}k_{i}\right)(\mathbf{0})=\bigvee_{i:I}\varepsilon_{*}(k_{i}).

By
Lemma
7.1
, we have
that
\left(\bigvee_{i:I}k_{i}\right)(\mathbf{0})=\bigvee_{i:I}k_{i}(\mathbf{0})
.
∎

This function defines a continuous map
\varepsilon:\mathsf{Patch}(X)\to X
, which we will
show to be the counit of the coreflection in consideration.

8.1.
\mathsf{Patch}
is Stone

Before we proceed to showing that the patch locale has the desired universal
property, we first need to show that
\mathsf{Patch}(X)
is Stone for any spectral
locale
X
. For this purpose, we need to (1) show that it is compact, and (2)
construct a basis for it consisting of clopens. We start with compactness.

Lemma 8.5
.

\mathsf{Patch}(X)
is a
compact locale

for any spectral locale
X
.

Proof.

Recall that the top element

\mathbf{1}
of
\mathsf{Patch}(X)
is defined as
\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{Patch}}\vcentcolon\equiv U\mapsto\mathbf{1}_{X}
.
Because
\varepsilon^{*}
is a frame
homomorphism, it must be the case that
\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{Patch}}=\varepsilon^{*}(\mathbf{1}_{X})

meaning it suffices to show
\varepsilon^{*}(\mathbf{1}_{X})\WayBelow\varepsilon^{*}(\mathbf{1}_{X})
. By
Lemma
5.6
, it suffices to show

\mathbf{1}_{X}\WayBelow\mathbf{1}_{X}

which is immediate by

(
SP1
)

.
∎

To construct a basis consisting of clopens, we will use the following fact,
which was already mentioned above:

Lemma 8.6
.

The open nucleus
\mathbf{o}(U)
is the Boolean complement of the closed
nucleus
\mathbf{c}(U)
.

Lemma 8.7
.

Let
X
be a spectral locale.
Given any perfect nucleus
j:\mathsf{Patch}(X)
, we have that

j=\bigvee_{K:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{K}}(X)}\mathbf{c}(j(K))\wedge\mathbf{o}(K)=\bigvee_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle K_{1},K_{2}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{K}}(X)}}{{K_{1}\leq j(K_{2})}}}\mathbf{c}(j(K_{1}))\wedge\mathbf{o}(K_{2}).

Proof.

The second equality in the statement is clear, so let us show the first one.
We use the fact, proved in
(
Johnstone, 1982
, Lemma II.2.7)
, that for any nucleus
j
on any locale,

j=\bigvee_{U:\mathcal{O}(X)}\mathbf{c}(j(U))\wedge\mathbf{o}(U).

Suppose now additionally that
X
is spectral and the nucleus
j
is Scott continuous. The inequality
\bigvee_{K:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{K}}(X)}\mathbf{c}(j(K))\wedge\mathbf{o}(K)\leq\bigvee_{U:\mathcal{O}(X)}\mathbf{c}(j(U))\wedge\mathbf{o}(U)=j
is trivial, so let us show the reverse one.
Let
K:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{K}}(X)
and notice that
\left(\mathbf{c}(j(K))\wedge\mathbf{o}(K)\right)(K)=(j(K)\vee K)\wedge(K\Rightarrow K)=j(K)
. Therefore

j(K)=\left(\mathbf{c}(j(K))\wedge\mathbf{o}(K)\right)(K)\leq\left(\bigvee_{K^{\prime}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{K}}(X)}\mathbf{c}(j(K^{\prime}))\wedge\mathbf{o}(K^{\prime})\right)(K),

so the required relation follows from Lemma
7.11
.
∎

Theorem 8.8
.

\mathsf{Patch}(X)
is a Stone locale, for every spectral locale
X
.

Proof.

Compactness

(
ST1
)

was given in Lemma
8.5
.
For

(
ST2
)

, let
j:\mathsf{Patch}(X)
. We take the intensional basis

\displaystyle B\quad

\displaystyle\vcentcolon\equiv\quad\left(\mathbf{c}(K_{1})\wedge\mathbf{o}(K_{2})\right)_{(K_{1},K_{2}):({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{K}}(X)\times{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{K}}(X))}

\displaystyle I_{j}\quad

\displaystyle\vcentcolon\equiv\quad\left(\left(\sum_{K_{1},K_{2}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{K}}(X)}{K_{1}\leq j(K_{2})}\right),\gamma\right)\ \text{where},

\displaystyle\gamma(K_{1},K_{2})\quad

\displaystyle\vcentcolon\equiv\quad\mathbf{c}(K_{1})\wedge\mathbf{o}(K_{2})

Even though this family is not a priori directed, we know that it can be
directified as explained in Remark
3.8
. We therefore
obtain a specified, small, intensional basis of clopens by
Lemma
8.7
. We denote the
directed form of the basis by
B^{\uparrow}
which is small by
Remark
3.8
since
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{K}}(X)
is small by

(
SP4
)

. Since in a compact locale, clopens are compact by
Lemma
4.23
, and so the clopens fall in the
basis by Lemma
4.10
. Therefore, we get the equivalence of types

\mathsf{C}(X)\simeq\mathsf{image}(B^{\uparrow}),

which concludes

(
ST3
)

by Lemma
3.9
.
∎

8.2.
The universal property of
\mathsf{Patch}

We now show that
\mathsf{Patch}
is the right adjoint to the inclusion
\mathbf{Stone}\hookrightarrow\mathbf{Spec}
.

Lemma 8.9
.

Given any spectral map
f:X\to A
from a Stone locale into a

spectral locale
,
define a map
\overline{f}^{*}:\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{Patch}(A))\to\mathcal{O}(X)
by

\bar{f}^{*}(j)\quad\vcentcolon\equiv\quad\bigvee_{K:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{K}}(A)}f^{*}(j(K))\wedge\neg f^{*}(K).

Then, the map
\bar{f}_{*}:\mathcal{O}(X)\to\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{Patch}(A))
defined by

\bar{f}_{*}(V)\quad\vcentcolon\equiv\quad f_{*}\circ\mathbf{c}(V)\circ f^{*}

is the right adjoint of
\bar{f}^{*}
.

Proof.

First, note that
f_{*}\circ\mathbf{c}(V)\circ f^{*}
is indeed a Scott continuous nucleus,
and both
\bar{f}^{*}
and
\bar{f}_{*}
are clearly monotone. Let us first
prove the forward implication.
Assume that for
j:\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{Patch}(A))
and
V:\mathcal{O}(X)

the relation
\bar{f}^{*}(j)\leq V
holds.
By Lemma
7.11
, in order to show that
j\leq\bar{f}_{*}(V)
, it suffices to show that for any compact open
K:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{K}}(A)
, the inequality
j(K)\leq\bar{f}_{*}(V)(K)
holds. Hence, let
K:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{K}}(A)
, and note that

f^{*}(j(K))\wedge\neg f^{*}(K)\leq\bar{f}^{*}(j)\leq V

Notice that
f^{*}(K)
is clopen, by the fact that
f^{*}
is a spectral map and
Lemma
4.24
.
It is therefore complemented in the lattice
\mathcal{O}(X)
, and so
we have
f^{*}(j(K))\leq V\vee f^{*}(K)=\mathbf{c}(V)(f^{*}(K))
, which by
adjunction yields
j(K)\leq f_{*}(\mathbf{c}(V)(f^{*}(K)))=\bar{f}_{*}(K)
, as
required.

Let us now show the reverse implication.
Let
j:\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{Patch}(A))
and
V:\mathcal{O}(X)

and assume that
j\leq\bar{f}_{*}(V)
.
Once again, by the definition of the ordering on
\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{Patch}(A))
, for all

K:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{K}}(A)
we have
j(K)\leq f_{*}(V\vee f^{*}(K))
, which by adjunction
equivalently yields
f^{*}(j(K))\leq V\vee f^{*}(K)
. Since
f^{*}(K)
is clopen, and
hence complemented
in the lattice
\mathcal{O}(X)
it follows that
f^{*}(j(K))\wedge\neg f^{*}(K)\leq V
. Hence,

\bar{f}^{*}(j)\leq V
.
∎

Theorem 8.10
.

Given any spectral map
f:X\to A
from a Stone locale into a

spectral locale
, there exists a unique spectral map
\bar{f}:X\to\mathsf{Patch}(A)

satisfying
\varepsilon\circ\bar{f}=f
, as illustrated in the following diagram in

\mathbf{Spec}
:
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{Patch}(A)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\bar{f}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varepsilon}

Proof.

Assume that a locale map
\bar{f}:X\to\mathsf{Patch}(A)
satisfies the condition in the theorem. Then, for any
j:\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{Patch}(A))
one has

\displaystyle\bar{f}^{*}(j)

\displaystyle\quad=\quad\bar{f}^{*}\left(\bigvee_{K:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{K}}(A)}\mathbf{c}(j(K))\wedge\mathbf{o}(K)\right)

\displaystyle[\text{Lemma~\ref{lem:johnstones-lemma}}]

\displaystyle\quad=\quad\bigvee_{K:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{K}}(A)}\bar{f}^{*}\left(\mathbf{c}(j(K))\wedge\mathbf{o}(K)\right)

\displaystyle[\text{$\bar{f}^{*}$ preserves small joins}]

\displaystyle\quad=\quad\bigvee_{K:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{K}}(A)}\bar{f}^{*}\left(\mathbf{c}(j(K))\right)\wedge\neg\bar{f}^{*}\left(\mathbf{c}(K)\right)

\displaystyle[\text{$\bar{f}^{*}$ preserves binary meets and complements}]

\displaystyle\quad=\quad\bigvee_{K:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{K}}(A)}f^{*}\left(j(K)\right)\wedge\neg f^{*}\left(K\right)

\displaystyle[\text{commutativity of the diagram}]

and hence
\bar{f}
is uniquely determined. If we now define a monotone map
\overline{f}^{*}:\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{Patch}(A))\to\mathcal{O}(X)
by

\bar{f}^{*}(j)\quad\vcentcolon\equiv\quad\bigvee_{K:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{K}}(A)}f^{*}(j(K))\wedge\neg f^{*}(K),

it is easy to show it preserves the top element (namely the top nucleus with constant value
\mathbf{1}_{A}
) because
\mathbf{0}_{A}
is compact. It also preserves binary (pointwise) meets as

\displaystyle\bar{f}^{*}(j_{1})\wedge\bar{f}^{*}(j_{2})

\displaystyle=\bigvee_{K_{1},K_{2}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{K}}(A)}f^{*}\left(j_{1}(K_{1})\right)\wedge\neg f^{*}\left(K_{1}\right)\wedge f^{*}\left(j_{2}(K_{2})\right)\wedge\neg f^{*}\left(K_{2}\right)

\displaystyle[\text{distributivity}]

\displaystyle=\bigvee_{K_{1},K_{2}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{K}}(A)}f^{*}\left(j_{1}(K_{1})\wedge j_{2}(K_{2})\right)\wedge\neg f^{*}\left(K_{1}\right)\wedge\neg f^{*}\left(K_{2}\right)

\displaystyle[\text{$f^{*}$ preserves binary meets}]

\displaystyle\leq\bigvee_{K_{1},K_{2}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{K}}(A)}f^{*}\left(j_{1}(K_{1}\vee K_{2})\wedge j_{2}(K_{1}\vee K_{2})\right)\wedge\neg f^{*}\left(K_{1}\right)\wedge\neg f^{*}\left(K_{2}\right)

\displaystyle[\text{monotonicity}]

\displaystyle=\bigvee_{K_{1},K_{2}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{K}}(A)}f^{*}\left((j_{1}\wedge j_{2})(K_{1}\vee K_{2})\right)\wedge\neg f^{*}\left(K_{1}\right)\wedge\neg f^{*}\left(K_{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\bigvee_{K_{1},K_{2}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{K}}(A)}f^{*}\left((j_{1}\wedge j_{2})(K_{1}\vee K_{2})\right)\wedge\neg f^{*}\left(K_{1}\vee K_{2}\right)

\displaystyle[\text{De Morgan law}]

\displaystyle=\bar{f}^{*}(j_{1}\wedge j_{2}).

\displaystyle[\text{${\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{K}}(X)$ closed under $(\mathchar 45\relax)\wedge(\mathchar 45\relax)$}]

Moreover, Lemma
8.9
and Theorem
5.2
ensure that
\bar{f}^{*}
preserves small joins and so it is a frame homomorphism.

Let us finally show that
\bar{f}
makes the diagram commute. Since compact opens form a small basis of
A
, it suffices to show that
\bar{f}^{*}(\mathbf{c}(K))=f^{*}(K)
for any
K:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{K}}(A)
. Let
K:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{K}}(A)
and note that

\displaystyle\bar{f}^{*}(\mathbf{c}(K))

\displaystyle\quad=\quad\bigvee_{K^{\prime}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{K}}(A)}f^{*}\left(K\vee K^{\prime}\right)\wedge\neg f^{*}\left(K^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle\quad=\quad\bigvee_{K^{\prime}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{K}}(A)}f^{*}\left(K\right)\wedge\neg f^{*}\left(K^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle[\text{$f^{*}$ preserves binary joins}]

\displaystyle\quad=\quad f^{*}(K)\wedge\bigvee_{K^{\prime}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{K}}(A)}\neg f^{*}\left(K^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle[\text{distributivity}]

\displaystyle\quad=\quad f^{*}(K)\wedge\mathbf{1}_{X}

\displaystyle[\mathbf{0}_{X}\ \text{is compact}]

\displaystyle\quad=\quad f^{*}(K),

as required.
∎

9.
Summary and discussion

We have constructed the patch locale of a spectral locale in the predicative and
constructive setting of univalent type theory. Furthermore, we have shown that
the patch functor

\mathsf{Patch}:\mathbf{Spec}\rightarrow\mathbf{Stone}

is the right adjoint to the
inclusion
\mathbf{Stone}\hookrightarrow\mathbf{Spec}
which is to say that
\mathsf{Patch}
exhibits the category

\mathbf{Stone}
as a coreflective subcategory of
\mathbf{Spec}
.
As we have elaborated on in Section
4
, answering this
question in a predicative setting involves several new ingredients, compared to
Escardó, 1999
;
Escardó, 2001
:

(1)

We have reformulated the notions of spectrality and Stone-ness in our
predicative type-theoretic setting and have shown that crucial topological
facts about these notions remain valid under these reformulations.

(2)

We have developed several notions that capture the locale-theoretic
notion of a basis and have shown their equivalences in
Theorem
4.17
and in Theorem
4.30
.

(3)

In Escardó’s
(
Escardó, 1999
)
construction of the
patch locale, the proof of the universal property relies on the existence of the
frame of
all
nuclei. As it is not clear that the poset of all nuclei
can be shown to form a frame predicatively, we developed a new proof of the universal
property using Lemma
8.9
, which is completely
independent of the existence of the frame of all nuclei.

We have formalized all of our development, most importantly
Theorem
8.8
and Theorem
8.10
. The formalization
has been carried out by the third-named
author
(
Tosun, 2023
)
as part of the
TypeTopology

library
(
Escardó and contributors, 2023
)
.

In previous work
(
Escardó, 1999
;
Escardó, 2001
)
, which forms the basis
of the present work, the patch construction was used to

(1)

exhibit
\mathbf{Stone}
as a coreflective subcategory of
\mathbf{Spec}
, and

(2)

exhibit the category of compact regular locales and continuous maps as a
coreflective subcategory of of stably compact locales and perfect maps.

In our work, we have focused on item (
1
). The question of
taking a predicative approach to item (
2
) was previously
tackled by
Coquand and Zhang, 2003
using formal topology. We conjecture that it is
possible to instead use the approach we have presented here, namely, working
with locales with small bases and constructing the patch as the frame of
Scott continuous nuclei. It should also be possible to show predicatively that the category
of small distributive lattices is dually equivalent to the category of spectral
locales as defined in this paper.
