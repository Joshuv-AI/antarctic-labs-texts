Efficiency in local differential privacy
Lukas Steinberger

Abstract

Even though efficient parameter estimation is a classical and well-studied problem in mathematical statistics, it leads to several non-trivial obstacles that need to be tackled when dealing with the LDP case. This result readily implies convolution and local asymptotic minimax theorems. In case p = 1 p=1 , the optimal asymptotic variance is found to be the inverse of the supremal Fisher-Information sup Q ∈ 𝒬 α I θ ​ ( Q ​ 𝒫 ) ∈ ℝ \sup_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}}I_{\theta}(Q{\mathcal{P}})\in{\mathbb{R}} , where the supremum runs over all α \alpha -differentially private (marginal) Markov kernels. Introduction

Consider iid data
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
from a probability distribution
P_{\theta}
belonging to a regular parametric model
{\mathcal{P}}=(P_{\theta})_{\theta\in\Theta}
, with parameter space
\Theta\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
, sample space
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{F})
and with Fisher-Information
I_{\theta}({\mathcal{P}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times p}
. If the data owners are to be protected by (non-interactive)
local
differential privacy, the analyst who wants to estimate the unknown parameter
\theta
does not get to see these original data but only observes sanitized versions
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}
such that individual
i
was able to generate
Z_{i}
using its sensitive data
X_{i}
and a (publicly known) Markov kernel
Q
that determines the conditional distribution
Z_{i}|X_{i}=x\thicksim Q(dz|x)
and obeys the
\alpha
-differential privacy constraint

Q(A|x)\leq e^{\alpha}\cdot Q(A|x^{\prime}),

(1.1)

for all
A
in a sigma algebra
\mathcal{G}
on some sample space
\mathcal{Z}
and for all
x,x^{\prime}
in the original sample space
\mathcal{X}
. Thus, the observed data
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}
are iid from
QP_{\theta}
, where
[QP_{\theta}](A)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}Q(A|x)P_{\theta}(dx)
. We will see below that the resulting statistical model
Q{\mathcal{P}}=(QP_{\theta})_{\theta\in\Theta}
is again regular with Fisher-Information denoted by
I_{\theta}(Q{\mathcal{P}})
. Hence, under standard regularity conditions, the MLE in
Q{\mathcal{P}}
is asymptotically distributed as
N(0,I_{\theta}(Q{\mathcal{P}})^{-1})
and it is well known to be efficient, i.e., this asymptotic covariance matrix is the smallest (in the partial Loewner ordering) that any (regular) estimator in the model
Q{\mathcal{P}}
can achieve. However, we are actually free to choose the privacy mechanism
Q
, as long as it satisfies (
1.1
). It is therefore natural to ask, at least in the case
p=1
, what is the mechanism
Q
that leads to the smallest asymptotic variance, or, in other words, to look for solutions of the optimization problem

\sup_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}}I_{\theta}(Q{\mathcal{P}}),

(1.2)

where
\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}:=\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{X}):=\bigcup_{(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{G})}\Big\{Q:\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{X}\to[0,1]\Big|Q
is a Markov kernel satisfying (
1.1
)
\Big\}
is the set of all
\alpha
-private marginal data release mechanisms. The problem (
1.2
) is challenging, because the constraint set is an infinite dimensional set of Markov kernels with even unspecified domain
\mathcal{Z}
and the supremum may not be attained. Moreover, we are trying to
maximize
a convex function over a convex domain and thus we expect to see many local maxima.
Furthermore, a (near) maximizer will typically depend on the unknown parameter
\theta
and the resulting privacy mechanism,
Q_{\theta}
say, is also unknown and can not be shared with the data owners to generate sanitized data
Z_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,n
.

In this paper we develop the theory of statistical efficiency for local differential privacy, extending the classical approach due to
Le Cam 1960
,
Hájek 1970
and
Jeganathan 1980
. We study regularity properties of locally private models, prove a general local asymptotic mixed normality result and derive a private convolution theorem. Finally, we prove exact attainability of the asymptotic lower bound. In particular, we develop an algorithm based on discretization of the model
{\mathcal{P}}
and a linear program proposed by
Kairouz, Oh and Viswanath 2016
to find a privacy mechanism (Markov kernel)
\hat{Q}_{\theta}
such that
I_{\theta}(\hat{Q}_{\theta}{\mathcal{P}})\approx\sup_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}}I_{\theta}(Q{\mathcal{P}})
. We employ a two-step (sequentially interactive) procedure that first consistently estimates
\theta
in an
\alpha
-private way with an estimator
\tilde{\theta}_{n_{1}}
based on a subsample
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n_{1}}
generated by some preliminary privacy mechanism
Q_{0}
. The individuals in the second part of the sample then use
\hat{Q}_{\tilde{\theta}_{n_{1}}}
to generate
Z_{n_{1}+1},\dots,Z_{n}
in an
\alpha
-private way. The MLE
\hat{\theta}_{n_{2}}
in the model
(\hat{Q}_{\tilde{\theta}_{n_{1}}}{\mathcal{P}})^{n_{2}}
is finally shown to be regular with asymptotic distribution
N(0,[\sup_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}}I_{\theta}(Q{\mathcal{P}})]^{-1})
(see Figure
1
for a graphical representation of this procedure). Notice that since this procedure is sequentially interactive rather than simply non-interactive (that is, the local randomization mechanism
\hat{Q}_{\tilde{\theta}_{n_{1}}}
used by each individual in the second group depends on the outcome of previous randomizations from the first group), it is no longer clear whether the reciprocal of (
1.2
) is indeed the smallest possible asymptotic variance among all mechanisms within this larger class of procedures. Thus, to establish asymptotic efficiency, we prove local asymptotic mixed normality (along subsequences) for every model of the form
Q^{(n)}{\mathcal{P}}^{n}=(Q^{(n)}P_{\theta}^{n})_{\theta\in\Theta}
,
\Theta\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
, where
{\mathcal{P}}
is regular (differentiable in quadratic mean) and
Q^{(n)}
is an arbitrary sequence of sequentially-interactive
\alpha
-differential privacy mechanisms. This result implies convolution and local asymptotic minimax theorems which show that the reciprocal of the expression in (
1.2
) is, indeed, the minimal asymptotic variance. Since our two-step procedure belongs to this class of sequentially-interactive mechanisms and we show that it is regular with the minimal asymptotic variance, it is, indeed, asymptotically efficient.

The paper is organized as follows. We finish this section with a discussion of related literature and we introduce the necessary notation for our exposition. In Section
2
we consider two very basic statistical models, the Bernoulli
(\theta)
and the Binomial
(2,\theta)
model, to illustrate some of the challenges a theory of statistical efficiency is facing under local differential privacy. In Section
3
we study how differentiability in quadratic mean (DQM) of statistical models for original sensitive data
X_{i}
is inherited to the model describing the sanitized data
Z_{i}
in the non-interactive case. We then establish local asymptotic mixed normality (LAMN) along subsequences for every model
Q^{(n)}{\mathcal{P}}^{n}
, where
{\mathcal{P}}
is DQM and
Q^{(n)}
is any sequence of sequentially interactive privacy mechanisms. Given this result, we can connect to the classical literature on LAMN models and immediately obtain asymptotic convolution and local asymptotic minimax theorems. In Section
4
we show that the lower bounds of the convolution and local minimax theorems can actually be attained by a two-step MLE approach. The crucial step towards this goal is to approximately solve (
1.2
) using discretization. We also show that an appropriate discretization generally exists. Finally, in Section
5
we consider the special cases of the Gaussian location and the Gaussian scale model where we can provide simple explicit discretizations. In these examples we also demonstrate that our numerical algorithm, which is based on the linear program of
Kairouz, Oh and Viswanath 2016
, is computationally feasible. Most technical results and proofs are collected in the supplementary material.

1.1
Related literature

Differential privacy and its
local
version, where there is no need for a trusted third party, has originated some 20 years ago in the computer science literature
(
Dinur and Nissim 2003
;
Dwork and Nissim 2004
;
Dwork et al. 2006
;
Dwork 2008
;
Evfimievski, Gehrke and
Srikant 2003
, cf.)
as a means for privacy protection that is insusceptible to any type of adversary. It took a few years until mathematical statisticians started to investigate the new notion, which is an inherently statistical one, and groundbreaking first contributions are
Wasserman and Zhou 2010
and
Duchi, Jordan and Wainwright 2018
. Recently, however, the statistical literature on differential privacy has exploded. Consider, for example,
Rohde and Steinberger 2020
;
Butucea et al. 2020
;
Butucea, Rohde and
Steinberger 2022
;
Duchi and Ruan 2023
;
Lalanne, Garivier and
Gribonval 2022
;
Acharya et al. 2022
;
Cai, Wang and Zhang 2021
;
Dunsche, Kutta and Dette 2022
, to mention only a few of the most recent contributions. Nevertheless some of the most basic statistical questions are still open under differential privacy. One such problem is that of asymptotically efficient parameter estimation. In the central paradigm of differential privacy, efficient estimation has been addressed early on by works of
Smith 2008
;
Smith 2011
. However, in the statistically more challenging local paradigm, where there is no trusted data curator, no analog to the classical theory of statistical efficiency has been available so far. An interesting contribution towards such a direction is
Barnes, Chen and
Özgür 2020
, who investigate and bound Fisher-Information when original data are perturbed using a differentially private randomization mechanism. But those authors have not attempted to find an optimal randomization mechanism or a subsequent estimation procedure. The work probably closest to our own approach is
Duchi and Ruan 2023
. They investigate the local minimax risk when data are subject to a local differential privacy constraint. They develop a new information quantity they call the
L^{1}
-information and use it to characterize the local minimax risk in the regular parametric case. However, while they are more ambitious than our present investigations in that they aim also at potentially non-parametric models, they can only characterize the difficulty of a regular parametric estimation problem up to certain numerical constants and thus all their attainability results are also only up to numerical factors. Hence, the question of existence and construction of asymptotically efficient (asymptotic minimum variance) locally private estimation procedures is still open. In this paper we begin to develop the statistical theory of asymptotic efficiency for local differential privacy by starting with simple regular parametric statistical models. This objective already poses several non-trivial challenges that need to be carefully addressed. For traditional results on statistical efficiency we mainly rely on the well established theory as presented in
van der Vaart 2007
and
Höpfner 2014
.

1.2
Preliminaries and notation

Let
({\mathcal{Z}}_{1},\mathcal{G}_{1}),\dots,({\mathcal{Z}}_{n},\mathcal{G}_{n})
,
({\mathcal{Z}},\mathcal{G})
,
({\mathcal{Z}}^{\prime},\mathcal{G}^{\prime})
and
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{F})
be measurable spaces and write
\mathcal{Z}^{(n)}:=\mathcal{Z}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathcal{Z}_{n}
and
\mathcal{G}^{(n)}:=\mathcal{G}_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathcal{G}_{n}
. Moreover, we use the conventions that
\mathcal{Z}^{(0)}=\varnothing=\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
and
A\times\varnothing=A
for any set
A
. Let
\mathfrak{P}({\mathcal{Z}})=\mathfrak{P}({\mathcal{Z}},\mathcal{G})
be the set of all probability measures on
(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{G})
and
\mathfrak{P}(\mathcal{X}\to{\mathcal{Z}})
be the set of all Markov kernels
Q:\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{X}\to[0,1]
from
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{F})
to
({\mathcal{Z}},\mathcal{G})
. Note that we can always think of a Markov kernel
Q\in\mathfrak{P}(\mathcal{X}\to{\mathcal{Z}})
as a linear mapping from
\mathfrak{P}(\mathcal{X})
to
\mathfrak{P}({\mathcal{Z}})
by defining
P\mapsto QP
as
[QP](dz):=\int_{\mathcal{X}}Q(dz|x)\,P(dx)
. In the same sense we can also consider compositions
[QR](dz|x)=\int_{{\mathcal{Z}}^{\prime}}Q(dz|z^{\prime})\,R(dz^{\prime}|x)
of Markov kernels
R\in\mathfrak{P}(\mathcal{X}\to{\mathcal{Z}}^{\prime})
and
Q\in\mathfrak{P}({\mathcal{Z}}^{\prime}\to{\mathcal{Z}})
. For
P\in\mathfrak{P}(\mathcal{X})
, we easily see that
Q[RP]=[QR]P
and thus it is allowed to just write
QRP
. For a statistical model
{\mathcal{P}}\subseteq\mathfrak{P}(\mathcal{X})
we also write
Q{\mathcal{P}}=\{QP:P\in{\mathcal{P}}\}
for the model obtained by applying
Q
to all original probability measures in
{\mathcal{P}}
. Notice that any measurable function
T:\mathcal{X}\to{\mathcal{Z}}
can also be represented as a (degenerate) Markov kernel
R_{T}\in\mathfrak{P}(\mathcal{X}\to{\mathcal{Z}})
by defining
R_{T}(dz|x)
to be the Dirac measure
\delta_{T(x)}
at
T(x)
. Hence, we sometimes write
T{\mathcal{P}}:=R_{T}{\mathcal{P}}
which is easily seen to coincide with the push-forward model
\{PT^{-1}:P\in{\mathcal{P}}\}
, since
[R_{T}P](A)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\delta_{T(x)}(A)\,P(dx)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mathds{1}_{T^{-1}(A)}(x)\,P(dx)=PT^{-1}(A)
. Similarly, we can also make sense of the composition of a Markov kernel
Q\in\mathfrak{P}({\mathcal{Z}}^{\prime}\to{\mathcal{Z}})
and the measurable function
T:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
, by setting
[QT](dz|x):=[QR_{T}](dz|x)=Q(dz|T(x))
. Throughout, we write
J_{p}
for the
p\times p
identity matrix and
\|\cdot\|_{r}
to denote the
\ell_{r}
as well as the
L_{r}(\mu)
norm whenever the meaning is clear from the context. We also use simply
\|\cdot\|
to denote the Euclidean norm of vectors and the spectral norm for matrices and we write
\preccurlyeq
to denote the partial Loewner ordering of matrices. If
\mathcal{P}=(P_{\theta})_{\theta\in\Theta}
is a parametric model with
\Theta\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
that is dominated by
\mu
, we write
p_{\theta}=\frac{dP_{\theta}}{d\mu}
and
{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta}
for a
\mu
-density and for the expectation operator with respect to
P_{\theta}
, respectively. If
V
is a random
p
-vector we also write
\Var[V]
to denote the corresponding
p\times p
covariance matrix. Finally,
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle R_{n}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}
denotes weak convergence with respect to the sequence of probability measures
(R_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
.

1.2.1
Sequentially interactive and non-interactive differential privacy

For
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
, define
d_{0}(x,x^{\prime}):=|\{i:x_{i}\neq x_{i}^{\prime}\}|
to be the Hamming distance, i.e., the number of differing components in
x
and
x^{\prime}
. Recall that for
\alpha\in(0,\infty)
, a Markov kernel
Q\in\mathfrak{P}(\mathcal{X}^{n}\to{\mathcal{Z}})
is called
\alpha
-differentially private
, if, for all
A\in\mathcal{G}
and for all
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
with
d_{0}(x,x^{\prime})=1
, we have

Q(A|x)\;\leq\;e^{\alpha}\,Q(A|x^{\prime}).

(1.3)

We sometimes also refer to such a Markov kernel as a
privacy mechanism
or a
channel
.
Note that this definition implies that the probability measures
Q(dz|x)
, for different
x\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
, are mutually absolutely continuous (equivalent). In particular, if
{\mathcal{P}}\subseteq\mathfrak{P}(\mathcal{X}^{n})
is a statistical model on
\mathcal{X}^{n}
, then all the measures
QP
, for
P\in{\mathcal{P}}
, are equivalent and they are also equivalent to
Q(dz|x)
, for any
x\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
. We now turn to
local differential privacy
, that is, the situation where there is no trusted data curator available and each individual is able to generate a sanitized version
Z_{i}
of their sensitive data
X_{i}
on their
local
machine without ever sharing
X_{i}
with anybody else.

First, we define the set of marginal
\alpha
-differentially private channels by

\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Z}):=\left\{Q\in\mathfrak{P}(\mathcal{X}\to{\mathcal{Z}}):Q(A|x)\leq e^{\alpha}Q(A|x^{\prime}),\;\forall A\in\mathcal{G},\forall x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}\right\}.

(1.4)

When searching for an optimal privacy mechanism, we want to impose no a priori restrictions on the space
(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{G})
from which the sanitized observations are drawn. Thus, we also consider the union over all possible measurable output spaces

\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}:=\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{X}):=\bigcup_{(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{G})}\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Z}).

(1.5)

The elements of
\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}
are the basic building blocks of locally private channels, because they can be executed by a single individual independently of anybody else, using only their own information
X_{i}=x
and producing a sanitized version
Z_{i}\thicksim Q(dz|x)
.
We also introduce two specific classes of
\alpha
-locally differentially private mechanisms. A Markov kernel
Q^{(n)}\in\mathfrak{P}(\mathcal{X}^{n}\to{\mathcal{Z}}^{(n)})
is said to be
sequentially interactive
if the following condition is satisfied: For every
i=1,\dots,n
, there exists a Markov kernel
Q_{i}\in\mathfrak{P}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Z}^{(i-1)}\to\mathcal{Z}_{i})
, such that for all
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in\mathcal{X}
,

\displaystyle Q^{(n)}\left(dz_{1:n}\Big|x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\right)=Q_{z_{1:n-1}}(dz_{n}|x_{n})Q_{z_{1:n-2}}(dz_{n-1}|x_{n-1})\cdots Q_{\varnothing}(dz_{1}|x_{1}),

(1.6)

where
Q_{z_{1:i-1}}(dz_{i}|x_{i}):=Q_{i}(dz_{i}|x_{i},z_{1:i-1})
,
z_{1:0}=\varnothing
and
z_{1:i}=(z_{1},\dots,z_{i})^{T}\in\mathcal{Z}^{(i)}
.
Here, the idea is that individual
i
can only use
X_{i}
and previous
Z_{j}
,
j<i
, in its local privacy mechanism, thus leading to the sequential structure in the above definition. If, in addition, for all
i\in[n]
and for all
z_{1:i-1}\in\mathcal{Z}^{(i-1)}
we have
Q_{z_{1:i-1}}\in\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Z}_{i})
, then, by the usual approximation of integrands by simple functions, it is easy to see that
Q
in (
1.6
) is
\alpha
-differentially private (i.e., satisfies (
1.3
)), in which case we call it
\alpha
-
sequentially interactive
. This notion coincides with the definition of sequentially interactive channels in
Duchi, Jordan and Wainwright 2018
and
Rohde and Steinberger 2020
.

An important subclass of sequentially interactive mechanisms are the so called
non-interactive
mechanisms
Q\in\mathfrak{P}(\mathcal{X}^{n}\to\mathcal{Z}^{(n)})
that are of product form

Q\left(dz_{1:n}\Big|x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\right)=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}Q_{i}(dz_{i}|x_{i}),\quad\quad\forall x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in\mathcal{X},

(1.7)

for some marginal kernels
Q_{i}\in\mathfrak{P}(\mathcal{X}\to{\mathcal{Z}}_{i})
.
Clearly, a non-interactive mechanism
Q
satisfies (
1.3
) if, and only if, for all
i=1,\dots,n
,
Q_{i}\in\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}
. In that case it is also called
\alpha
-non-interactive
.

2
Two simple illustrative examples

We begin our exposition by considering two very basic statistical models and investigate the possibility of efficient parameter estimation in these special cases in order to highlight some of the obstacles a general theory of efficiency has to overcome.

2.1
Bernoulli data

Perhaps one of the most basic models of statistics is the simple Bernoulli experiment with unknown success probability
\theta\in\Theta=(0,1)
and (marginal) sample space
\mathcal{X}=\{0,1\}
, that is,
p_{\theta}(x)=\theta^{x}(1-\theta)^{1-x}
,
x\in\mathcal{X}
. The model
{\mathcal{P}}=(p_{\theta})_{\theta\in\Theta}
is regular in the sense of differentiability in quadratic mean (see Definition
1
below) with score
s_{\theta}
and has Fisher-Information
I_{\theta}({\mathcal{P}})=[\theta(1-\theta)]^{-1}
. For arbitrary
Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{X})
, the model
Q{\mathcal{P}}
of the sanitized data
Z
is also regular with Fisher-Information denoted by
I_{\theta}(Q{\mathcal{P}})
(cf. Lemma
3.1
below).
We now show that the
\alpha
-private channel
Q_{0}\in\mathfrak{P}(\{0,1\}\to\{0,1\})
defined by its matrix representation

\displaystyle Q_{0}\triangleq\begin{pmatrix}e^{\alpha}&1\\
1&e^{\alpha}\end{pmatrix}\frac{1}{e^{\alpha}+1},

that is,
Q_{0}(0|0)=\frac{e^{\alpha}}{e^{\alpha}+1}=Q_{0}(1|1)
, maximizes the Fisher-Information
Q\mapsto I_{\theta}(Q{\mathcal{P}})
over all of
\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}
and for every
\theta\in(0,1)
. The channel
Q_{0}
actually coincides with Warner’s original randomized response mechanism
(
Warner 1965
)
. The following argument is inspired by
Kairouz, Oh and Viswanath 2016
.

Fix a mechanism
Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{X})
. For
\nu(dz):=Q(dz|0)
, choose
\nu
-densities
q(z|0)
and
q(z|1)
of
Q(dz|0)
and
Q(dz|1)
, respectively. In particular,
q(z|0)=1
. Consider the formal matrix inverse of the randomized response mechanism
Q_{0}
, that is,

\displaystyle Q_{0}^{-1}\triangleq\begin{pmatrix}e^{\alpha}&-1\\
-1&e^{\alpha}\end{pmatrix}\frac{1}{e^{\alpha}-1}.

Even though
Q_{0}^{-1}
does not define a Markov kernel (as it contains negative values), we can still study the composition
\bar{Q}:=QQ_{0}^{-1}
, which we define by its
\nu
-densities
\bar{q}(z|x):=\sum_{y\in\{0,1\}}q(z|y)Q_{0}^{-1}(y|x)=\frac{1}{e^{\alpha}-1}\left[q(z|x)e^{\alpha}-q(z|1-x)\right]
. Thus,
\bar{Q}(A|x)=\frac{1}{e^{\alpha}-1}\left[Q(A|x)e^{\alpha}-Q(A|1-x)\right]\geq 0
,
\bar{Q}({\mathcal{Z}}|x)=1
,
A\mapsto\bar{Q}(A|x)
is
\sigma
-additive and
x\mapsto\bar{Q}(A|x)
is measurbale, that is,
\bar{Q}
is a Markov kernel. Lemma
3.1
yields that both
Q_{0}\mathcal{P}
and
Q\mathcal{P}
are regular with scores
r_{\theta}
and
t_{\theta}
, say. By construction
\bar{Q}Q_{0}=Q
, which means that
(Z,X)\thicksim Q(dz|x)P_{\theta}(dx)
is the
(Z,X)
-marginal distribution of
(Z,Y,X)\thicksim\bar{Q}(dz|y)Q_{0}(dy|x)P_{\theta}(dx)
. Moreover,
X
and
Z
are conditionally independent given
Y
. Thus, in view of Lemma
3.1
,

\displaystyle I_{\theta}(Q\mathcal{P})

\displaystyle=\Var(t_{\theta}(Z))=\Var({\mathbb{E}}[s_{\theta}(X)|Z])=\Var({\mathbb{E}}[{\mathbb{E}}[s_{\theta}(X)|Y,Z]|Z])

\displaystyle=\Var({\mathbb{E}}[{\mathbb{E}}[s_{\theta}(X)|Y]|Z])=\Var({\mathbb{E}}[r_{\theta}(Y)|Z])

\displaystyle=\Var(r_{\theta}(Y))-{\mathbb{E}}[\Var(r_{\theta}(Y)|Z)]\leq\Var(r_{\theta}(Y))=I_{\theta}(Q_{0}\mathcal{P}).

Since
Q
was arbitrary, optimality of
Q_{0}
is established.

It is easy to see that

I_{\theta}(Q_{0}{\mathcal{P}})=\left[\frac{e^{\alpha}}{(e^{\alpha}-1)^{2}}+\theta(1-\theta)\right]^{-1}.

Moreover, if
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}
are iid from
Q_{0}P_{\theta}
(note that such a sample can be generated in an
\alpha
-non-interactive way by letting all
n
individuals use the same
Q_{0}
independently to generate
Z_{i}\thicksim Q_{0}(dz|x=X_{i})
), then the estimator

\hat{\theta}_{\text{priv}}:=\frac{e^{\alpha}+1}{e^{\alpha}-1}\left(\bar{Z}_{n}-\frac{1}{e^{\alpha}+1}\right),

is UMVUE among all non-interactive
\alpha
-private procedures, reaching the (minimal) Cramér-Rao lower bound of
I_{\theta}(Q_{0}{\mathcal{P}})^{-1}
, which is the smallest lower bound we can hope for in view of our choice
Q_{0}
. Thus, in the simple Bernoulli model, efficient estimation is possible even in finite samples.

Remark 2.1

(On the
L^{1}
-information of
Duchi and Ruan 2023
)
.

In a setup similar to ours – yet a more ambitious semi-parametric one –
Duchi and Ruan 2023
proposed the so-called
L^{1}
-information as an alternative to Fisher-Information as a complexity measure in local differential privacy. It is defined as
\mathbb{I}_{\theta}({\mathcal{P}}):={\mathbb{E}}_{\theta}[|s_{\theta}|]
, where
s_{\theta}
is the score at
\theta
in the model
{\mathcal{P}}
. One may wonder if this quantity, which was shown to play an important role in determining the rate of convergence in semi-parametric problems, also characterizes the difficulty of parametric estimation problems and how it compares to the optimal Fisher-Information
I_{\theta,\alpha}^{*}({\mathcal{P}}):=\sup_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{X})}I_{\theta}(Q{\mathcal{P}})
. From the risk bounds in
Duchi and Ruan 2023
one might conjecture that
4\mathbb{I}_{\theta}({\mathcal{P}})^{-2}
is the asymptotic variance of
\sqrt{n\alpha^{2}}(\hat{\theta}_{n}-\theta)
, for an
\alpha
-locally private and efficient estimator
\hat{\theta}_{n}
. This means that
\sqrt{n}(\hat{\theta}_{n}-\theta)
has asymptotic variance
\frac{4}{\alpha^{2}}\mathbb{I}_{\theta}({\mathcal{P}})^{-2}
. Our theory (see the discussion in Section
3.3
) predicts that this variance is equal to
I_{\theta,\alpha}^{*}({\mathcal{P}})^{-1}
. We can evaluate these expressions at least in the Bernoulli example, where we obtain
\frac{4}{\alpha^{2}}\mathbb{I}_{\theta}({\mathcal{P}})^{-2}=\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}
, whereas

I_{\theta,\alpha}^{*}({\mathcal{P}})^{-1}=\frac{e^{\alpha}}{(e^{\alpha}-1)^{2}}+\theta(1-\theta)
. Although the two quantities are clearly not equal for all
\theta\in\Theta
even after arbitrary rescaling, we see that at least in the high-privacy regime
\alpha\to 0
, we have

\frac{I_{\theta,\alpha}^{*}({\mathcal{P}})^{-1}}{\frac{4}{\alpha^{2}}\mathbb{I}_{\theta}({\mathcal{P}})^{-2}}\quad\xrightarrow[\alpha\to 0]{}\quad 1.

Hence, we might wonder if the
L^{1}
-information can at least characterize the optimal variance for small values of
\alpha
.

2.2
Binomial
(2,\theta)
data

Suppose now that the model
{\mathcal{P}}=(p_{\theta})_{\theta\in\Theta}
is only slightly more complicated than above and defined by
p_{\theta}(x)=\binom{2}{x}\theta^{x}(1-\theta)^{2-x}
, for
x\in\mathcal{X}=\{0,1,2\}
and
\theta\in\Theta=(0,1)
. By analogy with the Bernoulli model, an intuitively appealing candidate for the optimal
\alpha
-private channel distribution in this case is

\displaystyle Q_{0}\triangleq\begin{pmatrix}e^{\alpha}&1&1\\
1&e^{\alpha}&1\\
1&1&e^{\alpha}\end{pmatrix}\frac{1}{e^{\alpha}+2}.

However, at least for certain values of the parameter
\theta
, this can be improved. In fact,
Hucke 2019
showed
(
Kairouz, Oh and Viswanath 2016
, using results from)
that the following mechanism maximizes
Q\mapsto I_{\theta}(Q{\mathcal{P}})
over all
\alpha
-private channels with finite state space
{\mathcal{Z}}
:

\displaystyle Q_{\theta}^{*}=\begin{cases}\begin{pmatrix}e^{\alpha}&1&1\\
1&e^{\alpha}&e^{\alpha}\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\frac{1}{e^{\alpha}+1},&\text{if }0<\theta\leq\frac{1}{2}-c_{\alpha},\\
\begin{pmatrix}e^{\alpha}&1&1\\
1&e^{\alpha}&1\\
1&1&e^{\alpha}\end{pmatrix}\frac{1}{e^{\alpha}+2},&\text{if }\frac{1}{2}-c_{\alpha}<\theta<\frac{1}{2}+c_{\alpha},\\
\begin{pmatrix}e^{\alpha}&e^{\alpha}&1\\
1&1&e^{\alpha}\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\frac{1}{e^{\alpha}+1},&\text{if }\frac{1}{2}+c_{\alpha}\leq\theta<1,\end{cases}

(2.1)

for a constant
c_{\alpha}\in[0,\frac{1}{2})
that satisfies
c_{\alpha}=0
if, and only if,
\alpha\leq\log(3)
. In view of Lemma
4.9
below, the restriction of finite state space can be dropped and the channel
Q_{\theta}^{*}
is even optimal among all
\alpha
-private mechanisms in
\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{X})
.
Hucke 2019
also showed that if
\alpha<\log(3)
, then
I_{\theta}(Q_{0}{\mathcal{P}})<I_{\theta}(Q_{\theta}^{*}{\mathcal{P}})
, for all
\theta\in(0,1)
, showing that in the high privacy regime the intuitive choice
Q_{0}
is suboptimal at every point of the parameter space. Furthermore, irrespective of the value of
\alpha\in(0,\infty)
, there can not be another channel
Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{X})
that is independent of
\theta
and satisfies
I_{\theta}(Q{\mathcal{P}})=I_{\theta}(Q_{\theta}^{*}{\mathcal{P}})
, for all
\theta\in(0,1)
. To see this last claim, we first note that
Hucke 2019
also established non-differentiability of
\theta\mapsto I_{\theta}(Q_{\theta}^{*}{\mathcal{P}})
at
\frac{1}{2}-c_{\alpha}
and
\frac{1}{2}+c_{\alpha}
. However, Lemma
D.1
in the appendix shows that
\theta\mapsto I_{\theta}(Q{\mathcal{P}})
is differentiable on
(0,1)
for every channel
Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}(X)
.

This analysis leads to two conclusions. First, somewhat surprisingly, it can be beneficial to consider channels with smaller output/state space
{\mathcal{Z}}
than input/sample space
\mathcal{X}
, at least in some parts of the parameter space, as is the case in (
2.1
), where for
\theta
close to zero and one,
Q^{*}_{\theta}
has an entire row containing only zeros. Second, no non-interactive
\alpha
-differentially private mechanism with identical marginals can be optimal for estimation over the entire parameter space. The optimal (marginal) choice
Q_{\theta}^{*}
necessarily depends on the unknown parameter
\theta
and any a priori choice
Q^{*}_{\theta_{0}}
will be suboptimal for certain other parameters
\theta\neq\theta_{0}
. The last observation naturally leads to the idea of a data dependent choice of channel

Q_{\tilde{\theta}_{n_{1}}}^{*}

based on some preliminary
\alpha
-private estimator
\tilde{\theta}_{n_{1}}
from sanitized data
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n_{1}}
. Thus, the randomization mechanism used by the second group
i=n_{1}+1,\dots,n
of the data owners must depend on sanitized data from the first group of data providers. In other words, if we want to (at least asymptotically) reach the minimal variance
I_{\theta}(Q_{\theta}^{*}{\mathcal{P}})^{-1}
, we have to consider sequentially interactive mechanisms.

Notice, however, that turning to sequentially interactive procedures, we have left the class of statistical procedures within which we were trying to find an optimal one. In this section so far we had only considered iid models for data vectors
X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
of the form
{\mathcal{P}}^{n}=(P_{\theta}^{n})_{\theta\in\Theta}
and marginal mechanisms
Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}
that can be used by each data owner independently. Hence, we only considered non-interactive channels with identical marginals
Q^{(n)}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}Q_{i}
,
Q_{i}=Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}
. Therefore, the model of the sanitized observations
Z=(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n})
was again an iid model
Q^{(n)}{\mathcal{P}}^{n}=(Q^{(n)}P_{\theta}^{n})_{\theta\in\Theta}=([QP_{\theta}]^{n})_{\theta\in\Theta}
and the joint Fisher-Information is simply
I_{\theta}(Q^{(n)}{\mathcal{P}}^{n})=nI_{\theta}(Q{\mathcal{P}})
. So the question arises if it may be possible to carefully pick an
\alpha
-sequentially-interactive channel
Q^{(n)}
such that
\frac{1}{n}I_{\theta}(Q^{(n)}{\mathcal{P}}^{n})>\sup_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}}I_{\theta}(Q{\mathcal{P}})
. However, if the original data model is of the iid form
{\mathcal{P}}^{n}
, this hope is not justified, because one can show that

\frac{1}{n}I_{\theta}(Q^{(n)}{\mathcal{P}}^{n})\leq\sup_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}}I_{\theta}(Q{\mathcal{P}}).

Nevertheless, in what follows, we develop all our lower bound results for the larger class of
\alpha
-sequentially-interactive channels, since our efficient estimation procedure will also be of this type. We find that, indeed, the optimal asymptotic variance among all
\alpha
-sequentially-interactive procedures is given by

\left[\sup_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}}I_{\theta}(Q{\mathcal{P}})\right]^{-1}.

3
Asymptotic lower bounds for estimation in locally private models

In this section we show that the sequence of experiments
\mathcal{E}_{n}=(\mathcal{Z}^{(n)},\mathcal{G}^{(n)},Q^{(n)}{\mathcal{P}}^{n})
,
n\geq 1
, is locally asymptotically mixed normal
(
Jeganathan 1980
;
Jeganathan 1982
, LAMN, cf.)
, provided only that
Q^{(n)}
is
\alpha
-sequentially-interactive and
{\mathcal{P}}
is differentiable in quadratic mean (DQM). To be precise, we show a little bit less than that, namely, that every subsequence
n^{\prime}
has a further subsequence
n^{\prime\prime}
along which
\mathcal{E}_{n^{\prime\prime}}
is LAMN. This result will be sufficient to connect to the existing literature on convolution and local asymptotic minimax theorems for LAMN experiments. In the following subsection we begin by studying regularity properties of the marginal models
{\mathcal{P}}
and
Q{\mathcal{P}}
, for
Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}
. In Subsection
3.2
we then present our LAMN result and discuss its implications in Subsection
3.3
.

3.1
Regularity of locally private models

Consider a statistical model
{\mathcal{P}}=(P_{\theta})_{\theta\in\Theta}
on the sample space
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{F})
with parameter space
\Theta\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
. In the iid setting we consider here, the final data generating model will then simply be the
n
-fold product
{\mathcal{P}}^{n}=(P_{\theta}^{n})_{\theta\in\Theta}
, and a common way to define regularity of such a model and study issues of asymptotic efficiency is by means of differentiability in quadratic mean (DQM). We recall the following classical definition.

Definition 1

(Differentiability in Quadratic Mean)
.

A model
{\mathcal{P}}=(P_{\theta})_{\theta\in\Theta}
with
\Theta\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
, sample space
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{F})
and (
\sigma
-finite) dominating measure
\mu
is called
differentiable in quadratic mean (DQM) at the point
\theta\in\Theta
, if
\theta
is an interior point of
\Theta
and the
\mu
-densities
p_{\theta}=\frac{dP_{\theta}}{d\mu}
satisfy

\Psi_{\theta}(h):=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\left(\sqrt{p_{\theta+h}(x)}-\sqrt{p_{\theta}(x)}-\frac{1}{2}h^{T}s_{\theta}(x)\sqrt{p_{\theta}(x)}\right)^{2}\mu(dx)\;=\;o(\|h\|^{2})

as
h\to 0
, for some measurable vector valued function
s_{\theta}:\mathcal{X}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
. The function
s_{\theta}
is called the
score
at
\theta
.

Notice that this definition is independent of the choice of dominating measure
\mu
, because the quantity
\Psi_{\theta}(h)
does not depend on
\mu
. To see this, simply consider two dominating measures
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
and express the two resulting integrals as an integral with respect to
\mu=\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}
.

We say that a model is DQM or simply
regular
, if it is DQM at every point
\theta\in\Theta
. For convenience we sometimes write
dx
instead of
\mu(dx)
. We also recall that if the model
{\mathcal{P}}
is DQM at
\theta
, then the score satisfies
{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta}[s_{\theta}]=0
and the Fisher information matrix
I_{\theta}({\mathcal{P}}):={\mathbb{E}}_{\theta}[s_{\theta}s_{\theta}^{T}]\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times p}
exists and is finite
(see
van der Vaart 2007
, Theorem 7.2)
.
The cited theorem also implies that the sequence of resulting product experiments
\mathcal{E}_{n}=(\mathcal{X}^{n},\mathcal{F}^{n},{\mathcal{P}}^{n})
is locally asymptotically normal (LAN).
It is known
(
Bickel et al. 1993
, Proposition A.5)
that if
{\mathcal{P}}=(P_{\theta})_{\theta\in\Theta}
is DQM and
T:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Z}
is measurable, then the model of the transformed data
Z=T(X)
, which is given by
T{\mathcal{P}}=(P_{\theta}T^{-1})_{\theta\in\Theta}
, is also DQM with score
t_{\theta}(z)={\mathbb{E}}[s_{\theta}(X)|Z=z]
, where
(X,Z)\thicksim(X,T(X))
under
P_{\theta}
. For differential privacy we need to consider randomized functions
T
, or equivalently, Markov kernels. The following lemma provides the desired extension. It is a simple consequence of a more refined result that we deferred to Appendix
A
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Fix
\alpha\in(0,\infty)
. Suppose the model
{\mathcal{P}}=(P_{\theta})_{\theta\in\Theta}
is DQM at
\theta\in\Theta\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
, with score function
s_{\theta}
, and
Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Z})
is a privacy mechanism. Then the model
Q{\mathcal{P}}=(QP_{\theta})_{\theta\in\Theta}
is also DQM at
\theta

with score function

t_{\theta}(z):={\mathbb{E}}[s_{\theta}(X)|Z=z],

that is, a regular conditional expectation of
s_{\theta}(X)
given
Z=z
, where the joint distribution of
X
and
Z
is determined by
X\thicksim P_{\theta}
and
(Z|X=x)\thicksim Q(dz|x)
.
Furthermore, a version of the score can be chosen such that
\sup_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\|t_{\theta}(z)\|\leq e^{2\alpha}\sqrt{\trace{I_{\theta}({\mathcal{P}})}}
and the Fisher-Information matrix
I_{\theta}(Q\mathcal{P})
in the model
Q\mathcal{P}
satisfies

I_{\theta}(Q\mathcal{P})=\Var[{\mathbb{E}}[s_{\theta}(X)|Z]]=I_{\theta}(\mathcal{P})-{\mathbb{E}}[(s_{\theta}(X)-t_{\theta}(Z))(s_{\theta}(X)-t_{\theta}(Z))^{T}].

Let
q:\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{X}\to[e^{-\alpha},e^{\alpha}]
be as in Lemma
D.2
in the supplement. Now apply Lemma
A.1
with fixed,
z\in\mathcal{Z}
,
P_{0}:=P_{\theta}
,
c:={\mathbb{E}}_{P_{\theta}}[\|s_{\theta}\|^{2}]=\trace{I_{\theta}({\mathcal{P}})}<\infty
,
P_{1}:=P_{\theta+h}
, and
t_{\theta}(z):=\int_{\mathcal{X}}s_{\theta}(x)\frac{q(z|x)p_{0}(x)}{q_{0}(z)}\mu(dx)
, for sufficiently small
\|h\|
, such that
\theta+h\in\Theta
and
\|h\|\sqrt{c}<e^{-2\alpha}
. Notice that the resulting upper bound in Lemma
A.1
is
o(\|h\|^{2})
uniformly in
z\in\mathcal{Z}
, which (together with the fact that
\nu
is a probability measure) implies DQM of
Q{\mathcal{P}}
with score function
t=t_{\theta}(z)
. Since
q(z|x)p_{\theta}(x)
is a
\nu\otimes\mu
-density of
(Z,X)
, we see that the score is a version of the conditional expectation, as claimed. Appropriate boundedness of the score follows from

\left\|\int_{\mathcal{X}}s_{\theta}(x)\frac{q(z|x)p_{\theta}(x)}{\int_{\mathcal{X}}q(z|y)p_{\theta}(y)\mu(dy)}\mu(dx)\right\|\leq e^{2\alpha}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\|s_{\theta}(x)\|p_{\theta}(x)\mu(dx)\leq e^{2\alpha}\sqrt{{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta}[\|s_{\theta}\|^{2}]},

using the point-wise bounds on
q(z|x)
. The representation of
I_{\theta}(Q\mathcal{P})
is an immediate consequence of the variance decomposition formula

I_{\theta}(\mathcal{P})=\Var[s_{\theta}(X)]=\Var[{\mathbb{E}}[s_{\theta}(X)|Z]]+{\mathbb{E}}[\Var[s_{\theta}(X)|Z]].

∎

3.2
LAMN along subsequences

Recall from the discussion at the end of Section
2
that we need to work with sequentially interactive mechanisms
Q^{(n)}
in order to have any hope of achieving the asymptotic variance
[\sup_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}}I_{\theta}(Q{\mathcal{P}})]^{-1}
in general. This, however, requires us to consider dependent and possibly non-identically distributed sanitized observations
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}
. The corresponding model
Q^{(n)}{\mathcal{P}}^{n}
will therefore typically no longer be LAN. The necessary generalization is given by the notion of local asymptotic mixed normality
(
Jeganathan 1980
;
Jeganathan 1982
, LAMN, cf.)
. For a given parameter space
\Theta\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
, let
\mathcal{E}_{n}=(\Omega_{n},\mathcal{A}_{n},\{R_{n,\theta}:\theta\in\Theta\})
,
n\geq 1
, be a sequence of statistical experiments, where
R_{n,\theta}
is a probability measure on the measurable space
(\Omega_{n},\mathcal{A}_{n})
. For
\theta,\theta_{0}\in\Theta
, define the associated log-likelihood ratio
\Lambda_{n}(\theta,\theta_{0}):=\log\frac{dR_{n,\theta}}{dR_{n,\theta_{0}}}
, where
\frac{dR_{n,\theta}}{dR_{n,\theta_{0}}}
is the Radon-Nikodym derivative of the absolutely continuous part of
R_{n,\theta}
with respect to
R_{n,\theta_{0}}
and set
\Lambda_{n}(\theta,\theta_{0}):=\Lambda_{n}(\theta_{0},\theta_{0})=0
if
\theta\notin\Theta
.

Definition 2

(Local Asymptotic Mixed Normality)
.

Fix a parameter space
\Theta\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
and a sequence
(\delta_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of positive definite
p\times p
matrices with
\|\delta_{n}\|\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. We say the sequence of statistical experiments
\mathcal{E}_{n}=(\Omega_{n},\mathcal{A}_{n},\{R_{n,\theta}:\theta\in\Theta\})
satisfies the LAMN condition at
\theta\in\Theta
, if
\theta
is an interior point of
\Theta
and the following two conditions hold:

1.

There exists a sequence
(\Delta_{n,\theta})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of random vectors
\Delta_{n,\theta}:\Omega_{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
and a sequence
(\Sigma_{n,\theta})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of random nonnegative-definite matrices
\Sigma_{n,\theta}:\Omega_{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times p}
, such that

\Lambda_{n}(\theta+\delta_{n}h,\theta)-\left(h^{T}\Delta_{n,\theta}-\frac{1}{2}h^{T}\Sigma_{n,\theta}h\right)

converges to zero in
R_{n,\theta}
probability for every
h\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
.

2.

We have

\begin{pmatrix}\Delta_{n,\theta}\\
\Sigma_{n,\theta}\end{pmatrix}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle R_{n,\theta}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}\;\begin{pmatrix}\Sigma_{\theta}^{1/2}\Delta\\
\Sigma_{\theta}\end{pmatrix},

where
\Delta\thicksim N(0,J_{p})
is independent of
\Sigma_{\theta}
.

If the limiting matrix
\Sigma_{\theta}
in the above definition is non-random, the LAMN property reduces to the more classical notion of local asymptotic normality (LAN). It is a classical result
(cf.
van der Vaart 2007
, Theorem 7.2)
that if the model
\mathcal{P}=(P_{\theta})_{\theta\in\Theta}
on sample space
\mathcal{X}
is DQM at
\theta\in\Theta
, then the sequence of experiments
\mathcal{E}_{n}=(\mathcal{X}^{n},\mathcal{F}^{n},\{P_{\theta}^{n}:\theta\in\Theta\})
is LAN at
\theta
with
\delta_{n}=J_{p}/\sqrt{n}
and
\Sigma_{\theta}
equal to the Fisher-Information matrix
I_{\theta}(\mathcal{P})
, which is non-random. An immediate consequence of that observation is the following simple contiguity result that holds for any sequence
Q^{(n)}
of Markov kernels from
(\mathcal{X}^{n},\mathcal{F}^{n})
to
(\mathcal{Z}_{n},\mathcal{G}_{n})
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Consider a model
{\mathcal{P}}
on sample space
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{F})
that is DQM at
\theta\in\Theta\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
. Fix
h\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
and an arbitrary Markov kernel
Q^{(n)}
from
(\mathcal{X}^{n},\mathcal{F}^{n})
to
(\mathcal{Z}_{n},\mathcal{G}_{n})
. Then
R_{n,\theta}=Q^{(n)}P_{\theta}^{n}
and
R_{n,\theta+h/\sqrt{n}}=Q^{(n)}P_{\theta+h/\sqrt{n}}^{n}
are mutually contiguous.

Write
\theta_{n}:=\theta+h/\sqrt{n}
. The LAN property of
{\mathcal{P}}
implies
P_{\theta_{n}}^{n}\vartriangleleft\vartriangleright P_{\theta}^{n}

(
van der Vaart 2007
, Example 6.5)
. Now fix a sequence of events
A_{n}\in\mathcal{G}^{(n)}
such that
R_{n,\theta}(A_{n})\to 0
. In particular, for
T_{n}(x):=Q^{(n)}(A_{n}|x)\in[0,1]
, we have
{\mathbb{E}}_{P_{\theta}^{n}}[T_{n}]=R_{n,\theta}(A_{n})\to 0
, or, equivalently,
T_{n}\to 0
in
P_{\theta}^{n}
-probability. But by contiguity we get
T_{n}\to 0
in
P_{\theta_{n}}^{n}
-probability, which implies
R_{n,\theta_{n}}(A_{n})={\mathbb{E}}_{P_{\theta_{n}}^{n}}[T_{n}]\to 0
. Reversing the roles of
\theta
and
\theta_{n}
finishes the proof.
∎

Now, for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, consider an
\alpha
-sequentially-interactive privacy mechanism
Q^{(n)}
from
\mathcal{X}^{n}
to
({\mathcal{Z}}^{(n)},\mathcal{G}^{(n)})
, and a (marginal) model
{\mathcal{P}}=(P_{\theta})_{\theta\in\Theta}
on
\mathcal{X}
. The statistical experiment generating the sanitized observations
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}
is therefore given by
\mathcal{E}_{n}=({\mathcal{Z}}^{(n)},\mathcal{G}^{(n)},\{Q^{(n)}P_{\theta}^{n}:\theta\in\Theta\})
. The following theorem shows that this sequence of experiments possesses the LAMN property, provided that the underlying data generating model
{\mathcal{P}}
is DQM and an appropriate sequence of nonnegative-definite matrices
\Sigma_{n,\theta}
has a stochastic (weak) limit
\Sigma_{\theta}
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Fix
\alpha>0
and for each
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let
Q^{(n)}
be
\alpha
-sequentially-interactive (
1.6
) from
(\mathcal{X}^{n},\mathcal{F}^{n})
to
(\mathcal{Z}^{(n)},\mathcal{G}^{(n)})
and assume that all the
\sigma
-fields
\mathcal{F}
,
\mathcal{G}_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,n
, are countably generated. Suppose the model
{\mathcal{P}}=(P_{\theta})_{\theta\in\Theta}
on sample space
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{F})
is DQM at
\theta\in\Theta\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
with score
s_{\theta}
and define

\Sigma_{n,\theta}(z_{1:n-1}):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}I_{\theta}(Q_{z_{1:i-1}}\mathcal{P}),\quad z_{1:n-1}=(z_{1},\dots,z_{n-1})\in\mathcal{Z}^{(n-1)}.

Then
z_{1:i-1}\mapsto I_{\theta}(Q_{z_{1:i-1}}{\mathcal{P}})
is measurable and there exists a measurable function
z_{1:i}\mapsto t_{i,\theta}(z_{i}|z_{1:i-1})
such that for all
z_{1:i-1}\in\mathcal{Z}^{(i-1)}
,
z\mapsto t_{i,\theta}(z|z_{1:i-1})
is a score in the model
Q_{z_{1:i-1}}{\mathcal{P}}
at
\theta
.

If
\Sigma_{n,\theta}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle Q^{(n)}P^{n}_{\theta}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}\Sigma_{\theta}
for some random symmetric
p\times p
matrix
\Sigma_{\theta}
and
I_{\theta}({\mathcal{P}})\neq 0
, then the sequence
\mathcal{E}_{n}=({\mathcal{Z}}^{(n)},\mathcal{G}^{(n)},\{Q^{(n)}P_{\theta}^{n}:\theta\in\Theta\})
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, is LAMN at
\theta
with
\delta_{n}=J_{p}/\sqrt{n}
,
\Sigma_{n,\theta}
as defined above and

\Delta_{n,\theta}(z_{1:n})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i,\theta}(z_{i}|z_{1:i-1}).

The regularity assumptions of Theorem
3.3
are natural. The only real technical restriction that is new compared to the classical theory is the separability of the
\sigma
-fields. We need it to show existence of a score
t_{i,\theta}
in the conditional model
Q_{z_{1:i-1}}{\mathcal{P}}
that is also jointly measurable in
z_{1},\dots,z_{i}
. Furthermore, the weak convergence of
\Sigma_{n,\theta}
may seem restrictive. It is clearly a necessary condition for LAMN and it may indeed fail if we choose a very erratic sequence
Q^{(n)}
(see Example
A.2
in the supplement). Notice, however, that in view of
0\preccurlyeq I_{\theta}(Q_{z_{1:i-1}}\mathcal{P})\preccurlyeq I_{\theta}({\mathcal{P}})
(cf. Lemma
3.1
), the sequence
(\Sigma_{n,\theta})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is bounded and hence tight. Thus, by Prokhorov’s theorem, for every subsequence
n^{\prime}
there is a further subsequence
n^{\prime\prime}
such that
\Sigma_{n^{\prime\prime},\theta}
is weakly convergent and Theorem
3.3
yields that
(\mathcal{E}_{n^{\prime\prime}})_{n^{\prime\prime}\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is LAMN at
\theta
. We will show next that this LAMN property along subsequences is all we need to derive asymptotic lower bound results.

Remark 3.4
.

To prove Theorem
3.3
, one could try to use sufficient conditions for the LAMN property that are available in the literature. For example, to establish the representation of the log-likelihood-ratio
\Lambda_{n}
in part 1 of Definition
2
, one could essentially follow
Jeganathan 1982
, who also operates under a DQM framework. However, checking those assumptions in our privacy framework is hardly any easier than directly proving the required property of
\Lambda_{n}
. Furthermore, to establish the weak convergence in part 2 of Definition
2
,
Jeganathan 1982
(who relies on the martingale CLT of
Hall 1977
) requires that
\Sigma_{n,\theta}\to\Sigma_{\theta}
in probability (on some extended probability space on which we can define
\Sigma_{\theta}
). However, it is not clear if this stronger assumption can be satisfied (at least along subsequences) in the present setting of
\alpha
-sequentially interactive privacy without restricting the class of possible privacy mechanisms.
In contrast,
Basawa and Scott 1983
use a quite elegant direct argument to establish part 2 of Definition
2
, requiring only weak convergence of
\Sigma_{n,\theta}
, but they work with classical differentiability assumptions on the densities
p_{\theta}
rather than with DQM. We therefore decided to provide a direct and fully self-contained proof of both parts of Definition
2
, borrowing ideas from
Jeganathan 1982
for the first part and ideas from
Basawa and Scott 1983
for the second part. In particular, following
Basawa and Scott 1983
, we use moment generating functions to establish the joint weak convergence of
(\Delta_{n,\theta},\Sigma_{n,\theta})
, which is possible here because
\alpha
-differential privacy imposes convenient boundedness properties. This makes for a much shorter and more elegant argument than checking assumptions of an appropriate martingale CLT; see Appendix
A
for the proof of Theorem
3.3
.

3.3
A private convolution theorem

In this subsection we provide a private version of a convolution theorem, which shows that any regular
\alpha
-sequentially interactive estimation procedure for
\theta\in\Theta\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
has an asymptotic distribution
D_{\theta}
which is given as an (inverse) scale-mixture of the convolution of a centered Gaussian with some other distribution
L
. Here, the mixing measure is the distribution of any weak accumulation point of the sequence
(\Sigma_{n,\theta})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
defined in Theorem
3.3
. After the proof we explain how this result can be used in the case
p=1
for determining the most concentrated limiting distribution. A regular private estimation procedure reaching that limit will thus be called asymptotically efficient. Recall that a
\alpha
-sequentially interactive estimation procedure for
\theta\in\Theta\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
consists of a sequence
Q^{(n)}
of
\alpha
-sequentially-interactive privacy mechanisms as in (
1.6
) from
(\mathcal{X}^{n},\mathcal{F}^{n})
to
(\mathcal{Z}^{(n)},\mathcal{G}^{(n)})
as well as of a sequence of estimators
\hat{\theta}_{n}:\mathcal{Z}^{(n)}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
.

Theorem 3.5
.

Fix
\alpha>0
and for each
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let
Q^{(n)}
be
\alpha
-sequentially-interactive (
1.6
) from
(\mathcal{X}^{n},\mathcal{F}^{n})
to
(\mathcal{Z}^{(n)},\mathcal{G}^{(n)})
and assume that all the
\sigma
-fields
\mathcal{F}
,
\mathcal{G}_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,n
are countably generated. Suppose the model
{\mathcal{P}}=(P_{\theta})_{\theta\in\Theta}
on sample space
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{F})
is DQM at
\theta\in\Theta\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
and let
\Sigma_{n,\theta}
be defined as in Theorem
3.3
. Consider the joint distribution of the sanitized data
R_{n,\theta}:=Q^{(n)}P_{\theta}^{n}
and suppose that for every
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
R_{n,\theta}(\Sigma_{n,\theta}\text{ is positive definite})=1
and every weak accumulation point
\Sigma_{\theta}
of
(\Sigma_{n,\theta})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
under
R_{n,\theta}
is almost surely positive definite. Let
\hat{\theta}_{n}:\mathcal{Z}^{(n)}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
be an estimator sequence and
D_{\theta}
a random
p
-vector, such that

\sqrt{n}\left(\hat{\theta}_{n}-[\theta+h/\sqrt{n}]\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle R_{n,\theta+h/\sqrt{n}}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}D_{\theta},\quad\forall h\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}.

(3.1)

Then the limiting distribution
D_{\theta}
admits the representation

P(D_{\theta}\in A)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times p}}\left[N(0,\sigma^{-1})\star L_{\theta}(\sigma)\right](A)P_{\Sigma_{\theta}}(d\sigma),\quad\forall A\in\mathcal{B}({\mathbb{R}}^{p}),

where
P_{\Sigma_{\theta}}
is the distribution of any weak accumulation point
\Sigma_{\theta}
,
\{L_{\theta}(\sigma):\sigma\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times p}\}
is a family of probability distributions on
{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
possibly depending on
P_{\Sigma_{\theta}}
and
\star
denotes convolution.

Fix
h\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Notice that any subsequence of

D_{n}:=D_{n,\theta+h/\sqrt{n}}:=\sqrt{n}\left(\hat{\theta}_{n}-[\theta+h/\sqrt{n}]\right)

under
R_{n,\theta+h/\sqrt{n}}
has limiting distribution equal to
D_{\theta}
. By boundedness of
\Sigma_{n}:=\Sigma_{n,\theta}
, the sequence
(\Sigma_{n})_{n}
is tight under
R_{n,\theta+h/\sqrt{n}}
. Since marginal tightness implies joint tightness, also
(\Sigma_{n},D_{n})_{n}
is tight under
R_{n,\theta+h/\sqrt{n}}
. Thus,

(\Sigma_{n^{\prime}},D_{n^{\prime}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle R_{n^{\prime},\theta+h/\sqrt{n^{\prime}}}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}(\Sigma_{\theta},D_{\theta}),

along a subsequence
(n^{\prime})
. This verifies the regularity condition 7.8(b) of
Höpfner 2014
. By contiguity (cf. Lemma
3.2
) we also have

\Sigma_{n^{\prime}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle R_{n^{\prime},\theta}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}\Sigma_{\theta}.

Because
\Sigma_{n,\theta}\preccurlyeq I_{\theta}({\mathcal{P}})
and the lower bound is positive definite (a.s.), we have
I_{\theta}({\mathcal{P}})\neq 0
and Theorem
3.3
shows that
\mathcal{E}_{n^{\prime}}=({\mathcal{Z}}^{(n^{\prime})},\mathcal{G}^{(n^{\prime})},\{Q^{(n^{\prime})}P_{\theta}^{n^{\prime}}:\theta\in\Theta\})
,
n^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{N}}
, is LAMN at
\theta
. In view of the positive definiteness assumptions our definition of LAMN coincides with the one of
Höpfner 2014
. The proof is now finished by an application of Theorem 7.10(b) of
Höpfner 2014
.
∎

Now consider the case
p=1
. Notice that since
\Sigma_{n,\theta}\leq\sup_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}}I_{\theta}(Q{\mathcal{P}})
we must have
P(\Sigma_{\theta}\leq\sup_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}}I_{\theta}(Q{\mathcal{P}}))=1
, for every weak accumulation point
\Sigma_{\theta}
, and thus the most concentrated limiting distribution in the sense of Anderson’s Lemma
(
Höpfner 2014
, see, e.g., Corollary 5.7 in)
is

N\left(0,\left[\sup_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}}I_{\theta}(Q{\mathcal{P}})\right]^{-1}\right).

Hence, a regular estimator, in the sense of (
3.1
), achieving this limiting distribution is called efficient. In Section
4
below (see especially Theorem
4.12
), we present such an
\alpha
-sequentially interactive estimation procedure. Notice that even though we allow for any sequentially interactive mechanism
Q^{(n)}
, the minimal asymptotic variance
\left[\sup_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}}I_{\theta}(Q{\mathcal{P}})\right]^{-1}
is obtained by optimizing only over non-interactive mechanisms. Thus, we can not asymptotically beat non-interactive mechanisms by using a sequential one. However, this does not necessarily mean that there is always an efficient non-interactive mechanism, because a maximizer of
Q\mapsto I_{\theta}(Q{\mathcal{P}})
will typically depend on the unknown
\theta
and we need some interaction to estimate it.

In a similar way, one can derive an almost sure version of the convolution theorem, holding for Lebesgue–almost all
\theta\in\Theta
and avoiding the regularity assumption (
3.1
). Moreover, one can derive a local asymptotic minimax theorem. See Theorem 2 of
Jeganathan 1981
and Theorem 7.12 of
Höpfner 2014
for the corresponding classical results requiring only the LAMN property.

Remark 3.6
.

Notice that we consider the case
p=1
only in order for the maximization of Fisher-Information

\sup_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}}I_{\theta}(Q{\mathcal{P}}),

or the equivalent minimization of asymptotic variance

\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}}\left[I_{\theta}(Q{\mathcal{P}})\right]^{-1}=\left[\sup_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}}I_{\theta}(Q{\mathcal{P}})\right]^{-1},

to be well defined. In case of general
p\in{\mathbb{N}}
, it would be desirable to minimize
Q\mapsto\left[I_{\theta}(Q{\mathcal{P}})\right]^{-1}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times p}
in the partial Loewner ordering. However, we currently don’t know if there even exists such a minimal element. Of course, one can always fix a functional of interest
\eta:{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times p}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, such as the determinant or the trace, and try to solve

\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}}\eta\left(\left[I_{\theta}(Q{\mathcal{P}})\right]^{-1}\right).

We will consider this and other extensions elsewhere.

4
Efficiency of the private two-step MLE

In this section, at least in the single parameter case
p=1
, we develop an
\alpha
-sequentially interactive procedure that is regular and achieves the asymptotically optimal variance obtained in Subsection
3.3
. In other words, we present an efficient differentially private estimation procedure. As explained in the introductory example Section
2
, we rely on a two-step sample splitting procedure where the first (much smaller) part of the data is used in a differentially private way to consistently estimate (not necessarily at
\sqrt{n}
-rate) the unknown parameter
\theta\in\Theta\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
. Based on this preliminary estimate, the second (larger) part of the data is then used to construct an
\alpha
-private MLE that has minimal asymptotic variance.

4.1
Consistent non-interactive
\alpha
-private estimation

An important part of our efficient two-step procedure is a consistent and private preliminary estimator. We now present two different ways for how to obtain such consistent
\alpha
-private estimators. Throughout this subsection, we consider a parametric model
\mathcal{P}=(P_{\theta})_{\theta\in\Theta}
, with parameter space
\Theta\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
and sample space
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{F})
, that is dominated by
\mu
with corresponding densities
p_{\theta}
.

4.1.1
Maximum Likelihood

Fix a (marginal) channel
Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{X})
, pick
\nu(dz):=Q(dz|x^{*})
and
q(z|x)
as in Lemma
D.2
and write
q_{\theta}(z)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}q(z|x)p_{\theta}(x)\mu(dx)
for
\nu
-densities of the model
Q\mathcal{P}
. In particular, we have
q_{\theta}(z)\in[e^{-\alpha},e^{\alpha}]
. The log-likelihood of the observed (non-interactively) sanitized data
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}
is given by

\theta\mapsto\ell_{n}(\theta):=\ell_{n}(\theta;z_{1},\dots,z_{n}):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log q_{\theta}(z_{i}),

and we define the corresponding non-interactive
\alpha
-private MLE
\hat{\theta}_{n}
to be a measurable maximizer.
For ease of reference, we first recall Wald’s classical consistency result as presented in
van der Vaart 2007
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Fix
\theta_{0}\in\Theta\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
. Let
\theta\mapsto\log p_{\theta}(x)
be upper-semicontinuous on
\Theta
for
P_{\theta_{0}}
almost all
x\in\mathcal{X}
and suppose that for every sufficiently small ball
U\subseteq\Theta
the function
x\mapsto\sup_{\theta\in U}\log p_{\theta}(x)
is measurable and satisfies

{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta_{0}}\left[\sup_{\theta\in U}\log p_{\theta}\right]<\infty.

If
\theta\mapsto{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta_{0}}[\log p_{\theta}]
has a unique maximizer at
\theta_{0}
, then any estimator
\hat{\theta}_{n}
that maximizes the log-likelihood
\theta\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log p_{\theta}(x_{i})
satisfies

P_{\theta_{0}}^{n}(\|\hat{\theta}_{n}-\theta_{0}\|_{2}>\varepsilon\land\hat{\theta}_{n}\in K)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0,

for every
\varepsilon>0
and every compact set
K\subseteq\Theta
.

This can be translated into the following
\alpha
-private version, which comes with even weaker assumptions than the classical one.

Theorem 4.2
.

If
{\mathcal{P}}
is DQM at every
\theta\in\Theta\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
(in particular,
\Theta
is open) and the channel
Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{X})
is such that the model
Q{\mathcal{P}}
is identifiable, then any non-interactive
\alpha
-private MLE satisfies

(QP_{\theta_{0}})^{n}(\|\hat{\theta}_{n}-\theta_{0}\|_{2}>\varepsilon\land\hat{\theta}_{n}\in K)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0,

for every
\theta_{0}\in\Theta
, every
\varepsilon>0
and every compact set
K\subseteq\Theta
.

We check the assumptions of Theorem
4.1
with
q_{\theta}
instead of
p_{\theta}
. We can even establish continuity of
\theta\mapsto\log q_{\theta}(z)
for every
z\in{\mathcal{Z}}
, by using the DQM property to get

\displaystyle|q_{\theta+h}(z)-q_{\theta}(z)|

\displaystyle\quad=\left|\int_{\mathcal{X}}q(z|x)\left[\sqrt{p_{\theta+h}(x)}-\sqrt{p_{\theta}(x)}\right]\left[\sqrt{p_{\theta+h}(x)}+\sqrt{p_{\theta}(x)}\right]\mu(dx)\right|

\displaystyle\quad\leq e^{\alpha}2\sqrt{\int_{\mathcal{X}}\left(\sqrt{p_{\theta+h}(x)}-\sqrt{p_{\theta}(x)}\right)^{2}\mu(dx)}\xrightarrow[h\to 0]{}0.

This also implies that
z\mapsto\sup_{\theta\in U}\log q_{\theta}(z)
is measurable for any ball
U\subseteq\Theta\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
, because the supremum can be replaced by a countable one.
QP_{\theta_{0}}
-integrability of the supremum is now trivial, in view of
\log q_{\theta}(z)\in[-\alpha,\alpha]
. Uniqueness of the maximizer of
\theta\mapsto{\mathbb{E}}_{QP_{\theta_{0}}}[\log q_{\theta}]
follows, for example, from
van der Vaart 2007
, using identifiablity.
∎

The proof of Theorem
4.2
nicely highlights the smoothing and bounding effect that differential privacy has on the original model. Since
q_{\theta}(z)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}q(z|x)p_{\theta}(x)\mu(dx)
is an integrated version of
p_{\theta}
, the continuity in
L^{2}
(or even DQM) of
{\mathcal{P}}
nicely translates into continuity of
\theta\mapsto q_{\theta}(z)
. The boundedness condition of Theorem
4.1
immediately holds for
q_{\theta}
because of the convenient property that
e^{-\alpha}\leq q(z|x)\leq e^{\alpha}
, which we get from Lemma
D.2
.

4.1.2
Method of moments

Consider first the case where the original data
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
are observed. If, for some measurable function
g:\mathcal{X}\to G
(often a power function
g(x)=(x,x^{2},\dots,x^{p}
)) with range
G=\{g(x):x\in\mathcal{X}\}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
, the function
f:\Theta\to G
,
f(\theta):={\mathbb{E}}_{\theta}[g]
has a continuous inverse
f^{-1}:G\to\Theta\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
, then the moment estimator
\hat{\theta}_{n}:=f^{-1}(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(X_{i}))
is consistent. We can turn this into a non-interactive
\alpha
-private procedure by considering the truncation operation
\Pi_{\tau}(y):=y\mathds{1}_{\{\|y\|_{1}\leq\tau\}}
,
y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Suppose that there exists a measurable function
g:\mathcal{X}\to G
with range
G\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
, such that the function
f:\Theta\to G
,
f(\theta):={\mathbb{E}}_{\theta}[g]
has a continuous inverse
f^{-1}:G\to\Theta
. Generate
Z_{i}:=\Pi_{\tau_{n}}[g(X_{i})]+\frac{2\tau_{n}}{\alpha}W_{i}
, for independent random
p
-vectors
W_{i}
with iid standard Laplace components
W_{ij}\thicksim Lap(1)
. Then the estimator

\hat{\theta}_{n}=f^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}\right)

is non-interactive
\alpha
-private. If
\tau_{n}\to\infty
and
\tau_{n}^{2}/n\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, then it is also consistent.

For
\alpha
-privacy, simply notice that a conditional Lebesgue density of
Z_{i}
given
X_{i}
is given by
q(z|x)=\frac{\alpha}{4\tau_{n}}\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha}{2\tau_{n}}\|z-\Pi_{\tau_{n}}(x)\|_{1}\right)
, which is easily seen to satisfy
q(z|x)/q(z|x^{\prime})\leq e^{\alpha}
, using the reverse triangle inequality.

Now
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Pi_{\tau_{n}}[g(X_{i})]+\frac{2\tau_{n}}{\alpha}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}W_{i}
. The mean of the noise term
\frac{2\tau_{n}}{\alpha}\bar{W}_{n}
is zero and its components have variance equal to
\frac{8\tau_{n}^{2}}{n\alpha^{2}}
, which converges to zero as
n\to\infty
. For the
X_{i}
term note that
\Pi_{\tau_{n}}[g(X_{i})]-g(X_{i})=-g(X_{i})\mathds{1}_{\{\|g(X_{i})\|_{1}>\tau_{n}\}}
. Hence,

{\mathbb{E}}\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big[\Pi_{\tau_{n}}[g(X_{i})]-g(X_{i})\Big]\right\|_{1}\leq{\mathbb{E}}\left\|g(X_{1})\mathds{1}_{\{\|g(X_{1})\|_{1}>\tau_{n}\}}\right\|_{1}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0,

by dominated convergence. Hence
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(X_{i})+o_{P}(1)
converges in probability to
f(\theta)
and the proof is finished by an application of the continuous mapping theorem.
∎

We want to point out that the truncation approach actually comes at the price of a convergence rate of only
\tau_{n}/\sqrt{n}
rather than the usual
n^{-1/2}
. This is not an issue for the theory of our two-step procedure, since we don’t require a
\sqrt{n}
-consistent preliminary estimator, but it may of course be an issue for applications. Notice, however, that it may often be possible to avoid the truncation
\Pi_{\tau_{n}}
if the function
g
can be chosen such that its range space
G\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
is bounded by, say,
\|g(x)\|_{1}\leq c_{p}
, for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
, because then we can choose
\tau_{n}=c_{p}
. This typically works if
\mathcal{X}
itself is bounded and
g(x)=x
, but can also be used for unbounded
\mathcal{X}
.

4.2
Consistent estimation of the optimal privacy mechanism

Recall that in order to privately estimate
\theta\in\Theta\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
from iid
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}\thicksim QP_{\theta}
, we would ideally like to choose
Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{X})
such that
I_{\theta}(Q{\mathcal{P}})\in{\mathbb{R}}
is maximal. However, as we have seen in Section
2
, a maximizer, if it exists, will typically depend on the unknown parameter
\theta
. In the previous section we saw how
\theta
can be consistently estimated in a locally differentially private way, given that a preliminary privacy mechanism
Q_{0}\in\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Z}_{0})
can be found such that the model
Q_{0}{\mathcal{P}}
is identifiable. We will use such a consistent
\alpha
-private estimator
\tilde{\theta}_{n_{1}}
to construct a sequence
\hat{Q}_{n_{1}}\in\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{X})
such that
I_{\theta}(\hat{Q}_{n_{1}}{\mathcal{P}})\to\sup_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{X})}I_{\theta}(Q{\mathcal{P}})
in
(Q_{0}P_{\theta})^{n_{1}}
-probability, as the size
n_{1}
of the first part of the sample grows to infinity. An ad-hoc idea to obtain
\hat{Q}_{n_{1}}
would be to solve
\sup_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{X})}I_{\tilde{\theta}_{n_{1}}}(Q{\mathcal{P}})
. However, direct optimization over the vast set
\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}
is numerically challenging. Instead, we solve a discretized version of this optimization problem of which we can show that it approximates the original one well enough.

Before we outline the procedure for constructing
\hat{Q}_{n_{1}}
, we provide a complete list of all the regularity conditions imposed in this subsection. Even though our main results on maximizing Fisher-Information apply only in the case
p=1
, we here list our assumptions with general
p\in{\mathbb{N}}
because some of our preliminary results hold generally and may be of independent interest.

(C1)

The model
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{F},{\mathcal{P}})
is DQM at every
\theta\in\Theta\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
with score
s_{\theta}
and dominating measure
\mu
.

(C2)

The mappings
(x,\theta)\mapsto p_{\theta}(x):\mathcal{X}\times\Theta\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
and
(x,\theta)\mapsto s_{\theta}(x):\mathcal{X}\times\Theta\to{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
are measurable.

(C3)

The mapping
\theta\mapsto s_{\theta}\sqrt{p_{\theta}}:\Theta\to L_{2}(\mu,\|\cdot\|_{2})
is continuous.

Conditions
(C1)
and
(C3)
together with positive definiteness of
I_{\theta}({\mathcal{P}})
for all
\theta\in\Theta
, correspond exactly to what
Bickel et al. 1993
call
regularity
of the parametric model
{\mathcal{P}}
.
In our leading case
p=1
, positivity of the Fisher-Information is not needed for consistent estimation of the optimal privacy mechanism, because this problem is trivial if
I_{\theta}({\mathcal{P}})=0
. However, later on it will be necessary to ensure
I_{\theta}({\mathcal{P}})\geq\sup_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{X})}I_{\theta}(Q{\mathcal{P}})>0
in order to prove efficiency of the two-step private MLE. The measurability condition
(C2)
will be used to construct a measurable discretization of the original model and to show that the Fisher-Information of the discretized model and a maximizer thereof are appropriately measurable.

4.2.1
Outline of general approach

For a given
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, consider a measurable (discretization) map
T_{k}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}_{k}:=[k]
. Before applying a privacy mechanism, we discretize each observation
X_{i}
by recording only
Y_{i}=T_{k}(X_{i})
.
2
2

2

Clearly, local pre-processing preserves privacy (cf. Lemma
D.6
).
Thus, the model describing the quantized observations
Y_{i}
is given by
T_{k}{\mathcal{P}}=(P_{\theta}T_{k}^{-1})_{\theta\in\Theta}
.
With such a discretization at hand, we solve the optimization problem

\max_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{X}_{k}\to\mathcal{X}_{k})}I_{\tilde{\theta}_{n_{1}}}(QT_{k}{\mathcal{P}}),

(4.1)

using the LP formulation of Theorem 4 in
Kairouz, Oh and Viswanath 2016
(see also Lemma
4.5
below, which shows that the Fisher-Information satisfies the assumptions of that reference and is continuous in
Q
on the compact set
\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{X}_{k}\to\mathcal{X}_{k})
), to obtain
\hat{Q}_{n_{1}}\in\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{X}_{k}\to\mathcal{X}_{k})\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{k\times k}
. In order for the procedure to apply in high generality, we will even allow
k
and the quantizer
T_{k}
to depend on the preliminary estimate
\tilde{\theta}_{n_{1}}
and therefore we write
\hat{k}_{n_{1}}\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
\hat{T}_{{n_{1}}}:\mathcal{X}\to[\hat{k}_{n_{1}}]
.
3
3

3

In the applications of Section
5
below, however, we find that

can be chosen independently of the preliminary estimate.
We then show that

I_{\theta}(\hat{Q}_{n_{1}}\hat{T}_{{n_{1}}}{\mathcal{P}})\to\sup_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{X})}I_{\theta}(Q{\mathcal{P}}),\quad\quad\text{in }(Q_{0}P_{\theta})^{n_{1}}\text{-probability},

(4.2)

as
n_{1}\to\infty
. Thus, given this channel
\hat{Q}_{n_{1}}
and the discretization map
\hat{T}_{{n_{1}}}
(both of which are constructed using the preliminary estimate
\tilde{\theta}_{n_{1}}
based on sanitized data from a first group of individuals
i=1,\dots,n_{1}
) the second group of data owners (
i=n_{1}+1,\dots,n
) can locally generate
Z_{i}
by first discretizing their data
X_{i}
to get
Y_{i}=\hat{T}_{{n_{1}}}(X_{i})
and then draw
Z_{i}
from
\hat{Q}_{n_{1}}(dz_{i}|Y_{i})
(cf. Figure
1
). Notice that the composition
\hat{Q}_{n_{1}}\hat{T}_{{n_{1}}}
can be understood as a collection of Markov kernels indexed by the first group data
z_{1:n_{1}}
, or simply by
\tilde{\theta}_{n_{1}}
. In other words, we can think of it as a function
(C,x_{i},z_{1:n_{1}})\mapsto[\hat{Q}_{n_{1}}\hat{T}_{{n_{1}}}](C|x_{i},z_{1:n_{1}})
, for
C\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}
,
x_{i}\in\mathcal{X}
and

That efficient estimation based on sanitized

4.2.2
Technical aspects

We begin the rigorous proof of the consistency claim in (
4.2
) by a discuss of the precise requirements on the discretization map
T_{k}
. The following definition is motivated by Lemma
4.7
below.

Definition 3
.

Suppose Condition
(C1)
holds and let
\dot{p}_{\theta}(x):=s_{\theta}(x)p_{\theta}(x)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
. A sequence of measurable mappings
(x,\theta)\mapsto T_{m}(x,\theta):\mathcal{X}\times\Theta\to{\mathbb{N}}
,
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
, is called a
consistent quantizer
of the model
{\mathcal{P}}
, if there exists another sequence of measurable mappings
k_{m}:\Theta\to{\mathbb{N}}
, such that the following hold true:

a)

For all
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
\theta\in\Theta
and
x\in\mathcal{X}
we have
T_{m}(x,\theta)\in[k_{m}(\theta)]:=\{1,\dots,k_{m}(\theta)\}.

b)

Define the following quantities:
T_{m,\theta}(x):=T_{m}(x,\theta)
,
B_{j}:=B_{j,m}(\theta):=T_{m,\theta}^{-1}(\{j\})
,
J:=J_{m}(\theta):=\{l\in[k_{m}(\theta)]:0<\mu(B_{l,m}(\theta))<\infty\}
,
K:=K_{J,m}(\theta):=\bigcup_{j\in J}B_{j,m}(\theta)
,

\bar{p}_{\theta}(x):=\sum_{j\in J}\frac{\int_{B_{j}}p_{\theta}(y)\mu(dy)}{\mu(B_{j})}\mathds{1}_{B_{j}}(x),\;\text{ and }\;\bar{\dot{p}}_{\theta}(x):=\sum_{j\in J}\frac{\int_{B_{j}}\dot{p}_{\theta}(y)\mu(dy)}{\mu(B_{j})}\mathds{1}_{B_{j}}(x)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}.

We then have
\Delta_{m}(\theta_{m})\to 0
as
m\to\infty
, for every sequence
(\theta_{m})_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}}
in
\Theta
whose limit is also in
\Theta
, and where

\displaystyle\Delta_{m}(\theta):=\left[\Big\|(p_{\theta}-\bar{p}_{\theta})\mathds{1}_{K(\theta)}\Big\|_{L_{1}(\mu)}+\Big\|\|\dot{p}_{\theta}-\bar{\dot{p}}_{\theta}\|_{\ell_{2}}\mathds{1}_{K(\theta)}\Big\|_{L_{1}(\mu)}+\sqrt{P_{\theta}(K(\theta)^{c})}\right].

(4.4)

The idea of a consistent quantizer is that we want to be able to approximate the Fisher-Information
I_{\theta}(Q{\mathcal{P}})
of a privatized model
Q{\mathcal{P}}
by the Fisher-Information
I_{\theta}(QT_{m,\theta}{\mathcal{P}})
of a quantized and privatized model at the value of the preliminary estimate
\theta=\tilde{\theta}_{n_{1}}
, hence, the convergence is required along arbitrary sequences
(\theta_{m})_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}}
and not just pointwise. To approximate Fisher-Information, we need to approximate the density
p_{\theta}
and its ‘derivative’
\dot{p}_{\theta}
, which determine the Fisher-Information at
\theta
, by histograms. But to obtain negligibility of
\Delta_{m}(\theta_{m})
for every convergent sequence
\theta_{m}\in\Theta
, it may be necessary to allow the corresponding partition, and in some cases even the resolution
k_{m}
, to depend on
\theta_{m}
. For instance, if the quantizer, and thus the set
K
, wasn’t allowed to depend on
\theta_{m}
, then we would need to impose tightness of the model
{\mathcal{P}}
in order to guarantee that
P_{\theta_{m}}(K_{m}^{c})\to 0
holds for every convergent sequence.

The following lemma shows that consistent quantizers generally exist and the proof of that result, which is deferred to the supplement (Section
B
), also provides an explicit construction. However, in specific models one can often find simpler constructions with a
\theta
-independent
k_{m}
that also scales slowly with
m
while the approximation error in (
4.4
) converges faster (see Section
5
). The magnitude of
k_{m}
is, of course, essential for the numerical solvability of (
4.1
).

Lemma 4.4
.

If
\mathcal{X}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and Conditions
(C1)
and
(C2)
hold for a dominating measure

that is finite on compact sets, then a consistent quantizer of
{\mathcal{P}}
exists.

Assuming that we have a consistent quantizer at hand, the next step is to ensure that the objective function in (

the model

Q[T_{m,\theta}{\mathcal{P}}]=[QT_{m,\theta}]{\mathcal{P}}

Q[T_{m,\theta}P_{\theta^{\prime}}]=\int_{[k_{m}(\theta)]}Q(\cdot|y)[P_{\theta^{\prime}}T_{m,\theta}^{-1}](dy)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}Q(\cdot|T_{m,\theta}(x))P_{\theta^{\prime}}(dx)=[QT_{m,\theta}]P_{\theta^{\prime}}

5
Practical aspects

The two-step
\alpha
-private estimation procedure introduced in Section
4
has two practical limitations. First, the construction of a consistent quantizer (cf. Definition
3
as well as Lemma
4.4
and its proof) is quite involved and computationally challenging. This can be much simplified in more specific models. Second, the optimization problem in (
4.8
) of the estimation procedure outlined in Subsection

can be solved by the linear programming approach of

, but the runtime of that program is exponential in the resolution

of the quantizer

, for which the linear program can still be solved on standard hardware, the solution approximates the optimal mechanism

sufficiently well.

5.2
The Gaussian location model

Consider

and is finite for all

\Sigma_{n,\theta}

) and LeCam’s first lemma

we also have

. Thus, if we can show that
\exp\left(-\Gamma_{n,\theta}\right)\to 1
in

, we obtain
\mathcal{M}_{n}(\hbar)\to{\mathbb{E}}[v(\Sigma_{\theta})]={\mathbb{E}}[u(\Sigma_{\theta})\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}h^{T}\Sigma_{\theta}h\right)]
. But the latter expression is equal to

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[u(\Sigma_{\theta})\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}h^{T}\Sigma_{\theta}h\right)\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[u(\Sigma_{\theta}){\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp(h^{T}\Sigma_{\theta}^{1/2}\Delta)\Big|\Sigma_{\theta}\right]\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[u(\Sigma_{\theta})\exp(h^{T}\Sigma_{\theta}^{1/2}\Delta)\right],

for a random vector

independent of
\Sigma_{\theta}

\Gamma_{n,\theta}=o_{P}(1)
under

\exp\left(-\Gamma_{n,\theta}\right)
with respect to
R_{\theta+h_{n}}
. Since
\frac{1}{2}h^{T}\Sigma_{n,\theta}h
is bounded by a finite constant uniformly in

with probability converging to zero we have
{\mathbb{E}}_{n,\theta+h_{n}}[V_{n}\mathds{1}_{B_{n}}]\to 0
. For (i) simply observe

{\mathbb{E}}_{n,\theta+h_{n}}[V_{n}]={\mathbb{E}}_{n,\theta+h_{n}}\left[\exp(h^{T}\Delta_{n,\theta})\frac{dR_{n,\theta}}{dR_{n,\theta+h_{n}}}\right]={\mathbb{E}}_{n,\theta}\left[\exp(h^{T}\Delta_{n,\theta})\right],

which was already shown to be bounded by
\exp(\|h\|^{2}e^{4\alpha}I_{\theta}({\mathcal{P}})/2)
. Similarly, for (ii) write

{\mathbb{E}}_{n,\theta+h_{n}}[V_{n}\mathds{1}_{B_{n}}]={\mathbb{E}}_{n,\theta}\left[\exp(h^{T}\Delta_{n,\theta})\mathds{1}_{B_{n}}\right]\leq\sqrt{{\mathbb{E}}_{n,\theta}\left[\exp(2h^{T}\Delta_{n,\theta})\right]R_{n,\theta}(B_{n})},

the upper bound now converging to zero in view of contiguity
R_{n,\theta}\vartriangleleft R_{n,\theta+h_{n}}
. ∎

, because
\{\theta\in\Theta:F_{\theta}^{\dagger}(1-\gamma_{m})\leq t\}=\{\theta\in\Theta:1-\gamma_{m}\leq F_{\theta}(t)\}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{p}

\theta\mapsto N_{m,1}(\theta):=\min\{n\in{\mathbb{N}}:P_{\theta}(p_{\theta}\geq n)\leq\gamma_{m}\}

t\in{\mathbb{R}}
. By an analogous argument, also
\theta\mapsto N_{m,2}(\theta):=\min\{n\in{\mathbb{N}}:P_{\theta}(p_{\theta}<\frac{1}{n})\leq\gamma_{m}\}

L_{m}(\theta)
. To close the preliminary considerations, set

.

Lemma D.1
.

Let
{\mathcal{P}}=(P_{\theta})_{\theta\in\Theta}
be a statistical model with open parameter space
\Theta\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
and a finite sample space
\mathcal{X}
. Write

for the corresponding probability mass functions. If
\theta\mapsto p_{\theta}(x)
is twice differentiable on
\Theta

and

\alpha
-private channel on
\mathcal{X}

is differentiable on
\Theta
.

For given

, let
({\mathcal{Z}},\mathcal{G})
denote the measurable space on which the private data are generated, that is, for each
x\in\mathcal{X}
,
Q(\cdot|x)
is a probability measure on
({\mathcal{Z}},\mathcal{G})
. For simplicity, write
I_{\theta}=I_{\theta}(Q{\mathcal{P}})
. We will show that for every
\theta\in\Theta
the quotient
\frac{I_{\theta+h}-I_{\theta}}{h}
has a limit as
h\to 0
. First, let
q(z|x)
be as in Lemma
D.2
. In particular,
e^{-\alpha}\leq q(z|x)\leq e^{\alpha}
, for all
z\in{\mathcal{Z}}
and all
x\in\mathcal{X}
,
(z,x)\mapsto q(z|x)p_{\theta}(x)
is a
\nu\otimes\mu
-density of
Q(dz|x)P_{\theta}(dx)
and
\nu
is a probability measure on
({\mathcal{Z}},\mathcal{G})
. The model
Q{\mathcal{P}}
can thus be described by its
\nu

and

and we have

\displaystyle\frac{I_{\theta+h}-I_{\theta}}{h}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{Z}}\frac{1}{h}\left[\frac{\dot{q}_{\theta+h}^{2}(z)}{q_{\theta+h}(z)}-\frac{\dot{q}_{\theta}^{2}(z)}{q_{\theta}(z)}\right]\nu(dz)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{Z}}\frac{\dot{q}_{\theta+h}^{2}(z)-\dot{q}_{\theta}^{2}(z)}{h}\frac{1}{q_{\theta}(z)}\frac{q_{\theta}(z)}{q_{\theta+h}(z)}+\dot{q}_{\theta}^{2}(z)\frac{q_{\theta}(z)-q_{\theta+h}(z)}{hq_{\theta+h}(z)q_{\theta}(z)}\nu(dz).

(D.1)

as

and its derivatives. To see boundedness of the integrand in (

e^{-\alpha}\leq q_{\theta}(z)\leq e^{\alpha}
. Next, notice that with

\displaystyle\sup_{h\in U}\left|\frac{\dot{q}_{\theta+h}^{2}(z)-\dot{q}_{\theta}^{2}(z)}{h}\right|=\sup_{h\in U}\left|\frac{\left[\dot{q}_{\theta+h}(z)-\dot{q}_{\theta}(z)+\dot{q}_{\theta}(z)\right]^{2}-\dot{q}_{\theta}^{2}(z)}{h}\right|

Lemma D.2
.

\alpha\in(0,\infty)
and

\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{G}_{n}
are countably generated. Fix
x^{*}\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
,
i\in[n]
and define

\displaystyle Q^{(i)}(dz_{1:i}|x_{1:i})

\displaystyle:=Q_{z_{1:i-1}}(dz_{i}|x_{i})Q_{z_{1:i-2}}(dz_{i-1}|x_{i-1})\cdots Q_{\varnothing}(dz_{1}|x_{1})

\displaystyle\nu^{(i)}(dz_{1:i})

\displaystyle:=Q_{z_{1:i-1}}(dz_{i}|x_{i}^{*})Q_{z_{1:i-2}}(dz_{i-1}|x_{i-1}^{*})\cdots Q_{\varnothing}(dz_{1}|x_{1}^{*}),\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\nu_{z_{1:i-1}}(dz)

\displaystyle:=Q_{z_{1:i-1}}(dz_{i}|x_{i}^{*}),

where we adopt the convention that whenever a sub- or superscript is equal to
0
the corresponding quantity is omitted.
If the collection
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathfrak{P}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{F})
of probability measures is dominated by the
\sigma
-finite measure
\mu
with densities
p_{P}=\frac{dP}{d\mu}
, then the following hold true for each
i\in[n]
:

i)

For all
P\in\mathcal{P}
and all
A\in\mathcal{G}^{(i)}
we have
e^{-i\alpha}Q^{(i)}P^{i}(A)\leq\nu^{(i)}(A)\leq e^{i\alpha}Q^{(i)}P^{i}(A)
and we write
r_{i,P}:=\frac{dQ^{(i)}P^{i}}{d\nu^{(i)}}
for a corresponding density.

ii)

For every
i\in[n]
there exists a measurable function
q_{i}:\mathcal{Z}_{i}\times\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Z}^{(i-1)}\to[0,\infty)
, such that for every
P\in{\mathcal{P}}
and for every
z_{1:i-1}\in\mathcal{Z}^{(i-1)}
,

(z_{i},x_{i})\mapsto q_{i}(z_{i}|x_{i},z_{1:i-1})p_{P}(x_{i})

is a
\nu_{z_{1:i-1}}\otimes\mu
-density of the joint distribution
Q_{z_{1:i-1}}(dz_{i}|x_{i})P(dx_{i})
of
Z_{i},X_{i}
given
Z_{1:i-1}=z_{1:i-1}
and
q_{i}(z|x,z_{1:i-1})\in[e^{-\alpha},e^{\alpha}]
for
\nu_{z_{1:i-1}}\otimes\mu
-almost all
(z,x)
.

iii)

For every
P\in{\mathcal{P}}
and for
q_{i,P}(z_{i}|z_{1},\dots,z_{i-1}):=\int_{\mathcal{X}}q_{i}(z_{i}|x_{i},z_{1:i-1})P(dx_{i})
we have
q_{i,P}(z_{i}|z_{1},\dots,z_{i-1})r_{i-1,P}(z_{1:i-1})=r_{i,P}(z_{1:i})
,
\nu^{(i)}
-almost surely.

For
x\in\mathcal{X}^{i}
and
A\in\mathcal{G}^{(i)}
, by
\alpha
-differential privacy (
1.3
) we have
Q^{(i)}(A|x)\leq e^{i\alpha}Q^{(i)}(A|x^{*})=e^{i\alpha}\nu^{(i)}(A)
and hence

[Q^{(i)}P^{i}](A)=\int_{\mathcal{X}^{i}}Q^{(i)}(A|x)P^{i}(dx)\leq e^{i\alpha}\nu^{(i)}(A).

Exchanging the roles of
x
and
x^{*}
proves the claim in
i
).

For Part
ii
), first note that
\mu
is dominated by a probability measure
\bar{\mu}
, say, and write
m=\frac{d\mu}{d\bar{\mu}}
. Now fix
z_{1:i-1}\in\mathcal{Z}^{(i-1)}
and define the Markov kernels
\bar{R}(dz_{i},dx_{i}|z_{1:i-1}):=Q_{z_{1:i-1}}(dz_{i}|x_{i})\bar{\mu}(dx_{i})
,
\bar{S}(dz_{i},dx_{i}|z_{1:i-1}):=\nu_{z_{1:i-1}}(dz_{i})\otimes\bar{\mu}(dx_{i})
,
R(dz_{i},dx_{i}|z_{1:i-1}):=Q_{z_{1:i-1}}(dz_{i}|x_{i})\mu(dx_{i})
and
S(dz_{i},dx_{i}|z_{1:i-1}):=\nu_{z_{1:i-1}}(dz_{i})\otimes\mu(dx_{i})
. For
C\in\mathcal{G}_{i}\otimes\mathcal{F}
, we have

\displaystyle\bar{R}(C|z_{1:i-1})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}Q_{z_{1:i-1}}(C_{x}|x)\bar{\mu}(dx)\leq e^{\alpha}\int_{\mathcal{X}}Q_{z_{1:i-1}}(C_{x}|x^{*})\bar{\mu}(dx)=e^{\alpha}\bar{S}(C|z_{1:i-1})

and thus,
\bar{R}_{z_{1:i-1}}\ll\bar{S}_{z_{1:i-1}}
. By Theorem 58 in
Dellacherie and Meyer 1982
there exists measurable
\bar{q}_{i}:\mathcal{Z}_{i}\times\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Z}^{(i-1)}\to[0,\infty)
such that for all
z_{1:i-1}
,
(z,x)\mapsto\bar{q}_{i}(z|x,z_{1:i-1})
is a
\bar{S}_{z_{1:i-1}}
-density of
\bar{R}_{z_{1:i-1}}
, which is clearly independent of
P
. Thus,
(z,x)\mapsto q_{i}(z|x,z_{1:i-1}):=\frac{\bar{q}_{i}(z|x,z_{1:i-1})}{m(x)}
is a
{S}_{z_{1:i-1}}
-density of
{R}_{z_{1:i-1}}
.
Notice that for arbitrary
C\in\mathcal{G}_{i}\otimes\mathcal{F}
,

\displaystyle\int_{C}q_{i}(z|x,z_{1:i-1})\cdot p_{P}(x)\;S_{z_{1:i-1}}(dz,dx)=\int_{C}\frac{d{R}_{z_{1:i-1}}}{d{S}_{z_{1:i-1}}}\cdot p_{P}\;d{S}_{z_{1:i-1}}=\int_{C}p_{P}\;d{R}_{z_{1:i-1}}

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{\mathcal{X}}Q_{z_{1:i-1}}(C_{x}|x)p_{P}(x)\mu(dx)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}Q_{z_{1:i-1}}(C_{x}|x)P(dx).

Hence,
q_{i}\cdot p_{P}
is an
S_{z_{1:i-1}}
-density of
Q_{z_{1:i-1}}(dz|x)P(dx)
.
Furthermore, still for fixed
z_{1:i-1}
and for arbitrary
C\in\mathcal{G}_{i}\otimes\mathcal{F}
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{C}e^{-\alpha}dS_{z_{1:i-1}}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}e^{-\alpha}\nu_{z_{1:i-1}}(C_{x})\mu(dx)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathcal{X}}Q_{z_{1:i-1}}(C_{x}|x)\mu(dx)=R_{z_{1:i-1}}(C)=\int_{C}q_{i}dS_{z_{1:i-1}}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathcal{X}}e^{\alpha}\nu_{z_{1:i-1}}(C_{x})\mu(dx)=\int_{C}e^{\alpha}dS_{z_{1:i-1}}.

We conclude that
e^{-\alpha}\leq{q}_{i}\leq e^{\alpha}
, for
S_{z_{1:i-1}}
-almost all
(z,x)\in\mathcal{Z}_{i}\times\mathcal{X}
.

For
iii
) simply integrate the density in part
ii
) with respect to
\mu
.
∎

Lemma D.4
.

Fix
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
\alpha\in(0,\infty)
. For
Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Z})
and
B=(B_{j})_{j\in[k]}
a measurable partition of
\mathcal{X}
, the ‘projection’
Q_{k}
defined in (
4.6
) is an
\alpha
-differentially private Markov kernel, that is,
Q_{k}\in\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}([k]\to\mathcal{Z})
.

The Markov property is obvious from the definition and since
[k]
is a finite set. For
\alpha
-differential privacy, fix
C\in\mathcal{G}
and
j,j^{\prime}\in[k]
.

If
j,j^{\prime}\in J
, choose
x^{\prime}\in B_{j^{\prime}}
such that
Q(C|x^{\prime})\leq Q_{k}(C|j^{\prime})
, which is possible since the upper bound is equal to the expectation
{\mathbb{E}}_{P_{j^{\prime}}}[Q(C|\cdot)]
. Therefore, we have
Q_{k}(C|j)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}Q(C|x)P_{j}(dx)\leq e^{\alpha}Q(C|x^{\prime})\leq e^{\alpha}Q_{k}(C|j^{\prime})
.

If
j\in J
and
j^{\prime}\notin J
, then
Q_{k}(C|j)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}Q(C|x)P_{j}(dx)\leq e^{\alpha}Q(C|x_{0})=e^{\alpha}Q_{k}(C|j^{\prime})
. The case
j^{\prime}\in J
and
j\notin J
is analogous.

If both
j,j^{\prime}\notin J
, the desired inequality is trivially satisfied.
∎

Lemma D.5
.

For
\gamma>0
and
c,d\in{\mathbb{R}}
, we have

|c^{2}-d^{2}|\leq(1+\gamma)(c-d)^{2}+\frac{c^{2}\land d^{2}}{\gamma}.

Without loss of generality, let
|c|>|d|>0
and define

f(\gamma):=(1+\gamma)(c-d)^{2}+d^{2}(1+\gamma^{-1})-c^{2}.

We need to show that
f(\gamma)\geq 0
. For both
\gamma\to 0
and
\gamma\to\infty
we have
f(\gamma)\to\infty
. Thus, we only need to show that
f
is non-negative at its minimum. The first order condition for a minimum is

f^{\prime}(\gamma)=(c-d)^{2}-\gamma^{-2}d^{2}=0,

which is achieved at
\gamma^{*}=\frac{d}{c-d}
. But

f(\gamma^{*})=\left(\frac{c-d}{c-d}+\frac{d}{c-d}\right)(c-d)^{2}+d^{2}\left(\frac{d}{d}+\frac{c-d}{d}\right)-c^{2}=c(c-d)+cd-c^{2}=0,

which finishes the proof.
∎

Lemma D.6
.

Let
(\mathcal{W},\mathcal{E})
, (
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{F})
,
(\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{G})
and
({\mathcal{Z}},\mathcal{H})
be measurable spaces and consider
R\in\mathfrak{P}(\mathcal{W}\to\mathcal{X})
,
Q\in\mathfrak{P}(\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y})
and
T\in\mathfrak{P}(\mathcal{Y}\to{\mathcal{Z}})
. If
Q
is
\alpha
-private, then so are
QR
and
TQ
.

Clearly
QR\in\mathfrak{P}(\mathcal{W}\to\mathcal{Y})
. Fix
F\in\mathcal{F}
and
w,w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}
and pick
x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
such that
Q(F|x^{\prime})\leq\int_{\mathcal{X}}Q(F|x)R(dx|w^{\prime})
. To see that this is possible, first consider the case where
\inf_{x\in\mathcal{X}}Q(F|x)
is attained. In that case we can simply take
x^{\prime}
to be the minimizer. Otherwise, we have
\inf_{\bar{x}\in\mathcal{X}}Q(F|\bar{x})<Q(F|x)
, for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
. By strict monotonicity of the integral, this implies
\inf_{\bar{x}\in\mathcal{X}}Q(F|\bar{x})<QR(F|w^{\prime})
. Since the infimum can be approximated to arbitrary precision, we can find the desired
x^{\prime}
. Therefore, using
\alpha
-privacy of
Q
, we have
QR(F|w)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}Q(F|x)R(dx|w)\leq e^{\alpha}Q(F|x^{\prime})\leq e^{\alpha}QR(F|w^{\prime})
.

To establish
\alpha
-privacy of
TQ\in\mathfrak{P}(\mathcal{X}\to{\mathcal{Z}})
, fix
Z\in{\mathcal{Z}}
,
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
. Notice that for any
Y\in\mathcal{G}
, we have
\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\mathds{1}_{Y}(y)Q(dy|x)=Q(Y|x)\leq e^{\alpha}Q(Y|x^{\prime})=e^{\alpha}\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\mathds{1}_{Y}(y)Q(dy|x^{\prime})
and approximate the integrand
y\mapsto T(Z|y)
in
TQ(Z|x)=\int_{\mathcal{Y}}T(Z|y)Q(dy|x)
by simple functions.
∎

Lemma D.7
.

Let
\lambda\in(0,1)
,
x,y>0
and
a,b\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Then

\frac{(\lambda a+(1-\lambda)b)^{2}}{\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y}\leq\lambda\frac{a^{2}}{x}+(1-\lambda)\frac{b^{2}}{y}.

We expand the square and multiply both sides by
\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y
to obtain, after simplifying, the equivalent inequality

2ab\leq a^{2}\frac{y}{x}+b^{2}\frac{x}{y}.

Now define
h(a):=a^{2}\frac{y}{x}+b^{2}\frac{x}{y}-2ab
. We want to show that
h(a)\geq 0
for all
a\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Since
h^{\prime}(a)=2a\frac{y}{x}-2b
and
h^{\prime\prime}(a)=2\frac{y}{x}>0
, we easily see that
h
is strictly convex and attains its unique minimum at
a^{*}=b\frac{x}{y}
. Since
h(a)\geq h(a^{*})=0
the claim follows.
∎

Lemma D.8
.

For an open subset
\Theta\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
and a measure space
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{F},\mu)
, let
f:\Theta\times\mathcal{X}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be continuously differentiable in its first argument and such that
x\mapsto f(\theta,x)
and
x\mapsto\dot{f}(\theta,x):=\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}f(\theta,x)
are
\mu
-integrable for every
\theta\in\Theta
and
\theta\mapsto\|\dot{f}(\theta,\cdot)\|_{L_{1}(\mu)}
is continuous and finite. Let
q:\mathcal{X}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be measurable and bounded. Then
\theta\mapsto\hat{q}(\theta):=\int_{\mathcal{X}}q(x)f(\theta,x)\mu(dx)
is differentiable with derivative
\frac{d}{d\theta}\hat{q}(\theta)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}q(x)\dot{f}(\theta,x)\mu(dx)
. If, in addition,
\theta\mapsto\dot{f}(\theta,\cdot)
is continuous as a function from
\Theta
to
L_{1}(\mu)
, then the derivative
\theta\mapsto\frac{d}{d\theta}\hat{q}(\theta)
is also continuous.

For
h\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus\{0\}
,
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
\theta\in\Theta
, write
g_{h}(x):=\frac{1}{h}[f(\theta+h,x)-f(\theta,x)]\to\dot{f}(\theta,x)
as
h\to 0
, and note that

\frac{1}{h}[\hat{q}(\theta+h)-\hat{q}(\theta)]=\int_{\mathcal{X}}q(x)g_{h}(x)\mu(dx).

By the fundamental theorem of calculus,
g_{h}(x)=\int_{0}^{1}\dot{f}(\theta+uh,x)du
and thus
\|g_{h}\|_{L_{1}(\mu)}\leq\int_{0}^{1}\|\dot{f}(\theta+uh,\cdot)\|_{L_{1}(\mu)}du
. By assumption, the integral in the upper bound is finite and continuous as a function in
h
on a closed neighborhood
U
of
0
and thus also uniformly bounded on
U
. By the dominated convergence theorem the integral converges to
\|\dot{f}(\theta,\cdot)\|_{L_{1}(\mu)}
as
h\to 0
. We conclude that
\limsup_{h\to 0}\|g_{h}\|_{L_{1}(\mu)}\leq\|\dot{f}(\theta,\cdot)\|_{L_{1}(\mu)}
. Thus, Proposition 2.29 in
van der Vaart 2007
yields
\|g_{h}-\dot{f}(\theta,\cdot)\|_{L_{1}(\mu)}\to 0
, as
h\to 0
, and we obtain

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{h}[\hat{q}(\theta+h)-\hat{q}(\theta)]-\int_{\mathcal{X}}q(x)\dot{f}(\theta,x)\mu(dx)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathcal{X}}|q(x)||g_{h}(x)-\dot{f}(\theta,x)|\mu(dx)

\displaystyle\leq\|q\|_{\infty}\|g_{h}-\dot{f}(\theta,\cdot)\|_{L_{1}(\mu)}\xrightarrow[h\to 0]{}0.

For the continuity claim, simply note that

\left|\int_{\mathcal{X}}q(x)\dot{f}(\theta^{\prime},x)\mu(dx)-\int_{\mathcal{X}}q(x)\dot{f}(\theta,x)\mu(dx)\right|\leq\|q\|_{\infty}\|\dot{f}(\theta^{\prime},\cdot)-\dot{f}(\theta,\cdot)\|_{L_{1}(\mu)}\xrightarrow[\theta^{\prime}\to\theta]{}0.

∎
