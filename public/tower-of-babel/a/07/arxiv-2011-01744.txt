Factorization of Quaternionic Polynomials of Bi-Degree (n,1)
J. Lercher and D. F. Scharler and H.-P. Schröcker and J. Siegele

Abstract
. We consider polynomials of bi-degree ( n , 1 ) (n,1) over the skew field of quaternions where the indeterminates commute with each other and with all coefficients. Polynomials of this type do not generally admit factorizations. We recall a necessary and sufficient condition for existence of a factorization with univariate linear factors that has originally been stated by Skopenkov and Krasauskas. Such a factorization is, in general, non-unique by known factorization results for univariate quaternionic polynomials. We unveil existence of bivariate polynomials with non-unique factorizations that cannot be explained in this way and characterize them geometrically and algebraically. Existence of factorizations is related to the existence of special rulings of two different types (left/right) on the ruled surface parameterized by the bivariate polynomial in the projective space over the quaternions. Special non-uniqueness in above sense can be explained algebraically by commutation properties of factors in suitable factorizations. A necessary geometric condition for this to happen is degeneration to a point of at least one of the left/right rulings.

2.1.
Factorization of Univariate Polynomials

Factorizability of univariate quaternionic polynomials will turn out to be of great importance for our theory. For later reference, we formulate a theorem that states some crucial univariate factorization results. We provide short sketches of proofs even though the results are already well known. For more details we refer to the respective literature.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
Q\in\mathbb{H}[t]
be a quaternionic polynomial,
\deg(Q)\geq 1
. There is a unique representation
Q=\gcd(Q)Q^{\prime}
with a polynomial
Q^{\prime}\in\mathbb{H}[t]
and
\deg(Q^{\prime})=n
,
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

(a)

If
\gcd(Q)\neq 1
, the real polynomial

\gcd(Q)
admits a factorization into univariate linear factors over
\mathbb{H}
.

(b)

There exist up to
n
different monic linear
right factors of the polynomial
Q^{\prime}
.
1
1

1

We call a factor
t-h\in\mathbb{H}[t]
a linear
right factor
of a univariate polynomial
Q\in\mathbb{H}[t]
if
Q=S(t-h)
for an appropriate
S\in\mathbb{H}[t]
. Beware that this
common denotation in the context of non-commutative rings will change
its meaning in the context of star-one-polynomials (c. f. Section

5
).

(c)

There exist up to
n!
different factorizations of
Q^{\prime}
into
univariate linear factors over
\mathbb{H}
.

Sketch of proof.

The polynomial
\gcd(Q)
admits a factorization into linear factors over
\mathbb{C}
. All
factorizations of
\gcd(Q)
with linear factors over
\mathbb{H}
are found by replacing the
complex unit
\mathrm{i}
in each pair of conjugate complex linear factors by
a quaternion

satisfying

. These are precisely the quaternions
with zero real part and
h{h}^{\ast}=1
(c. f.
[
10
]
). This shows Part (a).

Since
Q^{\prime}
does not have a real polynomial factor of positive degree, the norm polynomial can be written as
Q^{\prime}{Q^{\prime}}^{\ast}=a{a}^{\ast}M_{1}\cdots M_{n}
with

and monic, quadratic, irreducible real polynomials

(the case

is also possible). By Part (a), each

can be
factored in infinitely many ways as

,

. There is

that is also a right factor of

(c. f.
[
1
,
3
]

such that

and

whence

. If

are

different right factors of

. This

In order to find a factorization of

into univariate linear factors, we use Part (b) and iteratively produce linear right factors of
Q^{\prime}
. The thus obtained factorization depends on an order of the
factors

, …,
M_{n}
. Using different orders one obtains all
factorizations of
Q^{\prime}
. In general (if the factors
M_{1}
, …,
M_{n}
are
pairwise different), there are

factorizations (c. f.
[
3
]
), which proves Part (c). ∎

.

Let

be a monic star-one-polynomial with

and
consider two representatives
(F_{h_{i},h,k_{j}})_{n,m}

(F_{\hat{h}_{i},\hat{h},\hat{k}_{j}})_{l,r}

[(F_{h_{i},h,k_{j}})_{n,m}]=[(F_{\hat{h}_{i},\hat{h},\hat{k}_{j}})_{l,r}]\in\mathrm{Fact}(Q)

,

It is clear that

and

\displaystyle Q=\

\displaystyle(F_{h_{i},h,k_{j}})_{n,m}=\underbrace{(t-h_{1})\cdots(t-h_{n})}_{=:P_{1}}(s-h)\underbrace{(t-k_{1})\cdots(t-k_{m})}_{=:P_{2}}

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle(F_{\hat{h}_{i},\hat{h},\hat{k}_{j}})_{n,m}=\underbrace{(t-\hat{h}_{1})\cdots(t-\hat{h}_{n})}_{=:\hat{P}_{1}}(s-\hat{h})\underbrace{(t-\hat{k}_{1})\cdots(t-\hat{k}_{m})}_{=:\hat{P}_{2}}

with

and
P_{2}{P_{2}}^{\ast}=\hat{P}_{2}{\hat{P}_{2}}^{\ast}
. Without loss of

for

and

for

and
\hat{P}_{2}

There exist polynomials

[

Now it is possible to cancel out the factor

5.
Factorizations and Null Lines

In this section, we assume that the monic polynomial
Q\in\mathbb{H}_{\ast 1}
with
\gcd(Q)=1
admits a factorization, that is

(4)

Q=(F_{h_{i},h,k_{j}})_{n,m}=(t-h_{1})\cdots(t-h_{n})(s-h)(t-k_{1})\cdots(t-k_{m})

with
h_{1},\ldots,h_{n}
,
h
,
k_{1},\ldots,k_{m}\in\mathbb{H}
. In the following, we
develop a criterion to decide whether this factorization is essentially unique,
that is, whether there exists only one equivalence class of factorizations. We
call each linear polynomial
t-h_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, a
left factor
of
factorization (
4
) since it arises as a factor on the left-hand
side of the
s
-factor
s-h
. Analogously, each linear polynomial
t-k_{i}
,

i=1,\ldots,m
, is called a
right factor
of factorization
(
4
).

For
i=1,\ldots,n
we define

(5)

M_{i}\coloneqq(t-h_{i}){(t-h_{i})}^{\ast}\in\mathbb{R}[t].

Analogously, for
j=1,\ldots,m
we set

(6)

N_{j}\coloneqq(t-k_{j}){(t-k_{j})}^{\ast}\in\mathbb{R}[t].

It holds that

(7)

Q{Q}^{\ast}=PR\text{ with }P=M_{1}\cdots M_{n}N_{1}\cdots N_{m}\text{ and }R=(s-h){(s-h)}^{\ast}.

Moreover, for
i=1,\ldots,n
and
j=1,\ldots,m
the polynomials
M_{i}
and
N_{j}
are
monic
and
irreducible
in
\mathbb{R}[t]
and
\deg M_{i}=2=\deg N_{j}
.

A polynomial
Q=Q_{0}+sQ_{1}\in\mathbb{H}_{\ast 1}
gives rise to a ruled surface in

\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{C}\mathbb{H})
which is parameterized as
2
2

2

Our parametric equation
(
8
) misses the curve
Q_{1}(t)
and the ruling spanned by the
leading coefficients of
Q_{0}
and
Q_{1}
. This defect could be overcome by
either homogenizing the polynomial and using projective parameters or by
properly defining evaluation at parameter values
s=\infty
,
t=\infty
.
For the sake of simplicity of notation we refrain from doing this. This will
not affect validity of our arguments.

(8)

\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{C}\mathbb{H})\colon(s,t)\mapsto[Q(s,t)]=[Q_{0}(t)+sQ_{1}(t)].

The
s
-parameter lines are the surface rulings. It will turn out that existence of a factorization of
Q
, its essential uniqueness and also the number of left and right factors are related to special rulings on this surface, namely rulings that lie on the
null quadric
:

Definition 5.1
.

Consider the symmetric bilinear form
b
defined as

\mathbb{C}\mathbb{H}\times\mathbb{C}\mathbb{H}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}\colon(h_{1},h_{2})\mapsto b(h_{1},h_{2})=\frac{1}{2}(h_{1}{h}^{\ast}_{2}+h_{2}{h}^{\ast}_{1}).

The quadric
\mathcal{N}\subseteq\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{C}\mathbb{H})
represented by this bilinear form is
called
null quadric
. The
points
of
\mathcal{N}
are precisely the elements

[h]\in\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{C}\mathbb{H})
with
b(h,h)=h{h}^{\ast}=0
, that is, elements in
\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{C}\mathbb{H})

represented by complex quaternions with zero norm. Lines in
\mathcal{N}
are called

null lines
.

Let us fix a complex number
z\in\mathbb{C}=\mathbb{R}+\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}
. For
i=1,2
, we view

Q_{i}\in\mathbb{H}[t]
as an element of
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{H}[t]
and define the
evaluation

Q_{i}(z)\in\mathbb{C}\mathbb{H}
by replacing the indeterminate
t
by
z
. This substitution is
uncritical since
z
commutes with all elements in
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{H}
.

Consider the projective span
[Q_{0}(z)]\vee[Q_{1}(z)]
which is parameterized by

\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{C}\mathbb{H}):s\mapsto[Q_{0}(z)+sQ_{1}(z)].

The projective span
[Q_{0}(z)]\vee[Q_{1}(z)]
is either a straight line or degenerates to a point
if
[Q_{0}(z)]=[Q_{1}(z)]
. Moreover, it may happen that one of the quaternions,

Q_{0}(z)
or
Q_{1}(z)
, equals
0
. If that is the case, we also use the notation

[Q_{0}(z)]\vee[Q_{1}(z)]
even though we actually just consider the point
[Q_{1}(z)]
or
[Q_{0}(z)]
.

It will turn out to be advantageous to classify null lines. It is known that
the null quadric
\mathcal{N}
is covered by two families of straight lines. Elements of
the first family
\mathcal{L}
are called
left rulings
, elements of the
second family
\mathcal{R}
are called
right rulings.
Any null line is
either a left ruling or a right ruling of
\mathcal{N}
. For more details we refer to

[
12
, Theorem 8.3.2.]
. Each point
[h]\in\mathcal{N}
is incident with
exactly one left ruling
L_{[h]}
and exactly one right ruling
R_{[h]}
. In

[
13
]
it is shown that these two straight lines are given by

(9)

L_{[h]}\coloneqq\{[q]\in\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{C}\mathbb{H}):q{h}^{\ast}=0\}\quad\text{ and }\quad R_{[h]}\coloneqq\{[q]\in\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{C}\mathbb{H}):{h}^{\ast}q=0\}.

The equations
q{h}^{\ast}=0
and
{h}^{\ast}q=0
are not equivalent since
multiplication of quaternions is not commutative. The two lines
L_{[h]}
and

R_{[h]}
are indeed different.

If
z\in\mathbb{C}
is a complex zero of the norm polynomial’s univariate factor
P\in\mathbb{R}[t]
, we obtain

(Q_{0}(z)+sQ_{1}(z)){(Q_{0}(z)+sQ_{1}(z))}^{\ast}=P(z)R=0

and hence the projective span
[Q_{0}(z)]\vee[Q_{1}(z)]
is (at least contained in) a null line.
(Note that
Q_{0}(z)=Q_{1}(z)=0
is not possible because of the assumption

\gcd(Q)=1
.) The following theorem provides a more precise geometric
interpretation for the zeros of
P
in terms of left and right rulings. Note the
careful formulation “contained in a left/right ruling”. It leaves open the
possibility that
[Q_{0}(z)]\vee[Q_{1}(z)]
is just a point.

Theorem 5.2
.

Assume that a monic polynomial
Q=Q_{0}+sQ_{1}\in\mathbb{H}_{\ast 1}
with
\gcd(Q)=1
admits a factorization of the form (
4
). Moreover, let
M_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
,
N_{j}
,
j=1,\ldots,m
, and
P
be defined as in (
5
), (
6
) and (
7
). Let
z\in\mathbb{C}
be a complex zero of
P
.

(a)

If there exists
j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
such that
N_{j}(z)=0
, then
[Q_{0}(z)]\vee[Q_{1}(z)]
is contained in a left ruling.

(b)

If there exists
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
such that
M_{i}(z)=0
, then
[Q_{0}(z)]\vee[Q_{1}(z)]
is contained in a right ruling.

Proof.

Let us first consider Part (a). There is nothing to show if
[Q_{0}(z)]\vee[Q_{1}(z)]
is just a
point. Hence, we can assume that
[Q_{0}(z)]
and
[Q_{1}(z)]
are two distinct
points. We want to show that
[Q_{1}(z)]
lies on the unique left ruling through

[Q_{0}(z)]
. By the defining condition (
9
) for left rulings, this is
equivalent to
Q_{1}(z){Q_{0}}^{\ast}(z)=0
(we use the denotation

{Q_{0}}^{\ast}(z)={(Q_{0}(z))}^{\ast}

Because
Q
admits a factorization of the form (
4
), we have

(10)

\displaystyle Q_{0}

\displaystyle=-(t-h_{1})\cdots(t-h_{n})h(t-k_{1})\cdots(t-k_{m}),

\displaystyle Q_{1}

\displaystyle=(t-h_{1})\cdots(t-h_{n})(t-k_{1})\cdots(t-k_{m})

(we already used these representations in the proof of
Proposition
4.3
). Hence

(11)

\displaystyle Q_{1}{Q_{0}}^{\ast}=\ -

\displaystyle(t-h_{1})\cdots(t-h_{n})(t-k_{1})\cdots(t-k_{m})

\displaystyle{\displaystyle(t-k_{m})}^{\ast}\cdots{(t-k_{1})}^{\ast}{h}^{\ast}{(t-h_{n})}^{\ast}\cdots{(t-h_{1})}^{\ast}

\displaystyle=\ -

\displaystyle(t-h_{1})\cdots(t-h_{n}){h}^{\ast}{(t-h_{n})}^{\ast}\cdots{(t-h_{1})}^{\ast}N_{1}\cdots N_{m}.

Evaluating at

yields

since

one can show that

Theorem
5.2
can be interpreted as follows: Each single left factor
of factorization (
4
) gives rise to two conjugate complex
parameter values
z
,
\overline{z}\in\mathbb{C}
with
[Q_{0}(z)]\vee[Q_{1}(z)]
(resp.

[Q_{0}(\overline{z})]\vee[Q_{1}(\overline{z})]

left rulings.

The algebraic criterion for
[Q_{0}(z)]\vee[Q_{1}(z)]
being contained in a left/right ruling is the
vanishing of
Q_{1}(z){Q_{0}}^{\ast}(z)
and
{Q_{0}}^{\ast}(z)Q_{1}(z)
, respectively. Denote by

(12)

A\coloneqq\{z\in\mathbb{C}:Q_{1}(z){Q_{0}}^{\ast}(z)=0\}\text{ and }B\coloneqq\{z\in\mathbb{C}:{Q_{0}}^{\ast}(z)Q_{1}(z)=0\}

the sets of complex zeros of the polynomials
Q_{1}{Q_{0}}^{\ast}
and
{Q_{0}}^{\ast}Q_{1}
. We
say that the multiplicity
\operatorname{mult}(H,z)
of

as a zero of

equals
\mu
if
(t-z)^{\mu}
divides
H
in
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{H}[t]
and
(t-z)^{\mu+1}
does
not. Since
(t-z)
is part of the center of
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{H}[t]
, we need not distinguish
between left- and right-division.

Let us briefly explain why
H(z)=0
is equivalent to
(t-z)
dividing
H
in

\mathbb{C}\mathbb{H}[t]
: Write
H=H_{0}+\mathbf{i}H_{1}+\mathbf{j}H_{2}+\mathbf{k}H_{3}
, where
H_{i}\in\mathbb{R}[t]
for

. The fact

implies

. Since
H_{i}\in\mathbb{R}[t]
is a

H_{i}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C}[t]
such that
H_{i}=H_{i}^{\prime}(t-z)
, whence

H=H^{\prime}(t-z)
with
H^{\prime}=H_{0}^{\prime}+\mathbf{i}H_{1}^{\prime}+\mathbf{j}H_{2}^{\prime}+\mathbf{k}H_{3}^{\prime}
. If
(t-z)
divides

H
in
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{H}[t]
, it also divides
H_{i}
in
\mathbb{C}[t]
, which implies
H_{i}(z)=0
and
hence
H(z)=0
.

Write
\lambda(z)\coloneqq\operatorname{mult}(Q_{1}{Q_{0}}^{\ast},z)
and
\varrho(z)\coloneqq\operatorname{mult}({Q_{0}}^{\ast}Q_{1},z)
. We then define the
multiplicity cardinalities

\#A\coloneqq\sum_{z\in A}\lambda(z),\quad\#B\coloneqq\sum_{z\in B}\varrho(z).

Remark 5.3
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
5.2
we define
N\coloneqq N_{1}\cdots N_{m}
and
M\coloneqq M_{1}\cdots M_{n}
. From equation (
11
) it follows that

\lambda(z)\geq\operatorname{mult}(N,z)\text{ for }z\in A

and similarly

\varrho(z)\geq\operatorname{mult}(M,z)\text{ for }z\in B.

Moreover, the
\deg N=2m
complex zeros of
N
(counted with multiplicities) are elements of
A
and the
\deg M=2n
complex zeros of
M
(counted with multiplicities) are elements of
B
, which shows that the multiplicity cardinalities
\#A
and
\#B
are bounded from below by

(14)

\#A\geq 2m\quad\text{and}\quad\#B\geq 2n.

The lower bounds (

Example 5.4
.

For
Q=(t-\mathbf{i})(s-\mathbf{j})(t-\mathbf{j})
we have

Q_{1}{Q_{0}}^{\ast}=(t^{2}+1)(\mathbf{j}t^{2}-2\mathbf{k}t-\mathbf{j})\quad\text{and}\quad{Q_{0}}^{\ast}Q_{1}=\mathbf{j}(t^{2}+1)^{2}.

Therefore,

,

,

,
\varrho(\pm\mathrm{i})=2

but

.

It is natural to relate the algebraic multiplicities
\lambda(z)
and

\varrho(z)
to multiplicities of left/right rulings on the ruled surface

.

Via the Klein mapping
\gamma
(c. f.

are mapped to points of the

in

Ruled surfaces are mapped to curves such that the intersection multiplicity of

of curves from projective differential geometry. If

is a straight line,
then

6.
Uniqueness of Factorizations

With the help of the multiplicity cardinalities
\#A
and
\#B
we are now able to state a condition which guarantees essential
uniqueness of a factorization of
Q
.

Theorem 6.1

(Uniqueness Theorem)
.

Let
Q=Q_{0}+sQ_{1}\in\mathbb{H}_{\ast 1}
,
\gcd(Q)=1
and
Q{Q}^{\ast}=PR
with
P\in\mathbb{R}[t]
and
R\in\mathbb{R}[s]
. Moreover, let
\#A
and
\#B
be the multiplicity cardinalities defined in (
13
). Then
\deg P=\#A+\#B
implies
|\mathrm{Fact}(Q)|=1
.

Proof.

The assumptions guarantee existence of one factorization
(F_{h_{i},h,k_{j}})_{n,m}
of
Q
of shape (
4
). Hence
\deg P=2(m+n)
and the equality
\deg P=\#A+\#B
implies
\#A=2m
and
\#B=2n
. (Note that
\#A\geq 2m
and
\#B\geq 2n
is always satisfied by Remark
5.3
.)

Suppose there exists a second factorization

(15)

Q=(F_{\hat{h}_{i},\hat{h},\hat{k}_{j}})_{l,r}=(t-\hat{h}_{1})\cdots(t-\hat{h}_{l})(s-\hat{h})(t-\hat{k}_{1})\cdots(t-\hat{k}_{r})

such that
[(F_{h_{i},h,k_{j}})_{n,m}]\neq[(F_{\hat{h}_{i},\hat{h},\hat{k}_{j}})_{l,r}]
. By definition of (non)-equivalence,

(16)

M\coloneqq\prod_{i=1}^{n}(t-h_{i}){(t-h_{i})}^{\ast}\neq\prod_{i=1}^{l}(t-\hat{h}_{i}){(t-\hat{h}_{i})}^{\ast}\eqqcolon\hat{M}

and also

(17)

N\coloneqq\prod_{i=1}^{m}(t-k_{i}){(t-k_{i})}^{\ast}\neq\prod_{i=1}^{r}(t-\hat{k}_{i}){(t-\hat{k}_{i})}^{\ast}\eqqcolon\hat{N}

(we already defined the polynomials
M
and
N
in Remark
5.3
).
From (
16
) and (
17
) one already obtains the decompositions of

M
,
\hat{M}
,
N
and
\hat{N}
into monic irreducible real factors. There are
two possible cases which ought to lead to the inequality
M\neq\hat{M}
:

Case 1:
There exists an irreducible factor
\hat{M}_{i}\coloneqq(t-\hat{h}_{i}){(t-\hat{h}_{i})}^{\ast}
of
\hat{M}
which occurs with higher power in the irreducible
factor decomposition of
\hat{M}
than in the one of
M
. More precisely, if
z\in\mathbb{C}
is a zero of
\hat{M}_{i}
, we have
\operatorname{mult}(M,z)<\operatorname{mult}(\hat{M},z)
.

Let us again use representation (
10
) for the polynomials
Q_{0}
and

Q_{1}
. Then we have
{Q_{0}}^{\ast}Q_{1}=HM
where

(18)

H=-{(t-k_{m})}^{\ast}\cdots{(t-k_{1})}^{\ast}{h}^{\ast}(t-k_{1})\cdots(t-k_{m}).

For each
z\in B
it holds that
H(z)\neq 0
. Indeed,
H(z)=0
would imply

\#B>2n
since each of the
\deg M=2n
complex zeros of
M
(counted with
multiplicities) is an element of
B
(c. f. Remark
5.3
). Therefore, one actually obtains
\varrho(z)=\operatorname{mult}(M,z)
.

Let us cause a contradiction by using factorization (
15
): This factorization yields another description of the polynomials
Q_{0}
and
Q_{1}
:

\displaystyle Q_{0}

\displaystyle=-(t-\hat{h}_{1})\cdots(t-\hat{h}_{l})\hat{h}(t-\hat{k}_{1})\cdots(t-\hat{k}_{r}),

\displaystyle Q_{1}

\displaystyle=(t-\hat{h}_{1})\cdots(t-\hat{h}_{l})(t-\hat{k}_{1})\cdots(t-\hat{k}_{r}).

We obtain
{Q_{0}}^{\ast}Q_{1}=\hat{H}\hat{M}
where

\hat{H}=-{(t-\hat{k}_{r})}^{\ast}\cdots{(t-\hat{k}_{1})}^{\ast}{\hat{h}}^{\ast}(t-\hat{k}_{1})\cdots(t-\hat{k}_{r}),

which implies the contradiction
\varrho(z)\geq\operatorname{mult}(\hat{M},z)>\operatorname{mult}(M,z)=\varrho(z)
.

Case 2:
There exists an irreducible factor
M_{i}\coloneqq(t-h_{i}){(t-h_{i})}^{\ast}
of
M
and
z\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\operatorname{mult}(\hat{M},z)<\operatorname{mult}(M,z)
. But then
\operatorname{mult}(\hat{N},z)>\operatorname{mult}(N,z)
and we can proceed analogously by using the representation
Q_{1}{Q_{0}}^{\ast}=KN
with

K=-(t-h_{1})\cdots(t-h_{n}){h}^{\ast}{(t-h_{n})}^{\ast}\cdots{(t-h_{1})}^{\ast}

and the fact that
\#A=2m
.
∎

Remark 6.2
.

The converse of Theorem
6.1
is also true. If

|\mathrm{Fact}(Q)|=1
, we obtain
\deg(P)=\#A+\#B
. However, this is not yet obvious
and we need further preparations to be able to formulate and prove the statement
(c.f. Theorem
7.3
in Section
7
).

Corollary 6.3
.

Suppose that
Q\in\mathbb{H}_{\ast 1}
satisfies the assumptions of Theorem
6.1
. If
[Q_{0}(z)]\vee[Q_{1}(z)]
is a straight line for all zeros
z\in\mathbb{C}
of the norm
polynomial’s univariate
t
-factor
P\in\mathbb{R}[t]
, then
|\mathrm{Fact}(Q)|=1
.

Proof.

Since
[Q_{0}(z)]\vee[Q_{1}(z)]
is a straight line for all zeros
z
of
P
, the sets
A
and
B
are disjoint. Therefore, each zero of
P
is
either
contained in
A

or
in
B
. More precisely,

\deg P=2(m+n)=\#A+\#B

and the statement follows from Theorem
6.1
.
∎

Theorem
6.1
provides us with a sufficient test whether a
factorization of
Q
is essentially unique. We can compute the multiplicity
cardinalities
\#A
and
\#B
of the sets
A
and
B
, respectively, whence

\deg P=\#A+\#B
implies
|\mathrm{Fact}(Q)|=1
.

The elements of

and

are the complex numbers

determined by
(

), the multiplicity cardinalities are the sums of multiplicities

\lambda(z)=\operatorname{mult}(Q_{1}{Q_{0}}^{\ast},z)
and
\varrho(z)=\operatorname{mult}({Q_{0}}^{\ast}Q_{1},z)
for
z\in A
and

, respectively. We illustrate this at hand of a couple of

is not true.

Example 6.4
.

For

.

Consider the polynomial

7.
Non-Uniqueness of Factorizations

A polynomial
Q\in\mathbb{H}_{\ast 1}
admits a factorization if it satisfies the
factorization condition of Definition
2.2
. By Theorem
6.1
the
factorization is in general essentially unique, that is, the set
\mathrm{Fact}(Q)
of
equivalence classes consists of only one element. However, exceptions do exist.
We study this in the simple case of
Q\in\mathbb{H}_{11}
.

Example 7.1
.

Consider the polynomial
Q=(t-h)(s-k)\in\mathbb{H}_{11}
with
h
,
k\in\mathbb{H}
. A
second factorization is necessarily of the shape
Q=(s-\hat{k})(t-\hat{h})
.
Equating and expanding gives

(t-h)(s-k)=t\,s-h\,s-k\,t+h\,k=t\,s-\hat{h}\,s-\hat{k}\,t+\hat{k}\,\hat{h}=(s-\hat{k})(t-\hat{h}).

Now we compare coefficients and find
h=\hat{h}
,
k=\hat{k}
and
hk=\hat{k}\hat{h}=kh
.
Thus, a second factorization exists if and only if
h
and
k
commute.

Building on this example we see that if a polynomial
Q\in\mathbb{H}_{\ast 1}
admits the factorization

Q=(t-h_{1})\cdots(t-h_{n})(s-h)(t-k_{1})\cdots(t-k_{m}),

then commutativity of
h_{n}
and
h
(or
h
and
k_{1}
) implies existence of a
second, non-equivalent, factorization. However, one may find examples where
non-equivalent factorizations do not arise in this simple way, at least not obviously.

Example 7.2
.

We again consider the polynomial
Q
of Example
6.7
. It satisfies the
necessary factorization condition. By Corollary
3.2
,
Q
completely
decomposes into univariate linear factors, for instance:

(19)

\displaystyle Q=(t-2-\mathbf{i})(s-\mathbf{i}-\mathbf{j})\left(t+\frac{4\mathbf{i}}{5}+\frac{3\mathbf{j}}{5}-1\right)\left(t+\frac{\mathbf{i}}{5}+\frac{7\mathbf{j}}{5}-1\right)

Another factorization of
Q
is given by

(20)

\displaystyle Q=(t+\mathbf{j}+\mathbf{k}-1)(t-\mathbf{k}-2)(s-\mathbf{i}-\mathbf{j})(t+\mathbf{j}-1).

Still,
s-\mathbf{i}-\mathbf{j}
does not commute with any
t
-factor in (
19
) or
(
20
). Obviously, the two factorizations are not equivalent.

As we have seen in Section
6
, non-uniqueness of
factorizations of
Q
is only possible if
\deg P<\#A+\#B
where
P
denotes the norm polynomial’s univariate
t
-factor and
\#A
and
\#B
are the multiplicity cardinalities. It turns out
that this necessary condition for existence of non-equivalent factorizations is
also sufficient.

Theorem 7.3

(Non-Uniqueness Theorem)
.

Let
Q=Q_{0}+sQ_{1}\in\mathbb{H}_{\ast 1}
,
\gcd(Q)=1
and
Q{Q}^{\ast}=PR
with
P\in\mathbb{R}[t]
and
R\in\mathbb{R}[s]
. Moreover, let
\#A
and
\#B
be the multiplicity cardinalities defined in (
13
). Then the following statements are equivalent:

(a)

|\mathrm{Fact}(Q)|>1
.

(b)

\deg P<\#A+\#B
.

Proof.

The assumptions guarantee existence of one factorization
(F_{h_{i},h,k_{j}})_{n,m}

of
Q
of shape (
4
). By Theorem
6.1
, (a)
implies
\deg P\neq\#A+\#B
. Statement (b) then follows from
\deg P=2(m+n)\leq\#A+\#B
.

Let us prove that (b) implies (a). From
\deg P=2(m+n)<\#A+\#B
we infer
\#A>2m
or
\#B>2n
. Let us assume
\#B>2n
. The elements of
B
are precisely the complex zeros of

{Q_{0}}^{\ast}Q_{1}
. Let us again use the representation
{Q_{0}}^{\ast}Q_{1}=HM
with

H=-{(t-k_{m})}^{\ast}\cdots{(t-k_{1})}^{\ast}{h}^{\ast}(t-k_{1})\cdots(t-k_{m})\quad\text{and}\quad M=\prod_{i=1}^{n}M_{i},

where
M_{i}\coloneqq(t-h_{i}){(t-h_{i})}^{\ast}
. Since
\deg M=2n
,
\#B>2n
implies
existence of at least one
z\in B
satisfying
H(z)=0
. But then we also obtain

H(\overline{z})=0
, where
\overline{z}
denotes the complex conjugate of
z
.
This is due to the fact that
H(z)=0
is equivalent to
H_{i}(z)=0
for

i=0,1,2,3
, where
H=H_{0}+\mathbf{i}H_{1}+\mathbf{j}H_{2}+\mathbf{k}H_{3}
(we already used this
representation in the paragraph following equation (
12
)). However,
H_{i}\in\mathbb{R}[t]
is a real polynomial, therefore
H_{i}(z)=0
also implies

H_{i}(\overline{z})=0
. As a consequence, we obtain
H(\overline{z})=0
. Hence

(21)

H=-{(t-k_{m})}^{\ast}\cdots{(t-k_{1})}^{\ast}{h}^{\ast}(t-k_{1})\cdots(t-k_{m})=N^{l}K,

where
l=\operatorname{mult}(H,z)
,
l\geq 1
,
N\coloneqq(t-z)(t-\overline{z})\in\mathbb{R}[t]
and
K\in\mathbb{H}[t]
with
N\nmid K
.

The class
[(F_{h_{i},h,k_{j}})_{n,m}]
of equivalent factorizations contains one element where
N
is the norm polynomial of the leftmost right factors (the
t
-factors
immediately following the
s
-factor). More precisely, there exists
r\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
such that
(t-k_{i}){(t-k_{i})}^{\ast}=N
for
i=1,\ldots,r
and
(t-k_{j}){(t-k_{j})}^{\ast}\neq N
for
j=r+1,\ldots,m
. It is no loss of generality to assume
that this is the given factorization
(F_{h_{i},h,k_{j}})_{n,m}
of
Q
.

Consider the product

(22)

(t-k_{1})\cdots(t-k_{r}).

This factorization into univariate linear factors is unique because all linear
factors have the same norm polynomial (c. f. Theorem
2.1
, Part (c)).
From (
21
) we obtain
N^{l}K=EF
where
E\coloneqq-{(t-k_{m})}^{\ast}\cdots{(t-k_{1})}^{\ast}{h}^{\ast}
and
F\coloneqq(t-k_{1})\cdots(t-k_{m})
. By shifting

{h}^{\ast}
to the left-hand side of the factorization, we get
E=-{h}^{\ast}(t-{(hk_{m}h^{-1})}^{\ast})\cdots(t-{(hk_{1}h^{-1})}^{\ast})
. Neither
E
nor
F

have a real polynomial factor of positive degree. Indeed, existence of such a
factor would imply
\gcd(Q)\neq 1
. However,
N^{l}
is a factor of
EF
. By

[
15
, Proposition 2.1]
, this is only possible if
l
linear right
factors of
E
are conjugate to
l
linear left factors of
F
and
N^{l}
is the
product of these factors. Due to the uniqueness of factorization
(
22
), we obtain

t-k_{i}=t-hk_{i}h^{-1}

for
i\in\{1,\ldots,l\}
. Therefore,

\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,l\}:hk_{i}=k_{i}h.

Hence
s-h
commutes with
t-k_{1}
, …,
t-k_{l}
. By letting some of these
t
-factors commute with the
s
-factor, one obtains another non-equivalent factorization.

If
\#A>2m
, we can argue similarly and see that
s-h
commutes with at least
one left factor.
∎

According to Theorem
7.3
,
|\mathrm{Fact}(Q)|>1
implies that each
equivalence class in
\mathrm{Fact}(Q)
can be represented by a factorization

(F_{h_{i},h,k_{j}})_{n,m}
where either
t-h_{n}
and
s-h
commute or
s-h
and
t-k_{1}

commute. If
t-h_{n}
and
s-h
commute, then
(F_{h_{i},h,\hat{k}_{j}})_{n-1,m+1}
with

\hat{k}_{1}=h_{n}
and
\hat{k}_{l}=k_{l-1}
for
l\in\{2,\ldots,m+1\}

is a new (non-equivalent) factorization. We call the transition from

[(F_{h_{i},h,k_{j}})_{n,m}]
to
[(F_{h_{i},h,\hat{k}_{j}})_{n-1,m+1}]
a
left jump
of
the
s
-factor. Similar statements hold true for commuting factors
s-h
and
t-k_{1}
. In this case we speak of a
right jump
of the
s
-factor.
Let us demonstrate the statements of Theorem
7.3
by means of an example.

Example 7.4
.

Consider the polynomial

(23)

Q=\left(t+\mathbf{k}\right)\left(t-\frac{2\mathbf{i}}{3}+\frac{2\mathbf{j}}{3}-\frac{\mathbf{k}}{3}\right)\left(t-\frac{\mathbf{i}}{3}+\frac{\mathbf{j}}{3}-\frac{5\mathbf{k}}{3}\right)\left(s-2\mathbf{k}\right)\left(t-\mathbf{i}-\mathbf{j}+\mathbf{k}\right).

Its norm polynomial is given by

Q{Q}^{\ast}=PR\quad\text{with}\quad P=(t^{2}+3)^{2}(t^{2}+1)^{2},\quad R=s^{2}+4.

From (
23
) we obtain
n=3
and
m=1
. Moreover,
\#A=6>2m
,

\#B=6=2n
and
\deg P<\#A+\#B
. Hence, by Theorem
7.3
,

|\mathrm{Fact}(Q)|>1
. Let us precisely investigate the elements of set
A
. It holds
that

A=\{\mathrm{i},-\mathrm{i},\sqrt{3}\mathrm{i},-\sqrt{3}\mathrm{i}\}.

The right factor
t-\mathbf{i}-\mathbf{j}+\mathbf{k}
corresponds to the complex numbers

\sqrt{3}\mathrm{i}
and
-\sqrt{3}\mathrm{i}
(we have

(t-\mathbf{i}-\mathbf{j}+\mathbf{k}){(t-\mathbf{i}-\mathbf{j}+\mathbf{k})}^{\ast}=t^{2}+3
). Moreover,
\lambda(\pm\sqrt{3}\mathrm{i})=1
.
Consequently,
s-2\mathbf{k}
does not perform a
left jump
by commuting with a
left factor of norm
t^{2}+3
.

Let us now consider the elements
\mathrm{i},-\mathrm{i}\in A
. It holds that
\lambda(\pm\mathrm{i})=2
. There does not exist a right factor of factorization (
23
) with norm polynomial
t^{2}+1
. Therefore, two left jumps of the
s
-factor are possible. Following the proof of Theorem
7.3
, we compute another (equivalent) factorization of
Q
where the two rightmost left factors have norm polynomial
t^{2}+1
. The respective factors commute with
s-2\mathbf{k}
:

Q=(t-\mathbf{i}+\mathbf{j}+\mathbf{k})(t-\mathbf{k})(t-\mathbf{k})(s-2\mathbf{k})(t-\mathbf{i}-\mathbf{j}+\mathbf{k})

Indeed,
(t-\mathbf{k})(s-2\mathbf{k})=(s-2\mathbf{k})(t-\mathbf{k})
. Further equivalence classes of factorizations of
Q
can be found in this way, that is

\displaystyle[(t-\mathbf{i}+\mathbf{j}+\mathbf{k})(t-\mathbf{k})(t-\mathbf{k})(s-2\mathbf{k})(t-\mathbf{i}-\mathbf{j}+\mathbf{k})],

\displaystyle[(t-\mathbf{i}+\mathbf{j}+\mathbf{k})(t-\mathbf{k})(s-2\mathbf{k})(t-\mathbf{k})(t-\mathbf{i}-\mathbf{j}+\mathbf{k})],

\displaystyle[(t-\mathbf{i}+\mathbf{j}+\mathbf{k})(s-2\mathbf{k})(t-\mathbf{k})(t-\mathbf{k})(t-\mathbf{i}-\mathbf{j}+\mathbf{k})].

By Theorem
7.6
below, these are all elements of
\mathrm{Fact}(Q)
.

In Corollary
6.3
we provided a geometric condition which
guarantees uniqueness of a factorization of
Q
: If
[Q_{0}(z)]\vee[Q_{1}(z)]
is a straight line
for all zeros
z\in\mathbb{C}
of the norm polynomial’s univariate factor
P\in\mathbb{R}[t]
,
we obtain
|\mathrm{Fact}(Q)|=1
. However, as shown in Example
6.4
, the converse
need not be true. Nevertheless, it turns out to be true if we require an
additional assumption to be satisfied:

Corollary 7.5
.

Let
Q\in\mathbb{H}_{\ast 1}
satisfy the assumptions of Theorem
7.3
. Define the polynomials
M
and
N
according to
(
16
)
and
(
17
)
and suppose that
\gcd(M,N)=1
. If there exists a zero
z\in\mathbb{C}
of the norm polynomial’s univariate
t
-factor
P\in\mathbb{R}[t]
such that
[Q_{0}(z)]\vee[Q_{1}(z)]
is just a point, we obtain
|\mathrm{Fact}(Q)|>1
.

Proof.

Let us first assume that
z
is a zero of
N
(note that
P=MN
). Since
[Q_{0}(z)]\vee[Q_{1}(z)]
is just a point, we obtain
{Q_{0}}^{\ast}(z)Q_{1}(z)=H(z)M(z)=0
, where
H
is defined according to (
18
). The fact
\gcd(M,N)=1
then implies
H(z)=0
. Following the proof of Theorem
7.3
, a
right jump
of the
s
-factor is possible. If
z
is a zero of
M
, one can perform a
left jump
of the
s
-factor. In both cases one obtains
|\mathrm{Fact}(Q)|>1
.
∎

Theorem 7.6
.

Suppose
Q\in\mathbb{H}_{\ast 1}
satisfies the assumptions of Theorem
7.3
. All elements of
\mathrm{Fact}(Q)
can be obtained by repeated application of left and
right jumps of the
s
-factor.

Proof.

Given two different classes
[(F_{h_{i},h,k_{j}})_{n,m}]
and

[(F_{\hat{h}_{i},\hat{h},\hat{k}_{j}})_{l,r}]
of factorizations we proceed as follows.
We define
M
and
\hat{M}
according to (
16
). For each complex zero

z\in\mathbb{C}
of the norm polynomial’s univariate factor
P\in\mathbb{R}[t]
we compute
the multiplicities
\operatorname{mult}(M,z)
and
\operatorname{mult}(\hat{M},z)
. If

\operatorname{mult}(M,z)<\operatorname{mult}(\hat{M},z)
, we follow the proof of Theorem
7.3

and successively perform
right jumps
of the
s
-factor of factorization

[(F_{h_{i},h,k_{j}})_{n,m}]
until the multiplicities coincide. Similarly, if

\operatorname{mult}(\hat{M},z)<\operatorname{mult}(M,z)
, we obtain equality of multiplicities by sequentially
applying
left jumps
of the
s
-factor of factorization

[(F_{h_{i},h,k_{j}})_{n,m}]
. We then obtain an equivalence class which is equal to

[(F_{\hat{h}_{i},\hat{h},\hat{k}_{j}})_{l,r}]
by Definition
4.1
.
∎

Example 7.7
.

Let us illustrate Theorem
7.6
at hand of the polynomial
Q
of
Examples
6.7
and
7.2
. We have already computed the two
non-equivalent factorizations

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle=(t-\mathbf{i}-2)(s-\mathbf{i}-\mathbf{j})\Bigl(t+\frac{4\mathbf{i}}{5}+\frac{3\mathbf{j}}{5}-1\Bigr)\Bigl(t+\frac{\mathbf{i}}{5}+\frac{7\mathbf{j}}{5}-1\Bigr)

\displaystyle=(t+\mathbf{j}+\mathbf{k}-1)(t-\mathbf{k}-2)(s-\mathbf{i}-\mathbf{j})(t+\mathbf{j}-1).

Moreover, we have

\displaystyle t^{2}-4t+5

\displaystyle=(t-\mathbf{i}-2){(t-\mathbf{i}-2)}^{\ast}

\displaystyle=(t-\mathbf{k}-2){(t-\mathbf{k}-2)}^{\ast},

\displaystyle t^{2}-2t+2

\displaystyle=\Bigl(t+\frac{4\mathbf{i}}{5}+\frac{3\mathbf{j}}{5}-1\Bigr){\Bigl(t+\frac{4\mathbf{i}}{5}+\frac{3\mathbf{j}}{5}-1\Bigr)}^{\ast}

\displaystyle=(t+\mathbf{j}-1){(t+\mathbf{j}-1)}^{\ast},

\displaystyle t^{2}-2t+3

\displaystyle=\Bigl(t+\frac{\mathbf{i}}{5}+\frac{7\mathbf{j}}{5}-1\Bigr){\Bigl(t+\frac{\mathbf{i}}{5}+\frac{7\mathbf{j}}{5}-1\Bigr)}^{\ast}

-factor, we should

The product of the two rightmost

\Bigl(t+\frac{4\mathbf{i}}{5}+\frac{3\mathbf{j}}{5}-1\Bigr)\Bigl(t+\frac{\mathbf{i}}{5}+\frac{7\mathbf{j}}{5}-1\Bigr)=(t+\mathbf{i}+\mathbf{j}-1)(t+\mathbf{j}-1).

This is already equivalent to the second factorization since

.

Remark 7.8
.

Suppose
Q\in\mathbb{H}_{\ast 1}
satisfies the assumptions of Theorem
7.3
. The number of possible jumps of the
s
-factor of a given factorization of
Q
can be counted with the help of the multiplicity cardinalities
\#A
and
\#B
. One can perform

\#A+\#B-\deg P

jumps of the

-factor. More precisely,
\#A-2m
left jumps and

right jumps are possible, where

denotes the number of right factors and

denotes the number of left factors of the given factorization of

. This immediately follows from the proof of Theorem
7.3
.

Corollary 7.9
.

Suppose
Q\in\mathbb{H}_{\ast 1}
satisfies the assumptions of Theorem
7.3
. All elements of
\mathrm{fact}(Q)
, that is, all possible factorizations of
Q
with monic univariate linear factors, can be found by performing the following three steps:

Step 1:

Compute a factorization of
Q
with monic univariate linear
factors (Theorem
3.1
, Corollary
3.2
).

Step 2:

Perform all possible left/right jumps of the
s
-factor to obtain

\#A+\#B-\deg P
representatives of different equivalence classes of

\mathrm{Fact}(Q)
. All representatives are of the form
A(s-h)B
with univariate
polynomials
A,B\in\mathbb{H}[t]
and
h\in\mathbb{H}

8.
Applications in Kinematics and Future Research

As mentioned in Section
1
, one motivation for our study are
applications in kinematics. This section explains the underlying ideas and
demonstrates, why the factorization theory of bivariate quaternionic polynomials
needs to be extended to polynomials of higher bi-degree in order to allow the
construction of interesting mechanisms. For background information in the
relation of quaternions to (spherical) kinematics we refer to
[
3
]
.

A vector
(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is identified with the
vectorial
quaternion
x=x_{1}\mathbf{i}+x_{2}\mathbf{j}+x_{3}\mathbf{k}
.
The quaternion
q\in\mathbb{H}\setminus\{0\}
acts on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
via

(24)

x\mapsto\frac{qx{q}^{\ast}}{q{q}^{\ast}}.

The map (
24
) is a rotation around the vector
q-{q}^{\ast}
(or the
identity if
q-{q}^{\ast}=0
). The action (
24
) can be extended to
quaternionic polynomials by replacing
q
with a quaternionic polynomial.
Univariate polynomials then give one-parametric rational spherical motions,
bivariate polynomials give two-parametric motions etc. For the sake of
simplicity, we henceforth do not distinguish between motions and polynomials.

In this sense, the linear polynomial
t-h\in\mathbb{H}[t]
is a rotation around the
fixed vector
h-{h}^{\ast}
. This important observation relates our factorizations
with linear univariate factors to mechanisms with revolute joints. A
factorization
(F_{h_{i},h,k_{j}})_{n,m}
of
Q\in\mathbb{H}_{\ast 1}
describes a mechanism
consisting of
n+1+m
revolute joints, connected in sequential order, that can
perform the motion
Q
. The mechanism’s movement when following the motion
Q

requires that the first
n
joints and the last
m
joints share the same motion
parameter. In order to achieve this mechanically, that is, without individually
controlling each joint parameter, it is necessary to further constrain the
mechanism. In the univariate case this can be done by considering further
factorizations, each yielding a new sub-mechanism or “leg” that can be added.
In this way, we may think of the univariate
t
-factors
T_{l}=(t-h_{1})\cdots(t-h_{n})
to the left and
T_{r}=(t-k_{1})\cdots(t-k_{m})
to the right
of the
s
-factor as “higher-order” joints with a single degree of freedom.
The “mechanism”
T_{l}(s-h)T_{r}
corresponds to all factorizations in one class
of equivalent factorizations and serves as one single leg.

The polynomial
Q
may admit non-equivalent factorizations but they cannot be
used as additional legs because they do not further constrain the mechanism: The
left and right jumps of Theorem
7.6
interchange commuting factors. These
have linearly dependent vector parts and hence correspond to identical revolute
joints. Therefore, the legs to non-equivalent factorizations are actually identical
from a mechanism science viewpoint.

This is illustrated at hand of a simple example in Figure
1
.
This figure refers to planar kinematics (all revolute axes are parallel) while
our results pertain to spherical kinematics (all revolute axes are concurrent).
The principle ideas and problems are the same but planar kinematics is easier
and clearer to visualize. We consider a polynomial
Q=T_{l}(s-h)(t-k)
where

\deg T_{l}=2
. The top row illustrates our original hopes: The first image
shows the mechanism to the equivalence class of the factorization

T_{l}(s-h)(t-k)
, the second image shows the mechanism to the equivalence class
obtained after a right jump of
s-h
. This mechanism has the desired two degrees
of freedom and could, in principle, be used as one leg. However, our results
imply that
s-h
and
t-k
commute so that the true situation is that of the
second row where the left and the right mechanisms are actually the same.
