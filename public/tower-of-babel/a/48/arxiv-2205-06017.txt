Doubled space and extended supersymmetry


Abstract
The doubled formulation of the worldsheet provides a description of string theory in which T-duality is promoted to a manifest symmetry. Here we extend this approach to 𝒩 = ( 2 , 2 ) \mathcal{N}=(2,2) superspace providing a doubled formulation for bi-Hermitian/generalised Kähler target spaces. The theory is described by a single function, a doubled-generalised Kähler potential, supplemented with a manifestly 𝒩 = ( 2 , 2 ) \mathcal{N}=(2,2) constraint. Several examples serve to illustrate this construction, including a discussion of the 𝒩 = ( 2 , 2 ) \mathcal{N}=(2,2) description of T-folds.

Introduction

The interplay between the physics of the string worldsheet and the geometry of target space is a rich and fascinating subject.
In this paper, we focus on the supersymmetric worldsheet non-linear
\sigma
-model.
While any target manifold,
{\cal M}
, allows for
{\cal N}=(1,1)
supersymmetry on the worldsheet, requiring greater amounts of supersymmetry enforces stringent conditions on the metric,
G
, and the Kalb-Ramond two-form
B
defined on
{\cal M}
.

Our interest is in
{\cal N}=(2,2)
supersymmetry, as originally investigated long ago
[
1
]
.
Requiring
{\cal N}=(2,2)
worldsheet supersymmetry gives particular conditions on the geometrical data in
G
and
B
.
When the two-form
B
is absent, the criteria is that the target space be Kähler.
With the inclusion of
B
, the metric is required to be Hermitian with respect to two (appropriately covariantly constant) complex structures and is accordingly said to be
bi-Hermitian
.

A seminal result of Gualtieri
[
2
]
is that bi-Hermitian geometry can be elegantly reinterpreted as a
Generalised Kähler Geometry
(GKG) structure on the generalised tangent bundle
E\simeq T{\cal M}\oplus T^{*}{\cal M}
. With
\dim{\cal M}=D
, the natural pairing between vectors and covectors endows
E
with an
\mathrm{O}(D,D)
invariant bilinear pairing, usually denoted by
\eta
. On
E
, in general and not just for a GKG, the geometric data,
G
and
B
, are combined together to form the components of a
generalised metric
denoted
{\cal H}
or with index raised
{\cal E}=\eta^{-1}{\cal H}
. This generalised metric itself is an
\mathrm{O}(D,D)
element, and hence obeys
{\cal E}^{2}=1
. In the specialisation to GKG, this data also is encoded in a pair
{\cal J}_{1}
,
{\cal J}_{2}
of commuting
generalised complex
structures on the generalised tangent bundle. The generalised metric is identified with the product of the two generalised complex structures, via
\mathcal{E}=-\mathcal{J}_{1}\mathcal{J}_{2}
.

The natural appearance of
\mathrm{O}(D,D)
means that the extended bundle
T{\cal M}\oplus T^{*}{\cal M}
is relevant more generally in string theory.
It is a natural space on which T-duality can act linearly.
When
{\cal M}
possesses
d
commuting isometries, such that
T^{d}\hookrightarrow{\cal M}\twoheadrightarrow{\cal B}
is a torus fibration over a base manifold
{\cal B}
, a subgroup
O(d,d;\mathbb{Z})
of this
O(D,D)
becomes the exact T-duality symmetry.
1
1

1

In supergravity these will both be
\mathrm{O}(d,d;\mathbb{R})
and
\mathrm{O}(D,D;\mathbb{R})
, but in the full string theory the true T-duality is
\mathrm{O}(d,d;\mathbb{Z})
. In this work, where the field is left unspecified in a group it should be assumed to be over
\mathbb{R}
.

T-duality symmetries are a powerful organisational tool shedding light on the full structure of string theory and its low energy effective descriptions. Generalised geometry, and the closely related double field theory, have been used to reframe supergravity in a way in which the
O(d,d)
symmetry becomes a manifest property
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
. This is closely related to ideologically similar pursuits in the context of the string
worldsheet
theory
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
]
.
In these worldsheet duality symmetric approaches, the idea is to consider a target space
\mathbb{M}
that is itself
doubled
, i.e. forms a bundle
T^{d}\times T^{d}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{M}\twoheadrightarrow{\cal B}
in which the torus fibres of which have been doubled (the base space remains the same).

This goes a step further than generalised geometry as it is the manifold itself (rather than its tangent bundle) that is enlarged. Heuristically, the democratic treatment of momentum and winding modes interchanged by T-duality is accommodated by the inclusion of
d
additional coordinated directions in this doubled torus target. An appealing feature of the doubled worldsheet theory is that it may allow for a description of strings in T-folds, a class of non-geometric backgrounds in which locally geometric patches are glued with T-duality transformations.

The purpose of this note is to understand how to combine the requirements of extended supersymmetry with the T-duality symmetric worldsheet. Given how well adapted concepts of generalised geometry are to both extended supersymmetry and T-duality, it is of some surprise that the combination of them is not straightforward.
There are, in fact, several technical reasons why this is challenging.

Firstly, in duality symmetric approaches one typically introduces an extra T-dual boson,
\tilde{x}
, for each boson,
x
, corresponding to a toroidal isometry direction. In extended supersymmetry however, the real field
x
is part of a multiplet whose bosonic content contains a second real scalar,
y
, together forming a complex superfield
z=y+ix
. The
y
coordinate need not (and generally will not) correspond to some adapted coordinate of an isometry. We will see that in order to implement the doubled torus fibration specified by fields
\mathbb{X}=(x,\tilde{x})
we are also compelled to double the base coordinates
\mathbb{Y}=(y,\tilde{y})
as well. Having implemented the doubling one should also have a procedure to constrain the theory so as to not introduce any additional physical degrees of freedom. On the
\mathbb{X}=(x,\tilde{x})
this amounts to imposing a worldsheet chirality constraint,
d\mathbb{X}=\star\mathcal{E}(d\mathbb{X})
, that encodes the canonical transformation between dual models. On the
\mathbb{Y}
we will require a
topological
constraint
d\mathbb{Y}=\mathcal{E}(d\mathbb{Y})
.
In fact, the entire theory can be understood as being defined by a single function–a doubled-generalised-Kähler potential
\mathbb{V}(\mathbb{Y})
–in terms of which the topological constraint on the
\mathbb{Y}
can be integrated to reveal an
algebraic
relation that simply undoes the over-doubling so introduced.

Secondly, implementing the chirality constraint on the
\mathbb{X}
at the Lagrangian level is challenging; doing so in the spirit of Floreanini-Jackiw and Tseytlin requires breaking manifest worldsheet Lorentz invariance which is evidently undesirable from the point of view of the supersymmetry algebra. The PST approach to chiral bosons does allow one to retain worldsheet Lorentz covariance, at the expense of introducing additional fields, but the extension of this to
{\cal N}=(2,2)
is very involved
[
10
,
11
]
. In this work we will somewhat side step this point and instead, as with Hull’s approach to the doubled world sheet
[
8
,
9
]
, view the chirality constraint as an additional ingredient to be imposed off shell by hand.

The third challenge is there exists a variety of
{\cal N}=(2,2)
multiplets (chiral, twisted chiral and semi-chiral) and T-duality can change the type of multiplets required to furnish a particular theory. To simplify the situation we will restrict our attention first to so-called BiLP geometries which are described without semi-chiral multiplets. Rather appealingly, within our approach once we have appropriately doubled the BiLP geometry we find that we can access T-duality frames in which the conventional description requires semi-chiral multiplets.

There exist previous studies in the literature making contact between the string worldsheet and generalised complex geometry.
The generalised complex structures of generalised Kähler geometry have been shown to appear naturally in the string worldsheet theory in first order or Hamiltonian approaches
[
12
,
13
,
14
]
. Our approach is rather fully covariant on the worldsheet (though see
[
15
]
for an outline of a ‘covariant Hamiltoninan’ alternative).

A doubled superspace formulation exists for certain backgrounds with extended
\mathcal{N}=(4,4)
supersymmetry.
This links to the T-folds mentioned earlier.
One of the most studied examples of a T-fold background is the exotic
5_{2}^{2}
brane obtained by T-dualising the NS5 brane on two traverse directions (see
[
16
]
for a detailed discussion).
This geometry admits extended
\mathcal{N}=(4,4)
worldsheet supersymmetry and can be studied via an
\mathcal{N}=(2,2)
description (with a generalised Kähler potential) such as in
[
17
]
.
It can alternatively be described via an
\mathcal{N}=(4,4)
gauged linear sigma model (GLSM).
In
[
18
]
a doubled
\mathcal{N}=(4,4)
GLSM was written down capable of describing the duality between the NS5 and Kaluza-Klein monopole, and providing a natural setting with which to discuss worldsheet instanton corrections to the naive Buscher T-duality rules.
Interestingly, here it was already noted that supersymmetry required the doubling of both the fibre and base directions.
This was later extended to include the
5_{2}^{2}
obtained via a further T-duality in
[
19
,
20
]
.
The generalised complex structures of this duality chain were investigated very recently
[
21
,
22
]
.

In section

The final section, section

In subsection

In subsection

Lastly, in subsection

2
The geometry of extended supersymmetry

2.1
Bi-Hermitian geometry and the
{\cal N}=(2,2)
worldsheet

Consider a non-linear sigma-model defined on a target
\{{\cal M},G,B\}
represented by the Lagrangian

{\cal L}=(G_{IJ}+B_{IJ})\partial_{+}x^{I}\partial_{-}x^{J}\,.

(2.1)

Regardless of the choice of metric
G
and two-form
B
this theory can be extended to one with
{\cal N}=(1,1)
supersymmetry by moving to superspace; replacing
x^{I}
with the superfield
X^{I}(x,\psi,F)
(
\psi
a fermion and
F
an auxiliary boson), and upgrading the derivatives
\partial_{\pm}
to super-covariant derivatives
D_{\pm}
and integrating:

{\cal L}=\int{\rm d}^{2}\theta\,(G_{IJ}+B_{IJ})D_{+}X^{I}D_{-}X^{J}\,.

(2.2)

One can then postulate the existence of an additional supersymmetry that mixes these superfields

\delta X^{I}=\epsilon_{+}(J_{+})^{I}{}_{J}D_{+}X^{J}+\epsilon_{-}(J_{-})^{I}{}_{J}D_{-}X^{J}\,.

(2.3)

The analysis of
[
1
]
shows for this transformation to be consistent that
J_{\pm}
are complex structures obeying, for all vectors
U,V\in\Gamma(T{\cal M})
,

J_{+}^{2}=J_{-}^{2}=-1\,,\qquad[U,V]+J_{\pm}[J_{\pm}U,V]+J_{\pm}[U,J_{\pm}V]-[J_{\pm}U,J_{\pm}V]=0\,.

(2.4)

To leave the Lagrangian invariant under the additional supersymmetry we further require that
the metric
G
is Hermitian with respect to both complex structures

G(J_{\pm}U,J_{\pm}V)=G(U,V)\,,

(2.5)

and that the complex structures define two-forms
\omega_{\pm}(U,V)=-G(U,J_{\pm}V)
which satisfy,

\mathrm{d}\omega_{\pm}(U,V,W)=\mp H(J_{\pm}U,J_{\pm}V,J_{\pm}W)\,,

(2.6)

for any three vectors
U,V,W
.
The last condition is equivalent to the requirement that the complex structures are covariantly constant with respect to the Levi-Civita connection
\pm
torsion
H=dB
. A target space possessing these properties is often called a bi-Hermitian geometry.

To manifest this second supersymmetry off-shell requires the introduction of
{\cal N}=(2,2)
superspace (see appendix for details). There are three pertinent superfields and which should be used depends crucially on the properties of
J_{\pm}
: chiral fields
z^{\alpha},\bar{z}^{\alpha}
parametrise
\mathrm{ker}(J_{+}-J_{-})
; twisted chiral fields
w^{\mu},\bar{w}^{\mu}
parametrise
\mathrm{ker}(J_{+}+J_{-})
; and semi-chiral fields
[
23
]

(l,r,\bar{l},\bar{r})
parametrise the remaining directions
\textrm{im}([J_{+},J_{-}]G^{-1})
. Should the target geometry be of the type that only chiral and twisted chiral superfields are required, i.e.
[J_{+},J_{-}]=0
, then it is said to be BiLP (bi-Hermitian local product). This BiLP class, which we will largely focus on in the present work, clearly encompasses Kähler geometry as a special case where
J_{+}=J_{-}
.

In
{\cal N}=(2,2)
superspace, a single real function known as the generalised Kähler potential,
V(z,w,\bar{z},\bar{w})
for a BiLP, specifies the Lagrangian

{\cal L}=\int{\rm d}^{2}\theta{\rm d}^{2}\hat{\theta}\,V(z,w,\bar{z},\bar{w})\,.

(2.7)

Passing back to
{\cal N}=(1,1)
superspace (discarding total derivatives) one obtains
2
2

2

We use the notation
V_{\alpha}\equiv\frac{\partial V}{\partial z^{\alpha}}
,
V_{\bar{\alpha}}\equiv\frac{\partial V}{\partial\bar{z}^{\alpha}}
, and so on.

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{L}&=2V_{\alpha\bar{\beta}}(D_{+}z^{\alpha}D_{-}\bar{z}^{\beta}+D_{+}\bar{z}^{\beta}D_{-}z^{\alpha})-2V_{\mu\bar{\nu}}(D_{+}w^{\mu}D_{-}\bar{w}^{\nu}+D_{+}\bar{w}^{\nu}D_{-}w^{\mu})\\
&\qquad-2V_{\alpha\bar{\mu}}(D_{+}z^{\mu}D_{-}\bar{w}^{\mu}-D_{+}\bar{w}^{\mu}D_{-}z^{\mu})-2V_{\bar{\alpha}\mu}(D_{+}\bar{z}^{\mu}D_{-}w^{\mu}-D_{+}w^{\mu}D_{-}\bar{z}^{\mu})\,,\end{split}

(2.8)

which shows that the metric and B-field are extracted as derivatives of this potential according to
3
3

3

The perhaps unseemly factors of 2 are to match with our later conventions involving real coordinates.

G_{\alpha\bar{\beta}}=2V_{\alpha\bar{\beta}}\,,\quad G_{\mu\bar{\nu}}=-2V_{\mu\bar{\nu}}\,,\quad B_{\alpha\bar{\mu}}=-2V_{\alpha\bar{\mu}}\,,\quad B_{\bar{\alpha}\mu}=-2V_{\bar{\alpha}\mu}\,.

(2.9)

If in addition semi-chiral multiplets are required similar, albeit significantly more involved, expressions are found for the geometric data in terms of derivatives of the generalised Kähler potential.

2.2
Generalized Kähler Geometry

We now briefly review how the bi-Hermitian geometry invoked by
{\cal N}=(2,2)
supersymmetry can be reformulated in terms of generalized Kähler geometry on the generalized tangent space
E\simeq T{\cal M}\oplus T^{\star}{\cal M}

[
2
]

[
24
]
. Given two sections of this bundle
\mathbb{U}=(U,\alpha)
and
\mathbb{V}=(V,\beta)
we can construct the
\mathrm{O}(D,D)
invariant paring

\eta(\mathbb{U},\mathbb{V})=\iota_{U}\beta+\iota_{V}\alpha

(2.10)

and the
H
twisted Courant bracket

[\mathbb{U},\mathbb{V}]_{H}:=[U,V]+\iota_{U}{\rm d}\beta-\iota_{V}{\rm d}\alpha+\tfrac{1}{2}{\rm d}\big(\iota_{U}\beta-\iota_{V}\alpha\big)+\iota_{V}\iota_{U}H\,.

(2.11)

Given a metric and two-form on
{\cal M}
we can also endow
E
with a generalised metric
{\cal H}
, a representative of the coset
\mathrm{O}(D,D)/O(D)\times O(D)
that provides an additional symmetric pairing. We shall often work in a basis of
E
for which the inner products are given by

\eta=\begin{pmatrix}0&{1}_{D}\\
{1}_{D}&0\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad\mathcal{H}=\begin{pmatrix}G-BG^{-1}B&-BG^{-1}\\
G^{-1}B&G^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\,.

(2.12)

In places it is more natural to view the generalised metric as an endomorphism of
E
defined by
\mathcal{E}=\eta^{-1}\cdot\mathcal{H}
. As
\mathcal{H}
is an
\mathrm{O}(D,D)
element it follows that
\mathcal{E}^{2}=\textrm{id}
and hence defines projectors

\displaystyle{\cal P}_{\pm}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\textrm{id}\pm{\cal E}\right)\,.

(2.13)

In general
{\cal E}
can be decomposed as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{1}_{D}&0\\
-B&{1}_{D}\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&G^{-1}\\
G&0\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{1}_{D}&0\\
B&{1}_{D}\end{array}\right)\,.

A generalised Kähler structure on
E
consists of a pair,
{\cal J}_{1}
and
{\cal J}_{2}
, of generalised complex structures such that
{\cal J}_{1}^{2}={\cal J}_{2}^{2}=-\textrm{id}
and
[{\cal J}_{1},{\cal J}_{2}]=0
which are Courant-integrable,

\displaystyle[\mathbb{U},\mathbb{V}]_{H}+{\cal J}_{1,2}[{\cal J}_{1,2}\mathbb{U},\mathbb{V}]_{H}+{\cal J}_{1,2}[\mathbb{U},{\cal J}_{1,2}\mathbb{V}]_{H}-[{\cal J}_{1,2}\mathbb{U},{\cal J}_{1,2}\mathbb{V}]_{H}=0\,\quad\forall\mathbb{U},\mathbb{V}\in\Gamma(E)\,.

(2.21)

Furthermore, a generalized metric can now be obtained from the product of two generalized complex structures

\displaystyle{\cal E}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{\cal J}_{1}{\cal J}_{2}\,.

(2.22)

A remarkable result is that the conditions of bi-Hermitian geometry of eqs. (
2.4
)-(
2.6
) can be equated to the definition of a generalised complex structure. This is achieved explicitly through the Gualtieri map

In which
{\cal J}_{1}
corresponds to the lower choice in
\pm
.

\displaystyle{\cal J}_{1,2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{1}_{D}&0\\
-B&{1}_{D}\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}J_{+}\pm J_{-}&\omega^{-1}_{+}\mp\omega^{-1}_{-}\\

2.3
Transformation of complex structures under T-duality

A virtue of the generalised Kähler perspective is that the action of
\mathrm{O}(D,D;\mathbb{R})
, i.e. the transformations
\mathscr{O}
that preserve
\eta=\mathscr{O}^{t}\eta\mathscr{O}
, have a linear action on the tensors:

\displaystyle\mathbb{U}\rightarrow\mathscr{O}^{-1}\mathbb{U}\,,\quad{\cal J}_{1,2}\rightarrow\,\mathscr{O}^{-1}{\cal J}_{1,2}\mathscr{O}\,,\quad{\cal E}\rightarrow\,\mathscr{O}^{-1}{\cal E}\mathscr{O}\,,\quad{\cal H}\rightarrow\mathscr{O}^{t}{\cal H}\,\mathscr{O}\,.

(2.30)

In this way the complicated T-duality transformations – which involve a subgroup of the full
\mathrm{O}(D,D;\mathbb{R})
acting only in isometry directions – of geometric quantities,
G,B,J_{\pm}
, can be made transparent. Here we illustrate this by rederiving the transformation rules under T-duality of the spacetime complex structure originally worked out by Hassan
[
25
]
.

Now let’s take the spacetime metric
G=(G_{IJ})
and choose for this a vielbein

e=(e^{A}{}_{I})
and flat metric
h=(h_{AB})
such that

G=e^{t}he
. In terms of this we can now construct a natural basis
\{{\cal V}_{+A},\,{\cal V}_{-A}\}
,
A\in\{1,\cdots,D\}
, for the generalized tangent bundle
E\simeq T{\cal M}\oplus T{\cal M}^{*}
,

\displaystyle{\cal V}_{\pm}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{1}&0\\
-B&{1}\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\pm e^{-1}\\
e^{t}h\end{array}\right)\,,

and its dual
\{\bar{\cal V}_{+}^{A},\,\bar{\cal V}_{-}^{A}\}
,

\displaystyle\bar{\cal V}_{\pm}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}e\,,\pm h^{-1}e^{-t}\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{1}&0\\
B&{1}\end{array}\right)\,.

This basis is orthonormal in the sense that:

\displaystyle\bar{\cal V}_{+}^{A}{\cal V}_{+B}=\delta^{A}_{B}\,,\quad\bar{\cal V}_{-}^{A}{\cal V}_{-B}=-\delta^{A}_{B}\,,\quad\bar{\cal V}_{+}^{A}{\cal V}_{-B}=0\,,\quad\bar{\cal V}_{-}^{A}{\cal V}_{+B}=0\,,

(2.40)

and enjoys the completeness relation:

\displaystyle\sum_{A}{\cal V}_{+A}\otimes\bar{\cal V}^{A}_{+}-{\cal V}_{-A}\otimes\bar{\cal V}^{A}_{-}={1}_{2D}\,.

(2.41)

Furthermore we can reconstruct the previously defined projectors via
\sum_{A}{\cal V}_{\pm A}\otimes\bar{\cal V}^{A}_{\pm}={\cal P}_{\pm}
.

As these vielbein inherit the natural action of
\mathrm{O}(D,D;\mathbb{R})
i.e.
\mathcal{V}_{\pm}\rightarrow\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{\pm}=\mathscr{O}^{-1}\mathcal{V}_{\pm}
,
\bar{\mathcal{V}}_{\pm}\rightarrow\tilde{\bar{\mathcal{V}}}_{\pm}=\bar{\mathcal{V}}_{\pm}\mathscr{O}
, we can use their transformation to deduce the T-duality rules on
e
to find a T-dual veilbein
\tilde{e}
. In fact, depending on if we consider
\mathcal{V}_{-}
or
\mathcal{V}_{+}
we find two different results for the T-dual veilbein
\tilde{e}_{\pm}
. This is neither a contradiction nor a surprise; as
\tilde{e}_{+}^{t}h\tilde{e}_{+}=\tilde{e}_{-}^{t}h\tilde{e}_{-}
we have that
\tilde{e}_{\pm}
are related by a local Lorentz transformation reflecting the fact that on the worldsheet left and right movers transform differently under T-duality. To make this precise we
we will need to use some facts about the structure of transformations
\mathscr{O}\in O(D,D;\mathbb{R})
.
Representing the matrix
\mathscr{O}
in a block diagonal form,

\displaystyle\mathscr{O}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{A}&\mathbb{C}\\
\mathbb{B}&\mathbb{D}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(2.42)

one verifies that
O(D,D)
invariance implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}^{t}\mathbb{A}+\mathbb{A}^{t}\mathbb{B}=0=\mathbb{D}^{T}\mathbb{C}+\mathbb{C}^{t}\mathbb{D}\,,\qquad\mathbb{B}^{t}\mathbb{C}+\mathbb{A}^{t}\mathbb{D}={1}_{D}\,,

(2.43)

or equivalently,

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}\mathbb{C}^{t}+\mathbb{C}\mathbb{A}^{t}=0=\mathbb{B}\mathbb{D}^{t}+\mathbb{D}\mathbb{B}^{t}\,,\qquad\mathbb{B}\mathbb{C}^{t}+\mathbb{D}\mathbb{A}^{t}={1}_{D}\,.

(2.44)

Then, starting from
\mathcal{V}_{+}\rightarrow\mathscr{O}^{-1}\mathcal{V}_{+}
one gets,

\displaystyle e\rightarrow\tilde{e}_{+}=e\left(\mathbb{C}^{t}E^{t}+\mathbb{D}^{t}\right)^{-1}\equiv e\,\mathbb{O}_{+}^{-1}\,,

(2.45)

and for
E=G+B
one finds,

\displaystyle E\rightarrow\tilde{E}=(E\mathbb{C}+\mathbb{D})^{-1}(E\mathbb{A}+\mathbb{B})\,.

(2.46)

On the other hand when starting from
\mathcal{V}_{-}
one gets,

\displaystyle e\rightarrow\tilde{e}_{-}=e\left(-\mathbb{C}^{t}E+\mathbb{D}^{t}\right)^{-1}\equiv e\,\mathbb{O}_{-}^{-1}\,,

(2.47)

and for
E
one finds,

\displaystyle E\rightarrow\tilde{E}=(\mathbb{A}^{t}E-\mathbb{B}^{t})(-\mathbb{C}^{t}E+\mathbb{D}^{t})^{-1}\,.

(2.48)

Using the identities eqs. (
2.43
) and (
2.44
) one easily shows that the transformation rules in eqs. (
2.46
) and (
2.48
) are the same.
One additionally has that
\Lambda=e\mathbb{O}_{+}^{-1}\mathbb{O}_{-}e^{-1}
is the Lorentz transformation relating
\tilde{e}_{+}
and
\tilde{e}_{-}
. The utility of this is revealed by noting that we can construct
\mathrm{O}(D,D)
invariant quantities by contracting generalised tensors with the above generalised vielbeins and in particular we have

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{V}}^{A}_{\pm}{\cal J}_{1}\mathcal{V}_{\pm B}=\pm(eJ_{\pm}e^{-1})^{A}{}_{B}\,,\quad\bar{\mathcal{V}}^{A}_{\pm}{\cal J}_{2}\mathcal{V}_{\pm B}=(eJ_{\pm}e^{-1})^{A}{}_{B}\,,\quad\bar{\mathcal{V}}^{A}_{\pm}{\cal J}_{i}\mathcal{V}_{\mp B}=0\,.

(2.49)

As these are inert under
\mathrm{O}(D,D)
transformations,
using equations (
2.45
) and (
2.47
), we immediately read off the transformation rules for the complex structures
J_{\pm}
:

\displaystyle J_{\pm}\rightarrow\tilde{J}_{\pm}=\mathbb{O}_{\pm}J_{\pm}\mathbb{O}_{\pm}^{-1}\,.

(2.50)

Example: bivector transformation of a Kähler geometry

We present a simple example where a T-duality transformation renders commuting complex structures non-commuting.

D_{\pm}\tilde{x}_{\mu}&=\mp V_{\mu\nu}(y)D_{\pm}x^{\nu}-V_{\mu\beta}(y)D_{\pm}x^{\beta}\,,\end{split}

which can be rewritten using

\displaystyle D_{\pm}\tilde{x}_{i}=\pm(g_{ij}\mp b_{ij})D_{\pm}x^{j}\,,

(3.34)

i.e. exactly recovering the anticipated chirality constraints (
3.8
) of the doubled
\mathcal{N}=(1,1)
string in the form (
3.23

We now return to the Lagrangian (
3.30

Although it would be inconsistent to use the constraints (
3.33

Once we eliminate the coordinates

\mathcal{N}=(1,1)

3.6

Furthermore, we can also directly obtain both the

3.33

For instance, the equation of motion of

follows from

\displaystyle\begin{split}0&=D_{-}D_{+}\tilde{x}_{\alpha}+D_{+}D_{-}\tilde{x}_{\alpha}\\
&=D_{-}(V_{\alpha\beta}(y)D_{+}x^{\beta}+V_{\alpha\nu}(y)D_{+}x^{\nu})+D_{+}(-V_{\alpha\beta}(y)D_{-}x^{\beta}+V_{\alpha\nu}(y)D_{-}x^{\nu})\,,\end{split}

(3.35)

while acting again with
\hat{D}_{\pm}

\displaystyle\begin{split}\hat{D}_{-}D_{+}\tilde{x}_{\alpha}=D_{+}D_{-}\tilde{y}_{\alpha}&=\hat{D}_{-}(V_{\alpha\beta}(y)D_{+}x^{\beta}+V_{\alpha\nu}(y)D_{+}x^{\nu})\\
&=-V_{\alpha\beta\gamma}D_{-}x^{\gamma}D_{+}x^{\beta}+V_{\alpha\beta\mu}D_{-}x^{\mu}D_{+}x^{\beta}-V_{\alpha\beta}D_{+}D_{-}y^{\beta}\\
&\quad-V_{\alpha\nu\gamma}D_{-}x^{\gamma}D_{+}x^{\nu}+V_{\alpha\mu\nu}D_{-}x^{\mu}D_{+}x^{\nu}+V_{\alpha\mu}D_{+}D_{-}y^{\mu}\,,\end{split}

(3.36)

and after substituting in for
\tilde{y}_{\alpha}
using (
3.18
) this can be checked to reproduce the

A final comment concerns the topological term of the

Whilst this term breaks

Here we point out that such a term arises in
\mathcal{N}=(2,2)
double model from the following quantity:

\displaystyle\varpi(y,\tilde{y})=\tfrac{1}{2}(y^{\alpha}\tilde{y}_{\alpha}-y^{\mu}\tilde{y}_{\mu})\,.

(3.37)

One has that
\hat{D}_{+}\hat{D}_{-}\varpi
gives (without dropping total derivatives)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\varpi}=\tfrac{1}{2}\Omega_{MN}D_{+}\mathbb{X}^{M}D_{-}\mathbb{X}^{N}+\tfrac{1}{2}\Omega_{MN}\mathbb{Y}^{M}D_{-}D_{+}\mathbb{Y}^{N}\,,

(3.38)

where the constant antisymmetric matrix
\Omega_{MN}
has non-zero components
\Omega^{\alpha}{}_{\beta}=+\delta^{\alpha}_{\beta}=-\Omega_{\beta}{}^{\alpha}
,
\Omega^{\mu}{}_{\nu}=+\delta^{\mu}{}_{\nu}=-\Omega_{\nu}{}^{\mu}

Each term in (
3.38

The doubled potential
\mathbb{V}
is such that

In the

For instance, flipping the sign allows us to pick out the totally T-dual potential

\displaystyle\widetilde{V}\equiv\mathbb{V}-\varpi=V-y^{\alpha}\tilde{y}_{\alpha}+y^{\mu}\tilde{y}_{\mu}\,.

(3.39)

An alternative choice such as

3.4.1
The constraint as a Lagrangian

Critical to the
\mathcal{N}=(2,2)
doubled model was the constraint of eq. (
3.18
) that encodes not only the duality relations between coordinates
x
and
\tilde{x}
interchanged by T-duality on the fibre but also an un-doubling of the formally doubled base coordinates
y
and
\tilde{y}
. To get a better understanding of this, and how T-duality acts on the doubled model, we first formulate the constraint (
3.18
) in a more democratic form.
From the definitions of
V^{(c)}
and
V^{(t)}
in (
3.13
), it follows that

\displaystyle d\mathbb{V}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\tilde{y}_{\alpha}dy^{\alpha}-y^{\alpha}d\tilde{y}_{\alpha}-\tilde{y}_{\mu}dy^{\mu}+y^{\mu}d\tilde{y}_{\mu})\,.

(3.40)

In terms of
\mathbb{Y}^{M}=(y^{\alpha}\ y^{\mu}\ \tilde{y}_{\alpha}\ \tilde{y}_{\mu})
we can recast this expression as

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{M}\equiv\frac{\partial\mathbb{V}}{\partial\mathbb{Y}^{M}}=\Theta_{MN}\mathbb{Y}^{N}\,,

(3.41)

with

\displaystyle\Theta_{MN}=\tfrac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}0&\sigma\\
-\sigma&0\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad\sigma\equiv\begin{pmatrix}{1}_{n_{c}}&0\\
0&-{1}_{n_{t}}\end{pmatrix}\,.

(3.42)

The object
\Theta_{MN}
is suggestive of a symplectic structure.
Indeed, we could regard (
3.40
) as being the statement that
d\mathbb{V}=\vartheta
with

\vartheta\equiv\Theta_{MN}\mathbb{Y}^{N}d\mathbb{Y}^{M}

a potential for a symplectic form
\omega=-d\vartheta
given by

\displaystyle\omega=\Theta_{MN}d\mathbb{Y}^{M}\wedge d\mathbb{Y}^{N}=d\tilde{y}_{\alpha}\wedge dy^{\alpha}-d\tilde{y}_{\mu}\wedge dy^{\mu}\,.

(3.43)

The constraint (
3.40
) implies that we have
\omega=0
on the constraint surface.
Hence, the doubled model is defined on a Lagrangian submanifold of the ‘doubled’ base space with coordinates
\mathbb{Y}^{M}
and symplectic form
\omega
.

3.4.2
Doubled and over-doubled complex structures

Although we have been working in coordinates manifestly adapted to both isometries and complex structures, it is helpful to note that the over-doubled space itself inherits complex structures:

\displaystyle\hat{D}_{\pm}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{X}\\
\mathbb{Y}\end{pmatrix}=\mathbb{J}_{\pm}D_{\pm}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{X}\\
\mathbb{Y}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(3.44)

with
\mathbb{J}_{\pm}
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{J}_{+}=\begin{pmatrix}0&j_{+}\\
-j_{+}&0\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad\mathbb{J}_{-}=\begin{pmatrix}0&j_{-}\\
-j_{-}&0\end{pmatrix}

(3.45)

where for
n_{c}
chiral,
n_{t}
twisted chiral,
n_{c}
dual twisted chiral and
n_{t}
dual chiral fields, with
n_{c}+n_{t}=d
, we have that
j_{+}
and
j_{-}
are
2d
matrices given by:

\displaystyle j_{+}={1}_{2d}\,,\quad j_{-}=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma&0\\
0&-\sigma\end{pmatrix}\,,

(3.46)

with
\sigma
as in (
3.42
).
A simple calculation then shows that the
\mathcal{N}=(1,1)
Lagrangian that follows on carrying out the integration over the extra
\mathcal{N}=(2,2)
fermionic coordinates has the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\mathbb{V}_{KL}j_{+}^{K}{}_{M}j_{-}^{K}{}_{N}D_{+}\mathbb{X}^{M}D_{-}\mathbb{X}^{N}+\mathbb{V}_{MK}j_{-}^{K}{}_{L}j_{+}^{L}{}_{N}D_{+}\mathbb{Y}^{M}D_{-}\mathbb{Y}^{N}\,,

(3.47)

where the derivatives of
\mathbb{V}(\mathbb{Y})
are always with respect to
\mathbb{Y}^{M}
.
Comparing with (
3.30
) this implies that the
\mathrm{O}(d,d)
generalised metric which appears in both terms is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{MN}=2\mathbb{V}_{KL}j_{+}^{K}{}_{M}j_{-}^{L}{}_{N}=2\mathbb{V}_{MK}j_{-}^{K}{}_{L}j_{+}^{L}{}_{N}\,.

(3.48)

Given that
j_{+}^{M}{}_{N}=\delta^{M}_{N}
, these two expressions are indeed equal.

We can further make an identification

\displaystyle\Theta_{MN}=\frac{1}{2}j_{-}^{K}{}_{M}j_{+}^{L}{}_{K}\eta_{LN}\,,

(3.49)

using
\eta
the
\mathrm{O}(d,d)
structure.
We then note that taking an exterior derivative of (
3.41
) implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{MN}d\mathbb{Y}^{N}=\Theta_{MN}d\mathbb{Y}^{N}\Rightarrow d\mathbb{Y}^{M}=\eta^{MN}\mathcal{H}_{NK}d\mathbb{Y}^{K}

(3.50)

which reproduces the constraint (
3.32
) (and to reiterate for emphasis, the absence of a Hodge star in this relation is in contradistinction to that of the T-duality relation obeyed by the
\mathbb{X}
).

3.4.3
Transformation rules

Let us now look at the T-duality transformation properties of our model.
From the
\mathcal{N}=(1,1)
case, we know that
{\cal O}\in\mathrm{O}(d,d)
acts on
\mathbb{X}
as,

\displaystyle\mathbb{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{X}^{\prime}={\cal O}^{-1}\,\mathbb{X}\,.

(3.51)

We require that these transformations respect the
\mathcal{N}=(2,2)
supersymmetry transformations, which implies we should also have an action of
{\cal O}
on
\mathbb{Y}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{Y}\rightarrow\mathbb{Y}^{\prime}={\cal O}^{-1}\,\mathbb{Y}\,.

(3.52)

This is accompanied by a transformation of the complex structures
\mathbb{J}_{\pm}
of (
3.45
):

\displaystyle\mathbb{J}_{\pm}\rightarrow\mathbb{J}_{\pm}^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{O}^{-1}&0\\
0&\mathcal{O}^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\mathbb{J}_{\pm}\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{O}&0\\
0&\mathcal{O}\end{pmatrix}\,.

(3.53)

In order to reproduce the behavior of the doubled action in
\mathcal{N}=(1,1)
superspace under
\mathrm{O}(d,d)
transformation we then have to require that the doubled potential transforms as a scalar,

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}(\mathbb{Y})\rightarrow\mathbb{V}^{\prime}(\mathbb{Y}^{\prime})=\mathbb{V}(\mathbb{Y})\,.

(3.54)

We must also require that the constraint transformations covariantly, in particular we need

\displaystyle\Theta^{\prime}={\cal O}^{T}\Theta{\cal O}\,.

(3.55)

The T-duality invariance of the doubled model means that although we initially defined the doubled potential starting from a BiLP geometry, it can describe more complicated cases.
For example, as we saw at the end of section
2.3
, T-duality transformations can take us from a BiLP geometry with commuting complex structures to a geometry with non-commuting complex structures. The latter would normally have to be described with semi-chiral superfields.
However, we can alternatively describe the geometry using appropriate combinations of our original doubled chiral and twisted chiral superfields, and thereby avoid introducing semi-chirals.
We will demonstrate this explicitly in an example in section
4.4
.

3.4.4
Transformation of complex structures in detail

In order to verify that the above transformation rules are sensible, we should consider more carefully the implications of the transformation (
3.53
) of the complex structures of (
3.45
).
These are initially given in adapted coordinates, and their geometric nature is unclear.
Despite this, we can show that their transformation (
3.53
) under
\mathrm{O}(d,d)
, together with the transformation of the constraint, allow us to recover the correct transformation of the usual complex structures
J_{\pm}
as derived in section
2.3
.

Firstly, we note that as we start with
\mathbb{J}_{+}
of the form (
3.45
) with
j_{+}={1}_{2d}
, the
\mathrm{O}(d,d)
transformation (
3.53
) always preserves
\mathbb{J}_{+}^{\prime}=\mathbb{J}_{+}
.
However, generically
\mathbb{J}_{-}
will transform non-trivially and lead to a very non-standard complex structure which in particular mixes physical and dual superfields.
The correct prescription to recover sensible complex structures acting solely on the physical superfields is to apply the constraint to eliminate the dual ones. Then, after applying
\mathrm{O}(d,d)
transformation and the constraint, one obtains new complex structures
J_{\pm}^{\prime}
, with the special feature that
J_{+}^{\prime}
is always in ‘diagonal’ (w.r.t. a complex basis) form.
This corresponds to doing a standard
\mathrm{O}(d,d)
transformation as in section
2.3
followed by a coordinate transformation of the non-isometric directions in order to bring
J_{+}^{\prime}
into the desired form.

We now prove this.
Let’s start with the complex structures corresponding to our
2d
-dimensional BiLP geometry with
n_{c}
chiral superfields and
n_{t}
twisted chiral superfields.
In adapted coordinates for the
d
isometry directions we can write (as in appendix
A.3
)

\displaystyle J_{+}=\begin{pmatrix}0&{1}_{d}\\
-{1}_{d}&0\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad J_{-}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\sigma\\
-\sigma&0\end{pmatrix}\,.

(3.56)

The metric and
B
-field are meanwhile given by

\displaystyle G+B=\begin{pmatrix}g+b&0\\
0&g+b\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad g=\begin{pmatrix}g_{cc}&0\\
0&g_{tt}\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad b=\begin{pmatrix}0&b_{ct}\\
-b_{ct}&0\end{pmatrix}\,,

(3.57)

where we schematically exhibit the non-zero metric and
B
-field components in chiral and twisted chiral directions.

The T-duality transformation rule (
2.50
) implies that under an
\mathcal{O}\in\mathrm{O}(d,d)\subset\mathrm{O}(D,D)
transformation with

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{A}&\mathcal{C}\\
\mathcal{B}&\mathcal{D}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(3.58)

we obtain new complex structures

\displaystyle J_{+}^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}0&O_{+}\\
-O_{+}^{-1}&0\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad J_{-}^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}0&O_{-}\sigma\\
-\sigma O_{-}^{-1}&0\end{pmatrix}\,,

(3.59)

where

\displaystyle O_{+}=\mathcal{C}^{t}(g-b)+\mathcal{D}^{t}\,,\quad O_{-}=-\mathcal{C}^{t}(g+b)+\mathcal{D}^{t}\,.

(3.60)

We ‘diagonalise’
J_{+}^{\prime}
by using a coordinate transformation
y\rightarrow y^{\prime}(y)
such that
\partial y^{\prime}/\partial y=O_{+}
.
This leads to:

\displaystyle J_{+}^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}0&{1}_{d}\\
-{1}_{d}&0\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad J_{-}^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}0&O_{-}\sigma O_{+}^{-1}\\
-O_{+}\sigma O_{-}^{-1}&0\end{pmatrix}\,.

(3.61)

We then have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\hat{D}_{-}x^{\prime}&=O_{-}\sigma O_{+}^{-1}D_{-}y^{\prime}=(-\mathcal{C}^{t}(g+b)+\mathcal{D}^{t})\sigma(\mathcal{C}^{t}(g-b)+\mathcal{D}^{t})^{-1}D_{-}y^{\prime}\,,\\
\hat{D}_{-}y^{\prime}&=-O_{+}\sigma O_{-}^{-1}D_{-}x^{\prime}=-(\mathcal{C}^{t}(g-b)+\mathcal{D}^{t})\sigma(-\mathcal{C}^{t}(g+b)+\mathcal{D}^{t})^{-1}D_{-}x^{\prime}\,.\end{split}

(3.62)

We wish to reproduce these relationships from our
\mathcal{N}=(2,2)
doubled model.
According to (
3.53
), we obtain new complex structures
\mathbb{J}^{\prime}_{\pm}
with
\mathbb{J}^{\prime}_{+}=\mathbb{J}_{+}
and

\displaystyle\mathbb{J}^{\prime}_{-}=\begin{pmatrix}0&j^{\prime}_{-}\\
-j^{\prime}_{-}&0\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad j^{\prime}_{-}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{D}^{t}\sigma\mathcal{A}-\mathcal{C}^{t}\sigma\mathcal{B}&\mathcal{D}^{t}\sigma\mathcal{C}-\mathcal{C}^{t}\sigma\mathcal{D}\\
\mathcal{B}^{t}\sigma\mathcal{A}-\mathcal{A}^{t}\sigma\mathcal{B}&\mathcal{B}^{t}\sigma\mathcal{C}-\mathcal{A}^{t}\sigma\mathcal{D}\end{pmatrix}\,.

(3.63)

Hence we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\hat{D}_{-}x^{\prime}&=(\mathcal{D}^{t}\sigma\mathcal{A}-\mathcal{C}^{t}\sigma\mathcal{B})D_{-}y^{\prime}+(\mathcal{D}^{t}\sigma\mathcal{C}-\mathcal{C}^{t}\sigma\mathcal{D})D_{-}\tilde{y}^{\prime}\\
\hat{D}_{-}y^{\prime}&=-(\mathcal{D}^{t}\sigma\mathcal{A}-\mathcal{C}^{t}\sigma\mathcal{B})D_{-}x^{\prime}-(\mathcal{D}^{t}\sigma\mathcal{C}-\mathcal{C}^{t}\sigma\mathcal{D})D_{-}\tilde{x}^{\prime}\,,\end{split}

(3.64)

We eliminate the dual coordinates from (
3.64
) using the transformed constraints:

\displaystyle\begin{split}D_{-}\tilde{y}^{\prime}&=(g^{\prime}-b^{\prime})D_{-}y^{\prime}=(\mathcal{A}^{t}(g-b)+\mathcal{B}^{t})O_{+}^{-1}D_{-}y^{\prime}\,,\\
D_{-}\tilde{x}^{\prime}&=-(g^{\prime}+b^{\prime})D_{-}y^{\prime}=-(\mathcal{A}^{t}(g+b)-\mathcal{B}^{t})O_{-}^{-1}D_{-}y^{\prime}\,,\end{split}

(3.65)

using (
2.46
) and (
2.48
) to express the transformation of the geometry.
Starting with
\hat{D}_{-}x^{\prime}
, we can thus write:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\hat{D}_{-}x^{\prime}&=\left((\mathcal{D}^{t}\sigma\mathcal{A}-\mathcal{C}^{t}\sigma\mathcal{B})O_{+}+(\mathcal{D}^{t}\sigma\mathcal{C}-\mathcal{C}^{t}\sigma\mathcal{D})(\mathcal{A}^{t}(g-b)+\mathcal{B}^{t})\right)O_{+}^{-1}D_{-}y^{\prime}\\
&=\Big(\left(\mathcal{D}^{t}\sigma(\mathcal{A}\mathcal{C}^{t}+\mathcal{C}\mathcal{A}^{t})-\mathcal{C}^{t}\sigma(\mathcal{B}\mathcal{C}^{t}+\mathcal{D}\mathcal{A}^{t})\right)(g-b)\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad+\mathcal{D}^{t}\sigma(\mathcal{A}\mathcal{D}^{t}+\mathcal{C}\mathcal{B}^{t})-\mathcal{C}^{t}\sigma(\mathcal{B}\mathcal{D}^{t}+\mathcal{D}\mathcal{B}^{t})\Big)O_{+}^{-1}D_{-}y^{\prime}\\
&=(-\mathcal{C}^{t}\sigma(g-b)\sigma+\mathcal{D}^{t})\sigma O_{+}^{-1}D_{-}y^{\prime}\,,\end{split}

(3.66)

where we used the conditions resulting from the fact that the blocks
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D}
give an
\mathrm{O}(d,d)
transformation.
Finally, as
\sigma(g-b)\sigma=g+b
we recover exactly the correct relationship (
3.62
).
The calculation of
\hat{D}_{-}y^{\prime}
proceeds similarly.

4
Examples

In this section we illustrate the construction of the doubled potential in a number of examples.
In each case the recipe is as follows:

•

We start with the generalised Kähler potential
V(z+\bar{z},w+\bar{w})
describing a BiLP geometry with isometries.

•

We construct the totally chiral and twisted chiral potentials,
V^{(c)}(z+\bar{z},\tilde{z}+\bar{\tilde{z}})
and
V^{(t)}(\tilde{w}+\bar{\tilde{w}},w+\bar{w})
, defined by Legendre transformation in (
3.13
).

•

We hence write down the doubled potential
\mathbb{V}
given by (
3.15
) and the constraints given by (
3.17
) or (
3.18
).

4.1
Torus with constant B-field

A first simple example is a torus with constant
B
-field.
This can be realised via the following generalised Kähler potential:

V(z,w)=\tfrac{1}{2}(z+\bar{z})^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}(w+\bar{w})^{2}+\lambda(z+\bar{z})(w+\bar{w})\,,

(4.1)

Defining
y_{1}=z+\bar{z}
be the real part of the chiral superfield and
y_{2}=w+\bar{w}
that of the twisted chiral superfield we can rewrite the potential as

V(y_{1},y_{2})=\tfrac{1}{2}y_{1}^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}y_{2}^{2}+\lambda y_{1}y_{2}\,.

(4.2)

The corresponding geometry is simply:

\displaystyle ds^{2}=dx_{1}^{2}+dy_{1}^{2}+dx_{2}^{2}+dy_{2}^{2}\,,\quad B=-\lambda dy_{1}\wedge dy_{2}-\lambda dx_{1}\wedge dx_{2}\,.

(4.3)

We now give the doubled
\mathcal{N}=(2,2)
description.
The dual potentials (
3.13
) used in the construction of the doubled potential are:

\displaystyle V^{(c)}(y_{1},\tilde{y}_{2})

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}(1+\lambda^{2})y_{1}^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\tilde{y}_{2}^{2}+\lambda y_{1}\tilde{y}_{2}\,,

(4.4)

\displaystyle V^{(t)}(\tilde{y}_{1},y_{2})

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{1}{2}\tilde{y}_{1}^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}(1+\lambda^{2})y_{2}^{2}+\lambda\tilde{y}_{1}y_{2}\,,

(4.5)

The doubled potential (
3.15
) is thus:

\displaystyle 2\mathbb{V}=\tfrac{1}{2}(1+\lambda^{2})y_{1}^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\tilde{y}_{2}^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}\tilde{y}_{1}^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}(1+\lambda^{2})y_{2}^{2}+\lambda(y_{1}\tilde{y}_{2}+\tilde{y}_{1}y_{2})\,.

(4.6)

and the constraints (
3.18
) are:

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{c}y_{1}\\
\tilde{y}_{1}\\
y_{2}\\
\tilde{y}_{2}\end{array}\right)=\begin{pmatrix}-\tilde{y}_{1}+\lambda y_{2}\\
(1+\lambda^{2})y_{1}+\lambda\tilde{y}_{2}\\
\tilde{y}_{2}+\lambda y_{1}\\
-(1+\lambda^{2})y_{2}+\lambda\tilde{y}_{1}\end{pmatrix}\,.

4.2
\mathrm{SU}(2)\times\mathrm{U}(1)

Another example is provided by the WZW model on
\mathrm{SU}(2)\times\mathrm{U}(1)

[
28

In its BiLP description, the generalized Kähler potential
[
29

\displaystyle V=-\frac{1}{2}\,(w+\bar{w})^{2}+\int^{z+\bar{z}-w-\bar{w}}\,dr\,\ln(1+e^{r})\,,

(4.12)

which in our conventions describes the geometry

\displaystyle\begin{split}ds^{2}&=\frac{1}{1+e^{y_{1}-y_{2}}}\left(e^{y_{1}-y_{2}}(dy_{1}^{2}+dx_{1}^{2})+dy_{2}^{2}+dx_{2}^{2}\right)\,,\\

(4.13)

The Legendre dual potentials are:

\displaystyle V^{(c)}(z+\bar{z},\tilde{z}+\bar{\tilde{z}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\,(z+\bar{z})^{2}+\int^{z+\bar{z}-p}dr\,\ln(1+e^{-r})+p(\tilde{z}+\bar{\tilde{z}}-1(z+\bar{z}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\,(w+\bar{w})^{2}+\int^{q-w-\bar{w}}\,dr\,\ln(1+e^{r})-q(\tilde{w}{}+\bar{\tilde{w}})

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\,(\tilde{w}+\bar{\tilde{w}}+(w+\bar{w}))^{2}-\int^{\tilde{w}+\bar{\tilde{w}}}dr\,\ln(1-e^{-r})\,.

Hence we get for the doubled-generalised Kähler potential,

\begin{split}\mathbb{V}(z+\bar{z},w+\bar{w},\tilde{w}+\bar{\tilde{w}},\tilde{z}+\bar{\tilde{z}})&=\frac{1}{2}\,\Big(\frac{1}{2}\,(\tilde{z}+\bar{\tilde{z}})^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\,(\tilde{w}+\bar{\tilde{w}}+w+\bar{w})^{2}\\
&\quad\qquad+\int^{\tilde{z}+\bar{\tilde{z}}-z-\bar{z}}_{\tilde{w}+\bar{\tilde{w}}}dr\,\ln(1-e^{-r})\Big)\,.\end{split}

(4.15)

The constraints are

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{c}z+\bar{z}\\
w+\bar{w}\\
\tilde{w}+\bar{\tilde{w}}\\
\tilde{z}+\bar{\tilde{z}}\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}w+\bar{w}+\ln(e^{\tilde{w}+\bar{\tilde{w}}}-1)\\
\ln(e^{\tilde{z}+\bar{\tilde{z}}}-e^{z+\bar{z}})\\
-\ln(1-e^{z+\bar{z}-\tilde{z}-\bar{\tilde{z}}})\\
w+\bar{w}+\tilde{w}+\bar{\tilde{w}}\end{array}\right)\,.

4.3
T-fold examples

We now consider some more novel examples of
\mathcal{N}=(2,2)

non-geometries
.
The general idea is the following.
Recall that a generalised Kähler potential
V(z,w)
is defined only up to generalised Kähler transformations:

\displaystyle V(z,w)\rightarrow V(z,w)+F(z,w)+\bar{F}(\bar{z},\bar{w})+G(\bar{z},w)+\bar{G}(z,\bar{w})\,,

(4.25)

for arbitrary functions
F
and
G
.
The examples we consider below will have this feature when we carry out some transformation of
z
and
w
under which the geometry is globally identified.
We will refer to this as a monodromy of the generalised Kähler potential.
After T-dualising, this will induce a non-trivial monodromy of the dual generalised Kähler potential, which in terms of the dual superfields
\tilde{z},\tilde{w}
, will not be intepretable as a generalised Kähler transformation.

More exactly, we will consider potentials with monodromies of the form

\displaystyle V(z+\bar{z},w+\bar{w})\rightarrow V(z+\bar{z},w+\bar{w})+\alpha_{1}(z+\bar{z})+\alpha_{2}(w+\bar{w})+\gamma(z+\bar{w})(w+\bar{w})\,,

(4.26)

with some constants
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\gamma,\delta
.
The parameter
\gamma
corresponds to a constant shift (large gauge transformation) of the
B
-field.
More precisely, we will phrase this monodromy as arising from some transformation acting on the real parts
y_{1}\equiv z+\bar{z}
,
y_{2}=w+\bar{w}
.
We will write this transformation as
(y_{1},y_{2})\rightarrow(y_{1}^{\star},y_{2}^{\star})=(y_{1}+n_{1},y_{2}+n_{2})
.
This can describe global shifts by constants
n_{1},n_{2}
, under which we make a periodic identification, or in the non-compact case rotations by
2\pi
(with
n_{1}=n_{2}=0
).
We assume the potential behaves as

\displaystyle V(y_{1}^{\star},y_{2}^{\star})=V(y_{1},y_{2})+\alpha_{1}y_{1}+\alpha_{2}y_{2}+\gamma y_{1}y_{2}+\delta\,.

(4.27)

We want to consider the effect of this monodromy on the dual coordinates and the dual potentials.
The Legendre transformations are:

\displaystyle V^{(c)}(y_{1},\tilde{y}_{2})=V(y_{1},y_{2})+y_{2}\tilde{y}_{2}\,\quad V^{(t)}(\tilde{y}_{1},y_{2})=V(y_{1},y_{2})-y_{1}\tilde{y}_{1}\,.

(4.28)

It follows that the corresponding shifts of the dual coordinates
\tilde{y}_{1}=V_{1}
and
\tilde{y}_{2}=-V_{2}
are:

\displaystyle\tilde{y}_{1}\rightarrow\tilde{y}_{1}^{\star}=\tilde{y}_{1}+\alpha_{1}+\gamma y_{2}\,,\quad\tilde{y}_{2}\rightarrow\tilde{y}_{2}^{\star}=\tilde{y}_{2}-\alpha_{2}-\gamma y_{1}\,.

(4.29)

By carefully considering the definition of the Legendre transformed potentials, we can show (see appendix
B
) that:

\displaystyle V^{(c)}(y_{1}^{\star},\tilde{y}_{2}^{\star})=V^{(c)}(y_{1},\tilde{y}_{2})+\alpha_{1}y_{1}+\delta+n_{2}(\tilde{y}_{2}-\alpha_{2}-\gamma y_{1})\,,

(4.30)

\displaystyle V^{(t)}(\tilde{y}_{1}^{*},y_{2}^{*})=V^{(t)}(\tilde{y}_{1},y_{2})+\alpha_{2}y_{2}+\delta-n_{1}(\tilde{y}_{1}+\alpha_{1}+\gamma y_{2})\,,

(4.31)

In both (
4.30
) and (
4.31
), the shift of the generalised Kähler potential is still a generalised Kähler transformation.
However, if we consider the doubly dual potential,
\widetilde{V}(\tilde{y}_{1},\tilde{y}_{2})
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widetilde{V}(\tilde{y}_{1}^{*},\tilde{y}_{2}^{*})&=\widetilde{V}(\tilde{y}_{1},\tilde{y}_{2})-\gamma y_{1}(\tilde{y}_{1},\tilde{y}_{2})y_{2}(\tilde{y}_{1},\tilde{y}_{2})+\delta\\
&\qquad-n_{1}(\tilde{y}_{1}+\alpha_{1}+\gamma y_{2}(\tilde{y}_{1},\tilde{y}_{2}))+n_{2}(\tilde{y}_{2}-\alpha_{2}-\gamma y_{1}(\tilde{y}_{1},\tilde{y}_{2}))\,.\end{split}

(4.32)

When expressed in terms of the dual superfields, this is not necessarily of the form of a generalised Kähler transformation.
The corresponding metric and
B
-field configuration will then not describe a geometric space but rather a T-fold.
We further see that while
\widetilde{V}
behaves less than optimally, both
V^{(c)}
and
V^{(t)}
transform nicely: and it is these that our doubled-generalised potential is constructed from.
We now describe two examples illustrating these points.

4.3.1
Torus fibration with constant H-flux and codimension-1 T-fold

We start with the example of a torus with constant
H
-flux. It is well-known that T-duality transformations of such backgrounds can lead outside the scope of conventional geometry, producing non-geometric spaces known as T-folds.
We consider again a setup with one chiral superfield (with real part
y_{1}
) and a twisted chiral superfield (with real part
y_{2}
) and the following potential:

\displaystyle V(y_{1},y_{2})=\tfrac{1}{2}\left((c+my_{2})y_{1}^{2}-cy_{2}^{2}-\tfrac{1}{3}my_{2}^{3}\right)\,,

(4.33)

where
c
and
m
are constants. This gives metric and
B
-field

\displaystyle ds^{2}=(c+my_{2})(dx_{1}^{2}+dy_{1}^{2}+dx_{2}^{2}+dy_{2}^{2})\,,\quad B=-my_{1}dy_{1}\wedge dy_{2}-my_{1}dx_{1}\wedge dx_{2}\,,

(4.34)

describing the transverse geometry to a thrice-smeared NS5 brane, which is of codimension 1.
If we take
y_{1},x_{1},x_{2}
to be periodic (with period
2\pi
) then this is a fibration of a three-torus over an interval parametrised by
y_{2}
. The three-torus has constant
H
-flux,
H=dB=-mdy_{1}\wedge dx_{1}\wedge dx_{2}
.
The gauge choice for the
B
-field implies that for
y_{1}\rightarrow y_{1}+2\pi
we patch by a large gauge transformation, which corresponds to a generalised Kähler transformation of the potential:

\displaystyle V(y_{1}+2\pi,y_{2})=V(y_{1},y_{2})+2\pi(c+my_{2})(y_{1}+\pi)\,,

(4.35)

or in complex terms

\displaystyle\begin{split}V(z+\bar{z}+2\pi,w+\bar{w})=V(z+\bar{z},w+\bar{w})+2\pi\big(&c\pi+c(z+\bar{z})+m\pi(w+\bar{w})\\
&\qquad+m(zw+\bar{z}\bar{w}+z\bar{w}+\bar{z}w)\big)\,.\end{split}

(4.36)

T-dualising the geometry (
4.34
) on the
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
directions leads to

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widetilde{ds}^{2}&=f(y_{2})(dy_{1}^{2}+dy_{2}^{2})+\frac{f(y_{2})}{f(y_{2})^{2}+(my_{1})^{2}}(d\tilde{x}_{1}^{2}+d\tilde{x}_{2}^{2})\,,\\
\tilde{B}&=-my_{1}dy_{1}\wedge dy_{2}+\frac{my_{1}}{f(y_{2})^{2}+(my_{1})^{2}}d\tilde{x}_{1}\wedge d\tilde{x}_{2}\,.\end{split}

(4.37)

For
y_{1}\rightarrow y_{1}+2\pi
this is not patched by any combination of diffeomorphisms or gauge transformations: instead the geometry is only well-defined up to a non-trivial T-duality transformation. This is associated to a so-called ‘exotic’
5_{2}^{2}
brane which is non-geometric in nature (see
[
16
]
for a detailed discussion).
Here we have the codimension-1 version of this exotic brane, which has been recently studied in
[
30
]
for example.

Now we consider how this T-fold behaviour manifests in the generalised Kähler potentials.
The Legendre dual coordinates are defined by

\displaystyle\tilde{y}_{1}=V_{1}=f(y_{2})y_{1}\,,\quad\tilde{y}_{2}=-V_{2}=-\tfrac{1}{2}my_{1}^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2m}(f(y_{2})^{2}-c^{2})\,,

(4.38)

where
f(y_{2})\equiv c+my_{2}
.
To solve for
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
, we write
y_{1}=\tilde{y}_{1}/f
where
f=f(y_{2}(\tilde{y}_{1},\tilde{y}_{2}))
is determined via (
4.38
). Explicitly:

\displaystyle f(\tilde{y}_{1},\tilde{y}_{2})^{2}=m\left(\tilde{y}_{2}+\tfrac{c^{2}}{2m}\pm\sqrt{\left(\tilde{y}_{2}+\tfrac{c^{2}}{2m}\right)^{2}+\tilde{y}_{1}^{2}}\right)\,.

(4.39)

The fully T-dual potential can then be written down as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widetilde{V}(\tilde{y}_{1},\tilde{y}_{2})&=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\tilde{y}_{1}^{2}}{f(\tilde{y}_{1},\tilde{y}_{2})}+\frac{f(\tilde{y}_{1},\tilde{y}_{2})-c}{m}\tilde{y}_{2}\\
&\qquad-\frac{1}{2m^{2}}\left(c(f(\tilde{y}_{1},\tilde{y}_{2})-c)^{2}-\frac{1}{3}(f(\tilde{y}_{1},\tilde{y}_{2})-c)^{3}\right)\,.\end{split}

(4.40)

From (
4.38
) we see that under
y_{1}\rightarrow y_{1}+2\pi
we have

\displaystyle\tilde{y}_{1}\rightarrow\tilde{y}_{1}+2\pi f\,,\quad\tilde{y}_{2}\rightarrow\tilde{y}_{2}-2m\pi(f^{-1}\tilde{y}_{1}+\pi)\,.

(4.41)

As the function
f
is originally independent of
y_{1}
, it is invariant under these shifts (this can be double checked at the level of the quadratic equation solved by
f^{2}
obtained from (
4.38
) on substituting
y_{1}=\tilde{y}_{1}/f
into the second equation).
It follows that the monodromy of the dual potential is

\displaystyle\widetilde{V}\rightarrow\widetilde{V}-4\pi\tilde{y}_{1}+2\pi cf^{-1}\tilde{y}_{1}-4\pi^{2}f+2\pi^{2}c\,,

(4.42)

which matches what one gets from the general expression (
4.32
).
To emphasise the unpleasant nature of this transformation, we write it more explicitly and in terms of complex coordinates as:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widetilde{V}&\rightarrow\widetilde{V}-4\pi(\tilde{w}+\bar{\tilde{w}})+\frac{2\pi c(\tilde{w}+\bar{\tilde{w}})}{\sqrt{m\left[\tilde{z}+\bar{\tilde{z}}+\tfrac{c^{2}}{2m}\pm\sqrt{(\tilde{z}+\bar{\tilde{z}})^{2}+(\tilde{w}+\bar{\tilde{w}})^{2}}\right]}}\\
&\qquad-4\pi^{2}\sqrt{m\left[\tilde{z}+\bar{\tilde{z}}+\tfrac{c^{2}}{2m}\pm\sqrt{(\tilde{z}+\bar{\tilde{z}})^{2}+(\tilde{w}+\bar{\tilde{w}})^{2}}\right]}+2\pi^{2}c\end{split}

(4.43)

In terms of the superfields
\tilde{z}
and
\tilde{w}
, this is not a generalised Kähler transformation.
This reflects the non-geometric nature of the background.
We could rewrite in terms of the original superfields that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widetilde{V}(\tilde{z},\tilde{w})&\rightarrow\widetilde{V}(\tilde{z},\tilde{w})-4\pi(c+m(w+\bar{w}))(z+\bar{z})\\
&\qquad+2\pi c(w+\bar{w})-4\pi^{2}(c+m(w+\bar{w}))+2\pi^{2}c\end{split}

(4.44)

which would be a generalised Kähler transformation if we had access to the dual description involving
z
and
w
. However, strictly speaking this requires going a doubled formalism.

Accordingly, we now construct the doubled potential
\mathbb{V}=\tfrac{1}{2}(V^{(c)}+V^{(t)})
using the two partial duals:

\displaystyle\begin{split}V^{(c)}&=\frac{1}{2}\left(f(y_{1},\tilde{y}_{2})y_{1}^{2}-\frac{1}{m^{2}}\left(c(f(y_{1},\tilde{y}_{2})-c)^{2}+\frac{1}{3}(f(y_{1},\tilde{y}_{2})-c)^{3}\right)\right)\\
&\quad+\frac{1}{m}\tilde{y}_{2}(f(y_{1},\tilde{y}_{2})-c)\,,\\
V^{(t)}&=-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\tilde{y}_{1}^{2}}{c+my_{2}}+cy_{2}^{2}+\tfrac{1}{3}my_{2}^{3}\right)\,,\end{split}

(4.45)

where

\displaystyle f(y_{1},\tilde{y}_{2})^{2}=m^{2}y_{1}^{2}+c^{2}+2m\tilde{y}_{2}^{2}\,.

(4.46)

The monodromy transformation arises from the following shifts:

\displaystyle y_{1}\rightarrow y_{1}+2\pi,\quad\tilde{y}_{1}\rightarrow\tilde{y}_{1}+2\pi f(y_{2})\,,\quad\tilde{y}_{2}\rightarrow\tilde{y}_{2}-2m\pi(y_{1}+\pi)\,,

(4.47)

in terms of which

\displaystyle\begin{split}V^{(c)}&\rightarrow V^{(c)}+2\pi c(y_{1}+\pi)\,,\\
V^{(t)}&\rightarrow V^{(t)}-2\pi(\tilde{y}_{1}+\pi f(y_{2}))\,.\end{split}

(4.48)

It follows that
\mathbb{V}
shifts by a linear function of the coordinates which is a generalised Kähler transformation of the doubled-generalised potential. Once separating
\tilde{V}
from
\mathbb{V}
These transformation turn into (
4.43
) upon expressing dual coordinates in terms of the physical ones.

4.3.2
Codimension-2 T-fold

Next, we start with the codimension-2 NS5 solution for which an
\mathcal{N}=(2,2)
description is also available
[
17
,
31
]
.
8
8

8

Doubled formulations of the codimension-2 NS5 to
5_{2}^{2}
duality in terms of
\mathcal{N}=(4,4)
gauged linear sigma models in superspace were studied in
[
18
,
19
,
20
]
.

Let
u=y_{1}+iy_{2}
and let
f(u)
be an arbitrary holomorphic function.
Then let
V=\mathrm{Im}(f(u))
.
The metric and
B
-field have components

\displaystyle g_{11}=g_{22}=\mathrm{Im}f^{\prime\prime}\,,\quad B_{12}=-\mathrm{Re}f^{\prime\prime}\,,

(4.49)

where
f^{\prime\prime}
denotes the second derivative of
f
.
The double T-dual of this model will describe a T-fold if there is an initial monodromy giving a constant shift of the original
B
-field.
To realise this, we view
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
as coordinates parametrising the
\mathbb{R}^{2}
transverse space of a codimension-2 brane.
We then consider functions which are not single-valued under rotations about the origin.
In particular, if
f(e^{2\pi i}u)=f(u)+\tfrac{\gamma}{2}u^{2}
then under such a rotation
V\rightarrow V+\gamma y_{1}y_{2}
and
B_{12}\rightarrow B_{12}-\gamma
.
A particular choice could be

\displaystyle f(u)=-\frac{i\gamma}{4\pi}u^{2}(\ln u-\tfrac{3}{2})\Rightarrow f^{\prime\prime}(u)=-\frac{i\gamma}{2\pi}\ln u\,.

(4.50)

Writing
y_{1}+iy_{2}=re^{i\theta}
,
the geometry is thus

\displaystyle ds^{2}=-\tfrac{\gamma}{2\pi}\ln r\left(dr^{2}+r^{2}d\theta^{2}+dx_{1}^{2}+dx_{2}^{2}\right)\,,\quad B=-\tfrac{\gamma}{2\pi}\theta rdr\wedge d\theta-\tfrac{\gamma}{2\pi}\theta dx_{1}\wedge dx_{2}\,.

(4.51)

This can be viewed as describing the geometry near a codimension-two NS5 brane.
The dual T-fold geometry is:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widetilde{ds}^{2}&=-\tfrac{\gamma}{2\pi}\ln r\left(dr^{2}+r^{2}d\theta^{2}\right)+\frac{-\tfrac{\gamma}{2\pi}\ln r}{(\tfrac{\gamma}{2\pi}\ln r)^{2}+(\tfrac{\gamma}{2\pi}\theta)^{2}}(d\tilde{x}_{1}^{2}+d\tilde{x}_{2}^{2})\\
\tilde{B}&=-\tfrac{\gamma}{2\pi}\theta rdr\wedge d\theta+\frac{\tfrac{\gamma}{2\pi}\theta}{(\tfrac{\gamma}{2\pi}\ln r)^{2}+(\tfrac{\gamma}{2\pi}\theta)^{2}}d\tilde{x}_{1}\wedge d\tilde{x}_{2}\end{split}

(4.52)

which transforms by a non-geometric T-duality for
\theta\rightarrow\theta+2\pi
.

Rather than explicitly determine the dual potentials in this case, we simply note that the general discussion at the start of this subsection allows us to infer that they will inherit monodromies under

\displaystyle\tilde{y}_{1}\rightarrow\tilde{y}_{1}^{\star}=\tilde{y}_{1}+\gamma y_{2}\,,\quad\tilde{y}_{2}\rightarrow\tilde{y}_{2}^{\star}=\tilde{y}_{1}-\gamma y_{1}\,,\quad

(4.53)

in terms of which
V^{(c)}
and
V^{(t)}
are in fact invariant

\displaystyle V^{(c)}(y_{1}^{\star},\tilde{y}_{2}^{\star})=V^{(c)}(y_{1},\tilde{y}_{2})\,,\quad V^{(t)}(\tilde{y}_{1}^{*},y_{2}^{*})=V^{(t)}(\tilde{y}_{1},y_{2})

(4.54)

but

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widetilde{V}(\tilde{y}_{1}^{*},\tilde{y}_{2}^{*})&=\widetilde{V}(\tilde{y}_{1},\tilde{y}_{2})-\gamma y_{1}(\tilde{y}_{1},\tilde{y}_{2})y_{2}(\tilde{y}_{1},\tilde{y}_{2})\,.\end{split}

(4.55)

Accordingly while
\widetilde{V}
in this case behaves poorly under the global shift leading to the T-duality identification, the doubled-generalised Kähler potential built from
V^{(c)}
and
V^{(t)}
will be well-behaved: it is invariant. Note in this case that the transformation of the doubled coordinates
\mathbb{Y}^{M}
leading to the monodromy is exactly that of a
B
-field shift in the original choice of polarisation with
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
as physical coordinates, and hence a bivector shift in the dual choice with
\tilde{y}_{1}
and
\tilde{y}_{2}
as physical coordinates.
9
9

9

This is a bivector shift acting on a geometry described by one chiral and one twisted chiral superfield, which preserves the fact that the complex structures commute. This is unlike the case of the bivector shift acting on a purely chiral or purely twisted chiral (i.e. Kähler) geometry, which appears in the next example, albeit not as a T-fold. It would be interesting to find explicit examples of T-folds which map from BiLP to non-BiLP geometries.

4.4
Semi-chiral geometry without semi-chirals

We now consider an example where we can describe a geometry that ordinarily requires semi-chiral superfields, without introducing semi-chiral superfields.
The starting point is the
D=2d
-dimensional Kähler geometry of (
2.77
).
In this case, the generalised Kähler potential is a genuine Kähler potential,
V=V(y^{\alpha})
, which we suppose to depend only on the real parts of
d
chiral superfields.
The doubled potential is then

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}(\mathbb{Y})=\tfrac{1}{2}(V(y)+\widetilde{V}(\tilde{y}))\,,

(4.56)

where
\widetilde{V}(\tilde{y}_{a})
is the Legendre dual of
V(y)
, and depends only on the real parts of
d
dual twisted chiral superfields.
We recall that the constraints (
3.18
) can be written democratically as

\displaystyle\mathbb{Y}^{M}=\Theta^{MN}\partial_{N}\mathbb{V}\,,\quad\Theta^{MN}=2\begin{pmatrix}0&-{1}\\
{1}&0\end{pmatrix}\,,

(4.57)

implying here

\displaystyle y^{\alpha}=-\widetilde{V}^{\alpha},\,\quad\tilde{y}_{\alpha}=V_{\alpha}\,.

(4.58)

The components of the original Kähler metric are
g_{\alpha\beta}=V_{\alpha\beta}

and we have
\widetilde{V}^{\alpha\beta}=-(V^{-1})^{\alpha\beta}
.

Now we consider an
\mathrm{O}(d,d)
transformation according to the prescription of section
3.4
, with
\mathbb{X}^{\prime M}=\mathcal{O}^{-1}{}^{M}{}_{N}\mathbb{X}^{N}
,
\mathbb{Y}^{\prime M}=\mathcal{O}^{-1}{}^{M}{}_{N}\mathbb{Y}^{N}
, and the bivector transformation given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}^{-1}{}^{M}{}_{N}=\begin{pmatrix}{1}&-\beta\\
0&{1}\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad\beta\equiv\lambda\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}\,.

(4.59)

Explicitly, this is (where possible we drop the indices in expresssions below):

\displaystyle x^{\prime}=x-\beta\tilde{x}\,,\quad\tilde{x}^{\prime}=\tilde{x}\,,

(4.60)

\displaystyle y^{\prime}=y-\beta\tilde{y}\,,\quad\tilde{y}^{\prime}=\tilde{y}\,,

(4.61)

with the doubled potential transforming as a scalar, hence

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}^{\prime}(\mathbb{Y}^{\prime})=\tfrac{1}{2}(V(y^{\prime}+\beta\tilde{y}^{\prime})+\widetilde{V}(\tilde{y}^{\prime}))\,.

(4.62)

The transformed constraint can be written as

\displaystyle\mathbb{Y}^{\prime M}=\Theta^{\prime MN}\partial^{\prime}_{N}\mathbb{V}^{\prime}\,,

(4.63)

with

\displaystyle\Theta^{\prime MN}=(\mathcal{O}^{-1}\Theta\mathcal{O}^{-T})^{MN}=2\begin{pmatrix}-2\beta&-{1}\\
{1}&0\end{pmatrix}

(4.64)

hence

\displaystyle y^{\prime\alpha}=-\widetilde{V}^{\alpha}-\beta^{\alpha\beta}V_{\beta}\,,\quad\tilde{y}^{\prime}_{\alpha}=V_{\alpha}

(4.65)

Using (
4.61
) this can be checked to be equivalent to the original constraints (
4.58
).

The initial set of superfields obey an equation of the form (
3.44
)
with over-doubled complex structures
\mathbb{J}_{\pm}
given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{J}_{+}=\begin{pmatrix}0&{1}_{2d}\\
-{1}_{2d}&0\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad\mathbb{J}_{-}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&{1}_{d}&0\\
0&0&0&-{1}_{d}\\
-{1}_{d}&0&0&0\\
0&{1}_{d}&0&0\\
\end{pmatrix}\,.

(4.66)

After the
\mathrm{O}(d,d)
transformation we obtain:

\displaystyle\mathbb{J}_{+}^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}0&{1}_{2d}\\
-{1}_{2d}&0\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad\mathbb{J}_{-}^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&{1}_{d}&2\beta\\
0&0&0&-{1}_{d}\\
-{1}_{d}&-2\beta&0&0\\
0&{1}_{d}&0&0\\
\end{pmatrix}\,.

(4.67)

This means that while (
\tilde{x}^{\prime}_{\alpha},\tilde{y}^{\prime}_{\alpha})
on their own obey the defining conditions of twisted chiral superfields, once we look also at (
x^{\prime\alpha},y^{\prime\alpha})
we have

\displaystyle\hat{D}_{+}x^{\prime}=+D_{+}y^{\prime}\,,\quad\hat{D}_{+}y^{\prime}=-D_{+}x^{\prime}\,,

(4.68)

\displaystyle\hat{D}_{-}x^{\prime}=+D_{-}y^{\prime}+2\beta D_{-}\tilde{x}^{\prime}\,,\quad\hat{D}_{-}y^{\prime}=-D_{-}x^{\prime}-2\beta D_{-}\tilde{x}^{\prime}\,.

(4.69)

In particular there is a mixing between the physical and dual superfields!

To make sense of this, we follow the general discussion in section
3.4.4
and take into account the constraints obeyed by the derivatives of the superfields.
Taking derivatives of (
4.65
) leads as expected to

\displaystyle D_{\pm}\tilde{y}^{\prime}=(g^{\prime}-b^{\prime})D_{\pm}y^{\prime}\,,\quad D_{\pm}\tilde{x}^{\prime}=\pm(g^{\prime}\mp b^{\prime})D_{\pm}x^{\prime}\,,

(4.70)

with the correct transformations of the metric and
B
-field:

\displaystyle g^{\prime}\pm b^{\prime}=(g^{-1}\pm\beta)^{-1}\,.

(4.71)

This agrees with (
2.84
) on noting that the
B
-field components restricted to non-isometric directions are a total derivative.

Using these constraints in the non-standard relationships (
4.69
) to eliminate
\tilde{x}_{\alpha}
and
\tilde{y}_{\alpha}
, we obtain (after some algebraic manipulation):

\displaystyle\hat{D}_{-}x^{\prime}=(1+\beta g)(1-\beta g)^{-1}D_{-}\tilde{x}^{\prime}\,,\quad\hat{D}_{-}y^{\prime}=-(1-\beta g)(1+\beta g)^{-1}D_{-}x^{\prime}\,.

(4.72)

From (
4.68
) and (
4.72
) we can read off the spacetime complex structures
J_{\pm}^{\prime}
for the coordinates
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
.
They match those obtained in (
2.83
) by using a bivector
\mathrm{O}(d,d)
transformation as well as a coordinate transformation of the non-isometric directions.
This shows that the somewhat peculiar
\mathrm{O}(d,d)
transformations of our doubled formulation correctly reproduce the usual T-duality, once the right coordinates have been identified to match with.

This is strongly reminiscent of the work in
[
32
]
where a potential containing semi-chiral fields was obtained as a (local) quotient of a space described solely in terms of chiral and twisted chiral superfields. This approach allows a model containing
n
semi-chiral multiplets to be viewed as the quotient space of a model where the semi-chiral subspace was doubled and described in terms of
n
chiral and
n
twisted chiral superfields.

Worldsheet superspace conventions

In this section we lay down two dimensional superspace conventions which are being used throughout the text. We follow the conventions used in
[
28
]
where more details are provided.

\tau,\sigma
, using which we can define light-cone coordinates as

\sigma^{{+\!\!\!+}}=\tau+\sigma,\qquad\sigma^{=}=\tau-\sigma.

(A.1)

The
\mathcal{N}=(1,1)
fermionic coordinates are denoted by
\theta^{+}
and
\theta^{-}

(A.3)

The
\mathcal{N}=(1,1)
integration measure is explicitly given by,

\displaystyle\int d^{2}\sigma\,d^{2}\theta=\int d\tau\,d\sigma\,D_{+}D_{-}|

(A.4)

Passing from
\mathcal{N}=(1,1)
to
\mathcal{N}=(2,2)
superspace requires the introduction of two more real fermionic coordinates
\hat{\theta}^{+}
and
\hat{\theta}^{-}

where the corresponding fermionic derivatives satisfy,

\displaystyle\hat{D}_{+}^{2}=-\frac{i}{2}\,\partial_{=\!\!\!|}\,,\qquad\hat{D}_{-}^{2}=-\frac{i}{2}\,\partial_{=}\,,

(A.5)

whereas all the anticommutators with the
(1,1)
superderivative vanish. The
\mathcal{N}=(2,2)
integration measure is extended to,

\displaystyle\int d^{2}\sigma\,d^{2}\theta\,d^{2}\hat{\theta}=\int d\tau\,d\sigma\,D_{+}D_{-}\,\hat{D}_{+}\hat{D}_{-}|

(A.6)

In order to effectively work with the constraint
(2,2)
superfields we introduce a complex basis as

\mathbb{D}_{\pm}\equiv\hat{D}_{\pm}+i\,D_{\pm}\,\qquad\bar{\mathbb{D}}_{\pm}\equiv\hat{D}_{\pm}-i\,D_{\pm}\,

(A.7)

satisfying

\{\mathbb{D}_{+},\bar{\mathbb{D}}_{+}\}=-2i\,\partial_{=\!\!\!|}\,,\qquad\{\mathbb{D}_{-},\bar{\mathbb{D}}_{-}\}=-2i\,\partial_{=}\,.

(A.8)

A.3
Complex structures in adapted coordinates

The passage from
(1,1)
to
(2,2)
superspace requires a choice of a target space complex structure. However, due to the passage to adapted coordinates this dependence is usually hidden. Here we write out the dependence of the
(2,2)
constraints on the complex structure as well as the constraints on the dual fields.

In order to do this we rewrite the above formulas in real coordinates
X=(x^{\alpha},x^{\mu},y^{\alpha},y^{\mu})
. The
N=(2,2)
constraint in which the complex structures appear explicitly then reads

\displaystyle\hat{D}_{\pm}X^{I}=(J_{\pm})^{I}{}_{J}D_{\pm}X\,,

(A.20)

and following our superfield conventions (summarised in appendix
A
) we have in these real coordinates that

\displaystyle J_{+}=\begin{pmatrix}0&I_{d}\\
-I_{d}&0\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad J_{-}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\sigma\\
-\sigma&0\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad{\footnotesize\sigma\equiv\begin{pmatrix}I_{n_{c}}&0\\
0&-I_{n_{t}}\end{pmatrix}}\,.

(A.21)

Explicitly, this means

\displaystyle\hat{D}_{\pm}x^{\alpha}=+D_{\pm}y^{\alpha}\,,\quad\hat{D}_{\pm}y^{\alpha}=-D_{\pm}x^{\alpha}\,,\quad\hat{D}_{\pm}x^{\mu}=\pm D_{\pm}y^{\mu}\,,\quad\hat{D}_{\pm}y^{\mu}=\mp D_{\pm}x^{\mu}\,.

(A.22)

For dual superfields

\displaystyle\tilde{w}_{\alpha}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\tilde{y}_{\alpha}+i\tilde{x}_{\alpha})\,,\quad\tilde{z}_{\mu}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\tilde{y}_{\mu}+i\tilde{x}_{\mu})\,.

(A.23)

we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{J}_{+}=-J_{+}^{t}=J_{+}\,,\quad\widetilde{J}_{-}=+J_{-}^{t}=-J_{-}\,,

(A.24)

which appear in the constraints
\hat{D}_{\pm}\widetilde{X}=\widetilde{J}_{\pm}D_{\pm}\widetilde{X}
with
\widetilde{X}=(\tilde{x}_{\alpha},\tilde{x}_{\mu},\tilde{y}_{\alpha},\tilde{y}_{\mu})
.
Explicitly,

\displaystyle\hat{D}_{\pm}\tilde{x}_{\alpha}=\pm D_{\pm}\tilde{y}_{\alpha}\,,\quad\hat{D}_{\pm}\tilde{y}_{\alpha}=\mp D_{\pm}\tilde{x}_{\alpha}\,,\quad\hat{D}_{\pm}\tilde{x}_{\mu}=+D_{\pm}\tilde{y}_{\mu}\,,\quad\hat{D}_{\pm}\tilde{y}_{\mu}=-D_{\pm}\tilde{x}_{\mu}\,.

(A.25)

In these adapted coordinates it is easy to extract expressions for the action in
\mathcal{N}=(1,1)
superspace since

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=\hat{D}_{+}\hat{D}_{-}V(X)\mid

(A.26)

\displaystyle=\partial_{IJ}V(J_{+}D_{+}X)^{I}(J_{-}D_{-}X)^{J}+\partial_{J}V(J_{-}J_{+})^{J}{}_{K}D_{+}D_{-}X^{K}

\displaystyle=(J_{+}D_{+}X)^{I}V_{IJ}(J_{-}D_{-}X)^{J}-D_{+}X^{I}V_{IJ}(J_{-}J_{+})^{J}{}_{K}D_{-}X^{K}\,.

For the case where we have isometries such that
V=V(y^{\alpha},y^{\mu})
then the Hessian matrix becomes

\textrm{Hess}=V_{IJ}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&M\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad M=\begin{pmatrix}V_{\alpha\beta}&V_{\alpha\mu}\\
V_{\mu\alpha}&V_{\mu\nu}\end{pmatrix}\,.

(A.27)

Then we find

E=G+B=J_{+}^{T}\,\textrm{Hess}\,J_{-}-\textrm{Hess}\,J_{+}J_{-}=\begin{pmatrix}M\sigma&0\\
0&M\sigma\end{pmatrix}\,.

(A.28)

Appendix B
Calculation of monodromies of Legendre transformed potentials

This appendix serves to verify the formula (
4.30
) for the monodromy of the Legendre transformed potential
V^{(c)}
. The calculations for
V^{(t)}
and
\widetilde{V}
are similar.
The monodromy is inherited from the transformations
y_{1}^{\star}=y_{1}+n_{1}
and
y_{2}^{\star}=y_{2}+n_{2}
of the original coordinates, in terms of which
V(y_{1}^{\star},y_{2}^{\star})
is given by (
4.27
).
Given the definitions of the dual coordinates we have
\tilde{y}_{1}^{\star}=\tilde{y}_{1}+\alpha_{1}+\gamma y_{2}
,
\tilde{y}_{2}^{\star}=\tilde{y}_{2}-\alpha_{2}-\gamma y_{1}
.
We write
V^{(c)}
as

\displaystyle V^{(c)}(y_{1},\tilde{y}_{2})=V(y_{1},\hat{y}_{2})+\hat{y}_{2}\tilde{y}_{2}

(B.1)

where
\hat{y}_{2}=\hat{y}_{2}(y_{1},\tilde{y}_{2})
by definition is derived from the equation
\tilde{y}_{2}=-V_{2}
.
Then,
\hat{y}_{2}^{\star}=\hat{y}_{2}(y_{1}+n_{1},\tilde{y}_{2}-\alpha_{2}-\gamma y_{1})
solves

\displaystyle\tilde{y}_{2}-\alpha_{2}-\gamma y_{1}=-V_{2}(y_{1}+n_{1},y_{2}-n_{2}+n_{2})=-V_{2}(y_{1},y_{2}-n_{2})\,,

(B.2)

hence
\hat{y}_{2}^{\star}=\hat{y}_{2}+n_{2}
.
It follows that

\displaystyle\begin{split}V^{(c)}(y_{1}^{\star},\tilde{y}_{2}^{\star})&=V(y_{1}^{\star},\hat{y}_{2}^{\star})+\hat{y}_{2}^{\star}\tilde{y}_{2}^{\star}\\
&=V(y_{1},\hat{y}_{2})+\alpha_{1}y_{1}+\alpha_{2}\hat{y}_{2}+\gamma y_{1}\hat{y}_{2}+\delta+(\hat{y}_{2}+n_{2})(\tilde{y}_{2}-\alpha_{2}-\gamma y_{1})\,.\end{split}

(B.3)

Multiplying out and cancelling leads to (
4.30
).
