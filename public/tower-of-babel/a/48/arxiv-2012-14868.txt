Minimum Excess Risk in Bayesian Learning
Aolin Xu, Maxim Raginsky

Abstract
We analyze the best achievable performance of Bayesian learning under generative models by defining and upper-bounding the minimum excess risk (MER): the gap between the minimum expected loss attainable by learning from data and the minimum expected loss that could be achieved if the model realization were known. The definition of MER provides a principled way to define different notions of uncertainties in Bayesian learning, including the aleatoric uncertainty and the minimum epistemic uncertainty. Two methods for deriving upper bounds for the MER are presented. The first method, generally suitable for Bayesian learning with a parametric generative model, upper-bounds the MER by the conditional mutual information between the model parameters and the quantity being predicted given the observed data. It allows us to quantify the rate at which the MER decays to zero as more data becomes available. Under realizable models, this method also relates the MER to the richness of the generative function class, notably the VC dimension in binary classification. The second method, particularly suitable for Bayesian learning with a parametric predictive model, relates the MER to the minimum estimation error of the model parameters from data. It explicitly shows how the uncertainty in model parameter estimation translates to the MER and to the final prediction uncertainty. We also extend the definition and analysis of MER to the setting with multiple model families and the setting with nonparametric models. Along the discussions we draw some comparisons between the MER in Bayesian learning and the excess risk in frequentist learning.

1
Introduction

Bayesian learning under generative models has been gaining considerable attention in recent years as an alternative to the frequentist learning.
In the Bayesian setting, the observed data
Z^{n}=((X_{1},Y_{1}),\ldots,(X_{n},Y_{n}))
is modeled as conditionally i.i.d. samples generated from a probabilistic model given the model realization
P_{Z|W}
, while the model is treated either as a random element of some parametric model family drawn according to a prior distribution of the model parameters
W
, or as a nonparametric random process
[
1
]
. The task of Bayesian learning is to predict a quantity of interest
Y
based on the observed data
(X,Z^{n})
and the structure of the model, while the quality of prediction can be assessed by the expected loss with respect to some loss function
\mathbf{E}[\ell(Y,\psi(X,Z^{n}))]
.
Although Bayesian learning often relies on posterior sampling or approximation techniques
[
2
,
3
]

and hence has much higher computational complexity than its frequentist counterpart, the Bayesian viewpoint has many attractive features, e.g., reducing overfitting
[
4
]
, quantifying uncertainty in making predictions
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
, enabling model compression
[
8
]
, etc.
In contrast with the growing attention to the algorithmic side of Bayesian learning, its performance analysis
is relatively scarce compared to the volume of literature on the theoretical analysis of frequentist learning.
In this paper, we set aside the computational issues in Bayesian learning and focus on analyzing its best achievable performance under the generative model with respect to general loss functions.

1.1
Overview of the presentation

In Section
2
, we define the
minimum excess risk
(MER) in Bayesian learning as the gap between the Bayes risk
R_{\ell}(Y|X,Z^{n})
, defined as the minimum expected loss attainable by learning from the data, and its fundamental limit
R_{\ell}(Y|X,W)
, defined as the minimum expected loss that would be achieved if the model parameters were known.
The MER is an algorithm-independent quantity that captures the uncertainty arising from the lack of knowledge of the underlying model parameters, commonly known as
epistemic uncertainty
. Its value and rate of convergence to zero reflect the difficulty of the learning problem.
The decomposition of the Bayes risk into its fundamental limit and MER also provides rigorous definitions of the aleatoric uncertainty and the minimum epistemic uncertainty in Bayesian learning, the quantification of which has become an important research topic in recent years
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
.
To the authors’ knowledge, the general definition of MER is new, and has not been systematically studied before.

We then present two methodologies for deriving upper bounds on the MER. We first show in Section
3
that, under a generic parametric generative model and for a wide range of loss functions, the MER can be upper-bounded in terms of the conditional mutual information between the model parameters and the quantity being predicted given the observed data,
I(W;Y|X,Z^{n})
. This leads to asymptotic upper bounds on the MER that scale as
O({d}/{n})
or
O({\sqrt{{d}/{n}}})
depending on the loss function, where
d
is the dimension of the parameter space and
n
is the data size.
It also reveals an MER-information relationship in Bayesian learning, echoing the generalization-information relationship in frequentist learning
[
12
]
. Under realizable models, it is shown that for any bounded loss function, the MER for binary classification scales as
O(d/n)
, where
d
is the VC dimension of the generative function class.
Next, in Section
4
, we explore two alternative methods for bounding the MER. One relies on the smoothness of the decision rule in the model parameters, while the other relies on the smoothness of the minimum expected loss as a functional of the predictive model.
The resulting upper bounds single out the dependence of the MER on the minimum achievable estimation error of the model parameters from the data, e.g., on the minimum mean square error (MMSE) of the estimated model parameters
R_{2}(W|X,Z^{n})
.
It explicitly shows how the difficulty of model parameter estimation translates into the difficulty of prediction due to the model uncertainty.

The analysis of the MER in the single model family setting can be extended to the setting with multiple model families.
The definition of MER can also be extended to the setting with nonparametric generative models, such as Gaussian processes, and the analysis of MER based on conditional mutual information carries over to this setting. These extensions are briefly discussed in Section
5
.
We close by summarizing the results and making some comparisons between the MER in Bayesian learning and the excess risk in frequentist learning in Section
6
.

1.2
Relation to existing works

1.2.1
Accumulated excess risk for log loss

The closest connection between this work and prior literature is the MER for the logarithmic (log) loss defined in this paper and the
accumulated
excess risks for the log loss defined in Bayesian universal source coding
[
13
]
, Bayesian sequential prediction
[
14
]
, Bayesian density estimation
[
15
]
, and Bayesian supervised learning
[
16
]
, all of which turn out to be the mutual information between the model parameters and the observed data, and are achieved by the posterior predictive distribution as a soft predictor.
The only work where more general loss functions is considered is the study of sequential prediction in
[
14
]
, where an upper bound on the accumulated excess risk for bounded loss functions is derived.
Our definition of MER goes beyond the log loss to general loss functions, which can be unbounded, and the MER in general is achieved not necessarily by the posterior predictive distribution, but by some hard predictor according to the loss function.
In Section
3
we show that the MER for the log loss is nevertheless an important quantity, as it can be used to upper-bound the MER for many other loss functions.
Most of the above works, with the exception of
[
16
]
, considered only unsupervised learning, while our results hold for both supervised and unsupervised learning.
In addition, the MER defined in this work is the
instantaneous
excess risk, instead of the accumulated risk studied in above works, thus is amenable to more refined analyses.
Another closely related work that considered both supervised learning and instantaneous risk is
[
17
]
, where the Bayes risk of binary classification with the zero-one loss is derived by relating it to the accumulated log loss, and is further related to the VC dimension of the generative function class. As only realizable models are considered in
[
17
]
, the Bayes risk there is equal to the MER. In Section
3.5
, we also study the MER under realizable models, but our results go beyond binary classification and the zero-one loss.

1.2.2
Convergence of posterior distribution

A classical frequentist analysis of Bayesian inference is the convergence of the
posterior parameter distribution
to the true model parameters, assuming the data is sampled from some
fixed
model with the true parameters
[
18
,
19
,
20
]
. This analysis has recently been extended to deep neural network models
[
21
]
.
The convergence of the
posterior predictive distribution
has also been studied under the same assumption
[
22
]
.
The main difference between these works and ours is the assumption on the data distribution. In our work, the underlying data distribution considered in the performance analysis stays the same as the generative model based on which the optimal predictor, or the learning algorithm, is derived. In other words, the model parameters are assumed to be randomly drawn from the prior, and the data samples are drawn from the model given the model parameters.
In addition, rather than the convergence of the posterior of the model parameters, we are interested in the accuracy of the predicted quantity of interest.
In Section
4
we reveal how this accuracy explicitly depends on the accuracy of the model parameter estimation, by studying the expected deviation of the
posterior predictive distribution
from the random true model.

1.2.3
PAC-Bayes

Another loosely related line of work in statistical learning is the PAC-Bayes framework in the frequentist setting
[
23
,
24
,
25
]

and its extension as the Bayes mixture model
[
26
]
.
The main difference between the Bayesian setting considered here and the PAC-Bayes framework is again the underlying data distribution. For the former, the data distribution is restricted to a parametric or nonparametric family of generative models with the data samples being
conditionally
i.i.d. given the model realization, and there is virtually no restriction on candidate predictors. For the latter, the data samples are drawn
unconditionally
i.i.d. according to a completely unknown distribution, and the hypothesized Bayes-like update takes place in a
hypothesis space
consisting of admissible predictors only. The excess risk studied in this paper is thus not directly related to the generalization error or excess risk in the PAC-Bayes method.
Nevertheless, the MER-information relationship in Theorem
6
is an interesting analogue of the generalization-information relationship in the frequentist setting
[
12
, Theorem 1]
that leads to an information-theoretic derivation of the PAC-Bayes algorithm.

1.3
A note on notation

Throughout the paper, random variables are denoted by uppercase letters and their realizations are in the corresponding lowercase letters. To keep the notation uncluttered, we may use
K_{U|v}
,
P_{U|v}
and
\mathbf{E}[U|v]
respectively to denote the probability transition kernel
K_{U|V=v}
, the conditional distribution
P_{U|V=v}
and the conditional expectation
\mathbf{E}[U|V=v]
. When the conditioning variables are written in uppercase letters, these quantities are random, and expectations can be taken with respect to the conditioning variables.
Throughout the paper,
D(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes a generic statistical distance, while the KL divergence is denoted by
D_{\rm KL}(\cdot\|\cdot)
.
All probability spaces considered in this paper are Borel spaces, and all functions are measurable functions.
We use natural logarithms throughout the paper.

2
Model and definitions

2.1
Bayesian learning under parametric generative model

The basic task in supervised learning is to construct an accurate predictor of
Y
, a quantify of interest, given an observation
X
, where the knowledge of the joint distribution of
X
and
Y
is vague but can be inferred from a historical dataset
((X_{1},Y_{1}),\ldots,(X_{n},Y_{n}))
. In the
model-based learning
framework, a.k.a. learning under a
generative model
, the joint distribution of
X
and
Y
is assumed to be an element of a known model family.
The model family can be either parametric or nonparametric.
We focus on the parametric case in this work, and defer a brief discussion on the nonparametric case to Section
5.2
.
In the case of parametric modeling, the model family is a collection of parametrized distributions
{\mathcal{M}}=\{P_{X,Y|w},w\in{\mathsf{W}}\}
, where
w
represents the vector of unknown model parameters belonging to some space
{\mathsf{W}}
.
Under the
Bayesian formulation
, the vector of model parameters
W
is itself treated as a random quantity with a prior distribution
P_{W}
, while the data samples are conditionally i.i.d. given
W
. Formally, the model parameters
W
, the data samples
Z^{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n})
with
Z_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(X_{i},Y_{i})
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, and the pair
Z=(X,Y)
consisting of the fresh observation
X
and the quantity
Y
to be predicted are assumed to be generated according to the joint distribution

\displaystyle P_{W,Z^{n},Z}=P_{W}\Big(\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}P_{Z_{i}|W}\Big)P_{Z|W},

(1)

where
P_{Z_{i}|W}=P_{Z|W}
for each
i
.
As an example of the above model, the
predictive modeling
framework, a.k.a. probabilistic discriminative model
[
1
]
, further assumes that
P_{Z|W}
factors as
P_{Z|W}=P_{X|W}K_{Y|X,W}
, with some probability transition kernel
K_{Y|X,W}
directly describing the true predictive distribution of the quantity of interest given the observation and model parameters. It is often further assumed that
X
is independent of
W
under the predictive modeling framework.
Note that the above models and the following definitions encompass the unsupervised learning problem as well, where one just ignores the observations
(X^{n},X)
so that
Z_{i}=Y_{i}
and
Z=Y
.

Under the generative model (
1
), the
Bayesian learning
problem can be phrased as a Bayes decision problem of predicting
Y
based on
X
and the labeled observations
Z^{n}
.
Given an action space
{\mathsf{A}}
and a loss function
\ell\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathsf{Y}}\times{\mathsf{A}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, a
decision rule

\psi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathsf{X}}\times{\mathsf{Z}}^{n}\rightarrow{\mathsf{A}}
that maps observations to an action is sought to make the expected loss
\mathbf{E}[\ell(Y,\psi(X,Z^{n}))]
small.
A decision rule that minimizes the expected loss among all decision rules is called a
Bayes decision rule
.
The corresponding minimum expected loss is defined as the
Bayes risk
in Bayesian learning:

Definition 1
.

In Bayesian learning, the Bayes risk with respect to a loss function
\ell
is defined as

\displaystyle R_{\ell}(Y|X,Z^{n})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\inf_{\psi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathsf{X}}\times{\mathsf{Z}}^{n}\rightarrow{\mathsf{A}}}\mathbf{E}[\ell(Y,\psi(X,Z^{n}))],

(2)

where the infimum is taken over all decision rules such that the above expectation is defined.

2.2
A data processing inequality for Bayes risk

To better understand the definition of
R_{\ell}(Y|X,Z^{n})
, we give a brief review of the general definition of the Bayes risk and prove a useful property of it.
Given a random element
Y
of
{\mathsf{Y}}
, the quantity

\displaystyle R_{\ell}(Y)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\inf_{a\in{\mathsf{A}}}\mathbf{E}[\ell(Y,a)]

(3)

is known as the
Bayes envelope

[
14
]
or the
generalized entropy

[
27
]
of
Y
. Given a random element
V
of some space
{\mathsf{V}}
jointly distributed with
Y
, the general definition of the
Bayes risk

\displaystyle R_{\ell}(Y|V)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\inf_{\psi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathsf{V}}\rightarrow{\mathsf{A}}}\mathbf{E}[\ell(Y,\psi(V))]

(4)

is the minimum expected loss of predicting
Y
given
V
.
It can be expressed as
the expectation of the
conditional Bayes envelope

R_{\ell}(Y|V=v)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\inf_{a\in{\mathsf{A}}}\mathbf{E}[\ell(Y,a)|V=v]

with respect to
V
, as

R_{\ell}(Y|V)=\int_{\mathsf{V}}P_{V}(\dif v)R_{\ell}(Y|V=v).

The Bayes risk
R_{\ell}(Y|V)
can thus be viewed as a
generalized conditional entropy
of
Y
given
V

[
28
,
29
]
.
The following lemma states that the Bayes risk satisfies a
data processing inequality
.

Lemma 1
.

Suppose the random variables
U
,
V
and
Y
form a Markov chain
U-V-Y
; in other words,
Y
and
U
are conditionally independent given
V
. Then, for any loss function
\ell
, the Bayes risk of predicting
Y
from
U
is at least as large as the Bayes risk of predicting
Y
from
V
, i.e.,

\displaystyle R_{\ell}(Y|U)\geq R_{\ell}(Y|V).

(5)

Proof.

Let
\psi
be a Bayes decision rule for predicting
Y
from
U
. Upon observing
V
, a random variable
U^{\prime}
can be sampled from
P_{U|V}
, conditionally independent of
(U,Y)
given
V
. Then
\psi(U^{\prime})
serves as a randomized prediction of
Y
from
V
.
As all probability spaces under consideration are Borel spaces, the sampling of
U^{\prime}
conditional on
V
can be realized by a function
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathsf{V}}\times[0,1]\rightarrow{\mathsf{U}}
of
V
and an independent random variable
T
uniformly distributed on
[0,1]
, such that
P_{f(V,T)|V}=P_{U|V}

[
30
, Lemma 3.22]
.
We have

\displaystyle R_{\ell}(Y|V)

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{t\in[0,1]}\mathbf{E}[\ell(Y,\psi(f(V,t)))]

(6)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{E}[\ell(Y,\psi(f(V,T)))]

(7)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}[\ell(Y,\psi(U^{\prime}))]

(8)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}[\ell(Y,\psi(U))]

(9)

\displaystyle=R_{\ell}(Y|U)

(10)

where (
6
) is due to the definition of
R_{\ell}(Y|V)
and the fact that
\psi(f(\cdot,t))
is a map from
{\mathsf{V}}
to
{\mathsf{Y}}
for each
t\in[0,1]
; (
7
) follows from the independence between
T
and
(V,Y)
;
(
9
) follows from the fact that
P_{U^{\prime}|V}=P_{U|V,Y}
due to the Markov chain
U-V-Y
, hence
P_{U^{\prime},V,Y}=P_{U,V,Y}
; and (
10
) follows from the definition of
\psi
.
∎

In view of the definition of
\mu
-entropy in (
14
) and (
15
) in Section
3.1
, the classic data processing inequality for mutual information stating that
I(U;Y)\leq I(V;Y)
in a Markov chain
U-V-Y

[
31
]
can be derived from Lemma
1
applied to the log loss.
More importantly, Lemma
1
extends the
value of information principle
in Bayes decision making
[
28
]
, which states that
R_{\ell}(Y)\geq R_{\ell}(Y|V)
, as it can be viewed as a special case of Lemma
1
when
U
is independent of
(V,Y)
. Lemma
1
also extends the
principle of total evidence

[
32
]
, a.k.a. the
value of knowledge theorem

[
33
]
, which states that
R_{\ell}(Y|V_{1})\geq R_{\ell}(Y|V_{1},V_{2})
for arbitrary random variables
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
jointly distributed with
Y
. While the original argument in
[
32
]
overlooked the randomness of
V_{1}
, this principle can be rigorously justified by Lemma
1
as
V_{1}-(V_{1},V_{2})-Y
always form a Markov chain.
It is also apparent from Lemma
1
or its proof that randomizing the decision rule does not help to decrease the expected loss in Bayes decision making, as
(T,V)-V-Y
form a Markov chain for any independent random variable
T
to be used in the randomized decision rule.

2.3
Definition of minimum excess risk

An immediate consequence of Lemma
1
in Bayesian learning is that the Bayes risk
R_{\ell}(Y|X,Z^{n})
decreases as the data size
n
increases, as
(X,Z^{n})-(X,Z^{n+1})-Y
form a Markov chain. A special case of this result for linear regression with quadratic loss appears in
[
34
]
. While
R_{\ell}(Y|X,Z^{n})
decreases in
n
, it will not necessarily vanish as
n\rightarrow\infty
. We define the
fundamental limit of the Bayes risk
as the minimum expected loss when the model parameters
W
are known, which is attained by some “omniscient” decision rule
\Psi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathsf{X}}\times{\mathsf{W}}\rightarrow{\mathsf{A}}
that can directly access the model parameters.

Definition 2
.

In Bayesian learning, the fundamental limit of the Bayes risk with respect to a loss function
\ell
is defined as

\displaystyle R_{\ell}(Y|X,W)=\inf_{\Psi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathsf{X}}\times{\mathsf{W}}\rightarrow{\mathsf{A}}}\mathbf{E}[\ell(Y,\Psi(X,W))].

(11)

For any feasible decision rule
\psi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathsf{X}}\times{\mathsf{Z}}^{n}\rightarrow{\mathsf{A}}
, we can define its
excess risk
as the gap between its expected loss
\mathbf{E}[\ell(Y,\psi(X,Z^{n}))]
and
R_{\ell}(Y|X,W)
.
In this work, our interest is in the gap between the Bayes risk
R_{\ell}(Y|X,Z^{n})
and its fundamental limit
R_{\ell}(Y|X,W)
, which is the minimum achievable excess risk among all feasible decision rules:

Definition 3
.

The minimum excess risk (MER) with respect to a loss function
\ell
is defined as

\displaystyle{\rm MER}_{\ell}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=R_{\ell}(Y|X,Z^{n})-R_{\ell}(Y|X,W).

(12)

The MER defined above is an algorithm-independent quantity. It quantifies the regret of the best decision rule that has access to data, but not to model parameters, relative to the best “omniscient” decision rule. It thus reflects the difficulty of the learning problem, which comes from the lack of knownedge of
W
.
This is better illustrated by decomposing the Bayes risk as

\displaystyle R_{\ell}(Y|X,Z^{n})=R_{\ell}(Y|X,W)+{\rm MER}_{\ell}.

(13)

If we view the Bayes risk as a measure of the minimum prediction uncertainty, this decomposition allows us to give formal definitions of the “aleatoric” uncertainty and the minimum “epistemic” uncertainty
[
9
]
.
The first term, the fundamental limit of the Bayes risk, can be viewed as the aleatoric part of the minimum prediction uncertainty, which exists even when the model parameters are known.
The second term, the MER, can be viewed as the epistemic part of the minimum prediction uncertainty, which is due to the lack of knowledge of
W
.
In
[
10
]
, a decomposition of uncertainty is proposed for the log loss and the quadratic loss, where the epistemic uncertainty is defined as
R_{\ell}(Y|X)-R_{\ell}(Y|X,W)
when expressed by our notation; however, this definition does not take the observed data into consideration, thus does not reflect the intuitive expectation that the epistemic uncertainty should decrease as the data size increases
[
11
]
.
On the contrary, defining the minimum epistemic uncertainty as the MER has the advantage that it becomes smaller as more data is observed, as asserted by the following result.

Theorem 1
.

For any loss function,
{\rm MER}_{\ell}
decreases in the data size
n
, and
{\rm MER}_{\ell}\geq 0
for all
n
.

Proof.

The claim that
{\rm MER}_{\ell}
decreases in
n
is due to the previously justified fact that
R_{\ell}(Y|X,Z^{n})
decreases in
n
as a consequence of Lemma
1
. The claim that
{\rm MER}_{\ell}\geq 0
is due to the Markov chain
(X,Z^{n})-(X,W)-Y
and Lemma
1
.
∎

Intuitively, we expect that
{\rm MER}_{\ell}\downarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. However, except for the log loss, there are few results in the literature regarding this convergence in the general case, or regarding how the MER depends on the estimation error of the model parameters.
In the following two sections, we use different methods to derive upper bounds on MER for general loss functions. We show that, in many cases, the MER can be upper-bounded either in terms of the conditional mutual information
I(W;Y|X,Z^{n})
, or in terms of the minimum achievable estimation error of
W
from
(X,Z^{n})
.
These results reflect how the MER depends on the joint distribution in (
1
), in particular on
P_{Z|W}
and
P_{W}
, as well as on the loss function and the data size.

3
Upper bounds via conditional mutual information

The first method for upper-bounding the MER is to relate it to the conditional mutual information
I(W;Y|X,Z^{n})
, which can be further bounded by
\frac{1}{n}I(W;Y^{n}|X^{n})
or
\frac{1}{n}I(W;Z^{n})
. In many cases, it can be shown that the mutual information
I(W;Z^{n})
is sublinear in
n

[
35
,
36
,
37
]
, which implies that the MER converges to zero as
n\to\infty
.

3.1
Logarithmic loss

We first consider the setting where one makes “soft” predictions, such that the action space is the collection of all probability densities
q
with respect to a common
\sigma
-finite positive measure
\mu
on
{\mathsf{Y}}
. The log loss
\ell(y,q)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=-\log q(y)
penalizes those densities that assign small probabilities to the outcome
y
.
Based on the definitions in (
3
) and (
4
), it can be shown that

\displaystyle R_{\log}(Y)=H_{\mu}(Y)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=-\int_{\mathsf{Y}}p_{Y}(y)\log p_{Y}(y)\mu(\dif y)

(14)

and

\displaystyle R_{\log}(Y|V)=H_{\mu}(Y|V)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=-\int_{\mathsf{V}}P_{V}(\dif v)\int_{\mathsf{Y}}p_{Y|v}(y)\log p_{Y|v}(y)\mu(\dif y),

(15)

which can be viewed as the
\mu
-entropy
of
Y
and the
conditional
\mu
-entropy
of
Y
given
V
, and the optimal actions are the unconditional density
p_{Y}
and the conditional density
p_{Y|v}
with respect to
\mu
, respectively.
For instance, if
{\mathsf{Y}}
is discrete and
\mu
is the counting measure, then
R_{\log}(Y)=H(Y)
and
R_{\log}(Y|V)=H(Y|V)
are the Shannon and the conditional Shannon entropy; while if
{\mathsf{Y}}=\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
\mu
is the Lebesgue measure, then
R_{\log}(Y)=h(Y)
and
R_{\log}(Y|V)=h(Y|V)
are the differential and the conditional differential entropy. (See
[
31
]
for further background on information theory.)
With these definitions, the MER for the log loss is the difference between two
\mu
-entropy terms:

\displaystyle{\rm MER}_{\log}

\displaystyle=H_{\mu}(Y|X,Z^{n})-H_{\mu}(Y|X,W).

(16)

A key observation is that
{\rm MER}_{\log}
can be expressed in terms of the conditional mutual information:

Lemma 2
.

For the log loss,

\displaystyle{\rm MER}_{\log}=I(W;Y|X,Z^{n}).

(17)

Proof.

The claim follows from the fact that
I(W;Y|X,Z^{n})=H_{\mu}(Y|X,Z^{n})-H_{\mu}(Y|X,W,Z^{n})
and that
H_{\mu}(Y|X,W,Z^{n})=H_{\mu}(Y|X,W)
. The second fact is due to the Markov chain
(X,W,Z^{n})-(X,W)-Y
encoded in (
1
) and the definition of the conditional
\mu
-entropy.
∎

Equation (
17

{\rm MER}_{\log}

3.2
Quadratic loss

While the log loss is naturally used for assessing “soft” predictions, it is also a common practice to make “hard” predictions, e.g., the actions can be elements in
{\mathsf{Y}}
.
When
{\mathsf{Y}}={\mathsf{A}}=\mathbb{R}
, a commonly used loss function is the quadratic loss
\ell(y,a)=(y-a)^{2}
. For any
V
that statistically depends on
Y
, the conditional Bayes envelope with respect to the quadratic loss is
R_{2}(Y|V=v)={\mathsf{var}}[Y|v]
, the optimal action is the conditional mean
\mathbf{E}[Y|v]
, and the corresponding Bayes risk

\displaystyle R_{2}(Y|V)=\mathbf{E}[{\mathsf{var}}[Y|V]]

(33)

is the minimum mean square error (MMSE) of estimating
Y
from
V
.
In this case, the MER in Bayesian learning turns out to be

\displaystyle{\rm MER}_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}\big[{\mathsf{var}}[Y|X,Z^{n}]\big]-\mathbf{E}\big[{\mathsf{var}}[Y|X,W]\big].

(34)

More generally, when
{\mathsf{Y}}={\mathsf{A}}=\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
\ell(y,a)=\|y-a\|^{2}
with
\|\cdot\|
denoting the

norm, the MER in this case is

\displaystyle{\rm MER}_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}\big[\|Y-\mathbf{E}[Y|X,Z^{n}]\|^{2}\big]-\mathbf{E}\big[\|Y-\mathbf{E}[Y|X,W]\|^{2}\big]

(35)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}\big[\|\mathbf{E}[Y|X,Z^{n}]-\mathbf{E}[Y|X,W]\|^{2}\big],

(36)

where the second equality follows from the fact that
\mathbf{E}[Y|X,W]=\mathbf{E}[Y|X,W,Z^{n}]
and the orthogonality principle in MMSE estimation
[
39
]
.

Under the assumption that
\|Y\|\leq b
, using a result that connects MMSE difference to conditional mutual information
[
40
, Theorem 10]
, we can upper-bound
{\rm MER}_{2}
in terms of
I(W;Y|X,Z^{n})

Theorem 4
.

If
{\mathsf{Y}}=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\|y\|\leq b\}
for some
b>0
, then for the quadratic loss,

\displaystyle{\rm MER}_{2}\leq{2b^{2}}I(W;Y|X,Z^{n}).

(37)

Proof.

[
40
, Theorem 10]
states that if
\|Y\|\leq b
, then for any
(U,V)
jointly distributed with
Y
,

\displaystyle R_{2}(Y|U)-R_{2}(Y|U,V)

\displaystyle\leq 2b^{2}I(V;Y|U).

(38)

Using this result and the fact that
R_{2}(Y|X,W)=R_{2}(Y|X,W,Z^{n})
, we obtain (

∎

With Theorem

, all the upper bounds on
{\rm MER}_{\log}
derived in Section

In particular, whenever (

3.3

Another loss function we consider for hard predictions is the zero-one loss

3.4
General loss functions

Now we derive a general upper bound for the MER with respect to a wide range of loss functions.
For an arbitrary loss function
\ell\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathsf{Y}}\times{\mathsf{A}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, let
\Psi^{*}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathsf{X}}\times{\mathsf{W}}\rightarrow{\mathsf{Y}}

\mathbf{E}[\ell(Y,\Psi^{*}(X,W))]=R_{\ell}(Y|X,W)

Given
(X,Z^{n})

W^{\prime}
be a sample from the posterior distribution
P_{W|X,Z^{n}}
conditionally independent of everything else given
(X,Z^{n})

\displaystyle{\rm MER}_{\ell}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{E}[\ell(Y,\Psi^{*}(X,W^{\prime}))]-\mathbf{E}[\ell(Y,\Psi^{*}(X,W))].

\Psi^{*}(X,W^{\prime})

is the mutual information between the data and the learned model parameters sampled from the posterior distribution
P_{W|Z^{n}}
.

Corollary
4
is an analogue of the generalization-information relationship in the frequentist learning
[
43
,
12
]
, where it is shown that the generalization error in frequentist learning can be upper-bounded in terms of the mutual information between the
observed data
and the
learned hypothesis
.
From (
54
), we also know that when the more general condition in Theorem
6
is satisfied, we have
{\rm MER}_{\ell}\leq\varphi^{*-1}(\frac{1}{n}I(Z^{n};W^{\prime}))
, which is analogous to upper bounds on the generalization error in
[
44
]
.

3.5
Realizable models and connection to VC dimension

In Section
3.3
we have presented the MER for the zero-one loss under the realizable model of binary classification. Here, we present a few results on the MER for general loss functions under general realizable models.
These results provide tighter asymptotic MER bounds under realizable models than directly using the general results obtained in the previous subsection.
Following the observations made in
[
17
]
, these results also show how the key quantities in classical frequentist learning theory, notably the Vapnik–Chervonenkis (VC) dimension, can be naturally brought into the MER analysis in Bayesian learning through the information-theoretic framework proposed in this work.

A realizable model is a model where the quantity of interest
Y
is determined by the observation
X
and the model parameters
W
through a
generative function

g\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathsf{X}}\times{\mathsf{W}}\rightarrow{\mathsf{Y}}
.
Under a realizable model,
g(X,W^{\prime})
can serve as a plug-in decision rule, where
W^{\prime}
is a sample from the posterior distribution
P_{W|X,Z^{n}}
, conditionally independent of everything else given
(X,Z^{n})
.
It is observed in a follow-up work
[
45
]
(which has appeared after the initial version of this paper was posted) that the generalization error bounds developed in
[
46
]
for the realizable setting of frequentist learning can be adapted to MER bounds for realizable models in Bayesian learning.
In particular,
[
45
, Lemma 3]
shows that for a loss function
\ell\in[0,b]
, if
R_{\ell}(Y|X,W)=0
, then
{\rm MER}_{\ell}\leq 3bI(W;Y|X,Z^{n})
.
Following this approach, the next result provides an upper bound for the MER under realizable models, with a better prefactor and a tighter conditional mutual information term.

Theorem 7
.

For a loss function
\ell\in[0,b]
, if there exists a function
g\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathsf{X}}\times{\mathsf{W}}\rightarrow{\mathsf{Y}}
such that
\ell(Y,g(X,W))=0
almost surely with respect to the joint distribution
P_{W,X,Y}
, then

\displaystyle{\rm MER}_{\ell}\leq\frac{b}{\log 2}I(g(W,X);Y|X,Z^{n}).

(63)

Proof.

We have the following chain of inequalities:

\displaystyle{\rm MER}_{\ell}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{E}[\ell(Y,g(X,W^{\prime}))]

(64)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}\big[\mathbf{E}[\ell(Y,g(X,W^{\prime}))|X,Z^{n}]\big]

(65)

\displaystyle\leq\int\frac{b}{\log 2}I(g(x,W);Y|X=x,Z^{n}=z^{n})P_{X,Z^{n}}({\rm d}x,{\rm d}z^{n})

(66)

\displaystyle=\frac{b}{\log 2}I(g(X,W);Y|X,Z^{n}),

(67)

where
(
64
) follows from the assumption that
\ell(Y,g(X,W))=0
, the minimum loss, which implies that
R_{\ell}(Y|X,W)=0
; (
66
) follows by applying Lemma
3
stated below to the joint distribution
P_{g(x,W),Y|x,z^{n}}
for all
(x,z^{n})
under
P_{X,Z^{n}}
.
∎

The following lemma used in the proof of Theorem
7
is adapted from
[
46
, Theorem 5.7]
, where it is developed for bounding the generalization error in the realizable setting of frequentist learning.

Lemma 3
.

Let
V
and
Y
be jointly distributed random variables on
{\mathsf{V}}\times{\mathsf{Y}}
. Let
V^{\prime}
be an independent copy of
V
, that is,
P_{V^{\prime}}=P_{V}
and
V^{\prime}
is independent of
(V,Y)
. For a function
\ell\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathsf{Y}}\times{\mathsf{V}}\rightarrow[0,b]
, if
\ell(Y,V)=0
almost surely with respect to
P_{V,Y}
, then

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[\ell(Y,V^{\prime})]\leq\frac{b}{\log 2}I(V;Y).

(68)

Proof.

We follow the symmetrization idea used in the proof of
[
46
, Theorem 5.7]
.
Let
\tilde{V}=(V_{0},V_{1})
with
V_{0}
and
V_{1}
being i.i.d. samples from
P_{V}
. Let
S
and
S^{\prime}
be i.i.d. uniform Bernoulli random variables independent of
\tilde{V}
, with
\overline{S}=1-S
, and
\overline{S^{\prime}}=1-S^{\prime}
. With these random variables at hand, we can construct
V=\tilde{V}_{S}
,
V^{\prime}=\tilde{V}_{\overline{S}}
, and
Y
to be jointly distributed with
V
and conditionally independent of everything else given
V
.
Then, following the technique used in the proof of
[
46
, Theorem 5.7]
, for any
u>0
and
t>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[\ell(Y,V^{\prime})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[\ell(Y,V^{\prime})]-\mathbf{E}[u\ell(Y,V)]

(69)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[\ell(Y,\tilde{V}_{\overline{S}})]-\mathbf{E}[u\ell(Y,\tilde{V}_{S})]

(70)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\big[\mathbf{E}[\ell(Y,\tilde{V}_{\overline{S}})-u\ell(Y,\tilde{V}_{S})|\tilde{V}]\big]

(71)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{t}\Big(I(S;Y|\tilde{V})+\mathbf{E}\Big[\log\mathbf{E}\big[\exp\big\{t\big(\ell(Y,\tilde{V}_{\overline{S^{\prime}}})-u\ell(Y,\tilde{V}_{S^{\prime}})\big)\big\}\big|\tilde{V}\big]\Big]\Big)

(72)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{t}\Big(I(S;Y|\tilde{V})+\mathbf{E}\Big[\log\mathbf{E}\big[\mathbf{E}\big[\exp\big\{t\big(\ell(Y,\tilde{V}_{\overline{S^{\prime}}})-u\ell(Y,\tilde{V}_{S^{\prime}})\big)\big\}|Y,S,\tilde{V}\big]\big|\tilde{V}\big]\Big]\Big)

(73)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{t}\Big(I(S;Y|\tilde{V})+\mathbf{E}\Big[\log\mathbf{E}\big[\tfrac{1}{2}e^{t\ell(Y,\tilde{V}_{\overline{S}})}+\tfrac{1}{2}e^{-ut\ell(Y,\tilde{V}_{\overline{S}})}\big|\tilde{V}\big]\Big]\Big)

(74)

where (
69
) is due to the assumption that
\ell(Y,V)=0
almost surely; (
72
) is due to the Donsker-Varadhan theorem, which implies that

\displaystyle D_{\rm KL}(P_{S,Y|\tilde{V}}\|P_{S^{\prime},Y|\tilde{V}})\geq\mathbf{E}\big[t\big(\ell(Y,\tilde{V}_{\overline{S}})-u\ell(Y,\tilde{V}_{S})\big)\big|\tilde{V}\big]-\log\mathbf{E}\big[\exp\big\{t\big(\ell(Y,\tilde{V}_{\overline{S^{\prime}}})-u\ell(Y,\tilde{V}_{S^{\prime}})\big)\big\}\big|\tilde{V}\big];

and (
74
) follows from the fact that
S^{\prime}
is equally likely to be
S
or
\overline{S}
conditional on
S
, writing out the inner-most expectation in this way, and by setting
\ell(Y,\tilde{V}_{S})
to
0
.

Setting
t=\frac{\log 2}{b}
and sending
u\rightarrow\infty
, we see that the inner expectation in (
74
) is upper-bounded by
1
, which leads to

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[\ell(Y,V^{\prime})]\leq\frac{b}{\log 2}I(S;Y|\tilde{V}).

(75)

The claim follows from the observation made in
[
47
]
that
I(S;Y|\tilde{V})\leq I(\tilde{V},S;Y)=I(V;Y)
,
where the equality holds because
Y
is conditionally independent of
(\tilde{V},S)
given
V=\tilde{V}_{S}
.
∎

Theorem
7
can be weakened by the data processing inequality of mutual information,

\displaystyle I(g(X,W);Y|X,Z^{n})\leq I(W;Y|X,Z^{n}),

(76)

which can be further bounded by
\frac{1}{n}I(W;Y^{n}|X^{n})
or
\frac{1}{n}I(W;Z^{n})
due to Theorem
2
or Corollary
1
. When
{\mathsf{Y}}
is discrete,
I(W;Y^{n}|X^{n})
can be further bounded by
H(Y^{n}|X^{n})
.
It implies that under a realizable model with discrete
{\mathsf{Y}}
, not necessarily binary, the MER with respect to a bounded loss function can be upper-bounded nonasymptotically on the order of
H(Y^{n}|X^{n})/n
.

With a realizable model, a natural question to ask is how the MER depends on the richness of the
generative function class

\mathcal{G}=\{g(\cdot,w)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathsf{X}}\rightarrow{\mathsf{Y}},w\in{\mathsf{W}}\}
. When
{\mathsf{Y}}=\{0,1\}
, one featuring combinatorial quantity that measures the richness of
\mathcal{G}
is its VC dimension, defined in (
42
).
The connection between
H(Y^{n}|X^{n})
and
V(\mathcal{G})
is observed in
[
17
]
, in the setting of binary classification with the zero-one loss. We make use of it here to obtain a corollary of Theorem
7
, which extends the results in Theorem
5
as it applies to more general loss functions.

Corollary 5
.

Under a realizable model with
{\mathsf{Y}}=\{0,1\}
, if the function class
\mathcal{G}=\{g(\cdot,w)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathsf{X}}\rightarrow{\mathsf{Y}},w\in{\mathsf{W}}\}
has VC dimension
d
, then for any loss function
\ell\in[0,b]
,

\displaystyle{\rm MER}_{\ell}\leq O\Big(\frac{b}{\log 2}\cdot\frac{d}{n}\Big)\quad\text{as $n\rightarrow\infty$}.

(77)

Proof.

By (
76
) and Theorem
2
, and the assumptions that the model is realizable and
{\mathsf{Y}}
is discrete, the upper bound in Theorem
7
can be weakened to

\displaystyle{\rm MER}_{\ell}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{b}{\log 2}H(Y|X,Z^{n})\leq\frac{b}{n\log 2}H(Y^{n}|X^{n}).

(78)

The Sauer-Shelah lemma (
43
) implies that
H(Y^{n}|X^{n})\leq d\log n+1
. In addition, from the chain rule of Shannon entropy and Lemma
A.2
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}(n+1)H(Y|X,Z^{n})=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{H(Y^{n}|X^{n})}{\log n}\leq d,

(79)

similar to the proof of (
45
) in Theorem
5
. This proves the claim in view of (
78
).
∎

The VC dimension plays a key role in the frequentist learning theory, in bounding the excess risk in terms of the richness of the
hypothesis class
, which amounts to the set of decision rules. In Bayesian learning, while there is no restriction on the decision rules, Corollary
5
shows that the VC dimension of the generative function class plays a similar role in upper-bounding the MER.

4
Upper bounds via functional and distributional continuities

In the previous section, the upper bounds are derived by relating the MER to
I(W;Y|X,Z^{n})
.
In this section, we explore alternative methods for bounding the MER, either in terms of the smoothness of the optimal omniscient decision rule in model parameters, or in terms of the smoothness of the minimum expected loss in the predictive model. These smoothness, or continuity properties enable us to bound the MER via the accuracy of estimated parameters from the data. The following lemma is instrumental for this approach.

Lemma 4

(
[
48
]
,
[
49
]
)
.

Let
({\mathsf{U}},\rho)
be a metric space. If
U
and
U^{\prime}
are two random elements of
{\mathsf{U}}
that are conditionally i.i.d. given another random element
V
of some space
{\mathsf{V}}
, i.e.,
P_{U,U^{\prime}|V=v}=P_{U|V=v}P_{U^{\prime}|V=v}
and
P_{U|V=v}=P_{U^{\prime}|V=v}
for all
v\in{\mathsf{V}}
, then

\mathbf{E}[\rho(U^{\prime},U)]\leq 2R_{\rho}(U|V).

Moreover, if
{\mathsf{U}}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then

\mathbf{E}[\|U^{\prime}-U\|^{2}]=2R_{2}(U|V).

As an aside, Lemma
4
provides us with a means for evaluating the performance of making randomized prediction by sampling from the posterior predictive distribution
P_{Y|X,Z^{n}}
, via upper-bounding the corresponding MER.
Let
Y^{\prime}
be sampled from
P_{Y|X,Z^{n}}
, which can be realized by first sampling
W^{\prime}
from
P_{W|X,Z^{n}}
then
Y^{\prime}
from
P_{Y|X,W^{\prime}}
. Then, for any metric
\ell
on
{\mathsf{Y}}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[\ell(Y,Y^{\prime})]\leq 2R_{\ell}(Y|X,W)+2{\rm MER}_{\ell}.

(80)

4.1
Via continuity of optimal omniscient decision rule

4.1.1
General upper bound

We start from (
52
) which states that
{\rm MER}_{\ell}\leq\mathbf{E}[\ell(Y,\Psi^{*}(X,W^{\prime}))]-\mathbf{E}[\ell(Y,\Psi^{*}(X,W))]
, where
\Psi^{*}
is the optimal omniscient decision that achieves
R_{\ell}(Y|X,W)
when
W
is known, and
W^{\prime}
is a sample from the posterior distribution
P_{W|X,Z^{n}}
conditionally independent of everything else given
(X,Z^{n})
.
The MER can be upper-bounded in terms of the smoothness of the function
\ell(y,\Psi^{*}(x,w))
in
w
and the accuracy of approximating
W
by
W^{\prime}
.

Theorem 8
.

If
{\mathsf{W}}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
W
is independent of
X
, then

\displaystyle{\rm MER}_{\ell}\leq\mathbf{E}\Big[\sup_{y\in{\mathsf{Y}}}\sup_{w\in{\mathsf{W}}}\|\nabla_{w}\ell(y,\Psi^{*}(X,w))\|\Big]\sqrt{2R_{2}(W|Z^{n})},

(81)

where
\Psi^{*}
is the optimal omniscient decision rule for the loss function
\ell
.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle{\rm MER}_{\ell}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{E}[\ell(Y,\Psi^{*}(X,W^{\prime}))-\ell(Y,\Psi^{*}(X,W))]

(82)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{E}\Big[\sup_{w\in{\mathsf{W}}}\|\nabla_{w}\ell(Y,\Psi^{*}(X,w))\|\cdot\|W^{\prime}-W\|\Big]

(83)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{E}\Big[\sup_{y\in{\mathsf{Y}}}\sup_{w\in{\mathsf{W}}}\|\nabla_{w}\ell(y,\Psi^{*}(X,w))\|\Big]\mathbf{E}[\|W^{\prime}-W\|]

(84)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{E}\Big[\sup_{y\in{\mathsf{Y}}}\sup_{w\in{\mathsf{W}}}\|\nabla_{w}\ell(y,\Psi^{*}(X,w))\|\Big]\sqrt{2R_{2}(W|Z^{n})},

(85)

where we used (
52
), Lemma
A.4
, the assumption that
X
and
W
are independent, and the fact that
\mathbf{E}[\|W^{\prime}-W\|]\leq\sqrt{E[\|W^{\prime}-W\|^{2}]}=\sqrt{2R_{2}(W|Z^{n})}
due to Lemma
4
.
∎

Example: constant
\Psi^{*}

An extreme case where Theorem
8
can be useful is when
\Psi^{*}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathsf{X}}\times{\mathsf{W}}\rightarrow{\mathsf{Y}}
does not dependent on
W
under certain loss functions, in which case Theorem
8
guarantees that the MER is zero. For example, if
Y_{i}=g(X_{i},W)V_{i}
and
Y=g(X,W)V
, with some
g\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathsf{X}}\times{\mathsf{W}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
(V^{n},V)
being i.i.d. zero-mean random variables independent of
(W,X^{n},X)
, then for the quadratic loss,

\displaystyle\Psi^{*}(X,W)=\mathbf{E}[g(X,W)V|X,W]=g(X,W)\mathbf{E}[V]\equiv 0,

(86)

hence
{\rm MER}_{2}=0
by Theorem
8
.
It implies that for this example

\displaystyle R_{2}(Y|X,Z^{n})=R_{2}(Y|X,W)=\mathbf{E}\big[g(X,W)^{2}V^{2}\big]=\mathbf{E}\big[g(X,W)^{2}\big]{\mathsf{var}}[V],

(87)

which shows that a small MER does not necessarily mean a small Bayes risk
R_{\ell}(Y|X,Z^{n})
.

Example: logistic regression

Another example where Theorem
8
can be applied to is bounding the MER for logistic regression with the log loss.
Bayesian logistic regression is an instance under the predictive modeling framework, where
{\mathsf{Y}}=\{0,1\}
,
W\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is assumed to be independent of
X
, and the predictive model is specified by

K_{Y|x,w}(1)=\sigma(w^{\top}\phi(x))
, with
\sigma(a)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=1/(1+e^{-a})
,
a\in\mathbb{R}
, being the logistic sigmoid function, and
\phi(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
being the feature vector of the observation.
For the log loss, the optimal omniscient decision rule
\Psi^{*}(x,w)
is the Bernoulli distribution with bias
\sigma(w^{\top}\phi(x))
.
Since
|\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}a}\log\sigma(a)|=|1-\sigma(a)|\leq 1
, from Theorem
8
we have

\displaystyle{\rm MER}_{\log}\leq\mathbf{E}[\|\phi(X)\|]\sqrt{2R_{2}(W|Z^{n})}.

(88)

This result explicitly shows that the MER in logistic regression depends on how well we can estimate the model parameters from data, as it is dominated by
R_{2}(W|Z^{n})
, the MMSE of estimating
W
from
Z^{n}
.
For logistic regression, a closed-form expression for this MMSE may not exist.
Nevertheless, any upper bound on it that is nonasymptotic in
d
and
n
will translate to a nonasymptotic upper bound on the MER.
In Section
4.3.3
we continue the discussion of this example with different upper-bounding methods, where the dependence on
R_{2}(W|Z^{n})
can be improved when it is small.

4.1.2
Realizable models with additive noise

The smoothness of
\Psi^{*}(x,w)
in
w
can lead to potentially tighter MER bounds under realizable models, possibly with additive noise.
Consider the generative model of the form
Y_{i}=g(X_{i},W)+V_{i}
and
Y=g(X,W)+V
, where the generative function
g\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathsf{X}}\times{\mathsf{W}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is some parametric nonlinearity in general, which could be approximated by a feedforward neural network, the parameter vector
W\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is independent of
(X^{n},X)
, and the additive noise
(V^{n},V)
are i.i.d. real-valued random variables independent of
(W,X^{n},X)
.
This model encompasses both linear and nonlinear Bayesian regression problems. We have the following MER bounds for the quadratic loss under this model.

Theorem 9
.

Under the model considered above, for the quadratic loss,

\displaystyle{\rm MER}_{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2R_{2}(g(X,W)|X,Z^{n})\leq 2\mathbf{E}\Big[\sup_{w\in{\mathsf{W}}}\|\nabla_{w}g(X,w)\|^{2}\Big]R_{2}(W|Z^{n}).

(89)

Proof.

Under the model considered above, for the quadratic loss,

\displaystyle\Psi^{*}(X,W)=\mathbf{E}[g(X,W)+V|X,W]=g(X,W)+\mathbf{E}[V].

(90)

We have

\displaystyle{\rm MER}_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{E}\big[(Y-g(X,W^{\prime})-\mathbf{E}[V])^{2}\big]-\mathbf{E}\big[(Y-g(X,W)-\mathbf{E}[V])^{2}\big]

(91)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}\big[(g(X,W^{\prime})-g(X,W))^{2}\big]+{\mathsf{var}}[V]-{\mathsf{var}}[V]

(92)

\displaystyle=2R_{2}(g(X,W)|X,Z^{n})

(93)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{E}\Big[\sup_{w\in{\mathsf{W}}}\|\nabla_{w}g(X,w)\|^{2}\cdot\|W^{\prime}-W\|^{2}\Big]

(94)

\displaystyle=2\mathbf{E}\Big[\sup_{w\in{\mathsf{W}}}\|\nabla_{w}g(X,w)\|^{2}\Big]R_{2}(W|Z^{n}),

(95)

where (
91
) follows from (
52
); (
92
) follows from the independence between
(X,W)
and
V
;
(
93
) follows from the fact that
g(X,W^{\prime})
and
g(X,W)
are conditionally independent given
(X,Z^{n})
, and Lemma
4
; (
94
) follows from (
92
) and Lemma
A.4
; and (
95
) follows from the independence between
X
and
W
, and Lemma
4
.
∎

Example: linear regression

Theorem
9
can be applied to bounding the MER of the linear regression problem with the quadratic loss.
Bayesian linear regression is an instance of the noisy realizable model considered above, where
g(x,w)=w^{\top}\phi(x)
,
\phi(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the feature vector of the observation
x
, and
(V^{n},V)
are i.i.d. samples from
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
.
With the Gaussian prior of model parameters
P_{W}=\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{W}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{d})
, the MMSE for estimating
W
from
Z^{n}
has a closed-form expression

\displaystyle R_{2}(W|Z^{n})=\mathbf{E}[{\rm tr}(C_{W|Z^{n}})],

(96)

where

\displaystyle C_{W|Z^{n}}=\Big(\frac{1}{\sigma_{W}^{2}}\mathbf{I}_{d}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\mathbf{\Phi}\mathbf{\Phi}^{\top}\Big)^{-1}

(97)

is the conditional covariance matrix of
W
given
Z^{n}
, which only depends on
X^{n}
through the
d\times n
feature matrix
\mathbf{\Phi}=[\phi(X_{1}),\ldots,\phi(X_{n})]
. Under this model, we also have
\nabla_{w}g(x,w)=\phi(x)
, hence Theorem
9
implies that

\displaystyle{\rm MER}_{2}\leq 2\mathbf{E}[\|\phi(X)\|^{2}]\mathbf{E}[{\rm tr}(C_{W|Z^{n}})].

(98)

Under the above model with Gaussian prior, it can be shown that the posterior predictive distribution
P_{Y|x,z^{n}}
is Gaussian with variance
\sigma^{2}+\phi(x)^{\top}C_{W|z^{n}}\phi(x)
. From this we can obtain exact expressions for the MER and alternative upper bounds:

\displaystyle{\rm MER}_{\log}=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{E}\Big[\log\Big(1+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\phi(X)^{\top}C_{W|Z^{n}}\phi(X)\Big)\Big]\leq\frac{\mathbf{E}[\|\phi(X)\|^{2}]}{2\sigma^{2}}\mathbf{E}[{\rm tr}(C_{W|Z^{n}})],

(99)

and

\displaystyle{\rm MER}_{2}=\mathbf{E}\big[\phi(X)^{\top}C_{W|Z^{n}}\phi(X)\big]\leq\mathbf{E}[\|\phi(X)\|^{2}]\mathbf{E}[{\rm tr}(C_{W|Z^{n}})].

(100)

The upper bounds in (
99
) and (
100
) are justified by noting that

\displaystyle\phi(X)^{\top}C_{W|Z^{n}}\phi(X)=\|C_{W|Z^{n}}^{1/2}\phi(X)\|^{2}\leq\sigma_{1}^{2}\big(C_{W|Z^{n}}^{1/2}\big)\|\phi(X)\|^{2}\leq{\rm tr}(C_{W|Z^{n}})\|\phi(X)\|^{2},

(101)

where
\sigma_{1}(\cdot)
is the largest singular value of the underlying matrix.
A special choice of the
d
feature functions composing the feature vector is such that they are orthonormal with respect to
P_{X}
, namely
\int_{\mathsf{X}}\phi_{i}(x)\phi_{j}(x)P_{X}({\rm d}x)=\mathbf{1}\{i=j\}
for
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
. In this case,
\Phi\Phi^{\top}\approx n\mathbf{E}[\phi(X)\phi(X)^{\top}]=n\mathbf{I}_{d}
, hence
\mathbf{E}[{\rm tr}(C_{W|Z^{n}})]\sim O(d/n)
, implying that
{\rm MER}_{2}
scale with
d
and
n
as
O(d/n)
according to (
100
).
It further implies that the upper bound (
98
) given by Theorem
9
is order-optimal for vanishing MER.
We continue the discussion of this example in Section
4.3.3
.

4.2
Deviation of posterior predictive distribution from true predictive model

Under the predictive modeling framework, the generative model is specified as
P_{Z|W}=P_{X|W}K_{Y|X,W}
, with a parametrized probability transition kernel
K_{Y|X,W}
describing the true predictive model of
Y
given
X
. An alternative method for upper-bounding the MER under this framework is by examining the deviation of the posterior predictive distribution
P_{Y|X,Z^{n}}
from the true predictive model
K_{Y|X,W}
in terms of a suitable
convex statistical distance
between them.
Here, by a convex statistical distance we mean any statistical distance
(P,Q)\mapsto D(P,Q)
that is convex in the first argument when the second one is held fixed, or convex in the second argument while the first one is held fixed.
For example, any
f
-divergence, including the commonly used total variation distance, KL divergence and
\chi^{2}
-divergence, is jointly convex in both arguments
[
50
]
.
As another example, consider the
p
th power of
p
-Wasserstein distance between two Borel probability measures
P
and
Q
on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
with finite second moments
[
51
]
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{p}^{p}(P,Q)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(P,Q)}\mathbf{E}_{(X,Y)\sim\pi}[\|X-Y\|^{p}],

(102)

where
\Pi(P,Q)
denotes the collection of all couplings of
P
and
Q
, i.e., Borel probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
with marginals
P
and
Q
. As shown in Lemma
A.3
in Appendix
A.4
,
(P,Q)\mapsto\mathcal{W}_{p}^{p}(P,Q)
is also jointly convex.
The following lemma is key for relating the deviation of
P_{Y|X,Z^{n}}
from
K_{Y|X,W}
to the estimation error of model parameters.

Lemma 5
.

Let
W^{\prime}
be a sample from the posterior distribution
P_{W|X,Z^{n}}
, such that
W
and
W^{\prime}
are conditionally i.i.d. given
(X,Z^{n})
.
Then for any
(w,x,z^{n})
and any statistical distance
D
that is convex in the first argument,

\displaystyle D(P_{Y|x,z^{n}},K_{Y|x,w})\leq\mathbf{E}[D(K_{Y|x,W^{\prime}},K_{Y|x,w})|x,z^{n}],

(103)

and consequently,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[D(P_{Y|X,Z^{n}},K_{Y|X,W})]\leq\mathbf{E}[D(K_{Y|X,W^{\prime}},K_{Y|X,W})]

(104)

where the expectations are taken with respect to the joint distribution of
(W,X,Z^{n},W^{\prime})
.
Similarly, for any
(w,x,z^{n})
and any statistical distance
D
that is convex in the second argument,

\displaystyle D(K_{Y|x,w},P_{Y|x,z^{n}})\leq\mathbf{E}[D(K_{Y|x,w},K_{Y|x,W^{\prime}})|x,z^{n}],

(105)

and consequently,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[D(K_{Y|X,W},P_{Y|X,Z^{n}})]\leq\mathbf{E}[D(K_{Y|X,W},K_{Y|X,W^{\prime}})].

(106)

Proof.

From the joint distribution in (
1
), it follows that for any
(w,z^{n},z)
,

\displaystyle P_{Y|x,z^{n}}(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathsf{W}}}K_{Y|x,w^{\prime}}(y)P_{W|x,z^{n}}({\rm d}w^{\prime})

(107)

If the statistical distance considered here is convex in the first argument, we have

\displaystyle D(P_{Y|x,z^{n}},K_{Y|x,w})\leq\int_{{\mathsf{W}}}D(K_{Y|x,w^{\prime}},K_{Y|x,w})P_{W|x,z^{n}}({\rm d}w^{\prime}),

(108)

which proves (
103
).
Taking expectations over the conditioning terms, we obtain (
104
).
The proof of (
105
) and (
106
) follows the same argument when
D
is convex in the second argument.
∎

Whenever the convex statistical distance
D(K_{Y|x,w^{\prime}},K_{Y|x,w})
can be upper-bounded via
\|w^{\prime}-w\|
or
\|w^{\prime}-w\|^{2}
, Lemma
4
can be used to further upper-bound
\mathbf{E}[D(K_{Y|X,W^{\prime}},K_{Y|X,W})]
in terms of the minimum achievable estimation error of
W
.
In the following two subsections, we use two different methods together with Lemma
5
and Lemma
4
to convert upper bounds on the deviation of
P_{Y|X,Z^{n}}
from
K_{Y|X,W}
into upper bounds on the MER for various loss functions.

4.3
From deviation of posterior predictive distribution to MER bound

4.3.1
Via conditional mutual information upper bound

For the log loss, we can directly upper-bound
I(W;Y|X,Z^{n})
in terms of the KL divergence between
K_{Y|X,W}
and
P_{Y|X,Z^{n}}
, and arrive at the following result with Lemma
5
.

Theorem 10
.

When
P_{Z|W}=P_{X|W}K_{Y|X,W}
, let
W^{\prime}
be a sample from the posterior distribution
P_{W|X,Z^{n}}
, conditionally independent of everything else given
(X,Z^{n})
. Then,

\displaystyle{\rm MER}_{\log}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{E}[D_{\rm KL}(K_{Y|X,W}\|K_{Y|X,W^{\prime}})]

(109)

where the expectation is taken with respect to the joint distribution of
(W,W^{\prime},X)
.

Proof.

From (
17
), we have

\displaystyle{\rm MER}_{\log}

\displaystyle=I(W;Y|X,Z^{n})

(110)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}[D_{\rm KL}(P_{Y|X,Z^{n},W}\|P_{Y|X,Z^{n}})]

(111)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}[D_{\rm KL}(P_{Y|X,W}\|P_{Y|X,Z^{n}})]

(112)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{E}[D_{\rm KL}(K_{Y|X,W}\|K_{Y|X,W^{\prime}})],

(113)

where (
112
) follows from the fact that
Y
is conditionally independent of
Z^{n}
given
(X,W)
; and (
113
) is from Lemma
5
and the fact that
D_{\rm KL}(P\|Q)
is convex in
Q
for a fixed
P
. ∎

In Section
4.3.3
we continue with the example of logistic regression, where Theorem
10
can be used with Lemma
4
to bound the MER in terms of the MMSE of estimating
W
from data.

4.3.2
Via continuity of generalized entropy

The second method for relating the MER to the deviation of posterior predictive distribution is directly comparing
R_{\ell}(Y|X,Z^{n})
against
R_{\ell}(Y|X,W)
, via the distributional continuity of the generalized entropy.
We examine classification and regression problems separately.

Classification

For classification problems where
{\mathsf{Y}}
is finite, we consider both the soft classification with the log loss and the hard classification with the zero-one loss. The MER upper bounds rely on the continuity properties of the Shannon entropy and the maximal probability, respectively, as stated in the following lemma, with proofs provided in Appendix
A.6
.
For more general discussions on the continuity of generalized entropy, the reader may refer to
[
52
,
53
]
.

Lemma 6
.

Let
P
and
Q
be distributions on a finite set
{\mathsf{Y}}
such that
\min_{y\in{\mathsf{Y}}}Q(y)>0
. Then

\displaystyle H(P)-H(Q)\leq\big(-\log{\min_{y\in{\mathsf{Y}}}Q(y)}\big)d_{\rm TV}(P,Q),

(114)

\displaystyle\max_{y\in{\mathsf{Y}}}Q(y)-\max_{y\in{\mathsf{Y}}}P(y)\leq d_{\rm TV}(P,Q),

(115)

where
d_{\rm TV}(P,Q)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{y\in{\mathsf{Y}}}|P(y)-Q(y)|
is the total variation distance between
P
and
Q
.

Compared with the well-known Shannon entropy difference bound in terms of the total variation distance
|H(P)-H(Q)|\leq 2d_{\rm TV}(P,Q)\log({|{\mathsf{Y}}|}/2d_{\rm TV}(P,Q))
when
d_{\rm TV}(P,Q)\leq{1}/{4}

[
31
, Theorem 17.3.3]
, the bound given in (
114
) is not as tight in
|{\mathsf{Y}}|
, but is tighter in
d_{\rm TV}(P,Q)
, which leads to sharper MER bounds when the data size is large.
Armed with Lemma
6
and Lemma
5
, we have the following MER bounds for classification problems.

Theorem 11
.

If
{\mathsf{Y}}
is finite, then for the log loss,

\displaystyle{\rm MER}_{\log}\leq\sup_{x\in{\mathsf{X}},\,w\in{\mathsf{W}}}(-\log\kappa(x,w))\mathbf{E}[d_{\rm TV}(K_{Y|X,W^{\prime}},K_{Y|X,W})],

(116)

where
\kappa(x,w)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\min_{y\in{\mathsf{Y}}}K_{Y|x,w}(y)
,
W^{\prime}
is a sample from
P_{W|X,Z^{n}}
, conditionally independent of everything else given
(X,Z^{n})
, and the expectation is with respect to
P_{W,W^{\prime},X}
.
In addition, for the zero-one loss,

\displaystyle{\rm MER}_{01}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{E}[d_{\rm TV}(K_{Y|X,W^{\prime}},K_{Y|X,W})].

(117)

Proof.

When
{\mathsf{Y}}
is finite, for the log loss,

\displaystyle{\rm MER}_{\log}

\displaystyle=H(Y|X,Z^{n})-H(Y|X,W)

(118)

\displaystyle=\int\big(H(Y|x,z^{n})-H(Y|x,w)\big)P({\rm d}w,{\rm d}x,{\rm d}z^{n})

(119)

\displaystyle\leq\int\Big(-\log{\min_{y\in{\mathsf{Y}}}K_{Y|x,w}(y)}\Big)d_{\rm TV}(P_{Y|x,z^{n}},P_{Y|x,w})P({\rm d}w,{\rm d}x,{\rm d}z^{n})

(120)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{w\in{\mathsf{W}},x\in{\mathsf{X}}}\big(-\log{\min\nolimits_{y\in{\mathsf{Y}}}K_{Y|x,w}(y)}\big)\mathbf{E}\big[d_{\rm TV}(P_{Y|X,Z^{n}},P_{Y|X,W})\big]

(121)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{w\in{\mathsf{W}},x\in{\mathsf{X}}}\big(-\log{\min\nolimits_{y\in{\mathsf{Y}}}K_{Y|x,w}(y)}\big)\mathbf{E}\big[d_{\rm TV}(P_{Y|X,W^{\prime}},P_{Y|X,W})\big]

(122)

where (
120
) follows from Lemma
6
; and (
122
) follows from Lemma
5
.

For the zero-one loss,

\displaystyle{\rm MER}_{01}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}[\max\nolimits_{y\in{\mathsf{Y}}}K_{Y|X,W}(y)]-\mathbf{E}[\max\nolimits_{y\in{\mathsf{Y}}}P_{Y|X,Z^{n}}(y)]

(123)

\displaystyle=\int\big(\max\nolimits_{y\in{\mathsf{Y}}}K_{Y|x,w}(y)-\max\nolimits_{y\in{\mathsf{Y}}}P_{Y|x,z^{n}}(y)\big)P({\rm d}w,{\rm d}x,{\rm d}z^{n})

(124)

\displaystyle\leq\int d_{\rm TV}(K_{Y|x,w},P_{Y|x,z^{n}})P({\rm d}w,{\rm d}x,{\rm d}z^{n})

(125)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{E}[d_{\rm TV}(K_{Y|X,W},K_{Y|X,W^{\prime}})]

(126)

where (
125
) follows from Lemma
6
, and (
126
) follows from Lemma
5
.
∎

Regression

Next, we consider regression problems with
{\mathsf{Y}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
under the assumption that both the marginal and various conditional distributions of
Y
are absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure. We consider both the soft prediction with the log loss, and the hard prediction with the quadratic loss.
For the soft setting,
{\rm MER}_{\log}
can be upper-bounded using the continuity of differential entropy with respect to the Wasserstein distance, as stated in the following lemma.

Lemma 7

(
[
54
]
)
.

Let
U
be a random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
with finite
\mathbf{E}[\|U\|^{2}]
, and
V
be a Gaussian random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
with covariance matrix
\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{p}
.
Then

\displaystyle h(U)-h(V)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\big(3\sqrt{\mathbf{E}[\|U\|^{2}]}+11\sqrt{\mathbf{E}[\|V\|^{2}]}\big)\mathcal{W}_{2}(P_{U},P_{V})

(127)

where
\mathcal{W}_{2}(P_{U},P_{V})
is the
2
-Wasserstein distance between
P_{U}
and
P_{V}
.

With Lemma
7
and Lemma
5
, we have the following bound for regression with the log loss.

Theorem 12
.

If
{\mathsf{Y}}=\mathbb{R}^{p}
,
and
K_{Y|x,w}
is Gaussian with covariance matrix
\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{p}
for all
(x,w)
, then for the log loss,

\displaystyle{\rm MER}_{\log}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{7}{\sigma^{2}}\sqrt{\mathbf{E}\big[\|Y\|^{2}\big]\mathbf{E}[\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(K_{Y|X,W^{\prime}},K_{Y|X,W})]},

(128)

where
W
and
W^{\prime}
are conditionally i.i.d. given
(X,Z^{n})
, and the expectation is with respect to
P_{X,W,W^{\prime}}
.

Proof.

For the log loss,

\displaystyle{\rm MER}_{\log}

\displaystyle=h(Y|X,Z^{n})-h(Y|X,W)

(129)

\displaystyle=\int\big(h(Y|x,z^{n})-h(Y|x,w)\big)P({\rm d}w,{\rm d}x,{\rm d}z^{n})

(130)

\displaystyle\leq\int\Big(\frac{3}{2\sigma^{2}}\sqrt{\mathbf{E}\big[\|Y\|^{2}|x,z^{n}\big]}+\frac{11}{2\sigma^{2}}\sqrt{\mathbf{E}\big[\|Y\|^{2}|x,w\big]}\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\mathcal{W}_{2}(P_{Y|x,z^{n}},K_{Y|x,w})P({\rm d}w,{\rm d}x,{\rm d}z^{n})

(131)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int\Big(\frac{3}{2\sigma^{2}}\sqrt{\mathbf{E}\big[\|Y\|^{2}|x,z^{n}\big]}+\frac{11}{2\sigma^{2}}\sqrt{\mathbf{E}\big[\|Y\|^{2}|x,w\big]}\Big)^{2}P({\rm d}w,{\rm d}x,{\rm d}z^{n})\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\quad\,\left(\int\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(P_{Y|x,z^{n}},K_{Y|x,w})P({\rm d}w,{\rm d}x,{\rm d}z^{n})\right)^{1/2}

(132)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{7}{\sigma^{2}}\big(\mathbf{E}\big[\|Y\|^{2}\big]\big)^{1/2}\left(\int\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(P_{Y|x,z^{n}},K_{Y|x,w})P({\rm d}w,{\rm d}x,{\rm d}z^{n})\right)^{1/2}

(133)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{7}{\sigma^{2}}\sqrt{\mathbf{E}\big[\|Y\|^{2}\big]\mathbf{E}[\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(K_{Y|X,W^{\prime}},K_{Y|X,W})]\big)}.

(134)

where (
131
) follows from Lemma
7
; (
132
) follows from Cauchy-Schwarz inequality; (
133
) follows from the triangle inequality of the
L_{2}
norm, which states that
\sqrt{\mathbf{E}[(U+V)^{2}]}\leq\sqrt{\mathbf{E}[U^{2}]}+\sqrt{\mathbf{E}[V^{2}]}
;
and (
134
) follows from Lemma
5
and Lemma
A.3
.
∎

For the hard setting, in scalar regression problems with
{\mathsf{Y}}={\mathsf{A}}=\mathbb{R}
and the quadratic loss, the MER as given by (
34
) is the expected difference between two variances.
The following results relate the variance difference between two probability distributions to their
2
-Wasserstein distance and KL divergence respectively.

Lemma 8

(
[
55
]
, proof given in Appendix
A.7
)
.

Let
U
and
V
be random variables over a set
{\mathsf{U}}\subset\mathbb{R}
with finite
\mathbf{E}[U^{2}]
and
\mathbf{E}[V^{2}]
. Then,

\displaystyle|{\mathsf{var}}[U]-{\mathsf{var}}[V]|\leq 2\big(\sqrt{\mathbf{E}[U^{2}]}+\sqrt{\mathbf{E}[V^{2}]}\big)\mathcal{W}_{2}(P_{U},P_{V}).

(135)

When
V
is Gaussian with variance
\sigma^{2}
, Lemma
8
with Talagrand’s inequality
[
56
]
states that

\displaystyle|{\mathsf{var}}[U]-{\mathsf{var}}[V]|\leq 2\big(\sqrt{\mathbf{E}[U^{2}]}+\sqrt{\mathbf{E}[V^{2}]}\big)\sqrt{2\sigma^{2}D_{\rm KL}(P_{U}\|P_{V})};

(136)

under the same condition, we also have a potentially tighter bound
[
53
]
:

\displaystyle|{\mathsf{var}}[U]-{\mathsf{var}}[V]|\leq 2\sigma^{2}\big(\sqrt{D_{\rm KL}(P_{U}\|P_{V})}+D_{\rm KL}(P_{U}\|P_{V})\big).

(137)

With Lemma
8
, we can derive the following upper bounds for
{\rm MER}_{2}
.

Theorem 13
.

For regression problems with
{\mathsf{Y}}=\mathbb{R}
, if
\mathbf{E}[Y^{2}|x,w]
is finite for all
(x,w)
, then for the quadratic loss,

\displaystyle{\rm MER}_{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4\sqrt{\mathbf{E}[Y^{2}]\mathbf{E}[\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(K_{Y|X,W^{\prime}},K_{Y|X,W})]},

(138)

where
W^{\prime}
is a sample from
P_{W|X,Z^{n}}
, conditionally independent of everything given
(X,Z^{n})
.

Proof.

Similar to the proof of Theorem
12
, for the quadratic loss, we have

\displaystyle{\rm MER}_{2}

\displaystyle=\int\big({\mathsf{var}}[Y|x,z^{n}]-{\mathsf{var}}[Y|x,w]\big)P({\rm d}w,{\rm d}x,{\rm d}z^{n})

(139)

\displaystyle\leq 2\int\big(\sqrt{\mathbf{E}[Y^{2}|x,z^{n}]}+\sqrt{\mathbf{E}[Y^{2}|x,w]}\big)\mathcal{W}_{2}(P_{Y|x,z^{n}},K_{Y|x,w})P({\rm d}w,{\rm d}x,{\rm d}z^{n})

(140)

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(\int\big(\sqrt{\mathbf{E}[Y^{2}|x,z^{n}]}+\sqrt{\mathbf{E}[Y^{2}|x,w]}\big)^{2}P({\rm d}w,{\rm d}x,{\rm d}z^{n})\right)^{1/2}\cdot

\displaystyle\qquad\!\left(\int\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(P_{Y|x,z^{n}},K_{Y|x,w})P({\rm d}w,{\rm d}x,{\rm d}z^{n})\right)^{1/2}

(141)

\displaystyle\leq 4\sqrt{\mathbf{E}[Y^{2}]\mathbf{E}[\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(P_{Y|X,Z^{n}},K_{Y|X,W})]}

(142)

\displaystyle\leq 4\sqrt{\mathbf{E}[Y^{2}]\mathbf{E}[\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(P_{Y|X,W^{\prime}},K_{Y|X,W})]}.

(143)

where (
140
) follows from Lemma
8
; (
141
) follows from Cauchy-Schwarz inequality; (
142
) follows from the triangle inequality of the
L_{2}
norm; and (
143
) follows from Lemma
5
and Lemma
A.3
.
∎

In Section
4.3.3
we make use of Theorem
11
,
12
and
13
with Lemma
4
to bound the MER in concrete learning problems in terms of the MMSE of estimating
W
from data.

4.3.3
Examples

Logistic regression (continued)

We continue with the example of logistic regression discussed in Section
4.1.1
, where
{\mathsf{Y}}=\{0,1\}
,
{\mathsf{W}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
K_{Y|x,w}(1)=\sigma(w^{\top}\phi(x))

with
\sigma(a)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=1/(1+e^{-a})
, and
X
is assumed to be independent of
W
.
As
\|\nabla_{w}\sigma(w^{\top}\phi(x))\|\leq\|\phi(x)\|/4
, from Lemma
A.4
we know that
\sigma(w^{\top}\phi(x))
is
\|\phi(x)\|/4
-Lipschitz in
w
, hence

\displaystyle d_{\rm TV}(K_{Y|x,w^{\prime}},K_{Y|x,w})=\big|\sigma(w^{\prime\top}\phi(x))-\sigma(w^{\top}\phi(x))\big|\leq\frac{1}{4}\|\phi(x)\|\|w^{\prime}-w\|.

(144)

Then, from Theorem
10
, Theorem
11
and Lemma
4
, the following bounds hold for the log loss and the zero-one loss.

Corollary 6
.

In binary logistic regression, for the log loss,

\displaystyle{\rm MER}_{\log}

\displaystyle\leq\log\Big(1+\frac{1}{2}s_{\phi}^{2}e^{s_{\phi}s_{\mathsf{W}}}R_{2}(W|Z^{n})\Big)

(145)

where
s_{\phi}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sup_{x\in{\mathsf{X}}}\|\phi(x)\|
and
s_{\mathsf{W}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sup_{w\in{\mathsf{W}}}\|w\|
;
while for the zero-one loss,

\displaystyle{\rm MER}_{01}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4}\mathbf{E}[\|\phi(X)\|]\sqrt{2R_{2}(W|Z^{n})}.

(146)

Proof.

For the log loss, from Theorem
10
,

\displaystyle{\rm MER}_{\log}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{E}\big[D_{\rm KL}(K_{Y|X,W}\|K_{Y|X,W^{\prime}})\big]

(147)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{E}\Big[\log\Big(1+\frac{2d_{\rm TV}^{2}(K_{Y|X,W},K_{Y|X,W^{\prime}})}{\min\{\sigma(W^{\prime\top}\phi(X)),1-\sigma(W^{\prime\top}\phi(X))\}}\Big)\Big]

(148)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{E}\Big[\log\Big(1+4e^{|W^{\prime\top}\phi(X)|}{d_{\rm TV}^{2}(K_{Y|X,W},K_{Y|X,W^{\prime}})}\Big)\Big]

(149)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{E}\Big[\log\Big(1+\frac{1}{4}e^{s_{\phi}s_{\mathsf{W}}}s_{\phi}^{2}\|W-W^{\prime}\|^{2}\Big)\Big]

(150)

\displaystyle\leq\log\Big(1+\frac{1}{2}e^{s_{\phi}s_{\mathsf{W}}}s_{\phi}^{2}R_{2}(W|Z^{n}))\Big)

(151)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}s_{\phi}^{2}e^{s_{\phi}s_{\mathsf{W}}}R_{2}(W|Z^{n})

(152)

where we used a reverse Pinsker’s inequality
[
57
, Theorem 28]
, the fact that
\max\{1/\sigma(w^{\top}\phi(x)),1/(1-\sigma(w^{\top}\phi(x)))\}\leq 2\exp\{|w^{\top}\phi(x)|\}
,
d_{\rm TV}(K_{Y|x,w^{\prime}},K_{Y|x,w})\leq\|\phi(x)\|\|w^{\prime}-w\|/4
from (
144
), Jensen’s inequality, and Lemma
4
.

For the zero-one loss, from Theorem
11
,

\displaystyle{\rm MER}_{01}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{E}[d_{\rm TV}(K_{Y|X,W^{\prime}},K_{Y|X,W})]

(153)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4}\mathbf{E}\big[\|\phi(X)\|\|W^{\prime}-W\|\big]

(154)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4}\mathbf{E}[\|\phi(X)\|]\sqrt{\mathbf{E}\big[\|W^{\prime}-W\|^{2}\big]}

(155)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\mathbf{E}[\|\phi(X)\|]\sqrt{2R_{2}(W|Z^{n})}

(156)

where we used (
144
) and Lemma
4
.
∎

The upper bound in (
145
) shows that the rate of convergence of
\rm MER_{\log}
in
n
for logistic regression is the same as that for
R_{2}(W|Z^{n})
, as
\log(1+u)\leq u
for
u>0
. This improves the upper bound given in (
88
) when
R_{2}(W|Z^{n})
is small, e.g., when
n
is large.

Nonlinear and linear regression (continued)

We also continue with the discussion on the nonlinear and linear regression problems in Section
4.1.2
, where
Y=g(X,W)+V
,
{\mathsf{W}}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
X
and
W
are independent, and
V\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
is independent of
(X,W)
.
Under this model,

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(K_{Y|x,w^{\prime}},K_{Y|x,w})=2\sigma^{2}D_{\rm KL}(K_{Y|x,w^{\prime}}\|K_{Y|x,w})=(g(x,w)-g(x,w^{\prime}))^{2}.

(157)

From Theorem
10
, Theorem
13
and Lemma
4
, we obtain the following upper bounds for nonlinear regression.

Corollary 7
.

For the above nonlinear regression problem, let
s_{g}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathbf{E}\big[\sup_{w\in{\mathsf{W}}}\|\nabla_{w}g(X,w)\|^{2}\big]
. Then for the log loss,

\displaystyle{\rm MER}_{\log}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}R_{2}(g(X,W)|X,Z^{n})\leq\frac{s_{g}}{\sigma^{2}}R_{2}(W|Z^{n}),

(158)

while for the quadratic loss,

\displaystyle{\rm MER}_{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4\sqrt{2(\mathbf{E}[g(X,W)^{2}]+\sigma^{2})R_{2}(g(X,W)|X,Z^{n})}

(159)

\displaystyle\leq 4\sqrt{2(\mathbf{E}[g(X,W)^{2}]+\sigma^{2})s_{g}R_{2}(W|Z^{n})}.

(160)

Proof.

For the log loss,

\displaystyle{\rm MER}_{\log}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{E}[D_{\rm KL}(K_{Y|X,W}\|K_{Y|X,W^{\prime}})]

(161)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\mathbf{E}\big[\big(g(X,W)-g(X,W^{\prime})\big)^{2}\big]

(162)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}R_{2}(g(X,W)|X,Z^{n})

(163)

Extensions

5.1
Multiple model families

Instead of being described by a single model family, in many cases the joint distribution of
X
and
Y
can be better represented by a finite class of model families

{\mathbb{M}}=\{{\mathcal{M}}_{m},m\in{\mathsf{M}}\}
all together, where each family
{\mathcal{M}}_{m}=\{P_{X,Y|w,m},w\in{\mathsf{W}}_{m}\}
is a collection of parametrized joint distributions of
(X,Y)
.
The class of model families
\mathbb{M}
is also known as the
model class
, and the index
m
of each family is also known as the
model index

[
59
]
.
In the Bayesian formulation, the model index
M
is treated as a random element of
{\mathsf{M}}
with prior
P_{M}
; given a model index
m
, the model parameters are represented as a random vector in
{\mathsf{W}}_{m}
with prior
P_{W|m}
.
As before, denoting
Z_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(X_{i},Y_{i})
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, as the observed data and
Z\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(X,Y)
as a fresh pair, the quantities under consideration are assumed to be generated from the joint distribution

\displaystyle P_{M,W,Z^{n},Z}=P_{M}P_{W|M}\Big(\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}P_{Z_{i}|W,M}\Big)P_{Z|W,M}

(172)

where
P_{Z_{i}|W,M}=P_{Z|W,M}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
.
In the same spirit in the single model family setting, we can define the MER in the above multi-model family setting as follows.

Definition 4
.

In the multi-model family setting, the fundamental limit of the Bayes risk with respect to the loss function
\ell
is defined as

\displaystyle R_{\ell}(Y|X,W,M)=\inf_{\Psi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathsf{X}}\times{\mathsf{W}}\times{\mathsf{M}}\rightarrow{\mathsf{A}}}\mathbf{E}[\ell(Y,\Psi(X,W,M))].

(173)

Definition 5
.

In the multi-model family setting, the minimum excess risk with respect to the loss function
\ell
is defined as

\displaystyle{\rm MER}_{\ell}=R_{\ell}(Y|X,Z^{n})-R_{\ell}(Y|X,W,M).

(174)

Similar to Lemma
2
and Theorem
10
in the single model family setting, for the log loss, the MER in the multi-model family setting can be related to the conditional mutual information
I(M,W;Y|X,Z^{n})
and its upper bounds.

Theorem 14
.

In the multi-model family setting, with the log loss,

\displaystyle{\rm MER}_{\log}=I(M,W;Y|X,Z^{n}),

(175)

which can be upper-bounded by

\frac{1}{n}I(M,W;Y^{n}|X^{n}).

Further, if
P_{X,Y|w,m}=P_{X|w,m}K_{Y|X,w,m}
for all
(m,w)\in{\mathsf{M}}\times{\mathsf{W}}_{m}
, then

\displaystyle{\rm MER}_{\log}\leq\mathbf{E}[D_{\rm KL}(K_{Y|X,W,M}\|K_{Y|X,W^{\prime},M^{\prime}})],

(176)

where
(M^{\prime},W^{\prime})
is a sample from the posterior distribution
P_{M,W|X,Z^{n}}
such that
(M,W)
and
(M^{\prime},W^{\prime})
are conditionally i.i.d. given
(X,Z^{n})
.

In addition, we can still bound the MER in terms of the deviation of the posterior predictive distribution from the true predictive model, similar to the results in Section
4.3
.
As in the predictive modeling framework, suppose that for each model family
\mathcal{M}_{m}\in\mathbb{M}
,
P_{X,Y|w,m}=P_{X|w,m}K_{Y|X,w,m}
for all
w\in{\mathsf{W}}_{m}
.
Then for any statistical distance

, a diameter-like quantity of the model class
\mathbb{M}
with respect to

\displaystyle{\rm diam}(\mathbb{M},D)=\max_{m\neq m^{\prime}\in{\mathsf{M}}}\sup_{w\in{\mathsf{W}}_{m},w^{\prime}\in{\mathsf{W}}_{m^{\prime}}}\sup_{x\in{\mathsf{X}}}D(K_{Y|x,w^{\prime},m^{\prime}},K_{Y|x,w,m}).

(177)

With the above definition we have the following general upper bound on the deviation of the posterior predictive distribution from the true predictive model.
The proof is given in Appendix
C
.

Theorem 15
.

In the multi-model setting, for any statistical distance
D
that is convex in the first argument,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[D(P_{Y|X,Z^{n}},K_{Y|X,W,M})]\leq\mathbf{E}[D(K_{Y|X,W^{\prime},M^{\prime}},K_{Y|X,W,M})],

(178)

where
(M^{\prime},W^{\prime})
is a sample from the posterior distribution
P_{M,W|X,Z^{n}}

(M,W)
and
(M^{\prime},W^{\prime})

(X,Z^{n})

The right side of (

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[D(K_{Y|X,W^{\prime},M},K_{Y|X,W,M})]+2{\rm diam}(\mathbb{M},D)R_{01}(M|X,Z^{n}),

(179)

where

W^{\prime}
is a sample from the posterior distribution

W^{\prime}
and

are conditionally i.i.d. given
(X,Z^{n},M)

If

Theorem

shows that under the multi-model family setting, the expected deviation consists of two parts: the first part can be related to the estimation error of the model parameters when the model index is correctly identified, which depends on the complexity of each model family; the second part is related to the penalty when the model index is wrongly identified, which depends on the overall complexity of the model class and the error probability of model index estimation.

As an example, for linear regression with multiple model families, the predictive model in the
m
th family can be described as
K_{Y|x,w,m}=\mathcal{N}(w^{\top}\phi(x,m),\sigma^{2})

In this case,

\displaystyle D_{\rm KL}(K_{Y|x,w^{\prime},m^{\prime}}\|K_{Y|x,w,m})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\big(w^{\prime\top}\phi(x,m^{\prime})-w^{\top}\phi(x,m)\big)^{2},

(180)

\displaystyle{\rm diam}(\mathbb{M},D_{\rm KL})=\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\max_{m\neq m^{\prime}\in{\mathsf{M}}}\sup_{w\in{\mathsf{W}}_{m},w^{\prime}\in{\mathsf{W}}_{m^{\prime}}}\sup_{x\in{\mathsf{X}}}\big(w^{\prime\top}\phi(x,m^{\prime})-w^{\top}\phi(x,m)\big)^{2}.

From Theorem

{\rm MER}_{\log}

\displaystyle{\rm MER}_{\log}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\mathbf{E}[\|\phi_{M}(X)\|^{2}]R_{2}(W|Z^{n},M)+H(M|Z^{n})

\displaystyle{\rm MER}_{\log}\leq\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\mathbf{E}[\|\phi_{M}(X)\|^{2}]R_{2}(W|Z^{n},M)+2{\rm diam}(\mathbb{M},D_{\rm KL})R_{01}(M|Z^{n})

\displaystyle R_{2}(W|Z^{n},M)=\sum_{m\in{\mathsf{M}}}P_{M}(m)\mathbf{E}[{\rm tr}(C_{W|Z^{n},m})]

We see that the MER consists of a part that depends on the minimum achievable model parameter estimation error given each model index, and a part that depends on the uncertainty of model index estimation and the “diameter” of the model class.

5.2
MER in nonparametric models

The definition of MER can also be extended to Bayesian learning under a nonparametric predictive model that can be specified in terms of a random process.
Formally, consider the case where
F
is a real-valued random process indexed by
x\in{\mathsf{X}}
, and the predictive model is a probability transition kernel
K_{Y|F(X)}
. It is further assumed that
F
is
a priori
independent of
X
.
The observed data and the fresh pair are assumed to be generated from the joint distribution

\displaystyle P_{F,Z^{n},Z}=P_{F}\Big(\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}P_{Z_{i}|F}\Big)P_{Z|F}

(185)

where
P_{Z_{i}|F}=P_{Z|F}=P_{X}K_{Y|F(X)}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
.
Two simple examples of the above model are 1) noiseless Gaussian process regression model, where
F
is a Gaussian process with a mean function
m\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathsf{X}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and a covariance function
k\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathsf{X}}\times{\mathsf{X}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, and
Y=F(X)
; and 2) binary classification model with Gaussian process as a latent function
[
7
]
, where
F
can be the same Gaussian process, and
K_{Y|F(X)}(1|f(x))=\sigma(f(x))
with
\sigma(\cdot)
being the logistic sigmoid function.

In the same spirit in the parametric case, the MER under the above nonparametric model can be defined as

\displaystyle{\rm MER}_{\ell}=R_{\ell}(Y|X,Z^{n})-R_{\ell}(Y|F(X)),

(186)

where
R_{\ell}(Y|X,Z^{n})
and
R_{\ell}(Y|F(X))
are defined according to the general definition of the Bayes risk in (
3
), and correspond to (
2
) and (
11
) respectively.
For the log loss, using the fact that
H_{\mu}(Y|F(X),Z^{n})=H_{\mu}(Y|F(X))
and following the same argument as in Corollary
1
, we have

\displaystyle{\rm MER}_{\rm log}

\displaystyle=I(F(X);Y|X,Z^{n})\leq\frac{1}{n}I(F(X);Y^{n}|X^{n}).

(187)

For the quadratic loss,

\displaystyle{\rm MER}_{2}

\displaystyle=R_{2}(Y|X,Z^{n})-R_{2}(Y|F(X)).

(188)

In the special case of noiseless Gaussian process regression model,
R_{2}(Y|F(X))=0
, which implies

\displaystyle{\rm MER}_{2}

\displaystyle=R_{2}(F(X)|F(X_{1}),\ldots,F(X_{n}))

(189)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}\big[k(X,X)-k(X,X^{n})^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(X^{n})k(X,X^{n})\big]

(190)

where
k(X,X^{n})
is the covariance vector between
F(X)
and
(F(X_{1}),\ldots,F(X_{n}))
, and
\Sigma(X^{n})
is the covariance matrix of
(F(X_{1}),\ldots,F(X_{n}))
.
The above expression may be further analyzed using the eigenfunction expansion of the covariance function
k

[
7
]
.
For the binary classification model with Gaussian process as the latent function, or more general models specified with non-Gaussian processes, the MER may not have a simple close-form expression.

6
Discussion

We have defined the minimum excess risk in Bayesian learning with respect to general loss functions, and presented general methods for obtaining upper bounds for this quantity.
How to lower-bound this quantity is left as an open problem.
We would like to close the paper by discussing the following two aspects.

6.1
Tightness and utility of the results

Two methods for deriving upper bounds on the MER have been presented: one method relates the MER to
I(W;Y|X,Z^{n})
; the other one relates it to
R_{2}(W|X,Z^{n})
.

With the precise asymptotic expansion of
I(W;Z^{n})
, the first method is suitable for asymptotic analysis for a wide range of loss functions. Using this method, we have shown that for any bounded loss function, the MER scales with the data size
n
as
O(\sqrt{1/n})
in general, while for the log loss, the squared loss with bounded
Y
, and bounded loss under realizable models, this convergence rate can be improved to
O(1/n)
. When the model parameter lies in a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, or when the VC dimension of the generative function class is
d
, the MER bounds can also capture the dependence on
d
, as
O(\sqrt{d/n})
or
O(d/n)
in different settings. An MER lower bound of
\Omega(d/n)
is derived in a follow-up work
[
45
, Theorem 10]
for the cases where the excess risk of using
\Psi^{*}(X,W^{\prime})
as the plug-in decision rule is lower bounded by
\|W-W^{\prime}\|^{2}
, which matches upper bounds in certain settings derived in this work.

The second method has the potential to provide us with nonasymptotic upper bounds.
The only explicit expression for
R_{2}(W|X,Z^{n})
we have so far is for linear regression, for which we have derived order-optimal upper bound for both
{\rm MER}_{\log}
and
{\rm MER}_{2}
.
In order to obtain explicit upper bounds for problems beyond linear regression, e.g. logistic regression or nonlinear regression, we would need upper bounds on
R_{2}(W|X,Z^{n})
, or other forms of minimum model parameter estimation error in these settings.
Nevertheless, from the examples on logistic regression, linear regression, and nonlinear regression, we see that the MER upper bounds obtained from the second method depend on
n
only through
R_{2}(W|X,Z^{n})
. This explicitly shows how the model uncertainty translates to the epistemic uncertainty and contributes to the overall prediction uncertainty.
The definition of MER provides such a principled way to define different notions of uncertainties in Bayesian learning,
and its study guides the analysis and estimation of these uncertainties, which is an increasingly important direction of research with wide applications.

6.2
MER in Bayesian learning vs. excess risk in frequentist learning

The distinguishing feature of Bayesian learning is that the generative model of data is assumed to be drawn from a known model family according to some prior distribution. As a result, the MER in Bayesian learning is determined by how accurate the model can be estimated, and there is no notion of
approximation error
unless the model family or the prior distribution is misspecified. This stands in contrast to the frequentist formulation of statistical learning where the data-generating model is assumed to be completely unknown, and the excess risk consists of an estimation error part and an approximation error part.

In the discussion on the setting with multiple model families in Section
5.1
, it is shown that the MER there not only depends on the accuracy of the model parameter estimation within a fixed model, but also on the product of the error probability of model index estimation and a diameter-like term measuring the largest statistical distance among the predictive models.
The latter quantity may be viewed as an analogue of the approximation error in the frequentist setting, as it
upper-bounds the penalty incurred by a wrong estimate of the model index. Its impact
on the MER vanishes as the data size increases though, as the error probability of model index estimation would eventually go to zero.

Another connection to the frequentist learning would be the identical expressions shared by the MER-information relationship in Corollary
4
and the generalization-information relationship in the frequentist setting of
[
12
, Theorem 1]
. It shows the important roles played by information-theoretic quantities in the theory of statistical learning.
