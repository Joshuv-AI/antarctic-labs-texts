Reconstruction of smooth shape defects in waveguides using locally resonant frequencies
Angèle Niclas, Laurent Seppecher

Abstract
This article aims to present a new method to reconstruct slowly varying width defects in 2D waveguides using locally resonant frequencies. At these frequencies, locally resonant modes propagate in the waveguide under the form of Airy functions depending on a parameter called the locally resonant point. In this particular point, the local width of the waveguide is known and its location can be recovered from boundary measurements of the wavefield. Using the same process for different frequencies, we produce a good approximation of the width in all the waveguide. Given multi-frequency measurements taken at the surface of the waveguide, we provide a L ∞ \displaystyle\text{L}^{\infty} -stable explicit method to reconstruct the width of the waveguide. We finally validate our method on numerical data, and we discuss its applications and limits.



1
Introduction

This article presents a new method to reconstruct width variations of a slowly varying waveguide from multi-frequency one side boundary measurements in dimension 2. The considered varying waveguide is described by

\Omega:=\left\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\,|\,0<y<h(x)\right\},

(1)

where
\displaystyle h\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathbb{R})\cap W^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{R})
is a positive profile function defining the top boundary. The bottom boundary is assumed to be flat (see an illustration in Figure
1
) but a similar analysis could be done when both boundaries are varying. In the time-harmonic regime, the wavefield
\displaystyle u
satisfies the Helmholtz equation with Neumann boundary conditions

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\Delta u+k^{2}u=-f&\text{ in }\Omega,\\
\partial_{\nu}u=b&\text{ on }\partial\Omega,\end{array}\right.

(2)

where
\displaystyle k\in(0,+\infty)
is the frequency,
\displaystyle f
is an interior source term, and
\displaystyle b
is a boundary source term. In this work, a waveguide is said to be slowly varying when there exists a small parameter
\displaystyle\eta>0
such that
\displaystyle\|h^{\prime}\|_{\text{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\leq\eta
and
\displaystyle\|h^{\prime\prime}\|_{\text{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\leq\eta^{2}
. Such waveguides are good models of ducts, corroded pipes, or metal plates (see
[
16
,
19
]
).

We focus in this work on the recovery of the function
\displaystyle h
modeling the waveguide shape from the knowledge of the wavefield
\displaystyle d^{\text{ex}}(x):=u(x,0)
on one surface of the waveguide and for multiple frequencies
\displaystyle k
. This model and inverse problem is inspired from non destructive monitoring of plates done in
[
5
,
18
,
13

\displaystyle u(x,y)
for
\displaystyle x\in I
where
\displaystyle I
is an interval of
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}
and
\displaystyle y=0
in a frequency interval
\displaystyle K\subset(0,+\infty)
, as shown in Figure

If
\displaystyle k

\displaystyle k=n\pi/h(x^{\star}_{k})

\displaystyle n\in\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle x^{\star}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}

[

\displaystyle x^{\star}_{k}

\displaystyle x^{\star}_{k}
for different frequencies, and then to recover the shape function
\displaystyle h

1.1
Scientific context

The detection and reconstruction of shape defects in a waveguide are mentioned in different works. In articles
[
20
,
2
,
1
]
, the authors use a conformal mapping to map the geometry of the perturbed waveguide to that of a regular waveguide. This method is beneficial to understand the propagation of waves in irregular waveguides. Still, it is not easily adaptable to the inverse problem and the reconstruction of defects since the transformation to a regular waveguide is not explicit and proves numerically expensive. Another approach based on the scattering field treatment is developed in
[
22
]
. Finally, articles
[
23
,
15
]
study the forward problem and give leads on how to use one side boundary measurements to reconstruct the width of a slowly varying waveguide.

Our work concerns a different approach, also used in
[
7
,
6
]
, where we assume the data to be available for a whole interval of frequencies. This provides additional information that should help to localize and reconstruct the shape of the defect. Moreover, the use of multi-frequency data often provides uniqueness of the reconstruction (see
[
4
]
) and a better stability (see
[
8
,
17
,
24
]
).

In a previous work
[
11
]
, we already
presented a method to reconstruct small width variations using an inverse scattering procedure. However,
we
avoided all the cut-off frequencies of the waveguide, which are frequencies
\displaystyle k>0
such that
\displaystyle k=n\pi/h(x^{\star}_{k})
for a mode
\displaystyle n\in\mathbb{N}
and a longitudinal position
\displaystyle x^{\star}_{k}
. Since experimental works presented in
[
5
,
13
]
suggest that these resonant frequencies are helpful to reconstruct width variations, we choose in this article to work only with these frequencies. Using the study of the forward problem already done in
[
10
]
, we know that if
\displaystyle k
is a locally resonant frequency, the wavefield
\displaystyle u
strongly depends on
\displaystyle x^{\star}_{k}
. The main idea of our reconstruction method is to use measurements of
\displaystyle u
to find back
\displaystyle x^{\star}_{k}
. Since
\displaystyle h(x^{\star}_{k})=n\pi/k
, it then gives up the information about the width of the waveguide in one point. By taking different locally resonant frequencies
\displaystyle k
, we show that one can obtain a complete approximation of the width
\displaystyle h
of the waveguide.

1.2
Outline of the paper

The key result of this paper is Theorem
2
, which proves that
\displaystyle u
is close to a three parameters Airy function, one parameter being
\displaystyle x^{\star}_{k}
. As explained in Proposition
3
, it enables us to find the value of
\displaystyle x^{\star}_{k}
for every locally resonant frequency, and to prove that our reconstruction method is
\displaystyle\text{L}^{\infty}
-stable. The paper is organized as follows. In section 2, we briefly recall results on the modal decomposition and the study of the forward problem. In section 3, we study the inverse problem with measurements taken at the surface of the waveguide and we provide a stability result for the reconstruction of the width of the waveguide. Finally, in section 4, we provide numerical reconstruction of different width defects.

2
Brief study of the forward problem

Before studying the inverse problem associated with the reconstruction of the width in a varying waveguide, we need to study the forward problem in order to find an approximation of the available data. In this section, we briefly recall all the main results on the study of the forward problem. These results and their proofs can be found in
[
11
,
12
]
.

A useful tool when working in waveguides is the modal decomposition. The following definition provides a modal decomposition in varying waveguides:

Definition 1
.

We define the sequence of functions
\displaystyle(\varphi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
by

\forall(x,y)\in\Omega,\quad\varphi_{n}(x,y):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}1/\sqrt{h(x)}&\text{if }n=0,\\
\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{h(x)}}\cos\left(\frac{n\pi y}{h(x)}\right)&\text{if }n\geq 1,\end{array}\right.

(3)

which for any fixed
\displaystyle x\in\mathbb{R}
defines an orthonormal basis of
\displaystyle\text{L}^{2}(0,h(x))
.

Hence, a solution
\displaystyle u\in\text{H}^{2}_{\text{loc}}\big(\Omega\big)
of (
2
) admits a unique modal decomposition

u(x,y)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}u_{n}(x)\varphi_{n}(x,y)\quad\text{where}\quad u_{n}(x):=\int_{0}^{h(x)}u(x,y)\varphi_{n}(x,y)\mathrm{d}y.

(4)

Note that
\displaystyle u_{n}
does not satisfy in general any nice equation. However, when
\displaystyle h
is constant (outside of
\displaystyle\supp(h^{\prime})
), it satisfies an equation of the form
\displaystyle u_{n}^{\prime\prime}+k_{n}^{2}u_{n}=-g_{n}
where
\displaystyle k_{n}^{2}=k^{2}-n^{2}\pi^{2}/h^{2}
is the wavenumber. When
\displaystyle h
is variable, the decomposition (
4
) motivates the following definition:

Definition 2
.

The local wavenumber function of the mode
\displaystyle n\in\mathbb{N}
is the complex function
\displaystyle k_{n}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
defined by

k_{n}^{2}(x):=k^{2}-\frac{n^{2}\pi^{2}}{h(x)^{2}},

(5)

with
\displaystyle\text{Re}(k_{n}),\text{Im}(k_{n})\geq 0
.

In this work, as
\displaystyle h(x)
is non constant,
\displaystyle k_{n}(x)
may vanish for some
\displaystyle x\in\mathbb{R}
and change from a positive real number to a purely imaginary one. We then distinguish three different situations:

Definition 3
.

A mode
\displaystyle n\in\mathbb{N}
falls in one of these three situations:

1.

If
\displaystyle n<kh(x)/\pi
for all
\displaystyle x\in\mathbb{R}
then
\displaystyle k_{n}(x)\in(0,+\infty)
for all
\displaystyle x\in\mathbb{R}
and the mode
\displaystyle n
is called propagative.

2.

If
\displaystyle n>kh(x)/\pi
for all
\displaystyle x\in\mathbb{R}
then
\displaystyle k_{n}(x)\in i(0,+\infty)
for all
\displaystyle x\in\mathbb{R}
and the mode
\displaystyle n
is called evanescent.

3.

If there exists
\displaystyle x^{\star}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\displaystyle n=kh(x^{\star}_{k})/\pi
the mode
\displaystyle n
is called locally resonant. Such points
\displaystyle x^{\star}_{k}
are called resonant points, and there are simple if
\displaystyle h^{\prime}(x^{\star}_{k})\neq 0
, and multiple otherwise.

A frequency
\displaystyle k>0
for which there exists at least a locally resonant mode is called a locally resonant frequency.

Using the wavenumber function, one can adapt the classic Sommerfeld (or outgoing) condition, defined in
[
11
]
for regular waveguides, to general varying waveguides
\displaystyle\Omega
. This condition is used to guarantee uniqueness for the source problem given in equation (
2
).

Definition 4
.

A wavefield
\displaystyle u_{k}\in\text{H}^{2}_{\text{loc}}\big(\Omega\big)
is said to be outgoing if it satisfies

\left|u_{n}^{\prime}(x)\frac{x}{|x|}-ik_{n}(x)u_{n}(x)\right|\underset{|x|\rightarrow+\infty}{\longrightarrow}0\qquad\forall n\in\mathbb{N},

(6)

where
\displaystyle u_{n}
is given in (
4
).

In all this work, we make the following assumptions:

Assumption 1
.

We assume that
\displaystyle h\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathbb{R})\cap W^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{R})
with
\displaystyle h^{\prime}
compactly supported and we then define two constants
\displaystyle 0<h_{\min}\leq h_{\max}<+\infty
such that

\forall x\in\mathbb{R}\quad h_{\min}\leq h(x)\leq h_{\max},

and
\displaystyle h(x)=h_{\min}
or
\displaystyle h(x)=h_{\max}
if
\displaystyle x\notin\supp(h^{\prime})
. For such a function, we define a parameter
\displaystyle\eta>0
that satisfies

\|h^{\prime}\|_{\text{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}<\eta\quad\text{ and }\quad\|h^{\prime\prime}\|_{\text{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}<\eta^{2}.

3
Shape Inversion using a monochromatic source

In this section, we describe the method to recover the width
\displaystyle h
from one side boundary measurements of the wavefield at locally resonant frequencies. We focus here on the reconstruction of the shape of
\displaystyle h
on
\displaystyle\supp(h^{\prime})
, assuming the
a priori
knowledge of the constants
\displaystyle h_{\min}
and
\displaystyle h_{\max}
and of an interval containing
\displaystyle\supp(h^{\prime})
. We detail in Appendix A how these constants can be estimated. To recover the shape function
\displaystyle h
on
\displaystyle\supp(h^{\prime})
, we start by studying the simpler case of a source term
\displaystyle f
generating only a single locally resonant mode in the waveguide. Hence we assume that
\displaystyle f
takes the form

\forall(x,y)\in\Omega\quad f(x,y)=f_{N}(x)\varphi_{N}(y),\qquad f_{N}\in\text{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}),

(14)

where
\displaystyle f_{N}
is compactly supported. We also assume the absence of boundary source term, meaning that
\displaystyle b=0
. This simplified situation is useful to understand the method of reconstruction. It will be generalized to any kind on internal and boundary sources in section
4
.

In the simplest case of a single internal source (
14
), we know from the study of the forward problem in Theorem
1
that the measured data without noise
\displaystyle d^{\text{ex}}
satisfies

d^{\text{ex}}(x):=u(x,0)\approx u_{N}^{\text{app}}(x)\varphi_{N}(0)\quad\text{where}\quad u_{N}^{\text{app}}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}G_{N}^{\text{app}}(x,s)f_{N}(s)\mathrm{d}s.

(15)

Our reconstruction method is based on the recovery of the resonant point
\displaystyle x^{\star}_{k}
for every locally resonant frequencies
\displaystyle k
. It can be seen in Theorem
1
that the approached Green function
\displaystyle G_{N}^{\text{app}}
depends on
\displaystyle x^{\star}_{k}
through the function
\displaystyle\xi
. However, this dependence is intricate and hardly usable to find a direct link between
\displaystyle d^{\text{ex}}
and
\displaystyle x^{\star}_{k}
. Thus, we need to find a simpler approximation of the measurements. In a first part, we provide an approximation of the measured data
\displaystyle d^{\text{ex}}
using a three parameters model function

d^{\text{app}}_{z,\alpha,x_{k}^{\star}}:x\mapsto z\mathcal{A}(\alpha(x_{k}^{\star}-x)),

(16)

where
\displaystyle z\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
is a complex amplitude,
\displaystyle\alpha>0
is a scaling parameter and
\displaystyle x_{k}^{\star}
is the resonant point playing the role of a longitudinal shift. In a second part, we first control the approximation error between this function and the exact measurements and then, we develop a stable way to reconstruct
\displaystyle x_{k}^{\star}
from this approximated data.

3.1
Wavefield approximation and measurements approximation

In order to find a reconstruction of
\displaystyle x^{\star}_{k}
, we need to find an exploitable link between
\displaystyle d^{\text{ex}}:=u(x,0)
and
\displaystyle x^{\star}_{k}
. To do so, we make a Taylor expansion of
\displaystyle G_{N}^{\text{app}}
around the point
\displaystyle x^{\star}_{k}
. For every frequency
\displaystyle k>0
and
\displaystyle R>0
, we denote

\Omega_{R}(x^{\star}_{k}):=\{(x,y)\in\Omega\ |\ |x-x^{\star}_{k}|<R\},\qquad\Gamma_{R}(x^{\star}_{k}):=(x^{\star}_{k}-R,x^{\star}_{k}+R),

(17)

and we consider the Taylor expansion on the interval
\displaystyle\Gamma_{R}
. Moreover, we assume that the source is located at the right of the interval
\displaystyle\Gamma_{R}
, and we thus we define

\Omega_{R}^{+}(x^{\star}_{k}):=\{(x,y)\in\Omega\,|\,x-x^{\star}_{k}>R\},\qquad\Gamma_{R}^{+}(x^{\star}_{k})=(x^{\star}_{k}+R,+\infty).

(18)

Both these sets are represented in Figure

.

The following Proposition shows that
\displaystyle u_{N}^{\text{app}}
can be approached by a three parameters function of type
\displaystyle d^{\text{app}}_{z,\alpha,x_{k}^{\star}}
if the shape function
\displaystyle h
is steep enough at
\displaystyle x_{k}^{\star}
.

Proposition 1
.

Assume that
\displaystyle\Omega
satisfies assumptions
1
, let
\displaystyle R>0
and
\displaystyle k>0
be a locally resonant frequency associated to the mode
\displaystyle N\in\mathbb{N}
and the locally resonant point
\displaystyle x_{k}^{\star}
that satisfies
\displaystyle h^{\prime}(x^{\star}_{k})\geq\theta\eta
for some
\displaystyle\theta>0
. There exist
\displaystyle z\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
,
\displaystyle\alpha>0
and a constant
\displaystyle C_{2}>0
depending only on
\displaystyle h_{\min}
,
\displaystyle h_{\max}
,
\displaystyle N
and
\displaystyle\theta
such that

\|u^{\text{app}}_{N}-d^{\text{app}}_{z,\alpha,x_{k}^{\star}}\|_{\text{L}^{2}(\Gamma_{R}(x^{\star}_{k}))}\leq C_{2}\left(R^{3/2}\eta^{5/6}+R^{5/2}\eta^{7/6}\right),

(19)

where
\displaystyle d^{\text{app}}_{z,\alpha,x_{k}^{\star}}
is defined in (
16
).

Proof.

Using the information about the support of the source term, we know that

u_{N}^{\text{app}}(x)=\int_{x^{\star}_{k}+R}^{+\infty}G_{N}^{\text{app}}(x,s)f_{N}(s)\mathrm{d}s.

Using the definition of
\displaystyle G_{N}^{\text{app}}
given in (
11
), there exists a function
\displaystyle q_{k}
such that for every
\displaystyle x\in\Gamma_{R}(x^{\star}_{k})
,

G_{N}^{\text{app}}(x,s)=q_{k}(s)\frac{(-\xi(x))^{1/4}}{\sqrt{k_{n}(x)}}\mathcal{A}(\xi(x)).

It follows that

u_{N}^{\text{app}}(x)=\frac{(-\xi(x))^{1/4}}{\sqrt{k_{n}(x)}}\mathcal{A}(\xi(x))\int_{x^{\star}_{k}+R}^{+\infty}q_{k}(s)f_{N}(s)\mathrm{d}s.

In the following, we denote
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(\cdot)
bounds depending only on
\displaystyle h_{\max}
,
\displaystyle h_{\min}
and
\displaystyle N
. We see that

k_{N}(x)^{2}=\frac{2N^{2}\pi^{2}h^{\prime}(x^{\star}_{k})}{h(x^{\star}_{k})^{3}}(x-x^{\star}_{k})+\mathcal{O}(\eta^{2}(x-x^{\star}_{k})^{2}).

From now on, we assume that
\displaystyle x>x^{\star}_{k}
, which leads to

k_{N}(x)=\sqrt{\frac{2N^{2}\pi^{2}h^{\prime}(x^{\star}_{k})}{h(x^{\star}_{k})^{3}}}(x-x^{\star}_{k})^{1/2}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\eta^{2}(x-x^{\star}_{k})^{3/2}}{\sqrt{h^{\prime}(x^{\star}_{k})}}\right),

(20)

and so

\xi(x)=\left(\frac{2N^{2}\pi^{2}h^{\prime}(x^{\star}_{k})}{h(x^{\star}_{k})^{3}}\right)^{1/3}(x^{\star}_{k}-x)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\eta^{2}(x-x^{\star}_{k})^{2}}{h^{\prime}(x^{\star}_{k})^{2/3}}\right).

Then,

\mathcal{A}(\xi(x))=\mathcal{A}\left(\left(\frac{2N^{2}\pi^{2}h^{\prime}(x^{\star}_{k})}{h(x^{\star}_{k})^{3}}\right)^{1/3}(x^{\star}_{k}-x)\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\eta^{2}(x-x^{\star}_{k})^{2}}{h^{\prime}(x^{\star}_{k})^{2/3}}\right),

and

\frac{(-\xi(x))^{1/4}}{\sqrt{k_{N}(x)}}=\left(\frac{2N^{2}\pi^{2}h^{\prime}(x^{\star}_{k})}{h(x^{\star}_{k})^{3}}\right)^{-1/6}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\eta^{2}(x-x^{\star}_{k})}{h^{\prime}(x^{\star}_{k})^{7/6}}\right).

We set

z=\left(\frac{2N^{2}\pi^{2}h^{\prime}(x^{\star}_{k})}{h(x^{\star}_{k})^{3}}\right)^{-1/6}\int_{x^{\star}_{k}+R}^{+\infty}q_{k}(s)f_{N}(s)\mathrm{d}s,\qquad\alpha=\left(\frac{2N^{2}\pi^{2}h^{\prime}(x^{\star}_{k})}{h(x^{\star}_{k})^{3}}\right)^{1/3},

(21)

and it follows that

u_{N}^{\text{app}}(x)=z\mathcal{A}(\alpha(x^{\star}_{k}-x))+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\eta^{2}(x-x^{\star}_{k})}{h^{\prime}(x^{\star}_{k})^{7/6}}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\eta^{2}(x-x^{\star}_{k})^{2}}{h^{\prime}(x^{\star}_{k})^{5/6}}\right).

The exact same study can be done in the case
\displaystyle x<x^{\star}_{k}
. Since
\displaystyle|x-x^{\star}_{k}|\leq R
, we conclude that

\|u_{N}^{\text{app}}-d^{\text{app}}_{z,\alpha,x_{k}^{\star}}\|_{\text{L}^{2}(\Gamma_{R}(x^{\star}_{k}))}=\eta^{2}\mathcal{O}\left(R^{3/2}(h^{\prime}(x^{\star}_{k}))^{-7/6}+R^{5/2}(h^{\prime}(x^{\star}_{k}))^{-5/6}\right).

To conclude, we use the fact that
\displaystyle h^{\prime}(x^{\star}_{k})\geq\theta\eta
.
∎

Corollary 1
.

Assume that
\displaystyle\Omega
satisfies assumptions
1
, let
\displaystyle R>0
and
\displaystyle k>0
be a locally resonant frequency associated to the mode
\displaystyle N\in\mathbb{N}
and the locally resonant point
\displaystyle x_{k}^{\star}
that satisfies
\displaystyle h^{\prime}(x^{\star}_{k})\geq\theta\eta
for some
\displaystyle\theta>0
. There exist
\displaystyle\eta>0
such that if
\displaystyle\eta\leq\eta_{0}
then there exist
\displaystyle z\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
,
\displaystyle\alpha>0
and a constant
\displaystyle C_{3}>0
depending only on
\displaystyle h_{\min}
,
\displaystyle h_{\max}
,
\displaystyle N
and
\displaystyle\theta
such that

\|d^{\text{ex}}-d^{\text{app}}_{z,\alpha,x_{k}^{\star}}\|_{\text{L}^{2}(\Gamma_{R}(x^{\star}_{k}))}\leq C_{3}\left(\delta(k)^{-8}R^{2}\eta+R^{3/2}\eta^{5/6}+R^{5/2}\eta^{7/6}\right).

(22)

Proof.

We apply Proposition
1
, Theorem
1
and trace results which prove that there exists a constant
\displaystyle\gamma>0
depending only on
\displaystyle R
,
\displaystyle h_{\min}
and
\displaystyle h_{\max}
such that

\|u(\cdot,0)-u^{\text{app}}(\cdot,0)\|_{\text{L}^{2}(\Gamma_{R}(x^{\star}_{k}))}\leq\|u-u^{\text{app}}\|_{\text{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma_{R}(x^{\star}_{k}))}\leq\gamma\|u-u^{\text{app}}\|_{\text{H}^{1}(\Omega_{R}(x^{\star}_{k}))}.

∎

This result indicates a strategy to determine
\displaystyle x_{k}^{\star}
from the exact data
\displaystyle d^{\text{ex}}
. Assuming that
\displaystyle\eta
is small enough to see
\displaystyle d^{\text{app}}_{z,\alpha,x_{k}^{\star}}
as a good approximation of
\displaystyle d^{\text{ex}}
, one may fit the three parameters
\displaystyle(\alpha,z,x_{k}^{\star})
that minimize the misfit
\displaystyle d^{\text{ex}}-d^{\text{app}}_{z,\alpha,x_{k}^{\star}}
for some norm. We also see from this result that the error in the Taylor expansion strongly depends on the values of
\displaystyle\theta
,
\displaystyle R
and
\displaystyle\delta(k)
. Since we plan on using different locally resonant frequencies, we need to get a uniform control over
\displaystyle k
on the Taylor expansion. Moreover, we need to control the length
\displaystyle R
of the measurement interval. If
\displaystyle R
is too large, the quality of the Taylor expansion diminishes and if
\displaystyle R
is too small, the data on the interval
\displaystyle\Gamma_{R}(x^{\star}_{k})
may not contain enough information to fit the three parameters
\displaystyle(z,\alpha,x_{k}^{\star})
with stability.

To prevent this,
\displaystyle R
needs to be scaled depending on the parameter
\displaystyle\alpha
. Indeed, the change of variable
\displaystyle x\mapsto\alpha(x^{\star}_{k}-x)
must cover a large enough interval to perform the desired fitting. Using the expression of
\displaystyle\alpha
given in (
21
), we say that
\displaystyle\alpha(x^{\star}_{k}-x)
covers an interval of fixed radius
\displaystyle\sigma>0
if

R\geq\frac{\sigma}{\alpha}\geq\frac{\sigma h_{\max}}{2\beta^{1/3}N^{2}\pi^{2}}\eta^{-1/3}.

This means that
\displaystyle R
needs to be scaled as
\displaystyle\eta^{-1/3}
and we assume now that

R:=r\eta^{-1/3}.

(23)

where
\displaystyle r>0
is a constant. We can now give a uniform approximation result between
\displaystyle d^{\text{ex}}
and
\displaystyle d^{\text{app}}_{z,\alpha,x_{k}^{\star}}
on the interval
\displaystyle\Gamma_{R(x^{\star}_{k})}
.

Theorem 2
.

Assume the same hypotheses than in Theorem
1
, and fix
\displaystyle N\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and
\displaystyle\theta>0
. There exists
\displaystyle\eta_{0}>0
such for any
\displaystyle\eta<\eta_{0}
and for any
\displaystyle k>0
associated to the mode
\displaystyle N
and the locally resonant point
\displaystyle x_{k}^{\star}
satisfying
\displaystyle h^{\prime}(x^{\star}_{k})\geq\theta\eta
, there exist
\displaystyle\alpha>0
and
\displaystyle z\in\mathbb{C}
such that the following approximation holds on the interval
\displaystyle\Gamma_{R}(x^{\star}_{k})
:

d^{\text{ex}}=d^{\text{app}}_{z,\alpha,x_{k}^{\star}}+\mathcal{O}(\eta^{1/3}).

More precisely, there exists a constant
\displaystyle C_{4}>0
depending only on
\displaystyle h_{\min}
,
\displaystyle h_{\max}
,
\displaystyle\theta
and
\displaystyle r
such that

\left\|{d^{\text{ex}}-d^{\text{app}}_{z,\alpha,x_{k}^{\star}}}\right\|_{\text{L}^{2}(\Gamma_{R}(x^{\star}_{k}))}\leq C_{4}\eta^{1/3}.

(24)

Proof.

To prove this result, we need to provide a uniform lower bound for
\displaystyle\delta(k)
under the hypothesis
\displaystyle h^{\prime}(x^{\star}_{k})\geq\theta\eta
. From the definition of
\displaystyle\delta(k)
, we assume without loss of generality that
\displaystyle\delta(k)
is reached by the mode number
\displaystyle N\in\mathbb{N}
and that
\displaystyle\delta(k)^{2}=\left|k^{2}-N^{2}\pi^{2}/h_{\max}^{2}\right|
. Hence, as
\displaystyle k
is locally resonant, we know that
\displaystyle k=N\pi/h(x_{k}^{\star})
and that

\delta(k)^{2}=N^{2}\pi^{2}\left|\frac{1}{h(x_{k}^{\star})^{2}}-\frac{1}{h_{\max}^{2}}\right|\leq\frac{2N^{2}\pi^{2}}{h_{\min}^{2}h_{\max}}(h_{\max}-h(x_{k}^{\star})).

Let us call
\displaystyle t\geq 0
such that
\displaystyle h(x_{k}^{\star}+t)=h_{\max}
, we necessarily have
\displaystyle h^{\prime}(x_{k}^{\star}+t)=0
. Using the hypothesis
\displaystyle h^{\prime\prime}\geq-\eta^{2}
, we know that
\displaystyle h^{\prime}(x_{k}^{\star}+s)\geq\eta\theta-\eta^{2}s
for all
\displaystyle s\in(0,t)
. This implies that
\displaystyle t\geq\theta/\eta
. Then,

h_{\max}-h(x_{k}^{\star})=\int_{0}^{t}h^{\prime}(x_{k}^{\star}+s)\mathrm{d}s\geq\eta\theta t-\eta^{2}\frac{t^{2}}{2}\geq\frac{\theta^{2}}{2}.

Then,

\delta(k)\geq\frac{N\pi}{h_{\min}\sqrt{h_{\max}}}\theta.

The proof of Theorem
2
is now straightforward, we simply replace
\displaystyle R
by
\displaystyle r\eta^{-1/3}
in Corollary
1
and use the lower bound on
\displaystyle\delta(k)
.
∎

3.2
Stable reconstruction of
\displaystyle x^{\star}_{k}

We proved so far that the one side boundary measurements are close to the three parameters function

d^{\text{app}}_{z,\alpha,x_{k}^{\star}}:x\mapsto z\mathcal{A}(\alpha(x_{k}^{\star}-x)),

(25)

on the interval
\displaystyle\Gamma_{r\eta^{-1/3}}(x^{\star}_{k})=(x_{k}^{\star}-r\eta^{-1/3},x_{k}^{\star}+r\eta^{-1/3})
. The question is now to understand if one can get a stable evaluation of
\displaystyle x_{k}^{\star}
from this approached data. We define then the following forward operator

F:\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathbb{C}^{*}\times(0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{R}&\longrightarrow&\mathcal{C}^{0}(\Gamma_{R}(\beta/\alpha))\\
(z,\alpha,\beta)&\longmapsto&\left(x\mapsto z\mathcal{A}(\beta-\alpha x)\right)\end{array}\right..

(26)

If the three parameters are uniquely defined, we can deduce an approximation of the position
\displaystyle x_{k}^{\star}
from the solution of
\displaystyle F(z,\alpha,\beta)=d^{\text{app}}_{z,\alpha,x^{\star}_{k}}
through the identification
\displaystyle x_{k}^{\star}=\beta/\alpha
. The following result guarantee the uniqueness of the solution to this problem.

Proposition 2
.

Let
\displaystyle d_{0}:=F(z_{0},\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})
, there exists
\displaystyle r_{0}>0
such that if
\displaystyle r>r_{0}
then the problem
\displaystyle F(z,\alpha,\beta)=d_{0}
has a unique solution
\displaystyle(z_{0},\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})
.

Proof.

If
\displaystyle r
is high enough, the interval
\displaystyle\Gamma_{r\eta^{-1/3}}
is large enough to contain the maximum amplitude of the function
\displaystyle d_{0}
at position
\displaystyle x_{\max}
, and the two first zeros of the function
\displaystyle d_{0}
called
\displaystyle x_{1}
and
\displaystyle x_{2}
. Hence
\displaystyle(z_{0},\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})
are uniquely determined by

z_{0}=\frac{d_{0}(x_{\max})}{\left\|{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{\infty}},\qquad\alpha_{0}=\frac{y_{1}-y_{2}}{x_{2}-x_{1}},\qquad\beta_{0}=\frac{y_{1}x_{2}-y_{2}x_{1}}{x_{2}-x_{1}},

(27)

where
\displaystyle y_{1}
and
\displaystyle y_{2}
are the two first zeros of the Airy function
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
.
∎

This result of uniqueness and the corresponding inversion formulas are not directly applicable. Indeed, these formulas are not robust to noise or data uncertainties and we remind the reader that an approximation is already made between
\displaystyle d^{\text{ex}}
and
\displaystyle d^{\text{app}}_{z,\alpha,x^{\star}_{k}}
.

A first strategy would be to use the explicit formula (
27
) and apply some data regularization with a low-pass filter before doing the inversion (see
[
21
]
for more details). This method works perfectly with exact data, and will be called from now the “direct parameters reconstruction method”.

However, few issues can be raised with this method. Firstly, it is not sufficiently robust if data are very noisy, as illustrated in Figure
10
where we use this method to reconstruct parameters with a random additive noise of increasing amplitude. Secondly, as mentioned in the previous subsection, we will only make measurements of the wavefield on the interval
\displaystyle\Gamma_{R}
, and we cannot be sure that neither the first two zeros nor the maximum of
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
will occur in this interval. Finally, it is more realistic to assume that we only have access to
\displaystyle d^{\text{app}}_{z,\alpha,x^{\star}_{k}}
on a finite number of receivers positions. For all this reasons, a least squares approach is introduced. On can keep the previous method to find an initial guess of the parameters
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{p}:=(z,\alpha,\beta)
.

We now assume that we have access to the data

d_{i}:=F(p)(t_{i}),\quad i=1,\dots,n,

(28)

where
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{p}:=(z,\alpha,\beta)
and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{t}:=(t_{i})_{i=1,\dots,n}\in\Gamma_{R}
is an increasing subdivision of
\displaystyle\Gamma_{R}
. We denote by
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{d}:=(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
the discrete data on the subdivision
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{t}
. We aim to minimize the least squares energy functional

J_{\boldsymbol{d}}(\boldsymbol{p}):=\frac{1}{2}\|F(\boldsymbol{p})(\boldsymbol{t})-\boldsymbol{d}\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}.

(29)

In this expression, the norm
\displaystyle\ell^{2}
is the normalized euclidean norm defined by

\|\boldsymbol{d}\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}^{2}.

(30)

Denoting by
\displaystyle\tau
the step of the subdivision
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{t}
, we know using quadrature results (see
[
14
]
for more details) that for all functions
\displaystyle f\in\text{H}^{2}(\Gamma_{R})
,

\left|\|f\|_{\text{L}^{2}(\Gamma_{R})}-\|f(\boldsymbol{t})\|_{\ell^{2}}\right|\leq 2\tau\|f\|_{\text{H}^{1}(\Gamma_{R})}.

(31)

It shows that the
\displaystyle\ell^{2}
norm is a good approximation of the
\displaystyle\text{L}^{2}(\Gamma_{R})
norm if the step of discretization
\displaystyle\tau
is small enough.

We assume the knowledge of an open set
\displaystyle U\subset\mathbb{C}^{*}\times(0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{R}
of the form

U:=B_{\mathbb{C}}(0,z_{\text{max}})\times(\alpha_{\min},\alpha_{\max})\times(\beta_{\min},\beta_{\max}),\quad z_{\max},\alpha_{\min},\alpha_{\max}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\star},\quad\beta_{\min},\beta_{\max}\in\mathbb{R},

(32)

containing the solution
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{p}
. The following proposition shows that the least squares problem is locally well-posed if the sampling size
\displaystyle n
is large enough, and it quantifies the error on the recovered parameters.

Proposition 3
.

There exists
\displaystyle n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
such that if
\displaystyle n\geq n_{0}
, then for every
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{p}_{0}\in U
and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{d}_{0}:=F(p_{0})(\boldsymbol{x})
, there exist
\displaystyle\varepsilon>0
and
\displaystyle U^{\prime}\subset U
such that for every
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfying
\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{d}-\boldsymbol{d}_{0}\|_{\ell^{2}}<\varepsilon
then the functional
\displaystyle J_{\boldsymbol{d}}
is strictly convex on
\displaystyle U^{\prime}
and admits a unique minimizer denoted
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{LS}}=(z_{\text{LS}},\alpha_{\text{LS}},\beta_{\text{LS}})
. Moreover, there exists a constant
\displaystyle C_{5}>0
depending only on
\displaystyle U
,
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{p}_{0}
and
\displaystyle n
such that

\|\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{LS}}-\boldsymbol{p}_{0}\|_{2}\leq C_{5}\|\boldsymbol{d}-\boldsymbol{d}_{0}\|_{\ell^{2}}.

(33)

Finally, if we denote

\Lambda:\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}B_{2}(\boldsymbol{d}_{0},\varepsilon)&\rightarrow&\mathbb{R}\\
\boldsymbol{d}&\mapsto&\frac{\beta_{\text{LS}}}{\alpha_{\text{LS}}}\end{array}\right.,

(34)

the operator which approach the value of
\displaystyle x^{\star}_{k}
, there exists a constant
\displaystyle C_{6}>0
depending only on
\displaystyle U
,
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{p}_{0}
and
\displaystyle n
such that

|\Lambda(\boldsymbol{d})-\Lambda(\boldsymbol{d}_{0})|\leq C_{6}\|\boldsymbol{d}-\boldsymbol{d}_{0}\|_{\ell^{2}}.

(35)

The proof of this result is given in Appendix B. In this proof we can see that the choice of the data discretization points
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{t}
is important in order to improve the accuracy of the reconstruction. We illustrate that by choosing
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{p}_{0}=(2+i,1.4,-2.8)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{t}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{t}_{2}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{t}_{3}

\displaystyle[-6,6]

5.2
Method of reconstruction

Using all the previous results, we summarize here all the steps to reconstruct the approximation
\displaystyle h^{\text{app}}
of the width
\displaystyle h
.

1.

Find an approximation of
\displaystyle\supp(h^{\prime})
,
\displaystyle k_{\min}
and
\displaystyle k_{\max}
using the method described at the beginning of Appendix A.

2.

Choose a set of frequencies
\displaystyle K\subset(k_{\min},k_{\max})
and sources
\displaystyle f
,
\displaystyle b_{\text{top}}
,
\displaystyle b_{\text{bot}}
. Then, for every frequency, measure the wavefield
\displaystyle d(x):=u(x,0)
solution of (
7
).

3.

Filter the data by eliminating in the Fourier transform responses around
\displaystyle k_{n}
for every a propagative mode
\displaystyle n
.

4.

Find an approximation of the coordinate
\displaystyle x_{\max}
where
\displaystyle|u(x,0)|
is maximal. Then, choose a length
\displaystyle R>0
, and discretize the interval
\displaystyle[x_{\max}-R,x_{\max}+R]
into the set
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{t}_{k}
. The available data
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{t}_{k}
are then the measurements of
\displaystyle u(\boldsymbol{t}_{k},0)
.

5.

For every frequency, minimize the quantity
\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{d}_{k}-F(\boldsymbol{p})(\boldsymbol{t}_{k})\|_{\ell^{2}}
using a gradient descent to find the approximation
\displaystyle x^{\star,\text{app}}_{k}
of
\displaystyle x^{\star}_{k}

6.

Compute
\displaystyle h^{\text{app}}
using expression (
41
).

In step
\displaystyle 3

\displaystyle\Gamma_{R}(x^{\star}_{k})

\displaystyle x^{\star}_{k}

\displaystyle x^{\star}_{k}

\displaystyle x_{\max}

\displaystyle[x_{\max}-R,x_{\max}+R]

\displaystyle\Gamma_{R^{\prime}}(x^{\star}_{k})
where
\displaystyle R^{\prime}>R

a priori

In this article, we have used the study of the forward problem in slowly varying waveguide presented in
[
10
]
and the approximation of the solutions as combination of Airy functions to develop a new inverse method to reconstruct the width of slowly varying waveguides. Given wavefield measurements at the surface of the waveguide for different locally resonant frequencies, we reconstruct the associated locally resonant points which lead to a good approximation of the width of the waveguide.

One main advantage of this new method is that is does not require any
a priori
information on the sources, and we believe that it could be applied to develop new non destructive passive monitoring methods. Moreover, using locally resonant modes, this method can detect small variations of the width with a high sensibility. More importantly, when we compare this new method with the usual back scattering method developed for instance in
[
11
]
, we notice that this new method seems a lot more precise: while the best relative reconstruction errors are of the order of
\displaystyle 8\%
in
[
11
]
, we reach in this papers relative errors of the order of less than
\displaystyle 1\%
. Even if measurements are not taken in the same area, this improvement of the reconstruction precision must be underlined.

We believe that this work could be extended to elastic waveguides in two dimensions, using the modal decomposition in Lamb modes presented in
[
23
]
. After generalizing it to elastic waveguide, we plan in future works to test the method developed in this article on experimental data to see if the good numerical results obtained using data generated by finite element methods can be reproduced. Indeed, different physical experiments have already been conducted to recover width defects using locally resonant frequencies (see
[
5
,
13
]
) and we hope that the present reconstruction method could both justify and improve these width reconstructions.

Appendix B: Proof of Proposition
3

Proof.

For every
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{p}\in U
, we compute

\nabla J_{\boldsymbol{d}}(\boldsymbol{p})=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{A}(\beta-\alpha t_{i})(z\mathcal{A}(\beta-\alpha t_{i})-d_{i})\\
\sum_{i=1}^{n}-zt_{i}\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(\beta-\alpha t_{i})(z\mathcal{A}(\beta-\alpha t_{i})-d_{i})\\
\sum_{i=1}^{n}z\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(\beta-\alpha t_{i})(z\mathcal{A}(\beta-\alpha t_{i})-d_{i})\end{array}\right),

(61)

\nabla^{2}J_{\boldsymbol{d}}(\boldsymbol{p})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}M_{i}(\beta-\alpha t_{i}),

(62)

with

M_{i}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\mathcal{A}^{2}&-t_{i}\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\times(2z\mathcal{A}-d_{i})&\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(2z\mathcal{A}-d_{i})\\
-t_{i}\times\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\times(2z\mathcal{A}-d_{i})&\begin{array}[]{c}zt_{i}^{2}\mathcal{A}^{\prime\prime}\times(z\mathcal{A}-d_{i})\\
\qquad+z^{2}t_{i}^{2}\times(\mathcal{A}^{\prime})^{2}\end{array}&\begin{array}[]{c}-zt_{i}\times\mathcal{A}^{\prime\prime}(z\mathcal{A}-d_{i})\\
\qquad-t_{i}z^{2}(\mathcal{A}^{\prime})^{2}\end{array}\\
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\times(2z\mathcal{A}-d_{i})&\begin{array}[]{c}-zt_{i}\times\mathcal{A}^{\prime\prime}(z\mathcal{A}-d_{i})\\
\qquad-t_{i}z^{2}\times(\mathcal{A}^{\prime})^{2}\end{array}&\begin{array}[]{c}z\mathcal{A}^{\prime\prime}\times(z\mathcal{A}-d_{i})\\
\qquad z^{2}(\mathcal{A}^{\prime})^{2}\end{array}\end{array}\right).

(63)

For every
\displaystyle h\in\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
\displaystyle h\neq 0
,

(\nabla^{2}J_{\boldsymbol{d}}(\boldsymbol{p})h|h)=A(\boldsymbol{p},h)+B(\boldsymbol{p},h),

(64)

where

A(\boldsymbol{p},h)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\mathcal{A}(\beta-\alpha t_{i})h_{1}+z\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(\beta-\alpha t_{i})(h_{3}-h_{2}t_{i}))^{2},

(65)

B(\boldsymbol{p},h)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(z\mathcal{A}(\beta-\alpha t_{i})-d_{i})(2\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(\beta-\alpha t_{i})h_{1}(h_{3}-h_{2}t_{i})+z\mathcal{A}^{\prime\prime}(\beta-\alpha t_{i})(h_{3}-h_{2}t_{i})^{2}).

(66)

We want to prove that
\displaystyle A(\boldsymbol{p},h)>0
. To do so, we use the following Lemma.

Lemma 1
.

For every
\displaystyle(h_{1},h_{2},h_{3})\in\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
and
\displaystyle(z,\alpha,\beta)\in U
, the set of zeros of

x\mapsto h_{1}\mathcal{A}(\beta-\alpha x)+z\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(\beta-\alpha x)\left(h_{3}-h_{2}x\right),

(67)

is finite, and at most equal to
\displaystyle 3\ell+3
where
\displaystyle\ell
is the number of zeros of
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
on

I:=[\beta_{\min}-t,\beta_{\max}+t],\qquad\text{where}\qquad t=\alpha_{\max}\max(|t_{1}|,|t_{n}|).

(68)

Proof.

We do a change of variable
\displaystyle u=\beta-\alpha x
, and we see that for every
\displaystyle x\in[x_{1},x_{n}]
,
\displaystyle u\in I
. We now look for
\displaystyle u\in I
satisfying

g_{1}(u):=h_{1}\mathcal{A}(u)+z\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(u)\left(h_{3}-\frac{h_{2}}{\alpha}(\beta-u)\right)=0.

(69)

We notice that if
\displaystyle h_{1}=0
then
\displaystyle g_{1}(u)=0
if and only if
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(u)=0
or
\displaystyle x=h_{3}/h_{2}
, which gives
\displaystyle\ell+1
zeros of
\displaystyle g_{1}
. Otherwise, we notice that if
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(u)=0
then
\displaystyle g_{1}(u)\neq 0
since every zero of the Airy function is simple (see
[
3
]
). It means that there exist
\displaystyle\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
and
\displaystyle\beta\in\mathbb{R}
such that

g_{1}(u)=0\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\frac{\mathcal{A}}{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}(u)=\alpha u+\beta.

(70)

We define

g_{2}(u)=\frac{\mathcal{A}}{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}(u)-\alpha u-\beta,

(71)

and then

g_{2}^{\prime}(u)=\left(1-u\left(\frac{\mathcal{A}(u)}{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(u)}\right)^{2}\right),\qquad g_{2}^{\prime\prime}(u)=\frac{\mathcal{A}(u)}{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(u)}\underbrace{\left(2-2u\left(\frac{\mathcal{A}(u)}{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(u)}\right)^{2}-\frac{\mathcal{A}(u)}{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(u)}\right)}_{>0}.

(72)

Between two zeros of
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
,
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
vanishes only once, meaning that depending of the value of
\displaystyle\alpha
,
\displaystyle g_{2}^{\prime}
vanishes at most twice, and so depending of the value of
\displaystyle\beta
,
\displaystyle g_{2}
vanishes at most three times.
∎

Back to the proof of Proposition
3
, we now set
\displaystyle n_{0}=3\ell+3
, and if
\displaystyle n>n_{0}
then it shows that
\displaystyle A(\boldsymbol{p},h)>0
. We denote
\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{p})=\min_{h\in\mathbb{R}^{3},h\neq 0}a(\boldsymbol{p},h)/\|h\|_{2}^{2}
. This function is continuous on a subset
\displaystyle U_{1}\subset U
, and we denote by
\displaystyle m
the minimum of
\displaystyle\lambda_{1}
on
\displaystyle U_{1}
. We also notice that

|b(\boldsymbol{p},h)|\leq\|z\mathcal{A}(\beta-\alpha X)-\boldsymbol{d}\|_{1}(\|\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}+2z\|\mathcal{A}^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty})(1+\|\boldsymbol{t}\|_{\infty})\|h\|_{2}^{2}.

(73)

We see that

\|z\mathcal{A}(\beta-\alpha X)-\boldsymbol{d}\|_{1}=\|F(\boldsymbol{p})-\boldsymbol{d}\|_{1}\leq\|F(\boldsymbol{p})-F(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})\|_{1}+\|\boldsymbol{d}_{0}-\boldsymbol{d}\|_{1}.

(74)

There exists a constant
\displaystyle M>0
depending on
\displaystyle U
such that for every
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{p}\in U
,

\|F(\boldsymbol{p})-F(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})\|_{1}\leq M\|\boldsymbol{p}-\boldsymbol{p}_{0}\|_{1}.

(75)

We define
\displaystyle U_{2}=U_{1}\cap B_{1}(p_{0},\varepsilon_{1}/M)
where

\varepsilon_{1}:=\frac{m}{4(\|\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}+2z_{\max}\|\mathcal{A}^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty})(1+\max(|x_{1}|,|x_{n}|))}.

(76)

It follows that for every
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{p}\in U_{2}
and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{d}\in B_{1}(\boldsymbol{d}_{0},\varepsilon_{1})
,

\|F(\boldsymbol{p})-F(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})\|_{1}\leq\varepsilon_{1},\quad|b(\boldsymbol{p},h)|\leq\frac{m}{2}\|h\|_{2}^{2}.

(77)

The operator
\displaystyle J_{\boldsymbol{d}}
is then strictly convex on
\displaystyle U_{2}
since

\|\nabla^{2}J_{\boldsymbol{d}}(\boldsymbol{p})\|_{2}\geq\min_{h\in\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{R}^{2},h\neq 0}\frac{|A(\boldsymbol{p},h)|-|B(\boldsymbol{p},h)|}{\|h\|_{2}^{2}}\geq\frac{m}{2}>0

(78)

We now need to prove that the minimum of
\displaystyle J_{\boldsymbol{d}}
is located inside of
\displaystyle U_{2}
and not on its boundary. To do so, we look for a point
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{p}\in U_{2}
such that
\displaystyle\nabla J_{\boldsymbol{d}}(\boldsymbol{p})=0
. We already know that
\displaystyle\nabla J_{\boldsymbol{d}_{0}}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0})=0

\displaystyle V\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}

\displaystyle B_{2}(\boldsymbol{d}_{0},\varepsilon)\subset G_{1}^{-1}(U^{\prime})\cap B_{1}(\boldsymbol{d}_{0},\varepsilon_{1})

G:\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}B_{2}(\boldsymbol{d}_{0},\varepsilon)&\rightarrow&U^{\prime}\\

(79)

is well-defined and continuously differentiable. Moreover, we also know that

\partial_{d_{j}}G(\boldsymbol{d})=-[\nabla^{2}J_{\boldsymbol{d}}(G(\boldsymbol{d}))]^{-1}[\partial_{d_{j}}\nabla J_{\boldsymbol{d}}(G(\boldsymbol{d}))].

(80)

We denote
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{LS}}=G(\boldsymbol{d})

\|\partial_{d_{j}}\nabla J_{\boldsymbol{d}}(\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{LS}})\|_{2}=\left\|\left(\begin{array}[]{c}-\mathcal{A}(\beta_{\text{LS}}-\alpha_{\text{LS}}t_{j})\\

\|\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{LS}}-\boldsymbol{p}_{0}\|_{2}=\|G(\boldsymbol{d})-G(\boldsymbol{d}_{0})\|_{2}\leq\frac{2\sqrt{n}c_{1}}{m}\|\boldsymbol{d}-\boldsymbol{d}_{0}\|_{2}.

|\Lambda(\boldsymbol{d})-\Lambda(\boldsymbol{d}_{0})|\leq\frac{1}{\alpha_{\min}}|\beta_{\text{LS}}-\beta_{0}|+\frac{\beta_{\max}}{(\alpha_{\min})^{2}}|\alpha_{\text{LS}}-\beta_{\text{LS}}|\leq\left(\frac{1}{\alpha_{\min}}+\frac{\beta_{\max}}{(\alpha_{\min})^{2}}\right)\frac{2\sqrt{n}c_{1}}{m}\|\boldsymbol{d}-\boldsymbol{d}_{0}\|_{2}.

(83)
