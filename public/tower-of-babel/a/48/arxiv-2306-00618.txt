Effective Structured Prompting by Meta-Learning and Representative Verbalizer
Weisen Jiang, Yu Zhang, James T. Kwok

Abstract
Prompt tuning for pre-trained masked language models (MLM) has shown promising performance in natural language processing tasks with few labeled examples. It tunes a prompt for the downstream task, and a verbalizer is used to bridge the predicted token and label prediction. Due to the limited training data, prompt initialization is crucial for prompt tuning. Recently, MetaPrompting ( Hou et al. 2022 ) uses meta-learning to learn a shared initialization for all task-specific prompts. However, a single initialization is insufficient to obtain good prompts for all tasks and samples when the tasks are complex. Moreover, MetaPrompting requires tuning the whole MLM, causing a heavy burden on computation and memory as the MLM is usually large. To address these issues, we use a prompt pool to extract more task knowledge and construct instance-dependent prompts via attention. We further propose a novel soft verbalizer (RepVerb) which constructs label embedding from feature embeddings directly. Combining meta-learning the prompt pool and RepVerb, we propose MetaPrompter for effective structured prompting. MetaPrompter is parameter-efficient as only the pool is required to be tuned. Experimental results demonstrate that MetaPrompter performs better than the recent state-of-the-arts and RepVerb outperforms existing soft verbalizers. ;

;

.

Prompt learning

The continuous prompt can be further combined

;

;

)
.

)
.

In this paper,
we use a pool

(

;
Wang et al. 2022a
;
Wang et al. 2022b
)

to extract task knowledge from meta-training tasks, and then construct instance-dependent prompts

)
.
The attention’s query vector is the instance’s feature embedding.
The prompt pool is the shared meta-knowledge and learned by the MAML algorithm

)

Specifically,

)

(
Zhang et al. 2022
)
, ProtoVerb
(
Cui et al. 2022
)
.
Besides,
the feature embedding learned by RepVerb is more discriminative.

The whole procedure, which
combines meta-learning the structured prompts and RepVerb,

Experiments are performed on six widely used classification data sets.
Results demonstrate that RepVerb outperforms existing soft verbalizers, and
is also beneficial to other prompt-based methods such as MetaPrompting.
Moreover, MetaPrompter
achieves better performance than the recent state-of-the-arts.

Our contributions are summarized as follows:

1.

We propose a
parameter-efficient algorithm MetaPrompter for effective structured prompting.

2.

We propose
a simple and effective soft verbalizer (RepVerb).

3.

Experimental results demonstrate the effectiveness and parameter-efficiency of MetaPrompter.

Preliminaries and Related Work

2.1
Prompt Learning

Recently, it is common to use a
pre-trained MLM
\mathcal{M}(\cdot;{\bm{\phi}})
,
with parameter
{\bm{\phi}}
, for various downstream tasks such as
language understanding
(
Dong et al. 2019
;
Yang et al. 2019
;
Song et al. 2020
)
,
machine translation
(
Conneau & Lample 2019
;
Guo et al. 2020
)
,
and
text classification
(
Brown et al. 2020
;
Lester et al. 2021
;
Liu et al. 2022b
)
.
Given a raw sentence represented as a sequence of
n
tokens
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
,
the MLM takes
{\bf x}=(\text{[CLS]},x_{1},\dots,x_{n},\text{[SEP]})
as input
(where
[CLS]

is the start token
and
[SEP]

is the separator), and encodes it into a sequence of hidden
representations
({\bf h}_{\text{[CLS]}},{\bf h}_{1},\dots,{\bf h}_{n},{\bf h}_{\text{[SEP]}})
. In standard fine-tuning
(
Howard & Ruder 2018
;
Devlin et al. 2019
)
, an extra classifier (e.g., a fully connected layer with softmax
normalization) is added on top of
{\bf h}_{\text{[CLS]}}
to predict the label
distribution.
This classifier,
together with
{\bm{\phi}}
,
are tuned to maximize the probability of correct
labels. As language models are large (e.g.,
175
billion parameters in
GPT-3
(
Brown et al. 2020
)
), fine-tuning all parameters can cause a heavy burden on
computation and memory.

On the other hand,
prompt learning
(
Brown et al. 2020
;
Shin et al. 2020
;
Ding et al. 2022
)

freezes the
pre-trained model and formulates the downstream task as a cloze-style MLM problem.
For example,
in topic classification,
“
Topic is [MASK]
”
can be used
as the
prompt,
where
[MASK]
is a special token for prediction.
The

discrete

tokens “
Topic is
” are also called
anchor tokens.
An
input text

{\bf x}

is wrapped with the prompt and mapped to an input embedding sequence

(\mathcal{E}({\bf x}),\mathcal{E}(\text{Topic}),\mathcal{E}(\text{is}),\mathcal{E}(\text{[MASK]}))
,
where
\mathcal{E}(\cdot)
denotes the input embedding.
Designing a suitable prompt requires domain expertise and a good
understanding of the downstream tasks
(
Brown et al. 2020
;
Sanh et al. 2022
)
. Thus, manually-designed prompts are likely to be sub-optimal.

Unlike discrete prompts, prompt tuning
(
Lester et al. 2021
;
Liu et al. 2021
)
uses a

continuous
prompt
{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{L_{p}\times d_{i}}
(of length
L_{p}
) to
directly wrap the input embedding sequence as
(\mathcal{E}({\bf x}),{\bm{\theta}},\mathcal{E}(\text{[MASK]}))
.
This can be further combined with anchor tokens
to form a
template

(
Liu et al. 2021
;
Schick & Schütze 2021
;
Ding et al. 2022
)
:

\tilde{{\bf x}}\equiv\mathbb{T}({\bf x};{\bm{\theta}})\!\!=\!\!(\mathcal{E}({\bf x}),{\bm{\theta}},\mathcal{E}(\text{Topic}),\mathcal{E}(\text{is}),\mathcal{E}(\text{[MASK]})).

The MLM then outputs
the hidden embedding

{\bf h}_{\text{[MASK]}}(\tilde{{\bf x}})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{o}}

of
[MASK]
,
and infers the token to be filled at the

[MASK]
position.

A
verbalizer

(
Lester et al. 2021
;
Ding et al. 2022
;
Hu et al. 2022b
)

bridges the prediction at the
[MASK]

position
and labels
in prompt learning.
Specifically,
it is a
hard
mapping from each label
y
to a set of label-relevant tokens
\mathcal{V}_{y}
. For example,
for
y=\text{SPORTS}
, we can have

\mathcal{V}_{y}=\{\text{sports, football, basketball}\}
.
Prompt tuning then optimizes

\hat{\mathbb{P}}(y|{\bf x};{\bm{\phi}},{\bm{\theta}})\!=\!\frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}_{y}|}\!\!\sum_{\text{w}\in\mathcal{V}_{y}}\!\!\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M}}(\text{[MASK]}=\text{w}|\mathbb{T}({\bf x};{\bm{\theta}})),

The verbalizer
is crucial to the performance of prompt

(
Lester et al. 2021
;
Ding et al. 2022
)
. However,

requires
intensive human labor. To address this problem, search-based

(
Schick et al. 2020
;
Shin et al. 2020
;
Gao et al. 2021
)
try
to find label tokens

from the training data.
However, searching in a

space is computationally

(
Schick et al. 2020
;
Shin et al. 2020
;
Gao et al. 2021
)
, especially with a

of labels or vocabulary.
Some recent works

(
Hambardzumyan et al. 2021
;
Zhang et al. 2022
;

)

propose

which
map each label to a

WARP
(
Hambardzumyan et al. 2021
)
and DART
(

)

(

)

(

;
Tian et al. 2020
)
.
However,

(
Gao et al. 2019
;
Bao et al. 2020
;
Han et al. 2021
;
Chen et al. 2022
;
Hou et al. 2022
)
,
as the number of samples per class is typically much smaller than

(e.g.,

for BERT
(
Devlin et al. 2019
)
).

2.2
Meta-Learning for Prompt Learning

In meta-learning
(
Bengio et al. 1991
;
Thrun & Pratt 1998
)
,
a collection
\mathcal{T}
of tasks are used to learn a shared
meta-parameter.
Each task
\tau\in\mathcal{T}
has a support set
\mathcal{S}_{\tau}
and a
query set
\mathcal{Q}_{\tau}
. Let
\mathcal{Y}_{\tau}
be the label set of
\tau
.
Typical meta-learning algorithms can be
metric-based
(
Vinyals et al. 2016
;
Snell et al. 2017
;
Bertinetto et al. 2018
;
Lee et al. 2019
)
, memory-based

(
Santoro et al. 2016
;
Munkhdalai & Yu 2017
)
, or
optimization-based
(
Finn et al. 2017
;
Rajeswaran et al. 2019
;
Raghu et al. 2020
;
Ye et al. 2021
;
Jiang et al. 2021
;
Jiang et al. 2022
;
Flennerhag et al. 2022
)
.
In general, the optimization-based approach is preferred due to its simplicity and
effectiveness.
A representative algorithm
is
model-agnostic meta-learning (MAML)
(
Finn et al. 2017
)
.

As prompt tuning is sensitive to prompt initialization in few-shot tasks
(
Lester et al. 2021
)
, meta-learning can be used to search for a good initialization.
MetaPrompting
(
Hou et al. 2022
)

uses
MAML
to learn a meta-initialization for the task-specific
prompts.
At iteration
t
, the base learner takes
a task
\tau
and meta-parameter
({\bm{\phi}}_{t-1},{\bm{\theta}}_{t-1})
, and builds a
task-specific model

({\bm{\phi}}_{t,J},{\bm{\theta}}_{t,J})
by
performing

J
gradient updates on the support set with
step size
\alpha
and initialization
({\bm{\phi}}_{t,0},{\bm{\theta}}_{t,0})\equiv({\bm{\phi}}_{t-1},{\bm{\theta}}_{t-1})
:

\displaystyle({\bm{\phi}}_{t,j},{\bm{\theta}}_{t,j})=({\bm{\phi}}_{t,j-1},{\bm{\theta}}_{{\bf x},j-1})

\displaystyle\;+\!\alpha\nabla_{({\bm{\phi}}_{t,j-1},{\bm{\theta}}_{{\bf x},j-1})}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\sum_{({\bf x},y)\in\mathcal{S}_{\tau}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\log\hat{\mathbb{P}}(y|{\bf x};{\bm{\phi}}_{t,j-1},{\bm{\theta}}_{{\bf x},j-1}).

The meta-learner then updates the meta-initialization by
maximizing the log-likelihood objective
on the query set with
step size
\eta
:

\displaystyle({\bm{\phi}}_{t},{\bm{\theta}}_{t})=({\bm{\phi}}_{t-1},{\bm{\theta}}_{t-1})

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;+\eta\nabla_{({\bm{\phi}}_{t-1},{\bm{\theta}}_{t-1})}\sum_{({\bf x},y)\in\mathcal{Q}_{\tau}}\log\hat{\mathbb{P}}(y|{\bf x};{\bm{\phi}}_{t,J},{\bm{\theta}}_{t,J}).

Though MetaPrompting achieves state-of-the-art performance in the few-shot
classification experiments

(
Hou et al. 2022
)
,
it suffers from the three problems discussed in Section
1
.

1.

When the tasks are complex,
it is challenging to use
a single meta-initialized prompt for adaptation to the various tasks.

2.

MetaPrompting uses
a hand-crafted verbalizer,
which is labor-intensive and not scalable as discussed in Section
2.1
.

3.

MetaPrompting needs to tune the MLM parameters, and thus is not parameter-efficient.

3.1
Representative Verbalizer
(RepVerb)

Instead of explicitly learning an embedding

{\bf v}_{y}

for each label
y

(
Hambardzumyan et al. 2021
;
Cui et al. 2022
;
Zhang et al. 2022
)
, we propose
the
Representative Verbalizer
(RepVerb),
which
constructs

{\bf v}_{y}

from
feature
embeddings of the corresponding training samples
(Algorithm
1
).
It does not require
learning additional parameters, and
is thus more effective on limited data as in few-shot learning.

Specifically, let

be the subset of samples
in
\mathcal{S}_{\tau}

with label

.
For an input
{\bf x}
, we wrap it with the template and feed

to the pre-trained MLM, and then
obtain

’s embedding

as its feature embedding.
Similar to ProtoNet
(
Snell et al. 2017
)
,
we propose to
construct
{\bf v}_{y}

for each

by averaging the corresponding
samples’ feature embeddings, as:

\displaystyle{\bf v}_{y}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}_{\tau,y}|}\sum_{({\bf x},y)\in\mathcal{S}_{\tau,y}}{\bf h}_{\text{[MASK]}}(\tilde{{\bf x}}).

(2)

3.2.1
Meta-Learn a Prompt Pool

While MetaPrompting uses only a single initialization for the prompt,
we propose to leverage
a pool of prompts to extract more task knowledge, which is particularly effective when the tasks
are complex and very different prompts may be
needed.
A prompt pool has
K

\{({\bf k}_{i},{\bm{\theta}}_{i}):i=1,\dots,K\}
, with
key
{\bf k}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{o}}

and
value
{\bm{\theta}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{L_{p}\times d_{i}}

(
Li et al. 2022
;
Wang et al. 2022a
;
Wang et al. 2022b
)
.
Note that the
size of
the prompt pool is negligible
compared with that of the MLM. For example, in our experiments,
the MLM has
109.52\times 10^{6}
parameters,
while the prompt pool has only

55,296
.

The prompt pool can be considered as shared meta-knowledge.
Given an input

{\bf x}
,
the attention weights
between
{\bf x}
and the
K
prompts are computed as
{\bf a}=\softmax(\frac{{\bf K}{\bf q}_{\bf x}}{\sqrt{d_{o}}})
,
where
\softmax(\cdot)
is the softmax function,

{\bf K}=[{\bf k}_{1}^{\top};\dots;{\bf k}_{K}^{\top}]
, and

{\bf q}_{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{o}}
is
the
embedding
of the
[MASK]

output by
a
pre-trained and frozen
MLM with the wrapped input (e.g.,
({\bf x}.\text{ Topic is [MASK]})
)

(
Wang et al. 2022a
;
Wang et al. 2022b
)
.
Such a mapping from
{\bf x}
to
{\bf q}_{\bf x}
is called the query function
q(\cdot)
.
An instance-dependent prompt is then generated by weighted averaging
over all the
values (
{\bm{\theta}}_{i}
’s):

\displaystyle{\bm{\theta}}_{\bf x}({\bf K},{\bm{\Theta}})=\sum_{i=1}^{K}a_{i}{\bm{\theta}}_{i},

(4)

where

{\bm{\Theta}}=[{\bm{\theta}}_{1};\dots;{\bm{\theta}}_{K}]
.
While

Wang et al. 2022a
;
Wang et al. 2022b

only select the
top-
N
most similar prompts
from the pool,
in
(
4
)
all the prompts are
used
and updated simultaneously.

The proposed procedure for meta-learning the
prompt pool

({\bf K},{\bm{\Theta}})
, which will be called
MetaPrompter,
is
shown in
Algorithm
2
. The MAML algorithm
(
Finn et al. 2017
)
is used here, but other meta-learning algorithms (e.g., Reptile
(
Nichol et al. 2018
)
, BMG
(
Flennerhag et al. 2022
)
) can also be used.
At iteration
t
, the base learner takes
({\bf K}_{t-1},{\bm{\Theta}}_{t-1})
and a task
\tau
to
optimize for a task-specific prompt pool
by gradient descent
(steps
0
-
0
).

({\bf K}_{t-1},{\bm{\Theta}}_{t-1})
is used as the initialization (step
0
).
For each inner iteration
j
,

({\bf K}_{t,j-1},{\bm{\Theta}}_{t,j-1})
constructs the
instance-dependent prompts
{\bm{\theta}}_{{\bf x},j}({\bf K}_{t,j-1},{\bm{\Theta}}_{t,j-1})
in
(
4
) (steps

0
and
0
).
Next,
{\bm{\theta}}_{{\bf x},j}
is used to predict the label
probability with a combination of the
hand-crafted
verbalizer
(step
0
) and soft verbalizer
(steps
0
and
0
):

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(y|{\bf x};{\bm{\theta}}_{{\bf x},j})\!=\!(1-\lambda)\hat{\mathbb{P}}(y|{\bf x};{\bm{\theta}}_{{\bf x},j})+\lambda\tilde{\mathbb{P}}(y|{\bf x};{\bm{\theta}}_{{\bf x},j}),

(5)

where
\lambda\in[0,1]
(in the experiments,
we set
\lambda=0.5
).
Let
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{S}_{\tau};{\bf K}_{t,j-1},{\bm{\Theta}}_{\!t,j-1})=-\sum_{({\bf x},y)\in\mathcal{S}_{\tau}}\log\mathbb{P}\left(y|{\bf x};{\bm{\theta}}_{{\bf x},j}\right)
be the loss on
\mathcal{S}_{\tau}
(step
0
).
The base learner builds a task-specific prompt pool
({\bf K}_{t,J},\!{\bm{\Theta}}_{t,J})

by taking
J
gradient updates
(
j=1,\dots,J
) at step
0
:

\displaystyle\!\!\!({\bf K}_{t,j\!},\!\!{\bm{\Theta}}_{\!t,j}\!)\!\!=\!\!({\bf K}_{t,j-1\!},\!\!{\bm{\Theta}}_{\!t,j-1}\!)\!\!-\!\alpha\!\nabla_{\!\!\scalebox{0.4}{$({\bf K}_{t,j-1\!},\!{\bm{\Theta}}_{\!t,j-1}\!)$}}\mathcal{L}\!(\!\mathcal{S}_{\tau};\!{\bf K}_{t,j-1\!},\!\!{\bm{\Theta}}_{\!t,j-1}\!).\!\!\!

The meta-learner takes
({\bf K}_{t,J},\!{\bm{\Theta}}_{t,J})
and
\mathcal{Q}_{\tau}

to update the meta-parameters (steps
0
-
0
). For

({\bf x},y)\in\mathcal{Q}_{\tau}
, we use
({\bf K}_{t,J},\!{\bm{\Theta}}_{t,J})
to generate
its prompt
{\bm{\theta}}_{{\bf x},J}({\bf K}_{t,J},{\bm{\Theta}}_{t,J})
(steps
0
and
0
),
which is used for make prediction
\mathbb{P}\left(y|{\bf x};{\bm{\theta}}_{{\bf x},J}\right)
(steps
0
and
0
).
Let
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{Q}_{\tau};{\bf K}_{t,J},\!{\bm{\Theta}}_{t,J})=-\sum_{({\bf x},y)\in\mathcal{Q}_{\tau}}\log\mathbb{P}\left(y|{\bf x};{\bm{\theta}}_{{\bf x},J}\right)
be the negative log-likelihood loss on
\mathcal{Q}_{\tau}
(step
0
).
The meta-learner updates the meta-parameters by performing one gradient descent step
on
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{Q}_{\tau};{\bf K}_{t,J},\!{\bm{\Theta}}_{t,J})

at step
0
:

\displaystyle\!\!\!({\bf K}_{t},\!{\bm{\Theta}}_{t}\!)\!=\!({\bf K}_{t-1},\!{\bm{\Theta}}_{t-1}\!)\!-\!\eta\nabla_{\!\!\scalebox{0.65}{$({\bf K}_{t-1\!},\!{\bm{\Theta}}_{\!t-1}\!)$}}\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{Q}_{\tau};\!{\bf K}_{t,J},\!{\bm{\Theta}}_{t,J}\!).

The meta-gradient
\nabla_{({\bf K}_{t-1},{\bm{\Theta}}_{t-1})}\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{Q}_{\tau};{\bf K}_{t,J},{\bm{\Theta}}_{t,J})=\nabla_{({\bf K}_{t,J},{\bm{\Theta}}_{t,J})}\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{Q}_{\tau};{\bf K}_{t,J},{\bm{\Theta}}_{t,J})\nabla_{({\bf K}_{t-1},{\bm{\Theta}}_{t-1})}({\bf K}_{t,J},{\bm{\Theta}}_{t,J})
requires back-propagating through the entire
inner optimization path, which is computationally infeasible for large models and
J
is large.
To reduce the computational cost,
we discard the second-order
derivative and use the first-order approximation

\nabla_{({\bf K}_{t-1},{\bm{\Theta}}_{t-1})}\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{Q}_{\tau};{\bf K}_{t,J},{\bm{\Theta}}_{t,J})\approx\nabla_{({\bf K}_{t,J},{\bm{\Theta}}_{t,J}\!)}\mathcal{L}(\!\mathcal{Q}_{\tau};\!{\bf K}_{t,J},\!{\bm{\Theta}}_{t,J}\!)

(step
0
)
as in
(
Finn et al. 2017
;
Hou et al. 2022
)
.

3.2.2
Meta-Testing

Given an unseen task
\tau^{\prime}=(\mathcal{S}_{\tau^{\prime}},\mathcal{Q}_{\tau^{\prime}})
,
the base learner takes

\mathcal{S}_{\tau^{\prime}}

and
({\bf K}_{T},{\bm{\Theta}}_{T})

to build a task-specific prompt pool

({\bf K}_{T,J},{\bm{\Theta}}_{T,J})
as in steps

0
-
0
.
This pool is then used to construct instance-dependent prompts
{\bm{\theta}}_{{\bf x},J}
for each

({\bf x},\cdot)\in\mathcal{Q}_{\tau^{\prime}}
.
The MLM receives the wrapped input
\tilde{{\bf x}}\equiv\mathbb{T}({\bf x};{\bm{\theta}}_{{\bf x},J})

and predicts the label probability by (
5
).

3.2.3
MetaPrompter is Parameter-Efficient

As MetaPrompter only tunes
({\bf K},{\bm{\Theta}})
, the total number of meta-parameters is

K(d_{o}+L_{p}d_{i})

(where
d_{i}
and
d_{o}
are the dimensions of the input and feature embeddings, respectively).
This is much smaller than that of MetaPrompting (which is equal to
d_{\bm{\phi}}+L_{p}d_{i}
, where
d_{\bm{\phi}}
is the size of
{\bm{\phi}}
), as it requires tuning the whole
MLM. For example, in the experiments,
we use BERT
(with
d_{o}=d_{i}=768
,
d_{\bm{\phi}}=109\times 10^{6}
)
and

K=L_{p}=8

in MetaPrompter.

Evaluation on MetaPrompter

We compare MetaPrompter with a variety of

state-of-the-art prompt-based methods of

1.

MetaPrompting
(
Hou et al. 2022
)
,
and its variants

2.

MetaPrompting+WARP / MetaPrompting+ProtoVerb / MetaPrompting+RepVerb,
which combine MetaPrompting
with the soft verbalizer of WARP / ProtoVerb / RepVerb, respectively.
Moreover, we also compare with the
non-prompt-based methods of:

3.

HATT
(
Gao et al. 2019
)
, which
meta-learns a
prototypical network
(
Snell et al. 2017
)
with a hybrid attention mechanism;

4.

DS
(
Bao et al. 2020
)

5.

MLADA
(
Han et al. 2021
)
, which uses an

to extract domain-invariant features
during meta-training; and

6.

ContrastNet
(
Chen et al. 2022
)

contrastive learning.

For MetaPrompter, hyperparameters
K
and
L_{p}

are chosen from
\{1,2,4,8,16,32,64\}

using the meta-validation set.
For the base learner,

\alpha=0.1
, and

J=5
(resp.
15
) at meta-training (resp. meta-validation or meta-testing).
We train the prompt pool
for
T=3,000
iterations using the Adam optimizer
(
Kingma & Ba 2015
)
with a learning rate of
0.001
.
To prevent overfitting, we evaluate the meta-validation
performance every
50
iteration and choose the checkpoint with the best meta-validation performance for meta-testing.
For the hand-crafted verbalizer
used in (
1
),
label tokens are obtained by tokenizing the class name and its synonyms as in
(
Hou et al. 2022
;
Hu et al. 2022b
)
.
Following
Lester et al. 2021
,
prompts are initialized from input

5
Conclusion

In this paper,
we proposed
MetaPrompter, an effective and parameter-efficient algorithm
for prompt tuning.
It combines structured prompting and a novel
verbalizer called RepVerb.
A prompt pool structure is used to construct instance-dependent prompts
by attention,
while
RepVerb
builds label embedding
by averaging feature embeddings of the corresponding
training samples.
The pool of prompts is meta-learned from the meta-training tasks.
Experimental results
demonstrate the effectiveness of
the proposed
MetaPrompter
and RepVerb.

One limitation is that MetaPrompter is based on meta-learning, and so requires the availability of a set of
meta-training tasks.

Appendix A
Implementation of WARP

WARP
(
Hambardzumyan et al. 2021
)

is developed for supervised learning with limited samples.
The proposed RepVerb
(Algorithm
1
)
is also designed for the supervised learning setting.
In the
meta-learning procedure in
Algorithm
2
,
it is used in
the inner level
(steps
0
-
0
) which
is also supervised.

Given a meta-testing
task
\tau^{\prime}=(\mathcal{S}_{\tau^{\prime}},\mathcal{Q}_{\tau^{\prime}})
with label set
\mathcal{Y}_{\tau^{\prime}}
,
let
{\bf V}\equiv\{{\bf v}_{y}:y\in\mathcal{Y}_{\tau^{\prime}}\}
be
\tau^{\prime}
’s learnable label
embeddings, and
{\bm{\phi}}
be the
MLM
parameter.
For an input
{\bf x}
,
the
distribution
for labels
y\in\mathcal{Y}_{\tau^{\prime}}

is predicted as:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(y|{\bf x};{\bm{\phi}},{\bf V})=\frac{\exp({\bf v}_{y}^{\top}{\bf h}_{\text{[MASK]}}(\tilde{{\bf x}}))}{\sum_{y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\tau}}\exp({\bf v}_{y^{\prime}}^{\top}{\bf h}_{\text{[MASK]}}(\tilde{{\bf x}}))},

(6)

where
{\bf h}_{\text{[MASK]}}(\tilde{{\bf x}})
is the
[MASK]
’s embedding of wrapped input
\tilde{{\bf x}}
.

({\bm{\phi}},{\bf V})

is learned by performing

T=5
gradient updates
to minimize the negative log-likelihood loss
on the support set
\mathcal{S}_{\tau^{\prime}}
:

\displaystyle\sum_{({\bf x},y)\in\mathcal{S}_{\tau^{\prime}}}-\log\mathbb{P}(y|{\bf x};{\bm{\phi}},{\bf V}).

{\bm{\phi}}
is initialized by the pre-trained MLM,
while
{\bf V}
is initialized randomly.
The learned
({\bm{\phi}},{\bf V})
is then evaluated on
\mathcal{Q}_{\tau^{\prime}}
.
For a test sample
({\bf x}^{\star},\cdot)\in\mathcal{Q}_{\tau^{\prime}}
, its prediction is
given by (
6
). We run
the WARP algorithm
on all meta-testing tasks and report the average meta-testing accuracy in Table
2
.
