Free-Form Variational Inference for Gaussian Process State-Space Models
Xuhui Fan, Edwin V. Bonilla, Terence J. O’Kane, Scott A. Sisson

Abstract
Gaussian process state-space models (GPSSMs) provide a principled and flexible approach to modeling the dynamics of a latent state, which is observed at discrete-time points via a likelihood model. However, inference in GPSSMs is computationally and statistically challenging due to the large number of latent variables in the model and the strong temporal dependencies between them. In this paper, we propose a new method for inference in Bayesian GPSSMs, which overcomes the drawbacks of previous approaches, namely over-simplified assumptions, and high computational requirements. Our method is based on free-form variational inference via stochastic gradient Hamiltonian Monte Carlo within the inducing-variable formalism. Furthermore, by exploiting our proposed variational distribution, we provide a collapsed extension of our method where the inducing variables are marginalized analytically. We also showcase results when combining our framework with particle MCMC methods. We show that, on six real-world datasets, our approach can learn transition dynamics and latent states more accurately than competing methods.

Introduction

State-space models

(
ssms
;
Murphy 2023
, Ch. 29)
characterize the underlying dynamics of a latent state given a set of observations via a transition function and an observation model.
As a modeling framework, they provide a general approach for understanding time-series data

(

)

Applications of

In this paper, we focus on Bayesian

A paradigmatic example of Bayesian

are

(

Due to their Bayesian non-parametric nature,

Having a

Within the

In outlining the most relevant approaches, our main object of interest is the approximate joint posterior over state trajectories

We consider two main aspects of this joint distribution: (i) whether the dependencies between state trajectories and inducing variables are captured and (ii) whether their corresponding distributions are unconstrained, i.e., not restricted to a sub-optimal parametric form.
The seminal

As shown by

Similarly, a Gaussian assumption on the state posterior or the inducing variable posterior is also very strong and, by definition, will not generally capture the true posterior even in the limit of infinite computation.
To address these issues, we propose a free-form variational inference approach to posterior estimation in

that models the full joint distribution over states and inducing variables,

Inference in Gaussian Process Models

Gaussian processs (
gps
)
are priors over functions where every subset of function values follow a Gaussian distribution.
We use
f({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}})\sim{\mathcal{GP}}\left(m_{f}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}),\kappa_{f}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}^{\prime};{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}})\right)
to denote that
f
is distributed according to a
gp
with mean function
m_{f}(\cdot)
and covariance function
\kappa_{f}(\cdot,\cdot;{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}})
, where
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}
are referred to as the
gp
hyper-parameters
2
2

2

We have simply assumed identity mean functions.
.
By definition, a
gp
prior over functions implies a finite prior over
T
function values
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}=[f({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{1}),\ldots,f({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{T})]^{\top}
, i.e.,
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}\sim\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}};{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}})
, where
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}
are obtained by evaluating the mean function and covariance function at all the inputs
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}:=\{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{T}\}
.

In supervised learning settings, we are given input-output observations
\{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t},y_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
and a conditional likelihood model
p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}})
. Inference involves estimating the posterior distribution
p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}})
and the hyper-parameters
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}
from data. Consequently, we can use these to estimate the posterior predictive distribution at a new point
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{\star}
, i.e.,
p(f({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{\star})\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{\star})
. Notoriously, these tasks have cubic time complexity as a function of the number of training observations, i.e.,
\mathcal{O}(T^{3})
, arising from algebraic operations involving the computation of the inverse covariance and its log determinant. This motivates the need for sparse approximations.

2.1
Sparse
gp
Approximations via Inducing Variables

To deal with the cubic computational complexity of inference in
gp
models, we focus on inducing-variable approximations based on variational inference, as originally proposed by
Titsias 2009
and made scalable to very large datasets by
Hensman et al. 2013
.
The main idea of these approximations is to augment the space of function values with a set of
M
inducing variables
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}:=\{u_{i}\}
and their corresponding inducing inputs
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}}:=\{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{i}\}
.
Thus, inference involves estimating the posterior
q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}})\approx p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}})
and the
gp
hyper-parameters
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}
via variational inference.
Under the assumption that
q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}):=q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}})p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}})
where
p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}})
is the conditional prior, the variational objective, the so-called
evidence lower bound (
elbo
)
, decomposes over the observations, and inference can be carried out with time complexity of
\mathcal{O}(M^{3})
, providing significant advantages when
M\ll T
.

3
Gaussian Process State-Space Models

Let us assume we are given a time series of
T
multi-dimensional observations
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{1:T}
and denote their corresponding latent states with
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T}
, where
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0}
is the initial state. Here we denote the time series
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t_{1}:t_{2}}:=\{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t_{1}},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t_{2}}\}
, and similarly for
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{1:T}
.
In general,
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
.

Gaussian process state-space models (
gpssms
)
formulate a discrete-time
state-space model (
ssm
)
where the transition dynamics is given by a
gp
. The full generative process is:

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0}

\displaystyle\sim p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0}),\text{ }f({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}})\sim{\mathcal{GP}}(m_{f}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}),\kappa_{f}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}^{\prime};{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}})),

(1)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{t}

\displaystyle:=f({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t-1}),\quad{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t};{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}),

(2)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{t}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}

\displaystyle\sim p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{t}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}),

(3)

where
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}
is the transition process covariance,
and
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}
is the vector of parameters of the conditional likelihood
p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{t}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}})
.
Although our framework does not make any parametric assumptions about this conditional likelihood, in our experiments in
Section

7
, we adopt the same setting as in previous works
(
Ialongo et al. 2019
;
Doerr et al. 2018
)
and set
p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{t}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}):=\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{t};{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{C}}}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}+{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{d}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}})
, with
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}=\{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{C}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{d}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}}\}
, where
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{C}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{d}}}
are the weights and bias of the linear transformation on
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}
and
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}}
is the observation covariance.

3.1
Joint Distribution

As shown by
Frigola 2015
, to sample
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{t}
, instead of conditioning on an infinite-dimensional function, we can condition only on the transitions seen up to (but not including) time
t
, i.e.,
\{({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{i-1},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{t-1}
. We can then write the joint distribution over latent variables and observations as:

p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{1:T},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{1:T})=\\
p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0})\prod_{t=1}^{T}p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{t}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{1:t-1},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:t-1})p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{t})p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{t}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}),

(4)

where
p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0}),p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{t}),p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{t}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t})
are defined as above and, for the edge case of
t=1
, we have:
p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{1}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0})=\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{1};m_{f}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0}),\kappa_{f}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0};{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}))
.
Furthermore, we recognize each conditional distribution
p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{t}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{1:t-1},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:t-1})
in
Eq.

4
as the
gp
prediction at a single point
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t-1}
using noiseless outputs
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{1:t-1}
and inputs
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:t-2}
,

\displaystyle p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{t}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{1:t-1},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:t-1})

\displaystyle=p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{t}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t-1},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{1:t-1},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:t-2})

(5)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{t};{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}}_{f},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{f}),

(6)

with conditional mean and covariance given by

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}}_{f}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}}}_{t-1}+{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{t-1,0:t-2}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{0:t-2}^{-1}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{1:t-1}-{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}}}_{0:T-2})

(7)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{f}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{t-1}-{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{t-1,0:t-2}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{0:t-2}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{0:t-2,t-1}),

(8)

where the subscript notation indicates the mean vectors and covariance matrices obtained from evaluating the mean function and covariance function, respectively, at the corresponding ranges,
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}}}_{t_{1}:t_{2}}:=m_{f}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t_{1}:t_{2}})
,
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}}}_{t}:={\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}}}_{t:t}
,
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{t_{1}:t_{2},t_{3}:t_{4}}:=\kappa_{f}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t_{1}:t_{2}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t_{3}:t_{4}};{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}})
,
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{t_{1}:t_{2}}:={\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{t_{1}:t_{2},t_{1}:t_{2}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{t}:={\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{t:t}
.

3.2
Multidimensional Latent States & Control Inputs

In the case of multidimensional latent states, i.e.,
d_{x}>1
, we assume independent
gps
on each dimension, each with its own mean function and covariance function.
Because of this independence assumption, each
gp
only has to condition on its own function evaluations. Therefore, for simplicity in the notation, we do not index the means and covariances with respect to their dimension
d
and consider the underlying
gps
, their means, and covariance functions as multi-dimensional.
Furthermore, as we shall see in
Section

7
, our experiments consider additional control inputs (
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{a}}}_{t}
) that affect the transitions in a Markovian way. This is easy to incorporate in our framework by augmenting our input space and, therefore, indexing the
gps
in the higher-dimensional space given by the concatenation
[{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}^{\top},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{a}}}^{\top}]^{\top}
. However, since the control inputs are fixed and deterministic, we do not need to include them as part of out inference method and do not make them explicit in our subsequent mathematical development.

3.3
Sparse
gpssm

In order to make the
gpssm
inherently scalable, similarly to the standard supervised regression setting with
gps
described in
Section

2
, we augment the full
gpssm
model with
M
inducing variables and corresponding inducing inputs
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}:=\{u_{i}\}_{i=1}^{M}
and
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}}:=\{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{M}
.
This gives rise to the sparse
gpssm
model:

p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{1:T},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{1:T},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}})=p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}})p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0})\\
\prod_{t=1}^{T}p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{t}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{1:t-1},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:t-1},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}})p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{t})p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{t}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}),

(9)

where the prior over the inducing variables is determined by the
gp
prior, i.e.,
p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}})=\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}}}_{Z},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{Z})

with
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}}}_{Z}:=m_{f}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}})
and
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{Z}:=\kappa_{f}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}};{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}})
.
Besides having a prior over the inducing variables
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}
, the main difference with our previous full-model formulation of
Section

3.1
is that the conditional distributions over
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{t}
have been augmented with inducing variables
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}
and corresponding inducing inputs
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}}
.
Each conditional,
p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{t}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{1:t-1},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:t-1},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}})=p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{t}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t-1},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{1:t-1},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:t-2},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}})
, is the predictive (
gp
regression) distribution of
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{t}
at test input
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t-1}
when observing
({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{1:t-1},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}})
at their respective locations
({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:t-2},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}})
.

4
Free-Form Variational Inference

In this section, we develop a posterior estimation method using
variational inference (
vi
)
. We show that under a specific form of the approximate posterior, we can estimate the joint posterior over
\{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}\}
in free-form, i.e., optimally, without making any assumptions such as independence between state trajectories and inducing variables, typical of mean-field approaches
(
Frigola et al. 2014
;
Eleftheriadis et al. 2017
)
, or constraining the form of the posterior to sub-optimal parametric forms
(
Ialongo et al. 2019
;
Doerr et al. 2018
)
.

4.1
Variational Family and Evidence Lower Bound

Variational inference
is underpinned by the maximization of the
evidence lower bound (
elbo
)
, which is equivalent to minimizing the
Kullback-Leibler (
kl
)
divergence between the approximate posterior and the true posterior. This objective is given by

\mathcal{L}_{\text{ELBO}}(q):=\mathbb{E}_{q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{1:T})}\left[\log{p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{1:T},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{1:T},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}})}\right.\\
\left.-\log{q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{1:T})}\right],

(10)

where
p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{1:T},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{1:T},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}})
is the joint distribution in
Eq.

9
and
q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{1:T})
is our proposed approximate joint posterior, which we define as

\displaystyle q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{1:T}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}})

\displaystyle:=\prod_{t=1}^{T}p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{t}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{1:t-1},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:t-1},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}}),

(11)

\displaystyle q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{1:T})

\displaystyle:=q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}})q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{1:T}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}).

(12)

It is easy to show that the
elbo
, as defined in
Eq.

10
, is a lower bound on the log marginal likelihood, i.e.,
\mathcal{L}_{\text{ELBO}}(q)\leq\log p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}})
.
We see that our joint variational posterior over state trajectories
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T}
, inducing variables
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}
and latent function values
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{1:T}
in
Eq.

12

uses the conditional prior over the latent function values in
Eq.

11
. This is, of course, an assumption and limits the flexibility of our approximate posterior. However, all previous scalable variational approaches to
gpssms
, such as
vgpssm
,
igpssm
,
prssm
and
vcdt
, have made the very same assumption.
Indeed, these types of approximations where the conditional prior is used to define the join variational posterior have become customary and
necessary
to avoid the cubic time complexity of inference in
gp
models.
See
Section

8
for further discussion on the limitations of our approach.

Nevertheless, our proposed joint variational distribution in
Eq.

12
, will allow us to derive an
optimal
variational distribution
q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}})
in free-form, without imposing any
parametric constraints over it and, instead, represent it via samples.
We will describe this in the next section.

4.2
Evidence Lower Bound Maximization

Our first step is to expand the expression for the
elbo
in
Eq.

10
using our joint model distribution in
Eq.

9
and our proposed variational distribution in
Eqs.

11
and
12
. We first note that our definition of the variational distribution in
Eq.

12
uses the same conditional prior as in the joint distribution in
Eq.

9
. Therefore, this term cancels out, avoiding the computation of operations on fully-coupled high-dimensional distributions over latent functions. Thus, we have that:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{ELBO}}(q)=\int q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}})\Bigg\{-\log q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}})

\displaystyle+\log\Big[p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0})p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}})\prod_{t=1}^{T}p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{t}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t})\Big]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}_{q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{1:T}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}})}\Big[\log\prod_{t=1}^{T}p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{t})\Big]\Bigg\}\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T}\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}.

(13)

Next we aim to maximize the
elbo
functional above with respect to
q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T-1},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}})
subject to the constraint

\int q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T-1},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}})\ \mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T-1}\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}=1
. We can do this by solving the corresponding Euler-Lagrange equation:

\frac{\partial}{\partial q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}})}\Bigg\{-q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}})\log q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}})\\
+q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}})\log\Big[p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0})p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}})\prod_{t=1}^{T}p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{t}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t})\Big]\\
+q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}})\mathbb{E}_{q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{1:T}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}})}\Big[\log\prod_{t=1}^{T}p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{t})\Big]\Bigg\}=0.

(14)

By doing the corresponding derivatives we obtain

-\log q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}})-1+\log\Big[p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0})p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}})\prod_{t=1}^{T}p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{t}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t})\Big]\\
+\mathbb{E}_{q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{1:T}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}})}\Big[\log\prod_{t=1}^{T}p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{t})\Big]=0.

(15)

Now the expectation above can be solved in closed form:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{1:T}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}})}\Big[\log\prod_{t=1}^{T}p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{t})\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{t}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t-1},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}})}\log p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{t})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left[\log\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t};{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}_{x_{t}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}})-\frac{1}{2}\trace({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}}_{t-1})\right],

(16)

where
p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{t}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t-1},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}})
is the
gp
predictive distribution over the function values
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{t}
at locations
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t-1}
given the inducing variables
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}
at inducing inputs
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{t}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t-1},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{t};{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}}_{x_{t}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}}_{t-1}),

(17)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}}_{x_{t}}

\displaystyle:={\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}}}_{t-1}+{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}_{t-1}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}-{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}}}_{Z}).

(18)

Here we have defined

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}_{t-1}

\displaystyle:={\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{t-1,Z}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{Z}^{-1},

(19)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}}_{t-1}

\displaystyle:={\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{t-1}-{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{t-1,Z}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{Z}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{Z,t-1},

(20)

and the cross-covariance term
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{t-1,Z}:=\kappa_{f}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t-1},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}};{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}})
and similarly for
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{Z,t-1}
. Finally,
\trace(\cdot)
is the trace operator and, as defined at the beginning of
Section

3
,
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}
is the transition noise covariance.

4.2.1
Optimal Variational Posterior

With this, we obtain the form of the optimal variational distribution
q^{*}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T})
up to a normalizing constant
Z_{q}
as:

\log q^{*}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T})=\log p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}})+\log p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0})\\
+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Big[\log p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{t}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t})+\log\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t};{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}}_{x_{t}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}})\\
-\frac{1}{2}\trace({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}}_{t-1})\Big]+\log Z_{q}.

(21)

Here we note that the function values
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{1:T}
have, effectively, been marginalized variationally.
The optimal joint posterior over inducing variables and state trajectories depends on the prior over the inducing variables
p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}})
stemming from the
gp
functional prior, the prior over the initial state
p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0})
and the conditional likelihood terms
p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{t}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t})
.
It also depends on the resulting transitions mapping
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t-1}
to
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}
via the densities
\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t};{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}}_{x_{t}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}})
, where
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}}_{x_{t}}
depends on
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t-1}
in a nonlinear way, as specified by
Eq.

19
.
The final trace term,
\trace({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}}_{t-1})
, can be seen as a regularization term acting on state transitions, encouraging higher transition variances and, therefore, helping prevent overfitting.

4.2.2
Alternative Perspective

An alternative way to obtain the optimal joint posterior over state trajectories and inducing variable is by bounding the true log joint marginal

\log p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{1:T},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}})
using Jensen’s inequality. We give the details in
Appendix

E
. This has been used by previous work in standard regression settings
(see, e.g.,
Rossi et al. 2021
, and references therein)
.
However, our setting considers the more complex case of
gpssms
.
Additionally, our development shows much more clearly the optimal nature of the variational posterior, as we have obtained it via calculus of variations.

4.3
Posterior Sampling

Having the form of the optimal posterior in
Eq.

21
, we can then set the latent variables
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Psi}}}:=\{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T}\}
and have
\tilde{q}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Psi}}})\propto q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Psi}}})\approx p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Psi}}}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{1:T})
. Thus, we can draw samples from our approximate posterior using
stochastic gradient Hamiltonian Monte Carlo

(
Chen et al. 2014
;
Havasi et al. 2018
,
sghmc
;)
and the energy function
U({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Psi}}})=-\log p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Psi}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{1:T})=-\log p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Psi}}}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{1:T})+\log Z_{q}\approx-\log\tilde{q}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Psi}}})+\log Z_{q}
.
Using this procedure, samples from the target distribution can be obtained even with noisy gradients (e.g., with mini-batches) without requiring the evaluation of Metropolis ratios. Importantly, other variables such as
gp
hyper-parameters
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}
and inducing locations
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}}
can be easily included in
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Psi}}}
using suitable priors and incorporating them in our objective in
Eq.

21
.

4.3.1
Computational Cost & Prior Whitening

An interesting aspect of
gpssm
models is that, despite their apparent Markovian nature, sampling at time
T
requires conditioning on all the previous
T-1
points.
This is due to the non-parametric coupled
gp
prior over the transition function, making inference in the full model
\mathcal{O}(T^{3})
in time.
Sparse variational inference approaches, such as those based on inducing variable approximations, still require expectations over entire trajectories and, unlike standard supervised i.i.d settings, their time complexity is inherently dependent on
T
.
Evaluation of the
stochastic gradient Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (
sghmc
)
objective in
Eq.

21
for sampling in our
free-form variational dynamics (
ffvd
)
algorithm is
\mathcal{O}(M^{2}T)
. this is the same cost as that attained for
elbo
evaluation in
variationally coupled dynamic trajectories (
vcdt
)
, which like our
ffvd
, models dependencies between state trajectories and inducing variables.

Here we expand on the details of the computational cost of our approach when compared to
vcdt
. Our
sghmc
objective in
Eq.

21
is very similar to the
elbo
used in
vcdt
. The
elbo
in
vcdt
requires expectations over
q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}})
and
q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t-1}|{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}})
. Given the factorization assumptions and the Gaussian constraints on these distributions, these expectations are estimated straightforwardly via Monte Carlo samples. The time complexity of evaluating
Eq.

21
or the
elbo
in
vcdt
once (using one sample) is the same, i.e.,
\mathcal{O}(M^{3}+M^{2}T)
.

The overall time complexity of both algorithms,
ffvd
and
vcdt
, depends on (i) the number of samples (noting, again, that
vcdt
also requires samples from the approximate posterior to estimate the gradients of the
elbo
) and (ii) the number of iterations (either the length of the
sghmc
chain in
ffvd
or the number of epochs for gradient-based optimization in
vcdt
). As described in
Appendix

I
, our experimental setting followed closely that of the original
vcdt
paper, which used
S=100
samples for training and
S=10^{5}
for predictions. Similarly, we used
S=100
samples for
ffvd
. Remarkably, the number of iterations in our experiments for
ffvd
was
50,000
while for
vcdt
was
200,000
to achieve convergence. Furthermore, our analysis in
Appendix

J
shows that, in fact, our
ffvd
algorithm converges in less than 10,000 iterations.

As in previous work
(
Hensman et al. 2015
, see, e.g.,)
, we have observed that whitening the prior over the inducing variables improves the performance of our algorithm. See details of our whitening procedure and the resulting unnormalized log posterior in
Appendices

D
and
E.1
.

4.4
Smoothing and Predictive Distributions

We are interested in estimating the smoothing distribution
p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{1:T})
and the predictive distribution
p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{T+1:T^{\prime}}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{1:T})
for
T^{\prime}\geq T
.
At the end of our
sghmc
procedure, we have
S
samples from our joint approximate posterior
q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}})\approx p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{1:T})
, i.e.,
\{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T}^{(s)},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}^{(s)}\}_{s=1}^{S}
and, therefore, the smoothing distribution (i.e., the marginal posterior over
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T}
) is readily available through this Monte Carlo approximation.

We can also make one-step-ahead predictions using our posterior samples. In particular, using
Eqs.

17
and
2
we have that:

p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t-1},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}})=\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t};{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}}}_{t-1}+{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}_{t-1}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}-{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}}}_{Z}),\\
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}}_{t-1}+{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}),

(22)

\forall t>T
. Thus, replacing the values of
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}_{t-1},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}}_{t-1}
using
Eqs.

19
and
20
we can make predictions for the next state using samples as

{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}^{(s)}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t-1}^{(s)},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}^{(s)}\sim\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t};{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}}_{t}^{(s)},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{t}^{(s)})\quad\text{with}\\
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}}_{t}^{(s)}={\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}}}_{t-1}^{(s)}+{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{t-1,Z}^{(s)}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{Z}^{-1}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}^{(s)}-{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}}}_{Z}),\\
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{t}^{(s)}={\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{t-1}^{(s)}-{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{t-1,Z}^{(s)}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{Z}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{Z,t-1}^{(s)}+{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}.

(23)

For a general likelihood model, we can sample the (noisy) targets using

{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{t}^{(s)}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}^{(s)}\sim p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{t}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}^{(s)},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}).

In the case of a Gaussian conditional likelihood, as described in
Section

3
, we can see that given samples from the latent state, the predictive distribution is a Gaussian

{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{t}^{(s)}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t-1}^{(s)},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}^{(s)}\sim\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{t};{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{C}}}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}}_{t}^{(s)}+{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{d}}},\\
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{C}}}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{t}^{(s)}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{C}}}^{\top}+{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}}).

(24)

where the samples of
\{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{T}^{(s)},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}^{(s)}\}
are readily available after running
sghmc
on their joint space.

5
Collapsing Inducing Variables

So far we have described our method to obtain samples from the optimal variational posterior over the joint distribution of state trajectories and inducing variables using
Eq.

21
and
sghmc
.
In this section we show that we can, in fact, integrate out the inducing variables
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}
from our joint variational distribution and obtain the optimal marginal distribution for latent states
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T}
.
We start by retaking
Eq.

21
and isolating the terms that depend on the inducing variables,

q^{*}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{0:T})=\int_{{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}}}q^{*}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}},{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{0:T})d{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}}\\
=p({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{0})\prod_{t=1}^{T}\left[p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{t}\,|\,{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{t})\exp(-\frac{1}{2}\trace({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}}_{t-1}))\right]\\
\int_{{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}}}p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}})\prod_{t=1}^{T}\mathcal{N}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{t};{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}}_{x_{t}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}})\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}},

(25)

where we note that
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}}_{x_{t}}
, as defined as in
Eq.

18
, depends on the inducing variables
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}
. With this, we can complete the square and identify the terms in the integral as products of Gaussian distributions, whose normalization constant is a Gaussian. Therefore, we have

q^{*}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{0:T})=p({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{0})\prod_{t}\Big[p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{t}|{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{t})\exp(-\frac{1}{2}\trace({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}}_{t-1}))\\
\mathcal{N}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{t};{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}}}_{t-1},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}})\Big]/\mathcal{N}(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}};\mathbf{0},\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}}_{x}),

(26)

where the Gaussian in the denominator is determined by

\displaystyle\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}}_{t-1}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}^{-1}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}-{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}}}_{t-1}),

(27)

\displaystyle\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}}_{x}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}}+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}}_{t-1}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}^{-1}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}}_{t-1},

(28)

\displaystyle\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}}_{t-1}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{t-1,Z}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{L}}}_{Z}^{\top})^{-1},

(29)

where
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{L}}}_{Z}
is the Cholesky decomposition of
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{Z}
, i.e.,

{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{Z}={\boldsymbol{\mathbf{L}}}_{Z}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{L}}}_{Z}^{\top}
. We note here that
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}
is actually a projection of the cumulative uncorrelated inputs
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}^{-1}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}-{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}}}_{t-1})
via the projection matrix
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{L}}}_{Z}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{Z,t-1}
and, therefore,
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
.

Furthermore, in this collapsed version, the terms unrelated to
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}
are kept the same as those in the original optimal joint distribution in
Eq.

21
. The individual Gaussian transitions for the latent states are now
\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t};{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}}}_{t-1},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}})
since
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}
has been integrated out and we recall that
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}}}_{t-1}=m_{f}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t-1})
.
The additional term, which is the inverse of a multivariate Gaussian distribution, records the cumulative projected trajectory with the corresponding projected variances. Maximizing
q^{*}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T})
would tend to minimize this density function, which pushes the latent states away from
\mathbf{0}
and also decreases the values of the
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}
regulated variance.

Our algorithm in this collapsed version runs
sghmc
using as energy function
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Psi}}}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T})=-\log q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T})
to obtain samples
\{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T}^{(s)}\}
from the marginal
q^{*}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T})
and then uses the closed-form expression for the conditional (
Appendix

G
) to obtain
\{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}^{(s)}|{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T}^{(s)}\}
.

5.1
Advantages of Collapsed Algorithm

Collapsing the inducing variables
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}
will generally tend to improve the convergence of our algorithm, as we are required to sample from a significantly lower number of latent variables.
The computational cost is similar to that of the uncollapsed algorithm since despite avoiding the computation of
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{Z}^{-1}
in
p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}})
, we require a similar term,
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{L}}}_{Z}^{-1}
, in the evaluation of the projected Gaussian distributions.

It is important to emphasize one particular difference in our approach with respect to the closely related
vcdt
algorithm of
Ialongo et al. 2019
.
As described before,
Ialongo et al. 2019
also propose a coupled joint posterior between state trajectories and inducing variables.
Their factorization is
q_{\lx@glossaries@gls@link{acronym}{VCDT}{{{}}\textsc{vcdt}}}=q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}})q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}})
, and they impose additional Gaussian constraints on these densities. Our implicit assumed factorization is
q_{\lx@glossaries@gls@link{acronym}{FFVD}{{{}}\textsc{ffvd}}}=q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T})q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T})
, which allows us to obtain the optimal variational distribution without imposing any additional parametric constraints, integrate out the inducing variables analytically and get a Monte Carlo approximation to the optimal marginal
q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T})
via samples.
We also provide an expression for the conditional
q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T})
in closed-form.
Details can be found in
Appendix

G
.

5.2
Particle Markov chain Monte Carlo (
pmcmc
)

It is clear that the latent states are constructed in a Markovian manner when the transition function
f(\cdot)
is given, as the value of the current latent state is dependent on the previous latent state’s value.
Therefore, we can use
pmcmc
methods
(
Andrieu et al. 2010
)
to infer the posterior distribution of the Markov structured latent states
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T}
.
This Bayesian treatment might improve performance over
sghmc
, as it incorporates the sequential nature of the problem into the sampling algorithm. The advantages of
pmcmc
over standard sequential Monte Carlo approaches have been documented previously, see. e.g.,
Andrieu et al. 2010
.
Here we note that
Frigola et al. 2013
also proposed a
pmcmc
treatment for
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T}
. However, their algorithm
is very different from ours as it is based on the fully-independent conditional approximation
(
Quinonero-Candela & Rasmussen 2005
, see, e.g.,)
. More details can be found in
Appendix

K
.

6
Related Work

We have already described the main differences between our method and closely-related approaches throughout the paper, e.g., in
Sections

1
,
4
and
5
.
We refer the reader to
Appendix

K
for more details.
Other works have considered the
gpssm
in partially observable unstable settings
(
Curi et al. 2020
)
, combined variational inference with the Laplace approximation
(
Lindinger et al. 2022
)
or used sample-based inference with a reduced rank approximation
(
Svensson et al. 2016
)
.
With regards to scalable
gps
, we note they have been
the subject of much research effort in machine learning, with extensions to more general frameworks such as compositional models
(
Wilson & Nickisch 2015
;
Salimbeni & Deisenroth 2017
;
Yu et al. 2019
;
Cutajar et al. 2017
;
Havasi et al. 2018
;
Rossi et al. 2021
;
Duncker et al. 2019
;
Heinonen et al. 2018
;
Hegde et al. 2022
;
Auzina et al. 2022
;
Bui 2018
, see, e.g.,)
.
Our approach can be seen as a generalization of the fully-Bayesian supervised learning method proposed by
Rossi et al. 2021
to state-space models, where we have included the non-trivial component of
gp
-transition dynamics and have proposed a collapsed optimal variational distribution for state trajectories.

Other models for
gps
on sequential data have been proposed, see, for example,
Frigola 2015
for an excellent overview.
Of interest here is the state-space model view of
gps
that for time-series data with
d_{x}=1
and Markovian covariance functions can provide exact inference in linear time
\mathcal{O}(T)

(
Solin 2016
)
.
This has been extended to non-Gaussian likelihood models and made more efficient using several computational primitives
(
Nickisch et al. 2018
)
.
More recently, there has been some work on using the signature kernel
(
Toth & Oberhauser 2020
;
Salvi et al. 2021
)
within
gp
models. In particular,
Lemercier et al. 2021
generalize variational orthogonal features
(
Burt et al. 2020
;
Hensman et al. 2017
)
to the sequential case, constructing inducing variables associated with the signature kernel that yield a variational inference algorithm that does not require any matrix inversion.

8
Conclusions, Limitations and Future Work

We have presented
ffvd
, a new variational inference algorithm for
gpssms
. Unlike previous approaches,
ffvd
does not make any independence or parametric assumptions on the joint variational posterior over state trajectories and inducing variables
q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}})
and, instead, represents the posterior via samples from the “optimal” variational distribution.
However, as described in
Section

4.1
, despite having a free-form posterior over state trajectories and inducing variables, our approach is still an approximation in that it assumes the conditional posterior over the latent functions to be the same as the conditional prior. This is a customary and necessary (albeit questionable) assumption in scalable variational methods for general
gp
models.

Our method also assumes independent
gps
over the state dimensions. However, one can expect the dynamics to be correlated across dimensions, bringing a need for the so-called multi-output/multi-task
gps
. This has been a fairly extensive area of research and
we believe a weight-space view of such models could be suitable, where dimensions are correlated through a weight matrix. Approximate inference algorithms in this setting can be developed in
\mathcal{O}(d_{x})
, where
d_{x}
is the state dimensionality, or even independent of
d_{x}

(see, e.g.,
Aglietti et al. 2019
, in the context of Cox process models)
. As we would like to scale up to very high-dimensional spaces, we leave this to future work.

Appendix A
Basic Results on Gaussian Distributions

A.1
Conditional Gaussian Distribution

Assuming the joint Gaussian distribution
p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}})=\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}};{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}})
over the random vector
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}
such that:

{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}=\begin{bmatrix}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{a}\\
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{b}\end{bmatrix},\quad{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}}=\begin{bmatrix}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}}_{a}\\
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}}_{b}\end{bmatrix},\quad{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}=\begin{bmatrix}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{aa}&{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{ab}\\
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{ba}&{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{bb},\end{bmatrix}

(30)

where
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{ba}={\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{ab}^{T}
, we have that the conditional distributions are given by:

p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{b}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{a})=\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{b};{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}}_{b}+{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{ba}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{aa}^{-1}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{a}-{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}}_{a}),{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{bb}-{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{ba}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{aa}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{ab}).

(31)

A.2
Expectation over log of Normal Distribution

With an approximate marginal posterior
q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{\star})
and a Normal distribution
p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{\star})
of the form:

\displaystyle q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{\star})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{\star};{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}}_{\star},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{\star}),

(32)

\displaystyle p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{\star})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}};{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{\star},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{y}),

(33)

we can compute

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{\star})}\log p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{\star})

\displaystyle=\log\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}};{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}}_{\star},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{y})+\trace({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{y}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{\star}).

(34)

Appendix B
Posterior Marginal in Sparse GP Models

In variational sparse GP models we usually have the joint Gaussian model
p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{\star},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}})
and a posterior distribution over
q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}})
with

\displaystyle p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{\star})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}}}_{\star},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{\star}),

(35)

\displaystyle p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}};{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}}}_{Z},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{Z}),

(36)

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}\text{ov}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{\star},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}})

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{\star,Z},

(37)

\displaystyle q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}};{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}),

(38)

\displaystyle q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{\star},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}})

\displaystyle:=q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}})p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{\star}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}),

(39)

and we wish to compute:

q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{\star})=\int q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{\star},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}})\operatorname{d}\!{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}},

(40)

where we have omitted conditional dependencies on
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{\star}
and
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}}
for simplicity in the notation. For example this is necessary to compute the expectations over the conditional log likelihood term during inferencce or to make predictions on a new test point.
Using
Eq.

31
we obtain:

p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{\star}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}})=\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{\star};{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}}}_{\star}+{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{\star,Z}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{Z}^{-1}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}-{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}}}_{Z}),{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{\star}-{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{\star,Z}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{Z}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{Z,\star}).

(41)

In order to integrate out over
q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}})
we know that the result is a Gaussian with mean and covariances obtained from the following linear transformation of
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}
:

{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{\star}={\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}}}_{\star}+{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{\star,Z}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{Z}^{-1}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}-{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}}}_{Z})+{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\epsilon}}},\quad{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\epsilon}}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{\star}-{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{\star,Z}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{Z}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{Z,\star}).

(42)

Thus, we obtain:

\displaystyle q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{\star})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}\left({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{\star};{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}}}_{\star}+{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{\star,Z}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{Z}^{-1}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}}-{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}}}_{Z}),{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{\star,Z}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{Z}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{Z}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{Z,\star}+{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{\star}-{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{\star,Z}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{Z}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{Z,\star}\right)

(43)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}\left({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{\star};{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}}}_{\star}+{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{\star,Z}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{Z}^{-1}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}}-{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}}}_{Z}),{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{\star}+{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{\star,Z}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{Z}^{-1}\left({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}-{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{Z}\right){\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{Z}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{Z,\star}\right).

(44)

Appendix D
Details of Prior Reparameterization: Whitening Prior over Inducing Variables

Here we show a more detailed derivation of the prior reparameterization and, consequently, the new form of the required conditional distribution. We know that our prior over the inducing variables is
p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}})=\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}};{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}}}_{Z},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{Z})
and that we use the new whitened prior
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}}={\boldsymbol{\mathbf{L}}}_{Z}^{-1}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}-{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}}}_{Z})
with
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{L}}}_{Z}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{L}}}_{Z}^{T}={\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{Z}
and
p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}})=\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}};\mathbf{0},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}}_{M})
. We also have that the marginal prior over
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{\star}
is
p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{\star})=\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{\star};{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}}}_{\star},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{\star})
. It follows that
p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}})
is a Gaussian with cross-covariance:

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}\text{ov}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{\star},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{\star}-{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}}}_{\star})({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{L}}}_{Z}^{-1}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}-{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}}}_{Z}))^{T}]

(45)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{\star}-{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}}}_{\star})({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}-{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}}}_{Z})^{T}]}_{\mathbb{C}\text{ov}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{\star},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}})}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{L}}}_{Z}^{-1})^{T}

(46)

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{\star,Z}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{L}}}_{Z}^{-1})^{T}.

(47)

Hence, using
Eq.

31
, we have that:

\displaystyle p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{\star}\,|\,,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{\star},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{\star};{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}}}_{\star}+{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{\star,Z}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{L}}}_{Z}^{-1})^{T}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{\star}-{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{\star,Z}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{L}}}_{Z}^{-1})^{T}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{L}}}_{Z}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{Z,t}),

(48)

\displaystyle p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{\star}\,|\,,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{\star},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{\star};{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}}}_{\star}+{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{\star,Z}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{L}}}_{Z}^{-1})^{T}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{\star}-{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{\star,Z}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{Z}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{Z,\star}).

(49)

Alternatively, we can simply obtain this result by replacing
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}
with
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}={\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}}}_{Z}+{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{L}}}_{Z}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}}
in the conditional distribution. Here we note that this is
consistent
with the
gp
definition in that if we were to integrate out the variables
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}}
from the joint model
p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{\star},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}})
we would obtain the exact
gp
prior
p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{\star})
.

D.1
Posterior Marginal

With the whitened join model
p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{\star},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}})
, Gaussian approximate posterior
q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}})
and a variationally sparse
gp
model we have:

\displaystyle p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{\star}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{\star};{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}}}_{\star}+{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{\star,Z}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{L}}}_{Z}^{-1})^{T}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{\star}-{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{\star,Z}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{Z}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{Z,\star}),

(50)

\displaystyle q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}};{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}}),

(51)

\displaystyle q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{\star},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}})

\displaystyle:=q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}})p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{\star}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}}).

(52)

It is easy to show that the posterior marginal
q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{\star})=\int q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{\star},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}})\operatorname{d}\!{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}}}
is:

\displaystyle q_{v}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{\star})=\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{\star};{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}}}_{\star}+{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{\star,Z}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{L}}}_{Z}^{-1})^{T}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{\star}+{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{\star,Z}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{L}}}_{Z}^{-1})^{T}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}}-{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}}_{M}){\boldsymbol{\mathbf{L}}}_{Z}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{Z,\star}).

(53)

Appendix E
Free-Form Posterior Estimation By Bounding the Log Marginal Likelihood Directly

Computing the log marginal:

\log p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{0:T-1},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T-1},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}})=\log\left[p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}})p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0})p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{0}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0})\prod_{t=1}^{T-1}p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{t}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t})\right]+\mathcal{L}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T-1},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}}),

(54)

where

\mathcal{L}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T-1},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}}):=\left[\log\int_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{1:T-1}}\prod_{t=1}^{T-1}p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{t})p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{t}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{1:t-1},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:t-1},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}})\ \mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{1:T-1}\right],

(55)

which we note is a log of an expectation, which we can bounded using Jensen’s inequality:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T-1},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}})

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{\prod_{t=1}^{T-1}p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{t}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{1:t-1},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:t-1},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}})}\log\prod_{t=1}^{T-1}p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{t})

(56)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}_{p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{t}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t-1},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}})}\log p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{t}),

(57)

where
p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{t}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t-1},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}})
is the
gp
predictive distribution over
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{t}
at
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t-1}
given the (pseudo observations) inducing variables
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}
at inducing inputs
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}}
:

\displaystyle p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{t}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t-1},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{t};{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}_{t-1}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}}_{t-1})\quad\text{with }

(58)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}_{t-1}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{t-1,Z}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{Z}^{-1}

(59)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}}_{t-1}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{t-1}-{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{t-1,Z}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{Z}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{Z,t-1}.

(60)

Hence, the expectations in
Eq.

57
can be computed in closed-form:

\mathbb{E}_{p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{t}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t-1},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}})}\log p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{t})=\log\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t};{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}_{t-1}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}})-\frac{1}{2}\trace({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}}_{t-1}).

(61)

Thus, we have that:

\log p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{0:T-1},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T-1},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}})\geq\log\left[p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}})p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0})p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{0}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0})\right]+\\
\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\left[\log p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{t}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t})+\log\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t};{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}_{t-1}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}})-\frac{1}{2}\trace({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}}_{t-1})\right].

(62)

Then setting the latent variables
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Psi}}}:=\{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T-1}\}
, we can obtain the approximate log unnormalized posterior:

\log\tilde{q}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Psi}}}):=\log p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}})+\log p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0})+\log p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{0}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0})\ +\\
\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\left[\log p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{t}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t})+\log\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t};{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}_{t-1}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}})-\frac{1}{2}\trace({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}}_{t-1})\right].

(63)

where
\tilde{q}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Psi}}})\propto q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Psi}}})\approx p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Psi}}}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{0:T-1})
. Thus, we can draw samples from our approximate posterior using
sghmc
and the energy function
U({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Psi}}})=-\log p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Psi}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{0:T-1})=-\log p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Psi}}}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{0:T-1})+C\approx-\log\tilde{q}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Psi}}})+C
.

E.1
Whitened Version

In the whitened version,
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}}={\boldsymbol{\mathbf{L}}}_{Z}^{-1}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}-{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}}}_{Z})
or
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}={\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}}}_{Z}+{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{L}}}_{Z}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}}
and the effective prior is

p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}})=\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}};\mathbf{0},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}}).

(64)

Letting
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}}=({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}},{\theta}),\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}}}=({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}},{\theta})
, the determinant of the Jacobian can be calculated as:

\displaystyle|\frac{\partial\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}}}{\partial\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}}}}|=

\displaystyle\left|\begin{array}[]{ccc}\frac{\partial({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}}}_{Z}+{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{L}}}_{Z}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}})}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}}}&\frac{\partial({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}}}_{Z}+{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{L}}}_{Z}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}})}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}}}&\frac{\partial({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}}}_{Z}+{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{L}}}_{Z}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}})}{\partial\theta}\\
\frac{\partial{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}}}&\frac{\partial{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}}}&\frac{\partial{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}}}{\partial\theta}\\
\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}}}&\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}}}&\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial\theta}\end{array}\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{L}}}_{Z}&\frac{\partial({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}}}_{Z}+{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{L}}}_{Z}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}})}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}}}&\frac{\partial({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}}}_{Z}+{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{L}}}_{Z}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}})}{\partial\theta}\\
0&{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}}&0\\
0&0&{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}}\end{array}\right|=\left|\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{L}}}_{Z}&0&0\\
0&{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}}&0\\
0&0&{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}}\end{array}\right|=|{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{L}}}_{Z}|=|{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{Z}|^{\frac{1}{2}}

Thus, we can re-write the approximate log unnormalized posterior as:

\log\tilde{q}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T-1})=\log|{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{Z}|^{\frac{1}{2}}-\log|{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{Z}|^{\frac{1}{2}}-\frac{1}{2}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}}+\log p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0})+\log p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{0}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0})+\\
\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\left[\log p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{t}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t})+\log\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t};{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{t-1,Z}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{L}}}_{Z}^{-1})^{T}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}})-\frac{1}{2}\trace({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}}_{t-1})\right].\\
=\log p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}})+\log p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0})+\log p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{0}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0})+\\
\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\left[\log p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{t}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t})+\log\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t};{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{t-1,Z}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{L}}}_{Z}^{-1})^{T}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}})-\frac{1}{2}\trace({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}}_{t-1})\right].

(74)

Appendix F
Collapsed Method

We can integrate out
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}

We contrast our variational distribution with that of the

;)
and the

(

While, implicitly, our variational distribution is

Appendix K
Extended Related Work

Here we give more details about the differences between our method and closely related approaches for inference in the
gpssm
.
The
vgpssm
of
Frigola et al. 2014
is the first developing variational approaches to inference in
gpssms
, and it uses mean-field assumptions to factorize the variational distribution of inducing variables
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}
and latent state trajectories
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T}
as
q_{\lx@glossaries@gls@link{acronym}{VGPSSM}{{{}}\textsc{vgpssm}}}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T})=q({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}})q({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T})
.
This assumption clearly overlooks the complex dependencies between
{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0:T}
.
Doerr et al. 2018
model these dependencies with their
prssm
algorithm, but make the unrealistic assumption of the posterior dynamics over the latent state trajectories being the same as the prior.
Ialongo et al. 2019
show that this assumption can have critical consequences on the posterior and predictive distributions and propose a more general algorithm called
vcdt
. Although a significant improvement over previous methods,
vcdt
assumes posterior Gaussian distributions. When considering our main object of interest, i.e., the posterior over the state trajectories, under the standard and most commonly used
gp
setting with non-linear kernels, a Gaussian posterior assumption never holds, as the latent states are inputs to the kernel. Hence, our approach is theoretically superior to
vcdt
and provides a new baseline with broad practical applicability.

As mentioned in the main paper,
Frigola et al. 2013
also proposed a
pmcmc
algorithm for inference over state trajectories.
However, the algorithm is based on the
fully-independent conditional
approximation
(
Quinonero-Candela & Rasmussen 2005
,
fic
;)
.
As reported in
Frigola et al. 2013
, a naïve implementation of their algorithm has a time complexity of
\mathcal{O}(M^{2}T^{2})
, which is significantly higher than ours.
A much more efficient implementation with
\mathcal{O}(M^{2}T)
time complexity that requires significant tracking of intermediate data structures and factorizations is hinted at in the original paper.
Unfortunately, the corresponding code has not been made publicly available.
Furthermore, model approximations such as those in
fic
can have surprising consequences, such as incorrectly estimating the noise variance to be almost zero or ignoring additional inducing inputs, see, e.g.,
Bauer et al. 2016
for a thorough discussion. Consequently, we see our variational approach as more principled.

K.1
Reduced-Rank Approaches

Here we discuss the related approach of
Svensson et al. 2016
, which we will refer to as
reduced rank (
rrank
)
. As with random feature approximations,
rrank
exploits the covariance function-spectral density duality via the Wiener-Khinchin theorem to approximate the underlying
gps
with a linear-in-the-parameters model, where the feature maps can be obtained analytically for specific kernels.

While these types of approaches have their own weaknesses and strengths with respect to inducing variable approximations, one crucial difference with our method is that their approximation is only applicable to stationary kernels. Therefore our method is, by definition, much more general. Furthermore, as noted by
Wilson et al. 2020
, those types of approximations to the covariance function based on finite-dimensional feature maps are known to exhibit undesirable pathologies at test time. In particular, the feature maps used by
Svensson et al. 2016
can become increasingly ill-behaved in high-data regimes. Consequently, we believe the advantages of our approach (that avoids the stationarity assumption and its corresponding practical deficiencies) justify its use as an additional tool for practitioners and researchers.

Nevertheless, here we briefly report some results using the Matlab code provided by
Svensson et al. 2016
. The
rmse
values obtained on Actuator, Ballbeam, Drive, Dryer, Flutter, and Furnace are 0.33, 0.11, 0.73, 1.35, 1.86, and 9.66, respectively. While these results are generally competitive with our approach and other baselines, they are inferior to the results reported with our algorithm.
