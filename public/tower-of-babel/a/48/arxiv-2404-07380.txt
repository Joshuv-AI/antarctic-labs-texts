Strong Bounds for Skew-Corner-Free Sets
Anthony Ostuni

Abstract
Motivated by applications to matrix multiplication algorithms, Pratt asked (ITCS’24) how large a subset of [ n ] × [ n ] [n]\times[n] could be without containing a skew corner : three points ( x , y ) , ( x , y + h ) , ( x + h , y ′ ) (x,y),(x,y+h),(x+h,y^{\prime}) with h ≠ 0 h\neq 0 . We prove any skew-corner-free set has size at most exp ⁡ ( − Ω ⁡ ( log 1 / 12 ⁡ n ) ) ⋅ n 2 \exp(-\Omega(\log^{1/12}n))\cdot n^{2} , nearly matching the best known lower bound of exp ⁡ ( − O ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) ) ⋅ n 2 \exp(-O(\sqrt{\log n}))\cdot n^{2} by Beker (arXiv’24). Our techniques generalize those of Kelley and Meka’s recent breakthrough on three-term arithmetic progression (FOCS’23), answering a question of Beker (arXiv’24). We note that a similar bound was obtained concurrently and independently by Milićević (arXiv’24).

Introduction

In an attempt to rule out certain groups as candidates for achieving an optimal matrix multiplication algorithm using the framework of
[
10
,
9
]
, Pratt
[
18
]
introduced
skew corners
: triples of points
(x,y),(x,y+h)
, and
(x+h,y^{\prime})
for arbitrary
x,y,y^{\prime},
and
h
. Note that the more traditional form of corners can be recovered by insisting
y=y^{\prime}
. We call a skew corner trivial if
h=0
and nontrivial otherwise, and we call a set skew-corner free if it does not contain any nontrivial skew corners. Pratt’s matrix multiplication results are conditional, holding if the largest skew-corner-free subset of
[n]\times[n]
has size
O(n^{1+\varepsilon})
for all
\varepsilon>0
, as well as weaker results for the upper bound
O(n^{4/3-\varepsilon})
for any fixed
\varepsilon>0

[
18
]
.

Very recently, these two conjectures were refuted. Pohoata and Zakharov
[
19
]
constructed a skew-corner-free set of size
\Omega(n^{5/4})
, which was improved shortly after to
\exp(-O(\sqrt{\log n}))\cdot n^{2}
by Beker
[
3
]
using a Behrend-type construction (see
[
2
]
). In the same paper, Beker also improved the best known upper bounds to
O((\log n)^{-c})\cdot n^{2}
for an absolute constant
c>0
, and went on to ask if one could use the techniques of the recent breakthrough on three-term arithmetic progressions (3APs)
[
13
]
(see also
[
8
,
7
]
) to obtain a bound of
\exp(-\Omega(\log^{c}n))\cdot n^{2}
for some constant
c>0
. We resolve this question affirmatively.

Theorem 1.1
.

Any skew-corner-free subset of
[n]\times[n]
has size at most
\exp\left\{-\Omega\left(\log^{1/12}n\right)\right\}\cdot n^{2}
.

In fact, we can prove such a result for any skew-corner-free subset of
G\times G
, where
G
is an arbitrary finite Abelian group. It is likely that the constant
1/12
can be somewhat improved using ideas similar to
[
7
]
. However, the overall technique’s limitations appear to prevent improvements significant enough to approach Beker’s lower bound
[
3
]
, so we instead prioritize a streamlined presentation.

As is somewhat standard, we first exhibit our argument in the simpler finite field setting. Here, we can obtain slightly stronger bounds.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
q
be a prime power. Any skew-corner-free subset of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\times\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
has size at most
q^{2n-\Omega(n^{1/9})}
.

A version of
Theorem

1.2
with the superior bound
O(q^{(2-c)n})
(for a constant
c>0
depending on
q
) is known via the polynomial method
[
19
]
. However, this technique does not appear to generalize to the integer case, whereas our analytic approach is able to do so relatively painlessly.

We largely view this work as a step toward better understanding the traditional corners problem of how large a subset of
[n]\times[n]
one can have without three points
(x,y),(x,y+h),(x+h,y)
. This problem is much older and better studied than the skew variant, yet the best upper bound of
O(n^{2}/(\log\log n)^{0.0137\cdots})

[
21
]
remains far from the lower bound of
\exp(-O(\sqrt{\log n}))\cdot n^{2}

[
2
]
(see also
[
14
,
11
,
12
]
).

There has been optimism (see, e.g.,
[
15
]
and
[
17
, Section 1.2]
) that the techniques recently used to dramatically improve the best known upper bounds on 3AP-free sets
[
13
]
could also be applied in the (traditional) corners setting. Our work provides evidence that this is a potentially fruitful direction. In fact, we are able to either black-box or closely follow the proofs of many lemmas from the excellent exposition of Bloom and Sisask
[
8
]
of Kelley and Meka’s work, raising the following question about the relationship between these problems.

Question 1.3
.

Suppose the largest 3AP-free subset of
[n]
has size
\delta n
. Does the largest skew-corner-free subset of
[n]\times[n]
have size
\text{poly}(\delta)\cdot n^{2}
? More generally, is it possible to directly convert bounds from one setting to another?

Concurrent work.

A similar result to
Theorem

1.1
was obtained concurrently and independently by Milićević
[
16
]
. His techniques are also based on the ideas of
[
13
]
, although the specifics diverge slightly from our own.

2
Background

Let
G
be a finite Abelian group throughout (although we will only truly be concerned with vector spaces over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
or the integers modulo a positive integer
\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}
).

Asymptotics.

We use standard asymptotic notation of
O(\cdot)
and
\Omega(\cdot)
to suppress fixed constants that do not depend on any parameters. Occasionally, we will use subscripts to hide a dependence on particular parameters (e.g.,
O_{\varepsilon}(\cdot)
).

Distributions.

A distribution
\nu
over
G
is a non-negative function
\nu\colon G\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
with
\E[\nu]=1
. For
f\colon G\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
define
\nu(f)=\E_{x\in G}\nu(x)f(x)
to be the average of
f
under
\nu
. We also define
\nu_{f}
to be
f
normalized to have expectation
1
under
\nu
; that is,
\nu_{f}(x)=\nu(x)f(x)/\nu(f)
. We use
\mu
to denote the uniform distribution on
G
, so
\mu\equiv 1
. Given a set
A\subseteq G
, we identify
A
with its indicator function
1_{A}
and define accordingly
\nu(A)=\nu(1_{A})
and
\nu_{A}=\nu_{1_{A}}
. In this case,
\mu_{A}=\frac{|G|}{|A|}\cdot 1_{A}
, and in particular
\E_{x\in G}\mu_{A}(x)=1
. In other words,
\mu_{A}
is the normalized indicator function of
A
.

Functions.

For functions
f,g\colon G\to\mathbb{R}
, we define inner products and norms with the normalized counting measure on
G
, namely

\langle f,g\rangle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x\in G}f(x)g(x)\quad\text{and}\quad\|f\|_{p}=\left(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x\in G}|f(x)|^{p}\right)^{1/p}\text{ for }1\leq p<\infty,

as well as
\|f\|_{\infty}=\max_{x\in G}|f(x)|
. We will also want to work with other measures on
G
. For a measure
\nu
on
G
, we write

\langle f,g\rangle_{\nu}=\mathop{\E}_{x\in G}\nu(x)f(x)g(x)\quad\text{and}\quad\|f\|_{p(\nu)}=\left(\mathop{\E}_{x\in G}\nu(x)|f(x)|^{p}\right)^{1/p}\text{ for }1\leq p<\infty.

Additionally, we define the convolution and the difference convolution as

(f\ast g)(x)=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{y\in G}f(y)g(x-y)\quad\text{and}\quad(f\circ g)(x)=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{y\in G}f(y)g(x+y),

and the
p
-fold convolution as
f^{(p)}=f\ast f^{(p-1)}
, where
f^{(1)}=f
. Note the useful adjoint property
\langle f,g\ast h\rangle=\langle h\circ f,g\rangle.
Occasionally, the notation
f^{g}(x)=f(x-g)
for some
g\in G
will be useful.

Let
\widehat{G}=\{\text{homomorphisms }\gamma:G\to\mathbb{C}^{\times}\}
be the dual group of
G
. The Fourier transform of
f
is
\widehat{f}\colon\widehat{G}\to\mathbb{C}
, where

\widehat{f}(\gamma)=\mathop{\E}_{x\in G}f(x)\gamma(-x).

If
\widehat{f}(\gamma)\geq 0
for all
\gamma\in\widehat{G}
, we say
f
is
spectrally non-negative
, denoted
\widehat{f}\geq 0
. Note the basic facts that
\widehat{f\ast g}=\widehat{f}\cdot\widehat{g}
and
\widehat{f\circ f}=|\widehat{f}|^{2}
. In particular,
f\circ f
is spectrally non-negative. We define the convolution for two functions
f,g\colon\widehat{G}\to\mathbb{C}
using the ordinary counting measure:

(f\ast g)(\gamma)=\sum_{\tau\in\widehat{G}}f(\tau)g(\gamma-\tau).

The use of the ordinary counting measure here is so that we have the identity
\widehat{f^{p}}=\widehat{f}^{(p)}
.

2.1
Skew corners

Sequences of evenly spaced integers, known as
arithmetic progressions
, are central objects of study in additive combinatorics. In an attempt to understand similar structure in higher dimensions, Ajtai and Szemerédi
[
1
]
considered the following objects.

Definition 2.1

(Corners)
.

A
corner
in
G\times G
is a triple of the form
(x,y),(x,y+h),(x+h,y)
for arbitrary
x,y,h\in G
. A corner is
nontrivial
if
h\neq 0
.

By relaxing the structure to allow the third point to lie anywhere on the vertical line
x+h
, one obtains
skew corners
, a generalization recently raised by Pratt
[
18
]
.

Definition 2.2

(Skew corners)
.

A
skew corner
in
G\times G
is a triple of the form
(x,y),(x,y+h),(x+h,y^{\prime})
for arbitrary
x,y,y^{\prime},h\in G
. A skew corner is
nontrivial
if
h\neq 0
. Additionally, a set is
skew-corner-free
is it does not contain nontrivial skew corners.

One immediate, yet useful, observation is that shifting a set will not change the number of skew corners it contains.

Observation 2.3
.

The number of skew corners in a set
A
is invariant to both horizontal shifting (
A\rightarrow\{(x+a,y):(x,y)\in A\}
) and vertical shifting (
A\rightarrow\{(x,y+b(x)):(x,y)\in A\}
).

The following lemma allows us to count skew corners in an analytic way.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
A\subseteq G\times G
and
A_{x}=\{y:(x,y)\in A\}
. Additionally, let
D\colon G\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be given by
D(x)=|A_{x}|
. Then, the number of skew corners in
A
is counted by

|G|^{2}\sum_{x\in G}\langle 1_{A_{x}}\circ 1_{A_{x}+x},D\rangle.

Proof.

Recall a skew corner in
A
is a triple of the form

(x,y),(x,y+h),(x+h,y^{\prime})

for arbitrary
x,y,y^{\prime},h\in G
. The number of skew corners is given by

\displaystyle\sum_{x,y,y^{\prime},h\in G}1_{A}(x,y)1_{A}(x,y+h)1_{A}(x+h,y^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y,y^{\prime},h}1_{A_{x}}(y)1_{A_{x}}(y+h)1_{A_{x+h}}(y^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y,h}1_{A_{x}}(y)1_{A_{x}+x}(x+y+h)D(x+h)

\displaystyle=|G|\sum_{x,h}(1_{A_{x}}\circ 1_{A_{x}+x})(x+h)D(x+h)

\displaystyle=|G|^{2}\sum_{x}\langle 1_{A_{x}}\circ 1_{A_{x}+x},D\rangle.

∎

Occasionally, the following normalized form will be more useful.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
A\subseteq G\times G
,
A_{x}=\{y:(x,y)\in A\}
, and
\eta\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. Additionally, let
f=\sum_{x\in G}1_{A_{x}}\circ 1_{A_{x}+x}
and
D\colon G\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be given by
D(x)=|A_{x}|
. If
\langle\mu_{f},\mu_{D}\rangle\geq\eta
, then the number of skew corners in
A
is at least

\eta\cdot\frac{|A|^{3}}{|G|^{2}}.

Proof.

Unraveling the definition of
\langle\mu_{f},\mu_{D}\rangle
, we have

\displaystyle\eta\leq\left\langle\frac{1}{\E[f]}\sum_{x\in G}1_{A_{x}}\circ 1_{A_{x}+x},\frac{D}{\E[D]}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\E[f]}\cdot\frac{1}{\E[D]}\sum_{x\in G}\left\langle 1_{A_{x}}\circ 1_{A_{x}+x},D\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{|G|^{2}}{\sum|A_{x}|^{2}}\cdot\frac{|G|}{\sum|A_{x}|}\sum_{x\in G}\left\langle 1_{A_{x}}\circ 1_{A_{x}+x},D\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\frac{|G|^{3}}{|A|^{2}}\cdot\frac{|G|}{|A|}\sum_{x\in G}\left\langle 1_{A_{x}}\circ 1_{A_{x}+x},D\right\rangle

(Cauchy-Schwarz)

\displaystyle=\frac{|G|^{2}}{|A|^{3}}\times\text{(number of skew corners in $A$)}.

(
Lemma

2.4
)  ∎

2.2
Bohr sets

To prove
Theorem

1.2
, we will take advantage of the abundance of subspaces in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
. As this no longer holds once we move to more general Abelian groups, we will turn to the machinery of
Bohr sets
, introduced by Bourgain
[
5
]
, to prove
Theorem

1.1
. We will only require the very basics, but one may entirely skip over this topic if they are solely interested in a high-level understanding of our results; all the main ideas are present in the simpler finite field case (
Section

3
).

Definition 2.6

(Bohr sets)
.

For a nonempty
\Gamma\subseteq\widehat{G}
and
\phi\in[0,2]
, we define the Bohr set

B=\mathrm{Bohr}(\Gamma,\phi)=\{x\in G:|1-\gamma(x)|\leq\phi\text{ for all }\gamma\in\Gamma\},

where
\Gamma
is the frequency set,
\phi
is the width (or radius), and
|\Gamma|
is the rank
\mathrm{rk}(B)
.

It follows from the definition that Bohr sets are symmetric, meaning that if
x\in B
, we must also have
-x\in B
. Note also that a Bohr set
B
does not uniquely define its frequency set or width. Thus, we always implicitly view
B
as the tuple
(\Gamma,\phi,\mathrm{Bohr}(\Gamma,\phi))
. However, the notation
B^{\prime}\subseteq B
only refers to containment of the actual set itself and does not depend on the frequency set or width. Indeed, there are Bohr sets
B,B^{\prime}
where
B^{\prime}\subset B
, but the frequency set of
B
is not contained in the frequency set of
B^{\prime}
, and the width of
B^{\prime}
is not smaller than than the width of
B
. To handle these situations, we follow
[
22
]
in defining
sub-Bohr
sets.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
B=\mathrm{Bohr}(\Gamma,\phi)
,
B^{\prime}=\mathrm{Bohr}(\Gamma^{\prime},\phi^{\prime})
be Bohr sets.
We say that
B^{\prime}
is a
sub-Bohr set
of
B
, denoted
B^{\prime}\leq B
, if
\Gamma^{\prime}\supseteq\Gamma
and
\phi^{\prime}\leq\phi
.

Observation 2.8
.

Let
B^{\prime}\leq B
for Bohr sets
B,B^{\prime}
. Then,
B^{\prime}\subseteq B
.

A common operation performed on Bohr sets is
dilation
. If
B=\mathrm{Bohr}(\Gamma,\phi)
is a Bohr set and
\rho>0
, then the corresponding
dilate
of
B
is
B_{\rho}=\mathrm{Bohr}(\Gamma,\rho\phi)
. The following observation about sub-Bohr set dilates will be useful in later arguments.

Observation 2.9
.

Let
B^{\prime}\leq B
for Bohr sets
B,B^{\prime}
. Then,
B^{\prime}_{\rho}\leq B_{\rho}
for all
\rho>0
. In particular,
B^{\prime}_{\rho}\subseteq B_{\rho}
.

If small dilations do not substantially change the size of a Bohr set
B
, we call
B

regular
. Regular Bohr sets will be particularly useful for our purposes, as informally, one can view a regular Bohr set of rank
r
analogously to a subspace of co-dimension
r
.

Definition 2.10

(Regular Bohr sets)
.

A Bohr set
B
of rank
r
is
regular
if for all
0\leq\kappa\leq 1/100r
, we have

|B_{1+\kappa}|\leq(1+100\kappa r)|B|\quad\text{and}\quad|B_{1-\kappa}|\geq(1-100\kappa r)|B|.

We record some useful lemmas, most of which are listed in the appendix of
[
8
]
. See
[
23
, Section 4.4]
or
[
4
, Chapter 6]
for proofs of the first two standard results, as well as a more thorough treatment of Bohr set machinery.

Lemma 2.11
.

For any Bohr set
B
, there exists
\rho\in[\frac{1}{2},1]
such that
B_{\rho}
is regular.

Lemma 2.12
.

If
\rho\in(0,1)
and
B
is a Bohr set of rank
r
, then
|B_{\rho}|\geq(\rho/4)^{r}|B|
.

Lemma 2.13

(
[
6
, Lemma 4.6]
)
.

There is a constant
c>0
such that the following holds. If
B
is a regular Bohr set of rank
r
and
\nu
is a probability measure supported on
B_{\rho}
, with
\rho\leq c/r
, then

\mu_{B}\leq 2\mu_{B_{1+\rho}}\ast\nu.

Lemma 2.14

(
[
6
, Lemma 4.5]
)
.

If
B
is a regular Bohr set of rank
r
and
\nu
is a probability measure supported on
B_{\rho}
, with
\rho\in(0,1)
, then

\|\mu_{B}\ast\nu-\mu_{B}\|_{1}\leq O(\rho r).

We will make use of the following corollary.

Corollary 2.15
.

If
B
is a regular Bohr set of rank
r
and
\nu
is a probability measure supported on
B_{\rho}
, with
\rho\in(0,1)
, and
f\colon G\to\mathbb{R}
, then

|\langle f\ast\nu,\mu_{B}\rangle-\langle f,\mu_{B}\rangle|\leq O\left(\|f\|_{\infty}\cdot\rho r\right).

Proof.

We have
\langle f\ast\nu,\mu_{B}\rangle=\langle f,\mu_{B}\circ\nu\rangle=\langle f,\mu_{B}\rangle+\langle f,\mu_{B}\circ\nu-\mu_{B}\rangle.

Since Bohr sets are symmetric, we can replace
\nu(x)
with
\nu(-x)
and maintain that
\nu
is supported on
B_{\rho}
. It suffices to bound

\displaystyle|\langle f,\mu_{B}\ast\nu-\mu_{B}\rangle|\leq\|f\|_{\infty}\|\mu_{B}\ast\nu-\mu_{B}\|_{1}\leq O(\|f\|_{\infty}\cdot\rho r),

which follows by
Lemma

2.14
and the triangle inequality.
∎

We briefly remark that in several subsequent arguments, we will again exploit the symmetry of subspaces and Bohr sets
V
to use the identity
\mu_{V}(x)=\mu_{V}(-x)
for all
x
.

2.3
Spread sets

A key definition underpinning Kelley and Meka’s breakthrough result on 3APs
[
13
]
is a pseudorandomness notion they call
spreadness
. A subset of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
is spread if it has no significant density increment by restricting to a large affine subspace.

Definition 2.16

(
(r,\lambda)
-spread)
.

Let
r\geq 1
and
\lambda>1
.
A set
A\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
is
(r,\lambda)
-spread
if for every subspace
V\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
of co-dimension at most
r
and shift
x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, we have

\frac{|(A-x)\cap V|}{|V|}\leq\lambda\cdot\frac{|A|}{|\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}|}.

In light of
Lemma

2.4
, we will need the following generalization of spreadness that applies to multiple sets. It should intuitively be clear that the definition we choose needs to be invariant to shifts, since the number of skew corners in a set has this property (
Observation

2.3
).

Definition 2.17

(
(r,\lambda)
-simultaneously spread)
.

Let
r\geq 1
and
\lambda>1
.
A collection
\{A_{i}\}_{i\in S}
of subsets of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
is
(r,\lambda)
-simultaneously spread
if for every subspace
V\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
of co-dimension at most
r
and shifts
\{x_{i}\}_{i\in S}
in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, we have

\sum_{i\in S}\left(\frac{|(A_{i}-x_{i})\cap V|}{|V|}\right)^{2}\leq\lambda\cdot\sum_{i\in S}\left(\frac{|A_{i}|}{|\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}|}\right)^{2}.

When our ambient space is a vector space
W\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, we will say the collection is
(r,\lambda)
-simultaneously spread
in
W
if it is
(r,\lambda)
-simultaneously spread after being mapped according to an isomorphism from
W
to
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\dim W}
.

Additionally, we require a version with Bohr sets replacing subspaces for general Abelian groups. For technical reasons, it will simplify later analysis to measure density with respect to a particular Bohr set rather than the ambient space (as in
Definition

2.17
).

Definition 2.18

(
(r,\delta,\lambda)
-simultaneously spread)
.

Let
r\geq 1
,
\delta\in(0,1)
, and
\lambda>1
.
A collection
\{A_{i}\}_{i\in S}
of subsets of a regular Bohr set
B\subseteq G
is
(r,\delta,\lambda)
-simultaneously spread in
B
if for every regular Bohr set
B^{\prime}\leq B
of rank at most
\mathrm{rk}(B)+r
and measure

\mu(B^{\prime})\geq\delta\mu(B)
, and shifts
\{x_{i}\}_{i\in S}
in
G
, we have

\sum_{i\in S}\left(\frac{|(A_{i}-x_{i})\cap B^{\prime}|}{|B^{\prime}|}\right)^{2}\leq\lambda\cdot\sum_{i\in S}\left(\frac{|A_{i}|}{|B|}\right)^{2}.

It was useful in
[
13
]
to use
\|\mu_{A}\ast\mu_{V}\|_{\infty}\leq\lambda
as an equivalent form of
Definition

2.16
. We will later need to exploit a similar relationship with an alternative definition of simultaneous spreadness. (The proof of this connection explains why the summands in
Definition

2.17
and
Definition

2.18
are squared.)

Lemma 2.19
.

Let
r\geq 1
,
\lambda>1
,
\{A_{i}\}_{i\in S}
be an
(r,\lambda)
-simultaneously spread collection of subsets of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, and
\alpha=\sum_{i\in S}(|A_{i}|/|\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}|)^{2}
. Then for every subspace
V\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
of co-dimension at most
r
, we have

\left\|\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{i\in S}1_{A_{i}}\circ 1_{A_{i}}\right)\ast\mu_{V}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\sqrt{\lambda}.

Proof.

Let
x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
be an element maximizing the input to the
L^{\infty}
-norm. Then by Cauchy-Schwarz,

\left\|\left(\sum_{i\in S}1_{A_{i}}\circ 1_{A_{i}}\right)\ast\mu_{V}\right\|_{\infty}=\sum_{i\in S}\langle 1_{A_{i}}\circ 1_{A_{i}},\mu_{V+x}\rangle\leq\sum_{i\in S}\|1_{A_{i}}\|_{1}\|1_{A_{i}}\ast\mu_{V}\|_{\infty}\leq\alpha^{1/2}\left(\sum_{i\in S}\|1_{A_{i}}\ast\mu_{V}\|_{\infty}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

Now let
x_{i}
be a shift which maximizes
|(A_{i}-x_{i})\cap V|
for each
i\in S
. By our spreadness assumption, we have

\sum_{i\in S}\|1_{A_{i}}\ast\mu_{V}\|_{\infty}^{2}=\sum_{i\in S}\left(\frac{|(A_{i}-x_{i})\cap V|}{|V|}\right)^{2}\leq\lambda\cdot\sum_{i\in S}\left(\frac{|A_{i}|}{|\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}|}\right)^{2}=\lambda\alpha.

Substituting the second equation into the first yields the result.
∎

3
The finite field case

In this section, we prove
Theorem

1.2
, restated below for convenience. Note that Pohoata and Zakharov
[
19
]
used the polynomial method to obtain the stronger bound
O(q^{(2-c)n})
(for a constant
c>0
depending on
q
). However, their techniques do not seem to generalize to the integer case.
See
1.2

The analogous result for 3APs in
[
13
]
is proved by repeatedly restricting the set of interest to a large, denser subspace until no such subspaces exist (i.e., the set is spread). Then, they show spread sets have approximately the same number of 3APs as a random set, and since there cannot be too many trivial 3APs, the original set size cannot be too large.

This intuition guides our approach, although the situation is more complicated for skew corners. It is helpful to think about the converse of the above discussion. For a set
A
of large density, if
A
contains no 3APs, then
A
must exhibit a density increment on a reasonably large subspace. In the skew corners case, we start with a large subset
A
of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\times\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, and denote by
\{A_{g}\}
its columns. We are asking if there are many solutions of the form
x-y+g\in D
for
x,y\in A_{g}
, where
D
can be thought of as the set of columns of decent density. By Kelley and Meka’s result, if each of the sets
A_{g}
was spread, then we would have many solutions and thus many skew corners. So, we know that if there are no skew corners, then most of the
A_{g}
are
not
spread, and most of the
A_{g}
exhibit a density increment on some large subspace
V_{g}
. The issue is, however, that a priori these subspaces
V_{g}
might be completely uncorrelated.

One might hope that if the sum of
1_{A_{g}}\circ 1_{A_{g}+g}
is far from uniform, then the individual deviations of the
1_{A_{g}}\circ 1_{A_{g}+g}
are cooperating, and so there should be a density increment which is compatible with the
A_{g}
on average. Indeed, this ends up being what happens, and we show that there is a common subspace
V
which “explains” the lack of uniformity for the sum of convolutions. Namely, for most
A_{g}
, there is some affine shift
V+x_{g}
which admits a density increment on
A_{g}
. For technical reasons, we will measure progress in terms of the sum of the squared densities
(|A_{g}\cap(V+x_{g})|/|V|)^{2}
.

It is tempting to ignore the collection of
A_{g}
, and simply proceed with a single
A_{h}
of highest density. The Kelley-Meka techniques allow one to restrict
A_{h}
to an affine subspace
V
on which the convolution
1_{A_{h}}\circ 1_{A_{h}+h}
is close to uniform. The issue, however, is that this approach does not ensure that
D
has large density on
V
. There may in fact be no nonempty columns indexed by elements from
V
, and so this naive approach does not work as is. For this reason, our density increment process needs access to many of the
A_{g}
at once, so that we can restrict the set of “rows” in which the
A_{g}
are contained, as well as the set of “columns” by which the
A_{g}
are indexed while maintaining density guarantees.

The main steps then are to argue that (1) a collection of sets
\{A_{g}\}_{g\in G}
can be restricted in such a way that it no longer admits a collective density increment on a subspace and (2) if a collection of
\{A_{g}\}_{g\in G}
contains enough points but fails to have skew corners, then there must be some subspace on which the
A_{g}
collectively obtain a density increment.

3.1
Obtaining simultaneous spreadness

A useful property of spreadness is that it is easy to obtain via an iterative density increment argument. The generalization to simultaneous spreadness preserves this property with only slightly more work.

(1)

4
The integer case

In this section, we prove the following theorem.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
G
be a finite Abelian group. Any skew-corner-free subset of
G\times G
has size at most

\exp\left\{-\Omega\left(\log^{1/12}|G|\right)\right\}\cdot|G|^{2}.

Note that
Theorem

4.1
immediately implies

1.1
(restated below), since embedding

in
\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}

See
1.1

All the main ideas of

Section

2.2
.) For the remainder of the discussion, we will assume we are working with

, but everything we are doing works over any finite Abelian group.

We will aim to replicate what we did in the finite field case when working with
\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}
. The main issue is that there are potentially no nontrivial subgroups to work with over
\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}
. This ends up not being so much of an issue, since we can replace subgroups with regular Bohr sets, which approximate subgroups in many important ways. And, not unlike the case over
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, we will be able to argue that if a collection
\{A_{g}\}_{g\in G}
contains very few skew corners, then there must be some collective density increment on a Bohr set

.

In the finite field case, when we found a subspace

with a collective density increment, we could restrict our attention to

. Here, we will not have this luxury, since Bohr sets are not necessarily subgroups! We will have to maintain our ambient group as
\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}

\{A_{g}\}_{g\in G}
to smaller and smaller Bohr sets. Thus, we will work with functions defined over
\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}
. The supports of these functions, however, will only be contained in some Bohr set

. So, when taking norms and inner products, we will want to zoom in on the action by taking everything with respect to a certain measure
\nu
. For the most part,
\nu
will be some convolution of dilates of
R
, which we will be able to control since Bohr sets behave well under convolutions. Dealing with these technicalities surrounding Bohr sets and non-uniform measures forms the major differences between working over
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
and
\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}
, but the overall proof structures are conceptually the same.

4.1
Obtaining simultaneous spreadness

As in the finite field case, we will want to perform a density increment argument on the rows and columns of
A\subseteq G\times G
until we obtain a collection
\{A_{g}\}_{g\in C}
of subsets of
R
for regular Bohr sets

which is simultaneously spread. For technical reasons, we will want
C
to be a translate of a dilate of
R
. This will end up being necessary when we prove

4.4
and

4.5
.

Another difference between the Abelian group case and the finite field case comes from our definition of simultaneous spreadness. In particular, if our sets
\{A_{g}\}_{g\in C}
are each contained in

, then the Bohr set

on which we find a density increment must be a sub-Bohr set of

. The reader will notice in the proof of

that this becomes necessary when trying to obtain

from

, it is not true in general that

.

Overall, the proof idea is conceptually the same as

3.1
in the finite field case. The main differences come from ensuring that the index

is a translate of an appropriate dilate of

Lemma 4.2
.

Let

Proof.

We proceed by a similar density increment argument as in the finite field case (

), where regular Bohr sets replace the role of vector spaces.

Let

(3)

From here, the proof proceeds by filtering the index set of our collection to be some translate of a dilate of

will be chosen later.
Consider the function

\frac{1}{|B_{\sigma}|}\sum_{g\in B_{\sigma}}\left(\frac{|A^{\prime}_{g}|}{|B^{\prime}|}\right)^{2}=\langle\mu_{B_{\sigma}},f\rangle\quad\text{ and }\quad\frac{1}{|B_{\sigma}||B^{\prime}_{\sigma^{\prime}}|}\sum_{g\in B_{\sigma}}\sum_{h\in B^{\prime}_{\sigma^{\prime}}}\left(\frac{|A^{\prime}_{g+h}|}{|B^{\prime}|}\right)^{2}=\langle\mu_{B_{\sigma}}\ast\mu_{B^{\prime}_{\sigma^{\prime}}},f\rangle.

We want to apply

2.15
to
B_{\sigma}
and
B^{\prime}_{\sigma^{\prime}}
.
Set

2.11

\displaystyle\langle\mu_{B_{\sigma}}\ast\mu_{B^{\prime}_{\sigma^{\prime}}},f\rangle

By our setting of

\frac{1}{|B_{\sigma}||B^{\prime}_{\sigma^{\prime}}|}\sum_{g\in B_{\sigma}}\sum_{h\in B^{\prime}_{\sigma^{\prime}}}\left(\frac{|A^{\prime}_{g+h}|}{|B^{\prime}|}\right)^{2}\geq(1-\varepsilon/4)\frac{1}{|B_{\sigma}|}\sum_{g\in B_{\sigma}}\left(\frac{|A^{\prime}_{g}|}{|B^{\prime}|}\right)^{2}.

By combining

The following lemma shows that if no corners are present, then we obtain some non-uniformity on our collection of sets in an appropriate

Lemma 4.4
.

There exists an absolute constant
c>0
such that the following holds. Let
\varepsilon>0
and

be a collection of subsets of a regular Bohr set

of rank

with

. Additionally, let

when

Proof.

Our proof follows closely
[
8
, Proposition 20]
. Assume the first conclusion does not hold, and note that

\displaystyle\left\langle\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{g\in C}\left(1_{A_{g}}-\alpha_{g}\mu_{R}\right)\circ\left(1_{A_{g}+g}-\alpha_{g}\mu_{R+g}\right),\mu_{D}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{g\in C}\left\langle 1_{A_{g}}\circ 1_{A_{g}+g},\mu_{D}\right\rangle-\mu(R)^{-1}\right)

(5)

\displaystyle-\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{g\in C}\alpha_{g}\langle 1_{A_{g}}\circ\mu_{R+g},\mu_{D}\rangle-\mu(R)^{-1}\right)

(6)

\displaystyle-\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{g\in C}\alpha_{g}\langle\mu_{R}\circ 1_{A_{g}+g},\mu_{D}\rangle-\mu(R)^{-1}\right)

(7)

\displaystyle+\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{g\in C}\alpha_{g}^{2}\langle\mu_{R}\circ\mu_{R+g},\mu_{D}\rangle-\mu(R)^{-1}\right).

(8)

We will bound the last three terms in magnitude, then apply Hölder’s inequality to the first term (

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{g\in C}\alpha_{g}\langle 1_{A_{g}}\circ\mu_{R+g},\mu_{D}\rangle-\mu(R)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{g\in C}\alpha_{g}\langle 1_{A_{g}},\mu_{R+g}\ast\mu_{-D}\rangle-\mu(R)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{g\in C}\alpha_{g}\langle 1_{A_{g}},\mu_{R}\ast\mu_{-D}^{g}\rangle-\mu(R)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{g\in C}\alpha_{g}\langle 1_{A_{g}},\mu_{R}\rangle\right)+\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{g\in C}\alpha_{g}\langle 1_{A_{g}},\mu_{R}\ast\mu_{-D}^{g}-\mu_{R}\rangle\right)-\mu(R)^{-1}.

(9)

First, since

, we know that

=
\alpha_{g}\mu(R)^{-1}
. Summing over

gives

\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{g\in C}\alpha_{g}\langle 1_{A_{g}},\mu_{R}\rangle=\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{g\in C}\alpha^{2}_{g}\cdot\mu(R)^{-1}=\mu(R)^{-1},

so the first and third terms of (

) cancel out. We next bound the second term of (
9
).
Since
\mu_{D}
is supported on

and
C
is symmetric, we have
\mu_{-D}
is supported on
C
as well. As

is supported on

. The last point to note is that

by Cauchy-Schwarz. We will now use the assumption that

for a small enough constant

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{g\in C}\alpha_{g}\langle 1_{A_{g}},\mu_{R}\ast\mu_{-D}^{g}-\mu_{R}\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{16\alpha}\sum_{g\in C}\alpha_{g}\varepsilon 2^{-d}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{16}\mu(R)^{-1}.

Similar reasoning bounds the third (

) and fourth (

) terms by the same quantity.
Finally, let
f_{0}=\sum_{g\in C}\left(1_{A_{g}}-\alpha_{g}\mu_{R}\right)\circ\left(1_{A_{g}+g}-\alpha_{g}\mu_{R+g}\right)
. Combining the above bounds gives

\left|\langle\mu_{f},\mu_{D}\rangle-\mu(R)^{-1}-\langle\mu_{f_{0}},\mu_{D}\rangle\right|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\mu(R)^{-1}.

For simplicity, define

An application of Hölder’s inequality, for any

4.2.2
Unbalancing

At this point, we have a non-uniformity assumption on the function

F_{0}=\sum_{g\in C}\left(1_{A_{g}}-\alpha_{g}\mu_{R}\right)\circ\left(1_{A_{g}+g}-\alpha_{g}\mu_{R+g}\right),

and we would like to switch to working with the function

has non-negative Fourier coefficients, while

in general may not. There are minor complications that arise when working with non-uniform measures, which accounts for the main differences between the following lemma and the corresponding one in the finite field case.

Lemma 4.5

such that the following holds. Let

p\geq 2

Lemma 4.6
.

There exists an absolute constant
c>0
such that the following holds. Let
p\geq 2
be an even integer and

For simplicity, we will define

\displaystyle\mu_{B}

Expanding, we have

\displaystyle\|F_{1}\|_{p(\mu_{B})}^{p}

\displaystyle=\E_{x\in G}\mu_{B}(x)F_{1}(x)^{p}

\displaystyle\leq 2\E_{x\in G}\left(\mu_{B_{1+\rho}}\ast\nu\right)(x)F_{1}(x)^{p}

\displaystyle=2\E_{t\in B_{1+\rho}}\E_{x\in G}\nu(x-t)F_{1}(x)^{p}.

\langle\mu_{B}\ast\mu_{M_{1}}\circ\mu_{M_{2}},1_{S}\rangle\geq 1-\varepsilon

\mathrm{rk}(B)\leq r+O_{\varepsilon}\left(d^{4}p^{4}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\mu(B)\geq\exp\left(-O_{\varepsilon}(rp\left(d+\log r\right)+d^{5}p^{5})\right)\mu(C^{\prime\prime}).

Proof.

Since
\mu_{M_{1}}\circ\mu_{M_{2}}
is supported on
C^{\prime}+C^{\prime\prime}-x
, we can assume
S
is supported on the same set. We can also shift
M_{2}
,
S
by
x
so that
M_{2}\subseteq C^{\prime\prime}
without changing any assumptions or conclusions. Thus, in order to apply
Theorem

4.10
, it suffices to check that
|C^{\prime}+C^{\prime\prime}|\leq 2|C^{\prime}|
. By regularity and our choice of
\sigma
, we have

|C^{\prime}+C^{\prime\prime}|=|C^{\prime}+C^{\prime}_{\sigma}|\leq(1+O(\sigma\cdot r))|C^{\prime}|\leq 2|C^{\prime}|.

The proof concludes by applying
Theorem

4.10
with
B\coloneqq C^{\prime}
,
B^{\prime}\coloneqq C^{\prime\prime}
and
M_{1},M_{2},S
as given.
∎

4.2.5
Combining steps

All that remains to prove
Theorem

4.3
(restated below) is to combine the prior steps.
See
4.3

Proof.

If we are not in the first condition, then
Lemma

4.4
implies there is some integer
p=O(d)
such that

\left\|\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{g\in C}\left(1_{A_{g}}-\alpha_{g}\mu_{R}\right)\circ\left(1_{A_{g}+g}-\alpha_{g}\mu_{R+g}\right)\right\|_{p(\mu_{C})}\geq\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon\mu(R)^{-1}.

We then apply
Lemma

4.5
to infer that

\left\|\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{g\in C}1_{A_{g}}\circ 1_{A_{g}}\right\|_{p^{\prime}(\mu_{C^{\prime}}\ast\mu_{C^{\prime\prime}-x})}\geq\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{16}\right)\mu(R)^{-1},

for some shift
x\in G
, where
C^{\prime}\subseteq C_{\tau_{1}}
and
C^{\prime\prime}\subseteq C^{\prime}_{\tau_{2}}
are regular Bohr sets for some
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}=O(r^{-1})
chosen so that
C^{\prime},C^{\prime\prime}
are regular. In particular, the choice of dilates is so that we can later use
Corollary

4.11
.
Let

S=\left\{x\in G:\sum_{g\in C}1_{A_{g}}\circ 1_{A_{g}}(x)>(1-\varepsilon/32)\left\|\sum_{g\in C}1_{A_{g}}\circ 1_{A_{g}}\right\|_{p^{\prime}(\mu_{C^{\prime}}\ast\mu_{C^{\prime\prime}-x})}\right\}.

Via
Corollary

4.9
, we obtain
M_{1}\subseteq C^{\prime}
,
M_{2}\subseteq C^{\prime\prime}
such that

\langle\mu_{M_{1}}\circ\mu_{M_{2}},1_{S}\rangle\geq 1-\varepsilon/128,

where
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
satisfy the density constraints

\min\left(\mu_{B}(M_{1}),\mu_{B^{\prime}}(M_{2})\right)\geq 2^{-O_{\varepsilon}(dp)}.

We then apply
Corollary

4.11
to produce a regular Bohr set
B\leq C^{\prime\prime}

such that

\langle\mu_{B}\ast\mu_{M_{1}}\circ\mu_{M_{2}},1_{S}\rangle\geq 1-\varepsilon/64,

where

\mathrm{rk}(B)=r+O_{\varepsilon}(d^{8})\quad\text{and}\quad\mu(B)\geq\exp\left(-O_{\varepsilon}(rd^{2}+rd\log r+d^{10})\right)\mu(C^{\prime\prime}).

Since
C=R_{\sigma}
,
C^{\prime}=R_{\sigma\tau_{1}}
, and
C^{\prime\prime}\subseteq R_{\sigma\tau_{1}\tau_{2}}
,
Lemma

2.12
implies

\mu(C^{\prime\prime})\geq\left(\frac{\sigma\tau_{1}\tau_{2}}{4}\right)^{r}\mu(R)\geq\exp\left(-O(r\log(1/\sigma)+r\log r)\right)\mu(R).

Combining the above inequalities gives

\mu(B)\geq\text{exp}\left(-O_{\epsilon}(r\log(1/\sigma)+rd^{2}+rd\log r+d^{10})\right).

The last step is to note that

\displaystyle(1+\varepsilon/80)\mu(R)^{-1}

\displaystyle\leq(1-\varepsilon/64)\left(1-\varepsilon/32\right)(1+\varepsilon/16)\mu(R)^{-1}

\displaystyle\leq\langle\mu_{B}\ast\mu_{M_{1}}\circ\mu_{M_{2}},\mu_{F^{\prime}}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\|\mu_{F^{\prime}}\ast\mu_{B}\|_{\infty}\|\mu_{M_{1}}\circ\mu_{M_{2}}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\|\mu_{F^{\prime}}\ast\mu_{B}\|_{\infty}.

∎
