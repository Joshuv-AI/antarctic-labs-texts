Abstract
Model reduction of Markov processes is a basic problem in modeling state-transition systems. Motivated by the state aggregation approach rooted in control theory, we study the statistical state compression of a discrete-state Markov chain from empirical trajectories. Through the lens of spectral decomposition, we study the rank and features of Markov processes, as well as properties like representability, aggregability, and lumpability. We develop spectral methods for estimating the transition matrix of a low-rank Markov model, estimating the leading subspace spanned by Markov features, and recovering latent structures like state aggregation and lumpable partition of the state space. We prove statistical upper bounds for the estimation errors and nearly matching minimax lower bounds. Numerical studies are performed on synthetic data and a dataset of New York City taxi trips.

Introduction

Model reduction is a central problem in scientific studies, system engineering, and data science. In many situations one needs to learn about a complex system from trajectories of noisy observations. When data is limited, the unknown system becomes difficult to model, analyze, infer and let alone optimize.

In this paper, we study the dimension reduction of a Markov chain
\{X_{0},X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\}
where the state space is discrete and finite but very large. There are two goals:
The first goal is data compression and recovery of a reduced-order Markov model.
The second goal is to extract features for state representation, which can be further used to find state aggregation or lumpable clusters. These two goals are closely tied to each other - achieving either one would trivialize the other one.
We refer to the combination of these two goals as the problem of
state compression
.

State compression of discrete Markov chains finds wide applications.
For an example of network analysis, records of taxi trips can be viewed as a fragmented sample path realized from a city-wide Markov chain
[
1
,
2
]
, and experiments suggest that one can estimate latent traffic network from sample paths
[
3
]
. Similar needs for analyzing Markov transition data also arise from ranking problems in e-commerce
[
4
,
5
]
, where clickstreams can be viewed as a random walk on the space of all possible clicks.

Our work is inspired by the state aggregation approach that is commonly used to reduce the complexity of reinforcement learning and control systems. State aggregation means to aggregate “similar” states into a small number of “meta states,” which are typically handpicked based on domain-specific knowledge
[
6
,
7
]
or based on given similarity metrics or feature functions
[
8
]
.
In the context of discrete-state Markov chains, the goal of state aggregation is to find a partition mapping
E
such that

\mathbb{P}(X_{t+1}\mid X_{t})\approx\mathbb{P}(X_{t+1}\mid E(X_{t})).

In fact, the state aggregation structure corresponds to a particular low-rank decomposition of the system’s transition kernel (see Proposition
3
). Another inspiring example is the use of membership models for modeling large Markov decision processes, where each observed state is mapped into a mixture over meta states
[
9
,
7
]
. This membership model, also known as soft state aggregation, corresponds to a low-rank decomposition structure of the transition kernel (see Proposition
2
). These existing approaches for dimension reduction of control and reinforcement learning mainly rely on
priorly known
meta-states or membership models. In contrast, we aim to learn the state aggregation structure from trajectorial data in an unsupervised manner.

Let us investigate the spectral decomposition of the Markov chain, of the form

{\mathbb{P}}(X_{t+1}\mid X_{t})\approx\sum_{k=1}^{r}f_{k}(X_{t})g_{k}(X_{t+1}),

where
f_{1},\ldots,f_{r},g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}
are some feature functions and
r
is the rank.
The spectral decomposition of the transition kernel provides a natural venue towards state compression, where
f_{1},\ldots,f_{r},g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}
can be used as basis functions to represent the state space using a small set of parameters.
There are many open fundamental questions: How to estimate the feature functions and the leading feature space? How to estimate the Markov model under a low-rank assumption? What are the statistical limits for state compression? In this paper, we plan to take a substantial step towards answering these questions.

We propose a class of
spectral state compression
methods for finite-state nonreversible Markov process with provably sharp statistical guarantees. Our main results are summarized as follows.

1.

Spectral properties of Markov chains, aggregability, and lumpability.
We study the spectral decomposition of Markov chains, and we show it is closely related to aggregability and lumpability of the process. Aggregability means that the states can be aggregated into blocks while preserving the transition probability distributions, while lumpability means that states can be clustered while preserving the strong Markov property.

2.

Sharp statistical guarantees for estimating low-rank Markov models.
For Markov chains with a
known
small rank, we provide a spectral method for estimating the transition matrices and establish upper bounds on the finite-sample total variation error. We also establish a nearly matching minimax lower bound. These results also extend to the estimation of general low-rank stochastic matrices that are not necessarily square.

3.

Sharp statistical guarantees for state space compression of general Markov chains.
For general Markov chains that is not low-rank, we show that the spectral method recovers the leading Markov feature space with high accuracy. Upper bounds and minimax lower bounds for the subspace recovery errors are established.
In special cases of aggregable or lumpable processes, we show that one can further recover the state aggregation or lumpable partition with statistical guarantees.

In numerical experiments, we apply state compression to analyze the New York City Yellow Cab data. By modeling taxi trips as sample transitions realized from a citywide random walk, our spectral state aggregation method indeed reveals latent traffic patterns and meaningful partition of NYC.

Markov Rank, Aggregability, and Lumpability

Let
\{X_{0},\ldots,X_{n}\}
be a Markov chain on the space
\Omega
. When
\Omega
is a finite set
\Omega=\{1,\ldots,p\}
, let the transition matrix be
\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
where

\mathbf{P}_{ij}={\mathbb{P}}(X_{k}=j|X_{k-1}=i,X_{k-2},\ldots,X_{0})
for all
k\geq 1
,
1\leq i,j\leq p
.
Throughout this paper, we assume
\{X_{0},\ldots,X_{n}\}
is ergodic so there exists an invariant distribution
\pi\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, i.e.,

\pi_{i}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}1_{\{X_{k}=i\}}.

Furthermore,
\pi
is an invariant distribution if and only if
\pi^{\top}\mathbf{P}=\pi^{\top},\pi_{i}\geq 0
, and
\sum_{i=1}^{p}\pi_{i}=1
. Let
\pi_{\min}=\min_{1\leq i\leq p}\pi_{i},\pi_{\max}=\max_{1\leq i\leq p}\pi_{i}
. Let
\mathbf{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
be the long-run frequency matrix

\mathbf{F}_{ij}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}1_{\{X_{k}=i,X_{k+1}=j\}},

so that
\mathbf{F}={\rm diag}(\pi)\mathbf{P}
.
For any
\varepsilon>0
, the
\varepsilon
-mixing time
of the Markov chain is defined as

\tau(\varepsilon)=\min\left\{k:\max_{1\leq i\leq p}\frac{1}{2}\left\|(\mathbf{P}^{k})_{[i,:]}-\pi^{\top}\right\|_{1}\leq\varepsilon\right\}.

(1)

We call
\tau_{\ast}=\tau(1/4)
the mixing time for short.
Please refer to
[
51
,
52
]
for comprehensive discussions on the theory of Markov chain and mixing times.

Let us consider Markov chains with a small rank. This notion was introduced for Markov processes with a general state space by
[
53
]
as an example of “dependence that is close to independence”.
For more examples and properties of the finite-rank Markov chain, please refer to
[

]
and

Definition 1

(Markov Rank, Kernel and Features)
.

The rank of a Markov chain
X_{0},\ldots,X_{n}
is the smallest integer
r
such that its transition kernel can be written in the form of

{\mathbb{P}}(X_{t+1}\mid X_{t})=\sum_{k=1}^{r}f_{k}(X_{t})g_{k}(X_{t+1}),

(2)

where
f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}
are real-valued functions and
g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}
are probability mass functions. The non-degenerate
r\times r
matrix
{\mathbf{C}}
such that
{\mathbf{C}}_{ij}=f_{j}^{\top}g_{i}
is referred to as the Markov kernel. We refer to
f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}
as left Markov features and
g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}
as right Markov features. If the Markov process has
p
discrete states,
f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}
,
g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}
are
p
-dimensional vectors and
{\mathbf{C}}_{ij}=\sum_{k=1}^{p}f_{j}(k)g_{i}(k)
.

A low-rank Markov chain admits infinitely many decompositions of the form (
2
), therefore the kernel
{\mathbf{C}}
and feature functions
f_{1},\ldots,f_{r},g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}
are not uniquely identifiable. In this paper, we will mainly focus what are identifiable, i.e., the transition kernel
\mathbf{P}
and the feature spaces spanned by
f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}
and
g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}
respectively.

Proposition
1
shows that Markov features are sufficient to represent the multi-step Markov transition and the stationary distribution.

Proposition 1

(Representability of Markov Features;
[
54
]
)
.

Suppose that the Markov chain
X_{0},\ldots,X_{n}
has a rank
r
taking the form of (
2
), then

1.

If the state space is finite, the transition matrix
\mathbf{P}
satisfies
\hbox{rank}(\mathbf{P})=r
.

2.

{\mathbb{P}}(X_{t+n}\mid X_{t})=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sum^{r}_{j=1}f_{i}(X_{t})({\mathbf{C}}^{n-1})_{ij}g_{j}(X_{t+n}).

3.

There exists
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
such that
\pi(\cdot)=\sum^{r}_{k=1}\gamma_{k}g_{k}(\cdot)
and
\gamma^{\top}{\mathbf{C}}=\gamma^{\top}
.

In addition, Markov features can be used as basis functions in the context of control and reinforcement learning for representing
value functions
.
For example consider the reward process
h(X_{0}),\ldots,h(X_{n})
, where
h:\Omega\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is a reward functio. In control and reinforcement learning, a central quantity for evaluating the current state of the system is the discounted cumulative
value function

v:\Omega\mapsto\mathbb{R}
, given by
v(x)=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum^{\infty}_{n=0}\alpha^{n}\ h(X_{n})\mid X_{0}=x\right]
, where
\alpha\in(0,1)
is a discount factor. Now if the Markov chain admits a decomposition of the form (
2
), we have
v(\cdot)=r(\cdot)+\sum^{r}_{k=1}w_{k}f_{k}(\cdot)
for some scalars
w_{1},\ldots,w_{r}
. In other words, the value function can be represented as a linear combination of left Markov features.

Next we introduce a notion of Markov non-negative rank, which is slightly more restrictive than the Markov rank.

Definition 2

(Markov Non-negative Rank)
.

The non-negative rank of a Markov chain is the smallest
r
such that its transition kernel can be written in the form of

{\mathbb{P}}(X_{t+1}\mid X_{t})=\sum_{k=1}^{r}f_{k}(X_{t})g_{k}(X_{t+1})

for some nonnegative functions
f_{1},\ldots,f_{r},g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}
.

This definition of nonnegative rank remains the same even if we restrict
g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}
are probability mass functions and for each
x\in\Omega
,
i\mapsto f_{i}(x)
is a probability mass function. Denote by
\hbox{rank}_{+}(\mathbf{P})
the nonnegative rank of
{\mathbf{P}}
.
It is easy to verify that
\hbox{rank}_{+}(\mathbf{P})=r
if and only if there exist nonnegative matrices
\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{p\times r}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{P}}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{r\times r}
such that

\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{U}\tilde{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{V}^{\top},

where
\mathbf{U}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1},\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1},\tilde{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1}.
This decomposition means that one can map the states into meta-states while preserving most of the system dynamics (see Figure
1
). In the context of control and dynamic programming, rows of
\mathbf{U}
are referred to as

and columns of
\mathbf{V}
are referred to as

56

It always holds that
\hbox{rank}(\mathbf{P})\leq\hbox{rank}_{+}(\mathbf{P})

Low-rank decomposition of the Markov chain is related to several reduced-order models. For example, the Markov chain with a small nonnegative rank is equivalent to a membership model.

Proposition 2

(Nonnegative Markov Rank and Membership Model)
.

The Markov chain with transition probability matrix
\mathbf{P}
has a nonnegative rank
\hbox{rank}_{+}(\mathbf{P})\leq r
if and only if there exists a stochastic process
\{Z_{t}\}\subset\{1,\ldots,r\}
such that

\begin{split}&{\mathbb{P}}(Z_{t}\mid X_{t})={\mathbb{P}}(Z_{t}\mid X_{1},\ldots,X_{t}),\\
&{\mathbb{P}}(X_{t+1}\mid Z_{t})={\mathbb{P}}(X_{t+1}\mid X_{1},\ldots,X_{t},Z_{t}).\end{split}

(3)

Next we consider an important special case of low-rank Markov processes that is amenable to state aggregation. State aggregation is a basic approach for describing complicated systems
[
56
,
7
]
and is particularly useful for approximating value functions in optimization, control theory, and reinforcement learning
[
57
]
. The idea is to partition the state space into disjoint blocks and treat each block as a single new state.

Definition 3

(Aggregability of Markov Chains)
.

A Markov chain is
r
-state aggregatable if there exists a partition
\Omega_{1},\ldots,\Omega_{r}
of
\Omega
such that

{\mathbb{P}}(X_{t+1}\mid X_{t}=i)={\mathbb{P}}(X_{t+1}\mid X_{t}=j),\qquad\forall i,j\in\Omega_{k},~k\in\{1,\ldots,r\}.

It is easy to show that aggregability corresponds to a particular non-negative decomposition.

Proposition 3

(Decomposition of Aggregatable Markov Chains)
.

If a Markov chain is state-aggregatable with respect to a partition
\Omega_{1},\ldots,\Omega_{r}
, its nonnegative rank is at most
r
and

{\mathbb{P}}(X_{t+1}\mid X_{t})=\sum_{k=1}^{r}1_{\Omega_{k}}(X_{t})g_{k}(X_{t+1})

for nonnegative functions
g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}:\Omega\mapsto\mathbb{R}_{+}
, where
1_{S}
denotes the indicator function of a set
S
.

Proposition
3
implies, the Markov chain with transition matrix
\mathbf{P}
is
r
-state aggregatable if and only if
\hbox{rank}_{+}(\mathbf{P})=r
and there exist
\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times r}
such that

\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}^{\top},

where
\mathbf{V}
is nonnegative and
\mathbf{U}=[\mathbf{1}_{\Omega_{1}},\ldots,\mathbf{1}_{\Omega_{r}}]
indicates the membership.

A Markov process is called lumpable if the state space can be partitioned into blocks while still preserving the strong Markov property

58

Definition 4

(Lumpability of Markov Chains
[

)
.

A Markov process
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
is lumpable with respect to a partition
\Omega_{1},\ldots,\Omega_{r}

{\mathbb{P}}(X_{t+1}\in\Omega_{\ell}\mid X_{t}=x)={\mathbb{P}}(X_{t+1}\in\Omega_{\ell}\mid X_{t}=x^{\prime}),\qquad\forall x,x^{\prime}\in\Omega_{k}.

(4)

If the Markov chain is lumpable, it has eigenvectors that are block structured and equal to indicators functions of the subsets
[
48

However, a lumpable Markov chain is not necessarily low-rank. There may exist other eigenvectors corresponding to local dynamics within a subset. See Figure

Proposition 4

(Decomposition of Lumpable Markov Chains)
.

Let the Markov chain with transition matrix
\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
be lumpable with respect to a partition
\Omega_{1}\ldots,\Omega_{r}
.
Then there exist
\mathbf{P}_{1},\mathbf{P}_{2}
such that

\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{P}_{1}+\mathbf{P}_{2}
and
\mathbf{P}_{1}\mathbf{P}_{2}^{\top}=0,

where
\mathbf{P}_{1}
can be written as

\mathbf{P}_{1}=\mathbf{Z}\cdot\bar{\mathbf{P}}\cdot{\rm diag}(|\Omega_{1}|^{-1},\ldots,|\Omega_{r}|^{-1})\cdot\mathbf{Z}^{\top},

where
\mathbf{Z}=[\mathbf{1}_{\Omega_{1}},\ldots,\mathbf{1}_{\Omega_{r}}]\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times r}
,
\bar{\mathbf{P}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
is the stochastic matrix such that

\bar{\mathbf{P}}_{kl}=\mathbb{P}(X_{t+1}\in\Omega_{l}\mid X_{t}\in\Omega_{k})
.
Let the SVD of
\mathbf{P}_{1}
be
\mathbf{P}_{1}=\mathbf{U}_{P_{1}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{P_{1}}\mathbf{V}_{P_{1}}^{\top}

(\mathbf{U}_{P_{1}})_{[i,:]}=(\mathbf{U}_{P_{1}})_{[i^{\prime},:]}
and

Minimax Estimation of Low-Rank Markov Models

In this section we focus on the
p
-state Markov chain
\{X_{0},\ldots,X_{n}\}
which has a priorly known rank
r
.
Under the low-rank assumption, we aim to estimate the transition probability matrix
\mathbf{P}
based on a sample path of

4.1
A Spectral Method for Markov Chain Estimation

Consider a Markov chain with transition matrix
\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
and frequency matrix
\mathbf{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
. Suppose that
\hbox{rank}(\mathbf{P})=\hbox{rank}(\mathbf{F})=r
and we are given a
(n+1)
-long trajectory
\{X_{0},\ldots,X_{n}\}
starting at an arbitrary initial state.
It is natural to estimate
\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{F}
via the
empirical frequency matrix
and
empirical transition matrix
, given by

\tilde{\mathbf{F}}=\left(\tilde{\mathbf{F}}_{ij}\right)_{1\leq i,j\leq p},\quad\tilde{\mathbf{F}}_{ij}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}1_{\{X_{k-1}=i,X_{k}=j\}};

(5)

\tilde{\mathbf{P}}=\left(\tilde{\mathbf{P}}_{ij}\right)_{1\leq i,j\leq p},\quad\tilde{\mathbf{P}}_{ij}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{n}1_{\{X_{k-1}=i,X_{k}=j\}}}{\sum_{k=1}^{n}1_{\{X_{k-1}=i\}}},&\text{if }\sum_{k=1}^{n}1_{\{X_{k-1}=i\}}\geq 1;\\
\frac{1}{p},&\text{if }\sum_{k=1}^{n}1_{\{X_{k-1}=i\}}=0.\end{array}\right.

(6)

Here,
1_{\{\cdot\}}
is the indicator function and
1_{p}
is the
p
-dimensional vector with all ones. Note that
\tilde{\mathbf{F}},\tilde{\mathbf{P}}
are in fact the maximum likelihood estimators and strongly consistent
[
60
]
. However, they do not account for the knowledge of a small Markov rank. Consider the special case of
r=1
, where the Markov chain reduces to a sequence of i.i.d. random variables. Knowledge of
r=1
reduces the matrix estimation problem into estimation of a
p
-state discrete distribution. In contrast, the empirical estimators essentially look for a
p^{2}
-state distributions and will incur larger estimation errors.

We propose the following spectral method for estimating low-rank Markov chains.

Algorithm 1

Spectral Estimation of Low-rank Markov Models

Input:

X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
,
r

1.

Construct
\tilde{\mathbf{F}}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{P}}
using (
5
)-(
6
).

2.

Let the singular value decomposition (SVD) of
\tilde{\mathbf{F}}
be
\tilde{\mathbf{F}}=\tilde{\mathbf{U}}_{F}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{F}\tilde{\mathbf{V}}_{F}^{\top}
, where
\tilde{\mathbf{U}}_{F}
,
\tilde{\mathbf{V}}_{F}
are
p
-by-
p
orthogonal matrices and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{F}
is a
p
-by-
p

3.

Denoting
(x)_{+}=\max\{x,0\}

\hat{\mathbf{F}}
be

\hat{\mathbf{F}}=(\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{0})_{+}/\|(\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{0})_{+}\|_{1},\text{where}\quad\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{0}=\tilde{\mathbf{U}}_{F,[:,1:r]}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{F,[1:r,1:r]}(\tilde{\mathbf{V}}_{F,[:,1:r]})^{\top}.

(7)

4.

Let the transition matrix estimator
\hat{\mathbf{P}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
be

\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{[i,:]}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{[i,:]}/\sum_{j=1}^{p}\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{ij},&\text{if }\sum_{j=1}^{p}\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{ij}>0,\\
\frac{1}{p}1_{p},&\text{if }\sum_{j=1}^{p}\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{ij}=0,\end{array}\right.\quad i=1,\ldots,p.

(8)

Output:

\hat{\mathbf{P}}
,
\hat{\mathbf{F}}
.

Note that
\hat{\mathbf{F}},\hat{\mathbf{P}}
are not necessarily low-rank, due to the nonnegativity-preserving step
(\cdot)_{+}
in (
7
). However, Algorithm
1
still enables data compression, because
\hat{\mathbf{F}},\hat{\mathbf{P}}
can be easily constructed based on the low-rank matrix
\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{0}
. As an alternative to
\hat{\mathbf{F}}
, we can also apply the algorithm by
[
61
]
to project
\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{0}
onto the probability simplex to obtain an estimation of
\mathbf{F}
, and obtain

\hat{\mathbf{F}}^{1}=\mathop{\rm arg\min}_{\hat{\mathbf{F}}^{1}}\|\hat{\mathbf{F}}^{1}-\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{0}\|_{F}^{2}\quad\text{subject to}\quad\hat{\mathbf{F}}^{1}_{ij}\geq 0,\sum_{ij}\hat{\mathbf{F}}^{1}_{ij}=1.

The proposed estimators
\hat{\mathbf{F}},\hat{\mathbf{P}}
are related to the hard singular value thresholding estimators (HSVT), which were previously studied in matrix denoising
[
62
,
63
]
and matrix completion
[
32
]
. Our method and its subsequent analysis differ from that of HSVT in two aspects.
First, our estimation problem requires
\mathbf{P}
,
\mathbf{F}
to be stochastic matrices that belong to particular simplexes (see Lemma
1
in the supplement). This is achieved by normalizing rows of the matrices and truncating negative values, which complicates the analysis of the estimation errors.
Second, the analysis needs to account for the Markov dependency of the data, while the data are typically independent in matrix denoising and matrix completion.

Algorithm
1
requires
r
be selected in advance, which is needed by many other spectral-based methods. In practice, this value can be chosen empirically, for example one can (1) draw a scree plot for the SVD of
\tilde{\mathbf{F}}
, i.e., the cumulative ratio of total variance as explained by the leading principal components, then select
r
as the location of the “elbow” in the scree plot; (2) evaluate the smallest
r
such that the first
r
principal components explain a certain level of total variation criterion, and (3) test by cross-validation. The readers are referred to
[
64
]
for a comprehensive discussion for rank selection.

4.2
Total Variation Upper Bound

Our first main result establishes the total variation distance upper bound between the proposed estimator and the truth.

Theorem 1

(Upper Bound)
.

Suppose
\{X_{0},\ldots,X_{n}\}
is generated by an ergodic Markov chain with transition probability matrix
\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
, invariant distribution
\pi\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, and mixing time
\tau_{\ast}
. Let
\hat{\mathbf{F}}
,
\hat{\mathbf{P}}
be the estimators given by (
7
)-(
8
). If
\hbox{rank}(\mathbf{P})=r
, we have

\mathbb{E}\|\hat{\mathbf{F}}-\mathbf{F}\|_{1}\leq\sqrt{\frac{Crp}{n}\cdot\pi_{\max}p\cdot\tau_{\ast}\log^{2}(n)}\wedge 2,

(9)

\mathbb{E}\|\hat{\mathbf{F}}^{1}-\mathbf{F}\|_{1}\leq\sqrt{\frac{Crp}{n}\cdot\pi_{\max}p\cdot\tau_{\ast}\log^{2}(n)}\wedge 2,

\mathbb{E}\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\|\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{[i,:]}-\mathbf{P}_{[i,:]}\|_{1}\leq\sqrt{\frac{Cr}{n}\cdot\frac{\pi_{\max}}{\pi_{\min}^{2}}\cdot\tau_{\ast}\log^{2}(n)}\wedge 2.

(10)

Let
\tilde{r}=\|\mathbf{F}\|_{F}^{2}/\sigma_{r}^{2}(\mathbf{F})
,
\kappa=p^{2}\max_{ij}\mathbf{F}_{ij}
. Then

\mathbb{E}\max_{1\leq i\leq p}\|\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{[i,:]}-\mathbf{P}_{[i,:]}\|_{1}\leq\sqrt{\frac{C\tilde{r}}{n}\cdot\frac{\kappa^{3}}{p\pi_{\min}^{2}}\cdot\tau_{\ast}\log^{2}(n)}\wedge 2,

(11)

where
\sigma_{r}(\mathbf{F})
is the
r
-th singular value of
\mathbf{F}
,
C
is a universal constant.

The proof of (
9
) and (
10
) relies on novel matrix Markov chain concentration inequalities with mixing time (Lemma
7
), which characterizes the 2-norm distance between
\tilde{\mathbf{F}}
and
\mathbf{F}
. Then based on the low-rank assumption of
\mathbf{F}
and
\mathbf{P}
, a careful spectral analysis (Lemma
3
) is performed to obtain the average error bound for
\hat{\mathbf{F}}
and
\hat{\mathbf{P}}
. The proof of (
11
) is more involved. By using similar arguments, we can prove that
\hat{\mathbf{F}}^{1}
also achieves the
\ell_{2}
risk upper bound in (
9
). Particularly, we derived concentration inequalities for projected Markov chains (Lemma
8
), performed a more careful algebraic analysis, and obtained the uniform upper bound of total deviation for
\hat{\mathbf{P}}
.
In what follows we make a few technical remarks.

Remark 1

(Spectral estimators
\hat{\mathbf{P}},\hat{\mathbf{F}}
vs. Empirical estimators
\tilde{\mathbf{P}},\tilde{\mathbf{F}}
)
.

Theorem
1
shows that
\mathbb{E}\|\hat{\mathbf{F}}-\mathbf{F}\|_{1}\asymp\mathbb{E}\frac{1}{p}\|\hat{\mathbf{P}}-\mathbf{P}\|_{1}\asymp\sqrt{pr/n}
, assuming all other parameters are fixed.
In comparison, we can show that
\mathbb{E}\|\tilde{\mathbf{F}}-\mathbf{F}\|_{1}\asymp\mathbb{E}\frac{1}{p}\|\tilde{\mathbf{P}}-\mathbf{P}\|_{1}\asymp\sqrt{p^{2}/n}
, based on minimax error bounds for discrete distribution estimation
[
25
,
26
]
. Therefore
the spectral estimators are much more efficient because they utilize the low-rank structure. Numerical comparisons between the spectral and empirical estimators are given in Section
6
.

Remark 2

(Dependence on the stationary distribution)
.

The error bounds of Theorem
1
rely on
\pi_{\max}
and
\pi_{\min}
, and they take smaller values if
\pi
does not deviate much from the uniform distribution. When
\pi_{\min}
is small, one has to pay a higher price for those states appearing the least frequently in the sample path. The dependence on
\pi_{max}
is due to a technical argument used in the proof to establish spectral norm concentration inequalities for asymmetric matrices (Lemma
7
), which may be improvable under additional assumptions like reversibility.

Remark 3

(Dependence on the mixing time)
.

The error bounds of
\hat{\mathbf{F}},\hat{\mathbf{P}}
involve a key quantity of Markov mixing time
\tau_{\ast}
, whose actual value could be difficult to evaluate in practice
[
65
]
. We further show that similar error bounds like those in Theorem
1
hold if the mixing time is replaced with some eigengap. Please see Section
B.2
Corollary for a generalization of Theorem
1
using an eigengap condition. Further improvement of the error bounds will require novel Markov concentration inequalities that have been developed in recent literature (see
[
66
,
67
]
).

Remark 4

(About the row-wise uniform bounds)
.

We introduce the entry-wise upper bound condition of
\kappa
for establishing the row-wise uniform upper bound (
11
). The dependence on
\kappa
suggests that estimating “overly-spiky” matrices is typically more difficult. Similar conditions were also used in the literature of low-rank matrix estimation (e.g.
[
27
,
28
]
).

4.3
Minimax Lower Bound for Estimating Low-Rank Markov Chains

Now we investigate the information-theoretic limits of recovering low-rank Markov models.
Consider the following class of low-rank transition matrices

\begin{split}\mathcal{P}_{p,r}=\left\{\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{p},\hbox{rank}(\mathbf{P})\leq r\right\},\end{split}

(12)

where
\mathcal{P}_{p}
is the class of all
p
-by-
p
transition matrices (see its definition in Eq. (
54
) in the supplementary material). Furthermore, we consider a more restricted class of low-rank Markov models with bounded mixing time and uniform ergodic distributions, given by

\begin{split}\mathcal{P}_{p,r}^{\ast}=\left\{\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{p,r}:\tau_{\ast}=1,\pi_{\max}=\pi_{\min}=1/p\right\}.\end{split}

(13)

We provide error lower bounds for recovering transition matrices within the aforementioned classes from finite trajectories.

Theorem 2

(Lower Bound)
.

Suppose we observe
(n+1)
consecutive transition states
\{X_{0},\ldots,X_{n}\}
, where the starting point
X_{0}
is randomly generated from the invariant distribution. Then

\begin{split}\inf_{\hat{\mathbf{P}}}\sup_{\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{p,r}}\mathbb{E}\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\left\|\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{[i,:]}-\mathbf{P}_{[i,:]}\right\|_{1}\geq\inf_{\hat{\mathbf{P}}}\sup_{\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{P}^{\ast}_{p,r}}\mathbb{E}\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\left\|\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{[i,:]}-\mathbf{P}_{[i,:]}\right\|_{1}\geq c\left(\sqrt{\frac{rp}{n}}\wedge 1\right),\end{split}

\begin{split}\inf_{\hat{\mathbf{F}}}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{F}={\rm diag}(\pi)\mathbf{P};\\
\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{p,r}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\left\|\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{[i,:]}-\mathbf{F}_{[i,:]}\right\|_{1}\geq\inf_{\hat{\mathbf{F}}}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{F}={\rm diag}(\pi)\mathbf{P};\\
\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{p,r}^{\ast}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\left\|\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{[i,:]}-\mathbf{F}_{[i,:]}\right\|_{1}\geq c\left(\sqrt{\frac{rp}{n}}\wedge 1\right),\\
\end{split}

where
c>0
is a universal constant,
\inf_{\hat{\mathbf{F}}}
and
\inf_{\hat{\mathbf{P}}}
are taken infimum over arbitrary estimators
\hat{\mathbf{P}}
and
\hat{\mathbf{F}}
, respectively.

The proof is by constructing a series of instances of low-rank Markov chains with uniform stationary distributions and constant mixing times.We show that these instances are not distinguishable based on
(n+1)
sample transitions, by using the generalized Fano’s lemma. See Section
B.3
for the full proof.

Let us compare Theorems
1
and
2
. The error upper bounds achieved by the spectral estimators
\hat{\mathbf{F}},\hat{\mathbf{P}}
are nearly minimax-optimal in their dependence on
r,p,n
(up to polylogarithmic terms), as long as parameters of the ergodic distribution
\pi_{\max}/\pi_{\min}
and the mixing time
\tau_{\ast}
are bounded by constants. This suggests that our spectral estimators are statistically efficient for fast mixing Markov processes as long as the ergodic distribution is balanced. It is not yet known whether the dependence on
\pi_{\max}/\pi_{\min}
and
\tau_{\ast}
is optimal.

4.4
Extension to Rectangular Probability Matrix

The proposed spectral method can be extended to estimating a broader class of probability matrices - not limited to transition matrices of Markov chains. An example of such an estimation problem arises from policy imitation in reinforcement learning, where one observes a sequence of state-action pairs generated by an expert policy that is applied in a Markov decision process. In this case, the expert policy can be represented using a transition probability matrix where each entry assigns the probability of choosing an action at a given state. The policy matrix is typically low-rank, as long as the Markov decision process admits state aggregation structures or can be represented using membership models.

Specifically, suppose we are given a stochastic process
\{(X_{0},Y_{0}),(X_{1},Y_{1}),\ldots,(X_{n},Y_{n})\}
. We assume that
\{X_{0},X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\}
is an ergodic Markov process on
p
states with invariant distribution
\pi
and Markov mixing time
\tau_{\ast}
. We are interested in estimating the transition matrix
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}
such that

\mathbf{Q}_{ij}=\mathbb{P}(Y_{k}=j\mid X_{k}=i),

for all
k,i,j.

Analogous to Section
4.1
, we propose a spectral estimator for

assuming that it has a priorly known rank

.

5
Spectral State Compression of Nearly Low-rank Markov Chains

In this section we consider general Markov processes with full rank.
Our aim is to recover the principal subspace associated with
\mathbf{P}
that is spanned by the leading Markov features. We also provide two state clustering methods that are able to partition the state space into disjoint blocks in accordance with the aggregability or lumpability.

5.1
Estimating the Leading Markov Feature Subspace

As noted in Section
3
, spectral decomposition of Markov chains provides feature functions that can be used to represent operators and functions on the state space. The Markov features also correspond to the block-partition membership when aggregability holds. Now we aim to estimate the space spanned by leading Markov features.

Let the singular value decomposition of
\mathbf{P}
and

\mathbf{P}=\left[\mathbf{U}_{P}~\mathbf{U}_{P\perp}\right]\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{P1}&0\\

5.2
Spectral State Compression For Aggregable Markov Chain

Next we develop an unsupervised state compression method based on the state aggregation model. According to Definition

\mathbf{P}

Algorithm 3

Solve the optimization problem

\hat{\Omega}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\Omega}_{r}=\mathop{\rm arg\min}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\hat{\Omega}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\Omega}_{r}\end{subarray}}\min_{\bar{v}_{1},\ldots,\bar{v}_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}}\sum_{s=1}^{r}\sum_{i\in\hat{\Omega}_{s}}\|(\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{P})_{[i,:]}-\bar{v}_{s}\|_{2}^{2}.

Output:
Blocks
\hat{\Omega}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\Omega}_{r}

We evaluate the state aggregation method using the following misclassification rate

M(\hat{\Omega}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\Omega}_{r})=\min_{\rho}\sum_{j=1}^{r}\frac{|\{i:i\in\Omega_{j},\text{ but }i\notin\hat{\Omega}_{\rho(j)}\}|}{|\Omega_{j}|},

(21)

where
\rho
is any permutation among the
r
group. We prove the following misclassification rate upper bound. The proof is given in the supplementary materials.

Theorem 6

(Misclassification Rate of Spectral State Aggregation)
.

Suppose the assumptions in Theorem
1
hold and the Markov chain is aggregable with respect to groups
\{\Omega_{1},\ldots,\Omega_{r}\}
. Assume
n\geq C\tau_{\ast}p\log^{2}(n)
. The estimated partition
\hat{\Omega}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\Omega}_{r}
given by Alg.
3
satisfies

\mathbb{E}M(\hat{\Omega}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\Omega}_{r})\leq\frac{C\|\mathbf{P}\|^{2}pr\cdot\tau_{\ast}\log^{2}(n)\cdot\pi_{\max}/(\pi_{\min}^{2}p)}{n\sigma_{r}^{2}(\mathbf{P})}\wedge r.

We remark that the state aggregation structure can only be uncovered from the
left
Markov features. As suggested by Prop.
3
, the left Markov features of a state-aggregable Markov process exhibit a block structure that corresponds to the latent partition. The right features do not carry such information.

The proposed method of spectral state aggregation can be viewed as a special variant of clustering. It provides an unsupervised approach to identify partition/patterns from random walk data. Similar to many known clustering methods, spectral state aggregation is based on spectral decomposition of some kernel matrix, and it is related to latent-variable models. Yet there is a critical distinction. While standard clustering methods are typically based on some similarity metric, spectral state aggregation is based on the notion of preserving the state-to-state transition dynamics of the time series. One may view that spectral state clustering yields a partition mapping
E
from the state space into a smaller alphabet such that
\mathbb{P}(X_{t+1}\mid X_{t})\approx\mathbb{P}(X_{t+1}\mid E(X_{t})).

It can be interpreted as a form of state compression while preserving the predictability of the state variables.

5.3
Spectral State Compression For Lumpable Markov Chain

Finally we develop the state compression method for lumpable Markov process. Recall the discussion in Section
3

7
Summary

Markov process is the most basic stochastic systems. Therefore we believe that state compression of the Markov process is naturally the first topic to investigate before moving on to more complicated problems. In this article, we studied the spectral decomposition of Markov processes and its connections to latent-variable process, aggregability and lumpability. We proposed a class of spectral state compression methods for analyzing Markov state trajectories, and established minimax upper and lower bounds for the estimation errors. For special cases where the Markov process is state-aggregatable or lumpable, we show that one can recover the underlying partition structure with theoretical guarantees. The numerical studies on both synthetic and real datasets illustrate the merits of the proposed methods. We hope that establishing the spectral state compression theory for Markov process would shed light on the estimation and system identification of higher-order processes that are not necessarily Markovian.

be the economic-size SVD. Then for any
k=1,\ldots,r
,
i,i^{\prime}\in\Omega_{k}
, i.e.,
i,i^{\prime}
belonging to the same block, by definition of

and
\mathbf{Z}

({\mathbf{P}}_{1})_{[i,:]}=\mathbf{Z}_{[i,:]}\bar{\mathbf{P}}{\rm diag}(|\Omega_{1}|^{-1},\ldots,|\Omega_{r}|^{-1})\mathbf{Z}^{\top}=\mathbf{Z}_{[i^{\prime},:]}\bar{\mathbf{P}}{\rm diag}(|\Omega_{1}|^{-1},\ldots,|\Omega_{r}|^{-1})\mathbf{Z}^{\top}=({\mathbf{P}}_{1})_{[i^{\prime},:]}.

Then,

(\mathbf{U}_{P_{1}})_{[i,:]}=(\mathbf{P}_{1})_{[i,:]}\mathbf{V}_{P_{1}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{P_{1}}^{-1}=(\mathbf{P}_{1})_{[i^{\prime},:]}\mathbf{V}_{P_{1}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{P_{1}}^{-1}=(\mathbf{U}_{P_{1}})_{[i^{\prime},:]}.

By the same argument, we can also show
(\mathbf{V}_{P_{1}})_{[i,:]}=(\mathbf{V}_{P_{1}})_{[i^{\prime},:]}
.

In fact, the frequency matrix
\mathbf{F}
also has the similar decomposition since
\mathbf{F}={\rm diag}(\pi)\mathbf{P}
,

\mathbf{F}=\mathbf{F}_{1}+\mathbf{F}_{2},\quad\text{where}\quad\mathbf{F}_{1}={\rm diag}(\pi)\mathbf{P}_{1},\mathbf{F}_{2}={\rm diag}(\pi)\mathbf{P}_{2}.

For this decomposition, we also have

\mathbf{F}_{1}\mathbf{F}_{2}^{\top}={\rm diag}(\pi)\mathbf{F}_{1}\mathbf{F}_{2}^{\top}{\rm diag}(\pi)=0.

Although
\mathbf{F}_{1}
is not necessarily symmetric and the columns of
\mathbf{F}_{1}
may not have piece-wise constant structure, the rows of
\mathbf{F}_{1}
is still piece-wise constant according to partition, i.e.,
\mathbf{F}_{1,ij}=\mathbf{F}_{1,ij^{\prime}}
if
j,j^{\prime}
belong to the same group. By the similar argument as the one for
\mathbf{P}_{1}
, we can show the right singular vectors

is also piece-wise constant, i.e.,
(\mathbf{V}_{F_{1}})_{[i,:]}=(\mathbf{V}_{F_{1}})_{[i^{\prime},:]}
if
i,i^{\prime}
belong to the same group.

B.2
Eigen-gap Condition

Eigengap condition:
When
\mathbf{P}
satisfies the
detailed balance condition
, i.e.,
\pi_{i}\mathbf{P}_{ij}=\pi_{j}\mathbf{P}_{ji}
for any
1\leq i,j\leq p
, or equivalently
\mathbf{F}
is symmetric, the corresponding Markov process is referred to as being
reversible
. The reversibility is an important and widely considered condition in stochastic process literature. When the Markov process is reversible, it is well-known that all eigenvalues of
\mathbf{P}
must be real and between
-1
and
1
; the largest eigenvalue of a reversible Markov transition matrix is always 1
[
52
, Chapter 12]
. Suppose the second largest eigenvalue of
\mathbf{P}
is
\lambda_{2}<1
, then
1-\lambda_{2}
plays an important role in regulating the connectivity of the Markov chain: the more close
\lambda_{2}
is to 1, the more likely the Markov chain is congested. Moreover, the eigengap of reversible Markov processes can be estimated from the observable states via a plug-in estimator
[
65
]
.

The following results hold as an extension of Theorem
1
based on eigengap assumption.

Corollary 1
.

Under the assumption of Theorem
1
, if
\mathbf{P}
is reversible and with second largest eigenvalue
\lambda_{2}<1
, then (
9
), (
10
), and (
11
) hold if one replace
\tau_{\ast}\log^{2}(n)
by
\log(n/\pi_{\min})\log(n)/(1-\lambda_{2})
.

Proof of Corollary
1
.
If the Markov process is reversible and
1-\lambda_{2}
is the eigengap (see Section
B.2
), by Lemma
7
, one has

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\max\{\|\tilde{\mathbf{F}}-\mathbf{F}\|,\|\tilde{\pi}-\pi\|_{\infty}\}\geq C\sqrt{\frac{\pi_{\max}\log(n/\pi_{\min})\log(n)}{n(1-\lambda_{2})}}\right)\leq n^{-c_{0}}.

By replacing
\tau_{\ast}\log^{2}(n)
by
\log(n/\pi_{\min})/(1-\lambda_{2})
, the proof for Corollary
1
immediately follows from the arguments in Theorem
1
.
\square

Proof of Theorem

First, we study the Kullback-Leibler divergence between two Markov processes with same the same state space
\{1,\ldots,p\}
but different transition matrices
\mathbf{P}
and

\mathbf{P}

\mathbf{Q}

=&\sum_{i_{0},\ldots,i_{n}\in[p]^{n+1}}\pi_{i_{0}}\mathbf{P}_{i_{0},i_{1}}\cdots\mathbf{P}_{i_{n-1},i_{n}}\log\left(\frac{\pi_{i_{0}}\mathbf{P}_{i_{0},i_{1}}\cdots\mathbf{P}_{i_{n-1},i_{n}}}{\pi_{i_{0}}\mathbf{Q}_{i_{0},i_{1}}\cdots\mathbf{Q}_{i_{n-1},i_{n}}}\right)\\
=&\sum_{i_{0},\ldots,i_{n-1}\in[p]^{n}}\sum_{i_{n}\in[p]}\pi_{i_{0}}\mathbf{P}_{i_{0},i_{1}}\cdots\mathbf{P}_{i_{n-1},i_{n}}\left\{\log\left(\frac{\pi_{i_{0}}\mathbf{P}_{i_{0},i_{1}}\cdots\mathbf{P}_{i_{n-2},i_{n-1}}}{\pi_{i_{0}}\mathbf{Q}_{i_{0},i_{1}}\cdots\mathbf{Q}_{i_{n-2},i_{n-1}}}\right)+\log\left(\frac{\mathbf{P}_{i_{n-1},i_{n}}}{\mathbf{Q}_{i_{n-1},i_{n}}}\right)\right\}\\
=&D_{KL}\left(\{x_{0}^{(1)},\ldots,x_{n-1}^{(1)}\}||\{x_{0}^{(2)},\ldots,x_{n-1}^{(2)}\}\right)+\sum_{i_{n-1}\in[p]}\pi_{i_{n-1}}\sum_{i_{n}\in[p]}\mathbf{P}_{i_{n-1},i_{n}}\log\left(\frac{\mathbf{P}_{i_{n-1},i_{n}}}{\mathbf{Q}_{i_{n-1},i_{n}}}\right)\\
=&D_{KL}\left(\{x_{0}^{(1)},\ldots,x_{n-1}^{(1)}\}||\{x_{0}^{(2)},\ldots,x_{n-1}^{(2)}\}\right)+\sum_{i\in[p]}\pi_{i}D_{KL}\left(\mathbf{P}_{[i,:]}||\mathbf{Q}_{[i,:]}\right).\end{split}

Then it is easy to use induction to show that

\begin{split}&D_{KL}\left(X^{(1)}||X^{(2)}\right)\\
=&D_{KL}\left(\{x^{(1)}_{0},\ldots,x_{n-1}^{(1)}\}||\{x^{(2)}_{0},\ldots,x_{n-1}^{(2)}\}\right)+\sum_{i\in[p]}\pi_{i}D_{KL}\left(\mathbf{P}_{[i,:]}||\mathbf{Q}_{[i,:]}\right)\\
=&\cdots=D_{KL}\left(x_{0}^{(1)}\Big|\Big|x_{0}^{(2)}\right)+n\sum_{i\in[p]}\pi_{i}D_{KL}\left(\mathbf{P}_{[i,:]}||\mathbf{Q}_{[i,:]}\right).\end{split}

(36)

Next, we prove the lower bound for estimating
\mathbf{F}

-\mathbf{R}^{(k)}~~\cdots~~-\mathbf{R}^{(k)}&~~\mathbf{R}^{(k)}~~\cdots~~~~~~\mathbf{R}^{(k)}&\mathbf{0}_{p_{0}\times(p-2l_{0}(r-1))}\\
\mathbf{0}_{(p-2p_{0})\times(l_{0}(r-1))}&\mathbf{0}_{(p-2p_{0})\times(l_{0}(r-1))}&\mathbf{0}_{(p-2p_{0})\times(p-2l_{0}(r-1))}\\
\end{bmatrix}\end{split}

(37)

Here

are i.i.d. Bernoulli

\hbox{rank}(\mathbf{R}^{(k)})\leq r-1
, we also have
\hbox{rank}(\mathbf{P}^{(k)})\leq r
. Additionally, it is easy to see that each entry of
\mathbf{P}^{(k)}
is between
(1/p-\eta/2p)
and
(1/p+\eta/(2p))
. Thus for any
1\leq i\leq p
,

\begin{split}\left\|e_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{P}^{(k)}-\pi\right\|_{1}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}|\mathbf{P}^{(k)}_{ij}-\pi_{j}|\leq p\cdot\eta/(2p)\leq 1/4,\end{split}

which means
\tau_{\ast}:=\tau(1/4)\leq 1
. By definitions of
\mathcal{P}_{p,r}
and
\mathcal{P}_{p,r}^{\ast}
, we have for any
k
that

\mathbf{P}^{(k)}\in\mathcal{P}^{\ast}_{p,r}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{p,r}.

(38)

Now for any
k\neq l
,

\begin{split}&\|\mathbf{F}^{(k)}-\mathbf{F}^{(l)}\|_{1}=\frac{1}{p}\|\mathbf{P}^{(k)}-\mathbf{P}^{(l)}\|_{1}=\frac{2l_{0}\eta}{p^{2}}\|\mathbf{R}^{(k)}-\mathbf{R}^{(l)}\|_{1}=\frac{2l_{0}\eta}{p^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{p_{0}}\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}\left|\mathbf{R}^{(k)}_{ij}-\mathbf{R}^{(l)}_{ij}\right|.\end{split}

It is easy to see that
\left\{\left|\mathbf{R}^{(k)}_{ij}-\mathbf{R}^{(l)}_{ij}\right|\right\}
are i.i.d. uniformly distributed on
\{0,2\}
. These random variables also satisfy

\mathbb{E}\left|\mathbf{R}^{(k)}_{ij}-\mathbf{R}^{(l)}_{ij}\right|=1,\quad{\rm Var}\left(\left|\mathbf{R}^{(k)}_{ij}-\mathbf{R}^{(l)}_{ij}\right|\right)=1,\quad\left|\left|\mathbf{R}^{(k)}_{ij}-\mathbf{R}^{(l)}_{ij}\right|-1\right|=1.

By Bernstein’s inequality, for any
\varepsilon>0
we have

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\left|\left\|\mathbf{F}^{(k)}-\mathbf{F}^{(l)}\right\|_{1}-\frac{2l_{0}\eta p_{0}(r-1)}{p^{2}}\right|\geq\frac{2l_{0}\eta}{p^{2}}\varepsilon\right)\leq 2\exp\left(\frac{-\varepsilon^{2}/2}{p_{0}(r-1)+\varepsilon/3}\right)

Set
\varepsilon=p_{0}(r-1)/2
,
m=\sqrt{\lfloor\exp(p_{0}(r-1)/28)\rfloor}
, then we further have

\begin{split}&{\mathbb{P}}\left(\forall 1\leq k<l\leq m,~~\frac{l_{0}\eta p_{0}(r-1)}{p^{2}}\leq\left\|\mathbf{F}^{(k)}-\mathbf{F}^{(l)}\right\|_{1}\leq\frac{3l_{0}\eta p_{0}(r-1)}{p^{2}}\right)\\
\geq&1-m(m-1)\exp\left(\frac{-p_{0}(r-1)}{28}\right)>1-m^{2}\exp\left(\frac{-p_{0}(r-1)}{28}\right)>0.\end{split}

By such an argument, we can see there exists
\left\{\mathbf{R}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{R}^{(m)}\right\}\subseteq\{-1,1\}^{p_{0}\times(r-1)}
such that

\forall 1\leq k<l\leq m,\quad\frac{l_{0}\eta p_{0}(r-1)}{p^{2}}\leq\left\|\mathbf{F}^{(k)}-\mathbf{F}^{(l)}\right\|_{1}\leq\frac{3l_{0}\eta p_{0}(r-1)}{p^{2}}.

(39)

We thus assume (
39
) is satisfied.

Next, we construct
m
Markov chains of length
(n+1)
:
\{X^{(1)},\ldots,X^{{(m)}}\}
. For each
k\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
,
x^{(k)}_{0}\sim\frac{1_{p}}{p}
, and the rest of the states are generated according to
\mathbf{P}^{(k)}
and
\mathbf{F}^{(k)}
. Based on the calculation in (
36
),

\begin{split}D_{KL}\left(X^{(k)}\Big|\Big|X^{(l)}\right)=\frac{n}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}D_{KL}\left(\mathbf{P}_{[i,:]}^{(k)}\Big|\Big|\mathbf{P}_{[i,:]}^{(l)}\right)\end{split}

Based on Lemma
4
and
1/(2p)\leq\mathbf{P}^{(k)}_{ij}\leq 3/(2p)
, we further have
D_{KL}\left(\mathbf{P}_{[i,:]}^{(k)}\Big|\Big|\mathbf{P}_{[i,:]}^{(l)}\right)\leq 3p\|\mathbf{P}_{[i,:]}^{(k)}-\mathbf{P}_{[i,:]}^{(l)}\|_{2}^{2}
. Thus, for any
1\leq k<l\leq m
,

\begin{split}&D_{KL}\left(X^{(k)}\Big|\Big|X^{(l)}\right)\leq 3n\sum_{i=1}^{p}\|\mathbf{P}_{[i,:]}^{(k)}-\mathbf{P}_{[i,:]}^{(l)}\|_{2}^{2}=3n\sum_{i,j=1}^{p}\left(\mathbf{P}_{ij}^{(k)}-\mathbf{P}_{ij}^{(l)}\right)^{2}\\
\leq&\frac{6n\eta}{p}\sum_{i,j=1}^{p}\left|\mathbf{P}_{ij}^{(k)}-\mathbf{P}_{ij}^{(l)}\right|\leq 6n\eta\cdot\|\mathbf{F}^{(k)}-\mathbf{F}^{(l)}\|_{1}\leq\frac{18n\eta^{2}l_{0}p_{0}(r-1)}{p^{2}}.\end{split}

Now, by the generalized Fano’s lemma (see, e.g.,
[
69
,
70
]
), we have

\inf_{\hat{\mathbf{F}}}\sup_{\mathbf{F}\in\{\mathbf{F}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{F}^{(m)}\}}\mathbb{E}\left\|\hat{\mathbf{F}}-\mathbf{F}\right\|_{1}\geq\frac{l_{0}\eta p_{0}(r-1)}{p^{2}}\left(1-\frac{18n\eta^{2}l_{0}p_{0}(r-1)/p^{2}+\log 2}{\log m}\right)

Finally, we set
\eta^{2}=\left\{\frac{p^{2}}{18nl_{0}p_{0}(r-1)}\left(\frac{1}{2}\log(m)-\log(2)\right)\right\}\wedge 1/2
and apply (
38
),

\begin{split}&\inf_{\hat{\mathbf{F}}}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{p,r}\\
\mathbf{F}={\rm diag}(\pi)\mathbf{P}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}\left\|\hat{\mathbf{F}}-\mathbf{F}\right\|_{1}\geq\inf_{\hat{\mathbf{F}}}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{p,r}\\
\mathbf{F}={\rm diag}(\pi)\mathbf{P}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}\left\|\hat{\mathbf{F}}-\mathbf{F}\right\|_{1}\geq\inf_{\hat{\mathbf{F}}}\sup_{\mathbf{F}\in\{\mathbf{F}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{F}^{(m)}\}}\mathbb{E}\left\|\hat{\mathbf{F}}-\mathbf{F}\right\|_{1}\\
\geq&\frac{p_{0}l_{0}(r-1)}{2p^{2}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{p^{2}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{2}\log(m)-\log(2)\right)}{18np_{0}l_{0}(r-1)}}\geq c\sqrt{\frac{pr}{n}\wedge 1}.\end{split}

Finally, since
\mathbf{P}^{(k)}=p\mathbf{F}^{(k)}
based on the set-up,

\begin{split}&\inf_{\hat{\mathbf{P}}}\sup_{\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{p,r}}\mathbb{E}\frac{1}{p}\left\|\hat{\mathbf{P}}-\mathbf{P}\right\|_{1}\geq\inf_{\hat{\mathbf{P}}}\sup_{\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{P}^{\ast}_{p,r}}\frac{1}{p}\mathbb{E}\left\|\hat{\mathbf{P}}-\mathbf{P}\right\|_{1}\geq\inf_{\hat{\mathbf{P}}}\sup_{\mathbf{P}\in\{\mathbf{P}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{P}^{(m)}\}}\frac{1}{p}\mathbb{E}\left\|\hat{\mathbf{P}}-\mathbf{P}\right\|_{1}\\
=&p\inf_{\hat{\mathbf{F}}}\sup_{\mathbf{F}\in\{\mathbf{F}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{F}^{(m)}\}}\frac{1}{p}\mathbb{E}\left\|\hat{\mathbf{F}}-\mathbf{F}\right\|_{1}\geq c\sqrt{\frac{pr}{n}\wedge 1}.\end{split}

\square

C.2
Proof of Theorem
4

By Lemma
7
, one has

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\|\tilde{\mathbf{F}}-\mathbf{F}\|\geq C\sqrt{\pi_{\max}\tau_{\ast}\log^{2}(n)/n}\right)\leq n^{-c_{0}}.

Wedin’s lemma
[
71
]
implies

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\max\left\{\|\sin\Theta(\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{F},\mathbf{U}_{F})\|,\|\sin\Theta(\hat{\mathbf{V}}_{F},\mathbf{V}_{F})\|\right\}\leq\frac{C\sqrt{\pi_{\max}\tau_{\ast}\log^{2}(n)/n}}{\sigma_{r}(\mathbf{F})-\sigma_{r+1}(\mathbf{F})}\right)\geq 1-n^{-c_{0}}.

Let
Q
be the event that the above inequality holds. Since the trivial bound

\max\{\|\sin\Theta(\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{F},\mathbf{U}_{F})\|,\|\sin\Theta(\hat{\mathbf{V}}_{F},\mathbf{V}_{F})\|\}\leq 1

holds, we must have

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\max\left\{\|\sin\Theta(\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{F},\mathbf{U}_{F})\|,\|\sin\Theta(\hat{\mathbf{V}}_{F},\mathbf{V}_{F})\|\right\}\\
\leq&\mathbb{E}\max\left\{\|\sin\Theta(\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{F},\mathbf{U}_{F})\|,\|\sin\Theta(\hat{\mathbf{V}}_{F},\mathbf{V}_{F})\|\right\}1_{Q}\\
&+\mathbb{E}\max\left\{\|\sin\Theta(\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{F},\mathbf{U}_{F})\|,\|\sin\Theta(\hat{\mathbf{V}}_{F},\mathbf{V}_{F})\|\right\}1_{Q^{c}}\\
\leq&\frac{C\sqrt{\pi_{\max}\tau_{\ast}\log^{2}(n)/n}}{\sigma_{r}(\mathbf{F})-\sigma_{r+1}(\mathbf{F})}+1\cdot{\mathbb{P}}(Q^{c})\leq\frac{C\sqrt{\pi_{\max}\tau_{\ast}\log^{2}(n)/n}}{\sigma_{r}(\mathbf{F})-\sigma_{r+1}(\mathbf{F})}+\frac{1}{n^{c_{0}}}.\end{split}

Since
\sum_{i,j}\mathbf{F}_{ij}=1
and
0\leq\mathbf{F}_{ij}\leq 1
, we must have

0\leq\sigma_{r}(\mathbf{F})-\sigma_{r+1}(\mathbf{F})\leq\left(\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}^{2}(\mathbf{F})\right)^{1/2}=\|\mathbf{F}\|_{F}=\left(\sum_{ij}\mathbf{F}_{ij}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leq\|\mathbf{F}\|_{1}^{1/2}\leq 1,\quad\pi_{\max}\geq 1/p.

Thus, if
c_{0}>1
, one has
1/n^{c_{0}}\leq\frac{C\sqrt{\pi_{\max}\tau_{\ast}\log^{2}(n)/n}}{\sigma_{r}(\mathbf{F})-\sigma_{r+1}(\mathbf{F})}
and

\mathbb{E}\max\left\{\|\sin\Theta(\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{F},\mathbf{U}_{F})\|,\|\sin\Theta(\hat{\mathbf{V}}_{F},\mathbf{V}_{F})\|\right\}\leq\frac{C\sqrt{\pi_{\max}\tau_{\ast}\log^{2}(n)/n}}{\sigma_{r}(\mathbf{F})-\sigma_{r+1}(\mathbf{F})}\wedge 1,

which implies (
20
).

Next we consider
\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{P}
, and
\hat{\mathbf{V}}_{P}
.
Note that
\|\mathbf{P}\|/(\sigma_{r}(\mathbf{P})-\sigma_{r+1}(\mathbf{P}))\geq 1
. If
n\leq Cp\pi_{\max}/(\pi_{\min}^{2}p)\tau_{\ast}\log^{2}(n)
, the trivial bound
\mathbb{E}\left(\|\sin\Theta(\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{P},\mathbf{U}_{P})\|\vee\|\sin\Theta(\hat{\mathbf{V}}_{P},\mathbf{V})\|\right)\leq 1
has already provided sharp enough result for proving (
20
). Thus for the rest of proof, we assume
n\geq Cp\pi_{\max}/(\pi_{\min}^{2}p)\tau_{\ast}\log^{2}(n)
for large enough constant
C
. Let
\tilde{\pi}
be the empirical distribution of
\pi
,

\tilde{\pi}\in\mathbb{R}^{p},\quad\tilde{\pi}_{i}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}1_{\{X_{k-1}=i\}}.

Provided that
n\geq C\frac{\pi_{\max}\tau_{\ast}\log^{2}(n)}{\pi_{\min}^{2}}
for large enough constant
C>0
, we have

\begin{split}\|\tilde{\pi}-\pi\|_{\infty}\leq C\sqrt{\frac{\pi_{\max}\tau_{\ast}\log^{2}(n)}{n}}\leq\frac{1}{2}\pi_{\min}.\end{split}

Then

\min_{i}\tilde{\pi}_{i}\geq\min_{i}\pi_{i}-\|\tilde{\pi}-\pi\|_{\infty}\geq\frac{1}{2}\pi_{\min},

(42)

and

\left|\pi_{i}/\tilde{\pi}_{i}-1\right|=\frac{|\pi_{i}-\tilde{\pi}_{i}|}{\tilde{\pi}_{i}}\leq 2\pi_{\min}^{-1}\cdot C\sqrt{\frac{\pi_{\max}\tau_{\ast}\log^{2}(n)}{n}}.

(43)

Since
\tilde{\mathbf{P}}=\tilde{\pi}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{F}}
, we have

\begin{split}&\left\|\tilde{\mathbf{P}}-\mathbf{P}\right\|=\left\|{\rm diag}(\tilde{\pi})^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{F}}-{\rm diag}(\pi)^{-1}\mathbf{F}\right\|\\
\leq&\left\|\tilde{\pi}^{-1}(\tilde{\mathbf{F}}-\mathbf{F})\right\|+\left\|\left({\rm diag}(\pi)^{-1}-{\rm diag}(\tilde{\pi})^{-1}\right)\mathbf{F}\right\|\\
\leq&\left\|\tilde{\pi}^{-1}\right\|\cdot\|\tilde{\mathbf{F}}-\mathbf{F}\|+\left\|{\mathbf{I}}-{\rm diag}(\pi/\tilde{\pi})\right\|\cdot\|{\rm diag}(\pi)^{-1}\mathbf{F}\|\\
\leq&\left(\min_{i}\tilde{\pi}_{i}\right)^{-1}\cdot\|\tilde{\mathbf{F}}-\mathbf{F}\|+\max_{i}|\pi_{i}/\tilde{\pi}_{i}-1|\cdot\|\mathbf{P}\|\\
\overset{\eqref{ineq:thm1-inter1}\eqref{ineq:thm1-inter2}}{\leq}&C\pi_{\min}^{-1}\sqrt{\frac{\pi_{\max}\tau_{\ast}\log^{2}(n)}{n}}+C\pi_{\min}^{-1}\sqrt{\frac{\pi_{\max}\tau_{\ast}\log^{2}(n)}{n}}\|\mathbf{P}\|.\\
\end{split}

Since
\|\mathbf{P}\|\geq\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}1_{p}^{\top}\mathbf{P}\|_{2}=1
, the inequality above further yields

\left\|\tilde{\mathbf{P}}-\mathbf{P}\right\|\leq C\pi_{\min}^{-1}\sqrt{\frac{\pi_{\max}\tau_{\ast}\log^{2}(n)}{n}}\|\mathbf{P}\|.

Finally, by Wedin’s perturbation bound, we have

\max\left\{\|\sin\Theta(\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{P},\mathbf{U}_{P})\|,\|\sin\Theta(\hat{\mathbf{V}}_{P},\mathbf{V}_{P})\|\right\}\leq\frac{C\|\mathbf{P}\|\cdot\sqrt{(p/n)\cdot\pi_{\max}/(p\pi_{\min}^{2})\cdot\tau_{\ast}\log^{2}(n)}}{\sigma_{r}(\mathbf{P})-\sigma_{r+1}(\mathbf{P})}

with probability at least
1-n^{-c_{0}}
. By similar argument as the one in Theorem
1
, one can finally show (
20
).
\square

C.3
Proof of Theorem
5

We focus on the proof for
\mathbf{U}_{P}
and
\mathbf{U}_{F}
and
r=2
, as the proof for
\mathbf{V}_{P}
and
\mathbf{V}_{F}
or
r\geq 3
essentially follows. Without loss of generality we also assume
p
is a multiple of 4. First, we construct a series of rank-
2
Markov chain transition matrices, which are all in
\mathcal{P}_{p,r,\delta_{P}}^{\ast}
. To be specific, let

\begin{split}&\mathbf{P}^{(k)}=\frac{1}{p}1_{p}1_{p}^{\top}\\
&+\frac{\sqrt{2}\delta_{P}}{p}\begin{bmatrix}\overbrace{~~1_{p/4}~~\cdots~~~~~1_{p/4}}^{p/2}&\overbrace{-1_{p/4}~~\cdots~~-1_{p/4}}^{p/2}&\\
-1_{p/4}~~\cdots~~~-1_{p/4}&~~1_{p/4}~~\cdots~~~~1_{p/4}&\\
~~\zeta\beta^{(k)}~~\cdots~~~~~\zeta\beta^{(k)}&-\zeta\beta^{(k)}~~\cdots~~-\zeta\beta^{(k)}&\\
-\zeta\beta^{(k)}~~\cdots~~~-\zeta\beta^{(k)}&~~\zeta\beta^{(k)}~~\cdots~~~~\zeta\beta^{(k)}&\\
\end{bmatrix}.\end{split}

(44)

Here
\{\beta^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{m}
are
m
copies of i.i.d. Rademacher
(p/4)
-dimensional random vectors,
0<\zeta\leq 1
and
m
are fixed values to be determined later.
It is not hard to check that the invariant distribution
\pi=\frac{1}{p}1_{p}
and the SVD of
\mathbf{P}^{(k)}
can be written as

\mathbf{P}^{(k)}=\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}1_{p}\right)\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}1_{p}\right)^{\top}+\sigma^{(k)}u^{(k)}(v^{(k)})^{\top},

(45)

where

\sigma^{(k)}=\frac{\sqrt{2}\zeta}{p}\sqrt{\frac{p^{2}}{2}(1+\zeta^{2})}\geq\zeta,

u^{(k)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\frac{p}{2}(1+\zeta^{2})}}\begin{bmatrix}1_{p/4}\\
-1_{p/4}\\
\zeta\beta^{(k)}\\
-\zeta\beta^{(k)}\end{bmatrix},\quad v^{(k)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}\begin{bmatrix}1_{p/2}\\
-1_{p/2}\\
\end{bmatrix}.

Thus,
\|\mathbf{P}^{(k)}\|=1
and
(\sigma_{2}(\mathbf{P}^{(k)})-\sigma_{3}(\mathbf{P}^{(k)}))/\|\mathbf{P}\|\geq\delta_{P}
.
Namely,
\mathbf{P}^{(k)}\in\mathcal{P}^{\ast}_{p,r,\delta_{P}}
,
k=1,\ldots,m
. Since
\delta_{P}\leq 1/(4\sqrt{2})
,
3/4\leq p\mathbf{P}_{ij}^{(k)}\leq 5/4
. Thus,

\forall 1\leq i\leq p,\quad\|e_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{P}^{(k)}-\pi\|_{1}\leq 1/4,

which implies
\tau_{\ast}:=\tau(1/4)=1
. In summary,
\mathbf{P}^{(k)}\in\mathcal{P}_{p,r,\delta_{P}}^{\ast}
.

Note that
(\beta^{(k)})^{\top}\beta^{(l)}
is a sum of
(p/4)
i.i.d. Rademacher random variables, by Bernstein’s inequality

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\frac{1}{p/4}\left|(\beta^{(k)})^{\top}\beta^{(l)}\right|\geq 1/2\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{p/4\cdot(1/2)^{2}}{2(1+1/3\cdot 1/2)}\right),

then

\begin{split}{\mathbb{P}}\left(\exists k\neq l,~~\text{s.t.}~~\frac{1}{p/4}\left|(\beta^{(k)})^{\top}\beta^{(l)}\right|\geq\frac{1}{2}\right)\leq 2\cdot\frac{m(m-1)}{2}\exp\left(-p/28\right)<m^{2}\exp\left(-p/28\right).\end{split}

(46)

If we set
m=\lceil\exp(-p/56)\rceil
, the probability in the right hand side of (
46
) is strictly less than 1, which means there must exists fixed
\left\{\beta^{(k)}\right\}_{k=1}^{m}
such that

|(\beta^{(k)})^{\top}\beta^{(l)}|<p/8,\quad\forall 1\leq k<l\leq m.

(47)

For the rest of the proof we assume (
47
) always hold. Now, for any
k\neq l
,

\begin{split}&\left\|\sin\Theta\left(\mathbf{U}^{(k)}_{P},\mathbf{U}^{(l)}_{P}\right)\right\|=\|\sin\Theta(u^{(k)},u^{(l)})\|=\sqrt{1-\left((u^{(k)})^{\top}v^{(l)}\right)^{2}}\\
=&\sqrt{1-\left(\frac{p/2+2\zeta^{2}(\beta^{(k)})^{\top}\beta^{(l)}}{p/2+\zeta^{2}p/2}\right)^{2}}\geq\sqrt{1-\left(\frac{p/2+\zeta^{2}p/4}{p/2+\zeta^{2}p/2}\right)^{2}}\\
=&\sqrt{1-\left(\frac{1+\zeta^{2}/2}{1+\zeta^{2}}\right)^{2}}=\sqrt{\frac{\zeta^{2}/2}{1+\zeta^{2}}\cdot\left(1+\frac{1+\zeta^{2}/2}{1+\zeta^{2}}\right)}\geq\sqrt{\frac{\zeta^{2}/2}{2}}=\frac{\zeta}{2}.\end{split}

Now for each
1\leq k\leq m
, suppose
X^{(k)}=\{x_{0}^{(k)},\ldots,x_{n}^{(k)}\}
is a Markov chain generated from transition matrix
\mathbf{P}^{(k)}
and initial distribution
x_{0}^{(k)}\sim\frac{1}{p}1_{p}
. Then based on the calculation in Theorem
2
, the KL-divergence between
X^{(k)}
and
X^{(l)}
satisfies

\begin{split}D_{KL}\left(X^{(k)}\Big|\Big|X^{(l)}\right)=&\frac{n}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}D_{KL}\left(\mathbf{P}_{[i,:]}^{(k)}\Big|\Big|\mathbf{P}_{[i,:]}^{(l)}\right)\\
\overset{\text{Lemma \ref{lm:equivalence-KL-l2}}}{\leq}&\frac{20n}{9}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\|\mathbf{P}_{[i,:]}^{(k)}-\mathbf{P}_{[i,:]}^{(l)}\|_{2}^{2}\overset{\eqref{eq:def-P^k}}{\leq}\frac{20n}{9}\cdot\frac{2\delta_{P}^{2}}{p^{2}}\cdot\left(2\zeta^{2}p^{2}\right)\leq\frac{80n\delta_{P}^{2}\zeta^{2}}{9}.\end{split}

Finally we set
\zeta=\sqrt{\frac{2\log(m)-\log 2}{80n\delta_{P}^{2}/9}}
. By generalized Fano’s lemma,

\begin{split}\inf_{\tilde{\mathbf{U}}_{P}}\sup_{\mathbf{P}\in\{\mathbf{P}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{P}^{(m)}\}}\mathbb{E}\left\|\sin\Theta(\tilde{\mathbf{U}}_{P},\mathbf{U}_{P})\right\|\geq&\frac{\zeta}{2}\left(1-\frac{80n\delta_{P}^{2}\zeta^{2}/9+\log 2}{\log m}\right)\geq\frac{\zeta}{4}\\
\geq&c\frac{\sqrt{p/n}}{\delta_{P}}\end{split}

for large
p
. We can finally finish the proof for the theorem by noting that
\{\mathbf{P}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{P}^{(m)}\}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{p,r,\delta_{P}}^{\ast}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{p,r,\delta_{P}}
. Note that the frequency matrix corresponding to
\mathbf{P}^{(k)}
is
\mathbf{F}^{(k)}={\rm diag}(\pi)\mathbf{P}^{(k)}=\mathbf{P}^{(k)}/p
for
k=1,\ldots,m
, the proof for the lower bound of
{\mathbf{U}}_{F}
exactly follows from the previous arguments.
\square

C.4
Proof of Theorem
6

Let
\mathbf{P}={\mathbf{U}}_{P}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{P}\mathbf{V}_{P}^{\top}
be the singular value decomposition of
\mathbf{P}
, where
{\mathbf{U}}_{P},\mathbf{V}_{P}\in\mathbb{O}_{p,r}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{P}
has non-negative diagonal entries in descending order. Let
\mathbf{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times r}
be the group membership indicator

\mathbf{Z}_{ij}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1,&i\in\Omega_{j};\\
0,&i\notin\Omega_{j},\end{array}\right.

By Proposition
3
, each column of
\mathbf{P}
is piece-wise constant with respect to partitions
\Omega_{1},\ldots,\Omega_{r}
and
\mathbf{P}
can be written as
\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{G}
. Since
{\mathbf{U}}_{P}
and
\mathbf{P}
share the same column space, we can write

\mathbf{U}_{P}={\mathbf{Z}}{\mathbf{X}},

where
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
satisfies
\mathbf{X}_{kj}=(\mathbf{U}_{P})_{ij}
,
\forall i\in[p],j\in[r],i\in\Omega_{k}
. Denote
n_{k}=|\Omega_{k}|,k=1,\ldots,r
. Since the columns of
\mathbf{U}_{P}
are orthonormal, we have
\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{U}_{P}^{\top}\mathbf{U}_{P}=\mathbf{I}_{r}
and

(\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{Z})_{kl}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\mathbf{Z}_{ik}\mathbf{Z}_{il}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}1_{\{i\in\Omega_{k}\text{ and }i\in\Omega_{l}\}}=|\Omega_{k}|\cdot 1_{\{k=l\}}.

Thus,
\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}={\rm diag}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{r})
and
\mathbf{X}^{\top}{\rm diag}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{r})\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{X}^{\top}{\mathbf{Z}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Z}}{\mathbf{X}}=\mathbf{I}_{r}
. This implies

Appendix D
Technical Lemmas

We collect the technical lemmas for the main results in this section. The first Lemma
1
demonstrates a sufficient and necessary condition for being transition and frequency matrices of some ergodic Markov chain.

Lemma 1

(Properties of transition and frequency matrices for ergodic Markov process)
.

{\mathbf{P}},\mathbf{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
are the transition matrix and frequency matrix of some ergodic finite-state-space Markov process if and only if

\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{p},\quad\mathcal{P}_{p}=\left\{\mathbf{P}:\begin{array}[]{l}0\leq\mathbf{P}_{ij}\leq 1;\forall 1\leq i\leq p,\sum_{j=1}^{p}\mathbf{P}_{ij}=1;\\
\forall I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,p\},\mathbf{P}_{[I,I^{c}]}\neq 0\end{array}\right\},

(54)

\text{and}\quad\mathbf{F}\in\mathcal{F}_{p},\quad\mathcal{F}_{p}=\left\{\mathbf{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}:\begin{array}[]{l}\mathbf{F}1_{p}=\mathbf{F}^{\top}1_{p},\quad 1_{p}^{\top}\mathbf{F}1_{p}=1,\\
\forall I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,p\},\mathbf{F}_{[I,I^{c}]}\neq 0\\
\end{array}\right\}.

(55)

Proof of Lemma
1
.
The proof for the transition matrix (
54
) is by definition. Then we consider the condition for
\mathbf{F}
. When
\mathbf{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
is the frequency matrix of some ergodic Markov chain, we have
\mathbf{F}={\rm diag}(\pi)\mathbf{P}
, where
\pi
and
\mathbf{P}
are the corresponding invariant distribution and stochastic matrix. Then

\mathbf{F}1_{p}={\rm diag}(\pi)\mathbf{P}1_{p}={\rm diag}(\pi)1_{p}=\pi,

\mathbf{F}^{\top}1_{p}=\mathbf{P}^{\top}{\rm diag}(\pi)1_{p}=\mathbf{P}^{\top}\pi=\pi=\mathbf{F}1_{p},

1_{p}^{\top}\mathbf{F}1_{p}=1_{p}^{\top}\pi=1.

Here we used the fact that
\pi^{\top}\mathbf{P}=\pi^{\top}
and
\mathbf{P}1_{p}=1_{p}
. Next, since the finite-state-space Markov process is ergodic,
\pi_{i}>0
for any
i
. Thus for any
I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,p\}
,
\mathbf{F}_{[I,I^{c}]}={\rm diag}(\pi_{I})\cdot\mathbf{P}_{[I,I^{c}]}\neq 0
. This implies
\mathbf{F}\in\mathcal{F}_{p}
.

On the other hand when
\mathbf{F}\in\mathcal{F}_{p}
, we define
\pi=\mathbf{F}1_{p}
,
\mathbf{P}={\rm diag}(\pi^{-1})\mathbf{F}
. Since
\mathbf{F}_{[\{i\},\{i\}^{c}]}\neq 0
, we have
\pi_{i}\neq 0
for any
1\leq i\leq p
. Then
\mathbf{P}
is well-defined. In addition,
\pi
and
\mathbf{P}
satisfies the following properties

\begin{split}&1_{p}^{\top}\pi=1_{p}^{\top}\mathbf{F}1_{p}=1,\quad\mathbf{P}_{ij}\geq 0,\quad\mathbf{P}1_{p}={\rm diag}(\pi^{-1})\mathbf{F}1_{p}={\rm diag}(\pi^{-1})\pi=1_{p},\\
&\pi^{\top}\mathbf{P}=\pi^{\top}{\rm diag}(\pi)^{-1}\mathbf{F}=1_{p}^{\top}\mathbf{F}=(\mathbf{F}^{\top}1_{p})^{\top}=(\mathbf{F}1_{p})^{\top}=\pi,\\
&\forall I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,p\},\mathbf{P}_{[I,I^{c}]}={\rm diag}(\pi_{I}^{-1})\cdot\mathbf{F}_{[I,I^{c}]}\neq 0.\end{split}

(56)

By comparing above properties with the definition of ergodic transition matrix (
54
), we can see
\mathbf{F}
is indeed a frequency matrix of some ergodic Markov process. Thus, we have finished the proof of this lemma.
\square

The next Lemma
2
characterizes the
\ell_{1}
distance between two vectors after
\ell_{1}
normalization, which will be used in the upper bound argument in the main context of the paper.

Lemma 2
.

Suppose

are two vectors of the same dimension, then

\left\|\frac{u}{\|u\|_{1}}-\frac{v}{\|v\|_{1}}\right\|_{1}\leq\frac{2\|u-v\|_{1}}{\max\{\|u\|_{1},\|v\|_{1}\}}.

(57)

Proof of Lemma
2
.

\begin{split}\left\|\frac{u}{\|u\|_{1}}-\frac{v}{\|v\|_{1}}\right\|_{1}\leq&\left\|\frac{u-v}{\|u\|_{1}}\right\|_{1}+\left\|\frac{v}{\|u\|_{1}}-\frac{v}{\|v\|_{1}}\right\|_{1}=\frac{\|u-v\|_{1}}{\|u\|_{1}}+\frac{\left|\|u\|_{1}-\|v\|_{1}\right|}{\|u\|_{1}}\\
\leq&\frac{2\|u-v\|_{1}}{\|u\|_{1}}.\end{split}

Similarly,
\left\|\frac{u}{\|u\|_{1}}-\frac{v}{\|v\|_{1}}\right\|_{1}\leq\frac{2\|u-v\|_{1}}{\|v\|_{1}}
, which implies (
57
).
\square

The following Lemma

demonstrate the error for truncated singular value decomposition.

Lemma 3
.

(58)

Particularly, if

\left\|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}_{\max(r)}-\mathbf{A}\right\|_{F}\leq 2\|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}-\mathbf{A}\|_{F}.

[

\begin{split}\|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}_{\max(r)}-\mathbf{A}\|_{F}\leq&\|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}_{\max(r)}-\mathbf{A}_{\max(r)}\|_{F}+\|\mathbf{A}_{-\max(r)}\|_{F}\leq\sqrt{2r}\|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}_{\max(r)}-\mathbf{A}_{\max(r)}\|+\|\mathbf{A}_{-\max(r)}\|_{F}\\

which yields (

\hbox{rank}(A)\leq r
, we have

\begin{split}\left\|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}_{\max(r)}-\mathbf{A}\right\|_{F}\leq&\|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}_{\max(r)}-\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\|_{F}+\|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}-\mathbf{A}\|_{F}=\min_{\hbox{rank}({\mathbf{M}})\leq r}\|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}-{\mathbf{M}}\|_{F}+\|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}-\mathbf{A}\|_{F}\\
\leq&\|\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}-{\mathbf{A}}\|_{F}+\|\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}-{\mathbf{A}}\|_{F}=2\|\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}-\mathbf{A}\|_{F}.\end{split}

which yields (
59
).
\square

Our next lemma characterizes the relation between KL divergence and
\ell_{2}
distance between two discrete distribution vectors.

Lemma 4
.

For any two distributions
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, such that
\sum_{i=1}^{p}u_{i}=1
,
\sum_{i=1}^{p}v_{i}=1
. If there exists
0<a\leq 1/p\leq b
such that
a\leq u_{i},v_{i}\leq b
for
1\leq i\leq p
, then the KL-divergence and
\ell_{2}
norm distance are equivalent, in the sense that,

\frac{a}{2b^{2}}\|u-v\|_{2}^{2}\leq D_{KL}(u||v)\leq\frac{b}{2a^{2}}\|u-v\|_{2}^{2},

(60)

Here
D_{KL}(u||v)=\sum_{i=1}^{p}u_{i}\log(u_{i}/v_{i})
is the KL-divergence between
u
and
v
.

Proof of Lemma
4
.
By Taylor’s expansion, there exists
\xi_{i}
between
u_{i}
and
v_{i}
, such that

\begin{split}\log(v_{i}/u_{i})=&\log(v_{i})-\log(u_{i})=\frac{v_{i}-u_{i}}{u_{i}}-\frac{(v_{i}-u_{i})^{2}}{2\xi_{i}^{2}},\end{split}

Thus,

\begin{split}&D_{KL}(u||v)=\sum_{i=1}^{p}-u_{i}\log(v_{i}/u_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\left\{-(v_{i}-u_{i})+\frac{u_{i}(v_{i}-u_{i})^{2}}{2\xi_{i}^{2}}\right\}\\
\leq&\sum_{i=1}^{p}\frac{b(u_{i}-v_{i})^{2}}{2a^{2}}=\frac{b}{2a^{2}}\|u-v\|_{2}^{2};\end{split}

\begin{split}&D_{KL}(u||v)=\sum_{i=1}^{p}-u_{i}\log(v_{i}/u_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\left\{-(v_{i}-u_{i})+\frac{u_{i}(v_{i}-u_{i})^{2}}{2\xi_{i}^{2}}\right\}\\
\geq&\sum_{i=1}^{p}\frac{a(u_{i}-v_{i})^{2}}{2b^{2}}=\frac{a}{2b^{2}}\|u-v\|_{2}^{2},\end{split}

which has finished the proof for this lemma.
\square

The following Lemma
5
establishes a Markov mixing time comparison inequality between
\tau(\varepsilon)
and
\tau(\delta)
for any values
\varepsilon
and
\delta
. This result is slightly more general than Theorem 4.9 in
[
52
]
.

Lemma 5

(Markov Mixing Rate)
.

Let
\tau(\varepsilon)
be the mixing time defined in (
1
) where
\varepsilon\leq\delta<1/2
, then

\tau(\varepsilon)\leq\tau(\delta)\cdot\left(\left\lceil\frac{\log(\varepsilon/\delta)}{\log(2\delta)}\right\rceil+1\right).

(61)

Proof of Lemma
5
.
We denote
\{e^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{p}
as the canonical basis for
\mathbb{R}^{p}
, namely
e^{(i)}
is equal to 1 in its
i
-th entry and equal to 0 elsewhere. For any vector
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, we also use
\theta_{+},\theta_{-}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
to denote the positive and negative parts of
\theta
, respectively, i.e.

\left(\theta_{+}\right)_{j}=\min\{\theta_{j},0\},\quad\left(\theta_{-}\right)_{j}=-\max\{\theta_{j},0\},\quad 1\leq j\leq p.

(62)

Clearly
\theta_{+}\geq 0,\theta_{-}\geq 0
, and
\theta=\theta_{+}-\theta_{-}
. Suppose
k=\tau(\delta)
, then for any distribution
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
with
\sum_{i}\theta_{i}=1,\theta_{i}\geq 0
, and any integer
k^{\prime}\geq k
, we must have

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{2}\left\|(\mathbf{P}^{\top})^{k^{\prime}}\theta-\pi\right\|_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{p}(\mathbf{P}^{\top})^{k^{\prime}}\theta_{i}e^{(i)}-\pi\right\|_{1}\\
\leq&\sum_{i=1}^{p}|\theta_{i}|\cdot\frac{1}{2}\left\|(\mathbf{P}^{\top})^{k^{\prime}}e^{(i)}-\pi\right\|_{1}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{p}|\theta_{i}|\cdot\delta=\delta.\end{split}

(63)

When
\theta
and
\pi
are both distributions,
\sum_{j=1}^{p}(\mathbf{P}^{\top})^{k}\theta_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\pi_{j}=1
, then
\sum_{j=1}^{p}((\mathbf{P}^{\top})^{k}\theta-\pi)_{j}=0
, and

\left\|\left((\mathbf{P}^{\top})^{k}\theta-\pi\right)_{+}\right\|_{1}=\left\|\left((\mathbf{P}^{\top})^{k^{\prime}}\theta-\pi\right)_{-}\right\|_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\left\|(\mathbf{P}^{\top})^{k}\theta-\pi\right\|_{1}.

(64)

Next, we consider any integer
k^{\prime}\geq 2k
, then
k^{\prime}-k
. One can calculate that

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{2}\left\|(\mathbf{P}^{\top})^{k^{\prime}}\theta-\pi\right\|_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\left\|(\mathbf{P}^{\top})^{k^{\prime}-k}((\mathbf{P}^{\top})^{k}\theta-\pi)\right\|_{1}\\
=&\frac{1}{2}\left\|(\mathbf{P}^{\top})^{k^{\prime}-k}\left[((\mathbf{P}^{\top})^{k}\theta-\pi)_{+}-((\mathbf{P}^{\top})^{k}e_{i}-\pi)_{-}\right]\right\|_{1}\\
\leq&\frac{1}{2}\left\|(\mathbf{P}^{\top})^{k^{\prime}-k}\frac{((\mathbf{P}^{\top})^{k}\theta-\pi)_{+}}{\|((\mathbf{P}^{\top})^{k}\theta-\pi)_{+}\|_{1}}-\pi\right\|_{1}\cdot\|((\mathbf{P}^{\top})^{k}\theta-\pi)_{+}\|_{1}\\
&+\frac{1}{2}\left\|(\mathbf{P}^{\top})^{k^{\prime}-k}\frac{((\mathbf{P}^{\top})^{k}\theta-\pi)_{-}}{\|((\mathbf{P}^{\top})^{k}\theta-\pi)_{-}\|_{1}}-\pi\right\|_{1}\cdot\|((\mathbf{P}^{\top})^{k}\theta-\pi)_{-}\|_{1}\\
\overset{\eqref{ineq:mixing-time-convergence-1}\eqref{ineq:mixing-time-convergence-2}}{\leq}&\delta\left(\left\|((\mathbf{P}^{\top})^{k}\theta-\pi)_{+}\right\|_{1}+\left\|((\mathbf{P}^{\top})^{k}\theta-\pi)_{-}\right\|_{1}\right)\leq\delta\|(\mathbf{P}^{\top})^{k}\theta-\pi\|\leq\frac{1}{2}(2\delta)^{2}.\end{split}

By induction, one can show for any integers
l
, we must have

\forall k^{\prime}\geq lk,\quad\frac{1}{2}\|(\mathbf{P}^{\top})^{k^{\prime}}\theta-\pi\|_{1}\leq\frac{1}{2}(2\delta)^{l}.

Note that
\delta<1/2
,
\varepsilon\leq\delta
, we set
l=\lceil\frac{\log(\varepsilon/\delta)}{\log(2\delta)}\rceil+1
. Then for any
k^{\prime}\geq kl
,

\frac{1}{2}\left\|(\mathbf{P}^{\top})^{k^{\prime}}\theta-\pi\right\|_{1}\leq\frac{1}{2}(2\delta)^{l}\leq\frac{1}{2}(2\delta)^{\frac{\log(\varepsilon/\delta)}{\log(2\delta)}+1}=\frac{1}{2}2\delta\cdot(\varepsilon/\delta)=\varepsilon,

(65)

which implies
\tau(\varepsilon)\leq kl=\tau(\delta)\cdot\left(\lceil\log(\varepsilon/\delta)/\log(2\delta)\rceil+1\right)
, and complete the proof for (
61
). Thus we have finished the proof for Lemma
5
.
\square

The next Lemma
6
relates the Markov mixing time to the eigengap condition.

Lemma 6

(Markov Mixing Time and Eigengap Condition (
[
52
]
, Theorem 12.3))
.

Suppose
\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
is the transition matrix of an ergodic and reversible Markov chain with invariant distribution
\pi
. Suppose
\lambda_{2}
is its second largest eigenvalue, then
\lambda_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
,
|\lambda_{2}|\leq 1
, and

\tau(\varepsilon)\leq\frac{1}{1-\lambda_{2}}\log\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon\pi_{\min}}\right).

(66)

Lemma 7

(Markov Chain Concentration Inequality)
.

Suppose
\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
is an ergodic Markov chain transition matrix on
p
states
\{1,\ldots,p\}
.
\mathbf{P}
is with invariant distribution
\pi
and the Markov mixing time
\tau(\varepsilon)
defined as (
1
). Recall the frequency matrix is
\mathbf{F}={\rm diag}(\pi)\mathbf{P}
. Given a Markov trajectory with
(n+1)
observable states
X=\{x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
from any initial state, we denote the empirical invariant distribution
\tilde{\pi}
and empirical frequency matrix as

\tilde{\pi}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}e_{x_{k}},\quad\text{where }e_{x_{k}}\text{is the indicator such that},\quad(e_{x_{k}})_{i}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1,&x_{k}=i;\\
0,&x_{k}\neq i;\end{array}\right.

(67)

\tilde{\mathbf{F}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbf{E}_{k},\quad\text{where }\mathbf{E}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p},\quad(\mathbf{E}_{k})_{ij}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1,&(x_{k-1},x_{k})=(i,j);\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\\
\end{array}\right.

(68)

Let
t>0,\alpha=\tau\left((t/2)\wedge\pi_{\max}\right)+1
. Recall
\|\cdot\|
is defined as the matrix 2-norm,
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
is defined as the vector
\ell_{\infty}
norm. Then

\forall t>0,\quad{\mathbb{P}}\left(\left\|\tilde{\mathbf{F}}-\mathbf{F}\right\|\geq t\right)\leq 2\alpha p\exp\left(-\frac{nt^{2}/8}{2\pi_{\max}\alpha+t\alpha/6}\right),

(69)

\forall t>0,\quad{\mathbb{P}}\left(\|\tilde{\pi}-\pi\|_{\infty}\geq t\right)\leq 2\alpha p\exp\left(-\frac{nt^{2}/8}{2\pi_{\max}\alpha+t\alpha/6}\right).

(70)

For any constant
c_{0}>0
, there exists constant
C>0
such that if
n\geq C\left(\tau(\sqrt{\pi_{\max}/n})\log(n)/\pi_{\max}\vee p\right)
, we have

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\left\|\tilde{\mathbf{F}}-\mathbf{F}\right\|\geq C\sqrt{\frac{\pi_{\max}\tau(\sqrt{\pi_{\max}/n})\log(n)}{n}}\right)\leq n^{-c_{0}},

(71)

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\left\|\tilde{\pi}-\pi\right\|_{\infty}\geq C\sqrt{\frac{\pi_{\max}\tau(\sqrt{\pi_{\max}/n})\log(n)}{n}}\right)\leq n^{-c_{0}},

(72)

Additionally, let
\tau_{\ast}=\tau(1/4)
. For any constant
c_{0}>0
, there exists constant
C>0
such that if
n\geq C\tau_{\ast}p\log^{2}(n)
, then

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\left\|\tilde{\mathbf{F}}-\mathbf{F}\right\|\geq C\sqrt{\frac{\pi_{\max}\tau_{\ast}\log^{2}(n)}{n}}\right)\leq n^{-c_{0}},

(73)

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\left\|\tilde{\pi}-\pi\right\|_{\infty}\geq C\sqrt{\frac{\pi_{\max}\tau_{\ast}\log^{2}(n)}{n}}\right)\leq n^{-c_{0}}.

(74)

When
\mathbf{P}
is reversible with second largest eigenvalue
\lambda_{2}<1
and
c_{0}>0
is any constant, there exists constant
C>0
such that if
n\geq Cp\log(n)\log(n/\pi_{\min})
, then

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\left\|\tilde{\mathbf{F}}-\mathbf{F}\right\|\geq C\sqrt{\frac{\pi_{\max}\log(n/\pi_{\min})\log(n)}{n(1-\lambda_{2})}}\right)\leq n^{-c_{0}},

(75)

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\left\|\tilde{\pi}-\pi\right\|_{\infty}\geq C\sqrt{\frac{\pi_{\max}\log(n/\pi_{\min})\log(n)}{n(1-\lambda_{2})}}\right)\leq n^{-c_{0}}.

(76)

Proof of Lemma
7
.
Let
n_{0}=\lfloor n/\alpha\rfloor
. Without loss of generality, assume
n
is a multiple of
\alpha
. We introduce the “thin” sequences as

\tilde{e}_{k}^{(l)}=e_{x_{k\alpha+l}}-\mathbb{E}\left(e_{x_{k\alpha+l}}\big|e_{x_{(k-1)\alpha+l}}\right),\quad l=1,\ldots,\alpha;k=1,\ldots,n_{0};

(77)

\begin{split}\tilde{\mathbf{E}}_{k}^{(l)}=\mathbf{E}_{k\alpha+l}-\mathbb{E}\left(\mathbf{E}_{k\alpha+l}|\mathbf{E}_{(k-1)\alpha+l}\right),\quad l=1,\ldots,\alpha;k=1,\dots,n_{0}.\end{split}

(78)

By Jensen’s inequality, for any
l=1,\ldots,\alpha,k=1,\ldots,n_{0}
,

\left\|\mathbb{E}\left(e_{x_{k\alpha+l}}\big|e_{x_{(k-1)\alpha+l}}\right)\right\|_{2}\leq\mathbb{E}\|e_{x_{k\alpha+l}}\|_{2}\leq 1,\quad\left\|\mathbb{E}\left(\mathbf{E}_{k\alpha+l}|\mathbf{E}_{(k-1)\alpha+l}\right)\right\|\leq\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{E}_{k\alpha+l}\|\leq 1,

(79)

which implies

\left\|\tilde{e}_{k}^{(l)}\right\|_{2}\leq 2,\quad\left\|\tilde{\mathbf{E}}_{k}^{(l)}\right\|\leq 2.

(80)

Now we develop the concentration inequalities of the partial sum sequences
\sum_{k=1}^{n_{0}}\tilde{\mathbf{E}}_{k}^{(l)}
for any fixed
l
. Note that for any given
\tilde{\mathbf{E}}_{k-1}^{(l)}
and
e_{\tilde{x}_{k-1}^{(l)}}
, i.e. given the values of
(x_{k\alpha+l-1},x_{k\alpha+l})
pair, the conditional distribution of
e_{x_{k\alpha+l-1}}
satisfies

x_{k\alpha+l-1}|x_{(k-1)\alpha+l}\sim e_{x_{(k-1)\alpha+l}}^{\top}\mathbf{P}^{\alpha-1},\quad k=1,\ldots,n_{0}.

For convenience, we denote
\tilde{\pi}_{(k,l)}=\left(e_{x_{(k-1)\alpha+l}}^{\top}\mathbf{P}^{\alpha-1}\right)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
. By the choice of
\alpha
and the mixing time property,

\|\tilde{\pi}_{(k,l)}-\pi\|_{1}=\left\|e_{x_{(k-1)\alpha+l}}^{\top}\mathbf{P}^{\alpha-1}-\pi\right\|_{1}\leq\min\{t/2,\pi_{\max}\}.

(81)

(
81
) will be crucial to our later analysis. Note that

\tilde{\mathbf{E}}_{k}^{(l)}=\mathbf{E}_{k\alpha+l}-\mathbb{E}\left(\mathbf{E}_{k\alpha+l}\Big|x_{(k-1)\alpha+l}\right),\quad\text{where}\quad\mathbf{E}_{k\alpha+l}=e_{x_{k\alpha+l-1}}\cdot e_{x_{k\alpha+l}}^{\top},

(82)

\begin{split}{\mathbb{P}}\left(\mathbf{E}_{k\alpha+l}=e_{i}e_{j}^{\top}\Big|x_{(k-1)\alpha+l}\right)=&{\mathbb{P}}\left((x_{k\alpha+l-1},x_{k\alpha+l})=(i,j)\Big|x_{(k-1)\alpha+l}\right)\\
=&\left(e^{\top}_{x_{(k-1)\alpha+l}}\mathbf{P}^{\alpha-1}\right)_{i}\cdot\mathbf{P}_{ij}=(\tilde{\pi}_{(k,l)})_{i}\mathbf{P}_{ij},\end{split}

(83)

we can further calculate that

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\left(\mathbf{E}_{k\alpha+l}\mathbf{E}_{k\alpha+l}^{\top}\Big|x_{(k-1)\alpha+l}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\sum_{j=1}^{p}e_{i}e_{i}^{\top}(\tilde{\pi}_{(k,l)})_{i}\mathbf{P}_{ij}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}e_{i}e_{i}^{\top}(\tilde{\pi}_{(k,l)})_{i}\\
=&{\rm diag}\left(\tilde{\pi}_{(k,l)}\right)={\rm diag}(\pi)+{\rm diag}\left(\tilde{\pi}_{(k,l)}-\pi\right)\\
\preceq&\pi_{\max}{\mathbf{I}}_{p}+\left\|\tilde{\pi}_{(k,l)}-\pi\right\|_{1}\cdot{\mathbf{I}}_{p}\preceq 2\pi_{\max}{\mathbf{I}}_{p};\end{split}

(84)

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\left(\mathbf{E}_{k\alpha+l}^{\top}\mathbf{E}_{k\alpha+l}\Big|x_{(k-1)\alpha+l}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\sum_{j=1}^{p}e_{j}e_{j}^{\top}\left\{(\tilde{\pi}_{(k,l)})_{i}\mathbf{P}_{ij}\right\}\\
=&\sum_{i=1}^{p}\sum_{j=1}^{p}e_{j}e_{j}^{\top}\left\{\pi_{i}\mathbf{P}_{ij}\right\}+\sum_{i=1}^{p}\sum_{j=1}^{p}e_{j}e_{j}^{\top}\left\{((\tilde{\pi}_{(k,l)})_{i}-\pi)_{i}\mathbf{P}_{ij}\right\}\\
\preceq&\sum_{j=1}^{p}e_{j}e_{j}^{\top}\pi_{j}+\sum_{j=1}^{p}e_{j}e_{j}^{\top}\left\|\tilde{\pi}_{(k,l)}-\pi\right\|_{1}\cdot\max_{ij}\mathbf{P}_{ij}\quad\text{(since $\pi^{\top}\mathbf{P}=\pi$)}\\
\preceq&\pi_{\max}{\mathbf{I}}_{p}+\left\|\tilde{\pi}_{(k,l)}-\pi\right\|_{1}\cdot{\mathbf{I}}_{p}\preceq 2\pi_{\max}{\mathbf{I}}_{p}.\end{split}

(85)

Therefore,

\begin{split}0\preceq&\mathbb{E}\left(\tilde{\mathbf{E}}_{k}^{(l)}(\tilde{\mathbf{E}}_{k}^{(l)})^{\top}\Big|\tilde{\mathbf{E}}_{k-1}^{(l)}\right)\\
=&\mathbb{E}\left\{\left(\mathbf{E}_{k\alpha+l}-\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{E}_{k\alpha+l}|x_{(k-1)\alpha+l})\right)\left(\mathbf{E}_{k\alpha+l}-\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{E}_{k\alpha+l}|x_{(k-1)\alpha+l})\right)^{\top}\Big|x_{(k-1)\alpha+l}\right\}\\
=&\mathbb{E}\left\{\mathbf{E}_{k\alpha+1}\mathbf{E}_{k\alpha+1}^{\top}\Big|x_{(k-1)\alpha+l}\right\}-\mathbb{E}\left\{\mathbf{E}_{k\alpha+l}\Big|x_{(k-1)\alpha+l}\right\}\mathbb{E}\left\{\mathbf{E}_{k\alpha+l}^{\top}\Big|x_{(k-1)\alpha+l}\right\}\\
\preceq&\mathbb{E}\left\{\mathbf{E}_{k\alpha+1}\mathbf{E}_{k\alpha+1}^{\top}\Big|x_{(k-1)\alpha+l}\right\}\preceq 2\pi_{\max}{\mathbf{I}}_{p}.\end{split}

(86)

Similarly,

\begin{split}0\preceq&\mathbb{E}\left((\tilde{\mathbf{E}}_{k}^{(l)})^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{E}}_{k}^{(l)}\Big|\tilde{\mathbf{E}}_{k-1}^{(l)}\right)\\
=&\mathbb{E}\left\{\left(\mathbf{E}_{k\alpha+l}-\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{E}_{k\alpha+l}|x_{(k-1)\alpha+l})\right)^{\top}\left(\mathbf{E}_{k\alpha+l}-\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{E}_{k\alpha+l}|x_{(k-1)\alpha+l})\right)\Big|x_{(k-1)\alpha+l}\right\}\\
=&\mathbb{E}\left\{\mathbf{E}_{k\alpha+1}^{\top}\mathbf{E}_{k\alpha+1}\Big|x_{(k-1)\alpha+l}\right\}-\mathbb{E}\left\{\mathbf{E}_{k\alpha+l}^{\top}\Big|x_{(k-1)\alpha+l}\right\}\mathbb{E}\left\{\mathbf{E}_{k\alpha+l}\Big|x_{(k-1)\alpha+l}\right\}\\
\preceq&\mathbb{E}\left\{\mathbf{E}_{k\alpha+1}^{\top}\mathbf{E}_{k\alpha+1}\Big|x_{(k-1)\alpha+l}\right\}\preceq 2\pi_{\max}{\mathbf{I}}_{p},\end{split}

(87)

which means for
1\leq k\leq n_{0},1\leq l\leq\alpha
,

\max\left\{\left\|\mathbb{E}\left((\tilde{\mathbf{E}}_{k}^{(l)})^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{E}}_{k}^{(l)}\Big|\tilde{\mathbf{E}}_{k-1}^{(l)}\right)\right\|,~\left\|\mathbb{E}\left(\tilde{\mathbf{E}}_{k}^{(l)}(\tilde{\mathbf{E}}_{k}^{(l)})^{\top}\Big|\tilde{\mathbf{E}}_{k-1}^{(l)}\right)\right\|\right\}\leq 2\pi_{\max}{\mathbf{I}}_{p}.

(88)

Next, the predictable quadratic variation process of the martingale
\left\{\tilde{\mathbf{E}}_{k}^{(l)}\right\}_{k=1}^{n_{0}}
satisfies

\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{n_{0}}\mathbb{E}\left(\tilde{\mathbf{E}}_{k}^{(l)}(\tilde{\mathbf{E}}_{k}^{(l)})^{\top}\Big|\tilde{\mathbf{E}}_{k-1}^{(l)}\right)\right\|\leq\sum_{k=1}^{n_{0}}\left\|\mathbb{E}\left(\tilde{\mathbf{E}}_{k}^{(l)}(\tilde{\mathbf{E}}_{k}^{(l)})^{\top}\Big|\tilde{\mathbf{E}}_{k-1}^{(l)}\right)\right\|\leq 2n_{0}\pi_{\max},

\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{n_{0}}\mathbb{E}\left((\tilde{\mathbf{E}}_{k}^{(l)})^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{E}}_{k}^{(l)}\Big|\tilde{\mathbf{E}}_{k-1}^{(l)}\right)\right\|\leq\sum_{k=1}^{n_{0}}\left\|\mathbb{E}\left((\tilde{\mathbf{E}}_{k}^{(l)})^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{E}}_{k}^{(l)}\Big|\tilde{\mathbf{E}}_{k-1}^{(l)}\right)\right\|\leq 2n_{0}\pi_{\max}.

Now by matrix Freedman’s inequality (Corollary 1.3 in
[
73
]
), we know

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\left\|\frac{1}{n_{0}}\sum_{k=1}^{n_{0}}\tilde{\mathbf{E}}_{k}^{(l)}\right\|\geq t/2\right)\leq 2p\exp\left(-\frac{(tn_{0})^{2}/8}{2n_{0}\pi_{\max}+tn_{0}/6}\right).

(89)

Here,
\|\cdot\|
represents the matrix 2-norm. Next, we shall note that

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\left(\mathbf{E}_{k\alpha+l}\Big|x_{(k-1)\alpha+l}\right)-{\rm diag}(\pi)\mathbf{P}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\sum_{j=1}^{p}e_{i}\left(e_{x_{(k-1)\alpha+l}}^{\top}\mathbf{P}^{\alpha-1}\right)_{i}\mathbf{P}_{ij}e_{j}^{\top}-{\rm diag}(\pi)\mathbf{P}\\
=&{\rm diag}\left(e_{x_{(k-1)\alpha+l}}^{\top}\mathbf{P}^{\alpha-1}\right)\mathbf{P}-{\rm diag}(\pi)\mathbf{P},\end{split}

thus

\begin{split}&\left\|\mathbb{E}\left(\mathbf{E}_{k\alpha+l}\Big|x_{(k-1)\alpha+l}\right)-{\rm diag}(\pi)\mathbf{P}\right\|\leq\left\|(\tilde{\pi}_{(k,l)}-\pi)\mathbf{P}\right\|\\
=&\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\\
\|u\|_{2}=\|v\|_{2}=1\end{subarray}}u^{\top}{\rm diag}(\tilde{\pi}_{(k,l)}-\pi)\mathbf{P}v\\
\leq&\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\\
\|u\|_{2}=\|v\|_{2}=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\left|u_{i}((\tilde{\pi}_{(k,l)})_{i}-\pi_{i})\mathbf{P}_{ij}v_{j}\right|\\
\leq&\sum_{i=1}^{p}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\left|((\tilde{\pi}_{(k,l)})_{i}-\pi_{i})\mathbf{P}_{ij}\right|\leq\|\tilde{\pi}_{(k,l)}-\pi\|_{1}\overset{\eqref{ineq:tilde_mu-mu}}{\leq}t/2.\end{split}

(90)

The last but one equality is due to
\sum_{j=1}^{p}|\mathbf{P}_{ij}|=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\mathbf{P}_{ij}=1
for all
i
. Combining (
78
), (
89
), and (
90
), we have for any
l=1,\ldots,\alpha
,

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\left\|\frac{1}{n_{0}}\sum_{k=1}^{n_{0}}\mathbf{E}_{k\alpha+l}-\mathbf{F}\right\|\geq t\right)\leq 2p\exp\left(-\frac{(tn_{0})^{2}/8}{2n_{0}\pi_{\max}+tn_{0}/6}\right).

(91)

Finally, we only need to combine these “thin” summation sequences by using a union bound,

\begin{split}&{\mathbb{P}}\left(\|\tilde{\mathbf{F}}-\mathbf{F}\|\geq t\right)={\mathbb{P}}\left(\left\|\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{l=1}^{\alpha}\frac{1}{n_{0}}\sum_{k=1}^{n_{0}}\mathbf{E}_{k\alpha+l}-\mathbf{F}\right\|\geq t\right)\\
\leq&{\mathbb{P}}\left(\max_{1\leq l\leq\alpha}\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{n_{0}}\frac{1}{n_{0}}\mathbf{E}_{k\alpha+l}-\mathbf{F}\right\|\geq t\right)\leq\alpha\max_{1\leq l\leq p}{\mathbb{P}}\left(\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{n_{0}}\frac{1}{n_{0}}\mathbf{E}_{k\alpha+l}-\mathbf{F}\right\|\geq t\right)\\
\leq&2\alpha p\exp\left(-\frac{(tn_{0})^{2}/8}{2n_{0}\pi_{\max}+tn_{0}/6}\right),\end{split}

(92)

which proves (
69
). Particularly by setting
t=C\sqrt{\frac{\pi_{\max}\tau(\sqrt{\pi_{\max}/n})\log(n)}{n}}
for large constant
C
, one further obtains (
71
). When
\tau_{\ast}=\tau(1/4)
, Lemma
5
implies

\tau(\sqrt{\pi_{\max}/n})\leq C\tau_{\ast}\log(\sqrt{n/\pi_{\max}})\leq C\tau_{\ast}\log(\sqrt{np})\leq C\tau_{\ast}\log(n),

thus (
73
) immediately follows from (
71
).

When
\mathbf{P}
is reversible and with second largest eigenvalue
\lambda_{2}<1
, Lemma
6
implies

\tau\left(\sqrt{\pi_{\max}/n}\right)\leq\frac{1}{1-\lambda_{2}}\log\left(\frac{\sqrt{n/\pi_{\max}}}{2\pi_{\min}}\right)\leq\frac{C}{1-\lambda_{2}}\left(\log(n)+\log(1/\pi_{\min})\right)=\frac{C}{1-\lambda_{2}}\log(n/\pi_{\min}).

Then (
75
) follows from (
71
).

The proof for the upper bounds
\|\tilde{\pi}-\pi\|_{\infty}
is similar. Recall the definition of
\tilde{e}_{k}^{(l)}
in (
77
). Note that for any index
j\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
,

\begin{split}\left(\tilde{e}_{k}^{(l)}\right)_{j}=&\left(e_{x_{k\alpha+l}}\right)_{j}-\mathbb{E}\left(\left(e_{x_{k\alpha+l}}\right)_{j}\Big|e_{x_{(k-1)\alpha+l}}\right)\\
=&1_{\{x_{k\alpha+l}=j\}}-\mathbb{E}\left(1_{\{x_{k\alpha+l}=j\}}\Big|x_{(k-1)\alpha+l}\right).\end{split}

Clearly
0\leq\mathbb{E}\left(1_{\{x_{k\alpha+l}=j\}}\Big|x_{(k-1)\alpha+l}\right)\leq 1
, which implies
\left|(\tilde{e}_{k}^{(l)})_{j}\right|\leq 1
. Additionally,

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\left(\tilde{e}_{k}^{(l)}\right)_{j}^{2}={\rm Var}\left(1_{\{x_{k\alpha+l=j}\}}\big|x_{(k-1)\alpha+l}\right)\leq\mathbb{E}\left(1^{2}_{\{x_{k\alpha+l=j}\}}\right)=\left(e^{\top}_{x_{(k-1)\alpha+l}}\mathbf{P}^{\alpha}\right)_{j}\\
\leq&\pi_{j}+\left(e^{\top}_{x(k-1)\alpha+l}\mathbf{P}^{\alpha}-\pi^{\top}\right)_{j}\leq 2\pi_{\max}.\end{split}

By Freedman’s inequality (e.g. Theorem 1.6 in
[
74
]
and Theorem 1.1 in
[
73
]
), for any
1\leq j\leq p
,

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\left|\sum_{k=1}^{n_{0}}(\tilde{e}_{k}^{(l)})_{j}\right|\geq t/2\right)\leq 2\exp\left(\frac{-t^{2}/8}{2n_{0}\pi_{\max}+t/6}\right)

On the other hand,

\left\|\mathbb{E}\left(e_{x_{k\alpha+l}}\Big|e_{x_{(k-1)\alpha+l}}\right)-\pi\right\|_{\infty}=\left\|e^{\top}_{x_{(k-1)\alpha+l}}\mathbf{P}^{\alpha}-\pi^{\top}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\left\|e^{\top}_{x_{(k-1)\alpha+l}}\mathbf{P}^{\alpha}-\pi^{\top}\right\|_{1}\leq\frac{t}{2}\wedge\pi_{\max}.

Combining the two inequality above and the definition (
77
), we have for any
1\leq j\leq p,1\leq l\leq\alpha
,

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\left|\sum_{k=1}^{n_{0}}(e_{k\alpha+l})_{j}-n_{0}\pi_{j}\right|\geq t\right)\leq 2\exp\left(\frac{-t^{2}/8}{2n_{0}\pi_{\max}+t/6}\right).

(93)

By a union bound, one can show

\begin{split}&{\mathbb{P}}\left(\|\tilde{\pi}-\pi\|_{\infty}\geq t\right)={\mathbb{P}}\left(\left\|\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{l=1}^{\alpha}\frac{1}{n_{0}}\sum_{k=1}^{n_{0}}e_{x_{k\alpha+l}}-\pi\right\|_{\infty}\geq t\right)\\
\leq&{\mathbb{P}}\left(\max_{1\leq l\leq\alpha}\max_{1\leq j\leq p}\left|\sum_{k=1}^{n_{0}}\frac{1}{n_{0}}(e_{x_{k\alpha+l}})_{j}-\pi_{j}\right|\geq t\right)\leq 2\alpha p\exp\left(\frac{-(tn_{0})^{2}/8}{2n_{0}\pi_{\max}+tn_{0}/6}\right),\end{split}

(94)

which has developed the upper bound for
\|\tilde{\pi}-\pi\|_{\infty}
(
70
). Finally, the proofs of (
72
), (
74
), and (
76
) are essentially follows from the previous argument for
\|\tilde{\mathbf{F}}-\mathbf{F}\|
.
\square

Lemma 8

(Rowwise Markov Concentration Inequality)
.

Suppose
\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{O}_{p,r}
is a fixed orthogonal matrices satisfying
\max_{i}\|\mathbf{V}^{\top}e_{i}\|_{2}\leq\delta\sqrt{r/p}
. Assume
n\geq Cp\tau_{\ast}\log^{2}(n)
and
\tau_{\ast}:=\tau(1/4)
. Under the same setting as Lemma
7
, for any
c_{0}>0
there exists
C>0
,

\max_{1\leq i\leq p}\|(\tilde{\mathbf{F}}\mathbf{V})_{i\cdot}-(\mathbf{F}\mathbf{V})_{i\cdot}\|_{2}\leq C\sqrt{\frac{\pi_{\max}\delta^{2}r\tau_{\ast}\log^{2}(n)}{np}}

with probability at least
1-Cn^{-c_{0}}
.

Proof of Lemma
8
.
We first focus on the
s
-th row of
\|\tilde{\mathbf{F}}\mathbf{V}_{i\cdot}-\mathbf{F}\mathbf{V}_{i\cdot}\|_{2}
. Similarly as the proof of Lemma
7
, let
\alpha=\tau\left(\min\{t/2,\pi_{\max}\}\right)+1
,
\mathbf{E}_{k}=e_{x_{k}}e_{x_{k+1}}^{\top},k=1,\ldots,n
.
t
is to be determined later. We similarly assume
n
is a multiple of
\alpha
and define
n_{0}=n/\alpha
. We further define

\mathbf{T}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times r},\quad\mathbf{T}_{k}=e_{s}^{\top}\mathbf{E}_{k}\mathbf{V},\quad k=1,\ldots,n;

and the “thin” matrix sequences for
l=1,\ldots,\alpha
,
k=1,\ldots,n_{0}
,

\begin{split}\tilde{\mathbf{T}}_{k}^{(l)}=\mathbf{T}_{k\alpha+l}-\mathbb{E}\left(\mathbf{T}_{k\alpha+l}|\mathbf{T}_{(k-1)\alpha+l}\right)=e_{s}^{\top}\mathbf{E}_{k\alpha+l}\mathbf{V}-\mathbb{E}\left(e_{s}^{\top}\mathbf{E}_{k\alpha+l}\mathbf{V}\Big|x_{(k-1)\alpha+l}\right).\end{split}

Then
\mathbf{T}_{k\alpha+l}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{T}}_{k}^{(l)}
satisfy the following 2-norm upper bound

\|\mathbf{T}_{k\alpha+l}\|=\max_{1\leq i,j\leq p}\|e_{s}^{\top}e_{i}e_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{V}\|=\max_{j}\|e_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{V}\|_{2}\leq\delta\sqrt{r/p}.

By Jensen’s inequality,
\|\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{T}_{k\alpha+l}|\mathbf{T}_{(k-1)\alpha+l})\|\leq\delta\sqrt{r/p}
, thus

\|\tilde{\mathbf{T}}_{k}^{(l)}\|\leq 2\delta\sqrt{r/p}\quad\text{almost surely}.

Next, we define
\tilde{\pi}_{(k,l)}=\left(e^{\top}_{x_{(k-1)\alpha+l}}\mathbf{P}^{\alpha-1}\right)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
. By the choice of
\alpha
and the mixing time property,

\|\tilde{\pi}_{(k,l)}-\pi\|_{1}=\left\|e_{x_{(k-1)\alpha+l}}^{\top}\mathbf{P}^{\alpha-1}-\pi\right\|_{1}\leq\min\{t/2,\pi_{\max}\}.

Then,

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\left(\mathbf{T}_{k\alpha+l}\mathbf{T}_{k\alpha+l}^{\top}\Big|x_{(k-1)\alpha+l}\right)=\sum_{i,j=1}^{p}e_{s}^{\top}e_{i}e_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{V}\mathbf{V}^{\top}e_{j}e_{i}^{\top}e_{s}(\tilde{\pi}_{(k,l)})_{i}\mathbf{P}_{ij}\\
=&\sum_{i,j=1}^{p}e_{s}^{\top}e_{i}e_{i}^{\top}e_{s}(\tilde{\pi}_{(k,l)})_{i}\mathbf{P}_{ij}\|\mathbf{V}^{\top}e_{j}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\sum_{i,j=1}^{p}e_{s}^{\top}e_{i}e_{i}^{\top}e_{s}(\tilde{\pi}_{(k,l)})_{i}\mathbf{P}_{ij}\cdot\delta r/p\\
=&\sum_{i=1}^{p}e_{s}^{\top}e_{i}e_{i}^{\top}e_{s}(\tilde{\pi}_{(k,l)})_{i}\delta^{2}r/p\leq\delta^{2}(r/p)\max_{i}(\tilde{\pi}_{(k,l)})_{i}\cdot e_{s}^{\top}\sum_{i=1}^{p}e_{i}e_{i}^{\top}e_{s}\\
\leq&\delta^{2}(r/p)\max_{i}(\tilde{\pi}_{(k,l)})_{i}\leq\delta^{2}(r/p)\left(\pi_{\max}+\|\pi-\tilde{\pi}_{(k,l)}\|_{1}\right)\leq 2\pi_{\max}\delta^{2}(r/p),\end{split}

By Jensen’s inequality,

\begin{split}&\left\|\mathbb{E}\left(\mathbf{T}_{k\alpha+l}^{\top}\mathbf{T}_{k\alpha+l}\Big|x_{(k-1)\alpha+l}\right)\right\|\leq\mathbb{E}\left(\left\|\mathbf{T}_{k\alpha+l}^{\top}\mathbf{T}_{k\alpha+l}\right\|\Big|x_{(k-1)\alpha+l}\right)\\
\leq&\mathbb{E}\left(\mathbf{T}_{k\alpha+l}\mathbf{T}_{k\alpha+l}^{\top}\Big|x_{(k-1)\alpha+l}\right)\leq 2\pi_{\max}\delta^{2}(r/p).\end{split}

Similarly as (
86
) and (
87
) in the proof of Lemma
7
, we can show

0\leq\mathbb{E}\left(\tilde{\mathbf{T}}_{k}^{(l)}(\tilde{\mathbf{T}}_{k}^{(l)})^{\top}\Big|\tilde{\mathbf{T}}_{k}^{(l)}\right)\leq 2\pi_{\max}\delta^{2}r/p,

0\preceq\mathbb{E}\left((\tilde{\mathbf{T}}_{k}^{(l)})^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{T}}_{k}^{(l)}\Big|\tilde{\mathbf{T}}_{k}^{(l)}\right)\preceq 2\pi_{\max}\delta^{2}(r/p){\mathbf{I}}_{r}.

Then the predictable quadratic variation process satisfies

\begin{split}\max\left\{\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{n_{0}}\mathbb{E}\left(\tilde{\mathbf{T}}_{k}^{(l)}(\tilde{\mathbf{T}}_{k}^{(l)})^{\top}\Big|\tilde{\mathbf{T}}_{k}^{(l)}\right)\right\|,\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{n_{0}}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{\mathbf{T}}_{k}^{(l)})^{\top}\left(\tilde{\mathbf{T}}_{k}^{(l)}\Big|\tilde{\mathbf{T}}_{k}^{(l)}\right)\right\|\right\}\leq 2n_{0}\pi_{\max}\delta^{2}r/p.\end{split}

By the Freedman’s inequality (Corollary 1.3 in
[
73
]
),

P\left(\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{n_{0}}\tilde{\mathbf{T}}_{k}^{(l)}\right\|\geq t/2\right)\leq(r+1)\exp\left(-\frac{(tn_{0})^{2}/8}{2n_{0}\pi_{\max}\delta^{2}r/p+tn_{0}\delta\sqrt{r/p}/3}\right).

Next, similarly as (
90
), (
91
), and (
92
) in the proof of Lemma
7
, we can show

\left\|\mathbb{E}\left(\mathbf{T}_{k\alpha+l}\Big|x_{(k-1)\alpha+l}\right)-{\rm diag}(\pi)\mathbf{P}\right\|\leq t/2,

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\left\|\frac{1}{n_{0}}\sum_{k=1}^{n_{0}}\mathbf{T}_{k\alpha+l}-e_{s}^{\top}\mathbf{F}\mathbf{V}\right\|\geq t\right)\leq(r+1)\exp\left(-\frac{(tn_{0})^{2}/8}{2n_{0}\pi_{\max}\delta^{2}r/p+tn_{0}\delta\sqrt{r/p}/3}\right),

and

\begin{split}{\mathbb{P}}\left(\left\|e_{s}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{F}}\mathbf{V}-e_{s}^{\top}\mathbf{F}\mathbf{V}\right\|\geq t\right)\leq&\alpha(r+1)\exp\left(-\frac{(tn_{0})^{2}/8}{2n_{0}\pi_{\max}\delta^{2}r/p+tn_{0}\delta\sqrt{r/p}/3}\right)\\
\leq&\alpha(r+1)\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}n/(8\alpha)}{2\pi_{\max}\delta^{2}r/p+t\delta\sqrt{r/p}/3}\right).\end{split}

By Lemma
5
,
\alpha=\tau\left(\min(t/2,\pi_{\max})\right)+1\leq C\tau_{\ast}\log(1/(t\wedge\pi_{\max}))
. Next, for any
c_{0}>0
, we set

t=C\sqrt{\frac{\pi_{\max}\delta^{2}r\tau_{\ast}\log^{2}(n)}{np}}+C\frac{\delta\sqrt{r/p}\cdot\tau_{\ast}\log^{2}(n)}{n}

for large constant
C>0
. By
n\geq Cp\tau_{\ast}\log^{2}(n)\geq Cr
and
\pi_{\max}\geq 1/p
, we have

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\left\|e_{s}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{F}}\mathbf{V}-e_{s}^{\top}\mathbf{F}\mathbf{V}\right\|\geq C\sqrt{\frac{\pi_{\max}\delta^{2}r\tau_{\ast}\log^{2}(n)}{np}}+C\frac{\delta\sqrt{r/p}\cdot\tau_{\ast}\log^{2}(n)}{n}\right)\leq n^{-c_{0}-1},

and

\begin{split}&\max_{i}\|(\tilde{\mathbf{F}}\mathbf{V})_{i\cdot}-(\mathbf{F}\mathbf{V})_{i\cdot}\|_{2}=\max_{1\leq s\leq p}\left\|e_{s}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{F}}\mathbf{V}-e_{s}^{\top}\mathbf{F}\mathbf{V}\right\|\\
\leq&C\sqrt{\frac{\pi_{\max}\delta^{2}r\tau_{\ast}\log^{2}(n)}{np}}+C\frac{\delta\sqrt{r/p}\cdot\tau_{\ast}\log^{2}(n)}{n}\leq C\sqrt{\frac{\pi_{\max}\delta^{2}r\tau_{\ast}\log^{2}(n)}{np}}\end{split}

with probability at least
1-n^{-c_{0}-1}p\geq 1-n^{-c_{0}}
.
\square
