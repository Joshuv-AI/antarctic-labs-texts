On the dynamical Lie algebras of quantum approximate optimization algorithms
Jonathan Allcock, Miklos Santha, Pei Yuan, Shengyu Zhang

Abstract
Dynamical Lie algebras (DLAs) have emerged as a valuable tool in the study of parameterized quantum circuits, helping to characterize both their expressiveness and trainability. In particular, the absence or presence of barren plateaus (BPs)—flat regions in parameter space that prevent the efficient training of variational quantum algorithms—has recently been shown to be intimately related to quantities derived from the associated DLA. In this work, we investigate DLAs for the quantum approximate optimization algorithm (QAOA), one of the most studied variational quantum algorithms for solving graph MaxCut and other combinatorial optimization problems. While DLAs for QAOA circuits have been studied before, existing results have either been based on numerical evidence, or else correspond to DLA generators specifically chosen to be universal for quantum computation on a subspace of states. We initiate an analytical study of barren plateaus and other statistics of QAOA algorithms, and give bounds on the dimensions of the corresponding DLAs and their centers for general graphs. We then focus on the n n -vertex cycle and complete graphs. For the cycle graph we give an explicit basis, identify its decomposition into the direct sum of a 2 2 -dimensional center and a semisimple component isomorphic to n − 1 n-1 copies of 𝔰 ​ 𝔲 ​ ( 2 ) \mathfrak{su}(2) . We give an explicit basis for this isomorphism, and a closed-form expression for the variance of the cost function, proving the absence of BPs. For the complete graph we prove that the dimension of the DLA is 𝒪 ⁡ ( n 3 ) \mathcal{O}(n^{3}) and give an explicit basis for the DLA.

Introduction

Variational quantum algorithms (VQA)
[
1
]
are one of the two main paradigms in quantum algorithm design. While structured algorithms—the other main pillar, which includes Grover search
[
2

[

[

to be run on current and near-future noisy, intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) computers. Comprising parameterized quantum circuits (PQC) with tunable parameters, the structure and size of the circuits can be tailored to the hardware available, and the parameters tuned to optimize the value of a given loss function in a manner similar to artificial neural network training.

The broad and flexible VQA framework comes, however, with a number of challenges, including how to (i) design the circuit to balance expressiveness with trainability (i.e., the ability to produce good solutions without requiring a surfeit of parameters); (ii) effectively encode input data into the circuit; (iii) maintain performance in the presence of noise; and (iv) efficiently compute and optimize the loss function.

While some challenges may be coped with by a trial-and-error approach and many experiments, training efficiency is a prominent difficulty encountered in almost all VQAs. The optimization of the loss function can be problematic for several reasons. Firstly, evaluating gradients on a quantum computer is typically a time-intensive procedure, requiring many measurements. Secondly, even if the gradients can be computed, or gradient-free optimization methods used, VQAs can be susceptible to the ‘Barren Plateaus’ (BP) phenomenon

While the connection between DLAs and BPs is of fundamental conceptual significance, making use of this as a practical tool for understanding specific VQAs relies on our ability to compute various properties of interest: (i) the dimension and set of basis vectors for a particular DLA; (ii) its decomposition into abelian and simple components; and (iii) basis vectors for these components (which may not be easily deduced from a basis for the whole DLA). For a specific VQA with a fixed number of qubits, it is straightforward to evaluate the dimension and basis numerically although, in the worst case, the time complexity can scale exponentially in the number of qubits. However, the evaluation of (ii) and (iii) is more challenging, yet necessary for the loss function variance to be lower-bounded.

2.1
Variational quantum algorithms

We consider variational quantum algorithms with a layered structure. Such algorithms can be specified by (i) an initial
n
-qubit state
\rho
; (ii) a parameterized quantum circuit acting on
n
-qubits corresponding to a unitary operator

\displaystyle U(\theta)

\displaystyle=\prod_{l=1}^{L}\left(\prod_{k=1}^{K}e^{i\theta_{l,k}H_{k}}\right),

(1)

where
\theta=(\theta_{1,1},\ldots,\theta_{L,K})
are real parameters, and
H_{1},\ldots,H_{K}\in\mathfrak{su}(2^{n})
are Hermitian operators; (iii) a measurement operator
O\in\mathfrak{u}(2^{n})
,
\lVert{O}\rVert_{F}^{2}\leq 2^{n}
. For a specific choice of parameters
\theta
, the output of the circuit is given by the loss function

\displaystyle\ell(\rho,O;\theta)

\displaystyle=\tr(U(\theta)\rho U^{\dagger}(\theta)O),

and training the quantum circuit involves tuning the parameters to minimize the loss. The ease with which this can be done can be captured, in part, by the variance of the loss function,

\var_{\theta}[\ell(\rho,O;\theta)].

If the variance decreases exponentially in
n
, the loss is said to exhibit a
barren plateau,
and the VQA is generally extremely hard to train by gradient-based methods—the methods most useful in practice for both classical machine learning and variational quantum algorithms.

2.2
Dynamical Lie algebras

Our analysis employs tools from Lie algebra. A Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
is a vector space over some field
\mathbb{F}
equipped with a bilinear map
[\cdot,\cdot]
called the
Lie bracket
that satisfies, for all
A,B,C\in\mathfrak{g}
, (i)
[A,A]=0
and (ii)
[A,[B,C]]+[B,[C,A]]+[C,[A,B]]=0
(Jacobi identity). Two important families of Lie algebras for us will be

\displaystyle\mathfrak{sl}(n,\mathbb{C})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm{def}}}{{=}}\{H\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}:\tr(H)=0\},\quad\quad\mathfrak{su}(n)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm{def}}}{{=}}\{H\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}:H^{\dagger}=-H,\tr(H)=0\}.

The
dynamical Lie algebra
corresponding to the PQC (Eq. (
1
)), denoted

\displaystyle\mathfrak{g}

\displaystyle=\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle_{\text{Lie},\mathbb{R}},

is the Lie algebra over
\mathbb{R}
spanned by all possible nested commutators (such as
[A,[B,C]]
) of elements in
\mathcal{A}
. Here,
\mathcal{A}=\{iH_{1},\ldots,iH_{L}\}
is the set of generators of
\mathfrak{g}
,
and the Lie bracket is taken to be
[A,B]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm{def}}}{{=}}AB-BA
. Due to the Jacobi identity, without loss of generality, all nested commutators can be assumed to take the form
[H_{1},[H_{2},[\ldots,H_{s}]]]
. As a subalgebra of
\mathfrak{su}(2^{n})
, the DLA of a parameterized quantum circuit admits a decomposition
[
65
]

\displaystyle\mathfrak{g}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{c}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{k}

into simple Lie algebras
\mathfrak{g}_{i}
and a
center

\mathfrak{c}
of elements that commute with all of
\mathfrak{g}
. The
semisimple
component of
\mathfrak{g}
is
[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm{def}}}{{=}}\spn\{[A,B]:A,B\in\mathfrak{g}\}=\mathfrak{g}_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{k}
. If
O\in i\mathfrak{g}
or
\rho\in i\mathfrak{g}
, and the circuit is deep enough to form a unitary 2-design, then it was shown that the variance of the loss function
\ell
is related to the DLA in the following way
[
29
]
:

\av_{\theta}[\ell(\rho,O;\theta)]=\tr(\rho_{\mathfrak{c}}O_{\mathfrak{c}}),\quad\var_{\theta}[\ell(\rho,O;\theta)]=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{\mathcal{P}_{\mathfrak{g}_{j}}(\rho)\mathcal{P}_{\mathfrak{g}_{j}}(O)}{\dim(\mathfrak{g}_{j})}.

(2)

Here
\mathcal{P}_{\mathfrak{s}}(H)
, the
\mathfrak{s}
-purity of a Hermitian operator
H\in i\mathfrak{u}(2^{n})
, with respect to a subalgebra
\mathfrak{s}
of
\mathfrak{u}(2^{n})
, is defined as

\mathcal{P}_{\mathfrak{s}}(H)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm{def}}}{{=}}\tr(H_{\mathfrak{s}}^{2})=\|H_{\mathfrak{s}}\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{\dim(\mathfrak{s})}|\tr(E_{j}^{\dagger}H)|^{2},

where
H_{\mathfrak{s}}
is the orthogonal projection of operator
H
onto
\mathfrak{s}_{\mathbb{C}}
(the complexification of
\mathfrak{s}
), and
\{E_{j}:j=1,2,\ldots,\dim(\mathfrak{s})\}
is an orthonormal basis for
\mathfrak{s}
.

Note that the dimension of
\mathfrak{g}
alone is not sufficient for making use of Eq. (
2
), nor is a basis for the whole of
\mathfrak{g}
, as the purities of
\rho
and
O
may distribute on different subspaces
\mathfrak{g}_{j}
, in which case the variance can be 0. Therefore, lower bounding the variance requires understanding the decomposition of the Lie algebra into simple subalgebras, and finding a basis for these subalgebras, so that one can determine how the purities distribute amongst the
\mathfrak{g}_{j}
.

2.3
QAOA

The quantum approximate optimization algorithm (QAOA)
[
38
]
was originally proposed to approximately solve combinatorial optimization tasks specified by
n
bits and
m
clauses
C_{1},\ldots,C_{m}:\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
, where the aim is to maximize the objective function

C(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}C_{j}(z),

over the set of bit strings
z\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm{def}}}{{=}}z_{1}\ldots z_{n}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
. QAOA begins by initializing an
n
-qubit system in the state
\lvert{+}\rangle^{\otimes n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{n}}}\sum_{z\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\lvert{z}\rangle
, and then executes the layered variational circuit

\displaystyle U(\gamma,\beta)

\displaystyle=U_{B}(\beta_{p})U_{C}(\gamma_{p})\ldots U_{B}(\beta_{1})U_{C}(\gamma_{1}),

where
p
is a positive integer,
\gamma=(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{p})
and
\beta=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{p})
with
\beta_{j}\in[0,\pi)
and
\gamma_{j}\in[0,2\pi)
, and

\displaystyle U_{B}(\beta_{j})=\prod_{j=1}^{n}e^{-i\beta_{j}X_{j}},\quad U_{C}(\gamma_{j})=\prod_{j=1}^{m}e^{-i\gamma_{j}C_{j}}.

At the end of the circuit one measures in the computational basis and evaluates
C(z)
. When applied to the MaxCut problem on an
n
-vertex graph
G=(V,E)
, the
U_{C}
and
U_{B}
operators take the form

\displaystyle U_{B}(\beta)=e^{-i\beta\sum_{j\in V}X_{j}},\quad U_{C}(\gamma)=e^{-i\gamma\sum_{(j,k)\in E}Z_{j}Z_{k}}.

By comparison with Eq. (
1
), for an undirected graph
G=(V,E)
, the DLA of the corresponding QAOA MaxCut algorithm is then

\displaystyle\mathfrak{g}_{G}

\displaystyle=\langle\{i\sum_{j\in V}X_{j},i\sum_{(j,k)\in E}Z_{j}Z_{k}\}\rangle_{\text{Lie},\mathbb{R}}.

As in
[
29
]
, we normalize the measurement
O
so that
\|O\|_{F}^{2}=2^{n}
. This can be motivated by noticing that
\|P\|_{F}^{2}=2^{n}
for any Pauli string measurement such as
X_{1}
or
Z_{1}Z_{2}\ldots Z_{n}
. In the case of QAOA, we can thus take the measurement to be
O=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|E|}}\sum_{(j,k)\in E}Z_{j}Z_{k}
. Note that
O\in i\mathfrak{g}_{G}
and
\|O\|_{F}^{2}=2^{n}
.

2.4
Results for general DLAs

We first prove two general results that apply to any DLA
\mathfrak{g}
. The first is a simple upper bound on the dimension of the center.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}=\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle_{{\rm Lie},\mathbb{F}}
, and
\mathfrak{c}
its center. If
\mathcal{A}
is a set of
s
linearly independent elements then
\dim(\mathfrak{c})\leq s
.

In the case of QAOA, which has two linearly independent DLA generators, this immediately gives
\dim(\mathfrak{c})\leq 2
. The second result is an upper bound on the dimension of
\mathfrak{g}
, when the elements of
\mathcal{A}
(which are sums of Pauli strings) are invariant under certain symmetries. More precisely, for any Pauli string
P=P_{1}P_{2}\ldots P_{n}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
,
and any permutation
\pi\in S_{n}
, define

\pi(P)=P_{\pi(1)}P_{\pi(2)}\ldots P_{\pi(n)}.

By linearity, the action of
\pi
can be extended to the whole of
\mathfrak{su}(2^{n})
. We then have:

Theorem 2
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathfrak{su}(2^{n})
,
\mathfrak{g}=\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle_{{\rm Lie},\mathbb{R}}
and
S\leq S_{n}
. If
\pi(A)=A
for all
A\in\mathcal{A}
and all
\pi\in S
, then

\displaystyle\dim(\mathfrak{g})\leq\left|{\mathcal{P}_{n}/S}\right|-1,

where
\left|{\mathcal{P}_{n}/S}\right|
is the cardinality of the set
\mathcal{P}_{n}/S
of orbits of the
n
-qubit Pauli strings under the group action of
S
.

Applied to QAOA-MaxCut on an arbitrary graph
G
, the generators of
\mathfrak{g}_{G}
are easily seen to be
Aut(G)
invariant, where
Aut(G)
is the automorphism group of
G
. Thus,
\dim(\mathfrak{g}_{G})\leq\left|{\mathcal{P}_{n}/Aut(G)}\right|-1
. As an application of this result, by additionally showing that
\mathfrak{g}_{G}
is contained in a subalgebra spanned by Pauli strings where the total number of
Y
and
Z
operators is even and non-zero, we obtain a simple dimension upper-bound for the DLA of the complete graph of

\displaystyle\dim(\mathfrak{g}_{K_{n}})

\displaystyle\leq\begin{cases}\frac{1}{12}\left(n^{3}+6n^{2}+14n-12\right)&\quad\text{($n$ even)}\\
\frac{1}{12}\left(n^{3}+6n^{2}+11n-18\right)&\quad\text{($n$ odd)}\end{cases}.

(3)

We then focus more deeply on the cases where
G=C_{n}
and
G=K_{n}
, the cycle and complete graphs on
n
vertices, respectively. Among other things, this allows us to exactly determine the respective DLA dimensions.

3
Discussion

In this work we initiated a study of the dynamical Lie algebras for QAOA, proved a number of dimension bounds that apply to arbitrary graphs and, in particular, gave a full characterization — including an isomorphism to a direct sum of simple Lie algebras, and explicit basis vectors for each component — of the DLA corresponding to the
n
-vertex cycle graph
C_{n}
, and a partial characterization of the DLA for the complete graph
K_{n}
. There are a number of natural open questions:

•

Comparison between Eqs. (
3
) and (
12
) shows that the dimension bounds based on symmetry and
YZ
-parity are close to tight for
K_{n}
. This is not the case for graph families with smaller automorphism groups. Can tighter dimension bounds be proven for general graphs
G
?

•

What is the the decomposition of
[\mathfrak{g}_{K_{n}},\mathfrak{g}_{K_{n}}]
into its constituent simple Lie algebra components? Since
K_{3}=C_{3}
, our results for the cycle graph give
[\mathfrak{g}_{K_{3}},\mathfrak{g}_{K_{3}}]\cong\mathfrak{su}(2)\oplus\mathfrak{su}(2)
. In the Supplementary Information we show that
[\mathfrak{g}_{K_{4}},\mathfrak{g}_{K_{4}}]\cong\mathfrak{su}(2)\oplus\mathfrak{su}(2)\oplus\mathfrak{su}(3)
. What about in general? Based on the dimension of
\mathfrak{g}_{K_{n}}
in Eq. (
12
), the fact that
\dim(\mathfrak{su}(n))=n^{2}-1
, we conjecture that, for
n\geq 2
,

\displaystyle[\mathfrak{g}_{K_{n}},\mathfrak{g}_{K_{n}}]

\displaystyle\cong\bigoplus_{j=1}^{n-1}\mathfrak{su}\left(\left\lfloor\frac{j+1}{2}\right\rfloor+1\right).

•

For what families of graphs can a full characterization of the DLAs be given? For which families of graphs are barren plateaus not present?

•

Beyond QAOA-MaxCut, which other variational quantum algorithm DLAs with generators that are sums of Pauli strings can be fully characterized? This scenario corresponds to parameter sharing, where a single tunable parameter is used to tune multiple Pauli strings at the same time.

In the longer term, a better understanding of DLAs for variational circuits with parameter sharing can hopefully be useful not only for analyzing quantum circuits, but also as a tool in algorithm design. For example, to accelerate algorithm convergence, one can consider reducing the depth of a circuit and correlating parameters
[
13
]
, or adding additional DLA generators that do not preserve the symmetries of solutions
[
42
]
. Knowing how a set of
\mathcal{A}
of generators is related to the corresponding DLA, and how adapting
\mathcal{A}
quantitatively changes the expressiveness and trainability of the circuit, is key to tailoring variational algorithms for improved performance.

4
Methods

In this section we prove, or outline the proofs of, the theorems and other claims made in the Results section. For brevity, we omit some of the proofs of supporting lemmas here. All missing proofs appear in the Supplementary Information.

Let us first review some concepts from the theory of Lie algebras. Given a Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
, if
[A,B]=0
for all
A,B\in\mathfrak{g}
then
\mathfrak{g}
is said to be
commutative
or
abelian
. A
Lie subalgebra

\mathfrak{h}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}
of a Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
is a linear subspace of
\mathfrak{g}
which is closed under the Lie bracket, i.e.
[H_{1},H_{2}]\in\mathfrak{h}
for all
H_{1},H_{2}\in\mathfrak{h}
. A Lie algebra is said to be
compact
if it is the Lie algebra of a compact Lie group (see
[
68
]
or
[
65
]
for further details). A Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
is said to be the direct sum of subalgebras
\mathfrak{g}_{1},\mathfrak{g}_{2},\ldots,\mathfrak{g}_{k}
, denoted

\displaystyle\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}_{1}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{2}\oplus\ldots\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{k},

if it is the direct sum of
\mathfrak{g}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{g}_{k}
as vector spaces, and
[\mathfrak{g}_{i},\mathfrak{g}_{j}]=0
for all
i\neq j
. An
ideal
of a Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
is a Lie subalgebra
\mathfrak{i}
of
\mathfrak{g}
satisfying the further requirement that
[A,B]\in\mathfrak{i}
for all
A\in\mathfrak{g}
and
B\in\mathfrak{i}
. An ideal
\mathfrak{i}
of
\mathfrak{g}
is
trivial
if
\mathfrak{i}=\mathfrak{g}
or
\mathfrak{i}=\{0\}
. For any
\mathfrak{g}
, one can define the following two ideals:

\displaystyle Z(\mathfrak{g})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm{def}}}{{=}}\{A\in\mathfrak{g}:[A,B]=0,\forall\,B\in\mathfrak{g}\},

\displaystyle\quad(\text{center of }\mathfrak{g})

\displaystyle[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm{def}}}{{=}}\spn\{[A,B]:A,B\in\mathfrak{g}\},

\displaystyle\quad(\text{commutator ideal})

A
simple Lie algebra
is a Lie algebra that is non-abelian and contains no nontrivial ideals. A
semisimple Lie algebra
is one which is the direct sum of simple Lie algebras. Given a Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
and any
A\in\mathfrak{g}
, the associated
adjoint map
is the linear transformation
ad_{A}:\mathfrak{g}\rightarrow\mathfrak{g}
given by
ad_{A}(B)=[A,B]
. A
Cartan subalgebra

\mathfrak{h}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}
of a complex semisimple Lie algebra is a maximal commutative subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}
, where
ad_{H}
is diagonalizable for all
H\in\mathfrak{h}
.

4.1
General DLAs

Here we give proofs of Theorems
1
and
2
, as well as the upper bound for the DLA of the complete graph given in Eq. (
3
).

Computing a basis
\mathcal{B}
for a DLA (and hence its dimension) from a set of generators
\mathcal{A}
is algorithmically straightforward, but has time polynomial in the DLA dimension, which can be exponential in the number of qubits
n
. The basis is constructed iteratively: first set
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{A}
. Then compute the set of all possible commutators
\{[A,B]:A\in\mathcal{A},B\in\mathcal{B}\}
and, for each commutator, if it is linearly independent from the elements in
\mathcal{B}
, add it to the basis. Repeat the process until no new linearly independent elements are found. Algorithm
1
in the Supplementary Information makes this procedure precise.

We now prove Theorem
1
, which we recall below.

See
1

Proof.

Suppose that
\mathcal{A}=\{H_{1},\ldots,H_{s}\}
, and Algorithm
1
outputs the basis

\{H_{1},\ldots,H_{s},\ldots,H_{m}\}.

Then by the algorithm, any element
H_{t}
with
t\geq s+1
is generated by taking the commutator of two previously generated matrices
H_{j}
and
H_{j^{\prime}}
.
H_{j}
and
H_{j^{\prime}}
are both in
\mathfrak{g}
, and thus
H_{t}=[H_{j},H_{j^{\prime}}]
is in
[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}]
. Thus
H_{s+1},\ldots,H_{m}
are all in
[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}]
. Since they are also linearly independent as required by the algorithm, we know that
[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}]
has dimension at least
m-s
. As
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{c}\oplus[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}]
, we obtain the claimed bound by

\dim(\mathfrak{c})=\dim(\mathfrak{g})-\dim([\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}])\leq s.

∎

We remark that if the elements of
\mathcal{A}
are not linearly independent, then
\dim(\mathfrak{c})
is upper-bounded by the maximum number of linearly independent elements of
\mathcal{A}
.

To prove Theorem
2
, first recall that a (left)

of a group
G

is a function
f:G\times X\rightarrow X
,
(g,x)\mapsto g.x
, such that (i)
e.x=x
, (ii)

, where

is the identity element of
G
. The

of

under the action of

is

\displaystyle G_{X}(x)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm{def}}}{{=}}\{g.x:g\in G\},

(13)

and the set of all orbits of

under the action of
G
, denoted

, forms a partition of
X
. Note that the orbit-sum of a Pauli string

(see Eq. (
4

under the action of a subgroup of
S_{n}
.

For any Pauli string
P=P_{1}P_{2}\ldots P_{n}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
,

\pi(P)=P_{\pi(1)}P_{\pi(2)}\ldots P_{\pi(n)}.

This defines an action

on the set

By linearity this can be extended to the whole of
\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{l}(2^{n},\mathbb{C})

\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{l}(2^{n},\mathbb{C})

.

7

Let

Now, any

Proof.

F
.

Case 1 (
n
