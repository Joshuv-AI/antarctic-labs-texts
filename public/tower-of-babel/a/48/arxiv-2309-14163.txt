Uniform multi-penalty regularization for linear ill-posed inverse problems Thanks: Submitted to the editors DATE.
Villiam Bortolotti, Germana Landi, Fabiana Zama

Abstract
This study examines, in the framework of variational regularization methods, a multi-penalty regularization approach which builds upon the Uniform PENalty (UPEN) method, previously proposed by the authors for Nuclear Magnetic Resonance data processing. The paper introduces two iterative methods, UpenMM and GUpenMM, formulated within the Majorization-Minimization framework. These methods are designed to identify appropriate regularization parameters and solutions for linear inverse problems utilizing multi-penalty regularization. The paper demonstrates the convergence of these methods and illustrates their potential through numerical examples, showing the practical utility of point-wise regularization terms in solving various inverse problems.



keywords

Linear ill-posed problems, multi-penalty regularization, Uniform Penalty Principle, Majorization-Minimization Methods, Balancing Principle.

Introduction

Variational regularization methods form the foundation for addressing a wide range of linear inverse ill-posed problems that can be expressed as:

A\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{e}=\mathbf{b}

where
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
represents noisy data,
\mathbf{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
is a Gaussian noise vector,
A\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}
,
M\geq N
, is a linear operator and
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is the exact object to be recovered (see for instance
[
1
]
and
[
2
]
for a survey). In the following, we assume
A
to be full rank. Variational methods involve minimizing an objective functional that comprises a data fidelity term,
denoted as
\phi(\mathbf{u})
, and a regularization term, denoted as
\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{u})
, i.e.:

\min\limits_{\mathbf{u}\in\Omega}\;\{\phi(\mathbf{u})+\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{u})\},

where
\Omega
is a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
used to
impose some physical constraints, such as, for example, non-negativity.
The data fidelity term reflects the problem formulation and accounts for the uncertainty in the measured data. On the other hand, the regularization term incorporates prior knowledge about the unknown solution, such as its smoothness or sparsity. The impact of the regularization term is governed by a scalar value
\lambda
called the regularization parameter.

To capture diverse and contrasting characteristics of solutions, we investigate a multi-penalty regularization approach setting

\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{u})=\sum_{i=i}^{p}\lambda_{i}\psi_{i}(\mathbf{u})

(1)

where
\psi_{i}(\mathbf{u})
,
i=1,\ldots,p
are regularization functions
and we propose a convergent numerical method to compute both
\mathbf{u}
and
\lambda_{i}
. In our experiments, we focus on the following point-wise penalties

\psi_{i}(\mathbf{u})=(L\mathbf{u})_{i}^{2}+\epsilon,\quad i=1,\ldots,N,\quad p=N

(2)

where
L\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
represents the discretization of the second-order derivative operator
with periodic boundary conditions,
and
\epsilon
is a positive constant. This approach was successfully proposed for the first time in 1998 in the context of one-dimensional Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) data processing, together with a heuristic numerical procedure for its realization, named Uniform PENalty (UPEN) method (Borgia et al.
[
3
,
4
]
).
The high quality of the results obtained, especially in the study of porous media properties, has been widely recognized by the scientific community, leading to the conference series Magnetic Resonance in Porous Media, started in Bologna in 1990 and yielding in 2012 the UPenWin software tool distributed by the University of Bologna (
https://site.unibo.it/softwaredicam/en/software/upenwin
).
In 2016, an extension to the two-dimensional case was developed, beginning the formalization of UPEN within the mathematical framework of inverse problems
[
5
]
. The first formulation of the Uniform Penalty Principle was introduced, with the proof that solutions conforming to this principle are regularized solutions of the data fitting model. Subsequent extensions have considered the case of multi-penalty regularization including
L_{1}
-based terms and more software tools
[
6
,
7
]
were created to tackle problems in the field of NMR relaxometry.
What has been missing until now is a formal analysis of the numerical method to prove its convergence properties. This is the main contribution of the present work. To obtain our main result, initially, contributions previously developed in the mathematical field of inverse problems are leveraged, particularly from the results of Ito et al. that, starting in 2011, introduce and analyze the Balancing Principle (BP) for multi-penalty regularization.
This principle, analyzed in
[
8
]
and
[
25
]
when
p=2
, is extended here to the case of point-wise penalties (
2
) and box constraints, and the solution and regularization parameters are characterized as critical points of an Augmented Tikhonov functional. In this way, the Uniform Penalty Principle is recognized as a particular case of the generalized Balancing Principle, and the properties of the resulting variational model are demonstrated. Then the heuristic numerical procedure UPEN is framed into the class of the Majorization-Minimization (MM) methods. This analysis allows us to establish the convergence of UPEN to the solution of the variational model, satisfying the Uniform Penalty Principle.

Multi-penalty regularization has gained growing interest in recent literature, but most of the authors consider the case of two-penalty terms. We remark that the famous elastic regression used in Statistics
[
9
]
is a multi-penalty regularization method combining the
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
penalties of the Lasso
[
10
]
and Ridge
[
11
]
methods. Moreover, most of the literature is devoted to the problem of developing a suitable parameter choice rule.
Lu, Pereverzev et al.
[
12
,
13
]
thoroughly investigate the case of two
L_{2}
-based terms and present a modified Discrepancy Principle for the computation of the two regularization parameters together with its numerical implementation.
The parameter choice issue is also discussed in
[
14
]
, where a multi-parameter generalization of the L-curve criterium is proposed, and in
[
15
]
, where an approach based on the Generalized Cross Validation method is suggested.
Reichel and Gazzola
[
16
]
consider regularization terms of the form

\psi_{i}(\mathbf{u})=\|D_{i}\mathbf{u}\|^{2},\qquad i=1,\ldots,p

(3)

where
D_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}\times N}
(
d_{i}\leq N
)
are proper regularization matrices and describe an approach to determine the regularization parameters based on the Discrepancy Principle focusing on the case
p=2
.
Fornasier et al.
[
17
]
propose a modified version of the discrepancy principle for multi-penalty regularization and provide a theoretical justification.
Multi-penalty regularization is also considered for unmixing problems with two penalty terms based on
L_{q}
and
L_{p}
norms,
0\leq q<2
and
2\leq p<\infty
, in
[
18
,
19
,
20
,
21
]
and in
[
22
]
, where the penalty terms are based on the
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
norms.
Finally, in
[
23
]
, two-penalty regularization, with
L_{0}
and
L_{2}
penalty terms, is considered for nonlinear ill-posed problems and its regularizing properties are analyzed.
Ito et. al. in
[
8
]
and subsequent papers
[
24
]
and
[
25
]
,
introduce the Balancing Principle to handle multiple-penalty regularization and present fixed-point-like algorithms for the case of two penalties.

Proposal of two iterative methods, named UpenMM and GUpenMM, developed within the framework of MM methods, to compute the regularization parameters and solutions of linear inverse problems using multi-penalty regularization.

2
Derivation of the uniform multi-penalty regularization

In this section, we present a generalization of the Balancing Principle, originally introduced in
[
24
]
and initially formulated for a single penalty term. This principle has been further extended to accommodate multiple penalties in subsequent works
[
8
,
25
]
, with a particular focus on the case of two penalty terms.
Here, we provide a detailed analysis of multipenalty regularization specifically for the case where there are more than two penalty terms, for example representing point-wise second-order smoothing terms as in (
2
).

We first present the regularization problem in a Bayesian framework, providing a statistical interpretation to the choice of regularization parameters and to the structure of the Augmented Tikhonov problem (
4
). Theorem
1
and Corollary
2
allow us to establish the existence of the minimum of the Augmented Tikhonov functional. Then,
introducing the value function (
9
), we show in Theorem
4

that the regularization parameters are critical points of a function
\Phi_{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
. This theorem links theoretical insights to practical computation, showing how to compute the regularization parameters that balance data fidelity with regularization terms effectively.

To start, we introduce the vector of the penalty parameters
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and the vector penalty function
\boldsymbol{\psi}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{p}
such that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{p})^{T},\quad\lambda_{i}>0,\;i=1,\ldots,p

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\psi}(\mathbf{u})=(\psi_{1}(\mathbf{u}),\ldots,\psi_{p}(\mathbf{u}))^{T},\quad\psi_{i}(\mathbf{u})\geq\epsilon,\;i=1,\ldots,p

where
\psi_{i}
are positive continuous functions and
\epsilon
is a small positive constant.
Assuming the experimental data
\mathbf{b}
to be corrupted by Gaussian white noise, we consider the following data-fit function

\phi(\mathbf{u})=\frac{1}{2}\|A\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{b}\|^{2}.

By employing a hierarchical Bayesian approach
[
26
]
,
the information about the unknown solution is contained in the Posterior Probability Density Function
P(\mathbf{u},\boldsymbol{\lambda},\tau|\mathbf{b})
where
\tau=1/\sigma^{2}
is the inverse variance of data noise. Extending the approach in
[
27
]
by applying it to
p
penalty parameters
\lambda_{i}
and regularization functions
\psi_{i}
,
we consider the critical points
of the following Augmented-Tikhonov problem:

\min_{(\mathbf{u},\boldsymbol{\lambda},\tau)}\;\left\{\frac{\tau}{2}\|A\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{b}\|^{2}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{T}\boldsymbol{\psi}(\mathbf{u})+\left[\beta_{0}\tau-\left(\frac{M}{2}+\alpha_{0}-1\right)\ln(\tau)\right]+\right.\\
\left.\sum_{i=1}^{p}\left[\beta_{i}\lambda_{i}-\left(\frac{N}{2}+\alpha_{i}-1\right)\ln(\lambda_{i})\right]\right\}

(4)

where
(\alpha_{i},\beta_{i})
, with
i\geq 1
, and
(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})
are parameter pairs representing the prior distributions. Following
[
27
]
, these distributions are assumed to be Gamma distributions, and they correspond to the hyperparameters
\lambda_{i}
and
\tau
respectively.

In the following theorem, we prove that (
4
) is well-posed.

Theorem 1
.

There exists at least one minimizer to the Augmented-Tikhonov function
\mathcal{J}
:

\mathcal{J}(\mathbf{u},\boldsymbol{\lambda},\tau)\equiv\frac{\tau}{2}\|A\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{b}\|^{2}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{T}\boldsymbol{\psi}(\mathbf{u})+\left[\beta_{0}\tau-\alpha_{0}^{\prime}\ln(\tau)\right]+\sum_{i=1}^{p}\left[\beta_{i}\lambda_{i}-\alpha_{i}^{\prime}\ln(\lambda_{i})\right]

(5)

with
\alpha_{0}^{\prime}=\left(\frac{M}{2}+\alpha_{0}-1\right)
,

\alpha_{i}^{\prime}=\left(\frac{p}{2}+\alpha_{i}-1\right)
,
i=0,\ldots,p
.

Proof.

The functional
\mathcal{J}(\mathbf{u},\boldsymbol{\lambda},\tau)
is continuous on
\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathcal{V}\times(0,\infty)

where

\mathcal{V}=\left\{\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{R}^{p}:z_{i}>0,\ i=1,\ldots,p\right\}
. Therefore it is sufficient to prove that it is bounded from below and it is coercive, i.e. it tends to infinity when approaching the boundary of
\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathcal{V}\times(0,\infty)
.
To prove that
\mathcal{J}(\mathbf{u},\boldsymbol{\lambda},\tau)
is bounded from below, we observe that

\mathcal{J}(\mathbf{u},\boldsymbol{\lambda},\tau)\geq\left[\beta_{0}\tau-\alpha_{0}^{\prime}\ln(\tau)\right]+\sum_{i=1}^{p}\left[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(\beta_{i}+\epsilon)}\lambda_{i}-\alpha_{i}^{\prime}\ln(\lambda_{i})\right]\equiv\nu(\tau)+\zeta(\boldsymbol{\lambda}).

We prove that
\zeta(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
is convex on
\mathcal{V}
. Let
\mathcal{H}
be the Hessian matrix of
\zeta(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
,
we observe that it is diagonal and positive definite
i.e.

\mathcal{H}_{k,j}=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\lambda_{k}\partial\lambda_{j}}\zeta(\boldsymbol{\lambda})=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{\alpha_{k}^{\prime}}{\lambda_{k}^{2}}>0,&k=j;\\
&0,&k\neq j;\end{aligned}\right.

therefore the critical point
\hat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
of
\zeta
:

\frac{\partial\zeta}{\partial\lambda_{i}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})=0,\quad\text{i.e.}\quad\hat{\lambda}_{i}=\frac{\alpha_{i}^{\prime}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\beta_{i}+\epsilon}}>0,\ i=1,\ldots,p

defines its minimum, i.e.
\zeta(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\geq\zeta(\hat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
,
\forall\boldsymbol{\lambda}\neq\hat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
.
Similarly,
\nu(\tau)
has a minimum
\hat{\tau}=\frac{\alpha_{0}^{\prime}}{\beta_{0}}>0
and

\nu(\tau)\geq\alpha_{0}^{\prime}-\alpha_{0}^{\prime}\ln(\frac{\alpha_{0}^{\prime}}{\beta_{0}}).

Therefore
\mathcal{J}(\mathbf{u},\boldsymbol{\lambda},\tau)
is bounded from below.

Next, we show that
\mathcal{J}
is coercive.
First we observe that:

\lim_{\tau\rightarrow 0^{+}}\nu(\tau)=+\infty,\quad\lim_{\tau\rightarrow+\infty}\nu(\tau)=+\infty.

Moreover, since

\|\boldsymbol{\lambda}\|\to 0,\quad\text{iff}\quad\lambda_{i}\to 0,\quad\forall i

and

\|\boldsymbol{\lambda}\|\to\infty,\quad\text{iff}\quad\exists i,\;1\leq i\leq p\;\text{s.t.}\;\lambda_{i}\to\infty

we have

\lim_{\|\boldsymbol{\lambda}\|\rightarrow 0^{+}}\zeta(\boldsymbol{\lambda})=+\infty\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{\|\boldsymbol{\lambda}\|\rightarrow+\infty}\zeta(\boldsymbol{\lambda})=+\infty.

Let
c_{0}
be
\nu(\hat{\tau})+\zeta(\hat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
, then

\mathcal{J}(\mathbf{u},\boldsymbol{\lambda},\tau)-c_{0}\geq\frac{\tau}{2}\|A\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{b}\|^{2}.

Since the quadratic form
\|A\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{b}\|^{2}
is positive definite, it is coercive and consequently,
\mathcal{J}
is coercive, i.e:

\lim_{\|\mathbf{u}\|\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{J}(\mathbf{u},\boldsymbol{\lambda},\tau)=+\infty

and it has a finite minimizer on
\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathcal{V}\times(0,\infty)
.

Corollary 2
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be the compact subset of admissible solutions
\mathbf{u}
, then there exists at least one minimizer of the functional
\mathcal{J}
in (
5
), where
\mathbf{u}\in\Omega
,
\tau>0
and
\lambda_{i}>0
,
\forall i
.

Proof.

Let
\boldsymbol{i}_{\Omega}(\mathbf{u})
be the indicator function of

\Omega
, i.e.

\boldsymbol{i}_{\Omega}(\mathbf{u})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\mathbf{u}\in\Omega;\\
\infty&\mathbf{u}\notin\Omega;\end{array}\right.

through the functional
\hat{\mathcal{J}}
:

\hat{\mathcal{J}}\equiv\mathcal{J}+\boldsymbol{i}_{\Omega}(\mathbf{u})

we can view the minimization of
\mathcal{J}
where
\mathbf{u}\in\Omega
as the minimization of
\hat{\mathcal{J}}
where
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, i.e.:

\min_{\mathbf{u}\in\Omega}\mathcal{J}=\min_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\hat{\mathcal{J}}.

The functional
\hat{\mathcal{J}}(\mathbf{u},\boldsymbol{\lambda},\tau)
is continuous on
\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathcal{V}\times(0,\infty)

where

\mathcal{V}=\left\{\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{R}^{p}:z_{i}>0,\ i=1,\ldots,p\right\}.

Observing that

\hat{\mathcal{J}}>\mathcal{J}

we can proceed as in Theorem
1

and prove that
\hat{\mathcal{J}}
is continuous, bounded from below and coercive. Hence there is at least one minimizer in
\Omega\times\mathcal{V}\times(0,\infty)
.

In the hierarchical Bayesian approach, the hyperparameter
\tau
serves as an estimator of the inverse of the noise variance
\sigma^{2}

[
27
]
.
The first-order optimality conditions of the augmented Tikhonov functional
\mathcal{J}(\mathbf{u},\boldsymbol{\lambda},\tau)
yield the following expression:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{J}}{\partial\tau}(\mathbf{u},\boldsymbol{\lambda},\tau)=\frac{1}{2}\|A\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{b}\|^{2}+\beta_{0}-\frac{\alpha^{\prime}_{0}}{\tau}=0.

By substituting
\alpha_{0}^{\prime}=\left(\frac{M}{2}+\alpha_{0}-1\right)
, we can derive the relationship for
\sigma^{2}
:

\sigma^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\|A\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{b}\|^{2}+2\beta_{0}}{\frac{M}{2}+\alpha_{0}-1}.

(6)

Analogously to the formulation in
[
25
]
(Eq. 2.1), we express the critical points of
\mathcal{J}(\mathbf{u},\boldsymbol{\lambda},\tau)
under the assumption of a noninformative prior, where
\beta_{0}\approx 0
and
\beta_{i}=0
, which is commonly adopted in practice
[
27
,
1
]
.
The critical points can be written as:

\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=&\arg\min_{\mathbf{u}}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\|A\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{b}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{\hat{\tau}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\psi}(\mathbf{u})\right\};\\
\hat{\lambda}_{i}=&\frac{\alpha_{i}}{\psi_{i}(\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})},\;i=1,\ldots,p;\\
\hat{\tau}=&\frac{\alpha_{0}}{\|A\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}-\mathbf{b}\|^{2}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\beta_{0}}}.\end{aligned}\right..

Here, we assume
\alpha_{i}=\alpha
, and by setting
\gamma=\alpha_{0}/\alpha
and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}/\hat{\tau}
,
we obtain

\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=&\arg\min_{\mathbf{u}}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\|A\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{b}\|^{2}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{T}\boldsymbol{\psi}(\mathbf{u})\right\};\\
\lambda_{i}=&\frac{\|A\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}-\mathbf{b}\|^{2}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\beta_{0}}}{\gamma\psi_{i}(\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})},\;\;i=1,\ldots,p.\\
\end{aligned}\right.

(7)

Following
[
24
]
and
[
25
]
, the multi-parameter Balancing Principle is derived by setting
\beta_{0}=0
in (
7
):

\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=&\arg\min_{\mathbf{u}}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\|A\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{b}\|^{2}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{T}\boldsymbol{\psi}(\mathbf{u})\right\};\\
\lambda_{i}=&\frac{\|A\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}-\mathbf{b}\|^{2}}{\gamma\psi_{i}(\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})},\;\;i=1,\ldots,p.\\
\end{aligned}\right.

(8)

In this formulation, the optimal regularization parameter
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
is obtained by balancing the least-squares fidelity term
\tfrac{1}{2}\|A\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{b}\|^{2}
with the penalty functions
\psi_{i}
.

Remark 3
.

It is worth noting that when the parameter
\alpha_{0}
is set to
1
and
\beta_{0}\approx 0
, the expression (
6
) simplifies further:

\sigma^{2}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\approx}\frac{\|A\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{b}\|^{2}}{M}.

This result indicates that the mean residual norm, obtained with the Balancing Principle, can be a reliable estimate of the noise variance.
The expression relates to the Morozov discrepancy principle
[
28
]
, where the residual norm is used to tune the regularization parameter to the noise level, thus aligning the model fidelity with the observed data’s noise characteristics.

Let
\Omega
be a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
; following
[
8
]
, we
define the value function as

F(\boldsymbol{\lambda})=\min_{\mathbf{u}\in\Omega}\;\{\phi(\mathbf{u})+{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\psi}(\mathbf{u})\}.

(9)

In
[
8
]
, it is proved that
F(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
is a concave and differentiable function whose derivatives, with respect to
\lambda_{j}
, are given by:

\frac{\partial F}{\partial\lambda_{j}}=\psi_{j}(\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}),\quad j=1,\ldots,p

(10)

where

\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=\arg\min_{\mathbf{u}\in\Omega}\;\{\phi(\mathbf{u})+{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\psi}(\mathbf{u})\}.

In the following theorem,
we present the variational characterization of the Balancing Principle (
8
),
by extending
the result shown in
[
25
]
for the case of
p=2
to the case
p\gg 2
.

Theorem 4
.

Let
\gamma
be a positive constant, and let us define

\Phi_{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\equiv\frac{F^{\gamma+p}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})}{\prod_{i=1}^{p}\lambda_{i}}

(11)

with
F(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
given in equation (
9
). Then, the function
\Phi_{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}):\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is continuous, differentiable and
the vector
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
with components

\lambda_{j}^{*}=\frac{\phi(\mathbf{u}_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{*}})}{\gamma\psi_{j}(\mathbf{u}_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{*}})},\quad j=1,\ldots,p

(12)

where

\mathbf{u}_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{*}}=\arg\min_{\mathbf{u}\in\Omega}\;\{\phi(\mathbf{u})+{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\psi}(\mathbf{u})\}

is a critical point of
\Phi_{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
.

Proof.

Continuity and differentiability of
\Phi_{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
follow from continuity and differentiability of the value function
F(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
which have been proved in
[
8
]
(cfr. Lemma 2.1 and Theorem 2.2).
Since
F
is differentiable, the critical point
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}
is defined by

\frac{\partial\Phi_{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*})}{\partial\lambda_{j}}=0,\qquad j=1,\ldots,p.

(13)

Computing the derivatives:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Phi_{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})}{\partial\lambda_{j}}=

\displaystyle\frac{(\gamma+p)F^{\gamma+p-1}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\cdot\frac{\partial F(\boldsymbol{\lambda})}{\partial\lambda_{j}}-F^{\gamma+p}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\cdot\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}}}{\left(\prod_{i=1}^{p}\lambda_{i}\right)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{F^{\gamma+p-1}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})}{\left(\prod_{i=1}^{p}\lambda_{i}\right)}\left[(\gamma+p)\frac{\partial F(\boldsymbol{\lambda})}{\partial\lambda_{j}}-\frac{F(\boldsymbol{\lambda})}{\lambda_{j}}\right]

and substituting the expression (
10
),
we obtain that the derivatives are null when the parameters
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{j}
satisfy the following linear equations:

(\gamma+p)\lambda_{j}\cdot\psi_{j}(\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})-F(\boldsymbol{\lambda})=0,\qquad j=1,\ldots,p.

(14)

Substituting the expression for
F(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
into (
14
), we obtain the following linear system:

(\gamma+p)\lambda_{j}\cdot\psi_{j}(\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})-\sum_{i=1}^{p}\lambda_{i}\psi_{i}(\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})=\phi(\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}),\quad j=1,\ldots,p

(15)

which can be written in matrix form as

\left(\mathbf{D}-\mathbf{1}\boldsymbol{\psi}(\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})^{T}\right)\boldsymbol{\lambda}=\phi(\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})\mathbf{1}

(16)

where
\mathbf{D}
is the diagonal matrix of order
p
with non-zero elements
D_{i,i}=(\gamma+p)\psi_{i}(\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
,
and
\boldsymbol{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is a column vector with elements equal to one.
We observe that the linear system has a matrix which is the sum of the diagonal
\mathbf{D}
and the rank-one update
-\mathbf{1}\boldsymbol{\psi}(\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})^{T}
. Since
\mathbf{D}
is non singular because
\psi_{i}(\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})>0
for all
i
and

1+\mathbf{1}^{T}D^{-1}\boldsymbol{\psi}(\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})=1-\frac{1}{\gamma+p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}1=1-\frac{p}{p+\gamma}=\frac{\gamma}{p+\gamma}\neq 0,

we can apply the Shermann-Morrison formula
[
29
]
and obtain (see details in Appendix
A
):

\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{D}-\mathbf{1}\boldsymbol{\psi}(\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})^{T}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p+\gamma}\left[\begin{pmatrix}\tfrac{1}{\psi_{1}(\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})}&\\
0&\tfrac{1}{\psi_{2}(\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})}&\\
0&0&\ddots&\\
0&\cdots&0&\tfrac{1}{\psi_{p}(\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})}\end{pmatrix}\right.

\displaystyle+\left.\frac{1}{\gamma}\begin{pmatrix}\tfrac{1}{\psi_{1}(\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})}&\tfrac{1}{\psi_{1}(\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})}&\cdots&\tfrac{1}{\psi_{1}(\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})}\\
\tfrac{1}{\psi_{2}(\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})}&\tfrac{1}{\psi_{2}(\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})}&\cdots&\tfrac{1}{\psi_{2}(\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\tfrac{1}{\psi_{p}(\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})}&\tfrac{1}{\psi_{p}(\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})}&\cdots&\tfrac{1}{\psi_{p}(\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})}\end{pmatrix}\right].

Hence the solution of the linear system (
16
) is:

\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}=\left(\mathbf{D}-\mathbf{1}\boldsymbol{\psi}(\mathbf{u}_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{*}})^{T}\right)^{-1}\phi(\mathbf{u}_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{*}})\mathbf{1}

whose components are

\lambda^{*}_{j}=\phi(\mathbf{u}_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{*}})\frac{1}{p+\gamma}\left(\frac{1}{\psi_{j}(\mathbf{u}_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{*}})}+\frac{p}{\gamma\psi_{j}(\mathbf{u}_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{*}})}\right)=\frac{\phi(\mathbf{u}_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{*}})}{\gamma\psi_{j}(\mathbf{u}_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{*}})},\quad j=1,\ldots,p

which concludes the proof.

This theorem shows that we can characterize the solution to the BP system as critical points of the function
\Phi_{\gamma}
(
11
).
The solution depends on the choice of the parameter
\gamma
; in
[
8
]
an iterative procedure has been proposed for its selection in the case
p=2
but a general criterion for
p\gg 2
is still missing.

The Uniform Penalty Principle introduced in
[
3
,
5
]
suggests choosing the regularization parameters so that they satisfy the following two conditions:

C1

All the penalty terms
\lambda_{i}\psi_{i}(\mathbf{u})
,
i=1,\ldots,p
, are uniform and equal to a constant value
c
, i.e.

\lambda_{i}\psi_{i}(\mathbf{u})=c,\quad i=1,\ldots,p

which gives
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{T}\boldsymbol{\psi}(\mathbf{u})=cp
.

C2

The multi-penalty term
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{T}\boldsymbol{\psi}(\mathbf{u})
is equal to the data fidelity term:

\phi(\mathbf{u})=\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{T}\boldsymbol{\psi}(\mathbf{u}).

Conditions
C1
and
C2
give

\lambda_{i}=\frac{\phi(\mathbf{u})}{p\psi_{i}(\mathbf{u})},\quad i=1,\ldots,p

which corresponds to
\gamma=p
in the Balancing Principle.

Considering the observed properties of the noise estimation (cfr. Remark
3
), when
p=M
, the penalty terms are uniform and equal to the
M
th fraction of the noise variance. For this reason, we will refer to the nonlinear system

\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=&\arg\min_{\mathbf{u}\in\Omega}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\|A\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{b}\|^{2}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{T}\boldsymbol{\psi}(\mathbf{u})\right\};\\
\lambda_{i}=&\frac{\|A\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{b}\|^{2}}{p\psi_{i}(\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})},\;i=1,\ldots,p;\end{aligned}\right.

(17)

as “Uniform Penalty System” (UPS).
It is evident that the UPS is a particular case of the BP obtained for
\gamma=p
.

3
The numerical method

In order to develop a suitable iterative method for computing the solution
(\mathbf{u}^{*},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*})
to the UPS (
17
), we use a majorization-minimization approach
[
30
]
to the optimization problem

\min\limits_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\;\Phi_{p}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}).

(18)

The following theorem shows that problem (
18
) is well defined.

Theorem 5
.

The function
\Phi_{p}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}):\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is coercive.

Proof.

Let
\{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(k)}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence such that
\|\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(k)}\|\rightarrow\infty
.
Since
\psi_{j}(\mathbf{u})\geq\epsilon
for all
j
and
\phi(\mathbf{u})\geq 0
, we get

\displaystyle\Phi_{p}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(k)})

\displaystyle=\displaystyle{\frac{\left[\min\limits_{\mathbf{u}\in\Omega}\;\phi(\mathbf{u})+\sum_{j=1}^{p}\lambda_{j}^{(k)}\psi_{j}(\mathbf{u})\right]^{2p}}{\prod_{j=1}^{p}\lambda_{j}^{(k)}}}

\displaystyle\geq\displaystyle{\frac{\left[\epsilon\sum_{j=1}^{p}\lambda_{j}^{(k)}\right]^{2p}}{\prod_{j=1}^{p}\lambda_{j}^{(k)}}}\geq\displaystyle{\frac{\left[\epsilon\max_{j}\lambda_{j}^{(k)}\right]^{2p}}{\left[\max_{j}\lambda_{j}^{(k)}\right]^{p}}}

where we have used
\sum_{j=1}^{p}\lambda_{j}^{(k)}\geq\max_{j}\lambda_{j}^{(k)}
. Summarizing, we have

\Phi_{p}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(k)})\geq\epsilon^{2p}\left[\max_{j}\lambda_{j}^{(k)}\right]^{p}

which gives the thesis.

In the following, we first revise the MM framework and then we derive our iterative scheme to address (
18
).

3.1
Majorization-minimization framework

Let
f(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
be a function to be minimized over
\mathbb{R}^{p}
. Given an initial guess
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(0)}
, at each iteration
k\in\mathbb{N}
of a MM method, the following two steps are performed.

i) Majorization Step.
A surrogate function
Q(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(k)})
is constructed which satisfies the majorization conditions

\begin{array}[]{l}Q(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(k)},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(k)})=f(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(k)}),\\
Q(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(k)})\geq f(\boldsymbol{\lambda}),\quad\text{for each}\;\boldsymbol{\lambda}.\end{array}

(19)

ii) Minimization step.
The successive iterate
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(k+1)}
is obtained by minimizing the surrogate function
Q(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(k)})
with respect to
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
, i.e:

\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(k+1)}=\arg\min\limits_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\;Q(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(k)}).

As a consequence of the majorization step, a MM method gives a sequence of iterates
\{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(k)}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
for which the objective function decreases monotonically:

f(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(k+1)})\leq Q(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(k+1)},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(k)})\leq Q(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(k)},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(k)})=f(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(k)}).

Under mild conditions, convergence of a MM sequence
\{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(k)}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
to stationary points can be proved by using the convergence results given in
[
31
]

for EM algorithms. For the sake of completeness, we report here a classical convergence result
[
32
,
33
]
.

Theorem 6
.

Consider a MM method with surrogate function
Q(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime})
applied to the optimization problem

\min\limits_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\;f(\boldsymbol{\lambda})

and suppose that

i)

f:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is continuous and differentiable;

ii)

the level set
\mathcal{L}=\{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\;|\;f(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\leq f(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(0)})\}
is compact;

iii)

Q(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime})
is continuous in both
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}
and differentiable in
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
.

Then, all the limit points of the generated sequence
\{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(k)}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
are stationary points of
f(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
and the sequence
\{f(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(k)})\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
converges monotonically to
f(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*})
for some stationary point
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}
.

3.2
The algorithm

In the MM framework, we derive an iterative method for (
18
) by defining the following surrogate function for
\Phi_{p}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
:

Q(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime})=\frac{\left(\phi(\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}})+\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{T}\boldsymbol{\psi}(\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}})\right)^{2p}}{\prod_{i=1}^{p}\lambda_{i}}

where

\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}}=\arg\min\limits_{\mathbf{u}\in\Omega}\;\{\phi(\mathbf{u})+(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime})^{T}\boldsymbol{\psi}(\mathbf{u})\}.

(20)

It immediately follows that

Q(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\lambda})=\Phi_{p}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}).

Moreover, we have

Q(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime})\geq\frac{\left(\min\limits_{\mathbf{u}}\;\left\{\phi(\mathbf{u})+\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{T}\boldsymbol{\psi}(\mathbf{u})\right\}\right)^{2p}}{\prod_{i=1}^{p}\lambda_{i}}=\Phi_{p}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}).

Thus, the function
Q(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime})
satisfies the surrogate conditions (
19
).
Observe that the surrogate function is continuous in both its arguments and differentiable with respect the first one since
\Phi_{p}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
is continuous and differentiable (cfr. Theorem
5
).
Then, convergence of our MM scheme immediately follows from Theorem
6
.
In Theorem
8
we prove that a local minimum to the surrogate function
Q(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(k)})
can be found in a closed form. To this purpose we need the following proposition.

Proposition 7
.

Let
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
be defined as follows:

z_{1}=-\frac{1}{\sigma_{1}},\quad z_{2}=-\frac{2p}{2p-1}\frac{1}{\sigma_{2}},

z_{k}=-\frac{2p}{((2p-1)-(k-2))}\frac{1}{\sigma_{k}},\qquad k\geq 3,

(21)

where

\sigma_{1}=\sqrt{2p-1}
,
\sigma_{2}=\sqrt{\frac{(2p-1)^{2}-1}{2p-1}}

and

\sigma_{k}=\left(\frac{(2p-1)^{2}-(k-2)(2p-1)-(k-1)}{(2p-1)-(k-2)}\right)^{1/2},\qquad k=3,\ldots,p.

(22)

Then

\sigma_{i}^{2}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{i-1}z_{\ell}^{2}=2p-1,\qquad i=1,\ldots,p,

and

\sum_{\ell=1}^{i-1}z_{\ell}^{2}+z_{i}\sigma_{i}=-1.

Proof.

The result will be proven by induction. Let us define the induction statement
P(i)
composed by two parts:

P(i),\qquad\sigma_{i}^{2}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{i-1}z_{\ell}^{2}=2p-1,\qquad\sum_{\ell=1}^{i-1}z_{\ell}^{2}+z_{i}\sigma_{i}=-1.

For
i=1
, we have

\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{1-1}z_{\ell}^{2}=\sigma_{1}^{2}=\left(\sqrt{2p-1}\right)^{2}=2p-1,

which satisfies the first part of
P(1)
. Similarly,

\sum_{\ell=1}^{1-1}z_{\ell}^{2}+z_{1}\sigma_{1}=z_{1}\sigma_{1}=-\frac{1}{\sigma_{1}}\sqrt{2p-1}=-1,

which satisfies the second part of
P(1)
.

Assume
P(k)
is true for some
k\geq 1
, i.e.,

\sigma_{k}^{2}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{k-1}z_{\ell}^{2}=2p-1,\qquad\sum_{\ell=1}^{k-1}z_{\ell}^{2}+z_{k}\sigma_{k}=-1.

Now, we will prove
P(k+1)
. Concerning the first part we have:

\sigma_{k+1}^{2}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}z_{\ell}^{2}=\sigma_{k+1}^{2}+z_{k}^{2}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{k-1}z_{\ell}^{2};

adding the term
(+\sigma_{k}^{2}-\sigma_{k}^{2})
and using
P(k)
we obtain

\sigma_{k+1}^{2}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}z_{\ell}^{2}=\sigma_{k+1}^{2}+z_{k}^{2}-\sigma_{k}^{2}+(2p-1).

Substituting the expressions in (
21
), (
22
) (see Appendix
B
) we can verify that:

\sigma_{k+1}^{2}+z_{k}^{2}-\sigma_{k}^{2}=0

hence

\sigma_{k+1}^{2}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}z_{\ell}^{2}=(2p-1).

Proceeding in a similar way, we can prove the second part of
P(k+1)
:

\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}z_{\ell}^{2}+z_{k+1}\sigma_{k+1}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{k-1}z_{\ell}^{2}+z_{k}^{2}+z_{k+1}\sigma_{k+1}+\sigma_{k}z_{k}-\sigma_{k}z_{k}

and using
P(k)
we obtain

\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}z_{\ell}^{2}+z_{k+1}\sigma_{k+1}=-1-\sigma_{k}z_{k}+z_{k}^{2}+z_{k+1}\sigma_{k+1}

where, substituting (
21
), (
22
)
(see Appendix
B
), we obtain:

-\sigma_{k}z_{k}+z_{k}^{2}+z_{k+1}\sigma_{k+1}=0.

Hence, we have proven
P(k+1)
, which completes the induction step.

We can now prove our main result, showing that the regularization parameters determined by the UPS (
17
) constitute a local minimum to the surrogate function.

Theorem 8
.

The vector
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
with components

\lambda_{i}^{*}=\frac{\phi(\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}})}{p\psi_{i}(\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}})},\;i=1,\ldots,p

is a local minimum for the surrogate function
Q(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime})
.

Proof.

We prove this results by showing that
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}
satisfies the second order sufficient condition.
We have

\frac{\partial Q(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime})}{\partial\lambda_{j}}=\frac{\left(\phi(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})+\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{T}\boldsymbol{\psi}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})\right)^{2p-1}}{\prod_{i=1}^{p}\lambda_{i}}\cdot\left(2p\psi_{j}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})-\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}}\left(\phi(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})+\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{T}\boldsymbol{\psi}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})\right)\right)

(23)

where, for easier notation,
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}
denotes
\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}}
. Since
\phi(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})+(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*})^{T}\boldsymbol{\psi}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})=2\phi(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})
, by evaluating the second term at
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}
, we obtain

2p\psi_{j}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})-\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}^{*}}\left(\phi(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})+(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*})^{T}\boldsymbol{\psi}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})\right)=2p\psi_{j}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})-\frac{p\psi_{j}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})}{\phi(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})}\cdot 2\phi(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})=0.

(24)

Thus
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}
is a critical point and the result is proved if we show that the Hessian matrix of
Q
at
(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime})
is positive definite. By combining (
23
) and (
24
), we obtain

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\lambda_{\ell}\partial\lambda_{j}}Q(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime})_{\big|\boldsymbol{\lambda}=\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}}=\\
\frac{\left(2\phi(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})\right)^{2p-1}}{\prod_{i=1}^{p}\lambda_{i}^{*}}\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_{\ell}}\left(2p\psi_{j}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})-\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}}\left(\phi(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})+\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{T}\boldsymbol{\psi}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})\right)\right)_{\big|\boldsymbol{\lambda}=\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}}

and

\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_{\ell}}\left(2p\psi_{j}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})-\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}}\left(\phi(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})+\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{T}\boldsymbol{\psi}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})\right)\right)=\\
\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle{-\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}}\psi_{\ell}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})},&\hbox{if $j\neq\ell$;}\\
\displaystyle{-\frac{1}{\lambda_{\ell}}\psi_{\ell}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})+\frac{1}{\lambda_{\ell}^{2}}\left(\phi(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})+\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{T}\boldsymbol{\psi}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})\right)},&\hbox{if $j=\ell$.}\end{array}\right.

Therefore

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\lambda_{\ell}\partial\lambda_{j}}Q(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime})_{\big|\boldsymbol{\lambda}=\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}}=\frac{\left(2\phi(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})\right)^{2p-1}}{\prod_{i=1}^{p}\lambda_{i}^{*}}\cdot\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle{-\frac{p}{\phi(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})}\psi_{j}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})\psi_{\ell}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})},&\hbox{if $j\neq\ell$;}\\
\displaystyle{\frac{p}{\phi(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})}(2p-1)\psi_{\ell}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})^{2}},&\hbox{if $j=\ell$;}\end{array}\right.

where we have used

-\frac{1}{\lambda^{*}_{\ell}}\psi_{\ell}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})+\frac{1}{{\lambda^{*}_{\ell}}^{2}}\left(\phi(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})+(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*})^{T}\boldsymbol{\psi}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})\right)=-\frac{p}{\phi(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})}\psi_{\ell}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})^{2}+2\phi(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})\frac{p^{2}}{\phi(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})^{2}}\psi_{\ell}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})^{2}.

Hence,

\nabla^{2}Q(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime})=\frac{p\left(2\phi(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})\right)^{2p-1}}{\phi(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})\prod_{i=1}^{p}\lambda_{i}^{*}}H

where
H
is the symmetric matrix defined as

H_{j\ell}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\psi_{j}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})\psi_{\ell}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime}),&\hbox{if $j\neq\ell$}\\
(2p-1)\psi_{\ell}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})^{2},&\hbox{if $j=\ell$}\end{array}\right.,\quad j,\ell=1,\ldots,p.

We prove that
H
is positive definite by showing that it possesses a unique Cholesky factorization
[
34
]
.
Indeed, applying the Cholesky procedure to
H

we obtain a lower triangular matrix
L
defined as follows:

\displaystyle L_{1,1}=\psi_{1}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})\sqrt{2p-1},

(25)

\displaystyle L_{i,1}=-\psi_{i}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})/\sqrt{2p-1},\qquad i=2,\ldots,p,

\displaystyle L_{2,2}=\psi_{2}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})\sigma_{2},\ \sigma_{2}=\sqrt{\frac{(2p-1)^{2}-1}{2p-1}},

\displaystyle L_{i,2}=-\psi_{i}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})\frac{2p}{2p-1}/\sigma_{2},\qquad i=3,\ldots,p,

\displaystyle k=3,\ldots,p

\displaystyle L_{k,k}=\psi_{k}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})\sigma_{k},\ \sigma_{k}=\left(\frac{(2p-1)^{2}-(k-2)(2p-1)-(k-1)}{(2p-1)-(k-2)}\right)^{1/2},

\displaystyle L_{i,k}=-\psi_{i}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})\frac{2p}{\sigma_{k}((2p-1)-(k-2)},\ i=k+1,\ldots,p.

Now we prove that
H=LL^{T}
and
L
has positive diagonal elements.

Defining
\sigma_{1}=\sqrt{2p-1}
we can write each row of
L
in (
25
) as follows:

L_{i,\cdot}=\psi_{i}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})\left(z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{i-1},\sigma_{i},0,\ldots,0\right)^{T},\quad i>1,

where

z_{1}=-\frac{1}{\sigma_{1}},\quad z_{2}=-\frac{2p}{2p-1}\frac{1}{\sigma_{2}},\qquad z_{k}=-\frac{2p}{((2p-1)-(k-2))}\frac{1}{\sigma_{k}},\qquad k\geq 3.

Using Proposition
7
with
N=p
, we can easily check the Cholesky factorization of
H
. In the case
i=j
we have

H_{i,i}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{p}L_{i,\ell}L_{i,\ell}=\left(\sigma_{i}^{2}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{i-1}z_{\ell}^{2}\right)\psi_{i}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})^{2}=(2p-1)\psi_{i}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})^{2},\qquad i=1,\ldots,p.

When
i\neq j
, for symmetry we consider
1<j<i\leq p
:

H_{i,j}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{p}L_{i,\ell}L_{j,\ell}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{j}L_{i,\ell}L_{j,\ell}=\psi_{i}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})\psi_{j}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{j-1}z_{\ell}^{2}+z_{j}\sigma_{j}\right)=-\psi_{i}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})\psi_{j}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime}).

We can check that the diagonal entries of
L
, given by:

L_{k,k}=\psi_{k}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})\sigma_{k}>0,\qquad\psi_{k}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})>0\ k=1,\ldots,p

are all positive. Indeed it is straighforward for
k=1,2
:

\sigma_{1}=\sqrt{2p-1}>0,\quad p>0

and
\sigma_{2}
in (
25
) is positive for
p>1
.
For the terms
k=3,\ldots,p
, we consider
f(k)\equiv\sigma_{k}^{2}
, i.e.

f(k)=\frac{(2p-1)^{2}-(k-2)(2p-1)-(k-1)}{(2p-1)-(k-2)},

and observe that
p+1\leq(2p-1)-(k-2)\leq 2p
, hence

f(k)\geq\frac{(2p-1)^{2}-(k-2)(2p-1)-(k-1)}{2p}\equiv\mathcal{G}(k).

Since
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}(k)=-1
then the minimum is reached at
k=p
, i.e.

f(k)\geq\frac{2p^{2}}{2p}=p,\quad p\geq 1.

Therefore
\sigma_{k}\geq\sqrt{p}>0
, for
k\geq 3
.
Computing the Cholesky factorization of
H
we have proved that it is positive definite for all
p
. This concludes the proof.

At the
k
th iteration of our MM scheme, the construction of the surrogate function
Q(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(k)})
in the majorization step requires the solution of a constrained quadratic optimization problem (
20
). When the feasible set
\Omega
is the nonnegative orthant, this task can be efficiently performed by using a gradient projection-type method
[
36
]
. By Theorem
8
, the
minimization step, leads to

\lambda_{i}^{(k+1)}=\frac{\phi(\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(k)}})}{p\psi_{i}(\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(k)}})},\;i=1,\ldots,p.

(26)

We remark that the regularization parameter vector
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(k+1)}
obtained via (
26
) is nonnegative.
Moreover, assumption
\psi_{i}(\mathbf{u})>\epsilon
,
i=1,\ldots,p
, prevents division by zero.
We refer to our iterative method for solving model (
17
) as UpenMM and we sketch it in Algorithm
1
where, for easier notation,
\mathbf{u}^{(k)}
denotes
\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(k)}}
.

Algorithm 1

UPenMM

1:

Choose
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(0)}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, and set
k=0
.

2:

repeat

3:

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{(k)}=\arg\min\limits_{\mathbf{u}\in\Omega}\;\left\{\frac{1}{2}\|A\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{b}\|^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{p}\lambda_{i}^{(k)}\psi_{i}(\mathbf{u})\right\}

4:

\lambda_{i}^{(k+1)}=\displaystyle{\frac{\|A\mathbf{u}^{(k)}-\mathbf{b}\|^{2}}{p\psi_{i}(\mathbf{u}^{(k)})}},\;i=1,\ldots,p

a
stopping criterion
is satisfied

In our implementation, we stop Algorithm

when the relative change in the
computed parameter vector
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
goes below a certain threshold
Tol_{\lambda}\in(0,1)
or a maximum number of iterations
k_{\text{max}}
is reached.

Remark 9
.

Algorithm
1
was introduced in
[
8
]
as a fixed-point like method for the numerical realization of the BP with two penalty terms. However, its convergence was not proven for the general case
p>1
.

3.3
Generalized UPenMM

UPenMM is a general framework which embeds different regularization functions
\psi_{i}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
.
We notice, however, that a converging procedure can still be obtained even if, instead of using the exact minimum of the surrogate function, we compute a vector
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(k+1)}
satisfying the descent condition
[
35
,
32
]

Q(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(k+1)},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(k)})\leq Q(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(k)},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(k)}).

(27)

Let us denote by
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
the exact minimizer of the surrogate function defined in (
26
), and let
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
be an approximation obtained by a different relation
(for easier notation we have omitted the index
k
). We propose a generalized UPenMM (GUPenMM) algorithm, that uses a convex combination of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
and
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
. More precisely, given
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, the next iterate
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(k+1)}
is the first element of the sequence

\{\;\varepsilon^{j}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}+(1-\varepsilon^{j})\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}},\;j=0,1,2,\ldots\}

(28)

which satisfies (
27
). We observe that (
28
) converges to
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
as
j\to\infty
, that obviously satisfies (
27
). The generalized algorithm, through appropriate strategies for computing
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
, allows for decisive improvements in the case of real data affected by noise.
GUPenMM is outlined in Algorithm
2
using the same
stopping criterion
as in Algorithm
1
.

GUPenMM

,
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, and set

.

repeat

3:

4:

Compute
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{(k+1)}
and
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{(k+1)}
; set
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(k+1)}=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{(k+1)}
and
j=0

5:

while

Q(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(k+1)},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(k)})>Q(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(k)},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(k)})

do

6:

j=j+1

7:

\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(k+1)}=\varepsilon^{j}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{(k+1)}+(1-\varepsilon^{j})\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{(k+1)}

is satisfied

4
Numerical Results

This section presents results derived from the application of UPenMM and GUPenMM to two-dimensional NMR relaxometry problems. We focus on Gaussian noise as it aligns with the data fitting function embedded in our model.
We analyze the convergence of UPenMM and GUPenMM in terms of the decrease of the surrogate function and evaluate their performance in efficiently reducing relative errors while recovering various complex features.
Analogous results are observed in one-dimensional tests, which are provided in the supplementary materials. These materials also include the convergence history and comparisons between GUPenMM and other methods, such as Tikhonov regularization, confirming the effectiveness of UPenMM and GUPenMM in recovering different signal features, including peaks, smooth rounded areas, and ramps, at varying noise intensities.
All experiments were conducted using Matlab R2023b on an Apple M1 computer with 16 GB of RAM. The codes used for the current experiments can be made available upon request to the authors.

The UPenMM and GUPenMM iterations are stopped according to the following

\|\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(k+1)}-\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(k)}\|\leq\|\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(k)}\|Tol_{\lambda}

The regularization model employed here has
N
local
L_{2}
penalty functions and one global
L_{1}

\psi_{i}(\mathbf{u})=(L\mathbf{u})_{i}^{2}+\epsilon

(32)

The modified penalty functions are given by:

\widetilde{\psi}_{i}(\mathbf{u}^{(k)})=\max_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{i}}(L\mathbf{u}^{(k)})_{i}^{2}+\max_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{i}}(P\mathbf{u}^{(k)})_{i}^{2}+\epsilon,\quad i=1,\ldots,N

denotes the index subsets of the neighborhood around the
i
th point, for
i=1,\ldots,N

Remark 10
.

The functions
\widetilde{\psi}_{i}(\mathbf{u})
are obtained by applying a nonlinear maximum filter.
The practice of introducing a nonlinear maximum filter in one or two dimensions was known from the earliest works
[
3
,
5
]
. It allows for better control of instabilities caused by noise and improves the quality of approximations. In this work, we demonstrate that it fits within the context of the generalized UPenMM method. By controlling the decrease of the surrogate function,
we obtain GUPenMM, an efficient algorithm where in general less than two convex combinations in (
28
) are requested (i.e.
j\leq 2
) for each step.
With this modification, the sequence of approximate solutions and regularization parameters rapidly converge, confirming what has been known heuristically for several years.

5
Conclusions

This paper provides the theoretical foundation of point-wise and multi-penalty regularization through the principle of uniform penalty. The numerical realization of such a principle has been in use since the ’90s and has been successfully applied to multiple application problems for its efficiency and relative simplicity. However, until now, there was a lack of a theoretical analysis that characterized the solutions and proved convergence in the most general case. With this work, we have provided the characterization of the solutions through the hierarchical Bayesian approach. We have extended to the case of point-wise and multi-penalty with bound constraints, what was available in the literature for unconstrained solutions. Moreover, using the fundamental contributions of Ito et al.
[
25
]
, we have formalized the proof of convergence of the UPenMM method for very general regularization functionals. To achieve this, it was essential to view this method in the context of majorization-minimization algorithms. This is another original contribution of this work: through the identification of an appropriate surrogate function, it was possible to verify the convergence of UPenMM. Finally, the introduction of a generalized MM approach allowed us to include, in this theoretical framework, heuristic techniques used to address distortions due to noise in real data.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates the formal validity of the original UPEN algorithm, developed heuristically, and lays the foundation for its extension to inverse problems of a different nature
compared to the application field (NMR relaxometry) for which UPEN was initially conceived.

Future work includes investigating such a strategy on data affected by non-Gaussian noise, which could lead to nonlinear inverse problems.
