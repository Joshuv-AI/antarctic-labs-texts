Building your path to escape from home
D. Figueiredo 1, G. Iacobelli 1, R. Oliveira 2, B. Reed 3 4, R. Ribeiro 2

Abstract
. Random walks on dynamic graphs have received increasingly more attention from different academic communities over the last decade. Despite the relatively large literature, little is known about random walks that construct the graph where they walk while moving around. In this paper we study one of the simplest conceivable discrete time models of this kind, which works as follows: before every walker step, with probability p p a new leaf is added to the vertex currently occupied by the walker. The model grows trees and we call it the Bernoulli Growth Random Walk (BGRW). We show that the BGRW walker is transient and has a well-defined linear speed c ⁡ ( p ) > 0 c(p)>0 for any 0 < p ≤ 1 0<p\leq 1 . We also show that the tree as seen by the walker converges (in a suitable sense) to a random tree that is one-ended. Some natural open problems about this tree and variants of our model are collected at the end of the paper. Keywords : random walks, random environments, dynamic random environments, local weak convergence, random trees, rooted graphs, transience

Random walks on graphs that change over time have received much attention over the past decades. Within this context, a large body of work assumes only edge (or node) weights change over time while the graph structure (i.e., edge set) remains constant. Theorem 1.1

(Positive speed)

For each

\lim_{n\to\infty}\,\frac{{\rm dist}_{T_{n}}(X_{n},x_{0})}{n}=c(p),\;\mathbb{P}_{T_{0},x_{0},p}\mbox{-almost surely.}

sees along the way. A more general question is what is the frequency of (finite) trees that

sees in a radius

For each

For each

1.2.
Intuition and some difficulties

Behind our Theorems, there is a simple probabilistic mechanism that explains our claims.

(1)

Given a constant
\ell
, the BGRW will often create new induced paths of length
\ell
. That is, there will be frequent times when the walker is at the “tip” of a path of length
\ell
.

(2)

On the other hand, the probability of backtracking on a path of length
\ell
by more than
\ell/2
steps goes to zero very fast with
\ell
.

The moments when BGRW creates a long path and does not backtrack by more than half its length are “local regeneration times” in the following precise sense: the sequence of neighborhoods of radius
\ell/2
seen by the walker from that point on are independent of the past. From this perspective, the Laws of Large Numbers implicit in Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
are not surprising.

As it turns out, there are difficulties in establishing the intuitive picture drawn by items
1
and
2
above. Creating paths looks easy enough: all one needs is to create a new leaf and jump to it
\ell
consecutive times. However, in order for this to happen often, the walker needs to come across lots of nodes of low degree. For this to take place, it is necessary that nodes are usually not visited too many times. To prove that returns to a vertex are unlikely to be numerous, one needs to show that the walk moves away fast enough from any vertex. The upshot is that a weaker form of
positive speed
is needed before we can justify the above items.

Proving that
{\rm dist}_{T_{n}}(X_{n},X_{0})
grows
at least linearly with time
will thus be the first major goal in our proof. Omer Angel (personal communication) suggested to us that this might be done comparing our process to a once reinforced random walk on trees. The rationale is that one may imagine that BGRW “discovers” new edges rather than create them. Unfortunately, we could not see a way to make the elegant arguments of Colevecchio
[
6
,
7
]
work in our setting. We thus resort to a bare-hands argument.

Given the preliminary result on the growth of
{\rm dist}_{T_{n}}(X_{n},X_{0})
, we can move on to establishing the existence of the speed and the convergence of the tree as seen by the walker. It will be crucial for us that this tree – or rather, the corresponding empirical measure – converges almost surely to an invariant measure
P_{p}
for the dynamics on rooted trees, for all “nice” initial conditions. There is a high-level similarity to the work of Lyons, Pemantle and Peres
[
15
]
on random walk on Galton-Watson trees, where the existence of a speed relies on ergodic-theoretic arguments in the space
\mathcal{T}_{*}
. However, in that paper the stationary measure can be described explicitly (leading to an explicit formula for the speed), which is not the case in our setting. Our analysis will also require more quantitative estimates on the convergence. Finally, we note that the random trees we consider are very different (eg. our tree is a.s. one-ended).

1.3.
Organization and main proof steps

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we define our process formally. One important conceptual point will be to define it on locally finite trees from the start.

The proof of linear growth of
\mathrm{dist}_{T_{n}}(X_{n},X_{0})
begins in Section
3
, where we show that, for any positive
M
, it is very likely that
\mathrm{dist}_{T_{m}}(X_{m},X_{0})\geq\log^{M}n
for some
m\leq n
. This requires comparisons with simple one dimensional random walk. The argument continues in Section
4
, where we prove that backtracking on a long path, if it happens at all, typically takes a really long time. For this we introduce a simplified “loop process” that only keeps track of the path itself along the way. Combining polylog distances and long times to backtrack, we prove in Section
5
that our process has positive drift away from
X_{0}
, and derive some consequences of this fact for the degrees in the tree.

We switch gears for the remainder of the paper, as our arguments become more abstract. In Section
6
we extend the definition of our process to the space of rooted locally finite trees. The statement in Theorem
1.2
, on the convergence of the tree as seen by the walker, can then be stated as a weak convergence result for the empirical measure of this process. Tightness and weak convergence criteria are discussed in this section, and tightness is proven right away.

Section
5
begins to study the loss-of-memory mechanism described above, whereby long paths are created and never backtracked on. Since we deal with infinite trees, we need some condition on the initial distribution to ensure that this takes place. We use this in Section
6
to prove that the averages of “local functions” along the trajectories of the process always converge almost surely. This is basically the last ingredient we need to prove stronger versions of Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
in Section
7
. One key point is that the limiting measure
P_{p}
is a stationary distribution for our process.

The paper wraps up with Section
8
, with some final comments, and an Appendix containing technical results.

. We deduce:

(3.8)

{\mathbb{P}}_{T,x,p}(F\cap\{\tau^{+k}_{x}<\tau_{y_{*}}\leq n\})\leq\left(1-\frac{p}{2}\right)^{k}.

Finally, we come to the third term in the RHS of (
3.6
). Consider the walk
X_{t}
at the sequence of time steps that it spends on the path from
x
to
y
, up to the time
\tau_{y_{*}}
. The resulting process is a simple random walk on the path with potential delays and reflecting barriers: indeed, since our graph is a tree at all times, whenever the random walk leaves the path, it must return to it (if it does return) at the same point that was last visited. Now, in order that
\tau_{x}^{+k}>\tau_{y_{*}}
, it must be that this simple random walk on the path hits
y_{*}
before returning
k
times to
x
. Since the path has length
\mathrm{dist}_{T}(x,y)=\lceil\log^{M}n\rceil-1
, the probability of this happening is
\leq k/(\lceil\log^{M}n\rceil-1)
, and we obtain:

(3.9)

{\mathbb{P}}_{T,x,p}(\tau^{+k}_{x}>\tau_{y_{*}})\leq\frac{k}{\lceil\log^{M}n\rceil-1}.

Combining the terms in (
3.7
), (
3.8
) and (
3.9
), we obtain a bound in (
3.6
):

(3.10)

\begin{split}{\mathbb{P}}_{T,x,p}(F)&\leq e^{-n^{1/4}}+\left(1-\frac{p}{2}\right)^{k}+\frac{k}{\lceil\log^{M}n\rceil-1}\\
&\leq\frac{\lceil(\log\log n)^{2}\rceil}{\log^{M}n}\left[e^{-n^{1/4}}\frac{\log^{M}n}{\lceil(\log\log n)^{2}\rceil}+e^{-\frac{p}{2}(\log\log n)^{2}}\frac{\log^{M}n}{\lceil(\log\log n)^{2}\rceil}+\frac{\log^{M}n}{\log^{M}n-1}\right],\\
\end{split}

where the last inequality follows by choosing
k:=\lceil(\log\log n)^{2}\rceil
. Choosing
n_{1}=n_{1}(p,M)
sufficiently large in (
3.10
) such that

e^{-n^{1/4}}\frac{\log^{M}n}{\lceil(\log\log n)^{2}\rceil}+e^{-\frac{p}{2}(\log\log n)^{2}}\frac{\log^{M}n}{\lceil(\log\log n)^{2}\rceil}+\frac{\log^{M}n}{\log^{M}n-1}<2
we obtain that, for all
n\geq n_{1}
,

(3.11)

\begin{split}{\mathbb{P}}_{T,x,p}(F)&\leq 2\frac{(\log\log n)^{2}}{\log^{M}n}.\end{split}

This proves the claim. Note that
n_{1}(p,M)
is not-increasing in
p
.

3.3.
The “harder” proposition

We now prove Proposition
3.2
, thus finishing the proof of Lemma
3.1
.

Proof of Proposition
3.2
.

Fix an admissible
M\geq\frac{1}{2}
. Define (with hindsight) two time lengths:

(3.12)

t_{*}:=\lceil\log^{M+1/2}\,n\rceil,\,\ell_{*}:=\left\lfloor\sqrt{n}\right\rfloor.

Both of these time lengths depend on
n
, but we omit this dependency from the notation to avoid clutter. The definitions of these quantities will probably seem misterious, but we comment on them in due time; see Remarks
3.1
,
3.2
and
3.3
below.

The fact that
M
is admissible and
\ell_{*}\to+\infty
when
n\to+\infty
implies that, for any
0<p\leq 1
, there exists
n_{2}(p,M)
such that if
n\geq n_{2}(p,M)
, we have the following properties for any finite tree
T_{0}
and any
x_{0}\in T_{0}
.

(1)

By the definition of admissibility (Definition
3.1
) applied with
\ell_{*}
replacing
n
:

(3.13)

{\mathbb{P}}_{T_{0},x_{0},p}\left(\exists m\leq\ell_{*},\mathrm{dist}_{T_{m}}(X_{m},y)\geq 2^{-M}\log^{M}n\right)\geq 1-e^{-\ell_{*}^{1/4}}\geq 1-e^{-\frac{1}{2}n^{1/8}}

(2)

By Claim
3.1
applied with
\ell_{*}
replacing
n
, if
T
is finite and
x,y\in T
with
\mathrm{dist}_{T}(x,y)\geq 2^{-M}\log^{M}n
, then:

(3.14)

{\mathbb{P}}_{T,x,p}\left(\mathrm{dist}_{T_{t}}(X_{t},y)=\mathrm{dist}_{T}(x,y)+1\mbox{ for some }t\leq\ell_{*}\right)\geq 1-2\frac{(\log\log n)^{2}}{2^{-M}\log^{M}n}.

Again we used that
\log\ell_{*}\approx\log n
.

Remark 3.1

(
\ell_{*}
is large enough)
.

In the above we used the fact that
\log\ell_{*}\approx\log n
to guarantee that the two events under consideration have high probability. We will later need that
n/\ell_{*}t_{*}
is large; see Remark
3.2
below.

We now define a sequence of stopping times
\sigma_{j}
and indicators
I_{j}
. Intuitively, we will want that
\mathrm{dist}_{T_{\sigma_{j}}}(X_{\sigma_{j}},y)>\mathrm{dist}_{T_{\sigma_{j-1}}}(X_{\sigma_{j-1}},y)
, and we will signal such a success by setting
I_{j}=1
. More formally, for
j=0
,
\sigma_{j}=0
and
I_{j}=0
. We define
\sigma_{j}
and
I_{j}
for
j>0
, with the following two choices.

(a)

When
X_{\sigma_{j-1}}
is too close to
y
:
if
\mathrm{dist}_{T_{\sigma_{j-1}}}(X_{\sigma_{j-1}},y)<2^{-M}\log^{M}n
, we let:

(3.15)

\displaystyle\sigma_{j}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\inf\{t\geq\sigma_{j-1}\,:\,\mathrm{dist}_{T_{t}}(X_{t},y)\geq 2^{-M}\log^{M}n\mbox{ or }t-\sigma_{j-1}=\ell_{*}\}\;

(3.16)

\displaystyle I_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{1}\{\mathrm{dist}_{T_{\sigma_{j}}}(X_{\sigma_{j}},y)\geq 2^{-M}\log^{M}n\}

(b)

When
X_{\sigma_{j-1}}
is not too close to
y
:
if
\mathrm{dist}_{T_{\sigma_{j-1}}}(X_{\sigma_{j-1}},y)\geq 2^{-M}\log^{M}n
, we set

(3.17)

\displaystyle\sigma_{j}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\inf\{t\geq\sigma_{j-1}\,:\,\mathrm{dist}_{T_{t}}(X_{t},y)=\mathrm{dist}_{T_{\sigma_{j-1}}}(X_{\sigma_{j-1}},y)+1\mbox{ or }t-\sigma_{j-1}=\ell_{*}\}\;

(3.18)

\displaystyle I_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{1}\{\mathrm{dist}_{T_{t}}(X_{t},y)=\mathrm{dist}_{T_{\sigma_{j-1}}}(X_{\sigma_{j-1}},y)+1\}

We note some basic properties of these new random variables. First, we have the deterministic bound
\sigma_{j}-\sigma_{j-1}\leq\ell_{*}
. In particular,
\sigma_{j}\leq\ell_{*}\,j
for all
j
.
Second, we observe that, if we have
m+1
consecutive successes, i.e. if
I_{j_{0}}=I_{j_{0}+1}=\dots=I_{j_{0}+m}=1
for some
j_{0},m\in\mathbb{N}\backslash\{0\}
, then:

\mathrm{dist}_{T_{\sigma_{j_{0}}}}(X_{\sigma_{j_{0}}},y)\geq 2^{-M}\log^{M}n

and

\mathrm{dist}_{T_{\sigma_{j_{0}+m}}}(X_{\sigma_{j_{0}+m}},y)=\mathrm{dist}_{T_{\sigma_{j_{0}+m-1}}}(X_{\sigma_{j_{0}+m-1}},y)+1=\dots=\mathrm{dist}_{T_{\sigma_{j_{0}}}}(X_{\sigma_{j_{0}}},y)+m.

We thus arrive at the following crucial observation (recall the time lengths
t_{*},\ell_{*}
in (
3.12
)).

Observation 3.1
.

Assume that there are
t_{*}+1
consecutive ones in the sequence
(I_{j})_{j=1}^{\lfloor n/\ell_{*}\rfloor}
. Then, there exists
t\leq n
such that

\mathrm{dist}_{T_{t}}(X_{t},y)\geq t_{*}\geq\log^{M+1/2}n.

Indeed, if we have
t_{*}+1
consecutive ones in this sequence there exists
j_{0}
with
(j_{0}+t_{*})\ell_{*}\leq n
and
I_{j_{0}}=I_{j_{0}+1}=\dots=I_{j_{0}+t_{*}}=1
. What we are after is to show that the probability of
t_{*}+1
consecutive ones is very likely. The upshot is that we may apply Lemma
3.2
above to control the probability that the RW reaches distance
\log^{M+1/2}n
from
y
in
n
time steps, i.e. that
M+1/2
is admissible.

Remark 3.2
.

Note that for the Lemma to be effective we need

n/\ell_{*}\gg 1
so that there are “many indicators” to consider, and also that
n/\ell_{*}t_{*}\gg 1
. It will later become clear that (for
t_{*}
polylogarithmic) it suffices that
n/\ell_{*}\geq n^{1/4+c+o(1)}
for some
c>0
.

To apply Lemma
3.2
, we must lower bound
{\mathbb{P}}_{T_{0},x_{0},p}(I_{1}=1)
and
{\mathbb{P}}_{T_{0},x_{0},p}\left(I_{j}=1\mid I_{1},\dots,I_{j-1}\right)
. By the strong Markov property, these quantities are lower bounded by

\inf_{T,x}{\mathbb{P}}_{T,x,p}\left(I_{1}=1\right)

Crucially, conditions
(a)
and
(b)
in the definition of
\sigma_{1}
and
I_{1}
correspond precisely to the situations in the two bounds (
3.13
) and (
3.14
) (respectively). It follows that, for any
0<p\leq 1
, there exists a
n_{3}=n_{3}(p,M)
such that, for all
n\geq n_{3}
,

\inf_{T,x}{\mathbb{P}}_{T,x,p}\left(I_{1}=1\right)\geq\mu:=1-2\,\frac{(\log\log n)^{2}}{2^{-M}\log^{M}n}.

Lemma
3.2
implies that:

{\mathbb{P}}_{T_{0},x_{0},p}\left(\mbox{at least $t_{*}+1$ consecutive ones in $(I_{j})_{j=1}^{\lfloor n/\ell_{*}\rfloor}$}\right)\geq 1-(1-\mu^{t_{*}+1})^{\left\lfloor\frac{\lfloor n/\ell_{*}\rfloor}{t_{*}+1}\right\rfloor}.

What is left to show is that, for any
0<p\leq 1
, there exists
n_{4}=n_{4}(p,M)
such that

(3.19)

\forall n\geq n_{4}\,:\,(1-\mu^{t_{*}+1})^{\left\lfloor\frac{\lfloor n/\ell_{*}\rfloor}{t_{*}+1}\right\rfloor}\leq\exp(-n^{1/4}),

which would imply

\forall n\geq n_{4}\,:\,{\mathbb{P}}_{T_{0},x_{0},p}\left(\mbox{at least $t_{*}$ consecutive ones in $(I_{j})_{j=1}^{\lfloor n/\ell_{*}\rfloor}$}\right)\geq 1-\exp(-n^{1/4}).

The latter bound, which is uniform in
T_{0},x_{0}
, together with
Observation
3.1
, will prove that
M+1/2
is admissible.

Remark 3.3
.

The important thing here is that, because
t_{*}=\log^{M+1/2}n
, the probability of
t_{*}+1
consecutive ones, i.e.
\mu^{t_{*}+1}
, goes to
0
slowly. Remark
3.2
shows we are considering “polynomially many indicators”, so such not-too-small probabilities make a long run of ones very likely.

Let us show that for our choices of
t_{*}
and
\ell_{*}
we can find an
n_{4}
sufficiently large such that Equation (
3.19
) holds. We point out that we will implicitly assume
n_{4}>n_{3}
so to guarantee that

\inf_{T,x}{\mathbb{P}}_{T,x,p}\left(I_{1}=1\right)\geq\mu
.
It is for this reason that
n_{4}
will depend on
p
(in particular,
n_{4}
will be non-increasing in
p
).

(1-\mu^{t_{*}+1})^{\left\lfloor\frac{\lfloor n/\ell_{*}\rfloor}{t_{*}+1}\right\rfloor}\leq\exp\left(-\mu^{t_{*}+1}\frac{n^{1/2}}{2(\log^{M+1/2}n+1)}\right)

Since
t_{*}=\lceil\log^{M+1/2}n\rceil
, for sufficiently large
n
we have

\mu^{t_{*}+1}\geq\exp\left(-\left(2(\log\log n)^{2}2^{M+1}+o((\log\log n)^{2})\right)\sqrt{\log n}\right)\mu=n^{-o(1)}\mu\,

where we are using that
\left(1-\frac{b_{n}}{a_{n}}\right)^{a_{n}}\approx e^{-b_{n}-o(b_{n})}
for sufficiently large
n
whenever
a_{n},b_{n}\to+\infty
and
b_{n}=o(a_{n})
. Thus,

(1-\mu^{t_{*}+1})^{\left\lfloor\frac{\lfloor n/\ell_{*}\rfloor}{t_{*}+1}\right\rfloor}\leq\exp\left(-n^{1/4}\,\frac{n^{1/4-o(1)}\mu}{2(\log^{M+1/2}n+1)}\right)\leq\exp\left(-n^{1/4}\right)

for large enough
n
.

4.
The loop process, or why it’s hard to go back

Now we stop the discussion about the
BGRW
process, to introduce a simpler process which will help us to understand how long the walker
X
stays on specific subgraphs of the random trees
\{T_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. Roughly speaking, a loop process on an initial graph
G
is a random walker such that at each step adds a loop to its position according to a coin and then chooses uniformly one edge of its current position to walk on. In other words, the process is quite similar to the
BGRW
but here the walker adds loops instead of leaves, which makes it possible for it to stand still.

We will be particularly interested in the loop process over specific graphs which we define before the definition of the process itself. We call a finite graph
\mathcal{B}
a
backbone
of length
\ell
if
\mathcal{B}
is a path of length
\ell
having a loop attached to its
\ell+1
-th vertex and possibly to its other vertices, see Figure
2
.

Figure 2.
A
backbone
of length
\ell

In this section, we will abuse the graph terminology saying
degree
of a vertex even though we do not count loops twice. We then reserve the special notation
\text{deg}_{t}(i)
to denote the number of edges attached to vertex
i
at time
t
.

The model has one parameter
p\in[0,1]
which is the parameter of a sequence
\{Z_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
of
i.i.d
random variables with law
\text{Ber}(p)
. The loop process on a backbone of length
\ell
is a Markov chain
\{(\mathcal{B}_{t},X^{\text{loop}}_{t})\}_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
where
\mathcal{B}_{t}
denotes the resultant backbone at time
t
and
X^{\text{loop}}_{t}
is one of its
\ell+1
vertices. The loop process is also defined inductively according to the update rules below

(1)

Obtain
\mathcal{B}_{t+1}
from
\mathcal{B}_{t}
by adding a new loop to
X^{\text{loop}}_{t}
whether
Z_{t+1}=1
;

(2)

Choose uniformly one edge attached to
X^{\text{loop}}_{t}
in
\mathcal{B}_{t+1}
. Whether the chosen edge is a loop set
X^{\text{loop}}_{t+1}
as
X^{\text{loop}}_{t}
. Otherwise,
X^{\text{loop}}_{t+1}
becomes the
X^{\text{loop}}_{t}
neighbor.

We stress out to the index
t+1
of
\mathcal{B}
on (2). It means that we may add a new loop at (1) and then choose it at (2).

For a fixed backbone
\mathcal{B}
of length
\ell
and
i\in\{0,1,\cdots,\ell\}
we let
{\mathbb{P}}_{i}(\cdot)
denote the law of
\{(\mathcal{B}_{t},X^{\text{loop}}_{t})\}_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
when
(\mathcal{B}_{0},X^{\text{loop}}_{0})\equiv(\mathcal{B},i)
.

Once we have defined the loop process, we are interested on the time it takes to go from one end of the backbone to another. More precisely, we would like to obtain bounds on the stopping time bellow

(4.1)

\eta^{\text{loop}}_{0}:=\inf\left\{t\geq 1\middle|X^{\text{loop}}_{t}=0\right\}

when the process starts from
\ell
. The next Lemma gives us some estimates.

Lemma 4.1
.

There exist positive constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
depending on
p
only, such that, for all integer
K

{\mathbb{P}}_{\ell}\left(\eta^{\text{loop}}_{0}\leq e^{K}\right)\leq 1-\left(1-\frac{1}{2\ell}\right)^{c_{2}K}+e^{-c_{1}K}

Proof.

We need some notation and definitions. Consider the following sequence of stopping times

(4.2)

\begin{split}&\tau_{0}\equiv 0,\\
&\tau_{k}:=\inf\left\{t>\tau_{k-1}|X^{\text{loop}}_{t}\neq X^{\text{loop}}_{\tau_{k-1}}\right\}.\end{split}

Observe that the probability of
X^{\text{loop}}_{t}
leaving its current position is at least
1/t
since the degree of a vertex at time
t
is at most
t
. This implies that
\tau_{k}
is finite a.s. for all
k
. This allow us to define the process

(4.3)

Y_{k}:=X^{\text{loop}}_{\tau_{k}}.

Note that by strong Markov Property,
\{Y_{k}\}_{k}
is a simple
RW
on
\{0,1,\cdots,\ell\}
with reflecting barriers.
Regarding the process
\{Y_{k}\}_{k}
we let
\sigma
be the following stopping time

(4.4)

\sigma:=\inf\left\{k>0|Y_{k}=0\right\}.

Observe that
\eta^{\text{loop}}_{0}=\tau_{\sigma}
.

We prove the Lemma by showing that
X^{\text{loop}}
spends at least
\exp\{K\}
steps on the vertex
\ell
. More precisely, we prove that the degree of
\ell
at time
\tau_{\sigma}
is at least
\exp\{K\}
,
w.h.p.
To do this, first observe that the degree of a vertex may be written in terms of
Y_{k}
and
\Delta\tau_{k}
as follows

(4.5)

\mathrm{deg}_{\tau_{n}}(\ell)=2+\sum_{k=0}^{n}\mathbb{1}\{Y_{k}=\ell\}\left(\sum_{m=\tau_{k}}^{\tau_{k+1}-1}Z_{m}\right)=2+\sum_{k=0}^{n}\mathbb{1}\{Y_{k}=\ell\}\mathrm{Bin}\left(\Delta\tau_{k},p\right).

Also notice that if
Y_{k}=\ell
, then the number of steps
X^{\text{loop}}
spends on
\ell
is exactly
\Delta\tau_{k}
, which satisfies

\Delta\tau_{k}\geq\mathrm{Geo}\left(1/\mathrm{deg}_{\tau_{k}}(\ell)\right).

To see the bound above, consider the random variable which counts the number of steps
X^{\text{loop}}
spends on
\ell
by choosing only the loops which were already attached on
\ell
when
X^{\text{loop}}
arrived at
\ell
. This random variable is clearly smaller than
\Delta\tau_{k}
and follows a geometric distribution of parameter
1/\mathrm{deg}_{\tau_{k}}(\ell)
. Thus, we have

(4.6)

\mathrm{deg}_{\tau_{n}}(\ell)\geq\sum_{k=0}^{n}\mathbb{1}\{Y_{k}=\ell\}\mathrm{Bin}\left(\mathrm{Geo}\left(1/\mathrm{deg}_{\tau_{k}}(\ell)\right),p\right).

Regarding the random variables
\mathrm{Bin}\left(\mathrm{Geo}\left(1/\mathrm{deg}_{\tau_{k}}(\ell)\right),p\right)
, we claim that

Claim 4.1
.

For all
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, there exists a positive constant
q=q(\varepsilon,p)
such that

(4.7)

{\mathbb{P}}_{\ell}\left(Y_{k}=\ell,\mathrm{Bin}\left(\mathrm{Geo}\left(1/\mathrm{deg}_{\tau_{k}}(\ell)\right),p\right)\geq\varepsilon p\mathrm{deg}_{\tau_{k}}(\ell)\middle|\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{k}}\right)\geq q\mathbb{1}\{Y_{k}=\ell\}.

Proof of the claim:
To simplify our writing, write

G_{k}:=\mathrm{Geo}\left(1/\mathrm{deg}_{\tau_{k}}(\ell)\right);\;d_{k}:=\mathrm{deg}_{\tau_{k}}(\ell)

and let
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{\tau_{k}}
be the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
G_{k}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{k}}
. Now, by Chernoff bounds, we have that

(4.8)

\begin{split}{\mathbb{P}}_{\ell}\left(Y_{k}=\ell,\mathrm{Bin}\left(G_{k},p\right)\geq\varepsilon pd_{k},G_{k}\geq d_{k}\middle|\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{\tau_{k}}\right)&\geq\left(1-e^{-(1-\varepsilon)^{2}pG_{k}}\right)\mathbb{1}\{Y_{k}=\ell,G_{k}\geq d_{k}\}\\
&\geq\left(1-e^{-(1-\varepsilon)^{2}pd_{k}}\right)\mathbb{1}\{Y_{k}=\ell,G_{k}\geq d_{k}\}.\end{split}

Recall that
d_{k}
is greater than
2
for all
k
. So, taking the conditional expectation
wrt

\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{k}}
on the above inequality yields

(4.9)

\begin{split}{\mathbb{P}}_{\ell}\left(Y_{k}=\ell,\mathrm{Bin}\left(G_{k},p\right)\geq\varepsilon pd_{k},G_{k}\geq d_{k}\middle|\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{k}}\right)&\geq\left(1-e^{-2(1-\varepsilon)^{2}p}\right){\mathbb{P}}_{\ell}\left(Y_{k}=\ell,G_{k}\geq d_{\tau_{k}}\middle|\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{k}}\right)\\
&\geq\left(1-e^{-2(1-\varepsilon)^{2}p}\right)\left(1-e^{-1}\right)\mathbb{1}\{Y_{k}=\ell\},\end{split}

which proves the claim.

\blacksquare

The above claim tells us that conditionally on the past, every time
\{Y_{k}\}_{k}
visits
\ell
it has probability at least
q
of increasing the degree of
\ell
by a factor of at least
1+\varepsilon p
. This points out that the degree of
\ell
must be at least exponential of the number of visits
\ell
receives from
\{Y_{k}\}_{k}
. So, let
N_{\sigma}(\ell)
the number of visits made by
Y
to
\ell
before it reaches vertex
0
. Since
Y
is a simple random walk on
\{0,1,\cdots,\ell\}
,
N_{\sigma}(\ell)
follows a geometric distribution of parameter
1/2{\ell}
. Moreover, the random variable
W
that counts how many times we have successfully multiplied the degree of
\ell
by
1+\varepsilon p
may be written as follows

(4.10)

W:=\sum_{k=0}^{\sigma}\mathbb{1}\{Y_{k}=\ell\}\mathbb{1}\{\mathrm{Bin}\left(\mathrm{Geo}\left(1/\mathrm{deg}_{\tau_{k}}(\ell)\right),p\right)\geq\varepsilon p\mathrm{deg}_{\tau_{k}}(\ell)\}

and dominates a random variable distributed as
\text{Bin}(N_{\sigma}(l),q)
. Consequently, by Chernoff bounds

(4.11)

\begin{split}{\mathbb{P}}_{\ell}\left(W\leq\frac{K}{\log(1+\varepsilon p)}\right)&\leq{\mathbb{P}}_{\ell}\left(\mathrm{Bin}(N_{\sigma}(\ell),q)\leq\frac{K}{\log(1+\varepsilon p)}\right)\\
&\leq{\mathbb{P}}_{\ell}\left(\mathrm{Bin}(N_{\sigma}(\ell),q)\leq\frac{K}{\log(1+\varepsilon p)}\middle|N_{\sigma}(\ell)\geq\frac{2K}{q\log(1+\varepsilon p)}\right)\\
&+{\mathbb{P}}_{\ell}\left(N_{\sigma}(\ell)<2q^{-1}K/\log(1+\varepsilon p)\right)\\
&\leq\exp\{-c_{1}K\}+1-\left(1-\frac{1}{2\ell}\right)^{c_{2}K}.\end{split}

Finally, observe that if
W\geq K/\log(1+\varepsilon p)
, then
\mathrm{deg}_{\tau_{\sigma}}(\ell)\geq 2e^{K}
which implies that
\tau_{\sigma}
is at least this amount, finishing the proof.

The following special case of the above Lemma will be particularly useful to our proposes.

Corollary 4.1
.

There exists a positive constant
C
depending on
p
only, such that

{\mathbb{P}}_{\ell}\left(\eta^{\text{loop}}_{0}\leq e^{\sqrt{\ell}}\right)\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{\ell}}.

Proof.

Letting
K=\sqrt{\ell}
on (
4.11
) of Lemma
4.1
we obtain

(4.12)

\begin{split}{\mathbb{P}}_{\ell}\left(W\leq\frac{\sqrt{\ell}}{\log(1+\varepsilon p)}\right)&\leq{\mathbb{P}}_{\ell}\left(\text{ Bin}(N_{\sigma}(\ell),q)\leq\frac{\sqrt{\ell}}{\log(1+\varepsilon p)}\right)\\
&\leq{\mathbb{P}}_{\ell}\left(\mathrm{Bin}(N_{\sigma}(\ell),q)\leq\frac{\sqrt{\ell}}{\log(1+\varepsilon p)}\middle|N_{\sigma}(\ell)\geq\frac{2\sqrt{\ell}}{q\log(1+\varepsilon p)}\right)\\
&+{\mathbb{P}}_{\ell}\left(N_{\sigma}(\ell)\leq 2q^{-1}\sqrt{\ell}/\log(1+\varepsilon p)\right)\\
&\leq\exp\{-c_{1}\sqrt{\ell}\}+1-\left(1-\frac{1}{2\ell}\right)^{c_{2}\sqrt{\ell}}.\end{split}

Since
\left(1-\frac{1}{2\ell}\right)^{c_{2}\sqrt{\ell}}\approx e^{-c_{3}/\sqrt{\ell}}
and
1-e^{-x}\leq x
, choosing properly the constants we obtain the desired result.

4.1.
Coupling the BGRW and the loop process

In this subsection we construct a coupling of the
BGRW
and loop processes in such way that the loop process is always closer to the root than the walker
X
. For this, let
T
be a rooted locally finite tree of height at least
2(\ell+1)
,
x
a vertex such that
\mathrm{dist}_{T}(x,\text{root})\geq\ell
and
d_{T}(x)\geq 2
and
y
its ancestor at distance
\ell
. Since
T
is a tree, there exists only one path
\mathcal{P}
connecting
x
to the
y
. With this in mind, we define a graph operation
\mathcal{B}
which associates to each pair
(T,x)
and ancestor
y
satisfying the aforementioned conditions a backbone

of length

as follows:

(1)

delete all vertices of

is at least

;

(2)

replace each edge

with

So far, we have shown that the

– away from the root. Now, we would like to argue that once it has gone so far, it takes too long to return. More generally, if the

and

\eta_{y}:=\inf\left\{n\geq 1\middle|X_{n}=y\right\}.

The way we bound

from below is comparing it with

The next proposition tells us that we may couple the

Proposition 4.14

(Coupling)
.

Let
T_{0}
be a rooted locally finite tree,
x_{0}
one of its vertices different from the root and
y
an ancestor of
x_{0}
. Then, there exists a coupling of
\{(T_{n},X_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
starting from
(T_{0},x_{0})
and a loop process
\{(\mathcal{B}_{n},X^{\text{loop}}_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
starting from
(\mathcal{B}(T_{0},y,x_{0}),x_{0})
such that

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\eta_{y}\geq\eta^{\text{loop}}_{0}\right)=1.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{P}
denote the path connecting
x_{0}
to its ancestor
y
on
T_{0}
and
\ell
its length. Also consider the following sequence of stopping times,

(4.15)

\zeta_{0}\equiv 0;\quad\zeta_{k}:=\inf\left\{m>\zeta_{k-1}\middle|X_{m}\in\mathcal{P}\right\},

and let
\{W_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of
i.i.d.
r.v’s independent of the process
BGRW
, such that
W_{k}\sim\text{Ber}(p)
. We couple a loop process
\{(\mathcal{B}_{k},X^{\text{loop}}_{k})\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
to the
BGRW
inductively as follows. Start by defining

(\mathcal{B}_{0},X^{\text{loop}}_{0}):=(\mathcal{B}(T_{0},y,x_{0}),x_{0})

and assume we have defined

in such way that this random vector is distributed as
k-1
steps of a loop process starting from
(\mathcal{B}_{0},\ell)
. Now, we define
(\mathcal{B}_{k},X^{\text{loop}}_{k})

•

If
\zeta_{k-1}<\infty
, we set
\mathcal{B}_{k}=\mathcal{B}(T_{\zeta_{k-1}+1},y,x_{0})
;

•

If
\zeta_{k-1}=\infty
, we make use of our independent sequence

. We modify
\mathcal{B}_{k-1}
adding a loop to

whether

;

Observe that if

. In fact, when

. On the other hand, when

– since

.

•

If

,

–

we set

, if

\mathcal{P}
;

–

otherwise, when

\mathcal{P}

be

.

In case

on

.

at distance

5.
Positive drift and its consequences

In this section we show that the
BGRW
has a positive drift away from the root. In particular, we show that

\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\mathrm{dist}_{T_{n}}(X_{n},\text{root})}{n}>0,

almost surely, which implies the
transience
of the walker
X
. We do that by tracking the distance of
X
from the
root
at random times and comparing it to a right biased simple random walk on
\mathbb{Z}
. Furthermore, this comparison with the right biased simple random walk allows us to improve the results given in Proposition
3.2
and Corollary
4.2
. Specifically, we can now prove

•

the walker achieves distances of order
n
in
n
steps
w.h.p
;

•

the probability of the walker going back long distances is exponentially rare.

Intuitively, the main message of this section is that if we look at the distance of
X
from the root properly normalized and at some “random times” we see a random walk on the line that dominates a right biased simple random walk. Let us begin by formally define what we mean by “random times”. For a fixed positive integer
r
, we define three stopping conditions from a vertex
x_{0}
.

(1)

\mathrm{dist}_{T_{n}}(X_{n},\text{root})-\mathrm{dist}_{T_{0}}(x_{0},\text{root})=r
;

(2)

\mathrm{dist}_{T_{n}}(X_{n},\text{root})-\mathrm{dist}_{T_{0}}(x_{0},\text{root})=-r
;

(3)

X
walks
\exp\{{\sqrt{r}}\}
steps and none of the previous conditions occurs.

We say
(1)-(3)
occurred from
X_{m}
whenever one of the three stopping conditions occurred when we put
x_{0}
as
X_{m}
. We must point out that if
\mathrm{dist}_{T_{m}}(X_{m},\text{root})<r
, then stopping condition
(2)
cannot be attained.

We define our sequence of stopping times as follows:

(5.1)

\begin{split}\sigma_{0}\equiv&0;\\
\sigma_{k}:=&\inf\left\{m>\sigma^{(r)}_{k-1}\mid(1)-(3)\text{ occurs from }X_{\sigma_{k-1}}\right\}.\end{split}

From the definition of
(1)-(3)
follows that, for all
k
,
\sigma^{(r)}_{k}
is bounded from above by
\exp\{{\sqrt{r}}\}k
. To avoid clutter, when
r
is fixed we suppress the upper script from the nation of
\sigma^{(r)}_{k}
.

Lemma 5.1

(Coupling to the biased random walk)
.

Let
T_{0}
be a rooted locally finite tree,
x_{0}
one of its vertices and
\left\{S_{k}\right\}_{k\geq 0}
a right biased simple random walk on
\mathbb{Z}
. Then, there exists a large enough
r=r(p)
depending on
p
only, such that the process
\{\mathrm{dist}_{T_{\sigma_{k}}}(X_{\sigma_{k}},\text{root})/r\}_{k\geq 0}
starting from
(T_{0},x_{0})
and
\left\{S_{k}\right\}_{k\geq 0}
starting from
\lfloor\mathrm{dist}_{T_{0}}(x_{0},\text{root})/r\rfloor
can be coupled in such way that

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\mathrm{dist}_{T_{\sigma_{k}}}(X_{\sigma_{k}},\text{root})\geq rS_{k},\forall k\right)=1.

Proof.

We begin by a few notations. To simplify our writing, put
d_{k}:=\mathrm{dist}_{T_{\sigma_{k}}}(X_{\sigma_{k}},\text{root})
. Note that the process
\left\{d_{k}\right\}_{k\geq 0}
is a random walk on the half line whose increments belong to the interval
[-r,r]
. Let
\left\{U_{k}\right\}_{k\geq 0}
be a sequence of
i.i.d
random variables independent of the
BGRW
and following uniform distribution on
[0,1]
. Also let
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{k}
be the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the
BGRW
process up to time
\sigma_{k}
and let
\mathcal{H}_{k}
be the
\sigma
-algebra encoding all information of
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{k}
and all the uniform random variables up to time
k
.

Regarding the increments of
\left\{d_{k}\right\}_{k\geq 0}
, we claim

Claim 5.1
.

There exist positive constants
r
and
C
depending on
p
only, such that for all
k

(5.2)

\inf_{T_{0},x_{0}}{\mathbb{P}}_{T_{0},x_{0},p}\left(\Delta d_{k+1}=r\middle|\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{k}\right)>1-\frac{C}{\sqrt{r}}.

Proof of the claim:
First of all, observe that
\Delta d_{k+1}=r
if, and only if,
\sigma_{k+1}
stops because of condition
(1)
. Also, by the strong Markov Property, it is enough to prove the claim for
\sigma_{1}
. Said that, let us first assume we start from an initial condition
(T_{0},x_{0})
with
\mathrm{dist}_{T_{0}}(x_{0},\text{root})\geq r
. We derive the desired lower bound by proving upper bounds for the probability of the events
\{\Delta d_{1}=-r\}
and
\{|\Delta d_{1}|<r\}
. The former occurs if, and only if,
\sigma_{1}
stops because condition
(2)
and the latter because of condition
(3)
.

Observe that if
\sigma_{1}
stops because of
(2)
then the walker
X
visited the ancestor
y
of
x_{0}
at distance
r
, since
\mathrm{dist}_{T_{0}}(x_{0},\text{root})\geq r
, spending at most
\exp\{\sqrt{r}\}
steps. So, using Corollary
4.2
we have

(5.3)

{\mathbb{P}}_{T_{0},x_{0},p}\left(\sigma_{1}\textit{ stops because of }(2)\right)\leq{\mathbb{P}}_{T_{0},x_{0},p}\left(\eta_{y}\leq e^{\sqrt{r}}\right)\leq\frac{C^{\prime}}{\sqrt{r}}.

Note that the above upper bound holds for all possible pairs of a rooted locally finite tree
T_{0}
and one of its vertex
x_{0}
at distance greater than
r
from the root.

Finally, if
X
stops because of the occurrence of
(3)
then
X
has walked for
e^{\sqrt{r}}
steps and has not visited the ancestor
y
of
x_{0}
at distance
r
neither has increased its distance from
y
by
r
. This is the same that we observe a process
\tilde{X}
on the subtree
T_{y}
that in
e^{\sqrt{r}}
steps does not be at distance
2r
from the root
y
. Applying Proposition
3.2
(page
3.2
) with
n=e^{\sqrt{r}}
and
M=4
we obtain that there exist
r_{0}=r(p)
such that for all
r\geq r_{0}
we have

(5.4)

\inf_{T_{0},x_{0}}{\mathbb{P}}_{T_{0},x_{0},p}\left(\exists m\leq e^{\sqrt{r}},\mathrm{dist}_{T_{m}}(\tilde{X}_{m},\tilde{\textit{root}})>r^{2}\right)\geq 1-\exp\{-e^{\sqrt{r}/4}\}.

Choosing
r
large enough so that
r\geq r_{0}
,
\exp\{-e^{\sqrt{r}/4}\}\leq C^{\prime}/\sqrt{r}
and
r^{2}>2r
we conclude that

(5.5)

\sup_{T_{0},x_{0}}{\mathbb{P}}_{T_{0},x_{0},p}\left(\sigma_{1}\textit{ stops because of }(3)\right)\leq\exp\{-e^{\sqrt{r}/4}\}.

To drop our assumption that we start at distance greater than
r
from the root, just recall that when this is not the case the condition
(2)
has probability zero and the above upper bound for condition
(3)
still holds. This implies that

(5.6)

\inf_{T_{0},x_{0}}{\mathbb{P}}_{T_{0},x_{0},p}\left(\sigma_{1}\textit{ stops because of }(1)\right)\geq 1-\frac{C}{\sqrt{r}}

which combined with strong Markov Property proves the claim.

\blacksquare

Since we may increase
r
, we choose it large enough so

(5.7)

q:=1-\frac{C}{\sqrt{r}}>\frac{1}{2}.

Now we couple the process
\{d_{k}/r\}_{k\geq 1}
and
\{S_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
in the following way. Set

(5.8)

S_{0}:=\left\lfloor\frac{\mathrm{dist}_{T_{0}}(x_{0},\text{root})}{r}\right\rfloor

and assume we have defined
\{S_{j}\}_{j=0}^{k-1}
in such way that it has the distribution of
k-1
steps of the desired right biased random walk. Let
Q_{k-1}
denote

(5.9)

Q_{k-1}:={\mathbb{P}}_{T_{0},x_{0},p}(\Delta d_{k}=r|\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{k-1})

and recall from Claim
5.1
that
Q_{k}\geq q
for all
k
. We then define
S_{k}
in the following way

(5.10)

S_{k}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}S_{k-1}+1&\mbox{if }\Delta d_{k}=r\text{ and }U_{k}\leq q/Q_{k-1},\\
S_{k-1}-1&\mbox{otherwise. }\end{array}\right.

In words, if at time
\sigma_{k}
the walker
X
increased its distance from the root by
r
, then
S_{k}
jumps to the right with probability
q/Q_{k-1}
. In this way, whenever the process
\{d_{k}/r\}_{k\geq 0}
jumps back (at most one unit), the
SRW
also jumps back one unit.

Now, we show that the process
\{S_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
does have the distribution we desire. We start by checking that the increments are
1
with probability
q
or
-1
with probability
1-q
. By the definition of
S
we have

(5.11)

\begin{split}{\mathbb{P}}_{T_{0},x_{0},p}\left(\Delta S_{k}=1\middle|\mathcal{H}_{k-1}\right)&={\mathbb{P}}_{T_{0},x_{0},p}\left(\Delta d_{k+1}=r,U_{k}\leq q/Q_{k-1}\middle|\mathcal{H}_{k-1}\right)\\
&={\mathbb{P}}_{T_{0},x_{0},p}(\Delta d_{k}=r|\mathcal{H}_{k-1})\frac{q}{Q_{k-1}}\\
&=q,\end{split}

since
U_{k}
is independent of
\mathcal{H}_{k-1}
and
Q_{k-1}
is measurable with respect to
\mathcal{H}_{k-1}
. Moreover, the equality below holds

Q_{k-1}={\mathbb{P}}_{T_{0},x_{0},p}(\Delta d_{k}=r|\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{k-1})={\mathbb{P}}_{T_{0},x_{0},p}(\Delta d_{k}=r|\mathcal{H}_{k-1})

since
\mathcal{H}_{k-1}
is
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{k-1}
added of information independent of the whole process
\{(T_{k},X_{k})\}_{k\geq 1}
. Equation (
5.11
) allows us to derive the independence of all the increments of our right biased random walk
\{S_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
. For any fixed set of indexes
k_{1}<k_{2}<\cdots<k_{j}
and any vector
(a_{1},\cdots,a_{j})\in\{-1,1\}^{j}
we have

\begin{split}{\mathbb{P}}\left(\Delta S_{k_{1}}=a_{1},\cdots,\Delta S_{k_{j}}=a_{j}\right)&={\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathbb{1}_{\{\Delta S_{k_{1}}=a_{1}\}}\cdots\mathbb{1}_{\{\Delta S_{k_{j-1}}=a_{j-1}\}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathbb{1}_{\{\Delta S_{k_{j}}=a_{j}\}}\middle|\mathcal{H}_{k_{j}-1}\right]\right]\\
&={\mathbb{P}}\left(\Delta S_{k_{1}}=a_{1},\cdots,\Delta S_{k_{j-1}}=a_{j-1}\right){\mathbb{P}}\left(\Delta S_{k_{j}}=a_{j}\right)\end{split}

which implies the independence of the increments
\Delta S_{k}
. So,
\{S_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
is distributed as simple random walk on
\mathbb{Z}
with probability
q>1/2
of jumping to the right starting at the left of
d_{0}/r
. And, by construction, we have that
d_{k}/r\geq S_{k}
for all
k
. This concludes the proof of the lemma.

As a consequence of the coupling above, we prove the transience of the walker.

Proposition 5.12

(Transience of the walker)
.

There exists a constant
c>0
depending on
p
only, such that

\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\mathrm{dist}_{T_{n}}(X_{n},\mathrm{root})}{n}\geq c,\;{\mathbb{P}}_{T_{0},x_{0},p}\textit{- almost surely},

for all initial conditions
(T_{0},x_{0})
.

Proof.

Lemma
5.1
guarantees the existence of a positive constant
r
, depending on
p
only, so that, for any initial condition on the
BGRW
,

(5.13)

\mathrm{dist}_{T_{\sigma_{k}}}(X_{\sigma_{k}},\text{root})\geq rS_{k},\textit{a.s.,}

where
\{S_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
is a right biased simple random walk on
\mathbb{Z}
. By the Strong Law of Large Numbers we also have that

(5.14)

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\frac{S_{k}}{k}=\mu(r)=\mu\textit{, a.s..}

This implies that

(5.15)

\liminf_{k\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\mathrm{dist}_{T_{\sigma_{k}}}(X_{\sigma_{k}},\text{root})}{k}\geq r\mu\textit{, a.s..}

On the other hand, by the definition of the stopping times
\sigma_{k}
we have that, for all
k
, the following inequality holds

|\sigma_{k+1}-\sigma_{k}|\leq\exp\{\sqrt{r}\},

almost surely, as well as

\left|\mathrm{dist}_{T_{\sigma_{k}}}\left(X_{\sigma_{k}},\text{root}\right)-\mathrm{dist}_{T_{\sigma_{k-1}}}\left(X_{\sigma_{k-1}},\text{root}\right)\right|\leq r,

almost surely. Thus, if
n
is an integer such that
n\in[\sigma_{k},\sigma_{k+1}]
we have

(5.16)

\frac{\mathrm{dist}_{T_{n}}\left(X_{n},\text{root}\right)}{n}\geq\frac{\mathrm{dist}_{T_{\sigma_{k+1}}}\left(X_{\sigma_{k+1}},\text{root}\right)-r}{e^{\sqrt{r}}(k+1)}

which combined with (
5.15
) proves the that

(5.17)

\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\mathrm{dist}_{T_{n}}(X_{n},\mathrm{root})}{n}=c>0,\textit{a.s},

for some positive constant
c
depending on
p
only.

5.1.
Controlling returns and degrees

The coupling gives us a picture of the dynamics of the walker: it is moving away from the root at linear speed. Before moving on, we use the coupling to show that vertices are not visited many times and (as a result) degrees in our tree tend to be small.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
(T_{0},x_{0})
be the initial state of the dynamics and
y\in T_{0}
be given. Then for any
0<p\leq 1
, the number
v(y)
of visits to
y
,

v(y):=|\{0\leq t<+\infty\,:\,X_{t}=y\}|

satisfies

\forall k\geq 0\,:\,{\mathbb{P}}_{T_{0},x_{0},p}(v(y)\geq k)\leq C\,e^{-\beta\,k}

and

{\mathbb{P}}_{T_{0},x_{0},p}(v(y)>0)\leq C\,e^{-\beta\,{\rm dist}_{T_{0}}(x_{0},y)}

for some
\beta,C>0
depend only on
p
. Moreover, if
p_{0}>0
and
p_{0}\leq p\leq 1
,
C
and
\alpha
can be chosen uniformly in
p_{0}
.

Proof.

Recall from the coupling we just constructed that we may choose
r
large enough so that the process
\{\mathrm{dist}_{T_{\sigma_{k}}}(X_{\sigma_{k}},y)/r\}_{k}
dominates a right biased random walk
\{S_{k}\}_{k}
on
\mathbb{Z}
starting on
s_{0}:=\lfloor\frac{\mathrm{dist}_{T_{0}}(x_{0},y)}{r}\rfloor
. We count the visits to
y
per time interval
(\sigma_{k-1},\sigma_{k}]
(with a possible additional visit at time
0
).

v(y):=\mathbb{1}_{\{X_{0}=y\}}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{t=\sigma_{k-1}+1}^{\sigma_{k}}\mathbb{1}_{\{X_{t}=y\}}.

Recalling that
\sigma_{k}-\sigma_{k-1}\leq e^{\sqrt{r}}
, we may rewrite the expression as:

v(y)\leq\mathbb{1}_{X_{0}=y}+e^{\sqrt{r}}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{1}_{\{X_{t}=y\text{ for some }\sigma_{k-1}<t\leq\sigma_{k}\}}.

Now, by the definition of the stopping time sequence
\{\sigma_{k+1}\}_{k}
, for
t\in[\sigma_{k},\sigma_{k+1}]
then

|\mathrm{dist}_{T_{\sigma_{k}}}(X_{\sigma_{k}},y)-\mathrm{dist}_{T_{t}}(X_{t},y)|\leq r.

As a result, if
X_{t}=y
for some
\sigma_{k-1}\leq t\leq\sigma_{k}
, then
S_{k}\leq\mathrm{dist}_{T_{\sigma_{k}}}(X_{\sigma_{k}},y)/r\leq 1
. In particular,

v(y)\leq\mathbb{1}_{\{X_{0}=y\}}+e^{\sqrt{r}}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{1}_{\{S_{k}\leq 1\}}.

The RHS is (up to a constant) the number of visits of a right-biased random walk started from
S_{0}\geq 0
to the interval
(-\infty,1]
. This number has an exponential tail that only depends on the bias. The first inequality follows because we can choose
r=r(p_{0})
to guarantee a bias of
1/3
(say) for all
p_{0}\leq p\leq 1
. The second inequality also follows (perhaps with changes to
C,\beta
) once we realize that
S_{0}\geq\mathrm{dist}_{T_{0}}(x_{0},y)/r
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Given
p_{0}>0
, there exist constants
C,\alpha>0
depending only on
p_{0}
such that, for all
p_{0}\leq p\leq 1
, all finite
(T_{0},x_{0})
, and all
n,k\geq 1
,

\sum_{t=0}^{n}{\mathbb{P}}_{T_{0},x_{0},p}(d_{T_{t}}(X_{t})\geq k)\leq C\,(n+|V(T_{0})|)\,e^{\alpha\,(\Delta(T_{0})-k)}.

Proof.

Assume without loss of generality that
V(T)=\{1,\dots,\ell\}
. Also let
{\ell+1},{\ell+2},\dots
be the vertices that the BGRW process started from
T,x
creates. Finally, we let
v_{t}(i)
denote the number of visits to
i
up to time
t
, so that
v_{t}(i)=0
if
i>t+\ell
. Note that
d_{t}(v_{i})\geq k
implies that
v_{n}(i)\geq k-\Delta(T)
, as the degree only grows at times when
i
is visited. Therefore, for all
0\leq t\leq n
,
\alpha>0
and
k\geq\ell+1
:

\begin{split}{\mathbb{P}}_{T,o,p}(d_{T_{t}}(X_{t})\geq k)=&\sum_{i=1}^{\ell+n}{\mathbb{P}}_{T,o,p}(X_{t}=i,d_{T_{t}}(i)\geq k)\\
&\leq e^{\alpha\,(\Delta(T)-k)}\,\sum_{i=1}^{\ell+n}{\mathbb{E}}_{T,o,p}[\mathbb{1}_{\{X_{t}=i\}}\,\exp(\alpha\,v_{n}(i))].\end{split}

As a consequence:

\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{n}{\mathbb{P}}_{T,o,p}(d_{T_{t}}(X_{t})\geq k)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle e^{\alpha\,(\Delta(T)-k)}\,\sum_{t=0}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell+n}{\mathbb{E}}_{T,o,p}[\mathbb{1}_{\{X_{t}=i\}}\,\exp(\alpha\,v_{n}(i))]

(use
\sum_{t\leq n}\mathbb{1}_{\{X_{t}=i\}}=V_{n}(i)
)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle e^{\alpha\,(\Delta(T)-k)}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell+n}{\mathbb{E}}_{T,o,p}[v_{n}(i)\,\exp(\alpha\,v_{n}(i))].

By Lemma
5.2
,
v_{n}(i)\leq v(i)
has an exponential tail uniformly in
i,n,T_{0},x_{0}
and
p\in[p_{0},1]
. Thus we may take
\alpha=\beta/2
(with
\beta
from that Lemma) and adjust
C
to obtain our result.

6.
The local point of view on infinite trees

In the last section we obtained that the distance between the random walker and its starting position grows at least like
n
. Obtaining sharper results will require a deeper understanding of the process. This section makes some progress in this direction by introducing the right state space for this purpose.

6.1.
Rooted graphs and trees

We recall the definition of rooted graphs, rooted trees and the local topology on these objects. All notions we need are defined and studied in Bordenave’s lecture notes
[
4
]
.

A
rooted graph
is a pair
(G,o)
where
G
is a connected, locally finite graph and
o
is vertex of
G
. Note that any rooted graph must have a countable vertex set which we may assume to be a subset of
\mathbb{N}
or
\mathbb{Z}
. Two rooted graphs
(G,o)
and
(G^{\prime},o^{\prime})
are rooted-isomorphic – denoted by
(G,o)\simeq(G,o^{\prime})
– if there is a bijection of the vertex sets of
G
and
G^{\prime}
that maps
o
to
o^{\prime}
and preserves edges. We let
[G,o]
denote the
equivalence class
of
(G,o)
under rooted isomorphisms, and let
\mathcal{G}_{*}
denote the set of all such
[G,o]
.

Given
r\in\mathbb{N}
and a rooted graph
(G,o)
,
(G,o)_{r}
denotes the graph obtained by retaining only the vertices of
G
within graph-theoretic distance
r
from
o
and the edges between those vertices. Clearly,
(G,o)\simeq(G^{\prime},o^{\prime})
implies
(G,o)_{r}\simeq(G^{\prime}o^{\prime})_{r}
for all
r>0
, and we may define
[G,o]_{r}
as the equivalence class of
(G,o)_{r}
. The
distance
between
[G,o],[G^{\prime},o^{\prime}]\in\mathcal{G}_{*}
is defined by:

\rho([G,o],[G^{\prime},o^{\prime}]):=\frac{1}{1+\sup\{r\in\mathbb{N}\,:\,[G,o]_{r}=[G^{\prime},o^{\prime}]_{r}\}}.

One can show that
(\mathcal{G}_{*},\rho)
is a Polish metric space.

The set
\mathcal{T}_{*}\subset\mathcal{G}_{*}
of rooted trees is the set of equivalence classes
[T,o]
where
T
is a locally finite tree. This is a closed subset of
\mathcal{G}_{*}
and is therefore a Polish space with the metric
\rho
.

Our
BGRW
dynamics (see, Section
2.2
) may be naturally extended to the set
\mathcal{T}_{*}
of random rooted trees. The idea is that the state
[T_{t},X_{t}]
describes the tree “rooted at the position of the walker”. With some abuse of notation, we use
K_{p}
to denote the Markov transition kernel of our process over this space as well.

6.2.
Empirical measures and local functions

Much of the remainder of the paper will be spent dealing with the tree as viewed by the walker. More precisely, we will study the
empirical measure of the tree around the walker
.

Definition 6.1
.

Given a realization
[T_{t},X_{t}]
of the process
K_{p}
, we let
\widehat{P}_{n}
denote the empirical measure, that is, the random probability measure over
\mathcal{T}_{*}
given by:

\widehat{P}_{n}=\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{t=0}^{n}\delta_{[T_{t},X_{t}]}.

Thus for a given element
[T,v]\in\mathcal{T}_{*}
and
r\in\mathbb{N}
,

(n+1)\widehat{P}_{n}([G,o]\in\mathcal{T}_{*}\,:\,[G,o]_{r}=[T,v]_{r})

counts the number of times
0\leq t\leq n
at which the ball of radius
r
around
X_{t}
in
T_{t}
is isomorphic to
[T,v]_{r}
.

In this section we show that, under suitable assumptions on
[T_{0},x_{0}]
, the walker has a well-defined positive speed and in Section
7
that
\widehat{P}_{n}\Rightarrow P_{p}
almost surely, where
P_{p}
is an invariant measure for the process
K_{p}
. In the proof of such results,
local functions
will play an important role.

Definition 6.2

(Local function)
.

A function
\psi:\mathcal{T}_{*}\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be
r
-local if
\psi([T,o])=\psi([T,o]_{r})
for all
[T,o]\in\mathcal{T}_{*}
. A function
\psi
is local if it is
r
-local for some
r\in\mathbb{N}
.

To avoid clutter, with a slight abuse of notation, we will write
\psi(G,o)
instead of
\psi([G,o])
.

The main result of this section is about the convergence of empirical averages of
bounded local functions
on the space
\mathcal{T}_{*}
along trajectories. In order to do that we restrict ourselves on measures on
\mathcal{T}_{*}
which have some nice properties. To define this class of measures we first define certain stopping times. Given
\ell\in\mathbb{N}\backslash\{0\}
, let
Q_{\ell}
be the path of length
\ell
, i.e. the graph with vertex set
\{0,1,\dots,\ell\}
and edges
\{(i-1),i\}
(
1\leq i\leq\ell
). We define
\tau_{\ell}
as the first time when the graph around
X_{t}
in
T_{t}
is
Q_{\ell}
.

\tau_{\ell}:=\inf\{t\geq 0\,:\,[T_{t},X_{t}]_{\ell}=[Q_{\ell},\ell]\}.

Definition 6.3
.

A measure
\mu
over pairs
(T,x)
is called
p
-escapable if
\tau_{\ell}<+\infty

{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu,p}
-almost surely for every
\ell\geq 1
.

We can now state the main theorem of this section.

Theorem 6.1
.

For each parameter
0<p\leq 1
and each bounded integer
r\geq 1
and all
r
-local function
\psi:\mathcal{T}_{*}\to\mathbb{R}
, there exists a constant
\mathcal{M}_{p}\psi
such that

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\widehat{P}_{n}\psi=\mathcal{M}_{p}\psi\mbox{ }\mathbb{P}_{\mu,p}\mbox{-a.s.},

for all
p
-escapable distribution
\mu
on
\mathcal{T}_{*}
.

As we will see in Section
7
, this result implies that “the tree seen by the walker” converges to an infinite random tree when
n\to+\infty
.

The proof of the theorem relies on the following results.
The first Lemma shows that the empirical measures of processes started from two
p
-escapable measures are asymptotically the same, i.e. the initial distribution is “forgotten”. The second Lemma shows that the empirical measures of local functions when our process starts from specific
p
-escapable measures, corresponding to
\delta_{[T_{0},x_{0}]}
with
T_{0}
finite, converges to a constant.

Lemma 6.1

(Forgetfulness of empirical measures; proof in §
6.5
)
.

Let
\mu
,
\nu
be two
p
-escapable measures. Let
r
be a positive integer and
\psi:\mathcal{T}_{*}\to\mathbb{R}
be a bounded
r
-local function. If there exists a constant
\mathcal{M}_{p}\psi
such that
\widehat{P}_{n}\psi\to\mathcal{M}_{p}\psi

{\mathbb{P}}_{\nu,p}
-almost-surely, then we also have
\widehat{P}_{n}\psi\to\mathcal{M}_{p}\psi

{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu,p}
-almost-surely.

The above statement allows us to restrict ourselves to subclasses of initial distributions and then extend the results to the whole class of
p
-escapable distributions. This procedure greatly reduces the amount of work in our proofs. The next lemma is a finite version of Theorem
6.1
.

Lemma 6.2

(proof in §
6.6
)
.

Consider an initial condition
(T_{0},x_{0})
with
T_{0}
finite. Fix
0<p\leq 1
and a bounded
r
-local function
\psi:\mathcal{T}_{*}\to\mathbb{R}
(with
r
a positive integer). Then there exists a constant
\mathcal{M}_{p}\psi
such that
\widehat{P}_{n}\psi\to\mathcal{M}_{p}\psi,\mbox{ }\mathbb{P}_{T_{0},x_{0},p}
-a.s.

Combining the two lemmas gives us a straightforward proof of Theorem
6.1
.

Proof of Theorem
6.1
.

Let
[T_{0},x_{0}]
be a finite rooted tree and consider
\nu=\delta_{[T_{0},x_{0}]}
. By Lemma
6.2
, we have that
\widehat{P}_{n}\psi\to\mathcal{M}_{p}\psi,\mbox{ }\mathbb{P}_{\nu,p}
-a.s.
But, by Lemma
5.3
,
\nu
is
p
-escapable. Therefore, by Lemma
6.1
,
\widehat{P}_{n}\psi\to\mathcal{M}_{p}\psi,\mbox{ }\mathbb{P}_{\mu,p}
-a.s.

Now we know how the main theorem follows from our lemmas we organize the remainder of this section as follows. In the next subsection we prove Theorem
1.1
as an application of Theorem
6.1
. Then, in Subsection
6.4
, we discuss the concept of
p
-escapable trees and give quantitative criteria for escapabality. Finally, in Subsections
6.5
and
6.6
, we prove lemmas
6.1
and
6.2
, respectively.

6.3.
Existence of the speed

In this section we prove a stronger version of Theorem
1.1
as an application of Theorem
6.1
. We prove that the random walker in the
BGRW
model has a well-defined positive speed for any
p
-escapable initial distribution on
\mathcal{T}_{*}
.

Theorem 6.2

(Linear speed of the walker)
.

For each
0<p\leq 1
, there exists a constant
0<c(p)\leq p
such that, for any
p
-escapable distribution
\mu
on
\mathcal{T}_{*}
,

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\mathrm{dist}_{T_{n}}(X_{n},\mathrm{root})}{n}=c(p)\;{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu,p}\textit{-a.s.}.

Proof.

Our strategy is to prove that the distance from the root at time
n
may be written as a sum of a bounded term, a martingale, and a sum of a
1
-local function computed along the trajectory. The result will then follow from Theorem
6.1
.

Fix a
p
-escapable measure
\mu
and define the following bounded
1
-local function:

\psi(T,x):=\frac{p(d_{T}(x)-1)}{d_{T}(x)+1}+\frac{(1-p)(d_{T}(x)-2)}{d_{T}(x)}.

Note that:

(6.1)

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{\mu,p}\left[\Delta\mathrm{dist}_{T_{n}}(X_{n},\mathrm{root})\mid\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]&=\psi(T_{n},X_{n})+(1-\psi(T_{n},X_{n}))\,\mathbb{1}_{\{X_{n}=\mathrm{root}\}}.\end{split}

Thus

(6.2)

\mathrm{dist}_{T_{n+1}}(X_{n+1},\mathrm{root})=M_{n+1}+\sum_{j=0}^{n}\psi(T_{j},X_{j})+C_{n+1},

where
\{M_{j}\}_{j}
is a martingale with bounded increments and

C_{n+1}:=\sum_{j=0}^{n}(1-\psi(T_{n},X_{n}))\,\mathbb{1}_{\{X_{n}=\mathrm{root}\}}

is bounded by the total number of visits to the root, which is a.s. finite because our process is transient.

Since the martingale has bounded increments, Azuma’s inequality implies that
M_{n}/n\to 0
almost surely. Since
C_{n}
is a.s. bounded,
C_{n}/n\to 0
almost surely as well. Theorem
6.1
gives us that

(6.3)

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\frac{\mathrm{dist}_{T_{n+1}}(X_{n+1},\mathrm{root})}{n+1}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{j=0}^{n}\psi(T_{j},X_{j})=c(p):=\mathcal{M}_{p}(\psi),\;{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu,p}\textit{-a.s},

with
\mathcal{M}_{p}(\psi)
is a constant depending on
p
and
\psi
only (but not on
\mu
). Proposition
5.12
implies
c(p)>0
.

6.4.
Escapable trees and mixing

The concept of escapable trees is closely related to the forgetfulness of the walker. Roughly speaking, once the walker has escaped it may never come back to its initial tree. In this section we prove a quantitative criteria for checking “escapability”. Let us notice that not all trees are
p
-escapable. The next example gives a useful example to keep in mind.

Example 6.1

(A hard tree)
.

Consider an infinite rooted tree
T_{\rm hard}
, with a root node
x_{\rm hard}
, such that each node at distance
h
from the root has
g(h)>0
children, with
1/g(h)
summable. Consider the BGRW from
(T_{\rm hard},x_{\rm hard})
. One can show via the Borel-Cantelli Lemma that there is a positive probability that
{\rm dist}_{T_{t}}(X_{t},x_{\rm hard})=t
for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
(i.e. the walker simply “walks down the tree” for eternity). For the same reason, for any
\ell\in\mathbb{N}\backslash\{0\}
there is a positive probability that
\tau_{\ell}=+\infty
.

The next Lemma (proven subsequently) explains one reason why
p
-escapability is important to us. The Lemma gives a quantitative criterion for “escapability”. In a way, it says that what makes the tree in Example
6.1
non-escapable is that
X_{t}
sees very large degrees along the way.

Lemma 6.3

(Quantitative escapability)
.

Assume
\mu
is a starting measure for which there exist
C,D,\alpha>0
such that, for all
n,k\geq 1
,

(6.4)

\sum_{t=0}^{n}{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu,p}(d_{T_{t}}(X_{t})\geq k)\leq C\,(n+D)\,e^{-\alpha\,k}.

Then
\mu
is
p
-escapable and

(6.5)

{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu,p}[\tau_{\ell}\wedge n]\leq\frac{\ell 2^{\ell-1}}{p^{\ell}}\left\lceil\frac{2\log(n+D)+\log C}{\alpha}\right\rceil+1.

Moreover, the following event holds with probability
\geq 1-1/n^{16}
: for all times
1\leq t\leq n
,

(6.6)

{\mathbb{E}}_{T_{t},X_{t},p}[\tau_{\ell}\wedge n]\leq\frac{\ell 2^{\ell-1}}{p^{\ell}}\left\lceil\frac{\log(2n+D)+17\log n+\log C}{\alpha}\right\rceil+1.

In particular, all measures
\mu=\delta_{(T,x)}
, with
T
a finite tree, satisfy the above inequalities, with
C\leq e^{\alpha\,\Delta(T)}
by virtue of Lemma
5.3
.

So Lemma
6.3
also guarantees that not only
\mu
is
p
-escapable, but also that, with high probability, each intermediate
[T_{t},X_{t}]
satisfies a quantitative escapability property. One important point is that the assumptions of Lemma
6.3
always apply to initial trees that are finite, by virtue of Lemma
5.3
.

Proof.

We first prove inequality (
6.5
) for
{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu,p}[\tau_{\ell}\wedge n]
, noting that it implies
p
-escapability because, assuming it, we can show:

{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu,p}(\tau_{\ell}=+\infty)\leq\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu,p}[\tau_{\ell}\wedge n]}{n}\leq\frac{1}{n}\left(\frac{\ell 2^{\ell-1}}{p^{\ell}}\left\lceil\frac{2\log(n+D)+\log C}{\alpha}\right\rceil+1\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n\to+\infty}}{{\to}}0,

Fix some
\Delta>0
. Let
\eta_{\Delta}
be the first time the walker “sees” a node with degree
\geq\Delta
,

(6.7)

\eta_{\Delta}:=\inf\left\{m\geq 0\;\middle|\;d_{T_{m}}(X_{m})\geq\Delta\right\}

and consider the event
A_{i}
in which at time
i\ell
the walker creates a new leaf, jumps to it and grows a path of length
\ell-1
from it in such way that at time
i\ell+\ell-1
the walker finds itself at the tip of a path of length
\ell
. Now we define another stopping time

(6.8)

\tilde{\tau_{\ell}}:=\inf\left\{i\geq 0\;\middle|\;A_{i}\text{ occurs }\right\}

and note that
\tau_{\ell}\leq\ell\tilde{\tau_{\ell}}
.

By the definition of the event
A_{i}
, we have that

{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu,p}\left(A_{i}\middle|\mathcal{F}_{i\ell}\right)=\frac{p}{d_{T_{i\ell}}(X_{i\ell})}\left(\frac{p}{2}\right)^{\ell-1}.

Thus, by the definition of the stopping time
\eta_{\Delta}
, we also have

(6.9)

\mathbb{1}\{\eta_{\Delta}>i\ell\}{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu,p}\left(A_{i}\middle|\mathcal{F}_{i\ell}\right)\geq\frac{p}{\Delta}\left(\frac{p}{2}\right)^{\ell-1}\mathbb{1}\{\eta_{\Delta}>i\ell\}.

Using the above inequality and noticing that
\mathbb{1}\{\eta_{\Delta}>k\ell\}\leq\mathbb{1}\{\eta_{\Delta}>(k-1)\ell\}
, we obtain

(6.10)

\begin{split}{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu,p}\left(\tilde{\tau_{\ell}}\geq k,\eta_{\Delta}>k\ell\right)&={\mathbb{E}}_{\mu,p}\left[{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu,p}\left[\mathbb{1}_{A^{c}_{k}}\middle|\mathcal{F}_{k\ell}\right]\mathbb{1}\{\eta_{\Delta}>k\ell\}\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}\mathbb{1}_{A^{c}_{i}}\right]\\
&\leq\left(1-\frac{p}{\Delta}\left(\frac{p}{2}\right)^{\ell-1}\right){\mathbb{P}}_{\mu,p}\left(\tilde{\tau_{\ell}}\geq k-1,\eta_{\Delta}>(k-1)\ell\right).\end{split}

Then, proceeding by induction, we obtain the following upper bound

(6.11)

{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu,p}\left(\tilde{\tau_{\ell}}\geq k,\eta_{\Delta}>k\ell\right)\leq\left(1-\frac{p}{\Delta}\left(\frac{p}{2}\right)^{\ell-1}\right)^{k}.

Since

{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu,p}\left(\tau_{\ell}\wedge n\geq k\ell\right)\leq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu,p}\left(\tilde{\tau_{\ell}}\geq k\right),&k\ell\leq n\\
0,&\mbox{otherwise};\end{array}\right.

we have:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu,p}\left(\tau_{\ell}\wedge n\geq k\ell\right)\leq{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu,p}\left(\tilde{\tau_{\ell}}\geq k,\eta_{\Delta}>k\ell\right)+{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu,p}\left(\eta_{\Delta}\leq k\ell\right),

and consequently,

(6.12)

{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu,p}\left[\tau_{\ell}\wedge n\right]\leq\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor\frac{n}{\ell}\rfloor}\ell{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu,p}\left(\tau_{\ell}\wedge n\geq j\,r\right)\leq\frac{\ell\,2^{\ell-1}}{p^{\ell}}\Delta+n\,{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu,p}\left(\eta_{\Delta}\leq n\right).

But, by our condition (
7.2
),

(6.13)

{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu,p}\left(\eta_{\Delta}\leq n\right)\leq\sum_{t=0}^{n}{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu,p}(d_{T_{t}}(X_{t})\geq\Delta)\leq C\,(n+1)\,e^{-\alpha\,\Delta}.

so

{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu,p}\left[\tau_{\ell}\wedge n\right]\leq\frac{\ell 2^{\ell-1}}{p^{\ell}}\Delta+C\,(n+1)^{2}e^{-\alpha\,\Delta}.

We may choose

\Delta=\left\lceil\frac{2\log(n+1)+\log C}{\alpha}\right\rceil

to finish the proof of (
6.5
).

To prove the last statement in the Theorem, we go back to (
6.13
) and note that was the only step in that proof where we used (
6.4
). In particular, we can go back to (
6.12
) and obtain:

{\mathbb{E}}_{T_{t},X_{t},p}\left[\tau_{\ell}\wedge n\right]\leq\frac{\ell 2^{\ell-1}}{p^{\ell}}\Delta+n\,{\mathbb{P}}_{T_{t},X_{t},p}(\eta_{\Delta}\leq n).

In particular, if
\Delta
is given and we consider the event

\mathcal{G}(\Delta):=\bigcap_{t=0}^{n}\{{\mathbb{P}}_{T_{t},X_{t},p}(\eta_{\Delta}\leq n)\leq 1/n)\},

we have that, when
\mathcal{G}(\Delta)
holds, then:

(6.14)

{\mathbb{E}}_{T_{t},X_{t},p}[\tau_{\ell}\wedge n]\leq\frac{\ell 2^{\ell-1}}{p^{\ell}}\Delta+1.

for each
0\leq t\leq n
.
To finish, we show that
{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu,p}(\mathcal{G}(\Delta))\geq 1-n^{-16}
for an appropriate choice of
\Delta
. Indeed, by union bound and Markov’s inequality:

1-{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu,p}(\mathcal{G}(\Delta))\leq n\sum_{t=0}^{n}{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu,p}[{\mathbb{P}}_{T_{t},X_{t},p}(\eta_{\Delta}\leq n)].

Now by the Simple Markov property, for
0\leq t\leq n

{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu,p}[{\mathbb{P}}_{T_{t},X_{t},p}(\eta_{\Delta}\leq n)]={\mathbb{P}}_{\mu,p}(\exists\;t\leq s\leq t+n\,:\,d_{T_{s}}(X_{s})\geq\Delta)\leq{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu,p}(\eta_{\Delta}\leq 2n).

And again, by condition (
6.4
),

1-{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu,p}(\mathcal{G})\leq n\,{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu,p}(\eta_{\Delta}\leq 2n)\leq n\,\sum_{t=0}^{2n}{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu,p}(d_{t}(X_{t})\geq\Delta)\leq C\,n\,(2n+D)\,e^{-\alpha\,\Delta}.

Taking

\Delta:=\left\lceil\frac{\log(2n+D)+17\log n+\log C}{\alpha}\right\rceil

guarantees
{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu,p}(\mathcal{G}(\Delta))\geq 1-n^{-16}
. Plugging this choice of
\Delta
into (
6.14
) gives (
6.6
) inside the event
\mathcal{G}(\Delta)
.

6.5.
Forgetfulness of empirical measures: proof of Lemma
6.1

The proof of the lemma will follow as a consequence of another result that highlight the relation between “escapabability” and forgetfulness. As we will see, it implies an approximated renewal property of the
BGRW
process in the sense that after a random time it may be coupled to a
BGRW
process starting from a semi-infinite path. Then, both walkers see around them the same tree structure as long as this coupling works.

For this purpose, we will need additional notation. Let
Q^{*}
be a semi-infinite path, i.e.,

(6.15)

Q^{*}=\{v_{0},v_{1},v_{2},\dots\}

rooted at
v_{0}
and consider a trajectory
(T^{*}_{t},X_{t}^{*})_{t\geq 0}
of
BGRW
started from
(Q^{*},v_{0})
with empirical measures
\widehat{P}^{*}_{n}
. Also, for each pair of integers
r
and
n
, let
\pi(r,n)
be

(6.16)

\pi(r,n):={\mathbb{P}}_{Q^{*}_{0},v_{0},p}\left(\exists m\leq n,\;X^{*}_{m}=x_{r}\right).

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
\psi:\mathcal{T}_{*}\to\mathbb{R}
be a
r
-local and bounded and
\mu
be a
p
-escapable distribution. Consider a trajectory
(T_{t},X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
of BGRW starting from
\mu
, with empirical measures
\widehat{P}_{n}
. Then,

(a)

for every
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, there exist a coupling of the processes
(T_{t},X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and
(T^{*}_{t},X^{*}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
such that:

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\lim_{n\to+\infty}|(\widehat{P}_{n}-\widehat{P}^{*}_{n})\psi|=0\right)\geq 1-\varepsilon.

(b)

\left|{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu,p}\left[\widehat{P}_{n}\psi\right]-{\mathbb{E}}_{Q^{*},v_{0},p}\left[\widehat{P}^{*}_{n}\psi\right]\right|\leq 2\|\psi\|_{\infty}\pi(r,n)+\frac{2\|\psi\|_{\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu,p}[\tau_{2r}\wedge n]}{n+1}.

Proof.

Part (a).

Take an
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
bigger than
2r
. Consider a chain started from measure
\mu
. Since
\mu
is
p
-escapable, the stopping time
\tau_{\ell}
is finite
{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu,p}
-almost-surely.

Let
y_{0}=X_{\tau_{\ell}}
,
y_{1}
,
\dots
,
y_{\ell}
be the unique vertices at distances
0,1,2,\dots,\ell
at time
\tau_{\ell}
. Define a random time
\tau^{\prime}_{\ell,r}
as follows:

\tau^{\prime}_{\ell,r}:=\inf\{t\geq 0\,:\,X_{\tau_{\ell}+t}=y_{\ell-r}\}.

Notice that this time may well be infinite. In fact, since
{\rm dist}(X_{\tau_{\ell}},y_{\ell-r})=\ell-r
, the second statement in Lemma
5.2
, combined with the strong Markov property and the
p
-escapability of
\mu
, implies.

{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu,p}\left(\tau^{\prime}_{\ell,r}<+\infty\right)\leq C\,e^{-\beta\,(\ell-r)}.

Note that
\tau_{\ell}
is the time
X_{t}
is at the tip of a path of length
\ell
and
\tau_{\ell}+\tau^{\prime}_{\ell,r}
is the first time at which
X_{t}
comes within distance

of the other end of that path.

Now consider the process
(T^{*}_{t},X^{*}_{t})
started on vertex

of the infinite path

. Let

This time may also be infinite. It is easy to see that one can couple

to
(T_{\tau_{\ell}+t},X_{\tau_{\ell}+t})
so that
\tau_{\ell,r}=\tau^{\prime}_{\ell,r}
and
[T^{*}_{t},X^{*}_{t}]_{r}=[T_{\tau_{\ell}+t},X_{\tau_{\ell}+t}]_{r}
for all
0\leq t\leq\tau^{\prime}_{\ell,r}
. Under this coupling, if
\tau^{\prime}_{\ell,r}>n
and
\tau_{\ell}\leq n
, then

\displaystyle\widehat{P}_{n}\psi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{i=0}^{n}\psi(T_{i},X_{i})

(6.17)

(use coupling and
r
-locality)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{i=0}^{\tau_{\ell}\wedge n-1}\psi(T_{i},X_{i})+\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{t=0}^{n-\tau_{\ell}\wedge n}\psi(T^{*}_{t},X^{*}_{t})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widehat{P}^{*}_{n}\psi+\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{i=0}^{\tau_{\ell}\wedge n-1}(\psi(T_{i},X_{i})-\psi(T_{n-\tau_{\ell}\wedge n+i+1},X_{n-\tau_{\ell}\wedge n+i+1})).

Since
\tau_{\ell}<+\infty
is bounded and
\widehat{P}_{n}\psi\leq\|\psi\|_{\infty}
, this implies that, under the coupling:

\tau^{\prime}_{\ell,r}>n\Rightarrow|\widehat{P}_{n}\psi-\widehat{P}^{*}_{n}\psi|\leq\frac{2\|\psi\|_{\infty}\,(\tau_{\ell}\wedge n)}{n+1}\to 0.

So, for any fixed
\ell
, we have

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\lim_{n\to+\infty}|(\widehat{P}_{n}-\widehat{P}^{*}_{n})\psi|=0\right)\geq{\mathbb{P}}(\tau^{\prime}_{\ell,r}=+\infty)\geq 1-C\,e^{-\beta\,(\ell-r)}.

In particular, this can be made greater than
1-\varepsilon
with an appropriate choice of
\ell
. This proves part
(a)
.

Part (b).
Put
\ell=2r
. From Equation (
6.17
) and the fact that under the coupling
\tau^{\prime}_{\ell,r}=\tau^{*}_{\ell,r}
, we may obtain that

(6.18)

\begin{split}\left|\widehat{P}_{n}\psi-\widehat{P}^{*}_{n}\psi\right|&=\left|\widehat{P}_{n}\psi-\widehat{P}^{*}_{n}\psi\right|\mathbb{1}\{\tau^{\prime}_{\ell,r}\leq n\}+\left|\widehat{P}_{n}\psi-\widehat{P}^{*}_{n}\psi\right|\mathbb{1}\{\tau^{\prime}_{\ell,r}>n\}\\
&\leq 2\|\psi\|_{\infty}\mathbb{1}\{\tau^{*}_{\ell,r}\leq n\}+\frac{2\|\psi\|_{\infty}\,(\tau_{\ell}\wedge n)}{n+1}\end{split}

taking the expected value both sides it is enough to proves part (b) and finally proves the lemma.

Now we see how Lemma
6.1
follows immediately from the above result.

Proof of Lemma
6.1
.

From part (a) of Lemma
6.4
, one sees that, given a deterministic

This holds for

-escapable measure

is
also

\widehat{P}_{n}\psi\to c

-a.s., the same must hold with

Convergence of average of local functions: proof of Lemma

The proof of Lemma

Lemma 6.5
.

Let

. Obseve that, for every

\begin{split}\widehat{P}_{kq-1}\psi=\frac{1}{kq}\sum_{i=0}^{kq-1}\psi(T_{i},X_{i})&=\frac{1}{k}\left(\frac{1}{q}\sum_{i=0}^{q-1}\psi(T_{i},X_{i})+\cdots+\frac{1}{q}\sum_{i=kq-q}^{kq-1}\psi(T_{i},X_{i})\right)\\

where,
S_{j}:=\displaystyle\frac{1}{q}\sum_{i=(j-1)q}^{jq-1}\psi(T_{i},X_{i})
, for
j\in\{1,\cdots,k\}

Let us denote by

\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{0}=\mathcal{F}_{0};\;\;\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{j}=\mathcal{F}_{jq-1}.

By the definition of

\{S_{j}\}_{j\geq 1}
is adapted to the filtration
\{\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{j}\}_{j\geq 0}

M_{k}:=\sum_{j=1}^{k}S_{j}-{\mathbb{E}}_{T_{0},x_{0},p}\left[S_{j}\middle|\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{j-1}\right]=M_{k-1}+S_{k}-{\mathbb{E}}_{T_{0},x_{0},p}\left[S_{k}\middle|\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{k-1}\right]

2

With all these definitions, by triangle inequality, we have that

(6.24)

\begin{split}\left|\widehat{P}_{n}\psi-{\mathbb{E}}_{Q^{*},v_{0},p}\left[\widehat{P}_{q}\psi\right]\right|&\leq\left|\widehat{P}_{n}\psi-\widehat{P}_{kq-1}\psi\right|\quad(a)\\
&+\left|\widehat{P}_{kq-1}(\psi)-\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}{\mathbb{E}}_{T_{0},x_{0},p}\left[S_{j}\middle|\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{j-1}\right]\right|\quad(b)\\

As far as
(a)

\begin{split}\left|\widehat{P}_{n}\psi-\widehat{P}_{kq-1}\psi\right|&=\left|\left(\frac{kq}{n+1}-1\right)\frac{1}{kq}\sum_{i=0}^{kq-1}\psi(T_{i},X_{i})+\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{i=kq}^{kq+s}\psi(T_{i},X_{i})\right|\\

since, by hypothesis,
\|\psi\|_{\infty}\leq 1

(6.25)

Using the simple observation that

{\mathbb{P}}_{T_{0},x_{0},p}\Big((b)+(c)\geq\varepsilon+err(q,r)\Big)\leq{\mathbb{P}}_{T_{0},x_{0},p}\left((b)\geq\frac{\varepsilon+err(q,r)}{2}\right)+{\mathbb{P}}_{T_{0},x_{0},p}\left((c)\geq\frac{\varepsilon+err(q,r)}{2}\right)\;,

\displaystyle(1):\qquad{\mathbb{P}}_{T_{0},x_{0},p}\left((b)\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)\leq 2\exp\{-\varepsilon^{2}k/32\}\,

To show

(6.26)

To show

{\mathbb{E}}_{T_{0},x_{0},p}\left[S_{j}\middle|\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{j-1}\right]=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{i=1}^{q}{\mathbb{E}}_{T_{(j-1)q-1},X_{(j-1)q-1},p}\left[\psi(T_{i},X_{i})\right]={\mathbb{E}}_{T_{(j-1)q-1},X_{(j-1)q-1},p}\left[\widehat{P}_{q}(\psi)\right].

Then, by Part (b) of Lemma

(6.29)

\begin{split}\left|{\mathbb{E}}_{T_{0},x_{0},p}\left[S_{j}\middle|\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{j-1}\right]-{\mathbb{E}}_{Q^{*},v_{0},p}\left[\widehat{P}_{q}(\psi)\right]\right|&\leq 2\pi(r,q)+\frac{2{\mathbb{E}}_{T_{(j-1)q-1},X_{(j-1)q-1},p}\left[\tau_{2r}\wedge q\right]}{q}\,,\end{split}

and implies

(c)\leq 2\pi(r,q)+\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{2{\mathbb{E}}_{T_{(j-1)q-1},X_{(j-1)q-1},p}\left[\tau_{2r}\wedge q\right]}{q}\;.

Therefore, it is enough to show that the following events

(6.30)

B:=\left\{\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\frac{2{\mathbb{E}}_{T_{jq-1},X_{jq-1},p}\left[\tau_{2r}\wedge q\right]}{k\,q}>\frac{r2^{2r}}{q\,p^{2r}}\left\lceil\frac{\log(2q+|V(T_{0})|)+17\log q+\alpha\Delta(T_{0})\log C}{\alpha}\right\rceil+\frac{1}{q}\right\}

which implies

7.
Weak convergence of empirical measures

In this section we prove Theorem
1.2
and Theorem
1.3
in the Introduction. In fact, we prove slightly stronger statements that only require the initial measure to be
p
-escapable, which in particular applies to all initial measures supported on finite trees (cf. Lemma
6.3
).
Specifically, we will show that, for any
p
-escapable initial measure
\mu
, there exits a measure
P_{p}
on the space
\mathcal{T}_{*}
such that, when
n\to+\infty
,

\displaystyle\widehat{P}_{n}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{t=0}^{n}\delta_{[T_{t},X_{t}]}\Rightarrow P_{p}\,\quad\mbox{ ${\mathbb{P}}_{\mu,p}$-a.s.}

\displaystyle P_{n}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{t=0}^{n}\,\mu K_{p}^{t}\Rightarrow P_{p}\,

where
K_{p}
denotes the Markov transition kernel of the BGRW process over
\mathcal{T}_{*}
.

In our proof we use the next proposition, which gives a convenient criterion for checking weak convergence of distributions over
\mathcal{T}_{*}
. Before stating the proposition we need to define a specific kind of local function.
Given
r\in\mathbb{N}
and
[T,o]\in\mathcal{T}_{*}
, let
D_{r}([T,o])
denote the largest degree of a vertex in
T
at distance
r
from

D_{r}(T,o):=\max\{d_{T}(x)\,:\,x\in V(T),\,{\rm dist}_{T}(x,o)\leq r\}.

One can check that
D_{r}:\mathcal{T}_{*}\to\mathbb{R}
is
(r+1)
-local.

Proposition 7.1

(Proof in Appendix
B.2
)
.

Suppose
\{Q_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of probability measures over
\mathcal{T}_{*}
. Then:

(1)

Tightness: If
\lim_{k\to+\infty}\limsup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}Q_{n}(\{[T,o]\in\mathcal{T}_{*}\,:\,D_{r}(T,o)\geq k\})=0
for any
r\geq 0
, then
\{Q_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is tight.

(2)

Weak convergence: Assume
\{Q_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies item (1). Then there exists a countable family
\mathcal{S}
of bounded local functions such that, if
\lim_{n}Q_{n}f
exists for all
f\in\mathcal{S}
, then
Q_{n}\Rightarrow Q
for some
Q
that is uniquely defined by:

Qf=\lim_{n}Q_{n}f,\,f\in\mathcal{S}.

We begin with addressing tightness for
\{P_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. The next Lemma shows Cèsaro-mean-type sequences of measures generated by our Markov chain are tight when the initial measure satisfy the quantitative criterion of
p
-escapability defined in Lemma

.

Lemma 7.1
.

Assume
\mu
is a measure over
\mathcal{T}_{*}
with the following property: there exist
C,\alpha,D>0
such that for all
n,k\geq 1
:

(7.2)

\sum_{t=0}^{n}{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu,p}(d_{T_{t}}(X_{t})\geq k)\leq C\,(n+D)\,e^{-\alpha\,k}.

Then the sequence

P_{n}:=\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{t=0}^{n}\mu K_{p}^{t}

7.1

P_{n}(\{[G,o]\in\mathcal{T}_{*}\,:\,D_{r}(G,o)\geq k\})\leq C\,\frac{(n+D+r)}{n+1}\,\left(\frac{(k+1)^{r+1}-1}{k}\right)\,e^{-\alpha\,k}.

Proof.

Note that:

(n+1)P_{n}(\{[G,o]\in\mathcal{T}_{*}\,:\,D_{r}(G,o)\geq k\})=\sum_{t=0}^{n}{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu,p}(D_{r}([T_{t},X_{t}])\geq k).

So it suffices to prove the following quantitative estimate: for all
r,n,k\geq 1
,

(7.3)

\mbox{\bf Goal: }\sum_{t=0}^{n}{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu,p}(D_{r}(T_{t},X_{t})\geq k)\leq C\,(n+D+r)\,\left(\frac{(k+1)^{r+1}-1}{k}\right)\,e^{-\alpha\,k},

which is true for
r=0
by our assumption (
7.2

Consider
r>0
and assume that we have proven that, for each
j\leq r-1
, there exists
C_{j}>0
depending only on
j
and
p
, and an exponent
\alpha
as in the base case, such that

\mbox{\bf(induction hyp.) }\forall m\in\mathbb{N}\,:\,\sum_{t=0}^{m}\,{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu,p}(D_{j}(T_{t},X_{t})\geq k)\leq C_{j}\,(m+D+j)\,e^{-\alpha\,k},

for all
k\geq 1
(again, this is true for

). We

that a similar statement holds for

with
C_{r}=C_{0}+kC_{r-1}
. A simple induction would then imply our goal:

C_{r}=C_{0}\,\sum_{i=0}^{r}(k+1)^{i}=\frac{(k+1)^{r+1}-1}{k}\,C.

To obtain our bound, note that

\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{n}\,{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu,p}(D_{r}(T_{t},X_{t})\geq k)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{n}\,{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu,p}(d_{T_{t}}(X_{t})\geq k)

\displaystyle+\sum_{t=0}^{n}\,{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu,p}(D_{r}(T_{t},X_{t})\geq k,d_{T_{t}}(X_{t})\leq k).

The first term in the RHS is
\leq C_{0}\,(n+D)\,e^{-\alpha\,k}
by the base case. For the second term, we observe that
D_{r}(T_{t},X_{t})\geq k
– i.e. some vertex at distance
r
from
X_{t}
has degree
\geq k
– means
D_{r-1}(T_{t},v)\geq k
for one of the neighbors of
X_{t}
in
T_{t}
. Now, if
d_{T_{t}}(X_{t})\leq k
, there is a chance of at least
1/(k+1)
that
X_{t+1}=v
and thus
D_{r-1}(T_{t+1},X_{t+1})\geq k
. We conclude:

\frac{{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu,p}(D_{r}(T_{t},X_{t})\geq k,d_{T_{t}}(X_{t})\leq k)}{k+1}\leq{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu,p}(D_{r-1}(T_{t+1},X_{t+1})\geq k).

So:

\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{n}\,{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu,p}(D_{r}(T_{t},X_{t})\geq k)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{0}\,(n+D)\,e^{-\alpha\,k}

\displaystyle+(k+1)\,\sum_{t=0}^{n}\,{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu,p}(D_{r-1}(T_{t+1},X_{t+1})\geq k)

(induction hyp. for
j=r-1,m=n+1
)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{0}\,(n+D)\,e^{-\alpha\,k}

\displaystyle+(k+1)C_{r-1}\,(n+r+D)\,e^{-\alpha k}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{r}\,(n+r+D)\,e^{-\alpha\,k},

where
C_{r}:=C+(k+1)\,C_{r-1}
as desired.

In the next result – a strengthening of Theorem
1.2
–, we prove convergence of empirical measures
\widehat{P}_{n}
and Cesàro-style convergence of the chain
K_{p}
from any initial measure that is
p
-escapable. We also characterize the limiting probability measure of the chain as a stationary distribution for
K_{p}
.

Theorem 7.1

(Convergence of the empirical measure; proof in §
7.1
)
.

Given
p\in(0,1]
, there exists a probability measure
P_{p}
on the space
\mathcal{T}_{*}
of rooted trees such that, for any
p
-escapable initial measure
\mu
,

\widehat{P}_{n}\Rightarrow P_{p}\;\mbox{ ${\mathbb{P}}_{\mu,p}$-a.s. \hskip 10.22217ptand \hskip 10.22217pt}\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{t=0}^{n}\,\mu K_{p}^{t}\Rightarrow P_{p}\;\mbox{ when $n\to+\infty$}.

For
0<p_{0}\leq p\leq 1
, the measure

satisfies:

(7.4)

\forall k,r\geq 1\,:\,P_{p}(\{[T,o]\in\mathcal{T}\,:\,D_{r}([T,o])\geq k\})\leq C\,\frac{(k+1)^{r+1}-1}{k}\,e^{\alpha\,(2-k)}

where

. Moreover,

is an invariant measure for

.

Note that the measure
P_{p}
itself is
p
-escapable, by Lemma
6.3
combined with (

). That is,
P_{p}
is the unique
p
-escapable invariant measure for
K_{p}
. On the other hand, the hard tree in Example

shows that one does not have convergence to

from all initial measures.

Our next result extends Theorem
1.3
from the introduction.

Theorem 7.2

(Support of

; §

)
.

Given

, let

be the limit measure in Theorem

. Then

is supported on one-ended infinite trees. Moreover, given a finite rooted tree
(S_{0},x_{0})
of diameter
\leq h
,

P_{p}(\{[T,o]\in\mathcal{T}_{*}\,:\,[T,o]_{h}=[S_{0},x_{0}])\})>0.

In particular, this shows that, if
\mu
is such that
\widehat{P}_{n}\Rightarrow P_{p}

{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu,p}
-a.s., then
{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu,p}(\tau_{\ell}<+\infty)=1
i.e.

is

.

7.1.
Weak convergence of empirical measures: proof of Theorem
7.1

We present here the proof of convergence to the stationary measure.

Proof of Theorem
7.1
.

Fix
\mu
and
p
. Theorem
6.1
guarantees that

(7.5)

{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu,p}(\widehat{P}_{n}\psi\to\mathcal{M}_{p}\psi)=1

for all bounded local functions, where
\mathcal{M}_{p}\psi
does not depend on
\mu
. If we write:

P_{n}:=\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{t=0}^{n}\,\mu K_{p}^{t},

we also have

(7.6)

P_{n}\psi={\mathbb{E}}_{\mu,p}[\widehat{P}_{n}\psi]\to\mathcal{M}_{p}\psi

by the Bounded Convergence Theorem.

We claim that the sequences
\{P_{n}\}_{n}
and
\{\widehat{P}_{n}\}_{n}
are
tight
(almost surely, in the second case). More specifically, we will apply the criterion in Proposition
7.1
, part
(1)
. In fact, fixing
k,r\in\mathbb{N}
, we may take

\phi_{k,r}(T,x):=\mathbb{1}_{\{D_{r}(T,x)\geq k\}}\,\,([T,x]\in\mathcal{T}_{*})

which is a
(r+1)
-local function, so that

P_{n}\phi_{k,r}\to\mathcal{M}_{p}\phi_{k,r},\;\text{ and }\;\widehat{P}_{n}\phi_{k,r}\to\mathcal{M}_{p}\phi_{k,r}\,\;\;\mbox{ ${\mathbb{P}}_{\mu,p}$-a.s.}

Now, to evaluate the limit in the RHS, we replace the initial measure
\mu
with a measure
\mu_{0}
supported on a finite tree with two vertices. Applying Lemma
7.1
in conjunction with Lemma
5.3
, and letting
n\to+\infty
, we obtain:

\mathcal{M}_{p}\phi_{k,r}\leq C\left(\frac{(k+1)^{r+1}-1}{k}\right)\,e^{\alpha\,(2-k)}.

Since the RHS of this inequality goes to
0
as
k\to+\infty
, we obtain the required tightness.

We now prove that the weak convergence criterion of Proposition
7.1
(part
(2)
) is also satisfied, which asks for the convergence of expectations of all bounded local functions. For
P_{n}
this is immediate from (
7.6
). For
\widehat{P}_{n}
there is the slight issue that we have:

\forall\mbox{ bounded local }\psi\,:\,{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu,p}(\widehat{P}_{n}\psi\to\mathcal{M}_{p}(\psi))=1

and we now need to “move the quantifier
\forall
inside the probability”. However, Proposition
7.1
shows that we only need to worry about a countable family of
\psi
, and there is no problem in moving the quantifier inside for a countable family.

The upshot is that, assuming this claim, we have that Proposition
7.1
assures that
P_{n}
and
\widehat{P}_{n}
converge weakly (almost surely, in the second case) to the
same
probability measure
P_{p}
, which is uniquely characterized by the fact that
P_{p}\psi=\mathcal{M}_{p}\psi
. In particular, this measure satisfies the property that:

\forall r,k\geq 1\,:\,P_{p}(\{[T,o]\,:\,D_{r}(T,o)\geq k)\leq C\left(\frac{(k+1)^{r+1}-1}{k}\right)\,e^{\alpha\,(2-k)}.

Finally, we show that
P_{p}
is an invariant measure for
K_{p}
.
To this aim, we need the following fact assuring that if
\psi
is bounded Lipschitz, so is
K_{p}(\psi)
.

Fact 7.1
.

Given a bounded measurable function
\psi:\mathcal{T}_{*}\to\mathbb{R}
, let

K_{p}(\psi)([T,o]):=\int_{\mathcal{T}_{*}}\,\psi([T^{\prime},o^{\prime}])\,K_{p}([T,o],{\rm d}[T^{\prime},o^{\prime}]).

If
\psi
is bounded and Lipschitz, so is
K_{p}(\psi)
.

Proof sketch.

This follows from the fact that, if
[T,o]
and
[S,v]
satisfy
\rho([T,o],[S,v])\leq 1/(1+r)
for some
r>0
, one can couple
[T^{\prime},o^{\prime}]\sim K([T,o],\cdot)
and
[S^{\prime},v^{\prime}]\sim K([S,v],\cdot)
so that
\rho([T^{\prime},o^{\prime}],[S^{\prime},v^{\prime}])\leq 1/r
.

So we may apply
P_{p}
to the function
K_{p}(\psi)
and obtain:

P_{p}K_{p}(\psi)=\lim_{n\to+\infty}P_{n}K_{p}(\psi)=\lim_{n\to+\infty}\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{t=0}^{n}\mu K_{p}^{t+1}(\psi)=\lim_{n\to+\infty}\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{t=1}^{n+1}\mu K_{p}^{t}(\psi)=P_{p}(\psi).

Since
\psi
is an arbitrary bounded Lipschitz function, this implies
P_{p}K_{p}=P_{p}
. So
P_{p}
is a stationary measure for the chain
K_{p}
.

7.2.
On the support of the limiting distribution: proof of Theorem
7.2

Proof of Theorem
7.2
.

That
P_{p}
is supported on infinite trees is immediate. We prove next the final statement in the theorem, namely:

P_{p}(\{[T,o]\in\mathcal{T}_{*}\,:\,[T,o]_{h}=[S,x_{0}])\})>0.

To see this, recall that
(S,x_{0})
has height
h
. Let
x_{1}
be a leaf node of
S
at distance
h
from
x_{0}
. One may traverse the tree
S
by first walking
h
steps from
x_{1}
to
x_{0}
and then doing a depth-first search on
S
from
x_{0}
, which will end back at
x_{0}
.

This traversal gives us a recipe for “creating”
S
inside
T_{t_{0}}
, for a certain
t_{0}
. Namely, all we need is that:

(1)

The walk creates leaves and jumps to them for
h
consecutive steps (this corresponds to moving from
x_{1}
to
x_{0}

(2)

The walk then follows the sequence of steps given by the DFS in
S
, adding leaves as needed.

The above sequence of steps has positive probability and, if followed, ensures
[T_{t_{0}},X_{t_{0}}]_{h}=[S,x_{0}]
. By stationarity of the process

P_{p}(\{[T,o]\in\mathcal{T}_{*}\,:\,[T,o]_{h}=[S,x_{0}])\})={\mathbb{P}}_{P_{p},p}([T_{t_{0}},X_{t_{0}}]_{h}=[S,x_{0}])>0.

We now prove one-endedness. Denote by
O(s)
the set of all rooted trees
[T,o]
that satisfy the following two properties:

(1)

T
is infinite (but locally finite, as all other trees in
\mathcal{T}_{*}
);

(2)

there exists a
h\geq s
such that all paths of length
\geq h
starting from
o
pass through a single vertex at distance
s
from
o
.

One may check that the set of one-ended trees is precisely
\cap_{s\in\mathbb{N}}O(s)
. Therefore, it is enough to show that
P_{p}(O(s))=1
for all
s\in\mathbb{N}
.

So fix
s\in\mathbb{N}
and take a trajectory
[T_{t},X_{t}]
of BGRW started from
P_{p}
. Since
P_{p}
is stationary,

\forall n\in\mathbb{N}\,:\,{\mathbb{P}}_{P_{p},p}([T_{n},X_{n}]\in O(s))=P_{p}(O(s)).

We will show that, for all
\varepsilon>0
, one can choose
n\in\mathbb{N}
so that the LHS above is
\geq 1-\varepsilon
, which suffices to finish the proof.

Recall that
P_{p}
is
p
-escapable, as we commented after the statement of Theorem
7.1
. This means that
\tau_{\ell}<+\infty
almost surely for all
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
. Take
\ell\geq s
. We will follow the reasoning in the proof Lemma
6.1
– more specifically the Claim at the end of the proof – and argue as follows. Let
X_{\tau_{\ell}}=v_{0},v_{1},\dots,v_{\ell}
is the (induced) path of length
\ell
around
X_{\tau_{\ell}}
at time
\tau_{\ell}
. Define

\tau^{\prime}_{\ell,s}:=\inf\{t\geq 0\,:\,X_{\tau_{\ell}+t}=v_{\ell-s}\}.

Notice that if
\tau_{\ell}\leq n<\tau_{\ell}+\tau^{\prime}_{\ell,s}
, then
[T_{n},X_{n}]\in O(s)
almost surely. Indeed,
\tau_{\ell}\leq n<\tau_{\ell}+\tau^{\prime}_{\ell,s}
has the following consequences:

(1)

X_{n}
and
v_{\ell}
lie on “opposite sides” of
v_{\ell-s}
, so
{\rm dist}_{T_{n}}(X_{n},v_{\ell})\geq{\rm dist}_{T_{n}}(v_{\ell-s},v_{\ell})=s
.

(2)

The subtree consisting of
v_{\ell-s}
and all nodes on the same side as
X_{n}
is finite, as it consists of
v_{i}
for
0\leq i\leq\ell-s
plus the new nodes added between times
\tau_{\ell}
and
n
.

(3)

The subtree consisting of
v_{\ell-s}
and all nodes on the same side as
v_{\ell}
is a.s. infinite, as it contains the tree
T_{0}
at time
0
.

Combining these properties, we see that all long enough paths in
T_{n}
that start from
X_{n}
must pass through
v_{\ell}
, which lies at distance
\geq s
from
X_{n}
. This implies
[T_{n},X_{n}]\in O(s)
, since these same paths must pass through the unique vertex at distance
s
from
X_{n}
that lies on the path from
X_{n}
to
v_{\ell}
. We have thus shown that if
\tau_{\ell}\leq n<\tau_{\ell}+\tau^{\prime}_{\ell,s}
, then
[T_{n},X_{n}]\in O(s)
, as claimed.

We deduce:

{\mathbb{P}}_{P_{p},p}([T_{n},X_{n}]\in O(s))\geq{\mathbb{P}}_{P_{p},p}(\tau_{\ell}\leq n<\tau_{\ell}+\tau^{\prime}_{\ell,s}).

Since

{\mathbb{P}}_{P_{p},p}(\tau_{\ell}\leq n<\tau_{\ell}+\tau^{\prime}_{\ell,s})\geq 1-{\mathbb{P}}_{P_{p},p}(\tau_{\ell}>n)-{\mathbb{P}}_{P_{p},p}(\tau^{\prime}_{\ell,s}<+\infty),

we may observe as in the proof of Lemma
6.1
that

{\mathbb{P}}_{P_{p},p}(\tau^{\prime}_{\ell,s}<+\infty)\leq Ce^{-\beta\,(\ell-s)}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}

if
\ell
is large enough. Since
P_{p}
is
p
-escapable, we may ensure that
{\mathbb{P}}_{P_{p},p}(\tau_{\ell}>n)\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}
by taking
n
large enough (in terms of
\ell
). So for some choice of
\ell,n
we obtain the desired inequality:

{\mathbb{P}}_{P_{p},p}(\tau_{\ell}\leq n<\tau_{\ell}+\tau^{\prime}_{\ell,s})\geq 1-\varepsilon.

8.
Final comments

Our analysis leaves open many problems about the BGRW process and its variants. We briefly discuss five of these.

Problem 8.1
.

Is the speed
c(p)
given by Theorem
1.1
a monotone function of
p
?

It is possible to show using our techniques that
p\mapsto c(p)
is continuous. Monotonicity is much less clear, though our (limited) simulations suggest it should hold.

Problem 8.2
.

Give bounds on
c(p)
as a function of
p
.

For small
p
, one can extract from our proof a lower bound of the form
c(p)\geq\exp(-Cp^{-C})
for some universal
C>0
. The best upper bound we know is
c(p)\leq p
, which comes from noting that, if
T_{0}
is finite, then
T_{n}
has
(1+o(1))\,pn
vertices a.s..

Problem 8.3
.

Prove more properties of the random measure
P_{p}
.

All we know about this measure is what is contained in Theorem
7.2
.

Problem 8.4
.

Consider a model where
p=p_{n}
decreases with
n
, and find a threshold function for transience.

That is, how fast can
p_{n}
decay while maintaining transience? Clearly, our arguments break down in this regime.

Problem 8.5
.

Consider models with cycles.

A “cheap” way to create cycles would be to connect the current vertex to some uniformly random vertex at distance
K
, with
K
a random variable. If
K
has light tails, it should still be possible to use the local topology to study the structure of our model.

Appendix B
Some facts on weak convergence and the local topology

B.1.
General facts

In this section,
(M,d)
is a Polish metric space and
{\rm Prob}(M,d)
is the set of all probability measures over the Borel
\sigma
-field over
M
. We need criteria to ascertain the weak convergence of a sequence
\{P_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset{\rm Prob}(M,d)
.

B.1.1.
Criterion for tightness

Proposition B.1
.

Suppose we are given a doubly-indexed family

F^{(r)}_{k}\,:\,r\in\mathbb{N},\,k\in\mathbb{N}

of closed subsets of
M
with the following two properties.

(1)

For each
r\in\mathbb{N}
, the sets
F_{k}^{(r)}
are increasing and satisfy

F_{k}^{(r)}\nearrow M\mbox{ as }k\to+\infty.

(2)

For all choices of
k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},\dots\in\mathbb{N}
, the set

K(\{k_{r}\}_{r=1}^{+\infty}):=\bigcap_{r=1}^{+\infty}\,F_{k_{r}}^{(r)}

is compact.

Assume
\{P_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset{\rm Prob}(M,d)
is such that

\forall r\in\mathbb{N}\,:\,\lim_{k\to+\infty}\liminf_{n\in\mathbb{N}}P_{n}(F^{(r)}_{k})=1.

Then
\{P_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is tight.

Proof.

Given
\epsilon>0
, we will argue that there exists a choice of sequence
\{k_{r}\}_{r=1}^{+\infty}
for which

\mbox{\bf Goal: }\forall n\in\mathbb{N}\,:\,P_{n}(K(\{k_{r}\}_{r=1}^{+\infty}))\geq 1-\varepsilon.

Notice that, no matter what sequence we choose, we get:

\forall n\in\mathbb{N}\,:\,1-P_{n}(K(\{k_{r}\}_{r=1}^{+\infty}))=P_{n}\left(\bigcup_{r=1}^{+\infty}\,(F^{(r)}_{k_{r}})^{c}\right)\leq\sum_{r=1}^{+\infty}P_{n}((F^{(r)}_{k_{r}})^{c}).

So it suffices to show that for each
r\in\mathbb{N}
we can choose
k_{r}\in\mathbb{N}
with
\sup_{n}P_{n}((K^{(r)}_{k_{r}})^{c})\leq\epsilon/2^{r}
.

To do this, fix some
r
. By our assumption that
\lim_{k\to+\infty}\liminf_{n\in\mathbb{N}}P_{n}(F^{(r)}_{k})=1
, we can find
k^{*}_{r}\in\mathbb{N}
,
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\forall n\geq n_{0}\,:\,P_{n}(F^{(r)}_{k^{*}_{r}})\geq 1-\epsilon/2^{r}.

On the other hand, for
n<n_{0}
we still have:

P_{n}(F^{(r)}_{k})\nearrow 1\mbox{ as }k\to+\infty\mbox{ due to assumption 1}.

Since there are only finitely many
n<n_{0}
, we can find
k^{**}_{r}
such that

\forall n<n_{0}\,:\,P_{n}(F^{(r)}_{k^{**}_{r}})\geq 1-\frac{\epsilon}{2^{r}}.

We may now choose
k_{r}:=\max\{k^{*}_{r},k^{**}_{r}\}
. Since the sets
F^{(r)}_{k}
are nested, we see at once that

\forall n\in\mathbb{N}\,:\,P_{n}(F^{(r)}_{k_{r}})\geq 1-\frac{\epsilon}{2^{r}},

which is the property we needed.

B.1.2.
Criterion for convergence

Proposition B.2
.

Assume
\{P_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a tight sequence of probability measures, with a sequence of compact sets
\{K_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
attesting tightness in the sense that.

\lim_{k}\{\liminf_{n}P_{n}(K_{k})\}=1.

Then there exists a countable family
\mathcal{A}
of bounded Lipschitz functions from
M
to
\mathbb{R}
, which depends only on
\{K_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, such that, if
\lim_{n}P_{n}f
exists for all
f\in\mathcal{A}
, then
P_{n}\Rightarrow P
for some probability measure
P
. In that case,
P
is uniquely characterized by the fact that
Pf=\lim_{n}P_{n}f
for all
f\in\mathcal{A}
.

Proof.

It is known that a tight sequence
\{P_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges weakly if and only
\lim_{n}P_{n}f
exists for each function in the set

\mathcal{A}_{0}:=\{f:M\to\mathbb{R}\,:\,\|f\|_{\infty}\leq 1\mbox{ and }\|f\|_{\rm Lip}\leq 1\}.

In that case,
Pf
is uniquely defined by the fact that
Pf=\lim_{n}P_{n}f
for all
f\in\mathcal{A}_{0}
.

Now consider a positive rational
\epsilon>0
. The fact that
\{P_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is tight implies that there exists a compact set
K_{k}\subset M
, with
k=k(\epsilon)
, so that with
P_{n}(K_{\epsilon})\geq 1-\epsilon
for all large enough
n
. For each such set, the functions
\mathcal{A}_{0}
restricted to
K_{k(\epsilon)}
form a bounded equicontinuous family. Thus the Ascoli-Arzèla Theorem implies that there exists a countable subset
A_{\epsilon}\subset\mathcal{A}_{0}
with

(B.3)

\forall\delta>0\,\forall f\in\mathcal{A}_{0}\,\exists f_{\epsilon,\delta}\in A_{\epsilon}\,:\,\sup_{x\in{k(\epsilon)}}|f(x)-f_{\epsilon,\delta}(x)|\leq\delta.

We
claim

\mathcal{A}:=\bigcup_{\epsilon\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}}A_{\epsilon}

is the set we are looking for. For assume that
\lim_{n}P_{n}f
exists for each
f\in\mathcal{A}
. For each
f\in\mathcal{A}_{0}
, one can choose
f_{\epsilon,\delta}\in\mathcal{A}
as in (
B.3
) and obtain:

|P_{n}(f-f_{\epsilon,\delta})|\leq|P_{n}\mathbb{1}_{K_{k(\epsilon)}}(f-f_{\epsilon,\delta})|+P_{n}(K_{k(\epsilon)}^{c})\leq\delta+\epsilon\mbox{ for large enough $n$}.

With this, one can show that
\{P_{n}f\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is Cauchy. Indeed,

\limsup_{m,n\to+\infty}|(P_{n}-P_{m})\,f|\leq 2(\epsilon+\delta)+\limsup_{m,n\to+\infty}|(P_{n}-P_{m})\,f_{\epsilon,\delta}|=2(\epsilon+\delta)

since we are assuming
\{P_{n}f_{\epsilon,\delta}\}_{n}
is Cauchy. Letting
\epsilon,\delta\to 0
, we obtain that
\{P_{n}f\}_{n}
is Cauchy, as desired. A similar reasoning shows that:

\lim_{n}P_{n}f=\lim_{\epsilon,\delta\to 0}\lim_{n}P_{n}f_{\epsilon,\delta}

exists for all
f\in\mathcal{A}_{0}
. So
P_{n}\Rightarrow P
. Passing to limits, we obtain from the above estimates that

|P(f-f_{\epsilon,\delta})|\leq\epsilon+\delta.

In particular,
P
is uniquely defined by the values of
Pf=\lim_{n}P_{n}f
for
f\in\mathcal{A}_{0}
, which are uniquely specified by the numbers
Pf=\lim_{n}P_{n}f
for
f\in\mathcal{A}
.

B.2.
Application to local topology over rooted graphs

The general theory of the previous section will now be applied to the space
\mathcal{T}_{*}
of rooted, locally finite trees.

Proof of Proposition
7.1
.

We apply the criteria for tightness and convergence in Propositions
B.1
and
B.2
. For each
r,k\in\mathbb{N}
the set:

F^{(r)}_{k}:=\{[T,o]\in\mathcal{T}_{*}\,:\,D_{r}([T,o])\leq k\}

is closed and
\cup_{k}F^{(r)}_{k}=\mathcal{T}_{*}
for each fixed
r
. Lemma 3.5 in
[
4
]
implies that all sets of the form
K(\{k_{r}\}_{r\in\mathbb{N}})=\cap_{r=1}^{+\infty}F^{(r)}_{k_{r}}
are compact. Moreover, Assumption
1
implies that

\forall r\in\mathbb{N}\,:\,\lim_{k}\liminf_{n}P_{n}(F^{(r)}_{k})=1.

So
\{P_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is tight by virtue of Proposition
B.1
.

We now use Proposition
B.2
to show convergence. That Proposition gives us a countable family of functions such that if
P_{n}f
converges for all
f\in\mathcal{A}
, then
P_{n}
converges. In the present setting, we need a countable family of
r
-local functions. To do this,
define for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
the projection map
\Pi_{m}:\mathcal{T}_{*}\to\mathcal{T}_{*}
that takes
[G,o]
to the
m
-neighborhood
[G,o]_{m}
. This map is a contraction of
\mathcal{T}_{*}
and is therefore continuous, and moreover

\forall[G,o]\in\mathcal{G}_{*}\,:\,d([G,o],\Pi_{m}([G,o]))=d([G,o],[G,o]_{m})\leq\frac{1}{m+1}.

In particular, this implies that for any bounded Lipschitz
f:\mathcal{T}_{*}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\sup\{|f([G,o])-f\circ\Pi_{m}([G,o])|\,:\,[G,o]\in K\}\leq\frac{\|f\|_{\rm Lip}}{m+1}.

It transpires that one can replace the family
\mathcal{A}
with

\mathcal{S}:=\{f\circ\Pi_{m}\,:\,f\in\mathcal{A},m\in\mathbb{N}\},

which is still countable, and obtain the desired result.
