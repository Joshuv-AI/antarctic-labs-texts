Wilf collapse in permutation classes
Michael Albert, Vít Jelínek, Michal Opler

Abstract
For a hereditary permutation class 𝒞 {\mathcal{C}} , we say that two permutations π \pi and σ \sigma of 𝒞 {\mathcal{C}} are Wilf-equivalent in 𝒞 {\mathcal{C}} , if 𝒞 {\mathcal{C}} has the same number of permutations avoiding π \pi as those avoiding σ \sigma . We say that a permutation class 𝒞 {\mathcal{C}} exhibits a Wilf collapse if the number of permutations of size n n in 𝒞 {\mathcal{C}} is asymptotically larger than the number of Wilf-equivalence classes formed by these permutations. Previously, only a few specific examples of classes were known to exhibit Wilf collapse. In this paper, we show that Wilf collapse is a surprisingly common phenomenon. Among other results, we show that Wilf collapse occurs in any permutation class with unbounded growth and finitely many sum-indecomposable permutations. Our proofs are based on encoding the elements of a permutation class 𝒞 {\mathcal{C}} as words and analyzing the structure of a random permutation in 𝒞 {\mathcal{C}} using this representation.

Introduction

Given a collection,
{\mathcal{C}}
, of finite structures one associates with it the
growth function

n\mapsto c_{n}
where
c_{n}
is the number of structures in
{\mathcal{C}}
of size
n
. There seems to be no
generally accepted word for the concept of “two classes having the same growth function” – we
have decided to say that such classes are
rank-equinumerous
. In the study of permutation
classes (exact definitions follow in the next section) much attention has been paid to examples of
rank-equinumerosity, perhaps the most famous being that the collection of permutations that do not
contain the permutation
231
as a subpermutation, and the class of those that do not contain
321

are rank-equinumerous. While such equivalences are interesting they are perhaps not too surprising
given that simple questions tend to have simple answers and there are only so many simple answers to
go around.

We are concerned with a special sort of rank-equinumerosity. This arises when we begin with a
universe,
{\mathcal{U}}
, of finite structures carrying a containment relation denoted
\leqslant
. The collections
we then consider are down-sets in
{\mathcal{U}}
, i.e., subcollections of
{\mathcal{U}}
closed under containment
(sometimes called
hereditary
subsets of
{\mathcal{U}}
). Even more specifically, we consider only those
down-sets that are defined by the
avoidance
of a single structure
A
, i.e., they consist of
all the elements of
{\mathcal{U}}
that do not contain
A
. We then say that
A
and
B
are

Wilf-equivalent
(in
{\mathcal{U}}
if the context is not clear), if the down-set of structures
avoiding
A
is rank-equinumerous to the down-set of structures avoiding
B
. For example, the
rank-equinumerosity mentioned in the previous paragraph arises then when
{\mathcal{U}}
is the set of all
permutations,
A=231
and
B=321
.

We will also say that
{\mathcal{U}}
exhibits a
Wilf collapse
if the number of Wilf-equivalence classes
on structures of size
n
is small when compared to the total number of structures of size
n
,
i.e., the average size of a Wilf class tends to infinity as
n
grows. We further say that
{\mathcal{U}}

exhibits an
exponential Wilf collapse
if the average size of a Wilf class is exponential
in
n
.

While there have been many previous investigations that deal with specific examples of Wilf
equivalence, or even a few general groups of Wilf-equivalent structures, there has been relatively
little attention paid to the phenomenon of Wilf collapse. In
[
2
]
it was demonstrated
that the universe of 312-avoiding permutations exhibits a Wilf collapse, and in
[
1
]
that
every permutation class with two basis elements of size 3 and itself having an unbounded growth
function (which is of course a prerequisite for Wilf collapse!) exhibits a Wilf collapse. Notably,
as of this moment, we still do not know if the universe of 321-avoiding permutations exhibits a Wilf
collapse and the results of this paper do not speak to this case.

In this paper, rather than focusing on individual examples of permutation classes, we derive general
structural criteria that imply Wilf collapse, or even exponential Wilf collapse. Our approach is
based on decomposing permutations into indecomposable blocks using the sum operation
(see Section
2
for precise definitions). Specifically, we can prove the following
results.

•

Any permutation class
{\mathcal{C}}
obtained as a sum-closure of finitely many
permutations exhibits an exponential Wilf collapse, except for the class of
21-avoiding permutations, whose growth function is bounded. See
Corollary
4.2
.

•

Any permutation class
{\mathcal{C}}
with unbounded growth function and with only finitely many
indecomposable permutations has a Wilf collapse. See Theorem
4.3
.

We remark that the first of these results is in fact a special case of a more general theorem
(Theorem
4.1
), which deals with sum-closures of possibly infinite sets satisfying
certain additional restrictions.

While our results focus on permutation classes, the underlying arguments can be generalised easily
to some other contexts. The basis of our approach is the observation that any permutation can be
uniquely expressed as a sum of a sequence of sum-indecomposable components. This yields a
representation of a permutation by a word over an alphabet consisting of the indecomposable
permutations. The containment of permutations then corresponds to a certain “greedy” embedding of
words. We then identify, for a permutation class
{\mathcal{C}}
satisfying suitable closure properties, a
number of “local modifications” of the word which preserve the Wilf class of the corresponding
permutation. These local modifications often take the form of applying a symmetry operation to a
subword. Finally, and this is usually the most difficult part of the argument, we analyse the
structure of a word representing a random permutation
\pi
of
{\mathcal{C}}
, and show that with high
probability it offers many opportunities for such local modifications, showing that
\pi
belongs to
a large Wilf class.

Our emphasis in this paper is simply on establishing the existence of a Wilf collapse: we make no
attempt to determine the precise number of Wilf classes, or even an accurate asymptotic estimate.
This is because our results are necessarily based on general criteria for Wilf equivalence, while
specific permutation classes may often admit additional rules or coincidences that cause further
collapse. Besides, even in quite simple settings such as those considered in
[
1
]
where
the precise nature of a collapse can be computed, dealing with the exact answers can become quite
technical. That is, demonstrating that certain groups of structures are Wilf-equivalent is easy, but
demonstrating that no others are seems hard. Similarly, in
[
2
]
there is a
conjectural description of the exact nature of the Wilf collapse within certain classes enumerated
by the Catalan numbers, and while the experimental evidence in its favour seems quite strong, there
is no known way to rule out some other “accidental” coincidences.

The structure of the remainder of this paper is as follows. In Section
2
, we provide
the basic definitions needed to discuss permutation classes and Wilf collapse.
Section
3
then carries out some necessary preparatory work about words and
generalisations of the subword relation. Section
4
is devoted to the statements and
proofs of our main results. Our two main results there are Theorem
4.1
which deals
with sum-closed classes, and Theorem
4.3
concerning classes having only finitely many
sum-indecomposable permutations. Finally, in Section
5
we discuss the
significance and limitations of our results, and pose some further questions which we consider
pertinent.

2
Basic definitions

We refer the reader to Vatter’s excellent survey
[
12
]
for a much more detailed consideration of permutation classes (as well as an historical introduction) providing here only the essential elements for our work. We are concerned only with permutations of size
n
which we generally think of in one-line notation i.e., as sequences of length
n
consisting of the elements of
[n]=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
in some order. We write
|\pi|
for the size of a permutation
\pi
1
1

1

We generally try to avoid using the word “length” here although it is quite common and natural due to a possible confusion with the notion of length of a permutation arising in algebraic combinatorics.
.

When we take a subsequence of size
k
of such a sequence and then relabel it so that its least element is labelled 1, its second least element 2, …, and its greatest element
k
then we obtain another permutation and this relationship defines the notion of containment between permutations (sometimes called “containment as patterns”). To rephrase: a permutation
\tau
of size
n

contains
a permutation
\pi
of size
k
if there is a subsequence of
\tau
consisting of
k
elements whose relabelling by relative value yields
\pi
. If this occurs we write
\pi\leqslant\tau
, and if not we say that
\tau

avoids

\pi
and write
\pi\not\leqslant\tau
. For instance the permutation
31524
contains the patterns
123
(as
124
) and
213
(as either
315
or
314
) but not the pattern
321
(since no three of its elements form a descending sequence). A
permutation class
is a collection of permutations,
{\mathcal{C}}
, closed downwards under containment, i.e., if
\tau\in{\mathcal{C}}
and
\pi\leqslant\tau
then
\pi\in{\mathcal{C}}
.

The partially ordered set
{\mathcal{S}}
of all finite permutations ordered by containment admits eight symmetries corresponding to the action of the dihedral group on a square. These symmetries are easy to understand if we think of a permutation
\pi
as being represented by the set of points
(i,\pi_{i})
contained in an axis-aligned square. Reflection in a vertical axis is called “reverse”, in a horizontal axis “complement”, and in an upward sloping diagonal “inverse”.

Given a permutation class
{\mathcal{C}}
other than the class of all permutations, there are some

\leqslant
-minimal permutations in its complement and these are called its
basis
. So
{\mathcal{C}}
can
also be described as the set of all permutations avoiding any permutation in its basis. If
X
is
any set of permutations then we write
\operatorname{Av}(X)
for the class of permutations that avoid every
element of
X
. If
X
is an antichain with respect to containment then
X
will be the basis of

\operatorname{Av}(X)
.

Given two permutations
\alpha
and
\beta
define their
sum

\alpha\oplus\beta
to be the
concatenation of
\alpha
and
a+\beta
where
a
is the size of
\alpha
. For instance
231\oplus 2413=2315746
. It is easy to see that this operation is associative on permutations. A
class
{\mathcal{C}}
is
sum-closed
if whenever
\alpha,\beta\in{\mathcal{C}}
then also
\alpha\oplus\beta\in{\mathcal{C}}
. A permutation is
sum-indecomposable
if it cannot be written as a proper sum of two
permutations. There is a dual notion of
skew-sum
(
\ominus
) where
\alpha\ominus\beta
is
the concatenation of
b+\alpha
with
\beta
(where the size of
\beta
is
b
). It is easy to see
that a permutation class is sum- (resp. skew-) closed if and only if all of its basis elements are
sum- (resp. skew-) indecomposable.

It is particularly convenient to work with (and within) sum-closed classes because there is a natural representation of any permutation
\alpha
in such a class as the unique sequence of sum-indecomposable permutations
\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\cdots\alpha_{k}
for which

\alpha=\alpha_{1}\oplus\alpha_{2}\oplus\cdots\oplus\alpha_{k}.

This identifies the class with the language of words over its sum-indecomposables. Given any set
X

of permutations there is a smallest class
{\mathcal{C}}
which is sum-closed and contains
X
(obtained simply
by finding all the sum-indecomposable permutations that are contained in some element of
X
and
then taking all sums of those). This class is called the
sum-closure
of
X
.

Let a class
{\mathcal{C}}
be given. Two permutations
\alpha,\beta\in{\mathcal{C}}
are
Wilf-equivalent in

{\mathcal{C}}
(written
\alpha\equiv_{{\mathcal{C}}}\beta
) if the two classes
{\mathcal{C}}\cap\operatorname{Av}(\alpha)
and
{\mathcal{C}}\cap\operatorname{Av}(\beta)
are rank-equinumerous, i.e., have the same growth functions. The equivalence classes of

\equiv_{\mathcal{C}}
are known as the
Wilf classes
.

Observation 2.1
.

If
\alpha\equiv_{{\mathcal{C}}}\beta
then
|\alpha|=|\beta|
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality suppose that
k=|\alpha|\leq|\beta|
. The number of permutations in

{\mathcal{C}}\cap\operatorname{Av}(\alpha)
of size
k
is exactly one less than the number of permutations in
{\mathcal{C}}
of
size
k
and for this to be true of
{\mathcal{C}}\cap\operatorname{Av}(\beta)
as well we must have
|\beta|=k
,
otherwise every permutation in
{\mathcal{C}}
of size
k
belongs to
{\mathcal{C}}\cap\operatorname{Av}(\beta)
.
∎

For a positive integer
n
, let
{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
denote the set of permutations in
{\mathcal{C}}
of size
n
, let

c_{n}
be the cardinality of
{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
, and let
w_{n}
denote the number of Wilf classes formed by the
permutations in
{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
. This allows us at last to define the fundamental concept which we
will investigate.

Definition 2.2
.

The class
{\mathcal{C}}
has a
Wilf collapse
if
w_{n}=o(c_{n})
and an
exponential Wilf collapse

if, for some
r<1
,
w_{n}=o(r^{n}c_{n})
.

If
{\mathcal{C}}
is closed under some symmetry
\phi
of
{\mathcal{S}}
and
\alpha\in{\mathcal{C}}
then
\alpha\equiv_{{\mathcal{C}}}\phi(\alpha)
. However, this never provides a Wilf collapse since
{\mathcal{S}}
has only eight symmetries.
That said, these equivalences will form the core of many of our constructions that do demonstrate
Wilf collapse.

3
A digression on words

Let
{\mathcal{A}}
be a set of symbols which we will call the
letters
of an
alphabet
. A

word
over
{\mathcal{A}}
is just a finite sequence (possibly empty) of elements of
{\mathcal{A}}
— the set of
all words over
{\mathcal{A}}
is denoted
{\mathcal{A}}^{\ast}
and the set of non-empty words is denoted
{\mathcal{A}}^{+}
. The
empty word is denoted
\epsilon
. The set
{\mathcal{A}}^{\ast}
has an associative operation which is
normally simply represented by concatenation.

We generally use lower case letters from near the beginning of the alphabet to denote elements of

{\mathcal{A}}
and upper case letters from near the end of the alphabet to denote words. That said, we will
freely identify a letter
a\in{\mathcal{A}}
with the corresponding word of length
1
, and thus treat
{\mathcal{A}}
as
a subset of
{\mathcal{A}}^{+}
.

If
W=a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{n}
then we say that
a_{i}
is the character of
index

i
. If
W\in{\mathcal{A}}^{+}
then
\operatorname{first}(W)
and
\operatorname{last}(W)
denote the first and last letter of
W
respectively; this
notation is not defined for the empty word, i.e., when used contains an implicit condition that
W\neq\epsilon
.

We will further assume that each letter
a\in{\mathcal{A}}
has a
weight
, denoted
\weight(a)
, which
is a positive integer. We extend the weight function to
{\mathcal{A}}^{\ast}
by setting

\weight(a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{k})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\weight(a_{i}).

An
embedding order
is any partial order
\leqslant
on
{\mathcal{A}}^{\ast}
satisfying these conditions:

•

For any
W\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\ast}
, we have
\epsilon\leqslant W
.

•

If
W\leqslant V
for some
V,W\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\ast}
with
W\neq V
, then
\weight(W)<\weight(V)
.

•

Suppose that
V=a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{k}
. Then, for any
W\in{\mathcal{A}}^{*}
, we have
W\leqslant V
if and only
if
W
admits a factorisation
W=W_{1}W_{2}\cdots W_{k}
such that
W_{i}\leqslant a_{i}
for each
i
.

A familiar example of an embedding order is the
subword order
, where
W=a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{k}
is
a subword of
V=b_{1}b_{2}\cdots b_{\ell}
if the sequence
a_{1},\dotsc,a_{k}
is a (not necessarily
consecutive) subsequence of
b_{1},\dotsc,b_{\ell}
. In fact, if
W
is a subword of
V
, then in any
embedding order
\leqslant
we must have
W\leqslant V
.

From now on, we assume that
\leqslant
is an embedding order for a weighted alphabet
{\mathcal{A}}
. A useful
feature of such orders is that containment can be tested by a natural “greedy” procedure, as shown
by the next proposition.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
W,V\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\ast}
be two words with
V=b_{1}b_{2}\cdots b_{m}
, let
X=b_{1}\cdots b_{i}
be a
prefix of
V
, and let
Y=b_{i+1}\cdots b_{m}
be the corresponding suffix. Let
P
be the maximal
prefix of
W
such that
P\leqslant X
, and write
W=PS
. Then
W\leqslant V
if and only if
S\leqslant Y
.

Proof.

If
P\leqslant X
and
S\leqslant Y
, then
W=PS\leqslant XY=V
by the properties of embedding order.
Conversely,
if
W\leqslant V
then we can write
W=W_{1}W_{2}\cdots W_{m}
with
W_{j}\leqslant b_{j}
. In particular,

W_{1}W_{2}\cdots W_{i}
is either
P
or a proper prefix of
P
. Then
S
is a (not necessarily proper)
suffix of
W_{i+1}\cdots W_{m}
and hence
S\leqslant Y
.
∎

Definition 3.2
.

Let
W,V\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\ast}
. If
W\leqslant V
and
W\not\leqslant P
for any proper prefix
P
of
V
then we
say that
V
is a
minimal container
for
W
and write
W\leqslant^{\ast}V
. Further we define
generating functions:

\displaystyle A(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in{\mathcal{A}}}x^{\weight(a)},

\displaystyle I_{W}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{W\leqslant V}x^{\weight(V)},

\displaystyle I_{W}^{\ast}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{W\leqslant^{\ast}V}x^{\weight(V)}.

Observe that
W\leqslant V
if and only if
V
can be written as
V=PZ
where
W\leqslant^{\ast}P
and
Z\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\ast}
is arbitrary; moreover
P
is uniquely determined as the minimal prefix of
V
which
is greater than or equal to
W
. This corresponds to the following identity of generating
functions.

Observation 3.3
.

For any
W\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\ast}

I_{W}(x)=\frac{I^{\ast}_{W}(x)}{1-A(x)}.

In particular
I_{W}(x)=I_{V}(x)
if and only if
I^{\ast}_{W}(x)=I^{\ast}_{V}(x)
.

Let us say that two words
W
and
V
are
equivalent
, denoted by
W\equiv V
, if

I_{W}(x)=I_{V}(x)
. Our goal is to show that, under certain assumptions about
\leqslant
, there are many
pairs of equivalent words.

Definition 3.4
.

Let
a,b\in{\mathcal{A}}
be two letters. The ordered pair
(a,b)
is
incompatible
if there is no
c\in{\mathcal{A}}
such that
ab\leqslant c
.

Definition 3.5
.

Let
W_{1},W_{2},\dots,W_{k}\in{\mathcal{A}}^{+}
. The factorisation
W=W_{1}W_{2}\cdots W_{k}
is

incompatible
if for
1\leqslant i<k
,
(\operatorname{last}(W_{i}),\operatorname{first}(W_{i+1}))
are incompatible pairs.

Proposition 3.6
.

Suppose that that
W=W_{1}W_{2}\cdots W_{k}
is an incompatible factorisation. Then

I_{W}(x)=\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{k}I^{\ast}_{W_{i}}(x)}{1-A(x)}.

Proof.

The proof is by induction on
k
. For
k=1
it is simply Observation
3.3
. Now
suppose that
k>1
and the result holds for all lesser
k
. To complete the proof, it suffices to
show that

I_{W}(x)=I^{\ast}_{W_{1}}(x)I_{W_{2}W_{3}\cdots W_{k}}(x),

(1)

and apply induction. To prove (
1
), we will show that a word
V\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\ast}
satisfies
W\leqslant V
if and only if
V
can be written as
V=XY
with
W_{1}\leqslant^{\ast}X
and
W_{2}W_{3}\cdots W_{k}\leqslant Y
, and moreover, the
X
and
Y
are then determined uniquely.

Clearly, for any choice of
X
and
Y
satisfying
W_{1}\leqslant^{\ast}X
and
W_{2}W_{3}\cdots W_{k}\leqslant Y
, we have
W\leqslant XY
. To prove the converse, choose
V
such that
W\leqslant V
. Then there
is a unique prefix
X
of
V
such that
W_{1}\leqslant^{\ast}X
. Let
V=XY
,
a=\operatorname{last}(W_{1})
,
b=\operatorname{first}(W_{2})
and
X=c_{1}c_{2}\cdots c_{n}
. By the definition of an embedding order,
W_{1}
can be
written as
W_{1}=Z_{1}Z_{2}\cdots Z_{n}
with
Z_{i}\leqslant c_{i}
. Moreover, from the minimality of
X
, it
follows that
Z_{n}\neq\epsilon
and that
W_{1}\not\leqslant c_{1}\cdots c_{n-1}
. In particular,

a=\operatorname{last}(Z_{n})\leq c_{n}
. Since
a
and
b
are incompatible, we know that

, and
therefore
W_{1}
is the longest prefix of
W
such that
W_{1}\leqslant X
. By
Proposition
3.1
, we get
W_{2}\cdots W_{k}\leqslant Y
, as claimed.
∎

The following direct corollary of Proposition

and Observation

Corollary 3.7
.

Suppose that for

3.1
Uniform sampling of words

We will often need to refer to the properties of uniformly random words of a
given weight in a given set
{\mathcal{A}}^{\ast}
. Recall that

A(x)=\sum_{a\in{\mathcal{A}}}x^{\weight(a)}

is the
generating function of the alphabet
{\mathcal{A}}
, and define

A^{*}(x)=\sum_{W\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\ast}}x^{\weight(W)}=\frac{1}{1-A(x)}.

Let
\rho_{A}
be the radius of convergence of
A(x)
. We say that
{\mathcal{A}}^{*}
is
supercritical
if

\lim_{x\to\rho_{A}^{-}}A(x)>1.

If
{\mathcal{A}}^{*}
is supercritical, then the radius of convergence of
A^{\ast}(x)
is the unique positive value
\kappa<\rho_{A}
such
that
A(\kappa)=1
.

We say that
{\mathcal{A}}^{\ast}
is
aperiodic
if the greatest common divisor of

\{\weight(a);\;a\in{\mathcal{A}}\}
is 1.

In our setting, where the alphabet
{\mathcal{A}}
will generally correspond to the sum-indecomposable elements
of a permutation class, aperiodicity is satisfied since there is a letter of weight
1
. But even
more generally it is not a significant restriction, since we can simply divide all the weights by
their greatest common divisor. Supercriticality, on the other hand, is a more fundamental property.

In the rest of Subsection
3.1
, we assume that
{\mathcal{A}}^{\ast}
is supercritical, with

A(x)
and
\kappa
as above, and we fix a probability measure on
{\mathcal{A}}
defined by

\mathbb{P}(a)=\kappa^{\weight(a)}
. This will be the underlying probability measure
whenever we speak of a random letter from
{\mathcal{A}}
.

Let
\overline{w}=\mathbb{E}[\weight(a)]
denote the expected weight of a letter from
{\mathcal{A}}
.
Then

\overline{w}=\sum_{a\in{\mathcal{A}}}\weight(a)\kappa^{\weight(a)}=\kappa A^{\prime}(\kappa),

where
A^{\prime}
is the derivative of
A
, and
A^{\prime}(\kappa)
is finite since
A
is analytic at
\kappa
.

As a technical tool, we will use the following concentration inequality, which follows from
standard probabilistic results; see e.g. the books of Dembo and Zeitouni
[
4
, Chapter 2.2]

or Flajolet and Sedgewick
[
6
, Chapter IX.10]
. We include the proof here for
completeness.

Proposition 3.8
.

For every
\varepsilon>0
there is a
\delta>0
such
that for a random word
W=a_{1}a_{2}\dotsb a_{k}
obtained by concatenating
k
random independent
letters from
{\mathcal{A}}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigl(\weight(W)\geq(1+\varepsilon)k\overline{w}\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\delta k}

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigl(\weight(W)\leq(1-\varepsilon)k\overline{w}\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\delta k}.

Proof.

Let
X
be the random variable on
{\mathcal{A}}
defined as
X=\weight(a)
where
a\in{\mathcal{A}}
is a random
letter. Define the function
K(t)=\ln(\mathbb{E}[e^{tX}])=\ln\left(\sum_{k\geq 0}\mathbb{E}[X^{k}]\frac{t^{k}}{k!}\right)
. In probability theory, the function
K
is known as the ‘cumulant
generating function’. It can also be written as
K(t)=\ln\left(A(\kappa e^{t})\right)
. We may then
easily check that
K(t)
is analytic at
t=0
and has a Taylor series expansion of the form

K(t)=\overline{w}t+O(t^{2})
in a neighborhood of
t=0
.

Let
W=a_{1}a_{2}\dotsb a_{k}
be a word of
k
letters chosen independently from
{\mathcal{A}}
, and let
X_{i}
be
the weight of
a_{i}
. In particular,
X_{1},\dotsc,X_{k}
are independent random variables of the same
distribution as
X
. Let
Y=X_{1}+X_{2}+\dotsb+X_{k}
.

Fix an
\varepsilon>0
and write
w^{+}=(1+\varepsilon)\overline{w}
. Let
Q
denote the event
Y\geq kw^{+}
, and let
\mathbb{1}_{Q}
be the indicator function of this event, i.e., the
function equal to 1 when the event occurs and 0 otherwise. Observe that for any
t\geq 0
, the
function
\mathbb{1}_{Q}
is bounded from above by
\exp(tY-tkw^{+})
. We then have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Y\geq kw^{+})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{1}_{Q}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[\exp(tY-tkw^{+})]

\displaystyle=\exp(-tkw^{+})\mathbb{E}[\exp(tY)]

\displaystyle=\exp(-tkw^{+})\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{k}\exp(tX_{i})\right]

\displaystyle=\exp(-tkw^{+})\prod_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(tX_{i})\right]

\displaystyle=\exp(-tkw^{+})\exp(kK(t))

\displaystyle=\exp\left(k(K(t)-tw^{+})\right).

Recalling that
K(t)=\overline{w}t+O(t^{2})
, we can find a sufficiently small value
t>0
such that

K(t)<tw^{+}
. Choosing such a
t
and putting
\delta=tw^{+}-K(t)>0
, we obtain
\mathbb{P}(Y\geq kw^{+})\leq e^{-\delta k}
, as claimed.

The second inequality of the proposition is proven by an analogous argument, except now we consider
the values
t\leq 0
.
∎

Our main concern will be to understand the structure of random words of fixed weight, which is
usually much more challenging than dealing with words of fixed length. Let
{\mathcal{A}}^{\ast}_{n}
be set of
words of
{\mathcal{A}}^{\ast}
of weight
n
. Clearly,
{\mathcal{A}}^{\ast}_{n}
is finite, and we will consider the uniform
probability measure on this set, i.e., the measure where every word
W\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\ast}_{n}
has probability

1/|{\mathcal{A}}^{\ast}_{n}|
.

To generate such a random word from
W\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\ast}_{n}
, we may use the following process, known as

Boltzmann sampler with rejection
. The process works in two phases, where in the first phase,
it generates a random word of weight at least
n
, and in the next phase, it rejects the generated
word if its weight is not exactly
n
. More precisely, the Boltzmann sampler works as follows.

First phase.

For
i=1,2,\dotsc
, select randomly and independently a letter
a_{i}\in{\mathcal{A}}
. Stop as soon as

\weight(a_{1}a_{2}\dotsb a_{i})\geq n
, and let
W=a_{1}a_{2}\dotsb a_{i}
be the generated word.

Second phase.
For the word
W
generated by the first phase, check whether
W
has
weight
n
. If it does, the second phase succeeds, and
W
is output. If not, the second phase
fails and the whole sampler is restarted.

Samplers of this form were analyzed by Duchon et al.
[
5
, Section 7]
. Let us summarize the
main results of their analysis.

Fact 3.9

(Duchon et al.
[
5
]
)
.

Suppose that
{\mathcal{A}}^{\ast}
is aperiodic and supercritical.
Then the second phase of the Boltzmann sampler with rejection succeeds with probability
\Omega(1)
.
The word output by the sampler is a uniformly random element of
{\mathcal{A}}^{\ast}_{n}
, i.e., each word

W\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\ast}_{n}
is generated with probability
1/{|{\mathcal{A}}^{\ast}_{n}|}
.

We will use the Boltzmann sampler to obtain an insight into the structure of a typical word in

{\mathcal{A}}^{\ast}_{n}
as
n
tends to infinity. For a word
P=p_{1}p_{2}\dotsb p_{k}
, a
P
-block

in another word
W=w_{1}w_{2}\dotsb w_{n}
is a sequence
w_{j+1}w_{j+2}\dotsb w_{j+k}
of consecutive
letters such that
p_{i}=w_{j+i}
for every
i=1,\dotsc,k
.

Proposition 3.10
.

For every word
P=p_{1}p_{2}\dotsb p_{k}
from
{\mathcal{A}}^{\ast}
, there is an
\varepsilon\equiv\varepsilon_{P}>0
such that, with probability at least
1-\frac{1}{2^{\Omega(n)}}
, a uniformly
random word
X\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\ast}_{n}
contains at least
\varepsilon n
pairwise disjoint
P
-blocks.

Proof.

The first phase of the Boltzmann sampler can equivalently be implemented by the
following procedure: first choose a sequence
V=a_{1}a_{2}\dotsb a_{n}
of
n
random independent letters
from
{\mathcal{A}}
, and then output the word
W=a_{1}a_{2}\dotsb a_{i}
determined as the shortest prefix of
V

of weight at least
n
.

Recall that
\overline{w}
is the expected weight of a letter in
{\mathcal{A}}
. Define
m=\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2\overline{w}}\right\rfloor
. We will consider two possible ‘bad’ outcomes of the above random
procedure: the first bad outcome is that the length
i
of
W
is smaller than
m
, the second bad
outcome is that the prefix of
V
of length
m
has fewer than
\varepsilon n
pairwise disjoint

P
-blocks, for an
\varepsilon>0
to be specified later. We will show that both bad outcomes have
exponentially small probability. Clearly, if neither of the two bad outcomes occurs, then the first
phase of the Boltzmann sampler generates a word with at least
\varepsilon n
pairwise disjoint

P
-blocks, and since the second phase succeeds with constant probability, this implies that only an
exponentially small fraction of the words in
{\mathcal{A}}^{\ast}_{n}
contain fewer than
\varepsilon n

pairwise disjoint
P
-blocks.

The probability of the first bad outcome (i.e.,
i<m
) is at most as large as the probability that
the first
m
letters of
V
have weight at least
n\geq 2m\overline{w}
, which is exponentially small by
Proposition
3.8
.

To estimate the probability of the second bad outcome, let
Y
be the prefix of
V
of length
m
.
Define
q=\left\lfloor\frac{m}{k}\right\rfloor
, and partition
Y
into subwords as
Y_{1}Y_{2}\dotsb Y_{q}Z
, where
Y_{1},\dotsc,Y_{q}
all have length exactly
k
, and
Z
is a possibly empty word of
length at most
k-1
. The words
Y_{1},\dotsc,Y_{q}
are pairwise independent, and each of them is a
random word of length
k
. In particular, there is a positive probability
\delta>0
depending on

P
such that for every
j\in[q]
we have
\mathbb{P}(Y_{j}=P)=\delta
.

There are, therefore, on average
\delta q
values of
j
for which
Y_{j}=P
, and each such value
corresponds to a
P
-block. By the standard Chernoff–Hoeffding bound
[
9
]
, the
probability that there are fewer than
\delta q/2
values of
j
satisfying
Y_{j}=P
is exponentially
small in
q
, and therefore also in
n
.

We conclude that the word output by the sampler, which is a uniformly random word from
{\mathcal{A}}^{\ast}_{n}
,
contains at least
\delta q/2
disjoint
P
-blocks, up to exceptions of exponentially small
probability. Since
q=\Omega(n)
this completes the proof.
∎

4
Wilf collapse

In this section, we will present our two main results demonstrating Wilf collapse in a permutation
class
{\mathcal{C}}
under different sets of assumptions. The two results deal with sum-closed classes and
with classes with finitely many sum-indecomposables, respectively. Their proofs all follow the same
general strategy:

•

Represent the elements of
{\mathcal{C}}
as words over the alphabet consisting of the sum-indecomposable
permutations in
{\mathcal{C}}
.

•

Using Corollary
3.7
characterise some “good” elements of
{\mathcal{C}}
whose
equivalence classes with respect to
\equiv_{{\mathcal{C}}}
are “large”,

•

Show that permutations in
{\mathcal{C}}
are bad with “sufficiently small” probability.

The number of Wilf classes for
{\mathcal{C}}
among elements of size
n
is bounded above by the sum of the
number of good permutations in
{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
divided by the smallest size of a good Wilf class, and the
number of bad permutations in
{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
. Therefore, the scheme above is sufficient to prove a Wilf
collapse provided that “large” implies tending to infinity, and “sufficiently small” means
tending to 0. To obtain an exponential Wilf collapse it is sufficient that “large” should mean
“of exponential size” and that the probability of a permutation being bad is exponentially small.

4.1
Sum-closed classes

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a sum-closed class. Take the alphabet
{\mathcal{A}}
to consist of the sum-indecomposable
permutations of
{\mathcal{C}}
with the weight of a letter simply being equal to its size. Then we already
have an obvious bijection between
{\mathcal{C}}
and
{\mathcal{A}}^{\ast}
which we now treat as implicit, i.e., we make
no distinction between a permutation in
{\mathcal{C}}
and its representation as (the sum of) a sequence of
sum-indecomposable permutations. In particular, we say that
{\mathcal{C}}
is supercritical whenever

{\mathcal{A}}^{\ast}
is. We also extend the containment order on
{\mathcal{C}}
to words of
{\mathcal{A}}^{\ast}
; that is, for

W,V\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\ast}
we write
W\leqslant V
if the permutation represented by
W
is contained in the
permutation represented by
V
. Observe that this partial order on
{\mathcal{A}}^{\ast}
is an
embedding order.

Theorem 4.1
.

Any supercritical sum-closed class,
{\mathcal{C}}
, that contains an incompatible pair has an exponential Wilf collapse, unless
{\mathcal{C}}
is the class of increasing permutations.

Proof.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a supercritical sum-closed class containing an incompatible pair
(a,b)
and not equal to the class of increasing permutations. The set,
{\mathcal{A}}
, of sum-indecomposable permutations in
{\mathcal{C}}
has at least the two elements
1

and
21
. Let
c
and
d
be two arbitrary distinct
elements from
{\mathcal{A}}
. Consider the words
X=bca
,
Y=bda
,
P=aXYb=abcabdab
, and
P^{\prime}=aYXb=abdabcab
.
Notice that both
P=aXYb
and
P^{\prime}=aYXb
are incompatible factorisations.

By Proposition
3.10
, there is an
\varepsilon>0
such that for every
n
, a uniformly
random permutation
\pi\in{\mathcal{C}}
of order
n
has a sum decomposition in which there are at least

\varepsilon n
disjoint
P
-blocks, except for an exponentially small fraction of ‘bad’
permutations. By Corollary
3.7
, if a permutation
\pi^{\prime}
is obtained from

\pi
by replacing some
P
-blocks by
P^{\prime}
-blocks, then
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
are Wilf-equivalent. In
particular, the Wilf class of a permutation that has at least
\varepsilon n
disjoint
P
-blocks
has size at least
2^{\varepsilon n}
. The theorem follows.
∎

Corollary 4.2
.

If
{\mathcal{C}}
is a sum-closed class that contains 21 and has only finitely many sum-indecomposable
permutations, then
{\mathcal{C}}
has an exponential Wilf collapse.

Proof.

Such a class is clearly supercritical. It also has an incompatible pair, e.g., 1 and any
sum-indecomposable permutation of maximum size, so Theorem
4.1
applies.
∎

4.2
Classes with finitely many sum-indecomposables

The aim of this subsection is to prove the following result.

Theorem 4.3
.

Any permutation class with finitely many sum-indecomposable permutations and an unbounded growth
function exhibits a Wilf collapse.

The proof of this result is rather technical so we will begin with a few words about its general
strategy. Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a permutation class with finitely many indecomposables and an unbounded growth
function. As in Subsection
4.1
, we will represent the elements of
{\mathcal{C}}
as words over
the alphabet
{\mathcal{A}}
of indecomposable elements of
{\mathcal{C}}
. However, not all words from
{\mathcal{A}}^{\ast}
now
correspond to elements of
{\mathcal{C}}
, so we cannot directly use the properties of
{\mathcal{A}}^{\ast}
to prove the
Wilf collapse of
{\mathcal{C}}
. Instead, we consider a finite state automaton over
{\mathcal{A}}
that accepts
only the words which represent elements of
{\mathcal{C}}
. The underlying graph of this automaton is directed
and acyclic except for loops on certain states. These loops represent certain sum-closed subclasses
of
{\mathcal{C}}
whose elements occur as consecutive subwords (“loop blocks”) within the elements of
{\mathcal{C}}
.

Since the classes corresponding to loop blocks are sum-closed,
Corollary
4.2
applies to them, and so, unless the only symbol that
allows for a loop at a given state is
1
, they have exponential Wilf collapse. We will then show
that under suitable technical assumptions, which a random permutation of
{\mathcal{C}}
satisfies with high
probability, the Wilf equivalences within the class generated by a loop block can be lifted to Wilf
equivalences for the whole class
{\mathcal{C}}
.

We begin with a general lemma dealing with the growth rate of a set of words generated by a finite
alphabet.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
{\mathcal{L}}
be a finite alphabet, with every letter
a\in{\mathcal{L}}
having a
positive integer weight
\weight(a)
. Let
k
be the largest weight of a letter of
{\mathcal{L}}
, and let

\alpha_{i}
be the number of letters in
{\mathcal{L}}
of weight
i
, for
i=1,\dotsc,k
. Assume that

\alpha_{1}=1
, i.e., there is a unique letter of weight 1. Let
L(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}x^{i}
be the
generating polynomial of
{\mathcal{L}}
.

The polynomial
1-L(x)
has a unique positive real root
\rho
, this root belongs to the interval

(0,1]
, has multiplicity 1, and any other complex root
\lambda
of
1-L(x)
satisfies

|\lambda|>\rho
. Moreover,
\rho
is equal to
1
if and only if
|{\mathcal{L}}|=1
. There are constants

c>0
and
\varepsilon>0
such that
|{\mathcal{L}}^{\ast}_{n}|=c\rho^{-n}+O((\rho+\varepsilon)^{-n})
.

Proof.

Noting that
\alpha_{1}=1
and
\alpha_{2},\dotsc,\alpha_{k}
are all nonnegative, we observe that

1-L(0)=1
and
1-L(1)\leq 0
, and therefore there is a
\rho\in(0,1]
such that
1-L(\rho)=0
. We
also see that
\rho=1
if and only if
L(x)=x
, or equivalently,
|{\mathcal{L}}|=1
.
Since the derivative of
1-L(x)
is negative for every
x>0
, we conclude that

\rho
is the unique positive root of
1-L(x)
, and that it has multiplicity 1.

Suppose now that
\lambda
is a complex root of
1-L(x)
, with
|\lambda|\leq\rho
. We claim
that
\lambda=\rho
. Let
\Re(z)
denote the real part of a complex number
z
. We then obtain

1=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}\lambda^{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}\Re(\lambda^{i})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}|\lambda|^{i}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}\rho^{i}=1.

In particular, all the inequalities hold with equality. Since for each
i\in[k]
, we have

\Re(\lambda^{i})\leq|\lambda|^{i}\leq\rho^{i}
, and since
\alpha_{1}>0
, we easily deduce that

\lambda=\rho
.

The generating function of
{\mathcal{L}}^{\ast}
is
L^{\ast}(x)=1/(1-L(x))
. We see that
L(x)
is a rational
function that has a simple pole at
x=\rho
, and any other pole has absolute value greater than

\rho+\varepsilon
, for some
\varepsilon>0
. From this, the asymptotics of
|{\mathcal{L}}^{\ast}_{n}|
follow,
by standard singularity analysis
[
6
]
.
∎

We call the value
\rho^{-1}
from the previous lemma the
growth rate
of
{\mathcal{L}}^{\ast}
.

Suppose from now on that
{\mathcal{C}}
is a permutation class with finitely many sum-indecomposable
permutations, and that its growth function is unbounded. Let
{\mathcal{A}}
be the set of sum-indecomposable
permutations in
{\mathcal{C}}
. By the above assumptions,
{\mathcal{A}}
is finite and contains at least two distinct
elements, namely
1
and
21
. Let
K
denote, from now on, the largest weight of a letter of
{\mathcal{A}}
.

As in Subsection
4.1
, we will represent the permutations in
{\mathcal{C}}
as words over the
alphabet
{\mathcal{A}}
, and assign to each letter of
{\mathcal{A}}
the weight equal to the size of the corresponding
sum-indecomposable permutation.
Since Corollary
4.2
deals with the sum-closed case, suppose
from now on that
{\mathcal{C}}
is not sum-closed, i.e., not every word in
{\mathcal{A}}^{\ast}
corresponds to a
permutation from
{\mathcal{C}}
.

Let
F
be the set of minimal elements of
{\mathcal{A}}^{\ast}
not belonging to
{\mathcal{C}}
; in particular, we have

{\mathcal{C}}={\mathcal{A}}^{\ast}\cap\operatorname{Av}(F)
. By the classical Higman Lemma
[
8
]
, the set
F
is finite. Let us
write
f=|F|
and
F=\{\phi^{1},\phi^{2},\dotsc,\phi^{f}\}
. The forbidden permutations
\phi^{i}
will
again be interpreted as words over
{\mathcal{A}}
. We let
\ell_{i}
denote the number of symbols of the
word
\phi^{i}
.

For a word
W=a_{1}a_{2}\dotsb a_{m}\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\ast}
, let
W_{\leq i}
denote its prefix
a_{1}a_{2}\dotsb a_{i}
, and
W_{\geq i}
its suffix
a_{i}a_{i+1}\dotsb a_{m}
. We will also use the notation
W_{<i}
and

W_{>i}
for
W_{\leq i-1}
and
W_{\geq i+1}
, respectively.

Let
W\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\ast}
be a word. The
prefix state
of
W
is the
f
-tuple

(p_{1},p_{2},\allowbreak\dotsc,p_{f})
where
p_{i}

is the length of the longest prefix of

\phi^{i}
that is contained in
W
, or in other words, for every
i=1,\dotsc,f
, the word

W
contains
\phi^{i}_{\leq p_{i}}
but avoids
\phi^{i}_{\leq p_{i}+1}
.

We say that the prefix state
p=(p_{1},\dotsc,p_{f})
is
valid
, if
p_{i}<\ell_{i}
for each
i
.
Observe that a word
W\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\ast}
is in
{\mathcal{C}}
if and only if its prefix state is valid. Let
{\mathcal{P}}
be
the set of all possible prefix states of the elements of
{\mathcal{C}}
. The empty word has prefix state

(0,0,\dotsc,0)
, which we will call
the initial prefix state
.

For two prefix states
p=(p_{1},\dotsc,p_{f})
and
p^{\prime}=(p^{\prime}_{1},\dotsc,p^{\prime}_{f})
, we write
p\leq p^{\prime}
if

p_{i}\leq p^{\prime}_{i}
for every
i\in[f]
, and we write
p<p^{\prime}
if
p\leq p^{\prime}
and
p\neq p^{\prime}
.

Let
X
be a word with a prefix state
p
, and let
a\in{\mathcal{A}}
be a symbol. The prefix state of
the word
Y=Xa
is then uniquely determined by
p
and
a
. Moreover, if
p^{\prime}
the prefix state of

Y
, then
p\leq p^{\prime}
. If
p=p^{\prime}
, we say that the symbol
a
is a
loop symbol for
p
,
otherwise we say that
a
is a
transition symbol from
p
to
p^{\prime}
. The
loop alphabet of

p
, denoted
{\mathcal{L}}_{p}
, is the set of the loop symbols of
p
.

A symbol
a\in{\mathcal{A}}
is in the loop alphabet of a valid prefix state
p=(p_{1},\dotsc,p_{f})
if
and only if for each
i=1,\dotsc,f
, the
(p_{i}+1)
-st symbol of
\phi^{i}
is not contained in
a
.
In particular, the loop alphabet of
p
is a down-set of
{\mathcal{A}}
.

Consider a word
W=a_{1}a_{2}\dotsb a_{m}
from
{\mathcal{C}}
. We will say that
W
has a
prefix transition

at position
i
if
W_{<i}
has a different prefix state than
W_{\leq i}
. Let
k
be the number of
prefix transitions in
W
, and let
i(1)<i(2)<\dotsb<i(k)
be the positions where the transitions
occur. We call the sequence of prefix states
p(0)<p(1)<\dotsb<p(k)
such that the transition at
position
i(j)
is from
p(j-1)
to
p(j)
the
prefix transition path
of
W
. The words in

{\mathcal{C}}
determine only finitely many possible prefix transition paths. The word
W
can
then be written as

W=B_{0}a_{i(1)}B_{1}a_{i(2)}B_{2}\dotsb B_{k-1}a_{i(k)}B_{k},

(2)

where
B_{j}
is a (possibly empty) word over the loop alphabet
{\mathcal{L}}_{p(j)}
. We call
B_{j}
the

j
-th loop block
of
W
, and we call the right-hand side of (
2
) the

prefix decomposition
of
W
.

Conversely, suppose that
p(0)<p(1)<\dotsb<p(k)
is an increasing sequence of valid prefix states
where
p(0)
is the initial state, that
t_{j}\in{\mathcal{A}}
is a transition symbol from
p(j-1)
to
p(j)
,
and that
B_{j}
is a possibly empty word over the alphabet
{\mathcal{L}}_{p(j)}
. Then the expression

B_{0}t_{1}B_{1}t_{2}B_{2}\dotsb B_{k-1}t_{k}B_{k}

is the prefix decomposition of a word from
{\mathcal{C}}
whose prefix transition path is
p(0)<p(1)<\dotsb<p(k)
.

For a prefix state
p\in{\mathcal{P}}
, the
growth rate of
p
, denoted
\gamma_{p}
, is the growth
rate of the language
{\mathcal{L}}_{p}^{\ast}
. The
dominant growth rate
of
{\mathcal{C}}
is the value

\gamma=\max_{p\in{\mathcal{P}}}\gamma_{p}
. We say that a prefix state
p
is
dominant
if

\gamma_{p}=\gamma
, and we call a loop block in a prefix decomposition
dominant
if it
corresponds to a dominant state. Let
D
denote the largest number of dominant states that can
appear on a single prefix transition path of a word from
{\mathcal{C}}
.

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
T=(p(0)<p(1)<\dotsb<p(k))
be a prefix transition path,
and let
\tau=(t_{1},\dotsc,t_{k})\in{\mathcal{A}}^{k}
be a sequence of symbols where
t_{j}
is a transition symbol
from
p(j-1)
to
p(j)
. Let
{\mathcal{C}}_{n}(T,\tau)
be the set of words in
{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
that have prefix
transition path
T
, with
j
-th transition on the symbol
t_{j}
. Let
\gamma_{T}
be the
maximum of
\gamma_{p(i)}
for
i\in\{0,\dotsc,k\}
, and let
d_{T}
be the number of values

i\in\{0,\dotsc,k\}
for which
\gamma_{p(i)}=\gamma_{T}
.

Then

|{\mathcal{C}}_{n}(T,\tau)|=\Theta\left(n^{d_{T}-1}\gamma_{T}^{n}\right).

Consequently,

|{\mathcal{C}}_{n}|=\Theta\left(n^{D-1}\gamma^{n}\right).

Proof.

Let
n^{\prime}=n-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\weight(t_{i})
. Let
\gamma_{j}
be the growth rate of
p(j)
. To count the
words
W\in{\mathcal{C}}_{n}(T,\tau)
, we will count their corresponding prefix decompositions

W=B_{0}t_{1}B_{1}\dotsb B_{k-1}t_{k}B_{k}
, or equivalently, the
(k+1)
-tuples
(B_{0},B_{1},\dotsc,B_{k})

with
\sum_{j=0}^{k}\weight(B_{j})=n^{\prime}
, where
B_{j}
is a word over the alphabet
{\mathcal{L}}_{p(j)}
.

Define the sets of indices
I=\{i\in\{0,\dotsc,k\};\;\gamma_{i}=\gamma_{T}\}
and
J=\{0,\dotsc,k\}\setminus I
. In particular,
|I|=d_{T}
. To estimate the number of prefix decompositions of
elements of
{\mathcal{C}}_{n}(T,\tau)
, we will first fix two integers
n_{I}
and
n_{J}
with
n_{I}+n_{J}=n^{\prime}
, and
then count the decompositions in which the loop blocks with growth rate
\gamma_{T}
have total weight

n_{I}
, and the remaining loop blocks have total weight
n_{J}
.

To count the possible choices for
(B_{i};\;i\in I)
with
\sum_{i\in I}\weight(B_{i})=n_{I}
, we first choose a
d_{T}
-tuple
(n_{i};\;i\in I)
satisfying
\sum_{i\in I}n_{i}=n_{I}
, and then choose
B_{i}\in{\mathcal{L}}^{\ast}_{p(i)}
of weight
n_{i}
. The number of the suitable

d_{T}
-tuples
(n_{i};\;i\in I)
is
\Theta(n_{I}^{d_{T}-1})
, and for any
i\in I
, there are

\Theta(\gamma_{T}^{n_{i}})
choices for
B_{i}
by Lemma
4.4
. Overall, the number of possible
choices of
(B_{i};\;i\in I)
for a fixed
n_{I}
is
\Theta(n_{I}^{d_{T}-1}\gamma_{T}^{n_{I}})
.

To count the choices of
(B_{j};\;j\in J)
satisfying
\sum_{j\in J}\weight(B_{j})=n_{J}
, let

\delta>0
be a value smaller than
\gamma_{T}
but larger than
\gamma_{j}
for any
j\in J
. We then
have
\Theta(n_{J}^{|J|-1})
ways to choose a
|J|
-tuple
(n_{j};j\in J)
with
\sum_{j\in J}n_{j}=n_{J}
, and for each
j\in J
,
\Theta(\gamma_{j}^{n_{j}})
ways to choose a block
B_{j}\in{\mathcal{L}}_{p(j)}^{\ast}
of weight
n_{j}
. The number of choices for
(B_{j};\;j\in J)
is thus

\mathcal{O}(\delta^{n_{J}})
.

This yields

|{\mathcal{C}}_{n}(T,\tau)|=\sum_{(n_{I},n_{J})}\mathcal{O}(\delta^{n_{J}})\Theta\left(n_{I}^{d_{T}-1}\gamma_{T}^{n_{I}}\right),

where the summation is over all pairs
(n_{I},n_{J})
satisfying
n_{I}+n_{J}=n^{\prime}
. We see that the summand
corresponding to
n_{J}=0
in this sum already has order
\Theta(n^{d_{T}-1}\gamma^{n})
, so we only need
an upper bound for
|{\mathcal{C}}_{n}(T,\tau)|
of the same order. Such an upper bound can be
obtained as follows:

\displaystyle|{\mathcal{C}}_{n}(T,\tau)|

\displaystyle=\sum_{(n_{I},n_{J})}\mathcal{O}(\delta^{n_{J}})\Theta\left(n_{I}^{d_{T}-1}\gamma_{T}^{n_{I}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\Theta\left(n^{d_{T}-1}\gamma_{T}^{n}\right)\sum_{(n_{I},n_{J})}\frac{\delta^{n_{J}}}{\gamma_{T}^{n_{J}}}

\displaystyle\leq\Theta\left(n^{d_{T}-1}\gamma_{T}^{n}\right)\sum_{n_{J}=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\delta}{\gamma_{T}}\right)^{n_{J}}

\displaystyle\leq\Theta\left(n^{d_{T}-1}\gamma_{T}^{n}\right).

The bound for
|{\mathcal{C}}_{n}|
then follows by summing
|{\mathcal{C}}_{n}(T,\tau)|
over all possible (finitely
many) choices of
T
and
\tau
, noting that these choices are independent of
n
.
∎

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
W
be a uniformly random word from the set
{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
. With probability
1-2^{-\Omega(\sqrt{n})}
,
the total weight of the non-dominant loop blocks in the prefix decomposition of
W
is smaller than

\sqrt{n}
.

Proof.

Proposition
4.5
shows that the fraction of words from
{\mathcal{C}}_{n}

whose prefix transition path
T
has no dominant state is exponentially small, so let us focus
on words with at least one dominant loop state. Applying the argument and the notation of the proof
of Proposition
4.5
to such
T
, we have
\gamma_{T}=\gamma
,
d_{T}\leq D
, and the
number of words in
{\mathcal{C}}_{n}(T,\tau)
whose non-dominant loop blocks have total weight at least
\sqrt{n}
is at most

\displaystyle\sum_{n_{J}=\sqrt{n}}^{n}\!\mathcal{O}\left(\delta^{n_{J}}n^{D-1}\gamma^{n-n_{J}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{O}\left(n^{D-1}\gamma^{n}\right)\sum_{n_{J}=\sqrt{n}}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\delta}{\gamma}\right)^{n_{J}}\leq\mathcal{O}\left(n^{D-1}\gamma^{n}\left(\frac{\delta}{\gamma}\right)^{\!\sqrt{n}}\right).

The lemma follows.
∎

Recall that
F=\{\phi^{1},\dotsc,\phi^{f}\}
is the set of minimal elements of
{\mathcal{A}}^{\ast}
not belonging
to
{\mathcal{C}}
, and that
\ell_{j}
is the length of
\phi^{j}
. To proceed with our argument, we now need to
also start considering suffix states of a word, which are analogous to prefix states. The

suffix state
of a word

is the
f
-tuple
(s_{1},\dotsc,s_{f})
where

is the length
of the longest suffix of
\phi^{j}
contained in
W
. We say that a suffix state

is

if
s_{j}<\ell_{j}
for each

, and we let
{\mathcal{S}}
be the set of all the possible suffix
states of the words from
{\mathcal{C}}
(which are necessarily valid).

In analogy with prefix states, we can associate to a suffix state

a
suffix loop

{\mathcal{L}}^{\leftarrow}_{s}
, which is the set of all the symbols
a\in{\mathcal{A}}
such that if
W
has suffix state

then

has suffix state
s
as well. We also say that a symbol
b
is a suffix transition
symbol from
s
to
s^{\prime}
, if for a word
W
of suffix state
s
, the word
bW
has suffix state
s^{\prime}
.

We can also define suffix decompositions, analogous to their prefix counterparts, but obtained when
scanning a word from right to left. We say that a word
W=a_{1}a_{2}\dotsb a_{m}\in{\mathcal{C}}
has a

transition
at position

has a different suffix state than

. Let

be all the positions where

be the corresponding suffix transition path; that is,

has suffix state

. The

Suffix decompositions satisfy analogous properties as their prefix counterparts. For instance,
Proposition
4.5
and Lemma

remain true when restated in the setting of
suffix decompositions. This implies, in particular, that the largest growth rate of a suffix state
is the same as the largest growth rate

of a prefix state, and the maximum number of

of dominant blocks
in a prefix decomposition.

Let
p=(p_{1},\dotsc,p_{f})
be a prefix state of a word

, and let

be a

. Recall that

is the length of the forbidden word

. We say that the two states

and

overlap

, we have

. The inequality

holds if and only if

. In particular,

and

{\mathcal{C}}
.
An invalid prefix state overlaps with any suffix state and vice versa. If

and

do not

.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
X
be a word with

dominant loop blocks in its prefix decomposition, and let

be a word
with

dominant loop blocks in its suffix decomposition. If

is in

, then

.

Proof.

If
d_{X}=0
or
d_{Y}=0
, the claim follows trivially, so assume that
d_{X}
and
d_{Y}
are both
positive.

Let
T_{X}
be the prefix transition path of
X
and
T_{Y}
the suffix transition path of
Y
. Let

{\mathcal{C}}_{n}(T_{X})
be the subset of
{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
of those words that have prefix transition path

, and

{\mathcal{C}}^{\leftarrow}_{n}(T_{Y})
be the subset of
{\mathcal{C}}_{n}

, while words in

. This means that a concatenation of a word from

On the other hand, we know that

Let us say that a prefix state

For a prefix state

Lemma 4.8
.

If

.

Suppose for contradiction that

b\in{\mathcal{L}}_{p}\setminus{\mathcal{L}}^{\leftarrow}_{s}

with prefix state
p
and a word

with

. Since

and

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
p\in{\mathcal{P}}
and
s\in{\mathcal{S}}
be a pair of compatible states with
{\mathcal{L}}_{p}={\mathcal{L}}^{\leftarrow}_{s}
. Then the following
are equivalent:

(I)

The states
p
and
s
do not match.

(II)

The state
p
has a prefix successor compatible with
s
.

(III)

The state
s
has a suffix successor compatible with
p
.

(IV)

There is a symbol
b\in{\mathcal{A}}\setminus{\mathcal{L}}_{p}
such that for any word
X
with prefix state
p

and any word
Y
with suffix state
s
, the word
XbY
is in
{\mathcal{C}}
.

Proof.

If (IV) holds, then the prefix state of
Xb
is a prefix successor of
p
compatible with
s
,
while the suffix state of
bY
is a suffix successor of
s
compatible with
p
, so (II) and (III)
hold as well, and clearly both (II) and (III) implies (I). We also easily see that (II) implies
(IV) and (III) implies (IV), and therefore (II), (III) and (IV) are equivalent. Finally, (I)
implies that (II) or (III) holds, and therefore (I) implies (IV), completing the proof.
∎

Lemma 4.10
.

and

, and that the state

and

Proof.

Suppose that
p
and

do not match. Then, by part (IV) of Lemma

, there is a

such that

is in
{\mathcal{C}}
. Let

be the word obtained by

|{\mathcal{P}}|

. Since

, which is equal to

, we know that

, namely

is compatible with

and

Fix now the constant

; this is because each

An

-th slice

In particular, each

. This means that in the

, as well as in the suffix decomposition of

, the free slice belongs

{\mathcal{C}}_{n}(j)
be the subset of
{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
containing

, which is also equal to the largest number of dominant loop blocks in a suffix

. We say that a word

is contained in a dominant loop block both in the prefix decomposition of

W
.

be the number
of dominant loop blocks in the prefix decomposition of

be the number of

. Then

.

3.

With
X_{j}
and

as above, let

be the prefix state of
X_{j}
and let
s_{j}
be the

. Then

and

match.

4.

Let
{\mathcal{L}}_{j}
be the prefix loop alphabet of the state

.
Then for any word

\max\{4,|{\mathcal{L}}_{j}|\}
over the alphabet

,

contains at least
\sqrt{n}

disjoint

Lemma 4.11
.

A uniformly random word

is typical with probability

.

Proof.

Lemma
4.6
shows that with probability
1-2^{-\Omega(\sqrt{n})}
, the total weight of
non-dominant
loop blocks in

is at most
\sqrt{n}
, and in particular, for

large enough, no slice can be
contained in a single non-dominant loop block. Therefore, any free slice is contained in a dominant
loop block, both in the prefix and in the suffix decomposition.

Let
W_{j}
be a free slice, and let
X_{j}
,
Y_{j}
,
p_{j}
and
s_{j}
be as in the definition of
typical word. Let
{\mathcal{L}}_{j}
be the prefix loop alphabet of

, and let
{\mathcal{L}}^{\leftarrow}_{j}
be the suffix loop

. Define

contains no prefix or suffix transitions,

.

Let us prove that with high probability,
{\mathcal{L}}_{j}={\mathcal{L}}^{\leftarrow}_{j}
. Suppose that this is not the case, and
without loss of generality assume that
{\mathcal{L}}
is a proper subset of
{\mathcal{L}}_{j}
. As we have seen,

. Since

{\mathcal{L}}
is a proper subset of

of

. Let

By Lemma

, the above conditions already imply that

and

match with
probability
1-\mathcal{O}(1/n)
.

To prove the last condition of typicality, choose a uniformly random
W\in{\mathcal{C}}_{n}(j)
for some fixed

j
. Let
W^{\prime}_{j}
be a word over
{\mathcal{L}}^{\ast}
that has the same weight and the same final symbol as

W_{j}
, and define
W^{\prime}=X_{j}W^{\prime}_{j}Y_{j}
. Note that
W^{\prime}
again belongs to
{\mathcal{C}}_{n}(j)
and that
W^{\prime}_{j}
is its

j
-th slice (the reason we require that
W^{\prime}_{j}
has the same final symbol as
W_{j}
is to ensure that
the boundary between the
j
-th and
(j+1)
-st slice is preserved). In particular, for a uniformly
random
W\in{\mathcal{C}}_{n}(j)
, the word obtained from
W_{j}
by removing its last symbol is a uniformly random
word over
{\mathcal{L}}^{\ast}
, that is, any two words from
{\mathcal{L}}^{\ast}
of the same weight are equally likely to
be obtained this way. From Proposition
3.10
, we then deduce that, for
n
large enough
and up to exponentially small probability, for every word
P\in{\mathcal{L}}^{*}
of constant length,
W_{j}
has
at least
\sqrt{n}
disjoint
P
-blocks. Since with probability
1-\mathcal{O}(1/n)
,
{\mathcal{L}}
is
equal to
{\mathcal{L}}_{j}
, the last condition of typicality follows.
∎

We are now almost ready to prove Theorem
4.3
. For the final argument, we will
distinguish two cases. First, we will deal with classes of exponential growth, i.e., those
with
\gamma>1
. Equivalently, those are the classes whose dominant loop alphabets contain more than
one element.

Next, we will handle the classes with
\gamma=1
, i.e., those whose every loop alphabet is either
empty or contains the single symbol
1
. In order to have unbounded growth, such a class must then
satisfy
D>1
.

Proposition 4.12
.

Any permutation class
{\mathcal{C}}
with growth rate greater than 1 and with finitely many
sum-indecomposables exhibits a Wilf collapse.

Proof.

It is enough to show that every typical word
W\in{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
belongs to a Wilf class of size

2^{\Omega(\sqrt{n})}
. We will assume throughout that
n
is large enough in comparison to the
constants
K
,
D
and
Q
.

Illustration of the proof of Proposition
4.12

Choose a typical word
W\in{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
, and let
W_{1}W_{2}\dotsb W_{Q}
be its equitable partition. By the
choice of
Q
, we know that there is an index
j\in\{2,\dotsc,Q-1\}
such that the three slices

W_{j-1}
,
W_{j}
and
W_{j+1}
are all free. Write
W
as
W=XW_{j-1}W_{j}W_{j+1}Y
, with
X=W_{1}\dotsc W_{j-2}
and

; see Figure

. Let
p
be the prefix state of
X

and

the suffix state of
Y
.

By typicality, we know that
p
and

match, and therefore they share a common loop

{\mathcal{L}}
. Moreover,

and

are dominant, and therefore
{\mathcal{L}}^{\ast}
has growth rate

\gamma>1
. It follows that

. Let

be a maximal symbol of
{\mathcal{L}}
in the

Write

contains the symbol

by typicality. In fact,

contains at least

\sqrt{n}-1
disjoint occurrences of

, for

, and

, so that the word

can be written as

.

We claim that if

is Wilf-equivalent to

in the class
{\mathcal{L}}^{\ast}
, then

is Wilf-equivalent to

. To see this, assume that

containing

. We now describe a

{\mathcal{C}}
containing

to those that contain

.

\widehat{W}\in{\mathcal{C}}
be a word that contains

. Let
\widehat{X}
be the shortest prefix of

that contains
X
, and
\widehat{Y}
the shortest suffix of
\widehat{W}

\widehat{W}
between
\widehat{X}
and

, i.e.,

: if

. This is a contradiction, as

is in

.

\widehat{X}^{+}
be the shortest prefix of

. Clearly,
\widehat{X}
is a prefix of

\widehat{X}^{+}
. It is possible that in an embedding of

into
\widehat{X}^{+}
, one or

. However, since

. Symmetrically, let
\widehat{Y}^{+}
be the shortest suffix of
\widehat{W}

containing

. We again conclude that the first symbol of
\widehat{Y}^{+}
is the symbol
a
.

\widehat{p}
be the prefix state of
\widehat{X}^{+}
and
\widehat{s}
the suffix state of
\widehat{Y}^{+}
. We
claim that both these states have loop alphabet

. To see this, let

be a word obtained by

in any order. By typicality,

has at least

\sqrt{n}
disjoint

\sqrt{n}-1

(recall that

has no occurrence of the maximal symbol

). When embedding

X^{+}
into
\widehat{X}^{+}
, at most
K

\widehat{X}
, but for

of these

\widehat{X}^{+}\setminus\widehat{X}
(i.e., the suffix of
\widehat{X}^{+}
that follows after
\widehat{X}
). That means
that
\widehat{X}^{+}\setminus\widehat{X}
contains
Q
disjoint blocks

, each
containing
U
as

are maximal in

, each

must in fact contain the

as a subsequence. Since
\widehat{X}^{+}
has at most

is a

which does not have any state transition. Therefore

is in a loop block of

whose loop alphabet

also all the symbols of

is a dominant loop alphabet, this means that

.
Since all the symbols of
\widehat{X}^{+}
after

, there are no more prefix state

has loop alphabet

, and a

Let
\widehat{Z}
be the part of
\widehat{W}
between
\widehat{X}^{+}
and
\widehat{Y}^{+}
. Since
\widehat{W}

contains

, we conclude that
\widehat{Z}
contains

. Note that here we use the fact that the last

is equal to the last symbol of

and similarly for

into
\widehat{W}
, no symbol from

or the first symbol of

, and in particular

. We

define

and

. Since
\widehat{Z}^{\prime}

contains

contains
W^{\prime}
. The mapping

is easily seen to be the required bijection
from words containing

to words containing

in the class
{\mathcal{C}}
.

To prove the proposition, it now suffices to show that there are many words Wilf-equivalent to

in the class
{\mathcal{L}}^{\ast}
. This, however, can be easily done. Recall that
a
is a maximal symbol of

{\mathcal{L}}
, and let

(here we use that

). The word

contains the

, which, by typicality, contains at least

disjoint block occurrences of

, replacing any such occurrence by a

is maximal in

and

-Wilf class of

-Wilf class of any typical word

.

Let us say that a permutation
{\mathcal{C}}
with finitely many sum-indecomposables is an
unbounded
polynomial class
if its growth rate
\gamma
is equal to 1 and its growth function is unbounded.
For the rest of this section, we will only consider unbounded polynomial classes. Since any such
class
{\mathcal{C}}
has growth rate
1
, it follows that every dominant prefix or suffix state has loop
alphabet
\{1\}
, while every non-dominant state has empty loop alphabet. In particular, any

W\in{\mathcal{C}}
has fewer than
Q
symbols not belonging to dominant loop blocks, and all these symbols
are transition symbols. Consequently, each
{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
has only a bounded number of words that have at
most one dominant loop block in their prefix or suffix decomposition. Since
|{\mathcal{C}}_{n}|
is unbounded,
it follows that
D>1
. Observe that Proposition
4.5
implies that with probability

1-\mathcal{O}(1/n)
, a uniformly random
W\in{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
has
D
dominant loop blocks in both its prefix and its
suffix decomposition.

Let us say that a loop block in the prefix or suffix decomposition of a word
W\in{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
is

large
if it has length (or equivalently weight) at least
2KQ+1
. We say that a letter in a
loop block is
central
if the loop block contains at least
KQ
letters preceding it and also
at least
KQ
letters following it. In particular, each large block has at least one central letter.

Lemma 4.13
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be an unbounded polynomial class. With probability

1-\mathcal{O}(1/n)
, in a uniformly random
W\in{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
, all the dominant loop blocks in the prefix and
suffix decomposition are large.

Proof.

Recall the notation
{\mathcal{C}}_{n}(T,\tau)
from Proposition
4.5
. Let

{\mathcal{C}}_{n}(T,\tau,i,j)
denote the set of those elements of
{\mathcal{C}}_{n}(T,\tau)
whose

i
-th dominant prefix loop block has weight
j
. It follows from the
calculations in the proof of Proposition
4.5
that

{\mathcal{C}}_{n}(T,\tau,i,j)
has size
O(n^{d_{T}-2})
for any fixed
i
and
j
, where

d_{T}
is the number of dominant states in
T
. Summing these contributions over
all
T
,
\tau
,
i\leq D
and
j\leq 2KQ
, we conclude that there are at most

O(n^{D-2})
words in
{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
that have a small dominant loop block.
∎

Lemma 4.14
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be an unbounded polynomial class. Let
W\in{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
be a word
with
k
dominant loop blocks in its prefix decomposition, and suppose that all these loop blocks
are large. Let
\widehat{W}\in{\mathcal{C}}
be a word that contains
W
. Then
\widehat{W}
has at least
k

dominant loop blocks in its prefix decomposition. Moreover, if
\widehat{W}
has exactly
k
dominant
loop blocks, then in every embedding of
W
into
\widehat{W}
, for any dominant loop block
B_{i}
of

W
, all the central symbols of
B_{i}
are mapped to loop symbols of
\widehat{W}
. Analogous properties
hold for suffix decompositions as well.

Proof.

Let
B_{1},\dotsc,B_{k}
be the dominant loop blocks in the prefix decomposition of
W
.
For
i\in[k-1]
, let
t_{i}
be the symbol of
W
immediately following
B_{i}
. Necessarily,
t_{i}
is
different from 1. In particular, for every embedding of
W
into
\widehat{W}
, the symbol
t_{i}
gets
mapped to a transition symbol
\widehat{t}_{i}
of
\widehat{W}
. For
2\leq i\leq k-1
, let
\widehat{W}_{i}
be the
subword of
\widehat{W}
between
\widehat{t}_{i-1}
and
\widehat{t}_{i}
, including the two symbols
\widehat{t}_{i-1}
and
\widehat{t}_{i}
themselves; we also define
\widehat{W}_{1}
as the prefix of
\widehat{W}
ending in

\widehat{t}_{1}
and
\widehat{W}_{k}
as the suffix of
\widehat{W}
starting in
\widehat{t}_{k-1}
. Since
B_{i}
is
mapped into
\widehat{W}_{i}
and
B_{i}
is large,
\widehat{W}_{i}
has at least
2Q+1
symbols, and therefore
each
\widehat{W}_{i}
has a loop symbol. Since each
\widehat{W}_{i}
begins or ends with a transition symbol,

\widehat{W}
has at least
k
nonempty loop blocks, which are necessarily dominant.

Suppose now that
\widehat{W}
has exactly
k
dominant loop blocks. It follows that each
\widehat{W}_{i}

contains a unique dominant loop block
\widehat{B}_{i}
of
\widehat{W}
. Therefore, all the symbols of
\widehat{W}_{i}
except perhaps the leftmost
Q
and the rightmost
Q
belong to the loop block
\widehat{B}_{i}
.
Since the block
B_{i}
is mapped into
\widehat{W}_{i}
, all its central symbols must get mapped into

\widehat{B}_{i}
.
∎

Proposition 4.15
.

Any unbounded polynomial class
{\mathcal{C}}
exhibits a Wilf collapse.

Proof.

Choose a uniformly random word
W\in{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
. With probability at least
1-\mathcal{O}(1/n)
, the word is
typical, has
D
dominant loop blocks in both the prefix and the suffix decomposition, and each of

has a sequence of three slices

that are all contained in a single prefix loop block

. We may assume,
without loss of generality, that

is not the rightmost dominant prefix loop block of

:
if

were the rightmost dominant loop block, we would consider suffix decompositions
instead of prefix ones, and apply the following argument symmetrically.

4.15

Let

be the prefix of

that contains all the symbols preceding

and the first

symbols of

. See Figure

. Let

be the suffix of

be the sequence of symbols of

. Note that for

, and in particular

be the number of dominant loop blocks in the prefix decomposition of

, and let

is not the rightmost dominant prefix loop block, we get

,

. Let

, with

Let

be the length of

and

the length of

. Note that

and

. Let us now fix a value

, and let

be the word obtained from

by removing

symbols from the block

and inserting these

in the class

, implying that

.

. Let

, let

containing

and those containing

.

and

are indeed equivalent in

, and the Wilf class of

. It follows that

exhibits a Wilf collapse.

Propositions
4.12
and

together establish Theorem

.

5
Concluding remarks

We have demonstrated that Wilf collapse occurs in a wide variety of permutation classes. As
mentioned in the introduction, the only ingredients we seem to need to trigger such a collapse are
a form of greedy embedding for detecting permutation involvement, together with a representation in
terms of words that combines with the greedy embedding to allow for local symmetries
that guarantee Wilf equivalence.

A notable example where our methods of establishing Wilf collapse fail is
\operatorname{Av}(321)
– the
class of permutations containing no occurrence of a 321 pattern. In
[
7
]
(see also

[
3
]
) a greedy approach to detecting involvement is described in this class but the
complexity of the ways in which sum-indecomposable permutations can be combined here (along perhaps
with the failure of super-criticality) have stymied our attempts to prove a Wilf collapse in

\operatorname{Av}(321)
. Furthermore, empirical evidence for this class suggests that if a collapse does occur it
is far less “robust” than we see in our other examples – the largest observed Wilf classes are
those containing the permutations of the form
(d+1)(d+2)\cdots n\,12\cdots d
(and some others)
previously considered in
[
10
,
11
]
.

A related permutation class, the class of “skew-merged” permutations (permutations that can be
written as the merge of a decreasing and an increasing subsequence) has none of the nice closure
properties that we might hope for, but again the existence of a greedy algorithm for pattern
detection and an underlying structure of “spirals” might yield a Wilf collapse.

Question 5.1
.

Does
\operatorname{Av}(321)
have a Wilf collapse? Does the class of skew-merged permutations?
