Family of attainable geometric quantum speed limits
Zi-yi Mai, Zheng Liu, Chang-shui Yu

Abstract
We propose a quantum state distance and develop a family of geometrical quantum speed limits (QSLs) for open and closed systems. The family of the QSL time includes an adjustable scalar function, by which we derive three QSL times with particularly chosen functions in this paper. Two of the derived QSL times are exactly the ones presented in Ref. [ 1 ] and [ 2 ] , respectively, and the third one can provide a unified QSL time for both open and closed systems. The three QSL times are attainable for any given initial state in the sense that dynamics exist, driving the initial state to evolve along the geodesic. We numerically compare the tightness of the three QSL times, which typically promises a tighter QSL time if optimizing the function.

The essence of QSL is a time optimization problem subject to some constraints, which has different understandings corresponding to various scenarios

For example, suppose an initial state and an evolution distance are given; the QSL time (QSLT) describes the deviation degree of evolution trajectory driven by the dynamics from the geodesic connecting the initial state and the final state. In this paper, we establish a family of attainable geometrical QSLTs for open systems based on our developed distance measure of quantum states, which, as a vital ingredient of the QSLT, is constructed by redefining the geometry of quantum states. Namely, it can be saturated by both the unitary and nonunitary dynamics. It reveals that both the unitary and nonunitary dynamics can drive the two-level system to evolve along the geodesics, which can be seen as the optimal evolution trajectory connecting two points. All three QSLTs are attainable for open systems because any initial state can evolve along the geodesic, as shown in FIG.

We first present the state map, define the distance of density matrices, and then establish a family of the function dependent QSLTs. Then, we explicitly study three QSLTs by choosing a particular function and demonstrating attainability through specific dynamics. Finally, we take numerical examples of dynamics to discuss the QSLT’s tightness.

The distance and the geometric QSL

The distance.-
To begin with, we will construct a
N
-dimensional metric space for the quantum state similar to the classical Bloch representation.
Let
f(x)
be a function of scalar
x\in[\frac{1}{N},1]
such that
f(x)\geq x
,
\frac{d}{dx}f(x)\leq 1
and
\sum_{i=1}^{2}\left|f(x_{i})-x_{i}\right|\neq 0
for
x_{1}\neq x_{2}
. For convenience, we denote the set consisting of all the functions
f
satisfying the above constraints as
\mathcal{F}
. Let
\mathcal{M}
denote the set of all the Hermitian matrices and
\mathcal{D}
denote the set of all the positive density matrices. Given a density matrix
\rho\in\mathcal{D}
, define a map
F:\mathcal{D}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
as

F(\rho)=\begin{cases}\frac{\mathbb{I}}{\sqrt{N}},&\mbox{if }\rho=\frac{\mathbb{I}}{N}\\
\frac{\rho+(\sqrt{N}\sqrt{f(Tr\rho^{2})-\mathrm{Tr}\rho^{2}}-1)\mathbb{I}/N}{\sqrt{f(Tr\rho^{2})-1/N}},&\mbox{otherwise},\end{cases}

(1)

where
\mathbb{I}
is the identity operator.
It is obvious that for a certain function
f(x)\in\mathcal{F}
,
F(\rho)
is uniqulely determined for a given
\rho
. It’s not difficult to verify that
F(\rho)
is continuous.
Consider the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product, i.e.,
\langle A,B\rangle=TrA^{\dagger}B=TrAB
for any pair of Hermitian matrices
A,B
.
One can find that
-1\leq\langle F(\rho),F(\sigma)\rangle\leq 1
for any pair of density matrices
\rho
and
\sigma
and
\langle F(\rho),F(\sigma)\rangle=1

\Leftrightarrow

\rho=\sigma
. Thus, we can define the distance of two density matrices as follows.

Definition 1.-
Given any two density matrices
\rho
and
\sigma
, their distance is defined as

D(\rho,\sigma)=\arccos\langle F(\rho),F(\sigma)\rangle

(2)

with
D(\rho,\sigma)=0
iff
\rho=\sigma
.

The above map indicates that each density matrix is mapped to a unit sphere in the Hermite matrix space
\mathcal{M}
equipped with a Hilbert Schmidt norm. We have proved that this mapping
F
is injective in Appendix B, so
F
essentially identifies each density operator using a unique point on the unit sphere. Then, the distance between the two density operators can be defined as the distance between the corresponding points on the sphere, which happens to be Eq. (
2
). Therefore, Eq. (
2
) is a well-defined distance metric. Fig.
2
can provide an intuitive understanding of the metric space. It can be seen that
\left\langle\frac{\mathbb{I}}{N},F(\rho)\right\rangle=\frac{\sqrt{f(\mathrm{Tr}\rho^{2})-\mathrm{Tr}\rho^{2}}}{\sqrt{N}\sqrt{f(\mathrm{Tr}\rho^{2})-\frac{1}{N}}}
, the intersection angle of
\mathbb{I}/N
and
F(\rho)
is entirely determined by the purity of
\rho
. Considering the function
f(x)\in\mathcal{F}
, it is equal to
\langle\frac{\mathbb{I}}{N},F(\rho)\rangle
is monotonically decreasing with respective to the purity of the density matrix, i.e., the density matrix with less mixedness shows larger intersection angle with the maximally mixed state. In this sense, the metric space
F(\mathcal{D})
is a unit spherical cap whose north pole is the maximally mixed state and whose latitudes consist of the density matrices with the same purity. The function
f\in\mathcal{F}
is used to adjust the distribution of the latitudes with different purities. For example of the unitary dynamics, let
f(x)=\mathrm{Tr}\rho_{0}^{2}
as a constant for a fixed
\rho_{0}

\mathrm{Tr}\rho_{0}^{2}

The QSLT.-
Now let’s consider a density matrix
\rho_{t}
at the moment
t
evolves an infinitesimal duration
dt
, the final state can be given as
\sigma=\rho_{t}+d\rho_{t}
with

d\rho_{t}=dt\dot{\rho}_{t}
determined by its dynamics. The distance between the initial state
\rho_{t}
and the final state
\rho_{t+dt}
can be written as
ds=D(\rho_{t},\rho_{t}+d{\rho}_{t})
, or equivalently,

\begin{split}&\cos D(\rho_{t},\rho_{t}+d{\rho}_{t})=1-\frac{1}{2}ds^{2}\\
=&\frac{\mathrm{Tr}\rho_{t}(\rho_{t}+d\rho_{t})-1/N+\sqrt{f_{t}-\mathrm{Tr}\rho_{t}^{2}}\sqrt{f_{t+dt}-\mathrm{Tr}\rho_{t+dt}^{2}}}{\sqrt{f_{t}-1/N}\sqrt{f_{t+dt}-1/N}},\end{split}

(3)

where
f_{t}=f(\mathrm{Tr}\rho_{t}^{2})
.
One can always expand the function
f_{t+dt}=f(Tr(\rho_{t}+d\rho_{t})^{2})
to the second order as

f_{t+dt}=f_{t}+2(f^{\prime})_{t}Tr\rho_{t}d\rho_{t}+(f^{\prime})_{t}Tr(d\rho_{t})^{2}+{2(f^{\prime\prime})_{t}(Tr\rho_{t}d\rho_{t})^{2}},

(4)

where
(f^{\prime})_{t}
and
(f^{\prime\prime})_{t}
denotes the first-order derivative
\frac{df(x)}{dx}\Big|_{x=\mathrm{Tr}\rho_{t}^{2}}
and the second-order derivative
\frac{d^{2}f(x)}{dx^{2}}\Big|_{x=\mathrm{Tr}\rho_{t}^{2}}
, respectively.
Similarly, one can find that

\frac{1}{\sqrt{f_{t+dt}-1/N}}=\frac{1-\frac{\Delta}{2(f_{t}-1/N)}+\frac{3\Delta^{2}}{8(f_{t}-1/N)^{2}}}{\sqrt{f_{t}-1/N}}

(5)

and

\frac{\sqrt{f_{t+dt}-Tr(\rho_{t}+d\rho_{t})^{2}}}{\sqrt{f_{t}-Tr\rho_{t}^{2}}}=1+\frac{\delta\left(1-\frac{\delta}{4}\right)}{2(f_{t}-Tr\rho_{t}^{2})},

(6)

where
\Delta=f_{t+dt}-f_{t}
and
\delta=\Delta-\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_{t+dt}^{2}-\rho_{t}^{2})
are both determined by Eq. (
3
).
Substituting Eqs. (
4
,
5
,
6

3

ds=\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(d\rho_{t}^{2})+\left(\frac{(f^{\prime})_{t}\left(2-3(f^{\prime})_{t}\right)}{f_{t}-\frac{1}{N}}+\frac{\left((f^{\prime})_{t}-1\right)^{2}}{f_{t}-\mathrm{Tr}\rho_{t}^{2}}\right)(\mathrm{Tr}\rho_{t}d\rho_{t})^{2}}{f_{t}-\frac{1}{N}}}.

(7)

Consider a dynamic evolution of a density matrix
\dot{\rho}_{t}
from an initial state
\rho_{0}
to the final state
\rho_{\tau}

D(\rho_{0},\rho_{\tau})\leq\int_{0}^{\tau}ds
. Define the average evolution speed as
\bar{v}=\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{0}^{\tau}{ds}
, then the QSLT can be given as

\displaystyle\tau_{qsl}^{f}=\frac{D(\rho_{0},\rho_{\tau})}{\bar{v}}=\frac{D(\rho_{0},\rho_{\tau})}{\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{0}^{\tau}\frac{ds}{dt}dt},

(8)

where

\frac{ds}{dt}=\sqrt{\frac{\mathcal{V}_{t}^{2}+\left(\frac{(f^{\prime})_{t}\left(2-3(f^{\prime})_{t}\right)}{f_{t}-\frac{1}{N}}+\frac{\left((f^{\prime})_{t}-1\right)^{2}}{f_{t}-\mathcal{P}_{t}}\right)(\frac{1}{2}\dot{\mathcal{P}}_{t})^{2}}{f_{t}-\frac{1}{N}}}

(9)

with
\mathcal{V}_{t}=\sqrt{\mathrm{Tr}(\dot{\rho}_{t}^{2})}
and
\mathcal{P}_{t}=\mathrm{Tr}\rho_{t}^{2}
, and
the superscript
f\in\mathcal{F}
denotes the dependence of
f
.

Eq. (
8
) is one of our main results. It can be seen that the evolution speed Eq. (
9
) depends on the quantities
\mathcal{V}_{t}
and the derivative of purity
\dot{\mathcal{P}}_{t}
.
\dot{\mathcal{P}}_{t}
is the changing rate of the purity of the quantum state, which is relevant to the strength of decoherence
[

\mathcal{V}_{t}
is the speed or the strength of the generator of

[

[

44

43

\dot{\rho}_{t}=\mathcal{L}\rho_{t}

\mathrm{Vec}(\dot{\rho}_{t})=\mathrm{Vec}(\mathcal{L}\rho_{t})=\mathfrak{L}\mathrm{Vec}(\rho)
with
\mathrm{Vec}(M)
denoting the vectorization of the matrix
M

\mathcal{V}_{t}=\left|\mathrm{Vec}(\dot{\rho}_{t})\right|\leq|\mathfrak{L}|\cdot\left|\mathrm{Vec}(\rho_{t})\right|\leq\left|\mathfrak{L}\right|,

(10)

where
\left|\cdot\right|
is the Fubinius norm of the matrix or the vector norm. So

is bounded by the characteristics of the system dynamics.

We want to emphasize that even though the QSLT depends on the function
f(\cdot)\in\mathcal{F}
, any given valid function

induces a valid QSLT. In addition, the optimal QSLT
\tau_{qsl}^{opt}
can also be naturally defined by the maximization over all potential functions

\tau_{qsl}^{opt}=\max_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\tau_{qsl}^{f}.

(11)

\tau_{uni}^{p_{0}}={\arccos\frac{\mathrm{Tr}\rho_{0}\rho_{\tau}-\frac{1}{N}}{\mathrm{Tr}\rho_{0}^{2}-\frac{1}{N}}}\biggl/\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{0}^{\tau}\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{Tr}\dot{\rho}_{t}^{2}}{\mathrm{Tr}\rho_{0}^{2}-\frac{1}{N}}},

We first emphasize that the attainability of

Let’s consider the pure depolarizing dynamics, i.e.,
\rho_{t}=p_{t}\rho_{0}+\left(1-p_{t}\right)\frac{\mathbb{I}}{N}
, where
p_{t}\in[0,1]

remains unchanged with a probability of

, and with a probability of
1-p_{t}

\dot{\rho}_{t}=\dot{p}_{t}(\rho_{0}-\frac{\mathbb{I}}{N}),

Tightness.-
Tightness is also an important index in QSLT. It is also closely related to understanding the QSLT. As mentioned above, our proposed three QSLTs are all attainable for any given initial state in the sense of evolution along the geodesic, as shown in FIG.
1
(a); thus, the tightness is meaningless. However, our QSLTs can also serve as the lower bound of the QSLT in the sense of given initial and final states; in this case, our QSLTs aren’t shown to be always attainable for any given pair of initial and final states, which implies the tightness is required to consider.
In the case of given initial and final states in open systems, as shown in FIG.
1
(c), the QSLT depends on the infinitely many evolution trajectories for a given pair of initial and final states. The QSLT could be attainable only for certain initial states or dynamics. Therefore, it is challenging to evaluate the tightness of a QSLT accurately.

From a different perspective, if one could find one QSLT is always more significant than the other for any allowed dynamics, the larger QSLT is tighter than the smaller one, even though neither is attainable. Of course, combining both bounds can provide a tighter one if two QSLTs present inconsistent forms for different trajectories. Even if the QSLT was attainable for any given pair of initial and final states, evaluating a QSLT for given dynamics can at least reveal how the evolution is close (tight) to the optimal (attainable) trajectory, besides whether the dynamics saturate the QSLT. In this sense, we will study the tightness of QSLT mainly by evaluating the QSLT subject to some dynamics with given initial states.

As mentioned, the tightness of QSLTs depends on the evolution trajectories; they’re comparable only when the trajectory is given, except they share the same form of the speed, i.e., the denominator of QSLTs; this point of view is also supported by Kavan Modi et al.
[
1
]

Discussion and conclusion

This paper established a family of quantum speed limits with a function. Different choices of the function can induce different QSLTs. We considered three functions and obtained three QSLTs. We reveal that two of these QSLTs align precisely with those previously presented in the works, and the third one emerges as a unified measure applicable to both open and closed quantum systems. All three QSLTs are practically achievable for any given initial quantum state, meaning that a dynamical process can steer the initial state to evolve along the corresponding geodesic path in the quantum state space.
In fact, for different dynamics, other optimization functions
f
could induce the tightest bound on time within our family of QSL. However, finding the optimal function
f
is a complex functional optimization problem, and it is generally difficult to obtain an analytical solution. Interestingly, for the unitary evolution case, the optimization on
f
is analytically solved, which shows the optimal one is precisely the QSLT presented in Ref.
[
29
]
. According to the attainability of
\tau_{qsl}^{f_{3}}
, there are always the optimal unitary and non-unitary dynamics saturating the QSL bound for the two-dimensional case. However, we have only found the optimal non-unitary dynamics for higher dimensional cases for any initial state. Whether a so-called optimal unitary dynamics exists to saturate the QSLT bound remains an open problem.
