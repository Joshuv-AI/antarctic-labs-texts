Rings whose non-invertible elements are uniquely strongly clean
Peter Danchev, Omid Hasanzadeh, Ahmad Moussavi

Abstract
. We define and explore in details the class of GUSC rings, that are those rings whose non-invertible elements are uniquely strongly clean. These rings are a common generalization of the so-called USC rings, introduced by Chen-Wang-Zhou in J. Pure & Appl. Algebra (2009), which are rings whose elements are uniquely strongly clean. These rings also generalize the so-called GUC rings, defined by Guo-Jiang in Bull. Transilvania Univ. Braşov (2023), which are rings whose non-invertible elements are uniquely clean.

1.
Introduction and Basic Concepts

Everywhere in the current paper, let
R
be an associative but
not
necessarily commutative ring having identity element, usually stated as
1
. Standardly, for such a ring
R
, the letters
U(R)
,
\rm{Nil}(R)
and
{\rm Id}(R)
are designed for the set of invertible elements (also termed as the unit group of
R
), the set of nilpotent elements and the set of idempotent elements in
R
, respectively. Likewise,
J(R)
denotes the Jacobson radical of
R
, and
{\rm Z}(R)
denotes the center of
R
. We also write
ucn(R)
for the set of all uniquely clean elements of
R
. The ring of
n\times n
matrices over
R
and the ring of
n\times n
upper triangular matrices over
R
are denoted by
{\rm M}_{n}(R)
and
{\rm T}_{n}(R)
, respectively. Standardly, a ring is said to be
abelian
if each of its idempotents is central, that is,
{\rm Id}(R)\subseteq{\rm Z}(R)
.

For all other unexplained explicitly notions and notations, we refer to the classical source
[
6
]
or to the cited in the bibliography research sources. For instance, consulting with
[
5
, Definition 3.4]
, we set

ucn_{0}(R)=\{e+j:e^{2}=e\in{\rm Z}(R),j\in J(R)\}.

In order to present our achievements here, we now need the necessary background material as follows: Mimicking
[
4
]
, an element
a
from a ring
R
is called
clean
if there exists
e\in{\rm Id}(R)
such that
a-e\in U(R)
. Then,
R
is said to be
clean
if each element of
R
is clean. In addition,
a
is called
strongly clean
provided
ae=ea
and, if each element of
R
are strongly clean, then
R
is said to
strongly clean
too.
On the other hand, imitating
[
10
]
,
a\in R
is called
uniquely clean
if there exists a unique
e\in{\rm Id}(R)
such that
a-e\in U(R)
. In particular, a ring
R
is said to be
uniquely clean
(or just
UC
for short) if every element in
R
is uniquely clean.

Generalizing these notions, in
[
1
]
was defined an element
a\in R
to be
uniquely strongly clean
if there exists a unique
e\in{\rm Id}(R)
such that
a-e\in U(R)
and
ae=ea
. In particular, a ring
R
is
uniquely strongly clean
(or just
USC
for short) if each element in
R
is uniquely strongly clean.

In a similar vein, expanding the first part of the above concepts, in
[
3
]
a ring is called a
generalized uniquely clean
ring (or just
GUC
for short) if any non-invertible element is uniquely clean.

Our work targets to extend the last definition by defining and exploring the following key instruments: A ring is called a
generalized uniquely strongly clean
ring (or just
GUSC
for short) if every non-invertible element is uniquely strongly clean.

2.
Examples and Main Results

We begin by marking the following simple but useful observations.

Example 2.1
.

(i)

Any UC ring is USC, but the converse is
not
true in general – in fact, the ring
{\rm T}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{2})
is USC that is
not
uniquely clean. However, USC rings are strongly clean.

(ii)

Any UC ring is GUC, but the converse is
not
true in general – indeed, the ring
\mathbb{Z}_{5}
is GUC that is
not
uniquely clean.

(iii)

Any USC ring is GUSC, but the converse is
not
true in general – in fact, the ring
\mathbb{Z}_{(3)}=\left\{\dfrac{a}{b}~\Big|~a,b\in\mathbb{Z},(3,b)=1\right\}
is GUSC that is
not
USC.

(iv)

Any GUC ring is GUSC, but the converse is
not
true in general – indeed, the ring
{\rm T}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{2})
is GUSC being USC that is
not
GUC.

(v)

Any GUSC ring is strongly clean, but the converse is
not
true in general. For example, for an arbitrary prime number
p
, let
\hat{\mathbb{Z}_{p}}
be the ring of
p
-adic integers. In view of
[
2
, Theorem 2.4]
,
{\rm M}_{2}(\hat{\mathbb{Z}}_{p})
is strongly clean that is
not
USC by virtue of
[
1
, Corollary 7]
. Also, a plain check shows that it is
not
too GUSC.

It is also clear that a ring
R
is GUC if, and only if,
R
is abelian GUSC.

We, furthermore, continue with a series of helpful technicalities.

Lemma 2.2
.

An element
a
in a ring
R
is USC if, and only if, so is
1-a
.

Proof.

Straightforward.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

If
R
is a GUSC ring, then
R
is strongly clean.

Proof.

Letting
a\in R
, so
a\in U(R)
or
a\notin U(R)
. If
a\in U(R)
, then
a
is known to be a strongly clean element. If, however,
a\notin U(R)
, then by assumption
a
is uniquely strongly clean element, and thus
a
is a strongly clean element. Finally, in both cases,
R
is strongly clean, as asserted.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

If
\displaystyle\prod_{i\in I}R_{i}
is GUSC, then each
R_{i}
is GUSC.

Proof.

Let
a_{i}\in R_{i}
, where
a_{i}\notin U(R_{i})
, whence
(1,1,\ldots,a_{i},1,\ldots)\notin U(\prod R_{i})
. So,
(1,1,\ldots,a_{i},1,\ldots)
is USC and hence
a_{i}
is USC. If, however, some
a_{i}
is not USC, then we have

a_{i}=e_{i}+u_{i}=f_{i}+v_{i},

where
e_{i}u_{i}=u_{i}e_{i}
,
f_{i}v_{i}=v_{i}f_{i}
,

u_{i},v_{i}\in U(R_{i})
and
e_{i},f_{i}\in Id(R_{i})
. Therefore,

(1,1,\ldots,a_{i},1,\ldots)=(0,0,\ldots,e_{i},0,\ldots)+(1,1,\ldots,u_{i},1,\ldots)=

=(0,0,\ldots,f_{i},0,\ldots)+(1,1,\ldots,v_{i},1,\ldots),

where

\displaystyle(0,0,\ldots,e_{i},0,\ldots)(1,1,\ldots,u_{i},1,\ldots)=(1,1,\ldots,u_{i},1,\ldots)(0,0,\ldots,e_{i},0,\ldots),

\displaystyle(0,0,\ldots,f_{i},0,\ldots)(1,1,\ldots,v_{i},1,\ldots)=(1,1,\ldots,v_{i},1,\ldots)(0,0,\ldots,f_{i},0,\ldots),

\displaystyle(0,0,\ldots,e_{i},0,\ldots),(0,0,\ldots,f_{i},0,\ldots)\in Id(\prod R_{i}),

\displaystyle(1,1,\ldots,u_{i},1,\ldots),(1,1,\ldots,v_{i},1,\ldots)\in U(\prod R_{i}).

That is why, we have two different strongly clean decompositions for
(1,1,\ldots,a_{i},1,\ldots)
, and so this is a contradiction, as pursued.
∎

We note that the converse of Lemma
2.4
is manifestly false. For example, both
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
and
\mathbb{Z}_{3}
are GUSC. But the direct product
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{3}
is
not
GUSC, because the element
(0,2)
is
not
invertible in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{3}
and
(0,2)=(1,0)+(1,2)=(1,1)+(1,1)
are two different strongly clean decompositions of
(0,2)
in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{3}
, so that
(0,2)
is really
not
uniquely strongly clean element, as required.

Proposition 2.5
.

The direct product
\displaystyle\prod_{i\in I}R_{i}
is GUSC if, and only if, each
R_{i}
is uniquely strongly clean.

Proof.

Letting each
R_{i}
be USC, then
\prod R_{i}
is USC employing
[
1
, Example 2]
and hence
\prod R_{i}
is trivially GUSC.

Conversely, assume that
\prod R_{i}
is GUSC and that
R_{j}
is
not
USC for some index
j\in I
. Then, there exists
a\in R_{j}
which is
not
USC. Hence,
(0,\ldots,0,a,0,\ldots)
is
not
USC in
\prod R_{i}
. But, it is readily seen that
(0,\ldots,0,a,0,\ldots)
is
not
invertible in
\prod R_{i}
and thus, by hypothesis,
(0,\ldots,0,a,0,\ldots)
is
not
USC, a contradiction. Therefore, each
R_{i}
is USC, as needed.
∎

As a consequence, we derive:

Let

be a ring and

R
is GUSC, then so is

. In addition, if

is non-trivial, then

Proof.

Let
R
be GUSC. We have
R=eRe\oplus(1-e)R(1-e)
. Hence,

is GUSC applying Lemma

. If
e
is
not

eRe
is USC utilizing Proposition

, as claimed.
∎

Example 2.7
.

If
R
is a local ring, then
R
is GUSC.

Proof.

Let
a\in R
and
a\notin U(R)
. So,
a=1+(a-1)
is the only strongly clean decomposition for

, and thus
a
is uniquely strongly clean, as stated.
∎

The converse of the Example

is false. For example, it is clear that
{\rm T}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{2})
is GUSC but is obviously

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
R
be a ring. Then, the following are equivalent:

(i)

R
is a local ring.

(ii)

R
is a GUSC ring and
{\rm Id}(R)=\{0,1\}
.

(iii)

R
is a strongly clean ring and
{\rm Id}(R)=\{0,1\}
.

(iv)

R
is a clean ring and
{\rm Id}(R)=\{0,1\}
.

(v)

R
is an exchange ring and
{\rm Id}(R)=\{0,1\}
.

(vi)

R
is a GUC ring and
{\rm Id}(R)=\{0,1\}
.

Proof.

(1)\Rightarrow(2)
. It is obvious owing to Example
2.7
.

(2)\Rightarrow(3)
. It is clear according to Lemma
2.3
.

(3)\Rightarrow(4)\Rightarrow(5)\Rightarrow(1)
. Evident.

(1)\Rightarrow(6)
. It is apparent that a similar proof to that of Example
2.7
works.

(6)\Rightarrow(1)
. Let
a\in R
, where
a\notin U(R)
. It suffices to show that
a-1\in U(R)
. To this goal, by hypothesis,
a
is uniquely clean, so we have
a=e+u
, where
e\in{rmId}(R)=\{0,1\}
and
u\in U(R)
. If
e=0
, it must be that
a=u\in U(R)
, a contradiction. If
e=1
, we obtain
a=1+u
and hence
a-1=u\in U(R)
, as desired.
∎

Proposition 2.11
.

Suppose
R
is an abelian ring and
I
is an ideal of
R
, where
I\subseteq J(R)
:

(i)

\dfrac{R}{I}
is a GUSC ring if, and only if,
R
is a GUSC ring.

(ii)

\dfrac{R}{J(R)}
is a GUSC ring if, and only if,
R
is a GUSC ring.

Proof.

(i)

Let
\dfrac{R}{I}
be a GUSC ring and
a\in R
, where
a\notin U(R)
. Write
a=e+u=f+v
, where
eu=ue
,
fv=vf
,
e,f\in{\rm Id}(R)
and
u,v\in U(R)
. We have to show that
e=f
. On the other hand, we write

a+I=(e+I)+(u+I)=(f+I)+\quad(v+I),

where

(e+I)(u+I)=eu+I=ue+I=(u+I)(e+I),

(f+I)(v+I)=fv+I=vf+I=(v+I)(f+I),

e+I,f+I\in{\rm Id}\left(\dfrac{R}{I}\right)
and
u+I,v+I\in U\left(\dfrac{R}{I}\right)
. By hypothesis,
a+I
is a USC element (as
a\in U(R)
, so
a+I\notin U(\dfrac{R}{I})
). Therefore,
e+I=f+I
, so
e-f\in I\subseteq J(R)
. Since
R
is abelian, we have
(e-f)^{3}=(e-f)
, so
e-f=0
and hence
e=f
.

Conversely, let
R
be a GUSC ring and
a+I\in\dfrac{R}{I}
, where
a+I\notin U\left(\dfrac{R}{I}\right)
. Write

a+I=(e+I)+(u+I)=\quad(f+I)+(v+I),

where

(e+I)(u+I)=(u+I)(e+I),(f+I)(v+I)=\quad(v+I)(f+I),

e+I,f+I\in{\rm Id}\left(\dfrac{R}{I}\right)
and
u+I,v+I\in U\left(\dfrac{R}{I}\right)
. Then, we have
a-(e+u)\in I\subseteq J(R)
, so
a=e+(u+j)
for
j\in J(R)
. Similarly, we have
a=f+(v+j^{\prime})
for
j^{\prime}\in J(R)
and know that
e,f\in{\rm Id}(R)
(because every GUSC ring is clean and hence idempotents lifts modulo
I\subseteq J(R)
) and
u+j
,
v+j^{\prime}\in U(R)
(because
U(R)+J(R)=U(R)
holds always for every ring
R
). Also,
e(u+j)=(u+j)e
and
f(v+j^{\prime})=(v+j^{\prime})f
hold, since
R
is abelian. And as
a
is a USC element, we deduce
e=f
. Consequently,
e+I=f+I
, as expected.

(ii)

The proof is similar to (1).

∎

Example 2.13
.

The ring
{\rm M}_{n}(R)
is never GUSC for any
n\geq 2
.

Proof.

Assuming in a way of contradiction that
{\rm M}_{n}(R)
is GUSC, then in virtue of Lemma
2.9
it is either local or USC. First, if
{\rm M}_{n}(R)
is local, then
{\rm Id}\left(M_{n}(R)\right)=\{0,1\}
which is a contradiction, because the matrix

\begin{pmatrix}1&0&\ldots&0\\
0&0&\ldots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\ldots&0\end{pmatrix}

is an idempotent in
{\rm M}_{n}(R)
that is not equal to
0,1
.

If, however,
{\rm M}_{n}(R)
is USC, then
1_{M_{n}(R)}
is not
2
-good in accordance with
[
1
, Lemma 6]
and this is again a contradiction, because we have always

\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
-1&1\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{in}\quad M_{2}(R).

Thus,
{\rm M}_{n}(R)
is
not
GUSC for every
n\geq 2
, as formulated.
∎

A ring
R
is called
left quasi-duo
(resp.,
right quasi-duo
) if every maximal left ideal (resp., maximal right ideal) of

Proposition 2.16
.

If
R
is a GUSC ring, then
R
is simultaneously left and right quasi-duo.

Proof.

If
R
is a GUSC ring, then
R
is either local or USC by Lemma
2.9
. Now, if
R
is local, then
R
has a unique maximal ideal. Thus,
R
is left and right quasi-duo.

Let us now
R
be USC and
I
a maximal left ideal in
R
. Since
\bar{R}=\dfrac{R}{J(R)}
is Boolean by
[
1
, Corollary 18]
, we have
\dfrac{\bar{R}}{\bar{I}}\cong\mathbb{Z}_{2}
. But then
\dfrac{R}{I}\cong\dfrac{\bar{R}}{\bar{I}}
has two elements, so that
\dfrac{R}{I}=\{I,1+I\}
, whence we have
R=I\cup(1+I)
. Now, given
x\in I
and

, we must show that
xr\in I
. In fact, this is clear if
r\in I
; otherwise,
r=1+y
and

. So,
xr=x+xy\in I
, as required.
∎

A ring
R
is called
semi-potent
if every one-sided ideal
not
contained in
J(R)
contains a non-zero idempotent.

Proposition 2.17
.

Let
R
be a ring. Then,
\dfrac{R}{J(R)}
is GUSC if, and only if,
\dfrac{R}{J(R)}
is GUC.

Proof.

The sufficiency is quite easy.

For the necessity, suppose
\dfrac{R}{J(R)}
is GUSC. So,
\bar{R}=\dfrac{R}{J(R)}
is semi-potent. Then,
[
7
, Theorem 2.1]
applies to get that there exists
\bar{e}\in{\rm Id}(\bar{R})
such that
\bar{e}\bar{R}\bar{e}\cong{\rm M}_{2}(S)
for a non-trivial ring

. We, however, know that by Example

{\rm M}_{2}(S)
is
not
GUSC, so
\bar{e}\bar{R}\bar{e}
is
not
GUSC, but this is a contradiction taking into account Corollary

. Hence,
\bar{R}
is reduced, so it is abelian and, therefore,
\bar{R}
is GUC, as required.

Proposition 2.22
.

If
R
is a GUSC ring, then for every non-invertible element
a\in R
, there exists a unique idempotent
e\in R
such that
ea=ae
and
a-e\in J(R)
.

Proof.

One sees from Corollary
2.10
that
\dfrac{R}{J(R)}
is either a boolean ring or a division ring. Assume first that
\dfrac{R}{J(R)}
is boolean. Letting
a\in R
, where
a\notin U(R)
, there exists a unique idempotent
e\in R
such that
u:=a-e\in U(R)
and
ae=ea
. As
\dfrac{R}{J(R)}
is boolean, it follows that
\bar{u}=\bar{1}
. This shows that
u\in 1+J(R)
. Hence,

a=(1-e)+(2e-1+u)\quad\text{with}\quad 2e-1+u\in J(R).

Write
a=f+j
with
f=f^{2}
,
j\in J(R)
and
af=fa
. Then,

a=(1-f)+(2f-1+j)

with
2f-1+j\in U(R)
. By the uniqueness, we have
e=1-f
, and so
f=1-e
, as desired.

Now, supposing
\dfrac{R}{J(R)}
is a division ring, we infer that
R
is local, so we can take
e=0
, as expected.
∎

We are now ready to attack and resolve
[

R

Proposition 2.23
.

The answer to this question is in the affirmative.

Proof.

First, we show that
{\rm Nil}(R)\subseteq J(R)
. Let
a\in{\rm Nil}(R)
. If
a\in U(R)
, then
a=0
and hence
a\in J(R)
. If, however,
a\notin U(R)
, so there exists an unique idempotent
e\in R
such that
a-e\in J(R)
. Then, we have
a+J(R)=e+J(R)
, and thus
a+J(R)\in{\rm Id}(\dfrac{R}{J(R)})
but
a+J(R)\in N(\dfrac{R}{J(R)})
. So,
a+J(R)=J(R)
and hence
a\in J(R)
. Now, we show that, for every non-invertible element
a\in R
, there exists an unique central idempotent
e\in R
such that
a-e\in J(R)
. Let
a\in R
, where
a\notin U(R)
, so by hypothesis there exists an unique idempotent
e\in R
such that
a-e\in J(R)
. It suffices to show that
e\in{\rm Z}(R)
. To this aim, for every
r\in R
,

er(1-e),(1-e)re\in{\rm Nil}(R)\subseteq J(R)

and

e+(1-e)re,e+er(1-e)\in{\rm Id}(R),

so we have

a-(e+er(1-e)),a-(e+(1-e)re)\in J(R).

However, by the uniqueness, we deduce

e+(1-e)re=e+er(1-e)=e.

Therefore,
er=ere=re
and hence
e\in{\rm Z}(R)
, as wanted. Finally, we conclude that
R
is GUC by
[
3
, Theorem 3.5]
, as promised.
∎

Let
R
be a ring and
M
a bimodule over
R
. The trivial extension of
R
and
M
is

T(R,M)=\{(r,m):r\in R,m\in M\}

with the addition defined component-wise and the multiplication defined by the equality

(r,m)(s,n)=(rs,rn+ms).

Proposition 2.24
.

If the trivial extension
T(R,M)
is a GUSC ring, then
R
is a GUSC ring. The converse holds, provided
ex=xe
for all
x\in M
and
e\in{\rm Id}(R)
.

Proof.

Choose
a\in R
, where
a\notin U(R)
, so that
(a,0)\notin U(T(R,M))
and thus
(a,0)
is USC. Then,
a
is USC, because otherwise, if
a
has two different strongly clean decompositions, then
(a,0)
has two different strongly clean decompositions, which contradicts to our assumption.

For the converse, choose
(a,m)\in T(R,M)
, where
(a,m)\notin U(T(R,M))
, so that
a\notin U(R)
(as for otherwise, if
a\in U(R)
, then
(a,m)\in U(T(R,M))
with inverse
(a^{-1},-a^{-1}ma^{-1})
, a contradiction), and thus by hypothesis
a
is USC. Therefore,
(a,m)
is USC, because otherwise, if
(a,m)=(e,0)+(u,m)=(f,0)+(v,m)
, where
(e,0),(f,0)\in{\rm Id}(T(R,M))
,
(u,m),(v,m)\in U(T(R,M))
and

(e,0)(u,m)=(u,m)(e,0)
,
(f,0)(v,m)=(v,m)(f,0)
, then we will have

a=e+u=f+v,

where
e,f\in{\rm Id}(R)
,
u,v\in U(R)
and
eu=ue
,
fv=vf
. Hence,
a
has two strongly clean decompositions, a contradiction.

Claim.
If
(e,m)^{2}=(e,m)\in T(R,M)
, then
e^{2}=e
and
m=0
.

In fact,
(e,m)^{2}=(e,m)
automatically gives by comparison
e^{2}=e
and
em=me=m
. So, by hypothesis, we have
2em=m
, whence
2em=em
, and hence
em=0
. So,
2em=0
and then
m=0
, proving the claim and thus the entire result.
∎

Let
R
be a ring and let
M
be an bimodule over
R
which is a non-unital ring in which the equalities
(mn)r=m(nr)
,
(mr)n=m(rn)
and
(rm)n=r(mn)
hold for all
m,n\in M
and
r\in R
. Then, the ideal-extension
I(R,M)
of
R
by
M
is defined to be the additive abelian group
I(R,M)=R\oplus M
with multiplication given by

(r,m)(s,n)=(rs,rn+ms+mn).

We are now in a position to establish the validity of the following statement.

Proposition 2.25
.

If the ideal extension
I(R,M)
is a GUSC ring, then
R
is a GUSC ring. The converse holds if the following conditions are satisfied:

(a)

If
e\in{\rm Id}(R)
, then
em=me
for all
m\in M
.

(b)

If
m\in M
, then
m+n+mn=0
for some
n\in M
.

Proof.

The necessity is analogical to the arguments of Proposition
2.24
.

To prove the sufficiency, first we know with the aid of (a), (b) and
[
10
, Proposition7]
that every idempotent of
I(R,M)
is of the type
(e,0)
, where
e^{2}=e
as well as that every unit of
I(R,M)
is of the type
(u,m)
, where
u\in U(R)
,
m\in M
. Now, let
(a,m)\in I(R,M)
, where
(a,m)\notin U(I(R,M))
, so that
a\notin U(R)
. Then, by hypothesis
a
is USC, and thus
(a,m)
is USC, because otherwise, if
(a,m)
has two different strongly clean decompositions, then arguing as in the proof of Proposition
2.24
, the element
a
has two different strongly clean decompositions, a contradiction.
∎

Let
A
be a ring, let
B
be a unital subring of
A
, and let
R[A,B]
stand for the set

\{(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n},b,b,\ldots):a_{i}\in A,b\in B,1\leq i\leq n\}

. Then,
R[A,B]
is a ring under the component-wise addition and multiplication.

We now state the following.

Proposition 2.26
.

Let
A
be a ring and let
B
be a unital subring of
A
. Then,
R[A,B]
is a GUSC ring if, and only if,
A
,
B
are both GUSC ring.

Proof.

Straightforward.
∎

3.
Group Rings

We know with the help of Lemma
2.9
that GUSC rings include USC rings and local rings, and vice versa. In addition, there are some results on USC group rings and local group rings proved in
[
11
]
and
[
8
]
, respectively. Correspondingly, we can also obtain some achievements about GUSC group rings that could be of some interest and importance. To this purpose, we recall that a group
G
is a
p
-group
if every element of
G
is a power of
p
, where
p
is a prime. Likewise, a group
G
is called
locally finite
if every finitely generated subgroup is finite. As usual, for an arbitrary ring
R
and an arbitrary group
G
, the symbol
RG
designates the group ring of
G
over
R
.

We can now offer the following two statements.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
R
be a ring and let
G
be a group. If
RG
is a GUSC ring, then
R
is a GUSC ring and
G
is a
p
-group such that
p\in J(R)
.

Proof.

We can argue in the same manner as in
[
3
, Proposition 4.2]
and in
[
1
, Proposition 24]
to finish the argumentation.
∎

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
R
be a ring with
\dfrac{R}{J(R)}\cong\mathbb{Z}_{2}
and let
G
be a locally finite group. Then,
RG
is a GUSC ring if, and only if,
G
is a
2
-group.

Proof.

Assuming
RG
is GUSC, we apply Lemma
2.9
to get that
RG
is either local or USC. Firstly, if
RG
is USC, then
G
is a
2
-group by
[
11
, Corollary 3.1]
. Secondly, if
RG
is local, then
G
is a
p
-group and
p\in J(R)
referring to
[
8
]
. Since
\dfrac{R}{J(R)}\cong\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, we conclude that
R
is uniquely clean by
[
10
, Theorem 15]
, so
R
is USC. Now, if
p\neq 2
, then
p\notin J(R)
using Lemma
2.12
. So,
p=2
and hence
G
is a
2
-group.

As for the converse implication, if
G
is a
2
-group, then
RG
is USC utilizing
[
11
, Theorem 3.1]
, and thus
RG
is GUSC, as stated.
∎

We now arrive at our chief result in this section which gives a criterion when a group ring of a locally finite group is GUSC.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
R
be a ring and let
G
be a locally finite group. Then,
RG
is a GUSC ring if, and only if,
R
is a GUSC ring and
G
is a
p
-group with
p\in J(R)
.

Proof.

Assuming
RG
is a GUSC ring, then the result follows directly from Proposition
3.1
.

Conversely, assume
R
is a GUSC ring and
G
is a
p
-group such that
p\in J(R)
. In view of Lemma
2.9
, we differ two cases as follows: if
R
is local, then
RG
is local by the usage of
[
8
]
. If, however,
R
is USC, then we apply Lemma
2.12
and
[
1
, Lemma 16]
to have
p=2
. So,
G
is a locally finite
2
-group and
R
is USC; thus, we infer that
RG
is USC in virtue of
[
11
, Theorem 3.1]
. Therefore,
RG
is a GUSC ring consulting with Lemma
2.9
, as expected.
∎

As an automatic consequence in the case when
p=2
, we yield:

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
R
be a ring and let
G
be a locally finite
2
-group. Then,
RG
is a GUSC ring if, and only if,
R
is a GUSC ring and
2\in J(R)
.

We close our work with the following construction.

Example 3.5
.

Let
C_{n}
denote the cyclic group of order
n
. If
n\geq 3
is odd and
R
is a boolean ring, then
RC_{n}
is
not
a GUSC ring.

Proof.

One finds with
[
3
, Example 4.7]
in hand that
1+g^{n-1}\notin U(RC_{n})
, both
g^{n-1},x+g^{n-1}\in U(RC_{n})
and

1+g+\cdots+g^{n-1}\in{\rm Id}(RC_{n}),

where
G=\{1,g,\ldots,g^{n-1}\}
and
x=g+g^{2}+\ldots+g^{n-1}
.

Thus, we receive

1+g^{n-1}=1+(g^{n-1})=(1+g+\cdots+g^{n-1})+(x+g^{n-1}).

Consequently, we have two strongly clean decompositions for
1+g^{n-1}
in
RC_{n}
. Therefore,
RC_{n}
is
not
GUSC, as claimed.
∎

Problem.

R

R
