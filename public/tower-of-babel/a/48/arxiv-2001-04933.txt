Matrix recovery from bilinear and quadratic measurements
Michalina Pacholska, Karen Adam, Adam Scholefield, Martin Vetterli

Abstract
Matrix (or operator) recovery from linear measurements is a well-studied problem. However, there are situations where only bilinear or quadratic measurements are available. A bilinear or quadratic problem can easily be transformed into a linear one, but it raises questions when the linearized problem is solvable and what is the cost of linearization. In this work, we study a few specific cases of this general problem and show when the bilinear problem is solvable. Using this result and certain properties of polynomial rings, we present a scenario when the quadratic problem can be linearized at the cost of just a linear number of additional measurements. Finally, we link our results back to two applications that inspired it: Time Encoding Machines and Continuous Localisation.



Keywords
: matrix recovery, bilinear and quadratic functionals, product of
frames, polynomials, polynomial rings, bandlimited functions, matrix rank,
linearization, linear system of equations, SLAMpling

Introduction

Given a sufficient number of linear equations, the problem of matrix recovery (or completion) does not differ from any other linear problem. However, when the problem is ill-posed, regularisations specific to matrix recovery are used. For example, a low-rank assumption is often used in matrix completion
[
1
]
, which leads to non-convex problems that are often solved with convex relaxation
[
2
]
.

Matrix recovery also appears in multidimensional signal estimation, for example in multi-channel audio processing or in source separation
[
3
,
4
]
, where the signal coefficients form a matrix with different rows corresponding to different dimensions/sources.

Such problems are rarely stated as matrix reconstruction from
bilinear

measurements not because of their structure (which often fits the bilinear formulation), but because they can be seen as a
special case of matrix recovery from linear measurements in the larger space.
Consider for example an unknown matrix
\boldsymbol{C}:\mathbb{R}^{J\times K}
that we would like to reconstruct. We can treat
\boldsymbol{C}
as a vector – an element of
\mathbb{R}^{JK}
, and try to solve a linear system of equations for
\boldsymbol{C}
.

There are situations when this approach is sufficient, for example if
the measurements have the form
b_{jk}=\boldsymbol{\phi}_{j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{k}
, where
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{j}
and
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{k}
are bases of
\mathbb{R}^{J}
and

\mathbb{R}^{K}
respectively. In this case, to reconstruct
\boldsymbol{C}
we need
all
JK
possible measurements (without any regularizers or priors). However, having a certain number of measurements is not sufficient to guarantee recovery. Indeed, consider the situation when
\boldsymbol{\psi}
is a frame consisting
of
JK
vectors. Then there are
JK
measurements of the form

b_{k}=\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{k}
,
k=0\dots JK-1
, but
they are insufficient to recover
\boldsymbol{C}
, at least if there are no assumptions on the structure of
\boldsymbol{C}
.

A second problem we consider is matrix reconstruction from quadratic
measurements, where
J=K
and the measurements have the form

b_{k}=\boldsymbol{\psi}_{k}^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{k}
, with
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
. Quadratic measurements appear for example in phase retrieval
[
5
,
6
]
.

We introduce the problem and assumptions used through this paper in Section
2
.
In Section
3
, we focus on bilinear measurements. We analyze if the set of measurements is sufficient to reconstruct the matrix in the case when one set of vectors is
a frame and the second is a set of pair-wise different vectors. We apply
our theory to the problem of encoding mixed bandlimited signals, studied in the
context of Time Encoding Machines (TEMs)
[
7

In Section
4

quadratic measurements, with additional assumptions on the measurement vectors

\boldsymbol{\psi}_{k}

Finally, we show how the quadratic case applies to continuous localization from range measurements

2
Problem statement

In this section we introduce the most general problem we consider, show how to linearize it and introduce the specific assumptions we make in this work. The problem, as well as the assumptions, are inspired by the Continuous Localisation
[
8
]
.

We consider the problem of recovering a matrix
\boldsymbol{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{J\times K}
from
N
measurements of the form

b_{n}=\boldsymbol{g}_{n}^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{f}_{n}+\boldsymbol{f}_{n}^{\top}\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{f}_{n},

(1)

where
\boldsymbol{g}_{n}
and
\boldsymbol{f}_{n}
are known vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{J}

and
\mathbb{R}^{K}
, respectively,
b_{n}
are measured scalars and
\boldsymbol{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K}
is an unknown matrix that does not need to be recovered.
In this work we only consider the noiseless case.

This problem can be also interpreted as a problem of recovering a bilinear operator
C(\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{g})=\boldsymbol{g}^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{f}
in the presence of the quadratic term
L(\boldsymbol{f})=\boldsymbol{f}^{\top}\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{f}
. In this work, we only use the matrix representation.

We transform (
1
) into a system of linear equations using properties of the trace. Since both elements of the sum in (
1
) are scalars we can write
\tr(\vect{g}_n^\top\vect{C}\vect{f}_n)=\tr((\vect{g}_n^\top\vect{C})^\top\vect{f}_n^\top)=\tr(\vect{C}^\top\vect{g}_n \vect{f}_n^\top)=\vectorised(\boldsymbol{g}_{n}\boldsymbol{f}_{n}^{\top})^{\top}\vectorised(\boldsymbol{C})
and similarly for the quadratic part. We then obtain a set of
N
linear equations:

b_{n}=\vectorised(\boldsymbol{g}_{n}\boldsymbol{f}_{n}^{\top})^{\top}\vectorised(\boldsymbol{C})+\vectorised(\boldsymbol{f}_{n}\boldsymbol{f}_{n}^{\top})^{\top}\vectorised(\boldsymbol{L}),

(2)

where the linear transformation
\vectorised:\mathbb{R}^{J\times K}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{JK}
flattens a matrix into a vector.

Bilinear Measurements

In this section we only consider the bilinear measurement problem – we assume that there is no quadratic term (
\boldsymbol{L}=0

such that

b_{n}=\boldsymbol{g}_{n}^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{f}_{n}.

Equivalently, we want to solve the following system of equations:

b_{n}=\vectorised(\boldsymbol{g}_{n}\boldsymbol{f}_{n}^{\top})^{\top}\vectorised(\boldsymbol{C}).

This system of equations can be solved if at least

Since we assume that the vectors

are different, we allow vectors

\mathcal{A}
be the set of unique vectors
\boldsymbol{g}_{n}

\boldsymbol{g}_{n}\in\mathcal{A}
for all

but
M:=|\mathcal{A}|\leq N

be the (unique) elements of

that every

elements of

\mathbb{R}^{J}

Under these assumptions, the following theorem holds:

Theorem 1

(Basis of Bilinear Measurements)
.

Consider the set of
N=KJ
vectors of the form

\vectorised(\boldsymbol{g}_{n}\boldsymbol{f}_{n}^{\top})
. It is a basis in
\mathbb{R}^{KJ}

if and only if no more than

\boldsymbol{g}_{n}

JK
of the measurements to be independent,

Second, we use some properties of permutations.

Two permutations

\displaystyle\forall_{k\in\{0,\dots,K-1\}}\{\sigma_{j}:\lfloor j/J\rfloor=k\}=\{\tau_{j}:\lfloor j/J\rfloor=k\}.

4
Quadratic measurements

In this section, we reintroduce the quadratic term from (
1
). We introduce additional assumptions that let us analyse the quadratic term separately (Observation
1
) and show how both terms can be connected (Lemma
4
). Finally we show how to use the aforementioned results in practice, using the example of trajectory reconstruction from
[
8
]
(Section
4.1
).

Recall that the entries
\boldsymbol{f}_{n}
are elements of the linear space of functions
\mathcal{F}
. In this section, we will additionally assume that
\mathcal{F}
can be extended to a polynomial ring
\mathcal{R}
over
\mathbb{R}
,
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathcal{R}
.

This assumption might seem abstract, but it encompasses a number of widely used linear spaces of functions.
A canonical example is of course the family of polynomials
f_{k}(t)=t^{k}
. We can set
\mathcal{F}
to be the space of polynomials of degree
smaller than
K
, and
\mathcal{R}
to be ring of polynomials

\mathcal{R}[t]
. Trigonometric polynomials, that is real symmetric
bandlimited functions on
(-\pi,\pi)
, can be extended to
\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{R}[\cos(t)]
, and similarly real bandlimited functions on

(-\pi,\pi)
can be extended to
\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{R}[X,Y]/_{[X^{2}+Y^{2}-1]}
, where we identify
X=\cos(t)
and
Y=\sin(t)
.
We can also take
\mathcal{F}
to be the space of complex bandlimited functions on
(-\pi,\pi)
and
\mathcal{R}
to be
\mathcal{R}[e^{it}]

Let us now consider the purely quadratic term of (
1
):

\tilde{b}_{n}=\boldsymbol{f}_{n}^{\top}\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{f}_{n}.

(20)

Since the matrices
\boldsymbol{f}_{n}\boldsymbol{f}_{n}^{\top}
are symmetric, it is clear that we can have only
K(K+1)/2
independent equations, and can recover
\boldsymbol{L}
only up to a subspace (without any additional assumptions on
\boldsymbol{L}
). It turns out that for polynomial rings the maximal number of independent measurements is even smaller, as per the observation below:

Observation 1

(On Polynomial Rings)
.

Assume that
\deg(f_{k})\leq\boldsymbol{\alpha}
, for some
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{N}^{m}
.
Then the entries of
\boldsymbol{f}_{n}\boldsymbol{f}_{n}^{\top}
are also polynomials over

\mathbb{F}
, of degrees
\leq 2\boldsymbol{\alpha}
, because
\deg(ab)=\deg(a)+\deg(b)
for
a,b\in\mathcal{R}
or
\deg(ab)=\deg(a)+\deg(b)

up to the appropriate modulo relation for a quotient ring.

This means that, among
M
different vectors
\vectorised(\boldsymbol{f}_{n}\boldsymbol{f}_{n}^{\top})
of degrees up to
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
, there are
at
most

(a_{0}+1)(a_{1}+1)(a_{2}+1)\dots
linearly independent vectors.

If additionally for every degree
\boldsymbol{\beta}<\boldsymbol{\alpha}
there is an
index
k
such that
\deg(f_{k})=\boldsymbol{\beta}
, then each degree
\leq 2\boldsymbol{\alpha}
will have a corresponding entry in
\boldsymbol{f}_{n}\boldsymbol{f}_{n}^{\top}
.
This means that for
f_{k}(t)=t^{k}
there are
exactly

\min(M,2K-1)
linearly independent vectors.
For a polynomial of two variables, with no equivalence relation and with

\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\sqrt{K},\sqrt{K})
, we would get
4K
different degrees
(so a
4
-fold increase).

For trigonometric polynomials, if
f_{k}
have degrees up to
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(1,(K-1)/2)
(where
K
is odd), then the maximal degree we can get would be

2\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(2,K-1)
, which would lead to
3K
different degrees.
But since for trigonometric polynomials we have the relation
X^{2}+Y^{2}=1
,
the highest possible degree we can have is
(1,K-1)
, which reduces the
possible number of different degrees to
2K
. For a standard basis of the
space of real, periodic bandlimited functions with bandwidth
K
, we do not
use polynomials of degree exactly
(1,(K-1)/2)
(the function
\sin(t)\cos(t(K-1)/2)

is not the element of the standard basis).
Thus we never get the degree
(1,K-1)
and the maximal number of different degrees is

2K-1
.

This means that we rarely have enough measurements to recover (even symmetric)
\boldsymbol{L}
. However, the low number of degrees of freedom can be advantageous if we consider both terms from (
1
), and are primarily interested in recovering
\boldsymbol{C}
. We will see a specific example of this in Section
4.1
.

Recall the linear system of equations corresponding to (

b_{n}=\vectorised(\boldsymbol{g}_{n}\boldsymbol{f}_{n}^{\top})\vectorised(\boldsymbol{C})+\vectorised(\boldsymbol{f}_{n}\boldsymbol{f}_{n}^{\top})\vectorised(\boldsymbol{L}).

(21)
