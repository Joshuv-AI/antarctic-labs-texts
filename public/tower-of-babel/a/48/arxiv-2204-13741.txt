Mert Kayaalp, Yunus İnan, Emre Telatar, Ali H. Sayed

Abstract
We study the asymptotic learning rates of belief vectors in a distributed hypothesis testing problem under linear and log-linear combination rules. We show that under both combination strategies, agents are able to learn the truth exponentially fast, with a faster rate under log-linear fusion. We examine the gap between the rates in terms of network connectivity and information diversity. We also provide closed-form expressions for special cases involving federated architectures and exchangeable networks.



I
Introduction

Statistical inference is the problem of learning a hidden variable from partially informative observations. It pertains to estimation and decision-making tasks, which are essential for engineering design problems. Many modern designs are large-scale and hence inference is apportioned to smaller devices. These devices — henceforward referred to as agents — have data acquiring and processing capabilities, and are possibly spatially dispersed. Distributed inference deals with the collaborative working of these agents to infer an unknown phenomenon of interest.

There are two main settings depending on the topology of agents. The first is a federated setting where agents send their information, e.g., raw data, opinion or belief about the hidden state, to a
fusion center
. The fusion center then aggregates the received messages and performs deduction
[
1
]
. For example, weather stations at different locations can measure barometric pressure or cloud coverage and send a statistic of the measurements to a centralized unit, e.g., National Weather Service. By combining and processing the information received, the service is able to provide weather forecasts. However, gathering all information at a central location is prone to failure and raises privacy issues. Peer-to-peer networks that rely only on localized interactions offer a remedy. These networks are
fully
decentralized, i.e., there is no central unit. Agents perform local computations and combine their immediate neighbors’ information
[
2
,
3
]
. Wireless sensor networks assigned to perform area/industrial monitoring or environmental sensing are examples of fully distributed multi-agent networks
[
4
]
.

A fundamental question that arises is how to aggregate information received from the peripheral agents, in federated architectures; or from the neighbors, in fully distributed architectures. There exists a variety of combination strategies and the optimal
pooling
strategy depends heavily on the application at hand
[
5
]
. We focus on two widely used strategies: arithmetic averaging (AA) and geometric averaging (GA). While the former is shown to be better with fusion of random variables or point estimates (
\nu
-fusion), the latter is more common in applications involving fusion of probability mass/density functions (
f
-fusion)
[
6
]
. Combining probability distributions is superior in the sense that a full description about the unknown variable of interest is utilized rather than some statistics of it, such as the mean or the median. Furthermore, this procedure is also compatible with heterogeneous data models across the agents. Therefore, in this work, we evaluate and compare the performance of AA and GA for the fusion of
beliefs
, i.e., distributions over the unknown quantity of interest.

Given the difficulty in performing full-blown Bayesian inference over graphs, social learning is a non-Bayesian inference paradigm that is able to perform distributed hypothesis testing over networks with (possibly bounded) rational agents by relaxing the full Bayesian requirement while still leading to truth learning with probability 1 (e.g.,

Knowing how much gain/loss in learning rate a distributed system would get if GA or AA is used, and how this gap is affected by the graph topology, are useful indicators for many applications. This work contributes to answering these questions.

Novelty and Contributions

Since AA and GA have different attributes that can be useful for different applications (see Section

) only one step of fusion is studied

. In contrast, in this work, we study the

confining to specific distributions. Moreover, we analyze the performance gap between AA and GA in detail. In particular, for social learning, we have the following results.

•

In Theorem
3
, we prove that the agents learn the truth

with the AA fusion rule under the standard diffusion algorithm
[
2
]
. Furthermore, the decay rate of beliefs over a wrong hypothesis — also called the learning rate — is
constant
and does not depend on the agent.

•

In Lemma
1
of Section
IV
, we provide upper and lower bounds for this decay rate using superadditive and subadditive functions on matrices. Also, an interesting “inept agent phenomenon” is discovered by using an appropriate superadditive function.

•

We provide a variational lower bound on the gap between the decay rates of AA and GA in Theorem
4
. The bound involves the Dobrushin coefficient
[
55
, Chapter 2.7]
as a network connectivity parameter. If the network is geometrically ergodic

]
(for which the Dobrushin coefficient is strictly smaller than 1), then the gap is zero if, and only if, the agents observe exactly the same data. Otherwise GA performs better in terms of learning rate.

•

For the special case of rank-one combination matrices, which is equivalent to architectures with fusion center in terms of performance, the exact decay rate of AA in closed form is given in Proposition
2
.

•

For exchangeable networks, where no permutation of data across agents can change the dynamics, we also provide a closed form expression for the gap between the decay rates of AA and GA in Theorem
5
.

II
Background and Problem Formulation

II-A

Inference Problem

Fig. 1:
An example network diagram.

We consider
K
agents collaboratively seeking to learn the true state of nature
\theta^{\circ}
from among a finite set of
H
hypotheses,
\Theta=\{1,2,\dots,H\}
. At time instant
i
, the confidence that agent
k
has that “hypothesis
\theta
is the true hypothesis” is denoted by
\bm{\mu}_{k,i}(\theta)
. In this notation, the symbol
\bm{\mu}_{k,i}
is a probability vector over all hypotheses. Agent
k
observes a partially informative and private signal
\bm{\xi}_{k,i}
, which is distributed according to some known marginal likelihood,
L_{k}(\cdot|\theta^{\circ})
. This observation process is independently and identically distributed (i.i.d.) over time
i
. However, independence across agents is not required. Agents know their own marginal likelihoods but do not know the likelihoods of other agents. In order to avoid pathological cases, we introduce the following assumption.

Assumption 1

(Finite KL divergences)
.

For each agent
k
and all hypotheses
\theta\in\Theta
,

\displaystyle D(L_{k}(\cdot|\theta^{\circ})||L_{k}(\cdot|\theta))<\infty.

(1)

\blacksquare

In addition, we assume for each wrong hypothesis
\theta\neq\theta^{\circ}
that there exists at least one clear-sighted agent who can distinguish this hypothesis from the true hypothesis. This is necessary for learning the truth.

Assumption 2

(Global identifiability)
.

For each wrong hypothesis
\theta\in\Theta\setminus\{\theta^{\circ}\}
, there exists at least one agent
k
with

\displaystyle D(L_{k}(\cdot|\theta^{\circ})||L_{k}(\cdot|\theta))>0.

(2)

\blacksquare

Observe that this assumption does not require
local
identifiability, which is the ability of inferring
\theta^{\circ}
without any cooperation. As a result, agents must exchange information in order to uniquely identify the true hypothesis in general.

II-B

Agent Topology

There are two architectures of interest.

II-B
1
Decentralized peer-to-peer networks

For this architecture, we consider a strongly-connected graph
[
2
]
governing the communication topology. In other words, for any agent pair
(\ell,k)
there exists a path in between and linking them and, moreover, there exists at least one agent in the network with positive self-reliance, denoted by
a_{kk}>0
for some
k
. This allows the information to diffuse across the network thoroughly. We associate non-negative combination coefficients
0\leq a_{\ell k}\leq 1
with each link from
\ell
to
k
and collect these weights into the combination matrix. The strong-connectedness of the graph translates into
A
being a primitive matrix
[
2
]
. Each
a_{\ell k}
represents the weight agent
k
assigns to the information received from agent
\ell
. It is non-zero if, and only if,
\ell\in\mathcal{N}_{k}
, i.e., agent
\ell
is an immediate neighbor of agent
k
. The matrix
A
is also left-stochastic. This means that the entries on each of its columns add up to 1. Such matrices have a unique eigenvalue at 1, and we denote the corresponding eigenvector
\pi
whose entries are normalized to add up to one:

\displaystyle\mathds{1}_{K}^{\T}\pi=1,\quad A\pi=\pi,\quad\mathds{1}_{K}^{\T}A=\mathds{1}_{K}^{\T}.

(3)

The vector
\pi
is the Perron vector of
A
, and by the Perron-Frobenius theorem, all entries of
\pi
are positive
[
2
]
.

II-B
2
Architectures with fusion center

Agents communicate with a central node instead of communicating with other agents. The central node aggregates the information received from peripheral agents by taking a weighted average. Note that the performance of this architecture is equivalent to the performance of a fully-connected network topology with a rank-one combination matrix
A
. Thus, in the following, we focus on general combination matrices
A
described for decentralized networks. For the special case of
A
being rank one, we obtain additional results on top of the general case — see Proposition
2
in Section
IV-C1
.

II-C

Pooling Functions

We compare linear (AA)
[
56
]
and log-linear (GA)
[
57
]
fusion of probability vectors in this work. The AA and GA rules in the context of social learning are presented in Section
II-D
. Here, we comment briefly on some features of AA and GA. In the current work, we do not focus on identifying various features of AA and GA. The reader may refer to
[
5
]
for other useful characteristics.

While GA is externally Bayesian, that is, Bayesian updates on the beliefs and the aggregation step are commutative, it nevertheless gives agents a
veto power
. That is, if one agent has zero belief on some hypothesis, then the composite belief will also be zero on that same hypothesis regardless of the beliefs by the other agents. The AA rule, on the other hand, does not give this much power to individual agents and hence can be more robust against adversarial attacks. Moreover, in the case of Gaussian beliefs over continuous hidden variables, repeated application of GA preserves Gaussianity and is related to the covariance-intersection method
[
30
]
. In contrast, the properties of AA-combined distributions diverge from the Gaussian distributions.

II-D

Diffusion Social Learning based on AA and GA

Under diffusion social learning, at each iteration
i
, agents first update their beliefs using a
local
Bayesian rule based on their private observations and compute the following intermediate beliefs:

\displaystyle\bm{\psi}_{k,i}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{L_{k}(\bm{\xi}_{k,i}|\theta)\bm{\mu}_{k,i-1}(\theta)}{\sum_{\theta^{\prime}}L_{k}(\bm{\xi}_{k,i}|\theta^{\prime})\bm{\mu}_{k,i-1}(\theta^{\prime})}.

(4)

The next step differs depending on whether AA or GA fusion is used. Under AA, each agent updates its belief vector by computing a weighted arithmetic average of its neighbors’ intermediate beliefs. More precisely, for agent
k
, the linear fusion is given by the following rule:

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}_{k,i}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{N}_{k}}{a_{\ell k}\bm{\psi}_{\ell,i}(\theta)}.\qquad\text{(AA-Diffusion)}

(5)

Since the
\{a_{\ell k}\}
are positive coefficients that add up to one, the resulting vector
\bm{\mu}_{k,i}
will be a belief vector with its entries adding up to one. Under GA, on the other hand, agents average the intermediate beliefs of their neighbors in a geometric manner and also perform a normalization step to ensure that the resulting vector continues to be a probability vector (with entries adding up to one):

\bm{\mu}_{k,i}(\theta)\!=\!\frac{\text{exp}\{\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{N}_{k}}a_{\ell k}\log\bm{\psi}_{\ell,i}(\theta)\}}{\sum_{\theta^{\prime}}\text{exp}\{\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{N}_{k}}a_{\ell k}\log\bm{\psi}_{\ell,i}(\theta^{\prime})\}}\>\>\text{(GA-Diffusion)}

(6)

Remark:
Observe that the support of the averaged belief vectors is the union of the supports in the AA case, while it is the intersection of the supports in the GA case. Hence, in GA, all agents need to have positive initial beliefs for all hypotheses in order not to discard any hypothesis. In contrast, it is enough for at least one agent to have a positive initial belief for any hypothesis in order not to get discarded in AA. ∎

Remark:
In the consensus algorithm, agents combine their intermediate beliefs with the previous beliefs of the neighbors instead. Compared with (
5
), the combination step of the AA-Consensus is given by:

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}_{k,i}(\theta)={a_{kk}\bm{\psi}_{k,i}(\theta)}\!+\!\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{N}_{k}\setminus\{k\}}{a_{\ell k}\bm{\mu}_{\ell,i}(\theta)}\qquad\quad\text{(AA-Consensus)}

(7)

Similarly, compared with (
6
), the combination step of the GA-Consensus is given by:

\bm{\mu}_{k,i}(\theta)=\dfrac{\text{exp}\Big\{a_{kk}\log\bm{\psi}_{k,i}(\theta)+\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{N}_{k}\setminus\{k\}}a_{\ell k}\log\bm{\mu}_{\ell,i}(\theta)\Big\}}{\sum_{\theta^{\prime}}\text{exp}\Big\{a_{kk}\log\bm{\psi}_{k,i}(\theta^{\prime})+\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{N}_{k}\setminus\{k\}}a_{\ell k}\log\bm{\mu}_{\ell,i}(\theta^{\prime})\Big\}}\qquad\text{(GA-Consensus)}

(8)

\blacksquare

III
Existence of Asymptotic Decay Rates

Recall that the true hypothesis is fixed and is denoted by
\theta^{\circ}
. To measure how fast the beliefs converge to the truth (i.e., how fast
\bm{\mu}_{k,i}(\theta^{\circ})\rightarrow 1
and
\bm{\mu}_{k,i}(\theta\neq\theta^{\circ})\rightarrow 0
), for each
k
, it is sufficient to study the asymptotic behavior of agent
k
’s belief on a false hypothesis
\theta\neq\theta^{\circ}
. More precisely, we will study the exponential decay rates of these beliefs, defined as

\bm{\rho}_{k}(\theta)\triangleq-\limsup_{i\to\infty}\frac{1}{i}\log\bm{\mu}_{k,i}(\theta)

(9)

although there are variations. For instance, in
[
21
]
, the agent regrets are defined as the total variation distance to the truth vector
e_{\theta^{\circ}}
, whose
\theta^{\circ}
th element is one and others are zero,

\mathbf{d}_{k,i}\triangleq\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{\mu}_{k,i}(\theta)-e_{\theta^{\circ}}\|_{1},

(10)

and the asymptotic behavior of the average regret on the network is analyzed, i.e.,

\displaystyle\bm{\rho}\triangleq\liminf_{i\to\infty}\frac{1}{i}\bigg|\log\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbf{d}_{k,i}\bigg)\bigg|.

(11)

Although they do seem different, it can be shown that for both definitions, the quantities of interest are
\bm{\rho}_{k}(\theta)
’s — see Appendix
-A
for the proof. Hence, we focus on (
9
) for the rest of this work. Note that some definitions of exponential convergence require
\bm{\mu}_{k,i}(\theta)\leq C\exp\{-\rho i\}
where
C
is a uniformly bounded constant. In this work, we adhere to the definition from the existing social learning literature, where exponential convergence refers to the presence of the exponent, and does not the require a uniform bound on
C
.

In
[
21
]
, the authors obtained upper and lower bounds for
\bm{\rho}
for the
consensus
algorithm. In this section, we show that under the AA diffusion rule (
5
) that
exact
limits exist in (
9
) with
\limsup
replaced by
\lim
and that these limits are independent of the agent index
k
. That is, we show that

\bm{\rho}^{(\mathrm{AA})}(\theta)\triangleq\lim_{i\to\infty}-\frac{1}{i}\log\bm{\mu}_{k,i}(\theta)

(12)

exists almost surely and does not depend on
k
. This means that the beliefs on false hypotheses decay exponentially at the same rate for all agents. In comparison to GA-diffusion, the analysis for AA-diffusion is much more involved. This is because GA-diffusion is amenable to an analysis that studies the evolution of log-belief ratios, and can benefit from an application of the strong law of large numbers.

III-A

Brief Analysis of GA-diffusion

We start with the GA-diffusion rule (
6
). Following the works
[
25
,
22
,
23
]
, we provide a summary of the studies done for finding the decay rates. It is convenient to introduce the vector of log-belief ratios,
\bm{\lambda}_{i}(\theta)\triangleq[\bm{\lambda}_{1,i}(\theta),\dots,\bm{\lambda}_{K,i}(\theta)]^{\T}
where

\bm{\lambda}_{k,i}(\theta)\triangleq\log\frac{\bm{\mu}_{k,i}(\theta^{\circ})}{\bm{\mu}_{k,i}(\theta)}.

(13)

It can be easily verified from (
4
) and (
6
) that the vector
\bm{\lambda}_{i}(\theta)
evolves according to the linear stochastic system

\displaystyle\bm{\lambda}_{i}(\theta)=A^{\T}(\bm{\lambda}_{i-1}(\theta)+\bm{x}_{i}(\theta))

(14)

where
\bm{x}_{i}(\theta)\triangleq[\bm{x}_{1,i}(\theta),\dots,\bm{x}_{K,i}(\theta)]^{\T}
is the vector of log-likelihood ratios (LLRs):

\bm{x}_{k,i}(\theta)\triangleq\log\frac{L_{k}(\bm{\xi}_{k,i}|\theta^{\circ})}{L_{k}(\bm{\xi}_{k,i}|\theta)}.

(15)

Repeated (
i
-fold) application of (
14
) yields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{i}\bm{\lambda}_{i}(\theta)=\frac{1}{i}\sum_{j=1}^{i}(A^{\T})^{i-j+1}\bm{x}_{j}(\theta)+\frac{1}{i}(A^{\T})^{i}\bm{\lambda}_{0}^{\T}(\theta).

(16)

Now recall that (
i
)
\bm{x}_{i}(\theta)
’s are i.i.d. random vectors, (
ii
)
A^{i}~\to~\pi\mathds{1}_{K}^{\T}
, with
\pi
being the Perron vector of
A
, and (
iii
)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\bm{x}_{k,i}(\theta)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\log\frac{L_{k}(\bm{\xi}_{k,i}|\theta^{\circ})}{L_{k}(\bm{\xi}_{k,i}|\theta)}\bigg]

\displaystyle=D(L_{k}(.|\theta^{\circ})||L_{k}(.|\theta))<\infty

(17)

by Assumption 1. Using these conditions we first note that the first term on the right-hand side of (
16
) tends to

\frac{1}{i}\sum_{j=1}^{i}(\mathds{1}_{K}\pi^{\T})\bm{x}_{j}(\theta),\quad i\to\infty.

(18)

Next, an application of the strong law of large numbers gives almost surely

\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}\frac{1}{i}\bm{\lambda}_{k,i}(\theta)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}\pi_{\ell}\mathbb{E}[\bm{x}_{\ell,i}(\theta)]>0

(19)

which does not depend on
k
. Result (
19
) readily implies that for all
\theta\neq\theta^{\circ}
,
\frac{\bm{\mu}_{k,i}(\theta)}{\bm{\mu}_{k,i}(\theta^{\circ})}\to 0
and since
\sum_{\theta}\bm{\mu}_{k,i}(\theta)=1
, then
\bm{\mu}_{k,i}(\theta^{\circ})\to 1
almost surely.
Hence, with GA-diffusion, the decay rates are the constants given by

\displaystyle\rho^{(\mathrm{GA})}(\theta)

\displaystyle\triangleq\lim_{i\to\infty}\frac{1}{i}\bm{\lambda}_{k,i}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\lim_{i\to\infty}\frac{1}{i}\log\frac{\bm{\mu}_{k,i}(\theta^{\circ})}{\bm{\mu}_{k,i}(\theta)}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}-\lim_{i\to\infty}\frac{1}{i}\log\bm{\mu}_{k,i}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}D(L_{k}(.|\theta^{\circ})||L_{k}(.|\theta))

(20)

where
(a)
follows from the fact that
\bm{\mu}_{k,i}(\theta^{\circ})
tends to 1 almost surely. Observe that the decay rates are characterized by a weighted average of the KL divergences of the agents — it is known that these entities reflect the inference capacity of an agent for a hypothesis testing problem
[
34
]
.

III-B

Asymptotic Decay Rate of AA-Diffusion

Unfortunately, a similar analysis is not possible for AA-diffusion — if we attempt to study
\log\bm{\mu}_{k,i}(\theta)
directly, we end up with

\displaystyle\log\bm{\mu}_{k,i}(\theta)=\log\bigg(\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{N}_{k}}{a_{\ell k}\bm{\psi}_{\ell,i}(\theta)}\bigg),

(21)

which does not provide a simple-to-analyze dynamical system like we had with (
14
). Thus, we need to resort to different methods in the following. The authors of
[
21
]
approached the problem of finding
\bm{\rho}^{(\mathrm{AA})}
by linearizing the dynamical system (
21
). With this method, they were able to lower bound
\bm{\rho}^{(\mathrm{AA})}
by the Lyapunov exponent of the linearized version. We take a different approach by constructing extremal processes that bound
\bm{\mu}_{k,i}(\theta)
.

III-B
1
Constructing the Extremal Process

Recall the evolution of the process
\{\bm{\mu}_{k,i}\}
given by (
4
) and (
5
) under AA-diffusion. We wish to simplify the analysis by obtaining an extremal process
\{\bm{\nu}_{k,i}\}
, which eventually remains above
\{\bm{\mu}_{k,i}\}
with probability 1. Studying
\{\bm{\nu}_{k,i}\}
will then lead to a bound on the decay rate for AA-diffusion. With this aim, we first recall the following theorem.

Theorem 1

(Truth learning
[
20
]
)
.

Under Assumptions 1 and 2, when AA-diffusion is executed, all agents learn the truth, i.e., for each agent
k
we have almost surely

\lim_{i\to\infty}\bm{\mu}_{k,i}(\theta^{\circ})=1.

(22)

\blacksquare

Recall that our setting lies in the probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
, where
\Omega
represents the space of all data sequence realizations over time
\omega\in\Omega
,
\mathcal{F}
represents the
\sigma
-field generated by the sequence of data, and
\mathbb{P}
represents the probability measure over sample paths
\omega\in\Omega
. In light of Theorem
1
, if we set
\epsilon>0
and define the event that all
\bm{\mu}_{k,i}(\theta^{\circ})
’s lie above
1-\epsilon
eventually as

\mathcal{G}(\epsilon)\triangleq\{\omega\in\Omega:\exists i_{0}\ \bm{\mu}_{k,i}(\theta^{\circ})\geq 1-\epsilon,\ \forall i>i_{0},\forall k\},

(23)

observe that
\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{G}(\epsilon)\right)=1
as a consequence of Theorem
1
. Note that
\mathcal{G}(\epsilon)
is also interpreted as the event that there exists an
\bm{i}_{0}(\omega)
— which is a random variable as it depends on
\omega
— such that for all agents the true beliefs remain greater than
1-\epsilon
after
\bm{i}_{0}^{\text{th}}
iteration. We now restrict ourselves to
\mathcal{G}(\epsilon)
and study the evolution of
\{\bm{\mu}_{k,i}\}
under such restriction.

Consider a false hypothesis
\theta\neq\theta^{\circ}
. We study the pathwise trajectories of
\{\bm{\mu}_{k,i}(\theta)\}
for an outcome in
\mathcal{G}(\epsilon)
, that is, we pick an
\omega\in\mathcal{G}(\epsilon)
. According to the definition of
\mathcal{G}(\epsilon)
, there exists an
\bm{i}_{0}(\omega)
such that for all
i>\bm{i}_{0}(\omega)
:

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}_{k,i}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{N}_{k}}a_{\ell k}\frac{\bm{\mu}_{\ell,i-1}(\theta)L_{\ell}(\bm{\xi}_{\ell,i}|\theta)}{\sum_{\theta^{\prime}}\bm{\mu}_{\ell,i-1}(\theta^{\prime})L_{\ell}(\bm{\xi}_{\ell,i}|\theta^{\prime})}

(24)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{N}_{k}}a_{\ell k}\frac{\bm{\mu}_{\ell,i-1}(\theta)L_{\ell}(\bm{\xi}_{\ell,i}|\theta)}{(1-\epsilon)L_{\ell}(\bm{\xi}_{\ell,i}|\theta^{\circ})}.

(25)

Let

\displaystyle\bm{r}_{k,i}(\theta)\triangleq\frac{L_{k}(\bm{\xi}_{k,i}|\theta)}{L_{k}(\bm{\xi}_{k,i}|\theta^{\circ})}

(26)

be the likelihood ratio of the freshly observed data by agent
k
at time
i
, between the hypotheses
\theta
and
\theta^{\circ}
. We define the extremal process
\{\bm{\nu}_{k,i}\}
as the process that evolves according to

\displaystyle\bm{\nu}_{k,i}(\theta)\triangleq(1-\epsilon)^{-1}\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{N}_{k}}a_{\ell k}\bm{\nu}_{\ell,i-1}(\theta)\bm{r}_{\ell,i}(\theta)

(27)

for all
i\geq\bm{i}_{0}(\omega)
and with
\bm{\nu}_{k,\bm{i}_{0}}(\theta)=\bm{\mu}_{k,\bm{i}_{0}}(\theta)
. Comparing (
27
) with (
25
), we see that
\{\bm{\mu}_{k,i}\}
is upper bounded by
\{\bm{\nu}_{k,i}\}
for all
i\geq\bm{i}_{0}(\omega)
.

Remark:
For the rest of the work, we fix a false hypothesis
\theta\neq\theta^{\circ}
and omit
\theta
as an argument for brevity. This is because the analysis is the same for all
\bm{\mu}_{k,i}(\theta)
. We write the dependencies whenever we want to emphasize them.
\blacksquare

The transition from
\bm{\nu}_{k,i-1}
to
\bm{\nu}_{k,i}
given by (
27
) is a random linear transform. Let
\bm{\nu}_{i}\triangleq[\bm{\nu}_{1,i},\dots,\bm{\nu}_{K,i}]^{\T}
and define the diagonal
K\times K
random diagonal matrices
\bm{R}_{i}
with their
k^{\text{th}}
diagonal element being
\bm{r}_{k,i}
. Then for all
\omega\in\mathcal{G}(\epsilon)
, expression (
27
) leads to the following vector relation:

\displaystyle\bm{\nu}_{i}=(1-\epsilon)^{-1}(A^{\T}\bm{R}_{i})\bm{\nu}_{i-1},\quad\forall i>\bm{i}_{0}(\omega).

(28)

Equation (
28
) highlights that the asymptotic behavior of the random matrix product

\bm{Y}_{i}\triangleq\prod_{j=1}^{i}(A^{\T}\bm{R}_{j})

(29)

plays an important role. Observe that since
\{\bm{R}_{i}\}
is a stationary sequence, the asymptotic behavior remains unchanged under any time shift. Hence, starting the product from
j=1
in equation (
29
) is without loss of generality. We now turn our attention to the analysis of the random matrices
\{\bm{Y}_{i}\}
.

III-B
2
Asymptotic Behavior of
\{{\it\bm{Y}}_{i}\}

The asymptotic behavior of random matrix products is an important and challenging problem with a long history, which includes the preliminary study
[
58
]
, and later the seminal work
[
59
]
. Although some results exist under certain assumptions on the structure of the random matrices to be multiplied, the problem remains open in general. One difficulty is that the non-commutativity of matrices under multiplication prevents the use of well-known convergence theorems such as the law of large numbers. Extending the result of
[
59
]
, reference
[
60
]
studied the more general case of “subadditive processes”, and derived, under some fairly general conditions, an ergodic theorem known as Kingman’s subadditive ergodic theorem
[
60
]
. The analysis of random matrix products turns out to be a special case of this result.

Theorem 2

(Kingman’s subadditive ergodic theorem
[
60
]
)
.

Consider a stationary sequence of random matrices
\{\mathbf{X}_{i}\}
and suppose that the elements of
\mathbf{X}_{i}
’s are positive and that their logarithms have finite expectations. Let
\bm{Y}_{i}\triangleq\prod_{j=1}^{i}\mathbf{X}_{j}
. Then, the limit

\bm{\gamma}=\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{i}\log\left[\bm{Y}_{i}\right]_{\ell k}

(30)

exists and is finite almost surely and in the mean, and does not depend on
\ell
or
k
. Furthermore, it holds that

\mathbb{E}[\bm{\gamma}]=\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{i}\mathbb{E}[\log\left[\bm{Y}_{i}\right]_{\ell k}].

(31)

\blacksquare

We now adapt the above theorem to our setting. First of all, observe that the
\{\bm{R}_{i}\}
is an i.i.d. sequence, and therefore the
\{A^{\T}\bm{R}_{i}\}
is also i.i.d., and hence, stationary. Note that we cannot simply replace the
\mathbf{X}_{i}
’s with
A^{\T}\bm{R}_{i}
’s because the matrix
A^{\T}
need not have all positive entries. However,
A
is a primitive matrix and thus there must exist some
n\geq 1
such that every entry of
A^{n}
is strictly positive
[
2
]
and
[
61
, Chapter 8]
. Using this observation, we arrive at the following corollary.

Corollary 1

(Limit of the random matrix product)
.

Consider
\bm{Y}_{i}=\prod_{j=1}^{i}(A^{\T}\bm{R}_{j})
. Under Assumptions 1 and 2, the finite limit

\displaystyle\gamma=\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{i}\log\left[\bm{Y}_{i}\right]_{\ell k}=\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{i}\mathbb{E}[\log\left[\bm{Y}_{i}\right]_{\ell k}]

(32)

exists almost surely, is a constant, and does not depend on
\ell
or
k
.

Proof.

See Appendix
-B
.
∎

Using Corollary
1
, we are able to characterize the asymptotic decay rate for AA-diffusion and conclude this section.

Theorem 3

(Asymptotic decay rate of AA-diffusion)
.

For any agent
k
, it holds almost surely:

\displaystyle\bm{\rho}_{k}^{(\mathrm{AA})}(\theta)=-\lim_{i\to\infty}\frac{1}{i}\log\bm{\mu}_{k,i}(\theta)=-\gamma(\theta).

(33)

Proof.

See Appendix
-C
.
∎

Remark:
A weaker result obtained in
[
21
]
ensures
\bm{\rho}\geq-\gamma
, with
\bm{\rho}
defined in (
11
). It can be verified that this is an implication of Theorem
3
.
\blacksquare

Theorem
3
states that if AA-diffusion is executed, the beliefs on a false hypothesis
\theta
decay exponentially almost surely, and the decay rate is constant and is the same among all agents. Note, however, that the decay rate may vary across
\theta
. This is because
\gamma=\gamma(\theta)
is the limit pertaining to the i.i.d. products of the matrices
A^{\T}\bm{R}_{i}(\theta)
, where
\bm{R}_{i}(\theta)
is defined in terms of the
\bm{r}_{k,i}(\theta)
— see (
27
).

IV
Bounds on the Asymptotic Decay Rate

It is stated in
[
60
]
that “pride of place among the unsolved problems of subadditive ergodic theory must go to the calculation of the constant
\gamma
”. To the best of our knowledge, no standard machinery exists to date for this end. Therefore, we make use of the special structure of the matrix
A^{\T}\bm{R}_{i}
to obtain bounds for
\gamma
in Sections
IV-B
and
IV-C
. But before those, we first provide some simple upper and lower bounds that hold for the general cases.

IV-A

Bounds Based on Subadditivity

First, it is useful to discuss why products of random matrices are related to subadditive processes. Let
\|X\|
denote any matrix norm that is submultiplicative, i.e., for any
X
and
Y

\displaystyle\|XY\|\leq\|X\|\|Y\|.

(34)

For our problem at hand, replacing
X
,
Y
with
\bm{Y}_{i}^{j}
,
\bm{Y}_{j}^{m}
whose general definition is given as

\bm{Y}_{i}^{m}\triangleq\prod_{t=i+1}^{m}(A^{\T}\bm{R}_{t}),

(35)

and taking the logarithms of the both sides yield the subadditive relation

\displaystyle\log\|\bm{Y}_{i}^{m}\|\leq\log\|\bm{Y}_{i}^{j}\|+\log\|\bm{Y}_{j}^{m}\|.

(36)

A well-known property of subadditive functions
[
62
]
is that

\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}\frac{1}{i}\log\|\bm{Y}_{i}\|=\inf_{i}\frac{1}{i}\log\|\bm{Y}_{i}\|,

(37)

where
\bm{Y}_{i}
is defined in (
29
). In fact, this observation is the starting point of the work
[
59
]
on random matrix products. Now, consider the norm of matrices with non-negative entries:
\|X\|_{1}\triangleq\max_{\ell}\sum_{k}[X]_{k\ell}
, which is submultiplicative. It is then imminent from Corollary
1
and (
37
) that for any
j

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle=\lim_{i\to\infty}\frac{1}{i}\mathbb{E}\big[\log[\bm{Y}_{i}]_{11}\big]\leq\lim_{i\to\infty}\frac{1}{i}\mathbb{E}\big[\log\|\bm{Y}_{i}\|_{1}\big]

\displaystyle=\inf_{i}\frac{1}{i}\mathbb{E}\big[\log\|\bm{Y}_{i}\|_{1}\big]\leq\frac{1}{j}\mathbb{E}\big[\log\|\bm{Y}_{j}\|_{1}\big],

(38)

which yields an upper bound for
\gamma
.

For the lower bound, we aim to create a supermultiplicative process. Let
\|X\|_{-}\triangleq\min_{\ell}\sum_{k}[X]_{k\ell}
be the minimum column sum of the matrix
X
. Note that this is not a norm. However, it is supermultiplicative for non-negative matrices, i.e.,

\displaystyle\|XY\|_{-}\geq\|X\|_{-}\|Y\|_{-}.

(39)

We then obtain the following result.

Lemma 1

(Bounds based on subadditivity)
.

For any
i
,
j\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{i}\mathbb{E}\big[\log\|\bm{Y}_{i}\|_{-}\big]\leq\gamma\leq\frac{1}{j}\mathbb{E}\big[\log\|\bm{Y}_{j}\|_{1}\big].

(40)

Proof.

The upper bound follows directly from (
IV-A
). For the lower bound, observe that
-\log\|\bm{Y}_{i}\|_{-}
is a subadditive process. We then have

\displaystyle\sup_{i}\frac{1}{i}\mathbb{E}\big[\log\|\bm{Y}_{i}\|_{-}\big]

\displaystyle=\lim_{i\to\infty}\frac{1}{i}\mathbb{E}\big[\log\|\bm{Y}_{i}\|_{-}\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\lim_{i\to\infty}\frac{1}{i}\log\|\bm{Y}_{i}\|_{-}\bigg]=\gamma.

(41)

where the interchange of the limit and expectation operations is due to the general form of the subadditive ergodic theorem — see Appendix
-D
for more detail.
∎

We point out that
\|\cdot\|_{-}
and
\|\cdot\|_{1}
can be replaced with any suitable functions
f
,
g
on positive matrices that are super/submultiplicative respectively; and satisfy the conditions of the subadditive ergodic theorem. This way, one obtains lower and upper bounds for the rate of AA-diffusion with a method similar to what we did in Lemma
1
.

In light of the above observation, we remark that
\|.\|_{-}
can also be replaced with an element of
\bm{Y}_{i}
, e.g., with any mapping
\bm{Y}_{i}\mapsto[\bm{Y}_{i}]_{kk}
, as long as it remains strictly positive for all
i
— this is to ensure that the logarithm remains finite. It can be verified that
[\bm{Y}_{i}]_{kk}
is supermultiplicative as long as
\bm{Y}_{i}
’s are non-negative. Recall that at least one agent has a self-loop, which implies
a_{kk}>0
for some
k
. Without loss of generality, assume
k=1
. The self-loop assumption then ensures that
[\bm{Y}_{i}]_{11}
remains strictly positive. Then, according to Lemma
1
, we have

\mathbb{E}[\log[\bm{Y}_{1}]_{11}]=\log a_{11}+\mathbb{E}[\log{\bm{r}_{1,1}}]\leq\gamma

(42)

which, by Theorem
3
, implies that

\displaystyle\rho^{(\mathrm{AA})}

\displaystyle\leq-\log a_{11}-\mathbb{E}[\log{\bm{r}_{1,1}}]

\displaystyle=-\log a_{11}+D(L_{1}(\cdot|\theta^{\circ})||L_{1}(\cdot|\theta)).

(43)

The inequality (
43
) gives rise to an interesting observation of the learning model under AA-diffusion. For a small
\delta>0
, suppose that
a_{11}=1-\delta
is close to one and
D(L_{1}(\cdot|\theta^{\circ})||L_{1}(\cdot\theta))\leq\delta
is small. This suggests that agent 1 is highly self-confident despite limited learning abilities. For such special case, observe that

\displaystyle\rho^{(\mathrm{AA})}

\displaystyle\leq-\log(1-\delta)+\delta\leq\delta/(1-\delta)+\delta

(44)

is also small. Therefore, since all agents learn the truth at the same rate
\rho^{(\mathrm{AA})}
by Theorem
3
, the inept and self-confident agent 1 drastically decreases the learning ability of the whole network. This phenomenon can be avoided under GA-diffusion: if the remaining agents do not trust agent 1, i.e., if
a_{1k}
’s are small, then the first element of the Perron vector,
\pi_{1}
, can be kept small as well. It is evident from (
III-A
) that such isolation of agent 1 from the network will preserve (and might even boost) the learning rate of the remaining agents. This observation is consistent with the numerical results in Section
V
.

IV-B

Bounds for the Special Case of Distributed Inference

In this section, we take advantage of the special structure of
\{\bm{Y}_{i}\}
in our distributed setting and derive bounds for
\gamma
. To that end, observe first that

\displaystyle[\bm{Y}_{i}]_{1k}=\bm{r}_{k,i}\sum_{\ell}[\bm{Y}_{i-1}]_{1\ell}a_{k\ell}.

(45)

This implies that

\displaystyle\log[\bm{Y}_{i}]_{1k}=\log\bm{r}_{k,i}+\log\bigg(\sum_{\ell}[\bm{Y}_{i-1}]_{1\ell}a_{k\ell}\bigg),

(46)

and averaging over the network with weights
\pi_{k}
yields that

\sum_{k}\pi_{k}\log\frac{[\bm{Y}_{i}]_{1k}}{\pi_{k}}=\sum_{k}\pi_{k}\log\bm{r}_{k,i}+\sum_{k}\pi_{k}\log\frac{\sum_{\ell}[\bm{Y}_{i-1}]_{1\ell}a_{k\ell}}{\pi_{k}}.

(47)

We subtract
\sum_{k}\pi_{k}\log\dfrac{[\bm{Y}_{i-1}]_{1k}}{\pi_{k}}
from both sides of this equation to obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{k}

\displaystyle\pi_{k}\log\frac{[\bm{Y}_{i}]_{1k}}{\pi_{k}}-\sum_{k}\pi_{k}\log\frac{[\bm{Y}_{i-1}]_{1k}}{\pi_{k}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\pi_{k}\log\bm{r}_{k,i}+\sum_{k}\pi_{k}\log\frac{\sum_{\ell}[\bm{Y}_{i-1}]_{1\ell}a_{k\ell}}{[\bm{Y}_{i-1}]_{1k}}.

(48)

If we take the time average of (
48
) from
j=2
to
i
, the left-hand side becomes a telescoping sum where the intermediate terms cancel each other, and we arrive at the following relation:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{i}\sum_{k}\pi_{k}\log\frac{[\bm{Y}_{i}]_{1k}}{\pi_{k}}-\frac{1}{i}\sum_{k}\pi_{k}\log\frac{[\bm{Y}_{1}]_{1k}}{\pi_{k}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{i}\sum_{j=2}^{i}\big(\sum_{k}\pi_{k}\log\bm{r}_{k,j}\big)+\frac{1}{i}\sum_{j=2}^{i}\sum_{k}\pi_{k}\log\frac{\sum_{\ell}[\bm{Y}_{j-1}]_{1\ell}a_{k\ell}}{[\bm{Y}_{j-1}]_{1k}}.

(49)

Assume
[\bm{Y}_{1}]_{1k}>0
for simplicity. Note that there is no loss of generality here since the strong connectivity assumption on the network ensures the primitiveness of the combination matrix
A
which in turn ensures
[\bm{Y}_{1}]_{1k}
to be strictly positive eventually— see Appendix
-B
. The left-hand side tends to
\gamma
by Theorem
3
, and since
\bm{r}_{k,i}
’s are i.i.d., the first term on the right-hand side tends to its mean by the law of large numbers and is equal to the negative of the decay rate of GA-diffusion — see (
19
), (
III-A
). In other words,

\displaystyle\gamma=\sum_{k}\pi_{k}\mathbb{E}[\log\bm{r}_{k,j}]+\lim_{i\to\infty}\frac{1}{i}\sum_{j=2}^{i}\sum_{k}\pi_{k}\log\frac{\sum_{\ell}[\bm{Y}_{j-1}]_{1\ell}a_{k\ell}}{[\bm{Y}_{j-1}]_{1k}}

(50)

and accordingly,

\displaystyle\rho^{{(\mathrm{GA})}}-\rho^{(\mathrm{AA})}\!=\!\lim_{i\to\infty}\frac{1}{i}\sum_{j=2}^{i}\sum_{k}\pi_{k}\log\frac{\sum_{\ell}[\bm{Y}_{j-1}]_{1\ell}a_{k\ell}}{[\bm{Y}_{j-1}]_{1k}}

(51)

The above equation quantifies the gap between the decay rates. The gap turns out to be the difference of two KL divergences averaged over time. To see this, let
\bm{u}
denote the probability vector obtained by normalization of the first row of
\bm{Y}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle[\bm{u}_{i}]_{k}\triangleq\frac{[\bm{Y}_{i}]_{1k}}{\sum_{\ell}[\bm{Y}_{i}]_{1\ell}}.

(52)

Then, it follows that

\rho^{{(\mathrm{GA})}}\!-\rho^{(\mathrm{AA})}\!=\!\!\lim_{i\to\infty}\frac{1}{i}\!\sum_{j=2}^{i}\!\!\big[D(\pi||\bm{u}_{j-1})-D(\pi||A\bm{u}_{j-1})\big]

(53)

Equation (
53
) has an interesting interpretation. Note that
A\bm{u}_{j-1}
is another probability vector that is obtained by passing
\bm{u}_{j-1}
through the Markov matrix
A
. Furthermore,
A\pi=\pi
is the unique invariant distribution of the kernel
A
and thus the difference term in (
53
) is equal to

\displaystyle D(\pi||\bm{u}_{j-1})-D(A\pi||A\bm{u}_{j-1}).

(54)

The well-known data processing theorem
[
62
]
ensures that this difference is non-negative. Hence,

Proposition 1
.

\rho^{(\mathrm{GA})}\geq\rho^{(\mathrm{AA})}
almost surely.
\blacksquare

The only case where the limit in (
53
) — the performance gap — tends to zero is when
D(\pi||\bm{u}_{i})\to 0
, or simply when
\bm{u}_{i}\to\pi
. We will show that if the network has sufficient connectivity, this can only happen when all agents receive the same data, i.e.,
\bm{r}_{k,i}=\bm{r}_{\ell,i}
for all agents
k
and
\ell
. It is obvious that in this case most fusion methods, and in particular AA and GA, are equivalent. In fact, there is no need for an agent to communicate with its neighbors — every neighbor is equivalent to the agent itself.

If the network is connected enough, and when the agents do not observe the same data,
\rho^{(\mathrm{GA})}
is
strictly
greater than
\rho^{(\mathrm{AA})}
, and the gap is quantified as the limit term in (
53
). To study this term, we regard
\{\bm{u}_{i}\}
as a Markov chain. We denote the
K
-dimensional probability simplex as
\mathbb{S}_{K}
, and define the time-homogeneous transition map
T:\mathbb{S}_{K}\times\mathbb{R}^{K}\to\mathbb{S}_{K}
as

\displaystyle T(u,\bm{R})\triangleq\frac{\bm{R}Au}{\sum_{\ell}[\bm{R}Au]_{\ell}}

(55)

where
\bm{R}
is the diagonal matrix with
[\bm{R}]_{kk}=\bm{r}_{k}\triangleq\bm{r}_{k,1}
due to time-homogeneity. Note that the
\bm{u}_{i}
’s in (
53
) obey this map with
\bm{u}_{i}=T(\bm{u}_{i-1},\bm{R}_{i})
, and with
\bm{R}_{i}
independent of
\bm{u}_{i-1}
. It can be verified that the Markov chain governed by the mapping
T
has at least one invariant distribution
\mathbb{Q}
on
\mathbb{S}_{K}
, i.e., if
\bm{u}
has distribution
\mathbb{Q}
, so has
T(\bm{u},\bm{R})
— this comes from the observation that
T
is Feller continuous and from Krylov-Bogolyubov theorem for compact spaces
[
63
]
. However, it may be a futile attempt to find such invariant distributions. Furthermore, it is not certain if there is a unique invariant distribution although the limit in (
53
) exists. Hence, we resort to a different method and study a lower bound for the gap.

We now give the intuition behind our derivation for the lower bound. Since the state space of the Markov chain
\mathbb{S}_{K}
is compact, there must exist a
u_{0}
and a small neighborhood
\mathcal{V}_{0}
around it that is visited infinitely often. So, the limit in (
53
) is lower bounded as

\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}\frac{1}{i}\sum_{j=2}^{i}\big[D(\pi||\bm{u}_{j-1})-D(\pi||A\bm{u}_{j-1})\big]

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{u\in\mathcal{V}_{0}}\mathbb{E}[D(\pi||\bm{u}_{1})-D(\pi||A\bm{u}_{1})]

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{u\in\mathbb{S}_{K}}\mathbb{E}[D(\pi||\bm{u}_{1})-D(\pi||A\bm{u}_{1})]

(56)

with
\bm{u}_{1}=T(u,\bm{R})
. Furthermore, we use the strong data processing inequality
[
64
]
to obtain a further lower bound. It is then useful to give the following definition.

Definition 1

(Contraction coefficient
[
64
]
)
.

Let
A_{K\times K}
be a probability transition matrix. Then the contraction coefficient associated with
A
is given by

\vskip-10.00002pt\eta_{A}\triangleq\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}u,v\in\mathbb{S}_{K}\\
u\neq v\end{subarray}}\frac{D(Au||Av)}{D(u||v)}\leq 1.

(57)

\blacksquare

The coefficient
\eta_{A}
is lower bounded by the second largest absolute eigenvalue of
A

[
64
]
. Hence, dense graphs usually lead to lower
\eta_{A}
that is close to 0 in value, while sparse graphs usually lead to
\eta_{A}
that is close to 1 in value. The strong data processing inequality implies that

D(Au||Av)\leq\eta_{A}D(u||v)

(58)

for all
K
-dimensional probability vectors
u
,
v
. Applying this inequality to (
56
), we obtain

\rho^{{(\mathrm{GA})}}-\rho^{(\mathrm{AA})}\geq(1-\eta_{A})\inf_{u\in\mathbb{S}_{K}}\mathbb{E}[D(\pi||\bm{u}_{1})].

(59)

Remark:
Applying the strong data processing inequality directly to (
53
), it is evident that

\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}\frac{1}{i}\sum_{j=1}^{i}D(\pi||\bm{u}_{j})

\displaystyle\geq\rho^{{(\mathrm{GA})}}-\rho^{(\mathrm{AA})}

\displaystyle\geq(1-\eta_{A})\lim_{i\to\infty}\frac{1}{i}\sum_{j=1}^{i}D(\pi||\bm{u}_{j}).

(60)

Therefore, when
\eta_{A}=0
, we have equality. This corresponds to the case when
A
is rank one, with every column of
A
being
\pi
. For this special case, we will show in Sec.
IV-C1
that
\gamma
has a simple form.
\blacksquare

We now focus on the infimization problem that shows up in (
59
). Writing explicitly, it is equivalent to

\displaystyle\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\\
v=uA^{\T}\end{subarray}}\!\!\mathbb{E}\bigg[\!\sum_{k}\pi_{k}\log\frac{\pi_{k}}{v_{k}\bm{r}_{k}}+\log(\sum_{k}v_{k}\bm{r}_{k})\bigg]

\displaystyle=\!\!\!\!\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\\
v=uA^{\T}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{k}\pi_{k}\log\frac{\pi_{k}}{v_{k}}+\log(\sum_{k}v_{k}\bm{r}_{k})\bigg]+\rho^{(\mathrm{GA})}

\displaystyle\geq\!\inf_{v}\sum_{k}\!\pi_{k}\log\frac{\pi_{k}}{v_{k}}+\mathbb{E}\bigg[\log(\sum_{k}\!v_{k}\bm{r}_{k})\bigg]\!+\!\rho^{(\mathrm{GA})}

(61)

We give the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions for this problem in the next result.

Lemma 2

(Optimality conditions)
.

The KKT conditions for the optimization problem in (
61
), namely the infimization of

F(v)\triangleq\sum_{k}\pi_{k}\log\frac{\pi_{k}}{v_{k}}+\mathbb{E}\bigg[\log(\sum_{k}v_{k}\bm{r}_{k})\bigg]+\rho^{(\mathrm{GA})},

(62)

are given by

\frac{\pi_{k}}{v_{k}}=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{\bm{r}_{k}}{\sum_{\ell}\bm{r}_{\ell}v_{\ell}}\bigg]\quad\forall k.

(63)

Proof.

See Appendix
-E
.
∎

Note that
F(v)
cannot approach the infimum over the boundary of the

tends to infinity close to the boundary. Using the KKT conditions above, and replacing
\dfrac{\pi_{k}}{v_{k}}

in the first summation term in

G(v)\triangleq\sum_{k}\pi_{k}\log\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{\bm{r}_{k}}{\sum_{\ell}v_{\ell}\bm{r}_{\ell}}\bigg]+\mathbb{E}\bigg[\log(\sum_{k}v_{k}\bm{r}_{k})\bigg]+\rho^{(\mathrm{GA})}.

(Variational lower bound to the gap)

The performance gap is lower bounded as

\displaystyle\rho^{(\mathrm{GA})}-\rho^{(\mathrm{AA})}\geq(1-\eta_{A})\inf_{v}F(v)\geq(1-\eta_{A})\inf_{v}G(v)

with

IV-C

Some Special Cases

The bounds given in the previous sections are in variational form. Although we have studied certain characteristics of these bounds, i.e., found the KKT conditions, still, the bounds are highly dependent on the joint distribution of the data across the users — recall that the decay rate of GA-diffusion,
\rho^{(\mathrm{GA})}
, has a closed form expression and only depends on the marginals. Moreover, the extremal points satisfying the KKT conditions are difficult to find in general. Hence, we study two special cases in this section.

IV-C
1
Rank-one Combination Matrices

When
A
is a rank one matrix, it turns out that
\rho^{(\mathrm{AA})}
has a closed form. In this case,
A
can be written as
A=\pi\mathds{1}_{K}^{\T}
. Then,

\displaystyle A^{\T}\bm{R}A^{\T}

\displaystyle=\mathds{1}_{K}\pi^{\T}\bm{R}\mathds{1}_{K}\pi^{\T}

\displaystyle=\big(\sum_{k}\pi_{k}\bm{r}_{k}\big)\mathds{1}_{K}\pi^{\T}

\displaystyle=\big(\sum_{k}\pi_{k}\bm{r}_{k}\big)A^{\T}

(68)

and it follows that

\bm{Y}_{i}=\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}(A^{\T}\bm{R}_{j})(A^{\T}\bm{R}_{i})=\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}\big(\sum_{k}\pi_{k}\bm{r}_{k,j}\big)(A^{\T}\bm{R}_{i}).

(69)

If we choose
k
,
\ell
such that
a_{\ell k}>0
, it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{i}\log[\bm{Y}_{i}]_{k\ell}=\frac{1}{i}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\log\big(\sum_{k}\pi_{k}\bm{r}_{k,j}\big)+\frac{1}{i}\log({a_{\ell k}\bm{r}_{\ell,i}})

(70)

and from the law of large numbers

\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}\frac{1}{i}\log[\bm{Y}_{i}]_{k\ell}=\mathbb{E}\Big[\log\big(\sum_{k}\pi_{k}\bm{r}_{k}\big)\Big].

(71)

Corollary
1
and Theorem
3
then lead to the following.

Proposition 2

(Exact rate under rank-one topologies)
.

\rho^{(\mathrm{AA})}=-\gamma=-\mathbb{E}\Big[\log\big(\sum_{k}\pi_{k}\bm{r}_{k}\big)\Big].

(72)

\blacksquare

Notice from above that the performance gap

\rho^{(\mathrm{GA})}-\rho^{(\mathrm{AA})}=\mathbb{E}\Big[\log\big(\sum_{k}\pi_{k}\bm{r}_{k}\big)-\sum_{k}\pi_{k}\log\bm{r}_{k}\Big]

(73)

is equal to the expectation of a Jensen’s inequality gap.

Fully-connected networks with rank-one combination matrices are equivalent to architectures with fusion center in terms of performance, because each agent computes the same weighted average of all beliefs across the network. In this sense, each agent acts like a fusion center. Therefore, this special case result is of interest for inference problems with central processors. Consider a federated system where (
i
) agents send their beliefs to the center, (
ii
) the center averages the peripheral agents’ beliefs with importance sampling, and (
iii
) sends the aggregated belief back to the agents. The learning rate of such system is given in Proposition
2
.

IV-C
2
Exchangeable Networks

In this special case, we assume that the data is exchangeable across the users. More precisely,
\{\bm{\xi}_{1}(\theta),\dots,\bm{\xi}_{K}(\theta)\}
constitutes a set of exchangeable random variables for every
\theta\in\Theta
.

Definition 2

(Exchangeable random variables)
.

A set of random variables is called exchangeable if the distribution of

\bm{\xi}_{1},\dots,\bm{\xi}_{K}
remains unchanged under any permutation over the index set, i.e., for all
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{k}

\mathbb{P}\left(\bm{\xi}_{1}\leq\xi_{1},\dots,\bm{\xi}_{K}\leq\xi_{k}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\bm{\xi}_{\sigma_{1}}\leq\xi_{1},\dots,\bm{\xi}_{\sigma_{K}}\leq\xi_{k}\right)

(74)

with
\sigma
being any permutation of
\{1,\dots,K\}
.
\blacksquare

Exchangeability is a weaker assumption than i.i.d., as the data need not be independent across the agents. Observe that exchangeable networks could be of particular interest as they model fair networks — since exchangeability requires identical distributions of data across the agents, no agent learns better than another if there was no cooperation.
For this particular example, we also assume that
A
is doubly stochastic, i.e.,
\sum_{k}a_{\ell k}=1
as well. Then, it is easily seen that each element of the Perron vector of
A
is
\pi_{k}=1/K
. Under this assumption, we are able to solve the KKT conditions of the problem (
61
).

Theorem 5

(Lower bound under exchangeable networks)
.

If the data across the agents is exchangeable and
A
is doubly stochastic,

\pi
is the unique solution of (
61
). Hence,

\displaystyle\rho^{(\mathrm{AA})}\leq\eta_{A}\rho^{(\mathrm{GA})}-(1-\eta_{A})\mathbb{E}\Big[\log\Big(\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k}\bm{r}_{k}\Big)\Big]

(75)

and moreover,

\displaystyle\rho^{(\mathrm{GA})}-\rho^{(\mathrm{AA})}

\displaystyle\geq(1-\eta_{A})\left(\rho^{(\mathrm{GA})}+\mathbb{E}\Big[\log\Big(\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k}\bm{r}_{k}\Big)\Big]\right)

\displaystyle=(1-\eta_{A})\mathbb{E}\Big[\log\big(\sum_{k}\frac{1}{K}\bm{r}_{k}\big)\!-\!\!\sum_{k}\frac{1}{K}\log\bm{r}_{k}\Big]

(76)

Proof.

It immediately follows that
\bm{r}_{1},\dots,\bm{r}_{K}
is also exchangeable. Also, the KKT conditions in (
63
) imply

\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\frac{\bm{r}_{1}v_{1}}{\sum_{\ell}\bm{r}_{\ell}v_{\ell}}\Bigg]=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\frac{\bm{r}_{2}v_{2}}{\sum_{\ell}\bm{r}_{\ell}v_{\ell}}\Bigg]

(77)

and because of exchangeability,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\frac{\bm{r}_{1}v_{1}}{\bm{r}_{1}v_{1}+\bm{r}_{2}v_{2}+\bm{s}}\Bigg]=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\frac{\bm{r}_{1}v_{2}}{\bm{r}_{2}v_{1}+\bm{r}_{1}v_{2}+\bm{s}}\Bigg]

(78)

where
\bm{s}\triangleq\sum_{\ell>2}\bm{r}_{\ell}v_{\ell}

v_{1}>v_{2}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\frac{\bm{r}_{1}v_{1}}{\bm{r}_{1}v_{1}+\bm{r}_{2}v_{2}+\bm{s}}-\frac{\bm{r}_{1}v_{2}}{\bm{r}_{2}v_{1}+\bm{r}_{1}v_{2}+\bm{s}}\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\!\!(v_{1}-v_{2})\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\frac{\bm{r}_{1}\bm{r}_{2}(v_{1}+v_{2})+\bm{r}_{1}\bm{s}}{(\bm{r}_{1}v_{1}+\bm{r}_{2}v_{2}+\bm{s})(\bm{r}_{2}v_{1}+\bm{r}_{1}v_{2}+\bm{s})}\Bigg].

(79)

Since we assumed
v_{1}>v_{2}
, the difference in (
78
) must be strictly greater than zero. A similar argument for
v_{2}>v_{1}
yields the same result. This implies
v_{1}=v_{2}
, and by symmetry,
v_{k}=v_{\ell}
for all
k,\ell
. Therefore,
v
must be equal to
\pi^{\T}
.
∎

It is seen from Theorem
5
that the lower bound on the performance gap increases as the network becomes more connected, i.e., small
\eta_{A}
. Moreover, the Jensen’s inequality gap in (
73
) and Theorem
5
increases when the observations become more diverse across the agents. The performance gap drifts away from zero. This results in an interesting observation: If the individual agents, or any subgroup of
M
agents, have the same learning abilities (implied by exchangeability), the overall learning speed of the network decreases with respect to the increased amount of collaboration under AA-diffusion. However, the learning abilities under GA-diffusion is not affected — every agent in the network learns at the same speed regardless of the amount of collaboration.

VI
Discussion

In this work, we compared the decay rates under arithmetic and geometric averaging of beliefs for social learning over networks. For arithmetic averaging strategies, we established that the beliefs on wrong hypotheses decay exponentially almost surely, and the decay rates are the same among all agents. We provided upper and lower bounds for the decay rates, one being the decay rate corresponding to geometric averaging (Proposition
1
) and the other one revealing an interesting phenomenon (at the end of Section
IV-A
), which we called the “inept agent” phenomenon. Specifically, if there is a highly self-confident agent in the network whose learning abilities are limited, the decay rates of every single agent in the network drop significantly. Such phenomenon is particular to arithmetic averaging and can be mitigated with geometric averaging if other agents follow a skeptical approach towards the information conveyed by the inept agent.

We studied the performance gap between arithmetic and geometric averaging in detail with an appropriate formulation that permitted the use of the strong data processing inequality. In turn, we have proved that for a broad class of networks, there is no performance gap if, and only if, all agents observe the same data. Furthermore, we have obtained closed form bounds and expressions for the performance gap for some special instances. The strong data processing coefficient
\eta_{A}
may not be sufficiently tight for our analyses and it might be replaced with
\tilde{\eta}_{A}
for obtaining tighter bounds for our problem. The calculation of
\tilde{\eta}_{A}
, however, seems challenging and might be an interesting problem on its own.

An interesting future direction is to examine how our results relate to the distributed estimation and filtering. Such methods primarily focus on the inference of continuous variables, and they typically rely on the fusion of point estimates. Nevertheless, they can be interpreted in terms of fusion of probability density functions
[
5
]
. Therefore, a natural question is the extend of applicability of our results for social learning to that line of work. Furthermore, all results in this work are asymptotic. In the subsequent work
[
68
]
, we established the asymptotic normality of AA for federated architectures. Employing the Berry-Esseen theorem
[
69
]
and related tools on top of this work to understand finite sample behavior of the AA algorithm can be another interesting direction to study.

Proof of Corollary

which turn out to have all positive entries. To see this, observe that

\displaystyle[\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{i}]_{11}=\sum_{\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{n-1}}a_{\ell_{1}1}a_{\ell_{2}\ell_{1}}\dots a_{1\ell_{n-1}}\bm{r}_{\ell_{1},1}\dots\bm{r}_{1,n}.

Since
A
is primitive, there must exist
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{n-1}
such that

\displaystyle[\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{i}]_{11}\geq a_{\ell_{1}1}a_{\ell_{2}\ell_{1}}\dots a_{1\ell_{n-1}}\bm{r}_{\ell_{1},1}\dots\bm{r}_{1,n}.

(92)

As mentioned,
\bm{r}_{k,i}
’s are strictly positive and thus
[\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{i}]_{11}
is strictly positive as well. Similar arguments hold for other
[\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{i}]_{\ell k}
’s as well, which proves that
\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{i}
is all positive. Note that
\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{i}
’s are also i.i.d.. The next step is to check if the logarithms of the entries of
\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{i}
have finite expectations. First of all, since
\mathbb{E}[\bm{R}_{i}]=I
and
\bm{R}_{i}
’s are i.i.d., it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{i}]=\mathbb{E}[A^{\T}\bm{R}_{n(i-1)+1}\dots A^{\T}\bm{R}_{ni}]=(A^{\T})^{n},

(93)

which further implies that

(94)

92
), we obtain

\mathbb{E}[\log[\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{i}]_{11}]\geq\mathbb{E}\log(a_{\ell_{1}1}a_{\ell_{2}\ell_{1}}\dots a_{1\ell_{n-1}})+\mathbb{E}[\log\bm{r}_{\ell_{1},1}]+\dots+\mathbb{E}[\log\bm{r}_{1,n}].

By Assumption 1, it is true that

\mathbb{E}[\log\bm{r}_{k,1}]=-D(L_{k}(.|\theta^{\circ})||L_{k}(.|\theta))>-\infty.

(96)

Consequently,
\mathbb{E}[\log[\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{i}]_{11}]

\mathbb{E}[\log[\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{i}]_{\ell k}]

[\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{i}]_{\ell k}

\bm{\tilde{\gamma}}=\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{i}\log[\widetilde{\bm{Y}}_{i}]_{\ell k}

\widetilde{\bm{Y}}_{i}=\bm{Y}_{ni}

\bm{\gamma}=\frac{\bm{\tilde{\gamma}}}{n}=\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{ni}\log[\bm{Y}_{ni}]_{\ell k}=\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{j}\log[\bm{Y}_{j}]_{\ell k}.

(98)

Moreover, since
\{A^{\T}\bm{R}_{i}\}
is an i.i.d. sequence, Kolmogorov’s zero-one law
[
70
]
implies that the finite limit
\bm{\gamma}
is almost surely a constant. Hence,

\gamma\triangleq\mathbb{E}[\bm{\gamma}]=\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{i}\mathbb{E}\big[\log[\bm{Y}_{i}]_{\ell k}\big].

and the proof is complete.

Proof of Theorem
3

It is sufficient to establish the result for one agent
k
; a similar argument applies to the other agents. We establish the proof in two parts:

•

(i)

\limsup\limits_{i\to\infty}\dfrac{1}{i}\log\bm{\mu}_{k,i}\leq\gamma
,

•

(ii)

\liminf\limits_{i\to\infty}\dfrac{1}{i}\log\bm{\mu}_{k,i}\geq\gamma
.

The part
(i)
of the proof makes use of the extremal process
\{\bm{\nu}_{i}\}
. Setting a
\delta>0
, we define the events

\mathcal{H}_{i_{0}}^{+}(\delta)\triangleq\Big\{\omega\in\Omega:\exists i_{1}\geq i_{0},\forall i\geq i_{1},\frac{1}{i-i_{0}}\max_{\ell,k}\log[\bm{Y}_{i_{0}}^{i}]_{\ell k}\leq\gamma+\delta\Big\}

(100)

for every
i_{0}\geq 1
with

\displaystyle\bm{Y}_{i_{0}}^{i}\triangleq\prod_{j=i_{0}+1}^{i}(A^{\T}\bm{R}_{j}).

(101)

In words,
\mathcal{H}_{i_{0}}^{+}(\delta)
is the event that the logarithms of all entries of
{\bm{Y}_{i_{0}}^{i}}
eventually become smaller than
(\gamma+\delta)(i-i_{0})
. Since
\{A^{\T}\bm{R}_{i}\}
is i.i.d.,
\bm{Y}_{i}
is stationary; and
\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{H}_{i_{0}}^{+}(\delta)\right)
does not depend on
i_{0}
. Corollary
1
states that
\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{H}_{0}^{+}(\delta)\right)=1
, and we deduce that
\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{H}_{i_{0}}^{+}(\delta)\right)=1
. As any countable intersection of unit-probability events is also unit-probability, we have
\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{H}^{+}(\delta)\right)=1
where

\mathcal{H}^{+}(\delta)\triangleq\cap_{i_{0}}\mathcal{H}_{i_{0}}^{+}(\delta).

(102)

Consider an
\omega\in\mathcal{G}(\epsilon)\cap\mathcal{H}^{+}(\delta)
, with
\mathcal{G}(\epsilon)
defined in (
23
). Repeated application of (
28
) yields

\bm{\nu}_{i}=(1-\epsilon)^{\bm{i}_{0}-i}\bm{Y}_{\bm{i}_{0}}^{i}\bm{\nu}_{\bm{i}_{0}},\quad\forall i\geq\bm{i}_{0}(\omega).

(103)

Furthermore, since
\omega\in\mathcal{H}^{+}(\delta)
implies

[\bm{Y}_{\bm{i}_{0}}^{i}]_{\ell k}\leq e^{(i-\bm{i}_{0})(\gamma+\delta)}

(104)

for all
i\geq\bm{i}_{1}(\omega)
,

\bm{\nu}_{k,i}\leq e^{(i-\bm{i}_{0})(\gamma+\delta+\epsilon^{\prime})},\quad\forall i\geq\bm{i}_{1}(\omega)

(105)

with some
\epsilon^{\prime}\triangleq-\log(1-\epsilon)
. Thus,

\limsup_{i\to\infty}\frac{1}{i}\log\bm{\mu}_{k,i}\leq\limsup_{i\to\infty}\frac{1}{i}\log\bm{\nu}_{k,i}\leq\gamma+\delta+\epsilon^{\prime}

(106)

for all
\omega\in\mathcal{G}(\epsilon)\cap\mathcal{H}^{+}(\delta)
. Since
\mathcal{G}(\epsilon)
and
\mathcal{H}^{+}(\delta)
are both probability one events, so is their intersection. This completes the part
(i)
of the proof.

The part (
ii
) of the proof requires the construction of another extremal process
\{\bm{z}_{k,i}\}
, which lower bounds
\{\bm{\mu}_{k,i}\}
. Similar to the part
(i)
, we define the events

\mathcal{H}_{i_{0}}^{-}(\delta)\triangleq\Big\{\omega\in\Omega:\exists i_{1}\geq i_{0},\forall i\geq i_{1},\frac{1}{i-i_{0}}\min_{\ell,k}\log[\bm{Y}_{i_{0}}^{i}]_{\ell k}\geq\gamma-\delta\Big\}

(107)

and

\mathcal{H}^{-}(\delta)\triangleq\cap_{i_{0}}\mathcal{H}_{i_{0}}^{-}(\delta).

(108)

Then, for any
\omega\in\mathcal{G}(\epsilon)\cap\mathcal{H}^{-}(\delta)
, we have
\bm{\mu}_{k,i}(\theta)\leq\epsilon
for
i\geq\bm{i}_{0}(\omega)
and

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}_{k,i}(\theta)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{N}_{k}}a_{\ell k}\dfrac{\bm{\mu}_{\ell,i-1}(\theta)\bm{r}_{\ell,i}(\theta)}{1+\epsilon\sum\limits_{\theta^{\prime}\neq\theta^{\circ}}\bm{r}_{\ell,i}(\theta)}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{N}_{k}}a_{\ell k}\dfrac{\bm{\mu}_{\ell,i-1}(\theta)\bm{r}_{\ell,i}(\theta)}{1+\epsilon\sum\limits_{\theta^{\prime}\neq\theta^{\circ}}\sum_{\ell}\bm{r}_{\ell,i}(\theta^{\prime})}.

(109)

We introduce the vector
\bm{z}_{i}=[\bm{z}_{1,i},\bm{z}_{2,i},\dots,\bm{z}_{K,i}]^{\T}
and define its evolution for
i\geq\bm{i}_{0}
as

\displaystyle\bm{z}_{i}=(A^{\T}\bm{R}_{i})\bm{z}_{i-1},\quad\bm{z}_{\bm{i}_{0}}=\bm{\mu}_{\bm{i}_{0}}.

(110)

This implies that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{i}\log\bm{\mu}_{k,i}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{i}\log\bm{z}_{k,i}-\frac{1}{i}\sum_{j=i_{0}}^{i}\log\bigg(1+\epsilon\sum_{\theta^{\prime}\neq\theta^{\circ}}\sum_{\ell}\bm{r}_{\ell,j}(\theta^{\prime})\bigg)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\geq}}\frac{1}{i}\log\bm{z}_{k,i}-\epsilon\frac{1}{i}\sum_{j=i_{0}}^{i}\sum_{\theta^{\prime}\neq\theta^{\circ}}\sum_{\ell}\bm{r}_{\ell,j}(\theta^{\prime})

(111)

where
(a)
follows from
\log(1+x)\leq x
.
Observe that by the strong law of large numbers

\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}\epsilon\frac{1}{i}\sum_{j=i_{0}}^{i}\sum_{\theta^{\prime}\neq\theta^{\circ}}\sum_{\ell}\bm{r}_{\ell,j}(\theta^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\epsilon K(H-1)\mathbb{E}[\bm{r}_{\ell,j}]

\displaystyle=\epsilon K(H-1).

(112)

Hence, proceeding similarly to the previous part we obtain that almost surely

(113)

Since
\delta

\epsilon
