Interface Identification constrained by Local-to-Nonlocal Coupling
Matthias Schuster, Volker Schulz

Abstract




Abstract.

Models of physical phenomena that use nonlocal operators are better suited for some applications than their classical counterparts that employ partial differential operators. However, the numerical solution of these nonlocal problems can be quite expensive. Therefore, Local-to-Nonlocal couplings have emerged that combine partial differential operators with nonlocal operators. In this work, we make use of an energy-based Local-to-Nonlocal coupling that serves as a constraint for an interface identification problem.

Keywords.
Shape optimization, energy-based Local-to-Nonlocal coupling, interface identification, Schwarz method.

Introduction

Nonlocal models have been successfully applied to a broad range of applications, e.g. anomalous or fractional diffusion
[
10
,

,
15
]
, peridynamics
[
35
,
23
]
, finance and jump processes
[

, machine learning
[

]
and image denoising
[

.
Typically, nonlocal operators are integral operators that are dependent on a function
\gamma
, which is called
kernel
. As a result, nonlocal operators allow interactions between different points in space and therefore some physical phenomena are better described in this nonlocal regime. One drawback is, that the computation of solutions regarding nonlocal equations can be quite costly. If, e.g. we apply the finite element method we need to assemble a stiffness matrix that is typically dense for nonlocal operators. Additionally, the weak formulation, as we see in Section
2
, consists of double integrals, which are computationally more demanding than single integrals that typically represent weak formulations of PDEs. Since these stiffness matrices are sparse for PDEs and some physical processes can also be modeled by PDEs, the coupling of nonlocal and ’local’ partial differential operators is one possibility to develop a model that is accurate but at the same time computationally not too expensive.
For more information on these Local-to-Nonlocal couplings we refer to the review paper
[
18
]
and the references therein.
As a result, the question arises, on which part of the domain the nonlocal operator should be deployed, which leads us to shape optimization and specifically to an interface identification problem as a first step to model this problem. An overview on the topic of shape optimization can be found in, e.g.
[
36
,
14
,
4
,
22
]
, and a similar interface identification problem constrained by partial differential equations is investigated in
[
41
]
. For an analysis of the interface identification problem constrained by completely nonlocal equations we refer to
[
33
]
.

The paper is organized as follows: First, we recall nonlocal Dirichlet problems and their well-posedness of the corresponding weak formulation. A nonlocal Dirichlet problem is part of the energy-based Local-to-Nonlocal(LtN) coupling that is outlined in Section
3.1
, which is followed by a small subsection on how to solve this LtN coupling with Schwarz methods. After that, we introduce an interface identification problem that is in our case constrained by the energy-based LtN coupling of Section
3.3
. Chapter
4
is then dedicated to shape optimization and contains basic notions of shape optimization as well as the development of the shape derivative for the reduced objective functional corresponding to the interface identification problem from Section
3.3
. Additionally, we describe a well-known shape optimization algorithm in Chapter
4.4
, which we apply in Section
5
to solve the interface identification problem constrained by an energy-based LtN coupling for two numerical examples.

2
Nonlocal Terminology and Framework

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an open, connected and bounded domain.
Moreover, we denote by
\gamma:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
a nonnegative
(interaction) kernel
and we let the
nonlocal boundary

\mathcal{I}
of
\Omega
be defined in the following way

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}:=\{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Omega:\int_{\Omega}\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})+\gamma(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})~d\mathbf{x}>0\}.

(1)

As in
[
16
,
40
]
, the kernel
\gamma
is supposed to fulfill the next two requirements.

(K1)

There exist
\delta\in(0,\infty)
and
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
with

\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\phi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\chi_{B_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})}(\mathbf{y}).

(K2)

There exist
\gamma_{0}\in(0,\infty)
and
\epsilon\in(0,\delta)
such that

\displaystyle\gamma_{0}\leq\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\text{ for }\mathbf{y}\in B_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{x})\text{ and }\mathbf{x}\in\Omega.

Then, we call an operator
\mathcal{L}
of the form

\displaystyle-\mathcal{L}u(\mathbf{x})=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(u(\mathbf{x})\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})-u(\mathbf{y})\gamma(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})\right)~d\mathbf{y}

(2)

a
nonlocal convection-diffusion operator
.
With an appropriate forcing term
f
and suitable boundary data
g
, which will both be specified later, we are now able to formulate a
nonhomogeneous steady-state Dirichlet problem with boundary constraints
as

\displaystyle\begin{split}-\mathcal{L}u&=f\text{ on }\Omega\\
u&=g\text{ on }\mathcal{I}.\end{split}

(3)

Similar to the case of partial differential equations, the theory of nonlocal problems focuses on finding a weak solution to (
3
). In order to derive such a weak formulation we multiply the first equation of (
3
) by a test function
v:\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with
v=0
on
\mathcal{I}
and then integrate over
\Omega
, which results in

\displaystyle\left(-\mathcal{L}u,v\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=\left(f,v\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\int_{\Omega}v(\mathbf{x})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(u(\mathbf{x})\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle-u(\mathbf{y})\gamma(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}))~d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{x}=\int_{\Omega}f(\mathbf{x})v(\mathbf{x})~d\mathbf{x}.

(4)

Making use of Fubini’s theorem and applying
v(\mathbf{y})=0
for
\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{I}
yields an equivalent representation for the integral on the left-hand side

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}v(\mathbf{x})\int_{\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}}(u(\mathbf{x})\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})-u(\mathbf{y})\gamma(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}))~d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\iint_{(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})^{2}}(v(\mathbf{x})-v(\mathbf{y}))(u(\mathbf{x})\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})-u(\mathbf{y})\gamma(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}))~d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{x}.

As a next step, we define the bilinear operator
A
and the linear functional
F
as follows

\displaystyle A(u,v)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\iint_{(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})^{2}}(v(\mathbf{x})-v(\mathbf{y}))(u(\mathbf{x})\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})-u(\mathbf{y})\gamma(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}))~d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{x}\text{ and}

\displaystyle F(v)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega}f(\mathbf{x})v(\mathbf{x})~d\mathbf{x}.

Then, we can introduce a (semi-)norm

\displaystyle\begin{split}|u|_{V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}&:=\sqrt{A(u,u)}\text{ and a norm }\\
||u||_{V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}&:=|u|_{V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}+||u||_{L^{2}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})},\end{split}

(5)

which we employ to define the following nonlocal energy spaces

\displaystyle V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})

\displaystyle:=\{u\in L^{2}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}):||u||_{V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}<\infty\}\text{ and}

\displaystyle V_{c}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})

\displaystyle:=\{u\in V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}):u=0\text{ on }\mathcal{I}\}.

With these definitions we can now formulate a
variational
or
weak
formulation of problem (
3
):

Definition 2.1
.

Given
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and
g\in V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})
, if
u\in V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})
solves

\displaystyle\begin{split}A(u,v)&=F(v)\text{ for all }v\in V_{c}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})\text{ and}\\
u&-g\in V_{c}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}),\end{split}

(6)

then
u
is called weak solution of (
3
).

Since
A(u,v)=A(u-g,v)+A(g,v)
and
u-g\in V_{c}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})
, we are able to rephrase problem
6
as a homogeneous Dirichlet problem

\displaystyle\begin{split}\textit{Given }f&\in L^{2}(\Omega)\textit{ and }g\in V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})\textit{, find }\tilde{u}\in V_{c}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})\textit{ s.t.}\\
A(\tilde{u},v)&=F(v)-A(g,v)\textit{ for all }v\in V_{c}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}),\\
\textit{then }u&:=\tilde{u}+g\in V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})\textit{ is a solution of \eqref{weak_formulation}.}\end{split}

(7)

Example 2.2
.

Two popular classes of symmetric kernels, where the well-posedness of (
6
) is shown in, e.g.
[
16
,
40
]
, are:

•

Integrable kernels:

There exist constants
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in(0,\infty)
with

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}\leq\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in\Omega}\int_{\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}}\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})~d\mathbf{y}\text{ and }\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\Omega}\int_{\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}}\left(\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\right)^{2}~d\mathbf{y}\leq\left(\gamma_{2}\right)^{2}.

Then the solution
u\in V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})
for (
6
) exists, is unique and in this case the spaces
\left(V_{c}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}),|\cdot|_{V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}\right)
and
\left(L_{c}^{2}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}),||\cdot||_{L^{2}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}\right)
as well as
\left(V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}),||\cdot||_{V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}\right)
and
\left(L^{2}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}),||\cdot||_{L^{2}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}\right)
are equivalent.

•

Singular symmetric kernels:

There exist constants
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in(0,\infty)
and
s\in(0,1)
with

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}<\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})||\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}||_{2}^{d+2s}<\gamma_{2}\text{ for all }\mathbf{y}\in B_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})\text{ and }\mathbf{x}\in\Omega.

Then the solution to (
6
) exists, is unique and the spaces
\left(V_{c}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}),|\cdot|_{V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}\right)
and

\left(H^{s}_{c}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}),|\cdot|_{H^{s}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}\right)
as well as
\left(V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}),||\cdot||_{V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}\right)
and
\left(H^{s}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}),||\cdot||_{H^{s}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}\right)
are equivalent.

In the coupling formulation, that is presented in the Chapter
3.1
, only integrable kernels with some additional assumptions, which will be specified later, are considered.

In the next Chapter, we present a Local-to-Nonlocal coupling, where we will observe a nonlocal Dirichlet problem on one subdomain. Additionally, we recapture under which assumptions this particular coupling is well-posed(see Lemma
3.5
and Corollary
3.6
).

3
Interface Identification constrained by an energy-based Local-to-Nonlocal Coupling

3.1
Energy-based Local-to-Nonlocal Coupling

In this section we introduce a specific coupling of the Laplacian operator on one domain and a nonlocal operator on the other domain, which was first formulated in
[
1
,
2
]
.

Here, for any two sets
\hat{\Omega}_{1},\hat{\Omega}_{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
we define the distance of those two domains as

\displaystyle\dist(\hat{\Omega}_{1},\hat{\Omega}_{2}):=\inf\limits_{\mathbf{x}\in\hat{\Omega}_{1},~\mathbf{y}\in\hat{\Omega}_{2}}||\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}||_{2}.

If
\hat{\Omega}_{1}
only consists of one element, i.e. there exists one
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\hat{\Omega}_{1}=\{\mathbf{x}\}
, we also use the short notation
\dist(\mathbf{x},\hat{\Omega}_{2}):=\dist(\{\mathbf{x}\},\hat{\Omega}_{2})
.

From now on, the set
\Omega
is assumed to be an open and bounded Lipschitz domain and can be described as
\Omega=\left(\overline{\Omega}_{l}\cup\overline{\Omega}_{nl}\right)^{\circ}
, where
\Omega_{l}
and
\Omega_{nl}
are open, bounded, connected, non-empty and disjoint sets. Moreover,
\Omega_{l}
is also supposed to be a Lipschitz domain and, since trivial couplings should be excluded,
\dist(\Omega_{l},\Omega_{nl})<\delta
is assumed to hold. An example configuration is shown in Picture
3.1
. From now on, we will refer to
\Omega_{l}
as the ’local’ and to
\Omega_{nl}
as the ’nonlocal’ domain.

Figure 3.1:
Here, the nonlocal domain
\Omega_{nl}
is depicted in the darker red and the corresponding nonlocal domain
\mathcal{I}_{nl}
of the nonlocal domain
\Omega_{nl}
is colored in the lighter red. Moreover, the ’local’ domain
\Omega_{l}
consists in this example of the nonlocal boundary
\mathcal{I}_{nl}
as well as the gray area.

In the remaining part of this work we denote by
\partial\Omega
,
\partial\Omega_{l}
and
\partial\Omega_{nl}
the
local boundary
of
\Omega
,
\Omega_{l}
and
\Omega_{nl}
, respectively. Moreover, the nonlocal boundary
\mathcal{I}_{nl}
regarding the nonlocal domain
\Omega_{nl}
is analogously defined as in (
1
), i.e.

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{nl}:=\{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Omega_{nl}:\int_{\Omega_{nl}}\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})+\gamma(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})~d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{x}>0\}.

Then, the coupling problem is formulated in the following way:

\displaystyle-\mathcal{L}_{l}(u)(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=f(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\text{for }\mathbf{x}\in\Omega_{l},

\displaystyle\partial_{n}u_{l}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on }\partial\Omega_{l}\cap\partial\Omega_{nl},

(8)

\displaystyle u_{l}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on }\partial\Omega_{l}\cap\partial\Omega,

\displaystyle-\mathcal{L}_{nl}(u)(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=f(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\text{for }\mathbf{x}\in\Omega_{nl},

\displaystyle u_{nl}

\displaystyle=u_{l}

\displaystyle\text{on }\mathcal{I}_{nl}\cap\Omega_{l},

(9)

\displaystyle u_{nl}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on }\mathcal{I}_{nl}\cap\mathcal{I},

where

\displaystyle-\mathcal{L}_{l}(u)(\mathbf{x}):=-\mathcal{L}_{l}(u_{l},u_{nl})(\mathbf{x}):=

\displaystyle-\Delta u_{l}(\mathbf{x})+\int_{\Omega_{nl}}\left(u_{l}(\mathbf{x})-u_{nl}(\mathbf{y})\right)\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})~d\mathbf{y}\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle-\mathcal{L}_{nl}(u)(\mathbf{x}):=-\mathcal{L}_{nl}(u_{l},u_{nl})(\mathbf{x}):=

\displaystyle 2\int\limits_{\Omega_{nl}}\left(u_{nl}(\mathbf{x})-u_{nl}(\mathbf{y})\right)\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})~d\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle+\int\limits_{\mathcal{I}_{nl}\cap\Omega_{l}}\left(u_{nl}(\mathbf{x})-u_{l}(\mathbf{y})\right)\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})~d\mathbf{y}+\int\limits_{\mathcal{I}_{nl}\cap\mathcal{I}}u_{nl}(\mathbf{x})\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})~d\mathbf{y}.

Basically, we solve a nonlocal Dirichlet problem on the nonlocal domain
\Omega_{nl}
, where the ’local’ solution
u_{l}
serves as boundary data on
\mathcal{I}_{nl}\cap\Omega_{l}
. For the ’local’ problem (
8
) the dependence on the nonlocal solution
u_{nl}
is more implicit.
Here, the operator
\mathcal{L}_{l}(u)(\mathbf{x})
is equivalent to the Laplacian
\Delta u_{l}(\mathbf{x})
, if
\dist(\mathbf{x},\Omega_{nl})\geq\delta
and otherwise, if
\dist(\mathbf{x},\Omega_{nl})<\delta
, some nonlocal flux is added to the Laplacian. Thus, in the second case the value of the operator
\mathcal{L}_{l}
is influenced by the ’nonlocal’ solution
u_{nl}
. Since
\gamma=0
almost everywhere in
\Omega_{nl}\times\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\left(\Omega_{nl}\cup\mathcal{I}_{nl}\right)\right)
we sometimes integrate over
\Omega_{l}
and
\mathcal{I}
instead of
\mathcal{I}_{nl}\cap\Omega_{l}
and
\mathcal{I}_{nl}\cap\mathcal{I}
in the remaining part of this work.

Remark 3.1
.

According to
[
2
]
the assumption that
\Omega_{nl}
needs to be connected can be relaxed to
\hat{\delta}
-connectedness for some
\hat{\delta}\in[0,\delta)
. A domain
\hat{\Omega}
is said to be
\hat{\delta}
-connected, if
\hat{\Omega}
cannot be written as a union of two disjoint open sets, whose distance is greater than
\hat{\delta}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\nexists\hat{\Omega}_{1},\hat{\Omega}_{2}\text{ open}:\ \hat{\Omega}_{1}\cap\hat{\Omega}_{2}=\emptyset,\ \hat{\Omega}_{1}\cup\hat{\Omega}_{2}=\hat{\Omega}\text{ and }\dist(\hat{\Omega}_{1},\hat{\Omega}_{2})>\hat{\delta}.

However, since
\hat{\delta}
-connectedness has to be checked after every deformation of
\Gamma
, we only consider the case, where
\Omega_{nl}
is (0-)connected. Thus, we do not apply the general concept of
\hat{\delta}
-connectedness.

In order to formulate a weak formulation of the LtN coupling we define the LtN bilinear operator
A^{LtN}
and the linear functional
F
as

\displaystyle F(v)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega_{l}}f(\mathbf{x})v_{l}(\mathbf{x})~d\mathbf{x}-\int_{\Omega_{nl}}f(\mathbf{x})v_{nl}(\mathbf{x})~d\mathbf{x}\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle A^{LtN}(u,v)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega_{l}}\nabla u_{l}(\mathbf{x})\nabla v_{l}(\mathbf{x})~d\mathbf{x}+\int_{\Omega_{nl}}\int_{\Omega_{nl}}\left(u_{nl}(\mathbf{x})-u_{nl}(\mathbf{y})\right)\left(v_{nl}(\mathbf{x})-v_{nl}(\mathbf{y})\right)\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})~d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega_{nl}}\int_{\Omega_{l}}(u_{nl}(\mathbf{x})-u_{l}(\mathbf{y}))(v_{nl}(\mathbf{x})-v_{l}(\mathbf{y}))\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})~d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega_{nl}}u_{nl}(\mathbf{x})v_{nl}(\mathbf{x})\int_{\mathcal{I}}\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})~d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{x}.

Then we can define an inner product
<\cdot,\cdot\cdot>_{H}
and the corresponding norm
||\cdot||_{H}
as

\displaystyle<u,v>_{H}:=A^{LtN}(u,v)\quad\text{and}\quad||u||_{H}:=\sqrt{A^{LtN}(u,u)}.

Definition 3.2
.

The energy space
(H,||\cdot||_{H})
is defined as

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle:=\{u=(u_{l},u_{nl}):u_{l}\in H_{\partial\Omega\cap\partial\Omega_{l}}^{1}(\Omega_{l}),\ u_{nl}\in L_{c}^{2}\left(\Omega_{nl}\cup\mathcal{I}_{nl}\right)\},

where the spaces
H_{\partial\Omega\cap\partial\Omega_{l}}^{1}(\Omega_{l})
and
L_{c}^{2}\left(\Omega_{nl}\cup\mathcal{I}_{nl}\right)
are set as follows

\displaystyle H_{\partial\Omega\cap\partial\Omega_{l}}^{1}(\Omega_{l})

\displaystyle:=\{v\in H^{1}(\Omega_{l}):v=0\text{ on }\partial\Omega\cap\partial\Omega_{l}\}\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle L_{c}^{2}\left(\Omega_{nl}\cup\mathcal{I}_{nl}\right)

\displaystyle:=\{v\in L^{2}(\Omega_{nl}\cup\mathcal{I}_{nl}):v=0\text{ on }\mathcal{I}_{nl}\}.

Definition 3.3
.

Given
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, we call
u=(u_{l},u_{nl})\in H
a weak solution to the coupling that consists of equations (
8
) and (
9
), if
u
fulfills

\displaystyle A^{LtN}(u,v)=F(v)\text{ for all }v=(v_{l},v_{nl})\in H.

(10)

Thus, equation (
10
) is the variational formulation of the combined problem.
Next, we would like to point out, why this coupling is called energy-based.
Therefore, we want to minimize the energy functional

\displaystyle\min_{u\in H}E(u)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}A^{LtN}(u,u)-F(v),

and derive the first order necessary condition

\displaystyle dE(u)(v)=A^{LtN}(u,v)-F(v)=0\text{ for all }v=(v_{l},v_{nl})\in H.

which is equivalent to the variational formulation (
10
).
Now, if we set the local component of the test vector
v_{l}=0
, we get the variational formulation regarding the nonlocal Dirichlet problem (
9
) of the LtN coupling

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{nl}}\int_{\Omega_{nl}}\left(v_{nl}(\mathbf{x})-v_{nl}(\mathbf{y})\right)\left(u_{nl}(\mathbf{x})-u_{nl}(\mathbf{y})\right)\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})~d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega_{nl}}v_{nl}(\mathbf{x})\int_{\Omega_{l}}\left(u_{nl}(\mathbf{x})-u_{l}(\mathbf{y})\right)\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})~d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{x}+\int_{\Omega_{nl}}v_{nl}(\mathbf{x})u_{nl}(\mathbf{x})\int_{\mathcal{I}}\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})~d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{nl}}f(\mathbf{x})v_{nl}(\mathbf{x})~d\mathbf{x}\text{ for all }v_{nl}\in V_{c}(\Omega_{nl}\cup\mathcal{I}_{nl})=L^{2}_{c}(\Omega_{nl}\cup\mathcal{I}_{nl}).

Analogously, by setting the nonlocal component of the test vector
v_{nl}=0
, we derive the weak formulation for the local subproblem (
8
) of the LtN coupling

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{l}}\nabla u_{l}(\mathbf{x})\nabla v_{l}(\mathbf{x})~d\mathbf{x}+\int_{\Omega_{l}}v_{l}(\mathbf{x})\int_{\Omega_{nl}}\left(u_{l}(\mathbf{x})-u_{nl}(\mathbf{y})\right)\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})~d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{l}}f(\mathbf{x})v_{l}(\mathbf{x})~d\mathbf{x}\text{ for all }v_{l}\in H_{\partial\Omega_{l}\cap\partial\Omega}^{1}(\Omega_{l}).

All in all, the coupling is called energy-based, because the weak formulations for (
8
), (
9
) and the combined problem can be derived by investigating first order necessary conditions for the minimization of a specific energy functional, where we only consider certain test functions.

For the purpose of having a well-posed LtN coupling, we need further restrictions. In addition to the assumptions (K1) and (K2) from Chapter
2
, the kernel
\gamma
is supposed to be

(K3)

bounded and

(K4)

translation invariant, i.e. there exists a function
J:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and

\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=J(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}).

Remark 3.4
.

In contrast to this work, in
[
2
]
the kernel
\gamma
is not assumed to be truncated. Instead they assume additionally the following conditions:

•

The kernel
\gamma
is integrable.

•

(Compactness): The operator
C:L^{2}(\Omega_{nl})\rightarrow L^{2}(\Omega_{nl})
with

C(u)(\mathbf{x})=\int_{\Omega_{nl}}J(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})u(\mathbf{y})~d\mathbf{y}

is compact.

Then, in
[
2

it is shown that the space
(H,<\cdot,\cdot\cdot>_{H})
is actually a Hilbert space.

In our case the truncatedness and boundedness of
\gamma
imply
\gamma\in L^{1}((\Omega_{nl}\cup\mathcal{I}_{nl})\times(\Omega_{nl}\cup\mathcal{I}_{nl}))
and
\gamma\in L^{2}((\Omega_{nl}\cup\mathcal{I}_{nl})\times(\Omega_{nl}\cup\mathcal{I}_{nl}))
since the domain
\Omega_{nl}
and therefore
\Omega_{nl}\cup\mathcal{I}_{nl}
are also bounded. As a result, the compactness condition holds, because the operator
C
is in this case a Hilbert-Schmidt operator and therefore compact(see
[
9
, Theorem 6.12]
).

Consequently, we derive the next Lemma.

Follows directly from

3.2
Schwarz Methods

In Chapter
5
, we solve the energy-based LtN coupling by applying the multiplicative Schwarz method as described in
[
2
]
. Schwarz methods were developed by H.A. Schwarz around 1870 in order to solve a PDE on an ’irregular’ domain
[
34
]
. In the following, we present the Schwarz method in the context of the LtN coupling problem.

Multiplicative Schwarz Method for LtN coupling

Given the solution

3.3
Problem Formulation

Interface identification is a popular problem in PDE-constrained shape optimization, see, e.g.
[
41
,
38
,
14
]
. Here, as mentioned earlier, we consider an open, bounded and connected domain
\Omega
with Lipschitz domain as it is depicted in Picture
3.1
. We then call
\Gamma:=\partial\Omega_{nl}\cap\partial\Omega_{l}

interface
and assume that
\Gamma
is a closed curve that decomposes
\Omega
into the Lipschitz domains
\Omega_{nl}
and
\Omega_{l}
, i.e.
\Omega=\Omega_{nl}\dot{\cup}\Gamma\dot{\cup}\Omega_{l}
. Therefore, we denote by
\Omega(\Gamma)
that
\Omega
is decomposed by
\Gamma
as described above. Moreover, the space
H
and the norm
||\cdot||_{H}
are also dependent on
\Gamma
, which we indicate by
||\cdot||_{H(\Gamma)}
and
H(\Gamma)
, respectively.

From now on, let
f_{\Gamma}:=f_{l}\chi_{\Omega_{l}}+f_{nl}\chi_{\Omega_{nl}}
with
f_{l},f_{nl}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
. Given some data
\bar{u}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
, that we would like to approximate by a weak solution
u
of the LtN coupling (
10
), we then call

\displaystyle\begin{split}\min_{u,\Gamma}J(u,\Gamma)&:=j(u,\Gamma)+j_{per}(\Gamma)=\int_{\Omega(\Gamma)}\left(u(\mathbf{x})-\bar{u}(\mathbf{x})\right)^{2}~d\mathbf{x}+\nu\int_{\Gamma}1~ds\\
\text{s.t.}\quad&A^{LtN}_{\Gamma}(u,v)=F_{\Gamma}(v)\text{ for all }v\in H(\Gamma),\end{split}

(11)

an
interface identification problem
, which is in our case constrained by a Local-to-Nonlocal coupling. Here, the subscripts of
F_{\Gamma}
and
A^{LtN}_{\Gamma}
indicate the dependence of these functions on
\Gamma
.
Since every admissible choice of
\Gamma\subset\Omega
yields a unique solution, this problem can be seen as only dependent on the choice of the interface
\Gamma
, i.e. we would like to find a shape
\Gamma
, such that the corresponding solution approximates our data
\bar{u}
as good as possible. The second term in the objective functional
j_{per}
is called perimeter regularization with parameter
\nu>0
and is often applied in optimization to avoid an ill-posedness of the problem
[
6
]
.

4.1
Basics of Shape Optimization

In order to introduce the definition of a shape derivative we need a family of mappings
\{\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{t}}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
with
\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{t}}:\overline{\Omega}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathbf{F}_{0}={\bf Id}
for a given sufficiently small constant
T\in(0,\infty)
. We can then deform a domain
\Gamma\subset\Omega
according to
\{\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{t}}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
and get a family of perturbed shapes
\{\Gamma^{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
, where
\Gamma^{t}:=\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{t}}(\Gamma)
.
In this work, as a family of mappings we will only use the so-called
perturbation of identity
.

Definition 4.1

(Perturbation of Identity)
.

For a vector field
\mathbf{V}\in C_{0}^{k}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
we define the
perturbation of identity
as

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{t}}:\overline{\Omega}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\quad\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}+t\mathbf{V}(\mathbf{x})=\left({\bf Id}+t\mathbf{V}\right)(\mathbf{x}),

(12)

where
{\bf Id}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the identity function.

In Figure
4.1
we present an example how the perturbation of identity maps
\Gamma
to a new interface
\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{t}}(\Gamma)
.

Figure 4.1:
The perturbation of identity moves a point
\mathbf{x}
in the direction of a vector field
\mathbf{V}
to the point
\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{x})
. Applied on
\Gamma
, this interface is shifted and deformed to a new interface
\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{t}}(\Gamma)
.

If
t
is sufficiently small, such that
||t\mathbf{V}||_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})}<1
, then the perturbation of identity
\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{t}}:\overline{\Omega}\rightarrow\overline{\Omega}
is Lipschitz, invertible and the inverse is also Lipschitz
[
22
]
. As a result, if
\Gamma
is a Lipschitz domain, the perturbed domain
\Gamma^{t}
will also have a Lipschitz boundary. We are now able to present the definition of a shape derivative.

Definition 4.2

(Shape Derivative)
.

Given a shape functional
J:\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, where
\mathcal{A}
is an appropriate shape space, the
Eulerian
or
directional derivative
is defined as

\displaystyle D_{\Gamma}J(\Gamma)[\mathbf{V}]=\lim_{t\searrow 0}\frac{J(\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{t}}(\Gamma))-J(\Gamma)}{t}.

(13)

If for every
\mathbf{V}\in C_{0}^{k}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})
the derivative
D_{\Gamma}J(\Gamma)[\mathbf{V}]
exists and the function
\mathbf{V}\mapsto D_{\Gamma}J(\Gamma)[\mathbf{V}]
is linear and continuous, the derivative
D_{\Gamma}J(\Gamma)[\mathbf{V}]
is called
shape derivative
at
\Gamma
in the direction
\mathbf{V}
.

In this work, we use the same framework as in
[
30
]
and, starting from an initial shape
\Gamma^{0}
, we define the corresponding shape space
\mathcal{A}(\Gamma^{0})
as follows:

Definition 4.3

(Shape Space)
.

Given a domain
\Gamma^{0}\subset\Omega
we set the corresponding
shape space

\mathcal{A}(\Gamma^{0})
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\Gamma^{0}):=\{\mathbf{T}(\Gamma^{0})|~\mathbf{T}:\overline{\Omega}\rightarrow\overline{\Omega}\text{ invertible}\}.

Since the composition
\mathbf{T}^{n}\circ\mathbf{T}^{n-1}\circ...\circ\mathbf{T}^{1}
of invertible functions
\mathbf{T}^{k}:\overline{\Omega}\rightarrow\overline{\Omega}
for
k=1,...,n
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
is again an invertible function, the shape space
\mathcal{A}(\Gamma^{0})
contains only domains that can be obtained by a finite number of suitable deformations of
\Gamma^{0}
. Because we only employ the perturbation of identity, the function
\mathbf{T}^{k}={\bf Id}+t\mathbf{V}
is invertible, if
||t\mathbf{V}||_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})}<1
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
as mentioned above.

Remark 4.4
.

Since it could be the case that parts of the nonlocal boundary
\mathcal{I}_{nl}
are outside of
\Omega
, i.e.
\mathcal{I}\cap\mathcal{I}_{nl}\neq\emptyset
, we extend every
\mathbf{V}\in C_{0}^{k}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})
to
\overline{\Omega}\cup\mathcal{I}
by zero. So, the boundary
\mathcal{I}
stays unchanged and only points inside
\Omega
can be moved according to

\mathbf{V}\in C_{0}^{k}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I},\mathbb{R}^{d}):=\{\mathbf{V}:\overline{\Omega}\cup\mathcal{I}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}|~\mathbf{V}=0\text{ on }\mathcal{I}\text{ and }\left.\mathbf{V}\right|_{\overline{\Omega}}\in C_{0}^{k}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})\}.

In the following, we only need vector fields that are one times continuously differentiable, i.e.
\mathbf{V}\in C_{0}^{1}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I},\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

4.2
Shape Derivative via the Averaged Adjoint Method

As mentioned in Section
3.3
, we assume the existence of a unique solution
u(\Gamma)
for every admissible shape
\Gamma
, i.e.
u(\Gamma)
satisfies
A^{LtN}_{\Gamma}(u(\Gamma),v)=F_{\Gamma}(v)
for all
v\in H(\Gamma)
. Therefore, we investigate the so-called
reduced problem

\displaystyle\min\limits_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{A}(\Gamma^{0})}\;

\displaystyle J^{red}(\Gamma):=J(u(\Gamma),\Gamma).

(14)

Since we would like to apply derivative based minimization algorithms, we need to compute the shape derivative of the reduced objective functional
J^{red}
. There are several ways how the shape derivative of the objective functional can be computed, which are discussed in
[
38
]
.
We choose the
averaged adjoint method (AAM)
of
[
37
,
25
,
38
]
, which is a material derivative-free approach to rigorously access the shape derivative of (
14
).
Therefore, we need the so-called
Lagrangian functional

\displaystyle L(u,\Gamma,v):=J(u,\Gamma)+A^{LtN}_{\Gamma}(u,v)-F_{\Gamma}(v).

With the Lagrangian we can express the reduced functional as

\displaystyle J^{red}(\Gamma)=L(u(\Gamma),\Gamma,v),\quad\text{for an arbitrary }v\in H(\Gamma),

where we apply that
u(\Gamma)
is a weak solution of the LtN coupling (
10
) regarding the interface
\Gamma
. We now fix
\Gamma
and denote by
\Gamma^{t}:=\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{t}}(\Gamma)
,
\Omega_{l}^{t}:=\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{t}}(\Omega_{l})
and
\Omega_{nl}^{t}:=\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{t}}(\Omega_{nl})
the deformed interior boundary and the deformed domains, respectively. Furthermore we indicate by writing
\Omega(\Gamma^{t})
that we use the decomposition
\Omega(\Gamma^{t})=\Omega_{1}^{t}\dot{\cup}\Gamma^{t}\dot{\cup}\Omega_{2}^{t}\left(=\Omega\right)
. As a consequence, the norm
||\cdot||_{H(\Gamma^{t})}
and therefore the space
H(\Gamma^{t})
differ from
||\cdot||_{H(\Gamma)}
and
H(\Gamma)
, since
\Gamma^{t}(=\partial\Omega_{l}^{t}\cap\partial\Omega_{nl}^{t})
indicates how
\Omega
is decomposed into
\Omega_{l}^{t}
and
\Omega_{nl}^{t}
, which determines the integration domains that are employed to describe the operator
A^{LtN}_{\Gamma}
. Then, the reduced objective functional regarding
\Gamma^{t}
can be written as

\displaystyle J^{red}(\Gamma^{t})=L(u(\Gamma^{t}),\Gamma^{t},v),\quad\text{for any }v\in H(\Gamma^{t}),

(15)

where
u(\Gamma^{t})\in H(\Gamma^{t})
.
In order to differentiate
L
with respect to
t
to compute the shape derivative, we would have to access the derivative for
u(\Gamma^{t})\circ\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{t}}
and
v\circ\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{t}}
, where
u(\Gamma^{t}),v\in H(\Gamma^{t})
may not be differentiable. Moreover, the norm
||\cdot||_{H(\Gamma^{t})}
and consequently the space
H(\Gamma^{t})
are also dependent on
t
. Instead, we can circumvent differentiating
u(\Gamma^{t})\circ\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{t}}
and additionally stay in
\left(H(\Gamma),||\cdot||_{H(\Gamma)}\right)
by using
\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{t}}
, which is a homeomorphism, as a so-called
pull-back function
. Then, for
u,v\in H(\Gamma^{t})
there exist functions
\tilde{u},\tilde{v}\in H(\Gamma)
, such that

\displaystyle u=\tilde{u}\circ\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{t}}^{-1}~\text{and}~v=\tilde{v}\circ\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{t}}^{-1}.

Moreover, for a sufficiently small
T\in(0,\infty)
, we can define

\displaystyle J:[0,T]\times

\displaystyle H(\Gamma)\rightarrow\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle J(t,u):=J(u\circ\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{t}}^{-1},\Gamma^{t}),

\displaystyle A^{LtN}:[0,T]\times

\displaystyle H(\Gamma)\times H(\Gamma)\rightarrow\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle A^{LtN}(t,u,v):=A^{LtN}_{\Gamma^{t}}(u\circ\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{t}}^{-1},v\circ\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{t}}^{-1}),

\displaystyle F:[0,T]\times

\displaystyle H(\Gamma)\rightarrow\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle F(t,v):=F_{\Gamma^{t}}(v\circ\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{t}}^{-1}),

\displaystyle\begin{split}G:[0,T]\times&H(\Gamma)\times H(\Gamma)\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\\
&G(t,u,v):=L(u\circ\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{t}}^{-1},\Gamma^{t},v\circ\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{t}}^{-1})=J(t,u)+A^{LtN}(t,u,v)-F(t,v).\end{split}

(16)

Now, let us briefly mention that the Local-to-Nonlocal bilinear form regarding
\Gamma^{t}
can be written as

\displaystyle\begin{split}&A^{LtN}(t,u^{0},v^{0})=A^{LtN}_{\Gamma^{t}}(u^{0}\circ\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{t}}^{-1},v^{0}\circ\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{t}}^{-1})=\int\limits_{\Omega_{l}}\left(\mathcal{B}(t)(\mathbf{x})\nabla u^{0}(\mathbf{x}),\nabla v^{0}(\mathbf{x})\right)~d\mathbf{x}\\
&+\int\limits_{\Omega_{nl}}\int\limits_{\Omega_{nl}\cup\mathcal{I}_{nl}}\left(v^{0}(\mathbf{x})-v^{0}(\mathbf{y})\right)\left(u^{0}(\mathbf{x})-u^{0}(\mathbf{y})\right)\gamma^{t}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\xi^{t}(\mathbf{x})\xi^{t}(\mathbf{y})~d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{x},\end{split}

(17)

where

\displaystyle\xi^{t}

\displaystyle:=\det D\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{t}},\ \gamma^{t}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}):=\gamma(\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{y}))\text{ and }

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(t)(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle:=\xi^{t}(\mathbf{x})D\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{t}}^{-1}(\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{x}))D\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{t}}^{-\top}(\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{x}))=\xi^{t}(\mathbf{x})\left(D\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{x})\right)^{-1}\left(D\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{x})\right)^{-\top}.

Then, (
15
) can be expressed as

\displaystyle J^{red}(\Gamma^{t})=G(t,u^{t},v),\quad\text{for any }v\in H(\Gamma),

where
u^{t}\in H(\Gamma)
is defined as the unique solution of the LtN coupling (
10
) for
\Gamma^{t}
, i.e.
u^{t}
solves

\displaystyle A^{LtN}(t,u^{t},v)-F(t,v)=0,\quad\forall v\in H(\Gamma).

(18)

Obviously,
A^{LtN}(t,u,v)-F(t,v)
is linear in
v
for all
(t,u)\in[0,T]\times H(\Gamma)
, which is one necessary condition of AAM.
Additionally, the following prerequisites have to hold in order to apply AAM.

•

Assumption (H0)
:
For every
(t,v)\in[0,T]\times H(\Gamma)

1.

[0,1]\ni s\mapsto G(t,su^{t}+(1-s)u^{0},v)
is absolutely continuous and

2.

[0,1]\ni s\mapsto d_{u}G(t,su^{t}+(1-s)u^{0},v)[\tilde{u}]\in L^{1}((0,1))
for all
\tilde{u}\in H(\Gamma)
.

•

For every
t\in[0,T]
there exists a unique solution
v^{t}\in H(\Gamma)
for the averaged adjoint equation

\int_{0}^{1}d_{u}G(t,su^{t}+(1-s)u^{0},v^{t})[\tilde{u}]ds=0\quad\text{for all }\tilde{u}\in H(\Gamma).

(19)

•

Assumption (H1)
:

The following equation holds

\lim_{t\searrow 0}\frac{G(t,u^{0},v^{t})-G(0,u^{0},v^{t})}{t}=\partial_{t}G(0,u^{0},v^{0}).

Since
A^{LtN}(t,\tilde{u},v^{t})
is linear in the second argument, the left-hand side of the averaged adjoint equation (

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}d_{u}G(t,su^{t}+(1-s)u^{0},v^{t})[\tilde{u}]~ds=A^{LtN}(t,\tilde{u},v^{t})+\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{1}{2}(u^{t}+u^{0})-\bar{u}^{t}\right)\tilde{u}\xi^{t}~d\mathbf{x},

where
\bar{u}^{t}(\mathbf{x}):=\bar{u}(\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{x}))
. Therefore, (

\displaystyle A^{LtN}(t,\tilde{u},v^{t})=-\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(u^{t}+u^{0}\right)-\bar{u}^{t}\right)\tilde{u}\xi^{t}~d\mathbf{x}\quad\forall\tilde{u}\in H(\Gamma).

(20)

We will make use of (

Moreover, for
t=0
we call the averaged adjoint equation

\displaystyle A^{LtN}(0,\tilde{u},v^{0})

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}(u^{0}-\bar{u})\tilde{u}~d\mathbf{x}\quad\forall\tilde{u}\in H(\Gamma)

(21)

adjoint equation and the function
v^{0}
, which solves (
21

Moreover, the LtN problem (

4.3
Deriving the Shape Derivative of the Reduced Functional

In order to rigorously compute the shape derivative we make use of the following Lemma.

Lemma 4.6

(After
[
36
, Proposition 2.32]
)
.

Let
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded domain with nonzero measure,
f\in W^{1,1}(D,\mathbb{R})
and
\mathbf{V}\in C_{0}^{1}(D,\mathbb{R}^{d})
, where
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Moreover, assume that
T>0
is sufficiently small, such that
\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{t}}:\overline{D}\rightarrow\overline{D}
is bijective for all
t\in[0,T]
. Then,
t\mapsto f\circ\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{t}}
is differentiable in
L^{1}(D)
with

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0}\left(f\circ\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{t}}\right)=\nabla f^{\top}\mathbf{V}.

Proof.

Since
C^{\infty}(D)
is dense in
W^{1,1}(D,\mathbb{R})
[
3
, Theorem 3.17]
, we only need to show the result for
f\in C^{\infty}(D)
.
Now, given
f\in C^{\infty}(D)
, applying the mean value theorem yields

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{x}))-f(\mathbf{x})=\int_{0}^{1}t\nabla f(\mathbf{x}+st\mathbf{V}(\mathbf{x}))^{\top}\mathbf{V}(\mathbf{x})~ds

for
\mathbf{x}\in D
. Consequently, we derive

\displaystyle\int_{D}\left|\frac{1}{t}\left(f(\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{x}))-f(\mathbf{x})\right)-\nabla f(\mathbf{x})^{\top}\mathbf{V}(\mathbf{x})\right|~d\mathbf{x}\leq\int_{D}\int_{0}^{1}\left|\left(\nabla f(\mathbf{x}+st\mathbf{V}(\mathbf{x}))-\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\right)^{\top}\mathbf{V}(\mathbf{x})\right|~ds~d\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\int_{D}\left|\left(\nabla f(\mathbf{x}+st\mathbf{V}(\mathbf{x}))-\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\right)^{\top}\mathbf{V}(\mathbf{x})\right|~d\mathbf{x}~ds,

(23)

where we changed the order of integration in the last step. Now, we just have to show, that the double integral (
23
) vanishes, when
t\rightarrow 0
.
For that reason we would like to show, that the inner integral converges to zero.
Since
\mathbf{V}\in C_{0}^{1}(D,\mathbb{R}^{d})
is continuous and thus bounded on
\overline{D}
and
\nabla f(\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{x}))
converges in
L^{2}(D,\mathbb{R}^{d})
and therefore also in
L^{1}(D,\mathbb{R}^{d})
to
\nabla f
due to Lemma
A.2
, we conclude by employing the dominated convergence theorem that

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}\int_{D}\left|\left(\nabla f(\mathbf{x}+st\mathbf{V}(\mathbf{x}))-\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\right)^{\top}\mathbf{V}(\mathbf{x})\right|~d\mathbf{x}=0\quad\text{for all }s\in[0,1].

Again, applying the dominated convergence theorem yields

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{D}\left|\left(\nabla f(\mathbf{x}+st\mathbf{V}(\mathbf{x}))-\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\right)^{\top}\mathbf{V}(\mathbf{x})\right|~d\mathbf{x}~ds=0.

∎

Corollary 4.7
.

Let
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an open and bounded domain with nonzero measure. If
\gamma\in W^{1,1}(D,\mathbb{R})
and
\tilde{\mathbf{V}}\in C_{0}^{1}(D,\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
, then
t\mapsto\gamma\circ\widetilde{\mathbf{F}}_{t}
, where
\widetilde{\mathbf{F}}_{t}:=(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})+t\tilde{\mathbf{V}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
, is Fréchet differentiable in
L^{1}(D,\mathbb{R})
at
t=0
and its derivative is given by

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0}\gamma\circ\widetilde{\mathbf{F}}_{t}=\nabla\gamma^{T}\tilde{\mathbf{V}}.

Remark 4.8
.

The statement of Lemma
4.6
and Corollary
4.7
is still valid, if
\mathbf{V}\in C_{0}^{1}(\hat{D},\mathbb{R}^{d})
, where the superset
\hat{D}\supset D
is a bounded and open domain.

In our case, we set
\tilde{\mathbf{V}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}):=\left(\mathbf{V}(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{V}(\mathbf{y})\right)
in order to use Corollary
4.7

Optimization Algorithm

In this section we outline the optimization procedure to solve the interface identification problem constrained by Local-to-Nonlocal coupling as described in Section
3.3
.
As shown in
[
30
]
, shape optimization techniques can be identified with corresponding methods in the Hilbert space
\left(\mathcal{H},g\right)
, where
\mathcal{H}\subset\{T|T:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
and
g
is an inner product on
\mathcal{H}

\left(\mathcal{H},g\right)
, see
[
30

.

In the optimization algorithm, that we will deploy, we would like to derive a Riesz-representation of the shape gradient
\nabla J^{red}
for a scalar product
b_{\Gamma}
that can depend on the current shape
\Gamma
. Then, given the state
u^{0}
and adjoint solution
v^{0}
, we compute
\nabla J^{red}
by solving

\displaystyle b_{\Gamma}(\nabla J^{red},\mathbf{V})=D_{\Gamma}J^{red}(\Gamma)[\mathbf{V}]\quad\text{for all }\mathbf{V}\in C_{0}^{1}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I},\mathbb{R}^{d}).

(25)

In our case, we choose the linear elasticity operator as an inner product, i.e.

\displaystyle b_{\Gamma}:C_{0}^{1}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I},\mathbb{R}^{d})\times C_{0}^{1}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I},\mathbb{R}^{d})\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\quad g(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V}):=\int_{\Omega}\sigma(\mathbf{U}):\epsilon(\mathbf{V})~d\mathbf{x},\text{ where}

\displaystyle\sigma(\mathbf{U}):=\lambda tr(\epsilon(\mathbf{U})){\bf Id}+2\mu\epsilon(\mathbf{U})\text{ and }\epsilon(\mathbf{U}):=\frac{1}{2}\left(\nabla\mathbf{U}+\nabla\mathbf{U}^{\top}\right).

Here,
\sigma(\mathbf{U})
is called the strain and
\epsilon(\mathbf{U})
stress tensor of
\mathbf{U}
with Lame parameters
\lambda
and
\mu
. According to
[
31
]
a locally varying choice for
\mu
can have a stabilizing effect on the mesh. So, we follow
[
31
]
and set
\lambda=0
and
\mu(\Gamma):=\mu
as the solution to

\displaystyle\Delta\mu

\displaystyle=0\quad

\displaystyle\text{on }\Omega,

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=\mu_{min}\quad

\displaystyle\text{on }\partial\Omega,

(26)

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=\mu_{max}\quad

As a consequence

\Gamma
and therefore the inner product

\Gamma

Further, we apply the finite element method to solve the described interface identification problem. Accordingly, the domain
\Omega
is represented by a mesh and the boundary
\Gamma
is characterized by edges. The triangle vertices and, as a consequence, also the edges are then deformed by a vector

is a linear combination of piecewise linear and continuous basis functions, i.e. we apply continuous Galerkin. On the other hand, the state and adjoint solution are chosen to be constructed by a linear combination of piecewise linear and continuous basis functions on

and on
\Omega_{l}

\Gamma

Furthermore, for vector fields that do not deform the interface
\Gamma

Remark 4.14
.

Since we use an iterative solver to compute solutions to the state and adjoint equation, the application of (multi-step) one-shot methods as in
[
7

and

after a few iterations is rather small. Consequently, if we need more outer iteration steps, (multi-step) one-shot methods do not yield a faster way to solve the interface identification problem constrained by a LtN coupling. On the other hand, we can only reduce the time by a few seconds, if we slightly increase the tolerance for solving the state and adjoint equation such that we still need the same number of outer iterations. Therefore, we keep the standard approach introduced in this Section and do not use one-shot methods.

6
Conclusion

As we have seen in this work, shape optimization techniques can be applied on interface identification problems that are constrained by an energy-based Local-to-Nonlocal coupling in order to find out where the nonlocal domain of this coupling should be located. Here, the shape derivative of the reduced functional is derived by the averaged adjoint method. In the future it could be interesting to enlarge this framework to applications in the field of Peridynamics, where most of these LtN combinations are currently employed.
