Acela: Predictable Datacenter-level Maintenance Job Scheduling
Yi Ding, Aijia Gao, Thibaud Ryden, Kaushik Mitra, Sukumar Kalmanje, Yanai Golany, Michael Carbin, Henry Hoffmann

Abstract
Datacenter operators ensure fair and regular server maintenance by using automated processes to schedule maintenance jobs to complete within a strict time budget. Automating this scheduling problem is challenging because maintenance job duration varies based on both job type and hardware. While it is tempting to use prior machine learning techniques for predicting job duration, we find that the structure of the maintenance job scheduling problem creates a unique challenge. In particular, we show that prior machine learning methods that produce the lowest error predictions do not produce the best scheduling outcomes due to asymmetric costs. Specifically, underpredicting maintenance job duration has results in more servers being taken offline and longer server downtime than overpredicting maintenance job duration. The system cost of underprediction is much larger than that of overprediction. We present Acela, a machine learning system for predicting maintenance job duration, which uses quantile regression to bias duration predictions toward overprediction. We integrate Acela into a maintenance job scheduler and evaluate it on datasets from large-scale, production datacenters. Compared to machine learning based predictors from prior work, Acela reduces the number of servers that are taken offline by 1.87–4.28 × \times , and reduces the server offline time by 1.40–2.80 × \times .

Introduction

Modern internet services are deployed on hyperscale datacenters that
include millions of servers
[
1
]
. At
this scale, poor system infrastructure can cause tremendous capacity
losses and impact billions of users. To reduce unplanned downtime and
improve system reliability, physical servers must be regularly
maintained
[

]
. A
maintenance job
is
the operation of fixing or upgrading physical servers to reduce the
likelihood of future faults. Typical maintenance jobs include
firmware and software
upgrades
[

,

,

.

Because each datacenter contains tens of thousands of servers, maintenance
job scheduling must be automated and planned in advance. In
particular, maintenance operations are conducted sequentially at the

maintenance unit
(i.e., a group of servers such as a
rack
[
6
]
) level. This process repeats on regular

maintenance cycles
, consistently revisiting every maintenance
unit in the datacenter. Figure

To ensure predictable, regular maintenance for each server, all jobs
scheduled for a maintenance unit in one maintenance cycle must
complete within a strict time budget. Importantly, if a scheduler
does not schedule a job in one cycle, it can raise the job’s priority
so that it will be scheduled in a subsequent cycle. However, if a
server cannot complete all its assigned jobs within the time budget
for a cycle, then it is taken offline. Thus, over-subscribing a
server in a maintenance cycle actually reduces datacenter capacity.
Therefore, the
maintenance job scheduling
problem is to
determine the maximum number of maintenance jobs that can fully
complete within a maintenance cycle’s time budget.

Maintenance job scheduling has an important distinction from
traditional job scheduling. Traditional job scheduling assigns jobs
to servers to minimize job duration. In maintenance job scheduling,
the server to job assignments are fixed. For example, one server
needs upgrades to its BIOS, NIC and FLASH firmware, while another
might just require a BIOS upgrade. Because the upgrades are fixed to
servers, the scheduling problem is to find the largest set of jobs
that run without exceeding the time budget and taking servers offline.
However, job duration is a complicated function of both the job type
and server, so the true job duration is unknown and can vary
tremendously. As we show in
section

2
the same firmware
upgrade can take very different amounts of time on different hardware.
Thus, it is essential to have an accurate method for predicting
maintenance job duration.

1.1
Challenges for Maintenance Job Scheduling

Traditional job scheduling (i.e., for user-level jobs) is well studied
in prior work

,

.
Several schedulers incorporate machine learning to predict job

.
Unfortunately, the unique nature of maintenance job
scheduling—specifically, the fact that servers with pending
maintenance jobs when the time budget expires are taken
offline—creates a unique challenge. Specifically,
while much prior work on predicting job durations has focused on
maximizing prediction accuracy (by minimizing mean squared error), we
find that strictly optimizing prediction accuracy using standard
machine learning methods does not provide the best
maintenance
outcome
(i.e., the number of maintenance jobs that can be
fully completed on a server that is not taken offline within the time
budget).

Example.

Table
1
contains an example that
demonstrates the gap between prediction accuracy and the maintenance
job scheduling objective. Consider a system consisting of a
maintenance unit with a single server with three maintenance jobs: A,
B, and C with corresponding true durations of 10s, 20s, and 21s. The
total time budget for a cycle is 50s. If we knew their true
durations, we could simply schedule any two jobs in one maintenance
cycle and the remaining one in the next cycle. In this way, the server
always completes all the maintenance jobs and returns to production in
a timely manner. We now examine two types of prediction inaccuracies:
underprediction (underestimating job duration) and
overprediction (overestimating job duration).

If a machine learning model underpredicts all three maintenance
jobs—as 9s, 19s, and 20s respectively—the total prediction error
is 3s (6%). With these predicted durations, all three
are scheduled for one maintenance cycle, but one will not complete in
the time budget. In this case, the server has not completed its
assigned maintenance within the budget so it is taken offline,
reducing the datacenter capacity.

If a machine learning model overpredicts all three maintenance
jobs—as 11s, 21s, and 22s respectively—the total prediction error
is still 3s (6%), same with underprediction. However, the scheduler will
select two maintenance jobs, both of which complete within the time
budget. In this case, the server stays online and the remaining
maintenance job will be scheduled in the next cycle.

Asymmetric costs.

The example above presents the key
concept of
asymmetric costs
; i.e., the system costs of
different types of mispredictions are not equivalent, even when they
are equivalent in error magnitude. For maintenance job scheduling,
asymmetric costs exist between under- and overprediction:

•

Underprediction means that job durations are predicted to be
shorter than they are. In this case, the scheduler may assign more
jobs than can finish in the time budget. Because a server with
pending jobs will be taken offline, underprediction can reduce
datacenter capacity.

•

Overprediction means that job durations are predicted to be
longer than they are. In this case, the scheduler may schedule fewer
jobs than what could be completed within the time budget. Doing so
means that the server may require more maintenance cycles to finish
all required maintenance, so we do not want to severely overpredict.
However, small overpredictions greatly reduce the chance that a
server is taken offline.

In summary, the asymmetry of maintenance outcomes leads to a much
larger cost for underprediction than overprediction. Since it is
impossible to have a perfect predictor with zero error for new jobs continously, we want to
bias towards overprediction in exchange for lower number of
underpredicted maintenance jobs. Because we also want to maximize the
number of maintenance jobs that actually complete within the time
budget, the predictor should be accurate but not overpredict too much.
In the example in Table
1
,
if the predictor predicts 40s for all three maintenance jobs, then
only one is scheduled. This is suboptimal as it will require three
maintenance cycles to complete all maintenance jobs.

1.2
Acela

To address the challenges of maintenance job scheduling, we introduce
Acela, a datacenter-level machine learning prediction system that
collects job features, trains machine learning models, and predicts
job durations. Specifically, Acela creates a function
f

that takes a vector of input features
x
which characterize a
maintenance job. Then
f(x)
is the predicted job duration.
Acela accounts for asymmetric costs using quantile regression to
train its predictor.
Quantile regression
assumes that jobs
have a distribution of durations, and predicts a
conditional
quantile

[
17
]
; i.e., a target quantile of that distribution for an input
set of job features. This is different
from standard regression because standard regression creates a function
g
such
that
g(x)
is the mean job duration for that set of features, while
quantile regression creates the model targeting a different point (different quantile) in
the distribution.
By modeling
high quantiles
(i.e.,
quantiles greater than 50%), Acela penalizes
underprediction more than overprediction, and then generates
prediction values higher than the conditional mean.

Figure 2:

(QR) with different quantiles and standard regression (SR).

Figure
2
visualizes the difference between
quantile regression and standard regression. We use a 1D sine function
as the grountruth and sample 100 data points with random noise, which
are the grey dots. The black curve is the conditional mean fitted from
standard regression. The green, orange, and purple curves are the
conditional quantiles fitted from quantile regressions at three
quantiles 10%, 50%, and 90%, respectively. Consider we want
to predict the value of the function when
x=0.25
(marked by the vertical red line), then the three blue
stars that intersect with the quantile regression curves are the
conditional quantiles at 10%, 50%, and 90%.
We can see that the 90% quantile is above
50% and 10% because given the same
x
(conditioning on
x=0.25
),
90% estimates a higher conditional quantile than 50% and 10%.
More importantly, the quantile output at 90% covers more grey dots
than the conditional mean (black curve) from standard regression. This
example explains why conditional quantile at high quantile estimates
higher value than conditional mean.

Note that strictly speaking, we should compare high conditional quantile to conditional median (i.e., conditional quantile at 50%) instead of conditional mean. Nevertheless, unless the conditional distribution is very concentrated, it is rare that a high conditional quantile (e.g., 90%) is lower than conditional mean. Therefore, throughout the paper, we compare high conditional quantile directly to conditional mean.

By construction models using quantile regression at high quantiles,
Acela reduces the likelihood of underprediction and thus avoids
scheduling more maintenance jobs than can actually finish within the
time budget, reducing the server downtime in a maintenance unit.

We make the following contributions:

•

We showcase an important machine learning for systems
problem where maximizing prediction accuracy is not enough to

•

We present a large-scale characterization of maintenance jobs

Motivational Example

We demonstrate how underprediction can have a larger negative impact
than overprediction for improving maintenance outcomes, despite higher
prediction accuracy. We predict job durations as a
function of job features using two machine learning models. The first
is based on a standard regressor that maximizes accuracy (and thus
treats all misprediction costs equally). The second is based on a
quantile regressor that models the high conditional quantile so that it is biased towards overprediction rather than underprediction (and thus sacrifices some accuracy but accounts
for asymmetric costs).

We run simulations on a real-world test maintenance unit in our datasets, where we integrate predictions from standard regressor and quantile regressors with a scheduler (see details in
section

4
). For both regressors, we train gradient boosting trees using two different loss
functions
[
26
]
: standard regressor uses mean squared error loss to
optimize accuracy, and quantile regressor uses quantile loss that is biased
towards overprediction to optimize maintenance outcome. We randomly sample 10% data from the training set as the validation set for hyperparameter tuning. For both regressors, we train separate models and tune their optimal hyperparameter (e.g., quantile, learning rate) separately for each firmware type. In particular, quantile regressor selects the quantile by meeting the constraint that at least 95% jobs on the validation set are over predicted with the highest prediction accuracy.

respectively, which indicates that standard regressor has higher

quantile regressor has a higher OPR (94%) than the
standard regressor (45%), which is not surprising since it is specifically
constructed to overpredict. Higher OPR pushes the predictions far away from the conditional mean, and thus lower prediction accuracy.

To evaluate the maintenance outcomes between two regressors, we compare their number of server, downtime, and number of unfinished job. We can see that standard regressor has 2
\times
more servers that are taken offline due to the running time exceeding the time budget than the quantile regressor. The standard regressor also has 1.26
\times
longer donwtime and 1.75
\times
more unfinished jobs than the quantile regressor. This is because although the quantile regressor overpredicts
and schedules possibly fewer jobs, it has enough time for scheduled
jobs to finish. Standard regressor, however, underpredicts many jobs
and schedules more jobs that can actually finish, which leads to longer downtime and more unfinished jobs.

This example demonstrates the importance of asymmetric costs: the
maintenance outcome disproportionately benefits from slight
overprediction, but suffers significantly from slight underprediction.
Although standard regression has a better prediction accuracy in MAPE (by
over 2
\times
), it in fact underpredicts more than half of the time. The
results of this example illustrates the most crucial intuition behind
the paper: high prediction accuracy is not enough to guarantee a good
maintenance outcome.

Acela

Based on these insights from previous section, we introduce Acela, a machine learning prediction system that collects features, trains machine learning models, and provides duration predictions for jobs that are to be scheduled.
In this section, we first introduce Acela’s prediction models based on quantile regression, and then describe the training and inference procedure, and conclude this section with some further discussions.

3.1
Quantile Regression

Acela predicts maintenance job durations with collected features, which can be formulated as a regression problem. Assuming that we have a feature space
\mathcal{X}
and duration outputs
\mathcal{Y}
, regression is estimating a mapping from
\mathcal{X}
to
\mathcal{Y}
, given a final sample of the form
({\mathbf{x}},y)
drawn from a distribution
\mathcal{D}
over
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
. The goal of standard regression is to minimize the squared error loss over the distribution
\mathcal{D}
, and the minimizer is the conditional mean because it treats all errors equally. To address the issue of asymmetric costs, Acela uses
quantile regression
, which models the conditional quantile
[
17
]
. We define conditional quantile as follows.

Definition 1
(Conditional quantile at
q
)

Let
0<q<1
, and
{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{X}
be a fixed prediction point.

f(x)
is a conditional quantile of conditional distribution at
q
if for all
x
:

\displaystyle P(Y\leq f({\mathbf{x}})|{\mathbf{x}})=q.

The conditional quantile at 50% is also known as median.

We summarize the differences between standard regression and quantile regression in Table
4
, where
y
is the true duration,
\hat{y}
is the predicted duration,
q
is the input quantile, and
\mathbb{I}(\cdot)=1
if its argument is true and 0 otherwise.

We can see that standard regression and quantile regression have different loss functions. Standard regression treats all errors equally and minimizes the squared error loss, and thus its minimizer is the conditional mean. Quantile regression, however, has an additional parameter
q
in its loss function, which specifies the conditional quantile at
q
, where
q\in(0,1)
. When quantile regression underpredicts,
\mathbb{I}(y\geq\hat{y})=1
, and the loss function becomes
q(y-\hat{y})
. When quantile regression overpredicts,
\mathbb{I}(y<\hat{y})=1
, and the loss function becomes
(q-1)(y-\hat{y})
.

We illustrate the asymmetric costs between underprediction and overprediction with different quantiles in Figure
4
. When
q=50\%
, the quantile loss penalizes equally between underprediction and overprediction. When
q=90\%
the quantile loss penalizes more on underprediction (
y\geq\hat{y}
) than overprediction (
y<\hat{y}
). When
q=10\%
the quantile loss penalizes more on overprediction (
y<\hat{y}
) than underprediction (
y\geq\hat{y}
). Therefore, in order to penalizes more on underprediction and less on overprediction, we just need to set the input quantile to be higher than 50%.

With quantile regression at high quantiles, Acela reduces the likelihood of underprediction and thus avoids scheduling more maintenance jobs than can actually finish within the
time budget, reducing the number of server being taken offline in the maintenance process.

3.2
Training, Retraining, and Inference Procedures

Training.

Acela collects data and trains/retrains models online. Specifically, Acela trains gradient boosting tree regressor with the quantile loss function as the quantile regressor
[
26
]
. To tune the optimal input quantile of the quantile regressor, Acela divides the collected data into training set and validation set; randomly choosing 90% to be the training set and the remaining 10% the validation set. Acela uses grid search to find the optimal input quantile for each quantile regressor. Note that Acela needs to balance the tradeoffs between overprediction rate and prediction accuracy to make sure that the number of jobs that can finish within the time budget is maximized (increasing overprediction rate) while not too much extra time is allocated (increasing prediction accuracy). In our experiments, we set the service level objective (SLO) to be at least 95% jobs on the validation set are over predicted with the highest prediction accuracy. Acela then tunes the optimal quantile based on this SLO.

Retraining.

Acela retrains model periodically because maintenance jobs evolve over time. Specifically, an old version upgrade may not appear or be needed in the future because it is outdated. A new upgrade version that has never been seen before can be used for a new job that is to be scheduled. Both situations can lead to distribution shift between training and inference, and thus decrease prediction accuracy. To address this issue, Acela retrains the quantile regressor when new data are collected. Currently, Acela retrains every week. In the future, Acela can be more adaptive in retraining. For example, Acela can keep monitoring the prediction accuracy at runtime. If the prediction accuracy changes nontrivially such as 5%, Acela will retrain the model with the updated training set.

Inference.

Acela predicts durations for new jobs continually at runtime. The quantile regressor takes job features as inputs, and outputs their predicted durations. The predicted durations are then sent to the scheduler for downstream job scheduling.

Putting It All Together

Figure

displays the overall workflow of Acela. Acela works in an online fashion: it continually collects data as new maintenance jobs are executed, and retrains models as new data come in. The input of Acela is the user SLO for model hyperparameter tuning, which is at least 95% jobs on the validation set are overpredicted with the highest prediction accuracy in our experiments. Acela includes three components: data pipeline, training/retraining, and inference. After collecting data from the data pipeline, Acela splits the data into training and validation set to tune the optimal quantile. Then, Acela will use the trained model to predict durations for new jobs. To adapt to the changes in job distributions over time, Acela retrains model periodically. The output job durations predictions will be sent to a scheduler for downstream job scheduling.

3.4
Discussion

How to select job features for training?

To predict job duration accurately, it is important to select predictive features that influence job durations. There are hundreds of features
that can be collected to model the maintenance process, but not all of
them are predictive for maintenance job durations. Based on our domain
knowledge, maintenance job duration is influenced by both the job type
and server it executes on. We therefore collect features for jobs and servers with some examples as follows. For the job-wise features, we collect firmware type, current upgrade version, and desired upgrade version. For the server hardware features, we collect number of cores, RAM size, disk size, flash size, and boot drive size. For the server manufacturing features, we collect the device information, server geographical location, CPU architecture, and time since last maintenance. Overall, we collected over 80 features to build models for Acela.

Why not using resource usage data as features?

Much prior work uses time-series resource usage data (e.g., CPU and memory usage) as features to predict durations
[
27
,
28
]
. The resource usage data are predictive in prior work because they predict future behavior given the previous measurements for the same workload. When executing maintenance, however, all services need to be drained from the server before executing the maintenance jobs, which means that the server executes different programs before and after draining. When the server is under maintenance, no resource usage data can be collected because the server is offline. Therefore, the only resource usage data that can be collected are from the services that run before the server is drained. Nevertheless, the resource usage data collected from these services are not predictive for maintenance jobs because they are different programs and workloads.
Empirically, we used resource usage data as features in our early evaluation and they showed poor predictability. Therefore, Acela does not include resource usage features in the model.

How to address the complexity of maintenance jobs in quantile regressors?

As illustrated in Figure
3
and Table
2
, different firmware types have different distributions of job durations, which suggest that different firmware types have different optimal quantile for maintenance optimization. 4
Integrating Acela with Scheduling: A Case
Study

We show how Acela’s duration predictions can be integrated with a
scheduler that allocates jobs to each server for maintenance job scheduling. The scheduler described in this section is designed to capture the essential components of a maintenance job scheduler used in production at a major technology company.
Other schedulers can use the
predictions in a similar way. An introduction of a new scheduler or a
full description of an existing scheduler is beyond the scope of this
paper. We focus on the key features of the existing scheduler to
understand how Acela’s duration predictions enhance maintenance
job scheduling.

Maintenance scheduler.

The datacenter’s maintenance
scheduler selects jobs for each server and expects all of them to
finish within the time budget. In practice the scheduler considers
both the predicted job durations (which are provided by Acela in
our case) and the job priorities. Priorities can be set in either two
ways. First, the datacenter operator may manually give a job high
priority based on conversations with stakeholders (e.g., for security reason).
Second, the scheduler may automatically
increase a job priority if it is not scheduled in a particular
maintenance cycle. This second method prevents a job from being
starved; e.g., if a server’s BIOS has not been upgraded for a long
time, its priority will be increased so that it is scheduled in the
next maintenance cycle.

Under these constraints, the existing scheduler design obeys some
rules. First, for each maintenance unit, at most
\beta
(
0<\beta<1
)
percent of servers can be under maintenance at any moment. This rule
guarantees that the majority of servers are available for serving
user-level jobs. This rule is useful especially in the case of
unexpected emergencies or failures. Second, there is an execution
time threshold
\tau
such that if the total running time of a server
exceeds this threshold, it will be taken offline. This threshold is
used to avoid unfair time allocation to extremely long running jobs in
each maintenance unit that could possibly delay the entire maintenance
process.

Leveraging Acela’s prediction for scheduling.

the existing scheduler in Algorithm

running maintenance job at any moment

Otherwise, the scheduling system will take different actions towards
different maintenance status. If a server just finishes a job and the

(

17

\tau

5.2
Evaluation Metrics

For maintenance outcomes, we compare the ratio of the number of servers that are taken offline due to exceeding the time budget of each method to Acela:

\displaystyle\text{OSR}=\frac{o_{\rm base}}{o_{\rm Acela{}}},

where
o_{\rm Acela{}}
is the number of servers that are taken offline of Acela, and
o_{\rm base}
is the number of servers that are taken offline of other baselines. OSR shows the reduction that Acela has servers taken offline than other baselines. Lower OSR is better.

We compare the ratio of downtime (time that a server needs to complete the unfinished jobs after the time buget) of each method to Acela:

\displaystyle\text{DTR}=\frac{d_{\rm base}}{d_{\rm Acela{}}},

where
d_{\rm Acela{}}
is the downtime of Acela, and
d_{\rm base}
is the downtime of other baselines. DTR shows the reduction in time that Acela needs to return to production compared to other baselines. Lower DTR is better.

We use the ratio of the number of jobs that fully complete on servers that are not taken offline at the time budget to the number of jobs that fully complete at the time budget:

\displaystyle\text{JCR}=\frac{n_{\rm online}}{n_{\rm all}}\times 100\%,

where
n_{\rm online}
is the number of jobs that fully complete on servers that are not taken offline at the time budget, and
n_{\rm all}
is the number of jobs that fully complete at the time budget. JCR shows the ratio of effective jobs that fully complete on servers that are not taken offline. Higher JCR is better.

For prediction accuracy, we use mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) metric:

\displaystyle\text{MAPE}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\frac{y_{i}-\hat{y}_{i}}{y_{i}}\right|,

where
n
is the number of jobs considered in a maintenance unit,
y_{i}
is the true duration, and
\hat{y}_{i}
is the predicted duration. Lower MAPE indicates higher prediction accuracy. Both MAPE and root mean squared error (RMSE) measure the prediction accuracy in minimizing conditional mean. We choose MAPE rather than RMSE because MAPE is scale free, while RMSE is scale dependent.

We compare the overprediction rate (fractions of predictions that are higher than the true values):

\displaystyle\text{OPR}=\frac{100\%}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}(\hat{y_{i}}>y_{i}),

where
n
is the number of jobs considered in a maintenance unit,
y_{i}
is the true duration,
\hat{y}_{i}
is the predicted duration, and
\mathbb{I}(\cdot)=1
if its argument is true and 0 otherwise. OPR is used to explain why Acela achieves better maintenance outcomes than other baselines.

Evaluation Methodology

We evaluate on the same datasets described in

In the training set, we randomly sample 90% to be the real training set, and the remaining 10% the validation set. This data split is used to tune hyperparameters such as the input quantile and learning rate for Acela and all the other baselines. To find the optimal quantile for each firmware type, we set a SLO that at least 95% jobs are overpredicted while with the best prediction accuracy in MAPE on the validation set. We then integrate the predictions from Acela and other baslines with the scheduler described in Algorithm

To study how Acela is sensitive to the input quantiles for the top six firmware types, Figure

The x-axis is the quantile, and the y-axis is the MAPE or OPR. Since high quantiles can be biased towards overprediction, we evaluate quantiles at [60, 65, 70, 75, 80, 85, 90, 95, 99]%.

We can see that with increased quantiles, MAPEs almost increase all the time because the predictions keep going further from the conditional mean. Similarly, OPR almost increase all the time because more jobs are overpredicted when higher conditional quantiles are modeled. A very high quantile (e.g., 99%) can have almost highest overprediction rate, but also very low prediction accuracy. It is equivalent to a conservative predictor that assigns excessive time for a single job. This may not lead any servers offline, but it will also require many maintenance cycles to complete all the maintenance that a sever needs, which decreases the datacenter maintenance efficiency. In contrast, a modest high quantile that overpredicts a large amount of jobs with relatively high prediction accuracy is better. These results imply that there is a tradeoff to balance between MAPE and OPR, and Acela addresses it by selecting the optimal quantile on the validation set.

8
Conclusion

Although physical server maintenance is crucial for ensure datacenter capacity and reliability, how to improve the maintenance efficiency for datacenters has been under-explored in the literature. In this paper, we define a new problem termed maintenance job scheduling, and showcases the importance of using predictions from machine learning to tackle it. We distinguish maintenance job scheduling from traditional job scheduling, and reason about why machine learning techniques used in traditional job scheduling should not be directly transferred to maintenance job scheduling. We introduce Acela, a machine learning prediction system that uses quantile regression to bias predictions towards overprediction. We evaluate on datasets from real-world production datacenters, and show that Acela achieves better maintenance outcomes than machine learning techniques from prior work, despite relatively low prediction accuracy. We hope that our work is an inspiration for system researchers to pay attention on maintenance job scheduling, and focus on optimize the outcomes that matter in the maintenance process rather than the prediction accuracy.
