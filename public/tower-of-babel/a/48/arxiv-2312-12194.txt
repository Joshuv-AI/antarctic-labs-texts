Representation of partially ordered sets over Von Neumann regular algebras. More prime, non-primitive regular rings
Giuseppe Baccella

Abstract
. Given an infinite cardinal ℵ \boldsymbol{\aleph} , let 𝐏𝐨𝐬 ℵ {\mathbf{P}\mathbf{o}\mathbf{s}}_{\boldsymbol{\aleph}} be the category whose objects are all partially ordered sets I I , having a finite cofinal subset and such that both I I and the set of all maximal chains of I I have at most cardinality ℵ \boldsymbol{\aleph} , while the morphisms are all maps which are order imbeddings of the domain as an upper subset of the codomain. Given a field 𝕂 \mathbb{K} , for every object I I of 𝐏𝐨𝐬 ℵ {\mathbf{P}\mathbf{o}\mathbf{s}}_{\boldsymbol{\aleph}} we find a unital, locally matricial and hence unit-regular 𝕂 \mathbb{K} -algebra 𝔅 ℵ , 𝕂 ​ ( I ) \mathfrak{B}_{\boldsymbol{\aleph},\mathbb{K}}(I) such that the lattice of all its ideals is order isomorphic to the lattice of all lower subsets of I I . We show that the Grothendieck group 𝐊 0 ​ ( 𝔅 ℵ , 𝕂 ​ ( I ) ) \mathbf{K}_{0}(\mathfrak{B}_{\boldsymbol{\aleph},\mathbb{K}}(I)) , with its natural partial order, is order isomorphic to the restricted Hahn power of ℤ \mathbb{Z} by I I ; this gives a contribution to solve the Realization Problem for Dimension Groups with order-unit . We also show that the algebra 𝔅 ℵ , 𝕂 ​ ( I ) \mathfrak{B}_{\boldsymbol{\aleph},\mathbb{K}}(I) has the following features: (a) 𝔅 ℵ , 𝕂 ​ ( I ) \mathfrak{B}_{\boldsymbol{\aleph},\mathbb{K}}(I) is prime if and only if I I is lower directed; (b) 𝔅 ℵ , 𝕂 ​ ( I ) \mathfrak{B}_{\boldsymbol{\aleph},\mathbb{K}}(I) is primitive if and only if I I has a coinitial chain; (c) 𝔅 ℵ , 𝕂 ​ ( I ) \mathfrak{B}_{\boldsymbol{\aleph},\mathbb{K}}(I) is semiartinian if and only if I I is artinian, in which the case I I is order isomorphic to the primitive spectra of 𝔅 ℵ , 𝕂 ​ ( I ) \mathfrak{B}_{\boldsymbol{\aleph},\mathbb{K}}(I) . Finally we show that the assignment I ↦ 𝔅 ℵ , 𝕂 ​ ( I ) I\mapsto\mathfrak{B}_{\boldsymbol{\aleph},\mathbb{K}}(I) extends to a pair of functors from 𝐏𝐨𝐬 ℵ {\mathbf{P}\mathbf{o}\mathbf{s}}_{\boldsymbol{\aleph}} to the category of 𝕂 \mathbb{K} -algebras, one covariant and the other contravariant.

Introduction

We recall that if
L
is a complete lattice, an element
a\in L
is said to be
compact
if whenever
a\leqslant\bigvee M
for some subset
M\subset L
, then
a\leqslant\bigvee N
for some finite subset
N\subset M
. Given a ring
R
(associative with multiplicative identity), the compact elements of the complete lattice
\mathbb{L}_{2}(R)
of ideals of
R
are the finitely generated ideals, consequently
\mathbb{L}_{2}(R)
is an
algebraic
lattice, meaning that all its elements are join of (possibly infinitely many) compact elements. It is well known that if, in addition,
R
is a von Neumann regular ring, then
\mathbb{L}_{2}(R)
is also distributive.

The problem of determining which algebraic and distributive lattices are isomorphic to
\mathbb{L}_{2}(R)
for some von Neumann regular ring
R
is one among the so called
Realization (or Representation) Problems for Von Neumann regular rings
. A first answer to this problem was given by Bergman in his famous 1986 unpublished note (see
[
12
]
):

Bergman’s Theorem.

An algebric distributive lattice
D
in which the greatest element is compact is isomorphic to
\mathbb{L}_{2}(R)
, for some unital von Neumann regular ring
R
, if either
D
has countably many compact elements, or every element of
D
is a join of
join-irreducible
compact elements
.

In
[
23
]
Goodearl and Wehrung wrote an extended account about the representation problems of distributive algebraic lattices and the connection with the analogous problems for distributive semilattices. The first condition stated in Bergman’s Theorem raised the obvious question: is it possible to remove the countability condition on the set of compact elements of
D
? A negative answer was given by Wehrung in
[
35
]
, where he proved that Bergman’s Theorem cannot be extended to those algebraic distributive lattices having
\boldsymbol{\aleph}_{2}
or more compact elements. However Wehrung proved in
[
37
]
that Bergman’s Theorem still holds for algebraic distributive lattices having at most
\boldsymbol{\aleph}_{1}
compact elements.

Concerning the second condition, we note that the algebric distributive lattices satisfying it are precisely those isomorphic to the lattice (partially ordered by inclusion)
\Downarrow\!\!I
of all lower subsets of some partially ordered set
I
; so, actually, given a partially ordered set
I
having a finite cofinal subset, Bergman had built a locally matricial, unital and hence unit-regular algebra
R
whose lattice of ideals is isomorphic to
\Downarrow\!\!I
.

Other important realization problems arise when one considers the additive abelian monoid
\mathcal{V}(R)
of isomorphism classes of finitely generated right modules over a ring
R
, with the addition defined as
[A]+[B]\,\,\colon\!\!=[A\oplus B]
, and the corresponding Grothendieck group
\mathbf{K}_{0}(R)
. If
R
is a regular ring, then
\mathcal{V}(R)
enjoys some fundamental and well known properties and the problem of deciding wether, given an abelian monoid
M
having the same properties, there exists a regular ring
R
such that
\mathcal{V}(R)
is isomorphic to
M
, was the first which was named as
Realization Problem for Von Neumann Regular Rings
(see
[
6
]
).

The group
\mathbf{K}_{0}(R)
carries a natural structure of a pre-ordered and directed abelian group with order-unit, which is partially ordered in case
R
is unit-regular (see
[
19
, Ch 15]
). This gives rise to the corresponding
Realization Problem
: which partially ordered, directed abelian groups with order unit are isomorphic to
\mathbf{K}_{0}(R)
for some regular ring
R
? (see
[
19
, Problem 29]
).

Partially ordered abelian groups of a special interest are the
dimension groups
, namely the directed, unperforated and interpolation groups. Originally, the name “dimension group” was introduced by Elliott in
[
16
]
to designate a group which is a direct limit of a countable sequence of simplicial groups, i. e. of groups each order-isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
, with the partial order induced by the submonoid
\mathbb{N}^{n}
as positive cone. Later on it was proved by Effros, Handelman and Shen (se
[
15
, Theorem 2.2]
) that a partially ordered abelian group is a (arbitrary) direct limit of simplicial groups if and only if it is a directed, unperforated and interpolation group. It was well known that a countable dimension group is isomorphic to
\mathbf{K}_{0}(R)
for an ultramatricial algebra
R
over a given field. Goodearl and Handelman proved in
[
21
]
that every dimension group with order unit having cardinality
\boldsymbol{\aleph}_{1}
can be realized as
\mathbf{K}_{0}(R)
for a locally matricial (and hence unit-regular) algebra
R
over a given field and, in addition, every infinite tensor product of such dimension groups can be realized similarly. By way of contrast, dimension groups which cannot be realized as
\mathbf{K}_{0}(R)
for a regular ring
R
were found by Wehrung in
[
34
]
and
[
36
]
. Actually the two realization problems for Von Neumann regular rings, the first concerning algebric distributive lattices and the second concerning dimension groups are intimately connected, as illustrated by P. P. Růžička in
[
31
]
and
[
32
]
.

Recently L.Vas accomplished in
[
33
]
the task of extending the concepts of a simplicial group and of a dimension group to partially ordered abelian groups having an additional action of a group
\varGamma
; by considering the
\varGamma
-graded Grothendieck group
\mathbf{K}_{0}^{\varGamma}(R)
of a
\varGamma
-graded ring
R
(as introduced in
[
24
]
) she studied the corresponding realization problems of the above graded dimension groups as graded Grothendieck groups.

The deep role of countable dimension groups in the theory of topological dynamical systems is widely displayed in the monograph
[
14
]
.

If
I
is a partially ordered set and
G(I)
denotes the free abelian group generated by
I
, the
(restricted) Hahn power
of
\mathbb{Z}
by

is the group

, equipped with the partial order defined by declaring positive those elements

of

such that

whenever

is a maximal element of the support of

(see Section

[

we proved that

is a dimension group and, if

has a finite cofinal subset, the sum of all maximal elements of

is a order unit; thus we proved that if

is an artinian poset with a finite cofinal subset, then

is realizable as
\mathbf{K}_{0}(R)
, where

is a unit-regular and semiartinian ring whose primitive spectrum is isomorphic to

.

Our main goal in the present work is to show that if
I
is any partially ordered set, with the only condition of having a finite cofinal subset, then there exists a locally matricial unital algebra
R
such that
\mathbf{K}_{0}(R)
is isomorphic to
G(I)
. In order to give some more detail, for a given
infinite
cardinal
\boldsymbol{\aleph}
, let us call
\boldsymbol{\aleph}
-poset
every partially ordered set

such that both the cardinalities of

and of the set

of all maximal chains of

do not exceed
\boldsymbol{\aleph}
. Given a field
\mathbb{K}
, for every
\boldsymbol{\aleph}
-poset

having a finite cofinal subset
we build a locally matricial, unital
\mathbb{K}
-algebra
\mathfrak{B}_{\boldsymbol{\aleph},\mathbb{K}}(I)
having the following main properties: the lattice of all ideals of

is order isomorphic to

. Thus, while the first property simply confirms one part of Bergman’s Theorem, with the second property we enlarge the class of dimension groups with order unit which are realizable as
\mathbf{K}_{0}
of some unital locally matricial algebra, by adding the restricted Hahn powers of
\mathbb{Z}
by any partially ordered set

having a finite cofinal subset.

The first two sections have a merely technical nature. We start with an
\boldsymbol{\aleph}
-poset

, where
\boldsymbol{\aleph}^{(I)}
denotes the set of all maps from

, and we establish the algebra

of all

, as

. By using suitable partitions of

, for every

we operate two subsequent selections of specially shaped matrices in

. With the first one we obtain a
\mathbb{K}
-subalgebra

of

, isomorphic to
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{F}\mathbb{M}_{\mathbb{K}}(\boldsymbol{\aleph})

if and only if

. With the second selection we find for each

\mathbb{K}

of

if and only if

are not comparable, while

if

.

In the third section we define the family

of “building blocks” which will enable us to define the algebra

is isomorphic to the algebra of all finite ranked linear tranformations of an

is a maximal element of

as a suitable subalgebra isomorphic to
\mathbb{K}
. We prove that

is an independent family of
\mathbb{K}
-subspaces of

with the following properties: given

, if

are not comparable then
H(I,i)H(I,j)=\{\mathbf{0}\}
otherwise, if

and

and

are not zero and are contained in

is a
\mathbb{K}
-subalgebra of

. We explore the conditions under which

has a multiplicative identity; in particular this turns out to be the case if

is an upper subset of

which is bounded by above by a finite number of maximal elements of

Starting with the fourth section we assume that the
\boldsymbol{\aleph}
-poset
I

has a finite cofinal subset
and, finally, we define
\mathfrak{B}_{\boldsymbol{\aleph},\mathbb{K}}(I)\,\,\colon\!\!=H(I,I)
, which is a unital
\mathbb{K}
-algebra by the above. We provide an explicit description of the ideals of
\mathfrak{B}_{\boldsymbol{\aleph},\mathbb{K}}(I)
; more generally we prove that, given any subset

defines an isomorphism from the lattice of all lower subsets of

to the lattice of ideals of

is a locally matricial

The fifth section is entirely devoted to the computation of the Grothendieck group of
\mathfrak{B}_{\boldsymbol{\aleph},\mathbb{K}}(I)
and, as we already mentioned previously, the main result is that
\mathbf{K}_{0}(\mathfrak{B}_{\boldsymbol{\aleph},\mathbb{K}}(I))

, the restricted Hahn power of
\mathbb{Z}
by

In the sixth section we investigate when the algebra
\mathfrak{B}_{\boldsymbol{\aleph},\mathbb{K}}(I)
is prime and when it is primitive.
In his great two volumes book “Ring Theory” Rowen wrote: “The abstract theory of noncommutative regular rings has been a disappointment” (see
[
29
, p.277]
) because some known basic facts about commutative regular rings, which were hoped to be true in general, turned out to fail. For instance, the question whether every prime regular ring is primitive has an obvious positive answer in the commutative case. By 1970 Kaplansky conjectured an affirmative answer in general (see
[
26
, p.2]
) and, again, Rowen commented in
[
30
, p.308]
that “…until recently the major question in the theory of (von Neumann) regular rings was whether a prime regular ring need be primitive”, a comment already mentioned in
[
2
]
. A first counterexample to Kaplansky conjecture was found in 1977 by Domanov
[
13
]
, but only thirty seven years later a whole class of counterexamples was found by Abrams, Bell and Rangaswamy; in
[
2
]
they found necessary and sufficient conditions for an oriented graph
E
in order that the Leavitt algebra
L(E)
(over any field) is prime and those for which
L(E)
is primitive. These conditions involve the preorder induced in the set
E_{0}
of vertices of
E
by the paths; namely, if

, then declare that
v\leqslant w
if and only if there is a path from
v
to
w
(including vertices as zero length paths); thus they proved that
L(E)
is prime if and only if
E_{0}
is downward directed, while
L(E)
is primitive if and only if: (i)
E_{0}
is downward directed, (ii) every cycle in
E
has an exit and (iii)
E_{0}
has a countable coinitial subset
S
. Now

is regular if and only if
E
is acyclic (see
[
2

, in which the case the above preorder

is a partial order; as a consequence, Abrams, Bell and Rangaswamy result gives rise to whole classes of prime and non-primitive regular rings.

With our result we get new classes of regular algebras which are prime but not primitive. In fact we prove that
\mathfrak{B}_{\boldsymbol{\aleph},\mathbb{K}}(I)
is prime if and only if the poset

is downward directed, while it is right primitive if and only if
I
has a coinitial chain, in which the case it is also left primitive; if
\boldsymbol{\aleph}>\boldsymbol{\aleph}_{0}
there are plenty of downward directed

-posets without a coinitial chain. Curiously enough, the set of conditions on an acyclic graph

for primitivity of the Leavitt algebra

is very close to the condition on a poset
I
in order that
\mathfrak{B}_{\boldsymbol{\aleph},\mathbb{K}}(I)
is primitive; in fact, it is not difficult to show that if a poset is downward directed and has a countable coinitial subset, then it has a countable coinitial chain, therefore Abrams, Bell and Rangaswamy result may be restated as follows: if
E
is an acyclic graph, then the regular algebra
L(E)
is primitive if and only if the set of vertices
E_{0}
, partially ordered by paths, has a countable coinitial chain.

By combining our result with the characterization of the ideals of

Lemma 1.2
.

With the above settings and notations, the following properties hold:

(1)

If
i,j\in I
and
i<j
, then
\mathcal{E}_{j}
is
\boldsymbol{\aleph}
-coarser than
\mathcal{E}_{i}
; specifically, for every

we have that

(1.3)

E_{v}=\bigcup\left\{E_{u}\left|\,\,u\in\boldsymbol{\aleph}^{(i\leqslant)}\text{ and }u\left|{}_{\{j\leqslant\}}\right.=v\right.\right\}.

(2)

Given

and a finite subset

, assume that there are

such that

. Consequently, if
j\in I

, then no member of
\mathcal{E}_{i}
is contained in some member of
\mathcal{E}_{j}
.

Proof.

(1) Assume that
i<j
and observe that

(1.4)

\{\,\{i\nleqslant\},\,\,\{i\leqslant\}\setminus\{j\leqslant\},\,\{j\leqslant\}\,\}

is a partition of
I
. Accordingly, every element
p\in\boldsymbol{\aleph}^{(I)}
decomposes uniquely as a disjoint union

where
r\in\boldsymbol{\aleph}^{(i\nleqslant)}
,
s\in\boldsymbol{\aleph}^{(\{i\leqslant\}\setminus\{j\leqslant\})}
and

. Consequently, for every

we have that

and hence (
1.3
) follows.

(2) Under the assumption of (2) suppose that, on the contrary,
i\not\in\{\leqslant J\}
. There are
u\in\boldsymbol{\aleph}^{(i\leqslant)}
,
j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}\in J
and

\alpha\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, such that

According to the assumption, if
p\in\boldsymbol{\aleph}^{(I)}
and
\displaystyle p\left|{}_{\{i\leqslant\}}\right.=u
, then there is some
\alpha\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
such that
\displaystyle p\left|{}_{\{j_{\alpha}\leqslant\}}\right.=u_{\alpha}
. Set

By the finiteness of

, we can choose some

and, since

for all

, we can define the map

by setting

Then we have that

. However, by the choice of

for every

. This means that

and we have a contradiction.
∎

Starting from this point we let
\mathbb{K}
be a given field. Given an
\boldsymbol{\aleph}
-poset

-algebra

-algebra

;

.

Notations 1.3
.

With the setting, we adopt the following notations:

•

We denote respectively by
\mathbf{0}
and
\mathbf{1}
the zero and the unital matrices of Q(I).

•

If
\mathbf{a}\in Q(I)
and

, we use the
symbol
\mathbf{a}(x,y)
to denote the entry at the intersection of the

x
-th row with the

-th column of
\mathbf{a}
(i. e. the
(x,y)
-entry of
\mathbf{a}
), instead of the more
traditional symbol
\mathbf{a}_{xy}

arrays, other than single letters, in order to designate the position of the entries of the matrices we deal with, our choice should guarantee a better readability. If

, then
\mathbf{a}(Y,Z)
denotes the
(Y,Z)
-block of
\mathbf{a}
, that is the
submatrix
(\mathbf{a}(x,y))_{y\in Y,z\in Z}
of
\mathbf{a}
.

•

For every

, we denote with
\mathbf{e}_{Y}
the
idempotent diagonal matrix such that
\mathbf{e}_{Y}(x,x)
is
1
if
x\in Y

and is
0
otherwise. If
x,y\in X(I)
, we write
\mathbf{e}_{x}
instead of
\mathbf{e}_{\{x\}}
, while
\mathbf{e}_{x,y}
stands for the usual
(x,y)
-matrix unit, namely the matrix whose
(x,y)
-entry is

•

\mathbb{F}\mathbb{R}_{X(I)}(\mathbb{K})
denotes the subset of

of all matrices having only finitely many nonzero rows.

Of course we shall consider
\mathbb{K}
as a
subring of
Q(I)
by identifying each element of
\mathbb{K}
with the
corresponding scalar matrix in
Q(I)
. Without any assumption on the ring
\mathbb{K}
(apart being unitary), it is well known that

and the following equality holds:

\mathbb{F}\mathbb{R}_{X(I)}(\mathbb{K})=\bigoplus\{\mathbf{e}_{x}Q(I)\mid x\in X(I)\};

moreover, for every

the following hold:

\displaystyle\mathbf{e}_{x}Q(I)=\mathbf{e}_{x}\mathbb{F}\mathbb{R}_{X(I)}(\mathbb{K}),

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}\mathbb{R}_{X(I)}(\mathbb{K})=\mathbb{F}\mathbb{R}_{X(I)}(\mathbb{K})\mathbf{e}_{x}\mathbb{F}\mathbb{R}_{X(I)}(\mathbb{K})=Q(I)\mathbf{e}_{x}Q(I).

With our assumption that
\mathbb{K}
is a field,

is regular and right selfinjective; moreover
\mathbb{F}\mathbb{R}_{X(I)}(\mathbb{K})=\Soc(Q(I))

as a right ideal; we emphasize that this latter does not depend on the specific isomorphism
\varphi
. We say that a set
\mathcal{S}
of idempotents of

is a
complete set of pairwise orthogonal and primitive idempotents

\mathcal{S}=\varphi(\mathcal{E})
for some isomorphism
\varphi
as above.

Theorem 1.5

4.

{\Downarrow\!\!J}

is a lower subset of

Suppose that

Remark 4.2
.

If

Proposition 4.3
.

If

We recall that a ring

if for every

there exists a unit

such that
x=xyx
. It is well known that a regular ring

implies

. If

is a locally matricial

-algebra, then

is regular and it is unit-regular in case it has multiplicative identity. Indeed, given

, there is a matricial
\mathbb{K}
-subalgebra

of

such that

. Since

is unit-regular, then there is a unit

of

such that
x=xyx
. If

denotes the multiplicative identity of

, we see immediately that

is a unit of

and

. Thus, given

, the algebra

is regular and, by Proposition
3.8
, it is unit-regular in case

is finitely sheltered in

; in particular
\mathfrak{B}(I)
is unit-regular.

If
J\subset I
, let us denote by
J_{1}
the set of all
minimal elements of
J
. The
dual classical
Krull filtration
of the poset

is the ascending chain

(J_{\alpha})_{0\leqslant\alpha}
of subsets of

, indexed over the ordinals, defined recursively as follows (see

[
3
]
):

\displaystyle J_{0}\,\,\colon\!\!=\emptyset,

\displaystyle J_{\alpha+1}\,\,\colon\!\!=J_{\alpha}\cup\left(J\setminus J_{\alpha}\right)_{1}\quad\text{ for all }\alpha,

\displaystyle J_{\alpha}\,\,\colon\!\!=\bigcup_{\beta<\alpha}J_{\beta}\quad\text{ if $\alpha$ is a limit
ordinal.}

Note that each

is a lower subset of

. Clearly there exists a smallest ordinal
\xi
such that
J_{\xi+1}=J_{\xi}
and hence

for every
\alpha>\xi
; moreover

is artinian (i. e. it satisfies the DCC or,
equivalently, every chain of

is well ordered) if and only if

and, in this case, the ordinal

is called the
dual classical Krull dimension
of

and the family
\left(J_{\alpha+1}\setminus J_{\alpha}\right)_{\alpha<\xi}
is a partition of

.

Given

, the next result illustrates the tight connection between the dual classical Krull filtration of

and the Loewy chain
(\Soc_{\alpha}(H(I,J)))_{0\leqslant\alpha}
of ideals of

.

We recall that if

is a regular ring (unitary or not), then every homogeneous component of
\Soc(R_{R})
is a minimal ideal and
\Soc(R_{R})=\Soc({{}_{R}R})
, from which one can easily infer that
\Soc_{\alpha}(R_{R})=\Soc_{\alpha}({{}_{R}R})
for every ordinal
\alpha
.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
J
be a subset of
I
. For every ordinal
\alpha
we have that

(4.2)

\Soc_{\alpha}(H(I,J))=H(I,J_{\alpha}).

Consequently

is semiartinian if and only if

is artinian, in which the case the Loewy length of

equals the dual classical Krull dimension of

. In particular
\mathfrak{B}(I)
is semiartinian if and only if

is artinian.

Proof.

Obviously (
4.2
) holds if
\alpha=0
. Now the ideal

is semisimple as a right ideal of

, because it is a direct sum of the simple and semisimple rings

, for

. On the other hand, since

is regular, each homogeneous component of
\Soc(H(I,J))
is a minimal ideal and so, by Theorem

, it is of the form

for some minimal element

of

. This shows that (

) holds if
\alpha=1
. Given an ordinal
\alpha>0
, assume inductively that

\Soc_{\beta}(H(I,J))=H(I,J_{\beta})

whenever
\beta<\alpha
. If
\alpha
is a limit ordinal, since
J_{\alpha}=\bigcup_{\beta<\alpha}J_{\alpha}
, then we have

\Soc_{\alpha}(H(I,J))=\bigcup_{\beta<\alpha}\Soc_{\beta}(H(I,J))=\bigcup_{\beta<\alpha}H(I,J_{\beta})=H(I,J_{\alpha}).

Suppose that

for some

and

\displaystyle\varphi_{J_{\beta},J}(H(I,J_{\beta+1}))=\varphi_{J_{\beta},J}[H(I,J_{\beta})\oplus H(I,J_{\beta+1}^{\bullet})]=H(I,J_{\beta+1}^{\bullet})

The last statement follows directly from the definitions, toghether with the fact that if

Remark 4.5
.

Given a division ring
D
and an artinian partially ordered set
I
, in
[
10
]
we presented a construction which produces a semiartinian and unit-regular ring
D_{I}
, which is isomorphic to a subring of
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{F}\mathbb{M}_{\boldsymbol{\aleph}}(D)
containing
D
(this latter identified with the subring of all scalar matrices), where
\boldsymbol{\aleph}
is the first infinite cardinal such that
\boldsymbol{\aleph}\geqslant|I|
and
\boldsymbol{\aleph}\geqslant|\mathcal{M}(I)|
. The construction, heavily based on ordinal arithmetic, turns out to be rather tricky and cumbersome with no possibility to extend it to non-artinian partially ordered sets. The present construction overcomes this problem and, in addition, if
I
is an artinian
\boldsymbol{\aleph}
-poset having a finite cofinal subset, the algebra
\mathfrak{B}(I)
satisfies the same properties (1), (2), (3), (4), (5) and (8) of
D_{I}
, as stated in
[
10
, Theorem 9.5]
;
mutatis mutandis
, their proofs are identical.

5.
\mathbf{K}_{0}(\mathfrak{B}(I))
.

We keep going on with the assumption that
I
is an
\boldsymbol{\aleph}
-poset having a finite cofinal subset. In this section we afford the task of investigating the structure of the partially ordered abelian group
\mathbf{K}_{0}(\mathfrak{B}(I))
. It will turn out that
\mathbf{K}_{0}(\mathfrak{B}(I))
is order isomorphic to a group which is a special instance of a more general construction, well known within the literature of lattice-ordered groups (see
[
4
]
, page 6 for instance). Specifically, given a family
\mathcal{H}=(H_{i})_{i\in I}
of partially ordered abelian groups, the
Hahn group

V(I,H_{i})
of
\mathcal{H}
is the subgroup of the direct product
\prod_{i\in I}H_{i}
consisting of all elements having noetherian support, endowed with the partial order induced by declaring positive those elements having positive coordinates relative to the maximal elements of the support. The subgroup
\varSigma(I,H_{i})
of
V(I,H_{i})
of those elements having finite support, endowed with the induced partial order, is called the
restricted Hahn group
; of course, as a group,
\varSigma(I,H_{i})
is merely the direct sum
\bigoplus_{i\in I}H_{i}
. When all
H_{i}
coincide with a single group
H
, the groups
V(I,H_{i})
and
\varSigma(I,H_{i})
are called respectively the
Hahn power
and the
restricted Hahn power of
H
by
I
. Our main result is to show that
\mathbf{K}_{0}(\mathfrak{B}(I))
is order isomorphic to the restricted Hahn power of
\mathbb{Z}
by
I
, whose support is just the free abelian group with
I
as a basis; we give here a description of it with some more details, suited for our needs.

Let us denote by
G(I)
the free abelian group with
I
as a basis; we keep the standard practice by representing each element
x\in G(I)
as a unique linear combination of elements of
I
with coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}
and, if
i\in I
, we denote by
x_{i}
the coefficient of
i
. As usual, the
support
of an element
x\in G(I)
is the set
\Supp(x)\,\,\colon\!\!=\{i\in I\mid x_{i}\neq 0\}
. As it was shown in
[
11
]
, the set

(5.1)

M(I)\,\,\colon\!\!=\{x\in G(I)\mid x_{i}>0\text{ if $i$ is a maximal element of $\Supp(x)$}\}

is a conical submonoid of
G(I)
, in the sense that if
x,y\in M(I)
and
x+y=0
, then
x=0=y
. We will consider
G(I)
partially ordered with
M(I)
as positive cone, so that
G(I)
is the
restricted Hahn power
of
\mathbb{Z}
by
I
. It was shown in
[
11
, Proposition 4.2]
that
G(I)
is a dimension group in which the sum of all maximal elements of
I
is an order-unit and, consequently,
G(I)
is directed and
M(I)
is a refinement monoid, in the sense that if
x,y,z\in M(I)
and
x\leqslant y+z
, then there exist
a,b\in M(I)
such that
a\leqslant x
,
b\leqslant y
and
x=a+b
(see
[
20
, Ch. 3]
).

The following two results will not be used in the present work; nonetheless these propositions illustrate remarkable properties of the group
G(I)
. We recall that an element
p
of an abelian monoid
M
is
prime
if it is not invertible and, whenever
p\leqslant x+y
for some
x,y\in M
, then either
p\leqslant x
or
p\leqslant y
(here “
\leqslant
” is the algebraic preorder: if
a,b\in M
, then
a\leqslant b
means that
b=a+c
for some
c\in M
). If
M
is a conical, cancellative refinement monoid (hence the algebraic preorder is a partial order), it is easy to see that a non-invertible element
p\in M
is prime if and only if, given
a,b\in M
, the equality
p=a+b
implies that either
p=a
or
p=b
.

Proposition 5.1
.

The prime elements of the monoid
M(I)
are precisely the minimal elements of
I
.

Proof.

Let us consider the subset

\mathcal{A}=\{i+x\mid i\in I,\Supp(x)\subset\{<i\}\text{ and $x_{j}<0$ for all $j\in\Supp(x)\setminus\{i\}$}\}

of
M(I)
, let
\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle
be the submonoid of
M(I)
generated by
\mathcal{A}
and let us prove first that
\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle=M(I)
. Given
x\in M(I)
, if
|\Supp(x)|\leqslant 1
, then it is clear that
x\in\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle
. Given a positive integer
n
, assume that
x\in\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle
whenever
x\in M(I)
and
|\Supp(x)|<n
, let
x\in M(I)
be such that
|\Supp(x)|=n
and let
m
be a maximal element of
\Supp(x)
. By setting

y=x_{m}m+\sum\{x_{i}i\mid i\in\{<m\}\text{ and }x_{i}<0\},

we have that
x^{\prime}=x-y\in M(I)
. In fact, let
k
be a maximal element of
\Supp(x^{\prime})
. If
k
is maximal in
\Supp(x)
too, since
\Supp(x^{\prime})=\Supp(x)\setminus\Supp(y)
we have that
x^{\prime}_{k}=x_{k}>0
. Otherwise there is some
h\in\Supp(x)
such that
k<h
. Necessarily
h\in\Supp(y)\subset\{\leqslant m\}
, from which
k\leqslant m
. Since
k\neq m
and
k\not\in\Supp(y)
, we conclude that
x_{k}>0
. As
|\Supp(x^{\prime})|<n
, by the inductive assumption we have that
x^{\prime}\in\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle
; since
y\in\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle
, it follows that
x=x^{\prime}+y\in\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle
, as wanted.

Let
p
be a prime element of
M(I)
. We have that
p=x_{(1)}+\cdots+x_{(n)}
for some
x_{(1)},\ldots,x_{(n)}\in\mathcal{A}
. Since
M(I)
is a conical, cancellative refinement monoid, then
p=x_{(\alpha)}
for some
\alpha\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, that is there are
i\in I
and
y\in G(I)
, with
\Supp(y)\subset\{<i\}
and
y_{j}<0
for
j\in\Supp(y)
, in such a way that
p=i+y
. Thus
p=(i+2y)+(-y)
and, since both
i+2y
and
-y
are in

and

, primality of

implies that
p=i+2y
. Consequently

and so
p=i
. If
i
were not minimal, that is

for some
j\in I
, then

. Again, since both

and

are in

, primality of

implies that either

or

, which is impossible.

Conversely, let
i
be a minimal element of
I
, let

, assume that

and let

be a maximal element of
\Supp(x)\cup\Supp(y)
such that

. Then
x_{j}+y_{j}=0
and so either
x_{j}<0
, or

: a contradiction with the assumption that

and

are nonzero elements of

. This means that
\Supp(x)\cup\Supp(y)=\{i\}
and, since

is cancellative, we conclude that either

or

.

If

is a partially ordered abelian group, we denote by

. A subgroup

(see
[

Proposition 5.2
.

The assignment

defines an isomorphism from the lattice of all lower subsets of

.

Proof.

Let
L
be a lower subset of

and let us prove first that

is convex. Given

and

, assume that

but

. Then there exists some

such that

and, since
\Supp(x)
is finite, there is a maximal element

of
\Supp(x)
such that

and
x_{m}>0
, because

; note that

otherwise, since

is a lower subset of

Next, let

6.
Primeness versus primitivity. The primitive spectrum.

In this section we investigate which properties of the
\boldsymbol{\aleph}
-poset

\mathfrak{B}(I)

is prime but not primitive. As we know (see Remark

\mathfrak{B}(I)
is isomorphic to

for a unique upper subset

of

is prime if and only if

is downward directed, while the existence of a coinitial chain in

is necessary and sufficient for

to be primitive.

At any rate, based on the results so far obtained, we can exhibit easily here a first special set of primitive factor rings of
\mathfrak{B}(I)

that

is homogeneous. If

such that

is a primitive ring and so

is a primitive ideal of

exactly when

In order to reach our goal we rely on the following three results, the first of which is a characterization of right primitive rings which was already used in

Proposition 6.1
.

A ring

\mathfrak{I}
such that
\mathfrak{I}+\mathfrak{A}=R
for every ideal
\mathfrak{A}\neq 0

The second is a generalization of

to be primitive.

Proposition 6.2
.

Let

be a unital ring, let

be any totally ordered set and assume that there is a family

of nonzero idempotents of

Proof.

The proof is totally the same as that of

, however we repeat it for completeness. Concerning rigth primitivity, it follows from the assumption that if

and so

is a chain of proper right ideals of

and so

is a proper right ideal of

. Let

be any nonzero ideal of

{\mathbf{P}\mathbf{o}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{b}}
whose objects are all partially ordered abelian groups and morphisms are all order preserving group homomorphisms.
Next we show that, given an infinite cardinal
\boldsymbol{\aleph}
and a field
\mathbb{K}
, the assignment
I\mapsto\mathfrak{B}(I)
extends to a pair of functors
\mathfrak{B}(-)
,
\mathfrak{B}^{*}(-)
, the first covariant and the second contravariant, from the subcategory
{\mathbf{P}\mathbf{o}\mathbf{s}}_{\boldsymbol{\aleph}}
of
{\mathbf{P}\mathbf{o}\mathbf{s}}
of all
\boldsymbol{\aleph}
-posets
having a finite cofinal subset
to the category
{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{l}\mathbf{g}}_{\mathbb{K}}
of
unital

\mathbb{K}
-algebras and
not necessarily unital

\mathbb{K}
-algebra homomorphisms. Finally, we will see that these four functors match with the functor
\mathbf{K}_{0}(-)
, in the sense that there are natural equivalences
\rho(-)\colon G(-)\approx\mathbf{K}_{0}(-)\cdot\mathfrak{B}(-)
and
\rho^{*}(-)\colon G^{*}(-)\approx\mathbf{K}_{0}(-)\cdot\mathfrak{B}^{*}(-)
.

Let
I
and
J
be partially ordered sets and let
f\colon I\to J
be an isotone map. As
G(I)
and
G(J)
are the free abelian groups generated by
I
and
J
respectively, the most “natural” group homomorphism
G(f):G(I)\to G(J)
to be assigned to
f
is clearly the unique homomorphism which extends
f
to
G(I)
, that is

G(f)\left(\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}i\right)=\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}f(i).

Of course we have that
G(1_{I})=1_{G(I)}
and, if
g
is a second isotone map and
fg
is defined, then
G(fg)=G(f)G(g)
.
We would like
G(f)
to be isotone, that is

(7.1)

G(f)(M(I))\subset M(J),

However this may fail if
f
is not injective. For example, suppose that
I=\{h,k\}
, where
h<k
, and
J=\{j\}
; then the unique map
f:I\to J
is obviously isotone. If we consider the element
x=-2h+k\in M(I)
, we have that
G(f)(x)=-j\not\in M(J)
. Fortunately (
7.1
) holds if
f
is injective. In fact, assume that
x\in M(I)
and let
m
be a maximal element of
\Supp(G(f)(x))=f(\Supp(x))
. Then the unique element
{l\in M(I)}
such that
m=f(l)
is maximal in
\Supp(x)
. In fact, if
i\in\Supp(x)
and
{l\leqslant i}
, then
m=f(l)\leqslant f(i)
and
f(i)\in\Supp(G(f)(x))
, therefore
m=f(i)
and hence
l=i
. Consequently
G(f)(x)_{m}=x_{l}>0
and this proves that
G(f)(x)\in M(J)
.

We are now allowed to speak about the covariant functor
G(-)
from the category of all partially ordered sets and
injective isotone maps
to the category
{\mathbf{P}\mathbf{o}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{b}}
.

Passing to consider the contravariant side, let again
I
and
J
be partially ordered sets and
f\colon I\to J
an isotone map. Again, in order to assign to
f
an order preserving group homomorphism from
G(J)
to
G(I)
we have at our disposal a “natural” path to follow. In fact, by identifying
G(J)
as the subgroup
\mathbb{Z}^{(J)}
of
\mathbb{Z}^{J}
of all maps from
J
to
\mathbb{Z}
having finite support, we see that
f
induces the homomorphism of groups

f^{*}\colon\mathbb{Z}^{(J)}\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{I}

defined by
f^{*}(x)=x\circ f
for all
x\in G(J)
, that is
(f^{*}(x))_{i}=x_{f(i)}
for all
i\in I
. Given
x\in G(J)
, we have that

\Supp(f^{*}(x))=f^{-1}(\Supp(x)),

therefore
f^{*}(x)\in G(I)
for all
x\in G(J)
if and only if
f^{-1}(j)
is finite for each
j\in J
. But, even if this latter condition holds, if
f
is not injective the homomorphism
f^{*}
may fail to be an isotone map, as the following example shows.

Example 7.1
.

Let
I=\{i,j,k\}
and
J=\{u,v\}
with
i<j,i<k,j\nleqslant k,k\nleqslant j
and
u<v
; next define
f\colon I\to J
with
f(i)=f(j)=u
and
f(k)=v
. If we consider
x=v-u\in M(J)
, then we see that
f^{*}(x)=-i-j+k\not\in M(I)
, because
j
is maximal in
I
, therefore
f^{*}
is not isotone.

Thus, also for the contravariant case we must restrict our attention to the injective isotone maps and, as we are going to see with the next result, only part of them are allowed. We observe that if
f\colon I\to J
is injective, then
f^{*}
is an epimorphism, as it is the projection of
\mathbb{Z}^{(J)}
onto
\mathbb{Z}^{(f(I))}\simeq\mathbb{Z}^{(I)}
with kernel
\mathbb{Z}^{(J\setminus f(I))}
. Moreover, for every
i\in I
and
j\in J
we have that

(f^{*}(j))_{i}=\begin{cases}1,\text{ if $j=f(i)$;}\\
0,\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Consequently:

(7.2)

\text{for every $i\in I$ and $j\in J$,}\qquad f^{*}(j)=\begin{cases}i,\text{ if $j=f(i)$;}\\
0,\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Proposition 7.2
.

Let
I,J
be partially ordered sets and let
f\colon I\to J
be an injective and isotone map. Then the homomorphism
f^{*}:G(J)\to G(I)
is isotone, namely

(7.3)

f^{*}(M(J))\subset M(I),

if and only if
f
is an order imbedding and
f(I)
is an upper subset of
J
.

Proof.

Suppose that (
7.3
) holds and let
i,j\in I
be such that
f(i)\leqslant f(j)
. Then
f(j)-f(i)\in M(J)
and consequently, by using (
7.2
), we see that
j-i=f^{*}(f(j)-f(i))\in M(I)
. As a result
i\leqslant j
and this proves that
f
is an imbedding. Next, let
i\in I
,
j\in J
and assume that
f(i)\leqslant j
but
j\not\in f(I)
. Then
-i=f^{*}(j-f(i))\in M(I)
again by (
7.2
), which is impossible. Thus necessarily
j\in f(I)
and so
f(I)
is an upper subset of
J
.

Conversely, assume that
f
is an imbedding and
f(I)
is an upper subset of
J
. Given
x\in M(J)
,
x\neq 0
, and a maximal element
m
of
\Supp(f^{*}(x))
, we claim that
(f^{*}(x))_{m}>0
. Suppose, on the contrary, that
(f^{*}(x))_{m}\leqslant 0
. Then
x_{f(m)}=(f^{*}(x))_{m}\leqslant 0
and so
f(m)
is a non-maximal element of
\Supp(x)
. Let
j\in\Supp(x)
be such that
f(m)<j
; then
j\in f(I)
by the assumption. If we let
i\in I
be such that
f(i)=j
, again from the assumption we have that
m<i
. But
(f^{*}(x))_{i}=x_{f(i)}=x_{j}\neq 0
, hence
i\in\Supp(f^{*}(x))
and this contradicts the maximality of
m
in
\Supp(f^{*}(x))
. Thus
(f^{*}(x))_{m}>0
and therefore
f^{*}(x)\in M(I)
.
∎

We are led to consider the category whose objects are all partially ordered sets and morphisms are those maps which are order imbedding of the domain as an upper subset of the codomain, which we denote by
{\mathbf{P}\mathbf{o}\mathbf{s}}
. It is obvious that
1_{I}^{*}=1_{G(I)}
for every object
I
of
{\mathbf{P}\mathbf{o}\mathbf{s}}
, while if
f\colon I\to J

g\colon J\to K
are morphisms in
{\mathbf{P}\mathbf{o}\mathbf{s}}
, then
(gf)^{*}=f^{*}g^{*}
. Thus we are legitimate to define the contravariant functor
G^{*}(-)
from the category
{\mathbf{P}\mathbf{o}\mathbf{s}}
to the category
{\mathbf{P}\mathbf{o}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{b}}
.

The condition of being an order imbedding of the domain as an upper subset of the codomain, for isotone maps between partially ordered sets, is very close to a known condition on morphisms between directed graphs; we feel it is worth to enlighten that connection.

We recall that a
(directed) graph
is an ordered 4-tuple

E=(E^{0},E^{1},s_{E},r_{E}),

where
E^{0},E^{1}
are two disjoint sets and

s_{E}\colon E^{1}\to E^{0},\qquad r_{E}\colon E^{1}\to E^{0}

are maps. The elements of
E^{0}
are called the
vertices
and those of
E^{1}
the
arrows
(or
edges
) of
E
. A
graph morphism
from a graph
E
to a graph
F
is an ordered pair
f=(f^{0},f^{1})
consisting of two maps
f^{0}:E^{0}\to F^{0}
and
f^{1}:E^{1}\to F^{1}
such that

s_{F}f^{1}=f^{0}s_{E}
and
r_{F}f^{1}=f^{0}r_{E}
. We denote by
\mathbf{D}\mathbf{i}\mathbf{G}\mathbf{r}
the category of all directed graphs and arbitrary graph morphisms.

Every partially ordered set
I
gives rise to its
associated graph

(I^{0},I^{1},s_{I},r_{I})
, where
I^{0}=I
,
I^{1}=\{(i,j)\in I\times I\mid i<j\}
and
s_{I}((i,j))=i
,
r_{I}((i,j))=j
for all
(i,j)\in I^{1}
. If
I,J
are partially ordered sets and
f:I\to J
is an isotone map, then we may consider the two maps
f^{0}:I^{0}\to J^{0}
,
f^{1}:I^{1}\to J^{1}
, where
f^{0}(i)=f(i)
for all
i\in I
and, if
i<j
in
I
, then
f^{1}((i,j))=(f^{0}(i),f^{0}(j))
; we can check immediately that the pair
(f^{0},f^{1})
is a graph morphism from
(I^{0},I^{1},s_{I},r_{I})
to
(J^{0},J^{1},s_{J},r_{J})
. Conversely, if
(f^{0},f^{1})\colon(I^{0},I^{1})\to(J^{0},J^{1})
is any graph morphism, then the map
f\,\,\colon\!\!=f^{0}\colon I\to J
is isotone. We say that
(f^{0},f^{1})
is the
graph morphism associated
to
f
.

In seeking for an appropriate subcategory of
\mathbf{D}\mathbf{i}\mathbf{G}\mathbf{r}
to which one can extend, as a functor, the map which assigns to every directed graph
E
its path
\mathbb{K}
-algebra
\mathbb{K}E
and its Leavitt path algebra
L_{\mathbb{K}}(E)
, in
[
22
]
Goodearl defines a graph morphism
f=(f^{0},f^{1})\colon E\to F
to be a
CK
-
morphism
(short for
Cuntz-Krieger morphism
) if it satisfies the following conditions:

(1)

f^{0}
and
f^{1}
are both injective;

(2)

For each
v\in E^{0}
which is neither a sink nor an infinite emitter,
f^{1}
induces a bijection from
s_{E}^{-1}(v)
to
s_{F}^{-1}(f^{0}(v))

(for row-finite graphs, meaning that each vertex emits at most finitely many arrows,
CK
-morphisms are precisely the
complete morphisms
of
[
5
]
, p. 161); then he shows that the assignments
E\mapsto\mathbb{K}E
and
E\mapsto L_{\mathbb{K}}(E)
admit natural functorial extensions to the subcategory
\mathbf{C}\mathbf{K}\mathbf{G}\mathbf{r}
of
\mathbf{D}\mathbf{i}\mathbf{G}\mathbf{r}
of all graphs together with
CK
-morphism. Let us say that
f
is a
strict
CK
-morphism
if it satisfies the above condition (1) together with the following stronger form of (2):

(3)

For
every

v\in E^{0}
,
f^{1}
induces a bijection from
s_{E}^{-1}(v)
to
s_{F}^{-1}(f^{0}(v))
.

Proposition 7.3
.

Let
I,J
be partially ordered sets and let
f\colon I\to J
be an isotone map. Then
f
is an order imbedding and
f(I)
is an upper subset of
J
if and only if the associated graph morphism
(f^{0},f^{1})\colon(I^{0},I^{1})\to(J^{0},J^{1})
is a strict
CK
-morphism.

Proof.

Suppose that
f
is an order imbedding and
f(I)
is an upper subset of
J
. Then both
f^{0}
,
f^{1}
are clearly injective. Given
i\in I
, we have that
f^{1}
induces an injective map from
s_{I}^{-1}(i)
to
s_{J}^{-1}(f^{0}(i))
, which is also surjective. In fact, given
(k,l)\in s_{J}^{-1}(f^{0}(i))
, necessarily
k=f^{0}(i)<l
. As
f^{0}(I)=f(I)
is an upper subset of
J
, there is some
j\in I
such that
l=f(j)
and, since
f
is an order imbedding, we have that
i<j
, thus
(i,j)\in I^{1}
and hence
(k,l)=f^{1}(i,j)
.

Conversely, suppose that
(f^{0},f^{1})
is a strict
CK
-morphism. Then
f=f^{0}
is injective. Let
i,j\in I
and assume that
f(i)<f(j)
, that is
(f(i),f(j))\in I^{1}
. As
(f(i),f(j))\in s_{J}^{-1}(f^{0}(i))
, by the assumption there is a unique
(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in s_{I}^{-1}(i)
such that
(f(i),f(j))=f^{1}(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})
. Necessarily
i^{\prime}=i
,
j^{\prime}=j
, so that
i<j
. Next, let
i\in I
and
k\in J
be such that
f(i)<k
. Then
(f(i),k)\in s_{J}^{-1}(f^{0}(i))
and, again from the assumption, we infer that
(f(i),k)=f^{1}((i,j))
for a unique
j\in I
. Thus
k=f(j)\in f(I)
and we are done.
∎

We now proceed to find the promised two functorial extensions of the assignment
I\mapsto\mathfrak{B}(I)

Let

{\mathbf{P}\mathbf{o}\mathbf{s}}_{\boldsymbol{\aleph}}

\left(\overline{f}_{i}(\mathbf{a})\right)((u,E),(v,F))=\mathbf{a}((u\cdot f|_{\{i\leqslant\}},f^{-1}(E)),(v\cdot f|_{\{i\leqslant\}}vf,f^{-1}(F)))

for all

,

\mathbb{K}

\alpha_{I,i}\colon\mathbb{F}\mathbb{R}_{X(i\leqslant)}(\mathbb{K})\longrightarrow H(I,i)

\alpha_{I,i}\colon\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{C}\mathbb{F}\mathbb{M}_{X(i\leqslant)}(\mathbb{K})}\mathbb{K}\longrightarrow H(I,i)=\mathbf{e}_{X(I)_{i}}\mathbb{K}

when

\widehat{f}_{i}\,\,\colon\!\!=\alpha_{J,f(i)}\overline{f}_{i}\alpha_{I,i}^{-1}\colon H(I,i)\longrightarrow H(J,f(i))

\widehat{f}\,\,\colon\!\!=\bigoplus_{i\in I}\widehat{f}_{i}\colon\bigoplus_{i\in I}H(I,i)=\mathfrak{B}(I)\longrightarrow\bigoplus_{i\in I}H(J,f(i))=H(J,f(I)).

Lemma 7.4
.

With the above setting and notations,
\widehat{f}
is a
\mathbb{K}
-algebra isomorphism.

Proof.

Given
i\in I
, let us first compute explicitly
\widehat{f}_{i}(\mathbf{a})
for each
\mathbf{a}\in H(I,i)
. First observe that, inasmuch as
H(I,i)\subset S(I,i)
, if

then for every

,

,

and

\mathbf{a}((s\cup u,C\cup E),(s\cup v,C\cup F))=\mathbf{a}((t\cup u,D\cup E),(t\cup v,D\cup F)).

This legitimates us to define the map

\beta\colon H(I,i)\longrightarrow\mathbb{F}\mathbb{R}_{X(i\leqslant)}(\mathbb{K})

as follows: given
\mathbf{a}\in H(I,i)

and

\beta(\mathbf{a})((u,E),(v,F))\,\,\colon\!\!=\mathbf{a}((t\cup u,C\cup E),(t\cup v,C\cup F)),

where

\alpha_{I,i}^{-1}=\beta.

Now we observe that, given

\begin{split}\widehat{f}_{i}(\mathbf{a})&((r\cup u,A),(r\cup v,B))=\left[\overline{f_{i}}\left(\alpha_{I,i}^{-1}(\mathbf{a})\right)\right]((u,A\cap\{f(i)\leqslant\}),(v,B\cap\{f(i)\leqslant\}))\\

\left[\widehat{f}(\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b})\right]((r\cup u,A),(r\cup v,B))=\left[\widehat{f}(\mathbf{a})\cdot\widehat{f}(\mathbf{b})\right]((r\cup u,A),(r\cup v,B))

\displaystyle\left[\widehat{f}(\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b})\right]((r\cup u,A),(r\cup v,B))

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b})(((r\cup u)f,D\cup E),((r\cup v)f,D\cup F))

If we write

v^{\prime}=v|_{\{f(i)\leqslant\}\setminus\{f(j)\leqslant\}},

\displaystyle\left[\widehat{f}(\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b})\right]((r\cup u,A),(r\cup v,B))

\displaystyle\cdot\mathbf{b}(((r\cup v^{\prime}\cup w)f,D\cup G),((r\cup v)f,D\cup F)).

On the other hand we have

\displaystyle\left[\widehat{f}(\mathbf{a})\cdot\widehat{f}(\mathbf{b})\right]((r\cup u,A),(r\cup v,B))

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in X(J)}\left\{\phantom{\bigotimes}\!\!\!\!\!\!\![\widehat{f}(\mathbf{a})]((r\cup u,A),y)\cdot[\widehat{f}(\mathbf{b})](y,(r\cup v,B))\right\}.

Here again, a single summand is not zero only if

y=(r\cup v^{\prime}\cup w,C\cup H),

where
w\in\boldsymbol{\aleph}^{(f(j)\leqslant)}
and
H
is a maximal chain of
\{f(i)\leqslant\}
which belongs to the set

\mathcal{H}\ =\bigl\{\phantom{\bigotimes}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!H\in\mathcal{M}(\{f(i)\leqslant\})\mid f(j)\in H,\,H\cap[f(i),f(j)]=B\cap[f(i),f(j)]\bigr\}.

Thus, by using (
7.4
) we obtain:

\begin{split}&\left[\widehat{f}(\mathbf{a})\cdot\widehat{f}(\mathbf{b})\right]((r\cup u,A),(r\cup v,B))\\
&\phantom{xxxxxxxx}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in\boldsymbol{\aleph}^{(f(j)\leqslant)}\\
E\in\mathcal{E}\end{subarray}}\left\{\phantom{\bigotimes}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\![\widehat{f}(\mathbf{a})]((r\cup u,A),(r\cup v^{\prime}\cup w,C\cup H))\right.\\
&\left.\phantom{xxxxxxxxxxxx}\cdot[\widehat{f}(\mathbf{b})]((r\cup v^{\prime}\cup w,C\cup H),(r\cup v,B))\right\}\\
&\phantom{xxxxxxxx}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in\boldsymbol{\aleph}^{(f(j)\leqslant)}\\
E\in\mathcal{E}\end{subarray}}\left\{\phantom{\bigotimes}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\mathbf{a}(((r\cup u)f,D\cup E),((r\cup v^{\prime}\cup w)f,D\cup f^{-1}(H))\right.\\
&\left.\phantom{xxxxxxxxxxxx}\cdot\mathbf{b}(((r\cup v^{\prime}\cup w)f,D\cup f^{-1}(H)),((r\cup v)f,D\cup F))\right\}.\end{split}

Finally, since
\mathcal{G}=\{f^{-1}(H)\mid H\in\mathcal{H}\}
, the latter sum coincides with the sum at the second member of (
7.7
), therefore the equality (
7.5
) holds. The case in which
i>j
is handled in the same manner and we conclude that
\widehat{f}
is a
\mathbb{K}
-algebra homomorphism.
∎

We are now in a position to define the
\mathbb{K}
-algebra homomorphisms

\mathfrak{B}(f)\colon\mathfrak{B}(I)\longrightarrow\mathfrak{B}(J)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathfrak{B}^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\aleph},\mathbb{K}}(f)\colon\mathfrak{B}^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\aleph},\mathbb{K}}(J)\longrightarrow\mathfrak{B}^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\aleph},\mathbb{K}}(I):

we take the first as the composition of
\widehat{f}
followed by the inclusion into
\mathfrak{B}(J)
and the second as the composition of the projection of
\mathfrak{B}(J)
onto
H(J,f(I))
, with kernel the ideal
H(J,J\setminus f(I))
(see
4.2
and note that
J\setminus f(I)
is a lower subset of
J
), followed by
\widehat{f}^{-1}
.

Proposition 7.5
.

With the above settings,
\mathfrak{B}(-)
and
\mathfrak{B}^{*}(-)
are functors from
{\mathbf{P}\mathbf{o}\mathbf{s}}_{\boldsymbol{\aleph}}
to the category
{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{l}\mathbf{g}}_{\mathbb{K}}
, the first covariant and the second contravariant.

Proof.

For every
i\in I
, as
\overline{\left(1_{I}\right)}_{i}=1_{\mathbb{F}\mathbb{R}_{X(i\leqslant)}(\mathbb{K})}
, it is clear that
\widehat{\left(1_{I}\right)}_{i}=1_{H(I,i)}
, consequently
\mathfrak{B}(1_{I})=1_{\mathfrak{B}(I)}=\mathfrak{B}^{*}(1_{I})
. Let
f\colon I\to J
and
g\colon J\to K
be morphisms in
{\mathbf{P}\mathbf{o}\mathbf{s}}_{\boldsymbol{\aleph}}
. A straightforward computation shows that

(\overline{gf})_{i}=\overline{g}_{f(i)}\cdot\overline{f_{i}},

from which we infer easily that

(\widehat{gf})_{i}=\widehat{g}_{f(i)}\widehat{f}_{i}\quad\text{and}\quad(\widehat{gf})^{-1}_{i}=\widehat{f}^{-1}_{i}\widehat{g}\,^{-1}_{f(i)}.

Consequently

\mathfrak{B}(gf)=\mathfrak{B}(g)\mathfrak{B}(f)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathfrak{B}^{*}(gf)=\mathfrak{B}^{*}(f)\mathfrak{B}^{*}(g).

∎

Remark 7.6
.

If
I\in{\mathbf{P}\mathbf{o}\mathbf{s}}_{\boldsymbol{\aleph}}
, we know that
\mathfrak{B}(I)
is a unital
\mathbb{K}
-algebra, specifilally

1_{\mathfrak{B}(I)}=\sum\{\mathbf{e}_{X(I)_{m}}|m\in\mathbf{M}(I)\}

(see Proposition
3.8
). If
f\colon I\to J
is a morphism of
{\mathbf{P}\mathbf{o}\mathbf{s}}_{\boldsymbol{\aleph}}
, while the
\mathbb{K}
-algebra homomorphism
\mathfrak{B}^{*}(f)
is always unital,
\mathfrak{B}_{\boldsymbol{\aleph},K}(f)
is unital if and only if
\varphi(\mathbf{M}(I))=\mathbf{M}(J)
.

Given a
unital
ring
R
, it is standard to define
\mathbf{K}_{0}(R)
as the Grothendieck group of the commutative monoid of isomorphism classes of all finitely generated right
R
-modules; this makes
\mathbf{K}_{0}(-)
a covariant functor from the category of all unital rings and all unital ring homomorphisms, to the category of partially ordered abelian group. Fortunately a natural extension of
\mathbf{K}_{0}(-)
to the category
\mathbf{R}\mathbf{n}\mathbf{g}
(this is a notation which seems due to Faith) of not necessarily unital rings and not necessarily unital ring homomorphisms is available (see Ch. 5, §7 in
[
28
]
). Specifically, given
R\in\mathbf{R}\mathbf{n}\mathbf{g}
, one considers first the “unitization” ring
R_{+}
of
R
, defined as the abelian group
R\times\mathbb{Z}
together with the multiplication
(x,m)(y,n)\,\,\colon\!\!=(xy+my+nx,mn)
, so that the assignment
x\mapsto(x,0)
identifies
R
as an ideal of
R_{+}
; thus, by considering the canonical epimorphism
\pi_{R}\colon R_{+}\to R_{+}/R\simeq\mathbb{Z}
, one defines
\mathbf{K}_{0}(R)
as the kernel of
\mathbf{K}_{0}(\pi_{R})
. If
\alpha\colon R\to S
is a morphism in
\mathbf{R}\mathbf{n}\mathbf{g}
and
\alpha_{+}\colon R_{+}\to S_{+}
is the unital homomorphism defined by
(x,m)\mapsto(\alpha(x),m)
, then
\mathbf{K}_{0}(\alpha)
is taken as the unique homomorphism which makes commutative the diagram

\begin{CD}\mathbf{K}_{0}(R)@>{\subset}>{}>\mathbf{K}_{0}(R_{+})\\
@V{\mathbf{K}_{0}(\alpha)}V{}V@V{}V{\mathbf{K}_{0}(\alpha_{+})}V\\
\mathbf{K}_{0}(S)@>{\subset}>{}>\mathbf{K}_{0}(S_{+}).\end{CD}

If
R
has a multiplicative identity
e
, then
(e,0)
is a central idempotent of
R_{+}
and so
R_{+}\simeq R\times\mathbb{Z}
; consequently the above definition of
\mathbf{K}_{0}(R)
matches with the standard one.

Suppose that
R
,
S
are unital regular rings and
\alpha\colon R\to S
is a morphism in
\mathbf{R}\mathbf{n}\mathbf{g}
. By the above, for every idempotent
e\in R
we have:

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}_{0}(\alpha)([eR])=\mathbf{K}_{0}(\alpha_{+})([(e,0)R_{+}])=[(e,0)R_{+}\otimes_{R_{+}}S_{+}]=[(\alpha(e),0)S_{+}]

\displaystyle=[\alpha(e)S\times\{0\}]=[\alpha(e)S].

Since
\mathbf{K}_{0}(R)
is generated by the set
\{[eR]\mid e=e^{2}\in R\}
, we see that
\mathbf{K}_{0}(\alpha)
is the unique homomorphism such that
\mathbf{K}_{0}(\alpha)([eR])=[\alpha(e)S]
for every idempotent
e\in R
.

Let us come back to the functors
\mathfrak{B}(-)
and
\mathfrak{B}^{*}(-)
. In view of all above, given a morphism
f\colon I\to J
of
{\mathbf{P}\mathbf{o}\mathbf{s}}_{\boldsymbol{\aleph}}
, it makes sense to speak about the homomorphism

\mathbf{K}_{0}(\mathfrak{B}(f))\colon\mathbf{K}_{0}(\mathfrak{B}(I))\to\mathbf{K}_{0}(\mathfrak{B}(J))
and hence about the composite functor
\mathbf{K}_{0}(\mathfrak{B}(-))
. If
I\in{\mathbf{P}\mathbf{o}\mathbf{s}}_{\boldsymbol{\aleph}}
, by Theorem
5.7
there is a unique isomorphism
\rho(I)\colon G(I)\to\mathbf{K}_{0}(\mathfrak{B}(I))
which sends each
i\in I
to
[\mathbf{u}\mathfrak{B}(I)]
, where
\mathbf{u}
is an arbitrary primitive idempotent of
H(I,i)
. We have then two diagrams

\begin{CD}G(I)@>{\rho(I)}>{}>\mathbf{K}_{0}(\mathfrak{B}(I))\\
@V{G(f)}V{}V@V{}V{\mathbf{K}_{0}(\mathfrak{B}(f))}V\\
G(J)@>{\rho(J)}>{}>\mathbf{K}_{0}(\mathfrak{B}(J)),\end{CD}\qquad\qquad\qquad\begin{CD}G^{*}(I)@>{\rho(I)}>{}>\mathbf{K}_{0}(\mathfrak{B}^{*}(I))\\
@A{G^{*}(f)}A{}A@A{}A{\mathbf{K}_{0}(\mathfrak{B}^{*}(f))}A\\
G^{*}(J)@>{\rho(J)}>{}>\mathbf{K}_{0}(\mathfrak{B}^{*}(J)).\end{CD}

Let
i\in I
and let
\mathbf{u}
be any primitive idempotent of
H(I,i)
. Then
(\mathfrak{B}(f))(\mathbf{u})=\widehat{f}_{i}(\mathbf{u})
is a primitive idempotent of
H(J,f(i))
and so we obtain:

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}_{0}(\mathfrak{B}(f))(\rho(I)(i))=\mathbf{K}_{0}(\mathfrak{B}(f))([\mathbf{u}\mathfrak{B}(I)]

\displaystyle=[(\mathfrak{B}(f)(\mathbf{u}))\mathfrak{B}(J)]=[(\widehat{f}_{i}(\mathbf{u}))\mathfrak{B}(J)]=\rho(J)(f(i))=\rho(J)(G(f)(i)).

Since
G(I)
is free with
I
as a basis, this shows that the first diagram commutes.

Next, let
j\in J
and let
\mathbf{v}
be a primitive idempotent of
H(J,j)
. If
j\not\in f(I)
, then
j\in\Ker(G^{*}(f))
(see (
7.2
)) and
H(J,j)\subset\Ker(\mathfrak{B}^{*}(f))
, hence both
\rho(I)(G^{*}(f)(j))
and
\mathbf{K}_{0}(\mathfrak{B}^{*}(f))(\rho(j))
are zero. If
j\in J
and
i
is the unique element of
I
such that
j=f(i)
, then:

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}_{0}(\mathfrak{B}^{*}(f))(\rho(J)(j))=\mathbf{K}_{0}(\mathfrak{B}^{*}(f))([\mathbf{v}\mathfrak{B}^{*}(J)]

\displaystyle=[(\mathfrak{B}^{*}(f)(\mathbf{v}))\mathfrak{B}^{*}(I)]=[(\widehat{f}^{-1}(\mathbf{v}))\mathfrak{B}^{*}(I)]=[(\widehat{f}^{-1}_{i}(\mathbf{v}))\mathfrak{B}^{*}(I)]

\displaystyle=\rho(I)(i)=\rho(I)(G^{*}(f)(j)).

Here again, since
G(J)
is free with
J
as a basis, this shows that the second diagram commutes.
Finally we may conclude as follows:

Proposition 7.7
.

With the above notations and settings, the assignment
I\mapsto\rho(I)
defines two natural equivalences

\rho(-)\colon G(-)\approx\mathbf{K}_{0}(-)\cdot\mathfrak{B}(-)\quad\text{and}\quad\rho^{*}(-)\colon G^{*}(-)\approx\mathbf{K}_{0}(-)\cdot\mathfrak{B}^{*}(-).
