The Spectrum of Self-Adjoint Extensions associated with Exceptional Laguerre Differential Expressions
Dale Frymark, Jessica Stewart Kelly

Abstract
. Exceptional Laguerre-type differential expressions make up an infinite class of Schrödinger operators having rational potentials and one limit-circle endpoint. In this manuscript, the spectrum of all self-adjoint extensions for a general exceptional Laguerre-type differential expression is given in terms of the Darboux transformations which relate the expression to the classical Laguerre differential expression. The spectrum is extracted from an explicit Weyl m m -function, up to a sign. The construction relies primarily on two tools: boundary triples, which parameterize the self-adjoint extensions and produce the Weyl m m -functions, and manipulations of Maya diagrams and partitions, which classify the seed functions defining the relevant Darboux transforms. Several examples are presented.

1.
Introduction

First introduced in
[
30
,
31
]
in 2009, exceptional orthogonal polynomials (XOPs) describe a class of Sturm–Liouville polynomial families where some (exceptional) degrees of polynomials are missing. These XOPs form eigenfunctions of rational Sturm–Liouville equations, but fall outside of the restrictions of the classical Böchner theorem
[
4
]
that characterizes the classical orthogonal polynomial (COP) systems of Hermite, Laguerre and Jacobi. The properties of XOP have been widely studied over the past 13 years
[
14
,
15
,
18
,
25
]
as this area has brought forth interesting connections between XOP families and their COP relatives as well as surprising observations such as the fact that although a finite number of degrees are omitted from the sequence of XOP solutions, each family is complete in the associated natural Hilbert space setting.

The motivation of
[
14
]
to study these families was two fold—first, in response to a generalized Böchner problem posed in
[
32
]
and second, an interest in quantum mechanics. As such, there are numerous applications to mathematical physics. Perhaps most significantly, XOPs are, up to a gauge factor, eigenfunctions of exactly solvable potentials obtained by taking Darboux transforms of potentials, called rational extensions, from quantum mechanics
[
20
,
26
,
33
]
. In particular, the harmonic oscillator, isotonic oscillator and trigonometric Darboux–Pösch–Teller potential have rational extensions defined by Hermite, Laguerre and Jacobi polynomials, respectively. Many of these rational extensions are translationally shape invariant
[
12
]
, and their corresponding Darboux transforms are usually called supersymmetric quantum mechanical (SUSY QM) partnerships.

Specific types of XOPs were initially introduced individually; well-known XOPs include Hermite XOPs
[
13
]
, Type I-III Laguerre XOPs
[
23
]
and Type I and II Jacobi XOPs
[
22
]
. The spectral properties of the operators in each case have also been determined
[
2
,
13
,
22
,
23
,
24
]
. However, it was shown in
[
11
]
that there are an infinite number of distinct XOP classes. Although all XOP families are derived from a COP family, most of which do not fit into the finite pre-existing “type” classifications. In this manuscript, the focus will be on Laguerre-type XOP families and in particular, the associated spectral analysis.

Generally speaking, every XOP family is related to a COP family by a sequence of Darboux transformations
[
16
,
17
,
21
,
34
]
. When a suitable Darboux transformation is applied to a COP, the result is an XOP that is a eigenfunction for an exceptional eigenvalue problem. Darboux transformations are often called “state-deleting” because when applied to an operator with a set of eigenfunctions (i.e. an operator with COP solutions) they remove one or more of the eigenfunctions from this set. With regard to the spectrum, which consists only of eigenvalues, the term “state-deleting” is misleading as the set may remain the same or even include new points after a Darboux transform is performed. It is also not immediately clear how the Darboux transformation impacts different self-adjoint extensions of the same symmetric differential expression; it is this affect of the Darboux transformation on the spectrum is what we aim to study. For instance, we ask does another extension compensate for these removed eigenfunctions in some way? Perturbation theory gives two immediate restrictions. First, because the spectrum of one self-adjoint extension is discrete, the spectrum of all self-adjoint extensions must be discrete, see e.g.
[
35
,
36
]
. Second, the eigenvalues of extensions must change continuously with the parameterization of the self-adjoint extension.

To study the affect of the Darboux transformation on self-adjoint expressions, we exploit the intertwining nature of the COP and XOP expressions. If the classical Laguerre expression is given in Schrödinger form, Darboux transforms can be classified via their changes to the potential thanks to the well-known Darboux–Crum formulas
[
7
]
. These changes are given by a Wronskian whose entries are eigenfunctions of the COP expression. The process of determining the affect of the Darboux transformation on the spectrum requires manipulating general Wronskians into a standard form from which information can be extracted. Fortunately, this study may utilize shape invariance where many multi-step Darboux transformations can be shown to be equivalent up to a constant. The manuscripts
[
6
]
and
[
12
]
both give equivalence relations for Wronskians, and the methods used therein can be adapted for our purposes.

To exploit spectral information from the manipulated Wronskians, we use the framework of boundary triples. This perspective is unique as compared to other spectral analyses of XOP families. The theory of boundary triples provides a natural parameterization of all self-adjoint extensions in order to produce a Weyl
m
-function. The Weyl
m
-function associated with an operator provides all of the relevant spectral data. Here we are primarily focused on only the location of eigenvalues, but is is possible to extract the spectral measure and even eigenfunctions
[
5
]
. The challenge of applying the theory in this context is that solutions to the general XOP must be normalized. This normalization simplifies to evaluating the transformed solution at
x=0
, which is achievable thanks to a standard form resulting from manipulation of the general Wronskians. Crucially, boundary triples for general XOP expressions are very easy to obtain, as they will carry the same structure as in the COP Laguerre expression with adjusted parameters.

2.
Preliminaries

Generally, a XOP sequence
\{p_{n}\}_{\mathbb{N}_{0}\backslash A}
, where
p_{n}
is a polynomial of degree
n
and
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}_{0}
is a non-empty finite set, satisfies the following properties:

(i)

each
y=p_{n}
is a solution to the associated second-order eigenvalue problem;

(ii)

the associated eigenvalue problem admits no polynomial eigenfunctions having degree
n\in A
;

(iii)

the sequence
\{p_{n}\}_{\mathbb{N}_{0}\backslash A}
is orthogonal on an open interval with respect to a positive measure; and

(iv)

all moments
\{\mu_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
exist and are finite.

Each XOP expression may be written as the composition of first-order operators or Darboux transformations; these first-order operators are derived from COP expressions. We outline the process below.

Suppose
\ell
is a second-order expression of the form

(2.1)

\ell[y](x)=p(x)y^{\prime\prime}(x)+q(x)y^{\prime}(x)+r(x)

whose associated eigenvalue equation is

(2.2)

\ell[y]=\lambda y,

where
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
.
Define rational functions

\displaystyle P(x)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\int\frac{q(z)}{p(z)}dz\right),

\displaystyle W(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{P(x)}{p(x)},\quad\mbox{ and }

\displaystyle R(x)

\displaystyle=r(x)W(x).

Multiplying Eq. (
2.2
) by
W(x)
yields the associated symmetric Sturm-Liouville form of Eq. (
2.1
)

(2.3)

P(y^{\prime})^{\prime}+Ry=\lambda Wy.

It follows that
W(x)
is the weight function associated with the expression
\ell
.

Two second-order expressions
\ell
and
\mathcal{L}
are
gauge-equivalent
if there exists a rational function
\sigma
such that

\mathcal{L}=\sigma\ell\sigma^{-1}.

In order to derive the XOP operator from its COP counterpart, we first need to rewrite
\ell
as a composition of two first-order operators. A quasi-rational function
\phi
is a
quasi-rational eigenfuction
for the expression
\ell
if
\ell[\phi]=\lambda\phi
for
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
. For example, in the case of the Laguerre COP, the quasi-rational eigenfunctions that produce XOP systems are:

(2.4)

\displaystyle\phi_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=L_{m}^{\alpha}(x)

(2.5)

\displaystyle\phi_{2}(x)

\displaystyle=e^{x}L_{m}^{\alpha}(-x)

(2.6)

\displaystyle\phi_{3}(x)

\displaystyle=x^{-\alpha}L_{m}^{-\alpha}(x)

(2.7)

\displaystyle\phi_{4}(x)

\displaystyle=e^{x}x^{-\alpha}L_{m}^{\alpha}(-x).

The quasi-rational eigenfunction Eq. (
2.6
) produces the Type I Laguerre XOP family. The reader is directed to Section
8
for more details into the decomposition of the COP Laguerre expression and formulation of the Type I expression.

Proposition 2.1
.

[
11

For a second-order differential operator

having rational coefficients, let

\displaystyle w

\displaystyle=\frac{\phi^{\prime}}{\phi},

\displaystyle=-w-\frac{q}{p}+\frac{\widehat{b}}{b}

The operators

\displaystyle\ell=BA+{\lambda}_{0},

The operator

\mathcal{L}[y](x)=p(x)y^{\prime\prime}(x)+\mathcal{Q}(x)y^{\prime}+\mathcal{R}(x)y(x)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}

\displaystyle=q+p^{\prime}-\frac{2pb^{\prime}}{b},

\displaystyle=\frac{pW}{b^{2}}=\frac{P}{b^{2}}.

When

The transformation

is a (rational)

. In fact, with appropriate assumptions, this process may be iterated

One crucial property that arises from this construction is the following:

(Intertwining Property)
.

[
11
, Proposition 3.2]

\displaystyle A\ell=\mathcal{L}A,\hskip 20.00003pt\ell B=B\mathcal{L}.

Some final observations in regards to notation—to simplify our discussion, we will refer to all self-adjoint extensions of a symmetric operator as a “family.” XOP and COP families thus refer to families that contain one extension which possesses a set of XOPs or COPs. The notation

2.2.
Maya Diagrams

Maya diagrams will be used to indicate the indices of seed functions within the Wronskians that define Darboux transformations. These Maya diagrams are vitally important to the construction of XOPs and will be manipulated throughout the manuscript. We include a brief overview here that mostly follows
[
6
]
. More information on Maya diagrams, Young diagrams and partitions relating to XOP families may be found in
[
6
,
12
]
.

A
Maya diagram

M
is a infinite subset of integers that contains finitely many non-negative integers and excludes finitely many negative integers. Visually, imagine an infinite row of boxes with a fixed origin. These boxes are either filled (the integer belongs to
M
) or empty (the integer does not belong to
M
). Thus,
M
is characterized by two finite sequences of integers: the positive integers included in
M
and the negative integers excluded by
M
.

Each of the non-negative integers
n_{i}
in
M
are labeled to make a decreasing sequence by

\displaystyle n_{1}>n_{2}>\dots>n_{r_{1}}\geq 0,

where
r_{1}
is the number of filled boxes to the right of the origin. If no boxes are filled to the right of the origin, the sequence is empty and
r_{1}=0
. Likewise, the number of empty boxes to the left of the origin can be denoted as
r_{4}
, the negative integers
k
corresponding to these boxes assigned values via
n^{\prime}=-k-1
and

\displaystyle n_{1}^{\prime}>n_{2}^{\prime}>\dots>n_{r_{4}}^{\prime}\geq 0.

The Maya diagram can then be succinctly described by these two sequences as

\displaystyle M=(n_{1}^{\prime},n_{2}^{\prime},\dots,n_{r_{4}}^{\prime}~|~n_{1},n_{2},\dots,n_{r_{1}}).

As an example, let
M
be the Maya diagram
M=(5,2,1~|~4,3,1)
, whose graphical representation may be seen in Figure
1
.

All boxes to the left of those shown in Figure

are filled, while those to the right are empty. The origin of a Maya diagram may be shifted to the left or right by adding or subtracting, respectively. The Maya diagram
\widetilde{M}=M+3
is represented by Figure

.

In particular, the length of the two subsequences,
r_{1}+r_{4}
, is not necessarily stable under such translations:
M
has
6
indices and
\widetilde{M}
has
5
.

A Maya diagram is in
canonical form
if there are no empty boxes to the left of the origin, but the first box to the right is empty. This form is useful for comparing diagrams and can always be obtained in the current context by applying a shift. If
M+t
puts a Maya diagram
M
into canonical form for some
t\in\mathbb{Z}
, we denote this shift as
t=t(M)
. In the context of the above example,
t(M)=6
, or
n_{1}^{\prime}+1
.

Alternatively, the
conjugate canonical form
of a Maya diagram is the position when there are no filled boxes to the right of the origin, but the first box to the left is filled. The required shift to put
M
into conjugate canonical form is denoted by
t^{\prime}=t^{\prime}(M)
. In the above example,
t^{\prime}(M)=-(n_{1}+1)=-5
and we have the associated representation in Figure
3
.

One of the goals of Section

will be to manipulate the Maya diagrams
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
of Eqs. (

) and (

) so that they are either in canonical or conjugate canonical position and can therefore each be described by a single finite decreasing sequence of non-negative integers. Naturally, such sequences describe a partition, see Eqs. (
4.12
) and (
4.13
). Hence, any given Maya diagram gives rise to a partition, when shifted into canonical form, and a conjugate partition, when shifted into conjugate canonical form.

3.
Motivating Example

We begin with an example: the Type I Laguerre exceptional family. The spectral properties of the extension
\mathcal{L}_{0}
are already known from
[
23
]
, but the authors find it instructive to present the construction of the boundary triple and
m
-functions in this specific context before moving on to the general setting.

The classical Laguerre differential expression
\ell^{{\alpha}}
acts via

(3.1)

\displaystyle\ell^{{\alpha}}[f](x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=-xf^{\prime\prime}(x)+(x-{\alpha}-1)f^{\prime}(x)\,.

For fixed
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
\ell^{{\alpha}}[f](x)
has the rational factorization

(3.2)

\displaystyle-\ell^{{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=B^{I,{\alpha}}_{m}\circ A^{I,{\alpha}}_{m}+{\alpha}+m+1,

where

(3.3)

\displaystyle A^{I,{\alpha}}_{m}[f]

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=L_{m}^{{\alpha}}(-x)f^{\prime}(x)-L_{m}^{{\alpha}+1}(-x)f(x),

(3.4)

\displaystyle B^{I,{\alpha}}_{m}[f]

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{xf^{\prime}(x)+(1+{\alpha})f(x)}{L^{{\alpha}}_{m}(-x)},

and
L_{m}^{\alpha}
is the classical Laguerre polynomial of degree
m
. The rational factorization in Eq. (
3.2
) is used to defined the differential expression
\mathcal{L}^{I,{\alpha}}_{m}
for the Type I Laguerre XOP operator

(3.5)

\mathcal{L}^{I,{\alpha}}_{m}=-(A^{I,{\alpha}-1}_{m}\circ B^{I,{\alpha}-1}_{m}+{\alpha}+m).

In this case,
\mathcal{L}^{I,{\alpha}}_{m}[y]=\lambda y
contains no polynomial solutions of degrees
0,1,\ldots,m-1
. The weight function associated with the XOP family is

(3.6)

\mathcal{W}_{m}^{I,{\alpha}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{x^{{\alpha}}e^{-x}}{\left[L_{m}^{{\alpha}-1}(-x)\right]^{2}},

and the associated maximal domain in the Hilbert space
L^{2}\left[(0,\infty),\mathcal{W}^{I,{\alpha}}_{m}\right]
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm max}}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\big\{f\mathchar 58\relax(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{C}\big|f,f^{\prime}

\displaystyle\in AC_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm loc}}(0,\infty);f,\mathcal{L}^{I,{\alpha}}_{m}[f]\in L^{2}\left[(0,\infty),W^{I,{\alpha}}_{m}\right]\big\}.

For
f,g\in\mathcal{D}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm max}}}
and
x\in(0,\infty)
, the sesquilinear form is given by

\displaystyle[f,g]_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathcal{L}}}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{x^{{\alpha}+1}e^{-x}}{[L_{m}^{{\alpha}-1}(-x)]^{2}}\left[f(x)g^{\prime}(x)-f^{\prime}(x)g(x)\right].

Frobenius analysis shows the endpoint
x=0
is limit-circle for
\mathcal{L}^{I,{\alpha}}_{m}
when
{\alpha}\in(0,1)
; and the endpoint
x=\infty
is limit-point for
{\alpha}>0
. Observe that the functions

\displaystyle\widetilde{y}_{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=-\frac{[L_{m}^{{\alpha}-1}(0)]^{2}}{{\alpha}}\hskip 5.0pt\text{ and }\hskip 5.0pt\widetilde{y}_{2}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=x^{-{\alpha}},

are both in
L^{2}\left[(0,\infty),\mathcal{W}^{I,{\alpha}}_{m}\right]
and are particular solutions to
\mathcal{L}^{I,{\alpha}}_{m}f=0
. Together they define maps using the quasi-derivatives:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{0}f

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=f^{[0]}(0)=[f,\widetilde{y}_{2}]_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathcal{L}}}(0)=\lim_{x\to 0^{+}}-\frac{{\alpha}f(x)+xf^{\prime}(x)}{[L_{m}^{{\alpha}-1}(0)]^{2}}

and

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1}f

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=f^{[1]}(0)=[f,\widetilde{y}_{1}]_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathcal{L}}}(0)=\lim_{x\to 0^{+}}\frac{x^{{\alpha}+1}}{{\alpha}}f^{\prime}(x).

Using results of Subsection
2.1
, it is easy to show that
\{\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1},\mathbb{C}\}
is a boundary triple for
\mathcal{D}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm max}}}
. Note that the choice of
\widetilde{y}_{1}
immediately yields
[{\varphi},x^{-{\alpha}}]_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathcal{L}}}(0)=1
. The boundary triple naturally defines two self-adjoint extensions
\mathcal{L}_{0}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
(suppressing the dependence on
m
and
{\alpha}
) that act via
\mathcal{L}_{m}^{I,{\alpha}}
on the domains

(3.7)

\displaystyle\dom(\mathcal{L}_{0})

\displaystyle=\left\{f\in\Delta_{m}^{I,{\alpha}}~\mathchar 58\relax~\Gamma_{1}(f)=0\right\},\text{ and}

\displaystyle\dom(\mathcal{L}_{\infty})

\displaystyle=\left\{f\in\Delta_{m}^{I,{\alpha}}~\mathchar 58\relax~\Gamma_{0}(f)=0\right\},

respectively.

The two linearly independent solutions of the Laguerre differential equation
\ell^{{\alpha}-1}
are given by confluent hypergeometric functions:

(3.8)

\displaystyle M(-{\lambda},{\alpha},x)=\,_{1}F_{1}(-{\lambda},{\alpha},x)\hskip 20.00003pt(\text{for }{\alpha}+1\notin-\mathbb{N}_{0}),

(3.9)

\displaystyle x^{-{\alpha}+1}M(1-{\lambda}-{\alpha},2-{\alpha},x)\hskip 20.00003pt(\text{for }{\alpha}+1\notin\mathbb{N}>1).

As a consequence of Lemma
2.2
, the Type I Laguerre XOP expression is therefore intertwined with the classical Laguerre expression
\ell^{{\alpha}-1}
. Therefore a fundamental system for the equation
(\mathcal{L}^{I,{\alpha}}_{m}-{\lambda})f=0
can be found by plugging the solutions of Eqs. (
3.8
) and (
3.9
) into the Darboux transformation
-A^{I,{\alpha}-1}
of Eq. (
3.3
). Thus

\displaystyle\Omega_{1,m}^{{\alpha}}(x,{\lambda})

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=-A^{I,{\alpha}-1}[M(-{\lambda},{\alpha},x)]

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(m+{\alpha})}{\Gamma(m+1)\Gamma({\alpha})}\Big[\frac{{\alpha}+m}{{\alpha}}M(-m,{\alpha}+1,-x)M(-{\lambda},{\alpha},x)

\displaystyle\hskip 50.00008pt+\frac{{\lambda}}{{\alpha}}M(-m,{\alpha},-x)M(-{\lambda}+1,{\alpha}+1,x)\Big],

and

\displaystyle\Omega_{2,m}^{{\alpha}}(x,{\lambda})

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=-A^{I,{\alpha}-1}[x^{-{\alpha}+1}M(1-{\lambda}-{\alpha},2-{\alpha},x)]

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(m+{\alpha}+1)}{\Gamma(m+1)\Gamma({\alpha}+1)}x^{-{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\hskip 30.00005pt\times\Bigg[\frac{{\alpha}(1-{\alpha})}{m+{\alpha}}M(-m,{\alpha},-x)M(1-{\lambda}-{\alpha},1-{\alpha},x)

\displaystyle\hskip 60.00009pt+xM(-m,{\alpha}+1,-x)M(1-{\lambda}-{\alpha},2-{\alpha},x)\Bigg]

are two linearly independent solutions of the XOP expression
(\mathcal{L}^{I,{\alpha}}_{m}-{\lambda})f=0
. Furthermore, a straightforward calculation shows

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\left(\Omega_{1,m}^{{\alpha}}\right)^{[0]}(0,{\lambda})&\left(\Omega_{2,m}^{{\alpha}}\right)^{[0]}(0,{\lambda})\\
\left(\Omega_{1,m}^{{\alpha}}\right)^{[1]}(0,{\lambda})&\left(\Omega_{2,m}^{{\alpha}}\right)^{[1]}(0,{\lambda})\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1/C^{{\alpha}}_{m}&0\\
0&1/D^{{\alpha}}_{m}\end{array}\right),

where

\displaystyle C^{{\alpha}}_{m}({\lambda})

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=-\frac{\Gamma(m+{\alpha})}{({\lambda}+m+{\alpha})\Gamma({\alpha})\Gamma(m+1)}\hskip 5.0pt\text{ and }\hskip 5.0ptD^{{\alpha}}_{m}({\lambda})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=-\frac{\Gamma(m+1)\Gamma({\alpha})}{(1-{\alpha})\Gamma(m+{\alpha})}.

The fundamental system composed of
u_{1}(x,{\lambda})
and
u_{2}(x,{\lambda})
must satisfy both
(\mathcal{L}^{I,{\alpha}}_{m}-{\lambda})f=0
and the initial conditions

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}u_{1}^{[0]}(0,{\lambda})&u_{2}^{[0]}(0,{\lambda})\\
u_{1}^{[1]}(0,{\lambda})&u_{2}^{[1]}(0,{\lambda})\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&1\end{array}\right).

If we suppress the dependence on
{\alpha}
and
m
, this can be accomplished by setting
u_{1}(x,{\lambda})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=C^{{\alpha}}_{m}({\lambda})\Omega_{1,m}^{{\alpha}}(x,{\lambda})
and
u_{2}(x,{\lambda})=D^{{\alpha}}_{m}({\lambda})\Omega_{2,m}^{{\alpha}}(x,{\lambda})
.

It remains only to compute the explicit Weyl
m
-function and extract spectral information for each self-adjoint extension. A general deficiency element satisfying
(\ell^{{\alpha}-1}-{\lambda})f=0
and denoted by
\chi(x,{\lambda})
may be written via the Tricomi confluent hypergeometric function
[
29
, Eq. (13.2.42)]
as

\displaystyle U(-{\lambda},{\alpha},x)=\frac{\Gamma(1-{\alpha})}{\Gamma(1-{\alpha}-{\lambda})}M(-{\lambda},{\alpha},x)+\frac{\Gamma({\alpha}-1)}{\Gamma(-{\lambda})}x^{-{\alpha}+1}M(1-{\lambda}-{\alpha},2-{\alpha},x).

The intertwining property in Lemma
2.2
implies that a general deficiency element
\widetilde{\chi}
of
(\mathcal{L}^{I,{\alpha}}_{m}-{\lambda})f=0
can be written as

\displaystyle\widetilde{\chi}(z,{\lambda})

\displaystyle=-A^{I,{\alpha}-1}[U(-{\lambda},{\alpha},x)]

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(1-{\alpha})}{\Gamma(1-{\alpha}-{\lambda})}\left(-A^{I,{\alpha}-1}[M(-{\lambda},{\alpha},x)]\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 80.00012pt+\frac{\Gamma({\alpha}-1)}{\Gamma(-{\lambda})}\left(-A^{I,{\alpha}-1}[x^{-{\alpha}+1}M(1-{\lambda}-{\alpha},2-{\alpha},x)]\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(1-{\alpha})}{\Gamma(1-{\alpha}-{\lambda})}\Omega_{1,m}^{{\alpha}}(x,{\lambda})+\frac{\Gamma({\alpha}-1)}{\Gamma(-{\lambda})}\Omega_{2,m}^{{\alpha}}(x,{\lambda})

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(1-{\alpha})}{C^{{\alpha}}_{m}({\lambda})\Gamma(1-{\alpha}-{\lambda})}u_{1}(x,{\lambda})+\frac{\Gamma({\alpha}-1)}{D^{{\alpha}}_{m}\Gamma(-{\lambda})}u_{2}(x,{\lambda}).

Hence, the initial conditions mean

\displaystyle\chi_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathcal{L}}}^{[0]}(0,{\lambda})=\frac{\Gamma(1-{\alpha})}{C^{{\alpha}}_{m}({\lambda})\Gamma(1-{\alpha}-{\lambda})}\hskip 36.135pt\text{ and }\hskip 36.135pt\chi_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathcal{L}}}^{[1]}(0,{\lambda})=\frac{\Gamma({\alpha}-1)}{D^{{\alpha}}_{m}\Gamma(-{\lambda})}.

Proposition
2.8
implies that if
{\lambda}\in\rho(\mathcal{L}_{\infty})
the Weyl
m
-function for the extension
L_{\infty}
is given by

\displaystyle M_{\infty}({\lambda})

\displaystyle=\frac{\chi_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathcal{L}}}^{[1]}(0,{\lambda})}{\chi_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathcal{L}}}^{[0]}(0,{\lambda})}=\left(\frac{\Gamma(m+{\alpha})}{\Gamma(m+1)\Gamma({\alpha})}\right)^{2}\frac{(1-{\alpha})\Gamma({\alpha}-1)\Gamma(1-{\alpha}-{\lambda})}{({\lambda}+m+{\alpha})\Gamma(-{\lambda})\Gamma(1-{\alpha})}

\displaystyle=-\left[L_{m}^{{\alpha}-1}(0)\right]^{2}\frac{\Gamma({\alpha})\Gamma(1-{\alpha}-{\lambda})}{({\lambda}+m+{\alpha})\Gamma(-{\lambda})\Gamma(1-{\alpha})}.

The spectrum of the self-adjoint operator
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
are those points which are poles of
M_{\infty}({\lambda})
. Recall that the Gamma function has no zeros, but does have simple poles at zero and the negative integers. Therefore,
M_{\infty}
has poles at
\sigma(\mathcal{L}_{\infty})=(-m-{\alpha})\cup\left\{n+1-{\alpha}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
and
\sigma(\mathcal{L}_{\infty})
is the spectrum of the self-adjoint operator
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
. The corresponding self-adjoint extension of the COP expression has the same spectrum with the point
-m-{\alpha}
replaced by
-{\alpha}
.

Likewise, the
m
-function of
\mathcal{L}_{0}
, denoted
M_{0}
, can be found by computing
-1/M_{\infty}({\lambda})
for
{\lambda}\in\rho(\mathcal{L}_{\infty})\cup\rho(\mathcal{L}_{0})
. This extension contains the Type I Laguerre XOP polynomials and is easily seen to have eigenvalues
{\lambda}=\left\{n\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
.

The
m
-function for any self-adjoint extension can also be written down in a standard way. Let
\tau\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\tau}
refer to the self-adjoint operator acting via
\mathcal{L}^{I,{\alpha}}_{m}
on

\displaystyle\dom(\mathcal{L}_{\tau})

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\left\{f\in\mathcal{D}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm max}}}\big|f\in\ker\left(\Gamma_{1}-\tau\Gamma_{0}\right)\right\}=\left\{f\in\mathcal{D}_{{}_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm max}}}\big|f^{[1]}(0)=\tau f^{[0]}(0)\right\}.

The corresponding Weyl
m
-function for
{\lambda}\in\rho(\mathcal{L}_{\infty})\cup\rho(\mathcal{L}_{\tau})
, as in Eq. (
2.19
), is

\displaystyle M_{\tau}({\lambda})

\displaystyle=\frac{1+\tau M_{\infty}({\lambda})}{\tau-M_{\infty}({\lambda})}

\displaystyle=\frac{({\lambda}+m+{\alpha})\Gamma(-{\lambda})\Gamma(1-{\alpha})-\tau\left[L_{m}^{{\alpha}-1}(0)\right]^{2}\Gamma({\alpha})\Gamma(1-{\alpha}-{\lambda})}{\tau({\lambda}+m+{\alpha})\Gamma(-{\lambda})\Gamma(1-{\alpha})+\left[L_{m}^{{\alpha}-1}(0)\right]^{2}\Gamma({\alpha})\Gamma(1-{\alpha}-{\lambda})}.

Hence, if
\tau\notin\{0\}\cup\{\infty\}
,
\mathcal{L}_{\tau}
will have eigenvalues precisely when

\displaystyle\tau({\lambda}+m+{\alpha})\Gamma(-{\lambda})\Gamma(1-{\alpha})=-\left[L_{m}^{{\alpha}-1}(0)\right]^{2}\Gamma({\alpha})\Gamma(1-{\alpha}-{\lambda}).

Solving for
\tau
shows that eigenvalues of
\mathcal{L}_{\tau}
are just the level curves where
M_{\infty}({\lambda})=\tau
. Our example is now complete.

There are two main obstacles to generalizing the example that the reader should keep in mind. The first is the manipulation of the general solutions to the XOP expression so that they are in a practical format. This step is not necessary in the above example as all calculations are explicit. The second is the determination of the normalizations for initial conditions; this is found by a simple calculation in the example and stated in Eq. (
3
). In the general case, these obstacles are the main subject of Sections
5
and
6
, respectively.

4.
Exceptional Laguerre Operators

We begin by discussing the behavior of solutions near the singular endpoints
x=0
and
x=\infty
for the XOP Laguerre expression found in Eq. (
2.9
).

Theorem 4.1
.

For
{\alpha}>0
, let
\mathcal{L}^{\alpha}
be the XOP differential expression on the interval
(0,\infty)
.

(a.)

\mathcal{L}^{\alpha}
is in the limit-circle case at
x=0
for
-1<{\alpha}<1
and is in the limit-point case at
x=0
when
\alpha\geq 1
.

(b.)

\mathcal{L}^{\alpha}
is in the limit-point case at
x=\infty
for any
{\alpha}>0
.

Proof.

The point
x=0
is, in the sense of Frobenius, a regular singular endpoint of the XOP expression
(\mathcal{L}^{\alpha}-\lambda)f=0
for any value
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
. Using the classical Laguerre expression Eq. (
3.1
) to derive
\mathcal{L}^{\alpha}
associated with Eq. (
2.9
) yields an indicial equation of

r(r+{\alpha})=0.

This indicial equation holds for any admissible choice of
\phi
and
b
. Meanwhile, the point
x=\infty
is an irregular singular point and a reduction of order method must be used to find two linearly independent solutions. The result may be shown using the techniques within the proof of
[
23
, Theorem 3.3]
.
∎

Since
\mathcal{L}^{\alpha}
is in the limit-circle case at
x=0
for
-1<{\alpha}<1
, Glazman–Krein–Naimark theory requires that one appropriate boundary condition be imposed in order to generate a self-adjoint extension of the minimal operator. Thus if
-1<{\alpha}<1
, the deficiency index of
\mathcal{L}
is
(1,1)
. Meanwhile if
{\alpha}\geq 1
, the deficiency index is
(0,0)
.

Assumption 4.2
.

The XOP Laguerre expression
\mathcal{L}^{\alpha}
is assumed to have parameter
{\alpha}>-1
.

In order to determine the spectrum of an XOP operator
\mathcal{L}
, it is first necessary to determine the general solutions to the eigenvalue problem
(\mathcal{L}-{\lambda})f=0
. These solutions can be found through the intertwining property of Lemma
2.2
. By
[
12
, Section 3]
the solutions may written as the Wronskian of a sequence of seed functions along with a solution to the eigenvalue problem of the COP operator.

Seed functions are the quasi-rational eigenfunctions of the Laguerre COP differential expression in Eqs. (
2.4
)–(
2.7
) and will be indexed via

(4.1)

\displaystyle f_{j}(x)

\displaystyle=L_{n_{j}}^{{\alpha}}(x),

\displaystyle j=1,\dots,r_{1},

(4.2)

\displaystyle f_{r_{1}+j}(x)

\displaystyle=e^{x}L_{m_{j}}^{{\alpha}}(-x),

\displaystyle j=1,\dots,r_{2},

(4.3)

\displaystyle f_{r_{1}+r_{2}+j}(x)

\displaystyle=x^{-{\alpha}}L^{-{\alpha}}_{m_{j}^{\prime}}(x),

\displaystyle j=1,\dots,r_{3},

(4.4)

\displaystyle f_{r_{1}+r_{2}+r_{3}+j}(x)

\displaystyle=e^{x}x^{-{\alpha}}L^{-{\alpha}}_{n_{j}^{\prime}}(-x),

\displaystyle j=1,\dots,r_{4},

with
r_{1}+r_{2}+r_{3}+r_{4}=r
,
n_{1}>\dots>n_{r_{1}}\geq 0
,
m_{1}>\dots>m_{r_{2}}\geq 0
,
n_{1}^{\prime}>\dots>n_{r_{1}}^{\prime}\geq 0
and
m_{1}^{\prime}>\dots>m_{r_{4}}^{\prime}\geq 0
. The degrees of seed functions appearing in the Wronskian can be better described via two Maya diagrams, see Subsection
2.2
. Denote these Maya diagrams by

(4.5)

M_{1}=\left(n_{1}^{\prime},\dots,n_{r_{4}}^{\prime}|n_{1},\dots,n_{r_{1}}\right),

and

(4.6)

M_{2}=\left(m_{1}^{\prime},\dots,m_{r_{3}}^{\prime}|m_{1},\dots,m_{r_{2}}\right).

The two general solutions of the classical Laguerre differential expression
\ell^{{\alpha}}
given by Eq. (
3.1
) are denoted

(4.7)

h^{{\alpha}}(x,{\lambda})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=M(-{\lambda},{\alpha}+1,x)=\,_{1}F_{1}(-{\lambda},{\alpha}+1,x)\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for }{\alpha}+1\notin-\mathbb{N}_{0},

and

(4.8)

\widetilde{h}^{{\alpha}}(x,{\lambda})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=x^{-{\alpha}}M(-{\lambda}-{\alpha},1-{\alpha},x)\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for }{\alpha}+1\notin\mathbb{N}>1.

A generalized Laguerre polynomial,
\Omega_{{}_{\scriptstyle M_{1},M_{2}}}^{{\alpha}}
, is a Wronskian of seed functions with the same parameter
{\alpha}
and distinct degrees. A prefactor is required to make it a polynomial. Let
\Omega_{{}_{\scriptstyle M_{1},M_{2}}}^{{\alpha}}
be defined by

(4.9)

\Omega_{{}_{\scriptstyle M_{1},M_{2}}}^{{\alpha}}(x)=e^{-(r_{2}+r_{4})x}x^{({\alpha}+r_{1}+r_{2})(r_{3}+r_{4})}\cdot\text{Wr}\left[f_{1},\dots,f_{r}\right],

where
f_{1},\dots,f_{r}
are as in Eqs. (
4.1
)-(
4.4
). If both Maya diagrams are trivial or the associated partitions are empty, the generalized Laguerre polynomial is a constant function.

Not every set of seed functions will produce a valid XOP expression. The following notion will determine admissibility; ensuring that the generalized Laguerre polynomial defines a
\phi
as in Subsection
2
. The Maya diagrams
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
of Eqs. (
4.5
) and (
4.6
) can be presented as partitions, denoted respectively by
\mu
and
\nu
in canonical position or
\mu^{\prime}
and
\nu^{\prime}
in conjugate canonical position. The lengths of these partitions, denoted
r(\mu)
and
r(\nu)
or
r(\mu^{\prime})
and
r(\nu^{\prime})
, are determined by using the values
t_{1}
,
t_{2}
and
t_{1}^{\prime}
,
t_{2}^{\prime}
that needed to shift
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
into their canonical and conjugate canonical positions. In particular,

(4.10)

\displaystyle r(\mu)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}r_{1}+n_{1}^{\prime}+1-r_{4}&t_{1}>0\\
r_{1}&t_{1}=0\\

and

(4.11)

\displaystyle r(\nu)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}r_{2}+m_{1}^{\prime}+1-r_{3}&t_{2}>0\\
r_{2}&t_{2}=0\\
r_{2}-\min\left\{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\mathchar 58\relax k\notin\{m_{i}\}_{i=1}^{r_{2}}\right\}&t_{2}<0\end{cases}.

The lengths of the conjugate partitions,

and

In canonical position, the Maya diagram can thus be relabeled and written as a partition defined by

(4.12)

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=\left(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{r(\mu)}\right),\hskip 20.00003pt\mu_{j}=n_{j}-r(\mu)+j,\hskip 20.00003pt

\displaystyle j=1,\dots,r(\mu),

and

\displaystyle\nu

\displaystyle=\left(\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{r(\nu)}\right),\hskip 20.00003pt\nu_{j}=m_{j}-r(\nu)+j,\hskip 20.00003pt

\displaystyle j=1,\dots,r(\nu).

where
r(\mu)+r(\nu)=r
.
Conjugate partitions are relabeled so that

and defined analogously. Partitions also define Young diagrams, where the number of boxes in each row corresponds to the entry of the partition. Conjugate partitions are then obtained by reflection over the main diagonal of the corresponding Young diagram.

Definition 4.3

In order for a Maya diagram to represent a valid Laugerre XOP expression, we make the following assumption:

Remark 4.5

It is clear that adding a column with a solution

or

to the Wronskian changes the expression from a polynomial to an infinite series. However, consider the case
{\lambda}=n
in Eq. (

Under our standard assumptions, these Laguerre XOPs form a complete set of orthogonal polynomials on the positive real line
[

,

]

Lemma 4.6

Suppose
{\alpha}+r>-1
and

is an even partition. Then the polynomials
L^{{\alpha}}_{\mu,\nu,n}
for

are orthogonal on

with respect to the positive weight function

\displaystyle W^{{\alpha}}_{\mu,\nu}(x)=\frac{x^{{\alpha}+r}e^{-x}}{\left[\Omega_{\mu,\nu}^{{\alpha}}(x)\right]^{2}},\hskip 20.00003ptx>0.

That is, if

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}L^{{\alpha}}_{\mu,\nu,n}(x)L^{{\alpha}}_{\mu,\nu,m}(x)W^{{\alpha}}_{\mu,\nu}(x)dx=0.

Moreover, they form a complete orthogonal set in

Note that the assumptions of Lemma

[

]
.

Also, the solution of the second kind defined by Eq. (

Finally, the construction of a general exceptional Laguerre expression requires beginning with the classical Laguerre expression, which has a parameter

9.
Conclusions

Exceptional Laguerre-type symmetric expressions
\mathcal{L}
, which have XOPs as eigenfunctions for a self-adjoint extension, may be derived from the classical Laguerre symmetric operator with parameter
{\alpha}
by applying Darboux transforms. These are written as Wronskians of seed functions that meet Assumption
4.4
and the seed functions are described via the Maya Diagrams
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
or, in special situations, as partitions
\mu
,
\nu
,
\mu^{\prime}
or
\nu^{\prime}
.

The expression
\mathcal{L}
acts on functions in the Hilbert space
L^{2}[(0,\infty),\mathcal{W}(x)]
that satisfy standard differentiability criteria. The weight function

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(x)=\frac{x^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}e^{-x}}{\left[\Omega^{{\alpha}^{\prime}}_{\mu,\nu}(x)\right]^{2}}\quad\text{ for }x>0,

where
{\alpha}^{\prime}={\alpha}-t_{1}-t_{2}
,
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}={\alpha}^{\prime}+r
, and
\Omega^{{\alpha}}_{\mu,\nu}(x)
is defined by Eq. (
4.9
). The expression
\mathcal{L}
is limit-circle at
x=0
and limit-point at
x=\infty
when
{\alpha}
is chosen so that
-1<{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}<1
.

Laguerre-type XOPs of order
n\in\mathbb{N}_{\mu,\nu}
are written as
L^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}_{\mu,\nu,n}
and the sequence forms a complete orthogonal set in
L^{2}[(0,\infty),\mathcal{W}]
by Lemma
4.6
, see Remark
4.5
for more details. These polynomials all belong to a family of eigenfunctions given by
\Omega_{{}_{\scriptstyle M_{1},M_{2}}}[h^{\alpha}(x,{\lambda})]
and are in a self-adjoint extension of
\mathcal{L}
denoted
\mathcal{L}_{0}
.

Eigenvalues of self-adjoint extensions of
\mathcal{L}
, denoted
\mathcal{L}_{\tau}
in general, can be extracted from an explicit Weyl
m
-function given by Eq. (
7.10
) generated by a boundary triple. The resulting
m
-function can be compared to the
m
-function of self-adjoint extensions of the classical Laguerre symmetric operator. Differences stem from two important processes: shifting Maya diagrams into canonical/conjugate canonical position, and evaluating Wronskians with these shifted diagrams at
x=0
. Other spectral information, such as the strength of the point masses in the spectral measure, can also be obtained from the
m
-function, see e.g.
[
5
]
.

The examples presented in Sections
3
and
8
provide insight into how eigenvalues are changed by different types of Maya diagrams. Inverse spectral theory, where a set of eigenvalues is given and a corresponding set of seed functions within the Wronskian is determined (thereby fixing the differential equation and self-adjoint extension), however, remains unknown in general and warrants further investigation.
