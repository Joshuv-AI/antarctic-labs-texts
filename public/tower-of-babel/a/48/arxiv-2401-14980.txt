Small-amplitude synchronisation in driven Potts models
Jan Meibohm, Massimiliano Esposito

Abstract
We study driven q q -state Potts models with thermodynamically consistent dynamics and global coupling. For a wide range of parameters, these models exhibit a dynamical phase transition from decoherent oscillations into a synchronised phase. Starting from a general microscopic dynamics for individual oscillators, we derive the normal form of the high-dimensional Hopf-Bifurcation that underlies the phase transition. The normal-form equations are exact in the thermodynamic limit and close to the bifurcation. Exploiting the symmetry of the model, we solve these equations and thus uncover the intricate stable synchronisation patterns of driven Potts models, characterised by a rich phase diagram. Making use of thermodynamic consistency, we show that synchronisation reduces dissipation in such a way that the most stable synchronised states dissipate the least entropy. Close to the phase transition, our findings condense into a linear dissipation-stability relation that connects entropy production with phase-space contraction, a stability measure. At finite system size, our findings suggest a minimum-dissipation principle for driven Potts models that holds arbitrarily far from equilibrium.



I
Introduction

Synchronisation is a striking collective phenomenon that occurs in systems of coupled oscillators
Pikovsky et al. 2001
;
Boccaletti et al. 2002
;
Acebrón et al. 2005
;
Arenas et al. 2008
. Due to interactions, a system of oscillators with different oscillation frequencies may phase-lock to a common frequency and oscillate coherently. Synchronisation has been observed across a range of disciplines in science
Rosenblum and Pikovsky 2003
such as biology
May 1972
;
Peskin 1975
;
Michaels et al. 1987
;
Jewett and Kronauer 1998
, neuroscience
Fell and Axmacher 2011
, chemistry
Taylor et al. 2009
, and physics
Bello et al. 2019
;
Chalkiadakis and Hizanidis 2022
.

In large systems of coupled oscillators, the emergence of synchrony is an instance of a non-equilibrium phase transition, characterised by the spontaneous occurrence of spatiotemporal order – in the form of phase locking – through the breaking of time-translational symmetry. To this day, non-equilibrium phase transitions lack a comprehensive theoretical understanding comparable to that achieved for equilibrium phase transitions in the past century.

In the study of equilibrium phase transitions, exactly solvable models have played an important role
Baxter 1982
. A crucial archetype of model whose equilibrium properties are mathematically accessible, is the
q
-state Potts model
Potts 1952
;
Wu 1982
. The model consists of
N
identical units of
q
different states, labelled
0,\ldots,q-1
, immersed in a heat bath at inverse temperature
\beta=1/(k_{\text{B}}T)
. One can think of each unit as a two dimensional vector (or spin) that points in one of
q
equally spaced directions, see Fig.
1
(a). In the version of the model we consider here, Potts spins interact via a global potential: The energy is reduced by
\mathscr{J}/N
whenever a pair of spins is in the same state, see Fig.
1
(b).

In non-equilibrium physics, exactly solvable models have played a crucial role, too. For synchronisation, in particular, the perhaps most influential solvable model is the Kuramoto model
Kuramoto 1975
;
Strogatz 2000
;
Acebrón et al. 2005
, which consists of
N\to\infty
globally coupled oscillators with continuous phases. The natural frequency of each oscillator is fixed but random, drawn from a given probability distribution. In the Kuramoto model, the competition between decoherence, introduced by the spread in the natural frequencies, and order, introduced by an aligning interaction potential, leads to a dynamical phase transition into a synchronised state at a critical coupling strength
Kuramoto 1975
;
Strogatz 2000
;
Acebrón et al. 2005
.

In this paper, we analyse driven
q
-state Potts models
Wood et al. 2006
;
Herpich et al. 2018
;
Herpich and Esposito 2019
, non-equilibrium versions of the classic Potts model. In driven Potts models, the spins are amended with thermodynamically-consistent stochastic dynamics that models the interaction between the spins and the heat bath. In addition, each Potts spin is driven by a non-conservative force
f
. To model this force, the dynamics is chosen to favor one direction of rotation over the other. With such a driving, the Potts spins become “Potts oscillators” that, on average, rotate in the direction of the driving, see red arrows in Fig.

.

The explicit choice of stochastic dynamics is part of any driven Potts model and it impacts its behaviour. More generally, the dependence on dynamics is characteristic for non-equilibrium physics. At equilibrium, by contrast, the Gibbs state is universal, whenever thermodynamic consistency, i.e., detailed balance, is preserved. Away from equilibrium, no such universality exists, and even thermodynamically consistent models may be sensitive to dynamics.

For a wide range of dynamics, we show that a large number
N\to\infty
of globally coupled Potts oscillators undergo a dynamical phase transition into a synchronised phase, in analogy with the Kuramoto model. The mechanism, however, is different here: Driven Potts models are intrinsically stochastic because they exchange energy with the heat bath. The synchronisation transition occurs due to a competition between decoherence from
thermal noise
and order due to the aligning interaction between oscillators.

As we will explain in detail, large systems of Potts oscillators are described by the occupation probability
\bm{p}(t)=(p_{0},\ldots,p_{q-1})^{\sf T}
of the states
0,\ldots,q-1
at time
t
. Decoherent oscillations correspond to uniform occupation,
\bm{p}(t)=\bm{p}^{*}\equiv(\frac{1}{q},\ldots,\frac{1}{q})^{\sf T}
. Figure
2
(a) shows the result of numerical simulations of a large system of initially decoherent, driven Potts oscillators with given dynamics. Shown are the deviations
\bm{x}(t)=\bm{p}(t)-\bm{p}^{*}
from decoherence for
q=9
. The initially decoherent system [
\bm{x}(t=0)=0
] develops a synchronous pattern characterised by four travelling maxima, highlighted by red arrows.

Potts oscillators have an intrinsic discrete rotational symmetry, so the periodic patterns observed in Fig.
2
(a) are described by discrete complex Fourier modes. As we explain below, there are four dynamical Fourier modes for
q=9
, with wave numbers
k=1,\ldots,4
, that we denote by
z_{1},\ldots,z_{4}
. The evolution of these modes in the complex plane is shown in Figs.
2
(b)-(m).

Initially all modes are inactive [Figs.
2
(b)–(e)], indicating the initial decoherent state. As time advances, the amplitudes of the Fourier modes with largest
k
grow [Figs.
2
(f)–(i)], until eventually [Figs.
2

z_{4}
is active and rotates counter-clockwise in the complex plane (green arrow), reflecting the emergence of the synchronisation pattern shown in Fig.
2
(a). The amplitudes of the remaining Fourier modes are essentially zero, and they rotate in different directions (green arrows).

Thermodynamic consistency of the model enables us to extract faithful thermodynamic observables. The average rate of dissipated work, shown in Fig.
2
(n), is initially at the large value
\langle\dot{\sigma}\rangle_{0}
, associated with the average dissipation rate of a single, uncoupled oscillator. During relaxation into the synchronised state, the rate of dissipated work decreases as function of time and relaxes to a smaller value
\langle\dot{\sigma}\rangle_{\text{f}}
, associated with the average dissipation rate per oscillator in the synchronised phase.

The phase-space contraction rate, shown in Fig.
2
(o), is a measure of the momentary stability of a given state. Its initial value
\mathcal{L}_{0}
is negative, reflecting that decoherent oscillations are unstable. But it increases as function of time, changes sign, and saturates at a positive value
\mathcal{L}_{\text{f}}>0
, indicating a stable synchronised final state. Comparing Figs.
2

We explain and extend these observations by analysing analytically the equations of motion that describe the macroscopic dynamics of large systems of coupled Potts oscillators in the thermodynamic limit. So far, no analytical treatment of synchronisation in driven Potts models has been reported. The existing results are numerical, restricted to small values of
q
, and to specific choices of the stochastic dynamics
Herpich et al. 2018
;
Herpich and Esposito 2019
. Here we solve the model exactly close to the synchronisation transition for arbitrary dynamics and arbitrary
q
. Apart from explaining the numerical simulations shown in Fig.
2
, our results reveal how the behaviour of the model depends on the choice of the dynamics and on the value of
q
.

From a linear stability analysis, we find that different dynamics may either stabilise or destabilise the decoherent phase compared to the equilibrium case. When decoherence is unstable, the model transitions into a synchronised state with coherent small-amplitude oscillations as shown in Fig.
2
. We show analytically how different dynamics give rise to a variety of stable synchronisation patterns that constitute a rich phase diagram of possible synchronised states. All stable oscillations have in common that only a small number of Fourier modes is active, as observed for
q=9
in Fig.
2
(j)–(m), where only a single Fourier mode, here
z_{4}
, is active.

This reveals a profound difference in the way in which driven Potts models and the Kuramoto model synchronise: While “Kuramoto-like” synchronisation occurs in real space, “Potts-like” synchronisation occurs in Fourier space.

The stability of the synchronised states is closely linked to the thermodynamics of the model, as we discuss in detail in Ref.
Meibohm and Esposito 2024
that appears together with this paper. We find that synchronisation in driven Potts models reduces dissipation in such a way that the most stable synchronised states dissipate the least entropy. Close to the synchronisation transition, we derive a linear stability-dissipation relation between states of least entropy production and states of largest phase-space contraction. As we argue in detail, this finding suggests a minimum-dissipation principle
Prigogine 1955
for driven Potts models at finite
N
, that holds arbitrarily far from equilibrium and independently of the stochastic dynamics.

Equilibrium Potts models have been realised experimentally for
q=3,4
using, e.g., spins in adsorbed systems
Barbara et al. 1978
;
Roelofs et al. 1981
;
Schick 1981
and fluid mixtures
Das and Griffiths 1979
. Synchronisation of oscillating spins was achieved by applying an external torque through a spin-polarised current
Tsoi et al. 2000
;
Mancoff et al. 2005
;
Awad et al. 2017
. Idealised models have proven instrumental in predicting the outcomes of these experiments
Mukamel et al. 1976
;
Domany et al. 1977
;
Slonczewski 1996
;
Berger 1996
. Combinations of these existing experimental approaches thus offer interesting future perspectives for the experimental observation of the Potts-like synchronisation established here.

II
Model

In this first Section, we give a detailed description of driven Potts models. We start by introducing a single uncoupled Potts oscillator, followed by a discussion of
N
uncoupled oscillators. In the last step, we consider the complete model of
N
interacting Potts oscillators with thermodynamically consistent stochastic dynamics.

II.1
Single uncoupled oscillator

When the interaction strength
\mathscr{J}
is set to zero, one may consider each Potts oscillator individually. Each oscillator
s_{m}
with index
m=1,\ldots,N
can occupy one of
q
states

\displaystyle s_{m}\in\{0,\ldots,q-1\}\,.

(1)

Microscopic transitions within an oscillator are induced by thermal fluctuations of the surrounding heat bath. Transitions are allowed only between adjacent states, i.e. only for

\displaystyle s_{m}\to s_{m}\pm 1\,,

(2)

where
s_{m}+1
corresponds to a counter-clockwise transition and
s_{m}-1
to a clockwise transition. All states are understood modulo
q
to implement the periodicity of Potts spins.

Fluctuations of the heat bath are assumed to occur on much shorter time scales than the dynamics of the system. We therefore model transitions (
2
) by a Markovian stochastic dynamics with transition rates
k^{\pm}
, that obey local detailed balance
Seifert 2012
;
den Broeck and Esposito 2015
;
Peliti and Pigolotti 2021

\displaystyle\frac{k^{\pm}}{k^{\mp}}=\text{e}^{\pm\beta f}\,.

(3)

Here,
f
and
\beta
denote the non-conservative force and the inverse temperature of the heat bath, respectively, introduced in Sec.
I
. Positive driving
f>0
favours counter-clockwise over clockwise transitions, leading to a mean probability flux in counter-clockwise direction, the direction of the driving. Local detailed balance (
3
) ensures that the model is amenable to a consistent thermodynamic description in the framework of Stochastic Thermodynamics
Seifert 2012
;
den Broeck and Esposito 2015
;
Peliti and Pigolotti 2021
.

The probability
p_{n}
of finding a single, uncoupled oscillator
s_{m}
in state
n
obeys the Master equation
Van Kampen 2007
;
Gardiner 2009

\displaystyle\dot{p}_{n}=j(p_{n},p_{n-1})\big|_{\mathscr{J}=0}-j(p_{n+1},p_{n})\big|_{\mathscr{J}=0}\,,

(4)

where
j(p_{n+1},p_{n})\big|_{\mathscr{J}=0}
is the probability flux from state
n
to state
n+1
at vanishing coupling:

\displaystyle j(p_{n+1},p_{n})\big|_{\mathscr{J}=0}=k^{+}p_{n}-k^{-}p_{n+1}\,.

(5)

Due to discrete rotational symmetry, Eq. (
4
) is diagonalised by discrete Fourier transform. The Fourier modes

\displaystyle\hat{p}_{k}=\sum_{n=0}^{q-1}\text{e}^{\frac{i2\pi kn}{q}}p_{n}\,,

(6)

obey the transformed Master equation

\displaystyle\dot{\hat{p}}_{k}=\left(\mu_{k}|_{\mathscr{J}=0}+i\omega_{k}\big|_{\mathscr{J}=0}\right)\hat{p}_{k}\,,

(7)

where
\mu_{k}|_{\mathscr{J}=0}\leq 0
represents the damping at vanishing coupling and
\omega_{k}
is the angular frequency of the
k^{\text{th}}
Fourier mode,

\displaystyle\mu_{k}|_{\mathscr{J}=0}=

\displaystyle-2(k^{+}+k^{-})\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi k}{q}\right)\,,

(8a)

\displaystyle\omega_{k}|_{\mathscr{J}=0}=

\displaystyle(k^{+}-k^{-})\sin\left(\frac{2\pi k}{q}\right)\,.

(8b)

The coefficients
\mu_{k}|_{\mathscr{J}=0}
are negative for all
k
except
k=0
, as a consequence of the Perron-Frobenius theorem
Perron 1907
;
Frobenius 1912
. Vanishing
k=0
corresponds to the decoherent state

\displaystyle\bm{p}^{*}\equiv\left(\frac{1}{q},\dots,\frac{1}{q}\right)^{\sf T}\,,

(9)

introduced in Sec.
I
, for which the probabilities are homogeneously distributed among all states
0,\ldots,q-1
. This is the unique steady state for a single, uncoupled Potts oscillator.

As we discuss in Sec.
IV
below, interactions between oscillators may change the sign of
\mu_{k}
when
\beta\!\!\mathscr{J}
reaches a critical value, rendering the decoherent phase unstable. The angular frequencies
\omega_{k}
, by contrast, are only weakly affected by interactions once the decoherent phase is unstable. In analogy with the natural frequencies of individual oscillators in the Kuramoto model, we call
\omega_{k}
the “natural frequencies” of driven Potts models.

II.2
N
uncoupled oscillators

In the next step, we consider the behaviour of an ensemble of
N
uncoupled, identical oscillators. This system is invariant under arbitrary permutations of oscillators. It is therefore convenient to introduce the
q
-dimensional occupation vector
\bm{N}=(N_{0},\ldots,N_{q-1})^{\sf T}
associated with a given microstate
\bm{s}
. The components
N_{n}
of
\bm{N}
specify the number of oscillators in state
n=0,1,\ldots,q-1
and are subject to the constraint
\sum_{n=0}^{q-1}N_{n}=N
.

The occupation vector
\bm{N}
provides a coarse-grained but complete description of the microstate. However, since the coarse-graining map
\bm{s}\mapsto\bm{N}
is many-to-one, the states described by
\bm{N}
carry a (dimensionless) internal entropy
Herpich et al. 2018
;
Herpich and Esposito 2019
;
Herpich et al. 2020

\displaystyle S_{\text{int}}(\bm{N})=\log\Omega(\bm{N})\quad\text{with}\quad\Omega(\bm{N})=\frac{N!}{\prod_{n=0}^{q-1}N_{n}!}\,,

(10)

where
\Omega
is the microscopic multiplicity of the state
\bm{N}
. Here and in the following we express all entropy measures in units of
k_{\text{B}}
.

An allowed microscopic transition (
2
) for
s_{m}=n
leads to a change of the occupation vector

\displaystyle\bm{N}\to\bm{N}+\bm{\Delta}^{\pm}_{n}\quad\text{with}\quad(\bm{\Delta}^{\pm}_{n})_{m}=\delta_{mn\pm 1}-\delta_{mn}\,,

(11)

where
\delta_{mn}
denotes the Kronecker symbol. The inverse transitions of
\bm{\Delta}^{\pm}_{n}
are given by

\displaystyle\bm{\Delta}^{\mp}_{n\pm 1}=-\bm{\Delta}^{\pm}_{n}\,.

(12)

In the non-interacting model, microscopic transitions occur independently. Therefore, the rates
W^{\pm}_{n}(\bm{N})\big|_{\mathscr{J}=0}
for the transitions (
11
) are the sums of the rates of all microscopic transitions that enter (
11
), i.e.,

\displaystyle W^{\pm}_{n}(\bm{N})\big|_{\mathscr{J}=0}=N_{n}k^{\pm}\,.

(13)

Consequently, the transition rates (
13
) of the non-interacting model are products of the entropic factors
N_{n}
, that stem from the combined contributions of individual microscopic oscillators in state
n
, and the microscopic transition rates
k^{\pm}
.

The transition rates (
13
) on the level of the occupation vector
\bm{N}
, obey a macroscopic version of the microscopic local detailed balance condition (
3
):

\displaystyle\frac{W^{\pm}_{n}(\bm{N})}{W^{\mp}_{n\pm 1}(\bm{N}+{\bm{\Delta}}_{n}^{\pm})}\bigg|_{\mathscr{J}=0}\hskip-9.95845pt=\exp\left[S_{\text{int}}(\bm{N}+\bm{\Delta}^{\pm}_{n})-S_{\text{int}}(\bm{N})\pm\beta f\right],

(14)

that involves the change in internal entropy

\displaystyle S_{\text{int}}(\bm{N}+\bm{\Delta}^{\pm}_{n})-S_{\text{int}}(\bm{N})=\log\left(\frac{N_{n}}{N_{n\pm 1}+1}\right)\,,

(15)

along the transition.

Given the transition rates (
13
), the probability
P(\bm{N},t)
to find the system in state
\bm{N}
at time
t
obeys the Master equation

\displaystyle\dot{P}(\bm{N},t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{q-1}\left[J_{n}(\bm{N},\bm{N}-{\bm{\Delta}}_{n}^{+})\big|_{\mathscr{J}=0}-J_{n}(\bm{N}+{\bm{\Delta}}_{n}^{+},\bm{N})\big|_{\mathscr{J}=0}\right]\,,

(16)

where

\displaystyle J_{n}(\bm{N}^{\prime},\bm{N})\big|_{\mathscr{J}=0}=N^{\prime}_{n}k^{+}P(\bm{N}^{\prime},t)-N_{n+1}k^{-}P(\bm{N},t)\,,

(17)

denotes the probability flux from
\bm{N}
to
\bm{N}^{\prime}
along state
n
.

II.3
Complete model

In the final step, we now discuss the complete driven Potts model, including interactions. The oscillators interact with each other pairwise by a global, ferromagnetic interaction of strength
\mathscr{J}/N
, whenever two oscillators are in the same state, see Fig.
1
(b). In other words, every microscopic pair
(m,m^{\prime})
with
s_{m}=s_{m^{\prime}}
,
m\neq m^{\prime}
, reduces the internal energy
E
of the system by
\mathscr{J}/N
.

The global nature of the interaction conserves the invariance of the system under arbitrary permutations of the oscillators, so we can describe the complete model using the occupation vector
\bm{N}
introduced in Sec.
II.2
. A simple calculation shows that the total energy
E
is expressed in terms of
\bm{N}
as

\displaystyle E(\bm{N})=-\frac{\mathscr{J}}{2N}\left(\bm{N}\cdot\bm{N}-N\right)\,.

(18)

Analogous to the non-interacting rates (
13
), the transition rates
W^{\pm}_{n}(\bm{N})
for the transitions
\bm{N}\to\bm{N}+\bm{\Delta}^{\pm}_{n}
of the interacting model,

\displaystyle W^{\pm}_{n}(\bm{N})=N_{n}K^{\pm}(N_{n\pm 1}-N_{n})\,,

(19)

are products of
N_{n}
and the functions
K^{\pm}(x)
that contain the microscopic interactions between spins as well as the non-conservative force. We assume that the functions
K^{\pm}(x)
depend on the energy change due to the transition,

\displaystyle E(\bm{N}+\bm{\Delta}^{\pm}_{n})-E(\bm{N})

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mathscr{J}}{N}\left(N_{n\pm 1}-N_{n}+1\right)\,,

(20)

but that they are otherwise independent of
n
. In contrast to the extensive total energy (
18
), the energy change (
20
) is intensive in the thermodynamic limit
N\to\infty
. Therefore, the dependence of
K^{\pm}(x)
on the energy change (
20
) ensures a consistent thermodynamic limit for the transition rates. The argument of
K^{\pm}(x)
, i.e.,
x=N_{n\pm 1}-N_{n}
in Eq. (
19
), then follows from the
\bm{N}
dependence given in Eq. (
20
).

Since the occupation vector
\bm{N}
carries an internal entropy
S_{\text{int}}(\bm{N})
, the free energy
F
of the system
Herpich et al. 2018
;
Herpich and Esposito 2019
;
Herpich et al. 2020

\displaystyle F(\bm{N})=E(\bm{N})-\beta^{-1}S_{\text{int}}(\bm{N})\,,

(21)

enters the local detailed balance condition for the transition rates of the interacting model, i.e.,

\displaystyle\frac{W^{\pm}_{n}(\bm{N})}{W^{\mp}_{n\pm 1}(\bm{N}+\bm{\Delta}^{\pm}_{n})}=\exp\left\{-\beta\left[F(\bm{N}+\bm{\Delta}^{\pm}_{n})-F(\bm{N})\mp f\right]\right\}\,.

(22)

The product form (
19
) of the transition rates further implies a local detailed balance condition for
K^{\pm}(x)
with respect to the energy change alone,

\displaystyle\frac{K^{\pm}(N_{n\pm 1}-N_{n})}{K^{\mp}(N_{n}-N_{n\pm 1})}=\exp\left\{-\beta\left[E(\bm{N}+\bm{\Delta}^{\pm}_{n})-E(\bm{N})\mp f\right]\right\}\,.

(23)

In the non-interacting limit
\mathscr{J}\to 0
, the rates
K^{\pm}(x)
reduce to the
\bm{N}
-independent
k^{\pm}
. Consequently, Eq. (
22
) reduces to (
14
) and Eq. (
23
) reduces to (
3
).

Finally, the Master equation for the probability
P(\bm{N},t)
of the complete model reads

\displaystyle\dot{P}(\bm{N},t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{q-1}\left[J_{n}(\bm{N},\bm{N}-{\bm{\Delta}}_{n}^{+})-J_{n}(\bm{N}+{\bm{\Delta}}_{n}^{+},\bm{N})\right]\,,

(24)

with probability fluxes for the transitions
\bm{N}\to\bm{N}^{\prime}
along
n
given by

\displaystyle J_{n}(\bm{N}^{\prime},\bm{N})=W^{+}_{n}(\bm{N})P(\bm{N},t)-W^{-}_{n+1}(\bm{N}^{\prime})P(\bm{N}^{\prime},t)\,.

(25)

II.4
Choice of dynamics

The local detailed balance conditions (
22
) and (
23
) fix the antisymmetric part of the logarithms of
K^{\pm}(x)
, but leave them otherwise arbitrary. Ideally, one would have a microscopic model for the (e.g. quantum mechanical) interaction between the heat bath and the Potts spins, that would allow one to derive
K^{\pm}(x)
explicitly. However, such a model is not known in general, which makes the freedom in choosing
K^{\pm}(x)
an integral part of Potts models. The best one can do is to regard the undetermined (symmetric) part of the logarithm of
K^{\pm}(x)
as a free parameter of the model and to explore the parameter space that this choice offers.

While it is possible to make general statements about the non-equilibrium properties of driven Potts models close to the synchronisation transition, as we will show, numerical simulations require specific choices of the transition rates. A typical choice uses mixed Arrhenius rates with

\displaystyle K_{\text{Arr}}^{\pm}(x)=\tau^{-1}\text{e}^{\frac{\beta}{2}\left[\frac{\mathscr{J}}{N}(1\mp\xi)\left(x+1\right)\pm f\right]}\,,

(26)

depending on the parameter
-1\leq\xi\leq 1

1
1

1

We note that the used term “Arrhenius rate” refers to the exponential form of the transition rates in Eq. (
26
) and not to its dependence on the initial and final energies.
. The time scale
\tau
corresponds to the typical, microscopic transition time of a single Potts spin. For
\xi=0
,
K_{\text{Arr}}^{\pm}(x)
correspond to the classic Arrhenius rates, studied in Refs.
Herpich et al. 2018
;
Herpich and Esposito 2019
, and used in the numerical simulations presented in Fig.
2
.

A second common choice uses Glauber rates

\displaystyle K_{\text{Gla}}^{\pm}(x)=\frac{2}{\tau}\frac{\text{e}^{\pm\frac{\beta f}{2}}}{\text{e}^{-\frac{\mathscr{J}}{N}\left(x+1\right)}+1}\,.

(27)

Arrhenius rates (
26
) with
\xi=0
and Glauber rates (
27
) have convenient properties, that we discuss in more detail in Sec.
IV.2
. For the numerical simulations presented in Sec.
VI
, we therefore use either of these two. Notwithstanding this, our analytical results hold in general and thus for all dynamics.

II.5
Thermodynamic observables

Thermodynamic consistency of the model in the form of the local detailed balance conditions (
22
) and (
23
), constrains the dynamics and allows us to define genuine stochastic thermodynamic quantities at the mesoscopic level, that are consistent with the macroscopic thermodynamics of the system
Falasco and Esposito 2025
.

In particular, the system entropy
S_{\text{sys}}
is given by the sum of the internal entropy and Shannon entropy
2
2

2

The additive form of the
S_{\text{sys}}
is a direct consequence of the coarse graining
\bm{s}\mapsto\bm{N}
, see e.g. the discussion in Ref.
Seifert 2019
.
:

\displaystyle S_{\text{sys}}(\bm{N},t)=S_{\text{int}}(\bm{N})+S_{\text{sha}}(\bm{N},t)\,,

(28)

where the stochastic Shannon entropy reads

\displaystyle S_{\text{sha}}(\bm{N},t)=-\log P(\bm{N},t)\,.

(29)

Averaging
S_{\text{sys}}
over
\bm{N}
and taking a time derivative, we write the average rate of change of system entropy
\langle\dot{S}_{\text{sys}}\rangle
in terms of the average total entropy production rate
\langle\dot{\Sigma}\rangle
and the rate of entropy change
\langle\dot{S}_{\text{env}}\rangle
of the environment:

\displaystyle\langle\dot{S}_{\text{sys}}\rangle=\langle\dot{\Sigma}\rangle-\langle\dot{S}_{\text{env}}\rangle\,,

(30)

where
\langle\cdot\rangle
denotes the average over
\bm{N}
. These quantities are expressed in terms of probabilities, fluxes, and rates as

\displaystyle\langle\dot{\Sigma}\rangle=\sum_{\bm{N}}\sum_{n=0}^{q-1}\log\left[\frac{W_{n}^{+}(\bm{N})P(\bm{N},t)}{W_{n+1}^{-}(\bm{N}+\bm{\Delta}_{n}^{+})P(\bm{N}+\bm{\Delta}_{n}^{+},t)}\right]J_{n}(\bm{N}+{\bm{\Delta}}_{n}^{+},\bm{N}),

(31a)

\displaystyle\langle\dot{S}_{\text{env}}\rangle=\sum_{\bm{N}}\sum_{n=0}^{q-1}\log\left[\frac{K^{+}(N_{n+1}-N_{n})}{K^{-}(N_{n}-N_{n+1})}\right]J_{n}(\bm{N}+{\bm{\Delta}}_{n}^{+},\bm{N})\,.

(31b)

With the local detailed balance condition (
23
), we rewrite
\langle\dot{S}_{\text{env}}\rangle
in terms of the energy change and the non-conservative force as

\displaystyle\langle\dot{S}_{\text{env}}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\beta\sum_{\bm{N}}\sum_{n=0}^{q-1}\left[E(\bm{N}+\bm{\Delta}^{+}_{n})-E(\bm{N})-f\right]J_{n}(\bm{N}+{\bm{\Delta}}_{n}^{+},\bm{N})\,,

\displaystyle=-\beta\langle\dot{Q}\rangle\,,

(31c)

where
\langle\dot{Q}\rangle
denotes the average heat flux into the system.

The first law of thermodynamics,
\langle\dot{E}\rangle=\langle\dot{Q}\rangle+\langle\dot{W}\rangle
, relates the average heat flux
\langle\dot{Q}\rangle
to the average rate of energy change
\langle\dot{E}\rangle
and the average rate of dissipated work
\langle\dot{W}\rangle
, identified as

\displaystyle\langle\dot{W}\rangle=f\sum_{\bm{N}}\sum_{n=0}^{q-1}J_{n}(\bm{N}+{\bm{\Delta}}_{n}^{+},\bm{N})\,.

(32)

All three quantities,
\langle\dot{\Sigma}\rangle
,
\langle\dot{S}_{\text{env}}\rangle
, and
\langle\dot{W}\rangle
, are measures of dissipation. The total entropy production rate
\langle\dot{\Sigma}\rangle
determines the irreversibility of the complete system of oscillators and heat bath as function of time and is therefore arguably the most physically relevant dissipation measure. On the other hand, the other two measures, the average rate of dissipated work
\langle\dot{W}\rangle
in particular, are much easier to compute along stochastic trajectories, because their expressions [cf. Eqs. (
31
)] do not explicitly involve the probability
P(\bm{N},t)
.

Fortunately, at steady state, i.e.
\dot{P}(\bm{N},t)=0
, we have
\langle\dot{E}\rangle=\langle\dot{S}_{\text{sys}}\rangle=0
, which implies that

\displaystyle\langle\dot{\Sigma}\rangle=\langle\dot{S}_{\text{env}}\rangle=\beta\langle\dot{W}\rangle.

(33)

This shows that the average rate of dissipated work
\langle\dot{W}\rangle
can be used as a proxy for the average entropy production rate
\langle\dot{\Sigma}\rangle
at steady state. In the thermodynamic limit, discussed in Sec.
III
, an equation similar to (
33
) holds even for non-steady states at long times, so the average rate of dissipated work is a faithful measure of dissipation in this case as well.

II.6
Inflow rate

In addition to dissipation, measured by the thermodynamic observables introduced in Sec.
II.5
, we now consider the stability of trajectories. A simple measure for the stability of a stochastic trajectory is the so-called inflow rate
Baiesi and Falasco 2015
, defined as the entrance rate
W_{\text{in}}(\bm{N})
, the rate of trajectories entering a given state
\bm{N}
, subtracted by the escape rate
W_{\text{out}}(\bm{N})
out of
\bm{N}
:

\displaystyle L(\bm{N})=W_{\text{in}}(\bm{N})-W_{\text{out}}(\bm{N})\,,

(34)

where

\displaystyle W_{\text{in}}(\bm{N})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{q-1}\left[W^{+}_{n}(\bm{N}-\bm{\Delta}_{n}^{+})+W^{-}_{n}(\bm{N}-\bm{\Delta}_{n}^{-})\right]\,,

(35a)

\displaystyle W_{\text{out}}(\bm{N})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{q-1}\left[W^{+}_{n}(\bm{N})+W^{-}_{n}(\bm{N})\right]\,.

(35b)

The inflow rate quantifies the tendency of probability to accumulate in
\bm{N}
when
L(\bm{N})>0
, and to be depleted when
L(\bm{N})<0
. As we discuss in Sec.
III
, in the thermodynamic limit
N\to\infty
the average
\langle L\rangle
of the (stochastic) inflow rate
L(\bm{N})
reduces to the (deterministic) phase-space contraction rate of the macroscopic dynamics, a measure of stability in dynamical systems theory
Ott 2002
.

III
Thermodynamic limit

In the thermodynamic limit
N\to\infty
, the law of large numbers ensures that intensive variables are well described by their mean values.

In particular, the rescaled occupation number
\bm{n}\equiv\bm{N}/N
is intensive and obeys such a law. As a consequence, fluctuations of
\bm{n}
become unimportant in the thermodynamic limit and
\bm{n}
converges to its (deterministic) mean value
\bm{n}\to\bm{p}(t)\equiv\langle\bm{n}\rangle

Van Kampen 2007
. The quantity
p_{n}(t)=\langle n_{n}\rangle
, in turn, gives the probability that a randomly chosen microscopic Potts oscillator
s_{m}
is in state
s_{m}=n
at time
t
.

Because fluctuations of
\bm{n}
vanish as
N\to\infty
, intensive averages of functions of
\bm{n}
have large-
N
limits that are deterministic functions of
\bm{p}
. For a given extensive quantity
A(\bm{N})
with the intensive limit
a(\bm{p})
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\langle A(\bm{N})\rangle=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}A(\langle\bm{n}\rangle N)\equiv a(\bm{p})\,.

(36)

Similarly, averages of ratios of two extensive quantities
A(\bm{N})
and
B(\bm{N})
, with individual intensive limits
a(\bm{p})
and
b(\bm{p})
, decompose as

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\Big\langle\frac{A(\bm{N})}{B(\bm{N})}\Big\rangle=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{A(\langle\bm{n}\rangle N)}{N}\left(\frac{B(\langle\bm{n}\rangle N)}{N}\right)^{-1}=\frac{a(\bm{p})}{b(\bm{p})}\,.

(37)

Using Eq. (
36
), we find the large-
N
limits

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\langle E(\bm{n}N)\rangle

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}E(\langle\bm{n}\rangle N)\equiv\mathcal{E}(\bm{p})\,,

(38a)

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\langle S_{\text{int}}(\bm{n}N)\rangle

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}S_{\text{int}}(\langle\bm{n}\rangle N)\equiv\mathcal{S}_{\text{int}}(\bm{p})\,,

(38b)

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\langle F(\bm{n}N)\rangle

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}F(\langle\bm{n}\rangle N)\equiv\mathcal{F}(\bm{p})\,,

(38c)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\bm{p})

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mathscr{J}}{2}\bm{p}\cdot\bm{p}\,,\qquad\mathcal{S}_{\text{int}}(\bm{p})=-\bm{p}\cdot\log\bm{p}\,,

(39)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\bm{p})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}(\bm{p})-\beta^{-1}\mathcal{S}(\bm{p})\,.

(40)

Here,
\mathcal{E}(\bm{p})
denotes the mean energy per oscillator,
\mathcal{S}_{\text{int}}(\bm{p})
is the mean internal entropy per oscillator, and
\mathcal{F}(\bm{p})
represents the mean free energy per oscillator.

The
N
-rescaled, average fluxes are also intensive and read

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\bm{N}}J_{n}(\bm{N}+\bm{\Delta}^{+}_{n},\bm{N})\equiv\frac{1}{N}\left[\langle W^{+}_{n}(\bm{n}N)\rangle-\langle W^{-}_{n+1}(\bm{n}N)\rangle\right]\,.

(41)

As
N\to\infty
the terms on the right-hand side of Eq. (
41
) become [cf. Eq. (
36
)]

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\langle W^{\pm}_{n}(\bm{n}N)\rangle=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{W^{\pm}_{n}(\langle\bm{n}\rangle N)}{N}\equiv w_{n}^{\pm}(\bm{p})\,,

(42)

where the rescaled transition rates
w_{n}^{\pm}
are given by

\displaystyle w_{n}^{\pm}(\bm{p})=k^{\pm}(p_{n\pm 1}-p_{n})p_{n}\,,

(43)

and

\displaystyle\quad k^{\pm}(p_{n\pm 1}-p_{n})=\lim_{N\to\infty}\langle K^{\pm}[(n_{n\pm 1}-n_{n})N]\rangle\,.

(44)

For the thermodynamic limit of the rescaled fluxes in Eq. (
41
), we then obtain

\displaystyle j(p_{n+1},p_{n})

\displaystyle\equiv\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\bm{N}}J_{n}(\bm{N}+\bm{\Delta}^{+}_{n},\bm{N})\,,

\displaystyle=k^{+}(p_{n+1}-p_{n})p_{n}-k^{-}(p_{n}-p_{n+1})p_{n+1}\,.

(45)

The function
j(p_{n+1},p_{n})
represents the average probability flux per oscillator from state
n
to state
n+1
in the thermodynamic limit.

Note that the average flux
j(x^{\prime},x)
in Eq. (
45
) has the same form as the flux (
5
) of a single oscillator. The difference is that in the interacting model with
\mathscr{J}>0
, the functions
k^{\pm}(x)
in Eq. (
45
) depend on
\bm{p}
.

III.1
Macroscopic dynamics

We now consider the dynamics of
\bm{p}=\langle\bm{n}\rangle
in the thermodynamic limit. To this end, we multiply Eq. (
24
) by
\bm{n}=\bm{N}/N
and average over
\bm{N}
. Using the same arguments as before, we obtain for
N\to\infty
the equation of motion

\displaystyle\tfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\bm{p}(t)\equiv\bm{h}[\bm{p}(t)]=\sum_{n=0}^{q-1}\left[\bm{\Delta}^{+}_{n}w_{n}^{+}(\bm{p})+\bm{\Delta}_{n}^{-}w_{n}^{-}(\bm{p})\right]\,.

(46)

To simplify Eq. (
46
), we use Eqs. (
11
) to remove the sum and express the right-hand side in terms of the average fluxes (
45
). This gives

\displaystyle\dot{p}_{n}=h_{n}(\bm{p})=j(p_{n},p_{n-1})-j(p_{n+1},p_{n})\,,

(47)

with all indices understood modulo
q
.

Again, Eq. (
47
) has the same form as Master equation (
4
) for a single, uncoupled oscillator. And just as for the linear dynamics (
4
), one can readily show that Eq. (
47
) conserves probability, i.e.,

\displaystyle\tfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\sum_{n=0}^{q-1}p_{n}(t)=0\,,\quad\sum_{n=0}^{q-1}p_{n}(t)=1\,.

(48)

Due to the interactions
\mathscr{J}>0
between Potts oscillators, however, the fluxes
j(y,x)
in (
47
) are non-linear functions of the probability
\bm{p}
, whereas Eq. (
4
) is linear and constrained by the Perron-Frobenius theorem
Perron 1907
;
Frobenius 1912
.

The macroscopic dynamics is inherently thermodynamically consistent and satisfies a macroscopic version of local detailed balance condition (
22
):

\displaystyle\frac{w^{\pm}_{n}(\bm{p})}{w^{\mp}_{n\pm 1}(\bm{p})}

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{-\beta\left[\bm{\Delta}^{\pm}_{n}\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{\!\!\bm{p}}\mathcal{F}(\bm{p})\mp f\right]\right\}\,,

(49)

which follows from applying Eq. (
37
) to Eq. (
22
). The change in free energy enters Eq. (
49
) as

\displaystyle\bm{\Delta}^{\pm}_{n}\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{\!\!\bm{p}}\mathcal{F}(\bm{p})

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\left\{\langle F[(\bm{n}+\bm{\Delta}^{\pm}_{k}/N)N]\rangle-\langle F(\bm{n}N)\rangle\right\}\,,

\displaystyle=-\mathscr{J}(p_{n\pm 1}-p_{n})+\beta^{-1}\left(\log p_{n\pm 1}-\log p_{n}\right)\,.

(50)

Here,
(\nabla_{\bm{p}})_{n}\equiv\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{n}}
denotes the gradient with respect to
\bm{p}
.

III.2
Macroscopic thermodynamic observables

The intensive versions of the thermodynamic observables introduced in Sec.
II.5
have large-
N
limits in terms of the probabilities
\bm{p}
.

We define the intensive entropy production rate
\langle\dot{\sigma}\rangle=\lim_{N\to\infty}\langle\dot{\Sigma}\rangle/N
, rate of entropy change of the environment
\langle\mathcal{\dot{S}}_{\text{env}}\rangle=\lim_{N\to\infty}\langle\dot{S}_{\text{env}}\rangle/N
, rate of dissipated work
\langle\mathcal{\dot{W}}\rangle=\lim_{N\to\infty}\langle\dot{W}\rangle/N
, and heat production rate
\langle\mathcal{\dot{Q}}\rangle=\lim_{N\to\infty}\langle\dot{Q}\rangle/N
.

From the thermodynamic limit of Eqs. (
31
), we find that the intensive thermodynamic observables
\langle\mathcal{\dot{O}}\rangle=\langle\mathcal{\dot{W}}\rangle,\langle\mathcal{\dot{S}}_{\text{env}}\rangle,\langle\dot{\sigma}\rangle
can be decomposed into

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{\dot{O}}\rangle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{q-1}\mathcal{\dot{O}}(p_{n+1},p_{n})\,,

(51)

with edge functions
\mathcal{\dot{O}}(y,x)
that are identical for all
n
. The edge functions read in terms of the average flux
j(y,x)
:

\displaystyle\dot{\sigma}(y,x)=

\displaystyle\left[\beta\!\!\mathscr{J}(y-x)+\beta f+\log\left(x/y\right)\right]j(y,x)\,,

(52a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{\dot{S}}_{\text{env}}(y,x)=

\displaystyle\beta\left[\mathscr{J}(y-x)+f\right]j(y,x)\,,

(52b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{\dot{W}}(y,x)=

\displaystyle fj(y,x)\,.

(52c)

Using these explicit forms, we write
\langle\mathcal{\dot{S}}_{\text{env}}\rangle
and
\langle\dot{\sigma}\rangle
in terms of
\langle\mathcal{\dot{W}}\rangle
as

\displaystyle\langle\dot{\sigma}\rangle=

\displaystyle\beta\langle\mathcal{\dot{W}}\rangle-\beta\tfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\mathcal{F}[\bm{p}(t)]\,,

(53a)

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{\dot{S}}_{\text{env}}\rangle=

\displaystyle\beta\langle\mathcal{\dot{W}}\rangle-\beta\tfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\mathcal{E}[\bm{p}(t)]\,.

(53b)

These relations allow us to generalise Eq. (
33
) outside the steady state in the thermodynamic limit. To this end, we take an additional long-time average over the observables

\displaystyle\llangle\mathcal{\dot{O}}\rrangle=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\text{d}t\langle\mathcal{\dot{O}}\rangle\,.

(54)

The integral in Eq. (
54
) turns the total derivatives in Eqs. (
53
) into boundary terms that vanish in the long-time limit. With the additional time average, we obtain a generalisation of Eq. (
33
),

\displaystyle\llangle\dot{\sigma}\rrangle=\llangle\mathcal{\dot{S}}_{\text{env}}\rrangle=\beta\llangle\mathcal{\dot{W}}\rrangle\,,

(55)

that is valid beyond steady states.

In the synchronised phase, when
\bm{p}
follows a periodic trajectory with period
T_{\text{p}}
, such that
\bm{p}(t)=\bm{p}(t+T_{\text{p}})
, the long-time average in Eq. (
54
) can be replaced by an average over one period:

\displaystyle\llangle\mathcal{\dot{O}}\rrangle=\frac{1}{T_{\text{p}}}\int_{0}^{T_{\text{p}}}\!\!\text{d}t\langle\mathcal{\dot{O}}\rangle\,.

(56)

Hence, with the appropriate averages, relations (
55
) and (
33
) imply that the average rate of dissipated work
\llangle\mathcal{\dot{W}}\rrangle
serves as convenient proxy for the dissipation of the system.

III.3
Phase-space contraction in thermodynamic limit

The average
\langle L\rangle
of the entrance rate
L(\bm{N})
in Eq. (
34
) possesses a well-defined thermodynamic limit

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\equiv\lim_{N\to\infty}\langle L(\bm{N})\rangle=\lim_{N\to\infty}\langle W_{\text{in}}(\bm{N})-W_{\text{out}}(\bm{N})\rangle\,,

(57)

that reads

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\bm{p})=-{\bm{\nabla}}_{\!\!\bm{p}}\cdot\sum_{n=0}^{q-1}\left[\bm{\Delta}^{+}_{n}w_{n}^{+}(\bm{p})+\bm{\Delta}_{n}^{-}w_{n}^{-}(\bm{p})\right]=-{\bm{\nabla}}_{\!\!\bm{p}}\cdot\dot{\bm{p}}\,.

(58)

Hence, the average entrance rate
\mathcal{L}
converges to the negative flow divergence
-{\bm{\nabla}}_{\!\!\bm{p}}\cdot\bm{h}
of
\bm{p}
as
N\to\infty
. The flow divergence determines the exponential contraction rate of infinitesimal volumes
\mathcal{V}
in the phase-space of
\bm{p}

Ott 2002
. One has

\displaystyle\tfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\log\mathcal{V}(t)=-\mathcal{L}(\bm{p})\,,

(59)

which is why we call
\mathcal{L}(\bm{p})
the phase-space contraction rate.

Phase-space probability accumulates in regions of positive
\mathcal{L}
, while it escapes regions of negative
\mathcal{L}
. Positive
\mathcal{L}
is a necessary condition for flow-invariant sets, such as fixed points and for periodic orbits to be stable, and thus to be approached in the long-time limit. These properties make
\mathcal{L}
(and
\langle L\rangle
) important measures of stability.

III.4
Symmetries of macroscopic dynamics

In addition to the global permutation symmetry among oscillators that we used to introduce the occupation vector
\bm{N}
, driven Potts models are invariant under simultaneous, discrete rotations of all oscillators. This symmetry carries over to
\bm{p}
and leads to equivariance
Golubitsky et al. 1988
;
Crawford and Knobloch 1991
;
Golubitsky and Stewart 2003
of the macroscopic dynamics (
47
) under the corresponding symmetry transformations, i.e.,

\displaystyle\bm{h}(\bm{p})=\gamma^{-1}\bm{h}(\gamma\,\bm{p})\,.

(60)

The transformations represented by
\gamma
are associated with the cyclic group
C_{q}

Golubitsky et al. 1988
. The group
C_{q}
consists of the
q
elements

\displaystyle C_{q}

\displaystyle=\{e,\rho,\rho^{2},\ldots,\rho^{q-1}\}\,,

(61)

where
e
denotes the identity element and
\rho
is the generator of the group, satisfying
\rho^{q}=e
. In terms of
\bm{p}
, the symmetry induced by
C_{q}
corresponds to cyclic permutations of the elements of
\bm{p}
:

\displaystyle(p_{0},p_{1},\ldots,p_{q-1})^{\sf T}\overset{\rho}{\mapsto}(p_{q-1},p_{0},p_{1},\ldots,p_{q-2})^{\sf T}\,,

(62)

where the generator
\rho
has the
q
-dimensional matrix representation

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}_{\rho}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0&\ldots&0\\
0&0&1&\ldots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\ldots&1\\
1&0&0&\ldots&0\end{pmatrix}\,.

(63)

All elements of
C_{q}
in Eq. (
61
) have a matrix representation of the kind (
63
) given by
\mathbb{R}_{\rho^{n}}=\mathbb{R}_{\rho}^{n}
, where
n=1,\ldots,q
. The corresponding
q
-dimensional representation of
C_{q}
is reducible, and it is decomposed into the irreducible representations of
C_{q}
by means of a discrete Fourier transform, which we exploit below.

To summarise this Section, in the thermodynamic limit, the stochastic dynamics of driven Potts models becomes deterministic, and follows the macroscopic equations of motion, Eqs. (
47
). These equations describe the evolution of the probabilities
p_{n}(t)
for a randomly chosen oscillator to be in state
n
at time
t
and they share the microscopic symmetries of Potts oscillators. The thermodynamic observables
\langle\dot{\Sigma}\rangle
,
\langle\dot{S}_{\text{env}}\rangle
, and
\langle\dot{W}\rangle
, as well as the stability measure
\langle L\rangle
, that we defined for finite systems, all have deterministic, macroscopic limits,
\langle\dot{\sigma}\rangle
,
\langle\mathcal{\dot{S}}_{\text{env}}\rangle
,
\langle\mathcal{\dot{W}}\rangle
, and
\mathcal{L}
, respectively, that depend on
\bm{p}
.

IV
Stability of decoherent phase

After having taken the thermodynamic limit in Sec.
III
, we now analyse the stability of decoherent phase in this limit.
The formulation (
47
) of the macroscopic dynamics in terms of
j(x^{\prime},x)
immediately shows that any constant probability vector is a fixed point of the dynamics (
47
), because

\displaystyle j(x,x)-j(x,x)=0\,,

(64)

for all
x
. Probability conservation (
48
) implies that
x=q^{-1}
, so the decoherent state
\bm{p}^{*}
[Eq. (
9
)] is a fixed point of the dynamics (
47
). We now establish the stability and the bifurcations of the decoherent fixed point
\bm{p}^{*}
for arbitrary
q
.

To this end, we first transform the coordinates
\bm{p}\to\bm{x}
as

\displaystyle\bm{p}=\bm{x}+\bm{p}^{*}\,.

(65)

The vector
\bm{x}
, mentioned already in Sec.
I
, characterises the deviations from the decoherent fixed point. In terms of
\bm{x}
, probability conservation (
48
) reads
\sum_{n=0}^{q-1}x_{n}=0
and the decoherent fixed point is located at
\bm{x}^{*}=0
. The transformed macroscopic dynamics (
47
) reads

\displaystyle\bm{\dot{x}}=\bm{h}(\bm{x}+\bm{p}^{*})\equiv\bm{\tilde{h}}(\bm{x})\,,

(66)

which inherits the
C_{q}
symmetry (
60
), because
\mathbb{R}_{\gamma}\bm{p}^{*}=\bm{p}^{*}
for all
\gamma
in
C_{q}
.

The stability of the decoherent fixed point
\bm{x}^{*}=0
is determined by the stability matrix

\displaystyle\mathbb{M}_{nm}=\partial_{m}\tilde{h}_{n}(\bm{x})\big|_{\bm{x}=0}\,.

(67)

The equivariance relation (
60
) then implies that
\mathbb{M}
commutes with the generator
\mathbb{R}_{\rho}
,

\displaystyle[\mathbb{R}_{\rho},\mathbb{M}]=0\,.

(68)

This in turn, entails that the stability matrix
\mathbb{M}
is circulant
Davis 1979
, and that it has the eigenvectors
\bm{v}_{k}
with components

\displaystyle(v_{k})_{n}=\exp\left(\frac{2\pi ikn}{q}\right)\,,

(69)

where
k,n=0,\ldots,q-1
. Equations (
68
) and (
69
) are a consequence of the
C_{q}
symmetry of driven Potts models but are otherwise general.

The non-zero elements of the stability matrix
\mathbb{M}
of the decoherent fixed point
\bm{p}^{*}
read

\displaystyle\mathbb{M}_{nn}=j_{10}-j_{01}\,,\quad\mathbb{M}_{n+1n}=j_{01}\,,\quad\mathbb{M}_{n-1n}=j_{10}\,,

(70)

where we denote by
j_{nm}
the derivatives of the average flux
j
at
\bm{p}^{*}
:

\displaystyle j_{nm}=\partial^{n}_{y}\partial^{m}_{x}j(y,x)|_{x=y=\frac{1}{q}}\,.

(71)

In order to determine the stability of the decoherent fixed point
\bm{x}^{*}
, we must diagonalise the stability matrix
\mathbb{M}
.

IV.1
Discrete Fourier transform

Because
\mathbb{M}
is circulant, a consequence of Eq. (
68
), it is diagonalised by Fourier transform. The discrete Fourier transform for sequences of length
q
is expressed by a
q\times q
matrix
\mathbb{F}
. The latter is constructed from the eigenvectors (
69
) according to

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}=\begin{pmatrix}\rule[2.15277pt]{10.76385pt}{0.5pt}&\bm{v}^{\sf T}_{0}&\rule[2.15277pt]{10.76385pt}{0.5pt}\\
\rule[2.15277pt]{10.76385pt}{0.5pt}&\bm{v}^{\sf T}_{1}&\rule[2.15277pt]{10.76385pt}{0.5pt}\\
\rule[2.15277pt]{10.76385pt}{0.5pt}&\bm{v}^{\sf T}_{2}&\rule[2.15277pt]{10.76385pt}{0.5pt}\\
&\vdots&\\
\rule[2.15277pt]{10.76385pt}{0.5pt}&\bm{v}^{\sf T}_{q-1}&\rule[2.15277pt]{10.76385pt}{0.5pt}\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad\text{i.e.,}\quad F_{kn}=\text{e}^{\frac{i2\pi kn}{q}}\,.

(72)

The backtransform is obtained through the inverse matrix
\mathbb{F}^{-1}
, given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}^{-1}=q^{-1}\mathbb{F}^{\dagger},

(73)

where
F^{\dagger}_{nk}=\bar{F}_{kn}
denotes Hermitian conjugation. The Fourier transform (
72
) maps a vector
\bm{x}
to its Fourier modes
\bm{\hat{x}}

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{x}}=\mathbb{F}\bm{x}\,.

(74)

Expressing the problem in Fourier modes takes advantage of the periodicity of the system. Since the vector
\bm{x}
is real, the components of
\bm{\hat{x}}
occur in complex conjugate pairs of Fourier modes
z_{k}
and
\bar{z}_{k}
with

\displaystyle\hat{x}_{k}=z_{k}\,,\qquad\hat{x}_{-k}=\bar{z}_{k}\,,

(75)

where
k=1,\ldots,\lfloor\frac{q}{2}\rfloor
(indices modulo
q
).

Note that up to two Fourier modes are real: First, we have

\displaystyle\hat{x}_{0}=\hat{x}_{-0}\,,\qquad z_{0}=\bar{z}_{0}\,.

(76)

This component can safely be neglected, because
\hat{x}_{0}=0
due to probability conservation (
48
). Disregarding
\hat{x}_{0}
reduces the dimensionality of the problem from
q
to
q-1
.

Second, for even
q
, the Fourier mode with
k=\frac{q}{2}
is real, since

\displaystyle\hat{x}_{\frac{q}{2}}=\hat{x}_{-\frac{q}{2}}\,,\qquad z_{\frac{q}{2}}=\bar{z}_{\frac{q}{2}}\,.

(77)

This Fourier mode is associated with alternating probability patterns, as we discuss in Sec.
IV.2
. Therefore, we henceforth call
z_{\frac{q}{2}}
“alternating mode”.

Altogether, the dynamics is
q-1
dimensional and described by the
\lfloor\frac{q}{2}\rfloor
dynamic Fourier modes
z_{1},\ldots,z_{\lfloor\frac{q}{2}\rfloor}
, as advertised for
q=9
in Sec.
I
, where
\lfloor\frac{q}{2}\rfloor=4
.

IV.2
Stability exponents

With help of the Fourier transform (
72
), we diagonalise
\mathbb{M}
as
\mathbb{D}=\mathbb{FMF}^{-1}
. The diagonal components
\mathbb{D}_{k}\equiv\mathbb{D}_{kk}

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}_{k}=\mu_{k}+i\omega_{k}\,,\qquad\mathbb{D}_{-k}=\mu_{k}-i\omega_{k}\,,

where we introduced the parameters

\displaystyle\Lambda=2(j_{10}-j_{01})\,,\qquad\Omega=j_{10}+j_{01}=qj_{00}\,.

(80)

V
Coherent small-amplitude oscillations

From the stability analysis in Sec.
IV
, we concluded that the decoherent fixed point becomes unstable for
\Lambda>0
, at a phase boundary that depends on the dynamics. We now establish the final states that the system evolves to in the long-time limit, for
0<\Lambda\ll 1
and
\Lambda\ll\beta f
. It turns out that the choice of dynamics not only affects the stability of the decoherent phase, but it also changes the characteristics of the coherent small-amplitude states that occur when the decoherent phase is unstable.

In order to show this, we first Fourier transform the equation of motion (
47
), to obtain the dynamics of the Fourier modes
\bm{\hat{x}}
.

V.1
Dynamics of Fourier modes

In terms of the Fourier modes (
72
), the equation of motion (
66
) takes the form

\displaystyle\dot{\bm{\hat{x}}}=\bm{\hat{h}}(\bm{\hat{x}})\,,\qquad\bm{\hat{h}}(\bm{\hat{x}})\equiv\mathbb{F}\bm{\tilde{h}}(\mathbb{F}^{-1}\bm{\hat{x}})\,.

(86)

Close to the bifurcation, we expect that the system remains in the vicinity of the decoherent fixed point
\bm{\hat{x}}=0
and expand
\bm{\hat{h}}(\bm{\hat{x}})
to third order in
\bm{\hat{x}}
:

\displaystyle\bm{\dot{\hat{x}}}\sim\bm{\hat{h}}^{(1)}\!\!(\bm{\hat{x}})+\bm{\hat{h}}^{(2)}\!\!(\bm{\hat{x}})+\bm{\hat{h}}^{(3)}\!\!(\bm{\hat{x}})\,,

(87)

where the partial derivatives of
\bm{\hat{h}}
at
\bm{\hat{x}}=0
determine the model-dependent coefficients of the expansion:

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{h}}^{(1)}\!\!(\bm{\hat{x}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{D}\bm{\hat{x}}\,,\quad\bm{\hat{h}}^{(2)}\!\!(\bm{\hat{x}})=\frac{1}{2!}\sum_{k^{\prime},k^{\prime\prime}=1}^{q-1}\partial_{k^{\prime}}\partial_{k^{\prime\prime}}\bm{h}(\bm{\hat{x}})\big|_{\bm{\hat{x}}=0}\hat{x}_{k^{\prime}}\hat{x}_{k^{\prime\prime}}\,,

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{h}}^{(3)}\!\!(\bm{\hat{x}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3!}\sum_{k^{\prime},k^{\prime\prime},k^{\prime\prime\prime}=1}^{q-1}\partial_{k^{\prime}}\partial_{k^{\prime\prime}}\partial_{k^{\prime\prime\prime}}\bm{h}(\bm{\hat{x}})\big|_{\bm{\hat{x}}=0}\hat{x}_{k^{\prime}}\hat{x}_{k^{\prime\prime}}\hat{x}_{k^{\prime\prime\prime}}\,.

(88)

Conveniently, these coefficients are constrained by the
C_{q}
symmetry [Eq. (
60
)], which translates to
\bm{\hat{h}}
as

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{h}}(\bm{\hat{x}})=\mathbb{\hat{R}}_{\gamma}^{-1}\bm{\hat{h}}(\mathbb{\hat{R}}_{\gamma}\bm{\hat{x}})\,,

(89)

where
\mathbb{\hat{R}}_{\gamma}\equiv\mathbb{F}\mathbb{R}_{\gamma}\mathbb{F}^{-1}
with
\mathbb{R}_{\gamma}=\mathbb{R}_{\rho}^{n}
,
n=0,\ldots,q-1
, and generator
\mathbb{R}_{\rho}
given in Eq. (
63
).

\mathbb{R}_{\rho}
is diagonalised by
\mathbb{F}
, and we have
\mathbb{\hat{R}}_{\rho}=\mathbb{F}\mathbb{R}_{\rho}\mathbb{F}^{-1}
with diagonal components
\mathbb{\hat{R}}_{k}\equiv(\mathbb{\hat{R}}_{\rho})_{kk}
given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{\hat{R}}_{\pm k}=\text{e}^{\pm 2\pi ik/q}\,,

(90)

for
k=0,\ldots,\lfloor\frac{q}{2}\rfloor
. All other symmetry transformations
\mathbb{\hat{R}}_{\gamma}
are constructed from the generator
\mathbb{\hat{R}}_{\rho}
by
\mathbb{\hat{R}}_{\gamma}=\mathbb{\hat{R}}^{n}_{\rho}
,
n=0,\ldots,q-1
.

Equation (
90
) highlights a major advantage of the Fourier-transformed coordinates: Through
\mathbb{F}
, the
q
-dimensional reducible representation of
C_{q}
associated with Eq. (
63
) is transformed into the direct sum of
q
one-dimensional irreducible representations of
C_{q}
, given in Eq. (
90
). Out of these, only the trivial representation with
k=0
is real for odd
q
. This representation is associated with the unphysical zero mode which has its origin in the constraint (
48
).

For even
q
, an additional real representation with generator
\mathbb{\hat{R}}_{\frac{q}{2}}=-1
is associated with the alternating mode [Eq. (
82
)]. The existence of this additional real representation of the symmetry group for even
q
is the group-theoretic explanation for the occurrence of
\bm{v}_{\frac{q}{2}}
in Eq. (
82
).

The decomposition (
90
) into irreducible representations is a significant simplification, because the Fourier-transformed symmetry transformations
\mathbb{\hat{R}}_{\rho}
act locally on the components
\bm{\hat{x}}
, instead of globally shuffling the components of
\bm{x}
as with
\mathbb{R}_{\rho}
in Eq. (
63
).

In particular, for the expansion (
87
), the symmetry implies that
\bm{\hat{h}}^{(2)}
and
\bm{\hat{h}}^{(3)}
[see Eq. (
V.1
)] contain only terms with
k^{\prime\prime}=k-k^{\prime}
and
k^{\prime\prime\prime}=k-k^{\prime}-k^{\prime\prime}
, respectively, which significantly simplifies the manipulations that follow.

V.2
Normal form

Close to the bifurcation, for
0<\Lambda\ll 1
and
\Lambda\ll\beta f
, the coupled non-linear equation (
87
) can be brought into normal form
Crawford 1991
by a non-linear transformation
\bm{\hat{x}}\mapsto\bm{\hat{x}^{\prime}}
. This normal form characterises bifurcations into small-amplitude states, i.e. synchronised states and stationary patterns. It is considerably simpler than Eq. (
87
) as it contains only the essential terms for the bifurcation. In terms of the transformed components
z^{\prime}_{k}
, the normal form reads

\displaystyle\dot{z}^{\prime}_{k}\sim\bigg(\mathbb{D}_{k}-\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor\frac{q}{2}\rfloor}\mathbb{C}_{kk^{\prime}}|z^{\prime}_{k^{\prime}}|^{2}\bigg)z^{\prime}_{k}\,,

(91)

where
\mathbb{C}_{kk^{\prime}}
,
k,k^{\prime}=1,\ldots,\lfloor\frac{q}{2}\rfloor
, are coefficients that determine the magnitudes of the essential bifurcation terms.

The reason why Eq. (
91
) is easier to analyse than Eqs. (
87
) is that Eq. (
91
) allows to decouple the phases and amplitudes of
z^{\prime}_{k}
by the coordinate transform
z^{\prime}_{k}=r_{k}\text{e}^{i\phi_{k}}
. The corresponding phase-amplitude equations for

\displaystyle\bm{r}\equiv(r_{1},\ldots,r_{\lfloor\frac{q}{2}\rfloor})^{\sf T}\,,\quad\text{and}\quad\bm{\phi}\equiv(\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{\lfloor\frac{q}{2}\rfloor})^{\sf T}

(92)

are analysed in Sec.
V.4
.

The
\lfloor\frac{q}{2}\rfloor\times\lfloor\frac{q}{2}\rfloor
coefficient matrix
\mathbb{C}
in Eq. (
91
) decomposes into its real and imaginary parts as

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}=\mathbb{A}+i\mathbb{B}\,,

(93)

where
\mathbb{A}
and
\mathbb{B}
are real. We obtain
\mathbb{C}
from a method outlined in Sec.
V.3
.

We note that in normal form (
91
) the expanded equations of motion (
87
) appear to have a higher degree of symmetry: While Eq. (
87
) is equivariant under discrete rotations with
\mathbb{\hat{R}}_{\rho}
[Eq. (
90
)], the normal form is equivariant under continuous rotations of the phases of
z^{\prime}_{k}
. However, the transformation that brings Eq. (
87
) into the normal form (
91
) generates higher-order terms that we neglect. These higher-order terms are equivariant under the discrete complex rotations (
90
), but they are inessential for the local dynamics close to the bifurcation.

Hence, the terms of lower symmetry have not actually disappeared from Eq. (
91
) but are merely transformed into the higher orders, leaving us with only the essential terms of the Hopf bifurcation.

V.3
Transformation into normal form

We now outline the method to bring the expanded macroscopic equation of motion (
87
) into the normal form (
91
). To third order in
\bm{\hat{x}}
this is achieved by the non-linear coordinate transformation
\bm{\hat{x}}\mapsto\bm{\hat{x}}^{\prime}
, where

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{x}^{\prime}}=\bm{\hat{x}}+\bm{f}^{(2)}(\bm{\hat{x}})+\bm{f}^{(3)}(\bm{\hat{x}})\,.

(94)

Here,
\bm{f}^{(n)}(\bm{\hat{x}})
contains polynomials of order
n=2,3
in
\bm{\hat{x}}
. Since
\bm{\hat{x}^{\prime}}\sim\bm{\hat{x}}
to linear order in
\bm{\hat{x}}
, the transformation does not affect the linear order in Eq. (
87
). Instead, the transformation can be chosen to eliminate the non-linear cross terms in Eq. (
87
) and to bring the dynamics in the normal form (
91
)
Elphick et al. 1987
.

As we explain below, we need only find the quadratic transformation
\bm{f}^{(2)}
explicitly. The explict form of the quartic transformation
\bm{f}^{(3)}
is not required.

In Appendix
A
we show that
\bm{f}^{(2)}
is given by the condition
Guckenheimer and Holmes 1983
;
Crawford 1991

\displaystyle\mathbb{L}(\bm{f}^{(2)})=\bm{\hat{h}}^{(2)}\,,\quad\mathbb{L}(\bm{f})\equiv(\bm{f}\cdot\nabla)\bm{\hat{h}}^{(1)}-\bm{\hat{h}}^{(1)\sf T}D\bm{f}\,,

(95)

where
(D\bm{f})_{kk^{\prime}}=\partial f_{k}/\partial\hat{x}_{k^{\prime}}
denotes the matrix of derivatives of a given vector valued function
\bm{f}
.
\mathbb{L}
is a linear mapping that maps the unknown function
\bm{f}^{(2)}
to the known function
\bm{\hat{h}}^{(2)}
.

To determine
\bm{f}^{(2)}
, we compute the inverse of
\mathbb{L}
and apply it to
\bm{\hat{h}}^{(2)}
:

\displaystyle\bm{f}^{(2)}=\mathbb{L}^{-1}(\bm{\hat{h}}^{(2)})\,.

(96)

A convenient matrix representation for
\mathbb{L}
is obtained by expressing
\bm{f}^{(2)}
and
\bm{\hat{h}}^{(2)}
in a basis in which
\mathbb{L}
is diagonal
Crawford 1991
. Such a basis is given by the elements
\bm{e}_{k^{\prime}}
with components

\displaystyle(\bm{e}_{k^{\prime}})_{k}=e_{k^{\prime}k}\equiv\begin{cases}{\hat{x}}_{k^{\prime}}{\hat{x}}_{k-k^{\prime}}\,,&k\neq k^{\prime}\\
0\,,&k=k^{\prime}\end{cases}\,,

(97)

where
k,k^{\prime}=1,\ldots q-1
. Substituting
e_{k^{\prime}k}
into
\mathbb{L}
, we find

\displaystyle\mathbb{L}_{k}(\bm{e}_{k^{\prime}})=\left[\mu_{k}-\mu_{k^{\prime}}-\mu_{k-k^{\prime}}+i\left(\omega_{k}-\omega_{k^{\prime}}-\omega_{k-k^{\prime}}\right)\right]e_{k^{\prime}k}\,.

(98)

Hence, in a basis formed by the elements in Eqs. (
97
),
\mathbb{L}
is diagonal and its inverse can be computed as

\displaystyle\mathbb{L}^{-1}_{k}(\bm{e}_{k^{\prime}})=\frac{e_{k^{\prime}k}}{\mu_{k}-\mu_{k^{\prime}}-\mu_{k-k^{\prime}}+i\left(\omega_{k}-\omega_{k^{\prime}}-\omega_{k-k^{\prime}}\right)}\,.

(99)

As shown in Appendix
B
,
\bm{h}^{(2)}
reads in terms of the elements (
97
),

\displaystyle\hat{h}_{k}^{(2)}=\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{q-1}\hat{H}^{(2)}_{kk^{\prime}}e_{k^{\prime}k}\,,

(100)

where

\displaystyle\hat{H}^{(2)}_{kk^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{q}\frac{j_{20}-j_{02}}{\Lambda}\mu_{k}-i\frac{j_{11}}{q^{2}j_{00}}\left(\omega_{k}-\omega_{k^{\prime}}-\omega_{k-k^{\prime}}\right)\,.

(101)

Given this, we can immediately compute
\bm{f}^{(2)}
using Eq. (
99
):

\displaystyle{\hat{f}}_{k}^{(2)}=\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{q-1}\frac{\hat{H}^{(2)}_{kk^{\prime}}e_{k^{\prime}k}}{\mu_{k}-\mu_{k^{\prime}}-\mu_{k-k^{\prime}}+i\left(\omega_{k}-\omega_{k^{\prime}}-\omega_{k-k^{\prime}}\right)}\,,

(102)

which provides the transformation required to eliminate the quadratic terms in Eq. (
87
).

In addition to removing
\bm{\hat{h}}^{(2)}
, the quadratic transformation
\bm{f}^{(2)}
also impacts the cubic and higher-order terms in Eq. (
87
). We show in Appendix
A
that
\bm{f}^{(2)}
alters the third-order terms as

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{h}}^{(3)}\mapsto\bm{\hat{g}}^{(3)}\equiv\bm{\hat{h}}^{(3)}+\bm{\hat{h}}^{(2)\sf T}D\bm{f}^{(2)}\,.

(103)

The role of the cubic transformation
\bm{f}^{(3)}
in Eq. (
94
), in turn, is to remove all inessential third-order terms contained in
\bm{\hat{h}}^{(3)}
and those generated by
\bm{f}^{(2)}
.
\bm{f}^{(3)}
is determined in a similar way as before, by constructing the inverse of the mapping
\mathbb{L}

Crawford 1991
. However, since we know that the cubic transformation
\bm{f}^{(3)}
removes the non-essential third-order terms, but otherwise affects only the terms of order four and higher, we do not need to determine
\bm{f}^{(3)}
explicitly. Instead, we only need to compute the effect of
\bm{f}^{(2)}
on the coefficients of the
essential
cubic terms in Eq. (
91
).

Hence, once we have computed
\bm{\hat{g}}^{(3)}
in Eq. (
103
), the coefficients
\mathbb{C}
in the normal form (
91
) are determined by projecting
\bm{\hat{g}}^{(3)}
onto the third-order terms given in Eq. (
91
). This procedure brings the original equations (
87
) into normal form (
91
) with coefficients
\mathbb{D}
and
\mathbb{C}
. The details of the projection of
\bm{\hat{g}}^{(3)}
and how it leads us to
\mathbb{C}
are summarised in Appendix
B
.

V.4
Amplitude equations

Once we have obtained the normal form (
91
), the equations for the amplitudes
\bm{r}
and the phases
\bm{\phi}
follow by applying the transformation
z^{\prime}_{k}=r_{k}\text{e}^{i\phi_{k}}
. The amplitudes obey the equations

\displaystyle\dot{r}_{k}\sim\big(\mu_{k}-\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{\lfloor\frac{q}{2}\rfloor}\mathbb{A}_{kk^{\prime}}r^{2}_{k^{\prime}}\big)r_{k}\,,

(104a)

with matrix
\mathbb{A}
[cf. Eq. (
93
)] given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}=A\left(\bm{u}\bm{v}^{\sf T}-\mathbb{W}\right)\,,\quad A=\frac{4}{q^{2}}(j_{03}-j_{30})\,,

\bm{u}

\displaystyle u_{k}

For the analysis of the amplitude equations, we focus on stable fixed points

Equation (

By inspection of Eq. (

To ease the notation, we use the subscripts

and
S_{ia}

\bm{r}^{*}
as decompositions of

\displaystyle\bm{r}^{*}_{ia}=0\,,\qquad\bm{r}^{*2}_{a}=\mathbb{A}^{-1}_{a}\bm{\mu}_{a}\,.

Here,
\bm{r}^{*2}_{a}

\displaystyle\alpha_{a}=\frac{\bm{v}_{a}^{\sf T}\mathbb{W}_{a}^{-1}\bm{\tilde{\mu}}_{a}}{\bm{v}_{a}^{\sf T}\mathbb{W}_{a}^{-1}\bm{u}_{a}-1}\,,\quad\bm{\tilde{\mu}}_{a}\equiv\frac{\bm{\mu}_{a}}{\Lambda}\,.

of active Fourier modes and on

For a set

Using Eq. (

\displaystyle\mathbb{N}_{ia}=-\Lambda\,\text{diag}(\alpha_{a}\bm{u}_{ia}-\bm{\tilde{\mu}}_{ia})\,,\quad\mathbb{N}_{a}=-(\bm{\tilde{u}}_{a}\bm{\tilde{v}}_{a}^{\sf T}-\mathbb{\tilde{W}}_{a})\,,

\displaystyle\tilde{u}_{k}=\sqrt{2A}r^{*}_{k}u_{k}\,,\quad\tilde{v}_{k^{\prime}}=\sqrt{2A}r^{*}_{k^{\prime}}v_{k^{\prime}}\,,

V.5
Phase equations

We now briefly analyse the phase equations. They determine the angular velocities
\bm{\dot{\phi}}
with which the active modes rotate in the complex plane. The phase equations read

\displaystyle\dot{\phi}_{k}\sim\omega_{k}-\sum_{k^{\prime}\in S_{a}}\mathbb{B}_{kk^{\prime}}r^{*2}_{k^{\prime}}\,,

(116a)

Here, the matrix
\mathbb{B}
has the entries

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}_{kk^{\prime}}=B\left(1-\frac{1}{2}\delta_{kk^{\prime}}\right)w_{k}y_{k^{\prime}}\,,\quad B=\frac{1}{q^{2}}\frac{(j_{20}-j_{02})^{2}}{qj_{00}}\,,

(116b)

with vectors
\bm{w}
and
\bm{y}
given by

\displaystyle w_{k}=\sin\left(\frac{2\pi k}{q}\right)\,,\quad y_{k^{\prime}}=\left(1-\frac{1}{2}\delta_{k^{\prime}\frac{q}{2}}\right)\left[1+b\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi k^{\prime}}{q}\right)\right]\,,

(116c)

and parameter

\displaystyle b=4\frac{(j_{20}+j_{02})^{2}-\frac{1}{3}(j_{30}+j_{03})qj_{00}}{(j_{20}-j_{02})^{2}}\,.

(117)

For Arrhenius and Glauber dynamics, we have
B=2\cosh^{2}\left(\beta f/2\right)/\sinh\left(\beta f/2\right)
, as well as
b=0
for Arrhenius and
b=4\tanh^{2}\left(\beta f/2\right)
for Glauber dynamics.

For
k\in S_{a}
and
k\neq\frac{q}{2}
we define the relative difference
\Delta\dot{\phi}_{k}
of the oscillation frequency compared to the natural frequency
\omega_{k}
as

\displaystyle\Delta\dot{\phi}_{k}\equiv\frac{\dot{\phi}_{k}-\omega_{k}}{|\omega_{k}|}=-\frac{B}{\Omega}\left(\bm{y}_{a}\cdot\bm{r}_{a}^{*2}-\frac{1}{2}y_{k}r_{k}^{*2}\right)\,.

(118)

The right-hand side of Eq. (
118
) is always negative. This predicts that coherent oscillations of the active modes are consistently slower than their natural frequency
\omega_{k}
, with a correction of order
0<\Lambda\ll 1
.

V.6
Thermodynamic observables in synchronised phase

To analyse the thermodynamics in small-amplitute states, we expand the edge functions
\mathcal{\dot{O}}
in Eq. (
51
) around the decoherent fixed point
\bm{x}^{*}=0
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{\dot{O}}(y,x)\sim\mathcal{\dot{O}}_{00}+x\mathcal{\dot{O}}_{01}+y\mathcal{\dot{O}}_{10}+\frac{1}{2}x^{2}\mathcal{\dot{O}}_{02}+\frac{1}{2}y^{2}\mathcal{\dot{O}}_{20}+xy\mathcal{\dot{O}}_{11}\,,

(119)

where
\mathcal{\dot{O}}_{nm}=\partial^{n}_{y}\partial^{m}_{x}\mathcal{\dot{O}}(y,x)\big|_{x=y=\frac{1}{q}}
. Substituting this expansion into Eq. (
51
) and Fourier transforming
\bm{x}\mapsto\bm{\hat{x}}
, we show in Appendix
D
that for
0<\Lambda\ll 1
, Eq. (
52
) takes the form

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{\dot{O}}\rangle\sim\langle\mathcal{\dot{O}}\rangle_{0}-\frac{4j_{11}}{q}\bm{v}_{a}\cdot\bm{r^{*2}}_{a}\,.

(120)

Here,
\langle\mathcal{\dot{O}}\rangle_{0}=q\mathcal{\dot{O}}_{00}
is the average rate of change of the observable
\mathcal{O}
for a single, uncoupled oscillator. We note that
\langle\mathcal{\dot{O}}\rangle
is time-independent, so that
\llangle\mathcal{\dot{O}}\rrangle\sim\langle\mathcal{\dot{O}}\rangle
to leading order in
\Lambda\ll 1
. For the relative change
\Delta\mathcal{\dot{O}}
at the bifurcation, we find

\displaystyle\Delta\mathcal{\dot{O}}\equiv\frac{\langle\mathcal{\dot{O}}\rangle-\langle\mathcal{\dot{O}}\rangle_{0}}{|\langle\mathcal{\dot{O}}\rangle_{0}|}\sim

\displaystyle-2\Gamma\lambda\alpha_{a}\,,\quad\Gamma=\frac{2j_{11}}{q^{2}j_{00}}>0\,,

(121)

where
\Gamma
is a dynamics-dependent parameter. For example,
\Gamma
takes the values
\Gamma=\frac{1}{2}
and
\Gamma=1
for Arrhenius [Eq. (
83
)] and Glauber [Eq. (
85
)] dynamics, respectively.

Equation (
121
) shows that
\Delta\mathcal{\dot{O}}
is negative for all thermodynamic observables
\langle\dot{\sigma}\rangle
,
\langle\mathcal{\dot{S}}_{\text{env}}\rangle
, and
\langle\mathcal{\dot{W}}\rangle
. Hence, dissipation is reduced in small-amplitude states, synchronised and stationary, for all dynamics and all
q
. Equation (
121
) generalises the observations made in Fig.
2
(k) to all dynamics and all
q
.

Whether synchronisation increases or decreases dissipation in coupled oscillators is a debated question in the literature. Zhang
et al.

Zhang et al. 2020
argue that synchronised states in a model with global, chemical interactions, modelled by exchange reactions between oscillator phases, dissipate more energy than if they oscillate decoherently. In their model, interactions between oscillators occur at a given rate, independently of the individual rotating motion of the oscillators. As a result, alignment, and thus synchronisation, requires additional energy.

In driven Potts models, by contrast, synchronisation reduces dissipation, because collective oscillatory motion of Potts spins reduces the rate of energy-barrier crossings in the global potential, compared to decoherent oscillations.

Equation (
121
) also implies that the derivative of the average entropy production rate
\langle\dot{\sigma}\rangle
with respect to the (reduced) bifurcation parameter
\lambda
has a finite jump of size
-2\Gamma\alpha_{a}\langle\dot{\sigma}\rangle_{0}
at
\lambda=0
. Similar discontinuities have been observed numerically in thermodynamically consistent chemical reaction models across synchronisation transitions
Nguyen et al. 2018
.

V.7
Stability of coherent oscillations

Finally, we analyse the phase-space contraction rate
\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=-\sum_{n=0}^{q-1}\frac{\partial\dot{p}_{n}}{\partial p_{n}}\,,

(122)

close to the transition. In Appendix
E
we show that
\mathcal{L}
takes the following form:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\sim

\displaystyle-\frac{q\Lambda}{2}+qA\,\bm{v}_{a}\cdot\bm{r^{*2}}_{a}=-\frac{q\Lambda}{2}+q\Lambda\alpha_{a}\,,

(123)

when
0<\Lambda\ll 1
, independent of time
t
. With the phase-space contraction rate at the decoherent fixed point
\mathcal{L}_{0}=-q\Lambda/2
, we then find for the relative change
\Delta\mathcal{L}
of the
\mathcal{L}
at the bifurcation

\displaystyle\Delta\mathcal{L}\equiv\frac{\mathcal{L}-\mathcal{L}_{0}}{|\mathcal{L}_{0}|}=2\alpha_{a}>0\,.

(124)

Hence, phase-space contraction increases upon crossing the phase transition. Combining this relation with Eq. (
121
) for
\mathcal{\dot{O}}=\dot{\sigma}
we find the linear relation

\displaystyle\Delta\dot{\sigma}\sim-\Gamma\lambda\Delta\mathcal{L}\,.

(125)

This stability-dissipation relation, the main subject of the accompanying Ref.
Meibohm and Esposito 2024
, states that the relative amount by which dissipation is reduced in small-amplitude states is proportional to the relative change in phase-space contraction rate
\Delta\mathcal{L}
. The prefactor is given by the positive constants
\lambda
and
\Gamma
, which depend on the dynamics through the derivatives of the average flux
j
at the decoherent fixed point. Although the values of these constants are dynamics dependent, relation (
125
) holds in general and is, in particular, valid arbitrarily far from equilibrium.

Previous works on the connection between stability and dissipation either refer to near-equilibrium situations
Prigogine and Glansdorff 1971
;
Kondepudi and Prigogine 2014
or employ definitions of entropy production without clear thermodynamic interpretation
Daems and Nicolis 1999
. For so-called thermostated, deterministic systems, the phase space contraction rate can be shown to coincide with the entropy production rate in the macroscopic limit
Ruelle 1996
;
Searles and Evans 2000
;
J Evans and P
Morriss 2007
. However, the thermodynamics of these systems is quite intransparent, in particular with respect to the physical interpretation of the deterministic thermostat. The dissipation-stability relation (
125
), by contrast, holds far from equilibrium, for thermodynamically consistent stochastic systems which carry a clear thermodynamic interpretation. Relation (
125
) shows that entropy production and phase-space contraction are not equal in stochastic systems with local detailed balance, and obey a linear relation only close to the decoherent state.

V.8
Implications for steady-state selection

We have shown in Eq. (
125
) that close to the synchronisation transition, dissipation is minimal in small-amplitude states with highest phase-space contraction rate. In the vicinity of this transition and in the presence of multistability, this suggests that driven Potts models favour, i.e. select, states that minimise dissipation, in accordance with a minimum-dissipation principle
Prigogine 1955
that holds far from equilibrium.

In the strict thermodynamic limit, where fluctuations are negligible, however, maximum stability alone does not guarantee that the most stable state is assumed by the system. In addition, the initial condition must lie in its basin of attraction. At large but finite
N
, however, finite-size fluctuations induce transitions between nearby non-equilibrium states, thus allowing all states to be visited in the long-time limit.

For well-separated stable states at large
N
, such noise-induced transitions occur as large deviations and are exponentially suppressed in
N

Falasco and Esposito 2025
. Close to the transition (
\lambda\ll 1
), however, the decoherent state and all stable small-amplitude states are only separated by distances
\sim\sqrt{\lambda}
and typical (Gaussian) fluctuations around these states are roughly of the order
\sim 1/\sqrt{\mathcal{L}N}
. With
\mathcal{L}\sim\Lambda\sim\lambda
[see Eq. (
123
)], we thus find that for
1\ll N\lesssim\lambda^{-2}
, noise-induced transitions between the stable small-amplitude states occur frequently, due to typical fluctuations around the deterministic dynamics.

In this case, all stable small-amplitude states can be accessed from the decoherent state and are visited regularly. As a consequence, the states with smallest dissipation are occupied most of the time, because they have the largest average inflow rate
\langle L\rangle
. In other words, once a (stochastic) trajectory has reached a state of low dissipation, it typically spends a long time there, compared to the average time it took the trajectory to enter that state. Hence, close to the transition, at large but finite systems size, driven Potts models are expected to probabilistically select, i.e. predominantly occupy, those small-amplitude states that dissipate the least entropy.

VI
Numerical simulations

We now compare our results with numerical simulations of the stochastic dynamics. To this end, we generate a large number of trajectories
\bm{N}(t)
that we evolve with the Gillespie algorithm
Gillespie 1976

VII
Conclusions

We have studied small-amplitude synchronisation and stationary states in driven Potts models with general dynamics and all numbers
q
of states. These models describe ferromagnetically interacting Potts spins in contact with a heat bath at inverse temperature
\beta
and driven by a non-conservative force
f
. The competition between decoherence from thermal noise and alignment due to ferromagnetic interactions, combined with non-equilibrium driving, results in a dynamical phase transition from the decoherent phase into either a synchronised phase or, in some cases, non-equilibrium stationary states.

To study the transition, we have derived and analysed the normal form of the high-dimensional Hopf bifurcation that underlies it. Close to the transition, we found stable synchronised and stationary states of small amplitude for a wide range of parameters. The stability of the decoherent phase as well as the characteristics of the small-amplitude states were found to depend on the dynamics in an intricate way.

In particular, dynamics may stabilise or destabilise the decoherent phase, compared to the equilibrium Potts model, depending on the location in parameter space where the bifurcation parameter
\Lambda
in Eq. (
80
) changes sign. In the synchronised phase,
q
and
a
[cf. Eq. (
105
)] span the parameter space of a rich phase diagram (Fig.
5
) of coherent small-amplitude oscillations.

Each synchronised state hosts a finite number of active Fourier modes that determine the shape of the probability distribution of oscillator states in the thermodynamic limit. The number of active modes was found to be much smaller than the maximum possible, with active modes being the ones with largest
k
. This shows that driven Potts oscillators synchronise differently than oscillators in the Kuramoto model: While the phases in Kuramoto oscillators localise in real space, Potts oscillators localise in Fourier space.

Connecting to the thermodynamics of the model, we found that in driven Potts models synchronisation reduces dissipation, independently of the dynamics and of the number
q
of states within each oscillator. Furthermore, we have shown that dissipation is minimised in the most stable synchronised state. Close to the synchronisation transition, this finding is summarised in a linear stability-dissipation relation, given Eq. (
125
) and discussed in Ref.
Meibohm and Esposito 2024
. At finite system size and close to the transition,
1\ll N\lesssim\lambda^{-2}
, our findings suggest the existence of a minimum-dissipation principle for driven Potts models, where the system probabilistically selects the least dissipating state based on maximum stability, i.e., maximum phase-space contraction. In practice, our numerical results indicate that the minimum-dissipation principle holds for significantly larger
N
and
\lambda
than explained by this estimate.

Our analysis opens the door to a deeper understanding of driven Potts models, synchronisation transitions, and non-equilibrium phase transitions in general. The phase diagram in Fig.
5
is an ideal starting point to study regimes outside the vicinity of the transition. We have conducted a preliminary study that indeed shows that the model exhibits additional transitions into quasiperiodic and possibly chaotic, synchronised states for large-enough
\lambda
. It would be interesting to investigate systematically how these additional transitions affect the thermodynamics
Gaspard 2020
, and, in particular, whether or not they reduce dissipation even further.

On the basis of the deterministic coherent oscillations we have studied here, another logical next step is the analysis of finite-
N
fluctuations. This would allow us to directly quantify the coherence of oscillations
Gaspard 2002a
;
Gaspard 2002b
;
Barato and Seifert 2017
, and to estimate the rates of transitions between stable small-amplitude states. This could provide a quantitative estimate of the probabilities to reside in given stable states, and thus possibly extend the minimum-dissipation principle to regimes outside the vicinity of the phase transition.

The linear stability-dissipation relation (
125
) has been shown to hold for all driven Potts models and all
q
, and might, with possibly model-specific adjustments, hold even for more general classes of models. Intriguing candidates to test this hypothesis are driven lattice Potts models with nearest-neighbour interactions
Wood et al. 2006
, for which preliminary studies show promising results, and perhaps also active versions of the model
Manacorda and Fodor 2023
. Driven lattice Potts models close to the synchronisation transition could exhibit interesting critical fluctuations, whose characteristics might affect the minimum-dissipation principle and the stability-dissipation relation established here.

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2q^{2}}\left\{2\frac{j_{03}-j_{30}}{\Lambda}\left(\tilde{\mu}_{k\frac{q}{3}}-2\mu_{\frac{q}{2}}\right)+\frac{i}{3}\frac{j_{30}+j_{03}}{qj_{00}}\tilde{\omega}_{k\frac{q}{2}}\right\}{z^{\prime}}^{3}_{\frac{q}{2}}\,.

Appendix C
Stable oscillations for
a<\frac{1}{3}

In this Section, we show that for
a<\frac{1}{3}
the stability matrix
\mathbb{N}
has at least
|S_{a}|-1
positive eigenvalues, as a consequence of the matrix determinant lemma
Brookes 2020
and an eigenvalue interlacing theorem
Bunch et al. 1978
;
Thompson 1976
;
Wilkinson 1965
.

As explained in the main text, we can bring the stability matrix
\mathbb{N}
in Eq. (
110
) into a form where the elements
l,m\in S_{a}
form a connected block
\mathbb{N}_{a}
, and
\mathbb{N}_{ia}
gives the diagonal part. The eigenvalues of
\mathbb{N}
are then given by the diagonal elements in
\mathbb{N}_{ia}
and the eigenvalues of the block
\mathbb{N}_{a}
.

We now show that the connected bloc
\mathbb{N}_{a}
has
|S_{a}|-1
positive eigenvalues for
a<\frac{1}{3}
. To this end, we symmetrise
\mathbb{N}
, writing
\mathbb{\tilde{N}}=\mathbb{S}\mathbb{N}\mathbb{S}^{-1}
, where
\mathbb{S}
is diagonal with elements

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}_{kk^{\prime}}=\sqrt{\frac{\tilde{v}_{k}}{\tilde{u}_{k}}}\delta_{kk^{\prime}}\,.

(161)

This transformation does not affect the eigenvalues and leaves the diagonal part of
\mathbb{N}
invariant. The block
\mathbb{N}_{a}
, however, transforms into the sum of a diagonal part and a symmetric order-one perturbation,

\displaystyle\mathbb{\tilde{N}}_{a}=-(\bm{\tilde{w}}_{a}\bm{\tilde{w}}_{a}^{\sf T}-\mathbb{\tilde{W}}_{a})\,,

(162)

with the symmetric rank-one perturbation
\bm{\tilde{w}}_{a}\bm{\tilde{w}}_{a}^{\sf T}
characterised by the vector
\tilde{w}
with components

\displaystyle\tilde{w}_{k}=\sqrt{\tilde{u}_{k}\tilde{v}_{k}}\,.

(163)

For matrices of the form (
162
), the Matrix determinant lemma
Brookes 2020
states that the determinant of
\mathbb{\tilde{N}}_{a}
, and thus of
\mathbb{N}_{a}
, is given by

\displaystyle\det\mathbb{\tilde{N}}_{a}

\displaystyle=\det(\mathbb{\tilde{W}}_{a})\left(1-\bm{\tilde{w}}_{a}^{\sf T}\mathbb{\tilde{W}}_{a}^{-1}\bm{\tilde{w}}_{a}\right)

(164)

The elements of
\mathbb{\tilde{W}}_{a}
are positive whenever all elements of
\mathbb{W}_{a}
are positive, which is guaranteed for
a<\frac{1}{3}
. A simple analysis shows that the term
1-\bm{\tilde{w}}_{a}^{\sf T}\mathbb{\tilde{W}}_{a}^{-1}\bm{\tilde{w}}_{a}
is negative for
a<\frac{1}{3}
, which implies that the determinant of
\mathbb{\tilde{N}}_{a}
is negative. Hence,
\mathbb{N}_{a}
must have at least one negative eigenvalue.

To extend this result, we now show that
\mathbb{\tilde{N}}_{a}
has exactly one negative eigenvalue and
|S_{a}|-1
positive eigenvalues. This follows directly from an eigenvalue interlacing theorem
Bunch et al. 1978
;
Thompson 1976
;
Wilkinson 1965
, which states that for symmetric matrices perturbed by a rank-one perturbation as in Eq. (
162
), the ordered eigenvalues
K_{n}
,
K_{1}\geq K_{2}\geq\ldots\geq K_{|S_{a}|}
, of
\mathbb{\tilde{N}}_{a}
(and thus of
\mathbb{N}_{a}
) have the property

\displaystyle K_{|S_{a}|}\leq\tilde{\Delta}_{|S_{a}|}\leq K_{|S_{a}|-1}\leq\tilde{\Delta}_{|S_{a}|-1}\ldots\leq\tilde{\Delta}_{1}\,,

(165)

where
\tilde{\Delta}_{k}
are the ordered diagonal elements of
\mathbb{\tilde{W}}_{a}
, such that
\tilde{\Delta}_{1}\geq\tilde{\Delta}_{2}\geq\ldots\geq\tilde{\Delta}_{|S_{a}|}
. In other words, the eigenvalues of
\mathbb{\tilde{N}}_{a}
are interlaced with the ordered diagonal elements
\tilde{\Delta}_{k}
of
\mathbb{\tilde{W}}_{a}
. Since all
\tilde{\Delta}_{k}
are positive for
a<\frac{1}{3}
, we find that all
K_{k}
with
k<|S_{a}|
must be positive.

But because the determinant
\det\mathbb{\tilde{N}}_{a}
in Eq. (
164
) is always negative, the smallest eigenvalue
K_{|S_{a}|}
must be negative. This means that
\mathbb{\tilde{N}}_{a}
has exactly one negative eigenvalue and
|S_{a}|-1
positive eigenvalues.

We conclude that the complete matrix
\mathbb{\tilde{N}}_{a}
(and thus the untransformed
\mathbb{N}_{a}
) has at least
|S_{a}|-1
unstable directions, which means that only states with a single active Fourier mode,
|S_{a}|=1
, can be stable for
a<\frac{1}{3}
, as stated in the main text.
