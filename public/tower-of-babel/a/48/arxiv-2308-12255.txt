Absorbing boundary conditions for the Helmholtz equation using Gauss-Legendre quadrature reduced integrations
Koki Sagiyama

Abstract
. We introduce a new class of absorbing boundary conditions (ABCs) for the Helmholtz equation. The proposed ABCs are obtained by using L L discrete layers and the Q N Q_{N} Lagrange finite element in conjunction with the N N -point Gauss-Legendre quadrature reduced integration rule in a specific formulation of perfectly matched layers. The proposed ABCs are classified by a tuple ( L , N ) (L,N) , and achieve reflection error of order O ⁡ ( R 2 ​ L ​ N ) O(R^{2LN}) for some R < 1 R<1 . The new ABCs generalise the perfectly matched discrete layers proposed by Guddati and Lim [Int. J. Numer. Meth. Engng 66 (6) (2006) 949-977], including them as type ( L , 1 ) (L,1) . An analysis of the proposed ABCs is performed motivated by the work of Ainsworth [J. Comput. Phys. 198 (1) (2004) 106-130]. The new ABCs facilitate numerical implementations of the Helmholtz problem with ABCs if Q N Q_{N} finite elements are used in the physical domain as well as give more insight into this field for the further advancement.



1.
Introduction

The Helmholtz equation is of interest
in many fields in physics and engineering.
We are particularly interested in solving the Helmholtz equation
in unbounded domains.
As the standard numerical methods such as finite element methods
require finite computational domains,
numerically solving such problem requires one to map
the original problem in an unbounded domain
to one in a bounded domain.
This is commonly achieved by truncating the unbounded domain
and applying an artificial boundary condition representing
the original unboundedness
on or in the vicinity of the surface of truncation.
Such artificial boundary conditions are called

absorbing boundary conditions
(ABCs).
The
accuracy

of an ABC is often measured by the
reflection coefficient
,
the ratio of the spurious incoming wave to the outgoing wave.
Many ABCs have been proposed in the last decades, and,
among them, the complete radiation boundary conditions (CRBCs) and
the perfectly matched discrete layers (PMDLs) are
known to be especially accurate and efficient.

ABCs have typically been studied for the wave equation, from which
those for the Helmholtz equation can readily be derived.
The early ABCs proposed and studied in

[
17
,
18
,
12
,
28
,
29
]

involved high-order temporal and spatial derivatives
on the artificial boundary.
The Higdon ABCs
[
28
]
were
designed to annihilate
L
purely propagating plane waves
and included
L
-th order temporal and normal derivatives
of the solution on the boundary.
The Givoli-Neta ABCs
[
20
]

introduced auxiliary functions on the boundary and
recast the classic Higdon ABCs
as systems of
L
equations that only involved first-order
temporal and normal derivatives,
greatly facilitating the numerical implementations.
Hagstrom-Warburton ABCs
[
25
]

symmetrised
the Givoli-Neta ABCs,
squaring the reflection coefficients.
The CRBCs
[
26
]

further improved the behaviour by directly taking into account
the waves that decays while propagating.
The significance of treating such wave mode was also noted in
[
29
]
.
This class of ABCs are generally known to show excellent performances
regardless of the choice of the parameters.
Since it was demanded that the auxiliary functions
were only defined
on
the boundary,
the above classes of ABCs required reformulations to remove
the unfavorable first-order normal derivatives.
In
[
20
]
the Givoli-Neta ABCs were rewritten in forms
that only involved up to second-order temporal and
tangential
derivatives;
such formulations are thus called
second-order formulations
.
The second-order formulations of the later ABCs were derived in

[
25
,
32
]
.
Further, in multi dimensions,
the above classes of ABCs required special treatment on edges and corners
on which multiple faces met.
The
edge/corner compatibility conditions
were derived for
the Hagstrom-Warburton ABCs in

[
25
]

and for the later ABCs in

[
32
]
.
The second-order formulations as well as
the edge/corner compatibility conditions
require one to work with complex systems of equations on the artificial boundary
that are distinct from the wave equation or the Helmholtz equation
solved in the physical domain.
As noted shortly, the PMDLs have a clear advantage in this regard
when the Helmholtz equation is considered.
Having been developed for the wave equation, however,
the CRBCs and the other related ABCs typically handle
the wave equation more directly and efficiently, but,
as we focus on the Helmholtz equation in this work,
we leave further discussion on the wave equation for future work.

The perfectly matched layers (PMLs) formulation is another class of
conditions widely used to approximate the effect of the unboundedness.
The PMLs were originally proposed in
[
13
]

for the time-domain electromagnetics, in which
artificial damping terms were added in the vicinity of
the domain of interest.
We call the domain of interest the
physical domain
,
the domain in which damping terms were added
the
artificial domain
, and
call the union of the two
the
computational domain
.
The addition of the artificial damping was later reinterpreted
as a
complex coordinate transformation
in the frequency-domain
in
[
16
]
.
As for the Helmholtz equation,
the PMLs have been more popular than the CRBCs and the other related ABCs,
presumably because, a single
Helmholtz-like equation
,
which reduces to the standard Helmholtz equation
for a particular choice of parameters,
monolithically governs the entire computational domain
even in existence of edges and corners,
which facilitates numerical implementations;
see, e.g.,
[
13
]
for the initial treatment of the corners.
Despite the popularity of the PMLs, however,
the accuracy of the PMLs are known to be sensitive to
the choice of the parameters, which are hard to tune
a priori
,
and the ABCs often outperform the PMLs in terms of accuracy.
An attempt to optimise the performance of the PMLs
for a particular finite difference scheme
[
7
]

then inspired the development of the PMDLs
[
22
,
24
]

designed for finite element methods.
The PMDLs formulation is effectively obtained by
first writing the PMLs formulation in the weak form,
then using the
linear
finite element approximation
with
midpoint
reduced integration in the artificial domain
in the direction normal to the artificial boundary.
It has been shown that the PMDLs are essentially equivalent to the CRBCs, but
the PMDLs being derived from the PMLs,
they inherit the favourable property of the PMLs, and
allow for working with the Helmholtz-like equation
across the computational domain even if edges and corners exist.
Specifically, if one chooses to use the
Q_{1}
tensor-product Lagrange finite element
in the physical domain,
that Helmholtz-like equation can be discretised monolithically
on the entire computational domain,
leaving one only having to use different quadrature rules
in the physical and artificial domains.
If one chooses to use the
Q_{N}
element with
N>1
in the physical domain,
the artificial domain must be treated separately
as the PMDLs require linear approximation in the artificial domain
in the direction normal to the boundary.
Even in such cases, however,
the PMDLs are generally more tractable than the CRBCs.

In this work we propose a new class of ABCs
for the Helmholtz equation that generalises the PMDLs.
The proposed ABCs are derived from a certain simple class of
perfectly matched layers with
L
discrete layers in the artificial domain,
using the
Q_{N}
tensor-product Lagrange finite elements in conjunction with
the
N
-point Gauss-Legendre quadrature reduced integration
in the artificial domain.
Thus, the proposed ABCs can be classified by a tuple
(L,N)
,
with the PMDLs represented by
(L,1)
.
The ABCs of type
(L,N)
and those of type
(LN,1)

introduce the same number of auxiliary degrees of freedom
in the artificial domain, and they are as accurate as each other.
Like the PMDLs, the proposed ABCs only involve a single
Helmholtz-like equation over the entire computational domain
even if edges and corners exist, but
they further allow for monolithic discretisations
over the computational domain
even when the
Q_{N}
finite elements with
N>1
are used in the physical domain.
We present an analysis of the proposed ABCs,
strongly motivated by the techniques used in
[
2
]
,
in which dispersive and dissipative behaviour of
high-order discontinuous Galerkin methods were studied,
and in the related works
[
1
,
4
,
3
]
,
that is to give more insight into the problems of ABCs.

In Sec.
2
and Sec.
3

we introduce the Helmholtz equation and the standard PMLs formulation.
In Sec.
4
we study a certain simple PMLs formulation in detail,
which will aid us with developing the new ABCs.
In Sec.
5
we introduce the discretised weak forms of the PMLs formulations
introduced in Sec.
3
and Sec.
4
.
In Sec.
6
we introduce and study the new class of ABCs.
In Sec.
7
we show one- and three-dimensional examples.
In Sec.
8
we summarise this work and discuss future works.

2.
The Helmholtz equation

The Helmholtz equation in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
to be solved for
u(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{d})
is given by:

(1)

\displaystyle s^{2}u-u_{,ii}

\displaystyle=f\quad\textrm{in }\mathbb{R}^{d},

where sum is taken over
i\in\{1,\cdots,d\}
,

s\in\mathbb{C}
is a complex number with
\text{Re}(s)\geq 0
, and

f
is a spatial function.
(
1
) can be obtained from the wave equation by taking the Laplace transform in time, in which case
\text{Re}(s)>0
, or by making the time-harmonic assumption
using the
\exp{(\mathfrak{i}\omega t)}
convention with
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
, in which case
s=\mathfrak{i}\omega
, and

\text{Re}(s)=0
.
Assuming that
f
has a compact support
in
x<-\delta
for some
\delta>0
, where
x:=x_{1}
is the first coordinate variable,
we have:

(2)

\displaystyle s^{2}u-u_{,ii}

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{in }x>-\delta.

In this work we mostly work with the following mapped problem of (
2
)
to be solved for
u(x)

(with an abuse of notation):

(3)

\displaystyle\gamma^{2}u-u_{,xx}=0\quad\textrm{in }x>-\delta,

where
\gamma:=\sqrt{s^{2}+k^{2}}
,
where
k:=\sqrt{k_{i}k_{i}}
(sum on
i\in\{2,\cdots,d\}
),
where
k_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
.
(
3
) is obtained by taking the Fourier transform of (
2
)
in all the coordinate variables except in
x:=x_{1}
following the Laplace transform in time
[
26
]
,
in which case,
k_{i}
is the dual variable of
x_{i}
, or
by making the plane wave assumption using the
\exp{(\mathfrak{i}k_{i}x_{i})}
(sum on
i\in\{2,\cdots,d\}
) convention
following the time-harmonic assumption.
In the former case,
\text{Re}(s)>0
, and we consider a Riemann surface with branch cut on

(-\mathfrak{i}\infty,-\mathfrak{i}k)\cup(+\mathfrak{i}k,+\mathfrak{i}\infty)
and
choose the branch of
\sqrt{s^{2}+k^{2}}
so that
\gamma=+k
when
s=0
.
In this case we have
\text{Re}(\gamma)>0
.
In the latter case,
s=\mathfrak{i}\omega
, and we define the value of
\gamma
as the limit in the above branch
along
s=\mathfrak{i}\omega+\epsilon
as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
.
In this case we assume that
s\neq\pm\mathfrak{i}k
.
The following two properties then hold for
\text{Re}(s)\geq 0
:

(4a)

\displaystyle[\text{Im}(\gamma)\!\cdot\!\text{Im}(s)>0\textrm{ and }\text{Re}(\gamma)\geq 0]\text{ or }[\text{Im}(\gamma)=0\textrm{ and }\text{Re}(\gamma)>0],

(4b)

\displaystyle\textrm{Re}\hskip-2.0pt\left(\frac{\gamma}{a\!\cdot\!s+b}\right)>0,\quad\forall a,b>0.

One can readily solve (
3
)
up to two undetermined coefficients,
c_{-}
and
c_{+}
, as:

(5)

\displaystyle u(x)=c_{-}e^{+\gamma x}+c_{+}e^{-\gamma x}.

Noting (
4a
), one can see that

e^{+\gamma x}
represents the wave
that is
incoming
and/or grows as
x\rightarrow+\infty
and

e^{-\gamma x}
represents the wave
that is
outgoing
and/or decays as
x\rightarrow+\infty
.
The pure outgoing/decaying wave would thus require
c_{-}=0
, or:

(6)

\displaystyle c_{-}=0\!\cdot\!c_{+}.

This ratio of
c_{-}
to
c_{+}
is called the

reflection coefficient
, and is an important measure
of the accuracy of absorbing boundary conditions (ABCs).
On the other hand, the pure outgoing/decaying wave solution would also satisfy:

(7)

\displaystyle u_{,x}(0)+\gamma u(0)=0,

which is known as the
Sommerfeld radiation condition
(at
x=0
).
The extent to which a given ABC
approximates (
7
)
gives another important measure of the accuracy of that condition.

Our domain of interest, or the
physical domain
, being
x<0
,
we are to truncate the right half-domain.
Although (
7
) gives the
exact

boundary condition for the outgoing/decaying wave at
x=0

for the one-dimensional problem (
3
)
for a given
k\in\mathbb{R}
,
its multidimensional counterpart that is exact for all
k
does not exist.
In the following sections
we work with several conditions applied in the vicinity of
x=0

outside the physical domain, which we call the
artificial domain
,
that are to approximate the effect of the unboundedness of
x>0
,
ideally only admitting the outgoing/decaying wave at
x=0
.

3.
Perfectly matched layers

The perfectly matched layers (PMLs) method was originally developed
for the time-domain electromagnetics in
[
13
]

by adding artificial damping terms in the artificial domain
so that the outgoing wave attenuates.
The addition of artificial damping was later reinterpreted
as
complex coordinate transformation
in
[
16
]
.
In this section we briefly introduce the PMLs formulation using the idea of
complex coordinate transformation.
Although the PMLs formulation is conventionally derived for a simplified version
of (
3
), which
one can obtain by setting
s=\mathfrak{i}\omega
and
k=0
, or
\gamma=\mathfrak{i}\omega
,
we here use (
3
) directly
to take into account the effect of multi dimensions.

The crucial step in deriving the PMLs formulation is to introduce
a
complex coordinate transformation
,

\tilde{\chi}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
,
which is sufficiently smooth and
satisfies
\tilde{\chi}(x)=x
in
x<0
.
We denote the transformed coordinate by
\tilde{x}:=\tilde{\chi}(x)
,
and consider solving the following modified version of (
3
)
for
\tilde{u}(\tilde{x})
:

(8)

\displaystyle\gamma^{2}\tilde{u}-\tilde{u}_{,\tilde{x}\tilde{x}}=0\quad x>-\delta.

Defining
u^{\dagger}:=\tilde{u}\circ\tilde{\chi}
,
one can rewrite (
8
) in terms of
x

as a problem to be solved for
u^{\dagger}(x)
as:

(9)

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma^{2}}{g^{\textrm{PML}}}u^{\dagger}-(g^{\textrm{PML}}u^{\dagger}_{,x})_{,x}

\displaystyle=0\quad x>-\delta,

where:

(10)

\displaystyle g^{\textrm{PML}}(x):=\frac{\thinspace\mathrm{d}x}{\thinspace\mathrm{d}\tilde{x}},

where the superscript
(\cdot)^{\textrm{PML}}
is used to represent PMLs.
Note that
g^{\textrm{PML}}(x)=1
in
x<0
, so (
9
)
reduces to the standard Helmholtz equation (
3
) in
x<0
.
By construction, the solution to (
9
) is readily obtained as:

(11)

\displaystyle u^{\dagger}(x)=c^{\dagger}_{-}e^{+\gamma\tilde{\chi}(x)}+c^{\dagger}_{+}e^{-\gamma\tilde{\chi}(x)}.

The complex transformation function
\tilde{\chi}(x)
is chosen so that
the outgoing/decaying wave, represented by
\exp{(-\gamma\tilde{\chi}(x))}
,
attenuates as
x\rightarrow+\infty
.
A popular choice in the common setting of
\gamma=\mathfrak{i}\omega
is

\tilde{\chi}(x)=x+(\omega_{0}/\mathfrak{i}\omega)\!\cdot\!\int_{-\infty}^{x}f^{\textrm{PML}}(\tau)\thinspace\mathrm{d}\tau
, where

\omega_{0}
is a positive constant for non-dimensionalisation and

f^{\textrm{PML}}(x)=0
in
x\leq 0
and

f^{\textrm{PML}}(x)>0
in
x>0
.
The outgoing wave solution then becomes

\exp{(-\gamma x)}\!\cdot\!\exp{(-\omega_{0}\int_{-\infty}^{x}f^{\textrm{PML}}(\tau)\thinspace\mathrm{d}\tau)}
,
which coincides with the exact outgoing wave solution
\exp{(-\gamma x)}

in
x<0
and decays exponentially as
x\rightarrow+\infty
.
For convenience, we here introduce a set of grid points,

\{x_{-1},x_{0},\cdots,x_{L}\}
, where

-\delta<x_{-1}<x_{0}=0<x_{1}<\cdots<x_{L}
.
The above observation justifies one in truncating the right half-space
x>x_{L}

for
x_{L}
sufficiently large such that
u^{\dagger}(x_{L})\approx 0
.
We thus obtain the PMLs formulation to be solved for
u^{\textrm{PML}}(x)

up to one undetermined coefficient as:

(12a)

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma^{2}}{g^{\textrm{PML}}}u^{\textrm{PML}}-(g^{\textrm{PML}}u^{\textrm{PML}}_{,x})_{,x}

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{ in }x_{-1}<x<x_{L},

(12b)

\displaystyle u^{\textrm{PML}}(x_{L})

\displaystyle=0,

(
12b
) is called the
termination condition
.
(
12a
) can readily be solved up to two undetermined
coefficients,
c^{\textrm{PML}}_{-}
and
c^{\textrm{PML}}_{+}
, as:

(13)

\displaystyle u^{\textrm{PML}}(x)=c^{\textrm{PML}}_{-}e^{+\gamma\tilde{\chi}(x)}+c^{\textrm{PML}}_{+}e^{-\gamma\tilde{\chi}(x)}.

Applying (
12b
), one can solve (
12
)
up to one undetermined coefficient as desired.
The reflection coefficient of the solution thus obtained is given as:

(14)

\displaystyle c^{\textrm{PML}}_{-}=-\left(e^{-\gamma\tilde{\chi}(x_{L})}\right)^{2}\!\cdot\!c^{\textrm{PML}}_{+},

and the solution satisfies the following approximate Sommerfeld radiation condition:

(15)

\displaystyle u^{\textrm{PML}}_{,x}(0)+\gamma\left(\frac{1+\left(e^{-\gamma\tilde{\chi}(x_{L})}\right)^{2}}{1-\left(e^{-\gamma\tilde{\chi}(x_{L})}\right)^{2}}\right)u^{\textrm{PML}}(0)=0.

In the spatially continuous setting

\exp{(-\gamma\tilde{\chi}(x_{L}))}^{2}

can be made as small as desired by manipulating
\tilde{\chi}(x)
, and
the expression for the reflection coefficient (
14
) and
the approximate Sommerfeld radiation condition (
15
)
can be made arbitrarily close to the exact expressions
(
6
) and (
7
).
As mentioned in Sec.
5
, however, this will no longer be the case
after discretisation.
Finally, we note that,
if
g^{\textrm{PML}}(x_{L})u^{\textrm{PML}}_{,x}(x_{L})+\gamma u^{\textrm{PML}}(x_{L})=0
,
which we call the
transformed Sommerfeld radiation condition
,
was applied at
x=x_{L}
in place of the termination condition (
12b
),
we would obtain
c^{\textrm{PML}}_{-}=0
and

u^{\textrm{PML}}(x)
would represent the exact outgoing/decaying wave solution in
x<0

despite the existence of the PMLs.

4.
Layerwise constant perfectly matched layers

Before introducing the new ABCs,
it is instructive to study the
layerwise constant perfectly matched layers
.
We first define a layerwise constant complex function,
g^{\textrm{LWC}}(x)
, as:

(16)

\displaystyle g^{\textrm{LWC}}(x):=\gamma_{l}\quad\textrm{ in }x_{l-1}<x\leq x_{l},\quad l\in\{0,\cdots,L\},

where
\gamma_{l}\in\mathbb{C}
is a complex constant and
the superscript
(\cdot)^{\textrm{LWC}}
is used
to represent layerwise constant PMLs.

\gamma_{0}
is defined to be
1
, and

\gamma_{l}
,
l\in\{1,\cdots,L\}
, is chosen so that
\text{Re}(\gamma/\gamma_{l})>0
;
this is achieved by letting
\gamma_{l}=a\cdot s+b
for some
a,b>0
,
according to Property (
4b
).

We solve a problem that is similar to (
12
), but
explicitly handling the discontinuities of
g^{\textrm{LWC}}(x)
.
Specifically, we define
u^{\textrm{LWC}}(x)
as:

(17)

\displaystyle u^{\textrm{LWC}}(x):=u^{\textrm{LWC}}_{l}(x)\quad\textrm{ on }[x_{l-1},x_{l}],\quad l\in\{0,\cdots,L\},

solving the following system of equations for
u^{\textrm{LWC}}_{l}(x)
,
l\in\{0,\cdots,L\}
,
which is assumed to be sufficiently smooth
in
(x_{l-1}-\epsilon,x_{l}+\epsilon)
for some
\epsilon>0
:

(18a)

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma^{2}}{\gamma_{l}}u^{\textrm{LWC}}_{l}-(\gamma_{l}u^{\textrm{LWC}}_{l,x})_{,x}

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{ in }(x_{l-1},x_{l}),

\displaystyle\hskip-40.0pt\forall l\in\{0,\cdots,L\},

(18b)

\displaystyle\gamma_{l}u^{\textrm{LWC}}_{l,x}(x_{l})-\gamma_{l+1}u^{\textrm{LWC}}_{l+1,x}(x_{l})

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\hskip-40.0pt\forall l\in\{0,\cdots,L-1\},

(18c)

\displaystyle u^{\textrm{LWC}}_{l}(x_{l})-u^{\textrm{LWC}}_{l+1}(x_{l})

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\hskip-40.0pt\forall l\in\{0,\cdots,L-1\},

(18d)

\displaystyle u^{\textrm{LWC}}_{L}(x_{L})

\displaystyle=0.

Once we solve (
18a
) up to two undetermined coefficients
for each
l
,
we will be left with a system of
L+L+1=2(L+1)-1
equations
for
2(L+1)
unknowns.

4.1.
Domain equations

One can readily solve (
18a
) for
u^{\textrm{LWC}}_{l}(x)

for each
l\in\{0,\cdots,L\}
up to two undetermined coefficients,

c^{\textrm{LWC}}_{l,\textrm{even}}
and
c^{\textrm{LWC}}_{l,\textrm{odd}}
, as:

(19)

\displaystyle u^{\textrm{LWC}}_{l}(x)=c^{\textrm{LWC}}_{l,\textrm{even}}\!\cdot\!\left(\frac{e^{+\frac{\gamma}{\gamma_{l}}\bigl(x-x_{l-\frac{1}{2}}\bigr)}+e^{-\frac{\gamma}{\gamma_{l}}\bigl(x-x_{l-\frac{1}{2}}\bigr)}}{2}\right)+c^{\textrm{LWC}}_{l,\textrm{odd}}\!\cdot\!\left(\frac{e^{+\frac{\gamma}{\gamma_{l}}\bigl(x-x_{l-\frac{1}{2}}\bigr)}-e^{-\frac{\gamma}{\gamma_{l}}\bigl(x-x_{l-\frac{1}{2}}\bigr)}}{2}\right),

where
x_{l-\frac{1}{2}}=(x_{l-1}+x_{l})/2
.
We are now left with
2(L+1)-1
equations for
2(L+1)
unknowns.

4.2.
Interface conditions

Using (
19
),
(
18c
) and (
18b
)
can be written as:

(20)

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}S^{\textrm{LWC}}_{\textrm{even}}(\alpha_{l})&S^{\textrm{LWC}}_{\textrm{odd}}(\alpha_{l})\\
S^{\textrm{LWC}}_{\textrm{odd}}(\alpha_{l})&S^{\textrm{LWC}}_{\textrm{even}}(\alpha_{l})\end{pmatrix}\hskip-5.0pt\begin{pmatrix}c^{\textrm{LWC}}_{l,\textrm{even}}\\
c^{\textrm{LWC}}_{l,\textrm{odd}}\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}\phantom{-\hskip-2.0pt}S^{\textrm{LWC}}_{\textrm{even}}(\alpha_{l+1})&\hskip-10.0pt-\hskip-2.0ptS^{\textrm{LWC}}_{\textrm{odd}}(\alpha_{l+1})\\
-\hskip-2.0ptS^{\textrm{LWC}}_{\textrm{odd}}(\alpha_{l+1})&\hskip-10.0pt\phantom{-\hskip-2.0pt}S^{\textrm{LWC}}_{\textrm{even}}(\alpha_{l+1})\end{pmatrix}\hskip-5.0pt\begin{pmatrix}c^{\textrm{LWC}}_{l+1,\textrm{even}}\\
c^{\textrm{LWC}}_{l+1,\textrm{odd}}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\end{pmatrix},

where
S^{\textrm{LWC}}_{\textrm{even}}(\alpha_{l})
and
S^{\textrm{LWC}}_{\textrm{odd}}(\alpha_{l})
are
defined as:

(21a)

\displaystyle S^{\textrm{LWC}}_{\textrm{even}}(\alpha_{l}):

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{+\frac{\alpha_{l}}{2}}+e^{-\frac{\alpha_{l}}{2}}}{2},

(21b)

\displaystyle S^{\textrm{LWC}}_{\textrm{odd}}(\alpha_{l}):

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{+\frac{\alpha_{l}}{2}}-e^{-\frac{\alpha_{l}}{2}}}{2},

where:

(22)

\displaystyle\alpha_{l}:=\frac{\gamma}{\gamma_{l}}h_{l},

where
h_{l}:=x_{l}-x_{l-1}
.
Note that
\gamma_{0}=1
and we chose

\gamma_{l}
,
l\in\{1,\cdots,L\}
, so that
\text{Re}(\alpha_{l})>0
.
Performing eigendecompositions of the matrices appearing
in (
20
), we have:

(23a)

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}S^{\textrm{LWC}}_{\textrm{even}}(\alpha_{l})&S^{\textrm{LWC}}_{\textrm{odd}}(\alpha_{l})\\
S^{\textrm{LWC}}_{\textrm{odd}}(\alpha_{l})&S^{\textrm{LWC}}_{\textrm{even}}(\alpha_{l})\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\Phi\begin{pmatrix}e^{+\frac{\alpha_{l}}{2}}&\hskip-5.0pt0\\
0&\hskip-5.0pte^{-\frac{\alpha_{l}}{2}}\end{pmatrix}\Phi^{\textrm{T}},

(23b)

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\phantom{-\hskip-2.0pt}S^{\textrm{LWC}}_{\textrm{even}}(\alpha_{l+1})&\hskip-10.0pt-\hskip-2.0ptS^{\textrm{LWC}}_{\textrm{odd}}(\alpha_{l+1})\\

[

we obtain the following identity that is similar to (
81
):

(83)

\displaystyle c_{1,-}^{\textrm{ABC}}=-\left(\frac{{}_{1}F_{1}^{N}(-\alpha_{1})}{{}_{1}F_{1}^{N}(+\alpha_{1})}\right)\prod_{l=2}^{L}\left(\frac{{}_{1}F_{1}^{N}(-\alpha_{l})}{{}_{1}F_{1}^{N}(+\alpha_{l})}\right)^{2}\!\cdot\!c_{1,+}^{\textrm{ABC}}.

The approximation to
u_{0,x}^{\textrm{ABC}}(0)
provided by the proposed ABCs,
which is weakly enforced to the problem solved in the physical domain
x<0
,
is denoted by
[u_{0,x}^{\textrm{ABC}}(0)]_{\textrm{weak}}
, and is given as:

(84)

\displaystyle[{u_{,x}^{\textrm{ABC}}}(0)]_{\textrm{weak}}=-\fint_{x_{0}}^{x_{1}}\frac{\gamma^{2}}{\gamma_{1}}u_{1}^{\textrm{ABC}}w_{0}^{\textrm{\tiny hat}}+\gamma_{1}u_{1,x}^{\textrm{ABC}}w_{0,x}^{\textrm{\tiny hat}}\thinspace\mathrm{d}x.

Using (
70b
) with
l=1
along with
(
48c
) with
l=0
and
using the second half of (
64
) with
l=0
, we obtain:

(85a)

\displaystyle u^{\textrm{ABC}}_{0}(0)

\displaystyle=S_{\textrm{even}}^{\textrm{ABC}}(\alpha_{1})\!\cdot\!c_{1,\textrm{even}}^{\textrm{ABC}}-S_{\textrm{odd}}^{\textrm{ABC}}(\alpha_{1})\!\cdot\!c_{1,\textrm{odd}}^{\textrm{ABC}},

(85b)

\displaystyle[u_{0,x}^{\textrm{ABC}}(0)]_{\textrm{weak}}

\displaystyle=-\gamma\left(S_{\textrm{odd}}^{\textrm{ABC}}(\alpha_{1})\!\cdot\!c_{1,\textrm{even}}^{\textrm{ABC}}-S_{\textrm{even}}^{\textrm{ABC}}(\alpha_{1})\!\cdot\!c_{1,\textrm{odd}}^{\textrm{ABC}}\right).

Using (
75
), and noting (
66
), one can rewrite
(
85
) in terms of

c_{1,-}^{\textrm{ABC}}
and
c_{1,+}^{\textrm{ABC}}
as:

(86a)

\displaystyle u^{\textrm{ABC}}_{0}(0)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}{}_{1}F_{1}^{N}(-\alpha_{1})\hskip 5.0pt{}_{1}F_{1}^{N}(+\alpha_{1})\end{pmatrix}\hskip-3.0pt\begin{pmatrix}c_{1,-}^{\textrm{ABC}}\\
c_{1,+}^{\textrm{ABC}}\end{pmatrix}.

(86b)

\displaystyle[u_{0,x}^{\textrm{ABC}}(0)]_{\textrm{weak}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\gamma\begin{pmatrix}-\hskip-2.0pt{}_{1}F_{1}^{N}(-\alpha_{1})\hskip 5.0pt{}_{1}F_{1}^{N}(+\alpha_{1})\end{pmatrix}\hskip-3.0pt\begin{pmatrix}c_{1,-}^{\textrm{ABC}}\\
c_{1,+}^{\textrm{ABC}}\end{pmatrix}.

Using (
86
) in conjunction with (
83
),
we obtain:

(87)

\displaystyle[{u_{0,x}^{\textrm{ABC}}}(0)]_{\textrm{weak}}+\gamma\left(\frac{1+\prod\limits_{l=1}^{L}\left(\frac{{}_{1}F_{1}^{N}(-\alpha_{l})}{{}_{1}F_{1}^{N}(+\alpha_{l})}\right)^{2}}{1-\prod\limits_{l=1}^{L}\left(\frac{{}_{1}F_{1}^{N}(-\alpha_{l})}{{}_{1}F_{1}^{N}(+\alpha_{l})}\right)^{2}}\right)\hskip-2.0pt{u^{\textrm{ABC}}_{0}}(0)=0,

which can be rewritten noting (
68
) as:

(88)

\displaystyle[{u_{0,x}^{\textrm{ABC}}}(0)]_{\textrm{weak}}+\gamma\left(\frac{1+\prod\limits_{l=1}^{L}\left([N/N]_{\exp{(-\alpha_{l})}}\right)^{2}}{1-\prod\limits_{l=1}^{L}\left([N/N]_{\exp{(-\alpha_{l})}}\right)^{2}}\right)\hskip-2.0ptu^{\textrm{ABC}}_{0}(0)=0,

which compares with (
35
).
As noted in the above, the product terms in (
88
)
decrease as
O(R^{2LN})
for some
R<1
as
L
and
N
increase, and
(
88
) approaches to
the exact Sommerfeld radiation condition (
7
).
The approximation obtained here, i.e., (
88
),
generalises that obtained in
[
24
]
for the PMDLs.

6.4.
Transformed Sommerfeld radiation condition

The analysis performed in
Sec.
6.1
, Sec.
6.2
, and Sec.
6.3

can readily be modified for a problem in which
we apply the transformed Sommerfeld radiation condition,

\gamma_{L}u_{L,x}^{\textrm{ABC}}(x_{L})+\gamma u_{L}^{\textrm{ABC}}(x_{L})=0
, at
x=x_{L}

weakly as the Neumann boundary condition,
in place of the termination condition (
48d
).
Specifically, we enforce the following condition instead of (
48d
):

(89)

\displaystyle\fint_{x_{L-1}}^{x_{L}}\frac{\gamma^{2}}{\gamma_{L}}u_{L}^{\textrm{ABC}}w_{L}^{\textrm{\tiny hat}}+\gamma_{L}u_{L,x}^{\textrm{ABC}}w_{L,x}^{\textrm{\tiny hat}}\thinspace\mathrm{d}x-(-\gamma u_{L}^{\textrm{ABC}}(x_{L})w_{L}^{\textrm{\tiny hat}}(x_{L}))=0,

Using the first half of (
64
) with
l=L
and
(
70a
) with
l=L
,
one can rewrite (
89
) as:

(90)

\displaystyle\gamma\left(S_{\textrm{odd}}^{\textrm{ABC}}(\alpha_{L})\!\cdot\!c_{L,\textrm{even}}^{\textrm{ABC}}+S_{\textrm{even}}^{\textrm{ABC}}(\alpha_{L})\!\cdot\!c_{L,\textrm{odd}}^{\textrm{ABC}}\right)+\gamma\left(S_{\textrm{even}}^{\textrm{ABC}}(\alpha_{L})\!\cdot\!c_{L,\textrm{even}}^{\textrm{ABC}}+S_{\textrm{odd}}^{\textrm{ABC}}(\alpha_{L})\!\cdot\!c_{L,\textrm{odd}}^{\textrm{ABC}}\right)=0,

Using (
75
), and noting (
66
),
one can rewrite (
90
)
in terms of
c_{L,-}^{\textrm{ABC}}
and
c_{L,+}^{\textrm{ABC}}
as:

(91)

\displaystyle\sqrt{2}\gamma\begin{pmatrix}{}_{1}F_{1}^{N}(+\alpha_{L})\hskip 5.0pt0\end{pmatrix}\hskip-3.0pt\begin{pmatrix}c_{L,-}^{\textrm{ABC}}\\
c_{L,+}^{\textrm{ABC}}\end{pmatrix}=0.

Using (
76
) in conjunction with (
91
),
instead of with (
80
),
we obtain:

(92)

\displaystyle c_{0,-}^{\textrm{ABC}}=0\!\cdot\!c_{0,+}^{\textrm{ABC}},

which compares with (
38
), and
implies that (
48
) with (
48d
)
replaced with (
89
)
only admits the outgoing/decaying wave solution.

Following the same lines as the discussion in Sec.
6.3
,
one can show,
without assuming that the
N
-point reduced integration is also used
in the physical domain, that:

(93)

\displaystyle[{u_{0,x}^{\textrm{ABC}}}(0)]_{\textrm{weak}}+\gamma u^{\textrm{ABC}}_{0}(0)=0,

which compares with (
39
),
and implies that the proposed ABCs with
the transformed Sommerfeld radiation condition at
x=x_{L}

would provide the exact Sommerfeld radiation condition
(
7
) at
x=0

for the problem solved in the physical domain.
This property has been discovered for the special case of the PMDLs

[

]
,

7.2.
Three-dimensional examples

In this section we follow the standard approach

[
13
,
24
]

to extend the one-dimensional formulation
of the proposed ABC introduced in Sec.
6

to three dimensions.

We consider a three-dimensional (
d=3
) domain
D^{\textrm{3D}}
defined as

D^{\textrm{3D}}:=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}|\boldsymbol{x}\in\prod_{i\in\{1,2,3\}}(0,B_{i}+Lh)\textrm{ and }\boldsymbol{x}\not\in[0,1]^{3}\}
,
representing a box with a cubic hole,
where
(B_{1},B_{2},B_{3})=(4,2,2)
and

h=2^{-\textrm{ref}}
with
\textrm{ref}\in\mathbb{N}
being the refinement level.
The physical domain is given as

\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}|\boldsymbol{x}\in\prod_{i\in\{1,2,3\}}(0,B_{i})\textrm{ and }\boldsymbol{x}\not\in[0,1]^{3}\}
, and
we are to use an ABC of type
(L,N)
in the artificial domain
in each direction.
We let

\partial D^{\textrm{3D}}_{0}:=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\partial D^{\textrm{3D}}|x_{i}=B_{i}+Lh\textrm{ for some }i\in\{0,1,2\}\}
,

\partial D^{\textrm{3D}}_{1}:=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\partial D^{\textrm{3D}}|\boldsymbol{x}\in[0,1]^{3}\}
, and

\partial D^{\textrm{3D}}_{2}:=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\partial D^{\textrm{3D}}|x_{i}=0\textrm{ for some }i\in\{0,1,2\}\}
.
We consider a mesh
\mathcal{T}^{\textrm{3D}}
of
D^{\textrm{3D}}

composed of cuboids of edge lengths
h
.
We then define function spaces on
\mathcal{T}^{\textrm{3D}}
,

V^{\textrm{3D}}
and
V^{\textrm{3D}}_{0}
, as:

(98a)

\displaystyle V^{\textrm{3D}}:

\displaystyle=\{w\in H^{1}(D^{\textrm{3D}})|w_{|K}\in\mathbb{Q}_{N,d},\forall K\in\mathcal{T}^{\textrm{3D}}\},

(98b)

\displaystyle V^{\textrm{3D}}_{0}:

\displaystyle=\{w\in V^{\textrm{3D}}|w_{|\partial D^{\textrm{3D}}_{0}\cup\partial D^{\textrm{3D}}_{1}}=0\},

where
\mathbb{Q}_{N,d}
is the space of

d
-variate polynomials of degree with respect to each variable at most
N
.
We use the
Q_{N}
tensor-product Lagrange finite element
with the Gauss-Lobatto nodes in each direction.
We then consider the following problem whose solution is to converge to the

exact
solution in the physical domain given as:

(99)

\displaystyle u^{\textrm{3D}}_{\textrm{exact}}=\frac{e^{-s\left|\!\left|x\right|\!\right|}}{\left|\!\left|x\right|\!\right|}:

Find
u^{\textrm{3D}}\in V^{\textrm{3D}}
such that

(100b)

\displaystyle\int_{D^{\textrm{3D}}}\frac{1}{\prod\limits_{i\in\{0,1,2\}}g^{\textrm{3D}}_{i}}\left(s^{2}u^{\textrm{3D}}\overline{w^{\textrm{3D}}}+\sum_{i\in\{0,1,2\}}(g^{\textrm{3D}}_{i})^{2}u_{,i}^{\textrm{3D}}\overline{w_{,i}^{\textrm{3D}}}\right)\thinspace\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}

\displaystyle=0,\quad\forall w^{\textrm{3D}}\in V^{\textrm{3D}}_{0},

(100c)

\displaystyle{u^{\textrm{3D}}}_{|\partial D^{\textrm{3D}}_{0}}

\displaystyle=0,

(100d)

\displaystyle{u^{\textrm{3D}}}_{|\partial D^{\textrm{3D}}_{1}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}(u^{\textrm{3D}}_{\textrm{exact}}),

where, for
i\in\{1,2,3\}
:

\displaystyle g^{\textrm{3D}}_{i}(x_{i}):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\gamma_{0},&x_{i}\leq B_{i},\\
\gamma_{l},&B_{i}+(l-1)h<x_{i}\leq B_{i}+lh,\quad l\in\{1,\cdots,L\},\end{array}\right.

where
\gamma_{0}=1
and:

(103)

\displaystyle\gamma_{l}=\left(\cos\phi_{l}\!\cdot\!s+\frac{\sin^{2}\phi_{l}}{\cos\phi_{l}}\right)\!\cdot\!\frac{1}{N+1}\!\cdot\!h,\quad\phi_{l}\in\left[0,\frac{\pi}{2}\right),\quad l\in\{1,\cdots,L\}.

In (
103
)
the term in the parentheses was proposed in
[
26
]

for the CRBCs for the case corresponding to
N=1

as optimisation of the ABC parameters is outside the scope of this paper,

simply choosing
\phi_{l}
in
[0,\pi/2)
according to the
L
-point
Gauss-Legendre quadrature.
The factors
1/(N+1)
and
h

were introduced specifically for the proposed ABC

normalise

\gamma_{l}

across different values of
N

Although the standard symbol was used for the integral
in (
100b
) for simplicity,
we only use the full integration rule in the physical domain, and
use the
N
-point reduced integration rule in
x_{i}>B_{i}

in the
x_{i}
-direction for each

100c

(

8.
Conclusion and future works

We proposed a new class of ABCs for the Helmholtz equation,
which is parametrised by a tuple
(L,N)
.
The ABCs of type
(L,N)

are derived from the layerwise constant PMLs with
L
absorbing layers
using the
Q_{N}
finite element
in conjunction with the
N
-point Gauss-Legendre quadrature reduced integration.
The proposed ABCs generalise the PMDLs
[
24
]
,
including them as type
(L,1)
,
which is known to outperform the standard PMLs and
to show equivalent performances to the CRBCs
[
26
]
,
in terms of the accuracy and also in terms of the insensitivity
to the choice of the parameters.
The proposed ABCs of type
(L,N)
inherit these favourable properties.
Like the PMDLs, the proposed ABCs only involve a single
Helmholtz-like equation over the entire computational domain,
even in existence of edges and corners.
As a generalisation of the PMDLs,
for any
N\in\mathbb{N}
,
the proposed ABCs allow for a monolithic discretisation of
the physical and the artificial domains
as long as a
Q_{N}
finite element is used in the physical domain.
These features were validated in the one- and three-dimensional
numerical examples.
The analysis presented in this work were motivated by
[
2
]
,
and appreciated the properties of the Jacobi polynomials.
We believe that this analysis gives more insight into the problems
and potentially advance the fields of ABCs in related areas.

We note that, though the ABCs of type
(L,N)
and those of type
(LN,1)

introduce the same number of auxiliary degrees of freedom
in the artificial domain and achieve the same order of accuracy,
the former only involve
L
complex parameters while
the latter involve
LN
;
thus, one will have a finer control of the
absorption profile with the latter, which might be advantageous
if, e.g., one had a good
a priori
knowledge of the target solution;
see also
[
23
]
.
Note also that
the matrix structure of the former is denser than that of the latter
in the artificial domain as the former use the
Q_{N}
finite element and
