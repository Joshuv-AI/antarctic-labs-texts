2-Adic Valuations of Quadratic Sequences


Abstract
We determine properties of the 2-adic valuation sequences for general quadratic polynomials with integer coefficients directly from the coefficients. These properties include boundedness or unboundedness, periodicity, and valuations at terminating nodes. We completely describe the periodic sequences in the bounded case. Throughout, we frame results in terms of trees and sequences.

Introduction

For
p
prime and
n\in\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,2,3,\ldots\}
, the exponent of the highest power of
p
that divides
n
is called the
p
-adic valuation of
n
, which we denote
\nu_{p}(n)
. The valuation of
0
is defined to be
+\infty
. Formally, the valuation of a positive integer
n
of the form
n=p^{k}d
, where
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
d
is an integer not divisible by
p
, is
\nu_{p}(n)=k
. We can find
p
-adic valuations of sequences by finding the valuation of each successive term. The present work considers 2-adic valuations of sequences generated from the natural numbers by evaluating quadratic functions of the form
f(n)=an^{2}+bn+c
where
a,b,c\in\mathbb{Z}
and
a\neq 0
.

Information about sequences of valuations can be viewed in two different ways: in terms of sequences and in terms of trees. We let
(\nu_{2}(f(n)))_{n\geq 0}
denote the sequence of 2-adic valuations for the quadratic function
f(n)
. Since every positive natural number
n
can be written in the form
n=2^{k}d
, where
d
is not divisible by 2, in many cases, we can determine the valuations of outputs of the quadratic function
f(n)
using characteristics of the coefficients
a
,
b
, and
c
. The main results are given in Theorems
1
and
2
; one would anticipate these results can be extended to odd primes with some modifications, which will be addressed in future work.

Theorem 1
.

Let
f(n)=an^{2}+bn+c
where
a,b,c\in\mathbb{Z}
with
a\neq 0
and, without loss of generality,
a,b,c
are not all even. Then

1.

If
a
and
b
are even and
c
is odd, then
\nu_{2}(f(n))=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

2.

If
a
is even and
b
is odd, then
(\nu_{2}(f(n)))_{n\geq 0}
is an unbounded sequence.

3.

If
a
is odd and
b
is even, then

(a)

if
b^{2}-4ac=0
, then
(\nu_{2}(f(n)))_{n\geq 0}
is an unbounded sequence;

(b)

if
b^{2}-4ac=4^{\ell}\Delta
for
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
as large as possible and
\Delta\equiv 1\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ 8\mbox{\rm)}
, then
(\nu_{2}(f(n)))_{n\geq 0}
is an unbounded sequence;

(c)

if
b^{2}-4ac=4^{\ell}\Delta
for
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
as large as possible,

\Delta\equiv m\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ 8\mbox{\rm)}
, and

, then the sequence
(\nu_{2}(f(n)))_{n\geq 0}
is bounded and its minimal period length equals

.

4.

If
a
and
b
are odd and
c
is even, then
(\nu_{2}(f(n)))_{n\geq 0}
is an unbounded sequence.

5.

If
a
,
b
, and
c
are odd, then
\nu_{2}(f(n))=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Theorem
1
is proved in Section
3
. Henceforward, we will refer to the
minimal period length
simply as the
period
. In Case 3, we use the discriminant to determine whether roots to
f(n)=0
lie in the 2-adic numbers
\mathbb{Q}_{2}
or the ring of 2-adic integers
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
. Corollary

takes care of Case 3(a). Even though the statement of this theorem only classifies these sequences as unbounded, the proofs of Cases 2 and 4 reveal more information about the 2-adic valuations. Theorem

represents a complete answer to when

is bounded or unbounded using only the coefficients of the quadratic polynomial. Furthermore, Theorem
1
gives an explicit period length for the bounded sequences which can be determined by the coefficients of the quadratic polynomial. In the unbounded cases we are able to describe what possible valuations will be for certain subsequences. Such statements are easier to frame in the sense of trees, which are discussed in Section
2
. Theorem
2
, proved in Sections

and
5

Parity and trees

Consider a quadratic function of the form
f(n)=an^{2}+bn+c,
where
a,b
and
c
are integers and
a
is nonzero. To prove the results stated in Theorems
1
and
2
, we consider the eight possible cases based on the parity of the coefficients
a
,
b
, and
c
. In the case where
a,b,
and
c
are all even, there exists an
i\in\mathbb{N}
such that
2^{i}
divides
a,b
, and
c
but
2^{i+1}
does not. Then
f(n)=2^{i}(a_{0}n^{2}+b_{0}n+c_{0})
and
it follows that
\nu_{2}(f(n))=i+\nu_{2}(a_{0}n^{2}+b_{0}n+c_{0})
. Hence, this case can be reduced to one of the other seven cases. So we assume, unless stated otherwise, that
a
,
b
, and
c
are not all even.

Two more cases of Theorem
1
are trivial (Case 1 where
a,b
are even, and Case 5, where
a,b,
and
c
are odd), since
\nu_{2}(f(n))=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. For the remaining five cases, we classify the behavior using trees. In the case that
a
is odd and
b
is even we show, with the help of the discriminant, that
f(n)=0
has a root in
\mathbb{Q}_{2}
. We must take some care since some quadratics may not have a zero in
\mathbb{Q}_{2}
.

As discussed in Section
1
, we can present information about the sequence of valuations using a tree. We begin the construction of the tree with the top node representing the valuation of the quadratic
f(n)
evaluated at any natural number
n
. If the
2
-adic valuation is constant for every
n
in this node, then we stop the construction, as
\nu_{2}(n)
is completely determined for the sequence. If
\nu_{2}(n)
is not constant, this node splits into two branches, where one branch represents all numbers of the form
n=2q
and the other branch represents all numbers of the form
n=2q+1
, where in both cases
q\in\mathbb{N}
. We then repeat this step as necessary to create the tree. The nodes correspond to the sets
\{2^{i}q+r_{i-1}|q\in\mathbb{N}\}
where

r_{i-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{i-1}\alpha_{k}2^{k},

(1)

for fixed coefficients
\alpha_{k}\in\{0,1\}
. This process does not always terminate. If it terminates, we say that the tree is
finite
; otherwise, the tree is
infinite
. We say a node is
non-terminating
if
(\nu_{2}(f(n)))_{n\geq 0}
is non-constant for every
n
in that equivalence class. We say a node is
terminating
if
(\nu_{2}(f(n)))_{n\geq 0}
is constant for every
n
in that equivalence class. In practice, we label the node with this constant valuation.

For each of the remaining five nontrivial cases on the parity of the coefficients
a
,
b
and
c
, either
(\nu_{2}(f(n)))_{n\geq 0}
produces a finite tree or an infinite tree. We say a finite tree has
\ell
levels
if there exists
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
such that for all
r_{\ell-1}\in\{0,1,2,\ldots,2^{\ell}-1\}
we have
(\nu_{2}(f(2^{\ell}q+r_{\ell-1})))_{q\geq 0}
constant for all
q\in\mathbb{N}
, and
\ell
is the smallest possible value. Every node at level
\ell
in a finite tree has a constant valuation, which depends on
r_{\ell-1}
.

Each node of a tree represents a subsequence of the sequence of 2-adic valuations. A finite tree of
\ell
levels represents a sequence with period equal to
2^{\ell}
.

In the literature, these finite trees are also called
finite automata.
The sequences generated via the 2-adic valuation are called
2-automatic sequences
and, in particular, the sequences
f(2^{i}q+r)
are known as the
2-kernel sequences.
See Allouche and Shallit’s book
[
1
]
and Bell’s paper
[
2
]
for more details.

2.1
2-adic numbers and selected lemmas

First, we state several well-known lemmas. The first is a well-established fact about the
p
-adic valuation, which can also be defined on the set
\mathbb{Q}
and extends to
\mathbb{Q}_{2}
; see Lemma 3.3.2 in
[
5
]
.

Lemma 3
.

Let
x,y\in\mathbb{Q}
, then
\nu_{p}(xy)=\nu_{p}(x)+\nu_{p}(y)
.

An element
n
in
\mathbb{Q}_{2}
can be represented in the form

n=\sum_{i=k}^{\infty}\alpha_{i}2^{i}

(2)

where
k=-\nu_{2}(n)
and
\alpha_{i}\in\left\{0,1\right\}
for all
i
; it is well-known that this representation is unique.

Lemma
3
and the construction of
\mathbb{Q}_{2}
are well-known
[
5
]
. Medina et al.
[
6
]
provide a useful characterization of the sequence of 2-adic valuations of a polynomial. Before we state the result, we recall the following characterization of the ring of 2-adic integers

\mathbb{Z}_{2}=\left\{n\in\mathbb{Q}_{2}:n=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{i}2^{i}\ \text{where}\ \alpha_{i}\in\left\{0,1\right\}\right\}.

Lemma 4
.

(
[
6
]
, Theorem 2.1) Let
f(n)\in\mathbb{Z}[n]
be a polynomial that is irreducible over
\mathbb{Z}
. Then
(\nu_{2}(f(n)))_{n\geq 0}
is either periodic or unbounded. Moreover,
(\nu_{2}(f(n)))_{n\geq 0}
is periodic if and only if
f(n)
has no zeros in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
. In the periodic case, the minimal period length is a power of
2
.

We assume that the quadratic
f(n)
is irreducible because, if not, by Lemma
3
,

\nu_{p}(f(n))=\nu_{p}(g(n)\cdot h(n))=\nu_{p}(g(n))+\nu_{p}(h(n)),

where
g(n)
and
h(n)
are irreducible.

Therefore, to determine whether
(\nu_{2}(f(n)))_{n\geq 0}
is periodic or unbounded, it suffices to determine if
f(n)
has zeros in
\mathbb{Q}_{2}
and then determine whether the zeros are also in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
.
The following lemmas will be used in Section
3
to identify when the square root of a number is in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
. The version of Hensel’s lemma stated below determines when a polynomial in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}[x]
has zeros in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
. Lemma
6
, which follows from Lemma
5
, specifically determines whether the polynomial
f(x)=x^{2}-a
has zeros in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
.

Lemma 5
.

(Hensel’s lemma,
[
7
, Sec. 6.4]
) Assume that
P\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}[x]
and
x_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}
satisfies

P(x_{0})\equiv 0\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ 2^{n}\mbox{\rm)}

If
\phi=\nu_{2}(P^{\prime}(x_{0}))<n/2
, then there exists a unique zero
\xi
of
P
in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
such that

\xi\equiv x_{0}\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ p^{n-\phi}\mbox{\rm)}\ \text{and}\ \nu_{2}(P^{\prime}(\xi))=\nu_{2}(P^{\prime}(x_{0}))

Lemma 6
.

(
[
7
, Sec. 6.6]
) The function
f(x)=x^{2}-a
has a zero in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\times}
, the set of invertible elements of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, if and only if
a\equiv 1\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ 8\mbox{\rm)}
.

Proof of Theorem
1
: unbounded cases and infinite trees

We now prove Theorem
1
. The main idea is to describe the roots to
f(n)=0
in
\mathbb{Q}_{2}
simply using the quadratic formula, the parity of the coefficients, and the lemmas presented in Section
2.1
. Moreover, according to Lemma
4
, if a zero exists in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, it manifests as an infinite branch in the tree. We begin with Cases 2 and 4.

To this end, note that in Case 2, we can write
a=2r
and
b=2k+1
for some
r,k\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then
an^{2}+bn+c=0
has roots of the form

x=\frac{-2k-1\pm\sqrt{1-8(rc-\beta)}}{4r},

(3)

where
\beta=(k^{2}+k)/2
. Set
j=rc-\beta
.

Also, in Case 4, we can write
a=2r+1
,
b=2k+1
, and
c=2p
. Then
an^{2}+bn+c=0
has roots of the form

x=\frac{-2k-1\pm\sqrt{1-8((2r+1)p-\beta)}}{2(2r+1)},

(4)

where
\beta=(k^{2}+k)/2
. Set
j=(2r+1)p-\beta
. Observe that in either case the roots contain
\sqrt{1-8j}
where
j\in\mathbb{Z}
. Since
\sqrt{1-8j}
is a zero of the function
g(x)=x^{2}-(1-8j)
, by Lemma
6
the zero is in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
.

Notice that both roots (
3
) and (
4
) have an even denominator. We still need to check if these roots are in
\mathbb{Q}_{2}
or
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
. Therefore, in light of Lemma
4
, Case 2 (Proposition
7
) and Case 4 (Proposition
8
) are proved by an inductive argument on the behavior of the tree. It turns out that, in Case 2,
f(n)
has exactly one zero in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
and in Case 4,
f(n)
has two zeros in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
. See Figure
3
in the Appendix for an example of a tree with one infinite branch and Figure

for an example of a tree with two infinite branches.

Proposition 7
.

If
a
is even and
b
is odd, then the 2-adic valuation tree of
f(n)=an^{2}+bn+c
has exactly one infinite branch. Furthermore, the valuation of the terminating node at the
i^{th}
level is
i-1
.

Proof.

Note that this Proposition corresponds to Case 2 of Theorem
1
. Substituting
a=2r
and
b=2k+1
for some
r,k\in\mathbb{Z}
, we get
an^{2}+bn+c=2(rn^{2}+kn)+n+c
. Now suppose that
c
is even. If
n
is even, then
2(rn^{2}+kn)+n+c
is divisible by 2 and so
\nu_{2}(f(2n))\geq 1
. If
n
is odd, then
2(rn^{2}+kn)+n+c
is not divisible by 2 and so
\nu_{2}(f(2n+1))=0
. An analogous argument shows that, for
c
odd,
\nu_{2}(f(2n))=0
and
\nu_{2}(f(2n+1))\geq 1
. Therefore, the conclusion of the proposition is valid at the initial step.

Now, arguing inductively, suppose that
n=2^{i}q+r_{i-1}
is the non-terminating node, that is
\nu_{2}(f(n))\geq i
. So
f(n)\equiv 0\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ 2^{i}\mbox{\rm)}
or
a(2^{i}q+r_{i-1})^{2}+b(2^{i}q+r_{i-1})+c=2^{i}\beta
where
\beta\in\mathbb{Z}
. Consider
f(n)
evaluated at the next level:

a(2^{i+1}q+r_{i-1})^{2}+b(2^{i+1}q+r_{i-1})+c\equiv ar_{i-1}^{2}+br_{i-1}+c\equiv 2^{i}\beta\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ 2^{i+1}\mbox{\rm)},

and

\displaystyle a(2^{i+1}q+2^{i}+r_{i-1})^{2}+b(2^{i+1}q+2^{i}+r_{i-1})+c

\displaystyle\equiv ar_{i-1}^{2}+2^{i}b+br_{i-1}+c

\displaystyle\equiv 2^{i}\beta+2^{i}b\equiv 2^{i}(\beta+b)\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ 2^{i+1}\mbox{\rm)}.

Since
b
is odd it follows that the valuation of one node is
i
and the other is greater than
i
depending on if
\beta
is odd or even. Therefore one node terminates and the other is non-terminating.
∎

Proposition 8
.

If
a
and
b
are odd, and

is even, then the 2-adic valuation tree of
f(n)=an^{2}+bn+c
has two infinite branches. Furthermore, the valuation of the terminating node at the

This proposition addresses Case 4 of Theorem

. Write
a=2r+1
,
b=2k+1
, and

and

. First note that both
a(2q)^{2}+b(2q)+c

In the

a(4q)^{2}+b(4q)+c\equiv c\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ 4\mbox{\rm)}

For the

Proposition 9

a

. Fix

has roots of the form

\Delta\equiv 1\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ 8\mbox{\rm)}
.

If
\Delta<0
then we can naturally write
\Delta=1-8j
where
j\in\left\{1,2,3,\ldots\right\}
.

If
\Delta>0
, then we can write
\Delta=1+8j=1-8(-j)

.

Thus in either case
\Delta=1-8j
where
j\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then

\sqrt{4k^{2}-4ac}=2^{\ell}\sqrt{1-8j}
. By Lemma

\sqrt{1-8j}
is in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
. Furthermore, since the denominator of

5
Structure of finite trees

The section describes the overall structure of finite trees, continuing the discussion of Case 3(c) of Theorem
1
, in which

b
is even,

Definition 17

(Translation operator,
[
4
]
)

For quadratics of the form

\tau^{s}(f)(n)=f(n-s)

, namely
\tau^{s}(f)(n)=a(n-s)^{2}+b(n-s)+c=an^{2}+(b-2as)n+(c+as^{2}-bs)
.

Proposition 18
.

Let the assumptions of Proposition
12
hold for the function
f(n)=an^{2}+bn+c
and suppose

.
Then we have the following relationship

\nu_{2}(f(2^{i}q+r_{i-1}))=\nu_{2}(\tau^{s}f(2^{i}q+(r_{i-1}+s)\bmod{2^{i}})).

That is the valuations
\nu_{2}(f(n))
at the node of the form
n=2^{i}q+r_{i-1}
are moved to the node of the form
n=2^{i}q+(r_{i-1}+s)\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ 2^{i}\mbox{\rm)}
under the operation
\tau^{s}
.

Proof.

Note that finite trees with
\ell
levels correspond to periodic sequences
with a period equal to

. Since
\tau^{s}
is a translation operator, every element in the sequence
(\nu_{2}(f(n)))_{n\geq 0}
is moved over

spaces.
∎

Definition 19

(

-operator)
.

Let

be a positive, odd integer. For quadratics of the form

S^{a^{-1}}(f)(n)=n^{2}+bn+ac
.

In general, the
S
-operator need not output a quadratic function with an integer constant term. However, the present work only applies

to functions whose output has integer coefficients.

(Dilation operator,
[
4
]
)

For quadratics of the form
f(n)=an^{2}+bn+c
we define
\delta^{s}(f)(n)=f(sn)
for

, namely
\delta^{s}(f)(n)=a(sn)^{2}+b(sn)+c
.

.

Proposition 23

Under the assumptions of Proposition

\nu_{2}(f(n))=\begin{cases}0,&\ \text{if}\ n\equiv 0\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ 2\mbox{\rm)};\\
2(i-1),&\ \text{if}\ n\equiv\sum_{k=0}^{i-2}2^{k}\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ 2^{i}\mbox{\rm)}\ \text{with}\ 2\leq i<\ell;\\
2(\ell-1),&\ \text{if}\ n\equiv\sum_{k=0}^{\ell-2}2^{k}\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ 2^{\ell}\mbox{\rm)}\ \text{and}\ m=6,2;\\

Proof.

In light of Lemma

, we know that if a node is non-terminating, then it produces two nodes that either both terminate (i.e., these nodes are at the
\ell^{th}

\displaystyle\nu_{2}(f(n))

\displaystyle=\nu_{2}(an^{2}+2an+c)=\nu_{2}(a(n+1)^{2}-a+c)

\displaystyle=\nu_{2}(a^{2}(n+1)^{2}-a^{2}+ac)=\nu_{2}(a^{2}(n+1)^{2}-4^{\ell-1}(m-8j))

\displaystyle=\nu_{2}(a^{2}(2^{i}q+2^{i-1}+2^{i-2}+\cdots+2+1+1)^{2}-4^{\ell-1}(m-8j))

\displaystyle=\nu_{2}(a^{2}(2^{i}q+2^{i})^{2}-4^{\ell-1}(m-8j))

\displaystyle=\nu_{2}(a^{2}4^{i}(q+1)^{2}-4^{\ell-1}(m-8j)).
