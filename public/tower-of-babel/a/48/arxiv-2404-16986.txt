Piecewise Stochastic Barrier Functions
Rayan Mazouz, Frederik Baymler Mathiesen, Luca Laurenti, Morteza Lahijanian

Abstract
This paper presents a novel stochastic barrier function (SBF) framework for safety analysis of stochastic systems based on piecewise (PW) functions. We first outline a general formulation of PW-SBFs. Then, we focus on PW-Constant (PWC) SBFs and show how their simplicity yields computational advantages for general stochastic systems. Specifically, we prove that synthesis of PWC-SBFs reduces to a minimax optimization problem. Then, we introduce three efficient algorithms to solve this problem, each offering distinct advantages and disadvantages. The first algorithm is based on dual linear programming (LP), which provides an exact solution to the minimax optimization problem. The second is a more scalable algorithm based on iterative counter-example guided synthesis, which involves solving two smaller LPs. The third algorithm solves the minimax problem using gradient descent, which admits even better scalability. We provide an extensive evaluation of these methods on various case studies, including neural network dynamic models, nonlinear switched systems, and high-dimensional linear systems. Our benchmarks demonstrate that PWC-SBFs outperform state-of-the-art methods, namely sum-of-squares and neural barrier functions, and can scale to eight dimensional systems.

Introduction

Autonomous systems deployed in
safety-critical
domains demand thorough verification to ensure reliability. Examples of such systems include autonomous vehicles in human-shared streets
[
1
]
, aerospace systems (from aerial transportation
[
2
]
to space exploration
[
3
,
4
]
), and surgical robotics
[
5
]
. However, this verification process faces major challenges arising from the complexities in dynamics as well as uncertainties due to the physics or randomized algorithms of the system.
One widely employed method to provide necessary safety guarantees is
stochastic barrier functions
(SBFs)
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
.
SBFs provide a lower bound on safety probabilities, hence enabling
safety certification
.
Despite the extensive studies on SBFs, existing tools largely remain limited to simplistic settings, namely simple dynamics with low dimensions.
This work aims to address these limitations by focusing on the development of a theoretical and computational framework for barrier certificate generation tailored to complex systems.

The literature presents two prominent methods for synthesizing SBFs:
Sum-of-Squares (SOS)
optimization
[
8
,
9
]
and
Neural Barrier Function (NBF)

[
10
,
11
]
.
SOS
optimization relies on a predefined
SOS
polynomial template for the SBF and aims to determine its coefficients using convex optimization. While powerful, this method suffers when the system dimensionality is large
[
12
]
or the safe set is non-convex. On the other hand,
NBF
employ neural networks (NNs) to act as barrier functions, addressing the conservatism of
SOS
in non-convex settings. Yet,
NBF
face scalability issues due to the need to verify the NN against the SBF conditions, which is particularly challenging for high-dimensional systems.

To illustrate these challenges, consider the 2-dimensional linear system with additive Gaussian noise in Figure

. Note that the safe set is non-convex. Figures
1(b)

[

In this manuscript, we introduce an SBF framework based on
piecewise
(PW) functions.
We first show a general formulation for PW-SBFs. Then, we focus on PW-Constant (PWC) SBFs, whose simplicity gives rise to an elegant formulation applicable to
general
stochastic systems. Our key insight is that the challenging operations of expectation and function composition required by the martingale condition of SBFs can be significantly mitigated by using constant functions, enabling both simplicity and scalability. Guided by this insight, we prove that the PWC-SBF synthesis problem reduces to a minimax optimization problem, for which we introduce three efficient computational methods.
First, we show that, even for nonlinear stochastic systems, the minimax optimization problem can be reduced to a linear program (LP) via a dual formulation. Second, to enable scalability, we introduce an iterative algorithm that requires solving two smaller LPs based on the
counter-example guided synthesis
(CEGS) principle.
CEGS however may need large memory.
Third, we show that the minimax problem can be solved using a Gradient Descent (GD) algorithm, which admits even better scalability and resolves the memory issue of CEGS. It however poses a challenge in designing a convergence criterion.
The power of PWC-SBFs is illustrated in Figure
1(d)
, where the same safety probability bound of
0.93
as
NBF
is achieved with
69
seconds computation time, i.e., an order of magnitude (
50\times
) faster in computation. Our extensive evaluations demonstrate the efficacy of the proposed methods, outperforming state of the art and scaling to eight dimensional systems.

In summary, the key contributions of this work are:

•

A general formulation for PW-SBFs with a particular focus on PWC-SBFs, providing a simple formulation for safety verification of general stochastic systems.

•

A derivation of the PWC-SBF synthesis problem as a minimax convex optimization problem.

•

Three efficient and scalable computational methods for PWC-SBF synthesis:

–

an LP duality-based approach with a proof of zero-duality gap, i.e., obtaining an exact solution to the minimax problem,

–

a CEGS algorithm with a proof of convergence to the optimal solution in finite time, and

–

a GD algorithm that offers significant reduction in computation time.

•

Extensive case studies to illustrate the performance of the proposed methods against state-of-the-art methods of
SOS
and
NBF
, and an analysis hereof.

Overall, this work offers a significant advancement in SBF synthesis for safety-critical systems, providing both theoretical insights and practical computational methods that enhance reliability and scalability in complex environments.

Related Work

Barrier functions, also known as barrier certificates
[
13
,
14
,
15
]
, offer a formal approach to ensure the safety of dynamical systems, particularly in nonlinear and hybrid models
[
13
,
16
]
.
Works
[
17
,
18
]
establish
the essential role of barrier certificates in providing safety guarantees for closed-loop systems.
The initial studies on barrier functions primarily focused on continuous-time and non-stochastic settings.

Recent works
[
7
,
8
,
19
,
9
,
10
]
consider safety analysis of uncertain systems via stochastic barrier functions (SBFs).
Initially proposed in
[
6
]
, SBFs offer a framework for studying the probabilistic safety of stochastic systems grounded in martingale theory. Building on this foundation,
[
7
]
introduces the concept of continuous-time SBFs, facilitating the construction of barrier certificates without the need for explicit computation of reachable sets. That method enables the handling of polynomial nonlinearities and uncertainty, ultimately providing an upper bound on the probability of unsafety.
Similar to those works, this paper focuses on probabilistic safety of stochastic systems, but assumes discrete-time dynamics.

Discrete-time stochastic system verification using SBFs was initially introduced in
[
8
]
, where the supermartingale condition was relaxed to a c-martingale for finite-time horizon problems. Similar to prior works, these barriers are computed through
SOS
optimization.
While this method offers benefits in terms of problem convexity, it presents significant challenges in scalability and struggles with non-convex safe sets. Moreover, its applicability is confined to systems with polynomial dynamics or a subset thereof.
In
[
19
]
, an alternative to
SOS
is proposed, leveraging counter-example guided inductive synthesis with SMT
[
20
]
or SAT
[
21
]
solvers. These approaches draw from techniques for synthesizing Lyapunov functions
[
22
]
and reachable sets
[
23
]
for switched systems using counter-examples. Those methods, however, cannot ensure termination within finite time.

To extend the
SOS
optimization technique to non-polynomial systems,
[
9
]
proposed a method by using tools from
[
24
]
to find piecewise affine bound on the dynamics.
These bounds are utilized for the verification and control of neural network dynamic models. Works
[
8
,
19
]
propose iterative approaches to synthesize a control strategy to enforce safety of affine-in-control stochastic dynamical systems over finite time horizons. In
[
9
]
, an exact method for the synthesis of safe controllers is proposed by utilizing the
SOS
convexity property of the barrier. That still remains largely limited by the considered class of
SOS
functions.

To address the limitations encountered by
SOS
, neural barrier functions (
NBF
)
[
10
,
11
]
have recently been proposed.

NBF
use the representational power of neural networks (NNs) to train a highly amenable SBF candidate,
which then needs to be verified to establish whether it is a proper certificate.
Work
[
25
]

combines
NBF
with reinforcement learning to
train a deep policy based on a novel formulation of reach-avoid martingales.
A major limitation of these methods is their reliance on NN verification, which suffer from scalability.
This paper aims to address the limitations of the
SOS
and
NBF
approaches in terms of both conservatism and scalability.
Our work mainly builds on the results of
[
26
]
, which establishes the relationship between barriers and dynamic programming. With this insight, we propose a novel certificate synthesis method based on piecewise functions.

Problem Formulation

We consider a stochastic process described by the following stochastic difference equation

\mathbf{x}_{k+1}=f(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{v}_{k}),

(1)

where state
\mathbf{x}_{k}
takes values in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and noise
\mathbf{v}_{k}
is an independent and identically distributed random variable taking values in
\mathbb{R}^{\mathrm{v}}
with associated probability distribution
p_{\mathbf{v}}
. Further,
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{\mathrm{v}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the vector field representing the one step dynamics of System (
1
).
We assume that
f
is almost everywhere continuous.
Intuitively,
\mathbf{x}_{k}
is a general model of a stochastic process with possibly nonlinear dynamics and non-additive noise.

To define a probability measure for System (
1
), for a measurable set
X
,
we define the one-step stochastic kernel
T(X\mid x)
as

T(X\mid x):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\mathrm{v}}}\mathbf{1}_{X}(f(x,v))p_{\mathbf{v}}(dv),

(2)

where
\mathbf{1}_{X}
is the indicator function for set
X
, defined as

\mathbf{1}_{X}(x)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }x\in X\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

It follows that for an initial condition
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

T
induces a unique probability measure
\mathrm{Pr}^{x_{0}}

[
27
]
, such that for measurable sets

X_{0},X_{k}\subseteq X
where

k\in\mathbb{N}
it holds that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}^{x_{0}}[\mathbf{x}_{0}\in X_{0}]=\mathbf{1}_{X_{0}}(x_{0}),

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}^{x_{0}}[\mathbf{x}_{k}\in X_{k}\mid\mathbf{x}_{k-1}=x]=T(X_{k}\mid x).

The definition of
\mathrm{Pr}^{x_{0}}
allows one to make probabilistic statements over the trajectories of System (
1
).
This work seeks to quantify
probabilistic safety
, which together together with its dual,
probabilistic
reachability

[
26
]
, are commonly used to quantify safety for stochastic dynamical systems. Work
[
28
]
presents a generalization of the notion of invariance.

Definition 1
(Probabilistic Safety)

Let
X_{\mathrm{s}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded set representing the safe set,
X_{0}\subseteq X_{\mathrm{s}}
be the initial set, and
N\in\mathbb{N}
be the time horizon. Then,
probabilistic safety
is defined as

P_{\mathrm{s}}(X_{\mathrm{s}},X_{0},N)=\inf_{x_{0}\in X_{0}}\mathrm{Pr}^{x_{0}}[\forall k\in\{0,...,N\},\mathbf{x}_{k}\in X_{\mathrm{s}}].

The problem we consider in this work is as follows.

Problem 1
(Safety Certificate)

Consider a safe set
X_{\mathrm{s}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and an initial set
X_{0}\subseteq X_{\mathrm{s}}
. Then, for a given threshold
\delta_{\mathrm{s}}\in[0,1]
, certify whether, starting from
X_{0}
, System (
1
) remains in
X_{\mathrm{s}}
for
N
time steps with at least probability
\delta_{\mathrm{s}}
, i.e.,

P_{\mathrm{s}}(X_{\mathrm{s}},X_{0},N)\geq\delta_{\mathrm{s}}.

Problem
1
seeks to compute the probability that
\mathbf{x}_{k}
remains within a given safe set, e.g., it avoids obstacles and undesirable states.
Computation of this probability is particularly challenging because System (
1
) is stochastic and
f
can be a nonlinear (and non-polynomial) function. Further, since set
X_{\mathrm{s}}
can be non-convex, the problem is generally non-convex. This implies that convex optimization tools cannot be used out-of-the-box for this problem in an efficient way.

Approach overview

Our approach to Problem
1
is based on SBFs, which are value functions to guarantee a lower bound on the probability of safety in the sense of Problem
1
. We review the theory of SBFs in Section
3
.
To address the inefficiencies of existing synthesis techniques for continuous barrier functions due to, e.g., non-convexity of
X_{\mathrm{s}}
, in Section
4
, we focus on
piecewise
functions.
We show that, based on such functions, barrier certificates can be provided for the safety of System (
1
) by establishing a lower bound on
P_{\mathrm{s}}
. Further, we show in Section
5
that by choosing a piecewise constant function, the synthesis problem reduces to a linear optimization problem, for which we introduce three efficient computational frameworks.

3
Stochastic Barrier Functions

In this section, we provide an overview of stochastic barrier functions.

Consider System (
1
) with safe set
X_{\mathrm{s}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n},
initial set
X_{0}\subseteq X_{\mathrm{s}},
and unsafe set
X_{\mathrm{u}}=\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus X_{\mathrm{s}}
. Then, a function
B:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a
stochastic barrier function
(SBF) if there exist scalars
\eta,\beta\geq 0
such that

\displaystyle B(x)\geq 0\qquad

\displaystyle\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

(3a)

\displaystyle B(x)\geq 1\qquad

\displaystyle\forall x\in X_{\mathrm{u}},

(3b)

\displaystyle B(x)\leq\eta\qquad

\displaystyle\forall x\in X_{0},

(3c)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[B(f(x,\mathbf{v}))]\leq B(x)+\beta\qquad

\displaystyle\forall x\in X_{\mathrm{s}},

(3d)

where
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a random variable with density
p_{\mathbf{v}}
.
Given an SBF
B
, then
a lower bound on the probability of safety for System (
1
) can be obtained according to the following proposition.

Theorem 1
(
[
26
, Theorem 2]
)

If there exists function
B
that satisfies Conditions (
3a
)-(
3d
), then for a horizon
N\in\mathbb{N}
, it follows that

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{s}}(X_{\mathrm{s}},X_{0},N)\geq 1-(\eta+\beta N).

(4)

Note that Equation (
4
) provides a lower bound on the probability of remaining in the safe set.
Hence,
B
is called a barrier certificate
if the right hand side of Equation (
4
) is greater than or equal to threshold
\delta_{\mathrm{s}}
, i.e.,

1-(\eta+\beta N)\geq\delta_{\mathrm{s}}.

A major benefit of using barrier certificates for safety analysis is that static Conditions (
3a
)-(
3d
) enable probabilistic reasoning on the time evolution of the stochastic system without the need to evolve the system.
A popular approach to finding an SBF
B
is to limit the search to the set of
SOS
polynomials of a given degree. Then, a
SOS
optimization problem can be formulated to synthesize the parameters of the barrier polynomial, where the objective function is
\min\eta+N\beta
, subject to Conditions (
3a
)-(
3d
)

[
7
,
8
,
9
]
.
The
SOS
approach however becomes very conservative when
X_{\mathrm{s}}
is non-convex.
Recent techniques address this issue by using the power of neural networks as universal approximators to learn a
NBF

[
10
,
11
]
.
Nevertheless, while training
NBF
s can be done efficiently, checking Conditions (
3a
)-(
3d
) against the network is challenging, which limits scalability of
NBF
s.
To tackle these challenges, we propose piecewise barrier functions below.

4
Piecewise Stochastic Barrier Functions

In this paper, we introduce the notion of
piecewise
SBF (PW-SBF).
Consider a partition
X_{1},\ldots,X_{K}
of safe set
X_{\mathrm{s}}
in
K
compact sets, i.e.,

\bigcup_{i=1}^{K}X_{i}=X_{\mathrm{s}}\quad\text{ and }\quad X_{i}\cap X_{j}=\emptyset,

for all
i\neq j\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
,
such that vector field
f
is continuous in each region
X_{i}
and we further assume that the boundary of each set has measure zero w.r.t.
T(\cdot\mid x)
for any
x\in X_{s}
.
Further, let
B_{i}:X_{i}\to\mathbb{R}
be a real-valued continuous function for every
i\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
. We define PW function

B(x)=\begin{cases}B_{i}(x)&\text{if }x\in X_{i}\\
1&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(5)

The following corollary characterizes the general result of Theorem
1
for the PW function
B
.

Corollary 1
(Piecewise SBF)

Piecewise function
B(x)
in Equation (
5
) is a stochastic barrier function for System (
1
) if
\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
there exist scalars
\beta_{i},\eta\geq 0
such that

\displaystyle B_{i}(x)\geq 0\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\forall x\in X_{i},

(6a)

\displaystyle B_{i}(x)\leq\eta\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\forall x\in X_{i}\cap X_{0},

(6b)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}[B_{j}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\mid\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in X_{j}]\cdot T(X_{j}\mid x)+T(X_{\mathrm{u}}\mid x)

\displaystyle\qquad\;\>\leq B_{i}(x)+\beta_{i}\qquad\qquad\qquad\forall x\in X_{i},

(6c)

where
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=f(x,\mathbf{v})
and

is the transition kernel in Equation (

).
Then, it holds that

P_{\mathrm{s}}(X_{\mathrm{s}},X_{0},N)\geq 1-(\eta+N\cdot\max_{i\in\{1,...,K\}}\beta_{i}).

.

For

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[B(

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}[B_{j}(f(x,\mathbf{v}))\mid f(x,\mathbf{v})\in X_{j}]\cdot T(X_{j}\mid x)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+1\cdot T(X_{\mathrm{u}}\mid x)-B_{i}(x)

enables one to formulate an optimization problem to synthesize a PW-SBF. Specifically, the objective is to find

\eta+N\cdot\max\{\beta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{K}
) subject to Conditions (
6a
)-(
6c
). The benefit of this formulation is that both the size of the partition and type of functions
B_{i}
s (e.g., linear, polynomial, exponential, etc.) are design parameters.
This provides flexibility for the SBF to fit different shapes of
X_{\mathrm{s}}
. This flexibility, however, may introduce challenges as it can lead to a non-convex optimization problem, even for simple choices for
B_{i}
such as polynomial or linear functions.
We introduce a set of simple but effective choices that lend themselves to efficient computational tools that outperform the state of the art SBF synthesis techniques.

A major difficulty in the optimization problem results from the product of the expectation term and the transition kernel function in Condition (
6c
), namely,

\mathbb{E}[B_{j}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\mid\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in X_{j}]\cdot T(X_{j}\mid x).

The first term not only requires to perform an expectation operation, but also a composition of
B_{j}
with the nonlinear function
f
. The second term
T
is a nonlinear function of
x
that involves an integral of probability density function
p_{\mathbf{v}}
.
To reduce complexity, one can choose to use constant values for
B_{i}
s, which simply avoids the need to perform the expectation and composition operations. This leads to a PW Constant (PWC) SBF.
Furthermore, while the analytical form of
T
may be hard to obtain, its bounds can be efficiently computed using, e.g., the procedure in
[
29
]
, for general
f
and non-standard
p_{\mathbf{v}}
(e.g., non-symmetric, non-unimodal, etc.). In the next section, we detail the optimization problem for these choices.

5
Piecewise Constant SBF Synthesis

In this section,
we first formally set up an optimization problem for synthesis of PWC-SBFs.
Then, we introduce three efficient computational methods to solve the optimization problem, namely, a
Linear Programming (LP)
duality-based approach, a
Counter-Example Guided Synthesis (CEGS)
procedure, and a
Gradient Descent (GD)
-based method.

5.1
PWC-SBF Synthesis

For
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
, let
\underline{p}_{ij},\overline{p}_{ij}\in[0,1]
denote the lower and upper bounds of the transition kernel
T(X_{j}\mid x)
for every
x\in X_{i}
, respectively, i.e.,

\displaystyle\quad\underline{p}_{ij}\leq T(X_{j}\mid x)\leq\overline{p}_{ij}\qquad\forall x\in X_{i}.

(11)

Similarly, we use
\underline{p}_{i\mathrm{u}},\overline{p}_{i\mathrm{u}}\in[0,1]
for the bounds of
T
to the unsafe set
X_{\mathrm{u}}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\underline{p}_{i\mathrm{u}}\leq T(X_{\mathrm{u}}\mid x)\leq\overline{p}_{i\mathrm{u}}\qquad\forall x\in X_{i}.

(12)

Note that these bounds can be computed efficiently for general
f
and
p_{\mathbf{v}}
by using, e.g., techniques in

[
30
,
31
,
32

29
]
.
We define the set of all feasible values for the transition kernel for all

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{i}=\Big\{

\displaystyle p_{i}=(p_{i1},\ldots,p_{iK},p_{i\mathrm{u}})\in[0,1]^{K+1}\quad s.t.

\displaystyle\qquad\sum_{j=1}^{K}p_{ij}+p_{i\mathrm{u}}=1,

\displaystyle\qquad\underline{p}_{ij}\leq p_{ij}\leq\overline{p}_{ij}\;\;\;\forall j\in\{1,\ldots,K,\mathrm{u}\}\Big\}.

(13)

Note that
\mathcal{P}_{i}
is a convex polytopic set, specifically a simplex.
The following theorem sets up an optimization problem for synthesis of a PWC-SBF.

Theorem 5.3

(PWC-SBF Synthesis)

Given a

\displaystyle B^{*}=\arg\min_{B\in\mathcal{B}_{K}}\;\max_{(p_{i})_{i=1}^{K}\in\mathcal{P}}\;\eta+N\beta

subject to

\displaystyle b_{i}\geq 0

\displaystyle b_{i}\leq\eta

Given a partition of

For optimality,

5.2
Computation of PWC-SBFs

The optimization problem in Theorem
5.3
is a minimax problem with the decision variables

\displaystyle b=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{K})\in\mathbb{R}^{K}_{\geq 0},

(16a)

\displaystyle p_{i}=(p_{i1},\ldots,p_{iK},p_{i\mathrm{u}})\in\mathcal{P}_{i}

\displaystyle\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,K\},

(16b)

\displaystyle\beta_{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}

\displaystyle\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,K\},

(16c)

\displaystyle\eta,\beta\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}.

(16d)

A major difficulty in computability of this optimization problem is in Constraint (
15c
), which includes products of decision variables
b_{j}
and
p_{ij}
.
As a consequence, the minimax optimization problem in Theorem
5.3
is bilinear, meaning that it is linear if either
b_{j}
or
p_{ij}
are fixed. This class of problems is generally non-convex; hence, convex solvers, which provide efficiency and guaranteed convergence, cannot be utilized.
We propose three approaches to (losslessly) convexify the problem, namely dual Linear Programming (LP), Counter-Example Guided Synthesis (CEGS), and Gradient Decent (GD) such that the optimal solution can be efficiently computed.
Each approach has its own advantages and disadvantages. The dual LP approach allows use of standard LP solvers to exactly compute the optimal solution at the cost of lesser scalability. CEGS mitigates the scalability problem by decoupling the inner maximization and outer minimization, but may still require prohibitive amounts of memory. To address this problem, GD is presented, which utilizes sparsity and provides efficient gradient computation. However, GD requires tweaking of hyperparameters to achieve good convergence in practice, due to the non-smoothness of the objective function. We first present the dual LP approach.

5.2.1
Dual Linear Program

Using duality, we first introduce an exact approach that encodes the optimization problem in Theorem
5.3
as a linear program.
We begin by observing that the set of feasible transition kernels
\mathcal{P}_{i}
in Equation (
13
) is a simplex. Hence, it can be represented as

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{i}=\{p_{i}:H_{i}\,p_{i}\leq h_{i}\},

(17)

where matrix

and vector

13

Next, we define vector

\displaystyle h_{i}^{\top}\lambda_{i}\leq b_{i}+\beta_{i},

(18a)

\displaystyle H_{i}^{\top}\lambda_{i}=\bar{b}.

Since both constraints are linear in the decision variables

Consider the following two optimization problems with decision variables

\displaystyle\min_{z}

\displaystyle\beta_{i}

\displaystyle\beta_{i}

Proof 5.10
.

The constraint

Computational Complexity

The time complexity of a standard primal linear program is
\mathcal{O}(n^{2}m)
where
n
is the number of decision variables and
m
is the number of constraints
[
33
]
.
The program in Theorem

has

where

is the number of regions intersecting with the initial set. Combining the complexity of a linear program with the number of decision variables and constraints for our dual linear program, we get
\mathcal{O}(n^{2}m)=\mathcal{O}(K^{6})
. For many applications this may be prohibitive, hence, in the following subsections, we introduce two iterative approaches that lead to an improved runtime complexity.

5.2.2
Counter-Example Guided Synthesis

Here, we introduce another PWC-SBF synthesis method that is exact and computationally more efficient than the dual LP approach.
The method is based on splitting the minimax problem in Theorem
5.3
into two separate LPs.
One LP generates a candidate PWC-SBF that optimizes the unsafety probability given a set of finite feasible distributions

\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{i}

5.2.3
Projected Gradient Descent

While
CEGS
is faster than the dual approach, it can be memory intensive. To alleviate this and allow better scalability, we present a third method for computing a PWC-SBF, namely, a gradient descent-based approach.

With an abuse of notation, let
\beta_{i}(b)
be martingale gap of region
X_{i}
for a given PWC-SBF defined by vector
b=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{K})
. Specifically,

\beta_{i}(b)=\sup_{p_{i}\in\mathcal{P}_{i}}\max\Big\{0,\;\sum_{j=1}^{K}b_{j}\cdot{p}_{ij}+p_{i\mathrm{u}}-b_{i}\Big\},

(21)

and
\beta(b)=\max_{i}\beta_{i}(b)
. Similarly, we denote

\eta(b)=\max_{i:X_{i}\cap X_{0}\neq\emptyset}b_{i}.

(22)

Then, we define loss (objective) function

\mathcal{L}(b)=\eta(b)+N\cdot\beta(b).

(23)

This loss function indeed describes the objective function of the minimax problem in Theorem
5.3
. Hence, by minimizing
\mathcal{L}(b)
, we solve the minimax problem. Below, we show that
\mathcal{L}(b)
is convex, and hence, we can use a gradient descent-based method for its optimization.
More precisely, our approach is based on projected subgradient descent, since the elements of
b
are constrained to
[0,1]
and the maximization and supremum in Equation (
21
) are not smooth, admitting only subgradients.

Theorem 5.13

(Convexity of
\mathcal{L}(b)
)
.

The objective function
\mathcal{L}(b)
in Equation (
23
) is convex in
b
.

Proof 5.14
.

The proof follows a standard structure from disciplined convex programming, where functions are composed under convexity-preserving operations. We start by proving that
\beta_{i}(b)
is convex in
b
. To this end, observe that
\sum_{j=1}^{K}b_{j}\cdot{p}_{ij}+p_{i\mathrm{u}}-b_{j}
is convex in
b
, invariant to the value
p_{i}
. Next,
\max(0,\cdot)
is a convexity-preserving function, thus
\beta_{i}(b)
is convex in
b
. The finite maximization for both
\eta
and
\beta
are once again convexity-preserving operations and finally, addition is convexity-preserving. This concludes the proof that
\mathcal{L}(b)
is convex in
b
.

Notice that the computation of
\beta_{i}(b)
in Equation (
21
) is equivalent to the inner optimization problem of
CEGS
. Hence, the
counterExDistLP
routine in Alg.
3
(based on O-maximization method) can be used for efficient computation of subgradients of
\beta_{i}
.
One subgradient for
\mathcal{L}(b)
is the following

\nabla_{b}\mathcal{L}(b)=\vec{1}_{\eta}(b)+N\cdot\vec{1}_{\beta}(b),

(24)

where
\vec{1}_{\eta}(b)=\vec{1}_{i}
is a one-hot vector with the 1 being in element
i=\mathrm{argmax}_{i:X_{i}\cap X_{0}\neq\emptyset}b_{i}
, and

\vec{1}_{\beta}(b)=\nabla_{b}\beta_{l}(b)=p_{l}^{*}-\vec{1}_{l},

(25)

where
l=\arg\max_{l}\beta_{l}(b)
, and
p_{l}^{*}=\arg\max_{p_{l}\in\mathcal{P}_{l}}\sum_{j=1}^{K}b_{j}\cdot{p}_{lj}+p_{l\mathrm{u}}-b_{l}
.

A challenge with applying subgradient methods in practice is that small step sizes are required due to the non-smoothness, slowing down the convergence.
We propose to ameliorate this by substituting the maximization in
\mathcal{L}(b)
with an
L_{p}
-norm where
1<p<\infty
.
Specifically, let
\tilde{\eta}(b)=(b_{i_{1}},\ldots,b_{i_{m}})
be a vector of PWC-SBF values corresponding to the regions that overlap with
X_{0}
, and
\tilde{\beta}(b)=(\beta_{1}(b),\ldots,\beta_{K}(b))
be a vector of
\beta(b)_{i}
values. Then, the proposed loss function is

\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(b)=\lVert\tilde{\eta}(b)\rVert_{p}+N\lVert\tilde{\beta}(b)\rVert_{p},

(26)

and the corresponding gradient is

\displaystyle\nabla_{b}\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(b)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\left(\frac{\tilde{\eta}(b)_{k}}{\lVert\tilde{\eta}(b)\rVert_{p}}\right)^{p-1}\vec{1}_{i_{k}}+

(27)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{K}\left(\frac{\tilde{\beta}_{i}(b)}{\lVert\tilde{\beta}(b)\rVert_{p}}\right)^{p-1}\nabla_{b}\beta_{i}(b).

The benefit of this loss is that
L_{p}
-norms are smooth, and that
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(b)\geq\mathcal{L}(b)
for every
p_{i}\in\mathcal{P}_{i}
and all
b
. Furthermore, the relative magnitude is bounded,
\lVert y\rVert_{p}\,/\,\lVert y\rVert_{\infty}\leq\sqrt[p]{r}
for any

.
With the

-norm, we may tune the over-approximation to a trade-off between smoothness and tighter approximating the
L_{\infty}
-norm.
The modified loss, through smoothness, also addresses the issue that each
\mathcal{P}_{i}
often is sparse, i.e.,
\overline{p}_{ij}\approx 0
for many

. Therefore,
\nabla_{b}\mathcal{L}(b)
is often 0 for most regions.
We remark that the modified loss
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(b)
is not smooth due to the maximization and supremum in the computation of
\beta_{i}(b)
.

The projected subgradient descent procedure is described in Alg.
4

27

7
Conclusion

In this work, we introduce a formulation for piecewise (PW) stochastic barrier functions (SBFs). We discuss the flexibility they provide at the cost of increased computational cost. To lay the groundwork, we focus on their simplest form, namely, PW constant (PWC) functions. We show that the synthesis of optimal PWC SBFs reduces to a constrained minimax optimization problem that includes bilinear terms. We further show that PWC SBFs can obtain the same or even higher probabilistic safety guarantees than continuous SBFs.

We also propose three efficient computational methods for solving the minimax problem by relaxing bilinear terms. This is achieved by (i) dual formulation in form of a linear program (LP), (ii) splitting the problem into two separate iterative LPs, where one synthesizes candidate function and the other provide counter examples, and finally (iii) designing a convex loss function that precisely captures the objective function of the minimax problem, enabling the use of gradient descent method.
To show the expressivity of PWC SBFs and scalability of the proposed computational methods, we provide
extensive evaluations on a range of benchmark problems. We also compare performance of the proposed methods against state-of-the-art
SOS
optimization and
NBF
learning. The results clearly show the efficacy and superiority of PWC SBFs. The PWC SBFs provide a new approach to ensuring safety for stochastic systems.

This study raises several interesting research questions for future investigations. One is how to perform adaptive refinement of the pieces of the PWC SBF to reduce computational overhead. Another interesting question is on the complexity of PW linear (and nonlinear) SBFs and whether they provide more expressivity (better safety guarantees) than PWC SBFs.
