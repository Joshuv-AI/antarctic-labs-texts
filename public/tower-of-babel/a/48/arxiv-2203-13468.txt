Asymptotic stability of kink with internal modes under odd perturbation
Scipio Cuccagna, Masaya Maeda

Abstract
We give a sufficient condition, in the spirit of Kowalczyk-Martel-Munoz-Van Den Bosch [ 26 ] , for the local asymptotic stability of kinks under odd perturbations. In particular, we allow the existence of quite general configuration of internal modes. The extension of our result to moving kinks remains an open problem.

1.1
Internal modes, Darboux transform and repulsivity assumption

We consider the Schrödinger operator

\displaystyle L_{1}=-\partial_{x}^{2}+W^{\prime\prime}(H).

(1.12)

By differentiating
H^{\prime\prime}=W^{\prime}(H)
, we obtain
H^{\prime}\in\mathrm{ker}L_{1}
.
Since
H^{\prime}>0
, we have
\mathrm{ker}L_{1}=\mathrm{span}\{H^{\prime}\}
.
By Proposition
1.1
,
W^{\prime\prime}(H)-\omega^{2}
decays exponentially.
Thus,
L_{1}
will have at most finitely many eigenvalues, which, since we are in 1D, are all simple.
We label the eigenvalues corresponding to the odd eigenfunctions as follows:

•

\sigma_{\mathrm{d}}(\left.L_{1}\right|_{L_{\mathrm{odd}}^{2}})=\{\lambda_{j}^{2}\ |\ j=1,\cdots,N\}
with

0<\lambda_{1}<\cdots<\lambda_{N}<\omega
.

We set
\phi_{j}\in L_{\mathrm{odd}}^{2}
to be the corresponding (odd, normalized and
{\mathbb{R}}
-valued) eigenfunctions, i.e.
L_{1}\phi_{j}=\lambda_{j}^{2}\phi_{j}
and
\|\phi_{j}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\left(2\lambda_{j}\right)^{-1}
.

Remark 1.5
.

The repulsivity condition in
[
26
]
implies
\sigma_{\mathrm{p}}\left(L_{1}\right)=\{0\}
and, therefore,
N=0
.

In the following, we assume
N\geq 1
.
The case
N=0
is contained in
[
26
]
.

By the Sturm-Liouville theory
L_{1}
will have a number
\widetilde{N}
, equal to
2N
or
2N+1
, of eigenvalues.
We consider
\widetilde{\lambda}_{j}>0
so that we have
\sigma_{\mathrm{d}}(L_{1})=\{\widetilde{\lambda}_{j}^{2}\}_{j=1}^{\widetilde{N}}
.
In this case we have
\widetilde{\lambda}_{1}=0
and
\lambda_{j}=\widetilde{\lambda}_{2j}
.

We set

\displaystyle\mathbf{L}_{1}:=\mathbf{J}\begin{pmatrix}L_{1}&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-L_{1}&0\end{pmatrix}\ \text{ and }\ \boldsymbol{\Phi}_{j}:=\begin{pmatrix}\phi_{j}\\
-{\rm i}\lambda_{j}\phi_{j}\end{pmatrix}.

(1.13)

This operator
\mathbf{L}_{1}
is relevant here because it is obtained linearizing (
1.2
) at
\mathbf{H}
.
Indeed, substituting
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{H}+\mathbf{r}
into (
1.2
), we have

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{L}_{1}\mathbf{r}+O(\mathbf{r}^{2}).

From now on, we will consider only odd functions. In particular
\mathbf{L}_{1}
will act only on odd in
x
functions.

By direct computation, we see that

\displaystyle\mathbf{L}_{1}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{j}=-{\rm i}\lambda_{j}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{j}\ \text{and}\ \mathbf{L}_{1}\overline{\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{j}}={\rm i}\lambda_{j}\overline{\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{j}}.

(1.14)

We consider

\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{g}\right)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}{{}^{t}\mathbf{f}}(x)\mathbf{g}(x)dx,

(1.15)

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathbf{f},\mathbf{g}\right\rangle=\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits\left(\mathbf{f},\overline{\mathbf{g}}\right)

(1.16)

and the symplectic form

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathbf{J}\mathbf{f},\mathbf{g}\right\rangle.

(1.17)

Notice that
\left\langle\mathbf{J}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{j},\overline{{\rm i}\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{j}\right\rangle=1
.

It is easy to check

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{d}}(\mathbf{L}_{1})=\{\pm{\rm i}\lambda_{j}\ |\ j=1,\cdots,N\}\ \text{and}\ \sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(\mathbf{L}_{1})={\rm i}\left((-\infty,-\omega]\cup[\omega,\infty)\right).

(1.18)

Notice also that
\mathbf{L}_{1}
leaves the following decomposition invariant,

\displaystyle L^{2}_{{\mathrm{odd}}}({\mathbb{R}},\mathbb{C}^{2})=L^{2}_{discr}\oplus L^{2}_{disp}\text{ where }L^{2}_{discr}:=\oplus_{\lambda\in\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{L}_{1})}\ker\left(\mathbf{L}_{1}-\lambda\right),

(1.19)

where
L^{2}_{disp}
is the
\left\langle\mathbf{J}\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle
–orthogonal of
L^{2}_{discr}
.

Thus, the linearized operator
\mathbf{L}_{1}
has neutral eigenvalues, which will create oscillating and non-decaying solutions in the linear level.
Such oscillations will last for long time in the full nonlinear problem, they will loose energy and oscillations will eventually decay.
The Fermi Golden Rule (FGR) non-degeneracy condition, which will be introduce in the next subsection, guarantees such phenomenon, but it has to be combined with dispersion of the continuous modes. To prove dispersion we use virial estimates of Kowalczyk et al.
[
26
]
. For this we need to assume that the potential
W^{\prime\prime}(H)
is “repulsive” after a series of Darboux transforms which eliminate the eigenvalues, as we explain now.
The discussion is similar to
[
8
]
, which was based on
[
11
]
.

1.1.1
Darboux Transformations

We inductively define the Schrödinger operator

(j=1,\cdots,\widetilde{N}+1)

(

1.

L_{1}=-\partial_{x}^{2}+W^{\prime\prime}(H)

\displaystyle L_{1}=A_{1}A_{1}^{*},

\displaystyle L_{2}:=A_{1}^{*}A_{1}.

2.

Inductively, given

and we define

\displaystyle L_{k+1}:=A_{k}^{*}A_{k}-\widetilde{\lambda}_{k}^{2}

3.

In the last step,
L_{\widetilde{N}+1}:=A_{\widetilde{N}}^{*}A_{\widetilde{N}}-\widetilde{\lambda}_{\widetilde{N}}^{2}

We set

\displaystyle L_{D}=L_{\widetilde{N}+1}=-\partial_{x}^{2}+V_{D}\text{ where, here, }V_{D}-\omega^{2}\in\mathcal{S}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{R}}).

(1.22)

For the above we refer to Section 3 of

Then, by simple computation we obtain.

(1.24)

We assume that

is repulsive, in the following sense:

for all

1.2
Refined profile and Fermi Golden Rule assumption

As in the asymptotic stability of solitons for nonlinear Schrödinger equations
[
8
]
, we introduce the notion of

.

We introduce some notation.
For
\mathbf{m}=(\mathbf{m}_{+},\mathbf{m}_{-})\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{2N}
, where
{\mathbb{N}}_{0}:={\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}

\overline{\mathbf{m}}=(\mathbf{m}_{-},\mathbf{m}_{+})
and
|\mathbf{m}|=\sum_{j=1}^{N}(m_{+j}+m_{-j})
.
We set
\mathbf{e}^{j}=(\delta_{j1},\cdots,\delta_{jN},0,\cdots,0)
.
We set

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\lambda}:=(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{N},-\lambda_{1},\cdots,-\lambda_{N}),

(1.25)

and

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\lambda}\cdot\mathbf{m}:=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\lambda_{j}(m_{+j}-m_{-j}).

(1.26)

We assume the following.

Assumption 1.8
.

For
M
be the largest number in
{\mathbb{N}}
such that
(M-1)\lambda_{1}<\omega
, then for a multi–index

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{m}\|\leq M\Longrightarrow\left(\mathbf{m}\cdot\boldsymbol{\lambda}\right)^{2}\neq\omega.

(1.27)

We also assume that for
\mathbf{m}=(\mathbf{m}_{+},\mathbf{m}_{-})\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{2N}

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{m}\|\leq 2M\text{ and }\mathbf{m}\cdot\boldsymbol{\lambda}=0\Longrightarrow\mathbf{m}_{+}=\mathbf{m}_{-}.

(1.28)

As in
[
8
]
, we set

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}

\displaystyle:=\{\mathbf{m}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{2N}\ |\ |\boldsymbol{\lambda}\cdot\mathbf{m}|>\omega\},

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{min}}

\displaystyle:=\{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbf{R}\ |\ \not\exists\mathbf{n}\in\mathbf{R}\ \mathrm{s.t.}\ \mathbf{n}\prec\mathbf{m}\},

\displaystyle\mathbf{I}

\displaystyle:=\{\mathbf{m}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{2N}\ |\ \exists\mathbf{n}\in\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{min}},\ \mathbf{n}\prec\mathbf{m}\}

\displaystyle\mathbf{NR}

\displaystyle:={\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{2N}\setminus(\mathbf{I}\cup\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{min}}),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{j}

\displaystyle:=\{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbf{NR}\ |\ \boldsymbol{\lambda}\cdot\mathbf{m}=\lambda_{j}\}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{0}

\displaystyle:=\{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbf{NR}\ |\ \boldsymbol{\lambda}\cdot\mathbf{m}=0\},

where the partial order

\displaystyle\mathbf{n}\prec\mathbf{m}\ \Leftrightarrow\ \forall j,n_{+j}+n_{-j}\leq m_{+j}+m_{-j}\ \text{and}\ |\mathbf{n}|<|\mathbf{m}|.

.

The following facts hold.

\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{min}}

If

Obviously, from the 1st claim it follows that if

If

\displaystyle=\pi\left\langle\delta(L_{1}-r_{\mathbf{m}}),|-{\rm i}r_{\mathbf{m}}(P_{c}\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{m}})_{1}+(P_{c}\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{m}})_{2}|^{2}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{r_{\mathbf{m}}}}\sum_{\pm}\left|\left[-{\rm i}r_{\mathbf{m}}\widehat{(P_{c}\mathcal{R}}_{\mathbf{m}})_{1}+\widehat{(P_{c}\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{m}})}_{2}\right](\pm\sqrt{r_{\mathbf{m}}})\right|^{2}\geq 0.

where
(P_{c}\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{m}})_{j}
are the two components of
P_{c}\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{m}}
for
j=1,2
and we are taking the distorted Fourier transform associated to operator
L_{1}
, for which we refer to Weder
[
46
]
and Deift and Trubowitz
[
11
]
.
By Assumption
1.12
there is a fixed
\Gamma>0
such that

\displaystyle\frac{\pi\boldsymbol{\lambda}\cdot\mathbf{m}}{2\sqrt{r_{\mathbf{m}}}}\sum_{\pm}\left|\left[-{\rm i}r_{\mathbf{m}}\widehat{(P_{c}\mathcal{R}}_{\mathbf{m}})_{1}+\widehat{(P_{c}\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{m}})}_{2}\right](\pm\sqrt{r_{\mathbf{m}}})\right|^{2}\geq\Gamma>0\text{ for all }\mathbf{m}\in\mathbf{R}_{\min}.

(9.24)

Hence we conclude

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{min}}}\boldsymbol{\lambda}\cdot\mathbf{m}|\mathbf{z}^{\mathbf{m}}|^{2}\left\langle\mathbf{J}P_{c}R^{+}_{{\rm i}\mathbf{L}_{1}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\cdot\mathbf{m})\chi_{B^{2}}\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{m}},\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{m}}\right\rangle\geq\Gamma\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{min}}}\boldsymbol{\lambda}\cdot\mathbf{m}|\mathbf{z}^{\mathbf{m}}|^{2}.

(9.25)

So we have expanded the integral in the left hand side of (
9.5
) as a sum of terms which are
o_{\varepsilon}(1)\epsilon^{2}
plus the integral in
(0,t)
of the left hand side of (
9.25
).
We conclude

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{min}}}\|\mathbf{z}^{\mathbf{m}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(I)}=o_{\varepsilon}(1)\epsilon^{2},

completing the proof of Proposition
3.7
. ∎

10
Repulsivity of the
\phi^{8}
model near the
\phi^{4}
model

In this section, we study that the following nonlinear potential,

\displaystyle W_{\epsilon}(u):=\frac{1}{4}(1+\epsilon)^{2}\left(u^{2}-1\right)^{2}\left(\epsilon u^{2}-1\right)^{2},\ \epsilon\in[0,1),

which appears in the
\phi^{8}
theory.
Notice that when
\epsilon=0
,
W_{0}
is the nonlinear potential of the
\phi^{4}
theory.
It was shown by
[
26
]
that for
2-\sqrt{3}\leq\epsilon<1
,
L_{2,\epsilon}
has repulsive potential, in the sense of the definition in
[
26
]
.
Here,
L_{1,\epsilon}
is given by
-\partial_{x}^{2}+W_{\epsilon}^{\prime\prime}(H_{\epsilon})
with
H_{\epsilon}
the odd kink satisfying
H_{\epsilon}^{\prime\prime}=W^{\prime}(H_{\epsilon})
and
L_{j,\epsilon}
given by Darboux transformations in section
1.1.1
.

Recall that the potential
V_{2,\epsilon}
of the 1st transformed operator
L_{2,\epsilon}=-\partial_{x}^{2}+V_{2,\epsilon}
is given by
-W_{\epsilon}^{\prime\prime}(H_{\epsilon})+\frac{(W_{\epsilon}^{\prime}(H_{\epsilon}))^{2}}{W_{\epsilon}(H_{\epsilon})}
.
So, to check the repulsivity of
V_{2,\epsilon}
, one only needs to study the function
-W_{\epsilon}^{\prime\prime}(x)+\frac{(W_{\epsilon}^{\prime}(x))^{2}}{W_{\epsilon}(x)}
in the domain
x\in[-1,1]
because
H_{\epsilon}
is monotone.
This was the very nice observation of
[
26
]
.

On the other hand, when
\epsilon=0
,
L_{1,0}
has two eigenvalues (
0
and
\frac{3}{2}
), so
L_{2,0}
is not repulsive and the 2nd transformed operator
L_{3,0}=-\partial_{x}^{2}+2
has a flat potential, which lies in the boundary of repulsive potential and is not a repulsive potential in our definition, Assumption
1.6
).

Since it seems that as
\epsilon
increases, the number of eigenvalues decreases, it is natural to expect that
V_{3,\epsilon}
is repulsive for
\epsilon\in(0,\epsilon_{*})
for the first
\epsilon_{*}>0
when
L_{1,\epsilon_{*}}
stops to have two eigenvalues.
We will confirm this observation by computing the 1st order expansion of
V_{3,\epsilon}=2+\epsilon\widetilde{V}_{3}+O(\epsilon^{2})
and by numerical computation.
First,
\widetilde{V}_{3}
can be computed explicitly.

Proposition 10.1
.

We have

\displaystyle\widetilde{V}_{3}=\frac{6}{5}{\mathrm{sech}}^{2}\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{2}}\right)+\frac{3}{5}{\mathrm{sech}}^{4}\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{2}}\right).

(10.1)

In particular, we have
x\widetilde{V}_{3}^{\prime}(x)<0
for
x\neq 0
.

Next, the result of numerically computation of
V_{3,\epsilon}
is given by the following graph.

Figure 1:
Graph of
V_{3,\epsilon}-2+4\epsilon^{2}-2\epsilon^{4}
generated by numerically computing
H_{\epsilon}
and
\varphi_{\epsilon}
.
The case
\epsilon=0
is not visible because it is flat.

Proof of Proposition
10.1
.

First, by multiplying
H_{\epsilon}^{\prime}
to
H_{\epsilon}^{\prime\prime}=W_{\epsilon}^{\prime}(H_{\epsilon})
and integrating it, we have

\displaystyle H_{\epsilon}^{\prime}=\sqrt{2W_{\epsilon}(H_{\epsilon})},

which gives a implicit representation of the kink
H_{\epsilon}
by

\displaystyle x=\int_{0}^{H_{\epsilon}}\frac{dh}{\sqrt{2W_{\epsilon}(h)}}.

The above formula holds for any nonlinear potential
W
.
In our case, we can compute the integral in the right hand side and obtain

\displaystyle\sqrt{2}(1-\epsilon^{2})x=\log(1+H)-\log(1-H)+\sqrt{\epsilon}\log(1-\sqrt{\epsilon}H)-\sqrt{\epsilon}\log(1+\sqrt{\epsilon}H)

(10.2)

When
\epsilon=0
, we can solve (
10.2
) w.r.t.
H_{0}
and obtain the
\phi^{4}
-kink:

\displaystyle H_{0}=\tanh\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{2}}\right)

(10.3)

Differentiating, (
10.2
) w.r.t.
\epsilon
, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{\epsilon}H_{\epsilon}=\frac{\left(1-H_{\epsilon}^{2}\right)\left(1-\epsilon H_{\epsilon}^{2}\right)}{2(1-\epsilon)}\left(-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\epsilon}}\left(\log(1-\sqrt{\epsilon}H_{\epsilon})-\log(1+\sqrt{\epsilon}H_{\epsilon})\right)+\frac{H_{\epsilon}}{1-\epsilon H_{\epsilon}^{2}}-2\sqrt{2}\epsilon x\right),

and by
\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\log(1+hH_{\epsilon})}{h}=H_{\epsilon}\left.\frac{d}{dh}\right|_{h=0}\log(1+h)=H_{\epsilon}
, we have

\displaystyle\left.\partial_{\epsilon}\right|_{\epsilon=0}H_{\epsilon}=H_{0}(1-H_{0}^{2}).

(10.4)

We set
\psi_{\epsilon}:=\psi_{0}+\epsilon\widetilde{\psi}_{\epsilon}
, with
\widetilde{\psi}_{\epsilon}\perp\psi_{0}
, to be the eigenfunction of

\displaystyle L_{\epsilon}:=-\partial_{x}^{2}+W_{\epsilon}^{\prime\prime}(H_{\epsilon}),

associated to the eigenvalue
\lambda_{\epsilon}=\frac{3}{2}+\epsilon\widetilde{\lambda}_{\epsilon}
, where

\displaystyle\psi_{0}(x):=\left(\frac{9}{8}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}\frac{\sinh(\frac{x}{\sqrt{2}})}{\cosh^{2}(\frac{x}{\sqrt{2}})}

(10.5)

is the normalized eigenvector of
L_{0}
satisfying
L_{0}\psi_{0}=\frac{3}{2}\psi_{0}
.

Remark 10.2
.

By the stability of eigenvalues,
L_{\epsilon}
has a unique eigenvalue near
\frac{3}{2}
.

We set
A_{1,\epsilon}=(H_{\epsilon}^{\prime})^{-1}\partial_{x}\left(H_{\epsilon}^{\prime}\cdot\right)
and
\varphi_{\epsilon}=A_{1,\epsilon}^{*}\psi_{\epsilon}
.
Since the 2nd transformed potential
V_{3,\epsilon}
is given by

\displaystyle V_{3,\epsilon}=\tilde{V}_{2,\epsilon}(H_{\epsilon})-2\left(\frac{\varphi_{\epsilon}^{\prime}}{\varphi_{\epsilon}}\right)^{\prime},

(10.6)

with
\tilde{V}_{2,\epsilon}
is given by
V_{2,\epsilon}=\tilde{V}_{2,\epsilon}(H_{\epsilon})
, which can be explicitly written as

\displaystyle\tilde{V}_{2,\epsilon}(x)

\displaystyle=-W_{\epsilon}(x)(\log W_{\epsilon}(x))^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=(1+3\epsilon+3\epsilon^{2}+\epsilon^{3})+(1-2\epsilon-6\epsilon^{2}-2\epsilon^{3}+\epsilon^{4})x^{2}-\epsilon(1+\epsilon)^{3}x^{4}+2\epsilon^{2}(1+\epsilon)^{2}x^{6},

it suffices to compute
\left.\partial_{\epsilon}\right|_{\epsilon=0}\varphi_{\epsilon}=A_{1,0}^{*}\widetilde{\psi}+\left(\left.\partial_{\epsilon}\right|_{\epsilon=0}A_{1,\epsilon}^{*}\right)\psi_{0}
.

Expanding
L_{\epsilon}\psi_{\epsilon}=\lambda_{\epsilon}\psi_{\epsilon}
, we have

\displaystyle\left(L_{0}-\frac{3}{2}\right)\widetilde{\psi}_{\epsilon}=-\frac{W_{\epsilon}^{\prime\prime}(H_{\epsilon})-W_{0}^{\prime\prime}(H_{0})}{\epsilon}\psi_{0}+\widetilde{\lambda}_{\epsilon}\psi_{0}+\epsilon\left(-\frac{W_{\epsilon}^{\prime\prime}(H_{\epsilon})-W_{0}^{\prime\prime}(H_{0})}{\epsilon}\widetilde{\psi}_{\epsilon}+\widetilde{\lambda}_{\epsilon}\widetilde{\psi}_{\epsilon}\right).

\displaystyle\left(L_{0}-\frac{3}{2}\right)\widetilde{\psi}_{0}=-\left(W_{0}^{\prime\prime\prime}(H_{0})\left.\partial_{\epsilon}\right|_{\epsilon=0}H_{\epsilon}+\left.\partial_{\epsilon}\right|_{\epsilon=0}W_{\epsilon}^{\prime\prime}(H_{0})\right)\psi_{0}+\widetilde{\lambda}_{0}\psi_{0}.
