Universal Mappings and Analysis of Functional Data on Geometric Domains
Soheil Anbouhi, Washington Mio, Osman Berat Okutan

Abstract
This paper employs techniques from metric geometry and optimal transport theory to address questions related to the analysis of functional data on metric or metric-measure spaces, which we refer to as fields. Formally, fields are viewed as 1-Lipschitz mappings between Polish spaces, with the domain possibly equipped with a probability measure. We establish the existence and uniqueness, up to isometry, of Urysohn fields; that is, universal and homogeneous elements for this class. We prove a characterization theorem for Urysohn fields and show how they relate to a notion of Gromov-Hausdorff distance for fields. For metric-measure domains, we introduce a field analogue of the Gromov-Wasserstein distance and investigate its properties. Adapting the notion of distance matrices to fields, we formulate a discrete model and obtain an empirical estimation result that provides a theoretical basis for its use in functional data analysis. We also prove an analogue of Gromov’s Reconstruction Theorem in this realm.



Keywords:
universal spaces, functional data, optimal transport.

Introduction

This paper addresses problems in
functional metric geometry
that arise in the study of data such as signals recorded on geometric domains or the nodes of networks. Formally, these may be viewed as functions defined on metric spaces, sometimes equipped with additional structure such as a probability measure, in which case the domain is referred to as a
metric-measure space
, or simply
mm
-space. Datasets comprising such objects arise in many domains of scientific and practical interest. Metric spaces underlying functional data are typically finite, so we discuss some motivating examples of this nature. On a social network, a probability distribution
\mu
on the set of nodes
V
may be used to describe how influential the various members of the network are. The edges normally represent some form of direct interaction, but a metric
d
on
V
, such as the shortest-path distance, the diffusion distance, or the commute-time distance
[
4
,
16
]
, is useful in quantifying indirect interactions as well. The triple
(V,d,\mu)
defines an
mm
-space. Attributes such as individual preferences, traits or characteristics may be viewed as a function
f\colon V\to B
, where
B
is a metric space such as
n
for vector-valued attributes, or
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
(binary words of length
n
) equipped with the
\ell_{1}
-norm for discrete attributes such as a like-or-dislike compilation of preferences. The quadruple
(V,d,\mu,f)
is a functional
mm
-space that can be employed for data representation in many different scenarios. Social networks are dynamic, with individuals joining and leaving the network, their relevance changing over time, as well as their attributes
[
23
,
21
,
5
]
. This leads to a family of functional
mm
-space
(V_{t},d_{t},\mu_{t},f_{t})
parametrized by time. To analyze, visualize and summarize structural and functional changes over time, it is important to define metrics that are sensitive to such changes and amenable to computation. One of the objectives of this paper is to develop representations and metrics for functional
mm
-spaces that brings us closer to this goal.

The study of the organization and shape of point clouds presented as finite metric spaces
(X,d_{X})
is a theme of central interest in geometric data analysis. The functional
mm
-spaces investigated in this paper can provide a useful tool in summarizing the organization of sub-communities of a point cloud and quantifying structural variation across point clouds. If the distance
d_{X}(x,y)
is interpreted as a measure of interaction between
x,y\in X
, then tightly structured sub-communities of
X
at a scale
r\geq 0
can be modeled as non-empty subsets
\sigma\subseteq X
such that
d_{X}(x,y)\leq r
,
\forall x,y\in\sigma
, which are the simplices of the Vietoris-Rips complex
V\!R(X,r)
. If
\tau
is a face of
\sigma
, then the sub-community represented by
\tau
is fully embedded in the sub-community represented by
\sigma
, so one may wish to only consider the sub-collection
K(X,r)\subseteq V\!R(X,r)
formed by the maximal simplices of
V\!R(X,r)
; that is, those that are not faces of any other simplex. We refer to
K(X,r)
as the
community hypergraph
of
X
at scale
r\geq 0
. At scale
r=0
, maximal communities are the singletons
\{x\}
,
x\in X
, but they can be simplices of higher dimension if
d_{X}
is a pseudo-metric. If
r_{0}=\text{diam}\,X
, then
K(X,r_{0})
contains a single simplex
\sigma=X
because
X
itself represents a community at that scale. One can metrize
V\!R(X,r)
, for example, by defining the distance between two simplices
\sigma
and
\tau
to be the Hausdorff distance
d_{H}(\sigma,\tau)
in
X
. Thus,
K(X,r)
may be viewed as a (finite) metric space with the metric inherited from
V\!R(X,r)
. Letting
|\sigma|
be the number of members in the sub-community represented by the simplex
\sigma
, we define a probability measure
\mu
on
K(X,r)
by
\mu(\sigma)=|\sigma|/N
, where

N=\sum_{\sigma\in K(X,r)}|\sigma|

is a normalizing constant. This distribution reflects how representative of
X
each sub-community is. In addition, one may use centrality functions to quantify more explicitly the importance of each simplex
\sigma\in K(X,r)
. For
p\geq 1
, define the
p
-centrality function
\lambda_{p}\colon K(X,r)\to\real
by

\lambda_{p}(\sigma):=\left(\int_{K(X,r)}d_{H}^{p}(\sigma,\tau)d\mu(\tau)\right)^{1/p}=\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\tau\in K(X,r)}(d_{H}^{p}(\sigma,\tau))|\tau|\right)^{1/p}

(cf.
[
11
]
). The more central a sub-community
\sigma
is, the lower the value of
\lambda_{p}(\sigma)
. Thus, we can approach the study of sub-communities of
X
at scale
r
via the community hypergraph viewed as a functional
mm
-space
(K(X,r),d_{H},\mu,\lambda_{p})
. For a family
\{(X_{i},d_{i})\colon 1\leq i\leq n\}
of point clouds, we can study variation in community organization via the associated family
\{(K(X_{i},r),d_{H,i},\mu_{i},\lambda_{p,i})\colon 1\leq i\leq n\}
of functional
mm
-spaces, potentially enhancing the analysis through the use of more sensitive metrics such as the ones developed in this paper.

Motivated by problems such as those outlined above, our primary goal is threefold: (i) to develop metrics that allow us to model and quantify variation in functional data, possibly with distinct domains; (ii) to investigate principled empirical estimations of these metrics; (iii) to construct a universal function that “contains” all functions whose domains and ranges are Polish (separable and complete metric) spaces, assuming Lipschitz regularity. The latter is much in the spirit of constructing universal spaces for structural data (metric spaces) whose investigation dates back to the early 20th century and are of classical interest in metric geometry
[
24
,
26
,
13
]
.

Modeling structural data as geometric objects has been a subject of extensive study using techniques from areas such as metric geometry and optimal transport theory. Shape contrasts or similarities in a family of compact metric spaces can be quantified via metrics such as the Gromov-Hausdorff distance
d_{GH}

[
3
]
. For
mm
-spaces, analogues of
d_{GH}
such as the Gromov-Wasserstein distance
[
19
,
22
]
, that highlight regions of larger probability mass, are also used for shape analysis. We study functional analogues of the Gromov-Hausdorff and Gromov-Wasserstein distances (cf.
[
25
]
) and also investigate discrete representations of functional
mm
-spaces based on random finite subsets by means of distance-matrix representations that encode information about their shape.

Another important angle in the investigation of a shape class is the study of existence and uniqueness of a universal element. Given a class of metric spaces
\mathcal{C}
, a space
U
is
\mathcal{C}
-universal if it contains an isometric image of each
X
in
\mathcal{C}
. Fréchet showed that any Polish space can be isometrically embedded in the space
l^{\infty}
of all bounded real sequences (with the sup norm)
[
8
]
. Banach and Mazur showed that the same holds for the space
C[0,1]
of real-valued continuous functions on the interval
[0,1]
(with the sup norm)
[
1
]
. However, these spaces are external to the class of Polish spaces, as they are not separable themselves. A
Urysohn universal space
(or simply, Urysohn space) is a Polish space
U
that is universal for Polish spaces and satisfies an additional homogeneity condition: any isometry between finite subsets of
U
extends to an isometry of
U
onto itself. Unlike more general universal spaces, Urysohn spaces are known to be unique up to isometry. For a more thorough discussion of a construction and properties of Urysohn spaces, we refer the reader to
[
12
]
(see also
[
13
,
26
]
). A
\mathcal{C}
-universal space provides a framework for the study of the class
\mathcal{C}
. For example, the collection of compact subsets of the Urysohn space
U
with the Hausdorff distance, modulo the action of the isometry group of
U
, is isometric to the space of isometry classes of compact metric spaces equipped with the Gromov-Hausdorff distance
[
10
]
. This paper addresses functional counterparts of these problems for 1-Lipschitz mappings between Polish spaces, including the construction of Urysohn universal mappings that satisfy a homogeneity condition akin to the homogeneity of Urysohn spaces.

1.1
Main Results

We study the class of 1-Lipschitz maps
\phi\colon X\to B
, where
X
and
B
are Polish spaces. These mappings are the morphisms in the category
Met
of metric spaces restricted to Polish spaces. We refer to such 1-Lipschitz mappings as
B
-valued fields
on
X
, or simply
B
-fields, and sometimes denote them as triples
\mathscr{X}=(X,B,\pi)
.

Universal Urysohn Fields.
We first fix the target space
B
and show that there exists a unique (up to isometry)
Urysohn universal
B
-field

\pi\colon U\to B
for the class of
B
-fields over Polish spaces. In Theorem
3.10
, we prove that the following properties characterize a Urysohn
B
-field
\pi\colon U\to B
:

(i)

for any
B
-field
\phi\colon X\to B
, there is an isometric embedding
\imath\colon X\to U
such that
\pi\circ\imath=\phi
;

(ii)

any (bijective) isometry
\phi\colon A\to A^{\prime}
between finite subsets of
U
such that
\pi\circ\phi=\pi|_{A}
can be extended to an automorphism of
\pi\colon U\to B
; that is, to a bijective isometry
\phi^{\prime}\colon U\to U
such that
\pi\circ\phi^{\prime}=\pi
.

Property (i) is referred to as universality, whereas property (ii) is a strong form of homogeneity.

Subsequently, we introduce the notion of
ultimate Urysohn field
to address universality and homogeneity for the full class of 1-Lipschitz maps between Polish spaces; that is, allowing the target space
B
to also vary. We prove an existence and uniqueness theorem for ultimate Urysohn fields, which are defined in terms of universality and homogeneity, but are characterized in Theorem
3.14
, as follows. A
B
-field
\pi\colon U\to B
is an ultimate Urysohn field if and only if it satisfies:

(i)

\pi
is Urysohn universal for
B
;

(ii)

B
is a Urysohn space.

As alluded to above, for compact metric spaces, a Urysohn space
U
allows us to interpret the Gromov-Hausdorff distance as a Hausdorff distance in
U
modulo the action of isometries. We prove an analogous result for compact fields; that is, fields with compact domains. To this end, in Section
4
, we introduce the notion of Gromov-Hausdorff distance between general compact fields.

Denote by
F(\mathscr{U})
the space of compact subfields of a field
\mathscr{U}
equipped with the Hausdorff distance in
\mathscr{U}
; that is, the Hausdorff distance between the domains of the compact subfields. Let
Aut(\mathscr{U})
be the group of automorphisms of
\mathscr{U}
, and
d_{F}^{\mathscr{U}}
the quotient metric induced by the Hausdorff distance on the quotient
F(\mathscr{U})/Aut(\mathscr{U})
.

Theorem
4.11
.

Let
\mathscr{U}
be an ultimate Urysohn field. The moduli space
(\mathcal{F},d_{GH})
of isomorphism classes of compact fields is isometric to the quotient space
(F(\mathscr{U})/Aut(\mathscr{U}),d_{F}^{\mathscr{U}})
.

Metric-Measure Fields.
We study metric-measure fields (
mm
-fields), that is, 1-Lipschitz functions
f\colon X\to B
between Polish spaces, where
X
is also equipped with a Borel probability measure
\mu
. These are denoted as quadruples
\mathcal{X}=(X,B,\pi,\mu)
. We develop a field analogue of the Gromov-Wasserstein distance that has been studied extensively for
mm
-spaces
[
19
,
22
]
. For fields
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
, the Gromov-Wasserstein distance is denoted
d_{GW,{p}}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
, where
1\leq p\leq\infty
is a parameter, and is used to address convergence questions. Among other things, we show that there are equidistributed sequences
(x_{n})
and
(y_{n})
in
X
and
Y
, respectively, such that

d_{GW,\infty}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})=\max\left\{\frac{1}{2}\sup_{i,j}m_{X,Y}(x_{i},y_{i},x_{j},y_{j}),\,\sup_{i}d_{X,Y}(x_{i},y_{i})\right\}.

This is further detailed in Theorem
5.6
. (A sequence
(x_{i})
in
X
is
\mu
-equidistributed if the empirical measures
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{x_{i}}/n
converge weakly to
\mu
.) With an eye toward principled empirical estimation of the distance between
mm
-fields, we introduce the notion of
extended distance matrices
, much in the way distance matrices are used to study
mm
-spaces (cf.
[
10
]
). For an
mm
-field
\mathcal{X}=(X,B,\pi,\mu)
and a sequence
\{x_{i}\}
in
X
,
i\geq 1
, form the countably infinite (pseudo) distance matrix
R=(r_{ij})\in\real^{\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}}
and the infinite sequence
b=(b_{i})\in B^{\mathbb{N}}
, where
r_{ij}=d_{X}(x_{i},x_{j})
and
b_{i}=\pi(x_{i})\in B
. We refer to the pair
(R,b)
as the
augmented distance matrix
of
\mathcal{X}
associated with the sequence, which records the shape of the graph of
\pi
restricted to the sequence. This construction let us define a Borel measurable mapping
F_{\mathcal{X}}\colon X^{\infty}\to\real^{\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}}\times B^{\infty}
, with both the domain and co-domain equipped with the weak topology. The pushforward of
\mu^{\infty}
under
F_{\mathcal{X}}
yields a probability measure on
\real{}^{\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}}\times B^{\infty}
that we denote by

\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}:=(F_{\mathcal{X}})_{\ast}(\mu^{\infty})\,,

(1)

and refer to as the
field curvature distribution
of
\mathcal{X}
. This terminology is motivated by the notion of
curvature set
of a metric space
[
10
]
. A similar construction for finite sequences
\{x_{i}\}
,
1\leq i\leq n
, gives a measure
\mathcal{D}^{n}_{\mathcal{X}}
on
\real{}^{n\times n}\times B^{n}
.

We prove a field version of Gromov’s Reconstruction Theorem for metric-measure spaces that states that the field curvature distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}
gives a faithful representation of
\mathcal{X}
.

Theorem
6.3

(Field Reconstruction Theorem)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}=(X,d_{X},\pi_{X},\mu_{X})
and
\mathcal{Y}=(Y,d_{Y},\pi_{Y},\mu_{Y})
be
mm
-fields over
B
such that
\mu_{X}
and
\mu_{Y}
are fully supported. Then,

\mathcal{X}\simeq\mathcal{Y}\text{ if and only if }\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}=\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}.

Here,
\mathcal{X}\simeq\mathcal{Y}
means that there exists a measure-preserving isometry between
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
.

Our main result supporting empirical estimation of the Gromov-Wasserstein distance between
mm
-fields is the following convergence theorem.

Theorem
6.5
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
be bounded
mm
-fields over
B
. Then, for any
1\leq p\leq\infty
, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}d_{W,p}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}^{n},\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}^{n})=d_{W,p}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}},\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{Y}})=d_{GW,\infty}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}).

1.2
Additional Remarks on Urysohn Space

In
[
20
]
, Mroẃka gave an example of an isometry between countable subsets of Urysohn space
U
that cannot be extended to an isometry of
U
, whereas Huhunaišvili showed that isometries between compact subsets of
U
can be extended to
U
. Additionally, in
[
17
]
, Melleray argued that compact homogeneity is the strongest general homogeneity property possible for Urysohn space. As it turns out, a metric space is Urysohn if and only if it has the following one-point-extension property for finite sets: given any finite subset
X\subseteq U
and one-point metric extension
(X^{\ast},d_{X}^{\ast})
of
X
, there is an isometric embedding of
X^{\ast}
in
U
that restricts to the inclusion map on
X
.

A well-known construction of Urysohn space is due to Katětov
[
13
]
. Starting with an arbitrary separable metric space
X_{0}
(e.g., a one-point space), a sequence of separable metric extensions
\{X_{i}\colon i\geq 0\}
is constructed inductively, as follows. For
i\geq 0
,
X_{i+1}
is the space of all pseudo-metric one-point extensions of
X_{i}
. The space
X_{i}
can be isometrically embedded into
X_{i+1}
by mapping
x\in X_{i}
to the one-point extension of
X_{i}
that simply repeats the point
x
. The colimit
X_{\infty}:=\varinjlim X_{i}
is separable and its completion is a Urysohn space.

Vershik approached Urysohn space by means of (countably) infinite distance matrices, which may be thought of as metrics on the set
\mathbb{N}
of natural numbers
[
26
]
. Distance matrices satisfying an
approximate one-point extension property
lead to Urysohn space by taking the completion of
\mathbb{N}
. Moreover, Vershik showed that such universal matrices are (topologically) generic and that a random matrix is universal in a probabilistic sense. Yet another approach uses somewhat different methodology from descriptive set theory and model theory to construct Urysohn space
[
28
,
14
]
and to address universality for spaces equipped with additional structures
[
6
]
.

Preliminaries

In this section, we introduce some notation and review basic concepts and results from metric geometry
(cf.
[
3
]
and
[
12
]
) that are used in the paper. We also introduce extensions of these basic concepts to the
realm of functional data.

Definition 2.1
.

An
extended metric

d
on a set
X
is a metric that allows infinite distances.
More precisely, a symmetric function
d:X\times X\to[0,\infty]
which is zero on the diagonal,
non-zero off the diagonal, and satisfies the triangle inequality.

Definition 2.2

(Kuratowski Space)
.

Given a set
X
, the
Kuratoswki space

(\kappa(X),d_{\infty})
is the extended metric space whose underlying
set is the collection of real valued functions on
X
and the extended metric
d_{\infty}
is defined by

d_{\infty}(f,g):=\sup_{x\in X}|f(x)-g(x)|\,.

If
(X,d_{X})
is a metric space, we define the following subspaces of
\kappa(X)
that
interact with the metric structure of
X
:

(a)

\mathcal{L}^{1}(X):=\{f\in\kappa(X)\colon f\text{ is 1-Lipschitz}\}.

(b)

\Delta(X):=\{f\in\kappa(X)\colon d_{X}(x,y)\leq f(x)+f(y),\forall x,y\in X\}.

(c)

\Delta^{1}(X):=\mathcal{L}^{1}(X)\cap\Delta(X)
.

Remark 2.3
.

A function
f\in\Delta^{1}(X)
may be interpreted as a one-point (pseudo-metric) extension of
(X,d_{X})
. Indeed, on the disjoint union
X^{\ast}=X\sqcup\{x^{\ast}\}
, define a (pseudo) metric
d_{X^{\ast}}\colon X^{\ast}\times X^{\ast}\to\real
by
d_{X^{\ast}}|_{X\times X}=d_{X}
and
d_{X^{\ast}}(x,x^{\ast})=d_{X^{\ast}}(x^{\ast},x):=f(x)
,
\forall x\in X
. Symmetry is clearly satisfied and conditions (a) and (b) above ensure that
d_{X^{\ast}}
satisfies the triangle inequality.

The following result is standard.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
(X,d_{X})
be a metric space. Then,
(\Delta^{1}(X),d_{\infty})
is a metric space and the
map
X\to\Delta^{1}(X)
, defined by
x\mapsto d_{X}(x,\cdot)
, is an isometric embedding. This
embedding is called the Kuratowski embedding. Furthermore, if
f\in\Delta^{1}(X)
, then
d_{\infty}(f,d_{X}(x,\cdot))=f(x)
.

Proof.

See Proposition 2.2 of
[
18
]
.
∎

Definition 2.5
.

Let
X
be a set and
\mathcal{F}
be a non-empty, pointwise bounded family of
real-valued functions on
X
. The
pointwise supremum
of
\mathcal{F}
is the function

p\sup(\mathcal{F}):X\to\real
given by

p\sup(\mathcal{F})(x):=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}f(x)\,.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
(X,d_{X})
be a metric space and
\mathcal{F}
be a non-empty pointwise bounded
family of real valued functions on
X
.

(i)

If
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{L}^{1}(X)
, then
p\sup(\mathcal{F})\in\mathcal{L}^{1}(X)
.

(ii)

If
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\Delta(X)
, then
p\sup(\mathcal{F})\in\Delta(X)
.

(iii)

If
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\Delta^{1}(X)
, then
p\sup(\mathcal{F})\in\Delta^{1}(X)
.

Proof.

We use the abbreviation
F:=p\sup(\mathcal{F})
.

(i) For any
x,y\in X
, we have

F(x)-F(y)=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}f(x)-F(y)\leq\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}f(x)-f(y)\leq d(x,y)\,.

(2)

Similarly,
F(y)-F(x)\leq d(x,y)
. Therefore,
F
is 1-Lipschitz.

(ii) If
f\in\mathcal{F}
, then
F(x)+F(y)\geq f(x)+f(y)\geq d_{X}(x,y)
, for all
x,y\in X
.

(iii) Follows from (i) and (ii).
∎

Definition 2.7

(Whitney-McShane Extension)
.

Let
(X,d_{X})
be a metric space and
\emptyset\neq A\subseteq X
. Given a
1
-Lipschitz function
f:A\to\real
, the
Whitney-McShane
extension of
f
is the function
\tilde{f}:X\to\real
given by

\tilde{f}(x):=\inf_{a\in A}(f(a)+d(a,x))\,.

Remark 2.8
.

It is simple to verify that the Whitney-McShane extension is the maximal
1
-Lipschitz
extension of
f
. This implies that if
\emptyset\neq A\subseteq B\subseteq X
, taking two consecutive Whitney-McShane extensions, first from
A
to
B
and then from
B
to
X
, is the same as taking the Whitney-McShane extension from
A
to
X
.

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
(X,d_{X})
be a metric space and
A,B\subseteq X
be non-empty subspaces. Denote by
\imath_{A}\colon\mathcal{L}^{1}(A)\to\mathcal{L}^{1}(X)
and
\imath_{B}\colon\mathcal{L}^{1}(B)\to\mathcal{L}^{1}(X)
the maps given by Whitney-McShane extension and by
r_{A}\colon\mathcal{L}^{1}(X)\to\mathcal{L}^{1}(A)
and
r_{B}\colon\mathcal{L}^{1}(X)\to\mathcal{L}^{1}(B)
the restriction maps. Then, the following statements hold:

(i)

\imath_{A}
is an isometric embedding.

(ii)

\imath_{A}(\Delta^{1}(A))\subseteq\Delta^{1}(X)
.

(iii)

For any
f\in\mathcal{L}^{1}(X)
,
d_{\infty}(\imath_{A}\circ r_{A}(f),\imath_{B}\circ r_{B}(f))\leq 2\,d_{H}^{X}(A,B)
, where
d_{H}^{X}
denotes the Hausdorff distance in
X
.

Proof.

(i) Let
f,g\colon A\to\real
be 1-Lipschitz and
\tilde{f},\tilde{g}\colon X\to\real
denote their Whitney-McShane extensions, respectively. Clearly,
d_{\infty}(f,g)\leq d_{\infty}(\tilde{f},\tilde{g})
. For the reverse inequality,
\forall x\in X
, we have:

\begin{split}\tilde{f}(x)-\tilde{g}(x)&=\tilde{f}(x)-\inf_{a\in A}\,(g(a)+d_{X}(a,x))=\sup_{a\in A}\,(\tilde{f}(x)-g(a)-d_{X}(a,x))\\
&\leq\sup_{a\in A}\,(f(a)+d_{X}(a,x)-g(a)-d_{X}(a,x))\leq d_{\infty}(f,g)\,.\end{split}

(3)

Similarly,
\tilde{g}(x)-\tilde{f}(x)\leq d_{\infty}(f,g)
,
\forall x\in X
. Thus,
d_{\infty}(f,g)=d_{\infty}(\tilde{f},\tilde{g})
, as claimed.

(ii) Let
f\in\Delta^{1}(A)
and
\tilde{f}=\imath_{A}(f)
. For any
x,y\in X
, we have that

\begin{split}\tilde{f}(x)+\tilde{f}(y)&=\inf_{a,a^{\prime}\in A}\,(f(a)+d_{X}(a,x)+f(a^{\prime})+d_{X}(a^{\prime},y))\\
&\geq\inf_{a,a^{\prime}\in A}\,(d_{X}(a,a^{\prime})+d_{X}(a,x)+d_{X}(a^{\prime},y))\geq d_{X}(x,y)\,.\end{split}

(4)

Therefore,
\tilde{f}\in\Delta^{1}(X)
.

(iii) If
r>d_{H}^{X}(A,B)
, there exists a function
\phi:B\to A
such that
d_{X}(b,\phi(b))<r
,
\forall b
in
B
. Then, for any
x\in X
, we have

\begin{split}\imath_{A}\circ r_{A}(f)(x)-\imath_{B}\circ r_{B}(f)(x)&=\imath_{A}\circ r_{A}(f)(x)-\inf_{b\in B}\,(f(b)+d_{X}(b,x))\\
&=\sup_{b\in B}\,(\imath_{A}\circ r_{A}(f)(x)-f(b)-d_{X}(b,x))\\
&\leq\sup_{b\in B}\,(f(\phi(b))+d_{X}(\phi(b),x)-f(b)-d_{X}(b,x))\\
&\leq\sup_{b\in B}\,(f(\phi(b))-f(b)+d_{X}(\phi(b),x)-d_{X}(b,x))\leq 2r\,.\end{split}

(5)

Similarly,
\imath_{B}\circ r_{B}(f)(x)-\imath_{A}\circ r_{A}(f)(x)\leq 2r
,
\forall x\in X
. Since
r>d_{H}(A,B)
is arbitrary, the claim follows.
∎

Definition 2.10
.

Let
(X,d_{X})
be metric space and
\emptyset\neq A\subseteq X
. A
1
-Lipschitz function
f:X\to\real
is said to be
supported in

A
if
f
is the Whitney-MacShane extension of its restriction to
A
. If
f
is supported in a finite subspace of
X
,
f
is said to be
finitely supported
. We define

E(X):=\{f\in\Delta^{1}(X)\colon f\ \text{is finitely supported}\}.

Remark 2.11
.

Let
(X,d_{X})
be a metric space and
x\in X
. Then,
d_{X}(x,\cdot)
is supported in
\{x\}
. Hence, the image of the Kuratowski embedding is contained in
E(X)
.

The main reason we introduce the space
E(X)
is that, in general, the separability of
X
does not imply the separability of
\Delta^{1}(X)
(e.g., if
X
a countable discrete metric space), whereas
E(X)
is separable (
[
18
]
, Section 2).

Proposition 2.12
.

If
(X,d_{X})
is a separable metric space, then
(E(X),d_{\infty})
is also separable.

Proof.

Let
C
be a countable dense subset of
X
. By Remark
2.8
and Proposition
2.9
, we have that
\imath_{C}(E(C))\subseteq E(X)
. First, we show that
\imath_{C}(E(C))
is dense in
E(X)
. Let
f\in E(X)
be supported in the finite set
A=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
and
\epsilon>0
. There exists a subset
B=\{x_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,x_{n}^{\prime}\}
of
C
such that
d_{X}(x_{i},x_{i}^{\prime})<\epsilon/2
, for
1\leq i\leq n
. Let
g\in E(C)
be the Whitney-McShane extension of
r_{B}(f)
to
C
. By Remark
2.8
,
\imath_{C}(g)=\imath_{B}\circ r_{B}(f)\in E(X)
. Moreover, since
f
is supported in
A
, we may write
f=\imath_{A}\circ r_{A}(f)
. Thus, Proposition
2.9
implies that

d_{\infty}(f,\imath_{C}(g))=d_{\infty}(\imath_{A}\circ r_{A}(f),\imath_{B}\circ r_{B}(f))\leq\epsilon\,.

(6)

This shows that
\imath_{C}(E(C))
is dense in
E(X)
. It remains to show that
\imath_{C}(E(C))
is separable. If
A
is finite,
E(A)\subseteq\kappa(A)
is separable, as the set of rational valued functions on
A
is countable and dense in
\kappa(A)
. Since

\imath_{C}(E(C))=\bigcup_{A\subseteq C\text{ finite}}\imath_{A}(E(A))\,,

(7)

it follows that
\imath_{C}(E(C))
is separable because it is a countable union of separable metric spaces.
∎

Spaces of type
E(X)
can be used as the building blocks of a Urysohn space (
[
12
]
, Theorem 3.4). As one of our goals is to extend the construction to Urysohn fields, we close this section by introducing functional analogues of
\Delta^{1}(X)
and
E(X)
. Recall that any
f\in\Delta_{1}(X)
may be interpreted as a one-point extension of
(X,d_{X})
to a pseudo-metric space

, where

d(x,x^{\ast})=f(x)
,
\forall x\in X
. In the functional setting, we also have a 1-Lipschitz function
\pi\colon X\to B
, where
B
is separable. As such, a one-point extension of the functional data also requires an extension of
\pi
to a 1-Lipschitz function on

. This motivates the next definition.

Definition 2.13
.

Let
\pi\colon X\to B
be a

-field. We define the metric spaces

.

Note that the mapping

given by

is an isometric embedding. Moreover, if we let

denote the projection onto

, then the diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces E(\pi\colon X\to B)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{B}}

(8)

commutes.

Remark 2.14
.

(i)

If
X
and

are separable metric spaces, then so is

.

(ii)

Let
\pi\colon X\to B
be
1
-Lipschitz and

. If

.

3
Universal and Urysohn Fields

Recall that a Polish space
X
is
universal
if any Polish space
Y
admits an isometric embedding
\imath\colon Y\to X
. In general, however, universal spaces lack good homogeneity properties, as shown in Proposition
3.7
below. Urysohn
spaces are universal spaces that satisfy a strong form of homogeneity: for any pair of isometric embeddings
\imath_{1},\imath_{2}\colon A\to X
,
where
A
is a finite metric space, there exists an isometry
\jmath\colon X\to X
such that
\jmath\circ\imath_{1}=\imath_{2}
. The reader may consult
[
17
]
for a detailed discussion of these properties. In this section, we investigate functional counterparts of universal and Urysohn spaces.

3.1
Existence and Uniqueness

.

A
B
-field
\mathscr{X}=(X,B,\pi)
is
B
-universal
if for any
B
-field
\mathscr{Y}=(Y,B,\phi)
, there is an isometric embedding

. In other words, the diagram

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi}

is commutative.

.

If

is a universal metric space and

is Polish, then the projection map

is a
B
-universal field, where

is endowed with the metric

d((x_{1},b_{1}),(x_{2},b_{2}))=\max\,\{d_{X}(x_{1},,x_{2}),d_{B}(b_{1},b_{2})\}.

Proof.

Let
\phi\colon Y\to B
be a
B
-field. By the universality of

, there exists an isometric embedding
\imath\colon X\to Y
. Since
\phi
is
1
-Lipschitz, the map
\imath\times\phi\colon Y\to X\times B
is an isometric embedding and the diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X\times B}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phi}

Remark 3.3
.

A universal
B
-field
\pi\colon X\to B
is a
1
-Lipschitz retraction. More precisely, by considering the identity map
B\to B
, we obtain an isometric embedding

Definition 3.4

(Urysohn Field)
.

A
B
-field
\pi\colon U\to B
is called a
Urysohn field over
B
if for each finite subspace
A
of
U
and
1
-Lipschitz map
\phi\colon A^{\ast}\to B
defined on a one-point metric extension

, satisfying
\phi|_{A}=\pi|_{A}
, there exists an isometric embedding
\imath\colon A^{*}\to U
such that the restriction
\imath|_{A}
is the inclusion map and

.

Theorem 3.5

(Existence and Uniqueness of Urysohn Fields)
.

If
B
is a Polish space, then the following statements hold:

(i)

there exists a Urysohn field
\pi\colon U\to B
;

(ii)

if
\pi\colon U\to B
and
\pi^{\prime}\colon U^{\prime}\to B
are Urysohn fields, then there exists a bijective isometry
\psi\colon U\to U^{\prime}
such that the diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces U}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces U^{\prime}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\psi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi^{\prime}}

is commutative.

Proof.

(i)
Existence.
Let
X_{0}=B
and
\pi_{0}\colon X_{0}\to B
be the identity map. Inductively, define
X_{n+1}:=E(X_{n}\to B)
and let
\pi_{n+1}\colon X_{n+1}\to B
be the projection
\pi_{n+1}(f,b)=b
. Then, the diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X_{0}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X_{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdots}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdots}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{0}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{2}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces id}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces id}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces id}

(11)

is commutative, where the mappings on the first row are isometric embeddings. By viewing the isometric embeddings
X_{n}\hookrightarrow X_{n+1}
as inclusions, we may write the metric co-limit as
X_{\infty}=\cup_{n\geq 0}X_{n}
. We denote the limiting 1-Lipschitz map by
\pi_{\infty}:X_{\infty}\to B
. Let
U
be the completion of
X_{\infty}
and
\pi\colon U\to B
be the
1
-Lipschitz extension of
\pi_{\infty}
. Note that
U
is a Polish space by Remark
2.14
(i). We now show that
\pi\colon U\to B
is Urysohn.

Let

be a finite subspace of
U
,
A^{\ast}=\{u_{1},\dots,u_{n},u^{\ast}\}
be a one-point metric extension of
A
, and
\phi\colon A^{\ast}\to B
a 1-Lipschitz extension of

. Let

to be the map

and

. Let
\tilde{f}
be the Whitney-McShane extension of

to
U
. By Remark

(\tilde{f},b)\in E(\pi\colon U\to B)
.

For each
\epsilon>0
, there exist
N^{\epsilon}\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
and
x^{\epsilon}_{1},\dots,x^{\epsilon}_{n}\in X_{N^{\epsilon}}
such that
d_{U}(u_{i},x^{\epsilon}_{i})\leq\epsilon
, for all

. Defining

, we have that

. Let
\tilde{f}^{\epsilon}
be the Whitney-McShane extension of
f^{\epsilon}
to
X_{N^{\epsilon}}
. By Remark
2.14
(ii),

(12)

By Proposition
2.9
,
d_{U}(x^{\epsilon},x^{\epsilon^{\prime}})\leq 2(\epsilon+\epsilon^{\prime})
. Therefore, there exists
x\in U
such that
\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}x^{\epsilon}=x
. Note that
\pi(x)=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\pi(x^{\epsilon})=b
and

d_{U}(x,u_{i})=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}d_{U}(x^{\epsilon},u_{i})=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}d_{U}(x^{\epsilon},x^{\epsilon}_{i})\\
=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\tilde{f}(x^{i}_{\epsilon})=f(u_{i})=d_{A^{\ast}}(u_{i},u^{\ast})\,.

(13)

Thus, by mapping

to
x
, we get an isometric embedding
\imath\colon A^{\ast}\hookrightarrow U
satisfying

, as desired.

(ii)
Uniqueness.
Let
\pi\colon U\to B
and
\pi^{\prime}\colon U^{\prime}\to B
be Uryhson fields, and

and

and

, respectively. We start by constructing nested sequences

and

. For

(14)

Assuming that

,

and

have been defined, we construct

,

and

, as follows. Since Uryhson fields have the one-point extension property, there exists

and a bijective isometry

that extends
\psi_{n}
and for which the diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C_{n}\cup\{x_{n+1}\}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C^{\prime}_{n}\cup\{x^{\prime}\}}

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\psi^{\prime}_{n}}

commutes. Similarly, there exists

and a bijective isometry

extending

such that the diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C_{n}\cup\{x_{n+1},x\}}

is commutative. Let

Proposition 3.6
.

A Urysohn field over

-field.

Let

, and let

Propositions

Proposition 3.7
.

Let

and

be Polish spaces and

be the product space endowed with the max metric. If

\pi_{B}\colon U\times B\to B

Arguing by contradiction, assume that

is Urysohn . Let

,

, and

an arbitrary point in

. Since

such that

. Let

,

(18)

and

-Lipschitz extension of

given by

. Since

is a Uryhson field, there is

Since

is endowed with the max metric, this implies that

and

, which is a contradiction since

.
∎

3.2
Characterization of Urysohn Fields

Definition 3.8

(Automorphisms and Matchings)
.

Let
\pi\colon X\to B
be a
B
-field.

(i)

An
automorphism
of
\pi\colon X\to B
is a bijective isometry
\psi\colon X\to X
that satisfies
\pi\circ\psi=\pi
.

(ii)

A
partial isometric matching
of
\pi\colon X\to B
is a bijective isometry
\phi\colon A\to A^{\prime}
between subspaces of
X
that satisfies
\pi|_{A^{\prime}}\circ\phi=\pi|_{A}
.

(iii)

A partial isometric matching is said to be
finite
if
|A|=|A^{\prime}|<\infty
.

Proposition 3.9
.

If
\pi\colon U\to B
is a Uryhson field, then every finite partial isometric matching of
\pi
can be extended to an automorphism of
\pi
.

Proof.

Let
\phi\colon A\to A^{\prime}
be an isometric matching of
\pi\colon X\to B
, where
A=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
,
A^{\prime}=\{x^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,x^{\prime}_{n}\}
, and
\phi(x_{i})=x^{\prime}_{i}
. Let
C=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},x_{n+1},\ldots\}
and

be countable dense sets in

. The isometry extending

is constructed as in the proof of the uniqueness part of Theorem
3.5
.
∎

Theorem 3.10

(Characterization of Uryhson fields)
.

A
B
-field
\pi\colon U\to B
is Urysohn if and only if it is universal and every finite isometric matching of
\pi
can be extended to an automorphism of
\pi
.

Proof.

The “only if” part follows from Proposition
3.6
and Proposition
3.9
. To prove the converse statement let
A=\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}\}
be a finite subspace of
U
,
A^{\ast}=A\sqcup\{a^{\ast}\}
a one-point extension of
A
, and
\phi\colon A^{\ast}\to B
a 1-Lipschitz map extending
\pi|_{A}
. Since
\pi\colon U\to B
is universal, there exists an isometric embedding
\imath\colon A^{\ast}\to U
such that
\phi=\pi\circ\imath
.

Let
u^{\prime}_{i}:=\imath(u_{i})
,
1\leq i\leq n
, and
A^{\prime}=\{u^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,u^{\prime}_{n}\}
. Then,
\imath_{A}\colon A\to A^{\prime}
is a finite isometric matching of
\pi\colon X\to B
. By assumption, there is an automorphism
\psi\colon X\to X
extending this matching. Let
u=\psi^{-1}(\imath(a^{\ast}))
. Note that
\pi(u)=\pi(\psi(u))=\pi(\imath(a^{\ast}))=\phi(a^{\ast})
and
d_{U}(u,u_{i})=d_{U}(\psi(u),\psi(u_{i}))=d_{U}(\imath(a^{\ast}),\imath(u_{i}))=d_{A^{\ast}}(u_{i},a^{\ast})
, as desired.
∎

.

Theorem 3.14

(Characterization of Ultimate Urysohn Fields)
.

Let

be a field. The following statements are equivalent:

(i)

\pi
is an ultimate Urysohn field.

(ii)

\pi
is a Urysohn
B
-field and
B
is a Urysohn space.

(iii)

\pi
is a strongly universal field and every finite isometric double matching of
\pi
can be extended to a double automorphism of

.

Proof.

\text{(i)}\Rightarrow\text{(ii)}
. By definition,
\pi
is a Uryhson field, so we need to show that

is a Urysohn space. Let

be a finite subset of

and

. Set

and let

be a one-point extension of

. Define

by

, which is clearly 1-Lipschitz. Since

is an ultimate Uryhson field, there is an isometric embedding
\jmath\colon Z^{\ast}\to B
extending the inclusion

satisfying the additional conditions of Definition

. Thus,

is a Urysohn space.

\text{(ii)}\Rightarrow\text{(iii)}
. The strong universality of
\pi
follows from Proposition
3.6
. With the notation of Definition

, let a finite isometric double matching of
\pi\colon X\to B
be given by a pair of isometries
\phi\colon A\to A^{\prime}
and
\theta\colon Z\to Z^{\prime}
. Since

is a Urysohn space,
\theta
can be extended to a bijective isometry
\tilde{\theta}\colon B\to B
. Since
\pi
is a Uryhson field and
\tilde{\theta}\colon B\to B
is an isometry,
\pi^{\prime}\colon\tilde{\theta}\circ\pi\colon U\to B
also is a Uryhson field. As in the proof of the uniqueness part of Theorem

, we can extend the isometry

(which satisfies

) to a bijective isometry

such that

. The pair

gives the desired double automorphism of

.

\text{(iii)}\Rightarrow\text{(i)}
. Let
A\subseteq U
and

be finite subspaces with
\pi(A)\subseteq Z
and let

be a 1-Lipschitz map between one-point extensions of

and
Z
such that

. Since

is a universal space, there exists an isometric embedding
\jmath\colon Z^{\ast}\to B
. The universality of the map

implies that there exists an isometric embedding

such that

.

Let

and

. Then, the maps

and

yield a finite isometric double matching of

, which extends to a

\tilde{\imath}\colon U\to U
and
\tilde{\jmath}\colon B\to B
. The maps

and
\tilde{\jmath}^{-1}\circ\jmath\colon Z^{\ast}\to B

give the desired isometric embeddings that extend the inclusions

and

,
respectively. The commutativity of the diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A^{\ast}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces U}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Z^{*}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\tilde{\imath}^{-1}\circ\,\imath}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\tilde{\jmath}^{-1}\circ\,\jmath}

follows from the construction.
∎

4
Functional Gromov-Hausdorff Distance

The primary goal of this section is to introduce a moduli space of compact fields equipped with a Gromov-Hausdorff distance and show how it relates to the Hausdorff distance between compact subfields of the Urysohn field, up to the action of automorphisms.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\mathscr{X}=(X,B,\pi_{X})
and
\mathscr{Y}=(Y,B,\pi_{Y})
be
B
-fields.

(i)

A mapping from
\mathscr{X}
to
\mathscr{Y}
over
B
, sometimes denoted
\Phi\colon\mathscr{X}\Rightarrow\mathscr{Y}
, consists of a 1-Lipschitz mapping
\phi\colon X\to Y
such that the diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{X}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{Y}}

commutes. We adopt the convention that uppercase letters denote maps between fields and the corresponding lowercase letters denote the associated mapping between their domains.

(ii)

\Phi\colon\mathscr{X}\Rightarrow\mathscr{Y}
is an
isometric embedding
if
\phi:X\to Y
is an isometric embedding of metric spaces.

(iii)

\Phi\colon\mathscr{X}\Rightarrow\mathscr{Y}
is an
isometry
if
\phi:X\to Y
is a bijective isometry. Moreover, we say that
\mathscr{X}
and
\mathscr{Y}
are
isometric
, denoted by
\mathscr{X}\simeq\mathscr{Y}
, if there is an isometry
\Phi
between them. If
\mathscr{X}=\mathscr{Y}
, we refer to an isometry as an
automorphism
of
\mathscr{X}
.

Definition 4.2
.

(Gromov-Hausdorff Distance for Fields)
Let
\mathscr{X}=(X,B,\pi_{X})
and
\mathscr{Y}=(Y,B,\pi_{Y})
be compact
B
-fields. The
Gromov-Hausdorff distance
is defined by

d_{GH}^{B}(\mathscr{X},\mathscr{Y}):=\inf_{Z,\Phi,\Psi}d^{Z}_{H}(\phi(X),\psi(Y)),

where the infimum is taken over all
B
-fields
\mathscr{Z}
and isometric embeddings
\Phi\colon\mathscr{X}\Rightarrow\mathscr{Z}
and
\Psi:\mathscr{Y}\Rightarrow\mathscr{Z}
over
B
.

Remark 4.3
.

(i)

The definition of
d_{GH}^{B}
only involves the Hausdorff distance between the domains of the functions once embedded in
\mathscr{Z}
. This is due to the fact that fields are 1-Lipschitz functions, so that differences in function values are bounded by distances in the domain.

(ii)

Similar to the case of compact metric spaces, the space
\mathcal{F}_{B}
of isometry classes of compact
B
-fields with the Gromov-Hausdorff distance is Polish (
[
3
]
, Sec. 7.3).

Henceforth, we denote the Urysohn field over
B
by
\mathscr{U}_{B}=(U,B,\pi_{U})
. The existence and uniqueness of
\mathscr{U}_{B}
, up to isometry, is guaranteed by Theorem
3.5
. An important step in relating the Gromov-Hausdorff distance to the Hausdorff distance in
\mathscr{U}_{B}
is to show that any isometry between compact subfields of
\mathscr{U}_{B}
extends to an automorphism of
\mathscr{U}_{B}
. We begin with the following one-point extension lemma.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\mathscr{U}_{B}
be a Urysohn field over
B
,
A\subseteq U
a compact subset, and

a field defined on a one-point metric extension
A^{\ast}=A\sqcup\{a^{\ast}\}
satisfying
f_{A}=\pi|_{A}
. Then, there exists an isometric embedding
\imath\colon A^{\ast}\to U
over
B
such that the restriction
\imath|_{A}
is the inclusion map and
\pi\circ\imath=f
.

Proof.

A proof can be found in Appendix
A
.
∎

Proposition 4.5
.

If
\mathscr{U}_{B}
is a Urysohn field and
A,B\subseteq U
are compact subsets, then any partial isometric matching
\phi\colon A\to B
(see Definition
3.8
) of
\mathscr{U}_{B}
extends to an automorphism of
\mathscr{U}_{B}
.

Proof.

Let
C=\{x_{1},x_{2},\ldots\}
and
C^{\prime}=\{x^{\prime}_{1},x^{\prime}_{2},\ldots\}
be countable dense sets in
U\setminus A
and
U\setminus B
, respectively. Using the one-point compact extension property established in Lemma
4.4
and a back-and-forth argument applied to
C
and
C^{\prime}
, as in the proof of the uniqueness part of Theorem
3.5
,
\phi
can be extended to an isometry of
\mathscr{U}_{B}
.
∎

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
\mathscr{X}=(X,B,\pi_{X})
and
\mathscr{Y}=(Y,B,\pi_{Y})
be compact
B
-fields and
\mathscr{U}_{B}=(U,B,\pi_{U})
be the Urysohn
B
-field. Then,

d_{GH}^{B}(\mathscr{X},\mathscr{Y})=\inf_{\Phi,\Psi}d^{U}_{H}(\phi(X),\psi(Y)),

where the infimum is taken over all isometric embeddings
\Phi\colon\mathscr{X}\Rightarrow\mathscr{U}_{B}
and
\Psi\colon\mathscr{Y}\Rightarrow\mathscr{U}_{B}
over
B
.

Proof.

The inequality
d_{GH}^{B}(\mathscr{X},\mathscr{Y})\leq\inf_{\Phi,\Psi}d^{U}_{H}(\phi(X),\psi(Y))
follows from the definition of
d_{GH}^{B}
. To prove the reverse inequality, let
\epsilon>0
. We show that there are isometric embeddings
\Phi\colon\mathscr{X}\Rightarrow\mathscr{U}_{B}
and
\Psi\colon\mathscr{Y}\Rightarrow\mathscr{U}_{B}
such that

(21)

By definition of the Gromov-Hausdorff distance, there is a
B

\mathscr{Z}
and isometric embeddings
\Phi^{\prime}\colon\mathscr{X}\Rightarrow\mathscr{Z}
and
\Psi^{\prime}\colon\mathscr{Y}\Rightarrow\mathscr{Z}
over

such that

d^{Z}_{H}(\phi^{\prime}(X),\psi^{\prime}(Y))\leq d_{GH}^{B}(\mathscr{X},\mathscr{Y})+\epsilon\,.

(22)

By the universality of
\mathscr{U}_{B}
, there is an isometric embedding
\Lambda\colon\mathscr{Z}\Rightarrow\mathscr{U}_{B}
over
B
. Letting
\Phi=\Lambda\circ\Phi^{\prime}
and
\Psi=\Lambda\circ\Psi^{\prime}
, we have

(23)

Clearly, the same inequality holds if the left-hand side of (

) is replaced with the infimum over
\Phi
and
\Psi
. Taking the limit as

, we obtain the desired inequality.
∎

Given an equivalence relation

on a metric space

that makes the quotient map

be the space of compact subfields of

, which acts on

, the quotient metric may be expressed as

(24)

.

The moduli space

of isometry classes of compact

For any compact

and

shows that

where

∎

5
Metric-Measure Fields

Recall that a metric measure space (
mm
-space) is a triple
(X,d,\mu)
, where
(X,d)
is a Polish metric space and
\mu
is a Borel probability measure on
X
. The next definition introduces an analogue for fields.

Definition 5.1
.

A
metric measure field
, or
mm
-field, is a quadruple
(X,B,\pi,\mu)
, where
X
and
B
are Polish metric spaces,
\pi\colon X\to B
is a
1
-Lipschitz map, and
\mu
is a Borel probability measure on
X
. Two
mm
-fields are
isomorphic
if there is a measure-preserving isometry between them.

Given fields
(X,B,\pi_{X})
and
(Y,B,\pi_{Y})
over
B
, we let
m_{X,Y}\colon(X\times Y)\times(X\times Y)\to\real
and
d_{X,Y}\colon X\times Y\to\real
be the functions given by

\begin{split}m_{X,Y}(x,y,x^{\prime},y^{\prime})&:=|d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})-d_{Y}(y,y^{\prime})|\\
d_{X,Y}(x,y)&:=d_{B}(\pi_{X}(x),\pi_{Y}(y))\,.\end{split}

(26)

Note that

\begin{split}|m_{X,Y}(x_{1},y_{1},x_{1}^{\prime},y_{1}^{\prime})-m_{X,Y}(x_{2},y_{2},x_{2}^{\prime},y_{2}^{\prime})|&\leq|d_{X}(x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime})-d_{X}(x_{2},x_{2}^{\prime})|+|d_{Y}(y_{1},y_{1}^{\prime})-d_{Y}(y_{2},y_{2}^{\prime})|\\
&\leq d_{X}(x_{1},x_{2})+d_{X}(x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime})+d_{Y}(y_{1},y_{2})+d_{Y}(y_{1}^{\prime},y_{2}^{\prime})\\
&\leq 4\max\{d_{X}(x_{1},x_{2}),d_{Y}(y_{1},y_{2}),d_{X}(x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime}),d_{Y}(y_{1}^{\prime},y_{2}^{\prime})\}.\end{split}

(27)

Therefore, if we endow
(X\times Y)\times(X\times Y)
with the product sup metric, then
m_{X,Y}
is
4
-Lipschitz. Similarly,
d_{X,Y}
is
2
-Lipschitz. Using this notation, we introduce a field version of the Gromov-Wasserstein distance in a manner similar to
[
25
]
.

Given
(X,d_{X},\mu_{X})
and
(Y,d_{Y},\mu_{Y})
, let
C(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y})
denote the set of all couplings between
\mu_{X}
and
\mu_{Y}
, the collection of all probability measures
\mu
on
X\times Y
that marginalize to
\mu_{X}
and
\mu_{Y}
, respectively.

Definition 5.2

(Gromov-Wasserstein Distance for Fields)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}=(X,B,\pi_{X},\mu_{X})
and
\mathcal{Y}=(Y,B,\pi_{Y},\mu_{Y})
be
mm
-fields. For
1\leq p<\infty
, the
Gromov-Wasserstein distance

d_{{GW,p}}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
is defined as

\begin{split}d_{{GW,p}}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}):=\inf_{P\in C(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y})}\max\bigg\{\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\int m_{X,Y}^{p}d(P\otimes P)\bigg)^{1/p},\bigg(\int d_{X,Y}^{p}dP\bigg)^{1/p}\bigg\}.\end{split}

For
p=\infty
,

d_{GW,\infty}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}):=\inf_{P\in C(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y})}\max\left\{\frac{1}{2}\sup_{\mathrm{supp}(P\otimes P)}m_{X,Y},\,\sup_{\mathrm{supp}P}\,d_{X,Y}\right\}.

The fact that
d_{GW,{p}}
and
d_{GW,{\infty}}
are metrics can be argued as in the case of metric measure spaces (see (
[
19
]
, Theorem 5.1).

Remark 5.3
.

As
m_{X,Y}
and
d_{X,Y}
are continuous, its supremum over a set does not change after taking the closure of the set. Since the support is the smallest closest set of full measure, the suprema in the definition of
d_{GW,\infty}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
are essential suprema.

Proposition 5.4
.

For each
1\leq p\leq\infty
,
d_{{GW,p}}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
is realized by a coupling. Furthermore,

\lim_{p\to\infty}d_{{GW,p}}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})=d_{GW,\infty}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}).

Proof.

For each integer
n\geq 1
, there exists
P_{n}\in C(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y})
such that the expression on the right-hand side in the definition of
d_{{GW,p}}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
is
\leq d_{{GW,p}}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})+1/n
. By Proposition
A.1
in the appendix, without loss of generality, we can assume that
P_{n}
converges weakly to a coupling
P
, which also implies that
P_{n}\otimes P_{n}
converges weakly to
P\otimes P
(
[
2
, Theorem 2.8]
). We show that
P
is an optimal coupling.

Case 1.
Suppose that
1\leq p<\infty
. Since
m_{X,Y}
and
d_{X,Y}
are continuous and bounded below, by
[
27
, Lemma 4.3]
we have

\int m_{X,Y}^{p}d(P\otimes P)\leq\liminf_{n}m_{X,Y}^{p}d(P_{n}\otimes P_{n})\quad\text{and}\quad\int d_{X,Y}^{p}dP\leq\liminf_{n}\int d_{X,Y}^{p}dP_{n}\,.

(28)

Using (
28
) and the fact that for any sequences
(a_{n})
and
(b_{n})
of real numbers the inequality

\max\,\{\liminf_{n}a_{n},\liminf b_{n}\}\leq\liminf_{n}\max\{a_{n},b_{n}\}

(29)

holds, we obtain

\begin{split}d_{{GW,p}}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})&\leq\max\bigg\{\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\int m_{X,Y}^{p}d(P\otimes P)\bigg)^{1/p},\bigg(\int d_{X,Y}^{p}dP\bigg)^{1/p}\bigg\}\\
&\leq\liminf_{n}\max\left\{\frac{1}{2}\left(\int m_{X,Y}^{p}d(P_{n}\otimes P_{n})\right)^{1/p},\left(\int d_{X,Y}^{p}dP_{n}\right)^{1/p}\right\}\\
&\leq\liminf_{n}\left(d_{{GW,p}}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})+1/n\right)=d_{{GW,p}}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}).\end{split}

(30)

This implies that
P
realizes the Gromov-Wasserstein distance, as claimed.

Case 2
For
p=\infty
, we adapt the proof of
[
9
, Proposition 3]
to the present setting. Note that if
1\leq q\leq q^{\prime}<\infty
, then

d_{GW,{q}}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})\leq d_{GW,{q^{\prime}}}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})\leq d_{GW,\infty}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}).

(31)

Hence, we have

\lim_{q\to\infty}d_{GW,{q}}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})=\sup_{q}d_{GW,{q}}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})\leq d_{GW,\infty}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}).

(32)

Let
(q_{n})
be a sequence of real numbers satisfying
q_{n}\geq 1
and
q_{n}\to\infty
. Let
P_{n}
be the optimal coupling realizing
d_{GW,{q_{n}}}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
. Without loss of generality, we can assume that
P_{n}
converges to
P
weakly, which implies that
P_{n}\otimes P_{n}
also converges weakly to
P\otimes P
. Let
0\leq r<\max\left\{\frac{1}{2}\sup_{\mathrm{supp}(P\otimes P)}m_{X,Y},\,\sup_{\mathrm{supp}P}\,d_{X,Y}\right\}
and set

U=\{(x,y,x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\colon m_{X,Y}(x,y,x^{\prime},y^{\prime})/2>r\}\quad\text{and}\quad V=\{(x,y)\colon d_{X,Y}(x,y)>r\}.

(33)

Either
P\otimes P(U)>0
or
P(V)>0
, let us first assume that
P\otimes P(U)=2m>0
. By the Portmanteau Theorem
[
7
, Theorem 11.1.1]
,

2m\leq\liminf P_{n}\otimes P_{n}(U).

(34)

By passing to a subsequence if necessary, we can assume that
P_{n}\otimes P_{n}(U)\geq m>0
, for all
n
. We then have

d_{GW,{q_{n}}}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})\geq\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\int m_{X,Y}^{q_{n}}d(P_{n}\otimes P_{n})\bigg)^{1/q_{n}}\geq r\,(P_{n}\otimes P_{n}(U))^{1/q_{n}}\geq r\,m^{1/q_{n}}.

(35)

Hence,

\lim_{p\to\infty}d_{{GW,p}}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})=\lim_{n}d_{GW,{q_{n}}}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})\geq r.

(36)

Since
r<\max\left\{\frac{1}{2}\sup_{\mathrm{supp}(P\otimes P)}m_{X,Y},\,\sup_{\mathrm{supp}P}\,d_{X,Y}\right\}
is arbitrary, we get

\begin{split}\max\left\{\frac{1}{2}\sup_{\mathrm{supp}(P\otimes P)}m_{X,Y},\,\sup_{\mathrm{supp}P}\,d_{X,Y}\right\}&\geq d_{GW,\infty}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})\\
&\geq\lim_{p\to\infty}d_{{GW,p}}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})\\
&\geq\max\left\{\frac{1}{2}\sup_{\mathrm{supp}(P\otimes P)}m_{X,Y},\,\sup_{\mathrm{supp}P}\,d_{X,Y}\right\}.\end{split}

(37)

This shows that the coupling
P
realizes
d_{GW,{\infty}}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
and also proves the convergence claim. The case
P(V)>0
is handled similarly.
∎

The next proposition establishes a standard relation between Gromov-Wasserstein and Wasserstein distances in the setting of
mm
-fields.

Proposition 5.5
.

Let
\mathcal{X}=(X,B,\pi_{X},\mu_{X})
and
\mathcal{Y}=(Y,B,\pi_{Y},\mu_{Y})
be
mm
-fields over
B
. Suppose that
\pi_{Z}\colon Z\to B
is 1-Lipschitz and
\iota_{X}\colon X\to Z
and
\iota_{Y}\colon Y\to Z
are isometric embeddings satisfying
\pi_{X}=\pi_{Z}\circ\iota_{X}
and
\pi_{Y}=\pi_{Z}\circ\iota_{Y}
. Then, for any
1\leq p\leq\infty
, we have

d_{{GW,p}}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})\leq d_{W,p}((\iota_{X})_{*}(\mu_{X}),(\iota_{Y})_{*}(\mu_{Y}))\,.

Proof.

Let
1\leq p<\infty
. Let
Q
be the optimal coupling between
(\iota_{X})_{*}(\mu_{X})
and
(\iota_{Y})_{*}(\mu_{Y})
realizing
d_{W,p}((\iota_{X})_{*}(\mu_{X}),(\iota_{Y})_{*}(\mu_{Y}))
. Since
\iota_{X}(X)\times\iota_{Y}(Y)
has full measure in
(Z\times Z,P)
, there is a measure
P
on
X\times Y
such that
(\iota_{X}\times\iota_{Y})_{*}(P)=Q
. Since
(\iota_{X})_{*}
and
(\iota_{Y})_{*}
are injective,
P\in C(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y})
. We have

\begin{split}\bigg(\int m_{X,Y}^{p}d(P\otimes P)\bigg)^{1/p}&=\bigg(\iint|d_{Z}(w,w^{\prime})-d_{Z}(z,z^{\prime})|^{p}dQ(w,z)dQ(w^{\prime},z^{\prime})\bigg)^{1/p}\\
&\leq\bigg(\iint(d_{Z}(w,z)+d_{Z}(w^{\prime},z^{\prime}))^{p}dQ(w,z)dQ(w^{\prime},z^{\prime})\bigg)^{1/p}\\
&\leq\bigg(\int d_{Z}(w,z)^{p}dQ(w,z)\bigg)^{1/p}+\bigg(\int d_{Z}(w^{\prime},z^{\prime})^{p}dQ(w^{\prime},z^{\prime})\bigg)^{1/p}\\
&=2d_{W,p}((\iota_{X})_{*}(\mu_{X}),(\iota_{Y})_{*}(\mu_{Y})).\end{split}

(38)

Similarly,

\begin{split}\bigg(\int d_{X,Y}^{p}dP\bigg)^{1/p}&=\bigg(\int d_{B}(\pi_{Z}(w),\pi_{Z}(z))^{p}dQ(w,z)\bigg)^{1/p}\\
&\leq\bigg(\int d_{Z}(w,z)^{p}dQ(w,z)\bigg)^{1/p}=d_{W,p}((\iota_{X})_{*}(\mu_{X}),(\iota_{Y})_{*}(\mu_{Y})).\end{split}

(39)

Hence,

d_{{GW,p}}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})\leq\max\bigg\{\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\int m_{X,Y}^{p}d(P\otimes P)\bigg)^{1/p},\bigg(\int d_{X,Y}^{p}dP\bigg)^{1/p}\bigg\}\leq d_{W,p}((\iota_{X})_{*}(\mu_{X}),(\iota_{Y})_{*}(\mu_{Y})).

(40)

Letting
p\to\infty
in (
40
), we get
d_{GW,\infty}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})\leq d_{W,\infty}((\iota_{X})_{*}(\mu_{X}),(\iota_{Y})_{*}(\mu_{Y}))
.
∎

Recall that, in a metric measure space
(X,d_{X},\mu_{X})
, a sequence
(x_{n})
is called uniformly distributed (u.d.) if
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{x_{i}}/n\to\mu_{X}
weakly. Let
U_{X}
denote the set of uniformly distributed sequences in
X
. It is known that
U_{X}
is a Borel set in
X^{\infty}
and
\mu_{X}^{\infty}(U_{X})=1

[
15
]
.

The next result provides a characterization of
d_{GW,\infty}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
in terms of uniformly distributed sequences.

Theorem 5.6
.

Let
\mathcal{X}=(X,d_{X},\pi_{X},\mu_{X})
and
\mathcal{Y}=(Y,d_{Y},\pi_{Y},\mu_{Y})
be bounded
mm
-fields over
B
. Then,

d_{GW,\infty}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})=\inf_{\tiny\begin{matrix}(x_{n})\in U_{X}\\
(y_{n})\in U_{Y}\end{matrix}}\max\left\{\frac{1}{2}\sup_{i,j}m_{X,Y}(x_{i},y_{i},x_{j},y_{j}),\,\sup_{i}d_{X,Y}(x_{i},y_{i})\right\}.

Furthermore, there are sequences
(x_{n})\in U_{X}
and
(y_{n})\in U_{Y}
that realize the infimum on the right-hand side.

Proof.

Let us denote the infimum on the right-hand side by
\alpha
and let
P
be an optimal coupling realizing
d_{GW,\infty}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
. Then,

d_{GW,\infty}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})=\max\left\{\frac{1}{2}\sup_{\mathrm{supp}(P\otimes P)}m_{X,Y},\,\sup_{\mathrm{supp}P}\,d_{X,Y}\right\}.

(41)

Let
(x_{n},y_{n})
be an equidistributed sequence with respect to
P
in
\mathrm{supp}P
. Then
(x_{n})\in U_{X}
and
(y_{n})\in U_{X}
, and we have

\begin{split}\alpha&\leq\max\left\{\frac{1}{2}\sup_{i,j}m_{X,Y}(x_{i},y_{i},x_{j},y_{j}),\,\sup_{i}d_{X,Y}(x_{i},y_{i})\right\}\\
&\leq\max\left\{\frac{1}{2}\sup_{\mathrm{supp}(P\otimes P)}m_{X,Y},\,\sup_{\mathrm{supp}P}\,d_{X,Y}\right\}=d_{GW,\infty}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}).\end{split}

(42)

Now we prove the inequality in the other direction. Let
p\geq 1
,
\epsilon>0
,
(x_{n})\in U_{X}
and
(y_{n})\in U_{Y}
. Let
\mathcal{E}_{n}
be the
mm
-field with domain
E_{n}=\{1,\dots,n\}
equipped with the (pseudo)-metric
d_{E_{n}}(i,j)=d_{X}(x_{i},x_{j})
, normalized counting measure, and the 1-Lipschitz function
\pi_{E}(i)=\pi_{X}(x_{i})
. Similarly, define
\mathcal{F}_{n}
using
(y_{n})
. By Proposition
5.5
, we have

d_{{GW,p}}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{E}_{n})\leq d_{W,p}(\mu_{X},\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{x_{i}}/n)\quad\text{and}\quad d_{{GW,p}}(\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{F}_{n})\leq d_{W,p}(\mu_{Y},\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{y_{i}}/n).

(43)

Since the Wasserstein distances in the above inequalities converge to
0
as
n\to\infty
(
[
27
, Theorem 6.9]
), we can choose
n
large enough so that the Gromov-Wasserstein distances in the above inequalities are
<\epsilon
. If we use diagonal coupling
P\in C(\mathcal{E}_{n},\mathcal{F}_{n})
given by
P({i,i})=1/n
, we get

d_{{GW,p}}(\mathcal{E}_{n},\mathcal{F}_{n})\leq\max\left\{\frac{1}{2}\sup_{i,j}m_{X,Y}(x_{i},y_{i},x_{j},y_{j}),\,\sup_{i}d_{X,Y}(x_{i},y_{i})\right\}.

(44)

This, in turn, implies that

d_{{GW,p}}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})\leq\max\left\{\frac{1}{2}\sup_{i,j}m_{X,Y}(x_{i},y_{i},x_{j},y_{j}),\,\sup_{i}d_{X,Y}(x_{i},y_{i})\right\}+2\epsilon.

(45)

Since
(x_{n})\in U_{X}
,
(y_{n})\in U_{Y}
, and
\epsilon>0
is arbitrary, we get
d_{{GW,p}}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})\leq\alpha
. As
p\geq 1
is arbitrary, Proposition
5.4
implies that

d_{GW,\infty}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})=\lim_{p\to\infty}d_{{GW,p}}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})\leq\alpha.

(46)

This also shows that the sequences
(x_{n})\in U_{X}
and
(y_{n})\in U_{Y}
constructed above realizes the infimum.
∎

6
Augmented Distance Matrices

Given a sequence
(x_{n})
is an
mm
-space
(X,d,\mu)
, one can form an associated (infinite) distance matrix
D=(d_{ij})
, where
d_{ij}=d(x_{i},x_{j})
. Gromov’s Reconstruction Theorem
[
10
]
states that the distribution of all distance matrices for
(X,d,\mu)
with respect to the product measure
\mu^{\infty}
is a complete invariant. This section introduces
augmented distance matrices
to establish a similar result for
mm
-fields and also studies relationships between the Gromov-Wasserstein distance between
mm
-fields and the Wasserstein distance between the corresponding augmented distance matrix distributions. For an integer
n>0
, let

\mathcal{R}^{n}:=\{(r_{ij})\in\real^{n\times n}:r_{ij}=r_{ji},r_{ii}=0,r_{ik}\leq r_{ij}+r_{jk}\}

(47)

denote the space of all
n\times n
(pseudo) distance matrices equipped with the metric

\rho_{n}((r_{ij},b_{i}),(r^{\prime}_{ij},b^{\prime}_{i})):=\max\,(\frac{1}{2}\sup_{ij}|r_{ij}-r^{\prime}_{ij}|,\,\sup_{i}d_{B}(b_{i},b^{\prime}_{i})).

(48)

Similarly, denoting the natural numbers by
\mathbb{N}
, let

\mathcal{R}:=\{(r_{ij})\in\real^{\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}}:r_{ij}=r_{ji},r_{ii}=0,r_{ik}\leq r_{ij}+r_{jk}\}

(49)

be the space of all countably infinite (pseudo) distance matrices equipped with the weak topology; that is, the coarsest topology that makes all projections
\pi_{n}\colon\mathcal{R}\to\mathcal{R}^{n}
(onto the northwest
n\times n
quadrant) continuous,
n>0
.

Definition 6.1
.

Let
B
be a Polish space.

(i)

The space of (countably infinite)
augmented distance matrices
(ADM) is defined as
\mathcal{R}_{B}:=\mathcal{R}\times B^{\infty}
.

(ii)

Similarly, for
n>0
, define the space of
n\times n

augmented distance matrices
as
\mathcal{R}_{B}^{n}:=\mathcal{R}^{n}\times B^{n}
.

In the study of
mm
-fields
(X,B,\pi,\mu)
, if
(x_{n})
is a sequence in
X
, we associate to
(x_{n})
the ADM defined by
r_{ij}=d(x_{i},x_{j})
and
b_{i}=\pi(x_{i})
.

Definition 6.2

(ADM Distribution)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}=(X,B,\pi,\mu)
be an
mm
-field and
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{X}}:X^{\infty}\to\mathcal{R}_{B}
the map
(x_{i})\mapsto(d_{X}(x_{i},x_{j}),\pi(x_{i}))
. The
augmented distance matrix distribution
of
\mathcal{X}
is defined as
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}=(\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{X}})_{*}(\mu^{\infty})
. Similarly, for
n>0
, define
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{X}}^{n}:X^{n}\to\mathcal{R}_{B}^{n}
and
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}^{n}:=(\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{X}}^{n})_{*}(\mu^{n})
.

Theorem 6.3
.

(Field Reconstruction Theorem)
Let
\mathcal{X}=(X,d_{X},\pi_{X},\mu_{X})
and
\mathcal{Y}=(Y,d_{Y},\pi_{Y},\mu_{Y})
be
mm
-fields over
B
such that
\mu_{X}
and
\mu_{Y}
are fully supported. Then,

\mathcal{X}\simeq\mathcal{Y}\text{ if and only if }\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}=\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}.

Proof.

Clearly,
\mathcal{X}\simeq\cal Y
implies that
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}=\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}
. Suppose that
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}=\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}
. The subset of
U_{X}\subseteq X^{\infty}
of all equidistributed sequences in
X
is a Borel measurable set of full measure
[
15
, Lemma 2.4]
. Hence, its image
C_{X}:=\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{X}}(U_{X})
is an analytical set that has full measure in the completion of
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}

[
7
, Theorem 13.2.6]
. Define
C_{Y}
similarly. By construction,
C_{X}\cap C_{Y}
is of full measure in the completion of
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}=\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}
. Let
(x_{n})\in U_{X}
,
(y_{n})\in U_{Y}
such that
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{X}}((x_{n}))=\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{Y}}((y_{n}))
. The map
\phi\colon\{x_{n}\colon n\geq 1\}\to\{y_{n}\colon n\geq 1\}
given by
\phi(x_{i})=y_{i}
is a well-defined isometry satisfying
\pi_{X}(x_{i})=\pi_{Y}(y_{i})
. Since
(x_{n})
and
(y_{n})
are dense in
X
and
Y
, respectively,
\phi
induces an isometry
\Phi\colon\mathcal{X}\Rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
.
∎

On the space
\mathcal{R}_{B}
, we also define the (extended) metric

\rho((r_{ij},b_{i}),(r^{\prime}_{ij},b^{\prime}_{i})):=\max(\frac{1}{2}\sup_{ij}|r_{ij}-r^{\prime}_{ij}|,\,\sup_{i}d_{B}(b_{i},b^{\prime}_{i})).

(50)

The metric
\rho_{n}
over
\mathcal{R}^{n}_{B}
is defined similarly. However, since
(\mathcal{R}_{B},\rho)
is not separable, instead of using
\rho
to define a topology on
\mathcal{R}_{B}
, we only employ it to formulate the Wasserstein distance in
\mathcal{R}_{B}
. The next lemma shows that
\rho
is sufficiently regular for the Wasserstein distance so defined to satisfy some desirable properties.

Lemma 6.4
.

The extended function
\rho\colon\mathcal{R}_{B}\times\mathcal{R_{B}}\to[0,\infty]
is lower semicontinuous.

Proof.

Let
\pi_{n}:\mathcal{R}_{B}\to\mathcal{R}_{B}^{n}
denote the projection map. Note that
\rho_{n}\circ\pi_{n}\uparrow\rho
pointwise. Hence, as a pointwise supremum of a sequence of continuous functions,
\rho
is lower semicontinuous.
∎

In the discussion below, the
p
-Wasserstein distances
d_{W,p}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}},\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{Y}})
and
d_{W,p}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}^{n},\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}^{n})
are taken with respect to the distance functions
\rho
and
\rho_{n}
, respectively.

Theorem 6.5
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
be bounded
mm
-fields over
B
. Then, for any
1\leq p\leq\infty
, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}d_{W,p}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}^{n},\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}^{n})=d_{W,p}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}},\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{Y}})=d_{GW,\infty}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}).

Proof.

The projection map
\pi_{n}^{n+1}\colon\mathcal{R}_{B}^{n+1}\to\mathcal{R}^{n}_{B}
is
1
-Lipschitz and has the property that

(\pi_{n}^{n+1})_{\ast}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}^{n+1})=\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}^{n}\quad\text{and}\quad(\pi_{n}^{n+1})_{\ast}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}^{n+1})=\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}^{n}\,.

(51)

This implies that
d_{W,p}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}^{n},\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}^{n})\leq d_{W,p}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}^{n+1},\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}^{n+1})
. By a similar argument, we get
d_{W,p}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}^{n},\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}^{n})\leq d_{W,p}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}},\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{Y}})
. Therefore, we have

\begin{split}\lim_{n\to\infty}d_{W,p}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}^{n},\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}^{n})=\sup_{n}d_{W,p}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}^{n},\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}^{n})\leq d_{W,p}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}},\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}).\end{split}

(52)

Since
\rho
is lower semicontinuous by Lemma
6.4
, using an argument similar to the proof of Proposition
5.4
, one can show that
d_{W,\infty}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}},\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{Y}})=\lim_{p\to\infty}d_{W,p}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}},\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{Y}})
. Therefore, without loss of generality, we can assume that
p<\infty
.

Now, we show that
d_{W,p}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}},\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{Y}})\leq d_{GW,\infty}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
. Let
P
be the optimal coupling between
\mu_{X}
and
\mu_{Y}
realizing
d_{GW,\infty}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
. Let
\psi_{X}:(X\times Y)^{\infty}\to\mathcal{R}_{B}
be the map given by
(x_{n},y_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\mapsto\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{X}}((x_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty})
. Define
\psi_{Y}
similarly. Then
Q:=(\psi_{X},\psi_{Y})_{*}(P^{\infty})
is a coupling between
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}
and
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}
. We have

\begin{split}d_{W,p}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}},\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{Y}})&\leq\bigg(\int\rho^{p}dQ\bigg)^{1/p}=\bigg(\int_{\mathrm{supp}P^{\infty}}\rho^{p}(\psi_{X}((x_{n},y_{n})_{n}),\psi_{Y}((x_{n},y_{n})_{n})dP^{\infty}((x_{n},y_{n})_{n})\bigg)^{1/p}\\
&=\bigg(\int_{(\mathrm{supp}P)^{\infty}}\max\big(\frac{1}{2}\sup_{i,j}m_{X,Y}(x_{i},y_{i},x_{j},y_{j}),\sup_{i}d_{X,Y}(x_{i},y_{i})\big)^{p}dP^{\infty}((x_{n},y_{n})_{n})\bigg)^{1/p}\\
&\leq\max\left\{\frac{1}{2}\sup_{\mathrm{supp}(P\otimes P)}m_{X,Y},\,\sup_{\mathrm{supp}P}\,d_{X,Y}\right\}=d_{GW,\infty}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}).\end{split}

(53)

It remains to show that
d_{GW,\infty}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})\leq\lim_{N}d_{W,p}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}^{n},\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}^{n})
. Let
0<\epsilon<1/2
. By Proposition
5.4
, there exists
1\leq q<\infty
so that
d_{GW,\infty}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})\leq d_{GW,{q}}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})+\epsilon
. Let

U_{X,q}^{n,\epsilon}:=\{(x_{i})\in X^{n}\colon d_{W,{q}}(\mu_{X},\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{x_{i}}/n)\leq\epsilon\}.

(54)

Define
U_{Y,q}^{n,\epsilon}
similarly. By Proposition
A.2
, if
n
large enough, then
\mu_{X}^{n}(U_{X,q}^{n,\epsilon})\geq 1-\epsilon
and
\mu_{Y}^{n}(U_{Y,q}^{n,\epsilon})\geq 1-\epsilon
. If we define
C_{X,q}^{n,\epsilon}:=\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{X}}^{n}(U_{X,q}^{n,\epsilon})
and
C_{Y,q}^{n,\epsilon}:=\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{X}}^{n}(U_{Y,q}^{n,\epsilon})
, both of these sets are analytical, hence measurable in the completion of
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}^{n}
and
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}^{n}
, respectively
[
7

. Moreover, the measures of these sets are
\geq 1-\epsilon

7
Summary and Discussion

This paper studied functional data, termed fields, defined on geometric domains. More precisely, the objects of study were 1-Lipschitz functions between Polish metric spaces with the domain possibly equipped with a Borel probability measure. We addressed foundational questions and developed new approaches to the analysis of datasets consisting of fields not necessarily defined on the same domains. We constructed Urysohn fields for a fixed target Polish space
B
; that is, universal and homogeneous objects for fields over
B
. We also constructed ultimate Urysohn fields that have analogous universality and homogeneity properties, but allow the target Polish space to also vary. We defined and investigated the basic properties of a Gromov-Hausdorff distance between compact fields and how it relates to the Hausdorff distance in the Urysohn field via functional isometric embeddings. Similarly, we studied a notion of Gromov-Wasserstein distance between fields with metric-measure domains.

We also introduced a representation of metric-measure fields as probability distributions on the space of (countably) infinite augmented distance matrices and proved a Reconstruction Theorem that extends to
mm
-fields a corresponding result for
mm
-spaces due to Gromov. This provides a pathway to discrete representations of
mm
-fields via distributions of finite-dimensional augmented distance matrices for which we proved a convergence theorem.

Questions that also are of interest but fall beyond the scope of this paper include: (i) the study of rate of convergence of the probabilistic model based on finite-dimensional augmented distance matrices; (ii) investigation of alternative cost functions in the formulation of the Gromov-Wasserstein distance between
mm
-fields; (iii) the development of computational models derived from augmented distance matrices.

Proposition A.4
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
,
\mathcal{Y}
be
mm
-fields over
B

\mu_{X}
and
\mu_{Y}

and define
\pi_{Z}\colon Z\to B

\pi_{Z}|_{X}:=\pi_{X}
and
\pi_{Z}|_{Y}=\pi_{Y}

d_{Z}(x,y)=d_{Z}(y,x):=r+\inf_{(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in\mathrm{supp}P}d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})+d_{Y}(y,y^{\prime})\,

is a metric on

\begin{split}d_{Z}(x_{1},y)+d_{Z}(y,x_{2})&=2r+\inf_{(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime},y^{\prime\prime})\in\mathrm{supp}(P\otimes P)}d_{X}(x_{1},x^{\prime})+d_{Y}(y^{\prime},y)+d_{Y}(y,y^{\prime\prime})+d_{X}(x_{2},x^{\prime\prime})\\
