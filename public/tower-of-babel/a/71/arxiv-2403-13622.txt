Nonlocality of the energy density of a spontaneously emitted single-photon from a Hydrogen atom
M. Federico, H.R. Jauslin

Abstract
We analyze through the expectation value of the energy density the spatial nonlocality of single photons emitted by the spontaneous decay of a Hydrogen atom. By using a minimal coupling between the quantized electromagnetic field and the atom, we compute the state of the photon under the assumption that only a single-photon is produced. The calculations are thus performed in the subspace of single-photon states which is essentially equivalent to the rotating wave approximation. We obtain a characterization of the spatial decay of the energy density. We compute the asymptotic limit of large distances from the atom at each given time, and find an algebraic behavior of 1 / r 6 1/r^{6} . This result confirms that the energy density of single-photon states is nonlocal and the algebraic decay is far from the maximal quasiexponential localization predicted by the theory.



I
Introduction

The description of the spontaneous decay of a Hydrogen atom is a standard calculation that can be done using either the Weisskopf-Wigner theory
Weisskopf and Wigner 1930
;
Spohn 2009
;
Novotny and Hecht 2012
or time-dependent perturbation theory, e.g., with the Fermi golden rule
Novotny and Hecht 2012
. However, these approaches in general consider a dipolar coupling between the field and the atom, taken as a two-level system, which prevents from computing the state of the emitted photons since there is a frequency Lamb shift given by a diverging intergral. The treatment of this divergence could be done by a renormalization procedure, as suggested in
Dereziński and Früboes 2002
. This problem was discussed, e.g., in
Moses 1973
;
Moses 1975a
;
Moses 1975b
;
Grimm and Ernst 1974
;
Grimm and Ernst 1975
;
Seke and Herfort 1988
and a way out was found by considering a minimal coupling instead of the dipole approximation, which provides an intrinsic ultraviolet cutoff function that avoids the need of a renormalization. In this article we analyze the spatial properties of the single-photon states that would be produced in this process. In view of the intrinsic spatial nonlocality of single-photons, as discussed, e.g., in
Knight 1961
;
Hegerfeldt 1974
;
Hegerfeldt 1994
;
Hegerfeldt 1997
;
Hegerfeldt 1998
;
Białynicki-Birula 1998
;
Białynicki-Birula and Białynicka-Birula 2009
;
Federico and Jauslin 2023a
, we would like to characterize this nonlocality from the perspective of spontaneous emission. To do so, we will consider the approach that uses the energy density to probe the nonlocality of photons, as it was originally done in
Białynicki-Birula 1998
;
Białynicki-Birula and Białynicka-Birula 2009
and recently extended to all single-photon states in
Federico and Jauslin 2023a
. In this article it was shown that for any single-photon state, the expectation value of the energy density in a small volume is different from zero anywhere in space.
This result leads to several natural questions if we consider the photons produced by some concrete source: (i) If one assumes that what is produced are single-photon states, what would be their spatial localization properties? (ii) How does the mean value of the energy density depend on the distance between the source and the detector?
We analyze these questions with the example of the spontaneous emission of a Hydrogen atom prepared in its first excited state.
We start with the complete Hamiltonian of the atom interacting with the quantized electromagnetic field. The hypothesis that only a single-photon is produced is implemented by projecting the model to the single-photon subspace. Furthermore we keep only the fundamental and the first excited state of the atom, corresponding to the Lyman-
\alpha
transition. The time evolution in the Schrödinger picture can be represented in terms of a system of Weisskopf-Wigner equations that can be solved exactly in terms of some integrals
Spohn 2009
. This provides the spatio-temporal description of the photon state, which we analyze at each fixed time as a function of the distance from the source.
The expectation value of the electromagnetic energy density is non-zero in any finite volume at arbitrary distances
r
from the source, as it was proven for all single-photon states in
Federico and Jauslin 2023a
.
The asymptotic analysis for large distances leads to the result that at each fixed time the energy density is proportional to
1/r^{6}
.

We conclude that the spontaneous emission cannot produce a strictly single-photon state since it would contradict causality
Berman 2004
;
Dolce et al. 2006
: At any fixed time
t
there would be a nonzero probability of detecting the photon with an instrument located at an arbitrary distance
r
from the source, in particular one could take
r>ct
, where
c
is the speed of light
Gulla and Skaar 2021
;
Ryen et al. 2022
;
Gulla et al. 2023
.
We remark that we made the analysis for the situation where the atom is in the first excited state at
t=0
, but a similar argument can be made if at
t=0
the atom is in a linear combination of the ground state and the first excited state. This could correspond to the state prepared with a
\pi
-pulse of finite duration.

Several works have already addressed related questions in the context of spontaneous emission
Hegerfeldt 1994
;
Biswas et al. 1990
;
Buchholz and Yngvason 1994
;
Keller 2000
;
Chan et al. 2002
;
Fedorov et al. 2005
. In particular, causality properties have been analyzed in
Debierre et al. 2015a
;
Debierre et al. 2015b
;
Debierre and Durt 2016
;
Debierre et al. 2018
, where different regimes, in the near- mid- and far-field regions were described in terms of wave functions constructed by Glauber’s extraction principle from a first order correlation function. This wave function is identical, up to a global constant, with the Białynicki-Birula representation of single-photon states
Białynicki-Birula 1996
, which is in turn equivalent to the representation of the states that we will use in this work (Landau-Peierls)
Federico and Jauslin 2023b
. However, we note that the formulas (25) in Ref.
Debierre and Durt 2016
do not provide the asymptotic behavior for large distances
r=\absolutevalue*{\vec x}
, since they still contain integrals over
k
involving functions of
\absolutevalue*{\vec x}
that will modify the asymptotic form for large
\absolutevalue*{\vec x}
. Moreover, we stress that our spatial analysis will not be performed for the state of the field but for its energy density expectation value. This is a major difference since it has been shown (see, e.g.,
Federico and Jauslin 2023a
) that in some particular situations, the state function can be spatially localized even though the corresponding energy density features a nonlocality. The nonlocality property is thus not a property of the states alone but of the joint representation of the states and the corresponding local observables.

Causality properties were also analyzed in
Compagno et al. 1990
where it was shown using the electric energy density observable that the nonresonant terms are needed for the spontaneous emission to be causal. This result is in agreement with the characterization we perform in this work for the resonant part, for which we also provide an expilcit asymptotic behavior. Such an asymptotic analysis, performed through perturbation theory calculations, was also done in
Compagno et al. 1995
but for the dressed vacuum only, i.e., atom in its ground state and zero photons.

Finally, as it was noted by Shirokov
Shirokov 1978
, since the eigenfunctions of the Hydrogen atom decrease exponentially in the radial variable, they are nonzero everywhere. Therefore the emision of photons cannot be expected to be local, but should have at least an exponential tail. This effect has been described as a blurring of the causal light cone. However, this is an exponential decay which is a relatively small effect compared to the nonlocality properties described below.

To analyze the spatial properties of the photon states, we will use throughout this work a position space formulation of the quantum theory of the electromagnetic field called the Landau-Peierls representation (see Appendix
A
for its definition). Any other position space representation could in principle be used and the result is independent of this choice
Federico and Jauslin 2023b
.

The structure and the main results of the article are as follows. In Section
II
we formulate the Weisskopf-Wigner model with the
\vec{\hat{A}}\cdot\vec{\hat{p}}
interaction and we use the vector spherical harmonics and helicity basis for single-photon states, as proposed in

Moses 1973
;
Moses 1975a
;
Moses 1975b
;
Grimm and Ernst 1974
;
Grimm and Ernst 1975
;
Seke and Herfort 1988
. This basis has the advantage that it leads easily to exact selection rules for the interaction with Hydrogen atoms.
In Section
III
we write the Weisskopf-Wigner solution in terms of integrals. This solution is exact under the condition that the spectrum of the associated Friedrichs operator is absolutely continuous, which is expected in the small coupling regime. Some of these integrals can be calculated explicitly if one makes a resonant approximation which has essentially the same effect as the Weisskopf-Wigner approximation, but without extending an integral to non-physical negative frequencies.
With the coupled atom-electromagnetic field state, we calculate in Section
IV
the expectation value of the energy density at an arbitrary position in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
We then analyze its dependence on the radial distance, in particular for the far-field, which characterizes the global extension of the nonlocality. We obtain the result that the expectation value of the energy density is proportional to
1/r^{6}
for large
r
, i.e., it has an algebraic decay, which is far from the theoretically maximal quasiexponential decay predicted in
Białynicki-Birula 1998
.

II
Weisskopf-Wigner model

We consider a non-relativistic Hydrogen atom represented by a two-level system corresponding to the Lyman-
\alpha
transition, i.e., with ground state
\ket{n_g=1;j_g=0;m_g=0}\equiv\ket{g}
and excited state
\ket{n_e=2;j_e=1;m_e=0,\pm 1}\equiv\ket{e}
. This transition is relevant since it can be prepared experimentally by applying, e.g., a resonant
\pi
-pulse on the ground state with a linearly polarized laser light for
m_{e}=0
and a circularly polarized laser light for
m_{e}=\pm 1
. The wavefunctions associated to the ground and excited states are, respectively,

\ket{g}=\varphi_{g}(r,\vartheta,\varphi)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi r_{\text{B}}^{3}}}e^{-\frac{r}{r_{\text{B}}}},\quad\quad\quad\ket{e}=\varphi_{e}(r,\vartheta,\varphi)=\beta_{m_{e}}(\vartheta,\varphi)\frac{r}{r_{\text{B}}}e^{-\frac{r}{2r_{\text{B}}}},

(1)

where
r_{\text{B}}=\hbar/(\alpha mc)
is the Bohr radius expressed, here, in terms of the fine structure constant
\alpha
, the electron mass
m
, and the speed of light
c
. The functions
\beta_{m_{e}}(\vartheta,\varphi)
depend on the choice that is made for
m_{e}=\{0,\pm 1\}
and read

\beta_{0}(\vartheta,\varphi)=\frac{1}{4\sqrt{2\pi r_{\text{B}}^{3}}}\cos\vartheta,\quad\quad\quad\beta_{\pm 1}(\vartheta,\varphi)=\mp\frac{1}{8\sqrt{\pi r_{\text{B}}^{3}}}\sin\vartheta\ e^{\pm i\varphi}.

(2)

The free Hamiltonian of the two-level atom can be expressed as

\hat{H}_{\text{at}}=E_{g}\ket{g}\bra{g}+E_{e}\ket{e}\bra{e},

(3)

where
E_{g}
and
E_{e}
are the energies of the ground and excited states, respectively.
To describe the spontaneous emission, the two-level Hydrogen atom is interacting with the quantized electromagnetic field which we write using the basis
\ket{k,J,M,\lambda}\equiv\vec{\psi}_{k,J,M}^{(\lambda)}
of helicity vector spherical harmonics (see Appendix
B
for more details about these functions)

\hat{H}_{\text{em}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}dk\sum_{J,M}\sum_{\lambda=\pm}\hbar\omega_{k}\hat{B}^{\dagger}_{\vec{\psi}_{k,J,M}^{(\lambda)}}\hat{B}_{\vec{\psi}_{k,J,M}^{(\lambda)}}.

(4)

We remark, here, that the creation-annihilation operators
\big(\hat{B}^{\dagger}_{\vec{\psi}_{k,J,M}^{(\lambda)}},\hat{B}_{\vec{\psi}_{k,J,M}^{(\lambda)}}\big)
are defined on functions in the Landau-Peierls representation
Landau and Peierls 1930
;
Federico and Jauslin 2023b
(see Appendix
A
), and fulfill the following commutation relation
\big[\hat{B}_{\vec{\psi}_{k,J,M}^{(\lambda)}},\hat{B}_{\vec{\psi}_{k^{\prime},J^{\prime},M^{\prime}}^{(\lambda^{\prime})}}^{\dagger}\big]=\delta(k-k^{\prime})\delta_{J,J^{\prime}}\delta_{M,M^{\prime}}\delta_{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}
.
For the interaction part, and following
Moses 1973
, we take an operator of the form

\hat{H}_{\text{int}}=-\frac{e}{m}\vec{\hat{p}}\cdot\vec{\hat{A}}(\vec{x}),

(5)

where
\vec{\hat{p}}=-i\hbar\nabla
is the momentum operator of the electron and
\vec{\hat{A}}(\vec{x})
is the vector potential operator in the Coulomb gauge. We have neglected the
\hat{A}^{2}
term coming from the minimal coupling since it has been shown by Moses
Moses 1973
(see also
Grimm and Ernst 1974
;
Grimm and Ernst 1975
) that taking an interaction of the form
\vec{\hat{p}}\cdot\vec{\hat{A}}
is enough to have a consistent model allowing to compute the photon state. Moreover, the typical order of magnitude for the effects related to the
\hat{A}^{2}
term have been estimated to be much smaller than those from the
\vec{\hat{p}}\cdot\vec{\hat{A}}
term
Mandel and Wolf 1995
.
The resulting total Hamiltonian is finally

\displaystyle\hat{H}

\displaystyle=\hat{H}_{\text{at}}+\hat{H}_{\text{em}}+\hat{H}_{\text{int}}

(6a)

\displaystyle=E_{g}\ket{g}\bra{g}+E_{e}\ket{e}\bra{e}+\int_{0}^{\infty}dk\sum_{J,M}\sum_{\lambda=\pm}\hbar\omega_{k}\hat{B}^{\dagger}_{\vec{\psi}_{k,J,M}^{(\lambda)}}\hat{B}_{\vec{\psi}_{k,J,M}^{(\lambda)}}-\frac{e}{m}\vec{\hat{p}}\cdot\vec{\hat{A}}(\vec{x}).

(6b)

In the following, since we are interested in the characterization of single photons, we project the system into the subspace generated by the vacuum and the single-photon states, which corresponds in the tensor product Hilbert space to the subspace generated by

\{\ket{e;\varnothing},\ket*{g;\vec\psi_{k,J,M}^{(\lambda)}}\},

i.e., the atom in its excited state and zero photons and the atom in its ground state and one photon. This approximation is essentially equivalent to the rotating wave approximation that is performed in general for the dipole coupling. Within this subspace, the interaction coefficient takes the form
Moses 1973

\displaystyle\rho(k,J,M,\lambda)

\displaystyle=-\frac{e}{m}\bra*{g;\vec\psi_{k,J,M}^{(\lambda)}}\vec{\hat{p}}\cdot\vec{\hat{A}}(\vec{x})\ket*{e;\varnothing}

(7a)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{7/2}\sqrt{\frac{\alpha^{5}}{\pi}}mc^{2}\frac{k/K}{\sqrt{k}}\frac{\delta_{J,1}\delta_{M,m_{e}}}{\left[1+\left(\frac{k}{K}\right)^{2}\right]^{2}}

(7b)

\displaystyle\equiv\rho(k)\delta_{J,1}\delta_{M,m_{e}},

(7c)

where
K=3/(2r_{\text{B}})
.
Here, we have chosen a slightly different convention for the global phases of the eigenfunctions
\vec{\psi}_{k,J,M}^{(\lambda)}
than the one of
Moses 1973
. Our convention has the advantage that
\rho
is real and independent of the helicity
\lambda
.

III
Solution in terms of integrals

Starting with a general state in the subspace of interest

\ket{\Psi}=c_{0}\ket{e;\varnothing}+\int_{0}^{\infty}dk\sum_{J,M,\lambda}c_{k,J,M,\lambda}\ket*{g;\vec\psi_{k,J,M}^{(\lambda)}},

(8)

we want to solve the Schrödinger equation

i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\ket{\Psi}=\hat{H}\ket{\Psi},

(9)

with the initial condition
c_{0}(t=0)=1
and
c_{k,J,M,\lambda}(t=0)=0
, for any
k,J,M
and
\lambda
. Expressed for the nonzero coefficients, we arrive at the set of equations

\displaystyle i\hbar\dot{c}_{0}(t)

\displaystyle=E_{e}c_{0}(t)+\int_{0}^{\infty}dk\sum_{\lambda=\pm}\rho(k)c_{k,\lambda}(t),

(10a)

\displaystyle i\hbar\dot{c}_{k,\lambda}(t)

\displaystyle=\rho(k)c_{0}(t)+\left(E_{g}+\hbar\omega_{k}\right)c_{k,\lambda}(t),

(10b)

where we have introduced the shorter notation
c_{k,J=1,M=m_{e},\lambda}\equiv c_{k,\lambda}
. Moreover, since
\rho(k)
is
\lambda
-independent, the last equation is also
\lambda
-independent and thus
c_{k,+}(t)=c_{k,-}(t)
for all
t
and one can define
d_{k}(t)=\sqrt{2}c_{k,\lambda}(t)
, to rewrite the set of dynamical equations as

\displaystyle i\dot{c}_{0}(t)

\displaystyle=\omega_{\text{a}}c_{0}(t)+\int_{0}^{\infty}dk\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\hbar}\rho(k)d_{k}(t),

(11a)

\displaystyle i\dot{d}_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\hbar}\rho(k)c_{0}(t)+\omega_{k}d_{k}(t),

(11b)

where we have set the energy of the ground state to
E_{g}=0
, and introduced the Bohr frequency of the two-level system
\omega_{\text{a}}=(E_{e}-E_{g})/\hbar
. Expressed in terms of
\omega_{k}=ck
, equations (
11
) can be written in a matrix form

i\begin{bmatrix}\dot{c}_{0}\\
\dot{D}_{\omega_{k}}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\omega_{\text{a}}&\bra{\tilde\rho}\ket{\cdot}\\
\tilde{\rho}&\omega_{k}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}c_{0}\\
D_{\omega_{k}}\end{bmatrix},

(12)

where
D_{\omega_{k}}=d_{\omega_{k}}/\sqrt{c}
and

\tilde{\rho}(\omega_{k})=\sqrt{\frac{2}{c\hbar^{2}}}\rho(\omega_{k}).

(13)

This formulation is known as the Friedrichs-Lee model
Friedrichs 1948
;
Lee 1954
and the exact solution in terms of integrals can be formulated as
Spohn 2009

\displaystyle c_{0}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\ g(\omega)e^{-i\omega t},

(14a)

\displaystyle D_{\omega_{k}}(t)

\displaystyle=-ie^{-i\omega_{k}t}\tilde{\rho}(\omega_{k})\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\ e^{i\omega_{k}t^{\prime}}c_{0}(t^{\prime}),

(14b)

with

g(\omega)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{\Gamma(\omega)}{(\omega-\omega_{e}-\Delta(\omega))^{2}+\frac{\Gamma(\omega)^{2}}{4}},

(15)

and

\Gamma(\omega)=2\pi\absolutevalue*{\tilde\rho(\omega)}^{2},\quad\quad\quad\Delta(\omega)=\text{pv}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega_{k}\ \frac{\absolutevalue*{\tilde\rho(\omega_k)}^{2}}{\omega-\omega_{k}},

(16)

where
pv
is the principal value.
In the weak coupling regime,
g(\omega)
has a high peak centered at
\omega=\omega_{\text{a}}
and one can thus make the following approximation
Spohn 2009

\displaystyle g(\omega)\simeq g_{\text{w}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{\Gamma(\omega_{\text{a}})}{(\omega-\omega_{\text{a}}-\Delta(\omega_{\text{a}}))^{2}+\frac{\Gamma(\omega_{\text{a}})^{2}}{4}}

(17a)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{\Gamma_{\text{a}}}{(\omega-\omega_{\text{a}}-\Delta_{\text{a}})^{2}+\frac{\Gamma_{\text{a}}^{2}}{4}},

(17b)

where
\Gamma_{\text{a}}=\Gamma(\omega_{\text{a}})
and
\Delta_{\text{a}}=\Delta(\omega_{\text{a}})
. This approximation allows to express
c_{0}(t)
as

c_{0}(t)\simeq\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\ g_{\text{w}}(\omega)e^{-i\omega t}=e^{-\frac{\Gamma_{\text{a}}}{2}t}e^{-i(\omega_{\text{a}}+\Delta_{\text{a}})t},

(18)

yielding a result that is essentially equivalent to the Weisskopf-Wigner approximation. From this, one can further compute

D_{\omega_{k}}(t)\simeq-ie^{-i\omega_{k}t}\tilde{\rho}(\omega_{k})\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\ e^{-\frac{\Gamma_{\text{a}}}{2}t^{\prime}}e^{-i(\omega_{\text{a}}-\omega_{k}+\Delta_{\text{a}})t^{\prime}}.

(19)

IV
Energy density expectation value

Following
Białynicki-Birula 1998
;
Federico and Jauslin 2023a
, we want to characterize the localization of the emitted single-photon state using the energy density operator

\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\text{em}}(\vec{x})=\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{2}:\left(\vec{\hat{E}}^{2}(\vec{x})+c^{2}\vec{\hat{B}}^{2}(\vec{x})\right):,

(20)

for which the expectation value for a single-photon state
\ket*{\vec\psi}
in the Landau-Peierls (LP) representation (see Appendix
A
) reads
Białynicki-Birula 1998
;
Federico and Jauslin 2023a

\langle\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\text{em}}(\vec{x})\rangle=\hbar\absolutevalue{\Omega^{1/2}\vec\psi^{(h+)}(\vec x)}^{2}+\hbar\absolutevalue{\Omega^{1/2}\vec\psi^{(h-)}(\vec x)}^{2}.

(21)

The exponents
(h±)
refer here to the positive and negative helicity parts of the LP representation (see Appendix
A
). This splitting into helicity components is an important feature of the result since it leads to the nonlocality of the energy density for all single-photon states
Federico and Jauslin 2023a
that we want to characterize for the spontaneous emission.

Using the results of the preceding section, we are going to express the state of the photon that is spontaneously emitted by the Lyman-
\alpha
transition of the Hydrogen atom in the single-photon approximation, and then compute the associated expectation value of the electromagnetic energy density.
We consider here only the photon part of the state, i.e.,

\vec{\psi}_{\text{ph}}(r,\vartheta,\varphi)=\int_{0}^{\infty}dk\sum_{\lambda=\pm}c_{k,\lambda}(t)\vec{\psi}^{(\lambda)}_{k}(r,\vartheta,\varphi),

(22)

where we use the abridged notation
\vec{\psi}^{(\lambda)}_{k}\equiv\vec{\psi}^{(\lambda)}_{k,J=1,M=m_{e}}
.
The positive and negative helicity parts of the state can be written as

\vec{\psi}_{\text{ph}}^{(h\pm)}(r,\vartheta,\varphi)=\int_{0}^{\infty}dk\frac{d_{k}(t)}{\sqrt{2}}\vec{\psi}^{(\pm)}_{k}(r,\vartheta,\varphi).

(23)

Therefore, to compute the mean value, one needs to express

\absolutevalue{\Omega^{1/2}\vec\psi_{\text{ph}}^{(h\lambda)}(r,\vartheta,\varphi)}^{2}=\absolutevalue{\int_0^\infty dk\sqrt{\frac{\omega_k}{2}}d_k(t)\vec\psi^{(\lambda)}_k(r,\vartheta,\varphi)}^{2}

(24)

for both helicities. To do so, we use the expression of
\vec{\psi}_{k}^{(\lambda)}
in terms of vector spherical harmonics and Bessel functions, as given in Appendix
B
, to obtain

\absolutevalue{\Omega^{1/2}\vec\psi_{\text{ph}}^{(h\lambda)}}^{2}=\sum_{L=0}^{2}F_{L}(r,t)\vec{Y}_{1,m_{e}}^{L}(\vartheta,\varphi),

(25)

where the
F_{L}
functions are

\displaystyle F_{0}(r,t)

\displaystyle=i\sqrt{\frac{c}{3\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dk\ k^{3/2}d_{k}(t)j_{0}(kr),

(26a)

\displaystyle F_{1}(r,t)

\displaystyle=\lambda\sqrt{\frac{c}{2\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dk\ k^{3/2}d_{k}(t)j_{1}(kr),

(26b)

\displaystyle F_{2}(r,t)

\displaystyle=-i\sqrt{\frac{c}{6\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dk\ k^{3/2}d_{k}(t)j_{2}(kr).

(26c)

In order to simplify the expression of the mean value, we perform an asymptotic analysis of the
F_{L}
functions for distances
r
far from the atom. The details of this analysis are given in the Appendix
C
. We obtain that the leading term is
F_{1}
which behaves as

F_{1}(r,t)\underset{r\to\infty}{\sim}-i\lambda\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{11/2}\sqrt{\frac{\alpha^{5}}{c}}\frac{mc^{2}r_{\text{B}}^{2}}{\pi\hbar}\mathcal{T}(t)\frac{1}{r^{3}},

(27)

where
\mathcal{T}(t)
is a dimensionless function of time given in equation (
66
). The expectation value of the energy density becomes thus in the asymptotic limit of large
r

\absolutevalue{\Omega^{1/2}\vec\psi_{\text{ph}}^{(h\lambda)}(\vec x)}^{2}\underset{r\to\infty}{\sim}\absolutevalue*{F_1(r,t)}^{2}\vec{Y}_{1,m_{e}}^{1\star}\cdot\vec{Y}_{1,m_{e}}^{1},

(28)

which can be computed for different values of
m_{e}
.

Figure 1:
Plot of the angular distribution
\gamma_{\absolutevalue*{m_e}}(\vartheta)
of the photon state emitted by spontaneous emission of the Lyman-
\alpha
transition of a Hydrogen atom, obtained in the asymptotic limit of large distances. The green solid line is the distribution for an excited state with
m_{e}=0
. The red dashed line is the distribution for an excited state with
\absolutevalue*{m_e}=1
.

For
m_{e}=0
, the angular part reads

\vec{Y}_{1,0}^{1\star}\cdot\vec{Y}_{1,0}^{1}=\frac{3}{8\pi}\sin^{2}\vartheta,

(29)

and for
\absolutevalue*{m_e}=1

\vec{Y}_{1,\pm 1}^{1\star}\cdot\vec{Y}_{1,\pm 1}^{1}=\frac{3}{16\pi}\left(1+\cos^{2}\vartheta\right).

(30)

In the end, the expectation value as a funtion of
m_{e}
can be expressed as

\langle\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\text{em}}\rangle=\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{10}\frac{m^{2}c^{3}r_{\text{B}}^{4}\alpha^{5}}{2\pi^{3}\hbar}\gamma_{\absolutevalue*{m_e}}(\vartheta)\absolutevalue{\mathcal{T}(t)}^{2}\frac{1}{r^{6}}+o\left(\frac{1}{r^{6}}\right),

(31)

where the angular distribution is given by the functions
\gamma_{\absolutevalue*{m_e}}
which read for the different values of
m_{e}

\gamma_{0}(\vartheta)=\sin^{2}\vartheta,\quad\quad\quad\gamma_{1}(\vartheta)=\frac{1}{4}(1+\cos^{2}\vartheta),

(32)

and are represented in FIG.
1
. We point out that the angular distribution we have found matches that of a classical dipole that is either oscillating linearly for
\gamma_{0}
or circularly for
\gamma_{1}

Jackson 1999
;
Steck 2023
. We further remark that in the
m_{e}=0
case, the leading term of the asymptotics is zero for
\vartheta=0
due to the shape of the angular distribution. However, we emphasize that the spontaneous emission in this direction is not zero but features a faster falloff.

The result obtained in equation (
31
) exhibits an algebraic decrease for the energy density which is therefore far from the quasiexponential limit introduced in
Białynicki-Birula 1998
, and cannot be attributed only to the blurring of the light cone as described in
Shirokov 1978
. This behavior is an illustration of the intrinsic nonlocality of the energy density of single-photon states, as shown for particular cases in
Białynicki-Birula and Białynicka-Birula 2009
and for all single-photon states in
Federico and Jauslin 2023a
. It is also an other demonstration of the importance of multiphoton components that are predicted by the coupling to guarantee a causal emission
Compagno et al. 1990
.

V
Conclusion and outlook

Under the assumption that the spontaneous emission of a Hydrogen atom produces a single-photon, we have determined the spatial nonlocality properties, and shown that at any given time the expectation value of the local energy density is not zero anywhere, and decreases asymptotically like a power
1/r^{6}
as a function of the distance
r
of the detector to the atom. We remark that this power law decrease is far from the maximal decrease, which is quasiexponential, as determined in
Białynicki-Birula 1998
.
It was shown in
Federico and Jauslin 2023a
that all single-photon states have a nonzero expectation value of the energy in any finite volume. Our result provides a concrete illustration of the intrinsic nonlocal property of single photons through a calculation that does not only consider the field but its source as well, and it shows that the nonlaclity exists even before the photon state is completely emitted. From these results, one can conclude that the process of spontaneous emission cannot produce a strict single-photon state since it would contradict causality

;
Dolce et al. 2006

;
Licht 1966
;
De Bièvre 2006
;
De Bièvre 2007

n

Appendix A
Position space representations and related operators

To analyze the spatial properties of photons, one needs to use a position space representation of the states. This can be done using several equivalent choices
Federico and Jauslin 2023b
. In this work, we have chosen to work with the Landau-Peierls (LP) representation which is constructed as follows

\vec{\psi}(\vec{x})=\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{2\hbar}}\left[\Omega^{1/2}\vec{A}(\vec{x})-i\Omega^{-1/2}\vec{E}(\vec{x})\right],

(33)

where
\vec{A}
is the classical potential vector in the Coulomb gauge and
\vec{E}
is the classical electric field. The operators
\Omega^{\pm 1/2}
are self-adjoint operators constructed from the frequency operator
\Omega=c(-\Delta)^{1/2}
for the Laplacian
\Delta
. These operators are well defined and Refs
Federico et al. 2022
;
Federico and Jauslin 2023b
;
Federico and Jauslin 2023a
give the more details of their construction. The LP representation is made in the Hilbert space

\mathcal{H}_{LP}=\left\{\vec{\psi}\ \Big|\nabla\cdot\vec{\psi}=0,\bra*{\vec\psi}\ket*{\vec\psi'}_{LP}<\infty\right\},

(34)

with the scalar product

\bra*{\vec\psi}\ket*{\vec\psi'}_{LP}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}d^{3}x\ \vec{\psi}^{\star}\cdot\vec{\psi}^{\prime},

(35)

and Maxwell’s equations written in this representation read

i\frac{\partial\vec{\psi}}{\partial t}=\Omega\vec{\psi},\quad\quad\quad\nabla\cdot\vec{\psi}=0.

(36)

This Hilbert space can then be used to construct the bosonic Fock space of states
as

\mathbb{F}^{\mathfrak{B}}(\mathcal{H}_{LP})=\bigoplus_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{H}_{LP}^{\otimes_{S}n},

(37)

where
\mathcal{H}_{LP}^{\otimes_{S}n}
is the symmetrized
n
-times tensor product of
\mathcal{H}_{LP}
with itself.
Annihilation and creation operators directly constructed on arbitrary pulse-shaped functions can then be used to write states of the fields from the vacuum
\ket{\varnothing}
:

\hat{B}_{\vec{\psi}}\ket{\varnothing}=0,\quad\quad\quad\hat{B}^{\dagger}_{\vec{\psi}}\ket{\varnothing}=\ket*{\vec\psi}.

(38)

They satisfy the general bosonic commutation relations
Garrison and Chiao 2008
;
Fabre and Treps 2020
;
Federico and Jauslin 2023b

\left[\hat{B}_{\vec{\psi}},\hat{B}^{\dagger}_{\vec{\psi}^{\prime}}\right]=\bra*{\vec\psi}\ket*{\vec\psi'}_{LP}.

(39)

We emphasize that the definition is done for any function of
\mathcal{H}_{LP}
. As usual, one can also use any generalized basis like the circularly polarized plane waves or the helicity vector spherical harmonics introduced in Appendix
B
, as we did in eq. (
4
). Defining a basis allows also to express the electromagnetic field operators. Indeed, if we consider a basis
\{\vec{\varphi}_{\kappa}\}
, the classical LP function
\vec{\psi}(\vec{x})
can be expanded as

\vec{\psi}(\vec{x})=\sum_{\kappa}\vec{\varphi}(\vec{x})z_{\kappa},

(40)

with
z_{\kappa}=\bra*{\vec\varphi_\kappa}\ket*{\vec\psi}_{LP}
. Its quantum equivalent is thus

\vec{\hat{\Psi}}(\vec{x})=\sum_{\kappa}\vec{\varphi}(\vec{x})\hat{B}_{\vec{\varphi}_{\kappa}},

(41)

from which the real electromagnetic field observables are expressed as the following Hermitian operators

\vec{\hat{A}}(\vec{x})=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2\varepsilon_{0}}}\Omega^{-1/2}\left(\vec{\hat{\Psi}}(\vec{x})+\vec{\hat{\Psi}}^{\dagger}(\vec{x})\right),\quad\quad\quad\vec{\hat{E}}(\vec{x})=i\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2\varepsilon_{0}}}\Omega^{1/2}\left(\vec{\hat{\Psi}}(\vec{x})-\vec{\hat{\Psi}}^{\dagger}(\vec{x})\right).

(42)

Another important tool that is used is the helicity operator, defined as

\Lambda=c\Omega^{-1}\nabla\times.

(43)

For transverse fields, its spectrum is
\{\pm 1\}
and one can thus
decompose any transverse field
\vec{v}
into a sum of a positive- and
a negative-helicity part
\vec{v}^{(h\pm)}
,

\vec{v}=\vec{v}^{(h+)}+\vec{v}^{(h-)},

(44)

where
\Lambda\vec{v}^{(h\pm)}=\pm\vec{v}^{(h\pm)}
. This splitting is an important property for the energy density mean value (
21
) since it leads to the nonlocality of the energy density of all single-photon sates
Federico and Jauslin 2023a
.
