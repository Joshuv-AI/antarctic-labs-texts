Fast and accurate evaluation of Biot–Savart integrals over spatial curves in periodic domains
Juan Ignacio Polanco

Abstract
The Biot–Savart law is relevant in physical contexts including electromagnetism and fluid dynamics. In the latter case, when the rotation of a fluid is confined to a set of very thin vortex filaments, this law describes the velocity field induced by the spatial arrangement of these objects. The Biot–Savart law is at the core of vortex methods used in the simulation of classical and quantum fluid flows. Naïve methods are inefficient when dealing with large numbers of vortex elements, which makes them inadequate for simulating turbulent vortex flows. Here we exploit a direct analogy between the Biot–Savart law and electrostatics to adapt Ewald summation methods, routinely used in molecular dynamics simulations, to vortex filament simulations in three-dimensional periodic domains. In this context, the basic idea is to split the induced velocity onto (i) a coarse-grained velocity generated by a Gaussian-filtered vorticity field, and (ii) a short-range correction accounting for near-singular behaviour near the vortices. The former component can be accurately and efficiently evaluated using the nonuniform fast Fourier transform algorithm. Analytical accuracy estimates are provided as a function of the parameters entering the method. We also discuss how to properly account for the finite vortex core size in kinetic energy estimations. Using numerical experiments, we verify the accuracy and the conservation properties of the proposed approach. Moreover, we demonstrate the O ⁡ ( N ​ log ⁡ N ) O(N\log N) complexity of the method over a wide range of problem sizes N N , considerably better than the O ⁡ ( N 2 ) O(N^{2}) cost of a naïve approach.

Introduction

The Biot–Savart law is well known for describing the magnetic field generated by
a steady electric current.
It is also very relevant in fluid dynamics, where it allows to obtain the fluid
velocity induced by a known vorticity field.
We are interested here in the specific case where the electric current or the
vorticity field are confined to spatial curves in three-dimensional space.
This is a commonly encountered problem in undergraduate electromagnetism
lectures, where the question is what is the magnetic field induced by a current
flowing through a thin conducting wire.
In the fluid dynamics case, the equivalent would be a very thin vortex
filament, which can be a reasonable idealised model for vortices found in
the small-viscosity limit.
In fact, it is also a very accurate description of vortices in certain superfluids such
as liquid helium-4 near absolute zero, where rotational motion is confined
to so-called quantum vortices of atomic-size thickness (the vortex core radius
is
a\approx${10}^{-10}\text{\,}\mathrm{m}$
), and the circulation (or strength) of each
vortex takes a constant value
\kappa\approx$9.97\text{\times}{10}^{-8}\text{\,}\mathrm{m}^{2}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{s}$
dictated by
quantum mechanical constraints.
The velocity induced at a point
\boldsymbol{x}
away from a vortex core is then given
by the Biot–Savart law,

\boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{\kappa}{4\pi}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}\frac{(\boldsymbol{s}-\boldsymbol{x})\times\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{s}}{|\boldsymbol{s}-\boldsymbol{x}|^{3}},

(1)

where
\mathcal{C}
denotes one or more oriented curves representing the vortex
filament geometry, and
\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathcal{C}
denotes a vortex location.

In classical fluid dynamics, the Biot–Savart law is at the core of vortex
methods
[
Cottet2000
,
Koumoutsakos2005
]
used to describe incompressible
viscous flows at relatively high Reynolds numbers.
These are commonly used in aerodynamics applications
[
Vermeer2003
]
, but
have also been applied to problems as varied as the simulation of
self-propelled swimmers in viscous fluids
[
Gazzola2014
]
.
Note that, in viscous fluids, vortex methods need to account for effects
including vorticity diffusion and energy dissipation due to viscosity.
To achieve this, these methods typically deal with vortex
particles
,
which should not be interpreted as physical objects but as an ensemble of
point charges generating a fluid flow.
In particular, the connectivity of vortex particles is not relevant to vortex
particle methods
[
Koumoutsakos2005
]
.

Here we focus on the conceptually simpler application of vortex methods to
superfluid helium-4, where viscous effects are absent near absolute zero
and vortex filaments are in fact the main physical object of interest.
In this context, the Biot–Savart law is the basic ingredient of the vortex filament model
(VFM), which is one of the most common approaches for describing superfluid
flows
[
Schwarz1985
,
Hanninen2014
,
Barenghi2023
]
.
This model is valid at scales much larger than the atomic vortex thickness, and
is therefore well adapted for describing macroscopic vortex motion.
Numerically, the standard approach for representing vortex filaments consists
in discretising them as a series of connected vortex points.
The connectivity is required in order to obtain derived geometrical information
such as local tangents and curvatures.
Each vortex point evolves in time according to the Biot–Savart law.
In fact, the integral
eq.

1
diverges when evaluated on a vortex
point
\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{s}_{0}
.
The singularity can be avoided by taking into
account the finite (but small) vortex core radius

a

[
Schwarz1985
]
,
which introduces a logarithmic dependence of the velocity on
a
(see
section

2
).
It is worth mentioning that this model shares many similarities with slender
body theory
[
Batchelor1970
,
Lauga2009
,
Mori2020
]
, which describes
the dynamics of thin fibers in Stokes flows.
Similarly to the VFM, the forces induced by a 3D fiber on the fluid are
approximated by a distribution of singularities along the fiber centreline.
Furthermore, the velocity induced by a fiber on itself also includes a logarithmic
dependence on its small thickness
a
.

This work is motivated by the study of quantum turbulence
[
Barenghi2023
]
,
which is a state of superfluid flows characterised by a wide range of
energetically active length scales.
Numerically, to investigate such a turbulent state, one needs to compute the
non-local interactions between large numbers of vortices, requiring in
particular a large number
N
of discrete vortex points.
If one explicitly accounts for all pair interactions,
obtaining the velocities at the
N
points has a
O(N^{2})
cost, which quickly
limits the size of the systems which can be numerically studied.
In the context of particle simulations, this problem has been solved for a long
time, using techniques such as Barnes–Hut (BH) trees
[
Barnes1986
]
or the fast
multipole method (FMM)
[
Greengard1987
]
, which reduce the complexity to
O(N\log N)

and
O(N)
respectively.
Such techniques have also been applied to vortex methods for classical
[
Cottet2000
]
and quantum
[
Baggaley2012f
]

fluids.

Here we are interested in periodic infinite systems, in which
a finite set of
N_{\text{f}}
vortices is replicated an infinite number of times in a
spatially periodic fashion.
Periodic boundary conditions are commonly used to model a variety of physical
systems when one wants to describe phenomena far from boundaries.
A natural way of dealing with periodicity in Cartesian domains is via a
Fourier series representation, as done for example in Fourier pseudo-spectral
methods
[
Canuto1988
,
Boyd2001
]
.
These take advantage of the fast Fourier transform (FFT) to
efficiently evaluate non-local operations.
This suggests the idea of using FFTs to evaluate costly far-field
interactions between particles or vortices in periodic systems.
However, since particles and vortices are respectively represented as 0D and 1D singularities,
one cannot directly describe the associated source fields (e.g. electric
charge density or vorticity) using a truncated Fourier series, which in practice precludes
the use of FFTs.
In particle simulations, one way around this issue is provided by
fast Ewald
summation
methods, which are commonly used in molecular dynamics simulations to speed-up
the evaluation of electrostatic interactions between charged particles in
periodic systems
[
Ewald1921
,
Hockney1988
,
Darden1993
,
Deserno1998
,
Arnold2005
]
.
There, the basic strategy is to additively split the singular source field onto a smooth
field responsible for far-field interactions and a correction field accounting
for interactions between nearby particles.
Fast Ewald summation methods are characterised by a
O(N\log N)
complexity in
the number of particles
N
.
In fluid dynamics contexts, fast Ewald methods have been used to describe 2D
point vortex dynamics
[
Monaghan1993
]
, fluid motions induced by
localised forces in the Stokes regime
[
Saintillan2005
,
Lindbo2010
,
Maxian2021
]
, and Darcy
flows at the interface between two immiscible fluids
[
Ambrose2013
]
.
In molecular dynamics simulation benchmarks
[
Arnold2013
]
, it has been
observed that FFT-based Ewald methods can perform slightly better than the
periodic FMM at the same accuracy, actually displaying near linear complexity over a
wide range of problem sizes
N
.
Moreover, when running at low accuracy levels, the
latter can display a slight energy drift over time which is not observed in the
former
[
Arnold2013
]
.

The aim of this paper is to adapt Ewald methods to the evaluation of the
Biot–Savart integral
eq.

1
in three-dimensional periodic systems
and evaluate the relevance of this approach.
The paper begins in
section

2
with an introduction to the VFM in the context of
quantum vortex dynamics.
We also propose an approach for accurately estimating the kinetic energy in
periodic systems.

Section

3
describes the Ewald-based method used to evaluate
Biot–Savart integrals.
Our approach takes advantage of the nonuniform fast Fourier transform (NUFFT)
algorithm to speed-up computations.
In
section

4
, we provide analytical estimates of the
approximation errors incurred by the proposed approach in terms of tunable
parameters.
The relevance of these estimates is then verified in

section

5
using numerical experiments of different test
cases.
That section also showcases the accuracy of energy and impulse conservation by
the method, and finishes with numerical evidence of near linear
O(N\log N)
complexity
over a wide range of problem sizes
N
.
Finally,
section

6
is devoted to conclusions.

2
The vortex filament model

The VFM introduced by Schwarz
[
Schwarz1985
]
is one
of the main approaches used to describe theoretically and numerically the
three-dimensional hydrodynamics of quantum fluids such as low-temperature
liquid helium
[
Hanninen2014
,
Barenghi2023
]
.
Unlike classical fluids, quantum fluids near absolute zero are
characterised by having zero viscosity and being irrotational almost
everywhere.
In fact, rotational motions are confined to very thin vortex filaments carrying
a quantised circulation
\kappa=h/m
, where
h
is Planck’s constant and

m
the mass of one atom in the case of helium-4.
In other words, a straight vortex filament induces a circular motion of the fluid around it with
velocity
v=\kappa/(2\pi r)
, where
r
is the distance to the vortex.

We restrict our attention to the zero temperature limit.
Indeed, in finite temperature superfluid helium, quantum vortices can be seen as
coexisting and interacting with a viscous normal fluid
[
Barenghi2014a
]
.
This is described by more complex models (some of them based on the VFM) which are
still the subject of active research
[
Barenghi2023
]
.
A second very important aspect which is not discussed here is the
reconnection of vortices when they are close to collision.
While this phenomenon is crucial for describing energy dissipation and quantum turbulence,
its modelling is orthogonal to the subject of this work, and can be disregarded
as long as vortex elements stay sufficiently far from each other.

2.1
Filaments as oriented curves

In the VFM, one is interested in describing the motion induced by a collection
of vortex filaments on themselves.
Each filament is represented as an oriented curve
\left\{\boldsymbol{s}(\xi),0\leq\xi\leq\mathcal{L}\right\}
where
\boldsymbol{s}(\xi)
is a
location on the curve,
\xi
is the arc length, and
\mathcal{L}
is the
total length of the curve.
Here the curve is represented using the natural arc length parametrisation, such that the
unit tangent to the curve is
\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}(\xi)=\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{s}/\mathrm{d}\xi
with

|\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}(\xi)|=1
for all
\xi
.
In practice, it is often more convenient to deal with arbitrary parametrisations
which will be denoted
\boldsymbol{s}(\tau)
for
0\leq\tau\leq\mathcal{T}
, such that

|\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{s}/\mathrm{d}\tau|\neq 1
in general.

We will consider the vortex filaments to be embedded in a
triply periodic domain, so that every filament is replicated an infinite number of
times along each Cartesian direction.
For simplicity, throughout this paper, the domain period is set to
L
in all
directions, but all definitions and results can be readily generalised to
different periodicities in each direction.
In fluid dynamics, Helmholtz’ theorems
[
Helmholtz1858
]
state that vortex lines cannot end in
the fluid.
Therefore, in the absence of solid boundaries,
they must either be closed curves or extend to infinity.
Hence we only consider these two cases, with the additional restriction
that infinite curves must be described by a periodic function matching the domain periodicity.
Both cases are defined by the property

\boldsymbol{s}(\mathcal{T}+\tau)=\boldsymbol{s}(\tau)+\boldsymbol{n}L
with
\boldsymbol{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}
.
In particular, closed curves satisfy this property with
\boldsymbol{n}=\boldsymbol{0}
.
An example of an infinite periodic curve is
\boldsymbol{s}(\tau)=[\cos(2\pi\tau/L),0,\tau]

with
\mathcal{T}=L
.
This curve satisfies the above property with
\boldsymbol{n}=[0,0,1]
, extending
infinitely along the third Cartesian direction.

2.2
The Biot–Savart law

We now consider a set of
N_{\text{f}}
vortex filaments and their periodic images,
and denote
\mathcal{C}=\cup_{j=1}^{N_{\text{f}}}\mathcal{C}_{j}
the set of spatial curves

\mathcal{C}_{j}
representing their locations.
The vorticity field
\boldsymbol{\omega}=\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\boldsymbol{v}
associated to these
filaments is then

\boldsymbol{\omega}(\boldsymbol{x})=\kappa\sum_{\boldsymbol{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}\delta(\boldsymbol{s}-\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{n}L)\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{s}

(2)

where
\delta(\boldsymbol{x})
is the Dirac delta function, and the infinite sum over

\boldsymbol{n}
accounts for the periodic vortex images.
Here
\kappa
is the circulation of the vortex filaments, related to the
magnitude of the velocity induced by them.
Throughout this work
\kappa
will be taken to be constant (as is actually the
case of quantum vortices), but in principle one could also consider a variable
circulation
\kappa(\boldsymbol{s})
.

Inverting the curl operator leads to the Biot–Savart law
eq.

1

describing the velocity induced by the set of vortex filaments on a point

\boldsymbol{x}
.
Including periodicity and finite core size effects, this law writes

\boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{\kappa}{4\pi}\sum_{\boldsymbol{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}\frac{(\boldsymbol{s}-\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{n}L)\times\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{s}}{|\boldsymbol{s}-\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{n}L|^{3}}\,\varphi_{v}(\boldsymbol{s}-\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{n}L).

(3)

Here
0\leq\varphi_{v}(\boldsymbol{r})\leq 1
accounts for the finite radius

a
of the vortex core.
As detailed in
section

2.3
, it allows to
desingularise the Biot–Savart integral when evaluating the velocity on a vortex.
For all practical purposes, it is constant and equal to
1
whenever
\boldsymbol{x}
is not located on a vortex.

Note that periodicity requires the total vorticity to be zero within a periodic
cell, or otherwise the curl operator cannot be inverted (the velocity diverges).
This condition corresponds to
\oint_{\mathcal{C}}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{s}=\boldsymbol{0}
.
This is trivially satisfied for closed filaments, while it requires special care
when dealing with infinite unclosed filaments.

2.3
Desingularisation of the Biot–Savart integral

In the VFM, one is generally interested in the velocity induced by the vortex
filaments
on the filaments themselves
.
In other words, one wants to evaluate
eq.

3
on positions

\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{s}_{0}\in\mathcal{C}
, where the Biot–Savart integral clearly
diverges
[
Callegari1978
]
.
More precisely, performing a Taylor expansion of
\boldsymbol{s}(\xi)
,
one can show that close to
\boldsymbol{s}_{0}=\boldsymbol{s}(\xi_{0})
the integrand behaves
as
[
Arms1965
]

\frac{[\boldsymbol{s}(\xi)-\boldsymbol{s}_{0}]\times\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}(\xi)}{|\boldsymbol{s}(\xi)-\boldsymbol{s}_{0}|^{3}}\,\mathrm{d}\xi=\frac{\boldsymbol{s}_{0}^{\prime}\times\boldsymbol{s}_{0}^{\prime\prime}}{2|\xi-\xi_{0}|}\,\mathrm{d}\xi+O(1),

(4)

where primes denote derivatives with respect to the arc length
\xi
.
In particular,
\boldsymbol{s}_{0}^{\prime}\equiv\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}(\xi_{0})
and
\boldsymbol{s}_{0}^{\prime\prime}\equiv\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime\prime}(\xi_{0})
are
respectively the unit tangent and the curvature vectors at
\boldsymbol{s}_{0}
.

The divergence of the Biot–Savart integral is unphysical since

eq.

2
does not account for the finite (but very small) radius

a
of the vortex core.
This issue can be circumvented in a physically consistent manner by defining
the
\varphi_{v}
term appearing in
eq.

3
such that

\varphi_{v}(\boldsymbol{r})=0
for
|\boldsymbol{r}|<\epsilon_{v}a
, and

1
otherwise,
where
\epsilon_{v}
is a cut-off coefficient.
In other words,
\varphi_{v}
is defined such that locations
\boldsymbol{s}
close
to the singularity at
\boldsymbol{s}_{0}
are omitted from the
integral
1
1

1

Interestingly, in the context of Stokes flows, slender body theory has been
recently reformulated using a similar cut-off procedure
[
Maxian2021
]
.

[
Saffman1993
, Ch. 11]
.
It is convenient to write
\epsilon_{v}=e^{\Delta}/2
,
where
\Delta
is a constant which depends on the actual vorticity profile
within the vortex core.
This constant can be analytically derived for commonly used vorticity profiles,
under the assumption that the local curvature radius
1/|\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime\prime}|
stays much
larger than the core size
a
.
In particular,
\Delta=1/4
for a circular vortex core with uniform vorticity
[
Saffman1993
, Ch. 10]
.

Discretisation of the Biot–Savart integral

\boldsymbol{s}_{0}
, the lengths
\ell^{-}
and
\ell^{+}
of

\boldsymbol{s}_{0}^{\prime}
and curvature vector
\boldsymbol{s}_{0}^{\prime\prime}
.
Numerically, non-local contributions are computed by approximating line
integrals by quadrature sums over each non-local segment.
As an example, the open red circles between points
\boldsymbol{s}_{j}
and

\boldsymbol{s}_{j+1}
are the quadrature points associated to that segment (here,
using
Q=3
point Gauss–Legendre quadratures on each segment).
These are the points where the integrand is actually evaluated when
computing non-local contributions.

In practice, the VFM is used to describe length scales which are several
orders of magnitude larger than
a
.
In numerical simulations, vortex filaments are discretised with a typical line
resolution
\ell\gg a
.
Therefore, directly computing the Biot–Savart integral with a cut-off of order

a
as described above can lead to large numerical error,
especially when quadrature rules are used to estimate the integrals.
For this reason, the usual approach is to split the evaluation of the
Biot–Savart integral on a discretised filament onto

local
and
non-local
contributions
[
Schwarz1985
]
, as represented in

fig.

1
for a single filament.
The local contribution is estimated by analytically integrating the leading-order term of
the Taylor expansion
eq.

4
on the local portion
\mathcal{C}_{0}
of the
discretised curve and excluding the cut-off region of length

2\epsilon_{v}a
.
This leads to

\boldsymbol{v}_{\text{local}}(\boldsymbol{s}_{0})=\frac{\kappa}{4\pi}\boldsymbol{s}_{0}^{\prime}\times\boldsymbol{s}_{0}^{\prime\prime}\left[\ln\left(\frac{2\sqrt{\ell^{-}\ell^{+}}}{a}\right)-\Delta\right],

(5)

where
\ell^{-}
and
\ell^{+}
are the lengths of the adjacent segments composing
\mathcal{C}_{0}
.
The local velocity is thus oriented along the local binormal direction and is
proportional to the local curvature
|\boldsymbol{s}_{0}^{\prime\prime}|
.
As illustrated in
fig.

1
, when filaments are
discretised by connecting vortex points and
\boldsymbol{s}_{0}
is one of these points,
the adjacent segments are usually defined as those linking
\boldsymbol{s}_{0}
to its neighbours.
Finally, accounting for the local term, the total induced velocity of a point

\boldsymbol{s}_{0}
on a discretised vortex can be written as

\boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{s}_{0})=\boldsymbol{v}_{\text{local}}(\boldsymbol{s}_{0})+\frac{\kappa}{4\pi}\sum_{\boldsymbol{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}}\int_{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}\frac{(\boldsymbol{s}-\boldsymbol{s}_{0}+\boldsymbol{n}L)\times\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{s}}{|\boldsymbol{s}-\boldsymbol{s}_{0}+\boldsymbol{n}L|^{3}},

(6)

where the prime over the integral indicates that integration is omitted on the
local portion
\mathcal{C}_{0}
of the filament containing
\boldsymbol{s}_{0}
.

2.5
Energy estimation in periodic systems

The VFM described above includes no dissipation mechanisms,
and energy is expected to be a conserved quantity.
It is crucial to be able to accurately estimate the total energy
of the system, as this may allow (i) to verify the accuracy of energy conservation at the
numerical level or (ii) to estimate energy decay rates when the model is extended with dissipative
mechanisms.

In a periodic domain, the kinetic energy per unit mass is defined as

E=\frac{1}{2V}\int_{\Omega}\left|\boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{x})\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}^{3}\boldsymbol{x}

(7)

where
\Omega={[0,L]}^{3}
is the main periodic cell and
V=L^{3}
is its volume.
In principle, this expression requires knowing the velocity field

\boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{x})
at every point
\boldsymbol{x}
in space.
In the VFM this is not only impractical – as it would require many evaluations
of the Biot–Savart law
eq.

3
– but is also delicate since the velocity
field presents strong gradients near vortex filaments.
For these reasons, in non-periodic VFM simulations
[
Samuels2001
,
Baggaley2011
,
Hanninen2013
]
the above expression is commonly replaced by
2
2

2

Here we express the energy is per unit
density
and not
mass
, since the
domain volume
V
(and thus the fluid mass) is infinite.

E_{\text{non-periodic}}=\kappa\oint\boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{s})\cdot(\boldsymbol{s}\times\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{s}),

(8)

which only requires knowing the vortex geometry and the velocity of the vortex filaments.
It is based on a similar expression initially derived as a volume
integral
[
Lamb1945
, p. 218]
(see also
[
Saffman1993
, p. 68]
), which is
expected be valid for spatially smooth vorticity fields.
It assumes that the velocity and vorticity fields decay to zero at infinity,
which is not the case in periodic domains.
More importantly, as we shall see below, it does not correctly account for the
vortex core size
a
and thus it is not properly conserved.

An alternative expression for the kinetic energy per unit mass can be obtained from

eq.

7
using integration by parts
[
Saffman1993
, p. 68]
,

E=\frac{1}{2V}\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\psi}(\boldsymbol{x})\cdot\boldsymbol{\omega}(\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathrm{d}^{3}\boldsymbol{x}=\frac{\kappa}{2V}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}\boldsymbol{\psi}(\boldsymbol{s})\cdot\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{s},

(9)

where the last equality is obtained using
eq.

2
.
Note that the boundary terms of the integration by parts vanish in periodic
domains.
In
eq.

9
, we have introduced the
streamfunction vector
\boldsymbol{\psi}(\boldsymbol{x})
(or
vector potential

[
Feynman2011
,
Saffman1993
]
), which is
related to the velocity by
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\boldsymbol{\psi}=\boldsymbol{v}
, and thus to the vorticity
by Poisson’s equation
-\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{\psi}=\boldsymbol{\omega}
.
In other words, the kinetic energy can be obtained from a line integral
requiring knowledge of streamfunction values on vortex filaments.
Interestingly,
eq.

9
also allows to interpret
the tangential streamfunction,
\psi_{t}=\boldsymbol{\psi}\cdot\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}
, as the
linear energy density of a vortex point (up to a multiplicative constant

\kappa/2V
), and thus as the contribution of a vortex element to the total
kinetic energy.
Finally, note that
eq.

9
is also valid in
non-periodic unbounded domains under the same assumptions leading to

eq.

8
.
To our knowledge, this expression has never been used before in the context of
the VFM.
Perhaps one of the reasons is that
\boldsymbol{\psi}(\boldsymbol{s})
is a priori not
available in VFM simulations, and computing it comes at an additional cost.

2.6
Obtaining the streamfunction vector

As mentioned above, the streamfunction is the solution of the Poisson equation

-\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{\psi}=\boldsymbol{\omega}
.
In three dimensions, the solution can be explicitly written as a
convolution of
\boldsymbol{\omega}
with the Green’s function

G(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=G(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y})=1/(4\pi|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}|)
,

\boldsymbol{\psi}(\boldsymbol{x})=(G\ast\boldsymbol{\omega})(\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{\kappa}{4\pi}\sum_{\boldsymbol{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{s}}{|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{s}+\boldsymbol{n}L|}\,\varphi_{\psi}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{s}+\boldsymbol{n}L),

(10)

where once again we have used
eq.

2
to express the vorticity
field.
In analogy with
eq.

3
, here
\varphi_{\psi}
accounts for the finite radius
a
of the vortex core.
Noting that
\boldsymbol{\nabla}G(\boldsymbol{r})=-\boldsymbol{r}/(4\pi r^{3})
, where
r=|\boldsymbol{r}|
, one can readily show
that taking the curl of
eq.

10
leads to the
Biot–Savart law
eq.

3
when
\boldsymbol{x}\notin\mathcal{C}
(in which case
\varphi_{\psi}=\varphi_{v}=1
everywhere).

Similarly to the velocity (
section

2.3
),
one must properly define the regularisation term
\varphi_{\psi}
in

eq.

10
to avoid the integral from diverging when
evaluating it on a filament location
\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{s}_{0}\in\mathcal{C}
.
As for the velocity, we define it as

\varphi_{\psi}(\boldsymbol{r})=0
for
|\boldsymbol{r}|<\epsilon_{\psi}a
, and

1
otherwise.
Crucially, the cut-off coefficient
\epsilon_{\psi}
need not be
equal to the coefficient
\epsilon_{v}=e^{\Delta}/2
used for the
velocity.
In fact, setting it to
\epsilon_{\psi}=e^{\Delta-1}/2
allows
to identify the resulting energy
eq.

9
with a
Hamiltonian for the vortex system as argued at the end of this section.
As verified numerically in
section

5.2
, this Hamiltonian
is accurately conserved in non-dissipative VFM simulations.

Using the same notation as in
eq.

4
, the integrand
of
eq.

10
behaves close to
\boldsymbol{s}_{0}=\boldsymbol{s}(\xi_{0})
as

\frac{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}(\xi)}{|\boldsymbol{s}(\xi)-\boldsymbol{s}_{0}|}\,\mathrm{d}\xi=\frac{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}_{0}}{|\xi-\xi_{0}|}\,\mathrm{d}\xi+O(1).

(11)

Then, in analogy with the local velocity
eq.

5
,
integrating over

\xi\in[\xi_{0}-\ell^{-},\xi_{0}-\epsilon_{\psi}a]\cup[\xi_{0}+\epsilon_{\psi}a,\xi_{0}+\ell^{+}]

(
\mathcal{C}_{0}
segments in
fig.

1
) leads to the local contribution

\boldsymbol{\psi}_{\text{local}}(\boldsymbol{s}_{0})=\frac{\kappa\boldsymbol{s}_{0}^{\prime}}{2\pi}\left[\ln\left(\frac{2\sqrt{\ell^{-}\ell^{+}}}{a}\right)-(\Delta-1)\right].

(12)

Interestingly, this term is tangent to the filament, and therefore it
fully contributes to the kinetic energy
eq.

9
.
In the context of vortex dynamics, this local contribution is commonly referred to as

vortex tension

[
Moore1972
,
Barenghi2023
]
.
Finally, in analogy with
eq.

6
, the streamfunction vector on a
discretised filament can be written as

\boldsymbol{\psi}(\boldsymbol{s}_{0})=\boldsymbol{\psi}_{\text{local}}(\boldsymbol{s}_{0})+\frac{\kappa}{4\pi}\sum_{\boldsymbol{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}}\int_{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{s}}{|\boldsymbol{s}-\boldsymbol{s}_{0}+\boldsymbol{n}L|},

(13)

where once again the prime over the integral denotes the omission of local
vortex elements around
\boldsymbol{s}_{0}
(
\mathcal{C}_{0}
segments in
fig.

1
).

To justify the above choice of
\epsilon_{\psi}
, we consider a
circular vortex ring of radius
R\gg a
.
From
eq.

3
(see also
[
Schwarz1985
]
), its self-induced
translational velocity is

V=\frac{\kappa}{4\pi R}\left[\ln\left(\frac{8R}{a}\right)-\Delta\right].

(14)

The total energy per unit mass
E
, satisfying Hamilton’s equation
V=\partial E/\partial p
where
p=(\kappa/V)\pi R^{2}
is the vortex ring impulse, is
then given by
[
Roberts1970
,
Sullivan2008
]

E=\frac{\kappa^{2}R}{2V}\left[\ln\left(\frac{8R}{a}\right)-(\Delta+1)\right].

(15)

One can analytically show that
\epsilon_{\psi}=e^{\Delta-1}/2
is
the only possible choice allowing to obtain

eq.

15
from expression
eq.

9
.
Later in
section

5.2
, we verify that
this choice leads to proper energy conservation up to numerical accuracy.
This appears to be the case not only for circular vortex rings, but also for
complex configurations containing multiple closed vortices of arbitrary shape,
and even unclosed vortices extending to infinity (as in
fig.

2
, centre),
in which case energy computations have been deemed impractical in the
past
[
Baggaley2011
]
.
Finally, note that the alternative energy expression
eq.

8

commonly used in open non-periodic systems is not consistent with Hamilton’s
equation for a circular ring, as it corresponds to replacing the
\Delta+1

term in the ring energy
eq.

15
with
\Delta
.
This suggests that
eq.

8
does not properly account for the
structure of the vortex core.

2.7
Discretisation of spatial curves and line integrals

We finish this section by discussing the approach we adopt to represent vortex
filaments and estimate line integrals in numerical simulations.
In the context of vortex filament simulations for describing quantum fluids,
filaments have been almost invariably described by a
set of points in space connected by straight segments
[
Schwarz1985
,
Samuels1992
,
Baggaley2012f
,
Hanninen2013
,
Yui2021
]
.
Integration of the Biot–Savart law over lines is then straightforward and can
even be done analytically over each straight segment.
Curve derivatives (local tangents, curvatures) are estimated on the
discrete points using e.g. finite difference approximations
[
Baggaley2012f
]
.
While this approach can be implemented with relative ease, it is a low-order
discretisation, often requiring very small distances between discretisation points
to achieve accurate results.
It also presents an inconsistency between the
C^{0}
continuity of the
lines and the
C^{2}
continuity required to estimate curvatures.

Here we take a different route and consider the filaments as smooth curves
passing through a set of points, as illustrated in
fig.

1
.
As introduced in
section

2.1
,
each curve is parametrised as
\boldsymbol{s}(\tau)
for some scalar parameter

\tau\in[0,\mathcal{T}]
.
The numerical degrees of freedom are the positions of discrete vortex points or

nodes
.
Some interpolation method is then applied to evaluate vortex positions
in-between nodes.
The interpolation also gives direct access to curve derivatives along vortex lines,
allowing to estimate local tangent and curvature vectors required for
Biot–Savart computations.
In the numerical experiments of
section

5
we use quintic
spline interpolations which have global continuity
C^{4}
(see
appendix

B
for details).

In the context of the Biot–Savart problem, a second difficulty is the need to
estimate integrals on vortex lines.
Here we estimate line integrals using Gauss–Legendre
quadratures on each segment connecting two neighbouring nodes.
Concretely, if one considers a closed curve
\mathcal{C}
parametrised as

\boldsymbol{s}(\tau)
with nodes

\left\{\boldsymbol{s}_{j}=\boldsymbol{s}(\tau_{j});j=1,\ldots,N\right\}
,
then an integral over such curve is approximated as

\int_{0}^{\mathcal{T}}F[\boldsymbol{s}(\tau)]\,\mathrm{d}\tau\approx\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{Q}w_{i}F\big[{\boldsymbol{s}_{j}^{(i)}}\big]\Delta\tau_{j},\quad\boldsymbol{s}_{j}^{(i)}\equiv\boldsymbol{s}(\tau_{j}+h_{i}\Delta\tau_{j}),

(16)

where
Q
is the number of quadrature points per segment (typically one chooses

Q\leq 4
in simulations).
Here,
F[\boldsymbol{s}]
is a functional which may depend on curve locations

\boldsymbol{s}(\tau)
as well as its derivatives.
For convenience, the quadrature rule above is defined in the domain
[0,1]
,
such that
h_{i}\in[0,1]
are the
quadrature locations, and
w_{i}
the associated
weights satisfying
\sum_{i=1}^{Q}w_{i}=1
.
Moreover,
\Delta\tau_{j}=\tau_{j+1}-\tau_{j}
is the parameter
increment associated to a single segment, with the convention that

\tau_{N+1}=\tau_{1}+\mathcal{T}
for a closed or an infinite periodic curve.
As an example, the open red circles in
fig.

1
are
the quadrature points associated to a single filament segment (using
Q=3
).

3
Fast Ewald summation for the Biot–Savart problem

In this section we adapt fast Ewald summation methods to the evaluation of
velocity and streamfunction fields induced by a set of vortex filaments.
This requires some adjustments since these methods generally deal with a scalar-valued
source term (e.g. electrostatic charge) supported on discrete points, while
here the source term (vorticity) is vector-valued and defined on spatial curves.
In the context of the VFM, the basic idea of these methods is to split the line integrals in

eqs.

6
and
13
onto
short- and long-range components.
Concretely, including the local terms appearing in those expressions, the streamfunction and
the velocity at a vortex location
\boldsymbol{s}_{0}
are decomposed as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\psi}(\boldsymbol{s}_{0})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\psi}_{\text{local}}(\boldsymbol{s}_{0})+\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\text{(n)}}(\boldsymbol{s}_{0})+\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\text{(f)}}(\boldsymbol{s}_{0})-\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\text{(f)}}_{\text{self}}(\boldsymbol{s}_{0}),

(17)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{s}_{0})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{v}_{\text{local}}(\boldsymbol{s}_{0})+\boldsymbol{v}^{\text{(n)}}(\boldsymbol{s}_{0})+\boldsymbol{v}^{\text{(f)}}(\boldsymbol{s}_{0})-\boldsymbol{v}^{\text{(f)}}_{\text{self}}(\boldsymbol{s}_{0}),

(18)

where the
(n)
and
(f)
superscripts denote near- and far-field components,
and
\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\text{(f)}}_{\text{self}}
and
\boldsymbol{v}^{\text{(f)}}_{\text{self}}
are corrections to
the latter which are discussed further below.

3.1
Ewald splitting

Ewald summation methods split the singular Green’s function

G(\boldsymbol{r})
associated to Poisson’s equation into (i) a fast decaying part

G^{\text{(n)}}(\boldsymbol{r})
accounting for short-range interactions and (ii) a slowly
decaying part
G^{\text{(f)}}(\boldsymbol{r})
, which is well-behaved at
\boldsymbol{r}=\boldsymbol{0}
and
describes long-range interactions.
In three dimensions, this is usually achieved via the identity
\erf(x)+\erfc(x)=1
,

G(\boldsymbol{r})=\frac{1}{4\pi r}=\frac{\erfc(\alpha r)}{4\pi r}+\frac{\erf(\alpha r)}{4\pi r}=G^{\text{(n)}}(\boldsymbol{r})+G^{\text{(f)}}(\boldsymbol{r}),

(19)

where
\erf(x)=(2/\sqrt{\pi})\int_{0}^{x}e^{-u^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}u

and
\erfc(x)=(2/\sqrt{\pi})\int_{x}^{\infty}e^{-u^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}u
are
respectively the error function and the complementary error function.
Due to linearity, the solution
\boldsymbol{\psi}=G\ast\boldsymbol{\omega}
to the Poisson equation
-\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{\psi}=\boldsymbol{\omega}
is accordingly split into

\boldsymbol{\psi}=\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\text{(n)}}+\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\text{(f)}}
.
Similarly, the velocity field can be decomposed as

\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\boldsymbol{\psi}=\boldsymbol{v}^{\text{(n)}}+\boldsymbol{v}^{\text{(f)}}
.
Importantly,
\alpha
in
eq.

19
is the Ewald splitting
parameter, which defines an inverse length scale setting the transition
between short- and long-range interactions.
This is a purely numerical parameter, as the physical fields
\boldsymbol{\psi}

and
\boldsymbol{v}
obtained by adding both contributions are in theory independent of
\alpha
.
To facilitate the interpretation of the splitting

eq.

19
, it is helpful to consider the
“far-field”

vorticity field defined by

\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\text{(f)}}=-\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\text{(f)}}
, which can be written as
the convolution of
\boldsymbol{\omega}
with
-\nabla^{2}G^{\text{(f)}}
.
In fact this is a Gaussian-filtering operation, since
-\nabla^{2}G^{\text{(f)}}(\boldsymbol{r})={(\alpha/\sqrt{\pi})}^{3}e^{-{(\alpha r)}^{2}}
.
In other words,
\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\text{(f)}}
is the streamfunction associated to a
coarse-grained (smoothed) version of the original (singular) vorticity
\boldsymbol{\omega}
.
Similarly,
\boldsymbol{v}^{\text{(f)}}=\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\text{(f)}}
is the corresponding
coarse-grained velocity field.

Taking the gradient of
eq.

19
,
one arrives to the corresponding splitting for
the Biot–Savart kernel
\boldsymbol{\nabla}G
,

\boldsymbol{\nabla}G(\boldsymbol{r})=-\frac{\boldsymbol{r}}{4\pi r^{3}}=-\left[g^{\text{(n)}}(\boldsymbol{r})+g^{\text{(f)}}(\boldsymbol{r})\right]\frac{\boldsymbol{r}}{4\pi r^{3}}=\boldsymbol{\nabla}G^{\text{(n)}}(\boldsymbol{r})+\boldsymbol{\nabla}G^{\text{(f)}}(\boldsymbol{r})

(20)

with the weight functions

g^{\text{(n)}}(\boldsymbol{r})=\erfc(\alpha r)+\frac{2\alpha r}{\sqrt{\pi}}\,e^{-{(\alpha r)}^{2}},\quad g^{\text{(f)}}(\boldsymbol{r})=\erf(\alpha r)-\frac{2\alpha r}{\sqrt{\pi}}\,e^{-{(\alpha r)}^{2}}.

(21)

It can be verified that the long-range kernels are non-singular, behaving as

G^{\text{(f)}}(\boldsymbol{r})=\alpha/({2\pi^{3/2}})+O(r^{2})
and

\boldsymbol{\nabla}G^{\text{(f)}}(\boldsymbol{r})=-\alpha^{3}\boldsymbol{r}/({3\pi^{3/2}})+O(r^{3})
near
r=0
.
On the other hand, the short-range kernels asymptotically decay as

G^{\text{(n)}}(\boldsymbol{r})\sim e^{-{(\alpha r)}^{2}}/\left[4\pi r(\alpha r)^{3/2}\right]

and

\boldsymbol{\nabla}G^{\text{(n)}}(\boldsymbol{r})\sim\alpha\boldsymbol{r}\,e^{-{(\alpha r)}^{2}}/\left(2\pi^{3/2}r^{2}\right)

for large
r
.

3.2
Estimation of short-range interactions

The near-field streamfunction
\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\text{(n)}}
is obtained by replacing
G(\boldsymbol{r})
in

eq.

10
with
G^{\text{(n)}}(\boldsymbol{r})
defined in

eq.

19
, resulting in

\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\text{(n)}}(\boldsymbol{s}_{0})=\frac{\kappa}{4\pi}\sum_{\boldsymbol{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}}\int_{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}f^{\text{(n)}}(\boldsymbol{s}-\boldsymbol{s}_{0}+\boldsymbol{n}L)\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{s}}{|\boldsymbol{s}-\boldsymbol{s}_{0}+\boldsymbol{n}L|},

(22)

where
f^{\text{(n)}}(\boldsymbol{r})=\erfc(\alpha r)
.
We recall that the prime over the integral symbol denotes the omission of local vortex
elements around
\boldsymbol{s}_{0}
(
\mathcal{C}_{0}
segments in
fig.

1
).
Similarly, following the decomposition

eq.

20
, the near-field velocity field is
explicitly given by the modified Biot–Savart integral

\boldsymbol{v}^{\text{(n)}}(\boldsymbol{s}_{0})=\frac{\kappa}{4\pi}\sum_{\boldsymbol{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}}\int_{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}g^{\text{(n)}}(\boldsymbol{s}-\boldsymbol{s}_{0}+\boldsymbol{n}L)\,\frac{(\boldsymbol{s}-\boldsymbol{s}_{0}+\boldsymbol{n}L)\times\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{s}}{|\boldsymbol{s}-\boldsymbol{s}_{0}+\boldsymbol{n}L|^{3}}.

(23)

The original integrals in
eqs.

6
and
13

are recovered by setting
\alpha=0
in the above expressions.
In practice, we approximate the above line integrals using quadrature sums over discrete
vortex line locations
\boldsymbol{s}_{j}^{(i)}
according to

eq.

16
.
Moreover,
since the short-range kernels
G^{\text{(n)}}
and
\boldsymbol{\nabla}G^{\text{(n)}}
decay exponentially with
(\alpha r)^{2}
,
one can safely define a cut-off distance
r_{\text{c}}
beyond which short-range
interactions can be neglected.
The truncation errors associated to the choice of
r_{\text{c}}
are discussed in
section

4
.

3.3
Estimation of long-range interactions

Since the long-range Green’s function
G^{\text{(f)}}
is non-singular,
one can use a truncated Fourier series
representation to indirectly solve

-\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\text{(f)}}=\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\text{(f)}}
.
We start by writing the periodic vorticity field as

\boldsymbol{\omega}(\boldsymbol{x})\approx\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{K}^{3}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\,e^{\mathrm{i}\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}}\quad\text{where}\quad\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}=\frac{1}{V}\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\omega}(\boldsymbol{x})\,e^{-\mathrm{i}\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}}\,\mathrm{d}^{3}\boldsymbol{x}.

(24)

Here
\mathbb{K}=\left\{\frac{2\pi m}{L};m=-\left\lfloor\frac{M}{2}\right\rfloor,\ldots,\left\lfloor\frac{M-1}{2}\right\rfloor\right\}

is the set of
M
resolved Fourier wavenumbers in each Cartesian direction and

\lfloor\cdot\rfloor
denotes the floor operation (for simplicity, we take

M
to be the same in all directions).
We recall that
\Omega={[0,L]}^{3}
represents the main periodic cell and
V=L^{3}
is its volume.
Since the vorticity
eq.

2
is singular and supported on
spatial curves, its Fourier coefficients are then

\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}=\frac{\kappa}{V}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}e^{-i\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{s}}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{s}\approx\frac{\kappa}{V}\sum_{l=1}^{N_{\text{f}}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{l}}\sum_{i=1}^{Q}w_{i}\,\Delta\tau_{jl}\,\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}_{ijl}\,e^{-\mathrm{i}\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{s}_{ijl}},

(25)

where the last expression is obtained from the quadrature approximation

eq.

16
.
The outermost sum is over the
N_{\text{f}}
vortex filaments of the system, each
being discretised by a possibly different number of nodes
N_{l}
.
Moreover,
\boldsymbol{s}_{ijl}
is a shorthand for
\boldsymbol{s}_{l}(\tau_{j}+h_{i}\Delta\tau_{j})
, where
\boldsymbol{s}_{l}(\tau)
is the parametrisation of the
l
-th filament,
while
\boldsymbol{s}_{ijl}^{\prime}
is the derivative with respect to
\tau
at that location
(aligned with the local tangent vector).
The triple sum in
eq.

25

can be interpreted as a sum of vector charges

\boldsymbol{q}_{ijl}=w_{i}\,\Delta\tau_{jl}\,\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}_{ijl}
on locations
\boldsymbol{s}_{ijl}
.
Note that using the same quadrature nodes for short- and long-range
computations means that interpolated values of
\boldsymbol{s}
and
\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}
can be
shared among both components, reducing the computational cost associated to interpolations.

Now, since
\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\text{(f)}}
is the convolution of
\boldsymbol{\omega}
with a Gaussian kernel, its
Fourier coefficients are

\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\text{(f)}}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\,e^{-k^{2}/4\alpha^{2}}
where
k=|\boldsymbol{k}|
.
Unlike
|\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}|
, the amplitudes

|\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\text{(f)}}|
can be expected to decay quickly for

k\gg\alpha
, which justifies the truncation of the Fourier series.
Moreover, solving Poisson’s equation amounts to division by
k^{2}
in Fourier space.
This ultimately allows us to express the far-field velocity in physical space as

\boldsymbol{v}^{\text{(f)}}(\boldsymbol{x})\approx\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{K}^{3}\\
|\boldsymbol{k}|\neq 0\end{subarray}}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\text{(f)}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\,e^{\mathrm{i}\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}}\quad\text{with}\quad\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\text{(f)}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}=\mathrm{i}\boldsymbol{k}\times\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{\text{(f)}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}=\mathrm{i}\boldsymbol{k}\times\frac{\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{\text{(f)}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}}{k^{2}}.

(26)

Note that this requires

\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{\boldsymbol{0}}=\boldsymbol{0}
, i.e. the mean vorticity within a periodic
cell must be zero.
The far-field streamfunction field
\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\text{(f)}}(\boldsymbol{x})
can be written
similarly to
eq.

26

from its Fourier coefficients
\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{\text{(f)}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{\text{(f)}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}/k^{2}
.

When evaluated on a vortex location
\boldsymbol{s}_{0}
, the obtained far-field velocity
and streamfunction vectors include contributions of the local segments
adjacent to
\boldsymbol{s}_{0}
.
However, integration over these segments must
be excluded according to
eqs.

6
and
13
.
This issue is analogous to the spurious self-interaction electrostatic potential
appearing in standard Ewald methods
[
Arnold2013
]
.
In the present case, the spurious local integrals are explicitly given by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\text{(f)}}_{\text{self}}(\boldsymbol{s}_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{\kappa}{4\pi}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{0}}f^{\text{(f)}}(\boldsymbol{s}-\boldsymbol{s}_{0})\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{s}}{|\boldsymbol{s}-\boldsymbol{s}_{0}|},

(27)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}^{\text{(f)}}_{\text{self}}(\boldsymbol{s}_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{\kappa}{4\pi}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{0}}g^{\text{(f)}}(\boldsymbol{s}-\boldsymbol{s}_{0})\frac{(\boldsymbol{s}-\boldsymbol{s}_{0})\times\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{s}}{|\boldsymbol{s}-\boldsymbol{s}_{0}|^{3}},

(28)

where
f^{\text{(f)}}(\boldsymbol{r})=\erf(\alpha r)
,
g^{\text{(f)}}(\boldsymbol{r})
is defined
in
eq.

21
,
and
\mathcal{C}_{0}
consists of the two segments adjacent to
\boldsymbol{s}_{0}
(see

fig.

1
).
They can be estimated using a variant of the quadrature procedure

eq.

16
, thus requiring
2Q
evaluations of the
integrand for each vortex location
\boldsymbol{s}_{0}
.
As indicated by

eqs.

17
and
18
, these two

“self-interaction”
terms must be subtracted from the long-range estimations
to avoid an unphysical dependence of the results on the Ewald parameter
\alpha
.

As in previous works
[
Hedman2006
,
Arnold2013
,
Pippig2013
]
, here we adopt
the NUFFT algorithm
[
Dutt1993
]
to
efficiently approximate the Fourier sums
eq.

25
and

eq.

26
.
These can be respectively obtained using type-1
(non-uniform to uniform) and type-2 (uniform to non-uniform) NUFFTs
[
Greengard2004
]
.
When relying on the NUFFT, the accuracy of long-range computations is then controlled by
the internal NUFFT parameters and the truncation wavenumber

k_{\text{max}}=(2\pi/L)\lfloor(M-1)/2\rfloor
.
These two sources of error are respectively discussed in
appendix

A

and
section

4
.

4
Truncation error estimates

We now provide estimates of the root-mean-square errors associated to the
short- and long-range cut-offs
r_{\text{c}}
and
k_{\text{max}}
.
Similar estimates have already been provided for the electrostatic
problem
[
Kolafa1992
,
Deserno1998a
]
, but these do not directly
apply to the VFM since (i) the quantities of interest are not the same, and
(ii) the singular sources in the present case are spatial curves and not
points.
We also show that the effect of the parameters
\alpha
,

r_{\text{c}}
and
k_{\text{max}}
on accuracy can be reduced to a unique non-dimensional coefficient

\beta
, thus greatly
simplifying the parameter selection procedure.

The derivation of the error estimates is detailed in

appendix

C
.
In summary, the estimates make the simplifying assumptions that (i) vortex
filaments are homogeneously distributed in the spatial domain, (ii) the
relative orientation of two vortex elements at a distance
r>r_{\text{c}}
is
completely decorrelated, and (iii)
k_{\text{max}}
is much larger than the typical
curvature
|\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime\prime}|
of the vortices.
Introducing the non-dimensional cut-off coefficients
\beta^{\text{(n)}}=\alpha r_{\text{c}}

and
\beta^{\text{(f)}}=k_{\text{max}}/2\alpha
, we show that both short- and long-range
errors decay exponentially with the square of these respective coefficients,
which justifies reducing them into a unique coefficient
\beta
.
This finally leads to the truncation error estimates

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{v}

\displaystyle\approx\kappa\left[\frac{\mathcal{L}}{\sqrt{\pi}\alpha V}+\frac{1}{\beta}{\left(\frac{\mathcal{L}}{8\pi V}\right)}^{\!\!1/2}\right]e^{-\beta^{2}},

(29)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\psi}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\kappa}{2\alpha}\left[\frac{\mathcal{L}}{\alpha V}+\frac{1}{\beta^{1/2}}{\left(\frac{\mathcal{L}}{8\pi V}\right)}^{\!\!1/2}\right]\frac{e^{-\beta^{2}}}{\beta^{3/2}},

(30)

where the short- and long-range contributions to the errors correspond to the
first and second terms within each square bracket.
The accuracy of the method is thus mainly driven by a unique
non-dimensional parameter
\beta
.
This sets both physical- and Fourier-space cut-offs
r_{\text{c}}=\beta/\alpha
and

k_{\text{max}}=2\beta\alpha
for a
given value of the inverse splitting distance
\alpha
, which is left as a free
parameter that can be adjusted to optimise performance (
section

5.3
).

5
Numerical experiments

We now perform different numerical experiments to evaluate the accuracy,
conservation properties and computational complexity of the proposed FFT-based
method.
All computations are performed in double precision arithmetic.
The methods are implemented in the open-source VortexPasta.jl
package
3
3

3

Available at

https://github.com/jipolanco/VortexPasta.jl
under the MPL-2.0 license.

[
VortexPasta
]
written in Julia
[
Bezanson2017
]
.
The online documentation includes installation instructions, usage examples and

This should allow a new user to quickly get started with running simulations
even without any prior knowledge of the Julia language.

In all numerical tests below, the domain is periodic with period
L=2\pi
in
all directions.
The vortex core size and the vorticity profile parameter – both appearing in the
locally induced velocity
eq.

5
– are respectively set to

a=10^{-8}
and
\Delta=1/4
, and the vortex circulation is set to
\kappa=1
.
Vortex lines are represented as smooth curves using quintic splines,
which allows to estimate derivatives
and evaluate quantities in-between discretisation points.
Moreover, line integrals are estimated using
Q=3
quadrature points per
vortex segment (see
section

2.7
).

5.1
Accuracy

To numerically assess the accuracy of the proposed method,
we consider three different test cases of varying complexity.
The test cases are detailed below and illustrated in

2
.

L^{3}=(2\pi)^{3}
.

.
Centre: infinitely extended straight lines.

Right: 40 ellipses with random locations, orientations,

Colours allow to distinguish between individual vortex lines.

1. Trefoil knot

The trefoil knot consists in a single knotted curve
(

The trefoil knot curve is parametrically defined by

\cos(\theta)-2\cos(2\theta)\\
-\sin(3\theta)\end{array}\right]\quad\text{for }\theta\in[0,2\pi],

(31)

where

We take

The curve is discretised by evaluating
eq.

31
on
N=512

equally spaced values of
\theta
.

2. Infinite lines

This test case consists of a set of straight infinitely extended lines inducing
a three-dimensional velocity field.
Each periodic cell is crossed by three pairs of lines aligned positively and
negatively with each Cartesian direction (
fig.

2
, centre).
The filament orientations are such that the velocity induced on each line is non-zero
and positively aligned with the line (
\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}>0
).
Since the lines have zero curvature, the velocity induced by a line on itself
is zero, and therefore the velocity of a vortex point is completely due to non-local
interactions.
Therefore, this test case emphasises the accuracy of long-range computations.
We discretise each filament with
N_{l}=128
independent nodes (for
l=1,\ldots,6
).

It consists of 40 ellipses which are

The size and aspect ratio of each ellipse is also random.
Concretely, the minor and major radii are random

[L/16,L/4]
.

5.2
Timestepping and conservation properties

[12]

The VFM as described in
section

2
is expected to conserve the total
energy
eq.

9
and the hydrodynamic impulse

\boldsymbol{p}=\frac{\kappa}{2V}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}\boldsymbol{s}\times\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{s}
.
To verify this, we perform a VFM simulation in which
the position of each vortex point
\boldsymbol{s}
evolves in time according to

\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{s}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=\boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{s}(t),t)
.
Here the right hand side is the
Biot–Savart velocity
eq.

3
evaluated at location
\boldsymbol{s}(t)
and at time
t
.
The test case we consider here is inspired by the classical problem of two
coaxial circular vortex rings which travel in the same direction.
Due to their mutual interaction, the rings are expected to continuously change
their sizes and therefore their translational velocities.
As a result, they pass through one another in a cyclic fashion in

“leapfrogging”
motion.
This phenomenon has received much attention in classical viscous
fluids
[
Shariff1992
]
and has also been investigated in VFM simulations
[
Wacks2014
]
.

[13]

Here we consider a slightly more complex variant of the classical problem:
instead of perfectly circular vortex rings we simulate elliptical ones, so
that not only their size changes in time but also their shape.
The two elliptical vortex rings evolve in a cubic periodic domain of size
L
.
The vortices are initially located on two parallel planes – both orthogonal to the
x

axis – at a distance
L/4
from each other.
Their minor and major radii are respectively set to
R_{a}=L/4
and

R_{b}=R_{a}\sqrt{2}
.
Their size comparable to
L
means that the effect of periodic images
cannot be fully neglected.
Besides, the ellipses are rotated relative to each other so that their major
axes are respectively aligned with the
y
and
z
axes.
This initial configuration is visible in
fig.

4
(a).

[14]

Aiming at a relative accuracy of
\sim 10^{-6}
, each vortex is discretised
with a relatively small number of points
N=32
.
Such a coarse discretisation can lead to errors in the local terms

eqs.

5
and
12
due to the use of Taylor expansions.
For this reason, we subdivide the local filament segments
(
\mathcal{C}_{0}
in
fig.

1
)
so that Taylor expansions are applied on a smaller central portion (about 10%
of the local segment length),
while 3-point Gauss–Legendre quadratures are used in the remaining
portions not in contact with the singularity.
Each discrete vortex point
\boldsymbol{s}_{i}
is evolved in time according to

\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{s}_{i}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{s}_{i})
.
For the temporal discretisation we adopt the standard fourth-order explicit
Runge–Kutta (RK4) scheme.
Spline coefficients are updated each time the points
\boldsymbol{s}_{i}
are advanced.
While the vortices can stretch and shrink over time, we do not perform any
remeshing of the filaments for the sake of simplicity (but this is indeed
needed in more complex problems).
To guarantee near 6-digit accuracy, we set
\beta=3.5

(
fig.

3
), while the
splitting parameter is
\alpha=7/L
so that the short-range cut-off distance
is
r_{\text{c}}=\beta/\alpha=L/2
.
4
4

4

The condition
r_{\text{c}}\leq L/2
ensures that, in short-range computations, a
pair of vortex points sees each other at most once.
This simplifies the implementation as one does not need to explicitly deal
with periodic images.
In molecular dynamics this is called the
minimum image
convention

[
Arnold2005
]
.

[15]

To preserve stability, the maximum allowed timestep
\Delta t
in VFM
simulations is roughly proportional to
\ell_{\text{min}}^{2}

[
Schwarz1985
,
Hanninen2014
]
where
\ell_{\text{min}}
is the smallest spacing between vortex points – in
this test case
\ell_{\text{min}}\approx 2\pi R_{a}/N
.
Physically, this condition can be understood by considering a
small-amplitude sinusoidal perturbation of wavelength
\lambda
on an otherwise
straight vortex.
The perturbation is physically expected to rotate about the vortex axis with a period

T_{\text{kw}}(\lambda)\approx\frac{2\lambda^{2}}{\kappa}{\left[\ln\left(\frac{\lambda}{\pi a}\right)+\frac{1}{2}-(\Delta+\gamma)\right]}^{-1}
,
where
\gamma\approx 0.57721
is the Euler–Mascheroni constant
[
Thomson1880
,
Baggaley2014
]
.
Such periodic motion is known as a Kelvin wave.
Numerically, the timestep
\Delta t
must therefore be small enough to capture
the Kelvin wave oscillations associated to the smallest resolved scale

\lambda\sim\ell_{\text{min}}
.
In practice, we find that setting
\Delta t=C\,T_{\text{kw}}(\ell_{\text{min}})
with
C=1
is
enough for the RK4 scheme to remain stable.
We finish by mentioning that the above restriction is actually imposed by the local
self-interaction term
eq.

5

[
Buttke1988
,
DeLaHoz2009
]
.
This suggests the use of splitting methods
[
McLachlan2002
,
Blanes2024
]
–
perhaps in combination with the Hasimoto transform
[
Hasimoto1972
,
Banica2024
]
to deal with the local term – to relax such strong restriction in
future works.

5.3
Performance

[22]

We finally investigate the computational complexity of the proposed method
in terms of the total number of vortex points
N
.
We start by discussing how to choose the splitting parameter
\alpha

to achieve optimal complexity for a fixed value of the accuracy parameter
\beta
.
Intuitively,
\alpha
controls the relative amount of work done by short- and
long-range computations.
From the point of view of short-range computations, one may attempt to choose

\alpha
so that it reduces their expected complexity from
O(N^{2})
to
O(N)
.
Assuming a spatially homogeneous vortex point distribution, the runtime associated to
the short-range part can be estimated as
T^{\text{(n)}}\propto QN^{2}(r_{\text{c}}/L)^{3}
.
In terms of
\alpha
and
\beta
, this becomes
T^{\text{(n)}}\propto QN^{2}\beta^{3}/(\alpha L)^{3}
.
Therefore, choosing

[23]

\alpha(N)=C_{\beta}N^{1/3}/L

(33)

can be expected to lead to linear complexity, namely
T^{\text{(n)}}\propto QN(\beta/C_{\beta})^{3}
.
While
C_{\beta}
should be regarded as a constant when
N
is varied, its
optimal value may vary with the accuracy parameter
\beta
, hence the notation.
The next question is how does the choice
eq.

33

impact the complexity of long-range computations.
These are dominated by the cost of the NUFFTs, which basically consist of
\propto QN

spreading and interpolation operations and
O(M^{3}\log M)
3D FFTs (see

appendix

A
).
The former are already linear in
N
and do not depend on
\alpha
.
As for the latter, the size of each 1D FFT is
M\approx k_{\text{max}}L/\pi=2\beta\alpha L/\pi
, so
that choosing
\alpha
according to
eq.

33
leads
to
M\propto\beta C_{\beta}N^{1/3}
and therefore
O(N\log N)
complexity.
In conclusion, choosing
\alpha
as in
eq.

33
can
be expected to lead, at worst, to
O(N\log N)
complexity of the full method.

[24]

To numerically assess the performance of the method, we adapt the random
ellipses test case introduced in
section

5.1
, illustrated in

fig.

(right).
Each ellipse is now discretised with
N_{l}=32
vortex points, and the
Biot–Savart velocity is evaluated on all vortex points for systems with up
to 8192 vortices (
N=2^{18}
points).
The benchmarks are executed on a single core of
an Intel Core i7-12700H laptop processor using Julia 1.10.3.
To speed up short-range computations, a modified cell list
algorithm
[
Mattson1999
]
is used to find neighbouring points, in which the
spatial domain is divided into cells of size
r_{\text{c}}/3
in each direction.
We consider 7 different accuracy levels, determined by
\beta
and the NUFFT

table

.
In each case, we choose

so that the time spent in short- and

Empirically, we find that

roughly fulfills this criterion, but
the proper choice this prefactor is likely dependent on the actual implementation
and on the machine where the tests are run.

6
Conclusions

[34]

We have introduced an efficient technique for the numerical evaluation of the
Biot–Savart law in periodic systems when the source field (e.g. current or
vorticity) is supported on three-dimensional spatial curves.
The approach is adapted from fast Ewald summation methods commonly used to
accelerate particle simulations.
The present work is relevant to applications in electromagnetism and fluid dynamics,
where the source fields respectively induce magnetic or velocity fields around the curves.
As an application, we have considered the vortex filament model (VFM)
describing superfluid helium flows near absolute zero.
In this context, the Fourier-space representation of the induced fields
is a very attractive feature of the method, as it allows the efficient
evaluation of physically relevant quantities such as energy
spectra
[
Barenghi2014
]
, coarse-grained velocity fields
[
Baggaley2012
,
Laurie2023
]

and velocity circulation
[
Muller2021
,
Polanco2021
]
.

[35]

Several tunable parameters are introduced by the method, including an inverse

“splitting”
length scale
\alpha
and two cut-off parameters
r_{\text{c}}

and
k_{\text{max}}
in physical and Fourier spaces.
We have shown that the choice of the latter two can be reduced to that of a
single non-dimensional coefficient
\beta
setting the accuracy of the method,
while
\alpha
can be tuned to maximise performance.
For a fixed accuracy level, choosing
\alpha\propto N^{1/3}
leads to near-linear
O(N\log N)
complexity of the method, where
N
is the number of vortex discretisation points.
To our knowledge, this parameter selection procedure contrasts with the
seemingly common approach in molecular dynamics which consists in first
specifying reasonable cut-offs
r_{\text{c}}
and
k_{\text{max}}
in dimensional
units, and then choosing a value of
\alpha
which maximises
accuracy given these cut-offs
[
Deserno1998a
,
Ballenegger2008
,
Arnold2013
]
.

[36]

In terms of performance, our implementation is already
capable of simulating turbulent systems with a reasonably large number
of vortices using thread-based parallelism.
Moreover, our NUFFT implementation (
appendix

A
) enables the use of GPUs
to accelerate the computation of long-range interactions.
The short-range part may also benefit in the near future from a GPU implementation
of the cell lists algorithm for neighbour finding.
Besides, these core algorithms are very amenable to distributed-memory
parallelism (via MPI) using standard domain decomposition approaches, which
could enable computations on multiple CPU or GPU nodes.
Such strategies will be investigated in the future.

[37]

A second challenge concerns the timestep stability requirement
\Delta t\propto\ell_{\text{min}}^{2}
, which becomes very restrictive as the smallest resolved scale

\ell_{\text{min}}
is decreased.
Within this work, this issue is partially remediated by representing spatial
curves using highly continuous splines, which allows to use relatively large
values of
\ell_{\text{min}}
.
Potential strategies for further improving on these limitations can include the use of
splitting
[
Blanes2024
]
or multirate
[
Sandu2019
]
timestepping methods, in which the
evolution equations would be split onto a fast term responsible for
oscillations (e.g. the local self-induced velocity) and a
slower term (e.g. all non-local contributions).

[38]

The proposed method can be readily generalised to cases where the
vortex circulation
\kappa
varies along the filaments.
In particular, it may be used in the implementation of vortex filament methods for
classical flows
[
Cottet2000
]
.
Another possible extension concerns the simpler two-dimensional (2D) case, in which
vortex filaments are replaced by point vortices.
Periodic point vortex systems have received much attention
[
Weiss1991
,
Newton2009
,
vanKan2021a
,
Krishnamurthy2023
,
Grotto2024
]
, in part due to their
link with statistical mechanics and with 2D turbulence.
In that case, Ewald summation can be achieved using a function splitting similar to

eq.

19

[
Hasimoto1959
,
Cichocki1989
]
.
Finally, and more directly relevant to the motivation of this work, the proposed method
may be applied to the study of
finite-temperature
liquid
helium-4, in which the superfluid flow coexists and interacts with a normal viscous fluid.
A very relevant model for describing this regime consists in coupling the VFM
for the superfluid with the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations governing
the normal fluid
[
Kivotides2000
,
Yui2018
,
Galantucci2020
]
.
When the latter are solved using standard pseudo-spectral methods
[
Canuto1988
]
,
the Fourier-space representation included in the present method
can enable an accurate and efficient two-way transfer of interaction forces between the
vortices and the normal fluid.

Appendix A
NUFFT details and influence on Biot–Savart accuracy

[45]

Here we consider the error induced by the NUFFT tolerance on the long-range
component of Biot–Savart computations.
Briefly, a type-1 NUFFT can be summarised in three steps: (i) spreading
values from a set of non-uniform locations onto a uniform grid using some
smoothing kernel, (ii) performing a regular FFT on that grid, and (iii) undoing the
spreading operation by deconvolution in Fourier space
[
Greengard2004
]
.
The type-2 NUFFT is the adjoint operation, roughly consisting of the same steps
in opposite order.
To reduce aliasing errors, the first two steps are done in a grid of
\sigma M

points in each direction, where
M
is the number of desired Fourier modes and

\sigma>1
is an oversampling factor.
The accuracy of the NUFFT is then determined by (i) the choice of
spreading kernel, (ii) the width of the
kernel (usually in number of grid points
w
), and (iii) the oversampling factor

\sigma

[
Dutt1993
,
Potts2003
,
Barnett2019
]
.
The computational complexity of a 3D NUFFT on
N
non-uniform points can be
estimated to be
O(w^{3}N+(\sigma M)^{3}\log M)
.

To evaluate NUFFTs, here we use the (backwards) Kaiser–Bessel function as spreading
kernel
[
Jackson1991
,
Potts2003
]
.
For a support in
x\in[-1,1]
, the kernel and its Fourier transform are

[49]

\phi(x)=\frac{\sinh\!{\left(\zeta\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\right)}}{\pi\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\quad\leftrightarrow\quad\hat{\phi}(k)=I_{0}\!\left(\sqrt{\zeta^{2}-k^{2}}\right),

(34)

where
I_{0}
is the zero-th order modified Bessel function of the first kind.
The shape parameter is chosen as
\zeta=\gamma w\pi(1-1/2\sigma)
to
minimise NUFFT errors
[
Potts2003
]
.
Here
\gamma=0.995
is an empirical
“safety
factor”

[
Barnett2019
]
which slightly improves accuracy.
For performance reasons, the kernel
\phi(x)
is evaluated via
a fast and accurate piecewise polynomial approximation
[
Barnett2019
,
Shamshirgar2021
]
which takes advantage of SIMD vectorisation on modern CPUs.
Our NUFFT implementation
[
NonuniformFFTs
]
is written in Julia and is available on

https://github.com/jipolanco/NonuniformFFTs.jl
(MIT License).
Compared to other existing libraries available in Julia
[
Barnett2019
,
Knopp2023
]
,
our implementation allows real-valued non-uniform data, in which case it takes
advantage of real-to-complex FFT routines of the FFTW library
[
Frigo2005
]
.
Moreover, it uses the KernelAbstractions.jl
[
Churavy2024
]
package
to provide a platform-agnostic GPU implementation, enabling the acceleration of
vortex simulations using different kinds of GPUs.

[50]

In
fig.

6
, we evaluate the effect of varying the NUFFT
accuracy on the relative error of long-range Biot–Savart computations,
for the cases illustrated in
fig.

2
.
Concretely, we set the oversampling factor to
\sigma=1.5
and vary the
spreading width
w
.
For comparison, we compute a reference solution with
\sigma=2
and

w=16
, which ensures that its accuracy is dominated by roundoff error.
As seen in
fig.

6
, the relative error associated to the
long-range streamfunction and velocity decay exponentially as

\sim 10^{-0.9w}
when using an oversampling factor
\sigma=1.5
.
For
\sigma=2
, the same errors decay as
\sim 10^{-w}
(not shown), but
at the expense of costlier FFTs.

Appendix B
Discretisation of spatial curves

[54]

The evaluation of the Biot–Savart law
eq.

3
and its local
regularisation
eq.

5
on vortex filaments require the estimation
of local tangent and curvature vectors from
a set of discrete points
{\left\{\boldsymbol{s}_{j}\right\}}_{j=1}^{N}
representing a
spatial curve.
This assumes that curves can be parametrised by some sufficiently regular
function
\boldsymbol{s}(\tau)
whose derivatives on discrete locations
\tau_{j}
(such
that
\boldsymbol{s}(\tau_{j})=\boldsymbol{s}_{j}
) are well defined.
Furthermore, the use of quadratures to estimate line integrals (see
eq.

16
)
requires being able to evaluate (interpolate)
\boldsymbol{s}(\tau)
and its
derivatives at any arbitrary location
\tau
.
To achieve these requirements, we choose to discretise curves using parametric
quintic splines.
First, note that in general the nodes
\boldsymbol{s}_{j}
are not equispaced, and that
the choice of the parametrisation (the
\tau_{j}
values) is arbitrary.
We adopt the common choice
of setting
\tau_{j}=\tau_{j-1}+|\boldsymbol{s}_{j}-\boldsymbol{s}_{j-1}|
for

j\in\left\{2,\ldots,N\right\}
,
\tau_{1}=0
, where
|\cdot|

represents the Euclidean distance.
In this case,
\tau
is a rough approximation of the curve arc length
\xi
,
tending to
\xi
as the number of points increases.

[55]

We consider parametric splines of order
k

[
Boor1978
]
.
Here by convention the polynomial order is
k-1
, so that
k=4
and
6

respectively correspond to cubic and quintic splines.
The basic idea is to parametrise a spatial curve in terms of a B-spline basis,

[56]

\boldsymbol{s}(\tau)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\boldsymbol{c}_{j}b_{j}(\tau),\quad\text{for }\tau\in[0,\mathcal{T}],

(35)

where
b_{j}(\tau)
is a B-spline basis function.
The basis functions are fully defined by the choice of spline order
k
and spline

knots

t_{j}
, which are a set of locations in
[0,\mathcal{T}]
.
Because the curves considered here are periodic, we can simply
set
t_{j}=\tau_{j}
for all
j
(free-ended curves would require some extra care).
An important and convenient property of the B-splines is their compact support.
Namely, for even
k
, the B-spline
b_{j}(\tau)
is zero outside of the interval

[t_{j-k/2},t_{j+k/2}]
.

[57]

Spline interpolation consists in determining the coefficients
\boldsymbol{c}_{j}
given
the equalities
\boldsymbol{s}(\tau_{i})=\boldsymbol{s}_{i}
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
.
This leads to a linear system of the form
A_{ij}\boldsymbol{c}_{j}=\boldsymbol{s}_{i}
where

A_{ij}=b_{j}(\tau_{i})
.
Thanks to the compact support of the B-splines,
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}
is a cyclic banded matrix,
which enables the use of specialised algorithms to efficiently solve the system.
More precisely, with our choice of knots,
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}
has
k-1
bands, as well
as a few out-of-bands entries in the top right and bottom left corners due to
periodicity.
In particular, for cubic splines (
k=4
),
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}
is almost tridiagonal
with two out-of-bands entries (one on each corner).
A standard solution is to convert the system to a fully tridiagonal one using
the Sherman–Morrison formula and then use the Thomas algorithm to solve the
system in
O(N)
time
[
Yarrow1989
]
.
A similar procedure
[
Lv2008
]
can be applied to efficiently solve the cyclic
pentadiagonal system arising from periodic quintic spline interpolation (
k=6
).

[58]

The derivative of a spline
\boldsymbol{s}(\tau)
of order
k
is itself a spline of
order
k-1
, whose coefficients
\boldsymbol{c}^{\prime}_{j}
can be directly obtained from the
coefficients
\boldsymbol{c}_{j}
using a local differentiation formula.
Moreover, splines are efficiently evaluated at locations
\tau
using de Boor’s
algorithm
[
Boor1978
]
.
Therefore, knowing the coefficients
\boldsymbol{c}_{j}
one can evaluate curve positions
and derivatives at any location
\tau
.

Appendix C
Derivation of truncation error estimates

C.1
Short-range errors

[63]

The absolute error associated to the truncation of the short-range velocity
integral

eq.

23
at a single location
\boldsymbol{s}_{0}
is given by

[64]

\boldsymbol{v}^{\text{(n)}}_{\text{err}}(\boldsymbol{s}_{0})=\frac{\kappa}{4\pi}\sum_{\boldsymbol{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}}\int_{r>r_{\text{c}}}g^{\text{(n)}}(\boldsymbol{r})\,\frac{\boldsymbol{r}\times\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{s}}{|\boldsymbol{r}|^{3}}\quad\text{with }\boldsymbol{r}=\boldsymbol{s}-\boldsymbol{s}_{0}+\boldsymbol{n}L,

(36)

where the integral is over all vortex elements at a distance
|\boldsymbol{r}|=r>r_{\text{c}}

from the point
\boldsymbol{s}_{0}
.
We first note that, for large
r
, the weight function

g^{\text{(n)}}(\boldsymbol{r})
defined in
eq.

21

behaves as
g^{\text{(n)}}(\boldsymbol{r})=\frac{2\alpha r}{\sqrt{\pi}}e^{-{(\alpha r)}^{2}}\left[1+O(r^{-5/2})\right]
.
Secondly, we assume that the vortices are homogeneously distributed in the
spatial domain, so that the total vortex length within a spherical shell of radii

[r,r+\mathrm{d}r]
with infinitesimal thickness
\mathrm{d}r
is the product
between the mean vortex line density and the volume of the shell,
namely
\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}=(\mathcal{L}/V)(4\pi r^{2}\mathrm{d}r)
.
Here
\mathcal{L}
is the total vortex length within a periodic cell of volume
V=L^{3}
.
Thirdly, we assume that the orientations of the vortex elements
\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{s}
within the
shell are random and independent of the separation vector
\boldsymbol{r}
.
Under these assumptions, we can formally estimate the root mean square (rms) short-range
velocity error to be

[65]

\varepsilon^{\text{(n)}}_{v}\approx\frac{\kappa}{4\pi}\int_{r_{\text{c}}}^{\infty}\frac{2\alpha r}{\sqrt{\pi}}e^{-{(\alpha r)}^{2}}\frac{r\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}}{r^{3}}=\frac{2\kappa\mathcal{L}}{\sqrt{\pi}\alpha V}\int_{\beta^{\text{(n)}}}^{\infty}x\,e^{-x^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x,

(37)

where
\beta^{\text{(n)}}=\alpha r_{\text{c}}
.
This integrates exactly to

[66]

\varepsilon^{\text{(n)}}_{v}\approx\frac{\kappa\mathcal{L}}{\sqrt{\pi}\alpha V}\,e^{-{(\beta^{\text{(n)}})}^{2}}.

(38)

The same procedure can be applied to the short-range streamfunction
eq.

22
.
Considering that

\erfc(x)=\frac{e^{-x}}{x^{3/2}}\left[1+O(x^{-2})\right]

for large
x
, one obtains the associated rms error

[67]

\varepsilon^{\text{(n)}}_{\psi}\approx\frac{\kappa}{4\pi}\int_{r_{\text{c}}}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-{(\alpha r)}^{2}}}{{(\alpha r)}^{3/2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}}{r}=\frac{\kappa\mathcal{L}}{\alpha^{2}V}\int_{\beta^{\text{(n)}}}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-x^{2}}}{\sqrt{x}}\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{\kappa\mathcal{L}}{2\alpha^{2}V}\,\Gamma\!\left(\frac{1}{4},{\left(\beta^{\text{(n)}}\right)}^{2}\right).

(39)

Here
\Gamma(s,x)=\int_{x}^{\infty}u^{s-1}e^{-u}\,\mathrm{d}u
is the
upper incomplete gamma function, which behaves asymptotically as

\Gamma(s,x)=x^{s-1}e^{-x}\left[1+O(x^{-1})\right]
.
Replacing this in
eq.

39
finally
leads to the estimate

[68]

\varepsilon^{\text{(n)}}_{\psi}\approx\frac{\kappa\mathcal{L}}{2\alpha^{2}V}\,\frac{e^{-{(\beta^{\text{(n)}})}^{2}}}{{(\beta^{\text{(n)}})}^{3/2}}.

(40)

In both cases, the truncation error is dominated by the exponential decay
with the square of the non-dimensional cut-off parameter
\beta^{\text{(n)}}
.

C.2
Long-range errors

[71]

The rms error associated to the long-range velocity field is defined by

[72]

{\left(\varepsilon^{\text{(f)}}_{v}\right)}^{2}=\frac{1}{V}\int_{\Omega}\left|{\boldsymbol{v}^{\text{(f)}}(\boldsymbol{x})-\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\text{(f)}}(\boldsymbol{x})}\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}^{3}\boldsymbol{x},

(41)

where
\boldsymbol{v}^{\text{(f)}}(\boldsymbol{x})
is the Fourier-truncated velocity field defined
in
eq.

26
, and

\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\text{(f)}}(\boldsymbol{x})
is the non-truncated velocity field
– obtained by replacing
\mathbb{K}
with

\mathbb{K}_{\infty}\equiv\left\{\frac{2\pi m}{L};m\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}
in

eq.

26
.
Using Parseval’s theorem, the error can be equivalently written as

[73]

{\left(\varepsilon^{\text{(f)}}_{v}\right)}^{2}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in{(\mathbb{K}_{\infty}\backslash\,\mathbb{K})}^{3}}|\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\text{(f)}}|^{2}\;\leq\;\sum_{|\boldsymbol{k}|>k_{\text{max}}}|\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\text{(f)}}|^{2},

(42)

where
\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\text{(f)}}
are the Fourier coefficients of the long-range velocity.
The last inequality results from converting from a
“cubic”
truncation
(
k_{i}>k_{\text{max}}
for each Cartesian component
i
) to a spherical truncation
|\boldsymbol{k}|>k_{\text{max}}
.

[74]

In physical terms, the velocity error
eq.

42

can be directly related to the kinetic energy spectrum
E(k)
, defined in a
periodic domain as

[75]

E(k)=\frac{1}{2\Delta k}\sum_{|\boldsymbol{k}|\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}|\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}|^{2}\quad\text{for }k=m\Delta k,\;m\in\mathbb{N},

(43)

where
\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
is a Fourier coefficient of the (unsmoothed) velocity field,

\Delta k=2\pi/L
is the distance between two successive discrete
wavenumbers, and the sum is over all 3D wavenumbers
\boldsymbol{k}
within the
spherical shell
\mathcal{I}_{k}\equiv\left[k-\frac{\Delta k}{2},k+\frac{\Delta k}{2}\right)
.
Formally, the total kinetic energy (per unit mass) is then
E=\int_{0}^{\infty}E(k)\,\mathrm{d}k
.
In the present context,
eq.

43
will allow us to quantify the effect of truncating
Fourier-space computations at a certain wavenumber
k_{\text{max}}
.

[76]

At sufficiently small scales – smaller than the typical
distance between vortices and the typical curvature radius of the
vortices – vortex filaments can be considered as isolated straight lines.
Under that assumption,
one can show that the energy
spectrum is
E(k)=\frac{\kappa^{2}\mathcal{L}}{4\pi L^{3}}\,k^{-1}
for sufficiently
large
k
. To see this, one can consider the energy spectrum associated to a single straight vortex.
Without loss of generality, the vortex is aligned with the
z
direction and passes through the origin, so
that its length within the cubic box is
\mathcal{L}=L
and its
induced velocity field is invariant in
z
.
We can thus consider the simpler problem of a point vortex in
two-dimensional space.
By
eq.

25
, the Fourier coefficients of its associated vorticity field are

\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}=\frac{\kappa}{L^{2}}e^{-i\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}}\,\boldsymbol{e}_{z}=\frac{\kappa}{L^{2}}\boldsymbol{e}_{z}
,
where
\boldsymbol{x}=(x,y)=(0,0)
is the vortex location,
\boldsymbol{k}=(k_{x},k_{y})
, and

\boldsymbol{e}_{z}
is a unitary vector.
Then, the associated velocity coefficients are

\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}=i\boldsymbol{k}\times\frac{\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}}{k^{2}}
, whose squared magnitudes are

|\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}|^{2}=\frac{\kappa^{2}}{L^{4}k^{2}}
(since
\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
is orthogonal to
\boldsymbol{k}
).
Besides, one can estimate the number of 2D Fourier modes intersecting the shell

\mathcal{I}_{k}
to be on average
N_{k}=2\pi m=kL
.
Since
|\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}|^{2}
only depends on the amplitude of
\boldsymbol{k}
,
one can assume that all non-zero
\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
within the shell
\mathcal{I}_{k}
are approximately equal.
Replacing in
eq.

43
, we finally obtain the energy spectrum

E(k)\approx\frac{L}{4\pi}N_{k}|\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}|^{2}\approx\frac{\kappa^{2}}{4\pi L^{2}}k^{-1}=\frac{\kappa^{2}\mathcal{L}}{4\pi L^{3}}k^{-1}
.

[77]

This prediction is accurately verified in
fig.

7
(blue curves) for the three
different vortex configurations illustrated in
fig.

2
.
Note that the relatively slow decay as
k^{-1}
suggests that the integral of this
spectrum (and thus the total energy) presents a logarithmic divergence.
In fact, physically this is not the case, since the spectrum is regularised near the
wavenumber
k_{a}=2\pi/a
associated to the vortex core size

a
, which is orders of magnitude smaller than the scales described by the VFM.

Figure 7:
Kinetic energy spectra associated to three different test cases.
The domain period is
L=2\pi
and the spectra are truncated at
k_{\text{max}}=64

(the number of Fourier modes in each direction is
M=128
).
Each panel shows the truncated energy spectrum associated to the unsmoothed
velocity field
(\alpha L=\infty)
, as well as the energy spectra
associated to two Gaussian-smoothed velocity fields (
\alpha L=240\text{ and }24
).
The spectra are normalised by the total kinetic energy
E_{\text{total}}
obtained via

eq.

9
.
At large wavenumbers
k
, the spectra are well approximated by

E(k)=\frac{\kappa^{2}\mathcal{L}}{4\pi V}\,k^{-1}e^{-k^{2}/2\alpha^{2}}

(black dashed lines), where
\mathcal{L}
is the total vortex length in
each test case.

[80]

To provide an estimate of the error in the computation of the coarse-grained
velocity
\boldsymbol{v}^{\text{(f)}}
, we now consider its associated kinetic energy
spectrum
E^{\text{(f)}}(k)=E(k)\,e^{-k^{2}/2\alpha^{2}}=\frac{\kappa^{2}\mathcal{L}}{4\pi V}\,k^{-1}e^{-k^{2}/2\alpha^{2}}
for large
k
.
Note that the integral of
E^{\text{(f)}}(k)
is the contribution
of the long-range velocity field to the total kinetic energy
E
.
Examples of
E^{\text{(f)}}(k)
are shown in
fig.

7
for two
different values of the smoothing parameter
\alpha
.
In the figure, the long-range truncation is at
k_{\text{max}}=64
and the domain period is
L=2\pi
.
It is clear that, if
\alpha
is too large (e.g.
\alpha L=240
in the
figure, corresponding to
\beta^{\text{(f)}}=k_{\text{max}}/2\alpha\approx 0.84
), then this level of
truncation is insufficient to capture the whole
long-range kinetic energy.
A smaller value of
\alpha
(
\alpha L=24
in the figure) means that the full
coarse-grained energy can be captured when truncating at the same
k_{\text{max}}
.

[81]

Assuming the truncation wavenumber
k_{\text{max}}
to be sufficiently large, we can now
estimate an upper bound for the absolute truncation error

eq.

42
associated to the long-range
velocity,

[82]

\frac{{\left(\varepsilon^{\text{(f)}}_{v}\right)}^{2}}{2}\approx\int_{k_{\text{max}}}^{\infty}E^{\text{(f)}}(k)\,\mathrm{d}k=\frac{\kappa^{2}\mathcal{L}}{4\pi V}\int_{k_{\text{max}}}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-k^{2}/2\alpha^{2}}}{k}\,\mathrm{d}k=\frac{\kappa^{2}\mathcal{L}}{8\pi V}\,E_{1}\!\left[2{(\beta^{\text{(f)}})}^{2}\right],

(44)

where
E_{1}(x)=\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-u}}{u}\,\mathrm{d}u
is the exponential
integral function.
For large
x
,
E_{1}(x)=\frac{e^{-x}}{x}\left[1-\frac{1}{x}+O(x^{-2})\right]
,
and therefore

{\left(\varepsilon^{\text{(f)}}_{v}\right)}^{2}\approx\frac{\kappa^{2}\mathcal{L}}{8\pi V}\,e^{-2{(\beta^{\text{(f)}})}^{2}}/{(\beta^{\text{(f)}})}^{2}
.
Finally, the estimated error associated to the long-range velocity field is

[83]

\varepsilon^{\text{(f)}}_{v}\approx\kappa\,{\left(\frac{\mathcal{L}}{8\pi V}\right)}^{\!\!1/2}\,\frac{e^{-{(\beta^{\text{(f)}})}^{2}}}{\beta^{\text{(f)}}}.

(45)

[84]

A similar estimation can be done for the long-range truncation error

\varepsilon^{\text{(f)}}_{\psi}
associated to the streamfunction.
This is obtained by integrating

[85]

\frac{{(\varepsilon^{\text{(f)}}_{\psi})}^{2}}{2}\approx\int_{k_{\text{max}}}^{\infty}\frac{E^{\text{(f)}}(k)}{k^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}k\approx\frac{\kappa^{2}}{4\alpha^{2}}\frac{\mathcal{L}}{4\pi V}\frac{e^{-2{(\beta^{\text{(f)}})}^{2}}}{{[2{(\beta^{\text{(f)}})}^{2}]}^{2}},

(46)

where we have used

\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-u}}{u^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}u=\frac{e^{-x}}{x}-E_{1}(x)=\frac{e^{-x}}{x^{2}}\left[1+O(x^{-1})\right]
.
This results in

[86]

\varepsilon^{\text{(f)}}_{\psi}\approx\frac{\kappa}{2\alpha}\,{\left(\frac{\mathcal{L}}{8\pi V}\right)}^{\!\!1/2}\,\frac{e^{-{(\beta^{\text{(f)}})}^{2}}}{{(\beta^{\text{(f)}})}^{2}}.

(47)

[87]

Similarly to short-range errors, the long-range truncation errors are dominated
by the exponential decay with the square of the cut-off parameter
\beta^{\text{(f)}}
.
We stress that the estimates

eqs.

45
and
47
are
expected to be valid for sufficiently large values of
k_{\text{max}}
(or

\beta^{\text{(f)}}
).
Furthermore, they can be expected to overestimate the actual errors due to the
inequality in
eq.

42
, which discards
the contributions of some large-wavenumber modes which are actually resolved in
the simulations (more precisely, a factor of
\sim\!6/\pi
Fourier modes is
discarded by the above estimates).

C.3
Combined truncation errors

[90]

To avoid unnecessary computations and achieve optimal accuracy for a given
computational cost, one would like the errors associated to the short- and long-range
components to be equivalent.
In light of the above estimations, it seems natural to set their respective
non-dimensional cut-off parameters to the same value,
\beta^{\text{(n)}}=\beta^{\text{(f)}}=\beta
, which also
helps reducing the size of the parameter space.
This finally leads to the following total truncation error estimates:

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{v}

\displaystyle\approx\kappa\left[\frac{\mathcal{L}}{\sqrt{\pi}\alpha V}+\frac{1}{\beta}{\left(\frac{\mathcal{L}}{8\pi V}\right)}^{\!\!1/2}\right]e^{-\beta^{2}},

(48)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\psi}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\kappa}{2\alpha}\left[\frac{\mathcal{L}}{\alpha V}+\frac{1}{\beta^{1/2}}{\left(\frac{\mathcal{L}}{8\pi V}\right)}^{\!\!1/2}\right]\frac{e^{-\beta^{2}}}{\beta^{3/2}}.

(49)

In summary, the accuracy of the method is mainly controlled by a unique
non-dimensional parameter
\beta
, from which both physical- and Fourier-space
cut-offs
r_{\text{c}}
and
k_{\text{max}}
can be obtained.
This still leaves the inverse splitting distance
\alpha
as a free parameter
that can be adjusted to optimise performance (
section

5.3
).

[91]
