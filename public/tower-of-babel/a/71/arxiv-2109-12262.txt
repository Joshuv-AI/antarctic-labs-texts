Recent advances on eigenvalues of matrix-valued stochastic processes Journal: Journal of Multivariate Analysis
Jian Song, Jianfeng Yao, Wangjun Yuan

Abstract
Since the introduction of Dyson’s Brownian motion in early 1960’s, there have been a lot of developments in the investigation of stochastic processes on the space of Hermitian matrices. Their properties, especially, the properties of their eigenvalues have been studied in great details. In particular, the limiting behaviors of the eigenvalues are found when the dimension of the matrix space tends to infinity, which connects with random matrix theory. This survey reviews a selection of results on the eigenvalues of stochastic processes from the literature of the past three decades. For most recent variations of such processes, such as matrix-valued processes driven by fractional Brownian motion or Brownian sheet, the eigenvalues of them are also discussed in this survey. In the end, some open problems in the area are also proposed.

Introduction

Stochastic processes with values in the space of symmetric matrices have been attracting the attention for some years.
Their introduction is commonly attributed to the celebrated work

[
16
]
by F. J. Dyson.By that time, Gaussian matrix ensembles were well known; the distribution of their eigenvalues has a density function of the form

G(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=C\exp\{-\beta W\},\quad W=W(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=-\sum_{i<j}\ln|x_{i}-x_{j}|+\sum_{i}(x_{i}^{2}/2a^{2}),

(1)

where
\beta,a>0
are parameters, and
C=C(\beta,a)
is a
normalization constant.
The distribution (
1
) also appears in the Coulomb gas model: it is the probability distribution of the positions of
n
point charges which are free to move on the real line
\mathbb{R}
under the forces derived from the potential energy
W
and in a state of thermodynamic equilibrium at a temperature
T=(k\beta)^{-1}
(
k
is the Boltzmann constant).
Note that the equation (
1
) is static and does not describe the evolution of the position of the point charges before reaching the equilibrium. Dyson brought in the Brownian motion to get a time-dependent model that describes the evolution of the positions from an initial distribution
F_{0}
. The Brownian motion, also called the time-dependent Coulomb gas has a simple structure, and the joint density function
F(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n};t)
of the positions of the
n
point charges at time
t>0
is fully characterized as a solution to the Smoluchowski equation

\displaystyle c\frac{\partial F}{\partial t}=\sum_{i}\left[\frac{1}{\beta}\frac{\partial^{2}F}{\partial x_{i}^{2}}-\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\{E(x_{i})F\}\right],

(2)

where
c
is a constant and

\displaystyle E(x_{i})=-\frac{\partial W}{\partial x_{i}}=\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{1}{x_{j}-x_{i}}-\frac{x_{i}}{a^{2}}

is an external electric force. In particular,
F(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n};t)
tends to the Coulomb gas distribution
G
in (
1
) as
t\to\infty
.

This extension of Coulomb gas from the static equilibrium state to a dynamical version also applies to the associated Gaussian matrix ensembles.
More precisely, Dyson introduced a stochastic process with values in the space of symmetric matrices, the eigenvalues of which coincide with the dynamical Coulomb gas model.
Amazingly, this process is extremely simple:
its elements are independent Ornstein-Uhlenbeck processes on the underlying field! (The underlying field is
\mathbb{R}
for
\beta=1
,
\mathbb{C}
for
\beta=2
, and the quaternion field for
\beta=4
).

This deep connection between stochastic processes with values in the space of symmetric (Hermitian) matrices and the induced dynamical system of its eigenvalues, had been however quite ignored during a while afterwards.
It was revived in the papers
[
50
,
6
]
where the idea of Dyson was extended to the space of positive-definite matrices (ellipsoids).
In the subsequent three decades, the study of these stochastic processes and their associated eigenvalue processes has been developed in much depth. Particularly, the symmetric (Hermitian) matrix-valued processes have covered Brownian motion, Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process and fractional Brownian motion.

Instead of considering the
N
particles (eigenvalues) with
N
fixed, the limits of the empirical measures of particles when
N
tends to infinity (high-dimensional limits) were studied in several models. In particular, the high-dimensional limit of the empirical measures of the Dyson’s Brownian motion is the famous Wigner’s semicircle law, which provides a dynamical version of Wigner’s Theorem for GOE and GUE (see, e.g.,
[
1
]
). In this aspect, the study of large particle systems is closely related to the random matrix theory. Moreover, the equation satisfied by the limits of the empirical measures of the Dyson’s Brownian motion is the so-called McKean-Vlasov equation, which appears in the study of propagation of chaos for large systems of interacting particles (see
[
3
,
30
,
62
]
).

Another motivation for studying high-dimensional limits of the empirical measures of eigenvalues arises from free probability theory. By
[
4
]
, the free additive Brownian motion can be viewed as the high-dimensional limit of a matrix Brownian motion with appropriate scaling. Moreover,
[
4
,
5
]
developed the stochastic calculus for free Brownian motion. Besides, the non-commutative fractional Brownian motion was introduced in
[
51
]
.

There is also a deep connection between matrix-valued stochastic processes and
multivariate statistical analysis. Here are a few applications of these
processes in recent statistical literature:

1.

Financial data analysis: multivariate volatility/co-volatility (variance/covariance) between stock returns or interest rates from different markets have been studied recently through Wishart processes, see
[
22
,
23
,
18
,
13
,
19
,
20
,
70
]
.

2.

Machine learning: an important task in machine learning using kernel functions is the determination of a suitable kernel matrix for a given data analysis problem (
[
61
]
). Such determination is referred as the kernel matrix learning problem. A kernel matrix is in fact a positive definite Gram-matrix of size
N\times N
where
N
, the sample size of the data, is usually large. An innovative method for kernel learning is proposed by
[
72
]
where unknown kernel matrix is modelled by a Wishart process prior. This approach has been followed in
[
40
,
44
]
.

3.

Computer vision: real-time computer vision often involves
tracking of objects of interest. At each time
t
, a target is
encoded into a
N
-dimensional vector
a_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
(feature
vector). It is therefore clear that measuring “distance” between
these vectors, say
a_{t}
and
a_{t+dt}
at two consecutive time
spots
t
and
t+dt
, is of crucial importance for object
tracking. Because the standard Euclidean distance
\|a_{t+dt}-a_{t}\|^{2}
is rarely optimal, it is more satisfactory to identify a
better metric of the form
(a_{t+dt}-a_{t})^{\intercal}M_{t}(a_{t+dt}-a_{t})
using a suitable positive definite matrix
M_{t}
. An
innovative model where the process
M_{t}
follows a Wishart process
is proposed in
[
42
]
.

This survey reviews a selection of results from the last three decades. In Section
2
, we provide a study of Dyson’s Brownian motion with full details. This includes a modern derivation of the process using Itô calculus.
A limit for the processes of empirical eigenvalue measures is derived when the number of eigenvalues, or electric charges, tends to infinity.
Besides, a limiting Gaussian process is derived in order to characterize the fluctuation of the empirical eigenvalue measures around their limit.
In Section
3
, we discuss two specific classes of stochastic processes with values in the space of positive-definite matrices, that is, Brownian motions of ellipsoids and Wishart processes.
In Section
4
, a more general form of stochastic processes on the space of Hermitian matrices is studied, and a link is also made with some familiar systems of interacting particles.
The following Sections
5
and
6
concern extensions of Dyson’s Brownian motion in two different directions. The first extension replaces the Brownian motions in the matrix by fractional Brownian motions, and the second one by Brownian sheets. Finally in Section
7
, we conclude with a discussion on open problems related to the results introduced in the preceding sections.

2
Dyson’s Brownian motion

In this section, we mainly focus on the Dyson’s Brownian motion. We discuss the system of SDEs satisfied by Dyson’s Brownian motion in Section
2.1
and the limiting behaviors of the eigenvalue empirical measure process in Section
2.2
.

2.1
Finite-dimensional results

Throughout the survey, we denote the complex imaginary by
\iota=\sqrt{-1}
.

Definition 1
.

Let
\{B_{i,j}(t),\tilde{B}_{i,j}(t),1\leq i\leq j\leq N\}
be a family of i.i.d. real valued standard Brownian motions. Let
H^{N,\beta}(t)=\left(H^{N,\beta}_{k,l}(t)\right)_{1\leq k\leq l\leq N}
be a real symmetric (
\beta=1
) or complex Hermitian (
\beta=2
)
N\times N
matrix-valued process with entries

\displaystyle H^{N,\beta}_{k,l}(t)=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\beta N}}\left(B_{k,l}(t)+\iota(\beta-1)\tilde{B}_{k,l}(t)\right){\large 1}_{\{k<l\}}+\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\beta N}}B_{l,l}(t){\large 1}_{\{k=l\}}.

Then
H^{N,1}(t)
is a real symmetric matrix Brownian motion and
H^{N,2}(t)
is a complex Hermitian matrix Brownian motion.

The following results state that the eigenvalue processes of real symmetric or complex Hermitian matrix Brownian motion never collide almost surely and are characterized by a system of stochastic differential equations (SDEs).

Theorem 1

(
[
1
]
, Theorem 4.3.2)
.

Let
X^{N,\beta}(0)
be a real symmetric (
\beta=1
) or complex Hermitian (
\beta=2
)
N\times N
deterministic matrix and let
X^{N,\beta}(t)=X^{N,\beta}(0)+H^{N,\beta}(t)
. Let
\lambda_{1}^{N,\beta}(t)\geq\lambda_{2}^{N,\beta}(t)\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{N}^{N,\beta}(t)
be the ordered eigenvalue processes of
X^{N,\beta}(t)
. Denote the first collision time of the eigenvalue processes by

\displaystyle\tau_{N,\beta}=\inf\left\{t>0:\exists\ i\neq j,~\lambda_{i}^{N,\beta}(t)=\lambda_{j}^{N,\beta}(t)\right\}.

(3)

Then

\mathbb{P}\left(\tau_{N,\beta}=+\infty\right)=1.

Furthermore, the ordered eigenvalue processes
\lambda_{1}^{N,\beta}(t)>\cdots>\lambda_{N}^{N,\beta}(t)
are the unique solution to the following system of SDEs:

\displaystyle d\lambda_{i}^{N,\beta}(t)=\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\beta N}}dW_{i}(t)+\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{j:j\neq i}\dfrac{dt}{\lambda_{i}^{N,\beta}(t)-\lambda_{j}^{N,\beta}(t)},~i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}.

(4)

Here,
\{W_{1}(t),\ldots,W_{N}(t)\}
is a family of independent standard Brownian motions.

Proof:
.

The proof is motivated by
[
24
, Theorem 3,5]
and
[
1
, Lemma 4.3.3]
. We only consider the real symmetric case
\beta=1
. The complex Hermitian case
\beta=2
is similar and thus is omitted. Since the dimension
N
is fixed, we may omit both
N
and
\beta
on subscript and superscript without ambiguity. For simplicity, we only give a proof under the condition
\lambda_{1}(0)>\cdots>\lambda_{N}(0)
. For the case that
X(0)
has collision eigenvalues, we refer the interested readers to
[
1
, Page 257]
. We divide the proof into three steps.

Step 1: Derivation of the system of SDEs for eigenvalue processes before the first collision time by Itô calculus and martingale theory.

We may use the Stratonovich differential notation, which can be founded in, for example,
[
29
, Chapter III]
. For two
N\times N
matrices
X
and
Y
, we have
X\circ dY=XdY+\frac{1}{2}dXdY
, where
XdY
is the Itô differential,
X\circ dY
is the Stratonovich differential and
dXdY=d\langle X,Y\rangle
. By matrix multiplication, for three
N\times N
matrices
X,Y
and
Z
, one can verify that

\displaystyle dX\circ(YZ)=(dX\circ Y)\circ Z=dXYZ+\dfrac{1}{2}\left(dXdYZ+dXYdZ\right),

\displaystyle(X\circ dY)\circ Z=X\circ(dY\circ Z)=XdYZ+\dfrac{1}{2}\left(dXdYZ+XdYdZ\right),

\displaystyle(X\circ dY)^{\intercal}=dY^{\intercal}\circ X^{\intercal}.

Moreover, by Itô formula and matrix multiplication, one can verify that

\displaystyle d(XYZ)=dX\circ YZ+X\circ dY\circ Z+XY\circ dZ.

(5)

For a real symmetric matrix process
X(t)
, consider its spectral decomposition
X(t)=P(t)D(t)P(t)^{\intercal}
, where
D(t)
is a diagonal matrix of eigenvalues of
X(t)
ordered decreasingly, and
P(t)
is an orthogonal matrix of eigenvectors of
X(t)
. According to
[
50
]
, the matrices
D(t),P(t)
can be chosen as smooth functions of
X(t)
for
t<\tau_{N,\beta}
. Let
Q(t)
be the matrix-valued processes satisfying

\displaystyle dQ(t)=P(t)^{-1}\circ dP(t)=P(t)^{\intercal}\circ dP(t).

The process
Q(t)
is known as the stochastic logarithm of
P(t)
. By the Itô formula (
5
), we have the following identity

\displaystyle 0=dI_{N}=d(P(t)^{\intercal}P(t))=dQ(t)+dQ(t)^{\intercal}.

(6)

Applying the Itô formula (
5
) to the spectral decomposition of
X(t)
and using (
6
), we have

\displaystyle dD(t)

\displaystyle=dP(t)^{\intercal}\circ X(t)P(t)+P(t)^{\intercal}\circ dX(t)\circ P(t)+P(t)^{\intercal}X(t)\circ dP(t)

\displaystyle=dP(t)^{\intercal}\circ P(t)D(t)+P(t)^{\intercal}\circ dX(t)\circ P(t)+D(t)P(t)^{\intercal}\circ dP(t)

\displaystyle=-dQ(t)\circ D(t)+P(t)^{\intercal}\circ dX(t)\circ P(t)+D(t)\circ dQ(t).

(7)

By considering the non-diagonal entries of (
2.1
), we have

\displaystyle dQ_{ij}(t)=-\left(P(t)^{\intercal}\circ dX(t)\circ P(t)\right)_{ij}\circ\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{i}(t)-\lambda_{j}(t)},~i\neq j.

(8)

On the other hand, the diagonal entries of (
2.1
) can be written as

\displaystyle d\lambda_{i}(t)=

\displaystyle\left(P(t)^{\intercal}\circ dX(t)\circ P(t)\right)_{ii}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(P(t)^{\intercal}dX(t)P(t)\right)_{ii}+\dfrac{1}{2}\Big(dP(t)^{\intercal}dX(t)P(t)+P(t)^{\intercal}dX(t)dP(t)\Big)_{ii}.

(9)

Recalling Definition
1
, one can see that
\{\left(P(t)^{\intercal}\circ dX(t)\circ P(t)\right)_{ii}\}_{1\leq i\leq N}
is a family of local martingales with quadratic covariation

\displaystyle\left(P(t)^{\intercal}\circ dX(t)\circ P(t)\right)_{ii}\left(P(t)^{\intercal}\circ dX(t)\circ P(t)\right)_{jj}=\left(P(t)^{\intercal}dX(t)P(t)\right)_{ii}\left(P(t)^{\intercal}dX(t)P(t)\right)_{jj}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k,l=1}^{N}P_{ki}(t)P_{li}(t)dX_{kl}(t)\sum_{k^{\prime},l^{\prime}=1}^{N}P_{k^{\prime}j}(t)P_{l^{\prime}j}(t)dX_{k^{\prime}l^{\prime}}(t)=\sum_{k,l,k^{\prime},l^{\prime}=1}^{N}P_{ki}(t)P_{li}(t)P_{k^{\prime}j}(t)P_{l^{\prime}j}(t)\left(\text{1}_{[k=k^{\prime}]}\text{1}_{[l=l^{\prime}]}+\text{1}_{[k=l^{\prime}]}\text{1}_{[l=k^{\prime}]}\right)\dfrac{dt}{N}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{2dt}{N}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}P_{ki}(t)P_{kj}(t)\right)^{2}=\dfrac{2}{N}\text{1}_{[i=j]}dt,

where we use the orthogonality of the columns of the matrix
P(t)
. Thus, by Knight’s theorem, there exists a family of independent standard
1
-dimensional Brownian motions
\{W_{1}(t),\ldots,W_{N}(t)\}
, such that

\displaystyle\left(P(t)^{\intercal}\circ dX(t)\circ P(t)\right)_{ii}=\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{N}}dW_{i}(t).

(10)

Note that
X(t)
is symmetric, by (
6
) and (
8
), we have

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2}\Big(dP(t)^{\intercal}dX(t)P(t)+P(t)^{\intercal}dX(t)dP(t)\Big)_{ii}=\Big(dP(t)^{\intercal}dX(t)P(t)\Big)_{ii}

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle\Big(dP(t)^{\intercal}P(t)P(t)^{\intercal}dX(t)P(t)\Big)_{ii}=\Big(dQ(t)^{\intercal}\left(P(t)^{\intercal}\circ dX(t)\circ P(t)\right)\Big)_{ii}

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle-\sum_{j=1}^{N}dQ_{ij}(t)\left(P(t)^{\intercal}\circ dX(t)\circ P(t)\right)_{ji}=\sum_{j:j\neq i}\dfrac{\left(P(t)^{\intercal}dX(t)P(t)\right)_{ij}\left(P(t)^{\intercal}dX(t)P(t)\right)_{ji}}{\lambda_{i}(t)-\lambda_{j}(t)}.

(11)

For
i\neq j
, we have

\displaystyle\left(P(t)^{\intercal}dX(t)P(t)\right)_{ij}\left(P(t)^{\intercal}dX(t)P(t)\right)_{ji}=\sum_{k,l=1}^{N}P_{ki}(t)P_{lj}(t)dX_{kl}(t)\sum_{k^{\prime},l^{\prime}=1}^{N}P_{k^{\prime}j}(t)P_{l^{\prime}i}(t)dX_{k^{\prime}l^{\prime}}(t)

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle\sum_{k,l,k^{\prime},l^{\prime}=1}^{N}P_{ki}(t)P_{lj}(t)P_{k^{\prime}j}(t)P_{l^{\prime}i}(t)\left(\text{1}_{[k=k^{\prime}]}\text{1}_{[l=l^{\prime}]}+\text{1}_{[k=l^{\prime}]}\text{1}_{[l=k^{\prime}]}\right)\dfrac{dt}{N}

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle\dfrac{dt}{N}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}P_{ki}(t)P_{kj}(t)\right)^{2}+\dfrac{dt}{N}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}P_{ki}(t)^{2}\right)\left(\sum_{l=1}^{N}P_{lj}(t)^{2}\right)=\dfrac{dt}{N}.

(12)

Substituting (
2.1
) to (
2.1
), we have

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2}\Big(dP(t)^{\intercal}dX(t)P(t)+P(t)^{\intercal}dX(t)dP(t)\Big)_{ii}=\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{j:j\neq i}\dfrac{dt}{\lambda_{i}(t)-\lambda_{j}(t)}.

(13)

Therefore, (
4
) follows from (
2.1
), (
10
) and (
13
).

Step 2: We prove that the system of SDEs (
4
) has a unique strong solution before its first collision time by approximating the singular drift with regular functions. For the existence and uniqueness of SDE, we refer to
[
33
]
.

For
R>0
, define

\displaystyle\psi_{R}(x)=x^{-1}{\large 1}_{\{|x|\geq R^{-1}\}}+R^{2}x{\large 1}_{\{|x|<R^{-1}\}}.

(14)

One can easily check that
\psi_{R}(x)
is continuous on
\mathbb{R}
satisfying

|\psi_{R}(x)|\leq(1+R^{2})(1+|x|^{2}).

Consider the following system of SDEs

\displaystyle d\lambda_{i}^{R}(t)=\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{N}}dW_{i}(t)+\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{j:j\neq i}\psi_{R}\left(\lambda_{i}^{R}(t)-\lambda_{j}^{R}(t)\right)dt,~i\in\{1,\ldots,N\},

(15)

with initial condition
\lambda_{i}^{R}(0)=\lambda_{i}(0)
for
1\leq i\leq N
. Noting that for each
R>0
, the coefficient functions in (
15
) are global Lipschitz and of linear growth, the existence of the strong solution of (
15
) follows from
[
33
, Theorem 2.9]
, and moreover, by
[
33
, Theorem 2.5]
, we also have the strong uniqueness.

For
R>0
, let

\displaystyle\tau(R)=\inf\left\{t>0:\min_{i\neq j}\left|\lambda_{i}^{R}(t)-\lambda_{j}^{R}(t)\right|<R^{-1}\right\}.

Then
\tau(R)
is a stopping time which is increasing with respect to
R
. We denote
\tau(+\infty)=\lim_{R\to+\infty}\tau(R)
, which may be
+\infty
. Let
R_{0}
be a positive number such that
R_{0}^{-1}=\min_{i\neq j}|\lambda_{i}(0)-\lambda_{j}(0)|
. For
R_{1}>R_{2}>R_{0}
, we have the following observation

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}^{R_{1}}(t)=\lambda_{i}^{R_{2}}(t),~\forall t\leq\tau(R_{2}),~\forall 1\leq i\leq N.

Thus, for
t<\tau(+\infty)
, we can define the processes
\lambda_{i}^{\infty}(t)
in a consistent way by

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}^{\infty}(t)=\lambda_{i}^{R}(t),~\text{if}~t<\tau(R)

for
1\leq i\leq N
. Then, recalling the definition (
14
) of
\psi_{R}
,
(\lambda_{1}^{\infty}(t),\ldots,\lambda_{N}^{\infty}(t))
solves (
4
) for
t<\tau(+\infty)
. Note that for any strong solution of (
4
), it solves (
15
) before the time when the least distance of its entries reaches
R^{-1}
for
R>R_{0}
. Thus, the strong uniqueness of (
4
) follows from the strong uniqueness of (
15
) by letting
R\to\infty
.

Step 3: We prove the almost sure non-collision of the eigenvalue processes by McKean’s argument (
[
46
, Proposition 4.3]
, see also
[
47
]
).

From Step 1 and Step 2, we can see that the eigenvalue processes of
H^{N,\beta}(t)
is the unique strong solution to (
4
), and thus
\tau_{N,\beta}
given by (
3
) is also the collision time for the strong solution to (
4
). For
t<\tau_{N,\beta}
, define

\displaystyle U(t)=\sum_{i<j}\ln|\lambda_{i}(t)-\lambda_{j}(t)|,

(16)

then by (
4
) and Itô formula, noting that
d\langle\lambda_{i}(t),\lambda_{j}(t)\rangle=0
for
i\neq j
, we have

\displaystyle dU(t)=

\displaystyle\sum_{i\neq j}\dfrac{d\lambda_{i}(t)}{\lambda_{i}(t)-\lambda_{j}(t)}-\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i\neq j}\dfrac{d\langle\lambda_{i}(t)\rangle}{\left(\lambda_{i}(t)-\lambda_{j}(t)\right)^{2}}

(17)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i\neq j}\dfrac{dW_{i}}{\lambda_{i}(t)-\lambda_{j}(t)}+\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i\neq j}\sum_{l:l\neq i}\dfrac{dt}{\left(\lambda_{i}(t)-\lambda_{j}(t)\right)\left(\lambda_{i}(t)-\lambda_{l}(t)\right)}-\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i\neq j}\dfrac{dt}{\left(\lambda_{i}(t)-\lambda_{j}(t)\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i\neq j}\dfrac{dW_{i}}{\lambda_{i}(t)-\lambda_{j}(t)}+\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i\neq j\neq l\neq i}\dfrac{dt}{\left(\lambda_{i}(t)-\lambda_{j}(t)\right)\left(\lambda_{i}(t)-\lambda_{l}(t)\right)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i\neq j}\dfrac{dW_{i}}{\lambda_{i}(t)-\lambda_{j}(t)}+\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i\neq j\neq l\neq i}\dfrac{\lambda_{l}(t)-\lambda_{j}(t)}{\left(\lambda_{i}(t)-\lambda_{j}(t)\right)\left(\lambda_{j}(t)-\lambda_{l}(t)\right)\left(\lambda_{l}(t)-\lambda_{i}(t)\right)}dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i\neq j}\dfrac{dW_{i}}{\lambda_{i}(t)-\lambda_{j}(t)}+\dfrac{1}{3N}\sum_{i\neq j\neq l\neq i}\dfrac{\left(\lambda_{l}(t)-\lambda_{j}(t)\right)+\left(\lambda_{i}(t)-\lambda_{l}(t)\right)+\left(\lambda_{j}(t)-\lambda_{i}(t)\right)}{\left(\lambda_{i}(t)-\lambda_{j}(t)\right)\left(\lambda_{j}(t)-\lambda_{l}(t)\right)\left(\lambda_{l}(t)-\lambda_{i}(t)\right)}dt=\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i\neq j}\dfrac{dW_{i}}{\lambda_{i}(t)-\lambda_{j}(t)}.

Here, we use the symmetry to change the summation index in the fifth equality. Therefore, by Lemma
1
below, we have
\tau_{N,\beta}=+\infty
almost surely.
The proof of Theorem
1
is complete.

The following lemma is used in the Step 3 of the proof and is known as the McKean’s argument, which can be found in

Lemma 1

(McKean’s argument,
[
46
]
, Proposition 4.3)
.

Let
Z=\{Z(t);0\leq t<\infty\}
be an adapted
\mathbb{R}_{+}
-valued
stochastic process that is right-continuous with finite left-hand
limits (RCLL) on a stochastic interval
[0,\tau_{0})
with
Z_{0}>0
,
where

\tau_{0}=\inf\{s>0:Z_{s-}=0\}.

Suppose that there exists a continuous function
h
satisfying the following:

(i)

For all
t\in[0,\tau_{0})
, we have

h(Z(t))=h(Z(0))+M(t)+P(t)
,
where
M
is a continuous local martingale on
[0,\tau_{0})

with
M(0)=0
, and
P
is an adapted RCLL process on

[0,\tau_{0})
such that almost surely and for each
T>0
,

\displaystyle\inf_{t\in[0,\tau_{0}\wedge T)}P(t)>-\infty.

(ii)

\lim_{z\downarrow 0}h(z)=-\infty
.

Then
\tau_{0}=\infty
almost surely.

Remark 1
.

The argument for non-collision in
[
1
]
is different. For
M>0
, it is shown that the first time for
U(t)
with
\lambda_{i}(t)
replaced by the
\lambda_{i}^{R}(t)
to exceed
M
is greater than any positive number almost surely via Markov inequality and Borel–Cantelli Lemma.

Remark 2
.

The unique solution to (
4
) is known as Dyson Brownian motion.

Remark 3
.

The process given in (
4
) with general
\beta\in(0,\infty)
is known as
\beta
-Dyson Brownian motion. By the same argument used in Step 2, one can show that (
4
) with general
\beta\in(0,\infty)
has a unique strong solution before the first collision time. Moreover, applying Itô formula to
U(t)
given in (
16
), we have

\displaystyle dU(t)=\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\beta N}}\sum_{i\neq j}\dfrac{dW_{i}}{\lambda_{i}^{N,\beta}(t)-\lambda_{j}^{N,\beta}(t)}+\left(1-\dfrac{1}{\beta}\right)\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i\neq j}\dfrac{dt}{\big(\lambda_{i}^{N,\beta}(t)-\lambda_{j}(t)^{N,\beta}\big)^{2}}.

Then the non-collision of the system of particles
\lambda_{1}^{N,\beta}(t)\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{N}^{N,\beta}(t)
follows from McKean’s argument for the case
\beta\geq 1
. It is well known that the
\beta
-Dyson Brownian motion has collisions for
\beta\in(0,1)
(see, e.g.,
[
24
, Remark 3]
).

Real symmetric matrix whose entries are i.i.d. Ornstein-Uhlenbeck processes (real symmetric matrix OU process) was considered in
[
12
]
. Let
X^{N}(t)
be a symmetric
N\times N
matrix-valued process that solves the following matrix SDE

\displaystyle dX^{N}(t)=\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{N}}\left(dB(t)+dB(t)^{\intercal}\right)-\dfrac{1}{2}X^{N}(t)dt,

(18)

where
B(t)
is a
N\times N
matrix Brownian motion. Then the entries
\{X^{N}_{i,j}(t)\}_{1\leq i\leq j\leq N}
are independent Ornstein-Uhlenbeck processes with invariant distribution
N(0,(1+\delta_{ij})/(2N))
. By Itô calculus and martingale theory,
[
12
]
derived the following system of SDEs for the eigenvalue processes
\{\lambda_{i}^{N}(t)\}_{1\leq i\leq N}
of
X^{N}(t)
in (
18
)

\displaystyle d\lambda_{i}^{N}(t)=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{N}}dB_{i}(t)+\left(-\dfrac{1}{2}\lambda_{i}^{N}(t)+\dfrac{1}{2N}\sum_{j:j\neq i}\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{i}^{N}(t)-\lambda_{j}^{N}(t)}\right)dt,~1\leq i\leq N.

(19)

By assuming the non-collision of the initial state
\lambda_{1}^{N}(0)>\cdots>\lambda_{N}^{N}(0)
, the non-collision of the eigenvalue processes was also established in
[
12
]
by an argument similar to the one used in the proof of Theorem
1
.

2.2
High-dimensional limits

Let
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
be the space of probability measures on
\mathbb{R}
equipped with the weak topology and corresponding metric
d_{\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})}
. For
T>0
, let
C([0,T],\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}))
be the space of continuous processes with values in
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
. Then the space
C([0,T],\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}))
endowed with the metric

\displaystyle d_{C([0,T],\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}))}\left(\mu^{(1)},\mu^{(2)}\right)=\sup_{t\in[0,T]}d_{\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})}\left(\mu^{(1)}(t),\mu^{(2)}(t)\right),

is complete. For a test function
f(x)
and a measure
\mu(dx)
on
\mathbb{R}
, we write

\langle f,\mu\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)\mu(dx).

Recall the definition of
X^{N,\beta}(t)
in Theorem
1
. Let
L_{N}^{\beta}(t)
be the empirical measure of the eigenvalue processes
\{\lambda_{i}^{N,\beta}(t)\}_{1\leq i\leq N}
of
X^{N,\beta}(t)
, that is

\displaystyle L_{N}^{\beta}(t)(dx)=\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\lambda_{i}^{N,\beta}(t)}(dx).

(20)

In connection with the theory of random matrices, it is of interest to investigate possible limits of these empirical measures
\{L_{N}^{\beta}(t),t\in[0,T]\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
when
N
grows to infinity.

Such high-dimensional limits are known in the literature only for some simple cases. An early result for eigenvalue empirical measure processes can be found in
[
12
]
: the exponential tightness of the sequence of corresponding eigenvalue empirical measure processes was established, which implies the almost sure convergence of the sequence. The equation satisfied by the limiting measure-valued process was also obtained, which is known as McKean-Vlasov equation. Moreover,
[
12
]
proved that the semi-circle law is the only equilibrium point of the equation (with finite moments of all orders).

The high-dimensional limit results were later generalized in
[
59
]
to the following system of symmetric matrix SDE

\displaystyle dX^{N}(t)=\sqrt{\dfrac{\alpha}{2N}}\left(dB(t)+dB(t)^{\intercal}\right)-\theta X^{N}(t)dt,

(21)

where
B(t)
is a
N\times N
matrix Brownian motion. Note that if we choose
\alpha=1
and
\theta=0
, then the
X^{N}(t)
in (
21
) is the real symmetric matrix Brownian motion appeared in Theorem
1
. The real symmetric matrix OU processes in (
18
) corresponds to the case
\alpha=1/2
and
\theta=1/2
. The eigenvalue processes
\{\lambda_{i}^{N}(t)\}_{1\leq i\leq N}
of
X^{N}(t)
in (
21
) are called the interacting Brownian particles in
[
59
]
and satisfy the following system of SDEs

\displaystyle d\lambda_{i}^{N}(t)=\sqrt{\dfrac{2\alpha}{N}}dB_{i}(t)+\left(-\theta\lambda_{i}^{N}(t)+\dfrac{\alpha}{N}\sum_{j:j\neq i}\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{i}^{N}(t)-\lambda_{j}^{N}(t)}\right)dt,\quad 1\leq i\leq N,~~t\geq 0.

(22)

In
[
59
]
, the non-collision and non-explosion of the particles (
22
) was established assuming initial state
\lambda_{1}^{N}(0)>\cdots>\lambda_{N}^{N}(0)
. Moreover,
[
59
, Theorem 1]
proved the weak convergence in law of the sequence of eigenvalue empirical measure processes by Itô calculus and a tightness argument that is similar to Theorem
3
. The equation that characterizes the limiting measure valued process was also derived.

The family of eigenvalue processes given in (
22
) was further generalized in
[
11
]
. More precisely, for some Lipschitz functions
b_{N}
,
\sigma_{N}
and positive constant
\gamma_{N}
,
[
11
]
proved that the following particle system

\displaystyle dx_{i}^{N}(t)=\sigma_{N}(x_{i}^{N}(t))dB_{i}(t)+\left(b_{N}(x_{i}^{N}(t))+\sum_{j:j\neq i}\dfrac{\gamma_{N}}{x_{i}^{N}(t)-x_{j}^{N}(t)}\right)dt,\quad 1\leq i\leq N,~~t\geq 0,

(23)

has a unique strong solution for all the time, even with collision. For the case
\gamma_{N}=2\gamma/N
,
[
11
, Theorem 4.2]
established the weak convergence in law of the sequence of eigenvalue empirical measure processes and derived the equation for all possible limits. In
[
11
, Theorem 5.1]
, the uniqueness of this equation was obtained if
b_{N}(x)
is linear and
\sigma_{N}(x)=\sigma_{N}>0
. The non-collision property was also established in
[
11
, Proposition 4.1]
under the assumptions that the particles are distinct at
t=0
,
b_{N}(x)
is linear, and
\sigma_{N}(x)=\sigma_{N}\in[0,\sqrt{2\gamma_{N}}]
. However, it is worth pointing out that the high-dimensional results does not require the non-collision of the particles.

Another generalization of the real symmetric matrix Brownian motion in Theorem
1
and real symmetric matrix OU process in (
18
) was introduced in
[
43
]
as the solution of the following matrix SDE

\displaystyle dX^{N}(t)=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2N}}\left(dB(t)+dB(t)^{\intercal}\right)-\dfrac{1}{2}V^{\prime}(X^{N}(t))dt,

(24)

whose ordered eigenvalue processes
\{\lambda_{i}^{N}(t)\}_{1\leq i\leq N}
satisfy

\displaystyle d\lambda_{i}^{N}(t)=\sqrt{\dfrac{2}{N}}dB_{i}(t)+\left(-\dfrac{1}{2}V^{\prime}\left(\lambda_{i}^{N}(t)\right)+\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{j:j\neq i}\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{i}^{N}(t)-\lambda_{j}^{N}(t)}\right)dt,\quad 1\leq i\leq N,~t\geq 0.

(25)

Here,
V
is an external potential functions in
C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
satisfying certain convexity conditions. The weak convergence in law of the sequence of eigenvalue empirical measure processes and the equation for the limiting process were obtained in
[
43
, Theorem 1.1]
.

For the real symmetric or complex Hermitian matrix Brownian motion
X^{N,\beta}
defined in Theorem
1
with null initial value
X^{N,\beta}(0)=0
, the high-dimensional limits was investigated in
[
9
]
by studying large deviation bounds. The exponential tightness of the sequence
\{L_{N}^{\beta}(t)\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
was established. In
[
9
, Corollary 1.2]
, the almost sure convergence of the sequence
\{L_{N}^{\beta}(t)\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
was obtained and the equation for the limit was derived. Moreover, the limit was proved to be the semi-circular law. The complex case was also studied in
[
57
, Proposition 3.1]
where the convergence in probability was obtained. We present
[
1
, Proposition 4.3.10]
below, where the high-dimensional limit of the sequence
\{L_{N}^{\beta}(t)\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
was recovered without assuming the null initial condition.

Theorem 2
.

Let
T>0
be a fixed number. Suppose that there exists a positive function
\varphi\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
with bounded first and second derivatives and satisfying
\lim_{|x|\to\infty}\varphi(x)=+\infty
, such that

\displaystyle C_{0}:=\sup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\langle\varphi,L_{N}^{\beta}(0)\rangle<\infty.

Assume that
L_{N}^{\beta}(0)
converges weakly as
N
tends to infinity towards a probability measure
\mu_{0}
.

Then the sequence
\{L_{N}^{\beta}(t),t\in[0,T]\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}

converges almost surely in
C([0,T],\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}))
. Its limit

\mu
is characterized by the following equation: for any
f\in C_{b}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
,

\displaystyle\langle f,\mu_{t}\rangle=\langle f,\mu_{0}\rangle+\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\dfrac{f^{\prime}(x)-f^{\prime}(y)}{x-y}\mu_{s}(dx)\mu_{s}(dy)ds,\quad\forall t\in[0,T].

(26)

Proof:
.

The idea of the proof comes from
[
1
, Proposition 4.3.10]
(see also
[
65
]
). We divide the proof into four steps.

Step 1: (Computation of
\langle f,L_{N}^{\beta}(t)\rangle
by Itô calculus.)
By the definition (
20
) of
L_{N}^{\beta}(t)
, for
f\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
,

\displaystyle\langle f,L_{N}^{\beta}(t)\rangle=\int f(x)L_{N}^{\beta}(t)(dx)=\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int f(x)\delta_{\lambda_{i}^{N,\beta}(t)}(dx)=\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}f(\lambda_{i}^{N,\beta}(t)).

By Itô’s formula and (
4
),

\displaystyle f(\lambda_{i}^{N,\beta}(t))

\displaystyle=f(\lambda_{i}^{N,\beta}(0))+\int_{0}^{t}f^{\prime}(\lambda_{i}^{N,\beta}(s))d\lambda_{i}^{N,\beta}(s)+\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}f^{\prime\prime}(\lambda_{i}^{N,\beta}(s))d\langle\lambda_{i}^{N,\beta}\rangle_{s}

\displaystyle=f(\lambda_{i}^{N,\beta}(0))+\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\beta N}}\int_{0}^{t}f^{\prime}(\lambda_{i}^{N,\beta}(s))dW_{i}(s)+\dfrac{1}{\beta N}\int_{0}^{t}f^{\prime\prime}(\lambda_{i}^{N,\beta}(s))ds

\displaystyle\qquad+\dfrac{1}{N}\int_{0}^{t}f^{\prime}(\lambda_{i}^{N,\beta}(s))\sum_{j:j\neq i}\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{i}^{N,\beta}(s)-\lambda_{j}^{N,\beta}(s)}ds.

Thus, using the convention
\frac{f^{\prime}(x)-f^{\prime}(y)}{x-y}=f^{\prime\prime}(x)
on
\{x=y\}
, we have

\displaystyle\langle f,L_{N}^{\beta}(t)\rangle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}f(\lambda_{i}^{N,\beta}(0))+\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\beta N^{3}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{t}f^{\prime}(\lambda_{i}^{N,\beta}(s))dW_{i}(s)+\dfrac{1}{\beta N^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{t}f^{\prime\prime}(\lambda_{i}^{N,\beta}(s))ds

\displaystyle+\dfrac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i\neq j}\int_{0}^{t}\dfrac{f^{\prime}(\lambda_{i}^{N,\beta}(s))}{\lambda_{i}^{N,\beta}(s)-\lambda_{j}^{N,\beta}(s)}ds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}f(\lambda_{i}^{N,\beta}(0))+\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\beta N^{3}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{t}f^{\prime}(\lambda_{i}^{N,\beta}(s))dW_{i}(s)+\dfrac{1}{\beta N^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{t}f^{\prime\prime}(\lambda_{i}^{N,\beta}(s))ds

\displaystyle+\dfrac{1}{2N^{2}}\sum_{i\neq j}\int_{0}^{t}\dfrac{f^{\prime}(\lambda_{i}^{N,\beta}(s))-f^{\prime}(\lambda_{j}^{N,\beta}(s))}{\lambda_{i}^{N,\beta}(s)-\lambda_{j}^{N,\beta}(s)}ds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}f(\lambda_{i}^{N,\beta}(0))+\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\beta N^{3}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{t}f^{\prime}(\lambda_{i}^{N,\beta}(s))dW_{i}(s)

\displaystyle+\left(\dfrac{1}{\beta}-\dfrac{1}{2}\right)\dfrac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{t}f^{\prime\prime}(\lambda_{i}^{N,\beta}(s))ds+\dfrac{1}{2N^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{t}\dfrac{f^{\prime}(\lambda_{i}^{N,\beta}(s))-f^{\prime}(\lambda_{j}^{N,\beta}(s))}{\lambda_{i}^{N,\beta}(s)-\lambda_{j}^{N,\beta}(s)}ds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle f,L_{N}^{\beta}(0)\rangle+\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\beta N^{3}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{t}f^{\prime}(\lambda_{i}^{N,\beta}(s))dW_{i}(s)+\left(\dfrac{1}{\beta}-\dfrac{1}{2}\right)\dfrac{1}{N}\int_{0}^{t}\langle f^{\prime\prime},L_{N}^{\beta}(s)\rangle ds

\displaystyle+\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\dfrac{f^{\prime}(x)-f^{\prime}(y)}{x-y}L_{N}^{\beta}(s)(dx)L_{N}^{\beta}(s)(dy)ds.

(27)

Step 2: We prove that the sequence
\{L_{N}^{\beta}(t),t\in[0,T]\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
is almost surely relatively compact in
C([0,T],\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}))
, that is, every subsequence has a further subsequence that converges in
C([0,T],\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}))
almost surely, following the argument
[
1
, Lemma 4.3.13]
.

Note that for
f\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
with bounded first and second derivatives, by mean value theorem, one can show
|\frac{f^{\prime}(x)-f^{\prime}(y)}{x-y}|\leq\|f^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}}
. Hence, by (
2.2
),

\displaystyle|\langle f,L_{N}^{\beta}(t)\rangle-\langle f,L_{N}^{\beta}(s)\rangle|\leq~

\displaystyle\left|\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\beta N^{3}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{s}^{t}f^{\prime}(\lambda_{i}^{N,\beta}(r))dW_{i}(r)\right|+\left(\dfrac{1}{\beta}-\dfrac{1}{2}\right)\dfrac{1}{N}\left|\int_{s}^{t}\langle f^{\prime\prime},L_{N}^{\beta}(r)\rangle dr\right|

\displaystyle~+\dfrac{1}{2}\left|\int_{s}^{t}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\dfrac{f^{\prime}(x)-f^{\prime}(y)}{x-y}L_{N}^{\beta}(r)(dx)L_{N}^{\beta}(r)(dy)dr\right|

\displaystyle\leq~

\displaystyle\left|\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\beta N^{3}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{s}^{t}f^{\prime}(\lambda_{i}^{N,\beta}(r))dW_{i}(r)\right|+\left(\dfrac{1}{2}+\dfrac{1}{\beta N}-\dfrac{1}{2N}\right)\|f^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}}|t-s|.

(28)

Note that
[0,T]
can be partitioned into small intervals of length
\eta<\|f^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{-8/7}
and the number of the intervals is
J=[T\eta^{-1}]
. Then by Markov inequality and Burkholder-Davis-Gundy inequality, we have, for
M>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{|t-s|\leq\eta}\left|\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\beta N^{3}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{s}^{t}f^{\prime}(\lambda_{i}^{N,\beta}(r))dW_{i}(r)\right|\geq M\eta^{1/8}\right)\leq\sum_{k=0}^{J-1}\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{k\eta\leq t\leq(k+1)\eta}\left|\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\beta N^{3}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{k\eta}^{t}f^{\prime}(\lambda_{i}^{N,\beta}(r))dW_{i}(r)\right|\geq\dfrac{M\eta^{1/8}}{3}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{J-1}\dfrac{81}{M^{4}\eta^{1/2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{k\eta\leq t\leq(k+1)\eta}\left|\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\beta N^{3}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{k\eta}^{t}f^{\prime}(\lambda_{i}^{N,\beta}(r))dW_{i}(r)\right|^{4}\right]\leq\sum_{k=0}^{J-1}\dfrac{324\Lambda_{2}}{M^{4}\eta^{1/2}\beta^{2}N^{6}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{k\eta}^{k\eta+\cdot}f^{\prime}(\lambda_{i}^{N,\beta}(r))dW_{i}(r)\right\rangle_{\eta}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{J-1}\dfrac{324\Lambda_{2}}{M^{4}\eta^{1/2}\beta^{2}N^{6}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{k\eta}^{(k+1)\eta}\left|f^{\prime}(\lambda_{i}^{N,\beta}(r))\right|^{2}dr\right)^{2}\right]\leq\dfrac{324\Lambda_{2}J\eta^{3/2}}{M^{4}\beta^{2}N^{4}}\|f^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{4}\leq\dfrac{324\Lambda_{2}T\eta^{1/2}}{M^{4}\beta^{2}N^{4}}\|f^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{4}.

(29)

Hence, noting that
\beta\in\{1,2\}
and
\eta^{1/8}>\eta\|f^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}}
, by (
2.2
) and (
2.2
), for
M>0
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{|t-s|\leq\eta}|\langle f,L_{N}^{\beta}(t)\rangle-\langle f,L_{N}^{\beta}(s)\rangle|\geq(M+1)\eta^{1/8}\right)

\displaystyle\leq~

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{|t-s|\leq\eta}\left|\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\beta N^{3}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{s}^{t}f^{\prime}(\lambda_{i}^{N,\beta}(r))dW_{i}(r)\right|\geq(M+1)\eta^{1/8}-\dfrac{\eta\|f^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq~

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{|t-s|\leq\eta}\left|\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\beta N^{3}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{s}^{t}f^{\prime}(\lambda_{i}^{N,\beta}(r))dW_{i}(r)\right|\geq M\eta^{1/8}\right)\leq\dfrac{324\Lambda_{2}T\eta^{1/2}}{M^{4}N^{4}}\|f^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{4}.

(30)

Let
\{\tilde{f}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be a family of
C_{b}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
functions that is dense in
C_{0}(\mathbb{R})
. Choose
\varepsilon_{k}=\left(1+k\|\tilde{f}_{k}^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}}\right)^{-1}
and define

\displaystyle C_{T}(\tilde{f}_{k},\varepsilon_{k})

\displaystyle=\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}\left\{\mu\in C([0,T],\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})):\sup_{|t-s|\leq n^{-4}}\left|\langle\tilde{f}_{k},\mu_{t}\rangle-\langle\tilde{f}_{k},\mu_{s}\rangle\right|\leq\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon_{k}\sqrt{n}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{\mu\in C([0,T],\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})):\sup_{|t-s|\leq n^{-4}}\left|\langle\tilde{f}_{k},\mu_{t}\rangle-\langle\tilde{f}_{k},\mu_{s}\rangle\right|\leq\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon_{k}\sqrt{n}},\forall n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{\mu\in C([0,T],\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})):t\rightarrow\langle\tilde{f}_{k},\mu_{t}\rangle\in C_{\left\|\tilde{f}_{k}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}}(\{(\varepsilon_{k}\sqrt{n})^{-1}\},\{n^{-4}\})\right\},

where the set

C_{M}(\{(\varepsilon_{k}\sqrt{n})^{-1}\},\{n^{-4}\})=\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}\left\{g\in C([0,T],\mathbb{R}):\sup_{|t-s|\leq n^{-4}}|g(t)-g(s)|\leq(\varepsilon_{k}\sqrt{n})^{-1},\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|g(t)|\leq M\right\},

is (sequentially) compact in
C([0,T],\mathbb{R})
according to Arzela-Ascoli Lemma. By (
2.2
),

\displaystyle\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}\sum_{k\geq 1}\mathbb{P}(L_{N}^{\beta}\notin C_{T}(\tilde{f}_{k},\varepsilon_{k}))\leq\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}\sum_{k\geq 1}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{|t-s|\leq n^{-4}}\left|\langle\tilde{f}_{k},L_{N}^{\beta}(t)\rangle-\langle\tilde{f}_{k},L_{N}^{\beta}(s)\rangle\right|>\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon_{k}\sqrt{n}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq~

\displaystyle\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}\sum_{k\geq 1}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{324\Lambda_{2}T}{(\varepsilon_{k}^{-1}-1)^{4}n^{2}N^{4}}\|\tilde{f}_{k}^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{4}=324\Lambda_{2}T\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{-2}\sum_{k\geq 1}\dfrac{\|\tilde{f}_{k}^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{4}}{(\varepsilon_{k}^{-1}-1)^{4}}\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{1}{N^{4}}

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle 324\Lambda_{2}T\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{-2}\sum_{k\geq 1}k^{-4}\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{1}{N^{4}}<\infty,

(31)

Since the function
\varphi
is positive and tends to infinity as
|x|\rightarrow+\infty
, the set

\displaystyle K(\varphi)=\left\{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}):\langle\varphi,\mu\rangle\leq 1+C_{0}+T\left\|\varphi^{\prime\prime}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\right\}

is tight, i.e., it is (sequentially) compact in
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
. By (
2.2
) for
f=\varphi
and
s=0
, Markov inequality and Burkholder-Davis-Gundy inequality, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\exists t\in[0,T],\ \mathrm{s.t.}\ L_{N}^{\beta}(t)\notin K(\varphi)\right)=\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\langle\varphi,L_{N}(t)\rangle>1+C_{0}+T\left\|\varphi^{\prime\prime}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq~

\displaystyle\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\beta N^{3}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{t}\varphi^{\prime}(\lambda_{i}^{N,\beta}(r))dW_{i}(r)\right|>1\right)\leq\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\beta N^{3}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{t}\varphi^{\prime}(\lambda_{i}^{N,\beta}(r))dW_{i}(r)\right|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq~

\displaystyle\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}\Lambda_{1}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\beta N^{3}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{\cdot}\varphi^{\prime}(\lambda_{i}^{N,\beta}(r))dW_{i}(r)\right\rangle_{T}\right]=\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{2\Lambda_{1}}{\beta N^{3}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{T}\left(\varphi^{\prime}(\lambda_{i}^{N,\beta}(r))\right)^{2}dr\right]

\displaystyle\leq~

\displaystyle\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{2\Lambda_{1}T\left\|\varphi^{\prime}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}}{\beta N^{2}}<\infty.

(32)

By
[
1
, Lemma 4.3.13]
(see also
[
64
, Lemma B.4]
), the set

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=\Bigl\{\mu\in C([0,T],\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})):\mu_{t}\in K(\varphi),\ \forall t\in[0,T]\Bigr\}\cap\bigcap_{k\geq 1}C_{T}(\tilde{f}_{k},\varepsilon_{k})

is compact in
C([0,T],\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}))
. By (
2.2
) and (
2.2
), we have

\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(L_{N}^{\beta}\notin\mathcal{H})\leq\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(\exists t\in[0,T],\ \mathrm{s.t.}\ L_{N}^{\beta}(t)\notin K(\varphi))+\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}\sum_{k\geq 1}\mathbb{P}(L_{N}^{\beta}\notin C_{T}(\tilde{f}_{k},\varepsilon_{k}))<\infty.

Therefore, the Borel-Cantelli Lemma implies that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\liminf_{N\rightarrow\infty}\{L_{N}^{\beta}\in\mathcal{H}\}\right)=1
.

Finally, the relative compactness of the family
\{L_{N}^{\beta}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
follows from the compactness of
\mathcal{H}
.

Step 3: We derive the equation (

) for any limit point
\mu
of the sequence

.

Let

be a subsequence such that

converges to

, by Markov inequality and Burkholder-Davis-Gundy inequality, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\beta N_{k}^{3}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}\int_{0}^{t}f^{\prime}(\lambda_{i}^{N_{k},\beta}(r))dW_{i}(r)\right|\geq\epsilon\right)\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\epsilon^{-2}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\beta N_{k}^{3}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}\int_{0}^{t}f^{\prime}(\lambda_{i}^{N_{k},\beta}(r))dW_{i}(r)\right|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq~

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{\Lambda_{1}}{\epsilon^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\beta N_{k}^{3}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}\int_{0}^{\cdot}f^{\prime}(\lambda_{i}^{N_{k},\beta}(r))dW_{i}(r)\right\rangle_{T}\right]=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{2\Lambda_{1}}{\epsilon^{2}\beta N_{k}^{3}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}\int_{0}^{T}\left(f^{\prime}(\lambda_{i}^{N_{k},\beta}(r))\right)^{2}dr\right]\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{2\Lambda_{1}T}{\epsilon^{2}\beta N_{k}^{2}}\left\|f^{\prime}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2},

which is finite since
N_{k}\geq k
. By Borel–Cantelli Lemma,

\displaystyle\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\beta N_{k}^{3}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}\int_{0}^{t}f^{\prime}(\lambda_{i}^{N_{k},\beta}(r))dW_{i}(r)\to 0,\quad k\to\infty,

(33)

uniformly with respect to
t
almost surely. Moreover, the boundedness of

yields

\displaystyle\left(\dfrac{1}{\beta}-\dfrac{1}{2}\right)\dfrac{1}{N_{k}}\int_{0}^{t}\langle f^{\prime\prime},L_{N_{k}}^{\beta}(s)\rangle ds,\to 0,\quad k\to\infty,

(34)

uniformly with respect to
t
almost surely. Therefore, by considering the subsequence
\{N_{k}\}
in (
2.2
), and using (
33
) and (

), we have

\displaystyle\langle f,\mu_{t}\rangle=\langle f,\mu_{0}\rangle+\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\dfrac{f^{\prime}(x)-f^{\prime}(y)}{x-y}\mu_{s}(dx)\mu_{s}(dy)ds.

To prove the uniqueness, it is convenient to choose

for

Recall the matrix Brownian motion

in Definition

, where

is the equality in distribution. Thus, we have the scaling property of the Stieltjes transform

\displaystyle G_{t}(z)=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{t}}G_{1}\left(\dfrac{z}{\sqrt{t}}\right).

Hence,

\displaystyle G_{t}(z)\partial_{z}G_{t}(z)=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{t}^{3}}G_{1}\left(\dfrac{z}{\sqrt{t}}\right)G_{1}^{\prime}\left(\dfrac{z}{\sqrt{t}}\right)=-\dfrac{1}{z}\dfrac{d}{dt}\left(G_{1}^{2}\left(\dfrac{z}{\sqrt{t}}\right)\right).

Letting

\displaystyle G_{t}(z)=

\displaystyle G_{0}(z)-\int_{0}^{t}G_{s}(z)\partial_{z}G_{s}(z)ds=G_{0}(z)+\dfrac{1}{z}G_{1}^{2}\left(\dfrac{z}{\sqrt{s}}\right)\Bigg|_{s=0}^{s=t}=G_{0}(z)+\dfrac{1}{z}G_{1}^{2}\left(\dfrac{z}{\sqrt{t}}\right),

of which the solution is

\displaystyle G_{1}(z)=\dfrac{z-\sqrt{z^{2}-4}}{2}.

(37)

Note that by definition,

\displaystyle\Im\left(G_{t}(z)\right)=\Im\left(\int\dfrac{1}{z-x}\mu_{t}(dx)\right)=\Im\left(\int\dfrac{\bar{z}-x}{|z-x|^{2}}\mu_{t}(dx)\right)=-\Im(z)\int\dfrac{1}{|z-x|^{2}}\mu_{t}(dx).

Lastly, (

In some literature (see for example

Theorem 3

(
[
57
]
, Proposition 3.1)
.

Assume that all the conditions in Theorem
2
hold. Then the sequence
\{L_{N}^{\beta}(t),t\in[0,T]\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
converges in probability in

Proof:
.

The equation (
26
) can be derived as in Theorem
2
by using Itô calculus and martingale theory. We only prove the convergence in law of the sequence
\{L_{N}^{\beta}(t),t\in[0,T]\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\left\langle f,L_{N}^{\beta}(t)\right\rangle-\left\langle f,L_{N}^{\beta}(s)\right\rangle\right|^{1+a}\right]\leq C_{f,T}|t-s|^{1+b},~\forall t,s\in[0,T],~\forall N\in\mathbb{N},

(39)

for some positive constants

with bounded first and second derivatives. Here

is a positive constant depending only on

and

. Then the tightness of the sequence
\{L_{N}^{\beta}(t),t\in[0,T]\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
in
C([0,T],\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}))
follows from (
39
) and
[
64
, Proposition B.3]
.

To establish (
39
), one may first obtain (
2.2
) by Itô calculus and then apply the Burkholder-Davis-Gundy inequality to get an upper bound for some even moment of the martingale term. In the following, we provide another approach to get (

), where the pathwise Hölder continuity of the matrix entries is used. This idea can also be found in
[

56

It is well known that almost all the paths of Brownian motion are

(1/2-\varepsilon)
-Hölder continuous for any

\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)
, and so are the paths of the entries in

X^{N,\beta}
. Consider the Hölder norm of the matrix entries

X_{i,j}^{N,\beta}(t)
, for

\displaystyle\left\|X_{i,j}^{N,\beta}\right\|_{0,T;1/2-\varepsilon}=\sup_{0\leq s<t\leq T}\dfrac{\left|X_{i,j}^{N,\beta}(t)-X_{i,j}^{N,\beta}(s)\right|}{|t-s|^{1/2-\varepsilon}}.

By the Fernique Theorem (
[
17
]
), we have the following estimation

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\alpha N\left\|X_{i,j}^{N,\beta}\right\|_{0,T;1/2-\varepsilon}^{2}\right)\right]<\infty,

(40)

where
\alpha=\alpha(\varepsilon,T)
is a positive constant depending on
(\varepsilon,T)
and

By mean value theorem and Hoffman-Wielandt inequality (
[
1

\displaystyle\left|\left\langle f,L_{N}^{\beta}(t)\right\rangle-\left\langle f,L_{N}^{\beta}(s)\right\rangle\right|^{2}=\left|\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(f\left(\lambda_{i}^{N,\beta}(t)\right)-f\left(\lambda_{i}^{N,\beta}(s)\right)\right)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq~

\displaystyle\dfrac{\|f^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left|\lambda_{i}^{N,\beta}(t)-\lambda_{i}^{N,\beta}(s)\right|^{2}\leq\dfrac{\|f^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}}{N}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\left|X^{N,\beta}(t)-X^{N,\beta}(s)\right|^{2}.

(41)

This together with Minkowski inequality, the pathwise Hölder continuity of
X_{i,j}^{N,\beta}
and Cauchy-Schwarz inequality yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\left\langle f,L_{N}^{\beta}(t)\right\rangle-\left\langle f,L_{N}^{\beta}(s)\right\rangle\right|^{4}\right]\leq\dfrac{\|f^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{4}}{N^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\left|X^{N,\beta}(t)-X^{N,\beta}(s)\right|^{2}\right)^{2}\right]\leq\dfrac{\|f^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{4}}{N^{2}}\left(\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\left|X^{N,\beta}(t)-X^{N,\beta}(s)\right|^{4}\right]\right)^{1/2}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq~

\displaystyle\dfrac{\|f^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{4}}{N^{2}}\left(\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|X_{i,j}^{N,\beta}\right\|_{0,T;1/2-\varepsilon}^{4}|t-s|^{2-4\varepsilon}\right]\right)^{1/2}\right)^{2}=\dfrac{\|f^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{4}|t-s|^{2-4\varepsilon}}{N^{4}}\left(\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[N^{2}\left\|X_{i,j}^{N,\beta}\right\|_{0,T;1/2-\varepsilon}^{4}\right]\right)^{1/2}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\dfrac{\|f^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{4}|t-s|^{2-4\varepsilon}}{N^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[N^{2}\left\|X_{i,j}^{N,\beta}\right\|_{0,T;1/2-\varepsilon}^{4}\right].

(42)

Recall that the matrix
X^{N,\beta}
is symmetric for
\beta=1
and Hermitian for
\beta=2
. Also note that the upper-diagonal entries are i.i.d., as well as the diagonal entries. Thus,

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[N^{2}\left\|X_{i,j}^{N,\beta}\right\|_{0,T;1/2-\varepsilon}^{4}\right]\leq~

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[N^{2}\left\|X_{1,1}^{N,\beta}\right\|_{0,T;1/2-\varepsilon}^{4}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[N^{2}\left\|X_{1,2}^{N,\beta}\right\|_{0,T;1/2-\varepsilon}^{4}\right]

\displaystyle\leq~

\displaystyle\dfrac{2}{\alpha^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\alpha N\left\|X_{1,1}^{N,\beta}\right\|_{0,T;1/2-\varepsilon}^{2}\right)\right]+\dfrac{2}{\alpha^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\alpha N\left\|X_{1,2}^{N,\beta}\right\|_{0,T;1/2-\varepsilon}^{2}\right)\right],

(43)

where we use the inequality
x^{2}/2\leq e^{x}
for

. Therefore, by (

), (
2.2
) and (

) with

and

3
Positive-definite symmetric matrix valued processes

3.1
Brownian motions of ellipsoids

The study of stochastic processes with values in the space of positive-definite symmetric matrices, or the space of ellipsoids, can be dated back to at least
[
15
]
, where a class of Markov processes were studied by using differential geometry. Later, the Brownian motions of ellipsoids were considered in
[
50
]
, and some of the results in
[
15
,
54
]
were recovered without using differential geometry.

Let
B(t)
be a
N\times N
matrix whose entries are i.i.d. standard Brownian motions (matrix Brownian motion). Let
G^{N}(t)
be a process on the group of invertible
N\times N
matrices that solves the following matrix SDE

\displaystyle dG^{N}(t)=dB(t)\circ G^{N}(t).

Then the process
\{G^{N}(t+u)G^{N}(u)^{-1}:t\geq 0\}
is identical in law to the process
G^{N}(t)
and is independent of the process
\{G^{N}(r):r\in[0,u]\}
for all
u>0
. The process
G^{N}(t)
is known as the right-invariant Brownian motion. Let
X^{N}(t)=G^{N}(t)G^{N}(t)^{\intercal}
and
Y^{N}(t)=G^{N}(t)^{\intercal}G^{N}(t)
, which are both Markov processes on the space of ellipsoids. The process
Y^{N}(t)
is known as Dynkin’s Brownian motion.

Suppose that
G^{N}(0)
is chosen such that
X^{N}(0)
has distinct eigenvalues.

Theorem 5

(
[
50
]
, Theorem A)
.

The eigenvalue processes of
X^{N}(t)
never collide and never hit
0
for all
t>0
almost surely. The ordered eigenvalue processes
\lambda_{1}^{N}(t)>\cdots>\lambda_{N}^{N}(t)(>0)
satisfy the following system of SDEs

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2}d\left(\ln\lambda_{i}^{N}(t)\right)=dW_{i}(t)+\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{j:j\neq i}\dfrac{\lambda_{i}^{N}(t)+\lambda_{j}^{N}(t)}{\lambda_{i}^{N}(t)-\lambda_{j}^{N}(t)}dt,

(44)

where
\{W_{1}(t),\ldots,W_{N}(t)\}
are independent standard Brownian motions. Moreover,

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\dfrac{\ln\lambda_{i}^{N}(t)}{t}=N+1-2i.

The system of SDEs for eigenvalue processes was derived in
[
50
]
by using Itô calculus and martingale theory as in Theorem
1
. The almost sure non-collision of the eigenvalue processes was proved by the theorem of time-change for local martingales (see
[
33
, Chapter 3, Theorem 4.6]
), which is of the same spirit as the McKean’s argument (Lemma
1
). The long time behavior of the eigenvalue processes was studied by constructing auxiliary processes with a comparison result.

The eigenvector processes of
X^{N}(t)
and
Y^{N}(t)
were also investigated in
[
50
]
by Itô calculus and their behavior is very different. The eigenvector matrix of
X^{N}(t)
ultimately behaves like Brownian motion on
O(N)
, while that of
Y^{N}(t)
converges to a limiting value.

3.2
Wishart processes

Wishart process was introduced in
[
6
]
to perform principal component analysis on a set of resistance data of Escherichia Coli to certain antibiotics. Let
B(t)
be a
N\times p
matrix whose entries are i.i.d. standard real Brownian motions (matrix Brownian motion). The
N\times N
symmetric matrix
X^{N}(t)=(B(t)+A)(B(t)+A)^{\intercal}
, where
A
is a
N\times p
real deterministic matrix, is the Wishart process. By
[
7
]
, the Wishart process
X^{N}(t)
solves the following matrix SDE

\displaystyle dX^{N}(t)=\sqrt{X^{N}(t)}dW(t)+dW(t)^{\intercal}\sqrt{X^{N}(t)}+pI_{N}dt,

(45)

where
W(t)
is a
N\times N
matrix Brownian motion. The ordered eigenvalue processes
\lambda_{1}^{N}(t)\geq\lambda_{2}^{N}(t)\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{N}^{N}(t)
of
X^{N}(t)
was studied first in
[
6
]
.

Theorem 6

(
[
6
]
, Theorem 1)
.

Assume that
X^{N}(0)
has
N
distinct eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}^{N}(0)>\lambda_{2}^{N}(0)>\cdots>\lambda_{N}^{N}(0)
. Denote the first collision time of the eigenvalue processes by

\displaystyle\tau_{N}=\inf\left\{t>0:\exists\ i\neq j,~\lambda_{i}^{N}(t)=\lambda_{j}^{N}(t)\right\}.

Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\tau_{N}=+\infty\right)=1.

Furthermore, the ordered eigenvalue processes
\lambda_{1}^{N}(t),\cdots,\lambda_{N}^{N}(t)
of
X^{N}(t)
satisfy the following system of SDEs

\displaystyle d\lambda_{i}^{N}(t)=2\sqrt{\lambda_{i}^{N}(t)}dW_{i}(t)+\left(p+\sum_{j:j\neq i}\dfrac{\lambda_{i}^{N}(t)+\lambda_{j}^{N}(t)}{\lambda_{i}^{N}(t)-\lambda_{j}^{N}(t)}\right)dt,~i\in\{1,\ldots,N\},

(46)

where
\{W_{1}(t),\ldots,W_{N}(t)\}
are independent standard Brownian motions.

Theorem
6
can be proved following the idea of the proof of Theorem
1
. Similarly, the system of SDEs for eigenvalue processes can be derived by Itô calculus and martingale theory. The almost sure non-collision of the eigenvalue processes can also be proved by the McKean’s argument.

The eigenvector processes were also studied in
[
6
]
by Itô calculus. Under the same assumption as in Theorem
6
, with an appropriate choice of unit eigenvalue vector processes, the system of SDEs for them were established in
[
6
, Theorem 2]
.

The assumption that the eigenvalues of
X^{N}(0)
are distinct in Theorem
6
automatically implies that
p\geq N-1
. For the case
p<N
, by
[
7
, Corollary 1]
, Theorem
6
is still valid for the set of non-trivial eigenvalue processes
\lambda_{1}^{N}(t),\cdots,\lambda_{p}^{N}(t)
. Note that the Wishart processes
X^{N}(t)
is positive semi-definite, and is degenerate when
p<N
for all
t
. In some situations, it interesting to know whether the Wishart processes is non-degenerated, which is equivalent to know the infiniteness of the hitting time of the least eigenvalue processes on
0
. By using the McKean’s argument,
[
7
, Proposition 1]
proved that
\lambda_{N}^{N}(t)>0
for all
t
almost surely for the case
p>N
. For the critical case,
p=N
, the set of hitting time on
0
(
\{t:\lambda_{N}^{N}(t)=0\}
) has zero Lebesgue measure almost surely.
[
7
]
also considered the matrix model (
45
) whenever
p>0
is not an integer, and proved that the conclusion of Theorem
6
holds for the unique solution to (
45
) for
p>N-1
(
[
7
, Theorem 2]
).

Let
Y^{N}(t)
be the complex analogue of
X^{N}(t)
, that is,
Y^{N}(t)=(\tilde{B}(t)+\tilde{A})(\tilde{B}(t)+\tilde{A})^{\intercal}
, where
\tilde{B}(t)
is a
N\times p
complex matrix whose real and imaginary parts are independent matrix Brownian motions, and
\tilde{A}
is a
N\times p
complex deterministic matrix. Then
Y^{N}(t)
is known as Laguerre process (
[
41
]
). With minor modification to the Wishart case (Theorem
6
), the non-collision property of the eigenvalue processes can be established and the following system of SDEs for eigenvalue processes can be obtained

\displaystyle d\lambda_{i}^{N}(t)=2\sqrt{\lambda_{i}^{N}(t)}dW_{i}(t)+2\left(p+\sum_{j:j\neq i}\dfrac{\lambda_{i}^{N}(t)+\lambda_{j}^{N}(t)}{\lambda_{i}^{N}(t)-\lambda_{j}^{N}(t)}\right)dt,~i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}.

(47)

See for example
[
58
, (2)]
,
[
37
, (1.2)]
.

The eigenvalue processes (
47
) were treated as particle system in
[
41
]
, and they were proved to evolve like
N
independent squared Bessel processes of dimension
2(p-N+1)
conditioned to no collision among each other, assuming
p\geq N
. For more properties of particle systems related to Brownian motions, we refer to
[
35
,
36
,
37
]
.

The high-dimensional limits of the normalized eigenvalue processes
\{\lambda_{i}^{N}(t)/N\}_{1\leq i\leq N}
of (
47
) was studied in
[
9
]
by proving large deviation bounds. Denote the empirical measure process by

\displaystyle L_{N}(t)(dx)=\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\lambda_{i}^{N}(t)/N}(dx).

The almost sure weak convergence of the sequence
\{L_{N}(t)\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
as well as the differential equation satisfied by the limiting measure-valued processes was established in
[
9
, Corollary 3.1]
, assuming that
p/N
converges to a positive number
c
. Moreover, the limit
\mu
is the well-known Marčenko-Pastur law (free Poisson distribution). The results were recovered in
[
58
, Theorem 3.3]
.

For
p\in\mathbb{N}
, the
p
-th moment of the sequence
\{L_{N}(t),t\in[0,T]\}
of normalized Laguerre process was considered in
[
58
]
. By using Itô calculus and martingale theory,
[
58
]
established a recursive formula for the sequence
\{\langle x^{p},L_{N}(t)\rangle:t\geq 0\}_{p\in\mathbb{N}}
for
N\in\mathbb{N}
, and proved that as

,
\langle x^{p},L_{N}(t)\rangle
converges to
\langle x^{p},\mu_{t}\rangle
uniformly in

almost surely and in

with
q\geq 1
for
p\in\mathbb{N}
. Moreover,
[
57
]
also investigated the largest eigenvalue processes
\lambda_{1}^{N}(t)
and the least eigenvalue processes
\lambda_{N}^{N}(t)
, and showed that

\displaystyle\max_{t\in[0,T]}\lambda_{1}^{N}(t)\to(1+\sqrt{c})^{2}\sqrt{T},~\min_{t\in[0,T]}\lambda_{N}^{N}(t)\to(1-\sqrt{c})^{2}\sqrt{T},~\text{as}~N\to\infty,

almost surely, where

.

The fluctuation of the sequence
\{L_{N}(t),t\in[0,T]\}
around its limit
\mu
has also been studied. Denote the random fluctuation

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{N}(f)(t)=N\left(\left\langle f,L_{N}(t)\right\rangle-\left\langle f,\mu_{t}\right\rangle\right)

for an appropriate test function
f
.
[
8
, Theorem 2.5]
established the CLT for a class of polynomial functions with null initial condition. Note that for monomials
f(x)=x^{p}
, the random fluctuation
\mathcal{L}_{N}(x^{p})(t)
is the fluctuation of the moment processes
\langle x^{p},L_{N}(t)\rangle
around the corresponding moment of the limit measure. By martingale theory,
[
57
, Theorem 4.3]
proved the convergence in distribution of
\mathcal{L}_{N}(x^{p})(t)
to a centred Gaussian process
\mathcal{L}(x^{p})(t)
whose distribution is characterized by recursive formulas.

The Wishart process (

) was generalized in
[

]
to a symmetric matrix valued process that solves the following matrix SDE

\displaystyle dX^{N}(t)=\sqrt{\left|X^{N}(t)\right|}dW(t)+dW(t)^{\intercal}\sqrt{\left|X^{N}(t)\right|}+pI_{N}dt.

(48)

Its ordered eigenvalue processes

satisfy the following system of SDEs

\displaystyle d\lambda_{i}^{N}(t)=2\sqrt{\left|\lambda_{i}^{N}(t)\right|}dW_{i}(t)+\left(p+\sum_{j:j\neq i}\dfrac{\left|\lambda_{i}^{N}(t)\right|+\left|\lambda_{j}^{N}(t)\right|}{\lambda_{i}^{N}(t)-\lambda_{j}^{N}(t)}\right)dt,~i\in\{1,\ldots,N\},

(49)

which is known as squared Bessel particle system.
[

, Theorem 1]
proved the existence and uniqueness of the non-colliding strong solution for all

. The conditions for the uniqueness of the strong solution were given in
[
26
, Theorem 2]

[
26
, Theorem 3]
.

4
Other matrix models and related particle systems driven by Brownian motion

In
[
7
]
, M.-F. Bru generalized her Wishart process to the following symmetric matrix valued process.

Theorem 7

(
[
7
]
, Theorem 2’)
.

Let

be a symmetric non-negative definite deterministic
N\times N
matrix with distinct eigenvalues. Let

\displaystyle dX^{N}(t)=\gamma\left(\sqrt{X^{N}(t)}dW(t)+dW(t)^{\intercal}\sqrt{X^{N}(t)}\right)+2\beta X^{N}(t)dt+p\gamma^{2}I_{N}dt,

(50)

has a unique weak solution in the set of symmetric
N\times N
matrices if

, and has a unique strong solution that is symmetric positive-definite if

The ordered eigenvalue processes
\lambda_{1}^{N}(t)\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{N}^{N}(t)
of the unique solution never collide almost surely, and satisfy the following system of SDEs

\displaystyle d\lambda_{i}^{N}(t)=2\gamma\sqrt{\lambda_{i}^{N}(t)}dW_{i}(t)+\left(p\gamma^{2}+2\beta\lambda_{i}^{N}(t)+\gamma^{2}\sum_{j:j\neq i}\dfrac{\lambda_{i}^{N}(t)+\lambda_{j}^{N}(t)}{\lambda_{i}^{N}(t)-\lambda_{j}^{N}(t)}\right)dt,~i\in\{1,\ldots,N\},

(51)

where
W_{1}(t),\ldots,W_{N}(t)
are independent standard Brownian motions.

If
p\geq N+1
,
\lambda_{N}^{N}(t)>0
for all
t>0
almost surely. Furthermore, if
p\in\{1,2,\ldots,N-1\}
, then the same results hold for the largest
p
eigenvalue processes
\lambda_{1}^{N}(t),\ldots,\lambda_{p}^{N}(t)
.

Theorem 8

related to (

,

locally uniformly on

as

of a weakly convergent subsequence in law is an element in

that satisfies

\displaystyle\langle f,\mu_{t}\rangle=

\displaystyle\langle f,\mu_{0}\rangle+\int_{0}^{t}\langle bf^{\prime},\mu_{s}\rangle ds+\dfrac{\beta}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\left[\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\dfrac{f^{\prime}(x)-f^{\prime}(y)}{x-y}G(x,y)\mu_{s}(dx)\mu_{s}(dy)\right]ds,

for all

, where

5
Matrix-valued stochastic processes driven by fractional Brownian motion

A common feature of the matrix-valued stochastic processes discussed so far is that they are all driven by independent Brownian motions. In contrast, the study of matrix-valued SDEs driven by fractional Brownian motions has a shorter history and is relatively limited.

Recall that a centred Gaussian process
B=\{B(t),t\geq 0\}
is called fractional Brownian motion with Hurst parameter
H\in(0,1)
if it has the covariance function

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[B(t)B(s)]=\dfrac{1}{2}\big(s^{2H}+t^{2H}-|t-s|^{2H}\big).

We refer the reader to
[
52
]
for more details.

To our best knowledge, the first paper in this area is
[
53
]
, where the real symmetric matrix fractional Brownian motion was introduced and studied.

Definition 2
.

Let
\{B_{i,j}(t),1\leq i\leq j\leq N\}
be a family of i.i.d. fractional Brownian motions with Hurst parameter
H\in(0,1)
. Let
H^{N}(t)=\left(H_{k,l}^{N}(t)\right)_{1\leq k\leq l\leq N}
be a real symmetric
N\times N
matrix-valued process whose entries are

H_{k,l}^{N}(t)=B_{k,l}(t){\large 1}_{\{k<l\}}+\sqrt{2}B_{l,l}(t){\large 1}_{\{k=l\}}.

Then
B(t)
is called the real symmetric matrix fractional Brownian motion with Hurst parameter
H
.

It is natural to consider the eigenvalue processes as we have done for the matrix Brownian motion. For
1\leq i\leq N
, denote by
\varPhi_{i}
the function that maps a
N\times N
real symmetric matrix to its
i
-th largest eigenvalue. The following results can be found in
[
53
]
.

Theorem 13

(
[
53
]
, Theorems 4.1 and 5.2)
.

Let
X^{N}(0)
be a real symmetric
N\times N
deterministic matrix and let
X^{N}(t)=X^{N}(0)+H^{N}(t)
, where
H^{N}(t)
is defined in Definition
2
with Hurst parameter
H\in(1/2,1)
. Let
\lambda_{1}^{N}(t)\geq\lambda_{2}^{N}(t)\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{N}^{N}(t)
be the ordered eigenvalue processes of
X^{N}(t)
. Denote the first collision time of the eigenvalue processes by

\displaystyle\tau_{N}=\inf\left\{t>0:\exists\ i\neq j,~\lambda_{i}^{N}(t)=\lambda_{j}^{N}(t)\right\}.

Then

\mathbb{P}\left(\tau_{N}=+\infty\right)=1
.
Furthermore, the ordered eigenvalue processes
\lambda_{1}^{N}(t),\cdots,\lambda_{N}^{N}(t)
satisfy

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}^{N}(t)=\lambda_{i}^{N}(0)+\left(\sum_{k\leq l}\int_{0}^{t}\dfrac{\partial\varPhi_{i}(X^{N}(s))}{\partial B_{k,h}}\delta B_{k,h}(s)\right)+2H\sum_{j:j\neq i}\int_{0}^{t}\dfrac{s^{2H-1}}{\lambda_{i}^{N}(s)-\lambda_{j}^{N}(s)}ds.

(62)

The main tool used in
[
53
]
is the fractional calculus and Malliavin calculus, for which we refer to
[
60
]
and
[
52
]
respectively. The non-collision property was obtained in
[
53
, Theorem 4.1]
by establishing an upper bound for negative moments of the difference of eigenvalue processes. The equation (
62
) for eigenvalue processes was derived in
[
53
, Theorem 5.2]
by employing a multidimensional version of the Itô’s formula for the Skorohod integral (
[
53
, Theorem 3.1]
, see also
[
56
, Theorem 2]
).

Remark 16
.

The complex version of Theorem
13
can be obtained by the same argument.

Remark 17
.

Unlike the Brownian motion case (
H=1/2
), the Lévy characterization theorem for fractional Brownian motion

is not applicable here, and it is conjectured that the second term in the right hand side of (

\displaystyle L_{N}(t)(dx)=\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\lambda_{i}^{N}(t)/N}(dx).

6
Matrix-valued stochastic processes driven by Brownian sheet

In stochastic analysis, multi-parameter processes, which are also known as random fields, are a natural extension of one-parameter processes. Various interactions exist between the theory of multi-parameter processes and other disciplines, such as analysis, algebra, mathematical statistics and statistical mechanics. The most important multi-parameter process is the Brownian sheet. Recall that the standard
1
-dimensional (
2
-parameter) Brownian sheet
\{B(s,t),(s,t)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}\}
is a centred Gaussian random
field with covariance function

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[B(s_{1},t_{1})B(s_{2},t_{2})\right]=(s_{1}\wedge s_{2})(t_{1}\wedge t_{2}).

We refer to
[
39
]
for more details on multi-parameter processes, in particular for properties of the Brownian sheet.

As shown in the review, there is a rich literature on eigenvalue processes of matrix-valued one-parameter processes. In contrast, the study on matrix-valued multi-parameter processes is just beginning. To our best knowledge,
[
63
]
is the only reference on this topic.

Let
\{B_{i,j}(s,t),s,t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\}_{i,j\geq 1}
be a family of independent standard
1
-dimensional Brownian sheets. The
N\times N
real symmetric matrix-valued stochastic process
H^{N}(s,t)=\left(H_{i,j}^{N}(s,t)\right)_{1\leq i,j\leq N}
with entries

\displaystyle H_{i,j}^{N}(s,t)=\begin{cases}B_{i,j}(s,t),&i<j,\\
\sqrt{2}B_{i,i}(s,t),&i=j,\end{cases}

(64)

is known as the real symmetric matrix Brownian sheet. Let
A^{N}
be a
N\times N
real symmetric deterministic matrix with distinct eigenvalues, and let

\displaystyle X^{N}(s,t)=H^{N}(s,t)+A^{N}.

(65)

In the case of symmetric matrix Brownian motion, multi-dimensional Itô’s formula for Brownian motions plays a key role when deriving the system of SDEs (
4
) for eigenvalue processes (see
[
1
, Theorem 4.3.2]
). The system of SDEs (
62
) for eigenvalue processes of real symmetric matrix fractional Brownian motion also heavily relies on the multi-dimensional Itô’s formula for the Skorohod integral with respect to fractional Brownian motion (see
[
53
]
). For the case of Brownian sheet, though the one-dimensional Itô’s formula has been established in
[
10
]
, the multi-dimensional version was not available until it was obtained in
[
63
]
by using the stochastic calculus on the plane developed in
[
10
]
. Using the multi-dimensional Itô’s formula,
[
63
]
derived the system of SPDEs for the ordered eigenvalue processes of
X^{N}(s,t)
. Moreover, the convergence of the sequence of the eigenvalue empirical measure processes of
\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}X^{N}(s,t)
was also studied in
[
63
]
.

Open problems

For the system of eigenvalue processes (
53
), the conditions of the uniqueness to (

) are still unknown. The uniqueness can be obtained under proper conditions for some special matrix-valued processes. It was established in
[
9
]
for real symmetric and complex Hermitian matrix Brownian motion and complex Wishart process using large deviation technique. For real symmetric and complex Hermitian matrix Brownian motion,
[
