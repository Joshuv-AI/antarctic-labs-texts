Low Row Rank Parity Check Codes
Ermes Franch, Chunlei Li

Abstract
In this paper we present an extended variant of low rank parity check matrix (LRPC) codes that have received significant interests in recent years. It is shown that the extension indeed yields a super-family of LRPC codes, which are termed low row rank parity check codes. The decoding method of the proposed codes is also investigated.



1
Introduction

Rank metric codes were independently introduced by Delsarte
[
1
]
, Gabidulin
[
2
]
and Roth
[
3
]
.
The Gabidulin codes, which are an equivalent of the Reed-Solomon codes for the rank metric, have found applications in network coding
[
4
]
, space-time coding
[
5
]
and cryptography
[
6
]
. In 2013 Gaborit, Murat and Zémor introduced a new family of rank metric codes, termed
low rank parity check
(LRPC) codes, which can be seen as the equivalent of LDPC
codes
[
7
]
.
With an efficient probabilistic decoding algorithm,
LRPC codes have been applied in several cryptosystems
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
as well as a signature scheme
[
11
]
, which are designed
for better resistance against quantum computers and smaller key size compared to the classic McEliece cryptosystem in Hamming metric
[
12
]
. Among these schemes, the cryptosystem ROLLO
[
10
]
, based on a subfamily of LRPC codes, has reached the second round
of the National
Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) post-quantum competition.

In this paper we will present an extended variant of the LRPC codes, which are called
low row rank parity check codes
, by applying the constraint of low rank weight only
on each row of the parity check matrix instead of on the whole parity check matrix as for LRPC codes. The proposed low rank parity check codes are shown to contain LRPC codes as a strictly proper subclass. Due to the loosened constraint,
the decoding technique of LRPC codes no longer works for the low row rank parity check codes.
We investigate the decoding process of these new codes
and discuss potential reasons for decoding failures.

2
Background on rank metric codes

2.1
Notations

Let
\mathbb{F}_{q}
the finite field of
q
elements, where
q
is a power of a prime
p
.
The extension field
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
can be deemed as an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-vector subspace of dimension
m
.
Given elements
v_{1},\ldots,v_{r}
in
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
,
the
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-vector subspace generated by
v_{i}
’s, denoted in the calligraphic upper case, is given by

\mathcal{V}=\langle v_{1},\ldots,v_{r}\rangle=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}v_{i}\,|\,\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\right\}.

We will denote vectors over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
in bold lower case as
\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})
and denote matrices in upper case as
M=(M_{i,j})
.
Given a
k\times n
matrix
M
and two non-empty ordered subsets
I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,k\},J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}
, the sub-matrix of
M
whose rows are indexed by
I
and columns are indexed by
J
is denoted as
M_{I,J}
. The transpose of the matrix
M
will be denoted as
M^{\intercal}
.

Let
\mathcal{V}=\langle v_{1},\ldots,v_{d}\rangle
be an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-vector space of dimension
d
. We denote by
\mathcal{V}^{*}
the set
\mathcal{V}\setminus\{0\}
and by
\mathcal{V}^{-1}
the set
\{v^{-1}|v\in\mathcal{V}^{*}\}
. Given another
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-vector space
\mathcal{U}=\langle u_{1},\ldots,u_{d}\rangle
of dimension
r
, the product vector space of
\mathcal{V}
and
\mathcal{U}
is defined as

\mathcal{V}.\mathcal{U}=\langle v_{1}u_{1},\ldots,v_{1}u_{d},\ldots,v_{r}u_{1},\ldots,v_{r}u_{d}\rangle,
which has dimension at most
rd
.

2.2
Basic definitions

We start with the definitions of rank weight and rank distance of elements in
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
.

Definition 1
.

Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
and let
\langle S\rangle
be the
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-vector subspace generated by all the elements of
S
. The
rank weight
of
S
is given by
\mathrm{rw}(S)=\dim(\langle S\rangle)
.

We can extend this definition in a natural way to vectors and matrices.

Definition 2
.

Let
\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})
be a vector over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
.
The
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-vector subspace
\mathcal{V}=\langle v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\rangle
is called the
support
of
\mathbf{v}
.
The
rank weight
of
\mathbf{v}
is given by

\mathrm{rw}(\mathbf{v}):=\dim\left(\mathcal{V}\right)=\dim(\langle v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\rangle).

The
rank distance
between two vectors
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}
is given by

\mathrm{d}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}):=\mathrm{rw}(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}).

Definition 3
.

The
weight

\delta
of a matrix
H
over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
is defined as the rank weight of the set of all its elements.
The matrix
H
is said to have
low weight
if
\delta<m
, particularly
\delta\ll m
.
A rank metric code
\mathcal{C}
over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
is said to be an
LRPC code
of weight
\delta
if
\mathcal{C}
admits a parity check matrix
H
of weight less than or equal to
\delta
.

Given an LRPC code
\mathcal{C}
with a low-weight parity check matrix
H
, it is easy to build another parity check matrix
H^{\prime}
for
\mathcal{C}
of full weight
m
. On the contrary, starting from a matrix
H^{\prime}
of weight
m
, it is hard to find an equivalent matrix
H
of low weight. It would be at least as hard as finding
n-k
linearly independent vectors of low weight in the dual code of
\mathcal{C}
(which are not guaranteed to exist if
\mathcal{C}
is not an LRPC code).

3
The row-LRPC codes

In this section we will introduce a super-family of LRPC codes by
applying the low-weight constraint only to rows of a parity check matrix instead of the whole matrix. The decoding process of those new codes will be examined in the next section.

Definition 4
.

The
row weight

\rho
of an
(n-k)\times n
matrix
H
over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
is defined as the maximum rank weight of all its rows:

\rho=\max\limits_{1\leq i\leq n-k}(\mathrm{rw}(\mathbf{h}_{i})),

where
\mathbf{h}_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq n-k
is the
i
-th row of
H
.
The matrix
H
is said to have
low row weight
if
\rho<m
, particularly
\rho\ll m
.
We call a rank metric code
\mathcal{C}
a low row rank parity check (shortly row-LRPC) code of row weight
\rho
if
\mathcal{C}
admits a parity check matrix
H
of
row weight
less than or equal to
\rho
.

In the above definition we use the abbreviation row-LRPC codes, instead of LRRPC codes, for better distinguishing them from LRPC codes.
For simplicity of presentation, we denote by
\mathrm{LRPC}_{q}(n,m,k,\delta)
the set of all the
[n,k]
linear LRPC codes over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
of weight
\delta
, and by row-
\mathrm{LRPC}_{q}(n,m,k,\rho)
the set of all the
[n,k]
row-LRPC codes over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
of row weight
\rho
.

Remark 1
.

From Definition
3
and Definition
, we have
\mathrm{LRPC}_{q}(n,m,k,\delta)\subseteq\mathrm{LRPC}_{q}(n,m,k,\delta^{\prime})
if
\delta\leq\delta^{\prime}
, similarly we have that
\text{row-}\mathrm{LRPC}_{q}(n,m,k,\rho)\subseteq\text{row-}\mathrm{LRPC}_{q}(n,m,k,\rho^{\prime})
if
\rho\leq\rho^{\prime}
.

Let
H
be an
(n-k)\times n
matrix with row weight
\rho
. The weight
\delta
of
H
satisfies
\rho\leq\delta\leq(n-k)\rho
. Hence, a row-LRPC code of row weight
\rho
may admit a parity check matrix with weight ranging from
\rho
up to
m
when
(n-k)\rho\geq m
.
In other words, it is clear that
\mathrm{LRPC}_{q}(n,m,k,\rho)\subseteq\text{row-}\mathrm{LRPC}_{q}(n,m,k,\rho).

The opposite inclusion is not true. As shown below, under certain condition it is always possible to construct a row-LRPC code of row weight
\rho\geq 2
that has all its parity check matrices of weight strictly greater than
\rho
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
2\leq\rho<m
and
2\rho-2\leq k\leq n-2
. Then we have

\mathrm{LRPC}_{q}(n,m,k,\rho)\subsetneq\text{row-}\mathrm{LRPC}_{q}(n,m,k,\rho)

Proof.

Suppose
\mathcal{A}=\langle a_{1},\ldots,a_{\rho}\rangle,\mathcal{B}=\langle b_{1},\ldots,b_{\rho}\rangle
are two
\rho
-dimensional
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-vector subspace of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
such that there exists no
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
satisfying
\mathcal{A}=\lambda\mathcal{B}
.
We build an
(n-k)\times n
parity check matrix
H
of row density
\rho
in the following way

H:=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&\cdots&a_{\rho}&0&\cdots&0&\mathbf{0}_{n-2\rho}\\
0&\cdots&0&b_{1}&\cdots&b_{\rho}&\mathbf{0}_{n-2\rho}\\
0&\cdots&0&0&\cdots&0&J\\
\end{pmatrix},

where
J
is an
{(n-k-2)\times(n-2\rho)}
matrix obtained by extending the identity matrix of order
n-k-2
with
k+2-2\rho
zeros at each row, and
\mathbf{0}_{n-2\rho}
is a
1\times(n-2\rho)
matrix of zeros.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a row-LRPC code admitting
H
as its parity check matrix.
We can write all the possible equivalent parity check matrices for
\mathcal{C}
as
H^{\prime}=TH
for some invertible
(n-k)\times(n-k)
matrix
T
over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
.
Since
T
is a square invertible matrix, all its columns have to be linearly independent.
Consider the first two columns of
T
, without loss of generality we can assume that the submatrix

T_{\{1,2\},\{1,2\}}=\begin{pmatrix}t_{1,1}&t_{1,2}\\
t_{2,1}&t_{2,2}\\
\end{pmatrix}

has non null determinant. The first
2
rows of
TH
can be given by

\begin{pmatrix}t_{1,1}a_{1}&\cdots&t_{1,1}a_{\rho}&t_{1,2}b_{1}&\cdots&t_{1,2}b_{\rho}&\mathbf{x}(T)\\
t_{2,1}a_{1}&\cdots&t_{2,1}a_{\rho}&t_{2,2}b_{1}&\cdots&t_{2,2}b_{\rho}&\mathbf{y}(T)\end{pmatrix},

where
\mathbf{x}(T)
and
\mathbf{y}(T)
are two vectors of length
(n-2\rho)
depending on
T
(which are both
0
-vectors in the worst case).

Let
\mathcal{T}
be the
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-vector subspace generated by the elements of the submatrix

TH_{\{1,2\},\{1,\ldots,2\rho\}}
we can describe it as

\mathcal{T}=t_{1,1}\mathcal{A}+t_{2,1}\mathcal{A}+t_{1,2}\mathcal{B}+t_{2,2}\mathcal{B}.

Since
T_{\{1,2\},\{1,2\}}
is invertible, it follows that either
t_{1,1}t_{2,2}\neq 0
or
t_{1,2}t_{2,1}\neq 0
.
We want to prove that
\dim(\mathcal{T})>\rho
. Without loss of generality we can assume
t_{1,1},t_{2,2}\neq 0
, since
t_{1,1}\mathcal{A}+t_{2,2}\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{T}
then

\dim(t_{1,1}\mathcal{A}+t_{2,2}\mathcal{B})\leq\dim(\mathcal{T}).

Observe that

\displaystyle\dim(t_{1,1}\mathcal{A}+t_{2,2}\mathcal{B})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dim(t_{1,1}\mathcal{A})+\dim(t_{2,2}\mathcal{B})-\dim(t_{1,1}\mathcal{A}\cap t_{2,2}\mathcal{B})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\rho-\dim(t_{1,1}\mathcal{A}\cap t_{2,2}\mathcal{B}).

This implies
\dim(\mathcal{T})\geq 2\rho-\dim(t_{1,1}\mathcal{A}\cap t_{2,2}\mathcal{B})
.
Since
\dim(t_{1,1}\mathcal{A}\cap t_{2,2}\mathcal{B})\leq\rho
, then

\dim(\mathcal{T})\geq\rho
, where the equality holds only if

\dim(t_{1,1}\mathcal{A}\cap t_{2,2}\mathcal{B})=\rho
. This is actually equivalent to

\mathcal{A}=t_{1,1}^{-1}t_{2,2}\mathcal{B},

which is a contradiction since by our choice of
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
there exists no
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
satisfying
\mathcal{A}=\lambda\mathcal{B}
.
Let
\mathrm{rw}(H)
denote the rank weight of the matrix
H
. For any
invertible matrix
T
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{rw}(TH)

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{rw}(TH_{\{1,2\},\{1,\ldots,n\}})

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{rw}(TH_{\{1,2\},\{1,\ldots,2\rho\}})>\rho.

The desired conclusion follows.
∎

Remark 2
.

Theorem 1 is does not cover the whole range of all parameters that make Theorem 1’s result hold. Indeed it is quite easy to find such examples for parameters.

Theorem 1 indicates that the extension of LRPC codes to row-LRPC codes is a proper extension for
\rho\geq 2
.
Nevertheless, this is not true for the case when the row weight equals
1
.

Lemma 1
.

\small{\text{row-}\mathrm{LRPC}_{q}(n,m,k,1)=\mathrm{LRPC}_{q}(n,m,k,1).}

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a row-LRPC code of dimension
k
and row weight
1
. The code
\mathcal{C}
admits an
(n-k)\times n
parity check matrix
H
whose rows have all rank weight
1
. This means
h_{i,j}\in\mathcal{H}_{i}=\langle h_{i}\rangle
for some
h_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{*}
, i.e.,
h_{i,j}=\lambda_{i,j}h_{i}
for some
\lambda_{i,j}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

The weight of the matrix
H
is
\dim(\langle h_{1},\ldots,h_{n-k}\rangle)
which can be any number between
1
and
\min(n-k,m)
. We want to prove that there exists as well another parity check matrix
H^{\prime}
of weight
1
for the same code
\mathcal{C}
. In fact, consider the diagonal matrix
L
with the entries
(i,i)
being
h_{i}^{-1}
and all other entries zero.
Then the matrix
H^{\prime}=LH
is still a parity check matrix of
\mathcal{C}
and its entries can be written as
h_{i,j}^{\prime}=h_{i}^{-1}h_{i,j}=h_{i}^{-1}\lambda_{i,j}h_{i}=\lambda_{i,j}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Therefore the weight of
H^{\prime}
is
1
.
∎

4
Decoding of Row-LRPC Codes

The syndrome decoding problem for Hamming distance is a fundamental problem in complexity theory, which was
proved to be an NP-complete problem Berlekamp, McEliece and van Tilborg
[
13
]
. When the code is embedded with rank metric, the corresponding rank syndrome decoding (RSD) problem is given as below.

Definition 5
.

[
6
,
14
]

Given an
(n-k)\times n
matrix over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
, a vector
\mathbf{s}
in
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n-k}
and an integer
r
,
find a vector
\mathbf{e}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n}
of rank weight
\mathrm{rw}(\mathbf{e})=r
satisfying

\mathbf{e}H^{\intercal}=\mathbf{s}.

By embedding linear codes in the Hamming space into
linear codes over an extension field equipped with the rank
metric, Gaborit and Zémor
[
15
]
proved the hardness of
the RSD problem under an unfaithful random
reduction, which indicates that if the RSD problem is in ZPP, then ZPP = NP.
The RSD problem has been intensively studied in recent years driven by its application in post-quantum cryptosystems
[
16
,
17
,
10
]
.
Early attacks on RSD appeared in
[
18
,
19
]
.
Some progress was lately made in
[
14
]
, where the authors proposed two new generic attacks:
support attack
and
polynomial annulator attack
for the RSD problem,
which outperform the combinatorial attack in
[
19
]
when the error vector has a relatively small rank weight
r
.
Very recently, algebraic attacks with Grobner basis technique
[
20
]
and without Grobner basis technique
[
21
]
were proposed, which have significantly advanced the RSD problem.

With the hardness of RSD problem, by far there are two general families of rank metric codes for which
(probabilistic) polynomial-time decoding algorithms have been found. Known decoding algorithms for the Gabidulin codes (e.g.,
[
2
]
)
rely on the strong algebraic structure of Gabidulin codes, and the probabilistic decoding algorithm for the LRPC codes
[
22
]
utilizes
the low-weight property of the codes. More specifically,
the decoding algorithm for LRPC codes can be divided into two steps. The first step is to recover the support
\mathcal{E}
of the error vector
\mathbf{e}
by intersecting the vector spaces derived from the parity check matrix
H
and the syndrome vector
\mathbf{s}
; the second step is to reconstruct each component of the error vector in the support
\mathcal{E}
by re-utilizing the syndrome equation
\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{e}H^{\intercal}
.

When a row-LRPC code of row weight
\rho\geq 2
is considered, its parity check matrix
H
may have weight
\delta
ranging from
\rho
to
\min(m,(n-k)\rho)
. Even in the non generic case when there exists a parity check matrix
H^{\prime}
for the same code of weight sufficiently small, there is no efficient algorithm to compute
H^{\prime}
from a randomly given
H
. Therefore, in general, we cannot use the decoding algorithm of LRPC codes for decoding row-LRPC codes.

In the following we shall explore the decoding process of row-LRPC codes, particularly when the row weight is sufficiently small.
Similar to the two families of efficiently decodable rank metric codes Gabidulin codes
[
2
]
and LRPC codes
[
22
,
9
]
, the critical step of decoding row-LRPC codes is to efficiently recover the error support.
Once the the error support is recovered, it is possible to expand the
n-k
linear equation given by the syndrome obtaining
m(n-k)
equation over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
and solve the linear system.

4.1
The simplest case
r=1

The easiest case to treat is when the error support
\mathcal{E}
has dimension
1
. In this case, we can write
\mathcal{E}=\langle\varepsilon\rangle
for some
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
. The error
\mathbf{e}
can be written as
(\lambda_{1}\varepsilon,\ldots,\lambda_{n}\varepsilon)
for some
\lambda_{j}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
. The general syndrome equation is then given by

s_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}h_{i,j}\lambda_{j}\varepsilon=\varepsilon\sum_{j=1}^{n}h_{i,j}\lambda_{j}=\varepsilon h_{i},\qquad h_{i}\in\mathcal{H}_{i},

(1)

where
\mathcal{H}_{i}
is the
\rho
-dimensional
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-vector subspace generated by the elements of the
i
-th row of
H
.
From equation (
1
), if
s_{i}\neq 0
then
h_{i}\neq 0
, so we have
\varepsilon\in s_{i}\mathcal{H}_{i}^{-1}
. That is equivalent to
\varepsilon^{-1}\in s_{i}^{-1}\mathcal{H}
. This second formulation has an advantage that
s_{i}^{-1}\mathcal{H}
is an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-vector subspace since

\lambda_{1}s_{i}^{-1}h_{1}+\lambda_{2}s_{i}^{-1}h_{2}=s_{i}^{-1}(\lambda_{1}h_{1}+\lambda_{2}h_{2})

belongs to
s_{i}^{-1}\mathcal{H}
for any
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
,
h_{1},h_{2}\in\mathcal{H}
. By intersecting several sets of the form
s_{i}^{-1}\mathcal{H}_{i}
, we will likely obtain the
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-vector space
\mathbb{F}_{q}\varepsilon^{-1}
from which it’s easy to find the support
\mathcal{E}
.

4.2
Cramer’s rule decoding for
r=2

We will present a method to recover the error support when the error has rank
r\geq 2
. Before treating the general case, we shall start with the case
r=2
as a simpler illustration in this subsection.

Notice that, as previously shown, the set
s\mathcal{H}=\{sh|h\in\mathcal{H}\}
is a
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-vector subspace
if
\mathcal{H}
is a
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-vector subspace. Moreover, it is clear that
s\mathcal{H}=\langle sh_{1},\ldots,sh_{\rho}\rangle
when
\mathcal{H}=\langle h_{1},\ldots,h_{\rho}\rangle
since any element in
s\mathcal{H}
can be linearly expressed by
sh_{1},\ldots,sh_{\rho}
. This fact will be frequently used in the subsequent decoding process.

Let
\mathcal{E}=\langle\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}\rangle
be the error support of
\mathbf{e}
and let
H
be a parity check matrix of row weight
\rho
. The general syndrome equation will be of the form

h_{i,1}\varepsilon_{1}+h_{i,2}\varepsilon_{2}=s_{i},\qquad h_{i,1},h_{i,2}\in\mathcal{H}_{i}

where
\mathcal{H}_{i}
is the
\rho
-dimensional
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-vector subspace generated by the elements of the
i
-th row of the parity check matrix
H
.
When
s_{i}\neq 0
we can multiply the whole equation by
s_{i}^{-1}
. Let
\mathcal{A}_{i}
be an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-vector space given by

\mathcal{A}_{i}=\begin{cases}s_{i}^{-1}\mathcal{H}_{i},&\text{if }s_{i}\neq 0,\\
\mathcal{H}_{i},&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

In this way, we can rewrite the equation
H\mathbf{e}^{\intercal}=\mathbf{s}^{\intercal}
as follows

A\cdot\mathbf{e}^{\intercal}=\mathbf{v}\Leftrightarrow\begin{pmatrix}a_{1,1}&a_{1,2}\\
a_{2,1}&a_{2,2}\\
\vdots&\vdots\\
a_{n-k,1}&a_{n-k,r}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon_{1}\\
\varepsilon_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}v_{1}\\
v_{2}\\
\vdots\\
v_{n-k}\end{pmatrix},

(2)

where
a_{i,j}\in\mathcal{A}_{i}
and
\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n-k})^{\intercal}
is a vector over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
such that
v_{i}=0
when
s_{i}=0
and is
1
otherwise.

Suppose that
v_{i}=v_{j}=1
, from (
2
) we can extract the linear system

A_{\{i,j\},\{1,2\}}\cdot\mathbf{e}^{\intercal}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{i,1}&a_{i,2}\\
a_{j,1}&a_{j,2}\\
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon_{1}\\
\varepsilon_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
1\end{pmatrix}.

(3)

There are at most
q^{4\rho}
possible configuration of the matrix
A_{\{i,j\},\{1,2\}}
.
If the matrix
A_{\{i,j\},\{1,2\}}
is invertible, we can solve (
3
) by Cramer’s rule
[
23
]
as

\varepsilon_{1}=\frac{\det\begin{pmatrix}1&a_{i,2}\\
1&a_{j,2}\end{pmatrix}}{\det\begin{pmatrix}a_{i,1}&a_{i,2}\\
a_{j,1}&a_{j,2}\end{pmatrix}},\quad\varepsilon_{2}=\frac{\det\begin{pmatrix}a_{i,1}&1\\
a_{j,1}&1\end{pmatrix}}{\det\begin{pmatrix}a_{i,1}&a_{i,2}\\
a_{j,1}&a_{j,2}\end{pmatrix}}.

(4)

After calculation of determinants this reduces to

\varepsilon_{1}=\frac{a_{i,2}-a_{j,2}}{a_{i,1}a_{j,2}-a_{i,2}a_{j,1}},\quad\varepsilon_{2}=\frac{a_{i,1}-a_{j,1}}{a_{i,1}a_{j,2}-a_{i,2}a_{j,1}}.

(5)

We define the set

B_{i,j}=\left\{\frac{a_{i,2}-a_{j,2}}{a_{i,1}a_{j,2}-a_{i,2}a_{j,1}}\,:\,a_{i,*}\in\mathcal{A}_{i},\,a_{j,*}\in\mathcal{A}_{j}\right\}.

(6)

Recall that
\mathcal{A}.\mathcal{B}
denotes the product vector space generated by the products of elements in the vector spaces
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
.
For the set
B_{i,j}
in (
6
), the denominators in the division
satisfy

a_{i,1}a_{j,2}-a_{i,2}a_{j,1}\in\mathcal{A}_{i}.\mathcal{A}_{j}

since
a_{i,*}a_{j,*}\in\mathcal{A}_{i}.\mathcal{A}_{j}
. This leads to an observation that

B_{i,j}\subseteq\frac{\mathcal{A}_{i}+\mathcal{A}_{j}}{\mathcal{A}_{i}.\mathcal{A}_{j}}.

In addition, due to the symmetry of the equations in (
5
), we have

\mathcal{E}\subsetneq B_{i,j}\subseteq\frac{\mathcal{A}_{i}+\mathcal{A}_{j}}{\mathcal{A}_{i}.\mathcal{A}_{j}}.

Since this is true for any
B_{i,j}
we can intersect different
B_{i,j}
in order to get a smaller set where to look for the error support
\mathcal{E}
.
Since the sets
B_{i,j}
depend on
4
parameters in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-vector spaces of dimension
\rho
, their size is upper bounded by
q^{4\rho}
.

Following the method just explained, we run some MAGMA simulations for the case
r=2
,
\rho=2
,
n=m=20
and
q=2,3
. Using
4
rows in the parity check matrix it was possible to recover the error support in
707/1000
cases for
q=2
and in
954/1000
cases for
q=3
. Increasing the number of parity check rows to
6
, the results improved to
988/1000
and
998/1000
, respectively.
In all of the above mentioned cases, it was possible to recover the error support after the intersection of just two
B_{i,j}
sets.

In Section
5
we will explain why and how increasing the number of rows of the parity check matrix can help us achieve higher success rates in decoding.

4.3
Cramer’s rule for the general case

The decoding process with Cramer’s rule for
r=2
can be easily extended to general cases.

Let
\mathcal{E}=\langle\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{r}\rangle
be the support of the error
\mathbf{e}=(e_{1},\ldots,e_{n})
. Given
H
a parity check matrix of row weight
\rho
we can rewrite the
i
-th syndrome equation
\mathbf{e}\cdot\mathbf{h}_{i}^{\intercal}=s_{i}
as

\varepsilon_{1}h_{i,1}+\cdots+\varepsilon_{r}h_{i,r}=s_{i}\qquad h_{i,*}\in\mathcal{H}_{i},

(7)

where
\mathcal{H}_{i}=\langle\mathbf{h}_{i}\rangle
is the
\rho
-dimensional vector space generated by the
i
-th row of
H
.

As we did in the previous section, we can manipulate each equations multiplying on both sides by
s_{i}^{-1}
when
s_{i}\neq 0
. We rename the variables and the
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-vector subspace with the same notation.
In this way we get a system that can be described by the matrix equation
A\cdot\mathbf{e}^{\intercal}=\mathbf{v}
, more specifically,

\begin{pmatrix}a_{1,1}&\cdots&a_{1,r}\\
a_{2,1}&\cdots&a_{2,r}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
a_{n-k,1}&\cdots&a_{n-k,r}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\varepsilon_{r}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}v_{1}\\
v_{2}\\
\vdots\\
v_{n-k}\end{pmatrix}.

(8)

This time we can select
r
rows of (
8
) and solve by the Cramer’s rule.
Let
I=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}\}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n-k\}
a set of
r
indices. The Cramer’s rule for the sub-system given by the the rows indexed by
I
is

\varepsilon_{j}=\frac{\det(A_{I,\{1,\ldots,j-1\}}|\mathbf{v}|A_{I,\{j+1,\ldots,r\}})}{\det(A_{I,\{1,\ldots,r\}})},

(9)

where the matrix
A_{I,\{1,\ldots j-1\}}|\mathbf{v}|A_{I,\{j+1,\ldots,r\}}
is the matrix
A_{I,\{1,\ldots,r\}}
where the
j
-th column is substituted by
\mathbf{v}
.
In this way we can define the set
B_{I}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
of size upper bounded by
q^{r^{2}\rho}
as:

B_{I}=\left\{\frac{\det(A_{I,\{1,\ldots,r-1\}}|\mathbf{v})}{\det(A_{I,\{1,\ldots,r\}})}\bigg|\quad a_{i,j}\in\mathcal{A}_{i}\right\}

(10)

where
a_{i,j}
indicates the entries of
A_{I,\{1,\ldots,r\}}
at the row
i
and column
j
. Notice that since the variables
a_{i,j}
are uniformly chosen from all the possible values of the
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-vector space
\mathcal{A}_{i}
, the quantity
\det(A^{{}^{\prime}}_{I,\{1,\ldots j-1\}}|\mathbf{v}|A^{{}^{\prime}}_{I,\{j+1,\ldots,r\}})
with
j=1,\ldots,r
can be obtained as well as
\det(A_{I,\{1,\ldots,r-1\}}|\mathbf{v})
, where
A
is obtained from
A^{\prime}
by renaming the columns after
j
and multiplying one column by
-1
when
\mathbb{F}_{q}
has odd characteristic.

Each
B_{I}
contains the error support
\mathcal{E}
. We can then intersect some different
B_{I}
’s to obtain a smaller set that can be exactly
\mathcal{E}
or at least a smaller set where we know
\mathcal{E}
is contained. The situation where we expect this algorithm to work is when the cardinality of
B_{I}
is much smaller than
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
(i.e.
r^{2}\rho\ll m
).

If we intersect two sets
B_{I}
and
B_{J}
such that
I\cap J\neq\emptyset
, we can expect to have a bigger intersection.
Suppose without loss of generality that the index
1
belongs to both
I
and
J
. Among all the possible configurations of
A_{I,\{1,\ldots,r\}}
and
A_{J,\{1,\ldots,r\}}
we will have one where the first row of both matrices is
(0,\ldots,0,a_{1,r})
. When we apply the Cramer’s rule to that matrix we will obtain
a_{1,r}^{-1}
, then we know without the need of computing that
\mathcal{A}_{1}^{-1}\subset B_{I}\cap B_{J}
.

For this reason, in order to do as few intersections as possible, it is better to use disjointed indexes. If we want to have at least two disjointed sets of indexes, we need
n-k\geq 2r
. Suppose we managed to recover the error support, to recover each component of the error
\mathbf{e}
we need to solve an expanded linear system of
nr
unknowns and
m(n-k)
equations. Therefore we need that
(n-k)\geq\lceil\frac{nr}{m}\rceil
. Then we have the condition on the dimension
k

k\leq\min\left(n-2r,n-\left\lceil\frac{nr}{m}\right\rceil\right).

The hardest part of this algorithm is ruled by the error support recovery. In particular it is given by the cost of computing the sets
B_{I}
, to obtain each element of
B_{I}
we need to solve a linear system in
r
unknowns. We can then give an estimation of the total complexity in the order of
O(r^{3}q^{r^{2}\rho})
.

5
Decoding failures

We implement the algorithm described in the previous section on MAGMA on an easy instance of the problem. In particular we created different instances of row-LRPC codes of row weight
2
and we did correct errors of rank weight
2
.
During the error support recovery, we noticed two possible causes of failure.

5.1
Syndrome contains some zeroes

Let
\mathcal{E}=\langle\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{r}\rangle
be the error support. The generic equation of the system is

a_{i,1}\varepsilon_{1}+\cdots+a_{i,r}\varepsilon_{r}=s_{i}.

(11)

We can consider
a_{i,*}
as independent variables uniformly distributed over
\mathcal{H}_{i}
, then
a_{i,j}\varepsilon_{j}
are independent uniformly distributed over
\mathcal{H}_{i}\varepsilon_{j}
. Therefore
s_{i}
is a variable uniformly distributed over the product space
\mathcal{H}_{i}.\mathcal{E}
which, with an high probability, has dimension
r\rho
when
r\rho\ll m

[
24
]
.

By our hypothesis

\mathcal{H}_{i}=\langle\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{\rho}\rangle
then each
a_{i,j}
can be rewritten as

\sum_{i\in\{1,\ldots,\rho\}}\lambda_{i,j}\alpha_{i}
for some

\lambda_{i,j}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
. The equation (
11
) can be rewritten as

\sum_{j\in\{1,\ldots,r\}}\sum_{l\in\{1,\ldots,\rho\}}\lambda_{l,j}\alpha_{l}\varepsilon_{j}=s_{i}\quad\lambda_{l,j}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}.

Suppose now that
s_{i}=0
and
r\rho\ll m
, with an high probability,
\alpha_{l}\varepsilon_{j}
are all
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linearly independent which implies
\lambda_{i,j}=0\quad\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,\rho\},\quad\forall j\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
, therefore
a_{i,1}=\cdots=a_{i,\rho}=0
.

Unfortunately in the case
s_{i}=0
we cannot get any information about the error support. Indeed, if all the coefficients
a_{i,*}
are zero, the equation (
11
) is trivially satisfied by all the possible sets of
r
elements in
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
.

If we consider
s_{i}
as independent uniformly distributed variables over the
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-vector subspace
\mathcal{H}_{i}.\mathcal{E}
of dimension
r\rho
, we have the probability
P(s_{i}=0)=q^{-r\rho}
. We expect to have at least
t
non-zero components of the syndrome with probability

\sum_{i=t}^{n-k}\binom{n-k}{i}(1-q^{-r\rho})^{i}q^{-r\rho(n-k-i)}
.

The equations leading to zero will not be totally discarded as they can still be used, after the recovery of the error support, to help us recovering the actual error component by component.

5.2
Intersecting subspaces

For simplicity here we will analyze the case when the error support has rank
2
.
In the error support recovery, after we discard all the equation corresponding to
s_{i}=0
, there is still a possible issue we will show in this paragraph.

Let
\mathcal{E}=\langle\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}\rangle

be the error support, consider the generic system:

\begin{cases}a_{i,1}\varepsilon_{1}+a_{i,2}\varepsilon_{r}=1\\
a_{j,1}\varepsilon_{1}+a_{j,2}\varepsilon_{r}=1\end{cases}

(12)

where
a_{i,*}\in\mathcal{A}_{i}=\mathcal{H}_{i}s_{i}^{-1}
and
a_{j,*}\in\mathcal{A}_{j}=\mathcal{H}_{j}s_{j}^{-1}
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{A}_{i}
and
\mathcal{A}_{j}
intersect non-trivially, the associated set
B_{i,j}
will be smaller than what we usually expect. A smaller set is not necessarily a good news. At the contrary, what we observed with MAGMA, is that many times this set does contain only part of the error support. When we intersect such a set with the other sets containing the whole support, we recover only part of the support instead of the whole as we wish.

Given two randomly generated
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-vector subspaces of dimension
\rho\ll m
, the probability of having a non-trivial intersection between the two should be negligible. What we observe with MAGMA, suggests that in our case this happens with a much higher frequency than one could naively expect.

Consider better the system (
12
), without loss of generality assume
i=1
and
j=2

\begin{cases}a_{1,1}\varepsilon_{1}+a_{1,2}\varepsilon_{2}=1\\
a_{2,1}\varepsilon_{1}+a_{2,2}\varepsilon_{2}=1\end{cases}

Consider the case
a_{1,2}=a_{2,2}=0
, this would imply
a_{1,1}\varepsilon_{1}=a_{2,1}\varepsilon_{1}=1
, then
\varepsilon_{1}^{-1}\in\mathcal{A}_{1}\cap\mathcal{A}_{2}
. A similar conclusion can be made from
a_{1,1}=a_{2,1}=0
. A bit less straight forward is the case
a_{1,1}=a_{1,2}\neq 0
. In this case we have
(\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})^{-1}\in\mathcal{A}_{1}
, if we also have
a_{2,1}=a_{2,2}\neq 0
then
(\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})^{-1}\in\mathcal{A}_{1}\cap\mathcal{A}_{2}
.
The coefficients
a_{i,*}
all belong to
\mathcal{A}_{i}
which, by our hypothesis, is a small set of size
q^{\rho}
. This explains why we observe non trivial intersections so often.

In the general case, after we consider a system of
r
rows, we will obtain an
r\times r
matrix
A
whose entries belong to some
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-vector subspace of dimension
\rho
, that multiplies the vector
\mathbf{e}=(\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{r})
containing a base of the error support. Intuitively, in the case the
j
-th column of
A
is all
0
s, we have no hope to get some information about
\varepsilon_{j}
. The likelihood of this event decreases exponentially in
r
. In the case
r>2
, we cannot find a relation between the
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-vector subspaces
\mathcal{A}_{i}
that tells us something about the matrix
A
.
Increasing the number of equation will increase the probability of success.
In the case of intersecting spaces, it will give us more
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-vector subspaces among which we can find more disjointed couples. In the case of too many zeroes in the syndrome, increasing the number of equations will increase the probability of having enough non-zero components.

6
Conclusion

This paper introduces a new family of rank metric codes, which can be seen as an extended variant of LRPC codes introduced in
[
22
]
. We showed that the proposed row-LRPC codes contain LRPC codes as a strictly proper subfamily when row weight is no less than
2
under certain condition, which confirms the validity of the extension. The decoding of row-LRPC codes is also studied by intersecting certain sets obtained from the syndrome equation, which has complexity upper bounded by
O(r^{3}q^{r^{2}\rho})
, where
\rho
is the row weight of the code in question and
r
is the rank weight of the error vector.

Due to high complexity of the current decoding approach, we will continue exploring the possibility of a polynomial-time decoding algorithm for the row-LRPC codes, which by then would have interesting applications in cryptography..
