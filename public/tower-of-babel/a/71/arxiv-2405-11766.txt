From SHAP Scores to Feature Importance Scores
Olivier Létoffé, Xuanxiang Huang, Nicholas Asher, Joao Marques-Silva

Abstract
A central goal of eXplainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) is to assign relative importance to the features of a Machine Learning (ML) model given some prediction. The importance of this task of explainability by feature attribution is illustrated by the ubiquitous recent use of tools such as SHAP and LIME. Unfortunately, the exact computation of feature attributions, using the game-theoretical foundation underlying SHAP and LIME, can yield manifestly unsatisfactory results, that tantamount to reporting misleading relative feature importance. Recent work targeted rigorous feature attribution, by studying axiomatic aggregations of features based on logic-based definitions of explanations by feature selection. This paper shows that there is an essential relationship between feature attribution and a priori voting power, and that those recently proposed axiomatic aggregations represent a few instantiations of the range of power indices studied in the past. Furthermore, it remains unclear how some of the most widely used power indices might be exploited as feature importance scores (FISs), i.e. the use of power indices in XAI, and which of these indices would be the best suited for the purposes of XAI by feature attribution, namely in terms of not producing results that could be deemed as unsatisfactory. This paper proposes novel desirable properties that FISs should exhibit. In addition, the paper also proposes novel FISs exhibiting the proposed properties. Finally, the paper conducts a rigorous analysis of the best-known power indices in terms of the proposed properties.



1
Introduction

The requirement of delivering trustworthy systems of
artificial intelligence (AI) and
machine learning (ML)
is arguably one of the most consequential challenges in current
computing research and practice.
A growing number of governments have put forth proposals for regulating
the use of systems of AI/ML (
US Government 2023
;
European Union 2024
),
some of which are in the process of becoming enforced
regulations. Many companies that are stakeholders in AI/ML are
actively involved in building trust into the operation of highly
complex systems of AI/ML. Furthermore, it is widely accepted that trustworthy AI/ML can only be
achieved through the comprehensive use of (trustable) methods for
explaining the operation of those complex systems of
AI/ML
(
High-Level Expert Group on
AI 2019
)
.
This critical importance of explainability justifies in part the
massive interest in eXplainable AI (XAI) in recent
years (
Montavon, Samek, and
Müller 2018
;
Guidotti et al. 2019
;

Samek et al. 2019
;
Samek et al. 2021
).
Furthermore, in domains where the uses of AI/ML are deemed of
high-risk or are safety-critical, XAI must ensure the rigor of
computed explanations.
Unfortunately, the most widely-used methods of XAI offer no guarantees
of rigor. Unsurprisingly, several results in recent years have
unveiled critical misconceptions of informal
XAI (
Ignatiev 2020
;
Marques-Silva 2023
).

Motivated by this state of affairs,
a formal XAI (
Marques-Silva and
Ignatiev 2022
;
Marques-Silva 2022
;

Darwiche 2023
) that builds on logic-based
definitions of explanations and that in turn is deployed using
highly efficient automated reasoners has emerged.
However, formal XAI also exhibits some drawbacks, including the
complexity of automated reasoning, but also the need to offer human
decision-makers mechanisms for summarizing information about
explanations. While non-rigorous methods of XAI have used so-called
SHAP scores
(
Lundberg and Lee 2017
)
as a mechanism to convey
to human decision-makers information about relative feature
importance, formal XAI focused instead on finding explanations based
on feature selection, i.e. finding sets of features, with no apparent
solution for addressing relative feature importance.

SHAP scores have become a highly popular method in
XAI (
Molnar 2023
;
Mosca et al. 2022
;

Mishra 2023
).
Nevertheless, recent work has demonstrated that the
rigorous computation of SHAP scores can be thoroughly
unsatisfactory (
Huang and
Marques-Silva 2023a
;
Huang and
Marques-Silva 2023b
;

Huang and Marques-Silva 2024
;
Marques-Silva and Huang 2024
).
Indeed, it is now known that even for very simple classifiers, the
computed SHAP scores can bear little relationship with self-evident
notions of feature importance. An immediate consequence is that
computed SHAP scores can be misleading, i.e. they can induce human
decision-makers in error, by assigning more importance to the wrong
features.
Motivated by these negative results, researchers have proposed alternative
solutions for assessing relative feature
importance (
Yu, Ignatiev, and
Stuckey 2023
;
Biradar et al. 2023
;

Yu et al. 2023
;
Biradar et al. 2024
), all of
which are based on logic-based definitions of explanations.
Furthermore, axiomatic aggregations
(
Biradar et al. 2024
)

also reveal that feature importance can be naturally related with
power indices, i.e. measures of voter importance in a priori voting
power (see
(
Felsenthal and
Machover 2015
)
for an overview), but also with
payoff vectors, i.e. measures of player rewards in characteristic
function games (see
(
Chalkiadakis, Elkind, and
Wooldridge 2012
)
).

It should be underscored that there is a crucial difference between
the focus of power indices for measuring a priori voting
power
(
Felsenthal and
Machover 2015
)
and the focus of SHAP
scores
(
Lundberg and Lee 2017
)
.
In power indices, voter importance measures how likely each voter is
to be
critical
for a set of voters to represent a winning
coalition (e.g. see
(
Shapley and Shubik 1954
)
).
In contrast, the existing definitions of SHAP scores
overlook

the criticality of features, and instead measure feature contributions
starting from the expected values of the ML model.
As discussed in this paper, this fundamental difference is at the core
of why SHAP scores can yield unsatisfactory results.

Furthermore, and although the connection between feature attribution
and power indices proposed in recent work
(
Biradar et al. 2024
)
is
intuitive, it is unclear which power indices are best suited for
feature attribution, or even which properties such indices should exhibit. Our paper addresses these issues.
In particular, our work extends recent work on axiomatic
aggregations
(
Biradar et al. 2024
)
and their relationship with formal
explanations in several
ways.
First, we uncover the tight connection between feature attribution in
XAI and power indices in
a priori
voting power.
Second, we argue that feature attribution in XAI requires a more
general formalization than power indices, and develop such a
formalization, namely feature importance scores (FISs).
Third, we overview several properties of interest for FISs,
and propose novel properties.
Fourth, and motivated by the results in this paper, we propose novel
FISs, which can also find application in other domains.
Fifth, and finally, we provide a detailed characterization of which
FISs exhibit which properties.

Preliminaries

Classification problems.

Let
{\mathcal{F}}=\{1,\ldots,m\}
denote a set of features and

{\mathcal{K}}=\{c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{K}\}
denote a set of classes.
Each feature

takes values from a domain
\mathbb{D}_{i}
which is either categorical or ordinal. If ordinal, a domains can be
discrete or real-valued.
Classes can also be categorical or ordinal.
Throughout the paper, we assume that domains are discrete-valued and that,
unless otherwise stated, classes are ordinal.

A feature space is defined by

\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{D}_{1}\times\mathbb{D}_{2}\times\ldots\times\mathbb{D}_{m}
.
The notation

denotes an arbitrary point in

x_{i}
is a variable taking values from

\mathbb{D}_{i}
. Moreover, the notation
\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{m})

represents a specific point in feature space, where each
v_{i},i=1,\ldots,m
is a constant representing one concrete value from

\mathbb{D}_{i}
.
An
instance
denotes a pair
(\mathbf{v},c)
, where

\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{F}
and
c\in{\mathcal{K}}
, and such that

c=\kappa(\mathbf{v})
.
An ML classifier
{\mathcal{M}}
is characterized by a
non-constant
classification function

\kappa
that maps feature
space
\mathbb{F}
into the set of classes
{\mathcal{K}}
,
i.e.
\kappa:\mathbb{F}\to{\mathcal{K}}
.
Given the above, we associate with a classifier
{\mathcal{M}}
, a tuple

({\mathcal{F}},\mathbb{F},{\mathcal{K}},\kappa)
.

Abductive and contrastive explanations.

Given an explanation problem
{\mathcal{E}}
, a
weak abductive
explanation
(WAXp) is a set of features
{\mathcal{S}}
such that
the prediction remains unchanged
when the features in
{\mathcal{S}}
are assigned the values dictated by

\mathbf{v}
:

\forall(\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}).\left(\land_{i\in{\mathcal{S}}}x_{i}=v_{i}\right)\limply\left(\kappa(\mathbf{x})=\kappa(\mathbf{v})\right)

(6)

(The fixed features in
{\mathcal{S}}
are said to be
sufficient
for
the given prediction.)
Moreover, an
abductive explanation
(AXp) is a subset-minimal
WAXp.
Similarly to the case of AXps, a
weak contrastive explanation

(WCXp) is a set of features
{\mathcal{S}}
such that the prediction can
change
when the features not in
{\mathcal{S}}
are assigned the values dictated by

\mathbf{v}
:

\exists(\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}).\left(\land_{i\in{\mathcal{F}}\setminus{\mathcal{S}}}x_{i}=v_{i}\right)\land\left(\kappa(\mathbf{x})\not=\kappa(\mathbf{v})\right)

(7)

A
contrastive explanation
(CXp) is a subset-minimal WCXp.
(Weak) (abductive/contrastive) explanations are associated with
predicates
\mathsf{WAXp},\mathsf{AXp},\mathsf{WCXp},\mathsf{CXp}:2^{{\mathcal{F}}}\to\{0,1\}
that hold
true when the respective condition is satisfied.
Progress in formal explainability is summarized in recent
overviews (
Marques-Silva and
Ignatiev 2022
;
Marques-Silva 2022
;

Darwiche 2023
).

A set of features is an AXp iff it is a minimal-hitting set (MHSes) of
the set of CXps
(
Ignatiev et al. 2020
)
. Similarly, a set of features is
a CXp iff it is an MHS of the set of AXps. This result is referred to as
MHS-duality, and builds on Reiter’s seminal work on model-based
diagnosis
(
Reiter 1987
)
.

Feature (ir)relevancy.

The set of features that are included in at least one (abductive)
explanation are defined as follows:

\mathfrak{F}({\mathcal{E}}):=\{i\in{\mathcal{X}}\,|\,{\mathcal{X}}\in 2^{{\mathcal{F}}}\land\mathsf{AXp}({\mathcal{X}})\}

(8)

(It is known that
\mathfrak{F}({\mathcal{E}})
remains unchanged if CXps
are used instead of AXps
(
Ignatiev et al. 2020
)
.)
Finally, a feature
i\in{\mathcal{F}}
is
irrelevant
, i.e. predicate

\textnormal{{{Irrelevant}}}(i)
holds true, if
i\not\in\mathfrak{F}({\mathcal{E}})
;
otherwise feature
i
is
relevant
, and predicate
\textnormal{{{Relevant}}}(i)

holds true.
Clearly, given some explanation problem
{\mathcal{E}}
,

\forall(i\in{\mathcal{F}}).\textnormal{{{Irrelevant}}}(i)\leftrightarrow\neg\textnormal{{{Relevant}}}(i)\,
.

A priori voting power.

A priori voting power (
Felsenthal and
Machover 2004
;

Felsenthal and
Machover 2015
) measures how each voter,
taken from some assembly of voters, has the potential to impact the
voting outcomes of coalitions made of voters from that assembly.
A
weighted voting game
(WVG) is defined for an assembly of
voters
{\mathcal{F}}=\{1,\ldots,m\}
, where each voter
i\in{\mathcal{F}}
has
some voting power
w_{i}
(which we assume to be a non-negative
integer). Furthermore, the game is characterized by a quota

Q\leq\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{F}}}w_{i}
. A subset of the voters

{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq{\mathcal{F}}
is a coalition voting the same way,
i.e. either in favor or against. A coalition is a
winning
coalition
if
\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{S}}}w_{i}\geq{Q}
; otherwise it is a losing
coalition.
A WVG
{\mathcal{W}}
is represented by

[Q;w_{1},\ldots,w_{m}]

(
Felsenthal and
Machover 2015
)
.
Given a WVG, a
power index
(or just
index
),

\mathsf{Idx}:{\mathcal{F}}\to\mathbb{R}
, maps each voter to a real number, measuring
the voter’s capacity to influence voting decisions relative to other voters.
In the more general setting of characteristic function
games
(
Chalkiadakis, Elkind, and
Wooldridge 2012
)
, power indices are referred to as

payoff vectors
.

Related Work

Power indices.

Work on a priori voting power traces back at least to the mid
1940s
(
Penrose 1946
)
.
Over the years, different power indices have been proposed as
mechanisms for assigning relative importance to voting members of an
assembly.
While comprehensive lists of power indices
exist (
Andjiga, Chantreuil, and
Lepelley 2003
;
Felsenthal and
Machover 2004
;

Felsenthal and
Machover 2015
), in this
paper we only consider some of the best-known power indices:
Shapley-Shubik
(
Shapley and Shubik 1954
)
,
Banzhaf
(
Banzhaf III 1965
)
,
Deegan-Packel
(
Deegan and Packel 1978
)
,
Johnston
(
Johnston 1978
)
,
Holler-Packel
(
Holler and Packel 1983
)
,
Andjiga
(
Andjiga, Chantreuil, and
Lepelley 2003
)
, and the
Responsibility index
(
Biradar et al. 2024
)
(which can be related with
earlier work
(
Chockler, Halpern, and
Kupferman 2008
)
).
For more details on power indices the reader is invited to look at the more general
setting of cooperative game theory
(
Chalkiadakis, Elkind, and
Wooldridge 2012
)
.

Axiomatic aggregations.

Motivated by a growing number of examples where SHAP scores yield
misleading relative feature importance, prior work has proposed
ways of computing measures of relative feature importance that address
the limitations of existing SHAP scores, namely
formal feature
attribution
(
Yu, Ignatiev, and
Stuckey 2023
;
Yu et al. 2023
),
and selected
axiomatic aggregation schemes
(
Biradar et al. 2023
;

Biradar et al. 2024
), all of which are based on abductive
explanations.
Even more importantly, recent work has demonstrated that formal feature
attribution corresponds to some proposed axiomatic
aggregation schemes
(
Biradar et al. 2024
)
, and that these correspond to a few
well-known power indices (
Deegan and Packel 1978
;

Holler and Packel 1983
;
Chockler, Halpern, and
Kupferman 2008
).

Corrected SHAP scores.

Other recent work observes that the problems with existing
SHAP scores result from the characteristic function
used
(
Létoffé, Huang, and
Marques-Silva 2024
)
, and proposes alternative characteristic
functions, which eliminate the known limitations of SHAP scores.

4
Formalizations of FISs

This section formalizes the best-known power indices, but targets
the context of explainability by feature attribution. The proposed
formalizations will be referred to as
Feature Importance Scores

(FISs).

Why power indices for feature attribution?

Most of the existing work on using Shapley values in explainability
relates with the original work of L. Shapley for assigning values to
the players of an
n
-player cooperative game. Furthermore, and as
noted earlier in
Section

2
, the characteristic function used
in the case of explainability has been the expected value of the ML
model (
Lundberg and Lee 2017
;
Van den Broeck et al. 2022
;

Arenas et al. 2023
).
3
3

3

For classification problems with unordered classes, the expected value would be ill-defined.

Recent work demonstrated that such characteristic function can produce
unsatisfactory relative orders of feature importance.
4
4

4

It should be unsurprising that not all characteristic
functions may be suitable for the purposes of explainability by
feature attribution using Shapley values. The question is then which
characteristic function(s) might be the most adequate.

In fact, it is apparent that feature importance is far more related
with a priori voting power than with arbitrary
n
-player games,
which impose no restrictions on the characteristic function used.
This relationship is exploited for example in recent
work
(
Biradar et al. 2024
)
.
Moreover, early work on voting power was clear about how to measure the
importance of each voter in voting games. Indeed, and quoting
from
(
Shapley and Shubik 1954
)
:
“
Our definition of the power of an individual member depends on
the chance he has of being critical to the success of a winning
coalition
”. All of the power indices briefly summarized above
explicitly measure the importance of an individual member (in our case a feature) at being

critical
.
The same insight can be used in explainability by feature attribution.
We can view feature attribution as a voting game, as follows. A set of
fixed features represents a coalition. A set of fixed features is a
winning coalition if those features are sufficient for the given
prediction. Furthermore, and adapting the quote
above
(
Shapley and Shubik 1954
)
:
the importance of a feature
depends on the chance it has of being critical for a set of fixed
features to be sufficient for a given prediction.

Moreover, it is plain to conclude that power indices and their use in
explainability will yield fundamentally different measures of feature
importance than what earlier work on SHAP scores
did
(
Lundberg and Lee 2017
)
.
Finally, one could argue that capturing feature attribution by
mimicking power indices should not be warranted. However, as the most
widely used version of SHAP scores confirms, by choosing a problematic
characteristic function, one may obtain unsatisfactory results.

Why FISs?

In cooperative game theory, each characteristic function defines a
concrete game. In this case, the Shapley values assign relative
importance to each player, for the given game
(
Shapley 1953
)
.
In the case of a priori voting power, the (usually implicit)
characteristic function captures the notion of a voter being critical
for a winning coalition; this is often reflected in terms of the
winning coalitions that are accounted for.
In the case of feature attribution for XAI, the situation is somewhat
more complex. We adopt the approach used for power indices, namely we
will look at the cases when a feature is critical for a winning
coalition (i.e. fixed features sufficient for prediction), and such
that such coalition may or may not be subset-minimal.
In addition, we will adapt the best-known power indices. However, we
associate a parameterizable characteristic function with each FIS,
which may or may not be left implicit. Then, each explanation problem
is used to set the characteristic function to consider, and as a
result produce the FIS associated with the different features.

In contrast with other work
(
Biradar et al. 2024
)
, FISs will be
parameterized given an explanation problem and a chosen characteristic
function. Afterwards, we detail the assumptions that must be made to
obtain the indices proposed elsewhere (
Yu, Ignatiev, and
Stuckey 2023
;

Biradar et al. 2023
;
Yu et al. 2023
;

Biradar et al. 2024
).

Choosing a characteristic function.

The recently proposed axiomatic aggregations based on AXps are special

\displaystyle\mathsf{Sc}_{R}(i;{\mathcal{E}})

Examples of unsatisfactory attributions.

For some of the scores studied in recent work

Example 1

4.4
Well-Known Power Indices as FISs

We now show how FISs can be obtained from the Shapley-Shubik, Banzhaf,

FISs based on abductive explanations.

The simple characteristic function proposed in
Section

4.3

serves for capturing both formal feature
attribution (
Yu, Ignatiev, and
Stuckey 2023
;
Yu et al. 2023
)
and axiomatic aggregations (
Biradar et al. 2023
;

Biradar et al. 2024
), all of which are based on AXps. However, the
same characteristic function
cannot
be used to capture either
the Shapley-Shubik or the Banzhaf indices (and this may indirectly
justify why these power indices have not been related with AXps
before).

As a result, we introduce a somewhat different characteristic
function:

\upsilon_{w}({\mathcal{S}};{\mathcal{E}}):=\textnormal{ITE}(\mathsf{WAXp}({\mathcal{S}};{\mathcal{E}}),1,0)
.
(As shown next,
\upsilon_{w}
enables capturing both the Shapley-Shubik
and the Banzhaf FISs, among others.)

Given
\upsilon_{w}
, we will obtain
\Delta_{i}({\mathcal{S}})=1
only for sets

{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq{\mathcal{F}}
such that,

\mathsf{WAXp}({\mathcal{S}})\land\neg\mathsf{WAXp}({\mathcal{S}}\setminus\{i\})
. (Evidently, we
can restrict further those sets with additional constraints.)
Observe that this definition of the characteristic function captures
the cases where feature
i
is
critical
for making
{\mathcal{S}}
a

winning coalition
, and so naturally relates with the intended
goals of Shapley-Shubik’s index
(
Shapley and Shubik 1954
)
.
However, the modified definition does not equate with
{\mathcal{S}}
being
an AXp. Indeed, feature
i
may be critical, but other features may
not be critical, and so
{\mathcal{S}}
might not be an AXp.

Given a set of features
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq{\mathcal{F}}
and a feature

i\in{\mathcal{F}}
, with
i\in{\mathcal{S}}
, define the predicate denoting
whether a feature
i
is critical for
{\mathcal{S}}
,

\mathsf{Crit}(i,{\mathcal{S}};{\mathcal{E}}):=\mathsf{WAXp}({\mathcal{S}};{\mathcal{E}})\land\neg\mathsf{WAXp}({\mathcal{S}}\setminus\{i\};{\mathcal{E}})

(18)

As a result, we can instantiate the Shapley-Shubik, Banzhaf, Johnston
and Andjiga template scores as follows:

\displaystyle\mathsf{Sc}_{S}(i;{\mathcal{E}})

\displaystyle:=\mathsf{TSc}_{S}(i;{\mathcal{E}},\upsilon_{w})=

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq{\mathcal{F}}\land\mathsf{Crit}(i,{\mathcal{S}};{\mathcal{E}})}\left(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\left(|{\mathcal{F}}|\times\binom{|{\mathcal{F}}|-1}{|{\mathcal{S}}|-1}\right)}}\right)

\displaystyle\mathsf{Sc}_{B}(i;{\mathcal{E}})

\displaystyle:=\mathsf{TSc}_{B}(i;{\mathcal{E}},\upsilon_{w})=

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq{\mathcal{F}}\land\mathsf{Crit}(i,{\mathcal{S}};{\mathcal{E}})}\left(\nicefrac{{1}}{{2^{|{\mathcal{F}}|-1}}}\right)

\displaystyle\mathsf{Sc}_{J}(i;{\mathcal{E}})

\displaystyle:=\mathsf{TSc}_{J}(i;{\mathcal{E}},\upsilon_{w})=

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq{\mathcal{F}}\land\mathsf{Crit}(i,{\mathcal{S}};{\mathcal{E}})}\left(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\Delta({\mathcal{S}})}}\right)

\displaystyle\mathsf{Sc}_{A}(i;{\mathcal{E}})

\displaystyle:=\mathsf{TSc}_{A}(i;{\mathcal{E}},\upsilon_{w})=

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq{\mathcal{F}}\land\mathsf{Crit}(i,{\mathcal{S}};{\mathcal{E}})}\left(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\left(|{\mathcal{S}}|\times|\mathbb{WA}|\right)}}\right)

As can be concluded, by selecting a suitable characteristic function,
we are able to define feature importance scores that mimic some of the best-known
indices in a priori voting power.

9

9

It should be noted that we could also use
\upsilon_{w}
to

5
Proposed Properties for FISs

As observed earlier, FISs raise a number of challenges not
faced either by cooperative games or by power indices. More
concretely, in the case of FISs, the selected characteristic
function will depend on the target explanation problem and so also on
the ML model being explained.
As a result, the goal of faithfully capturing feature importance
requires identifying additional properties, in addition to those that
Shapley values in cooperative games are well-known for.
10
10

10

Different FISs may yield different rankings of features,
similarly to what is observed with power
indices (
Freixas 2010
;
Doignon, Moretti, and
Öztürk 2022
).

This explains in part why recent work
(
Biradar et al. 2024
)
also
proposed additional properties in addition to those that characterize
Shapley values in cooperative game theory.

5.1
Properties Targeting Cooperative Games

The best-known properties for values assigned to players of
cooperative games are those proposed by L. Shapley

(
Shapley 1953
)
. We start by overviewing well-known properties
for indices (and so for template scores), which are parameterized on
the explanation problem and (every) characteristic function.
(We adopt the presentation from
(
Chalkiadakis, Elkind, and
Wooldridge 2012
)
.)

P01: Efficiency.

A cooperative game is efficient if,

\sum\nolimits_{i\in{\mathcal{F}}}\sv(i;{\mathcal{E}},\upsilon)=\upsilon({\mathcal{F}})-\upsilon(\emptyset)

In game theory, it is often assumed that
\upsilon(\emptyset)=0

(e.g.
(
Shapley 1953
)
). However, in the case of SHAP scores,

\upsilon(\emptyset)\not=0
. As a result, claims of efficiency for SHAP
scores usually ignore the term
\upsilon(\emptyset)
.

P02: Symmetry.

Two elements
i,j\in{\mathcal{F}}
are symmetric if

\upsilon({\mathcal{S}}\cup\{i\})=\upsilon({\mathcal{S}}\cup\{j\})
for

{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq{\mathcal{F}}\setminus\{i,j\}
.
An index respects the property of symmetry if, for any
{\mathcal{E}}
and
for any symmetric elements
i,j\in{\mathcal{F}}
,

\sv(i;{\mathcal{E}},\upsilon)=\sv(j;{\mathcal{E}},\upsilon)
.

P03: Additivity.

Let
\upsilon_{1}
and
\upsilon_{2}
represent two characteristic functions.
Furthermore, for any
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq{\mathcal{F}}
, let

\upsilon_{1{+}2}({\mathcal{S}})=\upsilon_{1}({\mathcal{S}})+\upsilon_{2}({\mathcal{S}})
.
An index respects the property of additivity if for
i\in{\mathcal{F}}
,

\mathsf{Sc}_{1{+}2}(i;{\mathcal{E}},\upsilon_{1{+}2})=\mathsf{Sc}_{1}(i;{\mathcal{E}},\upsilon_{1})+\mathsf{Sc}_{2}(i;{\mathcal{E}},\upsilon_{2})
.

P04: Dummy player.

For any element
i\in{\mathcal{F}}
, if
\upsilon({\mathcal{S}})=\upsilon({\mathcal{S}}\cup\{i\})

for any
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq{\mathcal{F}}
, then
i
is a
dummy player

(or element).
An index respects the dummy player property if for any dummy element

i\in{\mathcal{F}}
,
\sv(i)=0
.

5.2
Properties Devised for Axiomatic Aggregations

Work on axiomatic aggregations
(
Biradar et al. 2024
)
proposed a number
of properties that scores should respect. The properties described
below are of interest to the purposes of our work, and apply to
FISs.

P05: Minimal monotonicity.

Another property proposed for axiomatic
aggregations
(
Biradar et al. 2024
)
requires that, given any classifier,
whenever the set of AXps for feature
i
is included in the set of
AXps for feature
j
, then the score for
i
should be no greater than
the score for
j
,

\forall(i,j\in{\mathcal{F}}).(\mathbb{A}_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{A}_{j})\limply\sv(i;{\mathcal{E}},\upsilon)\leq\sv(j;{\mathcal{E}},\upsilon)

P06:
\gamma
-efficiency.

A final property used for axiomatic aggregations generalizes the
notion of efficiency.
Let
\gamma(;{\mathcal{E}})\in\mathbb{R}
. A score is
\gamma
-efficient if,

\sum\nolimits_{i\in{\mathcal{F}}}(\sv(i;{\mathcal{E}},\kappa))=\gamma(;{\mathcal{E}})

\gamma
-efficiency can be viewed as a mechanism of relaxing the
property of efficiency (see P01 above), and has been the subject of past research
(
Dubey, Neyman, and Weber 1981
)
.

Other properties.

We will not adopt other properties discussed in
(
Biradar et al. 2024
)
.
Some of the remaining properties, e.g.
null feature
, are
special cases of the properties discussed in the remainder of this
section.

5.3
Novel Properties Targeting Scores

As noted earlier in the paper, FISs can be viewed as a generalization
of power indices. Whereas the ML model can be viewed as representing
the weighted voting game, for each ML model there are many different
instances that need to be explained.

P07: Independence from class labeling.

Given a classifier
{\mathcal{M}}=({\mathcal{F}},\mathbb{F},{\mathcal{K}},\kappa)
, a class
relabeling consists in defining a new classifier

{\mathcal{M}}=({\mathcal{F}},\mathbb{F},{\mathcal{K}}^{\prime},\kappa^{\prime})
, where the elements of

{\mathcal{K}}^{\prime}
are related with the elements of
{\mathcal{K}}
by a bijection

\sigma:{\mathcal{K}}\to{\mathcal{K}}^{\prime}
, such that

\forall(\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}).(\kappa^{\prime}(\mathbf{x})=\sigma(\kappa(\mathbf{x})))
.
For
{\mathcal{M}}
and
{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}
, the respective explanation problems are

{\mathcal{E}}
and
{\mathcal{E}}^{\prime}
.

Let
\sv
and
\sv^{\prime}
represent two FISs defined, respectively, on

{\mathcal{E}}
and
{\mathcal{E}}^{\prime}
.
Then,
\sv
respects independence from class labeling if

\forall(i\in{\mathcal{F}}).(\sv(i;{\mathcal{E}})=\sv(i;{\mathcal{E}}^{\prime}))
.

P08: Consistency with relevancy.

An FIS is consistent with relevancy if, for any explanation problem

{\mathcal{E}}
,
\forall(i\in{\mathcal{F}}).(\textnormal{{{Relevant}}}(i)\lequiv\sv(i;{\mathcal{E}})\not=0
,
i.e. a feature takes a non-zero FIS iff it is relevant.
One can readily conclude that property P08 generalizes the
null
feature
property proposed in
(
Biradar et al. 2024
)
.

We underline that properties P07 and P08 should both be deemed

mandatory
. Otherwise, as shown in recent
work (
Huang and Marques-Silva 2024
;
Marques-Silva and Huang 2024
), one can construct
examples where relative feature importance is misleading.

P09: Consistency with duality.

MHS duality plays a key role in the enumeration of AXps and CXps.
Furthermore, it is known that CXps can be easier to enumerate than
AXps
(
Huang et al. 2021
)
. As a result, it is of interest to know
whether using (W)CXps instead of (W)AXps changes the computed FISs.
A dual characteristic function is obtained by replacing the references to
(W)AXps by references to (W)CXps. Similarly, a dual template score is
obtained by replacing the references to (W)AXps by references to
(W)CXps.
Moreover, a dual FIS is obtained by replacing the template score with
its dual template score, and using a dual characteristic function.

Given the above-defined characteristic function
\upsilon_{w}
, the
resulting dual characteristic function
\upsilon^{d}_{w}
is:

\upsilon^{d}_{w}({\mathcal{S}};{\mathcal{E}}):=\textnormal{ITE}(\mathsf{WCXp}({\mathcal{S}};{\mathcal{E}}),1,0)
.
Furthermore, for defining the dual template scores, sets defined using
AXps will be replaced by sets defined using CXps. As an example, the
dual predicate for a critical feature becomes,

\mathsf{Crit}_{d}(i,{\mathcal{S}};{\mathcal{E}}):=\mathsf{WCXp}({\mathcal{S}};{\mathcal{E}})\land\neg\mathsf{WCXp}({\mathcal{S}}\setminus\{i\};{\mathcal{E}})

(19)

When addressing duality, different forms of equivalence may or may not
be obtained.
Two FISs
\sv
and
\sv^{\prime}
are
equivalent
if they do not differ
by more than a strictly positive multiplicative constant. Formally,

\exists(\alpha>0)\forall({\mathcal{E}})\forall(i\in{\mathcal{F}}).\sv^{\prime}(i;{\mathcal{E}})=\alpha\times\sv(i;{\mathcal{E}})
.
Two FISs
\sv
and
\sv^{\prime}
are

8
Conclusions

Despite their widespread use,
SHAP scores
(
Lundberg and Lee 2017
)
can produce
inadequate measures of relative feature importance in XAI by feature
attribution
(
Marques-Silva and Huang 2024
)
. This is explained by the characteristic
function used for computing SHAP scores.

Contributions.

Motivated by recent work, this paper: i) develops an in-depth analysis
of applying power indices in XAI by feature attribution; ii) proposes
a novel framework for creating new scores measuring feature
importance in XAI; iii) proposes properties that such scores should exhibit;
iv) develops novel scores; and v) characterizes the different scores
in terms of the properties they exhibit.
Furthermore, the paper reveals novel connections between: i) automated
reasoning and its uses in logic-based abduction; ii) game theory and
its applications in a priori voting power and more generally in
characteristic function games; and iii) logic-based explainability and
its usage in machine learning.

Research directions.

Several topics of research can be envisioned.
First, address the complexity of computing
the FISs studied in the paper and other recent
work
(
Biradar et al. 2024
)
. This may enable choosing the most adequate
FIS, given the properties it exhibits and the computational complexity
for its computation.
Second, analyze the many possible FISs that can be envisioned, either
by devising novel characteristic functions, and relating them with the
template scores proposed in this paper, or by proposing new template
scores.
Third, devise new properties, aiming at developing a fine-grained
axiomatic but rigorous characterization of relative feature importance
in explainability.
Fourth, and finally, propose ways for the rigorous approximation of
FISs, namely when analyzing complex ML models.
