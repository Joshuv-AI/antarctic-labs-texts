A Rigorous Derivation of the Functional Renormalisation Group Equation
Jobst Ziebell 0000-0002-9715-6356 Theoretisch-Physikalisches Institut, Friedrich-Schiller-University, Jena, Germany

Abstract
The functional renormalisation group equation is derived in a mathematically rigorous fashion in a framework suitable for the Osterwalder-Schrader formulation of quantum field theory. To this end, we devise a very general regularisation scheme which retains reflection positivity as well as the infinite degrees of freedom including smoothness. Furthermore, it is shown how the classical limit is altered by the regularisation process leading to an inevitable breaking of translation invariance. We also give precise conditions for the convergence of the obtained theories upon removal of the regularisation.

1
Introduction

It is still unknown whether interacting and non-perturbative quantum field theories encoding relevant physical phenomena can be given precise mathematical meaning.
Most prominently, it is unclear how to proceed from perturbative calculations to exact ones except in some special cases such as conformal field theories.
A much celebrated result that is frequently used is the functional renormalisation group equation
[
Ell94
,
MOR94
]
(FRGE) in the form presented by Wetterich
[
Wet93
]
.
It is generally accepted as an exact equation encoding the flow from a classical action functional to its quantum counterpart and many results have been derived or conjectured from its exact properties or practical approximations.
Some notable reviews include
[
Del12
,
Dup+20
,
Gie12
]
.
However, the derivation of the FRGE has not yet been put into a mathematically satisfactory form but has remained restricted to formal respectively perturbative proofs
[
KKS97
]
.

In this work, we investigate the case of a real scalar field on Euclidean spacetime.
In the framework of the Osterwalder-Schrader theorem
[
OS73
,
OS75
]
as presented by Glimm and Jaffe
[
GJ12
]
it is modelled by a probability measure on the space of tempered distributions.
However, starting from a classical action it is not generally known how to define a corresponding measure.
Instead, it is customary to produce well-defined regularised theories and investigate the convergence of the corresponding measures upon the removal of the regularisation.
In this work we shall investiate how this procedure can be made to work in tandem with Wetterich’s equation.

We begin by listing some basic tools in section
2
that are necessary for the overall analysis.
In particular, we cover basic features of Radon Gaußian measures on locally convex spaces along with some standard results in convex analysis.
In section
3
we generalise the concept of lower semicontinuous envelopes and introduce the property of supercoercivity.
The latter enables a direct computation of a lower semicontinuous envelope.

Section
4
then studies the convergence of measures on the space of tempered distributions in terms of convergence of dual objects that are fundamental to Wetterich’s equation.
The connection is found to be given in terms of Mosco convergence and Attouch-Wets convergence, concepts which both enable a continuity theorem of the Legendre-Fenchel transform.

In section
5
we present a regularisation scheme tailored to the derivation of Wetterich’s equation.
ts interplay with reflection positivity is studied in section
6
and finally, the proof of Wetterich’s equation is given in section
7
.

The derivation may be summarised as follows:
We define a family
Z_{k}
of moment-generating functions (generating functionals) as well as their logarithms
W_{k}
of a regularised theory on a Hilbert completion of the space of tempered distributions.
Then, we proceed by showing that the corresponding Fréchet derivatives
DW_{k}
are linear bijections of Hilbert spaces such that the Legendre-Fenchel transforms (convex conjugates)
\Gamma_{k}
of
W_{k}
can be given in terms of
W_{k}
and
(DW_{k})^{-1}
.
It is then easily shown that
\Gamma_{k}
- also referred to as the
‘effective average action’
- satisfies a differential equation.
Furthermore, we compute the
‘classical limit’
of
\Gamma_{k}
which turns out to be the classical action modulo some contributions related to regularisation.

Finally, the full quantum field theory may be obtained by studying the convergence of the regularised effective average actions in terms of the result obtained in section
4
.
However, it it not clear whether - or in which cases - Wetterich’s equation itself survives the passage to the corresponding limit.

Definition 2.2
.

Remark 2.3

Let

(
[

A finite Radon measure on a locally convex space is uniquely determined by its characteristic function.

It is well known that Radon measures on locally convex spaces are supported on compactly embedded separable reflexive Banach spaces.

Definition 2.13
.

Let

A convex function is

Let

\phi\mapsto\sup_{T\in X}\left[\phi\left(T\right)-f\left(T\right)\right]

for all

.
If we equip

with a topology

\ast

We may then also define

]
.

(
[

)
.

Let

Theorem 2.23

Lemma 2.24
.

Let

be a Fréchet space.
Furthermore, let

Then

is continuous.

Proof.

By the pointwise convergence,

is clearly convex.
Hence, by theorem

is bounded from above on some open subset of

Let

A_{K,N}=\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq N}}f_{n}^{-1}\left(\left(-\infty,K\right]\right)

for

3
A Theorem on Lower Semicontinuous Envelopes

In the context of the Wetterich equation, a major role is played by the effective average action.
It is formally defined via the Legendre-Fenchel transform of the logarithm of the partition function which is convex and lower semicontinuous by lemma
2.7
.
Hence, it is clear that the study of the effective average action necessitates some features of convex analysis.

In this section we introduce the concepts of supercoercivity and lower semicontinuous envelopes and prove some simple results that the author believes to be novel.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
X
be a locally convex space and
f:X\to\bar{\mathbb{R}}
a convex function with

\sup_{x\in X}\left[p\left(x\right)-f\left(x\right)\right]<\infty

(15)

for all continuous seminorms
p
on
X
.
Then
f
is
supercoercive
.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
X
be a locally convex space,
Y
a normed space,
\iota:X\to Y
linear and continuous withe dense range and
f:X\to\bar{\mathbb{R}}
a convex and lower semicontinuous function.
Then the
lower semicontinuous envelope

LSC(f,\iota):Y\to\bar{\mathbb{R}}
of
f
with respect to
\iota
is given by

LSC(f,\iota)\left(x\right)=\inf\left\{\liminf_{n\to\infty}f\left(x_{n}\right)\,\big|\,(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\text{ in $X$ with }\lim_{n\to\infty}\left\|\iota\left(x_{n}\right)-x\right\|_{Y}=0\right\}

(16)

Remark 3.3
.

This definition is a generalisation of the one that is commonly used in literature, see e.g.
[
Zal02
]
.
It is easy to see that
LSC(f,\iota)
is lower semicontinuous.
Furthermore, if
\iota
is injective,
LSC(f,\iota)
is the largest lower semicontinuous function that is not greater than
f\circ\iota^{-1}
on
\iota(X)
.

The following lemma shows that our definition of lower semicontinuous envelopes plays well with Legendre-Fenchel conjugation.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
X
be a Hausdorff reflexive space,
p
a continuous seminorm on
X^{\prime}_{\beta}
,
\iota_{p}:X^{\prime}_{\beta}\to(X^{\prime}_{\beta})_{p}=Y
the natural map and
f:X\to\bar{\mathbb{R}}
a proper convex and lower semicontinuous function.
Let
f^{p}
denote the restriction of
f
to the Banach space
Y^{*}
considered as a subspace of
X
.
Then
LSC(f^{c},\iota_{p})^{c}=f^{p}
and if
f^{p}
is proper,
LSC(f^{c},\iota_{p})=(f^{p})^{c}|_{Y}
.

Remark 3.5
.

The transpose
{\vphantom{\!\iota}}{}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\iota_{p}:Y^{*}\to X
is injective by
[
SW99
, Chapter 4, §4, Corollary 2.3]
.

Proof.

By Fenchel-Moreau it suffices to show that
LSC(f^{c},\iota_{p})^{c}=f^{p}
.
Plugging in the definition of
LSC(f^{c},\iota_{p})^{c}
, for every
x\in Y^{*}
there is a sequence
(\phi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
Y
and a sequence
(\psi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
X^{\prime}_{\beta}
with
\lim_{n\to\infty}p(\phi_{n}-\iota_{p}\psi_{n})=0
such that

LSC(f^{c},\iota_{p})^{c}\left(x\right)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left[x\left(\phi_{n}\right)-f^{c}\left(\psi_{n}\right)\right]\,.

(17)

But since
x\in Y^{*}
, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left|x\left(\phi_{n}-\iota_{p}\psi_{n}\right)\right|\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}Cp\left(\phi_{n}-\iota_{p}\psi_{n}\right)=0

(18)

for some
C>0
.
Consequently,

LSC(f^{c},\iota_{p})^{c}\left(x\right)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left[x\left(\iota_{p}\psi_{n}\right)-f^{c}\left(\psi_{n}\right)\right]\leq f^{cc}\left({\vphantom{\!\iota}}{}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\iota_{p}x\right)=f^{p}\left(x\right)\,.

(19)

For the converse inequality, note that

\displaystyle LSC\left(f^{c},\iota_{p}\right)^{c}\left(x\right)

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{\phi\in X^{\prime}_{\beta}}\left[x\left(\iota_{p}\phi\right)-LSC(f^{c},\iota_{p})\left(\iota_{p}\phi\right)\right]

(20)

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{\phi\in X^{\prime}_{\beta}}\left[x\left(\iota_{p}\phi\right)-f^{c}\left(\phi\right)\right]=f^{cc}\left({\vphantom{\!\iota}}{}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\iota_{p}x\right)=f^{p}\left(x\right)\,.\qed

(21)

While this lemma demonstrates a useful property, it may in general be difficult to actually calculate the lower semicontinuous envelope of a given function.
In the supercoercive case, however, we obtain a particularly simple expression.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
X
be a Hausdorff reflexive space and
f:X\to\bar{\mathbb{R}}
a convex, lower semicontinuous and supercoercive function.
For any continuous seminorm
p
, let
\iota_{p}:X\to X_{p}
denote the natural map.
Then the lower semicontinuous envelope
g
of
f
with respect to
\iota_{p}
takes the form

g\left(x\right)=\begin{cases}\inf\left\{f\left(y\right):y\in\iota_{p}^{-1}\left(\left\{x\right\}\right)\right\}&x\in\iota_{p}\,,\left(X\right)\\
\infty&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(22)

and is supercoercive.

Proof.

It is immediately clear that
LSC(f,\iota_{p})(x)\leq g(x)
for all
x\in X_{p}
.
Suppose that
g(x)>LSC(f,\iota_{p})(x)
for some
x\in X_{p}
.
Then there is a sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
X
with
\lim_{n\to\infty}p(x-\iota_{p}x_{n})=0
such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}f(x_{n})<g(x)
.
If
x_{n}
is bounded in
X
there is a subnet
(y_{\alpha})
that is weakly converging to some
y\in X
with
x=\iota_{p}(y)
.
But
f
is weakly lower semicontinuous by
[
Zal02
, Theorem 2.2.1]
such that

f\left(y\right)\leq\lim_{\alpha}f\left(y_{\alpha}\right)=\lim_{n\to\infty}f\left(x_{n}\right)<g\left(x\right)\,,

(23)

which contradicts the definition of
g
.

If
x_{n}
is unbounded in
X
there is some subsequence
(y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and a continuous seminorm
q
on
X
with
\lim_{n\to\infty}q(y_{n})=\infty
.
By the supercoercivity of
f
, we must then have
\lim_{n\to\infty}f(x_{n})=\lim_{n\to\infty}f(y_{n})=\infty
which is again a contradiction.

For the supercoercivity, let
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be any sequence in
\iota_{p}(X)
.
Then for every
x_{n}
there is also some

with

\left|f\left(y_{n}\right)-g\left(x_{n}\right)\right|<\frac{1}{n}\,.

(24)

By the definition of
\iota_{p}
,
p(x_{n})=p(y_{n})
such that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\left[p\left(x_{n}\right)-g\left(x_{n}\right)\right]\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\left[p\left(y_{n}\right)-f\left(y_{n}\right)+\frac{1}{n}\right]<\infty

(25)

by the supercoercivity of
f
.
Because the sequence

was arbitrary,

is supercoercive.
∎

4
A Renormalisation Theorem

In 1973 Osterwalder and Schrader gave a rigorous interpretation of Quantum Field Theory as axiomatised by Gårding and Wightman
[
WG65
]
in terms of Wick-rotated correlation functions
[
OS73
,
OS75
]
.
A modern formulation in terms of a measure on a space of distributions may be found in the book by Glimm and Jaffe
[
GJ12
]
and a comprehensive introduction to the Gårding-Wightman axioms is given in the work by Jost
[
Jos65
]
.

The task of constructing measures reflecting physical aspects of reality typically requires a parameter-dependent regularisation.
The parameters can be thought of as a directed set of
‘windows of applicability’
and the corresponding measures are thought to reflect physics more or less accurately within such a window.
Common choices are the replacement of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with a finite lattice or the introduction of certain
‘cutoffs’
as in perturbative Quantum Field Theory, leading to models that agree well with experiments e.g within specific ranges of characteristic momenta
[p_{\mathrm{min}},p_{\mathrm{max}}]
.
The validity of this methodology comes from experiments in remarkable agreement with concrete calculations
[
HFG08
]
.

Collecting such regularised models in a box results in a net
(\omega_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in I}
of measures.
The big question that remains is whether it is possible to remove the regularisation completely, i.e to interpret the limit
\lim_{\alpha}\omega_{\alpha}
as a bona fide measure satisfying the Osterwalder-Schrader axioms.
Perhaps the most notable example where this program has worked out is the
\phi^{4}_{3}
model
[
BFS83
]
.

In this work, the relevant measures will live on
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\beta}
and we apply the mode of convergence given in definition
2.17
.

Theorem 4.1

(Lévy continuity theorem
[
Col66
]
)
.

Let
(\omega_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of Borel measures on
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\beta}
such that their characteristic functions converge pointwise to a function that is continuous at zero.
Then there is a Radon measure
\omega
on
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\beta}
such that
\omega_{n}
converges weakly to
\mu
.

However, in the context of Wetterich’s equation the objects under consideration are not the characteristic functions but rather the convex conjugates of the logarithms of corresponding moment-generating functions
Z_{\alpha}:\mathcal{S}\to\bar{\mathbb{R}}
with

\phi\mapsto\int_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\beta}}\exp\left[T\left(\phi\right)\right]\mathrm{d}\omega_{\alpha}\left(T\right)\,.

(26)

Consequently, we shall work out a convergence theorem that deals with (the logarithms of) moment-generating functions and their convex conjugates.

The convergence of convex conjugate functions in infinite dimensions was originally studied on reflexive Banach spaces by Mosco who introduced the now-called Mosco convergence
[
Mos71
]
.
It was later generalised to locally convex spaces by Beer and Borwein
[
BB90
]
, de Acosta
[

]
and Zabell
[

]
.
Following the latter, we give the following definition:

and since we shall not work on more general spaces, the given definitions suffice.

Definition 4.2
.

Let

that converges to

•

if for every

Clearly, Mosco convergence implies epi-convergence.

Using Mosco convergence it is possible to express a continuity theorem of the Legendre-Fenchel conjugation.
As Zabell proved in
[

]
, however, the Legendre-Fenchel conjugation is not a homeomorphism with respect to Mosco convergence.
A stronger notion offering this feature is given by the so-called

which we shall exploit in our final theorem.
Its precise formulation is somewhat complicated such that we refer to
[

]
for a definition.
In fact, we will not need to prove Attouch-Wets convergence from first principles, but only indirectly through the theorems presented in
[

]
such that a lack of definition appears tolerable to the author.
Another great achievement of the Attouch-Wets convergence, is its compatibility with pointwise convergence which was also worked out in
[

]

We may now formulate a sufficient condition for the weak convergence of a sequence of measures.

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
(\omega_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of Borel probability measures on
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\beta}
and
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
the corresponding moment-generating functions.
Suppose that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}Z_{n}\left(\phi\right)<\infty

(28)

for all
\phi\in\mathcal{S}
.
Then,
\omega_{n}
converges weakly to another Borel probability measure
\omega
if and only if

•

Z_{n}
Mosco converges to a convex, lower semicontinuous function
Z:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
and

•

for all continuous seminorms
p
on
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\beta}
the restrictions
Z_{n}^{p}
Mosco converge to the corresponding restriction
Z^{p}
.

Moreover, in the affirmative case
Z
is continuous and the moment-generating function of
\omega
.

Proof.

Suppose
\omega_{n}\to\omega
weakly and fix some
\phi\in\mathcal{S}
.
Then, we clearly have pointwise convergence of the characteristic functions
\widehat{\phi_{\ast}\omega_{n}}
of the one-dimensional pushforward measures to
\widehat{\phi_{\ast}\omega}
.
Because
Z_{n}(\phi)
and

are eventually finite it is known that
\widehat{\phi_{\ast}\omega_{n}}
has an analytic continuation
\overline{\phi_{\ast}\omega_{n}}
to

satisfying
[

]

\max\left\{Z_{n}\left(\phi\right),Z_{n}\left(-\phi\right)\right\}=\sup_{\left|z\right|\leq 1}\overline{\phi_{\ast}\omega_{n}}\left(z\phi\right)

(29)

where
z
is now a complex variable.
Hence the family
\{\overline{\phi_{\ast}\omega_{n}}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is eventually uniformly bounded within the unit ball of
\mathbb{C}
.
By the Vitali-Porter theorem
[

]
, pointwise convergence on the real axis implies pointwise convergence on the imaginary axis.
Since this is true for all
\phi\in\mathcal{S}
, we obtain pointwise convergence of
Z_{n}
to some real-valued function
Z
which is convex and continuous by lemma
2.24
.
This clearly implies that
Z_{n}
(M1)-converges to
Z
.
Moreover, by lemma
2.19
, the moment-generating function
M
of

is bounded by

and is, in particular, finite everywhere.
But then the analytic continuations of

and

\widehat{\phi_{\ast}\omega}
.
Consequently,

.
Regarding the (M2)-convergence, it is straightforward to see that the methods used for proving
[

apply also in our case such that for every weakly convergent sequence
\phi_{n}\to\phi
in
\mathcal{S}

\displaystyle Z(\phi)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\beta}}\exp\left[\phi\left(T\right)\right]\mathrm{d}\omega\left(T\right)=\int_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\beta}}\liminf_{n\to\infty,T^{\prime}\to T}\exp\left[\phi_{n}\left(T^{\prime}\right)\right]\mathrm{d}\omega\left(T\right)

(30)

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\beta}}\exp\left[\phi_{n}\left(T\right)\right]\mathrm{d}\omega_{n}\left(T\right)=\liminf_{n\to\infty}Z_{n}\left(\phi_{n}\right)\,,

where
T^{\prime}\to T
is considered in the topology of
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\beta}
.
The second equality follows from the boundedness of
(\phi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
by the definition of the strong dual topology.
Hence,
Z_{n}
(M2)-converges to
Z
.
Now, let

be a continuous seminorm on
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\beta}
.
Clearly
Z^{p}_{n}
(M2)-converges to

since every weakly convergent sequence in

is also weakly convergent in
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Finally, the pointwise convergence ensures (M1)-convergence of

to
Z^{p}
.

Conversely, assume that
Z_{n}
Mosco converges to
Z
and that the same holds for the corresponding restrictions as above.
For any

fix a continuous seminorm

on
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\beta}
such that

Then, by assumption,

and, in particular, also converges in the Painlevé-Kuratowski sense.
Furthermore, because all

are lower semicontinuous (see lemma

Since

by

.
Since,

.

In analogy to the first part of the proof, we may now use the pointwise convergence of
\overline{\phi_{\ast}\omega_{n}}
along the imaginary axis, the bound given in equation

and the Vitali-Porter theorem to conclude that

converges pointwise to some function

By theorem

By the continuity of

\displaystyle\sup_{\phi\in tU}\left|c\left(\phi\right)-c\left(0\right)\right|

A simple corollary to this theorem, is that we may in fact get rid of the Mosco convergence in

Corollary 4.8
.

Let
(\omega_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of Borel probability measures on
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\beta}
and
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
the corresponding moment-generating functions.
Suppose that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}Z_{n}\left(\phi\right)<\infty

(33)

for all
\phi\in\mathcal{S}
.
Then,
\omega_{n}
converges weakly to another Borel probability measure
\omega
if and only if there exists a lower semicontinuous, convex function
Z:\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
such that for all continuous norms
p
on
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\beta}
, the restrictions
Z_{n}^{p}
Mosco converge to
Z^{p}
.

Moreover, in the affirmative case
Z
is continuous and the moment-generating function of
\omega
.

Proof.

By theorem
4.7
, we just need to prove that the Mosco convergence of
Z_{n}^{p}
implies that of
Z_{n}
.
Since for every
\phi
there is a continuous seminorm
p
on
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\beta}
such that

, we clearly have that

(M1)-converges to

.

For the (M2)-convergence, note that for any weakly convergent sequence
\phi_{n}\to\phi
in
\mathcal{S}
, the set

B=\left\{\phi_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}\cup\left\{\phi\right\}\subset\mathcal{S}

(34)

is bounded and induces a continuous seminorm
q
on
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\beta}
.
Since,
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\beta}
is nuclear,
q
is majorised by some continuous Hilbert norm
p
.
Hence,
B
is a bounded subset of the reflexive Banach space

which is separable because

Consequently, every subsequence of
(\phi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
has a weakly convergent subsequence in

By assumption, it follows that
\phi_{n}
converges weakly to

The above corollary may appear rather inelegant and in fact we can do a lot better by using the following lemma.

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of proper convex and lower semicontinuous functions from
\mathcal{S}
to
\bar{\mathbb{R}}
.
Given another function
Z:\mathcal{S}\to\bar{\mathbb{R}}
, the following are equivalent:

(i)

For all continuous seminorms
p
on
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\beta}
, the restrictions
Z_{n}^{p}
converge uniformly on compact sets to
Z^{p}
.

(ii)

For all continuous seminorms
p
on
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\beta}
, the restrictions
Z_{n}^{p}
converge uniformly on bounded sets to
Z^{p}
.

Proof.

Let
p
be a continuous seminorm on
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\beta}
.
By nuclearity, there is a continuous seminorm
q>p
such that the natural map
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{q}\to\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{p}
has dense range and is nuclear, thus in particular is compact.
But then its transpose is compact and injective such that every bounded subset of
(\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{p})^{*}
is mapped injectively to a compact subset of
(\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{q})^{*}
on which we have uniform convergence.

The converse is clear, since every compact set is bounded.
∎

The uniform convergence on bounded sets enables the following corollary.

Corollary 4.10
.

Let
(\omega_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of Borel probability measures on
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\beta}
and
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

\limsup_{n\to\infty}Z_{n}(\phi)<\infty

\phi\in\mathcal{S}
and

converges weakly to another Borel probability measure
\omega
.

(ii)

There exists a convex and continuous function

p
on
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\beta}

Z^{p}_{n}
Attouch-Wets converge to
Z^{p}
.

(iii)

converges to some function

Moreover, in this case

is the moment-generating function of

.

Proof.

(i)\implies(ii)
: By corollary

, for all continuous seminorms
p
on
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}

.
In particular,

also converge to

in the Painlevé-Kuratowski sense which by

(ii)\implies(iii)

such that

Furthermore,

For the (M2)-convergence, note that any sequence

It would now be most tempting to conclude that there is some sort of Attouch-Wets topology on

However, to the knowledge of the author, there have not been many studies of generalisations of Attouch-Wets convergence to non-normed spaces.
Consequently, no such result is available at the time of this writing.
Before coming to the final theorem stating the weak convergence of measures in terms of Attouch-Wets convergence of conjugate functions, we need another short lemma.

Lemma 4.11
.

Let
f:\mathcal{S}\to\bar{\mathbb{R}}
be proper convex and lower semicontinuous.
Then
f
is a continuous function
\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
if and only if its convex conjugate (Legendre-Fenchel transform)

is supercoercive.

Proof.

Let
B\subseteq\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be balanced, bounded and hence precompact.
Hence, by Fenchel-Moreau,

\sup_{\phi\in B}f\left(\phi\right)=\sup_{\phi\in B,T\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\beta}}\left[T\left(\phi\right)-f^{c}\left(T\right)\right]=\sup_{T\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\beta}}\left[p_{B}\left(T\right)-f^{c}\left(T\right)\right]\,,

(36)

where
p_{B}
is the continuous seminorm induced by
B
.

\Rightarrow
:
Since all continuous seminorms
p
are bounded by some

the implication is clear.

\Leftarrow
:
Equation

shows that
f
is bounded on bounded subsets of
\mathcal{S}
.
Hence, it is finite everywhere and theorem
2.23
applies.
∎

With this lemma at hand, we arrive at the final theorem of this section with immediate relevance for the Wetterich equation.

Theorem 4.12
.

Let
(\omega_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of Borel probability measures on
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\beta}
,
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
the corresponding moment-generating functions and set

Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

\limsup_{n\to\infty}W_{n}(\phi)<\infty

5
A Functional Regularisation Scheme

The goal of Quantum Field Theory à la Osterwalder-Schrader can be phrased in making sense of Euclideanised Feynman integrals.
A typical example is the moment-generating function

Z\left(J\right)=\int\exp\left[-S\left(\psi\right)+\int J\psi\right]\mathrm{d}\psi\,,

(37)

where

•

the integral is taken over some path space,

•

the measure is taken to be translation invariant,

•

J
lies in a dual space of the configuration space and

•

S
is the Euclideanised classical action typically containing terms like
\int\psi^{4}
.

In view of the Osterwalder-Schrader theorem one would like the integral to run over
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\beta}
, which

•

is infinite-dimensional,

•

admits no non-trivial translation invariant measure and

•

makes expressions like
\psi^{4}
generally ill-defined.

Hence, one typically regularises the path space in some fashion e.g by restricting to a finite volume and enforcing a momentum cutoff, rendering the regularised path space finite-dimensional
[
RS18
, Chapter 8]
.
As outlined in the last section the resulting generating functions
Z
respectively the corresponding measures are then interpreted as to reflect real physics to a degree where the regularisation is assumed to introduce only negligible effects, e.g

•

physics in a volume much smaller than the finite volume introduced by the regularisation,

•

physical phenomena with characteristic momentum scales much smaller than the enforced cutoff.
2
2

2

These conditions are debatable in view of the Euclideanised theory not living on physical Minkowski space.

These particular regularisations define a directed subset of
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
where

\left(V,p_{\mathrm{max}}\right)\leq\left(V^{\prime},p^{\prime}_{\mathrm{max}}\right)\qquad\Leftrightarrow\qquad V\leq V^{\prime}\quad\text{and}\quad p_{\mathrm{max}}\leq p^{\prime}_{\mathrm{max}}\,.

(38)

Hence, all correspondingly regularised measures can be collected into a net
\omega_{V,p_{\mathrm{max}}}
and we may interpret the requirement that they should describe
the same physics
within their respective window of applicability as a kind of gluing instruction.
But this implies that for ever larger volumes and momentum cutoffs one expects to approach a limit that encompasses
all
physical phenomena.
If this limit is indeed a Quantum Field Theory, we may phrase this requirement as the existence of a measure
\omega
satisfying the Osterwalder-Schrader axioms and

\lim_{V,p_{\mathrm{max}}\to\infty}\omega_{V,p_{\mathrm{max}}}=\omega

(39)

in some appropriate sense.
As is well-known, the existence of such a limit generally requires
renormalising

S
, e.g in the case of having a term
\lambda\int\psi^{4}
, promoting
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
to a function depending on
V
and
p_{\mathrm{max}}
.

It is quite clear that the choice of regularisation scheme immensely affects concrete calculations.
For instance, the finite volume setting is typically implemented as a compactification of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to a torus leading to discrete eigenvalues of the Laplacian, i.e discrete admissible momenta.
The personal belief of the author, however, is that a regularisation scheme maintaining as much smoothness as possible is desirable for computational and analytic methods alike.
One such scheme is the following, where the limit
n\to\infty
corresponds to the removal of the regularistion:

1.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
split
S_{n}
into a free part
S_{n}^{\mathrm{free}}
and an interacting part
S_{n}^{\mathrm{int}}
such that

S_{n}^{\mathrm{free}}:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\phi\mapsto\left(\phi,B_{n}\phi\right)

(40)

for some continuous, bijective, linear operator
B_{n}
on
\mathcal{S}
which is bounded from below in the sense that there is an
\eta_{n}>0
such that for all
\phi\in\mathcal{S}

\left(\phi,B_{n}\phi\right)\geq\eta_{n}\left(\phi,\phi\right)\,.

(41)

The prototypical example is of course
B_{n}=m^{2}-\Delta
for a free, scalar, massive field theory on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Define the corresponding centred Radon Gaußian probability measure
\mu_{n}
on
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\beta}
given by its characteristic function

\hat{\mu}_{n}\left(\phi\right)=\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\left(\phi,B_{n}^{-1}\phi\right)\right]\,.

(42)

for all
\phi\in\mathcal{S}
.
Then,
\mu_{n}
encodes the free theory determined by
B_{n}
.

2.

Fix a sequence
(\mathcal{R}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of linear and continuous operators
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\beta}\to\mathcal{S}
with

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\iota\mathcal{R}_{n}\iota\right)\left(\phi\right)\left(\psi\right)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\mathcal{R}_{n}\iota\phi,\psi\right)=\left(\phi,\psi\right)=\iota\left(\phi\right)\left(\psi\right)

(43)

for all
\phi,\psi\in\mathcal{S}
.
A simple example is given by choosing

\displaystyle\chi_{n}\left(x\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\left\|x\right\|^{2}}{n^{2}K^{2}}\right]

(44)

\displaystyle\xi_{n}\left(x\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{n^{2}\Lambda^{2}}{2\pi}\right)^{d/2}\exp\left[-\frac{n^{2}\Lambda^{2}}{2}\left\|x\right\|^{2}\right]

for some
K,\Lambda>0
, all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and setting
\mathcal{R}_{n}T=\chi_{n}\cdot(\xi_{n}\ast T)
where
\ast
denotes convolution of functions.
Then,
n\Lambda
can be viewed as a momentum cutoff and
nK
as the radius of the ball to which we restrict the theory.
The contributions from outside these physical windows are heavily suppressed by the exponential decays of
\chi_{n}
and
\xi_{n}
respectively.

Furthermore, these particular
\mathcal{R}_{n}
commute with
\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that the rotational invariance is kept intact.
They also satisfy the stronger condition that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\iota\circ\mathcal{R}_{n}=\mathrm{id}
uniformly on bounded sets which may be beneficial in specific applications.
However, it can be shown that they break reflection positivity as is explained in section
6
.

3.

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
define the Radon probability measures
\nu_{n}=(\mathcal{R}_{n})_{*}\mu_{n}
and

\omega_{n}=\iota_{\ast}\left(\frac{\exp\left[-S_{n}^{\mathrm{int}}\right]}{\int\exp\left[-S_{n}^{\mathrm{int}}\right]\mathrm{d}\nu_{n}}\cdot\nu_{n}\right)

(45)

on
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\beta}
respectively.

In analogy to equation
39
, we now have a sequence
(\omega_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of measures corresponding to regularised theories and the ultimate goal is to find a limit
\omega
satisfying the Osterwalder-Schrader axioms.

Remark 5.1
.

The use of the
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
inner product in equations
41
and
45
could be generalised to any other continuous inner product on
\mathcal{S}
.
However, in most applications the
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
one suffices and is the simplest to work with such that there is hardly any practical benefit in admitting more general structures.

Remark 5.2
.

To demand that
\exp[-S_{n}^{\mathrm{int}}]\in L^{1}(\nu_{n})
is strictly necessary for this regularisation scheme to work.
As a matter of fact, this requirement is not trivial e.g. with regard to mass counterterms in the
\phi^{4}_{4}
modes.
It has, however, recently been proven in
[
HJZ23
, Theorem 1.1]
that it is indeed satisfied.

A Note on Reflection Positivity

Definition 6.1
.

Let
\mathcal{S}_{+}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
denote the set of Schwartz functions with support in
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
equipped with its subspace topology turning it into a reflexive nuclear space.
For any
\phi\in\mathcal{S}
, let
\theta\phi\in\mathcal{S}
with

\left(\theta\phi\right)\left(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{d}\right)=\phi\left(-x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{d}\right)

(46)

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Following
[
GJ12
]
, we call a finite Borel measure
\mu
on
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\beta}

reflection positive
if

\sum_{m,n=1}^{N}c_{m}^{*}c_{n}\hat{\mu}\left(\phi_{n}-\theta\phi_{m}\right)\geq 0

(47)

for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
as well as all sequences
(c_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathbb{C}
and
(\phi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathcal{S}_{+}
.

One can show that the simple example of
\mathcal{R}_{n}
given in the last section does not preserve reflection positivity in the sense that the resulting non-interacting measures
\iota_{*}\nu_{n}
are not reflection positive in general.
It is, however, possible to retain reflection possitivity if one is willing to break
\mathcal{O}(d)
covariance:

Example 6.2
.

First, define

\Phi\left(x\right)=\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\left\|x\right\|^{2}\right]\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Psi\left(x\right)=\begin{cases}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{x_{1}}\right]&x_{1}>0\\
0&\mathrm{else}\end{cases}\,.

(48)

Then, fix some
K,\Lambda,M>0
and set

\kappa_{n}\left(x\right)=\Psi\left(nMx\right)\,\text{,}\quad\xi_{n}(x)=\left(n\Lambda M\right)^{d}\frac{\Phi\left(n\Lambda x\right)\Psi(nMx)}{\left\|\Phi\Psi\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}}\,\text{,}\quad\chi_{n}\left(x\right)=\Phi\left(\frac{x}{nK}\right)

(49)

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Now, let

\mathcal{R}_{n}T=\kappa_{n}\cdot\chi_{n}\cdot\left(\theta\xi_{n}\ast T\right)+\theta\kappa_{n}\cdot\chi_{n}\cdot\left(\xi_{n}\ast T\right)

(50)

for all
T\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\beta}
.
Then, for any sequence
(\mu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of reflection positive finite Radon measures on
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\beta}
, the measures
\omega_{n}=\iota_{*}(\mathcal{R}_{n})_{*}\mu_{n}
are reflection positive,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\iota\circ\mathcal{R}_{n}\circ\iota\right)\left(\phi\right)=\iota(\phi)

(51)

for all
\phi\in\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{R}_{n}
commutes with spatial
\mathcal{O}(d-1)
rotations.

Proof.

That
\mathcal{R}_{n}
commutes with spatial
\mathcal{O}(d-1)
rotations follows directly from the corresponding invariance of
\Phi
and
\Psi
.
Furthermore, it is straightforward to verify that

{\vphantom{\!\mathcal{R}}}{}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\mathcal{R}_{n}\iota\phi=\xi_{n}\ast\left(\chi_{n}\cdot\kappa_{n}\cdot\phi\right)+\theta\xi_{n}\ast\left(\chi_{n}\cdot\theta\kappa_{n}\cdot\phi\right)

(52)

for all
\phi\in\mathcal{S}
from which it directly follows that
{\vphantom{\!\mathcal{R}}}{}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\mathcal{R}_{n}\circ\iota
commutes with
\theta
by the
\mathcal{O}(d)
invariance of
\chi_{n}
.
Consequently, for any
\phi,\psi\in\mathcal{S}
,

\hat{\omega}_{n}\left(\phi-\theta\psi\right)=\hat{\mu}_{n}\left({\vphantom{\!\mathcal{R}}}{}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\mathcal{R}_{n}\iota\phi-\theta{\vphantom{\!\mathcal{R}}}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\mathcal{R}_{n}\iota\psi\right)\,.

(53)

Now, reflection positivity follows if
{\vphantom{\!\mathcal{R}}}{}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\mathcal{R}_{n}\circ\iota
preserves the property of being supported in
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
.
This is easily seen since

\displaystyle\mathrm{supp}\,{\vphantom{\!\mathcal{R}}}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\mathcal{R}_{n}\iota\phi

\displaystyle=\mathrm{supp}\,\xi_{n}\ast\left(\chi_{n}\cdot\kappa_{n}\cdot\phi\right)

(54)

\displaystyle\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\,+\,\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{R}^{d-1}=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{R}^{d-1}

for any
\phi\in\mathcal{S}
supported in
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
.

To see the convergence, note that for any
\phi,\psi\in\mathcal{S}

\left(\iota\circ\mathcal{R}_{n}\circ\iota\right)\left(\phi\right)\left(\psi\right)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left[\kappa_{n}\cdot\chi_{n}\cdot\left(\theta\xi_{n}*\phi\right)\cdot\psi+\theta\kappa_{n}\cdot\chi_{n}\cdot\left(\xi_{n}*\phi\right)\cdot\psi\right]\,.

(55)

Finally, it is clear that

•

\xi_{n}*\phi\to\phi
and
\theta\xi_{n}*\phi\to\phi
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

•

\kappa_{n}\cdot\chi_{n}\cdot\psi\to\psi\cdot I(\mathbb{R}_{>0}\times\mathbb{R}^{d-1})
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

•

\theta\kappa_{n}\cdot\chi_{n}\cdot\psi\to\psi\cdot I(\mathbb{R}_{<0}\times\mathbb{R}^{d-1})
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

where
I(A)
denotes the indicator function of a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Consequently,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\iota\circ\mathcal{R}_{n}\circ\iota\right)\left(\phi\right)\left(\psi\right)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi\psi=\iota\left(\phi\right)\left(\psi\right)\,.

(56)

∎

Remark 6.3
.

Note that we do not have that
\iota\circ\mathcal{R}_{n}\to\mathrm{id}
.
Indeed, letting
\delta_{0}
be the Dirac distribution at zero, we have
\mathcal{R}_{n}\delta_{0}=0
for all

It is thus possible to define the operators

in a way that the resulting Gaussian measures are reflection positive.
When combining a reflection positive Gaussian measure with a density, some care must be taken to ensure that reflection positivity is maintained.
For some models on a lattice this is e.g. discussed in
[
GJ12

all work with the
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
inner product there is little practical reason to do so.

For brevity, define

\displaystyle N_{k}

\displaystyle=\int_{S}\exp\left[-S^{\mathrm{int}}\left(\psi\right)-\frac{1}{2}F_{k}\left(\psi,\psi\right)\right]\mathrm{d}\nu\left(\psi\right)\,,

(62)

\displaystyle f_{k}\left(\phi\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left[-S^{\mathrm{int}}\left(\phi\right)-\frac{1}{2}F_{k}\left(\phi,\phi\right)\right]

(63)

for all
k\in\mathbb{R}
and
\phi\in\mathcal{S}
.
Then, we may consider the family
\{f_{k}/N_{k}\cdot\nu:k\in\mathbb{R}\}
of probability measures on
\mathcal{S}
and in view of the last section the object of interest is
f_{0}/N_{0}\cdot\nu
.
By the properties of
S^{\mathrm{int}}
we clearly have that
f_{k}:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
is strictly positive and there exists a
q\in(1,\infty]
such that
f_{k}\in L^{q}(\nu)
for all
k\in\mathbb{R}
.
Let us now define a family
Z:\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\nu}\to\mathbb{R}
of moment-generating functions as

Z_{k}\left(T\right)=\frac{1}{N_{k}}\int_{\mathcal{S}}\exp\left[T\left(\psi\right)\right]f_{k}\left(\psi\right)\mathrm{d}\nu\left(\psi\right)\,.

(64)

By employing the Cameron-Martin theorem, we have

Z_{k}\left(T\right)=\frac{1}{N_{k}}\exp\left[\frac{1}{2}\left\langle T,T\right\rangle\right]\int_{\mathcal{S}}f_{k}\left(\psi+R_{\nu}T\right)\mathrm{d}\nu\left(\psi\right)

(65)

for all
k\in\mathbb{R}
and
T\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\nu}
such that
Z
is indeed well-defined (everywhere finite).
Also, by virtue of
[
Bog98
, Theorem 2.4.8]
each
Z_{k}:\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\nu}\to\mathbb{R}
is continuous.
A straightforward calculation - that we shall omit here - shows that one may differentiate under the integral sign:

Lemma 7.3
.

Z
is continuously Fréchet differentiable and its derivative at
(k,J)
is given by

D_{k,T}Z=\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{1}{2N_{k}}\int_{\mathcal{S}}F^{\prime}_{k}\left(\psi,\psi\right)\exp\left[T\left(\psi\right)\right]f_{k}\left(\psi\right)\mathrm{d}\nu\left(\psi\right)-Z_{k}\left(T\right)\partial_{k}\ln N_{k}\\
\frac{1}{N_{k}}\int_{\mathcal{S}}\psi\exp\left[T\left(\psi\right)\right]f_{k}\left(\psi\right)\mathrm{d}\nu\left(\psi\right)\end{pmatrix}

(66)

where the term in the second row (
D_{T}Z_{k}
) may be understood as a (generalised) Bochner integral in
\mathcal{S}

[
Tho75
, theorem 3]
and in fact
D_{T}Z_{k}\in H(\nu)
.

Let us also define
Y:\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\nu}\to H(\nu)
with
(k,T)\mapsto D_{T}Z_{k}
.

Lemma 7.4
.

Y
is continuously Fréchet differentiable and its derivative at
(k,J)
is given by

\left(D_{k,J}Y\right)\left(l,T\right)=\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{l}{2N_{k}}\int_{\mathcal{S}}\psi F^{\prime}_{k}\left(\psi,\psi\right)\exp\left[J\left(\psi\right)\right]f_{k}\left(\psi\right)\mathrm{d}\nu\left(\psi\right)-lD_{J}Z_{k}\cdot\partial_{k}\ln N_{k}\\
\frac{1}{N_{k}}\int_{\mathcal{S}}\psi T\left(\psi\right)\exp\left[J\left(\psi\right)\right]f_{k}\left(\psi\right)\mathrm{d}\nu\left(\psi\right)\end{pmatrix}

(67)

for all
l\in\mathbb{R},T\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\nu}
.
Both integrals may again understood to be generalised Bochner integrals in
\mathcal{S}
with values in
H(\nu)
.

These properties are inherited by
W:\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\nu}\to\mathbb{R},(k,T)\mapsto\ln Z_{k}(T)
, i.e
W
is continuously differentiable,
W_{k}
is twice continuously differentiable and
DW_{k}:\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\nu}\to H(\nu)
.
As a matter of fact,
DW_{k}
even turns out to be a bijection between
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\nu}
and
H(\nu)
.
The injectivity follows directly from the following positivity property of
D^{2}W_{k}
.

Theorem 7.5
.

For all
k\in\mathbb{R}
and
J\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\nu}
there exists a
C>0
such that for all
K\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\nu}

K\left[\left(D^{2}_{J}W_{k}\right)\left(K\right)\right]\geq C\left\langle K,K\right\rangle\,.

(68)

Proof.

By Hölder’s inequality,

\displaystyle K\left[\left(D^{2}_{J}W_{k}\right)\left(K\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{k}^{2}Z_{k}\left(J\right)^{2}}\int_{\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{S}}\left[K\left(\psi\right)^{2}-K\left(\psi\right)K\left(\phi\right)\right]

(69)

\displaystyle\times\exp\left[J\left(\psi\right)+J\left(\phi\right)\right]f_{k}\left(\psi\right)f_{k}\left(\phi\right)\mathrm{d}\left(\nu\times\nu\right)\left(\psi,\phi\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{N_{k}^{2}Z_{k}\left(J\right)^{2}}\int_{\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{S}}\left[\left|K\left(\psi\right)K\left(\phi\right)\right|-K\left(\psi\right)K\left(\phi\right)\right]

\displaystyle\times\exp\left[J\left(\psi\right)+J\left(\phi\right)\right]f_{k}\left(\psi\right)f_{k}\left(\phi\right)\mathrm{d}\left(\nu\times\nu\right)\left(\psi,\phi\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{k}^{2}Z_{k}\left(J\right)^{2}}\int_{\mathcal{S}}\left[\left|K\left(\psi\right)\right|-K\left(\psi\right)\right]\exp\left[J\left(\psi\right)\right]f_{k}\left(\psi\right)\mathrm{d}\nu\left(\psi\right)

\displaystyle\times\int_{\mathcal{S}}\left[\left|K\left(\psi\right)\right|+K\left(\psi\right)\right]\exp\left[J\left(\psi\right)\right]f_{k}\left(\psi\right)\mathrm{d}\nu\left(\psi\right)

which clearly is nonnegative.
Let us now suppose that there is a sequence
(K_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\nu}
with
\left<K_{n},K_{n}\right>=1
such that the first integral tends to zero, i.e

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathcal{S}}\left[\left|K_{n}\left(\psi\right)\right|-K_{n}\left(\psi\right)\right]\exp\left[J\left(\psi\right)\right]f_{k}\left(\psi\right)\mathrm{d}\nu\left(\psi\right)=0\,.

(70)

Then, there exists a subsequence
(L_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}|L_{n}(\psi)|-L_{n}(\psi)=0
for
\nu
-almost every
\psi
.
But since each
L_{n}
can in turn be written as the
\nu
-almost everywhere pointwise limit of linear functions, the above implies
\lim_{n\to\infty}L_{n}(\psi)=0
for
\nu
-almost every
\psi
.
One arrives at the same conclusion if one takes the second integral to go to zero instead.
By the finiteness of
\nu
we thus have that
L_{n}\to 0
in
\nu
-measure as
n\to\infty
.
Now, let
\epsilon>0
and pick any
\nu
-measurable
A\subset\mathcal{S}
with
\nu(A)<\epsilon^{2}/3
.
Then, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}

\int_{A}L_{n}\left(\psi\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}\nu\left(\psi\right)\leq\sqrt{\int_{S}L_{n}\left(\psi\right)^{4}\mathrm{d}\nu\left(\psi\right)}\sqrt{\nu\left(A\right)}\\
<\left\langle L_{n},L_{n}\right\rangle\epsilon=\epsilon\,.

(71)

Thus, Vitali’s convergence theorem tells us that
L_{n}
goes to zero in
L^{2}(\nu)
i.e in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\nu}
for
n\to\infty
which is a contradiction.
∎

Corollary 7.6
.

DW_{k}:\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\nu}\to H(\nu)
is injective for all
k\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
D_{J}W_{k}=D_{K}W_{k}
for some
J,K\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\nu}
.
Then, by Rolle’s theorem, there is a
t\in[0,1]
such that

\left(J-K\right)\left[\left(D^{2}_{tJ+\left(1-t\right)K}W_{k}\right)\left(J-K\right)\right]=0\,.

(72)

By theorem
7.5
this can only happen if
J=K
.
∎

Let us also record the following corollary which will be of paramount importance.

Corollary 7.7
.

For all
k\in\mathbb{R}
and
J\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\nu}
the linear map
D^{2}_{J}W_{k}:\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\nu}\to H(\nu)
is continuously invertible.

Proof.

The bilinear form
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\nu}\times\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\nu}\to\mathbb{R}
given by

\left(K,L\right)\mapsto K\left[\left(D^{2}_{J}W_{k}\right)\left(L\right)\right]

(73)

is symmetric which can be seen from writing it out explicitly.
By theorem
7.5
it is bounded from below.
By continuity
R_{\nu}^{-1}\circ D^{2}_{J}W_{k}
is thus self-adjoint, continuous and injective and as such has dense range in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\nu}
.
Since it is also bounded from below it is continuously invertible.
∎

The surjectivity of
DW_{k}
is substantially more involved.

Theorem 7.8
.

DW_{k}:\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\nu}\to H(\nu)
is surjective for all
k\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Let
\phi\in H(\nu)
.
Then
J\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\nu}
solves the equation
D_{J}W_{k}=\phi
if and only if for all
K\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\nu}

\int_{\mathcal{S}}K\left(\psi-\phi\right)\exp\left[J\left(\psi\right)\right]f_{k}\left(\psi\right)\mathrm{d}\nu\left(\psi\right)=0\,.

(74)

Since
\phi\in H(\nu)
we may apply the Cameron-Martin theorem to obtain the equivalent condition

\int_{\mathcal{S}}K\left(\psi\right)\exp\left[J\left(\psi\right)-\left(R_{\nu}^{-1}\phi\right)\left(\psi\right)\right]f_{k}\left(\psi+\phi\right)\mathrm{d}\nu\left(\psi\right)=0

(75)

for all
K\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\nu}
.
Let us make the ansatz
J=R_{\nu}^{-1}\phi+H
for some
H\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\nu}
.
Then the above is true precisely when
H
minimises the convex function

M_{\phi}:\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\nu}\to\mathbb{R}\qquad T\mapsto\int_{\mathcal{S}}\exp\left[T\left(\psi\right)\right]f_{k}\left(\psi+\phi\right)\mathrm{d}\nu\left(\psi\right)\,.

(76)

M_{\phi}
is clearly well-defined and continuous because it admits the representation

M_{\phi}\left(T\right)=\exp\left[\frac{1}{2}\left\langle T,T\right\rangle\right]\int_{\mathcal{S}}f_{k}\left(\psi+\phi+R_{\nu}T\right)\mathrm{d}\nu\left(\psi\right)

(77)

in analogy to
Z_{k}
in equation
65
.
We shall now assume that
(H_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a minimising sequence of
M_{\phi}
and the goal is to show that there is some bounded subsequence.

Since
f_{k}
is continuous and
\mathcal{S}
admits continuous norms, there is a continuous norm
p
on
\mathcal{S}
and some
\delta>0
such that

\forall\psi\in\mathcal{S}:\quad p\left(\psi\right)\leq\delta\implies f_{k}\left(\phi+\psi\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}f_{k}\left(\phi\right)\,.

(78)

Now, consider the three mutually exclusive cases

1
:

\limsup_{n\to\infty}p\left(R_{\nu}H_{n}\right)=0
,

2
:

there is a subsequence
(K_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}p\left(R_{\nu}K_{n}\right)\in\left(0,\infty\right)
,

3
:

\liminf_{n\to\infty}p\left(R_{\nu}H_{n}\right)=\infty
.

1
: Suppose there exists a continuous seminorm
q
on
\mathcal{S}
such that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\left(p+q\right)\left(R_{\nu}H_{n}\right)\neq 0\,.

(79)

We may then replace the previously used norm
p
by
p+q
such that we land in either case
2
or
3
.
If we have
\limsup_{n\to\infty}(p+q)(R_{\nu}H_{n})=0
for all continuous seminorms
q
on
\mathcal{S}
, we clearly have that

B:=\overline{\left\{R_{\nu}H_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}}+\left\{\phi\right\}\subset\mathcal{S}

(80)

is compact.
Also since
\nu
is Radon there is a compact set
C\subseteq\mathcal{S}
such that
\nu(C)>0
.
Clearly,

\displaystyle M_{\phi}\left(H_{n}\right)\geq\exp\left[\frac{1}{2}\left\langle H_{n},H_{n}\right\rangle\right]\int_{C}f_{k}\left(\psi+\phi+R_{\nu}H_{n}\right)\mathrm{d}\nu\left(\psi\right)\,.

(81)

Now,
f_{k}
is continuous so that it attains its infimum on
B+C
which cannot be zero.
Hence,

\inf_{\psi\in C}f_{k}(\psi+\phi+R_{\nu}H_{n})\geq\inf_{\psi\in B+C}f_{k}\left(\psi\right):=\alpha>0

(82)

and

M_{\phi}\left(H_{n}\right)\geq\alpha\exp\left[\frac{1}{2}\left\langle H_{n},H_{n}\right\rangle\right]\nu\left(C\right)\,.

(83)

But then,
H_{n}
cannot be unbounded in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\nu}
since it is a minimising sequence of
M_{\phi}
.

For the remaining two cases we shall restrict the integral to a ball of radius
0<r\leq\delta
in the norm
p
.
Furthermore,
\nu_{p}
, the pushforward measure of
\nu
to
\mathcal{S}_{p}
via the natural map
\iota_{p}:\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{S}_{p}
is a Gaußian measure on a Banach space and we obtain

M_{\phi}\left(H_{n}\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}f_{k}\left(\phi\right)\exp\left[\frac{1}{2}\left\langle H_{n},H_{n}\right\rangle\right]\nu_{p}\left(\overline{B_{r}\left(-\iota_{p}R_{\nu}H_{n}\right)}\right)

(84)

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Here,
\overline{B_{r}(-\iota_{p}R_{\nu}H_{n})}
denotes the closed ball of radius
r
around
-\iota_{p}R_{\nu}H_{n}
in
\mathcal{S}_{p}
.
Note that the transpose
{\vphantom{\!\iota}}{}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\iota_{p}:(\mathcal{S}_{p})^{*}\to\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\beta}
has dense range because
\iota_{p}
is injective since
p
is a norm and not just a seminorm (see
[
SW99
, Chapter 4, §4, Corollary 2.3]
) and
\mathcal{S}
is reflexive.
Hence, by the continuity of
M_{\phi}
we may assume that
H_{n}\in{\vphantom{\!\iota}}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\iota_{p}(\mathcal{S}_{p})^{*}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Furthermore, letting
R_{\nu_{p}}
denote the Hilbert isomorphism between the closure of
(\mathcal{S}_{p})^{*}
in
L^{2}(\nu_{p})
and
H(\nu_{p})
, it is straightforward to verify that
\iota_{p}R_{\nu}{\vphantom{\!\iota}}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\iota_{p}
is equal to the restriction of
R_{\nu_{p}}
to
(\mathcal{S}_{p})^{*}
.

2
: Restrict to a subsequence
({\vphantom{\!\iota}}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\iota_{p}K_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of
(H_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
with

•

\lim_{n\to\infty}p\left(R_{\nu}{\vphantom{\!\iota}}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\iota_{p}K_{n}\right)=:P\in(0,\infty)
,

•

\inf_{n\in\mathbb{N}}p\left(R_{\nu}{\vphantom{\!\iota}}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\iota_{p}K_{n}\right)>\frac{1}{2}P
,

•

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}p\left(R_{\nu}{\vphantom{\!\iota}}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\iota_{p}K_{n}\right)<2P
.

Set
\gamma=\min\{\delta,P/2\}
and in accordance with
[
KLL94
, Corollary 7]
(considering
t=1
only)

r=\frac{\gamma}{4}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\epsilon=1-\frac{3}{4}\frac{\gamma}{P}\in\left(0,1\right)\,.

(85)

Then,
r<(1-\epsilon)p(R_{\nu}{\vphantom{\!\iota}}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\iota_{p}K_{n})
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Now, define

\displaystyle g_{n}

\displaystyle=-\left(1-\frac{\epsilon\gamma}{8P}\right)R_{\nu_{p}}K_{n}

(86)

\displaystyle\text{implying}\quad p\left(-R_{\nu_{p}}K_{n}-g_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8}\epsilon\gamma\frac{p\left(R_{\nu}{\vphantom{\!\iota}}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\iota_{p}K_{n}\right)}{P}\leq\frac{1}{4}\epsilon\gamma=\epsilon r\,.

and note that
g_{n}\in R_{\nu_{p}}(\mathcal{S}_{p})^{*}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Hence, by
[
KLL94
, Corollary 7]
,

\nu_{p}\left(\overline{B_{r}\left(-\iota_{p}R_{\nu}K_{n}\right)}\right)\geq\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{\epsilon\gamma}{8P}\right)^{2}\left\langle{\vphantom{\!\iota}}{}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\iota_{p}K_{n},{\vphantom{\!\iota}}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\iota_{p}K_{n}\right\rangle\right]\nu_{p}\left(\overline{B_{\left(1-\epsilon\right)r}\left(0\right)}\right)

(87)

which combines with equation
84
to

M_{\phi}\left({\vphantom{\!\iota}}{}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\iota_{p}K_{n}\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}f_{k}\left(\phi\right)\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\left[1-\left(1-\frac{\epsilon\gamma}{8P}\right)^{2}\right]\left\langle{\vphantom{\!\iota}}{}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\iota_{p}K_{n},{\vphantom{\!\iota}}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\iota_{p}K_{n}\right\rangle\right)\nu_{p}\left(\overline{B_{\left(1-\epsilon\right)r}\left(0\right)}\right)

(88)

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
By lemma
2.16
the above is nonzero.
Furthermore,
\epsilon\gamma<8P
such that
({\vphantom{\!\iota}}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\iota_{p}K_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
must be bounded since it is a minimising sequence of
M_{\phi}
.

3
: Restrict to a subsequence
({\vphantom{\!\iota}}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\iota_{p}K_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of
(H_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
with
p(R_{\nu}{\vphantom{\!\iota}}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\iota_{p}K_{n})>2\delta
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and with the same notation as before, set

\epsilon=\frac{1}{2},\qquad r=\delta\qquad\text{and}\qquad g_{n}=-\left(1-\frac{\delta}{2p\left(R_{\nu_{p}}K_{n}\right)}\right)R_{\nu_{p}}K_{n}

(89)

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then, clearly

r<\left(1-\epsilon\right)p\left(R_{\nu_{p}}K_{n}\right)\qquad\text{and}\qquad p\left(-R_{\nu_{p}}K_{n}-g_{n}\right)\leq\epsilon r

(90)

such that

\displaystyle\nu_{p}

\displaystyle\left(\overline{B_{r}\left(-R_{\nu}{\vphantom{\!\iota}}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\iota_{p}K_{n}\right)}\right)

(91)

\displaystyle\geq\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{\delta}{2p\left(R_{\nu_{p}}K_{n}\right)}\right)^{2}\left\langle{\vphantom{\!\iota}}{}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\iota_{p}K_{n},{\vphantom{\!\iota}}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\iota_{p}K_{n}\right\rangle\right]\nu_{p}\left(\overline{B_{\left(1-\epsilon\right)r}\left(0\right)}\right)

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Now, note that by
[
Bog98
, Theorem 3.2.10(i)]
, there is a
C>0
such that
p(R_{\nu}K)\leq C\sqrt{\langle K,K\rangle}
for all
K\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\prime}_{\nu}
.
Then, since
p(R_{\nu_{p}}K_{n})>\delta/2
we arrive at

\displaystyle\nu_{p}

\displaystyle\left(\overline{B_{r}\left(-R_{\nu}{\vphantom{\!\iota}}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\iota_{p}K_{n}\right)}\right)

(92)

\displaystyle\geq\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{\delta}{2C\sqrt{\left\langle{\vphantom{\!\iota}}{}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\iota_{p}K_{n},{\vphantom{\!\iota}}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\iota_{p}K_{n}\right\rangle}}\right)^{2}\left\langle{\vphantom{\!\iota}}{}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\iota_{p}K_{n},{\vphantom{\!\iota}}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\iota_{p}K_{n}\right\rangle\right]\nu_{p}\left(\overline{B_{\left(1-\epsilon\right)r}\left(0\right)}\right)

which combines with equation
84
to

M_{\phi}\left({\vphantom{\!\iota}}{}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\iota_{p}K_{n}\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}f_{k}\left(\phi\right)\exp\left[\frac{\delta\sqrt{\left\langle{\vphantom{\!\iota}}{}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\iota_{p}K_{n},{\vphantom{\!\iota}}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\iota_{p}K_{n}\right\rangle}}{4C}-\frac{\delta^{2}}{8C^{2}}\right]\nu_{p}\left(\overline{B_{\left(1-\epsilon\right)r}\left(0\right)}\right)

(93)

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
As before, since
({\vphantom{\!\iota}}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\iota_{p}K_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a minimising sequence of
M_{\phi}
it must be bounded.

Since
M_{\phi}
is continuous and convex, it is also weakly lower semicontinuous and hence attains its minimum by the weak compactness of bounded balls in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\nu}
.
∎

So,
DW_{k}:\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\nu}\to H(\nu)
is a Fréchet differentiable bijection and by corollary
7.7

D^{2}_{J}W_{k}
is continuously invertible for all
J\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\nu}
and
k\in\mathbb{R}
.

Corollary 7.9
.

The map
\left(k,\phi\right)\mapsto(DW_{k})^{-1}(\phi)
is continuously Fréchet differentiable.

Proof.

Define
g:\mathbb{R}\times H(\nu)\times\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\nu}\to H(\nu)
with

\left(k,\phi,T\right)\mapsto D_{T}W_{k}-\phi=\frac{D_{T}Z_{k}}{Z_{k}\left(T\right)}-\phi\,.

(94)

It is continuously Fréchet differentiable by lemma
7.4
and for all
k\in\mathbb{R},\phi\in H(\nu)
and
K,T\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\nu}

\left(D_{k,\phi,T}g\right)\left(0,0,K\right)=\left(D^{2}_{T}W_{k}\right)\left(K\right)\,.

(95)

Since
D^{2}_{T}W_{k}
is continuously invertible,
g
satisfies the conditions of the implicit function theorem.
∎

Finally, we may define the
effective average action

\Gamma_{k}:H(\nu)\to\mathbb{R}
as

\phi\mapsto\sup_{J\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\nu}}\left[J\left(\phi\right)-W_{k}\left(J\right)\right]-\frac{1}{2}F_{k}\left(\phi,\phi\right)\,.

(96)

for all
k\in\mathbb{R}
.
It is well-defined since the supremum is attained precisely for
J=(DW_{k})^{-1}(\phi)
.
Hence,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{k}\left(\phi\right)

\displaystyle=\left(DW_{k}\right)^{-1}\left(\phi\right)\left(\phi\right)-W_{k}\left(\left(DW_{k}\right)^{-1}\left(\phi\right)\right)-\frac{1}{2}F_{k}\left(\phi,\phi\right)

(97)

for all
\phi\in H(\nu)
.
By the chain rule the above is also Fréchet differentiable with derivative
D\Gamma_{k}:H(\nu)\to\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\nu}
given by

\phi\mapsto\left(DW_{k}\right)^{-1}\left(\phi\right)-F_{k}\left(\phi,\cdot\right)

(98)

where we have used the continuous injection
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\beta}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\nu}
.
This is precisely the
quantum equation of motion
(see e.g.
[

The subtraction of the
\phi=0
contribution of the full propagator is quite remarkable because it is precisely what is done under the hood in concrete calculations involving the
‘non-regularised’
FRGE.
Furthermore, it motivates the common practise of expanding both sides in powers of
\phi
and solving the equation order by order.
In fact, it is easy to show that under rather mild conditions one may Gâteaux differentiate under the trace owing to the fact that
Z_{k}
is actually Fréchet-
C^{\infty}
.
Hence, solving order by order is justified but whether the resulting solution is analytic is not clear from these equations.
Resulting expressions including combinatorics may be extracted from
[

]
.

The achievement of theorem
7.16
is the rigorous derivation of the FRGE and the exposure of correct domains and boundary conditions of
\bar{\Gamma}_{k}
.
Notably, the
k\to\infty
limit depends on
\mathcal{R}
since

can be seen as the continuous extension of

An unfortunate consequence is that it becomes impossible to choose a trace-class
\mathcal{R}
in such a way that

is translation equivariant.
Hence, while the differential equation for

respects translation invariance the boundary condition at

On the bright side, the fact that the involved operators are trace class and self-adjoint on

implies that they have a complete basis of eigenvectors.
It seems likely that this fact can be exploited in numerical calculations.
All in all, Wetterich’s equation is shown to have a rigorous foundation and if one chooses to parametrise

by cutoffs instead of

and
