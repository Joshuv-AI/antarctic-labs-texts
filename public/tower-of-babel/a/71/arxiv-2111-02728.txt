Electric current on surface of a metal/superconductor

Abstract
We derive the electric current on surface of a normal metal or superconductor induced by the light bosonic dark matter, such as axion or hidden photon, by appropriately taking account of the boundary condition of the electric and magnetic field at the surface. We discuss detection possibility of such an electric current.

2
Electrodynamics of metal/superconductor

In this section we briefly summarize the basic equations of electric/magnetic field in the metal and superconductor for later use.
In this paper we take the dielectric constant
\epsilon
and permeability
\mu
of the material to be equal to unity for simplicity. This assumption does not much affect the result as far as
|\epsilon-1|,|\mu-1|\lesssim\sqrt{m/\sigma}
or
m/m_{A}
for a normal metal or superconductor respectively, where
m
is the DM mass,
\sigma
is the conductivity and
m_{A}
is the photon mass in the superconductor.

2.1
Metal

2.1.1
Maxwell equations

Let us consider the Lagrangian:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=-\frac{1}{4}F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}-eA_{\mu}j^{\mu}.

(1)

where
F_{\mu\nu}=\partial_{\mu}A_{\nu}-\partial_{\nu}A_{\mu}
is the electromagnetic field strength tensor,
e
is the gauge coupling constant (or
e=\sqrt{4\pi\alpha_{e}}
with
\alpha_{e}
being the fine structure constant) and
j_{\mu}
is the electric current.
The equation of motion (Maxwell equation) is given by

\displaystyle\partial_{\mu}F^{\mu\nu}=\Box A^{\nu}-\partial^{\nu}(\partial_{\mu}A^{\mu})=ej^{\nu}.

(2)

Applying
\partial_{\nu}
on both sides leads to the current conservation:
\partial_{\nu}j^{\nu}=0
.
In components, the equation of motion leads to

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&\nu=0~~:~~\Delta A^{0}+\vec{\nabla}\cdot\dot{\vec{A}}=ej^{0}\\
&\nu=i~~:~~\Box\vec{A}-\vec{\nabla}(\dot{A}^{0}+\vec{\nabla}\cdot\vec{A})=e\vec{j}\end{cases}

(3)

These equations are rewritten in terms of
\vec{E}=-\vec{\nabla}A^{0}-\dot{\vec{A}}
and
\vec{B}=\vec{\nabla}\times\vec{A}
:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&\nu=0~~:~~-\vec{\nabla}\cdot\vec{E}=ej^{0}\\
&\nu=i~~:~~\dot{\vec{E}}-\vec{\nabla}\times\vec{B}=e\vec{j}\end{cases}

(4)

From this we obtain

\displaystyle\Box\vec{E}=-e(\dot{\vec{j}}+\vec{\nabla}j^{0}),

(5)

\displaystyle\Box\vec{B}=e\vec{\nabla}\times\vec{j}.

(6)

On the other hand, there are identities:

\displaystyle\vec{\nabla}\cdot\vec{B}=0,~~~~~~~\vec{\nabla}\times\vec{E}=-\dot{\vec{B}}.

(7)

2.1.2
Plasma frequency

Let us express the current
\vec{j}
in terms of the velocity of free electrons in the metal.
#1
#1

#1

In our convention,
\vec{j}
represents the electron number current and the electric current is given by
-e\vec{j}
.

The equation of motion of free electron under the electric field is given by

\displaystyle m_{e}\dot{\vec{v}}_{e}=-e\vec{E},

(8)

where
m_{e}
is the electron mass. Thus the current is given by

\displaystyle\dot{\vec{j}}=n_{e}\dot{\vec{v}}_{e}=-\frac{en_{e}}{m_{e}}\vec{E},

(9)

where
n_{e}
is the electron number density.
Substituting this into (
5
) and assuming the spatial gradient
\vec{\nabla}j^{0}
is small enough, we obtain

\displaystyle\Box\vec{E}=\frac{e^{2}n_{e}}{m_{e}}\vec{E}\equiv\omega_{p}^{2}\vec{E}.

(10)

Here we have defined the plasma frequency
\omega_{p}
. It shows that the electric field obtains an effective plasma mass
\omega_{p}^{2}
.
From (
9
) we also obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(\vec{\nabla}\times\vec{j}-\frac{en_{e}}{m_{e}}\vec{B}\right)=0.

(11)

It is solved as

\displaystyle\vec{\nabla}\times\vec{j}=\frac{en_{e}}{m_{e}}(\vec{B}-\vec{B}_{0}),

(12)

where
\vec{B}_{0}
represents arbitrary constant. When
\vec{B}_{0}=0
, this is called the London equation as shown in the next subsection.
Substituting this into (
6
), we obtain

\displaystyle\Box\vec{B}=\omega_{p}^{2}(\vec{B}-\vec{B}_{0}).

(13)

To summarize, we obtained

\displaystyle\Box\vec{E}=\omega_{p}^{2}\vec{E}

(14)

\displaystyle\Box\vec{B}=\omega_{p}^{2}(\vec{B}-\vec{B}_{0}).

(15)

For the electric field, the static solution should be of the form

\displaystyle\vec{E}(x)=\vec{E}_{0}e^{-\omega_{p}x}.

(16)

For the magnetic field, the constant solution is allowed:

\displaystyle\vec{B}(x)=\vec{B}_{0}.

(17)

2.1.3
Finite conductivity

So far we have neglected the dissipation of the electron, i.e., we considered the limit of perfect metal.
Several effects such as impurities in the material and scattering with phonons can act as an effective dissipation for the electron motion.
We can phenomenologically introduce the effect of dissipation as

\displaystyle\dot{\vec{v}}_{e}+\Gamma\vec{v}_{e}=-\frac{e\vec{E}}{m_{e}},

(18)

where
\Gamma
denotes the dissipation coefficient.
By defining the Fourier transform as

\displaystyle\vec{E}(t)=\int\vec{E}_{\omega}e^{i\omega t}d\omega,~~~~~~\vec{E}_{\omega}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\vec{E}(t)e^{-i\omega t}dt,

(19)

and so on, we obtain

\displaystyle e\vec{j}_{\omega}=en_{e}\vec{v}_{\omega}=-\sigma\vec{E}_{\omega},~~~~~~\sigma\equiv\frac{1}{i\omega+\Gamma}\frac{e^{2}n_{e}}{m_{e}}=\frac{\omega_{p}^{2}}{i\omega+\Gamma}.

(20)

For a typical metal,
\omega_{p}\sim 10\,{\rm eV}
and
\Gamma\sim 0.1\,{\rm eV}
.
Later we will consider
\omega
equal to the DM mass, which is typically much smaller than
\Gamma
. For
\omega\ll\Gamma
, we have

\displaystyle e\vec{j}=-\sigma\vec{E};~~~~~~\sigma\simeq\frac{e^{2}n_{e}}{m_{e}\Gamma}=\frac{\omega_{p}^{2}}{\Gamma}.

(21)

Then Eq. (
5
) and (
6
) becomes

\displaystyle\Box\vec{E}-\sigma\dot{\vec{E}}=0,

(22)

\displaystyle\Box\vec{B}-\sigma\dot{\vec{B}}=0.

(23)

By assuming the solution of the form
e^{i(-\omega t+kx)}
, we obtain the dispersion relation as

\displaystyle k=(1+i)\delta(\omega)^{-1},~~~~~~\delta(\omega)\equiv\sqrt{\frac{2}{\sigma\omega}}.

(24)

Thus it describes the exponentially decaying solution
\propto e^{-x/\delta}
with a skin depth
\delta
.

2.2
Superconductor

Let us consider the following Lagrangian:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4}F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}-|(\partial_{\mu}-ieqA_{\mu})\Phi|^{2}-V(|\Phi|)

(25)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4}F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}-|\partial_{\mu}\Phi|^{2}-e^{2}q^{2}|\Phi|^{2}A_{\mu}A^{\mu}-ieqA_{\mu}(\Phi^{*}\partial^{\mu}\Phi-\Phi\partial^{\mu}\Phi^{*})-V(|\Phi|).

(26)

It is invariant under the gauge transformation
\Phi\to e^{iq\chi(x)}\Phi
with
q
being the charge of
\Phi
and
A_{\mu}\to A_{\mu}+(\partial_{\mu}\chi)/e
.
Let us suppose that the Higgs field obtains a VEV
|\Phi|=v_{\Phi}
. Note that there is an ambiguity of the phase:
\Phi=v_{\Phi}e^{i\theta(x)}
.
Neglecting the dynamics of the radial mode of the Higgs, the Lagrangian is also expressed as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=-\frac{1}{4}F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}-\frac{1}{2}m_{A}^{2}A_{\mu}A^{\mu}-eA_{\mu}j_{\theta}^{\mu},

(27)

where
m_{A}^{2}=2e^{2}q^{2}v_{\Phi}^{2}
and
j_{\theta}^{\mu}=iq(\Phi^{*}\partial^{\mu}\Phi-\Phi\partial^{\mu}\Phi^{*})=-2qv_{\Phi}^{2}\partial^{\mu}\theta
.
The equation of motion is given by

\displaystyle\partial_{\mu}F^{\mu\nu}=\Box A^{\nu}-\partial^{\nu}(\partial_{\mu}A^{\mu})=m_{A}^{2}A^{\nu}+ej_{\theta}^{\nu}.

(28)

One can also define the “current”
ej^{\nu}_{A}\equiv m_{A}^{2}A^{\nu}
so that the equation is rewritten as

\displaystyle\Box A^{\nu}-\partial^{\nu}(\partial_{\mu}A^{\mu})=e(j_{A}^{\nu}+j_{\theta}^{\nu}).

(29)

Notice that the current
j_{A}^{\nu}
and
j_{\theta}^{\nu}
are not gauge invariant, but the sum
j_{A}^{\nu}+j_{\theta}^{\nu}
is gauge invariant.
By using this gauge degree of freedom, one can take a unitary gauge
\theta(x)=0
so that
j_{\theta}^{\mu}=0
.

As a result, we obtain the Lagrangian in the unitary gauge
#2
#2

#2

The BCS theory implies
m_{A}^{2}\simeq e^{2}n_{e}/m_{e}
, which is the same expression as the plasma frequency of a metal.

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=-\frac{1}{4}F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}-\frac{1}{2}m_{A}^{2}A_{\mu}A^{\mu}.

(30)

The equation of motion is given by

\displaystyle\partial_{\mu}F^{\mu\nu}=\Box A^{\nu}-\partial^{\nu}(\partial_{\mu}A^{\mu})=m_{A}^{2}A^{\nu}=ej_{A}^{\nu}.

(31)

These equations are rewritten in terms of
\vec{E}
and
\vec{B}
:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&\nu=0~~:~~-\vec{\nabla}\cdot\vec{E}=ej_{A}^{0}\\
&\nu=i~~:~~\dot{\vec{E}}-\vec{\nabla}\times\vec{B}=e\vec{j}_{A}\end{cases}

(32)

From this we obtain

\displaystyle(\Box-m_{A}^{2})\vec{E}=0,

(33)

\displaystyle(\Box-m_{A}^{2})\vec{B}=0.

(34)

The static solution looks like

\displaystyle\vec{E}(x)=\vec{E}_{0}e^{-m_{A}x},~~~~~~\vec{B}(x)=\vec{B}_{0}e^{-m_{A}x}.

(35)

Therefore, not only
\vec{E}
, but also
\vec{B}
is screened in the superconductor. Note that we have

\displaystyle e\vec{\nabla}\times\vec{j}_{A}=m_{A}^{2}\vec{B}.

(36)

This is the London equation and it corresponds to the case of
\vec{B}_{0}=0
in Eq. (
12
).

3
Metal with dark matter background

Now let us consider the effects of DM on the electrodynamics of metal. First we assume axion DM and derive the modified Maxwell equations in the presence of axion. Next we will also consider the cases of hidden photon DM.

3.1
Axion dark matter

3.1.1
Basic equations

We are interested in the axion DM with the axion mass range of
\mu{\rm eV}\lesssim m_{a}\lesssim{\rm meV}
. The number density of such a light bosonic DM around the Earth is huge and we can regard the axion DM as a coherent field within its de Broglie wavelength. We take the axion field as

\displaystyle a(\vec{x},t)\simeq a_{0}\cos\left(m_{a}t-m_{a}\vec{v}_{a}\cdot\vec{x}\right).

(37)

The axion velocity is about
|\vec{v}_{a}|\sim 10^{-3}
around the Earth. In the most part of the following discussion, we neglect the spatial dependence of the axion field, which is justified as far as we consider the dynamics within one coherent time
\tau\sim 1/(v_{a}^{2}m_{a})
.
The axion energy density around the Earth is

\displaystyle\rho_{a}=\frac{1}{2}\dot{a}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}m_{a}^{2}a^{2}=\frac{1}{2}m_{a}^{2}a_{\rm 0}^{2}\simeq 0.3\,{\rm GeV/cm^{3}}.

(38)

The Lagrangian is

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=-\frac{1}{4}F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}-eA_{\mu}j^{\mu}-\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{\mu}a)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}m_{a}^{2}a^{2}+\frac{a}{4M}F_{\mu\nu}\widetilde{F}^{\mu\nu},

(39)

where
\widetilde{F}^{\mu\nu}\equiv\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}F_{\rho\sigma}/2
and the mass scale
M
represents the strength of the axion-photon coupling. The equation of motion is given by

\displaystyle\Box A^{\mu}-\partial^{\mu}(\partial_{\nu}A^{\nu})-\frac{1}{2M}\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\partial_{\nu}(aF_{\rho\sigma})=ej^{\mu}.

(40)

The conservation law of the current is satisfied:
\partial_{\mu}j^{\mu}=0
.
In components, the equation of motion leads to

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&\mu=0~~:~~\Delta A^{0}+\vec{\nabla}\cdot\dot{\vec{A}}=\frac{1}{M}(\vec{\nabla}a)\cdot\vec{B}+ej^{0}\\
&\mu=i~~:~~\Box\vec{A}-\vec{\nabla}(\dot{A}^{0}+\vec{\nabla}\cdot\vec{A})=-\frac{1}{M}\left(\dot{a}\vec{B}+\nabla a\times\vec{E}\right)+e\vec{j}\end{cases}

(41)

In terms of
\vec{E}
and
\vec{B}
, it is written as

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&\mu=0~~:~~-\vec{\nabla}\cdot\vec{E}=\frac{1}{M}(\vec{\nabla}a)\cdot\vec{B}+ej^{0},\\
&\mu=i~~:~~\dot{\vec{E}}-\vec{\nabla}\times\vec{B}=-\frac{1}{M}\left(\dot{a}\vec{B}+\nabla a\times\vec{E}\right)+e\vec{j}\end{cases}

(42)

Assuming that the axion is almost spatially homogeneous
(|\dot{a}|\gg|\nabla a|)
and the existence of background magnetic field
\vec{B}_{0}
much larger than the axion-induced electric field, we obtain:

\displaystyle-\Box\vec{E}\simeq-\frac{\ddot{a}}{M}\vec{B}_{0}+e\dot{\vec{j}}.

(43)

3.1.2
Electric field configuration

Let us consider an effective one-dimensional setup in which the
x<0
region is superconductor and
x>0
region is vacuum. The uniform external magnetic field
\vec{B}_{0}
is applied on the
z
direction. The equation of motion of the electric field is

\displaystyle-\Box\vec{E}\simeq-\frac{1}{M}\ddot{a}\vec{B}_{0}

\displaystyle(x>0)

(44)

\displaystyle-\Box\vec{E}\simeq-\frac{1}{M}\ddot{a}\vec{B}_{0}+e\dot{\vec{j}}

\displaystyle(x<0)

(45)

Here and in what follows we neglect the spatial dependence of the axion and just take
a(\vec{x},t)=a_{0}\cos(m_{a}t)
. As for the electric current, we take
e\vec{j}=-\sigma\vec{E}
. The general solution is given by the sum of particular solution and the homogeneous solution:

\displaystyle\vec{E}^{\rm(vac)}=\vec{E}_{a}^{\rm(vac)}+\vec{E}_{h}^{\rm(vac)}

\displaystyle(x>0)

(46)

\displaystyle\vec{E}^{\rm(m)}=\vec{E}_{a}^{\rm(m)}+\vec{E}_{h}^{\rm(m)}

\displaystyle(x<0).

(47)

Here the homogeneous solution should satisfy

\displaystyle\ddot{\vec{E}}_{h}+\sigma\dot{\vec{E}}_{h}-\partial_{x}^{2}\vec{E}_{h}=0.

(48)

Below we solve the equation in complex form for convenience, since the equations are linear. The axion field is taken to be

\displaystyle a(t)=a_{0}e^{im_{a}t}.

(49)

The particular solution that depends on the axion source term is given by

\displaystyle\vec{E}_{a}^{\rm(vac)}=-\frac{a_{0}B_{0}}{M}e^{im_{a}t}\vec{e}_{z}~~~~~~(x>0)

(50)

\displaystyle\vec{E}_{a}^{\rm(m)}=-\frac{m_{a}(m_{a}+i\sigma)}{m_{a}^{2}+\sigma^{2}}\frac{a_{0}B_{0}}{M}e^{im_{a}t}\vec{e}_{z}~~~~~~(x<0).

(51)

The general homogeneous solutions are summation of arbitrary frequency modes
\omega
, but in the present situation it is sufficient to only consider the
\omega=m_{a}
mode since otherwise the boundary conditions, explained below, will not be satisfied. We also only consider the out-going wave from the surface of the metal as a physically relevant situation. Thus the homogeneous solution is written as

\displaystyle\vec{E}_{h}^{\rm(vac)}=E_{1}e^{im_{a}(t-x)}\vec{e}_{z}~~~~~~~~~(x>0),

(52)

\displaystyle\vec{E}_{h}^{\rm(m)}=E_{2}e^{i(m_{a}t-k_{R}x)}e^{k_{I}x}\vec{e}_{z}~~~~~~~(x<0),

(53)

where
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
are constants, which will be determined by the boundary condition, and

\displaystyle k_{R}=-\sqrt{\frac{m_{a}}{2}}\left[1+\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{\sigma}{m_{a}}\right)^{2}}\right]^{1/2},~~~~~~k_{I}=\sqrt{\frac{m_{a}}{2}}\left[-1+\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{\sigma}{m_{a}}\right)^{2}}\right]^{1/2}.

(54)

The limiting form is given as

\displaystyle k_{R}\simeq\begin{cases}-m_{a}&(m_{a}\gg\sigma)\\
-\sqrt{m_{a}\sigma/2}&(m_{a}\ll\sigma)\end{cases},~~~~~~k_{I}\simeq\begin{cases}\sigma&(m_{a}\gg\sigma)\\
\sqrt{m_{a}\sigma/2}&(m_{a}\ll\sigma)\end{cases}.

(55)

Note that
\sqrt{m_{a}\sigma/2}^{-1}\equiv\delta
is the skin depth of the metal (
24
).

In order to determine the coefficients
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
in the homogeneous solutions, we need to take the boundary condition at
x=0
into account. The boundary conditions are given by

\displaystyle E_{z}^{\rm(vac)}=E_{z}^{\rm(m)}~~~{\rm at}~~~x=0,

(56)

\displaystyle B_{y}^{\rm(vac)}=B_{y}^{\rm(m)}~~~{\rm at}~~~x=0.

(57)

By using the Maxwell equation, it is also written as

\displaystyle E_{z}^{\rm(vac)}=E_{z}^{\rm(m)}~~~{\rm at}~~~x=0,

(58)

\displaystyle\partial_{x}E_{z}^{\rm(vac)}=\partial_{x}E_{z}^{\rm(m)}~~~{\rm at}~~~x=0.

(59)

By solving this, we obtain

\displaystyle E_{1}=\frac{k_{R}+ik_{I}}{k_{R}-m_{a}+ik_{I}}\frac{\sigma(\sigma-im_{a})}{m_{a}^{2}+\sigma^{2}}\frac{a_{0}B_{0}}{M},

(60)

\displaystyle E_{2}=\frac{m_{a}}{k_{R}-m_{a}+ik_{I}}\frac{\sigma(\sigma-im_{a})}{m_{a}^{2}+\sigma^{2}}\frac{a_{0}B_{0}}{M}.

(61)

Let us take a limit
m_{a}\ll\sigma
. Then we have

\displaystyle\vec{E}^{\rm(vac)}=\left[\left(1-\frac{1+i}{2}\sqrt{\frac{2m_{a}}{\sigma}}\right)e^{im_{a}(t-x)}-e^{im_{a}t}\right]\frac{a_{0}B_{0}}{M}\vec{e}_{z},

(62)

\displaystyle\vec{E}^{\rm(m)}=\left[-\frac{1+i}{2}\sqrt{\frac{2m_{a}}{\sigma}}e^{i(m_{a}t-k_{R}x)}e^{k_{I}x}-\frac{im_{a}}{\sigma}e^{im_{a}t}\right]\frac{a_{0}B_{0}}{M}\vec{e}_{z}.

(63)

By taking only the real part, we obtain

\displaystyle\vec{E}^{\rm(vac)}=\left\{\cos(m_{a}(t-x))-\cos(m_{a}t)+\sqrt{\frac{m_{a}}{2\sigma}}\left[\sin(m_{a}(t-x))-\cos(m_{a}(t-x))\right]\right\}\frac{a_{0}B_{0}}{M}\vec{e}_{z},

(64)

\displaystyle\vec{E}^{\rm(m)}=\left\{\sqrt{\frac{m_{a}}{2\sigma}}\left[\sin\left(m_{a}t-\frac{x}{\delta}\right)-\cos\left(m_{a}t-\frac{x}{\delta}\right)\right]e^{x/\delta}+\frac{m_{a}}{\sigma}\sin(m_{a}t)\right\}\frac{a_{0}B_{0}}{M}\vec{e}_{z}.

(65)

This result at
x>0
is the same as that of Ref.
[
23
]
in the limit
\sigma\to\infty
. We found a correction due to the finite conductivity that is suppressed by
\sqrt{m_{a}/\sigma}
. It is usually negligible for a normal metal. The electric field at the surface of the metal
x=0
is given as

\displaystyle\vec{E}(x=0)=\sqrt{\frac{m_{a}}{2\sigma}}\frac{a_{0}B_{0}}{M}\left[\sin\left(m_{a}t\right)-\cos\left(m_{a}t\right)\right]\vec{e}_{z}.

(66)

It is smaller by a factor
\sim\sqrt{m_{a}/\sigma}
than the estimate in Ref.
[
53
]
. The electric current is thus estimated as

\displaystyle-e\vec{j}(x=0)=\sigma\vec{E}(x=0)=\sqrt{\frac{m_{a}\sigma}{2}}\frac{a_{0}B_{0}}{M}\left[\sin\left(m_{a}t\right)-\cos\left(m_{a}t\right)\right]\vec{e}_{z}.

(67)

It is also smaller by a factor
\sim\sqrt{m_{a}/\sigma}
than the estimate in Ref.
[
53
]
.
Assuming a cylindrical metal with radius
r
, the current is induced at its surface with a skin depth
\delta
. The total current is oscillating with time, and its amplitude is given by

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{m_{a}\sigma}{2}}\frac{a_{0}B_{0}}{M}\times 2\pi r\delta

(68)

\displaystyle\simeq 1.1\times 10^{-16}\,{\rm A}~\left(\frac{B_{0}}{1\,{\rm T}}\right)\left(\frac{1\,{\rm\mu eV}}{m_{a}}\right)\left(\frac{10^{15}\,{\rm GeV}}{M}\right)\left(\frac{r}{1\,{\rm cm}}\right).

(69)

Note that the dependence on the conductivity
\sigma
is cancelled out.

3.2
Hidden photon dark matter

3.2.1
Basic equations

Next we consider the hidden photon DM with the mass range of
\mu{\rm eV}\lesssim m_{H}\lesssim{\rm meV}
.
As will soon be explained, the hidden photon field satisfies
\partial_{\mu}H^{\mu}=0
. We take the hidden photon DM field as

\displaystyle H_{0}(\vec{x},t)\simeq-\vec{v}_{H}\cdot\vec{H}_{c}\cos\left(m_{H}t-m_{H}\vec{v}_{H}\cdot\vec{x}\right),

(70)

\displaystyle\vec{H}(\vec{x},t)\simeq\vec{H}_{c}\cos\left(m_{H}t-m_{H}\vec{v}_{H}\cdot\vec{x}\right).

(71)

Thus the zeroth component
H_{0}
is suppressed by the velocity
|\vec{v}_{H}|\sim 10^{-3}
compared with the spatial component.
The hidden photon energy density around the Earth is

\displaystyle\rho_{H}\simeq\frac{1}{2}\dot{\vec{H}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}m_{H}^{2}{\vec{H}}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}m_{H}^{2}\vec{H}_{c}^{2}\simeq 0.3\,{\rm GeV/cm^{3}}.

(72)

The Lagrangian is

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=-\frac{1}{4}F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}-eA_{\mu}j^{\mu}-\frac{1}{4}H_{\mu\nu}H^{\mu\nu}-\frac{1}{2}m_{H}^{2}H_{\mu}H^{\mu}-\frac{\kappa}{2}F_{\mu\nu}H^{\mu\nu}.

(73)

where
H_{\mu\nu}\equiv\partial_{\mu}H_{\nu}-\partial_{\nu}H_{\mu}
is the field strength tensor of the hidden photon and
\kappa
denotes the kinetic mixing between the Standard Model photon and the hidden photon.
The equation of motion is given by

\displaystyle\Box H^{\mu}-\partial^{\mu}(\partial_{\nu}H^{\nu})-m_{H}^{2}H^{\mu}+\kappa\left(\Box A^{\mu}-\partial^{\mu}(\partial_{\nu}A^{\nu})\right)=0,

(74)

\displaystyle\Box A^{\mu}-\partial^{\mu}(\partial_{\nu}A^{\nu})+\kappa\left(\Box H^{\mu}-\partial^{\mu}(\partial_{\nu}H^{\nu})\right)=ej^{\mu}.

(75)

By multiplying
\partial_{\mu}
on Eq. (
74
), we find a constraint
\partial_{\mu}H^{\mu}=0
. We also find a current conservation
\partial_{\mu}j^{\mu}=0
. Then these equations become

\displaystyle\Box H^{\mu}-m_{H}^{2}H^{\mu}+\kappa\left(\Box A^{\mu}-\partial^{\mu}(\partial_{\nu}A^{\nu})\right)=0,

(76)

\displaystyle\Box A^{\mu}-\partial^{\mu}(\partial_{\nu}A^{\nu})+\kappa\Box H^{\mu}=ej^{\mu}.

(77)

In components, the equation of motion leads to

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&\mu=0~~:~~\Delta A^{0}+\vec{\nabla}\cdot\dot{\vec{A}}=-\kappa\Box H^{0}+ej^{0}\\
&\mu=i~~:~~\Box\vec{A}-\vec{\nabla}(\dot{A}^{0}+\vec{\nabla}\cdot\vec{A})=-\kappa\Box\vec{H}+e\vec{j}\end{cases}

(78)

In terms of
\vec{E}
and
\vec{B}
, it is written as

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&\mu=0~~:~~-\vec{\nabla}\cdot\vec{E}=-\kappa\Box H^{0}+ej^{0}\\
&\mu=i~~:~~\dot{\vec{E}}-\vec{\nabla}\times\vec{B}=-\kappa\Box\vec{H}+e\vec{j}\end{cases}

(79)

By neglecting the spatial dependence of
H_{\mu}
, we obtain

\displaystyle-\Box\vec{E}\simeq\kappa\frac{d^{3}}{dt^{3}}\vec{H}+e\dot{\vec{j}}.

(80)

4
Superconductor with dark matter background

In this section we consider the effect of axion or hidden photon DM on the electromagnetic configuration around the superconducting material. The most analysis is parallel to the case of metal performed in the previous section.
We assume that the DM mass is smaller than the energy gap of the superconducting material, which is typically
\mathcal{O}({\rm meV})
. Otherwise, the DM absorption destroys the Cooper pair and the excitation of quasi particles should be taken into account. The absorption of DM heavier than the superconductor gap has been considered in Refs.
[
42
,
43
]
. In this sense, we consider the regime complementary to these previous studies.

4.1
Axion dark matter

4.1.1
Basic equations

The Lagrangian of the electromagnetism in the superconductor, combined with the interaction with the axion, is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=-\frac{1}{4}F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}-\frac{1}{2}m_{A}^{2}A_{\mu}A^{\mu}-\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{\mu}a)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}m_{a}^{2}a^{2}+\frac{a}{4M}F_{\mu\nu}\widetilde{F}^{\mu\nu}.

(87)

The equation of motion is given by

\displaystyle\Box A^{\mu}-\partial^{\mu}(\partial_{\nu}A^{\nu})-\frac{1}{2M}\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\partial_{\nu}(aF_{\rho\sigma})=ej_{A}^{\mu},

(88)

where
ej_{A}^{\mu}=m_{A}^{2}A^{\mu}
. In components, the equation of motion leads to

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&\mu=0~~:~~\Delta A^{0}+\vec{\nabla}\cdot\dot{\vec{A}}=\frac{1}{M}(\vec{\nabla}a)\cdot\vec{B}+ej_{A}^{0}\\
&\mu=i~~:~~\Box\vec{A}-\vec{\nabla}(\dot{A}^{0}+\vec{\nabla}\cdot\vec{A})=-\frac{1}{M}\left(\dot{a}\vec{B}+\nabla a\times\vec{E}\right)+e\vec{j}_{A}\end{cases}

(89)

In terms of
\vec{E}
and
\vec{B}
, it is written as

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&\mu=0~~:~~-\vec{\nabla}\cdot\vec{E}=\frac{1}{M}(\vec{\nabla}a)\cdot\vec{B}+ej_{A}^{0},\\
&\mu=i~~:~~\dot{\vec{E}}-\vec{\nabla}\times\vec{B}=-\frac{1}{M}\left(\dot{a}\vec{B}+\nabla a\times\vec{E}\right)+e\vec{j}_{A}.\end{cases}

(90)

Assuming spatially homogeneous axion field, it leads to

\displaystyle(-\Box+m_{A}^{2})\vec{E}\simeq-\frac{1}{M}\ddot{a}\vec{B}_{0},

(91)

\displaystyle(-\Box+m_{A}^{2})\vec{B}\simeq\frac{1}{M}\dot{a}\vec{\nabla}\times\vec{B}.

(92)

(93)

The equation of motion of the electric field is

\displaystyle-\Box\vec{E}=-\frac{1}{M}\ddot{a}\vec{B}_{0}

\displaystyle(-\Box+m_{A}^{2})\vec{E}=-\frac{1}{M}\ddot{a}\vec{B}_{0}

\displaystyle(x<0).

(95)

Here and in what follows we neglect the spatial dependence of the axion and just take

The general solution is given by the sum of particular solution and the homogeneous solution:

(99)

The general homogeneous solutions are given by

\displaystyle\vec{E}_{h}^{\rm(vac)}=E_{1}^{\prime}\vec{e}_{z}\cos(m_{a}(t-x))+E_{1}^{\prime\prime}\vec{e}_{z}\sin(m_{a}(t-x)),

(100)

\displaystyle E_{z}^{\rm(vac)}=E_{z}^{\rm(sc)}~~~{\rm at}~~~x=0,

\displaystyle\partial_{x}E_{z}^{\rm(vac)}=\partial_{x}E_{z}^{\rm(sc)}~~~{\rm at}~~~x=0.

(103)

By solving this, we obtain

\displaystyle E_{1}^{\prime}=E_{2}^{\prime}=\frac{\sqrt{m_{A}^{2}-m_{a}^{2}}}{m_{A}}\frac{a_{0}B_{0}}{M},~~~~~~E_{1}^{\prime\prime}=E_{2}^{\prime\prime}=\frac{m_{a}}{m_{A}}\frac{a_{0}B_{0}}{M}.

(104)

Substituting this into Eqs. (

(105)

\displaystyle\vec{E}^{\rm(sc)}=\frac{a_{0}B_{0}}{M}\vec{e}_{z}\left[-\cos(m_{a}t)e^{m_{A}x}+\left(\frac{\sqrt{m_{A}^{2}-m_{a}^{2}}}{m_{A}}\cos(m_{a}t)+\frac{m_{a}}{m_{A}}\sin(m_{a}t)\right)e^{\sqrt{m_{A}^{2}-m_{a}^{2}}x}\right].

(106)

In particular, assuming
m_{a}\ll m_{A}
, the electric field at the boundary
x=0

\displaystyle\vec{E}(x=0)\simeq\frac{a_{0}B_{0}}{M}\frac{m_{a}}{m_{A}}\sin(m_{a}t)\vec{e}_{z},

(107)

It is smaller by a factor
\sim(m_{a}/m_{A})

[

]
.

\displaystyle e\vec{j}_{A}\simeq\frac{a_{0}B_{0}m_{A}}{M}\cos(m_{a}t)\vec{e}_{z}.

4.2
Hidden photon dark matter

4.2.1
Basic equations

Next we discuss the case of hidden photon DM with a superconductor. The Lagrangian is

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=-\frac{1}{4}F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}-\frac{1}{2}m_{A}^{2}A_{\mu}A^{\mu}-\frac{1}{4}H_{\mu\nu}H^{\mu\nu}-\frac{1}{2}m_{H}^{2}H_{\mu}H^{\mu}-\frac{\kappa}{2}F_{\mu\nu}H^{\mu\nu}.

(111)

The equation of motions are similar to the case of metal discussed in Sec.
3.2

ej^{\mu}\to ej_{A}^{\mu}=m_{A}^{2}A^{\mu}

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&\mu=0~~:~~\Delta A^{0}+\vec{\nabla}\cdot\dot{\vec{A}}=-\kappa\Box H^{0}+ej_{A}^{0}\\
&\mu=i~~:~~\Box\vec{A}-\vec{\nabla}(\dot{A}^{0}+\vec{\nabla}\cdot\vec{A})=-\kappa\Box\vec{H}+e\vec{j}\end{cases}

In terms of
\vec{E}

\vec{B}

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&\mu=0~~:~~-\vec{\nabla}\cdot\vec{E}=-\kappa\Box H^{0}+ej_{A}^{0}\\
&\mu=i~~:~~\dot{\vec{E}}-\vec{\nabla}\times\vec{B}=-\kappa\Box\vec{H}+e\vec{j}_{A}\end{cases}

(113)

Assuming the spatially homogeneous hidden photon DM, we can derive the equation of the electric field as

\displaystyle\left(-\Box+m_{A}^{2}\right)\vec{E}\simeq\kappa\frac{d^{3}}{dt^{3}}\vec{H}.

(114)

Electric field configuration

The equation of motion of the electric field is

\displaystyle-\Box\vec{E}=\kappa\frac{d^{3}}{dt^{3}}\vec{H}

\displaystyle(x>0)

(115)

\displaystyle(-\Box+m_{A}^{2})\vec{E}=\kappa\frac{d^{3}}{dt^{3}}\vec{H}

\displaystyle(x<0).

(116)

The general solution is given by the sum of particular solution and the homogeneous solution:

\displaystyle\vec{E}^{\rm(vac)}=\vec{E}_{H}^{\rm(vac)}+\vec{E}_{h}^{\rm(vac)}

\displaystyle(x>0)

(117)

\displaystyle\vec{E}^{\rm(sc)}=\vec{E}_{H}^{\rm(sc)}+\vec{E}_{h}^{\rm(sc)}

\displaystyle(x<0).

(118)

(121)

\displaystyle\vec{E}_{h}^{\rm(sc)}=\left(E_{2}^{\prime}\cos(m_{H}t)+E_{2}^{\prime\prime}\sin(m_{H}t)\right)\vec{e}_{z}e^{\sqrt{m_{A}^{2}-m_{H}^{2}}x}.

\displaystyle E_{1}^{\prime\prime}=\kappa m_{H}H_{c}\cos\theta,~~~~~~E_{2}^{\prime\prime}=-\frac{m_{H}^{2}}{m_{A}^{2}-m_{H}^{2}}\kappa m_{H}H_{c}\cos\theta.

(124)

Thus the full solution is

\displaystyle\vec{E}^{\rm(vac)}=\left[-\sin(m_{H}t)+\sin\left(m_{H}(t-x)\right)-\frac{m_{H}}{\sqrt{m_{A}^{2}-m_{H}^{2}}}\cos\left(m_{H}(t-x)\right)\right]\kappa m_{H}H_{c}\cos\theta\,\vec{e}_{z},

5
Detection possibility

In the previous sections we derived surface electric current on a material induced by axion/hidden-photon DM. Now let us discuss a possibility to measure such a current. Assuming a cylindrical metal, the total power compensated as the Joule heat is given by

\displaystyle P_{\rm J}=\int dV\sigma\vec{E}^{2}(x,t)\sim\sigma\vec{E}^{2}(x=0,t)\Delta V,

(131)

where
\Delta V\simeq 2\pi r\ell\delta
with
r
,
\ell
and
\delta
being the radius, height and the skin depth of the material, respectively.
Substituting
\vec{E}(x=0,t)
given in Eqs. (
66
) for the axion DM and (
83
) for the hidden photon DM and taking the time average over the DM oscillation time scale, we obtain

\displaystyle P_{\rm J}=\begin{cases}2m_{a}\rho_{a}\Delta V\left(\frac{B_{0}}{m_{a}M}\right)^{2}&{\rm for~axion}\\
2m_{H}\rho_{H}\Delta V(\kappa\cos\theta)^{2}&{\rm for~hidden~photon}\end{cases}.

(132)

On the other hand, the Johnson-Nyquist noise power at the temperature
T
and the frequency range
[\omega,\omega+\Delta\omega]
is given by

\displaystyle P_{\rm T}=\frac{T\Delta\omega}{2\pi}.

(133)

Taking the ratio, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{P_{\rm J}}{P_{\rm T}}\simeq 6\times 10^{-11}\left(\frac{\ell}{1\,{\rm m}}\right)\left(\frac{r}{1\,{\rm cm}}\right)\left(\frac{1\,{\rm K}}{T}\right)\left(\frac{10^{4}\,{\rm eV}}{\sigma}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{1\,{\rm\mu eV}}{m_{a}}\right)^{\frac{5}{2}}\left(\frac{10^{15}\,{\rm GeV}}{M}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{B_{0}}{10\,{\rm T}}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{\mathcal{A}}{10^{6}}\right),

(134)

for the axion DM, and

\displaystyle\frac{P_{\rm J}}{P_{\rm T}}\simeq 2\times 10^{-11}\left(\frac{\ell}{1\,{\rm m}}\right)\left(\frac{r}{1\,{\rm cm}}\right)\left(\frac{1\,{\rm K}}{T}\right)\left(\frac{10^{4}\,{\rm eV}}{\sigma}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{1\,{\rm\mu eV}}{m_{H}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{\kappa\cos\theta}{10^{-15}}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{\mathcal{A}}{10^{6}}\right),

(135)

for the hidden photon DM, where we defined
\mathcal{A}\equiv m_{a/H}/(2\Delta\omega)
.

The DM signal may be significantly enhanced if we use the LC circuit to detect the current on a metal. Let us connect the top and bottom of the cylindrical material with a conducting wire, which then forms a LC circuit. The cylindrical material under the DM background acts as an alternating current source of the circuit. In the LC circuit, there is a resonant frequency at
\omega=\omega_{0}\equiv 1/\sqrt{LC}
where
L
is the inductance and
C
is the capacitance. At
m_{a/H}=\omega_{0}
, the signal is enhanced by the
Q
-value of the circuit, where
Q=\omega_{0}/(2\Delta\omega)=(1/R)\sqrt{L/C}
with
R
being the resistance and
\Delta\omega=R/(2L)
the resonance width in a series LC circuit. The energy acquired by the coil is given by
U_{L}=LI^{2}(t)/2
and hence
\dot{U}_{L}\sim\omega_{0}LI_{0}^{2}\sim QRI_{0}^{2}
where
I_{0}
is the amplitude of the oscillating current. It is larger by a factor
Q
than the power (
131
).
Thus we may improve the sensitivity by measuring the oscillating current or the magnetic field in the coil using the resonant LC circuit.
Note that the time-averaged work done by the electromotive force, which is the DM-induced electric field, is given by
(1/T)\int_{0}^{T}\ell E(t)I(t)dt\sim\omega_{0}LI_{0}^{2}/Q\sim RI_{0}^{2}
and it is the same as (
131
).
#4
#4

#4

Note that the resistance is given by
R\simeq\ell/(2\pi\delta r\sigma)
.

The signal-to-noise ratio is then given by

\displaystyle\frac{S}{N}=\frac{P_{\rm J}}{P_{\rm T}}Q\sqrt{\frac{\Delta\omega\,t_{\rm obs}}{2\pi}}\simeq 9\times 10^{7}\left(\frac{Q}{10^{6}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{m_{a/H}}{1\,{\rm\mu eV}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{t_{\rm obs}}{1\,{\rm min}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{P_{\rm J}}{P_{\rm T}},

(136)

where
t_{\rm obs}
is the observation time at each band. Thus it is not very unrealistic that this method covers the QCD axion parameter region.
#5
#5

#5

In the case of QCD axion, the axion-photon coupling
M
is related to the axion mass
m_{a}
through
M=\mathcal{C}\times(5\times 10^{15}\,{\rm GeV})\times(1\,{\rm\mu eV}/m_{a})
with
\mathcal{C}
being
\mathcal{O}(1)
coefficients depending on the detail of the axion model.

This method can be effective when the coupling to photons is stronger.
In particular, it may have an advantage in the case where the Haloscope is not realistic because the mass is too small and the cavity size is too large, or when the mass is large and the cavity size and
Q
value are small.
