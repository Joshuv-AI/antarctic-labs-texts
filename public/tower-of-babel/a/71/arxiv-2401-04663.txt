Adaptive Deep Fourier Residual method via overlapping domain decomposition
Jamie M. Taylor, Manuela Bastidas, Victor M. Calo, David Pardo

Abstract
The Deep Fourier Residual (DFR) method is a specific type of variational physics-informed neural networks (VPINNs). It provides a robust neural network-based solution to partial differential equations (PDEs). The DFR strategy is based on approximating the dual norm of the weak residual of a PDE. This is equivalent to minimizing the energy norm of the error. To compute the dual of the weak residual norm, the DFR method employs an orthonormal spectral basis of the test space, which is known for rectangles or cuboids for multiple function spaces. In this work, we extend the DFR method with ideas of traditional domain decomposition (DD). This enables two improvements: (a) to solve problems in more general polygonal domains, and (b) to develop an adaptive refinement technique in the test space using a Döfler marking algorithm. In the former case, we show that under non-restrictive assumptions we retain the desirable equivalence between the employed loss function and the H 1 H^{1} -error, numerically demonstrating adherence to explicit bounds in the case of the L-shaped domain problem. In the latter, we show how refinement strategies lead to potentially significant improvements against a reference, classical DFR implementation with a test function space of significantly lower dimensionality, allowing us to better approximate singular solutions at a more reasonable computational cost.



1
Introduction

The design of an accurate scheme for solving partial differential equations (PDEs) with machine learning techniques, explicitly using artificial neural network models (NNs)
[
29
]
, requires a proper NN architecture, optimization method, and loss function to minimize. When it comes to the latter aspect, determining a suitable loss function is often complicated, and different machine learning models used for solving PDEs employ distinct loss functions. For instance, in methods like PINNs (Physics-Informed Neural Networks)
[
22
,
23
,
36
,
41
]
, the definition of the loss function relies on the strong formulation of the PDE. In contrast, in other methods like VPINNs, (Variational Physics-Informed Neural Networks)
[
24
,
25
,
26
,
27
]
, the loss function uses the weak formulation. All these strategies have their challenges; for example, the methods based on the strong formulation of the PDE, such as PINNs, fail when dealing with PDEs that inherently yield low-regularity solutions, and in the classical VPINNs, the PDE residual norms employed as a loss function are not consistent with the dual norm of the error; thus, their accuracy highly depends on the choice of the set of functions in the test space
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
.
As a remedy, the authors in
[
38
]
detail a theoretical framework in which an appropriate loss function for a VPINN-type model is a well-defined approximation of the dual norm of the PDE’s weak residual. Specifically, many classical PDEs can be reformulated in a weak form, casting them in terms of the weak residual
\mathcal{R}:H\to H^{*}
, where
H
is a Hilbert space and
H^{*}
is the corresponding dual space. In fact, for many well-posed problems, like the classical Poisson and time-harmonic Maxwell’s equations, the dual norm of the weak residual is a good estimate of the error’s norm. In this sense, if
u^{*}
is the exact solution of the PDE, then there exist constants
0<\gamma<M
such that

\frac{1}{M}\|\mathcal{R}(u)\|_{H^{*}}\leq\|u-u^{*}\|_{H}\leq\frac{1}{\gamma}\|\mathcal{R}(u)\|_{H^{*}},

(1)

where
u^{*}
is the exact solution and
u\in H
is arbitrary. In
[
38
]
, it is shown that the choice of the loss function as an approximation of
\|\mathcal{R}(u)\|_{H^{*}}
in a finite-dimensional subspace of
H^{*}
leads to a Robust version of Variational Physics-Informed Neural Networks (RVPINNs), in which the discrete loss is correlated with the
H
-norm of the error up to an oscillation term.

The Deep Fourier Residual (DFR) method proposed in
[
42
,
43
]
is a particular case of RVPINNs, in which the approximation of the dual norm of the weak residual
\|\mathcal{R}(u)\|_{H^{*}}
is calculated via a spectral representation of the residual. The advantage of the DFR method is that it avoids the primary bottleneck of RVPINNs by eliminating the need to compute (and invert) the Gram matrix corresponding to the chosen basis of the finite-dimensional test space. The loss computation heavily relies on the knowledge of an orthonormal basis for the space
H
. In
[
42
,
43
]
, the basis functions are given as eigenvectors of the Laplacian, which are generally unknown/not available, with certain exceptions in simple geometries such as
n
-rectangular domains (i.e., rectangles in 2D or rectangular cuboids in 3D). Indeed, the construction of such a basis is non-trivial for general domains.

Here, we present an approach that can be used beyond the setting of
n
-rectangular domains. Based on the ideas of traditional domain decomposition (DD) methods
[
10
,
17
,
44
]
, we represent the PDE’s domain
\Omega
by a finite union of overlapping
n
-rectangles. Through this construction, we define an auxiliary norm, the
\star
-norm, that approximates the dual norm of the weak residual and yields estimates (i.e., (
1
)) based on reasonable assumptions. We apply the DD over the
test
space rather than over the
trial
space. As a result, we do not iterate between subdomains or use transmission conditions to solve subproblems, unlike other PINN-like techniques that have used the ideas of DD methods
[
20
,
32
,
40
]
.

By partitioning the subdomain
\Omega
as an overlapping union of
n
-rectangles, we can also address PDEs with solutions with singularities or discontinuities due to factors like non-smooth parameters or localized sources. In those cases, the classic DFR method fails to provide accurate solutions because it requires many Fourier modes with support over the entire domain to represent phenomena that may occur only locally. However, by developing a subdomain decomposition of
\Omega
, we overcome this problem and focus on the regions of the domain where errors are both local and high frequency (singularities, interfaces, etc.). We propose using an error indicator to drive local subdomain refinements that captures such errors. Our error indicator is a local contribution of the loss function making the training and refinement consistent, improving the structure of the initial subdomain decomposition of
\Omega
, and reducing errors in regions with potential singularities, high gradients in the solution or high-frequency errors. Similar strategies for developing VPINNs combined with domain decomposition were proposed in
[
25
]
. Our contribution aligns with
[
25
]
in that adaptivity is performed over the test space. However, unlike in
[
25
]
, herein we consider spectral test functions; the residual is measured in a new
\star
-norm that we introduce in Section
6.1
, and we have overlapping subdomains. Additionally, in our implementations, we accelerate the training process by using a hybrid optimization method, where the weights in the last layer of the neural networks are found in one iteration by solving a least-squares (LS) system of equations
[
14
,
21
]
.

Integration errors are commonly encountered in PINN-related methodologies and usually lead to slow convergence of the optimization algorithm and inaccurate or unreliable values of the loss function
[
18
,
37
,
45
]
. In the numerical examples with singularities, addressing the numerical integration problems becomes a sensitive matter. Thus, we employ overkill mesh-integration techniques, and we leave the design of more sophisticated quadrature rules as future work (see
[
30
,
45
]
as possible avenues to improve quadrature).

We structure this work as follows: Section
2
describes the notation and the definition of the weak-form residual for problems with solutions in the space
H^{1}(\Omega)
. Sections
3
,
4
and
5
detail the structure of the proposed NN models, the framework of the DFR method on rectangular domains and the optimization method. Section
6
presents the theoretical basis for using domain decomposition ideas at the core of the DFR method. In Section
7.1
, we extend the DFR method to polygonal domains and in Section
7.2
, we propose using local refinements to improve the method’s accuracy on specific regions of
\Omega
. In Section
8
, we demonstrate the capabilities of the DFR method in 1D and 2D, solving problems with low-regularity solutions. Section
9
concludes and discusses future work. In the appendices
A
,
B
and
C
, we outline some of the technical results related to norms we employ in our work.

2
Variational formulation

For general domains
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
with
n=1,2,
or
3
and boundary
\partial\Omega
, we denote by
L^{2}(\Omega)
the space of squared-integrable real-valued functions equipped with the usual norm. For a general Hilbert space
H
,
(\cdot,\cdot)_{H}
represents the inner product, the corresponding norm is
\|v\|_{H}^{2}:=(v,v)_{H}
, and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{H^{*}\times H}
denotes the duality pairing between elements in
H
and its dual space
H^{*}
.

The space
H^{1}(\Omega)
is the space of
L^{2}(\Omega)
functions having weak derivatives in
L^{2}(\Omega)
. If the boundary of
\Omega
is Lipschitz and
{\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}},{\Gamma_{\mathrm{N}}}
are disjoint subsets of
\partial\Omega
such that
\partial\Omega=\overline{{\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}}}\cup\overline{{\Gamma_{\mathrm{N}}}}
, there exist trace operators
\gamma_{0}(u)=u|_{\partial\Omega}
and
\gamma_{\mathrm{D}}(u)=u|_{{\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}}}
of
u\in H^{1}(\Omega)
that allow these space definitions

H^{1}_{0}(\Omega):=\left\{u\in H^{1}(\Omega):\,\gamma_{0}(u)=0\right\},\quad\text{and}\quad H^{1}_{0,\mathrm{D}}(\Omega):=\left\{u\in H^{1}(\Omega):\,\gamma_{\mathrm{D}}(u)=0\right\}.

(2)

We denote
H^{-1}(\Omega)
the dual space of
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
and
H^{*}_{\mathrm{D}}(\Omega)
the dual space of
H^{1}_{0,\mathrm{D}}(\Omega)
.

For the sake of simplicity, we concentrate our attention on general PDEs with solutions in the space
H^{1}_{0,\mathrm{D}}(\Omega)
. An example of such PDE is Poisson’s equation that can be written in weak form as: find
u\in H^{1}_{0,\mathrm{D}}(\Omega)
satisfying

\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla v+f\cdot v\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}-\int_{{\Gamma_{\mathrm{N}}}}g_{u}\cdot v\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}=0\qquad\forall v\in H^{1}_{0,\mathrm{D}}(\Omega),

(3)

where
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and
g_{u}
is the Neumann boundary data.

In general and in terms of operators, solving the weak formulation of a PDE with solution in the space
H^{1}_{0,\mathrm{D}}(\Omega)
reduces to finding
u\in H^{1}_{0,\mathrm{D}}(\Omega)
such that

\langle\mathcal{R}(u),v\rangle_{H^{*}_{\mathrm{D}}\times H^{1}_{0,\mathrm{D}}}=b(u,v)-\ell(v)=0\qquad\forall v\in H^{1}_{0,\mathrm{D}}(\Omega),

(4)

where
\mathcal{R}:H^{1}_{0,\mathrm{D}}(\Omega)\to H^{*}_{\mathrm{D}}
is the weak residual operator,
b:H^{1}_{0,\mathrm{D}}(\Omega)\times H^{1}_{0,\mathrm{D}}(\Omega)\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a bilinear form and
\ell\in H^{*}_{\mathrm{D}}
. Moreover, if
u^{*}
is the exact solution of Eq. (
4
) and the bilinear form
b
satisfies the continuity condition

|b(u,v)|\leq M\|u\|_{H^{1}_{0,\mathrm{D}}(\Omega)}\|v\|_{H^{1}_{0,\mathrm{D}}(\Omega)}\qquad\forall u,v\in H^{1}_{0,\mathrm{D}}(\Omega),

(5)

and the inf-sup stability condition

\inf\limits_{u\in H^{1}_{0,\mathrm{D}}(\Omega)\setminus\{0\}}\,\sup\limits_{v\in H^{1}_{0,\mathrm{D}}(\Omega)\setminus\{0\}}\frac{|b(u,v)|}{\|u\|_{H^{1}_{0,\mathrm{D}}(\Omega)}\|v\|_{H^{1}_{0,\mathrm{D}}(\Omega)}}\geq\gamma,

(6)

with
0<\gamma<M
, one may estimate the error using the dual norm of the residual via Eq. (
1
) (for detailed proofs of these bounds see, e.g.,
[
11
,
12
]
).

Now, from Eq. (
1
), we obtain that the dual norm of the residual is equivalent to the norm of the error. Therefore, if one aims to minimize the error, the dual norm of the residual is an attractive choice of minimization target (loss function).

3
Discretization over the trial space using Neural Networks

A fully-connected feed-forward neural network is a mathematical model that consists of a collection of interconnected nodes. Each node is defined by a non-linear map applied to an affine combination of inputs
{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
to certain outputs. The nodes are arranged in (
L
) layers, and the output of each layer can be described as

\mathcal{N}_{j}=\sigma_{j}(\bm{W}_{j}\mathcal{N}_{j-1}+\bm{b}_{j})\quad\text{ for }j\in\{1,\dots,L\},

(7)

where
\sigma_{j}
represents non-linear activation functions for
j\in\{1,\dots,L\text{-}1\}
, and
\sigma_{L}
is the identity function. We take
\mathcal{N}_{0}={\bm{x}}
and
\bm{b}_{L}=\mathbf{0}
and denote our candidate solution
\tilde{u}:=\mathcal{N}_{L}
. The parameters of the neural network are the weights
\bm{W}_{j}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{j}\times d_{j-1}}
and the biases
\bm{b}_{j}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{j}}
, with
d_{j}
being the number of nodes on each layer. The weights and biases are determined during a training process, which involves the application of a gradient-based optimization algorithm to minimize a specific discretized loss function, denoted as
\mathcal{L}(u)
. Both the loss function and the optimization method are explained below.

When imposing homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions on our candidate solutions, we use a non-trainable cut-off function
\chi:\overline{\Omega}\to{\mathbb{R}}
. If
\tilde{u}
is the output of the fully connected feed-forward neural network, then our final output is
u=\chi\tilde{u}
where
\chi
is a function satisfying
\chi|_{\Gamma_{D}}=0
and
\chi>0
on
\bar{\Omega}\setminus\Gamma_{D}
.

4
Discretization over the test space

Given that the dual norm of the residual is an estimator of the energy norm of the error, as Eq. (
1
) shows, our primary goal is to choose
\mathcal{L}(u)
such that it accurately approximates
\|{\mathcal{R}(u)}\|_{H^{*}_{\mathrm{D}}}
. First, we define
\mathcal{V}
, a finite-dimensional subspace of
H^{1}_{0,\mathrm{D}}(\Omega)
, as the span of some basis functions
\{\Phi_{k}\}_{k\in\mathcal{J}}
, with
\mathcal{J}
being a finite set of (sorted) indices.

From the Riesz representation theorem
[
34
]
, we obtain that for each
\mathcal{R}(u)\in H^{*}_{\mathrm{D}}
there exists a corresponding
u_{\mathcal{R}}\in H^{1}_{0,\mathrm{D}}(\Omega)
, known as the
Riesz representative
of
\mathcal{R}(u)
, such that

(u_{\mathcal{R}},v)_{H^{1}_{0,\mathrm{D}}(\Omega)}=\langle\mathcal{R}(u),v\rangle_{H^{*}_{\mathrm{D}}\times H^{1}_{0,\mathrm{D}}},\,\forall v\in H^{1}_{0,\mathrm{D}}(\Omega),

and

\|{\mathcal{R}(u)}\|_{H^{*}_{\mathrm{D}}(\Omega)}=\|u_{\mathcal{R}}\|_{H^{1}_{0,\mathrm{D}}(\Omega)}.

Next, we project the Riesz representative of
\mathcal{R}(u)
onto
\mathcal{V}
and define
\hat{u}_{\mathcal{R}}:=\sum\limits_{k\in\mathcal{J}}\alpha_{k}(u)\Phi_{k}
, which is the unique element in
\mathcal{V}
satisfying

(\hat{u}_{\mathcal{R}},\Phi_{k})_{H^{1}_{0,\mathrm{D}}(\Omega)}=\langle\mathcal{R}(u),\Phi_{k}\rangle_{H^{*}_{\mathrm{D}}\times H^{1}_{0,\mathrm{D}}}\quad\forall k\in\mathcal{J}.

(8)

This equation can be expressed in vectorial form as follows:

\bm{G}{\bm{\alpha}}(u)=\bm{r}(u),

(9)

where
\bm{G}:=[(\Phi_{i},\Phi_{j})_{H^{1}_{0,\mathrm{D}}(\Omega)}]_{i,j\in\mathcal{J}}
is the Gram matrix,
{\bm{\alpha}}(u):=[\alpha_{j}(u)]_{j\in\mathcal{J}}
is the vector of unknown coefficients, and
\bm{r}(u):=[\langle\mathcal{R}(u),\Phi_{j}\rangle_{H^{*}_{\mathrm{D}}\times H^{1}_{0,\mathrm{D}}}]_{j\in\mathcal{J}}
. Consequently, we have

\|{\mathcal{R}(u)}\|_{H^{*}_{\mathrm{D}}(\Omega)}^{2}\approx\|\hat{u}_{\mathcal{R}}\|_{H^{1}_{0,\mathrm{D}}(\Omega)}^{2}={\bm{\alpha}}(u)^{T}\bm{G}{\bm{\alpha}}(u)=\bm{r}(u)^{T}\bm{G}^{-1}\bm{r}(u)

(10)

where the quality of the approximation depends on the capacity of the space
\mathcal{V}
to approximate
\hat{u}_{\mathcal{R}}
.

Therefore, an appropriate choice of a discrete loss function is

\mathcal{L}(u):=\bm{r}(u)^{T}\bm{G}^{-1}\bm{r}(u).

(11)

We refer to
[
38
]
for more details on the construction and the analysis of the loss function (
11
). The computation of Eq. (
11
) requires the (off-line) construction and inversion of the Gram matrix, which can be computationally expensive and numerically unstable for certain choices of the subspace
\mathcal{V}
and its basis functions. We address this computational challenge by concentrating on the ideas of the DFR method applied to rectangular domains, in which the basis is orthonormal, and the inversion of the Gram matrix is trivial. The construction of such a basis is described in
[
43
]
in the case of solving problems in the Hilbert space
H^{1}_{0,\mathrm{D}}(\Omega)
and in
[
42
]
for the case of solving the time-harmonic Maxwell’s equations. Section
4.1
summarizes this construction for
H^{1}_{0,\mathrm{D}}(\Omega)
on rectangular domains, while Section
7.1
extends it to the case of polygonal domains.

4.1
The DFR method on rectangular domains

Let
\Omega
be a rectangular subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
(referred to as an
n
-rectangular domain), which can be expressed as the Cartesian product of
n
intervals
\Omega:=\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n}(a_{j},b_{j})
(or its rotations). In this case, a well-known set of basis functions for the space
H^{1}_{0,\mathrm{D}}(\Omega)
is built using the eigenvectors of
(1-\Delta)
(a classical construction described in
[
15
,
43
]
). Specifically, the orthonormal basis functions of
H^{1}_{0,\mathrm{D}}(\Omega)
in
n
-dimensions are the tensor products of the following functions in 1D:

\Phi_{k}=\frac{\varphi_{k}}{\|\varphi_{k}\|_{H^{1}_{0,\mathrm{D}}(\Omega)}},\text{ with }\,{\varphi}_{k}(x)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{b-a}}\left\{\begin{array}[]{l l}\sin\left(k\left(\frac{\pi(x-a)}{b-a}\right)\right)&{\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}}=\{a,b\},\\
\sin\left(\left(k-\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(\frac{\pi(x-a)}{b-a}\right)\right)&{\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}}=\{a\},\\
\cos\left(\left(k-\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(\frac{\pi(x-a)}{b-a}\right)\right)&{\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}}=\{b\},\\
\cos\left((k-1)\left(\frac{\pi(x-a)}{b-a}\right)\right)&{\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}}=\emptyset,\end{array}\right.

(12)

where
k\in\mathcal{I}
, with
\mathcal{I}
being an infinite index set.

Given a cut-off frequency, we choose a finite index subset
\mathcal{J}
and define
\mathcal{V}=\text{span}\left(\{\Phi_{k}\}_{k\in\mathcal{J}}\right)
. Leveraging the orthogonality of the functions in the set
\{\Phi_{k}\}_{k\in\mathcal{J}}
, the DFR method approximates the solution of Eq. (
4
) using the following discrete loss function:

\mathcal{L}(u)=\sum\limits_{k\in\mathcal{J}}\langle\mathcal{R}(u),\Phi_{k}\rangle_{H^{*}_{\mathrm{D}}\times H^{1}_{0,\mathrm{D}}}^{2}=\sum\limits_{k\in\mathcal{J}}\left(b(u,\Phi_{k})-\ell(\Phi_{k})\right)^{2}.

(13)

Equations (
11
) and (
13
) are identical in the DFR method since its Gram matrix is the identity.

Remark 4.1

(Numerical integration)
.

We calculate
\mathcal{L}(u)
by numerically evaluating integrals involving the basis functions and their derivatives. Since the basis functions
\Phi_{k}
in Eq. (
12
) consist of products of sine and cosine functions, this naturally leads to the idea of using the Discrete Sine/Cosine Transforms (DST/DCT) as a quadrature rule
[
8
]
. Some scenarios, such as problems with low-regularity solutions, may require many integration points (and possibly non-uniformly distributed), translating to high computational costs. Nevertheless, we do not focus on designing efficient integration rules in this paper and leave this as part of our future work.

5
The optimization method

We use Adam
[
28
]
as the optimization method responsible for adjusting the hyper-parameters of our NNs. We enhance the optimizer performance calculating with a least squares (LS) approach the weights of the last hidden layer of the NN (see
[
14
,
21
]
for further details of this hybrid approach).

When using the loss function (
13
) in a neural network of the form (
7
), one defines a quadratic system of equations with respect to the parameters
\bm{W}_{L}
. Therefore, we can improve the solution by solving the following least-squares problem:

\bm{W}_{L}:=\text{arg}\min_{\bm{W}}\|\bm{A}\bm{W}-\bm{b}\|^{2}_{\ell^{2}},

(14)

where
\bm{A}:=[b(\mathcal{N}_{L\text{-}1}^{i},\Phi_{k})]_{i,k}
is not necessarily a square matrix,
\mathcal{N}_{L\text{-}1}^{i}
is the
i
-th node of the last hidden layer, and
\bm{b}:=[\ell(\Phi_{k})]_{k}
is the right hand side vector.
The solution to the least squares system (
14
) can be found by solving the normal equations. Thus, the weights of the final layer consistently correspond to optimal values, given the variable parameters
\bm{W}_{j}
and
\bm{b}_{j}
for
j\in\{1,\dots,L\text{-}1\}
, which are subsequently tuned through the gradient descent algorithm.

6
The DFR-based Domain Decomposition method

This section applies the DFR mathematical framework to solve problems of the form of Eq. (
4
) through domain decomposition. Here, the analysis is limited to domains expressed as finite unions of
n
-rectangles, specifically, domains meeting the following assumption:

Assumption 6.1
.

The open, bounded domain
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
with
n=1,2
or
3
, is represented by a finite union of open
n
-rectangles,
\Omega=\bigcup\limits_{i=1}^{m}\Omega_{i}
where each
\Omega_{i}
is a (possibly rotated) open
n
-rectangle.

If
\Omega
is connected, each subdomain must overlap with at least one other, and each interior point of
\Omega
is in the interior of at least one subdomain. Thus, Assumption
6.1
describes an overlapping subdomain decomposition of
\Omega
. Moreover, there exist
m_{c}\in{\mathbb{N}}
with
m_{c}\leq m
, such that

\text{card}\{i\,:\,x\in\Omega_{i}\}\leq m_{c}\quad\forall x\in\Omega.

Following Section
4
, a possible solution strategy for the problem (
4
) on a domain
\Omega
, satisfying Assumption
6.1
, is to construct a finite-dimensional subspace
\mathcal{V}
within
H^{1}_{0,\mathrm{D}}(\Omega)
as follows:

\mathcal{V}=\text{span}\left(\bigcup\limits_{i=1}^{m}\{\Phi_{k}^{(i)}\}_{k\in\mathcal{J}^{(i)}}\right),

(15)

where, for each
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
, the set
\{\Phi_{k}^{(i)}\}_{k\in\mathcal{J}^{(i)}}
is a chosen finite-dimensional subset of basis functions within the following space:

H_{i}:=\left\{v\in H^{1}(\Omega):\text{supp}(v)\subset\bar{\Omega}_{i},\,\gamma_{\mathrm{D}}(v)=0\right\}.

(16)

Using this approach, one constructs the Gram matrix involving all the basis functions in
\mathcal{V}
and employ Eq. (
11
) as a loss function. However, a significant challenge arises due to the overlapping of the subdomains
\Omega_{i}
. This overlapping results in the non-orthogonality of the basis functions and potentially a high condition number for the Gram matrix, leading to numerical instabilities and high computational costs. We avoid inverting the Gram matrix by extending the DFR method using a mathematical construction that circumvents the inversion requirement.

Assumption 6.2
.

Consider a Hilbert space
H
and a given set of closed subspaces
\{H_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
within
H
. For all
v\in H
, there exists
\{v_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
with
v_{i}\in H_{i}
for all
i
, and
v=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}
.

Guaranteeing Assumption
6.2
on general domains is not trivial. Constructing such a decomposition requires careful consideration of the complexity of each geometry, including challenging features like (possibly multiple) re-entrant corners and singular points. Section
7.1
and Appendix
A
describe an appropriate decomposition procedure for two cases of interest.

6.1
The
\star
-norm

Now, our goal is to bound (above and below) up to a constant the dual norm of the residual
\|{\mathcal{R}(u)}\|_{H^{*}_{\mathrm{D}}(\Omega)}
by the sum of dual norms over each of the subdomains.

Definition 6.3
.

Let
H
be a Hilbert space with dual space
H^{*}
. Given a function
f\in H^{*}
, we define the
\star
-norm as

\left\|f\right\|_{\star}=\sqrt{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\|f\|_{H^{*}_{i}}^{2}}\quad,

where
\{H_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
satisfies Assumption
6.2
and
H^{*}_{i}
denotes the dual space of
H_{i}
.

\|\cdot\|_{\star}
is an equivalent norm to the classical norm of the dual space.

Proposition 6.4
.

Let
H
be a Hilbert space with dual space
H^{*}
. If there exists a set of closed subspaces
\{H_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
satisfying Assumption
6.2
, then
f\in H^{*}
satisfies

\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\|f\|_{\star}\leq\|f\|_{H^{*}}\leq\xi\left(\{H_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}\right)\|f\|_{\star},

(17)

with

\xi\left(\{H_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}\right):=\sup\limits_{v\in H}\frac{1}{\|v\|_{H}}\min\left\{\sqrt{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\|v_{i}\|_{H}^{2}}:v_{i}\in H_{i}\text{ and }v=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}\right\}<+\infty.

(18)

Appendix
B
detils the proof of Proposition
6.4
.

From Eqs. (
1
) and (
17
), we estimate the error using the
\star
-norm of the residual as follows:

\frac{1}{M\cdot\sqrt{m}}\|\mathcal{R}(u)\|_{\star}\leq\|u-u^{*}\|_{H^{1}_{0,\mathrm{D}}(\Omega)}\leq\frac{\xi\left(\{H_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}\right)}{\gamma}\|\mathcal{R}(u)\|_{\star}.

(19)

When constructing a subdomain decomposition
\{\Omega_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
satisfying Assumption
6.1
, the overlap constant
m_{c}
allows us to regard the union of non-overlapping subdomains as one. In the theory of domain decomposition methods, this
coloring strategy

[
4
]
allows us to sharpen the estimates (
17
) and (
19
).
From Eq. (
19
), we obtain that the
{\star}
-norm of the weak residual estimates the error, but we highlight that the quality of this estimate deteriorates as
m_{c}\to\infty
, making it desirable to use as few overlapping subdomains as possible to cover
\Omega
.
Also, whilst our proof guarantees that
\xi
is finite (see Appendix
A
), it may, in principle, be large. Providing estimations in general domains is beyond the scope of this work; however, in Theorem
C.4
, we show that for the L-shaped domain, covered by two rectangular domains, the constant is at most
\sqrt{2}
.

6.2
The DFR-DD loss function

Applying the theory of Section
4.1
, and considering that each subdomain
\Omega_{i}
is an
n
-rectangle, we can approximate the dual norm of the residual within each subdomain
\Omega_{i}
. We use a suitable set of local basis functions to this aim, as Eq. (
12
) defines. Subsequently, the local approximations of the dual norm of the weak residual are used to calculate the
\star
-norm.

Let
\Omega=\bigcup\limits_{i=1}^{m}\Omega_{i}
where each
\Omega_{i}
is an
n
-rectangle, satisfying Assumption
6.1
. Given
\{H_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
satisfying Assumption
6.2
, for each
i
, we choose a cut-off frequency and denote
\mathcal{J}^{(i)}
the corresponding finite set of indices. The dual norm of the residual constrained to each subdomain
\Omega_{i}
is such that

\|{\mathcal{R}(u)}\|_{H_{i}^{*}}^{2}\approx\sum\limits_{k\in\mathcal{J}^{(i)}}\langle\mathcal{R}(u),\Phi_{k}^{(i)}\rangle_{H_{i}^{*}\times H_{i}}^{2},

(20)

where, for each
i
,
\{\Phi_{k}^{(i)}\}_{k\in\mathcal{J}^{(i)}}
is a set of orthonormal basis functions spanning a finite-dimensional subspace of
H_{i}
. Therefore, we compute the
\star
-norm of the residual operator as

\|{\mathcal{R}(u)}\|_{\star}^{2}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\|{\mathcal{R}(u)}\|_{H_{i}^{*}}^{2}\approx\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\sum\limits_{k\in\mathcal{J}^{(i)}}\langle\mathcal{R}(u),\Phi_{k}^{(i)}\rangle_{H_{i}^{*}\times H_{i}}^{2}.

(21)

Finally, we minimize the following loss function:

{\mathcal{L}}(u):={\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\hat{\mathcal{L}}(u;\Omega_{i})},\quad\text{ with }\quad\hat{\mathcal{L}}(u;\Omega_{i}):=\sum\limits_{k\in\mathcal{J}^{(i)}}\langle\mathcal{R}(u),\Phi_{k}^{(i)}\rangle_{H_{i}^{*}\times H_{i}}^{2}.

(22)

The calculation of
\hat{\mathcal{L}}(u;\Omega_{i})
at each training iteration step is independent of the others. Therefore, the local loss functions can be computed in parallel.

The accuracy of our DFR-based domain decomposition method relies on two crucial factors (apart from the errors introduced by numerical integration): the nature of the subdomain decomposition of
\Omega
and the truncation error associated with the number of Fourier modes (basis functions) used in the computation of Eq. (
22
). We elaborate on both aspects:

•

The accuracy of using
\|\mathcal{R}(u)\|_{\star}
as an estimate of the error
\|u-u^{*}\|_{H^{1}_{0,\mathrm{D}}(\Omega)}
depends on the constants appearing in the discrete versions of (
1
) and (
19
). Note that, the constants bounding the norm of the error in (
1
) are problem-dependent.

For instance, in Poisson’s problem, the dual norm of the residual and norm of the error are equal, as
M=\gamma=1
in (
1
). However, these constants may be highly disparate when the PDE is described by a bilinear form distinct from the canonical inner product on
H^{1}(\Omega)
, as in the Helmholtz equation. On the other hand, the bounds in (
1
) also depend on the number of overlapping subdomains used to cover
\Omega
. If the subspaces
H_{i}
are orthogonal, then it is straightforward to show that
\xi=1
, the
\star
-norm is precisely the dual norm. In our case of domain decompositions, the subdomains necessarily overlap, and thus, the spaces cannot be pairwise orthogonal. Therefore, we aim to compute the loss function (
22
) using the minimum feasible number of overlapping rectangles.

•

In Eq. (
22
), each term
\hat{\mathcal{L}}(u;\Omega_{i})
approximates the norm
\|{\mathcal{R}(u)}\|_{H_{i}^{*}}^{2}
. For
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
, we construct a finite set of indices
\mathcal{J}^{(i)}
by choosing a cut-off frequency in each coordinate direction. This choice introduces a truncation error, which decreases only as additional Fourier modes are used in the calculation. On the other side, the computational cost of the method increases significantly as the number of Fourier modes increases.

7.2
Local refinement and adaptivity

We now propose a strategy to increase the local accuracy of the DFR method. Since the subdomains
\Omega_{i}
in Assumption
6.1
may overlap, we propose a strategy that includes additional subdomains in the original decomposition of
\Omega
. By doing so, we cover the potential singularities of the solution and increase the resolution of the approximation only where it is needed.

7.2.1
Local refinement

Whilst the
\star
-norm is equivalent to the dual norm in certain circumstances, our capacity to evaluate it numerically is limited principally by using a finite set of spectral test functions on each subdomain. Considering a single rectangular domain, this issue would be apparent without domain decomposition. We may view the addition of higher frequency basis functions over the given subdomains as a case of
p
-refinement on a global level. However, the number of basis functions required for a particular problem, particularly in higher dimensional problems, may become numerically infeasible. As such, it would be more desirable to consider only local refinement to improve the computational costs of evaluating the loss function. We outline a heuristic argument in the following where, for simplicity, we consider only
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
as our function space.

Consider a set of subdomains
\Omega_{i}
, each having a corresponding set of spectral basis functions
\{\Phi_{k}^{(i)}\}_{k\in\mathcal{J}^{(i)}}
, each over a finite index set
\mathcal{J}^{(i)}
that may depend on
i
. Given a candidate solution
u
, as in Section
4
, let
\hat{u}_{\mathcal{R}}
denote the Riesz representative of the residual. If the bilinear form
b
is the canonical inner product on
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
, then
\hat{u}_{\mathcal{R}}=u-u^{*}
. Generally, however, the dependence will be non-local. Without integration errors, the component of the loss
\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{L}}(u;\Omega_{i})=\|Q_{\mathcal{J}^{(i)}}\hat{u}_{R}\|_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}^{2}
, where
\displaystyle Q_{\mathcal{J}^{(i)}}:H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\to\text{span}\left(\{\Phi_{k}^{(i)}\}_{k\in\mathcal{J}^{(i)}}\right)
is the orthogonal projection onto those basis functions.

If
u
has been trained so that the discretized loss function is sufficiently small, then, approximately speaking,
\hat{u}_{R}
is almost orthogonal to the entire set of basis functions
\bigcup\limits_{i=1}^{m}\{\Phi_{k}^{(i)}\}_{k\in\mathcal{J}^{(i)}}
. If
\hat{u}_{R}
is sufficiently localized, in the sense that a significant fraction of its norm is contained in a subdomain that we denote
\Omega_{m+1}
with corresponding function space
H_{m+1}=H^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{m+1})
, then it would be an obvious choice to include
\|\mathcal{R}(u)\|_{H^{*}_{m+1}}
into the loss so that, in a second-stage training, these localized errors can be mitigated. Of course, we are limited again by the finiteness of our test function space, yielding a new set of basis functions
\mathcal{J}^{(m+1)}
and discretized loss

{\mathcal{L}}(u):={\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m+1}\hat{\mathcal{L}}(u;\Omega_{i})},\quad\text{ with }\quad\hat{\mathcal{L}}(u;\Omega_{i})=\sum\limits_{k\in\mathcal{J}^{(i)}}\langle\mathcal{R}(u),\Phi_{k}^{(i)}\rangle_{H_{i}^{*}\times H_{i}}^{2},

(23)

and

\hat{\mathcal{L}}(u;\Omega_{m+1})=\sum\limits_{k\in\mathcal{J}^{(m+1)}}\langle\mathcal{R}(u),\Phi_{k}^{(i)}\rangle_{H^{*}_{m+1}\times H_{m+1}}^{2}=\|Q_{\mathcal{J}^{(m+1)}}\hat{u}_{\mathcal{R}}\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}.

(24)

This motivates the use of
\hat{\mathcal{L}}(u;\Omega_{m+1})
as both an error indicator for localized errors of the residual – recalling that these are, generally, not equivalent to localized errors of the solution – as well as a new component added to the loss in a post-refinement training stage.

However, there is a subtle issue behind this argument. The orthogonal projection
Q_{\mathcal{J}_{i}}\hat{u}_{\mathcal{R}}
may be zero over a subdomain even if
\hat{u}_{\mathcal{R}}
is non-zero, which will occur if, for example,
\hat{u}_{\mathcal{R}}
is harmonic over the given region. Our error estimator is a local error estimator for the Riesz representative of the residual
modulo functions that are harmonic on the given subdomain
. If, however,
\hat{u}_{\mathcal{R}}
is harmonic on the entirety of
\Omega
, the total error is necessarily zero due to the imposed boundary conditions. Qualitatively, our refinement strategy is thus to identify spatial regions where the deviation of
\hat{u}_{R}
from a harmonic function is large and include more localized test functions over this region into the loss to lead to a harmonic, and consequently zero, total error.

Although the use of (
23
) may deteriorate the correlation between the loss function and the error’s energy norm, alternative strategies such as choosing Eq. (
13
) as minimization target result in a Gram matrix that is expensive to invert (or singular), rendering the RVPINN strategy infeasible and strengthening our approach.

There are two options for refining a subdomain decomposition of
\Omega
. The first and simplest is to define the location and the dimensions of the new
n
-rectangles
ad-hoc
. This type of refinement is heavily influenced by prior knowledge about the problem and the solution. On the other hand, an automatic adaptive refinement of the subdomain decomposition is possible. That is, given an error indicator –in our case, the local contributions to the loss function– one adds smaller subdomains in the decomposition only within the marked regions. This idea relates to the traditional mesh refinement techniques used in finite element methods
[
1
,
35
]
and to recent refinement strategies using dual norms
[
9
,
39
,
13
]
. Adding new subdomains to the initial domain decomposition of
\Omega
increases the accuracy of the approximation, as in
h
-refinement techniques (see
[
2
,
16
]
). Conversely, one can add Fourier modes to specific subdomains, mimicking
p
-refinement techniques (see
[
3
,
2
]
). Hence, our methodology may be placed within the context of both
h
- and
p
-refinements in the test space.

9
Conclusions

This work extends the Deep Fourier Residual (DFR) method proposed in
[
43
]
to handle polygonal geometries and to deal with low-regularity solutions. We propose an iterative subdomain decomposition algorithm guided by an error indicator based on the neural network’s loss function. Our method overcomes some of the limitations of traditional Fourier-based approaches. We successfully approximate finer-scale structures in solutions using high-frequency test functions only in specific domain regions. Our adaptive approach eliminates the need for excessive Fourier modes with global support, making it a good fit for PDEs with solutions with peaks and singularities.

Under reasonable assumptions, our method provides good-quality solutions while avoiding the computational cost associated with the inversion of the Gram matrix, a requirement to calculate the loss function in the theory of the Robust Variational Physics-Informed Neural Networks
[
38
]
. Furthermore, integrating a hybrid optimization approach accelerates Adam’s convergence rate, making the training process more efficient. Our numerical experiments consistently validate the strong correlation between the loss function and the
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
-norm of the error. In 2D, we demonstrate the method’s performance using two non-rectangular domains, while in 1D, we provide examples that involve solutions with very low regularity, effectively highlighting the adaptive capabilities of the DFR method.

We emphasize that through our numerical experiments, we open up a discussion for future work on highly relevant topics, such as the selection of the initial learning rate when using adaptive processes, which may vary depending on the spatial resolution, as well as numerical integration methods that may require a-priori knowledge and on-line modifications when dealing with low-regularity solutions.

Appendix A
Domain decomposition over polygonal domains

Here we provide rigorous results to apply the DFR-based domain decomposition method to rectilinear polygons, in particular to the L-shape and pentagonal domains. Our aim is to show that Assumption
6.2
holds in the given domains, to ensure the validity of our method.

For simplicity, here we focus only on decompositions of functions in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
. An extension of our method to the case of Neumann (and Robin) boundary conditions is straightforward.

Consider
\{\Omega_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
to be a collection of rectangular open domains, not necessarily parallel. We define
\Omega=\bigcup\limits_{i=1}^{m}\Omega_{i}
. Our approach is to use partitions of unity. If a Lipschitz partition of unity over
\{\Omega_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
,
\{\rho_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
exists, then the mapping
v\mapsto\rho_{i}v
would give a decomposition of
v=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}
with
v_{i}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{i})
. Then:

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla(\rho_{i}v)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}\leq 2\int_{\Omega}v^{2}|\nabla\rho_{i}|^{2}+\rho_{i}^{2}|\nabla v|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}\leq 2\max(\|\nabla\rho_{i}\|_{\infty},1)\int_{\Omega}|\nabla v|^{2}+v^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}.

(35)

Unfortunately, some geometries do not permit Lipschitz partitions of unity. If the Lipschitz constant does not grow too rapidly, as we are considering functions in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
, we may employ Hardy’s inequality
[
33
]
, which states that there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{d({\bm{x}},\partial\Omega)^{2}}v^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}\leq C\int_{\Omega}|\nabla v|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}

(36)

for all
v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
. Thus, if
|\nabla\rho_{i}|^{2}\leq C\frac{1}{d({\bm{x}},\partial\Omega)^{2}}
, we may define the decomposition
v=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\rho_{i}v
, despite
\{\rho_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
having unbounded gradient at the boundary.

Here, the methodology is as follows:

1.

Define an appropriate continuous partition of unity over the subdomains (Definition
A.1
).

2.

Demonstrate that the partition is Lipschitz on closed subsets of
\bar{\Omega}
that do not contain certain singular points (Corollary
A.8
).

3.

Construct explicit local partitions of unity in neighbourhoods of the problematic points that, while not Lipschitz, have controlled growth of the gradient that permits
H^{1}_{0,\mathrm{D}}(\Omega)
-estimates (Propositions
A.9
and
A.10
).

4.

Combine the global partition of unity from (2) with the local partitions from (3) via cutoff-functions to provide the required decomposition (Theorem
A.11
).

Our approach to the first two steps is general. The local analysis of singular points will depend on the particular geometry, and we do not aim to provide a general theorem here, rather an outline on how the method may be applied to particular cases in a replicable way. The local partitions of unity will be based on a simple, explicit representation in polar coordinates.

Definition A.1
.

Let
\Omega=\bigcup\limits_{i=1}^{m}\Omega_{i}
be a Lipschitz polygon. We define the functions
p_{i}:\bar{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}
and
{\rho_{i}:\bar{\Omega}\setminus\{x:\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}({\bm{x}})=0\}\to\mathbb{R}}
by

p_{i}({\bm{x}})=d({\bm{x}},\Omega\setminus\Omega_{i})=\inf\limits_{u\in\Omega\setminus\Omega_{i}}|x-y|,\quad\text{ and }\quad\rho_{i}({\bm{x}})=\frac{p_{i}({\bm{x}})}{\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}p_{j}({\bm{x}})}.

(37)

We denote the partition by
\bm{\rho}=\{\rho_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
.

Proposition A.2
.

\bm{\rho}
defines a continuous partition of unity of
\Omega=\bigcup\limits_{i=1}^{m}\Omega_{i}
.

Proof.

As each
p_{i}
is a distance function, it is necessarily continuous and non-negative. If
{\bm{x}}\in\Omega_{i}
for some
i
, as
\Omega_{i}
is open,
p_{i}({\bm{x}})
is necessarily positive. In particular, the functions
\{\rho_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
are well-defined on
\Omega
as
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}({\bm{x}})\neq 0
on
\Omega
.
p_{i}({\bm{x}})
is trivially zero for
{\bm{x}}\in\Omega\setminus\Omega_{i}
. Thus it is immediate that
\rho_{i}
is positive on
\Omega_{i}
and zero otherwise, and the construction gives
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\rho_{i}({\bm{x}})=1
.
∎

Whilst
\bm{\rho}
is continuous on
\Omega
, we need to bound its gradient. Furthermore, we note that its definition is implicitly ill-defined at regions in
\partial\Omega
where
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}({\bm{x}})=0
. We introduce the following definition.

Definition A.3
.

Let
\Omega=\bigcup\limits_{i=1}^{m}\Omega_{i}
be a Lipschitz domain. We define a point
{\bm{x}}\in\bar{\Omega}
to be a
\bm{\rho}
-singular point if
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}({\bm{x}})=0
, and to be a
\bm{\rho}
-regular point otherwise.

We choose the name
\bm{\rho}
-singular point
as we will show later that these are precisely the points at which the derivative of the partition of unity we consider is unbounded, it is clear from the definition of
\bm{\rho}
that these points may introduce singularities. This is made precise in the following proposition

Proposition A.4
.

Each
\rho_{i}
is differentiable almost everywhere, and

|\nabla\rho_{i}({\bm{x}})|\leq\frac{n+1}{\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}p_{j}({\bm{x}})}

(38)

almost everywhere on
\bar{\Omega}
. In particular, each
\rho_{i}
is Lipschitz on closed subsets of
\bar{\Omega}
that do not contain any singular points.

Proof.

From the argument of Proposition
A.2
, we have that
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}({\bm{x}})>0
on
\Omega
. In particular, the set of singular points is of measure zero, as it is contained in
\partial\Omega
.
Each
p_{i}
is a distance function, and is thus Lipschitz with Lipschitz constant at most
1
and differentiable almost everywhere. It is then immediate that
\rho_{i}
differentiable almost everywhere on
\Omega
as each
p_{j}
is, and it remains only to estimate the derivative. This is straightforward, as

\nabla\rho_{i}({\bm{x}})=\frac{1}{\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}p_{j}({\bm{x}})}\nabla p_{i}({\bm{x}})-p_{i}({\bm{x}})\frac{\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}\nabla p_{j}({\bm{x}})}{\left(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}p_{j}({\bm{x}})\right)^{2}}.

(39)

We then use that each
p_{j}
is positive giving
\frac{p_{i}({\bm{x}})}{\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}p_{j}({\bm{x}})}\leq 1
, and that
|\nabla p_{i}({\bm{x}})|\leq 1
almost everywhere to obtain

|\nabla\rho_{i}({\bm{x}})|\leq\frac{1}{\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}p_{j}({\bm{x}})}+\frac{p_{i}({\bm{x}})}{\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}p_{j}({\bm{x}})}\frac{\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}|\nabla\rho_{j}({\bm{x}})|}{\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}p_{j}({\bm{x}})}\leq\frac{1+n}{\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}p_{j}({\bm{x}})}.

(40)

In particular, as each
p_{i}
is non-negative and continuous, we have that on any closed subset of
\bar{\Omega}
not containing any
\bm{\rho}
-singular points,
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}({\bm{x}})
is bounded away from zero and thus the gradient of
\rho_{i}
is bounded almost everywhere, giving that
\rho_{i}
is Lipschitz on closed sets containing no
\bm{\rho}
-singular points.
∎

Whilst the definition of
\bm{\rho}
-singular points is useful for estimating the regularity of the partition, it is not immediately clear how they may be identified in practice. Our next proposition gives an equivalent condition based on the geometry that will be more useful for identifying
\bm{\rho}
-singular points. In particular, this will be used to show that the set of
\bm{\rho}
-singular points is finite and can be be identified by inspection of the vertices of
\Omega
and
\Omega_{i}
, and a local analysis at each is then straightforward.

Proposition A.5
.

A point
{\bm{x}}\in\bar{\Omega}
is a
\bm{\rho}
-regular point if and only if there exists some
\delta>0
and
i=1,...,m
such that
B_{\delta}({\bm{x}})\cap\Omega=B_{\delta}({\bm{x}})\cap\Omega_{i}
.

Proof.

First, assume that
x
is a
\bm{\rho}
-regular point. This implies that
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}({\bm{x}})>0
, and in particular
p_{i}({\bm{x}})>0
for some
i
. Thus, as
d({\bm{x}},\Omega\setminus\Omega_{i})>0
, for sufficiently small
\delta
,
\emptyset=B_{\delta}({\bm{x}})\cap(\Omega\setminus\Omega_{i})=(B_{\delta}({\bm{x}})\cap\Omega\setminus(B_{\delta}({\bm{x}})\cap\Omega_{i})
. As
\Omega_{i}\subset\Omega
, this yields
B_{\delta}({\bm{x}})\cap\Omega=B_{\delta}({\bm{x}})\cap\Omega_{i}
.

For the reverse implication, if
B_{\delta}({\bm{x}})\cap\Omega=B_{\delta}({\bm{x}})\cap\Omega_{i}
for some
i=1,...,m
and
\delta>0
, then, by reversing the previous argument,
\emptyset=B_{\delta}({\bm{x}})\setminus(\Omega\setminus\Omega_{i})
, so that
B_{\delta}({\bm{x}})\subset\Omega\setminus\Omega_{i}
and thus
p_{i}({\bm{x}})=d({\bm{x}},\Omega\setminus\Omega_{i})>\delta
.
∎

With this equivalent characterisation of
\bm{\rho}
-singular and
\bm{\rho}
-regular points, we can demonstrate that any point that simultaneously belongs to an edge-segment of
\partial\Omega
and
\partial\Omega_{i}
for some

\bm{\rho}
-regular point.

For clarity, we refer to a region
\Gamma\subset\partial U

\partial U
. In particular, for small
\delta>0
, if
{\bm{x}}_{0}
is in an edge segment of

, then, for some
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and

,
B_{\delta}({\bm{x}}_{0})\cap U=\{{\bm{x}}\in B_{\delta}({\bm{x}}_{0}):{\bm{x}}\cdot\bm{v}<c\}

Proposition A.6
.

If
\Gamma\subset\partial\Omega
is an edge-segment of both
\Omega
and
\Omega_{i}
for some

, then each
{\bm{x}}\in\Gamma
is a regular point.

Proof.

Let
{\bm{x}}_{0}\in\Gamma
. As
\Gamma
is an edge segment for
\Omega_{i}
and
\Omega
, we have that there exists
\delta>0
,
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
with
B_{\delta}({\bm{x}}_{0})\cap\Omega=\{{\bm{x}}\in B_{\delta}({\bm{x}}_{0}):{\bm{x}}\cdot\bm{v}<c\}
. It must also hold that, for sufficiently small
\delta
,
B_{\delta}({\bm{x}}_{0})\cap\Omega=\{{\bm{x}}\in B_{\delta}({\bm{x}}_{0}):{\bm{x}}\cdot\bm{v}<c\}
with the same

and

\Omega_{i}
would not be a subset of
\Omega
. In particular,
B_{\delta}({\bm{x}}_{0})\cap\Omega=B_{\delta}({\bm{x}}_{0})\cap\Omega_{i}
, and thus
{\bm{x}}_{0}
is a regular point.
∎

Corollary A.7
.

If
\Omega=\bigcup\limits_{i=1}^{m}\Omega_{i}
is a Lipschitz polygon, then all
\bm{\rho}
-singular points of

\Omega_{i}
or vertices of
\Omega_{i}

Proof.

We have that all
\bm{\rho}
-singular points must be in
\partial\Omega
from Proposition

. From the previous proposition, any point which is in an edge segment of both
\Omega
and

\bm{\rho}

\Omega
or vertices of
\Omega_{i}

\{\Omega_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}

-singular points. One need only consider the vertices of each

and the vertices of

Theorem A.11
.

Let
\Omega=\Omega_{1}\cup\Omega_{2}
be the L-shape or pentagonal domain. Then, for every
v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)

v_{i}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{i})

v=v_{1}+v_{2}

Proof.

Let
\delta\ll 1
and take

for

\varphi({\bm{x}})=0
for

denote the
\bm{\rho}
-singular points of
\Omega
, with

in the case of the L-shape domain,

in the pentagonal domain. Write

. We then have that

A.9

.
∎

Appendix B
Bounds over the
\star
-norm

Given a Hilbert space
H
and a set of closed subspaces
\{H_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
within
H
, we define the minimal energy decomposition of
v\in H
as

\{P_{i}v\}_{i=1}^{m}:=\mathrm{arg}\min\left\{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\|v_{i}\|_{H}^{2}:v_{i}\in H_{i}\text{ and }v=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}\right\}.

(45)

To prove Proposition
6.4
, we refer to the following lemma, which states the well-posedness of the mappings
\{P_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
.

Lemma B.1
.

Let
H
be a Hilbert space. If there exists a set of closed subspaces
\{H_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
satisfying Assumption
6.2
, then the mappings
\{P_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
resulting from the minimal energy decomposition of
v\in H
are bounded linear operators from
H\to H_{i}
. Furthermore,

\xi\left(\{H_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}\right):=\sup\limits_{v\in H}\frac{1}{\|v\|_{H}}\min\left\{\sqrt{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\|v_{i}\|_{H}^{2}}:v_{i}\in H_{i}\text{ and }v=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}\right\}<+\infty.

(46)

Proof.

To prove this lemma, we first show that the mapping
(v_{i})_{i=1}^{m}\to\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}
is a closed mapping from a particular Hilbert space to
H
. We can then show that it has a bounded, linear inverse on the orthogonal complement of its kernel via the bounded inverse theorem, which we show to be the minimal energy decomposition. It then results that
\xi\left(\{H_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}\right)
is the norm of this mapping, which is necessarily finite.

We start by defining the Hilbert space
\hat{H}=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{m}H_{i}
, with the natural inner product

\langle(v_{i}^{1})_{i=1}^{m},(v_{i}^{2})_{i=1}^{m}\rangle_{\hat{H}}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\langle v^{1}_{i},v_{i}^{2}\rangle_{H}.

Using this definition, we observe that the minimisation problem (
45
) admits a unique solution, so that
\{P_{i}v\}_{i=1}^{m}
is well defined, as it corresponds to the minimisation of a strictly convex norm over a Hilbert space with a closed constraint.

We next consider the map
A:\hat{H}\to H
given by
A(v_{i})_{i=1}^{m}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}
, which is clearly continuous. In particular, its kernel is closed. Furthermore, its range is
H
by assumption. In this case, we may perform an orthogonal decomposition of
\hat{H}=\ker(A)\oplus\ker(A)^{\perp}
, where
A
is injective on
\ker(A)^{\perp}
. In particular, we see that if
\tilde{A}:\text{ker}(A)^{\perp}\to H
is the restriction of
A
, then
\tilde{A}
is a continuous, linear bijection between Hilbert spaces, and therefore admits a continuous inverse
\tilde{A}^{-1}:H\to\ker(A)^{\perp}
. We note that
\tilde{A}^{-1}v
solves the corresponding minimisation problem (
45
), as we decompose
(v_{i})_{i=1}^{m}=(v_{i}^{\perp})_{i=1}^{m}\oplus(v_{i}^{\parallel})_{i=1}^{m}\in\text{ker}(A)^{\perp}\oplus\text{ker}(A)
. Then
A(v_{i})_{i=1}^{m}=A(v_{i}^{\perp})_{i=1}^{m}
and thus if
A(v_{i})_{i=1}^{m}=v
,

\|(v_{i})_{i=1}^{m}\|^{2}_{\hat{H}}=\|(v_{i}^{\perp})_{i=1}^{m}\|^{2}_{\hat{H}}+\|(v_{i}^{\parallel})_{i=1}^{m}\|^{2}_{\hat{H}}\geq\|(v_{i}^{\perp})_{i=1}^{m}\|^{2}_{\hat{H}}.

(47)

We thus conclude that
\{P_{i}v\}_{i=1}^{m}=\tilde{A}^{-1}v
is a well-defined bounded linear operator.

Finally, we observe that the operator norm of
\tilde{A}^{-1}
, which is necessarily finite, can be expressed as

\begin{split}\|\tilde{A}^{-1}\|=&\sup\limits_{v\in H}\frac{\|\tilde{A}^{-1}v\|_{\hat{H}}}{\|v\|_{H}}=\sup\limits_{v\in H}\frac{1}{\|v\|_{H}}\sqrt{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\|P_{i}v\|_{H}^{2}}\\
=&\sup\limits_{v\in H}\frac{1}{\|v\|_{H}}\min\left\{\sqrt{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\|v_{i}\|_{H}^{2}}:v_{i}\in H,\,\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}=v\right\}=\xi\left(\{H_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}\right)\end{split}

(48)

∎

v=v_{1}^{h}+v_{2}^{h}
. Thus,

\max\limits_{v\in V}\left\{1+\frac{\int_{U_{1,2}}|\nabla(v^{h}_{1}-v^{h}_{2})|^{2}-|\nabla v|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}}{2\left(\int_{U_{1,2}}|\nabla v|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}+\int_{\Omega\setminus U_{1,2}}|\nabla v|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}\right)}\right\}=1+\frac{-2\int_{U_{1,2}}\nabla v_{1}^{h}\cdot\nabla v_{2}^{h}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}}{\int_{U_{1,2}}|\nabla(v_{1}^{h}+v_{2}^{h})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}+\int_{\Omega\setminus U_{1,2}}|\nabla v|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}}.

(62)

∎

Proposition C.3
.

Let
V_{2}=\{v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega):v|_{\partial U_{1,2}}=v_{0}\}
. Then

\begin{split}\sup\limits_{v\in V_{2}}\left\{1+\frac{-2\int_{U_{1,2}}\nabla v_{1}^{h}\cdot\nabla v_{2}^{h}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}}{\int_{U_{1,2}}|\nabla(v_{1}^{h}+v_{2}^{h})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}+\int_{\Omega\setminus U_{1,2}}|\nabla v|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}}\right\}=\\
\max\left(1,\frac{\int_{U_{1,2}}|\nabla v_{1}^{h}|^{2}+|\nabla v_{2}^{h}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}}{\int_{U_{1,2}}|\nabla v_{1}^{h}|^{2}+|\nabla v_{2}^{h}|^{2}+\nabla v_{1}^{h}\cdot\nabla v_{2}^{h}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}}\right)\end{split}

(63)

Proof.

The analysis of the supremum depends on the sign of
\int_{U_{1,2}}\nabla v_{1}^{h}\cdot v_{2}^{h}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}
. As the term appearing in the denominator is non-negative, if
\int_{U_{1,2}}\nabla v_{1}^{h}\cdot v_{2}^{h}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}\geq 0
, then the expression is bounded by
1
, and by taking
\int_{\Omega\setminus U_{1,2}}|\nabla v|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}
large, we see that the supremum of 1 is attainable. Assuming
\int_{U_{1,2}}\nabla v_{1}^{h}\cdot v_{2}^{h}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}<0
, then we have that the expression is a decreasing function of
\int_{\Omega\setminus U_{1,2}}|\nabla v|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}
, thus the maximiser corresponds to the minimiser of the seminorm. The restriction that
v\in V_{2}
prescribes boundary conditions, and by a similar argument as before we have that on
U_{1}
,
v
must satisfy the PDE

\begin{array}[]{r l l}\Delta v=&0&\text{ in }U_{1}\\
v=&v_{0}&\text{ on }\Omega_{1}\cap\partial U_{1}\\
v=&0&\text{ on }\Omega_{2}\cap\partial U_{1}\text{ and }\partial\Omega\cap\partial U_{1}.\end{array}

(64)

weakly. Due to symmetry considerations, as
U_{1}=\{(-x,y):(x,y)\in U_{1,2}\}
, with the correspodning mapping preserving boundary conditions, we then have that
v(x,y)=v_{1}^{h}(-x,y)
on
U_{1}
. Similarly, we have that
v(x,y)=v_{2}^{h}(x,-y)
on
U_{2}
. In particular,
\int_{\Omega\setminus U_{1,2}}|\nabla v|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}=\int_{U_{1,2}}|\nabla v_{1}^{h}|^{2}+|\nabla v_{2}^{h}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}
. Thus

\begin{split}\sup\limits_{v\in V_{2}}\left\{1+\frac{-2\int_{U_{1,2}}\nabla v_{1}^{h}\cdot\nabla v_{2}^{h}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}}{\int_{U_{1,2}}|\nabla(v_{1}^{h}+v_{2}^{h})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}+\int_{\Omega\setminus U_{1,2}}|\nabla v|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}}\right\}&=\max\left(1,1+\frac{-2\int_{U_{1,2}}\nabla v_{1}^{h}\cdot\nabla v_{2}^{h}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}}{\int_{U_{1,2}}|\nabla(v_{1}^{h}+v_{2}^{h})|^{2}+|\nabla v_{1}^{h}|^{2}+|\nabla v_{2}^{h}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}}\right)\\
=\max\left(1,1+\frac{-2\int_{U_{1,2}}\nabla v_{1}^{h}\cdot\nabla v_{2}^{h}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}}{2\int_{U_{1,2}}|\nabla v_{1}^{h}|^{2}+|\nabla v_{2}^{h}|^{2}+\nabla v_{1}^{h}\cdot\nabla v_{2}^{h}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}}\right)&=\max\left(1,\frac{\int_{U_{1,2}}|\nabla v_{1}^{h}|^{2}+|\nabla v_{2}^{h}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}}{\int_{U_{1,2}}|\nabla v_{1}^{h}|^{2}+|\nabla v_{2}^{h}|^{2}+\nabla v_{1}^{h}\cdot\nabla v_{2}^{h}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}}\right)\end{split}

(65)

∎

Then, our main theorem is the following:

Theorem C.4
.

For the L-shape domain,
\xi\left(\{H_{i}\}_{i=1}^{2}\right)\leq\sqrt{2}
.

Proof.

Due to the preceding propositions, we have that

\xi\left(\{H_{i}\}_{i=1}^{2}\right)^{2}=\max\limits_{v_{0}\in H^{\frac{1}{2}}(U_{1,2})}\max\left(1,\frac{\int_{U_{1,2}}|\nabla v_{1}^{h}|^{2}+|\nabla v_{2}^{h}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}}{\int_{U_{1,2}}|\nabla v_{1}^{h}|^{2}+|\nabla v_{2}^{h}|^{2}+\nabla v_{1}^{h}\cdot\nabla v_{2}^{h}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}}\right)

(66)

we then have that

\begin{split}\frac{\int_{U_{1,2}}|\nabla v_{1}^{h}|^{2}+|\nabla v_{2}^{h}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}}{\int_{U_{1,2}}|\nabla v_{1}^{h}|^{2}+|\nabla v_{2}^{h}|^{2}+\nabla v_{1}^{h}\cdot\nabla v_{2}^{h}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}}=&\left(1+\frac{\int_{U_{1,2}}\nabla v_{1}^{h}\cdot\nabla v_{2}^{h}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}}{\int_{U_{1,2}}|\nabla v_{1}^{h}|^{2}+|\nabla v_{2}^{h}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}}\right)^{-1}\end{split}

(67)

By a combination of Cauchy-Schwartz and Young’s inequality we see,

|\langle v_{1}^{h},v_{2}^{h}\rangle_{H^{1}}|\leq\|v_{1}^{h}\|_{H^{1}}\|v_{2}^{h}\|_{H^{1}}\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\|v_{1}^{h}\|_{H^{1}}^{2}+\|v_{2}^{h}\|_{H^{1}}^{2}\right),

(68)

so that
\frac{\int_{U_{1,2}}\nabla v_{1}^{h}\cdot\nabla v_{2}^{h}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}}{\int_{U_{1,2}}|\nabla v_{1}^{h}|^{2}+|\nabla v_{2}^{h}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}}\in\left(-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right)
. Then, as
x\mapsto(1+x)^{-1}
is a decreasing function on
\left(-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right)
, we have that

\left(1+\frac{\int_{U_{1,2}}\nabla v_{1}^{h}\cdot\nabla v_{2}^{h}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}}{\int_{U_{1,2}}|\nabla v_{1}^{h}|^{2}+|\nabla v_{2}^{h}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}}\right)^{-1}\leq\left(1-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{-1}=2.

(69)

Thus
\xi\left(\{H_{i}\}_{i=1}^{2}\right)^{2}\leq\max(1,2)=2
, giving the result.
∎

We note via the proof of Theorem
C.4
, that the constant
\sqrt{2}
may not indeed be sharp. The inequalities in (
68
) are equalities if and only if
v_{1}^{h}=\pm v_{2}^{h}
. This, however, is incompatible with the boundary conditions prescribed on
v_{1}^{h}
and
v_{2}^{h}
.
