Abstract
The use of optimal transport (OT) distances, and in particular entropic-regularised OT distances, is an increasingly popular evaluation metric in many areas of machine learning and data science. Their use has largely been driven by the availability of efficient algorithms such as the Sinkhorn algorithm. One of the drawbacks of the Sinkhorn algorithm for large-scale data processing is that it is a two-phase method, where one first draws a large stream of data from the probability distributions, before applying the Sinkhorn algorithm to the discrete probability measures. More recently, there have been several works developing stochastic versions of Sinkhorn that directly handle continuous streams of data. In this work, we revisit the recently introduced online Sinkhorn algorithm of [ Mensch and Peyré, 2020 ] . Our contributions are twofold: We improve the convergence analysis for the online Sinkhorn algorithm, the new rate that we obtain is faster than the previous rate under certain parameter choices. We also present numerical results to verify the sharpness of our result. Secondly, we propose the compressed online Sinkhorn algorithm which combines measure compression techniques with the online Sinkhorn algorithm. We provide numerical experiments to show practical numerical gains, as well as theoretical guarantees on the efficiency of our approach.

Introduction

A fundamental problem in data processing is the computation of metrics or distances to effectively compare different objects of interest
[
Peyré et al., 2019
]
. In the last decade, it has become apparent that many problems, including image processing
[
Ferradans et al., 2014
,
Ni et al., 2009
]
, natural language processing
[
Xu et al., 2020
]
and genomics
[
Schiebinger et al., 2019
]
, can be modelled using probability distributions and optimal transport (OT) or
Wasserstein distances
have become widely adopted as evaluation metrics. The use of such distances have become especially prevalent in the machine learning community thanks to the vast amount of research in computational aspects of entropic-regularised optimal transport
[
Cuturi, 2013
,
Peyré et al., 2019
]
. The use of entropic-regularised optimal transport has been especially popular since they can be easily computed using the celebrated Sinkhorn algorithm
[
Cuturi, 2013
]
, and such distances are known to have superior statistical properties, circumventing the curse of dimensionality
[
Genevay et al., 2019
]
.

Given the wide-spread interest in computational optimal transport and in particular, entropic regularised distances in large data-processing applications, there have been several lines of work on extending the Sinkhorn algorithm to handle large-scale datasets. The application of the Sinkhorn algorithm typically involves first drawing samples from the distributions of interest and constructing a large pairwise distance matrix, then applying the Sinkhorn algorithm to compute the distance between the sampled empirical distributions. While there have been approaches to accelerate the second step with Nyström compression
[
Altschuler et al., 2019
]
or employing greedy approaches
[
Altschuler et al., 2017
]
, in recent years, there has been an increasing interest in the development of online versions of Sinkhorn that can directly compute OT distances between continuous distributions. One of the computational challenges for computing the OT distance between continuous distributions is that the dual variables (Kantorovich potentials) are continuous functions and one needs to represent these functions in a discrete manner. Two of the main representations found in the literature include the use of reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces
[
Aude et al., 2016
]
and more recently, the online Sinkhorn algorithm
[
Mensch and Peyré, 2020
]
was introduced where the Kantorovich potentials are represented using sparse measures and special kernel functions that exploit the particular structure of OT distances.

Contributions

In this work, we revisit the online Sinkhorn algorithm of
[
Mensch and Peyré, 2020
]
: we improve their theoretical convergence result for this method, and propose a compressed version of this method to alleviate the large memory footprint of this method.
Our contributions can be summarised as follows

•

We provide an updated theoretical convergence rate that is under certain parameter choices is faster than the rate given in
[
Mensch and Peyré, 2020
]
. We also numerically verify that our theoretical analysis is sharp.

•

We propose a compressed version of the online Sinkhorn algorithm. The computational complexity of the online Sinkhorn algorithm grows polynomially with each iteration and it is natural to combine compression techniques with the online Sinkorn algorithm. As explained in Section
3
, the online Sinkhorn algorithm seeks to represent the continuous Kantorovich potentials as measures (super-position of Diracs) and there are some popular methods for measure compression such as kernel recombination
[
Cosentino et al., 2020
,
Hayakawa et al., 2022
,
Adachi et al., 2022
]
) and Nyström method
[
Zhang et al., 2008
]
. We apply compression with a Fourier-based moments approach. We present theoretical complexity analysis and numerical experiments to show that our approach can offer significant computational benefits.

Online Sinkhorn

2.1
The Sinkhorn algorithm

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{X})
denote the set of continuous functions
\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
.
The Kantorovich formulation was first proposed to study the transport plan between two probability distributions
\alpha,\beta
with the minimal cost
[
Kantorovich, 1942
]
:

\min_{\pi\in\Pi(\alpha,\beta)}\int C(x,y)\mathrm{d}\pi(x,y),

(1)

where
\Pi(\alpha,\beta)
is the set of positive measures with fixed marginals
\alpha
and
\beta
,

\Pi(\alpha,\beta)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\left\{\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}^{2})\colon\alpha=\int_{y\in\mathcal{X}}\mathrm{d}\pi(\cdot,y),\;\beta=\int_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathrm{d}\pi(x,\cdot)\right\},

(2)

and

C\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is a given cost function.

The solution to the optimisation problem equation
1
above can be approximated by a strictly convex optimisation problem by adding a regularisation term: The entropic regularised OT problem with the regularisation parameter
\varepsilon>0
is

\underset{\pi\in\Pi(\alpha,\beta)}{\min}\int C(x,y)\mathrm{d}\pi(x,y)+\varepsilon\text{KL}\left(\pi,\alpha\otimes\beta\right),

(3)

where
\alpha\otimes\beta
is the product measure on
\mathcal{X}^{2}
, and
\text{KL}\left(\pi,\alpha\otimes\beta\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\int\log\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\pi}{\mathrm{d}\alpha\otimes\beta}\right)\mathrm{d}\pi
is the Kulback–Leibler divergence
[
Cuturi, 2013
]
.

The following maximisation problem for
\varepsilon>0
is a dual formulation of the entropic regularised OT problem equation
3
:

F_{\alpha,\beta}(f,g)\coloneqq\underset{f,g\in\mathcal{C}\left(\mathcal{X}\right)}{\max}\int f(x)d\alpha(x)+\int g(y)d\beta(y)-\varepsilon\int e^{\frac{f\oplus g-C}{\varepsilon}}d\alpha(x)d\beta(y)

where
\left(f\oplus g-C\right)\left(x,y\right)\coloneqq f(x)+g(y)-C(x,y)
and
\left(f,g\right)
are defined to be the pair of dual potentials
[
Peyré et al., 2019
]
.

The Sinkhorn algorithm works by alternating minimization on the dual problem
F_{\alpha,\beta}
, and is for discrete distributions. So, to apply Sinkhorn, one first draws empirical distributions
\alpha_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{x_{i}}
and
\beta_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{y_{i}}
with
x_{i}\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\alpha
and
y_{i}\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\beta
, then compute iteratively:

\begin{split}u_{t+1}=\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{v_{t,j}}\exp\left(-\frac{C(x_{k},y_{j})}{\varepsilon}\right)\right)_{k=1}^{n},\quad v_{t+1}=\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{u_{t+1,i}}\exp\left(\frac{-C\left(x_{i},y_{k}\right)}{\varepsilon}\right)\right)_{k=1}^{n},\end{split}

(4)

where
u_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\exp\left(-f_{t}/\varepsilon\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
v_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\exp\left(-g_{t}/\varepsilon\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
f_{t},g_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
are the pair of dual potentials at step
t
. The computational complexity is
O\left(n^{2}\log\left(n\right)\delta^{-3}\right)
for reaching

\delta
accuracy
[
Altschuler et al., 2017
,
Mensch and Peyré, 2020
]
.

2.2
The online Sinkhorn algorithm

In the continuous setting, the Sinkhorn iterations operate on functions

where
x_{i}\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\alpha
and
y_{i}\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\beta
. For appropriate learning rate
\eta_{t}
, the online Sinkhorn iterations

2.2.1
Convergence analysis for online Sinkhorn

Following
[
Mensch and Peyré, 2020
]
, convergence can be established under the following three assumptions.

Assumption 1
.

The cost
C:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is
L
-Lipschitz continuous for some Lipschitz constant
L>0
.

Assumption 2
.

(\eta_{t})_{t}
is such that
\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\eta_{t}=\infty
and
\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\eta_{t}^{2}<\infty
, where
0<\eta_{t}<1
for all
t>0
.

Assumption 3
.

\left(b_{t}\right)_{t}
and
\left(\eta_{t}\right)_{t}
satisfy that
\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\frac{\eta_{t}}{\sqrt{b_{t}}}<\infty
.

In order to satisfy Assumptions
2
and
3
, from now on, we let
-1<b<-\frac{1}{2}
and
a-b>1
and take

\eta_{t}=t^{b}\quad\text{and}\quad b_{t}=t^{2a}.

For the online Sinkhorn algorithm, we obtain the following convergence result:

Theorem 1
.

Let
f^{*}
and
g^{*}
denote the optimal potentials. Let
{f}_{t}
and
{g}_{t}
be the output of Algorithm
1
after
t
iterations.
Suppose
\eta_{t}=t^{b}
for
-1<b<-\frac{1}{2}
, and
b_{t}=t^{2a}
with
a-b>1
. For a constant
c>0
,

\delta_{N}\lesssim\exp\left(-cN^{\frac{b+1}{2a+1}}\right)+N^{-\frac{a}{2a+1}}={O}\left(N^{-\frac{a}{2a+1}}\right),

(9)

where

\delta_{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\left\|\left(f_{t\left(N\right)}-f^{*}\right)/\varepsilon\right\|_{var}+\left\|\left(g_{t\left(N\right)}-g^{*}\right)/\varepsilon\right\|_{var},

and
{t\left(N\right)}
is the first iteration number for which
\sum_{i=1}^{t}b_{i}>N
.

Comparison with the previous rate

It was shown in
[
Mensch and Peyré, 2020
, Proposition 4]
that

\delta_{N}\lesssim\exp\left(-cN^{\frac{b+1}{2a+1}}\right)+N^{\frac{b}{2a+1}}.

By taking
b
close to
-1
and
a
close to 0, the asymptotic convergence rate can be made arbitrarily close to
{O}\left(N^{-1}\right)
. There however seems to be an error in the proof (see comment after the sketch proof and the appendix). In contrast, our asymptotic convergence rate is
{O}\left(N^{-\frac{a}{2a+1}}\right)
, which is at best
{O}(N^{-1/2})
. However, our convergence rate is faster whenever

a\geqslant-b
. See Figure
1
.

Sketch of proof

Let
U_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\left(f_{t}-f^{*}\right)/\varepsilon
,
V_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\left(g_{t}-g^{*}\right)/\varepsilon
,
e_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\|U_{t}\|_{var}+\|V_{t}\|_{var}
,
\zeta_{\hat{\beta}_{t}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\left(T_{\beta}(g_{t})-T_{\hat{\beta}_{t}}(g_{t})\right)/\varepsilon
and
\iota_{\hat{\alpha}_{t}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\left(T_{\alpha}(f_{t+1})-T_{\hat{\alpha}_{t}}(f_{t+1})\right)/\varepsilon
, where
T_{\mu}
is defined in Appendix
A.2
. It can be shown that

e_{t+1}\leqslant\left(1-\eta_{t}+\eta_{t}\kappa\right)e_{t}+\eta_{t}\left(\|\zeta_{\beta_{t}}\|_{var}+\|\iota_{\alpha_{t}}\|_{var}\right),

and

\mathbb{E}\left\|\zeta_{\hat{\beta}_{t}}\right\|_{var}\lesssim\frac{A_{1}(f^{*},g^{*},\varepsilon)}{\sqrt{b_{t}}}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}\left\|\iota_{\hat{\alpha}_{t}}\right\|_{var}\lesssim\frac{A_{2}(f^{*},g^{*},\varepsilon)}{\sqrt{b_{t}}},

where
A_{1}(f^{*},g^{*},\varepsilon),A_{2}(f^{*},g^{*},\varepsilon)
are constants depending on
f^{*},g^{*},\varepsilon
.

Denote
S=A_{1}(f^{*},g^{*},\varepsilon)+A_{2}(f^{*},g^{*},\varepsilon)
, then taking expectations

\mathbb{E}e_{t+1}\lesssim\left(1-\eta_{t}+\eta_{t}\kappa\right)\mathbb{E}e_{t}+\frac{S\eta_{t}}{\sqrt{b_{t}}}.

(10)

Applying the Gronwall lemma
[
Mischler, 2018
, Lemma 5.1]
to this recurrence relation, we get

\mathbb{E}e_{t+1}\lesssim\left(\mathbb{E}e_{1}+\frac{S}{a-b-1}\right)\exp\left(\frac{\kappa-1}{b+1}t^{b+1}\right)+t^{-a}.

(11)

The total number of samples
N=O(t^{(2a+1)})
and this substitution completes the proof.
∎

[
Mensch and Peyré, 2020

Compressed online Sinkhorn

3.1
Complexity of online Sinkhorn

The online Sinkhorn algorithm’s prolonged runtime is due to the evaluation of
(u_{t},v_{t})
in equation
8
on an increasing amount of newly drawn data.
In Algorithm
1
, due to the calculation of the cost matrix
C(x_{i},y_{j})
for
{i=(n_{t},n_{t+1}],j=(0,n_{t}]}
in equation
8
, the complexity is
O\left(n_{t}b_{t}\right)=O\left(t^{4a+2}\right)
and the memory cost for
\left(x_{i},y_{i}\right)_{i=(0,n_{t}]}
is
O\left(n_{t}\right)=O\left(t^{2a+1}\right)
. The complexity and memory cost increase polynomially with each iteration; therefore, the Algorithm
1
will require a computing unit with higher capacity as the iteration progresses.

3.2
Compressed online Sinkhorn

To reduce the complexity and memory costs, we propose the
compressed online Sinkhorn
algorithm.
To explain our derivation, we focus on obtaining a compressed version of
u_{t}
; the function
v_{t}
can be treated in a similar manner.
From equation
8
, we have
u_{t}(x)=\int\frac{K_{x}\left(y\right)}{\varphi\left(y\right)}\mathrm{d}\mu\left(y\right)
for
\mu\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{t}}\exp\left(\frac{q_{i,t}}{\varepsilon}\right)\varphi\left(y_{i}\right)\delta_{y_{i}}
,
K_{x}(y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\exp(-C(x,y)/\varepsilon)
and any positive function
\varphi
. The idea of our compression method is to exploit measure compression techniques to replace
\mu
with a measure
\hat{\mu}
made up of
m\ll n_{t}
Diracs. We then approximate
u_{t}
with
\hat{u}_{t}(x)=\int\frac{K_{x}\left(y\right)}{\varphi\left(y\right)}\mathrm{d}\hat{\mu}(y)
.
Let
\varphi=v_{t}=\exp\left(-\frac{g_{t}}{\varepsilon}\right)
, then
\exp\left(\frac{q_{i,t}}{\varepsilon}\right)\varphi\left(y_{i}\right)\leq 1
by the update
3
Algorithm
1
. The weights for
\mu
are hence bounded and it is easier to compress
\mu
.

To ensure that
\hat{u}_{t}\approx u_{t}
, we will enforce that the measure
\hat{\mu}
satisfies

\int P_{k}\left(y\right)\mathrm{d}\mu\left(y\right)=\int P_{k}\left(y\right)\mathrm{d}\hat{\mu}\left(y\right)

(12)

for some appropriate set of functions
\left\{P_{k}\;;\;k\in\Omega\right\}
, where
\Omega
is some set of cardinality
m_{t}
.
Let us introduce a couple of ways to choose such functions.

Example: Gaussian quadrature

In dimension 1 (covered here mostly for pedagogical purposes), one can consider the set of polynomials up to degree
2m-1
, denoted by
\mathbb{P}_{2m-1}
for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
. For well-ordered
y_{i}
, the constraints (
12
) defines the
m
-point Gaussian quadrature
\hat{\mu}=\sum\hat{w}_{i}\delta_{\hat{y}_{i}}
, and both new weights
\hat{w}_{i}
and new nodes
\hat{y}_{i}
are required to achieve this. Numerically, this can be efficiently implemented following the
OPQ
Matlab package
[
Gautschi, 2004
]
and the total complexity for the compression is
O\left(m^{3}+n_{t}m\right)
. To define
\hat{u}_{t}
, solve
e^{\hat{q}_{i,t}/\varepsilon}v_{t}(\hat{y}_{i})=\hat{w}_{i}
for
\hat{q}_{i,t}
and let
\hat{u}_{t}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\exp(\hat{q}_{i,t}/\varepsilon)K_{x}(\hat{y}_{i})
.

Example: Fourier moments

Since we expect that the compressed function
\hat{u}_{t}
satisfies
\hat{u}_{t}\approx u_{t}
, one approach is to ensure that the Fourier moments of
\hat{u}_{t}
and
u_{t}
match on some set of frequencies. Observe that the Fourier transform of
u_{t}(x)=\int\frac{K_{x}}{v_{t}}\left(y\right)\mathrm{d}\mu(y)
can be written as

\int\exp\left(-ikx\right)\int\frac{K_{x}}{v_{t}}\left(y\right)\mathrm{d}\mu\left(y\right)\mathrm{d}x=\int\frac{\hat{K}_{y}(k)}{v_{t}(y)}\mathrm{d}\mu\left(y\right),\quad\text{where}\quad\hat{K}_{y}(k)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\int K_{x}(y)\exp\left(-ikx\right)\mathrm{d}x.

(13)

We therefore let
P_{k}(y)=\frac{\hat{K}_{y}(k)}{v_{t}\left(y\right)}
in equation
12
, for suitably chosen frequencies
k
(see Appendix
A.5.2
).

In contrast to GQ, we retain the current nodes
y_{i}
and seek new weights
\hat{w}_{i}
such that
\hat{\mu}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\hat{w}_{i}\delta_{y_{i}}
satisfies

The complexity of calculating the matrix

, and for solving the linear system is

or
O\left(m^{3}\log n_{t}\right)

O\left(m^{3}+dmn_{t}\right)
or
O\left(m^{3}\log n+dmn_{t}\right)
.
In practice, we found the non-negative least squares solvers to be faster and to give good accuracy.

3.3
Complexity analysis for compressed online Sinkhorn

In this section, we analyse the numerical gains of our compression technique over online Sinkhorn.

The key assumptions

Our complexity analysis is based on the following assumptions for the compression in Steps
8
and
9
in Algorithm
2
.

Assumption 4
.

At step
t
, approximate
u_{t}=e^{-f_{t}/\varepsilon}
by
\hat{u}_{t}=e^{-\hat{f}_{t}/\varepsilon}
(and similarly
v_{t}
by
\hat{v}_{t}
) with compression size
m_{t}
. For a given
\zeta>0
depending on the compression method, assume that

\displaystyle\underset{x}{\sup}\left\lvert f_{t}(x)-\hat{f}_{t}(x)\right\rvert=O\left(m_{t}^{-\zeta}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\underset{y}{\sup}\left\lvert g_{t}(y)-\hat{g}_{t}(y)\right\rvert=O\left(m_{t}^{-\zeta}\right).

Assumption 5
.

The compression size is
m_{t}=t^{\lambda}
for
\lambda=\frac{a-b}{\zeta}
.

Let us return to our examples from Section
3.1
and show the assumptions are satisfied for Gaussian quadrature and Fourier moments compression. The details can be found in Appendix
A.5
.

Gaussian quadrature

Note that
K_{x}
and
v_{t}
are smooth (both have the same regularity as
e^{-C(x,y)/\varepsilon}
; see equation
8
). By
[
Gautschi, 2004
, Corollary to Theorem 1.48]
, the compression error by Gaussian quadrature is

u_{t}(x)-\hat{u}_{t}(x)=\int\frac{K_{x}}{v_{t}}(y)\mathrm{d}\mu(y)-\int\frac{K_{x}}{v_{t}}(y)\mathrm{d}\hat{\mu}(y)=O\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{m}m!}\right).

As
f_{t}(x)
is a Lipschitz function of
u_{t}(x)
, we also have
f_{t}(x)-\hat{f}_{t}(x)=O\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{m}m!}\right)
.
We see Assumption
4
holds for any
\zeta>0
, as
\left(m!\right)^{-1}={O}\left(m^{-\zeta}\right)
.

Fourier moments

We show in Appendix
A.5
that, for
C(x,y)=\left\|x-y\right\|^{2}
,

u_{t}(x)-\hat{u}_{t}(x)=\int\frac{K_{x}}{v_{t}}(y)d\mu(y)-\int\frac{K_{x}}{v_{t}}(y)d\hat{\mu}(y)=\int\frac{\varphi_{x}\left(z\right)}{v_{t}\left(y\right)}\mathrm{d}z,

(14)

where

\varphi_{x}\left(k\right)=\exp\left(i\,z\,k\right)\sqrt{\varepsilon\pi}\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon k^{2}}{4}\right)\int\exp\left(-i\,k\,y\right)\mathrm{d}\left(\mu-\hat{\mu}\right)\left(y\right).

(15)

By choice of
P_{k}
and equation
12
,
\varphi_{x}(k)=0
for
k\in\Omega
and the compression error via Gaussian QMC sampling
[
Kuo and Nuyens, 2016
]
is

O\left(\frac{\left\lvert\log m\right\rvert^{d}}{m}\right).

For any
\zeta<1
,
\frac{\left\lvert\log m\right\rvert^{d}}{m}={O}\left(m^{-\zeta}\right)
and Assumption
4
holds.

5
Conclusion and future work

In this paper, we revisited the online Sinkhorn algorithm and provided an updated convergence analysis. We also proposed to combine this algorithm with measure compression techniques and demonstrated both theoretical and practical gains. We focused on the use of Fourier moments as a compression technique, and this worked particularly well with quadratic costs due to the fast tail behaviour of
\exp(-x^{2})
. However, Fourier compression generally performs less well for non-quadratic costs (such as
C(x,y)=\left\|x-y\right\|_{1}
) due to the slower frequency decay of
\exp(-C)
. We leave investigations into compression of such losses with slow tail behaviour as future work. Another direction of future work is to further investigate the non-asymptotic convergence behaviour: In Theorem
1
, there is a linear convergence term which dominates in the non-asymptotic regime and it could be beneficial to adaptively choose the parameters
a,b
to exploit this fast initial behaviour.

\displaystyle=\left(\hat{f}_{t+1}-f_{t+1}\right)/\varepsilon+\left(f_{t+1}-f^{*}\right)/\varepsilon\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\text{err}_{f_{t+1}}+u_{t+1},

\displaystyle\hat{V}_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\left(\hat{g}_{t+1}-g_{t+1}\right)/\varepsilon+\left(g_{t+1}-g^{*}\right)/\varepsilon\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\text{err}_{g_{t+1}}+V_{t+1},

where
\text{err}_{f_{t+1}}=\left(\hat{f}_{t+1}-f_{t+1}\right)/\varepsilon
and
\text{err}_{g_{t+1}}=\left(\hat{g}_{t+1}-g_{t+1}\right)/\varepsilon
, and

\displaystyle\left\|\hat{U}_{t+1}\right\|_{var}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left\|\text{err}_{f_{t+1}}\right\|_{var}+\left\|U_{t+1}\right\|_{var},

(48)

\displaystyle\left\|\hat{V}_{t+1}\right\|_{var}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left\|\text{err}_{g_{t+1}}\right\|_{var}+\left\|V_{t+1}\right\|_{var}.

(49)

Recall from Theorem
1
that
e_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\|U_{t}\|_{var}+\|V_{t}\|_{var}
, and define
\hat{e}_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\|\hat{U}_{t}\|_{var}+\|\hat{V}_{t}\|_{var}
. By Lemma
8
, for
t
large enough

e_{t+1}\leqslant\left(1-\eta_{t}+\eta_{t}\kappa\right)\hat{e}_{t}+\eta_{t}\left(\|\hat{\zeta}_{\hat{\beta}_{t}}\|_{var}+\|\hat{\iota}_{\hat{\alpha}_{t}}\|_{var}\right).

(50)

Thus, by the inequalities equation
48
and equation
50
,

\begin{split}\hat{e}_{t+1}&\leqslant e_{t+1}+\left\|\text{err}_{f_{t+1}}\right\|_{var}+\left\|\text{err}_{g_{t+1}}\right\|_{var}\\
&\leqslant\left(1-\eta_{t}+\eta_{t}\kappa\right)\hat{e}_{t}+\eta_{t}\left(\|\hat{\zeta}_{\hat{\beta}_{t}}\|_{var}+\|\hat{\iota}_{\hat{\alpha}_{t}}\|_{var}\right)+\left\|\text{err}_{f_{t+1}}\right\|_{var}+\left\|\text{err}_{g_{t+1}}\right\|_{var}\end{split}

(51)

Take expectations on both sides of equation
51
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\hat{e}_{t+1}

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{E}e_{t+1}+\left\|\text{err}_{f_{t+1}}\right\|_{var}+\mathbb{E}\left\|\text{err}_{g_{t+1}}\right\|_{var}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(1-\eta_{t}+\eta_{t}\kappa\right)\mathbb{E}\hat{e}_{t}+\frac{S\eta_{t}}{\sqrt{b_{t}}}+\mathbb{E}\left\|\text{err}_{f_{t+1}}\right\|_{var}+\mathbb{E}\left\|\text{err}_{g_{t+1}}\right\|_{var},

where
S=A_{1}(f^{*},g^{*},\varepsilon)+A_{2}(f^{*},g^{*},\varepsilon)
.

Notice that
\left\|\text{err}_{f_{t+1}}\right\|_{var}=O\left(t^{-a+b}\right)
under Assumptions
4
and
5
. By Lemma
9
with
\theta=b-a
,

\mathbb{E}\hat{e}_{t+1}\lesssim\left(\mathbb{E}e_{1}+\frac{1}{-\theta-1}\right)\exp\left(\frac{\kappa-1}{b+1}t^{b+1}\right)+\frac{1}{\left(1-\kappa\right)}t^{-a},

(52)

where the right-hand side goes to
0
as
t\to\infty
.
∎

A.5
Compression errors

A.5.1
Gauss quadrature (GQ)

A quadrature rule uses a sum of specific points with assigned weights as an approximation to an integral, which are optimal with respect to a certain polynomial degree of exactness
[
Gautschi, 2004
]
. The
m
-point Gauss quadrature rule for
\mu
can be expressed as

\int_{\mathbb{R}}f\left(y\right)\mathrm{d}\mu(y)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}f\left(\hat{y}_{i}\right)+R_{m}\left(f\right),

for weights
w_{i}
and points
\hat{y}_{i}
,
where the remainder term
R_{m}
satisifes
R_{m}\left(\mathbb{P}_{2m-1}\right)=0
, and therefore the sum approximation on the RHS matches the integral value on the LHS for
f\in\mathbb{P}_{2m-1}
.

In general, by
[
Gautschi, 2004
, Corollary to Theorem 1.48]
, the error term
R_{m}
can be expressed as

R_{m}(f)=M\frac{f^{(2m)}(\xi)}{(2m)!},\quad\text{some $\xi\in\mathbb{R}$}

where
M=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left[\pi_{n}\left(t;d\mu\right)\right]^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu\left(t\right)
and
\pi_{n}\left(\cdot;d\mu\right)
is the numerator polynomial
[
Gautschi, 2004
, Definition 1.35]
.

In our case,
f=K_{x}/v_{t}
.
Note that
K_{x}
and
v_{t}
are smooth (both have the same regularity as
e^{-C(x,y)/\varepsilon}
; see equation
8
). Moreover, note that
v_{t}
is uniformly bounded away from 0 since
g_{t}
is uniformly bounded. For
K_{x}(\xi)=\exp\left(-\frac{C\left(x,\xi\right)}{\varepsilon}\right)
and, by the closed form of Gaussian functions,

\displaystyle K_{x}^{(2m)}(\xi)=\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)^{m}\exp\left(-\frac{\left(x-\xi\right)^{2}}{\varepsilon}\right)H_{2m}\left(\frac{x-\xi}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\right)=O\left(\frac{\left(2m\right)!}{m!\varepsilon^{m}}\right),

where
H_{n}
is the Hermite polynomial of
n
th order. It follows, by the Leibniz rule and the Faa di Bruno formula that,
R_{m}\left(f\right)=O\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{m}m!}\right)
. Hence,

\displaystyle u_{t}(x)-\hat{u}_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{K_{x}}{v_{t}}(y)\mathrm{d}\mu(y)-\int\frac{K_{x}}{v_{t}}(y)\mathrm{d}\hat{\mu}(y)=O\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{m}m!}\right).

Note that
f_{t}(x)=-\varepsilon\log(u_{t}(x))
. By the mean-value theorem,
|\log a-\log b|\leq\frac{1}{a}|a-b|
for
0<a<b
. Further,
u_{t}
is bounded away from zero (as a continuous and positive function on a compact set). Hence, we may find a Lipschitz constant
L^{\prime}
such that
|f_{t}(x)-\hat{f}_{t}(x)|\leq L^{\prime}|u_{t}(x)-\hat{u}_{t}(x)|
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
. Hence, Assumption
4
holds for any
\zeta>0
.

A.5.2
Fourier method

Consider
C(x,y)=\|x-y\|^{2}
. Take Fourier moments of
\int\frac{K_{x}}{v_{t}}\left(y\right)\mathrm{d}\mu\left(y\right)
,

\int\exp\left(-ikx\right)\int\frac{K_{x}}{v_{t}}\left(y\right)d\mu\left(y\right)dx=\int\frac{\hat{K}_{y}(k)}{v_{t}(y)}\mathrm{d}\mu\left(y\right)=\sqrt{\varepsilon\pi}\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon k^{2}}{4}\right)\int\frac{\exp\left(-iky\right)}{v_{t}(y)}\mathrm{d}\mu\left(y\right),

(53)

as
\hat{K}_{y}(k)
for
K_{x}\left(y\right)=\exp\left(-\frac{\left(x-y\right)^{2}}{\varepsilon}\right)
is given by

\begin{split}\hat{K}_{y}(k)&=\int\exp\left(-ikx\right)\exp\left(-\frac{\left(x-y\right)^{2}}{\varepsilon}\right)dx\\
&=\exp\left(-iky\right)\int\exp\left(-ikz\right)\exp\left(-\frac{z^{2}}{\varepsilon}\right)dz\\
&=\exp\left(-iky\right)\sqrt{\varepsilon\pi}\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon k^{2}}{4}\right).\end{split}

(54)

By the Fourier inversion theorem,

u_{t}(x)=\exp\left(-f_{t}\left(x\right)/\varepsilon\right)=\int\exp\left(ikx\right)\sqrt{\varepsilon\pi}\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon k^{2}}{4}\right)\int\frac{\exp\left(-iky\right)}{v_{t}(y)}\mathrm{d}\mu\left(y\right)\mathrm{d}k.

(55)

The compression error becomes

u_{t}(x)-\hat{u}_{t}(x)=\int\frac{K_{x}}{v_{t}}(y)d\mu(y)-\int\frac{K_{x}}{v_{t}}(y)d\hat{\mu}(y)=\int\frac{\varphi_{x}\left(z\right)}{v_{t}\left(y\right)}\mathrm{d}z,

(56)

where

\varphi_{x}\left(k\right)=\exp\left(i\,z\,k\right)\sqrt{\varepsilon\pi}\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon k^{2}}{4}\right)\int\exp\left(-i\,k\,y\right)\mathrm{d}\left(\mu-\hat{\mu}\right)\left(y\right),

(57)

and note that, by construction,
\varphi_{x}\left(k\right)=0
for all
k\in\Omega
.
The problem of finding the compression thus becomes a problem of evaluating the integral
\int\varphi_{x}\left(z\right)\mathrm{d}z
. Similarly to Gauss quadrature, we want to find
k\in\Omega=\left\{k_{1},\cdots,k_{m}\right\}
such that the
\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\varphi_{x}\left(k_{i}\right)
is an approximation to
\int\varphi_{x}\left(z\right)\mathrm{d}z
.

Let
\Omega=\left\{k_{1},\cdots,k_{m}\right\}
be the set of
n
elements that are QMC sampled from
X\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\frac{2}{\varepsilon}I\right)
. In practice, we use the implementation of
SciPy

[
Virtanen et al., 2020
]
.
Define
\chi\left(z\right)=\exp\left(\frac{\varepsilon z^{2}}{4}\right)\varphi_{x}\left(z\right)
. Notice that

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left(\chi\left(X\right)\right)&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(2\pi\right)^{-d/2}\left(2/\varepsilon\right)^{-d/2}\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon}{4}z^{2}\right)\psi\left(z\right)\mathrm{d}z\\
&=\left(\frac{4\pi}{\varepsilon}\right)^{-d/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi_{x}\left(z\right)\mathrm{d}z,\end{split}

(58)

and thus,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi_{x}\left(z\right)\mathrm{d}z=\left(\frac{4\pi}{\varepsilon}\right)^{d/2}\mathbb{E}\chi\left(X\right).

(59)

Following
[
Kuo and Nuyens, 2016
, Section 4.1]
, let
\Psi(x)
denote the cumulative distribution functions for
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. Then
\Psi(\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{{2}}}X_{i})\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1)
and

\mathbb{E}\left(\chi\left(X\right)\right)=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\chi\left(A\Psi^{-1}\left(z\right)\right)\mathrm{d}z,

(60)

where
A=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\varepsilon}}I
,
\Psi^{-1}\left(z\right)=\left(\Psi^{-1}\left(z^{1}\right),\cdots,\Psi^{-1}\left(z^{d}\right)\right)
.
Denote
h\left(z\right)=\chi\left(A\Psi^{-1}\left(z\right)\right)
, and
I_{d}\left(h\right)=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}h\left(z\right)\mathrm{d}t
, where
d
is the dimension, and
Q_{m,d}\left(h\right)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}h\left(k_{i}\right)
.
As
y\in\mathcal{X}
, a compact domain, the first derivative of
\chi(k)
is bounded and the integrand
h
has bounded variation.
Hence, by
[
Kuo and Nuyens, 2016
, Section 1.3]
,

\left\lvert I_{d}\left(h\right)-Q_{m,d}\left(h\right)\right\rvert=O\left(\frac{\left\lvert\log m\right\rvert^{d}}{m}\right).

(61)

Given that for
k\in\Omega
,
\varphi_{x}\left(k\right)=0
and hence,
Q_{m,d}\left(h\right)=0
.
Then, we know

\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi_{x}\left(z\right)\mathrm{d}z=\left(\frac{4\pi}{\varepsilon}\right)^{d/2}I_{d}\left(h\right)&\leqslant\left(\frac{4\pi}{\varepsilon}\right)^{d/2}\left(Q_{m,d}\left(h\right)+\left\lvert I_{d}\left(h\right)-Q_{m,d}\left(h\right)\right\rvert\right)\\
&=O\left(\left(\frac{4\pi}{\varepsilon}\right)^{d/2}\frac{\left\lvert\log m\right\rvert^{d}}{m}\right).\end{split}

(62)

Choosing
k
in equation
12
via Gaussian QMC, the compression error is then

u_{t}(x)-\hat{u}_{t}(x)=\exp\left(-f_{t}\left(x\right)/\varepsilon\right)-\exp\left(-\hat{f}_{t}\left(x\right)/\varepsilon\right)=O\left(\frac{\left\lvert\log m\right\rvert^{d}}{m}\right).

(63)

If we take
\zeta<1
, then
\frac{\left\lvert\log m\right\rvert^{d}}{m}=o\left(m^{-\zeta}\right)
. Again applying the Lipschitz argument,
f_{t}(x)-\hat{f}_{t}(x)=O\left(\frac{\left\lvert\log m\right\rvert^{d}}{m}\right)
and Assumption
4
holds.

A.6
Proof of Proposition
3

Proof.

Cost of online Sinkhorn

Now we calculate the complexity of Algorithm
2
up to step
T
. Recalled that in Section
2.2.1
, the computational complexity of Algorithm
1
is
\mathcal{C}=O\left(dT^{4a+2}\right)
. By Theorem
1
, the error at step
T
is
\delta=O\left(T^{-a}\right)
(where the hidden constant may depend on dimension). Taking
T=O(\delta^{-1/a})
, we have

\mathcal{C}=O\left(\delta^{-\left(4+\frac{2}{a}\right)}\right)
.

Cost of compressed online Sinkhorn

Assuming
m_{t}=t^{\frac{a-b}{\zeta}}
,
the total computational cost of Algorithm
2
is

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{C}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(C(n_{t-1},m_{t})+db_{t}m_{t}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(m_{t}^{3}+d\left(m_{t-1}+b_{t-1}\right)m_{t}+db_{t}m_{t}\right)

\displaystyle=O\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(t^{\frac{3\left(a-b\right)}{\zeta}}+d\left(t-1\right)^{2a}t^{\frac{a-b}{\zeta}}+dt^{2a+\frac{a-b}{\zeta}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}O\left(dT^{2a+\frac{a-b}{\zeta}+1}\right),&\text{ for }\zeta\geqslant\frac{a-b}{a}\\
O\left(T^{\frac{3\left(a-b\right)}{\zeta}+1}\right),&\text{ for }\zeta<\frac{a-b}{a}\end{cases}.

Under Assumptions
4
and
5
, the error at step
T
is
\delta=O\left(T^{-a}\right)
. Taking
T=\delta^{-1/a}
, we have

\hat{\mathcal{C}}=\begin{cases}O\left(\delta^{-\left(2+\frac{a-b}{a\zeta}+\frac{1}{a}\right)}\right),&\text{ for }\zeta\geqslant\frac{a-b}{a},\\
O\left(\delta^{-\left(\frac{3\left(a-b\right)}{a\zeta}+\frac{1}{a}\right)}\right),&\text{ for }\zeta<\frac{a-b}{a}.\end{cases}

The big-
O
constant for the accuracy
\delta
may depend on dimension.
∎

Notice that the ratio of
\hat{\mathcal{C}}
and
\mathcal{C}
,

\frac{\hat{\mathcal{C}}}{\mathcal{C}}=\begin{cases}{O}\left(\delta^{2+\frac{1}{a}-\frac{a-b}{a\zeta}}\right),&\text{ for }\zeta\geqslant\frac{a-b}{a},\\
{O}\left(\delta^{4+\frac{1}{a}-\frac{3\left(a-b\right)}{a\zeta}}\right),&\text{ for }\zeta<\frac{a-b}{a},\end{cases}

(64)

and the exponent of the ratio is positive when
\zeta>\frac{3\left(a-b\right)}{4a+1}
, which means the asymptotical convergence of the Compressed Online Sinkhorn is improved compared to the Online Sinkhorn.
