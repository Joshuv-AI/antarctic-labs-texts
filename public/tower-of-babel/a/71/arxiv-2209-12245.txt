Decentralised possibilistic inference with applications to target tracking
Jeremie Houssineau, Han Cai, Murat Uney, Emmanuel Delande

Abstract
Fusing and sharing information from multiple sensors over a network is a challenging task. Part of this challenge arises from the absence of a foundational rule for fusing probability distributions, with various approaches stemming from different principles. Yet, when expressing tracking algorithms within the framework of possibility theory, one specific fusion rule can be proved to be exact in the sense that it is equivalent to the non-distributed possibilistic approach. In this article, this fusion rule is applied to decentralised fusion, based on the possibilistic analogue of the Bernoulli filter. We then show that the proposed approach outperforms its probabilistic counterpart on simulated data.

Over the last decade, the increasing communication and computation capacity have made the view of multiple agents performing distributed inference particularly relevant in multi-sensor processing applications

,

in particular. There are challenges involved, however, such as the loss of precision that most approaches display when compared to non-distributed algorithms.

This work aims to highlight the capabilities in sensor fusion that can be leveraged when following an alternative version of Bayesian inference based on a particular formulation
[
9

]
of possibility theory
[
11
]
. Recently-derived target-tracking algorithms
[

14
]
have shown that this approach allows for a greater uncertainty about some crucial aspects of target tracking when compared to their probabilistic analogues. Yet, their potential for sensor fusion is unrelated to these advantages and pertains instead to the fundamental nature of possibility theory as a representation of information rather than randomness. Indeed, as will be demonstrated in this work, information can be naturally shared, and combined
[
15
,
16
]
in this context whereas these operations are less straightforward in probability theory and require the introduction of additional principles and approximations.

In order to demonstrate the generality of the proposed approach to decentralised fusion, we apply it to a target-tracking problem
[
17
,
18
]
where an object of interest, the
target
, is observed under a partial, noisy and corrupted observation process:

i)

partial
because the state of the target is not fully observed and because its detection can fail altogether, an event we refer to as a detection failure,

ii)

noisy
because the observed components of the target’s state are subject to observation errors, and

iii)

corrupted
because observations which do not originate from the target are also collected; we refer to these as false alarms.

The target tracking problem is made challenging by the absence of information on the data association, i.e. it is assumed that the observations do not contain any information regarding whether they are a false alarm or they originate from the target, hence making the problem combinatorially complex. The difficulty is exacerbated by the uncertainties in the location of target when it first appears and the time at which it disappears; these are often modelled by — borrowing from the population statistics jargon— a birth/death process. Solutions to this problem are often referred to as
Bernoulli filters

[
19
]
, with “Bernoulli” referring to the presence/absence of the target due to the birth/death process; a possibilistic Bernoulli filter has been introduced in
[
12
]
.

One of the objectives of this work is to show how the proposed principled framework for sensor fusion can be easily applied to a complex inference algorithm such as the possibilistic Bernoulli filter. This focus is justified by the hierarchical nature of Bernoulli filters, where data association and state estimation are jointly inferred, hence falling out of the scope of modern decentralised Kalman filtering techniques, e.g.
[
20
]
. The main contribution of this work are as follows:

1.

A decentralised possibilistic Bayesian inference method is introduced and proved to be (asymptotically) exact in the sense that it agrees with non-distributed possibilistic Bayesian inference.

2.

The proposed approach is detailed in the case of a Gaussian mixture implementation of the possibilistic Bernoulli filter, highlighting its applicability to inference problems beyond the linear-Gaussian setting of the Kalman filter.

3.

The performance of the proposed decentralised possibilistic Bernoulli filter is shown to largely improve existing approaches
[
21
,
22
]
, verifying the claim that the performance in the non-distributed case can be closely matched even with the necessary approximations and practical considerations (such as a small number of communication steps on the sensor network).

The structure of this article is as follows: An introduction to possibility theory is given in Section

II
Review of possibilistic inference

Possibility theory allows for modelling the absence of precise knowledge regarding an unknown but otherwise fixed quantity of interest
[
11
,
23
]
. This can be formalised by introducing a sample space
\Omega
as in probability theory; yet, instead of defining a probability distribution on
\Omega
, we simply acknowledge the presence of a true state of nature
\omega^{*}\in\Omega
and aim to infer the aspects of it that are relevant to the problem under consideration. To this end, an analogue of the concept of a random variable, referred to as an
uncertain variable
, is introduced as a mapping
\bm{x}
from
\Omega
to a given set
S
with
x^{*}=\bm{x}(\omega^{*})
being the true value of interest.

The information held about
\bm{x}
(or, equivalently, about
x^{*}
) is formalised as a non-negative function
f_{\bm{x}}
satisfying
\sup_{x\in S}f_{\bm{x}}(x)=1
. The interpretation of this function, referred to as a
possibility function
, is that the event
\bm{x}\in B
for some subset
B
of
S
has
credibility

\sup_{x\in B}f_{\bm{x}}(x)
; the term
credibility
itself can be understood informally as the degree of belief in an event or, more formally, as the maximum
subjective
probability that one would be ready to grant to this event. This probability is indeed subjective as the considered event is not associated to a random variable so that there is no true probability of the event happening.

An important aspect of possibility theory is that a given uncertain variable does not induce a unique possibility function. Instead, different agents might hold different information about the same unknown quantity. The least informative belief is represented by a possibility function
f_{\bm{x}}
which is equal to
1
everywhere on
S
. Such
f_{\bm{x}}
holds no information whatsoever; we denote this possibility function by
\bm{1}
and highlight that it has no proper probabilistic analogue when
S
is unbounded. On the other hand, some agents might have more informative possibility functions, i.e.
f_{\bm{x}}(x)<1
for some
x\in S
.

An agent can decide to forgo some of the information they hold by replacing their possibility function
f_{\bm{x}}
by another one, say
f^{\prime}_{\bm{x}}
, as long as it holds that
f_{\bm{x}}\leq f^{\prime}_{\bm{x}}
, i.e.
f_{\bm{x}}(x)\leq f^{\prime}_{\bm{x}}(x)
for any
x\in S
. This type of information loss can be motivated by gains in terms of analytical or computational simplicity. The fact that
f^{\prime}_{\bm{x}}
is less informative than
f_{\bm{x}}
can be illustrated using the interpretation of possibility functions as providing maximum subjective probabilities: The possibility function
f^{\prime}_{\bm{x}}
will give a larger maximum probability than
f_{\bm{x}}
for any given event, and therefore a large range of probabilities. In the extreme case where
f^{\prime}_{\bm{x}}=\bm{1}
, the probability of any event can take any value between
0
and
1
, which does correspond to the absence of information.

An important difference between probability density functions (p.d.f.s) and possibility functions is that the latter are not densities so that
f_{\bm{x}}(x)
can be directly interpreted as the credibility of the event
\bm{x}=x
. This difference has practical consequences, for instance, in the corresponding change of variable formula: for any mapping
T
on
S
, the uncertain variable
\bm{x^{\prime}}=T(\bm{x})
is described by

f_{\bm{x}^{\prime}}(x^{\prime})=\sup_{x\in T^{-1}(x^{\prime})}f_{\bm{x}}(x)

for any
x^{\prime}
in the image of
T
, where
T^{-1}(x^{\prime})
is the pre-image of
x^{\prime}
which is possibly a set, and where we assume that
\sup\emptyset=0
, e.g., for the case where there is no
x
such that
x^{\prime}=T(x)
. If
T
is bijective, then this relation simplifies to
f_{\bm{x}^{\prime}}(x^{\prime})=f_{\bm{x}}(T^{-1}(x^{\prime}))
, which does not contain a Jacobian as opposed to its probabilistic analogue.

II-A

Independence and marginal possibilities

If another uncertain variable
\bm{y}
is defined in a set
S^{\prime}
, then the joint information about
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
can be modelled by a possibility function
f_{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
on
S\times S^{\prime}
. The marginal possibility function of
\bm{x}
induced by
f_{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
is denoted by
f_{\bm{x}}
and given by

f_{\bm{x}}(x)=\sup_{y\in S^{\prime}}f_{\bm{x},\bm{y}}(x,y),\qquad x\in S.

(1)

The uncertain variables
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
are said to be
independently described
by
f_{\bm{x},\bm{y}}(x,y)
if there exist possibility functions
f_{\bm{x}}
and
f_{\bm{y}}
such that

f_{\bm{x},\bm{y}}(x,y)=f_{\bm{x}}(x)f_{\bm{y}}(y),\qquad(x,y)\in S\times S^{\prime}.

(2)

This definition of independence follows what is referred to as
numerical
possibility theory, although other notions of independence exist in possibility theory
[
24
]
.

Numerical possibility theory has strong connections with probability theory, as will be seen in Section
II-B
where the analogues of Bayes theorem and Gaussian distributions will be defined; however, despite these connections, the considered notion of independence differs fundamentally from the probabilistic one as it is not an intrinsic property of the considered uncertain variables, but rather a statement about the absence of a relation between the information we hold about
\bm{x}
and the one we hold about
\bm{y}
. In particular, uncertain variables can be
assigned
joint possibility functions that exhibit the independence property in (
2
) by using discounting, i.e. by bringing possibility functions to a given power. As opposed to p.d.f.s, possibility functions are closed under exponentiation, i.e.
f_{\bm{x}}^{w}
is still a non-negative function with a supremum that equals to
1
for any
w\in[0,1]
; in particular,
f_{\bm{x}}^{0}
is equal to
\bm{1}
.

We will show later on how one can split and merge the information characterised by a possibility function without incurring any loss or creating any artificial information. The following proposition focuses on the splitting part and considers the general case where a possibility function jointly describes the information about two unknown quantities. In this general case, some information will be lost in order to obtain independence. This is an important property of possibility functions: one can trade information for statistical properties, in this case independence.

1
.

Let
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
be two uncertain variables on
S
and
S^{\prime}
, respectively, which are jointly described by a possibility function
f_{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
. Let
f_{\bm{x}}
and
f_{\bm{y}}
be the marginal possibility functions of
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
, respectively, induced by
f_{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
as defined in (
1
). For any given scalar
w\in[0,1]
, let
f^{{\scriptscriptstyle(}w{\scriptscriptstyle)}}_{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
be a function defined as

f^{{\scriptscriptstyle(}w{\scriptscriptstyle)}}_{\bm{x},\bm{y}}(x,y)=f_{\bm{x}}(x)^{w}f_{\bm{y}}(y)^{1-w},

(3)

then
f^{{\scriptscriptstyle(}w{\scriptscriptstyle)}}_{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
is a possibility function which verifies
f_{\bm{x},\bm{y}}\leq f^{{\scriptscriptstyle(}w{\scriptscriptstyle)}}_{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
, i.e.
f_{\bm{x},\bm{y}}(x,y)\leq f^{{\scriptscriptstyle(}w{\scriptscriptstyle)}}_{\bm{x},\bm{y}}(x,y)
for all
(x,y)\in S\times S^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Since both marginals
f_{\bm{x}}
and
f_{\bm{y}}
are possibility functions, they are non-negative and have a supremum equal to 1. It follows that
f^{{\scriptscriptstyle(}w{\scriptscriptstyle)}}_{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
is also non-negative and

\displaystyle\sup_{(x,y)\in S\times S^{\prime}}f^{{\scriptscriptstyle(}w{\scriptscriptstyle)}}_{\bm{x},\bm{y}}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sup_{(x,y)\in S\times S^{\prime}}f_{\bm{x}}(x)^{w}f_{\bm{y}}(y)^{1-w}

\displaystyle=\bigg(\sup_{x\in S}f_{\bm{x}}(x)\bigg)^{w}\bigg(\sup_{y\in S^{\prime}}f_{\bm{y}}(y)\bigg)^{1-w}

\displaystyle=1,

so that
f^{{\scriptscriptstyle(}w{\scriptscriptstyle)}}_{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
is indeed a possibility function. We now aim to prove that
f_{\bm{x},\bm{y}}\leq f^{{\scriptscriptstyle(}w{\scriptscriptstyle)}}_{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
. Using the definition of the joint
f^{{\scriptscriptstyle(}w{\scriptscriptstyle)}}_{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
and of the marginals
f_{\bm{x}}
and
f_{\bm{y}}
, we obtain

f^{{\scriptscriptstyle(}w{\scriptscriptstyle)}}_{\bm{x},\bm{y}}(x,y)=\bigg(\sup_{x\in S}f_{\bm{x},\bm{y}}(x,y)\bigg)^{w}\bigg(\sup_{y\in S^{\prime}}f_{\bm{x},\bm{y}}(x,y)\bigg)^{1-w}.

Since it holds that

\displaystyle f_{\bm{x},\bm{y}}(x,y)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{y^{\prime}\in S^{\prime}}f_{\bm{x},\bm{y}}(x,y^{\prime})

\displaystyle f_{\bm{x},\bm{y}}(x,y)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{x^{\prime}\in S}f_{\bm{x},\bm{y}}(x^{\prime},y),

for any
(x,y)\in S\times S^{\prime}
, we obtain that

\displaystyle f^{{\scriptscriptstyle(}w{\scriptscriptstyle)}}_{\bm{x},\bm{y}}(x,y)

\displaystyle\geq\bigg(f_{\bm{x},\bm{y}}(x,y)\bigg)^{w}\bigg(f_{\bm{x},\bm{y}}(x,y)\bigg)^{1-w}

\displaystyle\geq f(x,y),

which concludes the proof of the proposition.
∎

Proposition
1
is important as it ensures that no artificial information is introduced when replacing
f_{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
by
f^{{\scriptscriptstyle(}w{\scriptscriptstyle)}}_{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
. This is true regardless of the strength of the correlation between
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
. As a result,
f^{{\scriptscriptstyle(}w{\scriptscriptstyle)}}_{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
can be extremely uninformative: For example, consider perfectly coupled
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
with
S=S^{\prime}
and
f_{\bm{x},\bm{y}}(x,y)=\mathbb{I}(x=y)
, where

\mathbb{I}(x=y)=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }x=y\text{~is true},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Then, there is no independent information about
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
in
f_{\bm{x},\bm{y}}
and
f^{{\scriptscriptstyle(}w{\scriptscriptstyle)}}_{\bm{x},\bm{y}}=\bm{1}
. The next step is to introduce ways of combining information; this is achieved through Bayes’ theorem in the following section.

II-B

Posterior possibility and conjugate priors

Assuming that the event
\bm{y}=y
has positive credibility, i.e.
f_{\bm{y}}(y)>0
, the analogue of Bayes theorem for possibility functions was introduced in
[
23
]
as

\displaystyle f_{\bm{x}|\bm{y}}(x\,|\,y)\triangleq

\displaystyle\dfrac{f_{\bm{x},\bm{y}}(x,y)}{f_{\bm{y}}(y)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{f_{\bm{y}|\bm{x}}(y\,|\,x)f_{\bm{x}}(x)}{\sup_{x^{\prime}\in S}f_{\bm{y}|\bm{x}}(y\,|\,x^{\prime})f_{\bm{x}}(x^{\prime})},

(4)

for any
x\in S
.

The concept of a
conjugate prior
translates directly to this form of posterior inference, e.g., if
S=\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
S^{\prime}=\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
and if both the
prior

f_{\bm{x}}
and the
likelihood

f_{\bm{y}|\bm{x}}(y\,|\,\cdot)
take the form of a Gaussian (quadratic exponential) possibility function, i.e.

\displaystyle f_{\bm{x}}(x)

\displaystyle=\overline{\mathrm{N}}(x;\mu,P)=\exp\Big(-\dfrac{1}{2}(x-\mu)^{\intercal}P^{-1}(x-\mu)\Big)

\displaystyle f_{\bm{y}|\bm{x}}(y\,|\,x)

\displaystyle=\overline{\mathrm{N}}(y;Hx,R)

for some
\mu\in S
, some
d^{\prime}\times d
matrix
H
, some
d\times d
positive definite matrix
P
and some
d^{\prime}\times d^{\prime}
positive definite matrix
R
, then the
posterior
possibility function
f_{\bm{x}|\bm{y}}(\cdot\,|\,y)
is also Gaussian
[
25
]
. Every probabilistic conjugate prior has a possibilistic analogue, up to differences in the set of parameters for which a given conjugate prior family is well-defined, due to the fact that possibility functions need to be bounded and p.d.f.s need to be integrable
[
9
]
.

Using more advanced tools, a version of Bayes theorem with a possibilistic prior and a probabilistic likelihood is introduced in
[
10
]
as

f_{\bm{x}|Y}(x\,|\,y)=\dfrac{p(y\,|\,x)f_{\bm{x}}(x)}{\sup_{x^{\prime}\in S}p(y\,|\,x^{\prime})f_{\bm{x}}(x^{\prime})},

(5)

where
y
is a realisation of a random variable
Y
whose probability density function given
\bm{x}=x\in S
is
p(\cdot\,|\,x)
. This alternative version is useful as it is common for the observation process to involve randomness even when the dynamics of the target do not.

II-C

Generalised conditional possibility and fusion

A general notion of conditioning can also be introduced for events regarding uncertain variables via

f_{\bm{u}}(u\,|\,\bm{v}\in A)=\dfrac{\sup_{v\in A}f_{\bm{u},\bm{v}}(u,v)}{\sup_{v\in A}f_{\bm{v}}(v)},

(6)

for uncertain variables
\bm{u}
and
\bm{v}
on sets
U
and
V
, and some subset
A
of
V
. The possibilistic Bayes rule in (
II-B
) corresponds to a special case where
\bm{u}=\bm{x}
,
\bm{v}=\bm{y}
, and
A=\{y\}
. The generalised conditional in (
6
) allows one to perform fusion, which will become clear later in this section. The following proposition provides an underpinning equality.

2
.

For uncertain variables
\bm{x}
and
\bm{z}
on the same set
S
, suppose that some possibility function
f_{\bm{x},\bm{z}}
is their joint descriptor. Then, the posterior possibility function describing
\bm{x}
(equivalently
\bm{z}
) given that
\bm{x}
and
\bm{z}
represent the same unknown quantity, i.e.
\bm{x}=\bm{z}
, is given by

f_{\bm{x}}(x\,|\,\bm{x}=\bm{z})=\dfrac{f_{\bm{x},\bm{z}}(x,x)}{\sup_{x^{\prime}\in S}f_{\bm{x},\bm{z}}(x^{\prime},x^{\prime})}.

Proof.

We use (
6
) with
\bm{u}=\bm{v}
,
\bm{v}=(\bm{x},\bm{z})
and
A=\{(x,z):x=z\}\subseteq S\times S
, and note that

f_{\bm{u},\bm{v}}=f_{\bm{x},\bm{z},\bm{x},\bm{z}}
, which verifies

f_{\bm{x},\bm{z},\bm{x},\bm{z}}(x,z,x^{\prime},z^{\prime})=\mathbb{I}(x=x^{\prime},z=z^{\prime})f_{\bm{x},\bm{z}}(x,z),

so that

\sup_{(x^{\prime},z^{\prime}):x^{\prime}=z^{\prime}}f_{\bm{x},\bm{z},\bm{x},\bm{z}}(x,z,x^{\prime},z^{\prime})=\mathbb{I}(x=z)f_{\bm{x},\bm{z}}(x,z).

We then obtain the posterior possibility function given by

f_{\bm{x},\bm{z}}(x,z\,|\,\bm{x}=\bm{z})=\mathbb{I}(x=z)\dfrac{f_{\bm{x},\bm{z}}(x,z)}{\sup_{x^{\prime}\in S}f_{\bm{x},\bm{z}}(x^{\prime},x^{\prime})},

(7)

for any
x,z\in S
. Note that such operations are often ill-defined for probability distributions and can lead to paradoxes (see, e.g.
[
26
, Chapter 15.7]
). Since
f_{\bm{x},\bm{z}}(\cdot\,|\,\bm{x}=\bm{z})
takes non-zero values on the diagonal
\Delta=\{(x,x):x\in S\}
of
S\times S
only, i.e.
f_{\bm{x},\bm{z}}(x,z\,|\,\bm{x}=\bm{z})=0
if
x\neq z
, this possibility function can be equivalently expressed as a univariate possibility function in either
\bm{x}
or
\bm{z}
, i.e.

\displaystyle f_{\bm{x}}(x\,|\,\bm{x}=\bm{z})

\displaystyle=f_{\bm{z}}(x\,|\,\bm{x}=\bm{z})

(8a)

\displaystyle=f_{\bm{x},\bm{z}}(x,x\,|\,\bm{x}=\bm{z}),

(8b)

which corresponds to a change of variable by the bijective mapping
T:\Delta\to S
defined by
T(x,x)=x
for any
{x\in S}
.
∎

In the context of fusion, the above result will be used together with Proposition
1
and specifically with (
3
). Before detailing this point as a remark, let us note that in the context of fusion,
\bm{x}
and
\bm{z}
are often independently described by some
f_{\bm{x}}
and
f_{\bm{z}}
, i.e. it is useful to say that “
f_{\bm{x}}
and
f_{\bm{z}}
represent the same unknown quantity” when it is known that
\bm{x}=\bm{z}
. Similarly, we will simply say that “
f_{\bm{x}}
and
f_{\bm{z}}
are independent” when
\bm{x}
and
\bm{z}
are independently described.

1
.

Proposition
1
, specifically (
3
), provides a way to define a joint possibility function
f^{{\scriptscriptstyle(}w{\scriptscriptstyle)}}_{\bm{x},\bm{z}}
when only the corresponding marginals
f_{\bm{x}}
and
f_{\bm{z}}
are known. Given that
\bm{x}
and
\bm{z}
are in the same set
S
and using Proposition
2
, we can fuse the obtained information given that
\bm{x}=\bm{y}
to obtain

f_{\bm{x}}(x\,|\,\bm{x}=\bm{z})=\dfrac{f_{\bm{x}}(x)^{w}f_{\bm{z}}(x)^{1-w}}{\sup_{x^{\prime}\in S}f_{\bm{x}}(x^{\prime})^{w}f_{\bm{z}}(x^{\prime})^{1-w}}.

This is reminiscent of the covariance intersection technique
[
27
,
28
]
, albeit with a different normalisation constant.

To simplify the notation, we will often denote a fused possibility function such as
f_{\bm{x}}(\cdot\,|\,\bm{x}=\bm{z})
by
\hat{f}
. The following corollary is a straightforward generalisation of Proposition
2
for an arbitrary number of sources of information.

1
.

If
\{f_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
is a collection of
n>1
independent possibility functions on
S
representing the same unknown quantity, then the corresponding fused possibility function is

\hat{f}(x)=\dfrac{\prod_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}(x)}{\sup_{x^{\prime}\in S}\prod_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}(x^{\prime})}.

(9)

Proof.

We denote by
\{\bm{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
the collection of uncertain variables on
S
independently described by the possibility functions
\{f_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, then one can define the joint possibility function
f_{\bm{x}_{1:n}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}(x_{i})
using the independence property. Following the same steps as in the proof of Proposition
2
, the fusion of these
n
sources of information is found to be

\displaystyle\hat{f}(x)

\displaystyle=f_{\bm{x}_{1:n}}(x,\dots,x\,|\,\bm{x}_{i}=\bm{x}_{j},\,i,j\in\{1,\dots,n\})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\prod_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}(x)}{\sup_{x^{\prime}\in S}\prod_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}(x^{\prime})},

as required.
∎

Alternative fusion rules in the context of possibility theory are studied in
[
29
]
. We now show how Propositions
1
and
2
can be used to split a single source of information into two independent pieces with no loss of information, i.e. these two pieces of information can be fused back into the original one. This is crucial for decentralised fusion where information is sequentially shared / fused between sensor nodes.

3
.

The information captured in the possibility function
f
is equivalent to a combination of that in the two independent possibility functions
f^{w}
and
f^{1-w}
, for any
w\in[0,1]
, when it holds that
f^{w}
and
f^{1-w}
represent the same unknown quantity.

Proof.

We provide a constructive proof which introduces, first, a splitting of the information in a possibility function, followed by proving the claimed equivalence.

Let us denote by
\bm{x}
the uncertain variable described by the possibility function
f
and introduce an additional uncertain variable
\bm{z}
as a copy of
\bm{x}
. The most informative possibility function jointly describing
\bm{x}
and
\bm{z}
is

f_{\bm{x},\bm{z}}(x,z)=\mathbb{I}(x=z)f(x),\qquad(x,z)\in S\times S.

(10)

In turn, both
\bm{x}
and
\bm{z}
have
f
as a marginal. Using (
3
), we introduce another possibility function
f^{{\scriptscriptstyle(}w{\scriptscriptstyle)}}_{\bm{x},\bm{z}}
jointly describing
\bm{x}
and
\bm{z}
in a way that is independent in the sense of (
2
), i.e.

f^{{\scriptscriptstyle(}w{\scriptscriptstyle)}}_{\bm{x},\bm{z}}(x,x^{\prime})=f(x)^{w}f(z)^{1-w},

(11)

for some
w\in[0,1]
. As shown before in Proposition
1
, the independent description
f^{{\scriptscriptstyle(}w{\scriptscriptstyle)}}_{\bm{x},\bm{z}}
is less informative than
f_{\bm{x},\bm{z}}
since
f^{{\scriptscriptstyle(}w{\scriptscriptstyle)}}_{\bm{x},\bm{z}}\geq f_{\bm{x},\bm{z}}
. The two simple operations characterised by (
10
) and (
11
) yield two independent pieces of information, modelled by the marginal possibility functions
f^{w}
and
f^{1-w}
of
f^{{\scriptscriptstyle(}w{\scriptscriptstyle)}}_{\bm{x},\bm{z}}
, from a single piece of information expressed as
f
.

Although
f^{{\scriptscriptstyle(}w{\scriptscriptstyle)}}_{\bm{x},\bm{z}}
is less informative than
f_{\bm{x},\bm{z}}
, we can use the fact that
f^{w}
and
f^{1-w}

III
Decentralised possibilistic Bayesian inference

In this section, we show how decentralised Bayesian inference can be asymptotically exact using possibility theory. We first focus on the case where observations are conditionally independent before showing how the principle of the existing geometric average fusion technique can be recovered in the case where there is a correlation between observations, with different levels of information regarding this correlation.

We consider a prior possibility function
f
on a given set
S
and model the evolution of the underlying state by a (possibilistic) Markov transition
g(\cdot\,|\,x)
, which is a possibility function on
S
for any
x\in S
. In this context, if
n
observations
y_{1},\dots,y_{n}
are received and if these observations are described by a possibility function of the form
h(y_{1},\dots,y_{n}\,|\,x)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}h^{{\scriptscriptstyle(}i{\scriptscriptstyle)}}(y_{i}\,|\,x)
, for any
x\in S
, then the standard (non-distributed) approach is to consider the network’s posterior possibility function

f(x\,|\,y_{1},\dots,y_{n})\propto\bigg[\prod_{i=1}^{n}h^{(i)}(y_{i}\,|\,x)\bigg]\sup_{x^{\prime}\in S}g(x\,|\,x^{\prime})f(x^{\prime}).

(13)

This posterior possibility function is what we are trying to recover in the decentralised case.

We now consider
n
sensors nodes
\mathrm{S}_{i}
with
i\in\mathcal{V}=\{1,\dots,n\}
and assume that the sensor network is modelled by an undirected connected graph
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
where
\mathcal{E}
is the set of edges over which the nodes can communicate. Each node collects its independent observations of the same underlying variable. We refer to this configuration as decentralised fusion. We will use the superscript
\cdot^{(i)}
to refer to quantities that are specific to sensor node
\mathrm{S}_{i}
. We denote by
\mathcal{N}_{i}=\{j\in\mathcal{V}:\{i,j\}\in\mathcal{E}\}
the set of neighbours of
i\in\mathcal{V}
. We assume that the total number of nodes in the network is known by every node; this can indeed be obtained in a decentralised manner via standard techniques
[
30
]
. The objective in this section is to show that the posterior possibility function
f(\cdot\,|\,y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
defined in (
13
) can be recovered by alternating between communicating on the network and fusing the received information at each node using (
9
).

One step of the decentralised algorithm is specified as follows: let us first follow the principles of Proposition
3
and share the prior information across the network after splitting into
f^{{\scriptscriptstyle(}i{\scriptscriptstyle)}}=f^{\omega_{i}}
at node
i\in\mathcal{V}
for some collection of non-negative weights
\{\omega_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{V}}
. Performing prediction at each node with the Markov transition
g
would cause a large overlap between each local possibility function: if all nodes were to be connected to a central node
\mathrm{C}
and fused, there would be no way of recovering the predicted possibility function
\sup_{x\in S}g(\cdot\,|\,x)f(x)
. Instead, we share the information about the evolution of the state by considering the Markov transition
g^{\varpi_{i}}(\cdot\,|\,x)
at
\mathrm{S}_{i}
, for some collection of non-negative weights
\{\varpi_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{V}}
.

Finally, we assume that the observations are local, i.e.
y_{i}
is the only observation at
\mathrm{S}_{i}
. As a result, the local posterior possibility function at node
i
can be computed as

f^{(i)}(x\,|\,y_{i})\propto h^{(i)}(y_{i}\,|\,x)\sup_{x^{\prime}\in S}g^{\varpi_{i}}(x\,|\,x^{\prime})f^{\omega_{i}}(x^{\prime}).

The method we propose is reminiscent of iterative message-passing approaches to decentralised inference
[
1
]
: we assume that each node
j\in\mathcal{V}
communicates with its neighbours and combines the information it receives by using a collection of non-negative weights
\{\Gamma_{i,j}\}_{{i,j}\in{\cal N}}
, where
\Gamma_{i,j}=0
if
i\notin{\cal N}_{j}
, using

\displaystyle f^{(j,l)}(x)\propto

\displaystyle{\Big(f^{(j,l-1)}(x)\Big)^{\Gamma_{j,j}}\mspace{-4mu}\prod_{i\in\mathcal{N}_{j}}\Big(f^{(i,l-1)}(x)\Big)^{\Gamma_{i,j}}},

(14)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{i\in\mathcal{V}}\Big(f^{(i,l-1)}(x)\Big)^{\Gamma_{i,j}}\quad l=1,\dots,L

The initial condition of the iterations is selected as
f^{{\scriptscriptstyle(i,0)}}=f^{{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}}(\cdot\,|\,y_{i})
, so that the independence of the terms on the right hand-side of (
14
) are maintained for all steps
l=1,\dots,L
following from the independence of the local posterior possibility functions
f^{{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}}(\cdot\,|\,y_{i})
at
l=1
.

Let us denote by
\Gamma
the weight matrix
(\Gamma_{i,j})_{i,j\in\mathcal{V}}
. The
l
th power of this matrix
\Gamma^{l}
is related to the locally fused result in (
14
) by

\displaystyle f^{(j,l)}(x)=

\displaystyle\prod_{i\in\mathcal{V}}\Big(f^{(i,0)}(x)\Big)^{\Gamma^{l}_{i,j}},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{i\in\mathcal{V}}\Big(f^{(i)}(x\,|\,y_{i})\Big)^{\Gamma^{l}_{i,j}},\quad l=1,\dots,L,

so, we focus on the limiting case
\Gamma^{l}\xrightarrow{l\to\infty}\Gamma^{*}
. In turn, the
(i,j)
-th entry of
\Gamma^{*}
is denoted by
\Gamma^{*}_{i,j}
, which we assume positive for any
i,j\in\mathcal{V}
. Therefore, the product of the locally fused possibility functions in the limit is given by

\displaystyle\hat{f}(x)\propto

\displaystyle\prod_{j\in\mathcal{V}}\bigg[\prod_{i\in\mathcal{V}}\Big(f^{(i)}(x\,|\,y_{i})\Big)^{\Gamma^{*}_{i,j}}\bigg],

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{i,j\in\mathcal{V}}\Big(f^{(i)}(x\,|\,y_{i})\Big)^{\Gamma^{*}_{i,j}}.

(15)

Let us now investigate the conditions under which the result of the decentralised inference scheme in (
14
), (
15
) matches the network posterior in (
13
). We consider the following conditions

A1

It holds that
\omega_{i}=\varpi_{i}
for all
i\in\mathcal{V}
and that
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}\omega_{i}=1

A2

The limit condition for the weights in (
14
) as
l\rightarrow\infty
is that
\Gamma^{*}_{i,j}=1/n
for all
i,j\in\mathcal{V}

The following theorem demonstrates that under these assumptions, the fused posterior
\hat{f}
is equal to the posterior possibility function
f(\cdot\,|\,y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
.

1
.

Under Assumptions A1 and A2, the proposed decentralised Bayesian inference algorithm is (asymptotically) exact, i.e. the right hand-side of (
15
) equals to the network’s posterior possibility in (
13
).

Before its proof, a crucial aspect of Theorem
1
should be noted; it makes no assumptions on the nature of the set
S
on which the fusion is performed. In particular, it does not rule out hierarchical problems such as the ones appearing in multi-target tracking, where both the number of targets and their respective state must be inferred. We illustrate this aspect in Section
V
where fusion is performed in a case where the existence of a target is uncertain; we then show in Section
VI
that Theorem
1
can be verified in practice for such problems despite the use of approximations.

Proof.

Let us express the fused result in the limit case given by (
15
) in a more explicit fashion as

\displaystyle\hat{f}(x)

\displaystyle\propto\prod_{i,j\in\mathcal{V}}\Big[h^{(i)}(y_{i}\,|\,x)\sup_{x^{\prime}\in S}g^{\varpi_{i}}(x\,|\,x^{\prime})f^{\omega_{i}}(x^{\prime})\Big]^{\Gamma^{*}_{i,j}}

\displaystyle\mspace{-5mu}=\prod_{i,j\in\mathcal{V}}h^{(i)}(y_{i}\,|\,x)^{\Gamma^{*}_{i,j}}\sup_{x^{\prime}\in S}g^{\varpi_{i}\Gamma^{*}_{i,j}}(x\,|\,x^{\prime})\big(f(x^{\prime})\big)^{\omega_{i}\Gamma^{*}_{i,j}}.

For
\hat{f}=f(\cdot\,|\,y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
to hold, a necessary (but not sufficient) condition is
\Gamma^{*}_{i,j}=1/n
; otherwise the contribution from the likelihood
h^{{\scriptscriptstyle(}i{\scriptscriptstyle)}}(y_{i}\,|\,\cdot)
would not match the one in (
13
) in general (that is, except if
h^{{\scriptscriptstyle(}i{\scriptscriptstyle)}}(y_{i}\,|\,\cdot)
takes value in
\{0,1\}
, in which case raising it to a positive finite power has no effect). It remains to match the contributions from the prediction step(s). We now have

\hat{f}(x)\propto\bigg[\prod_{i\in\mathcal{V}}h^{(i)}(y_{i}\,|\,x)\bigg]\bigg[\prod_{i\in\mathcal{V}}\sup_{x^{\prime}\in S}g^{\varpi_{i}}(x\,|\,x^{\prime})f^{\omega_{i}}(x^{\prime})\bigg].

Sufficient conditions for
\hat{f}=f(\cdot\,|\,y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
to hold are
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}\omega_{i}=1
and
\omega_{i}=\varpi_{i}
for all
i\in\mathcal{V}
. Indeed, under these conditions, we have

\displaystyle\prod_{i\in\mathcal{V}}\sup_{x^{\prime}\in S}g^{\varpi_{i}}(x\,|\,x^{\prime})f^{\omega_{i}}(x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\prod_{i\in\mathcal{V}}\Big[\sup_{x^{\prime}\in S}g(x\,|\,x^{\prime})f(x^{\prime})\Big]^{\omega_{i}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{x^{\prime}\in S}g(x\,|\,x^{\prime})f(x^{\prime}),

which concludes the proof of the theorem.
∎

Theorem
1
does not impose any constraints on the values of
\{\omega_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{V}}
other than that they are in the
(n-1)
-dimensional simplex. The choice
\omega_{i}=1/n
is particularly convenient as it leads to every node

containing exactly
1/n
-th of the posterior information, i.e. it holds that

f^{(i,\infty)}(x)=f(x\,|\,y_{1},\dots,y_{n})^{1/n},\qquad i\in\mathcal{V}.

In other words, the locally fused possibility function at each node can be raised to the power
n
to exactly find the network’s posterior; this scheme becomes an exact decentralised inference scheme.

We now consider an immediate corollary to Theorem
1
which considers the case where
\mathcal{G}
is complete, i.e. there is an edge between every pair of nodes, with the following modified assumption:

A2’

It holds that
\mathcal{G}
is complete and that
\Gamma_{i,j}=1/n
for all
i,j\in\mathcal{V}
.

2
.

Under Assumptions A1 and A2’, the proposed decentralised Bayesian inference algorithm is exact.

To prove Corollary

\Gamma=\Gamma^{*}
, i.e., the asymptotic regime can be achieved in a single iteration.

2

Let us consider the case where there is a central node
\mathrm{C}
to which all the sensors are connected and which is the only node performing fusion. We refer to this scheme as centralised fusion. Since decentralised fusion with a complete graph is equivalent to centralised fusion, it holds that centralised fusion can also be performed exactly under Assumptions A1 and A2’.

3
.

The results of Theorem

An example of such a likelihood is provided in Section

IV
The possibilistic Bernoulli filter

IV-A

General recursion

In this section, we recall the equations of the possibilistic Bernoulli filter
[
12
]
and the underlying notion of Bernoulli uncertain finite set (UFS). The latter can be introduced as an uncertain variable
\bm{X}
on the set
\mathsf{X}=\{\emptyset\}\cup\{\{x\}:x\in S\}
, which is the set of finite subsets of a given space
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
with no more than one element. The UFS
\bm{X}
can be described by a possibility function
F
on
\mathsf{X}
, and we will adopt the convention

F(X)=\begin{cases}\beta&if $X=\emptyset$\\
\alpha f(x)&if $X=\{x\}$ with $x\in S$.\end{cases}

(16)

The fact that
F
is a possibility function implies that
\alpha
and
\beta
are non-negative,
\max\{\alpha,\beta\}=1
, and
f
is a possibility function on
S
. The possibilistic Bernoulli filter is a recursion for a possibility function of the same form as
F
describing the possibility of existence
\alpha
of a target in
S
and the information about its state via
f
. We follow the notations of Section
III
to describe a target’s dynamics and observation when it exists. We model the changes in cardinality by a transition matrix
(\tau_{ij})_{i,j\in\{0,1\}}
, where
\tau_{ij}
corresponds to transitioning from cardinality
i\in\{0,1\}
to cardinality
j\in\{0,1\}
. For instance,
\tau_{01}
is the possibility of birth. The spatial information about birth is described by a possibility function
f_{\mathrm{b}}
on
S
. Denoting by
F_{k-1}
the Bernoulli possibility function describing the state and existence of the target at time
k-1
, given the observations up to time
k-1
, and following the convention of (
16
), we can now express
F_{k|k-1}
, the predicted Bernoulli possibility function at
k
, as

F_{k|k-1}(X)=\begin{cases}\beta_{k|k-1}&if $X=\emptyset$\\
\alpha_{k|k-1}f_{k|k-1}(x)&if $X=\{x\}$ with $x\in S$.\end{cases}

(17)

with

\displaystyle\beta_{k|k-1}

\displaystyle=\max\{\beta_{k-1}\tau_{00},\alpha_{k-1}\tau_{10}\}

(18a)

\displaystyle\alpha_{k|k-1}

\displaystyle=\max\{\beta_{k-1}\tau_{01},\alpha_{k-1}\tau_{11}\}

(18b)

\displaystyle f_{k|k-1}(x)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{k|k-1}^{-1}\max\big\{\beta_{k-1}\tau_{01}f_{\mathrm{b}}(x),\alpha_{k-1}\tau_{11}\tilde{f}(x)\big\},

(18c)

where
\tilde{f}(x)=\sup_{x^{\prime}\in S}g(x\,|\,x^{\prime})f_{k-1}(x^{\prime})
is the predicted possibility function describing the state of the target assuming that it exists at both times
k-1
and
k
.

As opposed to the approach in
[
12
]
, we consider a partially-probabilistic observation model where the detection and the data association are possibilistic and where the generation of false alarms and of the observation under the assumption of detection are probabilistic. We assume as is usual that at most one observation originates from the target. This yields a likelihood
\ell(Y_{k}\,|\,X)
for the observation set
Y_{k}
at time
k
characterised by
\ell(Y_{k}\,|\,\emptyset)=\kappa(Y_{k})
and

\ell(Y_{k}\,|\,\{x\})=\max\big\{\beta_{\mathrm{d}}\kappa(Y_{k}),\max_{y\in Y_{k}}\alpha_{\mathrm{d}}p(y\,|\,x)\kappa(Y_{k}\setminus\{y\})\big\},

where
\kappa
is the density of the random finite set characterising the false alarms, where
\alpha_{\mathrm{d}}
and
\beta_{\mathrm{d}}
are respectively the possibilities of detection and non-detection, and where
p(\cdot\,|\,x)
is the probability distribution of the observation given that the target is detected at state
x\in S
. The likelihood
\ell(\cdot\,|\,X)
is neither a possibility function nor a probability distribution and its derivation requires more advanced notions which are relegated to the appendix.

Despite the considered partially-probabilistic approach, the form of the likelihood is similar to the one in
[
12
]
and the updated possibility function
F_{k}
can be found in the same way: Following the same convention as before, we obtain

\displaystyle\beta_{k}

\displaystyle\propto\ell(Y_{k}\,|\,\emptyset)\beta_{k|k-1}=\kappa(Y_{k})\beta_{k|k-1}

(19a)

\displaystyle\alpha_{k}

\displaystyle\propto\alpha_{k|k-1}\sup_{x\in S}\ell(Y_{k}\,|\,\{x\})f_{k|k-1}(x)

(19b)

\displaystyle f_{k}(x)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\ell(Y_{k}\,|\,\{x\})f_{k|k-1}(x)}{\sup_{x\in S}\ell(Y_{k}\,|\,\{x\})f_{k|k-1}(x)},

(19c)

where
\max\{\alpha_{k},\beta_{k}\}=1
. Although it has limited influence on the form of the updated possibility function
F_{k}
, the introduction of the probabilistic elements
\kappa
and
p(\cdot\,|\,x)
allows for better connecting the proposed approach with the existing literature and hints at the potential for introducing more sophisticated mixed models in the future.

Implementation

For the sake of simplicity, we assume that the dynamics and likelihood are Gaussian, i.e. there exist matrices

,

,

\displaystyle\quad\max_{y\in Y_{k}}\alpha_{\mathrm{d}}\kappa(Y_{k}\setminus\{y\})\overline{\mathrm{N}}(x;H\mu_{k|k-1,i},HP_{k|k-1,i}H^{\intercal}+R)\big\},

Terms that are sufficiently close are then merged following the standard procedure, with the only difference being that the final weight of the merged term is the maximum of the weights of the terms being merged

Prediction

As shown in Section
III
, the Markov transition needs to be discounted to preserve the independence between sensor nodes. In the considered Gaussian max-mixture implementation of the possibilistic Bernoulli filter, the Markov transition
G(\cdot\,|\,X_{k-1})
on
\mathsf{X}
is characterised by

G(X_{k}\,|\,\emptyset)=\begin{cases}\tau_{00}&if $X_{k}=\emptyset$\\
\tau_{01}f_{\mathrm{b}}(x_{k})&if $X_{k}=\{x_{k}\}$,\end{cases}

and

G(X_{k}\,|\,\{x_{k-1}\})=\begin{cases}\tau_{10}&if $X_{k}=\emptyset$\\
\tau_{11}g(x_{k}\,|\,x_{k-1})&if $X_{k}=\{x_{k}\}$.\end{cases}

The discounted version
G(\cdot\,|\,X_{k-1})^{\omega}
of this Markov transition is of the same form, with each term simply being brought to the power
\omega\in[0,1]
. This allows for (

and

\tau_{11}
and
g(x_{k}\,|\,x_{k-1})
. Lines 5-11 in Algorithm
1
follow directly from these calculations and from the fact that Gaussian possibility functions are closed under exponentiation. A related and important result that is unique to possibility theory is that Gaussian max-mixtures are closed under power, indeed, for any Gaussian max-mixture
f
, say,

f(x)=\max_{i\in\{1,\dots,N\}}w_{i}\overline{\mathrm{N}}(x;\mu_{i},P_{i}),

for some
N\in\mathbb{N}
, some non-negative weights
w_{i}
such that
\max_{i\in\{1,\dots,N\}}w_{i}=1
, and some collection
\{(\mu_{i},P_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
of expected values and covariance matrices. It holds that
f^{\omega}
is also a Gaussian max-mixture for any
\omega\in[0,1]
. In particular, it holds that

f^{\omega}(x)=\max_{i\in\{1,\dots,N\}}w_{i}^{\omega}\overline{\mathrm{N}}(x;\mu_{i},P_{i}/\omega).

This result justifies the discounted birth model implemented in Lines 12-17 of Algorithm
1
.

Update

The
\mathrm{LocalUpdate}()

2
, implements directly the approach of Section

. Indeed, based on the assumed conditional independence of the observations between sensor nodes, there is no redundancy to be corrected and therefore no particular discount to be applied. Line 16 of Algorithm

[

VI
Simulations

We aim to show that the required approximations, i.e. pruning and merging of the Gaussian max-mixture, and a limited number of hops on the sensor network do not significantly lower the performance of the proposed method; recalling that, without these approximations and limitations, the proposed method would be asymptotically exact. The considered baselines are the decentralised fusion algorithms for the Bernoulli filter based on geometric average
[
21
]
, arithmetic average
[
22
]
as well as the iterated (i.e. non-distributed) versions of both the probabilistic and possibilistic Bernoulli filters.

We consider simulations designed using the units of the international system, which allows for making these units implicit. Specifically, we consider
K=25
time steps of duration
\Delta=1
, where targets evolve according to a nearly-constant velocity model in the 2-dimensional Euclidean plane, i.e.
X_{k}=GX_{k-1}+U_{k}
with
U_{k}\sim\mathrm{N}(0,Q)
independently for any
k

G=I_{2}\otimes\begin{bmatrix}1&\Delta\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad Q=\sigma^{2}I_{2}\otimes\begin{bmatrix}\Delta^{4}/4&\Delta^{3}/2\\
\Delta^{3}/2&\Delta^{2}\end{bmatrix},

and,
\sigma=0.5
is the standard deviation of the process noise. We assume that all the sensors have the same parameters and omit the superscript
\cdot^{(i)}
whenever appropriate. Only the location of targets is observed by the sensors, that is
Y_{k}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(}i{\scriptscriptstyle)}}=H(X_{k}-x_{\mathrm{s}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(}i{\scriptscriptstyle)}})+V_{k}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(}i{\scriptscriptstyle)}}
with
V_{k}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(}i{\scriptscriptstyle)}}\sim\mathrm{N}(0,\sigma^{\prime 2}I_{2})
independently for any
k
and any
i
, where
\sigma^{\prime}=5
is the standard deviation of the observational noise and where

H=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0&0\\
0&0&1&0\end{bmatrix}.

The state space is therefore defined as

and we assume the observation space to be of the form
S^{\prime}=[0,1000]\times[0,1000]
. We consider the case of
n=4

S^{\prime}
.

Any given sensor has an edge connecting it to the
2

for the target is born at any given time step and, when it is born, its state is drawn from

An important aspect of the considered possibilistic approach is that it does not take the above data-generating mechanism directly as a model, and instead translates it into possibility functions. The Gaussian possibility function describing the dynamics is assumed to take the same expected value and covariance matrices as in the true model, and target-wise parameters are translated as follows: for any

[
13

VII
Conclusion

In this article, we have demonstrated how possibility theory allows for fusing and sharing information on a sensor network while preserving the optimality of non-distributed solutions. Maintaining the independence between sensor nodes was key in allowing decentralised fusion to fully leverage the observations of all sensors, leveraging unique properties of possibility theory. Despite the fact that the Bernoulli filter and its possibilistic counterpart perform similarly in single-sensor problems, significant gains were observed for decentralised networks. Future work includes improving the robustness to potential calibration errors of some of the sensors, hence leveraging the capabilities of possibility theory in terms of robust inference
[
34
,
35
]
.

Likelihood for the possibilistic Bernoulli filter

We first consider separately the probabilistic and possibilistic components of the likelihood. We model the uncertainty about the detection of the target as epistemic, with a possibility function characterised by
\alpha_{\mathrm{d}}
for a detection and
\beta_{\mathrm{d}}
for a detection failure. The uncertainty in the data association is considered epistemic as there is a true data association which is simply unknown. For the sake of simplicity, we re-express the observation set
Y
as a vector
\tilde{y}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{m})
. Assuming that a detection has occurred, a possible association is defined as choosing a component of
\tilde{y}
as the true detection, so that the other components are all false alarms. There is no prior information about the data association, which we model as permutations of indices in
\{1,\dots,m\}
, so we choose
\bm{1}
as the corresponding possibility function.

If the target exists, is at state
x\in S
, and is detected, then the true observation
y
is characterised by the conditional probability distribution
p(\cdot\,|\,x)
and the false alarms are characterised by
\kappa
, with an underlying cardinality distribution
c
on
\mathbb{N}
and, assuming there are
n
false alarms, with a p.d.f.
p_{n}
on
(S^{\prime})^{n}
. In case of detection, the uncertainty in the observation is defined via an
outer probability measure

\bar{P}_{\mathrm{d}}

[
10
]
, characterised by

\bar{P}_{\mathrm{d}}(\varphi\,|\,x)=\sum_{n\geq 1}c(n-1)\int p(y^{\prime}_{1}\,|\,x)p_{n-1}(y^{\prime}_{2},\dots,y^{\prime}_{n})\times\\
\max_{\sigma}\big(\varphi(y^{\prime}_{\sigma(1)},\dots,y^{\prime}_{\sigma(n)})\big)\mathrm{d}y^{\prime}_{1}\dots\mathrm{d}y^{\prime}_{n}

for any real-valued function
\varphi
taking vectors of observations as argument, where the maximum ranges over all permutations
\sigma
of
\{1,\dots,n\}
. Setting
\varphi=\delta_{(y_{1},\dots,y_{m})}
yields

\bar{P}_{\mathrm{d}}(y_{1},\dots,y_{m}\,|\,x)=c(m-1)\times\\
\max_{\sigma}p(y_{\sigma^{-1}(1)}\,|\,x)p_{m-1}(y_{\sigma^{-1}(2)},\dots,y_{\sigma^{-1}(m)}),

which can be simplified using the definition of
p_{n-1}
as

\bar{P}_{\mathrm{d}}(y_{1},\dots,y_{m}\,|\,x)=\max_{y\in Y}p(y\,|\,x)\kappa(Y\setminus\{y\}).

Following the same steps, we find that the likelihood for the case where the target is not detecting is simply
\kappa(Y)
. Finally, taking the epistemic uncertainty regarding detection into account, we obtain the likelihood of interest.
