A new mathematical symbol : the termirial
Claude-Alexandre Simonetti

Abstract
Résumé The understanding of probability can be difficult for a few young scientists. Consequently, this new mathematical symbol, related to binomial coefficients and simplicial polytopic numbers, could be helpful to science education. Moreover, one can obtain kinds of remarkable identities and generalize them to a sort of “Newton’s binomial theorem”. Finally, this symbol could be perhaps useful to other scientific subjects as well, such as computer science.

I.1
Reminder

In a set of n elements, the number of combinations of parts of p elements is the following :

\binom{n}{p}=\frac{n!}{p!\penalty\ (n-p)!}

(1)

With
\binom{n}{p}
the binomial coefficient,
(n,p)\in{\mathbb{N}^{2}}
and
p\leq n
. And the exclamation point in equation (
1
) is named “factorial” and is defined as,
\forall n\in{\mathbb{N}^{*}}
:

\displaystyle n!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n\cdot(n-1)\cdot(n-2)\penalty\ ...\penalty\ 3\cdot 2\cdot 1

\displaystyle n!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{n}i

(2)

For
n=0
, by definition : 0 !=1.

I.2
From factorial to termirial

For its part, the termirial is defined as,
\forall n\in{\mathbb{N^{*}}}
:

\displaystyle n\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n+(n-1)+...+3+2+1

\displaystyle n\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}i

(3)

This is called a triangular number of order n, see reference
Dickson 1919
. The termirial symbol is almost like the factorial symbol, with just a little difference, however : the dot (
\cdot
) of the interrogation point, which could be a reminder of a multiplication, is replaced by a “plus” (
+
) sign. Indeed, instead of multiplying factors of a multiplication – factorial – one adds terms of an addition, hence the name of “termirial”. Up to this point, things look quite basic. However, as the termirial is the
n^{th}
partial sum of an arithmetic sequence
(U_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N^{*}}}}
with
U_{1}=1
as first term and
r=1
as common difference, one can notice a first thing,
\forall n\in{\mathbb{N^{*}}}
:

\displaystyle n\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{n\cdot(n+1)}{2}=\binom{n+1}{2}=\binom{n+1}{n-1}

(4)

One will get back to it later, with the “generalized” termirial.

I.3
Remarkable identity

It is possible to obtain a kind of remarkable identity. Indeed,
\forall(n,m)\in{\mathbb{N^{*}}^{2}}
:

\displaystyle(n+m)\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(n+m)\cdot(n+m+1)}{2}

\displaystyle(n+m)\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{n\cdot(n+1)}{2}+\frac{2\cdot n\cdot m}{2}+\frac{m\cdot(m+1)}{2}

\displaystyle(n+m)\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}+n\cdot m+m\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}

(5)

\forall(n,m)\in{\mathbb{N^{*}}^{2}}

II
Generalization of the termirial

II.1
The
p^{th}
termirial

Obviously, the the
p^{th}
termirial, which is called a (p+1)-simplicial polytopic number, see reference
Dickson 1919
, will be defined as,
\forall(n,p)\in{\mathbb{N^{*}}\times\mathbb{N^{*}}}
:

\displaystyle n\overset{(p)}{\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{n}\sum_{l=1}^{m}...\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sum_{i=1}^{j}i

(12)

\displaystyle n\overset{(p)}{\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}k\overset{(p-1)}{\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{p!}\prod_{i=0}^{p-1}(k+i)

(13)

\displaystyle n\overset{(p)}{\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\binom{n+p}{p+1}=\binom{n+p}{n-1}

(14)

\displaystyle n\overset{(p)}{\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(p+1)!}\prod_{i=0}^{p}(n+i)

(15)

With, for the equation (
12
), a number of
sigmas

(\sum)
symbols equal to
p
.

For
p=0
, one can define the
0^{th}
termirial as :

n\overset{(0)}{\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}}=n=\binom{n}{1}

For
p=-1
, one can define the
-1^{th}
termirial as :

n\overset{(-1)}{\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}}=1=\binom{n-1}{0}

The proof to go from (
13
) to (
14
) (or from (
13
) to (
15
)) can be done with mathematical induction as well. Indeed, admitting that the following
P(n)
proposition is true :

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{p!}\prod_{i=0}^{p}(k+i)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(p+1)!}\prod_{i=0}^{p}(n+i)

(16)

One have, for
P(1)
:

\displaystyle 1\overset{(p)}{\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}}=\frac{1}{p!}\prod_{i=0}^{p-1}(1+i)=\frac{p!}{p!}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(p+1)!}\prod_{i=0}^{p}(1+i)=\frac{(p+1)!}{(p+1)!}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1

(17)

And, for
P(n+1)
:

\displaystyle(n+1)\overset{(p)}{\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{p!}\prod_{i=0}^{p-1}(k+i)+\frac{1}{p!}\prod_{i=0}^{p-1}(n+1+i)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(p+1)!}\prod_{i=0}^{p}(n+i)+\frac{(p+1)}{(p+1)!}\prod_{i=1}^{p}(n+i)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(n+p+1)}{(p+1)!}\prod_{i=1}^{p}(n+i)

\displaystyle(n+1)\overset{(p)}{\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(p+1)!}\prod_{i=1}^{p+1}(n+i)=\binom{n+p+1}{p+1}

(18)

QED
.

\displaystyle(n+1)\overset{(p)}{\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}}+(n)\overset{(p+1)}{\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(n+1)\overset{(p+1)}{\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}}

II.3
Newton’s binomial theorem

With Newton’s binomial theorem as a reminder,
\forall(a,b)\in{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}}
and
\forall n\in{\mathbb{N}}
:

\displaystyle(a+b)^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{p=0}^{n}\binom{n}{p}a^{n-p}\cdot b^{p}

It is possible to adapt it as well to termirials,
\forall(n,m)\in{\mathbb{N}^{*}\times\mathbb{N}^{*}}
and
\forall p\in{\{\{-1\}\cup\mathbb{N}\}}
:

\displaystyle(n+m)\overset{(p)}{\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=-1}^{p}n\overset{(i)}{\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}}\cdot m\overset{(p-i-1)}{\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}}

(19)

The proof can be done with mathematical induction. Indeed, admitting that the following
P(p)
proposition is true :

\displaystyle(n+m)\overset{(p)}{\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=-1}^{p}n\overset{(i)}{\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}}\cdot m\overset{(p-i-1)}{\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}}

First of all, equations (
5
) and (
11
) respectively hold
P(1)
and
P(2)
, even if only
P(1)
– or only
P(2)
– is enough for this mathematical induction. Considering
P(p+1)
:

\displaystyle(n+m)\overset{(p+1)}{\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{n+m+p+1}{p+2}(n+m)\overset{(p)}{\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{n+m+p+1}{p+2}\sum_{i=-1}^{p}n\overset{(i)}{\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}}\cdot m\overset{(p-i-1)}{\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{n+m+p+1}{p+2}\sum_{i=-1}^{p}n\overset{(i)}{\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}}\cdot m\overset{(p-i-1)}{\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p+2}\sum_{i=-1}^{p}\{[n+(i+1)]+[m+p+1-(i+1)]\}\cdot n\overset{(i)}{\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}}\cdot m\overset{(p-i-1)}{\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p+2}\sum_{i=-1}^{p}[n+(i+1)]\cdot n\overset{(i)}{\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}}\cdot m\overset{(p-i-1)}{\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}}+\frac{1}{p+2}\sum_{i=-1}^{p}[m+p+1-(i+1)]\cdot n\overset{(i)}{\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}}\cdot m\overset{(p-i-1)}{\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p+2}\sum_{i=-1}^{p}(i+2)\cdot n\overset{(i+1)}{\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}}\cdot m\overset{(p-i-1)}{\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}}+\frac{1}{p+2}\sum_{i=-1}^{p}[p+2-(i+1)]\cdot n\overset{(i)}{\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}}\cdot m\overset{(p+1-i-1)}{\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p+2}\sum_{i=0}^{p+1}(i+1)n\overset{(i)}{\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}}m\overset{(p+1-i-1)}{\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}}+\sum_{i=-1}^{p}n\overset{(i)}{\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}}m\overset{(p+1-i-1)}{\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}}-\frac{1}{p+2}\sum_{i=-1}^{p}(i+1)n\overset{(i)}{\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}}m\overset{(p+1-i-1)}{\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n\overset{(p+1)}{\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}}\cdot m\overset{(-1)}{\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}}+\sum_{i=-1}^{p}n\overset{(i)}{\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}}m\overset{(p+1-i-1)}{\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}}

\displaystyle(n+m)\overset{(p+1)}{\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=-1}^{p+1}n\overset{(i)}{\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}}m\overset{(p+1-i-1)}{\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}}

QED
.

III
Pratical applications

III.1
Computational complexity

In computer science, the termirial can be used to calculate the complexity of computer programs with intricated “for” loops as follows : for example, with 4 “for” loops, with :

- for i from 1 to n=100  ;

- for j from 1 to i  ;

- for k from 1 to j  ;

- for l from 1 to k.

The complexity will be :

\displaystyle 100\overset{(4-1)}{\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\binom{100+3}{3+1}=\binom{103}{4}=\binom{103}{99}=4\penalty\ 421\penalty\ 275

So, approximatey 4,4 millions of operations.

More generally, the complexity of this kind of program will be, according to equation (
15
) and
\forall(n,p)\in{\mathbb{N^{*}}\times\mathbb{N^{*}}}
:
n\overset{(p-1)}{\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}}=\Theta(n^{p})
.

III.2
Pseudo-fractal aspect

Figure 3:
This figure tries to convert
p^{th}
termirial of 4 into fractal figures, for p=0, p=1 and p=2. Only the grey squares are counted.

The figure
3
tries to convert
p^{th}
termirial of 4 into fractal figures, for p=0, p=1 and p=2. Only the grey squares are counted. For example, for
4\overset{(2)}{\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}}
, 20 squares are in grey, which corresponds to
4\overset{(2)}{\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}}=20
. For each incrementation of p, the side of each square is divided by 2, and the same basic pattern is repeated :

-
4\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}
when the 4 previous squares are in grey  ;

-
3\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}
when the 3 previous squares are in grey  ;

-
2\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}
when the 2 previous squares are in grey  ;

-
1\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}
when only 1 previous square is in grey.

But the Hausdorff dimension (D) of this presumed fractal is not constant, and the limit of D as p approaches infinity is 2. Indeed, let
a
the side length of each square related to the
p^{th}
termirial. The total surface
S_{p}
of the squares in grey will be :

\displaystyle S_{p}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a^{2}\cdot n\overset{(p)}{\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}}=a^{2}\cdot\frac{1}{(p+1)!}\prod_{i=0}^{p}(n+i)

And for the
S_{p-1}
surface, related to the
(p-1)^{th}
termirial, for a side length of each square doubled in size :

\displaystyle S_{p-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4a^{2}\cdot n\overset{(p-1)}{\overset{|}{\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\tiny{+}}}}=4a^{2}\cdot\frac{1}{(1)!}\prod_{i=0}^{p-1}(n+i)

Hence, for
p>0
:

\displaystyle\frac{S_{p-1}}{S_{p}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4\cdot\frac{p+n}{p+1}=4\cdot\frac{1+n/p}{1+1/p}

Finally, this surface ratio, as p approaches infinity, is 4 :

\displaystyle\lim_{p\to\infty}\frac{S_{p-1}}{S_{p}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4

Consequently, the Hausdorff dimension (D) is 2, as
2^{D}=4
, when p approaches infinity. To conclude, as D is not constant, even if figure
3
looks interesting, termirials do not seem to be related to fractals.

IV
Conclusion

The termirial is a symbol which could be helpful to scientific students, for the understanding of probability. As well, it could be perhaps helpful in other subjects than science education, that are out of this pré-publication.
