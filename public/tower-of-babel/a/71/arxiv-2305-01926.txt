Superconducting Stiffness and Coherence Length of FeSe 0.5 Te 0.5 Measured in Zero-Applied Field
Amotz Peri, Itay Mangel, Amit Keren

Abstract
Superconducting stiffness ρ s \rho_{s} and coherence length ξ \xi are usually determined by measuring the penetration depth λ \lambda of a magnetic field and the upper critical field H c ​ 2 H_{c2} of a superconductor (SC), respectively. However, in magnetic SC, e.g. some of the iron-based, this could lead to erroneous results since the internal field could be very different from the applied one. To overcome this problem in Fe 1+y Se x Te 1-x with x ∼ 0.5 x\sim 0.5 and y ∼ 0 y\sim 0 (FST), we measure both quantities with the Stiffnessometer technique. In this technique, one applies a rotor-free vector potential A to a superconducting ring and measures the current density j via the ring’s magnetic moment m . ρ s \rho_{s} and ξ \xi are determined from London’s equation j = − ρ s ​ A \textbf{j}=-\rho_{s}\textbf{A} and its range of validity. This method is particularly accurate at temperatures close to the critical temperature T c T_{c} . We find weaker ρ s \rho_{s} and longer ξ \xi than existing literature reports, and critical exponents which agree better with expectations based on the Ginzburg–Landau theory.

The superconducting stiffness
\rho_{s}
is defined via the gauge-invariant relation between the current density
j
, the total vector potential
\textbf{A}_{\text{tot}}
from all sources, and the complex order parameter
\Psi(\textbf{r})=\psi(\textbf{r})\text{e}^{\text{i}\phi(\textbf{r})}
with
\psi(\textbf{r})>0
, according to

\textbf{j}=-\rho_{s}(\textbf{A}_{\text{tot}}-\frac{\Phi_{0}}{2\pi}\bm{\nabla}\phi)\,,

(1)

where
\Phi_{0}=2\pi\hbar/e^{*}
is the superconducting flux quanta,

\rho_{s}=\frac{\psi^{2}e^{*2}}{m^{*}}\,,

(2)

is the stiffness,
e^{*}
and
m^{*}
are the carrier’s charge and mass, respectively. For anisotropic stiffness see Ref.
Kapon et al. 2019
. When cooling the superconductor (SC) with
\textbf{A}_{\text{tot}}=0
in the London gauge, minimum kinetic energy requires
\bm{\nabla}\phi=0
. According to the second Josephson relation,
\phi
can only change by dissipating energy. Thus, Eq.
1
becomes the London equation

\textbf{j}=-\rho_{s}\textbf{A}_{\text{tot}}\,.

(3)

This relation holds as long
\bm{\nabla}\phi
does not change. The stiffness, in turn, is related to the penetration depth via

\rho_{s}=\frac{1}{\mu_{0}\lambda^{2}}\,.

(4)

However, every superconductor has a critical current density
j_{c}
determined by the penetration depth
\lambda
and coherence length
\xi
. When
\textbf{A}_{\text{tot}}
exceeds a certain value, it is worthwhile for the SC to change
\bm{\nabla}\phi
so as to keep
j
below
j_{c}
everywhere in the SC. According to the Josephson equation, dynamic changes in
\phi
lead to voltage, which, when combined with current, result in power and energy dissipation in the process. When this happens, the relation between
j
and
\textbf{A}_{\text{tot}}
is no longer linear and the system’s rigidity breaks. We used these properties to measure both
\rho_{s}
and
\xi
as a function of temperature in FST.

EXPERIMENT SETUP

The experiment is assembled from a ring-shaped SC cut out of a single crystal, shown in Fig.
1

IV.1

The excitation coil, ring, and second-order gradiometer are surrounded by a main coil, as in panel (d). The main coil is used to zero the field to less than 0.1
\mu
T on the ring, and for field-dependent measurement. Details of the dimensions of the different parts are given in the figure’s caption. EC current

It utilizes a lock-in amplifier to measure the SQUID output voltage at twice the vibration frequency. This output is proportional to the magnetic flux of a sample through the gradiometer, namely, the vector potential of the sample. It could also be represented by the magnetic moment of the sample.

Measurements

III.1
Stiffness and critical current

We cool the system below
T_{c}
with
I_{\text{ec}}=0

To isolate the superconducting signal we subtract the moment of the measurement with zero current, which is due to the ferromagnetic properties of FST and not its stiffness. In addition, we remove the current dependent of the signal above

. This signal is due to the EC’s finite length and asymmetry.

Typical behavior in our measurements, for low currents, is a linear relation between the ring’s moment and

and Sec.

;
Keren et al. 2022

T_{c}
of FST is higher than any commercially available superconducting wire. Instead, we calibrated the temperature at the ring position using an open ring. The calibration is discussed in Appx.
A

To extract the stiffness, we fit each

to a line in a temperature-dependent range due to the variation in the critical current. Such a fit is demonstrated in the inset of Fig.
2
(a). The slope represents

in the limit

. The temperature dependence of the slopes appears as blue circles in Fig.
2
(b). The measurements do not cover all the temperature ranges up to

since it becomes exceedingly difficult to define a linear region in the

, a knee appears in the temperature dependence of

The red down triangles in Fig.

Finally, in Fig.

IV
Analysis Model

The analysis of Stiffnessometer data is described in details in Ref.
Gavish et al. 2021
and is valid for systems with cylindrical symmetry. Here we provide only the major steps.

IV.1
Stiffness

In the low flux regime (low currents in the EC), the magnitude of the order parameter is constant almost all over the superconducting ring and zero outside
Gavish et al. 2021
. Substituting
\textbf{B}=\bm{\nabla}\times\textbf{A}
and London equation into amperes law gives

\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{\nabla}\times\textbf{A}_{\text{sc}}=-\mu_{0}\rho_{s}\textbf{A}_{\text{tot}}\,,

(7)

since on the ring
\bm{\nabla}\times\textbf{A}_{\text{ec}}=0
. In the London gauge
\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{\nabla}\times\textbf{A}=-\nabla^{2}\textbf{A}
, and the vector potential outside an infinitely long coil is given by

\textbf{A}_{\text{ec}}(r)=\Phi_{\text{ec}}/(2\pi r)\hat{\varphi}\,.

(8)

With Eq.
4
we arrive at the partial differential equation (PDE)

\nabla^{2}\textbf{A}_{\text{sc}}=\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\Big(\textbf{A}_{\text{sc}}+\frac{\Phi_{\text{ec}}}{2\pi r}\hat{\varphi}\Big)\,,

(9)

where
\lambda=\infty
outside the SC. Normalizing the spatial variables and vector potential as follows

\textbf{r}/R_{\text{pl}}\rightarrow\textbf{r}\;,\quad\textbf{A}_{\text{sc}}/\textbf{A}_{\text{ec}}(R_{\text{pl}})\rightarrow\textbf{A}\;,\quad\lambda/R_{\text{pl}}\rightarrow\lambda,

(10)

and using cylindrical coordinates where
\textbf{A}=A(r,z)\hat{\varphi}
, we end up with the following PDE

\frac{\partial^{2}A}{\partial z^{2}}+\frac{\partial^{2}A}{\partial r^{2}}+\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial A}{\partial r}-\frac{A}{r^{2}}=\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\Big(A+\frac{1}{r}\Big)\,.

(11)

We use the finite element-based FreeFem
++
software
Hecht 2012
to solve this PDE for different values of
\lambda
and the dimension of our FST ring, appearing in the caption of Fig.
1
. The equation is solved in a box such that
z\in[-L,\,L]
, and
r\in[0,\,8L]
with
L=R_{\text{pl}}=8.5
mm. Dirichlet boundary conditions are imposed.

As shown in Fig.
1
(b), the ring’s outer radius is not uniform. However, the solution of the Ginzburg-Landau (GL) equations
Gavish et al. 2021
shows that in the low flux regime, the current flow in a layer of width
\lambda
near the inner rim of the ring, so the system’s symmetry is not severely compromised. When the flux through the ring is increased, the current layer retreats toward the outer rim. This retraction ends when the current layer reaches the outer rim. In our case, we assume that it happens at the shortest distance of the outer rim from the center. We use this distance as the outer radius in the PDE
11
. Nevertheless, our assumption has not been tested numerically and the impact of a non perfect ring on the result is not clear yet.

The red line in the inset of Fig.
4
(a) depicts the numerical solution of PDE
11
. The Y-axis is the normalized vector potential
A
at the ring’s height
z=0
, and the pickup-loop radial location
r=R_{\text{pl}}
. The X-axis is
(R_{\text{pl}}/\lambda)^{2}
on a logarithmic scale.
Normalizing Eq.
5
by the vector potential of an infinite coil,

A_{\text{ec}}(R_{\text{pl}})=\frac{\mu_{0}nI_{\text{ec}}}{2R_{\text{pl}}}\sum_{i}r_{\text{ec},i}^{2}\,,

(12)

where

and
r_{\text{ec},i}
are windings per unit length in one layer, and radius of the

layer, respectively, we obtain the dimensionless vector potential

A(z=0,R_{\text{pl}})=\frac{g}{2\pi nR_{\text{pl}}\sum_{i}r_{\text{ec},i}^{2}}\cdot\frac{m}{I_{\text{ec}}}\,,

(13)

where
m
is the SC’s magnetic moment, and

a geometrical constant on the order of unity.

In reality, the coil is not infinite, and, as a result of cutting and drilling, the ring is not perfect, see Fig.
1
(b). Therefore, the calibration constant

is determined experimentally in two different methods: (1) We compare the saturated value of

from the solution of PDE

(see red line in Fig.
4
(a)-inset) to the saturated value of

A

at the same temperature to extract a second value for

. For this work, the stiffness was taken from Ref.
Biswas et al. 2010
. We found
g_{1}=0.5363
and
g_{2}=0.5336

IV.2
Coherence length

In the low flux regime
\Phi_{\text{ec}}/\Phi_{0}\ll r_{\text{in}}^{2}/\lambda\varepsilon
, and for
\lambda\ll r_{\text{out}}-r_{\text{in}}
and
h
, where
h
is the ring’s height, deep inside the ring
A_{\text{tot}}=0
, hence
A_{\text{sc}}=-A_{\text{ec}}
. In other words, the applied flux is matched by the flux generated by the ring in the hole. For
\Phi_{\text{ec}}/\Phi_{0}>r_{\text{in}}^{2}/\sqrt{8}\xi\lambda
, the current necessary to produce
A_{\text{sc}}
at
r_{\text{in}}
exceeds the local critical current
Gavish et al. 2021
. Then, it is energetically preferable for the order parameter magnitude to gradually diminish in the inner rim of the ring. Consequently, the superconducting ring hole effectively grows, and an effective inner radius
r_{\text{eff}}
is established.
At even higher flux,
r_{\text{eff}}
approaches
r_{\text{out}}
, and the SC is no longer able to expel the applied flux, namely, to cancel
A_{\text{ec}}
. This happens at a critical flux
Gavish et al. 2021

\frac{\Phi_{\text{c}}}{\Phi_{0}}=\frac{r_{\text{out}}^{2}}{\sqrt{8}\xi\lambda}\,.

(14)

While the derivation of Eq.
14
is in the limit
\xi\ll\lambda\ll r_{\text{out}}-r_{\text{in}}\ll h
, we believe it is valid for
\lambda\ll r_{\text{out}}-r_{\text{in}}
and
\lambda\ll h
separately.

For
\Phi>\Phi_{c}
, vortices are expected to penetrate from the inner rim towards the outer one so that the SC’s moment no longer grows with amplification of
I_{\text{ec}}
. These vortices are manifested in
\bm{\nabla}\phi
variations.

VII
DISCUSSION

We discuss the two major observations of this work, the knee, and the critical exponents, and examine the relation between measured quantities.

VII.1
The Knee

Mukasa
et al.
[
Mukasa et al. 2021
]
present the nematic transition temperatures
T_{s}
as a function of the Tellurium composition in Fe
1+y
Se
x
Te
1-x
. The lowest temperature measured by x-ray diffraction is
13.3
K, and there are no measurements close to the mid point
x=0.5
. Nevertheless, extrapolation of their data suggests that
T_{s}
and
T_{c}
cross each other near
x=0.5
and that
T_{s}
might drop below
T_{c}
. Perhaps nematic order is the origin of the knee. Alternatively, Peng Zhang
et al.
[
Zhang et al. 2018
], suggest the existence of surface superconductivity in FST. We speculate that this might lead to two different SC
T_{c}
s, one for the bulk and one for the surface. FST is also known to have multiple Fermi surfaces. It could be that the knee is a result of the different temperature dependence of the SC order parameters on different bands.

Finally, there is always the possibility that the knee is a result of the geometrical imperfection of the ring. Such imperfections are difficult to account for in numerical simulations.

VII.2
Critical Exponents

GL theory assumes, and BCS theory predicts, a linear temperature dependence of
\psi^{2}
. According to Eq.
2
this leads to the prediction that
n_{\rho}=1
. Our finding is not exactly as expected, but it is closer to unity than the results of
\mu
SR added to Fig.
4
(b). It should be pointed out that the
\mu
SR measurements are done in a fixed magnetic field, which becomes higher than
H_{c2}
as one approaches
T_{c}
. The discrepancy between techniques could also result from an interaction between the applied field and the underline ferromagnet, as mentioned before. Similarly, standard GL predicts
n_{\xi}=0.5
. In this case,
\xi
determined by the Stiffnessometer and
H_{c2}
are equally far from the expected value.

If we relax the linear assumption, the GL theory also predicts
n_{\rho}/n_{\xi}=2
. We find

n_{\rho}/n_{\xi}=2.22\pm 0.12\,.

(18)

The result obtained from the
\mu
SR and
H_{c2}
methods gives
n_{\rho}^{\mu\text{SR}}/n_{\xi}^{H_{c2}}=0.88\pm 0.08
, far from the GL expected value.

VII.3
First critical field

The first critical field,
H_{c1}
, is related to
\lambda
and
\xi

De Gennes and Pincus 2018
via

\mu_{0}H_{c1}=\frac{\Phi_{0}}{4\pi\lambda^{2}}\ln{\frac{\lambda}{\xi}}\,.

(19)

An attempt to test this equation fails severely regardless of the experimental method used to determine the different quantities. Bendele
et al.

Bendele et al. 2010
addressed this problem by considering the demagnetization factor
D
. They introduced the equation

B=\mu_{0}\left(m/V+H_{\text{int}}\right)\,,

(20)

where
H_{\text{int}}=H_{\text{ext}}-D\cdot m/V
,
H_{\text{int}}
and
H_{\text{ext}}
are the internal and externally applied field, respectively,
\mu_{0}H_{c1}\rightarrow B
in Eq.
19
. This calculation is very sensitive to the ring’s volume and
D
accuracy. In Sec.
III.2
we considered two options for
D
. If we adopt the disk option we get a much smaller
B
than measured. If we consider the ring option we find a negative
B
value. Sometimes additional constant is considered in Eq.
19
that includes the effect of the hard core of the vortex line
Bendele et al. 2010
;
Singh et al. 2018
;
Shang et al. 2019
, but in our case, this effect is negligible. Once again we speculate that the failure of Eq.
19
is a result of the underlining ferromagnetism in FST.

VIII
Conclusions

We developed a method, ideal for magnetic superconductors close to
T_{c}
, to measure both the penetration depth
\lambda
and coherence length
\xi
. For FST we find that
\lambda
and
\xi
are longer than previously reported and their temperature dependence agrees better with the GL predictions. A second transition, that looks like a knee, is observed at a temperature below
T_{c}
in the stiffness measurements. Further experiments are required to determine whether this transition is due to either nematic order, surface superconductivity, multiple Fermi surfaces, or a simple geometrical effect.
