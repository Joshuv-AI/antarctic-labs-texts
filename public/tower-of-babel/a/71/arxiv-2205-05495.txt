Calculating critical temperature and critical exponents by self-similar approximants


Abstract
Self-similar approximation theory allows for defining effective sums of asymptotic series. The method of self-similar factor approximants is applied for calculating the critical temperature and critical exponents of the O ⁡ ( N ) O(N) -symmetric φ 4 \varphi^{4} field theory in three dimensions by summing asymptotic ε \varepsilon expansions. This method is shown to be essentially simpler than other summation techniques involving complicated numerical calculations, while enjoying comparable accuracy.

1
Introduction

Asymptotic expansions are widely employed in physics and applied mathematics

[
1
,
2
]
. The expansion parameters are usually not small,
because of which one needs to involve some kind of effective summation of divergent
series. For this purpose, one often uses the method of Padé approximants

[
3
]
, Borel summation
[
4
]
and its variants, such as
Padé-Borel summation and Borel summation with conformal mapping. One also uses
the methods of renormalization group, conformal bootstrap, Monte Carlo simulations,
and other methods (see
[
5
]
) requiring quite heavy numerical calculations.

In this communication, we advocate another approach based on self-similar approximation
theory
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
]
. The idea of this theory is to
represent the transition from one approximation term to another as the motion of a
dynamical system, with the approximation order playing the role of discrete time. Then
the sequence of approximation terms is bijective to the dynamical-system trajectory and
the limit of the sequence is bijective to the fixed point of the trajectory. In the
vicinity of a fixed point, the evolution equation acquires the form of a self-similar
relation, which justifies the name of the self-similar approximation theory. Mathematical
details can be found in the review articles
[
10
,
11
]
. Here we only
give the main ideas of the general theory in Sec. 2. In Sec. 3, we formulate the method
of self-similar factor approximants. We do not plunge into the foundations of the theory,
but just give the scheme of the method application. We apply the method for calculating
the critical temperature shift for the
O(N)
-symmetric
\varphi^{4}
field theory in
three dimensions in Sec. 4 and the critical exponents of this model in Sec. 5. We show
that the method of self-similar factor approximants provides the accuracy comparable with
other elaborate techniques involving heavy numerical calculations, while being essentially
simpler. We compare our results with the most accurate values obtained by other methods
summarized in Refs.
[
5
,
12
,
13
,
14
]
. We compare our
results with Monte Carlo simulations, conformal bootstrap, hypergeometric Meijer summation,
Borel summation, Borel summation with conformal mapping, and the method of nonperturbative
renormalization group. This comparison shows good agreement of the self-similar factor
approximants with the results obtained by other methods. Sec. 6 concludes.

2
Self-similar approximation theory

Let the function of interest be presented as an asymptotic expansion over a parameter

x
assumed to be small,

f_{k}(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{k}a_{n}x^{n}\qquad(x\rightarrow 0)\;.

(1)

For concreteness, we keep in mind a real function of a real variable. Although the
expansion in powers of
x
requires that the latter be asymptotically small, in reality
this variable is finite, and sometimes even is rather large. It is the standard situation
that series (
1
) diverges, as
k
increases, for any finite value of
x
. Hence,
it is necessary to have in hands a method for ascribing to the divergent series an
effective sum. This can be done by resorting to self-similar approximation theory that
is based on the following ideas (see reviews
[
10
,
11
]
).

First, the given series has to be transformed by implanting in it control parameters,

\{f_{k}(x)\}\mapsto\{F_{k}(x,u_{k})\}\;.

(2)

These parameters are converted into control functions
u_{k}=u_{k}(x)
whose role is to
make convergent the series
\{F_{k}(x,u_{k}(x))\}
.

Then the transition between the terms
F_{k}
is reorganized so that to represent a motion
of a dynamical system, where the approximation order plays the role of discrete time,

F_{k}\mapsto F_{k+1}\mapsto\ldots\mapsto F^{*}\;.

(3)

The implantation of control functions can be done in different ways. For example, by
incorporating control parameters into the initial step of perturbation theory. Thus,
if the considered problem is characterized by a Hamiltonian
H
, one defines the
Hamiltonian

H_{\varepsilon}=H_{0}(u)+\varepsilon[\;H-H(u)\;]\qquad(\varepsilon\rightarrow 1)\;,

(4)

with a dummy parameter
\varepsilon
. Then perturbation theory is accomplished with
respect to this dummy parameter.

Another way of implanting control parameters is by a change of the variable, with a
control parameter entering this change, and the following re-expansion in powers of
a new variable. For instance, one can substitute a change of the variable
x=x(z,u)

into series (
1
) and then re-expand it in powers of
z
obtaining another series,

\sum_{n=0}^{k}a_{n}x^{n}~\mapsto~\sum_{n=0}^{k}b_{n}(u)z^{n}\;.

(5)

Also, it is possible to transform the given series so that the transformed series
would contain control parameters,

\hat{T}[\;u\;]\;f_{k}(x)=F_{k}(x,u_{k})\;.

(6)

The convertion of control parameters into control functions is based on the Cauchy
criterion of convergence, according to which the series converges if and only if for
any positive
\varepsilon
there exists
k^{*}=k^{*}(\varepsilon)
, such that

|\;F_{k+p}(x,u_{k+p})-F_{k}(x,u_{k})\;|<\varepsilon

(7)

for all
k>k^{*}
and
p>0
. The difference

C(F_{k+p},F_{k})\equiv F_{k+p}(x,u_{k+p})-F_{k}(x,u_{k})

(8)

is termed the Cauchy difference. The control functions are to be the minimizers of the
convergence cost functional:

\min_{u}\;\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k}C^{2}(F_{k+p},F_{k})~\mapsto~u_{k}(x)\;.

(9)

The sequence of terms
F_{k}(x,u_{k}(x)
is transformed into the trajectory of a dynamical
system in the following way. We impose the rheonomic constraint

F_{0}(x,u_{k}(x))=f

(10)

that defines the expansion function

x=x_{k}(f)\;.

(11)

Let us introduce the endomorphism

y_{k}(f):\;\mathbb{Z}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}~\mapsto~\mathbb{R}\;,

(12)

where

y_{k}(f)\equiv F_{k}(x_{k}(f),u_{k}(x_{k}(f)))\;.

(13)

The family of these endomorphsims forms a dynamical system in discrete time

\{y_{k}(f):\;\mathbb{Z}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}~\mapsto~\mathbb{R}\}\;,

(14)

with the initial condition

y_{0}(f)=f\;.

(15)

By construction, the dynamical system trajectory
\{y_{k}(f)\}
is bijective to the
sequence of approximants
\{F_{k}(x,u_{k}(x))\}
. If the limit of the approximation
sequence exists, it is bijective to the trajectory fixed point

y_{k}(y^{*}(f))=y^{*}(f)\;.

(16)

Since in practical applications we deal with finite approximation orders
k
, we
obtain the approximants

F^{*}_{k}(x)=F_{k}(x,u_{k}(x))\;.

(17)

In the vicinity of a fixed point, the self-similar relation

y_{k+p}(f)=y_{k}(y_{p}(f))

(18)

holds
[
10
,
11
]
. The family
\{y_{k}(f)\}
, with relation (
18
),
composes an approximation cascade. The approximation cascade can be embedded into an
approximation flow,

\{y_{k}(f):\;\mathbb{Z}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}~\mapsto~\mathbb{R}\}\subset\{y(t,f):\;\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}~\mapsto~\mathbb{R}\},

(19)

which implies that the flow, which is a dynamical system in continuous time, enjoys the
self-similar relation

y(t+t^{\prime},f)=y(t,y(t^{\prime},f))

(20)

and the continuous flow trajectory passes through all points of the cascade trajectory,

y(t,f)=y_{k}(f)\qquad(t=k)

(21)

starting at the same initial point

y(0,f)=f\;.

(22)

The self-simlar relation (
20
) can be represented as the Lie equation

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\;y(t,f)=v(y(t,f))

(23)

whose integration gives the evolution integral

\int_{F_{k}}^{F_{k}^{*}}\frac{dy}{v_{k}(y)}=t_{k}\;.

(24)

The entering here velocity can be defined by means of the Euler discretization

v_{k}(y)=y_{k+1}(f)-y_{k}(f)\;.

(25)

The upper limit of the evolution integral gives the self-similar approximant

f^{*}_{k}(x)=F^{*}_{k}(x,u_{k}(x))\;.

(26)

The procedure stability is characterized by calculating the local map multipliers

\mu_{k}(f)\equiv\frac{\partial y_{k}(f)}{\partial f}

(27)

showing that the procedure is stable provided that

\mu_{k}<1\;,\qquad\mu_{k}\equiv\sup_{f}|\;\mu_{k}(f)\;|\;.

(28)

Respectively, the found fixed point is stable, when the fixed-point multiplier

\mu_{k}^{*}(f)\equiv\frac{\partial y_{k}^{*}(f)}{\partial f}

(29)

satisfies the condition

\mu_{k}^{*}<1\;,\qquad\mu_{k}^{*}\equiv\sup_{f}|\;\mu_{k}^{*}(f)\;|\;.

(30)

Instead of local multipliers, one can consider the local Laypunov exponents

\lambda_{k}(f)=\frac{1}{k}\;\ln\;|\;\mu_{k}(f)\;|

(31)

in terms of which the stability condition reads as

\lambda_{k}<0\;,\qquad\lambda_{k}\equiv\sup_{f}\lambda_{k}(f)\;.

(32)

A particular example of transformation (
2
) is the fractal transformation

F_{k}(x,s)=x^{s}f_{k}(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{k}a_{n}x^{n+s}\;,

(33)

with the scaling exponent
s
playing the role of a control parameter, and satisfying
the scaling relation

\frac{F_{k}(bx,s)}{f_{k}(bx)}=\frac{F_{k}(x,s)}{f_{k}(x)}\;b^{s}\;.

(34)

The rheonomic contsraint (
10
) becomes

F_{0}(x,s)=x^{s}=f\;

(35)

giving the expansion function

x(f)=f^{1/s}\;.

(36)

Endomorphism (
13
) acquires the form

y_{k}(f)=\sum_{n=0}^{k}a_{n}f^{1+n/s}\;.

(37)

The cascade velocity (
25
) reduces to

v_{k}(f)=y_{k}(f)-y_{k-1}(f)=a_{k}f^{1+n/s}\;.

(38)

Finding the fixed point
y_{k}^{*}(f)
and accomplishing the inverse fractal
transformation yields the self-similar approximant

f_{k}^{*}(x)=x^{-s}y_{k}^{*}(x^{s})\;.

(39)

3
Self-similar factor approximants

Employing the ideas described above, a convenient type of approximants has been
derived, called self-similar factor approximants

[
10
,
11
,
15
,
16
]
. Let an asymptotic expansion
for a real function be given:

f_{k}(x)=f_{0}(x)\left(1+\sum_{n=1}^{k}a_{n}x^{n}\right)

(40)

where a real parameter
x
is assumed to be asymptotically small. However, we need
to find the value of the function at a finite value of the parameter or even for

x\rightarrow\infty
. In what follows, we assume that the sought function is sign-defined.
Without the loss of generality, it can be treated as positive (non-negative). This is
because a negative function
f
can always be replaced by a positive function
-f
.

The self-similar factor approximants, obtained from series (
40
), read as

f_{k}^{*}(x)=f_{0}(x)\prod_{j=1}^{N_{k}}(1+A_{j}x)^{n_{j}}\;,

(41)

where the number of factors is

\displaystyle N_{k}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}k/2,&~k=2,4,6,\ldots\\
(k+1)/2,&~k=3,5,7,\ldots\end{array}\right..

The parameters
A_{j}
and
n_{j}
are uniquely determined by the accuracy-through-order
procedure, by equating the like-order terms in the expansions at small
x
,

f_{k}^{*}(x)\simeq f_{k}(x)\qquad(x\rightarrow 0)\;.

(45)

This procedure gives the equations

\sum_{j=1}^{N_{k}}n_{j}A_{j}^{n}=D_{n}\qquad(n=1,2,\ldots,k)\;,

(46)

in which

D_{n}\equiv\frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}\;\lim_{x\rightarrow 0}\;\frac{d^{n}}{dx^{n}}\;\ln\left(1+\sum_{m=1}^{n}a_{m}x^{m}\right)\;.

(47)

For even orders
k
, Eq. (
46
) consists of
k
equations uniquely defining the

k/2
parameters
A_{j}
and
k/2
parameters
n_{j}
. For odd orders
k
, the system
of
k
equations (
46
) contains
k+1
unknowns, where one of the parameters
A_{j}
,
say
A_{1}
, is arbitrary. It is possible to use the normalization
A_{1}=1
, which makes
the system of equations (
46
) self-consistent and all parameters uniquely defined

[
10
,
17
]
. If the parameters lead to a complex-valued approximant,
it is replaced by the nearest real-valued approximant. The final result is given by
the average between the last two approximants
[f_{k}^{*}(x)+f_{k-1}^{*}(x)]/2
and the error
bar is defined as the half-difference between the last two different approximants

[f_{k}^{*}(x)-f_{k-1}^{*}(x)]/2
.

4
Critical temperature

The interaction strength of weakly interacting particles can be characterized by the
coupling parameter, also called gas parameter,

\gamma\equiv\rho^{1/3}a_{s}\;,

(48)

where
\rho
is average density and
a_{s}
, scattering length. Many-body systems are
often modeled by
O(N)
-symmetric
\varphi^{4}
field theory in three dimensions. When
diminishing temperature, these systems exhibit a phase transition with spontaneous
symmetry breaking.

An interesting problem that has attracted much attention is the dependence of the
phase transition temperature on the interaction strength
(see reviews
[
18
,
19
,
20
]
). One usually considers
the critical temperature shift

\frac{\Delta T}{T_{0}}\equiv\frac{T_{c}(\gamma)-T_{0}}{T_{0}}

(49)

describing the relative difference between the critical temperature
T_{c}(\gamma)
, as
a function of the gas parameter, and the critical temperature
T_{0}
of noninteracting
particles, when
\gamma=0
. At asymptotically weak interactions, the critical
temperature shift behaves as

\frac{\Delta T}{T_{0}}\simeq c_{1}\gamma\qquad(\gamma\rightarrow 0)\;.

(50)

The problem is to find the coefficient
c_{1}
.

This coeficient has been calculated
[
21
,
22
,
23
]
in the
loop expansion representing
c_{1}
as an asymptotic series

c_{1}=c_{1}(x)\simeq\sum_{n=1}^{5}a_{n}x^{n}\qquad(x\rightarrow 0)

(51)

in powers of the variable

x=(N+2)\;\frac{\lambda_{eff}}{\sqrt{\mu_{eff}}}\;,

(52)

where
\lambda_{eff}
is effective interaction strength and
\mu_{eff}
is effective
chemical potential. The difficulty arises at the phase transition temperature, where
the chemical potential
\mu_{eff}
tends to zero, hence the expansion variable
x

tends to infinity. Thus one comes to the necessity of giving to the value
c_{1}(\infty)

some meaning.

The problem can be solved by using self-similar factor approximants

[
20
,
24
]
. Then series (
51
) is transformed into the
expression

c_{1}^{*}(x)=a_{1}x\prod_{i=1}^{N_{k}}(1+A_{i}x)^{n_{i}}\;,

(53)

with the parameters
A_{i}
and
n_{i}
defined by the accuracy-through-order procedure.
At large
x
, expression (
53
) behaves as

c_{1}^{*}(x)\simeq B_{k}x^{\beta_{k}}\qquad(x\rightarrow\infty)\;,

(54)

with the amplitude and exponent

B_{k}=a_{1}\prod_{i=1}^{N_{k}}A_{i}^{n_{i}}\;,\qquad\beta_{k}=1+\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}n_{i}\;.

(55)

The required finiteness of
c_{1}
implies that
\beta_{k}
equals zero, which yields

c_{1}^{*}(\infty)=B_{k}\qquad(\beta_{k}=0)\;.

(56)

This procedure can be complemented by a correction accelerating convergence

[
20
,
24
]
.

The described scheme has been applied to expansion (
51
) for the number of
components
N=0,1,2,3,4
. The coefficients
a_{n}
of the loop expansion (
51
) can
be found in
[
20
,
23
]
. The results for
c_{1}
are presented in
Table 1 and compared with the available Monte Carlo simulations for
N=1

[
25
]
,
for
N=2

[
26
,
27
]
, and for
N=4

[
25
]
.

6
Conclusion

The method of self-similar factor approximants is a very simple and convenient tool
for the summation of asymptotic series. The basis of the method is the consideration
of the transfer from one approximation term to another as a motion of a dynamical
system, with the approximation order playing the role of time. The motion in the
vicinity of a fixed point is described by a self-similar relation. The fixed point
of the evolution equation defines the effective limit of the sequence. The method
can be applied to a large class of problems, as can be inferred from the review
articles
[
10
,
11
]
and the recent papers
[
49
,
50
]
.

The method of self-similar factor approximants is used for the summation of

\varepsilon
expansions for the
O(N)
- symmetric field theory in three dimensions.
The series of seventh order in
\varepsilon
are employed. The method is shown to
produce accurate approximations, at the same time being very simple. The results are
compatible with other known methods of summation.
