Operations on Fixpoint Equation Systems
Thomas Neele ORCID:0000-0001-6117-9129, Jaco van de Pol ORCID:0000-0003-4305-0625

Abstract
. We study operations on fixpoint equation systems (FES) over arbitrary complete lattices. We investigate under which conditions these operations, such as substituting variables by their definition, and swapping the ordering of equations, preserve the solution of a FES. We provide rigorous, computer-checked proofs. Along the way, we list a number of known and new identities and inequalities on extremal fixpoints in complete lattices.

1.
Introduction

This paper deals with operations on systems of fixpoint equations over
an arbitrary complete lattice. We investigate when these operations
preserve the solution of the equations. An example of a system of
equations is the set
\mathcal{E}:=\{X=f(X,Y,Z),\ Y=g(X,Y,Z),\ Z=h(X,Y,Z)\}
. For
most results, it is required that the functions
f,g,h
are monotonic
in the given lattice. Such systems may well have multiple
solutions. In order to specify particular solutions, we introduce
specifications, for example
\mathcal{S}:=[\mu X,\nu Y,\mu Z]
, indicating for
each variable whether we are interested in the minimal (
\mu
) or
maximal (
\nu
) solution. The order of the variables in these
specifications is relevant: the leftmost variable indicates the fixpoint
with the highest priority. A
Fixpoint Equation System
(FES)
[
Mad97
]
is
a pair
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S})
, where
\mathcal{E}
is a set of equations, and
\mathcal{S}
is a
specification. Several well known instances are obtained by
instantiating the complete lattice.

Well-known instances of FES

Boolean Equation Systems
(BES) arise as FES over the complete lattice

\bot<\top
, and were proposed
in
[
And94
,
AV95
]
for solving the model checking and
equivalence checking problems on finite labeled transition systems
(LTS).
BES received extensive study in
[
Mad97
,
MS03
,
GK04
,
Mat06
]
.
A generalisation to the domain
\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty,\infty\}
is

real equation systems
(RES)
[
GW23
]
.

An equivalent notion to BES is two-player
parity games

[
EJ91
]
,
see
[
Mad97
]
for a proof.
Algorithms for solving parity games receive a lot of attention, since this
is one of the few problems which is in NP and in co-NP, but not known to
be in P.
Recently, it has been shown that parity games (and thus BES) can be
solved in quasi-polynomial time
[
CJK+17
]
.
This result has also been lifted to the general setting of FES on finite
lattices
[
HS21
,
JMT22
]
.
Other types of games can also be seen as an instance of FES, for example
energy parity games
[
CD12
]
are FES on the lattice

\mathbb{Z}\to\{\bot,\top\}
, ordered pointwise.
A modern parity game solver is Oink
[
vD18
]
.

Parameterised Boolean Equation Systems
(PBES, also known as
first-order, or predicate BES) arise as FES over the powerset lattice

(2^{D},\subseteq)
, with
D
some data type, typically
representing the state space of a possibly infinite LTS.
In
[
Mat98
,
GM99
]
, PBES are proposed to encode the
model-checking problem of first-order mu-calculus on infinite LTSs;
they are studied in more detail in
[
GW05b
,
GW05a
]
. An
encoding of (branching) bisimulation of infinite LTSs in PBES is
proposed in
[
CPvdPW07
]
. Various procedures that operate on PBES
have been studied, for example to simplify
[
Nee22
,
OW10
]

or solve PBES
[
NWG20
,
NWWV22
,
PWW11
]
.
Algorithms for solving some timed fragments of PBES automatically are
studied in
[
ZC05
]
. PBES are implemented in the mCRL2
[
BGK+19
]

and CADP
[
GLMS13
]
model checking toolsets.
MuArith
[
KFG20
]
is similar to PBES, but the domain
D

is restricted to integers.

Fixpoint Equation Systems over arbitrary complete lattices (FES) are
defined in
[
Mad97
,
TC02
]
.
Some works refer to the same concept as

Hierarchical Equation Systems
(HES)
[
Sei96
,
KNIU19
]
,

Systems of Fixpoint Equations

[
BKP20
]
or

Nested Fixpoint Equations

[
JMT22
]
.
In
[
ZC05
]
it is recognized
that BES and PBES (and also
Modal Equation Systems

[
CS93
]
, an equational representation of the modal mu-calculus) are instances of
FES. FESs are mainly useful to provide generic definitions for all
these kinds of equation systems. We claim that the generic semantics of
a FES is more elegant than the semantics of PBES, as given in
e.g.
[
GW05a
]
. In particular, equations in FES are defined
in a semantic manner as functions on valuations, rather than on
syntactic expressions (possibly with binders).
Another advantage of FES is that one can derive
a number of basic theorems for equation systems over all lattices
in one stride, like in Chapter 3 of Mader’s thesis
[
Mad97
]
.

Abstract dependency graphs

[
EGLS19
]
are
similar to FES, but variables range over a Noetherian partial order
with a least element, instead of a complete lattice.
When assuming every right-hand side is effectively computable, minimal
fixpoints can be computed in an iterative fashion.
Dependency graphs do not contain fixpoint alternations.

Contributions

Our main goal is to study basic operations on FES, related with
substituting variables in the equations by their definition or final
solution, or swapping the order of equations in the specification.
Substitution operations form the basis of solving BES by so-called
Gauss-elimination
[
Mad97
]
. Also for PBES,
Gauss elimination plays a crucial role in their solution.
Reordering the variables in the
specification is useful, because it may give rise to independent
subspecifications that can be solved separately. Also, swapping the
order of variables may bring down the number of alternations between

\mu
and
\nu
, thus lowering the complexity of certain solution
algorithms.

Our results consist of equalities and inequalities between FES, expressing
under which conditions the basic operations preserve the solution of a FES.
The main results are summarized in Table
1
(Section
7
).
In particular:

(1)

Results on substitution for BES and PBES are generalized to FES.

(2)

Results on swapping variables are generalized and sorted out, by
weakening existing conditions, and by providing alternative conditions.

(3)

We provide rigorous proofs of our results. All
proofs in this paper have been proof-checked mechanically by the
Coq theorem prover
[
Ber08
,
S+23
]
(version 8.17)
as well as the PVS theorem prover
[
OS08
]
(version 7.1).
Our proofs are available online
[
NvdP24
]
.

2.
Fixpoint Laws in Complete Lattices

A
partial order
on a universe
U
is a binary relation

{\leq}\subseteq U\times U
, which is
reflexive
(
\forall x.\,x\leq x
),
anti-symmetric
(
\forall x,y.\,x\leq y\wedge y\leq x\Rightarrow x=y
) and
transitive
(
\forall x,y,z.\,x\leq y\wedge y\leq z\Rightarrow x\leq z
), where in all cases
x,y,z\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}U
.

Given partial orders
(U,\leq)
and
(V,\leq)
, we define partial
orders
(U\times V,\leq)
and
(U\to V,\leq)
pointwise:

(u_{1},v_{1})\leq(u_{2},v_{2})
iff
u_{1}\leq u_{2}\wedge v_{1}\leq v_{2}
, and

f\leq g
iff
\forall x\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}U.f(x)\leq g(x)
. Function
f:U\to V
is
called
monotonic
, iff
\forall x,y.\,x\leq y\Rightarrow f(x)\leq f(y)
.

Given a set
X\subseteq U
, we define its set of
lower bounds
in
U
as

lb(X):=\{y\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}U~|~\forall x\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}X.\,y\leq x\}
. If
y\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}lb(X)
and

z\leq y
for all
z\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}lb(X)
, then
y
is called the
greatest lower
bound
of
X
. A
complete lattice
is a triple
(U,\leq,glb)
, where

\leq
is a partial order, and
glb(X)
returns the greatest lower bound of

X
in
U
, for all (finite or infinite)
X\subseteq U
.

Given a complete lattice
(U,\leq,glb)
, define the partial order
(U,\geq)
,
by
x\geq y
iff
y\leq x
. We define the set of
upper bounds
of

X\subseteq U
by
ub(X):=\{y\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}U~|~\forall x\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}X.\,y\geq x\}
.
Define
lub(X):=glb(ub(X))
. Clearly, for all
y\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}ub(X)
,
lub(X)\leq y
.
But also
lub(X)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}ub(X)
, for if
x\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}X
, then
x\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}lb(ub(X)
, hence

x\leq glb(ub(X)
. So
lub(X)
yields the
least upper bound
of
X
, and

(U,\geq,lub)
is a complete lattice as well.

Given a complete lattice
(U,\leq,glb)
, we define the
least
fixpoint
(
\mu
) and
greatest fixpoint
(
\nu
)
of any function
f:U\to U
(not only for monotonic) as follows:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mu(f)&:=&glb(\{x\,|\,f(x)\leq x\})\\
\nu(f)&:=&lub(\{x\,|\,x\leq f(x)\})\\
\end{array}

For
\sigma\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}\{\mu,\nu\}
, we abbreviate
\sigma(\lambda x.f(x))
by
\sigma x.f(x)
. Note that by definition,
\nu
in
(U,\leq,glb)
equals
\mu
in

(U,\geq,lub)
, so theorems on
(\mu,\leq)
hold for
(\nu,\geq)
as well
“by duality”. Also note that
F:U\to U
is monotonic in
(U,\leq)
if and
only if it is monotonic in
(U,\geq)
. A direct consequence of the
definition of
\mu
is the following principle (and its dual):

f(x)\leq x~~\Rightarrow~~\mu(f)\leq x
(
\mu
-fixpoint
induction
)

x\leq f(x)~~\Rightarrow~~x\leq\nu(f)
(
\nu
-fixpoint
induction
)

We now have the following identities on fixpoint expressions:

Lemma 1
.

Let
(U,\leq,glb)
be a complete lattice.
Let
\sigma\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}\{\mu,\nu\}
,
A\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}U
, and let
F,G\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}U\to U
and

H,K\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}U\times U\to U
be monotonic functions. Then:

(1)

F(\sigma(F))=\sigma(F)
(computation rule)

(2)

\sigma x.\,A=A
(constant rule)

(3)

\sigma x.\,F(G(x))=F(\sigma x.\,G(F(x)))
(rolling rule)

(4)

\sigma x.\,F(F(x))=\sigma x.\,F(x)
(square rule)

(5)

\sigma
is monotonic (fixpoint monotonicity)

(6)

\sigma x.\,H(x,x)=\sigma x.\,\sigma y.\,H(x,y)
(diagonal rule)

(7)

\sigma x.\,H(x,x)=\sigma x.\,H(x,H(x,x))
(unfolding rule)

(8)

\sigma x.\,H(x,x)=\sigma x.\,H(x,\sigma x.\,H(x,x))
(solve rule)

(9)

\sigma x.\,H(x,\sigma y.\,K(y,x))=\sigma x.\,H(x,\sigma y.\,K(y,\sigma z.\,H(z,y)))
(Bekič rule)

Proof 2.1
.

We first prove the theorem for
\sigma=\mu
. By the observations above,
the theorem then follows for
\sigma=\nu
as well (“by duality”).

(1)

(a)

Let
y
with
F(y)\leq y
be given. Then by fixpoint induction,

\mu(F)\leq y
. By monotonicity,
F(\mu(F))\leq F(y)\leq y
.
Since
y
is arbitrary,

F(\mu(F))
is a lower bound of
\{x\,|\,F(x)\leq x\}
.
Hence
F(\mu(F))\leq glb(\{x\,|\,F(x)\leq x\})=\mu(F)

(b)

F(\mu(F))\leq\mu(F)
by (a), so by monotonicity,

F(F(\mu(F)))\leq F(\mu(F))
. By fixpoint induction,

\mu(F)\leq F(\mu(F))
.

Then by anti-symmetry
F(\mu(F))=\mu(F)
.

(2)

Follows directly from (1) by taking
F:=\lambda x.A

(which is monotonic)

(3)

Obviously,
\lambda x.\,F(G(x))
and
\lambda x.\,G(F(x))
are monotonic.

(a)

By (1),
F(G(F(\mu x.\,G(F(x)))))=F(\mu x.\,G(F(x)))
.
Hence by fixpoint induction,
\mu x.\,F(G(x))\leq F(\mu x.\,G(F(x)))
.

(b)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{\Rightarrow}}G(F(G(\mu x.\,F(G(x)))))\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(1)}}{{=}}}}G(\mu x.\,F(G(x)))

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\mbox{~~(by fixpoint induction)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{\Rightarrow}}\mu x.\,G(F(x))\leq G(\mu x.\,F(G(x)))

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\mbox{~~(by monotonicity)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{\Rightarrow}}F(\mu x.\,G(F(x)))\leq F(G(\mu x.\,F(G(x))))\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(1)}}{{=}}}}\mu x.\,F(G(x))

By anti-symmetry, we obtain
\mu x.\,F(G(x))=F(\mu x.\,G(F(x)))
.

(4)

(a)

Using (1) twice,
F(F(\mu x.\,F(x)))=F(\mu x.\,F(x))=\mu x.\,F(x)
.
So by fixpoint induction,
\mu x.\,F(F(x))\leq\mu x.\,F(x)
.

(b)

By (3), we get
F(\mu x.\,F(F(x)))=\mu x.\,F(F(x))
.
Hence by fixpoint induction,
\mu x.\,F(x)\leq\mu x.\,F(F(x))
.

Then by anti-symmetry,
\mu x.\,F(F(x))=\mu x.\,F(x)
.

(5)

Assume
f\leq g
. Let
y
with
g(y)\leq y
be given.
Then
f(y)\leq g(y)\leq y
, so
\mu(f)\leq y
by fixpoint induction.
Since
y
is arbitrary,
\mu(f)
is a lower bound for
\{x\,|\,g(x)\leq x\}
.
By definition
\mu(g)
is its greatest lower bound, so
\mu(f)\leq\mu(g)
.

(6)

(a)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{\Rightarrow}}H(\mu x.\,H(x,x),\mu x.\,H(x,x))\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(1)}}{{=}}}}\mu x.\,H(x,x)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\mbox{~~(by fixpoint induction, applied with $F:=\lambda x.\,H(x,x)$)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{\Rightarrow}}\mu y.\,H(\mu x.\,H(x,x),y)\leq\mu x.\,H(x,x)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\mbox{~~(by fixpoint induction)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{\Rightarrow}}\mu x.\,\mu y.\,H(x,y)\leq\mu x.\,H(x,x)

(b)

Let us abbreviate
A:=\mu x.\,\mu y.\,H(x,y)
. Using (5) one
can show that
\lambda x.\,\mu y.\,H(x,y)
is monotonic.
Then:

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{\Rightarrow}}A\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(1)}}{{=}}}}\mu y.\,H(A,y)\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(1)}}{{=}}}}H(A,\mu y.\,H(A,y))

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\mbox{~~(by congruence and both equations above)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{\Rightarrow}}H(A,A)=H(A,\mu y.\,H(A,y))=A

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\mbox{~~(by fixpoint induction)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{\Rightarrow}}\mu x.\,H(x,x)\leq A=\mu x.\,\mu y.\,H(x,y)

By anti-symmetry, we indeed get:
\mu x.\,H(x,x)=\mu x.\,\mu y.\,H(x,y)
.

(7)

Using (4) on
\lambda y.\,H(x,y)
yields

\mu x.\,\mu y.\,H(x,y)=\mu x.\,\mu y.\,H(x,H(x,y))
.
Applying (6) to both sides yields

\mu x.\,H(x,x)=\mu x.\,H(x,H(x,x))
.

(8)

We use (6) twice on the function
\lambda(y,x).H(x,y)
:

\displaystyle\phantom{{}\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(1)}}{{=}}}}{}}\mu x.\,H(x,x)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(6)}}{{=}}}}\mu y.\,\mu x.\,H(x,y)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(1)}}{{=}}}}\mu x.\,H(x,\mu y.\,\mu x.\,H(x,y))

\displaystyle\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(6)}}{{=}}}}\mu x.\,H(x,\mu x.\,H(x,x))

(9)

Define
F(y):=\mu x.\,H(x,y)
and
G(y):=\mu x.\,K(x,y)
. Then:

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{\Rightarrow}}\mu y.\,F(G(y))\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(3)}}{{=}}}}F(\mu y.\,G(F(y)))

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\mbox{~~~~~(by definition of $F$, $G$)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{\Rightarrow}}\mu y.\,\mu x.\,H(x,G(y))=F(\mu y.\,\mu x.\,K(x,F(y)))

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\mbox{~~~~~(by 6, applied to left- and right-hand side)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{\Rightarrow}}\mu x.\,H(x,G(x))=F(\mu y.\,K(y,F(y)))

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\mbox{~~~~~(by definition of $F$, $G$)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{\Rightarrow}}\mu x.\,H(x,\mu y.\,K(y,x))=\mu x.\,H(x,\mu y.\,K(y,\mu z.\,H(z,y)))

A careful analysis shows that all these identities can be derived in
an equational style from the identities 1, 3, 4 and 6. A natural
question is whether all true equalities (with
\mu
as
second order operation, and variables ranging over monotonic
functions) can be derived from these four identities in an equational
manner (thus excluding the fixpoint induction rule). We don’t know
the answer, but we expect that at least the equations

\mu x.\,F(x)=\mu x.\,F^{p}(x)
are needed for all primes
p
.
Results from universal algebra don’t apply directly,
due to the second order nature of the fixpoint operator.

By mixing least and greatest fixpoints, we also
obtain a number of inequalities. In particular, 4 is new,
as far as we know. Note the similarity of (4) with Bekič Rule,
Lemma
1
. We will call (4) Bekič Inequality.

Lemma 2
.

Let
(U,\leq,glb)
be a complete lattice.
Let
\sigma\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}\{\mu,\nu\}
,
A\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}U
, and let
F,G\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}U\to U
and

H,K\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}U\times U\to U
be monotonic functions. Then:

(1)

\mu(F)\leq\nu(F)

(2)

(a)

\mu x.x\leq A

(b)

A\leq\nu x.x

(3)

\mu x.\,\nu y.\,H(x,y)\leq\nu y.\,\mu x.\,H(x,y)

(4)

(a)

\mu x.\,H(x,\nu y.\,K(y,x))\leq\mu x.\,H(x,\nu y.\,K(y,\mu x.\,H(x,y)))

(b)

\nu x.\,H(x,\mu y.\,K(y,x))\geq\nu x.\,H(x,\mu y.\,K(y,\nu x.\,H(x,y)))

Proof 2.2
.

(1)

F(\nu(F))\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{fixcalculus}.1)}}{{=}}}}\nu(F)
, so by fixpoint
induction,
\mu(F)\leq\nu(F)
.

(2)

(a)
A\leq A
, hence by fixpoint induction,
\mu x.x\leq A
.
Then (b) follows by duality.

(3)

Define
F(x):=\nu y.\,H(x,y)
and
G(y):=\mu x.\,H(x,y)
.
Note that both
F
and
G
are monotonic, using Lemma
1
.5.
Then:

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{\Rightarrow}}\mu(F)\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{fixcalculus}.1)}}{{=}}}}F(\mu(F))\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{fixcalculus}.1)}}{{=}}}}H(\mu(F),F(\mu(F)))\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{fixcalculus}.1)}}{{=}}}}H(\mu(F),\mu(F))

\displaystyle\Rightarrow~~~~~\mbox{(by fixpoint induction)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{\Rightarrow}}G(\mu(F))=\mu x.\,H(x,\mu(F))\leq\mu(F)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow~~~~~\mbox{(monotonicity $F$)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{\Rightarrow}}F(G(\mu(F)))\leq F(\mu(F))\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{fixcalculus}.1)}}{{=}}}}\mu(F)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow~~~~~\mbox{(monotonicity $H$)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{\Rightarrow}}F(G(\mu(F))\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{fixcalculus}.1)}}{{=}}}}H(G(\mu(F)),F(G(\mu(F))))\leq H(G(\mu(F)),\mu(F))\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{fixcalculus}.1)}}{{=}}}}G(\mu(F))

\displaystyle\Rightarrow~~~~~\mbox{(by fixpoint induction)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{\Rightarrow}}\mu(F)\leq G(\mu(F))

\displaystyle\Rightarrow~~~~~\mbox{(by fixpoint induction for $\nu$)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{\Rightarrow}}\mu(F)\leq\nu(G)

(4)

(a) Define
F(y):=\mu x.\,H(x,y)
and
G(x):=\nu y.\,K(y,x)
.
Note that both
F
and
G
are monotonic, using Lemma
1
.5.
Then:

\displaystyle\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\phantom{(\ref{fixcalculus}.6)}}}{{\phantom{=}}}}}\mu x.\,H(x,\nu y.\,K(y,x))

\displaystyle\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{fixcalculus}.6)}}{{=}}}}\mu x.\,\mu z.\,H(z,\nu y.\,K(y,x))

\displaystyle\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\phantom{(\ref{fixcalculus}.6)}}}{{=}}}}\mu x.\,F(G(x))

\displaystyle\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{fixcalculus}.3)}}{{=}}}}F(\mu x.\,G(F(x)))

\displaystyle\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\phantom{(\ref{fixcalculus}.6)}}}{{\leq}}}}\mbox{~~~~~(using 1, and monotonicity of $F$)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\phantom{(\ref{fixcalculus}.6)}}}{{\phantom{=}}}}}F(\nu x.\,G(F(x)))

\displaystyle\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\phantom{(\ref{fixcalculus}.6)}}}{{=}}}}F(\nu x.\,\nu y.\,K(y,F(x)))

\displaystyle\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{fixcalculus}.6)}}{{=}}}}F(\nu y.\,K(y,F(y)))

\displaystyle\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\phantom{(\ref{fixcalculus}.6)}}}{{=}}}}\mu x.\,H(x,\nu y.\,K(y,\mu x.\,H(x,y)))

Then (b) follows by “duality” (reversing
\mu/\nu
and
{\leq}/{\geq}
).
More precisely, (b) is (a) in the reversed complete lattice
(U,\geq,lub)
.

Note that (1) and (3) are their own dual.

3.
Fixpoint Equation Systems

In this section we first formally define Fixpoint Equation Systems (FES).
We show by examples how they generalize Boolean and Predicate Equation
Systems. Subsection
3.2
introduces the semantics
of a FES by defining its solutions.
Finally, Subsection
3.3
defines the variable dependency graph
in a FES.

3.1.
Definition of Fixpoint Equation Systems

Fix a complete lattice
(U,\leq,glb)
, and a set of variables
\mathcal{X}
.
Throughout the paper, we assume that equality on variables is decidable.
We define
the set of
valuations

\mathcal{V}\!\mathit{al}:=\mathcal{X}\to U
. For
X\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}\mathcal{X}
,
\eta\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}\mathcal{V}\!\mathit{al}
,

P\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}U
, we denote by
\eta[X:=P]
the valuation that returns
P
on
X
and

\eta(Y)
on
Y\neq X
. As any function, valuations can be ordered pointwise,
i.e.
\eta_{1}\leq\eta_{2}
iff
\forall X\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}\mathcal{X}.\,\eta_{1}(X)\leq\eta_{2}(X)
. Note
that valuation update is monotonic, that is, if
P\leq Q
, then

\eta[X:=P]\leq\eta[X:=Q]
.
To indicate that two valuations agree on a set of variables
V\subseteq\mathcal{X}
,
we write
\eta_{1}\mathrel{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{$=$}\hfil\cr\hfil\raise 3.44444pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle V}\mkern 0.5mu$}\hfil\cr}}}\eta_{2}
, formally defined as

\forall X\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}V.\eta_{1}(X)=\eta_{2}(X)
.
The complement of
V
in
\mathcal{X}
is denoted
\overline{V}
.

A set of
mutually recursive equations
is a member of
\mathcal{E}\!\mathit{qs}:=\mathcal{V}\!\mathit{al}\to\mathcal{V}\!\mathit{al}
. The set
\mathcal{E}\!\mathit{qs}
is also ordered pointwise.

\mathcal{E}
is
monotonic
iff it is a monotonic function on
\mathcal{V}\!\mathit{al}
.
Note that this semantic view on equations escapes the need to introduce
(and be limited) to a particular syntax.

{exa}

Take
\mathcal{X}=\{X,Y,Z\}
and
U=\mathbb{B}
, the Boolean lattice
\bot<\top
.
We write
(a,b,c)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}\mathbb{B}^{3}
as a shorthand for the valuation

\{X{=}a,Y{=}b,Z{=}c\}
.
The system of equations
\{X=Y\wedge Z,\ Y=X\vee Z,\ Z=\neg X\}

is represented in our theory as the function

\mathcal{B}\ :=\ \lambda(X,Y,Z)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}\mathbb{B}^{3}.\,(Y\wedge Z,\ X\vee Z,\ \neg X)\kern 5.0pt.

It is not monotonic, because as valuations,

(\bot,\bot,\bot)\leq(\top,\top,\top)
, but

\mathcal{B}(\bot,\bot,\bot)=(\bot,\bot,\top)\not\leq(\top,\top,\bot)=\mathcal{B}(\top,\top,\top)\kern 5.0pt.

Note that
\mathcal{E}\!\mathit{qs}
is isomorphic with
\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{V}\!\mathit{al}\to U
. This motivates
the following slight abuse of notation: Given
\mathcal{E}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}\mathcal{E}\!\mathit{qs}
, we will often write

\mathcal{E}_{X}(\eta)
for
\mathcal{E}(\eta)(X)
. This expression denotes the definition of
X

in
\mathcal{E}
, possibly depending on other variables as represented by the
valuation
\eta
.
Similar to valuations, agreement on variables from
V\subseteq\mathcal{X}
is denoted

\mathcal{E}_{1}\mathrel{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{$=$}\hfil\cr\hfil\raise 3.44444pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle V}\mkern 0.5mu$}\hfil\cr}}}\mathcal{E}_{2}
, defined as

\forall\eta\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}\mathcal{V}\!\mathit{al},X\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}V.\mathcal{E}_{1}(\eta)(X)=\mathcal{E}_{2}(\eta)(X)
.

The set of
specifications
consists of finite lists of signed variables:

\mathcal{S}\!\mathit{pec}:=(\{\mu,\nu\}\times\mathcal{X})^{*}
. Note that a specification selects a
subset of variables to be considered, assigns a fixpoint sign to these
variables, and assigns an order to these variables. We use
\sigma X
as a
notation for
(\sigma,X)
, write
\varepsilon
for the empty list, and use

\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}
for list concatenation. We will identify a singleton list with its
element. For instance,
[\mu X,\nu Y]\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mu Z
denotes the specification

[(\mu,X),(\nu,Y),(\mu,Z)]
. We define

\mathit{dom}(\mathcal{S})\subseteq\mathcal{X}
as the set of variables that occur in some pair in
\mathcal{S}
.
Decidability of
X\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}\mathit{dom}(\mathcal{S})
follows from finiteness of
\mathcal{S}
and
decidability of equality on variables.
We define
\mathit{disjoint}(\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{2})
iff
\mathit{dom}(\mathcal{S}_{1})\cap\mathit{dom}(\mathcal{S}_{2})=\emptyset
.

We often require that valuations or equation systems agree on the variables
in a specification.
Accordingly, we overload
\mathrel{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{$=$}\hfil\cr\hfil\raise 3.44444pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\cdot}\mkern 0.5mu$}\hfil\cr}}}
so that

\eta_{1}\mathrel{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{$=$}\hfil\cr\hfil\raise 3.44444pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\mathcal{S}}\mkern 0.5mu$}\hfil\cr}}}\eta_{2}
(
resp.

\mathcal{E}_{1}\mathrel{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{$=$}\hfil\cr\hfil\raise 3.44444pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\mathcal{S}}\mkern 0.5mu$}\hfil\cr}}}\mathcal{E}_{2}
)
is defined as
\eta_{1}\mathrel{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{$=$}\hfil\cr\hfil\raise 3.44444pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\mathcal{S}}\mkern 0.5mu$}\hfil\cr}}}\eta_{2}
(
resp.

\mathcal{E}_{1}\mathrel{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{$=$}\hfil\cr\hfil\raise 3.44444pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\mathcal{S}}\mkern 0.5mu$}\hfil\cr}}}\mathcal{E}_{2}
).
This also applies when a complement is involved:
\eta_{1}\mathrel{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{$=$}\hfil\cr\hfil\raise 3.44444pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\overline{\mathcal{S}}}\mkern 0.5mu$}\hfil\cr}}}\eta_{2}
is
\eta_{1}\mathrel{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{$=$}\hfil\cr\hfil\raise 3.44444pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\overline{\mathit{dom}(\mathcal{S})}}\mkern 0.5mu$}\hfil\cr}}}\eta_{2}
.

Finally, a
fixpoint equation system
(FES)

\mathcal{F}
on
(U,\mathcal{X})
is simply a pair in
\mathcal{F}\!\mathit{es}:=\mathcal{E}\!\mathit{qs}\times\mathcal{S}\!\mathit{pec}
.
Before we present the semantics of FES, we first consider several instances of FES.

{exa}

The Boolean Equation System
[
Mad97
]
traditionally written as

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mu X&=&Y\wedge Z\\
\nu Y&=&X\vee Z\\
\nu Z&=&\neg X\end{array}

is represented in our theory as the pair
(\mathcal{B},[\mu X,\nu Y,\nu Z])
,
where
\mathcal{B}
is from Example
3.1
.
Note that this notation for BES integrates the set of equations and the specification into one, and these cannot be considered separately.

{exa}

A PBES (parameterized BES
[
GW05a
]
, or predicate equation system

[
ZC05
]
) is a FES over the complete lattice

U:=(\mathcal{P}(D),\subseteq)
for some data set
D
, or equivalently
(D\to\mathbb{B},\leq)
.
PBES thus generalise BES: each variable is now a predicate over domain
D
, allowing one to create complex expressions over data.
For our example, let
\mathcal{X}=\{X,Y\}
and
D=\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{B}
.
Again using the shorthand
(X,Y)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}(D\to\mathbb{B})^{2}
for valuations,
(\mathcal{B}^{\prime},[\mu X,\nu Y])
is a PBES, where

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathcal{B}^{\prime}&:=&\lambda(X,Y)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}(D\to\mathbb{B})^{2}.\\
&&\quad(\lambda m\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}\mathbb{N},b\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}\mathbb{B}.\ (b\rightarrow m>0\wedge Y(m,\bot))\wedge(\neg b\rightarrow m<5\wedge Y(m,\bot)),\\
&&\quad\phantom{(}\lambda m\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}\mathbb{N},b\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}\mathbb{B}.\ X(m-1,m>4)\vee Y(m+1,\bot))\end{array}

The function on the last line, which defines
Y
, does not contain an occurrence of the argument
b
.
However, in our theory we are required to include it so that both variables in
\mathcal{X}:=\{X,Y\}
are predicates over the same
D:=\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{B}
.
In the notation of
[
GW05a
]
, the same PBES is simply written as a pair of predicate definitions with accompanying fixpoint signs.
The argument
b
of
Y
may be left out:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mu X(m\colon\mathbb{N},b\colon\mathbb{B})&=&(b\rightarrow m>0\wedge Y(m))\wedge(\neg b\rightarrow m<5\wedge Y(m))\\
\nu Y(m\colon\mathbb{N})&=&X(m-1,m>4)\vee Y(m+1)\end{array}

Similar to BES, the PBES formalism as defined in
[
GW05a
]
does not consider the equations and the specification separately.

Remark 3
.

Our choice of separating the set of equations and the specification
makes it easier to perform induction proofs over the specification
(because one retains knowledge of all equations in the proof scope).
However, we have not required that all variables in
\mathcal{S}
are unique. This is
not needed in our formalization, because in Lemma
5
.3 we will
show that if
X\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}\mathit{dom}(\mathcal{S})
, then any set of equations
\mathcal{E}
has the same
semantics when combined with
S
or with
\sigma X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}S
, for any
\sigma\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}\{\mu,\nu\}
.
Do note that there is a hidden
assumption: even if
X
occurs multiple times in
\mathcal{S}
, possibly with
different signs, there can only be one defining equation for it,
because
\mathcal{E}
is a function. So when transferring the results to
traditional notation, one should add the (quite natural) requirement
that all equations have unique variable names.

{exa}

Modal equation systems
(MES)
[
CS93
]
is a very similar FES instance to PBES since it also uses the powerset lattice
(\mathcal{P}(D),\subseteq)
.
However, a MES is interpreted on a
labelled transition system
(LTS), so that
D
is equal to the set of states in the LTS.
MES includes, for every action
a
in the LTS, the modal operators
[a]\varphi
(“
\varphi
must hold after every possible transition labelled with
a
”) and
\langle a\rangle\varphi
(“there exists an
a
-transition after which
\varphi
holds”).
MES is an equational representation of the modal mu-calculus.
Adopting the notation from the previous examples, an example of a MES is:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\nu X&=&Y\\
\mu Y&=&[a]X\land[b]Y\end{array}

This MES expresses that on every path in the LTS, action
a
occurs infinitely often (and
b
may happen only finitely often in between).

3.2.
Semantics of FES and Basic Results

Next, the semantics of a FES,
\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket:\mathcal{V}\!\mathit{al}\to\mathcal{V}\!\mathit{al}
, is
defined recursively on
\mathcal{S}
:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\llbracket\mathcal{E},\varepsilon\rrbracket(\eta)&:=&\eta\\
\llbracket\mathcal{E},\sigma X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta)&:=&\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=\sigma(F)]),\\
&&\mbox{~~where }F\colon U\to U\mbox{~is defined as~}\\
&&\;F(P):=\mathcal{E}_{X}(\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=P]))\end{array}\right.

We now state the first results on the semantics of FES.
The lemma below states monotonicity properties for
the semantics. They ensure that fixpoints are well-behaved.
Below, recall that we use pointwise ordering,
e.g.
,

\mathcal{E}_{1}\leq\mathcal{E}_{2}
iff
\mathcal{E}_{1}(\eta)(X)\leq\mathcal{E}_{2}(\eta)(X)
for all
\eta
and
X
.

Lemma 4
.

Let
\mathcal{E}
,
\mathcal{E}_{1}
,
\mathcal{E}_{2}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}\mathcal{E}\!\mathit{qs}
and
\mathcal{S}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}\mathcal{S}\!\mathit{pec}
, then

(1)

If
\mathcal{E}
is monotonic, then
\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket
is monotonic.

(2)

If
\mathcal{E}_{1}
is monotonic and
\mathcal{E}_{1}\leq\mathcal{E}_{2}
,
then
\llbracket\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket\leq\llbracket\mathcal{E}_{2},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket
.

Proof 3.1
.

(1)

We prove
\forall\eta_{1},\eta_{2}.\ \eta_{1}\leq\eta_{2}\Rightarrow\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta_{1})\leq\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta_{2})

by induction on
\mathcal{S}
. The base case: assume
\eta_{1}\leq\eta_{2}
,
then

\llbracket\mathcal{E},\varepsilon\rrbracket(\eta_{1})=\eta_{1}\leq\eta_{2}=\llbracket\mathcal{E},\varepsilon\rrbracket(\eta_{2})
.
Induction step: Let
\eta_{1}\leq\eta_{2}
be given, then for any
P\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}U
,
we have
\eta_{1}[X:=P]\leq{\eta_{2}[X:=P]}
. So

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{\Rightarrow}}\mbox{~~~~~(induction hypothesis)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{\Rightarrow}}\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta_{1}[X:=P])\leq\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta_{2}[X:=P])

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\mbox{~~~~~($\mathcal{E}$ is monotonic)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{\Rightarrow}}\mathcal{E}_{X}(\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta_{1}[X:=P]))\leq\mathcal{E}_{X}(\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta_{2}[X:=P]))

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\mbox{~~~~~(fixpoint monotonicity, Lemma~\ref{fixcalculus}.5)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{\Rightarrow}}\sigma P.\,\mathcal{E}_{X}(\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta_{1}[X:=P]))\leq\sigma P.\,\mathcal{E}_{X}(\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta_{2}[X:=P]))

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\mbox{~~~~~(define
$F_{i}(P):=\mathcal{E}_{X}(\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta_{i}[X:=P]))$, for $i=1,2$)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{\Rightarrow}}\eta_{1}[X:=\sigma(F_{1})]\leq\eta_{2}[X:=\sigma(F_{2})]

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\mbox{~~~~~(induction hypothesis)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{\Rightarrow}}\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta_{1}[X:=\sigma(F_{1})])\leq\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta_{2}[X:=\sigma(F_{2})])

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\mbox{~~~~~(definition)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{\Rightarrow}}\llbracket\mathcal{E},\sigma X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta_{1})\leq\llbracket\mathcal{E},\sigma X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta_{2})

(2)

Assume
\mathcal{E}_{1}
is monotonic, and
\mathcal{E}_{1}\leq\mathcal{E}_{2}
(pointwise). We prove
by induction on
\mathcal{S}
that

\forall\eta.\,\llbracket\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta)\leq\llbracket\mathcal{E}_{2},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta)
.
The base case is simple: for all
\eta
, we have

\llbracket\mathcal{E}_{1},\varepsilon\rrbracket(\eta)=\eta=\llbracket\mathcal{E}_{2},\varepsilon\rrbracket(\eta)
.
For the induction step, let
\eta
be given, then for any
P\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}U
:

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{\Rightarrow}}\mbox{~~~~~(induction hypothesis)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{\Rightarrow}}\llbracket\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=P])\leq\llbracket\mathcal{E}_{2},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=P])

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\mbox{~~~~~(monotonicity of $\mathcal{E}_{1}$ and $\mathcal{E}_{1}\leq\mathcal{E}_{2}$, pointwise))}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{\Rightarrow}}\mathcal{E}_{1,X}(\llbracket\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=P]))\leq\mathcal{E}_{2,X}(\llbracket\mathcal{E}_{2},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=P]))

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\mbox{~~~~~(fixpoint monotonicity, Lemma~\ref{fixcalculus}.5)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{\Rightarrow}}\sigma P.\,\mathcal{E}_{1,X}(\llbracket\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=P]))\leq\sigma P.\,\mathcal{E}_{2,X}(\llbracket\mathcal{E}_{2},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=P]))

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\mbox{~~~~~(define $F_{i}(P):=\mathcal{E}_{i,X}(\llbracket\mathcal{E}_{i},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=P]))$,
for $i=1,2$)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{\Rightarrow}}\eta[X:=\sigma(F_{1})]\leq\eta[X:=\sigma(F_{2})]

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\begin{array}[t]{rl}&\llbracket\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=\sigma(F_{1})])\\
\leq&\mbox{~~~~~(monotonicity, part 1 of this lemma)}\\
&\llbracket\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=\sigma(F_{2})])\\
\leq&\mbox{~~~~~(induction hypothesis)}\\
&\llbracket\mathcal{E}_{2},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=\sigma(F_{2})])\\
\end{array}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\mbox{~~~(definition)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{\Rightarrow}}\llbracket\mathcal{E}_{1},\sigma X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta)\leq\llbracket\mathcal{E}_{2},\sigma X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta)

The next lemma states three sanity properties of the semantics:
(1) The semantics only modifies the valuation on elements
in the domain; (2) the semantics only depends on equations mentioned
in the domain; (3) the input valuation is only used for variables
outside the domain.

Lemma 5
.

Let
\mathcal{E},\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}\mathcal{E}\!\mathit{qs}
,

\mathcal{S}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}\mathcal{S}\!\mathit{pec}

Proof 3.2
.

(1)

\mathcal{S}
. The base case holds by definition. For the induction

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{=}}\llbracket\mathcal{E},\sigma Y\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta)(X)

\displaystyle=\mbox{~~~~~(define $F(P):=\mathcal{E}_{Y}(\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta[Y:=P])$)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{=}}\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta[Y:=\sigma(F)])(X)

\displaystyle=\mbox{~~~~~(induction hypothesis, note: $X\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 1.4pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}$}}{\raise 1.4pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}$}}{\raise 1.0pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}}\mathit{dom}(\mathcal{S})$)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{=}}\eta(X)

(2)

Induction on
\mathcal{S}
. The base case holds by definition. For the

\eta

\mathcal{E}_{1}\mathrel{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{$=$}\hfil\cr\hfil\raise 3.44444pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\sigma X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}}\mkern 0.5mu$}\hfil\cr}}}\mathcal{E}_{2}

P\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}U

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{\Rightarrow}}\mbox{~~~~~(induction hypothesis)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{\Rightarrow}}\llbracket\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=P])=\llbracket\mathcal{E}_{2},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=P])

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{\Rightarrow}}\mathcal{E}_{1,X}(\llbracket\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=P]))=\mathcal{E}_{2,X}(\llbracket\mathcal{E}_{2},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=P]))

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{\Rightarrow}}\eta[X:=\sigma(F_{1})]=\eta[X:=\sigma(F_{2})]

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\mbox{~~~~~(induction hypothesis)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{\Rightarrow}}\llbracket\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=\sigma(F_{1})])=\llbracket\mathcal{E}_{2},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=\sigma(F_{2})])

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\mbox{~~~~~(definition)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{\Rightarrow}}\llbracket\mathcal{E}_{1},\sigma X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta)=\llbracket\mathcal{E}_{2},\sigma X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta)

(3)

Induction on
\mathcal{S}
. The base case is trivial, because both
the assumption and the conclusion reduce to
\eta_{1}=\eta_{2}
.
The induction step is proved as follows (for arbitrary
P\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}U

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{\Rightarrow}}\mbox{~~~~~(assumption)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{\Rightarrow}}\eta_{1}\mathrel{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{$=$}\hfil\cr\hfil\raise 3.44444pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\overline{\sigma X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}}}\mkern 0.5mu$}\hfil\cr}}}\eta_{2}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\mbox{~~~~~(extensionality)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{\Rightarrow}}\eta_{1}[X:=P]\mathrel{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{$=$}\hfil\cr\hfil\raise 3.44444pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\overline{\mathcal{S}}}\mkern 0.5mu$}\hfil\cr}}}\eta_{2}[X:=P]

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\mbox{~~~~~(induction hypothesis)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{\Rightarrow}}\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta_{1}[X:=P])=\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta_{2}[X:=P])

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\mbox{~~~~~(define $F_{i}(P):=\mathcal{E}_{X}(\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta_{i}[X:=P]))$, for $i=1,2$)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{\Rightarrow}}\sigma(F_{1})=\sigma(F_{2})

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\mbox{~~~~~(extensionality)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{\Rightarrow}}\eta_{1}[X:=\sigma(F_{1})]\mathrel{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{$=$}\hfil\cr\hfil\raise 3.44444pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\overline{\mathcal{S}}}\mkern 0.5mu$}\hfil\cr}}}\eta_{2}[X:=\sigma(F_{2})]

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\mbox{~~~~~(induction hypothesis)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{\Rightarrow}}\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta_{1}[X:=\sigma(F_{1})])=\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta_{2}[X:=\sigma(F_{2})])

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\mbox{~~~~~(definition)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{\Rightarrow}}\llbracket\mathcal{E},\sigma X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta_{1})=\llbracket\mathcal{E},\sigma X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta_{2})

Next, we can prove that the semantics as defined above indeed
solves the equations for those variables occurring in the specification:

Lemma 6
.

Let
\mathcal{E}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}\mathcal{E}\!\mathit{qs}
be monotonic,
\mathcal{S}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}\mathcal{S}\!\mathit{pec}
,
\eta\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}\mathcal{V}\!\mathit{al}
and
X\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}\mathcal{X}
.

If
X\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}\mathit{dom}(\mathcal{S})
,
then
\mathcal{E}_{X}(\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta))=\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta)(X)
.

Proof 3.3
.

Let
\mathcal{E}
be monotonic.
By induction on
\mathcal{S}
, we will prove that for all

X\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}\mathit{dom}(\mathcal{S})
and for all
\eta
, it holds that
\mathcal{E}_{X}(\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta))=\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta)(X)
.
The base case trivially holds, because
X\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 1.4pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}$}}{\raise 1.4pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}$}}{\raise 1.0pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}}\mathit{dom}(\varepsilon)
.
For the induction step, assume
X\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}\mathit{dom}(\sigma Y\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S})
.
We distinguish cases.

If
X\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}\mathit{dom}(\mathcal{S})
:

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{=}}\llbracket\mathcal{E},\sigma Y\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta)(X)

\displaystyle=\mbox{~~~~~(define $F(P):=\mathcal{E}_{Y}(\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta[Y:=P]))$~)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{=}}\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta[Y:=\sigma(F)])(X)

\displaystyle=\mbox{~~~~~(induction hypothesis; $X\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}\mathit{dom}(\mathcal{S})$)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{=}}\mathcal{E}_{X}(\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta[Y:=\sigma(F)]))

\displaystyle=\mbox{~~~~~(definition)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{=}}\mathcal{E}_{X}(\llbracket\mathcal{E},\sigma Y\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta))

Otherwise, if
X\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 1.4pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}$}}{\raise 1.4pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}$}}{\raise 1.0pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}}\mathit{dom}(\mathcal{S})
then
X=Y
. We compute:

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{=}}\llbracket\mathcal{E},\sigma X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta)(X)

\displaystyle=\mbox{~~~~~(define $F(P):=\mathcal{E}_{X}(\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=P]))$~)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{=}}\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=\sigma(F)])(X)

\displaystyle=\mbox{~~~~~(Lemma~\ref{sanity}.1; $X\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 1.4pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}$}}{\raise 1.4pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}$}}{\raise 1.0pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}}\mathit{dom}(\mathcal{S})$)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{=}}\sigma(F)

\displaystyle=\begin{array}[t]{l}\mbox{~~~~~(computation rule, Lemma~\ref{fixcalculus}.1;
$F$ is monotonic because $\mathcal{E}_{X}$ }\\
\quad\mbox{is monotonic by assumption,
and $\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket$ is by Lemma~\ref{monotonic}.1) }\end{array}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{=}}F(\sigma(F))

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}_{X}(\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=\sigma(F)]))

\displaystyle=\mbox{~~~~~(definition)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{=}}\mathcal{E}_{X}(\llbracket\mathcal{E},\sigma X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta))

We have the following left-congruence result:

Lemma 7
.

For
\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}\mathcal{E}\!\mathit{qs}
,
\mathcal{S},\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{2}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}\mathcal{S}\!\mathit{pec}
,

\mathcal{E}_{1}\mathrel{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{$=$}\hfil\cr\hfil\raise 3.44444pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\mathcal{S}}\mkern 0.5mu$}\hfil\cr}}}\mathcal{E}_{2}

\llbracket\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{1}\rrbracket=\llbracket\mathcal{E}_{2},\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket

\llbracket\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{S}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{1}\rrbracket=\llbracket\mathcal{E}_{2},\mathcal{S}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket
.

Proof 3.4
.

Induction on
\mathcal{S}
. The base case is trivial.
For the induction step, assume
\llbracket\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{1}\rrbracket=\llbracket\mathcal{E}_{2},\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket

and
\mathcal{E}_{1}\mathrel{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{$=$}\hfil\cr\hfil\raise 3.44444pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\sigma X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}}\mkern 0.5mu$}\hfil\cr}}}\mathcal{E}_{2}
.
Then also
\mathcal{E}_{1}\mathrel{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{$=$}\hfil\cr\hfil\raise 3.44444pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\mathcal{S}}\mkern 0.5mu$}\hfil\cr}}}\mathcal{E}_{2}
. So, for any
P\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}U
:

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{\Rightarrow}}\mbox{~~~~~(induction hypothesis)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{\Rightarrow}}\llbracket\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{S}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{1}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=P])=\llbracket\mathcal{E}_{2},\mathcal{S}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=P])

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{\Rightarrow}}\eta[X:=\sigma(F_{1})]=\eta[X:=\sigma(F_{2})]

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\mbox{~~~~~(induction hypothesis)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{\Rightarrow}}\llbracket\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{S}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{1}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=\sigma(F_{1})])=\llbracket\mathcal{E}_{2},\mathcal{S}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=\sigma(F_{2})])

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\mbox{~~~~~(definition)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{\Rightarrow}}\llbracket\mathcal{E}_{1},\sigma X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{1}\rrbracket(\eta)=\llbracket\mathcal{E}_{2},\sigma X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket(\eta)

Remarkably, right-congruence doesn’t hold in general.

16

3.3.
The Dependency Graph between Variables

Since we introduced a semantic notion of equations, avoiding syntactic
expressions, we also need a semantic notion of dependence between variables.
Given
V_{1},V_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
, we define that
V_{1}
is
independent
of
V_{2}

with respect to
\mathcal{E}
, notation
\mathit{indep}(\mathcal{E},V_{1},V_{2})
, as follows:

\forall\eta_{1},\eta_{2}.\,(\eta_{1}\mathrel{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{$=$}\hfil\cr\hfil\raise 3.44444pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\overline{V_{2}}}\mkern 0.5mu$}\hfil\cr}}}\eta_{2})\Rightarrow(\mathcal{E}(\eta_{1})\mathrel{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{$=$}\hfil\cr\hfil\raise 3.44444pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle V_{1}}\mkern 0.5mu$}\hfil\cr}}}\mathcal{E}(\eta_{2}))\kern 5.0pt.

That is: the solution
of variables
X\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}V_{1}
is the same for all those
\eta
that differ
at most on the values assigned to
Y\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}V_{2}
. This is slightly more liberal
than the usual syntactic requirement that
Y

doesn’t occur syntactically

in
\mathcal{E}_{X}
.
We overload the definition of
\mathit{indep}
for individual variables and
specifications (and any combination of those),
e.g.
,

\mathit{indep}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{2})=\mathit{indep}(\mathcal{E},\mathit{dom}(\mathcal{S}_{1}),\mathit{dom}(\mathcal{S}_{2}))
and

\mathit{indep}(\mathcal{E},X,Y)=\mathit{indep}(\mathcal{E},\{X\},\{Y\})
.

This notion gives rise to the
variable dependency graph
of a
FES
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S})
. The variables in
\mathit{dom}(\mathcal{S})
form the nodes of this graph; the edges

X\mathrel{\smash{\mathrel{\mathchoice{{\displaystyle\mathop{\hbox to19.83319pt{$\displaystyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\displaystyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}}}}{{\textstyle\mathop{\hbox to19.83319pt{$\textstyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\textstyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}}}}{{\scriptstyle\mathop{\hbox to18.04163pt{$\scriptstyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}}}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathop{\hbox to18.04163pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}}}}}}}Y
are defined as
\neg\mathit{indep}(\mathcal{E},X,Y)
.
We define
X

depends (indirectly)
on
Y
(written
X\mathrel{\smash{{\mathrel{\mathchoice{{\displaystyle\mathop{\hbox to19.83319pt{$\displaystyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\displaystyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}}}}{{\textstyle\mathop{\hbox to19.83319pt{$\textstyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\textstyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}}}}{{\scriptstyle\mathop{\hbox to18.04163pt{$\scriptstyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}}}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathop{\hbox to18.04163pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}}}}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\rightarrow\;}}}Y
) as the
reflexive, transitive closure of
\mathrel{\smash{\mathrel{\mathchoice{{\displaystyle\mathop{\hbox to19.83319pt{$\displaystyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\displaystyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}}}}{{\textstyle\mathop{\hbox to19.83319pt{$\textstyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\textstyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}}}}{{\scriptstyle\mathop{\hbox to18.04163pt{$\scriptstyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}}}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathop{\hbox to18.04163pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}}}}}}}
. In other words,
there exists a path in the dependency graph from
X
to
Y
. We also
use the notation
X\mathrel{\smash{\mathrel{\mathchoice{{\displaystyle\mathop{\hbox to19.83319pt{$\displaystyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\displaystyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}}}}{{\textstyle\mathop{\hbox to19.83319pt{$\textstyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\textstyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}}}}{{\scriptstyle\mathop{\hbox to18.04163pt{$\scriptstyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}}}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathop{\hbox to18.04163pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}}}}}\hskip-3.99994pt\raisebox{5.0pt}{\small+}}}Y

4.
Substituting in FES Equations

In this section, we define two substitution operations on the
equations of a FES, and study under which conditions these operations
preserve solutions. The first operation allows substituting variables
by their definition. We show that this substitution preserves
solutions in some new cases (cf. Section
7
). The second
operation replaces a variable in an equation by its solution.

4.1.
Unfolding Definitions

We define
\mathit{unfold}(\mathcal{E},X,Y)
, where each occurrence of
Y
in the
definition of
X
is replaced by the definition of
Y
, as follows:

\mathit{unfold}(\mathcal{E},X,Y)(\eta):=\mathcal{E}(\eta)[X:=\mathcal{E}_{X}(\eta[Y:=\mathcal{E}_{Y}(\eta)])]

We will use the following observation several times. It basically states
that unfolding
Y
in
X
doesn’t affect other equations than that for
X
.

Lemma 8
.

If
X\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 1.4pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}$}}{\raise 1.4pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}$}}{\raise 1.0pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}}\mathit{dom}(\mathcal{S})
, then

(1)

\mathit{unfold}(\mathcal{E},X,Y)\mathrel{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{$=$}\hfil\cr\hfil\raise 3.44444pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\mathcal{S}}\mkern 0.5mu$}\hfil\cr}}}\mathcal{E}

(2)

\llbracket\mathit{unfold}(\mathcal{E},X,Y),\mathcal{S}\rrbracket=\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket

Proof 4.1
.

(1) holds by definition of
\mathit{unfold}
, as it only modifies the value
of
\mathcal{E}
on variable
X
. Then (2) holds by Lemma
5
.2.

It is known (cf. Example
7.1
) that in general one should
not unfold
Y
in
X
, if
Y
precedes
X
in the specification. As a
new result we show that we can substitute
X
in its own definition:

Lemma 9
.

Let
\mathcal{E}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}\mathcal{E}\!\mathit{qs}
be monotonic. Let
\mathcal{S}=\sigma X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{1}
,
and
X\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 1.4pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}$}}{\raise 1.4pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}$}}{\raise 1.0pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}}\mathit{dom}(\mathcal{S}_{1})
.
Then
\llbracket\mathit{unfold}(\mathcal{E},X,X),\mathcal{S}\rrbracket=\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket
.

Proof 4.2
.

For arbitrary
\eta
, define

\displaystyle F(P)

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{E}_{X}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left(\vbox to17.49985pt{}\right.$}}\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=P]){\hbox{$\textstyle\left)\vbox to17.49985pt{}\right.$}}

\displaystyle G(P)

\displaystyle:=\mathit{unfold}(\mathcal{E},X,X)_{X}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left(\vbox to17.49985pt{}\right.$}}\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=P]){\hbox{$\textstyle\left)\vbox to17.49985pt{}\right.$}}

\displaystyle H(P,Q)

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{E}_{X}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left(\vbox to17.49985pt{}\right.$}}\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=P])[X:=Q]{\hbox{$\textstyle\left)\vbox to17.49985pt{}\right.$}}

X\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 1.4pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}$}}{\raise 1.4pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}$}}{\raise 1.0pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}}\mathit{dom}(\mathcal{S}_{1})

we obtain:
\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=P])(X)=\eta[X:=P](X)=P

\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=P])[X:=P]~=~\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=P])

(*)

Next, we prove that

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{=}}\sigma P.\,G(P)

\displaystyle=\mbox{~~~~~(by definition of $\mathit{unfold}$)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{=}}\sigma P.\,\mathcal{E}_{X}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left(\vbox to17.49985pt{}\right.$}}\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=P]){\hbox{$\textstyle\left[\vbox to17.49985pt{}\right.$}}X:=\mathcal{E}_{X}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left(\vbox to17.49985pt{}\right.$}}\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=P]){\hbox{$\textstyle\left)\vbox to17.49985pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left]\vbox to17.49985pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left)\vbox to17.49985pt{}\right.$}}

\displaystyle=\mbox{~~~~~(by * above)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{=}}\sigma P.\,\mathcal{E}_{X}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left(\vbox to17.49985pt{}\right.$}}\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=P]){\hbox{$\textstyle\left[\vbox to17.49985pt{}\right.$}}X:=\mathcal{E}_{X}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left(\vbox to17.49985pt{}\right.$}}\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=P])[X:=P]{\hbox{$\textstyle\left)\vbox to17.49985pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left]\vbox to17.49985pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left)\vbox to17.49985pt{}\right.$}}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{=}}\llbracket\mathit{unfold}(\mathcal{E},X,X),\sigma X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{1}\rrbracket(\eta)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{=}}\llbracket\mathcal{E},\sigma X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{1}\rrbracket(\eta)

The following theorem is motivated in
[

as follows. Assume

for a variable

in
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S})
.
Then we can replace the definition of
X
by simply putting
X=a
. We
simplify the proof in
[
Mad97
]
, which is based on an infinite
series of FESs. Instead, we just use induction on
\mathcal{S}
and some properties
of complete lattices.

Theorem 11
.

Let
\mathcal{E}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}\mathcal{E}\!\mathit{qs}
be monotonic and let
a:=\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta)(X)
.
Then

\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta)=\llbracket\mathcal{E}[X\mapsto a],\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta)

Proof 4.4
.

We prove the theorem by induction on
\mathcal{S}
. The base case
is trivial, for

\llbracket\mathcal{E},\varepsilon\rrbracket(\eta)=\eta=\llbracket\mathcal{E}[X\mapsto a],\varepsilon\rrbracket(\eta)
.
For the induction step (
\sigma Y\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}
), we need to define the functions

a,b\colon\mathcal{V}\!\mathit{al}\to U
and

and

\begin{array}[]{rcl}a(\eta^{\prime})&:=&\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta^{\prime})(X)\\

\forall\eta^{\prime}.~\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta^{\prime})=\llbracket\mathcal{E}[X\mapsto a(\eta^{\prime})],\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta^{\prime})

\forall\eta.~\llbracket\mathcal{E},\sigma Y\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta)=\llbracket\mathcal{E}[X\mapsto b(\eta)],\sigma Y\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta)

5.
Swapping Variables in FES specifications

We now study swapping the order of variables in a specification. In
general, this operation doesn’t exactly preserve solutions. We first
show that adjacent variables with the same sign may be swapped without
changing the semantics (Section
5.1
). Subsequently, we will prove
that we can swap the order between blocks of equations, under certain
independence criteria (Section
5.2
). The main theorem of
that section is new (cf. Section
7
). Finally, we show
that swapping a
\mu/\nu
sequence by the corresponding
\nu/\mu
in
general leads to a greater or equal solution (Section
5.3
).

5.1.
Swapping Equations with the same Sign

Theorem 12
.

Assume
\mathcal{E}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}\mathcal{E}\!\mathit{qs}
is monotonic. Then

\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\sigma X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\sigma Y\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket=\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\sigma Y\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\sigma X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket

Proof 5.1
.

We first compute for arbitrary
\eta\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}\mathcal{V}\!\mathit{al}
:

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathcal{E},\sigma X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\sigma Y\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket(\eta)

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathcal{E},\sigma Y\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=\sigma(F_{2})])\mbox{, where}

\displaystyle\qquad\begin{aligned} F_{2}(P)&=\mathcal{E}_{X}(\llbracket\mathcal{E},\sigma Y\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=P]))\\
\end{aligned}

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=\sigma(F_{2}),\,Y:=\sigma(F_{3})])\mbox{, where}

\displaystyle\qquad\begin{aligned} F_{2}(P)&=\mathcal{E}_{X}(\llbracket\mathcal{E},\sigma Y\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=P]))\\
F_{3}(Q)&=\mathcal{E}_{Y}(\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=\sigma(F_{2}),\,Y:=Q]))\\
\end{aligned}

\displaystyle=\;

(unfold definition of
\llbracket~\rrbracket
in
F_{2}
, introduce
F_{1}
)

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=\sigma(F_{2}),\,Y:=\sigma(F_{3})])\mbox{, where}

\displaystyle\qquad\begin{aligned} F_{1}(P)(Q)&=\mathcal{E}_{Y}(\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=P,\,Y:=Q]))\\
F_{2}(P)&=\mathcal{E}_{X}(\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=P,\,Y:=\sigma(F_{1}(P))]))\\
F_{3}(Q)&=\mathcal{E}_{Y}(\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=\sigma(F_{2}),\,Y:=Q]))\\
\end{aligned}

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle A(\sigma(F_{2}),\sigma(F_{3}))\mbox{, where}

\displaystyle\qquad\begin{aligned} A(P,Q)&=\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=P,\,Y:=Q])\\
F_{1}(P)(Q)&=\mathcal{E}_{Y}(A(P,Q))\\
F_{2}(P)&=\mathcal{E}_{X}(A(P,\sigma(F_{1}(P))))\\
F_{3}(Q)&=\mathcal{E}_{Y}(A(\sigma(F_{2}),Q))\\
\end{aligned}

Symmetrically, we get:

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathcal{E},\sigma Y\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\sigma X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket(\eta)

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle B(\sigma(G_{2}),\sigma(G_{3}))\mbox{, where}

\displaystyle\qquad\begin{aligned} B(Q,P)&=\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket(\eta[Y:=Q,\,X:=P])\\
G_{1}(Q)(P)&=\mathcal{E}_{X}(B(Q,P))\\
G_{2}(Q)&=\mathcal{E}_{Y}(B(Q,\sigma(G_{1}(Q))))\\
G_{3}(P)&=\mathcal{E}_{X}(B(\sigma(G_{2}),P))\\
\end{aligned}

Note that the theorem is trivial when
X=Y
.
So we may assume
X\neq Y
. Hence
A(P,Q)=B(Q,P)
, and we have:

\displaystyle\sigma(F_{2})

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\sigma P.\,\mathcal{E}_{X}(A(P,\sigma(F_{1}(P))))

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\sigma P.\,\mathcal{E}_{X}(A(P,\sigma Q.\,\mathcal{E}_{Y}(A(P,Q))))

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\begin{array}[t]{l}\mbox{~~~~~(Beki\v{c} rule, Lemma~\ref{fixcalculus}.9,
with $H(p,q):=\mathcal{E}_{X}(A(p,q))$ }\\
\quad\mbox{~~~~~~and $K(p,q):=\mathcal{E}_{Y}(A(q,p))$, which are monotonic, because~}\\
\quad\mbox{~~~~~~$\mathcal{E}$ is by assumption, and
$\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket$ by Lemma~\ref{monotonic}.1)}\end{array}

\displaystyle\sigma P.\,\mathcal{E}_{X}(A(P,\sigma Q.\,\mathcal{E}_{Y}(A(\sigma P.\,\mathcal{E}_{X}(A(P,Q)),Q))))

\displaystyle=\;

(while
A(P,Q)=B(Q,P)
)

\displaystyle\sigma P.\,\mathcal{E}_{X}(B(\sigma Q.\,\mathcal{E}_{Y}(B(Q,\sigma P.\,\mathcal{E}_{X}(B(Q,P))))),P)

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\sigma P.\,\mathcal{E}_{X}(B(\sigma Q.\,\mathcal{E}_{Y}(B(Q,\sigma(G_{1}(Q)))),P))

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\sigma P.\,\mathcal{E}_{X}(B(\sigma(G_{2}),P))

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\sigma(G_{3})

We can now finish the proof:

(computation above, and full symmetry)

\displaystyle\sigma(F_{2})=\sigma(G_{3})\mbox{~~and~~}\sigma(F_{3})=\sigma(G_{2})

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\;

(because
A(P,Q)=B(Q,P)
)

\displaystyle A(\sigma(F_{2}),\sigma(F_{3}))=B(\sigma(G_{2}),\sigma(G_{3}))

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\;

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathcal{E},\sigma X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\sigma Y\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket(\eta)=\llbracket\mathcal{E},\sigma Y\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\sigma X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket(\eta)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\;

\displaystyle\mbox{~~~~~(Lemma~\ref{congruence}})

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\sigma X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\sigma Y\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket(\eta)=\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\sigma Y\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\sigma X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket(\eta)

5.2.
Migrating Independent Blocks of Equations

Our aim here is to investigate swapping blocks of equations that
are independent. We first need two technical lemmas.
The first lemma enables to commute updates to valuations with computing
solutions:

Lemma 13
.

If
X\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 1.4pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}$}}{\raise 1.4pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}$}}{\raise 1.0pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}}\mathit{dom}(\mathcal{S})
and
\mathit{indep}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S},X)
, then

\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=P])=(\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta))[X:=P]

Proof 5.2
.

Induction on
\mathcal{S}
. The base case is trivial:

\llbracket\mathcal{E},\varepsilon\rrbracket(\eta[X:=P])=\eta[X:=P]=\llbracket\mathcal{E},\varepsilon\rrbracket(\eta)[X:=P]

Case
\mathcal{S}=\sigma Y\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{1}
. Assume
X\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 1.4pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}$}}{\raise 1.4pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}$}}{\raise 1.0pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}}\mathit{dom}(\mathcal{S})
and
\mathit{indep}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S},X)
,
then
X\neq Y
, and also
X\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 1.4pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}$}}{\raise 1.4pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}$}}{\raise 1.0pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}}\mathit{dom}(\mathcal{S}_{1})
and
\mathit{indep}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1},X)
, so the
induction hypothesis can be applied. Then

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathcal{E},\sigma Y\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{1}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=P])

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=P,\,Y:=\sigma(F)])\mbox{, where}

\displaystyle\qquad\begin{aligned} F(Q):=\>&\;\mathcal{E}_{Y}(\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=P,\,Y:=Q]))\\
\>=\>&\;~~~~~\mbox{(induction hypothesis, and $X\neq Y$)}\\
&\;\mathcal{E}_{Y}(\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1}\rrbracket(\eta[Y:=Q])[X:=P])\\
\>=\>&\;~~~~~\mbox{($Y\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}\mathit{dom}(\mathcal{S})$ is independent of $X$)}\\
&\;\mathcal{E}_{Y}(\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1}\rrbracket(\eta[Y:=Q]))\\
\>=:&\;G(Q)\\
\end{aligned}

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=P,\,Y:=\sigma(G)])

\displaystyle=\;

(induction hypothesis, and
X\neq Y
)

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1}\rrbracket(\eta[Y:=\sigma(G)])[X:=P]

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathcal{E},\sigma Y\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{1}\rrbracket(\eta)[X:=P]

The next lemma states that independent specifications can be solved
independently.

Lemma 14
.

Let
\mathcal{E}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}\mathcal{E}\!\mathit{qs}
and
\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{2}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}\mathcal{S}\!\mathit{pec}
. If

\mathit{indep}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{2})
, then for all
\eta\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}\mathcal{V}\!\mathit{al}
,

\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket(\eta)=\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket(\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1}\rrbracket(\eta))
.

Proof 5.3
.

Induction on
\mathcal{S}_{1}
. In case
\mathcal{S}_{1}=\varepsilon
we obtain indeed:

\llbracket\mathcal{E},\varepsilon\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket(\eta)=\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket(\eta)=\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket(\llbracket\mathcal{E},\varepsilon\rrbracket(\eta))

Next, consider
\mathcal{S}_{1}=\sigma X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}
. Assume
\mathit{indep}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{2})
,
then it follows that
\mathit{indep}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S},\mathcal{S}_{2})
,
so we can use the induction hypothesis. Define:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}F(P)&:=&\mathcal{E}_{X}(\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=P]))\\
G(P)&:=&\mathcal{E}_{X}(\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=P]))\\
\end{array}

In order to show that
F=G
, it suffices (because
\mathcal{E}_{X}
is
independent of
\mathcal{S}_{2}
) to show that for any
P\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}U

and
Y\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 1.4pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}$}}{\raise 1.4pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}$}}{\raise 1.0pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}}\mathit{dom}(\mathcal{S}_{2})
:

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=P])(Y)

\displaystyle=\;

(induction hypothesis)

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket(\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=P]))(Y)

\displaystyle=\;

(Lemma
5
.1)

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=P])(Y)

Next, we finish the proof with the following calculation:

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathcal{E},\sigma X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket(\eta)

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=\sigma(F)])

\displaystyle=\;

(induction hypothesis)

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket(\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=\sigma(F)]))

\displaystyle=\;

(
F=G
, see above)

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket(\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=\sigma(G)]))

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket(\llbracket\mathcal{E},\sigma X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta))

The next theorem shows that two disjoint blocks of equations can be
swapped, provided one of them doesn’t depend on the other. Note that
a dependence in one direction is allowed, and that it doesn’t matter
in which direction by symmetry. Theorem
18
will
generalize this by adding left- and right-contexts under certain
conditions.

Theorem 15
.

Let
\mathit{disjoint}(\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{2})
and
\mathit{indep}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{2})
.
Then
\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket=\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{2}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{1}\rrbracket
.

Proof 5.4
.

Induction on
\mathcal{S}_{2}
.
The base case is trivial.
For the induction step, let
\mathcal{S}_{2}=\sigma X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}
.
If we assume
\mathit{disjoint}(\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{2})
and
\mathit{indep}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{2})
, then we
also obtain
\mathit{disjoint}(\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{S})
and
\mathit{indep}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{S})
, so we may
apply the induction hypothesis
\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}\rrbracket=\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{1}\rrbracket
.
Furthermore, from the same assumptions, we also
get
X\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 1.4pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}$}}{\raise 1.4pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}$}}{\raise 1.0pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}}\mathit{dom}(\mathcal{S}_{1})
and
\mathit{indep}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1},X)
.
Let
\eta
be arbitrary.

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\sigma X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta)

\displaystyle=\;

(Lemma
14
)

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathcal{E},\sigma X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1}\rrbracket(\eta))

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1}\rrbracket(\eta)[X:=\sigma(F)])\mbox{, where}

\displaystyle\qquad\begin{aligned} F(P):=\>&\;\mathcal{E}_{X}(\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1}\rrbracket(\eta)[X:=P])\\
=\>&\;\mbox{~~~~~(Lemma~\ref{indepenv})}\\
&\;\mathcal{E}_{X}(\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=P])))\\
=\>&\;\mbox{~~~~~(Lemma~\ref{indepsolve})}\\
&\;\mathcal{E}_{X}(\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=P]))\\
=:&\;G(P)\\
\end{aligned}

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1}\rrbracket(\eta)[X:=\sigma(G)])

\displaystyle=\;

(Lemma
13
)

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=\sigma(G)]))

\displaystyle=\;

(Lemma
14
)

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=\sigma(G)])

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathcal{E},\sigma X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{1}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta)

\displaystyle=\;

(Lemma
7
and induction hypothesis)

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathcal{E},\sigma X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{1}\rrbracket(\eta)

This migration theorem has several interesting corollaries.
First, we get right-congruence for independent specifications.

Corollary 16
.

Assume that
\mathit{indep}(\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{S})
,
\mathit{indep}(\mathcal{E}_{2},\mathcal{S}_{2},\mathcal{S})

\mathit{disjoint}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{S}_{1}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2})
and

.
Then
\llbracket\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{1}\rrbracket=\llbracket\mathcal{E}_{2},\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket

\llbracket\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{1}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}\rrbracket=\llbracket\mathcal{E}_{2},\mathcal{S}_{2}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}\rrbracket
.

We also get the near-reverse of Lemma
14
:

Corollary 17
.

Let
\mathit{indep}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{2},\mathcal{S}_{1})
and
\mathit{disjoint}(\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{2})
.
Then for all
\eta\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}\mathcal{V}\!\mathit{al}
, we have

\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket(\eta)=\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1}\rrbracket(\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket(\eta))
.

Proof 5.5
.

Under the given assumptions, we obtain from Theorem
15

(applied from right to left) and Lemma
14
:

\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket(\eta)=\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{2}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{1}\rrbracket(\eta)=\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1}\rrbracket(\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket(\eta))

.

Assume that
\mathit{disjoint}(\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{2}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{3})
.

\mathit{indep}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{2}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{3})

{\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{0}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{1}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{3}\rrbracket=\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{0}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{1}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{3}\rrbracket}
.

.

We also have
\mathit{disjoint}(\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{3})
, and either

\mathit{indep}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{3})
or
\mathit{indep}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{3},\mathcal{S}_{1})

Using Lemma
7
and Theorem
15
twice

\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{0}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{1}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{3}\rrbracket=\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{0}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{3}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{1}\rrbracket=\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{0}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{1}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{3}\rrbracket

5.3.
Inequalities by Swapping or Changing Signs

In this section, we will prove a few inequalities. Theorem
20

shows the consequence of swapping variables with a different sign;
Theorem
21
shows the effect of changing the sign of a variable.
But first, it will be shown that
\leq
is a left congruence:

Lemma 19
.

Let
\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}\mathcal{E}\!\mathit{qs}
and
\mathcal{S},\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{2}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}\mathcal{S}\!\mathit{pec}
.
If
\mathcal{E}_{1}
is monotonic,
\mathcal{E}_{1}\mathrel{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{$=$}\hfil\cr\hfil\raise 3.44444pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\mathcal{S}}\mkern 0.5mu$}\hfil\cr}}}\mathcal{E}_{2}
, and

\llbracket\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{1}\rrbracket\leq\llbracket\mathcal{E}_{2},\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket
, then

\llbracket\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{S}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{1}\rrbracket\leq\llbracket\mathcal{E}_{2},\mathcal{S}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket
.

Proof 5.7
.

Induction on
\mathcal{S}
. The base case is trivial.

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathcal{E}_{1},\sigma X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{1}\rrbracket(\eta)

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{S}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{1}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=\sigma(F)]),where

\displaystyle\qquad\begin{aligned} F(P):=\>&\;\mathcal{E}_{1,X}(\llbracket\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{S}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{1}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=P]))\\
\leq\>&\;\mbox{~~~~~(by induction hypothesis and $\mathcal{E}_{1}$ monotonic)}\\
&\;\mathcal{E}_{1,X}(\llbracket\mathcal{E}_{2},\mathcal{S}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=P]))\\
=\>&\;\mathcal{E}_{2,X}(\llbracket\mathcal{E}_{2},\mathcal{S}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=P]))\\
=:&\;G(P)\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\leq\;

(Using Lemma
4
.1)

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{S}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{1}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=\sigma(G)])

\displaystyle\leq\;

(by induction hypothesis)

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathcal{E}_{2},\mathcal{S}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=\sigma(G)])

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathcal{E}_{2},\sigma X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket(\eta)

Note that, by duality, the above lemma may also be applied if
\mathcal{E}_{2}

is monotonic instead of
\mathcal{E}_{1}
.
Moreover, note that (only) for monotonic
\mathcal{E}_{1}
, Lemma
7

would follow from Lemma
19
.

Theorem 20
.

Assume
\mathcal{E}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}\mathcal{E}\!\mathit{qs}
is monotonic and
X\neq Y
. Then

\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mu X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\nu Y\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket\leq\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\nu Y\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mu X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket

Proof 5.8
.

As in Theorem
12
, and using
X\neq Y
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mu X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\nu Y\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket(\eta)

\displaystyle=A(\mu(F_{2}),\nu(F_{3}))\mbox{, where}

\displaystyle\qquad\begin{aligned} A(P,Q)&=\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=P,\,Y:=Q])\\
F_{1}(P)(Q)&=\mathcal{E}_{Y}(A(P,Q))\\
F_{2}(P)&=\mathcal{E}_{X}(A(P,\nu(F_{1}(P))))\\
F_{3}(Q)&=\mathcal{E}_{Y}(A(\mu(F_{2}),Q))\\
\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathcal{E},\nu Y\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mu X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket(\eta)

\displaystyle=A(\mu(G_{3}),\nu(G_{2}))\mbox{, where}

\displaystyle\qquad\begin{aligned} G_{1}(Q)(P)&=\mathcal{E}_{X}(A(P,Q))\\
G_{2}(Q)&=\mathcal{E}_{Y}(A(\mu(G_{1}(Q)),Q))\\
G_{3}(P)&=\mathcal{E}_{X}(A(P,\nu(G_{2})))\\
\end{aligned}

By Lemma
2
.4(a),
\mu(F_{2})\leq\mu(G_{3})
, and
by Lemma
2
.4(b),
\nu(F_{3})\leq\nu(G_{2})
, whence
it follows that
\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mu X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\nu Y\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket(\eta)\leq\llbracket\mathcal{E},\nu Y\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mu X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket(\eta)
. The theorem then follows
by Lemma
19
.

We end this section with another inequality:

Theorem 21
.

If
\mathcal{E}
is monotonic, then

\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mu X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket\leq\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\nu X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket
.

Proof 5.9
.

Let
\eta
be an arbitrary valuation, and define
F:U\to U
by

F(P):=\mathcal{E}_{X}(\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=P]))
. We then have:

(Theorem
2
.1)

\displaystyle\mu P.\,F(P)\leq\nu P.\,F(P)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\;

(Monotonicity, Theorem
4
.1)

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=\mu P.\,F(P)])\leq\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=\nu P.\,F(P)])

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\;

(Definition semantics)

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mu X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta)\leq\llbracket\mathcal{E},\nu X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket(\eta)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\;

(
\eta
was arbitrary)

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mu X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}\rrbracket\leq\llbracket\mathcal{E},\nu X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\;

(Congruence, Theorem
19
)

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mu X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket\leq\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\nu X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket

In the next section, we will see sufficient conditions under which the
inequality signs of these theorems can be turned into
equalities. These conditions will be phrased in terms of the
dependency graph.

6.
Indirect Dependencies and Loops

In Section
5.2
, we studied direct dependencies between
variables. Basically, a direct dependency of
X
on
Y
means
that
Y
occurs in the definition of
X
. We will now study the
effect of indirect dependencies, written
X\mathrel{\smash{{\mathrel{\mathchoice{{\displaystyle\mathop{\hbox to19.83319pt{$\displaystyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\displaystyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}}}}{{\textstyle\mathop{\hbox to19.83319pt{$\textstyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\textstyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}}}}{{\scriptstyle\mathop{\hbox to18.04163pt{$\scriptstyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}}}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathop{\hbox to18.04163pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}}}}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\rightarrow\;}}}Y

(cf. the definitions in Section
3.3
)

Given a specification
\mathcal{S}
and a computable predicate
f
on variables, we define
\mathit{split}_{f}(\mathcal{S})=(\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{2})
, where
\mathcal{S}_{1}
is the sublist of
\mathcal{S}
with those
X
for which
f(X)
does
not
hold and
\mathcal{S}_{2}
is the sublist of
\mathcal{S}
with those
X
for which
f(X)

does
hold.
Notice that, within
\mathcal{S}_{1}
and
\mathcal{S}_{2}
, variables keep their order from
\mathcal{S}
.

The following basic facts follow directly from the definition of
\mathit{split}
.

Lemma 22
.

Let
\mathit{split}_{f}(\mathcal{S})=(\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{2})
, then
\mathit{dom}(\mathcal{S})=\mathit{dom}(\mathcal{S}_{1})\cup\mathit{dom}(\mathcal{S}_{2})
and
\mathit{disjoint}(\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{2})
.

We first show how the equations in a FES may be rearranged if the specification is
split in such a way that certain independence conditions are fulfilled.

Lemma 23
.

Let
f
be a predicate and
\mathcal{S}
a specification such that
\mathit{split}_{f}(\mathcal{S})=(\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{2})
and
\mathit{indep}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{2},\mathcal{S}_{1})
.
Then
\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket=\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket
.

Proof 6.1
.

We perform induction on
\mathcal{S}
.
The base case is trivial.
Let
\mathit{split}_{f}(\mathcal{S})=(\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{2})
and assume as induction hypothesis that
\mathit{indep}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{2},\mathcal{S}_{1})
implies
\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket=\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket
.
For the induction step, we consider the specification
\sigma Y\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}
and distinguish two cases.

If
f(Y)
does not hold, then we have
\mathit{split}_{f}(\sigma Y\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S})=(\sigma Y\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{2})
.
Accordingly, we assume
\mathit{indep}(\mathcal{E},\sigma Y\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2},\mathcal{S}_{1})
, which implies
\mathit{indep}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{2},\mathcal{S}_{1})
.
We can thus apply the induction hypothesis and congruence (Lemma
7
) to obtain

\llbracket\mathcal{E},\sigma Y\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}\rrbracket=\llbracket\mathcal{E},\sigma Y\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{1}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket
.

Otherwise, if
f(Y)
holds, we obtain
\mathit{split}_{f}(\sigma Y\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S})=(\mathcal{S}_{1},\sigma Y\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2})
.
Now we assume that
\mathit{indep}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{2},\sigma Y\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{1})
, which again implies
\mathit{indep}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{2},\mathcal{S}_{1})
.
We also have
\mathit{disjoint}(\mathcal{S}_{1},\sigma Y\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2})
(Lemma
22
), so we may apply Theorem
18
below:

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathcal{E},\sigma Y\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}\rrbracket

\displaystyle=\;

(by induction hypothesis and Lemma
7
)

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathcal{E},\sigma Y\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{1}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket

\displaystyle=\;

(by Theorem
18
)

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\sigma Y\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket

We simplify notation and write
\mathit{split}_{X,\mathcal{E}}(\mathcal{S})
for

\mathit{split}_{\mathit{dep}^{X}_{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}}}(\mathcal{S})
, defining the predicate

\smash{\mathit{dep}^{X}_{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}}}(Y)=X\mathrel{\smash{{\mathrel{\mathchoice{{\displaystyle\mathop{\hbox to19.83319pt{$\displaystyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\displaystyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}}}}{{\textstyle\mathop{\hbox to19.83319pt{$\textstyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\textstyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}}}}{{\scriptstyle\mathop{\hbox to18.04163pt{$\scriptstyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}}}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathop{\hbox to18.04163pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}}}}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\rightarrow\;}}}Y
.
If
\mathit{indep}(\mathcal{E},X,Y)
is computable (which we assume henceforward), then
X\mathrel{\smash{{\mathrel{\mathchoice{{\displaystyle\mathop{\hbox to19.83319pt{$\displaystyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\displaystyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}}}}{{\textstyle\mathop{\hbox to19.83319pt{$\textstyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\textstyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}}}}{{\scriptstyle\mathop{\hbox to18.04163pt{$\scriptstyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}}}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathop{\hbox to18.04163pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}}}}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\rightarrow\;}}}Y
is also computable since
\mathcal{S}
is finite.
Intuitively, if
\mathit{split}_{X,\mathcal{E}}(\mathcal{S})=(\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{2})
, then

\mathcal{S}_{1}
is the sublist of
\mathcal{S}
with those
Y
on which
X

does not
depend indirectly; and
\mathcal{S}_{2}

is the sublist of
\mathcal{S}
with those
Z
on which
X

does
depend
indirectly.
Notice that, if
X\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 1.4pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}$}}{\raise 1.4pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}$}}{\raise 1.0pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}}\mathit{dom}(\mathcal{S})
, then
\mathit{split}_{X,\mathcal{E}}(\mathcal{S})=(\mathcal{S},\varepsilon)
.

We have the following lemma about splitting a specification based on the dependencies of
X
:

Lemma 24
.

If
\mathit{split}_{X,\mathcal{E}}(\mathcal{S})=(\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{2})
, then
\mathit{indep}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{2},\mathcal{S}_{1})
.

Proof 6.2
.

Assume that some
Z\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}\mathit{dom}(\mathcal{S}_{2})

would use some
Y\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}\mathit{dom}(\mathcal{S}_{1})
in its definition in
\mathcal{E}
.
Then
X\mathrel{\smash{{\mathrel{\mathchoice{{\displaystyle\mathop{\hbox to19.83319pt{$\displaystyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\displaystyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}}}}{{\textstyle\mathop{\hbox to19.83319pt{$\textstyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\textstyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}}}}{{\scriptstyle\mathop{\hbox to18.04163pt{$\scriptstyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}}}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathop{\hbox to18.04163pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}}}}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\rightarrow\;}}}Z\mathrel{\smash{\mathrel{\mathchoice{{\displaystyle\mathop{\hbox to19.83319pt{$\displaystyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\displaystyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}}}}{{\textstyle\mathop{\hbox to19.83319pt{$\textstyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\textstyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}}}}{{\scriptstyle\mathop{\hbox to18.04163pt{$\scriptstyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}}}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathop{\hbox to18.04163pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}}}}}}}Y
, so
X\mathrel{\smash{{\mathrel{\mathchoice{{\displaystyle\mathop{\hbox to19.83319pt{$\displaystyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\displaystyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}}}}{{\textstyle\mathop{\hbox to19.83319pt{$\textstyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\textstyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}}}}{{\scriptstyle\mathop{\hbox to18.04163pt{$\scriptstyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}}}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathop{\hbox to18.04163pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}}}}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\rightarrow\;}}}Y
,
and
Y
would be in
\mathcal{S}_{2}
and not in
\mathcal{S}_{1}
.

The key theorem of this section states that the equations in a FES can
be rearranged, such that all equations that
X
depends on precede all
other equations, or vice versa. This is useful, because those parts
can be solved independently, using Lemma
14
. By
repeatedly picking a variable in a terminal strongly connected component
of the remaining variable dependency graph, one can thus solve all
SCCs one by one.
This idea already appeared in
[
Jur00
]
for parity games, although it
does not always provide performance benefits in practice
[
FL09
]
.
The theorem may also be used to reduce the number of fixpoint alternations
in a FES.

Theorem 25
.

Let
\mathit{split}_{X,\mathcal{E}}(\mathcal{S})=(\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{2})
. Then

\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket=\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket=\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{2}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{1}\rrbracket
.

Proof 6.3
.

The first equality follows from Lemmas
23
and
24
.
From Theorem
15
and Lemmas
22
and
24
it
follows that also
\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2}\rrbracket=\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{2}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{1}\rrbracket
.

Based on this reordering principle, we can prove three more interesting
results, which we will do in the next subsections.

6.1.
Swapping Signs and Dependency Loops

The first result (Theorem
28
) states that the sign of a variable

X
is only relevant if it depends on itself,
i.e.
,
X
is on a cycle in
\mathrel{\smash{\mathrel{\mathchoice{{\displaystyle\mathop{\hbox to19.83319pt{$\displaystyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\displaystyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}}}}{{\textstyle\mathop{\hbox to19.83319pt{$\textstyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\textstyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}}}}{{\scriptstyle\mathop{\hbox to18.04163pt{$\scriptstyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}}}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathop{\hbox to18.04163pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}}}}}\hskip-3.99994pt\raisebox{5.0pt}{\small+}}}
(recall that
\mathrel{\smash{\mathrel{\mathchoice{{\displaystyle\mathop{\hbox to19.83319pt{$\displaystyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\displaystyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}}}}{{\textstyle\mathop{\hbox to19.83319pt{$\textstyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\textstyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}}}}{{\scriptstyle\mathop{\hbox to18.04163pt{$\scriptstyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}}}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathop{\hbox to18.04163pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}}}}}\hskip-3.99994pt\raisebox{5.0pt}{\small+}}}
indicates a
non-empty path in the variable dependency graph).
We first need a couple of auxiliary lemmas:

Lemma 26
.

If
X\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 1.4pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}$}}{\raise 1.4pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}$}}{\raise 1.0pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}}\mathit{dom}(\mathcal{S})
and we have
\mathit{indep}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S},X)
as well as
\mathit{indep}(\mathcal{E},X,X)
,
then
\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mu X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}\rrbracket=\llbracket\mathcal{E},\nu X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}S\rrbracket
.

Proof 6.4
.

For
\sigma\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}\{\mu,\nu\}
and arbitrary valuation
\eta
, we have:

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathcal{E},\sigma X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta)

\displaystyle=\;

(by definition of semantics)

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=\sigma(F)])\mbox{, where}

\displaystyle\qquad\begin{aligned} F(P):=&\;\mathcal{E}_{X}(\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=P]))\\
=&\;\mbox{~~~~~(Lemma~\ref{indepenv}, and $X\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 1.4pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}$}}{\raise 1.4pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}$}}{\raise 1.0pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}}\mathit{dom}(\mathcal{S})$ and $\mathit{indep}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S},X)$}\\
&\;\mathcal{E}_{X}((\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket\eta)[X:=P])\\
=&\;\mbox{~~~~~(by definition of $\mathit{indep}(\mathcal{E},X,X)$)}\\
&\;\mathcal{E}_{X}(\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket\eta)\\
\end{aligned}

\displaystyle=\;

(constant rule, Lemma
1
.2)

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket(\eta[X:=\mathcal{E}_{X}(\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}\rrbracket\eta)])

So indeed
\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mu X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}\rrbracket=\llbracket\mathcal{E},\nu X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}\rrbracket
.

Lemma 27
.

If not
X\mathrel{\smash{\mathrel{\mathchoice{{\displaystyle\mathop{\hbox to31.72821pt{$\displaystyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\displaystyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mu X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}}}}{{\textstyle\mathop{\hbox to31.72821pt{$\textstyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\textstyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mu X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}}}}{{\scriptstyle\mathop{\hbox to28.5858pt{$\scriptstyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mu X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}}}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathop{\hbox to28.5858pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mu X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}}}}}\hskip-3.99994pt\raisebox{5.11108pt}{\small+}}}X
, and
X\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 1.4pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}$}}{\raise 1.4pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}$}}{\raise 1.0pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrel{{\notin}}}}\mathit{dom}(\mathcal{S})
, then

\llbracket\mathcal{E},\mu X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}\rrbracket=\llbracket\mathcal{E},\nu X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}\rrbracket
.

Proof 6.5
.

Let
\mathcal{S}_{1}
and
\mathcal{S}_{2}
be such that
\mathit{split}_{X,\mathcal{E}}(\sigma X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S})=(\mathcal{S}_{1},\sigma X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2})
, for
\sigma\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}\{\mu,\nu\}
.
Note that if not
\mathit{indep}(\mathcal{E},\mu X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2},X)
, then for some

Y\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.52972pt\hbox{${\scriptstyle\in}$}}{\raise 0.50458pt\hbox{${\scriptscriptstyle\in}$}}{\scriptscriptstyle\in}}\mathit{dom}(\mu X\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{2})
, by definition of

7.2.
Reordering Variables

Theorem
12
indicates that two adjacent variables with the
same sign may be interchanged. This theorem occurs already in

[
Mad97
, Lemma 3.21]
. For PBES it is repeated in
[
GW05a
, Lemma
21]
. However,
[
Mad97
,
GW05a
]
don’t give a full
proof, but refer to Bekič Lemma. In our proof, we show exactly how
Theorem
10
reduces to our version of Bekič Rule
(Lemma
1
.9).
In other works
[
Sei96
,
KNIU19
]
, adjacent variables
with the same sign are grouped in unordered
blocks
.
No claim is made about the correctness of such a definition.

Theorem
20
shows the inequality that arises when
interchanging adjacent variables with different sign. It occurs
already in
[
Mad97
, Lemma 3.23]
, but our proof is different. Our
proof depends on a probably new inequality in fixpoint calculus, which
we coin Bekič Inequality (Lemma
2
.4).

Theorem
30
is a new result. It states that in the special
case that
X
and
Y
are not on the same dependency loop, they can be
interchanged without modifying the solution. This generalizes

[
GW05a
, Lemma 19]
, which requires that the right-hand side of

Y
in
\mathcal{E}
is a constant,
i.e.
,
\mathit{indep}(\mathcal{E},Y,Z)
for all
Z
.

Finally, Theorem
18
in this form is new. It allows to
swap whole blocks of equations. Mader
[
Mad97
, Lemma 3.22]

claims a similar result, under the condition that both

\mathit{indep}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{2})
and
\mathit{indep}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}_{2},\mathcal{S}_{1})
. However,

[
GW05a
]
show a counter example to this. The repair
in
[
GW05a
, Lemma 22]
requires that
\mathcal{S}_{3}
is empty. We show
a stronger result: if
\mathcal{S}_{3}
is empty, only one of the requirements

\mathit{indep}(\mathcal{S}_{2},\mathcal{S}_{1})
or
\mathit{indep}(\mathcal{S}_{2},\mathcal{S}_{1})
is needed.

Notably, our result even applies to nonempty
\mathcal{S}_{3}
, provided we have

\mathit{indep}(\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{2}\mathop{\mathbf{\!;\!}}\mathcal{S}_{3})
(or its reverse), i.e.
\mathcal{S}_{1}
is also
independent on the variables in
\mathcal{S}_{3}
. Note that we allow arbitrary
(dependent) alternations within
\mathcal{S}_{1}
and
\mathcal{S}_{2}
, and even
\mathcal{S}_{2}

might depend on
\mathcal{S}_{1}
.
We lifted two other unnecessary restrictions:
surprisingly, this result doesn’t require monotonicity of
\mathcal{E}
. Also,
the results in
[
Mad97
,
GW05a
]
are for individual equations
only, while we can swap whole blocks at the same time.

We now show
an application of swapping blocks to reduce the number of
\mu/\nu
-alternations.

{exa}

Consider the following four Boolean Equation Systems:

\begin{array}[]{|c|c|c||c|}\hline\cr B_{3}&B_{4}&B_{5}&B_{6}\\
\hline\cr\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mu X&=&Y\\

\mu W&=&Z\\
\end{array}&\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mu X&=&Y\\
\nu Z&=&W\\
\mu Y&=&X\\
\mu W&=&Z\\
\end{array}&\begin{array}[]{rcl}\nu Z&=&W\\
\mu X&=&Y\\
\mu Y&=&X\\
\mu W&=&Z\\
\end{array}&\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mu X&=&Y\\
\mu Y&=&X\\
\mu W&=&Z\\

\end{array}\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

of two loops,
X\leftrightarrow Y
and

.

\mathit{indep}(\{X,Y\},\{Z,W\})
.

B_{3}
to
B_{5}

Theorem
12
cannot be applied, because the sign of

is
different from all the others.

Using Theorem
18
on individual equations, one can show

\llbracket B_{3}\rrbracket=\llbracket B_{4}\rrbracket

\mathit{indep}(Y,\{Z,W\})
. However, one
cannot derive
\llbracket B_{4}\rrbracket=\llbracket B_{5}\rrbracket

\mathit{indep}(X,\{Z,Y,W\})

\mathit{indep}(\{Z,Y,W\},X)

8.
Formalisation in Coq & PVS

We have formalised all of the above theory in both Coq
[
Ber08
,
S+23
]
and PVS
[
OS08
]
.
A replication artefact containing these proofs is available at
[
NvdP24
]
.
The formalized definitions and proofs follow the definitions and proof steps
in this paper quite closely.
Here, we highlight the main difference between the two formalisations.

In Coq, we captured the concepts of complete lattices and monotonic functions in typeclasses.
For these, we defined several typeclass instances, for example the product lattice and composition of monotonic functions.
In many cases, Coq is able to perform automatic typeclass resolution, saving us from manually proving monotonicity of complex functions, for example those in Lemma
2
.
Furthermore, Coq supports user-defined notation, allowing us to closely follow the notation used in the paper.
The proofs for showing decidability of
X\mathrel{\smash{{\mathrel{\mathchoice{{\displaystyle\mathop{\hbox to19.83319pt{$\displaystyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\displaystyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}}}}{{\textstyle\mathop{\hbox to19.83319pt{$\textstyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\textstyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}}}}{{\scriptstyle\mathop{\hbox to18.04163pt{$\scriptstyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}}}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathop{\hbox to18.04163pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathord{-}\mkern-9.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\mkern-3.0mu\mathord{-}\mkern-3.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-9.0mu\mathord{\rightarrow}$}}\limits^{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{S}}}}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\rightarrow\;}}}Y
are extensive, something that is not reflected in the paper.

Our PVS definitions and proofs were originally developed under PVS version 4.2, but could be ported to version 7.1 with minimal effort.
Contrary to Coq, PVS is built on classical logic and thus allows the law of excluded middle (for all propositions
P
, it holds
P\lor\neg P

9.
Conclusion

We provided several equalities and inequalities involving
a range of operations on fixpoint equation
systems (FES). We refer to Table
1
and
2

(Section
7
) for a summary of the theorems.
Lemmas
1
and
2
provide
a useful overview on equalities
and inequalities for nested fixed points in complete lattices.

We provided self-contained and detailed proofs of all results
and mechanised these proofs in two proof assistants, Coq and PVS.

By the generic nature of FES, these results carry over to other
formalisms such as Boolean equation systems (BES),
parity games (and variations thereof), and parameterised (first-order)
Boolean equation systems (PBES).
