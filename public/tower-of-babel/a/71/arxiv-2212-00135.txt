Answering Private Linear Queries Adaptively using the Common Mechanism
Yingtai Xiao 1 , Guanhong Wang 2 , Danfeng Zhang 1 , Daniel Kifer 1

Abstract
. When analyzing confidential data through a privacy filter, a data scientist often needs to decide which queries will best support their intended analysis. For example, an analyst may wish to study noisy two-way marginals in a dataset produced by a mechanism ℳ 1 {{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1} . But, if the data are relatively sparse, the analyst may choose to examine noisy one-way marginals, produced by a mechanism ℳ 2 {{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2} , instead. Since the choice of whether to use ℳ 1 {{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1} or ℳ 2 {{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2} is data-dependent, a typical differentially private workflow is to first split the privacy loss budget ρ \rho into two parts: ρ 1 \rho_{1} and ρ 2 \rho_{2} , then use the first part ρ 1 \rho_{1} to determine which mechanism to use, and the remainder ρ 2 \rho_{2} to obtain noisy answers from the chosen mechanism. In a sense, the first step seems wasteful because it takes away part of the privacy loss budget that could have been used to make the query answers more accurate. In this paper, we consider the question of whether the choice between ℳ 1 {{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1} and ℳ 2 {{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2} can be performed without wasting any privacy loss budget. For linear queries, we propose a method for decomposing ℳ 1 {{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1} and ℳ 2 {{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2} into three parts: (1) a mechanism ℳ ∗ {{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{*}}} that captures their shared information, (2) a mechanism ℳ 1 ′ {{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}^{\prime} that captures information that is specific to ℳ 1 {{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1} , (3) a mechanism ℳ 2 ′ {{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}^{\prime} that captures information that is specific to ℳ 2 {{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2} . Running ℳ ∗ {{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{*}}} and ℳ 1 ′ {{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}^{\prime} together is completely equivalent to running ℳ 1 {{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1} (both in terms of query answer accuracy and total privacy cost ρ \rho ). Similarly, running ℳ ∗ {{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{*}}} and ℳ 2 ′ {{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}^{\prime} together is completely equivalent to running ℳ 2 {{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2} . Since ℳ ∗ {{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{*}}} will be used no matter what, the analyst can use its output to decide whether to subsequently run ℳ 1 ′ {{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}^{\prime} (thus recreating the analysis supported by ℳ 1 {{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1} ) or ℳ 2 ′ {{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}^{\prime} (recreating the analysis supported by ℳ 2 {{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2} ), without wasting privacy loss budget.

1.
Introduction

Consider an analyst who is working with confidential demographics data through a differential privacy filter – the analyst poses queries and receives noisy answers. The analyst has a privacy loss budget
\rho
and wishes to use it to study age and race distributions in a region. The analyst wishes to get a noisy age by race marginal (115 age categories and 63 race categories based on the latest census, for a total of
63\times 115
cells). However, for small populations, this marginal would be sparse and the noise would swamp the data. In that case, the analyst could prefer two one-way marginals: one marginal on race and a separate marginal on age. The analyst does not know in advance whether the two-way race by age marginal (Option 1) is better for this region or if two one-way marginals (Option 2) are better.

In a typical workflow, the analyst would split the privacy loss budget
\rho
into two pieces
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
(with
\rho_{1}+\rho_{2}=\rho
). The first piece would be used to somehow determine which of the two options would provide a good signal to (privacy) noise ratio. For example, the analyst could ask for a noisy population total to make the decision, or could use the exponential mechanism
(
McSherry and
Talwar 2007
)
, which is a common technique for selecting among several options.
The remaining privacy loss budget
\rho_{2}
would be used to provide a noisy answer to the chosen option (either a noisy age by race marginal, or to the two one-way marginals).

Now, this procedure comes with some regret because, if the analyst had known in advance which option to pick, then the entire privacy loss budget
\rho
(instead of only
\rho_{2}
) could have been used to provide a noisy marginal, providing more accuracy. Thus the analyst may feel that
\rho_{1}
, the portion of the privacy loss budget used to select between the two options, was wasted or lost.

In this paper, we consider the question of how the analyst can choose between Options 1 and 2 so that no privacy loss budget is lost, and the entire
\rho
is spent on the chosen analysis. Suppose
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
is the mechanism used to provide noisy answers in Option 1 with privacy budget
\rho
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
is the mechanism used to provide noisy answers in Option 2 with privacy budget
\rho
. We show how to split
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
into two mechanisms
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{*}}}
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}^{\prime}
, so that running
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
with privacy budget
\rho
is completely equivalent to running
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{*}}}
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}^{\prime}
together. Similarly, we split
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
into
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{*}}}
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}^{\prime}
. This “common” mechanism
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{*}}}
represents information that is common to both
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
. That is, this is a piece of information that would be provided to the analyst by either mechanism. Meanwhile, the “residual”
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}^{\prime}
encapsulates information that is specific to
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
(one can think of
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}^{\prime}
as the result of removing the information in
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{*}}}
from
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
). Similarly,
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}^{\prime}
is the information that is specific to
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
.
The analyst’s workflow becomes the following.

1.

Given a dataset
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}
, first run
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{*}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}})
to get an output
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}}_{*}
.

2a.

Based on
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}}_{*}
, the analyst can choose to run the residual mechanism
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}^{\prime}_{1}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}})
to get an output
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}}^{\prime}_{1}
,

2b.

Or, based on
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}}_{*}
, the analyst can instead run the residual mechanism
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}^{\prime}_{2}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}})
to get an output
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}}_{2}^{\prime}
.

If the analyst runs
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{*}}}
followed by
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}^{\prime}
, the total privacy budget spent is
\rho
and the resulting outputs
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}}_{*}
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}}^{\prime}_{1}
provide the same information as if
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
had been run with privacy budget
\rho
. Similarly, if the analyst runs
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{*}}}
followed by
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}^{\prime}
, then the total privacy budget spent again is
\rho
and the resulting outputs
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}}_{*}
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}}^{\prime}_{2}
provide the same information as running
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
with privacy budget
\rho
.
Thus, the analyst adaptively chooses which mechanism to run without any wasted privacy budget.

Our technique works for any mechanism that answers linear queries with Gaussian noise. It is compatible with Renyi differential privacy
(
Mironov 2017
)
, zCDP
(
Bun and Steinke 2016
)
, Gaussian Differential Privacy
(
Dong
et al. 2022
)
,
(\epsilon,\delta)
-differential privacy
(
Dwork et al. 2006a
)
, and personal differential privacy
(
Ebadi
et al. 2015
)
.

This kind of scenario, where an analyst needs to choose between pre-specified analyses depending on which provides an appropriate signal to (privacy) noise ratio, is expected to become more common. For example, the 2020 Census Detailed Demographic and Housing Characteristics data products are going to include sex-by-age tabulations where the binning of age in a region is data-dependent
(
Haney et al. 2021
)
. For small regions, only the population totals will be published. For more populous regions, the age will be binned into 4, 9, or 23 buckets, depending on how populous the region is. To choose which bucketization to use, noise will be first added to the population in a region
(
Haney et al. 2021
)
. This noisy count will be checked against manually-specified thresholds to determine the buckets to use. We empirically show that our proposed approach is better at selecting the correct analysis, removes the need for manual tuning, and uses all of the privacy loss budget on the sex-by-age histograms instead of taking away some of it for the purposes of selecting the histogram to use.

The contributions of this paper are the following:

•

We provide a framework for adaptively choosing between two linear mechanisms
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
without requiring additional expenditure of privacy loss budget.

•

We formalize the definition of the common mechanism
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{*}}}
of
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
as an optimization problem, and also formalize the associated residual mechanisms
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}^{\prime},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}^{\prime}
. This framework can be extended to the case of multiple mechanisms (e.g.,
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{3},\dots
), but the optimization problem for the common mechanism has an analytical solution when dealing with two mechanisms.

•

We provide algorithms for computing the common and residual mechanisms.

•

We give suggestions on how to use the output of the common mechanism to decide between
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
, thus providing another tool for the construction of differentially private systems.

•

We demonstrate the efficacy of this approach using real datasets and apply it to a real-world application involving census data.

2.
Notation and Background

In this section, we explain our notation (summarized in Table
1
) and provide background information on differential privacy and the type of mechanisms we consider.

We denote vectors as bold lower-case letters (e.g.,
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}
), matrices as bold upper-case (e.g.,
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{B}}}
), scalars as non-bold lower-case (e.g.,
\sigma
). If
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
are positive semidefinite matrices, we say
\mathbf{B}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\preceq}}\mathbf{A}
if
\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{B}
is positive semidefinite (
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\succeq}}
is defined analogously). The relation
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\preceq}}
defines a partial ordering on semidefinite matrices called the
Loewner
order
(
Horn and Johnson 2012
)
.

A
dataset

{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}
is a table of records.
Following earlier work on differentially private linear queries
(
Yuan
et al. 2016
;
Li
et al. 2015
;
Yuan et al. 2012
;
Xiao
et al. 2021
)
, we assume the record attributes are categorical (or have been discretized). As in prior work, we represent the dataset
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}
as a vector
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}
of counts and we refer to it as the
data vector
. That is, letting
\{t_{0},t_{1},\dots t_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d}}-1}\}
be the set of possible record values,
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}[i]
is the number of times record
t_{i}
appears in
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}
.
For example, if each record consists of two attributes,
adult
(yes/no) and
Hispanic
(yes/no), then there are 4 possible types of records, which are
t_{0}=
"not adult, not Hispanic",
t_{1}=
“adult, not Hispanic”,
t_{2}=
“not adult, Hispanic”,
t_{3}=
“adult, Hispanic”. In our representation,
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}[3]
is the number of Hispanic adults in the dataset
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}
.

We say that two dataset vectors
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}^{\prime}
are
neighboring
(denoted as
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}\sim{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}^{\prime}
) if
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}
can be obtained from
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}^{\prime}
by adding or subtracting 1 from some component of
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}^{\prime}
(this means
||{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}-{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}^{\prime}||_{1}=1
) – this is the same as adding or removing 1 person from the underlying dataset.

A single
linear query

{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{q}}}
is a vector, whose answer is
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{q}}}\cdot{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}
.
A
query set
is a set of
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}m}}
linear queries represented by an
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}m}}\times{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d}}
matrix
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{B}}}
, where each row corresponds to a single linear query. We let
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}k}}
denote the rank of
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{B}}}
. The answers to the queries are obtained by matrix multiplication:
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{B}}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}
.
Continuing our running example of a two-attribute dataset, if
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{B}}}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1&0&1\\
1&1&1&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)
, then this is a set of two queries. The first query is the number of times records
t_{1}
or
t_{3}
appear in the dataset (i.e., the number of adults) and the second query is the total number of people.

A
mechanism

{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}
is an algorithm whose input is the confidential data (either
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}
or
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}
) and whose output
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}}
is considered safe to release (because it protects privacy). When we have two mechanisms
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{a}
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{b}
, we let
({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{a},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{b})
denote the mechanism that runs both of them on the data and releases their results. In other words, its output is
({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{a}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}),{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{b}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}))
.

2.1.
Differential Privacy

Differential Privacy
(
Dwork
et al. 2006b
;
Dwork et al. 2006a
;
Bun and Steinke 2016
;
Mironov 2017
;
Dong
et al. 2022
)
is a family of privacy definitions that place restrictions on how a mechanism
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}
can work. It has become a de facto standard for protecting confidentiality when creating publicly available data products, with an ever-increasing list of real-world deployments, including the U.S. Census Bureau
(
Machanavajjhala et al. 2008
;
Bureau [n.d.]b
;
Abowd et al. html
)
, Uber
(
Johnson
et al. 2018a
;
Johnson
et al. 2018b
)
, Apple
(
Team 2017
)
, Facebook
(
Messing et al. 2018
;
Opacus [n.d.]
)
, Microsoft
(
Ding
et al. 2017
)
, and Google
(
Erlingsson
et al. 2014
;
Google [n.d.]
;
Wilson et al. 2020
)
. Differential privacy provides a rigorous plausible deniability guarantee – it limits the ability of an attacker to determine whether a target person’s record was in the dataset or not. The most common variation of differential privacy is:

Definition 2.1 (Approximate Differential Privacy
(
Dwork et al. 2006a
)
).

Given privacy parameters
\epsilon>0
and
\delta\in(0,1)
, a randomized algorithm
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}
satisfies
(\epsilon,\delta)
-differential privacy if for all pairs of neighboring dataset vectors
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}^{\prime}
and all sets
S
, the following equations hold:

\displaystyle P({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}})\in S)\leq e^{\epsilon}P({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}^{\prime})\in S)+\delta

A mechanism
M
typically satisfies approximate differential privacy for infinitely many
(\epsilon,\delta)
pairs, which defines a curve in space. An example curve is shown in Figure
1
.

Figure 1.
Example of an
(\epsilon,\delta)
curve for the mechanism
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}})=\mathbf{1}^{T}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}+N(0,1)
.

There is also another popular variant known as
zero-concentrated differential privacy
(zCDP):

Definition 2.2 (zCDP
(
Bun and Steinke 2016
)
).

Given a privacy parameters
\rho>0
, a randomized algorithm
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}
satisfies
\rho
-zCDP if for all pairs of neighboring dataset vectors
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}^{\prime}
and all numbers
\alpha>1
,

\displaystyle\int_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}}P({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}})={{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}})\frac{P({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}})={{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}})^{\alpha-1}}{P({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}^{\prime})={{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}})^{\alpha-1}}~d{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}}\leq e^{(\alpha-1)\alpha\rho},

where
P({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}})={{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}})
is interpreted as a probability density function in the continuous case.

The parameters
(\epsilon,\delta)
are known as the
privacy loss budget
of approximate differential privacy and the privacy parameter
\rho
is known as the privacy loss budget of zCDP. Note that the privacy parameter of zCDP is difficult to interpret (see
(
Kifer et al. 2022
)
for an extended discussion) but easier to compute than approximate differential privacy. Thus, one typically determines the zCDP privacy loss parameter
\rho
of a mechanism
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}
and then converts it to
\epsilon
and
\delta
for interpretability
(
Bun and Steinke 2016
;
Canonne
et al. 2020
)
.

Each version of differential privacy also has a “personalized” version, in which each possible record type
t_{i}
is assigned a privacy loss budget. The privacy parameter for record type
t_{i}
can be obtained from Definition
2.1
or
2.2
by considering only neighbors
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}^{\prime}
that differ in their
i^{\text{th}}
coordinate
(
Ebadi
et al. 2015
)
.

2.2.
The Linear Gaussian Mechanism

The linear Gaussian mechanism adds Gaussian noise to the output of linear queries and is compatible with many versions of differential privacy. It is defined as follows.

Definition 2.3 (Linear Gaussian Mechanism).

Given a
query matrix

{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{B}}}
and nonsingular
covariance matrix

{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{\Sigma}}}
(not necessarily diagonal), the linear Gaussian mechanism
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}
is defined as
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}})={{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{B}}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}+N(\mathbf{0},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{\Sigma}}})
. The quantity
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{B}}}^{T}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{\Sigma}}}^{-1}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{B}}}
is called the
privacy cost matrix
of
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}
.

The importance of the privacy cost matrix is that the privacy parameters of the Gaussian mechanism for
(\epsilon,\delta)
-differential privacy and
\rho
-zCDP (both the basic and personalized versions) are all functions of this privacy cost matrix, as the following result shows:
1

(
Mironov 2017
)
and Gaussian differential privacy

Lemma 2.4 (
(

Let
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}
be the linear Gaussian mechanism (Definition
2.3
) defined as
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}})={{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{B}}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}+N(\mathbf{0},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{\Sigma}}})
with privacy cost matrix
\mathbf{C}={{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{B}}}^{T}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{\Sigma}}}^{-1}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{B}}}
. Let
c_{i}
be the
i^{\text{th}}
diagonal entry of
\mathbf{C}
and let
c_{max}=\max_{i}c_{i}
be the largest diagonal entry of
\mathbf{C}
. Let
\Phi
be the CDF of the standard normal distribution. Then:

•

{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}
satisfies

-differential privacy for

\delta=\Phi\left(\frac{\sqrt{c_{max}}}{2}-\frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{c_{max}}}\right)-e^{\epsilon}\Phi\left(-\frac{\sqrt{c_{max}}}{2}-\frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{c_{max}}}\right)

and
\delta
is an increasing function of
c_{max}
. In particular, this means the entire
(\epsilon,\delta)
curve of
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}
is determined by
c_{max}
.

•

The personalized approximate differential privacy parameters

3.
Problem Definition and Solution Overview

The motivation for our problem is the following. An analyst is interested in obtaining noisy linear query answers either from mechanism
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
or
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
, defined as follows:

\displaystyle{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}})

\displaystyle={{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{B}}}_{1}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}+N(0,{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{1})

\displaystyle{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}})

\displaystyle={{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{B}}}_{2}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}+N(0,{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{2}).

where
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
both satisfy zCDP with the same privacy parameter
\rho
.
2
2

2

For concreteness, we focus on zCDP, but, as we show later, this approach works for any post-processing invariant privacy definition.

For example,
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
could compute all the one-way marginals of a dataset, and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
could compute all the two-way marginals. However, the choice of which mechanism to use depends on the properties of the data that the analyst does not know.

For instance, if the dataset is “large enough” then the noisy two-way marginals (i.e.,
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
) will be very accurate with low relative error. Otherwise, the analyst would prefer to use the noisy one-way marginals produced by
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
. The problem is that the analyst does not know whether the dataset is “large enough”, or even how to precisely define what “large enough” means (i.e., exactly how many records are needed for the dataset to be considered large enough).

Thus, the analyst needs extra information about the data in order to make a choice between
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
. One option is to take some privacy budget
\rho_{0}
away from
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
. This
\rho_{0}
would be assigned to some other mechanism
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}^{\circ}
that queries that data. Based on the answers to
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}^{\circ}
, the analyst would modify either
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
or
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
to use the remaining privacy budget
\rho-\rho_{0}
(by rescaling the Gaussian covariance matrices
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{1}
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{2}
) and then run it on the data. This option produces noisier answers than the analyst wants, because only
\rho-\rho_{0}
instead of
\rho
is allocated towards the noisy answers. In this sense, this
\rho_{0}
can feel like a wasted expenditure of privacy loss budget since it takes away from the accuracy of the query answers that the analyst desires.

In this paper, we consider a second option – whether the analyst can make an informed choice without wasting any privacy loss budget. The main idea is to rewrite mechanism
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
as a sequence of two mechanisms
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{*}}}
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}^{\prime}
such that running
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
is exactly equivalent, both in terms of information content and privacy cost, to running
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{*}}}
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}^{\prime}
together. We similarly decompose
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
into
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{*}}}
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}^{\prime}
. This means that if the analyst wants to run
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
or
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
, then
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{*}}}
is going to be run no matter what.
Thus the analyst can first run
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{*}}}
and then can decide whether to run
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}^{\prime}
(to finish the execution of
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
) or to run
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}^{\prime}
(to finish the execution of
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
).
We refer to
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{*}
as the mechanism that is
common
to
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
since it represents the information that they share. We refer to
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}^{\prime}
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}^{\prime}
as the
residual
mechanisms since they represent the information that is specific to
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
, respectively.

We next formalize this discussion. We examine what it means for two mechanisms to be equivalent in Section
3.1
, we formally define the common mechanism of
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
in Section
3.2
, we formally define the residual mechanisms in Section
3.3
, and then we present the technical problem statement in Section
3.4
. We believe this technique may have wide applicability and discuss these possibilities, some of which are future work, in Section
3.5
.

3.1.
Answerability and Equivalence of Linear Gaussian Mechanisms

Suppose there are two mechanisms
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{a}
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{b}
and a randomized postprocessing algorithm
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{A}}}
such that for all data vectors
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}
, the output distribution of
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{b}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}})
is the same as the output distribution of
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{A}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{a}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}))
. This means that we can simulate the output of
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{b}
by taking the output of
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{a}
and feeding it to
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{A}}}
.

When this is the case, we say that
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{b}
is
answerable
from
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{a}
and it means that
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{a}
produces at least as much information as
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{b}
. For any post-processing invariant privacy definition (such as
\rho
-zCDP, Gaussian differential privacy, approximate differential privacy, etc.), the privacy cost of
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{a}
is also at least as large as the privacy cost of
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{b}
(e.g., the
\rho
parameter of
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{a}
under zCDP is greater than or equal to the
\rho
parameter of
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{b}
).

For linear Gaussian mechanisms, answerability can be defined as follows.

Definition 3.1.

Let
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{a}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}})={{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{B}}}_{a}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}+N(0,{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{a})
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{b}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}})={{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{B}}}_{b}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}+N(0,{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{b})
be linear Gaussian mechanisms. We say that
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{b}
is answerable from
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{a}
if there exist matrices
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{C}
such that for every
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}
,
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{b}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}})
has the same distribution as
\mathbf{A}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{a}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}})+\mathbf{C}N(0,\mathbf{I})
, where
\mathbf{I}
is the identity matrix. In other words,
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{b}
can be obtained from
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{a}
by applying a linear transformation and adding additional noise (that does
not
depend on the data).

For linear Gaussian mechanisms, answerability is easy to check using the following result whose proof is straightforward.

Lemma 3.2.

Let
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{a}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}})={{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{B}}}_{a}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}+N(0,{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{a})
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{b}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}})={{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{B}}}_{b}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}+N(0,{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{b})
be linear Gaussian mechanisms.
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{b}
is answerable from
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{a}
if and only if there exist matrices
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{C}
such that:

\displaystyle{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{B}}}_{b}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{A}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{B}}}_{a}

\displaystyle{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{b}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{A}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{a}\mathbf{A}^{T}+\mathbf{C}\mathbf{C}^{T}

Remark 3.4.

It is particularly noteworthy that even though
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{a}
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{b}
in Example
3.3
are answerable from each other,
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{a}
is obtained from
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{b}
by linear postprocessing
followed by noise addition
. The reason is because the rows of the query matrix
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{B}}}_{a}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\
1&0\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right]
in mechanism
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{a}
are
linearly dependent
. The sole purpose of the noise is to convert the 2-dimensional Gaussian distribution obtained by linear postprocessing of
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{b}
into the 3-dimensional Gaussian distribution that
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{a}
uses. This noise does not add or remove privacy, since
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{b}
can also be obtained from
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{a}
by linear postprocessing.

Another way to view this phenomenon is to note that the linear dependency in
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{B}}}_{a}
causes inconsistency – the first component of the output of
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{a}
is a noisy sum, and the sum of the 2nd and 3rd components is also a version of the noisy sum. Enforcing consistency
(
Hay
et al. 2010
;
Li
et al. 2015
)
would convert
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{a}
into
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{b}
, and the noise that is removed by consistency is the same noise that is added back when reconstructing
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{a}
from
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{b}
.

3.2.
Shared Information and Common Mechanisms

Intuitively, a piece of information is shared by
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
if that information can be derived from the output of
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}

and
it can also be derived from the output of
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
.

We formalize “information” as a mechanism
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{c}
that can be answered from
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
and from
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
. Such a mechanism is called a
common
mechanism of
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
.

Definition 3.7 (Common Mechanism).

A mechanism
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{c}
is common to
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
if
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{c}
is answerable from
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
and is answerable from
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
. When they are all linear Gaussian mechanisms:

\displaystyle{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}})

\displaystyle={{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{B}}}_{1}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}+N(\mathbf{0},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{1})

\displaystyle{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}})

\displaystyle={{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{B}}}_{2}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}+N(\mathbf{0},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{2})

\displaystyle{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{c}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}})

\displaystyle={{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{B}}}_{c}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}+N(\mathbf{0},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{c})

then by Theorem
3.5
,{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{c}
is common to
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}

3.4.
Formalizing Decision Making with the Common Mechanism

Having formally defined the, (maximally) common mechanism
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{*}}}
of linear Gaussian mechanisms
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
, and having defined the residual mechanisms
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}^{\prime}
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}^{\prime}
, the formal problem statement can be defined as the following sequence of steps:

(1)

Given two linear Gaussian query mechanisms
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
, compute their maximal common mechanism
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{*}}}
(algorithms are provided in Section
5
).

(2)

Given
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{*}}}
, compute the residual mechanisms
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}^{\prime}
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}^{\prime}
(algorithms are provided in Section
5
).

(3)

Run
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{*}}}
on the data to produce an output
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}}_{*}
.

(4)

Based on
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}}_{*}
, decide whether to run the residual mechanism
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}^{\prime}
or
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}^{\prime}
. An analyst is free to choose how to make a decision based on
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}}^{*}
, but for more automated approaches, we provide suggestions in Section
6
.

(5)

Based on the decision, either run
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}^{\prime}
on the data (and combine the result with
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}}_{*}
to obtain an answer to
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
) or run
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}^{\prime}
on the data (and combine the result with
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}}_{*}
to obtain an answer to
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
). Algorithms for recovering
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}

({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{*}}},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}^{\prime})

3.5.
Additional Potential Uses

We believe that the idea of explicitly factoring out information that is common to a set of mechanisms can be used widely as another tool in differential privacy applications. While we describe one application in this paper, there are additional possibilities for future work that we describe here.

3.5.1.
Reducing privacy cost for systems that interact with analysts.

When a data analyst queries a differentially private system in a sequential manner, the system may wish to use previously cached noisy answers to help answer new queries, thus reducing the privacy cost of answering the new queries. This is one of the goals of systems such as Apex
(
Ge
et al. 2019
;
Mazmudar
et al. 2020
)
. The use of common mechanisms could help such systems achieve their goals by factoring out what is common between the new queries and previously asked queries, computing the residual mechanism for the new queries. The residual mechanism captures the information in the new queries that are not contained in the previous queries. The system would then only need to expend the privacy budget on the residual mechanism instead of the entire set of new queries.

3.5.2.
Enhancing the exponential mechanism.

When one query needs to be privately selected from a set
\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{q}}}_{1},\dots,{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{q}}}_{k}\}
of
k
queries, the exponential mechanism
(
McSherry and
Talwar 2007
)
can often be used for this purpose. When the queries have common substructures, part of the privacy budget for the exponential mechanism can be used to select among the substructures, answer them, and then use this information and the remaining privacy budget to design an exponential mechanism quality function for choosing among the residual mechanisms. This possibility is an area of future work.

3.5.3.
Synthetic data generation.

There are several ways that the common mechanism can be used to enhance iterative algorithms for generating synthetic data (e.g.,
(
Hardt
et al. 2012
;
Liu
et al. 2021a
;
Aydore et al. 2021
;
McKenna
et al. 2022
)
) that first privately select queries and then use their noisy answers to update a data synopsis. First, one can take mechanisms that are common to subsets of the original queries, and add them to the list of queries that can be selected in each round. Another use is to take the previously selected queries from earlier rounds and to “remove” their information from the remaining queries (via residual mechanisms). For example, if a one-way marginal Age had been answered in an earlier round, any information answerable from this marginal could be removed from the remaining queries (i.e., replaced by query matrices of residual mechanisms), so that the remaining queries focus on information that has not yet been provided. Thus integrating different uses of the common mechanism framework into synthetic data generation is another direction for future work.

, data pre-processing
(

)
, PAC learning
(

Query selection can be performed in a
data-independent
way, meaning that the queries are chosen in advance and no privacy loss budget is spent on the choice. In the case of linear queries, the techniques that plan the optimal set of queries in advance are generally called
matrix mechanisms

(
Li
et al. 2015
;
McKenna et al. 2018
;
Yuan
et al. 2016
;
Yuan et al. 2012
;
Yuan et al. 2015
;
McKenna et al. 2018
;
Xiao
et al. 2021
)
. Matrix mechanisms
implicitly
take advantage of shared information between queries by setting up an optimization problem that finds a query strategy matrix that can answer the prespecified queries as accurately as possible under a privacy constraint.

Query selection can also be performed in a data-dependent way by allocating some of the privacy loss budget to specially designed selection mechanisms. The sparse vector technique
(
Dwork and Roth 2014
;
Lyu et al. 2017
)
, exponential mechanism
(
McSherry and
Talwar 2007
)
and various generalizations
(
Steinke and
Ullman 2017
;
Ligett
et al. 2017
;
Beimel
et al. 2016
;
Thakurta and
Smith 2013
;
Raskhodnikova and
Smith 2016
;
Chaudhuri
et al. 2014
;
Liu and Talwar 2018
;
Ding
et al. 2019
;
Ding
et al. 2022
)
are commonly used for this task, along with bespoke algorithms targeted at specific applications
(
Zhang
et al. 2014
;
Acs
et al. 2012
;
Xu
et al. 2013
;
Li
et al. 2014
)
.

Head-to-head comparisons between data-dependent and data-independent methods show that there is no clear winner
(
Hay et al. 2016
)
– in some situations, data-dependent selection provides the best choice of queries. In other situations, data-independent methods prevail. Thus it is important to keep expanding the available toolkit for query selection.

There is also a much smaller class of zero-waste differentially private selection algorithms
(
Xiao
et al. 2011
;
Koufogiannis
et al. 2015
;
Li
et al. 2011
;
Xiao
et al. 2009
)
whose purpose is to adaptively determine how much noise to add to a query, without wasting privacy loss budget. For example, suppose one is interested in the count of the number of people over 18. An analyst is prepared to spend up to
\epsilon_{1}
of her privacy loss budget (using pure
\epsilon
-differential privacy) to get the answer. But, if the true number is large, she would prefer to use a smaller privacy loss budget value
\epsilon_{2}
(this results in a higher absolute error but is tolerable when the true answer is large because it still results in a small relative error). NoiseDown, which was introduced by Xiao et al.
(
Xiao
et al. 2011
)
(but had a bug in the algorithm) and later corrected by Koufogiannis et al.
(
Koufogiannis
et al. 2015
)
, is one technique to solve this problem. The analyst adds Laplace noise with privacy budget
\epsilon_{2}
to the true answer. Based on this answer, the analyst can either keep it or refine the noise so that the total privacy loss budget is
\epsilon_{1}
and so that the accuracy is the same as if she had used Laplace noise with budget
\epsilon_{1}
in the first place. Variations for this single-query noise refinement were also studied for randomized response
(
Xiao
et al. 2009
)
and Gaussian noise
(
Li
et al. 2011
)
.

Our approach is also zero-waste, while being much more general. Instead of choosing between two versions of the same mechanism (the only difference being its privacy cost/amount of noise), our method allows the choice between two or more linear mechanisms that use Gaussian noise (and the mechanisms may also have different privacy costs). This adds another tool to the algorithmic toolbox for differential privacy. Although not necessarily a replacement for the exponential mechanism, we show empirically that our approach is useful in situations where it is difficult to specify the quality function that the exponential mechanism needs. One direction of future work is to combine the exponential mechanism with our technique – using the output of the common mechanism to fine-tune the construction of the exponential mechanism.

6.
Making decisions based on the common mechanism.

We next consider how one could use the output
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}}_{*}
of the common mechanism to decide whether to run the residual mechanism
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}^{\prime}
in order to do the analysis supported by
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
, or whether to run
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}^{\prime}
instead. In general, this would be user/application dependent, but we list some suggestions here. We first consider the general case where
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
are arbitrary linear Gaussian mechanisms in Section
6.1
and nested analyses, such as 1-way vs. 2-way marginals, in Section
6.2

6.1.
The General Case

One can use the answer
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}}_{*}
of the common mechanism to estimate how much potential variability there can be in the data
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}
and compare it to the amount of noise that
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
or
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
would add. One would then choose between them based on the potential signal-to-noise ratio. For example, if the amount of noise required by
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
(resp.,
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
) is larger than the range of potential values for
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{B}}}_{1}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}
(resp.,
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{B}}}_{2}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}
), then it would not be worthwhile to choose
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
(resp.,
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
).

Given the output
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}}_{*}
of the common mechanism, one can do the following postprocessing to estimate a range of possible datasets that are compatible with
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}}_{*}
.
First, one would generate a set of random vectors
\mathbf{c}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{c}_{r}
. For each
\mathbf{c}_{i}
, one would solve the following optimization problem (in Equation
2
) to get an estimate
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{i}
for a potential dataset, and then one would compute
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{B}}}_{1}\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{i}
. By computing the variance of all of these values
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{B}}}_{1}\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{1},\dots,{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{B}}}_{1}\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{r}
one would see the range of possible answers to the query matrix
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{B}}}_{1}
of
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
, and by comparing it to the (total variance of the) covariance matrix
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{1}
, one would determine whether the noise would mask these variations (meaning that nothing useful would be learned from a noisy answer).

6.2.
Nested Analyses

Another typical use-case occurs when analyses are nested. For example, an analyst may be interested in a primary analysis involving one-way marginals. However, there might also be interest in performing a secondary analysis using two-way marginals. This is an example of
nesting
because one-way marginals can be computed from two-way marginals.

When an analyst is only allowed to obtain marginals that are noisy, an important decision must be made.

•

If the analyst requests noisy one-way marginals via a mechanism
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}})={{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{B}}}_{1}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}+N(\mathbf{0},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{1})
then the analyst gets the most accurate noisy information needed for their primary analysis, but no secondary analysis can be performed.

•

If the analyst requests noisy two-way marginals via a mechanism
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}})={{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{B}}}_{2}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}+N(\mathbf{0},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{2})
. This would allow the secondary analysis to proceed. Since noisy two-way marginals can be used to compute noisier one-way marginals (these one-way marginals will be noisier than in the first option), then the primary analysis can also be performed if there was enough signal-to-noise ratio (low relative error).

Here we define the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) as the true (unknown) count of a cell divided by the standard deviation of the privacy noise applied to it.
Thus, if noisy two-way marginals have enough signal-to-noise ratio to perform the primary analysis, then

{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
is preferable since
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
also has additional uses. Otherwise,
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
would be preferable. The goal is for the analyst to use the output of the common mechanism to estimate what the SNR of each marginal cell would be if
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
or
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
were used.

We say that the analysis supported by query matrix
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{B}}}_{1}
(e.g., one-way marginals) is nested in the analysis supported by
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{B}}}_{2}
(e.g., two-way marginals) if there exists a matrix
\mathbf{A}
such that
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{B}}}_{1}=\mathbf{A}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{B}}}_{2}
. In this case,
\mathbf{A}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}})
(i.e, multiplying the output of
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
by
\mathbf{A}
) is a noisier version of
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
. We say that
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{B}}}_{1}
is the primary analysis and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{B}}}_{2}
is the secondary analysis.

Now, the common mechanism
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{*}}}
of
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
can provide a noisy answer to any query in the intersection of the row spaces of
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{B}}}_{1}
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{B}}}_{2}
(by Theorem
5.2
) and so there exists a matrix
\mathbf{A}_{*}
such that
E[{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}})]=E[\mathbf{A}_{*}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{*}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}})]
, which means that
\mathbf{A}_{*}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{*}}}
is also a noisier version of
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
, and the variance of the
i^{\text{th}}
query is the
i^{\text{th}}
diagonal element of
\mathbf{A}_{*}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{*}}}\mathbf{A}_{*}^{T}
, denoted by
(\mathbf{A}_{*}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{*}}}\mathbf{A}_{*}^{T})[i,i]
. Thus the output
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}}_{*}
of
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{*}}}
can be used to estimate the signal to noise ratio of using mechanism
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
to do the nested primary analysis as follows (all of this is a postprocessing of
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}}_{*}
):

•

The quantity
L_{i}=(\mathbf{A}_{*}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}}_{*})[i]-3\sqrt{(\mathbf{A}_{*}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{*}}}\mathbf{A}_{*}^{T})[i,i]}
is a 3 sigma lower confidence interval for the true value of the
i^{\text{th}}
query in
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{B}}}_{1}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}
. The quantity
U_{i}=(\mathbf{A}_{*}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}}_{*})[i]+3\sqrt{(\mathbf{A}_{*}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{*}}}\mathbf{A}_{*}^{T})[i,i]}
is the corresponding upper confidence interval.

•

The quantity
SNR\_Lower^{(1)}_{i}=\frac{L_{i}}{\sqrt{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{1}[i,i]}}
is a lower bound on the expected signal-to-noise ratio of using
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
to get a noisy answer to the
i^{\text{th}}
query in
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{B}}}_{1}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}
. Similarly,
SNR\_Upper^{(1)}_{i}=\frac{U_{i}}{\sqrt{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{1}[i,i]}}
would be an upper bound on the signal-to-noise ratio.

•

The quantity
SNR\_Lower^{(2)}_{i}=\frac{L_{i}}{\sqrt{(\mathbf{A}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{2}\mathbf{A}^{T})[i,i]}}
(resp.,

SNR\_Upper^{(2)}_{i}=\frac{U_{i}}{\sqrt{(\mathbf{A}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{2}\mathbf{A}^{T})[i,i]}}
) is a lower (resp., upper) bound on the expected signal-to-noise ratio of using
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
to get a noisy answer to the
i^{\text{th}}
query in
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{B}}}_{1}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}
(the nested analysis).

These quantities can be used in a variety of ways. For example, the user may want at least
x\%
of the queries of
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{B}}}_{1}
to have SNR above
y
. In this case, if
x\%
of the
SNR\_Lower^{(2)}_{i}
values are
\geq y
, then
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
is good enough for the primary analysis and also provides an opportunity to perform the secondary analysis. Thus, the user should decide to run the residual mechanism
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}^{\prime}_{2}
to get the answer to
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
.

Other possibilities also exist. If too many of the
SNR\_Upper^{(1)}_{i}
values are below the desired signal-to-noise ratio
y
, then even
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
is not accurate enough for the primary analysis and the analyst can stop here, without using any further privacy budget beyond what
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{*}}}
had cost. On the other hand, if the SNR bound
y
is between
SNR\_Lower^{(2)}_{i}
and
SNR\_Upper^{(2)}_{i}
for many
i
, then the analyst also has the option of using more privacy budget to help make the decision between
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
, or may opt for
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
just to be safe.

7.2.
Evaluation Measures

We consider the nested analysis setting (Section
6.2
) in which the primary analysis is represented by
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}})={{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{B}}}_{1}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}+N(\mathbf{0},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{1})
. The secondary analysis is represented by
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}})={{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{B}}}_{2}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}+N(\mathbf{0},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{2})
. Thus, the primary analysis can also be done, but less accurately, with the output of
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
. However,
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
supports additional analyses that
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
cannot.

The output
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}}_{*}
of the common mechanism is used to estimate whether the output of
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
is accurate enough to perform the primary analysis – if at least
x\%
of the marginal cells are believed to have an SNR at least
y
and then the residual mechanism
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}^{\prime}
is run to recreate the output of
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
without wasting privacy loss budget (otherwise the residual mechanism
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}^{\prime}
is used). An
alternate approach
, that doesn’t use the common mechanism, is to reserve some privacy budget to estimate the SNR (by getting a coarse noisy answer to
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{B}}}_{1}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}
and then using that noisy answer to estimate SNR as in Section
6.2
) and then run either
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
or
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
. The alternate approach uses the
optimal
Gaussian mechanism
(
Xiao
et al. 2021
)
that matches the variance of the common mechanism (for an apples-to-apples comparison) while minimizing privacy cost. We measure the following quantities:

•

\rho
: the concentrated differential privacy parameter (zcdp)
(
Bun and Steinke 2016
)
that represents the total privacy loss budget.

•

\%{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
,
\%{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
: based on the ground truth (computed from the true count divided by noise std), the percent of the time that
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
(resp.,
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
) should have been chosen. A good decision-making strategy should outperform the maximum of these two. In Section
7.5
, where we must choose between 4 mechanisms, we report
\%{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
,
\%{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
,
\%{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{3}
,
\%{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{4}
.

•

Acc
: accuracy of the selection based on the common mechanism. This is the percentage of time that the correct mechanism has been chosen without wasting any privacy budget.

•

\%
PLB Saved
: this is how much privacy loss budget the optimal alternate approach needs to allocate to the estimation of SNR in order to match the estimation quality of the common mechanism. This is how much privacy loss budget is saved by using the common mechanism methodology instead of the traditional alternate approach. We express this as a percentage of the total privacy loss budget. Note that
\%
PLB Saved depends only on the query matrices of
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
, so there is just one
\%
PLB Saved value per table.

7.5.
Census Application: Age/Gender Histograms

Our next set of experiments is a case study for a data product that will be released as part of the 2020 Decennial Census Detailed Demographics and Housing Characteristics
(
Haney et al. 2021
)
.

7.5.1.
Problem Description

The goal of this data product is to produce age-by-gender histograms for different sub-populations, such as for an ethnic group in a given region. Since the sub-population might be sparse, one of 4 pre-defined age bucketization schemes will be used
(
Haney et al. 2021
)
:

•

Total
. This consists of one age bucket:
[0,103)
. In this case, the age-by-gender histogram is simply the number of females and the number of males.

•

Age4
. This consists of the following four buckets:
[0,18)
;
[18,45)
;
[45,65)
;
[65,103]
.

•

Age9
. This consists of the following nine buckets:
[0,5)
;
[5,18)
;
[18,25)
;
[25,35)
;
[35,45)
;
[45,55)
;
[55,65)
;
[65,75)
;
[75,103]
.

•

Age23
. This consists of the following 23 buckets:
[0,5)
;
[510)
;
[10,15)
;
[15,18)
;
[18,20)
;
[20,21)
;
[21,22)
;
[22,25)
;
[25,30)
;
[30,35)
;
[35,40)
;
[40,45)
;
[45,50)
;
[50,55)
;
[55,60)
;
[60,62)
;
[62,65)
;
[65,67)
;
[67,70)
;
[70,75)
;
[75,80)
;
[80,85)
;
[85,103]
.

We note that these represent nested analyses, as
Age23
is a refinement of
Age9
, which is a refinement of
Age4
, which is a refinement of
Total
. The idea is that the smaller the sub-population is, the coarser the age buckets should be in order for the noise not to overwhelm the actual counts.

7.5.2.
The Census algorithm

One of the algorithms we compare against is the DHC algorithm, which is the one that will actually be used for the problem
(
Haney et al. 2021
)
by the Census Bureau. This DHC algorithm will use a fraction
\gamma
of the privacy loss budget to estimate the total size of the sub-population. There are also three threshold parameters
\theta_{1}<\theta_{2}<\theta_{3}
. If the noisy sub-population count is
<\theta_{1}
, the remaining privacy budget will be used to produce the gender by age histogram using the
total
bucketization. If the noisy sub-population count is in the range
[\theta_{1},\theta_{2})
, then the
Age4
bucketization will be used. If it is in the range of
[\theta_{2},\theta_{3})
, then
Age9
will be used. Otherwise,
Age23
will be used.

Thus the algorithm has 4 parameters that must be carefully tuned:
\gamma,\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\theta_{3}
. The decision on parameter values has not been made public (possibly a difficult decision) but the rest of the algorithm is public.

7.5.3.
The Common Mechanism Approach

In the problem setting, there are four algorithms to consider:
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{3},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{4}
, which produce noisy gender by age histograms with the bucketizations
total
,
Age4
,
Age9
,
Age23
, respectively, by adding independent Gaussian noise. We define 3 common mechanisms:
CM_{1234}
is common to
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{3},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{4}
(i.e., it is the maximal mechanism answerable from all of them). It is obtained by numerically solving the optimization in Equation
1
with a constraint added for each mechanism.
CM_{234}
is common to
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{3},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{4}
, and
CM_{34}
is common to
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{3}
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{4}
. It turns out that
CM_{1234}
is answerable from
CM_{234}
, which is answerable from
CM_{34}
, therefore allows us to perform the following procedure that wastes no privacy loss budget:

•

Get the output
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}}_{1234}
of
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1234}
and decide whether the
total
bucketization should be used based on SNR. If yes, run the residual mechanism
RM_{1}
such that
(CM_{1234},RM_{1})
is equivalent to
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
.

•

If no, run the residual mechanism
RM_{234}
such that
({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1234},RM_{234})
is equivalent to
CM_{234}
. Use that to decide whether to use
Age4
. If yes, run the residual mechanism
RM_{2}
such that
(CM_{234},RM_{2})
is equivalent to
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
.

•

If no, run the residual mechanism
RM_{34}
such that
({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{234},RM_{34})
is equivalent to
CM_{34}
. Use that to decide whether to use
Age9
. If yes, run the residual mechanism
RM_{3}
such that
(CM_{34},RM_{3})
is equivalent to
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{3}
.

•

If no, run the residual mechanism
RM_{4}
such that
(CM_{34},RM_{4})
is equivalent to
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{4}
.

Thus, at the end, one gets something that is equivalent to either
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{3}
or
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{4}
without wasting any privacy budget.

7.5.4.
The Alternative Approach.

We also consider a third approach that mirrors the previous experiments. Instead of making decisions based on the common mechanism, at each step, some privacy loss budget is allocated to obtain the best linear Gaussian mechanism to estimate the SNR of the histogram being considered. This is also an alternative to making decisions based on noisy population counts.

7.5.5.
Results

Since the input data that the DHC algorithm will use is not public, we use the
AgeGender
dataset described in Section
7.1
to produce an Age by Gender histogram at each census tract using the three algorithms described.

Since the DHC algorithm requires additional parameters, we give it a strong non-private advantage: the
\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\theta_{3}
are learned using a non-private logistic regression model, and the DHC algorithm is given the exact sub-population count (i.e., a noiseless threshold).

We set the desired SNR parameters to be
x=0.5
and
y=20
. In a histogram cell, a ratio of 20 between a count
c
and the privacy noise standard deviation
\sigma
means that
95\%
of the time, the relative error of the noisy cell count is at most
2\sigma/c=2/20=10\%
.

In Figure
2
, we compare the accuracy of the DHC algorithm to the common mechanism in choosing the right age bucketization to use. Even with the advantages we gave it (e.g., tuned parameters and noise-free thresholds), it is still outperformed by the common mechanism, which avoids all those tuning parameters. This shows that the noisy information provided by the common mechanism for free is more informative than the population thresholds, even when the population thresholds are completely accurate.

We next compare the common mechanism to the alternate approach (Section
7.5.4
) that, for an apples-to-apples comparison, allocates privacy budget to the optimal linear Gaussian mechanism that has equal variance to the common mechanism, but with smallest possible privacy cost. Table
7
shows the accuracy of choosing the correct age bucketization and the fraction of PLB saved by using the common mechanism (nearly
50\%
). Since the choice among the four bucketizations is done sequentially, the sooner one is chosen, the better for the alternate approach since it no longer has to allocate PLB for future choices. For this reason, the percentage of PLB saved varies with the experimental setting.

8.
Conclusions

In this paper, we formalized the problem and provided algorithms for the computation of the common mechanism
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{*}}}
for two linear Gaussian mechanisms
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
. The common mechanism represents information that is provided by both mechanisms, while the residual mechanisms
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}^{\prime}_{1}
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}^{\prime}_{2}
reflect the remaining information in
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
after the information from
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{*}}}
has been removed from them. We presented an application where an analyst can decide whether to get answers of
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
or
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{2}
using the help of the common and residual mechanisms. This represents another tool that can be used for differentially private algorithm design.
