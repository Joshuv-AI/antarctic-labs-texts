Abstract
. Lloyd Shapley’s cooperative value allocation theory stands as a central concept in game theory, extensively utilized across various domains to distribute resources, evaluate individual contributions, and ensure fairness. The Shapley value formula and his four axioms that characterize it form the foundation of the theory. Traditionally, the Shapley value is assigned under the assumption that all players in a cooperative game will ultimately form the grand coalition. In this paper, we reinterpret the Shapley value as an expectation of a certain stochastic path integral, with each path representing a general coalition formation process. As a result, the value allocation is naturally extended to all partial coalition states. In addition, we provide a set of five properties that extend the Shapley axioms and characterize the stochastic path integral. Finally, by integrating Hodge calculus, stochastic processes, and path integration of edge flows on graphs, we expand the cooperative value allocation theory beyond the standard coalition game structure to encompass a broader range of cooperative network configurations.



Keywords: Cooperative game, Shapley axiom, Shapley value, Shapley formula, Hodge theory, Poisson’s equation, edge flow, path integral, random coalition formation process

1.
Introduction

Lloyd Shapley’s value allocation theory for cooperative games has been one of the most central concepts in game theory. Shapley value is widely used in many fields, including economics, finance, and machine learning, to allocate resources, assess individual agent contributions, and determine the fairness of payouts. Among many excellent treatises on Shapley value, we refer to a recent treatise by
Algaba et al. 2019
, in which various authors discuss modern applications of the Shapley value to various game-theoretic and operations-research problems including genetics, social choice and social network, finance, politics, tax games, telecommunication and energy transmission networks, queueing problems, group decision making, spanning trees, and even aircraft landing fees problem. Recently, researchers have started to utilize the Shapley value in diverse fields such as machine learning
(
Ghorbani and Zou 2019
;
Mitchell et al. 2022
;
Schoch et al. 2022
;
Rozemberczki et al. 2022
)
, medicine
(
Rodríguez-Pérez and
Bajorath 2019
;
Smith and Alvarez 2021
)
, and sustainable energy
(
Pang et al. 2021
)
. This shows that Shapley’s cooperative value allocation theory remains a vibrant area of research, applied across various contexts, and continues to inspire researchers.

The Shapley value possesses two notable facets: Shapley’s renowned value allocation formula and his four defining axioms. These axioms are significant as they establish a framework of fairness criteria for evaluating the worth of a cooperative game and the individual contributions of players. By adhering to these axioms, the distribution of value within a game is perceived to be fair, transparent, and intuitively sensible. Given that the Shapley value emerges as the unique outcome that satisfies these axioms, it emerges as a compelling solution for value allocation in cooperative games. Furthermore, the Shapley formula holds significance as it provides a mathematical method for computing the value of a cooperative game and the contributions of individual players. This formula determines each player’s marginal contribution to the overall game value by considering all potential permutations of player coalitions. With a range of desirable properties inherited from the Shapley axioms, the Shapley formula is widely embraced as the preferred approach for evaluating individual player performance and distributing the value of a cooperative game.

In a coalitional TU game
v
, the Shapley value can be assigned under the assumption that all players will eventually form the grand coalition, and the Shapley axioms and formula are then used to determine a fair allocation. In other words, Shapley value does not address how to properly assess individual player contribution and allocate the value of a cooperative game when the players form any non-grand, but partial coalition state. While the Shapley value applied for each subgame of
v
could allocate the value
v(T)
assigned to the coalition
T\subsetneq N
, the formula implicitly assumes that the coalition only grows towards the target
T
, thereby failing to fully capture the entire game structure.

The objective of this study is to extend the Shapley value allocation theory to a broader cooperative framework. Specifically, applying this new theory to a coalitional TU game enables the generation of value allocations at each partial coalition state, where the allocation at the grand coalition coincides with the Shapley value.

To comprehensively depict the evolution of partial coalitions, we consider a coalition formation process where players can both join and depart from existing coalitions until the desired coalition is achieved. This approach offers a more realistic portrayal of coalition formation processes and serves as the inspiration for the average path integral value allocation formula, which extends the Shapley formula.

Furthermore, as we develop the new allocation theory using Hodge calculus, stochastic processes, and path integration on graphs, it becomes apparent that this generalization should not be confined to the coalition game graph framework. Consequently, we introduce the Hodge-Shapley value, a versatile allocation scheme applicable to any cooperative network, providing allocation values at any stage of cooperation. Finally, we demonstrate how the Hodge-Shapley value can be effectively computed on any graph, assuming that the underlying cooperative process adheres to a natural property.

2.
Review of Shapley axioms and the Shapley formula

We commence by revisiting the renowned Shapley value allocation theory (
Shapley 1953
), which remains a source of inspiration for researchers across diverse fields.

To begin, let
N=\{1,2,...,n\}
represent the set of players of the coalition games

{\cal G}_{N}=\{v:2^{N}\to{\mathbb{R}}\ |\ v(\emptyset)=0\}.

A coalition game (also called a characteristic function)
v
is a function on the subsets of
N
, where each
S\subseteq N
represents a coalition of players in
S
, and
v(S)
represents the value assigned to the coalition
S
, with the null coalition
\emptyset
receiving zero value. Given
v\in{\cal G}_{N}
, Shapley considered the question of how to split
the grand coalition value
v(N)
, known as the Shapley value. It is determined uniquely by the following result.

Theorem 2.1

(
Shapley 1953
)
.

There exists a unique allocation

v\in{\cal G}_{N}\mapsto\bigl(\phi_{i}(v)\bigr)_{i\in N}
satisfying the following conditions:

\cdot
efficiency:

\sum_{i\in N}\phi_{i}(v)=v(N)
.

\cdot
symmetry:

v\bigl(S\cup\{i\}\bigr)=v\bigl(S\cup\{j\}\bigr)
for all
S\subseteq N\setminus\{i,j\}
yields

\phi_{i}(v)=\phi_{j}(v)
.

\cdot
null-player:

v\bigl(S\cup\{i\}\bigr)-v(S)=0
for all

S\subseteq N\setminus\{i\}
yields
\phi_{i}(v)=0
.

\cdot
linearity:

\phi_{i}(\alpha v+\alpha^{\prime}v^{\prime})=\alpha\phi_{i}(v)+\alpha^{\prime}\phi_{i}(v^{\prime})
for all

\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
and
v,v^{\prime}\in{\cal G}_{N}
.

Moreover, this allocation is given by the following explicit formula:

(2.1)

\phi_{i}(v)=\sum_{S\subseteq N\setminus\{i\}}\frac{\lvert S\rvert!\bigl(n-\lvert S\rvert-1\bigr)!}{n!}\Bigl(v\bigl(S\cup\{i\}\bigr)-v(S)\Bigr).

The four terms denote different aspects of the allocation. [efficiency] indicates that the value obtained by the grand coalition is fully distributed among the players; [symmetry] indicates that equivalent players receive equal amounts; [null-player] indicates that a player who contributes no marginal value to any coalition receives nothing; and [linearity] indicates that the allocation is linear in terms of game values. The four conditions outlined above are known as the Shapley axioms, the vector
\bigl(\phi_{i}(v)\bigr)_{i\in N}
is referred to as the Shapley value, and (
2.1
) is denoted as the Shapley formula.

The Shapley formula (
2.1
) can be rewritten as follows: Suppose the players form the grand coalition by
joining, one-at-a-time, in the order defined by a permutation
\sigma

of
N
. That is, player
i
joins immediately after the coalition

S^{\sigma}_{i}=\bigl\{j\in N:\sigma(j)<\sigma(i)\bigr\}
has formed, contributing marginal value

v\bigl(S^{\sigma}_{i}\cup\{i\}\bigr)-v(S^{\sigma}_{i})
. Then
\phi_{i}(v)
is the average marginal value contributed by
player
i
over all
n!
permutations
\sigma
, that is,

(2.2)

\displaystyle\phi_{i}(v)=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma}\Bigl(v\bigl(S^{\sigma}_{i}\cup\{i\}\bigr)-v(S^{\sigma}_{i})\Bigr).

The well-known glove game below explains the formula (
2.2
) in a simple context.

Example 2.1

(Glove game)
.

Let

right-hand glove. A pair of gloves has value
1

\phi_{1}(v)=\tfrac{2}{3},\qquad\phi_{2}(v)=\phi_{3}(v)=\tfrac{1}{6}.

This is easily seen from (

\phi_{2}(v)=\phi_{3}(v)=\tfrac{1}{6}

3.
Extension of the Shapley value via random coalition processes

Before delving into our generalization of the Shapley value to various setups, let us initially conceptualize the Shapley value as the expected outcome of a random coalition formation process. Consider the hypercube graph, or coalition game graph
G=({\cal V},{\cal E})
, where
{\cal V}
denotes the set of nodes and
{\cal E}
the set of edges. This graph is defined by

(3.1)

{\cal V}:=2^{N},\ \ {\cal E}:=\bigl\{\bigl(S,S\cup\{i\}\bigr)\in{\cal V}\times{\cal V}\ |\ S\subseteq N\setminus\{i\},\ i\in N\bigr\}.

Notice that each coalition
S\subseteq N
can correspond to a vertex of the unit hypercube in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
. We assume that each edge is oriented in the direction of the inclusion
S\hookrightarrow S\cup\{i\}
. We also define the set of reverse (or negatively-oriented) edges

(3.2)

{\cal E}_{-}:=\bigl\{\bigl(S\cup\{i\},S\bigr)\in{\cal V}\times{\cal V}\ |\ S\subseteq N\setminus\{i\},\ i\in N\bigr\}.

The edges in
{\cal E}
are termed forward/positively-oriented edges. We set
\overline{{\cal E}}={\cal E}\cup{\cal E}_{-}
.

Next, recall that in the Shapley formula (

(3.3)

\displaystyle p_{S,T}:=1/n\ \text{ if }\ T\sim S,\quad p_{S,T}:=0\ \text{ if }\ T\not\sim S.

We emphasize the coalition process now allows a player to not only join but also leave the current coalition, i.e., the transition from
S

Let

T\in{\cal V}

\tau_{T}=\tau_{T}(\omega)\in{\mathbb{N}}

X_{\tau_{T}}(\omega)=T

Now given a coalition game
v\in{\cal G}_{N}

(3.4)

{\cal I}_{i}(v,T)={\cal I}_{i}(v,T)(\omega):=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau_{T}(\omega)}{\partial}_{i}v\big(X_{t-1}(\omega),X_{t}(\omega)\big),

where for each
i\in N
, the partial differential
{\partial}_{i}v:\overline{{\cal E}}\to{\mathbb{R}}

(3.5)

{\partial}_{i}v\bigl(S,S\cup\{j\}\bigr):=\begin{cases}v(S\cup\{i\})-v(S)&\text{if }\ j=i,\\
0&\text{if }\ j\neq i,\end{cases}

and
{\partial}_{i}v\bigl(S\cup\{j\},S\bigr):=-{\partial}_{i}v\bigl(S,S\cup\{j\}\bigr)
. Notice that

represents the marginal contribution of player

(3.6)

\Phi_{i}(v,T):={\mathbb{E}}[{\cal I}_{i}(v,T)]=\int_{\Omega}{\cal I}_{i}(v,T)(\omega)\,d{\cal P}(\omega)

represents player
i
’s expected total contribution given the state advances from
\emptyset
to
T
.

Now our interpretation of the Shapley value as the expected outcome of the random coalition formation process is stated as follows.

Theorem 3.1
.

The Shapley value and the value function
(\Phi_{i})_{i\in N}
evaluated at the grand coalition coincide. That is,
\phi_{i}(v)=\Phi_{i}(v,N)
for every
v\in{\cal G}_{N}
and
i\in N

Theorem

Remark 5.2

(The egalitarian Shapley values)
.

Given
v\in{\cal G}_{N}
, let
{\rm ED}_{i}(v):=\frac{v(N)}{n}
denote the equal division value. For

and

is nonnegative.

\mathsf{NPE}
states that when the entire society is productive (

), null players should not receive negative payoffs (because they do not cause harm to the society).

claims that if we no longer require null players’ payoffs to be zero, the nature of solidarity emerges in how null players are treated. Since null players are completely unproductive, their “selfish” payoffs, or Shapley payoffs, are zero. As a result, any nonzero payoff must be due to solidarity among the players. To prevent players from receiving unreasonable payoffs corresponding to the case

, then
\varphi_{i}(v)\geq\varphi_{j}(v)

Desirability compares two players in a game to ensure that their payoffs do not contradict their productivities as measured by marginal contributions.

A characterization of the class of egalitarian Shapley values is now given as follows.

(

)
.

A value

Our definition of the marginal value (

) clearly indicates that the

-Shapley value is related to the egalitarian Shapley value. To see this, we compute

\displaystyle\phi_{f_{\alpha,i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma}\sum_{j\in N}f_{\alpha,i}\big(S^{\sigma}_{j},S^{\sigma}_{j}\cup\{j\}\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n!}\bigg[\alpha\sum_{\sigma}\Bigl(v\bigl(S^{\sigma}_{i}\cup\{i\}\bigr)-v(S^{\sigma}_{i})\Bigr)+\frac{1-\alpha}{n-1}\sum_{j\neq i}\sum_{\sigma}\Bigl(v\bigl(S^{\sigma}_{j}\cup\{j\}\bigr)-v(S^{\sigma}_{j})\Bigr)\bigg]

\displaystyle=\alpha\phi_{i}(v)+\frac{1-\alpha}{n-1}\sum_{j\neq i}\phi_{j}(v)

\displaystyle=\frac{n\alpha-1}{n-1}\phi_{i}(v)+\frac{n(1-\alpha)}{n-1}\frac{v(N)}{n}

This demonstrates that the

Remark 6.2

(Efficiency)
.

Shapley’s efficiency axiom is a crucial ingredient for characterizing his value allocation scheme; without it, it is difficult to establish the uniqueness of the allocation. The efficiency axiom is equally important for our charaterization result (Theorem

) to yield a unique allocation for all coalitions.

Our description of the principal-agent allocation problem, on the other hand, shows that efficiency is merely a constraint, which is equivalent to declaring that the principal’s marginal surplus
v(T)-v(S)-\sum_{i}f_{i}(S,T)
is identically zero for every
(S,T)\in\overline{{\cal E}}
. The principal enters the problem as soon as we relax this vanishing constraint, and

then represents her value at each cooperative state. The crucial difference between principal and agents is that the value of the principal is represented as a function

on
{\cal V}
, whereas the (marginal) value of the agents is represented as edge flows

on
\overline{{\cal E}}
. Finally, we can recover the efficiency

for all

by imposing the following marginal efficiency condition

v(T)-v(S)=\sum_{i}f_{i}(S,T)\ \text{ for every }(S,T)\in\overline{{\cal E}}.

For example, the
\alpha

) satisfies this condition, hence is efficient.

Remark 6.3

(Myerson’s conference structures and fair allocation rules)
.

Myerson 1977

Myerson 1980

He suggests an allocation rule, which are functions that map conference structures to payoff allocations. The rule is characterized by a notion of fairness, which we briefly explain.

Let

be the set of players. Let

For any

Any

That is, the sets in

An allocation rule for the game

This condition asserts that if

For

\Psi_{i}(Q)-\Psi_{i}(Q-S)=\Psi_{j}(Q)-\Psi_{j}(Q-S)\ \text{ for all }Q\in{\rm CS},S\in Q,i\in S,j\in S.

The conference structure

7.
Reversible Markov chains and Poisson’s equations on graphs

The preceding discussions naturally lead to the question of how to evaluate the value function (
6.2
), which represents an infinite sum of all possible paths between states. As the network gets more complicated, this can quickly become intractable.

In this section, we establish the relationship between the value function (
6.2
) and the Poisson’s equation on graphs, when the cooperative process follows an important class of probability laws, known as reversible Markov chains. This will allow us to compute (
6.2
) by solving a system of linear equations, which is tractable.

To this end, we need to introduce basic linear operators, such as gradient, divergence, and Laplacian, between linear function spaces
\ell^{2}({\cal V})
and
\ell^{2}({\cal E})
. We refer to
Lim 2020
for an accessible introduction to combinatorial Hodge theory. Recently, the combinatorial Hodge decomposition has found application in game theory across diverse contexts, including noncooperative games
(
Candogan et al. 2011
)
, cooperative games
(
Stern and Tettenhorst 2019
)
, and the ranking of social preferences
(
Jiang et al. 2011
)
.

Let
\ell^{2}({\cal V})
denote the space of functions
{\cal V}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with the standard inner product

(7.1)

\langle u,v\rangle\coloneqq\sum_{S\in{\cal V}}u(S)v(S).

We recall that these are called coalition games if
{\cal V}=2^{N}
and
v(\emptyset)=0
. In contrast,
{\cal V}
can now be an arbitrary finite set and
v\in\ell^{2}({\cal V})
is not required to assume
0
anywhere.

Let
\lambda:\overline{{\cal E}}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
define the edge weight, satisfying
\lambda(T,S)=\lambda(S,T)\geq 0
(i.e., no sign alternation) for all
S,T\in{\cal V}
. We declare that there is an edge between
S
and
T
, i.e.,
(S,T)\in\overline{{\cal E}}
, if and only if
\lambda(S,T)>0
. Given an edge weight
\lambda
, we denote by
\ell^{2}_{\lambda}({\cal E})
the space of functions

\overline{{\cal E}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
equipped with the weighted inner product

(7.2)

\langle f,g\rangle_{\lambda}\coloneqq\sum_{(S,T)\in{\cal E}}\lambda(S,T)f(S,T)g(S,T).

with the sign changing property
f(T,S)=-f(S,T)
. Elements in
\ell^{2}_{\lambda}({\cal E})
are often called edge flows. Now the gradient operator
\mathrm{d}\colon\ell^{2}({\cal V})\rightarrow\ell_{\lambda}^{2}({\cal E})
is defined by

(7.3)

\mathrm{d}v(S,T)\coloneqq v(T)-v(S)\ \text{ for each }(S,T)\in\overline{{\cal E}}.

Given a game
v
on
{\cal V}
,
\mathrm{d}v
measures its marginal value for each edge
(S,T)\in\overline{{\cal E}}
.

Let
\mathrm{d}^{*}\colon\ell^{2}_{\lambda}({\cal E})\rightarrow\ell^{2}({\cal V})
denote the adjoint of
\mathrm{d}
.
\mathrm{d}^{*}
is called divergence operator, which is characterized by the defining relation for the adjoint operator

(7.4)

\langle\mathrm{d}v,f\rangle_{\lambda}=\langle v,\mathrm{d}^{*}f\rangle\ \text{ for every }v\in\ell^{2}({\cal V})\text{ and }f\in\ell_{\lambda}^{2}({\cal E}).

It is important to note that
\mathrm{d}
is defined by (
7.3
) and is independent of the edge weight
\lambda
. However,
\mathrm{d}^{*}
depends on the choice of
\lambda
due to its defining relationship (
7.4
), so it must be called a weighted divergence, but we simply call it divergence. In fact, (
7.4
) gives the following explicit form of the divergence

(7.5)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}^{*}f(S)=\sum_{T\sim S}\lambda(T,S)f(T,S)\ \text{ for every }S\in{\cal V},

where
T\sim S
means
\lambda(S,T)>0
, i.e.,
S
and
T
are adjacent in the network.

The Laplacian is the symmetric (self-adjoint) operator
\mathrm{L}=\mathrm{d}^{\ast}\mathrm{d}:\ell^{2}({\cal V})\to\ell^{2}({\cal V})
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{L}v(S)=\sum_{T\sim S}\lambda(S,T)\big(v(S)-v(T)\big).

\mathrm{L}v(S)
calculates the weighted sum of
v
’s marginal increment directed to each state
S
.

Poisson’s equation is a partial differential equation of broad utility with the form
\mathrm{L}u=h
, where
\mathrm{L}
represents the Laplace operator. Typically, a function
h
is given, and
u
is sought. Given a weighted graph
{\cal G}=({\cal V},{\cal E})
with weight
\lambda
and an edge flow
f\in\ell^{2}_{\lambda}({\cal E})
, we are interested in the following form of the Poisson’s equation

(7.6)

\mathrm{L}u=\mathrm{d}^{\ast}f.

The solution
u
can be interpreted as follows: given an edge flow
f
, the potential function
u:{\cal V}\to{\mathbb{R}}
solving (
7.6
) is such that its gradient flow
\mathrm{d}u:\overline{{\cal E}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is closest to the flow

. To see this, we recall the fundamental theorem of linear algebra:

(7.7)

\ell^{2}({\cal V})=\mathcal{R}(\mathrm{d}^{\ast})\oplus\mathcal{N}(\mathrm{d}),\qquad\ell^{2}_{\lambda}({\cal E})=\mathcal{R}(\mathrm{d})\oplus\mathcal{N}(\mathrm{d}^{\ast}),

where
\mathcal{R}(\cdot)
and
{\cal N}(\cdot)
represent the range and nullspace, and
\oplus
represents the orthogonal decomposition with respect to the inner products on
\ell^{2}({\cal V})
and
\ell^{2}_{\lambda}({\cal E})
.

Now given

, the equation
\mathrm{d}u=f
can be solved only if
f\in\mathcal{R}(\mathrm{d})
. In general, a least squares solution to
\mathrm{d}u=f
instead solves
\mathrm{d}u=f_{1}
where
f=f_{1}+f_{2}
with
f_{1}\in\mathcal{R}(\mathrm{d})
and
f_{2}\in\mathcal{N}(\mathrm{d}^{\ast})
. Applying
\mathrm{d}^{\ast}
yields
\mathrm{d}^{\ast}\mathrm{d}u=\mathrm{d}^{\ast}f_{1}=\mathrm{d}^{\ast}f
, which is (
7.6
).

We also note that any two solutions
u,v
to (
7.6
) differ by a constant if
{\cal G}
is connected. This is because
\mathrm{L}u=\mathrm{L}v
implies
\mathrm{d}u=\mathrm{d}v
(
\mathrm{L}u=0\Rightarrow\langle\mathrm{d}^{*}\mathrm{d}u,u\rangle=0\Rightarrow\langle\mathrm{d}u,\mathrm{d}u\rangle_{\lambda}=||\mathrm{d}u||_{\ell^{2}_{\lambda}({\cal E})}^{2}=0\Rightarrow\mathrm{d}u=0
). In particular, if
u
is specified at a state, say
u(O)=u_{0}
for some
O\in{\cal V}
, then
u
is uniquely determined by the equation (
7.6
).

Let
(X_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
denote the cooperative process valued in
{\cal V}
. In general, cooperative evolution can be described as any stochastic process satisfying the condition
(X_{t},X_{t+1})\in\overline{{\cal E}}
. However, to understand the connection between the average path integral (
6.2
) and the Poisson’s equation, we now focus on the processes whose governing law
{\cal P}
belongs to an important class in probability theory, known as the class of reversible Markov chains.

A Markov chain
(X_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
is characterized by its transition probabilities (or rates) between two adjacent states
S
and
T
, denoted by
p_{S,T}
, which is the probability of
X_{t+1}=T
given
X_{t}=S
. A Markov chain on a graph
{\cal G}
is called reversible if there exists an edge weight
\lambda:\overline{{\cal E}}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
such that the transition probabilities are defined by

(7.8)

\displaystyle p_{S,T}=\frac{\lambda(S,T)}{\sum_{U\sim S}\lambda(S,U)}.

(
7.8
) describes a (biased) random walk on the graph, where the parameter
\lambda
indicates the relative likelihood of the direction in which cooperation is likely to progress, thereby providing increased flexibility in modeling stochastic cooperative processes. This formulation includes the previous coalition formation process (
3.3
) as a particular case, where
\lambda
remains constant at 1 and
\mathcal{G}
represents the coalition graph (
3.1
).

If a Markov chain is reversible, there exists a stationary distribution
\pi=(\pi_{S})_{S\in{\cal V}}
such that
\pi_{S}p_{S,T}=\pi_{T}p_{T,S}
for all
S,T\in{\cal V}
. Another implication of reversibility is that every loop and its inverse loop have the same probability of being realized, that is,

(7.9)

p_{S,S_{1}}p_{S_{1},S_{2}}\dots p_{S_{n-1},S_{n}}p_{S_{n},S}=p_{S,S_{n}}p_{S_{n},S_{n-1}}\dots p_{S_{2},S_{1}}p_{S_{1},S}.

We illustrate the importance of these properties in establishing the following result.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let the Markov chain (
7.8
) be defined on a connected graph
G
with weight
\lambda
, and let
f_{i}\in\ell^{2}_{\lambda}({\cal E})
. Then
\Phi_{f_{i}}
in (
6.2
) uniquely solves the Poisson’s equation

(7.10)

\mathrm{L}\Phi_{f_{i}}=\mathrm{d}^{*}f_{i}\ \ \text{with the initial condition }\ \Phi_{f_{i}}(O)=0.

Proof.

Fix
T\in{\cal V}
and let
\{T_{1},...,T_{n}\}
be the set of all states adjacent to
T
(which is nonempty by the connectedness of
{\cal G}
), and set
\Lambda_{T}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda(T,T_{k})>0
.

By (
7.5
) and (
7.8
), for any
f_{i}\in\ell_{\lambda}^{2}({\cal E})
, we have

(7.11)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}^{*}{f_{i}}(T)/\Lambda_{T}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}p_{T,T_{k}}{f_{i}}(T_{k},T),\text{ and}

(7.12)

\displaystyle\mathrm{L}\Phi_{f_{i}}(T)/\Lambda_{T}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}p_{T,T_{k}}\big(\Phi_{f_{i}}(T)-\Phi_{f_{i}}(T_{k})\big)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}p_{T,T_{k}}\Phi_{f_{i}}^{T_{k}}(T)

where the last equality is from
6.3
which will be shown later. Now we can interpret the right side of (
7.12
) as the aggregation (
6.2
) of path integrals of
f_{i}
(
6.1
) along all loops beginning and ending at
T
, but in this aggregation of
f_{i}
we do not take into account the first move from
T
to
T_{k}
, since this first move is described by the transition rate
p_{T,T_{k}}
and not driven by
\Phi^{T_{k}}_{f_{i}}
. On the other hand, if we aggregate path integrals of
f_{i}
for all loops emanating from
T
, we get
0
due to the reversibility (
7.9
) and the sign changing property of
f_{i}
. This observation allows us to conclude:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\text{aggregation of path integrals of $f_{i}$ along all loops emanating from $T$}

\displaystyle=\text{aggregation of path integrals of $f_{i}$ along all loops except the first moves}

\displaystyle\quad+\text{aggregation of path integrals of $f_{i}$ for all first moves from $T$}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}p_{T,T_{k}}\Phi_{f_{i}}^{T_{k}}(T)+\sum_{k=1}^{n}p_{T,T_{k}}f_{i}(T,T_{k})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{L}\Phi_{f_{i}}(T)/\Lambda_{T}-\mathrm{d}^{*}f_{i}(T)/\Lambda_{T},

yielding
\mathrm{L}\Phi_{f_{i}}(T)=\mathrm{d}^{*}f_{i}(T)
.
∎

The theorem allows us to evaluate the potentially intractable value function
\Phi_{f_{i}}
by a feasible problem of solving a system of least-squares linear equations (
7.10
).

(Stern and Tettenhorst’s component games

Given a coalition game

and players’ marginal values
(f_{i})_{i\in N}
. Additionally,

’s main result establishes

Remark 7.2

(From infinite to finite paths)
.

The definition of the value
\Phi_{f_{i}}^{S}(T)
in (
6.2
) involves the sum of an infinite number of path integrals from
S
to
T
, even when the underlying graph has a finite number of nodes and edges. This is because we allow paths to contain loops. However, if two essential conditions are met – the sign changing property of edge flows
\ell^{2}_{\lambda}({\cal E})
and reversibility (
7.9
) – we can effectively reduce the sum to a finite number of appropriately weighted paths with no loops.

We say that a path
(X_{0},X_{1},\dots,X_{\tau})
has no (internal) loop if
X_{0},...,X_{\tau}
are all distinct, with the exception of the possibility
X_{0}=X_{\tau}
, i.e., the path itself can be a loop. If a graph
G=({\cal V},{\cal E})
is finite, then there are finitely many paths with no loops. Let
{\cal N}_{S,T}
represent the collection of such no-loop paths from
S
to
T
(where
S=T
is possible). For instance, in Figure
4
,
{\cal N}_{S,T}
consists of the following three paths:
(S,T)
,
(S,V,U,T)
,
(S,V,W,U,T)
. Let
{\cal C}_{S,T}
be the collection of all paths from
S
to
T
, i.e.,
X_{0}=S
,
X_{\tau}=T
, and
X_{t}\neq T
for all
t=1,2,...,\tau-1
. We now assert that the following equality holds:

(7.16)

\Phi_{f_{i}}^{S}(T)=\sum_{\tilde{\omega}=(X_{0},...,X_{\tau})\in{\cal N}_{S,T}}\mu(\tilde{\omega})\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}f_{i}(X_{t-1},X_{t})

for some probability
\mu
on
{\cal N}_{S,T}
, i.e.,
\sum_{\tilde{\omega}\in{\cal N}_{S,T}}\mu(\tilde{\omega})=1
with
\mu(\tilde{\omega})>0
for every
\tilde{\omega}
.

What probability
\mu
will make (
7.16
) hold? Let us say
\omega\in{\cal C}_{S,T}
and
\tilde{\omega}\in{\cal N}_{S,T}
are equivalent, denoted by
\omega\sim\tilde{\omega}
, meaning that if all internal loops in
\omega
are removed, it equals
\tilde{\omega}
. For example, in Figure
4
, the path
(S,V,W,U,V,W,U,T)
is equivalent to
(S,V,W,U,T)
. Observe that
\sim
yields a partition of
{\cal C}_{S,T}
, with each
\tilde{\omega}\in{\cal N}_{S,T}
representing a partition. We define the probability distribution
\mu
on
{\cal N}_{S,T}
as follows:

(7.17)

\mu(\tilde{\omega})={\cal P}(\{\omega\ |\ \omega\sim\tilde{\omega}\})=\int_{\{\omega\,|\,\omega\sim\tilde{\omega}\}}d{\cal P}(\omega)

where
{\cal P}
is the law of the Markov chain, so for a path
\omega=(X_{0},X_{1},...,X_{\tau})
, we have
d{\cal P}(\omega)=\prod_{t=1}^{\tau}p_{X_{t-1},X_{t}}=p_{X_{0},X_{1}}p_{X_{1},X_{2}}\dots p_{X_{\tau-1}X_{\tau}}
.

For example, consider the general two person game in Example
3.1
with initial and terminal states
S=\emptyset
and
T=\{1\}
. There are two no-loop paths:
\tilde{\omega}_{1}=(\emptyset,\{1\})
and
\tilde{\omega}_{2}=(\emptyset,\{2\},\{1,2\},\{1\})
. If we assign
1/2
to
\tilde{\omega}_{1}
and
(1/2)^{3}=1/8
to
\tilde{\omega}_{2}
according to their lengths, it fails to sum to
1
, and it will not yield a proper weight assignment.

Let
\{X_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
denote the random walk (
3.3
) with
X_{0}=\emptyset
. With probability
1/2
,
X_{1}=\{1\}
or
\{2\}
. In the latter case
X_{1}=\{2\}
, due to the graph symmetry,
X_{t}
will eventually arrive at
\{1\}
via
\emptyset
or
\{1,2\}
with equal probability
1/2
. This and (
7.17
) show
\mu(\tilde{\omega}_{1})=1/2+1/2\cdot 1/2=3/4
and
\mu(\tilde{\omega}_{2})=1/2\cdot 1/2=1/4
. Now the path integral of
{\partial}_{1}v
along
\tilde{\omega}_{1}
is
v_{1}
, while along
\tilde{\omega}_{2}
is
v_{12}-v_{2}
, resulting in player
1
’s value at
\{1\}
as
3/4\cdot v_{1}+1/4\cdot(v_{12}-v_{2})=(3v_{1}+v_{12}-v_{2})/4
. Similarly, the path integral of
{\partial}_{2}v
along
\tilde{\omega}_{1}
is
0
, while along
\tilde{\omega}_{2}
is
v_{2}+(v_{1}-v_{12})
, yielding player
2
’s value at
\{1\}
as
(v_{2}+v_{1}-v_{12})/4
. This coincides with (
3.7
), verifying (
7.16
) in this case. For establishing (
7.16
) in general, the sign changing property of edge flows and the reversibility of the Markov chain appear crucial, which implies that the path integral of an edge flow along a loop and its reverse loop must have the opposite sign while having the same probability of the loops being realized, and thus cancel out in the sum (
7.16
). If, on the other hand, at least one of the two conditions fails, the author does not expect the reduction (
7.16
) to hold in general. This further signifies the importance of the two conditions, and their interplay.

8.
Conclusion

This study aims to present a novel mathematical framework for cooperative games that goes beyond existing setups in the literature. Previous research on cooperative value allocation has primarily considered it as determined by a number of axioms. Theorem
4.1
can be interpreted as a continuation of this direction. However, the primary objective of this study is to propose a shift from the current axiomatic framework to a stochastic path-integral framework. While the scope of cooperative games whose value can be determined by the axiomatic framework may be limited, often necessitating a restrictive set of assumptions for valuation, and the resulting value is implicit, as evidenced in this paper, the modeling of players’ cooperative progress as a random process in cooperative graphs and the definition of value as the average path integral of players’ marginal values allows for the seamless incorporation of a broad range of cooperative graphs, progressions, games, and marginal values. Moreover, the value is explicitly represented in this framework. We believe that this change in perspective will enable the application of cooperative game theory to various scientific domains, particularly in machine learning and AI, where the classical Shapley value already holds significance. In the realm of economic applications, as alluded in Remark
4.2
, investigating the specification of the game network
\mathcal{G}
and cooperative process law
\mathcal{P}
, as well as establishing players’ marginal values
\bf f
in a manner tailored to the specific economic context at hand, presents an intriguing avenue for future research.

Proof of Theorem

First, we claim that A1–A5 determines the operator
\Phi
uniquely (if exists). For each player set
N
, define games
\delta_{S,N}\in{\cal G}_{N}
for each
\emptyset\neq S\subseteq N
by

\delta_{S,N}(S)=1,\quad\delta_{S,N}(T)=0\,\text{ if }\,T\neq S.

We proceed by an induction on
|N|
. The case
|N|=1
is already from A1. Suppose the claim holds for
|N|-1
, so
\Phi_{j}(\delta_{S,N\setminus\{i\}},\cdot)
are determined for all
i,j\in N
and
\emptyset\neq S\subseteq N\setminus\{i\}
. Define the games
\Delta_{(S,S\cup\{i\})}\in{\cal G}_{N}
for each
\emptyset\neq S\subseteq N\setminus\{i\}
by

\Delta_{(S,S\cup\{i\})}(T)=1\,\text{ if }\,T=S\text{ or }T=S\cup\{i\},\quad\Delta_{(S,S\cup\{i\})}(T)=0\,\text{ otherwise.}

Notice then A4 (and induction hypothesis) determines
\Phi
for all
\Delta_{(S,S\cup\{i\})}\in{\cal G}_{N}
. Then thanks to A2, to prove the claim, it is enough to show that A1–A5 can determine
\Phi
for the pure bargaining game
\delta:=\delta_{N,N}
, because for any
\emptyset\neq S\subseteq N
, we can write
\delta_{S,N}
as the following sign-alternating sum

\delta_{S,N}=\Delta_{(S,S\cup\{i_{1}\})}-\Delta_{(S\cup\{i_{1}\},S\cup\{i_{1},i_{2}\})}+\Delta_{(S\cup\{i_{1},i_{2}\},S\cup\{i_{1},i_{2},i_{3}\})}-\dots\pm\delta_{N,N}.

By A3,
\sum_{S\subseteq N}\Phi_{i}(\delta,S)
is constant for all
i\in N
, thus equals
1/|N|
by A1. Define

u_{i}(S):=\Phi_{i}(\delta,S)-\frac{1}{|N|2^{|N|}}\ \text{ for all }\ S\subseteq N

so that
u_{i}(\emptyset)=-\frac{1}{|N|2^{|N|}}
and
\sum_{S\subseteq N}u_{i}(S)=0
for all
i
. Now observe A5 implies:

u_{i}(S)+u_{i}(S\cup\{i\})\text{ is constant for all }S\subseteq N\setminus\{i\},\text{ hence it is zero.}

This determines
u_{i}
, thus
\Phi_{i}(\delta,\cdot)
, as follows: suppose
u_{i}(S)
has been determined for all
i
and
|S|\leq k-1
. Let
|T|=k\leq|N|-1
. Then we have
u_{i}(T)=-u_{i}(T\setminus\{i\})
for all
i\in T
and it is constant (say
c_{k}
) by A3. Using A1 and A3, we obtain

0=\delta(T)=\sum_{i\in N}\Phi_{i}(\delta,T)=\sum_{i\in N}\bigg(u_{i}(T)+\frac{1}{|N|2^{|N|}}\bigg),

yielding
\sum_{i\in N}u_{i}(T)=-1/2^{|N|}
. With
u_{i}(T)=c_{k}
for all
i\in T
, we deduce
u_{j}(T)=\frac{-1-2^{|N|}kc_{k}}{2^{|N|}(|N|-k)}
for all
j\notin T
. This shows
u_{i}(T)
is determined for all
|T|=k\leq|N|-1
. Of course,
\Phi_{i}(\delta,N)=1/|N|
for all
i\in N
by A1 and A3. By induction (on
|N|
and on
k
for each
N
), the proof of uniqueness of the operator
\Phi
is therefore complete.

It remains to show that
\Phi
represented by the average path integral (
3.6
) satisfies A1–A5. As remarked earlier, Theorems
3.1
and
7.1
show that
v_{i}(S)=\Phi_{i}(v,S)
for all
S\subseteq N
, where
(v_{i})_{i\in N}
is the unique solution to the Poisson’s equation

(9.1)

\mathrm{L}v_{i}=\mathrm{d}^{*}{\partial}_{i}v\ \text{ with }v_{i}(\emptyset)=0,\ \text{ for each }i\in N.

We will henceforth show that
(v_{i})_{i\in N}
in place of
(\Phi_{i}(v,\cdot))_{i\in N}
satisfies A1–A5. Firstly, A2 is clearly satisfied. To show that A1 is satisfied, we compute

\mathrm{L}\sum_{i\in N}v_{i}=\sum_{i\in N}\mathrm{L}v_{i}=\sum_{i\in N}\mathrm{d}^{\ast}{\partial}_{i}v=\mathrm{d}^{\ast}\sum_{i\in N}{\partial}_{i}v=\mathrm{d}^{\ast}\mathrm{d}v=\mathrm{L}v,

since
\mathrm{d}=\sum_{i\in N}{\partial}_{i}
. Hence by unique solvability of (
9.1
),
\sum_{i\in N}v_{i}=v
as desired.

Next, let
\sigma
be a permutation of
N
. Let
\sigma
act on
\ell^{2}(2^{N})
and
\ell^{2}({\cal E})
via

\sigma v(S)=v(\sigma(S))\ \text{and}\ \sigma f\bigl(S,S\cup\{i\}\bigr)=f\bigl(\sigma(S),\sigma(S\cup\{i\})\bigr),\ v\in\ell^{2}(2^{N}),\ f\in\ell^{2}({\cal E}).

It is easy to check
\mathrm{d}\sigma=\sigma\mathrm{d}
and

\mathrm{d}_{i}\sigma=\sigma\mathrm{d}_{\sigma(i)}
. We also have
\mathrm{d}^{\ast}\sigma=\sigma\mathrm{d}^{\ast}
, since

\langle v,\mathrm{d}^{\ast}\sigma f\rangle=\langle\mathrm{d}v,\sigma f\rangle=\langle\sigma^{-1}\mathrm{d}v,f\rangle=\langle\mathrm{d}\sigma^{-1}v,f\rangle=\langle\sigma^{-1}v,\mathrm{d}^{\ast}f\rangle=\langle v,\sigma\mathrm{d}^{\ast}f\rangle

for any
v\in\ell^{2}(2^{N})
,
f\in\ell^{2}({\cal E})
. Now let
\sigma
be the transposition of
i,j
. We have

\mathrm{L}(\sigma v)_{i}=\mathrm{d}^{\ast}{\partial}_{i}\sigma v=\mathrm{d}^{\ast}\sigma{\partial}_{j}v=\sigma\mathrm{d}^{\ast}{\partial}_{j}v=\sigma\mathrm{L}v_{j}=\mathrm{L}\sigma v_{j}

which shows
(\sigma v)_{i}=\sigma v_{j}
by the unique solvability. Notice this corresponds to A3.

For A4, let
v\in{\cal G}_{N}
,
i\in N
, and assume
{\partial}_{i}v=0
. Then from (
9.1
) we readily get
v_{i}\equiv 0
. Fix
j\neq i
, and let
\mathrm{\tilde{d}}
,
{\tilde{\partial}}_{j}
be the differential operators restricted on
2^{N\setminus\{i\}}
, and set
\tilde{v}=v_{-i}
, i.e.,
\tilde{v}
is the restriction of
v
on
2^{N\setminus\{i\}}
. Let
\tilde{v}_{j}:2^{N\setminus\{i\}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be the solution to the equation
\mathrm{\tilde{d}}^{\ast}\mathrm{\tilde{d}}\tilde{v}_{j}=\mathrm{\tilde{d}}^{\ast}{\tilde{\partial}}_{j}\tilde{v}
with
\tilde{v}_{j}(\emptyset)=0
. Finally, in view of A4, define
v_{j}\in{\cal G}_{N}
by
v_{j}=\tilde{v}_{j}
on
2^{N\setminus\{i\}}
and
{\partial}_{i}v_{j}=0
. Now observe that A4 will follow if we can verify that this
v_{j}
indeed solves the equation
\mathrm{d}^{\ast}\mathrm{d}v_{j}=\mathrm{d}^{\ast}{\partial}_{j}v
.

To show this, let
S\subseteq N\setminus\{i\}
. In fact the following string of equalities holds:

\mathrm{d}^{\ast}\mathrm{d}v_{j}(S\cup\{i\})=\mathrm{d}^{\ast}\mathrm{d}v_{j}(S)=\mathrm{\tilde{d}}^{\ast}\mathrm{\tilde{d}}\tilde{v}_{j}(S)=\mathrm{\tilde{d}}^{\ast}{\tilde{\partial}}_{j}\tilde{v}(S)=\mathrm{d}^{\ast}{\partial}_{j}v(S)=\mathrm{d}^{\ast}{\partial}_{j}v(S\cup\{i\})

which simply follows from the definition of the differential operators. For instance

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}^{\ast}\mathrm{d}v_{j}(S)=\sum_{T\sim S}\mathrm{d}v_{j}(T,S)=\sum_{T\sim S,\,T\neq S\cup\{i\}}\mathrm{d}v_{j}(T,S)=\mathrm{\tilde{d}}^{\ast}\mathrm{\tilde{d}}\tilde{v}_{j}(S)

where the second equality is due to
{\partial}_{i}v_{j}=0
. On the other hand, since
j\neq i
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}^{\ast}{\partial}_{j}v(S)=\sum_{T\sim S}{\partial}_{j}v(T,S)=\sum_{T\sim S}{\tilde{\partial}}_{j}\tilde{v}(T,S)=\mathrm{\tilde{d}}^{\ast}{\tilde{\partial}}_{j}\tilde{v}(S).

The first equality is due to the definition of
v_{j}
(i.e.
v_{j}=\tilde{v}_{j}
on
2^{N\setminus\{i\}}
and
{\partial}_{i}v_{j}=0
), and the last equality is due to
{\partial}_{i}v=0
. This verifies A4.

Finally, we verify A5. For this, we need to verify the following claim:

(9.2)

v_{i}(S)+v_{i}(S\cup\{i\})\,\text{ is constant over all }\,S\subseteq N\setminus\{i\}.

Let
S\subseteq N\setminus\{i\}
, and recall
\mathrm{d}^{\ast}{\partial}_{i}v(S)=v(S)-v(S\cup\{i\})=-\mathrm{d}^{\ast}{\partial}_{i}v(S\cup\{i\})
. Hence,
\mathrm{L}v_{i}(S)+\mathrm{L}v_{i}(S\cup\{i\})=0
. Define
w_{i}\in\ell^{2}(2^{N})
by
w_{i}(S)=v_{i}(S\cup\{i\})
and
w_{i}(S\cup\{i\})=v_{i}(S)
for all
S\subseteq N\setminus\{i\}
. Then clearly
\mathrm{L}v_{i}(S\cup\{i\})=\mathrm{L}w_{i}(S)
and
\mathrm{L}v_{i}(S)=\mathrm{L}w_{i}(S\cup\{i\})
. Thus
\mathrm{L}(v_{i}+w_{i})\equiv 0
, hence
v_{i}+w_{i}\in\mathcal{N}(\mathrm{d})
, meaning that
v_{i}+w_{i}
is constant. This proves the claim, hence the theorem.
