Sparse spectral methods for partial differential equations on spherical caps
Ben Snowball, Sheehan Olver

Abstract
In recent years, sparse spectral methods for solving partial differential equations have been derived using hierarchies of classical orthogonal polynomials on intervals, disks, disk-slices and triangles. In this work we extend the methodology to a hierarchy of non-classical multivariate orthogonal polynomials on spherical caps. The entries of discretisations of partial differential operators can be effectively computed using formulae in terms of (non-classical) univariate orthogonal polynomials. We demonstrate the results on partial differential equations involving the spherical Laplacian and biharmonic operators, showing spectral convergence.



1
Introduction

This paper develops sparse spectral methods for solving linear partial differential equations on certain subsets of the sphere—specifically spherical caps. More precisely, we consider the solution of partial differential equations on the spherical cap
\Omega
defined by

\displaystyle\Omega:=\{(x,y,z)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\quad|\quad\alpha<z<\beta,\>x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}=1\}

where
\alpha\in(-1,1)
and
\beta:=1
.

Remark
:
For simplicity we focus on the case of a spherical cap, though there is an extension to a spherical band by taking
\beta\in(\alpha,1)
. The methods presented here translate to the spherical band case by including the necessary adjustments to the weights and recurrence relations we present in this paper. These adjustments make the mathematics more involved, which is why they are omitted here, but the approach is the same.

We advocate using a basis that is polynomial in cartesian coordinates, that is, polynomial in
x
,
y
, and
z
, and orthogonal with respect to a prescribed weight: that is, multivariate orthogonal polynomials, whose construction was considered in
[
12
]
. Equivalently, we can think of these as polynomials modulo the vanishing ideal
\{x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}=1\}
, or simply as a linear recombination of spherical harmonics that are orthogonalised on a subset of the sphere. This is in contrast to more standard approaches based on mapping the geometry to a simpler one (e.g., a rectangle or disk) and using orthogonal polynomials in the mapped coordinates (e.g., a basis that is polynomial in the spherical coordinates
\varphi
and
\theta
). The benefit of the new approach is that we do not need to resolve Jacobians, and thereby we can achieve sparse discretisations for partial differential operators, including those with polynomial variable coefficients. Further, we avoid the singular nature at the poles or as
\alpha
approaches
0
that such a projection may give, since our new approach yields a smooth polynomial basis for all
\alpha\in[-1,1)
.

On the spherical cap, the family of weights we consider are of the form

\displaystyle W^{(a)}(x,y,z):=(z-\alpha)^{a},\quad\hbox{for}\quad(x,y,z)\in\Omega,

noting that
W^{(a)}(x,y,z)=0
for
(x,y,z)\in\partial\Omega
when
a>0
. The corresponding OPs denoted
Q_{n,k,i}^{(a)}(x,y,z)
, where
n
denotes the polynomial degree,
0\leq k\leq n
and
i\in\{0,\min(1,k)\}
. We define these to be orthogonalised lexicographically, that is,

\displaystyle Q_{n,k,i}^{(a)}(x,y,z)=C_{n,k,i}\>x^{k-i}\>y^{i}\>z^{n-k}+(\hbox{lower order terms})

where
C_{n,k,i}\neq 0
and
“lower order terms”
includes degree
n
polynomials of the form
x^{j-i}\>y^{i}\>z^{n-j}
where
j<k
. The precise normalization arises from their definition in terms of one-dimensional OPs in Definition
2
.

We consider partial differential operators involving the spherical Laplacian (the Laplace–Beltrami operator): in spherical coordinates

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle=\cos\varphi,

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle=\sin\varphi\cos\theta=\rho(z)\cos\theta,

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=\sin\varphi\sin\theta=\rho(z)\sin\theta.

where
\rho(z):=\sqrt{1-z^{2}}
, we have

\displaystyle\Delta_{\rm S}

\displaystyle={1\over\sin\varphi}{\partial\over\partial\varphi}\Big(\sin\varphi{\partial\over\partial\varphi}\Big)+{1\over\sin^{2}\varphi}{\partial^{2}\over\partial\theta^{2}}={1\over\rho}{\partial\over\partial\varphi}\Big(\rho{\partial\over\partial\varphi}\Big)+{1\over\rho^{2}}{\partial^{2}\over\partial\theta^{2}}

i.e.
\Delta_{\rm S}f(\mathbf{x})=\Delta f({\mathbf{x}\over\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|})
for
\mathbf{x}:=(x,y,z)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
. We do so by considering the component operators
\rho{\partial\over\partial\varphi}
and
{\partial\over\partial\theta}
applied to OPs with a specific choices of weight so that their discretisation is sparse, see Theorem
1
. Sparsity comes from expanding the domain and range of an operator using different choices of the parameter
a
, a la the ultraspherical spectral method for intervals
[
9
]
, triangles
[
10
]
and disk-slices and trapeziums
[
14
]
, and the related work on sparse discretisations on disks
[
16
]
and spheres
[
17
,
6
]
. As in the disk-slice case in 2D
[
14
]
, we use an integration-by-parts argument to deduce the sparsity structure.

The three-dimensional orthogonal polynomials defined here involve the same non-classical (in fact, semi-classical
[
7
, §5]
) 1D OPs as those outlined for the disk-slice, and so methods for calculating these 1D OP recurrence coefficients and integrals have already been outlined
[
14
]
. In particular, by exploiting the connection with these 1D OPs we can construct discretizations of general partial differential operators of size
(p+1)^{2}\times(p+1)^{2}
in
O(p^{3})
operations, where
p
is the total polynomial degree. This clearly compares favourably to proceeding in a naïve approach where one would require
O(p^{6})
operations.

Note that we consider partial differential operators that are not necessarily rotational invariant: for example, one can use these techniques for Schrödinger operators
\Delta_{\rm S}+v(x,y,z)
where
v
is first approximated by a polynomial. A nice feature though is that if the partial differential operator is invariant with respect to rotation around the
z
axis (e.g., a Schrödinger operator with potential
v(z)
) the discretisation decouples, and can be reordered as a block-diagonal matrix. This improves the complexity further to an optimal
O(p^{2}
), which is demonstrated in Figure
4
with
v(x,y,z)=\cos z
.

An overview of the paper is as follows:

Section
2
: We present our definition of a (one-parameter) family of 3D orthogonal polynomials (OPs) on the spherical cap domain
\Omega
, by combining 1D OPs on the interval
(\alpha,1)
with Chebyshev polynomials, to form 3D OPs on the spherical cap surface. We show that these families will lead to sparse Jacobi operators for multiplication by
x,y,z
and demonstrate how to obtain the 3D OPs.

Section
3
: We define several partial differential operators such as spherical Laplacians and show that these will be sparse when applied to a suitable choice of expansions in bases built from OPs on the spherical cap. We can exactly calculate the non-zero entries of these sparse operators using the quadrature rule associated with the non-classical 1D OPs.

Section
4
: We derive a quadrature rule on the spherical cap that can be used to expand a function in the OP basis up to a given order
N
, and demonstrate how to evaluate a function using the Clenshaw algorithm using the coefficients of its expansion.

Section
5
: We demonstrate the proposed technique for solving differential equations on the spherical cap such as the Poisson equation, variable coefficient Helmholtz equation, and Biharmonic equation.

2
Orthogonal polynomials on spherical caps

In this section we outline the construction and some basic properties of
Q_{n,k,i}^{(a)}(x,y,z)
.

2.1
Explicit construction

We can construct the 3D orthogonal polynomials on
\Omega
from 1D orthogonal polynomials on the interval
[\alpha,\beta]
, and from Chebyshev polynomials. We do so in terms of Fourier series, which, following
[
12
]
, we write here as orthogonal polynomials in
x
and
y
:

Definition 1
.

Define the unit circle
\omega:=\{\mathbf{x}=(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\>|\>x^{2}+y^{2}=1\}
, and define the parameter
\theta
for each
(x,y)\in\omega
by
x=\cos\theta
,
y=\sin\theta
. Define the polynomials
\{Y_{k,i}\}
for
k=0,1,\dots
,
i=0,1
on
(x,y)\in\omega
by

\displaystyle Y_{0,0}(\mathbf{x})\equiv Y_{0,0}(x,y)

\displaystyle:=Y_{0}=:Y_{0,0}(\theta)

\displaystyle Y_{k,0}(\mathbf{x})\equiv Y_{k,0}(x,y)\

\displaystyle:=T_{k}(x)=\cos k\theta=:Y_{k,0}(\theta),\quad k=1,2,3,\dots

\displaystyle Y_{k,1}(\mathbf{x})\equiv Y_{k,1}(x,y)

\displaystyle:=y\>U_{k-1}(x)=\sin k\theta=:Y_{k,1}(\theta),\quad k=1,2,3,\dots

where
Y_{0}:=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}
and
T_{k}
,
U_{k-1}
are the standard Chebyshev polynomials on the interval
[-1,1]
. The
\{Y_{k,i}\}
are orthonormal with respect to the inner product

\displaystyle\left\langle{p,\>q}\right\rangle_{Y}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\pi}\>\int_{0}^{2\pi}p(\mathbf{x}(\theta))\>q(\mathbf{x}(\theta))\>{\rm d}\theta

Note that we have defined
Y_{0}
so as to ensure orthonormality.

Proposition 1

(
[
12
]
)
.

Let
w:(\alpha,\beta)\>\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a weight function. For
n=0,1,2,\dots,
let
\{r_{n,k}\}
be polynomials orthogonal with respect to the weight
\rho(x)^{2k}w(x)
where
0\leq k\leq n
. Then the 3D polynomials defined on
\Omega

\displaystyle Q_{n,k,i}(x,y,z):=r_{n-k,k}(z)\>\rho(z)^{k}\>Y_{k,i}\!\left({{x\over\rho(z)},{y\over\rho(z)}}\right)

for
i\in{0,1},\>0\leq k\leq n,\>n=0,1,2,\dots
are orthogonal polynomials with respect to the inner product

\displaystyle\left\langle{p,\>q}\right\rangle

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega}p(x,y,z)\>q(x,y,z)\>w(z)\>{\rm d}A

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\cos^{-1}(\alpha)}\int_{0}^{2\pi}p\big(\sin\varphi\cos\theta,\sin\varphi\sin\theta,\cos\varphi\big)\>q\big(\sin\varphi\cos\theta,\sin\varphi\sin\theta,\cos\varphi\big)\>\times

\displaystyle\qquad w(\cos\varphi)\>\sin\varphi\>{\rm d}\theta\>{\rm d}\varphi

\displaystyle=\int_{\alpha}^{1}\int_{0}^{2\pi}p\big(\rho(z)\cos\theta,\rho(z)\sin\theta,z\big)\>q\big(\rho(z)\cos\theta,\rho(z)\sin\theta,z\big)\>w(z)\>{\rm d}\theta\>{\rm d}z

on
\Omega
, where
{\rm d}A=\sin\varphi\>{\rm d}\theta\>{\rm d}\varphi
is the uniform spherical measure on
\Omega
.

For the spherical cap, we can use Proposition
1
to create our one-parameter family of OPs. We first introduce notation for our family of non-classical univariate OPs that will be used as the
r_{n}
polynomials above.

Definition 2

(
[
14
]
)
.

Let
w_{R}^{(a,b)}(x)
be a weight function on the interval
(\alpha,1)
given by:

\displaystyle w_{R}^{(a,b)}(x)

\displaystyle:=(x-\alpha)^{a}\>\rho(x)^{b}

and define the associated inner product by:

\displaystyle\left\langle{p,\>q}\right\rangle_{w_{R}^{(a,b)}}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\omega_{R}^{(a,b)}}\>\int_{\alpha}^{1}p(x)\>q(x)\>w_{R}^{(a,b)}(x)\>{\rm d}x

(1)

where

\displaystyle\omega_{R}^{(a,b)}:=\int_{\alpha}^{1}\>w_{R}^{(a,b)}(x)\>{\rm d}x

(2)

is a normalising constant.
Denote the two-parameter family of orthonormal polynomials on
[\alpha,\beta]
by
\{R_{n}^{(a,b)}\}
, orthonormal with respect to the inner product defined in (
1
).

We can now define the 3D OPs for the spherical cap.

Definition 3
.

Define the one-parameter 3D orthogonal polynomials via:

\displaystyle Q_{n,k,i}^{(a)}(x,y,z):=R_{n-k}^{(a,2k)}(z)\>\rho(z)^{k}\>Y_{k,i}\!\left({\frac{x}{\rho(z)},\frac{y}{\rho(z)}}\right),\quad(x,y,z)\in\Omega.

(3)

By construction,
\{Q_{n,k,i}^{(a)}\}
are orthogonal with respect to the inner product

\displaystyle\left\langle{p,\>q}\right\rangle_{Q^{(a)}}

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega}p(\mathbf{x},z)\>q(\mathbf{x},z)\>w_{R}^{(a,0)}(z)\>{\rm d}A

\displaystyle=\int_{\alpha}^{1}\int_{0}^{2\pi}p(\rho(z)\cos\theta,\rho(z)\sin\theta,z)\>q(\rho(z)\cos\theta,\rho(z)\sin\theta,z)\>{\rm d}\theta\>w_{R}^{(a,0)}(z)\>{\rm d}z,

with

\displaystyle\left\|Q_{n,k,i}^{(a)}\right\|_{Q^{(a)}}^{2}

\displaystyle:=\left\langle{Q_{n,k,i}^{(a)},\>Q_{n,k,i}^{(a)}}\right\rangle_{Q^{(a)}}=\pi\>\omega_{R}^{(a,2k)}.

(4)

We note that the weight
w_{R}^{(a,b)}(z)
has been used in the construction of 2D orthogonal polynomials on disk-slices and trapeziums
[
14
]
, where a method for obtaining recurrence coefficients and evaluating integrals was established (the weight is in fact semi-classical, and is equivalent to a generalized Jacobi weight
[
7
, §5]
).

2.2
Jacobi matrices

We can express the three-term recurrences associated with
R_{n}^{(a,b)}
as

\displaystyle xR_{n}^{(a,b)}(x)

\displaystyle=\beta_{n}^{(a,b)}R_{n+1}^{(a,b)}(x)+\alpha_{n}^{(a,b)}R_{n}^{(a,b)}(x)+\beta_{n-1}^{(a,b)}R_{n-1}^{(a,b)}(x)

(5)

where the coefficients are calculatable (see
[
14
]
). We can use (
5
) to determine the 3D recurrences for
Q_{n,k,i}^{(a)}(x,y,z)
. Importantly, we can deduce sparsity in the recurrence relationships. We first require the following lemma.

Lemma 1
.

The following identities hold for
k=2,3,\dots
,
j=0,1,\dots
and
i,h\in\{0,1\}
:

\displaystyle 1)\quad\int_{0}^{2\pi}Y_{0}\>Y_{j,h}(\theta)\>\cos\theta\>{\rm d}\theta=Y_{0}\>\pi\>\delta_{0,h}\>\delta_{1,j}

\displaystyle 2)\quad\int_{0}^{2\pi}Y_{0}\>Y_{j,h}(\theta)\>\sin\theta\>{\rm d}\theta=Y_{0}\>\pi\>\delta_{1,h}\>\delta_{1,j}

\displaystyle 3)\quad\int_{0}^{2\pi}Y_{1,i}(\theta)\>Y_{j,h}(\theta)\>\cos\theta\>{\rm d}\theta=\pi\>\delta_{i,h}\>(Y_{0}\>\delta_{0,j}+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{2,j})

\displaystyle 4)\quad\int_{0}^{2\pi}Y_{1,i}(\theta)\>Y_{j,h}(\theta)\>\sin\theta\>{\rm d}\theta=\pi\>\delta_{|i-1|,h}\>((-1)^{i+1}\>Y_{0}\>\delta_{0,j}+(-1)^{i}\>\frac{1}{2}\>\delta_{2,j})

\displaystyle 5)\quad\int_{0}^{2\pi}Y_{k,i}(\theta)\>Y_{j,h}(\theta)\>\cos\theta\>{\rm d}\theta=\frac{1}{2}\>\pi\>\delta_{i,h}\>(\delta_{k-1,j}+\delta_{k+1,j})

\displaystyle 6)\quad\int_{0}^{2\pi}Y_{k,i}(\theta)\>Y_{j,h}(\theta)\>\sin\theta\>{\rm d}\theta=\frac{1}{2}\>\pi\>\delta_{|i-1|,h}\>((-1)^{i+1}\>\delta_{k-1,j}+(-1)^{i}\>\delta_{k+1,j}).

Proof.

Each follows from the definitions of
Y_{k,i}
and
Y_{0}
, as well as the relationships:

\displaystyle 2\cos k\theta\cos\theta

\displaystyle=\cos(k-1)\theta+\cos(k+1)\theta

\displaystyle 2\sin k\theta\cos\theta

\displaystyle=\sin(k-1)\theta+\sin(k+1)\theta

\displaystyle 2\cos k\theta\sin\theta

\displaystyle=-\sin(k-1)\theta+\sin(k+1)\theta

\displaystyle 2\sin k\theta\sin\theta

\displaystyle=\cos(k-1)\theta-\cos(k+1)\theta.

∎

Lemma 2
.

Define

\displaystyle\eta_{k}:=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }k<0\\
Y_{0}&\text{if }k=0\\
\frac{1}{2}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(6)

Q_{n,k,i}^{(a)}(x,y,z)

\displaystyle x\>Q_{n,k,i}^{(a)}(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=\alpha^{(a)}_{n,k,1}\>Q^{(a)}_{n-1,k-1,i}(x,y,z)+\alpha^{(a)}_{n,k,2}\>Q^{(a)}_{n-1,k+1,i}(x,y,z)

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\alpha^{(a)}_{n,k,3}\>Q^{(a)}_{n,k-1,i}(x,y,z)+\alpha^{(a)}_{n,k,4}\>Q^{(a)}_{n,k+1,i}(x,y,z)

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\alpha^{(a)}_{n,k,5}\>Q^{(a)}_{n+1,k-1,i}(x,y,z)+\alpha^{(a)}_{n,k,6}\>Q^{(a)}_{n+1,k+1,i}(x,y,z),

\displaystyle y\>Q_{n,k,i}^{(a)}(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=\beta^{(a)}_{n,k,i,1}\>Q^{(a)}_{n-1,k-1,|i-1|}(x,y,z)+\beta^{(a)}_{n,k,i,2}\>Q^{(a)}_{n-1,k+1,|i-1|}(x,y,z)

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\beta^{(a)}_{n,k,i,3}\>Q^{(a)}_{n,k-1,|i-1|}(x,y,z)+\beta^{(a)}_{n,k,i,4}\>Q^{(a)}_{n,k+1,|i-1|}(x,y,z)

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\beta^{(a)}_{n,k,i,5}\>Q^{(a)}_{n+1,k-1,|i-1|}(x,y,z)+\beta^{(a)}_{n,k,i,6}\>Q^{(a)}_{n+1,k+1,|i-1|}(x,y,z),

\displaystyle z\>Q_{n,k,i}^{(a)}(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=\gamma^{(a)}_{n,k,1}\>Q^{(a)}_{n-1,k,i}(x,y,z)+\gamma^{(a)}_{n,k,2}\>Q^{(a)}_{n,k,i}(x,y,z)+\gamma^{(a)}_{n,k,3}\>Q^{(a)}_{n+1,k,i}(x,y,z),

for

\displaystyle\alpha^{(a)}_{n,k,1}

\displaystyle:=\eta_{k-1}\>\left\langle{R_{n-k}^{(a,2k)},\>R_{n-k}^{(a,2(k-1))}}\right\rangle_{w_{R}^{(a,2k)}},

\displaystyle\alpha^{(a)}_{n,k,2}

\displaystyle:=\eta_{k}\>\left\langle{R_{n-k}^{(a,2k)},R_{n-k-2}^{(a,2(k+1))}}\right\rangle_{w_{R}^{(a,2(k+1))}},

\displaystyle\alpha^{(a)}_{n,k,3}

\displaystyle:=\eta_{k-1}\>\left\langle{R_{n-k}^{(a,2k)},\>R_{n-k+1}^{(a,2(k-1))}}\right\rangle_{w_{R}^{(a,2k)}},

\displaystyle\alpha^{(a)}_{n,k,4}

\displaystyle:=\eta_{k}\>\left\langle{R_{n-k}^{(a,2k)},R_{n-k-1}^{(a,2(k+1))}}\right\rangle_{w_{R}^{(a,2(k+1))}},

\displaystyle\alpha^{(a)}_{n,k,5}

\displaystyle:=\eta_{k-1}\>\left\langle{R_{n-k}^{(a,2k)},\>R_{n-k+2}^{(a,2(k-1))}}\right\rangle_{w_{R}^{(a,2k)}},

\displaystyle\alpha^{(a)}_{n,k,6}

\displaystyle:=\eta_{k}\>\left\langle{R_{n-k}^{(a,2k)},R_{n-k}^{(a,2(k+1))}}\right\rangle_{w_{R}^{(a,2(k+1))}},

\displaystyle\beta^{(a)}_{n,k,i,j}

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}-\alpha^{(a)}_{n,k,j}\quad&\text{if }(i=0\text{ and }j\text{ is odd})\text{ or }(i=1\text{ and }j\text{ is even})\\
\alpha^{(a)}_{n,k,j}\quad&\text{otherwise}\end{cases},

\displaystyle\gamma^{(a)}_{n,k,1}

Remark

Proof.

These coefficients are given by

\displaystyle c_{m,j}={\left\langle{x\>Q_{n,k,i}^{(a)},Q_{m,j,h}^{(a)}}\right\rangle_{Q^{(a)}}}{\left\|Q_{m,j,h}^{(a)}\right\|^{-2}_{Q^{(a)}}}

The recurrences in Lemma

2.3
Building the OPs

Following the triangle case
[
10
]
, we use a multivariate analogue of Clenshaw’s algorithm for evaluation, where we combine each system in (
7
) into a block-tridiagonal system, for any
(x,y,z)\in\Omega
:

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}1&&&\\
B_{0}-G_{0}(x,y,z)&A_{0}&&\\
C_{1}&B_{1}-G_{1}(x,y,z)&\quad A_{1}\quad&\\
&C_{2}&B_{2}-G_{2}(x,y,z)&\ddots\\
&&\ddots&\ddots\end{pmatrix}\mathbb{{Q}}^{(a)}(x,y,z)=\begin{pmatrix}Q^{(a)}_{0}\\
0\\
0\\
0\\
\vdots\\
\end{pmatrix},

where we note
Q^{(a)}_{0}:=Q^{(a)}_{0,0,0}(x,y,z)\equiv R_{0}^{(a,0)}\>Y_{0}
, and for each
n=0,1,2\dots
,

\displaystyle A_{n}

\displaystyle:=\begin{pmatrix}A^{(a)}_{x,n}\\
A^{(a)}_{y,n}\\
A^{(a)}_{z,n}\end{pmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3(2n+1)\times(2n+3)},\quad C_{n}:=\begin{pmatrix}C^{(a)}_{x,n}\\
C^{(a)}_{y,n}\\
C^{(a)}_{z,n}\end{pmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3(2n+1)\times(2n-1)}\quad(n\neq 0),

\displaystyle B_{n}

\displaystyle:=\begin{pmatrix}B^{(a)}_{x,n}\\
B^{(a)}_{y,n}\\
B^{(a)}_{z,n}\end{pmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3(2n+1)\times(2n+1)},\quad G_{n}(x,y,z):=\begin{pmatrix}xI_{2n+1}\\
yI_{2n+1}\\
zI_{2n+1}\end{pmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3(2n+1)\times(n+1)}.

For each
n=0,1,2\dots
let
D^{\top}_{n}
be any matrix that is a left inverse of
A_{n}
, i.e. such that
D^{\top}_{n}A_{n}=I_{2n+3}
. Multiplying our system by the preconditioner matrix that is given by the block diagonal matrix of the
D^{\top}_{n}
’s, we obtain a lower triangular system
[
5
, p78]
, which can be expanded to obtain the recurrence:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\mathbb{{Q}}^{(a)}_{-1}(x,y,z):=0\\
\mathbb{{Q}}^{(a)}_{0}(x,y):=Q^{(a)}_{0}\\
\mathbb{{Q}}^{(a)}_{n+1}(x,y)=-D^{\top}_{n}(B_{n}-G_{n}(x,y,z))\mathbb{{Q}}^{(a)}_{n}(x,y,z)-D^{\top}_{n}C_{n}\,\mathbb{{Q}}^{(a)}_{n-1}(x,y,z),\quad n=0,1,2,\dots.\end{cases}

Note that we can define an explicit
D^{\top}_{n}
as follows:

\displaystyle D^{\top}_{n}:=\begin{pmatrix}0&&&0&&&(\Gamma^{(a)}_{n,0,3})^{-1}&&\\
&\ddots&&&\ddots&&&\ddots\\
&&0&&&0&&&(\Gamma^{(a)}_{n,n,3})^{-1}\\
&&&&&\bm{\eta}^{\top}_{0}&&&\\
&&&&&\bm{\eta}^{\top}_{1}&&&\end{pmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{(2n+3)\times 3(2n+1)},

for
n=1,2,\dots
where again
\Gamma^{(a)}_{n,k,3}
are defined in equations (
8
,
9
) for
k=0,\dots,n
, and where
\bm{\eta}_{0},\bm{\eta}_{1}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3(2n+1)}
with entries given by

\displaystyle\big(\bm{\eta}_{0}\big)_{j}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\beta^{(a)}_{n,n,1,6}}&j=2(2n+1)\\
\frac{-\>\beta^{(a)}_{n,n,1,5}}{\beta^{(a)}_{n,n,1,6}\>\gamma^{(a)}_{n,n-1,3}}&j=3(2n+1)-3\\
0&o/w\end{cases}

\displaystyle\big(\bm{\eta}_{1}\big)_{j}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\alpha^{(a)}_{n,n,6}}&j=2n+1\\
\frac{-\>\alpha^{(a)}_{n,n,5}}{\alpha^{(a)}_{n,n,6}\>\gamma^{(a)}_{n,n-1,3}}&j=3(2n+1)-2\text{ and }n>1\\
0&o/w\end{cases}

For
n=0
, we can simply take

\displaystyle D^{\top}_{0}

\displaystyle:=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&\frac{1}{\gamma^{(a)}_{0,0,3}}\\
\frac{1}{\alpha^{(a)}_{0,0,6}}&0&0\\
0&\frac{1}{\beta^{(a)}_{0,0,6}}&0\end{pmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3\times 3}.

It follows that we can apply
D^{\top}_{n}
in
O(n)
complexity, and thereby calculate
\mathbb{{Q}}^{(a)}_{0}(x,y,z)
through
\mathbb{{Q}}^{(a)}_{n}(x,y,z)
in optimal
O(n^{2})
complexity.

.

The recurrence coefficient matrices associated with the OPs
\{Q_{n,k,i}^{(a)}\}
are given by the matrices

for

3
Sparse partial differential operators

In this section we will derive the entries of spherical partial differential operators applied to our basis, demonstrating their sparsity in the process. To this end, as alluded to in Section
2.2
, we introduce new notation for a different ordering of the OP vector, in order to exploit the orthogonality the polynomials
Y_{k,i}
will bring and thus ensure the operators will be block-diagonal. Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
and define:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{\tilde{Q}}}^{(a)}_{N,k}

\displaystyle:=\begin{pmatrix}Q^{(a)}_{k,k,0}(x,y,z)\\
Q^{(a)}_{k,k,1}(x,y,z)\\
\vdots\\
Q^{(a)}_{N,k,0}(x,y,z)\\
Q^{(a)}_{N,k,1}(x,y,z)\end{pmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2(N-k+1)},\quad k=1,\dots,N,

(10)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{\tilde{Q}}}^{(a)}_{N,0}

\displaystyle:=\begin{pmatrix}Q^{(a)}_{0,0,0}(x,y,z)\\
\vdots\\
Q^{(a)}_{N,0,0}(x,y,z)\\
\end{pmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N+1},

(11)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{\tilde{Q}}}^{(a)}_{N}

\displaystyle:=\begin{pmatrix}{\mathbb{\tilde{Q}}}^{(a)}_{N,0}\\
\vdots\\
{\mathbb{\tilde{Q}}}^{(a)}_{N,N}\end{pmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{(N+1)^{2}}

(12)

We further denote the weighted set of OPs on
\Omega

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}_{N}^{(a)}(x,y,z):=w_{R}^{(a,0)}(z)\>{\mathbb{\tilde{Q}}}^{(a)}_{N}(x,y,z),

The operator matrices we derive here act on coefficient vectors, that represent a function

defined on
\Omega
in spectral space – such a function is approximated by its expansion up to degree
N

\displaystyle f(x,y,z)={\mathbb{\tilde{Q}}}^{(a)}_{N}(x,y,z)^{\top}\bm{f}=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\sum_{i=0}^{1}f_{n,k,i}\>Q_{n,k,i}^{(a)}(x,y,z),

where

Lemma 4
.

The operator matrices in Definition

are sparse, with banded-block-banded structure. More specifically:

•

(0,2\tilde{a})

•

(2\tilde{a},0)

Proof.

We proceed with the case for the non-weighted operators

,

and

\displaystyle t_{m,j}=\left\|Q_{m,j,h}^{(a+\tilde{a})}\right\|^{-2}_{Q^{(a+\tilde{a})}}\>\left\langle{Q_{n,k,i}^{(a)},Q_{m,j,h}^{(a+\tilde{a})}}\right\rangle_{Q^{(a+\tilde{a})}}.

where

\displaystyle\left\langle{Q_{n,k,i}^{(a)},Q_{m,j,h}^{(a+\tilde{a})}}\right\rangle_{Q^{(a+\tilde{a})}}

\hat{\bm{n}}(x,y,z)
is the outward unit normal vector at the point
(x,y,z)
on the boundary, i.e.
\hat{\bm{n}}(x,y,z)=\hat{\bm{n}}(\bm{x}):={\bm{x}\over\left\|\bm{x}\right\|}=\bm{x}
. The matrix operator for the Biharmonic operator will take us from coefficients in the space
\mathbb{W}^{(2)}(x,y,z)
to coefficients in the space
{\mathbb{\tilde{Q}}}_{N}^{(2)}(x,y,z)
. To construct this, we can simply multiply together two of the spherical Laplacian operators defined in Definition
6
, namely
\mathcal{L}^{(0)\to(2)}
and
\mathcal{L}_{W}^{(2)\to(0)}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}^{(2)}_{W}:=\mathcal{L}^{(0)\to(2)}\>\mathcal{L}_{W}^{(2)\to(0)}.

Since the operator
\mathcal{L}_{W}^{(2)\to(0)}
acts on coefficients in the
\mathbb{W}^{(2)}(x,y,z)
space, we ensure that we satisfy the zero Dirichlet and Neumann boundary conditions – such a function could be written
u(x,y,z)=w_{R}^{(2,0)}(z)\>\tilde{u}(x,y,z)
and thus its spherical gradient would be zero on the boundary
z=\alpha
. This allows us to apply the
\mathcal{L}^{(0)\to(2)}
operator after, safe in the knowledge that boundary conditions have been accounted for. The sparsity and structure of this biharmonic operator are seen in Figure
1
.

4
Computational aspects

In this section we discuss how to expand and evaluate functions in our proposed basis, and take advantage of the sparsity structure in partial differential operators in practical computational applications.

4.1
Constructing
R_{n}^{(a,b)}(x)

It is possible to recursively obtain the recurrence coefficients for the
\{R_{n}^{(a,b)}\}
OPs in (
5

[
14
]
, by careful application of the Christoffel–Darboux formula
[
8
, 18.2.12]
.

4.2
Quadrature rule on the spherical cap

In this section we construct a quadrature rule exact for polynomials on the spherical cap
\Omega
that can be used to expand functions in the OPs
Q_{n,k,i}^{(a)}(x,y,z)
for a given parameter
a
.

Theorem 2
.

Let
M_{1},M_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
and denote the
M_{1}
Gauss quadrature nodes and weights on
[\alpha,1]
with weight
(t-\alpha)^{a}
as
(t_{j},w_{j}^{(t)})
. Further, denote the
M_{2}
Gauss quadrature nodes and weights
[-1,1]
with weight
(1-x^{2})^{-\frac{1}{2}}
as
(s_{j},w_{j}^{(s)})
.
Define for
j=1,\dots,M_{1},\>l=1,\dots,M_{2}
:

\displaystyle\big(x_{l+(j-1)M_{2}},\>y_{l+(j-1)M_{2}}\big)

\displaystyle:=\rho(t_{j})\>\mathbf{s}_{l},

\displaystyle z_{l+(j-1)M_{2}}

\displaystyle:=t_{j},

\displaystyle w_{l+(j-1)M_{2}}

\displaystyle:=w_{j}^{(t)}w_{l}^{(s)}.

Let
f(x,y,z)
be a function on
\Omega
, and
N\in\mathbb{N}
. The quadrature rule is then

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}f(x,y,z)\>w_{R}^{(a,0)}(z)\>{\rm d}A\approx\sum_{j=1}^{M}w_{j}\>\big[f(x_{j},y_{j},z_{j})+f(-x_{j},-y_{j},z_{j})\big],

where
M=M_{1}\>M_{2}
, and the quadrature rule is exact if
f(x,y,z)
is a polynomial of degree
\leq N
with
M_{1}\geq\frac{1}{2}(N+1),M_{2}\geq N+1
.

Remark
:
Note that the Gauss quadrature nodes and weights
(t_{j},w_{j}^{(t)})
will have to be calculated, however the Gauss quadrature nodes and weights
(s_{j},w_{j}^{(s)})
are simply the Chebyshev–Gauss quadrature nodes and weights given explicitly
[
8
, 3.5.23]
as
s_{j}:=\cos\!\left({\frac{2j-1}{2M_{2}}\pi}\right)
,
w_{j}^{(s)}:=\frac{\pi}{M_{2}}
.

Proof.

Let
f:\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}
. Define the functions
f_{e},f_{o}:\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}
by

\displaystyle f_{e}(x,y,z)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\Big(f(x,y,z)+f(-x,-y,z)\Big),\quad\forall(x,y,z)\in\Omega

\displaystyle f_{o}(x,y,z)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\Big(f(x,y,z)-f(-x,-y,z)\Big),\quad\forall(x,y,z)\in\Omega

so that
\mathbf{x}\mapsto f_{e}(\mathbf{x},z)
for fixed
z
is an even function, and
\mathbf{x}\mapsto f_{o}(\mathbf{x},z)
for fixed
z
is an odd function. Note that if
f
is a polynomial, then
f_{e}(\rho(t)x,\rho(t)y,t)
is a polynomial in
t\in[\alpha,1]
for fixed
(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
.

Firstly, we note that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}g\big(\cos\theta,\sin\theta\big)\>{\rm d}\theta=\int_{-1}^{1}\Big(g\big(x,\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\big)+g\big(x,-\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\big)\Big)\frac{{\rm d}x}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}

for some function
g
. Then, integrating the even function
f_{e}
we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}f_{e}(x,y,z)\>w_{R}^{(a,0)}(z)\>{\rm d}A

\displaystyle\quad\quad=\int_{\alpha}^{1}w_{R}^{(a,0)}(z)\>\Big(\int_{0}^{2\pi}f_{e}\big(\rho(z)\cos\theta,\rho(z)\sin\theta,z\big)\>{\rm d}\theta\Big)\>{\rm d}z

\displaystyle\quad\quad=2\int_{\alpha}^{1}w_{R}^{(a,0)}(z)\>\Big(\int_{-1}^{1}f_{e}\big(\rho(z)x,\rho(z)\sqrt{1-x^{2}},z\big)\>{\rm d}x\Big)\>{\rm d}z

\displaystyle\quad\quad\approx\int_{\alpha}^{1}w_{R}^{(a,0)}(z)\>\Big(\sum_{l=1}^{M_{2}}w_{l}^{(s)}f_{e}\big(\rho(z)s_{l},\rho(z)\sqrt{1-s_{l}^{2}},z\big)\Big)\>{\rm d}z\quad(\star)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\approx\sum_{j=1}^{M_{1}}w_{j}^{(t)}\sum_{l=1}^{M_{2}}w_{l}^{(s)}f_{e}\big(\rho(t_{j})s_{l},\rho(t_{j})\sqrt{1-s_{l}^{2}},t_{j}\big)\quad(\star\star)

\displaystyle\quad\quad=\sum_{k=1}^{M_{1}M_{2}}w_{j}\>f_{e}(x_{j},y_{j},z_{j}).

Suppose
f
is a polynomial in
x,y,z
of degree
N
, and hence that
f_{e}
is a degree
\leq N
polynomial. It follows that
s\mapsto f_{e}\big(\rho(z)s,\rho(z)\sqrt{1-s^{2}},z\big)
for fixed
z
is then a polynomial of degree
\leq N
. We therefore achieve equality at
(\star)
if
2M_{2}-1\geq N
and we achieve equality at
(\star\star)
if also
2M_{1}-1\geq N
.

Integrating the odd function
f_{o}
results in

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}f_{o}(x,y,z)\>w_{R}^{(a,0)}(z)\>{\rm d}A

\displaystyle\quad\quad=\int_{\alpha}^{1}w_{R}^{(a,0)}(z)\>\Big(\int_{0}^{2\pi}f_{o}\big(\rho(z)\cos\theta,\rho(z)\sin\theta,z)\big)\>{\rm d}\theta\Big)\>{\rm d}z

\displaystyle\quad\quad=\int_{\alpha}^{1}w_{R}^{(a,0)}(z)\>\Big(\int_{-1}^{1}\Big[f_{o}\big(\rho(z)x,\rho(z)\sqrt{1-x^{2}},z\big)+f_{o}\big(\rho(z)x,-\rho(z)\sqrt{1-x^{2}},z\big)\big]\>{\rm d}x\Big)\>{\rm d}z

\displaystyle\quad\quad=\int_{\alpha}^{1}w_{R}^{(a,0)}(z)\>\Big(\int_{-1}^{1}\Big[f_{o}\big(\rho(z)x,\rho(z)\sqrt{1-x^{2}},z\big)-f_{o}\big(\rho(z)x,\rho(z)\sqrt{1-x^{2}},z\big)\big]\>{\rm d}x\Big)\>{\rm d}z

\displaystyle\quad\quad=0.

since
f_{o}(x,y,z)=-f_{o}(-x,-y,z)
. Hence, for a polynomial
f
in
x,y,z
of degree
N
,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}f(x,y,z)\>w_{R}^{(a,0)}(z)\>{\rm d}A

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\Big(f_{e}(x,y,z)+f_{o}(x,y,z)\Big)\>w_{R}^{(a,0)}(z)\>{\rm d}A

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}f_{e}(x,y,z)\>w_{R}^{(a,0)}(z)\>{\rm d}A

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{M}w_{j}\>f_{e}(x_{j},y_{j},z_{j}),

where
M=M_{1}M_{2}
and
2M_{1}-1\geq N,2M_{2}-1\geq N
.
∎

4.3
Obtaining the coefficients for expansion of a function on the spherical cap

Fix
a\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Then for any function
f:\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}
we can express
f
by

\displaystyle f(x,y,z)\approx\sum_{k=0}^{N}{\mathbb{\tilde{Q}}}^{(a)}_{N,k}(x,y,z)^{\top}\>\bm{f}_{k}={\mathbb{\tilde{Q}}}^{(a)}_{N}(x,y,z)^{\top}\>\bm{f}

for N sufficiently large, where
{\mathbb{\tilde{Q}}}^{(a)}_{N,k},{\mathbb{\tilde{Q}}}^{(a)}_{N}
is defined in equations (
10
,
11
,
12
) and where

\displaystyle\bm{f}_{k}

\displaystyle:=\begin{pmatrix}f_{k,k,0}\\
f_{k,k,1}\\
\vdots\\
f_{N,k,0}\\
f_{N,k,1}\end{pmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2(N-k+1)}\quad\text{for }n=1,2,\dots,N,\quad\bm{f}_{0}:=\begin{pmatrix}f_{0,0,0}\\
\vdots\\
f_{N,0,0}\end{pmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N+1},

\displaystyle\bm{f}

\displaystyle:=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{f}_{0}\\
\vdots\\
\bm{f}_{N}\end{pmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2(N+1)^{2}},\quad\quad f_{n,k,i}:=\left\langle{f,\>Q_{n,k,i}^{(a)}}\right\rangle_{Q^{(a)}}\>\left\|Q_{n,k,i}^{(a)}\right\|^{-2}_{Q^{(a)}}.

Recall from equation (
4
) that
\left\|Q_{n,k,i}^{(a)}\right\|^{2}_{Q^{(a)}}=\omega_{R}^{(a,2k)}\>\pi
. Using the quadrature rule detailed in Section
4.2
for the inner product, we can calculate the coefficients
f_{n,k,i}
for each
n=0,\dots,N
,
k=0,\dots,n
,
i=0,1
:

\displaystyle f_{n,k,i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\>\omega_{R}^{(a,2k)}\>\pi}\sum_{j=1}^{M}w_{j}\big[f(x_{j},y_{j},z_{j})Q_{n,k,i}^{(a)}(x_{j},y_{j},z_{j})+f(-x_{j},-y_{j},z_{j})Q_{n,k,i}^{(a)}(-x_{j},-y_{j},z_{j})\big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M_{2}\>\omega_{R}^{(a,2k)}}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\big[f(x_{j},y_{j},z_{j})Q_{n,k,i}^{(a)}(x_{j},y_{j},z_{j})+f(-x_{j},-y_{j},z_{j})Q_{n,k,i}^{(a)}(-x_{j},-y_{j},z_{j})\big]

where the quadrature nodes and weights are those from Theorem
2
, and
M=M_{1}M_{2}
with
2M_{1}-1\geq N,M_{2}-1\geq N
(i.e. we can choose
M_{2}:=N+1
and
M_{1}:=\left\lceil\frac{N+1}{2}\right\rceil
).

4.4
Function evaluation

For a function
f
, with coefficients vector
\bm{f}
for expansion in the
\{Q_{n,k,i}\}
basis as determined via the method in Section
4.3
up to order
N
, we can use the Clenshaw algorithm to evaluate the function at a point
(x,y,z)\in\Omega
as follows. Let
A_{n},B_{n},D^{\top}_{n},C_{n}
be the Clenshaw matrices from Definition
5
, and define the rearranged coefficients vector
\tilde{\bm{f}}
via

\displaystyle{\bm{f}}_{n}

\displaystyle:=\begin{pmatrix}f_{n,0,0}\\
f_{n,1,0}\\
f_{n,1,1}\\
\vdots\\
f_{n,n,0}\\
f_{n,n,1}\end{pmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2(N+1)}\quad\text{for }n=1,2,\dots,N,\quad{\bm{f}}_{0}=f_{0,0,0}\in{\mathbb{R}},

\displaystyle{\bm{f}}

\displaystyle:=\begin{pmatrix}{\bm{f}}_{0}\\
\vdots\\
{\bm{f}}_{N}\end{pmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{(N+1)^{2}}.

The trivariate Clenshaw algorithm works similar to the bivariate Clenshaw algorithm introduced in
[
10
]
for expansions in the triangle:

\displaystyle\text{1) }\text{Set }\bm{\xi}_{N+2}=\mathbb{0},\>\bm{\xi}_{N+2}=\mathbb{0}.

\displaystyle\text{2) }\text{For }n=N:-1:0

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\text{set }\bm{\xi}_{n}^{T}={{\bm{f}}_{n}}^{T}-\bm{\xi}_{n+1}^{T}D^{T}_{n}(B_{n}-G_{n}(x,y,z))-\bm{\xi}_{n+2}^{T}D^{T}_{n+1}C_{n+1}

\displaystyle\text{3) }\text{Output: }f(x,y,z)\approx\bm{\xi}_{0}\>\mathbb{{Q}}^{(a)}_{0}=\xi_{0}\>Q^{(a)}_{0}

4.5
Calculating non-zero entries of the operator matrices

The proofs of Theorem
1
and Lemma
4
provide a way to calculate the non-zero entries of the operator matrices given in Definition
6
and Definition
7
. We can simply use quadrature to calculate the 1D inner products, which has a complexity of
\mathcal{O}(N^{2})
. This proves much cheaper computationally than using the 3D quadrature rule to calculate the surface inner products, which has a complexity of
\mathcal{O}(N^{3})
.

4.6
Obtaining operator matrices for variable coefficients

The Clenshaw algorithm outlined in Section
4.4
can also be used with Jacobi matrices
J^{(a)}_{x},J^{(a)}_{y},J^{(a)}_{z}
replacing the point
(x,y,z)
. Let
v:\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}
be the function that we wish to obtain an operator matrix
V
for
v
, so that

\displaystyle v(x,y,z)\>f(x,y,z)=v(x,y,z)\>\bm{f}\>\mathbb{{Q}}^{(a)}(x,y,z)=(V\bm{f})^{\top}\>\mathbb{{Q}}^{(a)}(x,y,z),

i.e.
V\bm{f}
is the coefficients vector for the function
v(x,y,z)\>f(x,y,z)
.

To this end, let
\tilde{\bm{v}}
be the coefficients for expansion up to order
N
in the
\{Q_{n,k,i}\}
basis of
v
(rearranged as in Section
4.4
so that
v(x,y,z)={\bm{v}}^{\top}\>\mathbb{{Q}}^{(a)}(x,y,z)
). Denote
X:=(J_{x}^{(a)})^{\top}
,
Y:=(J_{y}^{(a)})^{\top}
,
Z:=(J_{z}^{(a)})^{\top}
. The operator
V
is then the result of the following:

\displaystyle\text{1) }\text{Set }\bm{\xi}_{N+2}=\mathbb{0},\>\bm{\xi}_{N+2}=\mathbb{0}.

\displaystyle\text{2) }\text{For }n=N:-1:0

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\text{set }\bm{\xi}_{n}^{T}={\tilde{\bm{v}}_{n}}^{T}-\bm{\xi}_{n+1}^{T}D^{T}_{n}\big(B_{n}-G_{n}(X,Y,Z)\big)-\bm{\xi}_{n+2}^{T}D^{T}_{n+1}C_{n+1}

\displaystyle\text{3) }\text{Output: }V(X,Y,Z)\approx\bm{\xi}_{0}\>\mathbb{{Q}}^{(a)}_{0}=\xi_{0}\>Q^{(a)}_{0}

where at each iteration,
\bm{\xi}_{n}
is a vector of matrices.

\displaystyle g(x,y,z)

\displaystyle:=f(x,y,z)-k^{2}\>v(x,y,z)\>c(x,y)-\Delta_{\rm S}\>c(x,y)

for
(x,y,z)\in\Omega
. Notice that the spherical Laplacian applied to

\displaystyle\Delta_{\rm S}\>c(x,y)={1\over\rho(z)^{2}}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\sum_{i=0}^{1}c_{n,n,i}\>{\partial^{2}\over\partial{\theta}^{2}}Y_{n,i}(\theta)=-{1\over\rho(z)^{2}}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\sum_{i=0}^{1}n^{2}\>c_{n,n,i}\>Y_{n,i}(\theta)

since the coefficients

6
Conclusions

We have shown that trivariate orthogonal polynomials can lead to sparse discretizations of general linear PDEs on spherical cap domains, with Dirichlet boundary conditions on the
z=\alpha\in(0,1)
boundary. We have provided a detailed practical framework for the application of the methods described for quadratic surfaces of revolution
[
12
]
, by utilising the non-classical 1D OPs on the interval
[\alpha,1]
with the weight
(z-\alpha)^{a}\>(1-z^{2})^{b/2}
defined for the disk-slice case
[
14
]
. Generalisation to spherical bands (
\alpha\leq z\leq\beta
) is straightforward. This work thus forms a building block in developing an
hp-
finite element method to solve PDEs on the sphere by using spherical band and spherical cap shaped elements.

This work also serves as a stepping stone to constructing similar methods to solve partial differential equations on other 3D sub-domains of the sphere—it is clear from the construction in this paper that discretizations of spherical gradients and Laplacian’s are sparse on other suitable sub-components of the sphere. The resulting sparsity in high-polynomial degree discretizations presents an attractive alternative to methods based on bijective mappings (e.g.,
[
2
,
13
,
3
]
). Constructing these sparse spectral methods for surface PDEs on spherical triangles is future work, and has applications in weather prediction
[
15
]
, though it is not yet clear how to directly construct the necessary orthogonal polynomials.

The next stage is to develop an orthogonal basis for the tangent space of the spherical cap (or band), and obtain sparse differential operators for gradient, divergence etc. On the complete sphere, the vector spherical harmonics that form the orthogonal basis are simply the gradients and perpendicular gradients of the scalar spherical harmonics
[
1
]
which has been used effectively for solving PDEs on the sphere
[
17
,
6
]
– however, we do not have that luxury for the spherical cap or band, and hence the choice of basis will not be as straightforward.
