Abstract
We present a space–time virtual element method for the discretization of the heat equation, which is defined on general prismatic meshes and variable degrees of accuracy. Strategies to handle efficiently the space–time mesh structure are discussed. We perform convergence tests for the h h - and h ​ p hp -versions of the method in case of smooth and singular solutions, and test space–time adaptive mesh refinements driven by a residual-type error indicator. AMS subject classification : 35K05; 65N12; 65N30. Keywords : virtual element methods; heat equation; space–time methods; polytopic meshes.



1
Introduction

Space–time Galerkin methods aim at approximating solutions to time-dependent partial differential equations
treating the time variable as an additional space variable.
Even though the foundation of space–time Galerkin methods
traces back to the 70ies of the last century
[
15
]

and a few contributions were developed
in the twenty years to follow

[
2
,
3
,
8
,
14
,
9
]
,
only in the last two decades
there has been a growing attention on this topic,
mainly due to the improved performance of computers.

Compared to the time-stepping approach,
space–time Galerkin methods have some important
upsides and features:
the discrete solution can be evaluated on
the whole space–time domain and not only at a finite number of times
without additional post-processing of the discrete solution;
such methods allow for space–time adaptivity;
it is possible to design space–time parallel solvers.

In this paper, we focus on the approximation of solutions to the heat equation.
Several space–time methods have been designed to this aim
and they can be classified into two main groups.
The first one is based on the discretization of a standard Petrov-Galerkin formulation
[
6
]
;
see
[
1
,
21
]
for continuous finite element methods,

[
20
]
for a wavelet method,
[
26
,
4
]
for discontinuous Galerkin methods,

[
16
]
for an isogeometric method,

[
24
]
for a coercive method based on a Hilbert transformation of the test space,
and
[
25
]

for a mixed finite element method. Residual-type error indicators for the method of
[
21
]
were considered in
[
22
,
23
]
.
Possible drawbacks of employing continuous finite elements are that
suboptimal convergence rates are obtained for some singular solutions,
and incompatible boundary and initial conditions
cannot be naturally handled.

The second group is based on first order system least squares discretizations (FOSLS);
see
[
11
,
12
,
27
]
for finite element methods
and
[
17
]

for isogeometric methods. Space–time FOSLS finite elements naturally provide reliable and efficient error indicators;
see
[
11
,
18
,
12
]
.
However, they require the computation of an additional vector-valued flux variable.

In this paper, we extend the nonconforming space–time virtual element method of
[
13
]

to the case of general prismatic meshes and nonuniform degrees of accuracy.
This method allows for the use of space–time meshes with hanging facets (nodes, edges, faces)
and is based on discontinuous in time test and trial functions that are solutions to local space–time problems with polynomial data.
This approach provides a natural framework for space–time adaptivity without need of re-meshing neighbouring elements.
If the space–time mesh is decomposed into separate time-slabs,
the global linear system can be split into much smaller systems that can be solved sequentially.
Due to the nonconformity across time-like facets,
the design and implementation of the method
are independent of the spatial dimension.
No artificial compatibility of
initial and boundary conditions
is enforced on the trial virtual element space,
and optimal convergence rates have been achieved also for singular solutions.
However, the stability analysis of the method
relies on a discrete inf-sup condition
and the method requires a stabilization term for the spatial Laplacian
that needs to be carefully designed;
see
[
13
]
.

The main goals of this manuscript are:

•

to construct a space–time virtual element method on general prismatic meshes, possibly with hanging facets and variable degrees of accuracy;

•

to discuss suitable strategies to handle efficiently the space–time mesh structure;

•

to investigate
h
- and
hp
- refinements numerically;

•

to test space–time adaptive mesh refinements driven by a residual-type error indicator.

In the remainder of this section, we introduce some notation and the model problem.
Finally, we outline the structure of the paper.

1.2
Model problem

We consider the heat equation
on the space–time domain
Q_{T}:=\Omega\times(0,T)
,
where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d=1,\ 2,\ 3
,
and
T>0

are the (bounded) spatial domain and the final time, respectively.

Associated with
Q_{T}
,
we introduce the space-like surface
\Sigma_{0}:=\Omega\times\left\{0\right\}

and the time-like surface
\Sigma_{D}:=\partial\Omega\times(0,T)
.

Let
f:Q_{T}\to\mathbb{R}
,
u_{0}:\Sigma_{0}\to\mathbb{R}
, and
g:\Sigma_{D}\to\mathbb{R}

denote the source term, initial condition, and Dirichlet boundary condition, respectively.
We assume
f\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{-1}(\Omega))
.
We further consider
c_{H}>0
and
\nu>0

given positive constant volumetric heat capacity and thermal conductivity, respectively.

The heat equation in strong formulation reads:
find a function
u:Q_{T}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\begin{cases}c_{H}\partial_{t}{u}-\nu\Delta_{\mathbf{x}}u=f&\text{in }Q_{T},\\
u=u_{0}&\text{on }\Sigma_{0},\\
u=g&\text{on }\Sigma_{D}.\end{cases}

(3)

To simplify the presentation, we henceforth assume that
u_{0}=0
and
g=0
.
Nonetheless,
in Sections
4
and
5
,
we present numerical results for solutions with inhomogeneous initial and boundary conditions.
Inhomogeneous boundary conditions can be treated as in the elliptic case
and inhomogeneous initial conditions can be dealt with
by standard lifting arguments.

Define the space–time bilinear form

b(\cdot,\cdot):X\times Y\to\mathbb{R}
as

b(u,v):=\int_{0}^{T}\langle c_{H}\partial_{t}{u},v\rangle\mbox{dt}+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\nu\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}u\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}v\,\mbox{d}\mathbf{x}\mbox{dt}.

(4)

We consider the Petrov-Galerkin weak formulation of (
3
)
as in
[
6
]
:

\begin{cases}\text{find }u\in X\text{ such that}\\
b(u,v)=\int_{0}^{T}\langle f,v\rangle\mbox{dt}\qquad\forall v\in Y.\end{cases}

(5)

Problem (
5
) is well posed;
see, e.g.,
[
21
, Corollary
2.3
]
.

2.4
Global nonconforming space–time virtual element spaces

We design a global virtual element space
Y_{h}
,
consisting of functions that are discontinuous in time and
nonconforming in space.

To this aim, we introduce the jump operator
on each time-like facet
F

as the functional
\left[\!\left[\cdot\right]\!\right]_{F}:H^{\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon}(\mathcal{T}_{h})\to[L^{2}(F)]^{d+1}
,

\varepsilon>0
,
given by

\left[\!\left[v\right]\!\right]_{F}:=\begin{cases}v_{|\mathcal{K}_{1}}\mathbf{n}_{K_{1}}^{F}+v_{|\mathcal{K}_{2}}\mathbf{n}_{K_{2}}^{F}&\text{if }F\subset\partial\mathcal{K}_{1}\cap\partial\mathcal{K}_{2}\text{ is an internal facet, for }\mathcal{K}_{1},\mathcal{K}_{2}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\\
v_{|\mathcal{K}_{3}}\mathbf{n}_{K_{3}}^{F}&\text{if }F\subset\partial\mathcal{K}_{3}\text{ is a boundary facet, for }\mathcal{K}_{3}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}.\end{cases}

We define the Sobolev nonconforming space of order
p

\mathcal{T}_{h}
as

\begin{split}\mathcal{NC}_{p}(\mathcal{T}_{h}):=\Big\{v\in L^{2}(Q_{T})\Big|&\,v_{|\mathcal{K}}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{K}_{t},H^{1}(\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{x}}))\;\forall\mathcal{K}\in\mathcal{T}_{h};\\
&\int_{F}q_{p}^{F}\left[\!\left[v\right]\!\right]_{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{F}\mbox{d}S\ =0\quad\forall q_{p}^{F}\in\mathbb{P}_{p}\left(F\right),\;\forall F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}^{\text{time}}\Big\}.\end{split}

(15)

We define the global virtual element space as follows:

\begin{split}Y_{h}:=\Big\{v_{h}\in L^{2}(Q_{T})\ \Big|\ &v_{h}{}_{|\mathcal{K}}\in V_{h}(K)\;\;\forall K\in\mathcal{T}_{h};\ v_{h}\in\mathcal{NC}_{p}(\mathcal{T}_{h})\Big\}.\end{split}

2.5
The discrete bilinear forms

Since functions in the space–time virtual element space
Y_{h}
are not available in closed form,
we discretize the bilinear forms by computable counterparts.
On each element
\mathcal{K}
, define the local continuous bilinear form
and the induced norm

a^{\mathcal{K}}(u_{h},v_{h}):=\nu(\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}u_{h},\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}v_{h})_{0,\mathcal{K}},\qquad\qquad{\left|{v_{h}}\right|}_{Y(\mathcal{K})}^{2}:=a^{\mathcal{K}}(v_{h},v_{h})=\nu{\left\|{\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}v_{h}}\right\|}_{0,\mathcal{K}}^{2}.

Let
S^{K}:V_{h}(K)\times V_{h}(K)\to\mathbb{R}
be any symmetric bilinear form,
which is computable via the degrees of freedom and satisfies the following property:
there exist
0<c_{*}<c^{*}
independent of
K
such that

c_{*}{\left|{v_{h}}\right|}_{Y(\mathcal{K})}^{2}\leq\nu S^{K}(v_{h},v_{h})\leq c^{*}{\left|{v_{h}}\right|}_{Y(\mathcal{K})}^{2}\qquad\qquad\forall v_{h}\in V_{h}(K)\cap\ker(\Pi^{N}_{p}).

(16)

Define

a_{h}^{K}(u_{h},v_{h}):=a^{\mathcal{K}}(\Pi^{N}_{p}u_{h},\Pi^{N}_{p}v_{h})+\nu S^{K}((I-\Pi^{N}_{p})u_{h},(I-\Pi^{N}_{p})v_{h}).

(17)

Extending
[
13
, Lemma 2.7]

to the case of general prismatic-type elements
as in Section
2.1
,
it is possible to prove that there exist
0<\alpha_{*}<\alpha^{*}
independent of
K
such that
the following local stability bounds are valid:

\alpha_{*}{\left|{v_{h}}\right|}_{Y(\mathcal{K})}^{2}\leq a_{h}^{K}(v_{h},v_{h})\leq\alpha^{*}{\left|{v_{h}}\right|}_{Y(\mathcal{K})}^{2}\qquad\qquad\forall v_{h}\in V_{h}(K).

(18)

Besides, we introduce upwind-type terms,
which allows for imposing weakly the continuity in time of the trial functions:
for all space-like facets
K_{\mathbf{x}}\subset\Omega\times\{t^{*}\}
,
t^{*}\in[0,T]
,

\mathcal{U}^{K_{\mathbf{x}}}(u_{h}):=\begin{cases}c_{H}\Pi^{\star}_{p}u_{h}{}_{|\mathcal{K}}(\cdot,t^{*})&\text{if }t^{*}=0\\
c_{H}\left(\Pi^{\star}_{p}u_{h}{}_{|\mathcal{K}^{+}}(\cdot,t^{*})-\Pi^{\star}_{p}u_{h}{}_{|\mathcal{K}^{-}}(\cdot,t^{*})\right)&\text{if }t^{*}>0,\end{cases}

(19)

where
K_{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{K}}^{\text{space}}
if
t^{*}=0
,
and
K_{\mathbf{x}}\in F^{\text{space}}_{\mathcal{K}^{+}}
is a subset of
\partial\mathcal{K}^{+}\cap\partial\mathcal{K}^{-}
if
t^{*}>0
.

Remark 1
.

The case of nonzero initial conditions can be dealt with
by modifying the definition of the upwind functional at time
t=0
as follows: for all space-like facets
K_{\mathbf{x}}\subset\Omega\times\{0\}
,

\mathcal{U}^{K_{\mathbf{x}}}(u_{h}):=c_{H}\left(\Pi^{\star}_{p}u_{h}{}_{|\mathcal{K}}(\cdot,0)-u_{0}\right).

The computability of the upwind terms follows from the definition
of the space-like moment degrees of freedom (
10
)
and the computability of the projector
\Pi^{\star}_{p}
.

We define a discrete counterpart of the bilinear
form
b(\cdot,\cdot)
in (
4
) as follows:

b_{h}(u_{h},v_{h}):=\sum_{\mathcal{K}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\left[c_{H}(\partial_{t}{\Pi^{\star}_{p}}u_{h},v_{h})_{0,\mathcal{K}}+a_{h}^{K}(u_{h},v_{h})\right]+\sum_{K_{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{F}_{h}^{\text{space}}}\left(\mathcal{U}^{K_{\mathbf{x}}}(u_{h}),v_{h}{}_{|\mathcal{K}^{+}}\right)_{0,K_{\mathbf{x}}}.

(20)

A computable stabilization satisfying (
18
) is given by

\begin{split}S^{K}(u_{h},v_{h})&:=p^{2}h_{\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{x}}}^{-2}(\Pi^{0,\mathcal{K}}_{p-1}u_{h},\Pi^{0,\mathcal{K}}_{p-1}v_{h})_{0,\mathcal{K}}+\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{K}}^{\text{time}}}ph_{F_{\mathbf{x}}}^{-1}(\Pi^{0,F}_{p}u_{h},\Pi^{0,F}_{p}v_{h})_{0,F}\\
&\quad+ph_{\mathcal{K}_{t}}h_{\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{x}}}^{-2}(u_{h},v_{h})_{0,\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{x}}}.\end{split}

(21)

2.6
The method

Henceforth, we assume that
f
belongs to
L^{2}(Q_{T})
.
The space–time virtual element method for the approximation of solutions to (
5
) reads

\begin{cases}\text{find }u_{h}\in Y_{h}\text{ such that}\\
b_{h}(u_{h},v_{h})=(f,\Pi^{0}_{p-1}v_{h})_{0,\Omega}\qquad\forall v_{h}\in Y_{h}.\end{cases}

(22)

For Cartesian-product-in-time meshes, the well posedness of the method and

error estimates
were proven in
[

, Sections 3 and 4]
based on a discrete inf-sup argument.
That analysis can be extended to the meshes introduced in Section

, using the norms

\displaystyle{\left\|{v}\right\|}_{Y(\mathcal{T}_{h})}^{2}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{\mathcal{K}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}{\left\|{v}\right\|}_{Y(\mathcal{K})}^{2},

\displaystyle{\left\|{v}\right\|}_{X(\mathcal{T}_{h})}^{2}

\displaystyle:={\left\|{v}\right\|}_{Y(\mathcal{T}_{h})}^{2}+{\left\|{\mathfrak{N}_{h}\Pi^{\star}_{p}v}\right\|}_{Y(\mathcal{T}_{h})}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{c_{H}}{2}\Big({\left\|{\Pi^{\star}_{p}v(\cdot,0)}\right\|}_{0,\Omega}^{2}+\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\sum_{\scriptsize\begin{tabular}[]{c}$K_{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{K}}^{\text{space}}$\\
$K_{\mathbf{x}}\not\subset\Omega\times\{0\}$\end{tabular}}{\left\|{\mathcal{U}^{K_{\mathbf{x}}}(v)}\right\|}_{0,K_{\mathbf{x}}}^{2}+{\left\|{\Pi^{\star}_{p}v(\cdot,T)}\right\|}_{0,\Omega}^{2}\Big),

where the discrete Newton potential

is defined as follows: for all

,

\begin{split}a_{h}(\mathfrak{N}_{h}\phi_{h},v_{h})=c_{H}\Big((\partial_{t}{\phi_{h}},v_{h})_{0,Q_{T}}+\left(\phi_{h}(\cdot,0),v_{h}(\cdot,0)\right)_{0,\Omega}+\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\sum_{\scriptsize\begin{tabular}[]{c}$K_{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{K}}^{\text{space}}$\\

There exists

3.2
Time-slab flagging strategy

We present a flagging strategy that allows for the decomposition

To this aim, we assume that the first mesh of a sequence

Assume now that we are given a mesh at the refinement step

We explain here how to identify a time-slab partition of the refined mesh at step

The time interval

5
Numerical investigation: an adaptive procedure

We consider a standard adaptive algorithm of the form

\textbf{SOLVE}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\textbf{ESTIMATE}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\textbf{MARK}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\textbf{REFINE}.

(27)

We base the
MARK
step on a Dörfler marking strategy
[
7
]

with parameter
\theta

that we shall specify at each occurrence:

\text{mark a set }\mathcal{A}\qquad\text{ such that }\qquad\sum_{K\in\mathcal{A}}\eta_{K}^{2}\geq\theta\eta^{2}.

The
REFINE
step
involves only space–time mesh element refinements
as described in Section
3.1
.
For the
ESTIMATE
step,
we use the following local, computable residual-type error indicator:
given an element
\mathcal{K}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
,

\eta_{K}^{2}:=\sum_{i=1}^{5}\eta_{K,i}^{2},

where

\begin{split}\eta_{K,1}^{2}&:=\nu^{-1}\frac{h_{\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{x}}}^{2}}{p^{2}}{\left\|{f+\nu\Delta_{\mathbf{x}}\Pi^{N}_{p}u_{h}-c_{H}\partial_{t}{\Pi^{\star}_{p}}u_{h}}\right\|}_{0,\mathcal{K}}^{2},\\
\eta_{K,2}^{2}&:=\frac{1}{2}\nu^{-1}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\sum_{\scriptsize\begin{tabular}[]{c}$F\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{K}}^{\text{time}},$\\
$F\not\subset\partial\Omega\times(0,T)$\end{tabular}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\frac{h_{F_{\mathbf{x}}}}{p}{\left\|{\nu\left[\!\left[\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\Pi^{N}_{p}u_{h}\right]\!\right]}\right\|}_{0,F}^{2},\\
\eta_{K,3}^{2}&:=\nu\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\sum_{\scriptsize\begin{tabular}[]{c}$F\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{K}}^{\text{time}},$\\
$F\subset\partial\Omega\times(0,T)$\end{tabular}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!ph_{F_{\mathbf{x}}}^{-1}{\left\|{\Pi^{N}_{p}u_{h}}\right\|}_{0,F}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\nu\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\sum_{\scriptsize\begin{tabular}[]{c}$F\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{K}}^{\text{time}},$\\
$F\not\subset\partial\Omega\times(0,T)$\end{tabular}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!ph_{F_{\mathbf{x}}}^{-1}{\left\|{\left[\!\left[\Pi^{N}_{p}u_{h}\right]\!\right]}\right\|}_{0,F}^{2},\\
\eta_{K,4}^{2}&:=\sum_{K_{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{K}}^{\text{space}}}c_{H}^{-1}{\left\|{\mathcal{U}^{K_{\mathbf{x}}}(u_{h})}\right\|}^{2}_{0,K_{\mathbf{x}}},\\
\eta_{K,5}^{2}&:=\nu S^{K}((I-\Pi^{N}_{p})u_{h},(I-\Pi^{N}_{p})u_{h}).\end{split}

(28)

The above local residual-type error indicator consists of five terms:

\eta_{K,1}
is the internal residual of the projected discrete solution;

\eta_{K,2}
is the boundary residual involving the normal trace of the gradient of the projected discrete solution;

\eta_{K,3}
is a term due to the nonconformity in space
involving the jump of traces on time-like facets;

\eta_{K,4}
is related to the upwind terms in the scheme;

\eta_{K,5}
is a correction term due to the virtual element stabilization of the method.

The global error indicator and its parts read

\eta^{2}:=\sum_{i=1}^{5}\eta_{i}^{2},\qquad\qquad\eta_{i}^{2}:=\sum_{\mathcal{K}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\eta_{K,i}^{2}.

(29)

6
Conclusions

We extended the virtual element framework of
[
13
]

to the case of general prismatic space–time meshes with possible hanging nodes
and nonuniform degrees of accuracy, useful in
hp
-adaptive procedures.
We discussed flagging strategies to handle the space–time meshes data structure;
this improves the performance of the method.
We investigated numerically the
hp
-version of the method
and demonstrated the expected exponential convergence in terms of suitable roots of the number of degrees of freedom for singular solutions.
A residual-type error indicator was introduced
and an
h
-adaptive refinement procedure was tested based on it.
This error indicator appears to be reliable and efficient for the error
\mathcal{E}^{Y}

but not for the error
\mathcal{E}^{N}
in (
23
).
We investigated numerically the overall virtual element adaptive procedure.
The results obtained for certain singular solutions show that it outperforms a corresponding one for a continuous finite element method.
