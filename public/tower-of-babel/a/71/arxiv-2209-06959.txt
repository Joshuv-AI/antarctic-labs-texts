Conductivity and size quantization effects in semiconductor δ \delta -layer systems
Juan P. Mendez, Denis Mamaluy

Abstract
We present an open-system quantum-mechanical 3D real-space study of the conduction band structure and conductive properties of two semiconductor systems, interesting for their beyond-Moore and quantum computing applications: phosphorus δ \delta -layers in silicon and the corresponding δ \delta -layer tunnel junctions. In order to evaluate size quantization effects on the conductivity, we consider two principal cases: nanoscale finite-width structures, used in transistors, and infinitely-wide structures, electrical properties of which are typically known experimentally. For devices widths W < 10 W<10 nm, quantization effects are strong and it is shown that the number of propagating modes determines not only the conductivity, but the distinctive spatial distribution of the current-carrying electron states. For W > 10 W>10 nm, the quantization effects practically vanish and the conductivity tends to the infinitely-wide device values. For tunnel junctions, two distinct conductivity regimes are predicted due to the strong conduction band quantization.

Introduction

Our device consists of a semi-infinite source and drain, in contact with the engineering channel. The channel is composed of a lightly doped Si body and Si cap and a very thin, highly P doped-layer with an intrinsic gap of length

Highly conductive

;

. However, at the scale important for these applications

, i.e. devices with sub-20 nm physical gate/channel lengths and/or sub-20 nm widths, that could compete with the future CMOS, the conductive properties of such systems are expected to exhibit a strong influence of size quantization effects. At the same time, experimental assessment of the conductivity of
\delta

;
Goh and Simmons 2009
;
Reusch et al. 2008
;

.

The electronic structure and conductive properties of Si:P
\delta
-layer systems have been a subject of previous studies based on either effective mass

;
Mendez et al. 2020
;
Mamaluy et al. 2021
;

, tight-binding

. Recently it has been demonstrated in

;

;
Mendez et al. 2021
that to accurately extract the conductive properties of highly-conductive, highly-confined systems, an open-system quantum-mechanical analysis is necessary. Such open-system treatment, that can be conducted for instance using the Non-Equilibrium Green’s Function (NEGF) formalism

;

It has been also demonstrated in

that an open-system treatment in highly P doped
\delta

\delta

;

An accurate computational description of electron tunneling in semiconductor
\delta
-layer tunnel junctions (such the one shown in Fig.
1

\delta
-layer junction is affected not only by the gap length and the conductivity of the
\delta
-layers, but also by quantization of the conduction electrons in energy and space

.
In this work we employ an efficient computational open-system quantum-mechanical treatment to explore the conductive band structure and the conductive properties of phosphorus
\delta
-layer systems in silicon (Si:P
\delta
-layer) for device widths, from nano-scale (
<20
nm) to macro-scale (
>1

\mu
m) dimensions, and to analyze the influence of size quantization effects on the conductive properties for sub-12 nm device widths. All simulations are carried at the cryogenic temperature of
4

Goh et al. 2006
;
Mazzola et al. 2014
. The analysis of the influence of different kinds of non-idealities on the tunneling current is presented in
Mendez et al. 2022
. The open-system treatment is based on an application of Keldysh formalism
Keldysh 1965
, known as NEGF
Datta 1997
, and the effective mass theory. Generally for Si systems, the use of the effective mass approximation has been shown to be in a good agreement with tight-binding models for nanowire diameters down to 3 nm
Wang et al. 2005
;
Neophytou et al. 2008
; thus, the fidelity of this approximation start to decline for very narrow systems (
<3
nm). Here we have employed an efficient implementation of NEGF, refereed to as the Contact Block Reduction (CBR) method
Mamaluy et al. 2003
;
Mamaluy et al. 2004
;
Mamaluy et al. 2005
;
Khan et al. 2007
;
Gao et al. 2014
, which allows accurate computation of all quantum-mechanical quantities of interest (local density of states, transmission probability, current) and scales linearly
O(N)
with the system size. We find that at the scale most interesting for applications, i.e. for device widths
W<10
nm, quantization effects strongly affect both the conductivity and the spatial distribution of the current-carrying electron states, which, similarly to the charge distribution in the hydrogen atom, is determined by a ”quantum number”, i.e. the number of propagating modes. This strong spacial quantization of the current-carrying states can be utilized in novel electronic
\delta
-layer devices. Conversely, for
W>10
nm, the quantization effects practically vanish and the conductivity tend to the values of infinitely-wide devices. Finally, two distinct conductivity regimes are predicted due to the strong conduction band quantization for tunnel junctions.

The oscillations of the conductivity for narrow device widths

II.4
Conclusions

In this work, we have used an efficient open-system quantum-mechanical treatment to explore the conductive band structure and the conductive properties in Si:P
\delta
-layers for device widths ranging from nano-scale to macro-scale dimensions, and analyze the influence of size quantization effects on the conductive properties for sub-12 nm device widths. For device widths
W<10
nm, quantization effects strongly affect not only the conductivity, but also the spatial distribution of the current-carrying electron states. Conversely, for
W>10
nm, the quantization effects practically vanish and the conductivity tends to the values of infinitely-wide devices. Additionally, we have revealed and discussed the mechanism of two conductivity regimes in
\delta
-layer tunnel junctions with
L_{gap}>7
nm: a low-voltage regime, where conduction band quantization effects play a very significant role, and a high-voltage regime, where the quantum effects on the current are negligible.

Finally, we point out that the strong spacial quantization of the current-carrying states could be utilized in novel electronic
\delta
-layer switches, where the number of propagating modes and their match/mis-match could be controlled by external electric fields, thus strongly affecting the current. In regular
\delta
-layer conductors the particular distribution of current-carrying states directly affects their penetration depth into Si body and cap, which typically has a large concentration of impurities (see e.g.
Ward et al. 2020
). Thus, the control of the number of propagating modes may give an additional degree of control over the rate of impurity scattering.

III
Method

III.1
Open-system treatment

The open-system framework used in this work is based on an application of Keldysh formalism
Keldysh 1965
, known as Non-Equilibrium Green Function (NEGF)
Datta 1997
(Sect.
III.2
), and the effective mass theory (Sect.
III.5
). The NEGF formalism, described in Sect.
III.2
, defines the Green’s function matrix through the inverse
G(E)=[1-H_{0}-\Sigma(E)]^{-1}
, which allows to compute the electron density and current through a charge self-consistent scheme as described in Sect.
III.6
. However, the computation of an inverse matrix generally also scales as
O(N^{3})
, with
N
being the size of the device Hamiltonian matrix
H_{0}
(Sect.
III.5
). For instance, for a device of dimensions
12\text{nm}\times 15\text{nm}\times 50\text{nm}
with a uniform grid of 0.2 nm,
N>10^{6}
and
N^{3}>10^{18}
, making a direct computation of the inverse very challenging. III.4
Incomplete set of CBR eigenstates

The major feature of the CBR method is the ability to use a greatly reduced, incomplete set of specially defined eigenstates to represent the true open-system solution. As was shown in
Mamaluy et al. 2003

Mamaluy et al. 2003
, it can be accomplished by imposing a special kind boundary condition to the decouple device. The idea is to be able to rewrite the Green’s function matrix of the open system
G_{D}(E)
, Equation
4

\displaystyle G_{D}(E)

\displaystyle=\bigg(\bm{E}^{+}-\bm{H}_{D}^{0}-\bm{\Sigma}_{s}-\bm{\Sigma}_{d}\bigg)^{-1}

(21)

\displaystyle=\bigg(\bm{E}^{+}-\bm{H}_{D}^{N}-\bm{\Sigma}_{s}^{N}(E)-\bm{\Sigma}_{d}^{N}(E)\bigg)^{-1}

where
\bm{H}_{D}^{N}
is independent of the energy, and
\bm{\Sigma}_{s}^{N}(E)
and
\bm{\Sigma}_{d}^{N}(E)
tend toward zero for values of
E
that lies not far from the band edge. This enables us to solve the Dyson equation with an incomplete basis.

We start assuming that all nonzero coupling matrix elements of
\bm{W}_{s(d)D}
is equal to a real constant value,

. This leads to the following expression for the self-energy matrix
\bm{\Sigma}_{s(d)}
within each source(drain) contact,

Datta 1997

\bm{\Sigma}_{s(d)}=-W_{s(d)}\sum_{m}^{M_{T}}\bm{\chi}_{s(d)}^{m}\exp{(ik_{s(d)}^{m}a_{s(d)})}(\bm{\chi}_{s(d)}^{m})^{{\dagger}}

where

is the constant parameter of the lattice,

is the total number of propagating modes, and

\bm{H_{s(d)}^{0}}\bm{\chi}_{s(d)}^{m}=\epsilon_{s(d)}^{m}\bm{\chi}_{s(d)}^{m}

are functions of energy E as

The exponential term in Equation

\displaystyle-W_{s(d)}\exp{(ik_{s(d)}^{m}(E)a)}

\displaystyle=-W_{s(d)}-iW_{s(d)}ak_{s(d)}^{m}(E)+...

(24)

\displaystyle=-W_{s(d)}+F_{s(d)}(k_{s(d)}^{m}(E)),

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}_{s(d)}(E)=

\displaystyle-W_{s(d)}\delta_{ij}+

\end{cases}

\displaystyle\bm{G}_{D}(E)
