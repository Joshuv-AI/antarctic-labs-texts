The core of the Levi distribution
Gian Maria Dall’Ara, Samuele Mongodi

Abstract
. We introduce a new geometrical invariant of CR manifolds of hypersurface type, which we dub the “Levi core” of the manifold. When the manifold is the boundary of a smooth bounded pseudoconvex domain, we show how the Levi core is related to two other important global invariants in several complex variables: the Diederich–Fornæss index and the D’Angelo class (namely the set of D’Angelo forms of the boundary). We also show that the Levi core is trivial whenever the domain is of finite-type in the sense of D’Angelo, or the set of weakly pseudoconvex points is contained in a totally real submanifold, while it is nontrivial if the boundary contains a local maximum set. As corollaries to the theory developed here, we prove that for any smooth bounded pseudoconvex domain with trivial Levi core the Diederich–Fornæss index is one and the ∂ ¯ \overline{\partial} -Neumann problem is exactly regular (via a result of Kohn and its generalization by Harrington). Our work builds on and expands recent results of Liu and Adachi–Yum.



1.
Introduction

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
be a smooth pseudoconvex domain, and let
M
be its boundary. The purpose of this paper is to define a distribution
\mathfrak{C}
of complex subspaces:

p\in M\quad\longmapsto\quad\mathfrak{C}_{p}\subset\mathbb{C}\otimes T_{p}M,

which we call the
“Levi core”
of
M
, and to relate it to two important global invariants in analysis in several complex variables: the Diederich–Fornaess index of
\Omega
and the D’Angelo class of
M
, i.e., the set of D’Angelo forms of
M
(see below for precise definitions).

The Levi core
\mathfrak{C}
may be obtained as a special case of a general differential-geometric construction, which we describe first. If
\mathcal{D}=\{\mathcal{D}_{p}\}_{p\in M}
is a distribution of subspaces on a real smooth manifold
M
, that is,
\mathcal{D}_{p}
is a linear subspace of the tangent space
T_{p}M
for every
p\in M
, then we define the
derived distribution

\mathcal{D}^{\prime}=\{\mathcal{D}_{p}^{\prime}\}_{p\in M}
as

\mathcal{D}_{p}^{\prime}:=\mathcal{D}_{p}\cap T_{p}S_{\mathcal{D}},

where:

(1)

S_{\mathcal{D}}
is the support of
\mathcal{D}
, namely the set of points
p\in M
such that the fiber
\mathcal{D}_{p}
has positive dimension;

(2)

T_{p}S_{\mathcal{D}}\subset T_{p}M
is the
“
C^{\infty}
Zariski”
tangent space to the subset
S_{\mathcal{D}}\subset M
at the point
p
(since we do not make any regularity assumption on
\mathcal{D}
, its support
S_{\mathcal{D}}
need not be a submanifold and we need a notion of tangent space valid for general subsets, see Section
2.1
for details).

In other words, the vectors of the distribution
\mathcal{D}
that survive in the derived distribution
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
are those that are also tangent to the support of
\mathcal{D}
. In this way, we obtain a smaller distribution
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{D}
(i.e.,
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{p}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{p}
for every
p
). The operation can be iterated, yielding a decreasing sequence of distributions

\mathcal{D}\supseteq\mathcal{D}^{(1)}=\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\supseteq\mathcal{D}^{(2)}=(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})^{\prime}\supseteq\ldots

This sequence does not necessarily stabilize after finitely many steps, but it must do so
“eventually”
. More precisely, if one defines
\mathcal{D}^{(\alpha)}
for any ordinal
\alpha
by transfinite recursion, it turns out that there exists a countable ordinal
\alpha_{1}
such that
\mathcal{D}^{(\alpha_{1})}
equals its derived distribution (Theorem
2.9
).
In fact, this result (and the whole construction of
“iterated derived distributions”
) is a generalization of the very classical Cantor–Bendixson theorem in set theory (see Section
2
). This
“stable”
distribution
\mathcal{D}^{(\alpha_{1})}
will be called the
core
of the distribution
\mathcal{D}
, and denoted by
\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{D})
. Notice that the construction has an obvious generalization to the case of complex distributions of subspaces
\mathcal{D}=\{\mathcal{D}_{p}\}_{p\in M}
, that is, when
\mathcal{D}_{p}
is a complex linear subspace of the complexified tangent space
\mathbb{C}\otimes T_{p}M
for every
p
(and
M
is still a real manifold). It is this complex analogue that we need to define the Levi core.

Let then
M
be the boundary of a smooth pseudoconvex domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
(or, more generally, let
M
be a pseudoconvex CR manifold of hypersurface type). Denote by
T^{1,0}M\subseteq\mathbb{C}\otimes TM
, as usual, the CR bundle of
M
. Then possibly the most basic invariant of
M
is the complex distribution
\mathcal{N}\subseteq T^{1,0}M
consisting of null vectors for any Levi form
\lambda
of
M
:
Z_{p}\in\mathcal{N}_{p}
if and only if
Z_{p}\in T^{1,0}_{p}M
and
\lambda(Z_{p},Z_{p})=0
. We call
\mathcal{N}
the Levi distribution of
M
. As is well-known, its definition is independent of the choice of Levi form
\lambda
. We can now give our main definition.

Definition 1.1
.

The
Levi core
of
M
is the core
\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{N})
of the Levi distribution
\mathcal{N}
.

With a harmless abuse of language, we will also refer to
\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{N})
as the Levi core of the domain
\Omega
. A couple of comments may help to clarify this definition.

(1)

The pseudoconvex CR manifold
M
(equivalently, the domain
\Omega
of which
M
is the boundary) is strongly pseudoconvex if and only if its Levi null distribution is trivial, i.e.,
\mathcal{N}_{p}=0
for every
p\in M
. Since the Levi core
\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{N})
is typically much smaller than the Levi null distribution, one can view the category of smooth pseudoconvex domains with trivial Levi core as a wide class of
“nondegenerate”
pseudoconvex domains. In fact, in Section
3
we prove that weakly regular domains in the sense of Catlin
[
Cat84b
]
have trivial Levi core, and hence the same is true for pseudoconvex domains of finite-type in the sense of D’Angelo and pseudoconvex domains whose set of weakly pseudoconvex boundary points is a totally real submanifold.

(2)

At the opposite end of the spectrum of smooth pseudoconvex domains, we have the most degenerate of all, namely those containing (positive dimensional) complex submanifolds
N
in their boundary
M
. It is easy to see (Proposition
3.2
) that in this case
T^{1,0}N\subseteq\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{N})
, and hence the Levi core of
M
is nontrivial. In Section
3.2
we compute the Levi core for a family of three-dimensional pseudoconvex CR manifolds including the boundaries of generalized worm domains, showing how the Levi core captures the complex structure in the boundary, while forgetting additional
“benign”
weakly pseudoconvex points.

(3)

In Section
3.4
, we show that any local maximum set
K\subseteq M
in the sense of Slodkowski
[
Slo86
]
is contained in the support of the Levi core, thus relating it to the
weak Jensen boundary
of
\Omega
(see
[
OS98
]
for the definition and related results).

(4)

Finally, let us conclude by mentioning the issue of Levi-flatness, which is a global version of the
“most degenerate”
case discussed in point (2). In presence of a Levi-flat open set in
M
, our construction does not yield any additional information, as the Levi core is supported on the whole open set (again by Proposition
3.2
).

The Levi core interacts nicely with the two global invariants cited at the beginning of this introduction. We now turn our attention to them.

The first is the
Diederich–Fornaess index

\mathrm{DF}(\Omega)
, defined as the supremum of all exponents
\delta\in(0,1]
with the property that
-(-r)^{\delta}
is plurisubharmonic for at least a defining function
r
of
\Omega
(a notion originating from
[
DF77
]
). Among other reasons of interest, the Diederich–Fornaess index allows to formulate a sufficient condition for exact regularity of the
\overline{\partial}
-Neumann problem on a smooth bounded pseudoconvex domain
\Omega
(see Straube’s book
[
Str10
]
for background on the
\overline{\partial}
-Neumann problem). Slightly more precisely, by a theorem of Kohn
[
Koh99
]
, under the assumption that
\mathrm{DF}(\Omega)=1
and a quantitative control on a sequence of defining functions
r_{k}
achieving the supremum in the definition of
\mathrm{DF}(\Omega)
, the
\overline{\partial}
-Neumann problem on
\Omega
is exactly regular (see Section
6
for a precise statement and a generalization due to Harrington). Thus, it is of great interest to be able to compute or estimate the Diederich–Fornaess index of a domain, and in particular to decide whether
\mathrm{DF}(\Omega)=1
. In the last couple of years, some progress has been made on this quite difficult problem by Liu, Yum, and Adachi. In order to review these developments, we first need to say a few words about the second global invariant mentioned above, which we call (with a nonstandard terminology) the D’Angelo class.

The
D’Angelo class
is a collection
\mathcal{A}_{M}
of real smooth one-forms, called D’Angelo forms, that can be naturally attached to any pseudoconvex CR manifold of hypersurface type
M
, and that behaves as a sort of cohomology class
“when restricted to the Levi distribution
\mathcal{N}
”
, in the sense that:

i)

if
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{M}
, then for any other
\alpha^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}_{M}
there exists
f\in C^{\infty}(M,\mathbb{R})
such that
\alpha^{\prime}_{|\mathcal{N}}=\alpha_{|\mathcal{N}}+df_{|\mathcal{N}}
.

ii)

if
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{M}
, then the two-form
d\alpha
vanishes when restricted to
\mathcal{N}
.

The notion of D’Angelo class implicitly originated in papers of D’Angelo
[
D’A79
,
D’A87
]
, but it was in work of Boas and Straube
[
BS93
]
that its importance for the
\overline{\partial}
-Neumann problem was recognized. Boas and Straube proved that if
\Omega
is a smooth bounded pseudoconvex domain and the points of infinite type of
M=b\Omega
are all contained in a submanifold
N\subset M
whose real tangent bundle is contained in
\Re(\mathcal{N})
(the real part of the Levi distribution), then a sufficient condition for exact regularity of the
\overline{\partial}
-Neumann problem on
\Omega
is that the restriction of the D’Angelo class
\mathcal{A}_{M}
to
N
is trivial as an element of the first de Rham cohomology group
H^{1}_{\mathrm{dR}}(N,\mathbb{R})
. This restriction is in fact a genuine de Rham cohomology class on
N
, thanks to properties i) and ii) above. A few other papers where the D’Angelo class plays a more or less explicit rôle appeared later, e.g.,
[
SS02
,
SS03
,
Str08
,
FLT08
,
MT15
,
MT16
]
.

We can now discuss the recent work of Liu, Yum, and Adachi, establishing an interesting connection between the Diederich–Fornaess index of a smooth bounded pseudoconvex domain
\Omega
and the D’Angelo class of its boundary
M
. Liu
[
Liu19a
]
expressed
\mathrm{DF}(\Omega)
as the optimal constant in a rather complicated differential inequality on
M
, and he was also able to determine the explicit value of this constant when
\Omega
is a Diederich–Fornaess worm domain, thus succeeding in the exact computation of the
\mathrm{DF}
index of a domain in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
for which this quantity is strictly less than one, a result with remarkably no precedent in the literature (see
[
FS16
,
AB15
]
for related results in the setting of complex manifolds). Next, Yum
[
Yum21
]
recognized that Liu’s differential inequality could be neatly reformulated in terms of D’Angelo forms of
M
, and exploited this fact to prove the CR invariance of the
\mathrm{DF}
index and of a
“dual”
Steinness index, introduced in a previous paper
[
Yum19
]
. Finally, Adachi and Yum
[
AY21
]
generalized these results, replacing the ambient space
\mathbb{C}^{n}
with an arbitrary complex manifold, and the extrinsic computations of
[
Liu19a
]
and
[
Yum21
]
, which in particular relied on the flatness of the Euclidean metric, with intrinsic ones.

Let us discuss Adachi–Yum result in more detail. If
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{M}
is a D’Angelo form (for simplicity, in this introductory discussion we still assume that
M=b\Omega
, where
\Omega
is a smooth bounded pseudoconvex domain in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
), we define the norm-like quantity

(1.1)

\mathfrak{n}(\alpha):=\inf\{t>0\colon\ \alpha_{1,0}\wedge\alpha_{0,1}<t\overline{\partial}\alpha_{1,0}\quad\text{on }\mathcal{N}\}.

Here one may compute the
(1,0)
- and
(0,1)
-components of
\alpha
, and
\overline{\partial}\alpha_{1,0}
, by first extending
\alpha
to an open neighborhood of
M
. It turns out that both
\alpha_{1,0}\wedge\alpha_{0,1}
and
\overline{\partial}\alpha_{1,0}
restrict to well-defined Hermitian forms on the Levi null distribution
\mathcal{N}
(independently of the extension used to define them), and therefore the inequality in the sense of quadratic forms

\alpha_{1,0}\wedge\alpha_{0,1}<t\overline{\partial}\alpha_{1,0}

appearing in (
1.1
) makes sense. Next, we define

\mathfrak{n}:=\inf_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{M}}\mathfrak{n}(\alpha),

which should be thought of as a quantitative measure of the size of the D’Angelo class of
M
. Adachi–Yum main theorem (in the case of domains in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
) may be rephrased as follows.

Theorem 1.2

(Theorem 1.1. of
[
Yum21
]
; Theorem 2 of
[
AY21
]
)
.

(1.2)

\mathrm{DF}(\Omega)=\frac{1}{1+\mathfrak{n}}.

Since
\mathfrak{n}\in[0,+\infty)
(
\mathfrak{n}=+\infty
can be shown to be impossible if the ambient space is
\mathbb{C}^{n}
), one recovers the classical fact that
\mathrm{DF}(\Omega)>0
(originally proved by Diederich and Fornaess
[
DF77
]
), and one can also see that
\mathrm{DF}(\Omega)=1
if and only if
\mathfrak{n}=0
, a triviality condition for the D’Angelo class.

Formula (
1.2
) elegantly relates the Diederich–Fornaess index and the D’Angelo class. We now bring the Levi core into the picture, showing how it further clarifies it. We start by generalizing the norm-like object (
1.1
), embedding it into a family of similar gadgets:

(1.3)

\mathfrak{n}(\alpha;\mathcal{D}):=\inf\{t>0\colon\ \alpha_{1,0}\wedge\alpha_{0,1}<t\overline{\partial}\alpha_{1,0}\quad\text{on }\mathcal{D}\},

where the Levi null distribution
\mathcal{N}
has been replaced by an arbitrary sub-distribution
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathcal{N}
. It is clear that the definition makes sense, and that
\mathfrak{n}(\alpha;\mathcal{D})\leq\mathfrak{n}(\alpha;\mathcal{N})=\mathfrak{n}(\alpha)
for every D’Angelo form
\alpha
. Next, we set

\mathfrak{n}(\mathcal{D}):=\inf_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{M}}\mathfrak{n}(\alpha;\mathcal{D}).

Our main result is the following
“reduction to the core”
theorem.

Theorem 1.3
.

We have the identity:

(1.4)

\mathfrak{n}(\mathcal{N})=\mathfrak{n}(\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{N})).

Combining Theorem
1.2
and Theorem
1.3
, one can deduce a number of corollaries:

a)

If
\Omega
has trivial Levi core, then
\mathfrak{n}=\mathfrak{n}(\mathcal{N})=0
and thus
\mathrm{DF}(\Omega)=1
. In particular, any weakly regular domain has
\mathrm{DF}
index one. This includes the case of domains whose set of weakly pseudoconvex boundary points is a totally real submanifold, cf. Theorem 4.5 of
[
Liu19b
]
, in turn generalizing a result of Krantz, Liu, and Peloso
[
KLP18
]
.

b)

A more precise quantitative refinement of Theorem
1.3
(Theorem
5.1
) allows us to conclude that if the Levi core of
\Omega
is trivial, then Kohn’s theorem
[
Koh99
]
can be applied to conclude that the
\overline{\partial}
-Neumann problem is exactly regular (Theorem
6.7
).

c)

If
M=b\Omega
contains a complex submanifold
N
, then
\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{N})\supseteq T^{1,0}N
, as remarked after Definition
1.1
, and we have the trivial lower bound
\mathfrak{n}(\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{N}))\geq\mathfrak{n}(T^{1,0}N)
. In Section
4.2
, we prove that
\mathfrak{n}(T^{1,0}N)>0
if and only if the restriction of the D’Angelo class to
N
defines a nonzero element of
H^{1}_{\mathrm{dR}}(N,\mathbb{R})
(Theorem
4.4
). Hence, the Diederich–Fornaess index is strictly less than one whenever this happens. Notice that, while this conclusion already follows from Theorem
1.2
and Theorem
4.4
, by Theorem
1.3
one has the equality
\mathfrak{n}=\mathfrak{n}(T^{1,0}M)
whenever
\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{N})=T^{1,0}N
(cf. the examples of Section
3.2
).

The paper is organized as follows:

•

In Section
2
we define and prove the existence of the core of a distribution of subspaces
\mathcal{D}
on a real smooth manifold, under the sole assumption that
\mathcal{D}
is closed as a subset of the tangent bundle.

•

In Section
3
we introduce the Levi core in the context of abstract pseudoconvex CR manifolds of hypersurface type and prove a number of its properties.

•

Section
4
starts with a review of the basic theory of the D’Angelo class, again in the context of abstract pseudoconvex CR manifolds of hypersurface type. It continues with the definition of a norm-like function on the first de Rham cohomology of a complex manifold, and the proof of its nondegeneracy (Theorem
4.4
). This definition is then used as a motivation for introducing the quantites
\mathfrak{n}(\mathcal{D})
(see (
1.3
) above), and certain quantitative refinements
\mathfrak{n}_{K}(\mathcal{D})
of these (where
K<+\infty
is a parameter).

•

In Section
5
our main
“reduction to the core”
theorem is proved.

•

Finally, Section
6
discusses a refinement of Adachi–Yum theorem involving the norms
\mathfrak{n}_{K}(\mathcal{N})
, that allows in particular to deduce, via Kohn’s theorem
[
Koh99
]
, the exact regularity of the
\overline{\partial}
-Neumann problem on smooth bounded pseudoconvex domains with trivial Levi core.

•

In the Appendix, we give a quantitative estimate for the norm-like functions introduced in Section
4
, in the case where the complex manifold is a pseudoconvex domain in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
satisfying a weak regularity property.

2.
The core of a distribution of subspaces

Let us begin with a remark on terminology. From now on, we employ the term
“distribution”
, in place of the cumbersome
“distribution of subspaces”
, for the objects of Definition
2.1
below. This terminology conflicts with that of the theory of distributions, or
“generalized functions”
(and the expression
“support of a distribution”
makes the conflict even worse), but since generalized functions will play no rôle in what follows no confusion should ensue. We will also write
\mathbb{C}V
for the complexification
\mathbb{C}\otimes V
of a real vector space
V
.

2.1.
Distributions and closed distributions

Definition 2.1

(Distribution)
.

A
real distribution
on a real smooth manifold
M
is a subset
\mathcal{D}
of the tangent bundle
TM
such that the fiber
\mathcal{D}_{p}:=\mathcal{D}\cap T_{p}M
is a vector subspace of
T_{p}M
for every
p\in M
. A
complex distribution
on
M
is a subset
\mathcal{D}
of the complexified tangent bundle
\mathbb{C}TM
such that
\mathcal{D}_{p}:=\mathcal{D}\cap\mathbb{C}T_{p}M
is a complex vector subspace of
\mathbb{C}T_{p}M
for every
p\in M
.

If
\mathcal{D}
is a real distribution, then the complexification of
\mathcal{D}
is the complex distribution
\mathbb{C}\mathcal{D}
defined by

\mathbb{C}\mathcal{D}_{p}:=\{X_{p}+iY_{p}\colon X_{p},Y_{p}\in\mathcal{D}_{p}\}.

If
\mathcal{D}
is a complex distribution, then its real part is the real distribution
\Re(\mathcal{D})
defined by

\Re(\mathcal{D})_{p}:=\{Z_{p}+\overline{Z}_{p}\colon\ Z_{p}\in\mathcal{D}_{p}\}.

The
support

S_{\mathcal{D}}
of a (real or complex) distribution
\mathcal{D}
is the set of points
p\in M
such that
\mathcal{D}_{p}\neq\{0\}
.

As a first elementary example, we observe that distributions on a one-dimensional manifold are nothing but subsets.

Example 2.2

(Distributions on a one-dim. manifold)
.

If
M
is a one-dimensional manifold and
\mathcal{D}
is a real (resp. complex) distribution, for each
p\in M
we have either
\mathcal{D}_{p}=\{0\}
or
\mathcal{D}_{p}=T_{p}M
(resp.
\mathcal{D}_{p}=\mathbb{C}T_{p}M
). Therefore, we can recover
\mathcal{D}
from its support
S_{\mathcal{D}}\subseteq M
. Viceversa, it is clear that for every subset
S\subseteq M
there exists a unique real (resp. complex) distribution
\mathcal{D}
such that
S_{\mathcal{D}}=S
.

We will be mostly interested in
closed
distributions, that is, distributions
\mathcal{D}
that are closed subsets of
TM
, or
\mathbb{C}TM
.

The reader may verify that the complexification of a closed real distribution is closed. Another very basic property of closed distributions is contained in the next proposition, which implies in particular that the supports of closed distributions are closed.

Proposition 2.3
.

If
\mathcal{D}
is a closed real (resp. complex) distribution, then the dimension
\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{D}_{p}
(resp.
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\mathcal{D}_{p}
) is an upper semicontinuous function of the point
p
.

Proof.

We write the proof in the real case, the complex case being a trivial variant. We argue by contradiction, assuming that
\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{D}_{p}=k
, while
\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{D}_{p_{n}}\geq k+1
for a sequence of points
p_{n}
converging to
p
. Fixing an arbitrary Riemannian metric
g
on
M
, we may find orthonormal sets of vectors
\{v_{1}^{(n)},\ldots,v_{k+1}^{(n)}\}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{p_{n}}
. By a simple compactness argument, we may assume without loss of generality that
v_{j}^{(n)}
converges to
v_{j}\in T_{p}M
for every
j=1,\ldots,k+1
. By the closure assumption,
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k+1}
is an orthonormal subset of
\mathcal{D}_{p}
, contradicting the fact that
\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{D}_{p}=k
.
∎

Remark
.

Another interesting class of distributions is that of
smooth distributions
(see, e.g.,
[
Lav18
]
), i.e., distributions such that for every
p\in M
and every
v\in\mathcal{D}_{p}
there exists a local smooth vector field
X
on a neighborhood
U
of
p
such that
X_{p}=v
and
X_{q}\in\mathcal{D}_{q}
for every
q\in U
. One may easily prove that, for smooth distributions,
\dim\mathcal{D}_{p}
is a lower semicontinuous function of
p\in M
. It follows that a distribution is simultaneously smooth and closed if and only if it is a subbundle of the tangent bundle.

It is easy to see that a one-dimensional distribution
\mathcal{D}
as in Example
2.2
is closed if and only if its support
S_{\mathcal{D}}
is a closed subset of
M
.

Less obvious examples of closed distributions arise as null-distributions of continuous quadratic forms.

Example 2.4

(Null-distribution of a continuous quadratic form)
.

Let
E
be a subbundle of
TM
and let

B_{p}:E_{p}\times E_{p}\to\mathbb{R}

be a bilinear symmetric form depending continuously on
p
. Its null-space defines a closed distribution on
M
:

\ker B=\{(p,X_{p})\in E\ :\ B_{p}(X_{p},Y_{p})=0\quad\forall Y_{p}\in\ E_{p}\}\;.

To see that this distribution is closed, observe that

\ker B=\bigcap_{Y}\{(p,X_{p})\in E:\ B_{p}(X_{p},Y_{p})=0\},\;

where
Y
ranges over global continuous sections of
E
.

Analogously, one may define the null-distribution
\ker H
of a Hermitian form
H_{p}:E_{p}\times\overline{E_{p}}\to\mathbb{R}
depending continuously on
p
, where now
E_{p}
is a complex subbundle of
\mathbb{C}T_{p}M
. The resulting complex distribution
\ker H
is also closed.

Another general construction yielding closed distributions is provided by the following notion of Zariski tangent space in the smooth category.

Definition 2.5

(
Tangent distribution
to a subset)
.

Let
A
be an arbitrary subset of a real smooth manifold
M
. The (real) tangent distribution
TA
to
A
, whose fibers we denote by
T_{p}A
, is defined as follows:

X_{p}\in T_{p}A\ \Longleftrightarrow\ X_{p}f=0\quad\forall f\in C^{\infty}(M)\colon\ f_{|A}\equiv 0.

The complex tangent distribution to
A
is the complexified distribution
\mathbb{C}TA
.

Observe that
TA=T\overline{A}
for any set
A
, and that tangent distributions are local: if
U\subseteq M
is open, then
TA
and the tangent distribution to
A\cap U
as a subset of
U
have the same fiber at every
p\in U
.

Remark
.

If
A
is a closed subset of
M
and
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{A}
is the sheaf of germs of restrictions to
A
of smooth functions on
M
, the pair
(A,\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{A})
is a (reduced) differentiable space in the terminology of
[
NGSdS03
]
(see
[
NGSdS03
, Theorem 3.23]
). The space
T_{p}A
of Definition
2.5
coincides with the tangent space defined in
[
NGSdS03
, Sections 5.4 and 5.5]
.

The next straightforward proposition lists a few basic properties of tangent distributions.

Proposition 2.6
.

a)

The tangent distribution
TA
to any set
A
is a closed distribution, whose support is the set of limit points of
A
.

b)

If
A
is an embedded submanifold of
M
, then
T_{p}A
is the ordinary tangent space to
A
at every point
p\in A
(thought of as a subspace of
T_{p}M
).

c)

The dimension of
T_{p}A
is the minimal
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
A
is locally contained, near
p
, in a real embedded
k
-dimensional submanifold
V
of
M
. In this case,
T_{p}A=T_{p}V
.

Proof.

Notice that, by definition,

(2.1)

TA=\bigcap_{f}\ker df

where the intersection is taken over all the functions
f\in C^{\infty}(M)
such that
f_{|A}=0
.
Therefore
TA
is closed as a subset of
TM
.

If
p\in M
is not a limit point of
A
, we have a neighborhood
U
of
p
such that
U\cap A\subseteq\{p\}
. Let
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
be local coordinates centered at
p
and defined on such a neighborhood. Since every
x_{j}
is a function vanishing on
A
, one easily deduces that
T_{p}A=\{0\}
. On the other hand, if
T_{p}A=\{0\}
, by (
2.1
) and elementary linear algebra we find
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\in C^{\infty}(M)
vanishing on
A
and such that

\ker df_{1}(p)\cap\ldots\cap\ker df_{n}(p)=\{0\}.

Therefore these functions give a set of local coordinates on a neighborhood
U
of
p
, and their common zero set in
U
has to be
\{p\}
, which cannot be a limit point of
A
. This proves a).

Let us prove b). If
A
is a submanifold of
M
, for
p\in A
we have coordinates
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
on a neighborhood
U
of
p
such that

A\cap U=\{q\in U\ :\ x_{1}(q)=\ldots=x_{k}(q)=0\}\;.

We may assume that
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
are global smooth functions on
M
by multiplying them with a cut-off function and restricting
U
, as usual. The local coordinates give a frame
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
for
TM
over
U
such that
X_{j}x_{k}=\delta_{jk}
(Kronecker delta) and
T_{p}A=\mathrm{Span}\{X_{k+1,p},\ldots,X_{n,p}\}
. Moreover,
f\in C^{\infty}(M)
is such that
f_{|A}=0
if and only if we can write

f=x_{1}g_{1}+\ldots+x_{k}g_{k}\qquad\text{on }U

for some functions
g_{1},\ldots,g_{k}\in C^{\infty}(U)
. From this, one readily deduces that
T_{p}A
is spanned by
X_{k+1},\ldots,X_{n}
, i.e., it coincides with the ordinary tangent space to
A
at
p
.

We are left with
c)
. If
T_{p}A
has dimension
k
, by (
2.1
) we can find
n-k
functions
f_{k+1},\ldots,f_{n}\in C^{\infty}(M)
vanishing on
A
and such that

\ker df_{1}(p)\cap\ldots\cap\ker df_{n}(p)=T_{p}A\;.

Therefore, there is a neighborhood
U
of
p
such that

V=\{q\in U\ :\ f_{k+1}(q)=\ldots=f_{n}(q)=0\}

is a
k
-dimensional embedded submanifold which contains
A\cap U
, and such that
T_{p}V=T_{p}A
(here one uses part b)).
On the other hand, if
S
is locally contained in an embedded manifold
W
, then
T_{p}S\subseteq T_{p}W
, as any function that vanishes on
W
near
p
also vanishes on
S
near
p
. This shows the minimality of
k
and concludes the proof.
∎

2.2.
Derived distribution and core of a distribution

On a one-dimensional manifold
M
we can identify a distribution with its support (Example
2.2
). Hence, by Proposition
2.6
, the tangent distribution to a subset
S
of
M
can be identified with the set of limit points of
S
, i.e., the so-called derived set of
S
. This motivates the terminology in the following definition.

Definition 2.7

(
Derived distribution
)
.

Let
\mathcal{D}\subseteq TM
be a real distribution. The distribution

\mathcal{D}^{\prime}:=\mathcal{D}\cap TS_{\mathcal{D}}

will be called the derived distribution of
\mathcal{D}
. Analogously, if
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathbb{C}TM
is a complex distribution on
M
, its derived distribution is defined as

\mathcal{D}^{\prime}:=\mathcal{D}\cap\mathbb{C}TS_{\mathcal{D}}.

In analogy with the corresponding notion for sets, a distribution equal to its derived distribution will be called
perfect
.

Notice that while the definition makes perfect sense for general distributions, we will use it only for closed ones. We collect a few basic properties of derived distributions in the next proposition.

Proposition 2.8
.

a)

The derived distribution of a closed (real or complex) distribution is closed.

b)

If
S\subseteq M
is perfect (that is, it coincides with the set of its limit points), then its tangent distributions
TS
and
\mathbb{C}TS
are perfect.

c)

Complexification and the operation of taking the derived distribution commute:
\mathbb{C}\mathcal{D}^{\prime}=(\mathbb{C}\mathcal{D})^{\prime}
for every real distribution
\mathcal{D}
.

d)

If
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{E}
are two (real or complex) distributions such that
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathcal{E}
, then
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Parts a) and b) follow from part a) of Proposition
2.6
. Part c) is trivial. Part d) is an immediate consequence of the implications
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathcal{E}\Longrightarrow S_{\mathcal{D}}\subseteq S_{\mathcal{E}}\Longrightarrow(\mathbb{C})TS_{\mathcal{D}}\subseteq(\mathbb{C})TS_{\mathcal{E}}
plus part c).
∎

Remark
.

In one dimension, the derived distribution of the distribution supported on the closed set
S
(see Example
2.2
) is the distribution supported on its derived set. The same may not be true if
S
is not closed, because not every limit point of
S
is an element of
S
in general.

It is natural to iterate the operation of
“taking the derived distribution”
. Let then
\mathcal{D}
be a (real or complex) distribution and let
\alpha
be an ordinal. We define
\mathcal{D}^{(\alpha)}
by transfinite recursion (see
[
Cie97
]
, Theorem 4.3.1):

(1)

if
\alpha=0
, we set
\mathcal{D}^{(0)}:=\mathcal{D}
;

(2)

if
\alpha+1
is a successor ordinal, we set
\mathcal{D}^{(\alpha+1)}:=(\mathcal{D}^{(\alpha)})^{\prime}
;

(3)

if
\alpha
is a limit ordinal, we set
\mathcal{D}^{(\alpha)}:=\bigcap_{\beta<\alpha}\mathcal{D}^{(\beta)}
.

If
\mathcal{D}
is closed, then every
\mathcal{D}^{(\alpha)}
is closed (this is easily proved by transfinite induction). We have the following Cantor–Bendixson-type theorem (cf.
[
Cie97
, Thm. 6.2.4]
).

Theorem 2.9
.

If
\mathcal{D}
is a closed (real or complex) distribution, then there exists a countable ordinal
\alpha
such that
\mathcal{D}^{(\alpha)}
is perfect.

Proof.

As it is customary, we denote by
\omega_{1}
the smallest uncountable ordinal. Recall that
\omega_{1}=\{\alpha\colon\ \alpha<\omega_{1}\}
. The recursion above gives a function

\displaystyle\omega_{1}

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle\{\text{closed distributions on }M\}

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{(\alpha)}.

satisfying the monotonicity property

\alpha_{1}<\alpha_{2}\Longrightarrow\mathcal{D}^{(\alpha_{1})}\supseteq\mathcal{D}^{(\alpha_{2})}.

Assume by contradiction that no
\mathcal{D}^{(\alpha)}
, with
\alpha<\omega_{1}
, is perfect. Then the mapping above is injective, and this contradicts the fact that if
\{C_{\alpha}\colon\ \alpha<\beta\}
is a strictly decreasing family of closed subsets of
TM
, then the ordinal
\beta
is at most countable. This is part (iii) of Theorem 6.2.1 of
[
Cie97
]
(the result is stated there for
\mathbb{R}^{n}
in place of
TM
, but the generalization is straightforward).
∎

Thanks to Theorem
2.9
, we can give the main definition of this section.

Definition 2.10

(
Core of a distribution
)
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a (real or complex) closed distribution. Then
\mathcal{D}^{(\omega_{1})}
will be called the core of
\mathcal{D}
, and denoted
\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{D})
. Equivalently,
\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{D})=\mathcal{D}^{(\alpha)}
, where
\alpha
is the minimal ordinal such that
(\mathcal{D}^{(\alpha)})^{\prime}=\mathcal{D}^{(\alpha)}
.

In view of the remark after Proposition
2.8
, it is clear that there are plenty of examples of closed distributions
\mathcal{D}
for which
\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{D})\subsetneq\mathcal{D}^{(k)}
for every finite
k
. Nevertheless, in the examples considered in this paper, it is always the case that
\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{D})=\mathcal{D}^{(k)}
for some finite
k
.

It is worth looking explicitly at a simple two-dimensional example where the iteration operation stabilizes after more than one step.

Example 2.11
.

Let
M=\mathbb{R}^{2}
and consider the quadratic form on
T\mathbb{R}^{2}
defined by
B_{(x,y)}=xdx\otimes dx+ydy\otimes dy
. The closed distribution
\mathcal{D}:=\ker B
(cf. Example
2.4
) is easily seen to be:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{(x,y)}=\begin{cases}\mathbb{R}\partial_{x}\qquad&x=0,\ y\neq 0\\
\mathbb{R}\partial_{y}\qquad&x\neq 0,\ y=0\\
\mathbb{R}\partial_{x}+\mathbb{R}\partial_{y}\qquad&x=0,\ y=0\\
\{0\}\qquad&x\neq 0,\ y\neq 0\end{cases}

Hence,
S_{\mathcal{D}}
is the union of the coordinate axes and

\displaystyle(TS_{\mathcal{D}})_{(x,y)}=\begin{cases}\mathbb{R}\partial_{y}\qquad&x=0,\ y\neq 0\\
\mathbb{R}\partial_{x}\qquad&x\neq 0,\ y=0\\
\mathbb{R}\partial_{x}+\mathbb{R}\partial_{y}\qquad&x=0,\ y=0\\
\{0\}\qquad&x\neq 0,\ y\neq 0\end{cases}

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{(x,y)}=\begin{cases}\mathbb{R}\partial_{x}+\mathbb{R}\partial_{y}\qquad&x=0,\ y=0\\
\{0\}\qquad&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

The support of the derived distribution
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
is the origin, and one finally gets that
\mathcal{D}^{\prime\prime}
and
\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{D})
are the zero distribution.

The next elementary lemma may be of help in describing the core of a distribution.

Lemma 2.12
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{D}
is a closed (real or complex) distribution on
M
and that
\mathcal{E}
is a perfect (real or complex) distribution such that
\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{D}
. Then
\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{D})
.

Proof.

It is enough to show, by transfinite induction, that
\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{D}^{(\alpha)}
for every ordinal
\alpha
. The case of a successor ordinal follows from part d) of Proposition
2.8
. The rest is trivial.
∎

Notice that the closedness of
\mathcal{D}
is not explicitly used in the proof. However, it is needed to ensure the existence of the core.

2.3.
A compactness lemma

A subset
\Omega\subseteq(\mathbb{C})TM
is said to be
conical
if
v\in\Omega
implies
tv\in\Omega
for every
t>0
.

Proposition 2.13
.

Let
M
be compact and let
\mathcal{D}
be a closed real (resp. complex) distribution. Then any cover of
\mathcal{D}\setminus\left(M\times\{0\}\right)
by open conical subsets of the real (resp. complexified) tangent bundle has a finite subcover.

Proof.

Fix a metric
g
on
M
. Then we define
\mathbb{S}(\mathcal{D})
as the set of vectors of unit length in
\mathcal{D}
. The statement boils down to the compactness of
\mathbb{S}(\mathcal{D})
, a consequence of the closure of
\mathcal{D}
and the compactness of
M
.
∎

3.
The Levi core of a CR manifold of hypersurface type

In this section we apply the basic theory of Section
2
to CR manifolds of hypersurface type. In Section
3.1
we review the elements of CR geometry we need (cf.
[
DT06
]
for a deeper discussion) and we introduce the notion of Levi core. Then we look at a few examples (in Section
3.2
) and we specialize the discussion to real hypersurfaces of complex manifolds (in Section
3.3
). Finally, in Section
3.4
, we discuss the relation between Levi core and local maximum sets.

3.1.
Notation and basic definitions

We consider an orientable, connected
CR manifold

(M,T^{1,0}M)

of hypersurface type
(in an alternative common terminology, we require
(M,T^{1,0}M)
to be
“of type
(n,1)
”
). This means that
M
is a real smooth manifold of odd dimension
2n+1
(
n\geq 1
), and that
T^{1,0}M
is a complex subbundle of
\mathbb{C}TM
of complex rank
n
such that the following two properties hold:

(1)

T^{1,0}M\cap T^{0,1}M=0
, where
T^{0,1}M:=\overline{T^{1,0}M}
;

(2)

T^{1,0}M
is formally integrable, that is, the commutator of any pair of smooth sections of
T^{1,0}M
is also a section of
T^{1,0}M
.

From now on, we omit the specification
“of hypersurface type”
and we refer to any
M
equipped with a structure as above as a
“CR manifold”
.

We denote by
H(M)\subset TM
the maximal complex distribution of the CR manifold, i.e.,

H(M)=\Re(T^{1,0}M)=\Re(T^{1,0}M\oplus T^{0,1}M)\;.

The real vector bundle
H(M)
has real rank
2n
and carries a complex structure
J_{b}
defined by
J_{b}(Z+\overline{Z})=i(Z-\overline{Z})
for every section
Z
of
T^{1,0}M
. The real part
\Re:T^{1,0}M\rightarrow H(M)
is an isomorphism of complex vector bundles with respect to this structure.

Consider now the real line bundle
E
over
M
defined by

E_{p}=\{\omega\in T^{*}_{p}M\ :\ \ker\omega\supseteq H_{p}(M)\}\;\qquad(p\in M),

where
H_{p}(M)
is the fiber at
p
of the maximal complex distribution. It is easy to see that
E
is isomorphic to
TM/H(M)
. Since
M
is orientable and
H(M)
is oriented by the complex structure
J_{b}
, it follows that
E
is orientable. Being defined over a connected manifold, it is globally trivial. We denote by
\Theta(M,T^{1,0}M)
the set of global nowhere vanishing smooth sections of
E
, that is,
\theta\in\Theta(M,T^{1,0}M)
if and only if
\theta
is a real nowhere vanishing smooth one-form with the property that
\theta(Z)=0
for every section
Z
of
T^{1,0}M
. Elements of
\Theta(M,T^{1,0}M)
are called
pseudo-Hermitian structures
on
(M,T^{1,0}M)
.

Given a pseudo-Hermitian structure
\theta
, the associated
Levi form
is the Hermitian form on
T^{1,0}M
defined by

\lambda_{\theta}(Z,\overline{W}):=\dfrac{1}{2i}d\theta(Z,\overline{W})=\dfrac{i}{4}\theta([Z,\overline{W}])\;,

where
Z,W
are smooth sections of
T^{1,0}M
. If
\theta_{1},\ \theta_{2}\in\Theta(M,T^{1,0}M)
, then there exists a nowhere vanishing
g\in C^{\infty}(M,\mathbb{R})
such that
\theta_{1}=g\theta_{2}
, and we have

(3.1)

\lambda_{\theta_{1}}=g\lambda_{\theta_{2}}\;.

We say that
(M,T^{1,0}M)
is
pseudoconvex
if
\lambda_{\theta}
is semidefinite for some (and hence for every)
\theta\in\Theta(M,T^{1,0}M)
. If
(M,T^{1,0}M)
is pseudoconvex, we denote by
\Theta_{+}(M,T^{1,0}M)
the set of pseudo-Hermitian structures such that the corresponding Levi form
\lambda_{\theta}
is nonnegative definite.

From Equation (
3.1
), it follows that the null-distribution (see Example
2.4
) of the Levi form
\lambda_{\theta}
is independent of
\theta
: we call it the
Levi distribution
of the CR manifold and denote it by
\mathcal{N}
. As observed in Example
2.4
,
\mathcal{N}
is a closed complex distribution on
M
. Explicitly, for
p\in M
we have

\mathcal{N}_{p}=\{Z_{p}\in T^{1,0}_{p}M\ :\ \lambda_{\theta,p}(Z_{p},\overline{W}_{p})=0\quad\forall W_{p}\in T^{1,0}M\}\;.

If
(M,T^{1,0}M)
is pseudoconvex, by elementary linear algebra one sees that
\mathcal{N}_{p}
consists of those vectors
Z_{p}\in T_{p}^{1,0}M
such that
\lambda_{\theta,p}(Z_{p},\overline{Z}_{p})=0
. We finally recall the main definition of the paper (anticipated in the introduction).

Definition 3.1
.

The
Levi core
of a CR manifold is the core
\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{N})
of its Levi distribution
\mathcal{N}
(in the sense of Definition
2.10
).

The next two propositions allow to control, from below and above respectively, the Levi core in various situations.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
V\subset M
be an embedded submanifold of positive dimension and such that
T_{p}V
is a complex (i.e.,
J_{b}
-invariant) subspace of
H_{p}(M)
for every
p\in V
. Then

(3.2)

\{(p,Z_{p})\colon\ p\in V\text{ and }Z_{p}\in\mathbb{C}T_{p}V\cap T^{1,0}_{p}M\}\subseteq\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{N}).

As observed in the proof, the LHS of (
3.2
) is nontrivial for every
p\in V
, and therefore the proposition implies in particular that
S_{\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{N})}\supseteq V
.

Proof.

Denote by
\mathcal{E}
the complex distribution on
M
defined by the LHS of (
3.2
) (and trivial fiber for
p\notin V
). Thanks to Lemma
2.12
, our task boils down to proving the following two statements:

a)

\mathcal{E}
is a perfect distribution.

b)

\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{N}
.

To prove statement a) it is clearly enough to check that
V
is contained in the support of
\mathcal{E}
. Given
p\in V
and a nonzero
X_{p}\in T_{p}V
(it exists because
V
is positive dimensional), we have
X_{p}-iJ_{b}X_{p}\in\mathbb{C}T_{p}V\cap T^{1,0}M
, because
T_{p}V
is
J_{b}
-invariant. Thus, the fiber at
p
of
\mathbb{C}TV\cap T^{1,0}M
is nontrivial, as we wanted.

To verify the inclusion b), we pick
X_{p}\in T_{p}V
(
p\in V
) and check that
Z_{p}:=X_{p}-iJ_{b}X_{p}\in\mathcal{N}_{p}
. Let
X
be a local smooth section of
TM
extending
X_{p}
and such that
X_{q}\in T_{q}V
for every
q\in V
near
p
, and put
Z:=X-iJX
.
By the
J_{b}
-invariance assumption, the restriction of
Z
to
V
is a section of
\mathbb{C}TV
. Then
[Z,\overline{Z}]_{q}\in\mathbb{C}T_{q}V
at every
q\in V
near
p
. Because
\mathbb{C}T_{p}V\subseteq T^{1,0}_{p}M\oplus T^{0,1}_{p}M
, this implies in turn that
\theta([Z,\overline{Z}])
vanishes on
V
for every pseudo-Hermitian structure
\theta
. Since
M
is pseudoconvex, this means that
Z_{p}\in\mathcal{N}_{p}
, completing the proof of b).∎

Before stating the second proposition, we need a couple of definitions, where
M
is always a CR manifold of hypersurface type.

Definition 3.3

(
Zero holomorphic dimension
)
.

A subset
A\subseteq M
has zero holomorphic dimension at
p
if

\mathbb{C}T_{p}A\cap\mathcal{N}_{p}=\{0\}\;.

Notice that here
T_{p}A
is the tangent space in the sense of Definition
2.5
.

Definition 3.4

(
Weakly regular stratification
)
.

A weakly regular stratification of
M
is a finite collection of closed subsets
\Sigma_{i}\subseteq M
(
i=0,\ldots,N
) such that:

(1)

\Sigma_{N}\subset\Sigma_{N-1}\subset\ldots\subset\Sigma_{1}\subset\Sigma_{0}=M

(2)

for each
i=0,\ldots,N-1
, the set
\Sigma_{i}\setminus\Sigma_{i+1}
has zero holomorphic dimension at each of its points.

We say that
M
is
weakly regular
if there exists a weakly regular decomposition of
M
with
\Sigma_{N}=\emptyset
.

Notice that, since the
\Sigma_{i}
’s are closed and tangent distributions are local objects, the assumption is equivalent to
\mathbb{C}T_{p}\Sigma_{i}\cap\mathcal{N}_{p}=\{0\}
for every
p\in\Sigma_{i}\setminus\Sigma_{i+1}
and every
i=0,\ldots,N-1
.

Remark
.

The notion of zero holomorphic dimension originates from work of Kohn
[
Koh79
]
(see also
[
Cat84b
, p. 40]
). Definition
3.4
generalizes the notion of weakly regular boundary in
[
Cat84b
]
(which in turn had a precursor in
[
DF78
]
). In Catlin’s definition,
M
is the boundary of a smooth bounded domain, and
\Sigma_{N}
is empty. Our definition is phrased slightly differently in terms of smooth Zariski tangent spaces, but it is an immediate consequence of part c) of Proposition
2.6
that the two definitions coincide, modulo these additional assumptions.

Proposition 3.5
.

If
\{\Sigma_{j}\}_{j=0,\ldots,N}
is a weakly regular stratification of
M
, then
S_{\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{N})}\subseteq\Sigma_{N}
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that the Levi core is nontrivial. Let
j
be the largest index for which
S_{\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{N})}\subseteq\Sigma_{j}
, and assume by contradiction that
j<N
. By the stability of the Levi core,

\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{N})=\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{N})\cap\mathbb{C}TS_{\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{N})}\subseteq\mathcal{N}\cap\mathbb{C}T\Sigma_{j}.

If
p\in\Sigma_{j}\setminus\Sigma_{j+1}
is in the support of the Levi core, then
\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{N})_{p}\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{p}\cap\mathbb{C}T_{p}\Sigma_{j}=\{0\}
, contradicting the maximality of
j
.
∎

Applying Proposition
3.5
to the case of a weakly regular CR manifold, we obtain the following.

Corollary 3.6
.

If
M
is weakly regular, then its Levi core is trivial.

Finally, we define
\mathcal{N}^{\mathbb{R}}:=\Re(\mathcal{N})
, which is a real closed distribution on
M
. Since taking the real part of a vector field gives an isomorphism of complex vector bundles between
T^{1,0}M
and
H(M)
,
\mathcal{N}^{\mathbb{R}}_{p}
is a complex subspace of
H(M)_{p}
for every
p\in M
. For the same reason,
\Re(\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{N}))_{p}
is, at every
p\in M
, a complex subspace of
H(M)_{p}
. However,
\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{N}^{\mathbb{R}})_{p}
need not be.
We will be mostly using the distribution
\mathcal{N}
instead of
\mathcal{N}^{\mathbb{R}}
, even if the latter may have a more immediate geometric meaning, particularly when Levi-flat foliations or complex analytic varieties are present.

3.2.
A class of
3
-dimensional CR structures

In this section, we describe the Levi core for certain CR structures on the total space of (real) line and circle bundles on Riemann surfaces. A similar construction was examined in
[
Cor96
]
.

Let
Y
be a Riemann surface. We consider a fiber bundle
\pi:M\to Y
whose fibers are either
F=\mathbb{R}
or
F=\mathbb{S}^{1}
.
Let
t
be a global coordinate on
F
and
z
a local coordinate on
Y
. We define a CR structure on
M
as follows.

Consider a
(1,0)
-form
\omega
on
Y
and a smooth complex-valued function
g\in C^{\infty}(F)
; we define
T^{1,0}M
as the common kernel of the two
1
-forms:

dt-g(t)\omega\quad\text{and}\quad d\overline{z}\;.

If we write locally
\omega=u(z)dz
, then

L=\partial_{z}+g(t)u(z)\partial_{t}

is a local generator for
T^{1,0}M
; hence,

\theta=dt-2\Re(g(t)\omega)

is a pseudo-Hermitian structure for
(M,T^{1,0}M)
. The Levi form of
\theta
is locally given by

\lambda_{\theta}(L,\overline{L})=\frac{i}{4}\theta([L,\overline{L}])=\frac{1}{2}\Im(gu_{\bar{z}})+\frac{|u|^{2}}{2}\Im(\bar{g}g_{t})\;.

In order to have a pseudoconvex CR manifold, we want
\lambda_{\theta}
to be semidefinite, hence we want to control the sign of the previous quantity, in terms of
g
and
\omega
. In order to do that, we need a couple of observations. If
Y
is compact, there are a holomorphic
1
-form
\eta
and a smooth function
H
such that
\omega=\eta+\partial H
(it follows, for instance, by taking the conjugate of
[
For81
, Theorem 19.9]
); therefore, locally,

u(z)=f(z)+H_{z}(z)

with
f(z)
holomorphic. If instead
Y
is open, then it is Stein and
H^{p,q}(Y)=0
for
p\geq 0
,
q\geq 1
. So we can always find
H
such that
\omega=\partial H
. In both cases, we have that
u_{\bar{z}}=H_{z\bar{z}}
; therefore, even if
u
is only locally defined, we can express its derivative
u_{\bar{z}}
in terms of the Laplacian of a global function. Even if the precise value of such Laplacian depends on the choice of local holomorphic coordinate
z
, its sign does not.

We will now examine three possible choices of
g
and
\omega
for which
(M,T^{1,0}M)
is pseudoconvex, which give rise to three different behaviors. We dub the three resulting classes of CR structures Type I, II, and III, for ease of reference.

3.2.1.
Type I

If
F=\mathbb{S}^{1}
and
g\equiv 1
, then

2\lambda_{\theta}(L,\overline{L})=\Im(H_{z\bar{z}})=(\Im(H))_{z\bar{z}}\;.

So,
M
is pseudoconvex as soon as
\Im(H)
is a subharmonic (or superharmonic) function. Set
W_{1}:=\{p\in Y:\ \triangle\Im(H)(p)=0\}\subseteq Y
; the Levi-null distribution is supported on

S_{\mathcal{N}}=\pi^{-1}(W_{1}),

and thus one may easily verify that

TS_{\mathcal{N}}=(d\pi)^{-1}TW_{1},

where
d\pi:TM\rightarrow TY
is the differential of
\pi
. Denote by
W_{2}
the set of points
p\in Y
where
T_{p}W_{1}
is two-dimensional. By Proposition
2.3
,
W_{2}
is closed. The closed sets

\Sigma_{2}:=\pi^{-1}(W_{2})\subseteq\Sigma_{1}:=\pi^{-1}(W_{1})\subseteq\Sigma_{0}:=M

define a weakly regular stratification of
M
, essentially because if
L
is tangent to the support of
S_{\mathcal{N}}
at some point, necessarily
TW_{1}
is two-dimensional at that point. Thus, by Proposition
3.5
, the support of the Levi core is contained in
\Sigma_{2}
. It is not difficult to verify that if
H
is assumed to be real-analytic, then the extended stratification obtained setting
\Sigma_{3}:=\emptyset
is still weakly regular, and therefore the manifold has trivial Levi core.

As a particular case, if
\Im(H)
is harmonic, then
\lambda_{\theta}\equiv 0
, i.e. the distribution
H(M)
is integrable. In this case,
M
is said to be
Levi-flat
and it is foliated in complex leaves:
H(M)
, the tangent distribution to the leaves, has a natural complex structure.

Such a foliation is described by the
1
-form
\theta
.

Remark
.

If
Y
is compact, then
\Im(H)
is harmonic if and only if it is constant, hence

\theta=dt-\Re(\omega)=dt-\Re(\eta)-\Re(\partial H)

and, as
H
may be assumed to be real-valued,
\Re(\partial H)=dH
; moreover,
d\Re(\eta)=\Re(d\eta)=0
. So
d\theta=0
; the foliation on
M
is induced by a closed form, so every leaf of the foliation has trivial holonomy; hence, by a result of Sacksteder (cfr [Camacho-Neto, Notes to Chapter V, (2), p. 109]), the foliation has no exceptional minimal set, therefore, if no leaf is compact, then all leaves are dense.

If
Y
is open, then
\Im(H)
harmonic implies that locally there exists a real-valued harmonic function
K
such that
K-i\Im(H)
is antiholomorphic; therefore
v=H+K-i\Im(H)
is a real-valued function such that
\partial v=\partial H
. Again
\Re(\partial v)=dv
. So, locally,
\theta=dt-dv
; in conclusion, also in this case,
d\theta=0
.

3.2.2.
Type II

If
F=\mathbb{R}
,
g(t)=t
, then

2\lambda_{\theta}(L,\overline{L})=\Im(tH_{z\bar{z}})

which is of constant sign if and only if it vanishes, hence
\Im(H)_{z\bar{z}}\equiv 0
, i.e.
\Im(H)
is harmonic and
M
is again Levi-flat.

3.2.3.
Type III

If
F=\mathbb{S}^{1}
, let
g(t)=e^{it}
and
\omega
be given by the function
H(z)=ie^{-ih(z)}
with
h:Y\to\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}
. Then

H_{z}=h_{z}e^{-ih}\qquad H_{z\bar{z}}=h_{z\bar{z}}e^{-ih}-i|h_{z}|^{2}e^{-ih}\;.

So, locally,

2\lambda_{\theta}(L,\overline{L})=\Im(e^{i(t-h)}h_{z\bar{z}}-i|h_{z}|^{2}e^{i(t-h)})+|f+h_{z}e^{-ih}|^{2}\Im(e^{-it}ie^{it})

=h_{z\bar{z}}\sin(t-h)-|h_{z}|^{2}\cos(t-h)+|f|^{2}+|h_{z}|^{2}+2\Re(fh_{z}e^{i(t-h)})\;.

Suppose now also that
Y
is open, then
f(z)\equiv 0
, so

2\lambda_{\theta}(L,\overline{L})=h_{z\bar{z}}\sin(t-h)-|h_{z}|^{2}\cos(t-h)+|h_{z}|^{2}

which is always positive if and only if
h_{z\bar{z}}\equiv 0
, i.e. if and only if
h
is a harmonic function.

In such case

2\lambda_{\theta}(L,\overline{L})=|h_{z}|^{2}(1-\cos(t-h))

so,
\mathcal{N}
is supported at points where

h_{z}=0\qquad\textrm{or}\qquad t=h\;.

The second condition describes a section
s
of
\pi:M\to Y
, whose image is easily checked to be a complex submanifold of
M
and its tangent bundle will be contained in
\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{N})
, by Lemma
3.2
.

On the other hand, as
h
is harmonic, the points where
h_{z}=0
are isolated (if
h
is nonconstant) and, as
\partial_{t}
is never contained in
H(M)
, then the set

\{p\in Y\ :\ h_{z}(p)=0\}\times T\mathbb{S}^{1}

is not contained in
\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{N})
.

In conclusion,
\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{N})
is the tangent bundle to
s(Y)\subset M
.

3.3.
Real hypersurfaces

Given a complex manifold
X
of complex dimension
n+1
, any smooth real hypersurface
M\subset X
has a natural structure of CR manifold of hypersurface type; if
j:M\to X
is the inclusion, we define

T^{1,0}M=j^{*}(\mathbb{C}TM\cap T^{1,0}X)

and
(M,T^{1,0}M)
is then a CR manifold.

The formal integrability of
T^{1,0}M
follows from the formal integrability of
T^{1,0}X
and from the integrability of
\mathbb{C}TM
.

Proposition 3.7
.

A smooth one-form
\theta
on
M
is a pseudo-Hermitian structure if and only if there exist a neighborhood
U
of
M
in
X
and a smooth function
r:U\to\mathbb{R}
such that

M=\{p\in U\ :\ r(p)=0\}\qquad dr_{p}\neq 0\ \forall\,p\in M\qquad\theta=j^{*}d^{c}r

where
d^{c}:=i(\partial-\overline{\partial})
. Such a function is called a defining function for
M
on
U
.

Proof.

It is easy to see that

H(M)=j^{*}(T_{p}M\cap JT_{p}M)

as the latter is the maximal complex subspace of
T_{p}M
.

Consider a neighborhood
U
of
M
and a smooth defning function
r:U\to\mathbb{R}
.

If
J
is the complex structure of
X
, we have that
\ker dr_{p}=T_{p}M
for
p\in M
and
\ker d^{c}r_{p}=\ker(dr\circ J)_{p}=J(\ker dr_{p})=JT_{p}M
; therefore,

\ker j^{*}d^{c}r_{p}=j^{*}(\ker d^{c}r_{p}\cap\ker dr_{p})=j^{*}(T_{p}M\cap JT_{p}M)=H(M)_{p}\;.

Hence
\theta=j^{*}d^{c}r\in\Theta(M,T^{1,0}M)
.

On the other hand, any other
\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta(M,T^{1,0})
can be written as
\theta^{\prime}=g\theta
with
g\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(M)
never vanishing; there exist a neighborhood
U^{\prime}
of
M
and a function
f:U^{\prime}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
e^{f}
extends
g
from
M
to
U^{\prime}
; the function
e^{f}r
is again a defining function for
M
on
U^{\prime}
, as its zero set in
U^{\prime}
is again
M
and
d(e^{f}r)_{p}=r(p)d(e^{f})_{p}+e^{f(p)}dr_{p}=e^{f(p)}dr_{p}\neq 0
for
p\in M
.

We have
d^{c}e^{f}r=rd^{c}e^{f}+e^{f}d^{c}r
, so

j^{*}d^{c}(e^{f}r)=j^{*}(e^{f}d^{c}r)=gj^{*}d^{c}r=g\theta=\theta^{\prime}\;.

Therefore, every pseudo-Hermitian structure is obtained from a defining function and vice versa.
∎

We denote by
\mathrm{Def}(M)
the set of defining functions for
M
; the previous proposition implies that

\Theta(M,T^{1,0}M)=\{d^{c}r\ :\ r\in\mathrm{Def}(M)\}\;.

The following is an easy consequence of the last proposition, once we observe that
dd^{c}=2i\partial\overline{\partial}
.

Corollary 3.8
.

The CR manifold
M
is pseudoconvex if and only if there exists a defining function
r:U\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\partial\overline{\partial}r
gives a semidefinite Hermitian form on
T^{1,0}M
.

The equivalence relation
r\sim r^{\prime}
if and only if
r=e^{f}r^{\prime}
(up to shrikning the open neighborhood of
M
on which the functions are defined) partitions
\mathrm{Def}(M)
in two equivalence classes, by orientability; when
M
is pseudoconvex, we denote by
\mathrm{Def}_{+}(M)
the equivalence class containing a (and hence every) defining function such that
\partial\overline{\partial}r
is positive semidefinite on
T^{1,0}M
. Then

(3.3)

\Theta_{+}(M,T^{1,0}M)=\{d^{c}r\ :\ r\in\mathrm{Def}_{+}(M)\}\;.

Consider, in a complex manifold
X
, a precompact, smoothly bounded domain
\Omega\subseteq X
; its boundary
M=b\Omega
is a smooth hypersurface in
X
, hence it has a natural CR structure given by
T^{1,0}M
.

We define
\mathrm{Def}(\Omega)
as the set of defining functions for
\Omega
, i.e. smooth functions
r:U\to\mathbb{R}
such that

(1)

U
is an open set of
X
with
\Omega\Subset U

(2)

\Omega=\{x\in U\ :\ r(x)<0\}

(3)

dr_{p}\neq 0
for every
p\in b\Omega
.

The domain
\Omega
is
pseudoconvex
if
(M,T^{1,0}M)
is pseudoconvex and
\mathrm{Def}(\Omega)\subseteq\mathrm{Def}_{+}(M)
, i.e. if for one (and hence for every) defining function
r
for
\Omega
the Levi form
\partial\overline{\partial}r
is positive semidefinite on
T^{1,0}M
.

The proof of the following result is essentially contained in
[
Cat84b
]
, even if the statement contained there is a global one. We will make use of Theorem 3 in
[
Cat84b
]
, whose proof is presented in
[
Cat84a
]
.

Proposition 3.9
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq X
be a precompact, smoothly bounded, pseudoconvex domain and
M=b\Omega
; if
p\in M
is of finite type, then there exists a neighborhood
U
of
p\in M
such that
U\cap M
is weakly regular.

Proof.

Let
\mathfrak{M}(\cdot)
be the Catlin multitype, as defined in
[
Cat84b
]
. By
[
Cat84b
, Theorem 3 (2) and (5)]
, if
p
is of finite type
\tau(p)
, then there exists a neighborhood
U
of
p
such that
\mathfrak{M}(q)\leq\mathfrak{M}(p)
for all
q\in U\cap M
.
By the Remark following the statement of Theorem 3 in
[
Cat84b
]
, as
\mathfrak{M}(q)
is bounded by
\tau(p)
, it can only assume a finite number of values; the number of such values depends only on the bound on
\mathfrak{M}(q)
. So, let
M_{0}<M_{1}<\ldots<M_{N-1}
be the possible multitypes for
q\in U
and set

\Sigma_{k}=\{q\in M\cap U\ :\ \mathfrak{M}(q)\geq M_{k}\}

By the semicontinuity (​
[
Cat84b
, Theorem 3 (2)]
) of
\mathfrak{M}
, the sets
\Sigma_{k}
are closed in
U\cap M
and by
[
Cat84b
, Theorem 3(3)]
each
\Sigma_{k}\setminus\Sigma_{k+1}
is contained, up to shrinking
U
, in a submanifold
V_{k}
of
U
, with holomorphic dimension
0
, i.e. such that

\mathbb{C}T_{q}V_{k}\cap\mathcal{N}_{q}=\{0\}\;,\ \ \forall\;q\in V_{k}\;.

Therefore,
M\cap U
is weakly regular.
∎

Finally, in the context of hypersurfaces in complex manifolds, Lemma
3.2
can be reformulated naturally as follows, once we notice that the tangent distribution to a complex analytic set is given pointwise on
M
by complex subspaces of
T_{p}X
.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
M
be a real smooth hypersurface in a complex manifold
X
, with its natural structure of CR manifold; if
M
contains a complex analytic set
Z
(possibly with boundary), then
T^{1,0}Z\subseteq\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{N})
.

In the last part of this section we show how the CR structures introduced in Section
3.2
can be realized as hypersurfaces inside a complex manifold of dimension
2
.

Indeed, consider
X=Y\times\mathbb{C}
, let
z
be a local variable on
Y
and
w
a variable on
\mathbb{C}
and choose a function
h:Y\to\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}
; then the function

r(z,w)=\Im(we^{-ih(z)})

defines a hypersurface
M\subseteq Y\times\mathbb{C}
which is an embedded realization for type II CR-structures; if we set

r(z,w)=|w-e^{ih(z)}|^{2}-1

we obtain the hypersurface of type III. Finally, if

r(z,w)=|w|^{2}-|e^{iH(z)}|^{2}

with
H:Y\to\mathbb{C}
, then the zero set of
r
is CR-diffeomorphic to a hypersurface of type I.

By slightly altering the defining functions, we can add a smooth
“cap”
to our hypersufaces, making them closed without boundary, and then obtain relatively compact pseudoconvex domains whose boundary is, on some open set, CR-diffeomorphic to one of the examples. For instance, in the case of type III CR-structures, we set

r(z,w)=|w-e^{ih(z)}|^{2}-(1-\eta(z))

for a suitable function
\eta:Y\to[0,+\infty)
. However, particular care should be put into choosing the function
\eta
, if we want to obtain a pseudoconvex domain.

For example, for type III domains, we need the plurisubharmonicity of

|w|^{2}-2\Re(we^{-ih(z)})+\eta(z)\;.

As we are working locally, we can multiply everything by
e^{u(z)}
where
u(z)
is the harmonic conjugate of
h(z)
:

|w|^{2}e^{u(z)}-\Re(we^{f(z)})+e^{u(z)}\eta(z)\;,

where
f(z)=u(z)-ih(z)
is holomorphic, so that
e^{u(z)}=|e^{f(z)}|
is clearly plurisubharmonic.

Therefore, we need to make sure that the last term is plurisubharmonic as well; a convenient choice is to set
\eta(z)=\eta_{1}(h(z))
, with
\eta_{1}
convex and increasing. Computations easily show that then
e^{u(z)}\eta(z)
is plurisubharmonic. We notice that, with such a choice, in order for
\eta
to be
<1
on a compact subset of
Y
, it is vital that
Y
is of dimension
1
.

The well known worm domains introduced by Diederich and Fornaess are instances of this construction, where
Y
is an annulus and
h(z)=\log|z|^{2}
.

Finally, suppose that
Y
is an open Riemann surface that can be properly embedded in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
; it is well known that there exists a tubular neighborhood of
Y\subset\mathbb{C}^{2}
which is biholomorphic to a neighborhood of
Y\times\{0\}
in
Y\times\mathbb{C}
. By rescaling, we can then realize the capped versions of the hypersurfaces of type I and III as boundaries of bounded domains in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
.

In the type II case, one can construct a bounded domain in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
whose boundary contains an open set which is CR-diffeomorphic to a neighborhood (in
M
) of an open set of
Y
. As long as we are careful in adding the caps, the resulting boundary will be again pseudoconvex, if the original hypersurface was.

3.4.
Local maximum sets

Let
X
be a complex manifold, and let
M\subset X
be a real hypersurface. We proved that the support of the Levi core of
M
must contain any complex subvariety of
M
. In this section, we show that it also contains every local maximum set contained in
M
. These sets are a generalization of complex analytic sets in terms of the maximum principle for plurisubharmonic functions and, if
M
is the boundary of a pseudoconvex domain, they coincide with the complement of the weak Jensen boundary. They are also linked with the existence of positive currents of bidimension
(1,1)
(see for instance
[
OS98
,
Sib17
,
BM21
]
).

We will say that a set
K
is
locally closed
in
X
if for every
x\in K
there is a neighborhood
U
of
x
in
X
such that
\overline{U}\cap K
is closed in
X
.

Definition 3.11
.

Let
X
be a complex manifold and let
K\subset X
be locally closed. We say that
K
is a
local maximum set
if
every
x\in K
has a relatively compact neighbourhood
U

such that
\overline{U}
is contained in a coordinate neighborhood of
X
,
K\cap\overline{U}
is closed and for every
function
\psi
which is plurisubharmonic in a neighbourhood of
\overline{U}
, we have

\max_{\overline{U}\cap K}\psi=\max_{bU\cap K}\psi

where
bU=\overline{U}\setminus U
.

For some properties of local maximum sets, see
[
Slo86
]
. We will use the following result, which is Proposition 2.3, iv) of that paper.

Proposition 3.12
.

Let
K
be a locally closed subset of a complex manifold
X
. Then
K
is not a local maximum set if and only if there is a
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
strictly plurisubharmonic function
\phi
on some open set
W
such that
\phi|_{K\cap W}
has a strict maximum at some point of
K\cap W
.

Local maximum sets are, in many ways, generalizations of complex analytic sets; their presence influences the behaviour of plurisubharmonic functions around them. The next result generalizes, in this sense, Lemma
3.10
.

Theorem 3.13
.

Let
\Omega
be a precompact, smoothly bounded, pseudoconvex domain in a complex manifold
X
and consider
M=b\Omega
as a pseudoconvex CR manifold; if
K\subset M
is a local maximum set, then
K\subseteq S_{\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{N})}
.

Proof.

Consider the distribution
\mathcal{E}:=\mathbb{C}TK\cap\mathcal{N}
. We will show that its support contains
K
. Since this immediately implies that
\mathcal{E}
is perfect, the conclusion follows from Lemma
2.12
.

We prove our claim by contradiction, assuming that
x\in K
and
\mathbb{C}T_{x}K\cap\mathcal{N}_{x}=\{0\}
, that is,
T_{x}K\cap JT_{x}K\cap\mathcal{N}^{\mathbb{R}}_{x}=\{0\}
. From this, one can write

T_{x}K=H\oplus L\oplus F,

where:

•

L
is a complex subspace of
H(M)_{x}
such that the restriction of
dd^{c}r
to
\mathbb{C}L_{x}\cap T^{1,0}M
is positive definite, where
r\in\mathrm{Def}(\Omega)
,

•

H
is a totally real subspace of
H(M)_{x}
, i.e.
JH\cap H=\{0\}
,

•

F
is either
\{0\}
or a
1
-dimensional complement of
H(M)_{x}
in
T_{x}M
, depending on whether
T_{x}K\subseteq H(M)_{x}
or not.

We fix a neighborhood
U
of
x
where we have holomorphic coordinates
z_{j}=x_{j}+iy_{j}

(j=1,\ldots,n)
such that (for appropriate integers
k,\ell\geq 0
):

•

x
is the origin,

•

H
is generated by
\left\{\partial_{x_{1}},\ldots,\partial_{x_{k}}\right\},

•

\mathbb{C}L_{x}\cap T_{x}^{1,0}M
is generated (over
\mathbb{C}
) by
\left\{\partial_{z_{k+1}},\ldots,\partial_{z_{k+\ell}}\right\}

•

\partial_{y_{n}}
is an inward transversal vector field to
b\Omega
at
x=0
, and
F
is contained in the span of
\partial_{x_{n}}
(this is possible because
K\subseteq M
and hence
T_{x}K\subseteq T_{x}b\Omega
).

From now on, we identify points of
U
with their coordinates (e.g.,
x=0
). It is also useful to group the coordinates as follows:

z=(z^{\prime},z^{\prime\prime},z^{\prime\prime\prime},z_{n}),

z^{\prime}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})\in\mathbb{C}^{k},\quad z^{\prime\prime}=(z_{k+1},\ldots,z_{k+\ell})\in\mathbb{C}^{\ell},

z^{\prime\prime\prime}=(z_{k+\ell+1},\ldots,z_{n-1})\in\mathbb{C}^{n-k-\ell-1},z_{n}\in\mathbb{C}.

As
T_{0}K=H\oplus L\oplus F
, by Proposition
2.6
, up to shrinking
U
, we have a manifold
V\subseteq U
of real dimension
(k+2\ell)
(if
F=\{0\}
) or
k+2\ell+1
(if
F
is
1
-dimensional) such that
T_{0}V=T_{0}K
and
K\subseteq V
; by applying the Proposition directly to the manifold
b\Omega
, we can suppose that
V\subseteq b\Omega
.

The slice

U^{\prime}:=\{z\in U\ :\ z^{\prime}=z^{\prime\prime\prime}=0\}

is an open set of
\mathbb{C}^{\ell+1}_{z^{\prime\prime},z_{n}}
, and
\Omega^{\prime}:=U^{\prime}\cap\Omega
is a pseudoconvex domain in
\mathbb{C}^{\ell+1}
. With respect to the variables
(z^{\prime\prime},z_{n})
, the domain
\Omega^{\prime}
is given by the condition

r^{\prime}(z^{\prime\prime},z_{n})=r(0,z^{\prime\prime},0,z_{n})<0,

so
dd^{c}r^{\prime}
restricted to the maximal complex subspace of
T_{0}b\Omega^{\prime}\subseteq T_{0}\mathbb{C}^{\ell+1}
can be identified with
dd^{c}r
restricted to
L
. Therefore,
\Omega^{\prime}
is strictly pseudoconvex in
0
. Without loss of generality (that is, for an appropriate choice of the coordinate system), we may assume that
\Omega^{\prime}
is strictly convex at
0
. Notice that
V\cap U^{\prime}\subseteq b\Omega\cap U^{\prime}=b\Omega^{\prime}
.

Shrinking, if needed,
U
again, we now parametrize
V
with
T_{0}V
(identified with a linear subspace of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
); we treat the case where
\dim F=1
, the other being analogous. For
z\in U
, recall that
H
is given by
\Im(z^{\prime})=z^{\prime\prime}=z^{\prime\prime\prime}=z_{n}=0
and
U^{\prime}
by
z^{\prime}=z^{\prime\prime}=0
; the parametrization of
V
over
T_{0}V
will then be of the form

\Im(z^{\prime})=f^{\prime}(\Re(z^{\prime}),z^{\prime\prime},x_{n}),\qquad z^{\prime\prime\prime}=f^{\prime\prime}(\Re(z^{\prime}),z^{\prime\prime},x_{n}),\qquad y_{n}=f^{\prime\prime\prime}(\Re(z^{\prime}),z^{\prime\prime},x_{n})

where (for some
C_{1}>0
)

(3.4)

|f^{\prime}|,\ |f^{\prime\prime}|,\ |f^{\prime\prime\prime}|\leq C_{1}(\|\Re(z^{\prime})\|^{2}+\|z^{\prime\prime}\|^{2}+x_{n}^{2}).

By the strict convexity of
b\Omega^{\prime}
, one easily gets that

f^{\prime\prime\prime}(\Re(z^{\prime}),z^{\prime\prime},x_{n})=q(z^{\prime\prime},x_{n})+s(\Re(z^{\prime}))+b(\Re(z^{\prime});(z^{\prime\prime},x_{n}))+\textrm{h.o.t.},

where:

•

q
is homogeneous of degree
2
and positive definite as a real quadratic form on the real vector space
\mathbb{C}^{\ell}\times\mathbb{R}
,

•

s
is homogeneous of degree
2
,

•

b
is a bilinear map
\mathbb{R}^{k}\otimes\left(\mathbb{C}^{\ell}\times\mathbb{R}\right)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.

Hence, there is a constant
C_{2}>0
such that, for every
\eta>0
,

|b(\Re(z^{\prime});(z^{\prime\prime},x_{n}))|\leq C_{2}\|\Re(z^{\prime})\|\|(z^{\prime\prime},x_{n})\|\leq\frac{C_{2}}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\eta}\|\Re(z^{\prime})\|^{2}+\eta\|(z^{\prime\prime},x_{n})\|^{2}\right)

therefore, if
C_{2}\eta/2
is strictly less than the smallest eigenvalue of
q
, for appropriate constants
a,C_{3}>0
(and shrinking once more
U
) we have

(3.5)

f^{\prime\prime\prime}(\Re(z^{\prime}),z^{\prime\prime},x_{n})\geq a\|(z^{\prime\prime},x_{n})\|-C_{3}\|\Re(z^{\prime})\|^{2}.

Consider the function

\phi(z)=2C_{3}(\|\Im(z^{\prime})\|^{2}-\|\Re(z^{\prime})\|^{2})-y_{n}+\frac{C_{3}}{2}\|\Re(z^{\prime})\|^{2}+\|\Im(z^{\prime})\|^{2}+\frac{a}{2}(\|z^{\prime\prime}\|^{2}+x_{n}^{2})+\|z^{\prime\prime\prime}\|^{2}+y_{n}^{2}\;.

Notice that
\phi
is strictly plurisubharmonic, because both
\|\Im(z^{\prime})\|^{2}-\|\Re(z^{\prime})\|^{2}
and
y_{n}
are pluriharmonic. By (
3.4
) and (
3.5
), for every
z\in V
we have (for another constant
C_{4}>0
, and shrinking
U
one last time)

\displaystyle\phi(z)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-\frac{a}{2}(\|z^{\prime\prime}\|^{2}+x_{n}^{2})-\frac{C_{3}}{2}\|\Re(z^{\prime})\|^{2}+C_{4}(\|\Re(z^{\prime})\|^{2}+\|z^{\prime\prime}\|^{2}+x_{n}^{2})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-\frac{a}{4}(\|z^{\prime\prime}\|^{2}+x_{n}^{2})-\frac{C_{3}}{4}\|\Re(z^{\prime})\|^{2},

and in particular
\phi(0)=0
and
\phi(z)<0
for
V\setminus\{0\}
. Since
K\subseteq V
, by Proposition
3.12

K
is not a local maximum set, which is the desired contradiction. The proof is complete.
∎

4.
D’Angelo forms and D’Angelo class

In this section we discuss D’Angelo forms and the D’Angelo class. We review the basic theory first (in Section
4.1
), and then we introduce the norm-like quantities
\mathfrak{n}_{K}(\mathcal{D})
, in terms of which our
“reduction to the core”
theorem is formulated. Sections
4.2
and
4.3
prepare the ground for the definition of
\mathfrak{n}_{K}(\mathcal{D})
, discussing a similar notion in the context of complex manifolds and recalling a few basic properties of the Cauchy–Riemann complex on abstract CR manifolds, respectively.

4.1.
Review of the basic theory

Let
(M,T^{1,0}M)
be a connected and orientable CR manifold of hypersurface type, and fix a pseudo-Hermitian structure
\theta\in\Theta(M,T^{1,0}M)
. By orientability, there is a global smooth real vector field
T
such that
\theta(T)\equiv 1
. A vector field with this property will be said to be
\theta
-normalized
. If
T
is
\theta
-normalized, any other
\theta
-normalized vector field is obtained adding to
T
an appropriate smooth section of the maximal complex distribution
H(M)
. The choice of a
\theta
-normalized
T
yields a direct sum decomposition

\mathbb{C}TM=T^{1,0}M\oplus T^{0,1}M\oplus\mathbb{C}T.

We denote by
\pi_{1,0}
(resp.
\pi_{0,1}
) the projection onto the first (resp. second) factor. The projection onto the third factor is given by
X\mapsto\theta(X)T
, and
\pi_{1,0}(\overline{X})=\overline{\pi_{0,1}(X)}
.

The next proposition establishes the elementary theory of D’Angelo forms, collecting various known facts.

Proposition-Definition 4.1
.

Given a pseudo-Hermitian structure
\theta
and a
\theta
-normalized vector field
T
, we define, for a smooth complex vector field
X
,

(4.1)

\alpha_{\theta,T}(X):=\theta([T,X-\theta(X)T]).

The expression (
4.1
) is
C^{\infty}
-linear in
X
, and
\alpha_{\theta,T}(X)
is real if
X
is a real vector field. Hence, it defines a real one-form on
M
. Any such form
\alpha_{\theta,T}
will be called a
D’Angelo form
of
(M,T^{1,0}M)
. The following properties hold:

i)

\alpha_{\theta,T}(T)=0
. Thus, we may write
\alpha:=\alpha_{\theta,T}=\alpha_{1,0}+\alpha_{0,1}
, where
\alpha_{1,0}:=\alpha\circ\pi_{1,0}
,
\alpha_{0,1}:=\alpha\circ\pi_{0,1}
, and
\alpha_{0,1}=\overline{\alpha_{1,0}}
.

ii)

If
X
is a section of
T^{1,0}M\oplus T^{0,1}M
, then

\alpha_{\theta,T}(X)=-\mathcal{L}_{T}\theta(X),

where
\mathcal{L}
is the Lie derivative.

iii)

The restriction of
\alpha_{\theta,T}
to
\mathcal{N}\oplus\overline{\mathcal{N}}
is independent of the choice of
T
(
\mathcal{N}\oplus\overline{\mathcal{N}}
is the complex distribution whose fiber at
p
is
\mathcal{N}_{p}\oplus\overline{\mathcal{N}_{p}}
). Hence, we may safely denote it by
\alpha_{\theta}:\mathcal{N}\oplus\overline{\mathcal{N}}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
.

iv)

If
\theta^{\prime}=\pm e^{f}\theta
is another pseudo-Hermitian structure, where
f\in C^{\infty}(M,\mathbb{R})
, then

\alpha_{\theta^{\prime}}=\alpha_{\theta}+df\quad\text{on }\mathcal{N}\oplus\overline{\mathcal{N}}.

v)

Suppose that
(M,T^{1,0}M)
is pseudoconvex. If
X_{p},Y_{p}\in\mathcal{N}_{p}\oplus\overline{\mathcal{N}_{p}}
, then
d\alpha_{\theta,T}(X,Y)_{|p}=0
.

vi)

Let
X
be a complex manifold, and let
M\subset X
be a real hypersurface. Let
r\in\mathrm{Def}(M)
, and let
N
be a
(1,0)
-vector field defined in a neighborhood of
M
such that
Nr\equiv 1
. If
\theta:=d^{c}r
and
T:=\frac{1}{2i}(N-\overline{N})
, then

\alpha_{\theta,T}(Z)=\overline{\partial}r([\overline{N},Z])=2\partial\overline{\partial}r(Z,\overline{N})\qquad\forall Z\in T^{1,0}M.

In view of (
3.3
), the expressions above exhaust all possible restrictions of D’Angelo forms to the Levi-null distribution.

The set of all D’Angelo forms will be called the
D’Angelo class
of
(M,T^{1,0}M)
and denoted by
\mathcal{A}_{(M,T^{1,0}M)}
.

Proof.

i) The vanishing of
\alpha_{\theta,T}(T)
is trivial, and the rest follows from the reality of
\alpha
.

ii) By Leibnitz rule for the Lie derivative plus the identity
\mathcal{L}_{T}X=[T,X]
, we get

\mathcal{L}_{T}\theta(X)=T(\theta(X))-\theta([T,X]).

If
X
is a section of
T^{1,0}M\oplus T^{0,1}M
, then
\theta(X)\equiv 0
and the RHS equals
-\alpha_{\theta,T}(X)
. If
X=T
, then
\theta(X)\equiv 1
and the RHS vanishes. By i), we are done.

iii) If
T^{\prime}
is another
\theta
-normalized vector field and
X_{p}\in\mathcal{N}_{p}\oplus\overline{\mathcal{N}_{p}}
is extended to a section of
T^{1,0}M\oplus T^{0,1}M
, then

\alpha_{\theta,T^{\prime}}(X)=\alpha_{\theta,T}(X)+\theta([T^{\prime}-T,X]).

Since
T^{\prime}-T
is a section of
T^{1,0}M\oplus T^{0,1}M
, the second term vanishes at
p
.

iv) Choose a
\theta
-normalized vector field
T
. Then
T^{\prime}:=\pm e^{-f}T
is
\theta^{\prime}
-normalized. Extending
X_{p}\in\mathcal{N}_{p}\oplus\overline{\mathcal{N}_{p}}
to a section of
T^{1,0}M\oplus T^{0,1}M
as above, we have
\theta(X)=\theta^{\prime}(X)\equiv 0
and

\displaystyle\alpha_{\theta^{\prime},T^{\prime}}(X)

\displaystyle=\theta^{\prime}([T^{\prime},X])=\pm e^{f}\theta([\pm e^{-f}T,X])

\displaystyle=\pm e^{f}\theta(\pm e^{-f}[T,X]\mp X(e^{-f})T)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{\theta,T}(X)+Xf.

In view of iii), this is what we wanted.

v) Extend
X_{p},Y_{p}\in\mathcal{N}_{p}\oplus\overline{\mathcal{N}_{p}}
to sections of
T^{1,0}M\oplus T^{0,1}M
. Since exterior differentiation and Lie derivative commute, by ii) we get

\displaystyle d\alpha_{\theta,T}(X,Y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-(\mathcal{L}_{T}d\theta)(X,Y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-T\left(d\theta(X,Y)\right)+d\theta([T,X],Y)+d\theta(X,[T,Y])

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-T\left(d\theta(X,Y)\right)+d\theta([T,X],Y)-d\theta([T,Y],X).

Since
Y_{p}\in\mathcal{N}_{p}\oplus\overline{\mathcal{N}_{p}}
,
d\theta(Z,Y)_{|p}=0
for every section
Z
of
T^{1,0}M\oplus T^{0,1}M
. Hence,

d\theta([T,X],Y)_{|p}=\theta([T,X])_{|p}d\theta(T,Y)_{|p}=-\frac{1}{2}\theta([T,X])_{|p}\theta([T,Y])_{|p},

where we used Cartan’s identity. Thus,
d\theta([T,X],Y)_{|p}-d\theta([T,Y],X)_{|p}=0
.

To complete the proof, we need to show that
T\left(d\theta(X,Y)\right)_{|p}=0
. Since
d\theta(X,Y)=d\theta(\pi_{1,0}X,\pi_{0,1}Y)+d\theta(\pi_{0,1}X,\pi_{1,0}Y)
, we may assume for simplicity that
X
and
Y
are sections of
T^{1,0}M
such that
X_{p},Y_{p}\in\mathcal{N}_{p}
, and prove that
T\left(d\theta(X,\overline{Y})\right)_{|p}=0
. In fact, by the standard polarization identity, we may also assume that
X=Y
. By pseudoconvexity,
\frac{1}{2i}d\theta(X,\overline{X})=\lambda_{\theta}(X,\overline{X})
is nonnegative, and it vanishes at
p
. The conclusion follows immediately.

vi) Notice that the vector field
T
is real, tangent to
M
and satisfies
\theta(T)=1
, where
\theta=d^{c}r
. Since
[T,Z]
is also tangent to
M
,
dr([T,Z])=0
and

\displaystyle\alpha_{\theta,T}(Z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i(\partial r-\overline{\partial}r)([T,Z])

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2i\overline{\partial}r([T,Z])

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\overline{\partial}r([\overline{N},Z]),

because
[N,Z]
has type
(1,0)
. Cartan’s formula now yields

\displaystyle\overline{\partial}r([\overline{N},Z])

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2d\overline{\partial}r(\overline{N},Z)+\overline{N}(\overline{\partial}r(Z))-Z(\overline{\partial}r(\overline{N}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\partial\overline{\partial}r(\overline{N},Z)=2\partial\overline{\partial}r(Z,\overline{N}).

∎

Remark
.

On a Levi-flat CR manifold, the D’Angelo class gives a well-defined leafwise de Rham cohomology class on the Levi foliation; its interpretation in terms of the differential geometric properties of the foliation is discussed, for example, in
[
BDD07
]
at the end of Section 3.6.

In presence of a metric on
M
or at least on the transverse bundle of the foliation, the leafwise differential forms can be identified with
“global”
differential forms; for example, in
[
MT15
,
MT16
]
a condition involving the D’Angelo form in the Levi-flat case is needed in order to give an appropriate condition of positivity for a metric on the transverse bundle of the foliation.

Following the same lines of thought, one could also spot an instance of D’Angelo forms in the context of Levi foliations in the paper
[
FLT08
]
.

Assume now that
(M,T^{1,0}M)
is pseudoconvex. Parts iv) and v) of the Proposition-Definition lead one to think of the D’Angelo class as a sort of cohomology class, that happens to be well-defined only on the distribution
\mathcal{N}\oplus\overline{\mathcal{N}}
. In fact, if
\iota:V\hookrightarrow M
is an embedded submanifold such that
\mathbb{C}T_{p}V\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{p}\oplus\overline{\mathcal{N}}_{p}
for every
p\in M
, then the restricted D’Angelo form
\iota^{*}\alpha_{\theta,T}
is a real closed one-form on
V
independent of
T
, whose associated cohomology class
[\iota^{*}\alpha_{\theta,T}]\in H^{1}_{\mathrm{dR}}(V,\mathbb{R})
is independent of the choice of pseudo-Hermitian structure (cf.
[
BS93
]
). This happens in particular when
T_{p}V
is a complex (i.e.,
J_{b}
-invariant) subspace of
H_{p}(M)
for every
p\in M
: in this case
\mathbb{C}T_{p}V=\mathbb{C}T_{p}V\cap T^{1,0}M\oplus\mathbb{C}T_{p}V\cap T^{0,1}M\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{p}\oplus\overline{\mathcal{N}}_{p}
(cf. Proposition
3.2
). A standard application of the Newlander–Nirenberg theorem actually shows that the almost complex structure
(V,J_{b})
is integrable, i.e., it is induced by a structure of complex manifold on
V
.

Our next goal is to attach to any sub-distribution
\mathcal{D}
of the Levi distribution certain quantities
\mathfrak{n}_{K}(\mathcal{D})
, defined in terms of the D’Angelo class
\mathcal{A}_{(M,T^{1,0}M)}
and depending on a parameter
K\in(0,+\infty]
. If
\mathcal{D}
is the tangent distribution
\mathbb{C}TV
to a complex submanifold as in the preceding paragraph,
\mathfrak{n}_{\infty}(\mathcal{D})
coincides with a certain norm-like function
\mathfrak{n}_{V}:H^{1}_{\textrm{dR}}(V,\mathbb{R})\rightarrow[0,+\infty]
, evaluated at
[\iota^{*}\alpha_{\theta,T}]
. In the next section, we define
\mathfrak{n}_{V}
as a motivating preliminary to the definition of
\mathfrak{n}(\mathcal{D})
, which is given in Section
4.4
.

4.2.
A norm-like function on the first de Rham cohomology of a complex manifold

Let
X
be a complex manifold. All the one-forms are assumed to be smooth.

Proposition 4.2
.

If
\alpha
is a real closed one-form on
X
, then
\overline{\partial}\alpha_{1,0}
induces a Hermitian form on
T^{1,0}X
, that is,

\overline{\overline{\partial}\alpha_{1,0}(L,\overline{M})}=\overline{\partial}\alpha_{1,0}(M,\overline{L})\qquad\forall L,M\in T^{1,0}X.

Proof.

Since
\alpha
is real,
\alpha_{0,1}=\overline{\alpha_{1,0}}
. Since
d\alpha=0
,
\overline{\partial}\alpha_{1,0}=-\partial\alpha_{0,1}
. Thus,

\overline{\overline{\partial}\alpha_{1,0}(L,\overline{M})}=\partial\alpha_{0,1}(\overline{L},M)=-\overline{\partial}\alpha_{1,0}(\overline{L},M)=\overline{\partial}\alpha_{1,0}(M,\overline{L}).

∎

Definition 4.3
.

Let
X
be a complex manifold and
\alpha
a real closed one-form. Set

\mathfrak{n}_{X,0}(\alpha):=\inf\{\lambda\colon\ \lambda>0\quad\&\quad\alpha_{1,0}\wedge\alpha_{0,1}\leq\lambda\overline{\partial}\alpha_{1,0}\},

where the inequality is in the sense of Hermitian forms on
T^{1,0}X
and
\mathfrak{n}_{0}(\alpha)=+\infty
if the set above is empty. If
[\alpha]\in H^{1}_{\mathrm{dR}}(X,\mathbb{R})
, then we put

\displaystyle\mathfrak{n}_{X}([\alpha])

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\inf\{\mathfrak{n}_{X,0}(\beta)\colon\ [\alpha]=[\beta]\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf\{\mathfrak{n}_{X,0}(\alpha+df)\colon\ f\in C^{\infty}(X,\mathbb{R})\}.

Notice that the two functions
\mathfrak{n}_{0}
and
\mathfrak{n}
stand in the same relation as a norm on a vector space and the induced
“quotient norm”
on a quotient of that space. The next result shows that
\mathfrak{n}
satisfies weaker versions of the norm axioms.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
X
be a complex manifold and write
\mathfrak{n}=\mathfrak{n}_{X}
for simplicity. Then
\mathfrak{n}:H^{1}_{\textrm{dR}}(X,\mathbb{R})\rightarrow[0,+\infty]
satisfies the following properties:

i)

non-degeneracy:
\mathfrak{n}([\alpha])=0
if and only if
[\alpha]=0
.

ii)

homogeneity:
\mathfrak{n}(t[\alpha])=t\mathfrak{n}([\alpha])
for every
t\geq 0
and
[\alpha]\in H^{1}_{\textrm{dR}}(X,\mathbb{R})
.

iii)

quasi-triangle inequality:
\mathfrak{n}([\alpha]+[\beta])\leq 2\max\{\mathfrak{n}([\alpha]),\mathfrak{n}([\beta])\}
for every
[\alpha],[\beta]\in H^{1}_{\textrm{dR}}(X,\mathbb{R})
.

If
Y
is another complex manifold and
F:Y\rightarrow X
is a holomorphic mapping, then the map induced in cohomology
F^{*}:H^{1}_{\textrm{dR}}(X,\mathbb{R})\rightarrow H^{1}_{\textrm{dR}}(Y,\mathbb{R})
is
“norm”
-decreasing:

\mathfrak{n}_{Y}(F^{*}[\alpha])\leq\mathfrak{n}_{X}([\alpha])\qquad\forall[\alpha]\in H^{1}_{\textrm{dR}}(X,\mathbb{R}).

Proof.

Homogeneity is easily verified: if
t,\lambda>0
, then
\alpha_{1,0}\wedge\alpha_{0,1}\leq\lambda\overline{\partial}\alpha_{1,0}
if and only if
(t\alpha_{1,0})\wedge(t\alpha_{0,1})\leq\lambda t\overline{\partial}(t\alpha_{1,0})
, and therefore
\mathfrak{n}_{X,0}(t\alpha)=t\mathfrak{n}_{X,0}(\alpha)
, from which the homogeneity of
\mathfrak{n}
follows immediately. The case
t=0
is trivial (notice that we are using the convention that
0\cdot\infty=0
).

Let us now prove the quasi-triangle inequality. Since
\gamma_{1,0}\wedge\gamma_{0,1}\geq 0
for every real one-form
\gamma
, considering
\gamma=\alpha-\beta
we get

\alpha_{1,0}\wedge\beta_{0,1}+\beta_{1,0}\wedge\alpha_{0,1}\leq\alpha_{1,0}\wedge\alpha_{0,1}+\beta_{1,0}\wedge\beta_{0,1}.

Assume that
\alpha_{1,0}\wedge\alpha_{0,1}\leq\lambda\overline{\partial}\alpha_{1,0}
and
\beta_{1,0}\wedge\beta_{0,1}\leq\mu\overline{\partial}\beta_{1,0}
for some
\lambda,\mu>0
, which entails that
\overline{\partial}\alpha_{1,0}
and
\overline{\partial}\beta_{1,0}
are nonnegative. Then

\displaystyle(\alpha_{1,0}+\beta_{1,0})\wedge(\alpha_{0,1}+\beta_{0,1})\leq 2\alpha_{1,0}\wedge\alpha_{0,1}+2\beta_{1,0}\wedge\beta_{0,1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\lambda\overline{\partial}\alpha_{1,0}+2\mu\overline{\partial}\beta_{1,0}\leq 2\max\{\lambda,\mu\}\overline{\partial}(\alpha_{1,0}+\beta_{1,0}).

Taking the
\inf
over all allowable values of
\lambda
and
\mu
, we obtain
\mathfrak{n}_{X,0}(\alpha+\beta)\leq 2\max\{\mathfrak{n}_{X,0}(\alpha),\mathfrak{n}_{X,0}(\beta)\}
. Applying this inequality to
\alpha+df
and
\beta+dg
and taking one more
\inf
over all
f,g\in C^{\infty}(X,\mathbb{R})
, we obtain the desired quasi-triangle inequality.

The contraction property is a simple consequence of the facts that, since
F:Y\rightarrow X
is holomorphic, then pull-back along
F
preserves the decomposition into
(1,0)
and
(0,1)
parts of forms, the wedge operation, the nonnegativity of
(1,1)
-forms, and it commutes with the operator
\overline{\partial}
. From these properties one sees that, if
\alpha_{1,0}\wedge\alpha_{0,1}\leq\lambda\overline{\partial}\alpha_{1,0}
holds for
\lambda>0
and a real one-form
\alpha
on
X
, then
(F^{*}\alpha)_{1,0}\wedge(F^{*}\alpha)_{0,1}\leq\lambda\overline{\partial}(F^{*}\alpha)_{1,0}
also holds. The inequality
\mathfrak{n}_{Y,0}(F^{*}\alpha)\leq\mathfrak{n}_{X,0}(\alpha)
follows immediately, and this in turn gives the desired inequality.

We are left with the proof of non-degeneracy.
We first establish it on an annulus
X=\{z\in X\colon\ a<|z|<b\}
(
0<a<b
). The vanishing of
\mathfrak{n}([\alpha])
is equivalent to the existence of a sequence of smooth real one-forms
\alpha^{k}
such that
\alpha^{k}=\alpha+df^{k}
for smooth real-valued
f^{k}
and

\alpha_{1,0}^{k}\wedge\alpha_{0,1}^{k}\leq\frac{1}{k}\overline{\partial}\alpha_{1,0}^{k}.

Let
\chi
be a nonnegative test function identically equal to one on a smaller annulus
X^{\prime}:=\{a^{\prime}<|z|<b^{\prime}\}
, where
a<a^{\prime}<b^{\prime}<b
. Since
d\alpha^{k}=0
, we have the identities
\overline{\partial}\alpha_{1,0}^{k}=d\alpha_{1,0}^{k}=-d\alpha_{0,1}^{k}
. Observing that, if
\gamma
is a real one-form, then
\int_{X}i\gamma_{1,0}\wedge\gamma_{0,1}\geq 0
, an integration by parts yields

\displaystyle\int_{X}i\chi^{2}\alpha_{1,0}^{k}\wedge\alpha_{0,1}^{k}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k}\int_{X}i\chi^{2}\overline{\partial}\alpha_{1,0}^{k}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2k}\int_{X}i\chi^{2}d(\alpha_{1,0}^{k}-\alpha_{0,1}^{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k}\int_{X}i\left(\chi\alpha_{1,0}^{k}\wedge d\chi+d\chi\wedge\chi\alpha_{0,1}^{k}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k}\int_{X}i\left(\chi^{2}\alpha_{1,0}^{k}\wedge\alpha_{0,1}^{k}+\partial\chi\wedge\overline{\partial}\chi\right).

Hence,

\int_{X^{\prime}}i\alpha_{1,0}^{k}\wedge\alpha_{0,1}^{k}\leq\int_{X}\chi^{2}i\alpha_{1,0}^{k}\wedge\alpha_{0,1}^{k}\leq\frac{1}{k-1}\int_{X}i\partial\chi\wedge\overline{\partial}\chi.

Thus,
\alpha^{k}=\alpha+df^{k}
vanishes in
L^{2}(X^{\prime})
as
k
tends to
\infty
. In particular, the
df^{k}
are bounded in
L^{2}(X^{\prime})
uniformly in
k
and by Poincaré inequality, modulo adding an appropriate real constant to
f^{k}
, we may assume that the
f^{k}
are uniformly bounded in
L^{2}(X^{\prime})
. Passing to a subsequence, we may assume that
f^{k}
converges weakly, hence in the sense of distributions, to
f\in L^{2}(X^{\prime})
. This in turn implies that
\alpha+df^{k}
converges in the sense of currents (on
X^{\prime}
) to
\alpha+df
. Since we already proved that
\alpha+df^{k}
vanishes in
L^{2}(X^{\prime})
, we necessarily have
\alpha=-df
on
X^{\prime}
. Since
\alpha
is smooth,
f
must be smooth on
X^{\prime}
. Thus
\alpha
is exact on
X^{\prime}
, which in turn implies exactness of
\alpha
on the whole of
X
.

Let now
X
be arbitrary and
\mathfrak{n}([\alpha])=0
. We argue by contradiction, assuming that
[\alpha]\neq 0
. By the de Rham isomorphism theorem, there exists a smooth closed path
\gamma_{0}:\mathbb{S}^{1}\rightarrow X
such that
\int_{\gamma_{0}}\alpha\neq 0
. We now use the fact that any smooth mapping between real analytic manifolds can be uniformly approximated by a real analytic mapping (see
[
Shi64
]
, Section 1). In particular, there is a real analytic path
\gamma_{1}
homotopic to
\gamma_{0}
. Thus,
\int_{\gamma_{1}}\alpha\neq 0
. Identifying
\mathbb{S}^{1}
with the unit circle in the complex plane, we can analytically continue it to a neighborhood of
\mathbb{S}^{1}
, obtaining a holomorphic mapping
F:A:=\{1-\delta<|z|<1+\delta\}\rightarrow X
such that
F_{|\mathbb{S}^{1}}=\gamma_{1}
. This immediately implies that
\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}F^{*}\alpha\neq 0
, i.e.,
[F^{*}\alpha]\in H^{1}_{\mathrm{dR}}(A)
is nontrivial. By the first part of our proof
\mathfrak{n}_{A}([F^{*}\alpha])>0
, and by the contraction property of
\mathfrak{n}
along holomorphic mappings, we conclude that
\mathfrak{n}_{X}([\alpha])>0
, the sought-after contradiction. ∎

On complex manifolds with vanishing
H^{0,1}_{\overline{\partial}}X
, there is an alternative description of
\mathfrak{n}
in terms of pluriharmonic functions. This is based on known facts, which we rapidly recall. Denote by
\mathrm{PH}(X)
the set of smooth, real-valued and pluriharmonic functions on
X
, that is,

\mathrm{PH}(X):=\{h\in C^{\infty}(X,\mathbb{R})\colon dd^{c}h=0\},

where as usual
d^{c}=i(\overline{\partial}-\partial)
. We have the linear mapping

\displaystyle\mathrm{PH}(X)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow

\displaystyle H^{1}_{\mathrm{dR}}(X)

\displaystyle h

\displaystyle\longmapsto

\displaystyle[d^{c}h]

If
h=\Re(H)
, with
H
holomorphic, then

d^{c}h=i(\overline{\partial}-\partial)\left(\frac{H+\overline{H}}{2}\right)=\frac{1}{2i}(dH-d\overline{H})=d(\Im(H)),

and therefore
[d^{c}h]=0
and the mapping above descends to the quotient

(4.2)

\mathrm{PH}(X)/\Re(\mathcal{O}(X))\rightarrow H^{1}_{\mathrm{dR}}(X).

This mapping is always injective. In fact,
d^{c}h=df
, with
f
and
h
real-valued, happens if and only if
h+if
is holomorphic, that is, only if
h\in\Re(\mathcal{O}(X))
.

Lemma 4.5
.

Assume that
H^{0,1}_{\overline{\partial}}X=0
. Then the mapping (
4.2
) is an isomorphism.

Proof.

Let
\alpha
be a real closed one-form. Since
\overline{\partial}\alpha_{0,1}=0
and
H^{0,1}_{\overline{\partial}}X=0
, there exists a smooth complex valued function
g
such that
\overline{\partial}g=\alpha_{0,1}
. Since
\alpha
is real, this implies
\alpha_{1,0}=\partial\overline{g}
and we may write

\alpha=\partial\overline{g}+\overline{\partial}g=d^{c}(\Im(g))+d\Re(g).

Since
\partial\alpha_{1,0}=0
,
\partial\overline{\partial}g=0
and
\partial\overline{\partial}\overline{g}=0
. In particular,
\Im(g)\in\mathrm{PH}(X)
and the mapping (
4.2
) is surjective.
∎

In particular, any class
[\alpha]
in
H^{1}_{\mathrm{dR}}(X)
is represented by
d^{c}h
for some
h\in\mathrm{PH}(X)
and

(4.3)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{n}([\alpha])

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf\{\lambda\geq 0\colon\exists f\in C^{\infty}(X,\mathbb{R})\text{ s.t. }\partial(f+ih)\wedge\overline{\partial}(f-ih)\leq\lambda\overline{\partial}\partial f\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf\{\lambda\geq 0\colon\exists f\in C^{\infty}(X,\mathbb{R})\text{ s.t. }\partial(h+if)\wedge\overline{\partial}(h-if)\leq\lambda\partial\overline{\partial}f\}

4.3.
Remarks on the Cauchy–Riemann complex on abstract CR manifolds

To define the quantities
\mathfrak{n}_{K}(\mathcal{D})
on an abstract CR manifold of hypersurface type, we need to consider the Cauchy-Riemann complex on an abstract CR manifold; to keep the exposition self-contained and to avoid a too long detour, we will only recall a few notions and refer the reader to
[
Bog17
, Chapter 8]
, or
[
DT06
, Section 1.7]
for a metric-free approach.

We consider a CR manifold
(M,T^{0,1}M)
of hypersurface type and endow
\mathbb{C}TM
with a Hermitian metric
h
such that
T^{0,1}M\perp T^{1,0}M
; we denote by
E
the orthogonal complement of
T^{0,1}M\oplus T^{1,0}M
. The space of
(p,q)
-forms on
M
is the space of sections of the bundle

\Lambda^{p,q}T^{*}M=\Lambda^{p}(T^{1,0}M\oplus E)^{*}\widehat{{\otimes}}\,\Lambda^{q}(T^{0,1}M)^{*}\;.

In particular,
(1,0)
-forms are sections of
(T^{1,0}M\oplus E)^{*}
and
(0,1)
-forms are sections of
(T^{0,1}M)^{*}
.

The metric
h
gives an orthogonal decomposition

\Lambda^{r}T^{*}M=\Lambda^{0,r}T^{*}M\oplus\cdots\oplus\Lambda^{r,0}T^{*}M\;.

We denote by
\mathcal{E}^{p,q}_{M}(U)
the space of sections of
\Lambda^{p,q}T^{*}M
on
U
; we will omit the open set
U
, when it is unimportant.

Remark
.

In general, given a
1
-form
\alpha
on
M
, we have that

\alpha=\alpha_{1,0}+\alpha_{0,1}

however, if
\alpha
is real, we cannot say that
\alpha_{1,0}=\overline{\alpha_{0,1}}
. This is only true when we evaluate the two forms on a
(1,0)
vector-field, i.e. given
X\in\Gamma(M,T^{1,0}M)
,

\alpha_{1,0}(X)=\overline{\alpha_{0,1}}(X)\;.

It is true that
\overline{\alpha_{0,1}}\in\mathcal{E}^{0,1}_{M}
.

The operator

\overline{\partial}:\mathcal{E}^{p,q}_{M}\to\mathcal{E}^{p,q+1}_{M}

is defined as
\overline{\partial}=\pi_{p,q+1}\circ d
, where
\pi_{p,q+1}
is the orthogonal projection from
\Lambda^{p+q+1}T^{*}M
to
\Lambda^{p,q+1}T^{*}M
.

Remark
.

Given
\alpha
a
1
-form on
M
and
X,Y
sections of
T^{1,0}M
, then

\overline{\partial}\alpha_{1,0}(X,\overline{Y})=d\alpha_{1,0}(X,\overline{Y})\;.

Therefore, by Cartan’s identity

2\overline{\partial}\alpha_{1,0}(X,\overline{Y})=-\overline{Y}(\alpha_{1,0}(X))-\alpha_{1,0}([X,\overline{Y}])\;.

Due to the definition of
(1,0)
-forms, in general
\alpha_{1,0}([X,\overline{Y}])\neq\alpha([X,\overline{Y}]_{1,0})
; however, if
X_{p},Y_{p}\in\mathcal{N}_{p}
, then
[X,\overline{Y}]_{p}\in T^{1,0}M\oplus T^{0,1}M
, so

\alpha_{1,0}([X,\overline{Y}])_{p}=\alpha([X,\overline{Y}]_{1,0})_{p}\;.

In view of
[
Bog17
, Section 8.3, Theorem I]
, given an embedding
j:M\to X
of
M
as a CR hypersurface in a complex manifold
X
, the bundles
\Lambda^{p,q}T^{*}M
are isomorphic to certain subbundles of
\Lambda^{p+q}T^{*}X
via
j^{*}
(described in
[
Bog17
, Section 8.1]
), which we will denote by
\Lambda^{p,q}T^{*}j(M)
.

We note that, given a section
\alpha
of
\Lambda^{1,0}T^{*}j(M)\oplus\Lambda^{0,1}T^{*}j(M)
and given any extension
\tilde{\alpha}
to a neighbourhood of
j(M)
in
X
,
\tilde{\alpha}_{0,1}|_{T^{0,1}j(M)}=\alpha_{0,1}
, where in the left hand side we took the
(0,1)
component with respect to the Dolbeault splitting of
T^{*}X
.

We define a
\overline{\partial}_{j(M)}
on the embedded hypersuface via the relation
j^{*}\overline{\partial}_{J(M)}=\overline{\partial}j^{*}
. In particular, given
\alpha
a section of
(T^{1,0}X)^{*}|_{j(M)}
such that
j^{*}\alpha\in\Lambda^{1,0}T^{*}M
and
X,Y
sections of
T^{1,0}M
, then

\overline{\partial}(j^{*}\alpha)(X,\overline{Y})=\overline{\partial}r\wedge\overline{\partial}\tilde{\alpha}(j_{*}X,j_{*}\overline{Y},\overline{N})

where
r
is a defining function for
j(M)
in
X
,
N
is a
(1,0)
vector-field such that
Nr\equiv 1
and
\tilde{\alpha}
is an extension of
\alpha
to a neighborhood of
j(M)
.

We recall the following elementary variation on Cartan’s identity (see also
[
Yum21
, Lemma 4.4]
).

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
\alpha
be a real one-form, and let
Z
and
W
be vector fields of type
(1,0)
. We have the identities

\displaystyle 2\partial\alpha(Z,\overline{W})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Z\left(\alpha(\overline{W})\right)-\alpha([Z,\overline{W}]_{0,1}),

\displaystyle 2\overline{\partial}\alpha(Z,\overline{W})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\overline{W}\left(\alpha(Z)\right)-\alpha([Z,\overline{W}]_{1,0}).

By the definition of wedge product and Cartan’s identity,

2\overline{\partial}r\wedge\overline{\partial}\tilde{\alpha}(j_{*}X,j_{*}\overline{Y},\overline{N})=2\overline{\partial}\tilde{\alpha}(j_{*}X,j_{*}\overline{Y})=-(j_{*}\overline{Y})(\tilde{\alpha}(j_{*}X))-\tilde{\alpha}([j_{*}X,j_{*}\overline{Y}]_{0,1})

where
[j_{*}X,j_{*}\overline{Y}]_{0,1}
denotes the projection on
T^{0,1}X
.

If
(j_{*}X)_{x},(j_{*}Y)_{x}\in\mathcal{N}_{x}
(i.e. if
X_{p},Y_{p}\in\mathcal{N}_{p}
with
x=j(p)
), then the projection on
T^{0,1}X
is the same as the projection on
T^{0,1}j(M)
.

In conclusion, if
X,Y
are sections of
T^{1,0}M
such that
X_{p},Y_{p}
belong to
\mathcal{N}_{p}
, we have a CR embedding
j:M\to X
and
\alpha
is a section of
(T^{1,0}X)^{*}|_{j(M)}
such that
j^{*}\alpha\in\Lambda^{1,0}T^{*}M
, then the three expressions

\begin{array}[]{cl}\overline{\partial}j^{*}\alpha(X,\overline{Y})_{p}&\textrm{(intrinsic $\overline{\partial}$ on $M$)}\\
(\overline{\partial}r\wedge\overline{\partial}\tilde{\alpha})(j_{*}X,j_{*}\overline{Y},\overline{N})_{p}&\textrm{(extrinsic $\overline{\partial}$ on $j(M)$)}\\
\overline{\partial}\tilde{\alpha}(j_{*}X,j_{*}\overline{Y})_{p}&\textrm{(restriction of the $\overline{\partial}$ from $X$)}\end{array}

all coincide, where
\tilde{\alpha}
,
N
,
r
are as above.

In what follows, given
\alpha
a
1
-form on
(M,T^{1,0}M)
we will denote by
\overline{\partial}\alpha
the
(1,1)
-form
\overline{\partial}\alpha_{1,0}
, where
\alpha_{1,0}
is the
(1,0)
-component of
\alpha
; we will be mainly (almost exclusively) interested in the Hermitian form induced by
\overline{\partial}\alpha
on
\mathcal{N}
, where all the possible interpretations of the symbol
\overline{\partial}
coincide.

Likewise, given
f\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(M)
, we will denote by
\partial\overline{\partial}f
the
(1,1)
-form
-\overline{\partial}(df)_{1,0}
, which again defines a Hermitian form on
\mathcal{N}
.

As pointed out before, in the embedded case, we can compute the Hermitian form
\overline{\partial}\alpha
on
\mathcal{N}
by extending
\alpha
to the ambient complex manifold and then taking the
\overline{\partial}
in the classical sense.

4.4.
Definition of
\mathfrak{n}_{K}(\mathcal{D})

We now possess all the necessary tools to define the quantities
\mathfrak{n}_{K}(\mathcal{D})
in full generality; we start by studying the Hermitian forms
\partial\alpha
, where
\alpha
is a D’Angelo form. Let
(M,T^{1,0}M)
be a pseudoconvex CR manifold of hypersurface type.

Lemma 4.7
.

[cf.
[
AY21
, Lemma 2.6 and Lemma 2.9]
]
Let
\alpha:=\alpha_{\theta,T}\in\mathcal{A}_{M,T^{1,0}M}
be a D’Angelo form. Then the Hermitian form
\overline{\partial}\alpha
on
\mathcal{N}
is independent of the choice of
T
. If
\theta,\theta^{\prime}=e^{f}\theta\in\Theta_{+}(M)
, where
f\in C^{\infty}(M)
, and
\alpha
,
\alpha^{\prime}
are the associated D’Angelo forms, then we have

(4.4)

\overline{\partial}\alpha^{\prime}(Z,\overline{Z})_{|p}=\overline{\partial}\alpha(Z,\overline{Z})_{|p}-\partial\overline{\partial}f(Z,\overline{Z})_{|p}\qquad\forall Z\textrm{ s.t. }Z_{p}\in\mathcal{N}_{p}.

Proof.

Extend
Z_{p}\in\mathcal{N}_{p}
to a smooth section of
T^{1,0}M
, as usual. Since
\alpha(Z)=\theta([T,Z])
, independence of the choice of
T
boils down to checking that

\overline{W}\left(\theta([X,Z])\right)_{|p}+\theta([X,[Z,\overline{W}]])_{|p}=0

for every
X
such that
\theta(X)\equiv 0
. By Cartan’s formula and Jacobi identity,

\displaystyle\overline{W}\left(\theta([X,Z])\right)+\theta([X,[Z,\overline{W}]])

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2d\theta(\overline{W},[X,Z])+[X,Z]\left(\theta(\overline{W})\right)+\theta([\overline{W},[X,Z]])+\theta([X,[Z,\overline{W}]])

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2d\theta(\overline{W},[X,Z])-\theta([Z,[\overline{W},X]]).

Since
Z_{p}\in\mathcal{N}_{p}
,
[X,Z]_{p}\in\mathbb{C}H_{p}(M)
, and therefore
d\theta(\overline{W},[X,Z])_{|p}=0
, because
W_{p}\in\mathcal{N}_{p}
. Analogously,
[\overline{W},X]_{p}\in\mathbb{C}H_{p}(M)
and therefore
\theta([Z,[\overline{W},X]])_{|p}=0
too.

We are left with the proof of (
4.4
). By part iv) of Definition-Proposition
4.1
,

\alpha^{\prime}=\alpha+df

on sections of
\mathbb{C}H(M)
, from which we immediately get (
4.4
).
∎

To proceed, we need an analogue of the concept of comass for a
1
-form acting on a distribution
\mathcal{D}
.

Definition 4.8

(
(h,\mathcal{D})
-size)
.

Let
M
be a real smooth manifold. Fix a Hermitian metric
h
on
\mathbb{C}TM
and a complex distribution
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathbb{C}TM
. If
\alpha
is a one-form on
M
, we define the
(h,\mathcal{D})
-size of
\alpha
as

||\alpha||_{h,\mathcal{D}}:=\sup\{|\alpha(Z_{p})|\colon\ p\in M,\ Z_{p}\in\mathcal{D}_{p}\text{ s.t. }|Z_{p}|=1\},

where
|Z_{p}|^{2}=h_{p}(Z_{p},Z_{p})
.

Remark
.

If
h
and
h^{\prime}
are two comparable Hermitian metrics on
\mathbb{C}TM
, i.e., if there is a positive constant
C
such that

C^{-1}h_{p}(Z_{p},Z_{p})\leq h^{\prime}_{p}(Z_{p},Z_{p})\leq Ch_{p}(Z_{p},Z_{p})\qquad\forall p\in M,\quad\forall_{p}\in\mathbb{C}T_{p}M,

then the two corresponding notions of size given by Definition
4.8
are comparable:

C^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|\alpha\|_{h,\mathcal{N}}\leq\|\alpha\|_{h^{\prime},\mathcal{N}}\leq C^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\alpha\|_{h,\mathcal{N}}\;.

Notice that, when
M
is compact, all the Hermitian metrics on
\mathbb{C}TM
are comparable.

The next key definition provides a way to measure the size of the D’Angelo class on an arbitrary sub-distribution of the Levi null distribution.

Definition 4.9
.

Let
(M,T^{1,0}M)
be a pseudoconvex CR manifold of hypersurface type. Fix a Hermitian metric
h
on
\mathbb{C}TM
.

If
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{M}
is a D’Angelo form and
\mathcal{D}
is a sub-distribution of the Levi-null distribution
\mathcal{N}
, we put

\mathfrak{n}(\alpha;\mathcal{D}):=\inf\left\{t>0\colon\ \left(\overline{\alpha_{0,1}}\wedge\alpha_{0,1}\right)<t\overline{\partial}\alpha\text{ on }\mathcal{D}\right\}.

If
K\in(0,+\infty]
, then we define

\mathfrak{n}_{K}(\mathcal{D}):=\inf\left\{\mathfrak{n}(\alpha;\mathcal{D})\colon\ \alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{M}\quad\&\quad||\alpha||_{h,\mathcal{N}}<K\right\}.

We write
\mathfrak{n}(\mathcal{D}):=\mathfrak{n}_{+\infty}(\mathcal{D})=\inf\left\{\mathfrak{n}(\alpha;\mathcal{D})\colon\ \alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{M}\right\}
.

Notice that, if
M
is compact, then

(4.5)

\mathfrak{n}(\mathcal{D})=\inf_{K}\mathfrak{n}_{K}(\mathcal{D}),

for any sub-distribution
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathcal{N}
. This is a simple consequence of the fact that, when
M
is compact, any D’Angelo form has finite
(h,\mathcal{N})
-size.

To appreciate the meaning of Definition
4.9
, the reader may observe that the vanishing of
\mathfrak{n}(\mathcal{D})
is equivalent to the existence of a sequence of D’Angelo forms
\alpha^{(m)}
with the property that the inequality of Hermitian forms
\left(\overline{\alpha^{(m)}_{0,1}}\wedge\alpha^{(m)}_{0,1}\right)<m^{-1}\overline{\partial}\alpha^{(m)}
holds on
\mathcal{D}
, while the vanishing of
\mathfrak{n}_{K}(\mathcal{D})
for some
K<+\infty
amounts to the existence of a sequence
\alpha^{(m)}
as above, with the additional property that the
(h,\mathcal{N})
-size
||\alpha^{(m)}||_{h,\mathcal{N}}
remains bounded as
m
goes to
+\infty
. In view of the Remark above, the existence of a
K<+\infty
such that
\mathfrak{n}_{K}(\mathcal{D})=0
is independent of the choice of the metric
h
.

Proposition 4.10
.

Let
(M,T^{1,0}M)
be a pseudoconvex CR manifold of hyperstype and
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{(M,T^{1,0}M)}
be a D’Angelo form on
M
. Suppose that
M
contains a complex manifold
V
(i.e. containing a differentiable manifold
V
whose tangent space is a complex subbundle of
H(M)
) such that
[\alpha|_{V}]\in H^{1}_{\mathrm{dR}}(V)
is non trivial.

Then
\mathfrak{n}(\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{N}))>0
(hence obviously
\mathfrak{n}_{K}(\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{N}))>0
for all
K>0
).

Proof.

As a first remark, one should notice that
\mathbb{C}TV\cap T^{1,0}M\subseteq\mathcal{N}
and, in fact, as
\mathbb{C}TV\cap T^{1,0}M
is perfect,
\mathbb{C}TV\cap T^{1,0}M\subseteq\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{N})
, by Lemma
3.2
.

By Theorem
4.4
-i), as
[\alpha|_{V}]
is non trivial in
H^{1}_{\mathrm{dR}}(V)
,
\mathfrak{n}_{V}([\alpha|_{V}])>0
. Unravelling the definitions, this is equivalent to

\inf\{t>0\ :\ (\overline{\alpha_{0,1}}\wedge\alpha_{0,1})<t\overline{\partial}\alpha\ \mathrm{on}\ \mathbb{C}TV\ \mathrm{with}\ \alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{M}\}>0\;.

As it is obvious, if we take the infimum on a larger distribution (namely
\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{N})
), it will at most increase, thus showing that
\mathfrak{n}(\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{N}))>0
.
∎

In general, if
\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{D}
are distributions, then
\mathfrak{n}_{K}(\mathcal{E})\leq\mathfrak{n}_{K}(\mathcal{D})
for all
K\in(0,+\infty]
.

Remark
.

If
M
is a type III CR structure, as described in Section
3.2
, with local coordinates
t,z
, a D’Angelo form is given by

\alpha=\Re(g^{\prime}\omega)=\Re(ie^{it}u(z)dz)=\Im(e^{it}u(z)dz)\;.

As observed in Section
3.2
, type III CR structures contain a complex submanifold given by a section
s:Y\to M
of the projection
M\to Y
; if we write
u(z)=f(z)+h_{z}e^{-ih}
, then the image of such section is described by the equation
t=h(z)
and, on it, the D’Angelo form given before becomes

\alpha|_{s(Y)}=\Im(f(z)e^{ih}dz)+d^{c}h\;.

In the embedded realization given at the end of Section
3.3
, we consider only an open set of the compact Riemann surface
Y
, where we can suppose that
f(z)\equiv 0
, so that
\alpha=d^{c}h
; we see that, according to Proposition
4.10
,
\mathfrak{n}(\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{N}))
will be positive as soon as
d^{c}h
does not give a trivial cohomology class, which is the case, for example, in the Worm domain.

Proposition 4.11
.

Assume that
(M,T^{1,0}M)
is a compact pseudoconvex real hypersurface in a complex manifold
X
, and suppose that there exists a smooth strictly plurisubharmonic function in a neighborhood of
M
in
X
. Then the quantity
\mathfrak{n}(\mathcal{D})
is finite for every distribution
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathcal{N}
, and there exists
K<+\infty
such that
\mathfrak{n}_{K}(\mathcal{D})
is also finite. Moreover, if

\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}(\alpha;\mathcal{D}):=\inf\left\{t>0\colon\ \left(\overline{\alpha_{0,1}}\wedge\alpha_{0,1}\right)\leq t\overline{\partial}\alpha\text{ on }\mathcal{D}\right\},

then we have

\mathfrak{n}(\mathcal{D})=\inf\left\{\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}(\alpha;\mathcal{D})\colon\ \alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{M}\right\}

and

\mathfrak{n}_{K}(\mathcal{D})=\inf\left\{\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}(\alpha;\mathcal{D})\colon\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{M}\quad\&\quad||\alpha||_{h,\mathcal{N}}<K\right\}.

Proof.

Let
\alpha
be any D’Angelo form and
f
a smooth strictly plurisubharmonic function defined in a neighborhood of
M
. Then
\beta=\alpha-adf
is a D’Angelo form for every
a>0
(by Proposition
4.1
, part iv)) and the associated Hermitian form is
\overline{\partial}\beta=\overline{\partial}\alpha+a\partial\overline{\partial}f
, by Proposition
4.7
. If
a
is large enough, this form is positive, and the inequality of quadratic forms

\left(\overline{\beta_{0,1}}\wedge\beta_{0,1}\right)<t\overline{\partial}\beta

holds on
\mathcal{N}
for
t
large enough, showing that
\mathfrak{n}(\beta;\mathcal{N})
is finite. The statement is an immediate consequence of this fact.
∎

The hypotheses of the last proposition hold, for instance, when
M
is the boundary of a precompact, smoothly bounded, pseudoconvex domain in a Stein manifold.

5.
Reduction to the core

We can finally state and prove the main result of the paper.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
(M,T^{1,0}M)
be a compact pseudoconvex CR manifold of hypersurface type. For every
K\in(0,+\infty]
, we have

\mathfrak{n}_{K}(\mathcal{N})=\mathfrak{n}_{K}(\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{N})).

Proof.

By (
4.5
), it is enough to consider the case of
K
finite. We argue by transfinite induction. We need to show two facts:

(A)

If
\mathcal{D}
is a sub-distribution of the Levi-null distribution, then
\mathfrak{n}_{K}(\mathcal{D})=\mathfrak{n}_{K}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})
.

(B)

If
\lambda_{1}
is an ordinal and
\{\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda<\lambda_{1}}
is a decreasing sequence of sub-distributions of the Levi-null distribution, then
\inf_{\lambda<\lambda_{1}}\mathfrak{n}_{K}(\mathcal{D}_{\lambda})=\mathfrak{n}_{K}(\bigcap_{\lambda}\mathcal{D}_{\lambda})
.

Let us prove (A). Notice first that
\mathfrak{n}_{K}(\mathcal{D})\geq\mathfrak{n}_{K}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})
is trivial. To prove the reverse inequality, fix
t>\mathfrak{n}_{K}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})
. Then there exists a D’Angelo form
\alpha
such that
\mathfrak{n}(\alpha;\mathcal{D}^{\prime})<t
and
||\alpha||_{\mathcal{N}}<K
. If we show that there exists another one
\gamma
such that
\mathfrak{n}(\gamma;\mathcal{D})<t
and
||\gamma||_{\mathcal{N}}<K
, then the thesis will follow.

Let
\beta
be an arbitrary D’Angelo form. Consider the set

V(\beta):=\left\{L\in\mathbb{C}TM\colon\left(\overline{\beta_{0,1}}\wedge\beta_{0,1}\right)(L,\overline{L})<t\overline{\partial}\beta(L,\overline{L})\right\}.

The set
V(\beta)
is clearly open and conical in
T^{1,0}M
.

Recall that, by part iv) of Proposition
4.1
,
\alpha+df\in\mathcal{A}_{M}
for every
f\in C^{\infty}(M,\mathbb{R})
. Denote by
J
the ideal of smooth real-valued functions vanishing on the support of
\mathcal{D}
. We claim that

(5.1)

\bigcup_{g\in J}V(\alpha-d(g^{2}))\supseteq\mathcal{D}\setminus 0.

Let us prove (
5.1
). By assumption,
\mathfrak{n}(\alpha;\mathcal{D}^{\prime})<t
, that is,
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\setminus 0\subseteq V(\alpha)
, so our task reduces to proving that for every
p\in S_{\mathcal{D}}
and
L_{p}\in\mathcal{D}_{p}\setminus\mathbb{C}T_{p}S_{\mathcal{D}}
,
L_{p}\in V(\alpha-d(g^{2}))
for an appropriate choice of
g\in J
.

If
L_{p}\notin\mathbb{C}T_{p}S_{\mathcal{D}}
, there exists
g\in J
such that
L_{p}g\neq 0
. Let
\beta=\alpha-d((cg)^{2})
, where
c\in\mathbb{R}
. Notice that
d(g^{2})=0
at points of
S_{\mathcal{D}}
. Lemma
4.7
gives

(5.2)

\displaystyle t\overline{\partial}\beta(L_{p},\overline{L_{p}})-\left(\overline{\beta_{0,1}}\wedge\beta_{0,1}\right)(L_{p},\overline{L_{p}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle tc^{2}\overline{L}L(g^{2})(p)+t\overline{\partial}\alpha(L_{p},\overline{L_{p}})-\left(\overline{\alpha_{0,1}}\wedge\alpha_{0,1}\right)(L_{p},\overline{L_{p}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2tc^{2}|L_{p}g|^{2}+t\overline{\partial}\alpha(L_{p},\overline{L_{p}})-\left(\overline{\alpha_{0,1}}\wedge\alpha_{0,1}\right)(L_{p},\overline{L_{p}}).

Choosing
c
large, we may guarantee that (
5.2
) is positive, that is,
L_{p}\in V(\alpha-d((cg)^{2}))
, as we wanted. The proof of (
5.1
) is complete.

Now, by Proposition
2.13
there exists
g_{1},\dots,g_{N}
in the ideal
J
such that

(5.3)

\bigcup_{j=1}^{N}V(\alpha-d(g_{j}^{2}))\supseteq\mathcal{D}\setminus 0.

Define
\widetilde{\gamma}:=\alpha-d(\sum_{j=1}^{N}g_{j}^{2})
, which is clearly a D’Angelo form. A computation analogous to the one giving (
5.2
) yields

\displaystyle t\overline{\partial}\widetilde{\gamma}(L_{p},\overline{L_{p}})-\left(\overline{\widetilde{\gamma}_{0,1}}\wedge\widetilde{\gamma}_{0,1}\right)(L_{p},\overline{L_{p}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t\sum_{j=1}^{N}|L_{p}g_{j}|^{2}+t\overline{\partial}\alpha(L_{p},\overline{L_{p}})-\left(\overline{\alpha_{0,1}}\wedge\alpha_{0,1}\right)(L_{p},\overline{L_{p}}),

where
L_{p}\in\mathcal{N}_{p}
. By (
5.3
) and (
5.2
), we see immediately that the form above is positive on
\mathcal{D}
. This proves the desired bound
\mathfrak{n}(\widetilde{\gamma};\mathcal{D})<t
at the cost of an (unquantified) increase in size of the form. This is a drawback of the compactness argument employed, to which we now remedy.

Given
\varepsilon>0
, let
\chi_{\varepsilon}
be a smooth function on
M
with the following properties:

(1)

\chi_{\varepsilon}
is supported on an
\varepsilon
-neighborhood
S_{\varepsilon}
of
S_{\mathcal{D}}
;

(2)

0\leq\chi_{\varepsilon}\leq 1
and
\chi_{\varepsilon}
is identically equal to
1
in an open neighborhood of
S_{\mathcal{D}}
;

(3)

||d\chi_{\varepsilon}||_{\infty}\leq C\varepsilon^{-1}
, where
C
is uniform in
\varepsilon>0
.

The emended form is
\gamma:=\alpha-d(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\chi_{\varepsilon}^{2}g_{j}^{2})
for
\varepsilon
small enough. In fact, we have
\mathfrak{n}(\gamma;\mathcal{D})=\mathfrak{n}(\widetilde{\gamma};\mathcal{D})<t
, as a consequence of the second property above, while by the two other properties,

\displaystyle\left\|d(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\chi_{\varepsilon}^{2}g_{j}^{2})\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left\|\chi_{\varepsilon}g_{j}^{2}d\chi_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{\infty}+2\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left\|\chi_{\varepsilon}^{2}g_{j}dg_{j}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2C\varepsilon^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sup_{S_{\varepsilon}}|g_{j}|^{2}+2\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left\|dg_{j}\right\|_{\infty}\sup_{S_{\varepsilon}}|g_{j}|.

Since
g_{j}
vanishes on
S_{\mathcal{D}}
,
\sup_{S_{\varepsilon}}|g_{j}|\leq C_{j}\varepsilon
and one concludes that

\left\|\gamma\right\|_{\mathcal{N}}\leq\left\|\alpha\right\|_{\mathcal{N}}+\left\|d(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\chi_{\varepsilon}^{2}g_{j}^{2})\right\|_{\infty}\leq\left\|\alpha\right\|_{\mathcal{N}}+C^{\prime}\varepsilon,

which is the desired bound on the
\mathcal{N}
-size of
\gamma
, for
\varepsilon
small enough. This completes the proof of (A).

The proof of (B) is simpler. If
\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}=0
for some
\lambda<\lambda_{1}
, both sides of the identity equal
1
and the statement is trivial. We can therefore assume that all the distributions
\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}
are nontrivial.

Fix
t>\mathfrak{n}_{K}(\bigcap_{\lambda}\mathcal{D}_{\lambda})
. By definition, there exists a D’Angelo form
\alpha
of
\mathcal{N}
-size
<K
such that

\left(\overline{\alpha_{0,1}}\wedge\alpha_{0,1}\right)<t\overline{\partial}\alpha

as quadratic forms on
\bigcap_{\lambda}\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}
. Define
V(\alpha)
as above. Since
\bigcap_{\lambda}\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}
is contained in
\Omega
, the complements
\mathbb{C}TM\setminus\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}
, together with
\Omega
, form an open conical cover of
\mathbb{C}TM
. By Proposition
2.13
and the fact that the
\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}
are nested, it follows that there exists
\lambda_{0}
such that
\mathcal{D}_{\lambda_{0}}\subseteq\Omega
. This means that
\mathfrak{n}(\alpha;\mathcal{D}_{\lambda_{0}})\leq t
and therefore
\mathfrak{n}_{K}(\mathcal{D}_{\lambda_{0}})\leq t
. By the arbitrariness of
t>\mathfrak{n}_{K}(\bigcap_{\lambda}\mathcal{D}_{\lambda})
, we conclude that
\inf_{\lambda<\lambda_{1}}\mathfrak{n}_{K}(\mathcal{D}_{\lambda})\leq\mathfrak{n}_{K}(\bigcap_{\lambda}\mathcal{D}_{\lambda})
. Since the reverse inequality is trivial, the conclusion follows.
∎

6.
Regularized Diederich–Fornæss index

As anticipated in the introduction, the
“reduction to the core”
theorem allows to prove the exact regularity of the
\overline{\partial}
-Neumann problem on smooth bounded pseudoconvex domains
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
with trivial Levi core. For this, we need two ingredients:

(1)

Harrington’s generalization of a theorem of Kohn, giving a sufficient condition for exact regularity of the
\overline{\partial}
–Neumann problem;

(2)

a slight generalization of Adachi–Yum theorem (that is, Theorem
1.2
of the introduction), involving a
“regularized”
version of the classical Diederich–Fornæss index, which we introduce below.

Let us begin with the following pseudodistance on the space of defining functions.

Definition 6.1

(cf.
[
Har11
]
, Definition 6.1)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
be a smooth bounded pseudoconvex domain. Given
r_{1},r_{2}\in\mathrm{Def}(\Omega)
, let
f
be the smooth function, defined on the common domain of
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
, such that
r_{2}=e^{f}r_{1}
. We define

(6.1)

\sigma(r_{1},r_{2}):=\sup_{p\in M}\sup_{Z_{p}\in\mathcal{N}_{p}\colon|Z_{p}|=1}|Z_{p}f|,

where
|Z_{p}|
denotes the ordinary Euclidean norm of
Z_{p}
.

The pseudodistance
\sigma
has an easy relation with the
(h,\mathcal{D})
-size introduced above.

Proposition 6.2
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
be a smooth bounded pseudoconvex domain. If
\alpha_{j}
(
j=1,2
) is a D’Angelo form associated to
r_{j}\in\mathrm{Def}(b\Omega)
as in Proposition
4.1
, vi), then
\sigma(r_{1},r_{2})=||\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1}||_{\mathcal{N}}
, where
||\cdot||_{\mathcal{N}}
is the size of Definition
4.8
(the unspecified metric is the Euclidean one).

Proof.

Recall that
\alpha_{j}
is not uniquely defined, but its restriction to the Levi-null distribution is unique (by Proposition
4.1
, iii)). If
r_{2}=e^{f}r_{1}
, then
d^{c}r_{2}=e^{f}d^{c}r_{1}
on
M=b\Omega
. The conclusion follows from Proposition
4.1
, iv), and Definition
4.8
.
∎

The exact regularity theorem alluded to above is as follows.

Theorem 6.3

(combination of
[
Har11
]
, Theorem 5.1 and Theorem 6.2, cf. main theorem of
[
Koh99
]
)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
be a smooth bounded pseudoconvex domain. Assume that there exist
\delta_{k}>0
(
k\in\mathbb{N}
) such that
\delta_{k}\rightarrow 1-
and
r_{k}\in\mathrm{Def}(\Omega)
such that
-(-r_{k})^{\delta_{k}}
is plurisubharmonic and

(6.2)

\lim_{k\rightarrow+\infty}\sqrt{1-\delta_{k}}\sigma(r_{k},r_{0})=0,

where
r_{0}
is a fixed defining function. Then the
\overline{\partial}
-Neumann problem is exactly regular on
\Omega
.

Notice that the condition (
6.2
) is independent of the choice of
r_{0}
and it holds in particular if the sequence
\{r_{k}\}
is
\sigma
-bounded, that is, it is bounded as a subset of the pseudo-metric space
(\mathrm{Def}(\Omega),\sigma)
.

We can now recall the definition of Diederich–Fornæss index, and give a variant of this notion inspired by Theorem
6.3
.

Definition 6.4
.

Let
\Omega
be a precompact smooth pseudoconvex domain in a complex manifold. A Diederich–Fornæss (D–F in the sequel) exponent of
\Omega
, is any
\delta\in(0,1]
for which there exists a defining function
r\in\mathrm{Def}(\Omega)
with the property that
-(-r)^{\delta}
is plurisubharmonic on
\Omega
(that is, on the subset of
\Omega
on which it is defined). We define the Diederich–Fornæss index of
\Omega
, denoted by
\mathrm{DF}(\Omega)
, as the supremum of all D–F exponents of
\Omega
:

\mathrm{DF}(\Omega):=\sup\{\delta>0\colon\exists r\in\mathrm{Def}(\Omega)\text{ s.t. }-(-r)^{\delta}\text{ is plush. on }\Omega\}.

We say that
\Omega
has regularized Diederich–Fornæss index
1
if there exist sequences
\delta_{k}\in(0,1]
and
r_{k}\in\mathrm{Def}(\Omega)
such that:

(1)

\delta_{k}\rightarrow 1-
,

(2)

-(-r_{k})^{\delta_{k}}
is plurisubharmonic,

(3)

\{r_{k}\}_{k}
is
\sigma
-bounded.

We have the following immediate corollary of Theorem
6.3
.

Theorem 6.5
.

If
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
is a smooth bounded pseudoconvex domain with regularized D–F index
1
, then the
\overline{\partial}
-Neumann problem is exactly regular on
\Omega
.

Finally, we state and prove our slightly improved Adachi–Yum theorem, involving the quantities
\mathfrak{n}_{K}
of Definition
4.9
and the notion of
“regularized D–F index
1
”
.

Theorem 6.6
.

Let
\Omega
be a precompact smooth pseudoconvex domain in a complex manifold. Assume that there exists a smooth strictly plurisubharmonic function in a neighborhood of
b\Omega
. Then we have the identity

\mathrm{DF}(\Omega)=\frac{1}{1+\mathfrak{n}(\mathcal{N})}.

Moreover, the domain
\Omega
has regularized D–F index
1
if and only if there exists
K<+\infty
such that
\mathfrak{n}_{K}(\mathcal{N})=0
.

Putting together Theorem
5.1
, Theorem
6.5
, and Theorem
6.6
, we obtain the desired corollary.

Theorem 6.7
.

If
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
is a smooth bounded pseudoconvex domain with trivial Levi core, then the
\overline{\partial}
-Neumann problem is exactly regular on
\Omega
.

We are left with the proof of Theorem
6.6
, which follows closely the argument of
[
AY21
]
. We take the occasion to give a presentation of this argument that stresses the invariant nature of the result, e.g., relying on Cartan’s formula and avoiding as much as possible computations w.r.t. local coordinates and frames. We exploit the following two lemmas.

Lemma 6.8

(cf.
[
Yum21
]
, Prop. 4.6 and
[
Liu19a
]
, Lemma 2.1)
.

Let
M
be a real hypersurface in a complex manifold. Let
N
be a
(1,0)
-vector field defined in a neighborhood of
M
such that
Nr\equiv 1
. Set
\alpha:=\alpha_{\theta,T}
, where
T=\frac{1}{2i}(N-\overline{N})
. If
Z
is a
(1,0)
vector field such that
Z_{p}\in\mathcal{N}_{p}
at some point
p\in M
, then the identity

N\left(\partial\overline{\partial}r(Z,\overline{Z})\right)=\partial\alpha(Z,\overline{Z})-\frac{|\alpha(Z)|^{2}}{2}

holds at
p
.

Proof.

Notice that
N\left(\partial\overline{\partial}r(gZ,\overline{gZ})\right)=|g|^{2}N\left(\partial\overline{\partial}r(Z,\overline{Z})\right)
for every smooth function
g
and every
(1,0)
vector field
Z
such that
Z_{p}\in\mathcal{N}_{p}
. Thus, both sides of the identity are Hermitian forms on
\mathcal{N}_{p}
, and we may assume without loss of generality that
Z
is a smooth
(1,0)
vector field such that
Zr\equiv 0
on a neighborhood of
p
.

The
2
-form
\partial\overline{\partial}r=d\overline{\partial}r
is exact, thus closed. Cartan’s formula yields

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 3d\left(\partial\overline{\partial}r\right)(N,Z,\overline{Z})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N\left(\partial\overline{\partial}r(Z,\overline{Z})\right)-Z\left(\partial\overline{\partial}r(N,\overline{Z})\right)+\overline{Z}\left(\partial\overline{\partial}r(N,Z)\right)

\displaystyle-\partial\overline{\partial}r([N,Z],\overline{Z})+\partial\overline{\partial}r([N,\overline{Z}],Z)-\partial\overline{\partial}r([Z,\overline{Z}],N)

We now discuss each term separately.

By Proposition
4.1
and the fact that
\alpha
is real,
Z\left(\partial\overline{\partial}r(N,\overline{Z})\right)=Z\left(\alpha(\overline{Z})\right)/2
.

Next,
\partial\overline{\partial}r(N,Z)\equiv 0
by type considerations, and thus
\overline{Z}\left(\partial\overline{\partial}r(N,Z)\right)\equiv 0
.

Since both
Zr
and
Nr
are constant,
[N,Z]r=N(Zr)-Z(Nr)\equiv 0
. Thus,
[N,Z]\in T^{1,0}M
and, since
Z_{p}\in\mathcal{N}_{p}
,
\partial\overline{\partial}r([N,Z],\overline{Z})
vanishes at
p
.

The vector field
[N,\overline{Z}]
is tangent to
M
for the same reason as
[N,Z]
, but it has not definite type. At any rate,
[N,\overline{Z}]=g(N-\overline{N})\mod T^{1,0}M\oplus T^{0,1}M
, for some smooth function
g
. Applying
\partial r
to this identity, we get
g=\partial r([N,\overline{Z}])
. Using again the fact that
Z_{p}\in\mathcal{N}_{p}
and Proposition
4.1
, we get

\partial\overline{\partial}r([N,\overline{Z}],Z)=-g\partial\overline{\partial}r(\overline{N},Z)=\frac{|\alpha(Z)|^{2}}{2}.

Finally,
\partial\overline{\partial}r([Z,\overline{Z}],N)=-\partial\overline{\partial}r(N,[Z,\overline{Z}]_{0,1})=-\alpha([Z,\overline{Z}]_{0,1})/2
.

Putting everything together, we get

N\left(\partial\overline{\partial}r(Z,\overline{Z})\right)=\frac{Z\left(\alpha(\overline{Z})\right)}{2}-\frac{|\alpha(Z)|^{2}}{2}-\frac{\alpha([Z,\overline{Z}]_{0,1})}{2}.

To conclude, one uses Lemma
4.6
.
∎

Lemma 6.9
.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a neighborhood of the origin and
Q:U\times\mathbb{C}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
a
C^{2}
function such that
\vec{a}\mapsto Q(x,\vec{a})
is a quadratic form for every
x\in U
. Assume that:

(1)

Q(x,\cdot)
is nonnegative definite for every
x
such that
x_{n}=0
;

(2)

\partial_{x_{n}}Q(0,\vec{a})>0
for every nonzero
\vec{a}
in the null space of
Q(0,\cdot)
.

Then there exists a neighborhood of the origin
V\subseteq U
such that
Q(x,\cdot)
is positive definite for every
x\in V\cap\{x_{n}>0\}
.

Proof.

Assume without loss of generality that the null space of
Q(0,\cdot)
is spanned by the first
k
canonical basis vectors of
\mathbb{C}^{N}
. The second assumption and a simple compactness plus homogeneity argument gives

(6.3)

\partial_{x_{n}}Q(x,\vec{a})\geq\varepsilon|\vec{a}|^{2}\qquad\forall x\in V,\ \forall\vec{a}\in\mathcal{C}_{\varepsilon},

where
\varepsilon>0
,
V
is a neighborhood of the origin, and

\mathcal{C}_{\varepsilon}:=\{\vec{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{N})\in\mathbb{C}^{N}\colon|a_{k+1}|^{2}+\cdots+|a_{N}|^{2}\leq\varepsilon\left(|a_{1}|^{2}+\cdots+|a_{k}|^{2}\right)\}.

Writing
x=(x^{\prime},x_{n})
, where
x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
and
x_{n}>0
, Lagrange Theorem yields

Q(x,\vec{a})=Q((x^{\prime},0),\vec{a})+x_{n}\partial_{x_{n}}Q((x^{\prime},x_{*}),\vec{a})

for some
x_{*}\in[0,x_{n}]
. By the first assumption and (
6.3
), we get
Q(x,\vec{a})>0
for every
x\in V\cap\{x_{n}>0\}
(for a possibly smaller neighborhood of the origin
V
) and
\vec{a}\in\mathcal{C}_{\varepsilon}
. Since
Q(0,\vec{a})\geq c|\vec{a}|^{2}
for some
c>0
and every
\vec{a}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}\setminus\mathcal{C}_{\varepsilon}
, another compactness plus homogeneity argument gives
Q(x,\vec{a})\geq c|\vec{a}|^{2}
for every
x
in a neighborhood of
0
and
\vec{a}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}\setminus\mathcal{C}_{\varepsilon}
. This completes the proof.
∎

6.1.
Proof of the “
\leq
” and of the “only if” parts of Theorem
6.6

Since

(6.4)

\partial\overline{\partial}\left(-(-r)^{\delta}\right)=\delta(-r)^{\delta-1}\left(\partial\overline{\partial}r+(1-\delta)\frac{\partial r\wedge\overline{\partial}r}{-r}\right),

the D–F index is the supremum of the set of
\delta
’s such that
\delta\in(0,1]
and

\left(\partial\overline{\partial}r+(1-\delta)\frac{\partial r\wedge\overline{\partial}r}{-r}\right)(Z,\overline{Z})\geq 0\qquad\forall Z\in T^{1,0}(\Omega\cap V)

for some
r\in\mathrm{Def}(\Omega)
and a neighborhood
V
of the boundary. Fix
\delta
and
r
as above.

Let
N
be as in Lemma
6.8
and
Z
be a vector field of type
(1,0)
such that
Z_{p}\in\mathcal{N}_{p}
and
Zr\equiv 0
, defined in a neghborhood of a point
p\in b\Omega
. The matrix of the Hermitian form
\partial\overline{\partial}r+(1-\delta)\frac{\partial r\wedge\overline{\partial}r}{-r}
, restricted to the span of

and

, is

(6.5)

\begin{bmatrix}\partial\overline{\partial}r(Z,\overline{Z})&\partial\overline{\partial}r(Z,\overline{N})\\
\partial\overline{\partial}r(N,\overline{Z})&\partial\overline{\partial}r(N,\overline{N})+\frac{(1-\delta)}{-2r}\end{bmatrix}

Computing the determinant, we get the inequality

(-r)\left(\partial\overline{\partial}r(Z,\overline{Z})\partial\overline{\partial}r(N,\overline{N})-|\partial\overline{\partial}r(Z,\overline{N})|^{2}\right)+(1-\delta)\frac{\partial\overline{\partial}r(Z,\overline{Z})}{2}\geq 0,

valid inside

, in a neighborhood of

, its derivative along the real vector

must be nonpositive at

2|\partial\overline{\partial}r(Z,\overline{N})|^{2}+(1-\delta)\frac{(N+\overline{N})\partial\overline{\partial}r(Z,\overline{Z})}{2}\leq 0\qquad\text{at }p.

Proposition
4.1
and Lemma

yield

\frac{|\alpha(Z)|^{2}}{2}+(1-\delta)\left(\frac{\partial\alpha(Z,\overline{Z})}{2}-\frac{\overline{\partial}\alpha(Z,\overline{Z})}{2}-\frac{|\alpha(Z)|^{2}}{2}\right)\leq 0\qquad\text{at }p,

or, equivalently,
\delta|\alpha(Z)|^{2}+(1-\delta)\left(\partial\alpha(Z,\overline{Z})-\overline{\partial}\alpha(Z,\overline{Z})\right)\leq 0
. Here
\alpha=\alpha_{\theta,T}
, with

and

as in Proposition

. Since

at

, this inequality may be rewritten as

\left(\overline{\alpha_{0,1}}\wedge\alpha_{0,1}\right)(Z,\overline{Z})\leq(\delta^{-1}-1)\overline{\partial}\alpha(Z,\overline{Z})\qquad\text{at }p.

By the arbitrariness of

and

,
\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}(\alpha;\mathcal{N})\leq\delta^{-1}-1
and thus (by Proposition
4.11
)
\mathfrak{n}(\mathcal{N})\leq\delta^{-1}-1
. Taking the supremum over
\delta
, we get the inequality

\mathrm{DF}(\Omega)\leq\frac{1}{1+\mathfrak{n}(\mathcal{N})}.

Assume now that
\Omega
has regularized D–F index equal to

. Then the argument above proves that

(6.6)

\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}(\alpha_{k};\mathcal{N})\leq\delta_{k}^{-1}-1,

where
\delta_{k}\rightarrow 1-

) and recalling again Proposition

for

Assume that

\left(\overline{\alpha_{0,1}}\wedge\alpha_{0,1}\right)(Z_{p},\overline{Z_{p}})<t\overline{\partial}\alpha(Z_{p},\overline{Z_{p}})\quad\forall p\in M,\ Z_{p}\in\mathcal{N}_{p}.

By Proposition
4.1
, vi), we may assume that

, where

We claim that

\left(\partial\overline{\partial}r+\frac{t}{1+t}\frac{\partial r\wedge\overline{\partial}r}{-r}\right)(X,\overline{X})>0\qquad\forall X\in T^{1,0}(\Omega\cap V).

It is clearly enough to fix

(1,0)

on

Appendix A
A quantitative bound for
\mathfrak{n}_{X}

In this appendix, we provide a quantitative estimate of the function
\mathfrak{n}_{X}
of Section
4.2
, in the case where
X
is a pseudoconvex domain in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
satisfying an additional regularity property. Our estimate is a sort of interpolation inequality comparing
\mathfrak{n}_{\Omega}
with two other, possibly more natural, norm-like functions on the first de Rham cohomology of
\Omega
.

We use the following version of John–Nirenberg inequality, appearing in
[
HS93
]
. A domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
is said to satisfy a quasi-hyperbolic boundary condition with constants
a,b>0
if for some
x_{0}\in\Omega
the inequality

k_{\Omega}(x,x_{0})\leq a\log\frac{1}{d(x,b\Omega)}+b

holds for every
x\in\Omega
, where:

•

d(\cdot,b\Omega)
is the Euclidean distance to the boundary,

•

k_{\Omega}(x,y)
is the distance w.r.t. the Riemannian metric
d(x,b\Omega)^{-2}dx^{2}
.

Theorem A.1
.

[Theorem 3.5 of
[
HS93
]
]
Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfy a quasi-hyperbolic boundary condition with constants
a
and
b
. There are positive constants
c_{1}=c_{1}(n,a)
and
c_{2}=c_{2}(n,a)
such that if
u\in L^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
satisfies the BMO condition

[u]_{\mathrm{BMO}}:=\sup_{B}\frac{1}{|B|}\int_{B}|u-u_{B}|<+\infty,

where the supremum is over all Euclidean balls
B=B(x,r)
such that
B(x,\frac{9}{8}r)\subseteq\Omega
, then

\int_{\Omega}\exp\left(\frac{c_{1}|u-u_{\Omega}|}{[u]_{\mathrm{BMO}}}\right)\leq c_{2}|\Omega|.

Theorem A.2
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n}
be a pseudoconvex domain and
[\alpha]\in H^{1}_{\mathrm{dR}}(\Omega)
such that
\mathfrak{n}_{\Omega}([\alpha])<+\infty
(as defined in Section
4.2
). If
\Omega_{1}\subseteq\Omega
is a relatively compact pseudoconvex domain satisfying the quasi-hyperbolic boundary condition with constants
a
and
b
,

\mathfrak{n}_{L^{1}(\Omega_{1})}([\alpha])^{2}\leq c(n,a)\mathfrak{n}_{\Omega}([\alpha])\mathfrak{n}_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{1})}([\alpha])\qquad\forall[\alpha]\in H^{1}_{\mathrm{dR}}(\Omega),

where

\mathfrak{n}_{L^{1}(\Omega)}([\alpha]):=\inf_{h\in\mathrm{PH}(\Omega)\colon[d^{c}h]=[\alpha]}\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}|h|

and

\mathfrak{n}_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}([\alpha]):=\inf_{h\in\mathrm{PH}(\Omega)\colon[d^{c}h]=[\alpha]}||h||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}.

The quantities
\mathfrak{n}_{L^{1}(\Omega)}([\alpha])
and
\mathfrak{n}_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}([\alpha])
are well-defined thanks to Lemma
4.5
.

Proof.

Write
\mathfrak{n}
in place of
\mathfrak{n}_{\Omega}
for simplicity. Let
\mathfrak{n}([\alpha])<\lambda
and
h\in\mathrm{PH}(\Omega)
such that
[\alpha]=[d^{c}h]
, whose existence is guaranteed by Lemma
4.5
. Then, by (
4.3
), there exists
f\in C^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
such that

\partial(h+if)\wedge\overline{\partial}(h-if)\leq\lambda\partial\overline{\partial}f.

Tracing this inequality and using a trivial bound, we get

\frac{|\nabla f|^{2}}{2}\leq 2\lambda\Delta f+|\nabla h|^{2},

where
\nabla
and
\Delta
are the Euclidean gradient and Laplacian. Multiplying by
\eta^{2}\leq 1
with
\eta
test function supported on
B(x,\frac{17}{16}r)\subset B(x,\frac{9}{8}r)\subseteq\Omega_{1}
, equal to
1
on
B(x,r)
, and such that
|\nabla\eta|\leq 17r^{-1}
, we find

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\eta^{2}|\nabla f|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-4\lambda\int_{\Omega}\eta\nabla f\cdot\nabla\eta+\int_{\Omega}\eta^{2}|\nabla h|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\int_{\Omega}\eta^{2}|\nabla f|^{2}+16\lambda^{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\eta|^{2}+\int_{B(x,\frac{17}{16}r)}|\nabla h|^{2}.

Since
h
is harmonic, we have the standard inequality

\int_{B(x,\frac{17}{16}r)}|\nabla h|^{2}\leq C_{n}^{\prime}r^{-2}\int_{B(x,\frac{9}{8}r)}|h|^{2}\leq C_{n}r^{n-2}||h||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{1})}^{2},

which gives

\int_{B(x,r)}|\nabla f|^{2}\leq C_{n}r^{2n-2}(\lambda^{2}+||h||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{1})}^{2}).

Thus, by Hölder and Poincaré inequalities, if
B=B(x,r)
is a Euclidean ball such that
B(x,\frac{9}{8}r)\subseteq\Omega_{1}

\frac{1}{|B|}\int_{B}|f-f_{B}|\leq C_{n}(\lambda+||h||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{1})}).

By Theorem
A.1
, setting
g:=f-f_{\Omega_{1}}
we have

\int_{\Omega_{1}}\exp\left(\frac{c_{1}|g|}{\lambda+||h||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{1})}}\right)\leq c_{2}|\Omega_{1}|,

where
c_{1},c_{2}>0
depend only on
n
,
a
and
b
. Of course, we have

(A.1)

\partial(h+ig)\wedge\overline{\partial}(h-ig)\leq\lambda\partial\overline{\partial}g.

The
(0,1)
-form
\overline{\partial}(h-ig)
is clearly
\overline{\partial}
-closed. By (
A.1
) and Hörmander theorem, there exists
u
such that
\overline{\partial}u=\overline{\partial}(h-ig)
and

\int_{\Omega_{1}}|u|^{2}\exp\left(-\frac{c_{1}g}{\lambda+||h||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{1})}}\right)\leq\frac{\lambda(\lambda+||h||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{1})})}{c_{1}}\int_{\Omega_{1}}\exp\left(-\frac{c_{1}g}{\lambda+||h||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{1})}}\right).

Thus

\displaystyle\left(\int_{\Omega_{1}}|u|\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{1}}|u|^{2}\exp\left(-\frac{c_{1}g}{\lambda+||h||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{1})}}\right)\int_{\Omega_{1}}\exp\left(\frac{c_{1}g}{\lambda+||h||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{1})}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda(\lambda+||h||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{1})})}{c_{1}}\left\{\int_{\Omega_{1}}\exp\left(\frac{c_{1}|g|}{\lambda+||h||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{1})}}\right)\right\}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c_{2}^{2}c_{1}^{-1}\lambda(\lambda+||h||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{1})})|\Omega_{1}|^{2}.

Since
u+F=h-ig
for some holomorphic
F:\Omega_{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
, we have
h-\Re(F)=\Re(u)
and

\left(\int_{\Omega_{1}}|h-\Re(F)|\right)^{2}\leq c_{2}^{2}c_{1}^{-1}\lambda(\lambda+||h||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{1})})|\Omega_{1}|^{2}

Since
h
is any pluriharmonic function such that
[d^{c}h]=[\alpha]
, the same estimate holds for
h+\Re(G)
, where
G
is any holomorphic function on
\Omega_{1}
. Therefore, we get

\mathfrak{n}_{L^{1}(\Omega_{1})}([\alpha])^{2}\leq c_{2}^{2}c_{1}^{-1}\lambda(\lambda+\mathfrak{n}_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{1})}([\alpha])).

Since
\lambda>\mathfrak{n}([\alpha])
is arbitrary, we get

\mathfrak{n}_{L^{1}(\Omega_{1})}([\alpha])^{2}\leq c_{2}^{2}c_{1}^{-1}\mathfrak{n}([\alpha])(\mathfrak{n}([\alpha])+\mathfrak{n}_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{1})}([\alpha])).

If
\mathfrak{n}([\alpha])\leq\mathfrak{n}_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{1})}([\alpha])
, we get the thesis. Otherwise, it follows from the trivial inequality
\mathfrak{n}_{L^{1}(\Omega_{1})}([\alpha])\leq\mathfrak{n}_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{1})}([\alpha])
.
∎
