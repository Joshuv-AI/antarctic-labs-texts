Inaccuracy matters: accounting for solution accuracy in event-triggered nonlinear model predictive control
Omar J. Faqir, Eric C. Kerrigan

Abstract
We consider the effect of using approximate system predictions in event-triggered control schemes. Such approximations may result from using numerical transcription methods for solving continuous-time optimal control problems. Mesh refinement can guarantee upper bounds on the error in the differential equations which model the system dynamics. With the accuracy guarantees of a mesh refinement scheme, we show that the proposed event-triggering scheme — which compares the measured system with approximate state predictions — can be used with a guaranteed strictly positive inter-update time. We show that if we have knowledge of the employed transcription scheme or the approximation errors, then we can obtain better online estimates of inter-update times. We additionally detail a method of tightening constraints on the approximate system trajectory used in the nonlinear programming problem to guarantee constraint satisfaction of the continuous-time system. This is the first work to incorporate prediction accuracy in triggering metrics. Using the solution accuracy we can guarantee reliable lower bounds for inter-update times and perform solution dependent constraint tightening.



I
Introduction

Periodic control policies are often accepted without question.
A system measurement is taken at each sample period and used to calculate an appropriate control signal to apply to the plant.
Although the choice of periodic control policies is in part due to the discrete nature of digital platforms, processor clock speed is typically several orders of magnitude faster than the dominant plant dynamics.
More importantly, assumptions on sampling periodicity simplifies analysis
[
1
]
.
Even so, the choice of sample period is non-obvious. It is often determined through engineering know-how (e.g. an order of magnitude faster than dominant dynamics) or based on the Nyquist-Shannon theorem.
However, there is no explicit relationship between the Nyquist frequency and discretization accuracy, so sampling decisions are often conservative
[
2
]
.

Periodic sampling policies are inherently open-loop.
Control practitioners determine a
single
sample interval to satisfy performance and stability criteria for
all
possible initial conditions and disturbance realizations
[
3
]
.
This ‘worst-case’ periodic design leads to over allocation/utilization of communication and computation resources with no necessary improvement in control performance
[
1
]
.
Furthermore, the systems of interest have also evolved — we often don’t have compute units dedicated for control.
Embedded devices typically have a set of (real-time) tasks, of which control is only one.
The problem of resource allocation is exacerbated for (wireless) networked control systems ((W)NCS) where sensors, actuators and controllers may be spatially separated
[
4
,
5
]
and communication resources scarce.

The control loop is closed by measuring the plant, computing a control action, and updating the actuators.
Consider the following sets of times for each of these actions.
(i) The evenly sampled grid
\mathcal{T}_{g}:=\left\{t_{k}^{g}\right\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, where
t_{k+1}^{g}-t_{k}^{g}=h,\forall k\in\mathbb{N}
, for some
h\geq 0
. By setting
h=0
we can consider the archetype where the system is continuously monitored.
(ii) The set of times
\mathcal{T}_{s}:=\left\{t_{k}^{s}\right\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
where the system is sampled, and (iii)
\mathcal{T}_{u}=\left\{t_{k}^{u}\right\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
where the control action is updated.
Without considering delays, periodic control system design implicitly assumes that
\mathcal{T}_{g}=\mathcal{T}_{s}=\mathcal{T}_{u}
, where
h>0
is chosen sufficiently small to guarantee stability.
We feel that requiring the sets of update and sample times to be equivalent is overly restrictive.
Intuitively, it is only required that
\mathcal{T}_{u}\subseteq\mathcal{T}_{s}\subseteq\mathcal{T}_{g}
.
The left inclusion follows since any feedback is parametric in the most recent state measurement.
If
\mathcal{T}_{u}\not\subseteq\mathcal{T}_{s}
then the control is updated using outdated system information.
To characterize data losses and delays in NCS,
[
6
]
further distinguishes between times when the system measurements are taken, sent and received.

The work
[
3
]
makes the case that any metric of control performance should reflect the offline and online cost of implementing the controller.
The online cost is of particular importance in computationally demanding control methods, such as model predictive control (MPC).
In the exemplary work
[
7
]
a task model is proposed for joint determination of control and sampling of real-time systems.
The principle is to increase/decrease control resources as a function of system state and the number of other processes requesting compute resources.
The use of state feedback to dynamically determine sample times has since been central in the fields of event- and self-triggered control.
These strategies, which are formulated in Section
II
, only update the control when state-dependent conditions are met.

(Nonlinear) model predictive control ((N)MPC) is an extensively used control technique, where a finite time control trajectory is designed to optimize some performance metric of the predicted system evolution.
NMPC complements event- and self-triggering strategies, because the predictive information resulting from online optimization may allow for longer open-loop run times than using zero-order hold state feedback controllers
[
8
]
.
The computational burden of solving a nonlinear program (NLP) online is a key drawback.
However, aperiodic triggering schemes have the potential to increase the average compute time available between control updates.

An additional consideration is that numerical methods for solving the continuous-time optimal control problems that arise in NMPC only generate approximations of the continuous state and input trajectories.
The computational burden of NMPC may potentially be reduced by either solving a less complex NLP — resulting in
lower quality
predictions — or by solving the NLP less frequently — resulting in a more
outdated
prediction.
The quality of the NMPC solution is different from closed-loop performance, and may by guaranteed through mesh refinement schemes. This paper is the first work to consider the effect of these approximation errors in triggering schemes.

We investigate how the solution accuracy and triggering conditions will affect the inter-update times in an event-triggered NMPC framework.
We bound the inter-update time (IUT)

\min_{k\in\mathbb{N}}t_{k+1}^{u}-t_{k}^{u}

(1)

using certifications on the solution accuracy, guaranteed through a mesh refinement scheme, and the triggering protocol.
We further show how error information provided by online mesh refinement schemes can be used to generate less conservative IUT estimates.
A method for constraint tightening is proposed to guarantee that constraints placed on the approximate system predictions at a finite number of time instances ensure the real system trajectory satisfies constraints.

In this work we will first introduce a set of preliminaries (Section
II
) detailing basic event- and self- triggered control paradigms, the general nonlinear model predictive control optimization problem and a possible procedure for solving this problem, using direct collocation and mesh refinement. In Section
III
we use the guarantees from event-triggering and mesh refinement schemes to determine a guaranteed minimum time between control updates, which depends on the allowable prediction error and solution accuracy. This bound is improved in Section
IV
where we use the actual error in the solution of the optimization problem, rendering less conservative online estimates of the inter control update times. In Sections
VI
and
VII
these methods are exemplified for linear and nonlinear systems. This work significantly extends results from
[
9
]
by generalizing the system under consideration, the triggering conditions being employed and proposing constraint tightening schemes.

II-B

Nonlinear Model Predictive Control

In MPC a finite time sequence/trajectory of control inputs is designed to optimize some performance metric of the predicted plant evolution
[
17
]
.
The optimization problem is parametric in the most recent state measurements.
Typical MPC paradigms account for uncertainty by measuring/estimating the state and solving the associated optimization problem at periodic intervals.
Only the first portion of the computed actuation is applied to the system before the procedure is repeated.
Application of MPC has historically been restricted to slow or simple (linear) dynamical systems due to the complexity of performing optimization close to real-time. However, greater computational resources and tailor-made optimization algorithms nowadays allow for MPC of faster, more complex systems than in the past.

The continuous-time NMPC problem solved at time

may be cast in the general Bolza form,

\displaystyle(\hat{x}(t_{0}),\hat{x}(t_{f}))+\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{t_{f}}L(\hat{x}(t),\hat{u}(t),t)\ \mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\forall t\in[t_{0},t_{f}],

\displaystyle\dot{\hat{x}}(t)=f(\hat{x}(t),\hat{u}(t),t)\text{ a.e.}

\displaystyle c(\hat{x}(t),\hat{u}(t),t)\leq 0\text{ a.e.}

\displaystyle\phi(\hat{x}(t_{0}),\hat{x}(t_{f}))=0

\displaystyle\hat{x}(t_{0})=x(t_{i}^{u}).

Direct Collocation & Mesh Refinement

The dynamic optimization problem (

Except for the simplest cases, (

Direct collocation methods transcribe (

Minimum IUT

Define the error

\gamma_{i}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}(w_{i}+1)\alpha_{i}(\tau)+f_{i}(\tau,\tilde{x}(\tau),u(\tau))-f_{i}(\tau,x(\tau),u(\tau))-v_{i}(\tau)\mathrm{d}\tau

Through application of the triangle inequality, the prediction error magnitude of each state may be bounded from above as

\displaystyle\left|\gamma_{i}(t)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|(w_{i}+1)\int_{0}^{t}\alpha_{i}(\tau)\mathrm{d}\tau\right|+

\displaystyle\qquad\left|\int_{0}^{t}f_{i}(\tau,\tilde{x}(\tau),u(\tau))-f_{i}(\tau,x(\tau),u(\tau))\mathrm{d}\tau\right|+

(33)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\left|\int_{0}^{t}-v_{i}(\tau)\mathrm{d}\tau\right|

(34)

\displaystyle\leq(w_{i}+1)\int_{0}^{t}\left|\alpha_{i}(\tau)\right|\mathrm{d}\tau+

(35)

\displaystyle\qquad\int_{0}^{t}\left|f_{i}(\tau,\tilde{x}(\tau),u(\tau))-f_{i}(\tau,x(\tau),u(\tau))\right|\mathrm{d}\tau+

(36)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\int_{0}^{t}\left|v_{i}(\tau)\right|\mathrm{d}\tau.

(37)

Now, consider the set
\mathcal{T}_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{T}_{h}
of open intervals of
T
, i.e.

\mathcal{T}_{t}:=\{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}|T\cap\overline{(0,t)}\not=\emptyset\}

(38)

which is a mesh on an interval contained in
\Omega
and by construction satisfies the properties detailed in Definition
1
. Since the mesh refinement scheme guarantees
\epsilon_{k,i}\leq\hat{\epsilon}_{i},\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, the first term in (
37
) may be bounded from above as

(w_{i}+1)\int_{0}^{t}\left|\alpha_{i}(\tau)\right|\mathrm{d}\tau\leq(w_{i}+1)\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{t}}\int_{T}\left|\alpha_{i}(\tau)\right|\mathrm{d}\tau\leq(w_{i}+1)\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{t}}\hat{\epsilon}.

(39)

Through application of the resolution and quasi-uniformity properties of mesh
\mathcal{T}_{t}
, the smallest interval satisfies

\min_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{t}}\left|T\right|\geq\sigma h:=\underline{h}.

(40)

Using
\lceil\cdot\rceil
to denote the ceiling function, we may bound the maximum number of mesh segments in the interval
(0,t)
as
\lceil t\underline{h}^{-1}\rceil
, from which it follows that

\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{t}}\hat{\epsilon}_{i}\leq\left\lceil\frac{t}{\underline{h}}\right\rceil\hat{\epsilon}_{i}\leq\left(\frac{t}{\underline{h}}+1\right)\hat{\epsilon}_{i}.

(41)

From this, we can bound the prediction error in the
i^{\text{th}}
state as

\displaystyle\left|\delta_{i}(t)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{t}{\underline{h}}+1\right)(w_{i}+1)\hat{\epsilon}_{i}+\left|\theta_{i}(t)-\theta_{i}(0)\right|+

(42)

\displaystyle\ \int_{0}^{t}\left|f_{i}(\tau,\tilde{x}(\tau),u(\tau))-f_{i}(\tau,x(\tau),u(\tau))\right|\mathrm{d}\tau+\int_{0}^{t}\left|v_{i}(\tau)\right|\mathrm{d}\tau

(43)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{t}{\underline{h}}+1\right)(w_{i}+1)\hat{\epsilon}_{i}+\left|\theta_{i}(t)-\theta_{i}(0)\right|+

(44)

\displaystyle\ L_{x}\int_{0}^{t}\left|\delta_{i}(\tau)\right|\mathrm{d}\tau+\int_{0}^{t}\left|v_{i}(\tau)\right|\mathrm{d}\tau

(45)

where the second inequality results from the assumption of Lipschitz continuity of
f(\cdot)
.
Combining each element-wise inequality, we can bound the weighted norm of the prediction error as

\displaystyle\left\lVert\delta(t)\right\rVert_{M}

\displaystyle\leq\Bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{n}M_{i}\Bigg(\left(\frac{t}{\underline{h}}+1\right)(\hat{w}+1)\hat{\epsilon}_{i}+\left|\theta_{i}(t)-\theta_{i}(0)\right|+

(46)

\displaystyle\qquad L_{x}\int_{0}^{t}\left|\delta_{i}(\tau)\right|\mathrm{d}\tau+\int_{0}^{t}\left|v_{i}(\tau)\right|\mathrm{d}\tau\Bigg)^{p}\Bigg)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

(47)

Through application of the triangle inequality we may then bound the prediction error at time
t
as,

\displaystyle\left\lVert\delta(t)\right\rVert_{M}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{t}{\underline{h}}+1\right)\left\lVert(\hat{w}+1)\hat{\epsilon}\right\rVert_{M}+\left\lVert\theta(t)-\theta(0))\right\rVert_{M}+

(48)

\displaystyle\qquad L_{x}\int_{0}^{t}\left\lVert\delta(\tau)\right\rVert_{M}\mathrm{d}\tau+\int_{0}^{t}\left\lVert v(\tau)\right\rVert_{M}\mathrm{d}\tau

(49)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{t}{\underline{h}}+1\right)\left\lVert(\hat{w}+1)\hat{\epsilon}\right\rVert_{M}+\left\lVert M\right\rVert\left(2\hat{\theta}+t\left(L_{x}\Delta+\hat{v}\right)\right).

(50)

The second inequality follows from

\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{M}\leq\left\lVert M\right\rVert\left\lVert x\right\rVert.

(51)

We have also made use of the assumptions that
\left\lVert\theta(t)-\theta(0)\right\rVert\leq 2\hat{\theta}
and
\left\lVert v(t)\right\rVert\leq\overline{v}
.
We now have an expression which is dependent only on parameters known offline. We may guarantee the existence of a
\underline{\tau}^{u}>0
by showing the existence of a
t>0
such that

\left(\frac{t}{\underline{h}}+1\right)\left\lVert(\hat{w}+1)\hat{\epsilon}\right\rVert_{M}+\left\lVert M\right\rVert\left(2\hat{\theta}+t\left(L_{x}\Delta+\hat{v}\right)\right)\leq\Delta.

(52)

Through manipulation, this inequality is satisfied when,

t\leq\frac{\Delta-\left\lVert(\hat{w}+1)\hat{\epsilon}\right\rVert_{M}+\left\lVert M\right\rVert 2\hat{\theta}}{\frac{\left\lVert(\hat{w}+1)\hat{\epsilon}\right\rVert_{M}}{\sigma h}+\left\lVert M\right\rVert\left(L_{x}\Delta+\hat{v}\right)}.

(53)

Since all parameters are in
\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}
, the RHS is guaranteed to be strictly positive as long as
\Delta>\left\lVert(\hat{w}+1)\hat{\epsilon}\right\rVert_{M}+\left\lVert M\right\rVert 2\hat{\theta}
.
∎

In the special case, detailed in
[
18
, Ch. 4]
, where refinement occurs based only on the maximum relative error,
guaranteeing

\max_{i}\epsilon_{k,i}\leq\epsilon_{\text{max}},\forall k\in\mathcal{K}\Rightarrow\epsilon_{k,i}\leq\hat{\epsilon},\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,n\},k\in\mathcal{K},

(54)

then the resulting guaranteed IUT may be written as

\underline{\tau}^{u}:=\frac{\Delta-\left\lVert\hat{w}+1\right\rVert_{M}\epsilon_{\text{max}}-\left\lVert M\right\rVert 2\hat{\theta}}{\frac{\left\lVert\hat{w}+1\right\rVert_{M}\epsilon_{\text{max}}}{\sigma h}+\left\lVert M\right\rVert\left(L_{x}\Delta+\hat{v}\right)}.

(55)

In a basic mesh refinement scheme, the NLP resulting from (
9
) is solved on each new, increasingly dense mesh. In
[
18
]
it is suggested to use an initially sparse mesh, where the refinement adds grid points where necessary. Therefore, a small
\hat{\eta}
invariably results in more NLPs being solved for a single approximation
\tilde{x}(\cdot),u(\cdot)
.
Since (
26
) is a decreasing function of
\hat{\epsilon}
, we can conclude a trade off between solving more NLPs per control update — yielding a higher accuracy solution — but less frequently, so solving fewer NLPs at each update, but more frequently.

We refine the mesh of a direct collocation transcription using local error analysis. The driving principle of direct collocation is that local changes of the mesh or polynomial order will only affect local changes in the solution. Hence, although the above trade-off
is
true for any refinement scheme, we can also affect this trade-off by tailoring the operation of the refinement procedure used. The work
[
18
]
proposes using only the current error distribution of the primal variables as a means of estimating how the error may be reduced by adding additional mesh points in specific intervals. On the other hand,
[
21
]
refines using a metric based on the error distribution of the dual variables — this maintains important properties as a representation of solution quality while being computationally cheaper.
Regardless, adding too many mesh points increases the dimensionality of the following NLP unnecessarily. By contrast, adding too few mesh points results in more refinement iterations (and NLP solves) needed to achieve allowable local errors.

In this qualitative analysis we have also not considered the effect of warm starting the NLP solver (both at each mesh refinement iteration and each update time) with the previous solution.
We have also not considered mesh refinement schemes that may also
remove
grid points.
Both of these considerations will be useful for reducing the computational cost of online schemes.

Unfortunately the bound (
26
) is not tight.
For the update to be triggered at time
t_{k}^{u}+\underline{\tau}^{u}
, the approximation error metric would need to be maximized
\forall t\in[t_{k}^{u},t_{k}^{u}+\underline{\tau}^{u})
. However, this cannot be the case, because the refinement metric is reset to zero at time
t_{k}^{u}
. Since the dynamics are assumed smooth and approximating polynomials piecewise smooth, the refinement metric cannot be maximized immediately.
Another important point is that bound (
26
) will be finite even when
\hat{v}=0
and
\hat{\theta}=0
. We have not used knowledge of the discretization/interpolation schemes, in which case the bound may be improved.

If (
Pc
) includes simple bounds and rate constraints on all state variables we may guarantee a finite
\hat{w}
. For example, the bounds
\underline{x}\leq x_{i}(t)\leq\overline{x}
and
\underline{\dot{x}}\leq\dot{x}_{i}(t)\leq\overline{\dot{x}}
imply that

w_{i}\leq\max\Big\{\left|\underline{x}\right|,\left|\overline{x}\right|,\left|\underline{\dot{x}}\right|,\left|\overline{\dot{x}}\right|\Big\},

(56)

from which
\hat{w}
may be determined. Similarly, if we make the additional assumption that the origin is an equilibrium — common in some regulation-type problems — then

w_{i}\leq\max\Big\{\left|\underline{x}\right|,\left|\overline{x}\right|,L_{x}\left|\underline{x}\right|,L_{x}\left|\overline{x}\right|\Big\},

(57)

without needing to bound the state derivatives.

However, in certain cases it may not be natural or appropriate to enforce these constraints. Further, in non-regulation type problems,
x(t)
may be significantly within bounds, and so determination of
\hat{w}
through (
56
) or (
57
) could be extremely restrictive. Thus, in the general case it is difficult to quantitatively relate the relative local quadrature
\epsilon_{k}
with the absolute quadrature
\eta_{k}
. Instead, refining directly on the absolute error yields the following result.

Corollary 1
.

For the ETC scheme resulting from solving (
9
) to an accuracy satisfying (
20
) at times
\mathcal{T}_{u}
implicitly determined through triggering condition (
22
), the minimum IUT

\underline{\tau}_{u}:=\frac{\Delta-\left\lVert\hat{\eta}\right\rVert_{M}+\left\lVert M\right\rVert 2\hat{\theta}}{\frac{\left\lVert\hat{\eta}\right\rVert_{M}}{\sigma h}+\left\lVert M\right\rVert\left(L_{x}\Delta+\hat{v}\right)}

(58)

is guaranteed to be strictly positive for
\Delta>\left\lVert\hat{\eta}\right\rVert_{M}+\left\lVert M\right\rVert 2\hat{\theta}
.

Proof.

Follows the procedure for the proof of Theorem
1
, but with all
w_{i}=0
and replacing
\epsilon
by
\eta
.
∎

IV
Online estimation of IUT

The IUT bound (
26
) can be improved by using the actual error information provided by solving (
9
) with a mesh refinement procedure.
Theorem
3
is the first explicit definition of IUT to account for both problem data and the solution accuracy of (
9

Although the following results resemble STC conditions of the form (
8

Additional assumptions on problem structure would be needed for this.
Instead, we simply provide estimates of IUTs, which may be useful for process/resource scheduling.

First, we determine a lower bound on
\tau_{i}^{u}

\eta_{k}

Theorem 2

(Collocation Triggering (CT))
.

Let
L_{p,k}
be the Lipschitz constant of the approximating polynomial of the state used in segment
T_{k}

\begin{split}&\tau_{i}^{\text{CT}}=\sup\Bigg\{\tau_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}|\\
&\qquad\Bigg(\left\lVert\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}_{t}}\int_{T_{k}}L_{p,k}\frac{\left|\tau-t_{k}^{\text{col}}\right|}{(w+1)}+L_{x}\frac{\left|\tilde{x}_{i}(t_{k}^{\text{col}})-\tilde{x}_{i}(\tau)\right|}{(w+1)}\mathrm{d}\tau\right\rVert_{M}+\\
&\qquad\left\lVert M\right\rVert(2\hat{\theta}+\tau_{i}\hat{v})\Bigg)e^{L_{x}\tau_{i}}\leq\Delta\Bigg\}\end{split}

(59)

calculated from the approximate solution
\tilde{x}(\cdot)
to problem (
9
) at time
t_{i}^{u}
satisfies
\left\lVert\delta(t_{i}^{u}+\tau_{i})\right\rVert_{M}\leq\Delta,\forall\tau\leq\tau_{i}^{\text{CT}}

Proof.

The proof is similar to that of Theorem
1

We begin by noting that

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{t}&\left|\dot{\tilde{x}}_{i}(\tau)-f_{i}(\tau,\tilde{x}(\tau),u(\tau))\right|\mathrm{d}\tau\leq\\
&\qquad\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}_{t}}\int_{T_{k}}\left|\dot{\tilde{x}}_{i}(t)-f_{i}(\tau,\tilde{x}(\tau),u(\tau))\right|\mathrm{d}\tau,\end{split}

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}_{t}}\int_{T_{k}}\Big|\dot{\tilde{x}}_{i}(\tau)-\dot{\tilde{x}}_{i}(t_{k}^{\text{col}})+

(61)

\displaystyle\qquad f_{i}(t_{k}^{\text{col}},\tilde{x}(t_{k}^{\text{col}}),u(t_{k}^{\text{col}}))-f_{i}(\tau,\tilde{x}(\tau),u(\tau))\Big|\mathrm{d}\tau

(68)

where the second inequality results from noting that all polynomials

V
Constraint tightening

The proposed triggering conditions do not account for state proximity to constraint bounds. We wish to guarantee satisfaction of constraints (
Pc
) for the entire prediction horizon
t\in[t_{0},t_{f}]
.
However, when solving the transcribed NLP corresponding to problem (
9
), the constraints
c(\tilde{x}(t),u(t),t)\leq 0
are only enforced on the approximate trajectory
\tilde{x}(\cdot)
at a finite number of time instances: the mesh points
\mathcal{T}_{m}
.
One way to guarantee that the actual state
x(\cdot)
complies with state constraints is to employ constraint tightening methods.
For the following analysis we only consider the case where
c(\tilde{x}(t),u(t),t)\leq 0
is equivalent to the inclusion

\tilde{x}(t)\in\mathbb{X},\forall t\in[t_{0},t_{f}],

(79)

where the constraint set
\mathbb{X}
satisfies the following assumption.

Assumption 1
.

The set
\mathbb{X}
is closed and convex, and the reduced set

\mathbb{X}\ominus\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid x\leq\Delta\right\},

(80)

where
\ominus
denotes the Pontryagin difference, is non-empty.

The Pontryagin difference of two sets
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
is defined as
\mathcal{A}\ominus\mathcal{B}:=\{a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}|a+b\in\mathcal{A},\forall b\in\mathcal{B}\}
.
The Minkowski sum
\oplus
of
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
is
\mathcal{A}\oplus\mathcal{B}:=\{a+b|a\in\mathcal{A},b\in\mathcal{B}\}
. We would not expect this assumption to be particularly restrictive, since
\Delta
will be quite small in practice.
In the following result we evaluate by how much the state constraints on the approximate solution
\tilde{x}
must be tightened in order to guarantee that the actual state
x(t)\in\mathbb{X}
.

Lemma 1
.

Consider a mesh refinement scheme using the relative local error (
19
) or absolute local error (
15
) as a termination criteria.
If the approximate trajectory at time
t_{k}^{u}+s,s\geq 0,
predicted from measurement at time
t_{k}^{u}
satisfies
\tilde{x}(t_{k}^{u},s)\in\mathbb{X}_{s},
where

\mathbb{X}_{s}:=\mathbb{X}\ominus\mathcal{B}_{s},

(81)

and the ball
\mathcal{B}_{s}
is defined as

\begin{split}\mathcal{B}_{s}:=&\Bigg\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\\
&\qquad\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{M}\leq\min\Big\{\left(\left\lVert\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}_{s}}\hat{\xi}\right\rVert_{M}+\left\lVert M\right\rVert(2\hat{\theta}+s\hat{v})\right)e^{L_{x}s},\Delta\Big\}\Bigg\},\end{split}

(82)

where
\hat{\xi}:=\left(\hat{w}+1\right)\hat{\epsilon}
or
\hat{\xi}:=\hat{\eta}
depending on the refinement termination criteria, then the actual state trajectory satisfies
x(s)\in\mathbb{X}
.

Proof.

Take the inequality (
77
) used in the proof of Theorem
3
, but using error bounds instead of actual errors. We directly bound the norm between the approximate predicted and measured state at time
t_{k}^{u}+s
by

\left\lVert\delta(t_{k}^{u}+s)\right\rVert_{M}\leq\left(\left\lVert\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}_{s}}\hat{\xi}\right\rVert_{M}+\left\lVert M\right\rVert(2\hat{\theta}+s\hat{v})\right)e^{L_{x}s}.

(83)

Since the ETC scheme guarantees that the control and associated prediction is updated if
\left\lVert\delta(s)\right\rVert\geq\Delta
, we can make the bound less conservative, to become

\left\lVert\delta(t_{k}^{u}+s)\right\rVert_{M}\leq\min\left\{\left(\left\lVert\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}_{s}}\hat{\xi}\right\rVert_{M}+\left\lVert M\right\rVert(2\hat{\theta}+s\hat{v})\right)e^{L_{x}s},\Delta\right\}.

(84)

Tightening the constraint set
\mathbb{X}
by the RHS of this inequality results in
\mathbb{X}_{s}
and guarantees that the actual trajectory
x(t)\in\mathbb{X},\forall t\in[t_{k}^{u},t_{k+1}^{u}]
.
By Assumption
1
,
\mathbb{X}_{s}
is nonempty
\forall s\in[t_{k}^{u},t_{k}^{u}+T_{\text{op}})
.
∎

Lemma
1
shows that the amount of constraint tightening required depends on the solution accuracy, allowed prediction error
\Delta
, as well as measurement and disturbance noise bounds. This tightening is shown in Figure
2
.
However, the constraint

Theorem 4
.

Consider a mesh refinement scheme using the relative local error (
19
) or absolute local error (
15
) as a termination criterion. If we define

\alpha_{k}\geq\left\lVert\hat{\xi}\right\rVert+L_{f}\left|T_{k}\right|+g(t_{k}^{m},t_{k+1}^{m})

(87)

and the predicted trajectory
\tilde{x}(\cdot)
satisfies

d_{\mathbb{X}_{t_{k}^{m}}}(\tilde{x}(t_{k}^{m}))\leq-\alpha_{k},\forall k\in\mathcal{K}_{h},

(88)

then

d_{\mathbb{X}_{s}}(\tilde{x}(s))\leq 0,\forall s\in[t_{k}^{m},t_{k+1}^{m}],k\in\mathcal{K}_{h}

(89)

and

x(t)\in\mathbb{X},\forall s\in[t_{i}^{u},t_{i+1}^{u}].

(90)

Proof.

From the definition of the tightened constraint set
\mathbb{X}_{s}
in Lemma
1
,

\mathbb{X}_{s}\subseteq\mathbb{X}_{t_{k}^{m}},\forall s\in(t_{k}^{m},t_{k+1}^{m}].

(91)

We use the notation
\partial\mathcal{A}
to denote the boundary of the set
\mathcal{A}
.
Since
\mathbb{X}
is convex by Assumption
1
, the sets
\mathbb{X}_{s}
will be convex by construction. Therefore the shortest distance from any point in
\partial\mathbb{X}_{s}
to
\partial\mathbb{X}_{t_{k}^{m}}
satisfy

\min_{y_{1}\in\partial\mathbb{X}_{s}}\left\lVert x_{1}-y_{1}\right\rVert=\min_{y_{2}\in\partial\mathbb{X}_{s}}\left\lVert x_{2}-y_{2}\right\rVert,\forall x_{1},x_{2}\in\partial\mathbb{X}_{t_{k}^{m}}.

(92)

From the definition of the sets
\mathbb{X}_{s}
in (
81
) we define this distance
g:\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
between
\mathbb{X}_{S}
and
\mathbb{X}_{t_{k}^{m}}
as

g(s,t_{k}^{m}):=\min\big\{(\beta+\left\lVert M\right\rVert\hat{v}s)e^{L_{x}s},\Delta\big\}\\
-\min\big\{(\beta+\left\lVert M\right\rVert\hat{v}t_{k}^{m})e^{L_{x}t_{k}^{m}},\Delta\big\},

(93)

where,

\beta:=\left\lVert\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{k+1}^{m}}\hat{\xi}\right\rVert_{M}+\left\lVert M\right\rVert 2\hat{\theta}

(94)

From Assumption
1
on the non-emptiness of
\mathbb{X}_{s}
,
g(\cdot)
is always well defined.
We now consider the approximate state trajectory
\tilde{x}(\cdot)
at times
t_{k}^{m}
and
s\geq t_{k}^{m}
.
Then,

\displaystyle\left|d_{\mathbb{X}_{s}}(\tilde{x}(s))-d_{\mathbb{X}_{t_{k}^{m}}}(\tilde{x}(t_{k}^{m}))\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\tilde{x}(s)-\tilde{x}(t_{k}^{m})\right\rVert+g(s,t_{k}^{m})

(95)

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\int_{t_{k}^{m}}^{s}\dot{\tilde{x}}(t)\mathrm{d}t\right\rVert+g(s,t_{k}^{m})

(96)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{t_{k}^{m}}^{s}\left\lVert\dot{\tilde{x}}(t)\right\rVert\mathrm{d}t+g(s,t_{k}^{m}).

(97)

Application of the triangle inequality to the mesh refinement conditions (
19
) yields

\begin{split}\int_{T_{k}}\left\lVert\dot{\tilde{x}}(t)\right\rVert\mathrm{d}t-\int_{T_{k}}\left\lVert f(t,\tilde{x}(t),u(t))\right\rVert\mathrm{d}t\leq\\
\int_{T_{k}}\left\lVert\dot{\tilde{x}}(t)-f(t,\tilde{x}(t),u(t))\right\rVert\mathrm{d}t\leq\left\lVert\hat{\xi}\right\rVert,\end{split}

(98)

from which

\begin{split}\int_{t_{k}^{m}}^{s}\left\lVert\dot{\tilde{x}}(t)\right\rVert\mathrm{d}t&\leq\int_{T_{k}}\left\lVert\dot{\tilde{x}}(t)\right\rVert\mathrm{d}t\leq\left\lVert\hat{\xi}\right\rVert+\int_{T_{k}}\left\lVert f(t,\tilde{x}(t),u(t))\right\rVert\mathrm{d}t\\
&\leq\left\lVert\hat{\xi}\right\rVert+L_{f}\left|T_{k}\right|.\end{split}

(99)

Therefore we may bound the distance as

d_{\mathbb{X}_{s}}(\tilde{x}(s))-d_{\mathbb{X}_{t_{k}^{m}}}(\tilde{x}(t_{k}^{m}))\leq\left\lVert\hat{\xi}\right\rVert+L_{f}\left|T_{k}\right|+g(s,t_{k}^{m}).

(100)

We now need to prove that there is no
s<t_{k+1}^{m}
for which
d_{\mathbb{X}_{s}}(\tilde{x}(s))=0
. Following the argument in
[
23
]
, assume
\exists s_{1}<t_{k+1}^{m}
such that

d_{\mathbb{X}_{s_{1}}}(\tilde{x}(s_{1}))=0\text{ and }d_{\mathbb{X}_{s}}(\tilde{x}(s))>0,\forall s\in(s_{1},t_{k+1}^{m}].

(101)

Therefore,

\displaystyle d_{\mathbb{X}_{s_{1}}}(\tilde{x}(s_{1}))

\displaystyle\leq d_{\mathbb{X}_{t_{k}^{m}}}(\tilde{x}(t_{k}^{m}))+\left\lVert\hat{\xi}\right\rVert+L_{f}(s_{1}-t_{k}^{m})+g(s_{1},t_{k}^{m})

(102)

\displaystyle\leq-\alpha_{k}+\left\lVert\hat{\xi}\right\rVert+L_{f}(s_{1}-t_{k}^{m})+g(s_{1},t_{k}^{m}))

(103)

\displaystyle<-\alpha_{k}+\left\lVert\hat{\xi}\right\rVert+L_{f}\left|T_{k}\right|+g(t_{k}^{m},t_{k+1}^{m})\leq 0.

(104)

This contradicts our assumption. Therefore we may conclude that

d_{\mathbb{X}_{s}}(\tilde{x}(s))\leq 0,\forall s\in[t_{k}^{m},t_{k+1}^{m}],\forall k\in\mathcal{K}_{h}.

(105)

Combining this with the results of Lemma
1
guarantees

d_{\mathbb{X}}(x(t))\leq 0,\forall t\in[t_{i}^{u},t_{i+1}^{u}].

(106)

Theorem
4
shows that the constraints enforced at each mesh point
t_{k}^{m}
should be tightened by an amount that is affine in the length of the succeeding mesh segment
\left|T_{k}\right|
and dependent on the metric of allowable ODE errror
\hat{\xi}
.
This guarantees that the approximate trajectory is admissible for all time. The constraints must be further tightened by an amount
g(\cdot)
that relates the difference between approximate and actual trajectories.

We could use Theorem
4
to propose an OCP solution scheme where the constraints are successively loosened at each iteration of the refinement. As the mesh becomes increasingly refined, the constraints will become more and more relaxed. In this way constraint satisfaction of the true state
x(t)
would be guaranteed at every iteration of the refinement procedure. However, we find in practice that this procedure is particularly conservative, especially for very coarse meshes. This may affect the feasibility of the NLP solved in the initial iterations of the refinement procedure. We could instead start with a finer mesh, but this conversely has adverse effects on solution time.

Instead we consider a two-part procedure detailed in Algorithm
1
. From the condition in Theorem
4
,

\left|d_{\mathbb{X}_{s}}(\tilde{x}(s))-d_{\mathbb{X}_{t_{k}^{m}}}(\tilde{x}(t_{k}^{m}))\right|\leq\left\lVert\tilde{x}(s)-\tilde{x}(t_{k}^{m})\right\rVert+g(s,t_{k}^{m}),

(107)

we can guarantee that
d_{\mathbb{X}_{t_{k+1}^{m}}}(\tilde{x}(t_{k+1}^{m}))\leq 0
if

d_{\mathbb{X}_{t_{k}^{m}}}(\tilde{x}(t_{k}^{m}))\leq\underbrace{\max_{s\in T_{k}}\left\lVert\tilde{x}(s)-\tilde{x}(t_{k}^{m})\right\rVert+g(t_{k+1}^{m},t_{k}^{m})}_{\Upsilon(\tilde{x},T_{k})}.

(108)

We can similarly guarantee that
d_{\mathbb{X}_{t_{k}^{m}}}(\tilde{x}(t_{k}^{m}))\leq 0

if

d_{\mathbb{X}_{t_{k+1}^{m}}}(\tilde{x}(t_{k+1}^{m}))\leq\Upsilon(\tilde{x},T_{k}).

(109)

In practice, the difference is that (
108
) tightens constraints forwards in time, while (
109
) passes through the constraints backwards in time.

The left and right hand sides of (
108
) and (
109
) can be calculated from the approximate prediction
\tilde{x}(t)
. In Algorithm
1
we propose first iterating through a typical mesh-refinement procedure (lines
5
–
11
in Algorithm
1
) to generate a prediction
\tilde{x}
satisfying ODE error certificates in each segment.
This sufficiently accurate prediction is then used as a proxy to evaluate (
108
) on the refined mesh (lines
12
–
17
in Algorithm

If (
108
) is not satisfied at any time
t\in\mathcal{T}_{k}
then the constraint set is tightened by

\mathcal{B}_{\Upsilon_{k}}:=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\left\lVert x\right\rVert\leq\Upsilon(\tilde{x},T_{k})\right\},

(110)

as in line
13
, and the resulting NLP is solved. We may iterate our algorithm based on either the forwards inequality (
108
) or backwards inequality (
109

This procedure is repeated until (
108

\forall t\in T_{h}
.
Intuitively, we are robustifying the problem formulation to inaccuracies in our solution method.
This procedure is exemplified in Section

At this point we would like to briefly discuss input constraints.These are often simple box constraints of the form

u(t)\in\mathcal{U}:=\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:\underline{u}\leq u\leq\overline{u}\},

(111)

which may, for example, arise due to physical actuator limitations.
On the one hand, in our analysis we have assumed that the approximate solution
u(t)
is implemented exactly at the plant. So for the input we do not need to perform any equivalent of the constraint tightening detailed in Lemma

Instead we could seek to just guarantee that

satisfies (
111

111

\forall t\in T_{op}

For example, it may be the case that our optimal control software only allows for input discontinuities at (a particular subset of) mesh points. This is one approach taken that can be taken in ICOCS2
[

with the ‘multi-phase’ problem formulation. If we therefore know that

is going to be piecewise polynomial between mesh points then we may use a similar approach as in Theorem
2

We have found this approach to be overly conservative in the tightening unless an extremely dense mesh is used.
Additionally if a low order

VIII
Conclusions

Event- and self-triggering schemes attempt to reduce performance loss due to the growth in uncertainty as the system evolves in open-loop. Typically, uncertainty arising in the physical world may at best be unmodelled and at worst unstructured. However, uncertainty arising due to the solution of (
9
) is highly structured, therefore it should not be treated equivalently to, say,
v(t)
.

This is the first work that has considered using the accuracy of the solution of an optimal control problem as a metric for triggering times. This gives us the potential to use cheaper, less accurate, solutions without potentially sacrificing the guarantees of a triggered model predictive control scheme.
We have considered the use of approximate system predictions arising through numerical direct collocation solutions of optimal control problems in event-triggered control schemes. A commonly used metric for refining the state approximations has allowed us to guarantee a minimum inter-update time
\underline{\tau}^{u}
of the event-triggered control. If additionally the direct collocation software returns the approximation error metric as part of the solution, we may achieve a better estimate of the inter-update interval. Although the derived bounds may be conservative, they give us the ability to trade-off the computation burden of a single control update with the frequency of control updates.

This theme is also of importance in simulation of event- or self-triggered control which require potentially unstable numerical simulations to be run in open-loop for extended time steps, without the stabilizing effect of periodic state feedback.
Further work may consider the use of path constraint violations as a metric for mesh refinement and/or triggering.
