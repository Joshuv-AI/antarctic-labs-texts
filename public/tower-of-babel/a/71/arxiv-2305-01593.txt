Faster 0-1-Knapsack via Near-Convex Min-Plus-Convolution
Karl Bringmann, Alejandro Cassis

Abstract
We revisit the classic 0-1-Knapsack problem, in which we are given n n items with their weights and profits as well as a weight budget W W , and the goal is to find a subset of items of total weight at most W W that maximizes the total profit. We study pseudopolynomial-time algorithms parameterized by the largest profit of any item p max p_{\max} , and the largest weight of any item w max w_{\max} . Our main result are algorithms for 0-1-Knapsack running in time O ~ ​ ( n ​ w max ​ p max 2 / 3 ) \widetilde{O}(n\,w_{\max}\,p_{\max}^{2/3}) and O ~ ​ ( n ​ p max ​ w max 2 / 3 ) \widetilde{O}(n\,p_{\max}\,w_{\max}^{2/3}) , improving upon an algorithm in time O ⁡ ( n ​ p max ​ w max ) O(n\,p_{\max}\,w_{\max}) by Pisinger [J. Algorithms ’99]. In the regime p max ≈ w max ≈ n p_{\max}\approx w_{\max}\approx n (and W ≈ OPT ≈ n 2 W\approx\mathrm{OPT}\approx n^{2} ) our algorithms are the first to break the cubic barrier n 3 n^{3} . To obtain our result, we give an efficient algorithm to compute the min-plus convolution of near-convex functions. More precisely, we say that a function f : [ n ] ↦ 𝐙 f\colon[n]\mapsto\mathbf{Z} is Δ \Delta -near convex with Δ ≥ 1 \Delta\geq 1 , if there is a convex function f ˘ \breve{f} such that f ˘ ​ ( i ) ≤ f ⁡ ( i ) ≤ f ˘ ​ ( i ) + Δ \breve{f}(i)\leq f(i)\leq\breve{f}(i)+\Delta for every i i . Min-Plus Convolution

Given functions
f,g\colon[\,n\,]\mapsto\mathbf{Z}
, their min-plus convolution is the function
h\colon[\,2n\,]\mapsto\mathbf{Z}
defined as
h(x)=\min_{x^{\prime}}f(x^{\prime})+g(x-x^{\prime})
for
x\in[\,2n\,]
.
This can be trivially computed in time
O(n^{2})

n^{2}/2^{\Omega(\sqrt{\log n})}

[

Wil18

CW21
]

The lack of faster algorithms has led to the
Min-Plus Convolution Hypothesis

[

,
KPS17
]

Despite this hypothesis, there are structured instances of min-plus convolution that can be solved faster
[

,
BHSS18
,
BHWZ94
,
CL15
,

]
.

Our
Theorems

1
and
2
fall into the same category of improvements, as we design an efficient algorithm for a new class of structured instances of min-plus convolution, namely

functions:
We say that
f\colon[\,n\,]\mapsto\mathbf{Z}
is
\Delta
-near convex, if there is a convex function
\breve{f}\colon[\,n\,]\mapsto\mathbf{Q}
such that
\breve{f}(i)\leq f(i)\leq\breve{f}(i)+\Delta

Our theorem reads as follows:

Theorem 3

(Near Convex MinPlus Convolution)
.

Let
f\colon[\,n\,]\mapsto[\,-U\,.\,.\,U\,]
, and
g\colon[\,m\,]\mapsto[\,-U\,.\,.\,U\,]
be given as inputs where
n,m,U\in\mathbf{N}
.
Let
\Delta\geq 1
such that both
f
and
g
are
\Delta
-near convex.
Then the min-plus convolution of
f
and
g
can be computed in time
\widetilde{O}((n+m)\cdot\Delta)
.

We view our
Theorem

3
as a replacement for the prediction technique by Bateni et al.
[
BHSS18
]
. Indeed, all uses of the prediction technique exploit near-convexity to ensure its preconditions, and thus all uses that we are aware of can be replaced by our
Theorem

3
. Since the prediction technique is both difficult to state and difficult to apply, we view our
Theorem

3
as replacing the prediction technique by an
easily applicable tool with a concise statement
.
Moreover,
Theorem

3
provides a new tool for structured instances of min-plus convolution, which we use in this paper to make progress on 0-1-Knapsack, and which we believe has wider applicability.

Our Techniques

Our approach to prove
Theorem

3
is as follows.
Let
f,g\colon[\,n\,]\mapsto\mathbf{Z}
be the input functions, and let
h
be their min-plus convolution, which we aim to compute.
First we observe that we can obtain the convex approximations
\breve{f},\breve{g}
witnessing the
\Delta
-near convexity of
f
and
g
,
and compute their min-plus convolution
\breve{h}
efficiently.
By exploiting
\breve{h}
and the convexity of
\breve{f}
and
\breve{g}
, we identify a structured set
R\subseteq[\,n\,]^{2}

with the property that any
(i,j)\in[\,n\,]^{2}\setminus R
satisfies
f(i)+g(j)>h(j)
.
Then, we give a simple recursive algorithm to cover
R
with a collection
\mathcal{C}
of disjoint dyadic boxes
I\times J
, where
(I,J)\in\mathcal{C}
satisfies
I,J\subseteq[\,n\,]
and
I\times J\subseteq R
.
Thus, we can infer
h
by computing the
sumset

A:=\{\,(i,f(i))\mid i\in I\,\}+\{\,(j,g(j))\mid j\in J\,\}
and taking
h(k)=\min\{\,y\mid(k,y)\in A\,\}
for every
(I,J)\in\mathcal{C}
.
To do this efficiently we observe that inside
I
and
J
, the functions
f[I]
and
g[J]
are close to linear functions with the same slope up to an additive error of
\pm O(\Delta)
(which follows from their
\Delta
-near convexity).
This implies that their sumset is small; more precisely it has size
O((|I|+|J|)\Delta)
.
Finally, we make use of known tools that can compute a sumset in time proportional to its size.
The idea of identifying a covering with small sumsets to efficiently compute the min-plus convolution is inspired by Chan and Lewenstein’s
[
CL15
]
algorithm for bounded monotone sequences (in which they do not use convexity in any form).
Our algorithm shares some similarities with the prediction technique by Bateni et al.
[
BHSS18
]
.
In particular, the covering by dyadic boxes where functions are near-linear resembles the way in which they exploit the intervals
[\,x_{i}\,.\,.\,y_{i}\,]
required by their algorithm.

To obtain
Theorems

1
and
2
, we follow the
partition and convolve
paradigm that has been used in many recent algorithms for Subset Sum and Knapsack, see e.g.
[
Bri17
,
BHSS18
,
BC22
,
JR19
,
Cha18
,
KX19
,
DJM23
,
BN21
]
.
Specifically, we randomly split the items into
q
groups. In each group, we use the standard dynamic programming algorithm to compute for each weight
i
the maximum profit
f(i)
attainable with weight at most
i
using items from that group. Then we combine the functions
f
over all groups by min-plus convolution. The crucial observation is that due to the random splitting we only need to compute the values
f(i)
in a small weight interval.

Further Related Work

Cygan et al.
[
CMWW19
]
and Künnemann et al.
[
KPS17
]
showed that under the Min-Plus Convolution Hypothesis,
there is no truly subquadratic algorithm for Knapsack on instances with
w_{\max},W=\Theta(n)
and
p_{\max},\mathrm{OPT}=\Omega(n^{2})
,
and symmetrically, on instances with
p_{\max},\mathrm{OPT}=\Theta(n)
and
w_{\max},W=\Omega(n^{2})
.
This implies that Bellman’s dynamic programming algorithm is conditionally optimal in these settings.

Pseudopolynomial-time algorithms parameterized by
p_{\max}
and
w_{\max}
have also been studied for the closely related Unbounded Knapsack problem.
Here, the setup is the same as for 0-1 Knapsack but now a solution might include an arbitrary number of copies of each item.
Chan and He
[
CH22
]
gave an algorithm for this problem in time
\widetilde{O}(n\cdot\min\{\,p_{\max},w_{\max}\,\})
, which is optimal under the Min-Plus Convolution Hypothesis.
Bringmann and Cassis
[
BC22
]
gave an algorithm in time
\widetilde{O}(n+(p_{\max}+w_{\max})^{1.5})
which is better when
w_{\max}\approx p_{\max}\approx n
.

For any

Faster 0-1 Knapsack Algorithm

In this section we prove
Theorem

1
.
Let
(\mathcal{I},W)
be a 0-1 Knapsack instance. Throughout, we denote the number of items by
n:=|\mathcal{I}|
.
We identify the item set
\mathcal{I}
with
\{\,1,\dots,n\,\}
.
We represent a
solution
to the knapsack instance (i.e., a subset of
\mathcal{I}
), by an indicator vector
x\in\{\,0,1\,\}^{n}
.
For a subset of the items
\mathcal{J}\subseteq\mathcal{I}
, we put
w_{\mathcal{J}}(x):=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{J}}w_{i}x_{i}
and
p_{\mathcal{J}}(x):=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{J}}p_{i}x_{i}
.
We define the profit sequence
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{I}}[\cdot]
, where for each
j\in\mathbf{N}
we have

\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{I}}[j]=\max\{\,p_{\mathcal{I}}(x)\mid x\in\{\,0,1\,\}^{n},w_{\mathcal{I}}(x)\leq j\,\}.

Observe that
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{I}}
is monotone non-decreasing, and that
\mathrm{OPT}=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{I}}[W]
.
The textbook way to compute
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{I}}[\,0\,.\,.\,j\,]
is to use dynamic programming:

Fact 6
.

For any
j\in\mathbf{N}
the sequence
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{I}}[\,0\,.\,.\,j\,]
can be computed in time
O(nj)
.

Before presenting the algorithm, we make two simple observations about the given Knapsack instance
(\mathcal{I},W)
.
First, by ignoring items with weight larger than the capacity
W
, we can assume without loss of generality that
w_{\max}\leq W
.
Now every single item is a feasible solution, so we have
p_{\max}\leq\mathrm{OPT}
.
Second, observe that if
W\geq n\cdot w_{\max}
, then the instance is trivial since we can pack all items.
Thus, we can assume without loss of generality that
W\leq n\cdot w_{\max}
.
Moreover, since any feasible solution consists of at most all the
n
items, it follows that
\mathrm{OPT}\leq n\cdot p_{\max}
.

We now describe the algorithm.
Set parameters
q:=\min\{\,(n/p_{\max})^{2/3}(W/w_{\max})^{1/3},W/w_{\max}\,\}

We start by splitting the items
\mathcal{I}

The idea will be to compute an array

Correctness

We start by analyzing the correctness of the algorithm.
The following lemma shows that the weight of any solution restricted to one of the sets
\mathcal{I}^{\ell}_{j}
is concentrated around its expectation.

Lemma 7

(Concentration)
.

Let
x\in\{\,0,1\,\}^{n}
be a solution to the given Knapsack instance.
Fix a level
\ell\in[\,0\,.\,.\,\log q\,]
and
j\in[\,1\,.\,.\,q/2^{\ell}\,]
.
Then, with probability at least
1-1/n^{4}
it holds that:

\left|w_{\mathcal{I}^{\ell}_{j}}(x)-\frac{w_{\mathcal{I}}(x)\cdot 2^{\ell}}{q}\right|\leq\sqrt{\Delta 2^{\ell}}\cdot 10\log n.

Proof.

Recall that the item set
\mathcal{I}
is partitioned randomly into
\mathcal{I}^{0}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}^{0}_{q}
.
Thus, observe that
\mathcal{I}^{\ell}_{j}
is a random subset of
\mathcal{I}
, where each item is included with probability
p:=2^{\ell}/q
.
For
i\in[\,1\,.\,.\,n\,]
, let
Z_{i}
be a random variable which equals
w_{i}\cdot x_{i}
with probability
p
, and
0
with probability
1-p
.
Then, observe that
w_{\mathcal{I}^{\ell}_{j}}(x)
is distributed as
Z:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}
, and therefore,
\Ex(Z)=w_{\mathcal{I}}(x)p
.

To prove the statement, we will use Bernstein’s inequality (see e.g.
[
DP09
, Theorem 1.2]
) which states that

\displaystyle\Pr(|Z-\Ex(Z)|\geq t)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{2\Var(Z)+\tfrac{2}{3}t\cdot w_{\max}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(-\min\left\{\frac{t^{2}}{4\Var(Z)},\frac{t}{2w_{\max}}\right\}\right).

(1)

Set
t:=\sqrt{p\cdot w_{\max}W}\cdot 10\log n
.
We first bound
t^{2}/(4\Var(Z))
.
Note that we can give an upper bound on the variance as follows:

\Var(Z)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}p(1-p)w_{i}^{2}x_{i}^{2}\leq p\cdot w_{\max}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}x_{i}=p\cdot w_{\max}w_{\mathcal{I}}(x)\leq p\cdot w_{\max}W.

Therefore
t^{2}/(4\Var(Z))\geq 10\log n
.
Next, we bound
t/(2w_{\max})
. Using that
q\leq W/w_{\max}
, we have that
p=\tfrac{2^{\ell}}{q}\geq\tfrac{w_{\max}2^{\ell}}{W}\geq\tfrac{w_{\max}}{W}
.
Thus,

\frac{t}{2w_{\max}}=\frac{\sqrt{p\cdot w_{\max}W}\cdot 10\log n}{2w_{\max}}\geq 5\log n.

Combining the above, we obtain from (
1
) that

|w_{\mathcal{I}^{\ell}_{j}}(x)-w_{\mathcal{I}}(x)2^{\ell}/q|=|Z-\Ex(Z)|\leq t=\sqrt{pw_{\max}W}\cdot 10\log n=\sqrt{\Delta 2^{\ell}}\cdot 10\log n

holds with probability at least
1-2/n^{5}\geq 1-1/n^{4}
.
∎

Using

around

Let

C^{\ell}_{j}[w_{\mathcal{I}^{\ell}_{j}}(x)]\geq p_{\mathcal{I}^{\ell}_{j}}(x)

By

7
, for each
\ell\in[\,0\,.\,.\,\log q\,]
and

(2)

Note that

We start by showing the first item of the statement.

By (

\displaystyle|w_{\mathcal{I}^{\ell}_{j}}(x)-W2^{\ell}/q|

The last inequality holds since we can use that

Next, we prove the second item of the lemma by induction. Consider the base case

Now we proceed with the inductive step: Fix

Lemma 9

(Correctness of

1
)
.

Let

We can check in linear time

In particular,
x^{*}

, it holds that
C_{1}^{\log q}[w_{\mathcal{I}}(x^{*})]\geq p_{\mathcal{I}}(x^{*})=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{I}}[w_{\mathcal{I}}(x^{*})]

The following lemma shows that the max-plus convolution of two near-concave sequences remains near-concave.

Lemma 12
.

Let
f\colon[\,n\,]\mapsto\mathbf{Z}
be
\Delta_{f}
-near concave and
g\colon[\,m\,]\mapsto\mathbf{Z}
be
\Delta_{g}
-near concave.
Then,
h:=\textsc{MaxConv}(f,g)
is
\Delta_{h}
-near concave with
\Delta_{h}\leq\max\{\,\Delta_{f},\Delta_{g}\,\}
.

Proof.

Let
\breve{f},\breve{g}
be pointwise minimal concave functions with
\breve{f}\geq f
,
\breve{g}\geq g

and let
\breve{h}:=\textsc{MaxConv}(\breve{f},\breve{g})
.
We will show that
\breve{h}\geq h\geq\breve{h}-\max\{\,\Delta_{f},\Delta_{g}\,\}
, which implies the statement.

To show that
\breve{h}\geq h
, fix
k\in[\,n+m\,]
and let
i^{*}
be a witness for
h(k)
, i.e.,
h(k)=f(i^{*})+g(k-i^{*})
.
Then,
\breve{h}(k)\geq\breve{f}(i^{*})+\breve{g}(k-i^{*})\geq f(i^{*})+g(k-i^{*})=h(k)
. So
\breve{h}\geq h
.

To show that
h\geq\breve{h}-\Delta
for
\Delta:=\max\{\,\Delta_{f},\Delta_{g}\,\}
, fix
k\in[\,n+m\,]
and let
i^{*}
be a witness for
\breve{h}(k)
, i.e.,
\breve{h}(k)=\breve{f}(i^{*})+\breve{g}(k-i^{*})
.
Note that
\breve{f}
is piecewise a linear interpolation between points on
f
.
In particular, there exist
i_{L}\leq i^{*}\leq i_{R}
such that
\breve{f}(i_{L})=f(i_{L}),\breve{f}(i_{R})=f(i_{R})
and

\breve{f}(i)
is linear for
i\in[\,i_{L}\,.\,.\,i_{R}\,]
.
Similarly, for
j^{*}:=k-i^{*}
there exist
j_{L}\leq j^{*}\leq j_{R}
such that
\breve{g}(j_{L})=g(j_{L}),\breve{g}(j_{R})=g(j_{R})

and
\breve{g}(j)
is linear for
j\in[\,j_{L}\,.\,.\,j_{R}\,]
.
We pick the maximum
i_{L},j_{L}
and minimum
i_{R},j_{R}
with this property.

Let
\hat{\imath}_{L}:=\max\{\,i_{L},k-j_{R}\,\},\hat{\imath}_{R}:=\min\{\,i_{R},k-j_{L}\,\}
.
Observe that the function
\breve{s}(i):=\breve{f}(i)+\breve{g}(k-i)
is linear for
i\in[\,\hat{\imath}_{L}\,.\,.\,\hat{\imath}_{R}\,]
,
and that
\hat{\imath}_{L}\leq i^{*}\leq\hat{\imath}_{R}
.
Moreover, by definition of
\breve{h}
we have that
\breve{s}(i)=\breve{f}(i)+\breve{g}(k-i)\leq\breve{h}(k)
for
i\in[\,\hat{\imath}_{L}\,.\,.\,\hat{\imath}_{R}\,]
.
Since
i^{*}
is a witness of
\breve{h}(k)
, we have
\breve{s}(i^{*})=\breve{h}(k)
.
Combining the above, we obtain that
\breve{s}(i)=\breve{h}(k)
for all
i\in[\,\hat{\imath}_{L}\,.\,.\,\hat{\imath}_{R}\,]
.
In particular,
\breve{f}(\hat{\imath}_{L})+\breve{g}(k-\hat{\imath}_{L})=\breve{h}(k)
, and thus

\displaystyle h(k)

\displaystyle\geq f(\hat{\imath}_{L})+g(k-\hat{\imath}_{L})

\displaystyle=\breve{f}(\hat{\imath}_{L})+\breve{g}(k-\hat{\imath}_{L})+(f(\hat{\imath}_{L})-\breve{f}(\hat{\imath}_{L}))+(g(k-\hat{\imath}_{L})-\breve{g}(k-\hat{\imath}_{L}))

\displaystyle=\breve{h}(k)+(f(\hat{\imath}_{L})-\breve{f}(\hat{\imath}_{L}))+(g(k-\hat{\imath}_{L})-\breve{g}(k-\hat{\imath}_{L})).

(3)

Finally, since
\hat{\imath}_{L}\in\{\,i_{L},k-j_{R}\,\}
and
f(i_{L})=\breve{f}(i_{L})
and
g(j_{R})=\breve{g}(j_{R})
,
one of the two last summands in (
3
) must be 0.
Using the near-concavity of
f
and
g
, we can bound the other summand by
f(\hat{\imath}_{L})-\breve{f}(\hat{\imath}_{L})\geq-\Delta_{f}
or

g(k-\hat{\imath}_{L})-\breve{g}(k-\hat{\imath}_{L})\geq-\Delta_{g}
.
This yields
h(k)\geq\breve{h}(k)-\Delta_{f}
or
h(k)\geq\breve{h}(k)-\Delta_{g}
.
In any case, we conclude that
h(k)\geq\breve{h}(k)-\max\{\,\Delta_{f},\Delta_{g}\,\}
holds for every
k\in[\,n+m\,]
.
∎

The next lemma shows that the sequences we combine in

Lemma 13

(Near Concavity)
.

For every level
\ell\in[\,1\,.\,.\,q\,]
and every
j\in[\,1\,.\,.\,q/2^{\ell}\,]
, it holds that
C^{\ell}_{j}[J^{\ell}]
is

-near concave.

Proof.

We prove the statement using induction.
Focus in the base case
\ell=0
.
For each
j\in[\,1\,.\,.\,q\,]
, we have that
C_{j}^{0}[J^{0}]=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{I}_{j}^{0}}[J^{0}]
.
In what follows, we argue that
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{I}_{j}^{0}}
is
p_{\max}
-near concave.
Consider the fractional greedy solution for Knapsack: sort the items
(p_{1},w_{1}),\dots,(p_{m},w_{m})
in
\mathcal{I}_{j}^{0}
non-decreasingly by their profit-to-weight ratio, i.e., so that
p_{1}/w_{1}\geq p_{2}/w_{2}\geq\cdots\geq p_{m}/w_{m}
.
Let
M:=\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}
.
Then, construct the sequence
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}[\,0\,.\,.\,M\,]
by setting breakpoints

\tilde{\mathcal{P}}[0]=0,\,\tilde{\mathcal{P}}[w_{1}]=p_{1},\,\tilde{\mathcal{P}}[w_{1}+w_{2}]=p_{1}+p_{2},\,\dots,\,\tilde{\mathcal{P}}[w_{1}+\cdots+w_{m}]=p_{1}+\cdots+p_{m},

and a linear interpolation between every pair of consecutive breakpoints.
In this way,
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}[i]
corresponds the optimal solution to the
fractional
version of Knapsack with capacity
i
, i.e.,
in the setting where items can be fractionally packed in a solution.

Claim 14
.

The sequence
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}
is concave, and it holds that
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}[i]\geq\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{I}_{j}^{0}}[i]\geq\tilde{\mathcal{P}}[i]-p_{\max}
for every

.

For each

\tilde{\mathcal{P}}
is concave.

For each
i\in[\,M\,]
, it holds that
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}[i]\geq\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{I}_{j}^{0}}[i]
since
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}[i]
is the optimal solution of the fractional Knapsack.
Moreover, observe that the solution attaining
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}[i]
contains at most one item allocated fractionally.
By removing that item, we obtain a feasible (integral) solution to the 0-1 Knapsack of capacity
i
, and the profit is reduced by at most
p_{\max}
.
This implies that
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{I}_{j}^{0}}[i]\geq\tilde{\mathcal{P}}[i]-p_{\max}
.

∎By
14
, we conclude that
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{I}_{j}^{0}}[\,0\,.\,.\,M\,]
is
p_{\max}
-near concave (see
Definition

5
), and therefore
C_{j}^{0}[J^{0}]
is as well, which completes the proof of the base case.

For the inductive step, consider a level
\ell>0
.
Fix a
j\in[\,1\,.\,.\,q/2^{\ell}\,]
.
By the inductive hypothesis,
C_{2j-1}^{\ell-1}[J^{\ell-1}]
and
C_{2j}^{\ell-1}[J^{\ell-1}]
are
p_{\max}
-near concave.
Thus, by

we obtain that
C_{j}^{\ell}[J^{\ell}]=\textsc{MaxConv}(C^{\ell-1}_{2j-1}[J^{\ell-1}],C^{\ell-1}_{2j}[J^{\ell-1}])

-near concave, completing the proof.
∎

Lemma 15
.

Fix a level
\ell\in[\,1\,.\,.\,q\,]
and an iteration

.
The computation of

Lemma 16

(Running Time of
Algorithm

1
)
.

Algorithm

1
runs in time

\widetilde{O}((p_{\max}W)^{2/3}(nw_{\max})^{1/3}+nw_{\max}).

Proof.

Recall that

(up to a factor of 2).
Since
W\leq nw_{\max}
, we have that

.
Moreover, since we assume without loss of generality that

, note that
q<1
if and only if

. This implies that
p_{\max}>n\sqrt{W/w_{\max}}

But in this case, the claimed running time is

, so the standard

Thus, we can assume without loss of generality that

is a valid choice for the number of groups in which we split the item set
\mathcal{I}
.

We start bounding the running time of the base case, i.e., the computation of the arrays
C_{j}^{0}
for
j\in[\,1\,.\,.\,q\,]
in
5:
.
By
6
, and the definition
\Delta=w_{\max}W/q
this takes time

O\left(\sum_{j=1}^{q}|\mathcal{I}_{j}^{0}|(\tfrac{W}{q}+\sqrt{\Delta}\eta)\right)=O\left(n(\tfrac{W}{q}+\sqrt{\Delta}\eta)\right)=\widetilde{O}\left(n\tfrac{W}{q}+n\sqrt{\tfrac{w_{\max}W}{q}}\right).

(4)

Now we bound the time of the combination step done in
9:
to

.
At level
\ell\in[\,1\,.\,.\,q\,]
and iteration

takes time
\widetilde{O}(p_{\max}\sqrt{\Delta 2^{\ell}})
by

.
Thus, we can bound the overall time as

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{\log q}\sum_{j=1}^{q/2^{\ell}}\widetilde{O}(p_{\max}\sqrt{\Delta 2^{\ell}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\log q}\frac{q}{2^{\ell}}\widetilde{O}\left(p_{\max}\sqrt{\tfrac{w_{\max}W}{q}2^{\ell}}\,\right)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\log q}\widetilde{O}\left(p_{\max}\sqrt{\tfrac{qw_{\max}W}{2^{\ell}}}\right),

.
Combining this with (

Recalling that

, we obtain overall time

Finally, note that by the AM-GM inequality we have

\displaystyle(p_{\max}W)^{1/3}(nw_{\max})^{2/3}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{(p_{\max}W)^{2/3}(nw_{\max})^{1/3}nw_{\max}}

\displaystyle\leq O((p_{\max}W)^{2/3}(nw_{\max})^{1/3}+nw_{\max}).

Thus, the overall running time is
\widetilde{O}((p_{\max}W)^{2/3}(nw_{\max})^{1/3}+nw_{\max})

∎

Reconstructing an optimal solution

So far we were only concerned with returning the optimal profit of a given Knapsack instance.
To reconstruct a solution

After running

Lemma 18

(Monotone Paths)
.

For any
\delta\geq 0
,
P^{-}_{\delta},P^{+}_{\delta}
are monotone paths.

Proof.

Since for each

\breve{h}(k)
has a witness, there is a 0-relevant point
(i,k-i)
.
Since every 0-relevant point is also
\delta
-relevant, it follows that
P^{-}_{\delta}
contains exactly one point
(i_{k},j_{k})
with

In the following, we show that

Since

Since

(5)

Since

Finally, we need some background on

.
Given
A,B\subseteq[\,-U\,.\,.\,U\,]^{2}
where
U\in\mathbf{N}
, we define
A+B=\{\,a+b\mid a\in A,b\in B\,\}
as their sumset, where the addition

is done componentwise.
The naive way to compute

takes time
O(|A|\cdot|B|)
.
For our application, we want to compute the sumset in time near linear in its size
|A+B|
.
For this end, we will use the following tool to compute
sparse non-negative convolution
.
Given vectors
P,Q\in\mathbf{N}^{n}
, their
convolution

P\star Q\in\mathbf{N}^{2n-1}
is defined coordinate-wise by

(P\star Q)[k]=\sum_{i+j=k}P[i]\cdot Q[j]
.

Theorem 20

(Deterministic Sparse Convolution
[
BFN22
]
)
.

There is a deterministic algorithm to compute the convolution of two nonnegative vectors
A,B\in\mathbf{N}^{n}

in time
O(t\polylog(n\Delta))
, where
t
is the number of non-zero entries in
A\star B
and
\Delta
is the largest entry in
A
and
B
.

See also
[
BFN21
]
for improvements in the log-factors at the cost of randomization
and
[
CH02
,
Nak20
,
GGdC20
]
for prior randomized algorithms with similar guarantees.

Corollary 21

(Output Sensitive Sumset Computation)
.

Given
A,B\subseteq[\,-U\,.\,.\,U\,]^{2}
, with
|A+B|\leq N
,
A+B
can be computed in time
\widetilde{O}(N)
.

Proof.

Let
A^{\prime}:=\{\,(x+U)\cdot 5U+(y+U)\mid(x,y)\in A\,\}
and similarly, let
B^{\prime}:=\{\,(x+U)\cdot 5U+(y+U)\mid(x,y)\in B\,\}
.
Observe that this is a one-to-one embedding of
A,B\subseteq[\,-U\,.\,.\,U\,]^{2}
into
A^{\prime},B^{\prime}\subseteq[\,\Theta(U^{2})\,]
.
Moreover, one can check that given
C^{\prime}:=A^{\prime}+B^{\prime}
we can infer
C:=A+B

(the choice of

prevents any interactions between coordinates when summing them up).

Thus, it suffices to compute
A^{\prime}+B^{\prime}
.
To this end, construct their indicator vectors
P_{A^{\prime}},P_{B^{\prime}}\in\mathbf{N}^{\Theta(U^{2})}
and compute the convolution

P_{C^{\prime}}=P_{A^{\prime}}\star P_{B^{\prime}}
. The non-zero entries in

correspond to the elements of
A^{\prime}+B^{\prime}
.
By
Theorem

20
, this runs in time
O(|A^{\prime}+B^{\prime}|\polylog(N,U))=\widetilde{O}(N)
.

Correctness

We start by analyzing the correctness of the algorithm.

Lemma 23

(Correctness of
Algorithm

3
)
.

\textsc{RecMinConv}([\,0\,.\,.\,n\,],[\,0\,.\,.\,m\,])
(
Algorithm

3
) correctly computes
h=\textsc{MinConv}(f,g)
.

Proof.

Let
k\in[\,n+m\,]
and consider a point
(i^{*},j^{*})
in diagonal
k
such that
f(i^{*})+g(j^{*})=h(k)
, i.e., a witness for
h(k)
.
We argue that some recursive call computes
f(i^{*})+g(j^{*})
.
This is clear in Case 4, as
(i^{*},j^{*})
is covered by one recursive subproblem.
It is also clear in Case 3, since then
f(i^{*})+g(j^{*})
is explicitly computed.

To finish correctness, we argue that
(i^{*},j^{*})
can never be in a subproblem to which Case 1 or 2 applies.
Recall that Case 1 applies to a subproblem
I=[\,i_{A}\,.\,.\,i_{B}\,],J=[\,j_{A}\,.\,.\,j_{B}\,]
if
(i_{A},j_{B})
is above
P^{-}_{0}
and
(i_{A},j_{B})
is not
2\Delta
-relevant.
Since
(i_{A},j_{B})
is not
2\Delta
-relevant, by
Lemma

19

(i_{A},j_{B})
must be above
P^{+}_{2\Delta}
or below
P^{-}_{2\Delta}
.
Since
(i_{A},j_{B})
is above
P^{-}_{0}
, it can only be above
P^{+}_{2\Delta}
.
Since
(i_{A},j_{B})
is the lower right corner of
I\times J
, it follows that all points in
I\times J
are above
P^{+}_{2\Delta}
.
Thus, by
Lemma

19
all points in
I\times J
are not
2\Delta
-relevant.
If we assume for the sake of contradiction that
(i^{*},j^{*})\in I\times J
, then
Lemma

17
implies
f(i^{*})+g(j^{*})>h(k)
, contradicting the choice of
(i^{*},j^{*})
as a witness for
h(k)
.
Hence,
(i^{*},j^{*})
can never be in a Case 1 subproblem.
Case 2 is symmetric. This finishes the correctness proof.
∎

Running Time

Next, we analyze the running time.
The key insight is that in relevant regions both functions are essentially linear, with the same slope (see
Lemma

24
).
This implies that the sumset computed in Case 3 is small (see
Lemma

25
), so it can be computed efficiently using
Corollary

21
.
In the following two lemmas, let
I=[\,i_{A}\,.\,.\,i_{B}\,]\subseteq[\,n\,]
and
J=[\,j_{A}\,.\,.\,j_{B}\,]\subseteq[\,m\,]
be intervals of the same length
|I|=|J|
.

Lemma 24

(Near Linearity inside Relevant Region)
.

If
I\times J\subseteq R_{2\Delta}
then there are
a,b,c\in\mathbf{R}
such that

|f(i)-(a\cdot i+b)|\leq 2\Delta
for all
i\in I
and
|g(j)-(a\cdot j+c)|\leq 2\Delta
for all
j\in J
.

Proof.

Consider the linear interpolation between
(i_{A},\breve{f}(i_{A}))
and
(i_{B},\breve{f}(i_{B}))
:

F(x):=\frac{(i_{B}-x)\breve{f}(i_{A})+(x-i_{A})\breve{f}(i_{B})}{i_{B}-i_{A}}.

Similarly, consider

G(x):=\frac{(j_{B}-x)\breve{g}(j_{A})+(x-j_{A})\breve{g}(j_{B})}{j_{B}-j_{A}}.

By convexity of
\breve{f}
and
\breve{g}
, we have

\breve{f}(i)\leq F(i)\quad\forall i\in I,\quad\quad\breve{g}(j)\leq G(j)\quad\forall j\in J.

(9)

Consider the diagonal
k:=i_{A}+j_{B}
and note that for all
i\in I
we have
k-i\in J
due to
|I|=|J|
.
Thus, for each
i\in I
the point
(i,k-i)
is
2\Delta
-relevant, and we obtain

\breve{h}(k)\leq\breve{f}(i)+\breve{g}(k-i)\leq\breve{h}(k)+2\Delta\quad\forall i\in I

(10)

This implies

\breve{h}(k)\leq F(i)+G(k-i)\leq\breve{h}(k)+2\Delta\quad\forall i\in I,

(11)

since by (
10
) these inequalities hold for
i\in\{\,i_{A},i_{B}\,\}
and by the linear interpolation, they also hold in between.

Now for any
i\in I
we have

\breve{f}(i)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{proof:same-slope:eqn:1}}}{{\geq}}\breve{h}(k)-\breve{g}(k-i)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{proof:same-slope:eqn:0}}}{{\geq}}\breve{h}(k)-G(k-i)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{proof:same-slope:eqn:2}}}{{\geq}}\breve{h}(k)-(\breve{h}(k)+2\Delta-F(i))=F(i)-2\Delta.

Thus,
\breve{f}(i)\in[\,F(i)-2\Delta\,.\,.\,F(i)\,]
, and by

\breve{f}\leq f\leq\breve{f}+\Delta_{f}\leq\breve{f}+\Delta
, we obtain that
|f(i)-F(i)|\leq 2\Delta
.

For
\breve{g}(j)
for any
j\in J
we bound

\breve{g}(j)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{proof:same-slope:eqn:1}}}{{\geq}}\breve{h}(k)-\breve{f}(k-j)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{proof:same-slope:eqn:0}}}{{\geq}}\breve{h}(k)-F(k-j),

and

\breve{g}(j)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{proof:same-slope:eqn:0}}}{{\leq}}G(j)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{proof:same-slope:eqn:2}}}{{\leq}}\breve{h}(k)-F(k-j)+2\Delta.

Therefore,
|\breve{g}(j)-(\breve{h}(k)-F(k-j)+\Delta)|\leq\Delta
.
By linearity of
F
we can write
F(k-j)=F(k)-F(j)+F(0)
.
This yields
|\breve{g}(j)-(F(j)+\lambda)|\leq\Delta
for
\lambda:=\breve{h}(k)-F(k)-F(0)+\Delta
.
Since
|g(j)-\breve{g}(j)|\leq\Delta_{g}\leq\Delta
we obtain
|g(j)-(F(j)+\lambda)|\leq 2\Delta
.
Since
F
is linear, writing
F(i)=a\cdot i+b
and
F(j)+\lambda=a\cdot j+c
finishes the proof.
∎

Lemma 25

(Relevant Regions have Small Sumsets)
.

If
I\times J\subseteq R_{2\Delta}
then the sumset
\{\,(i,f(i))\mid i\in I\,\}+\{\,(j,f(j))\mid j\in J\,\}
has size
O(\Delta\cdot(|I|+|J|))
.

Proof.

By
Lemma

24
, for any
(i,j)\in I\times J
with
i+j=k
we have

f(i)+g(j)=(a\cdot i+b)+(a\cdot j+c)\pm O(\Delta)=a\cdot k+b+c\pm O(\Delta).

Thus, for each of the
|I|+|J|-1

x
-coordinates (i.e., choices of
i+j
), there are
O(\Delta)
different
y
-coordinates (i.e., values
f(i)+g(j)
) in the sumset.
∎

Lemma 26

(Running Time of
Algorithm

3
)
.

\textsc{RecMinConv}([\,0\,.\,.\,n\,],[\,0\,.\,.\,m\,])
(
Algorithm

3
) runs in time
\widetilde{O}(n\Delta)
.

Proof.

We first analyze the running time of one recursive subproblem, ignoring the cost of recursive calls.
Note that in Cases 1 and 2 it suffices to return a dummy value, i.e., we do not need to iterate over
k\in[\,i_{A}+j_{A}\,.\,.\,i_{B}+j_{B}\,]
to
explitly return
\tilde{h}(k)=\infty
. Thus, Cases 1 and 2 run in time
O(1)
.
We charge this time to the parent of the current subproblem, which is a Case 4-subproblem.

Consider Case 4.
Ignoring the cost of the recursive subproblems, Case 4 runs in time
O(1)
, which also covers the charging from children which fall in Cases 1 and 2.

Consider Case 3, and let
s:=i_{B}-i_{A}+1=j_{B}-j_{A}+1
be the current side length.
By
Lemma

25
, the sumset computed in
7:
has size
O(\Delta s)
.
Thus, it can be computed in time
\widetilde{O}(\Delta s)
using
Corollary

21
, and the function
\tilde{h}
can be inferred from it in time
O(\Delta s)
.

Now we bound the total running time across subproblems.
Fix a side length
s
and consider all possible subproblems of side length
s
, i.e., all boxes

B^{s}_{x,y}:=[\,x\cdot s\,.\,.\,x\cdot s+s-1\,]\times[\,y\cdot s\,.\,.\,y\cdot s+s-1\,],\text{ where }x,y\in[\,n/s\,].

Consider a diagonal
D_{s,d}:=\{\,B^{s}_{x,x+d}\mid x\in[\,n/s\,]\,\}
of these boxes, see
Figure

3(a)
.
Note that a box in
D_{s,d}
that lies fully above
P_{\Delta}^{+}
corresponds to a Case 1-subproblem.
A box in
D_{s,d}
that lies fully below corresponds to a Case 2-subproblem.
A box that is below or on
P_{2\Delta}^{+}
and above or on
P_{2\Delta}^{-}

The remaining boxes intersect
P_{2\Delta}^{+}
or
P_{2\Delta}^{-}
and correspond to Case 4.

Note that by monotonicity of
P_{2\Delta}^{+},P_{2\Delta}^{-}
, at most two boxes in
D_{s,d}
are intersected by
P_{2\Delta}^{+}
or
P_{2\Delta}^{-}
and thus at most two boxes in
D_{s,d}

Thus, Case 4 incurs time
O(1)
per diagonal.
We argue that among the boxes in
D_{s,d}
, at most two can appear as Case 3-subproblems.
Indeed, if these would be at least three such boxes, then the parent of the middle box would also be between

P^{+}_{2\Delta}
and

It remains to sum up over all side lengths

O(n/s)
diagonals
d
, to obtain total time

\sum_{\ell=1}^{\log n}O(n/2^{\ell})\cdot\widetilde{O}(\Delta 2^{\ell})=\widetilde{O}(\Delta n)
.
Note that the sum over
\ell
only adds another log-factor, which is hidden by the
\widetilde{O}
-notation.
∎
