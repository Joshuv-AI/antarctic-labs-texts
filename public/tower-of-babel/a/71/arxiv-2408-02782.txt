Log-concavity and log-convexity via distributive lattices
Jinting Liang

Abstract
The FKG inequality is a powerful tool for proving inequalities in distributive lattices. We show how a special case, which we call the Order Ideal Lemma, can be used to demonstrate a wide array of log-concavity and log-convexity results in a combinatorial manner. We use the Order Ideal Lemma to prove log-concavity and log-convexity of various sequences involving lattice paths (Catalan, Motzkin and large Schröder numbers), intervals in Young’s lattice, order polynomials, specializations of Schur and Schur Q Q -functions, Lucas sequences, descent and peak polynomials of permutations, pattern avoidance, set partitions, and noncrossing partitions. We end with a section with conjectures and outlining future directions.



1
Introduction

Let

(a_{n})_{n\geq 0}=a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},\ldots

be a sequence of real numbers. The sequence is
log-concave
if

a_{n}^{2}\geq a_{n-1}a_{n+1}

(1)

for all
n\geq 1
. A
log-convex
sequence is one satisfying

a_{n}^{2}\leq a_{n-1}a_{n+1}

(2)

for all
n\geq 1
. Log-concave and log-convex sequences abound in combinatorics, algebra, and geometry. See the survey articles of Stanley
[
Sta89
]
, Brenti
[
Bre94
]
, and Brändén
[
Br15
]
for more information. By specialization of the FKG inequality
[
FKG71
]
, we obtain a combinatorial tool for proving log-concavity and log-convexity using order ideals in distributive lattices. We then apply this method to obtain a host of such results both old and new in a unified way.

Let us review some basic concepts from the theory of partially ordered sets (posets). More comprehensive treatments can be found in the books of Sagan
[
Sag20
]
or Stanley
[
Sta12
]
.
All of our posets will be finite.
A
lower order ideal
in a poset
(P,\preceq)
is
I\subseteq P
such that if
x\in I
and
y\preceq x
then
y\in I
. Similarly, an
upper order ideal
is
J\subseteq P
satisfying
x\in J
and
y\succeq x
implies
y\in J
.
We will use “order ideal” to refer to a subset which could be either.
Say that poset
L
is a
lattice
if every pair
x,y\in L
has a greatest lower bound or
meet
,
x\wedge y
, as well as a least upper bound or
join
,
x\vee y
. The lattice is
distributive
if it satisfies either of the two equivalent distributive laws that, for all
x,y,z\in L
,

x\wedge(y\vee z)=(x\wedge y)\vee(x\wedge z)

and

x\vee(y\wedge z)=(x\vee y)\wedge(x\vee z).

We can now state our fundamental result which we call the Order Ideal Lemma. It is an easy consequence of the FKG Inequality
[
FKG71
]
. But since its proof involves concepts not needed in the rest of this paper, we postpone the demonstration until Subsection
8.1
. For a set
S
we will use both
|S|
and
\#S
for its cardinality.

Lemma 1.1

(The Order Ideal Lemma)
.

Let
L
be a distributive lattice and suppose that
I,J\subseteq L
are ideals.

(a)

If
I,J
are both lower ideals or both upper ideals then

|I|\cdot|J|\leq|I\cap J|\cdot|L|.

(b)

If one of
I,J
is a lower order ideal and the other is upper then

|I|\cdot|J|\geq|I\cap J|\cdot|L|.

We note that part (a) of the Order Ideal Lemma appeared in a paper of Daykin, Kleitman, and West
[
DKW79
, equation (2)]
. But it’s wide applicability does not seem to have been previously recognized.

Our general strategy for proving log-convexity of a sequence
(a_{n})_{n\geq 0}
will be to construct lattices
L_{n}
with
|L_{n}|=a_{n}
. If we can find inside
L_{n+1}
two lower order ideals
I,J
such
that
|I|=|J|=a_{n}
and
|I\cap J|=a_{n-1}
then we will be done by part (a) of the Order Ideal Lemma. Similarly, part (b) can be used to prove log-concavity.

The rest of this paper is structured as follows. In the next section we will use lattice paths to prove log-convexity of sequences involving the Catalan, Motzkin, and large Schröder numbers.
In Section
3
we use various intervals in Young’s lattice to give log-concavity and log-convexity results. Some of these specialize to the fact that show that various sequences of binomial coefficients are log-concave.
We begin Section
4
by showing that for any poset, the sequence obtained by evaluating its (enriched) order polynomial at nonnegative integers is always log-concave. As a consequence, we obtain
log-concavity of sequences of specializations of Schur and Schur
Q
-functions.
Section
5
is dedicated to generalized Lucas sequences which are those satisfying
a_{n}=a_{n-1}+a_{n-2}
for
n\geq 2
. We show that any such sequence which has positive initial conditions alternates between satisfying (
1
) and (
2
). In Section
6
we consider various sequences related to permutations, including those defined by pattern avoidance as well as sequences of descent and peak polynomials. The focus of Section
7
is set partitions and we show log-concavity of sequences involving Stirling numbers of the second kind and Narayana numbers.
The last section contains a proof of the Order Ideal Lemma as well as directions for future research.

In this section we will use lattice paths together with the Order Ideal Lemma to give unified proofs of the log-convexity of the sequences of Catalan, Motzkin, and large Schröder numbers. We begin with a review of some basic definitions.

A
lattice path
is a sequence
P:p_{0},p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}
of points in the integer lattice so
p_{i}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
for all
i
. A
step
of
P
is the vector
[x_{i},y_{i}]
from
p_{i-1}
to
p_{i}
.
When the initial vertex
p_{0}
of
P
is known, we can specify
P
by listing its steps.
An
up step
is a step
U=[1,1]
and a
down step
is
D=[1,-1]
. A
Dyck path of semilength
n
is a lattice path
P
satisfying

1.

P
starts at
p_{0}=(0,0)
and ends at
p_{2n}=(2n,0)
,

2.

P
uses steps
U
and
D
and never goes below the
x
-axis.

Figure
1
displays the five Dyck paths of semilength
3
. Let

{\cal D}_{n}=\{P\mid\text{$P$ is a Dyck path of semilength $n$}\}.

It is well known that the cardinality of
{\cal D}_{n}
is the Catalan number

C_{n}=\frac{1}{n+1}\binom{2n}{n}.

(3)

We wish to turn
{\cal D}_{n}
into a distributive lattice. If
P\in{\cal D}_{n}
then we let
A(P)
be the
area
of
P
which is the set of all points of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
between
P
and the
x
-axis. In Figure
1
the areas are shaded. We now define a partial order on
{\cal D}_{n}
by

P\preceq Q\text{ if and only if }A(P)\subseteq A(Q).

(4)

The Hasse diagram for
{\cal D}_{3}
is in Figure
1
. We note that
{\cal D}_{n}
is a distributive lattice.
This follows from the fact that it is isomorphic to an interval in Young’s lattice and we will discuss various interesting intervals in the next section.
It is also a consequence of a more general theorem of Ferrari and Pinzani
[
FP05
]
giving a criterion for a family of lattice paths ordered by (
4
) to be a distributive lattice.
The next result follows from easy algebraic manipulations of (
3
). But our proof is combinatorial and will generalize to other families of paths where a closed-form formula is not known.
We note that a
q
-analogue of this result has been proved by Butler and Flanigan
[
BF07
]
, although it does not use the standard definition of
q
-log convexity discussed in Subsection
8.3
below.

Theorem 2.1
.

The sequence
(C_{n})_{n\geq 0}
of Catalan numbers is log-convex.

Proof.

We begin with the distributive lattice
{\cal D}_{n+1}
and note that
|{\cal D}_{n+1}|=C_{n+1}
.

Let

I=\{P\in{\cal D}_{n+1}\mid\text{$P=UDP^{\prime}$ for some translated Dyck path $P^{\prime}$ of semilength $n$}\}.

it follows that
I
is a lower order ideal because if
P\in I
and
Q\preceq P
then (
4
) forces
Q=UDQ^{\prime}
for some
Q^{\prime}
.
Furthermore, we have an isomorphism of posets
I\cong{\cal D}_{n}
given by
P=UDP^{\prime}\mapsto P^{\prime}
. Thus
|I|=C_{n}
.

Now consider

J=\{P\in{\cal D}_{n+1}\mid\text{$P=P^{\prime}UD$ for some Dyck path $P^{\prime}$ of semilength $n$}\}.

Similar considerations to those in the previous paragraph show that
|J|=C_{n}
.
Furthermore

I\cap J=\{P\in{\cal D}_{n+1}\mid\text{$P=UDP^{\prime}UD$ for some Dyck path $P^{\prime}$ of semilength $n-1$}\}

so that
|I\cap J|=C_{n-1}
. Now applying part (a) of the Order Ideal Lemma gives

C_{n}^{2}=|I|\cdot|J|\leq|I\cap J|\cdot|L|=C_{n-1}C_{n+1}

finishing the proof.
∎

We now consider the Motzkin numbers. A
Motzkin path of length
n
is a lattice path
P
which satisfies

1.

P
starts at
p_{0}=(0,0)
and ends at
p_{n}=(n,0)
,

2.

P
uses steps
U
,
D
, and horizontal
H=[1,0]
and never goes below the
x
-axis.

Let

{\cal M}_{n}=\{P\mid\text{$P$ is a Motzkin path of length $n$}\}

so that

|{\cal M}_{n}|=M_{n},

the
n
th
Motzkin number
.
The set
{\cal M}_{n}
ordered by (
4
) is a distributive lattice as demonstrated in
[
FP05
]
.
Showing that the Motzkin sequence is log-convex is much like the proof of the previous theorem, with
I
and
J
replaced by

I=\{P\in{\cal M}_{n+1}\mid\text{$P=HP^{\prime}$ for some translated Motzkin path $P^{\prime}$ of length $n$}\},

and

J=\{P\in{\cal M}_{n+1}\mid\text{$P=P^{\prime}H$ for some Motzkin path $P^{\prime}$ of length $n$}\}.

So, we leave the details to the reader. This result was proved algebraically by Aigner
[
Aig98
]
. A combinatorial proof for both Motzkin and the large Schröder numbers was given by Chen, Wang, and Zheng
[
CWZ21
]
.

Theorem 2.2
.

The sequence
(M_{n})_{n\geq 0}
of Motzkin numbers is log-convex. ∎

Finally, we investigate the large Schröder numbers.
A
Schröder path of semilength
n
is a lattice path
P
satisfying

1.

P
starts at
p_{0}=(0,0)
and ends at
p_{n}=(n,0)
,

2.

P
uses steps
U
,
D
, and twice horizontal
T=[2,0]
and never goes below the
x
-axis.

If we let

{\cal S}_{n}=\{P\mid\text{$P$ is a Schr\"{o}der path of semilength $n$}\}

then

|{\cal S}_{n}|=S_{n},

the
n
th
large Schröder number
. As usual, we order
{\cal S}_{n}
using (
4
). However, this poset is not covered by the general theorem of
[
FP05
]
, although they remark that it can be shown that the poset is a lattice. It is, in fact, distributive.

Lemma 2.3
.

The poset
{\cal S}_{n}
is a distributive lattice.

Proof.

We first wish to show the existence of meets and joins. Suppose
K,L\in{\cal S}_{n}
. We claim that there is
K\wedge L\in{\cal S}_{n}
such that

A(K\wedge L)=A(K)\cap A(L).

To see this, consider any Schröder path
L:p_{0},p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}
. Since
L
starts at
(0,0)
and uses steps
U,H,T
it must be that the coordinates of each
p_{i}
have the same parity.
It follows that
K
and
L
can only intersect at points which are endpoints of steps in both paths and cannot cross in the middle of a step. From this, the assertion that
A(K)\cap A(L)
is the area under some Schröder path follows. Similarly, there is
K\vee L
defined by

A(K\vee L)=A(K)\cup A(L).

It is now an easy matter to verify that
K\wedge L
and
K\vee L
are indeed greatest lower bounds and least upper bounds. And distributivity follows from the fact that intersection distributes over union.
∎

Using the ideals

I=\{P\in{\cal S}_{n+1}\mid\text{$P=TP^{\prime}$ for some translated Schr\"{o}der path $P^{\prime}$ of semilength $n$}\},

and

J=\{P\in{\cal S}_{n+1}\mid\text{$P=P^{\prime}T$ for some Schr\"{o}der path $P^{\prime}$ of semilength $n$}\},

the next result follows in the way to which we have become accustomed.

Theorem 2.4
.

The sequence
(S_{n})_{n\geq 0}
of Schröder numbers is log-convex. ∎

3
Young’s lattice

In this section we collect various log-concavity and log-convexity results which can be proved using closed intervals in Young’s lattice,
Y
. Since
Y
is a distributive lattice, so is every closed interval and we need not check that part of the hypothesis of the Order Ideal Lemma. We start by establishing some standard definitions and notation, including

[n]=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}.

An
integer partition
is a weakly decreasing sequence
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{k})
of nonnegative integers called
parts
. Note that we allow zero as a part.
The
Young diagram
of
\lambda
consists of left-justified rows of boxes or cells with
\lambda_{i}
boxes in row
i
.
We will use English notation for Young diagrams with the first row on top and make no distinction between a partition and its Young diagram.
For example, the shape of
\lambda=(5,3,1)
is shown in the upper left in Figure
4
.

Young’s lattice
,
Y
, is the set of all partitions with partial order
\lambda\preceq\mu
if the Young diagram of
\lambda
is contained in that of
\mu
.

We first show how our proof of the log-convexity of the Catalan numbers can be viewed in this setting. Consider the interval

{\cal C}_{n}=[\emptyset,(n-1,n-2,\ldots,0)]

The southeast boundary of any
\lambda\in{\cal C}_{n}
can be considered as a lattice path of length
2n
from the lower left corner to the upper right corner of
(n-1,n-2,\ldots,0)
using the edges of squares as unit steps north or east. And the lattice path must stay above the line
y=x
if its initial point is taken as
(0,0)
. Such paths are another of the standard ways of describing Dyck paths. So the proof of Theorem
2.1
could have been given in the language of partitions.

For our next result, if
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{k})
is a partition and
n0
is an integer then we define

\lambda+n=(\lambda_{1}+n,\lambda_{2}+n,\ldots,\lambda_{k}+n).

(5)

Theorem 3.1
.

For any partition
\lambda
, the sequence
(\ \#[\lambda,\lambda+n]\ )_{n\geq 0}
is log-concave.

Proof.

Let
I_{n}=[\lambda,\lambda+n]
and consider the following as subsets of
I_{n+1}
:

I=[\lambda,\lambda+n]

and

J=[\lambda+1,\lambda+n+1].

As in the previous proof,
I
is an lower order ideal,
J
is an upper order ideal, and
I=I_{n}\cong J
.
As expected,
I\cap J=[\lambda+1,\lambda+n]\cong I_{n-1}
. So we are done by the Order Ideal Lemma.
∎

Note that even more generally,
\lambda
could be replaced by a skew partition in the previous result and the proof would go through without change.
As an application, suppose that
\lambda=(1^{k})
where we us the notation
i^{m}
to indicate that
i
is repeated
m
times
Then the
\mu\in[\lambda,\lambda+n]
are precisely the
k
-element multisubsets of
[n+1]
. These are counted by
\binom{n+k}{k}
and so we immediately get the following specialization of Theorem
3.1
.

Corollary 3.2
.

For any
k\geq 0
, the sequence
\left(\ \binom{n+k}{k}\ \right)_{n\geq 0}
is log-concave. ∎

The order polynomial of a labeled poset was introduced by Stanley in his thesis
[
Sta71
]
and has since been shown to be a fundamental invariant. In this section we prove that the sequence of values of the order polynomial of any labeled poset is log-concave. This permits us to prove log-concavity of sequences formed by specializing the Schur function corresponding to any partition. The enriched order polynomials of Stembridge
[
Ste97
]
are also shown to give rise to log-concave sequences, again independent of the underlying poset.
This gives rise to log-concave sequences of specializations of Schur
Q
-functions.
We begin, as usual, with the necessary definitions. Note that some of the inequalities have been reversed from Stanley’s original definitions, but this does not change the theory in any substantive way.

Let
(P,\preceq)
be a poset on
[p]
. The reader should be sure to distinguish the use of
\preceq
for the partial order on
P
and
\leq
for the total order on the integers.
A poset on
[3]
is displayed in Figure

.
A
P
-partition with range
[n]
is a map
f:P\rightarrow[n]
such that for all
x\prec y
we have

1.

f(x)\leq f(y)
(that is,
f
is order preserving), and

2.

if
x>y
then
f(x)<f(y)
.

Define

{\cal O}_{P}(n)=\{f\mid\text{$f$ is a $P$-partition with range $n$}\}.

Returning to the example poset in Figure

we have

{\cal O}_{P}(n)=\{f:P\rightarrow[n]\mid f(2)<f(1)\text{ and }f(2)\leq f(3)\}.

The

of

if and only if

for all

.

for both the partial order on functions and the total order on integers, context should distinguish them.

is naturally labeled (that is,

) by Chan, Pak and Panova

Theorem 4.2
.

For any
P
on
[p]
, the sequence
(\Omega_{P}(n))_{n\geq 0}
is log-concave.

Proof.

We first need to prove that
{\cal O}_{P}(n)
is a distributive lattice. Given
f,g\in{\cal O}_{P}(n)
we define a new function
f\wedge g:P\rightarrow[n]
by taking component-wise minima

(f\wedge g)(x)=\min\{f(x),g(x)\}

for all
x\in P
. This will clearly be a greatest lower bound provided that
f\wedge g
is a
P
-partition. Checking the two axioms are similar, so we only do the second. So suppose that

x\preceq y
and
x>y
. Then
f(x)<f(y)
and
g(x)<g(y)
. Without loss of generality we can assume that
f(y)\leq g(y)
. So,

\min\{f(x),g(x)\}\leq f(x)<f(y)=\min\{f(y),g(y)\}

as desired.

Similarly, we define
f\vee g
by

(f\vee g)(x)=\max\{f(x),g(x)\}

for all
x\in P
. The fact that this is a least upper bound is much like what was done for the meet. And the distributive law follows from the fact that maximum distributes over minimum.

To apply the Order Ideal Lemma, let

L={\cal O}_{P}(n+1)

so that
|L|=\Omega_{P}(n+1)
. Now define

I=\{f\in L\mid f(x)\leq n\text{ for all }x\in P\}.

Clearly
I
is a lower order ideal since we are applying an upper bound on
f
. Furthermore,
I={\cal O}_{P}(n)
so that
|I|=\Omega_{p}(n)
. Also consider

J=\{f\in L\mid f(x)\geq 2\text{ for all }x\in P\}.

This is an upper order ideal and
J\cong{\cal O}_{P}(n)
where the isomorphism is obtained by subtracting one from each
f(x)
. It follows that
|J|=\Omega_{P}(n)
. Similar considerations show that

I\cap J\cong{\cal O}_{P}(n-1)
so that
|I\cap J|=\Omega_{P}(n-1)
. This completes the proof.
∎

We now use the well-known connection between order polynomials and Schur functions to derive an interesting special case of the previous theorem. If
\lambda
is an integer partition then a

\lambda
is a filling of the boxes of
\lambda

The partition
\lambda=(5,3,1)
and a semistandard Young tableau

of that shape are displayed in the first row of Figure

We let
(i,j)
be the cell of
\lambda
in row
i
and column
j
where rows and columns are indexed as in a matrix.
Given an SSYT of shape
\lambda
we denote by

the element of
T
in box
(i,j)
.
In the tableau of Figure

Consider

\SSYT_{\lambda}=\{T\mid\text{$T$ is an SSYT of shape $\lambda$}\}.

Let

corresponding to

]

To make the connection with

(i,j)\preceq(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})

and

.

(6)

as its original Young diagram).
Then labeling the penultimate row left-to-right with

-partition is the same as an SSYT of shape

. The partition for the SSYT

for

for

.

Corollary 4.3

For any partition

We can also apply the Order Ideal Lemma to the enriched
P
-partitions of Stembridge
[
Ste97
]
. We put an unusual total order on the nonzero integers, where we denote
-n
by
\overline{n}
,

\overline{1}\triangleleft 1\triangleleft\overline{2}\triangleleft 2\triangleleft\overline{3}\triangleleft 3\triangleleft\ldots\

(7)

and also let

\langle{n}\rangle=\{\overline{1},1,\overline{2},2,\ldots,\overline{n},n\}.

An
enriched
P
-partition with range
\langle{n}\rangle
is a map

f:P\rightarrow\langle{n}\rangle
such that for all
x\prec y
we have

(E1)

f(x)\trianglelefteq f(y)
,

(E2)

f(x)=f(y)>0
implies
x<y
, and

(E3)

f(x)=f(y)<0
implies
x>y
.

We now let

{\cal O}_{P}^{e}(n)=\{f\mid\text{$f$ is an enriched $P$-partition with range $\langle{n}\rangle$}\}.

with corresponding
enriched order polynomial

\Omega_{P}^{e}(n)=\#{\cal O}_{P}^{e}(n).

As with the ordinary order polynomial, the enriched one is well named.

Theorem 4.4

(
[
Ste97
]
)
.

For any
P
on
[p]
we have
\Omega_{P}^{e}(n)
is a polynomial in
n
. ∎

We now turn
{\cal O}_{P}^{e}(n)
into a poset in exactly the same way as the ordinary case:

f\leq g
if and only if
f(x)\trianglelefteq g(x)
for all
x\in P
.

and so is omitted.

Theorem 4.5
.

For any
P
on
[p]
, the sequence
(\Omega_{P}^{e}(n))_{n\geq 0}
is log-concave. ∎

The appropriate tableaux for the enriched setting arise from shifted shapes. An integer partition

\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{k})
is
strict
if
\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}>\ldots>\lambda_{k}
.
A strict partition has an associated
shifted Young diagram
obtained from the ordinary Young diagram by shifting the
i
th row
i-1
boxes to the right for
i\in[k]
.
The shifted shape of
\lambda=(6,5,3,2)
is shown on the left in Figure
6
.
A
semistandard Young tableau of shifted shape
\lambda
is a filling
T
of the cells of
\lambda
with nonzero integers such that the following are satisfied.

(T1)

The rows and columns of
T
are weakly increasing with respect to the total order
7
.

(T2)

For each
m>0
there is at most one
\overline{m}
in each row and at most one
m
in each column.

We let

\SSYTS_{\lambda}=\{T\mid\text{$T$ is a semistandard Young tableau of shifted shape $\lambda$}\}

with generating function

Q_{\lambda}({\bf x})=\sum_{\SSYTS_{\lambda}}\prod_{(i,j)\in\lambda}x_{|T_{i,j}|}

which is called a
Schur
Q
-function
. These functions play a role in the projective representation theory of the symmetric group analogous to
s_{\lambda}({\bf x})
for ordinary representations.

The partial order on shifted shape is just the restriction of (
6
) to those cells in the shifted Young diagram. And the labeling to get a corresponding partition
P_{\lambda}^{e}
on
[|\lambda|]
is exactly the same as in the unshifted case, starting with the bottom row and working up. Now axiom (E1) for enriched
P
-partitions implies condition (T1) for semistandard shifted Young tableaux. And axioms (E2) and (E3) translate into condition (T2).
Thus

Q_{\lambda}(1^{n})=\Omega_{P_{\lambda}^{e}}(n)

and the next result is a special case of Theorem
4.4
.

Corollary 4.6
.

For any strict partition
\lambda
, the sequence
(Q_{\lambda}(1^{n}))_{n\geq 0}
is log-concave. ∎

5
Generalized Lucas sequences

A sequence
(l_{n})_{n\geq 0}
of real numbers is a
generalized Lucas sequence
if it satisfies the recursion

l_{n}=l_{n-1}+l_{n-2}

(8)

for
n\geq 2
. These sequences were originally studied by Lucas
[
Luc78a
,
Luc78b
,
Luc78c
]
. Both the sequences themselves and their
q
-analogues have many wonderful combinatorial properties, see
[
BP09
,
BCMS20
,
SS10
,
ST20
]
. Of course, the two most famous examples of such sequences are the
Fibonacci numbers
,
(F_{n})_{n\geq 0}
, and
(ordinary) Lucas numbers
,
(L_{n})_{n\geq 0}
, with initial conditions
F_{0}=F_{1}=1
and
L_{0}=2
,
L_{1}=1
, respectively.

In this section we will study
positive Lucas sequences
which are generalized Lucas sequences with
l_{0},l_{1}>0
.
In order to state our result precisely, call a sequence
(a_{n})_{n\geq 0}

log-concave at index
n
if

a_{n}^{2}\geq a_{n-1}a_{n+1}.

Note that this definition says nothing about indices other than
n
. Similarly define
log-convexity at index
n
.
We will show that any positive Lucas sequence, suitably reindexed, alternates between being log-concave at odd indices and log-convex at even ones.

It will be convenient in our approach to restrict the initial values even further. But we wish to first show that this restriction will be, in some sense, without loss of generality. To do this, we extend a generalized Lucas sequence to negative indices by insisting that the recurrence relation (
8
) continue to hold for
n<0
to give an
extended Lucas sequence

(l_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
. Call two extended Lucas sequences
(l_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
and
(l^{\prime}_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}

shift equivalent
if there is
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
such that

l_{n}=l^{\prime}_{n+k}

for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Suppose that
(l_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is a positive Lucas sequence. Then its extension is shift equivalent to an extended Lucas sequence
(l^{\prime}_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
such that

0<2l^{\prime}_{0}\leq l^{\prime}_{1}.

Proof.

Consider the reverse subsequence
l_{1},l_{0},l_{-1},\ldots
of the given Lucas sequence. Suppose first that this sequence contains a weak ascent
l_{j}\leq l_{j-1}
where
l_{j},l_{j-1}>0
. But then

l_{j+1}=l_{j}+l_{j-1}\geq 2l_{j}>0,

as desired.

Now suppose this sequence contains a weak ascent with
l_{j}=0
, Then
l_{j-1}>0
since otherwise all entries in the original Lucas sequence are nonpositive.
But now

l_{j+2}=l_{j+1}+l_{j}=l_{j+1},

where

l_{j+1}=l_{j}+l_{j-1}=l_{j-1}>0.

So

l_{j+3}=l_{j+2}+l_{j+1}=2l_{j+2}>0,

again giving the correct conclusion.

If there are no such weak ascents, then the sequence is strictly decreasing and so must eventually become negative. Let
m
be the index of minimum absolute value such that
l_{m}<0
. It follows that
l_{m+1}\geq 0
and

0\leq l_{m+2}=l_{m+1}+l_{m}\leq l_{m+1}.

But this is a weak ascent which is a contradiction.
∎

We will now introduce the posets whose lattices of order ideals will permit us to study the behaviour of positive Lucas sequences
(l_{n})_{n\geq 0}
. Say that such a sequence is
well-indexed
if

0<2l_{0}\leq l_{1}.

Note that, by the previous proposition, every positive Lucas sequence is shift equivalent to a well-indexed one. To simplify notation, we will relabel

r:=l_{0}\text{ and }s:=l_{1}

(9)

Define a poset
L_{n}(r,s)
to have elements
x_{1},\ldots,x_{s-1}
and
y_{2},\ldots,y_{n}

and order relation
\preceq

subject to the covers

1.

x_{1}\prec x_{2}\prec\ldots\prec x_{s-1}
,

2.

y_{2}\prec y_{3}\succ y_{4}\prec y_{5}\succ\ldots
, and

3.

y_{2}\prec x_{r}
.

So the
x_{i}
form a chain
C_{s-1}
and the
y_{j}
form what we will call an
alternating poset

A_{n-1}
.
For example, Figure
7
shows the poset
L_{5}(3,7)
.

Let
J(P)
denote the set of lower order ideals of a finite poset
P
. It is a fundamental result that
J(P)
is a distributive lattice for any
P
. This construction will permit us to prove the following theorem. Note that since the Fibonacci sequence satisfies

F_{n}^{2}=F_{n-1}F_{n+1}+(-1)^{n-1},

one sees immediately that the result is true in this case.

Theorem 5.2
.

A well-indexed Lucas sequence
(l_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is log-concave at odd indices and log-convex at even ones.

Proof.

We continue to use the notation in (
9
). We first verify log-concavity at index
n=1
since our approach with ideals will only start to work when
n\geq 2
. Since
r\leq s/2
we have

l_{0}l_{2}=r(r+s)\leq s/2(3s/2)<s^{2}=l_{1}^{2}.

For ease of notation, let
L_{n}=L_{n}(r,s)
. We will show by induction that

\#J(L_{n})=l_{n}

(10)

for
n\geq 1
. When
n=1
we have that
L_{1}
is a chain with

s-1
elements so that
\#J(L_{1})=s=l_{1}
. Poset
L_{2}
is obtained from
L_{1}
by placing
y_{2}
under the
r
th smallest element of the chain. So, counting the ideals with
0,1,2,\ldots
elements we get

\#J(L_{2})=1+\overbrace{2+\cdots+2}^{r-1}+\overbrace{1+\cdots+1}^{s-r+1}=r+s=l_{2}.

The induction step has two cases depending on whether
n
is odd or even. But they are similar so we will only do the former. In this case we have
y_{n-1}\prec y_{n}
. Every ideal
I\in J(L_{n})
either contains
y_{n}
or not. It follows that if
y_{n}\not\in I
then
I\in J(L_{n-1})
.
On the other hand, if
y_{n}\in I
then this forces
y_{n-1}\in I
and

I-\{y_{n-1},y_{n}\}\in J(L_{n-2})
.
From these observations and induction

\#J(L_{n})=\#J(L_{n-1})+\#J(L_{n-2})=l_{n-1}+l_{n-2}=l_{n}

which completes the proof of the claim.

We now construct the ideals
{\cal I}_{n},{\cal J}_{n}\subseteq J(L_{n+1})
needed to prove the theorem. Given a set of constraints
S
on ideals
I
we will use the notation

J_{n+1}(S)=\{I\in J(L_{n+1})\mid\text{$I$ satisfies $S$}\}.

Define

{\cal I}_{n}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}J_{n+1}(y_{n+1}\in I)&\mbox{if $n$ is odd,}\\
J_{n+1}(y_{n+1}\not\in I)&\mbox{if $n$ is even.}\end{array}\right.

Then

\#{\cal I}_{n}=l_{n}.

(11)

In fact we have an isomorphism
{\cal I}_{n}\cong J(L_{n})
given by the identity map on individual elements when
n
is even, and by removal of
y_{n+1}
when
n
is odd. So the claim follows from (
10
).
Note that the ideals in
{\cal I}_{n}
form either an upper or lower order ideal in
J(L_{n+1})
depending on whether
n
is odd or even, respectively.

Now let

{\cal J}_{n}=J_{n+1}(x_{r}\not\in I)\hskip 3.0pt\cup\hskip 3.0ptJ_{n+1}(x_{s-r}\not\in I\text{ and }y_{n+1}\not\in I).

Note that
{\cal J}_{n}
is a lower order ideal in
J(L_{n+1})
regardless of the parity of
n
. We wish to show

\#{\cal J}_{n}=l_{n}.

(12)

In view of (
11
), it suffices to show that the set differences
{\cal I}_{n}\setminus{\cal J}_{n}
and
{\cal J}_{n}\setminus{\cal I}_{n}
have the same cardinality. Again, we merely provide details when
n
is even. Now

{\cal I}_{n}\setminus{\cal J}_{n}=J_{n+1}(\text{$x_{r}\in I$, and $x_{s-r}\in I$, and $y_{n+1}\not\in I$}).

But
r\leq s-r
so that
x_{r}\leq x_{s-r}
in
L_{n+1}
. This makes the condition
x_{r}\in I
redundant and

{\cal I}_{n}\setminus{\cal J}_{n}=J_{n+1}(\text{$x_{s-r}\in I$, and $y_{n+1}\not\in I$}).

Note that
x_{s-r}
is greater than
y_{2}
and all the
x_{i}
below it. So those elements are forced to be in any ideal
I
we are considering. But then
I
must be constructed by adding to these elements some ideal of the
chain
x_{s-r+1},\ldots,x_{s-1}
and, because
n
is even, an ideal of the subposet of
L_{n+1}
induced on
y_{3},\ldots,y_{n}
. It follows that we have a product poset

{\cal I}_{n}\setminus{\cal J}_{n}\cong J(C_{r-1})\times J(A_{n-2}).

(13)

Now directly from the definitions we have

{\cal J}_{n}\setminus{\cal I}_{n}=J_{n+1}(\text{$x_{r}\not\in I$, and $y_{n+1}\in I$}).

Since
x_{r}\not\in I
, any ideal in the difference breaks into two pieces. One is an ideal in the
chain
x_{1},\ldots,x_{r-1}
and the other an ideal of the alternating poset on
y_{2},\ldots,y_{n+1}
which contains
y_{n+1}
. Since by parity,
y_{n+1}\succ y_{n}
, we must also have
y_{n}\in I
and so this part of
I
is determined as an ideal in the alternating poset on
y_{2},\ldots,y_{n-1}
. Comparing this with (
13
)
shows that
\#{\cal I}_{n}\setminus{\cal J}_{n}=\#{\cal J}_{n}\setminus{\cal I}_{n}
.

There remains to calculate
\#({\cal I}_{n}\cap{\cal J}_{n})
. But directly from the definitions we see that the identity map on elements gives an isomorphism
{\cal I}_{n}\cap{\cal J}_{n}\cong{\cal J}_{n-1}
. So by (
12
) we have

\#({\cal I}_{n}\cap{\cal J}_{n})=l_{n-1}

as desired.
∎

6
Permutations

We now prove various log-concavity and log-convexity results concerning subsets of the symmetric group
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
of permutations of
[n]
.
Sequences of evaluations of descent and peak polynomials will be shown to be log-concave. We will also use pattern avoidance to give a third proof of the log-convexity of the Catalan numbers.
In addition to the Order Ideal Lemma, one of our main tools will be the recently-defined middle order distributive lattice on
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
.

There are two standard partial orders on
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
: the weak and strong Bruhat orders.
Recently, Bouvel, Ferrari and Tenner
[
BFT24
]
defined a partial order which they call the middle order because it refines the weak order and is refined by the strong. This order has the advantage of being a distributive lattice and is built using inversions.
Given
\pi=\pi_{1}\pi_{2}\ldots\pi_{n}\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
in one-line notation, its

set of inversion (values)
is

\Inv\pi=\{(\pi_{i},\pi_{j})\mid i<j\text{ and }\pi_{i}>\pi_{j}\}.

We also say that
\pi_{i}
is an
inversion top
if
(\pi_{i},\pi_{j})\in\Inv\pi
for some
\pi_{j}
.
For example, if

\pi=415632

(14)

then

\Inv 415632=\{(4,1),\ (4,2),\ (4,3),\ (5,2),\ (5,3),\ (6,2),\ (6,3),\ (3,2)\}

so
4
is an inversion top in
3
inversions,
5
and
6
are inversion tops in
2
inversions each, and
3
is an inversion top in one inversion.
Clearly
i
can be an inversion top in anywhere from
0
to
i-1
inversions.
The
inversion table
of
\pi
is

\iota(\pi)=(\iota_{1},\iota_{2},\ldots,\iota_{n})

where

\iota_{i}=\text{ \# of inversions in which $i$ is an inversion top}.

Returning to our example

\iota(\pi)=(0,0,1,3,2,2).

Future directions

The purpose of this section is threefold. First, we will give a proof of the Order Ideal Lemma using the FKG inequality. Next, we will describe various sequences to which it might be possible to apply our method but which have so far resisted proof. 8.1
Proof of the Order Ideal Lemma

Let
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
denote the nonnegative real numbers. For any poset
(P,\preceq)
we call a function
f:P\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}

increasing
if

x\preceq y\text{ implies }f(x)\leq f(y).

Similarly define
f
to be
decreasing
.
Now suppose
(L,\preceq)
is a lattice. A function
\mu:L\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is
log-supermodular
if for all
x,y\in L
we have

\mu(x)\mu(y)\leq\mu(x\land y)\mu(x\lor y).

Finally, given a function
f:L\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
and a log-supermodular function

\mu:L\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
,
we use the notation

S(f)=\sum_{x\in L}f(x)\mu(x).

In particular, if
g:L\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
is another function then

S(fg)=\sum_{x\in L}f(x)g(x)\mu(x),

and, letting
{\bf 1}:L\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
be the function defined by

{\bf 1}(x)=1
for all
x\in L
,

S({\bf 1})=\sum_{x\in L}\mu(x).

We can now state the FKG inequality
[
FKG71
]
.

Theorem 8.1

(
[
FKG71
]
)
.

Suppose
(L,\preceq)
is a finite distributive lattice and

\mu:L\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is a log-supermodular function. Suppose also that

f,g:L\to{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
are two functions.

(a)

If
f,g
are both increasing or both decreasing then

S(f)\cdot S(g)\leq S(fg)\cdot S({\bf 1}).

(b)

If one of
f,g
is increasing and the other decreasing then

\rule{4.30554pt}{0.0pt}\hfill{\displaystyle S(f)\cdot S(g)\geq S(fg)\cdot S({\bf 1}).}\hfill\qed

The Order Ideal Lemma, which is restated here for convenience, is a corollary.

Lemma 8.2

(The Order Ideal Lemma)
.

Let
L
be a distributive lattice and suppose that
I,J\subseteq L
are ideals.

(a)

If
I,J
are both lower ideals or both upper ideals then

|I|\cdot|J|\leq|I\cap J|\cdot|L|.

(b)

If one of
I,J
is a lower order ideal and the other is upper then

|I|\cdot|J|\geq|I\cap J|\cdot|L|.

Proof.

We will prove (b) as (a) is similar. Suppose that
I
is the lower order ideal and
J
is the upper. Define indicator functions
f,g:L\to{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
by

f(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\mbox{if $x\in I$,}\\
0&\mbox{else,}\end{array}\right.

and

g(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\mbox{if $x\in J$,}\\
0&\mbox{else.}\end{array}\right.

Since
I
is a lower order ideal we have that
f
is decreasing and, by the same token,
g
is increasing. Also define
\mu={\bf 1}
which is clearly log-supermodular. Now, by the FKG Theorem, part (b),

|I|\cdot|J|=S(f)\cdot S(g)\geq S(fg)\cdot S({\bf 1})=|I\cap J|\cdot|L|

which finishes the proof.
∎

8.2
Other sequences

In this subsection we discuss various sequences to which we hope the Order Ideal Lemma might be applied.

A superset of the set of permutations (viewed as permutations matrices) is the set of alternating sign matrices. An
alternating sign matrix
or ASM is a matrix such that

1.

every entry is
\pm 1
or
0
, and

2.

in each row and each column the nonzero entries alternate
1
and
-1

The number of

\asm_{n}=\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{(3i+1)!}{(n+i)!}.

This formula was conjectured by Mills, Robbins, and Rumsey in 1983
[

]
and then given two proofs in 1996, the first by Zeilberger
[
Zei96
]
and the second by Kuperberg
[
Kup96
]
. The same numbers count descending plane partitions, totally symmetric self-complementary plane partitions, configurations of square ice with domain wall boundary conditions, and various other mathematical objects. Using cancellation of factorials, it is easy to prove the following result.

Theorem 8.3
.

The sequence
(\asm_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is log-convex. ∎

It is a natural question to find a proof of the previous resul using the Order Ideal Lemma and this has been done by Fischer, Konvalinka, Sagan, and Weigandt
[

]
using the corresponding corner sum matrices. These ideas can also be used to prove log-concavity and log-convexity for related objects which do not have a nice product formula.

. Parking functions were first defined by Konheim and Weiss
[

]
and have since been widely studied, in part because of their connections with noncrossing partitions, hyperplane arrangements, and other combinatorial constructs. The number of parking functions of length

We note that this is also the number of labeled trees on

It would be very interesting to give a combinatorial proof of this theorem using the Order Ideal Lemma. Unfortunately, we have been unable to find the appropriate distributive lattice.

Given a permutation, one can look at the values of the peaks rather than their indices. Specifically, the
pinnacle set
of
\pi\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
is

\Pin\pi=\{\pi_{i}\mid\pi_{i-1}<\pi_{i}>\pi_{i_{1}}\}.

Pinnacle sets were first studied by Strehl
[

]
although he called them peak sets. They were then rediscovered by Davis, Nelson, Petersen, and Tenner
[
DNKPT18
]
and have since been studied by a number of authors
[

,

,

,

,

,

FNT24

. Interestingly, they do not behave like peaks. For example, there does not seem to be any corresponding pinnacle polynomial.

Since pinnacles involve permutation values, it is natural to try and study them using the ordinary middle order. However, the posets obtained by restricting this order to all permutations with a given length and pinnacle set do not always have connected Hasse diagrams. Instead, consider the following variant which has occurred, for example, in the work of Rusu and Tenner
[
RT21
]
. Let
\sigma
be a permutation of distinct positive integers. Let

\Pin_{n}(\sigma)=\{\pi\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}\mid\text{the pinnacles of $\pi$ are the elements of $\sigma$ in that order}\}.

and

\pin_{n}(\sigma)=\#\Pin_{n}(\sigma).

Computation of examples raise the following questions.

Question 8.6
.

For all
n
and
\sigma
:

(a)

Is the restriction of the middle order to
\Pin_{n}(\sigma)
a distributive lattice?

(b)

Is the sequence
(\pin_{n}(\sigma))
log-concave?

It would be interesting if one could apply our methods to prove log-convexity of other pattern avoidance sequences. In fact, they are conjectured to satisfy a stronger property.
Call
(a_{n})_{n\geq 0}
a
Stieltjes moment sequence
if there exists a nonnegative measure
\mu
supported on
[0,\infty)
such that

a_{n}=\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{n}d\mu(x)

for all
n\geq 0
. This is equivalent to requiring that all of the determinants of the Hankel matrix

H=[a_{i+j}]_{i,j\in{\mathbb{N}}}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}a_{0}&a_{1}&a_{2}&\cdots\\
a_{1}&a_{2}&a_{3}&\cdots\\
a_{2}&a_{3}&a_{4}&\cdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{array}\right]

are non-negative. Note that this immediately implies log-convexity by taking adjacent
2\times 2
minors. In his thesis, Elvey Price posed the following question.

Question 8.7

(
[
Elv18
]
)
.

Suppose
\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
for
n\geq 2
. Is the sequence
(\#\Av_{n}(\sigma))_{n\geq 0}
always a Stieltjes moment sequence?

It is known that the answer to this question is “yes” for all
\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
where
2\leq n\leq 4
except possibly
\sigma=1324
and the other
\sigma
giving the same avoidance sequence. See the article of Vatter
[
Vat26
]
for details as well as more intriguing open questions related to pattern avoidance.

Finally, given the nice behaviour of the Stirling numbers of the second kind, one could ask what happens with those of the first. Recall that the
signless Stirling numbers of the first kind
are

c(n,k)=\#\{\pi\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}\mid\text{$\pi$ has $k$ cycles in its disjoint cycle decomposition}\}.

We have checked the following conjecture for
1\leq k\leq n\leq 100
.

Conjecture 8.8
.

Given
k
, there is an integer
N_{k}
such that
(c(n,k))_{n\geq 0}
is log-concave for
n<N_{k}
and log-convex for
n\geq N_{k}
.

8.3
q
-analogues,
{\bf x}
-analogues, and total positivity

Let
q
be a variable.
There is a
q
-analogue of the FKG inequality which makes it possible to prove generalizations of our results in a straightforward manner. Put a partial order
\leq
on the polynomial algebra
{\mathbb{R}}[q]
by defining

p(q)\leq s(q)\text{ if and only if }s(q)-p(q)\in{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}[q].

(26)

We can now define a sequence of such polynomials
(p_{n}(q))_{n\geq 0}
to be
q
-log-concave
if

p_{n}(q)^{2}\geq p_{n-1}(q)p_{n+1}(q)

for all
n\geq 1
and similarly for
q
-log-convexity
.

Suppose
(L,\preceq)
is a lattice and
{\mathbb{N}}
is the nonnegative integers. Call a function
r:L\rightarrow{\mathbb{N}}

modular
if

r(x)+r(y)=r(x\wedge y)+f(x\vee y).

If
L
is distributive, then it has a rank function which is an example of a modular function. If, in addition to
r
, we have a function
f:L\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
and a log-supermodular function
\mu:L\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
then we have an associated polynomial

S[f]=\sum_{x\in L}f(x)\mu(x)q^{r(x)}.

The following result was proved by Björner
[
Bj11
]
for the rank function.
Then
Chen, Pak, and Panova
[
CPP23
]
noted that the same proof would work for any modular function.

Theorem 8.9

(
[
Bj11
,
CPP23
]
)
.

Suppose
(L,\preceq)
is a finite distributive lattice,

\mu:L\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is a log-supermodular function,
and
r:L\rightarrow{\mathbb{N}}
is modular.
Suppose also that

f,g:L\to{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
are two functions.

(a)

If
f,g
are both increasing or both decreasing then

S[f]\cdot S[g]\leq S[fg]\cdot S[{\bf 1}].

(b)

If one of
f,g
is increasing and the other decreasing then

\rule{4.30554pt}{0.0pt}\hfill{\displaystyle S[f]\cdot S[g]\geq S[fg]\cdot S[{\bf 1}].}\hfill\qed

The following
q
-analogue of the Order Ideal Lemma follows from the previous theorem in much the same way that the original result follows from the FKG inequality. So the demonstration is omitted.
For a modular function
r
will use the notation

[I]_{q}=\sum_{x\in I}q^{r(x)}.

Lemma 8.10

(The
q
-Order Ideal Lemma)
.

Let
L
be a distributive lattice,
r:L\rightarrow{\mathbb{N}}
be modular, and suppose that
I,J\subseteq L
are ideals.

(a)

If
I,J
are both lower ideals or both upper ideals then

[I]_{q}\cdot[J]_{q}\leq[I\cap J]_{q}\cdot[L]_{q}.

(b)

If one of
I,J
is a lower order ideal and the other is upper then

\rule{4.30554pt}{0.0pt}\hfill{\displaystyle[I]_{q}\cdot[J]_{q}\geq[I\cap J]_{q}\cdot[L]_{q}.}\hfill\qed

We now show how to use the
q
-Order Ideal Lemma to obtain a generalization of Theorem
4.3
to principal specializations of Schur functions, noting that a similar extension works for the more general Theorem
4.2
.

Theorem 8.11
.

For any partition
\lambda
the sequence
(s_{\lambda}(q,q^{2},\ldots,q^{n}))_{n\geq 0}

is
q
-log-concave.

Proof.

For an SSYT
T
of shape
\lambda
we let

r(T)=\sum_{(i,j)\in\lambda}T_{i,j}.

Note that this is a modular function since the meet and join of tableaux are obtained by taking element-wise minima and maxima, and for any real numbers
x,y
we
have

x+y=\min\{x,y\}+\max\{x,y\}

Letting

\SSYT_{\lambda}(n)=\{T\in\SSYT_{\lambda}\mid\max T\leq n\}

we have

s_{\lambda}(q,q^{2},\ldots,q^{n})=\sum_{T\in\SSYT_{\lambda}(n)}\ \prod_{(i,j)\in\lambda}q^{T_{i,j}}=\sum_{T\in\SSYT_{\lambda}(n)}q^{r(T)}.

Now using using the lower order ideal

I=\{T\in\SSYT_{\lambda}(n+1)\mid\max T\leq n\},

the upper order ideal

J=\{T\in\SSYT_{\lambda}(n+1)\mid\min T\geq 2\},

and the
q
-Order Ideal Lemma completes the proof.
∎

There is an extension of
q
-log-concavity to several variables which would also be worth considering. Suppose that we have a sequence of positive real numbers
(a_{n})_{n\geq 0}
which is log concave. Rewriting the inequalities gives

\frac{a_{0}}{a_{1}}\leq\frac{a_{1}}{a_{2}}\leq\frac{a_{2}}{a_{3}}\leq\ldots\ .

Now cross-multiplying any two fractions gives the seemingly stronger, but actually equivalent, condition that

a_{m}a_{n}\geq a_{m-1}a_{n+1}

for all
0<m\leq n
.
We note that the analogous inequalities for polynomials in
q
are not equivalent to
q
-log-concavity and are called
strong
q
-log-concavity
.

Suppose that
\leq
is a partial order on the polynomial ring
{\mathbb{R}}[{\bf x}]
where
{\bf x}=\{x_{1},x_{2},\ldots\}
. Say that this order is
standard
if it satisfies the following three axioms.

1.

If
f({\bf x})\in{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}[{\bf x}]
then
f({\bf x})\geq 0
.

2.

If
f({\bf x})\leq g({\bf x})
then
f({\bf x})+h({\bf x})\leq g({\bf x})+h({\bf x})
for all

h({\bf x})\in{\mathbb{R}}[{\bf x}]
.

3.

If
f({\bf x})\leq g({\bf x})
then
f({\bf x})h({\bf x})\leq g({\bf x})h({\bf x})
for all

h({\bf x})\in{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}[{\bf x}]
.

Note that the order defined by (
26
) with
q
replaced by
{\bf x}
everywhere is standard. Call a sequence

(f_{n}({\bf x}))_{n\geq 0}

strongly
{\bf x}
-log-concave
with respect to a standard partial order if, for all
0<m\leq n
, we have

f_{m}({\bf x})f_{n}({\bf x})\geq f_{m-1}({\bf x})f_{n+1}({\bf x}).

Sagan
[
Sag92
]
proved the following result.

Theorem 8.12

(
[
Sag92
]
)
.

Fix
k\geq 0
and let
\leq
be a standard partial order on
{\mathbb{R}}[{\bf x}]
.
If the sequence
(x_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is strongly
{\bf x}
-log-concave then so are the sequences

(e_{k}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}))_{n\geq 0}\text{ and }(h_{k}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}))_{n\geq 0}

where
e_{k}
and
h_{k}
are the
k
th elementary and complete homogeneous symmetric functions, respectively. ∎

We note that Chan and Pak
[
CP23
, Theorem 6.1]
have proved a multivariate version of the Ahlswede Daykin (AD) inequality
[
AD78
]
. Since the ordinary AD inequality can be used to prove FKG, the Chan-Pak result implies a multivariate analogue of the Order Ideal Lemma which could yield interesting results.

It would be very interesting to prove an
{\bf x}
-analogue of the FKG inequality and apply it to prove (strong)
{\bf x}
-log-concavity and
{\bf x}
-log-convexity results.

Another way to generalized our results would be in terms of total positivity. Note that we can rewrite the log-concavity of a real sequence
(a_{n})_{n\geq 0}
in terms of a determinant

\left|\begin{array}[]{cc}a_{n}&a_{n+1}\\
a_{n-1}&a_{n}\end{array}\right|\geq 0.

Letting
a_{n}=0
for
n<0
, consider the infinite Toeplitz matrix

T=[a_{j-i}]_{i,j\in{\mathbb{N}}}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}a_{0}&a_{1}&a_{2}&\cdots\\
0&a_{0}&a_{1}&\cdots\\
0&0&a_{0}&\cdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{array}\right].

Call
(a_{n})_{n\geq 0}

totally positive
if all minors of
T
are nonnegative.
Note that this implies log-concavity because of the connected
2\times 2
minors (that is, those consisting of two adjacent rows and two adjacent columns). It would be very interesting to find an extension of the FKG inequality and the Order Ideal Lemma which could be used to prove total positivity.
