Line Marching Algorithm For Planar Kinematic Swarm Robots: A Dynamic Leader-Follower Approach
He Cai a , Shuping Guo a , Yuheng He a , Jieyi Yan a , Yingnan Zhen a , Huanli Gao a , and Xiangyang Li a

Abstract
Most of the existing formation algorithms for multiagent systems are fully label-specified, i.e., the desired position for each agent in the formation is uniquely determined by its label, which would inevitably make the formation algorithms vulnerable to agent failures. To address this issue, in this paper, we propose a dynamic leader-follower approach to solving the line marching problem for a swarm of planar kinematic robots. In contrast to the existing results, the desired positions for the robots in the line are not fully label-specified, but determined in a dynamic way according to the current state of the robot swarm. By constantly forming a chain of leader-follower pairs, exact formation can be achieved by pairwise leader-following tracking. Since the order of the chain of leader-follower pairs is constantly updated, the proposed algorithm shows strong robustness against robot failures. Comprehensive numerical results are provided to evaluate the performance of the proposed algorithm.

Dynamic leader-follower approach, line marching formation, multiagent system.

Introduction

Though each individual in a swarm is always subject to limited
sensing, computing, decision and execution capability,
the swarm as a whole exhibits marvelous performance.
For example, by working in groups, the ant colony can move food hundreds of times heavier than their weights,
thereby improving the foraging efficiency of the population

]
.
Another typical example is the formation flying of geese,
which helps to reduce energy expenditure and thus increase migration distance
[

.
Inspired by the biological behaviors of social animals, formation control has received extensive attention
which gives rise to a significant amount of research results revealing the underlying mechanism of the biological behaviors.
In addition, formation control is also appealing from the perspective of engineering applications,
such as cooperative searching, exploration, escort and
commercial performance

,

.

Roughly speaking, there are two main classes of control approaches to solving the formation control problem. The first class is the
virtual structure approach

,

,

,

,

,
11
,

,
which treats formation as a single entity, and the formation is maintained by keeping the
relative position between a virtual leader and each agent. Early works establishing the
virtual structure approach can be found in
[

,
7
]
.
Since after, the virtual structure approach has been advanced in several directions,
such as dealing with system nonlinearity
[

]
, integration with learning based approach
[

, and
adaptation to unreliable communication network
[
10
]
, just to name a few. Recently,
a simulated annealing algorithm was proposed by
[
11
]
featuring optimal multiphase homing tracking
trajectory and a novel formation guidance strategy in order to reduce the impact caused by the unpredictable environment.

[
12
]
proposed a formation control strategy for UAVs, where the formation control problem was
decomposed into the smooth trajectory generation problem and the trajectory tracking problem.
With the help of the super-twisting method, the airflow influences between UAVs can be compensated effectively
when performing formation flying missions.
A salient advantage of the
virtual structure approach lies in that it makes it easy to define, maintain and keep formation.
However, since the definition of formation is label-specified, once an agent fails, the formation will be incomplete unless further re-organization is made. As a result, the virtual structure approach
is not robust or flexible enough in the face of agent failures.

The second class is the leader-follower approach

chain of leader-follower pair, formation can be achieved by the regulation of

Seminal works for the leader-follower approach can be found in

a distributed control approach was synthesized by

Due to the simple design philosophy, the leader-follower approach is easy to understand and implement in practice. While,
since the order of the chain of leader-follower pairs in the existing results are fixed, once an agent fails, the
other agents subsequential to the failed agent in the chain will be greatly affected, which makes the
traditional leader-follower approach vulnerable to agent failures.

As mentioned above, it is difficult for both the virtual structure approach and the traditional leader-follower approach
to deal with agent failures. The reason behind this is these approaches are fully label-specified, i.e., the desired
position for each agent in the formation is uniquely determined by its label.
To address this issue, in this paper, we further propose
a dynamic leader-follower approach to solving
the line marching problem for a swarm of planar kinematic robots.
In contrast to the existing results, the desired positions for the robots
in the line are not fully
label-specified, but determined in a dynamic way according to the current state of the robot swarm.
By constantly forming a chain of leader-follower pairs, exact formation can be
achieved by pairwise leader-following tracking.
Since the order of the chain of leader-follower pairs
is constantly updated, the proposed
algorithm shows strong robustness against robot failures.
In particular, once a robot fails, the rest
robots will re-form a new chain of leader-follower pairs and form a new marching line.
The failed robot will be considered as an
obstacle by other robots. After the failed robot gets back to normal, it will rejoin the swarm
and the whole robot swarm will re-form, again, into a new line and keep marching forward.

In this paper, we consider a swarm of

For

.

Let

i
th obstacle.

||p_{i}(t)-p_{j}(t)||\geq\delta_{i}+\delta_{j}.

(3)

2.

for all

,

||p_{i}(t)-q_{j}(t)||\geq\delta_{i}+\nu_{j}.

(4)

•

1.

for all
i\in\underline{N}
,

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}(v_{i}(t)-v_{l})=0.

(5)

2.

for all
i,j\in\underline{N}
,

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\langle p_{i}(t)-p_{j}(t),e_{l}^{\perp}\rangle=0.

(6)

3.

3
System modeling and problem formulation

In this paper, we propose a novel dynamic leader-follower approach for the task of robots line marching.
The basic idea is to form a chain of leader-follower pairs in a self-organized way in the sense
that the order of the chain shall not be uniquely specified by the label of the robots, but rather
determined by the current state of the robot swarm.

3.1
Preliminaries

To begin with, we will introduce some indices which will be used subsequently.

1.

to indicate whether robot
i
is the head of the line or not, the following
head index is needed:

\Lambda_{i}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1,&\hbox{robot $i$ is the head of the line;}\\
0,&\hbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

(8)

2.

to indicate whether robot
i
has a follower or not, the following
follower index is needed:

\Delta_{i}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1,&\hbox{robot $i$ has a follower;}\\
0,&\hbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

(9)

3.

to indicate whether robot

\Gamma_{i}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0,&\hbox{robot $i$ fails;}\\
1,&\hbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

(10)

4.

to indicate whether robot
i
has a leader or not, the following
leader index is needed:

\Phi_{i}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1,&\hbox{robot $i$ has a leader;}\\
0,&\hbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

(11)

To achieve simultaneous line marching and collision avoidance,

v_{i}(t)=v_{i,lm}(t)+v_{i,ca}(t)

To avoid collision, for robot

, we let

\displaystyle\bar{v}_{i,ca}(t)

(13)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}\gamma(p_{i}(t)-p_{j}(t))\zeta\left(||p_{i}(t)-p_{j}(t)||,\delta_{i},\delta_{j},\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}\right)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{M}\gamma(p_{i}(t)-q_{j}(t))\zeta\left(||p_{i}(t)-q_{j}(t)||,\delta_{i},\nu_{j},\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}\right)

where
\kappa_{1}>1
,
\kappa_{2}>0
, and

\displaystyle\zeta(x,a,b,\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2})

(14)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\left(\frac{\kappa_{2}}{x-(a+b)}-\frac{\kappa_{2}}{(\kappa_{1}-1)(a+b)}\right),&a+b<x\leq\kappa_{1}(a+b)\\

To rule out the case that
the robot is stuck at some place when

, we let

v_{i,ca}(t)=\mathbf{R}(\theta_{i}(t))\bar{v}_{i,ca}(t)

(15)

where

where

are design parameters.
By choosing

would almost surely avoid the case of

For

\displaystyle v_{i,lm}^{j}(t)

\displaystyle=v_{l}+\alpha(\langle p_{j}(t)-p_{i}(t),e_{l}\rangle-\rho)e_{l}

\displaystyle+\beta\langle p_{j}(t)-p_{i}(t),e_{l}^{\perp}\rangle e_{l}^{\perp}

3.2
Algorithm design

Now, we are ready to present the linear marching algorithm by Algorithm 1.
The basic process is as follows.
Initially, all the robots consider themselves as the head of the line and
the velocity by default is set to be zero. After the initialization phase, the decision phase
starts.
Robot
i
checks the relative position
between itself and other robots in the ascending order of the robot label.
If there is at least one robot ahead of it along the marching direction, then
robot
i
changes the status of its head index. Moreover, if robot
i
further finds a robot, say, robot
j
,
ahead of it along the marching direction, which, in the meantime, has not been tailed,
then robot
j
will be taken as the leader for robot
i
, and the decision loop of robot
i
ends.
If a robot is the foremost one of the line, it automatically becomes the head of the line. If there are multiple robots situate as the foremost heads of the ensemble, only the one with the smallest label will be selected as the leader of the line and others will stay still temporarily to avoid the co-head situation. If a robot fails, it will also stay
where it is until it gets back to normal.

3.3
Discussions

Algorithm 1 can be either implemented in a centralized way, that is, there exists
a central controller taking control of the whole robot swarm, or implemented in a decentralized way,
that is, each robot makes its own decision based on inter-robot communication.
In particular, each robot is endowed with its own time slice to receive information,
make decision and then send out updated information, and
all the robots share a common cyclic communication loop.
The reason behind the requirement on the sequential way of information gathering and broadcasting
lies in the demand for exact formation, i.e., the inter-agent
distance in the marching line should be strictly equal to the designed value.
To the best of the authors’ knowledge, under the distributed communication mechanism,
it would be rather difficult, if possible, to simultaneously achieve exact formation and
autonomous formation reconfiguration when robots fail.

It is also worth mentioning that, in practice, the robots are always subject to
noises and external disturbances. As a result, the co-head situation will not happen.
Therefore, Algorithm 1 can be further
simplified to Algorithm 3, which will be shown later in Section
6
.

The design of Algorithm 1 indicates that the priority of
collision avoidance is higher than line marching for the reason that
v_{i,lm}(t)

is always bounded, while
\bar{v}_{i,ca}(t)
would go unbounded
when the distance between robot and obstacle, or two robots,
approaches the safety limit.

Application to the unicycle model

In this section, we consider the more practical situation
where the robot is described by the unicycle model, and the numerical
simulation is performed on the ROS platform.
In particular, for
i\in\underline{N}
, the kinematic equations of the
i
th robot are given by

\displaystyle\dot{p}_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=\upsilon_{i}(t)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\cos\vartheta_{i}(t)\\
\sin\vartheta_{i}(t)\\
\end{array}\right)

(20)

\displaystyle\dot{\vartheta}_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=\varpi_{i}(t)

where
p_{i}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
\vartheta_{i}(t)\in\mathbb{R}
denote the position and heading angle of the
i
th robot, respectively,
and
\upsilon_{i}(t)\in\mathbb{R}
,
\varpi_{i}(t)\in\mathbb{R}
denote the linear and angular velocity, respectively.

Let
l_{i}
denote the distance between the wheel pair of the
i
th robot, and then

\displaystyle\upsilon_{il}(t)

\displaystyle=\upsilon_{i}(t)-\frac{1}{2}l_{i}\varpi_{i}(t)

(21)

\displaystyle\upsilon_{ir}(t)

\displaystyle=\upsilon_{i}(t)+\frac{1}{2}l_{i}\varpi_{i}(t)

denote the speeds of left wheel and the right wheel of the robot, respectively.
Since the system (
20
) is nonlinear, the proposed line marching algorithm cannot be applied directly.
To tackle this issue, we perform the following coordinate transformation as in
[
22
]
:

\bar{p}_{i}(t)=p_{i}(t)+d_{i}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\cos\vartheta_{i}(t)\\
\sin\vartheta_{i}(t)\\
\end{array}\right)

(22)

where
d_{i}>0
is some small number. Then it follows that

\displaystyle\dot{\bar{p}}_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=\dot{p}_{i}(t)+d_{i}\varpi_{i}(t)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}-\sin\vartheta_{i}(t)\\
\cos\vartheta_{i}(t)\\
\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=\upsilon_{i}(t)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\cos\vartheta_{i}(t)\\
\sin\vartheta_{i}(t)\\
\end{array}\right)+d_{i}\varpi_{i}(t)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}-\sin\vartheta_{i}(t)\\
\cos\vartheta_{i}(t)\\
\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\cos\vartheta_{i}(t)&-d_{i}\sin\vartheta_{i}(t)\\
\sin\vartheta_{i}(t)&d_{i}\cos\vartheta_{i}(t)\\
\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\upsilon_{i}(t)\\
\varpi_{i}(t)\\
\end{array}\right).

Let

\Theta_{i}(t)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\cos\vartheta_{i}(t)&-d_{i}\sin\vartheta_{i}(t)\\
\sin\vartheta_{i}(t)&d_{i}\cos\vartheta_{i}(t)\\
\end{array}\right)

and

\bar{v}_{i}(t)=\Theta_{i}(t)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\upsilon_{i}(t)\\
\varpi_{i}(t)\\
\end{array}\right).

Then, we have

\dot{\bar{p}}_{i}(t)=\bar{v}_{i}(t).

(23)

Moreover, it can be verified that for all
t\geq 0
,
\Theta_{i}(t)
is nonsingular whose inverse is given by

\Theta_{i}(t)^{-1}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\cos\vartheta_{i}(t)&\sin\vartheta_{i}(t)\\
-\sin\vartheta_{i}(t)/d_{i}&\cos\vartheta_{i}(t)/d_{i}\\
\end{array}\right).

By using the coordinate transformation (
22
), the nonlinear system (
20
) is transformed
into a linear kinematic system (
23
), which is in the same form of (
1
) and thus the
proposed algorithm is applicable. After obtaining
\bar{v}_{i}(t)
,
the linear and angular velocity inputs to system (
20
) are given by

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\upsilon_{i}(t)\\
\varpi_{i}(t)\\
\end{array}\right)=\Theta_{i}(t)^{-1}\bar{v}_{i}(t)

(24)

and then the speeds of left and right wheel of the robot can be determined by
equation (
21
).

Note that in ROS platform,
the position measurements of the robots are subject to the Gaussian noise.
As a result, the co-leader situation would almost be impossible.

denoting the period.

7
Conclusion

This paper proposed a dynamic leader-follower approach to solving
the line marching problem for a swarm of planar kinematic robots.
In contrast to the existing exact formation algorithms, such as the virtual structure approach
or the traditional leader-follower approach, the proposed approach shows strong robustness
against robot failure by constantly updating the chain of leader-follower pairs.
Comprehensive numerical results using Matlab, Python and ROS
are provided to validate the effectiveness of the
proposed algorithm for continuous-time, discrete-time and unicycle systems, respectively.
