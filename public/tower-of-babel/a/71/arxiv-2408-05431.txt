Simple and Nearly-Optimal Sampling for Rank-1 Tensor Completion via Gauss-Jordan
Alejandro Gomez-Leos, Oscar López

Abstract
We revisit the sample and computational complexity of completing a rank-1 tensor in ⊗ i = 1 N ℝ d \otimes_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{R}^{d} , given a uniformly sampled subset of its entries. We present a characterization of the problem (i.e. nonzero entries) which admits an algorithm amounting to Gauss-Jordan on a pair of random linear systems. For example, when N = Θ ⁡ ( 1 ) N=\Theta(1) , we prove it uses no more than m = O ⁡ ( d 2 ​ log ⁡ d ) m=O(d^{2}\log d) samples and runs in O ⁡ ( m ​ d 2 ) O(md^{2}) time. Moreover, we show any algorithm requires Ω ⁡ ( d ​ log ⁡ d ) \Omega(d\log d) samples. By contrast, existing upper bounds on the sample complexity are at least as large as d 1.5 ​ μ Ω ⁡ ( 1 ) ​ log Ω ⁡ ( 1 ) ​ d d^{1.5}\mu^{\Omega(1)}\log^{\Omega(1)}d , where μ \mu can be Θ ⁡ ( d ) \Theta(d) in the worst case. Prior work obtained these looser guarantees in higher rank versions of our problem, and tend to involve more complicated algorithms.

Introduction

Tensor completion is a higher-order generalization of the well-known matrix completion problem
[
CT10
,
CR12
]
.
More precisely, a tensor
\mathcal{U}\in\otimes_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a multi-dimensional array whose entries
\mathcal{U}_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{N})}
are specified by an ordered tuple of
N
indices, each in
[d]:=\{1,\dots,d\}
.
Stated loosely, the problem is to recover the entirety of a tensor
\mathcal{U}
observing only a small subset of its entries.

If
\mathcal{U}
is arbitrary, then this task is impossible without observing all
d^{N}
entries of
\mathcal{U}
.
As in matrix completion, the situation becomes interesting when
\mathcal{U}
has a
low-rank
structure.
Conceptually, this means
\mathcal{U}

d^{N}
.
In practice, the framework of tensor completion enjoys several applications.
We refer to
[
KB09
,
SGCH19
]
for a broader overview of these.

In this work, we revisit the problem of rank-
1
tensor completion.
A tensor
\mathcal{U}\in\otimes_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{R}^{d}
is said to be rank-
1
if there exists
\{\mathbf{u}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{N}\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\mathcal{U}_{(i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{N})}=(\mathbf{u}_{1})_{i_{1}}\cdot(\mathbf{u}_{2})_{i_{2}}\cdot\ldots\cdot(\mathbf{u}_{N})_{i_{N}}\quad\quad\forall\,(i_{1},i_{2}\dots,i_{N})\in[d]^{N},

(1)

or for shorthand,
\mathcal{U}=\mathbf{u}_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathbf{u}_{N}
.
Perhaps the most fundamental variant, we consider the setting in which the estimation algorithm only has access to uniformly drawn entries and is required to be correct with bounded error probability.

Our Result

In this work, we prove the following.

Theorem 1.5
.

If
\mathcal{U}\in\otimes_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{R}_{\neq 0}^{d}
is a rank-1 tensor with
\|\mathcal{U}\|_{\infty}\leq\rho
for some
\rho>0
, then there exists an algorithm solving
Rank-1 Completion
using
m\lesssim(dN)^{2}\log d
uniformly drawn entries, and can be implemented in time
\lesssim qN+m(dN)^{2}
, where
q
is the number of queried entries of
\mathcal{U}
.

Moreover, there exists absolute constants
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
and
C>0
such that
dN\geq n_{0}
implies any estimator drawing less than
Cd\log dN
samples suffers error
\|\hat{\mathcal{U}}-\mathcal{U}\|_{F}\gtrsim\rho\sqrt{d^{N-1}}
with probability
\geq 1/3
.

Remark 1.6
.

Many lower bounds in the field are stated in a somewhat different sense—that if too few samples are taken, then there likely exists distinct tensors agreeing on the observed entries.
The standard conclusion is that any algorithm that solely bases its decision on the observed entries must fail
[
CT10
,
KS13
]
.
However, this may be unsatisfying since it does not indicate how much error might be suffered, or how well the adversary fares against an estimator with side information.
This motivates the style of our bound.

To prove the theorem, we show every rank-1 tensor in
\otimes_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{R}_{\neq 0}^{d}
is in correspondence with a pair of linear systems—one over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
, and the other over
\mathbb{R}
.
The former system "encodes information" about the signs of the tensor’s entries, whereas the latter system does so for their magnitude.
In a precise sense, we show that the "information" revealed by sampling an entry is "equivalent" to the "information" encoded by one row of the associated augmented matrix.
These systems are overdetermined, so it suffices to obtain just a small subset of their rows.
In essence, this characterization illuminates that the sample complexity can be studied as a matrix row sketch problem over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
.

At a high-level, our proof views
\Row_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(\mathbf{D}_{S}\mathbf{A})
as the result of a sequentially constructed subspace
W
of
\mathbf{A}
’s rowspace.
We correspond the sample paths of this random process to trajectories on a Markov chain whose states are indexed by
(\dim W,W)
.
Due to the first state coordinate, the chain jumps to a new state no more than
\rank_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(\mathbf{A})
times before hitting the absorbing state
(\rank_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(\mathbf{A}),\Row_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(\mathbf{A}))
.
As a result, the measure of the "bad" event
\{\Row_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(\mathbf{D}_{S}\mathbf{A})\neq\Row_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(\mathbf{A})\}
is given by the cumulative measure of the "bad" trajectories, i.e. those that stagnate and never hit the absorbing state.

It turns out to be very easy to prove the chain self-loops with probability
\leq 1-1/d
.
By pigeonholing, every "bad" trajectory of length
T>\rank_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(\mathbf{A})
has
\Omega(T-\rank_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(\mathbf{A}))

\mathbf{b}\leftarrow\mathbf{P}_{S}\tilde{f}(\mathcal{U})
and used
\mathbf{D}_{S}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y}_{2}=\mathbf{D}_{S}\tilde{f}(\mathcal{U})
, we would conclude
\mathbf{P}_{S}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y}_{2}=\mathbf{P}_{S}\tilde{f}(\mathcal{U})
.
Since
\mathbf{P}_{S}
is a row permutation, this implies

\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y}_{1}=f(\mathcal{U}),\quad\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y}_{2}=\tilde{f}(\mathcal{U}).

Hence, recalling the map
\varphi
and the definition of
f
and
\tilde{f}
,

\displaystyle\varphi^{-1}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y}_{1})

\displaystyle=(\sgn\circ\vect_{\pi})(\mathcal{U})

\displaystyle\exp(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y}_{2})

\displaystyle=(\text{abs}\circ\vect_{\pi})(\mathcal{U}).

Evidently,

\mathcal{U}_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{N})}=\varphi^{-1}(\langle\mathbf{A}_{\pi(i_{1},\dots,i_{N})},\mathbf{y}_{1}\rangle)\exp\left(\langle\mathbf{A}_{\pi(i_{1},\dots,i_{N})},\mathbf{y}_{2}\rangle\right)=\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{N})},

which is what we wanted to show.
∎

6
Proof of Theorem
1.5
, Lower Bound

As we’ll describe, the lower bound in Theorem
1.5
is a simple consequence of the following.

Lemma 6.1
.

Consider a variant of the coupon collector problem in which there are
N\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
urns, each containing
d\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
unique balls.
Suppose each draw is given by a uniformly random choice of one ball from each and every urn, for a total of
N
balls per draw.

There exists absolute constants
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
and
C>0
such that
dN\geq n_{0}
implies that if less than
Cd\log dN
draws are taken, then at least one ball is missed with probability
\geq 2/3
.

Remark 6.2
.

By the classic variant, it is easy to see
\Omega(d\log d)
is necessary.
Since our main result strives for optimal
d
dependence, this would be enough.
For this reason we leave the proof to Appendix
A
, which involves recursively applying Hoeffding’s lemma to a particular martingale sequence.
However, we feel this result clarifies the "coupon collector effect" frequently referred to in the tensor completion literature—often as a remark used to justify the presence of logarithmic factors in the upper bounds.
In contrast, our lower bound explicitly uses such an argument.

We now provide details of the proof.

Proof of Lower Bound, Theorem
1.5
.

Let
\mathbf{u}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}\text{Unif}(\{+1,-1\}^{d})
and consider the tensor
\mathcal{U}:=\rho\,(\mathbf{u}_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathbf{u}_{N})
which serves as input to the algorithm.
Recalling each entry of
\mathcal{U}
is dependent on
N
out of
dN
variables (c.f.(
1
)), we say a sampled entry
\mathcal{U}_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{N})}

collects
the variable
(\mathbf{u}_{k})_{\ell}
if the former is dependent on the latter.
We can correspond the samples with the coupon collecting procedure in Lemma
6.1
.
Concretely, we assign each
\mathbf{u}_{i}
to the urn
i
, and each coordinate variable
(\mathbf{u}_{i})_{j}
with the
j^{th}
ball in the
i^{th}
urn.

Let
m
denote the quantity indicated by (the proof of) Lemma
6.1
.
Suppose
\hat{\mathcal{U}}
is the output of some algorithm drawing less than
m
samples.
By Lemma
6.1
above, the algorithm will not
collect
some variable
(\mathbf{u}_{k^{\prime}})_{\ell^{\prime}}
with probability
\geq 2/3
.
Let
\mathrm{i}^{\prime}
denote any index such that
\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{i}^{\prime}}
depends on the variable
(\mathbf{u}_{k^{\prime}})_{\ell^{\prime}}
.
Even in the case the estimator knows the value of all the variables except
(\mathbf{u}_{k^{\prime}})_{\ell^{\prime}}
, the unobserved entry
\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{i}}
is
\pm\rho
with probability
1/2
each.
Thus, by pigeonholing
we have that
|\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{\mathrm{i}^{\prime}}-\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{i}^{\prime}}|\geq\frac{1}{2}\,2\rho
on at least one of the events
(\mathbf{u}_{k^{\prime}})_{\ell^{\prime}}
is
+1
or
-1
.
Since there are
d^{N-1}
such indices,
\|\hat{\mathcal{U}}-\mathcal{U}\|_{F}^{2}\geq\rho d^{N-1}
with probability
1/2
, i.e.
\|\hat{\mathcal{U}}-\mathcal{U}\|_{F}\geq\rho\sqrt{d^{N-1}}
with probability
\geq\frac{2}{3}\cdot\frac{1}{2}=\frac{1}{3}
, as was to be shown.
∎

7
Open Questions

As asserted, we believe the upper bound can be improved to
O(d\log d)
to match the lower bound in the
N=\Theta(1)
case, either by a refinement of our "matrix sketch over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
" viewpoint, or a different technique altogether.
A more difficult question is whether
\Theta(dN\log dN)
is the
correct sampling threshold for arbitrary
N
.
Indeed, given that
\mathbf{A}
is nonnegative, then one only needs to sketch the rows of
\mathbf{A}
as a real matrix.
Hence, one can apply well-known results from leverage score sampling (e.g.
[
CLM+15
]
) to yield a
O(dN\log dN)
upper bound.

Appendix A
Appendix A : Proof of Lemma
6.1

In this section, we prove Lemma
6.1
.

•

Let
Z_{t}^{i}
for
i\in[N]
denote the random variable counting the remaining balls in urn
i
yet to be seen by and including the
t^{th}
draw.

•

Let
I_{t}^{i}
for
i\in[N]
denote the indicator random variable which is ’1’ if the
t^{th}
draw sees a previously unseen ball in urn
i
, and is ’0’ otherwise.

•

Let
\mathcal{F}_{t}
denote the natural filtration generated by the random variables
\{Z_{1}^{i},Z_{2}^{i},\dots,Z_{t}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
.

Finally, denote
Z_{t}:=\sum_{i=1}^{N}Z_{t}^{i}
and
I_{t}:=\sum_{i=1}I_{t}^{i}
.
In what follows, it is helpful to note that
Z_{t+1}=Z_{t}-I_{t+1}
.

Claim A.1
.

For any
s>0
,
d\geq 2
, and
t\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
, we have

\mathbb{E}(e^{-sZ_{t+1}})\leq e^{\frac{s^{2}dN}{8}-s(1-\frac{1}{d})^{t}\frac{dN}{2}}

(13)

Proof.

Denoting
\alpha:=1-\frac{1}{d}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(e^{-sZ_{t+1}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(\mathbb{E}(e^{-s(Z_{t}-I_{t+1})}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(e^{-sZ_{t}}\mathbb{E}(e^{sI_{t+1}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}))

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}\mathbb{E}(e^{-sZ_{t}}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}(e^{sI_{t+1}^{i}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}))

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\leq}}\mathbb{E}\left(e^{-sZ_{t}}\prod_{i=1}^{N}e^{s\mathbb{E}(I_{t+1}^{i}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}))+\frac{s^{2}}{8}}\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(c)}}{{=}}e^{\frac{s^{2}N}{8}}\mathbb{E}\left(e^{-sZ_{t}}\prod_{i=1}^{N}e^{s\frac{Z_{t}^{i}}{d}}\right)

\displaystyle=e^{\frac{s^{2}N}{8}}\mathbb{E}\left(e^{-sZ_{t}}e^{s\frac{Z_{t}}{d}}\right)

\displaystyle=e^{\frac{s^{2}N}{8}}\mathbb{E}\left(e^{-s\alpha Z_{t}}\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(d)}}{{\leq}}e^{\frac{s^{2}N}{8}}\left(e^{\frac{\alpha^{2}s^{2}N}{8}}\mathbb{E}\left(e^{-s\alpha^{2}Z_{t-1}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\dots

\displaystyle\leq e^{\frac{s^{2}N}{8}(1+\alpha^{2}+\alpha^{4}+\dots+\alpha^{2(t-1)})}\mathbb{E}\left(e^{-s\alpha^{t}Z_{1}}\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(e)}}{{=}}e^{\frac{s^{2}N}{8}(1+\alpha^{2}+\alpha^{4}+\dots+\alpha^{2(t-1)})}e^{-s\alpha^{t}(d-1)N}

\displaystyle\leq e^{\frac{s^{2}N}{8}(1+\alpha+\alpha^{2}+\dots+\alpha^{(t-1)})}e^{-s\alpha^{t}(d-1)N}

\displaystyle\leq e^{\frac{s^{2}dN}{8}-s\alpha^{t}\frac{dN}{2}}

where
(a)
follows since each urn is sampled from independently;
(b)
applies Hoeffding’s lemma;
(c)
uses the observation that, given the filtration up to time
t
, the
i^{th}
urn at time
t+1
"sees" a new ball if sampling one of
Z_{t}^{i}
uncollected balls out of
d
;
(d)
is the first recursive application of the bound; and
(e)
uses the simple observation that the first draw always "sees"
N
balls, so
Z_{1}=(d-1)N
almost surely.

∎

We are now in a position to prove Lemma
6.1
.

Proof of Lemma
6.1
.

Fix
\beta\in(0,1)
to be decided later.
Assume that the following holds

t\leq\beta d\log dN.

(14)

We have
(1-\frac{1}{d})^{t}\gtrsim e^{-\frac{t}{d}}\geq(dN)^{-\beta}
, so that for any
s>0
and
\epsilon>0
, by Claim
A.1
,

\mathbb{P}(Z_{t+1}\leq\epsilon)\leq e^{s\epsilon}\mathbb{E}(e^{-sZ_{t+1}})\leq e^{s\epsilon+\frac{s^{2}dN}{8}-s(1-\frac{1}{d})^{t}\frac{dN}{2}}\lesssim e^{s\epsilon+\frac{s^{2}dN}{8}-\frac{s}{2}(dN)^{1-\beta}}.

Let us constrain
\epsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{2}(dN)^{1-\beta})
and take
s=\frac{4}{dN}(\frac{1}{2}(dN)^{1-\beta}-\epsilon)
to give

\mathbb{P}(Z_{t+1}\leq\epsilon)\lesssim\exp\left(-\frac{2}{dN}\left(\frac{1}{2}(dN)^{1-\beta}-\epsilon\right)^{2}\right).

Suppose
\epsilon=\frac{1}{4}(dN)^{1-\beta}
and
dN\geq 7
so that

\mathbb{P}(Z_{t+1}\leq\frac{1}{4}(dN)^{1-\beta})\leq\exp\left(-\frac{(dN)^{1-2\beta}}{8}\right).

In particular, for
\beta=1/4
and
dN\geq 78
, this implies

\mathbb{P}(Z_{t+1}\leq 1)\leq\mathbb{P}(Z_{t+1}\leq\frac{1}{4}(dN)^{3/4})\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\sqrt{dN}}{8}\right)\leq\frac{1}{3}.

Thus,
\mathbb{P}(Z_{t+1}>1)\geq\frac{2}{3}
.
In other words, with probability
\geq 2/3
there remains an unseen ball if (
14
) holds for
\beta=1/4
and
dN\geq 78
.
∎
