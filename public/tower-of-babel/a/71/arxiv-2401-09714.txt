Robust virtual element methods for coupled stress-assisted diffusion problems Thanks: Updated: August 7, 2026.
Rekha Khot, Andrés E. Rubiano, Ricardo Ruiz-Baier

Abstract
This paper aims first to perform robust continuous analysis of a mixed nonlinear formulation for stress-assisted diffusion of a solute that interacts with an elastic material, and second to propose and analyse a virtual element formulation of the model problem. The two-way coupling mechanisms between the Herrmann formulation for linear elasticity and the reaction-diffusion equation (written in mixed form) consist of diffusion-induced active stress and stress-dependent diffusion. The two sub-problems are analysed using the extended Babuška–Brezzi–Braess theory for perturbed saddle-point problems. The well-posedness of the nonlinearly coupled system is established using a Banach fixed-point strategy under the smallness assumption on data. The virtual element formulations for the uncoupled sub-problems are proven uniquely solvable by a fixed-point argument in conjunction with appropriate projection operators. We derive the a priori error estimates, and test the accuracy and performance of the proposed method through computational simulations.



keywords
Virtual element methods, stress-assisted diffusion, diffusion-induced stress, perturbed saddle-point problems, fixed-point operators.

1
Introduction

The process of diffusion within solid matter can result in the creation of mechanical stresses within the solid material, commonly referred to as chemical or diffusion-induced stresses. This phenomenon highlights a reciprocal relationship between chemical and mechanical driving forces, both of which play a key role in either facilitating or hindering the diffusion process. Stress-assisted diffusion for common materials has been introduced as a general formalism in
[
46
,
44
]
. Important examples include Lithium-ion battery cells, silicon rubber and hydrogen diffusion, polymer-based coatings, semiconductor fabrication, oxidation of silicon nanostructures, and enhancing of conductivity properties in soft living tissue. In these processes, heterogeneity (and possibly also anisotropy) of stress localisation affects the patterns of diffusion. Our contribution addresses one of the simplest models for such an interaction, incorporating stress-dependency directly in the diffusion coefficient for a given solute, and assuming that in the absence of stress one recovers Fickian diffusion (see, e.g.,
[
16
,
48
]
). We consider the regime of linear elastostatics and assume that the total Cauchy stress is also due to a (possibly nonlinear) diffusion-induced isotropic stress. On the other hand, the diffusion coefficient in the reaction-diffusion equation governing the distribution of a solute is taken as a nonlinear function of the Cauchy stress. The well-posedness of primal formulations of the coupled problem has been studied in, e.g.,
[
31
,
34
]
.

Some of the materials mentioned above are nearly incompressible, which is why we opt for a mixed formulation of the elastostatics in terms of displacement and Herrmann pressure
[
10
,
26
]
. This allows us to have stability robustly with respect to the Lamé parameter
\lambda
that goes to infinity when the Poisson ratio approaches
\frac{1}{2}
. Robustness with respect to the Lamé parameter
\mu
can be obtained from appropriate scaling of the inf-sup condition for the divergence operator, as done in, e.g.,
[
27
,
39
]
. Robustness with respect to the Lamé parameters is also required for the reaction-diffusion sub-problem, which is a priori not trivial since the diffusion coefficient depends on the Cauchy stress and in turn on the Lamé coefficients. Similarly to the diffusion-assisted elasticity, the stress-assisted reaction-diffusion problem is written as a perturbed saddle-point problem, and robustness is sought also with respect to the uniform bounds of the stress-assisted diffusion and of the reaction parameter.
To achieve such robustness properties we work with parameter-weighted norms and use the perturbed saddle-point theory from
[
11
,
12
]
. The continuous analysis is then adequately adapted to the discrete case, here using virtual element methods (VEMs). Note that using a primal formulation for the elasticity equations can lead to volumetric locking for some parameter regimes (and this is observed even using VE discretisations of low order
[
3
, Section 3]
).

There has been quite a few works on mixed FEMs for the stress-assisted diffusion problems, see e.g.,
[
20
,
21
,
22
,
24
]
. The standard finite element methods (FEM) are well-adapted for many realistic models, but from the past decade, polygonal methods are fast emerging due to their capabilities for mesh flexibility and a common framework for higher-degrees and for higher-dimensions. The VEM first introduced in
[
2
]
is one of the popular polygonal methods, which can be viewed as the generalisation of FEM. The VEM setting takes into consideration the typical terminologies in FEM like conforming, nonconforming, or mixed methods, and that allows to modify the well-developed tools from FEM in somewhat similar manner. The local VE spaces are defined as a set of solutions to problem-dependent partial differential equations combined with boundary conditions based on the underline nature of the method we are interested in (e.g., conforming or nonconforming). Of course a leap from triangular/rectangular elements to fairly general polygonal-shaped elements forces to include locally the non-polynomial shape functions in addition to polynomials. But VEM stands different from the other methods in a sense that it does not require to compute explicitly the possibly complex shape functions and the complete analysis can be performed through degrees of freedom (DoFs) of VE functions and their appropriate projections from the local discrete space to the polynomial subspace. A vast literature is available on VEM and we name just a few here
[
14
,
5
,
8
,
36
]
which are relevant to the model problem in this paper. Applications of VEM in coupled multiphysics are still emerging, see
[
1
,
23
]
for the Navier–Stokes equations coupled with the heat equation, and
[
45
]
for advection-diffusion systems coupled with Biot poroelasticity. To the best of authors’ knowledge, the present paper is the first one addressing coupled stress-assisted diffusion problems. We restrict VE spaces and analysis to 2D for simplicity, but extension to 3D is possible. For example, we refer to
[
7
]
and
[
17
]
for Stokes and mixed Darcy problems in three dimensions. We also mention that we have written an analysis that uses an adaptable common framework for general polynomial degree. Another evident advantage of VEM for this type of problems is the divergence-conforming character of the discretisation for the elasticity equations (which is non-trivial to achieve with, e.g., FEMs or with other conforming VEM which might require high polynomial degree to obtain this important property).
We stress that the proofs of well-posedness of the continuous and discrete problems are based on the Banach fixed-point strategy and therefore are associated with a contraction mapping property that holds for sufficiently small data. Should this not be satisfied, the literature contains alternative treatments including augmented Lagrangian techniques (see, e.g.,
[
23
,
33
]
in the context of VEM) and smoothing-type acceleration schemes at the algebraic level (see, e.g.,
[
32
,
40
]
). We do not address these issues here, but simply say that the numerical evidence in the paper indicates that the proposed methods are stable and convergent even beyond the parameter regime of small data.

Plan of the paper

The contents of this paper have been organised in the following manner. The remainder of this introductory section contains preliminary notational conventions and definitions of useful functional spaces. Section
2
presents the precise definition of the coupled stress-assisted diffusion model we will address, along with the derivation of its weak formulation in the form of two coupled perturbed saddle-point problems. The unique solvability of the model problem is studied in Section
3
, using the Banach fixed-point approach. Section
4
is devoted to designing a family of VEMs for the system under consideration, defining virtual spaces, and recalling appropriate projection operators. In Subsection
4.3
we use an abstract result for discrete perturbed saddle-point problems and again the fixed-point theory, to show that the discrete problem is well-posed. The a priori error analysis for the VE method is carried out in Section
5
. Finally, the convergence rates and robustness of the proposed formulation are tested numerically in Section
6
. To illustrate mesh flexibility, we also include numerical experiments with different types of polygonal meshes (e.g., kangaroo-shaped geometries).

Recurrent notation and Sobolev spaces

Let
D
be a polygonal Lipschitz bounded domain of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with boundary
\partial D
. In this paper we apply all differential operators row-wise. Hence, given a tensor function
\boldsymbol{\sigma}:D\to\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
and a vector field
\boldsymbol{u}:D\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we set the tensor divergence
\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\sigma}:D\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the vector gradient
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{u}:D\to\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
, and the symmetric gradient
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}):D\to\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
as

(\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\sigma})_{i}:=\sum_{j}\partial_{j}\sigma_{ij}
,
(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{u})_{ij}:=\partial_{j}u_{i}
,
and
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}):=\frac{1}{2}\left[\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{u}+(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{u})^{\tt t}\right]
.
The component-wise inner product of two matrices
\boldsymbol{\sigma},\,\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
is defined by
\boldsymbol{\sigma}:\boldsymbol{\tau}:=\sum_{i,j}\sigma_{ij}\tau_{ij}
.
For
s\geq 0
, we denote the usual Hilbertian Sobolev space of scalar functions with domain
D
by
H^{s}(D)
, and denote their vector and tensor counterparts as
\mathbf{H}^{s}(D)
and
\mathbb{H}^{s}(D)
, respectively.
The norm of
H^{s}(D)
is denoted
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{s,D}
and the corresponding semi-norm
|\cdot|_{s,D}
. We also use the convention
H^{0}(D):=L^{2}(D)
and the notation
L^{2}_{0}(D)
when the zero mean value condition is imposed, and
(\cdot,\cdot)_{D}
to denote the inner product in
L^{2}(D)
(similarly for the vector and tensor counterparts).
The space of vectors in
\mathbf{L}^{2}(D)
with divergence in
L^{2}(D)
is denoted
\mathbf{H}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,D)
and it is a Hilbert space equipped with the corresponding graph norm
\lVert\boldsymbol{\zeta}\rVert^{2}_{\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,D}:=\lVert\boldsymbol{\zeta}\rVert_{0,D}^{2}+\lVert\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\zeta}\rVert^{2}_{0,D}
. The same way around with rotational in
L^{2}(D)
which is a Hilbert space denoted by
\mathbf{H}(\mathop{\mathrm{rot}}\nolimits,D)
with the natural norm
\lVert\boldsymbol{\zeta}\rVert^{2}_{\mathop{\mathrm{rot}}\nolimits,D}:=\lVert\boldsymbol{\zeta}\rVert_{0,D}^{2}+\lVert\mathop{\mathrm{rot}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\zeta}\rVert^{2}_{0,D}
. Let
\boldsymbol{n}
be the outward unit normal vector to
\partial D
. The Green Formula
can be used to extend the normal trace operator
\mathbb{C}^{\infty}(\overline{D})\ni\boldsymbol{\zeta}\to(\boldsymbol{\zeta}|_{\partial D})\cdot\boldsymbol{n}
to a linear continuous mapping
(\cdot|_{\partial D})\cdot\boldsymbol{n}:\,\mathbf{H}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,D)\to H^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\partial D)
, where
H^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\partial D)
is the dual of
H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\partial D)
(see
[
19
, Theorem 1.7]
). Moreover, the well-known trace inequality

\left|\langle(\boldsymbol{\zeta}|_{\partial D})\cdot\boldsymbol{n},r^{*}\rangle_{\partial D}\right|\leq{\lVert\boldsymbol{\zeta}\rVert}_{\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,D}{\lVert r^{*}\rVert}_{\frac{1}{2},\partial D}
holds,
where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\partial D}
denotes the duality pairing between
H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\partial D)
and
H^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\partial D)
with respect to the inner product in
L^{2}(\partial D)
and the norm in the traces space
H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\partial D)
is defined as
\lVert r^{*}\rVert_{\frac{1}{2},\partial D}=\inf_{r|_{\partial D}=r^{*}}{\lVert r\rVert}_{1,\Omega}
. An analogous argument extends the notion of trace operators to subsets of
\partial D
.

We shall use the letter
C
to denote a generic positive constant independent of the mesh size
h
and physical constants, which might stand for different values at its different occurrences. Moreover, given any positive expressions
X
and
Y
, the notation
X\,\lesssim\,Y
means that a positive constant
C<\infty
exists such that
X\leq C\,Y
.

2
Governing equations

This section introduces the model problem in detail with the required assumptions on the involved coefficients and the corresponding weak formulation at the end.

2.1
Model statement

Let us consider a deformable body occupying the domain
\Omega
and satisfying the balance of linear momentum in the stationary regime

(2.1)

-\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\boldsymbol{f}\qquad\text{in $\Omega$},

where
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
is the Cauchy stress tensor defined in (
2.2
) below, and
\boldsymbol{f}
is a vector of external body loads. The coupling between solid deformation and a solute with concentration
\varphi
is considered using an active stress approach. Combined with Hooke’s law, this gives the specification

(2.2)

{\boldsymbol{\sigma}:=\mathcal{C}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})-\ell(\varphi)\mathbb{I}=2\mu\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})+\lambda\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}\,\mathbb{I}-\ell(\varphi)\mathbb{I}\qquad\text{in $\Omega$},}

where
\mathcal{C}
is the fourth-order elasticity tensor (symmetric and positive definite),
\mu,\lambda
are the Lamé parameters associated with the material properties of the solid substrate,
\ell
is a nonlinear function of the solute concentration that modulates the intensity and distribution of (isotropic) active stress (also known as diffusion-induced stress),
\boldsymbol{u}
is the displacement vector,
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})
is the tensor of infinitesimal strains (symmetrised gradient of displacement), and
\mathbb{I}
denotes the identity tensor in
\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
.

Equations (
2.1
)-(
2.2
) indicate that the solid deformation will be a response of both external loads and internal stress generation due to the coupling with the species
\varphi
. On the other hand, the solute steady transport in the solid is governed by the reaction-diffusion equation

\theta\varphi-\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits(\mathbb{M}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})\nabla\varphi)=g\qquad\text{in $\Omega$},

where
g
is a given net volumetric source of solute,
\theta
is a positive model parameter, and
\mathbb{M}
is the stress-assisted diffusion coefficient (a matrix-valued function of total stress, and assumed uniformly bounded away from zero). This term implies a two-way coupling mechanism between deformation and transport.
We also consider the auxiliary variable of diffusive flux

(2.3)

\boldsymbol{\zeta}=\mathbb{M}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})\nabla\varphi\qquad\text{in $\Omega$},

and therefore the reaction-diffusion equation reads

(2.4)

\theta\varphi-\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\zeta}=g\qquad\text{in $\Omega$}.

In
[
20
,
21
]
the contribution of
\ell
into the active stress is transferred (thanks to (
2.1
)) to the loading term as
\boldsymbol{f}=\boldsymbol{f}(\varphi)
. Here we maintain it as part of the volumetric stress through the Herrmann-type pressure (see
[
26
]
for the classical hydrostatic pressure formulation) which represents the
total volumetric stress
(see e.g.,
[
30
]
, but note that here we are including both elastic and active components)

(2.5)

\tilde{p}:=-\lambda\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}+\ell(\varphi)\qquad\text{in $\Omega$},

giving that the Cauchy stress reads
\boldsymbol{\sigma}=2\mu\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})-\tilde{p}\mathbb{I}
. This approach leads to a system similar to the total pressure formulation for linear poroelasticity in parameter-robust and conservative form (see, e.g.,
[
11
,
29
]
).

We adopt mixed loading boundary conditions for the coupled problem: the structure is clamped
(\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{0})
and a given concentration
\varphi=\varphi_{D}
are imposed on
\Gamma_{D}
, where the boundary subset
\Gamma_{D}\subset\partial\Omega
is of positive surface measure; and traction and zero solute flux are prescribed (
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\boldsymbol{n}=\boldsymbol{t}
and
\boldsymbol{\zeta}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0
) on
\Gamma_{N}:=\partial\Omega\setminus\Gamma_{D}
.

2.2
Weak formulation

In view of the boundary conditions, we define the Hilbert spaces

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}^{1}_{D}(\Omega):=\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega):\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{0}\quad\text{on }\Gamma_{D}\},\quad\mathbf{H}_{N}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega):=\{\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathbf{H}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega):\boldsymbol{\xi}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0\quad\text{on }\Gamma_{N}\},

with the boundary assignment understood in the sense of traces, and consider the following weak formulation for the system composed by (
2.1
), (
2.5
), (
2.3
), (
2.4
). For given
\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)
,
g\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, and
\varphi_{D}\in H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma_{D})
, find

(\boldsymbol{u},\tilde{p},\boldsymbol{\zeta},\varphi)\in\mathbf{H}_{D}^{1}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)\times\mathbf{H}_{N}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)
such that

(2.6a)

\displaystyle 2\mu\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}):\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v})-\int_{\Omega}\tilde{p}\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbf{H}_{D}^{1}(\Omega),

(2.6b)

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\tilde{q}\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}-\frac{1}{\lambda}\int_{\Omega}\tilde{p}\tilde{q}{-\frac{1}{\lambda}\int_{\Omega}\ell(\varphi)\tilde{q}}

\displaystyle={0}\qquad\forall\tilde{q}\in L^{2}(\Omega),

(2.6c)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{M}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\zeta}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}+\int_{\Omega}\varphi\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\xi}

\displaystyle=\langle\varphi_{D},\boldsymbol{\xi}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\rangle_{\Gamma_{D}}\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathbf{H}_{N}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega),

(2.6d)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\psi\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\zeta}-\theta\int_{\Omega}\varphi\psi

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}g\psi\qquad\forall\psi\in L^{2}(\Omega).

This formulation (mixed for the nonlinear reaction-diffusion part and mixed for the active linear elasticity part) results simply multiplying by test functions and integrating by parts the terms containing the shear and volumetric parts of stress, as well as the diffusive flux.

2.3
Two uncoupled perturbed saddle-point problems

Before we investigate the unique solvability of (
2.6
), we will regard the nonlinear coupled problem as two separate perturbed saddle-point problems. Commonly used forms for the stress-assisted diffusion coefficient include (see, e.g.,
[
16
,
20
,
25
]
)

\mathbb{M}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})=m_{0}\mathbb{I}+m_{1}\boldsymbol{\sigma}+m_{2}\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{2},\quad\mathbb{M}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})=m_{0}\exp(-m_{1}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\sigma})\mathbb{I},\quad\mathbb{M}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})=m_{0}\exp(-m_{1}\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{m_{2}}),

where
m_{0},m_{1},m_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
are model parameters, with
m_{0}>0
. On the other hand, a typical form of the active stress include the Hill-type function (i.e., with a modulation saturating at high concentration values see, e.g.,
[
35
,
37
,
38
]
)
or a scaling of the given concentration (see
[
43
]
), defined as

\ell(\vartheta)=K_{0}+\frac{\vartheta^{n}}{K_{1}+\vartheta^{n}},\quad\ell(\vartheta)=K_{0}\vartheta,

where
K_{0},K_{1}
and
n
(Hill coefficient) are model parameters. From now on, the stress-assisted diffusion will be written explicitly as a function of the infinitesimal strain and of the total volumetric stress
\mathbb{M}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{w}),\tilde{r})
. It is assumed that
\mathbb{M}(\cdot,\cdot)
is symmetric, positive semi-definite and uniformly bounded in
\mathbb{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)
, likewise for
\mathbb{M}^{-1}(\cdot,\cdot)
. More explicitly, there exists a constant
M
such that
0<\frac{1}{M}\leq M
and

(2.7a)

\displaystyle\mathbb{M}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{w}),\tilde{r})\in\mathbb{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega),\tilde{r}\in L^{2}(\Omega),

(2.7b)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{M}\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}\leq\boldsymbol{x}\cdot[\mathbb{M}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{w}),\tilde{r})\boldsymbol{x}]

\displaystyle\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2},

(2.7c)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}\cdot[\mathbb{M}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{w}),\tilde{r})\boldsymbol{x}]\leq M\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{y}

\displaystyle\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

For the active stress function
\ell(\cdot)
, we assume that
\ell:L^{2}(\Omega)\to{L^{2}(\Omega)}
and satisfies

(2.8)

\lVert\ell(\vartheta)\rVert_{0,\Omega}\lesssim\lVert\vartheta\rVert_{0,\Omega}\qquad\forall\vartheta\in L^{2}(\Omega).

Moreover, we assume that
\mathbb{M}^{-1}(\cdot,\cdot)
and
\ell(\cdot)
are Lipschitz continuous, i.e., for all
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{1},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{2}\in\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)
and
\tilde{r}_{1},\tilde{r}_{2},\vartheta_{1},\vartheta_{2}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
there exist positive constants
L_{\mathbb{M}}
and
L_{\ell}
that satisfy the following bounds

(2.9a)

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbb{M}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{1},\tilde{r}_{1})-\mathbb{M}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{2},\tilde{r}_{2})\rVert_{\infty,\Omega}

\displaystyle\leq L_{\mathbb{M}}\lVert(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{1},\tilde{r}_{1})-(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{2},\tilde{r}_{2})\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)},

(2.9b)

\displaystyle\lVert\ell(\vartheta_{1})-\ell(\vartheta_{2})\rVert_{0,\Omega}

\displaystyle\leq L_{\ell}\lVert\vartheta_{1}-\vartheta_{2}\rVert_{0,\Omega}.

For a fixed vector-valued function
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega)
and a fixed scalar function
\tilde{r}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, we define the following bilinear form
a_{2}^{\boldsymbol{w},\tilde{r}}:\mathbf{H}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega)\times\mathbf{H}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}
as

a_{2}^{\boldsymbol{w},\tilde{r}}(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\xi}):=\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{M}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{w}),\tilde{r})\boldsymbol{\zeta}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathbf{H}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega).

In addition, for a fixed scalar function
\vartheta\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, we define the linear functional
G_{1}^{\vartheta}:L^{2}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}
as

G_{1}^{\vartheta}(\tilde{q}):=-\frac{1}{\lambda}\int_{\Omega}\ell(\vartheta)\tilde{q}\qquad\forall\tilde{q}\in L^{2}(\Omega).

Then set the bilinear forms
a_{1}:\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega)\times\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}
,
b_{1}:\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}
,

c_{1},c_{2}:L^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}
,
b_{2}:\mathbf{H}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}
, and linear functionals
F_{1}:\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}
,
F_{2}:\mathbf{H}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}
,
G_{2}:L^{2}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}
as follows

\displaystyle a_{1}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}):=2\mu\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}):\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v}),\quad b_{1}(\boldsymbol{v},\tilde{q}):=-\int_{\Omega}\tilde{q}\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v},\quad c_{1}(\tilde{p},\tilde{q}):=\int_{\Omega}\tilde{p}\tilde{q},\quad F_{1}(\boldsymbol{v}):=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\boldsymbol{v},

\displaystyle b_{2}(\boldsymbol{\xi},\psi):=\int_{\Omega}\psi\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\xi},\quad c_{2}(\varphi,\psi):=\int_{\Omega}\varphi\psi,\quad F_{2}(\boldsymbol{\xi}):=\langle\varphi_{D},\boldsymbol{\xi}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\rangle_{\Gamma_{D}},\quad G_{2}(\psi):=-\int_{\Omega}g\psi,

for all
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega)
,
\tilde{p},\tilde{q},\varphi,\psi\in L^{2}(\Omega)
,
\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathbf{H}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega)
,
\varphi_{D}\in H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma_{D})
.

With these building blocks we define the following system of linear elasticity: for a given
\vartheta\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and
\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)
, find
(\boldsymbol{u},\tilde{p})\in\mathbf{H}_{D}^{1}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)
such that

(2.10a)

\displaystyle a_{1}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})+b_{1}(\boldsymbol{v},\tilde{p})

\displaystyle=F_{1}(\boldsymbol{v})\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbf{H}_{D}^{1}(\Omega),

(2.10b)

\displaystyle b_{1}(\boldsymbol{u},\tilde{q})-\frac{1}{\lambda}c_{1}(\tilde{p},\tilde{q})

\displaystyle=G_{1}^{\vartheta}(\tilde{q})\qquad\forall\tilde{q}\in L^{2}(\Omega).

We also consider the following reaction-diffusion equation in weak form: for a given
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbf{H}_{D}^{1}(\Omega)
,
\tilde{r}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
,
\varphi_{D}\in H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma_{D})
and
g\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, find
(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\varphi)\in\mathbf{H}_{N}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)
such that

(2.11a)

\displaystyle a_{2}^{\boldsymbol{w},\tilde{r}}(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\xi})+b_{2}(\boldsymbol{\xi},\varphi)

\displaystyle=F_{2}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathbf{H}_{N}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega),

(2.11b)

\displaystyle b_{2}(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\psi)-\theta c_{2}(\varphi,\psi)

\displaystyle=G_{2}(\psi)\qquad\forall\psi\in L^{2}(\Omega).

3
Well-posedness of the continuous problem

The unique solvability of the elasticity and reaction-diffusion equations will be established using the theory of saddle-point problems with penalty
[
12
, Lemma 3]
. First we state an extended version of the Babuška–Brezzi theory from
[
11
, Theorem 2.1]
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
V,Q_{b}
be Hilbert spaces endowed with the (possibly parameter-dependent) norms
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{V}
and
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{Q_{b}}
, let
Q
be a dense (with respect to the norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{Q_{b}}
) linear subspace of
Q_{b}
and three bilinear forms
a(\cdot,\cdot)
on
V\times V
(assumed continuous, symmetric and positive semi-definite),
b(\cdot,\cdot)
on
V\times Q_{b}
(continuous), and
c(\cdot,\cdot)
on
Q\times Q
(symmetric and positive semi-definite); which define the linear operators
A:V\rightarrow V^{\prime}
,
B:V\rightarrow Q^{\prime}_{b}
and
C:Q\rightarrow Q^{\prime}
, respectively. Suppose further that

(3.1a)

\displaystyle\lVert\hat{v}\rVert_{V}^{2}\lesssim a(\hat{v},\hat{v})\qquad\forall\hat{v}\in\mathrm{Ker}(B),

(3.1b)

\displaystyle\lVert q\rVert_{Q_{b}}\lesssim\sup_{v\in V}\frac{b(v,q)}{{\lVert v\rVert}_{V}}\qquad\forall q\in Q_{b}.

Assume that
Q
is complete with respect to the norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{Q}^{2}:=\lVert\cdot\rVert_{Q_{b}}^{2}+t^{2}|\cdot|_{c}^{2}
, where
|\cdot|_{c}^{2}:=c(\cdot,\cdot)
is a semi-norm in
Q
. Let
t\in[0,1]
and set the parameter-dependent energy norm as

\lVert(v,q)\rVert_{V\times Q}^{2}:=\lVert v\rVert_{V}^{2}+\lVert q\rVert_{Q}^{2}=\lVert v\rVert_{V}^{2}+\lVert q\rVert_{Q_{b}}^{2}+t^{2}|q|_{c}^{2}.

Assume also that the following inf-sup condition holds

(3.2)

\displaystyle\lVert u\rVert_{V}\lesssim\sup_{(v,q)\in V\times Q}{\frac{a(u,v)+b(u,q)}{\lVert(v,q)\rVert_{V\times Q}}}\qquad\forall u\in V.

Then, for every
F\in V^{\prime}
and
G\in Q^{\prime}
, there exists a unique
(u,p)\in V\times Q
satisfying

\displaystyle a(u,v)+b(v,p)=F(v)

\displaystyle\qquad\forall v\in V,

\displaystyle b(u,q)-t^{2}c(p,q)=G(q)

\displaystyle\qquad\forall q\in Q.

Furthermore, the following continuous dependence on data holds

(3.4)

\displaystyle\lVert(u,p)\rVert_{V\times Q}

\displaystyle\lesssim\lVert F\rVert_{V^{\prime}}+\lVert G\rVert_{Q^{\prime}}.

Remark 3.1
.

The form
c(\cdot,\cdot)
does not require to be bounded in the norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{Q_{b}}
.
The space
Q_{b}
allows us to absorb the dependency of the model parameters directly in the norms, yielding a robust continuous dependence on data. That is, the hidden constant

associated with (
3.4
) only depends on the constants associated with the Brezzi–Braess conditions (
3.1
)-(
3.2
), and the continuity constants.

3.1
Unique solvability of the decoupled sub-problems

In this section, we verify that the assumptions of Theorem
1
are satisfied for (
2.10
) and (
2.11
). Some of the arguments employed in these proofs are standard and we omit them. For the sake of completeness, their explicit derivations can be found in reference
[
28
]
.

Let us adopt the following notation for the functional spaces for displacement and total volumetric stress
\mathbf{V}_{1}:=\mathbf{H}_{D}^{1}(\Omega)
and
Q_{1}=Q_{b_{1}}:=L^{2}(\Omega)
, equipped with the following scaled norms and seminorms

\displaystyle\lVert\boldsymbol{u}\rVert_{\mathbf{V}_{1}}^{2}:=2\mu\lVert\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})\rVert_{0,\Omega}^{2},\quad\lVert\tilde{p}\rVert_{Q_{1}}^{2}:=\lVert\tilde{p}\rVert_{Q_{b_{1}}}^{2}+\frac{1}{\lambda}|\tilde{p}|_{c_{1}}^{2},\quad\lVert\tilde{p}\rVert_{Q_{b_{1}}}^{2}:=\frac{1}{2\mu}\lVert\tilde{p}\rVert_{0,\Omega}^{2},\quad|\tilde{p}|_{c_{1}}^{2}:=\lVert\tilde{p}\rVert_{0,\Omega}^{2}.

On the other hand, let us denote the functional spaces for diffusive flux and concentration as
\mathbf{V}_{2}=\mathbf{H}_{N}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega)
and
Q_{2}=Q_{b_{2}}=L^{2}(\Omega)
, furnished with the following norms and seminorms

\displaystyle\lVert\boldsymbol{\zeta}\rVert_{\mathbf{V}_{2}}^{2}:=\lVert\boldsymbol{\zeta}\rVert_{\mathbb{M}}^{2}+M\lVert\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\zeta}\rVert_{0,\Omega}^{2},\quad\lVert\varphi\rVert_{Q_{2}}^{2}:=\lVert\varphi\rVert_{Q_{b_{2}}}^{2}+\theta|\varphi|_{c_{2}}^{2},

\displaystyle\lVert\boldsymbol{\zeta}\rVert_{\mathbb{M}}^{2}:=a_{2}^{\boldsymbol{w},\tilde{r}}(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\zeta}),\quad\lVert\varphi\rVert_{Q_{b_{2}}}^{2}:=\frac{1}{M}\lVert\varphi\rVert_{0,\Omega}^{2},\quad|\varphi|_{c_{2}}^{2}:=\lVert\varphi\rVert_{0,\Omega}^{2}.

Lemma 2
.

For a fixed
\vartheta\in Q_{2}
, assume that
1\leq\lambda
and
0<\mu
. Then, there exists a unique solution
(\boldsymbol{u},\tilde{p})\in\mathbf{V}_{1}\times Q_{1}
to (
2.10
). Furthermore,
there exists a constant
C_{1}>0
independent of the physical parameters, such that

(3.5)

\displaystyle\lVert(\boldsymbol{u},\tilde{p})\rVert_{\mathbf{V}_{1}\times Q_{1}}\leq C_{1}\left(\lVert F_{1}\rVert_{\mathbf{V}^{\prime}_{1}}+\lVert G_{1}^{\vartheta}\rVert_{Q^{\prime}_{1}}\right).

Proof.

Clearly,
Q_{1}
is a dense linear subspace of
Q_{b_{1}}
with the respective norms. Thanks to Körn’s inequality we readily have that
\lVert\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v})\rVert_{0,\Omega}
forms an equivalent norm to the standard
\mathbf{H}^{1}_{D}
-norm in
\mathbf{V}_{1}
. Next, we invoke the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality to conclude the boundedness of
a_{1}(\cdot,\cdot)
on
\mathbf{V}_{1}
.
In addition, by definition of
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathbf{V}_{1}}
, we have that
a_{1}(\cdot,\cdot)
is symmetric, positive semi-definite and coercive in
\mathbf{V}_{1}
(giving, in particular, condition (
3.1a
)). The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, the triangle inequality
\lVert\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}\rVert_{0,\Omega}\leq\lVert\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\rVert_{0,\Omega}
, and Körn’s inequality implies the boundedness of
b_{1}(\cdot,\cdot)
on
\mathbf{V}_{1}\times Q_{b_{1}}
.
Thanks to the structure of the problem and of the scaled norms, in the context of Theorem
1
we can identify
t=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\in(0,1]
, since
1\leq\lambda
. Moreover,
c_{1}(\cdot,\cdot)=(\cdot,\cdot)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
is symmetric and positive semi-definite. On the other hand, Körn’s and Cauchy–Schwarz inequalities imply that

\displaystyle\left|F_{1}(\boldsymbol{v})\right|=\left|\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\right|\leq\lVert\boldsymbol{f}\rVert_{0,\Omega}\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\rVert_{0,\Omega}\lesssim\lVert\boldsymbol{f}\rVert_{0,\Omega}\lVert\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v})\rVert_{0,\Omega}=\lVert\boldsymbol{f}\rVert_{Q_{b_{1}}}\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\rVert_{\mathbf{V}_{1}}\quad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbf{V}_{1}.

Similarly, for a given
\vartheta\in Q_{2}
, the property (
2.8
) and Cauchy–Schwarz inequality leads to

\displaystyle\left|G_{1}^{\vartheta}(\tilde{q})\right|=\left|-\frac{1}{\lambda}\int_{\Omega}\ell(\vartheta)\tilde{q}\right|\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}\lVert\ell(\vartheta)\rVert_{0,\Omega}\lVert\tilde{q}\rVert_{0,\Omega}\lesssim\lVert\vartheta\rVert_{Q_{1}}\lVert\tilde{q}\rVert_{Q_{1}}\quad\forall\tilde{q}\in Q_{1},

and this yields that
G_{1}^{\vartheta}(\cdot)
is continuous in
Q_{1}
.

F_{1}(\cdot)
and
G_{1}^{\vartheta}(\cdot)
are continuous in
\mathbf{V}_{1}
and
Q_{1}
, respectively.
For condition (
3.1b
), note that the surjectivity of the divergence operator
\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits:\mathbf{H}_{D}^{1}(\Omega)\rightarrow L^{2}(\Omega)
(see
[
18
, Lemma 53.9]
) gives the following inf-sup condition

\lVert\tilde{q}\rVert_{0,\Omega}\lesssim\sup_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}_{D}(\Omega)}\frac{b_{1}(\boldsymbol{v},\tilde{q})}{\lVert\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\rVert_{0,\Omega}}\qquad\forall\tilde{q}\in L^{2}(\Omega).

Scaling this bound with

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\mu}}
, and using the equivalence between
\lVert\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v})\rVert_{0,\Omega}
and
\lVert\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\rVert_{0,\Omega}
we obtain

\lVert\tilde{q}\rVert_{Q_{b_{1}}}\lesssim\sup_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbf{V}_{1}}\frac{b_{1}(\boldsymbol{v},\tilde{q})}{\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\rVert_{\mathbf{V}_{1}}}\qquad\forall\tilde{q}\in Q_{b_{1}}.

Finally, the inf-sup condition (
3.2
) is obtained as follows, for
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbf{V}_{1}
, let
\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{u}
and
\tilde{q}=0
. We have

\displaystyle a_{1}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u})+b_{1}(\boldsymbol{u},0)=a_{1}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u})=\lVert\boldsymbol{u}\rVert_{\mathbf{V}_{1}}^{2}\quad\text{ and }\quad\lVert(\boldsymbol{u},0)\rVert^{2}_{\mathbf{V}_{1}\times Q_{1}}=\lVert\boldsymbol{u}\rVert_{\mathbf{V}_{1}}^{2}.

Therefore,

\lVert\boldsymbol{u}\rVert_{\mathbf{V}_{1}}\lesssim\sup_{(\boldsymbol{v},\tilde{q})\in\mathbf{V}_{1}\times Q_{1}}{\frac{a_{1}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})+b_{1}(\boldsymbol{u},\tilde{q})}{\lVert(\boldsymbol{v},\tilde{q})\rVert_{\mathbf{V}_{1}\times Q_{1}}}}\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbf{V}_{1},

verifying the conditions of Theorem
1
. Then the unique solution satisfies (
3.5
), and the proof is complete.

Lemma 3
.

For a fixed pair
(\boldsymbol{w},\tilde{r})\in\mathbf{V}_{1}\times Q_{1}
, suppose that suppose that
\theta\leq\frac{1}{M}
in addition to the assumptions of Section
2
.
Then, there exists a unique pair
(\boldsymbol{\xi},\varphi)\in\mathbf{V}_{2}\times Q_{2}
solution to (
2.11
). Further, the following bound is satisfied

(3.6)

\displaystyle\lVert(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\varphi)\rVert_{\mathbf{V}_{2}\times Q_{2}}\leq C_{2}\left(\lVert F_{2}\rVert_{\mathbf{V}^{\prime}_{2}}+\lVert G_{2}\rVert_{Q^{\prime}_{2}}\right),

where the constant
C_{2}>0
does not depend on the physical parameters.

Proof.

It is straightforward to see that
Q_{b_{2}}
is a dense linear subspace of
Q_{2}
with the corresponding norms. From the properties of
\mathbb{M}
in (
2.7
), for given
(\boldsymbol{w},\tilde{r})\in\mathbf{V}_{1}\times Q_{1}
, the norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathbf{V}_{2}}
is a equivalent norm in
\mathbf{H}_{N}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega)
. Indeed, the assumptions
\frac{1}{M}\leq M
and (
2.7
) lead to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{M}\lVert\boldsymbol{\xi}\rVert_{\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega}=\frac{1}{M}\lVert\boldsymbol{\xi}\rVert_{0,\Omega}+\frac{1}{M}\lVert\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\xi}\rVert_{0,\Omega}\leq\lVert\boldsymbol{\xi}\rVert_{\mathbb{M}}+M\lVert\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\xi}\rVert_{0,\Omega}=\lVert\boldsymbol{\xi}\rVert_{\mathbf{V}_{2}},\text{ and }\lVert\boldsymbol{\xi}\rVert_{\mathbf{V}_{2}}\leq M\lVert\boldsymbol{\xi}\rVert_{\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega.}

Therefore, the bilinear form
a_{2}^{\boldsymbol{w},\tilde{r}}(\cdot,\cdot)
is positive semi-definite, continuous over
\mathbf{V}_{2}
, and coercive in the nullspace of the linear operator
B_{2}

\mathrm{Ker}(B_{2})=\{\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathbf{V}_{2}:\int_{\Omega}\varphi\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\xi}=0\text{, }\forall\varphi\in Q_{2}\}=\{\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathbf{V}_{2}:\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\xi}=0\}.

In addition,
from Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and the definition of the norms we arrive at the continuity of
b_{2}(\cdot,\cdot)
on
\mathbf{V}_{2}\times Q_{b_{2}}
. Furthermore, the bilinear operator
c_{2}(\cdot,\cdot)
coincides with
(\cdot,\cdot)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
which is symmetric and positive semi-definite and
t=\sqrt{\theta}\in[0,1]
. Next, the trace inequality together with the equivalence between
\lVert\boldsymbol{\xi}\rVert_{\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega}
and
\lVert\boldsymbol{\xi}\rVert_{\mathbf{V}_{2}}
yields the continuity of
F_{2}(\cdot)
on
\mathbf{V}_{2}
.
Likewise,
G_{2}(\cdot)
is continuous on
Q_{2}
. To prove condition (
3.1b
), note that the divergence operator
\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits:\mathbf{H}_{N}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega)\rightarrow L^{2}(\Omega)
is surjective (see
[
18
, Lemma 51.2]
). Consequently, we obtain an inf-sup condition in non-weighted norms, which
is then multiplied by
\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}
, giving

\lVert\varphi\rVert_{Q_{b_{2}}}\lesssim\sup_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}\in\mathbf{V}_{2}}\frac{b_{2}(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\varphi)}{\lVert\boldsymbol{\zeta}\rVert_{\mathbf{V}_{2}}}\qquad\forall\varphi\in Q_{b_{2}}.

Lastly, we verify the Braess condition (
3.2
) as follows: for
\boldsymbol{\zeta}\in\mathbf{V}_{2}
, let
\boldsymbol{\xi}=\boldsymbol{\zeta}
and
\varphi=M\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\zeta}

\displaystyle a_{2}^{\boldsymbol{w},\tilde{r}}(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\zeta})+b_{2}(\boldsymbol{\zeta},M\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\zeta})=a_{2}^{\boldsymbol{w},\tilde{r}}(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\zeta})+Mb_{2}(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\zeta})=\lVert\boldsymbol{\zeta}\rVert_{\mathbf{V}_{2}}^{2},

\displaystyle\lVert(\boldsymbol{\zeta},M\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\zeta})\rVert^{2}_{\mathbf{V}_{2}\times Q_{2}}

\displaystyle=\lVert\boldsymbol{\zeta}\rVert_{\mathbf{V}_{2}}^{2}+\lVert M\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\zeta\rVert_{Q_{b_{2}}}^{2}+\theta|M\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\zeta}|_{c_{2}}^{2}\lesssim\lVert\boldsymbol{\zeta}\rVert_{\mathbf{V}_{2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{M}\lVert M\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\zeta}\rVert_{0,\Omega}^{2}\lesssim\lVert\boldsymbol{\zeta}\rVert_{\mathbf{V}_{2}}^{2}.

Therefore,

\lVert\boldsymbol{\zeta}\rVert_{\mathbf{V}_{2}}\lesssim\sup_{(\boldsymbol{\xi},\varphi)\in\mathbf{V}_{2}\times Q_{2}}{\frac{a_{2}^{\boldsymbol{w},\tilde{r}}(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\xi})+b_{2}(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\varphi)}{\lVert(\boldsymbol{\xi},\varphi)\rVert_{\mathbf{V}_{2}\times Q_{2}}}}\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{\zeta}\in\mathbf{V}_{2}.

This verifies the conditions in Theorem

and therefore we also have that the asserted continuous dependence on data (

3.2
Fixed-point strategy

Let us now define the following solution operators

\mathcal{S}_{1}:Q_{2}\to\mathbf{V}_{1}\times Q_{1},\quad\vartheta\mapsto\mathcal{S}_{1}(\vartheta)=(\mathcal{S}_{11}(\vartheta),\mathcal{S}_{12}(\vartheta)):=(\boldsymbol{u},\tilde{p}),

where
(\boldsymbol{u},\tilde{p})
is the unique solution to (
2.10
), confirmed in Section
3.1
; and

\mathcal{S}_{2}:\mathbf{V}_{1}\times Q_{1}\to\mathbf{V}_{2}\times Q_{2},\quad(\boldsymbol{w},\tilde{r})\mapsto\mathcal{S}_{2}(\boldsymbol{w},\tilde{r})=(\mathcal{S}_{21}(\boldsymbol{w},\tilde{r}),\mathcal{S}_{22}(\boldsymbol{w},\tilde{r})):=(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\varphi),

where
(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\varphi)
is the unique solution to (
2.11
), also confirmed in Section
3.1
.

The nonlinear problem (
2.6
) is thus equivalent to the following fixed-point equation:

\text{Find $\varphi\in Q_{2}$ such that $\mathcal{A}(\varphi)=\varphi$},

where
\mathcal{A}:Q_{2}\to Q_{2}
is defined as

\varphi\mapsto\mathcal{A}(\varphi):=(\mathcal{S}_{22}\circ\mathcal{S}_{1})(\varphi)
.
Next, we show the Lipschitz continuity of
\mathcal{A}
, i.e., the Lipschitz continuity of the solution operators
\mathcal{S}_{1}
and
\mathcal{S}_{2}
.

Lemma 4
.

The operator
\mathcal{S}_{1}
is Lipschitz continuous with Lipschitz constant
L_{\mathcal{S}_{1}}=\sqrt{M}C_{1}L_{\ell}
.

Proof.

Let
\vartheta_{1},\vartheta_{2}\in Q_{2}
and
(\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\tilde{p}_{1}),(\boldsymbol{u}_{2},\tilde{p}_{2})
be the unique solutions to the problem (
2.10
). Define the following auxiliary problem: find
(\boldsymbol{u},\tilde{p})\in\mathbf{V}_{1}\times Q_{1}
such that

\displaystyle a_{1}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})+b_{1}(\boldsymbol{v},\tilde{p})

\displaystyle=0\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbf{V}_{1},

\displaystyle b_{1}(\boldsymbol{u},\tilde{q})-\frac{1}{\lambda}c_{1}(\tilde{p},\tilde{q})

\displaystyle=(G_{1}^{\vartheta_{1}}-G_{1}^{\vartheta_{2}})(\tilde{q})\qquad\forall\tilde{q}\in Q_{1}.

Note that
G_{1}^{\vartheta_{1}}-G_{1}^{\vartheta_{2}}\in Q_{1}^{\prime}
.
Then, the unique solution is given by
(\boldsymbol{u},\tilde{p})=(\boldsymbol{u}_{1}-\boldsymbol{u}_{2},\tilde{p}_{1}-\tilde{p}_{2})
. Therefore, we apply the continuous dependence on data (
3.5
) together with (
2.9b
) to obtain

\displaystyle\lVert\mathcal{S}_{1}(\vartheta_{1})-\mathcal{S}_{1}(\vartheta_{2})\rVert_{\mathbf{V}_{1}\times Q_{1}}

\displaystyle=\lVert(\boldsymbol{u}_{1}-\boldsymbol{u}_{2},\tilde{p}_{1}-\tilde{p}_{2})\rVert_{\mathbf{V}_{1}\times Q_{1}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\sup_{\tilde{q}\in Q_{1}}\frac{|(G_{1}^{\vartheta_{1}}-G_{1}^{\vartheta_{2}})(\tilde{q})|}{\lVert\tilde{q}\rVert_{Q_{1}}}\leq C_{1}\lVert\ell(\vartheta_{1})-\ell(\vartheta_{2})\rVert_{0,\Omega}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}L_{\ell}\lVert\vartheta_{1}-\vartheta_{2}\rVert_{0,\Omega}\leq\sqrt{M}C_{1}L_{\ell}\lVert\vartheta_{1}-\vartheta_{2}\rVert_{Q_{2}},

where the norm equivalence has been used in the last step.

Lemma 5
.

The operator
\mathcal{S}_{2}
is Lipschitz continuous with Lipschitz constant

L_{\mathcal{S}_{2}}=\max\left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\mu}},\sqrt{2\mu}\right\}\sqrt{M^{3}}C_{2}^{2}L_{\mathbb{M}}\left(\lVert\varphi_{D}\rVert_{\frac{1}{2},\Gamma_{D}}+\lVert g\rVert_{0,\Omega}\right).

Proof.

Analogously, as in Lemma
4
, let
(\boldsymbol{w}_{1},\tilde{r}_{1}),(\boldsymbol{w}_{2},\tilde{r}_{2})\in\mathbf{V}_{1}\times Q_{1}
be such that
(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{1},\varphi_{1}),(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{2},\varphi_{2})
are the unique solutions of their respective problem (
2.11
) and consider the following auxiliary problem: find
(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\varphi)\in\mathbf{V}_{2}\times Q_{2}
such that

\displaystyle a_{2}^{\boldsymbol{w}_{1},\tilde{r}_{1}}(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\xi})+b_{2}(\boldsymbol{\xi},\varphi)

\displaystyle=F^{*}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathbf{V}_{2},

\displaystyle b_{2}(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\psi)-\theta c_{2}(\varphi,\psi)

\displaystyle=0\qquad\forall\psi\in Q_{2},

where

F^{*}(\boldsymbol{\xi})=\int_{\Omega}(\mathbb{M}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{w}_{1}),\tilde{r}_{1})-\mathbb{M}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{w}_{2}),\tilde{r}_{2}))\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{2}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}.

The problem above results from subtracting the problems associated with
(\boldsymbol{w}_{1},\tilde{r}_{1})
and
(\boldsymbol{w}_{2},\tilde{r}_{2})
and rewriting
a_{2}^{\boldsymbol{w}_{1},\tilde{r}_{1}}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{2},\boldsymbol{\xi})
in the first equation. Note that
F^{*}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\in\mathbf{V}_{2}^{\prime}
. Therefore, thanks to Lemma
3
, the unique solution is given by
(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\varphi)=(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{2},\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2})
. Using (
3.6
), Hölder’s inequality and (
2.9a
) we get

\displaystyle\lVert\mathcal{S}_{2}(\boldsymbol{w}_{1},\tilde{r}_{1})-\mathcal{S}_{2}(\boldsymbol{w}_{2},\tilde{r}_{2})\rVert_{\mathbf{V}_{2}\times Q_{2}}=\lVert(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{2},\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2})\rVert_{\mathbf{V}_{2}\times Q_{2}}\leq C_{2}\sup_{\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathbf{V}_{2}}\frac{|F^{*}(\boldsymbol{\xi})|}{\lVert\boldsymbol{\xi}\rVert_{\mathbf{V}_{2}}}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\sqrt{M}C_{2}\lVert\mathbb{M}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{w}_{1}),\tilde{r}_{1})-\mathbb{M}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{w}_{2}),\tilde{r}_{2})\rVert_{\infty,\Omega}\lVert\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{2}\rVert_{0,\Omega}

\displaystyle\quad\leq MC_{2}L_{\mathbb{M}}\lVert(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{w}_{1}),\tilde{r}_{1})-(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{w}_{2}),\tilde{r}_{2})\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)}(\lVert\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{2}\rVert_{\mathbf{V}_{2}}+\lVert\varphi_{2}\rVert_{Q_{2}})

\displaystyle\quad\leq\max\left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\mu}},\sqrt{2\mu}\right\}\sqrt{M^{3}}C_{2}^{2}L_{\mathbb{M}}\left(\lVert\varphi_{D}\rVert_{\frac{1}{2},\Gamma_{D}}+\lVert g\rVert_{0,\Omega}\right)\lVert(\boldsymbol{w}_{1},\tilde{r}_{1})-(\boldsymbol{w}_{2},\tilde{r}_{2})\rVert_{\mathbf{V}_{1}\times Q_{1}}.

Lemma 6
.

The operator
\mathcal{A}
is Lipschitz continuous with Lipschitz constant
L_{\mathcal{A}}=L_{\mathcal{S}_{2}}L_{\mathcal{S}_{1}}
.

Proof.

We can combine the results from Lemmas
4
and
5
to assert that

\displaystyle\lVert\mathcal{A}(\varphi_{1})-\mathcal{A}(\varphi_{2})\rVert_{Q_{2}}

\displaystyle=\lVert\mathcal{S}_{22}(\mathcal{S}_{1}(\varphi_{1}))-\mathcal{S}_{22}(\mathcal{S}_{1}(\varphi_{2}))\rVert_{Q_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq L_{\mathcal{S}_{2}}\lVert\mathcal{S}_{1}(\varphi_{1})-\mathcal{S}_{1}(\varphi_{2})\rVert_{\mathbf{V}_{1}\times Q_{1}}\leq L_{\mathcal{S}_{2}}L_{\mathcal{S}_{1}}\lVert\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2}\rVert_{Q_{2}},

for any
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}\in Q_{2}
,
where we have also used the definition of the solution operators.

Finally, let us define the following set

(3.7)

{W}=\left\{{w}\in Q_{2}\colon\lVert w\rVert_{Q_{2}}\leq\sqrt{M}C_{2}\left(\lVert\varphi_{D}\rVert_{\frac{1}{2},\Gamma_{D}}+\lVert g\rVert_{0,\Omega}\right)\right\},

and note that the operator

satisfies that

. Indeed, from the continuous dependence on data (

), it is readily seen that

{\lVert{\mathcal{A}}(\varphi)\rVert}_{Q_{2}}=\lVert S_{22}(S_{1}(\varphi))\rVert_{Q_{2}}\leq\lVert S_{2}(S_{1}(\varphi))\rVert_{\mathbf{V}_{2}\times Q_{2}}\leq\sqrt{M}C_{2}\left(\lVert\varphi_{D}\rVert_{\frac{1}{2},\Gamma_{D}}+\lVert g\rVert_{0,\Omega}\right),

which implies that
\mathcal{A}(W)\subseteq W
. The following theorem provides the well-posedness of the coupled problem.

Theorem 7
.

Under the assumptions of Lemmas
2
-
3
, let

be as in (

) and the small data be given such that

C_{1}L_{\ell}\max\left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\mu}},\sqrt{2\mu}\right\}M^{2}C_{2}^{2}L_{\mathbb{M}}\left(\lVert\varphi_{D}\rVert_{\frac{1}{2},\Gamma_{D}}+\lVert g\rVert_{0,\Omega}\right)<1.

\displaystyle\lVert(\boldsymbol{u},\tilde{p})\rVert_{\mathbf{V}_{1}\times Q_{1}}

The unique solvability follows directly from Lemma

Remark 3.3

Note that in the regime of large Lamé second parameter (

\displaystyle\lVert\varphi_{D}\rVert_{\frac{1}{2},\Gamma_{D}}+\lVert g\rVert_{0,\Omega}<\frac{1}{M^{2}\sqrt{2\mu}}\frac{1}{C_{2}^{2}C_{1}L_{\mathcal{M}}L_{\ell}}.

4
Virtual element discretisation

The aim of this section is to introduce and analyse a VEM for each decoupled problem (
2.10
) and (
2.11
) based on
[
2
,
5
,
8
]
. Next, similarly as for the continuous problem, we employ a discrete fixed-point argument to prove the well-posedness of the coupled discrete problem.

Mesh assumptions and recurrent notation

Let
\mathcal{T}_{h}
be a decomposition of
\Omega
into polygonal elements
E
with diameter
h_{E}
and let
\mathcal{E}_{h}
be the set of edges
e
of
\mathcal{T}_{h}
with length
h_{e}
. We assume that for every
E
there exists
\rho_{E}>0
such that
E
is star-shaped with respect to every point of a disk with radius
\rho_{E}h_{E}
and in addition
h_{e}\geq\rho_{E}h_{E}
for every edge
e
of
E
. When considering a sequence
\{\mathcal{T}_{h}\}_{h}
we assume that
\rho_{E}\geq\rho>0
for some
\rho
independent of
E
, where
h
is the maximum diameter over
\mathcal{T}_{h}
.

Given an integer
k\geq 0
the space of polynomials of degree smaller or equal to
k
on
E
is denoted by
\mathcal{P}_{k}(E)
and the space of the gradients of polynomials of grade
\leq k+1
on
E
is denoted as
\mathcal{G}_{k}(E):=\nabla\mathcal{P}_{k+1}(E)
with standard notation
\mathcal{P}_{-1}(E)=\{0\}
for
k=-1
. The space
\mathcal{G}_{k}^{\oplus}(E)
denotes the complement of the space
\mathcal{G}_{k}(E)
in the vector polynomial space
(\mathcal{P}_{k}(E))^{2}
, that is,
(\mathcal{P}_{k}(E))^{2}=\mathcal{G}_{k}(E)\oplus\mathcal{G}_{k}^{\oplus}(E)
. In particular, following
[
9
]
, we set
\mathcal{G}_{k}^{\oplus}(E):=\boldsymbol{x}^{\perp}\mathcal{P}_{k-1}(E)
where
\boldsymbol{x}^{\perp}:=(y,-x)^{\tt t}
. Next, we define the continuous space of polynomials along the boundary
\partial E
of
E
as

\mathcal{B}_{k}(\partial E):=\left\{v\in C^{0}(\partial E)\colon v|_{e}\in\mathcal{P}_{k}(e),\quad\forall e\subset\partial E\right\}.

Let
\boldsymbol{x}_{E}=(x_{E},y_{E})^{\tt t}
denote the barycentre of
E
and let
\mathcal{M}_{k}(E)
be the set of
(k+1)(k+2)/2
scaled monomials

\mathcal{M}_{k}(E):=\left\{\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{E}}{h_{E}}\right)^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}},0\leq|{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}|\leq k\right\},

where
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
is a non-negative multi-index with
|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|=\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}
and
\boldsymbol{x}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=x^{\alpha_{1}}y^{\alpha_{2}}
for
\boldsymbol{x}=(x,y)^{\tt t}
. In particular, we can take the basis of
\mathcal{G}_{k}(E)
and
\mathcal{G}_{k}^{\oplus}(E)
as
\mathcal{M}_{k}^{\nabla}(E):=\nabla\mathcal{M}_{k+1}(E)\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
and
\mathcal{M}_{k}^{\oplus}(E):=\mathbf{m}^{\perp}\mathcal{M}_{k-1}(E)
, respectively, where
\mathbf{m}^{\perp}:=(\frac{y-y_{E}}{h_{E}},\frac{x_{E}-x}{h_{E}})^{\tt t}
and
(\mathcal{M}_{k}(E))^{2}=\mathcal{M}_{k}^{\nabla}(E)\oplus\mathcal{M}_{k}^{\oplus}(E)
holds.

4.1
Discrete formulation for the elasticity problem

In order to state the discrete spaces for (
2.10
) we adapt the approach from
[
8
]
to our formulation which involves the presence of the operator
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
in the bilinear form
a_{1}(\cdot,\cdot)
and the active-stress term
\ell(\vartheta)
in
G_{1}^{\vartheta}(\cdot)
.

VE spaces and DoFs

For
k_{1}\geq 2
, the VE space for displacement locally solves the Stokes problem and is defined by

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{1}^{h,k_{1}}(E)=\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}(E)\colon

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}|_{\partial E}\in(\mathcal{B}_{k_{1}}(\partial E))^{2},\;\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{P}_{k_{1}-1}(E),

\displaystyle-2\mu\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v})-\nabla s\in\mathcal{G}_{k_{1}-2}^{\oplus}(E)\text{ for some }s\in L^{2}_{0}(E)\}.

Observe that
(\mathcal{P}_{k_{1}}(E))^{2}\subseteq\mathbf{V}_{1}^{h,k_{1}}(E)
. For the discrete pressure space
Q_{1}^{h,k_{1}}(E)
, we can just locally consider the polynomials
\mathcal{P}_{k_{1}-1}(E)
. Then, the global discrete spaces are defined as

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{1}^{h,k_{1}}

\displaystyle=\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbf{V}_{1}\colon\boldsymbol{v}|_{E}\in\mathbf{V}_{1}^{h,k_{1}}(E),\ \forall E\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\},\qquad Q_{1}^{h,k_{1}}=\{\tilde{q}\in Q_{1}\colon\tilde{q}|_{E}\in Q_{1}^{h,k_{1}}(E),\ \forall E\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\}.

The DoFs for
\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\mathbf{V}_{1}^{h,k_{1}}(E)
can be taken as follows

(4.1a)

\displaystyle\bullet\text{The values of }\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\text{ at the vertices of $E$},

(4.1b)

\displaystyle\bullet\text{The values of }\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\text{ on the $k_{1}-1$ internal Gauss--Lobatto quadrature points on each edge of}\;E,

(4.1c)

\displaystyle\bullet\int_{E}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}_{h})m_{k_{1}-1},\quad\forall m_{k_{1}-1}\in\mathcal{M}_{k_{1}-1}(E)\setminus\{1\},

(4.1d)

\displaystyle\bullet\int_{E}\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\cdot\mathbf{m}_{k_{1}-2}^{\oplus},\quad\forall\mathbf{m}_{k_{1}-2}^{\oplus}\in\mathcal{M}_{k_{1}-2}^{\oplus}(E).

On the other hand, the DoFs for
\tilde{q}_{h}\in Q_{1}^{h,k_{1}}(E)
are selected as

(4.2)

\bullet\int_{E}\tilde{q}_{h}m_{k_{1}-1},\quad\forall m_{k_{1}-1}\in\mathcal{M}_{k_{1}-1}(E).

The set of DoFs (
4.1a
)-(
4.1d
) (resp. (
4.2
)) are unisolvent for the VE space
\mathbf{V}_{1}^{h,k_{1}}(E)
(resp.
Q_{1}^{h,k_{1}}(E)
) (see
[
8
, Proposition 3.1]
). Furthermore, if
N_{e}
denotes the number of edges of
E
, it is not difficult to check that

\dim(\mathbf{V}^{h,k_{1}}_{1}(E))=2N_{e}k_{1}+\frac{(k_{1}-1)(k_{1}-2)}{2}+\frac{(k_{1}+1)k_{1}}{2}-1,\quad\dim(Q_{1}^{h,k_{1}}(E))=\frac{(k_{1}+1)k_{1}}{2}.

Projection operators

For
E\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
, denote the energy projection operator by
\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{1}^{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},k_{1}}:\mathbf{H}^{1}(E)\rightarrow(\mathcal{P}_{k_{1}}(E))^{2}
, defined locally through

\int_{E}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{1}^{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},k_{1}}\boldsymbol{v}_{h})\colon\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{m}_{k_{1}})=0\quad\forall\mathbf{m}_{k_{1}}\in(\mathcal{M}_{k_{1}}(E))^{2}.

Note that the above definition of projection involving symmetric gradient leads to
\mathbf{0}
for polynomials
\mathbf{p}\in\left\{(1,0),(0,1),(-y,x)\right\}
. Therefore, we need to impose three additional conditions to uniquely define
\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{1}^{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},k_{1}}
. For example, we can take the following conditions from
[
47
]

\sum_{i=1}^{N_{v}}(\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{1}^{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},k_{1}}\boldsymbol{v}_{h}(z_{i}),\mathbf{p}(z_{i}))=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{v}}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h}(z_{i}),\mathbf{p}(z_{i})),\quad\forall\mathbf{p}\in\left\{(1,0),(0,1),(-y,x)\right\},

where
N_{v}
is the total number of vertices
z_{i}
of
E
. Moreover, the projection
\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{1}^{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},k_{1}}\boldsymbol{v}_{h}
is computable for
\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\mathbf{V}_{1}^{h,k_{1}}(E)
from (
4.1
) (see
[
8
, Section 3.2]
).
We also introduce the
L^{2}
-projection
\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{1}^{0,k_{1}}:\mathbf{L}^{2}(E)\rightarrow(\mathcal{P}_{k_{1}}(E))^{2}
defined locally, for a given
v_{h}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(E)
and
E\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
, as

\displaystyle\int_{E}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{1}^{0,k_{1}}\boldsymbol{v}_{h})\cdot\mathbf{m}_{k_{1}}

\displaystyle=0,\quad\forall\mathbf{m}_{k_{1}}\in(\mathcal{M}_{k_{1}}(E))^{2}.

Notice that
\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{1}^{0,k_{1}-2}\boldsymbol{v}_{h}
can be computed for
\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\mathbf{V}_{1}^{h,k_{1}}(E)
from (
4.1
) (see
[
8
, Section 3.3]
). Finally, the discrete right-hand side is defined as
\boldsymbol{f}_{h}:=\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{1}^{0,k_{1}-2}\boldsymbol{f}
for
\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)
.

The following two lemmas are classical projection and interpolation estimates, written here in the scaled norms (for sake of robustness with respect to model parameters). Let us first define the semi-norms induced by the spaces
\mathbf{V}_{1}
and
Q_{b_{1}}
as

\displaystyle|\boldsymbol{v}|_{s_{1}+1,\mathbf{V}_{1}}^{2}=2\mu|\boldsymbol{v}|_{s_{1}+1,\Omega}^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad|\tilde{q}|_{s_{1}+1,Q_{b_{1}}}^{2}=\frac{1}{2\mu}|\tilde{q}|_{s_{1}+1,\Omega}^{2}\quad\text{with}\quad 0\leq s_{1}\leq k_{1}.

Lemma 8
.

For any
\boldsymbol{v}\in(\mathbf{H}^{s_{1}+1}(\Omega)\cap\mathbf{V}_{1},|\cdot|_{s_{1}+1,\mathbf{V}_{1}})
and
\tilde{q}\in(H^{s_{1}+1}(\Omega)\cap Q_{b_{1}},|\cdot|_{s_{1}+1,Q_{b_{1}}})
, the polynomial projections
\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{1}^{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},k_{1}}\boldsymbol{v}
and
\Pi_{1}^{0,k_{1}}\tilde{q}
satisfy the following estimates

(4.3a)

\displaystyle\lVert\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{1}^{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},k_{1}}\boldsymbol{v}\rVert_{\mathbf{V}_{1}}

\displaystyle\lesssim h^{s_{1}}|\boldsymbol{v}|_{s_{1}+1,\mathbf{V}_{1}},

(4.3b)

\displaystyle\lVert\tilde{q}-\Pi_{1}^{0,k_{1}}\tilde{q}\rVert_{Q_{b_{1}}}

\displaystyle\lesssim h^{s_{1}+1}|\tilde{q}|_{s_{1}+1,Q_{b_{1}}}.

Proof.

Note that locally the following estimates hold (see
[
13
]
)

\displaystyle|\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{1}^{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},k_{1}}\boldsymbol{v}|_{1,E}

\displaystyle\lesssim h_{E}^{s_{1}}|\boldsymbol{v}|_{s_{1}+1,E},\quad\lVert\tilde{p}-\Pi_{1}^{0,k_{1}}\tilde{q}\rVert_{0,E}\lesssim h_{E}^{s_{1}+1}|\tilde{q}|_{s_{1}+1,E}.

Therefore, the equivalence between
|\cdot|_{1,\Omega}
and
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathbf{V}_{1}}
already discussed in Lemma
2
and the additive property of the norm imply that

\displaystyle\lVert\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{1}^{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},k_{1}}\boldsymbol{v}\rVert_{\mathbf{V}_{1}}\lesssim\sum_{E\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\sqrt{2\mu}|\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{1}^{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},k_{1}}\boldsymbol{v}|_{1,E}\lesssim\sum_{E\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}h_{E}^{s_{1}}\sqrt{2\mu}|\boldsymbol{v}|_{s_{1}+1,E}\leq h^{s_{1}}|\boldsymbol{v}|_{s_{1}+1,\mathbf{V}_{1}}.

Similarly

\displaystyle\lVert\tilde{q}-\Pi_{1}^{0,k_{1}}\tilde{q}\rVert_{Q_{b_{1}}}=\sum_{E\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\mu}}\lVert\tilde{q}-\Pi_{1}^{0,k_{1}}\tilde{q}\rVert_{0,E}\lesssim\sum_{E\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}h_{E}^{s_{1}+1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\mu}}|\tilde{q}|_{s_{1}+1,E}\leq h^{s_{1}}|\tilde{q}|_{s_{1}+1,Q_{b_{1}}}.

Interpolation operator

We define the Fortin operator
\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{1}^{F,k_{1}}:\mathbf{H}^{1+\delta}(E)\rightarrow\mathbf{V}_{1}^{h,k_{1}}(E)
for
\delta>0
(see
[
36
]
) through the DoFs (
4.1
) as

\mathrm{dof}_{j}(\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{1}^{F,k_{1}}\boldsymbol{v})=0,\quad\text{for all}\;j=1,\dots,\dim(\mathbf{V}_{1}^{h,k_{1}}(E))\;\text{and for any}\;\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbf{H}^{1+\delta}(E).

Let
\Pi_{1}^{0,k_{1}-1}
be the
L^{2}
-projection of
L^{2}(E)
onto the space
\mathcal{P}_{k_{1}-1}(E)
. From (
4.1c
),
\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{1}^{F,k_{1}}\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{P}_{k_{1}-1}(E)
and the following commutative property holds

\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{1}^{F,k_{1}}\boldsymbol{v}=\Pi_{1}^{0,k_{1}-1}\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}.

Lemma 9
.

Given
\boldsymbol{v}\in(\mathbf{H}^{s_{1}+1}(\Omega)\cap\mathbf{V}_{1}),|\cdot|_{s_{1}+1,\mathbf{V}_{1}})
, the Fortin interpolation operator
\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{1}^{F,k_{1}}
satisfies

(4.5)

\displaystyle\lVert\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{1}^{F,k_{1}}\boldsymbol{v}\rVert_{\mathbf{V}_{1}}\lesssim h^{s_{1}}|\boldsymbol{v}|_{s_{1}+1,\mathbf{V}_{1}}.

Proof.

The proof of the standard interpolation estimate

\displaystyle|\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{1}^{F,k_{1}}\boldsymbol{v}|_{1,E}\lesssim h_{E}^{s_{1}}|\boldsymbol{v}|_{s_{1}+1,E}

on each element
E\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
is provided in
[
36
, Theorem 2.4]
. This with the obvious scaling shows that

\displaystyle\lVert\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{1}^{F,k_{1}}\boldsymbol{v}\rVert_{\mathbf{V}_{1}}\lesssim\sum_{E\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\sqrt{2\mu}|\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{1}^{F,k_{1}}\boldsymbol{v}|_{1,E}\lesssim\sum_{E\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}h_{E}^{s_{1}}\sqrt{2\mu}|\boldsymbol{v}|_{s_{1}+1,E}\leq h^{s_{1}}|\boldsymbol{v}|_{s_{1}+1,\mathbf{V}_{1}}.

Discrete forms

For
E\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
, let
a_{1}^{E}(\cdot,\cdot)
be the restriction of
a_{1}(\cdot,\cdot)
to
E
. Then its discrete counterpart is defined, for all
\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\mathbf{V}_{1}^{h,k_{1}}(E)
, by

\displaystyle a_{1}^{h,E}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h}):=a_{1}^{E}(\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{1}^{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},k_{1}}\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{1}^{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},k_{1}}\boldsymbol{v}_{h})+S_{1}^{E}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{1}^{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},k_{1}}\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{1}^{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},k_{1}}\boldsymbol{v}_{h}),

where
S_{1}^{E}(\cdot,\cdot)
is any symmetric and positive definite bilinear form defined on
\mathbf{V}_{1}^{h,k_{1}}(E)\times\mathbf{V}_{1}^{h,k_{1}}(E)
that scales like
a_{1}^{E}(\cdot,\cdot)
. In particular, there holds

(4.6)

\displaystyle a_{1}^{E}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})\lesssim S_{1}^{E}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})\lesssim a_{1}^{E}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h}),\quad\forall\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\ker(\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{1}^{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},k_{1}}).

Set the global discrete bilinear form as
a_{1}^{h}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})=\sum_{E\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}a_{1}^{h,E}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})
for all
\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\mathbf{V}_{1}^{h,k_{1}}
.
For the linear functional
F_{1}
, the corresponding global discrete form
F^{h}_{1}(\cdot)
is defined as

F_{1}^{h}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h})=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{1}^{0,k_{1}-2}\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\quad\text{for}\;\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\mathbf{V}_{1}^{h,k_{1}}.

The computability of
\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{1}^{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},k_{1}}
and
\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{1}^{0,k_{1}-2}
implies that of
a_{1}^{h,E}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})
and
F_{1}^{h,E}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h})
. On the other hand, the definition of
\mathbf{V}_{1}^{h,k_{1}}
and
Q_{1}^{h,k_{1}}
allow us to state that the bilinear forms
b_{1}(\cdot,\cdot)
,
c_{1}(\cdot,\cdot)
and the functional
G_{1}^{\vartheta_{h}}(\cdot)
(with
\vartheta_{h}
belonging to the space
Q_{2}^{h,k_{2}}
, to be defined explicitly later in (
4.8b
)) are computable:

\displaystyle b_{1}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},\tilde{q}_{h})=\sum_{E\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\int_{E}\tilde{q_{h}}\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}_{h},\quad c_{1}(\tilde{p}_{h},\tilde{q}_{h})=\sum_{E\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\int_{E}\tilde{p}_{h}\tilde{q}_{h},\quad\text{and}\quad G_{1}^{\vartheta_{h}}(\tilde{q}_{h})=\sum_{E\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\int_{E}\ell(\vartheta_{h})\tilde{q}_{h},

where a proper order in the quadrature rule is used to handle the term
\ell(\vartheta_{h})
in
G_{1}^{\vartheta_{h}}(\tilde{p}_{h})
.

Discrete formulation

For a given
\vartheta_{h}\in Q_{2}^{h,k_{2}}
, find
(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\tilde{p}_{h})\in\mathbf{V}_{1}^{h,k_{1}}\times Q_{1}^{h,k_{1}}
such that

(4.7a)

\displaystyle a_{1}^{h}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})+b_{1}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},\tilde{p}_{h})

\displaystyle={F_{1}^{h}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h})}\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\mathbf{V}_{1}^{h,k_{1}},

(4.7b)

\displaystyle b_{1}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\tilde{q}_{h})-\frac{1}{\lambda}c_{1}(\tilde{p}_{h},\tilde{q}_{h})

\displaystyle=G_{1}^{\vartheta_{h}}(\tilde{q}_{h})\qquad\forall\tilde{p}_{h}\in Q_{1}^{h,k_{1}}.

4.2
Virtual element approximation for the reaction-diffusion problem

In order to state the discrete counterpart of (
2.11
) using VE spaces, we adapt the approach from
[
5
]
to our formulation with the stress-assisted diffusion
\mathbb{M}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{w}),\tilde{r})
as part of the bilinear form
a_{2}^{\boldsymbol{w},\tilde{r}}(\cdot,\cdot)
.

VE spaces and DoFs

For
k_{2}\geq 0
, the discrete VE space locally solves a div-rot problem, that is,

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{2}^{h,k_{2}}(E)=\{\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathbf{H}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,E)\cap\mathbf{H}(\mathop{\mathrm{rot}}\nolimits,E)\colon

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\xi}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}|_{e}\in\mathcal{P}_{k_{2}}(e)\text{ for all }e\in\partial E,

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathcal{P}_{k_{2}}(E),\;\mathop{\mathrm{rot}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathcal{P}_{k_{2}-1}(E)\}.

In turn, the global discrete spaces are defined as follows

(4.8a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{2}^{h,k_{2}}

\displaystyle=\{\boldsymbol{\zeta}\in\mathbf{V}_{2}\colon\boldsymbol{\zeta}|_{E}\in\mathbf{V}_{2}^{h,k_{2}}(E),\quad\forall E\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\},

(4.8b)

\displaystyle Q_{2}^{h,k_{2}}

\displaystyle=\{\varphi\in Q_{2}\colon\varphi|_{E}\in\mathcal{P}_{k_{2}}(E),\quad\forall E\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\}.

The DoFs for
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}\in\mathbf{V}_{2}^{h,k_{2}}(E)
can be taken as

(4.9a)

\displaystyle\bullet\text{Values of }\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\text{ on the $k_{2}+1$ Gauss--Lobatto quadrature points of each edge of $E$},

(4.9b)

\displaystyle\bullet\int_{E}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}\cdot\mathbf{m}_{k_{2}-1}^{\nabla},\quad\forall\mathbf{m}_{k_{2}-1}^{\nabla}\in\mathcal{M}_{k_{2}-1}^{\nabla}(E),

(4.9c)

\displaystyle\bullet\int_{E}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}\cdot\mathbf{m}_{k_{2}}^{\oplus},\quad\forall\mathbf{m}_{k_{2}}^{\oplus}\in\mathcal{M}_{k_{2}}^{\oplus}(E),

whereas the DoFs for
\psi_{h}\in Q_{2}^{h,k_{2}}(E)
are

(4.10)

\displaystyle\bullet\int_{E}\psi_{h}m_{k_{2}},\quad\forall m_{k_{2}}\in\mathcal{M}_{k_{2}}(E).

Note that
(\mathcal{P}_{k_{2}}(E))^{2}\subseteq\mathbf{V}_{2}^{h,k_{2}}(E)
and
\mathcal{P}_{k_{2}}(E)=Q_{2}^{h,k_{2}}(E)
. The set of DoFs (
4.9a
)-(
4.9c
) (resp. (
4.10
)) are unisolvent for the local virtual space
\mathbf{V}_{2}^{h,k_{2}}(E)
(resp.
Q_{2}^{h,k_{2}}(E)
), see
[
14
, Proposition 3.5]
for a proof (although the DoFs are slightly different, the proof follows similarly). Moreover, it is readily seen that

\dim(\mathbf{V}^{h,k_{2}}_{2}(E))=N_{e}(k_{2}+1)+\frac{k_{2}(k_{2}-1)}{2}-1+\frac{(k_{2}+2)(k_{2}+1)}{2},\quad\dim(Q_{2}^{h,k_{2}}(E))=\frac{(k_{2}+1)k_{2}}{2}.

Projection operators

Let us define the local
L^{2}
-projection
\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{2}^{0,k_{2}}:\mathbf{L}^{2}(E)\rightarrow(\mathcal{P}_{k_{2}}(E))^{2}
by

\int_{E}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{2}^{0,k_{2}}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h})\cdot\mathbf{m}_{k_{2}}=0,\quad\forall\mathbf{m}_{k_{2}}\in(\mathcal{M}_{k_{2}}(E))^{2},\quad\forall E\in\mathcal{T}_{h}.

Note that the projection
\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{2}^{0,k_{2}}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}
is computable for
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h}\in\mathbf{V}_{2}^{h,k_{2}}(E)
(see
[
5
, Section 3.2]
).

For sake of the forthcoming analysis, let us define semi-norms induced by the spaces
\mathbf{V}_{2}
and
Q_{b_{2}}
as

\displaystyle|\boldsymbol{\xi}|_{s_{2}+1,\mathbf{V}_{2}}^{2}=M|\boldsymbol{\xi}|_{s_{2}+1,\Omega}^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad|\psi|_{s_{2}+1,Q_{b_{2}}}^{2}=\frac{1}{M}|\psi|_{s_{2}+1,\Omega}^{2},\quad\text{with}\quad 0\leq s_{2}\leq k_{2}.

Lemma 10
.

For any
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in(\mathbf{H}^{s_{2}+1}(\Omega)\cap\mathbf{V}_{2},|\cdot|_{s_{2}+1,\mathbf{V}_{2}})
and
\psi\in(H^{s_{2}+1}(\Omega)\cap Q_{b_{2}},|\cdot|_{s_{2}+2,Q_{b_{2}}})
, the polynomial projections
\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{2}^{0,k_{2}}\boldsymbol{\xi}
and
\Pi_{2}^{0,k_{2}}\psi
satisfy the following estimates

\displaystyle\lVert\boldsymbol{\xi}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{2}^{0,k_{2}}\boldsymbol{\xi}\rVert_{\mathbb{M}}\lesssim h^{s_{2}+1}|\boldsymbol{\xi}|_{s_{2}+1,\mathbf{V}_{2}},\quad\lVert\psi-\Pi_{2}^{0,k_{2}}\psi\rVert_{Q_{b_{2}}}\lesssim h^{s_{2}+1}|\psi|_{s_{2}+1,Q_{b_{2}}}.

Proof.

Classical results (see
[
13
]
) lead to the bounds

\lVert\boldsymbol{\xi}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{2}^{0,k_{2}}\boldsymbol{\xi}\rVert_{0,E}\lesssim h_{E}^{s_{2}+1}|\boldsymbol{\xi}|_{s_{2}+1,E},\qquad\lVert\psi-\Pi_{2}^{0,k_{2}}\psi\rVert_{0,E}\lesssim h_{E}^{s_{2}+1}|\psi|_{s_{2}+1,E}.

Since
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathbb{M}}\leq\sqrt{M}\lVert\cdot\rVert_{0,\Omega}
, we can easily see that

\displaystyle\lVert\boldsymbol{\xi}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{2}^{0,k_{2}}\boldsymbol{\xi}\rVert_{\mathbb{M}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{E\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\sqrt{M}\lVert\boldsymbol{\xi}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{2}^{0,k_{2}}\boldsymbol{\xi}\rVert_{0,E}\lesssim\sum_{E\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}h_{E}^{s_{2}+1}\sqrt{M}|\boldsymbol{\xi}|_{s_{2}+1,E}\leq h^{s_{2}+1}|\boldsymbol{\xi}|_{s_{2}+1,\mathbf{V}_{2}},

\displaystyle\lVert\psi-\Pi_{2}^{0,k_{2}}\psi\rVert_{Q_{b_{2}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{E\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\lVert\psi-\Pi_{2}^{0,k_{2}}\psi\rVert_{0,E}\lesssim\sum_{E\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}h_{E}^{s_{2}+1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}|\psi|_{s_{2}+1,E}\leq h^{s_{2}+1}|\psi|_{s_{2}+1,Q_{b_{2}}}.

Interpolation operator

The Fortin operator
\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{2}^{F,k_{2}}:\mathbf{H}^{1}(E)\rightarrow\mathbf{V}_{2}^{h,k_{2}}(E)
can be defined directly from (
4.9
) as

\displaystyle\mathrm{dof}_{j}(\boldsymbol{\xi}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{2}^{F,k_{2}}\boldsymbol{\xi})=0,\quad\text{for all}\;j=1,\dots,\dim(\mathbf{V}_{2}^{h,k_{2}}(E))\;\text{and for}\;\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}(E).

Moreover, (
4.9b
) and an integration by parts imply the following commutative property:

\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{2}^{F,k_{2}}\boldsymbol{\xi}=\Pi_{2}^{0,k_{2}}\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\xi}.

Lemma 11
.

The Fortin interpolation operator
\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{2}^{F,k_{2}}
satisfies the following estimate

(4.11)

\displaystyle\lVert\boldsymbol{\xi}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{2}^{F,k_{2}}\boldsymbol{\xi}\rVert_{\mathbb{M}}\lesssim h^{s_{2}+1}|\boldsymbol{\xi}|_{s_{2}+1,\mathbf{V}_{2}}\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{\xi}\in(\mathbf{H}^{s_{2}+1}(\Omega)\cap\mathbf{V}_{2},|\cdot|_{s_{2}+1,{\mathbf{V}_{2}}}).

The standard interpolation estimate from
[
5
]
shows
\|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{2}^{F,k_{2}}\boldsymbol{\xi}\|_{0,E}\lesssim h^{s_{2}+1}|\boldsymbol{\xi}|_{s_{2}+1,E}
.
Hence

\displaystyle\lVert\boldsymbol{\xi}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{2}^{F,k_{2}}\boldsymbol{\xi}\rVert_{\mathbb{M}}\leq\sum_{E\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\sqrt{M}\|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{2}^{F,k_{2}}\boldsymbol{\xi}\|_{0,E}\lesssim\sum_{E\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}h^{s_{2}+1}\sqrt{M}|\boldsymbol{\xi}|_{s_{2}+1,E}\leq h^{s_{2}+1}|\boldsymbol{\xi}|_{s_{2}+1,\mathbf{V}_{2}}.

Discrete forms

Given
{\overline{\boldsymbol{w}}_{h}:=\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{1}^{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},k_{1}}\boldsymbol{w}_{h}}\in\mathbf{V}_{1}^{h,k_{1}}
and
\tilde{r}_{h}\in Q_{1}^{h,k_{1}}

Finally, the definition of

\displaystyle b_{2}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h},\varphi_{h})=\sum_{E\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\int_{E}\varphi_{h}\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\xi}_{h},\quad c_{2}(\varphi_{h},\psi_{h})=\sum_{E\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\int_{E}\varphi_{h}\psi_{h},

and

Theorem 12
.

Let

\lVert\cdot\rVert_{V^{h}}
and
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{Q_{b}^{h}}

be a dense (with respect to the norm

and three bilinear forms

on
V^{h}\times V^{h}
(continuous, symmetric and positive semi-definite),
b^{h}(\cdot,\cdot)
on
V^{h}\times Q_{b}^{h}
(continuous), and
c^{h}(\cdot,\cdot)
on
Q^{h}\times Q^{h}
(symmetric and positive semi-definite); which define three linear operators
A^{h}:V^{h}\rightarrow(V^{h})^{\prime}
,
B^{h}:V^{h}\rightarrow(Q^{h}_{b})^{\prime}
and
C^{h}:Q^{h}\rightarrow(Q^{h})^{\prime}
, respectively. Suppose further that

(4.14a)

\displaystyle\lVert\hat{v_{h}}\rVert_{V^{h}}^{2}\lesssim a^{h}(\hat{v}_{h},\hat{v}_{h})\qquad\forall\hat{v}_{h}\in\mathrm{Ker}(B^{h}),

(4.14b)

\displaystyle\lVert q_{h}\rVert_{Q_{b}^{h}}\lesssim\sup_{v_{h}\in V^{h}}\frac{b^{h}(v_{h},q_{h})}{{\lVert v_{h}\rVert}_{V^{h}}}\qquad\forall q_{h}\in Q_{b}^{h}.

Assume that
Q^{h}
is complete with respect to the norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{Q^{h}}^{2}:=\lVert\cdot\rVert_{Q_{b}^{h}}^{2}+t^{2}|\cdot|_{c^{h}}^{2}
, where
|\cdot|_{c^{h}}^{2}:=c^{h}(\cdot,\cdot)
is a semi-norm in
Q^{h}
. Let
t\in[0,1]
and set the parameter-dependent energy norm as

\lVert(v_{h},q_{h})\rVert_{V^{h}\times Q^{h}}^{2}:=\lVert v_{h}\rVert_{V^{h}}^{2}+\lVert q_{h}\rVert_{Q^{h}}^{2}=\lVert v_{h}\rVert_{V^{h}}^{2}+\lVert q_{h}\rVert_{Q_{b}^{h}}^{2}+t^{2}|q|_{c^{h}}^{2}.

Assume also that the following inf-sup condition holds

(4.15)

\displaystyle\lVert u_{h}\rVert_{V^{h}}\lesssim\sup_{(v_{h},q_{h})\in V^{h}\times Q^{h}}{\frac{a^{h}(u_{h},v_{h})+b^{h}(u_{h},q_{h})}{\lVert(v_{h},q_{h})\rVert_{V^{h}\times Q^{h}}}}\qquad\forall u_{h}\in V^{h}.

Then, for every
F^{h}\in(V^{h})^{\prime}
and
G^{h}\in(Q^{h})^{\prime}
, there exists a unique
(u_{h},p_{h})\in V^{h}\times Q^{h}
satisfying

\displaystyle a^{h}(u_{h},v_{h})+b^{h}(v_{h},p_{h})=F^{h}(v_{h})

\displaystyle\qquad\forall v_{h}\in V^{h},

\displaystyle b^{h}(u_{h},q_{h})-t^{2}c^{h}(p_{h},q_{h})=G^{h}(q_{h})

\displaystyle\qquad\forall q_{h}\in Q^{h}.

Furthermore, the following continuous dependence on data holds

(4.17)

\displaystyle\lVert(u_{h},p_{h})\rVert_{V^{h}\times Q^{h}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\lVert F^{h}\rVert_{(V^{h})^{\prime}}+\lVert G^{h}\rVert_{(Q^{h})^{\prime}}.

The well-posedness of the uncoupled discrete problems (
4.7
) and (
4.13
) is given next.

Lemma 13
.

Given
\vartheta_{h}\in Q_{2}^{h,k_{2}}

\displaystyle\lVert(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\tilde{p}_{h})\rVert_{\mathbf{V}_{1}\times Q_{1}}\leq\overline{C}_{1}\left(\lVert F_{1}^{h}\rVert_{\mathbf{V}_{1}^{\prime}}+\lVert G_{1}^{\vartheta_{h}}\rVert_{Q_{1}^{\prime}}\right),

Proof.

The same arguments in Lemma
2
can be used to carry over the properties of

and

to the discrete formulation. On the other hand, the positive semi-definiteness of the stabilisation term

Indeed, concerning boundedness, the second inequality in (
4.6

\displaystyle a_{1}^{h,E}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\lesssim a_{1}^{E}(\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{1}^{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},k_{1}}\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{1}^{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},k_{1}}\boldsymbol{u}_{h})+a_{1}^{E}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{1}^{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},k_{1}}\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{1}^{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},k_{1}}\boldsymbol{u}_{h})=\lVert\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\rVert_{\mathbf{V}_{1}(E)}^{2}.

\lVert\tilde{q_{h}}\rVert_{0,\Omega}\lesssim\sup_{\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\mathbf{V}_{1}^{h,k_{1}}}\frac{b_{1}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},\tilde{q}_{h})}{\lVert\nabla\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\rVert_{0,\Omega}}\qquad\forall\tilde{q}_{h}\in Q_{1}^{h,k_{1}},

6.3
Example 3: Lithiation of an anode

In this example, we investigate the 2D version of the model presented in
[
43
]
, and simulate the microscopic lithiation of a perforated circular anode particle. The outer and inner radius of the particle are
\rho_{o}=$5\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}$
and
\rho_{i}=$1\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}$
, respectively. We discretise the domain with a Voronoi mesh of 10000 elements and the VE spaces are selected as in Section
4
with
k_{1}=2
and
k_{2}=1
. In this test, the particle is clamped and zero lithium fluxes are prescribed on the inner circumference
\Gamma_{i}
. The maximum lithium concentration
\hat{\Omega}=$2.29\text{\times}{10}^{-14}\text{\,}\frac{\mathrm{mol}}{\mu\mathrm{m}^{3}}$
is fixed on the outer circumference
\Gamma_{o}
. We check two cases: no traction and a traction of
-2\text{\times}{10}^{-4}\text{\,}\frac{\mathrm{N}}{\mu\mathrm{m}^{2}}
on
\Gamma_{o}
. The Young’s modulus is given by
E=$1\text{\times}{10}^{-2}\text{\,}\frac{\mathrm{N}}{\mu\mathrm{m}^{2}}$
and the Poisson ratio is
\nu=0.3
, and from these values, we compute
\lambda=\frac{E\nu}{(1+\nu)(1-2\nu)}
and
\mu=\frac{E}{2(1+\nu)}
. The diffusive source is zero and there is no body load force. The stress-assisted diffusion coefficient is given by
\mathbb{M}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})=m_{0}(\mathbb{I}+m_{0}m_{1}\sigma^{2})
with
m_{0}=$1\text{\times}{10}^{2}\text{\,}\frac{\mu\mathrm{m}^{2}}{\mathrm{s}}$
,
m_{1}=0
for the decoupled case and
m_{1}=$1\text{\times}{10}^{3}\text{\,}\frac{\mu\mathrm{m}^{2}}{\mathrm{N}}$
for the coupled case. The active stress is measured as follows
\ell(\varphi)=K_{0}\varphi
, where
K_{0}=\tilde{\Omega}\frac{2\mu+3\lambda}{3}
and the partial molar volume is
\tilde{\Omega}=$3.497\text{\times}{10}^{12}\text{\,}\frac{\mu\mathrm{m}^{3}}{\mathrm{mol}}$
. Finally, the remaining parameters are set as
\theta=M=1
.

In Figure
6.7
we can observe that the non–linear coupling term
\mathbb{M}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})
in each case (no traction and traction applied) has an impact on the behaviour of the concentration along the radial direction of the particle. In addition, the pressure profiles in Figure
6.6
show a change given by the traction boundary conditions imposed. The results presented coincide qualitatively with the simulations reported in
[
43
]
.
