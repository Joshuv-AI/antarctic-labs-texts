On Equivalence of Matrices footnoteinfo
Daizhan Cheng

Abstract
A new matrix product, called the semi-tensor product (STP), is briefly reviewed. The STP extends the classical matrix product to two arbitrary matrices. Under STP the set of matrices becomes a monoid (semi-group with identity). Some related structures and properties are investigated. Then the generalized matrix addition is also introduced, which extends the classical matrix addition to a class of two matrices with different dimensions. Motivated by STP of matrices, two kinds of equivalences of matrices (including vectors) are introduced, which are called matrix equivalence (M-equivalence) and vector equivalence (V-equivalence) respectively. The lattice structure has been established for each equivalence. Under each equivalence, the corresponding quotient space becomes a vector space. Under M-equivalence, many algebraic, geometric, and analytic structures have been posed to the quotient space, which include (i) lattice structure; (ii) inner product and norm (distance); (iii) topology; (iv) a fiber bundle structure, called the discrete bundle; (v) bundled differential manifold; (vi) bundled Lie group and Lie algebra. Under V-equivalence, vectors of different dimensions form a vector space 𝒱 {\mathcal{V}} , and a matrix A A of arbitrary dimension is considered as an operator (linear mapping) on 𝒱 {\mathcal{V}} . When A A is a bounded operator (not necessarily square but includes square matrices as a special case), the generalized characteristic function, eigenvalue and eigenvector etc. are defined. In one word, this new matrix theory overcomes the dimensional barrier in certain sense. It provides much more freedom for using matrix approach to practical problems.

1.2
Introduction

Matrix theory and calculus are two classical and fundamental mathematical tools in modern science and technology. There are two mostly used operators on the set of matrices: conventional matrix product and matrix addition. Roughly speaking, the object of matrix theory is
({\mathcal{M}},+,\times)
, where
{\mathcal{M}}
is the set of all matrices. Unfortunately, unlike the arithmetic system
({\mathbb{R}},+,\times)
, in matrix theory both “
+
” and “
\times
” are restricted by the matrix dimensions. Precisely speaking: consider two matrices
A\in{\mathcal{M}}_{m\times n}
and
B\in{\mathcal{M}}_{p\times q}
. Then the “product”,
A\times B
, is well posed, if and only if,
n=p
; the “addition”
A+B
, is defined, if and only if,
m=p
and
n=q
. Though there are some other matrix products such as Kronecker product, Hadamard product etc., but they are of different stories
[
24
]
.

The main purpose of this paper is to develop a new matrix theory, which intends to overcome the dimension barrier by extending the matrix product and matrix addition to two matrices which do not meet the classical dimension requirement. As generalizations of the classical ones, they should be consistent with the classical definitions. That is, when the dimension requirements of two argument matrices in classical matrix theory are satisfied, the newly defined operators should coincide with the original one.

Because of the extension of the two fundamental operators, many related concepts can be extended. For instance, the characteristic functions, the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of a square matrix can be extended to certain non-square matrices; Lie algebraic structure can also be extended to dimension-varying square matrices. All these extensions should be consistent with the classical ones. In one word, we are developing the classical matrix theory but not violating any of the original matrix theory.

When we were working on generalizing the fundamental matrix operators we meet a serious problem: Though the extended operators are applicable to certain sets of matrices with different dimensions, they fail to be vector space anymore. This drawback is not acceptable, because it blocked the way to extend many nice algebraic or geometric structures in matrix theory, such as Lie algebraic structure, manifold structure etc., to the enlarged set, which includes matrices of different dimensions. To overcome this obstacle, we eventually introduced certain equivalence relations. Then the quotient spaces, called the equivalence spaces, become vector spaces. Two equivalence relations have been proposed. They are
matrix equivalence
(M-equivalence) and
vector equivalence
(V-equivalence).

Then many nice algebraic, analytic, and geometric structures have been developed on the M-equivalence spaces. They are briefly introduced as follows:

•

Lattice structure: The elements in each equivalent class form a lattice. The class of spaces with different dimensions also form a lattice. The former and the latter are homomorphic. The lattices obtained for M-equivalence and V-equivalence are isomorphic.

•

Topological structure: A topological structure is proposed to the M-equivalence space, which is built by using topological sub-base. It is then proved that under this topology the equivalence space is Hausdorff (
T_{2}
) space and is second countable.

•

Inner product structure: An inner product is proposed on the M-equivalence space. The norm (distance) is also obtained. It is proved that the topology induced by this norm is the same as the topology produced by using the topological sub-base.

•

Fiber bundle structure. A fiber bundle structure is proposed for the set of matrices (as total space) and the equivalent classes (as base space). The bundle structure is named the discrete bundle, because each fiber has discrete topology.

•

Bundled manifold structure: A (dimension-varying) manifold structure is proposed for the M-equivalence space. Its coordinate charts are constructed via the discrete bundle. Hence it is called a bundled manifold.

•

Bundled Lie algebraic structure: A Lie algebra structure is proposed for the M-equivalence space. The Lie algebra is of infinite dimensional, but almost all the properties of finite dimensional Lie algebras remain available.

•

Bundled Lie group structure: For the M-equivalence classes of square nonsingular matrices a group structure is proposed. It has also the dimension-varying manifold structure. Both the algebraic and the geometric structures are consistent and hence it becomes a Lie group. The relationship of this Lie group with the bundled Lie algebra is also investigated.

Under V-equivalence, all the vectors of varying dimensions form a vector space
{\mathcal{V}}
, and any matrix
A
can be considered as a linear operator on
{\mathcal{V}}
. A very important class of
A
, called the bounded operator, is investigated in detail. For a bounded operator
A
, which could be non-square, its characteristic function is proposed. Its eigenvalues and the corresponding eigenvectors are obtained. A generalized
A
-invariant subspace has been discussed in detail.

2.2
M-equivalence of Matrices

The set of all matrices (over certain field
{\mathbb{F}}
) is denoted by
{\mathcal{M}}
, that is

{\mathcal{M}}=\bigcup_{m=1}^{\infty}\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}{\mathcal{M}}_{m\times n}.

It is obvious that STP is an operator defined as
\ltimes(~\mbox{or}~\rtimes):{\mathcal{M}}\times{\mathcal{M}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{M}}
. Observing the definition of STP carefully, it is not difficult to find that when we use STP to multiply
A
with
B
, instead of
A
itself, we modify
A
by Kronecker multiplying different sizes
of identity to multiply different
B
’s. In fact, STP multiplies an equivalent class of
A
, precisely,

\left<A\right>=\{A,A\otimes I_{2},A\otimes I_{3},\cdots\}
, with an equivalent class of
B
, that is,
\left<B\right>=\{B,~B\otimes I_{2},~B\otimes I_{3},\cdots\}
.

Motivated by this fact, we first propose an equivalence over set of matrices, called the matrix equivalence (
\sim_{\ell}
or
\sim_{r}
). Then STP can be considered as an operator over the equivalent classes. We give a rigorous definition for the equivalence.

Definition 12

Let
A,B\in{\mathcal{M}}
be two matrices.

1.

A
and
B
are said to be left matrix equivalent (LME), denoted by
A\sim_{\ell}B
, if there exist two identity matrices
I_{s}
,
I_{t}
,
s,t\in{\mathbb{N}}
, such that

A\otimes I_{s}=B\otimes I_{t}.

2.

A
and
B
are said to be right matrix equivalent (RME), denoted by
A\sim_{r}B
, if there exist two identity matrices
I_{s}
,
I_{t}
,
s,t\in{\mathbb{N}}
, such that

I_{s}\otimes A=I_{t}\otimes B.

Remark 13
.

It is easy to verify that the LME
\sim_{\ell}
(similarly, RME
\sim_{r}
) is an
equivalence relation. That is, it is (i) self-reflexive (
A\sim_{\ell}A
); (ii) symmetric (if
A\sim_{\ell}B
,
then
B\sim_{\ell}A
); and (iii) transitive (if
A\sim_{\ell}B
, and
B\sim_{\ell}C
, then
A\sim_{\ell}C
).

Definition 14

Given
A\in{\mathcal{M}}
.

1.

The left equivalent class of
A
is denoted by

\left<A\right>_{\ell}:=\left\{B\;|\;B\sim_{\ell}A\right\};

2.

The right equivalent class of
A
is denoted by

\left<A\right>_{r}:=\left\{B\;|\;B\sim_{r}A\right\}.

3.

A
is left (right) reducible, if there is an
I_{s}
,
s\geq 2
, and a matrix

, such that

A=B\otimes I_{s}
(correspondingly,

). Otherwise,

is left (right) irreducible.

Lemma 15
.

Assume
A\in{\mathcal{M}}_{\beta\times\beta}

Example 42

{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}^{s}\sqsubset{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}^{t}

i|j
and

Since

, we have

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{l}\varphi\left({\mathcal{M}}_{\mu}^{i}\times{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}^{s}\right)={\mathcal{M}}_{\mu\lambda}^{is},\\
\sqsubset{\mathcal{M}}_{\mu\lambda}^{jt}=\varphi\left({\mathcal{M}}_{\mu}^{j}\times{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}^{t}\right).\end{array}

That is,
\varphi
is an order-preserving mapping.

Consider two elements in product lattice as

and
\beta={\mathcal{M}}_{\mu}^{q}\times{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}^{t}
. Following the same arguments in the proof of Theorem

\begin{array}[]{l}\lcm(\alpha,~\beta)={\mathcal{M}}_{\mu}^{p\vee q}\times{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}^{s\vee t},\\
\gcd(\alpha,~\beta)={\mathcal{M}}_{\mu}^{p\wedge q}\times{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}^{s\wedge t}.\\
\end{array}

Then

\begin{array}[]{l}\varphi(\lcm(\alpha,~\beta))={\mathcal{M}}_{\mu\lambda}^{(p\vee q)(s\vee t)},\\
\varphi(\gcd(\alpha,~\beta))={\mathcal{M}}_{\mu\lambda}^{(p\wedge q)(s\wedge t)}.\\
\end{array}

Consider

\begin{array}[]{l}\varphi(\alpha)={\mathcal{M}}_{\mu\lambda}^{ps},\\
\varphi(\beta)={\mathcal{M}}_{\mu\lambda}^{qt}.\\
\end{array}

Now

\begin{array}[]{l}\lcm(\varphi(\alpha),\varphi(\beta))={\mathcal{M}}_{\mu\lambda}^{(ps)\vee(qt)},\\
\gcd(\varphi(\alpha),\varphi(\beta))={\mathcal{M}}_{\mu\lambda}^{(ps)\wedge(qt)}.\\
\end{array}

It is obvious that in general

(p\vee q)(s\vee t)\neq(ps)\vee(qt),

as well as,

(p\wedge q)(s\wedge t)\neq(ps)\wedge(qt).

Hence,
\varphi
is not a homomorphism.

3.3
Coordinate Frame on
\Sigma_{\mu}

It is obvious that
\left(\Sigma_{\mu},\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\pm$}\right)
is an infinite dimensional vector space. Since each
\left<A\right>\in\Sigma_{\mu}
has finite coordinate expression, we may try to avoid using a basis with infinite elements. To this end, we construct a set of “consistent” coordinate frames as
\{O_{1},O_{2},\cdots\}
. Then
\left<A\right>
can be expressed by
A_{1}\in\Span\{O_{i}\}
,
A_{2}\in\Span\{O_{i+1}\}
, and so on. Moreover,
O_{i}\subset O_{i+1}
is a subset (or
O_{i}
is part of coordinate elements in
O_{i+1}
). Then it seems that
\left<A\right>
can always be expressed in
O_{i}
no matter which representative is chosen. The purpose of this section is to build such a set of consistent coordinate sub-frames, which forms an overall coordinate frame.

Assume
A_{\alpha}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{\mu}^{\alpha}
,
A_{\beta}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{\mu}^{\beta}
,
A_{\alpha}\sim A_{\beta}
, and
\alpha|\beta
, then
A_{\alpha}\otimes I_{k}=A_{\beta}
, where
k=\beta/\alpha
.
Recall that the order determined by this space-subspace relation is denoted as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{M}}_{\mu}^{\alpha}\sqsubset{\mathcal{M}}_{\mu}^{\beta}.

(78)

One sees easily that we can define an embedding mapping
bd_{k}:{\mathcal{M}}_{\mu}^{\alpha}\rightarrow{\mathcal{M}}_{\mu}^{\beta}
as

\displaystyle bd_{k}(A):=A\otimes I_{k}.

(79)

In this way,
{\mathcal{M}}_{\mu}^{\alpha}
can be considered as a subspace of
{\mathcal{M}}_{\mu}^{\beta}
.

In the following we construct a proper coordinate frame on
{\mathcal{M}}_{\mu}^{\beta}
, which makes
{\mathcal{M}}_{\mu}^{\alpha}
its coordinate subspace, that is,
{\mathcal{M}}_{\mu}^{\alpha}
is generated by part of coordinate variables of
{\mathcal{M}}_{\mu}^{\beta}
. To this end, we build a set of orthonormal basis on
{\mathcal{M}}_{\mu}^{\beta}
as follows:

Assume
p=\mu_{y}
and
q=\mu_{x}
. Splitting
C\in{\mathcal{M}}_{\mu}^{\beta}
into
\alpha p\times\alpha q
blocks, where each block is of dimension
k\times k
, yields

C=\begin{bmatrix}C^{1,1}&C^{1,2}&\cdots&C^{1,\alpha q}\\
\vdots&~&~&~\\
C^{\alpha p,1}&C^{\alpha p,2}&\cdots&C^{\alpha p,\alpha q}\end{bmatrix}.

Then for each
C^{I,J}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{k\times k}
we construct a basis, which consists of three classes:

•

Class 1:

\displaystyle\Delta^{I,J}_{i,j}=(b_{u,v})\in{\mathcal{M}}_{k\times k},\quad i\neq j,

(80)

where

b_{u,v}=\begin{cases}1,\quad u=i,~v=j\\
0,\quad\mbox{otherwise}.\end{cases}

That is, for
(I,J)
-th block, at each given non-diagonal position
(i,j)
, set it to be
1
, and all other entries to be
0
.

•

Class 2:

\displaystyle D^{I,J}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}I_{k}^{I,J}.

(81)

That is, at each
(I,J)
-th block, set
D^{I,J}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}I_{k}
as a basis element.

•

Class 3:

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ccl}E^{I,J}_{t}&=&\frac{1}{\sqrt{t(t-1)}}\diag\left(\underbrace{1,\cdots,1}_{t-1},-(t-1),\underbrace{0,\cdots,0}_{k-t}\right),\\
&&\quad t=2,\cdots,k.\end{array}

That is, set
E^{I,J}_{t}
as the rest of basis elements of the diagonal subspace of
(I,J)
-th block, which are orthogonal to
D^{I,J}
.

Let
A,B\in{\mathcal{M}}_{m\times n}
. Recall that the Frobenius inner product is defined as
[
24
]

\displaystyle(A|~B)_{F}:=\mathop{\sum}\limits_{i=1}^{m}\mathop{\sum}\limits_{j=1}^{n}a_{i,j}b_{i,j}.

(84)

Correspondingly, the Frobenius norm is defined as

\displaystyle\|A\|_{F}:=\sqrt{(A|~A)_{F}}.

(85)

If
(A|~B)_{F}=0
, then
A
is said to be orthogonal with
B
.

Using Frobenius inner product, it is easy to verify the following result.

Proposition 72

1.

Set

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ccl}B^{I,J}&:=&\left\{\Delta^{I,J}_{i,j},\;1\leq i\neq j\leq k;\;D^{I,J};\right.\\
&&\left.E^{I,J}_{t},\;t=2,\cdots,k\right\},\end{array}

then
B^{I,J}
is an orthonormal basis for
(I,J)
-th block.

2.

Set

B:=\left\{B^{I,J}\;|\;I=1,2,\cdots,\alpha p;\;J=1,2,\cdots,\alpha q\right\},

then
B
is an orthonormal basis for
{\mathcal{M}}_{\mu}^{\beta}
.

3.

Set

D:=\left\{D^{I,J}\;|\;I=1,2,\cdots,\alpha p;\;J=1,2,\cdots,\alpha q\right\},

then
D
is an orthonormal basis for subspace
{\mathcal{M}}_{\mu}^{\alpha}\subset{\mathcal{M}}_{\mu}^{\beta}
.

3.4
Inner Product on
\Sigma_{\mu}

Let
A=\left(a_{i,j}\right),~B=\left(b_{i,j}\right)\in{\mathcal{M}}_{m\times n}
. It is well known that the Frobenius inner product of

and

is defined by (

The following lemma comes from a straightforward computation.

Lemma 77
.

Let

\displaystyle\left(A\otimes I_{k}\;|\;B\otimes I_{k}\right)_{F}=k(A\;|\;B)_{F}.

(92)

Definition 78

. Then the weighted inner product of

\displaystyle(A\;|\;B)_{W}:=\frac{1}{t}\left(A\otimes I_{t/\alpha}\;|\;~B\otimes I_{t/\beta}\right)_{F},

(93)

where

is the least common multiple of
\alpha
and
\beta
.

Using Lemma
77
and Definition

, we have the following property.

Proposition 79

Let

, if
A
and
B
are orthogonal, i.e.,

, then

A\otimes I_{\xi}
and

are also orthogonal.

Now we are ready to define the inner product on
\Sigma_{\mu}
.

Definition 80

Let
\left<A\right>,~\left<B\right>\in\Sigma_{\mu}
. Their inner product is defined as

\displaystyle(\left<A\right>\;|\;~\left<B\right>):=(A\;|\;~B)_{W}.

(94)

The following proposition shows that (

) is well defined.

Proposition 81

Definition
80
is well defined. That is, (
94
) is independent of the choice of the representatives
A
and

.

Proof
. Assume
A_{1}\in\left<A\right>
and
B_{1}\in\left<B\right>
are irreducible. Then it is enough to prove that

\displaystyle(A\;|\;~B)_{W}=(A_{1}\;|\;~B_{1})_{W},\quad A\in\left<A\right>,~B\in\left<B\right>.

(95)

Assume
A_{1}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{\mu}^{\alpha}
and
B_{1}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{\mu}^{\beta}

\end{array}

Definition 82

(
[
44
]
)

A real or complex vector space
X
is an inner-product space, if there is a mapping
X\times X\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}~(\mbox{or}~{\mathbb{C}})
, denoted by
(x|~y)
, satisfying

1.

(x+y\;|\;z)=(x\;|\;z)+(y\;|\;z),\quad x,y,z\in X.

2.

(x\;|\;y)=\overline{(y\;|\;x)},

where the bar stands for complex conjugate.

3.

(ax\;|\;y)=a(x\;|\;y),\quad a\in{\mathbb{R}}~(\mbox{or}~{\mathbb{C}}).

4.

(x\;|\;x)\geq 0,~\mbox{and}~(x\;|\;x)\neq 0~\mbox{if}~x\neq 0.

.

(\Sigma_{\mu},\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\pm$})

(96)

The following is some standard results for inner product space.

Theorem 84
.

Assume
\left<A\right>,\left<B\right>\in\Sigma_{\mu}
. Then we have the following

1.

(Schwarz Inequality)

\displaystyle|(\left<A\right>\;|\;\left<B\right>)|\leq\|\left<A\right>\|\|\left<B\right>\|;

(97)

(98)

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{l}\|\left<A\right>\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\pm$}\left<B\right>\|^{2}+\|\left<A\right>\vdash\left<B\right>\|^{2}\\

Finally, we present the generalized Pythagorean theorem:

Theorem 85
.

Let
\left<A\right>_{i}\in\Sigma_{\mu}
,

be an orthogonal set. Then

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{l}\|\left<A\right>_{1}\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\pm$}\left<A\right>_{2}\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\pm$}\cdots\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\pm$}\left<A\right>_{n}\|^{2}\\

A natural question is: “Is
\Sigma_{\mu}
a Hilbert space?” Unfortunately, this is not true. This fact is shown in the following counter-example.

Example 86

Define a sequence of elements, denoted as

\left\{\left<A\right>_{k}\;\big|\;k=1,2,\cdots\right\}
, as follows:

A_{1}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{\mu}^{1}
is arbitrary. Define
A_{k}
inductively as

A_{k+1}=A_{k}\otimes I_{2}+E_{k+1}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{\mu}^{2^{k}},\quad k=1,2,\cdots,

where
E_{s}=\left(e^{s}_{i,j}\right)\in{\mathcal{M}}_{\mu}^{2^{s-1}}
(
s\geq 2
) is defined as

e^{s}_{i,j}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2^{s}},&i=1,\;j=2,\\
0,&\mbox{Otherwise}.\end{cases}

First, we claim that
\left\{\left<A\right>_{k}:=\left<A_{k}\right>\;\big|\;k=1,2,\cdots\right\}
is a Cauchy sequence. Let
n>m
. Then

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{l}\left\|\left<A\right>_{m}\vdash\left<A\right>_{n}\right\|\\
\leq\left\|\left<A\right>_{m}\vdash\left<A\right>_{m+1}\right\|+\cdots+\left\|\left<A\right>_{n-1}\vdash\left<A\right>_{n}\right\|\\
\leq\frac{1}{2^{m+1}}+\cdots+\frac{1}{2^{n}}\leq\frac{1}{2^{m}}.\end{array}

Then we prove by contradiction that it does not converge to any element. Assume it converges to
\left<A_{0}\right>
, it is enough to consider the following three cases:

•

Case 1, assume
A_{0}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{\mu}^{2^{s}}
and
A_{0}=A_{s+1}
.
Then

\left\|\left<A_{0}\right>\vdash\left<A_{s+2}\right>\right\|=\left\|\left<A_{s+1}\right>\vdash\left<A_{s+2}\right>\right\|=\frac{1}{2^{s+2}}.

Similar to (
86
) we can prove that

\left\|\left<A_{0}\right>\vdash\left<A_{t}\right>\right\|>\frac{1}{2^{s+2}},\quad t>s+2.

Hence the sequence can not converge to
\left<A_{0}\right>
.

•

Case 2, assume
A_{0}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{\mu}^{2^{s}}
and
A_{0}\neq A_{s+1}
.
Note that
A_{0}\vdash A_{s+1}
is orthogonal to
E_{s+2}
, then it is clear that

\left\|\left<A_{0}\vdash A_{s+2}\right>\right\|>\left\|\left<A_{0}\vdash A_{s+1}\right>\right\|.

Note that by construction we have that as long as
t\geq s+2
then

A_{t}-A_{s+1}
and
A_{0}-A_{s+1}
are orthogonal. Using generalized Pythagorean theory, we have

\begin{array}[]{l}\left\|\left<A_{0}\vdash A_{t}\right>\right\|\\
=\sqrt{\left[\left<A_{0}\vdash A_{s+1}\right>\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\pm$}\left<A_{s+1}\vdash A_{t}\right>\right]^{2}}\\
=\sqrt{\left[\left<A_{0}\vdash A_{s+1}\right>\right]^{2}+\left[\left<A_{s+1}\vdash A_{t}\right>\right]^{2}}\\
>\|\left<A_{0}\vdash A_{s+1}\right>\|>0,\quad t\geq s+2.\end{array}

Hence the sequence can not converge to
\left<A_{0}\right>

This section considers the vector equivalence (V-equivalence) of vectors. Since many results are parallel and simpler than M-equivalence, some detailed discussions are omitted.

7.1

Consider the set of vectors on field
{\mathbb{F}}

\displaystyle{\mathcal{V}}:=\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}{\mathcal{V}}_{i},

(232)

where
{\mathcal{V}}_{i}
is the

{\mathcal{V}}

Our purpose is to build a vector space structure on

{\mathcal{V}}

Definition 201

1.

Let

.
X
and
Y
are said to be V-equivalent, denoted by

, if there exist two one-vectors
{\bf 1}_{s}
and

such that

\displaystyle X\otimes{\bf 1}_{s}=Y\otimes{\bf 1}_{t}.

(233)

2.

The equivalent class is denoted as

\lceil X\rceil:=\left\{Y\;\big|\;Y\leftrightarrow X\right\}.

3.

In an equivalent class
\lceil X\rceil
a partial order (
\leqslant
) is defined as:
X\leqslant Y
, if there exists a one-vector
{\bf 1}_{s}
such that
X\otimes{\bf 1}_{s}=Y
.
X_{1}\in\lceil X\rceil
is irreducible, if there are no
Y
and
{\bf 1}_{s}
,
s>1
, such that
X_{1}=Y\otimes{\bf 1}_{s}
.

Remark 202
.

1.

The equivalence defined above can be seen as the left equivalence. Formally, we set
\leftrightarrow:=\leftrightarrow_{l}
.

2.

The right equivalence can be defined as follows: Let
X,~Y\in{\mathcal{V}}
.
X
and
Y
are said to be right equivalent, denoted by
X\leftrightarrow_{r}Y
, if there exist two one-vectors
{\bf 1}_{s}
and
{\bf 1}_{t}
such that

\displaystyle{\bf 1}_{s}\otimes X={\bf 1}_{t}\otimes Y.

(234)

3.

The right equivalent class of
X
is denoted as
\lceil X\rceil_{r}
. Of course, we have
\lceil X\rceil_{l}=\lceil X\rceil
.

4.

Hereafter, the left equivalence is considered as the defaulted one. That is, we always assume
\leftrightarrow=\leftrightarrow_{l}
. But with some obvious modifications one sees easily that the arguments/results in the sequel are also valid for right equivalence.

The following properties of the M-equivalence are also true for V-equivalence. The proofs are also similar to the M-equivalence. Therefore, they are omitted.

Theorem 203

\Gamma

X_{1}
is irreducible.

.

1.

Consider an equivalent class
\lceil X\rceil

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{l}\left(A\ltimes B\right)\vec{\ltimes}X\\
=\left[(A\otimes I_{t/n})(B\otimes I_{t/p})\right]\vec{\ltimes}X\\
=\left\{\left[(A\otimes I_{t/n})(B\otimes I_{t/p})\right]\otimes I_{\frac{sp}{qt}}\right\}\left(X\otimes{\bf 1}_{s/r}\right)\\
=\left(A\otimes I_{\frac{sp}{nq}}\right)\left(B\otimes I_{s/q}\right)\left(X\otimes{\bf 1}_{s/r}\right)\\
=\left(A\otimes I_{\frac{sp}{nq}}\right)\left\{\left[\left(B\otimes I_{\ell/q}\right)\left(X\otimes{\bf 1}_{\ell/r}\right)\right]\otimes{\bf 1}_{\phi}\right\}\\
=\left(A\otimes I_{\frac{sp}{nq}}\right)\left[\left(B\vec{\ltimes}X\right)\otimes{\bf 1}_{\phi}\right],\end{array}

where

\begin{array}[]{l}t=n\vee p\\
s=\left(\frac{qt}{p}\right)\vee r\\
\ell=q\vee r\\
s=\ell\phi.\end{array}

Note that
B\vec{\ltimes}X\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\frac{p\ell}{q}}
. By definition if

\displaystyle\frac{n\vee\frac{p\ell}{q}}{n}=\frac{sp}{nq}

(325)

and

\displaystyle\frac{n\vee\frac{p\ell}{q}}{\frac{p\ell}{q}}=\phi,

(326)

then (
7.8
) becomes

A\vec{\ltimes}\left(B\vec{\ltimes}X\right),

and we are done.

It is clear that both (
325
) and (
326
) are equivalent to

\displaystyle n\vee\frac{p\ell}{q}=\frac{sp}{q}.

(327)

Hence as long as (
327
) holds, we are done.

In the following we prove (
327
).

Since
t=n\vee p
, assume

n\wedge p=u,

then

n=\alpha u,\quad p=\beta u,\quad t=\alpha\beta u,\quad\alpha\wedge\beta=1.

Next,

s=\frac{qt}{p}\vee r=q\alpha\vee r.

Assume
d_{1},d_{2},\cdots,d_{s}
are the set of prime factors of
q\wedge r
. Then we can express
q
and
r
as follows:

\begin{array}[]{l}q=d_{1}^{Q_{1}}d_{2}^{Q_{2}}\cdots d_{s}^{Q_{s}}q_{0},\\
r=d_{1}^{R_{1}}d_{2}^{R_{2}}\cdots d_{s}^{R_{s}}r_{0},\\
\end{array}

where
q_{0}\wedge r_{0}=1
,
q_{0}\wedge d_{i}=1
, and
r_{0}\wedge d_{i}=1
,
Q_{i}>0
,
R_{i}>0
,
i=1,\cdots,s
.

Assume
\epsilon_{1},\cdots,\epsilon_{t}
are the common prime factors of
\alpha
and
r
, which are distinct from
\{d_{1},\cdots,d_{s}\}
.
Then we can express
\alpha
and
r
respectively as follows:

\begin{array}[]{l}\alpha=d_{1}^{A_{1}}d_{2}^{A_{2}}\cdots d_{s}^{A_{s}}\epsilon_{1}^{B_{1}}\epsilon_{2}^{B_{2}}\cdots\epsilon_{t}^{B_{t}}\alpha_{0},\\
r=d_{1}^{R_{1}}d_{2}^{R_{2}}\cdots d_{s}^{R_{s}}\epsilon_{1}^{C_{1}}\epsilon_{2}^{C_{2}}\cdots\epsilon_{t}^{C_{t}}r_{00},\\
\end{array}

where
B_{i}>0
,
C_{i}>0
,
i=1,\cdots,t
,
A_{j}\geq 0
,
j=1,\cdots,s
.
A_{j}
might be zero, if
\alpha
has no such a factor. Now
\alpha_{0}\wedge r_{00}=1
,
\alpha_{0}\wedge d_{j}=1
,
r_{00}\wedge d_{j}=1
,
j=1,\cdots,s
,
\alpha_{0}\wedge\epsilon_{i}=1
,
r_{00}\wedge\epsilon_{i}=1
,

.

Now we can calculate the left hand side of (
327
):

\ell=q\vee r=d_{1}^{\max(Q_{1},R_{1})}\cdots d_{s}^{\max(Q_{s},R_{s})}\epsilon_{1}^{C_{1}}\cdots\epsilon_{t}^{C_{t}}q_{0}r_{00};

\frac{\ell}{q}=d_{1}^{\max(Q_{1},R_{1})-Q_{1}}\cdots d_{s}^{\max(Q_{s},R_{s})-Q_{s}}\epsilon_{1}^{C_{1}}\cdots\epsilon_{t}^{C_{t}}r_{00};

\frac{p\ell}{q}=d_{1}^{\max(Q_{1},R_{1})-Q_{1}}\cdots d_{s}^{\max(Q_{s},R_{s})-Q_{s}}\epsilon_{1}^{C_{1}}\cdots\epsilon_{t}^{C_{t}}r_{00}\beta u.

Hence, we have

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{l}n\vee\frac{p\ell}{q}=\left(u\alpha_{0}\delta_{1}^{A_{1}}\cdots\delta_{s}^{A_{s}}\epsilon_{1}^{B_{1}}\cdots\epsilon_{t}^{B_{t}}\right)\\
~~\vee\left(d_{1}^{\max(Q_{1},R_{1})-Q_{1}}\cdots d_{s}^{\max(Q_{s},R_{s})-Q_{s}}\epsilon_{1}^{C_{1}}\cdots\epsilon_{t}^{C_{t}}r_{00}\beta u\right)\\
~=u\alpha_{0}r_{00}\beta d_{1}^{\max(\max(Q_{1},R_{1})-Q_{1},A_{1})}\cdots\\
~~d_{s}^{\max(\max(Q_{s},R_{s})-Q_{s},A_{s})}\epsilon_{1}^{\max(C_{1},B_{1})}\cdots\epsilon_{t}^{\max(C_{t},B_{t})}.\end{array}

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{l}\frac{sp}{q}=d_{1}^{\max(A_{1}+Q_{1},R_{1})-Q_{1}}\cdots d_{s}^{\max(A_{s}+Q_{s},R_{s})-Q_{s}}\\
~~\epsilon_{1}^{\max(B_{1},C_{1})}\cdots\epsilon_{t}^{\max(B_{t},C_{t})}\beta u\alpha_{0}r_{00}.\end{array}

Comparing (

8
Concluding Remarks

Matrix theory is one of the most fundamental and useful tools in modern science and technology. But one of the major weaknesses is its dimension restriction. To overcome this barrier, the purpose of this paper is to set up a framework for an almost dimension-free matrix theory.

First, we review the STP (
\ltimes
), which extends the conventional matrix product to overall matrices
{\mathcal{M}}
. The related monoid structure for
\left({\mathcal{M}},~\ltimes\right)
is obtained. The M-equivalence
\sim
is proposed. A lattice structure over each equivalence class is obtained. The equivalence space, as the quotient space
{\mathcal{M}}/\sim
is introduced and discussed.

Second, the set of overall matrices is partitioned into subspaces as
{\mathcal{M}}=\bigcup_{\mu\in{\mathbb{Q}}_{+}}{\mathcal{M}}_{\mu}
. The STA (

\pm

) is proposed. Under this addition the quotient spaces
\Sigma_{\mu}={\mathcal{M}}_{\mu}/\sim

become vector spaces. Certain geometric and algebraic structures are revealed. Including topological structure, inner product structure, differential manifold structure, etc.

Particularly, when
\mu=1
, (corresponding to square matrices) we have extended the Lie algebra and Lie group theory to
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
. A fiber bundle structure, called the discrete bundle, is proposed for
{\mathcal{M}}_{\mu}
and the extended Lie group and Lie algebra.

Finally, the set of overall vectors
{\mathcal{V}}
are considered as a universal vector space, based on the vector equivalence
\leftrightarrow
. A matrix
A
of any dimension can be considered either a linear mapping on
{\mathcal{V}}
, or a subspace generated by its columns.
The
A
-invariant subspace is discussed in details. Many key concepts such as eigenvalue/eigenvector, characteristic polynomial of a matrix have been extended from square matrices to no-square matrices.

It was said by Asimov that “Only in mathematics is there no significant correction - only existence. Once the Greeks had developed the deductive method, they were correct in what they did, correct for all the time.”
[
43
]
All extensions we did in this paper consist with the classical ones. That is, when the dimension restrictions required by the classical matrix theory are satisfied the new operators proposed in this paper coincide with the classical ones.

There are many questions remain for further discussion. For instance, is it possible to construct an equivalence over
{\mathcal{M}}
, which is consistent with certain matrix product, such that the quotient space becomes a vector space?

The followings are some possible equivalences on
{\mathcal{M}}
.

1.

Equivalence 1:

Definition 266

Let
A,~B\in{\mathcal{M}}
.
A
is said to be equivalent to
B
, denoted by
A\simeq B
, if there exist
I_{i}
,
I_{j}
,
\I_{s}
,
\I_{t}

\displaystyle\I_{\alpha}^{T}\otimes A\otimes I_{i}=\I_{\beta}^{T}\otimes B\otimes I_{j}.

It is easy to verify that

Theorem 267

Assume

\Lambda

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{l}A=\I^{T}_{p}\otimes\Lambda\otimes I_{s},\\
B=\I^{T}_{q}\otimes\Lambda\otimes I_{t}.\end{array}

Hence the lattice structure similar to M-equivalence exists.

It may be considered as a combination of M- and V-equivalences. Unfortunately, (i) it is not consistent with STP (
\ltimes
