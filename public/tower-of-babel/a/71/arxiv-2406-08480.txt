Linear equations with monomial constraints and decision problems in abelian-by-cyclic groups
Ruiwen Dong

Abstract
We show that it is undecidable whether a system of linear equations over the Laurent polynomial ring ℤ ⁡ [ X ± ] \mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}] admit solutions where a specified subset of variables take value in the set of monomials { X z ∣ z ∈ ℤ } \{X^{z}\mid z\in\mathbb{Z}\} . In particular, we construct a finitely presented ℤ ⁡ [ X ± ] \mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}] -module, where it is undecidable whether a linear equation X z 1 ​ 𝒇 1 + ⋯ + X z n ​ 𝒇 n = 𝒇 0 X^{z_{1}}\boldsymbol{f}_{1}+\cdots+X^{z_{n}}\boldsymbol{f}_{n}=\boldsymbol{f}_{0} has solutions z 1 , … , z n ∈ ℤ z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\in\mathbb{Z} . This contrasts the decidability of the case n = 1 n=1 , which can be deduced from Noskov’s Lemma. We apply this result to settle a number of problems in computational group theory. We show that it is undecidable whether a system of equations has solutions in the wreath product ℤ ≀ ℤ \mathbb{Z}\wr\mathbb{Z} , providing a negative answer to an open problem of Kharlampovich, López and Miasnikov (2020). We show that there exists a finitely generated abelian-by-cyclic group in which the problem of solving a single (spherical) quadratic equation is undecidable, answering an open problem of Lysenok and Ushakov (2021). We also construct a finitely generated abelian-by-cyclic group, different to that of Mishchenko and Treier (2017), in which the Knapsack Problem is undecidable. In contrast, we show that the problem of Coset Intersection is decidable in all finitely generated abelian-by-cyclic groups.



Keywords:
linear equation over modules, Laurent polynomials, abelian-by-cyclic groups, Knapsack Problem, equation over groups, Coset Intersection

Introduction and main results

Linear equations with constraints

In the first part of this paper we consider linear equations over the Laurent polynomial ring
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]=\mathbb{Z}[X,X^{-1}]
with monomial constraints on certain variables.
Solving linear equations with additional constraints is widely studied in a much larger context.
Fix a commutative ring
R
(such as
\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{Q},\mathbb{Z}[X]
or
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]
) and a subset
S
of
R
(such as
\mathbb{N},\mathbb{Q}_{>0},\mathbb{N}[X]
or
\{X^{z}\mid z\in\mathbb{Z}\}
).
Given
m\geq n\geq 1
, as well as elements
a_{ij}\in R,i=0,1,\ldots,m;j=1,\ldots,d
, a central problem is to decide whether the system of linear equations

\displaystyle a_{11}x_{1}+\cdots+a_{n1}x_{n}+a_{n+1,1}x_{n+1}+\cdots+a_{m1}x_{m}

\displaystyle=a_{01},

\displaystyle a_{12}x_{1}+\cdots+a_{n2}x_{n}+a_{n+1,2}x_{n+1}+\cdots+a_{m2}x_{m}

\displaystyle=a_{02},

(1)

\displaystyle a_{1d}x_{1}+\cdots+a_{nd}x_{n}+a_{n+1,d}x_{n+1}+\cdots+a_{md}x_{m}

\displaystyle=a_{0d},

have solutions
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in S,x_{n+1},\ldots,x_{m}\in R
.

For example, if one takes
R=\mathbb{Z}
and its subset
S=\mathbb{N}
, then the problem of solving the system (
1
) is equivalent to Integer Programming, and is thus NP-complete.
If we take
R=\mathbb{Z}
and its subset
S=2^{\mathbb{N}}\coloneqq\{2^{n}\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}
, then solving the system (
1
) can be formulated as deciding a fragment of the existential theory of
Presburger arithmetic with power predicate
, which is decidable by a classic result of Semenov
[
Sem80
]
.
It has since developed numerous applications and connections to automata theory
[
BCM23
]
.

In this paper, we focus on the case where
R
is the ring
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]
of univariate Laurent polynomials over integers.
Most results on algorithmic problems can be applied interchangeably on the usual polynomial ring
\mathbb{Z}[X]
and the Laurent polynomial ring
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]
.
For the purpose of subsequent applications on computational group theory (see below), we choose to state our results over the Laurent polynomial ring
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]
, although they can be easily adapted to
\mathbb{Z}[X]
.
All polynomial rings appearing in this paper will thus be Laurent polynomial rings.

Solving the system (
1
) for
R=S=\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]
is a central topic in computational commutative algebra.
Numerous effective methods, such as the
Gröbner basis
(for modules)
[
Eis13
,
Sch80a
]
, have been developed for deciding the existence of solutions for
R=S=\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]
.
These algorithms also allows one to perform
variable elimination
, and hence decide the existence of solutions for
R=\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}],S=\mathbb{Z}
.
However, adding positivity constraints for
R=\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]
can yield undecidability results.
Narendran
[
Nar96
]
showed that it is undecidable whether the system (
1
) has solutions for
R=\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}],S=\mathbb{N}[X^{\pm}]
.

In this paper we consider the problem of solving the system (
1
) with “monomial constraints”, that is, deciding the existence of solutions for
R=\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]
and
S=X^{\mathbb{Z}}\coloneqq\{X^{z}\mid z\in\mathbb{Z}\}
.
We prove this to be undecidable.
To express this problem more concisely, define
\boldsymbol{f}_{i}=(a_{i1},\ldots,a_{id})\in\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]^{d},i=0,1,\ldots,m
, and denote by
Q
the
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]
-module generated by
\boldsymbol{f}_{n+1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{f}_{m}
. Then solving the system (
1
) for
R=\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}],S=X^{\mathbb{Z}},
is equivalent to finding solutions
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
for the equation
X^{z_{1}}\boldsymbol{f}_{1}+\cdots+X^{z_{n}}\boldsymbol{f}_{n}=\boldsymbol{f}_{0}
in the quotient module
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]^{d}/Q
.

Theorem 1.1
.

There exist an integer
n\in\mathbb{N}
, a finitely presented
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]
-module
\mathcal{A}=\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]^{d}/Q
, and elements
\boldsymbol{f}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{f}_{n}\in\mathcal{A}
, such that the following problem is undecidable.

Input:
an element
\boldsymbol{f}_{0}\in\mathcal{A}
.

Question:
whether there exist
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

X^{z_{1}}\boldsymbol{f}_{1}+\cdots+X^{z_{n}}\boldsymbol{f}_{n}=\boldsymbol{f}_{0}
.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
will be given in Section
3
.
Our undecidability result does not depend on working over
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]
. Indeed, the reader can check that
the same argument shows the undecidability for finitely presented
\mathbb{Q}[X^{\pm}]
-modules and
\mathbb{R}[X^{\pm}]
-modules.
Our proof is based on an embedding of the Hilbert’s tenth problem over the variables
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}
, and requires large
n
.
On the contrary, the case
n=1
is decidable.
This can be deduced from
Noskov’s Lemma
in commutative algebra:

Theorem 1.2

(Corollary of Noskov’s lemma
[
Nos82
]
)
.

Given a finitely presented
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]
-module
\mathcal{A}
as well as two elements
\boldsymbol{f}_{0},\boldsymbol{f}_{1}\in\mathcal{A}
, it is decidable whether
X^{z}\boldsymbol{f}_{1}=\boldsymbol{f}_{0}
has solution
z\in\mathbb{Z}
.

We will give a short deduction of Theorem
1.2
in Appendix
A
.

Our motivation behind studying linear equations over
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]
with monomial constraint comes from a series of algorithmic problems in infinite groups.
In the second part of this paper, we focus on four decision problems:
word equations
,
quadratic word equations
, the
Knapsack Problem
and
Coset Intersection
, in the class of
abelian-by-cyclic
groups.

Abelian-by-cyclic groups

A group is called
abelian-by-cyclic
if it admits an abelian normal subgroup
A
such that the quotient group
G/A
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}
.
See Section
2
for a structural description of abelian-by-cyclic groups.
Many well-studied groups, such as the
wreath product

\mathbb{Z}\wr\mathbb{Z}
and the
Baumslag-Solitar
group
\mathsf{BS}(1,p),p\geq 2
, fall into this category.
The groups
\mathbb{Z}\wr\mathbb{Z}
and
\mathsf{BS}(1,p)
can be intuitively understood as groups of
2\times 2
matrices over the ring of Laurent polynomials
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]
and over the ring
\mathbb{Z}[1/p]=\{\frac{a}{p^{n}}\mid a\in\mathbb{Z},n\in\mathbb{N}\}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}\wr\mathbb{Z}

\displaystyle\cong\left\{\begin{pmatrix}X^{b}&f\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\;\middle|\;f\in\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}],b\in\mathbb{Z}\right\},

(2)

\displaystyle\mathsf{BS}(1,p)

\displaystyle\cong\left\{\begin{pmatrix}p^{b}&f\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\;\middle|\;f\in\mathbb{Z}[1/p],b\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}.

(3)

The element

\begin{pmatrix}X^{b}&f\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}

(respectively

\begin{pmatrix}p^{b}&f\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}
)
can be thought of as a Turing machine configuration whose tape cells contain letters in
\mathbb{Z}
(resp.
\{0,1,\ldots,p-1\}
), which correspond to the coefficients of the polynomial
f
(resp. the base-
p
expansion of
f
), while the head of the machine is positioned at the cell
b
.
Multiplication in the group corresponds to operating the machine by moving the head and adding elements of
\mathbb{Z}
(resp.
\{0,1,\ldots,p-1\}
, with carrying) to the cell. See
[
CCZ20
]
and
[
LSZ15
]
for a complete description.
This analogy between group operations and Turing machines is shared among all abelian-by-cyclic groups, as the isomorphism
G/A\cong\mathbb{Z}
gives the Turing machine-like structure, with
\mathbb{Z}
representing the indices of the tape.

Abelian-by-cyclic groups have been extensively studied from the point of view of geometry and growth
[
FM00
,
HX21
]
, algorithmic problems
[
Bol76
]
, and random walks
[
PSC03
]
.
Our motivation to study algorithmic problems in abelian-by-cyclic is two-fold.
On one hand, they are the simplest non-abelian groups, and thus one of simplest classes of groups where a number of algorithmic problems remain open.
On the other hand, the similarity of abelian-by-cyclic groups to operations on Turing machines might motivate the development of analogous methods in automata theory.

Equations over groups and quadratic equations

The study of word equations over groups dates at least as far back as 1911, when Max Dehn proposed the word and conjugacy problems.
A major breakthrough in the study of equations over groups was made by Makanin in the 1980s.
Extending his seminal work on word equations in free semigroups
[
Mak77
]
, Makanin showed that the question of whether a general system of equations over a free group has a solution is decidable
[
Mak83
]
.
See
[
Rom12
,
DE17
,
CE19
,
GMO20
,
Lev22
]
for more recent developments.
Solving a system of equations over a groups
G
can be formulated as the following decision problem.
Let
\mathcal{X}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
be a finite alphabet, define
\mathcal{X}^{-1}
as a new alphabet
\{x_{1}^{-1},\ldots,x_{n}^{-1}\}
.
Suppose we are given a finite word
w
over the alphabet
\mathcal{X}\cup\mathcal{X}^{-1}\cup G
(the group
G
might be infinite).
For any elements
g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}\in G
, we can substitute each
x_{i}
by
g_{i}
(and
x_{i}^{-1}
by
g_{i}^{-1}
) in
w
, and obtain a word over
G
.
We denote by
w(g_{1},\ldots,g_{n})\in G
the product of this word.
The problem of solving a system of equations over a group
G
can then be formulated as follows:

Input:
finite words
w_{1},\ldots,w_{t}
over the alphabet
\mathcal{X}\cup\mathcal{X}^{-1}\cup G
.

Question:
whether there exist
g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}\in G
, such that

w_{1}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{n})=\cdots=w_{t}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{n})=e.

Here,
e
denotes the neutral element of
G
.
For example, the
conjugacy problem
, which asks for given
h,h^{\prime}\in G
, whether there exists
g\in G
such that
ghg^{-1}=h^{\prime}
, can be considered as a special case of solving an equation with
\mathcal{X}=\{x_{1}\},w_{1}=x_{1}hx_{1}^{-1}h^{\prime-1}
.

One class of equations over groups that generated much interest is the class of
quadratic equations
: these consist of only words where for each
i
, the numbers of occurrences of
x_{i}
and
x_{i}^{-1}
sum up to two (such as the word
w_{1}=x_{1}hx_{1}^{-1}h^{\prime-1}
).
Apart from being a generalization of the conjugacy problem, quadratic equations have also been observed to have tight connections with the theory of compact surfaces
[
Cul81
,
Sch80b
]
.
Recently, Mandel and Ushakov
[
MU23
]
showed that solving systems of quadratic equations is decidable and NP-complete in the Baumslag-Solitar group
\mathsf{BS}(1,p)
, while Ushakov and Weiers
[
UW23
]
showed that that solving systems of quadratic equations is NP-complete in the wreath product
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\wr\mathbb{Z}
(commonly known as the
lamplighter group
).
Kharlampovich, López and Miasnikov
[
KLM20
]
showed that solving systems of quadratic equations is decidable in the wreath product
A\wr\mathbb{Z}
, where
A
is any finitely generated abelian group.
They left as an open problem
1
1

1

In the
published version of Kharlampovich, López and Miasnikov’s paper
[
KLM20
]
, it was claimed that solving a general system of equations is
decidable
in
A\wr\mathbb{Z}
, with
A
finitely generated abelian. The proof unfortunately contained a gap. In the corrected version, the problem was again listed as open.
whether solving a
general
system of equations is decidable in
A\wr\mathbb{Z}
.
In this paper, we provide a negative answer to this open problem:

Theorem 1.3
.

Solving a system of equations is undecidable in
\mathbb{Z}\wr\mathbb{Z}
.

A fortiori, the existential theory of the group
\mathbb{Z}\wr\mathbb{Z}
is undecidable.
We also show that the decidability of solving systems of quadratic equations in
A\wr\mathbb{Z}
and
\mathsf{BS}(1,p)
cannot be extended to
all
abelian-by-cyclic groups:

Theorem 1.4
.

There exists a finitely generated abelian-by-cyclic group
\mathcal{A}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
, as well as its elements
h_{1},\ldots,h_{m}
, such that the following problem is undecidable.

Input:
an element
h_{0}\in\mathcal{A}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
.

Question:
whether there exist
g_{0},g_{1},\ldots,g_{m}\in\mathcal{A}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
, such that

(g_{0}h_{0}g_{0}^{-1})(g_{1}h_{1}g_{1}^{-1})\cdots(g_{m}h_{m}g_{m}^{-1})=e.

A fortiori, solving (a single) quadratic equation is undecidable in the abelian-by-cyclic group
\mathcal{A}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
.

Note that the quadratic equation appearing in Theorem
1.4
has the special form of a
spherical equation

[
LU21
]
.
This shows that solving spherical equations is undecidable in finitely generated metabelian groups, providing a negative answer to an open problem of Lysenok and Ushakov
[
LU21
]
.

Theorem
1.3
and
1.4
are our main group theory results, and will be proven in Subsections
4.2
and
4.1
, respectively.

Knapsack Problem

In
[
MNU15
]
, Myasnikov, Nikolaev, and Ushakov began the investigation of classical optimization problems, which are formulated over the integers, for noncommutative groups.
The
Knapsack Problem
was introduced for a finitely generated group
G
among other problems.
The input for the Knapsack Problem is a sequence of group elements
g_{1},\ldots,g_{n},g\in G
, and the question is whether there exist non-negative integers
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
g_{1}^{z_{1}}g_{2}^{z_{2}}\cdots g_{n}^{z_{n}}=g
.

On one hand, by taking
G
to be the group of integers
\mathbb{Z}
, we recover a variant of the classical knapsack problem.
On the other hand, the Knapsack Problem for an arbitrary group
G
can be seen as an essential special case of the
Rational Subset Membership Problem
(whether a given regular expression over
G
contains the neutral element).
Indeed, solving the Knapsack Problem often provides an important first step towards solving Rational Subset Membership
[
Bod24
]
.
The Knapsack Problem in non-commutative groups has lately received much attention from computational group theorists.
Figelius, Ganardi, König, Lohrey and Zetzsche showed decidability and and NP-completeness of the Knapsack Problem in wreath products
G\wr\mathbb{Z}
, where
G
is any non-trivial finitely generated abelian group
[
GKLZ18
]
, or any finite nilpotent group
[
FGLZ20
]
.
Nevertheless, decidability of the Knapsack Problem does not extend to general abelian-by-cyclic groups. Mishchenko and Treier
[
MT17
]
showed that there exist abelian-by-cyclic groups with undecidable Knapsack Problem.
In Section
5
, we apply Theorem
1.1
give a different and more direct proof of Mishchenko and Treier’s result:

Theorem 1.5
.

There exists a finitely generated abelian-by-cyclic group
\mathcal{A}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
, as well as its elements
g_{1},\ldots,g_{m}
, such that the following problem is undecidable.

Input:
an element
g\in\mathcal{A}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
.

Question:
whether there exist
z_{1},\ldots,z_{m}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

g_{1}^{z_{1}}g_{2}^{z_{2}}\cdots g_{m}^{z_{m}}=g
.

A fortiori, the Knapsack Problem is undecidable in the abelian-by-cyclic group
\mathcal{A}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
.

Coset Intersection

The last problem we study is
Coset Intersection
.
Given a finite subset
S
of a group
G
, denote by
\langle{S}\rangle
the subgroup generated by
S
, and let
h\langle{S}\rangle
denote the coset
\{hg\mid g\in\langle{S}\rangle\}
.
The input of the Coset Intersection problem is two finite subsets
\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H}\subset G
as well as an element
h\in G
, and the question is whether the intersection
\langle{\mathcal{G}}\rangle\cap h\langle{\mathcal{H}}\rangle
is empty.

The problem of Coset Intersection is motivated by numerous other areas such as Graph Isomorphism
[
Luk82
]
, vector reachability
[
PS19
]
and automata theory
[
DVZ18
]
.
Coset Intersection is related to the conjugacy problem and quadratic equations, as solving a system of equations of the form
w=x_{1}hx_{1}^{-1}h^{\prime-1}
boils down to deciding Coset Intersection.
At the same time, Coset Intersection is a much more tractable special case of Rational Subset Membership, compared to the Knapsack Problem.
Recent results by Lohrey, Steinberg and Zetzsche
[
LSZ15
]
showed decidability of Rational Subset Membership in
\mathbb{Z}_{p}\wr\mathbb{Z},\;p\geq 2
.
This result has been extended to
\mathsf{BS}(1,p),\;p\geq 2,
by Cadilhac, Chistikov and Zetzsche
[
CCZ20
]
.
In Section
6
, we show that Coset Intersection is in fact decidable in
all
finitely generated abelian-by-cyclic groups:

Theorem 1.6
.

Given as input a finitely generated abelian-by-cyclic group
\mathcal{A}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
, as well as two finite sets of elements
\mathcal{G}=\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{K}\},\mathcal{H}=\{h_{1},\ldots,h_{M}\}
and
h\in\mathcal{A}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
, it is decidable whether
\langle{\mathcal{G}}\rangle\cap h\langle{\mathcal{H}}\rangle=\emptyset
.

Preliminaries

Laurent polynomial ring and modules

A (univariate)
Laurent polynomial
with coefficients over
\mathbb{Z}
is an expression of the form

f=\sum_{i=p}^{q}a_{i}X^{i},\quad\text{where $p,q\in\mathbb{Z}$ and $a_{i}\in\mathbb{Z},i=p,p+1,\ldots,q$.}

The set of all Laurent polynomials with coefficients over
\mathbb{Z}
forms a ring and is denoted by
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]
.

Let
R
be a commutative ring.
An
R
-module is defined as an abelian group
(M,+)
along with an operation
\cdot\;\colon R\times M\rightarrow M
satisfying
f\cdot(a+b)=f\cdot a+f\cdot b
,
(f+g)\cdot a=f\cdot a+g\cdot a
,
fg\cdot a=f\cdot(g\cdot a)
and
1\cdot a=a
.
We will denote by
\boldsymbol{0}
the neutral element of an
R
-module
M
.
If
N
and
N^{\prime}
are
R
-submodule of
M
, then
N+N^{\prime}\coloneqq\{n+n^{\prime}\mid n\in N,n^{\prime}\in N^{\prime}\}
is again an
R
-submodule of
M
.

For any
d\in\mathbb{N}
, the direct power
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]^{d}
is a
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]
-module by
g\cdot(f_{1},\ldots,f_{d})\coloneqq(gf_{1},\ldots,gf_{d})
.
Throughout this paper, we use the bold symbol
\boldsymbol{f}
to denote a vector
(f_{1},\ldots,f_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]^{d}
.
Given
\boldsymbol{f}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{f}_{m}\in\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]^{d}
, we say they
generate
the
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]
-module

\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]\cdot\boldsymbol{f}_{i}\coloneqq\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}\cdot\boldsymbol{f}_{i}\;\middle|\;p_{1},\ldots,p_{m}\in\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]\right\}.

Given two
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]
-submodules
N,M
of
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]^{d}
such that
N\subseteq M
, we can define the quotient
M/N\coloneqq\{\overline{\boldsymbol{m}}\mid\boldsymbol{m}\in M\}
where
\overline{\boldsymbol{m}_{1}}=\overline{\boldsymbol{m}_{2}}
if and only if
\boldsymbol{m}_{1}-\boldsymbol{m}_{2}\in N
.
This quotient is also a
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]
-module.
We say that a
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]
-module
\mathcal{A}
is
finitely presented
if it can be written as a quotient
M/N
for two submodules
M,N
of
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]^{d}
for some
d\in\mathbb{N}
, where both
M
and
N
are generated by finitely many elements.
Such a pair
(M,N)
, given by their respective generators, is called a
finite presentation
of
\mathcal{A}
.
The element
\overline{\boldsymbol{m}}
of
\mathcal{A}
is effectively represented by
\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]^{d}
, this representation is unique modulo
N
.
In this paper, we will often write
\boldsymbol{m}
instead of
\overline{\boldsymbol{m}}
, when the context is clear that this represents an element of the quotient
\mathcal{A}=M/N
.

Abelian-by-cyclic groups

We now formally define abelian-by-cyclic groups.

Definition 2.1
.

A group
G
is called
abelian-by-cyclic
if it admits an abelian normal subgroup
\mathcal{A}
such that
G/\mathcal{A}\cong\mathbb{Z}
.

The abelian subgroup
\mathcal{A}
admits a natural
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]
-module structure in the following sense.
It is a classic result
[
Bol76
, p.17]
that every finitely generated abelian-by-cyclic group
G
can be written as a
semidirect product

\mathcal{A}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
:

\mathcal{A}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}\coloneqq\left\{(\boldsymbol{a},z)\;\middle|\;\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathcal{A},z\in\mathbb{Z}\right\},

(4)

where
\mathcal{A}
is a finitely presented
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]
-module.
The group law in
\mathcal{A}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
is defined by

(\boldsymbol{a},z)\cdot(\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime},z^{\prime})=(\boldsymbol{a}+X^{z}\cdot\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime},z+z^{\prime}),\quad(\boldsymbol{a},z)^{-1}=(-X^{-z}\cdot\boldsymbol{a},-z).

The neutral element of
\mathcal{A}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
is
(\boldsymbol{0},0)
.
Intuitively, the element
(\boldsymbol{a},z)
is analogous to a
2\times 2
matrix

\begin{pmatrix}X^{z}&\boldsymbol{a}\\
0&1\\
\end{pmatrix}
, where group multiplication is represented by matrix multiplication.
We naturally identify
\mathcal{A}
with the normal subgroup
\left\{(\boldsymbol{a},0)\;\middle|\;\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathcal{A}\right\}
of
\mathcal{A}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
.
In particular, the quotient
\left(\mathcal{A}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}\right)/\mathcal{A}
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}
, so
\mathcal{A}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
is indeed abelian-by-cyclic.

Example 2.2
.

Take
\mathcal{A}\coloneqq\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]
considered as a
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]
-module, then we recover the definition (
2
) of the wreath product
\mathbb{Z}\wr\mathbb{Z}=\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
.
If
\mathcal{A}\coloneqq\mathbb{Z}_{p}[X^{\pm}]=\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]/\left(\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]\cdot p\right)
, then we obtain the lamplighter group
\mathbb{Z}_{p}\wr\mathbb{Z}=\mathbb{Z}_{p}[X^{\pm}]\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
.
In general, if
A
is any finitely generated abelian group, and let
A[X^{\pm}]
denote the set of Laurent polynomials with coefficients in
A
, then
A[X^{\pm}]
is a finitely presented
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]
-module (but not necessarily a ring), and the wreath product
A\wr\mathbb{Z}
is defined as the semidirect product
A[X^{\pm}]\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
.

If we take
\mathcal{A}\coloneqq\mathbb{Z}[1/p]=\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]/\left(\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]\cdot(X-p)\right)
, then we recover the definition (
3
) of the Baumslag-Solitar group
\mathsf{BS}(1,p)=\mathbb{Z}[1/p]\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
.

Throughout this paper, a finitely generated abelian-by-cyclic group
G
is always represented as the semidirect product
\mathcal{A}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
, where
\mathcal{A}
is a
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]
-module given by a finite presentation.

3
Linear equation with monomial constraints

In this section, we prove undecidability of Theorem
1.1
by embedding Hilbert’s tenth problem.
For
f,g\in\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]
, we write
f\mid g
if there exists
h\in\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]
such that
g=fh
.
We show undecidability of Theorem
1.1
, even for the following special case:

Proposition 3.1
.

There exist
k,n\in\mathbb{N}
, polynomials
p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}\in\{0,(X-1)^{2},(X-1)^{3}\}
, and
f_{ij}\in\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]
,
i=1,\ldots,n;\;j=1,\ldots,k
, such that the following problem is undecidable:

Input:
polynomials
f_{0j}\in\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]
,
j=1,\ldots,k
.

Question:

whether there exist
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
that satisfy the following system:

\displaystyle p_{1}

\displaystyle\mid X^{z_{1}}f_{11}+\cdots+X^{z_{n}}f_{n1}-f_{01},

\displaystyle p_{2}

\displaystyle\mid X^{z_{1}}f_{12}+\cdots+X^{z_{n}}f_{n2}-f_{02},

\displaystyle p_{k}

\displaystyle\mid X^{z_{1}}f_{1k}+\cdots+X^{z_{n}}f_{nk}-f_{0k}.

(5)

In particular, when
p_{i}=0
, the expression
p_{i}\mid F
means
F=0
.

Note that Proposition
3.1
will immediately yield Theorem
1.1
. Indeed, let
p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]
be as in Proposition
3.1
, and let
\mathcal{A}
be the finitely presented
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]
-module
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]^{k}/Q
, where

Q\coloneqq\{(g_{1}p_{1},g_{2}p_{2},\ldots,g_{k}p_{k})\mid g_{1},\ldots,g_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]\}.

Then for
\boldsymbol{f}_{0}=(f_{01},\ldots,f_{0k}),\boldsymbol{f}_{1}=(f_{11},\ldots,f_{1k}),\ldots,\boldsymbol{f}_{n}=(f_{n1},\ldots,f_{nk})\in\mathcal{A}
, we have
X^{z_{1}}\boldsymbol{f}_{1}+\cdots+X^{z_{n}}\boldsymbol{f}_{n}=\boldsymbol{f}_{0}
if and only if the system (
5
) is satisfied. Therefore, Proposition
3.1
implies Theorem
1.1
.
We now start proving Proposition
3.1
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Suppose
z_{1},z_{2},z_{3}\in\mathbb{Z}
. We have

(X-1)^{3}\mid X^{z_{1}}+X^{z_{2}}(1-X)+X^{z_{3}}+(X-3)

(6)

if and only if
z_{2}=z_{1}^{2}
and
z_{3}=-z_{1}
.

Proof.

Note that
(X-1)^{3}\mid f
if and only if
f(1)=f^{\prime}(1)=f^{\prime\prime}(1)=0
, where
f^{\prime}
and
f^{\prime\prime}
denote the first and second derivatives of
f
. Taking
f=X^{z_{1}}+X^{z_{2}}(1-X)+X^{z_{3}}+(X-3)
, then
f(1)=f^{\prime}(1)=f^{\prime\prime}(1)=0
is equivalent to

0=z_{1}+z_{3}=z_{1}^{2}-z_{1}-2z_{2}+z_{3}^{2}-z_{3}=0,

which is equivalent to
z_{2}=z_{1}^{2},z_{3}=-z_{1}
.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Suppose
z_{1},z_{2},z_{3}\in\mathbb{Z}
. We have

(X-1)^{2}\mid X^{z_{1}}+X^{z_{2}}-X^{z_{3}}-1

if and only if
z_{3}=z_{1}+z_{2}
.

Proof.

Note that
(X-1)^{2}\mid f
if and only if
f(1)=f^{\prime}(1)=0
. Taking
f=X^{z_{1}}+X^{z_{2}}-X^{z_{3}}-1
, then
f(1)=f^{\prime}(1)=0
is equivalent to

0=z_{1}+z_{2}-z_{3}=0
.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Suppose
z_{i}\in\mathbb{Z},i=1,2,3
. There exist
z_{4},\ldots,z_{12}\in\mathbb{Z}
satisfying

\displaystyle(X-1)^{3}

\displaystyle\mid X^{z_{1}}+X^{z_{4}}(1-X)+X^{z_{10}}+(X-3),

(7)

\displaystyle(X-1)^{3}

\displaystyle\mid X^{z_{2}}+X^{z_{5}}(1-X)+X^{z_{11}}+(X-3),

(8)

\displaystyle(X-1)^{2}

\displaystyle\mid X^{z_{4}}+X^{z_{5}}-X^{z_{6}}-1,

(9)

\displaystyle(X-1)^{2}

\displaystyle\mid X^{z_{1}}+X^{z_{2}}-X^{z_{7}}-1,

(10)

\displaystyle(X-1)^{3}

\displaystyle\mid X^{z_{7}}+X^{z_{8}}(1-X)+X^{z_{12}}+(X-3),

(11)

\displaystyle(X-1)^{2}

\displaystyle\mid X^{z_{3}}+X^{z_{3}}-X^{z_{9}}-1,

(12)

\displaystyle(X-1)^{2}

\displaystyle\mid X^{z_{6}}+X^{z_{9}}-X^{z_{8}}-1,

(13)

if and only if
z_{3}=z_{1}z_{2}
.

Proof.

We apply Lemmas
3.2
or
3.3
to each of the equations (
7
)-(
13
).
Equations (
7
), (
8
) and (
9
) are equivalent to

z_{4}=z_{1}^{2},z_{5}=z_{2}^{2},z_{6}=x_{4}+x_{5}=z_{1}^{2}+z_{2}^{2},

and
z_{10}=-z_{1},z_{11}=-z_{2}
.
Equations (
10
) and (
11
) are equivalent to

z_{7}=z_{1}+z_{2},z_{8}=z_{7}^{2}=z_{1}^{2}+z_{2}^{2}+2z_{1}z_{2},

and
z_{12}=-z_{7}=-z_{1}-z_{2}
.
Finally, Equations (
12
) and (
13
) are equivalent to

z_{9}=z_{3}+z_{3},z_{8}=z_{6}+z_{9}=z_{6}+z_{3}+z_{3}.

Together with
z_{8}=z_{1}^{2}+z_{2}^{2}+2z_{1}z_{2}
and
z_{6}=z_{1}^{2}+z_{2}^{2}
, this yields
z_{3}=z_{1}z_{2}
.
∎

We now combine Lemma
3.3
and
3.4
to prove Proposition
3.1
(and consequently, Theorem
1.1
).

Proof of Proposition
3.1
and Theorem
1.1
.

By Matiyasevich’s proof of the undecidability of Hilbert’s tenth problem
[
Mat93
]
, there exists a polynomial
P_{uni}\in\mathbb{Z}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{d}]
, such that the following problem is undecidable:

Input:
an integer
a\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Question:
whether
P_{uni}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})=a
has a solution
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.

To embed the Hilbert’s tenth problem into Equation (
5
), we will construct
k,n\in\mathbb{N}
, polynomials
p_{1},\ldots,p_{k-1}\in\{0,(X-1)^{2},(X-1)^{3}\}
,
f_{ij}\in\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]
,
i=1,\ldots,n;\;j=1,\ldots,k
, and
f_{0j}\in\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]
,
j=1,\ldots,k-1
, such that
P_{uni}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})=a
if and only if there exist
z_{d+1},\ldots,z_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

\displaystyle p_{1}

\displaystyle\mid X^{z_{1}}f_{11}+\cdots+X^{z_{n}}f_{n1}-f_{01},

\displaystyle p_{2}

\displaystyle\mid X^{z_{1}}f_{12}+\cdots+X^{z_{n}}f_{n2}-f_{02},

\displaystyle p_{k-1}

\displaystyle\mid X^{z_{1}}f_{1,k-1}+\cdots+X^{z_{n}}f_{n,k-1}-f_{0,k-1},

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\mid X^{z_{n}}-X^{a}.

(14)

First we rewrite the equation
P_{uni}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})=z_{n}
into a system of polynomial equations, each of the form
z_{k}=z_{i}z_{j}
or
z_{k}=z_{i}+z_{j}
or
z_{k}=b,b\in\mathbb{Z}
, with
i,j,k\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
.
For example, “
z_{1}^{3}+2z_{1}z_{2}+7=z_{n}
” can be rewritten as “
z_{3}=z_{1}z_{1},z_{4}=z_{1}z_{3},z_{5}=z_{1}z_{2},z_{6}=z_{5}+z_{5},z_{7}=z_{4}+z_{6},z_{8}=7,z_{n}=z_{7}+z_{8}
”.

Then, we use Lemma
3.4
and
3.3
respectively to express the equations
z_{k}=z_{i}z_{j}
and
z_{k}=z_{i}+z_{j}
, and use
0\mid X^{z_{k}}-X^{b}
to express
z_{k}=b
. Finally, we add
0\mid X^{z_{n}}-X^{a}
to the system to fully express the equation
P_{uni}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})=a
.
The resulting system have the form (
14
) and is equivalent to
P_{uni}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})=a
(the variables
z_{d+1},\ldots,z_{n}
will be functions over
z_{1},\ldots,z_{d}
and do not impose extra constraints).

Notice that the input
a\in\mathbb{Z}
only appear once as
X^{a}
in the system (
14
), which corresponds to
f_{0k}=X^{a}
in the system (
5
). Therefore the decision problem stated in Proposition
3.1
is undecidable.
As explained after the statement of Proposition
3.1
, the correctness of Theorem
1.1
directly follows.
∎

4
Equations over abelian-by-cyclic groups

4.1 Quadratic equation undecidable in an abelian-by-cyclic group

In this subsection, we prove Theorem
1.4
, showing that solving quadratic equations is undecidable in the abelian-by-cyclic group
\mathcal{A}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
, where
\mathcal{A}
is the
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]
-module constructed in Theorem
1.1
.
Our strategy is a reduction from Theorem
1.1
, by embedding the equation
X^{z_{1}}\boldsymbol{f}_{1}+\cdots+X^{z_{n}}\boldsymbol{f}_{n}=\boldsymbol{f}_{0}
into a quadratic equation over
\mathcal{A}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
.
The key observation is that “multiplying by
X^{z}
” can be expressed by conjugacy:

(\boldsymbol{a},z)(\boldsymbol{f},0)(\boldsymbol{a},z)^{-1}=(X^{z}\boldsymbol{f},0)
.

See
1.4

Proof.

We will reduce the problem from Theorem
1.1
.
Let
\mathcal{A}
be the finitely presented
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]
-module constructed in the theorem.
Take
h_{1}=(\boldsymbol{f}_{1},0),h_{2}=(\boldsymbol{f}_{2},0)\ldots,h_{n}=(\boldsymbol{f}_{n},0)
, where
\boldsymbol{f}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{f}_{n}\in\mathcal{A}
are the elements constructed in Theorem
1.1
.
For an instance of the decision problem in Theorem
1.1
with input
\boldsymbol{f}_{0}\in\mathcal{A}
,
we take the input
h_{0}=(-\boldsymbol{f}_{0},0)
in the above decision problem.
We show that there exist
g_{0},g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}\in\mathcal{A}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
, such that

(g_{0}h_{0}g_{0}^{-1})(g_{1}h_{1}g_{1}^{-1})\cdots(g_{n}h_{n}g_{n}^{-1})=e,

(15)

if and only if there exist
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
, such that

X^{z_{1}}\boldsymbol{f}_{1}+\cdots+X^{z_{n}}\boldsymbol{f}_{n}=\boldsymbol{f}_{0}.

(16)

If there exist
g_{0},g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}\in\mathcal{A}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
that satisfy (
15
), write
g_{0}=(\boldsymbol{a}_{0},b_{0}),g_{1}=(\boldsymbol{a}_{1},b_{1}),\ldots
,
g_{n}=(\boldsymbol{a}_{n},b_{n})
.
Then for
i=1,\ldots,n
, we have

g_{i}h_{i}g_{i}^{-1}=(\boldsymbol{a}_{i},b_{i})(\boldsymbol{f}_{i},0)(-X^{-b_{i}}\boldsymbol{a}_{i},-b_{i})=(X^{b_{i}}\boldsymbol{f}_{i},0),

and
g_{0}h_{0}g_{0}^{-1}=(-X^{b_{0}}\boldsymbol{f}_{0},0)
.
Therefore, Equation (
15
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\left(-X^{b_{0}}\boldsymbol{f}_{0}+X^{b_{1}}\boldsymbol{f}_{1}+\cdots+X^{b_{n}}\boldsymbol{f}_{n},0\right)=\left(\boldsymbol{0},0\right),

which is equivalent to

X^{b_{1}-b_{0}}\boldsymbol{f}_{1}+\cdots+X^{b_{n}-b_{0}}\boldsymbol{f}_{n}=\boldsymbol{f}_{0}.

Thus, the integers
z_{1}=b_{1}-b_{0},z_{2}=b_{2}-b_{0},\ldots,z_{n}=b_{n}-b_{0}
satisfy Equation (
16
).

Conversely, if Equation (
16
) has solutions
z_{1},\ldots,z_{m}\in\mathbb{Z}
, take
g_{0}=(\boldsymbol{0},0),g_{1}=(\boldsymbol{0},z_{1}),\ldots,g_{n}=(\boldsymbol{0},z_{n})\in\mathcal{A}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
.
Then as above we have
g_{0}h_{0}g_{0}^{-1}=(-\boldsymbol{f}_{0},0)
and
g_{i}h_{i}g_{i}^{-1}=(X^{z_{i}}\boldsymbol{f}_{i},0)
for
i=1,\ldots,n
.
Therefore
g_{0},g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}
satisfy Equation (
15
).

By Theorem
1.1
, it is undecidable whether Equation (
16
) has integer solutions. Therefore, it is also undecidable whether Equation (
15
) has solutions in
\mathcal{A}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
.
∎

4.2 General system of equations undecidable in
\mathbb{Z}\wr\mathbb{Z}

Recall that
\mathbb{Z}\wr\mathbb{Z}
is defined as
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
.
Its elements are
(f,a)
where
f\in\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}],a\in\mathbb{Z}
.
In this subsection we show that solving a general system of equations is undecidable in
\mathbb{Z}\wr\mathbb{Z}
(Theorem
1.3
).

The difficulty of proving Theorem
1.3
, compared to Theorem
1.4
, is that we can no longer rely on the structure of the abelian-by-cyclic group to embed the information on the module
\mathcal{A}
from Theorem
1.1
.
We therefore need to dig deeper, and prove Theorem
1.3
by reducing it from Proposition
3.1
instead of Theorem
1.1
.
Recall that Proposition
3.1
states it is undecidable whether a system of equations of the form
p_{j}\mid X^{z_{1}}f_{1j}+\cdots+X^{z_{n}}f_{nj}-f_{0j},\;j=1,\ldots,k,
has solutions
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Furthermore, the divisors
p_{j}
have either the form
0
,
(X-1)^{2}
, or
(X-1)^{3}
.
The key idea of embedding these equations into equations over
\mathbb{Z}\wr\mathbb{Z}
is that divisibility by
(X-1)^{k}
can be expressed by an equation over
\mathbb{Z}\wr\mathbb{Z}
containing the
k
-th commutator words
.
For an alphabet
\mathcal{Y}=\{x,y,z,w\}
, define recursively the following commutator words over
\mathcal{Y}\cup\mathcal{Y}^{-1}
:

\displaystyle[x,y]

\displaystyle\coloneqq x^{-1}y^{-1}xy,

\displaystyle[[x,y],z]

\displaystyle\coloneqq[x^{-1}y^{-1}xy,z]=y^{-1}x^{-1}yxz^{-1}x^{-1}y^{-1}xyz,

\displaystyle[[[x,y],z],w]

\displaystyle\coloneqq[[x^{-1}y^{-1}xy,z],w]=z^{-1}y^{-1}x^{-1}yxzx^{-1}y^{-1}xyw^{-1}y^{-1}x^{-1}yxz^{-1}x^{-1}y^{-1}xyzw.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
(f,b)\in\mathbb{Z}\wr\mathbb{Z}
, let
e=(0,0)
denote the neutral element of
\mathbb{Z}\wr\mathbb{Z}
.

(1)

There exists
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}\wr\mathbb{Z}
such that
(f,b)\cdot[x,y]=e
, if and only if
(X-1)\mid f
and
b=0
.

(2)

There exists
x,y,z\in\mathbb{Z}\wr\mathbb{Z}
such that
(f,b)\cdot[[x,y],z]=e
, if and only if
(X-1)^{2}\mid f
and
b=0
.

(3)

There exists
x,y,z,w\in\mathbb{Z}\wr\mathbb{Z}
such that
(f,b)\cdot[[[x,y],z],w]=e
, if and only if
(X-1)^{3}\mid f
and
b=0
.

Proof.

(1) Write
x=(f_{1},a_{1}),y=(f_{2},a_{2})
. By direct computation,

[x,y]=(f_{1},a_{1})^{-1}(f_{2},a_{2})^{-1}(f_{1},a_{1})(f_{2},a_{2})=((1-X^{a_{2}})X^{-a_{1}-a_{2}}f_{1}+(X^{a_{1}}-1)X^{-a_{1}-a_{2}}f_{2},0).

If
(f,b)\cdot[x,y]=e
for some
x=(f_{1},a_{1}),y=(f_{2},a_{2})
, then
b=0
and
f=-(1-X^{a_{2}})X^{-a_{1}-a_{2}}f_{1}-(X^{a_{1}}-1)X^{-a_{1}-a_{2}}f_{2}
. Since
X-1\mid 1-X^{a}
for all
a\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have
(X-1)\mid f
.
Conversely, if
b=0
and
(X-1)\mid f
, then one can take
x=(0,-1),y=(\frac{f}{X-1},0)
, so we have
[x,y]=(-f,0)
and thus
(f,b)\cdot[x,y]=e
.

(2) By (1), for any
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}\wr\mathbb{Z}
, we can write
[x,y]
as
(F,0)
for some
(X-1)\mid F
. And conversely for any
(X-1)\mid F
we can find
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}\wr\mathbb{Z}
such that
[x,y]=(F,0)
.
Write
z=(f_{3},a_{3})
, then

[[x,y],z]=[(F,0),(f_{3},a_{3})]=((1-X^{a_{3}})X^{-a_{3}}F,0).

Therefore, if there are
x,y,z\in\mathbb{Z}\wr\mathbb{Z}
such that
(f,b)\cdot[[x,y],z]=e
, then
b=0
and
f=-(1-X^{a_{3}})X^{-a_{3}}F
. Since
(X-1)\mid F
and
(X-1)\mid 1-X^{a_{3}}
, we have
(X-1)^{2}\mid f
.
Conversely, if
b=0
and
(X-1)^{2}\mid f
, then by (1) we can find
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}\wr\mathbb{Z}
such that
[x,y]=(-\frac{f}{X-1},0)
. We then take
z=(0,-1)
, so
[[x,y],z]=(-f,0)
and
(f,b)\cdot[[x,y],z]=e
.

(3) follows from (2) the same way (2) follows from (1).
∎

See
1.3

Proof.

We will embed the decision problem from Proposition
3.1
into the problem of finding solutions to a system of equations in
\mathbb{Z}\wr\mathbb{Z}
.
Let
k,n\in\mathbb{N}
,
p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}\in\{0,(X-1)^{2},(X-1)^{3}\}
, and
f_{ij}\in\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]
,
i=1,\ldots,n;\;j=1,\ldots,k,
be as defined in Proposition
3.1
.
We will construct
k
words
w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{k}
over
\mathcal{X}\cup\mathcal{X}^{-1}\cup G
, that has solution over
\mathbb{Z}\wr\mathbb{Z}
if and only if the system of equations (
5
) has integer solutions
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}
.

For each
i=1,\ldots,n,
and
j=1,\ldots,k
, define
h_{ij}\coloneqq(f_{ij},0)
, and
h_{0j}\coloneqq(-f_{0j},0)
.
Let
\mathcal{X}
be the alphabet
\{x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}\cup\{x_{11},x_{12},x_{13},x_{14}\}\cup\cdots\cup\{x_{k1},x_{k2},x_{k3},x_{k4}\}
.
For each
j=1,\ldots,k,
define the word
w_{j}
over the alphabet
\mathcal{X}\cup\mathcal{X}^{-1}\cup G
:

\displaystyle w_{j}\coloneqq\begin{cases}(x_{0}h_{0j}x_{0}^{-1})(x_{1}h_{1j}x_{1}^{-1})\cdots(x_{n}h_{nj}x_{n}^{-1})\quad&\text{ if }p_{j}=0,\\
(x_{0}h_{0j}x_{0}^{-1})(x_{1}h_{1j}x_{1}^{-1})\cdots(x_{n}h_{nj}x_{n}^{-1})[[x_{j1},x_{j2}],x_{j3}]\quad&\text{ if }p_{j}=(X-1)^{2},\\
(x_{0}h_{0j}x_{0}^{-1})(x_{1}h_{1j}x_{1}^{-1})\cdots(x_{n}h_{nj}x_{n}^{-1})[[[x_{j1},x_{j2}],x_{j3}],x_{j4}]\quad&\text{ if }p_{j}=(X-1)^{3}.\end{cases}

We claim that there exist
g_{j1},g_{j2},g_{j3},g_{j4}\in\mathbb{Z}\wr\mathbb{Z}
, such that
g_{0}=(F_{0},b_{0}),g_{1}=(F_{1},b_{1}),\ldots,g_{n}=(F_{n},b_{n})
satisfy
w_{j}(g_{0},g_{1},\ldots)=e
, if and only if
p_{j}\mid X^{b_{1}}f_{1j}+\cdots+X^{b_{n}}f_{nj}-X^{b_{0}}f_{0j}
.

Indeed, as in the proof of Theorem
1.4
, we have

(x_{0}h_{0j}x_{0}^{-1})(x_{1}h_{1j}x_{1}^{-1})\cdots(x_{n}h_{nj}x_{n}^{-1})=\left(-X^{b_{0}}f_{0}+X^{b_{1}}f_{1j}+\cdots+X^{b_{n}}f_{nj},0\right)

If
p_{j}=0
, then
w_{j}(g_{0},g_{1},\ldots)=e
if and only if
-X^{b_{0}}f_{0}+X^{b_{1}}f_{1j}+\cdots+X^{b_{n}}f_{nj}=0
.
If
p_{j}=(X-1)^{2}
, then by Lemma
4.1
(2),
w_{j}(g_{0},g_{1},\ldots)=e
if and only if
p_{j}\mid-X^{b_{0}}f_{0}+X^{b_{1}}f_{1j}+\cdots+X^{b_{n}}f_{nj}
.
If
p_{j}=(X-1)^{3}
then Lemma
4.1
(3) gives the same result.

Therefore, the system of
k
word equations
w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{k}
, have solutions
g_{0},g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}
,
g_{11},g_{12}
,
g_{13},g_{14}
,
\ldots
,
g_{k1},g_{k2},g_{k3},g_{k4}\in\mathbb{Z}\wr\mathbb{Z}
, if and only if the system of equations

\displaystyle p_{1}

\displaystyle\mid X^{b_{1}}f_{11}+\cdots+X^{b_{n}}f_{n1}-X^{b_{0}}f_{01},

\displaystyle p_{2}

\displaystyle\mid X^{b_{1}}f_{12}+\cdots+X^{b_{n}}f_{n2}-X^{b_{0}}f_{02},

\displaystyle p_{k}

\displaystyle\mid X^{b_{1}}f_{1k}+\cdots+X^{b_{n}}f_{nk}-X^{b_{0}}f_{0k}.

(17)

admit solutions
b_{0},b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Note that this is equivalent to the system (
5
) admitting solutions
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
, by de-homogenizing or homogenizing the equations.
Therefore, Proposition
3.1
shows that it is undecidable whether a system of equations have solutions in

.
∎

5
Knapsack Problem

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.5
, showing that the Knapsack Problem is undecidable in the abelian-by-cyclic group
\mathcal{A}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
, where
\mathcal{A}
is the
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]
-module constructed in Theorem
1.1
.
Undecidability of the Knapsack Problem in certain abelian-by-cyclic groups has already been observed by Mishchenko and Treier
[
MT17
, Theorem 1]
.
Nevertheless, we deduce an alternative and more direct proof from Theorem
1.1
.
See
1.5

Proof.

Take the integer
n\in\mathbb{N}
and the
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]
-module
\mathcal{A}
from Theorem
1.1
.
First, we show that in the abelian-by-cyclic group
\mathcal{A}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
, there exist
h_{1},\ldots,h_{n}\in\mathcal{A}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
, such that the following “integer” variant of the Knapsack Problem is undecidable:

Input:
an element
h_{0}\in\mathcal{A}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
.

Question:
whether there exist
(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n+1})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n+1}
that satisfy the following equation:

(\boldsymbol{0},b_{1})\cdot h_{1}\cdot(\boldsymbol{0},b_{2})\cdot h_{2}\cdots(\boldsymbol{0},b_{n})\cdot h_{n}\cdot(\boldsymbol{0},b_{n+1})=h_{0}.

(18)

We will reduce the problem from Theorem
1.1
.
Let
\mathcal{A}
be the finitely presented
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]
-module constructed in Theorem
1.1
.
Take
h_{1}=(\boldsymbol{f}_{1},0),\ldots,h_{n}=(\boldsymbol{f}_{n},0)
where
\boldsymbol{f}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{f}_{n}\in\mathcal{A}
are defined in Theorem
1.1
.
For an instance of the decision problem in Theorem
1.1
with input
\boldsymbol{f}_{0}\in\mathcal{A}
,
we take the input
h_{0}=(\boldsymbol{f}_{0},0)
in the above decision problem.
We show that Equation (
18
) has a solution
(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n+1})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n+1}
if and only if

X^{z_{1}}\boldsymbol{f}_{1}+\cdots+X^{z_{n}}\boldsymbol{f}_{n}=\boldsymbol{f}_{0}.

(19)

has a solution
(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.

Indeed, direct computation shows that Equation (
18
) is equivalent to

\left(X^{b_{1}}\boldsymbol{f}_{1}+X^{b_{1}+b_{2}}\boldsymbol{f}_{2}+\cdots+X^{b_{1}+b_{2}+\cdots+b_{n}}\boldsymbol{f}_{n},b_{1}+\cdots+b_{n}+b_{n+1}\right)=\left(\boldsymbol{f}_{0},0\right).

(20)

If this has a solution
(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n+1})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n+1}
, then
z_{1}\coloneqq b_{1},z_{2}\coloneqq b_{1}+b_{2},\ldots,z_{n}\coloneqq b_{1}+\cdots+b_{n}
, is a solution for (
19
).
Conversely, if
(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is a solution for (
19
), then
b_{1}\coloneqq z_{1},b_{2}\coloneqq z_{2}-z_{1},\ldots,b_{n}\coloneqq z_{n}-z_{n-1},b_{n+1}\coloneqq-z_{n}
, is a solution for (
20
).
By Theorem
1.1
, it is undecidable whether Equation (
19
) has integer solutions. Therefore, it is also whether Equation (
18
) (equivalently, (
20
)) has solutions
(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n+1})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n+1}
.

We then prove Theorem
1.5
.
Equation (
18
) can be rewritten as

g_{1}^{b_{1}}g_{2}^{b_{2}}\cdots g_{n+1}^{b_{n+1}}=g,

(21)

where

g_{1}=(\boldsymbol{0},1),\;g_{2}=h_{1}(\boldsymbol{0},1)h_{1}^{-1},\;g_{3}=h_{1}h_{2}(\boldsymbol{0},1)h_{2}^{-1}h_{1}^{-1},\ldots,\;g_{n+1}=h_{1}h_{2}\cdots h_{n}(\boldsymbol{0},1)h_{n}^{-1}\cdots h_{2}^{-1}h_{1}^{-1},

and
g=h_{0}h_{n}^{-1}\cdots h_{2}^{-1}h_{1}^{-1}
.
Finally, Equation (
21
) has integer solutions
b_{1},\ldots,b_{n+1}\in\mathbb{Z}
if and only if the knapsack equation

g_{1}^{z_{1}}\left(g_{1}^{-1}\right)^{z^{\prime}_{1}}g_{2}^{z_{2}}\left(g_{2}^{-1}\right)^{z^{\prime}_{2}}\cdots g_{n+1}^{z_{n+1}}\left(g_{n+1}^{-1}\right)^{z^{\prime}_{n+1}}=g

(22)

has
non-negative
integer solutions
z_{1},z^{\prime}_{1},z_{2},z^{\prime}_{2},\ldots,z_{n+1},z^{\prime}_{n+1}\in\mathbb{N}
.
Therefore, it is undecidable for the input
g
, whether Equation (
22
) has solutions
z_{1},z^{\prime}_{1},z_{2},z^{\prime}_{2},\ldots,z_{n+1},z^{\prime}_{n+1}\in\mathbb{N}
.
∎

6
Coset Intersection

In this section we show that Coset Intersection is decidable in all finitely generated abelian-by-cyclic groups:

See
1.6

For reference, an overview of our decision procedure for Theorem

is given in Algorithm

.
Our solution depends on effective computation in finitely presented modules over polynomial rings.
We will make use of the following classic results.

Lemma 6.1

(
[
BCMI81
, Lemma 2.1, 2.2]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]
-module with a given finite presentation.
The following problems are effectively solvable:

(i)

(Submodule Membership) Given elements
\boldsymbol{a}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{a}_{k},\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathcal{A}
, decide whether
\boldsymbol{a}
is in the submodule generated by
\boldsymbol{a}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{a}_{k}
.

(ii)

(Computing Syzygies) Given elements
\boldsymbol{a}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{a}_{k}\in\mathcal{A}
, compute a finite set of generators for the
Syzygy module

S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]^{k}
:

S\coloneqq\left\{(f_{1},\ldots,f_{k})\in\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]^{k}\;\middle|\;f_{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{a}_{1}+\cdots+f_{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{a}_{k}=\boldsymbol{0}\right\}.

In particular, for Lemma
6.1
(ii), it states that one can compute the generators for the solution set of any homogeneous linear equation.

The following lemma shows we can effectively compute the intersection of a submodule of
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]^{k}
with
\mathbb{Z}^{k}
.

Lemma 6.2

(
[
BCMI81
, Corollary 2.5(2)]
)
.

Suppose we are given
k\in\mathbb{N}
and elements
\boldsymbol{g}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{g}_{n}
of the
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]
-module
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]^{k}

Let
\mathcal{M}
denote the
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]
-module generated by
\boldsymbol{g}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{g}_{n}

.
Then

is a
\mathbb{Z}
-module, and a finite set of generators for
\Lambda
can be effectively computed.

Let
d\geq 1
be a positive integer. One can define the Laurent polynomial ring over the variable
X^{d}
:

\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm d}]\coloneqq\left\{\sum_{i=p}^{q}a_{di}X^{di}\in\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]\;\middle|\;p,q\in\mathbb{Z},a_{dp},\ldots,a_{dq}\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}.

The elements of
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm d}]
are Laurent polynomials in
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]
whose monomials have degrees divisible by

.
For any

, the ring
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]
is naturally a
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm d}]
-module generated by the elements
1,X,\ldots,X^{d-1}
, and
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]
is isomorphic as a
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm d}]
-module to
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm d}]^{d}
.
Any finitely presented
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]
-module can be considered as a finitely presented
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm d}]
-module:

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
d\geq 2
.
Given a finite presentation of a
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]
-module
\mathcal{A}
, one can compute a finite presentation of
\mathcal{A}
as a
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm d}]
-module.
Furthermore, let
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathcal{A}
be given in the finite presentation of
\mathcal{A}
as
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]
-module, then one can compute the representation of
\boldsymbol{a}
in
\mathcal{A}
considered as a
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm d}]
-module.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]
-module given by a finite presentation.
Recall that we naturally identify
\mathcal{A}
with the subgroup
\left\{(\boldsymbol{a},0)\;\middle|\;\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathcal{A}\right\}
of
\mathcal{A}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
; that is, we will sometimes write

instead of

when the context is clear.
The following lemma effectively describes finitely generated subgroups of

.
Such a description follows from the general description of subgroups of finitely generated metabelian groups
[

.
Here we give a systematic reformulation in the context of abelian-by-cyclic groups.

Lemma 6.4

(Structural theorem of abelian-by-cyclic groups, see also
[
Rom74
]
)
.

Let
\langle{\mathcal{G}}\rangle
be a subgroup of
\mathcal{A}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
generated by the elements
\mathcal{G}=\{g_{1}\coloneqq(\boldsymbol{a}_{1},z_{1}),\ldots,g_{K}\coloneqq(\boldsymbol{a}_{K},z_{K})\}
.
Then

(i)

If
z_{1}=\cdots=z_{K}=0
, then
\langle{\mathcal{G}}\rangle
is contained in
\mathcal{A}
and it is the
\mathbb{Z}
-module generated by
\boldsymbol{a}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{a}_{K}
.

(ii)

If

are not all zero, then
\langle{\mathcal{G}}\rangle\not\subset\mathcal{A}
.
Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
denote the greatest common divisor of

. Consider the lattice

\Lambda\coloneqq\left\{(s_{1},\ldots,s_{K})\in\mathbb{Z}^{K}\;\middle|\;s_{1}z_{1}+\cdots+s_{K}z_{K}=0\right\}.

Let
(s_{11},\ldots,s_{1K}),\ldots,(s_{T1},\ldots,s_{TK})
be a finite set of generators for
\Lambda

Then
\langle{\mathcal{G}}\rangle\cap\mathcal{A}
is a
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm d}]
-submodule of
\mathcal{A}
, generated by the set of elements

S\coloneqq\left\{g_{i}g_{j}g_{i}^{-1}g_{j}^{-1}\;\middle|\;1\leq i<j\leq K\right\}\cup\left\{g_{1}^{s_{i1}}\cdots g_{K}^{s_{iK}}\;\middle|\;i\in[1,T]\right\}.

(23)

(iii)

In case (ii), let
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathcal{A}
be any element such that
(\boldsymbol{a},d)\in\langle{\mathcal{G}}\rangle
.
Then
\langle{\mathcal{G}}\rangle
is generated by
\langle{\mathcal{G}}\rangle\cap\mathcal{A}
and
(\boldsymbol{a},d)
as a group.
In other words, every element of

for some

.

We point out that in case (ii), the subgroup

is finitely generated as a
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm d}]
-module; but it is not necessarily finitely generated as a group.

Example 6.5
.

Let
\mathcal{A}=\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]
, considered as a
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]
-module.
Let
\langle{\mathcal{G}}\rangle
be the subgroup of
\mathcal{A}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
generated by the elements
g_{1}=(X,4),g_{2}=(1+X,-6)

Then

, and

is the
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm 2}]

g_{1}g_{2}g_{1}^{-1}g_{2}^{-1}=(X^{5}+X^{4}-1-X^{-5},0),\quad g_{1}^{3}g_{2}^{2}=(X^{13}+X^{12}+X^{9}+X^{7}+X^{6}+X^{5}+X,0).

For example, consider the element

.
By direct computation, its second entry is zero, therefore
g_{1}^{2}g_{2}g_{1}g_{2}\in\langle{\mathcal{G}}\rangle\cap\mathcal{A}
.
Furthermore,
g_{1}^{2}g_{2}g_{1}g_{2}
can be written as

g_{1}^{2}g_{2}g_{1}g_{2}=g_{1}^{2}(g_{2}g_{1})g_{2}=g_{1}^{2}(g_{2}g_{1}g_{2}^{-1}g_{1}^{-1})(g_{1}g_{2})g_{2}=g_{1}^{2}(g_{1}g_{2}g_{1}^{-1}g_{2}^{-1})^{-1}g_{1}g_{2}^{2}\\
=g_{1}^{2}(g_{1}g_{2}^{2})(g_{1}g_{2}^{2})^{-1}(g_{1}g_{2}g_{1}^{-1}g_{2}^{-1})^{-1}(g_{1}g_{2}^{2})=g_{1}^{3}g_{2}^{2}\cdot(g_{1}g_{2}^{2})^{-1}(g_{1}g_{2}g_{1}^{-1}g_{2}^{-1})^{-1}(g_{1}g_{2}^{2})\\
=(X^{13}+X^{12}+X^{9}+X^{7}+X^{6}+X^{5}+X,0)\cdot(g_{1}g_{2}^{2})^{-1}(X^{5}+X^{4}-1-X^{-5},0)^{-1}(g_{1}g_{2}^{2})\\
=(X^{13}+X^{12}+X^{9}+X^{7}+X^{6}+X^{5}+X)+X^{-8}\cdot(-1)\cdot(X^{5}+X^{4}-1-X^{-5}).

It is therefore indeed in the

Intuitively, modulo the generator

(in the above example,

Whereas the generator

(24)

Observation 6.6
.

Observation 6.7

Lemma 6.8
.

Let
z_{\mathcal{G}},z_{\mathcal{H}}
be integers such that
d_{\mathcal{G}}\mid z_{\mathcal{G}},\;d_{\mathcal{H}}\mid z_{\mathcal{H}}
and
z_{\mathcal{G}}=z_{\mathcal{H}}+z_{h}
.
The intersection
\langle{\mathcal{G}}\rangle\cap h\langle{\mathcal{H}}\rangle
is non-empty if and only if the equation

X^{zd}\cdot\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime}_{\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H}}-\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime\prime}_{\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H}}\in(X^{d}-1)\cdot\mathcal{M}^{\prime}

(27)

has solution
z\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Here,

\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime}_{\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H}}\coloneqq X^{z_{\mathcal{G}}}\cdot\left(\frac{X^{d}-1}{X^{d_{\mathcal{G}}}-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{a}_{\mathcal{G}}-\frac{X^{d}-1}{X^{d_{\mathcal{H}}}-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{a}_{\mathcal{H}}\right),

\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime\prime}_{\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H}}\coloneqq\frac{X^{d}-1}{X^{d_{\mathcal{G}}}-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{a}_{\mathcal{G}}-X^{z_{h}}\cdot\frac{X^{d}-1}{X^{d_{\mathcal{H}}}-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{a}_{\mathcal{H}}+(X^{d}-1)\cdot\boldsymbol{a}_{h}.

Proof.

Suppose the
\langle{\mathcal{G}}\rangle\cap h\langle{\mathcal{H}}\rangle
is non-empty. Let
(\boldsymbol{a},z^{\prime})\in\langle{\mathcal{G}}\rangle\cap h\langle{\mathcal{H}}\rangle
, then
d_{\mathcal{G}}\mid z^{\prime}
and
d_{\mathcal{H}}\mid(z^{\prime}-z_{h})
.
Hence
z^{\prime}=z_{\mathcal{G}}+zd=z_{\mathcal{H}}+zd+z_{h}
for some
z\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Since
(\boldsymbol{a},z^{\prime})\in\langle{\mathcal{G}}\rangle\cap h\langle{\mathcal{H}}\rangle
, the Equation (
26
) has solution with
md_{\mathcal{G}}=z^{\prime}=z_{\mathcal{G}}+zd,\;nd_{\mathcal{H}}=z^{\prime}-z_{h}=z_{\mathcal{H}}+zd
, meaning

\frac{X^{zd}\cdot\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime}_{\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H}}-\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime\prime}_{\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H}}}{X^{d}-1}=\frac{X^{z_{\mathcal{G}}+zd}}{X^{d_{\mathcal{G}}}-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{a}_{\mathcal{G}}-\frac{X^{z_{\mathcal{G}}+zd}}{X^{d_{\mathcal{H}}}-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{a}_{\mathcal{H}}-\frac{1}{X^{d_{\mathcal{G}}}-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{a}_{\mathcal{G}}+\frac{X^{z_{h}}}{X^{d_{\mathcal{H}}}-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{a}_{\mathcal{H}}-\boldsymbol{a}_{h}\\
=\frac{X^{z_{\mathcal{G}}+zd}-1}{X^{d_{\mathcal{G}}}-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{a}_{\mathcal{G}}-\frac{X^{z_{\mathcal{G}}+zd}-X^{z_{h}}}{X^{d_{\mathcal{H}}}-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{a}_{\mathcal{H}}-\boldsymbol{a}_{h}\\
=\frac{X^{z_{\mathcal{G}}+zd}-1}{X^{d_{\mathcal{G}}}-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{a}_{\mathcal{G}}-X^{z_{h}}\cdot\frac{X^{z_{\mathcal{H}}+zd}-1}{X^{d_{\mathcal{H}}}-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{a}_{\mathcal{H}}-\boldsymbol{a}_{h}=X^{z_{h}}\cdot\boldsymbol{c}-\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathcal{M}^{\prime}.

Therefore (
27
) is satisfied.

Conversely, suppose Equation (
27
) is satisfied.
Then we have

\frac{X^{z_{\mathcal{G}}+zd}-1}{X^{d_{\mathcal{G}}}-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{a}_{\mathcal{G}}-X^{z_{h}}\cdot\frac{X^{z_{\mathcal{H}}+zd}-1}{X^{d_{\mathcal{H}}}-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{a}_{\mathcal{H}}-\boldsymbol{a}_{h}=\frac{X^{zd}\cdot\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime}_{\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H}}-\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime\prime}_{\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H}}}{X^{d}-1}\in\mathcal{M}^{\prime},

so it can be written as
X^{z_{h}}\cdot\boldsymbol{c}-\boldsymbol{b}
for some
\boldsymbol{b}\in\langle{\mathcal{G}}\rangle\cap\mathcal{A},\;\boldsymbol{c}\in\langle{\mathcal{H}}\rangle\cap\mathcal{A}
.
Hence the system (
26
) has solutions
\boldsymbol{b}\in\langle{\mathcal{G}}\rangle\cap\mathcal{A},\;\boldsymbol{c}\in\langle{\mathcal{H}}\rangle\cap\mathcal{A},\;m=\frac{z_{\mathcal{G}}+zd}{d_{\mathcal{G}}},\;n=\frac{z_{\mathcal{H}}+zd}{d_{\mathcal{H}}}
.
∎

By Lemma
6.8
, it suffices to decide whether Equation (
27
) has a solution
z\in\mathbb{Z}
.
To do this, consider both
\mathcal{A}
and
(X^{d}-1)\cdot\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
as
\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm d}]
-modules.
Equation (
27
) is equivalent to the equation

\left(X^{d}\right)^{z}\cdot\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime}_{\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H}}=\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime\prime}_{\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H}}

(28)

in the quotient module
\mathcal{A}/\left((X^{d}-1)\cdot\mathcal{M}^{\prime}\right)
.
Applying Theorem
1.2
to the variable
X^{d}
(instead of
X

\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm d}]
-module
\mathcal{A}/\left((X^{d}-1)\cdot\mathcal{M}^{\prime}\right)
, we can decide whether the above equation has a solution
z\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Combining the three cases, we have proven Theorem

.

Algorithm for Coset Intersection

Input:

-module

, an element

(when
\langle{\mathcal{G}}\rangle\cap h\langle{\mathcal{H}}\rangle=\emptyset
) or
False
(when
\langle{\mathcal{G}}\rangle\cap h\langle{\mathcal{H}}\rangle\neq\emptyset
