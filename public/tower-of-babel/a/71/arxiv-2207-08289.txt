Generation of time-frequency entangled photon pairs propagating in separate waveguides in circuit QED setup
E. V. Stolyarov

Abstract
Time-frequency entangled photons constitute an important resource for a plethora of applications across the diverse quantum technology landscape. Thus, efficient and tunable setups for the generation of entangled photons are requisite for modern quantum technologies. In this work, we propose a generic cavity QED setup designed for on-demand generation of time-frequency entangled photon pairs, with each photon propagating in a separate waveguide. We outline a potential incarnation of this setup in the microwave superconducting circuit QED architecture. We derive and numerically solve the set of equations of motion governing the evolution of the quantum state of the system, allowing us to examine the photon emission dynamics. Using the Schmidt decomposition of the joint spectral amplitude of the emitted photon pair, we compute the entanglement entropy analyzing its dependence on the system parameters. We outline the potential extension of the proposed scheme for the generation of multiphoton time-frequency entangled states.

In the paper, we start with proposing a general scheme of the setup designed for the generation of TF entangled photons and then outline its potential experimental implementation as a microwave photonic device based on a superconducting circuit QED platform.
In this scheme, the correlated relaxation of a pair of excited quantum emitters is realized when the relaxation of one of the emitters into the open resonator triggers the relaxation of its counterpart leading to the emission of a pair of TF entangled photons.
One of the advantages of the proposed scheme for generation of TF entangled photon pairs is that it does not require driving the two-photon transitions of the artificial atoms.
Moreover, it can be extended for the generation of multiphoton entangled states.
We focus on studying the proposed device in the pulsed on-demand regime, when exactly only one pair of entangled photons are emitted.
Using a state-vector approach, we provide a real-time picture of photon emission dynamics.
We use the Schmidt decomposition of the joint spectral amplitude of the emitted photons for computing the von Neumann (entanglement) entropy, which is employed as a measure of their bipartite entanglement.

The structure of the paper is as follows.
In Sec.
II
, we discuss the general principles determining the operation of the proposed source of TF entangled photon pairs.
Its potential implementation within the microwave circuit QED architecture is outlined in Sec.
III

In Sec.
IV

In Sec.
V
, we derive the equations of motion governing the quantum-state evolution of the system and study the emission dynamics.
The dependence of the entanglement degree of the emitted photons on the system parameters is analyzed in Sec.
VI
.
We summarize our results and outline possible applications and extensions of the proposed source of entangled photon pairs in Sec.
VII
.
The additional considerations and details of derivations are delegated to the Appendixes.

II
General scheme and operational principle

To explain the key principles defining the operation of the proposed source of TF entangled photon pairs, let us consider a paradigmatic system schematically presented in Fig.
1
(a)
.
This system consists of an open resonator interacting with a pair of two-level emitters (2LEs).
The first 2LE is coupled to the resonator with strength
\displaystyle\textstyle g_{1}
, and the second 2LE interacts with the resonator with strength
\displaystyle\textstyle g_{2}
.
There is no direct interaction between the emitters.
For simplicity, we assume that the resonator and the 2LEs have identical frequencies
\displaystyle\textstyle\omega
.
The resonator is open due to its coupling to a waveguide, which leads to the photon leakage from the resonator with rate
\displaystyle\textstyle\kappa
.
Initially, the first 2LE resides in the ground state, while the second 2LE is prepared in the excited state.
Due to interaction with the open resonator, the second 2LE eventually decays, delivering the excitation (photon) to the waveguide.

The system, shown in Fig.
1
(a)
, can be represented as the excited-state 2LE interacting with the
V
-configuration three-level emitter (3LE) coupled to the waveguide.
Figure
1
(b)
illustrates the equivalent representation, whose detailed justification is provided in Appendix
A
.
The
V
-type 3LE is formed by the ground state
\displaystyle\textstyle|\mathcal{G}\rangle
and a pair of excited states
\displaystyle\textstyle|\mathcal{E}^{\pm}\rangle
.
The latter decay to the waveguide with the rate
\displaystyle\textstyle\kappa/2
.
The frequency of
\displaystyle\textstyle|\mathcal{G}\rangle\leftrightarrow|\mathcal{E}^{\pm}\rangle
transition is
\displaystyle\textstyle\omega\pm g_{1}
.
Both transitions are coupled to the 2LE with strength
\displaystyle\textstyle g_{2}/\sqrt{2}
.
The increase of coupling
\displaystyle\textstyle g_{1}
leads to stronger detuning of
\displaystyle\textstyle|\mathcal{G}\rangle\leftrightarrow|\mathcal{E}^{\pm}\rangle
transition from the 2LE frequency
\displaystyle\textstyle\omega
.
For
\displaystyle\textstyle g_{1}>g_{2}/\sqrt{2}
, the excitation exchange between the 2LE and the
V
3LE becomes inefficient, resulting in a suppression of the photon leakage into the waveguide.
These qualitative considerations are confirmed by the calculations revealing the suppression of the photon leakage into the waveguide with the increase of the ratio
\displaystyle\textstyle g_{1}/g_{2}
.
Details of calculations are given in Appendix
A
.

The above result implies that the process of photon emission can be manipulated by controlling the coupling parameter
\displaystyle\textstyle g_{1}
.
In this regard, let us consider a system comprised by a 3LE interacting with a single-mode resonator.
This system is sketched in Fig.
1
(c)
.
The ground state
\displaystyle\textstyle|g\rangle
and the excited states
\displaystyle\textstyle|e\rangle
and
\displaystyle\textstyle|f\rangle
of the 3LE are arranged in a ladder (
\displaystyle\textstyle\Xi
-type) configuration implying that only
\displaystyle\textstyle|g\rangle\leftrightarrow|e\rangle
and
\displaystyle\textstyle|e\rangle\leftrightarrow|f\rangle
transitions are allowed.
The frequencies of the resonator and
\displaystyle\textstyle|e\rangle\leftrightarrow|f\rangle
transition coincide, while
\displaystyle\textstyle|g\rangle\leftrightarrow|e\rangle
transition is strongly detuned from the resonator.
In this case, the interaction between the 3LE and the resonator is approximately described by the Hamiltonian
\displaystyle\textstyle{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{int}}=g\,(c^{\dagger}|e\rangle\langle f|+|f\rangle\langle e|c)
, where
\displaystyle\textstyle g
denotes the coupling parameter, and the operator
\displaystyle\textstyle c
(
\displaystyle\textstyle c^{\dagger}
) annihilates (creates) a resonator photon.
One can formally rewrite the resonator-emitter interaction Hamiltonian as
\displaystyle\textstyle{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{int}}=g(|e\rangle\langle e|+|f\rangle\langle f|)(c^{\dagger}|e\rangle\langle f|+|f\rangle\langle e|c)
.
Using the property
\displaystyle\textstyle|g\rangle\langle g|+|e\rangle\langle e|+|f\rangle\langle f|=\mathbb{1}
, with
\displaystyle\textstyle\mathbb{1}
being the unity operator, leads to

\displaystyle\textstyle{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{int}}=\tilde{g}(c^{\dagger}|e\rangle\langle f|+|f\rangle\langle e|c)
, where
\displaystyle\textstyle\tilde{g}\equiv g(\mathbb{1}-|g\rangle\langle g|)
can be interpreted as a resonator-emitter coupling dependent on the
\displaystyle\textstyle\Xi
3LE ground state population.
Thus, if the 3LE resides in the ground state
\displaystyle\textstyle|g\rangle
, its
\displaystyle\textstyle|f\rangle\leftrightarrow|e\rangle
transition is decoupled from the resonator mode.
This effect was utilized, e.g., in Ref.
Kyriienko and Sørensen 2016
for the implementation of a continuous-wave single-photon transistor and Ref.
Stolyarov 2020
for the implementation of a qubit-state-controlled single-photon switch.

Harnessing the effects outlined above, we propose a setup consisting of two emitters interacting with two resonators (marked as
\displaystyle\textstyle A
and
\displaystyle\textstyle B
) coupled to the output waveguides (marked as
\displaystyle\textstyle\alpha
and
\displaystyle\textstyle\beta
).
The considered setup is schematically shown in Fig.
1
(e)
.
In this setup, the first emitter is coupled to both resonators.
This emitter is represented by a
\displaystyle\textstyle\Xi
-configuration 3LE with the ground state
\displaystyle\textstyle|g\rangle_{1}
and a pair of excited states
\displaystyle\textstyle|e\rangle_{1}
and
\displaystyle\textstyle|f\rangle_{1}
.
The frequency of
\displaystyle\textstyle|g\rangle_{1}\leftrightarrow|e\rangle_{1}
transition coincides with the frequency
\displaystyle\textstyle\omega_{a}
of resonator
\displaystyle\textstyle A
, while the frequency of
\displaystyle\textstyle|e\rangle_{1}\leftrightarrow|f\rangle_{1}
transition coincides with the frequency
\displaystyle\textstyle\omega_{b}
of resonator
\displaystyle\textstyle B
.
The anharmonicity of the eigenlevels of the
\displaystyle\textstyle\Xi
3LE results in the inhibition of the excitation exchange between resonator
\displaystyle A
and
\displaystyle\textstyle|e\rangle_{1}\leftrightarrow|f\rangle_{1}
transition and between resonator
\displaystyle\textstyle B
and
\displaystyle\textstyle|g\rangle_{1}\leftrightarrow|e\rangle_{1}
transition.
The second emitter is represented by a 2LE with the ground state
\displaystyle\textstyle|g\rangle_{2}
and the excited state
\displaystyle\textstyle|e\rangle_{2}
.
This emitter is coupled only to resonator
\displaystyle\textstyle B
, and
the frequency of its
\displaystyle\textstyle|g\rangle_{2}\leftrightarrow|e\rangle_{2}
transition coincides with the resonator frequency
\displaystyle\textstyle\omega_{b}
.
The interrelation between the frequencies of the resonators and the transitions frequencies of the emitters is shown in Fig.
1
(d)
.

In the system outlined above, the
correlated relaxation
of the emitters emerges.
The mechanism of this relaxation is as follows.
When the 3LE resides in its excited state
\displaystyle\textstyle|e\rangle_{1}
, the relaxation of the 2LE into waveguide
\displaystyle\textstyle\beta
via resonator
\displaystyle\textstyle B
is inhibited due to the mechanism outlined in the second paragraph of this section.
In the course of the 3LE relaxation from the excited state
\displaystyle\textstyle|e\rangle_{1}
to the ground state
\displaystyle\textstyle|g\rangle_{1}
,
\displaystyle\textstyle|f\rangle_{1}\leftrightarrow|e\rangle_{1}
transition of the 3LE is getting decoupled from the resonator
\displaystyle\textstyle B
, as discussed earlier in this section, which triggers the relaxation of the 2LE excited state
\displaystyle\textstyle|e\rangle_{2}
.
Hence, the photons tend to be emitted into the waveguides one right after another, implying that they exhibit
time correlation
which, in turn, indicates their
frequency (energy) anti-correlation
.

III
Circuit QED setup

Having discussed the general principle of operation of the proposed source of TF entangled photon pairs, let us now outline its potential implementation in the circuit QED architecture.
The superconducting circuit QED platform provides all necessary components for implementation of the generic scheme discussed in Sec.
II
, such as microwave resonators and transmission lines, as well as Josephson-junction artificial atoms featuring tunable multilevel structure and long coherence times
Kjaergaard et al. 2020
.

The schematic illustration of the proposed circuit QED setup is shown in Fig.
2
.
In the considered setup, the quantum emitters are represented by the Xmon version
Barends et al. 2013
of the transmon – a charge-type artificial atom derived from a Cooper-pair box and featuring resilience to charge fluctuations
Koch et al. 2007
;
*houck2009
.
Transmon artificial atoms, in particular, their Xmon modifications offer a simple design, long coherence times, and tunable parameters
Chen et al. 2014
, making them a popular choice for the realization of various QIP systems
Barends et al. 2014
;
Kelly et al. 2015
;
Ristè et al. 2017
.
Moreover, using tantalum as a base superconductor along with improving processing techniques led to a recent impressive increase in the transmon excited state lifetime
Place et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2022
.
In our setup, the Xmons are capacitively coupled to the coplanar waveguide (CPW) resonators which, in turn, are coupled to the transmission-line waveguides serving as output channels for the emitted photons.

The states of the transmons are manipulated using the classical signals delivered via individual (XY) control lines
Krantz et al. 2019
.
Due to the weak anharmonicity of transmons, for their preparation in the lower excited state, one can rely on one of the elaborate techniques, providing high fidelities of preparation while avoiding excitation of the higher excited states
Motzoi et al. 2009
;
Chow et al. 2010
;
Machnes et al. 2018
.
Alternatively, for transmon control, one can use a single-flux quantum digital logic
Leonard et al. 2019
.
This approach allows integration of the control circuitry on a chip bringing most of the setup components into the cryogenic stage.

The output transmission lines are supplemented with the Purcell filters for suppression of the superfluous emission from the off-resonant transitions of the transmons (more details on the role of these elements are given in Appendix
B.2
).
The setup presented in Fig.
2
adopts the approach from Ref.
Reed et al. 2010
with the quarter-wavelength CPW resonators side-coupled to the output transmission lines.
These additional resonators serve as notch filters.

In the setup version shown in Fig.
2
, the fixed couplings between the circuit elements are used.
For achieving
in-situ
tunability of the setup parameters for better control over the characteristics of the emitted photons, instead of fixed couplings set on a chip fabrication stage, one can implement tunable couplings using, for example, superconducting quantum interference device couplers
Peropadre et al. 2013
;
Kounalakis et al. 2018
;
*wu2018
controlled by an external flux.

IV
The Model

The circuit QED system described above is modeled by the Hamiltonian

\begin{split}{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{sys}}=&{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{t}}+{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{r}}+{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{r}-\mathrm{t}}+{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{w}}+{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{r}-\mathrm{w}},\end{split}

(1)

where the first term describes the transmon-type artificial atoms, the second term is the Hamiltonian of the resonators, the third term describes the interaction between the resonators and transmons, and the last pair of terms describes the waveguides and their coupling to the resonators.
In what follows we set
\displaystyle\textstyle\hbar=1
, thus, measuring all energies in frequency units.

The Hamiltonian
\displaystyle\textstyle{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{t}}
represented in the basis of the transmons eigenstates reads as

{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{t}}=\sum_{j=1}^{2}\left(\omega_{j}^{ge}\sigma^{ee}_{j}+\omega^{gf}_{j}\sigma^{ff}_{j}\right),

(2)

where
\displaystyle\textstyle\omega^{ge}_{j}
,
\displaystyle\textstyle\omega^{gf}_{j}=\omega_{j}^{ge}+\omega_{j}^{ef}
, and
\displaystyle\omega^{ef}_{j}
stand for the frequency of
\displaystyle\textstyle|g\rangle_{j}\leftrightarrow|e\rangle_{j}
,
\displaystyle\textstyle|g\rangle_{j}\leftrightarrow|f\rangle_{j}
, and
\displaystyle\textstyle|e\rangle_{j}\leftrightarrow|f\rangle_{j}
transition of the
\displaystyle\textstyle j
-th transmon (
\displaystyle\textstyle j\in\{1,2\}
), respectively.
For convenience, in Eq. (
2
) we introduced the transmon operator
\displaystyle\textstyle\sigma_{j}^{ss^{\prime}}=|s\rangle_{j}\langle s^{\prime}|_{j}
where
\displaystyle\textstyle s,s^{\prime}\in\{g,e,f\}
is the index of the transmon eigenlevel.
Since we consider the case of only two excitations in the system, we restricted the subspace of the transmon states to the ground state
\displaystyle\textstyle|g\rangle_{j}
and a pair of the lowest excited states
\displaystyle\textstyle|e\rangle_{j}
and
\displaystyle\textstyle|f\rangle_{j}
.

The Hamiltonian of the resonators
\displaystyle\textstyle{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{r}}
has the form

{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{r}}=\omega_{a}{\mathcal{N}}_{a}+\omega_{b}{\mathcal{N}}_{b},

(3)

where
\displaystyle\textstyle\omega_{a}
and
\displaystyle\textstyle\omega_{b}
denote the frequencies of resonator
\displaystyle\textstyle A
and
\displaystyle\textstyle B
, respectively.
Operators
\displaystyle\textstyle{\mathcal{N}}_{a}=a^{\dagger}a
and
\displaystyle\textstyle{\mathcal{N}}_{b}=b^{\dagger}b
are the operators of the photon number in resonator
\displaystyle\textstyle A
and
\displaystyle\textstyle B
, where
\displaystyle\textstyle a
(
\displaystyle\textstyle a^{\dagger}
) and
\displaystyle\textstyle b
(
\displaystyle\textstyle b^{\dagger}
) stand for the photon annihilation (creation) operators in the corresponding resonator.

For clarity, we represent the resonator-transmons interaction Hamiltonian as
\displaystyle\textstyle{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{r}-\mathrm{t}}={\mathcal{H}}^{\mathrm{rsn}}_{\mathrm{r}-\mathrm{t}}+{\mathcal{H}}^{\mathrm{dsp}}_{\mathrm{r}-\mathrm{t}}
, where the first term describes the
resonant
interactions determining the dynamics of the system, while the second term describes the
dispersive
interactions giving rise to shifts in the frequencies of the resonators and the transmons.
In the rotating-wave approximation, the term
\displaystyle\textstyle{\mathcal{H}}^{\mathrm{res}}_{\mathrm{r-a}}
has the form

{\mathcal{H}}^{\mathrm{rsn}}_{\mathrm{r}-\mathrm{t}}=g_{1a}a^{\dagger}\sigma_{1}^{ge}+g_{1b}b^{\dagger}\sigma_{1}^{ef}+g_{2b}b^{\dagger}\sigma_{2}^{ge}+\textrm{H.c.},

(4)

and the dispersive term
\displaystyle\textstyle{\mathcal{H}}^{\mathrm{disp}}_{\mathrm{r-a}}
reads as

{\mathcal{H}}^{\mathrm{dsp}}_{\mathrm{r}-\mathrm{t}}=\eta_{1a}a^{\dagger}\sigma_{1}^{ef}+\eta_{1b}b^{\dagger}\sigma_{1}^{ge}+\eta_{2b}b^{\dagger}\sigma_{2}^{ef}+\textrm{H.c.}.

(5)

Parameters
\displaystyle\textstyle g_{1a}
and
\displaystyle\textstyle g_{1b}
are the coupling strengths of
\displaystyle\textstyle|g\rangle_{1}\leftrightarrow|e\rangle_{1}
and
\displaystyle\textstyle|e\rangle_{1}\leftrightarrow|f\rangle_{1}
transitions of the first transmon to resonators
\displaystyle\textstyle A
and
\displaystyle\textstyle B
, respectively.
Parameter
\displaystyle\textstyle g_{2b}
stands for the coupling strength of
\displaystyle\textstyle|g\rangle_{2}\leftrightarrow|e\rangle_{2}
transition of the second transmon to resonator
\displaystyle\textstyle B
.
Parameter
\displaystyle\textstyle\eta_{1a}
is the coupling strength of resonator
\displaystyle\textstyle A
to the first transmon
\displaystyle\textstyle|e\rangle_{1}\leftrightarrow|f\rangle_{1}
transition,
\displaystyle\textstyle\eta_{1b}
stands for the coupling strength of resonator
\displaystyle\textstyle B
to
\displaystyle\textstyle|g\rangle_{1}\leftrightarrow|e\rangle_{1}
transition of the first transmon, and
\displaystyle\textstyle\eta_{2b}
denotes the coupling strength of resonator
\displaystyle\textstyle B
to
\displaystyle\textstyle|e\rangle_{2}\leftrightarrow|f\rangle_{2}
transition of the second transmon.
For the transmon-type artificial atoms, one has
\displaystyle\textstyle\eta_{1a}\approx\sqrt{2}g_{1a}
,
\displaystyle\textstyle\eta_{1b}\approx g_{1b}/\sqrt{2}
, and
\displaystyle\textstyle\eta_{2b}\approx\sqrt{2}g_{2b}

Koch et al. 2007
.

The waveguides are modeled by the continua of non-interacting bosonic modes with the Hamiltonian
\displaystyle\textstyle{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{w}}
given by

{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{w}}=\int^{\infty}_{0}\mathrm{d}\nu\nu\left(\alpha^{\dagger}_{\nu}\alpha_{\nu}+\beta^{\dagger}_{\nu}\beta_{\nu}\right),

(6)

where
\displaystyle\textstyle\alpha_{\nu}(a^{\dagger}_{\nu})
and
\displaystyle\textstyle\beta_{\nu}(\beta^{\dagger}_{\nu})
stand for the annihilation (creation) operators of the photon with frequency
\displaystyle\nu
propagating in the waveguide
\displaystyle\alpha
and
\displaystyle\beta
, respectively.

The Hamiltonian
\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{r}-\mathrm{w}}
, describing the coupling between the resonators and waveguides, has the form:

{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{r}-\mathrm{w}}=\int^{\infty}_{0}\mathrm{d}\nu\left[f_{a}(\nu)a^{\dagger}\alpha_{\nu}+f_{b}(\nu)b^{\dagger}\beta_{\nu}+\textrm{H.c.}\right],

(7)

where
\displaystyle\textstyle f_{a}(\nu)
and
\displaystyle\textstyle f_{b}(\nu)
are the coupling strengths of resonator
\displaystyle\textstyle A
to waveguide
\displaystyle\textstyle\alpha
and resonator
\displaystyle\textstyle B
to waveguide
\displaystyle\textstyle\beta
, correspondingly.

Note that the model Hamiltonian in Eq. (
1
) does not explicitly include the Purcell filters.
Their contribution is absorbed into the frequency-dependent resonator-waveguide couplings
\displaystyle f_{a}(\nu)
and
\displaystyle f_{b}(\nu)
.
The effect of the Purcell filters is outlined in Appendix
B.2
.

The effective Hamiltonian

The dispersive coupling term
\displaystyle\textstyle{\mathcal{H}}^{\mathrm{dsp}}_{\mathrm{r-a}}
given by Eq. (
5
) can be treated as a perturbation of the Hamiltonian
\displaystyle\textstyle{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{sys}}
provided that
\displaystyle\textstyle|\lambda_{1a}|,|\lambda_{1b}|,|\lambda_{2b}|\ll 1
, where

\lambda_{1a}=\frac{\eta_{1a}}{\omega_{1}^{ef}-\omega_{a}},\,\lambda_{1b}=\frac{\eta_{1b}}{\omega_{1}^{ge}-\omega_{b}},\,\lambda_{2b}=\frac{\eta_{2b}}{\omega_{2}^{ef}-\omega_{b}}.

In this case, one can eliminate
\displaystyle\textstyle{\mathcal{H}}^{\mathrm{dsp}}_{\mathrm{r-a}}
via the Schrieffer-Wolff-type transformation
\displaystyle\textstyle{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{sys}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{U}}^{\dagger}{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{sys}}{\mathcal{U}}
with
\displaystyle{\mathcal{U}}
being the unitary operator given by
Klimov and Sanchez-Soto 2000
;
Blais et al. 2004

{\mathcal{U}}=\exp\left(\lambda_{1a}a^{\dagger}\sigma_{1}^{ef}+\lambda_{1b}b^{\dagger}\sigma_{1}^{ge}+\lambda_{2b}b^{\dagger}\sigma_{2}^{ef}-\textrm{H.c.}\right).

(8)

Keeping the terms contributing up to the first order in the small parameters
\displaystyle\textstyle\lambda_{1a}
,
\displaystyle\textstyle\lambda_{1b}
, and
\displaystyle\textstyle\lambda_{2b}
, one arrives at the effective Hamiltonian
\displaystyle\textstyle\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}
of the form (see details of derivation in Appendix
B
):

\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}={\bar{\mathcal{H}}}_{\mathrm{t}}+{\bar{\mathcal{H}}}_{\mathrm{r}}+{\mathcal{H}}^{\mathrm{rsn}}_{\mathrm{r}-\mathrm{t}}+{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{rr-tm}}+{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{w}}+{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{r}-\mathrm{w}}.

(9)

In the dressed basis, the Hamiltonian describing the transmons reads as

\begin{split}{\bar{\mathcal{H}}}_{\mathrm{t}}=\bar{\omega}_{1}^{ge}\sigma_{1}^{ee}+\bar{\omega}_{1}^{gf}\sigma_{1}^{ff}+\omega_{2}^{ge}\sigma_{1}^{ee}+\bar{\omega}_{2}^{gf}\sigma_{2}^{ff},\end{split}

(10)

with
\displaystyle\textstyle\bar{\omega}_{1}^{ge}=\omega_{1}^{ge}+\chi_{1b}
,
\displaystyle\textstyle\bar{\omega}_{1}^{gf}=\bar{\omega}_{1}^{ge}+\bar{\omega}_{1}^{ef}=\omega_{1}^{ef}+\chi_{1a}
, and
\displaystyle\textstyle\bar{\omega}_{2}^{gf}=\omega_{2}^{ge}+\bar{\omega}_{2}^{ef}=\omega_{2}^{gf}+\chi_{2b}
being the dressed (renormalized) frequencies of
\displaystyle\textstyle|g\rangle_{1}\leftrightarrow|e\rangle_{1}
and
\displaystyle\textstyle|g\rangle_{1}\leftrightarrow|f\rangle_{1}
transitions of the first transmon, and
\displaystyle\textstyle|g\rangle_{2}\leftrightarrow|f\rangle_{2}
transition of the second transmon, respectively.
Here, we introduced the notations
\displaystyle\textstyle\chi_{1a}=\lambda_{1a}\eta_{1a}
,
\displaystyle\textstyle\chi_{1b}=\lambda_{1b}\eta_{1b}
, and
\displaystyle\textstyle\chi_{2b}=\lambda_{2b}\eta_{2b}
.

The Hamiltonian of the resonators acquires the form

\begin{split}{\bar{\mathcal{H}}}_{\mathrm{r}}=&\,\big(\omega_{a}+\chi_{1a}\mathcal{Z}_{1}^{fe}\big)a^{\dagger}a\\
&\,+\big(\omega_{b}+\chi_{1b}\mathcal{Z}_{1}^{eg}+\chi_{2b}\mathcal{Z}_{2}^{fe}\big)b^{\dagger}b,\end{split}

(11)

where we introduced the notation
\displaystyle\textstyle\mathcal{Z}_{j}^{ss^{\prime}}\equiv\sigma_{j}^{ss}-\sigma_{j}^{s^{\prime}s^{\prime}}
.

The term
\displaystyle\textstyle{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{r}\mathrm{r}-\mathrm{t}}
in Eq. (
9
) is given by

{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{r}\mathrm{r}-\mathrm{t}}=\varUpsilon\big(a^{\dagger}b^{\dagger}\sigma_{1}^{gf}+\sigma_{1}^{fg}a\,b\big),

(12)

where
\displaystyle\textstyle 2\varUpsilon=\lambda_{1a}\eta_{1b}-\lambda_{1b}\eta_{1a}
.
This term describes the processes of simultaneous exchange of two excitations between the first transmon and resonators
\displaystyle\textstyle A
and
\displaystyle\textstyle B
.

In the further analysis, we work in the dressed basis and describe the dynamics of the system using the Hamiltonian
\displaystyle\textstyle\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}
expressed by Eqs. (
9
)–(
12
).

Note that here we focus on the unitary dynamics of the system, neglecting the dissipation.
We assume that dissipation processes occur on the timescales much longer than the timescales of the coherent processes in the system.
With such an approach, we simplify the theoretical treatment of the considered system while still grasping the essential features of its quantum dynamics.

Indeed, for the system parameters we work with, the photon leakage rates from the resonators to the output waveguides are
\displaystyle\textstyle\kappa/2\pi>1\,\mathrm{MHz}
, while the internal quality factors of the CPW resonators can exceed
\displaystyle\textstyle 10^{6}

Megrant et al. 2012
;
Bruno et al. 2015
, giving the intrinsic resonator photon loss rates
\displaystyle\textstyle\gamma_{\mathrm{res}}/2\pi\lesssim 0.01\,\mathrm{MHz}
.
The coaxial resonators
Reagor et al. 2016
;
Heidler et al. 2021
and the three-dimensional microwave cavities
Paik et al. 2011
;
Reagor et al. 2013
;
Flurin et al. 2015
offer even higher internal quality factors reaching up to
\displaystyle 10^{8}-10^{9}
.
Thus, the process of photon leakage to the output waveguides dominates over the photon dissipation inside the resonators.
The typical excited-state lifetimes of the state-of-the-art transmons reach
\displaystyle\textstyle 0.05-0.1\,\mathrm{ms}

Barends et al. 2013
;
Ristè et al. 2017
, with the recent experiments
Place et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2022
reporting lifetimes up to
\displaystyle\textstyle 0.5\,\mathrm{ms}
, which are more than an order of magnitude longer than the times the transmons dwell in their excited states for the system parameters we consider in this work.
Thus, the decoherence of transmons has a negligible effect on the emission dynamics.

V
Emission dynamics

We assume that initially (at
\displaystyle\textstyle t=0
), the system is prepared in the state
\displaystyle\textstyle|\Psi_{\mathrm{in}}\rangle
with the transmons residing in their excited states
\displaystyle\textstyle|e\rangle_{1}
and
\displaystyle\textstyle|e\rangle_{2}
, while the resonators and the waveguides are void of excitation.
Thus, the initial state of the system reads as
\displaystyle\textstyle|\Psi_{\mathrm{in}}\rangle=\sigma_{1}^{eg}\sigma_{2}^{eg}|\varnothing\rangle
,
where
\displaystyle\textstyle|\varnothing\rangle=|\varnothing\rangle_{\alpha}|\varnothing\rangle_{\beta}|0\rangle_{a}|0\rangle_{b}|g\rangle_{1}|g\rangle_{2}
stands for the vacuum state of the system – a state with a void of photons in the resonators and the waveguides and the transmons residing in their ground states.
In our analysis, we do not account for the effect of thermal excitations on the dynamics of the system.
This simplification is justified since the superconducting circuit QED systems usually operate at temperatures
\displaystyle\textstyle T_{\mathrm{sys}}\sim 10-30\,\mathrm{mK}
, while the typical working frequencies of microwave circuit QED setups are
\displaystyle\omega_{\mathrm{sys}}/2\pi\sim 5-20\,\mathrm{GHz}

Gu et al. 2017
;
Krantz et al. 2019
.
For these parameters, one obtains the estimate
\displaystyle\textstyle n_{\mathrm{th}}<10^{-3}
for the average number of thermal excitations in the system.

At the arbitrary moment of time
\displaystyle\textstyle t
, the state of the system
\displaystyle\textstyle|\varPsi(t)\rangle=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}t}|\Psi_{\mathrm{in}}\rangle
is expressed as

\begin{split}|\varPsi(t)\rangle=&\,\int^{\infty}_{0}\mathrm{d}\nu\int^{\infty}_{0}\mathrm{d}\nu^{\prime}\varPhi_{\nu,\nu^{\prime}}(t)\alpha^{\dagger}_{\nu}\beta^{\dagger}_{\nu^{\prime}}|\varnothing\rangle+\int^{\infty}_{0}\mathrm{d}\nu\left[\varXi^{\alpha}_{\nu}(t)\alpha^{\dagger}_{\nu}b^{\dagger}+\varXi^{\beta}_{\nu}(t)\beta^{\dagger}_{\nu}a^{\dagger}+\varTheta^{\alpha}_{\nu}(t)\alpha^{\dagger}_{\nu}\sigma_{2}^{eg}+\varTheta^{\beta}_{\nu}(t)\beta^{\dagger}_{\nu}\sigma_{1}^{eg}\right]|\varnothing\rangle\\
&\,+R(t)a^{\dagger}b^{\dagger}|\varnothing\rangle+Q(t)\sigma_{1}^{fg}|\varnothing\rangle+Y_{a}(t)a^{\dagger}\sigma_{2}^{eg}|\varnothing\rangle+Y_{b}(t)b^{\dagger}\sigma_{1}^{eg}|\varnothing\rangle+X(t)\sigma_{1}^{eg}\sigma_{2}^{eg}|\varnothing\rangle.\end{split}

(13)

The first term in Eq. (
13
) corresponds to the state of the system with both waveguides hosting a photon, with
\displaystyle\textstyle\varPhi_{\nu,\nu^{\prime}}
being the joint spectral amplitude.
The quantity
\displaystyle\textstyle S_{\nu,\nu}(t)=|\varPhi_{\nu,\nu^{\prime}}(t)|^{2}
determines the joint spectrum – the probability density distribution of finding a photon with frequency
\displaystyle\textstyle\nu
propagating in waveguide
\displaystyle\textstyle\alpha
and a photon with frequency
\displaystyle\textstyle\nu^{\prime}
propagating in waveguide
\displaystyle\textstyle\beta
at the moment of time
\displaystyle\textstyle t
.
The probability of finding the waveguides both accommodating photons is given by

p_{\alpha\beta}(t)=\int^{\infty}_{0}\mathrm{d}\nu\int^{\infty}_{0}\mathrm{d}\nu^{\prime}\,S_{\nu,\nu^{\prime}}(t).

(14)

The remaining terms in the upper line of Eq. (
13
) correspond to the states with photon propagating in only one of the waveguides:
\displaystyle\textstyle\varXi_{\nu}^{\alpha}(\varXi_{\nu}^{\beta})
is the amplitude of the state with the photon in resonator
\displaystyle\textstyle A(B)
and the transmons in their ground states,
\displaystyle\textstyle\varTheta_{\nu}^{\alpha}(\varTheta_{\nu}^{\beta})
is the amplitude of the state with the resonators void of photons and the first (second) transmon residing in the excited state
\displaystyle\textstyle|e\rangle_{1(2)}
.
The terms in the bottom line of Eq. (
13
) correspond to the states of the system with both waveguides void of excitations.

The equations of motion governing the evolution of the probability amplitudes in Eq. (
13
) can be compactly written as

\frac{\partial\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(t)}{\partial t}=-\mathrm{i}\,\boldsymbol{\Omega}\,\boldsymbol{\mu}(t),

(15)

where
\displaystyle\textstyle\boldsymbol{\mu}(t)=[\varPhi_{\nu,\nu^{\prime}}(t),\varXi^{\alpha}_{\nu}(t),\varXi^{\beta}_{\nu}(t),\varTheta^{\alpha}_{\nu}(t),\varTheta^{\beta}_{\nu}(t),R(t),Q(t),Y_{a}(t),Y_{b}(t),X(t)]^{\mathrm{T}}
and the matrix
\displaystyle\textstyle\boldsymbol{\Omega}
reads as

\boldsymbol{\Omega}=\begin{pmatrix}\nu+\nu^{\prime}&f_{b}(\omega_{b})&f_{a}(\omega_{a})&0&0&0&0&0&0&0\\
0&\nu+\widetilde{\omega}_{b}-\chi_{1b}&0&g_{2b}&0&f_{a}(\omega_{a})&0&0&0&0\\
0&0&\nu^{\prime}+\widetilde{\omega}_{a}&0&g_{1a}&f_{b}(\omega_{b})&0&0&0&0\\
0&g_{2b}&0&\nu+\omega_{2}^{ge}&0&0&0&f_{a}(\omega_{a})&0&0\\
0&0&g_{1a}&0&\nu^{\prime}+\bar{\omega}_{1}^{ge}&0&0&0&f_{b}(\omega_{b})&0\\
0&0&0&0&0&\widetilde{\omega}_{a}+\widetilde{\omega}_{b}-\chi_{1b}&\varUpsilon&g_{2b}&g_{1a}&0\\
0&0&0&0&0&\varUpsilon&\bar{\omega}^{gf}_{1}&0&g_{1b}&0\\
0&0&0&0&0&g_{2b}&0&\widetilde{\omega}_{a}+\omega_{2}^{ge}&0&g_{1a}\\
0&0&0&0&0&g_{1a}&g_{1b}&0&\widetilde{\omega}_{b}+\bar{\omega}_{1}^{ge}+\chi_{1b}&g_{2b}\\
0&0&0&0&0&0&0&g_{1a}&g_{2b}&\bar{\omega}_{1}^{ge}+\omega_{2}^{ge}\\
\end{pmatrix},

where
\displaystyle\textstyle\widetilde{\omega}_{a}=\omega_{a}-\mathrm{i}\kappa_{a}/2
and
\displaystyle\textstyle\widetilde{\omega}_{b}=\omega_{b}-\mathrm{i}\kappa_{b}/2
with

\displaystyle\textstyle\kappa_{a}=2\pi f^{2}_{a}(\omega_{a})
and
\displaystyle\textstyle\kappa_{b}=2\pi f^{2}_{b}(\omega_{b})
being the photon leakage rate from resonators
\displaystyle\textstyle A
and
\displaystyle\textstyle B
into waveguides
\displaystyle\textstyle\alpha
and
\displaystyle\textstyle\beta
, correspondingly.
The derivation of Eq. (
15
) is given in Appendix
C
and follows the approach demonstrated in Refs.
Stolyarov 2019
and
Sokolov and Stolyarov 2020
.

For computing the probability amplitudes standing in Eq. (
13
), we use the symbolic solution of Eq. (
15
) expressed as
\displaystyle\textstyle\boldsymbol{\mu}(t)=\exp(-\mathrm{i}\boldsymbol{\Omega}t)\boldsymbol{\mu}(0)
.
The matrix exponential is evaluated numerically using the built-in function
MatrixExp
of
Mathematica
.

VI
Photon entanglement

In the course of its evolution, the considered system eventually reaches the state when all emission processes are finished, the transmons have relaxed to their ground states, and the photons propagate in the waveguides as free excitations.
This final state of the system
\displaystyle\textstyle|\Psi_{\mathrm{fi}}\rangle
is given by

|\Psi_{\mathrm{fi}}\rangle=\int\mathrm{d}\nu\int\mathrm{d}\nu^{\prime}\Phi^{\mathrm{out}}_{\nu,\nu^{\prime}}\alpha^{\dagger}_{\nu}\beta^{\dagger}_{\nu^{\prime}}|\varnothing\rangle,

(21)

with
\displaystyle\textstyle\Phi^{\mathrm{out}}_{\nu,\nu^{\prime}}
being the joint spectral amplitude of the outgoing photon pair.
If the emitted photons are not entangled, their joint spectral amplitude can be factorized into a product of single-photon amplitudes
\displaystyle\textstyle\Phi^{\mathrm{out}}_{\nu,\nu^{\prime}}=\phi_{\alpha}(\nu)\phi_{\beta}(\nu^{\prime})
, while the joint spectral amplitude of TF entangled photon pair is not factorable
\displaystyle\textstyle\Phi^{\mathrm{out}}_{\nu,\nu^{\prime}}\neq\phi_{\alpha}(\nu)\phi_{\beta}(\nu^{\prime})
.
For testing the factorizability of the joint spectral amplitude of the emitted photons
\displaystyle\textstyle\Phi^{\mathrm{out}}_{\nu,\nu^{\prime}}
, we perform its Schmidt decomposition
Law et al. 2000
;
Eberly 2006
:

\Phi^{\mathrm{out}}_{\nu,\nu^{\prime}}=\sum_{j}\sqrt{\lambda_{j}}\,\varphi_{j,\nu}\vartheta_{j,\nu^{\prime}},

(22)

where the weights
\displaystyle\textstyle\lambda_{j}\geq 0
satisfying the condition
\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}=1
are usually referred to as the Schmidt coefficients, and the single-photon spectral amplitudes
\displaystyle\textstyle\varphi_{j,\nu}
and
\displaystyle\textstyle\vartheta_{j,\nu}
are called the Schmidt modes.
The latter constitute a complete set of orthonormal functions obeying the relations

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{j}\zeta^{*}_{j,\nu}\zeta_{j,\nu^{\prime}}=\delta(\nu-\nu^{\prime})
and
\displaystyle\textstyle\int\mathrm{d}\nu\,\zeta^{*}_{j,\nu}\zeta_{k,\nu}=\delta_{j,k}
,
where
\displaystyle\textstyle\zeta=\varphi
or
\displaystyle\vartheta
.

The Schmidt coefficients
\displaystyle\textstyle\lambda_{j}
are determined by solution of the eigenvalue problem
Law et al. 2000
:

\begin{split}\int\mathrm{d}\nu^{\prime}\,\mathcal{K}^{\varphi}_{\nu,\nu^{\prime}}\,\varphi_{j,\nu^{\prime}}=\lambda_{j}\varphi_{j,\nu},\\
\int\mathrm{d}\nu^{\prime}\,\mathcal{K}^{\vartheta}_{\nu,\nu^{\prime}}\,\vartheta_{j,\nu^{\prime}}=\lambda_{j}\vartheta_{j,\nu},\end{split}

(23)

where the integral kernels
\displaystyle\textstyle\mathcal{K}^{\varphi}_{\nu,\nu^{\prime}}
and
\displaystyle\textstyle\mathcal{K}^{\vartheta}_{\nu,\nu^{\prime}}
are defined as

\begin{split}\mathcal{K}^{\varphi}_{\nu,\nu^{\prime}}=\int\mathrm{d}\varpi\,(\Phi^{\mathrm{out}}_{\nu,\varpi})^{*}\Phi^{\mathrm{out}}_{\nu^{\prime},\varpi},\\
\mathcal{K}^{\vartheta}_{\nu,\nu^{\prime}}=\int\mathrm{d}\varpi\,(\Phi^{\mathrm{out}}_{\varpi,\nu})^{*}\Phi^{\mathrm{out}}_{\varpi,\nu^{\prime}}.\end{split}

(24)

\displaystyle\textstyle\pm\varDelta\omega
around the resonator frequencies
\displaystyle\textstyle\omega_{a}
and
\displaystyle\textstyle\omega_{b}
, where

\displaystyle\textstyle\int^{\omega_{a}+\varDelta\omega}_{\omega_{a}-\varDelta\omega}\mathrm{d}\nu\int^{\omega_{b}+\varDelta\omega}_{\omega_{b}-\varDelta\omega}\mathrm{d}\nu^{\prime}|\Phi^{\mathrm{out}}_{\nu,\nu^{\prime}}|^{2}\geq\Theta
with
\displaystyle\textstyle 0<\Theta<1
.

\displaystyle\textstyle\mathcal{K}^{\varphi}_{\nu,\nu^{\prime}}
and
\displaystyle\textstyle\mathcal{K}^{\vartheta}_{\nu,\nu^{\prime}}
in the grid nodes are computed by the numerical evaluation of the integrals in Eq. (

function.
The joint spectral amplitude of the outgoing photons is evaluated as
\displaystyle\textstyle\Phi^{\mathrm{out}}_{\nu,\nu^{\prime}}=\varPhi_{\nu,\nu^{\prime}}(t_{\infty})
, where the moment of time
\displaystyle\textstyle t_{\infty}
is determined as
\displaystyle\textstyle p_{\alpha\beta}(t_{\infty})>0.999
.
Then, we use the
Eigenvalues
function for determining the set of eigenvalues
\displaystyle\textstyle\{\Lambda_{j}\}
for the obtained matrices.
A finite discretization of the bounded domain of photon frequencies results in
\displaystyle\textstyle(\mathcal{I}\equiv\sum^{\mathsf{N}}_{j=1}\Lambda_{j})<1
, so we make a normalization
\displaystyle\textstyle\{\lambda_{j}\}=\{\Lambda_{j}\}/\mathcal{I}
to ensure that
\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}=1
.
We set
\displaystyle\textstyle\mathsf{N}=100
and
\displaystyle\textstyle\Theta=0.99
for all computations.
For the system parameters we use for computations, the extension of the frequency domain (by setting larger
\displaystyle\textstyle\Theta
) and using the finer grid (by increasing
\displaystyle\textstyle\mathsf{N}
) has only a minor effect on the evaluated values of the Schmidt coefficients and the entanglement entropies.

As a measure of entanglement of the emitted photons, we use the entanglement (von Neumann) entropy
\displaystyle\textstyle S_{\mathrm{ent}}
, which is expressed via the Schmidt coefficients as
Bennett et al. 1996
:

S_{\mathrm{ent}}=-\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}\log_{2}\lambda_{j}.

(25)

The non-zero entanglement entropy,
\displaystyle\textstyle S_{\mathrm{ent}}>0
, implies that emitted photons are entangled.

Let us elucidate some features of the obtained behavior of the entanglement entropy using the general considerations discussed in Sec.
II
.
The relaxation of the first transmon not only delivers the photon via resonator
\displaystyle\textstyle A
to waveguide
\displaystyle\textstyle\alpha
but also triggers the relaxation of the second transmon leading to the emission of the photon into waveguide
\displaystyle\textstyle\beta
.
For gaining stronger temporal correlations between the emitted photons, we need the photon to be emitted into the waveguide
\displaystyle\textstyle\beta
shortly after the emission of the photon into waveguide
\displaystyle\textstyle\alpha
triggered the relaxation of the second transmon.
That is attained by increasing the coupling between the second transmon and resonator
\displaystyle\textstyle B

\displaystyle\textstyle g_{2b}/g_{1a}
.
The larger ratios
\displaystyle\textstyle g_{1b}/g_{2b}
are required for the efficient inhibition of the second transmon relaxation until the moment the first transmon decays, delivering a photon to waveguide
\displaystyle\textstyle\alpha
.
The mechanism of that inhibition is clarified in the second paragraph of Sec.

.

VII
Discussion and Summary

Having outlined the scheme and operational principle of the on-demand source of microwave TF entangled photon pairs and investigated its performance, let us now briefly discuss the general idea of how the proposed scheme can be extended for the generation of multiphoton entangled states. For this purpose, we consider the setup for the generation of the three-photon entangled states illustrated in Fig.
8
.
Compared to the original setup for the generation of photon pairs shown in Fig.
2
, here we added one more resonator (marked as
\displaystyle\textstyle C
) with frequency
\displaystyle\textstyle\omega_{c}
coupled to the output transmission line and a third transmon coupled to resonators
\displaystyle\textstyle A
and
\displaystyle\textstyle C
.
Following the logic of Sec.
II
, the frequencies
\displaystyle\textstyle\omega^{ge}_{3}
and
\displaystyle\textstyle\omega^{ef}_{3}
of
\displaystyle|g\rangle_{3}\leftrightarrow|e\rangle_{3}
and
\displaystyle\textstyle|e\rangle_{3}\leftrightarrow|f\rangle_{3}
transitions of the third transmon are set that
\displaystyle\textstyle\omega^{ge}_{3}=\omega_{c}
and
\displaystyle\textstyle\omega^{ef}_{3}=\omega_{a}
.
The interrelation between the frequencies of the resonators and the transition frequencies of the transmons is schematically shown in Fig.
8
(b).
Such a choice of frequencies enables the resonant excitation exchange between
\displaystyle\textstyle|g\rangle_{3}\leftrightarrow|e\rangle_{3}
transition and resonator
\displaystyle\textstyle C
and
\displaystyle\textstyle|e\rangle_{3}\leftrightarrow|f\rangle_{3}
transition and resonator
\displaystyle\textstyle A
, while the excitation exchange between
\displaystyle\textstyle|g\rangle_{3}\leftrightarrow|e\rangle_{3}
transition and between resonator
\displaystyle\textstyle A
and
\displaystyle\textstyle|e\rangle_{3}\leftrightarrow|f\rangle_{3}
transition and resonator
\displaystyle\textstyle C
is inhibited due to detuning.
The relaxation of the third transmon excited state
\displaystyle\textstyle|e\rangle_{3}
decouples
\displaystyle\textstyle|e\rangle_{3}\leftrightarrow|f\rangle_{3}
transition from resonator
\displaystyle\textstyle A
, triggering the relaxation of the first transmon excited state
\displaystyle\textstyle|e\rangle_{1}
, which, in turn, triggers the relaxation of the second transmon.
Thus, one may anticipate that the photons are emitted into the corresponding waveguides in TF entangled triples.

Figure 8:
(a) The variant of extension of the proposed scheme of generation of TF entangled photons: schematics of the potential circuit QED setup for generation of three-photon entangled states. (b) Scheme illustrating the relationships between the frequencies of the resonators and the transition frequencies of the transmons in the setup shown in Fig.
8
(a)
.

To summarize, we proposed a feasible scheme for efficient on-demand generation of pairs of microwave TF entangled photons propagating in separate waveguides and analyzed its performance.
The design and working parameters of the considered setup are readily accessible for the state-of-the-art superconducting circuit QED technologies.
Although we outlined a rather concrete superconducting circuit QED implementation of an on-demand source of microwave TF entangled photon pairs, we should stress that the general principle of operation does not rely on the details of the circuit QED realization and can be applied to other physical systems.
We focused our analysis on the pulsed on-demand regime of photon pairs generation.
Therefore, the consideration of the continuous regime of operation is of interest for future work.
Besides, a detailed investigation of the extended schemes (as shown in Fig.
8
) for the generation of multiphoton TF entangled states constitutes a potential research direction as well.

Appendix A
Pair of 2LEs coupled to resonator

The paradigmatic system described in the first paragraph of Sec.
II
and schematically shown in Fig.

(a)
is modeled by the Hamiltonian as follows

\begin{split}{\mathcal{H}}=&\,\omega c^{\dagger}c+\sum_{j=1}^{2}(\omega+\Delta_{j})\sigma^{+}_{j}\sigma^{-}_{j}+\sum_{j=1}^{2}g_{j}(c^{\dagger}\sigma^{-}_{j}+\sigma^{+}_{j}c)\\
&\,+\int^{\infty}_{0}\mathrm{d}\nu\nu A^{\dagger}_{\nu}A_{\nu}+\int\mathrm{d}\nu f(\nu)(A^{\dagger}_{\nu}c+c^{\dagger}A_{\nu}).\end{split}

(26)

The first three terms in the above Hamiltonian describe the single-mode resonator with frequency
\displaystyle\textstyle\omega
coupled to a pair of 2LEs, where
\displaystyle\textstyle\Delta_{j}
stands for the detuning between the frequencies of the
\displaystyle\textstyle j
-th 2LE and the resonator, parameter
\displaystyle\textstyle g_{j}
is the coupling strength of the resonator to the
\displaystyle\textstyle j
-th 2LE (
\displaystyle\textstyle j\in\{1,2\}
).
Operator
\displaystyle\textstyle c
(
\displaystyle\textstyle c^{\dagger}
) annihilates (creates) a photon in the resonator, and
\displaystyle\textstyle\sigma^{+}_{j}
(
\displaystyle\textstyle\sigma^{-}_{j}
) rises (lowers) the state of the
\displaystyle\textstyle j
-th 2LE.
The last pair of terms in the Hamiltonian in Eq. (
26
) describes the waveguide, represented by a bath of independent bosonic modes, and its coupling to the resonator with strength
\displaystyle\textstyle f(\nu)
.
Operator
\displaystyle\textstyle A_{\nu}
(
\displaystyle\textstyle A^{\dagger}_{\nu}
) annihilates (creates) a photon with frequency
\displaystyle\textstyle\nu
propagating in the waveguide.

Now, let us demonstrate that in the single-excitation case, the paradigmatic system composed of a pair of 2LEs coupled to the resonator, considered in Sec.
II
and illustrated in Fig.
1
(a)
, can be equivalently represented as a 2LE coupled to the
V
-configuration 3LE.
To proceed, we formally represent the Hamiltonian
\displaystyle\textstyle{\mathcal{H}}
given by Eq. (
26
) in the eigenbasis of the 2LE-resonator Hamiltonian
\displaystyle\textstyle{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{JC}}
. The latter is constituted by the terms describing the resonator, the first 2LE, and their interaction in Eq. (
26
), which together form the Hamiltonian of the Jaynes-Cummings (JC) model
Shore and Knight 1993
.
The JC system ground state
\displaystyle|\mathcal{G}\rangle=|0\rangle_{\mathrm{r}}|g\rangle_{1}
is a state with a vacuum field in the resonator
\displaystyle\textstyle|0\rangle_{\mathrm{r}}
and the first 2LE in the ground state
\displaystyle\textstyle|g\rangle_{1}
.
The excited eigenstates of
\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{JC}}
are the superpositions of the state
\displaystyle\textstyle|n\rangle_{\mathrm{r}}|g\rangle_{1}
containing
\displaystyle\textstyle n
photons in the resonator with the ground-state 2LE and the state
\displaystyle\textstyle|n-1\rangle_{\mathrm{r}}|e\rangle_{1}
with
\displaystyle\textstyle n-1
photons in the resonator and the excited-state 2LE
Shore and Knight 1993
;
Blais et al. 2004
:

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}|\mathcal{E}_{n}^{-}\rangle\\
|\mathcal{E}_{n}^{+}\rangle\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\cos\mu_{n}&-\sin\mu_{n}\\
\sin\mu_{n}&\cos\mu_{n}\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}|n\rangle_{\mathrm{r}}|g\rangle_{1}\\
|n-1\rangle_{\mathrm{r}}|e\rangle_{1}\end{array}\right),

where

\tan\mu_{n}=\sqrt{\frac{\Lambda_{n}-\Delta_{1}}{\Lambda_{n}+\Delta_{1}}},\quad\Lambda_{n}=\sqrt{4ng^{2}+\Delta_{1}^{2}}.

The JC eigenstates
\displaystyle\textstyle|\mathcal{E}^{\pm}_{n}\rangle
correspond to the eigenfrequencies
\displaystyle\textstyle E^{\pm}_{n}=n\omega+(\Delta_{1}\pm\Lambda_{n})/2
.

Restricting ourselves to the resonant regime of the 2LE-resonator coupling (
\displaystyle\textstyle\Delta_{1}=\Delta_{2}=0
) and the single-excitation domain, as considered in Sec.
II
, we project the system Hamiltonian
\displaystyle\textstyle{\mathcal{H}}
on the lowest eigenstates
\displaystyle\textstyle|\mathcal{G}\rangle
and
\displaystyle\textstyle|\mathcal{E}^{\pm}_{1}\rangle
of the JC Hamiltonian.
Thus, one has
\displaystyle\textstyle{\mathcal{H}}\rightarrow\varPi{\mathcal{H}}\varPi
, where
\displaystyle\textstyle\varPi=|\mathcal{G}\rangle\langle\mathcal{G}|+\sum_{\pm}|\mathcal{E}^{\pm}_{1}\rangle\langle\mathcal{E}^{\pm}_{1}|
, which gives:

\begin{split}{\mathcal{H}}=&\,\omega\sigma^{+}_{2}\sigma^{-}_{2}+\sum_{\pm}(\omega\pm g_{1})|\mathcal{E}^{\pm}\rangle\langle\mathcal{E}^{\pm}|+\int^{\infty}_{0}\mathrm{d}\nu\nu A^{\dagger}_{\nu}A_{\nu}\\
&\,+\frac{g_{2}}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{\pm}\left(\sigma^{+}_{2}|\mathcal{G}\rangle\langle\mathcal{E}^{\pm}|+|\mathcal{E}^{\pm}\rangle\langle\mathcal{G}|\sigma^{-}_{2}\right)\\
&\,+\int^{\infty}_{0}\mathrm{d}\nu\,\frac{f(\nu)}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{\pm}(A^{\dagger}_{\nu}|\mathcal{G}\rangle\langle\mathcal{E}^{\pm}|+|\mathcal{E}^{\pm}\rangle\langle\mathcal{G}|A_{\nu}),\end{split}

where for brevity we dropped the subscripts indicating the photon number in the notations of JC eigenstates.
The above Hamiltonian describes the 3LE coupled to the 2LE and the waveguide.
The 3LE is constituted by the ground state
\displaystyle|\mathcal{G}\rangle
and a pair of excited states
\displaystyle|\mathcal{E}^{\pm}\rangle
.
The frequency of
\displaystyle\textstyle|\mathcal{G}\rangle\leftrightarrow|\mathcal{E}^{\pm}\rangle

\displaystyle\omega\pm g_{1}

The levels of the 3LE are arranged in the
\displaystyle\mathsf{V}

\displaystyle\mathsf{V}

\displaystyle g_{2}/\sqrt{2}

Dynamics of the resonator-2LEs system

Figure 9:
Effect of the 2LEs couplings ratio
\displaystyle\textstyle g_{1}/g_{2}

\displaystyle\textstyle P_{\mathrm{w}}(t)

The parameters used for calculations are as follows:
\displaystyle\textstyle g_{2}/2\pi=10\,\mathrm{MHz}

\displaystyle\textstyle\kappa/2\pi=2\,\mathrm{MHz}

The waveguide population at time
\displaystyle\textstyle t
is given by:

P_{\mathrm{w}}(t)=\kappa\int^{t}_{0}\mathrm{d}\tau\langle c^{\dagger}(\tau)c(\tau)\rangle,

(27)

with
\displaystyle\textstyle\kappa=2\pi f^{2}(\omega)
being the resonator decay rate into the waveguide.
The averaging in Eq. (
27

\displaystyle\textstyle|\psi_{0}\rangle=\sigma^{+}_{2}|0\rangle
with
\displaystyle\textstyle|0\rangle=|0\rangle_{\mathrm{r}}|g\rangle_{1}|g\rangle_{2}
being the vacuum state of the resonator-2LEs system.
In the single-excitation case, the resonator population
\displaystyle\textstyle\langle c^{\dagger}(t)c(t)\rangle
can be evaluated using the relation

\displaystyle\textstyle\langle c^{\dagger}(t)c(t)\rangle=\langle\psi_{0}|c^{\dagger}(t)|0\rangle\langle 0|c(t)|\psi_{0}\rangle
.
The evolution of the matrix element
\displaystyle\textstyle\langle 0|c(t)|\psi_{0}\rangle
is governed by the set of equations of motion:

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\!\left(\!\begin{array}[]{c}\langle 0|c(t)|\psi_{0}\rangle\\
\langle 0|\sigma^{-}_{1}(t)|\psi_{0}\rangle\\
\langle 0|\sigma^{-}_{2}(t)|\psi_{0}\rangle\end{array}\!\right)=-\mathrm{i}\begin{pmatrix}\widetilde{\omega}&g_{1}&g_{2}\\
g_{1}&\omega&0\\
g_{2}&0&\omega\end{pmatrix}\!\!\left(\!\begin{array}[]{c}\langle 0|c(t)|\psi_{0}\rangle\\
\langle 0|\sigma^{-}_{1}(t)|\psi_{0}\rangle\\
\langle 0|\sigma^{-}_{2}(t)|\psi_{0}\rangle\end{array}\!\right),

where
\displaystyle\textstyle\widetilde{\omega}=\omega-\mathrm{i}\kappa/2
.

Using the solution of the above set of equations in Eq. (
27

P_{\mathrm{w}}(t)=\frac{g_{2}^{2}}{g_{1}^{2}+g_{2}^{2}}\Bigg[1-F(t)\exp\left(-\frac{\kappa}{2}t\right)\Bigg],

(28)

with
\displaystyle\textstyle F(t)

F(t)=1+\kappa\sin(\varOmega t)\,\frac{\kappa\sin(\varOmega t)+4\varOmega\cos(\varOmega t)}{8\varOmega^{2}},

where
\displaystyle\textstyle\varOmega=\sqrt{g_{1}^{2}+g_{2}^{2}-(\kappa/4)^{2}}
.

\displaystyle\textstyle P_{\mathrm{w}}(t)

\displaystyle\textstyle g_{1}/g_{2}

\displaystyle\textstyle g_{1}/g_{2}
leads to the inhibition of the photon leakage into the waveguide.
It follows from Eq. (
28

\displaystyle\textstyle P_{\mathrm{w}}(t\rightarrow\infty)<1
for
\displaystyle\textstyle g_{1}\neq 0
.
For
\displaystyle\textstyle g_{1}\gg g_{2}

\displaystyle\textstyle P_{\mathrm{w}}(t\rightarrow\infty)\ll 1
implying that the leakage into the waveguide is suppressed and the photon remains trapped within the resonator-2LEs system.

\displaystyle\textstyle|f\rangle_{2}
.
These direct couplings of the transmon transitions to the waveguides lead to that both excitations can be emitted into one waveguide instead of being emitted into the separate waveguides.
For mitigating this superfluous emission processes, we use the Purcell filters.
In the proposed setup (see Fig.
2
), the latter are represented by the resonators side-coupled to the output waveguides.
The frequencies of these resonators correspond to the dressed frequencies
\displaystyle\textstyle\bar{\omega}_{1}^{ge}
and
\displaystyle\textstyle\bar{\omega}_{1}^{ef}
of
\displaystyle\textstyle|g\rangle_{1}\leftrightarrow|e\rangle_{1}
and
\displaystyle\textstyle|e\rangle_{1}\leftrightarrow|f\rangle_{1}
transitions.
The filter resonators reject the radiation in the narrow bands of frequencies in the vicinity of
\displaystyle\textstyle\bar{\omega}_{1}^{ge}
and
\displaystyle\textstyle\bar{\omega}_{1}^{ef}
and transmit the radiation with frequencies outside these bands
Reed et al. 2010
.
Thus, the Purcell filters inhibit the unwanted emission channels of the first transmon described by the first two terms in the Hamiltonian (
33
).
As we demonstrate in Sec.
V
, no more than one photon could reside in either of the resonators, so
\displaystyle\textstyle|e\rangle_{2}\rightarrow|f\rangle_{2}
transition of the second transmon is not involved in the dynamics of the system, and
\displaystyle\textstyle|f\rangle_{2}
level is not excited.
Thus, the Purcell filter cutting the emission from this transition into waveguide
\displaystyle\textstyle\beta
is not required.
Since the processes of direct relaxation of transmons into the waveguides are essentially suppressed by the Purcell filters and occur on the timescales much longer than the photon emission times, for simplicity, in the Hamiltonian
\displaystyle\textstyle\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}
, we drop the term
\displaystyle\textstyle{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{t}-\mathrm{w}}
describing these processes.

Appendix C
Derivation of evolution equations

For deriving Eq. (
15
) governing the evolution of the probability amplitudes, we follow the lines of Appendix B in Ref.
Stolyarov 2019
.
For this purpose, we start with the derivation of the equations of motion for the operators.

The effective Hamiltonian (
9
) generates the equations of motion for the waveguide variables
\displaystyle\textstyle\alpha_{\nu}(t)
and
\displaystyle\textstyle\beta_{\nu}(t)
as follows:

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\alpha_{\nu}(t)=-\mathrm{i}\nu\alpha_{\nu}(t)-\mathrm{i}f_{a}(\nu)a(t),

(34a)

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\beta_{\nu}(t)=-\mathrm{i}\nu\beta_{\nu}(t)-\mathrm{i}f_{b}(\nu)b(t),

(34b)

with the formal solutions written as

\alpha_{\nu}(t)=\tilde{\alpha}_{\nu}(t)-\mathrm{i}f_{a}(\nu)\int^{t}_{0}\mathrm{d}\tau\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\nu(t-\tau)}a(\tau),

(35a)

\beta_{\nu}(t)=\tilde{\beta}_{\nu}(t)-\mathrm{i}f_{b}(\nu)\int^{t}_{0}\mathrm{d}\tau\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\nu(t-\tau)}b(\tau),

(35b)

where
\displaystyle\textstyle\tilde{\alpha}_{\nu}(t)=\alpha_{\nu}(0)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\nu t}
and
\displaystyle\textstyle\tilde{\beta}_{\nu}(t)=\beta_{\nu}(0)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\nu t}
stand for the annihilation operators of a photon propagating as a free excitation in waveguide
\displaystyle\textstyle\alpha
and
\displaystyle\textstyle\beta
, respectively.

The equation of motion for the annihilation operator
\displaystyle\textstyle a(t)
of the photon in resonator
\displaystyle\textstyle A
reads as

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}a(t)=-\mathrm{i}\left[\omega_{a}+\chi_{1a}\mathcal{Z}_{1}^{fe}(t)\right]a(t)-\mathrm{i}g_{1a}\sigma_{1}^{ge}(t)-\mathrm{i}\varUpsilon b^{\dagger}(t)\sigma_{1}^{gf}(t)-\mathrm{i}\int^{\infty}_{0}\mathrm{d}\nu f_{a}(\nu)\alpha_{\nu}(t).

(36)

Plugging Eq. (
35a
) into the last term on the right-hand side of the above equation gives

\begin{split}\int^{\infty}_{0}\mathrm{d}\nu f_{a}(\nu)\alpha_{\nu}(t)&=\alpha_{\mathrm{in}}(t)-\mathrm{i}\int^{t}_{0}\mathrm{d}\tau\int^{\infty}_{0}\mathrm{d}\nu f^{2}_{a}(\nu)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\nu(t-\tau)}a(\tau),\end{split}

(37)

where
\displaystyle\textstyle\alpha_{\mathrm{in}}(t)=\int^{\infty}_{0}\mathrm{d}\nu\,f_{a}(\nu)\widetilde{\alpha}_{\nu}(t)
.
Using Eq. (
36
) and that
\displaystyle\textstyle|\chi_{1a}|\ll\omega_{a}
, one can represent the operator
\displaystyle\textstyle a(t)
as
\displaystyle\textstyle a(t)=\mathfrak{a}(t)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_{a}t}
, where
\displaystyle\textstyle\mathfrak{a}(t)
stands for the slowly-varying part of
\displaystyle a(t)
.
With such a representation, one can notice that only the frequencies in the vicinity of
\displaystyle\textstyle\omega_{a}
contribute significantly to the integral over
\displaystyle\textstyle\tau
on the right-hand side of Eq. (
37
).
Thus, one can extend the lower bound of integration over
\displaystyle\textstyle\nu
to
\displaystyle\textstyle-\infty
, and neglect the frequency dependence of the coupling parameter
\displaystyle\textstyle f_{a}(\nu)
assuming that
\displaystyle\textstyle f_{a}(\nu)\approx f_{a}(\omega_{a})
.
Using these approximations, one obtains

\int^{\infty}_{0}\mathrm{d}\nu f_{a}(\nu)\alpha_{\nu}(t)\approx\alpha_{\mathrm{in}}(t)-\mathrm{i}\frac{\kappa_{a}}{2}a(t),\quad\kappa_{a}=2\pi f^{2}_{a}(\omega_{a}).

(38)

Substituting this result into Eq. (
36
) yields

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}a(t)=-\mathrm{i}\left[\omega_{a}-\mathrm{i}\frac{\kappa_{a}}{2}+\chi_{1a}\mathcal{Z}_{1}^{fe}(t)\right]a(t)-\mathrm{i}g_{1a}\sigma_{1}^{ge}(t)-\mathrm{i}\varUpsilon b^{\dagger}\sigma_{1}^{gf}(t)-\mathrm{i}\alpha_{\mathrm{in}}(t).

(39)

The equation of motion for the operator
\displaystyle b(t)
annihilating a photon in the resonator
\displaystyle B
is obtained similarly to Eq. (
39
) and reads as

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}b(t)=-\mathrm{i}\left[\omega_{b}-\mathrm{i}\frac{\kappa_{b}}{2}+\chi_{1b}\mathcal{Z}_{1}^{eg}(t)+\chi_{2b}\mathcal{Z}_{2}^{fe}(t)\right]b(t)-\mathrm{i}g_{1b}\sigma_{1}^{ef}(t)-\mathrm{i}g_{2b}\sigma_{2}^{ge}(t)-\mathrm{i}\varUpsilon a^{\dagger}(t)\sigma_{1}^{gf}(t)-\mathrm{i}\beta_{\mathrm{in}}(t),

(40)

where
\displaystyle\textstyle\beta_{\mathrm{in}}(t)=\int^{\infty}_{0}\mathrm{d}\nu\,f_{b}(\nu)\tilde{\beta}_{\nu}(t)
.
For the derivation of Eq. (
40
), we used the approximate relation

\int^{\infty}_{0}\mathrm{d}\nu f_{b}(\nu)\beta_{\nu}(t)\approx\beta_{\mathrm{in}}(t)-\mathrm{i}\frac{\kappa_{b}}{2}b(t),\quad\kappa_{b}=2\pi f^{2}_{b}(\omega_{b}),

(41)

which is obtained by analogy to Eq. (
38
).

The equations of motion for the required ladder operators of the transmons read as follows:

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\sigma_{1}^{ge}(t)=-\mathrm{i}\left[\omega_{1}^{ge}+\chi_{1b}-\chi_{1a}{\mathcal{N}}_{a}(t)+2\chi_{1b}{\mathcal{N}}_{b}(t)\right]\sigma_{1}^{ge}(t)+\mathrm{i}g_{1a}\mathcal{Z}_{1}^{eg}(t)a(t)-\mathrm{i}g_{1b}b^{\dagger}(t)\sigma_{1}^{gf}(t)+\mathrm{i}\varUpsilon\sigma_{1}^{fe}(t)a(t)b(t),

(42a)

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\sigma_{1}^{gf}(t)=-\mathrm{i}\left[\omega_{1}^{ge}+\omega_{1}^{ef}+\chi_{1a}\right]\sigma_{1}^{gf}(t)-\mathrm{i}g_{1a}\sigma_{1}^{ef}(t)a-\mathrm{i}g_{1b}\sigma_{1}^{ge}(t)b(t)+\mathrm{i}\varUpsilon\mathcal{Z}_{1}^{fg}(t)a(t)b(t),

(42b)

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\sigma_{2}^{ge}(t)=-\mathrm{i}\left[\omega_{2}^{ge}-\chi_{2b}{\mathcal{N}}_{b}(t)\right]\sigma_{2}^{ge}(t)+\mathrm{i}g_{2b}\mathcal{Z}_{2}^{eg}(t)b(t).

(42c)

Using Eq. (
13
), one can express
\displaystyle\textstyle\varPhi_{\nu,\nu^{\prime}}(t)
as
\displaystyle\textstyle\varPhi_{\nu,\nu^{\prime}}(t)=\langle\varnothing|\alpha_{\nu}\beta_{\nu^{\prime}}|\varPsi(t)\rangle
.
Then, we use the standard quantum-mechanical relations
\displaystyle\textstyle|\varPsi(t)\rangle=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}t}|\varPsi(0)\rangle
and
\displaystyle\textstyle\mathcal{O}(t)=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}t}\mathcal{O}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}t}
, where
\displaystyle\textstyle\mathcal{O}
and
\displaystyle\textstyle\mathcal{O}(t)
denote the operators in the Schrödinger and Heisenberg representations, correspondingly.
This leads us to the expression
\displaystyle\textstyle\varPhi_{\nu,\nu^{\prime}}(t)=\langle\varnothing|\alpha_{\nu}(t)\beta_{\nu^{\prime}}(t)|\Psi_{\mathrm{in}}\rangle
, where we used that
\displaystyle\textstyle|\varPsi(0)\rangle\equiv|\Psi_{\mathrm{in}}\rangle
and
\displaystyle\textstyle\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}t}|\varnothing\rangle=|\varnothing\rangle
.
Using such an approach for the rest of the probability amplitudes entering the state vector given by Eq. (
13
), one obtains:

\boldsymbol{\mu}(t)=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\varPhi_{\nu,\nu^{\prime}}(t)\\
\varXi^{\alpha}_{\nu}(t)\\
\varXi^{\beta}_{\nu}(t)\\
\varTheta^{\alpha}_{\nu}(t)\\
\varTheta^{\beta}_{\nu}(t)\\
R(t)\\
S(t)\\
W^{a}(t)\\
W^{b}(t)\\
X(t)\end{array}\right]=\big\langle\varnothing\big|\!\left[\!\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha_{\nu}(t)\beta_{\nu^{\prime}}(t)\\
{\alpha}_{\nu}(t){b}(t)\\
{\beta}_{\nu}(t){a}(t)\\
{\alpha}_{\nu}(t){\sigma}_{2}^{ge}(t)\\
{\beta}_{\nu}(t){\sigma}_{2}^{ge}(t)\\
{a}(t){b}(t)\\
{\sigma}_{1}^{gf}(t)\\
{a}(t){\sigma}_{2}^{ge}(t)\\
{b}(t){\sigma}_{1}^{ge}(t)\\
{\sigma}_{1}^{ge}(t){\sigma}_{2}^{ge}(t)\end{array}\!\right]\!\big|\Psi_{\mathrm{in}}\big\rangle.

(43)

Then, using the equations of motion for the corresponding operators derived above, we derive the set of evolution equations for the matrix elements standing on the right-hand side of Eq. (
43
).
This leads us to the set of evolution equations for the probability amplitudes given by Eq. (
15
), where we used the narrowband approximation assuming that
\displaystyle\textstyle f_{a}(\nu)\approx f_{a}(\omega_{a})
and
\displaystyle\textstyle f_{b}(\nu)\approx f_{b}(\omega_{b})
.
