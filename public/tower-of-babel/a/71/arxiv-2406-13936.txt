Communication-Efficient Adaptive Batch Size Strategies for Distributed Local Gradient Methods
Tim Tsz-Kit Lau, Weijian Li, Chenwei Xu, Han Liu, Mladen Kolar

Abstract
Modern deep neural networks often require distributed training with many workers due to their large size. As the number of workers increases, communication overheads become the main bottleneck in data-parallel minibatch stochastic gradient methods with per-iteration gradient synchronization. Local gradient methods like Local SGD reduce communication by only synchronizing model parameters and/or gradients after several local steps. Despite an understanding of their convergence and the importance of batch sizes for training efficiency and generalization, optimal batch sizes for local gradient methods are difficult to determine. We introduce adaptive batch size strategies for local gradient methods that increase batch sizes adaptively to reduce minibatch gradient variance. We provide convergence guarantees under homogeneous data conditions and support our claims with image classification and language modeling experiments, demonstrating the effectiveness of our strategies for both training efficiency and generalization.

Introduction

Recent advances in deep learning, particularly large language models (LLMs) with more than billions of parameters, necessitate efficient distributed training on many workers such as GPUs or TPUs. Data-parallel optimization
(
Zinkevich et al., 2010
)
simplifies this by having each worker compute and average batch gradients from different i.i.d. data batches, followed by a global model update. Despite its efficiency with numerous workers, this method faces challenges such as costly gradient synchronization and reduced generalization from large batches. Frequent synchronization of model parameters or gradients among workers leads to significant communication overheads, exacerbated as worker counts and model sizes increase
(
Tang et al., 2021
;
Li et al., 2022
;
Xu et al., 2023
)
. This challenge intensifies with workers distributed across nodes with limited inter-node communication speeds. To mitigate this, communication-efficient distributed optimization methods
(
Lan et al., 2020
)
, such as local gradient methods
(
Stich, 2019a
;
Zhou and Cong, 2018
;
Yu et al., 2019
;
Haddadpour et al., 2019
;
Wang and Joshi, 2019
)
and their variants, have been developed. In local stochastic gradient methods (Local
SGD
), workers train locally and synchronize less frequently by averaging model parameters after several local steps, effectively reducing communication overheads. In data-parallel optimization, the total batch size is the sum of all workers’ local batch sizes, increasing with more workers. Attributed to the ever-rising memory of workers, large batch sizes are preferred to fully utilize worker memory, making large-batch training the
de facto
paradigm in large-scale model training. However, this approach often results in worse model generalization, a problem noted by
LeCun et al. (2002)
. Large-batch training typically creates a loss landscape with sharp minima, worsening generalization
(
Keskar et al., 2017
)
. Although scaling the learning rate with the batch size can mitigate the so-called
generalization gap

(
Hoffer et al., 2017
;
Smith and Le, 2018
;
Smith et al., 2018
)
, it cannot fully close it.
Keskar et al. (2017)
recommended adaptive sampling to gradually increase batch sizes during training
(
Byrd et al., 2012
;
Friedlander and Schmidt, 2012
)
, explored in
De et al. (2016)
;
De et al. (2017)
;
Lau et al. (2024)
;
Ostroukhov et al. (2024)
. However, this method is still confined to single- or few-worker scenarios and does not scale well with larger models and more workers.

Motivated by the above two challenges, we answer the following question affirmatively:

Can we develop communication-efficient and memory-efficient training strategies with desirable generalization performance for large-scale distributed training?

Contributions.

We make the following contributions: (i) We develop adaptive batch size strategies for
local gradient methods
by extending the adaptive sampling methods in
Byrd et al. (2012)
;
Bollapragada et al. (2018)
. Such strategies are for local batch sizes, i.e., batch sizes of individual workers. This is particularly relevant when the workers are heterogeneous devices with different computational speeds and memories, as well as different local objectives and datasets. The proposed strategies are
communication-efficient
by leveraging local gradient methods, and
memory-efficient
by leveraging large batch sizes at later stages of training. (ii) Under various standard assumptions, we provide convergence guarantees of the proposed approaches for smooth (strongly) convex and nonconvex objectives under a homogeneous data setting. (iii) We empirically demonstrate the efficacy of the proposed strategies with
ResNet
for image classification on the CIFAR-10 and ImageNet datasets and MicroLlama on the C4 dataset respectively, in terms of communication efficiency, memory efficiency, and model generalization performance. To grasp a thorough understanding of the strategies, we also study the effect of different hyperparameters such as the number of local steps and the one which controls the probability of increasing batch sizes.

Related Work

Minibatch gradient methods.

Minibatch SGD

(
Dean et al., 2012
;
Robbins and Monro, 1951
;
Bottou, 2010
;
Dekel et al., 2012
)

(

)
, and is easily extendable with data parallelism

)

)
, nonconvex

)
, and nonsmooth
(
Davis et al., 2020
)
objectives, under different gradient variance assumptions. However, frequent gradient synchronization among workers leads to communication overheads, posing a significant challenge in distributed training.

Local gradient methods.

Local SGD
(
Stich, 2019a
;
Zinkevich et al., 2010
;

)
, a communication-efficient variant of minibatch SGD, reduces overheads from frequent synchronization in distributed training. Recent studies
(
Liu et al., 2024
;
Douillard et al., 2023
)
highlight its efficacy in pre-training large language models with extensive computational resources. Optimization of Local SGD for varied data settings has shown promising convergence rates
(
Stich, 2019a
;
Woodworth et al., 2020b
;
Woodworth et al., 2020a
;
Koloskova et al., 2020
;
Khaled et al., 2020
;

;
Wang and Joshi, 2021
)
. It includes extensions like asynchronous SGD
(
Mishchenko et al., 2022
;
Koloskova et al., 2022
;
Nguyen et al., 2022
;
Leconte et al., 2024
;
Islamov et al., 2024
)
and hierarchical SGD
(
Lin et al., 2020
;
Castiglia et al., 2021
;

)
. Federated learning
(
McMahan et al., 2017
;
Chen et al., 2021
;
Kairouz et al., 2021
;
Karimireddy et al., 2020
;
Reddi et al., 2021
)
, another model focusing on efficiency, addresses heterogeneity and partial participation. While applicable, we defer exploring federated learning’s theoretical and empirical aspects to future work.

Generalization gap in large-batch training.

Large-batch training not only faces communication overheads in minibatch
SGD
under distributed data parallelism but also shows poorer generalization despite better hardware efficiency. It has been successful in tasks like ImageNet classification and language model training
(
You et al., 2020
;
Goyal et al., 2017
;
Akiba et al., 2017
;
Shallue et al., 2019
;
Liu et al., 2019
;
Puri et al., 2018
)
. Addressing these,
Lin et al. (2020)
introduced
Post-local SGD
, a hybrid of minibatch and Local
SGD
, where early training involves frequent synchronization of local models, followed by Local
SGD
. In large-scale environments,
Ortiz et al. (2021)
noted that Post-local
SGD
does not consistently outperform Local
SGD
in generalization, and the transition from minibatch to Local
SGD
presents a trade-off between communication efficiency and generalization. Following these findings,
Gu et al. (2024)
;
Gu et al. (2023)
analyzed Local
SGD
’s generalization via an SDE approximation and proposed the Quadratic Synchronization Rule (QSR) for adjusting local gradient steps during training.

Adaptive batch size strategies.

In stochastic optimization, adaptive sampling methods
(
Byrd et al., 2012
;
Friedlander and Schmidt, 2012
;
Bollapragada et al., 2018
)
adjust batch sizes based on gradient noise, enhancing training processes for smooth finite-sum problems. These methods, further explored in deep learning
(
De et al., 2016
;
De et al., 2017
;
Lau et al., 2024
;
Ostroukhov et al., 2024
)
, have not been applied to data parallelism with distributed batches. The strategies discussed here draw from batch size scheduling, a key technique in pretraining LLMs, evidenced by its use in GPT-3
(
Brown et al., 2020
)
, Nemotron-4
(
Parmar et al., 2024
)
, OLMo 65B
(
Groeneveld et al., 2024
)
, and DeepSeek-V2
(
DeepSeek-AI, 2024
)
, which involve increasing batch sizes in set stages. Despite their benefits for efficiency and parallelization, these strategies are heuristic and have unclear impacts on training. This work aims to develop theoretically grounded, communication-efficient adaptive batch size strategies akin to adaptive sampling, viewed as stochastic variance-reduction methods
(
Johnson and Zhang, 2013
;
Xiao and Zhang, 2014
;
Gower et al., 2020
)
, focusing on scalable batch size increments.

Several studies have explored adaptive batch size strategies in deep learning, including Big Batch
SGD

(
De et al., 2016
;
De et al., 2017
)
, CABS
(
Balles et al., 2017
)
, AdaBatch
(
Devarakonda et al., 2017
)
, and SimiGrad
(
Qin et al., 2021
)
. Conversely, AdaScale
SGD

(
Johnson et al., 2020
)
modifies learning rates for large batches instead of batch sizes. These methods, however, are often not theoretically robust, lacking solid convergence proofs or are restricted to
SGD
analyses under strict conditions like convexity or the Polyak–Łojasiewicz condition. Their batch size adjustment strategies usually depend on heuristic rules, such as geometric growth or decay
(
Qin et al., 2021
)
, which can lead to unreliable outcomes due to the lack of theoretical guarantees. Crucially, these methods overlook the distributed data-parallel context, prevalent in practice, thus failing to address the needs of adaptive batch size strategies in large-scale distributed training.

Hyperparameter tuning for Local
SGD
.

Besides batch sizes, recent research has also focused on optimizing learning rate schedules for Local
SGD
, particularly in identical data settings as discussed in
Balles et al. (2024)
. This optimization is crucial in federated learning with heterogeneous data, where local objectives differ significantly in geometry and smoothness, necessitating locally adaptive learning rate schedules
(
Mukherjee et al., 2024
;
Kim et al., 2024
)
. Our adaptive local batch size strategies complement these efforts.

\mathbb{R}^{d}

3.1
Problem Formulation

We consider a distributed training setting with
M
workers, each with a possibly heterogeneous (i.e. non-i.i.d.) underlying true data distribution
\mathbb{P}_{m}
(
m\in\llbracket M\rrbracket
). The true data distribution
\mathbb{P}_{m}
is usually unknown, but can be approximated by its empirical distribution
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{m}=\frac{1}{n_{m}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{m}}\updelta_{\xi_{i}^{m}}
, where
\mathcal{D}_{m}\coloneqq\{\xi_{i}^{m}\}_{i\in\llbracket n_{m}\rrbracket}
is the set of
n_{m}
data samples of worker
m
and
\xi_{i}^{m}\in\mathcal{Z}_{m}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}
for
i\in\llbracket n_{m}\rrbracket
. The goal is to find an approximate minimizer of the global objective
F
which is the average of the local objectives
F_{m}
:

\minimize_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\ F(x)\coloneqq\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}F_{m}(x),

where
F_{m}(x)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{m}}[f_{m}(x;\xi)]=\frac{1}{n_{m}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{m}}f_{m}(x;\xi_{i}^{m})
is the local objective for worker
m
.

At each iteration
k\in\mathbb{N}
, since the number of samples of each worker
n_{m}
could be large, the per-worker gradient
\nabla F_{m}
cannot be computed. It can however be approximated by its minibatch counterpart

(\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d})\quad\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}^{m}}(x)\coloneqq\frac{1}{b_{k}^{m}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{k}^{m}}\nabla f_{m}(x;\xi_{i}^{m}),

(1)

where
\mathcal{B}_{k}^{m}
is the
local batch
of worker
m
at iteration
k
and
b_{k}^{m}\coloneqq|\mathcal{B}_{k}^{m}|
is the corresponding
local batch size
, assuming that
f_{m}(\cdot;\xi^{m})
is continuously differentiable for any
\xi^{m}\in\mathcal{Z}_{m}
.

3.2
Minibatch and Local Stochastic Gradient Methods

We present both the formulations of minibatch
SGD
under data parallelism and Local
SGD
.

Minibatch
SGD
.

Minibatch
SGD
under data parallelism is a fully synchronized stochastic gradient method. In particular, at each iteration
k
, after each worker
m
has computed its local batch gradient
\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}^{m}}(x_{k})
, the global batch gradient
\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}(x_{k})
is computed by averaging all local batch gradients by
\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}(x_{k})=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}^{m}}(x_{k})
with a global batch size
b_{k}\coloneqq|\mathcal{B}_{k}|=\sum_{m=1}^{M}b_{k}^{m}=Mb_{k}^{1}
, assuming that all local batch sizes are equal. This is followed by the global model update, performed via
x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\alpha_{k}\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}(x_{k})
, where
\alpha_{k}
is the learning rate of iteration
k
. The same mechanism also applies to other optimizers such as
Adam

(
Kingma and Ba, 2015
)
and
AdaGrad

(
Duchi et al., 2011
)
.

Local
SGD
.

As opposed to minibatch
SGD
, Local
SGD

(
Stich, 2019a
)
reduces the communication frequency. Communication for
model averaging
(through
all-reduce
operations) is performed every
H
local gradient steps, Local
SGD
takes the following updates: for
m\in\llbracket M\rrbracket)
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

x_{k+1}^{m}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left(x_{k}^{m}-\alpha_{k}\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}^{m}}(x_{k}^{m})\right)&\text{if }H|k+1,\\
x_{k}^{m}-\alpha_{k}\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}^{m}}(x_{k}^{m})&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(2)

We also consider the following alternative representation that explicitly separates the numbers of local gradient steps
H
and communication rounds
K
. In particular, for
M
parallel workers,
K
rounds of communication,
H
local gradient steps per round, Local
SGD
updates can also be expressed as: for
m\in\llbracket M\rrbracket)
and
k\in\llbracket 0,K-1\rrbracket
,

\begin{cases}x_{k,h+1}^{m}=x_{k,h}^{m}-\alpha_{k,h}\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k,h}^{m}}(x_{k,h}^{m})&\hskip-5.69054pt\text{for }h\in\llbracket 0,H-1\rrbracket,\\
x_{k+1,0}^{m}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}x_{k,H}^{m}.\end{cases}

(3)

4
Adaptive Local Batch Size Strategies for Local Gradient Methods

The proposed adaptive local batch size strategies are based on the adaptive sampling method
(
Byrd et al., 2012
)
originally developed for unconstrained stochastic optimization problems using minibatch
SGD
. We outline its mechanism and its extension to Local
SGD
below.

4.1
Adaptive Sampling Methods

Introduced in
Byrd et al. (2012)
, the adaptive sampling method for minibatch
SGD
was designed for a single worker (
M=1
) with the objective
F(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(x;\xi_{i})
over samples
\mathcal{D}=\{\xi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
. This method relies on a key stochastic optimization principle: when the sample size
n
is large, the batch gradient
\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}
approximates the full gradient
\nabla F
. If
f(\cdot;\xi)
is continuously differentiable and convex for any
\xi\in\mathcal{Z}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}
, then
-\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}(x_{k})
serves as a descent direction for
F
at
x_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, provided there exists
\eta\in\left(0,1\right)
such that

\delta_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}(x_{k})\coloneqq\|\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}(x_{k})-\nabla F(x_{k})\|\leqslant\eta\|\nabla F(x_{k})\|.

(4)

Note that
\nabla F(x_{k})
is not available in minibatch
SGD
, but
\delta_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}(x_{k})
can be approximated by
\widehat{\delta}_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}(x_{k})^{2}\coloneqq\frac{1}{b_{k}}\mathrm{Var}_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}(\nabla f(x_{k};\xi_{i}))\cdot\frac{n-b_{k}}{n-1}
, where, for any vector-valued function
h\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the batch variance is defined by

\mathrm{Var}_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}(h(x_{k};\xi_{i}))\coloneqq\frac{1}{b_{k}-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}\left\lVert h(x_{k};\xi_{i})-\mathbb{E}_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}[h(x_{k};\xi_{i})]\right\rVert^{2},

(5)

with
\mathbb{E}_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}[h(x_{k};\xi_{i})]\coloneqq\frac{1}{b_{k}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}h(x_{k};\xi_{i})
. Hence, as
n\to\infty
, condition (
4
) can be approximated as

\frac{1}{b_{k}}\mathrm{Var}_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}(\nabla f(x_{k};\xi_{i}))\leqslant\eta^{2}\|\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}(x_{k})\|^{2}.

(6)

In practice, for each iteration
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the
dynamic sample gradient algorithm

(
Byrd et al., 2012
)
performs the following minibatch
SGD
update:
x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\alpha_{k}\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}(x_{k})
with learning rate
\alpha_{k}>0
, and then checks the following
(approximate) norm test
condition (
6
), which can also be viewed as an approximation of the
exact variance norm test
in the stochastic setting:

\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\|\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}(x_{k})-\nabla F(x_{k})\|^{2}\right]\leqslant\eta^{2}\|\nabla F(x_{k})\|^{2},

(7)

i.e., condition (
4
) holds in expectation. Here we abbreviate the conditional expectation on
\mathcal{F}_{k}
(i.e., the filtration
\mathcal{F}_{k}\coloneqq\sigma(\{x_{0},\mathcal{B}_{0},\mathcal{B}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{B}_{k-1}\})
) by
\mathbb{E}_{k}[\cdot]\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[\cdot\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{k}]
.
If condition (
6
) is violated, the batch size of the next iteration
b_{k+1}
is increased, determined by:

b_{k+1}=\left\lceil\frac{\mathrm{Var}_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}(\nabla f(x_{k};\xi_{i}))}{\eta^{2}\|\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}(x_{k})\|^{2}}\right\rceil,

(8)

and the next batch
\mathcal{B}_{k+1}
is sampled with this larger batch size accordingly. Otherwise, the next batch size remains the same as the current one.
The value of the pre-specified constant
\eta
controls the probability of obtaining a descent direction, thereby affecting the probability of increasing the batch size as well as the magnitude of the next batch size.

Condition (
7
) is more relaxed than the typical assumption of uniformly bounded gradient variance for minibatch stochastic gradient methods: for any i.i.d. batch
\mathcal{B}\subset\llbracket n\rrbracket
and some positive constant
\sigma>0
,
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{B}}\left[\|\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}}(x)-\nabla F(x)\|^{2}\right]\leqslant\sigma^{2}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which is usually unverifiable in practice. The adaptive batch size schedule using the approximate norm test may cause rapid batch size increases, undermining its purpose. To moderate these schedules,
Bollapragada et al. (2018)
introduced the (augmented) inner product test, which manages the variance between the batch and full gradient’s inner product. Given the preference for large batch sizes for memory efficiency, controlled via
\\
eta
, we focus on the norm test and defer exploring local variants of the (augmented) inner product test to future work.

4.2
Extensions to Local Gradient Methods

We extend the above adaptive sampling methods beyond the single worker setting with data parallelism using multiple workers, more specifically for Local
SGD
.
We have to invoke a local variant of the norm test (
7
), called the
(exact variance) local norm test
, at every iteration
k\in\mathbb{N}
, using formulation (
2
): for some sequence of positive constants
(\eta_{m})_{m\in\llbracket M\rrbracket}\subset(0,1)
, for all
m\in\llbracket M\rrbracket
,

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{F}_{k}^{m}}\left[\|\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}^{m}}(x_{k}^{m})-\nabla F_{m}(x_{k}^{m})\|^{2}\right]\leqslant\eta_{m}^{2}\left\lVert\nabla F_{m}(x_{k}^{m})\right\rVert^{2},

(9)

where
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{F}_{k}^{m}}[\cdot]\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[\cdot\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{k}^{m}]
denotes the conditional expectation on
\mathcal{F}_{k}^{m}\coloneqq\sigma(\{x_{0},\mathcal{B}_{0}^{m},\ldots,\mathcal{B}_{k-1}^{m}\})
.
Again,
\nabla F_{m}
is in general unavailable since the sample size
n_{m}
could be large. The above local norm test is approximately enforced by the following (approximate)
local norm test
: for all
m\in\llbracket M\rrbracket
,

\frac{1}{b_{k}^{m}}\mathrm{Var}_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{k}^{m}}\left(\nabla f_{m}(x_{k}^{m};\xi_{i}^{m})\right)\leqslant\eta_{m}^{2}\left\lVert\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}^{m}}(x_{k}^{m})\right\rVert^{2}.

(10)

Should the condition (
10
) not be met, the subsequent local batch sizes are determined by the formula:

(\forall m\in\llbracket M\rrbracket)\quad b_{k+1}^{m}=\left\lceil\frac{\mathrm{Var}_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{k}^{m}}(\nabla f_{m}(x_{k}^{m};\xi_{i}^{m}))}{\eta_{m}^{2}\|\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}^{m}}(x_{k}^{m})\|^{2}}\right\rceil,

(11)

and the local batches
\mathcal{B}_{k+1}^{m}
are augmented accordingly.
In a practical implementation, for simplicity, we can set
\eta_{m}\equiv\eta
for all
m\in\llbracket M\rrbracket
and choose the next local batch sizes to be, for all
m\in\llbracket M\rrbracket
,

b_{k+1}^{m}\coloneqq\max_{m\in\llbracket M\rrbracket}\ \left\lceil\frac{\mathrm{Var}_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{k}^{m}}(\nabla f_{m}(x_{k}^{m};\xi_{i}^{m}))}{\eta^{2}\|\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}^{m}}(x_{k}^{m})\|^{2}}\right\rceil,

which is particularly useful when homogeneous workers and identical data on the workers are used, so that there would not be discrepancy of training time for the local steps due to different local batch sizes and undesirable stragglers before model averaging.

The proposed adaptive batch size strategies extend to local variants of minibatch stochastic gradient optimizers beyond
SGD

(
Robbins and Monro, 1951
)
, including momentum
SGD

(
Sutskever et al., 2013
)
,
AdaGrad

(
Duchi et al., 2011
)
,
Adam

(
Kingma and Ba, 2015
)
, and
AdamW

(
Loshchilov and Hutter, 2019
)
.

In a data-parallel setting, adaptive global batch size strategies can be developed using minibatch
SGD
, leading to increased communication overheads from aggregating local batch gradient variances among workers to compute the quantities in (
6
).

4.3
Practical Considerations

The (approximate) norm test (
6
) and its local variant (
10
) extend the norm test
(
Byrd et al., 2012
)
for Local
SGD
, but implementing them in deep learning libraries like PyTorch
(
Paszke et al., 2019
)
is complex, as only batch gradients
\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}^{m}}(x_{k}^{m})
, not per-sample gradients
\nabla f_{m}(x_{k}^{m};\xi_{i}^{m})
, are available in the backward training step. Therefore, we cannot compute the variance of per-sample gradients without additional subroutines (e.g., using
torch.func
). These methods do not scale well to large models, as they require a full model replica and per-sample gradients matching the model’s parameter size. Increasing batch sizes during training elevates memory demands, necessitating many gradient accumulation steps (a serial computation) to simulate large batches. We, therefore, suggest an alternative to approximate the variance term in the (local) norm test.

We can use local batch gradients as estimators instead of per-sample gradients. To illustrate, consider minibatch
SGD
with data parallelism among multiple workers where
\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}(x_{k})=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}^{m}}(x_{k})
and
x_{k}^{m}=x_{k}
apply for
m\in\llbracket M\rrbracket
. Note that

\mathrm{Var}_{m\in\llbracket M\rrbracket}\left(\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}^{m}}(x_{k})\right)\coloneqq\frac{1}{M-1}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left\lVert\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}^{m}}(x_{k})-\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}(x_{k})\right\rVert^{2}
.
Then, by the definition of
\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}^{m}}(x_{k})
, we can write

\mathrm{Var}_{m\in\llbracket M\rrbracket}\left(\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}^{m}}(x_{k})\right)=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{k}^{m}}\frac{\mathrm{Var}_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{k}^{m}}\left(\nabla f(x_{k};\xi_{i}^{m})\right)}{(b_{k}^{m})^{2}}=\frac{1}{b_{k}^{m}}\mathrm{Var}_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}\left(\nabla f(x_{k};\xi_{i})\right)=\frac{M}{b_{k}}\mathrm{Var}_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}\left(\nabla f(x_{k};\xi_{i})\right),

since
b_{k}^{m}=b_{k}/M
. Therefore, for the norm test (
8
), we can instead compute

\mathrm{Var}_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}\left(\nabla f(x_{k};\xi_{i})\right)=b_{k}/M\cdot\frac{1}{M-1}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left\lVert\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}^{m}}(x_{k})-\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}(x_{k})\right\rVert^{2}
.

We extend this workaround to Local
SGD
, focusing on the i.i.d. setting where
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{m}\equiv\widehat{\mathbb{P}}
and
f_{m}\equiv f
, thus resulting in
F_{m}=F
for all
m\in\llbracket M\rrbracket
. Varying
x_{k}^{m}
’s across workers
m\in\llbracket M\rrbracket
due to different
\mathcal{B}_{k}^{m}
’s prevent local gradients from being evaluated at the same iterates as in minibatch
SGD
during updates. Thus, we instead compute

\mathrm{Var}_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}\left(\nabla f(x_{k}^{m};\xi_{i}^{m})\right)=\frac{b_{k}}{M}\cdot\frac{1}{M-1}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left\lVert\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}^{m}}(x_{k}^{m})-\overline{g}_{k}\right\rVert^{2},

where
\overline{g}_{k}\coloneqq\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}^{m}}(x_{k}^{m})
.

The actual
(exact variance) norm test for local gradient methods
is given by, for all
m\in\llbracket M\rrbracket
,

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{F}_{k}^{m}}\left[\|\nabla F_{\mathcal{B}_{k}^{m}}(x_{k}^{m})-\nabla F(x_{k}^{m})\|^{2}\right]\leqslant\eta_{m}^{2}\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k}^{m})\right\rVert^{2},

(12)

In our implementation, the above actual
(exact variance) norm test for local gradient methods
is approximately enforced by the following (approximate)
norm test
for local gradient methods:

\frac{1}{b_{k}}\mathrm{Var}_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}\left(\nabla f(x_{k}^{m};\xi_{i}^{m})\right)\leqslant\eta^{2}\left\lVert\overline{g}_{k}\right\rVert^{2}.

(13)

Should the condition (
13
) not be met, the subsequent local batch sizes are determined by the formula:

(\forall m\in\llbracket M\rrbracket)\;b_{k+1}^{m}=\left\lceil\frac{\mathrm{Var}_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}\left(\nabla f(x_{k}^{m};\xi_{i}^{m})\right)}{M\eta^{2}\left\lVert\overline{g}_{k}\right\rVert^{2}}\right\rceil,

(14)

and the local batches
\mathcal{B}_{k+1}^{m}
are augmented accordingly. Note that the computation of
\overline{g}_{k}
requires additional all-reduce communication of
g_{k}^{m}
among the
M
workers. As a result, to avoid unnecessary additional communication rounds due to the test, we only perform the test every
H
local gradients steps, i.e., performing model parameter and gradient synchronization at the same time and frequency. The algorithm pseudocode is detailed in
Appendix

A
.

5
Convergence Analysis

Our analysis focuses on the i.i.d. setting where
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{m}\equiv\widehat{\mathbb{P}}
and
f_{m}\equiv f
, and hence
F_{m}=F
for all
m\in\llbracket M\rrbracket
. We set
\eta_{m}\equiv\eta\in(0,1)
for all
m\in\llbracket M\rrbracket
, typical in datacenter environments for distributed training
(
Dean et al., 2012
)
, where workers access the entire data set. The analysis of heterogeneous data settings is deferred to future work. Our convergence analysis, following
Stich and Karimireddy (2020)
, does not assume bounded gradient variance across
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Instead, our adaptive local batch size strategies ensure the
expected strong growth
condition
(
Khaled and Richtárik, 2023
;
Bottou et al., 2018
)
at each local iterate
x_{k}^{m}
for all workers
m\in\llbracket M\rrbracket
. This approach relaxes the standard assumptions of uniform boundedness and expected strong growth conditions
(
Vaswani et al., 2019
)
, which are challenging to verify except in the interpolation regime
(
Ma et al., 2018
)
.

We might need to invoke the following assumptions on the objective function
F
.

Assumption 1

(
L
-Lipschitz smoothness)
.

The function
F\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is continuously differentiable, bounded below by
F^{\star}\coloneqq\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}F(x)=F(x^{\star})\in\mathbb{R}
with
x^{\star}=\argmin_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}F(x)
, and is
L
-Lipschitz smooth for some
L>0
, i.e., for any
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\left\lVert\nabla F(x)-\nabla F(y)\right\rVert\leqslant L\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert
.

Assumption 2

(
\mu
-strong convexity)
.

The function
F
is
\mu
-strongly convex for some
\mu\geqslant 0
, i.e., for any
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
F(x)-F(y)+\frac{\mu}{2}\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert^{2}\leqslant\langle\nabla F(x),x-y\rangle
. If
\mu=0
, we say that
F
is convex.

We derive the following convergence results for the proposed adaptive batch size strategies as follows. Their proofs are deferred to
Appendix

B
.

Theorem 1

(Strongly convex;
\mu>0
)
.

Suppose that
Assumptions

1
and
2
hold with
\mu>0
.
Let
(x_{k}^{m})_{k\in\mathbb{N},m\in\llbracket M\rrbracket}
be the sequence of the iterates of Local
SGD
(
2
) with the (exact variance) local norm test (
9
) with
\eta_{m}\equiv\eta\in(0,1)
and a constant learning rate
\alpha_{k}\equiv\alpha
. Then, for some
\alpha\leqslant 1/(10L(HM+\eta^{2}))
, we have

\mathbb{E}[F(x_{\mathsf{out}})]-F^{\star}=\widetilde{}{O}\left(L(HM+\eta^{2})\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\mu K}{10L(HM+\eta^{2})}}\left\lVert x_{0}-x^{\star}\right\rVert^{2}\right),

(15)

where
x_{\mathsf{out}}\in\{x_{k}^{m}\}_{k\in\llbracket 0,K-1\rrbracket,m\in\llbracket M\rrbracket}
is chosen as
x_{k}^{m}
with probability proportional to
(1-\mu\alpha/2)^{-k}
uniformly on
m\in\llbracket M\rrbracket
and
\widetilde{{O}}
hides logarithmic factors.

Theorem 2

(Convex;
\mu=0
)
.

Suppose that
Assumptions

1
and
2
hold with
\mu=0
.
Let
(x_{k}^{m})_{k\in\mathbb{N},m\in\llbracket M\rrbracket}
be the sequence of the iterates of Local
SGD
(
2
) with the (exact variance) local norm test (
9
) with
\eta_{m}\equiv\eta\in(0,1)
and a constant learning rate
\alpha_{k}\equiv\alpha
. Then, for some
\alpha\leqslant 1/(10L(HM+\eta^{2}))
, we have

\mathbb{E}[F(x_{\mathsf{out}})]-F^{\star}={O}\left(\frac{L(HM+\eta^{2})}{K}\left\lVert x_{0}-x^{\star}\right\rVert^{2}\right),

(16)

where
x_{\mathsf{out}}
is chosen uniformly at random from
\{x_{k}^{m}\}_{k\in\llbracket 0,K-1\rrbracket,m\in\llbracket M\rrbracket}
.

Theorem 3

(Nonconvex)
.

Suppose that
Assumption

1
holds.
Let
(x_{k}^{m})_{k\in\mathbb{N},m\in\llbracket M\rrbracket}
be the sequence of the iterates of Local
SGD
(
2
) with the (exact variance) local norm test (
9
) with
\eta_{m}\equiv\eta\in(0,1)
and a constant learning rate
\alpha_{k}\equiv\alpha
. Then, for some
\alpha\leqslant 1/(10L(HM+\eta^{2}))
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{\mathsf{out}})\right\rVert^{2}={O}\left(\frac{L(HM+\eta^{2})}{K}(F(x_{0})-F^{\star})\right),

(17)

where
x_{\mathsf{out}}
is chosen uniformly at random from
\{x_{k}^{m}\}_{k\in\llbracket 0,K-1\rrbracket,m\in\llbracket M\rrbracket}
.

The theorems confirm that Local
SGD
, using the local norm test with exact variance, achieves linear convergence rates for both convex and nonconvex objectives. This scaling is proportional to the number of local gradient steps
H
and workers
M
, akin to Local
SGD
under constant batch sizes and bounded gradient variance
(
Stich and Karimireddy, 2020
)
.

Remark 1

(Choice of
\eta
)
.

Convergence rates depend quadratically on
\eta
, which controls the likelihood and size of increased local batch sizes. Choosing smaller
\eta
values accelerates convergence but causes quick batch size escalation, defeating the original purpose of adaptive batch sizing and widening the generalization gap.

7
Concluding Remarks

This work presents adaptive batch size strategies for local gradient methods under data parallelism to reduce communication overhead by increasing local batch sizes. To address possible efficiency issues in practical implementation, we propose an approximation of the norm test under the context of local gradient methods, by taking advantage of the availability of local batch gradients.
Illustrated with experiments in both image classification and language modeling task, our proposed approach is able to reduce the generalization gap of large-batch training while only adding a small amount of additional training time.
Future research will examine convergence in varied settings and federated learning
(
McMahan et al., 2017
;
Kairouz et al., 2021
)
, including personalized approaches
(
Hanzely et al., 2023
)
. Asynchronous
SGD

(
Mishchenko et al., 2022
;
Koloskova et al., 2022
;
Nguyen et al., 2022
;
Leconte et al., 2024
;
Islamov et al., 2024
)
and hierarchical
SGD

(
Lin et al., 2020
;
Castiglia et al., 2021
;
Wang et al., 2022
)
extensions are promising for enhancing communication efficiency and tackling worker heterogeneity.

\frac{1}{MW_{K}}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{k=0}^{K}w_{k}\mathbb{E}[F(x_{k}^{m})-F^{\star}]\leqslant\frac{10}{W_{K}}\sum_{k=0}^{K}\left(\frac{w_{k}}{\alpha_{k}}\left(1-\frac{\mu\alpha_{k}}{2}\right)\mathbb{E}\left\lVert\overline{x}_{k}-x^{\star}\right\rVert^{2}-\frac{w_{k}}{\alpha_{k}}\mathbb{E}\left\lVert\overline{x}_{k+1}-x^{\star}\right\rVert^{2}\right).

(B.16)

We observe that
1-\mu\alpha/2\geqslant 1-\mu/(20L(HM+\eta^{2}))\geqslant 1-1/(8HM)
, we choose
w_{k}=(1-\mu\alpha/2)^{-k-1}
which are
2H
-slow increasing. Then by
Lemma

B.6
, we obtain the desired result.
∎

and (

), and define

, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{MW_{K}}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{k=0}^{K}w_{k}\mathbb{E}\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k}^{m})\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{W_{K}}\sum_{k=0}^{K}w_{k}\left(\frac{u_{k}}{4\alpha_{k}}-\frac{u_{k+1}}{4\alpha_{k}}\right)+\frac{2L\alpha_{k}}{MW_{K}}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{k=0}^{K}w_{k}\mathbb{E}\left\lVert x_{k}^{m}-\overline{x}_{k}\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{W_{K}}\sum_{k=0}^{K}w_{k}\left(\frac{u_{k}}{4\alpha_{k}}-\frac{u_{k+1}}{4\alpha_{k}}\right)+\frac{2\alpha_{k}}{15LMW_{K}}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{k=0}^{K}w_{k}\mathbb{E}\left\lVert\nabla F(x_{k}^{m})\right\rVert^{2},

Observations.

Note that due to the stochastic nature of drawing data samples when computing gradients, the adaptive batch size strategies might give rise to different adaptive batch size schedules when different random seeds. We hence perform the training runs for three different random seeds to evaluate the robustness of the proposed strategies (see results of the other two seeds in
Figures

4
and
5
). We observe from
Table

4
that, as the number of local steps
H
grows, constant batch sizes might have more varied performance (in terms of standard deviation of best validation accuracy) than the proposed strategies.

Also notice that, due to the use of relatively large batch sizes, all the experiments make use of the technique of gradient accumulation, which essentially consists of serial operations using for-loops. Therefore, the benefit of using large batch sizes is not reflected in the total training time. If a larger number of GPUs or stronger GPUs with more memory are used, training with large batch sizes will consume substantially shorter time. Consequently, it is better to measure the computational efficiency of different training runs using the number of total training steps
(
Shallue et al., 2019
)
