A small-time approximation of Girsanov’s exponential and short-time transition density estimations.
Ramiro Scorolli

Abstract
The main result of this article regards a small time approximation for the Girsanov’s exponential. We prove that the latter is well described over short time intervals by the solution of a deterministic partial differential equation.The rate of convergence of the approximation is of order one in the length of the interval. As a possible application we show that our approximation can be used to obtain an estimation of the short-time transition density of a Langevin equation representing the dynamics of a Brownian particle moving under the influence of an external, non-linear force. Using this approach is equivalent to consider a random ordinary differential equation, where the dynamics of the particle is deterministic and given by the aforementioned force and the stochasticity enters through the initial condition. The result is in accordance with those obtained by discretization methods such as Euler-Maruyama, implicit Euler and by approximating the associated Fokker-Planck-Smoluchowski equation.



Key words and phrases: Stochastic differential equations, Wong-Zakai approximation, Wick product, Fokker-Planck equation, Girsanov Theorem

AMS 2000 classification: 60H10; 60H30; 60H05.

1
Introduction and statement of the main results.

In this article we will derive a short-time approximation for the solution of the stochastic Cauchy problem:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}dM_{t}=f(\alpha+B_{t})M_{t}dB_{t},\;t\in[0,T]\\
M_{0}=1.\end{cases}

(1.1)

Here:

•

f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a function satisfying assumption
1.1
(see below).

•

\{B_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
is a standard
\mathbb{R}
-valued Brownian motion defined on some complete filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\},P)
satisfying the usual conditions denotes an admissible filtration.

•

\alpha
is a
\mathbb{R}
-valued,
\mathcal{F}_{0}
-measurable random variable with law given by
\mu
.

An straightforward application of Itô formula
[
1
]
shows that the solution of (
1.1
) is given by the following stochastic process:

\displaystyle M_{t}:=\exp\bigg\{\int_{0}^{t}f(\alpha+B_{s})dB_{s}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}|f(\alpha+B_{s})|^{2}ds\bigg\},\;t\in[0,T];

(1.2)

the latter will be referred to as the
Girsanov’s exponential
.

All throughout this article we will work under the following assumption:

Assumption 1.1
.

•

f\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
;

•

f,f^{\prime},f^{\prime\prime}
are bounded;

•

there exists a positive constant
\epsilon>0
such that
f(x)>\epsilon,\forall x\in\mathbb{R}
.

If the latter holds Girsanov’s theorem (e.g.
[
2
]
[
1
]
) states that there exists a measure
Q
on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
such that

\displaystyle W:[0,T]\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle(t,\omega)\mapsto B_{t}(\omega)-\int_{0}^{t}f(\alpha+B_{s}(\omega))ds

is a
Q
-Brownian motion and it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{dQ}{dP}=M_{T},

i.e. the
Girsanov’s exponential
evaluated at the end-time
T
is the Radon-Nikodym derivative of
Q
with respect to
P

It is important to stress out the fact that assumption
1.1
will be needed in order to prove our main result and that Girsanov’s theorem hold under much weaker conditions regarding
f
, in particular it’s sufficient to assume that
f
is bounded.

Our approach shares has many similarities with those used in
[
3
]

[
4
]
and
[
5
]
although the presence of a random diffusion coefficient force us to introduce certain modifications.

The main novelty is that of connecting equation (
1.1
) with a deterministic partial differential equation that, just as the original equation, defines a Radon-Nikodym derivative. The latter will be done by exploiting the connection between the Wick product and Itô integration and the fact that under certain conditions the Wick product behaves like a differential operator.

Later on we will show that this approximation can be used in conjunction with Girsanov’s theorem in order to describe the short-time transition density of an associated diffusion process that describes the behavior of a Brownian particle moving under the influence of an external force.

We now present our main theorem;

Theorem 1.2
.

Let assumption
1.1
be in force, and for
T>0
let

\displaystyle[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}\ni(t,x)\mapsto u(t,x)

be the classical solution of the following deterministic Cauchy problem

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u=-\partial_{x}\left[f\left(\alpha+x\right)u\right]+\frac{f\left(\alpha+x\right)u\,x}{T},\;t\in]0,T]\\
u(0,x)=1.\end{cases}

(1.3)

Then there exists a measure
\mathcal{Q}
on
(\Omega,\mathcal{B})
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{T}(\omega):=u(T,B_{T}(\omega))=\frac{d\mathcal{Q}}{dP}

i.e.
\mathcal{M}_{T}
it is the Radon-Nikodym derivative of
\mathcal{Q}
with respect to
P
.

Furthermore for any
p\geq 1
it holds that

\displaystyle\big\|M_{T}-\mathcal{M}_{T}\big\|_{p}\leq CT.

(1.4)

where
\|\bullet\|_{p}
denotes the
\mathbb{L}^{p}(\Omega)
-norm and
C
is a suitable constant depending on
p
and the other parameters of the model.

The proof and the motivation explaining why we consider a deterministic Cauchy problem will be postponed to the last section.

2
Estimations of the short-time transition densities.

Let’s consider the following stochastic Cauchy problem

\displaystyle\begin{cases}dX_{t}=f(X_{t})dt+dB_{t},\;t\in[0,T]\\
X_{0}=\alpha,\end{cases}

(2.1)

where again
f
satisfies the assumption
1.1
and
\alpha
is some
\mathcal{F}_{0}
measurable random variable.
This could be seen as Langevin equation representing the dynamics of a Brownian particle influenced by an external force given by
f(\bullet)
and where the initial state of the system is random.

If we let
f(x)=x
and
\alpha\equiv a\in\mathbb{R}
in such a case the solution is given by the well-known Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process. The latter example is the only case, at least known to the author, in which the solution of an equation of the form of (
2.1
) can be obtained explicitly besides the trivial case where
f(\bullet)\equiv c\in\mathbb{R}
.

Remark 2.1
.

The fact that we are using a unit diffusion coefficient in (
2.1
) by no means entails a loss of generality in our discussion.
Consider a general diffusion given by:

\displaystyle dY_{t}=b(Y_{t})dt+\sigma(Y_{t})dB_{t},\;t\in[0,T].

Under mild assumptions on
b
and
\sigma
we are able to transform the latter into a diffusion with the same structure as (
2.1
). The latter is sometimes referred to as Lamperti’s Transformation ( e.g.
[
6
]
[
7
]
). Of course in our case, we would still have to check that the new drift coefficient satisfies the assumption
1.1
.

In most cases we are more interested in the law of
X_{t}
for any arbitrary
t\in[0,T]
rather than the trajectory itself since the former gives a much more complete summary of the dynamics into play. In the context of Markovian stochastic processes (such as Itô diffusions
[
1
]
) a complete description of the law of a given process can be obtained by means of the transition density and the Chapman-Kolmogorov equation
[
8
]

[
9
]
.

For this reason in the following we will set ourselves to find approximations of the short-time transition density of the diffusion given by (
2.1
).

In order to calculate the short-time transition density function
p(T,x|0,x^{\prime})
of an Itô diffusion one in general can proceed in two different ways.
The first is to work on the stochastic differential equation, i.e. to employ approximation methods such like the Euler-Maruyama scheme and the Backward-Euler scheme (e.g.
[
10
]
) on a single interval that would become arbitrarily small.
In both cases one is naturally led to consider transformations of
B_{T}
, and from there it’s easy to obtain a density function by inverting this transformation (see for instance
[
11
]
).

On the other hand one could work instead with the associated Fokker-Planck-Smoluchowski equation. In
[
12
]

[
9
]
the authors show how to obtain approximations of the transition probability by performing a discretization of the time interval. These estimations of the transition probabilities are then used to construct
path integrals solutions
of the Fokker-Planck equation.
It’s important to notice that in general the approximations of the transition probability obtained by the aforementioned methods are not actually densities, in particular they do not integrate to
1
.

We propose a third alternative that makes use of Girsanov’s theorem and the approximation obtained in the previous section.

For the sake of completeness the estimations obtained using the aforementioned approaches will be presented:

2.1
Backward-Euler scheme

For a single interval the approximation is given by

\displaystyle X_{T}=x^{\prime}+f(X_{T})T+B_{T}.

(2.2)

Notice that this describes a transformation
B_{T}\mapsto X_{T}
, whose inverse is given by

\displaystyle B_{T}=X_{T}-x^{\prime}+f(X_{T})T.

Then the density of the approximated solution is given by (e.g.
[
13
]
)

\displaystyle p(T,x|0,x^{\prime})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi T}}e^{-\frac{1}{2T}[x-x^{\prime}-f(x)T]^{2}}\times|1-f^{\prime}(x)T|.

Using the fact that
|1-f^{\prime}(x)T|\approx e^{-f^{\prime}(x)T}
we arrive to the following approximation of the transition density

\displaystyle p(T,x|0,x^{\prime})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi T}}e^{-\frac{1}{2T}[x-x^{\prime}-f(x)T]^{2}-f^{\prime}(x)T}.

(2.3)

2.2
Euler-Maruyama scheme

Using the standard Euler-Maruyama method the approximation is given by

\displaystyle X_{T}=x^{\prime}+f(x^{\prime})T+B_{T},

(2.4)

again this is a transformation
B_{T}\mapsto X_{T}
and the inverse is given by

\displaystyle X_{T}-x^{\prime}-f(x^{\prime})T=B_{T}.

Then the approximated density is given by

\displaystyle p(T,x|0,x^{\prime})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi T}}e^{-\frac{1}{2T}[x-x^{\prime}-f(x^{\prime})T]^{2}}.

(2.5)

2.3
Using approximated Girsanov’s exponential.

It’s well known that Girsanov’s theorem can be used to construct weak solutions of equations of the form of (
2.1
) (see for instance
[
2
]
or
[
1
]
). The basic idea is that the law of the random variable
X_{t}(\omega)
for some fixed time
t\in[0,T]
equals the law of
B_{t}(\omega)
under the measure
dQ=M_{T}dP
, where again
M_{T}
denotes the Girsanov’s exponential evaluated at the end time.

It’s then reasonable to think that if we replace the Girsanov’s exponential
M_{T}
with our approximation
\mathcal{M}_{T}
we could obtain an approximation (in law)
\mathcal{X}_{T}
of
X_{T}
as suggested by the following calculation

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}_{P}(g(X_{T}))-\mathbb{E}_{P}(g(\mathcal{X}_{T}))|

\displaystyle=|\mathbb{E}_{P}(g(\alpha+B_{T})M_{T})-\mathbb{E}_{P}(g(\alpha+B_{T})\mathcal{M}_{T})|

\displaystyle\leq\|g\|_{\infty}\|M_{t}-\mathcal{M}_{T}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq CT,

for any bounded Borel function
g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
.

Again for an arbitrary bounded function
g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[g(\mathcal{X}_{T})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[g(\alpha+B_{T})\cdot\mathcal{M}_{T}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi T}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(a+b)\left(\frac{f\left(\Lambda^{-1}\left(\Lambda(a+b)-T\right)\right)}{f(a+b)}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\dots\times e^{-\frac{1}{2T}\left[\Lambda^{-1}\left(\Lambda(a+b)-T\right)-a\right]^{2}}db\;\mu(da),

where we used the fact that
B_{T}\sim N(0,T)
,
\mu
denotes the law of the random variable
\alpha
, and we wrote
\mathcal{M}_{T}
in explicit form (the reader is referred to section
3
for the definition of
\Lambda
and
\Lambda^{-1}
).

This means that the approximated transition density function is given by

\displaystyle\tilde{p}(T,x|0,x^{\prime})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi T}}\left(\frac{f\left(\Lambda^{-1}\left(\Lambda(x)-T\right)\right)}{f(x)}\right)e^{-\frac{1}{2T}\left[\Lambda^{-1}\left(\Lambda(x)-T\right)-x^{\prime}\right]^{2}}.

(2.6)

By letting
y=\Lambda^{-1}\left(\Lambda(x)-T\right)
(see equations (
3.5
) and (
3.6
)) it’s straightforward to see that the latter is indeed a density with respect to the Lebesgue measure, i.e. is a non-negative function which integrates to
1
; this change of variables also entails the following.

Remark 2.2
.

The function
\tilde{p}
is the probability density function of the random variable
\mathcal{X}_{T}:=\Lambda^{-1}(\Lambda(x^{\prime}+B_{T})+T)
. Furthermore notice that
\{\mathcal{X}_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
is the solution of the ordinary differential equation:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{d\mathcal{X}_{t}}{dt}=f(\mathcal{X}_{t}),\;t\in]0,T]\\
\mathcal{X}_{0}=x^{\prime}+B_{T},\end{cases}

(2.7)

which implies

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{T}=x^{\prime}+B_{T}+\int_{0}^{T}f(\mathcal{X}_{s})ds.

(2.8)

Then our approach is equivalent to solving an ODE (that describes the dynamics of the particle in a deterministic scenario) where the stochasticity enters through the initial condition (such equations are sometimes referred to as “crypto-deterministic”
[
14
]
). It’s important to stress out the fact that in certain cases
2.7
can be solved under less restrictive conditions than assumption
1.1
, an straightforward example being
f(x)=x
.
This approach lies somewhere in the middle between solving the actual SDE and using a discrete approximation since we only discretize the “random component”( cf. (
2.8
), (
2.2
) and (
2.4
)).
The latter can also be seen if one notices that (
2.6
) is the solution of

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\tilde{p}(t,x)=-\partial_{x}[f(x)\tilde{p}(t,x)],\;t\in[0,T]\\
\tilde{p}(0,x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi T}}e^{\frac{-(x-x^{\prime})^{2}}{2T}},\end{cases}

(2.9)

evaluated at
t=T
, i.e. a Liouville equation (e.g.
[
15
]

[
16
]
) that describes the evolution of the probability density function of a process
\{X_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
that evolves in a deterministic setting (according to the force
f(\bullet)
) and the initial condition is a Gaussian random variable with mean
x^{\prime}
and variance
T
.

Equation (
2.9
) can be compared to the Fokker-Planck-Smoluchowski equation associated with (
2.1
):

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}p(t,x)=-\partial_{x}[f(x)p(t,x)]+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{xx}^{2}p(t,x),\;t\in[0,T]\\
p(0,x)=\delta(x-x^{\prime}).\end{cases}

(2.10)

where
p(t,x)
is a shorthand for
p(t,x|0,x^{\prime})
.

Notice that the first-order differential operator is the same in both equations, but the second-order operator associated with the heat-semigroup does not appear in (
2.9
). Still the “gaussianity” enters in the equation through the initial condition.

A solution for (
2.10
) cannot be obtain in general but a class of equivalent approximations is available (e.g.
[
12
]
).
Following
[
9
]
one starts by considering a small time interval
[0,T]
and hence:

\displaystyle p(T,x|0,x^{\prime})=[1+T\mathcal{L}_{FP}+\mathcal{O}(T^{2})]\delta(x-x^{\prime})

where
\mathcal{L}_{FP}\bullet=-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}f(x)\bullet+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}\bullet
.
Using the integral representation of the delta function and integration by parts (see
[
9
]
) one arrives to the following approximation

\displaystyle p(T,x|0,x^{\prime})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi T}}e^{-\frac{1}{2T}[x-\alpha-f(x)T]^{2}-f^{\prime}(x)T}.

(2.11)

It’s worth to notice that the latter in general does not integrate to
1
, i.e. it’s not an actual density.
Furthermore notice that this approximation is non-unique as stressed out in
[
9
]
.

We can treat (
2.9
) in an analogous way:

\displaystyle\tilde{p}(T,x|0,x^{\prime})=[1-T\mathcal{L}+\mathcal{O}(T^{2})]\tilde{p}(0,x),

(2.12)

where
\mathcal{L}\bullet=-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}f(x)\bullet
.

The latter yields

\displaystyle\tilde{p}(T,x|0,x^{\prime})=(2\pi T)^{-1/2}e^{-x^{2}/2T}[1-f^{\prime}(x)T+f(x)x+\mathcal{O}(T^{2})].

Now notice that in
[
12
]
the author argue that in order to obtain a “correct” solution one should have complete agreement up to and including terms of the order of
T
, same thing happens in
[
17
]
where the author adds and subtract arbitrary terms of order
\mathcal{O}(T^{2})
to the analogous of (
2.12
).

Using the same logic we can add

f(x)^{2}\frac{T^{2}}{2}
and neglect the
\mathcal{O}(T^{2})
inside the square brackets.
Then, within the aforementioned level of accuracy, we have:

\displaystyle\tilde{p}(T,x|0,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi T}}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2T}}\left[1-\left(f^{\prime}(x)+\frac{f(x)x}{T}+\frac{f(x)^{2}T}{2}\right)T\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi T}}e^{-\frac{1}{2T}[x-f(x)T]^{2}-f^{\prime}(x)T},

which coincides with (
2.11
).
These somewhat informal calculations allows us to see that the approximation (
2.6
) is part of the class of equivalent representations described by
[
12
]
.

Remark 2.3
.

The fact that (
2.11
) can be obtained by obtained by making further approximations to (

) lead us to believe that ours is more precise, but since the exact solution isn’t known we haven’t been able to prove this conjecture rigorously. Further research will be done on this particular.

We can use our short-time transition density and Chapman-Kolmogorov equation in order to obtain path integral solutions (e.g.

\displaystyle p(T,x|0,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}N\to\infty\\

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\dots\times e^{-\frac{1}{2\tau}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[\Lambda^{-1}\left(\Lambda(x_{i})-\tau\right)-x_{i-1}\right]^{2}}\Bigg)

Remark 2.5
.

If in (

) instead of replacing the Brownian motion appearing in the diffusion coefficient with

we replace it with

we obtain a different partial differential equation, namely:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\tilde{u}=-f(\alpha)\partial_{x}\tilde{u}+\frac{f(\alpha)\tilde{u}x}{T}\\

Solving this equation and proceeding as explained in

3
Proof of Theorem
1.2

We start this proof by explaining the motivations that led us to consider a deterministic Cauchy problem as an approximation for an SDE.

Remark 3.1
.

Consider the stochastic differential equation (SDE) that defines the Girsanov’s exponential, i.e.

\displaystyle\begin{cases}dM_{t}=f(\alpha+B_{t})M_{t}dB_{t},\;t\in[0,T]\\
M_{0}=1.\end{cases}

Using the connection the Wick product and the Itô integration one could formally rewrite equation (
1.1
) as

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{dM_{t}}{dt}=f(B_{t})M_{t}\diamond\frac{dB_{t}}{dt},\\
X_{0}=1,\end{cases}

(3.1)

where
\diamond
denotes the Wick product (e.g.
[
19
]
) and
\frac{dB_{t}}{dt}
denotes the (distributional) time-derivative of the Brownian motion (see for instance
[
20
]
[
21
]
[
22
]
for a complete account on the connection between Wick product, white noise and Itô integration).

A natural approximation for
\frac{dB_{t}}{dt}
is given by
\frac{B_{T}}{T}
which amounts to consider the Wong-Zakai approximation
[
23
]
with the coarser partition of the interval
[0,T]
.
Furthermore, following the same logic (in the spirit of the Backward-Euler scheme) we will replace the Brownian motion at time
t
with the Brownian motion evaluated at the end of the interval, in remark
2.5
we will discuss what would happens if instead we replace
B_{t}
with
B_{0}=0
(in the spirit of the Euler-Mayurama method).

With this in hand we can introduce the following “approximating” equation

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{d\mathcal{M}_{t}}{dt}=\left[f\left(\alpha+B_{T}\right)\cdot\mathcal{M}_{t}\right]\diamond\left(\frac{B_{T}}{T}\right),t\in]0,T]\\
\mathcal{M}_{0}=1.\end{cases}

(3.2)

Under certain conditions the Wick product between a random variable
F
and an element of the first Wiener chaos
I(g)
is given by (e.g.
[
20
]
,
[
21
]
)

\displaystyle F\diamond I(g)=F\cdot I(g)-D_{g}X

(3.3)

where
D_{g}
stands for the directional Malliavin derivative in the direction of the deterministic function
g(\bullet)
and
I(\bullet)
denotes the Wiener-Itô integral. Since
B_{T}=\int_{0}^{T}dB_{s}=I(1_{[0,T]})
we can formally write

\displaystyle\frac{d{\mathcal{M}_{t}}}{dt}=\frac{1}{T}\bigg\{\left[f\left(\alpha+B_{T}\right)\mathcal{M}_{t}\right]\cdot B_{T}-\bigg\langle D\left[f\left(\alpha+B_{T}\right)\mathcal{M}_{t}\right],1_{]0,T]}\bigg\rangle\bigg\}.

If we now set ourselves to find a solution of the form:

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(t,\omega)=u(t,B_{T}(\omega)),\;t\in]0,T]

for some continuous function
u:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
to be determined, we can use the chain rule for Malliavin derivative to show that
u
must solve

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u=-\partial_{x}\left[f\left(\alpha+x\right)u\right]+\frac{f\left(\alpha+x\right)u\,x}{T},\;(t,x)\in]0,T]\times\mathbb{R}\\
u(0,x)=1.\end{cases}

(3.4)

It is important to stress the fact that this calculations are just formal and simply provide a candidate for the approximation; if we additionally assume that the random variable
F
belongs to the Malliavin-Sobolev space
\mathbb{D}^{1,p},p>1
(e.g
[
24
]
) then all the calculations are justified (see for instance
[
20
]

[
5
]
).

We are now ready to start the proof of the theorem;
all throughout this proof we will denote with
C
a constant whose value may change at each appearance.
The starting point will be the following deterministic Cauchy problem (
3.4
)
which is related with (
1.1
) by the heuristic considerations discussed above.
The transformation
v(t,x):=f(\alpha+x)u(t,x)e^{\frac{-x^{2}}{2T}}
and a simple application of the chain rule allows us to see that the following must hold

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}v=-f\left(\alpha+x\right)\partial_{x}v,\;t\in]0,T]\\
v(0,x)=f(\alpha+x)e^{\frac{-x^{2}}{2T}}=:\varphi(x).\end{cases}

This equation can be solved by a simple application of the method of characteristics which yields

\displaystyle v(t,x)=\varphi\left(\Lambda^{-1}\left(\Lambda(x)-t\right)\right),

where
\Lambda:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is given by

\displaystyle\Lambda(x):=\int\frac{1}{f(\alpha+x)}dx

(3.5)

and
\Lambda^{-1}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies

\displaystyle\Lambda^{-1}(\Lambda(x))=x;

(3.6)

notice that all this functions are well defined under assumption
1.1
.

Inverting the transformation shows that the solution to (
3.4
) is given by

\displaystyle u(t,x)

as desired.

On the other hand using the fact that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[u(T,B_{T})]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi T}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\frac{f\left(\Lambda^{-1}\left(\Lambda(a+b)-T\right)\right)}{f(a+b)}\right)

For the sake of simplicity and without loss of generality from now on we will assume that

We start by noticing that we can write

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{T}

\displaystyle=\exp\Bigg\{\left(\frac{\ln\left[f\left(\Lambda^{-1}\left(\Lambda(B_{T})-T\right)\right)\right]-\ln\left[f(B_{T})\right]}{T}\right)T

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt-\frac{1}{2T}\left[\left(\Lambda^{-1}\left(\Lambda(B_{T})-T\right)\right)^{2}-B_{T}^{2}\right]\Bigg\},

\displaystyle\big\|\mathcal{M}_{T}-M_{T}\big\|_{p}

\displaystyle=\Bigg\|\exp\bigg\{\left(\frac{\ln\left[f\left(\Lambda^{-1}\left(\Lambda(B_{T})-T\right)\right)\right]-\ln\left[f(B_{T})\right]}{T}\right)T

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt-\frac{1}{2T}\left[\left(\Lambda^{-1}\left(\Lambda(B_{T})-T\right)\right)^{2}-B_{T}^{2}\right]\bigg\}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt-\exp\bigg\{\int_{0}^{T}f(B_{s})dB_{s}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}|f(B_{s})|^{2}ds\bigg\}\Bigg\|_{p}.

\displaystyle\big\|\mathcal{M}_{T}-M_{T}\big\|_{p}

Remark 3.2
.

Proof.

We will start by showing the latter for the Girsanov’s exponential.

Notice that

\displaystyle|M_{T}|^{q}=\exp\bigg\{q\int_{0}^{T}f(B_{s})dB_{s}-\frac{q}{2}\int_{0}^{T}|f(B_{s})|^{2}ds\bigg\},

at this point we can define

and then the latter reads

\displaystyle|M_{T}|^{q}=\exp\bigg\{\int_{0}^{T}g(B_{s})dB_{s}-\frac{1}{2q}\int_{0}^{T}|g(B_{s})|^{2}ds\bigg\},

or which is equivalent

\displaystyle|M_{T}|^{q}

\displaystyle=\exp\bigg\{\int_{0}^{T}g(B_{s})dB_{s}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}|g(B_{s})|^{2}ds\bigg\}

\displaystyle\times\exp\bigg\{\frac{q-1}{2q}\int_{0}^{T}|g(B_{s})|^{2}ds\bigg\}.

At this point take the expectation of the expression above and notice that since

is a bounded function,

is bounded by some constant that remains unchanged as

becomes arbitrarily small; the boundedness also implies that the Novikov’s condition

holds and hence the first exponential has mean equal to

, this proves the result for

We now show the boundedness of

\displaystyle\big\|\mathcal{M}_{T}\big\|_{q}

\displaystyle=\Bigg\|\left(\frac{f\left(\Lambda^{-1}\left(\Lambda(B_{T})-T\right)\right)}{f(B_{T})}\right)e^{-\frac{1}{2T}\left[\left(\Lambda^{-1}\left(\Lambda(B_{T})-T\right)\right)^{2}-B_{T}^{2}\right]}\Bigg\|_{q}.

implies that the ratio is bounded by a constant that does not depend on

\displaystyle\big\|\mathcal{M}_{T}\big\|_{q}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{C}{\sqrt{2\pi T}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-\frac{q}{2T}\left[\left(\Lambda^{-1}\left(\Lambda(x)-T\right)\right)^{2}\right]}e^{\frac{q-1}{2T}x^{2}}dx\right)^{\frac{1}{q}},

where we used the fact that

In order to evaluate the integral above we take a Taylor expansion of the function
\Lambda^{-1}(\bullet)
around

which yields

\displaystyle\Lambda^{-1}(x_{0}-T)

\displaystyle=x-f(x)T+\frac{1}{2}f(\xi)f^{\prime}(\xi)T^{2},

for some

. Then plugging (

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{C}{\sqrt{2\pi T}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-\frac{q}{2T}\left[x-f(x)T+\frac{1}{2}f(\xi(x))f^{\prime}(\xi(x))T^{2}\right]^{2}}e^{\frac{q-1}{2T}x^{2}}dx\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp\bigg\{\frac{q}{2}\bigg[2xf(x)-xf(\xi(x))f^{\prime}(\xi(x))T-f(x)^{2}T

\displaystyle+f(x)f(\xi(x))f^{\prime}(\xi(x))T^{2}-\frac{1}{4}f(\xi(x))^{2}f^{\prime}(\xi(x))^{2}T^{3}\bigg]\bigg\}e^{\frac{-x^{2}}{2T}}dx\Bigg)^{\frac{1}{q}}.

4
Discussion

The Girsanov’s theorem is a powerful and quite useful tool in stochastic analysis. In this article we showed that the Girsanov’s exponential can be approximated over short time intervals by the solution of a deterministic partial differential equation. This approximation can be used in order to estimate the transition density of the solution of a Langevin equation for small time intervals.

Even though our estimations involve inverting arbitrary functions which in general doesn’t lead to closed forms, our approach could be used in conjunction with interpolation methods in order to obtain numerical simulations. Further research will be done in this direction.
Finally, the extension of this result to the multi-dimensional case is a work in progress.
