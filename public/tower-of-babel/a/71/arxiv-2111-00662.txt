An Index Formula For Cauchy-Riemann Operators on Surfaces with boundary punctures
Dylan Cant

Abstract
. We give a self-contained proof of a formula computing the Fredholm index for asymptotically non-degenerate Cauchy-Riemann operators on surfaces with boundary punctures using the method of large anti-linear deformations. This method for computing the index was introduced in the case of closed surfaces by [ Tau96 ] and generalized to the case with interior punctures by [ Ger18 ] , as explained in [ Wen20 ] . One novel feature arising from our proof is that the Euler characteristic term in the index formula involves a non-standard weighted count of boundary zeros. We hope that this formulation of the index formula will be useful to other researchers.

1.
Introduction

The main goal of this paper is to prove an index formula for
asymptotically non-degenerate Cauchy-Riemann operators
on surfaces with boundary punctures. We generalize the result stated in
[
Sch95
, Theorem 3.3.11]
(see also
[
Ger18
, Theorem 3.1.2]
and
[
Wen20
, Theorem 5.4]
).

To actually prove the index formula we adopt the technique introduced in
[
Tau96
, Section 7]
(subsequently generalized by
[
Ger18
, Chapter 3]
), and deform our Cauchy-Riemann operator
D
by an anti-linear lower order term
\sigma B
. As explained in
[
Tau96
]
,
[
Ger18
]
, and
[
Wen20
]
, as
\sigma\to\infty
the kernel of
D+\sigma B
concentrates near the positive zeros of
B
and the cokernel of
D+\sigma B
is represented by sections supported near the negative zeros of
B
. With some further analysis, one concludes that the signed count of zeros of
B
equals the index of
D+\sigma B
.

Our argument is complicated by the boundary
\partial\Sigma
. The most apparent difference is that the anti-linear perturbation
B
can have zeros on the boundary, and, as we will show, the boundary zeros split into
four
cases, two of which contribute
0
to the index, and the other two contribute
+1
and
-1
. See Figure
1
and
10
. This phenomenon leads to the “Euler characteristic” term in the index formula depending on the signs of punctures – this is a novel phenomenon when compared with the
\partial\Sigma=\emptyset
case. See Section
6.4
for the computation which leads to some of the boundary zeros contributing
0
.

Remark 1
.

If the asymptotics of
D
do not match the asymptotics of
D+\sigma B
for
\sigma
large, then the Fredholm index will likely change during the deformation
\sigma\to\infty
. There are two approaches to deal with this: one way is to try to find
B
so that the asymptotics of
D
match the asymptotics
D+\sigma B
for all
\sigma\in[0,\infty)
– this is the approach taken in
[
Ger18
, Chapter 3]
and
[
Wen20
, Section 5.8]
. The other approach is to pick
B
without regard to
D
, and then analyze the change in index as an “index gluing” problem. This is the approach considered in this paper, and it leads to a natural definition of the Conley-Zehnder indices as Fredholm indices of certain operators. The necessary analytic ingredient to make this work is the
linear kernel gluing operation
(see Section
5
). See
[
Sch95
, Section 3.2]
and
[
FH93
]
for similar gluing problems.

Remark 2
.

There is other work which proves index formulas for Cauchy-Riemann operators on surfaces with boundary punctures. See, for instance,
[
CEJ10
, Appendix A]
. Our work differs from theirs in how we present the index formula (e.g. they do not define Conley-Zehnder indices for Reeb chords), and how we prove the result.

3.3.
Cauchy-Riemann operators with non-degenerate asymptotics

A first order partial differential operator
D:\Gamma(E)\to\Gamma(\Lambda^{0,1}\otimes E)
is called a
Cauchy-Riemann operator
if

D(f\otimes\xi)=\mathrm{d}f\otimes\xi+(\mathrm{d}f\cdot j)\otimes J\xi+f\cdot D\xi

for all real-valued functions
f
and smooth sections
\xi
. Here
j
is the complex structure on
\Sigma
and
J
is the fiber-wise complex structure on
E
.

We begin with a discussion of the local coordinate representations of Cauchy-Riemann operators. If
z=s+it
is a holomorphic coordinate, then
\mathrm{d}s-i\mathrm{d}t
trivializes
\Lambda^{0,1}
. Suppose that
\tau:E\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
is a complex linear trivialization over
E
. Then
\tau^{-1}(e_{k})=X_{k}
and
\tau^{-1}(ie_{k})=JX_{k}
define a local frame for
E
.

Write
\xi=\tau^{-1}(u)=\sum_{k}u_{k}X_{k}=\sum_{k}a_{k}X_{k}+b_{k}JX_{k}
, where
u=a+ib
is a
\mathbb{C}^{n}
-valued function. We obtain:

\displaystyle D(\sum a_{k}X_{k})

\displaystyle=\sum\mathrm{d}a_{k}\otimes X_{k}+(\mathrm{d}a_{k}\cdot j)\otimes JX_{k}+a_{k}\cdot DX_{k}

\displaystyle D(\sum b_{k}JX_{k})

\displaystyle=\sum\mathrm{d}b_{k}\otimes JX_{k}-(\mathrm{d}b_{k}\cdot j)\otimes X_{k}+b_{k}\cdot D(JX_{k}).

Hence, using
c\otimes JX_{k}=ic\otimes X_{k}
(for sections of
\Lambda^{0,1}\otimes E
) we obtain:

D(\tau^{-1}(u))=\sum(\mathrm{d}u_{k}+i\cdot\mathrm{d}u_{k}\cdot j)\otimes X_{k}+a_{k}\cdot DX_{k}+b_{k}\cdot D(JX_{k}).

It is straightforward to compute
\mathrm{d}u_{k}+i\cdot\mathrm{d}u_{k}\cdot j=(\partial_{s}u_{k}+i\partial_{t}u_{k})(\mathrm{d}s-i\mathrm{d}t)
. In particular, we have

D(\tau^{-1}(u))=\sum_{k=1}^{n}(\partial_{s}u_{k}+i\partial_{t}u_{k})\cdot(\mathrm{d}s-i\mathrm{d}t)\otimes X_{k}.

We note that
(\mathrm{d}s-i\mathrm{d}t)\otimes X_{k}
is a local (complex) frame for
\Lambda^{0,1}\otimes E
. We denote the inverse trivialization by
\tau_{1}
. If conjugate
D
by the complex trivializations
\tau^{-1}
and
\tau_{1}^{-1}:w\mapsto w(\mathrm{d}s-i\mathrm{d}t)\otimes X
, we conclude that

(1)

\tau_{1}\circ D\circ\tau^{-1}=:D_{\tau}(u)=\partial_{s}u+i\partial_{t}u+S(s,t)u,

where
S(s,t)
is some smooth family of real linear matrices. Note that
D_{\tau}
depends both on the holomorphic coordinates used on the base and on the trivialization
\tau
.

Now let
\tau
be an asymptotic trivialization for
E
. Using the holomorphic coordinate in the ends
C
, we can compute the coordinate representation for
D
using
\tau
. We say that
D
has
non-degenerate asymptotics
provided that (
1
) satisfies

(2)

\sup_{t}\text{$\left|\partial_{s}^{k}\partial_{t}^{\ell}(S(s,t)-S_{\infty}(t))\right|\to 0$ as $\left|s\right|\to\infty$,}

for all
k,\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, for some smooth family of
symmetric
matrices
S_{\infty}
, and the corresponding
asymptotic operator

(3)

A_{\tau}=-i\partial_{t}-S_{\infty}(t):\left\{\begin{aligned} C^{\infty}([0,1],\mathbb{C}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n})\to C^{\infty}([0,1],\mathbb{C}^{n})\\
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{C}^{n})\to C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{C}^{n})\end{aligned}\right.

is injective. In this case we say that
A_{\tau}
is
non-degenerate
. The two cases in (
3
) are whether the cylindrical end corresponds to a boundary or interior puncture. The notation
C^{\infty}([0,1],\mathbb{C}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n})
means “smooth
\mathbb{C}^{n}
-valued functions which are
\mathbb{R}^{n}
-valued at
t=0,1
.”

Since the transition function between two asymptotic trivializations is a smooth
s
-independent family of unitary matrices, the condition that
D
has non-degenerate asymptotics is independent of the chosen
\tau
. Indeed, if
\tau_{1}\tau_{2}^{-1}=\Omega(t)
, then

A_{\tau_{2}}=\Omega(t)^{-1}A_{\tau_{1}}\Omega(t)=-i\partial_{t}-i\Omega(t)^{-1}\Omega^{\prime}(t)-\Omega(t)^{-1}S_{\infty}(t)\Omega(t).

A straightforward computation shows that
i\Omega(t)^{-1}\Omega^{\prime}(t)-\Omega(t)^{-1}S_{\infty}(t)\Omega(t)
is still symmetric.

3.4.
Some facts about non-degenerate asymptotic operators

In this section we fix a non-degenerate asymptotic operator
A=-i\partial_{t}-S(t)
on
[0,1]
. The analogous results with
[0,1]
replaced by
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
are left to the reader.

Proposition 8
.

The map
A:C^{\infty}([0,1],\mathbb{C}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n})\to C^{\infty}([0,1],\mathbb{C}^{n})
extends to a self-adjoint isomorphism

A:W^{1,2}([0,1],\mathbb{C}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n})\to L^{2}([0,1],\mathbb{C}^{n}).

By self-adjoint we mean that
\left\langle Av,w\right\rangle=\left\langle v,Aw\right\rangle
for all
v,w\in W^{1,2}([0,1],\mathbb{C}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

See
[
Wen20
, Corollary 3.14]
for an alternative approach, yielding a proof in the
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
case.

Proof
.

It is clear that
A
extends to a bounded linear operator between the advertised Banach spaces. Since
C^{\infty}([0,1],\mathbb{C}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n})
is dense in
W^{1,2}([0,1],\mathbb{C}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n})
it suffices to prove the self-adjointness for smooth functions
u,v
. This follows from a straightforward integration-by-parts computation, using the fact that the matrix
S(t)
is symmetric, and
i
is anti-symmetric. We leave this computation to the reader. Note that it is crucial that both
u,v
take boundary values in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, otherwise the integration by parts will fail.

It suffices to prove that
A
is a bijection, since continuous bijections between Banach spaces are isomorphisms. Observe that any element in the kernel of
A
must be smooth (by 1-dimensional elliptic regularity). Since we assume that
u\mapsto Au
is injective for smooth
u
, we conclude that
A
is injective on
W^{1,2}
.

Now we will prove that
A
is surjective. Fix a smooth
\eta
, and we attempt to solve
A(\xi)=\eta
for a smooth
\xi
:

(4)

\displaystyle i\frac{\partial\xi}{\partial t}+S(t)\xi=-\eta(t)\iff\frac{\partial\xi}{\partial t}-iS(t)\xi=i\eta(t).

\displaystyle\iff\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\exp(\Sigma(t))\xi(t))=\exp(\Sigma(t))i\eta(t),

\displaystyle\iff\xi(t)=\exp(-\Sigma(t))\xi(0)+\exp(-\Sigma(t))\int_{0}^{t}\exp(\Sigma(t^{\prime}))i\eta(t^{\prime})\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}.

where
\Sigma^{\prime}(t)=-iS(t)
and
\Sigma(0)=0
. This shows that we can solve
A(\xi)=\eta
for many different choices of
\xi
, namely there is an
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
dimensional family of solutions corresponding to the choice of
\xi(0)
. We claim that (exactly) one of these solutions will satisfy
\xi(0),\xi(1)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. To see why, consider the affine map:

F:\xi(0)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mapsto\exp(-\Sigma(1))\xi(0)+\exp(-\Sigma(1))\int_{0}^{1}\exp(\Sigma(t^{\prime}))i\eta(t^{\prime})\mathrm{}t^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}.

This map parameterizes an
n
-dimensional affine subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
. Note that the associated linear subspace
\exp(-\Sigma(1))\mathbb{R}^{n}
is transverse to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(otherwise we could find a vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
so
\exp(-\Sigma(1))v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and the above computation with
\eta=0
would imply
\xi(t)=\exp(-\Sigma(t))v
lies in the kernel of
A
).

Therefore
F(\mathbb{R}^{n})
intersects
\mathbb{R}^{n}
in a unique point
F(\xi(0))=\xi(1)
. Thus (
4
) with this special
\xi(0)
shows that
A
is surjective onto the smooth elements
\eta
.

To show that
A
is surjective in general, it suffices to prove that the image of
A
is closed. This follows from the estimate

\left\lVert\xi\right\rVert_{W^{1,2}}\leq C(\left\lVert A(\xi)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}+\left\lVert\xi\right\rVert_{L^{2}})

and the fact that
W^{1,2}\to L^{2}
is a compact inclusion. This completes the proof of the lemma.

Proposition 9
.

There exists an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}([0,1],\mathbb{R}^{n})
consisting of (smooth) eigenvectors of
A
. The union of all the eigenvalues is a discrete set
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}
disjoint from
0
.

Proof
.

The key observation is that the following composition is a compact self-adjoint operator (called the
resolvent
of
A
):

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{L^{2}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{W^{1,2}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{L^{2}}.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A^{-1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\subset}

This is because
W^{1,2}\subset L^{2}
is a compact inclusion (Proof: if
\partial_{t}f_{n}
is bounded in
L^{2}
then
\left|f_{n}(x)-f_{n}(x+t)\right|\leq ct^{1/2}
, and hence
f_{n}
is equicontinuous). Self-adjoint compact operators have orthonormal eigenbases whose spectrum accumulates only at
0
(see
[
Sim15
, Theorem 3.2.3]
). The desired result follows.

3.5.
Formal adjoints

The purpose of this section is to define the
formal adjoint
of a Cauchy-Riemann operator. Formal adjoints will play an important role in establishing the Fredholm property. A good reference in the case when
\partial\Sigma=\emptyset
is
[
Wen20
, Section 4.7]
.

Let
D
be a Cauchy-Riemann operator for the data
(\Sigma,\partial\Sigma,\Gamma_{\pm},C,E,F,[\tau])
as explained above. Fix a
j
-invariant Riemannian metric
g
on
T\Sigma
which is the Euclidean metric in the cylindrical ends. The corresponding volume form is given by
\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}=g(j-,-)
.

Pick a Hermitian structure on
(E,F)
which agrees with the asymptotically Hermitian structure in the cylindrical ends
C
. This means that
E
is equipped with a fiber-wise metric
g
which is
J
-invariant and
F
is
g
-orthogonal to
JF
. In other words,
F
is Lagrangian for the symplectic form
g(J-,-)
.

We define
\mathbb{C}
-valued Hermitian metrics on
E
(and
T\dot{\Sigma}
) by the formulas:

\mu(X,Y)=g(X,Y)+ig(JX,Y).

By our conventions,
\mu(X,JY)=i\mu(X,Y)
and
\mu(JX,Y)=-i\mu(X,Y)
.

The bundle isomorphism
Y\mapsto\mu(-,Y)
identifies
T\dot{\Sigma}
with
\Lambda^{0,1}
. We use this to push forward a Hermitian metric onto
\Lambda^{0,1}
.

Given two complex vector bundles
E_{1},E_{2}
with Hermitian metrics
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}
we can endow a Hermitian metric
\mu_{1}\otimes\mu_{2}
on
E_{1}\otimes E_{2}
by the formula:

\mu_{1}\otimes\mu_{2}(X_{1}\otimes X_{2},Y_{1}\otimes Y_{2})=\mu_{1}(X_{1},Y_{1})\mu_{2}(X_{2},Y_{2}).

Via this construction, the bundles
E
and
\Lambda^{0,1}\otimes E
are both equipped with Hermitian metrics.

With these preliminaries out of the way, we say that
D^{*}:\Gamma(\Lambda^{0,1}\otimes E)\to\Gamma(E)
is a
formal adjoint
of
D
if

(5)

\mathrm{Re}\int_{\dot{\Sigma}}\mu(D(\xi),\eta)\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}=\mathrm{Re}\int_{\dot{\Sigma}}\mu(\xi,D^{*}(\eta))\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol},

for all
\xi\in\Gamma_{0}(E,F)
and
\eta\in\Gamma_{0}(\Lambda^{0,1}\otimes E,F^{*})
. Here
F^{*}\subset\Lambda^{0,1}\otimes E
is the totally-real sub-bundle of maps which map
T\partial\Sigma
into
F
, and
\Gamma_{0}(E,F)
is the set of smooth compactly supported sections of
E
which take boundary values in
F
.

Since
\mathrm{Re}(\mu)
is a Riemannian metric, formal adjoints are necessarily unique. We will derive a formula for the formal adjoint in local trivializations below. By patching together the local descriptions we deduce that formal adjoints always exist.

Let
z=s+it
be a holomorphic coordinate and
\tau:E\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
a local unitary trivialization of
E
defined on the domain of
z
. Let
X_{1},\cdots,X_{n}
be the unitary frame of
E
induced by
\tau
. Recall that we have an associated trivialization
\tau_{1}:\Lambda^{0,1}\otimes E\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
which satisfies

\tau_{1}^{-1}(w)=\sum_{k}w_{k}(\mathrm{d}s-i\mathrm{d}t)\otimes X_{k}.

The equation (
1
) shows
\tau_{1}\circ D\circ\tau^{-1}(u)=\partial_{s}u+i\partial_{t}u+S(s,t)u
.

To incorporate the boundary conditions, we require that
z
takes values in
\mathbb{R}\times[0,1]
,
z(\partial\dot{\Sigma})\subset\mathbb{R}\times\left\{0,1\right\}
, and
\tau
identifies
F
with
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We do not require that
z
is surjective, e.g., it could take values in
D(1)\cap\overline{\mathbb{H}}
, or
i/2+D(1/2)
.

Lemma 10
.

If
D^{*}
is a formal adjoint for
D
, then for sections
w
with compact support in the above coordinate chart we have

(6)

\left|\partial_{s}\right|^{2}\tau\circ D^{*}\circ\tau_{1}^{-1}(w)=-\partial_{s}w+i\partial_{t}w+S(s,t)^{T}w,

where
\left|\partial_{s}\right|^{2}=\mu(\partial_{s},\partial_{s})
and
S(s,t)^{T}
is the transpose matrix.

Proof
.

The first thing we do is derive formulas for the Hermitian metrics
\mu
. Because
\tau
is a unitary transformation, we have

\mu(\tau^{-1}(u),\tau^{-1}(v))=\sum_{k}\overline{u_{k}}v_{k}=:\mu_{0}(u,v).

Unfortunately,
\tau_{1}
is not a unitary transformation because
\mathrm{d}s-i\mathrm{d}t
is not a unitary frame of
\Lambda^{0,1}
. We easily compute
\mu(-,\partial s)=\left|\partial_{s}\right|^{2}(\mathrm{d}s-i\mathrm{d}t)
(by inserting
\partial_{s},\partial_{t}
into both sides). Since the Hermitian metric on
\Lambda^{0,1}
is pushed forward from
T\Sigma
we have

\left|\partial_{s}\right|^{4}\mu(\mathrm{d}s-i\mathrm{d}t,\mathrm{d}s-i\mathrm{d}t)=\mu(\partial_{s},\partial_{s})=\left|\partial_{s}\right|^{2}\implies\left|\mathrm{d}s-i\mathrm{d}t\right|^{2}=\left|\partial_{s}\right|^{-2}.

We can therefore compute

\mu(\tau_{1}^{-1}(u),\tau_{1}^{-1}(v))=\sum_{k,\ell}\mu(u_{k}(\mathrm{d}s-i\mathrm{d}t)\otimes X_{k},v_{\ell}(\mathrm{d}s-i\mathrm{d}t)\otimes X_{\ell})=\left|\partial_{s}\right|^{-2}\mu_{0}(u,v).

It is also easy to compute that
\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}=\left|\partial_{s}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t
.

Now let
w
and
u
be
\mathbb{C}^{n}
valued functions which takes values in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
on
\mathbb{R}\times\left\{0,1\right\}
. We compute

\mu(\tau^{-1}(u),D^{*}\circ\tau_{1}^{-1}(w))=\mu_{0}(u,\tau\circ D^{*}\circ\tau_{1}^{-1}(w)).

On the other hand, we have

\mu(D\circ\tau^{-1}(u),\tau_{1}^{-1}(w))=\left|\partial_{s}\right|^{-2}\mu_{0}(\tau_{1}\circ D\circ\tau^{-1}(u),w).

Since real linear combinations of
X_{k}
lie in
F
, we are allowed to apply the formal adjoint property with
\xi=\tau^{-1}\circ u
and
\eta=\tau_{1}^{-1}\circ w
:

\mathrm{Re}\int\mu(D\circ\tau^{-1}(u),\tau_{1}^{-1}(w))\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}=\mathrm{Re}\int\mu(\tau^{-1}(u),D^{*}\circ\tau_{1}^{-1}(w))\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}.

This implies that:

\mathrm{Re}\int\mu_{0}(\tau_{1}\circ D\circ\tau^{-1}(u),w)\mathrm{d}s\wedge\mathrm{d}t=\mathrm{Re}\int\mu_{0}(u,\tau\circ D^{*}\circ\tau_{1}^{-1}(w))\left|\partial_{s}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\wedge\mathrm{d}t.

In particular,
\left|\partial_{s}\right|^{2}\tau\circ D^{*}\circ\tau_{1}^{-1}
is the formal adjoint of
D_{\tau}:=\tau_{1}\circ D\circ\tau^{-1}
with respect to the standard metric
\mu_{0}
and volume form
\mathrm{d}s\wedge\mathrm{d}t
. Equation (
1
) gives a formula for
D_{\tau}
and so we can explicitly compute its adjoint:

\mathrm{Re}\int\mu_{0}(\partial_{s}u+i\partial_{t}u+S(s,t)u,w)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t=\mathrm{Re}\int\mu_{0}(u_{k},-\partial_{s}w+i\partial_{t}u+S(s,t)^{T}w)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t.

The boundary terms in the integration by parts are given by

\mathrm{Re}\int_{\mathbb{R}\times\left\{0,1\right\}}\mu_{0}(iu,w)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t=0.

It follows that
\left|\partial_{s}\right|^{2}\tau\circ D^{*}\circ\tau_{1}^{-1}=-\partial_{s}+i\partial_{t}+S(s,t)^{T}
as desired.

As a consequence, if
s+it
is the coordinate system in a cylindrical end
C_{z}
and
\tau
is an asymptotic trivialization, then

\tau\circ D^{*}\circ\tau_{1}^{-1}=-\partial_{s}+i\partial_{t}+S(s,t)^{T}\to-\partial_{s}-A\text{ as $s\to\infty$},

where
A_{\tau}w=-i\partial_{t}w-S_{\infty}(t)w
is the asymptotic operator for
D_{\tau}
in the end
C_{z}
.

4.
Regularity and the Fredholm property

The references for this section are
[
Sal97
, Section 2.3]
,
[
Wen20
, Chapter 4]
,
[
Sch95
, Chapter 3]
, and
[
MS12
, Appendix B]
(for the local
L^{p}
elliptic estimates).

4.1.
Local elliptic estimates

Our first result is the following local elliptic estimate for
u\mapsto\partial_{s}u+i\partial_{t}u
.

Theorem 11
.

Fix
r<1
and
q>1
. There is a constant
c_{q,r}
so that for all smooth maps
u:\overline{D}(1)\cap\overline{\mathbb{H}}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
satisfying
u(D(1)\cap\mathbb{R})\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
we have

\int_{D(r)\cap\overline{\mathbb{H}}}\left|u\right|^{q}+\left|\partial_{x}u\right|^{q}+\left|\partial_{y}u\right|^{q}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y\leq c_{q,r}\int_{D(1)\cap\overline{\mathbb{H}}}\left|u\right|^{q}+\left|\partial_{x}u+i\partial_{y}u\right|^{q}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y.

Proof
.

The theorem follows from
[
MS12
, Theorem B.3.2]
which concerns weak solutions of the equation

\Delta w=f_{0}+\partial_{x}f_{1}+\partial_{y}f_{2},

with
w,f_{0},f_{1},f_{2}\in L^{p}(D(1))
. The conclusion is that the
W^{1,q}
size of
w
on a smaller disk is bounded by the
L^{q}
sizes of
w,f_{0},f_{1},f_{2}
. This uses the Calderon-Zygmund inequality proved in
[
MS12
, Section B.2]
.

To apply their result to our setting, we extend
u
across the boundary by

u(x,-y)=\overline{u(x,y)}.

The extended function is no longer smooth. Let
\eta=\partial_{x}u+i\partial_{y}u
(which potentially has a jump discontinuity along
\mathbb{R}
, but is still in
L^{q}
). We note that

\eta(x,-y)=\partial_{x}\overline{u}-i\partial_{y}\overline{u}=\overline{\partial_{x}u+i\partial_{y}u}=\overline{\eta(x,y)}.

In particular, the size
\left|\eta\right|
is invariant under
y\mapsto-y
.

Using the fact that
(\partial_{x}-i\partial_{y})(\partial_{x}+i\partial_{y})=\Delta
we have

\int_{D(1)}g_{0}(u,\Delta\phi)\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y=-\int_{D(1)}g_{0}(\eta,(\partial_{x}+i\partial_{y})\phi)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y.

To see why, apply Stokes’ Theorem separately on the upper and lower half-disks, and then observe that the boundary terms will cancel; this uses
u(D(1)\cap\mathbb{R})\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The equality above satisfies the hypothesis of
[
MS12
, Theorem B.3.2]
and allows us to conclude that
W^{1,q}
size of
u
is controlled by the
L^{q}
size of
\eta
and
u
. This implies the desired result.

4.2.
Local elliptic regularity

In this section we wish to prove that weak solutions of
D^{*}(\eta)=f
are in fact smooth, provided
f
is smooth. In order to talk about
D^{*}(\eta)
, we require the choice of Hermitian metrics
\mu
on
E,T\dot{\Sigma}
, as in Section
3.5
.

More precisely, we wish to prove the following:

Proposition 12
.

Let
q>1
. If
\eta\in L^{q}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Lambda^{0,1}\otimes E)
,
f
is smooth, and
D^{*}(\eta)=f
weakly in the sense that

\mathrm{Re}\int_{\dot{\Sigma}}\mu(D(\xi),\eta)\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}=\mathrm{Re}\int_{\dot{\Sigma}}\mu(\xi,f)\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}

for all
\xi\in\Gamma_{0}(E,F)
, then
\eta
is in smooth and lies in
\Gamma(\Lambda^{0,1}\otimes E,F^{*})
.

The same holds true with
(\eta,\Lambda^{0,1}\otimes E,F^{*},D^{*})
swapped with
(\xi,E,F,D)
throughout the statement.

Remark 13
.

Note that “weakly” solving the equation implicitly incorporates the boundary conditions, since we allow the test functions to be non-zero along the boundary. We do require, however, that the test functions take values the appropriate sub-bundle
F
.

Since smoothness is a local property, we can prove Proposition
12
by restricting our attention to a coordinate chart
z=s+it
on which we have a unitary trivialization
\tau:E\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Without loss of generality, let us suppose that
z
takes values in
D(1)\cap\overline{\mathbb{H}}
. Writing
\tau(\xi)=u
,
\tau_{1}(\eta)=w
, and
f:=\tau(f)
, we compute

\displaystyle D^{*}(\eta)=f\text{ weakly}

\displaystyle\implies-\partial_{s}w+i\partial_{t}w+S(s,t)^{T}w=\left|\partial_{s}\right|^{-2}f\text{ weakly}

\displaystyle D(\xi)=f\text{ weakly}

\displaystyle\implies\partial_{s}u+i\partial_{t}u+S(s,t)u=f\text{ weakly}.

We can simplify this a bit further by observing that

\displaystyle-\partial_{s}w+i\partial_{t}w+S(s,t)^{T}w

\displaystyle=\left|\partial_{s}\right|^{-2}f

\displaystyle\iff\partial_{s}\overline{w}+i\partial_{t}\overline{w}-CS(s,t)^{T}C\overline{w}

\displaystyle=-\left|\partial_{s}\right|^{-2}\overline{f},

where
C
is the matrix representing complex conjugation. Thus, Proposition
12
follows from:

Lemma 14
.

Let
q>1
. Write
\Omega(r)=D(r)\cap\overline{\mathbb{H}}
, and suppose that

u\in L^{q}(\Omega(1),\mathbb{C}^{n})
,
f\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega(1)},\mathbb{C}^{n})
, and
S\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega(1)},\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2n})

satisfy

(7)

\partial_{s}u+i\partial_{t}u+S(s,t)u=f\text{ weakly,}

in the sense that

\mathrm{Re}\int_{\Omega(1)}\mu_{0}(u,-\partial_{s}\varphi+i\partial_{t}\varphi+S(s,t)^{T}\varphi)=\mathrm{Re}\int_{\Omega(1)}\mu_{0}(f,\varphi),

for all compactly supported test functions
\varphi
which take values in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
on
\Omega(1)\cap\mathbb{R}
. Then
u
is smooth and takes boundary values in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Moreover for
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
r<1
, there exists a constant
c=c(k,q,S)
so that

(8)

\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{W^{k,q}(D(r)\cap\mathbb{H})}\leq c(\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{q}(D(1)\cap\mathbb{H})}+\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{W^{k-1,q}(D(1)\cap\mathbb{H})}).

Proof
.

Throughout the argument we will need to shrink the domain countably many times. For this purpose, fix a sequence
1>r_{1}>r_{2}>\cdots>r_{\infty}=r
. Each time we need to shrink the domain we will pass from
\Omega(r_{j})
to
\Omega(r_{j+1})
. To obtain the constant in (
8
), we will only need to shrink the domain finitely many times.

Our first goal is to upgrade
u
to a
W^{1,q}
distribution. We observe that

(\partial_{s}+i\partial_{t})u=-S(s,t)u+f\text{ weakly}.

Notice that the right hand side lies in
L^{q}
. More generally, let us consider equations of the form

(\partial_{s}+i\partial_{t})u=F\text{ weakly},

where
F\in L^{q}
. Our strategy is to approximate
u
by a sequence of smooth sections
u_{n}
taking real values along the boundary so that:

(i)

u_{n}\to u\in L^{q}
and

(ii)

\left\lVert(\partial_{s}+i\partial_{t})u_{n}\right\rVert_{L^{q}(\Omega(r_{1}))}
is bounded by
c_{1}\left\lVert F\right\rVert_{L^{q}(\Omega(1))}
.

We will explain how to do this approximation at the end of the proof. See
[
Wen20
, Section 2.4]
for another approach in the case with
\overline{\mathbb{H}}
replaced by
\mathbb{C}
. See
[
MS12
, Section B.4]
for a similar bootstrapping argument (in a non-linear context).
The estimate from Theorem
11
then implies that
u_{n}
is bounded in the
W^{1,q}
topology (on a smaller domain
\Omega(r_{2})
). Indeed, we have

\left\lVert u_{n}\right\rVert_{W^{1,q}(\Omega(r_{2}))}\leq c_{2}(\left\lVert u_{n}\right\rVert_{L^{q}(\Omega(1))}+\left\lVert F\right\rVert_{L^{q}(\Omega(1))}).

Since the
W^{1,q}
spaces are reflexive, the Banach-Alaoglu theorem implies that some subsequence of
u_{n}
converges in the weak topology to an element
u^{\prime}\in W^{1,q}
. Since
(L^{q})^{*}\subset(W^{1,q})^{*}
we conclude that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\left\langle u_{n},w\right\rangle=\left\langle u^{\prime},w\right\rangle
for all
w\in(L^{q})^{*}
. However, the same holds with
u^{\prime}
replaced by
u
(because
u_{n}
converges to
u
in the
L^{q}
norm). Thus
u=u^{\prime}
, and hence
u\in W^{1,q}
. Moreover, the Banach-Alaoglu theorem implies the
W^{1,q}
norm of
u
is bounded above by
\limsup\left\lVert u_{n}\right\rVert_{W^{1,q}}
, and hence we conclude that

\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{W^{1,q}(\Omega(r_{2}))}\leq c_{2}(\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{q}(\Omega(1))}+\left\lVert F\right\rVert_{L^{q}(\Omega(1))}).

Now, suppose that we have shown that
u
is of class
W^{k,q}
on some region
\Omega(r_{2k})
. Moreover, suppose that the
\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{W^{k,q}(\Omega(r_{2k}))}
is bounded by
c(\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{q}(\Omega(1))}+\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{W^{k-1,q}(\Omega(1))})
for some
c
. Then we can differentiate the equation (
7
)
k
times in the
s
-direction to conclude:

(9)

(\partial_{s}+i\partial_{t})\partial_{s}^{k}u=\partial_{s}^{k}f-\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}\partial_{s}^{\ell}S(s,t)\cdot\partial_{s}^{k-\ell}u=F_{k}\text{ weakly}.

This differentiation is a bit subtle because the “weak” condition incorporates the boundary conditions; we will explain this step in greater detail at the end of the proof.

By our assumption on
u
, the right hand side is in
L^{q}
. The same argument given above implies that
\partial_{s}^{k}u
is in
W^{1,q}
on a smaller region
\Omega(r_{2k+2})
and that

\left\lVert\partial_{s}^{k}u\right\rVert_{W^{1,q}(\Omega(r_{2k+2}))}\leq c^{\prime}(\left\lVert\partial_{s}^{k}u\right\rVert_{L^{q}(\Omega(r_{2k}))}+\left\lVert\partial_{s}^{k}f\right\rVert_{L^{q}(\Omega(r_{2k}))}+C(S)\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{W^{k,q}(\Omega(r_{2k}))}).

Now it is straightforward to use (
7
) to establish that, for
a+b=k
,

\partial_{s}^{a}\partial_{t}^{b}u=i^{b}\partial_{s}^{k}u+\text{lower order terms}.

This equality should be interpreted as saying that both sides agree when integrated against a test function which is supported in the interior of the domain (i.e., we do not need to worry about the boundary).
Since
\partial_{s}^{k}u
and the “lower order terms” are of class
W^{1,q}
we conclude that all the
k
th order derivatives are in
W^{1,q}(\Omega(r_{2k+2}))
, and hence
u
is in
W^{k+1,q}(\Omega(r_{2k+2}))
. Keeping track of the various estimates implies that

\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{W^{k+1,q}(\Omega(r_{2k+2}))}\leq c^{\prime\prime}(\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{q}(\Omega(1))}+\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{W^{k,q}(\Omega(1))}).

The Sobolev embedding theorem
[
MS12
, Theorem B.1.11]
implies that
u
is smooth on
\Omega(r_{\infty})
. Part of the conclusion of the Sobolev embedding theorem is that
u
extends smoothly to the boundary. We claim that
u
takes
\mathbb{R}^{n}
values along the boundary. This follows from (
7
); pick any test function
\varphi
taking boundary values in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. It is easy to see (by integration by parts) that

\mathrm{Re}\int_{D(1)\cap\overline{\mathbb{H}}}\mu_{0}(\partial_{s}u+i\partial_{t}u,\varphi)-\mu_{0}(u,-\partial_{s}\varphi+i\partial_{t}\varphi)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t=\mathrm{Re}\int_{D(1)\cap\mathbb{R}}\mu_{0}(u,i\varphi)\mathrm{d}s.

If
u
did not take
\mathbb{R}^{n}
-values along
D(1)\cap\mathbb{R}
, we could pick
\varphi
so that the right hand side was non-zero. This would contradict (
7
).

This completes the proof, modulo our explanation of how to pick the approximations
u_{n}\to u
so that (i) and (ii) hold, and also why we can differentiate the weak equation with respect to
\partial_{s}
to obtain (
9
).

First we explain how to differentiate the weak equation. Suppose that
(\partial_{s}+i\partial_{t})w=F
weakly and
w,F\in W^{1,q}
. Then for any test function
\varphi
taking real-values along the boundary,
\partial_{s}\varphi
still takes real values along the boundary, and hence

(10)

\mathrm{Re}\int\mu_{0}(w,(-\partial_{s}+i\partial_{t})\partial_{s}\varphi)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t=\mathrm{Re}\int\mu_{0}(F,\partial_{s}\varphi)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t.

The distributional derivative
\partial_{s}
is defined (by duality) by how it integrates against sections
\psi
supported in the
interior
of
\Omega(r)
:

\mathrm{Re}\int\mu_{0}(F,\partial_{s}\psi)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t=\mathrm{Re}\int-\mu_{0}(\partial_{s}F,\psi)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t,

However, the above holds even if
\psi
is non-zero along the boundary
\Omega(r)\cap\mathbb{R}
. To see why, observe that

\mathrm{Re}\int_{\Omega(r)}\mu_{0}(\partial_{s}F,\psi)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t=\mathrm{Re}\lim_{\delta\to 0}\int_{\Omega(r)}\mu_{0}(\partial_{s}F,\beta(t/\delta)\psi)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t,

where
\beta:[0,\infty)\to[0,1]
vanishes near
0
and equals
1
on
[1,\infty)
. Since
\beta(t/\delta)
is independent of
s
, we can integrate by parts and conclude

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}\int_{\Omega(r)}\mu_{0}(\partial_{s}F,\psi)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Re}\lim_{\delta\to 0}\int_{\Omega(r)}\mu_{0}(F,\beta(t/\delta)\partial_{s}\psi)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Re}\int_{\Omega(r)}\mu_{0}(F,\partial_{s}\psi)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t.

In particular, this observation applied to (
10
) yields

\mathrm{Re}\int\mu_{0}(\partial_{s}w,(-\partial_{s}+i\partial_{t})\varphi)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t=\mathrm{Re}\int\mu_{0}(\partial_{s}F,\varphi)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t,

which implies that
(\partial_{s}+i\partial_{t})\partial_{s}w=\partial_{s}F
still holds weakly.

Finally, we explain how to choose the approximations
u_{n}\to u
so that (i) and (ii) hold. First we extend
u
as an
L^{q}
distribution to
D(1)
by
E(u)(s,-t)=\overline{u}(s,t)
for
t\leq 0
. This can be defined in the sense of distributions as

\left\langle E(u),\varphi\right\rangle=\mathrm{Re}\int_{D(1)\cap\Omega}\mu_{0}(u(s,t),\varphi(s,t)+\overline{\varphi}(s,-t))\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t.

Let
\Phi
be a radially symmetric bump function of unit mass supported in
D(1)
, and let
\Phi_{n}(s,t)=\Phi(sn,tn)
. Then define
u_{n}=\Phi_{n}\ast E(u).
Clearly (i) holds. It can be shown that

\left\langle\Phi_{n}\ast E(u),(-\partial_{s}+i\partial_{t})\varphi\right\rangle=\left\langle E(u),(-\partial_{s}+i\partial_{t})(\Phi_{n}\ast\varphi)\right\rangle=\left\langle E(F),(\Phi_{n}\ast\varphi)\right\rangle.

This uses the distributional definition of
E(u)
and the assumption that
(\partial_{s}+i\partial_{t})u=F
weakly. It also uses the fact that convolution commutes with
\partial_{s}+i\partial_{t}
(as it is a differential operator with constant coefficients).

We therefore conclude that
L^{q}
size of
(\partial_{s}+i\partial_{t})(\Phi_{n}\ast E)(u)
is bounded by the
L^{q}
size of
F
. This proves (ii). We observe that since
\Phi_{n}
is a radially symmetric and
u(s,-t)=\overline{u}(s,t)
,
u_{n}
must take real values along the real axis. This completes the proof.

Note that a consequence of the above proof is the following smooth approximation result:

Proposition 15
.

Let
\Omega(r)=\overline{\mathbb{H}}\cap D(r)
and
q>1
. Suppose that
u\in L^{q}(\Omega(1),\mathbb{C}^{n})
has the property that
\partial_{s}u+i\partial_{t}u=F
holds weakly for some
F\in L^{q}(\Omega(1),\mathbb{C}^{n})
. Then for any
r<1
, the doubling
E(u)
lies in
W^{1,q}(D(r))
and there is a family of smooth functions
u_{n}
on
\Omega(r)
taking real values on
\partial\Omega(r)
so that
u_{n}\to u
in
W^{1,q}(\Omega(r))
.

Proof
.

Let
E(u)
be the doubling of
u
, as in the previous proof, and recall that
\Phi_{n}\ast E(u)
converges to
E(u)
in
L^{q}(D(r^{\prime}))
and is bounded in
W^{1,q}(D(r^{\prime}))
. As we argued above, this implies that some subsequence of
\Phi_{n}\ast E(u)
converges to
E(u)
in the weak topology for
W^{1,q}(\Omega(r^{\prime}))
. In particular
E(u)
is in
W^{1,q}(D(r^{\prime}))
. Basic properties of convolutions ensure that
\Phi_{n}\ast E(u)
converges to
E(u)
in the
W^{1,q}(D(r))
norm. Thus we can set
u_{n}=\Phi_{n}\ast E(u)
, as desired.

Remark 16
.

Let
u\in W^{1,q}(\Omega(1),\mathbb{C}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n})
, i.e.,
E(u)\in W^{1,q}(D(1),\mathbb{C}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Then
(\partial_{s}+i\partial_{t})\Phi_{n}\ast E(u)=F_{n}
converges to some element
F\in L^{q}
(in the sense of distributions). We claim that

\partial_{s}u+i\partial_{t}u=F\text{ holds weakly}.

This is a sort of converse to the above proposition. Indeed, if
\varphi
takes real-values along the boundary, we compute

\left\langle u,-\partial\varphi\right\rangle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left\langle\Phi_{n}\ast E(u),-\partial\varphi\right\rangle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left\langle\overline{\partial}\Phi_{n}\ast E(u),\varphi\right\rangle=\left\langle F,\varphi\right\rangle,

as desired.

Remark 17
.

If
u\in W^{1,p}(\Omega(1),\mathbb{C}^{n})
with
p>2
, then
u
has well-defined boundary values. Suppose that
u
takes real values along the boundary. We will show that
E(u)\in W^{1,p}_{\mathrm{loc}}(D(1),\mathbb{C}^{n})
. Let
\varphi
be a test function taking real values along the boundary. Let
h:\overline{\mathbb{H}}\to[0,1]
be a function which (a) vanishes on
\mathbb{R}\times[0,1]
, (b) which equals
1
on
\mathbb{R}\times[2,\infty)
and (c) which depends only on the
t
coordinate. Let
F=\overline{\partial}u
(an
L^{p}
distribution). We compute:

\left\langle u,-\partial\varphi\right\rangle=\lim_{\sigma\to\infty}\left\langle h(\sigma t)u,-\partial\varphi\right\rangle=\lim_{\sigma}[\sigma\left\langle\overline{\partial}(h)(\sigma t)u,\varphi\right\rangle+\left\langle h(\sigma t)F,\varphi\right\rangle].

Note that
\overline{\partial}(h)(\sigma t)
is concentrated on a region
R_{\sigma}
of area at most
\sigma^{-1}
. Since
\overline{\partial}(h)
is purely imaginary, we can write

\left\langle\overline{\partial}(h)(\sigma t)u,\varphi\right\rangle=\left\langle\overline{\partial}(h)(\sigma t)\mathrm{Im}(u),\text{Re}(\varphi)\right\rangle+\left\langle\overline{\partial}(h)(\sigma t)\mathrm{Re}(u),\text{Im}(\varphi)\right\rangle.

Our discussion of
R_{\sigma}
and
\overline{\partial}(h)
implies that

\left|\sigma\left\langle\overline{\partial}(h)(\sigma t)u,\varphi\right\rangle\right|\leq C(\sup_{R_{\sigma}}\left|\mathrm{Re}(\varphi)\right|\left|\mathrm{Im}(u)\right|+\left|\mathrm{Re}(u)\right|\left|\mathrm{Im}(\varphi)\right|).

Because
\mathrm{Im}(u)
and
\mathrm{Im}(\varphi)
are both continuous and vanish on the boundary, we can take the limit as
\sigma\to\infty
and conclude that
\lim_{\sigma}\sigma\left\langle\overline{\partial}(h)(\sigma t)u,\varphi\right\rangle
vanishes. We are left with

\left\langle u,-\partial\varphi\right\rangle=\left\langle F,\varphi\right\rangle

This says that
\overline{\partial}u=F
weakly. As a consequence of Proposition
15
we conclude that
E(u)\in W^{1,p}(D(r))
and
u
can be approximated in
W^{1,p}(D(r))
by smooth functions taking real values along the boundary. This completes the proof.

4.3.
Injectivity estimates for translation invariant operators

The next result concerns various estimates for operators of the form

u\mapsto\partial_{s}u+i\partial_{t}u+S(t)u=\partial_{s}u-Au

on the infinite strip
\mathbb{R}\times[0,1]
with
A
a non-degenerate asymptotic operator. See
[
Sal97
, Lemma 2.4]
,
[
Wen20
, Section 4.4]
, and
[
Sch95
, Section 3.1.2]
for similar results for the infinite cylinder.

Proposition 18
.

Let
D(u)=\partial_{s}u-Au
on the infinite strip
\mathbb{R}\times[0,1]
, where
A
is a non-degenerate asymptotic operator. Let
\left\lVert-\right\rVert
denote the
L^{2}
norm over
[0,1]
.

There exist constants
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3,p}
so that, for all
u\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}\times[0,1],\mathbb{C}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we have

(i)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left\lVert u\right\rVert^{2}+\left\lVert\partial_{s}u\right\rVert^{2}+\left\lVert\partial_{t}u\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}s\leq c_{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left\lVert D(u)\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}s
,

(ii)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left\lVert u\right\rVert^{p}\mathrm{d}s\leq c_{2}^{p}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left\lVert D(u)\right\rVert^{p}\mathrm{d}s
,

(iii)

\displaystyle\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{W^{1,p}(\mathbb{R}\times[0,1])}\leq c_{3,p}\left\lVert D(u)\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}\times[0,1])}
, for
p\geq 2
.

The same result holds with
[0,1]
replaced by
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
.

Remark 19
.

Before we prove the theorem, we wish to make a few remarks.

(1)

All of these estimates roughly measure the injectivity of
D
.

(2)

The results are proved for smooth functions with compact support, although they imply estimates on various Banach space completions of
C^{\infty}_{0}
by taking smooth approximations. For instance, the reflection plus convolution technique used in the proof of Lemma
14
can be used to approximate
u\in W^{1,p}(\mathbb{R}\times[0,1],\mathbb{C}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n})
by smooth
u_{n}
taking real values along the boundary.

(3)

Note that (i) is (iii) in the case
p=2
. After we prove the proposition, we will be able to upgrade (iii) to include the case
q<2
. See Theorem
21
.

(4)

Note that (ii) can be considered as an estimate on a mixed
(2,p)
norm.

(5)

We will give an elementary proof of (i), which is similar to the one given in
[
Sch95
]
. See
[
Wen20
]
for an alternate proof of (i) which considers the Fourier transformation in the
s
-variable.

(6)

Our proofs of (ii) and (iii) are directly inspired by
[
Sal97
]
. The proof of (ii) will use the spectral properties of
A
proved in Proposition
9
. See
[
Sch95
]
for an alternative proof of (iii).

Proof

(of Proposition
18
)
.

Suppose that
D(u)=\eta
, i.e.,
\partial_{s}u=Au+\eta
. To prove (i), the idea is to consider the quantity
\gamma(s)=\left\lVert u(s,t)\right\rVert^{2}=\left\langle u,u\right\rangle
, where
\left\langle-,-\right\rangle
denotes the real inner product on
L^{2}([0,1],\mathbb{C}^{n})
. We differentiate
\gamma(s)
twice:

\displaystyle\gamma^{\prime\prime}(s)

\displaystyle=\left\langle\partial_{s}u,\partial_{s}u\right\rangle+\left\langle u,\partial_{s}\partial_{s}u\right\rangle=\left\lVert\partial_{s}u\right\rVert^{2}+\left\langle u,\partial_{s}(Au+\eta)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\partial_{s}u\right\rVert^{2}+\left\langle Au,\partial_{s}u\right\rangle+\partial_{s}\left\langle u,\eta\right\rangle-\left\langle\partial_{s}u,\eta\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\partial_{s}u\right\rVert^{2}+\left\lVert Au\right\rVert^{2}+\left\langle Au,\eta\right\rangle+\partial_{s}\left\langle u,\eta\right\rangle-\left\langle\partial_{s}u,\eta\right\rangle.

Here we have used the fact that
\partial_{s}Au=A\partial_{s}u
and
\left\langle f,Ag\right\rangle=\left\langle Af,g\right\rangle
. Now we integrate this equality over
\mathbb{R}
. Since
u
is smooth and compactly supported the integrals of
\gamma^{\prime\prime}(s)
and
\partial_{s}\left\langle u,\eta\right\rangle
both vanish. We are left with:

\int\left\lVert\partial_{s}u\right\rVert^{2}+\left\lVert Au\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}s=\int\left\langle\partial_{s}u-Au,\eta\right\rangle\mathrm{d}s.

Now using Cauchy-Schwarz and
2ab\leq a^{2}+b^{2}
we have

\displaystyle\int\left\langle\partial_{s}u-Au,\eta\right\rangle\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq\int\left\lVert\partial_{s}u\right\rVert\left\lVert\eta\right\rVert+\left\lVert Au\right\rVert\left\lVert\eta\right\rVert\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\int\left\lVert\partial_{s}u\right\rVert^{2}+\left\lVert Au\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}s+\int\left\lVert\eta\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}s.

Recalling that
D(u)=\eta
, it follows that

\int\left\lVert\partial_{s}u\right\rVert^{2}+\left\lVert Au\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}s\leq 2\int\left\lVert D(u)\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}s.

Finally, using the fact that
A:W^{1,2}([0,1],\mathbb{C}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
is an isomorphism, we conclude a constant
c\geq 1
so that
\left\lVert u\right\rVert^{2}+\left\lVert\partial_{t}u\right\rVert^{2}\leq c\left\lVert Au\right\rVert^{2}
, and hence

\int\left\lVert u\right\rVert^{2}+\left\lVert\partial_{s}u\right\rVert^{2}+\left\lVert\partial_{t}u\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}s\leq 2c\int\left\lVert D(u)\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}s,

as desired. This completes the proof of (i).

For (ii) we use the spectral properties of
A
. Let
E_{\pm}
denote the splitting of
L^{2}([0,1],\mathbb{C}^{n})
into positive and negative eigenspaces of
A
. The operator
\exp(-sA)
converges on
E_{+}
for
s\geq 0
while the operator
\exp(-sA)
converges on
E_{-}
for
s\leq 0
.

We can decompose
u=u_{+}+u_{-}
where
u_{+}(s,-)\in E_{+}
and
u_{-}(s,-)\in E_{-}
for all
s
. It is straightforward to show that

\displaystyle\text{for $s\geq 0$:}\hskip 8.5359pt\partial_{s}(\exp(-sA)u_{+}(s+s_{0},t))

\displaystyle=\exp(-sA)\eta_{+}(s_{0}+s,t),

\displaystyle\text{for $s\leq 0$:}\hskip 8.5359pt\partial_{s}(\exp(-sA)u_{-}(s+s_{0},t))

\displaystyle=\exp(-sA)\eta_{-}(s_{0}+s,t),

where
\eta_{\pm}=\partial_{s}u_{\pm}-Au_{\pm}
. Integrate the first ODE over
[0,\infty)
and integrate the second ODE over
(-\infty,0]
, concluding that

(11)

\displaystyle u_{+}(s_{0},t)

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp(-sA)\eta_{+}(s_{0}+s,t)\mathrm{d}s,

\displaystyle u_{-}(s_{0},t)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{0}\exp(-sA)\eta_{-}(s_{0}+s,t)\mathrm{d}s.

Following
[
Sal97
, Lemma 2.4]
, the idea is now to interpret this as a convolution
u_{\pm}=K_{\pm}\ast\eta_{\pm}
, and then apply Young’s convolution inequality to conclude (ii).

Here are the details of the argument. First, we show the mixed
(2,p)
norm satisfies a variational definition:

\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left\lVert u\right\rVert^{p}\mathrm{d}s\right]^{1/p}=\sup\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left\langle u,g\right\rangle\mathrm{d}s:\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left\lVert g\right\rVert^{q}\mathrm{d}s=1,\text{ where $p^{-1}+q^{-1}=1$}\right\}.

It is easy to show that
\geq
holds, and to show
\leq
it suffices to prove it when the left hand side is
1
. In this case we can simply take
g=\left\lVert u\right\rVert^{p-2}u
(if
p>2
this is fine, if
p<2
then we can take a sequence
g
approximating
\left\lVert u\right\rVert^{p-2}u
).

Now fix
g
and compute

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left\langle u_{+}(s_{0}),g(s_{0})\right\rangle\mathrm{d}s_{0}=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left\langle\exp(-sA)\eta_{+}(s_{0}+s),g(s_{0})\right\rangle\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}s_{0}.

It is straightforward to check that

\left\langle\exp(-sA)\eta_{+}(s_{0}+s),g(s_{0})\right\rangle\leq e^{-s\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}^{+}}\left\lVert\eta_{+}(s_{0}+s)\right\rVert\left\lVert g(s_{0})\right\rVert,

where
\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}^{+}
is the smallest positive eigenvalue.

Switching the order of integration and using Hölder’s inequality yields:

\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left\langle\exp(-sA)\eta_{+}(s_{0}+s),g(s_{0})\right\rangle\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}s_{0}\right|\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-s\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}^{+}}\mathrm{d}s\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left\lVert\eta_{+}\right\rVert^{p}\mathrm{d}s_{0}\right]^{1/p}.

As a consequence the variational definition of the mixed
2,p
norm implies that

\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left\lVert u_{+}(s)\right\rVert^{p}\mathrm{d}s\right]^{1/p}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}^{+}}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left\lVert\eta_{+}\right\rVert^{p}\mathrm{d}s\right]^{1/p}.

A similar argument shows that

\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left\lVert u_{-}(s)\right\rVert^{p}\mathrm{d}s\right]^{1/p}\leq\frac{-1}{\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}^{-}}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left\lVert\eta_{-}\right\rVert^{p}\mathrm{d}s\right]^{1/p},

where
\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}^{-}
is the largest negative eigenvalue.

Using the fact that
\left\lVert\eta_{\pm}\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert\eta\right\rVert
we conclude that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left\lVert u\right\rVert^{p}\mathrm{d}s\leq(\frac{1}{\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}^{+}}-\frac{1}{\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}^{-}})^{p}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left\lVert D(u)\right\rVert^{p}\mathrm{d}s.

This proves (ii).

To prove (iii) we again follow
[
Sal97
, Lemma 2.4]
. Fix
p>2
. Let
\Omega(r)=[-r,r]\times[0,1]
. It is straightforward to apply Theorem
11
and Sobolev embedding
[
MS12
, Theorem B.1.11]
to conclude constants
\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2},\kappa_{3},\kappa_{4}
so that

\displaystyle\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{W^{1,p}(\Omega(1))}

\displaystyle\leq\kappa_{1}(\left\lVert D(u)\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega(1.5))}+\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega(1.5))}),

\displaystyle\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega(1.5))}

\displaystyle\leq\kappa_{2}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{W^{1,2}(\Omega(1.5))},

\displaystyle\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{W^{1,2}(\Omega(1.5))}

\displaystyle\leq\kappa_{3}(\left\lVert D(u)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega(2))}+\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega(2))}),

\displaystyle\left\lVert D(u)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega(2))}

\displaystyle\leq\kappa_{4}\left\lVert D(u)\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega(2))}.

The constant
\kappa_{4}
can be explicitly computed as
4^{1-1/p}
. Combining these yields

\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{W^{1,p}(\Omega(1))}\leq\kappa_{1}(1+\kappa_{2}\kappa_{3}\kappa_{4})\left\lVert D(u)\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega(1.5))}+\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}\kappa_{3}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega(2))}.

Using
(a+b)^{p}\leq 2^{p}(a^{p}+b^{p})
we conclude

\left\lVert u\right\rVert^{p}_{W^{1,p}(\Omega(1))}\leq 2^{p}\kappa^{p}_{1}(1+\kappa_{2}\kappa_{3}\kappa_{4})^{p}\left\lVert D(u)\right\rVert^{p}_{L^{p}(\Omega(2))}+(\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}\kappa_{3})^{p}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega(2))}^{p}.

It is straightforward to compute that

\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega(2))}^{p}=[\int_{-2}^{2}\left\lVert u\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}s]^{p/2}\leq 4^{p/2-1}\int_{-2}^{2}\left\lVert u\right\rVert^{p}\mathrm{d}s.

The above holds with
\Omega(r)
replaced by
2k+\Omega(r)
for the same constants since
D
is translation invariant. Hence we conclude that there is a constant
C
so that

\left\lVert u\right\rVert^{p}_{W^{1,p}(2k+\Omega(1))}\leq C(\left\lVert D(u)\right\rVert^{p}_{L^{p}(2k+\Omega(2))}+\int_{2k-2}^{2k+2}\left\lVert u\right\rVert^{p}\mathrm{d}s).

Summing over all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
yields

\left\lVert u\right\rVert^{p}_{W^{1,p}(\mathbb{R}\times[0,1])}\leq 2C(\left\lVert D(u)\right\rVert^{p}_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}\times[0,1])}+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left\lVert u\right\rVert^{p}\mathrm{d}s).

The factor of
2
is because the domains
2k+\Omega(2)
cover
\mathbb{R}\times[0,1]
“twice over.” Finally, using part (ii), we conclude that

\left\lVert u\right\rVert^{p}_{W^{1,p}(\mathbb{R}\times[0,1])}\leq 2C(1+c_{2}^{p})\left\lVert D(u)\right\rVert^{p}_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}\times[0,1])}.

Setting
c_{3,p}=(2C(1+c_{2}^{p}))^{1/p}
completes the proof.

The formula (
11
) can be used to prove the following regularity result. We still assume that
D=\partial_{s}-A
is a translation invariant operator on the infinite strip or cylinder. As in the proof of Proposition
18
we assume that
\lambda^{-}_{\mathrm{max}}<0<\lambda^{+}_{\mathrm{min}}
are the maximal negative and minimal positive eigenvalues of
A
.

Lemma 20
.

Let
q>1
. If
u\in L^{q}
and
D(u)=\eta
for smooth
\eta
with compact support, then
u
is smooth. Moreover, for all
k,\ell\in\mathbb{N}
there are constants
C^{+}_{k,\ell}
and
C^{-}_{k,\ell}
depending on
\eta
so that

(12)

\displaystyle\left|\partial^{k}\partial^{\ell}u(s,t)\right|

\displaystyle\leq C_{k,\ell}^{+}e^{\lambda_{\max}^{-}s}\text{ as $s\to+\infty$},

\displaystyle\left|\partial^{k}\partial^{\ell}u(s,t)\right|

\displaystyle\leq C_{k,\ell}^{-}e^{\lambda_{\min}^{+}s}\text{ as $s\to-\infty$}.

Both estimates are of exponential decay type. In particular,
u\in W^{k,q}
for all
k
and all
q
.

Proof
.

Let
\Omega(r)=[-r,r]\times[0,1]
. By the local elliptic regularity result (Lemma
14
) we know that
u
is smooth, and that it satisfies elliptic estimates of the form

\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{W^{k+1,q}(s+\Omega(1))}\leq c(\left\lVert\eta\right\rVert_{W^{k,q}(s+\Omega(2))}+\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{q}(s+\Omega(2))}).

We can take
c
to be independent of
s
since
D
is translation invariant. In particular, it is clear that the
L^{2}([0,1])
size
\left\lVert u(s,-)\right\rVert
decays as
s\to\infty
.

Decompose
u=u_{+}+u_{-}
. It is straightforward to show that
u_{\pm}
are still elements of
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R},W^{1,2}([0,1],\mathbb{C}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n}))
. As in the proof of Proposition
18
, we think of the equation
\partial_{s}u_{+}-Au_{+}=\eta_{+}
as an ordinary differential equation which we can explicitly solve:

\displaystyle\partial_{s}(\exp(-sA)u_{+}(s_{0}+s))=\exp(-sA)\eta(s_{0}+s)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\exp(-NA)u_{+}(s_{0}+N)-u_{+}(s_{0})=\int_{0}^{N}\exp(-sA)\eta(s_{0}+s)\,\mathrm{d}s.

Taking the limit as
N\to\infty
and using the fact that
\lim_{N\to\infty}\left\lVert u_{+}(s_{0}+N)\right\rVert=0
we conclude that

u_{+}(s_{0},t)=-\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp(-sA)\eta_{+}(s_{0}+s,t)\,\mathrm{d}s.

A similar argument shows that the other equation in (
11
) also holds, and hence we have:

u(s_{0},t)=\int_{-\infty}^{0}\exp(-sA)\eta_{-}(s_{0}+s,t)\,\mathrm{d}s-\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp(-sA)\eta_{+}(s_{0}+s,t)\,\mathrm{d}s.

Now suppose that
\eta
is supported in
[-R,R]
. Then for
s_{0}<-R
, the first integral always vanishes, and the second integrand is supported on the region where
s>-s_{0}-R
and so we have:

\left\lVert u(s_{0},-)\right\rVert\leq e^{(s_{0}+R)\lambda^{+}_{\mathrm{min}}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left\lVert\eta_{+}\right\rVert\mathrm{d}s=C(\eta_{-},R)e^{s_{0}\lambda^{+}_{\mathrm{min}}}\hskip 14.22636pt\text{ (as $s_{0}\to-\infty$)}.

A similar deduction proves that

\left\lVert u(s_{0},-)\right\rVert\leq C(\eta_{+},R)e^{s_{0}\lambda^{-}_{\mathrm{max}}}\hskip 14.22636pt\text{ (as $s_{0}\to+\infty$)}.

Now, by simply integrating the norm
\left\lVert u(s,-)\right\rVert
over
s\in[s_{0}-2,s_{0}+2]
we conclude that

(13)

\displaystyle\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{2}(s+\Omega(2))}

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}e^{s\lambda^{-}_{\mathrm{max}}}\text{ as $s\to+\infty$}

\displaystyle\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{2}(s+\Omega(2))}

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}e^{s\lambda^{+}_{\mathrm{max}}}\text{ as $s\to-\infty$}.

Using the elliptic estimates for
q=2
, we conclude that the
W^{k,2}
size of
u
on
s+\Omega(1)
also decays exponentially like (
13
). Since the
C^{\ell}
size is controlled by the
W^{k+2,2}
size, we ultimately conclude the desired result (
12
).

We can upgrade the injectivity estimates to the following important result:

Theorem 21
.

Let
D=\partial_{s}u-Au
with
A
a non-degenerate asymptotic operator. Let
q>1
. The induced map
D:W^{1,q}(\mathbb{R}\times[0,1],\mathbb{C}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n})\to L^{q}(\mathbb{R}\times[0,1],\mathbb{C}^{n})
is an isomorphism.

Proof
.

First we prove the case when
q\geq 2
. Part (iii) of Proposition
18
implies that
D
is injective and has closed image. Thus it suffices to prove that the image of
D
is dense. If
\eta
is a smooth function with compact support, then (
11
) gives an explicit formula for some
u
satisfying
D(u)=\eta
. As in the proof of Lemma
20
,
u
is smooth and the formula (
11
) implies that
u
and its derivatives decay exponentially as
s\to\pm\infty
, hence
u\in W^{1,p}
.

Now we prove the case when
q<2
. We follow the argument outlined in
[
Sal97
, Exercise 2.5]
. The idea is to prove an injectivity estimate for
D:L^{q}\to W^{-1,q}
, and then upgrade this to a
D:W^{1,q}\to L^{q}
injectivity estimate.

By definition, we set
W^{-1,q}=(W^{1,p})^{*}
where
p
is Hölder dual to
q
, and

\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{-1,q}=\sup_{\left\lVert\varphi\right\rVert_{1,p}=1}\left\langle u,\varphi\right\rangle.

Let
D^{*}=-\partial_{s}-A
. By the above results (applied to
-D^{*}
) we conclude
D^{*}
is an isomorphism
W^{1,p}\to L^{p}
. Thus

c^{-1}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{q}}\leq\sup_{\left\lVert\varphi\right\rVert_{1,p}=1}\left\langle u,D^{*}(\varphi)\right\rangle\leq c\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{q}}.

Observe that
D^{*}=-\partial_{s}-A
is the formal adjoint to
D
, and hence (using distributional definitions) we have:

\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{q}}\leq c\left\lVert D(u)\right\rVert_{-1,q}.

In particular, if
v\in W^{1,q}
, then we can apply the above to
u=\partial_{s}v
and conclude that

\left\lVert\partial_{s}u\right\rVert_{L^{q}}\leq c\left\lVert D(\partial_{s}u)\right\rVert_{-1,q}.

Now it is clear that, in the sense of distributions, we have
D(\partial_{s}u)=\partial_{s}D(u)
. We claim that

\left\lVert\partial_{s}D(u)\right\rVert_{-1,q}\leq c_{2}\left\lVert D(u)\right\rVert_{L^{q}}.

This is easy to see using the above variational definition of the
W^{-1,q}
norm. Thus we conclude that

\left\lVert\partial_{s}u\right\rVert_{L^{q}}\leq c_{3}\left\lVert D(u)\right\rVert_{L^{q}}.

It is clear that
W^{-1,q}
norm is less than the
L^{q}
norm, hence
\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{q}}\leq c\left\lVert D(u)\right\rVert_{L^{q}}
. It then follows easily that
\left\lVert\partial_{t}u\right\rVert_{L^{q}}\leq c_{4}\left\lVert D(u)\right\rVert_{L^{q}}
, and so we conclude the desired injectivity estimate

\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{W^{1,q}}\leq c\left\lVert D(u)\right\rVert_{L^{q}}.

It follows easily that
D(u)
has closed range and hence it suffices to prove that the image of
D
is dense. The arguments given in (
11
) and Lemma
20
show that we can (explicitly) solve for compactly supported smooth functions and the solutions are certainly of class
W^{1,q}
. Thus
D
is surjective. This completes the proof that
D
is an isomorphism.

4.4.
Proof of the Fredholm property

The main result of this section is the following:

Proposition 22
.

Let
p>1
and let
D
be an asymptotically non-degenerate Cauchy-Riemann operator for the data
(\Sigma,\Gamma_{\pm},E,F,C,[\tau])
. Then the induced maps

D:W^{1,p}(E,F)\to L^{p}(\Lambda^{0,1}\otimes E)\text{ and }D^{*}:W^{1,p}(E,F)\to L^{p}(\Lambda^{0,1}\otimes E)

are Fredholm.

Similar arguments can be found in
[
Sal97
, Section 2.3]
,
[
Sch95
, Proposition 3.1.30]
, and
[
Wen20
, Section 4.5]
.

Proof
.

Let
\varphi_{\rho}
be a cutoff function supported in the ends which equals
0
on
\Sigma(\rho-1)
and equals
1
on
C(\rho)
. We can choose
\varphi_{\rho}
so that its derivatives are bounded as
\rho\to\infty
.

We observe that

D(\varphi_{\rho}u)=\partial_{s}(\varphi_{\rho}u)-Au(\varphi_{\rho}u)+\Delta(s)\varphi_{\rho}u,

where
\Delta(s)
is a lower order term which converges to
0
as
s\to\pm\infty
. We know that
\partial-A:W^{1,p}\to L^{p}
is an isomorphism and so
\left\lVert\varphi_{\rho}u\right\rVert_{W^{1,p}}\leq C\left\lVert(\partial_{s}-A)(\varphi_{\rho}u)\right\rVert
for some
C
. We estimate

\displaystyle\left\lVert\varphi_{\rho}u\right\rVert_{W^{1,p}}

\displaystyle\leq C(\left\lVert D(\varphi_{\rho}u)\right\rVert_{L^{p}}+\left\lVert\Delta(s)\varphi_{\rho}u\right\rVert_{L^{p}})

\displaystyle\leq C(\left\lVert D(u)\right\rVert_{L^{p}}+\left\lVert\Delta(s)\varphi_{\rho}u\right\rVert_{L^{p}}+\left\lVert\overline{\partial}\varphi_{\rho}\cdot u\right\rVert_{L^{p}})

\displaystyle\implies\left\lVert\varphi_{\rho}u\right\rVert_{L^{p}}

\displaystyle\leq C^{\prime}(\left\lVert D(u)\right\rVert_{L^{p}}+\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\Sigma(\rho))}),

where we pick
\rho
large enough so that
C\left|\Delta(s)\right|<0.5
on the support of
\varphi_{\rho}
. We also use that
\overline{\partial}\varphi_{\rho}
is supported in
\Sigma(\rho)
.

Next we combine the local elliptic estimates from
14
(to finitely many disks covering
\Sigma(\rho)
) and conclude some constant
C(\rho)
so that

\left\lVert(1-\varphi_{\rho})u\right\rVert_{W^{1,p}}\leq C(\rho)(\left\lVert Du\right\rVert_{L^{p}}+\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\Sigma(\rho+1))}).

Combining our two estimates (and updating the constant) yields

(14)

\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{W^{1,p}}\leq C(\left\lVert Du\right\rVert_{L^{p}}+\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\Sigma(\rho+1))}).

Crucially,
\rho
does not depend on
u
. Since
W^{1,p}\to L^{p}(\Sigma(\rho+1))
(inclusion followed by restriction) is a compact operator, we conclude from (
14
) that
D
is semi-Fredholm; i.e., has closed image and finite dimensional kernel. See
[
MS12
, Appendix A]
for the argument. The same argument shows that
D^{*}
is semi-Fredholm.

Now suppose that
D
were not surjective. Since the image of
D
is closed, we can apply the Hahn-Banach theorem to find
w\in L^{q}(\Lambda^{0,1}\otimes E)
so that
\left\langle D(u),w\right\rangle=0
for all
u\in W^{1,p}(E,F)
and
w\neq 0
. But then
w
is smooth and takes boundary values in
F^{*}
by the local regularity results. Moreover, in the ends we have
(\partial_{s}+A)w=\Delta^{*}w
which implies that
w\in W^{1,q}(\Lambda^{0,1}\otimes E,F^{*})
(using the injectivity estimates for
\partial_{s}+A
). We conclude that
w\in\ker D^{*}
. Since
D^{*}
is semi-Fredholm, its kernel is finite dimensional. This implies that
\mathrm{coker}\,D
is finite dimensional, and this completes the proof that
D
is Fredholm. The same argument works for
D^{*}
.

5.
Conley-Zehnder indices and kernel gluing

In this section our goal is to prove that the index behaves additively under a gluing operation. See
[
Sch95
, Section 3.2]
for a similar argument.

Throughout this section we fix an asymptotic trivialization
\tau
(i.e., fix
\tau_{z}
for each
z\in\Gamma
). Suppose that
D
is an asymptotic operator on
(\Sigma,\Gamma_{\pm},E,F)
whose restriction to the cylindrical ends
C_{z}
equals
D=\partial_{s}-A_{z}
with respect to
\tau_{z}
and where each
A_{z}
is a non-degenerate asymptotic operator. We have shown that
D
is Fredholm. Moreover, it is clear that if
D^{\prime}
has the same asymptotic operators
A_{z}
(in the same trivialization), then we can homotope
D
to
D^{\prime}
while remaining in the space of Fredholm operators. Then the index of
D
will equal the index of
D^{\prime}
. Therefore, the index depends only on the choice of non-degenerate asymptotic operators
z\mapsto A_{z}
(and
(\Sigma,\Gamma,E,F)
of course).

Introduce the reference operator
D^{\mathrm{al}}
whose restrictions to the cylindrical ends equals
\partial_{s}+i\partial_{t}+C
with respect to the same trivialization
\tau
. Here
C
is the matrix of complex conjugation, i.e., in each end we have
D^{\mathrm{al}}(u)=\partial_{s}u+i\partial_{t}u+\overline{u}
. The “
\mathrm{al}
” stands for “anti-linear.” The associated asymptotic operator is
A^{\mathrm{al}}=-i\partial_{t}-C
. In other words,
D^{\mathrm{al}}
has all of its asymptotics equal to
A^{\mathrm{al}}
.

In this section we will prove the following formula for index difference

\mathrm{ind}(D)-\mathrm{ind}(D^{\mathrm{al}})=\sum_{z\in\Gamma_{+}}\mu_{\mathrm{CZ}}(A_{z})-\sum_{z\in\Gamma_{+}}\mu_{\mathrm{CZ}}(A_{z}),

where
\mu_{\mathrm{CZ}}(A_{z})
is the
Conley-Zehnder
index of
A_{z}
, defined in Section
5.1
below. This formula determines how the index depends on changing asymptotic operators (i.e. we can compute
\mathrm{ind}(D_{1})-\mathrm{ind}(D_{2})
for any pair
D_{1},D_{2}
). In Section
6
we will prove that
\mathrm{ind}(D^{\mathrm{al}})=n\mathrm{X}+\mu_{\mathrm{Mas}}^{\tau}(E,F)
, which will complete the proof of the index formula.

5.1.
Conley-Zehnder indices

First we need to show that
D^{\mathrm{al}}
is actually Fredholm. This follows from:

Lemma 23

For

Proof
.

We prove the strip case, leaving the (very similar)
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
case to the reader. Suppose

t=0
and

\partial_{t}u_{j}=i\sigma\overline{u_{j}}\iff\partial_{t}(x_{j}+iy_{j})=\sigma(y_{j}+ix_{j})\implies u_{j}=x_{j}(0)(\cosh(\sigma t)+i\sinh(\sigma t)).

It is clear that

\beta

See

5.2.
Stabilizing Cauchy-Riemann operators

Let
D
be a Cauchy-Riemann operator on
(E,F,\Sigma,\Gamma,C,[t])
as usual.

As we have seen in Section
4.4
, the operator
D:W^{1,p}(E,F)\to L^{p}(\Lambda^{0,1}\otimes E)
has a finite dimensional cokernel which can be identified with
\ker D^{*}\subset W^{1,p}(\Lambda^{0,1}\otimes E,F^{*})
.

Pick a basis
c:\mathbb{R}\to\ker(D^{*})
, considered as a map
c:\mathbb{R}\to L^{p}(\Lambda^{0,1}\otimes E)
.

(15)

\left[\,\begin{matrix}{D}&{c}\end{matrix}\,\right]:W^{1,p}(E,F)\oplus\mathbb{R}\to L^{p}(\Lambda^{0,1}\otimes E).

For our choice of
c
, this operator is surjective and its kernel is
\ker(D)\oplus 0
. Since
\ker(D)
is finite dimensional, the above operator has a right inverse. Since having a right inverse is open in the norm topology, we can smoothly “cut-off” the cokernel elements
c_{1},\cdots,c_{d}
so that they vanish outside of
\Sigma(\rho_{0})
for
\rho_{0}
sufficiently large (i.e. they vanish on the ends
C(\rho_{0})
).

This leads us to the following definition: a
stabilized
operator for
D
is any surjective operator
D_{\mathrm{st}}
of the form (
15
) where
d=\dim\mathrm{coker}(D)

(resp.

Finally, fix

a transverse section of

This completes the proof of the reduction to the line bundle case.

6.3.
Bochner-Weitzenböck estimates and a linear compactness result

Following
[

Lemma 30

(Bochner-Weitzenböck estimates)
.

Let

\left\lVert D_{0}\xi\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}+\sigma^{2}\left\lVert B(\xi)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\left\lVert D^{\sigma}\xi\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\sigma\left\lVert\xi\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\left\lVert D^{*}_{0}(B(\xi))+B^{*}(D_{0}(\xi))\right\rVert_{L^{2}}.

Moreover,
\xi\mapsto D_{0}^{*}(B(\xi))+B^{*}(D_{0}(\xi))
is a

order operator (which is translation invariant in the ends). Similarly, if
\eta\in W^{1,2}(\Lambda^{1,0}\otimes E,F^{*})

\left\lVert D^{\ast}_{0}\eta\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}+\sigma^{2}\left\lVert B^{*}\eta\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\left\lVert D^{\sigma,\ast}\eta\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\sigma\left\lVert\xi\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\left\lVert D_{0}(B^{*}(\eta))+B(D^{*}_{0}(\eta))\right\rVert_{L^{2}}.

and
\eta\mapsto D_{0}(B^{*}(\eta))+B(D_{0}^{*}(\eta))
is also a zeroth order operator (also translation invariant in the ends). We therefore conclude a constant
C=C(D_{0},B)
so that for all
\xi,\eta
as above we have

(30)

\displaystyle\left\lVert B\xi\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sigma^{-2}\left\lVert D^{\sigma}\xi\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}+C\sigma^{-1}\left\lVert\xi\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}},

\displaystyle\left\lVert B^{*}(\eta)\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sigma^{-2}\left\lVert D^{\sigma,\ast}\eta\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}+C\sigma^{-1}\left\lVert\eta\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}.

In particular, if

to concentrate near the zeros of
B
.

Proof

Thanks to Proposition

\displaystyle\left\lVert D^{\sigma}\xi\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\xi,D^{\sigma,\ast}D^{\sigma}\xi\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\xi,D^{\ast}_{0}D_{0}\xi\right\rangle+\sigma\left\langle\xi,D_{0}^{\ast}(B(\xi))+B^{*}(D_{0}(\xi))\right\rangle+\sigma^{2}\left\lVert B(\xi)\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert D_{0}\xi\right\rVert^{2}+\sigma\left\langle\xi,D^{\ast}_{0}(B(\xi))+B^{*}(D_{0}(\xi))\right\rangle+\sigma^{2}\left\lVert B(\xi)\right\rVert^{2}.

for which

\displaystyle\left\lVert\overline{\partial}v\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}+\left\lVert zv\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\overline{\partial}v\pm z\overline{v}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+2\left\lVert v\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\left\lVert\overline{\partial}v\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}+\left\lVert zv\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\overline{\partial}v\pm\overline{z}\overline{v}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Proof
.

Using the smooth approximation result Proposition
15
, we may suppose that
v
is smooth, compactly supported, and takes real values along the boundary.

To prove the inequalities, we will need to integrate by parts two times. Let us focus on the first estimate. We start by computing:

(-\partial\pm zC)(\overline{\partial}\pm zC)v=-\partial\overline{\partial}v\pm z\overline{\overline{\partial}v}-\pm z\partial\overline{v}-\pm 2\overline{v}+\left|z\right|^{2}v.

Using the fact that
\overline{\overline{\partial}v}=\partial\overline{v}
we conclude that two terms cancel and we are left with

(35)

(-\partial\pm zC)(\overline{\partial}\pm zC)v=-\partial\overline{\partial}v\mp 2\overline{v}+\left|z\right|^{2}v.

The naive idea is to multiply both sides by
\mathrm{Re}\,\mu_{0}(v,-)
, integrate, and use the formal adjoint property for
-\partial\pm zC=(\overline{\partial}\pm zC)^{*}
and
-\partial=\overline{\partial}^{*}
. This naive argument would require that
\overline{\partial}v
and
\overline{\partial}v\pm z\overline{v}
take real values along the boundary, which we do not assume. However, as in the previous section, the fact that we integrate by parts twice will imply that the failures of formal adjointness will cancel out.

Indeed, we compute

\mathrm{Re}\int\mu_{0}(v,-\partial\overline{\partial}v)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t=\mathrm{Re}\int\mu_{0}(v,-\partial_{s}\overline{\partial}v)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{Re}\int\mu_{0}(v,i\partial_{t}\overline{\partial}v)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t.

It is clear that the can integrate by parts with respect to
\partial_{s}
, and conclude

\mathrm{Re}\int\mu_{0}(v,-\partial\overline{\partial}v)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t=\mathrm{Re}\int\mu_{0}(\partial_{s}v,\overline{\partial}v)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{Re}\int\mu_{0}(v,i\partial_{t}\overline{\partial}v)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t.

We can also integrate by parts with respect to
\partial_{t}
, at the expense of a boundary integral term, and (after some simplification) end up with:

\mathrm{Re}\int\mu_{0}(v,-\partial\overline{\partial}v)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t=\mathrm{Re}\int\mu_{0}(\overline{\partial}v,\overline{\partial}v)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t-\mathrm{Re}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mu_{0}(v,i\overline{\partial}v)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t.

We do the same computation with
\overline{\partial}
replaced by
D=\overline{\partial}\pm zC
, and conclude that

\mathrm{Re}\int\mu_{0}(v,D^{*}Dv)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t=\mathrm{Re}\int\mu_{0}(Dv,Dv)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t-\mathrm{Re}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mu_{0}(v,iDv)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t.

Finally, we observe that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mu_{0}(v,iDv)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Re}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mu_{0}(v,i\overline{\partial}v)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t\pm\mathrm{Re}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mu_{0}(v,iCz\overline{v})\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Re}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mu_{0}(v,i\overline{\partial}v)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t,

where we have used the fact that
Cz\overline{v}
takes real values along the boundary. Therefore

\mathrm{Re}\int\mu_{0}(v,D^{*}Dv)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t-\left\lVert Dv\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}=\mathrm{Re}\int\mu_{0}(v,\overline{\partial}^{*}\overline{\partial}v)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t-\left\lVert\overline{\partial}v\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Applying
\mathrm{Re}\,\mu_{0}(v,-)
to (
35
) and integrating implies

\left\lVert\overline{\partial}v\pm z\overline{v}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}=\left\lVert\overline{\partial}v\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}\mp 2\mathrm{Re}\int\mu_{0}(v,\overline{v})+\left\lVert zv\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

We rearrange and estimate to conclude that

\left\lVert\overline{\partial}v\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}+\left\lVert zv\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\left\lVert\overline{\partial}v\pm z\overline{v}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+2\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2},

as desired. The second estimate in the statement of the lemma (with
D=\overline{\partial}\pm\overline{z}C
) is proved in the same manner.

6.4.
Classifying the kernels of
D^{1}
(six cases).

The second estimate in Lemma
31
implies that
\overline{\partial}v\pm\overline{z}\overline{v}=0
has no non-zero solutions – this takes care of three of the six kinds of operators.

Our next lemma shows that
\overline{\partial}v\pm z\overline{v}=0
has either a one-dimensional space of solutions or a zero-dimensional space of solutions.

Lemma 32
.

Suppose that
v:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
is in
L^{2}
, then

\displaystyle\overline{\partial}v-z\overline{v}=0

\displaystyle\iff v=ci\exp(-\frac{1}{2}\left|z\right|^{2})\text{ for $c\in\mathbb{R}$},

\displaystyle\overline{\partial}v+z\overline{v}=0

\displaystyle\iff v=c\exp(-\frac{1}{2}\left|z\right|^{2})\text{ for $c\in\mathbb{R}$}.

On the other hand if
v:\overline{\mathbb{H}}\to\mathbb{C}
is in
L^{2}
and takes real values along the boundary, then

\displaystyle\overline{\partial}v-z\overline{v}=0

\displaystyle\iff v=0

\displaystyle\overline{\partial}v+z\overline{v}=0

\displaystyle\iff v=c\exp(-\frac{1}{2}\left|z\right|^{2})\text{ for $c\in\mathbb{R}$}.

Morally, this says that positive interior zeros and
(+,+)
zeros contribute one dimension to the kernel, but
(+,-)
zeros do not contribute to the kernel.

Proof
.

Observe that if we set
v^{\prime}=iv
, then

\partial_{s}v^{\prime}+i\partial_{t}v^{\prime}-z\overline{v^{\prime}}=i(\partial_{s}v+i\partial_{t}v+z\overline{v}),

and hence it suffices to study the equation
\overline{\partial}v-z\overline{v}=0
. Following
[
Wen20
, Proposition 5.22]
, we prove that the real part of
v
must vanish identically.

The second estimate from Lemma
31
implies that
\overline{\partial}{v}\in L^{2}
and
zv\in L^{2}
(proof: both
\left\lVert\overline{\partial}(\rho(z\delta)v)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}
and
\left\lVert z\rho(z\delta)v\right\rVert_{L^{2}}
remain bounded as
\delta\to 0
). The
L^{2}
elliptic estimates then imply that
v\in W^{1,2}
.

Let
y=\mathrm{Re}(v)
. Since
-\Delta v+2\overline{v}+\left|z\right|^{2}v=0
, we have

0=-\Delta y+(2+\left|z\right|^{2})y.

Apply
\mathrm{Re}\,\mu_{0}(\rho(z\delta)y,-)
to both sides, and integrate by parts to conclude

0=\int\rho(z\delta)\left|\overline{\partial}y\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{Re}\int\mu_{0}(\overline{\partial}(\rho(z\delta))\cdot y,\overline{\partial}y)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t+\int\rho(z\delta)(2+\left|z\right|^{2})\left|y\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t.

When we integrate by parts, we use
\partial_{t}y=0
(which holds in our case). We can now take the limit
\delta\to 0
, since we have verified that
\overline{\partial}y,zy\in L^{2}
, and conclude that

0=\left\lVert\overline{\partial}y\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\left\lVert(2+\left|z\right|^{2})^{1/2}y\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\implies y=0,

using
\overline{\partial}(\rho(z\delta))=O(\delta)
. It follows that any
L^{2}
solution of
\overline{\partial}v-z\overline{v}=0
as in the statement of the lemma is purely imaginary (and hence vanishes along the boundary, if the boundary exists).

We now observe that
v=i\exp(-\frac{1}{2}\left|z\right|^{2})
is certainly in
L^{2}
and solves
\overline{\partial}v-z\overline{v}=0
. Clearly any other solution
v^{\prime}
will be
v^{\prime}=gv
for some
holomorphic

g
; moreover, by what we have shown above,
g
must be real. There are no non-constant holomorphic functions defined on
\mathbb{C}
or
\mathbb{H}
which take only real values (the rank of the derivative matrix would be always
0
). Thus
g=c
must be a real number.

In the case when
v
is defined on
\overline{\mathbb{H}}
, the only possibility is
c=0
, since otherwise
v
would take non-zero imaginary values along the boundary.

Finally, we return to the second equation from the statement,
\partial_{s}v+i\partial_{t}v+z\overline{v}=0
. We have shown that this solution is conjugate to the first equation under multiplication by
i
. Therefore all solutions on the disk or half-plane are given by
v=c\exp(-\frac{1}{2}\left|z\right|^{2})
for some real
c
. In this case we
can
have non-zero
c
when
v
is defined on
\overline{\mathbb{H}}
.

This completes the proof.

Proposition 35

The operator

See
[

Proof
.

We summarize the strategy. First prove that

is eventually uniformly injective, in the sense that there are constants

so that

(42)

\sigma>\sigma_{0}\implies\left\lVert(\xi,\mathbf{c})\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq C\left\lVert D^{\sigma}_{\mathrm{st}}(\xi,\mathbf{c})\right\rVert_{L^{2}}.

Second, we show that
D^{\sigma}_{\mathrm{st}}
is eventually surjective. Then
D^{\sigma}_{\mathrm{st}}
is eventually an isomorphism, as desired.

We prove (
42
) by contradiction; suppose not and then conclude a sequence
\sigma_{n}\to\infty
and elements
(\xi_{n},\mathbf{c}_{n})
so that
\left\lVert(\xi_{n},\mathbf{c}_{n})\right\rVert_{L^{2}}=1
but
\left\lVert D^{\sigma_{n}}_{\mathrm{st}}(\xi_{n},\mathbf{c}_{n})\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\to 0
. Let’s agree to abbreviate

to avoid excessive subscripts during the course of this argument.

It is clear that

\left\lVert D^{\sigma}(\xi_{n})\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq\left\lVert D^{\sigma}_{\mathrm{st}}(\xi_{n},\mathbf{c}_{n})\right\rVert_{L^{2}}+C\left\lVert\mathbf{c}_{n}\right\rVert_{L^{2}},

for a fixed constant

. In particular, we can apply our compactness result to
\xi_{n}
and conclude that, after passing to a subsequence
\Pi_{\sigma}(\xi_{n})
converges to
\mathbf{k}
and
\mathrm{R}_{\sigma}(\xi_{n})
converges to

. However, since
\mathbf{k}_{\zeta}
form an orthogonal basis for
\ker D^{1}
we have

\mathbf{k}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{\zeta}\left\lVert\mathbf{k}_{\zeta}\right\rVert^{-2}\left\langle\Pi_{\sigma}(\xi_{n}),\mathbf{k}_{\zeta}\right\rangle\mathbf{k}_{\zeta}.

Therefore

implies that
\mathbf{k}=0

\Pi_{\sigma}(\xi_{n})
converges to zero in

, and since we know
\mathrm{R}_{\sigma}(\xi_{n})\to 0

converges to zero in

In order to contradict our initial assumption, it suffices to show that the inner product

\left\langle\Phi_{\sigma}(\mathbf{c}_{n}),D^{\sigma}(\xi_{n})\right\rangle=\left\langle\mathbf{c}_{n},\Pi_{\sigma}(D^{\sigma(\xi_{n})})\right\rangle=\sigma^{1/2}\left\langle\mathbf{c}_{n},D^{1}(\Pi_{\sigma}(\xi_{n}))\right\rangle+o(1)=o(1),

where we use the fact that

. This completes the proof by contradiction, and hence we have (

To prove that
D^{\sigma}_{\mathrm{st}}
is eventually surjective, we also argue by contradiction. Suppose that it were not. Then by standard properties of Hilbert spaces, we could find a unit norm sequence
\eta_{n},\mathbf{k}_{n}
(with

\left\langle D^{\sigma}(\xi)+\Phi_{\sigma}(\mathbf{c}),\eta_{n}\right\rangle+\left\langle\Pi_{\sigma}(\xi),\mathbf{k}_{n}\right\rangle=0\text{ for all $n,\xi,\mathbf{c}$},

Using
\Pi_{\sigma}^{*}=\Phi_{\sigma}
and
\mathbf{c}=0
, we conclude that
D^{\sigma,\ast}(\eta_{n})=-\Phi_{\sigma}(\mathbf{k}_{n})
. Since this is bounded in
L^{2}

\Pi_{\sigma}(\eta_{n})
converges to a solution of
\ker D^{1,\ast}
. However the assumption that

\left\langle\Phi_{\sigma}(\mathbf{c}),\eta_{n}\right\rangle=0,

for
all
