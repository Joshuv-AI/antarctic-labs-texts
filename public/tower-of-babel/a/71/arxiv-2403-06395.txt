A Linear Algebra approach to monomiality and operational methods
Luis Verde-Star

Abstract
We use linear algebraic methods to obtain general results about linear operators on a space of polynomials that we apply to the operators associated with a polynomial sequence by the monomiality property. We show that all such operators are differential operators with polynomial coefficients of finite of infinite order. We consider the monomiality operators associated with several classes of polynomial sequences, such as Appell and Sheffer, and also orthogonal polynomial sequences that include the Meixner, Krawtchouk, Laguerre, Meixner-Pollaczek, and Hermite families. AMS classification: 15A30, 33C45, 44A45. Keywords: Monomiality principle, orthogonal polynomials, Sheffer polynomial sequences, operational methods, generalized derivatives.



1
Introduction

Polynomials in one variable are among the simplest and most useful functions and
sequences of polynomials have been studied since the times of Euler and Bernoulli. In particular, sequences

\{p_{n}(t)\}_{n\geq 0}
in one real or complex variable such that
p_{n}
has degree
n
, for
n\geq 0
, which are called
polynomial sequences
, play a central role in several areas of mathematics and its applications. Properties of polynomial sequences related to combinatorics, numerical analysis, approximation theory, probability, coding theory, etc. have been extensively studied using diverse approaches. Around 1996 G. Dattoli and some collaborators
[
6
]
introduced operational methods based on the concept of monomiality, or quasi-monomiality. A polynomial sequence
\{p_{n}(t)\}_{n\geq 0}
has the
monomiality property
if there exist linear operators
\hat{P}
and
\hat{M}
that satisfy

\displaystyle\hat{P}p_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle np_{n-1}(t),\qquad n\geq 0,

(1)

\displaystyle\hat{M}p_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p_{n+1}(t),\qquad n\geq 0.

(2)

The concept of monomiality has its origin in the poweroids of Steffensen, which appear in a natural way in the theory of finite differences. See the recent book
[
11
, p. 62–64]
for more information about the genesis of the monomiality principle.

A large number of papers that use monomiality to study diverse types of polynomial sequences have been published during the last 25 years. The methods of the umbral calculus are often used in combination with monomiality. See
[
11
]
and the references therein. In some of those papers several generalizations of Hermite, Laguerre and other orthogonal polynomial families have been obtained.

In this paper, we use linear algebraic methods to obtain general results about linear operators on the vector spaces of polynomials and formal power series. Then we apply the general results to study the operators related with the monomiality principle. We obtain our results working with the elements of an algebra
\mathcal{L}
of infinite matrices that contains all the lower triangular infinite matrices. Each element of
\mathcal{L}
can be interpreted as a sequence of polynomials and also as a linear operator on the space of polynomials. Certain particular and simple elements of
\mathcal{L}
represent the operators of differentiation and multiplication by the independent variable on the vector spaces of polynomials and formal power series and may be considered as generators of the algebra
\mathcal{L}
.

Some results related with monomiality can be easily obtained in our algebraic setting. For example, the proof of the fact that every polynomial sequence has the monomiality property is almost trivial. We also show that all the lower triangular matrices in
\mathcal{L}
represent differential operators of infinite order with polynomial coefficients, and this also holds for all the operators
\hat{P}
and
\hat{M}
associated with a polynomial sequence.

Our main objective in this paper is to show that our linear algebraic approach can produce relevant results in the theory of monomiality and operational methods. For this purpose we present examples where we show how our methods are applied to several classes of polynomial sequences, such as those of Appell and Sheffer, and also some orthogonal polynomial sequences.

In our previous papers
[
18
]
and
[
20
]
we used our approach to study hypergeometric and basic hypergeometric orthogonal polynomial sequences, and in
[
17
]
we showed how to obtain linearization and connection coefficients for general polynomial sequences.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section 2 we introduce the algebra
\mathcal{L}
of generalized Hessenberg matrices and present the basic definitions and properties of the matrices that we use in the rest of the paper. In Section 3 we show that every lower triangular matrix can be written as a matrix that represents a differential operator of infinite order with polynomial coefficients and how this results applies to the monomiality operators of arbitrary polynomial sequences. The class of Appell polynomial sequences is considered in Section 4.
In Section 5 we consider polynomial sequences of binomial type, which correspond to modified composition matrices. Sheffer sequences are studied in Section 6.
In Section 7 we deal with a class of orthogonal polynomial sequences that includes the Meixner, Krawtchouk, Meixner-Pollaczek, Laguerre, and Hermite families of orthogonal polynomials. Finally, in Section 8 we present a simple way to extend the theory by replacing the differentiation operator with generalized derivatives, such as the Jackson
q
-derivative and Ward derivatives.

2
The algebra of generalized lower Hessenberg matrices

In this section we define an algebraic setting in which we can study polynomial sequences, sequences of formal power series, and linear operators acting on spaces of polynomials or formal power series. Here we define the algebra
\mathcal{L}
of generalized lower Hessenberg matrices and present some of their basic properties that will be used in the rest of the paper.
For additional information about the algebra
\mathcal{L}
see
[
18
]
and
[
19
]
.

An infinite matrix
A=[a_{j,k}]
, where the indices run over the non-negative integers and the entries are complex numbers is a
lower generalized Hessenberg matrix
if there exists an integer
m
such that
a_{j,k}=0
whenever

j-k<m
. We denote by
\mathcal{L}
the set of all such matrices.

We say that the entry
a_{j,k}
of
A
lies in the
n
-th diagonal of
A
if
j-k=n
. If
m>n
then the
m
-th diagonal lies below (to the left of) the
n
-th diagonal.
A nonzero element of
\mathcal{L}
is a
diagonal matrix
if all of its nonzero elements lie in a single diagonal.
If
A
is a nonzero element of
\mathcal{L}
and
m
is the minimum integer such that
A
has at least one nonzero entry in the
m
-th diagonal, then we say that
A
has
index

m
and write ind
(A)=m
. The index of the zero matrix is infinity, by definition.

It is clear that
\mathcal{L}
is a complex vector space with the usual addition of matrices and multiplication by scalars. It is also closed under matrix multiplication.
If
A
and
B
are in
\mathcal{L}
, with ind
(A)=m
and ind
(B)=n
, then the product
C=AB
is a well defined element of
\mathcal{L}
and

c_{i,k}=\sum_{j=k+n}^{i-m}a_{i,j}b_{j,k},\qquad i-k\geq m+n.

(3)

Note that ind
(AB)\geq m+n
and that the multiplication in
\mathcal{L}
involves only finite sums.

A sufficient, but not necessary, condition for
A
to have a two-sided inverse is that ind
(A)=0
and
a_{k,k}\neq 0
for
k\geq 0
. We denote by
\mathcal{G}
the set of all matrices that satisfy such condition. It is clear that
\mathcal{G}
is a group under matrix multiplication. The unit is the identity matrix
I
, whose entries on the 0-th diagonal are equal to 1 and all other entries are zero.

We define next some particular elements of
\mathcal{L}
that will be used often in the rest of the paper. Let
X
denote the diagonal matrix of index -1 with
X_{j,j+1}=1
for
j\geq 0
, and denote by
\hat{X}
the transpose of
X
. Note that
\hat{X}
is diagonal of index 1,
X\hat{X}=I
and
\hat{X}X=J_{0}
, where
J_{0}
is the diagonal matrix of index zero that has its entry in the position
(0,0)
equal to zero and its entries in positions
(j,j)
equal to 1 for
j\geq 1
.
Therefore,
\hat{X}
is a right-inverse for
X
, but it is not a left-inverse.

We say that a matrix
A\in\mathcal{L}
of index
m
is
monic
if all the entries of
A
in the diagonal of index
m
are equal to 1. Note that a monic matrix of index -1 is a unit lower Hessenberg matrix.

If
m
is a positive integer then
X^{m}
is the diagonal matrix of index
-m
with all its entries in the
(-m)
-th diagonal equal to 1. Analogously,
\hat{X}^{m}
is diagonal of index
m
and all its entries in the
m
-th diagonal are equal to 1.

We write
H=\hbox{Diag}(h_{0},h_{1},h_{2},\ldots)
to describe a diagonal matrix
H
of index zero with
H_{k,k}=h_{k}
for
k\geq 0
.
Let
\mathcal{D}
be the group of invertible diagonal matrices of index zero. Let us define
N=\hbox{Diag}(1,2,3,\ldots)
and
F=\hbox{Diag}(0!,1!,2!,\ldots)
. It is clear that
F
and
N
are elements of
\mathcal{D}
.

Define
D=F\hat{X}F^{-1}
. Note that
D
is diagonal of index one,
D_{k+1,k}=k+1
for
k\geq 0
,
XD=N
, and
DX=\hbox{Diag}(0,1,2,3,\ldots)
. Let
\hat{D}
be the transpose of
D
. Then
\hat{D}
is diagonal of index minus one. The notation
\hat{X}
and
\hat{D}
to denote the transposes of
X
and
D
will only be used for these two matrices.

We also have the commutation relation

XD-DX=I,

(4)

where
I
denotes the identity matrix
I=\hbox{Diag}(1,1,1,\ldots)
.

From
XD-DX=I
it is easy to obtain the Pincherle differentiation formulas

XD^{j}-D^{j}X=jD^{j-1},\qquad j\geq 0,

(5)

and

X^{j}D-DX^{j}=jX^{j-1},\qquad j\geq 0,

(6)

and using induction we obtain

X^{k}\dfrac{D^{j}}{j!}=\sum_{i=0}^{\min(j,k)}\binom{k}{i}\dfrac{D^{j-i}}{(j-i)!}X^{k-i},\qquad k,j\in\mathbb{N}.

(7)

If
B
is a diagonal matrix of index
m>0
then
B^{k}
is diagonal of index
km\geq 0
for
k\geq 0
and therefore
\sum_{k\geq 0}a_{k}B^{k}
is a well defined element of
\mathcal{L}
for any sequence of numbers
\{a_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
.

If
C
is a diagonal matrix of index
m<0
then
\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}B^{k}
is a banded element of
\mathcal{L}
of index at least equal to
nm
for any finite sequence of numbers
a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
.

The following almost trivial Proposition will be used later.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
A
be an element of the group
\mathcal{G}
and let
U
be an element of
\mathcal{L}
. Then we have

(i) There exists a unique
U_{r}
in
\mathcal{L}
such that
AU_{r}=UA
.

(ii) There exists a unique
U_{\ell}
in
\mathcal{L}
such that
U_{\ell}A=AU
.

Proof.
Let
U_{r}=A^{-1}UA
and
U_{\ell}=AUA^{-1}
. Then we have
AU_{r}=AA^{-1}UA=UA
and
U_{\ell}A=AUA^{-1}A=AU
.

\Box

2.1
Sequences of polynomials and formal power series

A matrix
A=[a_{k,j}]
of index
m
determines a sequence of polynomials
u_{k}(t)
defined by

u_{k}(t)=\sum_{j=0}^{k-m}a_{k,j}t^{j},\qquad k\geq 0.

(8)

That is, the entries in the
k
-th row of
A
are the coefficients of
u_{k}
with respect to the basis of monomials
\{t^{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
.
If
m>0
then
u_{j}(t)=0
for
0\leq j\leq m-1
, and if
A
is monic then each nonzero
u_{k}
is monic and has degree
k-m
.
If
A
is in the group
\mathcal{G}
then
m=0
and
u_{k}(t)
has degree
k
, for
k\geq 0
. That is,
\{u_{k}(t)\}_{k\geq 0}
is a
polynomial sequence
.

Every matrix
B=[b_{k,j}]
in
\mathcal{L}
determines a linear operator
\hat{B}
on the vector space
\mathcal{P}
of polynomials defined by

\hat{B}t^{k}=\sum_{j\geq 0}b_{k,j}t^{j},\qquad k\geq 0,

(9)

that is, the image of
t^{k}
is the polynomial associated with the
k
-th row of
B
.
Therefore, if
A
is in
\mathcal{L}
then multiplication on the right by
B
can be interpreted as applying the map
\hat{B}
to each of the polynomials associated with the rows of
A
.

Each column of a matrix
A\in\mathcal{L}
determines a formal power series
f_{j}(x)
defined by

f_{j}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k,j}x^{k},\qquad j\geq 0.

(10)

If
A
is in
\mathcal{G}
then
f_{j}(x)
has lower degree equal to
j
, for
j\geq 0
.

Every matrix
B=[b_{k,j}]
in
\mathcal{L}
determines a linear operator
\check{B}
on the vector space
\mathcal{F}
of formal power series
f(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}c_{j}x^{j}
.
The operator
\check{B}
is defined by

\check{B}x^{j}=\sum_{k\geq 0}b_{k,j}x^{k},\qquad j\geq 0.

(11)

If a series
f(x)=\sum_{k\geq 0}c_{k}x^{k}
is represented by the column vector
c=[c_{0},c_{1},c_{2},\ldots]^{\mathsf{T}}
then

\check{B}f(x)=\sum_{k\geq 0}e_{k}x^{k},

(12)

where

[e_{0},e_{1},e_{2},\ldots]^{\mathsf{T}}=Bc,

that is

e_{k}=\sum_{j\geq 0}b_{k,j}c_{j},\qquad k\geq 0.

These sums are finite since only a finite number of entries in the
k
-th row of
B
can be nonzero.
Therefore, if
A
is in
\mathcal{L}
then multiplication on the left by
B
can be interpreted as applying the map
\check{B}
to each of the formal power series associated with the columns of
A
.

2.2
The basic shifts and differentiation operators

Let
A=[a_{k,j}]
be in
\mathcal{L}
, let
u_{k}(t)
be the polynomials associated with the rows of
A
, and let
f_{j}(x)
be the formal power series associated with the columns of
A
. The matrices
X
and
\hat{X}
defined previously act as shifts on the entries of
A
as follows.

(i) The rows of
AX
are the rows of
A
shifted one place to the right, with initial entry equal to zero.

(ii) The rows of
A\hat{X}
are the rows of
A
shifted one place to the left.

(iii) The columns of
XA
are the columns of
A
shifted one place upwards.

(iv) The columns of
\hat{X}A
are the columns of
A
shifted one place downwards, with initial entry equal to zero.

From (i) we see that the polynomial corresponding to the
k
-th row of
AX
equals
tu_{k}(t)
.
From (ii) it follows that the polynomial of the
k
-th row of
A\hat{X}
is equal to
(u_{k}(t)-u_{k}(0))/t
.
From (iii) we see that the series of the
j
-th column of
XA
is equal to
(f_{j}(x)-f_{j}(0))/x
, and
(iv) says that the series of the
j
-th column of
\hat{X}A
is equal to
xf_{j}(x)
.

The matrices
D
and
\hat{D}
, defined previously, act on
A
as follows.
For
k\geq 0
and
j\geq 0
we have

(1)
(AD)_{k,j}=(j+1)a_{k,j+1}
.

(2)
(A\hat{D})_{k,j}=ja_{k,j-1}
.

(3)
(DA)_{k,j}=ka_{k-1,j}
.

(4)
(\hat{D}A)_{k,j}=(k+1)a_{k+1,j}
.

From (1) it follows that the polynomial of the
k
-th row of
AD
is equal to
u_{k}^{\prime}(t)
.
From (2) we see that that the polynomial of the
k
-th row of
A\hat{D}
is equal to
t(tu_{k}(t))^{\prime}
.
From (3) we see that the series of the
j
-th column of
DA
is equal to
x(xf_{j}(x))^{\prime}
, and (4) gives us that the series of the
j
-th column of
\hat{D}A
is
f_{j}^{\prime}(x)
.

We can also describe the action of the operators considered above by their effects on the sequence of polynomials
\{u_{k}(t)\}_{k\geq 0}
and the sequence of formal power series
\{f_{j}(x)\}_{j\geq 0}
associated with
A
. From property (iii) we see that multiplying
A
by
X
on the left sends
u_{k}(t)
to
u_{k+1}(t)
, for
k\geq 0
, and property (3) says that multiplying
A
on the left by
D
sends
u_{k}(t)
to
ku_{k-1}(t)
, for
k\geq 0
. In a similar way we can see from (2) that multiplying
A
by
\hat{D}
on the right sends
f_{j}(x)
to
jf_{j-1}(x)
, for
j\geq 0
, and from (ii) that multiplying
A
by
\hat{X}
on the right sends
f_{j}(x)
to
f_{j+1}(x)
, for
j\geq 0
.

3
Differential operators on the space of polynomials

In this Section we show that every linear operator on the space
\mathcal{P}
of polynomials that is represented by a lower triangular matrix is a differential operator of infinite order with polynomial coefficients. Such operators send
t^{n}
to a polynomial of degree at most
n
, for
n\geq 0
. We also present a representation of the dual basis of a polynomial sequence associated with a matrix
A
that uses the matrix
AXA^{-1}
, and some properties of the matrices
M
and
P
corresponding to a general element
A
of the group
\mathcal{G}
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
A=[a_{i,j}]
be a matrix of index zero. Then there exists a sequence of polynomials
\{p_{k}(t)\}_{k\geq 0}
such
p_{k}(t)
has degree at most equal to
k
, for
k\geq 0
, and

A=\sum_{k\geq 0}\dfrac{D^{k}}{k!}p_{k}(X).

(13)

Proof.
Let
r
and
k
be integers such that
k\geq r\geq 0
. The matrix
\frac{1}{k!}D^{k}X^{k-r}
is diagonal of index
r
and it is easy to see that its
(i,i-r)
entry equals
\binom{i}{k}
for
i\geq 0
. Define

d(k,r)=\sum_{j=r}^{k}\binom{k}{j}(-1)^{k-j}a_{j,j-r}.

(14)

The matrix

E_{r}=\sum_{k\geq 0}d(k,r)\dfrac{D^{k}}{k!}X^{k-r},\qquad r\geq 0,

is a well-defined diagonal matrix of index
r
. We show next that
E_{r}
coincides with the diagonal of index
r
of
A
. Let
i
be a non-negative integer.
Then we have

\displaystyle(E_{r})_{i,i-r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 0}d(k,r)\binom{i}{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 0}\sum_{j=r}^{k}\binom{k}{j}\binom{i}{k}(-1)^{k-j}a_{j,j-r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=r}^{i}a_{j,j-r}\sum_{k=j}^{i}\binom{i}{j}\binom{i-j}{k-j}(-1)^{k-j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a_{i,i-r}.

The last equality follows from

\sum_{k=j}^{i}\binom{i-j}{k-j}(-1)^{k-j}=(1-1)^{i-j}=\delta_{i,j}.

Therefore
E_{r}
coincides with the diagonal of index
r
of
A
and then we have

A=\sum_{r\geq 0}E_{r}=\sum_{r\geq 0}\sum_{k\geq 0}d(k,r)\dfrac{1}{k!}D^{k}X^{k-r}.

(15)

Define

p_{k}(t)=\sum_{r=0}^{k}d(k,r)t^{k-r},\qquad k\geq 0.

(16)

Changing the order of the sums in
15
we obtain

A=\sum_{k\geq 0}\dfrac{D^{k}}{k!}p_{k}(X).

\Box

Let
\{u_{k}(t)\}_{k\geq 0}
be the sequence of polynomials associated with
A
, that is,

u_{k}(t)=\sum_{j=0}^{k}a_{k,j}t^{j},\qquad k\geq 0.

Using the definitions
14
and
16
it is easy to obtain the inverse relations

\displaystyle p_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}u_{j}(t)(-t)^{k-j},

(17)

\displaystyle u_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}p_{j}(t)t^{k-j}.

(18)

These relations can be expressed in terms of generating functions as follows:

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}p_{k}(t)\dfrac{x^{k}}{k!}=\exp(-tx)\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}u_{k}(t)\dfrac{x^{k}}{k!}.

(19)

For
i\geq 0
the sequence of polynomials associated with
A\frac{D^{i}}{i!}
is
\left\{\frac{u_{j}^{(i)}(t)}{i!}\right\}_{j\geq 0}
. By the previous Theorem and
17
we have

A\dfrac{D^{i}}{i!}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{D^{k}}{k!}s_{k,i}(X),\qquad i\geq 0,

(20)

where

s_{k,i}(t)=\sum_{j=i}^{k}\binom{k}{j}\dfrac{u_{j}^{(i)}(t)}{i!}(-t)^{k-j}.

(21)

Note that
s_{k,i}(t)=0
if
i>k
.

Using
17
we can write
s_{k,i}(t)
in terms of the polynomials

as

s_{k,i}(t)=\sum_{j=0}^{i}\binom{k}{j}\dfrac{p_{k-j}^{(i-j)}(t)}{(i-j)!}.

(22)

Lower triangular matrices can be multiplied using their representation as differential operators given in Theorem
3.1
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
A
be a matrix of index zero and let
\{u_{k}(t)\}_{k\geq 0}
be its associated sequence of polynomials. Let

. Then we have

\displaystyle AB

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i\geq 0}\sum_{k\geq 0}\dfrac{D^{k}}{k!}s_{k,i}(X)q_{i}(X)

\Box

, where

. Then we have

From the Pincherle differentiation formulas

(26)

3.1
Monomiality operators of general polynomial sequences

Let
A
be an invertible matrix of index zero, that is, an element of the group
\mathcal{G}
. We obtain next some properties of the matrices
M=A^{-1}XA
and
P=A^{-1}DA
. We will see how
M
and
P
are related with the Pincherle derivatives of
A
.

Let
\{u_{k}(t)\}_{k\geq 0}
be the polynomial sequence associated with
A
and let
p_{k}(t)
for
k\geq 0
be the polynomials that satisfy

A=\sum_{k\geq 0}\dfrac{D^{k}}{k!}p_{k}(X).

For a general element
A
of
\mathcal{G}
the degree of
p_{k}(t)
is at most
k
. If
A
is a monic matrix in
\mathcal{G}
, or the diagonal of
A
of index zero is a multiple of the identity matrix, then, from the first equation in
17
, we can see that the degree of
p_{k}(t)
is less that
k
, for
k\geq 1.

Since
X\hat{X}=I
if

Let

.
Therefore

Since

Note that if

In an analogous way we obtain

(29)

Since

4
Monomiality and Appell polynomial sequences

Let
A=[a_{k,j}]
be a monic element of the group
\mathcal{G}
and let
\{u_{k}(t)\}_{k\geq 0}
be the polynomial sequence associated with the rows of
A
. Then
A
is invertible and
u_{k}(t)
is a monic polynomial of degree
k
for
k\geq 0
. In Section
2
we saw that
A\rightarrow XA
corresponds to the map
u_{k}(t)\rightarrow u_{k+1}(t)
, and also that
A\rightarrow DA
corresponds to
u_{k}(t)\rightarrow ku_{k-1}(t)
. Now we use the simple Proposition
2.1
. Let
M=A^{-1}XA
and
P=A^{-1}DA
. Then it is clear that
AM=XA
and
AP=DA
. Since
\{u_{k}(t)\}_{k\geq 0}
is a basis for the space of polynomials it is clear that multiplication of
A
on the right by the matrices
M
and
P
defines linear operators on the space
\mathcal{P}
of polynomials in
t
. If we use
\hat{M}
and
\hat{P}
to denote the corresponding linear operators then we have

\hat{M}u_{k}(t)=u_{k+1}(t),\quad\hbox{and}\quad\hat{P}u_{k}(t)=ku_{k-1}(t),\qquad k\geq 0.

(36)

Equation
36
means that the polynomial sequence
\{u_{k}(t)\}_{k\geq 0}
has the
monomiality property
.
Therefore we have shown that every monic polynomial sequence has the monomiality property.
It is easy to see that, with a suitable re-scaling, we can show that every polynomial sequence has the monomiality property. See
[
1
]
for a different proof of this result.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be the set of all formal power series of the form
g(t)=\sum_{k\geq 0}g_{k}t^{k}
, where the coefficients
g_{k}
are complex numbers. The set
\mathcal{F}
, with the usual addition and multiplication of power series, is a commutative algebra over the complex numbers.

Let
\mathcal{F}_{0}
be the subset of
\mathcal{F}
of all the series
g(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}g_{k}t^{k}
, with
g_{0}\neq 0
. It is well-known that
\mathcal{F}_{0}
is a commutative group under the usual multiplication of formal power series.

The map
g(t)\rightarrow g(\hat{X})=\sum_{k\geq 0}g_{k}\hat{X}^{k}
is an algebra homomorphism from
\mathcal{F}
into
\mathcal{L}
. The matrices
g(\hat{X})
are Toeplitz matrices of index zero and they are invertible if
g_{0}\neq 0
. The image of
\mathcal{F}_{0}
under such map is the group of invertible Toeplitz matrices of index zero, and it is a subgroup of
\mathcal{G}
. See
[
5
]
.

Let us recall that
D=F\hat{X}F^{-1}
, where
F=\hbox{Diag}(0!,1!,2!,\ldots)
. Then for every
g(t)
in
\mathcal{F}_{0}
we have
Fg(\hat{X})F^{-1}=g(D)
. Therefore the set of all matrices
g(D)
with
g(t)
in
\mathcal{F}_{0}
is isomorphic to the group of invertible Toeplitz matrices of index zero.

Let
f(t)=\sum_{k\geq 0}f_{k}\frac{t^{k}}{k!}
be an element of the group
\mathcal{F}_{0}
. We define the matrix

f(D)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}f_{k}\dfrac{D^{k}}{k!}.

(37)

The map
f(t)\rightarrow f(D)
is a linear homomorphism from the group
\mathcal{F}_{0}
into the group
\mathcal{G}
.
Therefore we have
(f(D))^{-1}=(1/f)(D).

Let
\{u_{k}(t)\}_{k\geq 0}
be the polynomial sequence associated with the rows of
f(D)
.
Then it is easy to see that

u_{k}(t)=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}f_{k-j}t^{j}=f(\partial_{t})t^{k},\qquad k\geq 0,

(38)

where
\partial_{t}
denotes differentiation with respect to
t
.
From
38
it follows that
u_{k}^{\prime}(t)=ku_{k-1}(t)
for
k\geq 0
.

We also have the generating function

f(z)e^{tz}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}u_{k}(t)\dfrac{z^{k}}{k!}.

(39)

Therefore
\{u_{k}(t)\}_{k\geq 0}
is an
Appell polynomial sequence
.

Since
f(D)
commutes with
D
it is clear that
P=(1/f)(D)Df(D)=D
.

Let
M=(1/f)(D)\,X\,f(D)
. Then we have
f(D)\,M=X\,f(D).

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
f(t)=\sum_{k\geq 0}f_{k}\frac{t^{k}}{k!}
, with
f_{0}\neq 0
, and let

h(t)=\frac{f^{\prime}(t)}{f(t)}
. Then we have

M=\left(\dfrac{1}{f}\right)(D)Xf(D)=X+h(D).

Proof.

By the Pincherle differentiation formula
XD^{k}-D^{k}X=kD^{k-1}
, for
k\geq 1
we get

Xf(D)-f(D)X=f^{\prime}(D)
and then

(1/f)(D)f^{\prime}(D)=(1/f)(D)Xf(D)-X=M-X,

and we obtain
M=h(D)+X
.

\Box

The previous Theorem shows that the linear operator
M
that acts on the polynomials
u_{k}(t)
has a representation as multiplication by
t
plus a differential operator of infinite order, or of finite order, when
h(t)
is a polynomial.

Let
h(t)
be as in Theorem
4.4
and let
\tilde{h}(t)\in\mathcal{F}
be an anti-derivative of
h(t)
, that is,
(\tilde{h})^{\prime}(t)=h(t)
. Then we have
f(t)=ce^{\tilde{h}(t)}
for some nonzero constant
c
and therefore we obtain
f(D)=ce^{\tilde{h}(D)}
. This result can be used to construct examples of matrices
f(D)
for which the operator
M
is equal to
X
plus a polynomial in
D
. If
h(t)
is a polynomial of degree
m-1
then
\tilde{h}(t)
is a polynomial of degree
m
,
f(D)=f_{0}e^{\tilde{h}(D)}
, and
M=X+h(D)
.
Let
\tilde{h}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{m}y_{k}t^{k}
. Then

u_{n}(t)=\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{m}y_{k}\partial_{t}^{k}\right)t^{n},\qquad n\geq 0,

(40)

and this equation shows that
u_{n}
is a polynomial function of
y_{1},y_{2},y_{3},\ldots,y_{m}
and
t
. A simple case is obtained with
\tilde{h}(t)=y_{1}t+y_{2}t^{2}
. In this case the polynomials
u_{n}(t)
are generalized Hermite polynomials with two parameters. See
[
3
]
,
[
7
]
and
[
8
]
for some related results about generalized Hermite polynomials.

We will find next the matrices
f(D)
for which
\{u_{k}(t)\}_{k\geq 0}
is an orthogonal polynomial sequence. Let
f(t)
be a series in
\mathcal{F}_{0}
and define
L=f(D)X(1/f)(D)
. Since the logarithmic derivative of
1/f
is equal to
-h(t)
, where
h(t)=f^{\prime}(t)/f(t)
, by Theorem
4.4
we get
L=X-h(D)
. From the equation
Lf(D)=f(D)X
we obtain the recurrence relation

tu_{n}(t)=u_{n+1}(t)+\sum_{j=0}^{n}L_{n,j}u_{j}(t),\qquad n\geq 0.

(41)

If
h(t)=h_{0}+h_{1}t
, with
h_{1}\neq 0
, then
L=X-h_{0}I-h_{1}D
is tridiagonal with nonzero entries in the diagonal of index one. In this case the recurrence relation
41
becomes the three-term recurrence relation

tu_{n}(t)+nh_{1}u_{n-1}(t)+h_{0}u_{n}(t)-u_{n+1}(t)=0,\qquad n\geq 1.

(42)

Therefore the polynomial sequence
\{u_{k}(t)\}_{k\geq 0}
determined by
f(D)
is orthogonal. If
h(t)
is a polynomial of degree one then, in terms of the coefficients of
f(t)
it is

h(t)=\dfrac{f_{1}}{f_{0}}+\dfrac{f_{0}f_{2}-f_{1}^{2}}{f_{0}^{2}}t,

(43)

and therefore the Jacobi matrix
L
is

L=X-\dfrac{f_{1}}{f_{0}}I-\dfrac{f_{0}f_{2}-f_{1}^{2}}{f_{0}^{2}}D,

(44)

and the series
f(t)
is

f(t)=f_{0}\exp\left(\dfrac{f_{1}}{f_{0}}t+\dfrac{f_{0}f_{2}-f_{1}^{2}}{2f_{0}^{2}}t^{2}\right)

(45)

In this case the orthogonal polynomial sequence
\{u_{k}(t)\}_{k\geq 0}
is a simple generalization of the Hermite polynomial sequence, since we have the parameters
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
. The coefficient
f_{0}
can be taken equal to one.

5
Modified composition matrices and polynomial sequences of binomial type

Let
f(z)=\sum_{k\geq 1}f_{k}z^{k}
, with
f_{1}=1
. Then
f^{n}(z)=z^{n}
plus terms with higher powers of
z
. Let
C_{f}
be the lower triangular matrix that has in the
n
-th column the coefficients of
f^{n}(z)
, that is,
(C_{f})_{k,n}
equals the coefficient of
z^{k}
in the series
f^{n}(z)
. Let
g(z)=\sum_{k\geq 0}g_{k}z^{k}
. Then

C_{f}[g_{0},g_{1},g_{2},\ldots]^{\mathsf{T}}=[h_{0},h_{1},h_{2},\ldots]^{\mathsf{T}},

where

h(z)=\sum_{k\geq 0}h_{k}z^{k}=g(f(z))=\sum_{k\geq 0}g_{k}f^{k}(z).

The matrix
C_{f}
is called the composition matrix of
f(z)
, it is an invertible lower triangular matrix and its inverse is the composition matrix of a series
\tilde{f}(z)
that satisfies
f(\tilde{f}(z))=z=\tilde{f}(f(z))
. The composition matrices form a non-commutative subgroup of
\mathcal{G}
. See
[
5
]
and
[
21
]
.

We introduce next a set of modified composition matrices.
Let
f(z)=\sum_{k\geq 1}f_{k}\frac{z^{k}}{k!}
, with
f_{1}=1
,
and let

G(z,t)=\sum_{k\geq 0}f^{k}(z)\dfrac{t^{k}}{k!}=\exp(tf(z)).

The polynomial sequence
\{u_{k}(t)\}_{k\geq 0}
is defined by the generating function:

G(z,t)=\sum_{k\geq 0}u_{k}(t)\dfrac{z^{k}}{k!}.

(46)

Since
G(z,t)G(z,x)=G(z,t+x)
it is easy to see that

u_{n}(t+x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}u_{k}(t)u_{n-k}(x),\qquad n\geq 0.

This means that
\{u_{k}(t)\}_{k\geq 0}
is a
polynomial sequence of binomial type
. Such sequences have been extensively studied. See
[
14
]
and
[
15
]
.

Let
B
be the lower triangular matrix associated with the sequence
\{u_{k}(t)\}_{k\geq 0}
. Then
B
is a monic element of the group
\mathcal{G}
. It is easy to verify that

B=FC_{f}F^{-1},

where
C_{f}
is the composition matrix of
f(z)=\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{f_{k}}{k!}z^{k}
and
F=\hbox{Diag}(0!,1!,2!,\ldots)
. Since the set of composition matrices is a subgroup of
\mathcal{G}
so is the set of all matrices of the form
FC_{f}F^{-1}
, where
C_{f}
is a composition matrix.

It is clear that
B^{-1}=FC_{\tilde{f}}F^{-1}
, where
\tilde{f}
is the inverse under composition of
f
. Therefore, since
F^{-1}XF=\hat{D}
we have

M=B^{-1}XB=FC_{\tilde{f}}F^{-1}XFC_{f}F^{-1}=FC_{\tilde{f}}\hat{D}C_{f}F^{-1}.

Let us compute the
n
-th column of
M
using the properties of the factors in the previous equation. We identify a power series in
z
with the infinite column vector of its coefficients.
Let
e_{n}
be the infinite column vector that has its
n
-th coordinate equal to 1 and all the other coordinates equal to zero. If we identify
e_{n}
with the series
z^{n}
then
F^{-1}z^{n}=\frac{z^{n}}{n!}
and
C_{f}F^{-1}z^{n}=\frac{f^{n}(z)}{n!}
.

Let us recall that multiplication of a column vector that corresponds to a series
g(z)
on the left by
\hat{D}
gives the vector that corresponds to
g^{\prime}(z)
. Then we have

\hat{D}\frac{f^{n}(z)}{n!}=f^{\prime}(z)\dfrac{f^{n-1}(z)}{(n-1)!},

and multiplication on the left by
C_{\tilde{f}}
yields

C_{\tilde{f}}\left(f^{\prime}(z)\dfrac{f^{n-1}(z)}{(n-1)!}\right)=f^{\prime}(\tilde{f}(z))\dfrac{z^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}.

Since
f(\tilde{f}(z))=z
we have
f^{\prime}(\tilde{f}(z))=1/{\tilde{f}^{\prime}(z)}.

Let

\dfrac{1}{\tilde{f}^{\prime}(z)}=\sum_{k\geq 0}a_{k}\dfrac{z^{k}}{k!}.

Finally, the
n
-th column of
M
corresponds to the series

Mz^{n}=F\left(\sum_{k\geq 0}a_{k}\dfrac{z^{k+n-1}}{k!(n-1)!}\right)=\sum_{k\geq 0}\binom{k+n-1}{k}a_{k}z^{k+n-1},

and therefore the entries in the diagonal of index
k-1
of
M
are

M_{n+k-1,n}=\binom{n+k-1}{k}a_{k},

and, since the
0
-th column of
M
equals zero, this implies that

M=\left(\sum_{k\geq 0}a_{k}\dfrac{D^{k}}{k!}\right)X=\left(\dfrac{1}{\tilde{f}^{\prime}}\right)(D)\,X.

(47)

Note that
M
determines a differential operator of infinite order with coefficients that are polynomials of degree one.

Now let
P=B^{-1}DB
. Then

P=FC_{\tilde{f}}F^{-1}DFC_{f}F^{-1}.

Since
F^{-1}DF=\hat{X}
the
n
-th column of
P
is given by

Pz^{n}=FC_{\tilde{f}}\hat{X}\dfrac{f^{n}(z)}{n!}=FC_{\tilde{f}}\left(\dfrac{zf^{n}(z)}{n!}\right),

and then

Pz^{n}=F\left(\tilde{f}(z)\dfrac{z^{n}}{n!}\right).

Let
\tilde{f}(z)=\sum_{k\geq 1}g_{k}\frac{z^{k}}{k!}
. Then we have

Pz^{n}=F\sum_{k\geq 1}g_{k}\dfrac{z^{k+n}}{k!n!}=\sum_{k\geq 1}g_{k}\binom{k+n}{n}z^{k+n}.

This equation shows that, for
k\geq 1
, the entries of the diagonal of index
k
of
P
are given by

P_{n+k,n}=g_{k}\binom{n+k}{k},\qquad k\geq 1,n\geq 0.

Therefore we have

P=\sum_{k\geq 1}g_{k}\dfrac{D^{k}}{k!}=\tilde{f}(D).

(48)

This equation and
47
give us immediately

PM=\left(\dfrac{\tilde{f}}{\tilde{f}^{\prime}}\right)(D)\,X.

6
Sheffer polynomial sequences

In this Section we consider matrices associated with polynomial sequences called Sheffer sequences
[
14
]
. The Sheffer polynomial sequences have been extensively studied using diverse approaches. See
[
9
]
,
[
12
]
, and
[
15
]
.

Let
g(t)=\sum_{k\geq 0}g_{k}\frac{t^{k}}{k!}
with
g_{0}\neq 0
and let
A=g(D)
. Let
f(z)=\sum_{k\geq 1}f_{k}\frac{z^{k}}{k!}
, let
C_{f}
be the composition matrix of
f
, and let
B=FC_{f}F^{-1}
.

The matrix
S=BA
is called Sheffer matrix
[
4
]
. The set of all Sheffer matrices is a group and it is isomorphic to the group of the Riordan matrices, which are of the form
C_{f}g(\hat{X})
. Using the results in the previous Sections we can find easily the matrices
M_{S}=S^{-1}XS
and
P_{S}=S^{-1}DS
, expressed as matrices of differential operators.

Let
\tilde{f}
be the inverse under composition of
f
. By
47

we have

M_{S}=S^{-1}XS=A^{-1}B^{-1}XBA=\left(\dfrac{1}{g}\right)(D)\left(\dfrac{1}{\tilde{f}^{\prime}}\right)(D)Xg(D).

From the Pincherle differentiation formula
5
we get
Xg(D)-g(D)X=g^{\prime}(D)
and therefore

\displaystyle M_{S}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\dfrac{1}{g\tilde{f}^{\prime}}\right)(D)\left(g^{\prime}(D)+g(D)X\right)

(49)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\dfrac{g^{\prime}}{g\tilde{f}^{\prime}}\right)(D)+\left(\dfrac{1}{\tilde{f}^{\prime}}\right)(D)X

(50)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\dfrac{1}{\tilde{f}^{\prime}}\right)(D)\left(\left(\dfrac{g^{\prime}}{g}\right)(D)+X\right).

(51)

By
48
we have

P_{S}=S^{-1}DS=A^{-1}B^{-1}DBA=\left(\dfrac{1}{g}\right)(D)\tilde{f}(D)g(D)=\tilde{f}(D).

Therefore

P_{S}M_{S}=\left(\dfrac{\tilde{f}}{\tilde{f}^{\prime}}\right)(D)\left(\left(\dfrac{g^{\prime}}{g}\right)(D)+X\right).

Since the group of Riordan matrices is isomorphic to the group of Sheffer matrices we can easily study the monomiality properties of the polynomial sequences associated with Riordan matrices.

7
Some orthogonal polynomial sequences

In this Section we consider a set of orthogonal polynomial sequences that are associated with invertible lower triangular matrices
A
for which the matrices
M=A^{-1}XA
and
P=A^{-1}DA
have a relatively simple structure.

Let us define polynomials
f(t)=f_{0}+f_{1}t+f_{2}t^{2}
and
g(t)=g_{0}+g_{1}t
, where
g_{1}\neq 0
, and the matrix

B=Dg(X)+\dfrac{D^{2}}{2}f(X).

(52)

Note that
B
is a banded matrix of index zero. Define

H=g_{1}DX+f_{2}\dfrac{D^{2}}{2}X^{2}.

(53)

The matrix
H
is diagonal of index zero and coincides with the diagonal of index zero of
B
.
Let
A
be a monic matrix of index zero that satisfies the equation
AB=HA
.
Then
L=AXA^{-1}
is a tridiagonal matrix of index -1 and the polynomial sequence
\{u_{k}(t)\}_{k\geq 0}
associated with
A
is orthogonal with respect to a linear functional on the space of polynomials. See
[
18
]
and
[
20
]
.

The collection of all the polynomial sequences associated with matrices
A
constructed as described above includes almost all the hypergeometric orthogonal polynomial sequences. See
[
10
]
. We consider next the subset of the polynomial sequences obtained as described above when the coefficient
f_{2}
is equal to zero.

If
f_{2}=0
the tridiagonal Jacobi matrix
L=AXA^{-1}
is

L=X-\dfrac{g_{0}}{g_{1}}I-\dfrac{f_{1}}{g_{1}}DX+\dfrac{f_{1}g_{0}-f_{0}g_{1}}{2g_{1}^{2}}D+\dfrac{f_{1}^{2}}{4g_{1}^{2}}D^{2}X.

(54)

Here we have the coefficients
f_{0},f_{1},g_{0},g_{1}
as parameters. Giving appropriate values to the parameters we can obtain the Jacobi matrices of the Meixner-Pollaczek, Meixner, Krawtchouk, Laguerre, and Hermite orthogonal polynomial families. See
[
10
]
and
[
16
]
.

When
f_{2}=0
the matrix
M=A^{-1}XA
is tetradiagonal of index -1 and

M=w_{0}(X)+Dw_{1}(X)+\frac{1}{2}D^{2}w_{2}(X),

(55)

where

\displaystyle w_{0}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{g_{0}}{g_{1}}+t,

(56)

\displaystyle w_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{f_{0}g_{1}+f_{1}g_{0}}{2g_{1}^{2}}+\dfrac{f_{1}}{g_{1}}t,

(57)

\displaystyle w_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{f_{0}f_{1}}{2g_{1}^{2}}+\dfrac{f_{1}^{2}}{2g_{1}^{2}}t.

(58)

The linear operator on the space of polynomials determined by
M
is multiplication by the variable
t
plus a differential operator of order two with polynomial coefficients of order one.

The matrix
P=A^{-1}DA
in this case is

P=\sum_{k\geq 0}\left(\dfrac{-f_{1}}{2g_{1}}\right)^{k}D^{k+1}=D\exp\left(\dfrac{-f_{1}}{2g_{1}}D\right).

(59)

Note that the linear operator on the space of polynomials determined by
P
is differentiation followed by a translation operator, and such operations commute.

It is well-known that a change in the value of the parameter
g_{1}
corresponds to a linear change of variable in the orthogonal polynomials
u_{k}(t)
. Two orthogonal polynomial sequences that differ by a linear change of variable are usually considered as equivalent sequences.

The case of the generalized Laguerre polynomials is discussed in the next section, where the corresponding matrix is obtained as a series of powers of a generalized differentiation operator.

In the cases with
f_{2}\neq 0
the matrices
M
and
P
are more complicated, but can be obtained with the methods presented above.

8
Series of generalized derivatives

In this Section we consider a class of polynomial sequences determined by the rows of invertible matrices that are power series of a generalized derivative
D_{c}
. We will show that the results of Section
4
can be easily generalized and can be applied to a larger class of polynomial sequences. The generalized derivatives that we consider here are usually called Ward derivatives.

Let
c_{1},c_{1},c_{3},\ldots
be a sequence on nonzero complex numbers. Define the function
f_{c}
on the set on non-negative integers by
f_{c}(n)=c_{1}c_{2}\cdots c_{n}
, for
n\geq 1
, and
f_{c}(0)=1
. We call
f_{c}
the
c
-factorial function.
The
c
-binomial coefficients are defined by

cbin(n,k)=\dfrac{f_{c}(n)}{f_{c}(k)f_{c}(n-k)},\qquad n\geq k\geq 0.

Define the diagonal matrix of index zero

F_{c}=\hbox{Diag}\left(\dfrac{f_{c}(0)}{0!},\dfrac{f_{c}(1)}{1!},\dfrac{f_{c}(2)}{2!},\ldots\right).

(60)

The
c
-derivative matrix
D_{c}
is defined by
D_{c}=F_{c}DF_{c}^{-1}
. It is a diagonal matrix of index one and its entries are
(D_{c})_{k+1,k}=c_{k+1}
, for
k\geq 0
.

The
c
-shift matrix
X_{c}
is defined by
X_{c}=F_{c}XF_{c}^{-1}
. It is a diagonal matrix of order -1 and its entries are
(X_{c})_{k,k+1}=\frac{k+1}{c_{k+1}}
, for
k\geq 0
.
Since
XD-DX=I
, it is clear from the definitions of
Dc
and
X_{c}
that
X_{c}D_{c}-D_{c}X_{c}=I
.

Let

g(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}g_{k}\dfrac{t^{k}}{f_{c}(k)},

be such that
g_{0}\neq 0
, that is,
g(t)
is in the group
\mathcal{F}_{0}
.
Then the matrix
g(D_{c})
is a well-defined element of the group
\mathcal{G}
.
Let
\{u_{k}(t)\}_{k\geq 0}
be the polynomial sequence determined by the rows of
g(D_{c})
. Then we have

u_{n}(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}cbin(n,k)g_{n-k}t^{k},\qquad n\geq 0.

(61)

The
c
-exponential series is

e_{c}(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{t^{k}}{f_{c}(k)}.

(62)

It is easy to verify that the sequence
\{u_{k}(t)\}_{k\geq 0}
has the following generating function

g(z)e_{c}(tz)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}u_{k}(t)\dfrac{z^{k}}{f_{c}(k)}.

(63)

We say that
\{u_{k}(t)\}_{k\geq 0}
is a sequence of generalized Appell type.

Since
g(D_{c})
commutes with
D_{c}
we have
P=(1/g)(D_{c})D_{c}g(D_{c})=D_{c}.

We define the matrix
M=(1/g)(D_{c})X_{c}g(D_{c})
. It is clear that
g(D_{c})M=X_{c}g(D_{c})
and therefore the operator
M
sends the polynomial
u_{k}(t)
, which corresponds to the
k
-th row of
g(D_{c})
, to
\frac{k+1}{c_{k+1}}u_{k+1}(t)
, that corresponds to the
k
-th row of
X_{c}G(D_{c})
.

Since we have the identity
X_{c}D_{c}-D_{c}X_{c}=I
, the proof of the following Theorem is analogous to the proof of Theorem
4.4
.

Theorem 8.5
.

Let
h(t)=\frac{g^{\prime}(t)}{g(t)}
. Then we have
M=X_{c}+h(D_{c})
.

As in the case of matrices that are power series in
D
, the matrices
g(D_{c})
for which the operator
M
is equal to
X_{c}
plus a polynomial in
D_{c}
are those for which the logarithmic derivative of
g(t)
is a polynomial.

Let
L=g(D_{c})X_{c}(1/g)(D_{c})
. In this case we also have
L-X_{c}=-(M-X_{c})
, since the logarithmic derivative of
1/g(t)
is the negative of the logarithmic derivative of
g(t)
. Therefore
L
is tridiagonal if
g^{\prime}(t)/g(t)
is a polynomial of degree one. In such case we obtain

L=X_{c}-\dfrac{g_{1}}{g_{0}c_{1}}I-\dfrac{2c_{1}g_{0}g_{2}-c_{2}g_{1}^{2}}{c_{1}^{2}c_{2}g_{0}^{2}}D_{c}.

(64)

This matrix satisfies the equation
Lg(D_{c})=g(D_{c})X_{c}
, which looks like the equation that gives the three-term recurrence relation of an orthogonal polynomial sequence, but the linear operator on the rows of
g(D_{c})
determined by multiplication on the right by
X_{c}
is, in the general case, not equal to the operator of multiplication by the variable
t
. That occurs only when
D_{c}=D
, that is, when
c_{k}=k
, for
k\geq 1
. Therefore, if
D_{c}\neq D
then the tridiagonal matrix
L
given in
64
is not the Jacobi matrix of an orthogonal polynomial sequence.

If the matrix
L
is tridiagonal then the row polynomials
u_{k}(t)
of
g(D_{c})
are related to an orthogonal polynomial sequence
\{v_{k}(t)\}_{k\geq 0}
as we show next. Let
f_{k}=(k!/f_{c}(k))g_{k}
, for
k\geq 0
, and let

f(D)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{f_{k}}{k!}D^{k}.

Then we have
g(D_{c})=F_{c}f(D)F_{c}^{-1}
. Since the logarithmic derivative of
g(t)
is a polynomial of degree one, so is the logarithmic derivative of
f(t)
and by the results in the previous Section the row polynomials
v_{k}(t)
of the matrix
f(D)
are orthogonal. If
v_{k}(t)=\sum_{j=0}^{k}a_{k,j}t^{j}
then, from
g(D_{c})=F_{c}f(D)F_{c}^{-1}
we obtain

u_{k}(t)=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\dfrac{f_{c}(k)j!}{f_{c}(j)k!}a_{k,j}t^{j},\qquad k\geq 0.

(65)

Therefore the polynomials
u_{k}(t)
determined by the matrix
g(D_{c})
are obtained by a modification of a sequence of generalized Hermite polynomials.

Let us note that the results in this Section hold for any sequence
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3},\ldots
of nonzero numbers.
We consider next some examples that correspond to particular choices of the sequence
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3},\ldots
.

Example 1.

If we take
c_{k}=k^{2}
, for
k\geq 1
, then
(D_{c})_{k+1,k}=(k+1)^{2}
, for
k\geq 0
, and
(X_{c})_{k,k+1}=1/(k+1)
, for
k\geq 0
. Let us note that
X_{c}D=I
, that is
Xc
is a left inverse of
D
. Note also that
D_{c}=DXD
.
In this case we have
f_{c}(n)=(1)(4)(9)\cdots(n^{2})
, for
n\geq 1
.

Let

g(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}g_{k}\dfrac{t^{k}}{f_{c}(k)},

where
g_{0}\neq 0
, be an element of the group
\mathcal{F}_{0}
. Define the matrix

g(D_{c})=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}g_{k}\dfrac{D_{c}^{k}}{f_{c}(k)},

and let
\{u_{k}(t)\}_{k\geq 0}
be the corresponding polynomial sequence.

If
g(t)
is such that its logarithmic derivative
h(t)
is a polynomial of degree one then

h(t)=\dfrac{g_{1}}{g_{0}}+\dfrac{g_{0}g_{2}-2g_{1}^{2}}{2g_{0}^{2}}t,

and therefore

M=X_{c}+\dfrac{g_{1}}{g_{0}}I+\dfrac{g_{0}g_{2}-2g_{1}^{2}}{2g_{0}^{2}}D_{c},

and the matrix
L
is

L=X_{c}-\dfrac{g_{1}}{g_{0}}I-\dfrac{g_{0}g_{2}-2g_{1}^{2}}{2g_{0}^{2}}D_{c}.

We also have

g(t)=g_{0}\exp\left(\dfrac{g_{1}}{g_{0}}t+\dfrac{g_{0}g_{2}-2g_{1}^{2}}{4g_{0}^{2}}t^{2}\right).

(66)

The polynomial sequence
\{u_{k}(t)\}_{k\geq 0}
obtained in this case satisfies the relation

\dfrac{2g_{1}^{2}-g_{0}g_{2}}{2g_{0}^{2}}k^{2}u_{k-1}(t)-\frac{g_{1}}{g_{0}}u_{k}(t)+\dfrac{1}{k+1}u_{k+1}(t)=J_{c}u_{k}(t),\qquad k\geq 1,

(67)

where
J_{c}
denotes the indefinite integration operator, with integration constant equal to zero, which is the linear operator on the space of polynomials that corresponds to multiplication on the right by
X_{c}
.
Notice that
67
does not have the form of the three-term recurrence relation of an orthogonal polynomial sequence. The polynomials
u_{k}(t)
are related to orthogonal polynomials
v_{k}(t)
of Hermite-type by
65
. If we set
g_{0}=1,g_{1}=z
, and
g_{2}=2z^{2}+4y
then

g(t)=\exp\left(zt+yt^{2}\right),

the polynomials
u_{k}(t)
are functions of
y,z
, and
t
, and
M=X_{c}+zI+yD_{c}
. We also have
u_{0}(t)=1
, and

u_{1}(t)=t+z,\ u_{2}(t)=t^{2}+4zt+2z^{2}+4y,\ u_{3}(t)=t^{3}+9zt^{2}+(18z^{2}+36y)t+6z^{3}+36yz.

Example 2.

Let
c_{k}=ak+yk^{2}
for
k\geq 1
, where
a
and
y
are complex numbers and
a\neq 0
.
Let
U
be the diagonal matrix of index one with entries
U_{k+1,k}=(k+1)^{2}
, for
k\geq 0
. Then we have
D_{c}=a(D+yU)
.
Notice that
D_{c}
is diagonal of index one.

Let us define

A=\exp(-D_{c}).

(68)

Then
A
is a monic element of
\mathcal{G}
and
A^{-1}=\exp(D_{c})
.
A simple computation yields

L=AXA^{-1}=X+a(1+y)I+D(a^{2}y(1+y)+2ayX)+a^{2}y^{2}D^{2}X,

and

M=A^{-1}XA=X-a(1+y)I+D(a^{2}y(1+y)-2ayX)+a^{2}y^{2}D^{2}X.

Note that in this case
L
and
M
are tridiagonal and hence the polynomial sequences associated with
A
and
A^{-1}
are orthogonal. Note also that
M
is obtained from
L
by substitution of
a
with
-a
.

If
y=1/a
then
L=(1+a)I+D((1+a)I+2X)+D^{2}X
is the Jacobi matrix of the monic Laguerre polynomials. See
[
10
]
. Therefore the polynomial sequence
\{u_{k}(t)\}_{k\geq 0}
associated with
A
is a generalization of the Laguerre polynomials that has
a
and
y
as parameters. In
[
16
]
some results about Laguerre polynomials are obtained using umbral and operational methods. See also
[
13
]
.

In this case the matrix
P=A^{-1}DA
is

P=\sum_{k\geq 0}a^{k}y^{k}D^{k+1},

and thus
P=f(D)
, where
f(t)=t/(1-ayt)
.

This example can be easily generalized by taking
c_{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}a_{j}k^{j}
, but the polynomial sequences obtained with such
c_{k}
are not orthogonal if
m>2
.

Example 3.

Now we consider the Jackson
q
-derivative
\mathcal{D}_{q}
, defined by

\mathcal{D}_{q}p(t)=\dfrac{p(qt)-p(t)}{qt-t},\qquad p(t)\in\mathcal{P}.

(69)

Let
q
be a complex number that is not a root of unity, and define the basic numbers, or
q
-numbers, by

[n]=\dfrac{q^{n}-1}{q-1},\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z}.

(70)

Let
c_{k}=[k]
, for
k\geq 1.
Then the
c
-factorial function is
f_{c}(n)=[1][2][3]\cdots[n]
, which we denote by
[n]!
,
the matrix
D_{c}
is diagonal of index one with entries
(D_{c})_{k+1,k}=[k+1]
, for
k\geq 0
, and
X_{c}
is the diagonal matrix of index -1 with entries
(X_{c})_{k,k+1}=(k+1)/[k+1]
, for
k\geq 0
. Let
\tilde{D}_{c}
be the diagonal of index -1 with entries
(\tilde{D}_{c})_{k,k+1}=1/[k+1]
, for
k\geq 0
. Then
\tilde{D}_{c}D_{c}=I
and
X_{c}=XD\tilde{D}_{c}=\tilde{D}_{c}DX
.

Let
f_{0}=1
,

f(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}f_{k}\dfrac{t^{n}}{[n]!},

and

f(D_{c})=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}f_{k}\dfrac{D_{c}^{n}}{[n]!}.

Then the matrix
f(D_{c})
is lower triangular and invertible and its inverse is
(1/f)(D_{c})
. In this case we have
P=D_{c}
and
M=X_{c}+h(D_{c})
, where
h(t)
is the logarithmic derivative of
f(t)
.

A simple example is obtained when
h(t)
is a polynomial of degree one. In this case we have

h(t)=f_{1}+\dfrac{2f_{2}-[2]f_{1}^{2}}{[2]}t,

and then

f(t)=\exp\left(f_{1}t+\dfrac{2f_{2}-[2]f_{1}^{2}}{2[2]}t^{2}\right),

and

M=X_{c}+f_{1}I+\dfrac{2f_{2}-[2]f_{1}^{2}}{[2]}D_{c}.

The corresponding polynomials
u_{k}(t)
have
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
as parameters and can be expressed as a transformation of generalized Hermite polynomials using
65
.
