Percolation of terraces, and enhancements for the orthant model
Mark Holmes, Thomas S. Salisbury

Abstract
We study a model of an i.i.d. random environment in general dimensions d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 , where each site is equipped with one of two environments. The model comes with a parameter p p which governs the frequency of the first environment, and for each dimension d d there is a critical parameter p c ​ ( d ) p_{c}(d) at which there is a phase transition for the geometry of a particular connected cluster (the cluster is infinite for all p p ). We use the celebrated methodology of enhancements in this novel setting to prove that p c ​ ( d ) p_{c}(d) is strictly monotone in d d for this model. To do so we study the discrete geometry and percolation theory of higher-dimensional structures called terraces.



Keywords:
percolation, enhancement, random environment, orthant model, critical point, terraces, discrete geometry.

Introduction

In this paper, we study certain random directed subgraphs of the lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
(called the
orthant model
and the
half-orthant
model - see Examples
1
and
2
below) in which (with probability
p
) a vertex will assume a configuration that blocks immediate passage to sites below it. This leads to a phase transition in which passage downwards is feasible for small
p
but not for large
p
. These random directed graphs have been studied earlier in the case
d=2
, in
[
9
]
and
[
10
]
. More recently,
[
13
]
defined a critical point
p_{c}(d)
for these (and other) models, while
[
14
]
examined the asymptotic shape of the set of sites in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
that can be reached from the origin
o
when
p
is large. Beekenkamp
[
3
]
extended the validity of the shape theorem for
p
down to a (potentially different) critical value
p^{\prime}_{c}(d)
. Random walks in various directed graphs including these kinds were studied in
[
11
]
and
[
12
]
.

The fact that
p_{c}(d)
is monotone increasing in
d
is straightforward.
The main result of this paper is that
p_{c}(d)
is
strictly
monotone in
d\geq 2
- see Theorem

. This turns out to be highly non-trivial and requires a detailed excursion into discrete geometry, because the argument centres on the study of percolation for higher dimensional structures called

.

1.1
Notation and Results

Let
d\geq 2

Example 2

The orthant model and its phase transition in 2 dimensions are studied in

The following result concerning the half-orthant model is proved in

Theorem 0

(
[

)

For the half-orthant model (Example

(I)

(II)

It is shown in
[

that in any dimension the orthant and half-orthant models share the same

), in the sense that

Beekenkamp’s

In the next section we will define a geometric class of subsets of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
that we call
terraces
. We can then express the above phase transition in terms of percolation of terraces as follows.

Theorem 1
.

The critical point
p_{c}(d)
of the orthant or half-orthant model is the infimum of those
p
such that there a.s. exists a terrace
\Lambda
with
\Lambda\subset\Omega_{1}
.

This paper is therefore a contribution to the general program of extending standard percolation techniques to such higher-dimensional structures. For other examples of high-dimensional percolation structures, see
[
6
,
7
]
. Our main result is the following.

Theorem 2
.

For the orthant or half-orthant model
p_{c}(d)<p_{c}(d+1)
.

We will prove Theorem
2
via the methodology of enhancements, introduced by Aizenman and Grimmett in
[
1
]
. This is the “standard” methodology for proving strict inequality results for critical points in percolation models (see e.g.
[
15
,
2
]
). Application of this methodology usually requires verifying some combinatorial statement. Sometimes the statement in question is straightforward (or even obvious), while elsewhere it can be extremely challenging (see e.g.
[
8
]
, where strict inequality between the critical values of bond and site percolation is established in various lattices). The full story of enhancements of site percolation on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is complicated and currently
incomplete (see e.g.
[
2
]
).

Our use of enhancements involves the deformation of terraces, which poses a significant challenge.
Sections
2.1
–
2.3
are entirely geometric, establishing deterministic facts about terraces that will be required in Section
3
, where the main theorem is proved. The approach in Section
3
is to use a slab to interpolate between
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
\mathbb{Z}^{d+1}
. We analyze this slab by perturbing the
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
model at points
V_{d}
of a sub-lattice, which is taken to be sparse in order to ensure sufficient independence. The enhancement approach then requires us to estimate the number of pivotal sites in
V_{d}
.

We will do that by modifying a given terrace locally in order to pass through some point of
V_{d}
. The obstacle to achieving this is that our terraces can be tightly constrained by boundary conditions, which can in some cases impose a high degree of rigidity on the terraces allowed. A consequence of the ideas of Section
2
, that illustrates this issue, is that the kinds of boundary configurations that make the terrace rigid must already force it to go through points of
V_{d}
. Our approach to this problem is to show that the terraces already contain (or can be deformed locally in order to contain) long linear segments.

Remark 3
.

An alternate approach which would also establish our main result (while bypassing the interpolation via slabs) would be to fix the dimension
d
and to consider two models, one with sets
E_{0},F_{0}
and the other with sets
E_{1},F_{1}
, satisfying the following:

•

F_{0}=\mathcal{E}
and
\{e_{1}\}\subset E_{0}\subsetneq\mathcal{E}_{+}
, and

•

F_{1}=\mathcal{E}
and
E_{0}\subsetneq E_{1}\subset\mathcal{E}_{+}
,

Strict monotonicity would follow from establishing that the critical points for the above two models are not equal. However, this approach seems to require an understanding of a more general class of geometric objects than the terraces considered here.

In this section, we examine corners of terraces. We will really use local versions of these results (stated in the next section), but we will prove the terrace versions first, as they are both simpler and of more intrinsic interest.

Lemma 13
.

Let
\Lambda
be a terrace, with a corner at

. Then:

(i)

\Lambda_{+}\setminus\{z\}
is good;

(ii)

\Lambda\setminus\{z\}\subset\lambda_{\Lambda_{+}\setminus\{z\}}\subset(\Lambda\setminus\{z\})\cup\{z+e_{j}:j\in[d]\}
.

Proof.

\Lambda_{+}\setminus\{z\}
is bounded below because
\Lambda_{+}
is, and it is saturated because
\Lambda_{+}
was saturated and
\Lambda_{+}\setminus\{z\}
contains
z+e_{j}
for each
j\in[d]
. So let us prove that
\Lambda_{+}\setminus\{z\}
is solid above.

Let
x\in\Lambda_{+}\setminus\{z\}
and
y\in x_{+}
. Then
y\in\Lambda_{+}
, and we need only show that
y\neq z
. So assume
z=y
. Then
z\in x_{+}
and
x\neq z
, so we have
z\in(x+e_{i})_{+}
for some

. Hence

. But
z-e_{i}\notin\Lambda
, because
z
is a corner, so
z-e_{i}\in\Lambda_{+}\setminus\Lambda
. By Lemma
7
(vi) we conclude that

which is a contradiction. So
z\neq y
as required.

To prove (ii), note that if
x\in\Lambda\setminus\{z\}
then
x=\gamma_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Lambda_{+}}}(x,i)
for some
i
. Thus
x=\gamma_{\Lambda_{+}\setminus\{z\}}(x,i)
as well, so
x\in\lambda_{\Lambda_{+}\setminus\{z\}}
. This proves the first half of (ii). For the second half, let
x\in\lambda_{\Lambda_{+}\setminus\{z\}}
. Then
x\in\Lambda_{+}\setminus\{z\}
and
x-e_{j}\notin\Lambda_{+}\setminus\{z\}
for some

. The latter implies that either
x-e_{j}\notin\Lambda_{+}
or
x-e_{j}=z
. In the first case,

and in the second,

. Either way, (ii) is shown.

In this section

We require a local formulation for the notion of a terrace.

Definition 16

We call

Note that

The first observation is elementary.

Lemma 17

If
\Lambda

\Lambda_{+}\cap Q=\Delta_{+}\cap Q

Let
Q=[a,b]

. This proves that

and the reverse inclusion is trivial.
∎

Recall from Definition

Lemma 22
.

Let
Q=[a,b]
. Let

be a
Q
-terrace.

(i)

Set

\bar{\Delta}\subset\Delta
, with
\Delta\setminus\bar{\Delta}\subset\partial Q
.

(ii)

Proof.

If
\Delta=\varnothing

\Lambda
be a terrace such that
\Lambda\cap Q=\Delta
. If

then

, and the only nontrivial statement among (i) and (ii) is that

. But

We may therefore assume that

is nonempty (which shows that

) and

then
a\in\Lambda_{+}\cap\Lambda_{-}
so by Lemma
21

.

To show (i), let

. Then

and there is an

(the equality holds by Lemma

). In particular,

If

, but for every

. Also

Thus,

To prove (ii), note that by Lemma

(i),
(\bar{\Delta})_{+}=(\Delta_{+}\cap Q)_{+}

\Delta_{+}\subset(\Delta_{+}\cap Q)_{+}

∎

Lemma 27

Let

be boxes, and let

be an

-terrace. Let

has no corners in

If

Otherwise we proceed recursively. For

We know that
z^{\scriptscriptstyle{(}m_{d})}\in z^{\scriptscriptstyle{(}m_{j})}_{-}

either
z^{\scriptscriptstyle{(}m_{d})}-e_{j}\notin R
or
z^{\scriptscriptstyle{(}m_{d})}-e_{j}\in R\setminus\Delta_{+}

z^{\scriptscriptstyle{(}m_{d})}
is a corner of
\Delta

.

.
