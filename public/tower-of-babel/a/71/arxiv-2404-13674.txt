On de Bruijn Covering Sequences and Arrays
Yeow Meng Chee 1, Tuvi Etzion 1 2, Hoang Ta 1, Van Khu Vu 1

Abstract
An ( m , n , R ) (m,n,R) -de Bruijn covering array (dBCA) is a doubly periodic M × N M\times N array over an alphabet of size q q such that the set of all its m × n m\times n windows form a covering code with radius R R . An upper bound of the smallest array area of an ( m , n , R ) (m,n,R) -dBCA is provided using a probabilistic technique which is similar to the one that was used for an upper bound on the length of a de Bruijn covering sequence. A folding technique to construct a dBCA from a de Bruijn covering sequence or de Bruijn covering sequences code is presented. Several new constructions that yield shorter de Bruijn covering sequences and ( m , n , R ) (m,n,R) -dBCAs with smaller areas are also provided. These constructions are mainly based on sequences derived from cyclic codes, self-dual sequences, primitive polynomials, an interleaving technique, folding, and mutual shifts of sequences with the same covering radius. Finally, constructions of de Bruijn covering sequences codes are also discussed.

II
A probabilistic construction

Does a dBCA whose area is close to the sphere covering bound exist?
In this section, using a similar approach as in
[
17
]
, but with a slightly more delicate analysis,
we show such an upper bound for a small area of a dBCA.

Theorem 1
.

Let
m,n
be nonnegative integer numbers. For any
M\geq m
, there exists an
M\times N

(m,n,R)
-dBCA
such that
MN=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{q^{mn}}{V_{q}(mn,R)}\cdot(\log m+\log n)\right)
,
for fixed
q
and
R
.

Before proving Theorem
1
, we first present the following primary result.
For an array
A=(a_{i,j})\in\Sigma^{M\times N}
, let
C_{A}
be the set of elements that are at a distance
of at most
R
from
S_{A}
, where
S_{A}
is the set of all windows of size
m\times n
of
A
.
The following lemma can be easily verified.

Lemma 2
.

If for an array
A\in\Sigma^{M\times N}
,
C_{A}
does not contain exactly
L
elements
of
\Sigma^{m\times n}
, then there exists an
(m,n,R)
-dBCA

whose
area is
\mathcal{O}\left(M\cdot(N+\frac{mnL}{M})\right)
.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

We prove the theorem using the probabilistic method.
A random array
A=(a_{i,j})\in\Sigma^{M\times N}
(where
N
will be chosen later)
is constructed as follows. For each
(i,j)\in[M]\times[N]
,

takes a uniformly
random value in
\Sigma
. For each
v\in\Sigma^{m\times n}
, let
E_{[v]}
be the event that
the element

is not contained in
C_{A}
and denote
L=\sum_{v\in\Sigma^{m\times n}}\Pr(E_{[v]})
.
Then
L
is the expected number of elements of
\Sigma^{m\times n}
not contained
in
C_{A}
. By the linearity of expectation and symmetry, we have that

\displaystyle L=\sum_{v\in\Sigma^{m\times n}}\Pr(E_{[v]})=q^{mn}\Pr(E_{[0]})\,,

where
[0]\in\Sigma^{m\times n}
is the all-zeros matrix.

Let
V
be the set of elements at a distance of at most
R
from
[0]\in\Sigma^{m\times n}
.
The event
E_{[0]}
occurs if and only if
S_{A}
does not intersect
V
.
For each
(i,j)\in[M]\times[N]
, denote by
B_{i,j}
the event that
s_{i,j}\in S_{A}
belongs to
V
,
where
s_{ij}
represents the window of

with its upper-left corner at position
(i,j)
. For these definitions we have that

\displaystyle\Pr(E_{[0]})=\Pr\left(\bigwedge_{i,j=1}^{M,N}\overline{B}_{i,j}\right)\,.

For
(i,j),(\ell,k)\in[M]\times[N]
,
(i,j)\neq(\ell,k)
we denote
{(i,j)\sim(\ell,k)}

if
s_{i,j}
and
s_{\ell,k}
intersect. We define

\mu=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\Pr(B_{i,j})\,,

\Delta=\sum_{(i,j)\sim(\ell,k)}\Pr(B_{i,j}\wedge B_{\ell,k}),

and

\delta=\sum_{(1,1)\sim(i,j)}\Pr(B_{i,j}).

Using Suen’s inequality
[
24
, Theorem 3]
, we have that

\displaystyle\Pr\left(\bigwedge_{i,j=1}^{M,N}\overline{B}_{i,j}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\min\left(\frac{\mu^{2}}{8\Delta},\frac{\mu}{2},\frac{\mu}{6\delta}\right)\right)\,.

We will estimate
\mu,\Delta
and
\delta
. First, for
\mu

\displaystyle\mu=MN\cdot\Pr(B_{1,1})=MN\cdot\frac{V_{q}(mn,R)}{q^{mn}}\,.

Next, for any
(i,j)\in[M]\times[N]
there are
(2m-1)(2n-1)-1
pairs

such
that
(i,j)\sim(\ell,k)
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\delta=(4mn-2m-2n)\Pr(B_{1,1})\leq 4mn\frac{V_{q}(mn,R)}{q^{mn}}=o(1)\,.

For estimating
\Delta
, we have the following lemma whose proof can be found in the Appendix.

Lemma 3
.

We have
\Delta\leq(1+o(1))\mu
.

From the estimation of
\Delta,\mu
, and
\delta
, we have that

\displaystyle\min\left(\frac{\mu^{2}}{8\Delta},\frac{\mu}{2},\frac{\mu}{6\delta}\right)\geq c\mu\,\text{ for some positive constant c}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\Pr\left(\bigwedge_{i,j=1}^{M,N}\overline{B}_{i,j}\right)\leq\exp\left(-c\mu\right)\,.

By choosing
N
such that

\displaystyle MN

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{c}\frac{q^{mn}}{V_{q}(mn,R)}\log(mn\cdot V_{q}(mn,R))\,.

Now, we have that

\displaystyle\Pr(E_{[0]})=\Pr\left(\bigwedge_{i,j=1}^{M,N}\overline{B}_{i,j}\right)\leq\frac{1}{mn\cdot V_{q}(mn,R)}\,.

Therefore,

\displaystyle L=q^{mn}\Pr(E_{[0]})\leq\frac{q^{mn}}{mn\cdot V_{q}(mn,R)}\,.

On the other hand, since

and
R
are fixed, we have that
\log(mn\cdot V_{q}(mn,R))=\mathcal{O}\left(\log m+\log n\right)
. Using Lemma
2
, the proof of the theorem is completed.
∎

III
Folding of a dBCS into a dBCA

We start by folding an
(mn,R)
-dBCS into an
(m,n,R)
-dBCA.
The given
(mn,R)
-dBCS

of length

-dBCA. Each entry

of the

-dBCS will be in the

consecutive entries of

that start in

folded row by row.

To achieve these goals the

s_{jn+1},s_{jn+2},\ldots,s_{jn+n},s_{jn+n+1},\ldots,s_{jn+2n-1},

Consider now

\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
s_{(j+m-1)n+i}&s_{(j+m-1)n+i+1}&\cdots&s_{(j+m-1)n+i+n-1},\end{array}

where indices are taken modulo

This construction implies the following consequence

Theorem 4
.

If
\mathcal{S}
is an
(mn,R)
-dBCS of length

, where

, then there exists a doubly periodic

-dBCA of size

If
\mathcal{S}
is an
(mn,R)
-dBCS of length

(m,n,R)
-dBCA of size

Next, we will be interested in

(m,n,R)
-dBCAs for which

is small as possible, where there exists

(nm,R)
-dBCS sequence

whose length is

Let

N^{\prime}=2n-1

M^{\prime}\leq\left\lceil\frac{\kappa}{n}\right\rceil+m-1

The
j
th row of the array is defined by

x_{jn+1},x_{jn+2},\ldots,x_{jn+2n-1},

Once all the

and

Let

Construction from cyclic covering codes:

This is one of the most effective constructions. Let
\mathcal{C}
be a cyclic code of length
n
and covering radius
R
.
Since
\mathcal{C}
is cyclic, it follows that we can partition the codewords into equivalence classes, where
two codewords are in the same equivalence class if one is a cyclic shift of the other.
The size of an equivalence class is a divisor of
n
and usually most classes are of size
n
.
For each equivalence class of size
d
we construct a string of length
n+d-1
.
All these strings are now combined with possible overlap between them to reduce the length
of the dBCS. Cyclic covering codes were considered
for example by
[
25
,
26
,
27
,
28
]
.

Example 9
.

For
n=15
, we consider the cyclic Hamming code of length 15.
It has 134 classes of size 15, 6 classes of size 5, 2 classes of size 3, and 2 classes of size 1.
They are combined to form a
(15,1)
-dBCS of length 3600 compared to a lower bound 2048 obtained by the
sphere covering bound.

\blacksquare

differs from

only in the last bit. For each string
Z
of even weight and length 8, which starts
with a
zero
the following two sequences are constructed.

[Z,Z+X,\bar{Z},\bar{Z}+X]\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ [Z,Z+Y,\bar{Z},\bar{Z}+Y]\penalty\ .

In this way, we form 64 pairs of cyclic sequences of length 32. Each pair can be merged into another sequence

[Z,Z+X,\bar{Z},\bar{Z}+X,Z,Z+Y,\bar{Z},\bar{Z}+Y]\penalty\ .

The idea of constructing such self-dual sequences appeared already in

,

for constructions of other types of sequences.
These 64 sequences form a
(16,1)
-dBCSC. Trivially, each such sequence can be extended
with its first 15 bits to an acyclic sequence. These 64 sequences can be concatenated to form
a
(16,1)
-dBCS of length
{64\cdot(64+15)=5056}
. We can also merge the sequences with overlaps
of suffixes and prefixes of sequences and obtain a
(16,1)
-dBCS of length 4476 (the known lower bound
is 4096). The same idea can be applied recursively now on the 64 pairs of sequences of length 32
to obtain an upper bound on the shortest length of a
