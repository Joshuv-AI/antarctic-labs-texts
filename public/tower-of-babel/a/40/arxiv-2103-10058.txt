Bartlett correction of an independence test in a multivariate Poisson model
Rolf Larsson

Abstract
We consider a system of dependent Poisson variables, where each variable is the sum of an independent variate and a common variate. It is the common variate that creates the dependence. Within this system, a test of independence may be constructed where the null hypothesis is that the common variate is identically zero. In the present paper, we consider the maximum log likelihood ratio test. For this test, it is well-known that the asymptotic distribution of the test statistic is an equal mixture of zero and a chi square distribution with one degree of freedom. We examine a Bartlett correction of this test, in the hope that we will get better approximation of the nominal size for moderately large sample sizes. This correction is explicitly derived, and its usefulness is explored in a simulation study. For practical purposes, the correction is found to be useful in dimension two, but not in higher dimensions.



1
Introduction

Bartlett correction is a simple technique to obtain small sample corrections of asymptotic distributions, in particular useful for maximum log likelihood ratio tests. It was introduced in Bartlett (1937). Let the sample size be
n
, and write
Q_{n}=-2\log\Lambda_{n}
, where
\Lambda_{n}
is the ratio between the maximum likelihoods under the null and the alternative hypotheses, respectively.
Denoting the distributional limit of
Q_{n}
by
Q_{\infty}
, the simple idea is to write

Q_{n}\approx\frac{EQ_{n}}{EQ_{\infty}}Q_{\infty},

where

EQ_{n}=EQ_{\infty}+n^{-1}R+o(n^{-1}),

where
E
is the expectation operator and
o(n^{-1})
is an error term that tends to zero faster than
n^{-1}
as
n\to\infty
. Hence,

Q_{n}\approx(1+n^{-1}R^{\prime})Q_{\infty},

(1)

for a constant
R^{\prime}=R/EQ_{\infty}
. Obviously, the approximation in (
1
) asymptotically corrects the first moment of the statistic, in the sense that the error is of smaller order than
n^{-1}
. In fact, for maximum log likelihood ratio tests, Lawley (1956) shows that under regularity conditions, all higher moments are corrected in the same way. Hayakawa (1977) went on to prove that this practice also corrects the fractiles of the distribution.

In standard situations, it is well known that, under the null hypothesis, the asymptotic distribution of the maximum log likelihood ratio (LR) test (
Q_{\infty}
above) is
\chi^{2}
with one degree of freedom,
\chi^{2}(1)
. However, the present paper deals with a case where the parameter of the null hypothesis is on the boundary of the parameter space. Here, the standard result does not apply. Indeed, Self and Liang (1987) have shown that the corresponding distribution is an equal mixture of a point mass at one and
\chi^{2}(1)
. If the parameter is
\lambda
(say), and we test null hypothesis
H_{0}
:
\lambda=0
vs
H_{1}
:
\lambda>0
, then the asymptotic probability that the maximum likelihood estimator
\hat{\lambda}=0
is
1/2
, and the asymptotic distribution of
(Q_{n}|\hat{\lambda}>0)
is
\chi^{2}(1)
.

In the present paper, we will deal with a dependent Poisson model, see e.g. Karlis (2003). This model is as follows: Assume that
U
,
X_{1},X_{2},...,X_{m}
are independent Poisson with parameters
\lambda
,
\mu_{1},\mu_{2},...,\mu_{m}
, respectively. Then, construct the dependent Poisson variates
(Y_{1},Y_{2},...,Y_{m})
through
Y_{j}=U+X_{j}
,
j=1,2,...,m
. It is clear that the
Y_{j}
are simultaneously independent if and only if
\lambda=0
. Hence, it is of great interest to test
H_{0}
:
\lambda=0
vs
H_{1}
:
\lambda>0
. Doing this with the LR test is the focus of the present paper.

One application of such a test can be found in Larsson (2020), where it can be seen as a building block in the search algorithm for the best discrete factor analysis model. (There, the best model is defined as the one with the smallest AIC, but comparing AIC values is equivalent to using the LR test on some significance level, if the null model is a special case of the alternative model.)

The rest of the paper unfolds as follows. Section 2 gives the asymptotic approximation of the expectation of the LR test under the null. In section 3, Bartlett correction is discussed. Section 4 gives small sample simulation results, and section 5 concludes.

2
Asymptotic approximation of the expectation

Suppose we have a random sample
(y_{i1},y_{i2},...,y_{im})
,
i=1,2,...,n
, from the distribution of
(Y_{1},Y_{2},...,Y_{m})
of the dependent Poisson model by Karlis (2003).
We want to test
H_{0}
:
\lambda=0
(independence) vs
H_{1}
:
\lambda>0
(dependence) by using the likelihood ratio test.

The first object is to find the maximum likelihood estimates (MLEs) of the parameters.

Let
y_{i}=\min(y_{i1},y_{i2},...,y_{im})
for all
i
. The likelihood is

\displaystyle L(\lambda,\mu_{1},\mu_{2},...,\mu_{m})

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{u=0}^{y_{i}}e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^{u}}{u!}\prod_{j=1}^{m}e^{-\mu_{j}}\frac{\mu_{j}^{y_{ij}-u}}{(y_{ij}-u)!}

\displaystyle=e^{-n(\lambda+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mu_{j})}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{m}\mu_{j}^{n\bar{y}_{j}}\right)\prod_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{u=0}^{y_{i}}\frac{1}{u!\prod_{j=1}^{m}(y_{ij}-u)!}\left(\frac{\lambda}{\prod_{j=1}^{m}\mu_{j}}\right)^{u},

(2)

where
\bar{y}_{j}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{ij}
for
j=1,2,...,m
.

Let
\hat{\lambda}
and
\hat{\mu}_{j}
be the MLEs of
\lambda
and
\mu_{j}
,
j=1,2,...,m
. Since it is known that (Karlis, 2003, Larsson, 2020)
\hat{\lambda}+\hat{\mu}_{j}=\bar{y}_{j}
, the task is to maximize the likelihood in (
2
) reformulated as

L(\lambda)=e^{-n\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{m}\bar{y}_{j}-(m-1)\lambda\right\}}\left\{\prod_{j=1}^{m}(\bar{y}_{j}-\lambda)^{n\bar{y}_{j}}\right\}\prod_{i=1}^{n}S_{i}(\lambda),

(3)

where

S_{i}(\lambda)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{u=0}^{y_{i}}\frac{\xi(\lambda)^{u}}{u!\prod_{j=1}^{m}(y_{ij}-u)!},

(4)

with

\xi(\lambda)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm{def}}}{{=}}\frac{\lambda}{\prod_{j=1}^{m}(\bar{y}_{j}-\lambda)}.

(5)

As usual, it seems to be easier to focus on maximizing the log likelihood, which via (
3
) is given as

\displaystyle l(\lambda)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm{def}}}{{=}}\log\{L(\lambda)\}

\displaystyle=n\left\{(m-1)\lambda-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\bar{y}_{j}\right\}+n\sum_{j=1}^{m}\bar{y}_{j}\log(\bar{y}_{j}-\lambda)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}(\lambda),

(6)

where
s_{i}(\lambda)=\log\{S_{i}(\lambda)\}
. The derivative of (
6
) with respect to
\lambda
is

l^{\prime}(\lambda)=n\left(m-1-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{\bar{y}_{j}}{\bar{y}_{j}-\lambda}\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}^{\prime}(\lambda).

(7)

The derivative of
s_{i}(\lambda)
w.r.t
\lambda
is, emphasizing the dependency on the observations
{\boldsymbol{y}_{i}}=(y_{i1},y_{i2},...,y_{im})
,

s_{i}^{\prime}(\lambda)=\frac{S_{i}^{\prime}(\lambda;{\boldsymbol{y}_{i}})}{S_{i}(\lambda;{\boldsymbol{y}_{i}})},

where from (
4
), denoting
I\{A\}
as the indicator of the event
A
,

\displaystyle S_{i}^{\prime}(\lambda;{\boldsymbol{y}_{i}})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm{def}}}{{=}}\xi^{\prime}(\lambda)\sum_{u=1}^{y_{i}}\frac{\xi(\lambda)^{u-1}}{(u-1)!\prod_{j=1}^{m}(y_{ij}-u)!}I\{y_{i}>0\}

\displaystyle=\xi^{\prime}(\lambda)\sum_{u=1}^{(y_{i}-1)+1}\frac{\xi(\lambda)^{u-1}}{(u-1)!\prod_{j=1}^{m}\{(y_{ij}-1-(u-1)\}!}I\{y_{i}>0\}

\displaystyle=\xi^{\prime}(\lambda)S_{i}(\lambda;\boldsymbol{y}_{i}-\boldsymbol{1})I\{y_{i}>0\},

where
\boldsymbol{1}=(1,1,...,1)
.
Hence, because by (
5
),

\xi^{\prime}(\lambda)=\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{m}(\bar{y}_{j}-\lambda)-\lambda\frac{d}{d\lambda}\prod_{j=1}^{m}(\bar{y}_{j}-\lambda)}{\left\{\prod_{j=1}^{m}(\bar{y}_{j}-\lambda)\right\}^{2}}=\frac{1+\lambda\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\bar{y}_{j}-\lambda)^{-1}}{\prod_{j=1}^{m}(\bar{y}_{j}-\lambda)},

it follows that

s_{i}^{\prime}(\lambda)=\frac{1+\lambda\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\bar{y}_{j}-\lambda)^{-1}}{\prod_{j=1}^{m}(\bar{y}_{j}-\lambda)}\frac{S_{i}(\lambda;\boldsymbol{y}_{i}-\boldsymbol{1})}{S_{i}(\lambda;\boldsymbol{y_{i}})}I\{y_{i}>0\}.

Thus, via (
7
), and the identity

\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{\bar{y}_{j}}{\bar{y}_{j}-\lambda}=m+\lambda\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\bar{y}_{j}-\lambda)^{-1},

we have

\displaystyle l^{\prime}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\left\{1+\lambda\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\bar{y}_{j}-\lambda)^{-1}\right\}

\displaystyle\cdot\left\{-n+\frac{1}{\prod_{j=1}^{m}(\bar{y}_{j}-\lambda)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{S_{i}(\lambda;\boldsymbol{y}_{i}-\boldsymbol{1})}{S_{i}(\lambda;\boldsymbol{y_{i}})}I\{y_{i}>0\}\right\}.

(8)

To find the MLE
\hat{\lambda}
, we need to solve the equation
l^{\prime}(\lambda)=0
. In practice, this has to be done by numerical methods. For our purposes, it is enough to derive an asymptotic approximation.

To this end, we Taylor expand (
4
) as (here,
y_{ij(2)}=y_{ij}(y_{ij}-1)
etcetera, and
O
in fact means order in probability)

\displaystyle S_{i}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{\prod_{j=1}^{m}y_{ij}!}

\displaystyle\left\{1+\prod_{j=1}^{m}y_{ij}\xi(\lambda)+\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{m}y_{ij(2)}}{2!}\xi(\lambda)^{2}+\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{m}y_{ij(3)}}{3!}\xi(\lambda)^{3}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{m}y_{ij(4)}}{4!}\xi(\lambda)^{4}+O(\lambda^{5})\right\}.

(9)

Here, Taylor expansion of (
5
) yields (the sums and products are over
1\leq j\leq m
, etcetera), introducing
P=\prod_{j}\bar{y}_{j}
,

\displaystyle\xi(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\lambda P^{-1}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{\bar{y}_{j}}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\lambda P^{-1}\prod_{j}\left(1+\frac{\lambda}{\bar{y}_{j}}+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{\bar{y}_{j}^{2}}+...\right)

\displaystyle=\lambda P^{-1}\left\{1+\sum_{j}\frac{1}{\bar{y}_{j}}\lambda+\left(\sum_{j}\frac{1}{\bar{y}_{j}^{2}}+\sum_{j<k}\frac{1}{\bar{y}_{j}\bar{y}_{k}}\right)\lambda^{2}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\left(\sum_{j}\frac{1}{\bar{y}_{j}^{3}}+\sum_{j<k}\frac{1}{\bar{y}_{j}^{2}\bar{y}_{k}}+\sum_{j<k}\frac{1}{\bar{y}_{j}\bar{y}_{k}^{2}}+\sum_{j<k<l}\frac{1}{\bar{y}_{j}\bar{y}_{k}\bar{y}_{l}}\right)\lambda^{3}+O(\lambda^{4})\right\},

(10)

which inserted into (
9
) implies

\displaystyle S_{i}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\prod_{j}y_{ij}!}\left[1+\prod_{j}y_{ij}\left\{P^{-1}\lambda+\frac{1}{2}a_{2}({\boldsymbol{y}}_{i})P^{-2}\lambda^{2}+\frac{1}{6}a_{3}({\boldsymbol{y}}_{i})P^{-3}\lambda^{3}\right\}+O(\lambda^{4})\right],

where

a_{2}({\boldsymbol{y}}_{i})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm{def}}}{{=}}2P\sum_{j}\bar{y}_{j}^{-1}+\prod_{j}(y_{ij}-1),

(11)

and

\displaystyle a_{3}({\boldsymbol{y}}_{i})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm{def}}}{{=}}6P^{2}\left(\sum_{j}\bar{y}_{j}^{-2}+\sum_{j<k}\bar{y}_{j}^{-1}\bar{y}_{k}^{-1}\right)+6P\prod_{j}(y_{ij}-1)\sum_{k}\bar{y}_{k}^{-1}

\displaystyle+\prod_{j}(y_{ij}-1)(y_{ij}-2).

(12)

Now,

\displaystyle\frac{S_{i}(\lambda;\boldsymbol{y}_{i}-\boldsymbol{1})}{S_{i}(\lambda;\boldsymbol{y}_{i})}=\prod_{j}y_{ij}\cdot

\displaystyle\frac{1+\prod_{j}(y_{ij}-1)\left\{P^{-1}\lambda+\frac{1}{2}a_{2}(\boldsymbol{y}_{i}-\boldsymbol{1})P^{-2}\lambda^{2}++\frac{1}{6}a_{3}(\boldsymbol{y}_{i}-\boldsymbol{1})P^{-3}\lambda^{3}\right\}+O(\lambda^{4})}{1+\prod_{j}y_{ij}\left\{P^{-1}\lambda+\frac{1}{2}a_{2}(\boldsymbol{y}_{i})P^{-2}\lambda^{2}+\frac{1}{6}a_{3}(\boldsymbol{y}_{i})P^{-3}\lambda^{3}\right\}+O(\lambda^{4})},

(13)

and by Taylor expansion,

\displaystyle\frac{S_{i}(\lambda;\boldsymbol{y}_{i}-\boldsymbol{1})}{S_{i}(\lambda;\boldsymbol{y}_{i})}

\displaystyle=\prod_{j}y_{ij}\left[1+\left\{\prod_{j}(y_{ij}-1)-\prod_{j}y_{ij}\right\}P^{-1}\lambda\right.

\displaystyle\left.+b_{2}(\boldsymbol{y}_{i})P^{-2}\lambda^{2}+b_{3}(\boldsymbol{y}_{i})P^{-3}\lambda^{3}+O(\lambda^{4})\right],

(14)

where

\displaystyle b_{2}(\boldsymbol{y}_{i})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm{def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2}\left\{\prod_{j}(y_{ij}-1)a_{2}(\boldsymbol{y}_{i}-\boldsymbol{1})-\prod_{j}y_{ij}a_{2}(\boldsymbol{y}_{i})\right\}

\displaystyle+\prod_{j}y_{ij}^{2}-\prod_{j}y_{ij}(y_{ij}-1).

(15)

and

\displaystyle b_{3}(\boldsymbol{y}_{i})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm{def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{6}\left\{\prod_{j}(y_{ij}-1)a_{3}(\boldsymbol{y}_{i}-\boldsymbol{1})-\prod_{j}y_{ij}a_{3}(\boldsymbol{y}_{i})\right\}

\displaystyle-\prod_{j}y_{ij}^{3}+\prod_{j}y_{ij}^{2}a_{2}(\boldsymbol{y}_{i})+\prod_{j}y_{ij}^{2}(y_{ij}-1)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\prod_{j}y_{ij}\left\{\prod_{j}(y_{ij}-1)a_{2}(\boldsymbol{y}_{i}-\boldsymbol{1})+\prod_{j}y_{ij}a_{2}(\boldsymbol{y}_{i})\right\}.

(16)

Moreover, let

C_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm{def}}}{{=}}n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\prod_{j}y_{ij}\left\{\prod_{j}(y_{ij}-1)-\prod_{j}y_{ij}\right\},

(17)

C_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm{def}}}{{=}}n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\prod_{j}y_{ij}b_{2}(\boldsymbol{y}_{i}),

(18)

and

C_{3}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm{def}}}{{=}}n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\prod_{j}y_{ij}b_{3}(\boldsymbol{y}_{i}),

(19)

Now, via (
14

17

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm{def}}}{{=}}n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{S_{i}(\lambda;\boldsymbol{y}_{i}-\boldsymbol{1})}{S_{i}(\lambda;\boldsymbol{y}_{i})}I\{y_{i}>0\}

\displaystyle=\bar{y}_{11...1}+P^{-1}C_{1}\lambda+P^{-2}C_{2}\lambda^{2}+P^{-3}C_{3}\lambda^{3}+O(\lambda^{4}),

(20)

where

\bar{y}_{11...1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm{def}}}{{=}}n^{-1}\prod_{j}y_{ij}.

(21)

From (

\prod_{j}(\bar{y}_{j}-\lambda)=S
,

\displaystyle\prod_{j}(\bar{y}_{j}-\lambda)=P\prod_{j}\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{\bar{y}_{j}}\right)

\displaystyle=P\left(1-\sum_{j}\bar{y}_{j}^{-1}\lambda+\sum_{j<k}\bar{y}_{j}^{-1}\bar{y}_{k}^{-1}\lambda^{2}-\sum_{j<k<l}\bar{y}_{j}^{-1}\bar{y}_{k}^{-1}\bar{y}_{l}^{-1}\lambda^{3}\right)+O(\lambda^{4}),

(22)

where

\displaystyle d_{0}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{n}\left(\bar{y}_{11...1}-P\right),

(23)

\displaystyle d_{1}

\displaystyle=P^{-1}C_{1}+P\sum_{j}\bar{y}_{j}^{-1},

(24)

\displaystyle d_{2}

\displaystyle=P^{-2}C_{2}-P\sum_{j<k}\bar{y}_{j}^{-1}\bar{y}_{k}^{-1},

(25)

\displaystyle d_{3}

\displaystyle=P^{-3}C_{3}+P\sum_{j<k<l}\bar{y}_{j}^{-1}\bar{y}_{k}^{-1}\bar{y}_{l}^{-1}.

(26)

It will be shown below that as

,

,

Now, putting
\lambda=n^{-1/2}c_{1}+n^{-1}c_{2}+n^{-3/2}c_{3}+O(n^{-2})
and inserting into (

), we get

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=n^{-1/2}d_{0}+d_{1}(n^{-1/2}c_{1}+n^{-1}c_{2}+n^{-3/2}c_{3})+d_{2}(n^{-1/2}c_{1}+n^{-1}c_{2})^{2}

\displaystyle+d_{3}(n^{-1/2}c_{1})^{3}+O(n^{-2})

\displaystyle=n^{-1/2}(d_{0}+d_{1}c_{1})+n^{-1}(d_{1}c_{2}+d_{2}c_{1}^{2})+n^{-3/2}(d_{1}c_{3}+2d_{2}c_{1}c_{2}+d_{3}c_{1}^{3})

\hat{\lambda}=-\frac{n^{-1/2}d_{0}}{d_{1}}\left\{1+\frac{n^{-1/2}d_{0}d_{2}}{d_{1}^{2}}+\frac{n^{-1}d_{0}^{2}(2d_{2}^{2}-d_{1}d_{3})}{d_{1}^{4}}+O(n^{-3/2})\right\}.

(27)

3
The log likelihood ratio test

Putting
\lambda=0
in (
2
) we get the likelihood under the null of independence. Then, inserting the MLEs
\hat{\mu}_{j}=\bar{y}_{j}
, we get the maximized likelihood under
H_{0}
as

L_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm{def}}}{{=}}e^{-n\sum_{j=1}^{m}\bar{y}_{j}}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{m}\bar{y}_{j}^{n\bar{y}_{j}}\right)\prod_{i=1}^{n}\prod_{j=1}^{m}(y_{ij}!)^{-1}.

(28)

Now, define the log likelihood ratio test statistic as

Q_{n}=-2\log\left(\frac{L_{0}}{L_{1}}\right)=2(\log L_{1}-\log L_{0}),

where
L_{1}
is the maximized likelihood under
H_{1}
. In the following, we will implicitly condition on the event
\hat{\lambda}>0
.

Via (
6

\frac{1}{2}n^{-1}Q_{n}=(m-1)\hat{\lambda}+\sum_{j}\bar{y}_{j}\log\left(1-\frac{\hat{\lambda}}{\bar{y}_{j}}\right)+n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}^{*}(\hat{\lambda}),

\displaystyle S_{i}(\hat{\lambda})\prod_{j}y_{ij}!

Now, (

where

\displaystyle e_{2i}

\displaystyle=\prod_{j}y_{ij(2)}-\left(\prod_{j}y_{ij}\right)^{2},

\displaystyle e_{3i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\prod_{j}y_{ij(3)}-\frac{3}{2}\prod_{j}y_{ij}\prod_{j}y_{ij(2)}+\left(\prod_{j}y_{ij}\right)^{3},

(33)

\displaystyle e_{4i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{6}\prod_{j}y_{ij(4)}-\frac{2}{3}\prod_{j}y_{ij}\prod_{j}y_{ij(3)}-\frac{1}{2}\left(\prod_{j}y_{ij(2)}\right)^{2}

4
Bartlett correction

In proposition 1 and corollary 2, asymptotically the expectation of
Q_{n}|\hat{\lambda}>0
approaches 1, and we write
E(Q_{\infty}|\hat{\lambda}>0)=1
.

Now, consider a situation where no conditioning is involved. If, for the unconditional distribution, a result such as proposition 1 is present, the principle of Bartlett correction is to adjust the asymptotic statistic
Q_{\infty}
according to

Q_{n}\approx\frac{E(Q_{n})}{E(Q_{\infty})}Q_{\infty}\approx(1+n^{-1}R)Q_{\infty}.

(246)

It is clear from (
246
) that the first moment of
\{E(Q_{n})/E(Q_{\infty})\}Q_{\infty}
equals the first moment of
Q_{n}
. In fact, Lawley (1956) shows that under very general conditions, this is in fact true even for all higher moments.

Now, define the
\alpha
percentile of
Q_{n}
,
q_{n}(\alpha)
, through

\alpha=P(Q_{n}>q_{n}(\alpha))
. Then, from (
246
),

\alpha\approx P\left\{(1+n^{-1}R)Q_{\infty}>q_{n}(\alpha)\right\}=P\left\{Q_{\infty}>\frac{q_{n}(\alpha)}{1+n^{-1}R}\right\}.

Now, in the standard situation where
Q_{\infty}
is
\chi^{2}_{1}
distributed, we have

\alpha=P\{Q_{\infty}>\chi^{2}_{1}(\alpha)\}
, i.e.

q_{n}(\alpha)\approx(1+n^{-1}R)\chi^{2}_{1}(\alpha).

(247)

However, in our case we have that the distribution of
Q_{\infty}

conditional on

\hat{\lambda}>0
is
\chi^{2}_{1}
. With
\pi_{n}=P(\hat{\lambda}>0)
, we have

\alpha=P\{Q_{n}>q_{n}(\alpha)\}=\pi_{n}P\{Q_{n}>q_{n}(\alpha)|\hat{\lambda}>0\},

i.e.
\pi_{n}^{-1}\alpha=P\{Q_{n}>q_{n}(\alpha)|\hat{\lambda}>0\}
. Then, analogous to (
247
), we have

q_{n}(\alpha)\approx(1+n^{-1}R)\chi^{2}_{1}(\pi_{n}^{-1}\alpha).

(248)

Furthermore, we have that as
n\to\infty
,
\pi_{n}\to 1/2
. In a practical situation, we do not know
\pi_{n}
. Neither do we know
R
, but we can replace it by an estimate,
\hat{R}
, where we insert
\bar{y}_{j}
for
\mu_{j}
, for all
j=1,2,...,m
. In particular, if
m=2
, corollary 2 gives us

\hat{R}=\frac{1}{6\bar{y}_{1}\bar{y}_{2}}(1+9\bar{y}_{1}+9\bar{y}_{2}+15\bar{y}_{1}\bar{y}_{2}).

(249)

Hence, putting
\pi_{n}=1/2
, the practical analogue of (
248
) is

\hat{q}_{n}(\alpha)=(1+n^{-1}\hat{R})\chi^{2}_{1}(2\alpha).

(250)

5
Simulations

To evaluate the Bartlett correction in a simulation study, we may compare estimates of

1.

P\{Q_{n}>\chi^{2}_{1}(2\alpha)\}

2.

P\{Q_{n}>(1+n^{-1}\hat{R})\chi^{2}_{1}(2\alpha)\}

3.

P\{Q_{n}>(1+n^{-1}R)\chi^{2}_{1}(2\alpha)\}

4.

P\{Q_{n}>(1+n^{-1}R)\chi^{2}_{1}(\hat{\pi}_{n}^{-1}\alpha)\}

These should go from worse to better. Note that the first one is the asymptotic approximation, the second one is the workable version of the Bartlett approximation while the other two are not useful in practice, but interesting by means of comparison. In the last one,
\hat{\pi}_{n}
simply denotes the empirical proportion of positive
\hat{\lambda}
in the simulation.

To check the validity and usefulness of the Bartlett correction, we have performed a small simulation study. Matlab R2109a was used. In each case, 1 000 000 replications were run. Optimization was performed using the procedure
fmincon
, and program codes are available upon request.

Below, in each case studied we depict expected simulated statistics together with the Bartlett approximation from proposition 1 (corollary 2 for
m=2
). Observe that asymptotically,

tends to
1/2
, and in the figures we give
2E(Q_{n})
as the expectation. This is motivated by the fact that

when
\pi_{n}\approx 1/2
. Because of this, we give the fractions of positive
\hat{\lambda}
for different models in a separate graph at the end of the section. We also separately graph the simulated variances of
Q_{n}
for the different models.

Casewise, we also graph the empirical rejection probabilities (

\hat{\lambda}
into account. In all cases, the nominal test sizes are 5%.

We start by looking at dimension

. The first case studied is when

in steps of 20 up to

\hat{\lambda}
, shown in figure 9, is lower than 0.5 for small samples, which means that the up weighting of the simulated

by a factor 2 is too little in these cases.

As for the empirical test sizes (in per cent) in figure 2, we can see that the asymptotic
erp
values are too high for small

In view of figure 1, it is also to be expected that the Bartlett approximation over corrects, producing sizes a bit below the nominal 5%. The simulated Bartlett correction does not suffer from this problem, and it also produces fairly accurate

It may perhaps be a bit surprising to see that, when taking positive

proportions into account, we get slight over estimations. But this is probably due to the fact that the simulated variances are a bit large for small

\hat{\lambda}
shifts it to the left. In this case, when it comes to the simulated Bartlett correction with no correction for
\hat{\lambda}
, it seems that these two effects compensate each other. Imposing the
\hat{\lambda}
correction, only the variance effect remains, and we get over estimation.

The cases
\mu_{1}=\mu_{2}=5
and
\mu_{1}=\mu_{2}=20

Regrettably, for dimensions

Still, it seems from figure 8 that Bartlett correction works quite well for

Many more cases were simulated by the author, and further results may be obtained at request.

Finally, figure 9 gives the estimated proportions of positive

6
Conclusion

In the present paper, we have presented Bartlett corrections for the maximum log likelihood test of independence in the multivariate Poisson model proposed by e.g. Karlis (2003). This topic may be considered of interest, if not only by itself, so for giving an example of the Bartlett correction in the non standard type of situation where the parameter of the null hypothesis lies on the border of the parameter space.

In general, the resulting Bartlett correction factor is given by a very complicated formula, although explicit. But in the special case of dimension two (which is the case to be applied in Larsson, 2020), it simplifies into a nice and short expression. Our simulation studies also indicate that it is in dimension two that the correction is useful in practice. In higher dimensions, it seems that for sample sizes of practical interest, the expected statistic is far from a linear function of the sample size.

One possible improvement of the correction would be to incorporate the probability of a positive parameter estimate in some way. Another improvement could be to make the correction non linear. Computer intensive methods could also come into play. These would be interesting scopes for future research on the subject.
