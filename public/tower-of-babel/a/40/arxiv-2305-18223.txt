A quantum field comonad
Jethro van Ekeren

Abstract




Abstract.
We encapsulate the basic notions of the theory of vertex algebras into the construction of a comonad on an appropriate category of formal distributions. Vertex algebras are recovered as coalgebras over this comonad.

1
Introduction

The monad is a basic categorical notion, especially popular in the universal algebra and theoretical computer science communities. By definition a monad consists of a functor
M:\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
from a category
\mathcal{C}
to itself, together with natural morphisms
X\rightarrow M(X)
and
M^{2}(X)\rightarrow M(X)
for all
X\in\mathcal{C}
, satisfying certain identities. Monads are ubiquitous since if
L
and
R
form a pair of adjoint functors (
L
left adjoint and
R
right adjoint) then
M=R\circ L
is an example of a monad. There is a notion of “algebra” over a monad
M
, namely an object
X\in\mathcal{C}
together with a morphism
m:M(X)\rightarrow X
compatible with the structure of
M
in a precise sense.

A simple example of a monad is the functor
\mathbf{Set}\rightarrow\mathbf{Set}
sending a set
S
to the set of all finite strings of elements of
S
. An algebra over this monad is a monoid (a set endowed with an associative binary product and a unit). Another example, a case of the general construction of monads from adjoint pairs, is
\mathbf{Vec}_{k}\rightarrow\mathbf{Vec}_{k}
the composition of the symmetric algebra functor (from the category of vector spaces over the field
k
to commutative
k
-algebras) with the obvious forgetful functor (from commutative algebras to vector spaces). An algebra over this monad is a commutative
k
-algebra. The theory of operads, which encompasses many familiar algebraic structures, can itself be put on a monadic footing: in
[
13
, Section 5.5]
operads are given a definition as algebras over a suitable monad of rooted trees.

There are examples with a quite different flavour too. The ultrafilter monad
\beta:\mathbf{Set}\rightarrow\mathbf{Set}
sends a set
S
to the set of all ultrafilters on
S
. A
\beta
-algebra is a compact Hausdorff topological space
[
14
, Proposition 5.5]
! The functor
\mathbf{Vec}_{k}\rightarrow\mathbf{Vec}_{k}
taking a vector space
V
to its double dual
(V^{*})^{*}
is a monad. An algebra over this monad is an object of the opposite category
\mathbf{Vec}_{k}^{\text{op}}
, which is the same thing as a linearly compact topological vector space over
k
(this result, expressed in a different terminology, goes back to
[
11
, Section II.6]
).

The categorical dual notion of comonad also appears naturally. The power set
P(X)
of a set
X
may be made into a category by giving a morphism for each inclusion of subsets. A topology
\tau
on
X
yields a notion of interior of a subset, and the operation
\Int_{\tau}
taking a subset to its interior defines a functor from
P(X)
to itself which is in fact a comonad. In this example we have
\Int_{\tau}^{2}=\Int_{\tau}
(an interior always equals its own interior), so
\Int_{\tau}
is an example of an idempotent comonad. For another topological example: the functor sending a pointed topological space to its universal cover defines a comonad on the category of (locally contractible, path connected) pointed topological spaces
[
15
, Section 5.3]
.

The notion of vertex algebra has been introduced in
[
4
]
in the guise of a vector space equipped with an infinite collection of bilinear products, satisfying identities analogous to the skew-symmetry and Jacobi identity of Lie algebras. The chiral algebra perspective of Beilinson and Drinfeld makes the connection with Lie algebras explicit; a chiral algebra over an algebraic curve
X
being defined as a Lie algebra within a certain pseudotensor category of
\mathcal{D}
-modules on the Ran space of
X

[
2
]
.

A different approach (which appears to have entered the mathematical literature in
[
8
]
, and developed in
[
12
]
,
[
9
]
and
[
1
]
) starts from a formal notion of quantum field on a vector space, subject to a form of mutual locality. Through a series of elegant manipulations, one arrives at Borcherds’ notion of vertex algebra.

The purpose of this note is to point out the monadic nature (or more precisely, comonadic nature) of these constructions. Described informally, there is a comonad in which the state-field correspondence serves as the counit, and in which the comultiplication corresponds to an operation

a(w)\mapsto\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}a(w)_{(n)}(-)x^{-n-1}

in which a quantum field
a(w)
on a vector space
V
is sent to a quantum field on an appropriate vector space of quantum fields on
V
. Locality is identified as the condition making sensible this notion of “quantum field on a set of quantum fields”. The construction of such a comonad
E
is spelled out in detail below, and it is proved that vertex algebras are
E
-coalgebras.

Many of the constructions presented here are part of now standard introductory material on vertex algebras, and in particular many results are cited from the textbook
[
9
]
. In the book
[
10
]
, vector spaces were exchanged for topological algebras, and the construction of the comonad given in this note relies on some technical advantages of the latter viewpoint. In particular the enveloping topological associative algebra of a vertex algebra, described in
[
7
]
, plays a part in our construction.

The basic objects in our construction are certain formal series, which we follow
[
10
]
in referring to as continuous distributions, with coefficients in a topological associative algebra. To dispell possible ambiguities, we record the definition of topological algebra in the sense that we shall use it.

Definition 3
.

A topological algebra over a field
k
consists of a unital associative algebra
U
equipped with a set of left ideals

U_{0}\supset U_{1}\supset U_{2}\supset\ldots,

such that:

1.

\cap_{p}U_{p}=0
,

2.

for each
u\in U
and
p\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, there exists
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
such that
U_{N}u\subset U_{p}
.

3.

U
is complete, i.e., for any sequence
u_{p}\in U/U_{p}
such that
u_{p+1}\equiv u_{p}\bmod{U_{p}}
for all
p\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, there exists
u\in U
such that
u_{p}\equiv u\bmod{U_{p}}
for all
p\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
.

A morphism
\phi:U^{1}\rightarrow U^{2}
of topological algebras is a homomorphism of unital associative algebras such that, for each
p_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
there exists
p_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
such that
\phi(U^{1}_{p_{1}})\subset U^{2}_{p_{2}}
.

We remark that a choice of topology on the base field
k
does not enter into this definition, so a better name might be “linearly topologised associative algebra”.

Definition 4
.

Let
U
be a topological algebra. A
U
-module
M
is said to be smooth if, for all
x\in M
there exists
p\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
such that
U_{p}x=0
.

Example 1
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}=\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathfrak{g}_{n}
be a
\mathbb{Z}
-graded Lie algebra, consider the limit

U=\lim U(\mathfrak{g})/U(\mathfrak{g})\mathfrak{g}_{\geq p},

under the system of morphisms
U(\mathfrak{g})/U(\mathfrak{g})\mathfrak{g}_{\geq p+1}\rightarrow U(\mathfrak{g})/U(\mathfrak{g})\mathfrak{g}_{\geq p}
. Set
U_{p}\subset U
to be the kernel of the canonical morphism
U\rightarrow U(\mathfrak{g})/U(\mathfrak{g})\mathfrak{g}_{\geq p}
. Intuitively
U
consists of infinite sums
\sum_{i}u_{i}
where
u_{i}\in U(\mathfrak{g})
for each
i
, and for each
p
all but finitely summands lie in
U_{p}
. The second condition of Definition
3
is satisfied since
\mathfrak{g}
is graded, and this condition in turn ensures that the product in
U
is well defined.

Definition 5
.

Let
U
be a topological algebra. A continuous distribution on
U
is a formal series

a(w)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{(n)}w^{-n-1},\qquad a_{(n)}\in U

such that, for each
p\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, there exists
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
such that
a_{(n)}\in U_{p}
for all
n\geq N
.

The term-by-term product
a(w)b(w)
of two continuous distributions is not in general well defined. Instead we have the normally ordered product, originating in quantum field theory:

\displaystyle:a(w)b(w):=a(w)_{+}b(w)+b(w)a(w)_{-},

where

\displaystyle a(w)_{+}=\sum_{n<0}a_{(n)}w^{-n-1},\qquad a(w)_{-}=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{(n)}w^{-n-1},

and its generalisation the “
n^{\text{th}}
product” operations, given in the following definition. (The normally ordered product is recovered as
:a(w)b(w):=a(w)_{(-1)}b(w)
.)

Definition 6
.

Let
a(w)
and
b(w)
be continuous distributions on
U
, and let
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. The
n^{\text{th}}
product of

and

is another continuous distribution on

, defined by the formula

\displaystyle a(w)_{(n)}b(w)=\res_{z}\left(a(z)b(w)i_{z,w}(z-w)^{n}-b(w)a(z)i_{w,z}(z-w)^{n}\right).

(1)

Here

denotes the formal residue, i.e., extraction of the coefficient of

. The expressions

denote the two distinct binomial series expansions

\displaystyle i_{z,w}(z-w)^{n}

\displaystyle=z^{n}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}\binom{n}{j}z^{-j}w^{j}\in k((z))((w))

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad i_{w,z}(z-w)^{n}

\displaystyle=(-w)^{n}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}\binom{n}{j}w^{-j}z^{j}\in k((w))((z)).

We observe that the definition of
a(w)_{(n)}b(w)
involves certain infinite sums of elements of

Definition 7
.

We say that
a(w)

are mutually local if
(z-w)^{N}[a(z),b(w)]=0

a(w)_{(n)}b(w)=0
for all

This is a convenient point to state the definition of vertex algebra.

Definition 8
.

A vertex algebra consists of a vector space
V
, a vector
\left|0\right>\in V
, and a collection of bilinear products
V\otimes V\rightarrow V
, denoted
a\otimes b\mapsto a_{(n)}b
for
n\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

1.

for each pair of elements
a,b\in V
there exists
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
such that
a_{(n)}b=0
for all
n\geq N
,

2.

for each

we have
a_{(n)}\left|0\right>=0
for all
n\geq 0
,

3.

the linear map

V\rightarrow(\en{V})[[w,w^{-1}]],\qquad a\mapsto Y(a,w)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{(n)}w^{-n-1}

satisfies

\displaystyle Y(\left|0\right>,w)

\displaystyle=I_{V}

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad Y(a_{(n)}b,w)

\displaystyle=Y(a,w)_{(n)}Y(b,w),

for all

. Here the

This definition of vertex algebra is equivalent to various others which appear in the literature. For a discussion of equivalent definitions, see
[
6

Lemma 1

Let

Let

Proposition 6

Let
(U,\mathcal{F})\in\CQ

is continuous. We must verify that these distributions are mutually local. Indeed

\displaystyle[a(w)(x),b(w)(y)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{m,n\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}\binom{m}{j}(a(w)_{(j)}b(w))_{[m+n-j]}x^{-m-1}y^{-n-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}(a(w)_{(j)}b(w))_{[\ell]}y^{-\ell-1}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\binom{m}{j}x^{-m-1}y^{m-j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}\frac{1}{j!}\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}(a(w)_{(j)}b(w))(y)\partial_{y}^{j}\delta(x,y),

where
\delta(x,y)=(i_{x,y}-i_{y,x})(x-y)^{-1}
is the formal Dirac delta function. It follows that

(x-y)^{N}[a(w)(x),b(w)(y)]=0

whenever
N
is sufficiently large that
(z-w)^{N}[a(z),b(w)]=0
.
∎

Proposition 7

\Delta:E\Rightarrow E^{2}

Proof.

Let

The morphism we seek is, of course,

3
Adjoint functors and the quantum field comonad

Let
E:\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
be a comonad. The Eilenberg-Moore category
\mathcal{C}^{E}
is defined to be the category of
E
-coalgebras, i.e., the category whose objects are objects of
\mathcal{C}
equipped with an
E
-coalgebra structure, and whose morphisms are morphisms of
\mathcal{C}
compatible with
E
-coalgebra structures. The Kleisli category
\mathcal{C}_{E}
is defined to be the full subcategory of
\mathcal{C}^{E}
consisting of free
E
-coalgebras.

Let us denote by
\vertex
the category of vertex algebras. From the results of the previous section,
\CQ^{E}
is equivalent to
\vertex
, and the comonad
E
is recovered from a pair of adjoint functors as follows. Define the functor
C:\CQ\rightarrow\vertex
taking
(U,\mathcal{F})\in\CQ
to the vertex algebra
\overline{\mathcal{F}}
. Define the functor
U:\vertex\rightarrow\CQ
taking a vertex algebra
V
to the pair
(U(V),\mathcal{F}_{V})
, where
U(V)
is the associative enveloping algebra of
V
constructed as in Definitions
11
and
12
above, and
\mathcal{F}_{V}
is the set of formal distributions

\{a(w)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{[n]}w^{-n-1}\mid a\in V\}

on
U(V)
. Then
E=UC
.

In fact it is possible to factor
C
through a sort of dual Rees construction in the following manner. Let
\prevertex
denote the category of pairs
(W,\mathcal{F})
consisting of a vector space
W
and a set
\mathcal{F}
of mutually local quantum fields on
W
. We refer to such an object as a
prevertex algebra
and denote the category of prevertex algebras as
\prevertex
. We introduce a functor

R:\CQ\rightarrow\prevertex

defined on objects as follows. Let
(U,\mathcal{F})\in\CQ
, then
R(U,\mathcal{F})=(W,\mathcal{F}_{1})
where

W=\bigoplus_{p\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}U/U_{p}

and
\mathcal{F}_{1}
is the set of fields on
W
induced from elements of
\mathcal{F}
in the obvious way. Since
\mathcal{F}
consists of continuous distributions,
\mathcal{F}_{1}
consists of quantum fields. Now there is a functor
G:\prevertex\rightarrow\vertex
, taking
(W,\mathcal{F})
to the closure
\overline{\mathcal{F}}
, defined in essentially the same way as in the proof of Proposition
5
. As in that case, the vector space
\overline{\mathcal{F}}
acquires a natural structure of vertex algebra.

We thus have three categories and three functors, as follows:

\textstyle{\vertex\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{U}
\textstyle{\CQ\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{R}
\textstyle{\prevertex\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{G}

The functor
C
is recovered as
C=GR
, and the comonad
E
as
E=UGR
. We remark that
GRU
is naturally equivalent to the identity functor on
\vertex
, a fact which implies idempotency of
E
(and seems to be a little stronger).

The relationship between the categories
\prevertex
and
\vertex
is intuitively similar to the relationship between
\CQ
and
\vertex
, without the additional complications of topological algebras and completions. However the functor
G
is not adjoint to
RU
as there is no natural morphism relating
RUG(X)
and
X
for all
X\in\prevertex
, although one of the two rigidity axioms, namely

\textstyle{U\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\Id}
\textstyle{U\circ(FR\circ U)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{=}
\textstyle{U}
\textstyle{(U\circ FR)\circ U\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

is satisfied, being a formal consequence of the fact that
(U,GR)
is an adjoint pair. Nor is there a relation of adjointness between

and the obvious forgetful functor
\vertex\rightarrow\prevertex
, for similar reasons. Thus, although
\prevertex
is a natural starting point, it seems necessary to deviate from it to the category
\CQ
in order to get a good adjoint pair and our quantum field comonad
