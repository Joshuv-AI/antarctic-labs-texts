1.
Introduction

The classical Boltzmann equation describes the behavior of a rarefied collisional gas, consisting of a very large number of identical
particles undergoing perfectly elastic binary collisions. The velocity distribution function
f(t,x,v)
is the probable number density of particles at time
t
, at point
x
, having speed
v
. The Boltzmann equation is written as

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f=Q(f,f),\quad f(0,x,v)=f_{0}(x,v)

for
x\in\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
v\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
t\geq 0
.
On the left-hand side of the Boltzmann equation we see a transport operator which describes the translation of a particle distribution without the presence of other forces and collisions. The nonlinear quadratic term,
Q(f,f),
on the right-hand side is added to account for the collisions between particles. When two particles, initially traveling at velocities
v
and
v_{*}
, collide with each other, their velocities change due to the interaction. After the collision, the new velocities of the particles are denoted as
v^{\prime}
and
v_{*}^{\prime}
, respectively. These post-collisional velocities
v^{\prime}
and
v_{*}^{\prime}
are determined by the specific dynamics of the collision process, which can depend on factors such as the masses of the particles and the nature of the interaction between them. We assume that the mass of each particle is identical and normalize it to be 1 throughout the paper. Under elastic collisions, according to the laws of conservation of momentum and energy, the following relationships hold:

v+v_{*}=v^{\prime}+v_{*}^{\prime},\quad|v|^{2}+|v_{*}|^{2}=|v^{\prime}|^{2}+|v_{*}^{\prime}|^{2}.

(1.1)

From these conservation laws, we derive four constraints on the six variables
v^{\prime}
and
v_{*}^{\prime}
, leaving two degrees of freedom. Specifically, the post-collisional velocities
v^{\prime}
and
v_{*}^{\prime}
can be expressed as:

v^{\prime}=v-((v-v_{*})\cdot n)n\quad\text{and}\quad v_{*}^{\prime}=v_{*}+((v-v_{*})\cdot n)n,

(1.2)

using the vector
n\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{2}
. Physically, if gas particles are represented as billiard balls, the vector
n\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{2}
, known as the impact direction, denotes the unit vector connecting the centers of the balls at the moment of collision, as illustrated in Figure
2
.

In this paper, we frequently use an alternative representation of the post-collisional velocities
v^{\prime}
and
v_{*}^{\prime}
involving another angular variable
\sigma\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
. This representation is given by:

v^{\prime}=\frac{v+v_{*}}{2}+\frac{|v-v_{*}|}{2}\sigma\quad\text{and}\quad v_{*}^{\prime}=\frac{v+v_{*}}{2}-\frac{|v-v_{*}|}{2}\sigma.

In Figure
1
, we illustrate the possible locations of
v^{\prime}
and
v_{*}^{\prime}
for given
v
and
v_{*}
. As shown in the figure, the polar angle of
\sigma
along the
z
-axis, corresponding to
v-v_{*}
, varies from
0
to
\pi
, while the polar angle of
n
ranges from
0
to
\frac{\pi}{2}
, placing
n
in
\mathbb{S}^{2}_{+}
. Interestingly, for fixed
v
and
v_{*}
,
v^{\prime}
and
v_{*}^{\prime}
always lie on a single sphere, and the Jacobian determinant between
(v,v_{*})
and
(v^{\prime},v_{*}^{\prime})
is always equal to 1.

Next, we define the collision operator using the variables
v
,
v_{*}
,
v^{\prime}
,
v_{*}^{\prime}
, and
n
as described in (

). It is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{split}Q(f,f)(t,v)&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{S}_{+}^{2}}B(|v-v_{*}|,\cos\theta)\left(f(t,v^{\prime})f(t,v^{\prime}_{*})-f(t,v)f(t,v_{*})\right)\;dndv_{*},\\
&:=Q^{+}(f,f)(t,v)-f(t,v)Lf(t,v),\end{split}

(1.3)

where

\displaystyle\cos\theta=\left(\frac{v_{*}-v}{|v_{*}-v|},n\right),\quad\theta\in\left[0,\frac{\pi}{2}\right].

Here, the operators are local in
t
and
x
; we abuse notation by writing
f(t,x,v)=f(t,v)
. The gain term,
Q^{+}(f,f)(t,v)
, represents an increase in the density of particles with velocity
v
due to collisions involving velocities
v^{\prime}
and
v_{*}^{\prime}
. Because the equations in (
1.1
) are time-reversible, if particles with velocities
v
and
v_{*}
collide to become
v^{\prime}
and
v_{*}^{\prime}
, then particles with velocities
v^{\prime}
and
v_{*}^{\prime}
can collide to revert to
v
and
v_{*}
.
The loss term,
f(t,v)Lf(t,v)
, indicates a decrease in the density of particles with velocity
v
due to collisions with particles of velocity
v_{*}
, resulting in their disappearance.

Now, we explain the collision kernel. Depending on the assumptions regarding the interaction potentials (e.g., hard-sphere potential or inverse-power-law potential), the collision kernel can be expressed as:

B(|v-v_{*}|,\cos\theta)=b(\cos\theta)|v-v_{*}|^{\gamma}

for some constant
\gamma
. For instance, when particles collide like billiard balls (hard-sphere potential), we write
B(|v-v_{*}|,\cos\theta)=|(v-v_{*})\cdot n|
.
If
\int_{\mathbb{S}_{+}^{2}}b(\cos\theta)\,dn<\infty
or if
b(\cos\theta)\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}_{+}^{2})
, the collision kernel is called a cut-off kernel (or is with angular cutoff), following Grad’s angular cutoff criterion in
[
34
]
. Otherwise, it is referred to as a non-cutoff kernel (or is without angular cutoff). In the case of a non-cutoff collision kernel, there is a nonintegrable singularity around zero for
\theta
. (Some authors place the singularity near
\pi/2-\theta\approx 0
.) Recently, the vanishing angular singularity limit from the inverse-power-law potential to the hard-sphere potential has been studied in
[
45
]
.

By the famous Boltzmann H-theorem, we formally see that the total entropy of the system
-\iint f\log fdxdv
will monotonically increase in time. Then we expect that the distribution will converge (in some sense) to an entropy maximizing state. One of the candidates for the states of maximum entropy is so-called the (local) Maxwellian distribution which is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(t,x,v)=\rho(t,x)\frac{e^{-\frac{|v-u(t,x)|^{2}}{2T(t,x)}}}{(2\pi T(t,x))^{3/2}},

where the Boltzmann constant
k_{B}
is assumed to be 1. Here,
\rho,

u
, and
T
correspond to the physical terms such as mass density, macroscopic fluid velocity, and local temperature, respectively. A simple calculation can show that
Q(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{M})(t,x,v)=~0
. If
\rho
,
u
, and
T
are chosen so that the left-hand side of the Boltzmann equation satisfies
(\partial_{t}+v\cdot\nabla_{x})\mathcal{M}=0
,
(\rho,u,T)
solves some macroscopic conservation laws. In this case, the local Maxwellian
\mathcal{M}
can be written in some specific forms depending on the domain and boundary conditions. We refer to
[
25
]
for specific forms. In the paper, Desvillettes actually
proved that the renormalized solution of the Boltzmann equation converges weakly in
L^{1}
to a local time-dependent Maxwellian state in the large-time asymptotics within a bounded domain. It was shown that this local Maxwellian further satisfies
(\partial_{t}+v\cdot\nabla_{x})\mathcal{M}=0
.
Additionally, before this result, Arkeryd proved in
[
9
]
the convergence of a renormalized solution to the global Maxwellian. The local stability of the global Maxwellian has now been very well-known. See
[
55
,
1
,

,

]
for the proof of local stability nearby equilibrium for both cutoff and noncutoff cases. The existence of global solutions was firstly proved by Carleman
[

in 1933 for the spatially homogeneous case. Then for the spatially inhomogeneous case, the global existence was proved in the perturbative framework
[

]
and near vacuum
[
41
]
. Then the global existence of renormalized solutions to the Boltzmann equation by DiPerna-Lions
[
27

in 1989 which does not require size restriction on the initial data. This framework has been extended to the noncutoff case by Alexandre and Villani in
[

. The nonlinear energy method was developed to prove the global existence to the global wellposedness of the Vlasov(-Poisson or -Maxwell)-Boltzmann system in
[
37
,
38
,
59
]
. See also
[
51
]
for the energy method for the Boltzmann equation.
In
[

,

]
, the local stability of the Maxwellian for the Landau equation was proved based on the construction of the classical solution via the nonlinear energy method developed in
[

]
. Regarding the global existence and stability in a bounded domain with general physical boundary conditions (e.g., inflow, bounce-back, specular, and diffuse reflection), see
[

,
50
,

]
.

In contrast to an elastic collision, an inelastic collision is a collision in which kinetic energy is not conserved, and this type of gas is referred to as granular gas. Physically, in each inelastic collision, there is some loss of momentum in the impact direction, though the total momentum remains conserved. Let
0<\alpha\leq 1
be the coefficient of normal restitution. For the impact direction
n\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{2}
, the following equations hold:

\displaystyle\begin{split}(v^{\prime}-v_{*}^{\prime})\cdot n&=-\alpha(v-v_{*})\cdot n,\\
(v^{\prime}-v_{*}^{\prime})-((v^{\prime}-v_{*}^{\prime})\cdot n)n&=(v-v_{*})-((v-v_{*})\cdot n)n.\end{split}

(1.4)

If
\alpha=1
, the collision is elastic, satisfying (
1.1
). If
\alpha=0
, the collision is sticky, meaning the particles travel together after the collision. Generally, the coefficient
\alpha
depends on
|v-v_{*}|
and
\theta
, the angle between
v-v_{*}
and
n
. However, in many cases, this dependence is not considered significant, and a constant restitution coefficient is often used.
Further details about inelastic collisions and the Boltzmann equation will be covered in Section
1.4
, Preliminaries.

Many physicists and mathematicians are interested in the behavior of solutions to both the elastic and inelastic Boltzmann equations. Numerous numerical, physical, and mathematical studies have been conducted on this topic. In mathematics, the
L^{\infty}
norm, weighted
L^{\infty}
norm,
L^{1}
norm, and weighted
L^{1}
norm of the Boltzmann equation were among the first to be studied.

The
L^{\infty}
norm is particularly important because it allows us to consider the pointwise bounded behavior of the solution, which is much stronger than merely being energy-bounded (i.e.,
\int f|v|^{2}\,dv<+\infty
). Based on
L^{\infty}
estimates, the upper Maxwellian bounds were established in
[
32
]
, which are used in the local existence theory of strong solutions as seen in
[
33
,
49
]
. Additionally, the Hölder continuity modulus and the lower Maxwellian bounds are dependent on the
L^{\infty}
norm of the solution, as discussed in
[
43
,
44
]
. This lower bound is crucial for demonstrating the convergence of solutions to Maxwellian equilibrium, as shown in
[
26
]
.
One of the most significant properties of inelastic collisions is that they are not time-reversible, making it impossible to determine the direction of entropy with respect to time. In the case of elastic collisions, the maximum entropy principle and Boltzmann’s
H
-theorem ensure that the solution converges to the Maxwellian distribution. However, for inelastic collisions, the absence of the
H
-theorem makes it more challenging to establish an equilibrium state.

Moreover, the behavior of solutions varies depending on the additional physical forces, such as a heat bath and friction, that are incorporated into the kinetic model. Without external forces, the kinetic energy decreases until all gas particles stop, a state referred to as the
cooling state
. Details about these models are introduced in
[
65
]
by Villani. Notably, in
[
54
]
, Mischler, Mouhot, and Ricard studied the
cooling process
and proved that the solutions converge to a Dirac mass in the
weak-*
measure sense asymptotically over a long time.

If kinetic energy is lost but the total density is conserved, it is predicted that the number of particles with low velocities will increase over time, while the number of particles with high velocities will decrease. Moreover, as velocity approaches zero, the density function increases rapidly over time (see Figure
3
). This motivates our study of the behavior of solutions over time for inelastic collisions without any external forces in this paper.

In this paper, we study the
L^{\infty}
norm of the solution of the homogeneous inelastic Boltzmann equation for hard spheres. We will then extend the
L^{\infty}
norm to establish upper Maxwellian bounds. During the cooling process, as the solution tends towards
\delta_{v=0}
and the kinetic energy approaches zero over time, it becomes crucial to derive pointwise upper bounds for the solution.

If

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\infty,0}=\|f\|_{\infty}=\esssup_{v\in\mathbb{R}^{3}}|f(x)|.

The indicator function of a subset

\displaystyle\chi_{A}(x)=\begin{cases}1,\quad x\in A,\\

Known results for the elastic collisions

In particular, if the velocity distribution function

1.3.
Known results for the inelastic collisions

When gas particles collide with each other and lose energy, it is termed an inelastic collision, and such a gas is referred to as a granular gas. The behavior of a granular gas differs significantly from that of an ideal gas undergoing elastic collisions. The basic concepts of kinetic theory and exciting phenomena in granular gas dynamics were introduced in
[

In 2004, Bobylev, Gamba, and Panferov

\displaystyle v^{\prime}=v-\beta((v-v_{*})\cdot n)n,\quad v_{*}^{\prime}=v_{*}+\beta((v-v_{*})\cdot n)n.

In Figure

(1.8)

1.4.2.
Inelastic Boltzmann collision operator

Now we introduce the spatially homogeneous inelastic Boltzmann equation. It goes by

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f=Q(f,f),\quad f(0,v)=f_{0}(v),

(1.15)

where
v\in\mathbb{R}^{3},\;t\geq 0
and
f\geq 0
. (The operator is local in
t
and we abbreviate
t
for notational convenience.) Taking
\phi(v)
to be a suitably regular test function, the weak form of the inelastic Boltzmann collision operator
Q(f,g)(t,v)
is written by

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}Q(f,g)(t,v)\phi(v)\;dv

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{S}_{+}^{2}}B(|v-v_{*}|,\cos\theta)f(t,v_{*})g(t,v)(\phi(v^{\prime})-\phi(v))\>dndv_{*}dv,

(1.16)

where

\displaystyle\cos\theta=\left(\frac{v_{*}-v}{|v_{*}-v|},n\right),\quad\theta\in\left[0,\frac{\pi}{2}\right].

(1.17)

In the case
f=g
in (
1.16
), we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}Q(f,f)(t,v)\phi(v)\;dv&=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{S}_{+}^{2}}B(|v-v_{*}|,\cos\theta)f(t,v_{*})f(t,v)\\
&\quad\quad\quad\times(\phi(v^{\prime})+\phi(v_{*}^{\prime})-\phi(v)-\phi(v_{*}))\>dndv_{*}dv.\end{split}

(1.18)

Taking
\phi(v)=1,|v|^{2},
and
v_{i}
into above equation, for any
t>0
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f(t,v)\;dv=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f_{0}(v)\;dv,\text{\quad and \quad}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f(t,v)|v|^{2}\;dv\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f_{0}(v)|v|^{2}\;dv

(1.19)

by (
1.14
), and

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f(t,v)(v\cdot e_{i})\;dv=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f_{0}(v)(v\cdot e_{i})\;dv

(1.20)

for
i=1,2,3
. Here
e_{1}=(1,0,0),\;e_{2}=(0,1,0)
and
e_{3}=(0,0,1)
. In this paper, we suppose

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f_{0}(v)\;dv=1,\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f_{0}(v)(v\cdot e_{i})\;dv=0,\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f_{0}(v)|v|^{2}\;dv=1

(1.21)

for
i=1,2,3
, without loss of generality. See also
[
31
,
52
,
53
,
54
]
in which the authors consider the same initial conditions (

). By (

)-(
1.21
), it holds that

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\|f(t,v)\|_{1,0}=\|f_{0}(v)\|_{1,0}=1,\quad\|f(t,v)\|_{1,2}\leq\|f_{0}(v)\|_{1,2}<+\infty\\
&\quad\quad\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f_{0}(v)(v\cdot e_{i})\;dv=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f(t,v)(v\cdot e_{i})\;dv=0\end{split}

(1.22)

for
t>0
and
f_{0}\in L^{1}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
. Moreover,
the initial entropy is denoted as

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f_{0}(v)\log f_{0}(v)\;dv=H_{0}.

(1.23)

Throughout the paper, we will consider a hard sphere model

\displaystyle B(|v-v_{*}|,\cos\theta)=|(v-v_{*})\cdot n|=|v-v_{*}||\cos\theta|

for
\theta\in[0,\frac{\pi}{2}]
. Since

is approximated by

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{S}_{+}^{2}}|(v-v_{*})\cdot n|f(t,v_{*})g(t,v)(\delta(v^{\prime}-v_{1})-\delta(v-v_{1}))\>dndv_{*}dv

\displaystyle:=Q^{+}(f,g)(t,v_{1})-g(t,v_{1})Lf(t,v_{1}).

By changing variables

with Jacobian determinant

\displaystyle\begin{split}Q^{+}(f,g)(t,v_{1})&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{S}_{+}^{2}}|(v-v_{*})\cdot n|f(t,v_{*})g(t,v)\delta(v^{\prime}-v_{1})\>dndv_{*}dv\\

And we have

(1.25)

1.4.3.
Carleman type representation for
granular gases

In 1957, Carleman introduced a new representation of the gain term collision operator for the Boltzmann equation with a cutoff kernel. When
v^{\prime}
and
v
are fixed, the set of possible vectors for
v_{*}^{\prime}
forms a hyperplane orthogonal to
v-v^{\prime}
and passing through
v
(see Figure
1
). Using this fact, he obtained the gain term integrated over the variables
v^{\prime}
and
v_{*}^{\prime}
, where the range is in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and a hyperplane for each, as described in
[
22
]
. Moreover, there is a Carleman-type representation for granular gas presented in
[
52
, Proposition 1.5]
by Mischler and Mouhot. To obtain a Carleman-type representation, we define a vector
P
as:

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\beta}v^{\prime}-\left(\frac{1}{\beta}-1\right)v,

which is located on the extension line of
v
and
v^{\prime}
in ratio

\displaystyle|P-v|:|P-v^{\prime}|=1:1-\beta

for
\beta\neq 1
as in Figure
4
. For
\beta=1
(elastic collisions), the vector
P
equals
v^{\prime}
. Then
P
also satisfies
P-v\;\bot\;P-v_{*}
.
Next, we introduce a 2-dimensional hyperplane
E^{v^{\prime}-v}_{P}
which is normal to the vector
v^{\prime}-v
around the vector
P
; it is defined as

\displaystyle\begin{split}E^{v^{\prime}-v}_{P}=\bigg\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:\;(x-P)\;\bot\;(v-v^{\prime})\text{\; where \;}P=\frac{1}{\beta}v^{\prime}-\left(\frac{1}{\beta}-1\right)v\;\bigg\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}.\end{split}

(1.32)

We notice that if
v
and
v^{\prime}
are fixed,
v_{*}^{\prime}
is in the hyperplane
E^{v^{\prime}-v}_{P}
. Then
Q^{+}(f,g)(t,v_{1})
is expressed by

\displaystyle\begin{split}&Q^{+}(f,g)(t,v_{1})\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}Q^{+}(f,g)(t,v)\delta(v-v_{1})\;dv\\
&=\frac{1}{\beta^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\delta(v-v_{1})g(t,{{}^{\prime}v})|v-{{}^{\prime}v}|^{-1}\int_{{{}^{\prime}v}_{*}\in E^{v-{{}^{\prime}v}}_{P}}f(t,{{}^{\prime}v}_{*})\;dE_{{{}^{\prime}v}_{*}}d{{}^{\prime}v}dv\\
&=\frac{1}{\beta^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\delta(v^{\prime}-v_{1})g(t,v)|v^{\prime}-v|^{-1}\int_{v_{*}\in E^{v^{\prime}-v}_{P}}f(t,v_{*})\;dE_{v_{*}}dvdv^{\prime},\end{split}

(1.33)

where
dE_{{{}^{\prime}v}_{*}}
and
dE_{v_{*}}
are the Lebesgue measures on the plane
E^{v-{{}^{\prime}v}}_{P}
and
E^{v^{\prime}-v}_{P}
, respectively. This Carleman-type representation is used crucially for the estimates of the gain term,
Q^{+}(f,f)
, through the paper.

1.5.
Main results

We are now ready to state our main theorems. Given
t>0
and
v\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, we prove that the solution

is bounded pointwise from above by
C_{1,f_{0}}(1+t)^{3}
in the region including zero velocity, and by

).
We have also derived an upper bound that depends on the coefficient of normal restitution constant

and the time

, and it becomes a constant when

Moreover, the first part of the minimum in (

3

propagation in the first part of the minimum in (

Here, we define the cooling time

in the cooling process as

\displaystyle\begin{split}T_{c}:&=\inf{\{T\geq 0,\;\mathcal{E}(t)=0\;\;\forall t>T\}}\\
&=\sup{\{S\geq 0,\;\mathcal{E}(t)>0\;\;\forall t<S\}},\end{split}

where

is the total kinetic energy, which is defined as

Theorem 1.1
.

Let

be the coefficient of normal restitution constant, which satisfies (
1.4

) holds and

for

\displaystyle f(t,v)\leq C_{1}(\|f_{0}\|_{\infty,s})\min{\left\{\frac{(1+t)^{3}}{1+|v|},\frac{e^{C_{2}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}-1\right)t}}{(1+|v|)^{2}}\right\}}

and for every

.

such that

\displaystyle f(t,v)\leq C_{3}(\|f_{0}\|_{\infty,s})\min{\left\{\frac{1+t}{|v|^{2}},\frac{e^{C_{2}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}-1\right)t}}{(1+|v|)^{2}}\right\}}

In this Section, we estimate the

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
h_{1}(t)
and
h_{2}(t)
be continuous real functions on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
h_{1}(t)>0
. If

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}f+h_{1}f\leq h_{2}

for
t>0
, then

\displaystyle f(t)\leq\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\frac{h_{2}(s)}{h_{1}(s)}+f(0)

for
t>0
.

Proof.

From

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(e^{\int^{t}_{0}h_{1}(s)ds}f\right)\leq e^{\int^{t}_{0}h_{1}(s)ds}h_{2}(t),

we obtain

\displaystyle f(t)

\displaystyle\leq\int^{t}_{0}e^{-\int^{t}_{\tau}h_{1}(s)ds}h_{2}(\tau)\;d\tau+f(0)

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-e^{-\int^{t}_{0}h_{1}(s)ds}\right)\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\frac{h_{2}(s)}{h_{1}(s)}+f(0)\leq\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\frac{h_{2}(s)}{h_{1}(s)}+f(0)

for
t>0
.
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

Assume that (
1.21
) holds, and that the initial entropy
H_{0}
in (
1.23
) is finite. Then
there exists a positive constant
C
that depends only on
\|f_{0}\|_{1,2}
, such that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f(t,v)\log f(t,v)dv\leq Ct\left(\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}-1\right)+H_{0}

(2.1)

for
t>0
.

Proof.

We denote

. From the weak form of the collision operator (

), we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f\log f\;dv=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}Q(f,f)(t,v)\log f\;dv

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{S}_{+}^{2}}ff_{*}\log\frac{f^{\prime}f_{*}^{\prime}}{ff_{*}}|(v-v_{*})\cdot n|\;dndvdv_{*}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{S}_{+}^{2}}ff_{*}\left(\log\frac{f^{\prime}f_{*}^{\prime}}{ff_{*}}-\frac{f^{\prime}f_{*}^{\prime}}{ff_{*}}+1\right)|(v-v_{*})\cdot n|\;dndvdv_{*}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{S}_{+}^{2}}\left(f^{\prime}f_{*}^{\prime}-ff_{*}\right)|(v-v_{*})\cdot n|\;dndvdv_{*}.

(2.2)

Taking a change of variables
(v,v_{*},n)\rightarrow(v^{\prime},v_{*}^{\prime},-n)
with the Jacobian determinant
\alpha
and (
1.4
), we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{S}_{+}^{2}}f^{\prime}f_{*}^{\prime}|(v-v_{*})\cdot n|\;dndvdv_{*}\\
&=\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{S}_{+}^{2}}ff_{*}|(v-v_{*})\cdot n|\;dndvdv_{*}.\end{split}

(2.3)

By using

for

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f\log f\;dv\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}-1\right)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{S}_{+}^{2}}ff_{*}|(v-v_{*})\cdot n|\;dndvdv_{*}.

(2.4)

By the assumption (

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{S}_{+}^{2}}ff_{*}|(v-v_{*})\cdot n|\;dndvdv_{*}

\displaystyle\lesssim 1.

Lastly, by integrating (
