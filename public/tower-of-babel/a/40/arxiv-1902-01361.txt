Commuting Ordinary Differential Operators and the Dixmier Test This paper is a contribution to the Special Issue on Algebraic Methods in Dynamical Systems. The full collection is available at https://www.emis.de/journals/SIGMA/AMDS2018.html
Emma PREVIATO †, Sonia L. RUEDA ‡ and Maria-Angeles ZURRO §

Abstract
The Burchnall–Chaundy problem is classical in differential algebra, seeking to describe all commutative subalgebras of a ring of ordinary differential operators whose coefficients are functions in a given class. It received less attention when posed in the (first) Weyl algebra, namely for polynomial coefficients, while the classification of commutative subalgebras of the Weyl algebra is in itself an important open problem. Centralizers are maximal-commutative subalgebras, and we review the properties of a basis of the centralizer of an operator L L in normal form, following the approach of K.R. Goodearl, with the ultimate goal of obtaining such bases by computational routines. Our first step is to establish the Dixmier test , based on a lemma by J. Dixmier and the choice of a suitable filtration, to give necessary conditions for an operator M M to be in the centralizer of L L . Whenever the centralizer equals the algebra generated by L L and M M , we call L L , M M a Burchnall–Chaundy (BC) pair. A construction of BC pairs is presented for operators of order 4 4 in the first Weyl algebra. Moreover, for true rank r r pairs, by means of differential subresultants, we effectively compute the fiber of the rank r r spectral sheaf over their spectral curve.

Preliminaries

We are primarily interested in the ring of differential operators
{\mathcal{D}}
, but it is useful to view it as a subring of the ring of formal pseudodifferential operators
\Psi
, namely the set

\displaystyle\Psi=\left\{\sum_{j=-\infty}^{N}u_{j}(x)\partial^{j},\ u_{j}\ \text{\rm analytic in
some connected neighborhood of}\ x=0\right\}.

If we think of these symbols as acting on functions of
x
by multiplication and differentiation:
(u(x)\partial)f(x)=u\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}f
, and formally integrate by parts:
\int(uf^{\prime})=uf-\int(u^{\prime}f)
, we can motivate the composition rules

\displaystyle\partial u=u\partial+u^{\prime},

\displaystyle\partial^{-1}u=u\partial^{-1}-u^{\prime}\partial^{-2}+u^{{}^{\prime\prime}}\partial^{-3}-\cdots

and easily check an extended Leibnitz rule for
A,B\in\Psi
:

\displaystyle A\circ B=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{i!}\tilde{\partial}^{i}A\ast\partial^{i}B,

where
\tilde{\partial}
is a partial differentiation w.r.t. the symbol

\partial
and
\ast
has the effect of bringing all functions to the
left and powers of
\partial
to the right. Observe that the first Weyl algebra
A_{1}({\bf C})
is a subring of the ring of differential operators
{\bf C}(x)[\partial]
with
\partial=\partial=\partial/\partial x
and
[\partial,x]=1
. Hence a subring of
\Psi
.

The differential ring
\Psi
contains the differential subring
{\mathcal{D}}
of differential operators
A=\sum\limits_{0}^{N}u_{j}\partial^{j}
and we denote by
(\ )_{+}
the projection
B_{+}=\sum\limits_{0}^{N}u_{j}\partial^{j}
where
B=\sum\limits_{-\infty}^{N}u_{j}\partial^{j}
.

We also see that if
L
has order
n>0
and its leading coefficient is regular, i.e.,
u_{n}(0)\not=0
, then
L
can be brought to standard form

\displaystyle L=\partial^{n}+u_{n-2}(x)\partial^{n-2}+u_{n-3}(x)\partial^{n-3}+\cdots+u_{0}(x)

by using change of variable and conjugation by a function, which are the only two automorphisms of
{\mathcal{D}}
; we shall always assume
L
to be in standard form, i.e.,
u_{1}(x)=0
. We note that in
[
2
]
, for completeness, the authors recall a(n essentially formal) proof of the facts we mentioned, to bring
L
into standard form.

Remark 2.1
.

The coefficients
u_{j}(x)
in the definition of
\Psi
are often required to be analytic functions near
x=0
, because the algebro-geometric constructions preserve this restriction; typically, statements of differential algebra hold formally, and in particular, our results are mostly concerned with polynomial coefficients, therefore we do not aim at complete generality. Analytic/formal cases of the ring
\Psi
are treated in
[
41
]
, with emphasis on certain types of modules over
\Psi

2.1
Centralizers for ODOs

Unless otherwise specified, we will work with a differential field

(

Lemma 2.3

(
[

)

If

\exists\,p\in{\bf Z}\cup\{-\infty\}

\operatorname{ord}[M,L]=p+\operatorname{ord}L

If

Remark 2.5
.

The analog of Corollary

In

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}=\partial+u_{-1}(x)\partial^{-1}+u_{-2}(x)\partial^{-2}+\cdots.

The next result can be shown by using the fact that

Theorem 2.6

(I. Schur,
[

]
)
.

{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{D}}(L)=\left\{\sum_{-\infty}^{N}c_{j}{\mathcal{L}}^{j},\,c_{j}\in{\bf C}\right\}\cap{\mathcal{D}}.

Remark 2.7
.

Schur’s theorem shows that the quotient field of

is a function field of one variable; indeed, a

can be viewed as the inverse of an (analytic) local parameter

2.2
True rank

The
rank
of a subset of
{\mathcal{D}}
is the greatest common divisor of the orders of all the elements of that subset. However, we are mainly interested in the rank of the subalgebra generated by the subset. In particular, given commuting differential operators
L
and
M
, let us denote by
\operatorname{rk}(L,M)
the rank of the pair, which we will compare with the rank
\operatorname{rk}({\bf C}[L,M])
of the algebra
{\bf C}[L,M]
they generate.

A polynomial with constant coefficients satisfied by a commuting pair of differential operators is called a
Burchnall–Chaundy
(
BC
)
polynomial
, since the first result of this sort appeared is the
1923
paper
[
3
]
by Burchnall and Chaundy. In fact, they showed that the converse is also true, namely if two (non-constant) operators satisfy identically a polynomial in two indeterminates
\lambda
,
\mu
that belongs to
\mathbf{C}[\lambda,\mu]
, then they commute.

Let us assume that
n=\operatorname{ord}(L)
and
m=\operatorname{ord}(M)
. The idea is that by commutativity
M
acts on
V_{\lambda}
, the
n
-dimensional vector space of solutions

of
Ly=\lambda y
(
L
is regular);

is the characteristic polynomial of this operator; to see that
f(L,M)\equiv 0
it is enough to remark that
f(\lambda,\mu)=0
iff
L
,
M
have a “common eigenfunction”:

\displaystyle Ly=\lambda y,

\displaystyle By=\mu y,

hence
f(L,M)
would have an infinite-dimensional
kernel (eigenfunctions belonging to distinct eigenvalues

\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k}
are independent by a Vandermonde
argument).

What brings out the algebraic structure of the problem,
and of the polynomial
f
, is the construction of the Sylvester matrix
S_{0}(L,M)
. This is the coefficient matrix of the extended system of differential operators

\displaystyle\Xi_{0}(L,M)=\big\{\partial^{m-1}L,\ldots,\partial L,L,\partial^{n-1}M,\ldots,\partial M,M\big\}.

(2.1)

Observe that
S_{0}(L,M)
is a squared matrix of size
n+m
and entries in
K
. We define the

of

to be

\partial{\rm Res}(L,M):=\det(S_{0}(L,M))
. For a recent review on differential resultants see
[
24
]

It is well known that

\displaystyle f(\lambda,\mu)=\partial{\rm Res}(L-\lambda,M-\mu)

(2.2)

is a polynomial with constant coefficients satisfied by the operators
L
and

, see
[
38
,
45
]
. Moreover the plane algebraic curve

defined by

[
3
]
.

Remark 2.8
.

Since the algebra
{\bf C}[L,B]
has no zero-divisors, it can be viewed as the affine ring

Using the parameter
k
,
Segal and Wilson give an illustration of what can be viewed as a containment
of commutative subalgebras, and the surjective morphisms between the attendant
spectral curves
[
43
, Section 6]
. In particular, if

\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{D}}(L)=\mathbf{C}[L,B]
, the spectral curve is special,
in that it can be embedded in the plane with only one smooth point at
infinity; the noted Klein quartic curve gives a non-example of such a curve
[
15
]
. Of course, in the case of a hyperelliptic curve defined by
B^{2}
equalling a polynomial in
L
, the ring of the affine curve is indeed

\mathbf{C}[L,B]
, unless the curve has singular points and
in that case the ring of the desingularization is larger; examples of this can be constructed by transference, but in order to stay in the Weyl algebra, one has to ensure that after conjugation the ring still has polynomial coefficients.

In the remaining parts of this paper we will consider differential operators in the first Weyl algebra

. In this section we define an appropriate filtration of

to use a lemma by Dixmier
[
10

that we call the

.

Next, we present some well known techniques for grading the first Weyl algebra

, for a field of zero characteristic
{\bf C}
, see for instance
[

,
6
]
. For non zero
P\in A_{1}({\bf C})
, say

\mathcal{N}(P)=\big\{(i,j)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}\,|\,a_{ij}\not=0\big\}

Given non negative integers

, we consider the linear form

\displaystyle\Lambda_{p,q}(i,j)=pi+qj.

Lemma 5.1

(see
[
6
]
)
.

With the previous notation, the function

\displaystyle\delta\colon\ A_{1}({\bf C})\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}\cup\{-\infty\},\qquad\delta(P)=\max\{\Lambda_{p,q}(i,j)\,|\,(i,j)\in\mathcal{N}(P)\}

is an admissible order function on

. Moreover, the family of
{\bf C}
-vector spaces

\displaystyle G_{\delta}^{s}=\{P\in A_{1}({\bf C})\,|\,\delta(P)\leq s\},\qquad s\in\mathbb{Z},

is an increasing exhaustive separated filtration of

, and it is called the
\delta_{p,q}
-filtration of

(
associated to the linear form
\Lambda_{p,q})
.

Let us consider the commutative ring of polynomials
{\bf C}[\chi,\xi]
and the
{\bf C}
-algebra isomorphism:

\displaystyle\phi\colon\ {\bf C}[\chi,\xi]\rightarrow\operatorname{gr}_{\delta}(A_{1}({\bf C})),\qquad\phi(\chi)=\sigma(x),\qquad\phi(\xi)=\sigma(\partial),

where

is the principal symbol of the operator

with respect to the
\delta_{p,q}
-filtration. Moreover

, that is
\deg\big(\chi^{i}\xi^{j}\big)=\Lambda_{p,q}(i,j)=pi+qj

\displaystyle\sigma(LM)=\sigma(L)\sigma(M).

(5.1)

Let
P
be an operator with
m=\delta(P)
. We call
the initial part of the operator

the homogeneous operator:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ini}(P)=\sum_{\Lambda(i,j)=m}a_{ij}x^{i}\partial^{j}.

Remark 5.2
.

From now on we identify
\sigma(P)
and
\phi^{-1}(\sigma(P))
for each operator

.

For the convenience of the reader we recall a result from Dixmier work
[

that will be useful in the next sections. The next result is
[

, Lemma 2.7]
, using the previous terminology. We will call this result the

Lemma 5.3

(
Dixmier test
)
.

With the previous notation, let us consider the
\delta_{p,q}
-filtration of
A_{1}({\bf C})
.
Given

and

1.

(b)

\delta(U)<v+w-(p+q)

(b)

\frac{\partial\sigma(L)}{\partial\chi}\frac{\partial\sigma(M)}{\partial\xi}-\frac{\partial\sigma(L)}{\partial\xi}\frac{\partial\sigma(M)}{\partial\chi}=0

If

By means of Lemma

-filtration associated to the linear form defined in Lemma

Remark 6.1

In

6.1
Centralizers

Our goal is to prove that the centralizer

of

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
L_{4}
be an irreducible operator of order
4
in

as in (
6.2

\deg(V)>\max\big\{\frac{1}{2}\deg(U),\frac{1}{2}\deg(W)\big\}

commuting with

has even order.

Proof.

Let
p=\deg(V)
be an odd integer. Let us consider the
\delta_{2,p}
-filtration of

, with
\Lambda_{2,p}(i,j)=2i+pj
. By (
5.1
), we have
\sigma(L_{4})=\big[\sigma\big(\partial^{2}+V\big)\big]^{2}=\big(\xi^{2}+\chi^{p}\big)^{2}
. But, by

(Corollary
5.5
),
\sigma(M)^{4p}=\sigma(L_{4})^{pm}=\big(\xi^{2}+\chi^{p}\big)^{2pm}
, with
m=\operatorname{ord}(M)
. Therefore, since
\xi^{2}+\chi^{p}
is irreducible in

,
\sigma(M)=\big(\xi^{2}+\chi^{p}\big)^{q}
for some

. Then

. Thus,

has even order in this case.

Next assume

, an even integer. Now, we have

\displaystyle\sigma(M)^{4p}=\sigma(L_{4})^{pm}=\big(\xi^{2}+\chi^{p}\big)^{2pm}=\big(\xi+i\chi^{s}\big)^{2pm}\big(\xi-i\chi^{s}\big)^{2pm},\qquad\text{with}\quad m=\operatorname{ord}(M).

Then

is an unique factorization domain. Hence, comparing multiplicities, we have

and

. So,

has even order, as was stated in the theorem.
∎

The construction.
From now on we assume that

of order

does not exist for

,

is a BC pair, that is

and

, with

The procedure to obtain

is based on an iterated division process. Observe that the ring of differential operators

is a (left) Euclidean domain that contains

Recall that

with

. Let us fix

.
We use the left division algorithm in

to construct a sequence of quotients and remainders to rewrite

as follows.
First, by left division by

, we compute the remainder sequence

\displaystyle\Delta(M)=\{\texttt{R}_{1},\ldots,\texttt{R}_{g+1}\},

where

\displaystyle M=\sum_{j=0}^{g}L_{4}^{j}\texttt{R}_{j+1}+L_{4}^{g+1}\texttt{Q}_{g+1}.

Observe that

for the given

Recall that we are looking for

With this purpose, for the fixed

assumed to be of order less than

Let us define the polynomial

\displaystyle p_{\vec{a}}(\lambda)=1+a_{1}\lambda+\cdots+a_{d(g)}\lambda^{d(g)}+\lambda q(\lambda),\qquad\text{where}\qquad d(g):=\min\{q-g,g\}

,
and the operator

\displaystyle B_{\vec{a}}^{g}:=L_{4}^{g}Q_{g,B}+\sum_{j=0}^{g-1}L_{4}^{j}R_{j+1,B}\qquad\text{in}\qquad K[\vec{a}][\partial].

(6.6)

and
\operatorname{ord}(\texttt{R}_{j})
are smaller than the order of

\displaystyle\texttt{R}_{1}=R_{1,B},\qquad\texttt{R}_{j+1}=\begin{cases}R_{j+1,B}+a_{1}R_{j,B}+\cdots+a_{j}R_{1,B}&\text{if }0<j<d(g),\\

From the term in

\displaystyle\texttt{R}_{g+1}=Q_{g,B}+a_{1}R_{g,B}+a_{2}R_{g-1,B}+\cdots+a_{d(g)}R_{g-d(g)+1,B}.

(6.8)
