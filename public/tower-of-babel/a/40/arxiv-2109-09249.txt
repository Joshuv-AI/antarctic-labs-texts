On average hitting time and Kemeny’s constant for weighted trees
Ji Zeng

Abstract
For a connected graph G G , the average hitting time α ⁡ ( G ) \alpha(G) and the Kemeny’s constant κ ⁡ ( G ) \kappa(G) are two similar quantities, both measuring the time for the random walk on G G to travel between two randomly chosen vertices. We prove that, among all weighted trees whose edge-weights form a fixed multiset, α \alpha is maximized by a special type of ”polarized” paths and is minimized by a unique weighted star graph. We also give a short proof of the fact that, among all simple trees of a fixed size, κ \kappa is maximized by the path and is minimized by the star graph. Our proofs are based on the forest formulas for the average hitting time and the Kemeny’s constant.



1
Introduction

In this paper, we consider random walks on graphs whose edges are assigned with positive weights. For the random walk on a connected weighted graph
G=(V,E)
, the transition probability from vertex
u
to vertex
v
is proportional to the weight of the edge
uv
(see
[
8
]
Section 1.4). The
hitting time

H_{G}(u,v)
is the expected number of steps to walk from the vertex
u
to the vertex
v
. We shall study the
average hitting time
, defined as the arithmetic mean of all hitting times of
G
,

\displaystyle\alpha(G):=\frac{1}{|V|^{2}}\sum_{u\in V}\sum_{v\in V}H_{G}(u,v).

The
stationary distribution
of
G
is a unique probability vector
\pi_{G}
satisfying
\pi_{G}^{T}=\pi_{G}^{T}P_{G}
, where
P_{G}
is the transition probability matrix for the random walk on
G
(see
[
8
]
Exercise 2.1). The
Kemeny’s constant
of
G
, similar to
\alpha(G)
but more complicated, is defined as

\displaystyle\kappa(G):=\sum_{v\in V}H_{G}(u,v)\pi_{G}(v).

In words,
\kappa(G)
is the expected time to travel from a fixed starting vertex
u
to a random destination sampled from the stationary distribution. Formally, this definition of
\kappa(G)
depends on the choice of
u
, but Kemeny and Snell
[
5
]
observed the surprising fact that the value
\kappa(G)
is independent of
u
(see
[
8
]
Exercise 2.48). The Kemeny’s constant has applications in network theory(
[
6
]
), robotics(
[
9
]
) and chemistry(
[
7
]
).

The original motivation of this paper is to prove the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

Among all simple (i.e. unweighted) trees of a fixed size, the path has the largest
\alpha
and
\kappa
, and the star graph has the smallest
\alpha
and
\kappa
.

However, our methods generalize to a weighted setting: For a multiset (i.e. set with multiplicities)
W
of positive numbers, we let
\mathcal{T}_{W}
be the collection of all weighted trees
T
such that
W
equals
\{\omega(e)|e\in E(T)\}
as a multiset. Here,
\omega(e)
denotes the weight of the edge
e
. See Figure
1
for an example of
\mathcal{T}_{W}
. Notice that when
W
only consists of multiple
1
’s,
\mathcal{T}_{W}
is the collection of all simple trees of a fixed size. We will investigate which elements in
\mathcal{T}_{W}
maximize or minimize
\alpha
and
\kappa
for a general
W
.

Clearly, there’s a unique element
S_{W}
in
\mathcal{T}_{W}
whose underlying graph structure is a star graph. On the other hand, there are many weighted paths in
\mathcal{T}_{W}
due to various ways to assign the edge-weights. We care about a special type of ”polarized” paths: For a weighted path
P=v_{1}v_{2}\dots v_{n}
, we denote its edges as
e_{i}:=v_{i}v_{i+1}
and define
c(e_{i}):=\min\{i,n-i\}
for
i\in[n-1]
, then we say
P
is
polarized
provided
\omega(e_{i})\geq\omega(e_{j})
whenever
c(e_{i})\leq c(e_{j})
.

Intuitively,
c(e_{i})
measures how centrally
e_{i}
is located in
P
: We regard
e_{1}
and
e_{n-1}
, the edges containing the leaves of
P
, as the least central edges hence they have the smallest
c(e_{1})=c(e_{n-1})=1
; We regard
e_{2}
and
e_{n-2}
as the second least central edges hence they have the second smallest
c(e_{2})=c(e_{n-2})=2
and so on; We regard the middle edge
e_{\lceil\frac{n-1}{2}\rceil}
as the most central edge hence it has the largest
c(e_{\lceil\frac{n-1}{2}\rceil})=\lceil\frac{n-1}{2}\rceil
. So, in words, a weight path is polarized if edges located more centrally are assigned with smaller weights. See Figure
1
for an illustration.

Figure 1:
From left to right: a weighted tree in
\mathcal{T}_{W}
where
W=\{7,5,4,2,2,1\}
and the numbers indicate edge-weights; the weighted star
S_{W}
; a polarized path in
\mathcal{T}_{W}
where
c(e_{1})=c(e_{6})=1
,
c(e_{2})=c(e_{5})=2
, and
c(e_{3})=c(e_{4})=3
.

Theorem 1.2
.

In
\mathcal{T}_{W}
, where
W
is a multiset of positive numbers, the polarized paths are the elements that maximize
\alpha
; and
S_{W}
is the unique element that minimizes
\alpha
.

By a slight modification of the proof of Theorem
1.2
, we obtain a similar result for the Kemeny’s constant.

Theorem 1.3
.

In
\mathcal{T}_{W}
, where
W
is a multiset of positive numbers, the elements that maximize
\kappa
are among the weighted paths; and
S_{W}
is the unique element that minimizes
\kappa
.

In Theorem
1.3
, we fail to characterize which elements among the weighted paths in
\mathcal{T}_{W}
maximize
\kappa
for a general
W
. In Remark
5.2
, we give an example where the
\kappa
-maximizing path isn’t polarized for some
\mathcal{T}_{W}
. Nevertheless, there’s only one simple path with a given size, so Theorem
1.1
is just a corollary of Theorem
1.2
and Theorem
1.3
.

We note that, among all simple trees of a fixed size, Ciardo et al.
[
3
]
previously proved the star graph minimizes
\kappa
; and recently, Faught et al.
[
4
]
proved the path maximizes
\kappa
. However, the discovery of our results is independent of their ideas. Our proofs are based on the forest formulas for the average hitting time and the Kemeny’s constant which we will introduce in Section
2
.

There are also formulas that express the the average hitting time and the Kemeny’s constant through eigenvalues of graph-theoretic Laplacian matrices, see Remark
5.1
. Consequently, our results can be interpreted as extremal characterizations of the Laplacian spectra of weighted trees.

During our proofs, we introduce a type of operations on trees called ”edge-transfers”. The edge-transfer operations induce a partial order
\succeq
on the set of simple trees of a fixed size. We observed similarity between this partial order and another partial order previously defined by Sidorenko
[
10
]
using graph homomorphisms, see Remark
5.3
.

2
Preliminaries

Let
G=(V,E)
be a connected weighted graph without loops. For each edge
e=uv\in E
, we denote its
weight
by
\omega(e)=\omega(uv)>0
. It’s required that
\omega(e)=0
for
e\not\in E
. For any subgraph
H
of
G
, we define its weight by

\displaystyle\omega(H)

\displaystyle=\prod_{e\in E(H)}\omega(e).

For each vertex
u
, we define its
degree
by
d_{G}(u):=\sum_{v\in V}\omega(uv)
. The
volume
of a subset
U\subset V
refers to the quantity
\text{vol}_{G}(U):=\sum_{u\in U}d_{G}(u)
. The volume of a subgraph
H\subset G
refers to the volume of
V(H)\subset V
and is denoted by
\text{vol}_{G}(H)
. We remind our readers that in general
\text{vol}_{G}(H)\neq\text{vol}_{H}(H)
. The
size
of
G
refers to the number of its vertices and is denoted by
|G|
.

A
tree
is a graph that’s acyclic and connected. A
2
-forest
is a graph that’s acyclic and has two connected components. A subgraph
H\subset G
is said to be
spanning
if
V(H)=V(G)
. We use
\mathbb{F}_{1}(G)
to denote the set of all spanning sub-trees of
G
, and use
\mathbb{F}_{2}(G)
to denote the set of all spanning sub-
2
-forests of
G
.

We shall use the next two lemmas in our proofs. They express
\alpha(G)
and
\kappa(G)
as weighted enumerations of elements in
\mathbb{F}_{1}(G)
and
\mathbb{F}_{2}(G)
. For simplicity of presentation, we define the quantity

\displaystyle\tau(G):=\sum_{T\in\mathbb{F}_{1}(G)}\omega(T);

And we define for any
2
-forest
F\in\mathbb{F}_{2}(G)
, whose two components denoted as
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
,

\displaystyle S(F):=|T_{1}||T_{2}|\qquad\text{and}\qquad V_{G}(F):=\text{vol}_{G}(T_{1})\text{vol}_{G}(T_{2}).

Lemma 2.1

(
[
2
]
Corollary 4.5)
.

For a connected weighted graph
G
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{|G|}{\text{vol}_{G}(G)}\alpha(G)=\frac{1}{|G|\tau(G)}\sum_{F\in\mathbb{F}_{2}(G)}S(F)\omega(F).

Lemma 2.2

(
[
2
]
Corollary 4.4)
.

For a connected weighted graph
G
, we have

\displaystyle\kappa(G)=\frac{1}{\text{vol}_{G}(G)\tau(G)}\sum_{F\in\mathbb{F}_{2}(G)}V_{G}(F)\omega(F).

We remark that Lemma
2.2
is also established in
[
1
]
prior to
[
2
]
.

T
: Take two adjacent edges
e_{1}=v_{1}v_{2}
and
e_{2}=v_{2}v_{3}
, and name the component of
T\backslash\{e_{1},e_{2}\}

as
T_{i}
, for
i=1,2,3
. If
|T_{1}|>|T_{2}|
, we construct another tree
T^{\prime}
from
T
by deleting the edge
e_{2}
and adding an edge
e_{2}^{\prime}=v_{1}v_{3}
with weight
\omega(e_{2}^{\prime}):=\omega(e_{2})
. In this case we say
T
edge-transfers to
T^{\prime}
with respect to size
. See Figure

Lemma 3.1
.

If
T
edge-transfers to
T^{\prime}
with respect to size, then
\alpha(T)>\alpha(T^{\prime})
.

Proof.

We keep the notations as in the previous paragraph. Since
T
and

are trees, by definition of

and
\tau(T^{\prime})
, we have
\tau(T)=\omega(T)=\omega(T^{\prime})=\tau(T^{\prime})=:\tau
. It’s also obvious that
|T|=|T^{\prime}|=:n
and
\text{vol}_{T}(T)=\text{vol}_{T^{\prime}}(T^{\prime})=:\text{vol}
.

Notice that for any edge
e\not\in\{e_{1},e_{2},e_{2}^{\prime}\}
, the
2
-forests
T\backslash e
and
T^{\prime}\backslash e
give the same component partition of vertices, that is, two vertices
x,y
are in the same component of

if and only if they are in the same component of

. See Figure
3

S(T\backslash e)=S(T^{\prime}\backslash e)
. We also have
\omega(T\backslash e)=\tau/\omega(e)=\omega(T^{\prime}\backslash e)
. The two
2
-forests

and

are equal as graphs, so

and
\omega(T\backslash e_{2})=\omega(T^{\prime}\backslash e^{\prime}_{2})=\tau/\omega(e_{2})
. Therefore, the difference between
\alpha(T)
and
\alpha(T^{\prime})
in the formula of Lemma
2.1
is only contributed by the
2
-forests
T\backslash e_{1}
and
T^{\prime}\backslash e_{1}
:

\displaystyle\frac{n}{\text{vol}}(\alpha(T)-\alpha(T^{\prime}))=\frac{1}{n\omega(e_{1})}(S(T\backslash e_{1})-S(T^{\prime}\backslash e_{1})).

(3.1)

Here we used
\omega(T\backslash e_{1})=\tau/\omega(e_{1})=\omega(T^{\prime}\backslash e_{1})
.

The two components of

are

and
T_{2}\cup T_{3}
respectively, and the two components of
T^{\prime}\backslash e_{1}
are

\displaystyle S(T\backslash e_{1})-S(T^{\prime}\backslash e_{1})=|T_{1}|(|T_{2}|+|T_{3}|)-(|T_{1}|+|T_{3}|)|T_{2}|=(|T_{1}|-|T_{2}|)|T_{3}|>0,

) concludes

. By Lemma

,

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

As a consequence of Lemma
3.1
and Lemma
3.2
, the elements that maximize
\alpha
are among the weighted paths in
\mathcal{T}_{W}
, and
S_{W}
is the unique element that minimizes
\alpha
.

Let
P=v_{1}v_{2}\dots v_{n}
, where
n=|W|+1
, be a weighted path and name the edges
e_{i}:=v_{i}v_{i+1}
for
i\in[n-1]
. We now assign edge-weights from
W
to the

’s to maximize
\alpha(P)
. Since
S(P\backslash e_{i})=i(n-i)
is independent of the choice of edge-weights, to maximize
\alpha(P)
in the formula of Lemma
2.1
, we need to assign the edge-weights such that larger

is paired with larger
\omega(P\backslash e_{i})
. Since
\omega(P\backslash e_{i})=\omega(e_{i})^{-1}(\prod_{w\in W}w)
, we should pair larger
S(P\backslash e_{i})
with smaller
\omega(e_{i})
. Recall from Section
1
that
c(e_{i})=\min\{i,n-i\}
. We can check
c(e_{i})>c(e_{j})
if and only if
S(P\backslash e_{i})>S(P\backslash e_{j})
. Hence, in order to maximize
\alpha(P)
, the edges with larger
c(e_{i})
should have smaller
\omega(e_{i})
, meaning that
P
has to be polarized.

Now it suffices to show all polarized paths in
\mathcal{T}_{W}
have the same
\alpha
. To see this, we write
W=\{w_{1},w_{2},\dots,w_{n}\}
such that
w_{i}\geq w_{i+1}
for
i\in[n-1]
. We also partition the edge set of
P
into
E_{i}:=\{e_{i},e_{n-i}\}
for
i=1,\dots,\lceil\frac{n-1}{2}\rceil
. Since

if and only if

, to generate all polarized paths in
\mathcal{T}_{W}
, it suffices to assign the weights
w_{2i-1}
and
w_{2i}
to edges in
E_{i}
for all

. Because

, the two possible assignments for
E_{i}
,
(\omega(e_{i}),\omega(e_{n-i}))=(w_{2i-1},w_{2i})
or
(\omega(e_{i}),\omega(e_{n-i}))=(w_{2i},w_{2i-1})
, won’t affect
\alpha
in the formula of Lemma

. Hence, all weighted paths generated by this process have the same
\alpha
.

Proof of Theorem
1.3

We consider another operation on a tree
T
: Take two adjacent edges
e_{1}=v_{1}v_{2}
and
e_{2}=v_{2}v_{3}
, and name the component of
T\backslash\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
containing
v_{i}
as
T_{i}
, for
i=1,2,3
. If
\text{vol}_{T_{1}}(T_{1})>\text{vol}_{T_{2}}(T_{2})
, we construct another tree
T^{\prime}
from
T
by deleting the edge
e_{2}
and adding an edge
e_{2}^{\prime}=v_{1}v_{3}
with weight
\omega(e_{2}^{\prime}):=\omega(e_{2})
. In this case we say
T
edge-transfers to
T^{\prime}
with respect to volume
.

Lemma 4.1
.

If
T
edge-transfers to
T^{\prime}
with respect to volume, then
\kappa(T)>\kappa(T^{\prime})
.

Proof.

We keep the notations as in the previous paragraph. Same as in the proof of Lemma
3.1
, we have
\tau(T)=\omega(T)=\omega(T^{\prime})=\tau(T^{\prime})=:\tau
and
\text{vol}_{T}(T)=\text{vol}_{T^{\prime}}(T^{\prime})=:\text{vol}
.

We have
V_{T}(T\backslash e)=V_{T^{\prime}}(T^{\prime}\backslash e)
for any edge
e\not\in\{e_{1},e_{2},e_{2}^{\prime}\}
. Indeed, denoting the component of
T\backslash e
(
T^{\prime}\backslash e

v_{1}
as
T_{1}
(
T_{1}^{\prime}
resp.), and the other component as
T_{2}
(
T^{\prime}_{2}

T_{2}=T_{2}^{\prime}
as graphs. See Figure

for an illustration. This implies

\displaystyle\text{vol}_{T}(T_{2})=\text{vol}_{T_{2}}(T_{2})+\omega(e)=\text{vol}_{T^{\prime}_{2}}(T^{\prime}_{2})+\omega(e)=\text{vol}_{T^{\prime}}(T^{\prime}_{2}),

and
\text{vol}_{T}(T_{1})=\text{vol}-\text{vol}_{T}(T_{2})=\text{vol}-\text{vol}_{T^{\prime}}(T^{\prime}_{2})=\text{vol}_{T^{\prime}}(T^{\prime}_{1})
. So
V_{T}(T\backslash e)=V_{T^{\prime}}(T^{\prime}\backslash e)
as claimed. We also have
\omega(T\backslash e)=\tau/\omega(e)=\omega(T^{\prime}\backslash e)
. A similar argument gives us
V_{T}(T\backslash e_{2})=V_{T^{\prime}}(T^{\prime}\backslash e^{\prime}_{2})
and
\omega(T\backslash e_{2})=\tau/\omega(e_{2})=\tau/\omega(e^{\prime}_{2})=\omega(T^{\prime}\backslash e^{\prime}_{2})
. Therefore, the difference between
\kappa(T)
and
\kappa(T^{\prime})
in the formula of Lemma
2.2
is only contributed by the
2
-forests
T\backslash e_{1}
and
T^{\prime}\backslash e_{1}
:

\displaystyle\kappa(T)-\kappa(T^{\prime})=\frac{1}{\text{vol}\cdot\omega(e_{1})}(V_{T}(T\backslash e_{1})-V_{T^{\prime}}(T^{\prime}\backslash e_{1})).

(4.1)

Here we used
\omega(T\backslash e_{1})=\tau/\omega(e_{1})=\omega(T^{\prime}\backslash e_{1})
.

Now we write
\omega_{1}:=\omega(e_{1})
and
\omega_{2}:=\omega(e_{2})=\omega(e^{\prime}_{2})
. The two components of
T\backslash e_{1}
are
T_{1}
and
T_{2}\cup T_{3}
respectively. Notice that

and

for any
v\neq v_{1}
in
T_{1}
, we have

\displaystyle\text{vol}_{T}(T_{1})=\text{vol}_{T_{1}}(T_{1})+\omega_{1}.

Also notice that
d_{T}(v_{2})=d_{T_{2}}(v_{2})+\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}
,
d_{T}(v_{3})=d_{T_{2}}(v_{3})+\omega_{2}
,
d_{T}(v)=d_{T_{2}}(v)
for any
v\neq v_{2}
in
T_{2}
, and
d_{T}(v)=d_{T_{3}}(v)
for any
v\neq v_{3}
in
T_{3}
. So we have

\displaystyle\text{vol}_{T}(T_{2}\cup T_{3})=\text{vol}_{T_{2}}(T_{2})+\omega_{1}+2\omega_{2}+\text{vol}_{T_{3}}(T_{3}).

Similarly, the two components of
T^{\prime}\backslash e_{1}
are
T_{1}\cup T_{3}
and
T_{2}
respectively, and we can check
\text{vol}_{T^{\prime}}(T_{1}\cup T_{3})=\text{vol}_{T_{1}}(T_{1})+\omega_{1}+2\omega_{2}+\text{vol}_{T_{3}}(T_{3})
and
\text{vol}_{T^{\prime}}(T_{2})=\text{vol}_{T_{2}}(T_{2})+\omega_{1}
. So we have the following computation.

\displaystyle V_{T}(T\backslash e_{1})-V_{T^{\prime}}(T^{\prime}\backslash e_{1})

\displaystyle=(\text{vol}_{T_{1}}(T_{1})+\omega_{1})(\text{vol}_{T_{2}}(T_{2})+\omega_{1}+2\omega_{2}+\text{vol}_{T_{3}}(T_{3}))

\displaystyle\hskip 20.0pt-(\text{vol}_{T_{1}}(T_{1})+\omega_{1}+2\omega_{2}+\text{vol}_{T_{3}}(T_{3}))(\text{vol}_{T_{2}}(T_{2})+\omega_{1})

\displaystyle=(\text{vol}_{T_{3}}(T_{3})+2\omega_{2})(\text{vol}_{T_{1}}(T_{1})-\text{vol}_{T_{2}}(T_{2}))>0,

since
\text{vol}_{T_{1}}(T_{1})>\text{vol}_{T_{2}}(T_{2})
by our assumption of the edge-transfer. This inequality together with (
4.1
) concludes
\kappa(T)>\kappa(T^{\prime})
.
∎

Let’s write
T\succeq_{v}T^{\prime}
if
T^{\prime}
can be obtained after applying multiple edge-transfers with respect to volume starting with
T
. As a consequence of Lemma
4.1
, the relation
\succeq_{v}
defines a partial order on
\mathcal{T}_{W}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

For any multiset
W
of positive numbers, the maximal elements of the poset
(\mathcal{T}_{W},\succeq_{v})
are the weighted paths, and the minimal element of
(\mathcal{T}_{W},\succeq_{v})
is the weighted star
S_{W}
.

We skip the proof of Lemma
4.2
since it’s very similar to the proof of Lemma
3.2
.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

This theorem follows directly from Lemma
4.1
and Lemma
4.2
.
∎

Remarks

Remark 5.1
.

Theorem
1.2
and Theorem
1.3
can be interpreted as extremal characterizations of the Laplacian spectra for weighted trees in
\mathcal{T}_{W}
. This is because
\alpha
and
\kappa
satisfy the following spectral formulas: For a connected weighted graph
G
of size
n
, write the eigenvalues of its
combinatorial Laplacian matrix
as
0=\lambda_{0}<\lambda_{1}\leq\dots\leq\lambda_{n-1}
, and the eigenvalues of its
normalized Laplacian matrix
as
0=\mu_{0}<\mu_{1}\leq\dots\leq\mu_{n-1}
, then we have

\displaystyle\frac{n}{\text{vol}_{G}(G)}\alpha(G)=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\lambda_{i}^{-1}\text{\qquad and \qquad}\kappa(G)=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\mu_{i}^{-1}.

We refer our readers to
[
2
]
for definitions of Laplacian matrices and proofs of these identities.

Remark 5.2
.

After Theorem
1.3
, it’s natural to ask which weighted paths in
\mathcal{T}_{W}
maximize
\kappa
for a general
W
. For a path
P=v_{1}v_{2}\dots v_{n}
in
\mathcal{T}_{W}
, where
n=|W|+1
, if we write
\omega_{i}:=\omega(v_{i}v_{i+1})
for
i\in[n-1]
, we can check through the formula of Lemma
2.2
that the maximization of
\kappa(P)
is equivalent to the maximization of

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\sum_{k=i+1}^{n-1}\frac{\omega_{j}\omega_{k}}{\omega_{i}}\text{, where $\{\omega_{i}\}=W$ as multisets.}

This optimization problem is very different from the trivial one we encounter in Theorem
1.2
. For example, when
W=\{10,8,1,1,0.1\}
,
\kappa(P)
is maximized by taking
\omega_{1}=10
,
\omega_{2}=0.1
,
\omega_{3}=1
,
\omega_{4}=1
and
\omega_{5}=8
, which corresponds to a weighted path in
\mathcal{T}_{W}
that’s not polarized according to the definition in Section
1
.

Remark 5.3
.

The two partial orders
\succeq_{s}
and
\succeq_{v}
are identical for simple trees. Indeed, if
T
is simple in the definitions of
\succeq_{s}
and
\succeq_{v}

\text{vol}_{T_{1}}(T_{1})=2|T_{1}|-1
and
\text{vol}_{T_{2}}(T_{2})=2|T_{2}|

|T_{1}|>|T_{2}|
if and only if
\text{vol}_{T_{1}}(T_{1})>\text{vol}_{T_{2}}(T_{2})
. Hence, we can write
\succeq:=\succeq_{s}=\succeq_{v}
if we only consider simple trees. Figure
4
illustrates the Hasse diagram of
\succeq
on simple trees of of size
7
