Revisiting the Divergent Multipole Expansion of Atom–Surface Interactions: Hydrogen and Positronium, 𝜶 \bm{\alpha} –Quartz, and Physisorption
Ulrich D. Jentschura

Abstract
We revisit the derivation of multipole contributions to the atom-wall interaction previously presented in [G. Łach et al. , Phys. Rev. A 81, 052507 (2010)]. A careful reconsideration of the angular-momentum decomposition of the second-, third- and fourth-rank tensors composed of the derivatives of the electric-field modes leads to a modification for the results for the quadrupole, octupole and hexadecupole contributions to the atom-wall interaction. Asymptotic results are given for the asymptotic long-range forms of the multipole terms, in both the short-range and long-range limits. Calculations are carried out for hydrogen and positronium in contact with α \alpha -quartz; a reanalysis of analytic models of the dielectric function of α \alpha -quartz is performed. Analytic results are provided for the multipole polarizabilities of hydrogen and positronium. The quadrupole correction is shown to be numerically significant for atom-surface interactions. The expansion into multipoles is shown to constitute a divergent, asymptotic series. Connections to van-der-Waals corrected density-functional theory and applications to physisorption are described.

In Ref.
Łach et al. 2010
,

Here, we update the
analysis presented in Ref.
Łach et al. 2010

of angular-momentum components of the
quantized electric field
near the wall.

Let us discuss the basis
for the update, in terms of the initial expressions
used for the evaluations, both for the
cases discussed in Ref.
Łach et al. 2010

as well as for the modifications discussed here.
Specifically, according to
Eq. (22) of Ref.
Łach et al. 2010

(in accordance with Ref.
Milonni 1994
),
the dipole part of the atom-wall
interaction can be expressed as follows,

\mathcal{E}_{\ell=1}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\vec{k}\lambda}\sum_{i}\alpha_{\ell=1}(\omega)\big|E^{i}_{\vec{k}\lambda}(\vec{r})\big|^{2}\,,

(1)

where
\vec{E}_{\vec{k}\lambda}(\vec{r})
is the
(in general complex)
mode function of the electric field near the
wall,
\vec{k}
is the wave vector,
and
\lambda
is the polarization.
The index
i
enumerates the Cartesian components.
The dipole polarizability of the atom
is
\alpha_{\ell=1}(\omega)=\alpha_{1}(\omega)
, and it
is evaluated at the angular frequency

\omega=\omega_{k}=c\,|\vec{k}|
,
where
c
is the speed of light.
The mode function
\vec{E}_{\vec{k}\lambda}(z)

of the electric field is related to the
mode function
\vec{A}_{\vec{k}\lambda}(z)

of the vector potential by the relation

\vec{E}_{\vec{k}\lambda}(\vec{r})=\mathrm{i}\omega_{k}\,\vec{A}_{\vec{k}\lambda}(\vec{r})\,,

(2)

where the mode functions
in Eqs. (18), (19) and (20) of Ref.
Łach et al. 2010
.
The formalism for the treatment of the
dipole contribution is well established.

The starting point for the analysis of the
quadrupole, octupole, and hexadecupole corrections to the
atom-wall interaction has been
given by Eq. (8) of Ref.
Łach et al. 2010

as follows,

\mathcal{E}_{\ell=2}=-\frac{1}{12}\,\sum_{\vec{k}\lambda}\sum_{ij}\alpha_{2}(\omega)\,\big|\nabla^{j}E^{i}_{\vec{k}\lambda}(\vec{r})\big|^{2}\,,

(3)

where
\nabla^{j}\equiv\partial/\partial x^{j}

and the indices
i
and
j
stand for the Cartesian components,
and
\alpha_{2}
is the quadrupole polarizability of the
atom.
We reserve the index
i
for the component of the
electric field.
The octupole energy shift has been analyzed
in Ref.
Łach et al. 2010
based on the expression

\mathcal{E}_{\ell=3}=-\frac{1}{180}\sum_{\vec{k}\lambda}\sum_{ijk}\alpha_{3}(\omega)\,\big|\nabla^{j}\nabla^{k}E^{i}_{\vec{k}\lambda}(\vec{r})\big|^{2}\,,

(4)

where
\alpha_{3}=\alpha_{\ell=3}
is the octupole polarizability of the atom.
The expressions (
3
) and (
4
)
need to be substantiated by the specification
of the angular-momentum component
relevant to the analysis. Namely,
the virtual atomic transitions contributing
to the quadrupole, octupole and hexadecupole polarizabilities
of the atom connect the atomic ground state
to virtual states with angular momenta
\ell=2
and
\ell=3
.
However, when one calculates sums such as

\sum_{ij}\big|\nabla^{j}E^{i}_{\vec{k}\lambda}(\vec{r})\big|^{2}\,,

(5)

without separating the (
\ell=2
)-component of the
second-rank tensor
\nabla^{j}E^{i}
first, then
one sums over the (
\ell=0
)- and (
\ell=1
)-components
of the second-rank tensor
T^{ji}=\nabla^{j}E^{i}

as well. While the proper isolation
of the angular-momentum components does not
change the functional form of the results
reported in Ref.
Łach et al. 2010
,
some prefactors receive modifications.
For the quadrupole term,
the proper isolation of the (
\ell=2
)-component
leads to the modified expression

\mathcal{E}_{\ell=2}=-\frac{1}{12}\,\sum_{\vec{k}\lambda}\sum_{ij}\alpha_{2}(\omega)\,\big|\left.\{\nabla^{j}E^{i}_{\vec{k}\lambda}(\vec{r})\}\right|_{\ell=2}\big|^{2}\,.

(6)

where the (
\ell=2
)-component of a tensor is
given by the traceless, symmetric component as
follows,

\left.T^{ij}\right|_{\ell=2}=\frac{1}{2}\,\left(T^{ij}+T^{ji}\right)-\frac{1}{3}\,\delta^{ij}\,{\text{Tr}}(T)\,,

(7)

\delta^{ij}
is the Kronecker delta, and

{\text{Tr}}(T)=\sum_{i}T^{ii}
is the trace.
The octupole term is substantiated by the
expression

\mathcal{E}_{3}=-\frac{1}{180}\sum_{\vec{k}\lambda}\sum_{ijk}\alpha_{3}(\omega)\,\big|\left.\{\nabla^{j}\nabla^{k}E^{i}_{\vec{k}\lambda}(\vec{r})\}\right|_{\ell=3}\big|^{2}\,,

(8)

where the isolation of the octupole
(
\ell=3
)-component of a third-rank tensor
has recently been discussed by Itin and Reches in Ref.
Itin and Reches 2022
.
The isolation of the
(
\ell=3
)-component of a third-rank tensor
is not completely trivial.
The modified expressions given in Eqs. (
6
)
and (
8
) have phenomenological consequences
which are discussed in the following.

Finally, the hexadecupole energy shift
is calculated as follows,

\mathcal{E}_{4}=-\frac{1}{5040}\sum_{\vec{k}\lambda}\sum_{ijk}\alpha_{4}(\omega)\,\big|\left.\{\nabla^{j}\nabla^{k}\nabla^{l}E^{i}_{\vec{k}\lambda}(\vec{r})\}\right|_{\ell=4}\big|^{2}\,,

(9)

The isolation of the (
\ell=4
)-component from a Cartesian
tensor is discussed in Ref.
Andrews and Ghoul 1982
.

We use Systéme International (SI mksA) units in the following,
before we switch to atomic units in Sec.
IV
.
The basis for the treatment of
multipole interactions is discussed in Sec.
II

the case of a perfect conductor in Sec.
II.2
,
and dielectric materials in Sec.
III
.
Interactions with hydrogen and positronium atoms

IV
.

II
Multipole Interactions

II.1
General Formulation

In order to put things into perspective,
and in terms of some orientation,
we briefly review the
concept of the multipole oscillator strength,
inspired by Chap. 5 of Ref.
Jentschura and Adkins 2022
.
The
2^{\ell}
-pole polarizability is
written as

\alpha_{\ell}(\omega)=\frac{1}{2\ell+1}\sum_{m}\left<1S\left|Q_{\ell m}\left(\frac{1}{H-E_{1S}+\hbar\omega}\right.\right.\right.\\
+\left.\left.\left.\frac{1}{H-E_{1S}-\hbar\omega}\right)Q^{*}_{\ell m}\right|1S\right>\,,

(10)

where the
Q_{\ell m}
tensor is given as

Q_{\ell m}=\sum_{a}e\,\sqrt{\frac{4\pi}{2\ell+1}}\,r_{a}\,Y_{\ell m}(\hat{r}_{a})\,,

(11)

the
r_{a}
are the electron coordinates,
and the sum over
a
runs over the
atomic electrons.
The spherical harmonic is
Y_{\ell m}
,
and the shorthand notation
\hat{r}_{a}

summarizes the polar and azimuth angles
\theta_{a}
and

\varphi_{a}
which define
\hat{r}_{a}
uniquely, and vice versa.
Here,

\left.T^{ij}\right|_{\ell=2}=\frac{1}{2}\,\left(T^{ij}+T^{ji}\right)-\frac{1}{3}\,\delta^{ij}\,\mathrm{trc}(T)

(12)

is the traceless quadrupole component of a general
tensor
T^{ij}
. The octupole energy shift (
\ell=3
) is
evaluated according to Eq. (
8
),

\mathcal{E}_{3}=-\frac{1}{180}\sum_{\vec{k}\lambda}\sum_{ijk}\alpha_{3}(\omega)\,\big|\left.\{\nabla^{j}\nabla^{k}E^{i}_{\vec{k}\lambda}(\vec{r})\}\right|_{\ell=3}\big|^{2}\,.

(13)

The (
\ell=3
)-component can be extracted from
a third-rank tensor
T^{ijk}
as follows.
One first decomposes
T^{ijk}
into a
totally symmetric part
S^{ijk}
, a
totally skew-symmetric part
A^{ijk}
,
and a remainder term
N^{ijk}
,

\displaystyle S^{ijk}=

\displaystyle\;T^{(ijk)}

(14a)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\frac{1}{6}\left(T^{ijk}+T^{jki}+T^{kij}+T^{jik}+T^{kji}+T^{ikj}\right)\,,

\displaystyle A^{ijk}=

\displaystyle\;T^{[ijk]}

(14b)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\frac{1}{6}\left(T^{ijk}+T^{jki}+T^{kij}-T^{jik}-T^{kji}-T^{ikj}\right)\,,

\displaystyle N^{ijk}=

\displaystyle\;T^{[ijk]}-S^{ijk}-A^{ijk}\,.

(14c)

The matrix
S^{ijk}
contains both
components with
\ell=1
and
\ell=3
.
One then forms a vector
\alpha^{k}
which ones
promotes to a matrix
K^{ijk}
,

\displaystyle\alpha^{k}=

\displaystyle\;\delta^{ij}\,S^{ijk}=\frac{1}{3}\,\left(\delta^{ij}\,T^{ijk}+\delta^{ij}\,T^{ikj}+\delta^{ij}\,T^{kij}\right)\,,

\displaystyle K^{ijk}=

\displaystyle\;\frac{1}{5}\,\left(\alpha^{i}\,\delta^{jk}+\alpha^{j}\,\delta^{ik}+\alpha^{k}\,\delta^{ij}\right)\,.

(15)

Finally,
the (
\ell=3
)-component which we need for
our considerations is obtained as

\left.T^{ijk}\right|_{\ell=3}=S^{ijk}-K^{ijk}\,.

(16)

For the hexadecupole energy shift,
we need the (
\ell=4
)-component of the
Cartesian tensor

T^{ijk\ell}=\nabla^{i}\nabla^{j}\nabla^{k}E^{\ell}_{\vec{k}\lambda}(\vec{r})\,.

(17)

It can be calculated as follows
Andrews and Ghoul 1982
,

\left.T^{ijk\ell}\right|_{\ell=4}=T^{(ijk\ell)}-\frac{1}{7}\left(\delta^{ij}T^{(\rho\rho k\ell)}+\delta^{ik}T^{(\rho\rho j\ell)}\right.\\
\left.+\delta^{i\ell}T^{(\rho\rho jk)}+\delta^{jk}T^{(\rho\rho i\ell)}+\delta^{j\ell}T^{(\rho\rho ik)}+\delta^{k\ell}T^{(\rho\rho ij)}\right)\\
+\frac{1}{35}\left(\delta^{ij}\delta^{k\ell}+\delta^{ik}\delta^{j\ell}+\delta^{i\ell}\delta^{jk}\right)T^{(\rho\rho\sigma\sigma)}\,.

(18)

Here, the Einstein summation is understood,
with dummy indices (
\rho
and
\sigma
) being
summed over. Furthermore, the
symmetrization of a tensor is defined as follows,

T^{(ijk\ell)}=\frac{1}{24}\,\bigl[T^{ijk\ell}+T^{ij\ell k}+T^{ikj\ell}+T^{ik\ell j}+T^{i\ell jk}\\
+T^{i\ell kj}+T^{jik\ell}+T^{ji\ell k}+T^{jki\ell}+T^{jk\ell i}+T^{j\ell ik}+T^{j\ell ki}\\
+T^{kij\ell}+T^{ki\ell j}+T^{kji\ell}+T^{kj\ell i}+T^{k\ell ij}+T^{k\ell ji}+T^{\ell ijk}\\
+T^{\ell ikj}+T^{\ell jik}+T^{\ell jki}+T^{\ell kij}+T^{\ell kji}\bigr]

(19)

For the case
\ell=4
, just as is the case for
\ell=3
,
the isolation of the
component of highest angular momentum is a
nontrivial exercise
Andrews and Ghoul 1982
.

II.2
Perfect Conductor

Based on the formalism outlined in Secs.
I

and (
II
), and in Chap. 5 of Ref.
Jentschura and Adkins 2022
,
it is relatively straightforward
to evaluate the atom-wall interaction
for a perfect conductor.
We first recall the known result for
the atomic-dipole contribution,
e.g., from Eq. (27) of Ref.
Łach et al. 2010
,

\mathcal{E}_{1}(z)=-\frac{\hbar}{16\pi^{2}\epsilon_{0}z^{3}}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\!{\rm d}\omega\,\alpha_{1}(\mathrm{i}\omega)\\[0.4878pt]
\times\left[1\!+\!\frac{2\omega z}{c}+2\left(\frac{\omega z}{c}\right)^{2}\right]\!\exp\left(-\frac{2\omega z}{c}\right)\,.

(20)

The
(\ell=2)
-contribution to the
energy shift is as follows,

\mathcal{E}_{2}(z)=-\frac{\hbar}{16\pi^{2}\epsilon_{0}z^{5}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega\,\alpha_{2}(\mathrm{i}\,\omega)\,\exp\left(-\frac{2\omega z}{c}\right)\\[0.4878pt]
\times\left[\frac{3}{4}+\frac{3}{2}\,\frac{\omega z}{c}+\frac{4}{3}\,\left(\frac{\omega z}{c}\right)^{2}+\frac{2}{3}\,\left(\frac{\omega z}{c}\right)^{3}+\frac{1}{6}\left(\frac{\omega z}{c}\right)^{4}\right]\,.

(21)

This result constitutes a
correction to a result previous
derived in Eq. (32) of Ref.
Łach et al. 2010
.
For the octupole term, we have

\mathcal{E}_{3}(z)=-\frac{\hbar}{16\pi^{2}\epsilon_{0}z^{7}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega\,\alpha_{3}(\mathrm{i}\,\omega)\mathrm{e}^{-2\omega z/c}\,\\[0.4878pt]
\times\left[\frac{2}{3}+\frac{4}{3}\,\frac{\omega z}{c}+\frac{92}{75}\,\left(\frac{\omega z}{c}\right)^{2}+\frac{152}{225}\,\left(\frac{\omega z}{c}\right)^{3}\right.\\[0.4878pt]
\left.+\frac{6}{25}\,\left(\frac{\omega z}{c}\right)^{3}+\frac{4}{75}\left(\frac{\omega z}{c}\right)^{4}+\frac{4}{675}\left(\frac{\omega z}{c}\right)^{6}\right]\,.

(22)

For large distances, the multipole polarizabilities
are suppressed by higher powers of
z
.
Specifically, the
2^{\ell}
-pole contribution
scales as
z^{-2\ell+1}
in the short-range limit
and as
z^{-2\ell+2}
for
z\to\infty
.
The general structure of the
short-range asymptotic limit is given
in Eq. (
52
),
which in the limit
\epsilon(\mathrm{i}\omega)\to\infty

has the same functional dependence
but a different prefactor than
Eq. (49) of Ref.
Łach et al. 2010
.
In the context of the current investigations,
it is useful to clarify that we understand by
short range (see also Ref.
Łach et al. 2010
)
the distance regime
a_{0}\ll z\ll a_{0}/\alpha
,
where
a_{0}
is the Bohr radius and
\alpha
is the
fine-structure constant. As explained in
Ref.
Zaremba and Kohn 1976
, the first inequality

a_{0}\ll z
should
be taken with a grain of salt in this context;
the short-range expressions
derived below are actually valid down to
distance regions of a few angstroms away
from the surface
Zaremba and Kohn 1976
;
Tao and Rappe 2014
.
From the point of view of physisorption,
what we refer to as the short-range regime
rather constitutes a long-range distance
Zaremba and Kohn 1976
;
Tao and Rappe 2014
.
By contrast, the long-range regime as considered in the
current investigation
refers to atom-surface distances
z\gg a_{0}/\alpha
.

Finally, on the basis of Eqs. (
9
) and (
18
),
while employing, otherwise, the formalism of Ref.
Łach et al. 2010
,
the hexadecupole energy shift
is obtained as follows,

\mathcal{E}_{4}(z)=-\frac{\hbar}{16\pi^{2}\epsilon_{0}z^{9}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega\,\alpha_{4}(\mathrm{i}\,\omega)\mathrm{e}^{-2\omega z/c}\,\\[0.4878pt]
\left[\frac{5}{8}+\frac{5}{4}\,\frac{\omega z}{c}+\frac{115}{98}\,\left(\frac{\omega z}{c}\right)^{2}+\frac{100}{147}\,\left(\frac{\omega z}{c}\right)^{3}\right.\\[0.4878pt]
+\frac{79}{294}\,\left(\frac{\omega z}{c}\right)^{4}+\frac{11}{147}\left(\frac{\omega z}{c}\right)^{5}+\frac{32}{2205}\left(\frac{\omega z}{c}\right)^{6}\\[0.4878pt]
\left.+\frac{4}{2205}\left(\frac{\omega z}{c}\right)^{7}+\frac{1}{8820}\left(\frac{\omega z}{c}\right)^{8}\right]\,.

(23)

This concludes the discussion of interactions
with a perfect conductor.

III
Dielectric Surface

III.1
Dipole Term

In order to analyze the multipole contributions
for a dielectric, one consults the
transverse electric (TE)
and transverse magnetic (TM) modes
given in Eqs. (18), (19) and (20) of Ref.
Łach et al. 2010
.
In this case, TE stands for incident waves whose
electric field is transverse to the plane of incidence, whereas TM
stands for waves whose magnetic field is
transverse to the plane of incidence.
The calculation is described in detail in Ref.
Łach et al. 2010
.
For given wave vector
\vec{k}
, one has two polarization
vectors, one for the the TE mode, and another one for the TM mode.
With these ideas in mind, it is relatively easy to
rederive the following result
for a dipole polarizable
particle in contact with a dielectric surface,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{1}(z)=

\displaystyle\;-\frac{\hbar}{8\pi^{2}\epsilon_{0}\,c^{3}}\,\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega\,\omega^{3}\,\alpha_{1}(\mathrm{i}\omega)\,

\displaystyle\;\times\int_{1}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}p\,\exp\left(-\frac{2\,p\,\omega\,z}{c}\right)\,H(\epsilon(\mathrm{i}\omega),p)\,,

(24)

where

H(\epsilon,p)=\frac{\sqrt{\epsilon-1+p^{2}}-p}{\sqrt{\epsilon-1+p^{2}}+p}+(1-2p^{2})\frac{\sqrt{\epsilon-1+p^{2}}-p\,\epsilon}{\sqrt{\epsilon-1+p^{2}}+p\,\epsilon}.

(25)

For the convenience of the reader,
a remark might be in order. Namely, in order to
obtain Eq. (
III.1
) from Ref.
Lifshitz 1955
,
one sets, in Ref.
Lifshitz 1955
,
\epsilon_{2}(\omega)=1+N_{V}\,\alpha_{1}(\omega)/\epsilon_{0}
, for material number 2, one expands to
first order in the volume density
N_{V}=N/V
, where
N
is the number of atoms
and
V
is the volume, and one applies the principle of virtual work.
This leads to the potential given in Eq. (
III.1
).
The connection of Ref.
Lifshitz 1955

to the atom-surface interaction was also
pointed out in Ref.
Zaremba and Kohn 1976
,
in Ref.
Dzyaloshinskii et al. 1961b
,
and in the second paragraph of p. 6 of Ref.
Bordag et al. 2009
.
Specifically, after the pioneering paper
Lifshitz 1955
,
steps toward the calculation of the long-range and short-range limits of
Eq. (
III.1
) were considered
in Eqs. (4.37)—(4.39) of Ref.
Dzyaloshinskii et al. 1961b
,
and in Eqs. (3) and (4) of Ref.
Derjaguin et al. 1965
.
The interpolating formula (
III.1
)
has been been given in Eqs. (18) and (21) of Ref.
Antezza et al. 2004
,
and in Eqs. (63a) and (63b) of Ref.
Łach et al. 2010
.

It is useful to investigate the function

K(\epsilon,z)=\int_{1}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}p\,\exp\left(-\frac{2\,p\,\omega\,z}{c}\right)\,H(\epsilon,p)\,.

(26)

For
\epsilon\to\infty
, we have

H(\epsilon,p)\approx 2\,p^{2}
,
and

K(\epsilon,z)\mathop{\approx}^{\epsilon\to\infty}\frac{1}{2}\,\left(\frac{c}{z\omega}\right)^{3}\,\left[1+2\,\frac{z\omega}{c}+2\,\left(\frac{z\omega}{c}\right)^{2}\right]\,,

(27)

which shows that the formula (
34
)
is consistent with the result
for the dipole interaction with a perfect conductor.
For
z\to 0
, we have, on the other hand,

K(\epsilon,z)=\frac{1}{2}\,\left(\frac{c}{z\,\omega}\right)^{3}\,\frac{\epsilon-1}{\epsilon+1}+{\mathcal{O}}(z^{-1})\,.

(28)

Inserting (
28
) into (
34
), we have
the short-range limit as

\mathcal{E}_{1}(z)\mathop{=}^{z\to 0}-\frac{\hbar}{16\pi^{2}\,\epsilon_{0}\,z^{3}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega\,\alpha_{1}(\mathrm{i}\omega)\,\frac{\epsilon(\mathrm{i}\omega)-1}{\epsilon(\mathrm{i}\omega)+1}\,.

(29)

The long-range limit is obtained as a
function of the static polarizability

\alpha(0)
and the static dielectric function
\epsilon(0)

as follows,

\mathcal{E}_{1}(z)\mathop{=}^{z\to\infty}-\frac{3c\hbar\,\alpha_{1}(0)}{32\pi^{2}\,\epsilon_{0}\,z^{4}}\,\Psi_{1}(\epsilon(0))\,.

(30)

Here,
\Psi_{1}(\epsilon)
is a function
which is normalized to unity in the limit

\epsilon(0)\to\infty
(limit of a perfect
conductor) and which can otherwise be expressed
as follows,

\Psi_{1}(\epsilon)=A_{1}(\epsilon)+B_{1}(\epsilon)\,\ln\left(\frac{\sqrt{\epsilon-1}-\sqrt{\epsilon}+1}{\sqrt{\epsilon-1}+\sqrt{\epsilon}-1}\right)\\
+C_{1}(\epsilon)\,\ln\left(\frac{\sqrt{\epsilon+1}-\sqrt{\epsilon}+1}{\sqrt{\epsilon+1}+\sqrt{\epsilon}-1}\right)\,.

(31)

The coefficients involve both fractional and integer
powers of
\epsilon
,

\displaystyle A_{1}(\epsilon)=

\displaystyle\;\frac{6\epsilon^{2}-3\epsilon^{3/2}-4\epsilon-3\sqrt{\epsilon}+10}{6(\epsilon-1)}\,,

(32a)

\displaystyle B_{1}(\epsilon)=

\displaystyle\;\frac{2\epsilon^{3}-4\epsilon^{2}+3\epsilon+1}{2(\epsilon-1)^{3/2}}\,,

(32b)

\displaystyle C_{1}(\epsilon)=

\displaystyle\;-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{\sqrt{\epsilon+1}}\,.

(32c)

The expansion about the perfect-conductor limit is

\Psi_{1}(\epsilon)=1-\frac{5}{4\sqrt{\epsilon}}+\frac{22}{15\epsilon}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon^{3/2}}\right)\,,

(33)

which is tantamount to an expansion about a
large static dielectric function
\epsilon\equiv\epsilon(0)
.
We reemphasize that the long-range limit is consistent with
Eqs. (4.37)—(4.39) of Ref.
Dzyaloshinskii et al. 1961b
,
with Eq. (23) of Ref.
Antezza et al. 2004
,
and with Eqs. (27)—(29) of Ref.
Moore et al. 2022
.

III.2
Quadrupole Term

The generalization to the quadrupole polarizability
reads as follows,

\mathcal{E}_{2}(z)=-\frac{\hbar}{16\pi^{2}\,\epsilon_{0}\,c^{5}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega\,\omega^{5}\,\alpha_{2}(\mathrm{i}\omega)\\
\times\int_{1}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}p\,\mathrm{e}^{-2p\omega z/c}\left(\frac{p^{2}}{2}-\frac{1}{3}\right)\,H(\epsilon(\mathrm{i}\omega),p)\,.

(34)

In the perfect conductor limit (
\epsilon\to\infty
),
this result is in agreement with
the previously derived result given in Eq. (
21
).
The short-range limit reads as follows,

\mathcal{E}_{2}(z)\mathop{=}^{z\to 0}-\frac{3\hbar}{64\pi^{2}\,\epsilon_{0}\,z^{5}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega\,\alpha_{2}(\mathrm{i}\omega)\,\frac{\epsilon(\mathrm{i}\omega)-1}{\epsilon(\mathrm{i}\omega)+1}\,.

(35)

The long-range limit is obtained as follows,

\mathcal{E}_{2}(z)\mathop{=}^{z\to\infty}-\frac{35c\hbar\,\alpha_{2}(0)}{384\pi^{2}\,\epsilon_{0}\,z^{6}}\,Psi_{2}(\epsilon(0))\,,

(36)

where
\Psi_{2}
has the same structure as
\Psi_{1}
,

\Psi_{2}(\epsilon)=A_{2}(\epsilon)+B_{2}(\epsilon)\,\ln\left(\frac{\sqrt{\epsilon-1}-\sqrt{\epsilon}+1}{\sqrt{\epsilon-1}+\sqrt{\epsilon}-1}\right)\\
+C_{2}(\epsilon)\,\ln\left(\frac{\sqrt{\epsilon+1}-\sqrt{\epsilon}+1}{\sqrt{\epsilon+1}+\sqrt{\epsilon}-1}\right)\,.

(37)

The coefficients are given as follows,

\displaystyle A_{2}(\epsilon)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{140(\epsilon-1)^{2}}\left[-120\epsilon^{4}+60\epsilon^{7/2}+380\epsilon^{3}\right.

\displaystyle\left.-180\epsilon^{5/2}-364\epsilon^{2}+75\epsilon^{3/2}+348\epsilon+75\sqrt{\epsilon}-224\right]\,,

(38a)

\displaystyle B_{2}(\epsilon)

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{28}\frac{8\epsilon^{5}-28\epsilon^{4}+40\epsilon^{3}-34\,\epsilon^{2}+7\epsilon+5}{(\epsilon-1)^{5/2}}\,,

(38b)

\displaystyle C_{2}(\epsilon)

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{7}\,\frac{\epsilon^{2}(2\epsilon-1)}{\sqrt{\epsilon+1}}\,.

(38c)

The expansion about the perfect-conductor limit is

\Psi_{2}(\epsilon)=1-\frac{31}{28\sqrt{\epsilon}}+\frac{338}{245\epsilon}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon^{3/2}}\right)\,.

(39)

III.3
Octupole Term

For the interaction with a dielectric surface,
the octupole energy shift reads as follows,

\mathcal{E}_{3}(z)=-\frac{\hbar}{16\pi^{2}\,\epsilon_{0}\,c^{7}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega\,\omega^{7}\alpha_{3}(\mathrm{i}\omega)\int_{1}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}p\mathrm{e}^{-2p\omega z/c}\\
\times\left(\frac{8}{135}\,p^{4}-\frac{16}{225}\,p^{2}+\frac{4}{225}\right)\,H(\epsilon(\mathrm{i}\omega),p)\,.

(40)

The calculation of the dielectric response function for
imaginary input frequencies is a nontrivial problem.
The short-range limit is given by the expression

\mathcal{E}_{3}(z)\mathop{=}^{z\to 0}-\frac{\hbar}{24\pi^{2}\,\epsilon_{0}\,z^{7}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega\,\alpha_{3}(\mathrm{i}\omega)\,\frac{\epsilon(\mathrm{i}\omega)-1}{\epsilon(\mathrm{i}\omega)+1}\,.

(41)

The long-range limit is

\mathcal{E}_{3}(z)\mathop{=}^{z\to\infty}-\frac{77c\hbar\,\alpha_{3}(0)}{800\pi^{2}\,\epsilon_{0}\,z^{8}}\,\Psi_{3}(\epsilon(0))\,,

(42)

where
\Psi_{3}
reads as follows,

\Psi_{3}(\epsilon)=A_{3}(\epsilon)+B_{3}(\epsilon)\,\ln\left(\frac{\sqrt{\epsilon-1}-\sqrt{\epsilon}+1}{\sqrt{\epsilon-1}+\sqrt{\epsilon}-1}\right)\\
+C_{3}(\epsilon)\,\ln\left(\frac{\sqrt{\epsilon+1}-\sqrt{\epsilon}+1}{\sqrt{\epsilon+1}+\sqrt{\epsilon}-1}\right)\,.

(43)

The coefficients are given as follows,

\displaystyle A_{3}(\epsilon)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{11088(\epsilon-1)^{3}}\biggl[-2520\epsilon^{11/2}-28560\epsilon^{5}

\displaystyle\;+13860\epsilon^{9/2}+66528\epsilon^{4}-31290\epsilon^{7/2}

\displaystyle\;-76336\epsilon^{3}+29120\epsilon^{5/2}+62016\epsilon^{2}

\displaystyle\;-2415\epsilon^{3/2}-48675\epsilon-6195\sqrt{\epsilon}+19888\biggr]\,,

(44a)

\displaystyle B_{3}(\epsilon)

\displaystyle=\frac{5}{528(\epsilon-1)^{7/2}}\bigl[48\epsilon^{7}-288\epsilon^{6}+712\epsilon^{5}

\displaystyle\;-976\epsilon^{4}+890\epsilon^{3}-444\epsilon^{2}+5\epsilon+59\bigr]\,,

(44b)

\displaystyle C_{3}(\epsilon)

\displaystyle=-\frac{5}{33}\,\frac{3\epsilon^{2}-6\epsilon+1}{\sqrt{\epsilon+1}}\,.

(44c)

The expansion about the perfect-conductor limit is

\Psi_{3}(\epsilon)=1-\frac{265}{264\sqrt{\epsilon}}+\frac{914}{693\epsilon}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon^{3/2}}\right)\,.

(45)

III.4
Hexadecupole Term

For the interaction with a dielectric surface,
the octupole energy shift reads as follows,

\mathcal{E}_{4}(z)=-\frac{\hbar}{16\pi^{2}\,\epsilon_{0}\,c^{9}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega\,\omega^{9}\alpha_{4}(\mathrm{i}\omega)\int_{1}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}p\mathrm{e}^{-2p\omega z/c}\\
\times\left(\frac{1}{252}\,p^{6}-\frac{1}{147}\,p^{4}+\frac{1}{294}\,p^{2}-\frac{1}{2205}\right)\,H(\epsilon(\mathrm{i}\omega),p)\,.

(46)

The short-range limit is

\mathcal{E}_{4}(z)\mathop{=}^{z\to 0}-\frac{5\hbar}{128\pi^{2}\,\epsilon_{0}\,z^{9}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega\,\alpha_{4}(\mathrm{i}\omega)\,\frac{\epsilon(\mathrm{i}\omega)-1}{\epsilon(\mathrm{i}\omega)+1}\,.

(47)

The long-range limit is obtained as follows,

\mathcal{E}_{4}(z)\mathop{=}^{z\to\infty}-\frac{1287c\hbar\,\alpha_{4}(0)}{12544\pi^{2}\,\epsilon_{0}\,z^{10}}\,\Psi_{4}(\epsilon(0))\,,

(48)

where
\Psi_{4}
reads as follows,

\Psi_{4}(\epsilon)=A_{4}(\epsilon)+B_{4}(\epsilon)\,\ln\left(\frac{\sqrt{\epsilon-1}-\sqrt{\epsilon}+1}{\sqrt{\epsilon-1}+\sqrt{\epsilon}-1}\right)\\
+C_{4}(\epsilon)\,\ln\left(\frac{\sqrt{\epsilon+1}-\sqrt{\epsilon}+1}{\sqrt{\epsilon+1}+\sqrt{\epsilon}-1}\right)\,.

(49)

The coefficients are given as follows,

\displaystyle A_{4}(\epsilon)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{205920(\epsilon-1)^{4}}\biggl[-40320\epsilon^{8}+20160\epsilon^{15/2}

\displaystyle\;-690270\epsilon^{5/2}-2110464\epsilon^{2}-62265\epsilon^{3/2}

\displaystyle\;+1300000\epsilon+118125\epsilon^{1/2}-376480\biggr]\,,

\displaystyle B_{4}(\epsilon)

\displaystyle=-\frac{7}{4576(\epsilon-1)^{9/2}}\bigl[128\epsilon^{9}-1216\epsilon^{8}+4608\epsilon^{7}

\displaystyle\;-9600\epsilon^{6}+12720\epsilon^{5}-12120\epsilon^{4}

\displaystyle\;+8080\epsilon^{3}-2580\epsilon^{2}-405\epsilon+375\bigr]\,,

(50b)

\displaystyle C_{4}(\epsilon)

\displaystyle=\frac{7}{143}\,\frac{4\epsilon^{3}-18\epsilon^{2}+12\epsilon-1}{\sqrt{\epsilon+1}}\,.

\Psi_{4}(\epsilon)=1-\frac{1323}{1430\sqrt{\epsilon}}+\frac{18050}{14157\epsilon}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon^{3/2}}\right)\,.

III.5
Some General Short–Range Results

In the short-range limit,
a few simplifications and generalizations
are possible, especially in regard to
two-body bound systems like hydrogen and
positronium.
First, we may point out the
generalization of the short-range
expressions given in
Eqs. (
29
), (
35
), (
41
),
and (

), to arbitrary multipole orders.
Indeed, the general result for the

-pole effect

\mathcal{E}_{\ell}(z)\mathop{=}^{z\to 0}-\frac{\hbar}{16\pi^{2}\,\epsilon_{0}\,z^{2\ell+1}}\,\\
\times\frac{\ell+1}{2\ell}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega\,\alpha_{\ell}(\mathrm{i}\omega)\,\frac{\epsilon(\mathrm{i}\omega)-1}{\epsilon(\mathrm{i}\omega)+1}\,.

(52)

This result has the same functional form as
Eq. (49) of Ref.
Łach et al. 2010
,
but a different, updated prefactor.

IV
Calculation of Multipole Corrections

IV.1
Multipoles
for Hydrogen and Positronium

We aim to give an update on
multipole corrections to atom-surface interactions,
beyond the discussion in Ref.
Łach et al. 2010
.
Hydrogen and positronium constitute
atomic systems for which the exact
evaluation of multipole polarizabilities
is possible analytically (see Appendix
A
).
Hence, we focus on these two atomic systems,
for definiteness, while
stressing that other atomic systems
could be more interesting from
the point of view of applications
(see Sec.
IV.3
).
The derivation of the multipole polarizabilities
of hydrogen and positronium uses the
Sturmian decomposition of the Schrödinger–Coulomb
Green function, and the evaluation of radial
matrix elements, according to the formalism
outlined in Chap. 4 of Ref.
Jentschura and Adkins 2022
.
In order to ensure concise formulas, we now switch to atomic units
(see Chap. 2 of Ref. [13]) with
a_{0}=1
,
E_{h}=1
,

\epsilon_{0}=1/(4\pi)
,
\hbar=1
, and
e=1
(unit elementary charge).

Using the results given in Appendix
A
,
we are now in the position to derive some more closed-form
expressions for interactions with a perfect
conductor, i.e., in the limit

\epsilon(\mathrm{i}\omega)\to\infty
.
Specifically, for multipole interactions with atomic hydrogen,
one obtains the following result
for the integral over the
2^{\ell}
-pole polarizability,

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega\,\alpha_{\ell}(\mathrm{i}\omega)=\frac{\pi\,\Gamma(2\ell+3)}{2^{2\ell+1}\,(2\ell+1)}\frac{a_{0}^{2\ell}}{\hbar}\,.

(53)

The general result for the
2^{\ell}
-pole
energy shift for hydrogen (H) interacting
with a perfect conductor thus is as follows,

\mathcal{E}^{({\rm H})}_{\ell}(z)\mathop{=}^{z\to 0,\epsilon\to\infty}-\frac{(\ell+1)\,\Gamma(2\ell+3)}{2^{2\ell+4}\,\ell\,(2\ell+1)}\,\frac{E_{h}}{(z/a_{0})^{2\ell+1}}\,.

(54)

Here,
E_{h}
is the Hartree energy,
and
a_{0}
is the Bohr radius.
The ratio
z/a_{0}
is equal to the
atom-wall distance, expressed in atomic units
(see Chap. 2 of Ref.
Jentschura and Adkins 2022
).
For positronium (Ps), one obtains the result

\mathcal{E}^{({\rm Ps})}_{\ell}(z)\mathop{=}^{z\to 0,\epsilon\to\infty}-\frac{(\ell+1)\,\Gamma(2\ell+3)}{16\,\,\ell\,(2\ell+1)}\,\frac{E_{h}}{(z/a_{0})^{2\ell+1}}\,.

(55)

In the short-range regime,
the expansion into multipoles
constitutes an expansion in powers of

a_{0}/z
, where
a_{0}
is the Bohr
radius and
z
is the atom-wall distance.

The general results given in
Eqs. (
54
)
and (
55
)
demonstrate that the sum over
the multipole potentials (at least for hydrogen
and positronium interacting with
a perfect conductor)
constitutes a divergent, asymptotic series.
The divergence, for any distance
z
,
happens due to the factorial growth of the prefactor

\Gamma(2\ell+3)
.
Optimal truncation of the multipole
expansion at the smallest term
of the series then constitutes a valid
procedure for obtaining theoretical
predictions
Caliceti et al. 2007
,
while Borel summation can be used in order
to sum the divergent series
Jentschura 2000
.

IV.2
Hydrogen, Positronium and
{{\alpha}}
–Quartz

We aim to combine the analysis of the
multipole polarizabilities given in
Appendix
A
and
Sec.
IV.1
with an update on

\alpha
-quartz (Ref.
Łach et al. 2010
).
For the data presented in
Phillipp
,
we used the following fit formula
discussed in Ref.
Łach et al. 2010
,

\displaystyle\rho(\omega)\equiv

\displaystyle\;\frac{\epsilon(\omega)-1}{\epsilon(\omega)+2}=\frac{[n(\omega)+\mathrm{i}\,k(\omega)]^{2}-1}{[n(\omega)+\mathrm{i}\,k(\omega)]^{2}+2}

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle\;\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\alpha_{k}\,\omega_{k}^{2}}{\omega_{k}^{2}-\mathrm{i}\,\gamma_{k}\,\omega-\omega^{2}}\,.

(56)

Here,
n(\omega)
are
k(\omega)
are
the dispersive and absorptive parts of the
index of refraction,
while the functional form
is inspired by the Clausius–Mossotti equation.
We take the opportunity to
point out the missing factor
\omega_{k}^{2}
in the
numerator of the fitting function
given in Eq. (70) of Ref.
Łach et al. 2010
.
The missing prefactor had previously been
supplemented in Eq. (21) of Ref.
Jentschura et al. 2016
.
For completeness, it might be
useful to point out that
the expression
(\epsilon(\omega)-1)/(\epsilon(\omega)+1)
,
which appears in the integrand of the
short-range
expressions (
29
), (
35
), (
41
),
and (
47
),
can be obtained from
\rho(\omega)
as follows,

\frac{\epsilon(\omega)-1}{\epsilon(\omega)+1}=\frac{3\rho(\omega)}{\rho(\omega)+2}\,.

(57)

For intrinsic silicon
Moore et al. 2022
,
we have recently found that a better
analytic representation can be
obtained based on the following fit formula,

\displaystyle\rho(\omega)\simeq

\displaystyle\;\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\alpha_{k}\,(\omega_{k}^{2}-\mathrm{i}\,\gamma^{\prime}_{k}\,\omega)}{\omega_{k}^{2}-\mathrm{i}\,\gamma_{k}\,\omega-\omega^{2}}\,.

(58)

where the expression
[\alpha_{k}\,(\omega_{k}^{2}-\mathrm{i}\,\gamma^{\prime}_{k}\,\omega)]

can be regarded as a complex oscillator
strength that includes a
phenomenological model of radiative reaction
Moore et al. 2022
.
We find
that such a model represents the data
from Ref.
Phillipp
very well
(see Figs.
1
and
2
,
as well as Table
1
).
This finding is nontrivial in view of the
necessity for any fit function to
fulfill the Kramers–Kronig relationships
(see, e.g., Chap. 6 of Ref.
Jentschura 2017
),
which relate the real and imaginary
parts of
\rho
and
\epsilon
.
The functional form of our model (
58
) fulfills
the Kramers–Kronig relationships automatically.
While the oscillator strengths and resonance
frequencies for vibrational
excitations differ in between the ordinary
and the extraordinary axis of
\alpha
-quartz
(see Table
1
),
we find that the influence of the
low-frequency vibrational excitations on the leading short-range
expansion coefficients reported below is
numerically negligible.
Similar approaches as described by Eq. (
58
)
have been discussed (for rutile) in Eq. (1) of Ref.
Ribarsky
,
in Eq. (4) of Ref.
Tropf
(for cubic thallium),
in Eq. (1) of Ref.
Palik and Khanna
(for sodium nitrate),
and in Eq. (5) of Ref.
Fuller et al.
(for orthorhombic sulphur).

We are now in the position to analyze
the multipole corrections for
\alpha
-quartz.
In general, we can say that
these intriguing corrections to atom-surface interactions
have generated considerable interest
[see, e.g., Ref.
Łach et al. 2011
,
Eq. (2) of Ref.
Tao and Rappe 2014
,
and Eq. (8) of Ref.
Zheng et al. 2017
].
In order to put this finding into
perspective, we should point out that it
has recently become possible to
calculate the multipole corrections
to polarizabilities
more accurately from first principle,
for general multi-electron
atoms
Kaur et al. 2022
.
In this article, in order to facilitate the
analysis of the multipole corrections,
we have evaluated exact expressions
for hydrogen and positronium up to the
hexadecupole order (see Appendix
A
and Sec.
IV.1
).

It is indicated to include a brief
discussion on the magnitude of the
multipole corrections. We continue to use
atomic units.
A priori
, the short-range approximations are
valid for
z\leq 1/\alpha\approx 137.036

in atomic units, i.e., for distances smaller than
about 137 Bohr radii
Łach et al. 2010
;
Jentschura and Moore 2023
.
The upper end of the range of validity of the short-range,
nonretarded approximation and its dependence
on the atomic species has recently
been discussed in Ref.
Das et al.
.
The leading term for the
2^{\ell}
-pole multipole term
is proportional to
1/z^{2\ell+1}
.
It is well known that Lifshitz theory
cannot be used for arbitrarily close
approach to the surface.
The probability density of
atomic wave functions (for ground-state hydrogen atoms)
decrease with a probability

|\psi|^{2}\sim\exp(-2r)
,
where
r
is the distance from the
nucleus in atomic units.
For a distance of
z=0.5\,{\rm nm}
,
which is roughly equal to

z=10
in atomic units,
one has
|\psi|^{2}\sim 10^{-9}
,
eliminating the overlap as
a possible limiting factor.
It has been stressed in Ref.
Zaremba and Kohn 1976
that, for vanishing
overlap of the atomic wave function with
the surface, the exchange of electrons between the
atom and the substrate can
be neglected, which, in turn,
makes it possible to treat the electrons
(and nuclei) of the atom and the surface
as distinguishable.
Furthermore, in the seminal paper
Zaremba and Kohn 1976
,
it has been stressed in remarks following
Eq. (2.39) that even for separations
typically encountered in physisorption
(
\sim
4-7 Bohr radii), the formula

V(z)\approx-C_{3}/(z-z_{0})^{3}
,
is applicable.
Here,
C_{3}
is given implicitly in Eq. (
29
).
Here,
z_{0}
is the position
of a suitably defined reference plane
given by Eq. (2.38) of Ref.
Zaremba and Kohn 1976
.
The position of the reference plane
(see also Fig. 1 of Ref.
Zaremba and Kohn 1976
)
is given by an integral which depends on
both the susceptibility of the atom
and also [via the integral
given in Eq. (2.28) of Ref.
Zaremba and Kohn 1976
]
on the susceptibility of the solid.
One accepted path toward the calculation
of physisorption energies has involved
the addition of an ultrashort-range
(overlap) contribution to the
energy, which is calculated on the basis
of density-functional (DFT) theory,
and the van-der-Waals energy,
the latter being
calculated according to the ideas
outlined in Ref.
Zaremba and Kohn 1976
.
One possible pathway toward the calculation
of the contact contribution (the DFT part) is based on
DFT–GGA, where GGA stands for the generalized
gradient approximation
Perdew et al. 1996
.
The entire procedure is often referred to as
the van-der-Waals-corrected DFT
approach
Grimme 2004
;
Grimme 2006
;
Grant 2010
;
Silvestrelli et al. 2012
.
A calculation of the reference-plane position
z_{0}

for
\alpha
-quartz is beyond the scope of
the current paper.
We merely use an exemplary distance of

z=10
atomic units for the calculations
reported below, in order to illustrate the
magnitude of the multipole corrections
for close approach,
and furthermore, assume positronium
to be at rest (cf. Refs.
Manson and Ritchie 1984
;
Paranjape and Pathak 1985
).

We can thus use an exemplary distance of

z=10
atomic units, in order to
analyze the magnitude of the multipole
corrections.
A further remark is in order.
It has recently been shown in Ref.
Jentschura and Moore 2023

that higher-order terms in the atom-surface
potential contain logarithms of the
atom-wall distance,
\ln(z)
,
leading to a semi-analytic expansion
of the atom-surface potential in powers
of
z
and
\ln(z)
, described by coefficients
with two indices.
Hence, we will refer to the leading short-range
coefficient multiplying for the atom-surface dipole
and quadrupole terms as
C_{30}
and
C_{50}

(rather than
C_{3}
and
C_{5}
),
where the first index counts the power of
z

and the second index (equal to zero) indicates the
absence of a logarithm
Jentschura and Moore 2023
.

For a perfect conductor, we thus write
the relations

\mathcal{E}_{1}(z)\approx-\frac{C_{30}}{z^{3}}\,,\quad\mathcal{E}_{2}(z)\approx-\frac{C_{50}}{z^{5}}\,,\quad 1\ll z\ll\alpha^{-1}\,,

(59)

with the following exemplary results
(for the dipole and quadrupole coefficients)
for perfect conductors
[see Eq. (
54
) and (
55
)]

\displaystyle C_{30}^{({\rm H})}\;\mathop{=}^{\epsilon\to\infty}\;

\displaystyle\;\frac{1}{4}\,,\qquad

\displaystyle C_{50}^{({\rm H})}\;\mathop{=}^{\epsilon\to\infty}\;\frac{27}{32}\,,

(60)

\displaystyle C_{30}^{({\rm Ps})}\;\mathop{=}^{\epsilon\to\infty}\;

\displaystyle\;1\,,\qquad

\displaystyle C_{50}^{({\rm Ps})}\;\mathop{=}^{\epsilon\to\infty}\;\frac{27}{2}\,.

(61)

For
\alpha
-quartz, we refer to Tables
2
and
3

for the multipole coefficients.
For positronium, at
z=10
atomic units, one has the
following ratio of the quadrupole corrections
to the leading dipole term,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{E}^{({\rm Ps})}_{2}(z=10)}{\mathcal{E}^{({\rm Ps})}_{1}(z=10)}=

\displaystyle\;\frac{C^{({\rm Ps})}_{50}}{C^{({\rm Ps})}_{30}\times(10)^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}0.135&\;\;\mbox{(conductor)}\\
0.124&\;\;\mbox{($\alpha$--quartz)}\\
\end{array}\right.\,.

A deviation by 13.5% is larger than the uncertainty
of many current measurements of the dielectric function
of materials
Palik 1985
.
For hydrogen, the quadrupole correction is

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{E}^{({\rm H})}_{2}(z=10)}{\mathcal{E}^{({\rm H})}_{1}(z=10)}=

\displaystyle\;\frac{C^{({\rm H})}_{50}}{C^{({\rm H})}_{30}\times(10)^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}0.0336&\;\;\mbox{(conductor)}\\
0.0296&\;\;\mbox{($\alpha$--quartz)}\\
\end{array}\right.\,,

which is of the order of a few percent.
These observations are consistent with the
literature
Tao and Rappe 2014
.
In the study of atom-wall interactions,
atomic systems with an exceptionally
large static polarizability
have attracted considerable attention.
One example is metastable helium
(in the metastable spin-triplet state)
with a static polarizability of
315 a.u. (see Ref.
Caride et al. 2005
).
For such systems, one can expect even larger
corrections due to quadrupole effects.
Investigations in these directions are currently
in progress. In the current section, we restrict our attention
to hydrogen and positronium, for which the
multipole polarizabilities can be
evaluated in closed analytic form
(see Appendix
A
).

IV.3
Applications to Physisorption

As already outlined above,
the application of van der Waals corrected
density-functional theory to the adsorption
of rare gases on surfaces is a standard
process in surface
physics
Zaremba and Kohn 1976
;
Perdew et al. 1996
;
Grimme 2004
;
Grimme 2006
;
Grant 2010
;
Da Silva and Stampfl 2008
.
In this context, one adds the van der Waals
energy which is due to the interaction
with all atoms in the solid, evaluated at the adsorption coordinate,
to a DFT term, which results from the interaction
with the neareast neighbors at the adsorption site.
This approach had been mentioned in the text in
the upper right column of p. 2280 of Ref.
Zaremba and Kohn 1976
.
The method has been further developed over a couple of
decades; the justification for this approach and the general theoretical
background are being
discussed in Refs.
Zaremba and Kohn 1976
;
Perdew et al. 1996
;
Grimme 2004
;
Grimme 2006
;
Grant 2010
;
Da Silva and Stampfl 2008
;
D.-L. et al. 2012
;
Silvestrelli et al. 2012
;
Tao and Rappe 2014
.

One of the most important results
of the current study is the
additional factor
(\ell+1)/(2\ell)

in Eq. (
54
) as compared to
the results communicated in Ref.
Łach et al. 2010
.
This correction factor evaluates to
3/4
for
the quadrupole term.
This correction factor also affects a few
results recorded in the literature, for example,
the
C_{5}
coefficients reported in
Ref.
Kaur et al. 2022
.
In Ref.
Zaremba and Kohn 1976
, the authors
take into account the effect of the
reference plane at
z_{0}
in the modified
expansion

V(z)=-\frac{C_{3}}{(z-z_{0})^{3}}-\frac{C^{\prime}_{5}}{(z-z_{0})^{5}}-\cdots\,,

(66)

where
C^{\prime}_{5}=C_{5}+6C_{3}z_{0}^{2}
, and
C_{5}

is the quadrupole coefficient from Eq. (
54
),
which reads as follows (in atomic units),

C_{5}=\frac{3}{16\pi}\,\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega\,\alpha_{2}(\mathrm{i}\omega)\,\frac{\epsilon(\mathrm{i}\omega)-1}{\epsilon(\mathrm{i}\omega)+1}\,.

(67)

Let us consider two examples taken from
Table III of Ref.
Tao and Rappe 2014
,
namely, Kr on Cu(111) and Ar on Pd(111).
For Kr on Cu(111), one has
C_{3}=0.558
and

C^{\prime}_{5}=3.963
in atomic units
according to Table III of Ref.
Tao and Rappe 2014
,
as well as
z_{\rm eq}=5.99
(equilibrium position)
and
z_{0}=0.39
. One can easily solve for

C_{5}=3.453
(before correction) and

C_{5}=2.590
(after correction with the
multiplicative factor
3/4
).
Adding the result from DFT-GGA from
Table III of Ref.
Tao and Rappe 2014
,
which 20.3 meV, one obtains the
modified van der Waals corrected adsorption
energy of 121 meV which is even closer to the
value of 119 meV from Ref.
Silvestrelli et al. 2012

than the value of 126 meV given in
Table III of Ref.
Tao and Rappe 2014
.

For Ar on Pd(111), one has
C_{3}=0.476
and

C^{\prime}_{5}=2.584
in atomic units
according to Table III of Ref.
Tao and Rappe 2014
,
as well as
z_{\rm eq}=5.59
(equilibrium position)
and
z_{0}=0.26
. One then solves for

C_{5}=2.391
(before correction) and

C_{5}=1.793
(after correction).
Adding the result for DFT-GGA
from Table III of Ref.
Tao and Rappe 2014
,
which 14.9 meV, one obtains the
modified van der Waals corrected adsorption
energy of 113 meV which is a bit closer to the
comparison value of 110 meV from Ref.
Da Silva and Stampfl 2008

than the value of 117 meV originally given in
Table III of Ref.
Tao and Rappe 2014
.
Further considerations on adsorption energies
are currently in progress.

V
Conclusions

In this article,
we have (re-)derived (see Ref.
Łach et al. 2010
)
the quadrupole, octupole and hexadecupole
corrections to the atom-wall interaction,
with a special emphasis on the isolation
of the relevant angular-momentum components from the derivative
tensors of the electric field.
The functional form of our results is the
same as the one obtained in
in Ref.
Łach et al. 2010
,
but important differences are obtained
for the prefactors.

The general results for the
dipole, quadrupole, octupole and hexadecupole contributions
to the atom-wall interactions have
been given in Eqs. (
III.1
), (
34
), (
40
)
and (
46
), respectively.
Short-range limits have been given for
either term in
Eqs. (
29
), (
35
), (
41
),
and (
47
),
and the long-range limits
have been analyzed in
Eqs. (
30
), (
36
), (
42
)
and (
48
).
For the long-range limit, we have found rather concise
formulas for the dependence of the coefficient
multiplying the term proportional
to
z^{-2\ell-2}
, in terms
of logarithms [see Eqs. (
31
), (
37
), (
43
)
and (
49
)].
The analytic results for the long-range limit
constitute an important addition to the
results originally reported in Refs.
Łach et al. 2010
.

Furthermore, we find that the expansion
into multipole terms constitutes an asymptotic,
divergent series [see Eqs. (
54
)
and (
55
)]. Series with factorially
divergent coefficients can, in many cases,
be summed using generalizations of the
Borel method
Jentschura 2000
;
Caliceti et al. 2007
.
Furthermore, a truncation of the series
at the smallest term yields an excellent
approximation to the complete result,
so that the divergent character of the series
is not an obstacle to the deduction of
theoretical predictions.
We find that it is possible to express
the quadrupole, octupole, and hexadecupole
polarizabilities of hydrogen
and positronium in closed analytic form (see Appendix
A
).
This enables us to reanalyze multipole
corrections, with a special
emphasis on hydrogen and positronium.
We find that the quadrupole correction
is phenomenologically relevant [see Eqs. (
IV.2
)
and (
IV.2
)]. A concrete application
is discussed in Sec.
IV.3
.
A reanalysis of the dielectric
function of
\alpha
-quartz using the functional form
given in Eq. (
58
)
reveals very good agreement with
numerical data from Ref.
Phillipp
.
For the short-range coefficients
of the multipole corrections to the atom-surface
interactions (perfect conductor and
\alpha
-quartz),
we present results in Tables
2
and
3
.
These data confirm the rapid growth of the
coefficients multiplying the multipole corrections,
both for interactions with perfect conductor and
\alpha
-quartz,
consistent with the eventual factorial divergence
of the series.
Applications to physisorption (van der Waals
corrected density-functional theory) are discussed
in Sec.
IV.3
.
The modified result for the quadrupole correction
derived here yields important
corrections to results previously communicated
in Ref.
Tao and Rappe 2014
.

Appendix A
Multipole Polarizabilties of Hydrogen and Positronium

Hydrogen and positronium constitute
atomic systems for which the exact
evaluation of multipole polarizabilities
is possible analytically.
Hence, we focus on these two atomic sytems,
for definiteness, while
stressing that other atomic systems
could be more interesting from
the point of view of applications
(see Sec.
IV.3
).
In this Appendix, we use atomic units
(see Chap. 2 of Ref.
Jentschura and Adkins 2022
)
with
a_{0}=1
,
E_{h}=1
,
\epsilon_{0}=1/(4\pi)
,

\hbar=1
and
e=1
(unit elementary charge).
The atoms of interest are
hydrogen and positronium.
With techniques outlined in Ref.
Gavrila and Costescu 1970

and in Chap. 4
of Ref.
Jentschura and Adkins 2022
,
it is possible to derive closed-form expressions
for hydrogen and positronium
which we use in the nonrelativistic, and non-recoil limit.
We consider the specialization of the
tensor (
11
) to a one-electron
atom and work with the matrix element

(68)

where the superscript indicates the atom
(H stands for hydrogen). From

,
one obtains the
2^{\ell}
-pole polarizability as

\alpha^{({\rm H})}_{\ell}(\omega)=Q_{\ell}^{({\rm H})}(\omega)+Q_{\ell}^{({\rm H})}(-\omega)\,.

(69)

The polarizability of positronium

(70)

According to Eq. (4.154) of Ref.

,
the dipole term can be expressed in terms
of a variable

(71)
