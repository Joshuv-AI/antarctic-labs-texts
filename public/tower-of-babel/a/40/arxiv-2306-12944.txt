Quantum Optimal Transport and Weak Topologies
Laurent Lafleche

Abstract
. Several extensions of the classical optimal transport distances to the quantum setting have been proposed. In this paper, we investigate the pseudometrics introduced by Golse, Mouhot and Paul in [Commun Math Phys 343:165–205, 2016] and by Golse and Paul in [Arch Ration Mech Anal 223:57–94, 2017]. These pseudometrics serve as a quantum analogue of the Monge–Kantorovich–Wasserstein distances of order 2 2 on the phase space. We prove that they are comparable to negative Sobolev norms up to a small term due to a positive "self-distance" in the semiclassical approximation, which can be bounded above using the Wigner–Yanase skew information. This enables us to improve the known results in the context of the mean-field and semiclassical limits by requiring less regularity on the initial data.

Introduction

There has been a recent surge of interest in the analogue of quantum optimal transportation within the realm of quantum mechanics, as well as in non-commutative settings in general. The objective of this paper is to compare the pseudometrics introduced in
[
15
,
16

which are a quantum analogue of the (Monge–Kantorovich)–Wasserstein distances, with the quantum analogue of negative Sobolev norms for a wide variety of operators. These tools were used in particular to study the mean-field and classical limit from many body quantum mechanics in
[

. Furthermore, additional properties of these pseudometrics were investigated in

The pseudometrics are created by analogy with the classical Kantorovich formulation of the optimal transport problem with quadratic cost, and we refer for example to
[
30
,
27
]
for the definition of the classical optimal transport problem and distances. The use of the word "pseudometric" is due to the fact that these quantum analogues of the optimal transport distances do not strictly qualify as distances. This is because the distance between an element and itself is strictly positive, although vanishing in the semiclassical limit. It can be seen as an uncertainty principle. Our first main result in this paper will be an estimation of this "self-distance".

Other contexts and other formulations of the classical optimal transport distances have brought several definitions of quantum analogues of optimal transport distances. One of the first proposals to measure the optimal transport distance between quantum states can be found in
[
32
]
, which involves utilizing the classical Wasserstein distance of the Husimi transforms of the operators. Other Wasserstein-type functionals were proposed to study the behavior of quantum Markov processes by Agredo in
[
2
]
taking inspiration from the Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality, in
[
5
]
, with the gradient flow point of view and following the ideas of Benamou and Brenier. Proposals have also been made in
[
6
,
11
,
10
,
20
,
29
]
with some applications to quantum machine learning, where the cost function is refined to improve some properties of the distance. Let us in particular give a slightly longer comment on the pseudometric built by De Palma and Trevisan in
[
10
]
, which is also inspired by the Kantorovich formulation of optimal transport, but with a transposition in the cost. In the context of this pseudometric, the "self-distance" is actually proved to be exactly equal to the Wigner–Yanase information.

1.1.
Quantum optimal transport pseudometrics

We now introduce more precisely the pseudometrics as well as other definitions and notations used in this paper. The pseudometrics are defined between probability densities on the phase space, whose set will be denoted by
\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
, and density operators, whose set is defined as

\mathcal{P}(\mathfrak{h}):=\set{\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mathcal{L}^{\infty}(\mathfrak{h}):\boldsymbol{\rho}\geq 0\text{ and }h^{d}\tr\!\left(\boldsymbol{\rho}\right)=1}

where
\mathcal{L}^{\infty}(\mathfrak{h})
is the set of bounded operators acting on
\mathfrak{h}=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Notice that our normalization
h^{d}\tr\!\left(\boldsymbol{\rho}\right)=1
is different from the one used in
[
15
,
16
]
, but one can easily pass from one to the other by considering
R:=h^{d}\boldsymbol{\rho}
, which is of trace
1
. We also define the space of densities with
n
bounded moments

(1)

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})

\displaystyle:=\set{f\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{R}^{2d}),\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\left|{z}\right|^{n}f(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z)<\infty}

(2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{n}(\mathfrak{h})

\displaystyle:=\set{\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathfrak{h}),h^{d}\tr\!\left(\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\left(\left|{x}\right|^{n}+\left|{\boldsymbol{p}}\right|^{n}\right)\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right)<\infty}

where the momentum operator is defined by

\boldsymbol{p}:=-i\hbar\nabla

and for any operator
A
, its modulus is defined by
\left|{A}\right|=\sqrt{A^{*}A}
where
A^{*}
is the adjoint of
A
. In particular, we will write
\left|{\boldsymbol{p}}\right|^{n}=\hbar^{n}\left(-\Delta\right)^{n/2}
.

To define the pseudometrics, we first introduce the notion of coupling. If
f\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
and
\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathfrak{h})
, the set of couplings of
f
and
\boldsymbol{\rho}
, denoted by
\mathcal{C}(f,\boldsymbol{\rho})
is defined as the set of operator valued measures
\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\boldsymbol{\gamma}(z)
such that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\boldsymbol{\gamma}(z)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z=\boldsymbol{\rho}\quad\text{ and }\quad h^{d}\tr\!\left(\boldsymbol{\gamma}(z)\right)=f(z)

where the second identity should in general be interpreted in the sense of distributions. The semiclassical optimal transport pseudometric between
f
and
\boldsymbol{\rho}
is then defined in
[
16
,
19
]
as
1
1

1

Again, the
h^{d}
appears in the definition because of the choice of normalization. This is indeed the same quantity as the quantity
\mathfrak{d}
in
[
19
]
since if
R=h^{d}\,\boldsymbol{\rho}
, then
\W_{2,\hbar}(f,\boldsymbol{\rho})=\mathfrak{d}(f,R)
.

\W_{2,\hbar}(f,\boldsymbol{\rho})^{2}:=\inf_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\mathcal{C}(f,\boldsymbol{\rho})}h^{d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\tr\!\left(\boldsymbol{\gamma}(z)^{1/2}\,\boldsymbol{c}(z)\,\boldsymbol{\gamma}(z)^{1/2}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z

with
z=(y,\xi)
and
\boldsymbol{c}(z)=\left|{y-x}\right|^{2}+\left|{\xi-\boldsymbol{p}}\right|^{2}
. We also allow to invert the position of the function and the operator in the notation by defining
\W_{2,\hbar}(\boldsymbol{\rho},f):=\W_{2,\hbar}(f,\boldsymbol{\rho})
. Similarly, if
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathfrak{h})
is another density operator, then the set of couplings between
\boldsymbol{\rho}
and
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2}
, denoted by
\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{\rho},\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2})
, is defined as the set of operators
\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathfrak{h}\otimes\mathfrak{h})
such that

h^{d}\tr_{1}(\boldsymbol{\gamma})=\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2}\quad\text{ and }\quad h^{d}\tr_{2}(\boldsymbol{\gamma})=\boldsymbol{\rho},

where
\tr_{1}
denotes the partial trace with respect to the first variable and
\tr_{2}
the partial trace with respect to the second variable. The quantum optimal transport pseudometric between
\boldsymbol{\rho}
and
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2}
is then defined in
[
15
, Definition 2.2]
and
[
19
]
as

(3)

\W_{2,\hbar}(\boldsymbol{\rho},\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2})^{2}:=\inf_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{\rho},\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2})}h^{2d}\tr\!\left(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{1/2}\,\boldsymbol{c}\,\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{1/2}\right)

where
\boldsymbol{c}=\left|{x-x_{2}}\right|^{2}+\left|{\boldsymbol{p}-\boldsymbol{p}_{2}}\right|^{2}
is an unbounded operator on
\mathfrak{h}\otimes\mathfrak{h}
. These quantities are not distances since, as proved in
[
16
, Theorem 2.4]
and
[
15
, Theorem 2.3]
, they satisfy for any
(f,\boldsymbol{\rho},\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2})\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathfrak{h})^{2}
,

\W_{2,\hbar}(f,\boldsymbol{\rho})^{2}\geq d\,\hbar\quad\text{ and }\quad\W_{2,\hbar}(\boldsymbol{\rho},\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2})^{2}\geq 2\,d\,\hbar.

However, some inequalities allow us to compare them to true distances up to terms that vanish when
\hbar
is small. To present them, let us first introduce the usual correspondence between operators and functions of the phase space. To any sufficiently nice function
f
of the phase space, one can associate an operator called its Weyl quantization by the formula

\boldsymbol{\rho}_{f}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\widehat{f}(y,\xi)\,e^{2i\pi\,z\cdot\mathbf{z}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}w,

with
z=(y,\xi)
and
\mathbf{z}=(x,\boldsymbol{p})
, so that
z\cdot\mathbf{z}
is the operator
y\cdot x+\xi\cdot\boldsymbol{p}
where
x
identified with the operator of multiplication by
x
. Equivalently, this is the operator with integral kernel

\boldsymbol{\rho}_{f}(x,y)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-2i\pi\left(y-x\right)\cdot\xi}\,f(\tfrac{x+y}{2},h\xi)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi.

The inverse operation is called the Wigner transform and can be defined as follows. To any operator
\boldsymbol{\rho}
one associates the function

f_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}(x,\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-i\,y\cdot\xi/\hbar}\,\boldsymbol{\rho}(x+\tfrac{y}{2},x-\tfrac{y}{2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y

which satisfies
f_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{f}}=f
. These two formulas can more generally be understood in the sense of distributions. The Wigner transform satisfies
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}f_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}=h^{d}\tr\!\left(\boldsymbol{\rho}\right)
, and
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}f_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{1}}\,f_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2}}=h^{d}\tr\!\left(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{1}\,\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2}\right)
, and the transform of a positive operator is always real but might not be nonnegative. Hence, to work with probability distributions, one can look instead at the Husimi transform, which can be defined by

(4)

\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}=g_{h}*f_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\quad\text{ where }\quad g_{h}(z)=\left(2/h\right)^{d}e^{-\left|{z}\right|^{2}/\hbar}.

Similarly, the Weyl quantization of a nonnegative function is a self-adjoint operator but it might not be a positive operator, and so one can consider the Wick quantization or Toeplitz operator associated to
f
, which is the operator

(5)

\,\tilde{\!\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{f}:=\frac{1}{h^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}f(z)\ket{\psi_{z}}\bra{\psi_{z}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z

where
\psi_{z}(y)=\left(\pi\,\hbar\right)^{-\frac{d}{4}}e^{-\left|{y-x}\right|^{2}/(2\hbar)}\,e^{i\,\xi\cdot\left(y-\frac{x}{2}\right)/\hbar}
is such that
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{g_{h}}=h^{-d}\,\ket{\psi_{0}}\bra{\psi_{0}}
.

Thanks to the Husimi transform, the above pseudometrics can be bounded from below by the classical Wasserstein distance up to a term of order
\hbar
. More precisely, if
f\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
is a phase space density and
\boldsymbol{\rho},\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathfrak{h})
are density operators, the following bounds proved in
[
15
, Theorem 2.3]
and
[
16
, Theorem 2.4]
hold

(6)

\displaystyle\W_{2}(f,\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\W_{2,\hbar}(f,\boldsymbol{\rho})^{2}+d\,\hbar

(7)

\displaystyle\W_{2}(\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{1}},\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2}})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\W_{2,\hbar}(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{1},\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2})^{2}+2\,d\,\hbar.

One of the main result of this paper is the other direction, that is upper bounds for the pseudometrics. Let us first indicate that upper bounds are known in the particular case of operators that can be written as Toeplitz operators, that is
\boldsymbol{\rho}=\,\tilde{\!\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{g}
for some probability density
g\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
. Then, as proved again in
[
15
, Theorem 2.3]
and
[
16
, Theorem 2.4]
, it holds

\displaystyle\W_{2,\hbar}(f,\,\tilde{\!\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{g})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\W_{2}(f,g)^{2}+d\,\hbar

\displaystyle\W_{2,\hbar}(\,\tilde{\!\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{f},\,\tilde{\!\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{g})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\W_{2}(f,g)^{2}+2\,d\,\hbar.

Notice that it follows, in particular, from the above inequalities that we can deduce the "self-distances" of these operators

\W_{2,\hbar}(f,\,\tilde{\!\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{f})^{2}=d\,\hbar\quad\text{ and }\quad\W_{2,\hbar}(\,\tilde{\!\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{f},\,\tilde{\!\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{f})^{2}=2\,d\,\hbar.

In this case it is even better known (see
[
15
, Proof of Theorem 2.3]
and
[
18
, Corollary 3.4]
) that the optimal couplings are respectively

\boldsymbol{\gamma}(z)=\frac{1}{h^{d}}\,f(z)\,\ket{\psi_{z}}\bra{\psi_{z}}\quad\text{ and }\quad\boldsymbol{\gamma}(z)=\frac{1}{h^{2d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}f(z)\ket{\psi_{z}}\bra{\psi_{z}}\otimes\ket{\psi_{z}}\bra{\psi_{z}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z.

In
[
18
]
, an upper bound was found for more general states, thanks to two main ingredients: the analogue of the triangle inequalities and an upper bound for the "self-distance". The main triangle inequalities for the pseudometrics are the following.

Proposition 1.1

(Theorem 3.5,
[
18
]
, Theorem A,
[
19
]
)
.

If
(f,g,\boldsymbol{\rho})\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})^{2}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathfrak{h})
, then

(8)

\W_{2,\hbar}(f,\boldsymbol{\rho})\leq\W_{2}(f,g)+\W_{2,\hbar}(g,\boldsymbol{\rho}).

Proposition 1.2

(Theorem A,
[
19
]
)
.

If
(g,\boldsymbol{\rho},\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2})\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathfrak{h})^{2}
, then

(9)

\W_{2,\hbar}(\boldsymbol{\rho},\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2})\leq\W_{2,\hbar}(\boldsymbol{\rho},g)+\W_{2,\hbar}(g,\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2}).

From these and the previous inequalities one can then deduce approximate inequalities in the other cases, such as the case of three operators, see again
[
19

. In particular, from the first of these propositions, one deduces that

\W_{2,\hbar}(f,\boldsymbol{\rho})\leq\W_{2}(f,\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}})+\W_{2,\hbar}(\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}},\boldsymbol{\rho})

and the first term on the right-hand side is a true distance, but the size of the last term, which can also be thought of as a "self-distance", is unclear. A bound on this term was found in
[

]
by a weighted norm with
7

\boldsymbol{\rho}
. In this paper, we improve this bound by obtaining an estimate in terms of a norm involving only one derivative of this Wigner transform.

1.2.
The self-distance bound

One of our main results is the following, which controls the "self-distances" of an operator in the optimal transport pseudometrics.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathfrak{h})
. Then

(10)

\displaystyle\W_{2,\hbar}(\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}},\boldsymbol{\rho})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq d\,\hbar+\hbar^{2}\,\lVert\nabla f_{\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})}^{2}

(11)

\displaystyle\W_{2,\hbar}(\boldsymbol{\rho},\boldsymbol{\rho})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4\,d\,\hbar+4\,\hbar^{2}\,\lVert\nabla f_{\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})}^{2}.

Remark 1.1
.

In particular, for any operator that is smooth in the sense that
f_{\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}\in H^{1}
uniformly in
\hbar
, there exists a constant
C_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}>0
independent of
\hbar\in(0,1)
such that
\W_{2,\hbar}(\boldsymbol{\rho},\boldsymbol{\rho})\leq C_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\sqrt{\hbar}
and
\W_{2,\hbar}(\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}},\boldsymbol{\rho})\leq C_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\sqrt{\hbar}
. As discussed in the previous section, it follows from these results and inequalities (
6
) and (
9
) that the semiclassical optimal transport is comparable to the classical optimal transport in the sense that

\W_{2}(f,\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}})-\sqrt{d\,\hbar}\leq\W_{2,\hbar}(f,\boldsymbol{\rho})\leq\W_{2}(f,\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}})+C_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\sqrt{\hbar}.

One might notice that while Inequality (
10
) gives an upper bound of order
\hbar^{2}
to the positive quantity
\W_{2,\hbar}(\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}},\boldsymbol{\rho})^{2}-d\,\hbar
in the case when
f_{\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}\in H^{1}
uniformly in
\hbar
, this is not the case of Inequality (
11
) which only gives an upped bound of order
\hbar
to the positive quantity
\W_{2,\hbar}(\boldsymbol{\rho},\boldsymbol{\rho})^{2}-2\,d\,\hbar
. Interestingly, one can get a better order in
\hbar
by considering instead the distance between
\boldsymbol{\rho}
and its "semiclassical convolution by a Gaussian"
\,\tilde{\tilde{\!\boldsymbol{\rho}}}=\,\tilde{\!\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}
, thanks to the following inequality proved in Proposition
2.2
,

(12)

\W_{2,\hbar}(\,\tilde{\!\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{f},\boldsymbol{\rho})^{2}\leq\W_{2,\hbar}(f,\boldsymbol{\rho})^{2}+d\,\hbar,

which allows to compare the quantum and semiclassical pseudometrics. By
[
19
, Theorem 5.4]
, a similar inequality holds in the other direction in the form

(13)

\W_{2,\hbar}(\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{1}},\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2})^{2}\leq\W_{2,\hbar}(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{1},\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2})^{2}+d\,\hbar.

It gives the following result.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathfrak{h})
and define
\,\tilde{\tilde{\!\boldsymbol{\rho}}}:=\,\tilde{\!\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}
. Then it holds

(14)

\W_{2,\hbar}(\boldsymbol{\rho},\,\tilde{\tilde{\!\boldsymbol{\rho}}})^{2}\leq 2\,d\,\hbar+\hbar^{2}\,\lVert\nabla f_{\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})}^{2}.

Let us recall that the
L^{2}
norm of the Wigner transform is proportional to the Hilbert–Schmidt norm of its associated operator, and more precisely, one can introduce semiclassical Schatten norms

(15)

\left\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{p}}:=h^{\frac{d}{p}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right\|_{p}=h^{\frac{d}{p}}\tr\!\left(\left|{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right|^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}

so that
\left\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}=\left\|{f_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})}
. Another remark is the fact that the gradient of the Wigner transform corresponds to Wigner transforms of commutators, as given by the correspondence principle. One can introduce quantum gradients

(16)

\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!x}\boldsymbol{\rho}:=\left[{\nabla,\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right]\quad\text{ and }\quad\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!\xi}\boldsymbol{\rho}:=\left[{\frac{x}{i\hbar},\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right],

so that
f_{\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!x}\boldsymbol{\rho}}=\nabla_{x}f_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
and
f_{\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!\xi}\boldsymbol{\rho}}=\nabla_{\xi}f_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
. More generally, we will write
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\rho}:=(\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!x}\boldsymbol{\rho},\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!\xi}\boldsymbol{\rho})
, so that
\left|{\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right|^{2}=\left|{\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!x}\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right|^{2}+\left|{\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!\xi}\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right|^{2}
. Hence, one can for example rewrite Inequality (
10
) in terms of quantum Sobolev norms as follows

\displaystyle\W_{2,\hbar}(\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}},\boldsymbol{\rho})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq d\,\hbar+\hbar^{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\nabla}{\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}^{2}.

This can also be written

\W_{2,\hbar}(\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}},\boldsymbol{\rho})^{2}\leq d\,\hbar+\left\|{\left[{x,\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}\right]}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}^{2}+\left\|{\left[{\hbar\nabla,\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}\right]}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}^{2}.

The Hilbert–Schmidt norms of the commutator with the square root of an operator is known as the Wigner–Yanase skew information. More precisely, if we define

\mathcal{I}_{K}(\boldsymbol{\rho}):=\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\left[{K,\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}\right]}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}^{2}

then Inequality (
10
) can also be written

\W_{2,\hbar}(\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}},\boldsymbol{\rho})^{2}\leq d\,\hbar+2\,\mathcal{I}_{x}(\boldsymbol{\rho})+2\,\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{p}}(\boldsymbol{\rho}).

In the particular case of pure states of the form
\boldsymbol{\rho}=h^{-d}\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}
with
\psi
of norm
1
, the skew information is nothing but the variance of
K
for the state
\psi

\mathcal{I}_{K}(\boldsymbol{\rho})=\frac{1}{2}\tr\!\left(\left|{\left[{K,\ket{\psi}\!\bra{\psi}}\right]}\right|^{2}\right)=\left\langle|K-\left\langle K\right\rangle_{\psi}|^{2}\right\rangle_{\psi}=:\sigma_{K}(\psi)^{2}

where
\left\langle K\right\rangle_{\psi}=\Braket{\psi|K\psi}
. In particular, Inequality (
10
) is a generalization of
[
18
, Proposition 9.1]
which states that

(17)

\W_{2,\hbar}(\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}},\boldsymbol{\rho})^{2}\leq 2\,\sigma_{x}(\psi)^{2}+2\,\sigma_{\boldsymbol{p}}(\psi)^{2}+2\,d\,\hbar.

Another generalization of the above formula to the case of density operators is given in
[
18
]
by replacing
\left\langle A\right\rangle_{\psi}
by
\left\langle A\right\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}=h^{d}\tr\!\left(\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\,A\,\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right)
. This gives for example

\sigma_{x}(\boldsymbol{\rho})^{2}=h^{d}\tr\!\left(\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\,|x-\left\langle x\right\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\!|^{2}\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right)=\left\langle\left|{x}\right|^{2}\right\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}-\left\langle x\right\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{2}.

The quantity on the right-hand side of Formula (
17
) will only converge to
0
in the semiclassical limit
\hbar\to 0
in the case of concentrating solutions, that is if the Wigner transform
f_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
converges to a Dirac delta in phase space. In general, by the generalized Heisenberg inequality,
\sigma_{x}(\boldsymbol{\rho})^{2}+\sigma_{\boldsymbol{p}}(\boldsymbol{\rho})^{2}\geq 2\,\sigma_{x}(\boldsymbol{\rho})\,\sigma_{\boldsymbol{p}}(\boldsymbol{\rho})\geq d\,\hbar
. On the contrary, Inequality (
10
) applies to a large category of operators. For
K
self-adjoint, the quantity
\mathcal{I}_{K}(\boldsymbol{\rho})
is indeed smaller than the variance of
K
as follows by writing
\mathcal{I}_{K}(\boldsymbol{\rho})=h^{d}\tr\!\left(\boldsymbol{\rho}\,K^{2}\right)-h^{d}\tr\!\left(\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\rho}}K\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\rho}}K\right)
and
\sigma_{K}(\boldsymbol{\rho})^{2}=h^{d}\tr\!\left(\boldsymbol{\rho}\,K^{2}\right)-h^{2d}\tr\!\left(\boldsymbol{\rho}\,K\right)^{2}
and noticing that by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality

\tr\!\left(\boldsymbol{\rho}\,K\right)^{2}=\tr\!\left(\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\,\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\frac{1}{4}}\,K\,\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\frac{1}{4}}\right)^{2}\leq\tr\!\left(\boldsymbol{\rho}\right)\tr\!\left(\left|{\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\frac{1}{4}}\,K\,\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\frac{1}{4}}}\right|^{2}\right)=\frac{\tr\!\left(\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\rho}}K\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\rho}}K\right)}{h^{d}}.

An analogue of the Heisenberg inequality for the Skew information is the quantum Sobolev inequality proved in
[
23
]
which states that there exists a constant
C_{d}
depending only on the dimension
d
such that

2\,\sqrt{\mathcal{I}_{x}(\boldsymbol{\rho})\,\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{p}}(\boldsymbol{\rho})}=\hbar^{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!x}\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!\xi}\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}\geq C_{d}\,\hbar^{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{\frac{d}{d-1}}}.

The power
\hbar^{2}
appearing on the left-hand side of the above formula is sharp, showing that the skew information can indeed be much smaller than the variance in general.

\displaystyle\left[{\left|{\boldsymbol{p}}\right|^{2},\boldsymbol{\mu}}\right]

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{p}\cdot\left[{\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{\mu}}\right]+\left[{\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{\mu}}\right]\cdot\boldsymbol{p}=i\hbar\left(\boldsymbol{p}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!x}\boldsymbol{\mu}+\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!x}\boldsymbol{\mu}\cdot\boldsymbol{p}\right)

and so Equation (
18
) can be interpreted as an estimate by a weighted analogue of the
W^{1,\infty}
norm. It implies for example

\W_{2,\hbar}(\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}},\boldsymbol{\rho})^{2}\leq C\,\hbar\,\left\|{\boldsymbol{m}\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}}\left\|{\left\langle\mathbf{z}\right\rangle\boldsymbol{\nabla}(\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\,\boldsymbol{m})}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}}

with
\left\langle\mathbf{z}\right\rangle^{2}=1+\left|{x}\right|^{2}+\left|{\boldsymbol{p}}\right|^{2}
. By
[
24
]
, this kind of bound is however not compatible with projection operators, at the contrary of Theorem
1.1
, as will be proved in Section
1.3.2
. Theorem
1.1
also improves the above bound as it gives a correction of order
\hbar^{2}
to the positive quantity
\W_{2,\hbar}(\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}},\boldsymbol{\rho})^{2}-d\,\hbar
in the case of smooth operators.

1.3.
Application: thermal states, spectral projections and Toeplitz operators.

In this section, estimates on the "self-distance" for several particular families of operators are obtained.

1.3.1.
Thermal states

Since
\W_{2,\hbar}(\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}},\boldsymbol{\rho})^{2}\geq d\,\hbar
, Inequality (
10
) has sharp first order term in
\hbar
for nice operators such that
\|\boldsymbol{\nabla}{\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}
is bounded uniformly in
\hbar
. A physically important family of operators for which this is the case are the thermal states. They are states of the form

(19)

\boldsymbol{\rho}=Z_{\beta}^{-1}\,e^{-\beta\,H}

with an Hamiltonian given by

(20)

H=-\hbar^{2}\Delta+V

where the potential
V
is the operator of multiplication by the function
x\mapsto V(x)
. The normalization constant
Z_{\beta}
is the usual partition function
Z_{\beta}=h^{d}\tr\!\left(e^{-\beta\,H}\right)
. We will assume that
V
converges to
+\infty
at infinity, and more precisely that there exist some positive constants
a
,
b
,
\kappa
and
\kappa_{2}
such that for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

(21)

\displaystyle V(x)

\displaystyle\geq\kappa\left|{x}\right|^{a}

(22)

\displaystyle\left|{\nabla V(x)}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\kappa_{2}\left|{x}\right|^{b}.

It gives the following result.

Proposition 1.3
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\rho}
be of the form (
19
) with
V
verifying (
21
) and (
22
). Then it holds

\W_{2,\hbar}(\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}},\boldsymbol{\rho})^{2}\leq d\,\hbar+C\,\hbar^{2}

where
C\leq C_{d,a,b}\,Z_{\beta}^{-1}\,\kappa^{-d/a}\,\beta^{1-d\left(\frac{1}{a}+\frac{1}{2}\right)}\left(1+\kappa_{2}\,\beta\,\max\left((\kappa\hbar)^{2b/(1+a)},(\kappa\beta)^{-b/a}\right)\right)
for some constant
C_{d,a,b}
depending only on
d
,
a
and
b
. In particular,
C
is uniformly bounded in
\hbar\in(0,1)
.

The semiclassical smoothness of other type of thermal states such as the Fermi–Dirac equilibrium is also considered in
[
8
]
, which leads to the same bound with another constant
C
.

1.3.2.
Spectral projections

Another important case of application of Inequality (
10
) is the case of projection operators such as spectral functions of Schrödinger operators. These operators correspond for example to the one-particle density operators associated to Slater determinants. More precisely, we consider operators of the form

(23)

\boldsymbol{\rho}=Z_{0}^{-1}\,\mathds{1}_{(-\infty,0]}(-\hbar^{2}\Delta+V(x))

where
V
is such that there exists
\varepsilon>0
and open sets
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
bounded and
\Omega
verifying
\overline{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}\subset\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

(24)

\left\{\ \begin{aligned} &V\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)\cap L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega^{c})\\
&V\geq\varepsilon\text{ on }\Omega_{\varepsilon}^{c},\end{aligned}\right.

and
Z_{0}=h^{d}\tr\!\left(\mathds{1}_{(-\infty,0]}(-\hbar^{2}\Delta+V(x))\right)
. This normalization constant is bounded uniformly in
\hbar
by the Cwikel–Lieb–Rozenblum inequality
[
9
]
. By the Weyl law, it converges when
\hbar\to 0
to
\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\mathds{1}_{\left|{\xi}\right|^{2}+V(x)\leq 0}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi=\frac{\omega_{d}}{d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}V_{-}^{d/2}
where
\omega_{d}
is the size of the unit sphere of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
V_{-}=\max(0,-V)
is the negative part of
V
.

As proved in
[
24
]
, such operators are not as smooth as thermal states as they converge to characteristic functions of the phase space in the limit
\hbar\to 0
. Inequality (
10
) is however still sufficiently strong to give the following result.

Proposition 1.4
.

Assume that
\boldsymbol{\rho}
is of the form (
23
) with
V
satisfying Hypothesis (
24
). Then there exists a constant
C>0
independent of
\hbar
such that

\W_{2,\hbar}(\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}},\boldsymbol{\rho})\leq C\,\sqrt{\hbar}.

When
V
is less regular however, it is expected that there might be cases where
\sqrt{\hbar}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\nabla}{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}
does not converge to
0
(see
[
12
,
24
]
). It is not clear however that there are potentials such that the above proposition is false.

1.3.3.
Powers of Toeplitz operators

Another particular case is when the operator can be written as a suitably normalized even power of a Wick (Toeplitz) quantization. In this case, no derivative is needed on the symbol of the operator. Moreover, in the case of additional regularity of the symbol, one would obtain a result similar to the one of thermal states with a sharp constant for the first order term in
\hbar
. This generalizes the case of Toeplitz operators proved in
[
15
]
.

Proposition 1.5
.

Let
f\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})\cap L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})\setminus\set{0}
and
n
be an even nonzero integer, and define

\boldsymbol{\rho}:=C_{f}\,\,\tilde{\!\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{f}^{n},

where
C_{f}=\lVert\,\tilde{\!\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{f}\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{n}}^{-n}
is such that
h^{d}\tr\!\left(\boldsymbol{\rho}\right)=1
. Then, the following inequality holds

(25)

\displaystyle\W_{2,\hbar}(\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}},\boldsymbol{\rho})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(d+\tfrac{n^{2}}{2\,e}\,C_{f}\left\|{f}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})}^{n-2}\left\|{f}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})}^{2}\right)\hbar

where the normalization factor
C_{f}
is bounded uniformly in
\hbar\in(0,1)
and satisfies

(26)

C_{f}\leq\left\|{f}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})}^{n-2}\left\|{g_{1}*f}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})}^{2\left(1-n\right)}.

Remark 1.2
.

When
n=2
, then it is sufficient to have
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})\setminus\set{0}
, and the above result yields

\W_{2,\hbar}(\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}},\boldsymbol{\rho})^{2}<\left(d+C_{f}\left\|{f}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})}^{2}\right)\,\hbar.

with
C_{f}\leq\left\|{g_{1}*f}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})}^{-2}
.

1.4.
Quantum optimal transport and Sobolev spaces

In this section, we compare the quantum optimal transport distances with the quantum Sobolev norms. We recall the definition of these quantum norms, as defined in
[
23
]
. Let us first define the phase space translation operators by

\mathsf{T}_{z}\boldsymbol{\rho}:=\tau_{z}\,\boldsymbol{\rho}\,\tau_{-z}

where
\tau_{z}
is the operator defined for
z_{0}=(x_{0},\xi_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
and for any
\varphi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
by
\tau_{z_{0}}\varphi(x)=e^{-i\,\xi_{0}\cdot\left(x-\frac{x_{0}}{2}\right)/\hbar}\,\varphi(x-x_{0})
. They satisfy
\mathsf{T}_{z+z^{\prime}}=\mathsf{T}_{z}\mathsf{T}_{z^{\prime}}
and
f_{\mathsf{T}_{z}\boldsymbol{\rho}}(z^{\prime})=f_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}(z^{\prime}-z)
. Then, for
p\in[1,\infty]
and
s\in\mathbb{R}
we define for any compact operator
\boldsymbol{\rho}
its quantum Sobolev norm of order
s
by

\displaystyle\left\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right\|_{\dot{\mathcal{W}}^{s,p}}

\displaystyle=\left\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{p}}

\displaystyle\text{ if }s=0

\displaystyle\left\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right\|_{\dot{\mathcal{W}}^{s,p}}

\displaystyle=\left\|{\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{p}}

\displaystyle\text{ if }s=1

\displaystyle\left\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right\|_{\dot{\mathcal{W}}^{s,p}}

\displaystyle=c_{s,p}\left\|{\frac{\left\|{\mathsf{T}_{z}\boldsymbol{\rho}-\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{p}}}{\left|{z}\right|^{s+2d/p}}}\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})}

\displaystyle\text{ if }s\in(0,1)

\displaystyle\left\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right\|_{\dot{\mathcal{W}}^{s,p}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\left\|{B}\right\|_{\dot{\mathcal{W}}^{-s,p^{\prime}}}\leq 1}h^{d}\tr\!\left(\boldsymbol{\rho}B\right)

\displaystyle\text{ if }s<0

where
c_{s,p}=1
if
p=\infty
and
c_{s,p}^{p}=\frac{p\,\left|{\omega_{-2s}}\right|}{4\,\omega_{2d+sp}}\left(\frac{\pi\,\omega_{p+1}}{s^{(p-2)/2}}\right)^{s}
with
\omega_{d}=|\mathds{S}^{d-1}|=\frac{2\,\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2)}
in the other cases, and where
p^{\prime}=\frac{p}{p-1}
denotes the Hölder conjugate
2
2

2

With
p^{\prime}=\infty
when
p=1
.
. The choice of the constant
c_{s,p}
implies in particular that

\left\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right\|_{\dot{\mathcal{H}}^{s}}:=\left\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right\|_{\dot{\mathcal{W}}^{s,2}}=\left\|{f_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}\right\|_{\dot{H^{s}}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})}=\lVert(-\Delta)^{s/2}f_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})}.

We start by a proposition giving an upper bound to the quantum optimal transport cost in terms of quantum Sobolev norms with
p=2
. This is a quantitative analogue of the fact that bounded moments and weak convergence of measures implies convergence in Wasserstein distances (see e.g.
[
27
, Theorem 5.11]
).

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
n>2
and
(f,\boldsymbol{\rho},\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2})\in\mathscr{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})\times\mathcal{P}_{n}(\mathfrak{h})^{2}
uniformly in
\hbar
. Then there exists constants
C=C_{n,f,\boldsymbol{\rho}}
and
C_{2}=C_{n,\boldsymbol{\rho},\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2}}
independent of
\hbar\in(0,1)
such that

\displaystyle\W_{2,\hbar}(f,\boldsymbol{\rho})

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|{f-\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}\right\|_{\dot{W}^{-1,1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})}^{\theta}+\sqrt{d\hbar}+D_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\,\hbar

\displaystyle\W_{2,\hbar}(\boldsymbol{\rho},\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2})

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}-\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2}}\right\|_{\dot{\mathcal{W}}^{-1,1}}^{\theta}+2\,\sqrt{d\hbar}+\left(D_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{1}}+D_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2}}\right)\hbar,

where
D_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}=\|\boldsymbol{\nabla}{\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}
and
\theta=\frac{n-2}{2\left(n-1\right)}
.

The quantity
D_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
is the quantity discussed in Section
1.2
, and so is for example uniformly bounded with respect to
\hbar
for operators with uniformly-in-
\hbar
smooth symbols. The difference
\lVert f-\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\rVert_{\dot{W}^{-1,1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})}
can be bounded both by a quantum or a classical Sobolev norm. More precisely, it holds

\displaystyle\left\|{f-\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}\right\|_{\dot{W}^{-1,1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|{f-f_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}\right\|_{\dot{W}^{-1,1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})}+c_{d}\sqrt{\hbar}

\displaystyle\left\|{f-\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}\right\|_{\dot{W}^{-1,1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}-\boldsymbol{\rho}_{f}}\right\|_{\dot{\mathcal{W}}^{-1,1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})}+c_{d}\sqrt{\hbar},

where
c_{d}=\frac{\Gamma(d+1/2)}{\Gamma(d)}<\sqrt{d}
, as follows by using the fact that
\lVert f-g_{h}*f\rVert_{\dot{W}^{-1,1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})}\leq c_{d}\sqrt{\hbar}\left\|{f}\right\|_{L^{1}}
and the triangle inequality.

In the other direction, it is also possible to bound by below the quantum optimal transport cost by negative order Sobolev norms, as was already proved in
[
14
,
18
]
. More precisely, it is proved in
[
14
, Proposition B.5]
that

h^{d}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}-\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2}}\right\|_{\mathcal{W}^{-2-\left\lfloor{d/2}\right\rfloor,\infty}}\leq\W_{2,\hbar}(\boldsymbol{\rho},\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2})+C_{d}\,\sqrt{\hbar}

for some constant
C_{d}
depending only on the dimension. The left-hand side of the inequality can however be not very well behaved in terms of the Planck constant because of the
h^{d}
appearing in front. In
[
18
, Proposition B.3]
, it is proved that

\lVert f_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}-f_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2}}\rVert_{(\mathrm{Lip}\cap H^{1})^{\prime}}\leq\W_{2,\hbar}(\boldsymbol{\rho},\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2})+\sqrt{\hbar}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}-\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}.

where
\left\|{f}\right\|_{(\mathrm{Lip}\cap H^{1})^{\prime}}=\sup\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}f\,\varphi
with the supremum taken over all
\varphi
such that
\left\|{\varphi}\right\|_{W^{1,\infty}}\leq 1
and
\left\|{\varphi}\right\|_{H^{1}}\leq 1
. As noticed in
[
18
, Proposition B.2]
, the norm appearing on the left-hand side of the inequality can be compared to quantum Sobolev norms of operators by the inequality

(h/2)^{d}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}-\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2}}\right\|_{\mathcal{W}^{-1,\infty}}\leq\lVert f_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}-f_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2}}\rVert_{(\mathrm{Lip}\cap H^{1})^{\prime}}\,.

However, this makes again appear a positive power of the Planck constant on the left-hand side. We propose here as another alternative the following quantum analogue of the classical Loeper inequality
[
25
]
.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
d>1
and
\left(f,\boldsymbol{\rho},\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2}\right)\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})\otimes\mathcal{P}(\mathfrak{h})^{2}
. Then for any
s\in[-1,1]
,

\displaystyle\left\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}-\boldsymbol{\rho}_{f}}\right\|_{\dot{\mathcal{H}}^{-1}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\infty}^{1/2}\left(\W_{2,\hbar}(f,\boldsymbol{\rho})^{2}+d\,\hbar\right)^{1/2}+\left(\tfrac{\hbar}{4}\right)^{\frac{s+1}{2}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right\|_{\dot{\mathcal{H}}^{s}}

\displaystyle\left\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}-\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2}}\right\|_{\dot{\mathcal{H}}^{-1}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{C}_{\infty}^{1/2}\left(\W_{2,\hbar}(\boldsymbol{\rho},\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2})^{2}+2\,d\,\hbar\right)^{1/2}+\left(\tfrac{\hbar}{4}\right)^{\frac{s+1}{2}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}-\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2}}\right\|_{\dot{\mathcal{H}}^{s}}

where
C_{\infty}=\max\!\left(\left\|{f}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})},\left\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}}\right)
in the first equation, and where
\mathcal{C}_{\infty}=\max\!\left(\left\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}},\left\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}}\right)
in the second equation.

Notice that the left-hand side of the above inequalities could also be written in terms of the classical
\dot{H}^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
norm of the Wigner transform since
\left\|{f_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})}=\left\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}
, so that, for example,
\lVert\boldsymbol{\rho}-\boldsymbol{\rho}_{f}\rVert_{\dot{\mathcal{H}}^{-1}}=\left\|{f_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}-f}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})}
. These inequalities are of course particularly interesting when
\mathcal{C}_{\infty}
is bounded uniformly with respect to
\hbar
, which is the case for most of the previously discussed examples and is often relevant in the case of fermions due to the Pauli principle (see e.g.
[
7
]
). The remainder term on the right-hand side is smaller when
\boldsymbol{\rho}-\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2}\in\mathcal{H}^{s}
with
s
larger. In the particular case when
s=0
, one obtains the following simplified inequality.

Corollary 1.1
.

Let
d>1
and
\left(\boldsymbol{\rho},\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2}\right)\in\mathcal{P}(\mathfrak{h})^{2}
satisfy
0\leq\boldsymbol{\rho},\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2}\leq 1
. Then it holds

\displaystyle\left\|{f-f_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})}

\displaystyle\leq\W_{2,\hbar}(f,\boldsymbol{\rho})+\left(\tfrac{1}{2}+\sqrt{d}\right)\sqrt{\hbar}

\displaystyle\left\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}-\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2}}\right\|_{\dot{\mathcal{H}}^{-1}}

\displaystyle\leq\W_{2,\hbar}(\boldsymbol{\rho},\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2})+\left(1+\sqrt{2d}\right)\sqrt{\hbar}.

1.5.
Application to the mean-field and semiclassical limits

One of the main applications of the quantum optimal transport pseudometrics described above is that they allow to get quantitative bounds on the errors made when using approximate equations in the case of a large number of particles or in the context of a semiclassical approximation
[
15
,
16
,
21
,
22
,
26
]
. This is in particular useful in the context of numerical schemes
[
14
]
. As an example of application of the above bounds, we consider the Hartree equation

(27)

i\hbar\,\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\rho}=\left[{H_{\boldsymbol{\rho}},\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right]

with Hamiltonian
H_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}=\frac{-\hbar^{2}\Delta}{2}+V_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}(x)
where the mean-field potential
V_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
is defined by
V_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}(x)=K*\rho(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K(x-y)\,\rho(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y
with
\rho(x)=h^{d}\,\boldsymbol{\rho}(x,x)
, and the Vlasov equation

(28)

\partial_{t}f+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f+E\cdot\nabla_{x}f=0

where
E=-\nabla V_{f}=-\nabla K*\rho_{f}
with
\rho_{f}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x,\xi)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi
. Then the following result follows from the above bounds and
[
16
, Theorem 2.5]
.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
K
be an even bounded function of class
C^{1,1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then for any solution
f
of the Vlasov equation with initial data
f^{\mathrm{in}}\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
and for any solution
\boldsymbol{\rho}
of the Hartree equation with initial data
\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\mathrm{in}}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathfrak{h})
, it holds

W_{2}(f,\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}})^{2}\leq e^{\lambda\,t}\left(W_{2}(f^{\mathrm{in}},\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\mathrm{in}}})^{2}+2\,d\,\hbar+D_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{2}\,\hbar^{2}\right)

where
D_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}=\|\boldsymbol{\nabla}{\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\mathrm{in}}}}\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}
and
\lambda=1+\max(1,4\left\|{\nabla K}\right\|_{W^{1,\infty}}^{2})
.

When
f^{\mathrm{in}}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
and
\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\mathrm{in}}\in\mathcal{L}^{\infty}
both have moments higher than
2
bounded, then one can express the above result in terms of negative Sobolev norms and of the Wigner transform.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
n>2
and
K
be an even bounded function of class
C^{1,1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then for any solution
f
of the Vlasov equation with initial data
f^{\mathrm{in}}\in\mathscr{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
satisfying
f^{\mathrm{in}}\leq 1
and for any solution
\boldsymbol{\rho}
of the Hartree equation with initial data
\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\mathrm{in}}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}(\mathfrak{h})
uniformly in
\hbar
satisfying
\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\mathrm{in}}\leq 1
, there exists a constant
C
independent of
\hbar
such that

\left\|{f-f_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})}\leq e^{\lambda\,t}\left(C\left\|{f^{\mathrm{in}}-\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\mathrm{in}}}}\right\|_{\dot{W}^{-1,1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})}^{\theta}+3\sqrt{d\hbar}+D_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\,\hbar\right)

where
D_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}=\|\boldsymbol{\nabla}{\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\mathrm{in}}}}\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}
,
\theta=\frac{n-2}{2\left(n-1\right)}
,
\lambda=1+2\left\|{\nabla K}\right\|_{W^{1,\infty}}^{2}
, and
C
depends only on
n
,
d
and the moments of the densities at initial time.

One can similarly modify the results of
[
15
,
16
,
21
,

,

]
to get results with more singular potentials, magnetic fields and results for the mean-field limit.

2.
Proof of the "self-distance" bounds

2.1.
Classical case

Let us first comment on the strategy by giving a simple proof that can be thought of as the analogous proof in the classical setting. The idea is to replace
\W_{2,\hbar}(\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}},\boldsymbol{\rho})
by the classical quantity
W_{2}(g_{h}*f,f)
with
g_{h}
given by Equation (
4
) and
f\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
some probability density on the phase space. It is indeed not difficult to find an upper bound to this latter quantity. Indeed, for any
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
,

\W_{2}(g_{h}(\cdot-z),\delta_{z})^{2}=d\,\hbar

with optimal transport plan
\gamma_{z}(z_{1},z_{2})=g_{h}(z_{1}-z)\,\delta_{z}(z_{2})
. This is a measure of how close a Gaussian function and a Dirac measure are. More generally, since
f(z)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}f\,\delta_{z}
can be seen as a convex combination of Dirac measures and
g_{h}*f=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}f\,g_{h}(\cdot-z)
can be seen as a convex combination of Gaussian functions, we can look at the coupling obtained by convex combination under the form

\gamma(z_{1},z_{2})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}f(z)\,\gamma_{z}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}f(z)\,g_{h}(z_{1}-z)\,\delta_{z}(z_{2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z=f(z_{1})\,g_{h}(z_{1}-z_{2}).

This is indeed a coupling of
f
and
g_{h}*f
. It gives

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4d}}\left|{z_{1}-z_{2}}\right|^{2}\gamma(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z_{1},\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z_{2})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}f*(\left|{z}\right|^{2}g_{h})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\left|{z}\right|^{2}g_{h}(z)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z=d\hbar,

from which we deduce that

W_{2}(g_{h}*f,f)^{2}\leq d\,\hbar.

The proof in the quantum case will be a non-commutative analogue of the above proof. It requires the control of additional error terms due to the non-commutativity.

2.2.
Quantum case

In this section, we prove the first inequality of Theorem
1.1
, which is the main novelty of this paper and follows immediately from the next proposition.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathfrak{h})
. Then

(29)

\W_{2,\hbar}(\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}},\boldsymbol{\rho})\leq\sqrt{d\hbar+\hbar^{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\nabla}{\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}^{2}}.

Remark 2.1
.

More generally, one could replace
\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
by any function of the form
f=g*f_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
where
g
is a (possibly
\hbar
dependent) non-negative function which is the Weyl quantization of a positive operator, and is such that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}z\,g(z)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z=0
. Then
d\hbar
has to be replaced by
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\left|{z}\right|^{2}g(z)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z
.

Remark 2.2
.

Notice that for any
s\in[0,1]
,

\left\|{\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!\xi}\boldsymbol{\mu}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}^{2}=\frac{h^{d}}{\hbar^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\left|{x-y}\right|^{2}\left|{\boldsymbol{\rho}(x,y)}\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\\
\leq\frac{C_{s}\,h^{d}}{\hbar^{2\left(1-s\right)}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\left|{x}\right|^{2(1-s)}\left|{\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\xi}^{s/2}\boldsymbol{\mu}(x,y)}\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y

where
C_{s}=2^{1+(1-2s)_{+}}
, and similarly by exchanging
x
and
\xi
. Hence

Lemma 2.1

Let

Proof.

The convolution of Wigner transforms can be seen as a function-valued convolution of operators by writing

to define a function-valued convolution of two operators. Formula (

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\left|{f_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}\right|

where we used the convexity of

To finish the proof, replace

h^{d}\tr_{1}\!\left(\left|{x_{1}-x_{2}}\right|^{2}\mathsf{T}_{z}\boldsymbol{\rho}_{g_{h}}\right)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\left|{x_{1}-x_{2}}\right|^{2}g_{h}(z_{1}-z)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z_{1}=\left|{x-x_{2}}\right|^{2}+\frac{d\hbar}{2}.

By symmetry, an analogous result holds by replacing the operators
x_{k}
by
\boldsymbol{p}_{k}
and
x
by
\xi
, and so one obtains finally for any
z=(x,\xi)\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
,

h^{d}\tr_{1}\!\left(\left|{\mathbf{z}_{1}-\mathbf{z}_{2}}\right|^{2}\mathsf{T}_{z}\boldsymbol{\rho}_{g_{h}}\right)=\left|{z-\mathbf{z}_{2}}\right|^{2}+d\hbar.

Using the definition (
3
) of the quantum pseudometric and inserting the above identity in Formula (

\W_{2,\hbar}(\,\tilde{\!\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{f},\boldsymbol{\rho})^{2}\leq h^{2d}\tr\!\left(\left|{\mathbf{z}_{1}-\mathbf{z}_{2}}\right|^{2}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{2}\right)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}h^{d}\tr\!\left(\left|{z-\mathbf{z}_{2}}\right|^{2}\boldsymbol{\gamma}(z)\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z

where
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
and the trace in the second formula act on functions of the variable

. The result then follows by taking the supremum over all couplings

∎

Proof of Theorem

3.
Comparison with negative order Sobolev norms

3.1.
The upper bound

In this section we prove Theorem
1.3
which provides an upper bound on the quantum optimal transport pseudometrics in terms of classical or quantum Sobolev norms. A more detailed version of the theorem is presented in the following proposition.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
n>2
and
(\boldsymbol{\rho},\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2})\in\mathcal{P}_{n}(\mathfrak{h})^{2}
uniformly with respect to
\hbar
. Then there exists a constant
C_{\boldsymbol{\rho},\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2}}
independent of
\hbar\in(0,1)
such that

\displaystyle\W_{2,\hbar}(f,\boldsymbol{\rho})

\displaystyle\leq C_{f,\boldsymbol{\rho}}\left\|{f-\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}\right\|_{\dot{W}^{-1,1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})}^{\frac{n-2}{2\left(n-1\right)}}+C_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\,\sqrt{\hbar}

\displaystyle\W_{2,\hbar}(\boldsymbol{\rho},\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2})

\displaystyle\leq C_{\boldsymbol{\rho},\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}-\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2}}\right\|_{\dot{\mathcal{W}}^{-1,1}}^{\frac{n-2}{2\left(n-1\right)}}+\left(C_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}+C_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2}}\right)\sqrt{\hbar}.

The following bounds hold

\displaystyle C_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{d+\hbar\left\|{\boldsymbol{\nabla}{\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}^{2}}

\displaystyle C_{\boldsymbol{\rho},\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n-1}{\left(n-2\right)^{\frac{n-2}{n-1}}}\left(P(\boldsymbol{\rho})+P(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2})\right)^{\frac{n}{2n-2}}

with

P(\boldsymbol{\rho})^{n}=3^{n-2}h^{d}\tr\!\left(\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\left(\left|{x}\right|^{n}+\left|{\boldsymbol{p}}\right|^{n}+\left((d+n-2)\hbar\right)^{n/2}\right)\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right).

We first begin with the following lemma on classical Wasserstein distances, which is just a quantitative estimate of the fact that weak convergence of measures implies convergence in the
\W_{2}
distance when sufficiently many moments are bounded.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
n>2
, then for any probability measures
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
on
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
, it holds

\W_{2}(f_{1},f_{2})\leq C_{n}\left(Z_{n}(f_{1})+Z_{n}(f_{2})\right)^{\theta}W_{1}(f_{1},f_{2})^{1-\theta}

where
Z_{n}(f)^{n}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\left|{z}\right|^{n}f(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z)
,
\theta=\frac{n}{2\left(n-1\right)}
and
C_{n}=\left(n-1\right)\left(n-2\right)^{\frac{2-n}{n-1}}
. In particular, it follows from the Kantorovich–Rubinstein theorem that

\W_{2}(f_{1},f_{2})\leq C_{n}\left(Z_{n}(f_{1})+Z_{n}(f_{2})\right)^{\theta}\left\|{f_{1}-f_{2}}\right\|_{\dot{W}^{-1,1}}^{1-\theta}.

Proof.

Let
\gamma
be the optimal transport associated to
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
, so that

\W_{2}(f_{1},f_{2})^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4d}}\left|{z_{1}-z_{2}}\right|^{2}\gamma(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z_{12}),

where
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z_{12}=\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z_{1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z_{2}
. Then for any
R>0
, by the triangle inequality

\displaystyle\W_{2}(f_{1},f_{2})

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int_{\left|{z_{1}-z_{2}}\right|\leq R}\left|{z_{1}-z_{2}}\right|^{2}\gamma(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z_{12})\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\left(\int_{\left|{z_{1}-z_{2}}\right|\geq R}\left|{z_{1}-z_{2}}\right|^{2}\gamma(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z_{12})\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{R}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4d}}\left|{z_{1}-z_{2}}\right|\gamma(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z_{12})\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+R^{\frac{2-n}{2}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4d}}\left|{z_{1}-z_{2}}\right|^{n}\gamma(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z_{12})\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

Using the definition of the
W_{1}
distance to control the first term and the triangle inequality to get a bound on the second term, we deduce that for any
r=\sqrt{R}>0
,

\displaystyle\W_{2}(f_{1},f_{2})

\displaystyle\leq r\,W_{1}(f_{1},f_{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}+r^{2-n}\left(\left\|{f_{1}\left|{z}\right|^{n}}\right\|_{L^{1}}^{\frac{1}{n}}+\left\|{f_{2}\left|{z}\right|^{n}}\right\|_{L^{1}}^{\frac{1}{n}}\right)^{\frac{n}{2}}

where we used the fact that
\gamma
is a coupling of
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
. The result then follows by optimizing with respect to
r
.
∎

We now use the above lemma to get its quantum analogue.

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

Notice that for any
\varphi\in\dot{W}^{1,\infty}
, since the Wick quantization is non-expansive from
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
to
\mathcal{L}^{\infty}

\left\|{\,\tilde{\!\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{\varphi}}\right\|_{\dot{\mathcal{W}}^{1,\infty}}=\left\|{\boldsymbol{\nabla}\,\tilde{\!\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{\varphi}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}}=\left\|{\,\tilde{\!\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{\nabla\varphi}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}}\leq\left\|{\nabla\varphi}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}=\left\|{\varphi}\right\|_{\dot{W}^{1,\infty}}.

Hence, we deduce that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\,\varphi=h^{d}\tr\!\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}\,\,\tilde{\!\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{\varphi}\right)\leq\sup_{\left\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right\|_{\dot{\mathcal{W}}^{1,\infty}\leq 1}}h^{d}\tr\!\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}\,\boldsymbol{\rho}\right)=\left\|{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\right\|_{\dot{\mathcal{W}}^{-1,1}}

that is,
\left\|{\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}\right\|_{\dot{W}^{-1,1}}\leq\left\|{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\right\|_{\dot{\mathcal{W}}^{-1,1}}
. In particular, taking
\boldsymbol{\mu}=\boldsymbol{\rho}-\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2}
, it follows from the linearity of the Husimi transform that

(37)

\left\|{\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}-\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2}}}\right\|_{\dot{W}^{-1,1}}\leq\left\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}-\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2}}\right\|_{\dot{\mathcal{W}}^{-1,1}}.

On another side, notice that it follows from the positivity of
\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
and the triangle inequality that

(38)

Z_{n}(\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}})^{2}\leq\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\left|{x}\right|^{n}\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z)\right)^{\frac{2}{n}}+\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\left|{\xi}\right|^{n}\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z)\right)^{\frac{2}{n}}

where we use the notation
z=(x,\xi)
. Since
\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}=e^{\hbar\Delta_{x}/4}e^{\hbar\Delta_{\xi}/4}f_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
, defining
\rho(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi
, we see that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\left|{x}\right|^{n}\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|{x}\right|^{n}e^{\hbar\Delta/4}\rho(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x).

Defining
u(t,x)
the solution of
\partial_{t}u=\Delta u
with initial condition
u(0,x)=\rho(x)
, and
m_{n}(t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u(t,x)\left|{x}\right|^{n}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
, it follows by integration by parts that
\partial_{t}m_{n}=n\left(d+n-2\right)m_{n-2}
. By Hölder’s inequality, this yields

\partial_{t}m_{n}\leq n\left(d+n-2\right)m_{0}^{2/n}m_{n}^{1-2/n}

where
m_{0}=m_{0}(t)=m_{0}(0)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho
. It follows from Grönwall’s inequality that
m_{n}(t)^{2/n}\leq m_{n}(0)^{2/n}+\left(\frac{d+n}{2}-1\right)\hbar\,m_{0}^{2/n}
, which for
t=\hbar/4
yields

\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|{x}\right|^{n}e^{\hbar\Delta/4}\rho(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x)\right)^{\frac{2}{n}}\leq\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|{x}\right|^{n}\rho(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x)\right)^{\frac{2}{n}}+\left(\tfrac{d+n}{2}-1\right)\hbar\,m_{0}^{2/n}.

Recalling the well-known property that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|{x}\right|^{n}\rho(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x)=h^{d}\tr\!\left(\boldsymbol{\rho}\left|{x}\right|^{n}\right)
and performing the same analysis for the second term in the right-hand side of Inequality (
38
), one deduces

Z_{n}(\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}})^{2}\leq N_{n}(\boldsymbol{\rho})^{\frac{2}{n}}+M_{n}(\boldsymbol{\rho})^{\frac{2}{n}}+\left(d+n-2\right)\hbar\,m_{0}^{\frac{2}{n}}

where
N_{n}(\boldsymbol{\rho})=h^{d}\tr\!\left(\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\left|{x}\right|^{n}\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right)
and
M_{n}(\boldsymbol{\rho})=h^{d}\tr\!\left(\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\left|{\boldsymbol{p}}\right|^{n}\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right)
. We can now use Lemma
3.1
with
f_{1}=\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
and
f_{2}=\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2}}
to get

(39)

\W_{2}(\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}},\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2}})\leq C_{n}\left(Z_{n}(\boldsymbol{\rho})+Z_{n}(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2})\right)^{\theta}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}-\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2}}\right\|_{\dot{\mathcal{W}}^{-1,1}}^{1-\theta}

where
Z_{n}(\boldsymbol{\rho})^{2}=N_{n}(\boldsymbol{\rho})^{\frac{2}{n}}+M_{n}(\boldsymbol{\rho})^{\frac{2}{n}}+\left(d+n-2\right)\hbar\,m_{0}^{\frac{2}{n}}
. To finish the proof, it just remains to use the triangle inequalities (
9
) and (
8
) to get

\displaystyle\W_{2,\hbar}(\boldsymbol{\rho},\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2})

\displaystyle\leq\W_{2,\hbar}(\boldsymbol{\rho},\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}})+\W_{2,\hbar}(\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}},\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2})

\displaystyle\leq\W_{2,\hbar}(\boldsymbol{\rho},\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}})+\W_{2}(\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}},\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2}})+\W_{2,\hbar}(\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2}},\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2})

which by Theorem
1.1
and Inequality (
39
) yields the result.
∎

3.2.
The lower bound

In this section we prove Theorem
1.4
, which gives a lower bound on the quantum optimal transport pseudometrics in terms of quantum Sobolev norms. The next proposition proves the second inequality of this theorem, and the first inequality follows by a similar proof.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\rho},\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathfrak{h})
. Then for any
d>1
and
s\in[-1,1]
,

\left\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}-\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2}}\right\|_{\dot{\mathcal{H}}^{-1}}-\left(\tfrac{\hbar}{4}\right)^{\frac{s+1}{2}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}-\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2}}\right\|_{\dot{\mathcal{H}}^{s}}\leq\mathcal{C}_{\infty}^{1/2}\left(\W_{2,\hbar}(\boldsymbol{\rho},\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2})^{2}+2d\hbar\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

where
\mathcal{C}_{\infty}=\max\!\left(\left\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}},\left\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}}\right)
.

Proof.

We know by
[
25
]
that

\left\|{f-f_{2}}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{-1}}\leq\sqrt{\max(\left\|{f}\right\|_{L^{\infty}},\left\|{f_{2}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}})}\,\W_{2}(f,f_{2})

where
\dot{H}^{-1}=\dot{H}^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
. Applying this inequality to the Husimi transforms
f=\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
and
f_{2}=\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2}}
and using the well-known fact that
\lVert\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})}\leq\left\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}}
and Inequality (
7
) leads to the following inequality

\left\|{\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}-\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2}}}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{-1}}^{2}\leq\max\!\left(\left\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}},\left\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}}\right)\left(\W_{2,\hbar}(\boldsymbol{\rho},\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2})^{2}+2d\hbar\right).

Since the function
r\mapsto\frac{1-e^{-r}}{r^{\alpha}}
is smaller than
1
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
, it follows that for any
s\in[-1,1]
,

\left\|{\tilde{g}-g}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{-1}}=\left(\tfrac{\hbar}{4}\right)^{\frac{s+1}{2}}\left\|{\frac{1-e^{\hbar\Delta/4}}{(-\hbar\Delta/4)^{(s+1)/2}}(-\Delta)^{s/2}g}\right\|_{L^{2}}\leq\left(\tfrac{\hbar}{4}\right)^{\frac{s+1}{2}}\left\|{g}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}

we deduce that

\displaystyle\left\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}-\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2}}\right\|_{\dot{\mathcal{H}}^{-1}}

\displaystyle=\left\|{f_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}-f_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2}}}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{-1}}\leq\left\|{\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}-\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2}}}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{-1}}+\left\|{f_{\boldsymbol{\rho}-\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2}}-\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}-\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2}}}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{-1}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{C}_{\infty}^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\W_{2,\hbar}(\boldsymbol{\rho},\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2})^{2}+2d\hbar\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\left(\tfrac{\hbar}{4}\right)^{\frac{s+1}{2}}\left\|{f_{\boldsymbol{\rho}-\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2}}}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}

The result then follows from the fact that the Wigner transform is an isometry from
\dot{\mathcal{H}}^{s}
to
\dot{H}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
.
∎

Powers of Toeplitz operators

.

to

by

.
∎

4.3.
Estimates for thermal states

In this section, we prove Proposition
1.3
. As in the two previous sections, we start by computing the size of the moments and the commutators of thermal states and conclude using Theorem
1.1
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
H=-\hbar^{2}\Delta+V
with
V
satisfying Hypothesis (
21
). Then for any
n>0
and
\beta>0
,

(40)

\displaystyle\left\|{\left|{\boldsymbol{p}}\right|^{n}e^{-\beta\,H}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{a}\,n^{n}}{\kappa^{\frac{d}{a}}\,\beta^{n+d\left(\frac{1}{a}+\frac{1}{2}\right)}}

(41)

\displaystyle\left\|{\left|{x}\right|^{n}e^{-\beta\,H}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{a}\max\!\left((2n\kappa\hbar)^{\frac{2\,n}{1+a}},(\tfrac{2n}{\kappa a\beta})^{\frac{n}{a}}\right)}{\kappa^{\frac{d}{a}}\,\beta^{d\left(\frac{1}{a}+\frac{1}{2}\right)}}

where
C_{a}=\frac{2\,\Gamma(d/a)}{a\,\Gamma(d/2)}\,\pi^{d}
.

Proof.

Let
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\beta}=e^{-\beta\,H}
. Let
\psi=\psi(t,x)
be the solution of
\partial_{t}\psi=-H\psi
with
\psi(0,x)=\varphi(x)
, so that
\psi=e^{-tH}\varphi
, and let
y=\|\Delta^{n/2}\psi\|_{L^{2}}^{2/n}
with
\Delta^{s}=-\left(-\Delta\right)^{s}
. Then

\partial_{t}y^{n}=2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\Delta^{\frac{n}{2}}\psi\right)\left(\hbar^{2}\Delta-V\right)\!\left(\Delta^{\frac{n}{2}}\psi\right)=-2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\hbar^{2}\left|{\Delta^{\frac{n+1}{2}}\psi}\right|^{2}+\left|{\Delta^{\frac{n}{2}}\psi}\right|^{2}V.

By interpolation between Sobolev norms and using the fact that
c^{n/2}:=\left\|{\varphi}\right\|_{L^{2}}\geq\left\|{\psi}\right\|_{L^{2}}
, we obtain

\left\|{\Delta^{\frac{n}{2}}\psi}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\left\|{\Delta^{\frac{n+1}{2}}\psi}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{2\,n}{n+1}}\left\|{\psi}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{2}{n+1}}\leq\left\|{\Delta^{\frac{n+1}{2}}\psi}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{2\,n}{n+1}}c^{\frac{n}{n+1}}.

Since
V\geq 0
, we deduce that
\partial_{t}y^{n}\leq-2\,\hbar^{2}\,c^{-1}\,y^{n+1}
, or equivalently,

\partial_{t}y\leq-\frac{2\,\hbar^{2}}{n\,c}\,y^{2}.

This implies that

\left\|{\Delta^{n/2}e^{-tH}\varphi}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2/n}=y(t)\leq\frac{n\,c\,y(0)}{n\,c+2\,\hbar^{2}\,t\,y(0)}\leq\frac{n}{2\,\hbar^{2}\,t}\left\|{\varphi}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2/n}.

Recalling that
\left|{\boldsymbol{p}}\right|^{n}=\hbar^{n}\left(-\Delta\right)^{n/2}
and taking
t=\beta
and the supremum over all
\varphi\in L^{2}
, we get

(42)

\left\|{\left|{\boldsymbol{p}}\right|^{n}\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\beta}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}}\leq\left(\frac{n}{2\,\beta}\right)^{n/2}.

On the other hand, by the Golden–Thomson inequality (see e.g.
[
28
, Section 8.1]
)

\left\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\beta}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}^{2}=h^{d}\tr\!\left(e^{-2\beta H}\right)\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}e^{-2\beta\left(\left|{\xi}\right|^{2}+V(x)\right)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi

and so by Hypothesis (
21
),

(43)

\left\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\beta}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}^{2}\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}e^{-2\beta\left(\left|{\xi}\right|^{2}+\kappa\left|{x}\right|^{a}\right)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi=C_{a}\left(2\,\beta\right)^{-d\left(\frac{1}{a}+\frac{1}{2}\right)}\,\kappa^{-d/a}.

Combining Inequality (
43
) and Inequality (
42
) gives

\left\|{\left|{\boldsymbol{p}}\right|^{n}\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\beta}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}^{2}\leq\left\|{\left|{\boldsymbol{p}}\right|^{n}\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\beta/2}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}}^{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\beta/2}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}^{2}\leq C_{a}\,\kappa^{-\,\frac{d}{a}}\left(\frac{n}{\beta}\right)^{n}\left(\frac{1}{\beta}\right)^{d\left(\frac{1}{a}+\frac{1}{2}\right)}.

This gives Formula (
40
). Inequality (
41
) is obtained following the same strategy. By the same proof as in
[
8
, Lemma 3]
one obtains from Condition (
21
) that

\left\|{\left|{x}\right|^{n}\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\beta}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}}\leq\max\!\left((2n\kappa\hbar)^{\frac{2}{1+a}},(\tfrac{n}{\kappa a\beta})^{\frac{1}{a}}\right)^{n/2}

and the result follows by combining this estimate with Inequality (
43
).
∎

We can now estimate the gradient of the square root of the thermal state.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
H=-\hbar^{2}\Delta+V
with
V
satisfying hypotheses (
21
) and (
22
). Then for any
\beta>0
,

\left\|{\boldsymbol{\nabla}\sqrt{e^{-\beta H}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}^{2}\leq\frac{C_{d,a}}{\kappa^{\frac{d}{a}}\beta^{d\left(\frac{1}{a}+\frac{1}{2}\right)-1}}\left(2^{4}+\kappa_{2}\,\beta\max\!\left((2b\kappa\hbar)^{\frac{2b}{1+a}},(\tfrac{8b}{\kappa a\beta})^{\frac{b}{a}}\right)\right)

where
C_{d,a}=2^{2d\left(\frac{1}{a}+\frac{1}{2}\right)+1}\,\frac{\Gamma(d/a)}{a\,\Gamma(d/2)}\,\pi^{d}
.

Proof.

Let
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\beta}=e^{-\beta\,H}
. As in
[
8
]
and using the fact that
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\beta}^{s}=\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\beta s}
, it holds

\boldsymbol{\nabla}\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\beta}}=-\beta\int_{0}^{1}\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\beta\left(1-s\right)/2}\,\boldsymbol{\nabla}H\,\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\beta s/2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

where
\boldsymbol{\nabla}H=\left(\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!x}H,\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!\xi}H\right)=\left(\nabla V,2\,\boldsymbol{p}\right)
. In particular, by invariance of the Schatten norms by taking the adjoint

\left\|{\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!\xi}\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\beta}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}\leq 2\,\beta\int_{0}^{1}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\beta\left(1-s\right)/2}\,\boldsymbol{p}\,\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\beta s/2}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s=4\,\beta\int_{1/2}^{1}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\beta\left(1-s\right)/2}\,\boldsymbol{p}\,\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\beta s/2}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s.

Using the fact that
0\leq\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\beta\left(1-s\right)/2}\leq 1
and Inequality (
40
), it gives

\left\|{\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!\xi}\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\beta}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}\leq 4\,\beta\int_{1/2}^{1}\left\|{\left|{\boldsymbol{p}}\right|\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\beta s/2}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\leq\frac{2^{2+d\left(\frac{1}{a}+\frac{1}{2}\right)}\sqrt{C_{a}}}{\kappa^{\frac{d}{2a}}\beta^{\frac{d}{2}\left(\frac{1}{a}+\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{d}\right)}}.

Similarly, to bound the gradient with respect to
x
, we use the fact that
\left|{x}\right|^{-b}\,\nabla V
is a bounded operator by Hypothesis (
22
), to get

\left\|{\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!x}\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\beta}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}\leq 2\,\beta\,\kappa_{2}\int_{1/2}^{1}\left\|{\left|{x}\right|^{b}\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\beta s/2}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\\
\leq\frac{2^{d\left(\frac{1}{a}+\frac{1}{2}\right)}\kappa_{2}\,\sqrt{C_{a}}}{\kappa^{\frac{d}{2a}}\beta^{\frac{d}{2}\left(\frac{1}{a}+\frac{1}{2}\right)-1}}\max\!\left((2b\kappa\hbar)^{\frac{b}{1+a}},(\tfrac{8b}{\kappa a\beta})^{\frac{b}{2a}}\right).

The result follows by using the fact that
\left\|{\boldsymbol{\nabla}\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\beta}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}^{2}=\left\|{\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!x}\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\beta}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}^{2}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\!\xi}\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\beta}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}^{2}
.
∎

The result of Proposition
1.3
now follows from the two above lemmas.

Proof of Proposition
1.3
.

By Lemma
4.2
and the definition of the normalization constant
Z_{\beta}
, we get that
\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathfrak{h})
. On the other hand, Lemma
4.3
gives a bound on
\lVert\boldsymbol{\nabla}\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}
. The proposition then follows from these two facts and Theorem
1.1
.
∎
