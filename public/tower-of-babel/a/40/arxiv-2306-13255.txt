Precise Asymptotic Generalization for Multiclass Classification with Overparameterized Linear Models
David X. Wu, Anant Sahai

Abstract
We study the asymptotic generalization of an overparameterized linear model for multiclass classification under the Gaussian covariates bi-level model introduced in Subramanian et al. 2022 , where the number of data points, features, and classes all grow together. We fully resolve the conjecture posed in Subramanian et al. 2022 , matching the predicted regimes for generalization. Furthermore, our new lower bounds are akin to an information-theoretic strong converse: they establish that the misclassification rate goes to 0 or 1 asymptotically. One surprising consequence of our tight results is that the min-norm interpolating classifier can be asymptotically suboptimal relative to noninterpolating classifiers in the regime where the min-norm interpolating regressor is known to be optimal. The key to our tight analysis is a new variant of the Hanson-Wright inequality which is broadly useful for multiclass problems with sparse labels. As an application, we show that the same type of analysis can be used to analyze the related multilabel classification problem under the same bi-level ensemble.



1
Introduction

In this paper, we directly follow up on a specific line of work initiated by
Subramanian et al. 2022
;
Wu and Sahai 2023
. For the sake of self-containedness, we briefly reiterate the context, directing the reader to
Subramanian et al. 2022
and the references cited therein for more. A broader story can be found in
Bartlett et al. 2021
;
Belkin 2021
;
Dar et al. 2021
;
Oneto et al. 2023
.

Classical statistical learning theory intuition predicts that highly expressive models, which can interpolate random labels
(
Zhang et al. 2016
;
Zhang et al. 2021
)
, ought not to generalize well. However, deep learning practice has seen such models performing well when trained with good labels. Resolving this apparent contradiction has recently been the focus of a multitude of works, and this paper builds on one particular thread of investigation that can be rooted in
Bartlett et al. 2020
;
Muthukumar et al. 2020
where the concept of benign/harmless interpolation was crystallized in the context of overparameterized linear regression problems and conditions given for when this can happen. In
Muthukumar et al. 2021
, a specific toy "bi-level model" with Gaussian features was introduced to study overparameterized binary classification and show that successful generalization could happen even beyond the conditions for benign interpolation for regression. Following the introduction of the corresponding multi-class problem in
Wang et al. 2021
with a constant number of classes, an asymptotic setting where the number of classes can grow with the number of training examples was introduced in
Subramanian et al. 2022
where a conjecture was presented for when minimum-norm interpolating classifiers will generalize. We are now in a position to state our main contributions; afterwards, we expand on the related works.

Our contributions

Our main contribution is crisply identifying the asymptotic regimes where an overparameterized linear model that performs minimum-norm interpolation does and does not generalize for multiclass classification under a Gaussian features assumption, thus resolving the main conjecture posed by
(
Subramanian et al. 2022
)
. We improve on the analysis of
Subramanian et al. 2022
;
Wu and Sahai 2023
, covering all regimes with the asymptotically optimal misclassification rate.
When the model generalizes, it does so with a misclassification rate
o(1)
, and we show a matching "strong converse" establishing when it misclassifies, it does so with rate
1-o(1)
, where the explicit rate is nearly identical to that of random guessing. The critical component of our analysis is a new variant of the Hanson-Wright inequality, which applies to bilinear forms between a vector with subgaussian entries and a vector that is bounded and has
soft sparsity
, a notion we will define in
Section

4.2
. We show how this tool can be used to analyze other multiclass problems, such as multilabel classification.

1.1
Brief treatment of related work

Our thread begins with a recent line of work that analyzes the generalization behavior of overparameterized linear models for regression
(
Hastie et al. 2022
;
Mei and Montanari 2022
;
Bartlett et al. 2020
;
Belkin et al. 2020
;
Muthukumar et al. 2020
)
. These simple models demonstrate how the capacity to interpolate noise can actually aid in generalization: training noise can be harmlessly absorbed by the overparameterized model without contaminating predictions on test points.
In effect, extra features can be regularizing (in the context of descent algorithms’ implicit regularization
(
Soudry et al. 2018
;
Ji and Telgarsky 2019
;
Engl et al. 1996
;
Gunasekar et al. 2018
)
), but an excessive amount of such regularization causes regression to fail because even the true signal will not survive the training process. Although works in this thread focus on very shallow networks,
Chatterji and Long 2023
established that deeper networks can behave similarly. Note that recently,
Mallinar et al. 2022
called out an alternative regime (behaving like 1-nearest-neighbor learning) called "tempered" overfitting in which training noise is not completely absorbed but the true signal does survive training.

The thread continues in a line of work that studies binary classification
(
Muthukumar et al. 2021
;
Chatterji and Long 2021
;
Wang and Thrampoulidis 2021
)
in similar overparameterized linear models.
While confirming that the basic story is similar to regression, these works identify a further surprise: binary classification can work in some regimes where the corresponding regression problem would not work
1
1

1

Regression failing in the overparameterized regime is linked to the empirical covariance of the limited data not revealing the spiked reality of the underlying covariance
(
Wang and Fan 2017
)
. See Appendix J of
Subramanian et al. 2022
. When regression doesn’t generalize, we also get ”support-vector proliferation” in classification problems
(
Muthukumar et al. 2021
;
Hsu et al. 2021
)
which is also intimately related to the phenomenon of ”neural collapse”
(
Papyan et al. 2020
)
as discussed, for example, in
Xu et al. 2023
.
due to the regularizing effect of overparameterization being too strong. Just as in the regression case, the results here are sharp in toy models: we can exactly characterize where binary classification using an interpolating classifier asymptotically generalizes.

With binary classification better understood, the thread continues to multiclass classification. After all, the current wave of deep learning enthusiasm originated in breakthrough performance in multiclass classification, and we have seen a decade of ever larger networks trained on ever larger datasets with ever more classes
Kaplan et al. 2020
. Using similar toy models
(
Muthukumar et al. 2020
;
Muthukumar et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2022
)
, the constant number of classes case was studied in
Wang et al. 2021
to recover results similar to binary classification and subsequently generalized to general convex losses with regularization in
Loureiro et al. 2021
and student-teacher networks in
Cornacchia et al. 2023
.

Subramanian et al. 2022
further introduced a model where the number of classes grows with the number of training points and proved an achievability result on how fast the number of classes can grow while still allowing the interpolating classifier to asymptotically generalize. While
Subramanian et al. 2022
gave a conjecture for what the full region should be, there was no converse proof, and they could not show generalization in the entire conjectured region.
Wu and Sahai 2023
proved a partial weak converse; they showed that the misclassification rate is bounded away from
0
— rather than tending to
1
— in some of the predicted regimes.

The model, formally defined in the following section, is a stylized version of the well-known spiked covariance model
(
Johnstone 2001
;
Donoho et al. 2018
)
. On the theoretical front, it is related to several problems such as PCA variants
(
Montanari and Richard 2015
;
Richard and Montanari 2014
)
and community detection in the stochastic block model
(
Abbe 2017
)
. These models have also been applied in practice for climate studies and functional data analysis
(
Johnstone 2001
)
. At a high level, spiked covariance models can be interpreted as a linearized version of the manifold hypothesis.

\displaystyle\hat{\ell}=\argmax_{1\leq m\leq k}\widehat{{\bm{f}}}_{m}^{\top}{\bm{x}}_{\mathsf{test}}^{w}.

\displaystyle\argmax_{1\leq m\leq k}{\bm{x}}_{\mathsf{test}}[m]\neq\argmax_{1\leq m\leq k}\widehat{{\bm{f}}}_{m}^{\top}{\bm{x}}_{\mathsf{test}}^{w}.

(Bi-level ensemble)
.

The bi-level ensemble is parameterized by

Main results

In this section, we state our main results and compare them to what was known and conjectured previously.

Subramanian et al. 2022

(13)

Our main theorem establishes that

Theorem 3.2

(Generalization for bi-level-model)

Under the bi-level ensemble model (

The other condition on

Technical overview

We now sketch out the proof for our main theorem. As in
Subramanian et al. 2022
, the starting point is writing out the necessary and sufficient conditions for misclassification.

Assume without loss of generality that the test point
{\bm{x}}_{\mathsf{test}}\sim N(0,{\bm{I}}_{d})
has true label
\alpha
for some
\alpha\in[k]
. Let
{\bm{x}}_{\mathsf{test}}^{w}
be the weighted version of this test point.
From (
10
), an equivalent condition for misclassification is that for some
\beta\neq\alpha,\beta\in[k]
, we have
\widehat{{\bm{f}}}_{\alpha}^{\top}{\bm{x}}_{\mathsf{test}}^{w}<\widehat{{\bm{f}}}_{\beta}^{\top}{\bm{x}}_{\mathsf{test}}^{w}
, i.e. the score for
\beta
outcompetes the score for
\alpha
. Define the Gram matrix
{\bm{A}}\triangleq{\bm{X}}^{w}({\bm{X}}^{w})^{\top}
, the relative label vector
\bm{\Delta}y\triangleq{\bm{y}}_{\alpha}-{\bm{y}}_{\beta}\in\quantity{-1, 0, 1}^{n}
, and the relative survival vector
\widehat{{\bm{h}}}_{\alpha,\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
which compares the signal from
\alpha
and
\beta
:

\displaystyle\widehat{{\bm{h}}}_{\alpha,\beta}[j]

\displaystyle\triangleq\lambda_{j}^{-1/2}(\widehat{{\bm{f}}}_{\alpha}[j]-\widehat{{\bm{f}}}_{\beta}[j])

(16)

\displaystyle={\bm{z}}_{j}^{\top}\bm{A}^{-1}\bm{\Delta}y,

(17)

where to obtain the last line we have used (
8
).
By converting the misclassification condition into the unweighted feature space we see that we will have errors when

\displaystyle\lambda_{\alpha}\widehat{{\bm{h}}}_{\alpha,\beta}[\alpha]{\bm{x}}_{\mathsf{test}}[\alpha]-\lambda_{\beta}\widehat{{\bm{h}}}_{\beta,\alpha}[\beta]{\bm{x}}_{\mathsf{test}}[\beta]<\sum_{j\notin\{\alpha,\beta\}}\lambda_{j}\widehat{{\bm{h}}}_{\beta,\alpha}[j]{\bm{x}}_{\mathsf{test}}[j].

(18)

Define the contamination term
\mathsf{CN}_{\alpha,\beta}
:

\mathsf{CN}_{\alpha,\beta}\triangleq\sqrt{\sum_{j\notin\{\alpha,\beta\}}\lambda_{j}^{2}(\widehat{{\bm{h}}}_{\beta,\alpha}[j])^{2}}.

(19)

Note that
\mathsf{CN}_{\alpha,\beta}
normalizes the RHS of (
18
) into a standard Gaussian. Indeed, define

Z^{(\beta)}\triangleq\frac{1}{\mathsf{CN}_{\alpha,\beta}}\sum_{j\notin\{\alpha,\beta\}}\lambda_{j}\widehat{{\bm{h}}}_{\beta,\alpha}[j]{\bm{x}}_{\mathsf{test}}[j]\sim N(0,1).

(20)

Since
\alpha,\beta\in[k]
are favored, we have
\lambda_{\alpha}=\lambda_{\beta}=\lambda_{F}
. Hence an equivalent condition for misclassification is that there exists some
\beta\neq\alpha
,
\beta\in[k]
such that

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{F}}{\mathsf{CN}_{\alpha,\beta}}(\widehat{{\bm{h}}}_{\alpha,\beta}[\alpha]{\bm{x}}_{\mathsf{test}}[\alpha]-\widehat{{\bm{h}}}_{\beta,\alpha}[\beta]{\bm{x}}_{\mathsf{test}}[\beta])<Z^{(\beta)}.

(21)

We now translate the above criterion into
sufficient
conditions for correct classification and misclassification and analyze these two cases separately.

Correct classification:

For correct classification, it suffices for the minimum value of the LHS of
Eq.

21
to outcompete the maximum value of the RHS, where the max is taken over
\beta\in[k],\beta\neq\alpha
. Some algebra, as in
Subramanian et al. 2022
, shows that we correctly classify if

\displaystyle\underbrace{\frac{\min_{\beta}\lambda_{F}\widehat{{\bm{h}}}_{\alpha,\beta}[\alpha]}{\max_{\beta}\mathsf{CN}_{\alpha,\beta}}}_{{\textsf{SU}}/\mathsf{CN}\text{ ratio}}\Bigg(\underbrace{\min_{\beta}\left({\bm{x}}_{\mathsf{test}}[\alpha]-{\bm{x}}_{\mathsf{test}}[\beta]\right)}_{\text{closest feature margin}}-\underbrace{\max_{\beta}|{\bm{x}}_{\mathsf{test}}[\beta]|}_{\text{largest competing feature}}

\displaystyle\cdot\underbrace{\max_{\beta}\left|\frac{\widehat{{\bm{h}}}_{\alpha,\beta}[\alpha]-\widehat{{\bm{h}}}_{\beta,\alpha}[\beta]}{\widehat{{\bm{h}}}_{\alpha,\beta}[\alpha]}\right|}_{\text{survival variation}}\Bigg)

\displaystyle>\underbrace{\max_{\beta}Z^{(\beta)}}_{\text{normalized contamination}}.

(22)

We will show that under the conditions specified in
3.1
, with high probability, the relevant survival to contamination ratio
{\textsf{SU}}/\mathsf{CN}
grows at a polynomial rate
n^{v}
for some
v>0
, whereas the term in the parentheses shrinks at a subpolynomial rate
\omega(n^{-\delta})
for any
\delta>0
. Further, by standard subgaussian maximal inequalities, the magnitudes of the
normalized contamination
are no more than
O(\sqrt{\log(nk)})
with high probability. Thus with high probability the LHS outcompetes the RHS, leading to correct classification. See
Section

4.1
for more discussion on how we prove tight bounds on the survival-to-contamination ratios.

Misclassification:

On the other hand, for misclassification it suffices for the maximum
absolute
value of the LHS of
Eq.

21
to be outcompeted by the maximum value of the RHS. Some manipulations yield the following sufficient condition for misclassification:

\displaystyle\underbrace{\frac{\max_{\beta}\lambda_{F}\quantity(\abs{\hhat_{\a,\b}[\a]} + \abs{\hhat_{\b,\a}[\b]})}{\min_{\beta}\mathsf{CN}_{\alpha,\beta}}}_{{\textsf{SU}}/\mathsf{CN}\text{ ratio}}\cdot\underbrace{\max_{\gamma\in[k]}\absolutevalue{\xtest[\gamma]}}_{\text{largest label-defining feature}}<\underbrace{\max_{\beta}Z^{(\beta)}}_{\text{normalized contamination}}.

(23)

We show that within the misclassification regimes in
3.1
, the survival-to-contamination ratio
{\textsf{SU}}/\mathsf{CN}

shrinks
at a polynomial rate
n^{-w}
for some
w>0
. By standard subgaussian maximal inequalities, the largest label-defining feature is
O(\sqrt{\log(nk)})
with high probability. Gaussian anticoncentration implies that for some
\beta\neq\alpha,\beta\in[k]
,
Z^{(\beta)}
outcompetes the LHS with probability at least
\frac{1}{2}-o(1)
.
Hence, we conclude that the model will misclassify with rate at least
\frac{1}{2}
asymptotically.

Let us now describe how to boost the misclassification rate to
1-o(1)
. Notice that the above argument only considered the competition between the LHS of
Eq.

23
and one of the
Z^{(\beta)}
’s on the RHS instead of the maximum
Z^{(\beta)}
. It’s not hard to see from the definition of
Z^{(\beta)}
in
Eq.

20
that the
Z^{(\beta)}
are jointly Gaussian. For intuition’s sake, assuming the
Z^{(\beta)}
were
independent
, then
\max_{\beta}Z^{(\beta)}
would outcompete with probability
(\frac{1}{2}-o(1))^{k-1}
.

In reality, the
Z^{(\beta)}
are correlated, but we are able to show that the maximum correlation between the
Z^{(\beta)}
is
\frac{1}{2}+o(1)
with high probability. An application of Slepian’s lemma (
Slepian 1962
) and some explicit bounds on orthant probabilities (
Pinasco et al. 2021
) implies that
\max_{\beta}Z^{(\beta)}>0
with probability at least
1-\frac{1}{k^{1+o(1)}}
. Another application of anticoncentration implies that
\max_{\beta}Z^{(\beta)}>n^{-w}
with probability
1-o(1)
, which finishes off the proof.

4.1
Bounding the survival-to-contamination ratio

Note that the critical
survival-to-contamination
ratio appears in both
Eqs.

22
and
23
. The most involved part of the proof is nailing down the correct order of growth of the survival to contamination ratio; a similar analysis tightly bounds the survival variation and the correlation structure of the
Z^{(\beta)}
.

To understand the relative survival and contamination, we must analyze the bilinear forms
\widehat{{\bm{h}}}_{\alpha,\beta}[j]={\bm{z}}_{j}^{\top}\bm{A}^{-1}\bm{\Delta}y
. Similarly, to control the correlation of the
Z^{(\beta)}
, we must understand the correlation between the
\widehat{{\bm{h}}}_{\alpha,\beta}
vectors, which reduces to understanding the bilinear forms
{\bm{z}}_{j}^{\top}\bm{A}^{-1}{\bm{y}}_{\alpha}
for
j\in[d],\alpha\in[k]
. The main source of inspiration for bounding these bilinear forms is the heuristic style of calculation carried out in Appendix K of
Subramanian et al. 2022
that leads to
3.1
.

To simplify the discussion, we temporarily restrict to the regime where regression fails (
q+r>1)
. However, our main technical tool seamlessly generalizes to the regime where regression works (
q+r<1
). In the regime where regression fails,
\bm{A}^{-1}
turns out to have a
flat
spectrum:
\bm{A}^{-1}\approx\rho{\bm{I}}
for some constant
\rho>0
. Assume for now that
\bm{A}^{-1}
is
exactly
equal to a scaled identity matrix. Then the survival is proportional to
{\bm{z}}_{\alpha}^{\top}\bm{\Delta}y
, which is a random inner product. Similarly, to bound the contamination terms we must control the random inner product
{\bm{z}}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\Delta}y
for
j\not\in\quantity{\a, \b}
.

Since
\bm{\Delta}y
is a sparse vector — it only has
\frac{2n}{k}
nonzero entries in expectation — a quick computation reveals that
\mathbb{E}[{\bm{z}}_{\alpha}^{\top}\bm{\Delta}y]=\tilde{O}(\frac{n}{k})
and
\mathbb{E}[{\bm{z}}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\Delta}y]=0
. The deciding factor, then, is how tightly these quantities concentrate around their means. A naïve application of Hoeffding implies a concentration radius of order
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{n})
, which would lead to looseness in the overall result. The hope is to exploit sparsity to get a concentration radius of order
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{n/k})
. This is where our new technical tool
Theorem

4.2
comes in, which may be of independent interest; we present it in the following section.

4.2
A new variant of the Hanson-Wright inequality

In reality, even in the regime where regression fails,
\bm{A}^{-1}
is not actually perfectly flat. Even worse, in the regime where regression works,
\bm{A}^{-1}
is actually spiked. Thus, we cannot simply reduce the bilinear form
{\bm{z}}_{j}^{\top}\bm{A}^{-1}\bm{\Delta}y
to an inner product. Instead, we turn to the well-known Hanson-Wright inequality
(
Rudelson and Vershynin 2013
)
, which tells us that quadratic forms of random vectors with independent, mean zero, subgaussian entries concentrate around their mean. It was used extensively to study binary classification
(
Muthukumar et al. 2021
)
, and multiclass classification
(
Subramanian et al. 2022
)
.

However, just as the classic Hoeffding inequality is loose, so too is the standard form of Hanson-Wright, because it also does not exploit sparsity. We restate the classic version of Hanson-Wright below.

Define the subgaussian norm
\|\xi\|_{\psi_{2}}

(
Vershynin 2018
)
as

\displaystyle\norm{\xi}_{\psi_{2}}

\displaystyle=\inf_{K>0}\quantity{K: \EE\exp(\xi^2 / K^2) \le 2},

(24)

Theorem 4.1

(Hanson-Wright for quadratic forms, from
Rudelson and Vershynin 2013
)
.

Let
{\bm{x}}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a random vector composed of independent random variables that are zero mean with
\norm{X_i}_{\psi_{2}}\leq K
.
There exists universal constant
c>0
such that for any deterministic
{\bm{M}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and
\epsilon\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\Pr\left[|{\bm{x}}^{\top}{\bm{M}}{\bm{x}}-\mathbb{E}[{\bm{x}}^{\top}{\bm{M}}{\bm{x}}]|>\epsilon\right]\leq 2\exp(-c\min\qty{\frac{\epsilon^2}{K^4\norm{\mM}_F^2}, \frac{\epsilon}{K^2\norm{\mM}_2}}).

The original Hanson-Wright inequality quoted above only applies to quadratic forms, and moreover assumes that the matrix
{\bm{M}}
is deterministic. In our setting, we have a bilinear form
{\bm{z}}_{j}^{\top}\bm{A}^{-1}\bm{\Delta}y
, where
\bm{A}^{-1}
is random. One can condition on the realization of
\bm{A}^{-1}
, but this removes independence and alters the distributions of the random variables involved. Of course, if we condition on a random matrix which is independent of the random vectors, then there is no issue.

Assuming a way around the independence issue, one could decompose the bilinear form with the identity

\displaystyle 4{\bm{z}}_{j}^{\top}\bm{A}^{-1}\bm{\Delta}y

\displaystyle=({\bm{z}}_{j}+\bm{\Delta}y)^{\top}\bm{A}^{-1}({\bm{z}}_{j}+\bm{\Delta}y)-({\bm{z}}_{j}-\bm{\Delta}y)^{\top}\bm{A}^{-1}({\bm{z}}_{j}-\bm{\Delta}y).

(25)

This trick is used in both
Muthukumar et al. 2021
;
Subramanian et al. 2022
. In the binary classification case, one regains complete independence by using a leave-one-out trick. More precisely, define the leave-one-out matrix
{\bm{A}}_{-j}=\sum_{i\neq j}\lambda_{i}{\bm{z}}_{i}{\bm{z}}_{i}^{\top}
and let
{\bm{y}}\in\quantity{\pm 1}^{n}
be the binary label vector. Then
{\bm{A}}_{-j}
is evidently independent of
{\bm{z}}_{j}
and
{\bm{y}}
, and the Sherman-Morrison formula implies that
{\bm{z}}_{j}^{\top}\bm{A}^{-1}{\bm{y}}=\frac{{\bm{z}}_{j}^{\top}{\bm{A}}_{-j}^{-1}{\bm{y}}}{1+{\bm{z}}_{j}^{\top}{\bm{A}}_{-j}^{-1}{\bm{z}}_{j}}
. Because the denominator is a scalar which concentrates well due to Hanson-Wright, this allows for a completely tight characterization of binary classification.

However, this trick does not immediately work in the multiclass setting, because the labels depend on all of the
k>1
label-defining features. Here, one needs to potentially remove
\omega(1)
features from the Gram matrix
{\bm{A}}
to regain independence. In
Subramanian et al. 2022
, they eschew Sherman-Morrison entirely and directly exploit the fact that
\bm{A}^{-1}
is flat (as
q+r>1
). More precisely, they decompose
\bm{A}^{-1}=\rho{\bm{I}}_{n}+{\bm{E}}
, where
\norm{\mE}_{2}\ll\rho
. This essentially shoves all the dependencies into
{\bm{E}}
. While the
\rho{\bm{I}}_{n}
portion reduces to the inner product calculation discussed in
Section

4.1
, they must use Cauchy-Schwarz to handle the dependent
{\bm{E}}
portion. This leads to inevitable looseness in the regimes for Theorem
3.2
, as Cauchy-Schwarz is a
worst-case
bound. Interestingly, their Cauchy-Schwarz bound leverages the sparsity of the label vectors to gain a factor of
\sqrt{k}
, but the bound is still loose by a factor of
\sqrt{n}
.

This motivates a new variant of Hanson-Wright which fully leverages the (soft) sparsity inherent to multiclass problems with an increasing number of classes. We first formally define the notions of soft and hard sparsity.

Definition 2

For

5
Discussion

In this paper we resolve the main conjecture of
Subramanian et al. 2022
, identifying the exact regimes where an overparameterized linear model succeeds at multiclass classification. Our techniques also lay the foundation for investigating related generalization for other multiclass tasks and nonlinear algorithms. We hope that by bringing the rigorous proofs closer to the heuristic style of calculation, we open the path for analyzing more complicated and realistic models.

An important next step is to extend our results to more realistic spiked covariance models. For example, one typically observes power-law decay for the extreme eigenvalues in applications. We expect that the bi-level model can be relaxed to allow for constant deviations in the weightings for the favored, non-label defining features and power law decay for the unfavored features. The former change would likely only affect constants in certain areas of the argument that do not crucially depend on the exact constants involved, whereas the latter would likely just change the effective degree of overparameterization
(
Bartlett et al. 2020
)
. However, even constant fluctuations in weighting for the label-defining features can lead to significant subtleties, as these constant deviations manifest as polynomially large variations in the number of examples of each class. Such heterogeneity between label-defining directions would likely lead to significantly messier conditions for generalization.

Another future direction is to move beyond Gaussian features. It is plausible that similar results would hold for vector subgaussian features which are rotationally invariant, allowing us to rotate into the basis where the features have diagonal covariance. One place where Gaussianity is crucially used is to obtain an explicit lower bound on the margin between the features.

As an example application, we sketch out how our proof techniques imply precise conditions for a variant of the learning task called multilabel classification. In a simple model for multilabel classification, each datapoint can have several of
k
possible labels — corresponding to the positive valued features — but in the training set only one such correct label is provided at random for each datapoint. We deem that the model generalizes if for any queried label it successfully labels test inputs as positive or negative. We can use the MNI approach here to learn classifiers.

Some thought reveals that the main difference between multilabel classification and multiclass classification from a survival and contamination perspective is that positive features no longer need to outcompete other features. Thus, the main object of study would be the bilinear forms
{\bm{z}}_{j}^{\top}\bm{A}^{-1}{\bm{y}}_{\alpha}
, which is possible thanks to
Theorem

4.2
. The survival and contamination terms are only affected by the expected values of these bilinear forms, but the expected values match the multiclass behavior up to log factors, which do not affect the regimes where
{\textsf{SU}}/\mathsf{CN}
will grow or shrink polynomially. A similar analysis thus reveals that MNI will generalize in exactly the same regimes as in
3.1
. Here, the model generalizes in the sense that with high probability over the labels the model will correctly classify, and failure to generalize means that the model will do no better than a coin toss.

Perhaps surprisingly, resolving
3.1
also implies that MNI is asymptotically
suboptimal
compared to a natural
non-interpolative
approach: simply make
\widehat{{\bm{f}}}_{m}
equal to the average
6
6

6

Note that
Frei et al. 2023
point out that even leaky ReLU networks trained with a gradient flow can behave like averages of training examples.
of all positive training examples of class
m
. A straightforward analysis, detailed in the supplementary material, reveals this scheme fails to generalize exactly when
t<\min\quantity{1-r, p+1-2(q+r)}
, even in the regime where regression succeeds (
q+r<1
). This is particularly interesting because we have shown that in the regime where regression succeeds, MNI generalizes only when
t<\min\quantity{1-r, p-1}
, which is a smaller region. In light of this gap, it would be interesting to identify the
information-theoretic
barrier for multiclass classification, especially within the broader context of statistical-computation gaps (see e.g.
Wu and Xu 2021
;
Brennan and Bresler 2020
).

\displaystyle\norm{\xi}_{\psi_{2}}

\displaystyle\norm{\xi}_{\psi_{1}}

\displaystyle=\inf_{K>0}\quantity{K: \EE\exp(\abs{\xi}/ K) \le 2}.

(51)

.

(Hanson-Wright for bilinear forms without sparsity)

and

are all independent of each other (however

and

\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]=\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}]=0

. Then there exists an absolute constant

B.2
Gram matrices and the Woodbury formula

In order to apply Hanson-Wright to the bilinear form
{\bm{x}}^{\top}{\bm{M}}{\bm{y}}
, we need to have a deterministic matrix
{\bm{M}}
such that the hypotheses are satisfied. However, in our setting we study bilinear forms such as
{\bm{z}}_{j}^{\top}\bm{A}^{-1}\bm{\Delta}y
. Here, the inverse Gram matrix
\bm{A}^{-1}
is not independent of
{\bm{z}}_{j}
or
\bm{\Delta}y
, so we cannot simply condition on
\bm{A}^{-1}
. The way around this is to cleverly decompose
\bm{A}^{-1}
using the so-called Woodbury inversion formula (stated formally below), which generalizes the leave-one-out trick and Sherman-Morrison used to study binary classification in
Muthukumar et al. 2021
. To that end, we will explicitly decompose the Gram matrix
{\bm{A}}\triangleq\sum_{j\in[d]}\lambda_{j}{\bm{z}}_{j}{\bm{z}}_{j}^{\top}
based on whether the features
{\bm{z}}_{j}
are favored or not.

We now introduce some notation to keep track of which matrices contain or leave out which indices. In general, we use subscripts to denote which sets of features we preserve or leave out; we use a minus sign to signify leaving out.
The
k
label-defining features are represented with a subscript
k
, whereas the
s-k
favored but not label defining features are represented with a subscript
F
. The rest of the
d-s
unfavored features are represented with a subscript
U
.

For notational convenience, we introduce some new notation for the weighted features, as the superscript
w
to denote weighted features is rather cumbersome. We denote the weighted label-defining feature matrix by
{\bm{W}}_{k}\triangleq\matrixquantity[\lx@physics@matrix\vw_1 & \cdots& \vw_k\endlx@physics@matrix]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
, where the vectors
{\bm{w}}_{i}\triangleq\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}{\bm{z}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
denote the weighted observations for feature
i
. Define the unweighted label-defining feature matrix
{\bm{Z}}_{k}\triangleq\matrixquantity[\lx@physics@matrix\vz_1 & \cdots& \vz_k\endlx@physics@matrix]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
. Similarly, define
{\bm{W}}_{F}\triangleq\matrixquantity[\lx@physics@matrix\vw_{k+1} & \cdots& \vw_s\endlx@physics@matrix]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(s-k)}
, which contains the rest of the weighted favored features and the corresponding unweighted version
{\bm{Z}}_{F}
.

Let
{\bm{A}}_{-k}\triangleq\sum_{i\not\in[k]}{\bm{w}}_{i}{\bm{w}}_{i}^{\top}
denote the leave-
k
-out Gram matrix which removes the
k
label-defining features. Similarly, let
{\bm{A}}_{-F}\triangleq\sum_{i\not\in[s]\setminus[k]}{\bm{w}}_{i}{\bm{w}}_{i}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
to denote leave-
(s-k)
-out Gram matrix which removes the favored but not label-defining features. Finally,
let
{\bm{A}}_{U}\triangleq\sum_{i\not\in[s]}{\bm{w}}_{i}{\bm{w}}_{i}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
denote the leave-
s
-out matrix which only retains the unfavored features. We will also sometimes write
{\bm{A}}_{-s}
instead of
{\bm{A}}_{U}
to emphasize that the
s
favored features have all been removed.

Define the so-called hat matrices by

\displaystyle{\bm{H}}_{k}

\displaystyle\triangleq{\bm{W}}_{k}^{\top}{\bm{A}}_{-k}^{-1}{\bm{W}}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}

(53)

\displaystyle{\bm{H}}_{F}

\displaystyle\triangleq{\bm{W}}_{F}^{\top}{\bm{A}}_{-F}^{-1}{\bm{W}}_{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{(s-k)\times(s-k)}.

(54)

These hat matrices appear in the Woodbury inversion formula. For the sake of notational compactness, define

\displaystyle{\bm{M}}_{k}

\displaystyle\triangleq{\bm{W}}_{k}({\bm{I}}_{k}+{\bm{H}}_{k})^{-1}{\bm{W}}_{k}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}

(55)

\displaystyle{\bm{M}}_{F}

\displaystyle\triangleq{\bm{W}}_{F}({\bm{I}}_{s-k}+{\bm{H}}_{F})^{-1}{\bm{W}}_{F}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}.

(56)

The Woodbury inversion formula yields

\displaystyle\bm{A}^{-1}

\displaystyle=({\bm{W}}_{k}{\bm{W}}_{k}^{\top}+{\bm{A}}_{-k})^{-1}

(57)

\displaystyle={\bm{A}}_{-k}^{-1}-{\bm{A}}_{-k}^{-1}{\bm{W}}_{k}({\bm{I}}_{k}+{\bm{H}}_{k})^{-1}{\bm{W}}_{k}^{\top}{\bm{A}}_{-k}^{-1}

(58)

\displaystyle={\bm{A}}_{-k}^{-1}-{\bm{A}}_{-k}^{-1}{\bm{M}}_{k}{\bm{A}}_{-k}^{-1}.

(59)

Left multiplying (
58
) by
{\bm{W}}_{k}^{\top}
yields

\displaystyle{\bm{W}}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}^{-1}

\displaystyle={\bm{W}}_{k}^{\top}{\bm{A}}_{-k}^{-1}-{\bm{H}}_{k}({\bm{I}}_{k}+{\bm{H}}_{k})^{-1}{\bm{W}}_{k}^{\top}{\bm{A}}_{-k}^{-1}

(60)

\displaystyle=({\bm{I}}_{k}-{\bm{H}}_{k}({\bm{I}}_{k}+{\bm{H}}_{k})^{-1}){\bm{W}}_{k}^{\top}{\bm{A}}_{-k}^{-1}

(61)

\displaystyle=({\bm{I}}_{k}+{\bm{H}}_{k})^{-1}{\bm{W}}_{k}^{\top}{\bm{A}}_{-k}^{-1}.

(62)

We can derive completely analogous identities using
{\bm{A}}_{-F}^{-1}
instead of
{\bm{A}}_{-k}^{-1}
. The above exposition is summarized by the following lemma.

Lemma B.2
.

We have

\displaystyle{\bm{W}}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}^{-1}\bm{\Delta}y

\displaystyle=({\bm{I}}_{k}+{\bm{H}}_{k})^{-1}{\bm{W}}_{k}^{\top}{\bm{A}}_{-k}^{-1}\bm{\Delta}y

(63)

\displaystyle{\bm{W}}_{F}^{\top}\bm{A}^{-1}\bm{\Delta}y

\displaystyle=({\bm{I}}_{s-k}+{\bm{H}}_{F})^{-1}{\bm{W}}_{F}^{\top}{\bm{A}}_{-F}^{-1}\bm{\Delta}y.

(64)

Lemma
B.2
is quite powerful. Indeed, consider the action of the linear operator
{\bm{W}}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}^{-1}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
on
\bm{\Delta}y
. The action is identical to that of the linear operator
{\bm{W}}_{k}^{\top}{\bm{A}}_{-k}^{-1}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
, up to some invertible transformation. This new linear operator is nice because
{\bm{A}}_{-k}^{-1}
is independent of
{\bm{W}}_{k}
and
\bm{\Delta}y
, as it removes all of the label-defining features. Reclaiming independence sets the stage for using our variant of Hanson-Wright.

How does the invertible operator
({\bm{I}}_{k}+{\bm{H}}_{k})^{-1}
act? Our general strategy is to show that
{\bm{H}}_{k}
is itself close to a scaled identity matrix, i.e.
{\bm{H}}_{k}\approx\nu{\bm{I}}_{k}
for an appropriately defined
\nu
. Then for any
i\in[k]
, we have that

{\bm{w}}_{i}^{\top}\bm{A}^{-1}\bm{\Delta}y\approx(1+\nu)^{-1}{\bm{w}}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{A}}_{-k}^{-1}\bm{\Delta}y.

Of course, there will be some error in this approximation, as
{\bm{H}}_{k}
is not
exactly
equal to
\nu{\bm{I}}_{k}
. Nevertheless, we can bound away the error that arises from this approximation.

B.3
Concentration of spectrum

As foreshadowed in the previous section, we will leverage the fact that the hat matrices such as
{\bm{H}}_{k}
are close to a scaled identity. To formalize this, we appeal to random matrix theory and show that the spectra of various random matrices are very close to being flat (i.e. all eigenvalues are within
1+o(1)
of each other). To that end, we present the following standard characterization of the spectrum of a standard Wishart matrix, which is Equation 2.3 in
Rudelson and Vershynin 2010
.

Lemma B.3

(Concentration of spectrum for Wishart matrices)
.

Let
{\bm{M}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times m}
with
M>m
be a real matrix with iid
N(0,1)
entries. Then for any
\epsilon\geq 0
, we have with probability at least
1-2e^{-\epsilon^{2}/2}
that

\sqrt{M}-\sqrt{m}-\epsilon\leq\sigma_{\min{}}({\bm{M}})\leq\sigma_{\max{}}({\bm{M}})\leq\sqrt{M}+\sqrt{m}+\epsilon.

(65)

In other words, the singular values of
{\bm{M}}
satisfy subgaussian concentration.

Since
\mu_{m}({\bm{M}}^{\top}{\bm{M}})=\sigma_{\min{}}({\bm{M}})^{2}
and
\mu_{1}({\bm{M}}^{\top}{\bm{M}})=\sigma_{\max{}}({\bm{M}})^{2}
, we can conclude that if
m=o(M)
, then for any
\epsilon>0
we have

M-2\sqrt{Mm}-\epsilon+o(\sqrt{Mm})\leq\mu_{m}({\bm{M}}^{\top}{\bm{M}})\leq\mu_{1}({\bm{M}}^{\top}{\bm{M}})\leq M+2\sqrt{Mm}+\epsilon+o(\sqrt{Mm}),

(66)

with probability at least
1-2e^{-\epsilon^{2}/2}
.

On the other hand, consider
{\bm{M}}{\bm{M}}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
. Its spectrum is just that of
{\bm{M}}^{\top}{\bm{M}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
with an additional
M-m
zeros corresponding to the fact that
m<M
.

We can use
Lemma

B.3
to prove concentration of the spectrum of the various matrices introduced in
Section

B.2
. Let us summarize some convenient forms of these results; their proofs are deferred to
Appendix

I
.

Proposition B.4

(Gram matrices have a flat spectrum)
.

Recall that
{\bm{A}}_{U}={\bm{A}}_{-s}=\sum_{j>s}\lambda_{j}{\bm{z}}_{j}{\bm{z}}_{j}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is the unfavored Gram matrix and
{\bm{A}}_{-k}=\sum_{j>k}\lambda_{j}{\bm{z}}_{j}{\bm{z}}_{j}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is the leave-
k
-out Gram matrix.

Then the following hold with probability at least
1-2e^{-n}-2e^{-\sqrt{n}}
,

(a)

For all
i\in[n]
, we have
\mu_{i}({\bm{A}}_{U})=n^{p}(1\pm O(n^{-\kappa_{\ref{kappa_au}}}))
.

(b)

For all
i\in[s-k]
, we have

\displaystyle\mu_{i}({\bm{A}}_{-k})=(1+\mu^{-1})n^{p}(1\pm O(n^{-\kappa_{\ref{kappa_ak_spiked}}})),

(67)

where
\kappa_{\ref{kappa_ak_spiked}}
is a positive constant.
Moreover, for all
i\in[n]\setminus[s-k]
, we have

\displaystyle\mu_{i}({\bm{A}}_{-k})=n^{p}(1\pm O(n^{-\kappa_{\ref{kappa_au}}})),

(68)

where
\kappa_{\ref{kappa_au}}
is a positive constant.

As a simple corollary, we can obtain the following cruder bounds on the trace and spectral norm of
{\bm{A}}_{-k}^{-1}
and
{\bm{A}}_{-s}^{-1}

(Trace and spectral norm of

\displaystyle=n^{1-p}(1\pm O(n^{-\kappa_{\ref{kappa_au}}}))\sqrt{\log k}

(69)

\displaystyle\Tr(\Akinv)

\displaystyle=n^{1-p}(1\pm O(n^{-\kappa_{\ref{kappa_akinv_trace}}}))\sqrt{\log k}

(70)

and

\max\quantity{\norm{\Akinv}_2, \norm{\Auinv}_2}\leq c_{\ref{const_akinv_spectral_norm}}n^{-p},

(71)

where

,
\kappa_{\ref{kappa_au}}
, and
\kappa_{\ref{kappa_akinv_trace}}
are all positive constants.

Proof.

We prove the claim for

If

\displaystyle\Tr(\Akinv)

Appendix C
Utility bounds: applying the tools

Wishart concentration allows us to tightly bound the hat matrix and pass to studying bilinear forms of the form
{\bm{w}}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{A}}_{-k}^{-1}\bm{\Delta}y
rather than
{\bm{w}}_{i}^{\top}\bm{A}^{-1}\bm{\Delta}y
. Since
{\bm{A}}_{-k}^{-1}
is independent of
{\bm{W}}_{k}
and
\bm{\Delta}y
, we can condition on
{\bm{A}}_{-k}^{-1}
and then apply Hanson-Wright (
Theorem

4.2
) to these bilinear forms for every realization of
{\bm{A}}_{-k}^{-1}
. In this section, we will explicitly calculate the scaling of the typical value of these bilinear forms using the bi-level ensemble scaling; these will prove to be useful throughout the rest of the paper.

We first state the following proposition which bounds the correlation between the relevant label-defining features and the label vectors; it is a combination of Propositions D.5 and D.6 in
[
Subramanian et al. 2022
]
.

Proposition C.1
.

For any distinct
\alpha,\beta\in[k]
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi\ln 2}}\cdot\frac{n}{k}\cdot\sqrt{\ln k}\leq\mathbb{E}[{\bm{z}}_{\alpha}^{\top}{\bm{y}}_{\alpha}]\leq\sqrt{2}\cdot\frac{n}{k}\cdot\sqrt{\ln k}

(76)

and

\displaystyle-\sqrt{2}\cdot\frac{n}{k}\cdot\frac{1}{k-1}\cdot\sqrt{\ln k}\leq\mathbb{E}[{\bm{z}}_{\alpha}^{\top}{\bm{y}}_{\beta}]\leq-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi\ln 2}}\cdot\frac{n}{k}\cdot\frac{1}{k-1}\cdot\sqrt{\ln k}

(77)

With the above proposition in hand, we can prove the following lemma which gives concentration of the bilinear forms that we study.

Lemma C.2
.

Let
i\in[d]
and
\bm{\Delta}y={\bm{y}}_{\alpha}-{\bm{y}}_{\beta}
where
\alpha,\beta\in[k]
and
\beta\neq\alpha
. Let
{\bm{M}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be a (random) matrix which is independent of
{\bm{z}}_{i}
and
\bm{\Delta}y
. Then conditioned on
{\bm{M}}
, with probability at least
1-1/nk
,

\absolutevalue{\vz_i^\top\mM\dely- \EE[\vz_i^\top\mM\dely| \mM]}\leq c_{\ref{chansonwright}}\sqrt{\frac{n}{k}}\norm{\mM}_{2}\sqrt{\log(nk)},

and the same holds with
\bm{\Delta}y
replaced with
{\bm{y}}_{\alpha}
.
Here,
c_{\ref{chansonwright}}
is an appropriately chosen universal positive constant.

Moreover, we have

(1)

For any distinct
\alpha,\beta\in[k]
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\bm{z}}_{\alpha}^{\top}{\bm{M}}\bm{\Delta}y|{\bm{M}}]

\displaystyle=c_{\ref{const_alpha}}\frac{\sqrt{\log k}}{k}\tr(\mM)=-\mathbb{E}[{\bm{z}}_{\beta}^{\top}{\bm{M}}\bm{\Delta}y]

(78)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\bm{z}}_{\alpha}^{\top}{\bm{M}}{\bm{y}}_{\alpha}|{\bm{M}}]

\displaystyle=c_{\ref{const_ya}}\frac{\sqrt{\log k}}{k}\tr(\mM),

(79)

where
c_{\ref{const_alpha}}
and
c_{\ref{const_ya}}
are positive constants.

(2)

For
i\in[d]\setminus\quantity{\a, \b}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\bm{z}}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{M}}\bm{\Delta}y|{\bm{M}}]

\displaystyle=0.

(80)

(3)

For
i\in[d]\setminus\quantity{\a}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\bm{z}}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{M}}{\bm{y}}_{\alpha}|{\bm{M}}]

\displaystyle=-c_{\ref{const_zi_ya}}\frac{\sqrt{\log k}}{k(k-1)},

(81)

where
c_{\ref{const_zi_ya}}
is a positive constant.

Proof.

Let us check the conditions for our new variant of Hanson-Wright with soft sparsity (
Theorem

4.2
).
We want to apply it to the random vectors
({\bm{z}}_{i},\bm{\Delta}y)=({\bm{z}}_{i}[j],\bm{\Delta}y[j])_{j=1}^{n}
. Some of the hypotheses are immediate by definition. Evidently,
({\bm{z}}_{i}[j],\bm{\Delta}y[j])
are independent across
j
, and are mean zero. Since
{\bm{z}}_{i}[j]\sim N(0,1)
, it is subgaussian with parameter at most
K=2
. For the bounded and soft sparsity assumption, we clearly have
\absolutevalue{\dely[j]}\leq 1
and
{\bm{y}}_{\alpha}[j]\leq 1
almost surely. Also, since
\bm{\Delta}y[j]^{2}\sim\mathsf{Ber}(\frac{2}{k})
, we have
\mathbb{E}[\bm{\Delta}y[j]^{2}]=\frac{2}{k}
. Similarly,
\mathbb{E}[{\bm{y}}_{\alpha}[j]^{2}]=\frac{1}{k}(1-\frac{1}{k})^{2}+(1-\frac{1}{k})\frac{1}{k^{2}}\leq\frac{2}{k}
.

The more complicated condition is the subgaussianity of
{\bm{z}}_{i}[j]
conditioned on the value of
\bm{\Delta}y[j]
or
{\bm{y}}_{\alpha}[j]
. Regardless of whether we’re conditioning on
\bm{\Delta}y
or
{\bm{y}}_{\alpha}
, it suffices to instead prove that
{\bm{z}}_{i}[j]
is subgaussian conditioned on whether feature
i
won the competition for datapoint
j
. First, suppose
i
won, i.e.
{\bm{y}}^{\mathsf{oh}}_{i}[j]=1
. Then the Borell-TIS inequality
[
Adler et al. 2007
, Theorem 2.1.1]
implies that
{\bm{z}}_{i}[j]
satisfies a subgaussian tail inequality. By the equivalent conditions for subgaussianity
Vershynin 2018
, it follows that
{\bm{z}}_{i}[j]-\mathbb{E}[{\bm{z}}_{i}[j]|{\bm{y}}^{\mathsf{oh}}_{i}[j]=1]
conditionally has subgaussian norm bounded by some absolute constant
K
. If
i
doesn’t win (or doesn’t participate in the competition), then Proposition D.2 in
Subramanian et al. 2022
implies that
{\bm{z}}_{i}[j]-\mathbb{E}[{\bm{z}}_{i}[j]|{\bm{y}}^{\mathsf{oh}}_{i}[j]=0]
conditionally has subgaussian norm bounded by
6
.

Finally, since
{\bm{M}}
is independent of
{\bm{z}}_{i}
and
\bm{\Delta}y
, we can condition on
{\bm{M}}
and apply
Theorem

4.2
to the bilinear form for every realization of
{\bm{M}}
.

Hence, we conclude that with probability at least
1-1/nk
we have

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{\vz_i^\top\mM\dely- \EE[\vz_i^\top\mM\dely| \mM]}\leq c_{\ref{chansonwright}}\sqrt{\frac{n}{k}}\norm{\mM}_{2}\sqrt{\log(nk)},

(82)

where
c_{3}
is an appropriately chosen absolute constant based on
K
and the constant

defined in
Theorem

4.2
.

Now we can compute

to prove the rest of the theorem. If

\mathbb{E}[{\bm{z}}_{\alpha}^{\top}{\bm{M}}\bm{\Delta}y|{\bm{M}}]=\tr(\mM\EE[\dely\vz_\a^\top]).

Appendix D
Bounding the survival

Recall that the relative survival was defined to be
\lambda_{F}\widehat{{\bm{h}}}_{\alpha,\beta}[\alpha]=\lambda_{F}{\bm{z}}_{\alpha}^{\top}\bm{A}^{-1}\bm{\Delta}y

Proof.

Bounding the contamination

In this section we give a tight analysis of the contamination term. First, we rewrite the squared contamination term and separate it out into the contamination from the
k-2
label-defining features which are not
\alpha
or
\beta
, the rest of the
s-k
favored features, and the remaining
d-s
unfavored features. From
Eq.

30
, we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{CN}_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in[d]\setminus\quantity{\a, \b}}\lambda_{j}^{2}({\bm{z}}_{j}^{\top}\bm{A}^{-1}\bm{\Delta}y)^{2}

(98)

\displaystyle=\bm{\Delta}y^{\top}\bm{A}^{-1}\quantity(\sum_{j \in[d] \setminus\qty{\a, \b}} \lambda_j^2 \vz_j \vz_j^\top)\bm{A}^{-1}\bm{\Delta}y

(99)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\bm{\Delta}y^{\top}\bm{A}^{-1}\quantity(\sum_{j \in[k] \setminus\qty{\a, \b}} \lambda_j^2 \vz_j \vz_j^\top)\bm{A}^{-1}\bm{\Delta}y}_{\triangleq\mathsf{CN}_{\alpha,\beta,L}^{2}}+\underbrace{\bm{\Delta}y^{\top}\bm{A}^{-1}\quantity(\sum_{j \in[s] \setminus[k]} \lambda_j^2 \vz_j \vz_j^\top)\bm{A}^{-1}\bm{\Delta}y}_{\triangleq\mathsf{CN}_{\alpha,\beta,F}^{2}}

(100)

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\underbrace{\bm{\Delta}y^{\top}\bm{A}^{-1}\quantity(\sum_{j > s} \lambda_j^2 \vz_j \vz_j^\top)\bm{A}^{-1}\bm{\Delta}y}_{\triangleq\mathsf{CN}_{\alpha,\beta,U}^{2}}.

\mathsf{CN}_{\alpha,\beta,F}
corresponds to contamination from favored features, and
\mathsf{CN}_{\alpha,\beta,U}
corresponds to contamination from unfavored features. The reason for separating out the contamination into these three subterms is that we will need slightly different arguments to bound each of them, although Hanson-Wright and Woodbury are central to all of the arguments. In
Section

we prove the upper bound on

Section

E.2
we prove the lower bound. Finally, in

we bound

. After putting these bounds together, we will obtain the main bounds on the contamination, which we restate here for reference.

Lemma E.1

In the same setting as

\mathsf{CN}_{\alpha,\beta,U}^{2}\leq c_{\ref{const_cnu_upper}}^{2}n^{1-t-p}\log(nsk).

Proof.

By Woodbury, we have

\displaystyle\bm{A}^{-1}={\bm{A}}_{U}^{-1}-{\bm{A}}_{U}^{-1}{\bm{M}}_{s}{\bm{A}}_{U}^{-1},

(132)

and
{\bm{H}}_{s}\triangleq{\bm{W}}_{s}^{\top}{\bm{A}}_{-s}^{-1}{\bm{W}}_{s}
.

Now we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{CN}_{\alpha,\beta,U}^{2}

\displaystyle=\bm{\Delta}y^{\top}\bm{A}^{-1}{\bm{A}}_{U}\bm{A}^{-1}\bm{\Delta}y

\displaystyle=\bm{\Delta}y^{\top}({\bm{A}}_{U}^{-1}-{\bm{A}}_{U}^{-1}{\bm{M}}_{s}{\bm{A}}_{U}^{-1}){\bm{A}}_{U}({\bm{A}}_{U}^{-1}-{\bm{A}}_{U}^{-1}{\bm{M}}_{s}{\bm{A}}_{U}^{-1})\bm{\Delta}y

(135)

By

(136)

where

is a positive constant.

On the other hand, we have that

\displaystyle{\bm{M}}_{s}{\bm{A}}_{U}^{-1}{\bm{M}}_{s}

\displaystyle={\bm{W}}_{s}({\bm{I}}_{s}+{\bm{H}}_{s})^{-1}{\bm{W}}_{s}^{\top}{\bm{A}}_{U}^{-1}{\bm{W}}_{s}({\bm{I}}_{s}+{\bm{H}}_{s})^{-1}{\bm{W}}_{s}^{\top}

\displaystyle={\bm{W}}_{s}({\bm{I}}_{s}-({\bm{I}}_{s}+{\bm{H}}_{s})^{-1})({\bm{I}}_{s}+{\bm{H}}_{s})^{-1}{\bm{W}}_{s}^{\top}

\displaystyle={\bm{W}}_{s}(({\bm{I}}_{s}+{\bm{H}}_{s})^{-1}-({\bm{I}}_{s}+{\bm{H}}_{s})^{-2}){\bm{W}}_{s}^{\top}

for all

\min\quantity{\mu, 1}(1\pm o(1))\bm{\Delta}y^{\top}{\bm{A}}_{U}^{-1}{\bm{W}}_{s}{\bm{W}}_{s}{\bm{A}}_{U}^{-1}\bm{\Delta}y

Proposition

C.4

1-O(1/nk)

\displaystyle\min\quantity{\mu, 1}(1\pm o(1))\bm{\Delta}y^{\top}{\bm{A}}_{U}^{-1}{\bm{W}}_{s}{\bm{W}}_{s}{\bm{A}}_{U}^{-1}\bm{\Delta}y

(137)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\leq c_{\ref{const_norm_bilinear}}^{2}\lambda_{F}\min\quantity{\mu, 1}(1\pm o(1))(n^{2-2t-2p}+n^{r+1-t-2p})\log(nsk)

(138)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\leq c_{\ref{const_norm_bilinear}}^{2}\min\quantity{1, \mu^{-1}}(1\pm o(1))(n^{1-2t-p}+n^{r-t-p})\log(nsk)

For

and

. Hence if

then by union bound we have with probability at least

\displaystyle\mathsf{CN}_{\alpha,\beta,U}^{2}

\displaystyle=c_{\ref{const_quadratic_sparse}}^{2}(1\pm o(1))n^{1-t-p},

Appendix F
Obtaining tight misclassification rate

In this section, we will prove
Proposition

A.5
.
Let us restate the main proposition and sketch out its proof more formally.

See
A.5

Proof sketch.

Note that the
Z^{(\beta)}
’s that must outcompete the decaying survival to contamination ratio are jointly Gaussian, as they are projections of a standard Gaussian vector
{\bm{x}}_{\mathsf{test}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Hence if we want to study the probability that
\max_{\beta}Z^{(\beta)}
outcompetes
n^{-u}
, we have to understand the correlation structure of the
Z^{(\beta)}
’s.

We will argue that for
\beta,\gamma\in[k]
with
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
pairwise distinct, the correlation between
Z^{(\beta)}
and
Z^{(\gamma)}
is
\frac{1}{2}\pm o(1)
with high probability. To that end, we want to look at the correlation (inner product) between the vectors
\quantity{\lambda_j\hhat_{\a, \b}[j]}
for
j\not\in\quantity{\a, \b}
and
\quantity{\lambda_j\hhat_{\a, \gamma}[j]}
for
j\not\in\quantity{\a, \gamma}
. However, note that by independence of the components of
{\bm{x}}_{\mathsf{test}}
from every other random variable and the fact that they are mean zero, we have

\mathbb{E}[\widehat{{\bm{h}}}_{\alpha,\beta}[\gamma]{\bm{x}}_{\mathsf{test}}[\gamma]\widehat{{\bm{h}}}_{\alpha,\gamma}[\beta]{\bm{x}}_{\mathsf{test}}[\beta]]=0.

Hence it suffices to look at the correlation for
j\not\in\quantity{\a, \b, \gamma}
.

We assume WLOG that
\alpha=1,\beta=2
,
\gamma=3
. Let

\Lambda_{\alpha,\beta}\triangleq\mathrm{diag}(1-\bm{1}_{j=\alpha}-\bm{1}_{j=\beta})_{j\in[d]}\circ\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{j})_{j\in[d]}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}

represent the diagonal matrices containing the squared feature weights with indices
\alpha,\beta
zeroed out.
Next, let
{\bm{v}}_{\alpha,\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote the vector with
{\bm{v}}_{\alpha,\beta}[\alpha]={\bm{v}}_{\alpha,\beta}[\beta]=0
and
{\bm{v}}_{\alpha,\beta}[j]=\lambda_{j}\widehat{{\bm{h}}}_{\alpha,\beta}[j]
for
j\in[d],j\not\in\quantity{\a, \b}
. Hence
{\bm{v}}_{\alpha,\beta}=\Lambda_{\alpha,\beta}^{1/2}(\widehat{{\bm{f}}}_{\alpha}-\widehat{{\bm{f}}}_{\beta})
. Since
Z^{(\beta)}=\expectationvalue{\vv_{\a, \b}, \xtest}
, in order to analyze the correlations between
Z^{(\beta)}
and
Z^{(\gamma)}
, it suffices to analyze
{\bm{v}}_{\alpha,\beta}
. Indeed, we will show that the weighted halfspaces
\Lambda_{\alpha,\beta}^{1/2}\widehat{{\bm{f}}}_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\Lambda_{\alpha,\beta}^{1/2}\widehat{{\bm{f}}}_{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are asymptotically orthogonal.

In other words, we need to show that

\frac{\expectationvalue{\lambdaab^{1/2} \fhat_{\a}, \lambdaab^{1/2} \fhat_{\b}}}{\norm{\lambdaab^{1/2} \fhat_{\a}}_{2}\norm{\lambdaab^{1/2} \fhat_{\b}}_{2}}=o(1)

with probability at least
1-O(1/nk)
; we can then union bound against all choices of
\beta
. This is the most technically involved part of the proof, and is the content of
Proposition

F.1
.

This in turn will imply (see
Lemma

F.2
) that the maximum (and minimum) correlation between the
{\bm{v}}_{\alpha,\beta}
for different
\beta
is
\frac{1}{2}\pm o(1)
. Let
(\overline{Z}_{\beta})_{\beta\in[k],\beta\neq\alpha}
be equicorrelated gaussians with correlation
\overline{\rho}=\frac{1}{2}+o(1)
, and
(\underline{Z}_{\beta})_{\beta\in[k],\beta\neq\alpha}
be equicorrelated gaussians with correlation
\underline{\rho}=\frac{1}{2}-o(1)
. By Slepian’s lemma, for any
u>0
, the probability of
\max_{\beta}Z^{(\beta)}
losing to
n^{-u}
is sandwiched as

\displaystyle\Pr[\max_{\beta}\underline{Z}_{\beta}\leq n^{-u}]\leq\Pr[\max_{\beta}Z^{(\beta)}\leq n^{-u}]\leq\Pr[\max_{\beta}\overline{Z}_{\beta}\leq n^{-u}],

where we have adopted the shorthand
\max_{\beta}
to denote
\max_{\beta\in[k],\beta\neq\alpha}
.

Theorem 2.1 of
Pinasco et al. 2021
shows that jointly gaussian vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
with equicorrelation
\rho
lie in the positive orthant with probability
\Theta(k^{1-1/\rho})
. In particular, applied to
\overline{Z}_{\beta}
, with correlation
\overline{\rho}=\frac{1}{2}+o(1)
, we find that

\displaystyle\Pr[\max_{\beta}\overline{Z}_{\beta}\leq 0]=\Theta(k^{-1+o(1)}),

and similarly for
\underline{Z}_{\beta}
. Anticoncentration for Gaussian maxima
[
Chernozhukov et al. 2015
, Corollary 1]
implies that we can transfer over the bound on
\Pr[\max_{\beta}\overline{Z}_{\beta}\leq 0]
to a bound on
\Pr[\max_{\beta}\overline{Z}_{\beta}\leq n^{-u}]
to show that for every
\epsilon>0
, we have

\displaystyle\Theta(k^{-1-o(1)})-\epsilon\leq\Pr[\max_{\beta}Z^{(\beta)}\leq n^{-u}]\leq\Theta(k^{-1+o(1)})+\epsilon

(142)

for sufficiently large
n
. Taking the complement of the above event concludes the proof.
∎

F.1
Main results for tight misclassification rates

The main result in this section is the following proposition, which states that the halfspace predictions are asymptotically orthogonal. Its proof is deferred to the subsequent sections.

Proposition F.1
.

Assume we are in the bi-level ensemble model (
Definition

1
), the true data generating process is 1-sparse (Assumption
1
), and the number of classes scales with
n
(i.e.

).

For any distinct
\alpha,\beta\in[k]
, with probability at least
1-O(1/nk)
, we have

\frac{\expectationvalue{\lambdaab^{1/2} \fhat_{\a}, \lambdaab^{1/2} \fhat_{\b}}}{\norm{\lambdaab^{1/2} \fhat_{\a}}_{2}\norm{\lambdaab^{1/2} \fhat_{\b}}_{2}}=o(1).

Given
Proposition

F.1
, we can show that the
Z^{(\beta)}
have correlations that approach
\frac{1}{2}
. The intuitive reason that this correleation approaches
\frac{1}{2}
is that the contribution from
\alpha
is common. The following lemma formalizes this intuition.

Lemma F.2

(Correlation of relative differences of almost orthogonal vectors)
.

Suppose that we have
n
unit vectors
{\bm{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{x}}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\absolutevalue{\ev{\vx_i, \vx_j}}\leq\gamma
for
\gamma>0
. Then for any distinct
i,j,k\in[n]
, we have

\absolutevalue{\frac{\ev{\vx_j - \vx_i, \vx_i - \vx_k}}{\norm{\vx_j - \vx_i}\norm{\vx_i - \vx_k}} - \frac{1}{2}}\leq\frac{2\gamma}{1-\gamma}.

Proof.

For any
i\neq j
, we have
\norm{\vx_i - \vx_j}^{2}=2-2\expectationvalue{\vx_i, \vx_j}
. Hence we have

2-2\gamma\leq\norm{\vx_i - \vx_j}^{2}\leq 2+2\gamma.

Also

\displaystyle 2-2\gamma

\displaystyle\leq\norm{\vx_j - \vx_k}^{2}

\displaystyle=\norm{\vx_i - \vx_j}^{2}+\norm{\vx_i - \vx_k}^{2}-2\expectationvalue{\vx_j - \vx_i, \vx_i - \vx_k}

\displaystyle\leq 4+4\gamma-2\expectationvalue{\vx_j - \vx_i, \vx_i - \vx_k}.

Since
\norm{\vx_i - \vx_j}\geq\sqrt{2-2\gamma}
, we can rearrange and obtain that

\frac{\expectationvalue{\vx_j - \vx_i, \vx_i - \vx_k}}{\norm{\vx_j - \vx_i}\norm{\vx_i - \vx_k}}\leq\frac{1+3\gamma}{2-2\gamma}.

Similarly we can reverse the inequalities and get

2+2\gamma\geq 4-4\gamma-2\expectationvalue{\vx_j - \vx_i, \vx_i - \vx_k},

so

\frac{\expectationvalue{\vx_j - \vx_i, \vx_i - \vx_k}}{\norm{\vx_j - \vx_i}\norm{\vx_i - \vx_k}}\geq\frac{1-3\gamma}{2+2\gamma}.

∎

Plugging in the result of

\displaystyle\Tr(\mA_s^{-2} \EE[\yb\ya^\top]).

Clearly,
\mathbb{E}[{\bm{y}}_{\beta}{\bm{y}}_{\alpha}^{\top}]

Appendix G
Comparison to the straightforward non-interpolative scheme

In this section, we quickly give calculations for how well a straightforward non-interpolating scheme for learning classifiers can work asymptotically. However, a similar analysis using the tools developed to prove our main results should give a rigorous proof of the below derivation.

This scheme simply uses the sum/average of all positive training examples of a class as the vector we take an inner-product with to generate scores for classifying test points. For
m\in[k]
, define

\displaystyle\widehat{{\bm{f}}}_{m}=\sum_{i:\ell_{i}=m}{\bm{x}}^{w}_{i}.

(181)

To understand how well this will do asymptotically, it is easy to see that the for the true label-defining direction, the positive exemplars in the bi-level model will be tightly concentrating around
\sqrt{2\log k}\sqrt{\lambda_{F}}
which, keeping only the polynomial-order scaling, will be like
n^{\frac{p-q-r}{2}}
. There will be roughly
\frac{n}{k}=n^{1-t}
positive examples for every class with high probability. For simplicity, let us just look at
m=1
and consider
\frac{k}{n}\widehat{{\bm{f}}}_{1}=n^{t-1}\widehat{{\bm{f}}}_{1}
. We see

\displaystyle n^{t-1}\widehat{{\bm{f}}}_{1}[1]=\Theta(n^{\frac{p-q-r}{2}}).

(182)

For the other directions that are not true-label defining, we will just have random Gaussians. The favored directions will be Gaussian with variance
\lambda_{F}=n^{p-q-r}
while the unfavored directions will essentially be Gaussian with unit variance. By averaging over
n^{1-t}
examples, those variances will be reduced by that factor. This means that for the
s=n^{r}
favored directions, the variance of the average will be
n^{p-q-r-(1-t)}
each and for the essentially
n^{p}
unfavored directions, the variance of the average will be
n^{t-1}
each.

On a test point, we are going to take the inner product of
n^{t-1}\widehat{{\bm{f}}}_{m}
with an independent random draw of
{\bm{x}}_{\mathsf{test}}^{w}
.
For classification to succeed, we need this inner product to be dominated by the true
m
-th feature-defining direction.
When that happens, the correct label will win the comparison.
One can easily see that the contribution from the true feature-defining direction will be a Gaussian with mean 0 and variance
\lambda_{F}\cdot(n^{\frac{p-q-r}{2}})^{2}=\lambda_{F}^{2}=n^{2p-2q-2r}
.
Meanwhile, the
s
favored features will have their scaled variances sum up in the score to give a total variance of
n^{r}\cdot\lambda_{F}\cdot n^{p-q-r-(1-t)}=n^{2p-2q-r-(1-t)}
.
And finally, the unfavored features will also have their variances sum up in the score to give a total variance of
n^{p}\cdot 1\cdot n^{t-1}=n^{p+t-1}.

For the true-feature-defining direction to dominate the contamination from other favored directions, we need

\displaystyle 2p-2q-2r>2p-2q-r-(1-t)

(183)

which immediately gives the condition
t<1-r
.

For the true-feature-defining direction to dominate the contamination from other unfavored directions, we need

\displaystyle 2p-2q-2r>p+t-1

(184)

which gives the condition
t<p+1-2(q+r)
.

Here, there is no difference between regimes in which regression works or does not work. The condition for classification to asymptotically succeed is
t<\min(1-r,p+1-2(q+r))
.

Notice that when MNI regression does not work
q+r>1
, this is identical to the tight characterization given for MNI classification in (
13
). But in the regime where MNI regression
does
work
q+r<1
, this is different. For MNI classification, (
13
) tells us that we require
t<\min(1-r,p-1)
. Consider
q=0.1,r=0.5
and
p=1.1
. MNI classification can only allow
t<0.1
. Meanwhile, the non-interpolating average-of-positive-examples classifier will work as long as
t<0.5
. This demonstrates the potential for significant suboptimality (in terms of the number of distinct classes that can be learned) of MNI classifiers in this regime of benign overfitting for regression.

Appendix H
A new variant of the Hanson-Wright inequality with soft sparsity

In this section, we prove
Theorem

4.2
. First, we outline a high level idea of the proof.
The starting point of the proof is to explicitly decompose the quadratic form into diagonal and off-diagonal terms

\displaystyle{\bm{x}}^{\top}{\bm{M}}{\bm{y}}-\mathbb{E}[{\bm{x}}^{\top}{\bm{M}}{\bm{y}}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}m_{ij}X_{i}Y_{j}-\sum_{i}m_{ii}\mathbb{E}[X_{i}Y_{i}]

(185)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\sum_{i}m_{ii}(X_{i}Y_{i}-\mathbb{E}[X_{i}Y_{i}])}_{\triangleq S_{\mathrm{diag}}}+\underbrace{\sum_{i\neq j}m_{ij}X_{i}Y_{j}}_{\triangleq S_{\mathrm{offdiag}}}

(186)

where in the first line we have used the fact that for
i\neq j
,
X_{i}
and
Y_{j}
are independent and mean zero to conclude that
\mathbb{E}[X_{i}Y_{j}]=0
.

We can start with the upper tail inequality
\PP[{\bm{x}}^{\top}{\bm{M}}{\bm{y}}-\mathbb{E}[{\bm{x}}^{\top}{\bm{M}}{\bm{y}}]>t]
and conclude the lower tail inequality by replacing
{\bm{M}}
with
-{\bm{M}}
. To bound
S_{\mathrm{diag}}
and
S_{\mathrm{offdiag}}
, we will proceed by explicitly bounding the MGF and applying Chernoff’s inequality.

H.1
Diagonal terms

For the diagonal terms, we want to bound the MGF of
S_{\mathrm{diag}}=\sum_{i}m_{ii}(X_{i}Y_{i}-\mathbb{E}[X_{i}Y_{i}])
. For
\lambda^{2}<\frac{1}{2C_{\ref{c_quadratic_taylor}}K^{2}\max_{i}m_{ii}^{2}}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\exp(\lambda S_\diag)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{X_{i},Y_{i}}\exp(\lambda m_{ii} (X_iY_i - \EE[X_iY_i]))

(187)

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{Y_{i}}\mathbb{E}_{X_{i}}\quantity[\exp(\lambda m_{ii}Y_i(X_i - \EE[X_i|Y_i])) | Y_i]

(188)

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{Y_{i}}\exp(\Const{c_quadratic_taylor}\lambda^2 m_{ii}^2 K^2 Y_i^2)

(189)

where we have applied Jensen’s inequality in the second line and the subgaussian assumption on
X_{i}
conditioned on
Y_{i}
in the last line. Here,
C_{1}
is a universal positive constant relating the equivalent formulations of subgaussianity
Vershynin 2018
. Continuing with our calculation, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\exp(\lambda S_\diag)

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{Y_{i}}[1+2C_{\ref{c_quadratic_taylor}}\lambda^{2}m_{ii}^{2}K^{2}Y_{i}^{2}]

(190)

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1+2C_{\ref{c_quadratic_taylor}}\pi\lambda^{2}K^{2}m_{ii}^{2})

(191)

\displaystyle\leq\exp(2\Const{c_quadratic_taylor}\pi\lambda^2 K^2\sum_{i=1}^n m_{ii}^2).

(192)

where in the first line we have used the inequality
\exp(x)\leq 1+2x
valid for
x\leq\frac{1}{2}
, in the second line we have used the soft sparsity assumption on
Y_{i}
, and in the last line we have used the inequality
1+x\leq\exp(x)
, valid for all
x
.

Now Markov’s inequality yields for
\epsilon>0
that

\displaystyle\Pr[S_{\mathrm{diag}}>\epsilon]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}\exp(\lambda S_\diag)}{\exp(\lambda\epsilon)}

(193)

\displaystyle\leq\exp(-\lambda\epsilon+ 2\pi\Const{c_quadratic_taylor}K^2\lambda^2 \sum_{i=1}^n m_{ii}^2),

(194)

and optimizing
\lambda
in the region
\lambda^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2C_{\ref{c_quadratic_taylor}}K^{2}\max_{i}m_{ii}^{2}}
yields

\lambda=\min\quantity{\frac{\epsilon}{2\Const{c_quadratic_taylor}K^2 \pi\sum_{i=1}^n m_{ii}^2}, \frac{1}{2\Const{c_quadratic_taylor}K \max_i \abs{m_{ii}}}}.

(195)

Plugging in this value of
\lambda
into the Markov calculation yields the desired upper tail bound. We can repeat the argument with
-{\bm{M}}
to get the lower tail bound. A union bound completes the proof.

H.2
Offdiagonal terms

Following
Rudelson and Vershynin 2013
, for the offdiagonal terms we can decouple the terms in the sum. More precisely, the terms in
S_{\mathrm{offdiag}}
involving indices
i
and
j
are precisely
m_{ij}X_{i}Y_{j}+m_{ji}Y_{i}X_{j}
. The issue is that
Y_{i}
can be correlated with
X_{i}
, which complicates the behavior of this random variable. Decoupling ensures that for any
j\in[n]
we will have exactly one term which involves either
X_{j}
or
Y_{j}
, so in particular we will regain independence of the terms, allowing us to bound the MGF more easily.

Let
\quantity{\delta_i}_{i\in[n]}
denote iid Bernoulli’s with parameter
1/2
, which are independent of all other random variables.

Let

S_{\delta}\triangleq\sum_{i\neq j}m_{ij}\delta_{i}(1-\delta_{j})X_{i}Y_{j}.

Since
\mathbb{E}[\delta_{i}(1-\delta_{j})]=\frac{1}{4}
, we have

S_{\mathrm{offdiag}}=4\mathbb{E}_{\delta}[S_{\delta}],

Hence, Jensen’s inequality yields

\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}}\exp(\lambda S_\offdiag)\leq\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}},\delta}\exp(4\lambda S_\delta),

where we have used the independence of
\delta
and all other random variables. It follows that it suffices to upper bound the MGF of
S_{\delta}
.

Define the random set
\Lambda_{\delta}=\quantity{i \in[n]: \delta_i = 1}
to denote the indices selected by
\delta
. For a vector
{\bm{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
we also introduce the shorthand
{\bm{u}}_{\Lambda_{\delta}}
to denote the subvector of
{\bm{u}}
where
\delta_{i}=1
and
{\bm{u}}_{\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}}
to denote the subvector of
{\bm{u}}
where
\delta_{i}=0
.

Hence, we can rewrite
S_{\delta}\triangleq\sum_{i\in{\Lambda_{\delta}},j\in\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}}m_{ij}X_{i}Y_{j}
. For
\absolutevalue{\lambda}\leq\frac{1}{2C_{\ref{c_quadratic_taylor}}K\norm{\mM}_{2}}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\exp(\lambda S_\offdiag)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\exp(4\lambda S_\delta)

(196)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\delta}\prod_{i\in{\Lambda_{\delta}},j\in\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}_{{\Lambda_{\delta}}},{\bm{y}}_{\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}}}[\exp(\lambda m_{ij} X_i Y_j)]

(197)

Now we can use the fact that the
X_{i}
and
Y_{j}
are mean zero and independent because
i\in{\Lambda_{\delta}}
and
j\in\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}
, to show that

\displaystyle\prod_{i\in{\Lambda_{\delta}},j\in\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}_{{\Lambda_{\delta}}},{\bm{y}}_{\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}}}[\exp(\lambda m_{ij} X_i Y_j)]

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{i\in{\Lambda_{\delta}},j\in\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{y}}_{\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}}}[\exp(\Const{c_quadratic_taylor}\lambda^2 K^2 m_{ij}^2 Y_j^2)]

(198)

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{i\in{\Lambda_{\delta}},j\in\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{y}}_{\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}}}[1+2C_{\ref{c_quadratic_taylor}}\lambda^{2}K^{2}m_{ij}^{2}Y_{j}^{2})]

(199)

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{i\in{\Lambda_{\delta}},j\in\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}}(1+2\pi C_{\ref{c_quadratic_taylor}}\lambda^{2}K^{2}m_{ij}^{2}Y_{j}^{2})

(200)

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{i\in{\Lambda_{\delta}},j\in\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}}\exp(2\pi\Const{c_quadratic_taylor}\lambda^2 K^2 m_{ij}^2 Y_j^2)

(201)

\displaystyle\leq\exp(2\pi\Const{c_quadratic_taylor}\lambda^2 K^2 \norm{\mM}_F^2).

(202)

In the first line, we have used the subgaussianity of
X_{i}
; in the second line, we have used the assumption on
\lambda
, in the third line, we have used the variance bound on
Y_{j}
.

Again, we can apply Markov’s inequality and to see that for
\epsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\Pr[S_{\mathrm{diag}}>\epsilon]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}\exp(\lambda S_\diag)}{\exp(\lambda\epsilon)}

(203)

\displaystyle\leq\exp(-\lambda\epsilon+ 2\pi\Const{c_quadratic_taylor}K^2\lambda^2 \norm{\mM}_F^2),

(204)

Picking

\lambda=\min\quantity{\frac{\epsilon}{2\Const{c_quadratic_taylor}K^2 \pi\norm{\mM}_F^2}, \frac{1}{2\Const{c_quadratic_taylor}K \norm{\mM}_2}}

(205)

yields the desired result.

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}\exp(\lambda S_\diag)}{\exp(\lambda t)}

(249)

\displaystyle\leq\exp(-\lambda t + \pi\Const{c_quadratic_taylor_new}K^4\lambda^2 \sum_{i=1}^n m_{ii}^2),

(250)

and optimizing
\lambda
in the region
\absolutevalue{\lambda}\leq\frac{1}{2C_{\ref{c_moment}}eK^{2}\max_{i}\absolutevalue{m_{ii}}}
yields

\lambda=\min\quantity{\frac{t}{2\Const{c_quadratic_taylor_new}K^4 \pi\sum_{i=1}^n m_{ii}^2}, \frac{1}{2\Const{c_moment}eK^2 \max_i \abs{m_{ii}}}}.

(251)

Plugging this into the inequality yields the desired bound. We can repeat the argument with
-{\bm{M}}
to get the lower tail bound. A union bound completes the proof.
∎

Before we prove the offdiagonal bound, we prove
Lemma

J.3
.

Proof of
Lemma

J.3
.

By Lemma 2.7.7 in
[
Vershynin 2018
]
, we have
\norm{X_iY_i}_{\psi_{1}}\leq\norm{X_i}_{\psi_{2}}\norm{Y_i}_{\psi_{2}}\leq K^{2}
. Set
W_{i}\triangleq\frac{X_{i}Y_{i}}{\norm{X_iY_i}_{\psi_{1}}}
and
t_{i}=\lambda m_{ii}\norm{X_iY_i}_{\psi_{1}}
. Hence,
\norm{W_i}_{\psi_{1}}=1
. For the rest of this proof, all expectations are understood to be taken with respect to
W_{i}
without chance of confusion. By Proposition 2.7.1 in
Vershynin 2018
, this implies that there exists an absolute constant
C_{2}
such that
\mathbb{E}[\absolutevalue{W_i}^{p}]\leq C_{\ref{c_moment}}^{p}p^{p}
for all
p\geq 1
.

Now, we can use the Taylor expansion of
\exp(x)
to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\exp(t_iW_i)

\displaystyle=1+t_{i}\mathbb{E}[W_{i}]+\sum_{k\geq 2}\frac{t_{i}^{k}\mathbb{E}[W_{i}^{k}]}{k!}

(252)

\displaystyle\leq 1+t_{i}\mathbb{E}[W_{i}]+\sum_{k\geq 2}\frac{\absolutevalue{t_i}^{k}\mathbb{E}[\absolutevalue{W_i}^{k}]}{k!}

(253)

\displaystyle\leq 1+t_{i}\mathbb{E}[W_{i}]+\sum_{k\geq 2}\frac{C_{\ref{c_moment}}^{k}\absolutevalue{t_i}^{k}k^{k}}{k!}.

(254)

Now, we use the nonasymptotic version of Stirling’s formula, which is cited in Proposition 2.5.2 of
Vershynin 2018
, which says that
k!\geq\quantity(\frac{k}{e})^{k}
for
k\geq 1
. Plugging this into the above yields that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\exp(t_iW_i)

\displaystyle\leq 1+t_{i}\mathbb{E}[W_{i}]+\sum_{k\geq 2}(C_{\ref{c_moment}}\absolutevalue{t_i}e)^{k}.

(255)

Now, if
\absolutevalue{\lambda}<\frac{1}{2C_{\ref{c_moment}}eK^{2}\max_{i}\absolutevalue{m_{ii}}}
, then using the definition of
t_{i}
we see

\displaystyle C_{\ref{c_moment}}e\absolutevalue{t_i}

\displaystyle=C_{\ref{c_moment}}e\lambda\absolutevalue{m_{ii}}\norm{X_iY_i}_{\psi_{1}}

(256)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{\ref{c_moment}}e\absolutevalue{m_{ii}}\norm{X_iY_i}_{\psi_{1}}}{2C_{\ref{c_moment}}eK^{2}\max_{i}\absolutevalue{m_{ii}}}

(257)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2},

(258)

so it follows from a geometric series estimate that

\mathbb{E}\exp(t_iW_i)\leq 1+t_{i}\mathbb{E}[W_{i}]+C_{\ref{c_quadratic_taylor_new}}K^{4}\lambda^{2}m_{ii}^{2},

where
C_{3}=2C_{\ref{c_moment}}^{2}e^{2}
.

Recalling our definition of
W_{i}
and
t_{i}
yields the desired result. In particular,
t_{i}W_{i}=\lambda m_{ii}X_{i}Y_{i}
, so

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\exp(\lambda m_{ii} X_iY_i)\leq 1+\lambda m_{ii}\mathbb{E}[X_{i}Y_{i}]+C_{\ref{c_quadratic_taylor_new}}K^{4}\lambda^{2}m_{ii}^{2},

(259)

as desired.
∎

J.2
Offdiagonal terms

We now turn to the offdiagonal terms, which is Lemma 2 in
Park et al. 2022
.

Lemma J.4

(Offdiagonal concentration)
.

Assume the setting of
Theorem

4.3
.
There exists a constant
c>0
such that for any
{\bm{M}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and
t>0
, we have

\displaystyle\PP\quantity[\abs{S_{\offdiag}}> t]\leq 2\exp(-c\min\qty{\frac{t^2}{K^4\pi\sum_{i \neq j} m_{ij}^2}, \frac{t}{K^2\norm{\mM}_2}})

(260)

As before, we will achieve this concentration inequality by explicitly bounding the MGF. The high level idea for this is summarized in Section 2. Following
Rudelson and Vershynin 2013
, we decouple the terms in the sum. More precisely, the terms in
S_{\mathrm{offdiag}}
involving indices
i
and
j
are precisely
m_{ij}\gamma_{j}X_{i}Y_{j}+m_{ji}\gamma_{i}Y_{i}X_{j}
. The issue is that
\gamma_{i}
and
Y_{i}
can be correlated with
X_{i}
, which complicates the behavior of this random variable. Decoupling ensures that for any
j\in[n]
we will have exactly one term which involves either
X_{j}
or
Y_{j}
, so in particular we will regain independence of the terms. We can then reduce the problem to standard normals and integrate out both the normal variables and the Bernoulli mask
{\bm{\gamma}}
.

Proof.

We begin with the same steps as in the diagonal case. Recall

S_{\mathrm{offdiag}}\triangleq\sum_{i\neq j}m_{ij}\gamma_{j}X_{i}Y_{j}

(261)

Here
\lambda
is a parameter to be optimized later; we consider the MGF

\mathbb{E}\exp(\lambda S_\offdiag).

First, we decouple the terms.

Decoupling the terms:

Let
\quantity{\delta_i}_{i\in[n]}
denote iid Bernoulli’s with parameter
1/2
, which are independent of all other random variables.

Let

S_{\delta}\triangleq\sum_{i\neq j}m_{ij}\delta_{i}(1-\delta_{j})\gamma_{j}X_{i}Y_{j}.

Since
\mathbb{E}[\delta_{i}(1-\delta_{j})]=\frac{1}{4}
, we have

S_{\mathrm{offdiag}}=4\mathbb{E}_{\delta}[S_{\delta}],

Hence, Jensen’s inequality yields

\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}},{\bm{\gamma}}}\exp(\lambda S_\offdiag)\leq\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}},{\bm{\gamma}},\delta}\exp(4\lambda S_\delta),

where we have used the independence of
\delta
and all other random variables. It follows that it suffices to upper bound the MGF of
S_{\delta}
.

Define the random set
\Lambda_{\delta}=\quantity{i \in[n]: \delta_i = 1}
to denote the indices selected by
\delta
. For a vector
{\bm{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
we also introduce the shorthand
{\bm{u}}_{\Lambda_{\delta}}
to denote the subvector of
{\bm{u}}
where
\delta_{i}=1
and
{\bm{u}}_{\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}}
to denote the subvector of
{\bm{u}}
where
\delta_{i}=0
.

We can rewrite

S_{\delta}=\sum_{i\in\Lambda_{\delta},j\in\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}}m_{ij}\gamma_{j}X_{i}Y_{j}=\sum_{j\in\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}}Y_{j}\quantity(\sum_{i \in\Lambda_\delta} \gamma_j m_{ij}X_i).

Note that
S_{\delta}
only depends on
\delta
,
{\bm{x}}_{{\Lambda_{\delta}}}
,
{\bm{\gamma}}_{\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}}
, and
{\bm{y}}_{\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}}
. Hence we have

\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}},{\bm{\gamma}},\delta}\exp(4\lambda S_\delta)=\mathbb{E}_{\delta}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\gamma}}_{\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}}}\quantity[\EE_{\vx_{\lamdel}}\qty[\EE_{\vy_{\lamdelc}}[\exp(4\lambda S_\delta) | \vx_{\lamdel}, \vgamma_{\lamdelc}, \delta]]]

(262)

Recall that by assumption,
\gamma_{j}
is independent of
Y_{j}
. Furthermore, since
i\neq j
we have that
Y_{j}
is independent of
X_{i}
. Also,
Y_{j}
is a mean zero subgaussian random variable with subgaussian norm
K
. So if we condition on
{\bm{\gamma}}_{\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}}
,
{\bm{x}}_{{\Lambda_{\delta}}}
, and
\delta
, we find that
S_{\delta}=f({\bm{\gamma}}_{\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}},{\bm{x}}_{{\Lambda_{\delta}}},\delta)
is (conditionally) a mean zero subgaussian random variable.

Let

\sigma_{\delta,{\bm{\gamma}}}^{2}\triangleq\sum_{j\in\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}}\quantity(\sum_{i \in\Lambda_\delta} \gamma_j m_{ij}X_i)^{2}.

(263)

Note that
\sigma_{\delta,{\bm{\gamma}}}^{2}
is a random variable which depends on
{\bm{\gamma}}_{\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}}
,
{\bm{x}}_{{\Lambda_{\delta}}}
, and
\delta
, which are all independent of
{\bm{y}}
.
Then Proposition 2.6.1 in
Vershynin 2018
implies that
S_{\delta}
conditionally has subgaussian norm which satisfies

\displaystyle\norm{S_\delta}_{\psi_{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\ref{c_S_delta_subgaussian}}\sum_{j\in\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}}\norm{Y_j \sum_{i \in\lamdelc} \gamma_j m_{ij} X_i}_{\psi_{2}}^{2}

(264)

\displaystyle\leq C_{\ref{c_S_delta_subgaussian}}K^{2}\sum_{j\in\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}}\quantity(\sum_{i \in\lamdelc} \gamma_j m_{ij} X_i)^{2}

(265)

\displaystyle\leq C_{\ref{c_S_delta_subgaussian}}K^{2}\sigma_{\delta,{\bm{\gamma}}}^{2},

(266)

with
C_{4}
being a positive constant.

Now, since
S_{\delta}
is a mean zero subgaussian random variable, Proposition 2.5.2(e) of
Vershynin 2018
yields that

\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{y}}_{\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}}}\quantity[\exp(4\lambda S_\delta) | \vgamma_{\lamdelc}, \vx_{\lamdel}, \delta]\leq\exp(\Const{c_S_delta_subgaussian}K^2\lambda^2 \sigma_{\delta, \vgamma}^2).

(267)

Note that the RHS of
Eq.

267
is a random variable.
Now define

E_{\delta}\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\gamma}}_{\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}}}\quantity[\EE_{\vx_{\lamdel}}\qty[\exp(\Const{c_S_delta_subgaussian}K^2\lambda^2 \sigma_{\delta, \vgamma}^2)|\delta]],

(268)

which is well-defined because given
\delta
,
{\bm{x}}_{{\Lambda_{\delta}}}
is independent of
{\bm{\gamma}}_{\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}}
. Hence taking expectations with respect to
{\bm{\gamma}}_{\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}}
and
{\bm{x}}_{{\Lambda_{\delta}}}
in
Eq.

267
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\gamma}}_{\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}}}\quantity[\EE_{\vx_{\lamdel}}\qty[\EE_{\vy_{\lamdelc}}\qty[\exp(4\lambda S_\delta) | \vgamma_{\lamdelc}, \vx_{\lamdel}, \delta]]]\leq E_{\delta}.

(269)

This exact reduction is used in
Rudelson and Vershynin 2013
, and we turn to bounding
E_{\delta}
.

Reducing to normal random variables:

The RHS of
Eq.

267
is strikingly similar to the MGF of a gaussian. We can use this to our advantage to reduce to the normal case. In particular, let
{\bm{g}}=(G_{1},\ldots,G_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
G_{i}\sim N(0,1)
and independent of all other random variables. Then define the random variable

Z=\sum_{j\in\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}}G_{j}\quantity(\sum_{i \in\lamdel} \gamma_j m_{ij}X_i),

so conditioned on
{\bm{\gamma}}_{\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}},{\bm{x}}_{{\Lambda_{\delta}}},\delta
, we have
Z\sim N(0,\sigma_{\delta,{\bm{\gamma}}}^{2})
.
Hence, the conditional MGF

\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{g}}}[\exp(sZ)|{\bm{\gamma}}_{\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}},{\bm{x}}_{{\Lambda_{\delta}}},\delta]=\exp(s^2 \sigma_{\delta, \vgamma}^2).

(270)

If we select
s
such that
s^{2}=C_{\ref{c_S_delta_subgaussian}}K^{2}\lambda^{2}
, then we can match the RHS of
Eq.

267
, which replaces the
Y_{j}
’s with
G_{j}
’s.

Hence, taking expectations over
{\bm{\gamma}}_{\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}}
and
{\bm{x}}_{{\Lambda_{\delta}}}
in
Eq.

270
gives
E_{\delta}
on the RHS. Plugging this into
Eq.

269
yields

\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\gamma}}_{\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}},{\bm{x}}_{{\Lambda_{\delta}}}}\quantity[\EE_{\vy_{\lamdelc}}[ \exp(4\lambda S_\delta) | \vgamma_{\lamdelc}, \vx_{\lamdel}, \delta]]\leq\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\gamma}}_{\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}},{\bm{x}}_{{\Lambda_{\delta}}}}\quantity[\EE_{\vg}[ \exp(sZ) | \vgamma_{\lamdelc}, \vx_{\lamdel}, \delta]]

Now, since
{\bm{g}}
is independent of all other random variables, we have

E_{\delta}=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\gamma}}_{\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}}}\quantity[\EE_{\vx_{\lamdel}}\qty[\EE_{\vg}[\exp(sZ) | \vgamma_{\lamdelc}, \vx_{\lamdel}, \delta]]]=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\gamma}}_{\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}}}\quantity[\EE_{\vg}\qty[\EE_{x_{\lamdel}}[\exp(sZ) | \vg, \vgamma_{\lamdelc}, \delta]]]

(271)

Now, let us obtain an upper bound on the innermost term on the RHS of
Eq.

271
.
Swapping the order of summation in
Z
yields

Z=\sum_{i\in{\Lambda_{\delta}}}X_{i}\quantity(\sum_{j \in\lamdelc} G_j \gamma_j m_{ij}).

Since we don’t condition on
{\bm{\gamma}}_{{\Lambda_{\delta}}}
, and
{\bm{g}}
and
\delta
are independent of
{\bm{x}}
, we have by assumption that each
X_{i}
in the sum is still a centered subgaussian variable of subgaussian norm
K
.
We can thus repeat the same argument we used for
Y_{j}
that culminated in
Eq.

267
, except this time we condition on
{\bm{g}}
,
{\bm{\gamma}}_{\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}}
, and
\delta
. In particular, there exists a positive constant
C_{5}
such that

\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}_{{\Lambda_{\delta}}}}[\exp(sZ)|{\bm{g}},{\bm{\gamma}}_{\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}},\delta]\leq\exp(\Const{c_Z_subgaussian}K^4 \lambda^2 \sum_{i \in\lamdel} \qty(\sum_{j \in\lamdelc} G_j \gamma_j m_{ij})^2).

Plugging this into
Eq.

271
, we get

E_{\delta}\leq\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\gamma}}_{\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}}}\quantity[\EE_{\vg}\qty[\exp(\Const{c_Z_subgaussian}K^4 \lambda^2 \sum_{i \in\lamdel} \qty(\sum_{j \in\lamdelc} G_j \gamma_j m_{ij})^2)]]

Integrating out the normal variables:

Define the projection matrices
{\bm{P}}_{\delta}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
to denote coordinate projection onto the indices in
{\Lambda_{\delta}}
and
\bm{\Gamma}=\mathrm{diag}({\bm{\gamma}})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
to denote the masking action of
{\bm{\gamma}}
. Define

{\bm{B}}_{\delta,{\bm{\gamma}}}\triangleq{\bm{P}}_{\delta}{\bm{M}}({\bm{I}}_{n}-{\bm{P}}_{\delta})\bm{\Gamma}.

(272)

When
{\bm{B}}_{\delta,{\bm{\gamma}}}
acts on
{\bm{g}}
, it first removes indices
j
where
\gamma_{j}=0
or
j\not\in\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}
. Then
{\bm{M}}
acts on the remaining vector, and finally it kills those indices
i
such that
i\not\in{\Lambda_{\delta}}
. Hence,

\sum_{i\in{\Lambda_{\delta}}}\quantity(\sum_{j \in\lamdelc} G_j \gamma_j m_{ij})^{2}=\norm{\mB_{\delta, \vgamma} \vg}_{2}^{2}

(273)

Let
\sigma_{i}
,
i\in[n]
denote the singular values of
{\bm{B}}_{\delta,{\bm{\gamma}}}
.

Since the spectral norm of projection matrices are at most 1, we can use the definition of
{\bm{B}}_{\delta,{\bm{\gamma}}}
in
Eq.

272
to conclude that

\max_{i\in[n]}\sigma_{i}=\norm{\mB_{\delta, \vgamma}}_{2}\leq\norm{\mM}_{2}.

(274)

On the other hand, since
\gamma_{j}^{2}=\gamma_{j}
we have

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}^{2}=\Tr(\mB_{\delta, \vgamma}^2)=\sum_{i\in{\Lambda_{\delta}}}\sum_{j\in\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}}\gamma_{j}m_{ij}^{2}

(275)

By the rotation invariance of
{\bm{g}}\sim N(0,{\bm{I}}_{n})
, we have that
\norm{\mB_{\delta, \vgamma} \vg}_{2}^{2}
is distributed identically to
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}^{2}g_{i}^{2}
. Hence

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{g}}}\quantity[\exp(\Const{c_Z_subgaussian}K^4\lambda^2 \sum_{i \in\lamdel} \qty(\sum_{j \in\lamdelc} G_j \gamma_j m_{ij})^2) | \vgamma_{\delta^c}, \delta]

(276)

\displaystyle\quad\quad=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{g}}}\quantity[\exp(\Const{c_Z_subgaussian}K^4\lambda^2 \sum_{i=1}^n \sigma_i^2 g_i^2) | \vgamma_{\delta^c}, \delta]

(277)

\displaystyle\quad\quad=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{g}}}\quantity[\exp(\Const{c_Z_subgaussian}K^4\lambda^2 \sigma_i^2 g_i^2) | \vgamma_{\delta^c}, \delta].

(278)

Recall the folklore fact that
g_{i}^{2}
is a
\chi^{2}
distribution with 1 degree of freedom whose MGF satisfies

\mathbb{E}\exp(sg_i^2)\leq(1-2s)^{-1/2}\leq\exp(2s)\quad\text{ for }s<\frac{1}{4};

(279)

this is used in
Rudelson and Vershynin 2013
. For
\lambda^{2}<\frac{1}{4C_{\ref{c_Z_subgaussian}}K^{4}\norm{\mM}_{2}^{2}}
, we have

C_{\ref{c_Z_subgaussian}}K^{4}\lambda^{2}\sigma_{i}^{2}<\frac{C_{\ref{c_Z_subgaussian}}K^{4}\sigma_{i}^{2}}{4C_{\ref{c_Z_subgaussian}}K^{4}\norm{\mM}_{2}^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{4},

(280)

due to
Eq.

274
.
Hence, we can use the
\chi^{2}
MGF bound to obtain

\displaystyle E_{\delta}

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\gamma_{\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}}}\quantity[ \EE_{\vg}\qty[2\exp(\Const{c_Z_subgaussian}K^4\lambda^2 \sigma_i^2)| \vgamma_{\lamdelc}, \delta]]

(281)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\gamma_{\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}}}\quantity[ \exp(2\Const{c_Z_subgaussian}K^4\lambda^2 \sum_{i \in\lamdel} \sum_{j \in\lamdelc} \gamma_j m_{ij}^2) | \delta],

(282)

where in the last line we used
Eq.

275
.

Integrating out the Bernoulli mask:

Now, we can use Lemma 5 in
Park et al. 2022
.

Lemma J.5

(MGF bound for Bernoulli random variables)
.

For
0<s<\frac{1}{2\norm{\mM}_{2}^{2}}
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\gamma}}_{\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}}}\quantity[\exp(s\sum_{i \in\lamdel} \sum_{j \in\lamdelc} \gamma_jm_{ij}^2) | \delta]\leq\exp(2sp\sum_{i\neq j}m_{ij}^2).

The proof of this lemma uses similar proof techniques, so we defer its proof to the end of the section. The key step is to use the inequality
e^{x}\leq 1+2x
for
x\leq\frac{1}{2}
and
1+x\leq e^{x}
for all
x
. This allows us to pass the expectation into the exponential function and replace
\gamma_{j}
with
p
.

For
\lambda^{2}<\frac{1}{4C_{\ref{c_Z_subgaussian}}K^{4}\norm{\mM}_{2}^{2}}
, we can take
s=2C_{\ref{c_Z_subgaussian}}K^{4}\lambda^{2}
and verify that
s<\frac{1}{2\norm{\mM}_{2}^{2}}
. Hence the lemma yields that

E_{\delta}\leq\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\gamma}}_{\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}}}\quantity[\exp(2\Const{c_Z_subgaussian}K^4 \lambda^2\sum_{i \in\lamdel} \sum_{j \in\lamdelc} \gamma_jm_{ij}^2) | \delta]\leq\exp(4\Const{c_Z_subgaussian}K^4 \lambda^2\pi\sum_{i\neq j}m_{ij}^2)

(283)

Finishing off the proof:

Let us collect our requirements on
\lambda
. To use the
\chi^{2}
bound to obtain
Eq.

282
and then bound it using
Lemma

J.5
, we required
\absolutevalue{\lambda}\leq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{C_{\ref{c_Z_subgaussian}}}K^{2}\norm{\mM}_{2}}
.

Hence, for
\absolutevalue{\lambda}\leq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{C_{\ref{c_Z_subgaussian}}}K^{2}\norm{\mM}_{2}}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\exp(\lambda S_\offdiag)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\delta}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\gamma}}_{\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}}}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}_{{\Lambda_{\delta}}}}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{y}}_{\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}}}\quantity[\exp(4\lambda S_{\delta})|\vgamma_{\lamdelc}, \vx_{\lamdel}, \delta]

(284)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\delta}E_{\delta}

(285)

\displaystyle\leq\exp(4C_2\lambda^2\pi\sum_{i \neq j} m_{ij}^2).

(286)

where in the first line we have used
Eq.

262
, in the second line we have used
Eq.

269
, and in the last line we have used
Eq.

283
.

Applying Markov as in the diagonal case yields the desired result.
∎

We now prove
Lemma

J.5
.

Proof of
Lemma

J.5
.

Since all random variables are independent across
j
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\gamma}}_{\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}}}\quantity[\exp(s\sum_{i \in\lamdel} \sum_{j \in\lamdelc} \gamma_jm_{ij}^2) | \delta]

\displaystyle=\prod_{j\in\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\gamma}}_{\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}}}\quantity[\exp(s \gamma_j\sum_{i \in\lamdel} m_{ij}^2) | \delta]

(287)

\displaystyle=\prod_{j\in\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}}\quantity[(1-\pi) + \pi\exp(s \sum_{i \in\lamdel} m_{ij}^2)].

(288)

We use the approximation
e^{x}\leq 1+2x
, which holds for
0\leq x\leq\frac{1}{2}
and the inequality
1+x\leq e^{x}
which holds for all
x
. In order for us to apply these inequalities, we need to check that
s\sum_{i\in{\Lambda_{\delta}}}m_{ij}^{2}<\frac{1}{2}
. Indeed, note that

\sum_{i\in{\Lambda_{\delta}}}m_{ij}^{2}\leq\max_{j}\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{ij}^{2}\leq\norm{\mM}_{2}^{2},

(289)

so for
s<\frac{1}{2\norm{\mM}_{2}^{2}}
we have

s\sum_{i\in{\Lambda_{\delta}}}m_{ij}^{2}<\frac{\norm{\mM}_{2}^{2}}{2\norm{\mM}_{2}^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{2}.

(290)

Hence, we have

\displaystyle\prod_{j\in\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}}\quantity[(1-\pi) + \pi\exp(s \sum_{i \in\lamdel} m_{ij}^2)]

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{j\in\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}}\quantity(1 + 2s p\sum_{i \in\lamdel} m_{ij}^2)

(291)

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{j\in\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}}\exp(2s\pi\sum_{i \in\lamdel} m_{ij}^2)

(292)

\displaystyle\leq\exp(2s \pi\sum_{j \in\lamdelc} \sum_{i \in\lamdel} m_{ij}^2)

(293)

\displaystyle\leq\exp(2s \pi\sum_{i \neq j} m_{ij}^2).

(294)

This proves that

\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\gamma}}_{\Lambda_{\delta}^{c}}}\quantity[\exp(s\sum_{i \in\lamdel} \sum_{j \in\lamdelc} \gamma_jm_{ij}^2) | \delta]\leq\exp(2s\pi\sum_{i \neq j} m_{ij}^2),

(295)

irrespective of
\delta
, as desired.
∎

Appendix K
Alternative framing of bi-level ensemble

In this section we provide an alternative exposition for the bi-level ensemble, which may be more intuitive for some readers.

The high level conceptual picture of the setup is as follows. The learner simply observes high dimensional jointly Gaussian zero-mean features with some unknown covariance
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. It then performs min-norm interpolation of (essentially) one-hot-encoded labels to learn the score functions for the
k
different classes. These score functions are used at test-time to do multiclass classification.

Note that the learning algorithm has no knowledge of
\Sigma
, nor does it even know that the features are jointly Gaussian — all it has is the training data. For analysis purposes, we parameterize
\Sigma
. In the spirit of spiked covariance models, where a low-dimensional subspace has higher variance, we study the case that the eigenvalues of
\Sigma
follow the simplified bi-level model parameterized by
(p,q,r)
.

The bi-level model stipulates that (i) the number of features is
d=n^{p}
and (ii) there are two discrete variance levels for the Gaussian features. The higher variance component resides in a low-dimensional subspace of dimension
s=n^{r}
