A High Dimensional Model for Adversarial Training: Geometry and Trade-Offs
Kasimir Tanner, Matteo Vilucchio, Bruno Loureiro, Florent Krzakala

Abstract
This work investigates adversarial training in the context of margin-based linear classifiers in the high-dimensional regime where the dimension d d and the number of data points n n diverge with a fixed ratio α = n / d \alpha=n/d . We introduce a tractable mathematical model where the interplay between the data and adversarial attacker geometries can be studied, while capturing the core phenomenology observed in the adversarial robustness literature. Our main theoretical contribution is an exact asymptotic description of the sufficient statistics for the adversarial empirical risk minimiser, under generic convex and non-increasing losses. Our result allow us to precisely characterise which directions in the data are associated with a higher generalisation/robustness trade-off, as defined by a robustness and a usefulness metric. In particular, we unveil the existence of directions which can be defended without penalising accuracy. Finally, we show the advantage of defending non-robust features during training, identifying a uniform protection as an inherently effective defence mechanism.



I
Introduction

The susceptibility of machine learning models to adversarial attacks — subtle yet strategically crafted data perturbations — has been an ongoing concern for various machine learning models, from linear classifiers to deep neural networks.
In particular, this vulnerability is intrinsic to margin-based classifiers
ilyas_adversarial_2019
.
Also in image classification, seemingly innocuous modifications, like tiny stickers on road signs, can dramatically mislead models that otherwise exhibit strong generalisation
PavlitskaLambingZoellner2023_1000163579
.

The problem has been theoretically studied in linear models where solutions could be obtained analytically. Previous studies were focusing on the fundamental limits of the trade-off between adversarial and generalisation errors
javanmard_precise_tradeoffs_2020
or on including a non trivial data covariance and comparing the performances of adversarial training to Bayes optimal errors
taheri_asymptotic_2021
.

Nonetheless a comprehensive understanding of how structure defines feature types — robust, useful, or both — affect model performance is still developing.
The interplay of the data structure with attack and training geometries remains a particularly fertile ground for investigation, with potential implications for enhancing adversarial robustness and developing more effective protection methods.

In this paper, we introduce a structured, high-dimensional model for studying adversarial classification under margin-based classifiers. Our
main contributions
are threefold:

•

We introduce a mathematically tractable model for investigating the interplay between the data, attack and defence geometries. Despite its simplicity, we show our model is rich enough to capture the key phenomenology observed in practical adversarial training setups.

•

We show that the relevant statistical properties of the adversarial empirical risk minimiser can be exactly characterised in terms of a finite set of sufficient statistics in the high-dimensional proportional limit where the number of samples and covariate dimension diverge at a constant rate.

•

Finally, we leverage our result to investigate the interplay between data and attack geometry in the effectiveness of adversarial training. In particular, we show how directional attacks can be decomposed in directions leading to a robustness/generalisation trade-off and directions that can be successfully defended without sacrificing accuracy.

II
Setting Specification

Consider a supervised binary classification problem with training data
\mathcal{D}=\quantity{(\x_\datidx, y_\datidx)}_{i=1}^{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\{-1,+1\}
. In the following, we focus on a generalised estimation problem, where for each
i=1,\cdots,n
we assume the covariates are independently drawn from
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}\quantity(\boldsymbol{0}, \Sigmax)
and the labels are drawn as:

\displaystyle y_{i}\sim\mathbb{P}(y|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}).

(1)

for a fixed parameter
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}\quantity(\boldsymbol{0}, \Sigmatheta)

Although our theoretical results in

P(y|z)
, for concreteness the discussion in

\mathbb{P}\left(y|z\right)=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\erfc\left(-\nicefrac{{z}}{{\sqrt{2}\tau}}\right)
, where the parameter
\tau>0
control the noise level. In particular, note that for
\tau\to 0
we have
P(y|z)=\delta(y-\operatorname{sign}(z))
.

Our choice of Gaussian covariates echoes the recent findings in Gaussian universality
Goldt_2021
;
montanari2022universality
;
pmlr-v162-wei22a
;
gerace2023gaussian

suggesting their broad scope in the context of high-dimensional generalised linear estimation, often mirroring real-world datasets.

Given the training data
\mathcal{D}
, our goal in the following is to investigate the capacity of margin-based linear classifiers:

\hat{y}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\boldsymbol{x})=\operatorname{sign}\quantity(\frac{\what^\top\x}{\sqrt{d}})\,,

(2)

in robustly and efficiently classifying the data under adversarial attacks, where
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathcal{D})
is an estimator that has been learned from the training data data.

The problem introduced above is often refereed to as a
teacher-student
setting, and is widespread in the high-dimensional statistics literature, e.g.
doi:10.1073/pnas.1810420116
;
taheri_asymptotic_2021
;
Clarte_uncertanty
;
clarte_double_descent
.

In machine learning, a pivotal concern is evaluating the extent to which a model generalises beyond its training data. The cornerstone metric for this assessment is the
generalisation error
defined as

E_{\mathrm{gen}}=\mathbb{E}_{y,\boldsymbol{x}}\quantity[
\mathbb{1}(y \neq\hat{y} (\what, \x))
]

(3)

where the expectation is taken over input-output pairs generated using the same
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
as in training.

Our focus is to study the consequences of an adversary attacking the student estimate
\hat{y}
. The adversary can manipulate the data to fool the student. Given an input sample
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
, the adversary aims to find a perturbation
\boldsymbol{\upsilon}_{i}
such that
y(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\neq\hat{y}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\boldsymbol{x}_{i}+\boldsymbol{\upsilon}_{i})
, which leads the student to wrongly classify the sample.

The efficacy of such attacks hinges on the adversary’s knowledge. We focus on a
white-box
adversary who has access to the student estimate
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, the dataset
\mathcal{D}
and possibly to the true teacher parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
.
Additionally the allowed perturbations must have a
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\upsilon}}
-induced norm smaller or equal than an attack strength, beyond which an attack could be identified —
\norm{\boldsymbol{\upsilon}}_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\upsilon}}^{-2}}\leq\varepsilon_{g}
.
This model generalises the case considered in
javanmard_precise_tradeoffs_2020
;
taheri_asymptotic_2021
where they only consider the cases of bounded attacks in
\ell_{2}
norm.

Introducing this attack geometry is crucial to distinguish features that are easier or harder to attack. Often, we normalise
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\upsilon}}
allowing us to interpret the global strength of the attack to be proportional to
\varepsilon_{g}
among different geometries.

Given these adversarial dynamics, we introduce the
adversarial generalisation error
. This metric quantifies the student’s performance under adversarial attack

E_{\mathrm{adv}}=\mathbb{E}_{y,\boldsymbol{x}}\quantity[
\max_{\norm{\vecdelta}_{\Sigmaupsilon^{-2}} \leq\advgencost} \mathbb{1}(y \neq\hat{y} (\what, \x+ \vecdelta))
]\,,

(4)

where the expectation is taken over pairs of input-output generated with the same
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
used during training.
Notably, this adversarial generalisation error is an extension of the standard generalisation error, with the latter being the special case where
E_{\mathrm{gen}}=E_{\mathrm{adv}}(\varepsilon_{g}=0)
.

The current definition of adversarial error has a draw back with respect to real adversarial attacks, it allows for attacks that fool both the student and the teacher. For this reason we propose the
class-preserving adversarial error
, which we define as

E_{\mathrm{CP}}=\mathbb{E}_{y,\boldsymbol{x}}\quantity[
\max_{
\substack{
\norm{\vecdelta}_{\Sigmaupsilon^{-2}} \leq\advgencost\\
y \wstar^\top(\x+ \vecdelta) > 0
}
} \mathbb{1}(y \neq\hat{y} (\what, \x+ \vecdelta) )
]\,,

(5)

where a given attack is not allowed to fool the teacher. We achieve this by constraining the attack strength whenever it would fool the teacher.

II.1
Empirical Risk Minimisation and Adversarial Case

To estimate a ‘student’ model with minimal (adversarial) generalisation error, our goal is to optimise the metrics defined in
Eqs.

3
and
4
by choosing
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
. Typically, this optimisation is approached by substituting these metrics with a convex surrogate and replacing the expectation with an empirical mean over the available dataset. This method, known as Empirical Risk Minimization (ERM), is a cornerstone in machine learning for parameter estimation
vapnik_principles_1991
.
This approach is adapted in the context of training neural networks robust to adversarial attacks, starting with the adversarial generalisation error as the primary metric.
Formally, the ERM estimated weights
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, are the minimiser of the following empirical risk function

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\max_{\norm{\vecdelta_\datidx}_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{-2}}\leq\varepsilon_{t}}g\quantity(y_\datidx\frac{\w^\top\qty(\x_\datidx+ \vecdelta_\datidx)}{\sqrt{d}})+r(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,,

(6)

where
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}
is a metric chosen for perturbations and
r(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is a regularisation term. We will normalise
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}
in the same way as
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\upsilon}}
.

By opting for a decreasing convex surrogate
g
, we can simplify the inner maximisation problem. For each sample
i=1,...n
, we have a binary label
y_{i}\in\quantity{\pm 1}
, and thus the optimal perturbation
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{i}
is given by
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{i}=-y_{i}\varepsilon_{t}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\boldsymbol{\theta}/\norm{\w}_{2}
. Having solved the inner maximisation, the equivalent risk function to minimise is

\sum_{i=1}^{n}g\quantity(
y_\datidx\frac{\w^\top\x_\datidx}{\sqrt{d}}
- \advtrainingcost\frac{\w^\top\Sigmadelta\w}{\sqrt{d} \, \norm{\w}_2}
)+r(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,.

(7)

In the rest of the paper, we fix the function
g(x)=\log( 1 + \exp(-x))
to be the logistic loss. While our framework is versatile enough to accommodate various convex regularisation functions for
r(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, our analysis specifically focuses on
\ell_{2}
regularisation. We choose
r(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{\lambda}{2}\norm{\w}_{2}^{2}
due to its prevalent use in machine learning.

Our analysis primarily centres on examining the behaviour of (adversarial) generalisation error in high-dimensional scenarios. Specifically, we investigate settings where both the dimension
d
and the number of training samples
n
are large
d,n\to\infty
, whilst maintaining finite sample complexity
\alpha=n/d
. We refer to this limit as the
high-dimensional proportional limit
.

II.2
Block Feature Data Model

The model is inspired by the discourse on feature categorisation in adversarial contexts, notably distinguishing between useful and robust features. Pioneering work in this area
ilyas_adversarial_2019
posits that adversarial vulnerability increases with highly predictive yet non-robust features, a scenario frequently encountered in image classification tasks.
On the one hand, usefulness is a measure of the correlation between the output and the feature. On the other hand, robustness is a measure of the correlation between the output and the given feature when an attack is performed.

More formally, we define usefulness by

\mathcal{U}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x},y}[y\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}]\,,

(8)

and robustness by

\mathcal{R}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x},y}\quantity[
\inf_{\norm{\vecdelta}_{\Sigmaupsilon^{-2}} \leq\advgencost} y \wstar^\top( \x+ \vecdelta)
]\,.

(9)

These metrics can also be defined empirically in terms of the student estimate and can be used to find non-robust features as we demonstrate in
Appendix

G
.

The Block Feature Model

While our theoretical results stated in the next section hold for a wide array of data models, we want to study adversarial robustness in a reductionist spirit. To achieve this, we propose the following Block Feature Model (BFM), which allows to systematically vary feature usefulness and robustness to understand their impact on (adversarial) generalisation metrics.

For a given dimension
d
we define
k\leq d
blocks with individual sub-dimensions
\quantity{d_\blockidx}_{\ell=1}^{k}
satisfying
\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}d_{\ell}=d
.
This allows us to write the quantities of interest as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{x}}}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{blockdiag}\quantity( \psi_1 \mathbb{1}_{d_1}, \psi_2 \mathbb{1}_{d_2}, \cdots, \psi_k \mathbb{1}_{d_k} )\,,

(10)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{blockdiag}\quantity( \Delta_1 \mathbb{1}_{d_1}, \Delta_2 \mathbb{1}_{d_2}, \cdots, \Delta_k \mathbb{1}_{d_k} )\,,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\upsilon}}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{blockdiag}\quantity( \Upsilon_1 \mathbb{1}_{d_1}, \Upsilon_2 \mathbb{1}_{d_2}, \cdots, \Upsilon_k \mathbb{1}_{d_k} )\,,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{blockdiag}\quantity( t_1 \mathbb{1}_{d_1}, t_2 \mathbb{1}_{d_2}, \cdots, t_k \mathbb{1}_{d_k} )\,,

where each
\psi_{\ell},\Delta_{\ell},t_{\ell}
and
\Upsilon_{\ell}
is greater than zero for each
\ell=1,\dots k
to preserve the positive definiteness. Notice, that this model can easily be extended to allow for power-law distributions on the eigenvalues by considering
d_{\ell}=1
and
\psi_{\ell}=\ell^{-\beta_{\psi}}
for some exponent
\beta_{\psi}
.

The BFM allows for the expression of artificial datasets capturing the simplest definitions of structure, whilst capturing the intricacies of power-law distributed data, as can be found in images
wainwright_scale_1999
;
simoncelli_natural_2001
.

Additionally, we define the Strong Weak Feature Model (SWFM) as a special case of the BFM. In the SWFM, we only consider two blocks
k=2
where the relative sizes are tuned as
d_{\ell}/d\to\phi_{\ell}\in(0,1)
.

E_{\mathrm{adv}}=E_{\mathrm{gen}}(\vartheta,\mathcal{U}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}})+\int_{0}^{\varepsilon_{g}\frac{A}{\sqrt{q}\sqrt{N}}}f(\xi;\vartheta,\mathcal{U}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}})\differential{\xi}

(20)

where we introduce the variable
\vartheta=m/\sqrt{\rho q}
, directly related to the angle between the teacher weights
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
and the student estimate
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
and define
f(\xi;\vartheta)
to be a positive function for
\xi\in[0,+\infty)
and
\forall\vartheta,\,\mathcal{U}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}
.

On the one hand, the generalisation error is a monotonically decreasing function of
\vartheta
. In other words, to improve generalisation error, it is best to align well with
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
.
On the other hand, the boundary error inversely depends on the angle: for a fixed attack strength, the boundary error is a decreasing function of the angle as the integral with fixed bounds increases.
To reduce the boundary error, adversarial training must choose an angle such that attacked samples are perturbed with respect to the student estimate, but not the teacher estimate.
This property hurts generalisation, and is at the heart of the trade-off. In summary, a worse student estimate
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
decreases boundary error at the cost of generalisation error, as we depict in
Fig.

1
.

The key insight for the rest of this paper, is that the boundary error dominates adversarial error only when the student is able to align with the teacher. Otherwise, the phenomena are driven by generalisation error.

In
Appendix

C
, we prove that the adversarial error always goes to a constant

\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}\partial_{\alpha}E_{\mathrm{adv}}=0\,,

(21)

where the limit is taken after the high-dimensional proportional limit. Additionally, we show that the constant to which
E_{\mathrm{adv}}
converges, can be zero only if
\tau=\varepsilon_{g}=0
.

In
Section

D.1
, we show that the adversarial generalisation error can be formulated in terms of Owen’s T function.

IV.1
Building Non-Robust, but Useful Features

To study the effect of robustness and usefulness on the error metrics, the simplest model to consider is a SWFM with a single block. In the following, we explain this setting with an experiment in
Fig.

2
.
As we explained in the previous section, generalisation error dominates the low sample complexity, because the angle is not yet very good. Conversely, boundary error starts to dominate once the student vector aligns with the teacher vector. Hence, adversarial training
\varepsilon_{t}>0
start to influence performance only at high sample complexities. Furthermore, we provide asymptotic results for the generalisation and boundary errors in
Appendix

C
. We interpret both in terms of

Usefulness

Usefulness controls the alignment between the student and the teacher direction. At low usefulness the plateau reached by the generalisation error
E_{\mathrm{gen}}
in terms of the sample complexity is higher than in the case of high usefulness, in which the generalisation error reaches a lower value and hence better value.
If in the high sample complexity region we have that
\vartheta\to 1
the plateau for the generalisation error is

E_{\mathrm{gen}}\!=\!E_{\mathrm{gen}}\quantity(\vartheta, \usefulmetric_{\wstar})\!\underset{\alpha\rightarrow\infty}{=}\!\frac{1}{\pi}\arccos( \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2 \rho}} \usefulmetric_{\wstar} )\,.

(22)

We note that
\rho
and
\mathcal{U}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}
are responsible for the asymptotic value of the generalisation error. The key quantities defining them are
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{x}}}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
.

Robustness

High robustness implies a lower boundary term and low robustness a high boundary term. Robust features have less samples within an attack range of the student decision boundary.
We can explain the boundary error as a function of both
\mathcal{U}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}
and
\mathcal{R}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}
, it is given by

\int_{0}^{\varepsilon_{g}\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}(\mathcal{U}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}-\mathcal{R}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}})}f(\xi;\vartheta,\mathcal{U}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}})\differential{\xi}

(23)

Increasing the intensity of adversarial training reduces the boundary error. However, this can only occur through a worsening of generalisation error. Hence, the adversarial training reduces the usefulness in favour of higher robustness.

The CP error qualitatively mimics the adversarial error at low sample complexities. In contrast, at high sample complexities, the CP error dips below the adversarial error as more and more attacks are disregarded. The dependency of the CP error on adversarial training also becomes apparent, as it influences the CP error through the angle of alignment found between the student and teacher.

IV.2
Optimal Defence Geometry

Depending on the attack geometry
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\upsilon}}
one can choose different defence geometries
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}
and ask if and for which, protection without trade-off is possible. This question translates to asking if we can minimise
A/\sqrt{qN}
without decreasing the angle
\vartheta=m/\sqrt{\rho q}
.

As explained in
Appendix

F
we can split any attack in directions orthogonal to the teacher and directions aligned with the teacher. Both have very different roles in the adversarial trade-off. The directions orthogonal to the teacher are simple to protect against. We can achieve it by reducing the norm of the weights in these directions, effectively reducing
E_{\mathrm{adv}}
to
E_{\mathrm{gen}}
. The directions aligned to
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
however, are at the heart of the trade-off and cannot be regularised away.

We demonstrate this behaviour in
Fig.

3
. We consider two kinds of attacks, an attack that is on average directed along the teacher direction and an attack that is on average directed along a direction orthogonal to the teacher. We protect against the attack geometry by setting
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\upsilon}}
and examine, how the error metrics behave as the adversarial training strength
\varepsilon_{t}
increases.

IV.3
Directional Defences on Structured Data

A popular and successful idea in the adversarial literature is to defend non-robust features more strongly. To precisely characterise this phenomenon we build a SWFM with two blocks of equal relative size. Both blocks have the same constant unity teacher vector, the first block has a much higher data variance
\psi_{1}
than the second. This construction induces high robustness for the first block of features and low robustness for the second half. We then consider three different trace-normalised protection matrices
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}
corresponding to three defensive strategies and we show the results in
Fig.

4
(Left). The attack matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\upsilon}}
is uniform and this implies that we will inevitably see a trade-off.

The defensive strategies are dependent on the robustness of the features. In
Appendix

C
we explain how our expression for the boundary term in
Eq.

23
is affected by the variance of the features. Features with a low variance increase the total boundary error and are hence non-robust, conversely, features with a high variance decrease the boundary error and are more robust.
We compare the following defence strategies in the experiment in
Fig.

4
(Left).

•

Defend the robust features
-
In the high sample complexity regime, we observe also a trade-off between boundary and generalisation error. There is no need to tune the angle so strongly, but conversely, there are still a lot of weakly protected low-robustness features. This leads to a high boundary error.
Interestingly, such adversarial training leads to increased boundary error in the small sample complexity regime.

•

Uniformly defend all the features
- The trade-off achieved by an uniform defence is only slightly weaker than the trade-off achieved by protecting the non-robust features.

•

Defend the non-robust features
- In the high sample complexity ratio, protecting the non-robust features improves adversarial generalisation error. This provokes a higher trade-off in generalisation error.

In

Realistic datasets usually have power-law distributed eigenvalues on the covariance matrix. We show two experiments with a power-law BFM in

Adversarial training can be understood as an

It is, however, not only an

By expansion of the loss function, we provide the rationale for an approximate loss to the one used in

. The form for the approximate loss is

\sum_{i=1}^{n}g\quantity( y_\datidx\frac{\w^\top\x_\datidx}{\sqrt{d}} )+\tilde{\lambda}_{1}\frac{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\boldsymbol{\theta}}{\,\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta}}}+\tilde{\lambda}_{2}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\boldsymbol{\theta}\,,

This intuitively explains the phenomenology seen previously in

parikh_boyd_proximal_algo
. We will be using the

\partial_{\omega}\mathcal{M}_{Vg(y,\cdot)}(\omega)=V^{-1}\left(\omega-\mathcal{P}_{Vg(y,\cdot)}(\omega)\right)\,.

A.6
Generalised AMP mapping of our problem

In the landscape of high-dimensional statistical inference, Approximate Message Passing (AMP) algorithms have emerged as a cornerstone for efficiently solving problems like compress sensing
Donoho_AMP_compress_sensing_2010
;
Krzakala_2012
.
At their hearts AMP algorithms are iterative schemes, inspired by the ISTA algorithm
ista_2009
, that in addition leverage the statistical properties of high-dimensional random matrices to remove correlation at each step.
A key feature of AMP algorithms is their connection with state evolution (SE), a powerful analytical tool that tracks the evolution of the AMP algorithm’s performance across iterations.
State evolution provides a set of deterministic equations that accurately predict the algorithm’s behaviour in the limit of large system sizes, thus offering deep insights into the convergence properties and asymptotic accuracy of AMP algorithms. This method has been extensively used to understand problems like the learning of Gaussian mixtures
Loureiro_gaussian_mixtures_2021
or learning curves of ensembling methods
pmlr-v162-loureiro22a
.
This kind of connection and algorithm has been also know by statistical physicist
zdeborova_inference_2016
.

In the case of estimation of an i.i.d. random vector observed through a linear transform followed by a component-wise, probabilistic (possibly nonlinear) measurement channel an optimal algorithm, called generalised approximate message passing (GAMP) and its respective SE has been introduced by
rangan_GAMP_2011
. The main lemma for the SE has been proven later by
javanmard_montanari_gamp_2013
.
Thus the idea is that the fixed point equations could be seen as the state evolution equations for a specific Generalised AMP algorithm that minimises the equivalent minimisation problem in
Eq.

96
. This way of proving the result also gives the advantage of defining an algorithm the could in principle be run to estimate the vector
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
.

In
Algorithm

1
, we present the advGAMP that will be the object of the study of this subsection. The notation
\odot
represents the component-wise product and
\oslash
the component-wise division. After an initialisation, we have to update the variables alternating by output channel variables and input channel variables until some convergence condition of the variables is reached.

This algorithm can be seen as a specialisation of a GAMP algorithm where the denoising function change at each iteration because dependant on the constants
P,N,\hat{P},\hat{N}
that are updated in Lines 8, 9, 14, 15.

Enforcing these constraints at each step guarantees that the
\hat{P},\hat{N}
(respectively Lines 14 and 15) variables will be such that they minimise the Lagrangian with respect to the primal variables. Additionally for the way the
P,N
variables are updated in
Algorithm

1
(respectively Lines 8 and 9) it is guaranteed that the definition is satisfied. In other words these constraints satisfy the conditions for having a zero gradient in
Eqs.

35
and
36
. Once these constraints are enforced we have that the same steps for the proof of Theorem 1 in
rangan_gamp_admm
follows. Thus we also have that advGAMP is like an ADMM algorithm where the dual step in the constraint variable are exact each time.

The SE equations corresponding to the GAMP algorithm defined in
rangan_GAMP_2011
;
rangan_gamp_admm
correspond exactly the the equations in
Eq.

with the dependence on the constants
P,N,\hat{P},\hat{N}

B.1
Generalisation

In machine learning, particularly in the context of adversarial training, we are concerned with how well our model, referred to as the student model, can make correct predictions on data that has not been altered or perturbed. We quantify this ability using a metric called the generalisation error. The generalisation error, denoted by
E_{\mathrm{gen}}
, is the expected value over all possible data points of whether the model’s prediction
\hat{y}
is incorrect. When the model’s predictions are based on the estimated parameter vector
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, the generalisation error is mathematically expressed as

E_{\mathrm{gen}}=\mathbb{E}_{y,\boldsymbol{x}}\quantity[\mathbb{1}[y \neq\hat{y}(\hat{\w} ; \x)]]

(44)

This can also be represented in terms of overlaps using

E_{\mathrm{gen}}=\int\differential{y}\differential{\mu}(\nu,\lambda)P(y\mid\lambda)\mathbb{1}\quantity[y \neq f(\nu)]=\frac{1}{\pi}\arccos\quantity(\frac{m}{\sqrt{ (\rho+ \tau^2 ) q}})

(45)

where this integral can be simplified further in the case of simple models,
e.g.
the case of a noiseless channel.
The final form in the case of in the noiseless case has been presented in
aubin_generalization_2020
Appendix II. For the noisy case the derivation can be found in
Clarte_uncertanty
;
clarte_double_descent
.
The general form can be found in
Gerace_2021
, Appendix D.

However, in the case of adversarial learning, we’re not only interested in the generalisation error under normal conditions but also under adversarial attacks.
The adversarial generalisation error measures the model’s robustness against such attacks by evaluating the probability of misclassification when the input data is perturbed within a certain norm bound determined by
\varepsilon_{g}
and the covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\upsilon}}
.
Formally, the adversarial generalisation error is given by

E_{\mathrm{adv}}=\mathbb{E}_{y,\boldsymbol{x}}\quantity[\max_{
\norm{\vecdelta}_{\Sigmaupsilon^{-2}} \leq\advgencost} \mathbb{1}[y \neq\hat{y}(\hat{\w}(\alpha) ; \x+ \vecdelta)]]=\mathbb{E}_{y,\boldsymbol{x}}\quantity[\mathbb{1}[y \neq\hat{y}\qty(\hat{\w}(\alpha) ; \x- y \advgencost\frac{\Sigmaupsilon\what}{ \norm{\what}_2})]]

(46)

where the inner maximisation, for a fixed choice of
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, is solved explicitly by choosing
\boldsymbol{\delta}=-y\varepsilon_{g}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\upsilon}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}/\norm{\what}_{2}
.

Again by using the idea of local fields one can compute the the value of the adversarial error as a function of the overlaps. The definitions of the overlap parameters
A,F
is fixed after training and thus it doesn’t get included in the average. We have that the from of the error is

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{adv}}=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\erfc\left(\frac{\frac{m}{\sqrt{q}}\xi}{\sqrt{2\left(\rho+\tau^{2}-m^{2}/q\right)}}\right)\frac{e^{-\frac{\xi^{2}}{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\differential{\xi}

(47)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\varepsilon_{g}\frac{A}{\sqrt{q}\sqrt{N}}}\erfc\left(\frac{-\frac{m}{\sqrt{q}}\xi}{\sqrt{2\left(\rho+\tau^{2}-m^{2}/q\right)}}\right)\frac{e^{-\frac{\xi^{2}}{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\differential{\xi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{gen}}+\int_{0}^{\varepsilon_{g}\frac{A}{\sqrt{q}\sqrt{N}}}\erfc\left(\frac{-\frac{m}{\sqrt{q}}\xi}{\sqrt{2\left(\rho+\tau^{2}-m^{2}/q\right)}}\right)\frac{e^{-\frac{\xi^{2}}{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\differential{\xi}

One could modify the equation even more to relate it to the Owen’s T function as we show in
Section

D.1

B.3
Class-preserving adversarial attacks

We propose a more refined metric called the class-preserving generalisation error. This metric only considers attacks that mislead the student model while not affecting the teacher model’s classification or its confidence in that classification. The teacher’s confidence is quantified by a margin, denoted as
\gamma
, which represents the minimum allowable distance from the decision boundary to consider a classification confident.

This is an artificial metric, as it would be inaccessible to a student. We are interested in measuring the error with respect to the noisy labels by only considering attacks which are fair with respect to the ground truth.

We focus on attacks that attempt to deceive the student model within a specified norm bound but also ensure that the teacher model does not misclassify the perturbed data. The class-preserving generalisation error, incorporating these considerations, is defined as:

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{CP}}(\varepsilon_{g},\gamma)=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{y,\boldsymbol{x}}\quantity[
\max_{
\substack{
\norm{\vecdelta}_{\Sigmaupsilon^{-2}} \leq\advgencost\\
y \boldsymbol{\theta}_0^\top(\x+ \vecdelta) > \sqrt{d} \gamma}
} \mathbb{1}(y \neq\hat{y} (\what, \x+ \vecdelta) )
\mathbb{1}(y \wstar^\top\x> \sqrt{d} \gamma)
]+

(52)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{y,\boldsymbol{x}}\quantity[
\mathbb{1}(y \neq\hat{y} (\what, \x) )
\mathbb{1}(y \wstar^\top\x\leq\sqrt{d} \gamma)
]

The constraint within the maximisation ensure that the perturbation does not cause the teacher model’s confidence classification is not diminished beyond the margin
\gamma
and that the total length of the perturbation doesn’t surpass the value
\varepsilon_{g}
. The two constraints can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\upsilon}}^{-2}\boldsymbol{\delta}\leq\varepsilon_{g}^{2}\,,

(53)

\displaystyle y\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\delta}>\gamma-y\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}\,,

(54)

this means that the projection of the perturbation on the teacher should always be greater in absolute value than the projection of the data,
i.e.
the data point classified from the teacher should not change.

To simplify further one can consider the vanishing margin case as in the main text, formally

E_{\mathrm{CP}}(\varepsilon_{g})=\lim_{\gamma\to 0^{+}}E_{\mathrm{CP}}(\varepsilon_{g},\gamma)\,.

(55)

We want to fool the student, so we consider a perturbation in the direction of
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
. Based on different conditions we have different attacks and thus different norm of this perturbation. We will indicate the norm as

\boldsymbol{\delta}_{(\cdot)}=\alpha_{(\cdot)}\frac{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\upsilon}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}{\norm{\what}_{2}}

(56)

where the subscript can indicate any of the cases and we will define them later.

The first case is if the point
\boldsymbol{x}
already has a margin with the teacher that it is smaller than
\gamma
. The condition for this to happen is that

y\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}<\gamma\quad\implies\quad\alpha_{\mathrm{LM}}=0\,,\quad\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\mathrm{LM}}=\boldsymbol{0}\,,

(57)

in this case we do not perturb the input data point.

By just considering
Eq.

53
we have that the maximum norm the perturbation can have is

\alpha_{\mathrm{MAX}}=-y\varepsilon_{g}\,,\quad\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\mathrm{MAX}}=-y\varepsilon_{g}\frac{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\upsilon}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}{\norm{\what}_{2}}

(58)

this is the strongest attack, but we can only use it if the perturbed image does not cross the margin
\gamma
of the teacher
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
. In equations, it means that
Eq.

54
is satisfied, which means that

y\frac{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\mathrm{MAX}})}{\sqrt{d}}\geq\gamma\,\iff\,y\frac{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}}{\sqrt{d}}-\varepsilon_{g}\frac{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\upsilon}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}{\sqrt{d}\norm{\what}_{2}}\geq\gamma

(59)

thus if this previous condition is satisfied we can perturb with
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\mathrm{MAX}}
.

If instead
Eqs.

54
and
59
are not satisfied we can proceed the attack but with a smaller norm, that is found by imposing that the final margin is equal to
\gamma
. By doing that we can solve for the final norm and obtain

\alpha_{\gamma}=\frac{\sqrt{d}\norm{\what}_{2}}{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\upsilon}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\quantity(y \gamma- \frac{\wstar^\top\x}{\sqrt{d}})\,,\quad\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\gamma}=\frac{\sqrt{d}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\upsilon}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\upsilon}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\quantity(y \gamma- \frac{\wstar^\top\x}{\sqrt{d}})

(60)

where remember that
y
is just a sign.

One can thus rewrite explicitly the maximisation in
Eq.

52
as follows

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{CP}}(\varepsilon_{g},\gamma)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{y,\boldsymbol{x}}\quantity[
\mathbb{1}\qty(y \neq\hat{y} (\what, \x+ \vecdelta_{\gamma}))
\mathbb{1}\qty(y \frac{\wstar^\top\x}{\sqrt{d}} - \advgencost\frac{\wstar^\top\Sigmaupsilon\what}{\sqrt{d} \norm{\what}_2} < \gamma)
\mathbb{1}(y \wstar^\top\x> \sqrt{d} \gamma)
]

(61)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}_{y,\boldsymbol{x}}\quantity[
\mathbb{1}\qty(y \neq\hat{y} (\what, \x+ \vecdelta_{\mathrm{MAX}}))
\mathbb{1}\qty(y \frac{\wstar^\top\x}{\sqrt{d}} - \advgencost\frac{\wstar^\top\Sigmaupsilon\what}{\sqrt{d} \norm{\what}_2} \geq\gamma)
\mathbb{1}(y \wstar^\top\x> \sqrt{d} \gamma)
]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}_{y,\boldsymbol{x}}\quantity[
\mathbb{1}(y \neq\hat{y} (\what, \x) )
\mathbb{1}(y \wstar^\top\x\leq\sqrt{d} \gamma)
]

where each one of the lines correspond to one of the previously explained cases. To compute the expectations in
Eq.

61
, because of the presence of
y
one should consider the actual channel that generates the data. Nevertheless the form in
Eq.

61
is well suited for estimation from a given test dataset by replacing the expectations with empirical averages over that training dataset.

D.2
How fair are our attacks?

In the context of adversarial attacks, it is crucial to distinguish between transformations that change the underlying class of a data point and those that do not. Consider an example in image recognition: if an image of a cat is altered so extensively that it appears to be a dog, it is appropriate for a model to classify it as a dog. However, this does not constitute a meaningful adversarial attack. A sensible adversarial attack modifies the image in a way that does not alter the class identified by the original teacher model, which serves as a reference for correct classifications.

The following expression tells us the ratio of wrongly classified samples with respect to the noisy labels when we use the optimal attack we introduced earlier.

\mathbb{E}_{y,\boldsymbol{x}}\quantity[
\sign\qty( \frac{\wstar^\top(\x+ \boldsymbol{\upsilon})}{\sqrt{d}} ) \neq y
]=1-\int_{\varepsilon_{g}\frac{F}{\sqrt{N}}}^{\infty}d\nu\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\rho}}\exp\quantity( - \frac{\nu^2}{2 \rho} )\quantity( 1 + \erf( \frac{\nu}{\sqrt{2 \tau^2 }} ) ))

(91)

Notice that there is a nuance regarding the knowledge of the student and the adversary. The metric above tells us the wrongly classified ratio among all samples as a student not knowing ground truth would assess it. Instead of considering the noisy labels
y=\operatorname{sign}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}/\sqrt{d}+\tau\chi)
, one could also ask for the fairness achievable with respect to the teacher ground truth by instead considering the noiseless activation inaccessible to the student
y=\operatorname{sign}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}/\sqrt{d})
. The metrics for generalisation and training are using the noisy labels accessible by the student.

Appendix F
Defendable Attacks and Inevitable Trade-Offs

In this Appendix we want to study the relation between the attack geometry
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\upsilon}}
and the defence geometry
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}
. We assume to know the attack geometry. If we can successfully defend against a geometry, without incurring a trade-off, we call the geometry
defendable
, otherwise
not defendable
. A trade-off does not occur, if the adversarial error is dominated by the generalisation error and if the generalisation error behaves roughly as a constant as a function of the protection strength
\varepsilon_{t}
.

To make a fair comparison between the geometries we are going to consider a normalised version of the matrices
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\upsilon}}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}
and consider the total cost of the attack/defence to be tuned by
\varepsilon_{g}
/
\varepsilon_{t}
.
We are asking how to spend a fixed budget among the defence directions, to protect most effectively against a given attack. Since both matrices are symmetric we can write them as
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}^{\top}
where
\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}=1
and the eigenvectors are normalised
\norm{\boldsymbol{v}_i}_{2}=1
.

To keep things simple, we can suppose that the attack matrix is
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\upsilon}}=\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}
. We will refer to
\boldsymbol{v}
as the unit vector that is composed of a sum between
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
and another vector
\boldsymbol{u}
perpendicular to
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
.

We can thus decompose the attack matrix as

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\upsilon}}=(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}+\boldsymbol{u})(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}+\boldsymbol{u})^{\top}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}^{\top}+\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}\boldsymbol{u}^{\top}+\boldsymbol{u}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}^{\top}+\boldsymbol{u}\boldsymbol{u}^{\top}

(98)

To reduce the attack strength, we want to minimise the the attack overlap
A
which is
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\upsilon}}\boldsymbol{\theta}
.

To minimise the term
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}\boldsymbol{u}\boldsymbol{u}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta}
, we would like to make the ERM procedure chose a vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}
that is perpendicular to
\boldsymbol{u}
. This can be done without affecting generalisation performances as
\boldsymbol{u}\perp\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
. A way to remove this, is to regularise in the direction
\boldsymbol{u}
. This can be done with a directional
\ell_{2}
regularisation or with adversarial training and choosing
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}=\boldsymbol{u}\boldsymbol{u}^{\top}
. With this choice of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}
, the values
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}\boldsymbol{u}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}\boldsymbol{u}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta}
can be decreased in the same way.

For the last term
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta}
, this recipe does not work. We can interpret this term as being proportional to the square of the overlap
m
for isotropic data. Thus, it is obvious that protecting such a direction, can only be achieved by reducing the norm of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, which reduces the overlap
m
, which in turn cannot be changed without hurting the angle between teacher and student and thus generalisation error. This term is thus at the heart of the trade-off between generalisation and boundary term.

Thus shown that all the components of the eigenvectors of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\upsilon}}
that are perpendicular to the teacher can be “regularised away”.

G.1
Finding Non-Robust Features

The margin-based robustness given in
Eq.

9

\boldsymbol{\theta}

\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{emp.}}_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})_{j}-\frac{\varepsilon_{g}\boldsymbol{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\boldsymbol{w}}{\sqrt{\boldsymbol{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{w}}}

(99)

which can be evaluated component-wise.

Our aim is to apply this measure on components obtained by PCA. The motivation for choosing PCA as a method of feature extraction and dimensionality reduction is twofold, on the one hand it creates uncorrelated variables and on the other hand, it creates them in order of explained variance. This produces a data-set similar to the BFM with a power-law distributed spectrum.

We begin our procedure by computing the principal components and projecting all test and training data. Next, we optimise the weights with
\varepsilon_{t}=0

\varepsilon_{t}
and evaluate the component-wise robustness-measure
Eq.

4000
FashionMNIST) for the component-wise robustness-measure to produce a mask for robust features. All values higher than the cutoff are considered to be robust. We choose the constant such that the boundary error is explained to a large degree through the non-robust features. This is evaluated by constraining the attack to all features, the robust features and the non-robust features. The results of this experiment is shown in

To be explicit, we perform three different adversarial training runs with different directional choices for

Appendix I
Replica Computation

In this Section we give a full derivation of the results in
Theorem

III.1
by means of the replica approach, a standard method developed in the realm of statistical physics of disordered systems.
Our computational approach can be found in
Loureiro_2022
;
Gerace_2021
;
aubin_generalization_2020
. For a foundational understanding of this effective yet heuristic method, we suggest the following books
mezard1987spin
;
mezard2009information
.

I.1
Gibbs minimisation

The starting point is to define the following Gibbs measure over weights
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We want the most probable states to be the ones that minimises the ERM problem in the first place. Then to select only these states we will consider the zero temperature limit by taking the parameter
\beta\to\infty
.
The measure that we are interested in is

\mu_{\beta}(\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}}e^{-\beta\left[\sum_{\mu=1}^{n}g\left(y^{\mu},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}^{\mu},\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\delta}},\varepsilon_{t}\right)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{T}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{w}}\boldsymbol{\theta}\right]}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}=\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}}\underbrace{\prod_{\mu=1}^{n}e^{-\beta g\left(y^{\mu},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}^{\mu},\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\delta}},\varepsilon_{t}\right)}}_{P_{g}}\underbrace{e^{-\frac{\beta\lambda}{2}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{T}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{w}}\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathrm{~d}w_{i}}_{P_{w}}

(100)

Where
P_{g}
is the probability distribution associated with the channel and
P_{w}
is the prior probability distribution.

Here,
\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}
, is the partition function that normalises the Gibbs measure and it is given by

\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}e^{-\frac{\beta\lambda}{2}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{T}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{w}}\boldsymbol{\theta}}\prod_{\mu=1}^{n}e^{-\beta g\left(y^{\mu},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}^{\mu},\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\delta}},\varepsilon_{t}\right)}

(101)

You do need attention, but the free energy density is sufficient. In the zero temperature limit,
\beta\to\infty
the Gibbs measure in
Eq.

100
concentrates around the solutions of the ERM problem. With the replica method, we can compute the free energy density, it is given by:

\beta f_{\beta}=-\lim_{d\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{d}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\log\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}

(102)

To evaluate the quenched average of the free energy is to use the replica trick

\lim_{d\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{d}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\log\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}=\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\lim_{d\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{d}\frac{\partial_{r}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\mathcal{Z}^{r}}{1}

(103)

Note that we introduced three limits up to here. The first is the zero temperature limit ensuring that we find the ground state of our Gibbs measure which corresponds to the minimum of our ERM problem. The second is the thermodynamic limit of very large dimension whilst keeping the sampling ratio fixed. And the third limit stems from the replica trick allowing us to compute the logarithm of the partition function, it corresponds to setting the number of replicated systems to zero.

This computation follows for the first part the one in
Loureiro_2022
. So we start with the initial definition of replicated partition function the difference we have in our case is that we have a dependence on
\varepsilon_{t}
on the output probability.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}^{r}

\displaystyle=\prod_{\mu=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}^{\mu}}\prod_{a=1}^{r}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}P_{w}\left(\differential{\w^a}\right)P\left(y^{\mu}\mid\frac{\boldsymbol{x}^{\mu}\cdot\boldsymbol{\theta}^{a}}{\sqrt{d}}\right)

(104)

\displaystyle=\prod_{\mu=1}^{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\differential{y}^{\mu}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{p}}P_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}\left(\differential{\wstar}\right)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}}\left(\prod_{a=1}^{r}P_{w}\left(\differential{\w^a}\right)\right)\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}^{\mu}}\left[P_{0}\left(y^{\mu}\mid\frac{\boldsymbol{x}^{\mu}\cdot\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\prod_{a=1}^{r}P_{g}\left(y^{\mu}\mid\frac{\boldsymbol{x}^{\mu}\cdot\boldsymbol{\theta}^{a}}{\sqrt{d}},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\delta}},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{a},\varepsilon_{t}\right)\right]

explicitly we have that the term in

- \beta g\qty(

)
)

(105)

note that

can be a general noisy channel distribution.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}^{\mu}}\left[P_{0}\left(y^{\mu}\mid\frac{\boldsymbol{x}^{\mu}\cdot\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\prod_{a=1}^{r}P_{g}\left(y^{\mu}\mid\frac{\boldsymbol{x}^{\mu}\cdot\boldsymbol{\theta}^{a}}{\sqrt{d}},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\delta}},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{a},\varepsilon_{t}\right)\right]

Appendix J
Numerical Empirical Risk Minimisation

Computing the loss, gradient and hessian of the logistic loss, let alone the adversarially perturbed logistic loss pose well-known numerical challenges
pedregosa_how_2019
.
In this section we explain in detail how to numerically evaluate the ERM-estimator. To begin, here is the loss function over a data set
{(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mu},y_{\mu})}\>\mu=1,...,N
.

\mathcal{L}=\sum_{\mu=1}^{N}\log\left(1+\exp\quantity(-y_\mu\RawPrediction+ \OptAttack)\right)

(157)

To compute the gradient, we switch from a -1/1 labelling to a 0/1 labelling. We can write the following:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu=1}^{N}\log\left(1+\exp\quantity(-y_\mu\RawPrediction+ \OptAttack)\right)

(158)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu=1}^{N}\delta_{y_{\mu},+1}\log\left(1+\exp\quantity(-\RawPrediction+ \OptAttack)\right)+\delta_{y_{\mu},-1}\log\left(1+\exp\quantity(\RawPrediction+ \OptAttack)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu=1}^{N}y_{\mu}\log\left(1+\exp\quantity(-\RawPrediction+ \OptAttack)\right)+(1-y_{\mu})\log\left(1+\exp\quantity(\RawPrediction+ \OptAttack)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu=1}^{N}-y_{\mu}\log\left(\frac{\exp\quantity(\RawPrediction- \OptAttack)}{1+\exp\quantity(\RawPrediction- \OptAttack)}\right)+(1-y_{\mu})\log\left(1+\exp\quantity(\RawPrediction+ \OptAttack)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu=1}^{N}-y_{\mu}\quantity(\RawPrediction- \OptAttack)+y_{\mu}\log\left(1+\exp\quantity(\RawPrediction- \OptAttack)\right)

\displaystyle+(1-y_{\mu})\log\left(1+\exp\quantity(\RawPrediction+ \OptAttack)\right)

In the second step we are free to choose the labelling to be 0/1 as there is no more explicit dependence on the
y_{\mu}
instance.

J.1
Computing the Loss

To accurately compute the loss we base our computation on the work of
machler_accurately_nodate
who showed how to accurately compute
\log(1+\exp(x) )
.
The idea is to choose the most suitable approximation depending on the argument of the function.

Finally, we can write the loss with 0/1 labels as

\mathcal{L}=\sum_{\mu=1}^{N}-y_{\mu}\frac{\boldsymbol{x}_{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{w}}{\sqrt{d}}+y_{\mu}\frac{\varepsilon_{t}\boldsymbol{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\boldsymbol{w}}{\sqrt{d}\sqrt{\boldsymbol{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{w}}}+(1-y_{\mu})\log\left(1+\exp\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{x}_{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{w}}{\sqrt{d}}+\frac{\varepsilon_{t}\boldsymbol{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\boldsymbol{w}}{\sqrt{d}\sqrt{\boldsymbol{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{w}}}\right)\right)+y_{\mu}\log\left(1+\exp\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{x}_{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{w}}{\sqrt{d}}-\frac{\varepsilon_{t}\boldsymbol{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\boldsymbol{w}}{\sqrt{d}\sqrt{\boldsymbol{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{w}}}\right)\right)

(159)

In
machler_accurately_nodate
the authors showed how to accurately compute
\log( 1 + \exp(x) )
, additionally
scikit-learn
inspire us to achieve faster computation by extending the case distinction by
machler_accurately_nodate
by the
\leq 2
and using directly the log function.

\log( 1 + \exp(x) ):=\begin{cases}\exp(x)&x\leq-37\\
\operatorname{log1p}(\exp(x))&-37<x\leq x_{0}:=-2\\
\log( 1 + \exp(x) )&x_{0}<x\leq x_{1}:=18\\
x+\exp(-x)&x_{1}<x\leq x_{2}:=33.3\\
x&x>x_{2}\end{cases}

(160)

J.2
Computing the Gradient

First we notice that

\derivative{z}\quantity[ \log\qty(1 + e^{z}) ]=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}\,,\quad\derivative{z}\quantity[ \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}} ]=\frac{e^{z}}{(1+e^{z})^{2}}\,,\quad\derivative{z}\quantity[ \frac{1}{1 + e^{z}} ]=-\frac{1}{2\cosh(z)+2}\,.

(161)

also we remember the following derivatives

\gradient_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\quantity[ \w^\top\Sigmadelta\w]=2\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\boldsymbol{\theta}\,,\quad\operatorname{Hess}\quantity(\w^\top\Sigmadelta\w)=2\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\,,\quad\gradient_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\quantity[ \norm{\w}_2 ]=\frac{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{\norm{\w}_{2}}\,,\quad\operatorname{Hess}\quantity(\norm{\w}_2 )=\frac{\mathbf{1}}{\norm{\w}_{2}}-\frac{\boldsymbol{\theta}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}}{\norm{\w}_{2}^{3}}\,,

(162)

since we suppose that
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}
is a symmetric matrix.

Call the derivative of the optimal attack
\boldsymbol{h}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{h}=\gradient_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}B=\gradient_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\quantity[ \OptAttack]

\displaystyle=\frac{2\varepsilon_{t}}{\sqrt{d}\norm{\w}_{2}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\boldsymbol{\theta}-\frac{\varepsilon_{t}}{\sqrt{d}\norm{\w}_{2}^{3}}\boldsymbol{\theta}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\boldsymbol{\theta}\>\boldsymbol{\theta}

(163)

We define the arguments of the sigmoid activation
C
and
\overline{C}

C_{\mu}:=\frac{\boldsymbol{x}_{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{w}}{\sqrt{d}}+\frac{\varepsilon_{t}\boldsymbol{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\boldsymbol{w}}{\sqrt{d}\sqrt{\boldsymbol{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{w}}}\,,\qquad\overline{C}_{\mu}=\frac{\boldsymbol{x}_{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{w}}{\sqrt{d}}-\frac{\varepsilon_{t}\boldsymbol{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\boldsymbol{w}}{\sqrt{d}\sqrt{\boldsymbol{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{w}}}\,,

(164)

Then we can write the derivative of the loss as

\displaystyle\gradient_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu=1}^{N}\left[-y_{\mu}\frac{\boldsymbol{x}_{\mu}}{\sqrt{d}}+y_{\mu}\boldsymbol{h}+\frac{(1-y_{\mu})}{1+\exp\left(-C_{\mu}\right)}\quantity( \frac{\x_\mu}{\sqrt{d}} + \boldsymbol{h} )+\frac{y_{\mu}}{1+\exp\left(-\overline{C}_{\mu}\right)}\quantity(\frac{\x_\mu}{\sqrt{d}} - \boldsymbol{h} )\right]

(165)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu=1}^{N}\left[\boldsymbol{h}\left(\frac{(1-y_{\mu})}{1+\exp\left(-C_{\mu}\right)}+\frac{y_{\mu}}{1+\exp\quantity( \overline{C}_\mu)}\right)+\frac{\boldsymbol{x}_{\mu}}{\sqrt{d}}\left(\frac{(1-y_{\mu})}{1+\exp\left(-C_{\mu}\right)}-\frac{y_{\mu}}{1+\exp\quantity( \overline{C}_\mu)}\right)\right]

With this it is easy to see what the loss per sample is when factoring out the data
\boldsymbol{x}
and the derivative of the optimal attack
\boldsymbol{h}
. We see that their respective contributions are similar.
One can avoid overflows by placing the exponential part of the sigmoid carefully depending on the argument.
