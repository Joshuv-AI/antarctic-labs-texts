Matrix-analytic methods for solving Poisson’s equation with applications to Markov chains of G ​ I / G / 1 GI/G/1 -type
Jinpeng Liu, Yuanyuan Liu, Yiqiang Q. Zhao

Abstract
In this paper, we are devoted to developing matrix-analytic methods for solving Poisson’s equation for irreducible and positive recurrent discrete-time Markov chains (DTMCs). Two special solutions, including the deviation matrix D D and the expected additive-type functional matrix K K , will be considered. The results are applied to Markov chains of G ​ I / G / 1 GI/G/1 -type and M ​ A ​ P / G / 1 MAP/G/1 queues with negative customers. Further extensions to continuous-time Markov chains (CTMCs) are also investigated. Keywords: Markov chains, Poisson’s equation, matrix-analytic methods, Markov chains of G ​ I / G / 1 GI/G/1 -type, the deviation matrix, the expected additive-type functional matrix, M ​ A ​ P / G / 1 MAP/G/1 queues AMS 2010 Subject Classification: 60J10, 60J22, 60J27.

Introduction

Let
P=(P(i,j))_{i,j\in E}
be the transition matrix of a DTMC
\mathbf{\Phi}=\{\Phi_{k},k\geq 0\}
on a countable state space
E
.
It is assumed that
P
is irreducible and positive recurrent with the unique invariant probability vector
{\bm{\pi}}

such that
{\bm{\pi}}^{T}P={\bm{\pi}}^{T}
and
{\bm{\pi}}^{T}{\bm{e}}=1
, where
\bm{e}
is a column vector of ones.
Let
\bm{g}:E\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a function (or vector) satisfying
\bm{\pi}^{T}|\bm{g}|<\infty
.
For a given transition matrix
P
and a function
\bm{g}
, Poisson’s equation is written as

(I-P)\bm{f}=\overline{\bm{g}},

(1.1)

where
I
is the identity matrix and
\overline{\bm{g}}=\bm{g}-(\bm{\pi}^{T}\bm{g})\bm{e}
.
In general, we refer to the functions
\bm{g}
and
\bm{f}
as the forcing function and the solution of Poisson’s equation
(\ref{poi-fun-0})
, respectively.

Poisson’s equation has an important influence on the development of Markov chain theory.
In
[
1
]
, Glynn and Ormoneit established a Hoeffding’s inequality, which provides an upper bound for the tail probability of the law of large numbers,
for strong ergodic DTMCs via the solution of Poisson’s equation.
Further progress along this direction can be found in
[
2
,
3
]
and their references.
Poisson’s equation may also be associated to central limit theorems.
In
[
4
]
, Glynn and Meyn pointed out that the solution of Poisson’s equation can be used to express the variance constant,
which is a key parameter in the central limit theorem.
Please refer to
[
5
,
6
]
and references therein for recent developments in this filed.
In Markov decision processes, Poisson’s equation was known as the dynamic programming
equation, see e.g.,
[
7
,
8
]
, and the functions
\bm{g}
and
\bm{f}
were called the cost function and the value function, respectively.
Poisson’s equation is also applied to perturbation theory
[
9
,
10
]
, augmented truncation approximations
[
11
,
12
]
, machine learning
[
13
,
14
]
and others.

For real applications, it is crucial to solve or estimate the solution of Poisson’s equation.
In the literature, the solution of Poisson’s equation had been investigated for birth-death processes
[
15
,
16
]
, single-birth processes
[
17
,
18
]
,
single-death processes
[
19
,
20
]
,
M/G/1
queues
[
21
]
,
PH/PH/1
queues
[
22
]
, among others.
In addition, there are some approximate schemes for the solution of Poisson’s equation, see e.g.,
[
23
,
13
]
.
Recently, Liu et al.
[
20
]
established augmented truncation approximations for the solution of Poisson’s equation.

In this paper, we use matrix-analytic methods to solve Poisson’s equation.
Since Neuts
[
24
,
25
]
introduced and studied matrix-analytic methods for stochastic models,
matrix-analytic methods had been widely used in queueing theory, supply chain management, inventory theory, reliability, telecommunications networks,
risk and insurance analysis, finance mathematics, and biostatistics, see e.g.,
[
26
,
27
,
28
]
.
In
[
29
]
, Dendievel et al. used matrix-analytic methods to solve Poisson’s equation for quasi-birth-and-death (QBD) processes.
Further progress has been made in
[
5
,
30
,
31
]
.
Here, we extend matrix-analytic methods in solving Poisson’s equation for general Markov chains.
Specifically, we try to find a matrix
X
, with which we may represent the solution
\bm{f}
to Poisson’s equation (
1.1
) in the form of
\bm{f}=X\bm{g}
.
In this sense, we rewrite Poisson’s equation (
1.1
) as the following matrix form

(I-P)X=I-\bm{e\pi}^{T}.

(1.2)

Our first main result, presented in Theorem
3.1
, gives a general matrix solution
\widetilde{X}
that satisfies Poisson’s equation (
1.2
).
Moreover, the matrix
\widetilde{X}
is a unique solution in the sense of up to a constant matrix under some additional conditions, i.e.
X=\widetilde{X}+\bm{e\beta}^{T}
,
where
\bm{\beta}
is an arbitrary constant vector.
Then, two special solutions, which are called the deviation matrix
D
, see e.g.,
[
32
,
29
]
,
and the expected additive-type functional matrix
K
, see e.g.,
[
21
,
31
]
, will be investigated.
Particularly, we will focus on the latter because it needs a weaker existence condition than that for the deviation matrix
D
.

To apply our results, we consider the matrix solution
\widetilde{X}
for Markov chains of
GI/G/1
-type,
which are class of block-structured Markov chians with many applications in queueing theory, see e.g.,
[
33
,
34
,
35
,
28
]
.
Mathematically, a DTMC
\mathbf{\Phi}
on state space
E=\bigcup\nolimits_{i=0}^{\infty}{\ell(i)}
is called a Markov chain of
GI/G/1
-type if its transition probability matrix
P
is given by

\displaystyle P=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccccc}B_{0}&B_{1}&B_{2}&B_{3}&\cdots\\
B_{-1}&A_{0}&A_{1}&A_{2}&\cdots\\
B_{-2}&A_{-1}&A_{0}&A_{1}&\cdots\\
B_{-3}&A_{-2}&A_{-1}&A_{0}&\cdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{array}\right],

where
\ell(i):=\{(i,j),i\geq 0,1\leq j\leq m\}
denotes the level set,
the matrix sequences
\{B_{i},i\in\mathbb{Z}\}
and
\{A_{i},i\in\mathbb{Z}\}
are non-negative matrices of size
m<\infty
,
where
\mathbb{Z}:=\{0,\pm 1,\pm 2,\cdots\}
.
Suppose that
\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}B_{i}
,
B_{-j}+\sum_{i=-j+1}^{\infty}A_{i}
and
\sum_{i=-\infty}^{\infty}A_{i}
are stochastic.
Markov chains of
GI/G/1
-type include QBD processes if
B_{i}=B_{-i}=A_{i}=A_{-i}=0
for
i\geq 2
,
Markov chains of
GI/M/1
type if
B_{i}=A_{i}=0
for
i\geq 2
and Markov chains of
M/G/1
type if
B_{-i}=A_{-i}=0
for
i\geq 2
.

The rest of this paper is organized in to 6 sections.
Section 2 introduces preliminaries of the solution of Poisson’s equation and the censored Markov chains, which play a key role for the subsequently proposed matrix-analytic methods.
In section 3, we present a general matrix solution
\widetilde{X}
and specific matrix solution
K
of Poisson’s equation for DTMCs.
Section 4 applies the results in section 3 to Markov chains of
GI/G/1
-type.
In section 5, we give numerical calculations of

queues with negative customers.
Section 6 considers the extension of the matrix solution
\widetilde{X}
for DTMCs to CTMCs and section 7 presents concluding remarks.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
The solution of Poisson’s equation

For a finite state Markov chain
\mathbf{\Phi}
, there exists a unique solution of Poisson’s equation (
1.2
), see e.g.,
[
29
]
.
Moreover, the solution is given by

X=(I-P)^{\#}+\bm{e\beta}^{T},

(2.1)

where
\bm{\beta}
is an arbitrary constant vector that requires additional information to be determined
and
(I-P)^{\#}
is the group inverse, see e.g.,
[
36
,
37
]
.
The group inverse
W^{\#}
of a finite square matrix
W
is defined to be the unique matrix such that

WW^{\#}W=W,\ \ W^{\#}WW^{\#}=W^{\#}\ \ and\ \ W^{\#}W=WW^{\#}.

In the special case of
W=I-P
, the group inverse
(I-P)^{\#}
can be easily determined by

(I-P)^{\#}=(I-P+\bm{e\pi}^{T})^{-1}-\bm{e\pi}^{T}.

(2.2)

For infinite state Markov chains, the uniqueness of the solution does not necessarily hold. From Proposition 17.4.1 in
[
38
]
,
we obtain the following lemma, which presents a sufficient criterion for the uniqueness of the solution of Poisson’s equation (
1.2
).

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\mathbf{\Phi}
be an irreducible and positive recurrent Markov chain. Suppose that
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
are two solutions of Poisson’s equation (
1.2
)
with
\bm{\pi}^{T}(|X_{1}|+|X_{2}|)<\infty
. Then for some constant vector
\bm{\beta}
, we have
X_{1}=X_{2}+\bm{e\beta}^{T}
.

In general, the solution of Poisson’s equation (
1.2
) can be presented via the deviation matrix
D
or the expected additive-type functional matrix
K
.
It is well known that the deviation matrix
D
is defined as

D:=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(P^{n}-\bm{e\pi}^{T}\right),

(2.3)

see e.g.,
[
32
]
. It is not difficult to verify that
D
satisfies Poisson’s equation (
1.2
) and
\bm{\pi}^{T}{D}=\bm{0}^{T}
,
where
\bm{0}
is the zero vector.
Moreover, we know that the elements of
D
can be expressed in terms of the expected first return times:

D(i,i)=\pi(i)\left(\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\pi}}\left[\tau_{i}\right]-1\right)

(2.4)

and

D(j,i)=D(i,i)-\pi(i)\mathbb{E}_{j}\left[\tau_{i}\right],\ \ j\neq i,

(2.5)

where
\tau_{i}:=\inf\{k\geq 1:\Phi_{k}=i\}
is the first return time to the state
i\in E

and
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\pi}}[\cdot]

(
or
\mathbb{E}_{i}[\cdot]
)
denotes the conditional expectation with respect to the initial distribution
\bm{\pi}

(
or initial state
i
)
.

From (
2.3
)–(
2.5
) and
[
29
]
,
we know that if the deviation matrix
D
exists, then the chain must be aperiodic and
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\pi}}\left[\tau_{i}\right]<\infty
for some
i\in E
.
In fact, we can construct the expected additive-type functional matrix
K_{\alpha}=(K_{\alpha}(i,j))_{i,j\in E}
, which is called the solution kernel in Glynn
[
21
]
,
that has a weaker existence condition than that for the deviation matrix
D
.
For a fixed state
\alpha\in E
,
we define the matrix
K_{\alpha}
such that

K_{\alpha}(i,j):=\mathbb{E}_{i}\bigg[\sum_{k=0}^{\tau_{\alpha}-1}\overline{\mathbb{I}}(\Phi_{k}=j)\bigg],\ \ i,j\in E,

(2.6)

where
\mathbb{I}(\cdot)
denotes the indicator function and
\overline{\mathbb{I}}(\Phi_{k}=j)={\mathbb{I}}(\Phi_{k}=j)-\pi(j)
.
For the convenience of subsequent analysis, we simplify
K_{\alpha}
to
K
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\mathbf{\Phi}
be an irreducible and positive recurrent Markov chain.
Then, the matrix
K
is one solution of Poisson’s equation (
1.2
) with
K(\alpha,j)=0
for any state
j\in E
.

Proof.

We first prove
K(\alpha,j)=0
,
j\in E
. It follows form Theorem 10.4.9 in
[
38
]
that

\pi(\alpha)\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}\bigg[\sum_{k=0}^{\tau_{\alpha}-1}{\mathbb{I}}(\Phi_{k}=j)\bigg]=\pi(j).

From Kac’s Theorem, we obtain

\pi(\alpha)=\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}[\tau_{\alpha}]}.

Applying (
2.6
) yields
K(\alpha,j)=0
.

Now, we fix state
j\in E
.
Combining (
2.6
) and the strong Markov property, we have for any
i\in E
,

K(i,j)=\bar{\mathbb{I}}(i=j)+\sum_{l\neq\alpha}P(i,l)K(l,j).

Since
K(\alpha,j)=0
, we obtain

K(i,j)=\bar{\mathbb{I}}(i=j)+\sum_{i\in E}P(i,l)K(l,j),

from which, we have

(I-P)K=I-\bm{e\pi}^{T}.

The proof is completed.
∎

Remark 2.1
.

For a given function
\bm{g}
satisfying
\bm{\pi}^{T}|\bm{g}|<\infty
, the functions
\bm{f_{D}}:=D\bm{g}
and
\bm{f_{K}}:=K\bm{g}
are solutions of Poisson’s equation (
1.1
), which are given by

{f_{D}}(i)=\mathbb{E}_{i}\bigg[\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\overline{g}(\Phi_{k})\bigg],\ \ i\in E,

(2.7)

and

{f_{K}}(i)=\mathbb{E}_{i}\bigg[\sum_{k=0}^{\tau_{\alpha}-1}\overline{g}(\Phi_{k})\bigg],\ \ i\in E,

(2.8)

respectively. See Remark 4 in
[
39
]
and Theorem 3.3 in
[
21
]
.
Moreover, combining (
2.7
)–(
2.8
) and the strong Markov property, we have
{f_{D}}(i)-{f_{K}}(i)={f_{D}}(\alpha)
for any
i\in E
.

2.2
The censored Markov chain

In the following, we introduce the censoring technique.
Let
A
be a non-empty subset of
E
.
Let
\theta_{k}
be the
k^{th}
time that
\mathbf{\Phi}
successively visits a state in
A
,
i.e.
\theta_{0}:=\inf\{m\geq 0:\Phi_{m}\in A\}
and
\theta_{k+1}:=\inf\{m\geq\theta_{k}+1:\Phi_{m}\in A\}
.
The censored Markov chain
\mathbf{\Phi}^{(A)}=\{\Phi^{(A)}_{k},k\geq 0\}
on
A

is defined by
\Phi^{(A)}_{k}=\Phi_{\theta_{k}}
,
k\geq 0
, and its transition matrix and the invariant probability vector are denoted by
P^{(A)}
and
\bm{\pi}^{(A)}
, respectively.
Let
P_{{}_{A_{1}A_{2}}}=(P_{{}_{A_{1}A_{2}}}(i,j))_{i\in A_{1},j\in A_{2}}
, where
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
are subsets of
E
.
According to
A
and its complement
B:=A^{C}
, we partition the transition matrix
P
as

\displaystyle P=\hbox{}\;\vbox{\kern 30.83331pt\hbox{$\kern 61.53442pt\kern-8.75pt\left[\kern-61.53442pt\vbox{\vbox{\halign{$#$\hfil\kern 2\p@\kern\@tempdima& \thinspace\hfil$#$\hfil&& \quad\hfil$#$\hfil\cr\hfil\crcr\kern-12.0pt\cr$\hfil\kern 2.0pt\kern 8.75pt&A&B\crcr\kern 2.0pt\cr A$\hfil\kern 2.0pt\kern 8.75pt&P_{AA}&P_{AB}\cr B$\hfil\kern 2.0pt\kern 8.75pt&P_{BA}&P_{BB}\crcr\cr}}}\,\right]$}}.

From section 5 in
[
26
]
, we have the following lemma of the censored Markov chain.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\mathbf{\Phi}
be an irreducible and positive recurrent Markov chain with the invariant probability vector
{\bm{\pi}}
and let
A
be a non-empty subset of
E
.
Then, the censored Markov chain
\mathbf{\Phi}^{(A)}
is also irreducible and positive recurrent, whose transition probability matrix is given by

\displaystyle P^{(A)}=P_{AA}+P_{AB}\widehat{P}_{BB}P_{BA}

(2.15)

with
\widehat{P}_{BB}:=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}P_{BB}^{k}
. Moreover, the invariant probability vector
\bm{\pi}^{(A)}
of
\mathbf{\Phi}^{(A)}
is given by

\pi^{(A)}(i)=\frac{\pi(i)}{\sum_{j\in A}\pi(j)},\ \ i\in A.

(2.16)

Remark 2.2
.

(i)
\widehat{P}_{BB}
is the minimal nonnegative solution of

X(I-P_{BB})=(I-P_{BB})X=I.

(ii)
\widehat{P}_{BB}
is finite since
P_{BB}
is strictly substochastic.

(iii)
\widehat{P}_{BB}(i,j)
is the expected number of visits to state
j\in B
before entering
A
given that the process starts from state
i\in B
, i.e.

\widehat{P}_{BB}(i,j)=\mathbb{E}_{i}\bigg[\sum_{k=0}^{\tau_{A}-1}{\mathbb{I}}(\Phi_{k}=j)\bigg],\ \ i,j\in B,

(2.17)

where
\tau_{A}:=\inf\{k\geq 1:\Phi_{k}\in A\}
is the first return time to the set
A
.

On the contrary, we can also obtain the invariant probability vector
\bm{\pi}
when we know
\bm{\pi}^{(A)}
and
\widehat{P}_{BB}
.
Let us partition
\bm{\pi}^{T}
as
(\bm{\pi}_{A}^{T},\bm{\pi}_{B}^{T})
.
From (
2.2
) and
\bm{\pi}^{T}P=\bm{\pi}^{T}
, we have

\bm{\pi}_{A}^{T}P_{AB}+\bm{\pi}_{B}^{T}P_{BB}=\bm{\pi}_{B}^{T}.

Then,

\bm{\pi}_{B}^{T}(I-P_{BB})=\bm{\pi}_{A}^{T}P_{AB}.

(2.18)

Postmultiplying both sides of (
2.18
) by
\widehat{P}_{BB}
, we obtain

\bm{\pi}_{B}^{T}=\bm{\pi}_{A}^{T}P_{AB}\widehat{P}_{BB}.

(2.19)

According to
\bm{\pi}^{T}\bm{e}=1
, we have

\bm{\pi}_{A}^{T}\left[I,P_{AB}\widehat{P}_{BB}\right]\bm{e}=1.

(2.20)

Lemma
2.3
and (
2.20
) yield

\sum_{i\in A}\pi(i)=\left(\left(\bm{\pi}^{(A)}\right)^{T}\left[I,P_{AB}\widehat{P}_{BB}\right]\bm{e}\right)^{-1}.

(2.21)

Thus, the invariant probability vector
\bm{\pi}
can be obtained by using (
2.16
), (
2.19
) and (
2.21
).

3
General Markov chains

In this section, we will use matrix-analytic methods to solve Poisson’s equation (
1.2
) for general Markov chains.
Let
O
denote the zero matrix with appropriate numbers of rows and columns.
Similar, the matrix
I
and vector
\bm{e}
defined previously will adapt to the dimensions in the following analysis.
Furthermore, let us partition the matrix
X
as

X=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}X_{A}\\
X_{B}\\
\end{array}\right].

(3.1)

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\mathbf{\Phi}
be an irreducible and positive recurrent Markov chain and let
A
be a finite non-empty subset of
E
.
Then, the matrix
\widetilde{X}
, given by

\widetilde{X}_{A}=\left(I-{P}^{(A)}\right)^{\#}\left(\left[I,P_{AB}\widehat{P}_{BB}\right]-\left(\bm{e}+P_{AB}\widehat{P}_{BB}\bm{e}\right)\bm{\pi}^{T}\right)

(3.2)

and

\widetilde{X}_{B}=\widehat{P}_{BB}P_{BA}\widetilde{X}_{A}+\left[O,\widehat{P}_{BB}\right]-\widehat{P}_{BB}\bm{e}\bm{\pi}^{T},

(3.3)

is one solution of Poisson’s equation (
1.2
).
Moreover, if
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\pi}}\left[\tau_{i}\right]<\infty
for some
i\in E
, then the matrix
\widetilde{X}

is the unique matrix solution of Poisson’s equation (
1.2
) in the set of matrices
X
such that
\bm{\pi}^{T}|X|<\infty
.

Proof.

We first prove that
\widetilde{X}
satisfies Poisson’s equation (
1.2
).
From (
2.2
) and (
3.1
), we rewrite Poisson’s equation (
1.2
) as

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}I-P_{AA}&-P_{AB}\\
-P_{BA}&I-P_{BB}\\
\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{c}X_{A}\\
X_{B}\\
\end{array}\right]=I-\bm{e\pi}^{T}.

We obtain

(I-P_{AA})X_{A}=P_{AB}X_{B}+\left[I,O\right]-{\bm{e\pi}^{T}}

(3.5)

and

(I-P_{BB})X_{B}=P_{BA}X_{A}+\left[O,{I}\right]-\bm{e\pi}^{T}.

(3.6)

Premultiplying both sides of (
3.6
) by
\widehat{P}_{BB}
gives us

\displaystyle X_{B}=\widehat{P}_{BB}P_{BA}X_{A}+\widehat{P}_{BB}\left[O,I\right]-\widehat{P}_{BB}{\bm{e\pi}^{T}}.

(3.7)

Substituting (
3.7
) into (
3.5
), we have

\displaystyle(I-P_{AA})X_{A}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P_{AB}\widehat{P}_{BB}P_{BA}X_{A}+\left[O,P_{AB}\widehat{P}_{BB}\right]-P_{AB}\widehat{P}_{BB}{\bm{e\pi}^{T}}+\left[I,O\right]-{\bm{e\pi}^{T}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P_{AB}\widehat{P}_{BB}P_{BA}X_{A}+\left[I,P_{AB}\widehat{P}_{BB}\right]-\left(\bm{e}+P_{AB}\widehat{P}_{BB}\bm{e}\right){\bm{\pi}^{T}}.

Thus, we have

\left(I-P^{(A)}\right)X_{A}=\left[I,P_{AB}\widehat{P}_{BB}\right]-\left(\bm{e}+P_{AB}\widehat{P}_{BB}\bm{e}\right){\bm{\pi}^{T}}.

(3.8)

It follows from (
2.1
) and (
3.8
) that

X_{A}=\left(I-P^{(A)}\right)^{\#}\left(\left[I,P_{AB}\widehat{P}_{BB}\right]-\left(\bm{e}+P_{AB}\widehat{P}_{BB}\bm{e}\right){\bm{\pi}^{T}}\right)+{\bm{e\beta}^{T}}=\widetilde{X}_{A}+{\bm{e\beta}^{T}}.

(3.9)

From (
3.7
) and (
3.9
), we obtain

X_{B}=\widehat{P}_{BB}P_{BA}\left(\widetilde{X}_{A}+{\bm{e\beta}^{T}}\right)+\widehat{P}_{BB}\left[O,I\right]-\widehat{P}_{BB}{\bm{e\pi}^{T}}.

According to Remark
2.2
(iii), we know that
\widehat{P}_{BB}P_{BA}
denotes the probability of first hitting
A
from
B
. We thus have

\widehat{P}_{BB}P_{BA}{\bm{e}}={\bm{e}}.

Then, we obtain

\displaystyle X_{B}=\widehat{P}_{BB}P_{BA}\widetilde{X}_{A}+\left[O,\widehat{P}_{BB}\right]-\widehat{P}_{BB}{\bm{e\pi}^{T}}+{\bm{e\beta}^{T}}=\widetilde{X}_{B}+\bm{e\beta}^{T}.

(3.10)

Combining (
3.9
) with (
3.10
), we find that
{X}=\widetilde{X}+\bm{e\beta}^{T}
, i.e. the matrix
\widetilde{X}
is one solution of Poisson’s equation (
1.2
).

Now, we prove that under the condition of
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\pi}}[\tau_{i}]<\infty
,
\widetilde{X}
defined by (
3.2
–
3.3
) satisfies
\bm{\pi}^{T}|\widetilde{X}|<\infty
, i.e.

\sum_{i\in E}\pi(i)\left|\widetilde{X}(i,n)\right|<\infty,\ \ n\in E.

Since
A
is a finite set, we can find large enough positive constant
\gamma_{n}
for any
n\in E
, such that

\sum_{i\in A}\left|\widetilde{X}(i,n)\right|<\gamma_{n}<\infty.

Since
\mathbf{\Phi}
is irreducible and
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\pi}}[\tau_{i}]<\infty
,
this shows that
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\pi}}[\tau_{A}]<\infty
. Thus, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in E}\pi(i)\left|\widetilde{X}(i,n)\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\gamma_{n}\bm{\pi}_{A}^{T}\bm{e}+\sum_{i\in B}\pi(i)\left|\widetilde{X}(i,n)\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\gamma_{n}\bm{\pi}_{A}^{T}\bm{e}+\sum_{i\in B}\pi(i)\sum_{j\in A}\mathbb{P}_{i}(\tau_{A}=j)\left|\widetilde{X}(j,n)\right|

\displaystyle+\sum_{i\in B}\pi(i)\mathbb{E}_{i}\left[\tau_{A}\right]+\sum_{i\in B}\pi(i)\mathbb{E}_{i}\bigg[\sum_{k=0}^{\tau_{A}-1}\mathbb{I}(\Phi_{k}\in B)\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\gamma_{n}\bm{\pi}_{A}^{T}\bm{e}+\gamma_{n}\bm{\pi}_{B}^{T}\bm{e}+2\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\pi}}[\tau_{A}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma_{n}+2\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\pi}}[\tau_{A}]<\infty,

where
\mathbb{P}_{i}(\cdot)
denotes the conditional probability with respect to the initial state
i
.

By Lemma
2.1
, all matrix solutions
X
of
(I-P)X=I-\bm{e\pi}^{T}

such that
\bm{\pi}^{T}|X|<\infty
are given by
X=\widetilde{X}+\bm{e\beta}^{T}
for some constant vector
\bm{\beta}
. This completes the proof of Theorem
3.1
.
∎

Remark 3.1
.

For an irreducible and positive recurrent Markov chain
\mathbf{\Phi}
,

\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\pi}}[\tau_{i}]<\infty
for some (then for every)
i\in E
is equivalent to
\mathbb{E}_{i}[\tau_{i}^{2}]<\infty
, see Lemma 2.6 in
[
29
]
.

Under the uniqueness assumption, we can use the matrix solution
\widetilde{X}
to characterize the deviation matrix
D
.

Corollary 3.1
.

Let
\mathbf{\Phi}
be an irreducible, positive recurrent and aperiodic Markov chain. If
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\pi}}\left[\tau_{i}\right]<\infty
for some
i\in E
, then

D=(I-\bm{e\pi}^{T})\widetilde{X}
,
where
\widetilde{X}
is determined by (
3.2
)–(
3.3
).

Proof.

Since
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\pi}}\left[\tau_{i}\right]<\infty
, we know that
D
exists and
D
is one solution of Poisson’s equation (
1.2
).
Moreover, it follows from (
2.4
)–(
2.5
) that

\sum_{i\in E}\pi(i)|D(i,n)|\leq|D(n,n)|+\pi(n)\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\pi}}\left[\tau_{n}\right]<\infty

for any
n\in E
, i.e.
\bm{\pi}|D|<\infty
. From Theorem
3.1
, we have

D=\widetilde{X}+\bm{e\beta}^{T}.

Since
\bm{\pi}^{T}D=\bm{0}
, we obtain
\bm{\beta}=-\bm{\pi}^{T}\widetilde{X}
. Thus, this proof is completed.
∎

In fact, we could have derived the matrix
K
under the same condition
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\pi}}\left[\tau_{i}\right]<\infty
by using the same averments of Corollary
3.1
.
However, we can derive the matrix
K
without the condition
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\pi}}\left[\tau_{i}\right]<\infty
by using different arguments.

For a finite non-empty subset
A
of
E
, let
N=(N(i,j))_{i,j\in E}
denote a matrix such that

N(i,j):=\mathbb{E}_{i}\bigg[\sum_{k=0}^{\tau_{A}-1}\overline{\mathbb{I}}(\Phi_{k}=j)\bigg],\ \ i,j\in E.

The following lemma reveals a relationship between the matrix
K
and the matrix
N
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\mathbf{\Phi}
be an irreducible and positive recurrent Markov chain and let
A
be a finite non-empty subset of
E
.
Then, for any fixed state
\alpha\in A
, we have

K(i,j)=N(i,j)+\sum_{l\in A}\mathbb{P}_{i}(\Phi_{\tau_{A}}=l)K(l,j),\ \ i,j\in E.

(3.11)

Proof.

Since
\alpha\in A
,
\tau_{\alpha}=\max\{\tau_{\alpha},\tau_{A}\}
. Using (
2.6
) and the strong Markov property,
we obtain

\displaystyle K(i,j)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{i}\bigg[\sum_{k=0}^{\max\{\tau_{\alpha},\tau_{A}\}-1}{\overline{\mathbb{I}}}(\Phi_{k}=j)\bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{i}\bigg[\sum_{k=0}^{\tau_{A}-1}{\overline{\mathbb{I}}}(\Phi_{k}=j)\bigg]+\left(\mathbb{E}_{i}\bigg[\sum_{k=\tau_{A}}^{\tau_{\alpha}-1}{\overline{\mathbb{I}}}(\Phi_{k}=j)\bigg]\right)\mathbb{I}(\tau_{\alpha}>\tau_{A})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N(i,j)+\sum_{l\in A,l\neq\alpha}\mathbb{P}_{i}(\Phi_{\tau_{A}}=l)\left(\mathbb{E}_{l}\bigg[\sum_{k=0}^{\tau_{\alpha}-1}{\overline{\mathbb{I}}}(\Phi_{k}=j)\bigg]\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N(i,j)+\sum_{l\in A,l\neq\alpha}\mathbb{P}_{i}(\Phi_{\tau_{A}}=l)K(l,j).

From Lemma
2.2
, we have

K(\alpha,j)=0.

Thus,

K(i,j)=N(i,j)+\sum_{l\in A}\mathbb{P}_{i}(\Phi_{\tau_{A}}=l)K(l,j).

The proof is completed.
∎

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\mathbf{\Phi}
be an irreducible and positive recurrent Markov chain and let
A
be a finite non-empty subset of
E
.
Then, for any fixed state
\alpha\in A
,
we have
K=\widetilde{X}-\bm{e}\bm{\beta}^{T}
,
where
\beta(j)=\widetilde{X}(\alpha,j)
for any
j\in E
and
\widetilde{X}
is determined by (
3.2
)–(
3.3
).

Proof.

In (
3.11
), we consider the two cases of
i\in A
and
i\in B=A^{C}
, separately.
For the former case,
let
M_{A}=(M_{A}(i,j))_{i,j\in A}
denote the matrix such that

M_{A}(i,j):=\mathbb{P}_{i}(\Phi_{\tau_{A}}=j).

It is easy to verify that

\displaystyle M_{A}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P_{AA}+P_{AB}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{P}^{n}_{BB}P_{BA}

(3.12)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P_{AA}+P_{AB}\widehat{P}_{BB}P_{BA}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P^{(A)}.

Combining (
3.11
) and (
3.12
), we have

(I-P^{(A)})K_{A}=N_{A}.

(3.13)

It follows from (
2.1
) and (
3.13
) that

K_{A}=(I-{P}^{(A)})^{\#}N_{A}+\bm{e}\bm{\beta}^{T}.

Our task now is to solve the matrix
N_{A}
. For any state
j\in A
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{i}\bigg[\sum_{k=0}^{\tau_{A}-1}\mathbb{I}(\Phi_{k}=j)\bigg]=\mathbb{I}(i=j).

(3.14)

If
j\in B
, it follows from the strong Markov property and Remark
2.2
(iii) that

\mathbb{E}_{i}\bigg[\sum_{k=0}^{\tau_{A}-1}\mathbb{I}(\Phi_{k}=j)\bigg]=\sum_{l\in B}P_{AB}(i,l)\widehat{P}_{BB}(l,j).

(3.15)

From the strong Markov property, we have for
i\in A
,

\mathbb{E}_{i}\left[\tau_{A}\right]={1}+\sum_{l\in B}P_{AB}(i,l)\mathbb{E}_{l}\left[\tau_{A}\right].

(3.16)

According to Remark
2.2
(iii), it is clear that for
i\in B
,

\mathbb{E}_{i}\left[\tau_{A}\right]=\sum_{j\in B}\mathbb{E}_{i}\bigg[\sum_{k=0}^{\tau_{A}-1}\mathbb{I}(\Phi_{k}=j)\bigg]=\sum_{j\in B}\widehat{P}_{BB}(i,j).

(3.17)

Combining (
3.14
)–(
3.17
), we obtain

N_{A}=\left[I,P_{AB}\widehat{P}_{BB}\right]-\left(\bm{e}+P_{AB}\widehat{P}_{BB}\bm{e}\right)\bm{\pi}^{T},

(3.18)

from which, we have

K_{A}=\left(I-{P}^{(A)}\right)^{\#}\left(\left[I,P_{AB}\widehat{P}_{BB}\right]-\left(\bm{e}+P_{AB}\widehat{P}_{BB}\bm{e}\right)\bm{\pi}^{T}\right)+\bm{e}\bm{\beta}^{T}=\widetilde{X}_{A}+\bm{e}\bm{\beta}^{T}.

(3.19)

Now, we consider the case of
i\in B
.
Let
M_{B}=(M_{B}(i,j))_{i\in B,j\in A}
denote the matrix such that

M_{B}(i,j):=\mathbb{P}_{i}(X_{\tau_{A}}=j),

and

M_{B}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{P}^{n}_{BB}P_{BA}=\widehat{P}_{BB}P_{BA}.

(3.20)

From (
3.11
) and (
3.19
), we have

\displaystyle K_{B}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N_{B}+\widehat{P}_{BB}P_{BA}\left(\widetilde{K}_{A}+{\bm{e}}{\bm{\beta}}^{T}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N_{B}+\widehat{P}_{BB}P_{BA}\widetilde{K}_{A}+\widehat{P}_{BB}P_{BA}{\bm{e}}{\bm{\beta}}^{T}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N_{B}+\widehat{P}_{BB}P_{BA}\widetilde{K}_{A}+{\bm{e}}{\bm{\beta}}^{T}.

For any state
j\in A
, it is easy to see that

\mathbb{E}_{i}\bigg[\sum_{k=0}^{\tau_{A}-1}\mathbb{I}(\Phi_{k}=j)\bigg]=0.

(3.21)

According to (
3.21
), Remark
2.2
(iii) and (
3.17
), it follows that

N_{B}=\left[O,\widehat{P}_{BB}\right]-\widehat{P}_{BB}\bm{e}\bm{\pi}^{T},

(3.22)

from which, we have

K_{B}=\left[O,\widehat{P}_{BB}\right]-\widehat{P}_{BB}\bm{e}\bm{\pi}^{T}+\widehat{P}_{BB}P_{BA}\widetilde{X}_{A}+{\bm{e}}{\bm{\beta}}^{T}=\widetilde{X}_{B}+{\bm{e}}{\bm{\beta}}^{T}.

Finally, we obtain
\beta(j)=-\widetilde{X}(\alpha,j)
for any
j\in E
by Lemma
2.2
.
This completes the proof of Theorem
3.2
.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

(i) Combining Theorem
3.1

of Poisson’s equation for

In this section, we apply our results to Markov chains of

-type with the transition matrix given by (

For simplicity, we write

and

for the complement of

We now introduce

-measures

and

-type, see e.g.,
[

For

For
i>j\geq 0

is defined as a matrix of size

whose

Due to the property of repeating rows, we can write simply
R_{i,n}=R_{n-i}
and

Let

Thus, for any

\Psi_{i}=P^{(n)}_{n-i,n}\ \ \mbox{and}\ \ \Psi_{-i}=P^{(n)}_{n,n-i},\ \ \textrm{for}\ n>i.

Furthermore, we have

R_{i}=\Psi_{i}(I-\Psi_{0})^{-1},\ \ G_{i}=(I-\Psi_{0})^{-1}\Psi_{-i},\ \ i\geq 1.

In fact, the matrices

and

\Psi_{0}
. From Theorem 10 and Theorem 12 in

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\mathbf{\Phi}
be an irreducible and positive recurrent
Markov chain of

-type. Then, we have

R_{i}(I-\Psi_{0})=A_{i}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}R_{i+k}(I-\Psi_{0})G_{k},\ \ i\geq 1,

\Psi_{0}=A_{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}R_{k}(I-\Psi_{0})G_{k},

If the Markov chain of
GI/G/1
-type
\mathbf{\Phi}
is irreducible and positive recurrent,

\bm{\pi}_{n}^{T}=\bm{\pi}_{0}^{T}R_{0,n}+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\bm{\pi}_{k}^{T}R_{n-k},\ \ n\geq 1,

where we denote

The matrix

5
MAP/G/1 Queues with Negative Customers

In this section, we give numerical calculations of the matrix
\widetilde{X}
for
MAP/G/1
queues with negative customers.
Queueing systems with negative arrivals have a lot of applications in various areas, such as computer, manufacturing systems, neural and communication networks, see e.g.,
[
41
,
42
]
.

For a single-server FIFO queue, we suppose that there are two types of independent arrivals, positive and negative.
Positive arrivals correspond to customers who upon arrival, join the queue with the intention of being served and then leaving the system.
When a negative customer arrives at the queue, it immediately removes one or more positive customers if present.
Here, we consider the RCA rule, i.e. arrival of a negative customer which removes all the customers in the system.
Furthermore, we assume that the arrivals of both positive and negative customers are Markovian arrival processes (MAP)
and the service times are independent of the two arrival processes of positive and negative customers and obey a general distribution.
Then the above queueing model is a
MAP/G/1
queue with negative customers.

In
[
42
]

6
Continuous-time Markov chains

It is of the same feasibility to investigate continuous-time Markov chains (CTMCs) by using matrix-analytic methods.
Let
Q=(Q(i,j))_{i,j\in E}
be a totally stable and regular generator of the CTMC
\mathbf{\Phi}=\{\Phi_{t},t\geq 0\}
on a countable state space
E
.
It is assumed that
\mathbf{\Phi}
is irreducible and positive recurrent with the unique invariant probability vector
{\bm{\pi}}
.
For a given
Q
, Poisson’s equation is written as

-QX=I-\bm{e\pi}^{T}.

(6.1)

For a non-empty subset
A
of
E
, let
\mathbf{\Phi}^{(A)}=\{\Phi^{(A)}_{t},t\geq 0\}
be the censored Markov chain on
A
with the generator
Q^{(A)}
.
According to section 5 in
[
26
]
, the generator
Q^{(A)}
is given by

Q^{(A)}=Q_{AA}+Q_{AB}\widehat{Q}_{BB}Q_{BA},

(6.2)

where
B
is the complement of set
A
, and
\widehat{Q}_{BB}
, defined by

\widehat{Q}_{BB}:=\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp(Q_{BB}t)dt,

is the minimal nonnegative solution of

X(-Q_{BB})=(-Q_{BB})X=I.

Using the similar arguments in the proof of Theorem
3.1
leads the following results. The proof will be omitted.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
\mathbf{\Phi}
be an irreducible and positive recurrent CTMC and let
A
be a finite subset of
E
.
Then, the matrix
\widetilde{X}
, given by

\widetilde{X}_{A}=\left(-{Q}^{(A)}\right)^{\#}\left(\left[I,Q_{AB}\widehat{Q}_{BB}\right]-\left(\bm{e}+Q_{AB}\widehat{Q}_{BB}\bm{e}\right)\bm{\pi}^{T}\right)

(6.3)

and

\widetilde{X}_{B}=\widehat{Q}_{BB}Q_{BA}\widetilde{X}_{A}+\left[O,\widehat{Q}_{BB}\right]-\widehat{Q}_{BB}\bm{e}\bm{\pi}^{T},

(6.4)

is one solution of Poisson’s equation (
6.1
).

Remark 6.1
.

For CTMCs, we can also define the deviation matrix
D
and the expected integrable-type functional matrix
K
, see e.g., [7, 11].
By using Theorem
6.1
, we can obtain
D
and
K
in term of similar arguments in Corollary
3.1
and Theorem
3.2
, respectively.
For CTMCs of GI/G/1type, we can also obtain parallel results to that in sections 4 and 5.

7
Concluding remarks

In the previous sections, we have investigated the matrix solution
\widetilde{X}
of Poisson’s equation for general DTMCs by developing matrix-analytic methods.
Interestingly, we obtain the connection between the matrix solution
\widetilde{X}
and the matrix solution
\left(I-P^{(A)}\right)^{\#}

of Poisson’s equation for the censored Markov chain
\mathbf{\Phi}^{(A)}
in the process of solving the matrix solution
K
.
Furthermore, we derive an explicit expression of the matrix solution
\widetilde{X}
for Markov chains of
GI/G/1
-type,
which includes results of
QBD
processes, Markov chains of
GI/M/1
-type and Markov chains of
M/G/1
-type.

There are other areas in which one might extend our studies.
The first possible extension is to consider level dependent Markov chains of
GI/G/1
-type.
It can be expected that the calculations of the
R
-measures and
G
-measures will become more challenging for the level dependent case.
The other possible extension is to consider Poisson’s equation for positive recurrent fluid queues.
The arguments in this paper and Soares and Latouche
[
43
]
may be modified to use,
but evidently it requires essentially different arguments to deal with the case of non-countable state space.
