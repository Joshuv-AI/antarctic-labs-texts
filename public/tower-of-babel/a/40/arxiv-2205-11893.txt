Frequency-frequency correlations of single-trajectory spectral densities of Gaussian processes
Alessio Squarcini, Enzo Marinari, Gleb Oshanin, Luca Peliti & Lamberto Rondoni

Abstract
We investigate the stochastic behavior of the single-trajectory spectral density S ⁡ ( ω , 𝒯 ) S(\omega,\mathcal{T}) of several Gaussian stochastic processes, i.e., Brownian motion, the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, the Brownian gyrator model and fractional Brownian motion, as a function of the frequency ω \omega and the observation time 𝒯 \mathcal{T} . We evaluate in particular the variance and the frequency-frequency correlation of S ⁡ ( ω , 𝒯 ) S(\omega,\mathcal{T}) for different values of ω \omega . We show that these properties exhibit different behaviors for different physical cases and can therefore be used as a sensitive probe discriminating between different kinds of random motion. These results may prove quite useful in the analysis of experimental data.



Keywords: Gaussian stochastic process, power spectral density, frequency-frequency correlation function

2
General

In this section we sketch the method to evaluate the frequency-frequency correlation function of the PSD for a general Gaussian process.

The single-trajectory spectral density
S(\omega,\mathcal{T})
of the process
X(t)
is defined by

S(\omega,\mathcal{T})=\frac{1}{\mathcal{T}}\left\lvert{\int_{0}^{\mathcal{T}}\mathrm{d}t\;\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega T}\,X(t)}\right\rvert^{2}.

(4)

With this definition,
the frequency-frequency correlation function of
S(\omega,\mathcal{T})
is given by

\begin{split}\overline{S(\omega_{1},\mathcal{T})S(\omega_{2},\mathcal{T})}&=\frac{1}{\mathcal{T}^{2}}\int_{0}^{\mathcal{T}}\mathrm{d}t_{1}\int^{\mathcal{T}}_{0}\mathrm{d}t_{2}\int^{\mathcal{T}}_{0}\mathrm{d}t_{3}\int^{\mathcal{T}}_{0}\mathrm{d}t_{4}\;\overline{X(t_{1})X(t_{2})X(t_{3})X(t_{4})}\\
&\times\cos\left(\omega_{1}(t_{1}-t_{2})\right)\cos\left(\omega_{2}(t_{3}-t_{4})\right)\,.\end{split}

(5)

Concentrating from now on exclusively on Gaussian processes
X(t)
, we take advantage of Wick’s theorem to obtain

\begin{split}\overline{X(t_{1})X(t_{2})X(t_{3})X(t_{4})}&=\overline{X(t_{1})X(t_{2})}\,\overline{X(t_{3})X(t_{4})}\\
&\qquad{}+\overline{X(t_{1})X(t_{3})}\,\overline{X(t_{2})X(t_{4})}+\overline{X(t_{1})X(t_{4})}\,\overline{X(t_{2})X(t_{3})}\,.\end{split}

(6)

Noticing next that the second and the third terms on the right-hand-side of the latter expression provide identical contributions
to
\overline{S(\omega_{1},\mathcal{T})S(\omega_{2},\mathcal{T})}
, we rewrite eq. (
5
) in the form

\begin{split}\overline{S(\omega_{1},\mathcal{T})S(\omega_{2},\mathcal{T})}&=\frac{1}{\mathcal{T}^{2}}\int_{0}^{\mathcal{T}}\mathrm{d}t_{1}\int^{\mathcal{T}}_{0}\mathrm{d}t_{2}\;\overline{X(t_{1})X(t_{2})}\cos\left(\omega_{1}(t_{1}-t_{2})\right)\\
&\qquad{}\times\int^{\mathcal{T}}_{0}\mathrm{d}t_{3}\int^{\mathcal{T}}_{0}\mathrm{d}t_{4}\;\overline{X(t_{3})X(t_{4})}\cos\left(\omega_{2}(t_{3}-t_{4})\right)\\
&\qquad{}+\frac{2}{\mathcal{T}^{2}}\int_{0}^{\mathcal{T}}\mathrm{d}t_{1}\int^{\mathcal{T}}_{0}\mathrm{d}t_{2}\int^{\mathcal{T}}_{0}\mathrm{d}t_{3}\int^{\mathcal{T}}_{0}\mathrm{d}t_{4}\;\overline{X(t_{1})X(t_{3})}\,\,\,\overline{X(t_{2})X(t_{4})}\\
&\qquad{}\times\cos\left(\omega_{1}(t_{1}-t_{2})\right)\cos\left(\omega_{2}(t_{3}-t_{4})\right)\\
&=\mu(\omega_{1},\mathcal{T})\,\mu(\omega_{2},\mathcal{T})+J(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})\,,\end{split}

where

\mu(\omega,\mathcal{T})=\overline{S(\omega,\mathcal{T})},

(7)

is the mean spectral density at frequency
\omega
, while
J(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})
is given by

\begin{split}J(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})&=\frac{2}{\mathcal{T}^{2}}\int_{0}^{\mathcal{T}}\mathrm{d}t_{1}\int^{\mathcal{T}}_{0}\mathrm{d}t_{2}\int^{\mathcal{T}}_{0}\mathrm{d}t_{3}\int^{\mathcal{T}}_{0}\mathrm{d}t_{4}\;\overline{X(t_{1})X(t_{3})}\,\,\,\overline{X(t_{2})X(t_{4})}\\
&\qquad{}\times\cos\left(\omega_{1}(t_{1}-t_{2})\right)\cos\left(\omega_{2}(t_{3}-t_{4})\right)\\
&=2W_{\mathrm{cc}}^{2}\left(\omega_{1},\omega_{2};\mathcal{T}\right)+2W_{\mathrm{ss}}^{2}\left(\omega_{1},\omega_{2};\mathcal{T}\right)\\
&\qquad{}+2W_{\mathrm{cs}}^{2}\left(\omega_{1},\omega_{2};\mathcal{T}\right)+2W_{\mathrm{sc}}^{2}\left(\omega_{1},\omega_{2};\mathcal{T}\right)\,.\end{split}

(8)

Here we have defined

\begin{split}W_{\mathrm{cc}}\left(\omega_{1},\omega_{2};\mathcal{T}\right)&=\frac{1}{\mathcal{T}}\int^{\mathcal{T}}_{0}\mathrm{d}t_{1}\int^{\mathcal{T}}_{0}\mathrm{d}t_{2}\;\overline{X(t_{1})X(t_{2})}\cos\left(\omega_{1}t_{1}\right)\cos\left(\omega_{2}t_{2}\right)\,,\\
W_{\mathrm{ss}}\left(\omega_{1},\omega_{2};\mathcal{T}\right)&=\frac{1}{\mathcal{T}}\int^{\mathcal{T}}_{0}\mathrm{d}t_{1}\int^{\mathcal{T}}_{0}\mathrm{d}t_{2}\;\overline{X(t_{1})X(t_{2})}\sin\left(\omega_{1}t_{1}\right)\sin\left(\omega_{2}t_{2}\right)\,,\\
W_{\mathrm{cs}}\left(\omega_{1},\omega_{2};\mathcal{T}\right)&=\frac{1}{\mathcal{T}}\int^{\mathcal{T}}_{0}\mathrm{d}t_{1}\int^{\mathcal{T}}_{0}\mathrm{d}t_{2}\;\overline{X(t_{1})X(t_{2})}\cos\left(\omega_{1}t_{1}\right)\sin\left(\omega_{2}t_{2}\right)\,,\\
W_{\mathrm{sc}}\left(\omega_{1},\omega_{2};\mathcal{T}\right)&=\frac{1}{\mathcal{T}}\int^{\mathcal{T}}_{0}\mathrm{d}t_{1}\int^{\mathcal{T}}_{0}\mathrm{d}t_{2}\;\overline{X(t_{1})X(t_{2})}\sin\left(\omega_{1}t_{1}\right)\cos\left(\omega_{2}t_{2}\right)\,.\end{split}

(9)

We stress that the expressions (
2
) to (
9
) hold for arbitrary Gaussian processes. Correspondingly, the Pearson correlation coefficient of the random variables
S(\omega_{1},\mathcal{T})
and
S(\omega_{2},\mathcal{T})
, defined by

\displaystyle\rho(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})=\frac{\overline{S\left(\omega_{1},\mathcal{T}\right)S\left(\omega_{2},\mathcal{T}\right)}-\mu(\omega_{1},\mathcal{T})\mu(\omega_{2},\mathcal{T})}{\sqrt{\left(\overline{S^{2}\left(\omega_{1},\mathcal{T}\right)}-\mu^{2}(\omega_{1},\mathcal{T})\right)\left(\overline{S^{2}\left(\omega_{2},\mathcal{T}\right)}-\mu^{2}(\omega_{2},\mathcal{T})\right)}}\,,

(10)

is given by

\displaystyle\rho(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})=\frac{J(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})}{\sqrt{J(\omega_{1},\omega_{1})J(\omega_{2},\omega_{2})}}\,,

(11)

an expression which also holds for an arbitrary Gaussian process.

Lastly, we note that the covariance function in the frequency domain of the random amplitude
b_{\omega}
defined in
(
3
) can be formally expressed through the Pearson correlation coefficient via the relation

\displaystyle\overline{b_{\omega_{1}}b_{\omega_{2}}}=\frac{\overline{S(\omega_{1},\mathcal{T})S(\omega_{2},\mathcal{T})}}{\mu(\omega_{1},\mathcal{T})\mu(\omega_{2},\mathcal{T})}=1+\gamma(\omega_{1},\mathcal{T})\gamma(\omega_{2},\mathcal{T})\rho(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})\,,

(12)

where
\gamma(\omega,\mathcal{T})
is the coefficient of variation defined in eq. (
1
). As we show in what follows,
\overline{b_{\omega_{1}}b_{\omega_{2}}}
attains distinctly different values for Gaussian sub-diffusive processes, Brownian motion and super-diffusive processes, and thus offers an interesting criterion that permits to distinguish between these three cases from the analysis of correlations of
b
in the frequency domain.

3
Brownian motion and the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process

As a warming-up exercise, we start with two “simple” cases, namely, the
standard Brownian motion (BM) and the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) process, for which
we can obtain comparatively compact expressions that hold for arbitrary frequencies and observation times.

3.1
Brownian motion

The trajectory
X(t)
of a BM satisfies the stochastic Langevin equation

\displaystyle\dot{X}

\displaystyle=\zeta(t)\,,

(13)

where the dot stands for the time derivative, the viscosity is set for simplicity equal to unity,
and
\zeta
is a Gaussian zero-mean white noise satisfying

\displaystyle\overline{\zeta(t)}=0,\quad\overline{\zeta(t)\zeta(t^{\prime})}=2k_{\mathrm{B}}T\,\delta(t-t^{\prime})\,,

(14)

where
k_{\mathrm{B}}
is the Boltzmann constant (set to 1 from now on),
and
\delta(t)
is Dirac’s delta function. Equations (
13
) and (
14
) imply that
the two-time correlation function
\overline{X(t_{1})X(t_{2})}
obeys

\displaystyle\overline{X(t_{1})X(t_{2})}=2\,T\,\min(t_{1},t_{2})\,.

(15)

As a consequence, for the BM, the standard spectral density
\mu(\omega,\mathcal{T})
, defined in eq. (
7
), is given for arbitrary
\omega
and
\mathcal{T}
by

\displaystyle\mu(\omega,\mathcal{T})=\frac{4T}{\omega^{2}}\left[1-\frac{\sin(\omega\mathcal{T})}{\omega\mathcal{T}}\right]\,\qquad\mu(\omega)=\lim_{\mathcal{T}\to\infty}\mu(\omega,\mathcal{T})=\frac{4T}{\omega^{2}}\,.

(16)

See, e.g.
[
30
]
for more details.

\begin{split}J_{\mathrm{BM}}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})&=\frac{8T^{2}}{\omega_{1}^{2}\omega_{2}^{2}}\left[1-\frac{2\sin\left(\omega_{1}\mathcal{T}\right)}{\omega_{1}\mathcal{T}}-\frac{2\sin\left(\omega_{2}\mathcal{T}\right)}{\omega_{2}\mathcal{T}}\right.\\

&\qquad\left.{}-\frac{2\left(\left(\omega_{1}^{2}+\omega_{2}^{2}\right)\cos\left(\omega_{1}\mathcal{T}\right)\cos\left(\omega_{2}\mathcal{T}\right)+2\omega_{1}\omega_{2}\sin\left(\omega_{1}\mathcal{T}\right)\sin\left(\omega_{2}\mathcal{T}\right)\right)}{\left(\omega_{1}^{2}-\omega_{2}^{2}\right)^{2}\mathcal{T}^{2}}\right]\,.\end{split}

(17)

J_{\mathrm{BM}}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})
is rather rich and deserves a detailed discussion. First of all,
J_{\mathrm{BM}}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})
is an oscillatory and symmetrical function of both frequencies. When, e.g.,
\omega_{2}

. We observe that
S(\omega,\mathcal{T})
and
S(\alpha\,\omega,\mathcal{T})
are completely correlated within some well-defined
region of values of
\omega\mathcal{T}
, and such a correlation drops rapidly to the constant level
2/5
upon a further increase of
\omega\mathcal{T}
. The size of the window in which
S(\omega,\mathcal{T})
and
S(\alpha\omega,\mathcal{T})
are completely correlated increases as
\alpha
approaches
1
, which is not counter-intuitive.

Finally, summarizing our results for the BM and recalling that
\gamma_{\mathrm{BM}}(0,\mathcal{T})=\sqrt{2}
and
\gamma_{\mathrm{BM}}(\omega>0,\mathcal{T}=\infty)=\sqrt{5}/2

[
30
]
, we find that the covariance function of the random amplitude
b_{\omega}
(see eqs. (
3
) and (
12
)) in the frequency domain is given, as
\mathcal{T}\to\infty
, by

\overline{b_{\omega_{1}}b_{\omega_{2}}}=\begin{cases}3/2\,,&\text{for }\omega_{1}\neq\omega_{2},\quad\omega_{1},\omega_{2}>0;\\
7/4\,,&\text{for }\omega_{1}=0,\quad\omega_{2}>0;\\
9/4\,,&\text{for }\omega_{1}=\omega_{2}>0;\\
3\,,&\text{for }\omega_{1}=\omega_{2}=0.\end{cases}

(24)

This implies that the
b_{\omega}
exhibit some degree of correlation for different values of
\omega
.

3.2
Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process

The OU process obeys the Langevin equation

\displaystyle\dot{X}

\displaystyle=-X+\zeta(t)\,,

(25)

where
\zeta(t)
is a Gaussian noise whose properties are defined in eq. (
14
). At variance with the BM, this process approaches a stationary distribution when
t\to\infty
. The solution of eq. (
25
) for the initial condition
X(0)=0
and a given realization of the noise is given by

\displaystyle X(t)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-t}\int^{t}_{0}\mathrm{d}\tau\;\mathrm{e}^{\tau}\,\zeta(\tau)\,.

(26)

Consequently, the two-time correlation function of
X(t)
for
t_{2}\leq t_{1}
is given by

\displaystyle\overline{X(t_{1})X(t_{2})}=T\left[\exp\left(-(t_{1}-t_{2})\right)-\exp\left(-(t_{1}+t_{2})\right)\right]\,.

(27)

The correlation function
\overline{X(t_{1})X(t_{2})}
for
t_{2}\geq t_{1}
is obtained by merely interchanging
t_{2}
and
t_{1}
in this relation. Note that, in contrast to the BM whose mean-squared displacement grows without bounds in time, the variance of OU process is bounded. Indeed, setting
t_{1}=t_{2}=t
in eq. (
27
), we obtain
\overline{X^{2}(t)}=T\left(1-\exp(-2t)\right)
, and therefore the mean-squared displacement from the origin approaches the constant value
T
for
t\to\infty
.

Inserting the expression (
27
) into eqs. (
9
) and performing the integrations, we eventually find a rather lengthy expression for
J_{\mathrm{OU}}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})
, which holds for arbitrary
\omega_{1}
,
\omega_{2}
, and
\mathcal{T}
and it is unnecessary to copy here. We show in fig.
3
the behavior of

J_{\mathrm{OU}}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})
as a function of
\omega_{1}
for two values of the frequency
\omega_{2}
:
\omega_{2}=2
and
\omega_{2}=0
(see panels (a) and (b)). We observe that
J_{\mathrm{OU}}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})
behaves differently from the BM case in several aspects. First of all, for
\omega_{2}=2
the excess frequency-frequency correlation function exhibits only the peaks at
\omega_{1}=\pm\omega_{2}
, while the peak at
\omega_{1}=0
is absent. Moreover, the height of the peak at
\omega_{1}=\pm\omega_{2}\neq 0
is given by

\begin{split}J_{\mathrm{OU}}(\omega,\omega)&=\frac{4T^{2}}{(1+\omega^{2})^{2}}+\frac{4T^{2}\left(\omega^{2}-3\right)}{\left(1+\omega^{2}\right)^{3}\mathcal{T}}+\frac{2T^{2}\left(1+7\omega^{2}-\omega^{4}+\omega^{6}\right)}{\omega^{2}(1+\omega^{2})^{4}\mathcal{T}^{2}}\\
&\qquad{}+\frac{2T^{2}\left(3\omega^{2}-1\right)\cos\left(2\omega\mathcal{T}\right)}{\omega^{2}(1+\omega^{2})^{3}\mathcal{T}^{2}}+\frac{2T^{2}\left(\omega^{2}-3\right)\sin\left(2\omega\mathcal{T}\right)}{\omega(1+\omega^{2})^{3}\mathcal{T}^{2}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathcal{T}}\right)\,,\end{split}

(28)

where the symbol
\mathcal{O}\left(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathcal{T}}\right)
signifies that the omitted terms decay exponentially with the observation time. Therefore, the height of the peaks approaches a
\mathcal{T}
-independent value as

\omega\to\infty

J_{\mathrm{BM}}(\omega,\omega)
(see eq. (

\omega\to 0
of the expression (

J_{\mathrm{OU}}(0,0)

the height of the peak in case
\omega_{1}=\omega_{2}=0
must be evaluated by setting
\omega_{1}=0

\omega_{2}=0

\displaystyle J_{\mathrm{OU}}(0,0)=8T^{2}-\frac{24T^{2}}{\mathcal{T}}+\frac{18T^{2}}{\mathcal{T}^{2}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathcal{T}}\right)\,,

(29)

where the coefficient in front of

is twice what would be obtained directly from eq. (
28

We now dwell on the case
\omega_{1}\neq\omega_{2}
. Here, rather lengthy calculations show that

has the following large-
\mathcal{T}
asymptotic form:

\displaystyle J_{\mathrm{OU}}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})=\frac{2T^{2}A_{\mathrm{OU}}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})}{\mathcal{T}^{2}}+O\left(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathcal{T}}\right)\,,

(30)

where the omitted terms decay, in the leading order, in proportion to

A_{\mathrm{OU}}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})
in the first term on the right-hand-side of eq. (

\begin{split}A_{\mathrm{OU}}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})&=\frac{8\left(\omega_{1}^{2}+\omega_{2}^{2}\right)+\left(\omega_{1}^{2}-\omega_{2}^{2}\right)^{2}}{\left(1+\omega_{1}^{2}\right)\left(1+\omega_{2}^{2}\right)\left(\omega_{1}^{2}-\omega_{2}^{2}\right)^{2}}\\
&\qquad{}-\frac{4\left(\left(1+\omega_{1}\omega_{2}\right)^{2}+\omega_{1}\omega_{2}\left(\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}\right)^{2}\right)}{\left(1+\omega_{1}^{2}\right)^{2}\left(1+\omega_{2}^{2}\right)^{2}\left(\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}\right)^{2}}\cos\left(\left(\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}\right)\mathcal{T}\right)\\

&\qquad{}-\frac{2\left(3+\omega_{1}^{2}+\omega_{2}^{2}-\omega_{1}^{2}\omega_{2}^{2}\right)}{\left(1+\omega_{1}^{2}\right)^{2}\left(1+\omega_{2}^{2}\right)^{2}}\frac{\sin\left((\omega_{1}+\omega_{2})\mathcal{T}\right)}{\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}}\,.\end{split}

(31)

This expression contains the
\mathcal{T}
-independent first term, while the following ones oscillate with
\mathcal{T}
.

4
Brownian gyrator

We focus next on the Brownian gyrator (BG) model

[
37
]
, defined as
a pair of coupled OU processes
X(t)
and
Y(t)
, which obey
the following system of Langevin equations:

\begin{split}\dot{X}&=-X+u\,Y+\zeta_{x}(t)\,,\\
\dot{Y}&=-Y+u\,X+\zeta_{y}(t)\,.\end{split}

Here
\zeta_{x,y}
are Gaussian zero-mean white noises such that

\displaystyle\overline{\zeta_{i}(t)\zeta_{j}(t^{\prime})}=2T_{i}\delta_{i,j}\delta(t-t^{\prime})\,,\quad i,j=x,y\,,

(33)

where
\delta_{i,j}
is the Kronecker delta, while
T_{x}
and
T_{y}
are the temperatures (measured in units of the Boltzmann constant) of two thermal baths.

Despite its simplicity, the model defined by eqs. (
4
) exhibits a rather non-trivial physical behaviour. It was in fact realized in
[
38
]
that it represents a minimal model of a heat machine. When
u=0
,
X(t)
and
Y(t)
decouple and form two independent OU processes, leading us back to the case studied in the previous section
3.2
. On the other hand, when
0<|u|<1
and
T_{x}\neq T_{y}
, the system eventually reaches a non-equilibrium steady state, characterized by a non-vanishing average rotation frequency in the
(X,Y)
plane. This explains the name of the model.
Various aspects of the BG dynamical behaviour and of its steady-state properties
have been studied, see, e.g.,
[
24
,
25
]
and
[
40
,
41
,
42
,
39
,
43
,
44
,
45
,
46
,
47
,
48
,
49
]
.
In particular, a non-trivial fluctuation theorem was established by considering the response of the BG to external regular forces, what allowed to define
explicitly an effective temperatures
[
45
,
46
,
47
]
. We also remark that the
setting with constant forces exerted
on the BG is mathematically identical to
the one-dimensional bead-spring model studied via Brownian-dynamics simulations in

[
50
]
and analytically in
[
51
]
and
[
52
]
. A generalization of the BG model
for a system of two coupled noisy Kuramoto oscillators has been discussed in
[
53
]
.

Solving
equations (
4
) for the trajectories, with the initial condition
X(0)=Y(0)=0
, we find that for given realisations of noises
\zeta_{x}(\tau)
and
\zeta_{y}(\tau)
,
X(t)
and
Y(t)
are given by

\begin{split}X(t)&=\mathrm{e}^{-t}\int^{t}_{0}\mathrm{d}\tau\;\mathrm{e}^{\tau}\cosh\left(u(t-\tau)\right)\zeta_{x}(\tau)+\mathrm{e}^{-t}\int^{t}_{0}\mathrm{d}\tau\;\mathrm{e}^{\tau}\sinh\left(u(t-\tau)\right)\zeta_{y}(\tau)\,,\\
Y(t)&=\mathrm{e}^{-t}\int^{t}_{0}\mathrm{d}\tau\;\mathrm{e}^{\tau}\sinh\left(u(t-\tau)\right)\zeta_{x}(\tau)+\mathrm{e}^{-t}\int^{t}_{0}\mathrm{d}\tau\;\mathrm{e}^{\tau}\cosh\left(u(t-\tau)\right)\zeta_{y}(\tau)\,.\end{split}

(34)

We thus obtain the following expressions for the two-time correlation function of the
X
-component. For
t_{1}\geq t_{2}
we have

\begin{split}&\overline{X(t_{1})X(t_{2})}=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-t_{1}-t_{2}}}{2(1-u^{2})}\Bigg[\cosh\left(u(t_{1}-t_{2})\right)\\
&\qquad{}\times\Big(T_{y}+\mathrm{e}^{t_{2}}\Big(T_{x}\cosh(t_{2})+\left(3T_{x}+2u^{2}\left(T_{y}-T_{x}\right)\right)\sinh(t_{2})\Big)\Big)\\
&\qquad{}-\left(T_{x}+T_{y}\right)\\
&\qquad{}\times\Big(\cosh\left(u(t_{1}-t_{2})\right)+u\Big(\sinh\left(u(t_{1}-t_{2})\right)+\mathrm{e}^{-2t_{2}}\sinh\left(u(t_{1}-t_{2})\right)\Big)\Big)\Bigg].\end{split}

(35)

For
t_{2}\geq t_{1}
we have

\begin{split}&\overline{X(t_{1})X(t_{2})}=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-t_{1}-t_{2}}}{2(1-u^{2})}\Bigg[\cosh\left(u(t_{2}-t_{1})\right)\\
&\qquad{}\times\Big(T_{y}+\mathrm{e}^{t_{1}}\Big(T_{x}\cosh(t_{1})+\left(3T_{x}+2u^{2}\left(T_{y}-T_{x}\right)\right)\sinh(t_{1})\Big)\Big)\\
&\qquad{}-\left(T_{x}+T_{y}\right)\\
&\qquad{}\times\Big(\cosh\left(u(t_{1}+t_{2})\right)+u\Big(\sinh\left(u(t_{1}+t_{2})\right)-\mathrm{e}^{2t_{1}}\sinh\left(u(t_{2}-t_{1})\right)\Big)\Big)\Bigg]\,.\end{split}

(36)

As one may readily check, the variance
\overline{X^{2}(t)}
of the
X
-component is bounded in the

\displaystyle\overline{X^{2}(t)}=\frac{T_{x}+(u^{2}/2)(T_{y}-T_{x})}{1-u^{2}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\mathrm{e}^{-(1-|u|)t}\right)\,.

(37)

The latter expression naturally reduces to the one obtained for the OU process when
u=0
. A similar expression holds for the
Y

We now consider the PSD of the

-component of the BG. First, by
inserting the expressions (

5
Fractional Brownian motion

We finally consider the fractional Brownian motion (FBM)
[
54
]
. This is a Gaussian process with zero mean and covariance function

\displaystyle\overline{X(t_{1})X(t_{2})}=D\left(t_{1}^{2H}+t_{2}^{2H}-|t_{1}-t_{2}|^{2H}\right)\,,

(44)

where the
Hurst index

H
is a real number such that
0<H<1
.
Notice that the FBM is an
H
-parametrized family of anomalous diffusion processes (except for the case
H=1/2
, when one recovers the standard BM with independent increments).
For
H<1/2
, the process is sub-diffusive as one can readily infer from eq. (
44
) by setting
t_{1}=t_{2}=t
to find that the mean-squared displacement obeys
\overline{X^{2}(t)}=2D\,t^{2H}
, where
2H<1
. One can show with little effort that the increments of the process in this case have negative long-ranged correlations.
On the contrary, for
H>1/2
the exponent
2H
exceeds unity and one observes a super-diffusive behaviour. In this latter case the increments have positive long-ranged correlations.
Therefore, one naturally expects for such a family of anomalous diffusion processes a richer behaviour than the one discussed in previous sections.

H
satisfying
0<H<1/2
. In this case we take advantage of the fact that for
H=1/4
we can obtain an explicit, albeit lengthy, expression of the excess correlation function
J_{\mathrm{FBM}}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})
. We checked that the behavior for different values of
H
in this range, obtained by numerical integration of eq. (
8
), is qualitatively similar.

(solid green curve) and

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{FBM}}(\omega,0)\simeq\frac{1+H}{\sqrt{2}c_{H}}\frac{1}{\left(\omega\mathcal{T}\right)^{1-2H}}

(49)

(see eq. (

)), the asymptotic form in eq. (

\mathcal{T}
is fixed and
\omega\to\infty

Second,

\omega

, but the numerical value is wrong. The point is that

We consider at last the behaviour in a more general case when

an expression that holds for any
H\in(0,1)

48

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{FBM}}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})\simeq\frac{2}{c_{H}^{2}(\omega_{1}\omega_{2})^{1-2H}{\cal T}^{2-4H}}\,.

Hence, in this more general case

Summarizing, for a sub-diffusive FBM the two-frequency correlation function in eq. (

\mathcal{T}\to\infty
,

\overline{b_{\omega_{1}}b_{\omega_{2}}}=\begin{cases}1\,,&\text{for }\omega_{1}\neq\omega_{2};\qquad\omega_{1},\omega_{2}>0;\\
1\,,&\text{for }\omega_{1}=0,\,\omega_{2}>0;\\
2\,,&\text{for }\omega_{1}=\omega_{2}\neq 0;\\
3\,,&\text{for }\omega_{1}=\omega_{2}=0.\end{cases}

This implies again that for
\omega_{1}\neq\omega_{2}
the

Super-diffusive fractional Brownian motion

We turn finally to super-diffusive FBM, i.e., to the case with

6
Conclusions

In conclusion, we examined the correlations of the reduced spectral density
b_{\omega}=S(\omega,\mathcal{T})/\mu(\omega,\mathcal{T})
for a class of Gaussian random processes, where
S(\omega,\mathcal{T})
is the spectral density of a single trajectory
X(t)
of the process, eq. (
4
),
\omega
is the frequency,
\mathcal{T}
is the observation time, and
\mu(\omega,\mathcal{T})=\overline{S(\omega,\mathcal{T})}
is the average of
S(\omega,\mathcal{T})
over all realizations of the process. It was shown in previous works that for a number of different classes of Gaussian stochastic processes, the spectral density
S(\omega,\mathcal{T})
is a random variable whose probability distribution depends on
S(\omega,\mathcal{T})
and
\mu(\omega,\mathcal{T})
only in the combination
b_{\omega}=S(\omega,\mathcal{T})/\mu(\omega,\mathcal{T})
. (The resulting distribution depends moreover on
\omega
only via the coefficient of variation
\gamma(\omega,\mathcal{T})
.) We have therefore looked at
b_{\omega}
as an
\omega
-dependent random quantity. Looking at the behavior of this quantity for different values of
\omega
and for different cases, we have shown that the values of
b_{\omega}
can be either fully uncorrelated (e.g., for the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, for the Brownian gyrator, and for the subdiffusive Brownian motion), partially correlated (e.g., for the ordinary, diffusive Brownian motion) or totally correlated (e.g., for superdiffusive Brownian motion, when both frequencies are non-vanishing). Therefore
b_{\omega}
can be used as a sensitive probe of the nature of the process, that is available upon inspection of a single trajectory, provided the observation time
\mathcal{T}
is large enough. It can thus supplement in this role the coefficient of variation
\gamma
. Indeed, it was suggested in
[
31
]
to use
\gamma
as a criterion that permits to distinguish between different kinds of Gaussian processes. This requires, however, to evaluate either the large-
\omega
asymptotical behaviour of
\gamma
in case of sub-diffusion and diffusion, or its ageing properties (i.e.,its
\mathcal{T}
-dependence) for superdiffusion, what may be somewhat difficult to access. Thus using
b_{\omega}
appears advantageous, because it does not require to evaluate the asymptotical behavior, and the values of the frequencies can be arbitrarily chosen. In particular, if we look at the
\overline{b_{\omega}b_{\omega^{\prime}}}
correlation function when one of the frequencies vanish in the super-diffusive FBM case, we are able to extract the value of the Hurst parameter
H
.

It remains the challenging problem of extending this kind of analysis to non-Gaussian processes.

Appendix A
Analytical results for FBM.

A.1
Excess correlation function
J_{\mathrm{FBM}}(0,\omega)
.

We take advantage of the representation of the
covariance function of the FBM with arbitrary

H
in the integral form (see
[
57
]
), in which the kernel
has a convenient factorised dependence on
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
:

\begin{split}t_{1}^{2H}+t_{2}^{2H}-|t_{1}-t_{2}|^{2H}&=\frac{2c_{H}}{\pi}\int^{\infty}_{0}\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{z^{2H+1}}\\
&\qquad{}\times\left[\sin(zt_{1})\sin(zt_{2})+(1-\cos(zt_{1}))(1-\cos(zt_{2}))\right]\,,\end{split}

(56)

where

c_{H}=\Gamma(2H+1)\sin(\pi H).

(57)

Inserting this expression into the definition of
J(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})
, we perform the integrations over the time variables, and eventually, over
z
. In doing so, we find that
J(0,\omega)
is given by

J_{\mathrm{FBM}}(0,\omega)=D^{2}\mathcal{T}^{2+4H}\mathcal{J}_{H}(w)\,,\quad w=\omega\mathcal{T}\,,

(58)

where
\mathcal{J}_{H}(w)
is the following function of the dimensionless variable
w
only:

\mathcal{J}_{H}(w)=\frac{1}{2(1+H)^{2}(1+2H)^{4}}\left[\frac{w^{2}}{(2H+3)^{2}}\mathcal{K}_{1}^{2}(w,H)+\mathcal{K}_{2}^{2}(w,H)\right]\,.

(59)

In this expression, one has

\begin{split}\mathcal{K}_{1}(w,H)&=(2H+3)\,_{1}F_{2}\left(1;H+\frac{3}{2},H+2;-\frac{w^{2}}{4}\right)\\
&\qquad{}-2\,_{1}F_{2}\left(2;H+2,H+\frac{5}{2};-\frac{w^{2}}{4}\right)\\
&\qquad{}-(1+2H)\left(4H^{2}+8H+3\right)\,_{1}F_{2}\left(H+1;\frac{3}{2},H+2;-\frac{w^{2}}{4}\right)\\
&\qquad{}+2(1+H)(1+2H){}_{1}F_{2}\left(H+\frac{3}{2};\frac{3}{2},H+\frac{5}{2};-\frac{w^{2}}{4}\right)\\
&\qquad{}-2(1+H)(1+2H)(2H+3)\frac{(1-\cos(w))}{w^{2}}\,,\end{split}

(60)

and

\begin{split}\mathcal{K}_{2}(w,H)&=-2(H+1)\,_{1}F_{2}\left(1;H+1,H+\frac{3}{2};-\frac{w^{2}}{4}\right)\\
&\qquad{}-\,_{1}F_{2}\left(H+1;\frac{1}{2},H+2;-\frac{w^{2}}{4}\right)\\
&\qquad{}-2H\,_{1}F_{2}\left(H+1;\frac{1}{2},H+2;-\frac{w^{2}}{4}\right)\\
&\qquad{}+\,_{2}F_{3}\left(1,\frac{3}{2};\frac{1}{2},H+\frac{3}{2},H+2;-\frac{w^{2}}{4}\right)\\
&\qquad{}+2\left(2H^{2}+3H+1\right)\,_{1}F_{2}\left(H+\frac{1}{2};\frac{1}{2},H+\frac{3}{2};-\frac{w^{2}}{4}\right)\\
&\qquad{}+2(H^{2}+3H+1)\frac{\sin(w)}{w}\,,\end{split}

(61)

where
{}_{1}F_{2}
and
{}_{2}F_{3}
are hypergeometric functions. The expression (
58
) is valid for arbitrary

H\in(0,1)
and for arbitrary values of
\mathcal{T}
and
\omega
. In particular, for
\omega=0
it reduces to expression (
46
).

We next consider the large-
w
behaviour of
J_{\mathrm{FBM}}(0,\omega)
in eq. (
58
), which is realised when either
\omega
is fixed and
\mathcal{T}
tends to infinity, or alternatively,
\mathcal{T}
is fixed but
\omega\to\infty
. Expanding the hypergeometric functions in the limit when
w=(\omega\mathcal{T})\to\infty
, we find

\begin{split}&J_{\mathrm{FBM}}(0,\omega)=\frac{2D^{2}\mathcal{T}^{4H}}{\omega^{2}}\left[\Bigg(\sin(w)-\frac{c_{H}}{w^{2H}}-\frac{1+(1-2H)\cos(w)}{w}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{w^{2H+1}}\right)\Bigg)^{2}\right.\\
&\qquad\qquad\left.{}+\Bigg(\cos(w)-\frac{c_{H}\cot(w)}{w^{2H}}+\frac{(1-2H)\sin(w)}{w}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{w^{2H+1}}\right)\Bigg)^{2}\right],\end{split}

(62)

where
c_{H}
is defined in eq. (
57
). This expression is dominated by the first terms in the parentheses, that oscillate as functions of
w
. We thus arrive at the asymptotic form in eq. (
45
). Notice that
this expression is valid
for any
H
such that
0<H<1
. The finite-
w
corrections to the leading behaviour in eq. (
45
) are however different in the cases
H<1/2
,
H=1/2
or
H>1/2
. Indeed, in case of sub-diffusion, the dominant correction terms is given by the second terms in brackets
in eq. (
62
), while in the super-diffusive case is given by the third terms.

A.2
Large-
\mathcal{T}
asymptotic behaviour of the excess correlation function
J_{\mathrm{FBM}}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})
.

Here we discuss the large-
\mathcal{T}
behaviour of the excess correlation function
J_{\mathrm{FBM}}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})
in the more general case when
\omega_{1}\neq\omega_{2}
and both frequencies are non-vanishing. We start by appropriately rescaling the integration variables in eqs. (
9
) to get, e.g., for

, the following representation

\frac{W_{\mathrm{cc}}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2};\mathcal{T})}{D\mathcal{T}^{2H+1}}=\int^{1}_{0}\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}\;\cos(\omega_{1}\mathcal{T}\tau_{1})\int^{1}_{0}\mathrm{d}\tau_{2}\;\left(\tau_{1}^{2H}+\tau_{2}^{2H}-|\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}|^{2H}\right)\cos(\omega_{2}\mathcal{T}\tau_{2})\,.

(63)

Similar expression can be obtained for
W_{\mathrm{cs}}
,
W_{\mathrm{sc}}
and
W_{\mathrm{ss}}
and will differ from the one in eq. (
63
) only by the sine and cosine factors.
The integrals corresponding to the first and the second terms in the covariance function can be performed analytically
and give, in the leading in the limit order for
\mathcal{T}\to\infty
,

\begin{split}&\int^{1}_{0}\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}\;\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}\cos(\omega_{1}\mathcal{T}\tau_{1})\\
\sin(\omega_{1}\mathcal{T}\tau_{1})\\
\end{array}\right\}\int^{1}_{0}\mathrm{d}\tau_{2}\;\tau_{2}^{2H}\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}\cos(\omega_{2}\mathcal{T}\tau_{2})\\
\sin(\omega_{2}\mathcal{T}\tau_{2})\\
\end{array}\right\}\\
&\qquad=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}\sin(\omega_{1}\mathcal{T})\\
1-\cos(\omega_{1}\mathcal{T})\\
\end{array}\right\}\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}\sin(\omega_{2}\mathcal{T})\\
-\cos(\omega_{2}\mathcal{T})\\
\end{array}\right\}\frac{1}{\omega_{1}\omega_{2}\mathcal{T}^{2}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\mathcal{T}^{3}}\right)\,.\end{split}

(64)

This asymptotic expression is valid for any
0<H<1
. The analysis of the integrals involving the third, non-local term in the covariance of the FBM is a bit more delicate and we first would like to cast it in a more transparent form. To this end, we expand both cosine functions in powers of
\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{2}
, perform the integrals, and then resum the series. In doing so, we get

\begin{split}&\int^{1}_{0}\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}\;\cos(\omega_{1}\mathcal{T}\tau_{1})\int^{1}_{0}\mathrm{d}\tau_{2}\;|\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}|^{2H}\cos(\omega_{2}\mathcal{T}\tau_{2})\\
&\qquad=\frac{\Gamma(2H+1)}{(\omega_{2}\mathcal{T})^{2H+2}}\int^{\omega_{2}\mathcal{T}}_{0}u^{2H+1}du\,\cos\left(\frac{\omega_{1}}{\omega_{2}}u\right)\,E_{2,2(H+1)}(-u^{2})\\
&\qquad\qquad{}+\frac{\Gamma(2H+1)}{(\omega_{1}\mathcal{T})^{2H+2}}\int^{\omega_{1}\mathcal{T}}_{0}u^{2H+1}du\,\cos\left(\frac{\omega_{2}}{\omega_{1}}u\right)\,E_{2,2(H+1)}(-u^{2})\,,\end{split}

(65)

where

\displaystyle E_{2,2(H+1)}(u)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{u^{k}}{\Gamma(2k+2(H+1))}

(66)

is the Mittag-Leffler function, whose behaviour is well-documented
[
58
]
. In a similar way, we obtain
the following results:

\begin{split}&\int^{1}_{0}\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}\;\cos(\omega_{1}\mathcal{T}\tau_{1})\int^{1}_{0}\mathrm{d}\tau_{2}\;|\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}|^{2H}\sin(\omega_{2}\mathcal{T}\tau_{2})\\
&\qquad=\frac{\Gamma(2H+1)}{(\omega_{2}\mathcal{T})^{2H+2}}\int^{\omega_{2}\mathcal{T}}_{0}u^{2H+2}\mathrm{d}u\;\cos\left(\frac{\omega_{1}}{\omega_{2}}u\right)\,E_{2,2H+3}(-u^{2})\,;\end{split}

(67)

\begin{split}&\int^{1}_{0}\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}\;\sin(\omega_{1}\mathcal{T}\tau_{1})\int^{1}_{0}d\tau_{2}\,|\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}|^{2H}\cos(\omega_{2}\mathcal{T}\tau_{2})\\
&\qquad=\frac{\Gamma(2H+1)}{(\omega_{1}\mathcal{T})^{2H+2}}\int^{\omega_{1}\mathcal{T}}_{0}u^{2H+2}\mathrm{d}u\;\cos\left(\frac{\omega_{2}}{\omega_{1}}u\right)\,E_{2,2H+3}(-u^{2})\,;\end{split}

(68)

\begin{split}&\int^{1}_{0}\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}\;\sin(\omega_{1}\mathcal{T}\tau_{1})\int^{1}_{0}\mathrm{d}\tau_{2}\;|\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}|^{2H}\sin(\omega_{2}\mathcal{T}\tau_{2})\\
&\qquad=\frac{\Gamma(2H+1)}{(\omega_{1}\mathcal{T})^{2H+2}}\int^{\omega_{1}\mathcal{T}}_{0}u^{2H+2}\mathrm{d}u\;\sin\left(\frac{\omega_{2}}{\omega_{1}}u\right)\,E_{2,2H+3}(-u^{2})\\
&\qquad\qquad{}+\frac{\Gamma(2H+1)}{(\omega_{2}\mathcal{T})^{2H+2}}\int^{\omega_{2}\mathcal{T}}_{0}u^{2H+2}\mathrm{d}u\;\sin\left(\frac{\omega_{1}}{\omega_{2}}u\right)\,E_{2,2H+3}(-u^{2})\,.\end{split}

(69)

We now turn to the analysis of the leading large-
{\cal T}
asymptotic behaviour of expressions (
67
) to (
69
). To this end, we first note that the leading large-
u
asymptotic behaviour of the Mittag-Leffler function obeys

\displaystyle E_{2,\beta}(-u^{2})\simeq\frac{1}{\Gamma(\beta-2)}\frac{1}{u^{2}}\,.

(70)

Consequently, the integral which enters eq. (
67
) diverges on the upper terminal of
integration in the super-diffusive case, and converges to a finite value when
{\cal T}\to\infty
in the sub-diffusive case. This yields

\begin{split}&\int^{1}_{0}\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}\cos(\omega_{1}{\cal T}\tau_{1})\int^{1}_{0}\mathrm{d}\tau_{2}\;|\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}|^{2H}\cos(\omega_{2}{\cal T}\tau_{2})\\
&\qquad\simeq\begin{cases}\dfrac{2H}{\omega_{1}\omega_{2}{\cal T}^{3}}\left(\dfrac{\sin(\omega_{1}{\cal T})}{\omega_{2}}+\dfrac{\sin(\omega_{2}{\cal T})}{\omega_{1}}\right)\,,&\text{for }H\in(1/2,1),\\
\dfrac{\cos(\pi H)\Gamma(2H+1)\left(\omega_{2}^{2H}-\omega_{1}^{2H}\right)}{\omega_{1}^{2H}\omega_{2}^{2H}\left(\omega_{2}^{2}-\omega_{1}^{2}\right){\cal T}^{2H+2}}\,,&\text{for }H\in(0,1/2)\,,\end{cases}\end{split}

(71)

where we took advantage of the identity

\displaystyle\int^{\infty}_{0}u^{2H+1}\,\mathrm{d}u\;\cos(\lambda u)\,E_{2,2(H+1)}(-u^{2})=\frac{\cos(\pi H)}{|\lambda|^{2H}\left(1-\lambda^{2}\right)},

(72)

which holds for
H\in(0,1/2)
. Comparing eq. (
67
) and eq. (
64
), we infer that the dominant contribution to the large-
{\cal T}
behaviour of
W_{\mathrm{cc}}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2};{\cal T})
is provided by the latter, such that
W_{\mathrm{cc}}
has the following asymptotic form

\displaystyle W_{\mathrm{cc}}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2};{\cal T})\simeq\frac{2D\sin(\omega_{1}{\cal T})\sin(\omega_{2}{\cal T})}{\omega_{1}\omega_{2}}{\cal T}^{2H-1}\,,

(73)

which is valid for any
H
.

Further on, inspecting the kernels in the integrals in eqs. (
67
), (
68
) and (
69
), we realise that the large-
u
tails do not decay fast enough to ensure the convergence of the integrals even in the sub-diffusive case. Performing some rather straightforward calculations, we then find that for any
H\in(0,1)
the leading asymptotic behaviour of the integrals is given by

\begin{split}&\int^{1}_{0}\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}\;\cos(\omega_{1}{\cal T}\tau_{1})\int^{1}_{0}\mathrm{d}\tau_{2}\;|\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}|^{2H}\sin(\omega_{2}{\cal T}\tau_{2})\simeq\frac{\sin(\omega_{1}{\cal T})}{\omega_{1}\omega_{2}{\cal T}^{2}}\,,\\
&\int^{1}_{0}\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}\;\sin(\omega_{1}{\cal T}\tau_{1})\int^{1}_{0}\mathrm{d}\tau_{2}\;|\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}|^{2H}\cos(\omega_{2}{\cal T}\tau_{2})\simeq\frac{\sin(\omega_{2}{\cal T})}{\omega_{1}\omega_{2}{\cal T}^{2}}\,,\\
\end{split}

(74)

and

\begin{split}&\int^{1}_{0}\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}\;\sin(\omega_{1}{\cal T}\tau_{1})\int^{1}_{0}\mathrm{d}\tau_{2}\;|\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}|^{2H}\sin(\omega_{2}{\cal T}\tau_{2})\simeq-\frac{\cos(\omega_{1}{\cal T})+\cos(\omega_{2}{\cal T})}{\omega_{1}\omega_{2}{\cal T}^{2}}\,.\end{split}

(75)

Taking into account the expressions (
64
), we eventually find

\begin{split}W_{\mathrm{cs}}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2};{\cal T})&\simeq-\frac{2D\sin(\omega_{1}{\cal T})\cos(\omega_{2}{\cal T})}{\omega_{1}\omega_{2}}{\cal T}^{2H-1}\,,\\
W_{\mathrm{sc}}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2};{\cal T})&\simeq-\frac{2D\cos(\omega_{1}{\cal T})\sin(\omega_{2}{\cal T})}{\omega_{1}\omega_{2}}{\cal T}^{2H-1}\,,\end{split}

(76)

and

\displaystyle W_{ss}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2};{\cal T})

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{2D\cos(\omega_{1}{\cal T})\cos(\omega_{2}{\cal T})}{\omega_{1}\omega_{2}}{\cal T}^{2H-1}\,,

(77)

which yields our asymptotic expression for
J_{\mathrm{FBM}}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})
, eq. (
50
).
