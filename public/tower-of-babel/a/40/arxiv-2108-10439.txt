Large Weyl sums and Hausdorff dimension
Roger C. Baker, Changhao Chen, Igor E. Shparlinski

Abstract
. We obtain the exact value of the Hausdorff dimension of the set of coefficients of Gauss sums which for a given α ∈ ( 1 / 2 , 1 ) \alpha\in(1/2,1) achieve the order at least N α N^{\alpha} for infinitely many sum lengths N N . For Weyl sums with polynomials of degree d ⩾ 3 d\geqslant 3 we obtain a new upper bound on the Hausdorff dimension of the set of polynomial coefficients corresponding to large values of Weyl sums. Our methods also work for monomial sums, match the previously known lower bounds, just giving exact value for the corresponding Hausdorff dimension when α \alpha is close to 1 1 . We also obtain a nearly tight bound in a similar question with arbitrary integer sequences of polynomial growth.

11L15, 11K55

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Set-up and motivation

For
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in\mathsf{T}_{d}

where

\mathsf{T}_{d}=[0,1]^{d}

is the
d
-dimensional unit cube, we define the
Weyl sums

of length
N
as

S_{d}(\mathbf{x};N)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}{\mathbf{\,e}}\left(x_{1}n+\ldots+x_{d}n^{d}\right)

where
{\mathbf{\,e}}(z)=\exp(2\pi iz)
. These sums were originally introduced by Weyl to study equidistribution of fractional parts of polynomials and later find their applications to the circle method and Riemann zeta function.

For many applications of Weyl sums, the key problem is to estimate the size of the sum
S_{d}(\mathbf{x};N)
. There are often three kinds of estimates of Weyl sums, namely individual bounds, mean value bounds and almost all bounds. Despite more than a century since these sums were introduced, their behaviour for individual values of
\mathbf{x}
is not well understood, see
[
10
,
11
,
32
]
.

Much more is known about the average behaviour of
S_{d}(\mathbf{x};N)
. The recent advances of Bourgain, Demeter and Guth
[
9
]
(for
d\geqslant 4
)
and Wooley
[
39
]
(for
d=3
) (see also
[
40
]
) towards the optimal form Vinogradov mean value theorem imply the estimate

N^{s(d)}\leqslant\int_{\mathsf{T}_{d}}|S_{d}(\mathbf{x};N)|^{2s(d)}d\mathbf{x}\leqslant N^{s(d)+o(1)},

where

s(d)=d(d+1)/2

and is best possible up to
o(1)
in the exponent of
N
.

We study exceptional sets of
\mathbf{x}\in\mathsf{T}_{d}
, which generate abnormally large
Weyl sums
S_{d}(\mathbf{x};N)
.

The first results concerning the almost all behaviour of Weyl sums are due to Hardy and Littlewood
[
27
]
who have estimated the following special sums

\mathsf{G}(x,N)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}{\mathbf{\,e}}\left(xn^{2}\right),

in terms of the continued fraction expansion of
x
. Among other things Hardy and Littlewood
[
27
]
proved that for almost all
x\in\mathsf{T}
,

\left|\mathsf{G}(x,N)\right|\leqslant N^{1/2+o(1)},\quad\text{as}\quad N\rightarrow\infty.

Their idea has been expanded upon by Fiedler, Jurkat and Körner
[
25
, Theorem 2]
who give the following optimal lower and upper bounds.
Suppose that
\{f(n)\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is a non-decreasing sequence of positive numbers. Then for almost all
x\in\mathsf{T}
one has

(1.1)

\uplim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{|\mathsf{G}(x,N)|}{\sqrt{N}f(N)}<\infty\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{nf(n)^{4}}<\infty.

For the sums
S_{2}(\mathbf{x};N)
,
\mathbf{x}\in\mathsf{T}_{2}
, Fedotov and Klopp
[
24
]
have given a similar result, however
adding the term
{\mathbf{\,e}}(x_{1}n)
leads to more cancellations in the sums
S_{2}(\mathbf{x};N)
. Suppose that
\{g(n)\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is a non-decreasing sequence of positive numbers. Then for almost all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathsf{T}_{2}
one has

(1.2)

\uplim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\left|S_{2}(\mathbf{x};N)\right|}{\sqrt{N}g(\ln N)}<\infty\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{g(n)^{6}}<\infty.

It is natural to expect that analogues of (
1.1
), (
1.2
) hold for Weyl sums
S_{d}(\mathbf{x};N)
with any
d\geqslant 3
, however this question seems to be still open. However, we have the following nearly sharp bounds. For
d\geqslant 3
, Chen and Shparlinski
[
14
,
16
]
have shown in two different ways in
[
14
, Appendix A]
, and
[
16
, Theorem 2.1]
that for almost all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathsf{T}_{d}
one has

(1.3)

\left|S_{d}(\mathbf{x};N)\right|\leqslant N^{1/2+o(1)}\quad\text{as}\quad N\rightarrow\infty.

Recently, Chen, Kerr, Maynard and Shparlinski
[
13
, Theorem 2.3]
have shown that the exponent
1/2
is optimal, that is, there exists a constant
c>0
such that for almost all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathsf{T}_{d}
, the inequality

(1.4)

|S_{d}(\mathbf{x};N)|\geqslant cN^{1/2}

holds for infinitely many
N
.

This motivates our study of the “the exceptional sets” of Weyl sums. Precisely, for
1/2<\alpha<1
define

{\mathcal{E}}_{d,\alpha}=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathsf{T}_{d}:\penalty\ |S_{d}(\mathbf{x};N)|\geqslant N^{\alpha}\text{ for infinitely many }N\in{\mathbb{N}}\}.

Chen and Shparlinski
[
14
, Theorem 1.3]
show that for any
d\geqslant 2
and
1/2<\alpha<1
the set
{\mathcal{E}}_{d,\alpha}
is of second category in the sense of Baire, and the proof of
[
14
, Theorem 1.3]
implies that the set
{\mathcal{E}}_{d,\alpha}
is a dense subset of
\mathsf{T}_{d}
. Therefore, the
Minkowski dimension
(or box dimension)
of
{\mathcal{E}}_{d,\alpha}
is
d
. See
[
23
]
for more details on the Minkowski dimension.

The above results (
1.2
) and (
1.3
) imply that
{\mathcal{E}}_{d,\alpha}
is of zero Lebesgue measure for all
d\geqslant 2
and any
\alpha\in(1/2,1)
.
For sets of Lebesgue measure zero, it is common to use the
Hausdorff dimension
to describe their size and structure, and we are going to estimate the Hausdorff dimension of the set
{\mathcal{E}}_{d,\alpha}
for any
d\geqslant 2
and any
\alpha\in(1/2,1)
. We first recall the formal definition of Hausdorff dimension, and we refer to
[
23
,
33
]
for
more details.

Definition 1.1
.

The Hausdorff dimension of a set
{\mathcal{F}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is defined as

\displaystyle\dim{\mathcal{F}}=\inf\Bigl\{s>0:\penalty\ \forall\,

\displaystyle\varepsilon>0,\penalty\ \exists\,\{{\mathcal{U}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty},\ {\mathcal{U}}_{i}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d},

\displaystyle\text{such that }{\mathcal{F}}\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}{\mathcal{U}}_{i}\text{ and }\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\left(\operatorname{diam}{\mathcal{U}}_{i}\right)^{s}<\varepsilon\Bigr\},

where

\operatorname{diam}{\mathcal{U}}=\sup\{\|u-v\|:\penalty\ u,v\in{\mathcal{U}}\}

and
\|w\|
is the Euclidean norm in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

We remark that we could also define the set
{\mathcal{E}}_{d,\alpha}
for
\alpha\in(0,1/2]
. However, by (
1.4
)
the set
{\mathcal{E}}_{d,\alpha}
is of full Lebesgue measure. This, by [17, Theorem 2.5] and the definition of the Hausdorff dimension, is enough to conclude that

\dim{\mathcal{E}}_{d,\alpha}=d.

For an integer
d\geqslant 3
and real

\alpha\in(1/2,1)
some explicit upper and lower bounds on
\dim{\mathcal{E}}_{d,\alpha}
have been given
in
[
14
,
15
,
16
]
. In particular, for any
\alpha\in(1/2,1)
, there are explicit functions
\mathfrak{l}(d,\alpha),\mathfrak{u}(d,\alpha)
such that

0<\mathfrak{l}(d,\alpha)\leqslant\dim{\mathcal{E}}_{d,\alpha}\leqslant\mathfrak{u}(d,\alpha)<d.

We show more details in the following. For
d\geqslant 2
, let

\kappa_{d}=\max_{\nu=1,\ldots,d}\min\left\{\frac{1}{2\nu},\frac{1}{2d-\nu}\right\}.

For each
1/2<\alpha<1
and any cube
{\mathfrak{Q}}\subseteq\mathsf{T}_{d}
we have the following lower bounds of
\dim{\mathcal{E}}_{d,\alpha}
:

(i)

for
d=2
,

\dim{\mathcal{E}}_{2,\alpha}\cap{\mathfrak{Q}}\geqslant 3(1-\alpha)/2;

(ii)

for
d\geqslant 3
,

\dim{\mathcal{E}}_{d,\alpha}\cap{\mathfrak{Q}}\geqslant 2\kappa_{d}(1-\alpha).

For the upper bound of
\dim{\mathcal{E}}_{d,\alpha}
with
d\geqslant 2
and
1/2<\alpha<1
, we have

\dim{\mathcal{E}}_{d,\alpha}\leqslant\mathfrak{u}(d,\alpha),

where

\mathfrak{u}(d,\alpha)=\min_{k=0,\ldots,d-1}\frac{(2d^{2}+4d)(1-\alpha)+k(k+1)}{4-2\alpha+2k}.

It is not hard to show
\mathfrak{u}(d,\alpha)<d
for any
\alpha\in(1/2,1)
.

In fact for
\alpha\rightarrow 1
the behaviour of

\dim{\mathcal{E}}_{d,\alpha}
is understood reasonably well as a combination of the above mentioned lower and upper bounds, implies that there are positive constants
c_{1}(d),c_{2}(d)
such that

c_{1}(d)\leqslant\liminf_{\alpha\rightarrow 1}\,(1-\alpha)^{-1}\dim{\mathcal{E}}_{d,\alpha}\leqslant\limsup_{\alpha\rightarrow 1}\,(1-\alpha)^{-1}\dim{\mathcal{E}}_{d,\alpha}\leqslant c_{2}(d).

For
\alpha\in(1/2,1)
, some heuristic arguments have been given in
[
13
]
towards the following:

Conjecture 1.2
.

For any
\alpha\in(1/2,1)
, the set
{\mathcal{E}}_{d,\alpha}
is of Hausdorff dimension

\dim{\mathcal{E}}_{d,\alpha}=\min_{j=1,\ldots,d}\frac{d+1+j\vartheta_{j}-\sum_{i=1}^{j}\vartheta_{i}}{1+\vartheta_{j}},

where

\vartheta_{i}=\frac{i}{2(1-\alpha)}-1,\qquad i=1,\ldots,d.

1.2.
New results and methods

In this paper, we confirm the Conjecture
1.2
for
d=2
, and we obtain new upper bounds of
\dim{\mathcal{E}}_{d,\alpha}
when
d\geqslant 3
and
\alpha
is close to
1
. Moreover, we also consider the following one parametric family of exponential sums. Namely, for a real sequence
f(n)
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

x\in\mathsf{T}
and
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
we denote

(1.5)

V_{f}(x;N)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}{\mathbf{\,e}}\left(xf(n)\right).

Chen and Shparlinski
[
16
, Corollary 2.2]
shows that for any polynomial
f\in{\mathbb{Z}}[X]
with
\deg f\geqslant 2
we have for almost all
x\in\mathsf{T}
,

\left|V_{f}(x;N)\right|\leqslant N^{1/2+o(1)}\qquad\text{as}\ N\rightarrow\infty.

Similarly to the definition of
{\mathcal{E}}_{d,\alpha}
, for
\alpha\in(1/2,1)
we define the set

{\mathcal{F}}_{f,\alpha}=\{x\in\mathsf{T}:\penalty\ |V_{f}(x;N)|\geqslant N^{\alpha}\text{ for infinitely many $N\in{\mathbb{N}}$ }\}.

Perhaps the most interesting sums of this type are sums with monomials
xn^{d}
, in which
case we denote this special quantity by
\mathscr{F}_{d,\alpha}
, that is,

\mathscr{F}_{d,\alpha}=\left\{x\in\mathsf{T}:\penalty\ \left|\sum_{n=1}^{N}{\mathbf{\,e}}\left(xn^{d}\right)\right|\geqslant N^{\alpha}\text{ for infinitely many $N\in{\mathbb{N}}$ }\right\}.

Some lower bounds of
\dim\mathscr{F}_{d,\alpha}
have been obtained in
[
14
, Theorem 1.7]
.
In particular,
[
14
, Theorem 1.7]
implies that for
\alpha\in(1/2,1)

(1.6)

\dim\mathscr{F}_{2,\alpha}\geqslant 2(1-\alpha),

and for
d\geqslant 3
and
\alpha\in[d/(d+2),1)
,

(1.7)

\dim\mathscr{F}_{d,\alpha}\geqslant\left(1+\frac{1}{d}\right)(1-\alpha).

Some heuristic arguments have been given in
[
13
, Section 8]
, suggesting that in a certain range of
\alpha
we may have

\dim\mathscr{F}_{d,\alpha}=4(1-\alpha)/d,

which is consistent with (
1.6
) and (
1.7
) for
d=2
and
d=3
.

Moreover, for
\alpha\in(0,1/2)
the set
\mathscr{F}_{d,\alpha}
is of positive Lebesgue measure (see
[
13
]
for more details), and hence,

\dim\mathscr{F}_{d,\alpha}=1.

It is very likely that for
f\in{\mathbb{Z}}[X]
the bounds (
1.6
) and (
1.7
) can be extended to
the sets
{\mathcal{F}}_{f,\alpha}
.

To obtain these results we develop two different approaches:

•

For
\alpha
close to
1
, we employ the classification of Baker
[
4
,
5
]
in the form given in
[
6
]
.

•

For smaller values of
\alpha
(which means that
\alpha
is close to
1/2
), and also for sums
V_{f}(x;N)
with non-polynomial functions when
the above classification is not available, we link Hausdorff dimension of the sets
{\mathcal{E}}_{d,\alpha}
and

{\mathcal{F}}_{f,\alpha}
to various mean value theorems.

The above arguments are complemented by the use of the Frostman Lemma
(see
[
23
, Corollary 4.12]
) and the Gál–Koksma Theorem
[
26
, Theorem 4]
.

U=O(V)
,

,

2.
Results for sets of very large sums

2.1.
Multiparametric families of Gauss sums and Weyl sums

Here we confirm the Conjecture
1.2
for
d=2
, that is, for the Gauss sums

G(\mathbf{x};N)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}{\mathbf{\,e}}\left(x_{1}n+x_{2}n^{2}\right),

14

{\mathcal{E}}_{2,\alpha}

Theorem 2.1
.

For any
\alpha\in(1/2,1)
we have

\dim{\mathcal{E}}_{2,\alpha}=\begin{cases}7/2-3\alpha&\text{if}\ 5/6\geqslant\alpha>1/2,\\
6(1-\alpha)&\text{if}\ 1>\alpha>5/6.\end{cases}

For
d\geqslant 3
, by applying the same idea as in the proof of Theorem
2.1
and combining some other new ideas, we obtain the following upper bound, which improves the previous bound of
[

\alpha
is close to
1
.
For

we always write

(2.1)

D=\min\{2^{d-1},2d(d-1)\}.

Theorem 2.2
.

For
d\geqslant 3
and any
\alpha\in(1-1/D,1)
, where

is given by (

\dim{\mathcal{E}}_{d,\alpha}\leqslant\min_{h=1,\ldots,d}\frac{(d^{2}+1)(1-\alpha)}{h}+\frac{h-1}{2}.

2.2.
One parametric families of Weyl sums with real polynomials

We now obtain upper bounds on
\dim{\mathcal{F}}_{f,\alpha}
, which in the case of monomial sums and large values of
\alpha
matches the lower bounds (
1.6
) and (
1.7
). Depending on using different methods,
we divide the results on
\dim{\mathcal{F}}_{f,\alpha}
into two subsections.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
f\in{\mathbb{R}}[X]
be a polynomial of degree
d
.
For any
\alpha\in(1-1/D,1)
, where
D
is given by (

), we have

\dim{\mathcal{F}}_{f,\alpha}\leqslant\begin{cases}2(1-\alpha)&\text{if}\ d=2,\\
\left(1+1/d\right)(1-\alpha)&\text{if}\ d\geqslant 3.\end{cases}

Combining Theorem
2.3
with (

) and (

) and noticing
that for

,

\frac{d}{d+2}\leqslant 1-\frac{1}{D},

we obtain the following exact values in the case of monomial sums.

Corollary 2.4
.

For any
\alpha\in(1-1/D,1)
, where
D
is given by (
2.1
), we have

\dim\mathscr{F}_{d,\alpha}=\begin{cases}2(1-\alpha)&\text{if}\ d=2,\\
\left(1+1/d\right)(1-\alpha)&\text{if}\ d\geqslant 3.\end{cases}

As we have mentioned, we believe that in the case
f\in{\mathbb{Z}}[X]
the lower bounds (
1.6
) and (
1.7
)
and thus Corollary
2.4
,
can be extended to

.

We observe that Theorem
2.3
can be applied to estimate
\dim{\mathcal{E}}_{d,\alpha}\cap{\mathcal{L}}
where

{\mathcal{L}}
is a straight line in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
passing through the origin. Precisely, let
\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{d})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
\mathbf{v}\neq{\mathbf{0}}
, and

{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathbf{v}}=\{\lambda\mathbf{v}:\penalty\ \lambda\in\mathsf{T}\}.

For
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathbf{v}}
for some

we have

S_{d}(\mathbf{x};N)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}{\mathbf{\,e}}\left(\lambda f(n)\right),

where

.
It follows that

\dim\left({\mathcal{E}}_{d,\alpha}\cap{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathbf{v}}\right)\leqslant\dim{\mathcal{F}}_{f,\alpha},

and by Theorem
2.3
we derive the following explicit bound.

Corollary 2.5

Let
\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{k},0,\ldots,0)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with

v_{j}=0
when

. Let

D_{k}=\min\{2^{k-1},2k(k-1)\}.

Then for any

\dim{\mathcal{E}}_{d,\alpha}\cap{\mathcal{L}}_{v}\leqslant\begin{cases}2(1-\alpha)&\text{if}\ k=2,\\

We remark that Corollary
2.4
implies that the bounds of Corollary

is sharp in general when
\alpha
is close to

. This follows by choosing
\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{d})
such that

and

when

.
Moreover, motivated from the research on Diophantine approximation on manifolds

), we pose the following general question.

Question 2.6
.

Given an “interesting” surface
\Gamma\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, for example, an algebraic hypersurface or

.

2.3.
Comparison

We observe that Theorem
2.2
improves the upper bound

(2.2)

\dim{\mathcal{E}}_{d,\alpha}\leqslant\min_{k=0,\ldots,d-1}\frac{(2d^{2}+4d)(1-\alpha)+k(k+1)}{4-2\alpha+2k}

of
[
15
, Theorem 1.1]
for the range
\alpha\in(1-1/D,1)
. Indeed, consider the functions

F(h,\beta)=\frac{(d^{2}+1)\beta}{h}+\frac{h-1}{2},\qquad G(k,\beta)=\frac{(2d^{2}+4d)\beta+k(k+1)}{2+2\beta+2k}

and note that it is enough to verify that for
\beta\in(0,1/D)
and
h=1,\ldots,d
we have

(2.3)

F(h,\beta)<G(h-1,\beta).

Clearly, the inequality (
2.3
) is equivalent to

\frac{(d^{2}+1)\beta}{h}+\frac{h-1}{2}<\frac{(2d^{2}+4d)\beta+h(h-1)}{2\beta+2h}

or

\left(2(d^{2}+1)\beta+h(h-1)\right)\left(\beta+h\right)<\left((2d^{2}+4d)\beta+h(h-1)\right)h.

Simplifying we obtain an equivalent inequality

2(d^{2}+1)\beta^{2}<\beta\left(4dh-h(h+1)\right)

and finally

2(d^{2}+1)\beta<4dh-h(h+1).

Since
\beta<1/D
and the right hand side of above inequality is increasing with
h\in[1,d]

it is only enough to check that

2(d^{2}+1)/D<4d-2,

which can be numerically verified for
3\leqslant d\leqslant 7
and established via elementary calculus for
d\geqslant 8
.
This shows that (
2.3
) holds and thus Theorem
2.2
is stronger than (
2.2
)
for any
d\geqslant 3
and all admissible values of
\alpha
.

Clearly, when
\alpha
is close to
1
then the choice of
h=1
in Theorem
2.2
is optimal.
However sometimes other choices of
h
give better result. For example, if

\alpha\in(1-1/D,1-1/(d^{2}+1)
than then choice of
h=2
is better than
h=1
.
The above range is non-empty provided that
d^{2}+1\geqslant D
, and this happens when
3\leqslant d\leqslant 6
only.
Moreover, for larger
d
(say
d\geqslant 7
) and
\alpha\in(1-1/D,1)
the value
\alpha
is quite near the value
1
, thus we may take
h=1
only in Theorem
2.2
when
d\geqslant 7
.
Although for our applications here the values
h\geqslant 3
are never used, we present the argument in full generality
as we believe it can be used to study Weyl sums with other polynomials.

2.4.
Ideas behind the proofs

We concentrate on the ideas in the proof of Theorem
2.1
.
Before doing this, we remark that a similar argument to the proof of the upper bound on
\dim{\mathcal{E}}_{2,\alpha}
also implies an upper bound on
\dim{\mathcal{E}}_{d,\alpha}
and
\dim{\mathcal{F}}_{f,\alpha}
in Theorems
2.2
and
2.3
. However, the idea for obtaining the lower bound on
\dim{\mathcal{E}}_{2,\alpha}
does not work for
d\geqslant 3
. The main reason is that we do not have a version of Lemma
7.1
when
d\geqslant 3
and in fact
for a prime
p\equiv 2\pmod{3}
there are many vectors
(a,b,c)\in[p]^{3}
such that

\sum_{n=1}^{p}{\mathbf{\,e}}_{p}(an+bn^{2}+cn^{3})=0,

see
[
14
, Remark 2.8]
for more details.

Upper bound:

Our argument is based on a combination of the Frostman Lemma
(see
[
23
, Corollary 4.12]
) and the Gál–Koksma Theorem
[
26
, Theorem 4]
,
which are presented in Section
4
.

For any
t<\dim{\mathcal{E}}_{2,\alpha}
, by the
Frostman Lemma
(see
[
23
, Corollary 4.12]

or Lemma
4.1
below), there exists a Radon measure
\mu
on
{\mathcal{E}}_{2,\alpha}
with

\mu({\mathcal{E}}_{2,\alpha})>0\quad\text{ and }\quad\mu(B(\mathbf{x},r))\ll r^{t}

for all
\mathbf{x}
and
r>0
. Applying the description of Baker
[
4
, Theorem 3]
of the structure of large Gauss sums, we obtain the following type
L^{\rho}
bound: for any
M,N\in{\mathbb{N}}
we have

\int_{\mathsf{T}_{2}}\left|\sum_{n=M+1}^{M+N}{\mathbf{\,e}}(x_{1}n+x_{2}n^{2})\right|^{\rho}d\mu(\mathbf{x})\leqslant N^{s_{1}+o(1)}(N+M)^{s_{2}},

where the exponents
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
depend on
\rho
and
t
. By a result
of Gál and Koksma
[
26
, Theorem 4]
, we obtain that for almost all
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2})
with respect to
\mu

\sum_{n=1}^{N}{\mathbf{\,e}}(x_{1}n+x_{2}n^{2})=o\left(N^{(s_{1}+s_{2})/\rho}\right),\quad N\rightarrow\infty.

Since
\mu({\mathcal{E}}_{2,\alpha})>0
, there is a set of
(x_{1},x_{2})
of positive
\mu
-measure such that

\left|\sum_{n=1}^{N}{\mathbf{\,e}}(x_{1}n+x_{2}n^{2})\right|\geqslant N^{\alpha}

for infinitely many
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
. It follows that

\alpha\leqslant(s_{1}+s_{2})/\rho.

By taking the concrete parameters we obtain

t<\min\{1/2+3(1-\alpha),6(1-\alpha)\}.

Note that this inequality holds for any
t<\dim{\mathcal{E}}_{2,\alpha}
. Thus we obtain

\dim{\mathcal{E}}_{2,\alpha}\leqslant\min\{1/2+3(1-\alpha),6(1-\alpha)\},

which yields the desired upper bound.

Proofs of Theorems
2.2
and
2.3
follow a similar idea, albeit in a more technically involved way.

Lower bound:

we make the heuristic argument of
[
13
, Section 8]
rigorous for the case
d=2
. In brief, Gauss sums are large at rational points and their small neighbourhood, and we know the size of them from Diophantine analysis. Indeed, first note that the Gauss sums are large at rational points, for instance for any
a,b,p
where
p
is a prime number,
(p,b)=1
we have

\left|\sum_{n=1}^{p}{\mathbf{\,e}}_{p}(an+bn^{2})\right|=\sqrt{p},

where
{\mathbf{\,e}}_{p}(z)=\exp(2\pi iz/p)
,
and hence, by periodicity, for suitably large
N
we have

\left|\sum_{n=1}^{N}{\mathbf{\,e}}_{p}(an+bn^{2})\right|\approx\frac{N}{\sqrt{p}}\approx N^{\alpha}.

Here
z\approx Z
means that
Z/C\leqslant z\leqslant CZ
for some absolute positive constant
C
. By the continuity of the Gauss sums, we have

\left|\sum_{n=1}^{N}{\mathbf{\,e}}(xn+yn^{2})\right|\approx N^{\alpha},

provided that

(2.4)

\left|x-\frac{a}{p}\right|<p^{-\frac{1}{2(1-\alpha)}},\quad\left|y-\frac{b}{p}\right|<p^{-\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}.

It follows that if
(x,y)
satisfies (
2.4
) for infinitely many
a,b,p
then
(x,y)\in{\mathcal{E}}_{2,\alpha}
, and we denote the collection of these
(x,y)
by
W_{\alpha}
. Thus
W_{\alpha}\subseteq{\mathcal{E}}_{2,\alpha}
.
By Rynne
[
36
, Theorem 1]
, for
\alpha\in(1/2,1)
we have

\dim W_{\alpha}=\min\left\{1/2+3(1-\alpha),6(1-\alpha)\right\},

and thus yields the desired lower bound.

3.
Results for sets of exponential sums of arbitrary size

3.1.
One parametric families of Weyl sums with integer polynomials

Theorem
2.3
says nothing for
\dim\mathscr{F}_{f,\alpha}
when
\alpha\in(1/2,1-1/D)
.
Our next result fills that gap, and in particular for any polynomial
f\in{\mathbb{Z}}[X]
with degree
d\geqslant 2
and any
\alpha\in(1/2,1)
we have

(3.1)

\dim{\mathcal{F}}_{f,\alpha}<1.

In fact, Theorem
3.5
below implies that for any real polynomial
f\in{\mathbb{R}}[X]
with degree
d\geqslant 2
the nontrivial bound (
3.1
) still holds.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
f\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x]
be a polynomial with degree
d\geqslant 2
. For any real
\alpha\in(1/2,1)
, we have

\dim{\mathcal{F}}_{f,\alpha}\leqslant\min\{U_{1}(d,\alpha),U_{2}(d,\alpha)\},

where

\displaystyle U_{1}(d,\alpha)=\min_{r=1,\ldots,d}\frac{d+1-\alpha+2^{r}(1-\alpha)-r}{d+1-\alpha},

\displaystyle U_{2}(d,\alpha)=\min_{r=1,\ldots,d}\frac{d+1-\alpha+r(r+1)(1-\alpha)-r}{d+1-\alpha}.

Now we extract some easier upper bounds for
\dim{\mathcal{F}}_{f,\alpha}
. Taking
r=2
and
r=d
in the definition of
U_{1}(d,\alpha)
, we obtain

U_{1}(d,\alpha)\leqslant\min\left\{\frac{d+3-5\alpha}{d+1-\alpha},\frac{(2^{d}+1)(1-\alpha)}{d+1-\alpha}\right\}.

Furthermore, taking
r=d
in the definition of
U_{2}(d,\alpha)
we obtain

U_{2}(d,\alpha)\leqslant\frac{(d^{2}+d+1)(1-\alpha)}{d+1-\alpha}.

We formulate the following corollary.

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
f\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x]
be a polynomial with degree
d\geqslant 2
. For any real
\alpha\in(1/2,1)
, we have

\dim{\mathcal{F}}_{f,\alpha}\leqslant\min\left\{\frac{d+3-5\alpha}{d+1-\alpha},\frac{(2^{d}+1)(1-\alpha)}{d+1-\alpha},\frac{(d^{2}+d+1)(1-\alpha)}{d+1-\alpha}\right\}.

Corollary
3.2
implies that for any
f\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x]
be a polynomial with degree
d\geqslant 2
and any
\alpha\in(1/2,1)
we have

\dim{\mathcal{F}}_{f,\alpha}<1.

Furthermore, for monomials we have yet abother bound.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
d\geqslant 2
. For any real
\alpha\in(1/2,1)
, we have

\dim\mathscr{F}_{d,\alpha}\leqslant\frac{(1+s_{0})(1-\alpha)}{d+1-\alpha},

where

s_{0}=d(d-1)+\min_{r=1,\ldots,d}\frac{2d+(r-1)(r-2)}{r}.

Furthermore, for “polynomial-like” sequences, such in the special case of
Piatetski-Shapiro
sequences
f(n)=\left\lfloor n^{\tau}\right\rfloor

we have the following result.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
f(n)=\left\lfloor n^{\tau}\right\rfloor
for some
\tau\geqslant 1
.
For any
\alpha\in(1/2,1)
, we have

\dim{\mathcal{F}}_{f,\alpha}\leqslant\begin{cases}1-\frac{4\alpha+\tau-4}{\tau+1-\alpha}&\text{if}\ \tau<2,\\
1-\frac{4\alpha-2}{\tau+1-\alpha}&\text{if}\ \tau\geqslant 2.\end{cases}

Note that for any
\alpha\in(1/2,1)
Theorem
3.4
provides nontrivial upper bound, that is,
\dim{\mathcal{F}}_{f,\alpha}<1
, in a wide range of parameters
\alpha
and
\tau
, for instance, when

2>\tau>4-4\alpha
.

3.2.
One parametric families of exponential sums with arbitrary sequences

Let
f(n)
be a real sequence. We extend the definition of exponential sums

in (

the sets

to exponential sums with an arbitrary real sequence

First we consider sequences with a given rate of their growth on average. Namely, we assume

(3.2)

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}|f(n)|\ll N^{\tau}.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
f(n)
be a real sequence such that

For any real

\dim{\mathcal{F}}_{f,\alpha}\leqslant 1-\frac{2\alpha-1}{\tau+1-\alpha}.

Clearly for any

1-\frac{2\alpha-1}{\tau+1-\alpha}<1.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let

be a strictly convex integer sequence that satisfies (

) for some
\tau>0
.
Then we have

\dim{\mathcal{F}}_{f,\alpha}\leqslant\min\{U_{1}(\tau,\alpha),U_{2}(\tau,\alpha)\},

\displaystyle U_{1}(\tau,\alpha)=\frac{\tau+45/13-5\alpha}{\tau-\alpha},

\displaystyle U_{2}(\tau,\alpha)=\inf_{k\geqslant 3,\,k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\frac{\tau-\alpha+2k-1+2^{-k+1}-2k\alpha}{\tau+1-\alpha}.

Next we consider sequences with a restriction on the growth of individual terms rather than
on average as in (

Theorem 3.7
.

Let

Here we mention some related work on

First Salem

For technical reasons (completion technique), we in fact use an auxiliary exponential sums

More precisely,

4.
Frostman Lemma and Gál–Koksma Theorem

4.1.
Frostman Lemma

For a real
s\geqslant 0
and a set
{\mathcal{F}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
denote

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}^{s}_{\delta}({\mathcal{F}})=\inf\biggl\{\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\left(\operatorname{diam}{\mathcal{U}}_{i}\right)^{s}:\penalty\

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}{\mathcal{U}}_{i},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{U}}_{i}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\text{ and }\operatorname{diam}({\mathcal{U}}_{i})\leqslant\delta,i\in{\mathbb{N}}\biggr\},

and the
s
-dimensional Hausdorff measure of the set
{\mathcal{F}}
is defined as

{\mathcal{H}}^{s}({\mathcal{F}})=\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}{\mathcal{H}}^{s}_{\delta}({\mathcal{F}}).

Moreover, alternatively the Hausdorff dimension of
{\mathcal{F}}
can also be defined as

\dim{\mathcal{F}}=\inf\{s\geqslant 0:\penalty\ {\mathcal{H}}^{s}({\mathcal{F}})=0\}=\sup\{s\geqslant 0:\penalty\ {\mathcal{H}}^{s}({\mathcal{F}})=\infty\}.

We also need the following result, which is known as the
Frostman Lemma
, see, for
instance,
[
23
, Corollary 4.12]
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
{\mathcal{F}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be a Borel set with
0<{\mathcal{H}}^{s}({\mathcal{F}})\leqslant\infty
. Then there exists a compact set
{\mathcal{E}}\subseteq{\mathcal{F}}
such that
0<{\mathcal{H}}^{s}({\mathcal{E}})<\infty
and

{\mathcal{H}}^{s}({\mathcal{E}}\cap B(\mathbf{x},r))\ll r^{s}

for all
\mathbf{x}
and
r>0
.

We remark that for our application of Lemma
4.1
we take
\mu
to be the restriction of the
s
-Hausdorff measure
{\mathcal{H}}^{s}
on
{\mathcal{E}}
, that is for any
{\mathcal{A}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

\mu({\mathcal{A}})={\mathcal{H}}^{s}({\mathcal{E}}\cap{\mathcal{A}}).

It follows that
\mu
is a Radon measure such that
\mu({\mathcal{E}})>0
and

\mu(B(\mathbf{x},r))\ll r^{s}

for all
\mathbf{x}
and
r>0
.

4.2.
Gál–Koksma Theorem

We first recall the following result of Gál and Koksma
[
26
, Theorem 4]
, which shows that mean value bounds imply almost all bounds with respect to the same measure. Note that
[
26
]
treats the case of the Lebesgue measure on a set
{\mathcal{S}}
rather than an arbitrary Radon measure
\mu
, however the proof goes through without change.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\mu
be a Radon measure on
\mathsf{T}_{d}
and
let
f_{1}(\mathbf{x}),f_{2}(\mathbf{x}),\ldots
be a sequence of Borel measurable function on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Suppose that we have the bound, for some
\rho>0
and for all
M\geqslant 0,N\geqslant 1
,

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\left|\sum_{n=M+1}^{M+N}f_{n}(\mathbf{x})\right|^{\rho}d\mu(\mathbf{x})\leqslant C\Psi(N)\Phi(M,N),\quad\Phi(M,N)\geqslant 1,

where
C
is an absolutely constant,
\Psi(N)
and
\Phi(M,N)
are some positive functions, and
\Psi(N)/N^{1+\gamma}
is nondecreasing for some positive
\gamma
. Let
\psi(N)>0
be nondecreasing and

(4.1)

\psi(2^{n})\geqslant\Phi(0,2^{n})+\sum_{\lambda=1}^{n}2^{(\lambda-n)(1+\beta)}\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n-\lambda}-1}\Phi(2^{n}+k2^{\lambda},2^{\lambda-1}),

where
0<\beta<\gamma
is a constant. Let
\chi(N)
be a positive nondecreasing function with

(4.2)

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(N\chi(N))^{-1}<\infty.

Then for almost all
\mathbf{x}
with respect to
\mu
, we have

\sum_{n=1}^{N}f_{n}(\mathbf{x})=o(\Psi(N)\psi(N)\chi(N))^{1/p},\quad N\rightarrow\infty.

For convenience of our application, we formulate the following particular case of Lemma
4.2
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\mu
be a Radon measure on
\mathsf{T}_{d}
and let
f_{1}(\mathbf{x}),f_{2}(\mathbf{x}),\ldots
be a sequence of Borel measurable function on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Suppose that we have the bound, for some
\rho>0
and for all
M\geqslant 0,N\geqslant 1
,

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\left|\sum_{n=M+1}^{M+N}f_{n}(\mathbf{x})\right|^{\rho}d\mu(\mathbf{x})\leqslant N^{s_{1}+o(1)}(M+N)^{s_{2}}

for some constants
s_{1}>1
and
s_{2}>0
. Then for almost all
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with respect to
\mu
we have

\left|\sum_{n=1}^{N}f_{n}(\mathbf{x})\right|\leqslant N^{(s_{1}+s_{2})/\rho+o(1)},\qquad N\rightarrow\infty.

Proof.

We show that
\psi(N)=CN^{s_{2}}
satisfies (
4.1
) when
C
is some large constant. To see this,
we note that for
1\leqslant\lambda\leqslant n
, we have

\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n-\lambda}-1}(2^{n}+k2^{\lambda}+2^{\lambda-1})^{s_{2}}\ll 2^{n-\lambda}2^{ns_{2}},

and for some small
\beta>0
,

\sum_{\lambda=1}^{n}2^{(\lambda-n)(1+\beta)}2^{n-\lambda}2^{ns_{2}}=\sum_{\lambda=1}^{n}2^{-(n-\lambda)\beta}2^{ns_{2}}\ll 2^{ns_{2}}.

Moreover, for any
\varepsilon>0
the function
\chi(N)=N^{\varepsilon}
satisfies (
4.2
). By Lemma
4.2
we obtain that for almost all
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with respect to
\mu
we have

\left|\sum_{n=1}^{N}f_{n}(\mathbf{x})\right|\leqslant N^{(s_{1}+s_{2}+\varepsilon)/\rho+o(1)},\quad N\rightarrow\infty.

By the arbitrary choice of
\varepsilon>0
we obtain the desired bound.

5.1.

Lemma 5.2
.

We fix some

\displaystyle\left|x_{1}-\frac{b_{1}}{q}\right|\leqslant(M+N)q^{-1}A^{-2}N^{o(1)},

\displaystyle\left|x_{2}-\frac{b_{2}}{q}\right|\leqslant q^{-1}A^{-2}N^{o(1)}.

Proof.

Elementary arithmetic shows that

By Lemma

such that

\begin{split}&\left|x_{1}+2Mx_{2}-\frac{a_{1}}{q}\right|\leqslant q^{-1}A^{-2}N^{1+o(1)},\\
&\left|x_{2}-\frac{a_{2}}{q}\right|\leqslant q^{-1}A^{-2}N^{o(1)}.\end{split}

Clearly
A^{-2}N^{o(1)}<1
, we conclude that
0\leqslant a_{2}\leqslant q
, and we take
b_{2}=a_{2}
.

We now search for an integer
b_{1}
with the desired property. From (
5.1
) we obtain

\left|x_{1}-\frac{a_{1}-2Ma_{2}}{q}\right|\leqslant(M+N)q^{-1}A^{-2}N^{o(1)}.

Suppose that
(M+N)A^{-2}N^{o(1)}\leqslant 1

0\leqslant a_{1}-2Ma_{2}\leqslant q,

and we take

Suppose to the contrary that

then trivially there exists

with
0\leqslant b_{1}\leqslant q
and such that

\left|x_{1}-\frac{b_{1}}{q}\right|\leqslant q^{-1}\leqslant(M+N)q^{-1}A^{-2}N^{o(1)},

which finishes the proof.

5.2.
Frequency of large Gauss sums

Let
\mu
be a Radon measure on
\mathsf{T}_{2}
such that

(5.3)

\mu(B(\mathbf{x},r))\ll r^{t}

holds for some
t>0
and for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathsf{T}_{2}
and
r>0
. Let
\mathfrak{R}
be a rectangle with side length
a<b
, then we have

\mu(\mathfrak{R})\ll\min\{ba^{t-1},b^{t}\}.

Indeed, we can either include
\mathfrak{R}
in a ball of radius
O(b)
or cover it by
O(b/a)
balls of radius
a
.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
\mu
be a Radon measure satisfying (
5.3
) and
A
be a real number with
1\leqslant A\leqslant N
. For fixed
M,N\in{\mathbb{N}}
denote

{\mathcal{E}}_{B}=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathsf{T}_{2}:\penalty\ |G(\mathbf{x};M,N)|\geqslant B\}.

Then we have

\mu({\mathcal{E}}_{B})\leqslant N^{6-2t+o(1)}B^{-6}\min\{M+N,(M+N)^{t}\}.

Proof.

Denote

Q=(NB^{-1})^{2}N^{o(1)}.

From Lemma
5.1
we conclude that

\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathsf{T}_{2}:\penalty\ |G(\mathbf{x};M,N)|\geqslant B\}\subseteq\bigcup_{q\leqslant Q}\bigcup_{(a_{1},a_{2})\in[q]^{2}}\mathfrak{R}_{q,b_{1},b_{2}},

where
\mathfrak{R}_{q,b_{1},b_{2}}
is a rectangle with side lengths

(M+N)B^{-2}q^{-1}N^{o(1)}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad B^{-2}q^{-1}N^{o(1)}.

For
Z\geqslant 1
, we write
z\sim Z
to denote that
Z/2<z\leqslant Z
. Denote

\delta_{z}=B^{-2}z^{-1}.

Taking a dyadic partition of the interval
[1,Q]
, we see that there is a number
1\leqslant Z\leqslant Q
such that

\displaystyle\mu({\mathcal{E}}_{B})

\displaystyle\leqslant N^{o(1)}\sum_{q\sim Z}\sum_{(a_{1},a_{2})\in[q]^{2}}\mu(\mathfrak{R}_{q,a_{1},a_{2}})

\displaystyle\leqslant N^{o(1)}Z^{3}\min\{(M+N)\delta_{Z}^{t},(M+N)^{t}\delta_{Z}^{t}\}

\displaystyle\leqslant N^{o(1)}\min\{I,J\},

where

I=Z^{3}(M+N)\delta_{Z}^{t}\leqslant Z^{3-t}B^{-2t}(M+N)\leqslant N^{6-2t}B^{-6}(M+N),

and

J=Z^{3}(M+N)^{t}\delta_{Z}^{t}\leqslant N^{6-2t}B^{-6}(M+N)^{t},

which finishes the proof.

5.3.
Bounds of Weyl sums

Corresponding to the bounds of Gauss sums, we have the following estimation for Weyl sums which is needed for the proof of Theorem
2.2
.

An integer number

is called

•

r
-th power free
if any prime number
p\mid n
satisfies
p^{r}\nmid n
;

•

r
-th power full
if any prime number
p\mid n
satisfies
p^{r}\mid n
.

We note that
1
is both cube free and cube full.

For any integer

it is convenient to denote

\mathscr{Q}_{i}=\{n\in{\mathbb{N}}:\penalty\ \text{$n$ is $i$-th power full}\}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathscr{Q}_{i}(x)=\mathscr{Q}_{i}\cap[1,x].

The classical result of Erdős and Szekeres
[
21
]
gives an asymptotic
formula for the cardinality of
\mathscr{Q}_{i}(x)
which we present here in a very relaxed form
as the upper bound

(5.4)

\#\mathscr{Q}_{i}(x)\ll x^{1/i}.

The following Lemma

comes from
[

, some
\varepsilon>0

Lemma 5.6

We fix

\varepsilon>0

The coefficient of

6.1.
Mean values of Gauss sums

We need the following mean value estimate for Gauss sums with respect to an arbitrary Radon measure,
which is interesting in its own right.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
\mu
be a Radon measure on
\mathsf{T}_{2}
such that

\mu(B(\mathbf{x},r))\ll r^{t}

holds for some
t>0
and for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathsf{T}_{2}
and
r>0
. Then for all
M,N
we have

\int_{\mathsf{T}_{2}}\left|G(\mathbf{x};M,N)\right|^{6}d\mu(\mathbf{x})\leqslant N^{6-2t+o(1)}(M+N)^{\min\{1,t\}}.

Proof.

Let us fix some
\varepsilon>0
. Denote

K=N^{1/2+\varepsilon}.

By a dyadic partition argument, there exits
B\in[K,N]
such that

(6.1)

\int_{\mathsf{T}_{2}}\left|G(\mathbf{x};M,N)\right|^{6}d\mu(\mathbf{x})\leqslant K^{6}\mu(\mathsf{T}_{2})+B^{6}\mu({\mathcal{E}}_{B})N^{o(1)}.

By Lemma
5.4
we have

\mu({\mathcal{E}}_{B})\leqslant N^{6-2t+o(1)}B^{-6}(M+N)^{\min\{1,t\}}.

which after substitution in (
6.1
) implies

\int_{\mathsf{T}_{2}}\left|G(\mathbf{x};M,N)\right|^{6}d\mu(\mathbf{x})\leqslant N^{3+6\varepsilon}+N^{6-2t+o(1)}(M+N)^{\min\{1,t\}}.

Since
t\leqslant 2
and
\varepsilon
is arbitrary, the result now follows.

\sqcap
\sqcup

6.2.
Concluding the proof

We now turn to the proof of the upper bound of Theorem
2.1
. Let
t\in(0,\dim{\mathcal{E}}_{2,\alpha})
. Then
{\mathcal{E}}_{2,\alpha}
has infinite
{\mathcal{H}}^{t}
-measure. By Lemma
4.1
, there exists a Radon measure
\mu
on
\mathsf{T}_{2}
with

\mu({\mathcal{E}}_{2,\alpha})>0\quad\text{ and }\quad\mu(B(\mathbf{x},r))\ll r^{t}

for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathsf{T}_{2}
and
r>0
. Taking the function

f_{n}(x_{1},x_{2})={\mathbf{\,e}}(x_{1}n+x_{2}n^{2})

and applying Lemmas
4.3
and
6.1
, we immediately derive that for
almost all
(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathsf{T}_{2}
with respect to
\mu
,

(6.2)

|G(x_{1},x_{2};N)|\leqslant N^{1-t/3+\min\{1/6,t/6\}+o(1)}.

Since
\mu({\mathcal{E}}_{2,\alpha})>0
, there is a set of
(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathsf{T}_{2}
of positive
\mu
-measure
such that

|G(x_{1},x_{2};N)|\geqslant N^{\alpha}

for infinitely many
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Combining with (
6.2
) we derive

\alpha\leqslant 1-t/3+\min\{1/6,t/6\},

which implies

t\leqslant\min\{1/2+3(1-\alpha),6(1-\alpha)\}.

Since this holds for any

, we conclude that

\dim{\mathcal{E}}_{2,\alpha}\leqslant\min\{1/2+3(1-\alpha),6(1-\alpha)\},

which yields the desired upper bound.

Proof of the lower bound of Theorem

7.1.
Large values of Gauss sums

The main purpose of this subsection is to show Lemma
7.4
. We start from recalling the following property of Gaussian sums,
see
[
31
, Equation (1.55)]
.

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
p\geqslant 3
and
a,b\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
with
b\neq 0
, then

\left|\sum_{n=0}^{p-1}{\mathbf{\,e}}_{p}\left(an+bn^{2}\right)\right|=\sqrt{p}.

Using the Gauss bound together with the standard
completion technique, see
[
31
, Sections 11.11 and 12.2]
we also immediately obtain:

Lemma 7.2
.

For any prime
p
and any
a\in{\mathbb{F}}_{p}\setminus\{0\}
we have

\max_{1\leqslant M,N\leqslant p}\left|\sum_{M+1\leqslant n\leqslant M+N}{\mathbf{\,e}}_{p}\left(an^{2}\right)\right|\ll\sqrt{p}\log p.

The continuity of Gauss sums yields the following result.

Lemma 7.3
.

For
N\gg p\log p
we have

\displaystyle|G(x_{1},x_{2};N)

\displaystyle-G(a/p,b/p;N)|

\displaystyle\ll Np^{-1/2}(|x_{1}-a/p|N+|x_{2}-b/p|N^{2}).

Proof.

We use the following version of summation by parts.
Let
a_{n}
be a sequence and for each
t\geqslant 1
denote

A(t)=\sum_{1\leqslant n\leqslant t}a_{n}.

Let
\psi:[1,N]\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}
be a continuously differentiable function. Then

\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_{n}\psi(n)=A(N)\psi(N)-\int_{1}^{N}A(t)\psi^{\prime}(t)dt.

Let
\delta_{1}=x-a/p
,
\delta_{2}=x_{2}-b/p
. Then define

(7.1)

\begin{split}\Delta&=G(x_{1},x_{2};N)-G(a/p,b/p;N)\\
&=\sum_{n=1}^{N}{\mathbf{\,e}}(na/p+n^{2}b/p)\left({\mathbf{\,e}}(\delta_{1}n+\delta_{2}n^{2})-1\right).\end{split}

For an integer
M
with
1\leqslant M\leqslant N
, we split the sum
G(a/p,b/p;M)
into
O(N/p)
complete sums and at most one
incomplete sum, applying Lemmas
7.1
and
7.2
, we derive

\max_{1\leqslant M\leqslant N}|G(a/p,b/p;M)|\ll Np^{-1/2}+p^{1/2}\log p.

Hence, applying to the sum in (
7.1
) summation by parts with
a_{n}={\mathbf{\,e}}(na/p+n^{2}b/p)
and
\psi(t)={\mathbf{\,e}}(\delta_{1}t+\delta_{2}t^{2})-1
, we derive
that

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle\ll\max_{1\leqslant M\leqslant N}|G(a/p,b/p;M)|\left(|\delta_{1}|N+|\delta_{2}|N^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\ll\left(Np^{-1/2}+p^{1/2}\log p\right)\left(|\delta_{1}|N+|\delta_{2}|N^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\ll Np^{-1/2}\left(|\delta_{1}|N+|\delta_{2}|N^{2}\right),

which finishes the proof.

\sqcap
\sqcup

From Lemma
7.1
and Lemma
7.3
we obtain the following, which is the main purpose of this subsection.

Lemma 7.4
.

We fix
\alpha\in(1/2,1)
. Let
p\geqslant 3
and
a,b\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
with
b\neq 0
. Let
N
be the smallest number such that
p|N
and

N\geqslant p^{\frac{1}{2(1-\alpha)}}.

Then there exists a sufficiently small number
\eta>0
such that for any
(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathsf{T}_{2}
with

\left|x_{1}-\frac{a}{p}\right|<\eta p^{-\frac{1}{2(1-\alpha)}}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\left|x_{2}-\frac{b}{p}\right|<\eta p^{-\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}

we have

G(x_{1},x_{2};N)\gg N^{\alpha}.

Proof.

Recalling that
z\approx Z
means
Z/C\leqslant z\leqslant CZ
for some absolute positive constant
C
.
First note that the choice of
N
implies

N\approx p^{\frac{1}{2(1-\alpha)}}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad Np^{-1/2}\approx N^{\alpha}.

By Lemma
7.3
we have

|G(x_{1},x_{2};N)-G(a/p,b/p;N)|\ll\eta N^{\alpha}.

Lemma
7.1
implies
G(a/p,b/p;N)\approx N^{\alpha}
. Therefore, we obtain the desired bound by choosing
a sufficiently small
\eta
.

\sqcup

7.2.
Simultaneous Diophantine approximations

Let
\bm{\vartheta}=(\vartheta_{1},\ldots,\vartheta_{d})
be a vector of positive real numbers and let
{\mathfrak{Q}}
an arbitrary set of positive
integers. Without losing generality, assuming that
\vartheta_{1}\leqslant\ldots\leqslant\vartheta_{d}
. We denote by
W_{{\mathfrak{Q}},\bm{\vartheta}}
be the collection of points
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in\mathsf{T}_{d}
for which there are infinitely many
q\in{\mathfrak{Q}}
such that

\left\|qx_{i}\right\|<q^{-\vartheta_{i}},\qquad i=1,\ldots,d,

where
\left\|x\right\|=\min\{|x-n|:\penalty\ n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}
. Denote

\nu({\mathfrak{Q}})=\inf\left\{\nu\in{\mathbb{R}}:\penalty\ \sum_{q\in{\mathfrak{Q}}}q^{-\nu}<\infty\right\}.

We need the following result of Rynne
[
36
, Theorem 1]
.

Lemma 7.5
.

Suppose that

\vartheta_{1}+\ldots+\vartheta_{d}\geqslant\nu({\mathfrak{Q}}),

then we have

\dim W_{{\mathfrak{Q}},\bm{\vartheta}}=\min_{1\leqslant j\leqslant d}\frac{d+\nu({\mathfrak{Q}})+j\vartheta_{j}-\sum_{i=1}^{j}\vartheta_{i}}{1+\vartheta_{j}}.

For
d=2
and
\nu({\mathfrak{Q}})=1
we have the following.

\dim W_{{\mathfrak{Q}},\bm{\vartheta}}=\min\left\{\frac{3}{1+\vartheta_{1}},\frac{3+\vartheta_{2}-\vartheta_{1}}{1+\vartheta_{2}}\right\}.

We now turn to the proof of the lower bound of Theorem
2.1
. Indeed this follows by combining Lemma
7.4
and Lemma
7.5
. Let
{\mathfrak{Q}}
be the collection of prime numbers. Clearly we have
\nu({\mathfrak{Q}})=1
. By Lemma
7.4
, for any
\varepsilon>0
we obtain

W_{{\mathfrak{Q}},\vartheta}\subseteq{\mathcal{E}}_{2,\alpha-\varepsilon}

with

\vartheta_{1}=\frac{1}{2(1-\alpha)}-1\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\vartheta_{2}=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}-1.

Since
\alpha\in(1/2,1)
, we have
\vartheta_{1}+\vartheta_{2}\geqslant 1
. Thus by Lemma
7.5
we obtain

\dim W_{{\mathfrak{Q}},\vartheta}=\min\{1/2+3(1-\alpha),6(1-\alpha)\}.

It follows that

\dim{\mathcal{E}}_{2,\alpha-\varepsilon}\geqslant\min\{1/2+3(1-\alpha),6(1-\alpha)\}.

Since
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrary, we obtain the desired bound.

8.1.
Mean values of Weyl sums

We need the following mean value estimate of Weyl sums with respect to a general measure.

Lemma 8.1
.

Let
\mu
be a Radon measure on
\mathsf{T}_{d},d\geqslant 3
such that

\mu(B(\mathbf{x},r))\ll r^{t}

holds for some
t>0
and for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathsf{T}_{d}
and
r>0
. Then for any integer
h
with
1\leqslant h\leqslant d
we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathsf{T}_{d}}|S_{d}(\mathbf{x};M,N)|^{d^{2}+1}

\displaystyle d\mu(\mathbf{x})\leqslant N^{(1-1/D)(d^{2}+1)+o(1)}

\displaystyle+N^{d^{2}+1-dt+o(1)}(M+N)^{(d-h)t+h(h-1)/2},

where
D
is given by (
2.1
).

Proof.

Let us fix some
\varepsilon>0
. Denote

K=N^{1-1/D+\varepsilon}.

Similar to the proof of Lemma
6.1
, taking a dyadic partition of the interval
[K,N]
, there exists a number
B\in[K,N]
such that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathsf{T}_{d}}|S_{d}(\mathbf{x};M,N)|^{d^{2}+1}d\mu(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant K^{d^{2}+1}+N^{o(1)}B^{d^{2}+1}\mu(\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathsf{T}_{d}:B\leqslant S_{d}(\mathbf{x};M,N)|\leqslant 2B\}).

Combining with Lemma
5.7
we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathsf{T}_{d}}|S_{d}(\mathbf{x};M,N)|^{d^{2}+1}d\mu(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant N^{(1-1/D)(d^{2}+1)+\varepsilon(d^{2}+1)}+N^{o(1)}N^{d^{2}+1-dt}(M+N)^{(d-h)t+h(h-1)/2}.

By the arbitrary choice of
\varepsilon>0
we obtain the desired bound.

\sqcap
\sqcup

8.2.
Concluding the proof

We now turn to the proof of Theorem
2.2
. Let
t\in(0,\dim{\mathcal{E}}_{d,\alpha})
. Then we see that
{\mathcal{E}}_{d,\alpha}
has infinite
{\mathcal{H}}^{t}
-measure. By Lemma
4.1
, there exists a Radon measure
\mu
on
\mathsf{T}_{d}
with

\mu({\mathcal{E}}_{2,\alpha})>0\quad\text{ and }\quad\mu(B(\mathbf{x},r))\ll r^{t}

for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathsf{T}_{d}
and
r>0
.

Let
1\leqslant h\leqslant d
be an integer. There are two cases to consider.

Case 1.
Suppose that the ‘total exponent’

d^{2}+1-dt+(d-h)t+h(h-1)/2=d^{2}+1-ht+h(h-1)/2

in the second term in the bound of Lemma
8.1
is at least as large as the exponent of the first term, that is,

d^{2}+1-ht+h(h-1)/2\geqslant(1-1/D)(d^{2}+1).

Since
t<\dim{\mathcal{E}}_{d,\alpha}\leqslant d
, we have
d^{2}+1-dt>1

4.3
we derive that for almost all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathsf{T}_{d}
with respect to
\mu
,

(8.1)

|S_{d}(\mathbf{x};N)|\leqslant N^{\frac{d^{2}+1-ht+h(h-1)/2}{d^{2}+1}+o(1)}.

Since
\mu({\mathcal{E}}_{d,\alpha})>0
, there is a set of
\mathbf{x}\in\mathsf{T}_{d}
of positive
\mu
-measure such that

|S_{d}(\mathbf{x};N)|\geqslant N^{\alpha}

for infinitely many
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Combining with (
8.1
) we derive

(8.2)

\alpha\leqslant\frac{d^{2}+1-ht+h(h-1)/2}{d^{2}+1},

which implies

(8.3)

Case 2.
Suppose that

8.1
implies that

|S_{d}(\mathbf{x};N)|\leqslant N^{d^{2}+1-dt+o(1)}(M+N)^{(1-1/D)(d^{2}+1)-(d^{2}+1-dt)}.

By Lemma
4.3
we conclude that for almost all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathsf{T}
with respect to

|S_{d}(\mathbf{x};N)|\leqslant N^{1-1/D+o(1)}.

Then applying the similar argument to
Case 1

(8.4)

\alpha\leqslant 1-1/D,

which contradicts our assumption that
\alpha\in(1-1/D,1)
.
Thus we are in
Case 1
and we have (
8.3
) for any integer
1\leqslant h\leqslant d
.
Since (
8.3
) holds for any
t<\dim{\mathcal{E}}_{d,\alpha}

10.1.
Mean values of exponential polynomials

For a real sequences
f(n)
we define the sums

(10.1)

W_{f}(\mathbf{x};N)=\sum_{h=-N}^{N}\frac{1}{|h|+1}\left|\sum_{n=1}^{N}{\mathbf{\,e}}\left(hn/N+xf(n)\right)\right|.

Then a special form of
[
16
, Lemma 3.2]
implies
for
\mathbf{x}\in\mathsf{T}
and
1\leqslant M\leqslant N
we have

(10.2)

V_{f}(\mathbf{x};M)\ll W_{f}(\mathbf{x};N),

where
W_{f}(\mathbf{x};N)
is given by (
10.1
).

Our method is based on mean value estimates on the sums
W_{f}(x;N)
.
However our next result shows that for integer-valued sequences the mean value of
W_{f}(x;N)
is controlled by the mean value of
V_{f}(x;N)
provided that the exponent is some even integer. It follows by using a similar argument to
the proof of
[
18
, Lemma 2.4]
. We give a proof here for completeness.

Lemma 10.1
.

Let
f\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x]
be an integer sequence such that for some even number
s>0
and some real
t>0
one has

\int_{\mathsf{T}}|V_{f}(x;N)|^{s}dx\ll N^{t},

then we have

\int_{\mathsf{T}}W_{f}(x;N)^{s}dx\ll N^{t}(\log N)^{s}.

Proof.

Write

\displaystyle W_{f}(\mathbf{x};N)=\sum_{h=-N}^{N}\left(\frac{1}{|h|+1}\right)^{1-1/s}

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{|h|+1}\right)^{1/s}

\displaystyle\times\left|\sum_{n=1}^{N}{\mathbf{\,e}}\left(hn/N+xf(n)\right)\right|.

Applying the Hölder inequality, we obtain

(10.3)

\begin{split}W_{f}(\mathbf{x};N)^{s}\ll\left(\log N\right)^{s-1}&\sum_{h=-N}^{N}\frac{1}{1+|h|}\\
&\quad\quad\quad\times\left|\sum_{n=1}^{N}{\mathbf{\,e}}\left(hn/N+xf(n)\right)\right|^{s}.\end{split}

For any
h
and
N
and even number
s
, opening the integral and applying the orthogonal property of
{\mathbf{\,e}}(x)
, we derive

\displaystyle\int_{\mathsf{T}}

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{n=1}^{N}{\mathbf{\,e}}\left(hn/N+xf(n)\right)\right|^{s}dx

\displaystyle=\#\left\{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{s}):\penalty\ 1\leqslant n_{i}\leqslant N,\ \sum_{i=1}^{s/2}(f(n_{i})-f(n_{s/2+i})=0\right\}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathsf{T}}\left|\sum_{n=1}^{N}{\mathbf{\,e}}\left(xf(n)\right)\right|^{s}dx.

Combining with (
10.3
) we obtain the desired bound.

\sqcap
\sqcup

Observe that Lemma
10.1
implies that for integer-values sequences,
we only need to estimate the moments of the sums
V_{f}(x;N)
rather than of
W_{f}(x;N)

We now recall some mean value estimates on the sums
V_{f}(x;N)
in (

) when

is a polynomial.
We first recall the following result of Hua
[
29
]
, see also
[
40

Lemma 10.2
.

Let
f\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x]
be a polynomial with degree
d\geqslant 2
, then for each natural number
1\leqslant r\leqslant d
,

\int_{T}|V_{f}(x;N)|^{2^{r}}dx\leqslant N^{2^{r}-r+o(1)}.

Wooley
[
40
, Corollary 14.2]
(see also
[
8
, Theorem 10]
) obtains the following better bound when
r
is large.

Lemma 10.3
.

Let
f\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x]
be a polynomial with degree
d\geqslant 2
, then for each natural number
1\leqslant r\leqslant d

\int_{T}|V_{f}(x;N)|^{r(r+1)}dx\leqslant N^{r^{2}+o(1)}.

Furthermore, for the case of monomials Wooley
[
40
, Corollary 14.7]

gives a stronger result.

Lemma 10.4
.

Let
d\geqslant 2
and

s_{0}=d(d-1)+\min_{r=1,\ldots,d}\frac{2d+(r-1)(r-2)}{r}.

Then

\int_{T}\left|\sum_{n=1}^{N}{\mathbf{\,e}}\left(xn^{d}\right)\right|^{s_{0}}dx\leqslant N^{s_{0}-d+o(1)}.

We now turn to mean value theorems for sums with arbitrary sequences.

In particular, we have the following simple bound on the second moment of the sums
W_{f}(x;N)
, defined by (
1.5
) with well-spaced sequences.

Lemma 10.5
.

Let
f(n)
be a real sequence such that
f(n)-f(m)\gg 1
for all
m\neq n
. Then for any interval
{\mathcal{I}}
we have

\int_{{\mathcal{I}}}W_{f}(x;N)^{2}dx\ll N(\log N)^{3},

where
W_{f}(\mathbf{x};N)
is given by (
10.1
).

Proof.

By the Cauchy inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle W_{f}(x;N)^{2}

\displaystyle\ll\log N\sum_{h=-N}^{N}\frac{1}{|h|+1}\left|\sum_{n=1}^{N}{\mathbf{\,e}}\left(hn/N+xf(n)\right)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\ll\log N\sum_{h=-N}^{N}\frac{1}{|h|+1}\left(N+\Sigma(x)\right),

where

\Sigma(x)=\sum_{1\leqslant n\neq m\leqslant N}{\mathbf{\,e}}(h(n-m)/N+x(f(n)-f(m)).

The condition
|f(n)-f(m)|\gg 1
implies that the values
f(1),\ldots,f(N)
are separated

Mean values and Hausdorff dimension for polynomially growing sequences

We have the following general result about the upper bound on
\dim{\mathcal{F}}_{f,\alpha}
.

Lemma 10.10
.

Let
f(n)
be a real sequence that satisfies (
3.2
) for some constant
\tau>0
. Suppose that there are positive constants
s,t
such that

\int_{\mathsf{T}}|W_{f}(x;N)|^{s}dx\leqslant N^{t+o(1)}.

Then

\dim{\mathcal{F}}_{f,\alpha}\leqslant\frac{\tau+1-\alpha+t-s\alpha}{\tau+1-\alpha}.

Proof.

For each
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
denote

B_{N}=\{x\in T:\penalty\ W_{f}(x;N)\geqslant N^{\alpha}\}.

Let
N_{i}=2^{i}
,
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
\eta>0
. Applying (
10.2
) we obtain

{\mathcal{F}}_{f,\alpha+\eta}\subseteq\bigcap_{q=1}^{\infty}\bigcup_{i=q}^{\infty}B_{N_{i}}.

Indeed, let
x\in{\mathcal{F}}_{f,\alpha+\eta}
and suppose to the contrary that

x\notin\bigcap_{q=1}^{\infty}\bigcup_{i=q}^{\infty}B_{N_{i}}.

Then
W_{f}(x;N_{i})<N_{i}^{\alpha}
holds for all large
N_{i}
. For any
N
there
exists a number
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
N_{i}\leqslant N<N_{i+1}
, and for large enough
N
by Lemma
10.9
we have

V_{f}(x;N)\ll W_{f}(x;N_{i+1})\ll N_{i+1}^{\alpha}\ll N^{\alpha},

which contradicts our assumption.

For each
N_{i}
by Lemma
10.9
we obtain

B_{N_{i}}\subseteq\bigcup_{I\in{\mathcal{I}}_{N_{i}}}{\mathcal{I}}_{N_{i}},

where
|I|\leqslant N^{\alpha-\tau-1-\varepsilon}
for each
I\in{\mathcal{I}}_{N_{i}}
and
\#{\mathcal{I}}_{N_{i}}\leqslant N_{i}^{t-s\alpha+\tau+1-\alpha+2\varepsilon}
.

From the definition of the Hausdorff dimension, using the above notation, we have the following inequality

(10.4)

\dim{\mathcal{F}}_{f,\alpha+\eta}\leqslant\inf\left\{\nu>0:\penalty\ \sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sum_{I\in{\mathcal{I}}_{N_{i}}}|I|^{\nu}<\infty\right\}.

Note that

\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sum_{I\in{\mathcal{I}}_{N_{i}}}|I|^{\nu}\ll\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}N_{i}^{t-s\alpha+\tau+1-\alpha+2\varepsilon}N_{i}^{(\alpha-\tau-1)\nu},

thus to make the series convergent it is sufficient to have

\nu>\frac{t-s\alpha+\tau+1-\alpha+2\varepsilon}{\tau+1-\alpha}.

Combining with (
10.4
) and the arbitrary choice of
\varepsilon>0
we obtain

\dim{\mathcal{F}}_{f,\alpha+\eta}\leqslant\frac{\tau+1-\alpha+t-s\alpha}{\tau+1-\alpha}.

Since this holds for any
\eta>0
, we obtain the desired bound.

12.1.
Preliminaries

Lemma 12.1
.

\displaystyle=N+\sum_{1\leqslant n\neq m\leqslant N}\int_{\mathsf{T}_{d}}{\mathbf{\,e}}\left(\left\langle\mathbf{x},(A_{n}-A_{m})\mathbf{h}\right\rangle\right)d\mathbf{x}.

\int_{\mathsf{T}_{d}}{\mathbf{\,e}}\left(\left\langle\mathbf{x},(A_{n}-A_{m})\mathbf{h}\right\rangle\right)d\mathbf{x}=0,

which yields the desired identity.
