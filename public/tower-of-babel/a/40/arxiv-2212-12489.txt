Partitions into semiprimes
Madhuparna Das, Nicolas Robles, Alexandru Zaharescu, Dirk Zeindler

Abstract
. Let ℙ \mathbb{P} denote the set of primes and 𝒩 ⊂ ℕ \mathcal{N}\subset\mathbb{N} be a set with arbitrary weights attached to its elements. Set 𝔭 𝒩 ​ ( n ) \mathfrak{p}_{\mathcal{N}}(n) to be the restricted partition function which counts partitions of n n with all its parts lying in 𝒩 \mathcal{N} . By employing a suitable variation of the Hardy-Littlewood circle method we provide the asymptotic formula of 𝔭 𝒩 ​ ( n ) \mathfrak{p}_{\mathcal{N}}(n) for the set of semiprimes 𝒩 = { p 1 p 2 : p 1 , p 2 ∈ ℙ } \mathcal{N}=\{p_{1}p_{2}:p_{1},p_{2}\in\mathbb{P}\} in different set-ups (counting factors, repeating the count of factors, and different factors). In order to deal with the minor arc, we investigate a double Weyl sum over prime products and find its corresponding bound thereby extending some of the results of Vinogradov on partitions. We also describe a methodology to find the asymptotic partition 𝔭 𝒩 ​ ( n ) \mathfrak{p}_{\mathcal{N}}(n) for general weighted sets 𝒩 \mathcal{N} by assigning different strategies for the major, non-principal major, and minor arcs. Our result is contextualized alongside other recent results in partition asymptotics.

: partitions, semiprimes, Hardy-Littlewood circle method, Weyl sums, Vinogradov’s bound, prime zeta function, logarithmic singularities, Meissel-Mertens constant.

1.
Introduction and results

A partition of a positive integer
n
is a non-decreasing sequence of positive integers whose sum is
n
. Suppose that
\mathcal{N}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
and let
\mathfrak{p}_{\mathcal{N}}(n)
denote the restricted partition function which counts partitions of
n
lying within
\mathcal{N}
. When
\mathcal{N}=\mathbb{N}
, we obtain the unrestricted partition function
\mathfrak{p}_{\mathbb{N}}(n)
studied by Hardy and Ramanujan
[
15
]
in 1918 through the use of the Hardy-Littlewood method. Their result states that

\displaystyle\mathfrak{p}_{\mathbb{N}}(n)\sim\frac{1}{4n\sqrt{3}}\exp\bigg(\pi\sqrt{\frac{2n}{3}}\bigg)\quad\textnormal{as}\quad n\to\infty.

Partitions into prime numbers have been studied by various authors such as Bateman and Erdős
[
1
,
2
]
, Browkin
[
6
]
, Kerawala
[
18
]
, Roth and Szekeres
[
26
]
, as well as Yang
[
35
]
. In 2008 Vaughan
[
28
]
was able to simplify and improve most of the literature on prime partitions. Let
\mathbb{P}=\{p:p\textnormal{ is prime}\}
. We will now study the partitions into semiprimes. Our main result is as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\mathbb{P}_{2}=\{p_{1}p_{2}:p_{1},p_{2}\in\mathbb{P}\}
and semiprimes are counted only once, i.e.
p_{1}p_{2}
and
p_{2}p_{1}
are considered the same. The number
\mathfrak{p}_{\mathbb{P}_{2}}
of such partitions of
n
with all parts lying in
\mathbb{P}_{2}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathfrak{p}_{\mathbb{P}_{2}}(n)

\displaystyle\sim\mathfrak{c}_{1}n^{-\frac{3}{4}}(\log(n/2))^{-\frac{1}{4}}(\log\log n+\mathfrak{c}_{2})^{\frac{1}{4}}

\displaystyle\quad\times\exp\bigg\{\mathfrak{c}_{3}\bigg(\frac{n}{\log(n/2)}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}(\log\log n+\mathfrak{c}_{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigg(1+O\bigg(\frac{\log\log n}{\log n}\bigg)\bigg)\bigg\},

as
n\to\infty
and the constants are given by

\displaystyle\mathfrak{c}_{1}=\frac{(4\zeta(2))^{\frac{1}{4}}}{2^{\frac{1}{4}}\sqrt{\pi}},\quad\mathfrak{c}_{2}=M-\log 2,\quad\textnormal{and}\quad\mathfrak{c}_{3}=(2^{\frac{1}{2}}+2^{-\frac{3}{2}})(4\zeta(2))^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Here
M
is the Meissel-Mertens constant
M=\gamma+\sum_{p\in\mathbb{P}}(\log(1-\frac{1}{p})+\frac{1}{p})\approx 0.26149721\ldots
where
\gamma
is the Euler constant.

The technique we present is versatile enough to answer questions about other types of restricted sets of primes.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\mathbb{P}_{2}^{\neq}=\{p_{1}p_{2}:p_{1},p_{2}\in\mathbb{P},p_{1}\neq p_{2}\}
. The number
\mathfrak{p}_{\mathbb{P}_{2}^{\neq}}
of such partitions of
n
with all parts lying in
\mathbb{P}_{2}^{\neq}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathfrak{p}_{\mathbb{P}_{2}^{\neq}}(n)

\displaystyle\sim\mathfrak{c}_{1}n^{-\frac{3}{4}}(\log(n/2))^{-\frac{1}{4}}(\log\log n+\mathfrak{c}_{2})^{\frac{1}{4}}

\displaystyle\quad\times\exp\bigg\{\mathfrak{c}_{3}\bigg(\frac{n}{\log(n/2)}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}(\log\log n+\mathfrak{c}_{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigg(1+O\bigg(\frac{\log\log n}{\log n}\bigg)\bigg)\bigg\},

as
n\to\infty
and where the constants are the same as in Theorem
1.1
.

Now set
\mathbb{P}_{2}^{\sharp}=\{(p_{1},p_{2})\in\mathbb{N}^{2}:p_{1},p_{2}\in\mathbb{P}\}
.
Further, we identify an element
(p_{1},p_{2})\in\mathbb{P}_{2}^{\sharp}
with the part
p_{1}p_{2}
.
Here semiprimes are counted twice since
(p_{1},p_{2})\neq(p_{2},p_{1})
unless
p_{1}=p_{2}
.
The number of partitions with all parts in
\mathbb{P}_{2}^{\sharp}(n)
is denoted by
\mathfrak{p}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}^{\sharp}(n)
and
the asymptotic behaviour of
\mathfrak{p}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}^{\sharp}(n)
is stated in Theorem
1.3
below.
The techniques employed to prove Theorem
1.1
, Theorem
1.2
, and Theorem
1.3
are the same.
In fact, we can prove all three theorems in one single bundle by studying a slightly more general case covering all three results simultaneously. We do this by assigning to squares and non-squares different weights in the corresponding generating function, see Section
3.1
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\mathbb{P}_{2}^{\sharp}(n)
be defined as above. The number
\mathfrak{p}_{\mathbb{P}_{2}^{\sharp}}
of such partitions of
n
with all parts lying in
\mathbb{P}_{2}^{\sharp}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathfrak{p}_{\mathbb{P}_{2}^{\sharp}}(n)

\displaystyle\sim\mathfrak{c}_{1}n^{-\frac{3}{4}}(\log n)^{-\frac{1}{4}}(\log\log n+\mathfrak{c}_{2})^{\frac{1}{4}}

\displaystyle\quad\times\exp\bigg\{\mathfrak{c}_{3}\bigg(\frac{n}{\log n}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}(\log\log n+\mathfrak{c}_{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigg(1+O\bigg(\frac{\log\log n}{\log n}\bigg)\bigg)\bigg\},

as
n\to\infty
and where the constants are the same as in Theorem
1.1
.

Theorem 1.4
.

Using the notation and definitions above, we have

\displaystyle\mathfrak{p}_{\mathbb{P}_{2}}(n+1)-\mathfrak{p}_{\mathbb{P}_{2}}(n)\sim\mathfrak{c}_{4}\bigg(\frac{\mathfrak{c}_{2}+\log\log n+o(1)}{n\log(n/2)}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathfrak{p}_{\mathbb{P}_{2}}(n)

as
n\to\infty
and where the constants are the same as in Theorem
1.1
with
\mathfrak{c}_{4}=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{1}{3}}
.

1.1.
Previous results in the literature

Over the last decade there has been a strong interest in studying partitions by employing the Hardy-Littlewood circle method and the technique pioneered in
[
28
]
and then in
[
30
]
.
An important result using this technique was established by Gafni
[
13
]
in 2016 and generalized to arithmetic progressions in 2018 by Berndt, Malik, and Zaharescu
[
3
]
.

Theorem 1.5

(Gafni)
.

If
\mathcal{N}_{k}=\{x^{k}:x\in\mathbb{N},k\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}\}
, then as
n\to\infty
one has that

\displaystyle\mathfrak{p}_{\mathcal{N}_{k}}(n)\sim\mathfrak{c}_{1}\exp(\mathfrak{c}_{2}n^{\frac{1}{k+1}})n^{-\frac{3k+1}{2(k+1)}},

where
\mathfrak{c}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{c}_{2}
are positive constants that can be made explicit and depend only on
k
.

Theorem 1.6

(Berndt-Malik-Zaharescu)
.

If
\mathcal{N}_{k,a,b}=\{x^{k}:x\equiv a\operatorname{mod}b,k\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2},(a,b)=1\}
, then as
n\to\infty
one has that

\displaystyle\mathfrak{p}_{\mathcal{N}_{k,a,b}}(n)\sim\mathfrak{c}_{1}\exp(\mathfrak{c}_{2}n^{\frac{1}{k+1}})n^{-\frac{b+bk+2ak}{2b(k+1)}},

where
\mathfrak{c}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{c}_{2}
are positive constants that can be made explicit and depend only on
a,b
and
k
.

One can think of the sets
\mathcal{N}_{k}
and
\mathcal{N}_{k,a,b}
as integer values of the
x^{k}
and
(a+bx)^{k}
with
(a,b)=1
, respectively. Therefore this begged the question of whether these results could be extended to polynomials of arbitrary degree. This was accomplished in
[
9
]
by considering an exotic zeta function.

Theorem 1.7

(Dunn-Robles)
.

Let
f
be the polynomial of degree
d
with
d\geq 2
and set
\mathcal{N}_{f}=\{f(x):x\in\mathbb{N}\}
. If
\mathcal{N}_{f}\subset\mathbb{N}
and
\gcd(\mathcal{N}_{f})=1
, then as
n\to\infty
one has that

\displaystyle\mathfrak{p}_{\mathcal{N}_{f}}(n)\sim\mathfrak{c}_{1}\exp(\mathfrak{c}_{2}n^{\frac{1}{d+1}})n^{-\frac{2d(1-\zeta(0,\alpha))+1}{2(d+1)}},

where
\zeta(0,\alpha)
is a value of an appropriate Matsumoto-Weng zeta function
[
23
]
. The positive constants
\mathfrak{c}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{c}_{2}
can be made explicit and depend only on
f
.

Other very recent results include
[
4
]
and
[
5
]
. We now move on to restricted partitions over primes and we state an important result of Gafni
[
12
]
from 2021.

Theorem 1.8

(Gafni)
.

If
\mathbb{P}^{k}=\{p^{k}:p\in\mathbb{P},k\in\mathbb{N}\}
, then as
n\to\infty
one has that

\displaystyle\mathfrak{p}_{\mathbb{P}^{k}}(n)\sim\mathfrak{c}_{1}\exp\bigg(\mathfrak{c}_{2}\frac{n^{\frac{1}{k+1}}}{(\log n)^{\frac{k}{k+1}}}(1+o(1))\bigg)n^{-\frac{2k+1}{2k+2}}(\log n)^{-\frac{k}{2k+2}},

where
\mathfrak{c}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{c}_{2}
are positive constants that can be made explicit and depend only on
k
.

The case
k=1
had been settled by Vaughan
[
28
]
in 2008. The case
k=2
will be needed to remove the squared primes from our count of duplicated semiprimes.

A very interesting recent development is due to Debryune and Tenenbaum
[
8
]
by using the saddle-point method for restricted partitions. One can think of the saddle-point method as a coarse version of the circle method, see
[
12
]
, in which only the major arc is the principal arc at the origin. Debryune and Tenenbaum were able to show asympototic formulas for monomials
\mathfrak{p}_{\mathcal{N}_{k}}(n)
, monomials in arithmetic progressions
\mathfrak{p}_{\mathcal{N}_{k,a,b}}(n)
, and polynomials
\mathfrak{p}_{\mathcal{N}_{f}}(n)
, thereby encompassing some of the results above. However, as explained by Gafni in
[
12
]
sets like the set of primes, prime powers (or semiprimes) cannot be studied with the technique provided in
[
8
]
. The reason is that one of the requirements is that the resulting Dirichlet series for the major arcs (see Section
2
below) must be meromorphically continued to the half-plane
\real(s)\geq-\varepsilon
for
\varepsilon>0
. In our case of semiprimes the resulting Dirichlet series is
(\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s))^{2}
where

(1.1)

\displaystyle\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s)=\sum_{p\in\mathbb{P}}\frac{1}{p^{s}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(n)}{n}\log\zeta(ns),

which has essential singularities at every zero of the Riemann zeta-function. In addition, the line
\real(s)=0
represents a natural boundary as the singularities cluster near all points of this line. Indeed, in
[
22
]
Landau and Walfisz showed that
\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s)
cannot be continued beyond the line
\real(s)=0
due to the fact that we face a clustering of singular points along the imaginary axis emanating from the non-trivial zeros of
\zeta(s)
on the critical line. Therefore, although the method put forward in
[
8
]
is very powerful, it does not cover certain difficult cases and in the next section we propose a method, partially based on
[
12
]
, to deal with sets that yield Dirichlet series with essential singularities and branch cuts.

We shall also show that by employing tailored versions of the prime number theorem (in our case an asymptotic for
\sum_{p_{1}p_{2}\leq x}1
) along with Mertens’ estimates also provides an alternative approach – and sometimes quicker way – of evaluating the principal major arcs. However, this methodology requires a minimum of information on the error of the standard prime number theorem. Using elementary methods to obtain the prime number theorem is sufficient to get the order of magnitude of the main asymptotic term but further refinements from the zero-free region lead to more accurate main and error terms. This provides an implicit way of linking information about the zeros of
\zeta(s)
and partitions.

Throughout the paper we set the notation
\operatorname{e}(x)=\exp(2\pi ix)
. We shall also use the convention that
\varepsilon
denotes an arbitrarily small positive quantity that may not be the same at each occurrence. The logarithmic integral will be defined by the Cauchy principal value

\displaystyle\operatorname{li}(x):=\mathop{\lim}\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0+}\bigg(\int_{0}^{1-\varepsilon}+\int_{1+\varepsilon}^{x}\bigg)\frac{dt}{\log t}\quad\textnormal{and}\quad\operatorname{Li}(x):=\int_{2}^{\infty}\frac{dt}{\log t}=\operatorname{li}(x)-\operatorname{li}(2).

Euler’s constant will be denoted by
\gamma=C_{0}\approx 0.577216\ldots
; Fröberg’s constant will be denoted
\mathcal{D}=\sum_{n\geq 2}\frac{\mu(n)}{n}\log\zeta(n)\approx-0.315718\ldots
. Finally, Mertens’ first constant is
M_{1}=M\approx 0.261497\ldots
and it comes from
\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{1}{p}=\log\log x+M_{1}+o(1)
where the sum is taken over primes.

3.1
.

2.
Strategies for restricted weighted partitions

The generating function for partitions with weights into members of a general set
\mathcal{N}
is given by

\displaystyle\Psi_{\{\mathcal{N},w\}}(z):=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathfrak{p}_{\{\mathcal{N},w\}}(n)z^{n}=\prod_{n\in\mathcal{N}}(1-z^{n})^{-w(n)}.

Here
w(n)\in\mathbb{N}
is the weight placed on each element
n\in\mathcal{N}
. It is useful to deal with the logarithm of this function

(2.1)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\{\mathcal{N},w\}}(z):=\log\Psi_{\{\mathcal{N},w\}}(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}}\frac{w(n)}{j}z^{nj}.

An application of Cauchy’s integral formula yields

(2.2)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{p}_{\{\mathcal{N},w\}}(n)=\rho^{-n}\int_{0}^{1}\exp(\Phi_{\{\mathcal{N},w\}}(\rho\operatorname{e}(\alpha)))\operatorname{e}(\alpha)d\alpha=\rho^{-n}\int_{0}^{1}\Psi_{\{\mathcal{N},w\}}(\rho\operatorname{e}(\alpha))\operatorname{e}(\alpha)d\alpha,

where
\rho<1
. Therefore our problem at hand consists in evaluating
\Phi_{\{\mathcal{N},w\}}(z)
as given by the middle side of (
2.2
), insert into (
2.1
) and compute the resulting integral. Usually when one implements the circle method, the integral in (
2.2
) has to be divided into major and minor arcs. The major arcs will typically contribute to the main term of the asymptotic formula whereas one has to show that the minor arcs are error terms of smaller magnitude. However, when dealing with restricted weighted partitions, the contribution from the major arc at the origin is substantially larger than the contributions from the rest of the major arcs away from the origin
[
12
,
28
]
. This implies that we split our integral as follows

\displaystyle\mathfrak{p}_{\{\mathcal{N},w\}}(n)=\rho^{-n}\bigg(\int_{\mathfrak{M}(1,0)}+\int_{\mathfrak{M}\backslash\mathfrak{M}(1,0)}+\int_{\mathfrak{m}}\bigg)\exp(\Phi_{\{\mathcal{N},w\}}(\rho\operatorname{e}(\alpha)))\operatorname{e}(\alpha)d\alpha.

The first integral above, representing the major arcs, is treated by the use of contour integration or a by variation of the prime number theorem then followed by the saddle-point method. The major arcs for
\mathfrak{M}(q,a)
with
q>1
can be shown to yield a term smaller than
\mathfrak{M}(1,0)
. This means that the main term of the asymptotic
\mathfrak{p}_{\{\mathcal{N},w\}}(n)
will be dictated only by the first integral when
\alpha
is close to the origin.

In order to evaluate the main arc at the origin, one needs detailed information about the set
\mathcal{N}
and its corresponding weights
w(n)
. The technique consists in writing the associated Dirichlet function of the set
\mathcal{N}
as a function of known zeta functions (e.g. Riemann, Hurwitz, Dirichlet, Matsumoto-Weng, or prime zeta functions). In other words, we will need to write

\displaystyle\zeta_{\{\mathcal{N},w\}}(s):=\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}}\frac{w(n)}{n^{s}}=f(\zeta(s),\zeta(s,a),\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s),L(s,\chi),\cdots),

for some function
f
to be determined. For instance, most unrestricted partitions can be mapped to the Riemann zeta-function, whereas unrestricted partitions in arithmetic progressions can be mapped to the Dirichlet
L
-function or to the Hurwitz zeta-function. In the case of prime partitions, which are restrictive, the mapping goes to the prime zeta function. We therefore leverage the analytic properties of the zeta functions including their convergence, the location of their zeros, their analytic continuations, their singularities, as well as residues at the poles. However, in our case of interest, we will be dealing with (products of) prime zeta functions so we will
also
have to account for logarithmic singularities as well as branch cuts.

The non-principal major arcs require an elaborate setup, namely leveraging the distribution of the set
\mathcal{N}
in residue classes and our best weapon in this case is the Siegel-Walfisz theorem
[
27
,
33
]
.

Lastly, the minor arcs are by far the most difficult to bound. To get an idea of their contribution one needs specific technology for Weyl sums of the form

\displaystyle S_{\{\mathcal{N},w\}}(\beta,y)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq y\\
n\in\mathcal{N}\end{subarray}}w(n)\operatorname{e}(\beta n)\quad\textnormal{with}\quad\operatorname{e}(x)=\exp(2\pi ix).

For instance, in the case of restricted partitions over primes, one will need Vinogradov’s bound
\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq y\end{subarray}}\operatorname{e}(\beta p)\ll(yq^{-\frac{1}{2}}+y^{\frac{4}{5}}+y^{\frac{1}{2}}q^{\frac{1}{2}})(\log 2y)^{3}
, where the sum is taken over primes, provided that
|\beta-\frac{a}{q}|\leq\frac{1}{q^{2}}
and
(a,q)=1
. For prime powers, Gafni
[
12
]
resorted to a bound by Kawada and Wooley
[
17
]
. It is worth remarking that the bound from Kawada and Wooley is given in
[
17
, Lemma 3.3]
in dyadic form as

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P<p<2P\\
p\in\mathbb{P}\end{subarray}}\operatorname{e}(\alpha p^{k})\ll P^{1-2^{-k-1}+\varepsilon}+\frac{q^{\varepsilon}w_{k}(q)^{1/2}P(\log P)^{4}}{(1+P^{k}|\alpha-a/q|)^{1/2}},

for
P\geq 2
and where
w_{k}(q)
is defined by

\displaystyle w_{k}(p^{uk+v})=\begin{cases}kp^{-u-1/2}&\mbox{ when $u\geq 0$ and $v=1$,}\\
p^{-u-1}&\mbox{ when $u\geq 0$ and $2\leq v\leq k$.}\end{cases}

This bound is proved for integers
k
such that
k\geq 4
. However, in
[
20
]
Kumchev explains that Wooley settled the case
k=3
in
[
34
]
. Moreover, one could use the work from Ghosh
[
14
]
to settle the case
k=2
, and lastly the case
k=1
is due to Vinogradov
[
32
]
and greatly simplified by Vaughan
[
29
]
. In our case of semiprimes we will need a more elaborated version of Vinogradov’s lemma for
\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{1}p_{2}\leq y\end{subarray}}\operatorname{e}(\beta p_{1}p_{2})
and where the sum is taken over primes
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
, namely

\displaystyle\sum_{p_{1}p_{2}\leq X}\operatorname{e}(\beta p_{1}p_{2})\ll\frac{X}{q^{\frac{1}{6}}}(\log X)^{\frac{7}{3}}+X^{\frac{16}{17}}(\log X)^{\frac{39}{17}}+X^{\frac{7}{8}}q^{\frac{1}{8}}(\log X)^{\frac{9}{4}},

where
\beta
satisfies the above mentioned Diophantine conditions. We also remark that Lemmas 3.1, 3.2 and 3.3 from
[
20
]
do not work as suitably as the bilinear form supplied in Lemma 13.8 from of Iwaniec and Kowalski
[
16
]
. At any rate, the technology to bound the Weyl sums is arguably the most critical part of a successful application of the Hardy-Littlewood circle method.

Summarizing, only by having a satisfactory understanding of these three components for a given set
\mathcal{N}
along with its weights
w(n)
, can we obtain the asymptotics for
\mathfrak{p}_{\{\mathcal{N},w\}}(n)
. As argued in
[
12
]
, it is exceedingly rare to find a set
\mathcal{N}
, let alone with weights
w
, for which we have a good picture of these three components. As we shall see in the next sections, the Dirichlet series and its associated contour integral while technically difficult is in fact the most straightforward part, whereas the Siegel-Walfisz theorem will have to be applied many times in intricate ways, alongside the prime number theorem with the standard zero-free region, to get the bound of the non-principal major arcs. Finally, the machinery to deal with our associated Weyl sum will be supplied in Theorem
5.1
.

3.
Generating functions and arcs used

In this section, we specify the generating functions we are working with as well as the arcs that we shall use.

3.1.
Generating functions

We will work with the generating function

(3.1)

\displaystyle\Psi_{\lambda,\mu}(z):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathfrak{p}_{\lambda,\mu}(n)z^{n}:=\exp\left(\Phi_{\lambda,\mu}(z)\right)

with
\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{R}
and
\Phi_{\lambda,\mu}(z)=\lambda\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{2}}(z)+\mu\Phi_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(z)
and where

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{2}}(z):=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j}\sum_{p_{1}\in\mathbb{P}}\sum_{p_{2}\in\mathbb{P}}z^{(p_{1}p_{2})j}\quad\text{and}\quad\Phi_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(z):=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j}\sum_{p\in\mathbb{P}}z^{p^{2}j}.

The function
\Psi_{\lambda,\mu}(z)
in (
3.1
) covers all generating functions we require for the theorems in Section
1
. Taking
\lambda=\mu=\frac{1}{2}
leads to
\mathbb{P}_{2}
, taking
\lambda=\frac{1}{2}
and
\mu=-\frac{1}{2}
yields
\mathbb{P}_{2}^{\neq}
, and setting
\mu=1
and
\lambda=0
gives
\mathbb{P}_{2}^{\sharp}
.
In other words, we have

(3.2)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathfrak{p}_{\mathbb{P}_{2}}(n)z^{n}=\Psi_{\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}}(z),\quad\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathfrak{p}_{\mathbb{P}_{2}^{\neq}}(n)z^{n}=\Psi_{\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2}}(z),\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathfrak{p}_{\mathbb{P}_{2}^{\sharp}}(n)z^{n}=\Psi_{1,0}(z).

Furthermore,
\Phi_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(z)
has been studied in detail in
[
12
]
.
Thus we have to study
\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{2}}(z)
only to obtain the theorems in Section
1
.

Remark 3.1
.

In order to connect
\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{2}^{\sharp}}(z)
with a weighted set, we could take

\displaystyle\Phi_{\{\mathbb{P}_{2},w\}}(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{P}_{2}}\frac{w(m)}{j}z^{mj}.

The weight function will be
w(n)=w_{2}(n)
with

\displaystyle w_{2}(n)=\begin{cases}2,\quad&\mbox{if $n$ is a product of two distinct primes},\\
1,\quad&\mbox{if $n$ is the square of a prime,}\\
0,\quad&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{cases}

In other words,
w_{2}(n)
is the number of representations of
n
in the form
p_{1}p_{2}
where
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
are primes. Lastly, we define
\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{P}_{2}}(n)
be the indicator function of semiprimes.

3.2.
Set up of the arcs

Applying Cauchy’s theorem to the generating function in (
3.2
), we get

\displaystyle\mathfrak{p}_{\lambda,\mu}(n)

\displaystyle=\rho^{-n}\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\Psi_{\lambda,\mu}(\rho\operatorname{e}(\alpha))\operatorname{e}(-n\alpha)d\alpha

(3.3)

Theorem 4.1

. Then for any

\displaystyle\bigg(\rho\frac{d}{d\rho}\bigg)^{m}\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{2}}(\rho)

4.1.
The method of contour integration

The first method of proof is shown here.

Proof of Theorem (
4.1
).

Recall that we have

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{2}}(\rho)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j}\sum_{p_{1}}\sum_{p_{2}}\rho^{p_{1}p_{2}j}.

Inserting the definition of
\rho
yields

\displaystyle\bigg(\rho\frac{d}{d\rho}\bigg)^{m}\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{2}}(\rho)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}j^{m-1}\sum_{p_{1}}p_{1}^{m}\sum_{p_{2}}p_{2}^{m}e^{-p_{1}p_{2}j/X}.

We now employ the Mellin transform of the Gamma function so that

\displaystyle\bigg(\rho\frac{d}{d\rho}\bigg)^{m}\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{2}}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{(c)}X^{s}\bigg(\sum_{p_{1}}\frac{1}{p_{1}^{s-m}}\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{p_{2}}\frac{1}{p_{2}^{s-m}}\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j^{s+1-m}}\bigg)\Gamma(s)ds

(4.3)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{(c)}X^{s}(\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s-m))^{2}\zeta(s+1-m)\Gamma(s)ds,

since
c>m+1
and where
\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s)
is the prime zeta function defined in (
1.1
) for
\real(s)>1
. The next step is to note that
\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s)=\log\zeta(s)-\mathcal{D}(s)
where

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(s)=\sum_{j\geq 2}\frac{1}{j}\sum_{p}\frac{1}{p^{js}}.

For any
\delta>0
we have that
\mathcal{D}(s)
converges absolutely and uniformly for
\real(s)\geq\frac{1}{2}+\delta
. If we make the replacement
(\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s-m))^{2}=(\log\zeta(s-m))^{2}-2\mathcal{D}(s-m)\log\zeta(s-m)+(\mathcal{D}(s-m))^{2}
in (
4.1
), then we could move the contour of integration to the line
\real(s)=c_{0}
for any
c_{0}>m+\tfrac{1}{2}
. The task at hand is therefore to compute the contribution from these three parts. We start with the first, and most difficult, case, that of
(\log\zeta(s-m))^{2}
, i.e.

(4.4)

\displaystyle\Omega(m,X):=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{(c)}X^{s}(\log\zeta(s-m))^{2}\zeta(s+1-m)\Gamma(s)ds.

We begin by noting that the integral is analytic in the zero-free region for
\zeta(s-m)
except for a logarithmic singularity at
s=m+1
. If we choose
T=\exp(\sqrt{\log X})
, then the integral can be truncated at height
T
with an acceptable error term. The remaining part of the integral can be shifted to the left of the line
\real(s)=m+1-\tfrac{c}{\log T}
where
c
is a suitable positive constant, except for a keyhole contour around the essential singularity at
s=m+1
. This keyhole contour runs counter clockwise along the top and the bottom of the branch cut located at
\{s=\sigma\,:\,\sigma\leq m+1\}
, as in Figure
4.1
.
Setting
\Xi
to denote this contour and since the integrand is analytic in
\Xi
we have, by Cauchy’s theorem, that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\Xi}X^{s}(\log\zeta(s-m))^{2}\zeta(s+1-m)\Gamma(s)ds

(4.5)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\bigg(\int_{\Xi_{1}}+\int_{\Xi_{2}}+\cdots+\int_{\Xi_{8}}\bigg)X^{s}(\log\zeta(s-m))^{2}\zeta(s+1-m)\Gamma(s)ds.

Ignoring for the moment the top and bottom parts, all the remaining segments and curves of the contour are well-controlled and will only contribute to the error term.

Figure 4.1.
The contour of integration
\Xi
alongside the zero-free region of
\zeta(s)
in blue.

Let us now concentrate on the top part of the branch cut which is given by

\displaystyle\Omega_{\textnormal{top}}(m,X)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{m+1-\tfrac{c}{\log T}}^{m+1}X^{s}(\log\zeta(s-m))^{2}\zeta(s+1-m)\Gamma(s)ds.

We make the change of variables
s=m+1-u
and realize that on the top branch of the cut we are dealing with
u\to u+i\varepsilon
for
\varepsilon>0
. In this case, our integral becomes

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{0}^{\tfrac{c}{\log T}}X^{m+1-(u+i\varepsilon)}(\log\zeta(1-(u+i\varepsilon)))^{2}\zeta(2-(u+i\varepsilon))\Gamma(m+1-(u+i\varepsilon))du.

If we use the identity

\displaystyle\log\zeta(s)=-\log(s-1)+g(s)\quad\textnormal{where}\quad g(s)=\log((s-1)\zeta(s)),

as well as
\log(a+ib)=\tfrac{1}{2}\log(a^{2}+b^{2})+i\theta
where
a+ib=\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}e^{i\theta}
for
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
, then
the above becomes

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{0}^{\tfrac{c}{\log T}}X^{m+1-(u+i\varepsilon)}

\displaystyle\bigg(-\frac{1}{2}\log(u^{2}+\varepsilon^{2})+i\theta+g(1-(u+i\varepsilon))\bigg)^{2}

\displaystyle\times\zeta(2-(u+i\varepsilon))\Gamma(m+1-(u+i\varepsilon))du.

Letting
\varepsilon\to 0
and realizing that on the top of the branch we have
\theta=\pi
leads us to

\displaystyle\Omega_{\textnormal{top}}(m,X)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{0}^{\tfrac{c}{\log T}}X^{m+1-u}(-\log u+i\pi+g(1-u))^{2}\zeta(2-u)\Gamma(m+1-u)du.

A similar reasoning on the bottom part of the branch cut, where
\theta=-\pi
, shows that

\displaystyle\Omega_{\textnormal{bottom}}(m,X)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{0}^{\tfrac{c}{\log T}}X^{m+1-u}(-\log u-i\pi+g(1-u))^{2}\zeta(2-u)\Gamma(m+1-u)du.

Therefore the contribution along the two segments of the branch cut is given by

\displaystyle\Omega(m,X)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{0}^{\tfrac{c}{\log T}}X^{m+1-u}\vartheta(u)\zeta(2-u)\Gamma(m+1-u)du,

where we have defined

\displaystyle\vartheta(u)=(-\log u+i\pi+g(1-u))^{2}-(-\log u-i\pi+g(1-u))^{2}=4\pi i(-\log u+g(1-u)).

Inserting this into our integral of interest yields

\displaystyle\Omega(m,X)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{0}^{\tfrac{c}{\log T}}4\pi i(-\log u+g(1-u))X^{m+1-u}\zeta(2-u)\Gamma(m+1-u)du.

For any
m
we have that

\displaystyle\zeta(2-u)\Gamma(m+1-u)=\zeta(2)\Gamma(m+1)+O(u)\quad\textnormal{and}\quad g(1-u)=-\gamma u+O(u)^{2}

uniformly for
u\in[0,1/2]
and where
\gamma
is the Euler constant. Thus the contribution from the cuts is given by

\displaystyle\Omega(m,X)=2\zeta(2)\Gamma(m+1)X^{m+1}\bigg(-\int_{0}^{\tfrac{c}{\log T}}X^{-u}\log udu+O\bigg(X^{m+1}\int_{0}^{\frac{c}{\log T}}X^{-u}udu\bigg)\bigg).

To compute this integral we invoke the formula

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{c/\log T}X^{-u}\log udu=-\frac{\gamma+\log\log X+\Gamma(0,\frac{c\log X}{\log T})+X^{-\frac{c}{\log T}}\log\frac{c}{\log T}}{\log X},

provided that
\real(\log X)>0
and where
\Gamma(a,z):=\int_{z}^{\infty}t^{a-1}e^{-t}dt
is the incomplete gamma function. This can be shown by considering (see e.g.
[
25
, Equation (6.2)]
or
[
19
,
\mathsection
3.3)]
)

\displaystyle I(a,b):=\int_{0}^{a}b^{-u}\log udu\quad\textnormal{and}\quad\log x=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint\frac{1}{x^{z}}\frac{dz}{z^{2}},

where the integral is taken over a small circle around the origin. We use this to swap the order of integration so that

\displaystyle I(a,b)

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{a}b^{-u}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint\frac{1}{u^{z}}\frac{dz}{z^{2}}du=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint\int_{0}^{a}b^{-u}\frac{du}{u^{z}}\frac{dz}{z^{2}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint\frac{a^{-z}}{\log b}(-a\operatorname{E}(z,a\log b)\log b+\Gamma(1-z)(a\log b)^{z})\frac{dz}{z^{2}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\log b}(\gamma+\Gamma(0,a\log b)+b^{-a}\log a+\log\log b),

by a direct residue calculus computation and where
\operatorname{E}(n,z):=\int_{1}^{\infty}e^{-zt}/t^{n}dt
is the exponential integral function. Thus we arrive at

\displaystyle\Omega(m,X)

\displaystyle=2\zeta(2)\Gamma(m+1)X^{m+1}\bigg(\frac{\gamma+\log\log X+\Gamma(0,\frac{c\log X}{\log T})+X^{-\frac{c}{\log T}}\log\frac{c}{\log T}}{\log X}\bigg)

\displaystyle\quad+O\bigg(X^{m+1}\frac{1-X^{-\frac{c}{\log T}}(1+\frac{c\log X}{\log T})}{\log^{2}X}\bigg)

\displaystyle=2\zeta(2)\Gamma(m+1)X^{m+1}\bigg(\frac{\gamma+\log\log X}{\log X}+\frac{\Gamma(0,c\sqrt{\log X})}{\log X}+\frac{e^{-\sqrt{\log X}}\log\frac{c}{\sqrt{\log X}}}{\log X}\bigg)

(4.6)

\displaystyle\quad+O\bigg(X^{m+1}\frac{1-e^{-c\sqrt{\log X}}(1+c\sqrt{\log X})}{\log^{2}X}\bigg)

since
T=\exp(\sqrt{\log X})
. Employing (
4.1
) we end up with

\displaystyle\Omega(m,X)=\frac{2\zeta(2)\Gamma(m+1)X^{m+1}(\gamma+\log\log X)}{\log X}\bigg(1+O\bigg(\frac{1}{\log X}\bigg)\bigg),

where we have used that

\displaystyle\Gamma(0,c\sqrt{\log X})=e^{-c\sqrt{\log X}+O(\frac{1}{X})}\bigg(\frac{-1+c\sqrt{\log X}}{c^{2}\log X}+O\bigg(\frac{1}{X}\bigg)\bigg)

as
X\to\infty
for
c>0
. We now need to compute the piece coming from
-2\mathcal{D}(s-m)\log\zeta(s-m)
which is given by

(4.7)

\displaystyle\tilde{\Omega}(m,X):=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{(c)}X^{s}(-2\mathcal{D}(s-m)\log\zeta(s-m))\zeta(s+1-m)\Gamma(s)ds.

The technique is nearly identical to the one we just described except with
\tilde{\vartheta}(u)=(-\log u+i\pi+g(1-u))-(-\log u-i\pi+g(1-u))=2\pi i
and we will end up having

\displaystyle\tilde{\Omega}(m,X)

\displaystyle=-2\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{0}^{\tfrac{c}{\log T}}\tilde{\vartheta}(u)X^{m+1-u}\mathcal{D}(1-u)\zeta(2-u)\Gamma(m+1-u)du

\displaystyle=-\frac{2\mathcal{D}(1)\zeta(2)\Gamma(m+1)X^{m+1}}{\log X}\bigg(1+O\bigg(\frac{1}{\log X}\bigg)\bigg),

where
\mathcal{D}(1)=\mathcal{D}
with
\mathcal{D}=\sum_{j\geq 2}\frac{1}{j}\sum_{p}\frac{1}{p^{j}}=-\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(n)}{n}\log\zeta(n)\approx 0.315718452\ldots
being Fröberg’s constant
[

]

in

\mathsection
6]

(4.8)

\displaystyle\tilde{\tilde{\Omega}}(m,X):=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{(c_{0})}X^{s}(\mathcal{D}(s-m))^{2}\zeta(s+1-m)\Gamma(s)ds\ll X^{c_{0}}.

We now choose

4.8

4.1
) follows. We can close the statement of the lemma by a standard argument. First the case

4.2.
Moment method for the major arcs

We now show that this main term can be obtained by the method of moments and a tailored prime number theorem. We choose to work with the weakest error term of the prime number theorem, i.e. knowing that
\zeta(s)\neq 0
for
\real(s)=1
in order to showcase the fewer resources needed to partially achieve our goal. The proof we present can be adapted to include a more refined error term.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
L>1
,
\lambda>0
,
a,b\geq 0
and
M\geq 0
be given. One has that

(4.9)

\displaystyle\int_{L}^{\infty}e^{-at}t^{\lambda}\frac{\log\log t+M}{(\log t)^{b}}\,dt=\frac{\Gamma(\lambda+1)(\log\log(1/a)+M)}{a^{\lambda+1}(\log(1/a))^{b}}\left(1+O\left(\frac{\log\log(1/a)}{\log(1/a)}\right)\right),

as

.

The proof of this result will be very similar to the proof of Lemma
6.1
which will be shown in Section
6
and thus we postpone it for later.

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
f:[L,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
with
L>1
be a function such that
f(t)=o(t)
as
t\to\infty
.
We then have as
a\to 0
for
a>0

(4.10)

\displaystyle\int_{L}^{\infty}e^{-at}f(t)\frac{\log\log t}{\log t}\,dt=o\left(\frac{\log\log(1/a)}{a^{2}\log(1/a)}\right).

Proof.

The proof is almost the same as the proof of Lemma

.
Indeed, there exists a

such that

for all

.
Thus we can use for the integrals over

.

Now we state and prove a prime number theorem for semiprimes.

Lemma 4.3
.

We have

\displaystyle\pi_{2}^{*}(x):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{1}p_{2}\leq x\\
p_{1},p_{2}\in\mathbb{P}\end{subarray}}1\sim 2x\frac{\log\log x}{\log x}.

as
x\to\infty
.

Proof.

We denote by
\pi(x)=\sum_{p\leq x}1
the usual prime counting function.
Then we get

\displaystyle\pi_{2}^{*}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p_{1}\leq x^{1/2}}\sum_{p_{2}\leq x/p_{1}}1+\sum_{p_{2}\leq x^{1/2}}\sum_{p_{1}\leq x/p_{2}}1-\sum_{p_{1}\leq x^{1/2}}1\sum_{p_{2}\leq x^{1/2}}1

\displaystyle=2\sum_{p\leq x^{1/2}}\pi(x/p)-(\pi(x^{1/2}))^{2}.

Using that
\pi(x)=\frac{x}{\log x}(1+o(1))
and
x^{1/2}\leq x/p\leq x
, we obtain

(4.11)

\displaystyle\pi_{2}^{*}(x)

\displaystyle=2\bigg(\sum_{p\leq x^{1/2}}\frac{x}{p\log(x/p)}\bigg)(1+o(1))+O\left(\frac{x}{(\log x)^{2}}\right).

Mertens’ theorem states that
\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{\log p}{p}=\log x+O(1)
, see
[
24
,
\mathsection
6]
.
Combining this with Abel’s summation formula with
f(t)=\frac{1}{\log(x/t)\log t}
gives

\displaystyle\sum_{p\leq x^{1/2}}\frac{1}{p\log(x/p)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p\leq x^{1/2}}\frac{\log p}{p}f(p)=f(x^{1/2})\bigg(\sum_{p\leq x^{1/2}}\frac{\log p}{p}\bigg)-\int_{2}^{x^{1/2}}f^{\prime}(t)\bigg(\sum_{p=2}^{t}\frac{\log p}{p}\bigg)dt

\displaystyle=\int_{2}^{x^{1/2}}(\log t+O(1))\frac{\log x-2\log t}{t(\log t)^{2}(\log(x/t))^{2}}dt+\frac{2}{\log x}+O\left(\frac{1}{(\log x)^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{2}^{x^{1/2}}\frac{\log x}{t(\log t)(\log(x/t))^{2}}\,dt-2\int_{2}^{x^{1/2}}\frac{1}{t(\log(x/t))^{2}}\,dt

(4.12)

\displaystyle\quad+O\bigg(\int_{2}^{x^{1/2}}\frac{\log x-2\log t}{t(\log t)\log^{2}(x/t)}dt\bigg)+O\left(\frac{1}{\log x}\right).

We now look at the three integrals in (
4.12
) separately.
Since
\frac{1}{2}\log t\leq\log(t/x)\leq\log t
, for the last integral we have

(4.13)

\displaystyle\int_{2}^{x^{1/2}}\frac{\log x-2\log t}{t(\log t)^{2}\log^{2}(x/t)}dt\ll\frac{1}{\log x}\int_{2}^{x^{1/2}}\frac{dt}{t(\log t)^{2}}\ll\frac{1}{(\log x)^{2}}.

Further,

(4.14)

\displaystyle\int_{2}^{x^{1/2}}\frac{dt}{t\log^{2}(x/t)}=\frac{1}{\log x}+O\left(\frac{1}{(\log x)^{2}}\right).

Using that
1/(1-r)^{2}\leq 1+4r
for
|r|\leq 1/2
, we get

\displaystyle\int_{2}^{x^{1/2}}\frac{\log x}{t\log(t)\log^{2}(x/t)}dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\log x}\int_{2}^{x^{1/2}}\frac{dt}{t\log(t)(1-\frac{\log t}{\log x})^{2}}

(4.15)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\log x}\int_{2}^{x^{1/2}}\frac{dt}{t\log t}+4\int_{2}^{x^{1/2}}\frac{dt}{t(\log t)^{2}}=\frac{\log\log x}{\log x}+O\left(\frac{1}{\log x}\right).

Inserting (
4.13
), (
4.14
) and (
4.15
) in to (
4.12
) gives

(4.16)

\displaystyle\sum_{p=2}^{x^{1/2}}\frac{1}{p\log(x/p)}=\frac{\log\log x}{\log x}+O\left(\frac{1}{\log x}\right).

Inserting (
4.16
) into (
4.11
) completes the proof.
∎

Combining (
4.18
) and
4.19
gives

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}\right)^{m}\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{2}}(\rho\operatorname{e}(\alpha))

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j}\sum_{p_{1},p_{2}}\operatorname{e}(jp_{1}p_{2}\alpha)\int_{p_{1}p_{2}}^{\infty}(j^{m}t^{m}-mj^{m-1}t^{m-1}X)\frac{j}{X}e^{-\frac{j}{X}t}\,dt

(4.20)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j}\int_{2^{2}}^{\infty}(j^{m}t^{m}-mj^{m-1}t^{m-1}X)\frac{j}{X}e^{-\frac{j}{X}t}\sum_{p_{1}p_{2}\leq t}\operatorname{e}(jp_{1}p_{2}\alpha)\,dt.

Equipped with these tools we may now prove the main result.

Lemma 4.4
.

We have for all
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
that

\displaystyle\left(\rho\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}\right)^{m}\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{2}}(\rho)=2\frac{\zeta(2)\Gamma(m+1)X^{m+1}\log\log X}{\log X}\left(1+o(1)\right).

Proof.

We can write (
4.20
) as

(4.21)

\displaystyle\left(\rho\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}\right)^{m}\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{2}}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j}\int_{2^{2}}^{\infty}(j^{m}t^{m}-mj^{m-1}t^{m-1}X)\frac{j}{X}e^{-\frac{j}{X}t}\pi_{2}^{*}(t)\,dt.

We first derive an upper bound for integral in (
4.21
).
The variable substitution
u=\frac{j}{X}t
gives

\displaystyle\left|\int_{2^{2}}^{\infty}(j^{m}t^{m}-mj^{m-1}t^{m-1}X)\frac{j}{X}e^{-\frac{j}{X}t}\pi_{2}^{*}(t)\,dt\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{0}^{\infty}((jt)^{m}+mX(jt)^{m-1})\frac{jt}{X}e^{-\frac{j}{X}t}dt

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{X}{j}\int_{0}^{\infty}((Xu)^{m}+mX(Xu)^{m-1})ue^{-u}du

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{X^{m+1}}{j}\int_{0}^{\infty}(u^{m+1}+mu^{m})e^{-u}du

\displaystyle=C\frac{X^{m+1}}{j}(\Gamma(m+2)+m\Gamma(m+1))\ll\frac{X^{m+1}}{j}.

We now split the sum in (
4.21
) into the cases
j>x^{1/2}
and
j\leq x^{1/2}
.
Inserting the above bound and using that
\sum_{j>x^{1/2}}^{\infty}j^{-2}\ll x^{-1/2}
gives

(4.22)

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}\right)^{m}\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{2}}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\leq x^{1/2}}\frac{1}{j}\int_{2^{2}}^{\infty}(j^{m}t^{m}-mj^{m-1}t^{m-1}X)\frac{j}{X}e^{-\frac{j}{X}t}\pi_{2}^{*}(t)\,dt+O(X^{m+1/2}).

Next we compute the integrals in (
4.22
).
Using Lemma
4.1
with
b=c=1
and Corollary
4.2
yields

\displaystyle\int_{2^{2}}^{\infty}j^{m}t^{m}\frac{j}{X}e^{-\frac{j}{X}t}\pi_{2}^{*}(t)dt

\displaystyle=\frac{2j^{m+1}}{X}\int_{2^{2}}^{\infty}t^{m+1}e^{-\frac{j}{X}t}\frac{\log\log t}{\log t}(1+o\left(1\right))dt

\displaystyle=\frac{2j^{m+1}}{X}\frac{\Gamma(m+2)\log\log(X/j)}{(j/X)^{m+2}\log(X/j)}(1+o\left(1\right))

(4.23)

\displaystyle=2\frac{X^{m+1}}{j}\frac{\Gamma(m+2)\log\log(X/j)}{\log(X/j)}(1+o\left(1\right)).

The
o(1)
term in the last two equations has to be interpreted

as
X/j\to\infty
.
Note that this
o(1)
depends only on the ratio
X/j
, but not on
X
and
j
.
Inserting (
4.23
) with
m
and
m-1
into (
4.22
) leads us to

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}\right)^{m}\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{2}}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\leq x^{1/2}}2\frac{X^{m+1}}{j^{2}}\frac{\Gamma(m+2)-m\Gamma(m+1)}{\log(X/j)}\log\log(X/j)(1+o(1))+O(X^{m+1/2})

(4.24)

\displaystyle=2X^{m+1}\Gamma(m+1)\sum_{j\leq x^{1/2}}\frac{1}{j^{2}}\frac{\log\log(X/j)}{\log(X/j)}(1+o(1))+O(X^{m+1/2}).

Using that

, we get

\displaystyle\frac{\log\log(X/j)}{\log(X/j)}=\frac{\log\log X}{\log X}+O\left(\frac{\log\log X}{(\log X)^{2}}\right).

Inserting this into (

) and that

∎

5.
The minor arcs

5.1.
The fundamental estimate for the double Weyl sum

In order to show that every sufficiently large odd number is a sum of three primes in
[
32
]
, Vinogradov studied the Weyl sum
S_{1}(\beta,x):=\sum_{p\leq x}\operatorname{e}(\beta x)
where the sum is taken over primes
p
and
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
. Vinogradov showed that if
|\beta-\frac{a}{q}|\leq\frac{1}{q^{2}}
with
(a,q)=1
, then one has that
S_{1}(\beta,x)
is bounded by
(\tfrac{x}{\sqrt{q}}+x^{\frac{4}{5}}+\sqrt{x}\sqrt{q})(\log 2x)^{3}
. In
[
7
,
\mathsection
25]
, Davenport states that this bound is sharp, even in the special case
\beta=a/q
, but that if the hypothesis is weakened then the corresponding bound will also be weakened. For our purposes, we need to extend the hypothesis by creating a generalization which is weaker but whose special case contains Vinogradov’s bound. Our result, interesting in its own right and useful for other purposes outside the scope of the paper, is as follows.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
. If
a\in\mathbb{Z}
and
q\in\mathbb{N}
,
\Upsilon>0
and

\displaystyle\bigg|\beta-\frac{a}{q}\bigg|\leq\frac{\Upsilon}{q^{2}}\quad\textnormal{with}\quad(a,q)=1,

then one has that

\displaystyle S_{1}(\beta,x)=\sum_{p\leq x}\operatorname{e}(\beta p)\ll\Upsilon\bigg(\frac{x}{\sqrt{q}}+x^{\frac{4}{5}}+\sqrt{x}\sqrt{q}\bigg)(\log x)^{3},

where the sum runs over primes
p
.

Proof.

The technique to prove this relies on Vaughan’s identity, see
[
29
]
and
[
7
,
\mathsection
25]
as well as
[
31
]
. We define the truncated the Dirichlet series

\displaystyle F(s)=\sum_{m\leq U}\Lambda(m)m^{-s},\quad G(s)=\sum_{d\leq V}\mu(d)d^{-s}

and note the identity

(5.1)

\displaystyle-\frac{\zeta^{\prime}}{\zeta}(s)=F(s)-\zeta(s)F(s)G(s)-\zeta^{\prime}(s)G(s)+\bigg(-\frac{\zeta^{\prime}}{\zeta}(s)-F(s)\bigg)(1-\zeta(s)G(s)),

valid for
\sigma>1
. The Dirichlet coefficients of the four functions on the right-hand side of (
5.1
) can be calculated and we see that

\displaystyle\Lambda(n)=a_{1}(n)+a_{2}(n)+a_{3}(n)+a_{4}(n),

where the first two coefficients are given by

\displaystyle a_{1}(n)=\begin{cases}\Lambda(n)\quad&\mbox{if $n\leq U$},\\
0\quad&\mbox{if $n>U$},\end{cases}\quad a_{2}(n)=-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}mdr=n\\
m\leq U\\
d\leq V\end{subarray}}\Lambda(m)\mu(d),

and the last two coefficients are

\displaystyle a_{3}(n)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}hd=n\\
d\leq V\end{subarray}}\mu(d)\log h,\quad\textnormal{and}\quad a_{4}(n)=-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}mk=n\\
m>U\\
k>1\end{subarray}}\Lambda(m)\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|k\\
d\leq V\end{subarray}}\mu(d)\bigg).

From this we can construct the sum

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq N}f(n)\Lambda(n)=S_{1}+S_{2}+S_{3}+S_{4}\quad\textnormal{with}\quad S_{i}=\sum_{n\leq N}f(n)a_{i}(n)\quad\textnormal{for}\quad i=1,2,3,4.

This can be shown to satisfy

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq N}f(n)\Lambda(n)

\displaystyle\ll U+(\log N)\sum_{t\leq UV}\max_{w}\bigg|\sum_{w\leq r\leq N/t}f(rt)\bigg|

\displaystyle\quad+N^{\frac{1}{2}}(\log N)^{3}\max_{U\leq M\leq N/V}\max_{V\leq j\leq N/M}\bigg(\sum_{v<k\leq N/M}\bigg|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}M<n\leq 2M\\
m\leq N/k\\
m\leq N/j\end{subarray}}f(mj)\overline{f(mk)}\bigg|\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Only the last term represents a difficulty from the known case in which
\Upsilon=1
. Suppose that
a,q
and
\Upsilon
are as in (
5.1
). From this we can write

(5.2)

\displaystyle\gamma=\beta-a/q\implies\beta=\gamma+a/q\implies|\gamma|\leq\frac{\Upsilon}{q^{2}}.

Recall that for positive integers
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
one has

\displaystyle\sum_{n=N_{1}}^{N_{2}}\operatorname{e}(n\beta)=\frac{\operatorname{e}((N_{2}+1)\beta)-\operatorname{e}(N_{1}\beta)}{\operatorname{e}(\beta)-1}\ll\min\bigg(N_{2}-N_{1},\frac{1}{||\beta||}\bigg),

where
||x||
denotes the distance from
x
to the nearest integer. Hence

(5.3)

\displaystyle\sum_{t\leq T}\max_{w}\bigg|\sum_{w\leq r\leq N/t}\operatorname{e}(rt\beta)\bigg|\ll\sum_{t\leq T}\min\bigg(\frac{N}{t},\frac{1}{||t\beta||}\bigg).

For now we assume that the right-hand side of (
5.3
) is

(5.4)

\displaystyle\ll\Upsilon\bigg(\frac{N}{q}+T+q\bigg)\log(2qT),

for
\beta
satisfying (
5.2
), a bound that we will prove shortly. In that case, following
[
7
,
\mathsection
25]
we can deduce that
S_{1}(\beta)
satisfies

\displaystyle S_{1}(\beta)

\displaystyle\ll U+\Upsilon\bigg(\frac{N}{q}+UV+q\bigg)(\log 2qN)^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+N^{\frac{1}{2}}(\log N)^{3}\max_{U\leq M\leq N/V}\max_{V<j\leq N/M}\bigg(\sum_{V<k\leq N/M}\min\bigg(M,\frac{\Upsilon}{||(k-j)\beta||}\bigg)\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

The last term of the above equation is

\displaystyle\ll N^{\frac{1}{2}}(\log N)^{3}\max_{U\leq M\leq N/V}\bigg(M+\sum_{1<m\leq N/M}\min\bigg(\frac{N}{m},\frac{\Upsilon}{||m\beta||}\bigg)\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

By employing (
5.4
) this expression is seen to be

\displaystyle\ll N^{\frac{1}{2}}(\log N)^{3}\max_{U\leq M\leq N/V}\bigg(M+\Upsilon\bigg(\frac{N}{M}+\frac{N}{q}+q\bigg)\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}(\log qN)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\ll\Upsilon(NV^{-1/2}+NU^{-1/2}+Nq^{-1/2}+N^{\frac{1}{2}}q^{\frac{1}{2}})(\log qN)^{3}.

Hence, collecting all the terms we arrive at

\displaystyle S_{1}(\beta)\ll\Upsilon(UV+q+NU^{-1/2}+NV^{-1/2}+Nq^{-1/2}+N^{\frac{1}{2}}q^{\frac{1}{2}})(\log qN)^{3}.

If
q>N
then the lemma holds trivially. We then assume that
q\leq N
and obtain the statement of the lemma by taking
U=V=N^{\frac{1}{2}}
.

The last item in the proof is to show that indeed (
5.4
) holds. Take
t=hq+r
with
1\leq r\leq q
and put
\gamma=\beta-a/q
. In that case

\displaystyle\sum_{t\leq T}\min\bigg(\frac{N}{t},\frac{\Upsilon}{||t\beta||}\bigg)\ll\sum_{0\leq h\leq T/q}\sum_{r=1}^{q}\min\bigg(\frac{N}{hq+r},\Upsilon\bigg|\bigg|ra/q+hq\gamma+r\gamma\bigg|\bigg|^{-1}\bigg).

First we consider
h=0
and
1\leq r\leq\tfrac{q}{2\Upsilon}
. For such
r
we have
|r\gamma|\leq\frac{1}{2q}
, so that the contribution from these terms is

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{1\leq r\leq\frac{q}{2\Upsilon}}\frac{1}{||\tfrac{ra}{q}||-\tfrac{1}{2q}}\ll q\log q.

We have at most
O(\Upsilon)
such sums with
r\in[1,q]
, and hence the contribution of all
r
in the interval
[1,q]
is
O(\Upsilon q\log q)
.
For all remaining terms we have
hq+r\gg(h+1)q
. Let
h\in\mathbb{Z}
be given. Then for any
b\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
the interval
J=[b/q,(b+1)/q]
has length
1/q
. Let
J_{1}
and
J_{2}
be subintervals of
[b/q,(b+1+\Upsilon)/q]
such that their lengths
|J_{1}|,|J_{2}|
satisfy
|J_{1}|,|J_{2}|\leq\Upsilon/q
and such that
\tfrac{ra}{q}\in J_{1}\cup J\cup J_{2}
. There are at most
2\Upsilon+2
values of
r
with
1\leq r\leq q
for which

\displaystyle\frac{ra}{q}+hq\gamma+r\gamma\in[b/q,(b+1)q]\;\operatorname{mod}1\implies\frac{ra}{q}+r\gamma\in[b/q-h\gamma q,(b+1)/q-h\gamma q]\;\operatorname{mod}1.

Therefore, we end up with

\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq h\leq T}\sum_{r=1}^{q}\min\bigg(\frac{N}{(h+1)q},\Upsilon\bigg|\bigg|\frac{ra}{q}+hq\gamma+r\gamma\bigg|\bigg|^{-1}\bigg)

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{0\leq h\leq T/q}\bigg(\frac{N}{(h+1)q}+\Upsilon q\log 2q\bigg)

\displaystyle\ll\Upsilon\bigg(\frac{N}{q}+T+q\bigg)\log 2qT,

thereby completing the final ingredient of the proposition.
∎

Before proceeding to the estimate on the Weyl sum over two primes, namely
\sum_{p_{1}p_{2}\leq x}\operatorname{e}(\alpha p_{1}p_{2})
, we need an auxiliary technical lemma.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
F
,
G_{1}
,
G_{2}
and
G_{3}
be continuous, real valued functions on
(0,\infty)
such that F is strictly decreasing and all
G_{i}
are increasing.
Further, suppose that for
i=1,2,3

(5.5)

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}F(x)=\lim_{x\to 0}G_{i}(x)=0\ \text{ and }\ \lim_{x\to 0}F(x)=\lim_{x\to\infty}G_{i}(x)=\infty.

Set
G(x):=\max\{G_{1}(x),G_{2}(x),G_{3}(x)\}
and
H(x):=\max\{F(x),G(x)\}
.
We then have

(5.6)

\displaystyle\min_{x\in(0,\infty)}H(x)=F(\min\{M_{1},M_{2},M_{3}\})=G(\min\{M_{1},M_{2},M_{3}\}),

where
M_{i}
is the solution of the equation
F(M_{i})=G_{i}(M_{i})
for
i=1,2,3
.

The assumptions on
F
,
G_{1}
,
G_{2}
and
G_{3}
imply that the solutions
M_{i}
exist and are unique.
Furthermore, the lemma also holds for more than three functions
G_{i}
or just two of them.
We have formulated Lemma
5.1
with three
G_{i}
since we need it in this form below.

Proof of Lemma
5.1
.

We can assume that
M_{1}\leq M_{2}\leq M_{3}
. Otherwise we just relabel the functions
G_{i}
.
First, we show that

(5.7)

\displaystyle G_{1}(M_{1})\geq G_{2}(M_{2})\geq G_{2}(M_{1}).

Since
G_{2}
is increasing and
M_{2}\geq M_{1}
, the second inequality follows immediately.
To show the first inequality, we use the definition of
M_{i}
and that
F
is decreasing.
We have

\displaystyle G_{1}(M_{1})=F(M_{1})\geq F(M_{2})=G_{2}(M_{2}).

Thus (
5.7
) holds and therefore
G_{1}(M_{1})\geq G_{2}(M_{1})
.
Similarly
G_{1}(M_{1})\geq G_{3}(M_{1})
. Thus

(5.8)

\displaystyle F(M_{1})=G_{1}(M_{1})=\max\{G_{1}(M_{1}),G_{2}(M_{1}),G_{3}(M_{1})\}=G(M_{1})=H(M_{1}).

Moreover, since
F
is decreasing and
G_{1}
is increasing, we get

(5.9)

\displaystyle H(M_{1})\leq\begin{cases}F(x)&\text{for }x\leq M_{1},\\
G_{1}(x)&\text{for }x\geq M_{1}.\end{cases}

This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and
a\in\mathbb{Z}
as well as
q\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\displaystyle\bigg|\alpha-\frac{a}{q}\bigg|\leq\frac{1}{q^{2}}\quad\textnormal{with}\quad(a,q)=1.

For any complex numbers
\xi_{m},\eta_{n}
with
|\xi_{m}|\leq 1
and
|\eta_{n}|\leq 1
we have

\displaystyle\operatorname*{\sum\sum}_{\begin{subarray}{c}mn\leq x\\
m>M\\
n>N\end{subarray}}\xi_{m}\eta_{n}\operatorname{e}(\alpha mn)\ll\bigg(\frac{x}{M}+\frac{x}{N}+\frac{x}{q}+q\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}x^{\frac{1}{2}}(\log x)^{2}.

Proof.

See Lemma 13.8 from
[
16

6.
The non-principal major arcs

Our first result is understanding the behavior of the following integral.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
\gamma=\gamma_{1}+i\gamma_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
with
\gamma_{1}>0
with
\gamma_{2}\ll\gamma_{1}(\log(1/\gamma_{1}))^{A}
for some
A>0
.
We then have as
\gamma_{1}\to 0

\displaystyle\int_{2}^{\infty}\frac{t\log\log t}{\log t}\exp(-\gamma t)dt=\frac{\log\log(1/\gamma_{1})}{\gamma^{2}\log(1/\gamma_{1})}+O\left(\frac{\log\log(1/\gamma_{1})}{\gamma_{1}^{2}\log^{2}(1/\gamma_{1})}\right).

Proof.

We split the interval
[2,\infty]
into intervals
[2,d],[d,u]
and
[u,\infty]
with

\displaystyle d=\frac{1}{\gamma_{1}(\log(1/\gamma_{1}))^{A+1}}\quad\text{and}\quad u=\frac{(\log(1/\gamma_{1}))^{2}}{\gamma_{1}}.

We now have that

\displaystyle\frac{\log\log t}{\log t}\geq 0\text{ for }t\geq e\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\log\log t}{\log t}=0.

Thus there exist a constant
C>0
such that
|\frac{\log\log t}{\log t}|\leq C
for all
t\geq 2
. Since
\gamma_{1}>0
and
|\exp(z)|=\exp(\real(z))
, the integral over
[2,d]
can be estimated as

\displaystyle\left|\int_{2}^{d}\frac{t\log\log t}{\log t}\exp(-\gamma t)dt\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{2}^{d}t\exp(-\gamma_{1}t)dt\leq C\int_{2}^{d}t\,dt\ll\frac{1}{\gamma_{1}^{2}(\log(1/\gamma_{1}))^{2A+2}}.

For the integral over
[u,\infty]
, we use that
\frac{\gamma_{1}}{2}t\leq\exp(\frac{\gamma_{1}}{2}t)
for
t\geq 0
. Furthermore
\frac{\log\log t}{\log t}
is monotonically decaying for
t
large enough.
This gives

\displaystyle\left|\int_{u}^{\infty}\frac{t\log\log t}{\log t}\exp(-\gamma t)dt\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{\log\log u}{\log u}\frac{2}{\gamma_{1}}\int_{u}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\gamma_{1}}{2}t\right)\exp(-\gamma_{1}t)dt

\displaystyle=\frac{C\log\log u}{\log u}\frac{2}{\gamma_{1}}\int_{u}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\frac{\gamma_{1}}{2}t\right)dt

\displaystyle\ll\frac{\log\log(1/\gamma_{1})}{\gamma_{1}^{2}(\log(1/\gamma_{1}))^{3}}.

We have used on the last line that
\gamma_{1}u=\log^{2}(1/\gamma_{1})
.
For the computation of the remaining integral, we use the observation that for
t\in[d,u]
one has that

\displaystyle\frac{\log\log t}{\log t}=\frac{\log\left(\log(1/\gamma_{1})+\log(\gamma_{1}t)\right)}{\log(1/\gamma_{1})+\log(\gamma_{1}t)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\log\log(1/\gamma_{1})+\log(1+\frac{\log(\gamma_{1}t)}{\log(1/\gamma_{1})})}{\log(1/\gamma_{1})(1-\frac{\log\gamma_{1}t}{\log(1/\gamma_{1})})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\log\log(1/\gamma_{1})}{\log(1/\gamma_{1})}+O\left(\frac{\log\log(1/\gamma_{1})^{2}}{\log(1/\gamma_{1})^{2}}\right).

This implies together with a similar bound as above that

\displaystyle\int_{d}^{u}\frac{t\log\log t}{\log t}\exp(-\gamma t)dt

\displaystyle=\frac{\log\log(1/\gamma_{1})}{\log(1/\gamma_{1})}\int_{d}^{u}t\exp(-\gamma t)dt+O\left(\frac{\log\log(1/\gamma_{1})^{2}}{\log(1/\gamma_{1})^{2}}\int_{d}^{u}t\exp(-\gamma_{1}t)dt\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\log\log(1/\gamma_{1})}{\log(1/\gamma_{1})}\int_{d}^{u}t\exp(-\gamma t)dt+O\left(\frac{\log\log(1/\gamma_{1})^{2}}{\gamma_{1}^{2}\log(1/\gamma_{1})^{2}}\right).

Since
\gamma d=\log^{-1}(1/\gamma_{1})
by construction, we get

\displaystyle\int_{d}^{u}t\exp(-\gamma t)dt

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(\gamma d+1\right)e^{-\gamma d}}{\gamma^{2}}-\frac{\left(\gamma u+1\right)e^{-\gamma u}}{\gamma^{2}}

(6.1)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}+O\left(\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}\log(1/\gamma_{1})}\right)+O\left(\frac{\left(\gamma u+1\right)e^{-\gamma_{1}u}}{\gamma^{2}}\right).

This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

We can apply our two previous lemmas to find the asymptotic of an infinite exponential sum over a product of primes that satisfies the congruence of our previous lemma.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
\gamma=\gamma_{1}+i\gamma_{2}
with
\gamma_{1}>0
and
\gamma_{2}\ll\gamma_{1}(\log(1/\gamma_{1}))^{A}
for some
A>0
.
Moreover let
q\in\mathbb{N}
with
q\ll(\log(1/\gamma))^{A}
and define

\displaystyle U(\gamma,\ell,q):=\sum_{p_{1}p_{2}\equiv\ell\bmod q}\exp(-p_{1}p_{2}\gamma),

with
(\ell,q)=1
. Then one has that

\displaystyle U(\gamma,\ell,q)=\frac{2}{\varphi(q)}\frac{\log\log(1/\gamma)+M}{\gamma\log(1/\gamma)}+O\bigg(\frac{1}{\varphi(q)\gamma_{1}}\bigg(\frac{\log\log(1/\gamma_{1})}{\log(1/\gamma_{1})}\bigg)^{2}\bigg),

where
\varphi(q)
is the Euler totient function.

Proof.

First, we apply Abel’s summation to
U(\gamma,\ell,q)
.
For this, we define
A(t)=\sum_{n\leq t}a_{n}
with

(6.5)

\displaystyle a_{n}=\begin{cases}2,\quad&\mbox{if $n=p_{1}p_{2}$ with $p_{1}\neq p_{2}$ and $n\equiv\ell\operatorname{mod}q$,}\\
1,\quad&\mbox{if $n=p^{2}$ and $n\equiv\ell\operatorname{mod}q$,}\\
0,\quad&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Note that this
A(t)
agrees with
A(t)
in Theorem
6.1
.
Moreover, set
f(n)=\exp(-\gamma n)
. We get

(6.6)

\displaystyle U(\gamma,\ell,q)=\sum_{n\leq t}a_{n}f(n)=A(x)f(x)\big|_{x=2}^{\infty}-\int_{2}^{\infty}A(t)f^{\prime}(t)dt=\gamma\int_{2}^{\infty}A(t)\exp(-\gamma t)dt.

We used in the last equality that
A(t)=0
for
t\leq 2
and
A(t)\leq 2t
otherwise.
Inserting Theorem
6.1
, we see that

\displaystyle U(\gamma,\ell,q)=\gamma\int_{2}^{\infty}D(t)\exp(-\gamma t)dt+O\left(\gamma\int_{2}^{\infty}t(\log t)^{-C}\exp(-\gamma t)dt\right)

where
D
is given by

\displaystyle D(t)=\,

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\varphi(q)}\int_{2}^{\sqrt{t}}\big(\log\log u+M+O((\log u)^{-C})\big)\left(\frac{t}{u\log(t/u)}-\operatorname{Li}(t/u)\right)du

\displaystyle-\frac{\operatorname{Li}^{2}(\sqrt{t})}{\varphi(q)}+\frac{2\sqrt{t}}{\varphi(q)}\operatorname{Li}(\sqrt{t})\left({\log\log t-\log 2+M}\right).

Using Lemma
4.1
with
C>2A+2
, the assumption on
\gamma_{2}
and that
\varphi(q)\leq q
, we get

\displaystyle\left|\gamma\int_{2}^{\infty}t(\log t)^{-C}\exp(-\gamma t)\,dt\right|

\displaystyle\leq|\gamma|\int_{2}^{\infty}t(\log t)^{-C}\exp(-\gamma_{1}t)\,dt\ll|\gamma|\frac{1}{\gamma_{1}^{2}(\log(1/\gamma_{1}))^{C}}

\displaystyle\ll\frac{2\gamma_{1}(\log(1/\gamma_{1}))^{A}}{\gamma_{1}^{2}(\log(1/\gamma_{1}))^{2A+2}}\ll\frac{1}{\varphi(q)\gamma_{1}(\log(1/\gamma_{1}))^{2}}.

This implies that

\displaystyle U(\gamma,\ell,q)=\gamma\int_{2}^{\infty}D(t)\exp(-\gamma t)dt+O\left(\frac{1}{\varphi(q)\gamma_{1}(\log(1/\gamma_{1}))^{2}}\right).

Since
\operatorname{Li}(t)=t/\log t+O(t/(\log t)^{2})
, we have

\displaystyle D(t)\ll\int_{2}^{\sqrt{t}}\left(\log\log t+M+O((\log u)^{-C})\right)\frac{tdu}{u(\log(t/u))^{2}}+\frac{t\log\log t}{\log t}\ll\frac{t\log\log t}{\log t}

Thus, we get with partial integration that

\displaystyle\gamma\int_{2}^{\infty}D(t)\exp(-\gamma t)dt=\int_{2}^{\infty}D^{\prime}(t)\exp(-\gamma t)dt+O(1).

For the derivative of
D
we have

\displaystyle D^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\varphi(q)}\int_{2}^{\sqrt{t}}\left(\log\log u+M+O((\log u)^{-C})\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\frac{t}{u\log(t/u)}-\operatorname{Li}(t/u)\right)du

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{2}{\varphi(q)2\sqrt{t}}\left(\log\log\sqrt{t}+M+O((\log\sqrt{t})^{-C}))\right)\left(\frac{\sqrt{t}}{\log\sqrt{t}}-\operatorname{Li}(\sqrt{t})\right)-\frac{2\operatorname{Li}(\sqrt{t})}{\varphi(q)\sqrt{t}\log t}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\frac{\operatorname{Li}(\sqrt{t})(\log\log t-\log 2+M)}{\sqrt{t}}+\frac{2}{\varphi(q)}\frac{\operatorname{Li}(\sqrt{t})}{\sqrt{t}\log t}+\frac{2}{\varphi(q)}\frac{\log\log t-\log 2+M}{\log t}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{\varphi(q)}\int_{2}^{\sqrt{t}}\frac{\log\log u+M+O((\log u)^{-C})}{u\log^{2}(t/u)}du

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{4}{\varphi(q)}\frac{\log\log t-\log 2+M}{\log t}+O\left(\frac{1}{\varphi(q)(\log t)^{C+2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\varphi(q)}\left(\frac{\log(t/2)\log\log(t/2)+\log 2(\log\log 2)}{\log(t/2)\log t}-\frac{\log\log\sqrt{t}}{\log\sqrt{t}}+\frac{M}{\log(t/2)}-\frac{M}{\log\sqrt{t}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{4}{\varphi(q)}\frac{\log\log t-\log 2+M}{\log t}+O\left(\frac{\log\log t}{\varphi(q)(\log t)^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\varphi(q)}\frac{\log\log t+M}{\log t}+O\left(\frac{\log\log t}{\varphi(q)(\log t)^{2}}\right).

We thus arrive at

\displaystyle U(\gamma,\ell,q)=\int_{2}^{\infty}\left(\frac{2}{\varphi(q)}\frac{\log\log t+M}{\log t}+O\left(\frac{\log\log t}{\varphi(q)(\log t)^{2}}\right)\right)e^{-\gamma t}dt+O\left(\frac{1}{\varphi(q)\gamma_{1}(\log(1/\gamma_{1}))^{2}}\right).

Applying a small variation of Lemma
6.1
finally completes the proof. ∎

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{2}}(\rho\operatorname{e}(\alpha))=2\zeta(2)\frac{X\log\log X}{(1-2\pi i\alpha X)\log X}+O\bigg(\frac{X\log\log\log\log X}{\log X}\bigg).

Proof.

We have

is equivalent to

.
Thus can use Lemma
6.3
with
q=1
,

.
We have in this case

Thus we obtain

\displaystyle=2X\frac{\log\log X}{(1-2\pi i\alpha X)\log X}\sum_{j\leq\sqrt{X}}\frac{1}{j^{2}}+O\bigg(\frac{X\log\log\log\log X}{\log X}\bigg).

Inserting

∎

The previous results from this section now allow us to conclude that the non-principal major arcs will not contribute to the main term and therefore will be absorbed in the error term.

Theorem 7.1

(7.1)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{p}_{\lambda,\mu}(n)=\frac{\rho^{-n}\Psi_{\lambda,\mu}(\rho)}{(2\pi\Phi_{\lambda,\mu,(2)}(\rho))^{\frac{1}{2}}}(1+O(n^{-\frac{1}{6}}))

Proof.

Recall that

\displaystyle\mathfrak{p}_{\lambda,\mu}(n)=\rho^{-n}\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\Psi_{\lambda,\mu}(\rho\operatorname{e}(\alpha))\operatorname{e}(-n\alpha)d\alpha=\rho^{-n}\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\exp(\Phi_{\lambda,\mu}(\rho\operatorname{e}(\alpha))\operatorname{e}(-n\alpha))d\alpha.

\displaystyle\left(\rho\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}\right)^{m}\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{2}}(\rho)=2\frac{\zeta(2)\Gamma(m+1)X^{m+1}}{\log X}(M+\log\log X)\bigg(1+O\bigg(\frac{1}{\log X}\bigg)\bigg).

Furthermore, it was shown in

(7.3)

\displaystyle\left(\rho\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}\right)^{m}\Phi_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(\rho)=\frac{\Gamma(m+1/2)\zeta(3/2)X^{m+1/2}}{\log X}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{\log X}\right)\right).

Thus, for any

and

sufficiently large, Lemma
5.3
and Lemma
6.6
imply

(7.4)

\displaystyle\real\big(\Phi_{\lambda,\mu}(\rho\operatorname{e}(\alpha))\big)

\displaystyle=\lambda\real\big(\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{2}}(\rho\operatorname{e}(\alpha))\big)+\mu\real\big(\Phi_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(\rho\operatorname{e}(\alpha))\big)\leq\frac{3}{4}\lambda\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{2}}(\rho)+|\mu|\Phi_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(\rho)

Further, (
4.1
) implies with

that

.
Thus we get

\displaystyle\left|\int_{[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]\setminus\mathfrak{M}(1,0)}\exp(\Phi_{\lambda,\mu}(\rho\operatorname{e}(\alpha))\operatorname{e}(-n\alpha))d\alpha\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]\setminus\mathfrak{M}(1,0)}\exp\big(\real(\Phi_{\lambda,\mu}(\rho\operatorname{e}(\alpha)))\big)d\alpha

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(\frac{5}{6}\Phi_{\lambda,\mu}(\rho)\right)=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{6}\Phi_{\lambda,\mu}(\rho)\right)\exp\left(\Phi_{\lambda,\mu}(\rho)\right)

\displaystyle\ll\left(\Phi_{\lambda,\mu}(\rho)\right)^{-C}\exp\left(\Phi_{\lambda,\mu}(\rho)\right)\ll n^{-B}\exp\left(\Phi_{\lambda,\mu}(\rho)\right),

where

can be chosen arbitrarily large.
Therefore

(7.6)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{p}_{\lambda,\mu}(n)=\rho^{-n}\int_{\mathfrak{M}(1,0)}\exp(\Phi_{\lambda,\mu}(\rho\operatorname{e}(\alpha))\operatorname{e}(-n\alpha))d\alpha+O(\rho^{-n}\Psi_{\lambda,\mu}(\rho)n^{-B})

for any constant
B\geq 1
.
Recall,
\mathfrak{M}(1,0)=\{\alpha\in[-1/2,1/2];|\alpha|\leq X^{-1}(\log X)^{A}\}
with
A>18
.
We now spilt
\mathfrak{M}(1,0)
into the three regions

\displaystyle I_{1}=\{\alpha\in\mathfrak{M}(1,0);\,|\alpha|\leq\eta\},\ I_{2}=\{\alpha\in\mathfrak{M}(1,0);\,\eta<|\alpha|\leq\beta\}\ \text{ and }\ I_{3}=\{\alpha\in\mathfrak{M}(1,0);\,\beta<|\alpha|\}

with

and
\beta=(9\pi X)^{-1}
.
Next we show that the integrals over
I_{2}
and
I_{3}
are of lower order.
For
I_{3}
we use Corollary
6.5
and for
I_{2}
the Taylor approximation of
\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{2}}(\rho\operatorname{e}(\alpha))
at
\alpha=0
.
We begin with
I_{3}
. Using that

for

, we obtain for
\alpha\in I_{3}
that

\displaystyle\real\left(\frac{1}{1+2\pi i\alpha X}\right)=\frac{1}{1+4\pi^{2}\alpha^{2}X^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{1+4\pi^{2}\beta^{2}X^{2}}\leq 1-2\pi^{2}\beta^{2}X^{2}=\frac{79}{81}.

Combining this computation with Corollary
6.5
and Theorem
4.1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\real\big(\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{2}}(\rho\operatorname{e}(\alpha))\big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{79}{81}\left(2X\zeta(2)\frac{\log\log X}{\log X}\right)+O\bigg(\frac{X\log\log\log\log X}{\log X}\bigg)\leq\frac{80}{81}\Phi_{\mathbb{P}_{2}}(\rho)

for
X
large enough.
To show this, we used
\log\log\log\log X=o(\log\log X)
and thus for
X

\frac{X\log\log\log\log X}{\log X}\leq\frac{1}{81}\frac{\log\log X}{\log X}.

Combining this with the same argument as in (
7.5
), we deduce immediately that for
\alpha\in I_{3}

\displaystyle\real\big(\Phi_{\lambda,\mu}(\rho\operatorname{e}(\alpha))\big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{161}{162}\Phi_{\lambda,\mu}(\rho).

We can now use exactly same argument as above to show that the integral over
I_{3}
is
\ll\rho^{-n}\Psi_{\mathbb{P}_{2}}(\rho)n^{-B}
with

arbitrary and thus of lower order.
As next we look at the integral over
I_{2}
.
We use here the Taylor approximation of

6.5

We have

(7.7)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\lambda,\mu}(\rho\operatorname{e}(\alpha))

\displaystyle=\Phi_{\lambda,\mu}(\rho)+2\pi i\alpha a_{n}-2\pi^{2}\alpha^{2}b_{n}+4\pi^{3}R(\rho,\alpha)

Equations (

\displaystyle\eta=X^{-\frac{17}{12}}\asymp\left(\frac{n\log n}{\log\log n}\right)^{-\frac{17}{12}},\quad b_{n}\sim 2\left(\frac{n}{\lambda}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}\bigg(\frac{\log n}{4\zeta(2)(\log\log n)}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\ \text{ and }\ c_{n}\ll n^{2}\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}.

where the constants are now identified to be

\displaystyle\mathfrak{c}_{1}=\frac{(4\zeta(2))^{\frac{1}{4}}}{2\sqrt{\pi}\lambda^{\frac{3}{4}}},\quad\mathfrak{c}_{2}=M-\log 2,\quad\textnormal{and}\quad\mathfrak{c}_{3}=\frac{\lambda+\lambda^{-1}}{\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}}(4\zeta(2))^{\frac{1}{2}},

9.
Conclusion and future work

So far we have considered only two primes in different setups. A natural question to ask is how to go beyond semiprimes into powerful almost-primes or smooth numbers.

Let
m
be a positive integer with canonical decomposition
m=p_{1}^{m_{1}}p_{2}^{m_{2}}\cdots p_{r}^{m_{r}}
where
p_{k}\in\mathbb{P}
for
k=1,\cdots,r
and where
m_{1},m_{2},\cdots,m_{r}
are positive integers. Now let
\mathcal{M}=\{p_{1}^{m_{1}}p_{2}^{m_{2}}\cdots p_{r}^{m_{r}}:p_{k}\in\mathbb{P}\textnormal{ for }k=1,\cdots,r\}
denote the set of
m
-powerful
r
-almost primes. In this situation,
\mathfrak{p}_{\mathcal{M}}(n)
denotes the partitions of an integer
n
in terms of a canonical decomposition of a desired integer
m
. If
m=p^{k}
with
p\in\mathbb{P}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, then this corresponds to the case studied by Gafni
[
12
]
; and if
k=1
, then this corresponds to the case studied by Vaughan
[
28
]
. Moreover, if
r=2
and
m_{1}=m_{2}=1
, then this corresponds to the work presented in this paper. Within this framework we have a substantially richer arithmetical structure and we may study questions such as the partitions into integers that are products of, say, three primes or squares of two primes or the product of a prime and the cube of another prime.

Another direction is to manufacture prime zeta functions such as almost-prime zeta functions. For instance, we could define the prime zeta function

\displaystyle\zeta_{\mathbb{P}}(k,s):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\geq 1\\
\Omega(n)=k\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{n^{s}},

where
\Omega(n)
is the total number of prime factors of
n
. Clearly when
k=1
, this reduces to the prime zeta function, i.e.
\zeta_{\mathbb{P}}(1,s)=\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s)
. However, using Newton’s identities one observes that

\displaystyle\zeta_{\mathbb{P}}(2,s)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s)^{2}+\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(2s))

\displaystyle\zeta_{\mathbb{P}}(3,s)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{6}(\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s)^{3}+\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s)\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(2s)+2\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(3s))

\displaystyle\zeta_{\mathbb{P}}(4,s)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{24}(\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s)^{4}+6\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s)^{2}\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(2s)+3\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(2s)^{2}+8\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s)\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(3s)+6\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(4s)),

and in general for
n\in\mathbb{N}
one has

\displaystyle\zeta_{\mathbb{P}}(n,s)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1}+2k_{2}+\cdots+nk_{n}=n\\
k_{1},k_{2},\cdots,k_{n}\geq 0\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(is)^{k_{i}}}{k_{i}!i^{k_{i}}},

effectively making products of powers of
\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(ks)
the main component behind the arithmetic of the partitions associated with
\zeta_{\mathbb{P}}(n,s)
. Therefore if we choose
\zeta_{\mathcal{A},w}(s)=\zeta_{\mathbb{P}}(n,s)
, then the analysis of the partitions is essentially reduced to choosing
\zeta_{\mathcal{A},w}(s)=\prod_{k=1}^{n}\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(ks)^{j}
, again with the choice
w(a)=1
for all
a
.
