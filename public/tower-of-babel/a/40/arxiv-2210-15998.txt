Spatially Random Disorder in Unitary Fermion System in ( 4 − ϵ ) (4-\epsilon) -Dimensions and Effective Action at Finite Temperature
Rajesh Kumar Gupta,, Meenu

Abstract
Non-relativistic conformal field theory is significant to understand various aspects of an ultra-cold system. In this paper, we study a non-relativistic system of two-component fermions interacting with a complex boson with Yukawa-like interactions near d = 4 d=4 -spatial dimensions in the presence of a quenched disorder. The homogeneous theory flows to an interacting fixed point describing a unitary fermion system. In the presence of the disorder, we find that the system has an interesting phase structure in the space of the coupling constants and exhibits an interacting disorder fixed point in ϵ \epsilon -expansion. The correlation function obeys Lifshitz scaling behaviour at the disorder fixed point with the anisotropic exponent being z = 2 + γ E z=2+\gamma_{E} . We also study the disorder system at finite temperature and compute the leading contribution to the 1PI effective action.

Introduction

Generically, a system of study is pure. A pure system is homogeneous in nature, and the Lagrangian description of such systems involves interactions that enjoy translational invariance. In contrast, most often, a real physical system comes with impurities. These impurities are localized around a point or along a
d
-dimensional surface, breaking the translational invariance of the underlying system.
Such systems exhibit interesting and sometimes new physical phenomena such as Anderson localization.
These systems are of great interest to condensed matter and statistical physicists, and to study the effect of the impurities on the thermodynamic and critical properties of the system.

Random impurities could arise in many different ways
Cardy:1996xt
, e.g. a pure system of magnetic ions doped with a diamagnetic atom whose location is random or the Ising model with some random sites left vacant. Depending on the disorder configurations of the underlying system, the impurities could be dynamical or non-dynamical. In other words, while doing statistical mechanics of the impure system, one may or may not need to integrate over the degrees of freedom associated with impurities. Random impurities of dynamical nature are called annealed disorders, while non-dynamical random impurities are called quenched disorders.

As quenched disorder breaks the translational invariance of the system, we can treat these impurities in field theory language by introducing terms in the Lagrangian where the disorder field couples to one or more operators. These couplings are coordinate-dependent and, since these are quenched, destroy the translational symmetry of the original pure system.
One can then compute various thermodynamic quantities like free energy by taking an average over the ensemble of the system with disorder taking values from a probability distribution. It gives quenched average values of the thermodynamic quantities. From the technical perspective, there is also an advantage of averaging. As we will see, it restores the translational invariance of the system, provided the probability distribution for the disorder is also translational invariant.

The field theory analysis of the disorder is often performed using the replica trick, which we will review below. An interesting situation occurs when the system flows to an interacting fixed point with non-zero values of the disorder strength. A characteristic feature of such fixed points in the quantum disorder case is the emergence of Lifshitz scaling,
t\rightarrow\lambda^{z}t
. Therefore, the physics at the fixed point is generally described by a Lifshitz invariant field theory. Quenched average correlation functions obey the generalized Callan-Symanzik equation, and their scaling behaviour at the fixed point receives anomalous contributions from the dynamical exponent,
z

Motivated by these analyses, we would like to investigate the effect of the quantum disorder in the non-relativistic system. We think the investigation might be useful for understanding the critical behaviour of the ultra-cold system in the presence of a disorder.

A system with quench disorder is challenging to solve. The difficulty arises because the averaging is done not at the level of the partition function but at the level of the thermodynamic quantity of interests. This difficulty can be overcome using the replica trick as we now briefly review
Aharony:2018mjm
.
Suppose we have a pure system described by an action
S_{0}
. The system is then doped with non-dynamical impurities, which we assume to be described by a disorder field
h(x)
. The disorder field couples to an operator
\mathcal{O}_{0}(t,x)
. The action of the disordered system is

S=S_{0}+\int dt\,d^{d}x\,h(x)\mathcal{O}_{0}(t,x)\,.

(1)

Note that the quantum disorder,
h(x)
, is homogeneous in time. We then consider an ensemble of the disordered system where the probability of finding a specific profile of the disorder field is given by
P[h]
.
The spatial randomness implies that the distribution
P[h]
is such that the spatial correlation of the disorder field is much smaller than the intrinsic length scale of the system, e.g. lattice spacing. A convenient choice to achieve this is the Gaussian distribution,

P[h]=\mathcal{N}\,e^{-\frac{1}{2v}\int d^{d}x\,h^{2}(x)}\,.

(2)

Now, the partition function (or the generating function when we include sources) for a given profile of the disorder field is

Z[h]=e^{W[h]}=\int[d\eta^{\dagger}][d\eta]\,e^{iS_{0}(\eta,\eta^{\dagger})+i\int dt\,d^{d}x\,h(x)\mathcal{O}_{0}(t,x)}\,.

(3)

The above is the partition function of a system in the ensemble for a specific disorder profile. Connected correlation functions for a given disorder profile are obtained by computing the derivatives of
W[h]
(in the presence of sources). The quenched average correlation function is obtained by averaging over the disorder distribution. For example, the quenched average free energy (and its derivatives) is given by,

\overline{F}=\int[dh]P[h]W[h]\,.

(4)

Since the distribution (
2
) is translational invariant, the quenched average quantities are also translational invariant.

The quenched average free energy (and its derivatives) can be calculated using the replica trick. In the replica trick, we introduce
\underline{n}
-copies of the original theory with the same profile of the disorder field. The fields and the disorder operator are now labelled by replica index
A
, where
A=1,...\underline{n}
. The partition function is then defined as,

Z^{\underline{n}}[h]=\int\prod_{A=1}^{\underline{n}}[d\eta_{A}^{\dagger}][d\eta_{A}]e^{i\sum_{A=1}^{\underline{n}}S_{0,A}(\eta_{A},\eta_{A}^{\dagger})+i\int dt\,d^{d}x\,h(x)\sum_{A=1}^{\underline{n}}\mathcal{O}_{0,A}(t,x)}\,.

(5)

The above partition function is computed for the integer
\underline{n}
. Assuming that the analytic continuation exists for real values of
\underline{n}
, the quenched average free energy is given by

\displaystyle\overline{F}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{\underline{n}\rightarrow 0}\frac{\partial}{\partial\underline{n}}\int D[h]P[h]Z^{\underline{n}}[h]\,,

(6)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{\underline{n}\rightarrow 0}\frac{\partial}{\partial\underline{n}}\int\prod_{A=1}^{\underline{n}}[d\eta_{A}^{\dagger}][d\eta_{A}]\,e^{i\sum_{A=1}^{\underline{n}}S_{0,A}(\eta_{A},\eta_{A}^{\dagger})-\frac{v}{2}\int dt\,dt^{\prime}\,d^{d}x\,\sum_{A,B=1}^{\underline{n}}\mathcal{O}_{0,A}(t,x)\mathcal{O}_{0,B}(t^{\prime},x)}\,,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{\underline{n}\rightarrow 0}\frac{\partial}{\partial\underline{n}}\int\prod_{A=1}^{\underline{n}}[d\eta_{A}^{\dagger}][d\eta_{A}]\,e^{iS_{\text{repl.}}}\,,

where the action for the replicated theory is

S_{\text{repl.}}=\sum_{A=1}^{\underline{n}}S_{0,A}(\eta_{A},\eta_{A}^{\dagger})+\frac{iv}{2}\int dt\,dt^{\prime}\,d^{d}x\,\sum_{A,B=1}^{\underline{n}}\mathcal{O}_{0,A}(t,x)\mathcal{O}_{0,B}(t^{\prime},x)\,.

(7)

Thus, we see that the replica trick reduces the computation of the quenched average free energy (and its derivatives) to a path integral computation, but with a price that the fields with different replica indexes couple with each other. We also observe a couple of the effects of averaging over the probability distribution: Firstly, the replicated action is complex, and secondly, it is non-local in time.

We will be interested in the quenched average correlation functions and the Callan-Symanzik equation satisfied by these. The quenched average correlation function is given by

\overline{<\mathcal{O}(t_{1},x_{1}).....\mathcal{O}(t_{\ell},x_{\ell})>}=\int[dh]P[h]<\mathcal{O}(t_{1},x_{1}).....\mathcal{O}(t_{\ell},x_{\ell})>_{h}\,,

(8)

where the correlation function
<\mathcal{O}(t_{1},x_{1}).....\mathcal{O}(t_{\ell},x_{\ell})>_{h}
is computed for a given profile of the disorder field. Following the replica trick, one can express the quenched average connected correlation function in terms of the correlation function computed in the replicated theory. More precisely, the relation is

\overline{<\mathcal{O}(t_{1},x_{1}).....\mathcal{O}(t_{\ell},x_{\ell})>_{\text{conn.}}}=\lim_{\underline{n}\rightarrow 0}\frac{\partial}{\partial\underline{n}}<\mathcal{\sum}_{A_{1}}O_{A_{1}}(t_{1},x_{1}).....\sum_{A_{\ell}}\mathcal{O}_{A_{\ell}}(t_{\ell},x_{\ell})>_{\text{repl.}}\,.

(9)

The above relation makes it simpler to compute the average correlation function. It amounts to computing the correlation function in the replicated theory using the standard tools of the perturbative quantum field theory, and then analytically continuing the result for real values of
\underline{n}
.
Note that the RHS of (
9
) need not be the connected correlation function.

The quenched average connected correlation function obeys generalized Callan Symanzik equation. This can be obtained starting from the relation (
9
).
We begin with the Callan-Symanzik equation satisfied by the renormalized Green’s function in the replicated theory (we assume that the operators
O_{A}(t,x)
do not mix with any other operator)

\Big(\mu\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}+\beta_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial g_{i}}+\ell\,\gamma_{\mathcal{O}}\Big)<\mathcal{O}_{A_{1}}(t_{1},x_{1}).....\mathcal{O}_{A_{\ell}}(t_{\ell},x_{\ell})>^{\text{renor.}}_{\text{repl.}}=0\,,

(10)

where
\mu
is the renomalization scale,
\beta_{i}
are beta functions for all the coupling constants and
\gamma_{\mathcal{O}}
is the anomalous dimension. Then we have

\displaystyle\Big(\mu\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}+\beta_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial g_{i}}+\ell\,\gamma_{\mathcal{O}}\Big)\frac{\partial}{\partial\underline{n}}<\sum_{A_{1}}\mathcal{O}_{A_{1}}(t_{1},x_{1}).....\sum_{A_{\ell}}\mathcal{O}_{A_{\ell}}(t_{\ell},x_{\ell})>^{\text{renor.}}_{\text{repl.}}

\displaystyle+\Big(\mu\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}+\frac{\partial\beta_{i}}{\partial\underline{n}}\frac{\partial}{\partial g_{i}}+\ell\,\frac{\partial\gamma_{\mathcal{O}}}{\partial\underline{n}}\Big)<\sum_{A_{1}}\mathcal{O}_{A_{1}}(t_{1},x_{1}).....\sum_{A_{\ell}}\mathcal{O}_{A_{\ell}}(t_{\ell},x_{\ell})>^{\text{renor.}}_{\text{repl.}}=0\,.

(11)

Since the replica symmetry implies that
<\sum_{A_{1}}\mathcal{O}_{A_{1}}(t_{1},x_{1}).....\sum_{A_{\ell}}\mathcal{O}_{A_{\ell}}(t_{\ell},x_{\ell})>^{\text{renor.}}_{\text{repl.}}
will be at least proportional to
\underline{n}
, we see that when we take the limit
\lim\underline{n}\rightarrow 0
, we obtain

\Big(\mu\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}+\lim_{\underline{n}\rightarrow 0}\beta_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial g_{i}}+\ell\,\lim_{\underline{n}\rightarrow 0}\gamma_{\mathcal{O}}\Big)\overline{<\mathcal{O}(t_{1},x_{1}).....\mathcal{O}(t_{\ell},x_{\ell})>_{\text{conn.}}^{\text{renor.}}}=0

(12)

Thus, the beta function and the anomalous dimension of the disordered theory are
\lim\limits_{\underline{n}\rightarrow 0}\beta_{i}
and
\lim\limits_{\underline{n}\rightarrow 0}\gamma_{\mathcal{O}}
. In particular, the fixed point of the resultant beta function gives the disordered fixed point.

4
Disorder system at finite temperature

This section aims to study the thermodynamics and phases of the disorder unitary fermion system at finite temperatures. To understand the phase diagram, we need to find the free energy of the system as a function of temperature and chemical potential. The thermodynamics of the unitary fermion system with non-zero chemical potential has been studied in
PhysRevA.75.063618
. In the paper, the author investigated the phases of the unitary system in the imaginary time formalism in
\epsilon
-expansion when the chemical potential is of the order of
\epsilon
.
In the present section, we follow their analysis, but in the presence of disorder.

The basic idea would be to compute the quenched average free energy at a finite temperature with chemical potentials. It is given as

(58)

where
T
and
\mu_{i}
are temperature and chemical potentials, respectively.
In the above we have defined the replicated effective potential as

e^{-\Gamma_{\text{repl.}}}=\int\prod_{A=1}^{\underline{n}}[d\eta_{A}^{\dagger}][d\eta_{A}]\,e^{-S_{\text{repl.}}}\,.

(59)

As we will see below
\Gamma_{\text{repl.}}
will depend on the number of replicas and vanishes in the limit
\underline{n}\rightarrow 0
. Our goal would be to study the behaviour of the quenched average free energy as a function of the temperature and chemical potential.

\displaystyle=\beta\delta_{A,B}\delta_{n_{1},n_{2}}\int\frac{d^{d}k}{(2\pi)^{d}}\frac{-e^{-i\vec{k}\cdot(\vec{x}_{1}-\vec{x}_{2})}}{i\omega_{n_{1}}-(\frac{\epsilon_{k}}{2}+\delta)}\,,

\displaystyle=\beta\delta_{A,B}\delta_{n_{1},n_{2}}\int\frac{d^{d}k}{(2\pi)^{d}}\frac{-e^{-i\vec{k}\cdot(\vec{x}_{1}-\vec{x}_{2})}}{(i\omega^{f}_{n_{1}})^{2}-(\epsilon_{k}-\tilde{\mu})^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\begin{pmatrix}i\omega^{f}_{n_{1}}+(\epsilon_{k}-\tilde{\mu})&0\\
0&i\omega^{f}_{n_{1}}-(\epsilon_{k}-\tilde{\mu})\end{pmatrix}\,.

One finds the terms
\delta_{\phi},\delta v
and
\delta r
by looking at the divergences in the above expressions. At this order (upto one loop) in the computation, these are

\displaystyle\delta_{\phi}=\frac{g_{0}^{2}}{8\pi^{2}\epsilon},\quad\delta r=-\frac{g_{0}^{2}\tilde{\mu}}{4\pi^{2}\epsilon}-{\underline{n}}\frac{2v\delta}{\pi^{2}\epsilon},\quad\delta v=-{\underline{n}}\frac{2v^{2}}{\pi^{2}\epsilon}\,.

(73)

The above divergences can be removed by introducing counter terms. However, these divergences, in particular
\delta v
, reveal something interesting. It shows that the running of the disorder coupling constant, as observed in section
3.1
, is primarily due to the Wilsonian RG flow in the zero mode sector. The contribution of the non-zero modes to the
\beta
-function is proportional to
{\underline{n}}
and disappears from the generalized Callan-Symanzik equation.

4.2
Effective potential for constant mode near
4
-spatial dimensions

In the present section, we compute the one-loop effective potential for the scalar in
(4-\epsilon)
-dimensions. For the computation, we assume that the coupling constant
g
and
\lambda
do not scale with
\epsilon
. In the next section, we will relax this assumption.
We expand the action about the replica symmetric vacuum.

\phi_{A}=\phi_{0}+\hat{\phi}_{A},\quad\phi^{\dagger}_{A}=\phi_{0}^{\dagger}+\hat{\phi}^{\dagger}_{A}\,.

(74)

We are interested in the effective potential for the field
\phi_{0}
and
\phi_{0}^{\dagger}
, and, therefore, we assume that these are independent of space and time coordinates. Note that the assumption is consistent with the fact that the replicated theory respects the translational invariance of space and time.

We first integrate out the fermions. The relevant terms in the action are

\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau\int d^{d}x\Big[\Psi_{A}^{\dagger}(\partial_{\tau}-\sigma_{3}\frac{\nabla^{2}}{2})\Psi_{A}\Big]-\int d\tau\,d^{d}x\Big[\tilde{\mu}\Psi_{A}^{\dagger}\sigma_{3}\Psi_{A}+g\Psi_{A}^{\dagger}\sigma_{+}\Psi_{A}\phi_{0}+g\Psi_{A}^{\dagger}\sigma_{-}\Psi_{A}\phi_{0}^{\dagger}\Big]\,.

(75)

The one loop contribution of fermions to the 1PI (replicated) effective potential is (
\Gamma_{\text{repl.}}=\beta\text{Vol.}V_{\text{repl.}}
)

V^{f}_{\text{repl.}}=-\underline{n}\,T\sum_{\omega^{f}_{m}}\int_{\vec{p}}\ln\Big[(\omega^{f}_{m})^{2}+E_{p}^{2}\Big]=-\underline{n}\int_{\vec{p}}\Big[E_{p}+2T\ln(1+e^{-\frac{E_{p}}{T}})\Big]\,,

(76)

where
E_{p}^{2}=(\epsilon_{\vec{p}}-\tilde{\mu})^{2}+g_{0}^{2}\phi_{0}\phi_{0}^{\dagger},\,\,\omega^{f}_{m}=2\pi(m+\frac{1}{2})T
and
Vol.
is the spatial volume. Also, in the above we have used the notation
\int_{\vec{p}}
to indicate the integration over spatial momentum, i.e.
\int_{\vec{p}}=\int\frac{d^{d}p}{(2\pi)^{d}}
.

Next, we look at the contribution from the scalar fluctuations. The quadratic part of the action which is relevant for our computation is (we have ignored the term linear in
\hat{\phi}_{A}
, since it does not contribute to 1PI effective action)

\displaystyle\int d\tau\,d^{d}x\sum_{A}\Big[\hat{\phi}_{A}^{\dagger}(\partial_{\tau}-\frac{\nabla^{2}}{4})\hat{\phi}_{A}\Big]+\int d\tau\,d^{d}x\sum_{A}\Big[\delta(\phi_{0}^{\dagger}\phi_{0}+\hat{\phi}_{A}^{\dagger}\hat{\phi}_{A})\Big]

\displaystyle-\frac{\lambda}{2}\int d^{d}x\,d\tau\,d\tau^{\prime}\,\sum_{A,B=1}^{\underline{n}}\Big[(\phi_{0}^{\dagger}\phi_{0})^{2}+2\phi_{0}^{\dagger}\phi_{0}\Big(\hat{\phi}_{A}^{\dagger}(x,\tau)\hat{\phi}_{A}(x,\tau)+\hat{\phi}_{A}(x,\tau)\hat{\phi}_{B}^{\dagger}(x,\tau^{\prime})\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+(\phi_{0}^{\dagger})^{2}\hat{\phi}_{A}(x,\tau)\hat{\phi}_{B}(x,\tau^{\prime})+(\phi_{0})^{2}\hat{\phi}_{A}^{\dagger}(x,\tau)\hat{\phi}^{\dagger}_{B}(x,\tau^{\prime})\Big]\,.

Thus, the contribution of the scalar to the 1PI effective potential at one loop is

\displaystyle V^{s}_{\text{repl.}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underline{n}\,\delta\,\phi_{0}^{\dagger}\phi_{0}-\frac{\lambda}{2T}\underline{n}^{2}(\phi_{0}^{\dagger}\phi_{0})^{2}+T\sum_{\omega_{m}}\int_{\vec{p}}\Big[\frac{\underline{n}}{2}\ln\Big(\omega_{m}^{2}+\Big(\frac{\epsilon_{\vec{p}}}{2}+\delta-\frac{\underline{n}\,\lambda}{T}\phi_{0}^{\dagger}\phi_{0}\Big)^{2}\Big)\Big]

(78)

\displaystyle+\frac{T}{2}\int_{\vec{p}}\ln\frac{\frac{\epsilon_{\vec{p}}}{2}+\delta-\frac{3\underline{n}\,\lambda}{T}\phi_{0}^{\dagger}\phi_{0}}{\frac{\epsilon_{\vec{p}}}{2}+\delta-\frac{\underline{n}\,\lambda}{T}\phi_{0}^{\dagger}\phi_{0}}\,.

Note that the first two terms are classical contributions. Summing over the frequencies, we obtain

\displaystyle V^{s}_{\text{repl.}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underline{n}\,\delta\,\phi_{0}^{\dagger}\phi_{0}-\frac{\lambda}{2T}\underline{n}^{2}(\phi_{0}^{\dagger}\phi_{0})+\underline{n}\int_{\vec{p}}\Big[\frac{1}{2}(\frac{\epsilon_{\vec{p}}}{2}+\delta-\frac{\underline{n}\,\lambda}{T}\phi_{0}^{\dagger}\phi_{0}\Big)+T\ln\Big(1-e^{-\frac{\frac{\epsilon_{\vec{p}}}{2}+\delta-\frac{\underline{n}\,\lambda}{T}\phi_{0}^{\dagger}\phi_{0}}{T}}\Big)\Big]

(79)

\displaystyle+\frac{T}{2}\int_{\vec{p}}\ln\frac{\frac{\epsilon_{\vec{p}}}{2}+\delta-\frac{3\underline{n}\,\lambda}{T}\phi_{0}^{\dagger}\phi_{0}}{\frac{\epsilon_{\vec{p}}}{2}+\delta-\frac{\underline{n}\,\lambda}{T}\phi_{0}^{\dagger}\phi_{0}}\,.

Finally, the quenched average effective potential at one loop (
\overline{V}=\overline{F}
) is

\displaystyle\overline{V}(T,\mu_{i},\phi_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta\,\phi_{0}^{\dagger}\phi_{0}-\int_{\vec{p}}\Big[E_{p}+2T\ln(1+e^{-\frac{E_{p}}{T}})\Big]+\int_{\vec{p}}\Big[\frac{1}{2}(\frac{\epsilon_{\vec{p}}}{2}+\delta\Big)+T\ln\Big(1-e^{-\frac{\frac{\epsilon_{\vec{p}}}{2}+\delta}{T}}\Big)\Big]

(80)

\displaystyle-\lambda\phi_{0}^{\dagger}\phi_{0}\int_{\vec{p}}\frac{1}{\frac{\epsilon_{\vec{p}}}{2}+\delta}\,.

Note that the third term is a constant, i.e. independent of
\phi_{0}^{\dagger}\phi_{0}
.

4.2.1
Renormalization of the effective potential

The effective potential (
80
) suffers from UV divergence in
d=4
-spatial dimension; however, the divergence comes from the
T=0
part of the integration. We can remove these divergences by adding counter terms
\delta r
and
\Lambda
. With these, the effective potential is

\displaystyle\overline{V}(T,\mu_{i},\phi_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\delta+\delta r)\,\phi_{0}^{\dagger}\phi_{0}-\int_{\vec{p}}\Big[E_{p}+2T\ln(1+e^{-\frac{E_{p}}{T}})\Big]+\int_{\vec{p}}\Big[\frac{1}{2}(\frac{\epsilon_{\vec{p}}}{2}+\delta\Big)+T\ln\Big(1-e^{-\frac{\frac{\epsilon_{\vec{p}}}{2}+\delta}{T}}\Big)\Big]

(81)

\displaystyle-\lambda\phi_{0}^{\dagger}\phi_{0}\int_{\vec{p}}\frac{1}{\frac{\epsilon_{\vec{p}}}{2}+\delta}+\Lambda\,.

We choose
\delta_{r}
such that at
T=0
, there are no divergence in the
\frac{\partial^{2}\overline{V}}{\partial\phi\partial\phi^{\dagger}}\Big|_{\phi=0}
. Similarly, we determine
\Lambda
by requiring that
\overline{V}(T,\mu_{i},\phi_{0}=0)=0
.
The first condition implies that

\delta r-\frac{g^{2}\tilde{\mu}}{4\pi^{2}\epsilon}+\frac{2\delta\,\lambda}{\pi^{2}\epsilon}=0,\quad\Rightarrow\quad\delta r=\frac{g^{2}\tilde{\mu}}{4\pi^{2}\epsilon}-\frac{2\delta\lambda}{\pi^{2}\epsilon}\,.

(82)

The second renormalization condition implies that

\Lambda=\int_{\vec{p}}|(\epsilon_{p}-\tilde{\mu})|-\int_{\vec{p}}\frac{1}{2}\Big(\frac{\epsilon_{\vec{p}}}{2}+\delta\Big)

(83)

With these renormalizations, the effective potential is finite and is given by

\displaystyle\overline{V}(T,\mu_{i},\phi_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\delta+\delta r)\,\phi_{0}^{\dagger}\phi_{0}-\int_{\vec{p}}\Big[E_{p}-|(\epsilon_{p}-\tilde{\mu})|+2T\ln(1+e^{-\frac{E_{p}}{T}})\Big]+T\int_{\vec{p}}\ln\Big(1-e^{-\frac{\frac{\epsilon_{\vec{p}}}{2}-\delta}{T}}\Big)

(84)

\displaystyle-\lambda\phi_{0}^{\dagger}\phi_{0}\int_{\vec{p}}\frac{1}{\frac{\epsilon_{\vec{p}}}{2}+\delta}\,.

One can analyse the above potential for various values of
T
. For a fixed value of
T
, the potential is a function of the chemical potentials and
\phi_{0}
.

4.2.2
Disorder system at
T=0

We look at the effective potential for
T=0
. In this case, the effective potential is

\overline{V}(0,\mu_{i},\phi_{0})=(\delta+\delta r)\,\phi_{0}^{\dagger}\phi_{0}-\int_{\vec{p}}\Big[\sqrt{(\epsilon_{\vec{p}}-\tilde{\mu})^{2}+g^{2}\phi_{0}\phi_{0}^{\dagger}}-|(\epsilon_{p}-\tilde{\mu})|\Big]-\lambda\phi_{0}^{\dagger}\phi_{0}\int_{\vec{p}}\frac{1}{\frac{\epsilon_{\vec{p}}}{2}+\delta}\,.

(85)

In the above, we have ignored the
\phi_{0}
-independent terms.
Next, we want to see if there is a non-trivial minimum of the effective potential as a function of
\phi_{0}
. For this, we need to calculate the integral

\displaystyle f(|\phi_{0}|)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\frac{d^{d}p}{(2\pi)^{d}}\frac{g^{2}|\phi_{0}|}{\sqrt{(\epsilon_{\vec{p}}-\tilde{\mu})^{2}+g^{2}|\phi_{0}|^{2}}}

(86)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{g^{2}|\phi_{0}|\tilde{\mu}^{\frac{d}{2}-1}S_{d-1}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{0}^{\infty}q^{d-1}dq\frac{1}{\sqrt{(\frac{q^{2}}{2}-1)^{2}+\tilde{a}^{2}}}\,.

Here
S_{d-1}=\frac{2\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})}
and
\tilde{a}=\frac{g|\phi_{0}|}{\tilde{\mu}}
. Also, we have assumed that
\tilde{\mu}>0
.
The above integration can be performed explicitly (
120
). We find that for
d=(4-\epsilon)
and to the leading order in
\epsilon
,

\displaystyle f(|\phi_{0}|)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{g^{2}\tilde{\mu}|\phi_{0}|}{2\pi^{2}\epsilon}+\frac{g^{2}\tilde{\mu}|\phi_{0}|}{4\pi^{2}}\Big(\ln\frac{4\pi}{g|\phi_{0}|}-\gamma\Big)

(87)

\displaystyle-\frac{g^{2}\tilde{\mu}|\phi_{0}|}{4\pi^{2}}\Big(\sqrt{1+\tilde{a}^{2}}-\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{\sqrt{1+\tilde{a}^{2}}+1}{\sqrt{1+\tilde{a}^{2}}-1}\Big)\,,

where
\gamma
is Euler’s constant.
Let us evaluate the above expression for
\tilde{\mu}<<g|\phi_{0}|
and
\tilde{\mu}>>g|\phi_{0}|
. In the first case, we have

f(|\phi_{0}|)=\frac{g^{2}|\phi_{0}|}{4\pi^{2}}\Big[-(\gamma\tilde{\mu}+g|\phi_{0}|+\tilde{\mu}\ln\frac{g|\phi_{0}|}{4\pi})+\frac{2\tilde{\mu}}{\epsilon}\Big]\sim\frac{g^{2}|\phi_{0}|}{4\pi^{2}}\Big[-g|\phi_{0}|+\frac{2\tilde{\mu}}{\epsilon}\Big]\,.

(88)

Thus the first derivative of the potential is

\overline{V}(0,\mu_{i},\phi_{0})^{\prime}\sim 2(\delta+\delta r)\,|\phi_{0}|-\frac{g^{2}|\phi_{0}|}{4\pi^{2}}\Big[-g|\phi_{0}|+\frac{2\tilde{\mu}}{\epsilon}\Big]+\lambda|\phi_{0}|\Big(\frac{4\delta}{\pi^{2}\epsilon}-\frac{2\delta(\gamma-1+\ln\frac{\delta}{4\pi})}{\pi^{2}}\Big)\,.

(89)

The counter terms
\delta r
cancels the
\epsilon^{-1}
-divergences, and thus, we get

\overline{V}(0,\mu_{i},\phi_{0})^{\prime}\sim 2\delta\,|\phi_{0}|+\frac{g^{3}|\phi_{0}|^{2}}{4\pi^{2}}-2\lambda|\phi_{0}|\frac{\delta(\gamma-1+\ln\frac{\delta}{4\pi})}{\pi^{2}}=2\delta^{\prime}\,|\phi_{0}|+\frac{g^{3}|\phi_{0}|^{2}}{4\pi^{2}}\,,

(90)

where
\delta^{\prime}=\delta(1-\frac{\lambda}{\pi^{2}}(\gamma-1+\ln\frac{\delta}{4\pi}))
.
We see from the above that there is a non-trivial minima of the potential for

|\phi_{0}|\sim-\frac{8\pi^{2}\delta^{\prime}}{g^{3}}\,,

(91)

provided
\lambda(\gamma-1+\ln\frac{\delta}{4\pi})>\pi^{2}
.

Next, we look at the potential in the region
\tilde{\mu}>>g|\phi_{0}|
. In this case, the function
f(\phi_{0})
to the leading order in
\frac{g|\phi_{0}|}{\tilde{\mu}}
is

f(\phi_{0})=\frac{g^{2}|\phi_{0}|\tilde{\mu}}{2\pi^{2}\epsilon}-\frac{g^{2}|\phi_{0}|\tilde{\mu}}{4\pi^{2}}\Big(1+\gamma-\ln\frac{8\pi\tilde{\mu}}{g^{2}|\phi_{0}|^{2}}\Big)\,.

(92)

and the derivative of the potential is

\displaystyle\overline{V}(0,\mu_{i},|\phi_{0}|)^{\prime}=2\delta^{\prime}|\phi_{0}|+g^{2}|\phi_{0}|\frac{\tilde{\mu}}{4\pi^{2}}(1+\gamma-\ln\frac{8\pi\tilde{\mu}}{g^{2}|\phi_{0}|^{2}})\,.

(93)

In this case, the solution seems to be at

\ln\frac{g|\phi_{0}|}{2\sqrt{2\pi\tilde{\mu}}}=-\frac{1}{2}(1+\gamma+\frac{8\pi^{2}\delta^{\prime}}{g^{2}\mu})\,.

(94)

Summing over all these diagrams, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda^{n}\underline{n}\phi_{0}\phi_{0}^{\dagger}\beta\,V_{d}\Big(\int\frac{d^{d}k}{(2\pi)^{d}}\frac{1}{(\frac{\epsilon_{k}}{2}+\delta)^{2}}\Big)^{n-1}\int\frac{d^{d}p}{(2\pi)^{d}}\frac{1}{\frac{\epsilon_{p}}{2}+\delta}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad=\underline{n}\phi_{0}\phi_{0}^{\dagger}\beta\,V_{d}\frac{\lambda\int\frac{d^{d}p}{(2\pi)^{d}}\frac{1}{\frac{\epsilon_{p}}{2}+\delta}}{1-\lambda\int\frac{d^{d}k}{(2\pi)^{d}}\frac{1}{(\frac{\epsilon_{k}}{2}+\delta)^{2}}}=\underline{n}\phi_{0}\phi_{0}^{\dagger}\beta\,V_{d-1}\mathcal{C}\,.

(104)

Evaluating the above integrals, we obtain

\int\frac{d^{d}p}{(2\pi)^{d}}\frac{1}{\frac{\epsilon_{p}}{2}+\delta}=-\frac{2\delta}{\pi^{2}\epsilon}+\mathcal{O}(1),\quad\int\frac{d^{d}p}{(2\pi)^{d}}\frac{1}{(\frac{\epsilon_{p}}{2}+\delta)^{2}}=\frac{2}{\pi^{2}\epsilon}+\mathcal{O}(1)\,.

(105)

Thus, to the leading order in
\epsilon
, we have

\mathcal{C}=-\delta\frac{\frac{2\lambda}{\pi^{2}\epsilon}}{1-\frac{2\lambda}{\pi^{2}\epsilon}}\,.

(106)

Similarly, we can add all the contributions of the diagram consisting of vertices
(4)
and
(6)
together with an arbitrary number of the vertex
(7)
. We find

\lambda^{2}\phi_{0}^{\dagger}\phi_{0}\underline{n}\beta\,V_{d}\Big(X_{0}+\frac{X_{1}Y_{0}}{1-\lambda\int\frac{d^{d}k}{(2\pi)^{d}}\frac{1}{(\frac{\epsilon_{k}}{2}+\delta)^{2}}}\Big)\,,

(107)

where

\displaystyle X_{0}=\lambda^{2}\int\frac{d^{d}k\,d^{d}p}{(2\pi)^{2d}}\frac{1}{(\frac{\epsilon_{k}}{2}+\delta)(\frac{\epsilon_{p}}{2}+\delta)(\frac{\epsilon_{k+p}}{2}+\delta)},\quad Y_{0}=\lambda\int\frac{d^{d}k}{(2\pi)^{d}}\frac{1}{(\frac{\epsilon_{k}}{2}+\delta)}\,,

\displaystyle X_{1}=\lambda^{2}\int\frac{d^{d}k\,d^{d}p}{(2\pi)^{2d}}\frac{1}{(\frac{\epsilon_{k}}{2}+\delta)(\frac{\epsilon_{p}}{2}+\delta)^{2}(\frac{\epsilon_{k+p}}{2}+\delta)}\,.

(108)

Evaluating each of these integrals (see appendix
A
) to the leading order in
\epsilon
, we get

X_{0}=-\frac{6\lambda^{2}\delta}{\pi^{4}\epsilon^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon),\quad X_{1}=\frac{2\lambda^{2}}{\pi^{4}\epsilon^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon),\quad Y_{0}=-\frac{2\lambda\delta}{\pi^{2}\epsilon}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)\,.

(109)

Thus, the contribution (
107
) to a leading order in
\epsilon
is

-\Big(\frac{2\lambda^{2}\delta}{\pi^{4}\epsilon^{2}}\Big)\frac{3-\frac{4\lambda}{\pi^{2}\epsilon}}{1-\frac{2\lambda}{\pi^{2}\epsilon}}\,.

(110)

Adding the contributions (
106
) and (
110
), we obtain

-\underline{n}\phi_{0}\phi_{0}^{\dagger}\beta\,V_{d}\frac{2\lambda\delta}{\pi^{2}\epsilon}\times\frac{1+\frac{3\lambda}{\pi^{2}\epsilon}-\frac{4\lambda^{2}}{\pi^{4}\epsilon^{2}}}{1-\frac{2\lambda}{\pi^{2}\epsilon}}\,.

(111)

Note that the above contribution has a Landau pole in
\lambda
.

Finally, the effective potential becomes

\displaystyle\overline{V}(T,\mu_{i},\phi_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta^{\prime}\,\phi_{0}^{\dagger}\phi_{0}-\int_{\vec{p}}\Big[E_{p}+2T\ln(1+e^{-\frac{E_{p}}{T}})\Big]+\int_{\vec{p}}\Big[\frac{1}{2}(\frac{\epsilon_{\vec{p}}}{2}+\delta\Big)+T\ln\Big(1-e^{-\frac{\frac{\epsilon_{\vec{p}}}{2}+\delta}{T}}\Big)\Big]\,.

where

\delta^{\prime}=\delta\Big(1+\frac{2\lambda}{\pi^{2}\epsilon}+\frac{4\lambda^{2}}{\pi^{4}\epsilon^{2}}+\frac{\frac{6\lambda^{2}}{\pi^{4}\epsilon^{2}}}{1-\frac{2\lambda}{\pi^{2}\epsilon}}\Big)\,,

(113)

and

E_{p}^{2}=(\epsilon_{\vec{p}}-\tilde{\mu})^{2}+g_{0}^{2}\phi_{0}\phi_{0}^{\dagger}\,.

(114)

In this case, however, we do not encounter any non-trivial minima at
T=0
as a function of chemical potentials.

4.4
A different scaling

In the above analysis, we had a situation where
\phi_{0}
and chemical potentials do not scale with
\epsilon
. Now, we consider the case where
\phi_{0}\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}
,
\delta\sim\epsilon
and
\mu\sim\epsilon
. In this case, however, the contribution of
\epsilon
from a given diagram differs from what we have done previously. Taking
\delta\hat{\phi}_{A}^{\dagger}\hat{\phi}_{A}
as an interaction term, we find that a Feynman diagram diverges at most as
\epsilon^{V_{\delta}-1}
, where the
V_{\delta}
is the number of the vertex
\delta\hat{\phi}_{A}^{\dagger}\hat{\phi}_{A}
in the Feynman diagram. The contribution is independent of the number of cubic and quartic vertices. Therefore, for the
\mathcal{O}(1)
contributions, we have to consider the diagrams with
V_{\delta}=1
and an arbitrary number of cubic (even number) and quartic vertices. As a result, all the monomials of
\phi_{0}^{\dagger}\phi_{0}
will equally contribute to the effective potential, i.e., summing over all 1PI diagrams, we expect the contribution to the effective potential would be

\delta\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}C_{n}(\lambda)(\phi_{0}^{\dagger}\phi_{0})^{n}\,.

(115)

This analysis is more complicated, and it would be nice to develop a systematic way to compute all these coefficients. We leave it for the future exercise.

Appendix A
Useful Integration

In the main text, we encountered an integral of the following form:

\int\frac{d^{d}p}{(2\pi)^{d}}\frac{d^{d}q}{(2\pi)^{d}}\frac{1}{(p^{2}+b_{3}^{2})(q^{2}+b_{2}^{2})((p-q)^{2}+b_{3}^{2})}\,.

(116)

We are interested in obtaining the leading divergent contribution of the above integral. This can be obtained as follows:

\displaystyle\int\frac{d^{d}p}{(2\pi)^{d}}\frac{d^{d}q}{(2\pi)^{d}}\frac{1}{(p^{2}+b_{3}^{2})(q^{2}+b_{2}^{2})((p-q)^{2}+b_{3}^{2})}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\int\frac{d^{d}p}{(2\pi)^{d}}\frac{d^{d}q}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{0}^{1}dx\,dy\,dz\frac{2\delta(x+y+z-1)}{\Big(xp^{2}+yq^{2}+z(p-q)^{2}+b^{2}\Big)^{3}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\int\frac{d^{d}p}{(2\pi)^{d}}\frac{d^{d}q}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{0}^{1}dx\,dy\,dz\frac{2\delta(x+y+z-1)}{\Big(\alpha p^{2}+\beta q^{2}+b^{2}\Big)^{3}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\int\frac{d^{d}p}{(2\pi)^{d}}\frac{d^{d}q}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{0}^{1}dx\,dy\,dz\,\delta(x+y+z-1)\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,t^{2}\,e^{-t(\alpha p^{2}+\beta q^{2}+b^{2})}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\frac{\Gamma(3-d)}{(4\pi)^{d}}\int_{0}^{1}dx\,dy\,dz\,\delta(x+y+z-1)(\alpha\beta)^{-\frac{d}{2}}(b^{2})^{d-3}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\frac{\Gamma(3-d)}{(4\pi)^{d}}\int_{x+y\leq 1}dx\,dy\frac{1}{(y+x-x^{2}-y^{2}-xy)^{\frac{d}{2}}}\Big(y(b_{2}^{2}-b_{3}^{2})+b_{3}^{2}\Big)^{d-3}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\sim\frac{\Gamma(3-d)}{(4\pi)^{d}}\frac{2}{\epsilon}\Big(2(b_{3}^{2})^{d-3}+(b_{2}^{2})^{d-3}\Big)+\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{\epsilon})\,.

(117)

In the above, we have

\alpha=x+z,\quad\beta=y+z-\frac{z^{2}}{x+z}=\frac{xy+yz+zx}{x+z},\quad b^{2}=y(b_{2}^{2}-b_{3}^{2})+b_{3}^{2}\,.

(118)

Thus, it boils down to the integration over the Feynman parameters. Note that the above integral is divergent in
d=4
. The divergence come from the neighbourhood of three points
(x,y)=(0,0),(0,1)
and
(1,0)
. Near each neighbourhood, the leading divergent is
\frac{2}{\epsilon}
. The result (
A
) is consistent with the exact answer obtained in
Kleinert2001CriticalPO
.
