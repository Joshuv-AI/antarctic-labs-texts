A finite element method for Allen–Cahn equation on deforming surface
Maxim Olshanskii, Xianmin Xu, Vladimir Yushutin

Abstract
The paper studies an Allen–Cahn-type equation defined on a time-dependent surface as a model of phase separation with order–disorder transition in a thin material layer. By a formal inner-outer expansion, it is shown that the limiting behavior of the solution is a geodesic mean curvature type flow in reference coordinates. A geometrically unfitted finite element method, known as a trace FEM, is considered for the numerical solution of the equation. The paper provides full stability analysis and convergence analysis that accounts for interpolation errors and an approximate recovery of the geometry.



keywords

Allen–Cahn, surface PDEs, evolving surfaces, Trace FEM, geodesic mean curvature flow

Introduction

Phase separation may happen in thin material layers such as polymer films, lipid bilayers, binary alloy interfaces or biophotonic nanostructures. One example of such essentially 2D phenomenon is the lipid rafts formation in a multi-component plasma membrane, while the membrane is advected by an extracellular fluid flow and exhibit tangential motion due to the membrane lateral fluidity
[
47
,
49
]
. In this and some other applications the thin layer is compliant so that a continuum based model represents it by a surface underdoing radial and lateral deformations.
Motivated by these examples we adopt the model of Allen and Cahn
[
1
]
to describe the phase evolution on a surface with a prescribed material motion. The model uses a smooth indicator function
u
(order parameter) to characterize ordered / disordered states and a transition region. This renders the model as a diffusive interface approach.

Before applying a numerical method to the derived Allen–Cahn type equation, the paper addresses well-posedness of the problem and the limiting behaviour of
u
when the width of the transition region tends to zero. The latter is done here by extending the standard technique of inner (with respect to the transition layer) and outer expansions for the solution. In a steady domain the asymptotic behaviour is well known to be the mean curvature flow
[
12
]
for the limit sharp interface (or the mean geodesic curvature flow for surfaces
[
10
]
). In the case of the deforming surface
\Gamma(t)
we obtain that the for each time
t
the material velocity of the sharp interface is defined by (instantaneous) geodesic mean curvature, which can be also seen as a mean curvature type flow in reference coordinates.

The main focus of the paper is a finite element analysis of the Allen–Cahn type equation posed on an evolving surface.
The paper introduces a geometrically unfitted finite element method, known as a trace FEM
[
38
,
37
]
, to discretize the problem. The method considers a sharp representation of
\Gamma(t)
(e.g., as a zero level of a level set function) and uses degrees of freedom tailored to an ambient tetrahedral mesh, which can be chosen independent of the surface and its evolution.
The numerical approach benefits from the embedding
\Gamma(t)\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
by using tangential calculus to define surface differential operators. Tangential calculus assumes an extension of functions from
\Gamma(t)
to its (narrow) neighborhood. The latter is also used here to define a time-stepping numerical procedure following the ideas from
[
29
,
40
]
.
We prove stability and error estimates for the numerical method. The error analysis accounts for all types of discretization errors, e.g., those resulting from the time stepping, polynomial interpolation and the geometric consistency error due to a possible inexact integration over
\Gamma(t)
. Besides the difficulties associated with time-dependent domains and the treatment of tangential quantities, the current analysis is complicated by the following factor.
While in a stationary domain (e.g., in a non-compliant material surface) the Allen–Cahn model defines the evolution of the order parameter as the
L^{2}
-gradient flow of the Ginzburg–Landau energy functional, such minimization property fails to hold for time-dependent domains.

Computational methods and numerical analysis for Allen–Cahn type equations in planar and volumetric domains have received much attention in the literature, see e.g.
[
46
,
19
,
32
,
24
,
25
]
among recent publications.
At the same time, numerical treatment of surface Allen-Cahn equations is a relatively recent topic in the literature. Work has been done on developing a closest point finite difference method
[
26
]
, a mesh free method
[
34
]
, and finite elements methods (FEMs)
[
8
,
10
,
51
,
50
]
as the most versatile and mathematically sound approach. Among those papers
[
10
]
allows deformation of the surface due to line tension forces and applies a (fitted) FEM on a triangulated surface. The authors of
[
51
]
applied unfitted (trace) FEM to phase-field models on stationary surfaces. Numerical analysis for equations governing phase separation on (evolving) surfaces is largely an open topic. Another two closely related studies
[
9
,
52
]
deal with FEMs for the Cahn–Hilliard equation on a time-dependent surface: in
[
9
]
the authors develop numerical analysis of a fitted FEM and
[
52
]
applies the trace FEM.
Trace FEM is a member of a large family of geometrically unfitted finite element methods along with such as XFEM
[
33
]
, immersed interface FEM
[
30
]
and cutFEM
[
3
]
, the most closely related approach.
For the later approach, other authors considered stabilized space–time formulations
[
16
,
23
]
and semi-Lagrangian type methods
[
22
]
to integrate in time PDEs posed on evolving surfaces.

The outline of the paper is as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce the model. The weak formulation of the problem and its well-posedness are discussed in Section
4
. An asymptotic behaviour of the solution to the problem is studied in Section
3
. After necessary preliminaries, the numerical method is introduced in Section
5
. Error and stability analyses are carried out in Section
6
. Section
7
supplements the paper with numerical examples.

2
Allen–Cahn equation on an evolving surface

Consider a material surface
\Gamma(t)\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
t\in[0,T]
, with density distribution
\rho:\Gamma(t)\to\mathbb{R}
. Assume
\Gamma(t)
is passively advected by a smooth velocity field
\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{x},t)
,
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, and for all times
\Gamma(t)
stays smooth, closed (
\partial\Gamma(t)=\emptyset
), connected and orientable. We are interested in a phase separation process on
\Gamma(t)
with a transition between order and disorder states. The state of matter at
\mathbf{x}\in\Gamma(t)
is characterized by a smooth indicator function
u(\mathbf{x},t)
,
u\,:\,\Gamma(t)\to[-1,1]
, with
u\simeq-1
in the less ordered phase and
u\simeq 1
in the more ordered phase.

To describe an evolution of phases, we follow the classical approach of Allen and Cahn
[
1
]
and assume that an instantaneous change in the order per area
s(t)\subset\Gamma(t)
is proportional to the variation of the total specific free energy for
s(t)
:

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{s(t)}\rho u\,ds=-\int_{s(t)}c_{k}\frac{\delta e(u)}{\delta u}\,ds,

(1)

where
c_{k}
is a positive kinetic coefficient, and the energy density is given by

e(u)=\rho\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\\
F(u)+|\nabla_{\Gamma}u|^{2}\right),

where
\nabla_{\Gamma}u
is the tangential gradient of
u
(
cf. definition in (
7
)
).
The energy of a homogeneous state
F(u)
has a double–well form of Ginzburg–Landau potential to allow for phase separation, and
\epsilon
is a characteristic width of a transition region between phases. Further we choose
F(u)=(1-u^{2})^{2}/4
.

Application of the surface Reynolds transport theorem (also known as the Leibniz formula for evolving surfaces, e.g.,
[
11
]
) to (
1
) gives

\int_{s(t)}(\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{(\rho u)}+\rho u{\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}\mathbf{w})\,ds=-\int_{s(t)}c_{k}\frac{\delta e(u)}{\delta u}\,ds.

By
\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{f}
we denote the material derivative of a smooth function
f
defined on
\Gamma(t)
for
t\in[0,T]
and
{\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}
stands for the surface divergence (
cf. (
7
)
).
Computing the functional derivative of
F(u)
with respect to
u
,
f(u)=F^{\prime}(u)
, and varying
s(t)
for any fixed
\Gamma(t)
,
t\in[0,T]
leads to the Allen–Cahn equation on the deforming surface:

\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{(\rho u)}+\rho u{\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}\mathbf{w}=-\rho c_{k}({\epsilon}^{-2}f(u)-{\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}(\rho\nabla_{\Gamma}{}u))\quad\text{on}~\Gamma(t),~t\in(0,T).

(2)

Likewise, the conservation of mass and the surface Reynolds transport theorem yield the identity

\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{\rho}+\rho{\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}\mathbf{w}=0\quad\text{on}~\Gamma(t).

(3)

Thanks to (
3
), the surface Allen–Cahn equation (
2
) can be written in the equivalent form

\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{u}=-c_{k}\left(\epsilon^{-2}f(u)-\frac{1}{\rho}{\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}(\rho\nabla_{\Gamma}{}u)\right)\quad\text{on}~\Gamma(t),~t\in(0,T).

(4)

The equation should be complemented with the initial condition
u(\mathbf{x},0)=u_{0}(\mathbf{x})
,
\mathbf{x}\in\Gamma(0)
, describing the state of matter at time
t=0
.

Equations (
2
) or (
4
) are solved for the order parameter
u
with given
\rho
satisfying (
3
).
In this paper, we assume
\rho=\mbox{const}
. In practice, this assumption is plausible for surfaces with initially homogeneous density distribution and exhibiting small or area-preserving deformations.
The latter is characterised by
{\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}\mathbf{w}=0
and is a valid assumption for several types of biological membranes, such as lipid mono- or bi-layers
[
31
,
45
]
. Due to this assumption, the model (slightly) simplifies to the following system of equation and initial condition:

\left\{\begin{split}\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{u}&=-{\epsilon^{-2}}f(u)+\Delta_{\Gamma}u\quad\text{on}~\Gamma(t),~t\in(0,T),\\
u&=u_{0}\quad\text{on}~\Gamma(0),\end{split}\right.

(5)

\Delta_{\Gamma}
is the Laplace–Beltrami operator and we set
c_{k}=1
.

We close this section by noting the analogy between Allen–Cahn equations (
2
) or (
4
) and those
describing the compressible two-phase fluid flow (in the Euclidean space) with phase transition; see
[
2
]
.

2.1
Preliminaries

We need more precise assumptions for the evolution of
\Gamma(t)
. To formulate them, assume that
\mathbf{w}
and
\Gamma_{0}
are sufficiently smooth such that for all
y\in\Gamma_{0}
the ODE system

\Phi(y,0)=y,\quad\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial t}(y,t)=\mathbf{w}(\Phi(y,t),t),\quad t\in[0,T],

has a unique solution
x:=\Phi(y,t)\in\Gamma(t)
, which defines the Langrangian mapping
\Phi:\Gamma_{0}\to\Gamma(t)
. The inverse mapping is given by
\Phi^{-1}(x,t):=y\in\Gamma_{0}
,
x\in\Gamma(t)
. With the help of
\Phi
, we define the bijection
\Psi
between
\Gamma_{0}\times[0,T]
, with
\Gamma_{0}:=\Gamma(0)
,and the space-time manifold

\mathcal{G}:=\bigcup\limits_{t\in(0,T)}\Gamma(t)\times\{t\},\quad\mathcal{G}\subset\mathbb{R}^{4}

as follows

\Psi:\,\Gamma_{0}\times[0,T]\to\mathcal{G},\quad~\Psi(y,t):=(\Phi(y,t),t).

(6)

We assume
\Psi
is a
C^{2}
-diffeomorphism between these manifolds
.

For
\Gamma(t)
, consider a signed distance function
\phi(t)
(positive in the exterior and negative in the interior of
\Gamma(t)
). Let
\mathcal{O}_{\delta}(\mathcal{G})
be a tubulate
\delta
-neighborhood of
\Gamma
:

\mathcal{O}_{\delta}(\mathcal{G}):=\{(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}\,:\,|\phi(x,t)|\leq\delta\}.

The above assumptions imply that for sufficiently small
\delta>0
it holds
\phi\in C^{2}(\mathcal{O}_{\delta}(\mathcal{G}))
and the normal projection onto
\Gamma(t)
,
\mathbf{p}:\mathcal{O}_{\delta}(\mathcal{G})\to\Gamma(t)
is well defined for each
t\in[0,T]
. We fix such
\delta
and further often skip it in notation
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{G})=\mathcal{O}_{\delta}(\mathcal{G})
. Likewise, we shall write
\mathcal{O}_{\delta}(\Gamma(t))
to denote a
\delta
-neighborhood of
\Gamma(t)
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
\mathcal{O}(\Gamma(t))=\mathcal{O}_{\delta}(\Gamma(t))
for
\delta
as above. For every fixed
t\in[0,T]
, the gradient of
\phi
defines in
\mathcal{O}(\Gamma(t))
normal direction to
\Gamma(t)
with
\mathbf{n}=\nabla\phi
being the outward normal vector on
\Gamma(t)
,
here and below
\nabla
is spacial gradient in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

For a smooth
u
defined on
\mathcal{G}
, a function
u^{e}
denotes the extension of
u
to
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{G})
along spatial normal directions to the level-sets of
\phi
, it holds

\nabla u^{e}\cdot\nabla\phi=0
in
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{G})
,
u^{e}=u
on
\mathcal{G}
, and
u^{e}(\mathbf{x})=u^{e}(\mathbf{p}(\mathbf{x}))
in
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{G})
. The extension
u^{e}
is smooth once
\phi
and
u
are both smooth.
Further, we use the same notation
u
for the function on
\mathcal{G}
and its extension to
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{G})
.

Once a function
u
on

\mathcal{G}
is identified with its extension on
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{G})
, one can write the surface differential operators arising in the model, in terms of tangential calculus:

\nabla_{\Gamma}u=(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{n}\times\mathbf{n}^{T})\nabla u,\quad{\operatorname{\rm div}}_{\Gamma}\mathbf{w}=\mbox{tr}\left((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{n}\times\mathbf{n}^{T})\nabla\mathbf{w}\right),\quad\Delta_{\Gamma}u={\operatorname{\rm div}}_{\Gamma}\nabla_{\Gamma}u.

(7)

Furthermore, one can expand the intrinsic surface quantity
\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{u}
in Eulerian terms:

\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{u}=\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}+\mathbf{w}\cdot\nabla u.

(8)

Identity (
8
) allows us to rewrite (
5
) as follows:

\left\{\begin{split}\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}+\mathbf{w}\cdot\nabla u&=-\epsilon^{-2}f(u)+\Delta_{\Gamma}u\quad\text{on}~~\Gamma(t),\\
\nabla u\cdot\nabla\phi&=0\quad\text{in}~~\mathcal{O}(\Gamma(t))\end{split}~~t\in(0,T],\right.

(9)

subject to
u=u_{0}
on
\Gamma(0)
.
This formulation will be useful for the design of a finite element method in Section
5
.
We note that equalities (
7
)–(
8
) are valid for any smooth extension (not necessarily a normal one).

3
Asymptotic analysis

In this section, we study an asymptotic behaviour of
u
solving (
5
)
when
\varepsilon
goes to zero. Our analysis follows the inner-outer expansion arguments, which are now standard for phase-field equations defined on Euclidean domains in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d=2,3
,
[
4
,
5
,
41
]
and also has been used recently to study sharp interface limits of two phase-field models defined on surfaces
[
11
,
36
]
.

We assume
t\geq t_{0}
sufficiently large such that the separation of phases happened and

exhibits an inner layer (diffuse interface) of width
O(\varepsilon)
. Consider the central line of the diffuse interface defined as the zero level of

\gamma(t):=\{\mathbf{x}\in\Gamma(t)\,:\,u(\mathbf{x},t)=0\}
. For all
t\in(t_{0},T)
we assume that
\gamma(t)
is a smooth closed curve on
\Gamma(t)
. The interior and exterior domains with respect to
\gamma(t)
are denoted by
\Gamma^{\pm}(t):=\{\mathbf{x}\in\Gamma(t)\,:\,\pm u(\mathbf{x},t)>0\}
.

Outer expansion.
Denote by
u^{\pm}
the order parameter restricted to
\Gamma^{\pm}
.
Following, e.g.,
[
5
]
we assume that away from the interfacial layer around
\gamma(t)
, both
u^{\pm}

can be expanded in the form

u^{\pm}(\mathbf{x},t)=u^{\pm}_{0}(\mathbf{x},t)+\varepsilon u^{\pm}_{1}(\mathbf{x},t)+\cdots,\quad

(10)

with smooth
u^{\pm}_{k}(\mathbf{x},t)
.
Substituting (
10
) into (
5
) and using
the Taylor expansion for
f(u)
,

f(u^{\pm})=f(u_{0}^{\pm})+\varepsilon f^{\prime}(u_{0}^{\pm})u^{\pm}_{1}+\cdots,

yield

\displaystyle\big(\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{u}_{0}^{\pm}(\mathbf{x},t)+\varepsilon\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{u}_{1}^{\pm}(\mathbf{x},t)+\cdots\big)-\Delta_{\Gamma}\big(u_{0}^{\pm}(\mathbf{x},t)+\varepsilon u_{1}^{\pm}(\mathbf{x},t)+\cdots\big)+\varepsilon^{-2}\big(f(u_{0}^{\pm})+\varepsilon f^{\prime}(u_{0}^{\pm})u_{1}+\cdots\big)=0.

Considering the leading order term with respect to

gives

f(u_{0}^{\pm})=0.

Therefore, away from the layer it holds

u_{0}^{\pm}(\mathbf{x},t)=\pm 1.

(11)

Inner expansion.
Denote by
d_{\gamma}
the signed geodesic distance on
\Gamma(t)
for any fixed
t
, and
\pm d_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x})>0
for
\mathbf{x}\in\Gamma^{\pm}
.
Consider the inner layer
U_{\varepsilon}(\gamma(t))
, which we define as an

\gamma(t)

U_{\varepsilon}(\gamma(t)):=\{\mathbf{x}\in\Gamma(t)\,:\,|d_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x})|\leq c_{0}\,\varepsilon\}
, with sufficiently large
c_{0}
, independent of
\varepsilon
. We assume
\varepsilon
to be sufficiently small such that the geodesic closest point projection
\mathbf{q}(\mathbf{x}):\,U_{\varepsilon}(\gamma(t))\to\gamma(t)
is well-defined so that
(\mathbf{q}(\mathbf{x}),d_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x}))
is the local (time dependent) coordinate system in
U_{\varepsilon}(\gamma(t))
. In
U_{\varepsilon}(\gamma(t))
the conormal directions are defined by the tangential vector field
\mathbf{m}=\nabla_{\Gamma}d_{\gamma}
. For
\mathbf{x}\in\gamma(t)
,
\mathbf{m}(\mathbf{x})
is a unit conormal of
\gamma(t)
pointing into
\Gamma^{+}(t)
.

Following
[
5
,
41
]
, we introduce a fast variable in
U_{\varepsilon}(\gamma(t))
by re-scaling the coordinate in the conormal direction
\xi=\frac{d_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x})}{\varepsilon}
, and represent
u(\mathbf{x},t)
as

u(\mathbf{x},t)=\tilde{u}(\mathbf{x},\xi,t)\quad\text{for}~\mathbf{x}\in U_{\varepsilon}(\gamma(t)),

(12)

where
\tilde{u}(t):\,U_{\varepsilon}(\gamma(t))\times(-c_{0},c_{0})\to\mathbb{R}
is defined as
\tilde{u}(\mathbf{y},\xi,t):=u(\mathbf{x},t)
for
\mathbf{x}\in U_{\varepsilon}(\gamma(t))
such that
\mathbf{q}(\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{q}(\mathbf{x})
and
\xi=d_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x})/{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\varepsilon}
.
Given the new variables we find the identities:

\nabla_{\Gamma}u=\nabla^{\mathbf{x}}_{\Gamma}\tilde{u}+\varepsilon^{-1}\partial_{\xi}\tilde{u}\,\mathbf{m},\quad\Delta_{\Gamma}u=\Delta^{\mathbf{x}}_{\Gamma}\tilde{u}+\varepsilon^{-2}\partial_{\xi\xi}\tilde{u}+\varepsilon^{-1}\partial_{\xi}\tilde{u}\Delta_{\Gamma}d_{\gamma},

(13)

where for the second equality we used
\mathbf{m}\cdot\nabla^{\mathbf{x}}_{\Gamma}\tilde{u}=0
and
\mbox{div}_{\Gamma}\mathbf{m}=\Delta_{\Gamma}d_{\gamma}

(same identities in terms of fast and slow surface variables are deduced by slightly different arguments in
[
36
]
and
[
14
]
)
.
Denoting by
\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{\tilde{u}}
the material derivative of
\tilde{u}(\mathbf{y},\xi,t)
we also compute

\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{{u}}=\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{\tilde{u}}+\varepsilon^{-1}\partial_{\xi}\tilde{u}\,\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{d_{\gamma}}.

(14)

We assume that
\tilde{u}
in the layer can be expanded

\tilde{u}(\mathbf{x},\xi,t)=\tilde{u}_{0}(\mathbf{x},\xi,t)+\varepsilon\tilde{u}_{1}(\mathbf{x},\xi,t)+\cdots,

with smooth
\tilde{u}_{0},\tilde{u}_{1},\dots
.
Substituting this in (
5
), using (
13
)–(
14
) and Taylor expansion for
f(\tilde{u})
,
i.e.
f(\tilde{u})=f(\tilde{u}_{0})+\varepsilon f^{\prime}(\tilde{u}_{0})\tilde{u}_{1}+\cdots
,
we find that

-\partial_{\xi\xi}\tilde{u}_{0}+f(\tilde{u}_{0})=0.

(15)

Accounting for

order terms we obtain

\partial_{\xi}\tilde{u}_{0}\left(\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{d_{\gamma}}-\Delta_{\Gamma}d_{\gamma}\right)-2\nabla_{\Gamma}d_{\gamma}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}(\partial_{\xi}\tilde{u}_{0})-\partial_{\xi\xi}\tilde{u}_{1}+f^{\prime}(\tilde{u}_{0})\tilde{u}_{1}=0.

To proceed we need conditions on
\tilde{u}_{0}
for

(which can be allowed if

).

Matching conditions.
We now have a representation of the solution in the narrow layer around
\gamma(t)
and another representation valid away from the interface. Following

,

]
we consider matching conditions between these two representations. We formulate the conditions below, while details of derivation can be found in

]

Denote
u^{\pm}_{k}(\mathbf{x},t)=\lim\limits_{s\rightarrow\pm 0}u^{\pm}_{k}(\mathbf{x}+s\mathbf{m},t)

\displaystyle\tilde{u}_{0}(\mathbf{x},\xi,t)

\displaystyle=u_{0}^{\pm}(\mathbf{x},t),

\displaystyle\hbox{as }\xi\rightarrow\pm\infty,~\varepsilon\xi\rightarrow 0

(17)

\displaystyle\tilde{u}_{1}(\mathbf{x},\xi,t)

\displaystyle=u_{1}^{\pm}(\mathbf{x},t)+\xi\mathbf{m}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}u_{0}^{\pm}(\mathbf{x},t),

\displaystyle\hbox{as }\xi\rightarrow\pm\infty,~\varepsilon\xi\rightarrow 0

\displaystyle\partial_{\xi}\tilde{u}_{1}(\mathbf{x},\xi,t)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{m}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}u_{0}^{\pm}(\mathbf{x},t),

From condition (

where

\partial_{\xi}\tilde{u}_{1}=0
for

. Using these limit values for the integration by parts, we obtain

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!\partial_{\xi}\tilde{u}_{0}[\partial_{\xi\xi}\tilde{u}_{1}-f^{\prime}(\tilde{u}_{0})\tilde{u}_{1}]d\xi=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!\partial_{\xi}\tilde{u}_{0}\partial_{\xi\xi}\tilde{u}_{1}-\partial_{\xi}f(\tilde{u}_{0})\tilde{u}_{1}d\xi=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\![\partial_{\xi\xi}\tilde{u}_{0}-f(\tilde{u}_{0})]\partial_{\xi}\tilde{u}_{1}d\xi=0,

where for the last equality we use (

Equation (

\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{d_{\gamma}}-\Delta_{\Gamma}d_{\gamma}=0.

(22)

Consider the limiting interface
\gamma(t)
as the zero level of the order-parameter as

.
Equation (

\gamma(t)
on the passively evolving material surface
\Gamma(t)
.
The quantity
\Delta_{\Gamma}d_{\gamma}=\kappa_{g}
is
the geodesic curvature of
\gamma(t)
on
\Gamma(t)
satisfying that
\kappa_{g}(\mathbf{x})
is positive when
\Gamma(t)^{-}
is convex
at
\mathbf{x}
. While
\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{d_{\gamma}}=0
corresponds to the passive evolution along material trajectories,
\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{d_{\gamma}}=\kappa_{g}
can be seen as an active evolution or a mean curvature type flow in the reference (Lagrangian) coordinates. The (tangential) geometric evolution of the sharp interface is defined by the conormal velocity of
\gamma(t)
given by
\mathbf{m}\cdot\mathbf{w}-\kappa_{g}
.

4
Weak formulation and well-posedness

Consider a slightly more general problem:

\displaystyle\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{u}+\alpha u-\Delta_{\Gamma}u+\varepsilon^{-2}{f(u)}=0\quad\text{on}~~\Gamma(t),

(23)

\displaystyle u(\mathbf{x},0)=u_{0}\quad\text{for}~~\mathbf{x}\in\Gamma(0),

(24)

with an
L^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})
function
\alpha
, and let
\alpha_{\infty}:=\|\alpha\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})}
. Following
[
46
]
we consider a modified double-well potential
{F}
such that for some
M>1

\displaystyle{F}^{\prime}(x)={f}(x)=\begin{cases}({3M^{2}-1})x-2M^{3},\quad x>M,\\
x(x^{2}-1),\qquad\qquad\quad x\in[-M,M],\\
({3M^{2}-1})x+2M^{3},\quad x<-M.\end{cases}

(25)

Function
f(x)
satisfies the following growth conditions with
L=3M^{2}-1

\displaystyle|{f}(x)|\leq L|x|,\quad f(x)x\geq-x,

(26)

and Lipschitz condition:

\displaystyle-1\leq\frac{{f}(x)-{f}(y)}{x-y}\leq{}L,\quad\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R},~x\neq y.

(27)

Given our assumptions on the evolution of
\Gamma(t)
, the scalar product

(u,v)_{0}=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Gamma(t)}uv\,dsdt

induces a norm
\|\cdot\|_{0}
on
L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
equivalent to the standard
L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
-norm.
Besides standard Lebesgue spaces
L^{q}(\mathcal{G})
,
1\leq q\leq\infty
, and Sobolev spaces
H^{k}(\mathcal{G})
,
k=1,2,\dots
, we need the following analogues of standard Bochner spaces:

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=\{u\in L^{2}(\mathcal{G})\,:\,\|\nabla_{\Gamma}u\|_{0}<\infty\},\quad\text{with}~(u,v)_{H}=(u,v)_{0}+(\nabla_{\Gamma}u,\nabla_{\Gamma}v)_{0},

\displaystyle L^{\infty}_{1}

\displaystyle=\{u\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})\,:\,\mbox{ess}\sup\limits_{t\in[0,T]}\|\nabla_{\Gamma}u\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t))}\leq\infty\},

\displaystyle W

\displaystyle=\{u\in L^{\infty}_{1}\,:\,\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{u}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{G})\},\quad\|u\|^{2}_{W}=\|u\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}_{1}}+\|\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{u}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathcal{G})}.

From
[
39
,
9
]
we know that
H
is a Hilbert space and smooth functions are everywhere dense in
H
and
W
.

Exploiting the smoothness properties of the mapping
\Psi
between
\Gamma_{0}\times(0,T)
and
\mathcal{G}
one shows (cf.
[
39
,
9
]
) that the following isomorphisms hold algebraically and topologically:
H\cong L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Gamma{}_{0}))
and
W\cong L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}(\Gamma{}_{0}))\cap H^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Gamma{}_{0}))
.

We consider the following weak formulation of (
23
): For
u_{0}\in H^{1}(\Gamma_{0})
, find
u\in{W}
such that
u(0)=u_{0}
and

(\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{u},v)_{0}+(\alpha u+\varepsilon^{-2}{f(u)},v)_{0}+(\nabla_{\Gamma}u,\nabla_{\Gamma}v)_{0}=0,\quad\text{for all }v\in H.

(28)

Lemma 1
.

The week formulation (
28
) is well posed.

Proof.

A standard approach to the analysis of Allen-Cahn type equations solvability is based on the energy minimization principle, which does not hold in the case of equations posed in the evolving domain.
Hence we consider a different argument. For
\hat{u}=u\circ\Psi\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}(\Gamma{}_{0}))\cap H^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Gamma{}_{0}))
,
v\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Gamma{}_{0}))
we rewrite (
28
) in the reference cylinder
\widehat{S}=\Gamma{}_{0}\times(0,T)
:

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Gamma_{0}}\left\{(\hat{u}_{t}+\alpha\hat{u}+\varepsilon^{-2}f(\hat{u}))\hat{v}+(\nabla_{\Gamma}F)^{-T}\nabla_{\Gamma}\hat{u}:(\nabla_{\Gamma}F)^{-T}\nabla_{\Gamma}\hat{v}\right\}\mu d\hat{s}dt=0,

(29)

for all
\hat{v}\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Gamma{}_{0}))
. Here
\mu\in C^{1}(\overline{\widehat{S}})
,
\nabla_{\Gamma}\Psi\in C^{1}(\overline{\widehat{S}})^{3\times 3}
are such that
\mu>0
and
\nabla_{\Gamma}\Psi(\nabla_{\Gamma}\Psi)^{T}
is uniformly bounded on
\widehat{S}
. Therefore, the problem (
29
) can be formulated to fit an abstract framework from
[
44
]
:
Find
\hat{u}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}(\Gamma{}_{0}))\cap H^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Gamma{}_{0}))
such that
\hat{u}(0)=u_{0}\in H^{1}(\Gamma{}_{0})
and

M\hat{u}^{\prime}+B\hat{u}+\gamma(\hat{u})=0\quad\text{in}~H^{-1}(\Gamma{}_{0})\quad\text{for a.e.}~t\in[0,T],

(30)

where operators
M\in\mathcal{L}(L^{2}(\Gamma_{0}))
,
B\in\mathcal{L}(H^{1}(\Gamma{}_{0}),H^{-1}(\Gamma{}_{0}))
and
\gamma:H^{1}(\Gamma{}_{0})\to L^{2}(\Gamma{}_{0})
are defined by the identities

\displaystyle M\hat{w}=\mu\hat{w},\quad\langle B\hat{u},\hat{v}\rangle=\int_{\Gamma_{0}}(\nabla_{\Gamma}\Psi)^{-T}\nabla_{\Gamma}\hat{u}:(\nabla_{\Gamma}\Psi)^{-T}\nabla_{\Gamma}\hat{v}\mu d\hat{s},\quad\gamma(\hat{u})=(\alpha\hat{u}+\varepsilon^{-2}f(\hat{u}))\mu,

for all
\hat{w}\in L^{2}(\Gamma_{0})
,
\hat{u},\hat{v}\in H^{1}(\Gamma{}_{0})
.
It is easy to verify that
M
is positive definite,
B
is such that

\langle Bv,v\rangle\geq\hat{c}_{1}\|v\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\Gamma{}_{0})}-\hat{c}_{2}\|v\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma{}_{0})},\quad\text{with some}~\hat{c}_{1}>0,~\hat{c}_{2}\geq 0,

(31)

and
\gamma
is continuous and, thanks to (
26
),

\begin{split}\|\gamma(v)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma{}_{0})}&\leq\hat{C}_{1}+\hat{C}_{2}\|v\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\Gamma{}_{0})},\quad\text{with some}~\hat{C}_{1}>0,~\hat{C}_{2}>0,\\
(\gamma(v)-\gamma(w),v-w)_{L^{2}(\Gamma{}_{0})}&\geq-\hat{C}_{0}\|v-w\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma{}_{0})},\quad\text{with some}~\hat{C}_{0}>0,\end{split}

(32)

for all
v,w\in H^{1}(\Gamma{}_{0})
.

Problem (
30
)–(
32
) is well posed (
[
44
, Theorem 2.1]
) and so is (
28
).
∎

5
Discretization method

To set up a numerical method, one needs to define a time-stepping procedure, spatial discretization approach and a practical way of handling surface integrals and derivatives. The approach taken here benefits from the embedding of
\Gamma(t)
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
for all
t\in[0,T]
, which allows to use tangential calculus in an ambient (bulk) functional space (rather than computations in intrinsic time-dependent surface coordinates). The bulk space supports well-defined traces of functions on
\Gamma(t)
and functions from the bulk space are further approximated in a standard time-independent finite element space. Our time-stepping procedure exploits an observation made earlier in section
2.1
that a function on
\Gamma(t)
can be identified with its smooth extension to a neighborhood of the surface. Finally, the geometry representation is based on the implicit definition of
\Gamma_{h}(t)
, an approximation of
\Gamma(t)
, as a zero level of a finite element function.
Altogether, this approach resembles the trace finite element method for partial differential equations on evolving surfaces introduced and analyzed in
[
40
,
29
]
for the diffusion problem on
\Gamma(t)
. The approach is also known as a hybrid FD in time – trace FEM in space, since a (standard) finite difference scheme is adopted for treating the time dependence and an unfitted finite element method is used in space.

We start with explaining the time-stepping method.

5.1
Time-stepping scheme

Consider a uniformly distributed time nodes
t_{n}=n\Delta t
,
n=0,\dots,N
, with the uniform time step
\Delta t=T/N
.
It is crucial to assume that
\Delta t
is sufficiently small that

\Gamma(t_{n})\subset\mathcal{O}(\Gamma(t_{n-1}))\quad~n=1,\dots,N.~

(33)

Recall that
\mathcal{O}(\Gamma(t))
is a neighborhood of the surface, where the normal projection on
\Gamma(t)
is well defined, and so are the extensions of surface quantities.

Using the notation
u^{n}
for an approximation to
u(t_{n})
, and
\phi^{n}=\phi(t_{n})
, we consider the following semi-implicit first order method for the Eulerian formulation (
9
) of the Allen-Cahn surface problem:

\left\{\begin{split}(1+\beta_{s}\Delta t)\frac{u^{n}-u^{n-1}}{\Delta t}+\mathbf{w}^{n}\cdot\nabla u^{n}-\Delta_{\Gamma}u^{n}&=-\varepsilon^{-2}f(u^{n-1})\quad\text{on}~~\Gamma(t_{n}),\\
\nabla u^{n}\cdot\nabla\phi^{n}&=0\quad\text{in}~~\mathcal{O}(\Gamma(t_{n})).\end{split}\right.

(34)

Here
\beta_{s}>0
is a stabilization parameter as suggested in
[
46
]
to allow the explicit treatment of the non-linear part on the right-hand side of (
34
). This leads to a linear problem with respect to
u^{n}
on each time step. More important is that the function
u^{n-1}
is well-defined on
\Gamma(t_{n})
through its extension. Indeed, if one considers (
34
) with index shifted
n\to n-1
, i.e. eq. (
34
) written for the previous time step, then the second equation defines the extension of
u^{n-1}
to
\mathcal{O}(\Gamma(t_{n-1}))
and because of (
33
) it defines an extension to
\Gamma(t_{n})\subset\mathcal{O}(\Gamma(t_{n-1}))
. Therefore, all terms in (
34
) on the current step are well defined.

For a finite element method, we shall need the integral formulation of (
34
), where we enforce the second equation weakly, as a constraint: Any smooth
u^{n}
solving (
34
) satisfies

\int_{\Gamma(t_{n})}\!\!\big((1+\beta_{s}\Delta t)\frac{u^{n}-u^{n-1}}{\Delta t}+\mathbf{w}^{n}\cdot\nabla u^{n}\big)v\,ds+\int_{\Gamma(t_{n})}\!\nabla_{\Gamma}u^{n}\,\!\cdot\!\nabla_{\Gamma}v\,ds\\
+\rho\int_{\mathcal{O}(\Gamma(t_{n}))}(\nabla u^{n}\cdot\nabla\phi^{n})(\nabla v\cdot\nabla\phi^{n})\,dx=-\varepsilon^{-2}\!\!\int_{\Gamma(t_{n})}\!\!f(u^{n-1})v\,ds,

(35)

for all sufficiently smooth test functions
v:\mathcal{O}(\Gamma(t_{n}))\to\mathbb{R}
.
\rho>0
is an augmentation parameter for the normal extension condition,
and
\mathbf{w}_{T}=\mathbf{w}-(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{n})\mathbf{n}
is the tangential part of
\mathbf{w}
.

We need the integration by parts identity:

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma(t)}(\mathbf{w}\cdot\nabla u)v\,ds

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Gamma(t)}({\mathbf{w}_{T}}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}uv-{\mathbf{w}_{T}}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}vu)\,ds-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Gamma(t)}(\operatorname{\rm div}_{\Gamma}{\mathbf{w}_{T}})uv\,ds

(36)

for sufficiently smooth
u,v
such that
\mathbf{n}\cdot\nabla u=\mathbf{n}\cdot\nabla v=0
(recall that
\mathbf{n}=\nabla\phi
on
\Gamma
).

5.2
Finite element method

To reduce the repeated use of generic but unspecified constants, further in the paper we write
x\lesssim y
to state that the inequality
x\leq cy
holds for quantities
x
,
y
with a constant
c
, which is independent of the mesh parameters
h
,
\Delta t
, time
instance
t_{n}
, and the position of
\Gamma
and
\Gamma_{h}
in the bulk mesh. Similarly we give sense to
x\gtrsim y
.

Consider a family
\{\mathcal{T}_{h}\}_{h>0}
of shape-regular consistent triangulations of the bulk domain
\Omega
, with
\max\limits_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\mbox{diam}(T)\leq h
. The bulk triangulation supports a standard finite element space of piecewise polynomial
continuous functions of a fixed degree
k\geq 1
:

V_{h}=\{v_{h}\in C(\Omega)\,:\,v_{h}|_{S}\in P_{k}(S),\forall S\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\}.

(37)

We next approximate the sign distance function
\phi
by a finite element distance function
\phi_{h}

of degree
q
, i.e.
\phi_{h}\in V_{h}
for
k=q
, such that

\|\phi-\phi_{h}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{O}(\Gamma(t)))}+h\|\nabla(\phi-\phi_{h})\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{O}(\Gamma(t)))}\lesssim\,h^{q+1},\quad\forall~t\in[0,T],

(38)

where we need to assume
\phi\in C^{q+1}(\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{G}))
.
Following
[
28
]
, we also assume that
\nabla\phi_{h}(\mathbf{x},t)\neq 0
in
\mathcal{O}(\Gamma(t))
,
t\in[0,T]
, and that on every time interval
I_{n}=[t_{n-1},t_{n}]
there holds

\displaystyle\|\phi_{h}^{n-1}-\phi_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\,\Delta t\|\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{n}\|_{\infty,I_{n}},

(39a)

\displaystyle\|\nabla\phi_{h}^{n-1}-\nabla\phi_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\,\Delta t\left(\|\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{n}\|_{\infty,I_{n}}+\|\nabla(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{n})\|_{\infty,I_{n}}\right),\text{ for }n=1,\dots,N,

(39b)

where
\phi_{h}^{n}(\mathbf{x})=\phi_{h}(\mathbf{x},t_{n}),~n=0,\dots,N
, and

\|v\|_{\infty,I_{n}}:=\sup\limits_{t\in I_{n}}\|v\|_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma(t))},

for
v
defined on
\Gamma(t)
.

We now introduce the “discrete” surfaces
\Gamma^{n}_{h}
as the zero level of
\phi_{h}^{n}
,

\Gamma^{n}_{h}:=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\,:\,\phi_{h}^{n}(\mathbf{x})=0\}.

Thanks to (
38
) it approximates the original surface
\Gamma
in the following sense

\operatorname{dist}(\Gamma^{n}_{h},\Gamma(t_{h}))=\max_{x\in\Gamma^{n}_{h}}|\phi^{n}(\mathbf{x})|=\max_{x\in\Gamma^{n}_{h}}|\phi^{n}(\mathbf{x})-\phi_{h}^{n}(\mathbf{x})|\leq\|\phi^{n}-\phi_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\lesssim h^{q+1}.

(40)

For the normal vector to
\Gamma^{n}_{h}
,
\mathbf{n}_{h}^{n}=\nabla\phi_{h}^{n}/|\nabla\phi_{h}^{n}|
, and the extended normal vector to
\Gamma(t_{n})
,
\mathbf{n}^{n}=\nabla\phi^{n}
, the following consistency bound follows from (
38
):

|\mathbf{n}_{h}^{n}(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{n}^{n}(\mathbf{x})|\lesssim|\nabla\phi_{h}^{n}(\mathbf{x})-\nabla\phi^{n}(\mathbf{x})|\lesssim h^{q},\quad\mathbf{x}\in\Gamma^{n}_{h}.

(41)

For practical reasons, the finite element method does not look for an extension of the discrete solution to the whole neighborhood
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{G})
. Instead it provides an extension to a
narrow
band around
\Gamma^{n}_{h}
. For each
n
, the extension band consists of all tetrahedra on a
\delta_{n}
distance from
\Gamma^{n}_{h}
, for

\delta_{n}:=c_{\delta}\|\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{n}\|_{\infty,I_{n}}~\Delta t

(42)

and
c_{\delta}\geq 1
, an
O(1)
mesh-independent constant. More precisely, we define the
mesh-dependent
narrow band as

{\mathcal{O}}_{h}(\Gamma^{n}_{h})={\bigcup}\left\{\overline{S}\,:\,S\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\,:|\phi_{h}^{n}(\mathbf{x})|\leq\delta_{n}\text{ for some }\mathbf{x}\in S\right\}.

We also need a subdomain of
{\mathcal{O}}_{h}(\Gamma^{n}_{h})
only consisting of tetrahedra intersected by
\Gamma^{n}_{h}
,

{\mathcal{O}}_{\Gamma}(\Gamma^{n}_{h}):={\bigcup}\left\{\overline{S}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\,:\,S\cap\Gamma^{n}_{h}\neq\emptyset\right\}.

Since
\mbox{dist}(\Gamma^{n}_{h},\Gamma(t_{n}))\lesssim h^{q+1}
, the narrow band width
\delta_{n}
and
h
can be assumed small enough such that

{\mathcal{O}}_{h}(\Gamma^{n}_{h})\subset\mathcal{O}(\Gamma(t_{n})).

(43)

This and (
42
)

implies the restriction on the time step of the form

\Delta t\leq c_{0}(c_{\delta}\|\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{n}\|_{\infty,I_{n}})^{-1}=O(1),~n=1,\dots,N,

(44)

with some
c_{0}
sufficiently small, but independent of
h
,
\Delta t
and
n
.

On one time step from
t_{n-1}
to
t_{n}
, the surface may travel up to
\Delta t\|\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{n}\|_{\infty,I_{n}}
distance in normal directions, which is thus the maximum distance from
\Gamma_{h}^{n}
to
\Gamma_{h}^{n-1}
.
Therefore
,
c_{\delta}
can be taken sufficiently large, but independent of
h
, such that

{\mathcal{O}_{\Gamma}}(\Gamma^{n}_{h})\subset{\mathcal{O}}_{h}(\Gamma^{n-1}_{h}).

(45)

To see this, one applies (
42
) to determine
\delta_{n-1}
, which in turn defines
{\mathcal{O}}_{h}(\Gamma^{n-1}_{h})
.

This condition is the discrete analog of (
33
) and it is essential for the well-posedness of the finite element formulation below.

Next we define test and trial finite element spaces of degree
m\geq 1
as restrictions of the time-independent bulk space
V_{h}
,
k=m
, on all tetrahedra from
{\mathcal{O}}(\Gamma^{n}_{h})
:

V_{h}^{n}=\{v\in C({\mathcal{O}}_{h}(\Gamma^{n}_{h}))\,:\,v\in P_{m}(S),~\forall S\in\mathcal{T}_{h},S\subset{\mathcal{O}}(\Gamma^{n}_{h})\},\quad m\geq 1.

(46)

We further use
V_{h}^{n}
as test and trial spaces in the integral formulation (
35
), where we use identity (
36
) and replace
\Gamma(t_{n})
by
\Gamma^{n}_{h}
,
\mathcal{O}(\Gamma(t_{n}))
by
{\mathcal{O}}_{h}(\Gamma^{n}_{h})
. The resulting FE formulation reads:
For a given
u_{h}^{0}\in V_{h}^{0}
find
u_{h}^{n}\in V_{h}^{n}
,
n=1,\dots,N
, solving

\!\!\int\limits_{\Gamma^{n}_{h}}\!\left\{\!\!(1+\beta_{s}\Delta{}t)\frac{u_{h}^{n}-u_{h}^{n-1}}{\Delta t}v_{h}+\frac{1}{2}\left({\mathbf{w}_{T}^{e}}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}u^{n}_{h}v_{h}-{\mathbf{w}_{T}^{e}}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}v_{h}u_{h}^{n}-(\operatorname{\rm div}_{\Gamma_{h}}{\mathbf{w}_{T}^{e}})u_{h}^{n}v_{h}\right)\!\right\}\!ds_{h}\\
+\int\limits_{\Gamma^{n}_{h}}\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}u^{n}_{h}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}v_{h}\,ds_{h}+\rho_{n}\int\limits_{{\mathcal{O}}_{h}(\Gamma^{n}_{h})}(\mathbf{n}_{h}^{n}\cdot\nabla u_{h}^{n})(\mathbf{n}_{h}^{n}\cdot\nabla v_{h})d\mathbf{x}=-\varepsilon^{-2}\!\!\int\limits_{\Gamma^{n}_{h}}\!\!f(u_{h}^{n-1})v_{h}\,ds_{h},

(47)

for all
v_{h}\in V_{h}^{n}
. Here
\mathbf{n}_{h}=\nabla\phi_{h}^{n}/|\nabla\phi_{h}^{n}|
in
{\mathcal{O}}_{h}(\Gamma^{n}_{h})
,
\rho_{n}>0
is a parameter,
\mathbf{w}^{e}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{p}^{n}(\mathbf{x}))
is a lifted data on
\Gamma^{n}_{h}
from
\Gamma(t_{n})
. The terms involving
u^{n-1}
are well-defined thanks to condition (
45
).
With suitable restrictions on problem parameters the last term on the left-hand side of (
47
) ensures the whole bilinear form is elliptic on
V_{h}^{n}
; see (
59
). Therefore, on each time step we obtain a FE solution defined in
{\mathcal{O}}_{h}(\Gamma^{n}_{h})
(not just on
\Gamma^{n}_{h}
and this can be seen as an implicit extension procedure).
As discussed in many places in the literature, see, e.g.
[
29
]
, this term also stabilizes the problem algebraically, i.e. the resulting systems of algebraic equations are well-conditioned independent on how the surface
\Gamma_{h}
cuts through the ambient triangulation.

We finally note that an accurate integration over
\Gamma^{n}_{h}
may be not feasible using standard quadrature rules for higher than second order surface representation, i.e. for
q>1
. More sophisticated numerical integration techniques should be applied as discussed in the literature
[
13
,
27
,
17
,
35
,
43
,
48
]
.

6
Analysis of the finite element method

In this section we address stability and error analysis of the finite element formulation (

).
For a proper control of the geometric error, the analysis requires the following mild restriction on the mesh step,

h^{2q}\lesssim\Delta t.

(48)

We recall that
q\geq 1
is the degree of geometry approximation from (
38
).

We shall need the following two Lemmas from
[
29
]
. The result of the first lemma allows the control of the

in the narrow band by its

and a term similar to the normal volume stabilization in (

by its values on

Lemma 2
.

Assume conditions (

) and (

\|v_{h}\|_{{\mathcal{O}}_{h}(\Gamma^{n}_{h})}^{2}\lesssim(\delta_{n}+h)\|v_{h}\|_{\Gamma^{n}_{h}}^{2}+(\delta_{n}+h)^{2}\|\mathbf{n}_{h}^{n}\cdot\nabla v_{h}\|_{{\mathcal{O}}_{h}(\Gamma^{n}_{h})}^{2}.

(49)

Lemma 3
.

In addition to (
42
) and (
44
) assume (

) is satisfied.

Assume
\mathcal{V}_{h}
is a subset of

that supports the following inequalities:

\|\nabla v\|_{S}\leq Ch^{-1}\|v\|_{S},\quad\|\nabla v\|_{D}\leq C|D||S|^{-1}\|\nabla v\|_{S},\quad\|\mathbf{n}_{h}^{n-1}\cdot\nabla v\|_{D}\leq C|D||S|^{-1}\|\mathbf{n}_{h}^{n-1}\cdot\nabla v\|_{S},

(50)

for all

,

,

, where

is a subdomain in

, and

Then for any

\|v\|_{\Gamma^{n}_{h}}^{2}\leq(1+c_{1}\Delta t)\|v\|_{\Gamma^{n-1}_{h}}^{2}+c_{2}\delta_{n-1}(\delta_{n-1}+h)^{-1}\|\mathbf{n}_{h}^{n-1}\cdot\nabla v\|_{{\mathcal{O}}_{h}(\Gamma^{n-1}_{h})}^{2},

(51)

for some

and

independent of

,
\Delta t
and

.

Proof.

For
\mathcal{V}_{h}=V_{h}^{n}
the result is found as Lemma 9 in
[

]
. The examination of the proof reveals that
inequalities in (

) are the only assumptions required to extend the result from

to a more general subset of

.

6.1
Stability analysis

In addition to (
44
), we need another
O(1)
restriction on the time step:

\Delta t\leq(4\xi_{h})^{-1}\text{ with }\xi_{h}:=\frac{1}{2}\max_{n=0,..,N}\|\operatorname{\rm div}_{\Gamma_{h}}{\mathbf{w}_{T}^{e}}\|_{{\infty},\Gamma^{n}_{h}}.

(52)

6.2
Consistency estimate

For parameter

&\quad+\underset{I_{3,b}}{\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Gamma(t_{n})}\ \operatorname{\rm div}_{\Gamma}({\mathbf{w}_{T}})u^{n}v_{h}^{\ell}\,ds-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Gamma^{n}_{h}}\operatorname{\rm div}_{\Gamma_{h}}({\mathbf{w}_{T}^{e}})u^{n}v_{h}\,ds_{h}}}\\
&\quad+\underset{I_{4}}{\underbrace{\int_{\Gamma^{n}_{h}}\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}u^{n}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}v_{h}\,ds_{h}-\int_{\Gamma(t_{n})}\nabla_{\Gamma}u^{n}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}v_{h}^{\ell}\,ds}}\\

Lemma 5
.

Let

The consistency error satisfies the bound

|\mathcal{E}_{C}^{n}(v_{h})|\lesssim(\Delta t+h^{q})\|u\|_{W^{2,\infty}(\mathcal{G})}\left(\|v_{h}\|_{\Gamma^{n}_{h}}+\|\nabla_{\Gamma}v_{h}\|_{\Gamma^{n}_{h}}+\rho_{n}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|(\mathbf{n}_{h}^{n}\cdot\nabla v_{h})\|_{{\mathcal{O}}_{h}(\Gamma^{n}_{h})}\right).

The required estimate for

is found in
[
29
]
. The last term

gets estimated as

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{2}|I_{5}|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{\Gamma^{n}_{h}}\left(f(u^{n-1})-f(u^{n})\right)v_{h}\,ds_{h}+\int_{\Gamma^{n}_{h}}f(u^{n})v_{h}\,ds_{h}-\int_{\Gamma(t_{n})}f(u^{n})v_{h}^{\ell}\,ds\right|

\beta_{s}
leads to

\|e_{h}^{n}\|^{2}_{\Gamma^{n}_{h}}+{\Delta t}\|\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}e_{h}^{n}\|^{2}_{\Gamma^{n}_{h}}+{\Delta t}{\rho_{n}}\|\mathbf{n}_{h}^{n}\cdot\nabla e_{h}^{n}\|_{{\mathcal{O}}_{h}(\Gamma^{n}_{h})}^{2}\leq{}(1+c\Delta{}t)\|e_{h}^{n-1}\|_{\Gamma^{n-1}_{h}}^{2}\\
+\Delta t\rho_{n-1}\|\mathbf{n}_{h}^{n-1}\cdot\nabla e_{h}^{n-1}\|_{{\mathcal{O}}_{h}(\Gamma^{n-1}_{h})}^{2}+c\Delta{}t\,\|u\|_{W^{m+1,\infty}(\mathcal{G})}^{2}(\Delta t^{2}+h^{2q}+h^{2m}),

with a constant
c
independent of
h
,
\Delta t
,
n
and of the position of the surface in the background mesh.
Applying the discrete Gronwall inequality proves the theorem.

We give a brief derivation of (
76
).
On a sphere of a varying radius
R(t)
consider a circle
C(t)
of radius
r(t)
(see Figure
3
).
Assume the circle evolves according to the geodesic curvature flow given by (
22
).
The geodesic curvature can be computed as the curvature of the circle projection on the tangential planes:

\kappa_{g}=\frac{1}{r}\cos\theta=\frac{\sqrt{R^{2}-r^{2}}}{rR}.

This determines the co-normal velocity of
C(t)
, while the normal velocity is given by
{R}_{t}
.
Therefore, the material velocity of the points on
C(t)
is given by

-\frac{\sqrt{R^{2}-r^{2}}}{rR}\mathbf{m}+{R}_{t}\mathbf{n}.

(77)

Then the time derivative of the radius
r
can be explicitly computed to be

r_{t}=-\frac{\sqrt{R^{2}-r^{2}}}{rR}\cos\theta+{R}_{t}\sin\theta=\frac{r^{2}-R^{2}}{rR^{2}}+\frac{r}{R}R_{t}.
