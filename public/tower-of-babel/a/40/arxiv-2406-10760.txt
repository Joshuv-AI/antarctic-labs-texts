Joint parameter estimations for spin glasses
Wei-Kuo Chen ⋆, Arnab Sen †, Qiang Wu ‡

Abstract
. Spin glass models with quadratic-type Hamiltonians are disordered statistical physics systems with competing ferromagnetic and anti-ferromagnetic spin interactions. The corresponding Gibbs measures belong to the exponential family parametrized by (inverse) temperature β > 0 \beta>0 and external field h ∈ ℝ h\in\mathbb{R} . Given a sample from these Gibbs measures, a statistically fundamental question is to infer the temperature and external field parameters. In 2007, Chatterjee (Ann. Statist. 35 (2007), no. 5, 1931–1946) first proved that in the absence of external field h = 0 h=0 , the maximum pseudolikelihood estimator for β \beta is N \sqrt{N} -consistent under some mild assumptions on the disorder matrices. It was left open whether the same method can be used to estimate the temperature and external field simultaneously. In this paper, under some easily verifiable conditions, we prove that the bivariate maximum pseudolikelihood estimator is indeed jointly N \sqrt{N} -consistent for the temperature and external field parameters. The examples cover the classical Sherrington-Kirkpatrick model and its diluted variants.

1.
Introduction and Main Results

Ising model possesses the Gibbs measure that belongs to the bivariate exponential family and takes the form, for
{\sigma}\in\{-1,+1\}^{N}
,

P_{\beta,h}({\sigma})=\frac{1}{Z_{N}(\beta,h)}\exp(\beta\langle{\sigma},J{\sigma}\rangle/2+h\langle{\sigma},\boldsymbol{1}\rangle),

where
\beta>0
is the (inverse) temperature,
h\in\mathbb{R}
is the external field, and
J\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a real-valued coupling matrix. In the past decades, estimation problems for the Ising model have received a great deal of attention from various communities due to its broad downstream applications, such as in statistics and computer science etc. One of the major research branches is, if the coupling matrix
J
is known, about estimating
\beta
,
h
, or both from a given sample of the Gibbs measure. One classical approach to this estimation problem is based on the maximum likelihood method. In the case of mean field Ising model, also known as Curie-Weiss model, where
J
is the adjacency matrix of complete graph with proper scaling, Comets and Gidas
[
10
]
showed that for
\beta>0
and
h\neq 0
, the maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) for
\beta
given
h
or vice versa is always
\sqrt{N}
-consistent. However, joint estimation of
(\beta,h)
is impossible. In some cases, maximum likelihood estimation for the Ising model can be very challenging since it involves the normalizing constant
Z_{N}(\beta,h)
whose computation, in particular, is nontrivial for models with
J
having both positive and negative entries. For example, in the setting of spin glass models, the interaction matrix
J
consists of random entries; as a result, understanding the limiting behavior of the log-normalizing constant as
N\to\infty
is generally a challenging problem and only a few cases are solvable in the literature. Arguably, one of the most intriguing results was the famous Parisi formula
[
16
,
17
]
that provided a variational representation for this limit established in the seminal work of Talagand
[
20
]
. Nonetheless, it is still a mystery to understand spin glass models for large but finite
N
.

Due to the intractability of MLE approach, it is desirable to seek for some other candidate methods that do not involve computing the log-normalizing constants. To this end, the pseudolikelihood approach
[
4
,
5
]
was proposed, where the pseudolikelihood function is defined as the product of conditional distributions such that the normalizing constant gets cancelled out. In this case, the maximum pseudolikelihood estimator (MPLE) can indeed alleviate the issue of dealing with a possibly complicated normalizing constant.

The MPLE approach has been studied a lot recently due to its efficiency and simplicity. The first work for the Ising-type model was initiated by Chatterjee
[
8
]
, where the author set
h=0
and considered the single-parameter estimation problem for
\beta>0
. Under the assumption that
J
has bounded operator norm
and
\liminf_{N\to\infty}N^{-1}\ln Z_{N}(\beta,h)>0
, it was shown that the MPLE is
\sqrt{N}
-consistency estimation for
\beta
. In particular, the result includes examples of spin glasses, such as the Sherrington-Kirkpatrick (SK) model
[
19
]
and the Hopfield model
[
14
]
. Afterwards, the result of
[
8
]
was extended to other generalized Ising models
[
6
,
15
]
. All those results have been restricted to the single parameter estimation setting. As we saw in the Curie-Weiss model, joint estimation is generally a much more challenging task. In fact, sometimes MPLE does not be even exist as opposed to the scenario for the single parameter. Incidentally, extending the single parameter estimation results to the joint parameters estimation setting was proposed as one of the open problems by Chatterjee
[
8
]
. An attempt to resolve this question was obtained by Ghosal and Mukherjee
[
13
]
in the setting of the ferromagnetic Ising model where
J
has nonnegative entries and they established a set of sufficient conditions under which joint estimation for
(\beta,h)
is possible with
\sqrt{N}
-consistency.

In this paper, we aim to show that MPLE is a
\sqrt{N}
-jointly consistent estimator for
(\beta,h)
allowing
J
to possess positive and negative entries. More precisely, the matrix
J
is a weighted adjacency matrix of a graph, where the weights are i.i.d. centered random variable with finite third moment and the graph requires that its averaging degree diverges at least logarithmically fast and is comparable to the maximum degree. This setting covers a collection of spin glass models such as the celebrated SK model and its diluted variants. We show that the validity of our result requires checking two major conditions: the boundedness of the operator norm of
J
, and the positivity of the Hessian matrix of the pseudolikelihood function, see Theorem
1.1
. Among them, the second condition plays a central role and it also appeared in
[
13
]
. Incidentally, the authors therein verified this positivity condition critically relying on the nonnegativity assumption of coupling coefficients which allowed them to use the nonlinear large deviation theory for random graphs
[
7
,
3
,
12
,
9
]
. However, such technique does not seem to work when the coupling matrix
J
has both positive and negative entries. Until now, it remained as a main obstacle to extend joint estimation results beyond the non-negative coupling coefficients setting. We are able to check the non-singularity condition with high probability in the setting of random coupling matrices via
the small ball probability arguments
[
18
,
21
]
.

1.1.
Notation

Let
\mathcal{G}:=([N],E)
be a graph with vertex set
[N]=\{1,2,\ldots,N\}
and edge set
E
. Denote by
d_{i}
and
d
the
degree
of vertex
i\in[N]
and the
average degree
of
\mathcal{G}
, respectively. In addition,
d_{\max}:=\max_{i\in[N]}d_{i}
denotes the
maximum degree
of
\mathcal{G}
. Let
G=(g_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{N}
be a symmetric matrix and vanishes along the diagonal,
i.e.,

g_{ii}=0
for
i\in[N]
. Set

(1)

\displaystyle J:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}A\circ G,

where
\circ
denotes the Hadmard product and

A
is the adjacency matrix of
\mathcal{G}
, that is,

\displaystyle A_{ij}=\begin{cases}1,\quad\text{if $(i,j)\in E$},\\
0,\quad\text{if $i=j$ or $(i,j)\notin E$. }\end{cases}

If
U_{N}
and
V_{N}
are two non-negative random variables sampled from some probability measure
P
, we say
U_{N}=O_{p}(V_{N})
if
(U_{N}/V_{N})_{N\geqslant 1}
is a tight family under
P
, i.e., for each
\epsilon>0
, there exists
C
such that

P(U_{N}/V_{N}\leqslant C)\geqslant 1-\epsilon\ \ \text{ for all }N\geqslant 1.

The notation
\Omega_{p}
is defined similarly.

1.2.
Basic Setting of MPLE

For any
\beta>0,h\in\mathbb{R}
, the Gibbs measure associated to
J
is defined as

(2)

\displaystyle P_{\beta,h}({\sigma})=\frac{\exp(\beta\langle J{\sigma},{\sigma}\rangle/2+h\langle{\sigma},\boldsymbol{1}\rangle)}{Z_{N}(\beta,h)},

for all
{\sigma}\in\Sigma_{N}:=\{-1,+1\}^{N}
,
where

Z_{N}(\beta,h)=\sum_{{\sigma}\in\Sigma_{N}}\exp(\beta\langle J{\sigma},{\sigma}\rangle/2+h\langle{\sigma},\boldsymbol{1}\rangle)

is the normalizing constant, also known as the partition function in statistical physics. For any
\tau\sim P_{\beta,h}
and
f:\Sigma_{N}\to\mathbb{R}
, denote by
\langle f(\tau)\rangle
the expectation of
f(\tau)
with respect to the Gibbs measure. The free energy is defined as

(3)

\displaystyle F_{N}(\beta,h)=\frac{1}{N}\log Z_{N}(\beta,h).

We proceed to formulate the MPLE for
(\beta,h).
In the sequel, we assume that
\beta_{0},h_{0}
are the ground truth parameters and a sample
{\tau}\sim P_{\beta_{0},h_{0}}(\cdot)
. For
{\sigma}\in\{-1,+1\}^{N}
, the
pseudolikelihood function
is defined as

\displaystyle L(\beta,h|{\sigma})

\displaystyle=\log\prod_{i=1}^{N}P_{\beta,h}(\sigma_{i}|\sigma_{j\neq i})

(4)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}\Bigl(\beta\sum_{j=1}^{N}J_{ij}\sigma_{j}+h\Bigr)-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\cosh\Bigl(\beta\sum_{j\neq i}J_{ij}\sigma_{j}+h\Bigr)-\log 2.

Taking the partial derivatives of the pseudolikelihood function w.r.t. the parameters
\beta,h
, we have the corresponding
score functions
,

(5)

\displaystyle S(\beta,h|{\sigma})

\displaystyle:=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\beta}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}m_{i}({\sigma})(\sigma_{i}-\tanh(\beta m_{i}({\sigma})+h)),

(6)

\displaystyle Q(\beta,h|{\sigma})

\displaystyle:=\frac{\partial L}{\partial h}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\sigma_{i}-\tanh(\beta m_{i}({\sigma})+h)),

where

m_{i}({\sigma}):=\sum_{j=1}^{N}J_{ij}\sigma_{j}=\sum_{j\neq i}J_{ij}\sigma_{j}.

We further compute the negative Hessian matrix
H(\beta,h|{\sigma})
for later use as

(7)

\displaystyle H(\beta,h|{\sigma}):=\begin{pmatrix}&\sum_{i=1}^{N}m_{i}({\sigma})^{2}\theta_{i}(\beta,h|{\sigma})&\sum_{i=1}^{N}m_{i}({\sigma})\theta_{i}(\beta,h|{\sigma})\\
&\sum_{i=1}^{N}m_{i}({\sigma})\theta_{i}(\beta,h|{\sigma})&\sum_{i=1}^{N}\theta_{i}(\beta,h|{\sigma})\end{pmatrix},

where for
i\in[N],

\theta_{i}(\beta,h|{\sigma}):=\sech^{2}(\beta m_{i}({\sigma})+h).

From a straightforward computation, the determinant of the Hessian is

\left|H(\beta,h|{\sigma})\right|=\frac{N^{2}}{2}\widetilde{T}_{N}(\beta,h|{\sigma}),

where

(8)

\displaystyle\widetilde{T}_{N}(\beta,h|{\sigma}):=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i,j\in[N]}\theta_{i}({\sigma})\theta_{j}({\sigma})(m_{i}({\sigma})-m_{j}({\sigma}))^{2}.

A quantity accompanied with
\widetilde{T}_{N}(\beta,h|{\sigma})
is

(9)

\displaystyle T_{N}(\beta,h|{\sigma}):=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i,j\in[N]}(m_{i}({\sigma})-m_{j}({\sigma}))^{2}=\frac{2}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}(m_{i}({\sigma})-\bar{{m}}({\sigma}))^{2},

where

\bar{{m}}({\sigma}):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}m_{i}({\sigma}).

For notational brevity, we will often suppress the dependence of
\theta_{i},T_{N},
and
\widetilde{T}_{N}
on
\beta
and
h
.

We assume that the coupling matrix
J
is given and we have access to a single sample from the ground truth
P_{\beta_{0},h_{0}}
. Our ultimate aim is to determine the conditions on the model so that the pseudolikelihood estimator
exists and is consistent. The following theorem presents a set of sufficient conditions to attend this purpose.

Theorem 1.1

(Existence and consistency of MPLE)
.

Suppose that the coupling matrix
J\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
is symmetric and has zeros entries along the diagonal. For fixed
\beta_{0}>0
and
h_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, let
{\tau}\in\{-1,+1\}^{N}
be a sample drawn from the Gibbs measure
P_{\beta_{0},h_{0}}
. Assume that the following conditions hold.

(i)

Boundedness of
J
:

\displaystyle\|J\|=O(1),\quad\text{where $\|J\|$ is the operator norm of $J$.}

(ii)

Positivity of the Hessian
:
Under
P_{\beta_{0},h_{0}}
,

\displaystyle\widetilde{T}_{N}({\tau})=\widetilde{T}_{N}(\beta_{0},h_{0}|{\tau})=\Omega_{p}(1).

Then with
P_{\beta_{0},h_{0}}
-probability tending to one, the MPLE exists, i.e., the bivariate equation

(10)

\displaystyle(S(\beta,h|{\tau}),Q(\beta,h|{\tau}))=(0,0),

has a unique solution
(\hat{\beta}({\tau}),\hat{h}({\tau}))\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Moreover,
(\hat{\beta}({\tau}),\hat{h}({\tau}))
is a
\sqrt{N}
-consistent estimator of the ground truth
(\beta_{0},h_{0})
in the sense that under
P_{\beta_{0},h_{0}}
,

\|(\hat{\beta}({\tau})-\beta_{0},\hat{h}({\tau})-h_{0})\|_{2}=O_{p}(N^{-1/2}).

Note that this theorem holds for arbitrary real-valued symmetric matrices
J
with zeros along the diagonal and they do not need to be of the form (
1
). We further remark that the results can be easily extended to include the regime
\beta_{0}<0
. For notational and technical convenience, we focus on the regime
\beta_{0}>0
as stated above.

Remark 1.2
.

Under different assumptions, Ghosal-Mukherjee
[
13
]
obtained similar existence and consistency results as Theorem 1.2, where the matrix
J
has nonnegative entries and uniformly bounded row sums.
Our proof for the consistency part in Theorem
1.1
is similar to that of Theorem 1.2 in
[
13
]
. However, the approach for the existence part in the same theorem does not apply to our setting.

Remark 1.1
.

If
h=0
, Theorem
1.1
reduces to the single parameter estimation problem for
\beta
studied in
[
8
]
and it was known that the conditions (i) and (ii) imply the invertibility of the corresponding score function analogous to (iii). However, in the joint estimation problem, (i) and (ii) are not enough to imply (iii) as can be seen in the Curie-Weiss model.

1.3.
Main Results

Our work considers the setting of the disordered spin system, namely,
G
is a symmetric matrix of independent random entries. The main result establishes three major theorems that validate the conditions (i), (ii), and (iii) in Theorem
1.1
individually with high probability. As a consequence, we conclude that the pseudolikelihood estimator exists and is
\sqrt{N}
-consistent in the joint estimation problem. To state our main results, we begin by introducing the following assumption on the graph structure
\mathcal{G}
.

Assumption 1.3
.

For the underlying graph
\mathcal{G}=([N],E)
, we assume the average degree
d
and the maximum degree
d_{\max}
satisfy

(i)

d\gg\log N
,

(ii)

d_{\max}\leqslant Cd
,

where
C>0
is a constant independent of
N.

Heuristically, the above condition specifies that the underlying graph can neither be too sparse nor have some vertices with very large degrees. Ghosal and Mukherjee’s work focused on non-disordered systems, where their coupling matrix
J
is defined as
J=\frac{1}{d}\cdot A
, with
A
being the adjacency matrix of the underlying graph and
d
the average degree. This contrasts with our setup, which employs a scaling factor of
\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}
and assigns independent, mean-zero random weights to each edge. Their paper operates under the standing assumption of row sum boundedness for
J
, implying
d_{max}\leqslant Cd,
a condition we also assume.
Furthermore, their results show that

(i)

If
d=O(1)
, the MPLE is
\sqrt{N}
-consistent.

(ii)

If
d\to\infty
, depending on whether the underlying graph is irregular or regular, the MPLE is either
\sqrt{N}
-consistent and the estimation is not possible respectively.

To compare with our results, we do not have any results in the regime
d=O(1)
due to the our standing assumption
d\gg\log N
. Second, under assumption
d\gg\log N
, we do not need any conditions on the regularity of the underlying graph.

Recall that a random variable
X
is
\kappa^{2}
-subgaussian if
\mathbb{E}X=0
and
\mathbb{E}e^{sX}\leqslant e^{\kappa^{2}s^{2}/2}
for all
s\in\mathbb{R}.
We say that
G=(g_{ij})_{i,j\in[N]}
is a symmetric
\kappa^{2}
-subgaussian matrix with vanishing diagonal if its upper triangular entries are independent
\kappa^{2}
-subgaussian. Now we state the first result that verifies the boundedness of the operator norm of
J
.

Theorem 1.4
.

Suppose that
G
is a symmetric
\kappa^{2}
-subgaussian matrix with vanishing diagonal and Assumption
1.3
holds.
Then there exist constants
C,K>0
, depending on
\kappa
, such that

\mathbb{P}(\left\|J\right\|\geqslant C)\leqslant K\exp(-d/\log N).

As we shall see below, the proofs for the above theorem are not too difficult, which borrow some standard ideas and results from the concentration inequalities for sparse random matrices
[
2
,
11
]
. The much more difficult component of this work lies on the verification of the strict positivity of the Hessian stated below.

Theorem 1.5
.

Suppose that

G
is a symmetric random matrix with vanishing diagonal so that its upper triangular entries satisfy

(11)

\displaystyle\text{$(g_{ij})_{i>j}$ are independent with}\ \E{g_{ij}}=0,\,{\E[g_{ij}^{2}]}=1,\,\text{and}\,\E|g_{ij}|^{3}\leqslant B,

where
B>0
is some finite constant independent of
N
. Furthermore, suppose that Assumption
1.3

(i)
is satisfied and
d\to\infty
as
N\to\infty
. Then there exist positive constants
c_{0},c
, and
K
such that

\mathbb{P}\bigl(\min_{{\sigma}}\widetilde{T}_{N}({\sigma})\leqslant c_{0}\bigr)\leqslant K\exp(-cN).

For the sample
{\tau}
from ground truth
P_{\beta_{0},h_{0}}
, obviously
\widetilde{T}_{N}({\tau})\geqslant\min_{{\sigma}}\widetilde{T}_{N}({\sigma})
holds. The above theorem suggests that
\widetilde{T}_{N}({\tau})\leqslant c_{0}
holds with exponentially decaying annealed probability.

Remark 1.6
.

If the minimization is over a continuous sphere
S_{N}:=\{{\sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:\|{\sigma}\|^{2}=N\}
instead of discrete cube
\{-1,+1\}^{N}
, then the statement of Theorem
1.5
is not true any more.
For example, if
J
is nonsingular with probability one, which, in particular, holds for the SK model, then we take
{\sigma}^{*}=\gamma J^{-1}\mathbf{1}
for
\gamma:=N^{-1/2}\left\|J^{-1}\mathbf{1}\right\|
,
so that
m({\sigma}^{*})=\gamma\mathbf{1}
and consequently,

\widetilde{T}_{N}({\sigma}^{*})\leqslant T_{N}({\sigma}^{*})=0.

Therefore, we always have

\min_{{\sigma}\in S_{N}}\widetilde{T}_{N}({\sigma})=0.

As of now, with high probability, all conditions in Theorem
1.1
have been verified under varying assumptions. Putting them all together, we obtain the asserted joint estimation for
(\beta,h).

Theorem 1.7
.

For
\beta_{0}>0
and
h_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, suppose
{\tau}\sim P_{\beta_{0},h_{0}}
. Suppose that

(1)

G
is a symmetric
\kappa^{2}
-subgaussian random matrix with vanishing diagonal,

(2)

Assumption
1.3
holds.

Then with
\mathbb{P}
-probability tending to one, the bivariate MPLE,
(\hat{\beta}({\tau}),\hat{h}({\tau})),
is jointly
\sqrt{N}
-consistent.

Example 1.8
.

Letting the upper triangular entries of
G
being i.i.d. standard Gaussian, Theorem
1.7
covers the classical SK model, for which
\mathcal{G}
is a complete graph so that
d_{\max}=d=N-1\gg\log N
satisfying Assumption
1.3
. Another example is the diluted SK model, where
\mathcal{G}
is a sparse Erdös-Rényi random graph
\mathcal{G}=G(N,p_{N})
with
p_{N}\gg{\log N}/{N}
.

Remark 1.9
.

It was indicated in
[
13
]
that the MPLE estimators are not jointly consistent when
J
is the scaled adjacency matrix of any asymptotically regular graph
\mathcal{G}
with the average degree diverging to infinity. In particular, consider the case when
\mathcal{G}=G(N,p_{N})
is a dense Erdös-Rényi random graph with
p_{N}=p
for some fixed
p\in(0,1]
.
For
t>0
, let
\Theta_{t}=\{(\beta,h)\in(0,\infty)^{2}:t=\tanh(\beta t+h)\}.

It was shown in
[
13
, Theorem 1.6]
that for any
(\beta_{0},h_{0})\in\Theta_{t}
, the measure
\mathbb{Q}_{n}\times\mathcal{G}
is contiguous to
P_{\beta_{0},h_{0}}\times\mathcal{G}
, where
\mathbb{Q}_{n}
is the product of Rademacher measures on
\{-1,1\}^{N}
with mean
\tanh(t)
. Now, note that for any
(\beta_{0},h_{0})\in(0,\infty)^{2}
, there exists a unique
t\in(0,1)
that satisfies
t=\tanh(\beta_{0}t+h_{0})
and that one can always find a distinct
(\beta_{1},h_{1})\in\Theta_{t}.
This statement readily implies that there does not exist any estimators that are jointly consistent for any
(\beta_{0},h_{0})\in(0,\infty)^{2}
. However, for the same interaction graph
\mathcal{G}
, if
J
is of the form as (
1
), then we know from Theorem
1.7
that the MPLE are jointly consistent for any
\beta_{0}>0
and
h_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
. This example suggests that the estimation problem when
J
has only non-negative entries is significantly different from the case when
J
is allowed to have both positive and negative entries.

1.4.
Proof Sketches

Theorem
1.1
consists of existence and consistency parts. The latter follows from a standard Delta method argument similar to
[
13
, Theorem 1.2]
. For existence, note that if the pseudolikelihood function diverges to the negative infinity as
|\beta|+|h|\to\infty
, then it has a unique maximizer (i.e., the MPLE exists) as it is already strictly concave thanks to the condition
(ii)
in Theorem
1.1
. Almost the entirety of the proof of existence part is devoted to showing this divergence. We prove that this is indeed true if
(\tau_{i},m_{i}(\tau))_{i\in[N]}
contains enough different signs. More precisely, the sufficient condition we need is stated in Lemma
2.3
and we then show that this property holds w.h.p. under the assumptions
(i)
and
(ii)
. Even though this is intuitively clear since the spins and the fields
m_{i}
should not be in favor of certain choices of signs overwhelmingly, proving it rigorously takes some effort. The key role is played by a general concentration result, see Lemma
2.1
below, which says that for
\tau\sim P_{\beta,h}
and any suitably Lipschitz function
f
, the linear statistics
N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}f(\tau_{i},m_{i}(\tau))
stays around its conditional mean
N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\langle f(\tau_{i},m_{i}(\tau))|\tau_{l},\forall l\neq i\rangle.
As an immediate consequence, our lemma implies the concentration for the score functions
S(\beta,h|\tau)
and
Q(\beta,h|\tau)
(see Corollary
2.2
below), which was previously established in
[
8
]
using Stein’s method for exchangeable pairs. Our result holds for more general functions and its proof relies on the cavity argument instead.

The proof of Theorem
1.4
follows straightforwardly by using the asymptotic results
[
2
]
and the concentration bound
[
11
]
for the operator norm of sparse subgaussian matrices. The proof of Theorem
1.5
is the central ingredient of this work.
For simplicity, let us sketch an argument to show that

\mathbb{P}\bigl(\min_{{\sigma}}T_{N}({\sigma})\leqslant c_{0}\bigr)\leqslant K\exp(-cN).

We will prove the above by taking a union bound over the spin configuration
{\sigma}\in\{-1,1\}^{N}
. Notice that

(12)

T_{N}({\sigma})=\frac{2}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}(m_{i}({\sigma})-\bar{{m}}({\sigma}))^{2}=\frac{2}{N}\inf_{\gamma\in\mathbb{R}}\left\|{m}({\sigma})-\gamma\boldsymbol{1}\right\|_{2}^{2},

It is easy to check using Markov inequality that
\|{m}({\sigma})\|_{\infty}\leqslant M:=2^{N}
with probability at least
1-C(1.5)^{-N}
. On this event,
the infimum over
\gamma
in (
12
) can be restricted to the interval
[-M,M]
, which can further be approximated by taking minima over an
\epsilon
-net
\mathcal{D}
of size
O(2^{N}\varepsilon^{-1})
for small but constant
\epsilon>0
. Therefore, it suffices to show that
uniformly for any
{\sigma}\in\{-1,1\}^{N}
and any
\gamma\in\mathcal{D}
,

(13)

\mathbb{P}\bigl(\left\|{m}({\sigma})-\gamma\boldsymbol{1}\right\|_{2}\leqslant c_{1}\sqrt{N}\bigr)\leqslant K10^{-N}

for appropriate positive constant
c_{1}
.
The event on the left-hand side can immediately be recognized as a small ball probability event. Had the entries of
J
been independent, the local fields
m_{i}({\sigma})
were independent. In that case, we can use the standard small ball probability result (e.g., Berry-Esseen theorem) for the linear combinations of independent random variables to infer that for each
i
, the probability that
|m_{i}({\sigma})-\gamma|<c_{1}
is at most
1/10
for sufficiently small
c_{1}
and then tensorizes the bound to obtain (
13
). However, since
J
is symmetric,
m_{i}({\sigma})
are not independent anymore and the tensorization breaks down.

To overcome this obstacle, we borrow an idea from
[
21
]
. We only use the randomness of a principal submatrix of
J
of linear size while conditioning out the rest of the entries. Let
T\subset[N]
be the set of the row (and the column) indices belonging to this principal submatrix, which we denote by
J_{T}
. Dealing with this smaller matrix instead of
J
has the critical advantage that the entries of
J_{T}
are independent (as long as
|T|\leqslant N/2
). We can then apply the previous small ball probability argument for
J_{T}\sigma_{T}
. However, successfully implementing this strategy requires that the matrix
J_{T}
is not too sparse in the sense that in the graph
\mathcal{G}
the number of edges between each vertex of
T
and
[N]\setminus T
tends to
\infty
as
N\to\infty
. We apply Erdös’ probabilistic method to guarantee the existence of such a ‘good’ deterministic set
T
provided
\mathcal{G}
satisfies Assumption
1.3
(i)
and
d\to\infty
as
N\to\infty
. This gives the lower bound for
\min_{{\sigma}}T_{N}({\sigma})
.

The original term
\widetilde{T}_{N}({\sigma})
has an additional complication since
\theta_{i}({\sigma}):=\sech^{2}(\beta m_{i}({\sigma})+h)
can be small if
m_{i}({\sigma})
is big. However, it can be shown that with overwhelming probability, there exists a (random) linear subset of indices of
T
satisfying
|m_{i}({\sigma})|\leqslant C
, which then yields the lower bound for
\min_{{\sigma}}\widetilde{T}_{N}({\sigma})
as it follows from the previous step that the local fields
(m_{i}({\sigma}))_{i\in T}
have sufficient variability.

Let
\beta>0
and

. For

\displaystyle L_{i}({\sigma})

\displaystyle=\langle f_{i}(\tau_{i},m_{i}(\tau))|\tau_{l}=\sigma_{l},\forall l\neq i\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{\beta m_{i}({\sigma})+h}f_{i}(1,m_{i}({\sigma}))+e^{-\beta m_{i}({\sigma})-h}f_{i}(-1,m_{i}({\sigma}))}{e^{\beta m_{i}({\sigma})+h}+e^{-\beta m_{i}({\sigma})-h}}

for
{\sigma}\in\Sigma_{N},

where
\langle\cdot\rangle
is the Gibbs expectation with respect to
\tau\sim P_{\beta,h}.

Our first lemma establishes the following concentration for the linear statistics of
(\tau_{i},m_{i}(\tau))_{i\in[N]}

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
{\tau}
be sampled from
P_{\beta,h}
for some
\beta>0
and
h\in\mathbb{R}
.
Let
\{f_{i}\}_{i\in[N]}
be a collection of functions defined on
\{-1,+1\}\times\mathbb{R}
with
\max_{i\in[N]}|f_{i}(x,y)|\leqslant C(1+|y|)
for all
(x,y)\in\{-1,+1\}\times\mathbb{R}
for some positive constant
C
and
\max_{i\in[N]}(\|\partial_{y}f_{i}\|_{\infty},\|\partial_{y}^{2}f_{i}\|_{\infty})<\infty.

Then there exists a constant
K
depending only on
\beta
and
C
such that for any
N\geqslant 1,
we have that

\displaystyle\Bigl\langle\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigl(f_{i}(\tau_{i},m_{i}({\tau}))-L_{i}({\tau})\bigr)\Bigr)^{2}\Bigr\rangle

\displaystyle\leqslant K(1+\|J\|+\|J\|^{2})\Bigl(N+N\|J\|^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|\partial_{y}f_{i}\|_{\infty}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|\partial_{y}^{2}f_{i}\|_{\infty}^{2}\Bigr).

Proof.

For any
i,j\in[N]
, set

m_{j}^{(i)}({\sigma}):=\sum_{k\neq i,j}J_{jk}\sigma_{k}

and let
L^{(i)}_{j}({\sigma})
be the same as
L_{j}({\sigma})
with
m_{j}({\sigma})
replaced by
m_{j}^{(i)}({\sigma})
.
Since
L_{i}(\tau)
is the expectation of
f_{i}(\tau_{i},m_{i}({\tau}))
conditionally on
(\tau_{l})_{l\neq i}
, we have

\displaystyle\bigl\langle\bigl(f_{i}(\tau_{i},m_{i}({\tau}))-L_{i}({\tau})\bigr)\bigl(f_{j}(\tau_{j},m_{j}^{(i)}({\tau}))-L_{j}^{(i)}({\tau})\bigr)\bigr\rangle=0,

which implies

\displaystyle\bigl\langle\bigl(f_{i}(\tau_{i},m_{i}({\tau}))-L_{i}({\tau})\bigr)\bigl(f_{j}(\tau_{j},m_{j}({\tau}))-L_{j}({\tau})\bigr)\bigr\rangle

(14)

\displaystyle=\bigl\langle X_{i}(\tau)\big(f_{j}(\tau_{j},m_{j}({\tau}))-f_{j}(\tau_{j},m_{j}^{(i)}({\tau}))\big)\bigr\rangle

(15)

\displaystyle+\bigl\langle X_{i}(\tau)c_{ij}({\tau})\bigr\rangle,

where

\displaystyle c_{ij}({\tau})

\displaystyle:=L_{j}^{(i)}({\tau})-L_{j}({\tau}),

\displaystyle X_{i}(\tau)

\displaystyle:=f_{i}(\tau_{i},m_{i}({\tau}))-L_{i}({\tau}).

We bound (
15
) first.
Note that

(16)

\displaystyle|X_{i}(\tau)|\leqslant 2C(1+|m_{i}(\tau)|).

For
t\in[0,1],
let
J^{(i,j)}(t)
be a symmetric square matrix with
J_{ab}^{(i,j)}(t)=J_{ab}
for all
(a,b)\neq(i,j)
and
(j,i)
and
J_{ij}^{(i,j)}(t)=J_{ji}^{(i,j)}(t)=(1-t)J_{ij}.
Denote
\mathcal{L}_{ij}(t)
by the function
L_{j}(\tau)
with
J
being replaced by
J^{(i,j)}(t)
. Using Taylor’s formula yields that

\displaystyle L_{j}^{(i)}({\tau})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{ij}(1)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{ij}(0)+\mathcal{L}_{ij}^{\prime}(0)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}(1-s)\mathcal{L}_{ij}^{\prime\prime}(s)ds

\displaystyle=L_{j}(\tau)+J_{ij}Y_{j}+J_{ij}^{2}Z_{j}^{(i)},

where

\displaystyle Y_{j}

\displaystyle=-\partial_{J_{ij}}L_{j}(\tau),

\displaystyle Z_{j}^{(i)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}(1-s)\partial_{J_{ij}}^{2}L_{j}(\tau)\Big|_{J=J^{(i,j)}(s)}ds.

We emphasize that here
Y_{j}
depends only on
j
since

\displaystyle\partial_{J_{ij}}L_{j}({\tau})

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{\beta m_{j}({\tau})+h}\partial_{y}f_{j}(1,m_{j}({\tau}))+e^{-\beta m_{j}({\sigma})-h}\partial_{y}f_{j}(-1,m_{j}({\tau}))}{e^{\beta m_{j}({\tau})+h}+e^{-\beta m_{j}({\tau})-h}}

\displaystyle+\beta\frac{e^{\beta m_{j}({\tau})+h}f_{j}(1,m_{j}({\tau}))-e^{-\beta m_{j}({\tau})-h}f_{j}(-1,m_{j}({\tau}))}{e^{\beta m_{j}({\tau})+h}+e^{-\beta m_{j}({\tau})-h}}

\displaystyle-\beta\frac{e^{\beta m_{j}({\tau})+h}f_{j}(1,m_{j}({\tau}))+e^{-\beta m_{j}({\tau})-h}f_{j}(-1,m_{j}({\tau}))}{e^{\beta m_{j}({\tau})+h}+e^{-\beta m_{j}({\tau})-h}}\tanh(\beta m_{j}({\tau})+h).

Now from the given assumption,

\displaystyle|\partial_{J_{ij}}L_{j}({\tau})|

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\beta C(1+|m_{j}(\tau)|)+\|\partial_{y}f_{j}\|_{\infty}=:K_{j}.

Similarly one can compute the second derivative of
L_{j}({\tau})
with respect to
J_{ij}
; the exact form is not important here, but it is crucial to realize that

\displaystyle|\partial_{J_{ij}}^{2}L_{j}({\tau})|

\displaystyle\leqslant\tilde{C}\bigl(1+|m_{j}(\tau)|+\|\partial_{y}f_{j}\|_{\infty}+\|\partial_{y}^{2}f_{j}\|_{\infty}\bigr)

for some constant
\tilde{C}
depending only on
C
and
\beta.
It follows from these bounds that

(17)

\displaystyle|Y_{j}|\leqslant K_{j}

and

\displaystyle|Z_{j}^{(i)}|

\displaystyle\leqslant\tilde{C}\Bigl(1+\int_{0}^{1}(1-s)|(J^{(i,j)}(s)\tau)_{j}\bigr|ds+\|\partial_{y}f_{j}\|_{\infty}+\|\partial_{y}^{2}f_{j}\|_{\infty}\Bigr)

(18)

\displaystyle\leqslant\tilde{C}\bigl(1+2\|J\|+\|\partial_{y}f_{j}\|_{\infty}+\|\partial_{y}^{2}f_{j}\|_{\infty}\bigr)=:\tilde{K}_{j},

where we have used the bound
\|J^{(i,j)}(s)\|\leqslant\|J\|+(1-s)|J_{ij}|\leqslant 2\|J\|.

Now, write

\Big|\sum_{i\neq j}X_{i}(\tau)c_{ij}(\tau)\Big|\leqslant\Big|\sum_{i\neq j}J_{ij}X_{i}(\tau)Y_{j}(\tau)\Big|+\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{J_{ij}^{2}}{2}|X_{i}(\tau)||Z_{j}^{(i)}|.

Note that
J_{ij}=0
when
i=j
. Using (
16
) and (
17
) leads to

\displaystyle\Bigl|\sum_{i\neq j}J_{ij}X_{i}(\tau)Y_{j}(\tau)\Bigr|

\displaystyle\leqslant 2C\|J\|\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1+|m_{i}(\tau)|)^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}\Bigl(\sum_{j=1}^{N}K_{j}^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}.

Similarly, from (
16
) and (
18
) and using
\|J\circ J\|\leqslant\|J\|^{2},

\displaystyle\sum_{i\neq j}J_{ij}^{2}|X_{i}(\tau)||Z_{j}^{(i)}|

\displaystyle\leqslant C\|J\|^{2}\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1+|m_{i}(\tau)|)^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tilde{K}_{i}^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}.

From these, (
15

\displaystyle\Big|\sum_{i\neq j}X_{i}(\tau)c_{ij}(\tau)\Big|

\displaystyle\leqslant 2C\|J\|\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1+|m_{i}(\tau)|)^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}\Bigl(\sum_{j=1}^{N}K_{j}^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}

\displaystyle+C\|J\|^{2}\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1+|m_{i}(\tau)|)^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tilde{K}_{i}^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}.

As for (
14

\displaystyle\Bigl|f_{i}(\tau_{j},m_{j}({\tau}))-f_{i}(\tau_{j},m_{j}^{(i)}({\tau}))+J_{ij}\tau_{i}\partial_{y}f_{i}(\tau_{j},m_{j}({\tau}))\Bigr|

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{2}J_{ij}^{2}\|\partial^{2}_{y}f_{i}\|_{\infty}.

Here, we can control similar to the above,

\displaystyle\Bigl|\sum_{i\neq j}X_{i}(\tau)\bigl(f_{j}(\tau_{j},m_{j}({\tau}))-f_{j}(\tau_{j},m_{j}^{(i)})\bigr)\Bigr|

\displaystyle\leqslant\Big|\sum_{i,j}J_{ij}(X_{i}(\tau)\tau_{i})\big(\partial_{y}f_{j}(\tau_{j},m_{j}({\tau}))\big)\Big|+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}J_{ij}^{2}|X_{i}(\tau)|\|\partial^{2}_{y}f_{j}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2C\|J\|\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1+|m_{i}(\tau)|)^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|\partial_{y}f_{i}\|_{\infty}^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}

\displaystyle+C\|J\|^{2}\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1+|m_{i}(\tau)|)^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|\partial_{y}^{2}f_{i}\|_{\infty}^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}.

Putting everything together and noting that

, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i\neq j}\bigl\langle\bigl(f_{i}(\tau_{i},m_{i}({\tau}))-L_{i}({\tau})\bigr)\bigl(f_{i}(\tau_{j},m_{j}({\tau}))-L_{j}({\tau})\bigr)\bigr\rangle

\displaystyle\leqslant K(\|J\|+\|J\|^{2})\Bigl(N+N\|J\|^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|\partial_{y}f_{i}\|_{\infty}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|\partial_{y}^{2}f_{i}\|_{\infty}^{2}\Bigr)

for some constant
K
depending only on
\beta
and
C.

Finally, for the sum over
i=j
, it can be easily bounded by
C^{\prime\prime}N(+\|J\|^{2})
for some constant
K^{\prime}
depending only on
C
and this completes our proof.

\blacksquare

We begin with the following lemma, which identifies sufficient conditions for the existence of MPLE.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\sigma\in\{-1,+1\}^{N}
and assume that there exist distinct
1\leqslant i,j,k,\ell\leqslant N
and
a\in\mathbb{R}
such that

(19)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\sigma_{i}=1,m_{i}({\sigma})>a,\,\,\sigma_{j}=-1,m_{j}({\sigma})>a,\\
&\sigma_{k}=1,m_{k}({\sigma})<a,\,\,\sigma_{l}=-1,m_{l}({\sigma})<a.\end{split}

then
\lim_{|\beta|+|h|\to\infty}\bar{L}(\beta,h|\sigma)=-\infty.

Proof.

For brevity of notation, we denote
m_{s}(\sigma)
by
m_{s}
. Noting that

\displaystyle\bar{L}(\beta,h|{\sigma})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{s=1}^{N}\Big(\sigma_{s}(\beta m_{s}+h)-\log\cosh(\beta m_{s}+h)-\log 2\Big)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{N}\sum_{s=1}^{N}\Big(|\beta m_{s}+h|-\log\cosh(\beta m_{s}+h)-\log 2\Big)

and each term in the above sum is non-positive, it is enough to show that given
{\sigma}
, for any
K>0
, there exists some
C>0
such that the following statement holds: for any
(\beta,h)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
|\beta|+|h|\geqslant C,
there exists some
s=s(\beta,h)\in[N]
such that

(20)

\sigma_{s}(\beta m_{s}+h)\leqslant-K.

We will argue by contradiction. Assume that
i,j,k,
and
l
satisfy (
19
) and each of them violates (
20
). Then we must have

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}=1,\quad\beta m_{i}+h>-K

\displaystyle\sigma_{j}=-1,\quad\beta m_{j}+h<K

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}=1,\quad\beta m_{k}+h>-K

\displaystyle\sigma_{l}=-1,\quad\beta m_{l}+h<K,

This confines
h
to the interval:

(21)

\max(-\beta m_{i}-K,-\beta m_{k}-K)<h<\min(-\beta m_{j}+K,-\beta m_{l}+K).

For this interval to be non-empty, its upper bound must be greater than its lower bound.

Case
\beta\geqslant 0
: The conditions
m_{j}>a>m_{k}
imply
m_{j}>m_{k}
. The non-empty interval constraint implies
-\beta m_{k}-K<-\beta m_{j}+K
, which simplifies to
\beta(m_{j}-m_{k})<2K
. Therefore, we have

(22)

\beta<2K/(m_{j}-m_{k}).

Case
\beta<0
: The conditions
m_{i}>a>m_{l}
imply
m_{i}>m_{l}
. The constraint implies
-\beta m_{i}-K<-\beta m_{l}+K
, which simplifies to
-\beta(m_{i}-m_{l})<2K
.
Therefore, we have

(23)

-\beta<2K/(m_{i}-m_{l}).

Combining (
22
) and (
23
), we obtain

|\beta|<\max\left(\frac{2K}{m_{j}-m_{k}},\frac{2K}{m_{i}-m_{l}}\right),

which, in combination with (
21
) forces
|h|
to be bounded. So,
|\beta|+|h|
can not be arbitrarily large. This concludes the proof of the lemma.

\blacksquare

The following lemma, which is purely deterministic, states a key step to verify the conditions in Lemma
2.3
. Let

\tilde{T}_{N}:=N^{-2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\sech^{2}(\beta_{0}m_{i}+h_{0})\sech^{2}(\beta_{0}m_{j}+h_{0})(m_{i}-m_{j})^{2}.

Note that this is a deterministic object and different from (
8
).

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\beta_{0}>0,h_{0}\in\mathbb{R},
and
c>0
. There exist positive constants
\delta,\epsilon,K
, depending only on
\beta_{0},h_{0}
and
c
, such that for any sequence
m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{N}
of real numbers satisfying
\tilde{T}_{N}\geqslant c
,
there exists an integer
r
with
|r|\leqslant K/\delta
and
-K\leqslant(r-1)\delta<r\delta\leqslant K
such that

(24)

\displaystyle|\{i\in[N]:m_{i}\in[-K,(r-1)\delta]\}|\geqslant\varepsilon N\text{ and }|\{i\in[N]:m_{i}\in[r\delta,K]\}|\geqslant\varepsilon N.

Proof.

Since
\sech^{2}(\beta_{0}x+h_{0})\leqslant\min(1,4e^{-2(\beta_{0}|x|-|h_{0}|)})
, we can choose
K>0
sufficiently large so that

(25)

\sum_{i,j:|m_{i}|,|m_{j}|\leqslant K}(m_{i}-m_{j})^{2}\geqslant\frac{c}{2}N^{2},

which implies that at least
\eta N
of the
m_{i}
’s lie within
[-K,K]
, where
\eta=\sqrt{c/2}/(2K)
. Set

\varepsilon=\min\left(\frac{\eta}{4},\frac{c}{80K^{2}}\right)\,\,\text{and}\,\,\delta=\min\left(\frac{\sqrt{c}}{5},\frac{K}{2}\right)

and define

r=\max\left\{s\in\mathbb{Z}:\left|\left\{i\in[N]:m_{i}\in[s\delta,K]\right\}\right|\geqslant\varepsilon N\right\}.

Suppose, for contradiction, that

\left|\left\{i\in[N]:m_{i}\in[-K,(r-1)\delta]\right\}\right|<\varepsilon N.

This implies that at most
2\varepsilon N
of the
m_{i}
can lie in the interval
[-K,K]\setminus[(r-1)\delta,(r+1)\delta]
. We now derive an upper bound on the left-hand side of (
25
).

(I)
The total contribution from pairs
i,j
such that at least one of
m_{i}
or
m_{j}
lies outside
[(r-1)\delta,(r+1)\delta]
is at most

2\cdot N\cdot 2\varepsilon N\cdot 4K^{2}\leqslant\frac{c}{5}N^{2}.

(II)
The total contribution from pairs
i,j
such that both
m_{i}
and
m_{j}
lie within
[(r-1)\delta,(r+1)\delta]
is at most

N^{2}\cdot(2\delta)^{2}\leqslant\frac{c}{5}N^{2}.

By the choice of
\varepsilon
and
\delta
, the sum of these contributions is at most
2cN^{2}/5
, which contradicts (
25
). Therefore,

\left|\left\{i\in[N]:m_{i}\leqslant(r-1)\delta\right\}\right|\geqslant\varepsilon N

and the lemma follows.

\blacksquare

\displaystyle|S_{-K,a}|\geqslant\epsilon N\,\,\mbox{and}\,\,|S_{a+\delta,K}|\geqslant\epsilon N

Noting that there are only finitely many possible

2.3.
Proof of Theorem
1.1
: Consistency of MPLE

We begin with a simple lemma.

Lemma 2.5
.

Fix
\beta>0
and
h\in\mathbb{R}
. Let
{\sigma}\in\{-1,+1\}^{N}
.
Suppose that
\|J\|=O(1)
and

\widetilde{T}_{N}({\sigma})\geqslant c.
Then there exists a constant
M>0
, which depends only on
c,\beta,h,
and
\|J\|
such that

\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i,j\in I_{M}({\sigma})}(m_{i}({\sigma})-m_{j}({\sigma}))^{2}\geqslant\frac{c}{2},

where
I_{M}({\sigma})=\{i\in[N]:|m_{i}({\sigma})|\leqslant M\}.

Proof.

Let
M
be sufficiently large such that
\beta M>|h|
. We have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i,j\in I_{M}({\sigma})}(m_{i}({\sigma})-m_{j}({\sigma}))^{2}

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i,j\in I_{M}({\sigma})}\theta_{i}({\sigma})\theta_{j}({\sigma})(m_{i}({\sigma})-m_{j}({\sigma}))^{2}

\displaystyle\geqslant c-\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{\{i,j\}\cap I_{M}^{c}({\sigma})\neq\emptyset}\theta_{i}({\sigma})\theta_{j}({\sigma})(m_{i}({\sigma})-m_{j}({\sigma}))^{2}

\displaystyle\geqslant c-\frac{\sech^{2}(\beta M-|h|)}{N^{2}}\sum_{i,j}(m_{i}({\sigma})-m_{j}({\sigma}))^{2}.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i,j}(m_{i}({\sigma})-m_{j}({\sigma}))^{2}\leqslant\frac{2}{N}\sum_{i}m_{i}({\sigma})^{2}\leqslant 2\|J\|^{2}.

Combining these two estimates, the lemma follows that if we choose
M
large enough to satisfy

\blacksquare

The proof for the consistency of MPLE in Theorem
1.1
proceeds as follows.
For notational clarity, we will make the dependence of the quantities
\theta_{i}
and
\widetilde{T}_{N}
on parameters
\beta
and

Let
{\tau}\sim P_{\beta_{0},h_{0}}
. For

, let

be the minimum eigenvalue of the negative Hessian matrix,

.
Note that

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(\beta,h|{\tau})

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{\left|H(\beta,h|{\tau})\right|}{\mathrm{Tr}(H(\beta,h|{\tau}))}=\frac{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\theta_{i}(\beta,h|{\tau})\theta_{j}(\beta,h|{\tau})(m_{i}({\tau})-m_{j}({\tau}))^{2}}{\mathrm{Tr}(H(\beta,h|{\tau}))},

where

To bound the right hand side of (

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\theta_{i}(\beta,h|{\tau})\theta_{j}(\beta,h|{\tau})(m_{i}({\tau})-m_{j}({\tau}))^{2}

\displaystyle\geqslant\sech^{4}(\beta M+|h|)\sum_{i,j\in I_{M}({\tau})}(m_{i}({\tau})-m_{j}({\tau}))^{2}

for any

. Also, note that

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(\beta,h|{\tau})\geqslant\frac{\sech^{4}(\beta M+|h|)}{2(1+C^{2})N}\sum_{i,j\in I_{M}({\tau})}(m_{i}({\tau})-m_{j}({\tau}))^{2}.

This yields the following locally uniform lower bound for

that for any

, there exist some positive

\displaystyle\inf_{\left\|\beta-\beta_{0},h-h_{0}\right\|\leqslant r}\lambda_{1}(\beta,h|{\tau})\geqslant\frac{\eta}{N}\sum_{i,j\in I_{M}({\tau})}(m_{i}({\tau})-m_{j}({\tau}))^{2}.

Next, we construct an interpolation between the pseudolikelihood estimator and the ground truth by letting

\displaystyle\beta(t)=t\hat{\beta}+(1-t)\beta_{0}\,\,\mbox{and}\,\,h(t)=t\hat{h}+(1-t)h_{0}

for

\displaystyle f(t)=(\hat{\beta}-\beta_{0})S(\beta(t),h(t)|{\tau})+(\hat{h}-h_{0})Q(\beta(t),h(t)|{\tau}).

For any

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=(\hat{\beta}-\beta_{0},\hat{h}-h_{0})H(\beta(t),h(t)|{\tau})(\hat{\beta}-\beta_{0},\hat{h}-h_{0})^{T}

From the fact

\displaystyle f(1)-f(0)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}f^{\prime}(t)dt\geqslant\int_{0}^{\min\big(1,\frac{r}{Y_{N}}\big)}f^{\prime}(t)dt

(32)

\displaystyle\geqslant\min\big(1,\frac{r}{Y_{N}}\big)\frac{\eta Y_{N}^{2}}{N}\sum_{i,j\in I_{M}({\tau})}(m_{i}({\tau})-m_{j}({\tau}))^{2}.

From the assumption (iii) of Theorem

, it follows that given

On the other hand, (

Proof of Theorem

We first establish the bound for the expected operator norm of

. It relies on the following fundamental result in Corollary 3.3 of
[
2
]
, which we recall as follows.

Lemma 3.1

(Corollary 3.3
[
2
]
)
.

Consider the
N\times N
symmetric random matrix

whose entries
X_{ij}:=b_{ij}g_{ij}
for

, where
b_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}
and

are independent centered subgaussian. Then

\E\left\|X\right\|\lesssim\max_{i}\sqrt{\sum_{j}b_{ij}^{2}}+\sqrt{\log N}\cdot\max_{ij}\left|b_{ij}\right|.

Appling this result in our setting for the matrix
J
, it immediately gives the following lemma.

Lemma 3.2
.

If the entries of the symmetric matrix
G
are independent centered subgaussian random variables, then we have for a universal constant
C>0
such that

\E\left\|J\right\|\leqslant C\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log N}{d}}+\sqrt{\frac{d_{\rm{max}}}{d}}\right).

Proof.

The proof is immediate after applying the Corollary 3.3 in
[
2
]
.

\blacksquare

Now it can be seen that under the Assumption
1.3
, we have
\E\left\|J\right\|=O(1)
. To complete the proof of Theorem
1.4
, the rest is to prove the following concentration results for
\left\|J\right\|

Lemma 3.3
.

If the entries of the symmetric matrix
G
are independent centered

-subgaussian random variables for some

, then we have for some universal constant

\mathbb{P}(\left|\left\|J\right\|-\E\left\|J\right\|\right|\geqslant t)\leqslant 2\exp\left(-\frac{Cdt^{2}}{\kappa^{4}\log(Nd)}\right).

The major ingredient for the proof of Lemma

is the following concentration result for subgaussian matrices appeared in
[
11
]
. We include this result for reader’s convenience.

Lemma 3.4

(Proposition A.1
[
11
]
)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{X}:=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{d})
be a vector of independent

-subgaussian random variables for some

. Let

be a convex 1-Lipschitz function. Then for

\mathbb{P}(\left|f(\boldsymbol{X})-\E f(\boldsymbol{X})\right|\geqslant s)\leqslant 2\exp\left(-\frac{cs^{2}}{K^{2}\log d}\right),

where

is some universal constant. Moreover,
\E f(\boldsymbol{X})
can be replaced by any median of

.

Proof of Lemma
3.3
.

We will apply the Proposition A.1 in
[
11
]
for subgaussian random matrix to complete the proof. Note that in our setting, the operator norm is obviously a convex function. On the other hand, since for general matrices

,

\left|\left\|A\right\|-\left\|B\right\|\right|\leqslant\left\|A-B\right\|_{F},

where

denotes the Frobenius norm. This implies the Lipschitz property of the operator norm. There are in total

many nonzero entries in

[

\mathbb{P}(\left|\left\|J\right\|-\E\left\|J\right\|\right|\geqslant t)\leqslant 2\exp\left(-\frac{Cdt^{2}}{\kappa^{4}\log(Nd)}\right).

\blacksquare

Now we put the above two lemmas together to complete the proof of Theorem

.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Using the Assumption
1.3
, it is easy to see that
\E\left\|J\right\|=O(1)
from Lemma
3.2
. On the other hand, since

, the r.h.s in the concentration bound of Lemma

can be bounded as follows,

2\exp\left(-\frac{Cdt^{2}}{K^{2}\log(Nd)}\right)\leqslant 2\exp\left(-\frac{C^{\prime}dt^{2}}{K^{2}\log(N)}\right).

Using

in the Assumption

, we have the desired results.

\blacksquare

\blacksquare

It follows that

\sup_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{\left|A\right|}}\mathbb{P}\bigl(\bigl\|(D_{2}{\sigma}^{(2)})_{i\in A}-\boldsymbol{v}\bigr\|_{2}^{2}\leqslant\delta^{2}N\bigr)\leqslant(C_{1}K\delta)^{N/(32C)}\lesssim 10^{-N},

where
C_{1}
is as given in the Lemma
4.7
and the last inequality follows by taking
\delta>0
sufficiently small constant.

Recall
{m}_{A}({\sigma})=(m_{i}({\sigma}))_{i\in A}
.
Using the previous decomposition of
J
in (
34
), we observe that

\left\|{m}_{A}({\sigma})-\gamma\boldsymbol{1}_{A}\right\|_{2}^{2}\geqslant\bigl\|(D_{1}{\sigma}^{(1)}+D_{2}{\sigma}^{(2)})_{i\in A}-\gamma\boldsymbol{1}_{A}\bigr\|_{2}^{2}.

Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigl(\left\|{m}_{A}({\sigma})-\gamma\boldsymbol{1}_{A}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leqslant\delta^{2}N\bigr)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigl(\bigl\|(D_{1}{\sigma}^{(1)}+D_{2}{\sigma}^{(2)})_{i\in A}-\gamma\boldsymbol{1}_{A}\bigr\|_{2}^{2}\leqslant\delta^{2}N\bigr)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\E_{D_{1}}\Bigl[\mathbb{P}\bigl(\bigl\|(D_{1}{\sigma}^{(1)}+D_{2}{\sigma}^{(2)})_{i\in A}-\gamma\boldsymbol{1}_{A}\bigr\|_{2}^{2}\leqslant\delta^{2}N\big|(D_{1}{\sigma}^{(1)})_{i\in A}\bigr)\Bigr]

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\E_{D_{1}}\Bigl[\sup_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{\left|A\right|}}\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|(D_{2}{\sigma}^{(2)})_{i\in A}-\boldsymbol{v}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leqslant\delta^{2}N\right)\Bigr]

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{\left|A\right|}}\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|(D_{2}{\sigma}^{(2)})_{i\in A}-\boldsymbol{v}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leqslant\delta^{2}N\right)\leqslant\frac{K}{10^{N}}

for some constant
K
. Since
A
is arbitrary subset of
T
with
\left|A\right|\geqslant\left|T\right|/2
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\min_{A\subseteq T:|A|\geqslant\frac{\left|T\right|}{2}}\left\|{m}_{A}({\sigma})-\gamma\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{A}\right\|_{2}\leqslant\delta\sqrt{N}\right)\leqslant\frac{K}{10^{N}}.

\blacksquare

4.2.
Proof of Theorem
1.5

In this section, we will complete the proof of Theorem
1.5
. First, we prove the following result that states that with high probability, for each
{\sigma}
, the variables
m_{i}({\sigma})
are bounded for at least half of the indices in
T
.

Proposition 4.8
.

Assume
d_{\max}\leqslant Cd
. There exist constants
M,K>0
such that the following event

\min_{{\sigma}}|\{i\in T:|m_{i}({\sigma})|\leqslant M\}|\geqslant\frac{|T|}{2}

occurs with probability at least
1-K\cdot 10^{-N}
.

Proof.

Consider the matrix
J_{T}:=(D_{1},D_{2})
, where
D_{1},D_{2}
were defined in (
34
). Note that for each fixed
{\sigma}
,

m_{T}({\sigma})=J_{T}{\sigma}=D_{1}{\sigma}^{(1)}+D_{2}{\sigma}^{(2)},

Decompose
D_{1}
as

D_{1}=D_{1}^{+}+D_{1}^{-},

where

(D_{1}^{+})_{ij}:=\begin{cases}(D_{1})_{ij},&\text{if $i<j$,}\\
0,&\text{if $i\geqslant j$,}\end{cases}\,\,\text{and}\,\,(D_{1}^{-})_{ij}:=\begin{cases}(D_{1})_{ij},&\text{if $i>j$,}\\
0,&\text{if $i\leqslant j$.}\end{cases}

Accordingly, we have the decomposition for
J_{T}=J_{T}^{+}+J_{T}^{-}
with

J_{T}^{+}:=(D_{1}^{+},D_{2})\,\,\text{and}\,\,J_{T}^{-}:=(D_{1}^{-},\boldsymbol{0}).

In this way, it can be seen that the entries in
J_{T}^{+}
are independent and so are the entries of
J_{T}^{-}
. We let
m_{T}^{+}({\sigma}):=J_{T}^{+}{\sigma}
and
m_{T}^{-}({\sigma}):=J_{T}^{-}{\sigma}
, whose coordinates are now independent. For
i\in T
, notice

\displaystyle\E m_{i}^{\pm}({\sigma})=0\,\,\text{and}\,\,\var(m_{i}^{\pm}({\sigma}))=\E[(m_{i}^{\pm}({\sigma}))^{2}]\leqslant\frac{d_{i}}{d}\leqslant C.

The last inequality in the variance bound used the assumption
d_{\max}\leqslant Cd
and the independence among the entries of
J_{T}^{+},J_{T}^{-}
. Consequently, from Chebyshev’s inequality, we have that for
i\in T
,

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\left|m_{i}^{\pm}({\sigma})\right|\geqslant\frac{M}{2}\Bigr)\leqslant\frac{4C}{M^{2}}.

Next, set

\displaystyle B^{+}_{{\sigma}}:=\Bigl\{i\in T,\Big|\left|m_{i}^{+}({\sigma})\right|\leqslant\frac{M}{2}\Bigr\}\,\,\text{and}\,\,B^{-}_{{\sigma}}:=\Bigl\{i\in T,\Big|\left|m_{i}^{-}({\sigma})\right|\leqslant\frac{M}{2}\Bigr\}.

Evidently,

\left|B_{{\sigma}}^{+}\right|\sim\mathrm{Binomial}\left(\left|T\right|,p_{+}\right)\,\,\text{and}\,\,\left|B_{{\sigma}}^{-}\right|\sim\mathrm{Binomial}\left(\left|T\right|,p_{-}\right),

where
p_{-},p_{+}
are some constants greater than
1-{4C}/{M^{2}}
.
Now, by the binomial concentration, one can choose sufficiently large
M
such that

P\Bigl(\left|B_{{\sigma}}^{+}\right|,\left|B_{{\sigma}}^{-}\right|\geqslant\frac{4}{5}\left|T\right|\Bigr)\geqslant 1-\frac{K}{20^{N}}

for some constant
K>0
independent of
N.

Let
B_{{\sigma}}:=B_{{\sigma}}^{+}\cap B_{{\sigma}}^{-}
. Using the identity

|B_{{\sigma}}|=|B_{{\sigma}}^{+}|+|B_{{\sigma}}^{-}|-|B_{{\sigma}}^{+}\cup B_{{\sigma}}^{-}|,

with probability at least
1-K/20^{N}
,

\left|B_{{\sigma}}\right|\geqslant\frac{4}{5}\left|T\right|+\frac{4}{5}\left|T\right|-\left|T\right|\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\left|T\right|,

which readily implies that

\displaystyle\left|m_{i}({\sigma})\right|\leqslant\left|m_{i}^{+}({\sigma})\right|+\left|m_{i}^{-}({\sigma})\right|\leqslant\frac{M}{2}+\frac{M}{2}=M

for
i\in B_{{\sigma}}
and as a consequence,

|\{i\in T:|m_{i}({\sigma})|\leqslant M\}|\geqslant\frac{|T|}{2}.

Finally, applying union bound with resepct to
{\sigma}\in\{-1,+1\}^{N}
leads to the desired result.

\blacksquare

We proceed to establish the proof of Theorem
1.5
via this proposition.

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

For each fixed
{\sigma}
, let

B_{{\sigma}}=\{i\in T:\left|m_{i}({\sigma})\right|\leqslant M\}.

By Proposition
4.8
, there exist a constant
M>0
sufficiently large such that with probability at least
1-K/10^{N}
,
we have
\min_{{\sigma}}\left|B_{{\sigma}}\right|\geqslant{\left|T\right|}/{2}\geqslant{N}/{(32C)}
.
Consequently, for
i\in B_{{\sigma}}

\theta_{i}({\sigma})=\sech(\beta m_{i}({\sigma})+h)\geqslant c^{\prime\prime}:=\sech(\beta M+|h|).

Note that

\displaystyle\widetilde{T}_{N}({\sigma})

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i,j\in B_{{\sigma}}}(m_{i}({\sigma})-m_{j}({\sigma}))^{2}\theta_{i}({\sigma})\theta_{j}({\sigma})

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{(c^{\prime\prime})^{2}}{N^{2}}\sum_{i,j\in B_{{\sigma}}}(m_{i}({\sigma})-m_{j}({\sigma}))^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{2(c^{\prime\prime})^{2}}{N}\sum_{i\in B_{{\sigma}}}(m_{i}({\sigma})-\bar{m}_{B_{{\sigma}}}({\sigma}))^{2},

where
\bar{m}_{B_{{\sigma}}}({\sigma})
is the average of
{m}_{B_{{\sigma}}({\sigma})}({\sigma}).

Therefore, on the event
|B_{{\sigma}}|\geqslant\left|T\right|/{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{T}_{N}({\sigma})

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{2(c^{\prime\prime})^{2}}{N}\inf_{\gamma\in[-M,M]}\sum_{i\in B_{{\sigma}}}(m_{i}({\sigma})-\gamma)^{2}

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{2(c^{\prime\prime})^{2}}{N}\inf_{\gamma\in[-M,M]}\min_{A\subseteq T:|A|\geqslant|T|/2}\sum_{i\in A}(m_{i}({\sigma})-\gamma)^{2}

(37)

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{2(c^{\prime\prime})^{2}}{N}\inf_{\gamma\in\mathcal{D}}\min_{A\subseteq T:|A|\geqslant|T|/2}\Bigl(\left\|{m}_{A}({\sigma})-\gamma\cdot\boldsymbol{1}\right\|_{2}-\frac{\delta\sqrt{N}}{2}\Bigr)_{+}^{2},

where
\mathcal{D}
is an arbitrary partition of
[-M,M]
with mash
(\delta/2)
and
|\mathcal{D}|\leqslant 10M\delta^{-1}
.
Applying Proposition
4.4
for a fixed
\gamma
and
{\sigma}
in (
37
) and then taking a union bound over
\gamma\in\mathcal{D}
and
{\sigma}
, we obtain, for
c_{0}=(c^{\prime\prime})^{2}\delta^{2}/2
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigl(\min_{{\sigma}}\widetilde{T}_{N}({\sigma})\leqslant c_{0}\bigr)\leqslant K10^{-N}+|\mathcal{D}|2^{N}\cdot K10^{-N}.

The proof of Theorem
1.5
now immediately follows.

\blacksquare
