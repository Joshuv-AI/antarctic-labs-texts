Null controllability for the singular heat equation with a memory term

Orabona 470125 Bari - Italyemail: genni.fragnelli@uniba.it Jawad Salhi Faculté des Sciences et Techniques,Université Hassan 1er, Laboratoire MISI,B.P. 577, Settat 26000, Moroccoemail: sj.salhi@gmail.com

Abstract
In this paper we focus on the null controllability problem for the heat equation with the so-called inverse square potential and a memory term. To this aim, we first establish the null controllability for a nonhomogeneous singular heat equation by a new Carleman inequality with weights which do not blow up at t = 0 t=0 . Then the null controllability property is proved for the singular heat equation with memory under a condition on the kernel, by means of Kakutani’s fixed-point Theorem.



Keywords: Controllability, heat equation with memory, singular potential, Carleman estimates

Introduction

In this paper, we address the null controllability for the following singular heat equation with memory:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}y_{t}-y_{xx}-\displaystyle\frac{\mu}{x^{2}}y=\int_{0}^{t}a(t,s,x)y(s,x)\,ds+1_{\omega}u,&(t,x)\in Q,\\
y(t,0)=y(t,1)=0,&t\in(0,T),\\
y(0,x)=y_{0}(x),&x\in(0,1),\end{array}\right.

(1.1)

where
y_{0}\in L^{2}(0,1)
,
T>0
is fixed,
\mu
is a real parameter,

Q:=(0,T)\times(0,1)
and
1_{\omega}
stands for a characteristic function of a nonempty open subset
\omega
of
(0,1)
.
Here
y
and
u
are the state variable and the control variable respectively,
a
is a given
L^{\infty}
function defined on

(0,T)\times Q
.

The analysis of evolution equations involving memory terms is a topic in continuous development.
In the last decades, many researchers have started devoting their attention to this branch of mathematics, motivated by many applications in modelling phenomena in which the processes are affected not only by its current state but also by its history.
Indeed, there is a large spectrum of situations in which the presence of the memory may render the description of the phenomena more accurate.
This is particularly the case for models such as heat conduction in materials with memory, viscoelasticity, theory of population dynamics and nuclear reactors, where there is often a need to reflect the effects of the memory of the system (see for instance
[
3
,
7
,
29
,
35
]
).

Controllability problems for evolution equations with memory terms have been extensively studied in the past. Among other contributions, we mention
[
4
,
20
,
23
,
25
,
26
,
28
,
30
,
36
,
38
]
which, as in our case, deal with parabolic type equations. We also refer to
[
34
]
for an overview of the bibliography on control problems for systems with persistent memory. The first results for a degenerate parabolic equation with memory can be found in
[
1
]
.

In this work, for the first time to our knowledge, we study the null controllability for (
1.1
). We underline that here we consider not only a memory term but also a singular potential one.
In other words, given any
y_{0}\in L^{2}(0,1)
, we want to show that there exists a control function
u\in L^{2}(Q)

such that the corresponding solution
y
to (
1.1
) satisfies

y(T,x)=0
for every
x\in[0,1]
. First results in this direction are obtained in
[
42
]
in the absence of a memory term when
\mu\leq\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}
(see also
[
41
]
for the wave and Schrödinger equations and
[
10
]
for boundary singularity).
Indeed, for the equation

u_{t}-\Delta u-\mu\frac{1}{|x|^{2}}u=0,\quad(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\Omega,

(1.2)

with associated Dirichlet boundary conditions in a bounded domain
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
containing the singularity
x=0
in the interior, the value of the parameter
\mu
determines the behavior of the
equation: if
\mu\leq{1}/{4}
(which is the optimal constant of the
Hardy inequality, see
[
8
]
) global positive solutions exist, while, if
\mu>{1}/{4}
, instantaneous and complete blow-up occurs
(for other comments on this argument we refer to
[
40
]
). In the case of global positive solutions, hence if
\mu\leq\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}
and using Carleman estimates, in
[
42
]
it has been proved that such equations can be controlled (in
any time
T>0
) by a locally distributed control. On the contrary, if
\mu>\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}
, the null controllability fails as shown in
[
13
]
. After these first results, several other works followed extending them in various situations (see for instance
[

,

,

,

,

,

,

,
18
,
19
,
33
,
40
]
).

However, when
\mu=0
and
a=1
, (
1.1
) becomes the following control system
associated to the classical heat equation with memory:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}y_{t}-y_{xx}=\int_{0}^{t}y(s)\,ds+1_{\omega}u,&(t,x)\in Q,\\
y(t,0)=y(t,1)=0,&t\in(0,T),\\
y(0,x)=y_{0}(x),&x\in(0,1).\end{array}\right.

(1.3)

In this case, as shown in
[
23
,
45
]
, there exists a set of initial conditions such that the null controllability property for
(
1.3
) fails whenever the control region

is fixed, independent of time. For some related works in this respect we also refer to

,

.

Nevertheless, since the positive controllability results are important in real world applications, it is natural to analyze whether it is possible that control properties for (
1.1
) could be obtained. For this reason, under suitable conditions on the singularity parameter
\mu
and on the kernel
a
, we establish that (
1.1
) is null controllable.

Our approach is inspired from the techniques presented in the work
[
38
]
for
the Laplace operator, suitably adapted in order to deal with the additional inverse-square potential. In particular, the technique that we will use is based on appropriate Carleman estimates and on the fixed-point Theorem of Kakutani.

The paper is organized as follows: Section

A final comment on the notation: by

2
Nonhomogeneous singular heat equation

In this section, we prove the null controllability for a nonhomogeneous singular heat equation using a new modified Carleman inequality. This null controllability result is the key tool for the controllability of the heat equation with memory. Thus, as a first step, we consider the following problem:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}y_{t}-y_{xx}-\displaystyle\frac{\mu}{x^{2}}y=f+1_{\omega}u(t),&(t,x)\in Q:=(0,T)\times(0,1),\\
y(t,0)=y(t,1)=0,&t\in(0,T),\\
y(0,x)=y_{0}(x),&x\in(0,1),\end{array}\right.

(2.1)

where
f\in L^{2}(Q)
is a given source term.

Prior to null controllability is the well-posedness of (
2.1
), a question
we address in the next subsection.

2.1
Functional framework and well-posedness

We analyze here existence and uniqueness of solutions for the
heat problem (
2.1
). To simplify the presentation, we first focus on the
well-posedness of the following inhomogeneous singular problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}y_{t}-y_{xx}-\displaystyle\frac{\mu}{x^{2}}y=f,&(t,x)\in Q,\\
y(t,0)=y(t,1)=0,&t\in(0,T),\\
y(0,x)=y_{0}(x),&x\in(0,1).\end{array}\right.

(2.2)

In this framework, in order to deal with the singularity of the potential, a fundamental tool is the very famous Hardy inequality.
To fix the ideas, we recall here the basic form of the Hardy inequality in dimension one
(see, for example,
[
27
, Theorem 327]
or
[
12
, Lemma 5.3.1]
):

\frac{1}{4}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{y^{2}}{x^{2}}\,dx\leq\int_{0}^{1}y^{2}_{x}\,dx,

(2.3)

which is valid for every
y\in H^{1}(0,1)
with
y(0)=0
.

Now, for any
\mu\leq\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}
, we define

\displaystyle H_{0}^{1,\mu}(0,1):=\Big\{y\in L^{2}(0,1)\cap H^{1}_{loc}((0,1])\,

\displaystyle\mid z(0)=z(1)=0,

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad\int_{0}^{1}(z_{x}^{2}-\mu\frac{z^{2}}{x^{2}})\,dx<+\infty\Big\}.

Note that
H_{0}^{1,\mu}(0,1)
is a Hilbert space
obtained as the completion of
C^{\infty}_{c}(0,1)
, or
H^{1}_{0}(0,1)
, with respect to the norm

\|y\|_{\mu}:=\Big(\int_{0}^{1}(y_{x}^{2}-\mu\frac{y^{2}}{x^{2}})\,dx\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}},\qquad\forall\;y\in H^{1}_{0}(0,1).

In the case of a sub-critical parameter
\mu<\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}
, thanks to the Hardy inequality (
2.3
),
one can see that
\|\cdot\|_{\mu}
is equivalent to the standard norm of
H^{1}_{0}(0,1)
, and thus
H_{0}^{1,\mu}(0,1)=H_{0}^{1}(0,1)
.
In the critical case
\mu=\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}
, it is proved
(see
[
43
]
) that this identification does not hold anymore and the space
H_{0}^{1,\mu}(0,1)
is slightly (but strictly) larger than

H_{0}^{1}(0,1)
.

Now, define the operator
A:D(A)\subset L^{2}(0,1)\rightarrow L^{2}(0,1)
corresponding to the heat equation
with an inverse square potential in the following way:

Ay:=-y_{xx}-\displaystyle\frac{\mu}{x^{2}}y

\forall\;y\in D(A):=\left\{y\in H^{2}_{loc}((0,1])\cap H_{0}^{1,\mu}(0,1):y_{xx}+\displaystyle\frac{\mu}{x^{2}}y\in L^{2}(0,1)\right\}.

In this context,
A
is self-adjoint, nonpositive on
L^{2}(0,1)
and it generates an analytic semi-group of contractions in
L^{2}(0,1)
for the
equation (
2.2
) (see
[
43
]
). Consequently, the singular heat equation (
2.2
) is well-posed.
To be precise, the next result holds.

Theorem 2.1
.

For all
f\in L^{2}(Q)
and
y_{0}\in L^{2}(0,1)
, there exists a unique solution

y\in\mathcal{W}:=C\big([0,T];L^{2}(0,1)\big)\cap L^{2}\big(0,T;H_{0}^{1,\mu}(0,1)\big)

of (
2.2
) such that

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|y(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,1)}+\int_{0}^{T}\|y(t)\|^{2}_{\mu}dt\leq C_{T}\left(\|y_{0}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,1)}+\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2}(Q)}\right),

(2.4)

for some positive constant
C_{T}
. Moreover, if
y_{0}\in H_{0}^{1,\mu}(0,1)
, then

y\in\mathcal{Z}:=H^{1}\big(0,T;L^{2}(0,1)\big)\cap L^{2}\big(0,T;D(A)\big)\cap C\big([0,T];H_{0}^{1,\mu}(0,1)\big),

(2.5)

and there exists a positive constant
C
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left(\|y(t)\|^{2}_{\mu}\right)+\int_{0}^{T}\left(\left\|y_{t}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,1)}+\left\|y_{xx}+\frac{\mu}{x^{2}}y\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,1)}\right)dt\leq C\left(\|y_{0}\|^{2}_{\mu}+\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2}(Q)}\right).

(2.6)

Proof.

In
[
43
]
, the authors use semigroup theory to obtain the well-posedness result for the problem (
2.2
) (see also
[
33
]
).
Thus, in the rest of the proof, we will prove only (
2.4
)-(
2.6
). First, being

A
the generator of a strongly continuous semigroup on

L^{2}(0,1)
, if
y_{0}\in L^{2}(0,1)
, then the solution
y
of (
2.2
) belongs to
C\big([0,T];L^{2}(0,1)\big)\cap L^{2}\big(0,T;H^{1,\mu}_{0}(0,1)\big)
, while,
if
y_{0}\in D(A)
, then
y\in H^{1}\big(0,T;L^{2}(0,1)\big)\cap L^{2}\big(0,T;D(A)\big)
.

Now, by a usual energy method we shall prove (
2.5
) and (
2.6
), from which the last required
regularity property for
y
will follow by standard linear arguments.
First, take
y_{0}\in D(A)
and multiply the equation of (
2.2
) by
y
.
By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality we obtain for every
t\in(0,T]
,

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|y(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,1)}+\|y(t)\|^{2}_{\mu}\leq\frac{1}{2}\|f(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,1)}+\frac{1}{2}\|y(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,1)}.

(2.7)

From (
2.7
) and using Gronwall’s inequality, we get

\|y(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,1)}\leq e^{T}\left(\|y(0)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,1)}+\|f\|_{L^{2}(Q)}^{2}\right)

(2.8)

for every
t\leq T
. From
(
2.7
) and (
2.8
) we immediately obtain

\int_{0}^{T}\|y(t)\|^{2}_{\mu}dt\leq C_{T}\left(\|y(0)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,1)}+\|f\|_{L^{2}(Q)}^{2}\right)

(2.9)

for some universal constant
C_{T}>0
. Thus, by
(
2.8
) and (
2.9
), (
2.4
) follows
if
y_{0}\in D(A)
. Since
D(A)
is dense in
L^{2}(0,1)
(see
[
39
,
43
]
),
the same inequality holds if
y_{0}\in L^{2}(0,1)
.

Now, multipling the equation by
\displaystyle-y_{xx}-\frac{\mu}{x^{2}}y
, integrating on
(0,1)
and using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,
we easily get

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\|y(t)\|^{2}_{\mu}+\|y_{xx}(t)+\frac{\mu}{x^{2}}y(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,1)}\leq\|f(t)\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}^{2}

for every
t\in[0,T]
, so that, as before, we find
C_{T}^{\prime}>0
such that

\|y(t)\|^{2}_{\mu}+\int_{0}^{T}\|y_{xx}(t)+\frac{\mu}{x^{2}}y(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,1)}dt\leq C_{T}^{\prime}\left(\|y(0)\|_{\mu}+\|f\|_{L^{2}(Q)}^{2}\right)

(2.10)

for every
t\!\leq\!T
.​
Finally, from
y_{t}=y_{xx}+\displaystyle\frac{\mu}{x^{2}}y+f
, squaring and integrating on
Q
,
we find

\!\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\|y_{t}(t)\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}^{2}\!\leq\!C\left(\!\int_{0}^{T}\|y_{xx}+\frac{\mu}{x^{2}}y\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,1)}\!\!+\!\!\|f\|_{L^{2}(Q)}^{2}\!\right),

and together with (
2.10
) we find

\int_{0}^{T}\|y_{t}(t)\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}^{2}\leq C\left(\|y(0)\|^{2}_{\mu}+\|f\|_{L^{2}(Q)}^{2}\right).

(2.11)

In conclusion, (
2.7
), (
2.8
), (
2.10
) and (
2.11
)
give (
2.4
) and (
2.6
). Notice that, (
2.5
) and (
2.6
)
hold also if
y_{0}\in H_{0}^{1,\mu}(0,1)
.
∎

2.2
Carleman estimates for a singular problem

In this subsection we prove a new Carleman estimate for the adjoint parabolic equation associated to (
2.1
), which will provide that the nonhomogeneous singular heat equation (
2.1
) is null controllable.
Hence, in the following, we concentrate on the
next adjoint problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-z_{t}-z_{xx}-\displaystyle\frac{\mu}{x^{2}}z=g,&(t,x)\in Q,\\
z(t,0)=z(t,1)=0,&t\in(0,T),\\
z(T,x)=z_{T}(x),&x\in(0,1).\end{array}\right.

(2.12)

Following
[
42
]
, for every
0<\gamma<2
, let us introduce the weight
function

\varphi(t,x):=\theta(t)\psi(x),

(2.13)

where

\psi(x):=\mathfrak{c}(x^{2}-d),\quad\theta(t):=\left(\frac{1}{t(T-t)}\right)^{k},\quad k:=1+\frac{2}{\gamma},

(2.14)

\mathfrak{c}>0
and
d>1
. A more precise restriction on the parameters
k,\mathfrak{c}
and
d
will be needed later.
Observe that

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\theta(t)=\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow T^{-}}\theta(t)=+\infty,
and

\psi(x)<0\qquad\text{for every}\quad x\in[0,1].

Using the previous weight functions and the following improved Hardy-Poincaré inequality given in
[
40
]
:

For all
\eta>0
, there exists some positive
constant
C=C(\eta)>0
such that, for all
z\in C^{\infty}_{c}(0,1):

\int_{0}^{1}x^{\eta}z_{x}^{2}\,dx\leq C\int_{0}^{1}\left(z_{x}^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\frac{z^{2}}{x^{2}}\right)\,dx,

(2.15)

one can prove the following Carleman estimate for the case of a purely singular parabolic equation:

Lemma 2.1
.

[
40
, Theorem 5.1]

Assume that
\mu\leq\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}
. Then, there exists

C>0
and
s_{0}>0
such that, for all
s\geq s_{0}
, every solution
z
of (
2.12
)
satisfies

\displaystyle\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s^{3}\theta^{3}x^{2}z^{2}e^{2s\varphi}\,dx\,dt+\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s\theta\big(z_{x}^{2}-\mu\frac{z^{2}}{x^{2}}\big)e^{2s\varphi}\,dx\,dt+\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s\theta\frac{z^{2}}{x^{\gamma}}e^{2s\varphi}\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle\leq C\Big(\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}g^{2}e^{2s\varphi}\,dx\,dt+\int_{0}^{T}s\theta z_{x}^{2}(t,1)e^{2s\varphi(t,1)}\,dx\,dt\Big).

(2.16)

Observe that, if the term

\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s\theta\big(z_{x}^{2}-\mu\frac{z^{2}}{x^{2}}\big)e^{2s\varphi}\,dx\,dt

is not positive, then the estimate (
2.1
) is not of great importance.
In fact, the Hardy inequality (
2.3
) only ensures the positivity of of the quantity

\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s\theta\big(z_{x}^{2}-\mu\frac{z^{2}}{x^{2}}\big)\,dx\,dt.

However, from
[
40
, Remark 3]
and similarly
as in
[
24
]
, we will rewrite the result given in Lemma
2.1
in a more practical way.

Lemma 2.2
.

Assume that
\mu\leq\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}
. Then, there exist

C>0
and
s_{0}>0
such that, for all
s\geq s_{0}
, every solution
z
of (
2.12
)
satisfies

\mathfrak{J}_{\varphi,\eta,\gamma}(z)\leq C\Big(\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}g^{2}e^{2s\varphi}\,dx\,dt+\int_{0}^{T}s\theta z_{x}^{2}(t,1)e^{2s\varphi(t,1)}\,dx\,dt\Big),

(2.17)

where

\displaystyle\mathfrak{J}_{\varphi,\eta,\gamma}(z)

\displaystyle=\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s^{3}\theta^{3}x^{2}z^{2}e^{2s\varphi}\,dx\,dt+\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s\theta z_{x}^{2}e^{2s\varphi}\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle\quad+\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s\theta\frac{z^{2}}{x^{2}}e^{2s\varphi}\,dx\,dt+\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s\theta\frac{z^{2}}{x^{\gamma}}e^{2s\varphi}\,dx\,dt,

(2.18)

if
\mu<\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}
, and

\displaystyle\mathfrak{J}_{\varphi,\eta,\gamma}(z)

\displaystyle=\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s^{3}\theta^{3}x^{2}z^{2}e^{2s\varphi}\,dx\,dt+\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s\theta x^{\eta}z_{x}^{2}e^{2s\varphi}\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle\quad+\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s\theta\frac{z^{2}}{x^{\gamma}}e^{2s\varphi}\,dx\,dt,

(2.19)

if
\mu=\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}
. Here
\gamma
is as in (
2.14
)

Proof.

Case 1: If
\mu<\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}
.

Let
Z=ze^{s\varphi}
. In order to prove
[
40
, Theorem 5.1]
, the author has derived the following estimate

\displaystyle\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s^{3}\theta^{3}x^{2}Z^{2}\,dx\,dt+\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s\theta\big(Z_{x}^{2}-\mu\frac{Z^{2}}{x^{2}}\big)\,dx\,dt+\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s\theta\frac{Z^{2}}{x^{\gamma}}\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle\leq C\Big(\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}g^{2}e^{2s\varphi}\,dx\,dt+\int_{0}^{T}s\theta Z_{x}^{2}(t,1)\,dx\,dt\Big).

(2.20)

Let
\delta<\inf(1,(1-4\mu))
be a fixed positive constant. We have

\displaystyle\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s\theta\big(Z_{x}^{2}-\mu\frac{Z^{2}}{x^{2}}\big)\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle=(1-\delta)\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s\theta\big(Z_{x}^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\frac{Z^{2}}{x^{2}}\big)\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle\quad+\delta\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s\theta Z_{x}^{2}\,dx\,dt+\left(\frac{1}{4}(1-\delta)-\mu\right)\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s\theta\frac{Z^{2}}{x^{2}}\,dx\,dt.

(2.21)

By (
2.2
) and (
2.2
), we obtain

\displaystyle\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s^{3}\theta^{3}x^{2}Z^{2}\,dx\,dt+(1-\delta)\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s\theta\big(Z_{x}^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\frac{Z^{2}}{x^{2}}\big)\,dx\,dt+\delta\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s\theta Z_{x}^{2}\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle+\left(\frac{1}{4}(1-\delta)-\mu\right)\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s\theta\frac{Z^{2}}{x^{2}}\,dx\,dt+\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s\theta\frac{Z^{2}}{x^{\gamma}}\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle\leq C\Big(\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}g^{2}e^{2s\varphi}\,dx\,dt+\int_{0}^{T}s\theta Z_{x}^{2}(t,1)\,dx\,dt\Big).

On the other hand, from (
2.15
), for all
\eta>0
there exists a constant
c_{0}=c_{0}(\eta)>0
such that

\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s\theta\big(Z_{x}^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\frac{Z^{2}}{x^{2}}\big)\,dx\,dt\geq c_{0}\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s\theta x^{\eta}Z_{x}^{2}\,dx\,dt.

(2.22)

Hence,

\displaystyle\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s^{3}\theta^{3}x^{2}Z^{2}\,dx\,dt+(1-\delta)c_{0}\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s\theta x^{\eta}Z_{x}^{2}\,dx\,dt+\delta\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s\theta Z_{x}^{2}\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle+\big(\frac{1}{4}(1-\delta)-\mu\big)\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s\theta\frac{Z^{2}}{x^{2}}\,dx\,dt+\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s\theta\frac{Z^{2}}{x^{\gamma}}\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle\leq C\Big(\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}g^{2}e^{2s\varphi}\,dx\,dt+\int_{0}^{T}s\theta Z_{x}^{2}(t,1)\,dx\,dt\Big).

(2.23)

Using the definition of
Z
, we have

Z^{2}=z^{2}e^{2s\varphi},

(2.24)

\displaystyle Z_{x}=z_{x}e^{s\varphi}+s\theta\psi_{x}Z\quad\text{and}\quad z_{x}^{2}e^{2s\varphi}\leq 2Z_{x}^{2}+cs^{2}\theta^{2}x^{2}Z^{2},

(2.25)

for a positive constant
c
.
Then,

\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s\theta z_{x}^{2}e^{2s\varphi}\,dx\,dt\leq 2\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s\theta Z_{x}^{2}\,dx\,dt+c\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s^{3}\theta^{3}x^{2}Z^{2}\,dx\,dt.

(2.26)

Combining (
2.2
)-(
2.26
), we obtain the desired estimate (
2.17
). Indeed, defining

a_{0}=\min\left\{\frac{1}{1+c},\frac{\delta}{2},\left(\frac{1}{4}(1-\delta)-\mu\right)\right\}>0,

we have

\displaystyle a_{0}\left(\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s^{3}\theta^{3}x^{2}z^{2}e^{2s\varphi}\,dx\,dt+\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s\theta z_{x}^{2}e^{2s\varphi}\,dx\,dt+\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s\theta\frac{z^{2}}{x^{2}}e^{2s\varphi}\,dx\,dt+\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s\theta\frac{z^{2}}{x^{\gamma}}e^{2s\varphi}\,dx\,dt\right)

\displaystyle\leq a_{0}\left((1+c)\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s^{3}\theta^{3}x^{2}Z^{2}\,dx\,dt+2\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s\theta Z_{x}^{2}dxdt+\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s\theta\frac{Z^{2}}{x^{2}}\,dx\,dt+\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s\theta\frac{Z^{2}}{x^{\gamma}}\,dx\,dt\right)

\displaystyle\leq\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s^{3}\theta^{3}x^{2}Z^{2}\,dx\,dt+\delta\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s\theta Z_{x}^{2}dxdt+\left(\frac{1}{4}(1-\delta)-\mu\right)\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s\theta\frac{Z^{2}}{x^{2}}\,dx\,dt+\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s\theta\frac{Z^{2}}{x^{\gamma}}\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle\leq\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s^{3}\theta^{3}x^{2}Z^{2}\,dx\,dt+(1-\delta)c_{0}\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s\theta x^{\eta}Z_{x}^{2}\,dx\,dt+\delta\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s\theta Z_{x}^{2}\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle+\left(\frac{1}{4}(1-\delta)-\mu\right)\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s\theta\frac{Z^{2}}{x^{2}}\,dx\,dt+\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s\theta\frac{Z^{2}}{x^{\gamma}}\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle\leq C\Big(\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}g^{2}e^{2s\varphi}\,dx\,dt+\int_{0}^{T}s\theta Z_{x}^{2}(t,1)\,dx\,dt\Big).

Thus, the conclusion follows.

Case 2: If
\mu=\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}
.

As before, let

and define

and

by (

), (
2.22
), (
2.24
) and (
2.25
), that still hold if
\mu=\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}
, we have

\displaystyle a_{0}\left(\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s^{3}\theta^{3}x^{2}z^{2}e^{2s\varphi}\,dx\,dt+\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s\theta x^{\eta}z_{x}^{2}e^{2s\varphi}\,dx\,dt+\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s\theta\frac{z^{2}}{x^{\gamma}}e^{2s\varphi}\,dx\,dt\right)

(2.27)

\displaystyle\leq a_{0}\left(\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s^{3}\theta^{3}x^{2}Z^{2}\,dx\,dt+2\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s\theta x^{\eta}Z_{x}^{2}\,dx\,dt+c\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s^{3}\theta^{3}x^{2}Z^{2}\,dx\,dt+\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s\theta\frac{Z^{2}}{x^{\gamma}}\,dx\,dt\right)

\displaystyle\leq a_{0}(1+c)\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s^{3}\theta^{3}x^{2}Z^{2}\,dx\,dt+a_{0}\frac{2}{c_{0}}\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s\theta\big(Z_{x}^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\frac{Z^{2}}{x^{2}}\big)\,dx\,dt+a_{0}\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}s\theta\frac{Z^{2}}{x^{\gamma}}\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle(\text{by \eqref{step1}})

\displaystyle\leq C\Big(\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}g^{2}e^{2s\varphi}\,dx\,dt+\int_{0}^{T}s\theta z_{x}^{2}(t,1)e^{2s\varphi(t,1)}\,dx\,dt\Big).

Hence, also in this case the conclusion follows.

We point out that the Carleman estimates stated above are not appropriate to achieve our goal. In fact, all these estimates does not have the observation term in the interior of the domain. However, we use them to obtain the main Carleman estimate stated in Proposition
2.2
.
More precisely, from the boundary Carleman estimates (
2.17
), we will deduce a global Carleman estimate for the adjoint problem (
2.12
)
with a distributed observation on a subregion

\omega^{\prime}:=(\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})\subset\subset\omega.

(2.28)

To do so, we recall the following weight functions associated to nonsingular Carleman estimates which are suited to our purpose:

\Phi(t,x):=\theta(t)\Psi(x)

where
\theta
is defined in (

) and
\Psi(x)=e^{\rho\sigma}-e^{2\rho\|\sigma\|_{\infty}}
. Here
\rho>0
,

is such that
\sigma(x)>0
in
(0,1)

\sigma(0)=\sigma(1)=0

\sigma_{x}(x)\neq 0
in
[0,1]\setminus\tilde{\omega}
, being
\tilde{\omega}
an arbitrary open subset of
\omega

In the following, we choose the constant

in (

\mathfrak{c}\geq\frac{e^{2\rho\|\sigma\|_{\infty}}-1}{d-1}.

By this choice one can prove that the function

\varphi(t,x)\leq\Phi(t,x)\qquad\text{for every}\quad(t,x)\in[0,T]\times[0,1].

Thanks to this property, we can prove the main Carleman estimate of this paper whose proof is based also on the following Caccioppoli’s inequality:

Proposition 2.1

(Caccioppoli’s inequality)
.

Let
\omega^{\prime}
and
\omega^{\prime\prime}
be two nonempty open subsets of

such that
\overline{\omega^{\prime\prime}}\subset\omega^{\prime}

\phi(t,x)=\theta(t)\varrho(x)

\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q_{\omega^{\prime\prime}}}z_{x}^{2}e^{2s\phi}\,dx\,dt\leq C\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q_{\omega^{\prime}}}(g^{2}+s^{2}\theta^{2}z^{2})e^{2s\phi}\,dx\,dt,

where

.

The proof of the previous result is similar to the one given, for instance, in

Now, we are ready to prove the following result:

Proposition 2.2
.

Assume that
\mu\leq\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}
. Then, there exist two positive constants

and
s_{0}
such that, the solution
z
of equation (

) satisfies, for all

\mathfrak{J}_{\varphi,\eta,\gamma}(z)\leq C\Big(\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}g^{2}e^{2s\Phi}\,dx\,dt+\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q_{\omega^{\prime}}}s^{3}\theta^{3}z^{2}e^{2s\Phi}\,dx\,dt\Big).

(2.31)

Here
\mathfrak{J}_{\varphi,\eta,\gamma}(\cdot)
is defined in (
2.2
) or (
2.2
).

Proof.

Let us set

\omega^{\prime\prime}=(\alpha^{\prime\prime},\beta^{\prime\prime})\subset\subset\omega^{\prime}

and consider a smooth cut-off function
\xi\in C^{\infty}([0,1])
such that
0\leq\xi(x)\leq 1
for

,
\xi(x)=1
for

and
\xi(x)=0
for
x\in[\beta^{\prime\prime},1]
. Define
w:=\xi z
where

is the solution of (

). Then,

satisfies the following problem:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}-w_{t}-w_{xx}-\displaystyle\frac{\mu}{x^{2}}w=\xi g-\xi_{xx}z-2\xi_{x}z_{x},&&(t,x)\in Q,\\
w(t,1)=w(t,0)=0,&&t\in(0,T),\\

(2.32)

First of all, we prove the first intermediate Carleman estimate for
z
in
(0,T)\times(0,\alpha^{\prime})
(recall that

in
[0,\alpha^{\prime}]
):

\displaystyle\mathfrak{J}_{\varphi,\eta,\gamma}(w)

\displaystyle\leq C\Big(\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}\xi^{2}g^{2}e^{2s\varphi}\,dx\,dt+\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q_{\omega^{\prime}}}(g^{2}+s^{2}\theta^{2}z^{2})e^{2s\varphi}\,dx\,dt\Big)

(2.33)

\displaystyle\leq C\Big(\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}\xi^{2}g^{2}e^{2s\Phi}\,dx\,dt+\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q_{\omega^{\prime}}}(g^{2}+s^{2}\theta^{2}z^{2})e^{2s\Phi}\,dx\,dt\Big).

The second inequality in (

) follows by (
2.29

).
Applying the Carleman estimate (
2.17
) to (

\mathfrak{J}_{\varphi,\eta,\gamma}(w)\leq C\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}\Big(\xi^{2}g^{2}+\big(\xi_{xx}z+2\xi_{x}z_{x}\big)^{2}\Big)e^{2s\varphi}\,dx\,dt.

(2.34)

From the definition of
\xi
and the Caccioppoli inequality (

), we obtain

\displaystyle\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}\big(\xi_{xx}z+2\xi_{x}z_{x}\big)^{2}e^{2s\varphi}\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle\leq C\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q_{\omega^{\prime\prime}}}(z^{2}+z_{x}^{2})e^{2s\varphi}\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle\leq C\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q_{\omega^{\prime}}}(g^{2}+s^{2}\theta^{2}z^{2})e^{2s\varphi}\,dx\,dt.

(2.35)

Combining (
2.34
) and (
2.2
) we obtain (
2.33
).

Now, using the non degenerate Carleman estimate of

[
21
, Lemma 1.2]
, we are going to show a second estimate of
z
in
(0,T)\times(\beta^{\prime},1)
.
For this purpose, let
v=\zeta z
where
\zeta:=1-\xi
(hence

in
[\beta^{\prime},1]
).
Clearly, the function
v
is a solution of the uniformly parabolic equation

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}-v_{t}-v_{xx}-\displaystyle\frac{\mu}{x^{2}}v=\zeta g-\zeta_{xx}z-2\zeta_{x}z_{x},&&(t,x)\in(0,T)\times(\alpha^{\prime},1),\\
v(t,1)=v(t,\alpha^{\prime})=0,&&t\in(0,T),\\

(2.36)

Since
\zeta
has its support in
[\alpha^{\prime\prime},\beta^{\prime\prime}]
,

[

, Lemma 1.2]
we have

\displaystyle\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}\Big(s\theta v_{x}^{2}+s^{3}\theta^{3}v^{2}\Big)e^{2s\Phi}\,dx\,dt=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\alpha^{\prime}}^{1}\Big(s\theta v_{x}^{2}+s^{3}\theta^{3}v^{2}\Big)e^{2s\Phi}\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle\leq C\Bigg(\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\alpha^{\prime}}^{1}\Big(\zeta^{2}g^{2}+\big(\zeta_{xx}z+2\zeta_{x}z_{x}\big)^{2}\Big)e^{2s\Phi}\,dx\,dt+\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q_{\omega^{\prime\prime}}}s^{3}\theta^{3}v^{2}e^{2s\Phi}\,dx\,dt\Bigg)

\displaystyle\leq C\Bigg(\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}\zeta^{2}g^{2}e^{2s\Phi}\,dx\,dt+\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q_{\omega^{\prime\prime}}}(z^{2}+z^{2}_{x})e^{2s\Phi}\,dx\,dt+\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q_{\omega^{\prime\prime}}}s^{3}\theta^{3}v^{2}e^{2s\Phi}\,dx\,dt\Bigg).

Therefore, by the previous estimate, by (
2.29
) and using the Caccioppoli inequality (
2.30
), we
deduce

\displaystyle\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}\Big(s\theta v_{x}^{2}+s^{3}\theta^{3}v^{2}\Big)e^{2s\varphi}\,dx\,dt\leq\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}\Big(s\theta v_{x}^{2}+s^{3}\theta^{3}v^{2}\Big)e^{2s\Phi}\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle\leq C\Bigg(\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}\zeta^{2}g^{2}e^{2s\Phi}\,dx\,dt+\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q_{\omega^{\prime}}}\big(g^{2}+s^{3}\theta^{3}z^{2}\big)e^{2s\Phi}\,dx\,dt\Bigg).

Thus, since
v=\zeta z
has its support in
[0,T]\times[\alpha^{\prime\prime},1]
, that is far away from the singularity point

, one can prove that there exists a constant

such that:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{J}_{\varphi,\eta,\gamma}(v)

\displaystyle\leq C\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}\Big(s\theta v_{x}^{2}+s^{3}\theta^{3}v^{2}\Big)e^{2s\varphi}\,dx\,dt

(2.38)

\displaystyle(\text{by \eqref{star9}})

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}\zeta^{2}g^{2}e^{2s\Phi}\,dx\,dt+\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q_{\omega^{\prime}}}\big(g^{2}+s^{3}\theta^{3}z^{2}\big)e^{2s\Phi}\,dx\,dt\right).

Note that

z^{2}=(w+v)^{2}\leq 2(w^{2}+v^{2})\qquad\text{and}\qquad z_{x}^{2}=(w_{x}+v_{x})^{2}\leq 2(w_{x}^{2}+v_{x}^{2}).

Therefore, adding (
2.33
) and (
2.38
), (
2.31
) follows immediately.

For our purposes in the next section, we concentrate now on a Carleman
inequality for solutions of (
2.12
) obtained via weight functions not exploding at

.
To this end, we will apply a classical argument that can be found, for instance, in
[

]
and recently in
[

]
for a degenerate parabolic equation with memory.
More precisely, let us consider the function:

\nu(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\theta(\frac{T}{2}),&t\in\left[0,\displaystyle\frac{T}{2}\right],\\
\theta(t),&t\in\left[\displaystyle\frac{T}{2},T\right],\end{array}\right.

(2.39)

and the following associated weight functions:

\displaystyle\tilde{\varphi}(t,x):=\nu(t)\psi(x),\qquad\tilde{\Phi}(t,x):=\nu(t)\Psi(x),

(2.40)

\displaystyle\hat{\Phi}(t):=\displaystyle\max_{x\in[0,1]}\tilde{\Phi}(t,x),\quad\hat{\varphi}(t):=\displaystyle\max_{x\in[0,1]}\tilde{\varphi}(t,x)\quad\text{and}\quad\check{\varphi}(t):=\displaystyle\min_{x\in[0,1]}\tilde{\varphi}(t,x).

Now we are ready to state and prove this new modified Carleman estimate for the adjoint problem (
2.12
).

Lemma 2.3
.

Assume that
\mu\leq\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}
. Then, there exist two positive constants
C
and
s_{0}
such that every solution
z
of (
2.12
) satisfies, for all
s\geq s_{0}

\displaystyle\|e^{s\hat{\varphi}(0)}z(0)\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}^{2}+\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}\nu z^{2}e^{2s\tilde{\varphi}}\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle\leq Ce^{2s[\hat{\varphi}(0)-\check{\varphi}(\frac{5T}{8})]}\Big(\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}g^{2}e^{2s\tilde{\Phi}}\,dx\,dt+\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q_{\omega}}s^{3}\nu^{3}z^{2}e^{2s\tilde{\Phi}}\,dx\,dt\Big).

(2.41)

Proof.

By the definitions of
\nu
and
\tilde{\varphi}
and using Proposition
2.2
, it results that there exists a positive constant

C
such that all the solutions to equation (
2.12
)
satisfy

\displaystyle\int_{\frac{T}{2}}^{T}\!\!\!\!\int_{0}^{1}\nu z^{2}e^{2s\tilde{\varphi}}\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{\frac{T}{2}}^{T}\!\!\!\!\int_{0}^{1}\theta z^{2}e^{2s\varphi}\,dx\,dt\leq C\int_{\frac{T}{2}}^{T}\!\!\!\!\int_{0}^{1}s\theta\frac{z^{2}}{x^{\gamma}}e^{2s\varphi}\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle\leq C\Big(\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}g^{2}e^{2s\Phi}\,dx\,dt+\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q_{\omega^{\prime}}}s^{3}\theta^{3}z^{2}e^{2s\Phi}\,dx\,dt\Big).

(2.42)

Let us introduce a function
\tau\in\mathrm{C}^{1}([0,T])
such that

\tau=1
in
\left[0,\displaystyle\frac{T}{2}\right]
and
\tau\equiv 0
in
\left[\displaystyle\frac{5T}{8},T\right]
. Denote
\tilde{\tau}=e^{s\hat{\varphi}(0)}\sqrt{\nu}\tau
, where

e^{s\hat{\varphi}(0)}=\displaystyle\max_{0\leq t\leq T}e^{s\hat{\varphi}(t)}
.

Let
\tilde{z}=\tilde{\tau}z
, then
\tilde{z}
satisfies

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\tilde{z}_{t}-\tilde{z}_{xx}-\displaystyle\frac{\mu}{x^{2}}\tilde{z}=-\tilde{\tau}_{t}z+\tilde{\tau}g,&(t,x)\in Q,\\
\tilde{z}(t,0)=\tilde{z}(t,1)=0,&t\in(0,T),\\
\tilde{z}(T,x)=0,&x\in(0,1).\end{array}\right.

(2.43)

Thanks to the estimate of
\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\tilde{z}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,1)}
(see the energy estimate (
2.4
)), we have

\|\tilde{z}(0)\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}^{2}+\|\tilde{z}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}^{2}\leq C\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}(\tilde{\tau}_{t}z+\tilde{\tau}g)^{2}\,dx\,dt,

which implies

\nu(0)\|e^{s\hat{\varphi}(0)}z(0)\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}^{2}+\|e^{s\hat{\varphi}(0)}\sqrt{\nu}\tau z\|_{L^{2}(Q)}^{2}\leq C\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}(\tilde{\tau}_{t}z+\tilde{\tau}g)^{2}\,dx\,dt.

By using the boundedness of
\theta
in
\left[\displaystyle\frac{T}{2},\displaystyle\frac{5T}{8}\right]
, the definitions of
\tau
and of
\nu
in
\left[\displaystyle 0,\displaystyle\frac{5T}{8}\right]
and the fact that
\nu_{t}(t)=0
in
\left[0,\displaystyle\frac{T}{2}\right]
and
\tau(t)=0
in
\left[\displaystyle\frac{5T}{8},T\right]
, it holds that

\displaystyle\bar{c}\left(\|e^{s\hat{\varphi}(0)}z(0)\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}^{2}+\int_{0}^{\frac{5T}{8}}\!\!\!\!\int_{0}^{1}\nu\tau^{2}z^{2}e^{2s\hat{\varphi}}\,dx\,dt\right)

\displaystyle\leq\nu(0)\|e^{s\hat{\varphi}(0)}z(0)\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}^{2}+\int_{0}^{\frac{5T}{8}}\!\!\!\!\int_{0}^{1}\nu\tau^{2}z^{2}e^{2s\hat{\varphi}}\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle\leq C\Big(\int_{\frac{T}{2}}^{\frac{5T}{8}}\!\!\!\!\int_{0}^{1}(\theta^{2}(t)+\theta(t))z^{2}e^{2s\hat{\varphi}(0)}\,dx\,dt+\int_{0}^{\frac{5T}{8}}\!\!\!\!\int_{0}^{1}\nu g^{2}e^{2s\hat{\varphi}(0)}\,dx\,dt\Big)

\displaystyle\leq C\Big(\int_{\frac{T}{2}}^{\frac{5T}{8}}\!\!\!\!\int_{0}^{1}z^{2}e^{2s\hat{\varphi}(0)}\,dx\,dt+\int_{0}^{\frac{5T}{8}}\!\!\!\!\int_{0}^{1}g^{2}e^{2s\hat{\varphi}(0)}\,dx\,dt\Big),

where
\bar{c}:=\min\{\nu(0),1\}
.
That is,

\displaystyle\|e^{s\hat{\varphi}(0)}z(0)\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}^{2}+\int_{0}^{\frac{T}{2}}\!\!\!\!\int_{0}^{1}\nu z^{2}e^{2s\tilde{\varphi}}\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle\leq C\Big(\int_{\frac{T}{2}}^{\frac{5T}{8}}\!\!\!\!\int_{0}^{1}z^{2}e^{2s(\hat{\varphi}(0)-\tilde{\varphi})}e^{2s\tilde{\varphi}}\,dx\,dt+\int_{0}^{\frac{5T}{8}}\!\!\!\!\int_{0}^{1}g^{2}e^{2s(\hat{\varphi}(0)-\tilde{\varphi})}e^{2s\tilde{\varphi}}\,dx\,dt\Big).

Observe that

\check{\varphi}\left(\frac{5T}{8}\right)\leq\tilde{\varphi}\;\quad\text{in}\quad\left(0,\frac{5T}{8}\right)\times(0,1)

so that,

\displaystyle\|e^{s\hat{\varphi}(0)}z(0)\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}^{2}+\int_{0}^{\frac{T}{2}}\!\!\!\!\int_{0}^{1}\nu z^{2}e^{2s\tilde{\varphi}}\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle\leq Ce^{2s(\hat{\varphi}(0)-\check{\varphi}(\frac{5T}{8}))}\Big(\int_{\frac{T}{2}}^{\frac{5T}{8}}\!\!\!\!\int_{0}^{1}z^{2}e^{2s\tilde{\varphi}}\,dx\,dt+\int_{0}^{\frac{5T}{8}}\!\!\!\!\int_{0}^{1}g^{2}e^{2s\tilde{\varphi}}\,dx\,dt\Big).

(2.44)

As in (
2.2
), one can prove that there exists a positive constant

C
such that

\int_{\frac{T}{2}}^{\frac{5T}{8}}\!\!\!\!\int_{0}^{1}z^{2}e^{2s\tilde{\varphi}}\,dx\,dt\leq C\Big(\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}g^{2}e^{2s\Phi}\,dx\,dt+\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q_{\omega}}s^{3}\theta^{3}z^{2}e^{2s\Phi}\,dx\,dt\Big).

Using this last inequality in (
2.2
), we have

\displaystyle\|e^{s\hat{\varphi}(0)}z(0)\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}^{2}

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{\frac{T}{2}}\!\!\!\!\int_{0}^{1}\nu z^{2}e^{2s\tilde{\varphi}}\,dx\,dt\leq Ce^{2s(\hat{\varphi}(0)-\check{\varphi}(\frac{5T}{8}))}\Big(\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}g^{2}e^{2s\Phi}\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle+\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q_{\omega}}s^{3}\theta^{3}z^{2}e^{2s\Phi}\,dx\,dt+\int_{0}^{\frac{5T}{8}}\!\!\!\!\int_{0}^{1}g^{2}e^{2s\tilde{\varphi}}\,dx\,dt\Big).

(2.45)

From (
2.29
) and by the definition of the modified
weights, notice that, in particular
\tilde{\varphi}\leq\tilde{\Phi}
and
\Phi\leq\tilde{\Phi}
in
Q
.
This, together with (
2.2
) and (
2.2
), implies that

\displaystyle\|e^{s\hat{\varphi}(0)}z(0)\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}^{2}

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\!\int_{0}^{1}\nu z^{2}e^{2s\tilde{\varphi}}\,dx\,dt\leq Ce^{2s(\hat{\varphi}(0)-\check{\varphi}(\frac{5T}{8}))}\Big(\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}g^{2}e^{2s\tilde{\Phi}}\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle+\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q_{\omega}}s^{3}\theta^{3}z^{2}e^{2s\Phi}\,dx\,dt\Big).

(2.46)

To conclude, it suffices to remark that for

, the function

is nonincreasing for

sufficiently large.
So, since
\nu(t)\leq\theta(t)
by taking

s^{3}\theta^{3}e^{2s\Phi}\leq s^{3}\nu^{3}e^{2s\tilde{\Phi}},

which, together with (

), provides the desired inequality.
∎

2.3
Null controllability result

Following the classical method as in
[
21
]
, with the modified Carleman inequality proved in the previous subsection, we can get a null controllability result for
(
2.1
). However, as explained in
[
38
]
, this null controllability result cannot help to solve the controllability for integro-differential equations. Indeed, we will need to prove the null controllability of the singular heat equation (
2.1
), for more regular solutions. For this reason, to formulate our results we introduce the following function space where the controllability will be solved:

X_{s}:=\big\{y\in\mathcal{Z}:e^{-s\tilde{\Phi}}y\in L^{2}(Q)\big\}

equipped with the norm

\displaystyle\|y\|_{X_{s}}:=\|e^{-s\tilde{\Phi}}y\|_{L^{2}(Q)}.

Observe that, since
\tilde{\Phi}<0
, we have that the function
e^{-s\tilde{\Phi}}
tends to
+\infty
for
t\rightarrow T^{-}
.
Therefore,
y\in X_{s}
requires that the solution
y
has more regularity than the one in Lemma
2.1
. Moreover,

\text{ if }y\in X_{s}\;\text{ then }\;y(T,x)=0\text{ in }(0,1).

(2.47)

From now on, we denote by
s_{0}
the parameter defined in Lemma
2.3
. Our first result, stated as follows, ensures the null controllability for (
2.1
).

Theorem 2.2
.

Assume that
\mu\leq\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}
and
y_{0}\in H_{0}^{1,\mu}(0,1)
. If

e^{-s\tilde{\varphi}}f\in L^{2}(Q)
with
s\geq s_{0}
, then there exists a control function
u\in L^{2}(Q)
, such that the associated solution
y

of (
2.1
) belongs to
X_{s}
.

Moreover, there exists a positive constant
C
such that
y
satisfies the following
estimate:

\displaystyle\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}y^{2}e^{-2s\tilde{\Phi}}\,dx\,dt+\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q_{\omega}}s^{-3}\nu^{-3}u^{2}e^{-2s\tilde{\Phi}}\,dx\,dt

(2.48)

\displaystyle\leq Ce^{2s[\hat{\varphi}(0)-\check{\varphi}(\frac{5T}{8})]}\Big(\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}f^{2}e^{-2s\tilde{\varphi}}\,dx\,dt+\|y_{0}e^{-s\hat{\varphi}(0)}\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}^{2}\Big).

Proof.

Following the ideas in
[
9
,
38
]
, fixed
s\geq s_{0}
, let us consider the functional

J(y,u)=\Big(\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}y^{2}e^{-2s\tilde{\Phi}}\,dx\,dt+\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q_{\omega}}s^{-3}\nu^{-3}u^{2}e^{-2s\tilde{\Phi}}\,dx\,dt\Big),

(2.49)

where
(y,u)
satisfies

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}y_{t}-y_{xx}-\displaystyle\frac{\mu}{x^{2}}y=f+1_{\omega}u(t),&(t,x)\in Q,\\
y(t,0)=y(t,1)=0,&t\in(0,T),\\
y(0,x)=y_{0}(x),\quad y(T,x)=0&x\in(0,1),\end{array}\right.

(2.50)

with
u\in L^{2}(Q)
.

By means of standard arguments, it is easy to prove (see
[
31
,
32
]
) that
J
attains its minimizer at a unique point denoted as
(\bar{y},\bar{u})
.

We set

L_{\mu}y:=y_{t}-y_{xx}-\frac{\mu}{x^{2}}y\qquad\text{in}\quad Q.

We will first prove that there exists a dual variable
\bar{z}
such that

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\bar{y}=e^{2s\tilde{\Phi}}L_{\mu}^{\star}\bar{z},&\quad\text{in}\quad Q,\\
\bar{u}=-s^{3}\nu^{3}e^{2s\tilde{\Phi}}\bar{z},&\quad\text{in}\quad(0,T)\times\omega,\\
\bar{z}=0,&\quad\text{on}\quad(0,T)\times\{0,1\},\end{array}\right.

(2.51)

where
L_{\mu}^{\star}
is the (formally) adjoint operator of
L_{\mu}
.

Let us start by introducing the following linear space

\mathcal{P}_{0}=\big\{z\in C^{\infty}(\overline{Q}):z=0\quad\text{on}\quad(0,T)\times\{0,1\}\big\},

and introduce the bilinear form
a
:

a(z_{1},z_{2})=\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}e^{2s\tilde{\Phi}}L_{\mu}^{\star}z_{1}L_{\mu}^{\star}z_{2}\,dx\,dt+\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q_{\omega}}s^{3}\nu^{3}e^{2s\tilde{\Phi}}z_{1}z_{2}\,dx\,dt,\quad\forall\;z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathcal{P}_{0}.

Then, if the functions
\bar{y}
and
\bar{u}
given by (
2.51
) satisfy the parabolic problem (
2.50
), we
must have

a(\bar{z},z)=\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}fz\,dx\,dt+\int_{0}^{1}y_{0}z(0)\,dx,\quad\forall\;z\in\mathcal{P}_{0}.

(2.52)

The key idea in this proof is to show that there exists exactly one
\bar{z}

satisfying (
2.52
) in an appropriate class. We will then define
\bar{y}
and
\bar{u}
using (
2.51
)
and we will check that the couple
(\bar{y},\bar{u})
fulfills the desired properties.

Observe that the modified Carleman inequality (
2.3
) holds for all
z\in\mathcal{P}_{0}
. Consequently,

\|e^{s\hat{\varphi}(0)}z(0)\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}^{2}+\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}\nu z^{2}e^{2s\tilde{\varphi}}\,dx\,dt\leq Ce^{2s[\hat{\varphi}(0)-\check{\varphi}(\frac{5T}{8})]}a(z,z).

(2.53)

In particular,
a(\cdot,\cdot)
is a strictly positive and symmetric bilinear form, that is,
a(\cdot,\cdot)
is a scalar product in
\mathcal{P}_{0}
.

Denote by
\mathcal{P}
the Hilbert space which is the completion of
\mathcal{P}_{0}
with respect to the norm associated
to
a(\cdot,\cdot)
(which we denote by
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{P}}
). Let us now consider the linear form
l
, given by

l(z)=\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}fz\,dx\,dt+\int_{0}^{1}y_{0}z(0)\,dx,\quad\forall\;z\in\mathcal{P}.

By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and in view of (
2.53
), we have that

\displaystyle|l(z)|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|f\frac{e^{-s\tilde{\varphi}}}{\sqrt{\nu}}\right\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\|z\sqrt{\nu}e^{s\tilde{\varphi}}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}+\|y_{0}e^{-s\hat{\varphi}(0)}\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}\|z(0)e^{s\hat{\varphi}(0)}\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}

\displaystyle\leq Ce^{s[\hat{\varphi}(0)-\check{\varphi}(\frac{5T}{8})]}\Big(\|fe^{-s\tilde{\varphi}}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}+\|y_{0}e^{-s\hat{\varphi}(0)}\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}\Big)\|z\|_{\mathcal{P}},

and then
l
is a linear continuous form on
\mathcal{P}
. Hence, in view of Lax-Milgram’s Lemma, there
exists one and only one
\bar{z}\in\mathcal{P}
satisfying

a(\bar{z},z)=l(z),\quad\forall\;z\in\mathcal{P}.

(2.54)

Moreover, we have

\|\bar{z}\|_{\mathcal{P}}\leq Ce^{s[\hat{\varphi}(0)-\check{\varphi}(\frac{5T}{8})]}\Big(\|fe^{-s\tilde{\varphi}}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}+\|y_{0}e^{-s\hat{\varphi}(0)}\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}\Big).

(2.55)

Let us set

\bar{y}=e^{2s\tilde{\Phi}}L_{\mu}^{\star}\bar{z}\quad\text{and}\quad\bar{u}=-1_{\omega}s^{3}\nu^{3}e^{2s\tilde{\Phi}}\bar{z}.

(2.56)

With these definitions and by (
2.55
), it is easy to check that
\bar{y}
and
\bar{u}
satisfy

\displaystyle\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}\bar{y}^{2}e^{-2s\tilde{\Phi}}\,dx\,dt+\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q_{\omega}}s^{-3}\nu^{-3}\bar{u}^{2}e^{-2s\tilde{\Phi}}\,dx\,dt

(2.57)

\displaystyle\leq Ce^{2s[\hat{\varphi}(0)-\check{\varphi}(\frac{5T}{8})]}\Big(\|fe^{-s\tilde{\varphi}}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}^{2}+\|y_{0}e^{-s\hat{\varphi}(0)}\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}^{2}\Big),

which implies (
2.48
).

It remains to check that
\bar{y}
is the solution of (
2.50
) corresponding to
\bar{u}
. First of all, it is immediate that
\bar{y}\in X_{s}
and

\bar{u}\in L^{2}(Q)
. Denote by
\tilde{y}
the (weak) solution of (
2.1
)
associated to the control function
u=\bar{u}
, then
\tilde{y}
is also the unique solution of (
2.1
) defined by transposition. In other words,
\tilde{y}
is the unique function in
L^{2}(Q)
satisfying

\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}\tilde{y}h\,dx\,dt=\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}1_{\omega}\bar{u}z\,dx\,dt+\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}fz\,dx\,dt+\int_{0}^{1}y_{0}z(0)\,dx,\quad\forall\;h\in L^{2}(Q),

(2.58)

where
z
is the solution to

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-z_{t}-z_{xx}-\frac{\mu}{x^{2}}z=h,&(t,x)\in Q,\\
z(t,0)=z(t,1)=0,&t\in(0,T),\\
z(T,x)=0&x\in(0,1).\end{array}\right.

According to (
2.54
) and (
2.56
), we see that
\bar{y}
also satisfies (
2.58
). Therefore,
\bar{y}=\tilde{y}
. Consequently, the control
\bar{u}\in L^{2}(\omega\times(0,T))
drives the state
\bar{y}\in X_{s}
exactly to zero at time
T
.

∎

3
Singular heat equation with memory

Prior to null controllability is the well-posedness of problem (
1.1
).
From the results in
[
22
]
, we recall that in the nonsingular case
(\mu=0)
, it is well known that the heat operator with memory
gives rise to well-posed Cauchy-Dirichlet problems. Likewise in
[
22
]
, by an application of the Contraction Mapping Principle and invoking Theorem
2.1
, we have that (
1.1
) is well-posed in the following sense:

Proposition 3.1
.

Assume that
\mu\leq\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}
. If
y_{0}\in L^{2}(0,1)
and
u\in L^{2}(Q)
, then there exists a unique solution
y
of
(
1.1
) such that

y\in C\big([0,T];L^{2}(0,1)\big)\cap L^{2}\big(0,T;H_{0}^{1,\mu}(0,1)\big).

Now, we pass to derive our main result, which concerns the null controllability of the singular heat equation with memory (
1.1
).
Hence, in what follows, we assume that the function
a
satisfies

e^{\frac{4^{k}s\mathfrak{c}d}{T^{k}(T-t)^{k}}}a\in L^{\infty}((0,T)\times Q),

(3.1)

where
\mathfrak{c},d,k
are the constants defined in (
2.14
) and
s
is the same of Theorem
2.2
.

Remark 1
.

It is worth mentioning that, from the results in Guerrero and Imanuvilov
[
23
]
, it seems that the null controllability property
of parabolic equations with memory may fail without any additional conditions on the kernel. On the other hand, observe that the condition (
3.1
) just restricts the function
a
very near
T
, which is due to the fact that the function
\nu
blows up only at
t=T
.

For our proof, we are going to employ a fixed point strategy.
For
R>0
, we define

X_{s,R}=\big\{w\in X_{s}:\,\|e^{-s\tilde{\Phi}}w\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\leq R\big\},

which is a bounded, closed, and convex subset of
L^{2}(Q)
.

For any
w\in X_{s,R}
, let us consider the control problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}y_{t}-y_{xx}-\displaystyle\frac{\mu}{x^{2}}y=\int_{0}^{t}a(t,s,x)w(s,x)\,ds+1_{\omega}u,&(t,x)\in Q,\\
y(t,0)=y(t,1)=0,&t\in(0,T),\\
y(0,x)=y_{0}(x),&x\in(0,1).\end{array}\right.

(3.2)

By Theorem
2.2
we first derive a null controllability result for (
3.2
); then, as a second step, we will obtain the same controllability result for (
1.1
) applying Kakutani’s fixed point Theorem.

Our main result is thus the following.

Theorem 3.1
.

Assume that
\mu\leq\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}
. If the function
a
satisfies (
3.1
), then for any
y_{0}\in H_{0}^{1,\mu}(0,1)
, there exists a control function
u\in L^{2}(Q)
such that the associated solution
y\in\mathcal{Z}
of (
1.1
) satisfies

y(T,\cdot)=0\qquad\text{in}\quad(0,1).

(3.3)

Proof.

Setting
C_{0}:=\frac{4^{k}\mathfrak{c}d}{T^{k}}
, by (
3.1
) and the estimate
e^{-s\tilde{\varphi}}\leq e^{\frac{sC_{0}}{(T-t)^{k}}}
, we get that

\displaystyle\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}\Big(e^{-s\tilde{\varphi}}\int_{0}^{t}a(t,s,x)w(s,x)\,ds\Big)^{2}\,dx\,dt\leq C\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\frac{2C_{0}s}{(T-t)^{k}}}a^{2}(t,s,x)w^{2}(s,x)\,ds\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle\leq C\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}w^{2}\,dx\,dt\leq C\Big(\displaystyle\sup_{(t,x)\in\overline{Q}}e^{2s\tilde{\Phi}}\Big)\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}e^{-2s\tilde{\Phi}}w^{2}\,dx\,dt\leq CR^{2}<+\infty.

(recall that
w\in X_{s,R}
).
Thus, the result in Theorem
2.2
holds for
the equation (
3.2
), i.e. for any
y_{0}\in H_{0}^{1,\mu}(0,1)
, there exists a control function
u\in L^{2}(Q)
such that the associated solution
y
of (
3.2
) is in
X_{s}
and

y(T,\cdot)=0\qquad\text{in}\quad(0,1).

Let us now introduce, for every
w\in X_{s,R}
, the multivalued map

\Lambda:X_{s,R}\subset X_{s}\rightarrow 2^{X_{s}}

with

\displaystyle\Lambda(w)=\displaystyle\Big\{

\displaystyle y\in X_{s}:\text{ for some}\;u\in L^{2}(Q)\;\text{satisfying}

\displaystyle\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q_{\omega}}s^{-3}\nu^{-3}u^{2}e^{-2s\tilde{\Phi}}\,dx\,dt\leq Ce^{2s[\hat{\varphi}(0)-\check{\varphi}(\frac{5T}{8})]}\Big(R^{2}+\int_{0}^{1}y_{0}^{2}e^{-2s\hat{\varphi}(0)}\,dx\,dt\Big)

\displaystyle y\text{ solves }\eqref{problem20}\Big\}.

Observe that if
y\in\Lambda(w)
, then
y(T,\cdot)=0
in
(0,1)
via (
2.47
).

To achieve our goal, it will suffice to show that
\Lambda
possesses at least one fixed point.
To this purpose, we shall apply Kakutani’s fixed point Theorem (see
[
9
, Theorem 2.3]
).

It is readily seen that
\Lambda(w)
is a nonempty, closed and convex subset of
L^{2}(Q)

for every
w\in X_{s,R}
. Then, we prove that
\Lambda(X_{s,R})\subset X_{s,R}
with sufficiently large
R>0
.
By (
2.48
) and condition (
3.1
), and arguing as before we have

\displaystyle\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}y^{2}e^{-2s\tilde{\Phi}}\,dx\,dt+\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q_{\omega}}s^{-3}\nu^{-3}u^{2}e^{-2s\tilde{\Phi}}\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle\leq Ce^{2s[\hat{\varphi}(0)-\check{\varphi}(\frac{5T}{8})]}\Big(\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}e^{-2s\tilde{\varphi}}\Big(\int_{0}^{t}a(t,s,x)w(s,x)\,ds\Big)^{2}\,dx\,dt+e^{-2s\hat{\varphi}(0)}\int_{0}^{1}y_{0}^{2}\,dx\Big)

\displaystyle\leq Ce^{2s[\hat{\varphi}(0)-\check{\varphi}(\frac{5T}{8})]}\Big(\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}w^{2}(t,x)\,dx\,dt+e^{-2s\hat{\varphi}(0)}\int_{0}^{1}y_{0}^{2}\,dx\Big)

\displaystyle\leq Ce^{2s[\hat{\varphi}(0)-\check{\varphi}(\frac{5T}{8})]}\Big(\displaystyle\sup_{(t,x)\in\overline{Q}}e^{2s\tilde{\Phi}}\Big)\Big(\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}e^{-2s\tilde{\Phi}(t,x)}w^{2}(t,x)\,dx\,dt\Big)+Ce^{-2s\check{\varphi}(\frac{5T}{8})}\int_{0}^{1}y_{0}^{2}\,dx.

By virtue of
\hat{\varphi}(0)\leq\hat{\Phi}(0)
and
\tilde{\Phi}\leq\hat{\Phi}(0)
in
Q
, we get

\displaystyle\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}y^{2}e^{-2s\tilde{\Phi}}\,dx\,dt+\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q_{\omega}}s^{-3}\nu^{-3}u^{2}e^{-2s\tilde{\Phi}}\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle\leq Ce^{s[2\hat{\varphi}(0)-2\check{\varphi}(\frac{5T}{8})+2\hat{\Phi}(0)]}\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}e^{-2s\tilde{\Phi}(t,x)}w^{2}(t,x)\,dx\,dt+Ce^{-2s\check{\varphi}(\frac{5T}{8})}\int_{0}^{1}y_{0}^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle\leq Ce^{s[4\hat{\Phi}(0)-2\check{\varphi}(\frac{5T}{8})]}R^{2}+Ce^{-2s\check{\varphi}(\frac{5T}{8})}\int_{0}^{1}y_{0}^{2}\,dx.

(3.4)

Now, choosing the constant
\mathfrak{c}
(see (
2.14
)) in the interval

\left(\frac{e^{2\rho\|\sigma\|_{\infty}}-1}{d-1},\frac{16}{15}\frac{e^{2\rho\|\sigma\|_{\infty}}-e^{\rho\|\sigma\|_{\infty}}}{d-1}\right),

which is not empty for
\rho
sufficiently large, we have

\displaystyle 2\hat{\Phi}(0)-\check{\varphi}\left(\frac{5T}{8}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{4}{T^{2}}\right)^{k}\Big[2(e^{\rho\|\sigma\|_{\infty}}-e^{2\rho\|\sigma\|_{\infty}})+\mathfrak{c}d\left(\frac{16}{15}\right)^{k}\Big]

\displaystyle<\left(\frac{4}{T^{2}}\right)^{k}\left(-2+\frac{d}{d-1}\left(\frac{16}{15}\right)^{k+1}\right)(e^{2\rho\|\sigma\|_{\infty}}-e^{\rho\|\sigma\|_{\infty}}).

Therefore, taking the parameters
d
and
k
defined in (
2.14
) in such a way that
d>3
and
2<k<\frac{\ln(4/3)}{\ln(16/15)}-1
, we infer that

2\hat{\Phi}(0)-\check{\varphi}(\frac{5T}{8})<0.

Hence for
s
sufficiently large, increasing the parameter
s_{0}
if necessary, we obtain

\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}y^{2}e^{-2s\tilde{\Phi}}\,dx\,dt+\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q_{\omega}}s^{-3}\nu^{-3}u^{2}e^{-2s\tilde{\Phi}}\,dx\,dt\leq\frac{1}{2}R^{2}+Ce^{-2s\check{\varphi}(\frac{5T}{8})}\int_{0}^{1}y_{0}^{2}\,dx.

Then, for
s
and
R
large enough, we obtain

\int\!\!\!\!\!\int_{Q}y^{2}e^{-2s\tilde{\Phi}}\,dx\,dt\leq R^{2}.

It follows that
\Lambda(X_{s,R})\subset X_{s,R}
.
Furthermore, let
\{w_{n}\}
be a sequence of
X_{s,R}
. The regularity assumption on
y_{0}

and Theorem
2.1
, imply that the associated solutions
\{y_{n}\}
are bounded in
H^{1}\big(0,T;L^{2}(0,1)\big)\cap L^{2}\big(0,T;D(A)\big)
.
Therefore,
\Lambda(X_{s,R})
is a relatively compact subset of
L^{2}(Q)
by
the Aubin-Lions Theorem
[
37
]
.

In order to conclude, we have to prove that
\Lambda
is upper-semicontinuous under the
L^{2}
topology.
First, observe that for any
w\in X_{s,R}
, we have at least
u\in L^{2}(Q)
such that the corresponding solution
y\in X_{s,R}
.
Hence, taking
\{w_{n}\}
a sequence in
X_{s,R}
, we can find a sequence of controls
\{u_{n}\}
such that the corresponding solutions

\{y_{n}\}
is in
L^{2}(Q)
. Thus, let
\{w_{n}\}
be a sequence satisfying
w_{n}\rightarrow w
in
X_{s,R}
and

y_{n}\in\Lambda(w_{n})
such that
y_{n}\rightarrow y
in
L^{2}(Q)
. We must prove that

y\in\Lambda(w)
.
For every
n
, we have a control
u_{n}\in L^{2}(Q)
such that the system

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle y_{n,t}-y_{n,xx}-\frac{\mu}{x^{2}}y_{n}=\int_{0}^{t}a(t,s,x)w_{n}(s,x)\,ds+1_{\omega}u_{n},&&(t,x)\in Q,\\
y_{n}(t,0)=y_{n}(t,1)=0,&&t\in(0,T),\\
y_{n}(0,x)=y_{0}(x),&&x\in(0,1)\end{array}\right.

(3.5)

has a least one solution
y_{n}\in L^{2}(Q)
that satisfies

y_{n}(T,\cdot)=0\qquad\text{in}\quad(0,1).

From Theorem
2.1
and (
3
), it follows (at least for a subsequence) that

\displaystyle u_{n}\rightarrow u\quad

\displaystyle\text{weakly in}\;L^{2}(Q),

\displaystyle y_{n}\rightarrow y\quad

\displaystyle\text{weakly in}\;H^{1}\big(0,T;L^{2}(0,1)\big)\cap L^{2}\big(0,T;D(A)\big),

\displaystyle\text{strongly in}\;C(0,T;L^{2}(0,1)).

Passing to the limit in (
3.5
), we obtain a control
u\in L^{2}(Q)
such that the corresponding solution
y
to (
3.2
)
satisfies (
3.3
). This shows that
y\in\Lambda(w)
and, therefore, the map
\Lambda
is upper-semicontinuous.

Hence, the multivalued map
\Lambda
possesses at least one fixed point, i.e., there exists
y\in X_{s,R}
such that
y\in\Lambda(y)
.
By the definition of
\Lambda
, this implies that there exists at least one pair
(y,u)
satisfying the conditions of Theorem
3.1
.
The uniqueness of
y
follows by Proposition
3.1
.
This ends the proof of Theorem
3.1
.

∎

As a consequence of the previous theorem one has the next result.

Theorem 3.2
.

Assume that
\mu\leq\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}
. If the function
a
satisfies (
3.1
), then for any
y_{0}\in L^{2}(0,1)
, there exists a control function
u\in L^{2}(Q)
such that the associated solution
y\in\mathcal{W}
of (
1.1
) satisfies

y(T,\cdot)=0\qquad\text{in}\quad(0,1).

Proof.

Consider the following singular parabolic problem:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}w_{t}-w_{xx}-\displaystyle\frac{\mu}{x^{2}}w=\int_{0}^{t}a(t,s,x)w(s,x)\,ds&&(t,x)\in\left(0,\displaystyle\frac{T}{2}\right)\times(0,1),\\
w(t,0)=w(t,1)=0,&&t\in\left(0,\displaystyle\frac{T}{2}\right),\\
w(0,x)=y_{0}(x),&&x\in(0,1),\end{array}\right.

where
y_{0}\in L^{2}(0,1)
is the initial condition in (
1.1
).

By Theorem
2.1
, the solution of this system belongs to

\mathcal{W}\left(0,\frac{T}{2}\right):=L^{2}\left(0,\frac{T}{2};H_{0}^{1,\mu}(0,1)\right)\cap C\left(\left[0,\frac{T}{2}\right];L^{2}(0,1)\right).

Then, there exists
t_{0}\in(0,\frac{T}{2})
such that

w(t_{0},\cdot):=\tilde{w}(\cdot)\in H_{0}^{1,\mu}(0,1)
.

Now, we consider the following controlled parabolic system:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}z_{t}-z_{xx}-\displaystyle\frac{\mu}{x^{2}}z=\int_{0}^{t}a(t,s,x)z(s,x)\,ds+1_{\omega}h&&(t,x)\in(t_{0},T)\times(0,1),\\
z(t,0)=z(t,1)=0,&&t\in(t_{0},T),\\
z(t_{0},x)=\tilde{w}(x),&&x\in(0,1).\end{array}\right.

We start by observing that, since Theorem
3.1
holds also in a general domain
(t_{0},T)\times(0,1)
with suitable changes,
we can see that there exists a control function
h\in L^{2}((t_{0},T)\times(0,1))
such that the associated solution

z\in\mathcal{Z}(t_{0},T):=L^{2}(t_{0},T;D(A))\cap H^{1}(t_{0},T;L^{2}(0,1))\cap C\left(\left[t_{0},T\right];H_{0}^{1,\mu}(0,1)\right)

satisfies

z(T,\cdot)=0\qquad\text{in}\;(0,1).

Finally, setting

\displaystyle y:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}w,&\text{in}\quad\big[0,t_{0}\big],\\
z,&\text{in}\quad\big[t_{0},T\big]\end{array}\right.\quad\text{and}\quad u:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}0,&\text{in}\quad\big[0,t_{0}\big],\\
h,&\text{in}\quad\big[t_{0},T\big],\end{array}\right.

one can prove that
y\in\mathcal{W}
is the solution to the system (
1.1
) corresponding to
u
and satisfies

y(T,\cdot)=0\qquad\text{in}\;(0,1).

Hence, our assertion is proved.
∎
