A constructive proof of the convergence of Kalantari’s bound on polynomial zeros
Matt Hohertz

Abstract
In his 2006 paper, Jin proves that Kalantari’s bounds on polynomial zeros, indexed by m ≥ 2 m\geq 2 and called L m L_{m} and U m U_{m} respectively, become sharp as m → ∞ m\rightarrow\infty . That is, given a degree n n polynomial p ⁡ ( z ) p(z) not vanishing at the origin and an error tolerance ϵ > 0 \epsilon>0 , Jin proves that there exists an m m such that L m ρ m ​ i ​ n ≥ 1 − ϵ , \frac{L_{m}}{\rho_{min}}\geq 1-\epsilon, where ρ m ​ i ​ n := min ρ : p ⁡ ( ρ ) = 0 | ρ | \rho_{min}:=\displaystyle\min_{\rho:p(\rho)=0}\left|\rho\right| . In this paper we derive a formula that yields such an m m , thereby constructively proving Jin’s theorem. In fact, we prove the stronger theorem that this convergence is uniform in a sense, its rate depending only on n n and a few other parameters. We also give experimental results that suggest an optimal m m of (asymptotically) O ⁡ ( 1 ϵ d ) O\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon^{d}}\right) for some d ≪ 2 d\ll 2 . A proof of these results would show that Jin’s method runs in O ⁡ ( n ϵ d ) O\left(\frac{n}{\epsilon^{d}}\right) time, making it efficient for isolating high-degree polynomial zeros.

3
Discussion

We anticipate three primary objections to our method:

1.

Equation (
48
) does not yield an obvious asymptotic bound for
m_{\epsilon}
.
Indeed, we have not yet succeeded in rigorously proving such a bound. However, the experimental results we detail in Section
4
suggest that
m_{\epsilon}=O\left(\frac{C}{\epsilon^{d}}\right)
for some constant
C\leq 1.1
and
d\in\left(1.28,1.42\right)
. Note that this estimate does not depend on
n
.

2.

The function
g(m_{\epsilon};n,\alpha,\beta)
approximates
\frac{L_{m_{\epsilon}}}{\rho_{min}}
yet depends on
\beta
, which is itself a function of
\rho_{min}
.
This apparent circularity can be overcome by replacing
\rho_{min}
in the formula for
\beta
with the modulus of a known root, or a known upper bound of
\rho_{min}
. In particular, this allows the estimate
\beta=n
if at least one root of
p(z)
is known to lie in
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
.

Consider, for example,
p(z)=3z^{7}-z^{2}+2
. By Viete’s formulas,
p(z)
has at least one zero in
\mathbb{D}
; thus we may estimate
\beta
with
n=7
to obtain the equation

\left[\frac{\Gamma\left(7\right)}{\frac{3}{2}\cdot 7\cdot\left(m_{\epsilon}+1+\frac{7}{2}\right)^{7}}\right]^{\frac{1}{m_{\epsilon}+1}}=1-\epsilon

(49)

for
m_{\epsilon}
. For
\epsilon=0.05
, the solution is
m_{\epsilon}=828
; indeed,
L_{828}\approx 0.876282
and
\rho_{min}\approx 0.88
, yielding

\frac{L_{828}}{\rho_{min}}\approx 0.995817\gg 0.95.

(50)

3.

The function
g(m_{\epsilon};n,\alpha,\beta)
tends to overestimate the minimum
m
necessary for error tolerance
\epsilon
.
Returning to the previous example, for
p(z)=3z^{7}-z^{2}+2
only
m=20
calculations, and not
m_{\epsilon}=828
, are required to approximate

to
95\%
accuracy. In the next section we suggest a formula for an

(generally less than
m_{\epsilon}
) that tends to suffice in practice.

6
Conclusion

All of our experiments suggest the independence of the value
m
in Jin’s method from the polynomial degree
n
: this striking conjecture would have powerful implications if true and warrants further study. In particular, we encourage research into proof or disproof of Conjecture
I

m
required for error tolerance
\epsilon
(or a value for the exponent

holds). Finally, acknowledging the theoretical significance of our Equation (

, for which we would like to find a closed formula.
