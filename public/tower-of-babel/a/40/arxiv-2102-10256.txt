I
Introduction

Group testing
[
1
]
is the non-linear sparse recovery process of identifying a small subset of defective items from a larger set of items based on a series of judiciously designed tests. Each test is carried out on a subset of items, and each binary outcome indicates whether or not the test includes at least one defective item. In other words, the test outcome is specified by the ‘‘OR’’ function. In designing the testing scheme, it is desirable to minimize the number of tests while still enabling high probability of correct identification of the subset of defective items.
1
1

1

A significant part of the group-testing literature focuses on zero-error recovery – see for instance the survey in
[
2
]
. However, even in the context of classical group-testing more stringent recovery criterion comes at the cost of requiring a number of tests than high-probability recovery requires. Further, in the context of this work, where test outcomes are probabilistic in nature, zero-error recovery is impossible, hence we focus on high-probability recovery.

The group testing paradigm has found applications in a wide variety of contexts, including biology
[
3
]
, pattern finding
[
4
]
, wireless communications
[
5
,
6
]
, and testing for diseases recently COVID-19 testing
[
7
]
.

Many variants of the classical group testing paradigm have already been considered in the literature. For example, Damaschke
[
8
]
considered
threshold test functions
: the test outcome is negative if the number of defectives in a test is no larger than the lower threshold
\ell
; positive if no smaller than the upper threshold
u
; and arbitrary (negative or positive) otherwise. Let
n
and
d
be the number of all items and the number of defective items, respectively. For

,
[
8
]
proposed an adaptive algorithm with the number of tests scaling as
{\cal O}\left((d+u^{2})\log n\right)
to exactly identify the defectives. However, for
u>\ell+1
, they proved that the defectives cannot be exactly identified, but
{\cal O}\left((dn^{b}+d^{u})\log n\right)
adaptive tests suffice to identify the defectives if up to
(u-1)(1+\frac{1}{b})-\ell
misidentifications are allowed (here
b>0
is an arbitrary constant). Chen and Fu
[
9

proposed a non-adaptive algorithm for which the number of tests scales as
{\cal O}\left(\sigma d^{u+1}\log(\frac{n}{d})\right)
if up to
u-\ell-1

For Bernoulli gap stochasticity, the test outcome is equally likely to be negative or positive whenever the number of defectives in a test is in the interval

I-A

Our contributions

In this paper, motivated by physical considerations such as the effect of dilution on the chemistry of group tests, we formulate and analyze group testing with a general monotonically increasing stochastic test function
f(\cdot)
(i.e.,
x\geq y\Rightarrow f(x)\geq f(y)
) that takes as input the number of defective items in a test and outputs the probability of the given test having a positive outcome. This formulation subsumes as special cases a variety of noiseless and noisy group-testing models in the literature. Our contributions are as follows.

•

For any monotone test function
f(\cdot)
we present a non-adaptive generalized group-testing scheme that identifies all defective items with probability at least
1-\varepsilon
. Our scheme requires at most
{\cal O}\left({H(f)}d\log\left(\frac{n}{\varepsilon}\right)\right)
tests, where
{H(f)}
is a suitably defined “sensitivity parameter” of
f(\cdot)
, and is never larger than
{\cal O}(d^{1+o(1)})
, but indeed can be substantially smaller for a variety of
f(\cdot)
. The computational complexity of decoding is
{\cal O}\left(n{H(f)}d\log\left(\frac{n}{\varepsilon}\right)\right)
.

•

We argue that any non-adaptive group testing scheme that has a probability of error of at most
\varepsilon
requires at least
\Omega\left((1-\varepsilon){h(f)}d\log\left(\frac{n}{d}\right)\right)
tests, where
{h(f)}
is a “concentration parameter” of
f(\cdot)
, and
{h(f)}\in\Omega{(1)}
.

•

We prove that our sample-complexity bounds for generalized group testing are information-theoretically near-optimal for a variety of sparse-recovery group-testing models in the literature. That is,

–

For
any
“noisy” test function
f(\cdot)

(i.e.,
0<f(0)<f(d)<1
) we show that

\frac{{H(f)}}{{h(f)}}\in\Theta(1)
. This implies that for noisy test functions our non-adaptive scheme has order-wise optimal sample complexity.

–

For a variety of “noiseless” test functions
f(\cdot)
(i.e.
f(0)=0
,
f(d)=1
) studied in the literature we also show that
\frac{{H(f)}}{{h(f)}}\in\Theta(1)
. Hence for these test-functions as well the sample complexity of our non-adaptive scheme is order-wise optimal.

–

As a by-product we tightly characterize the heretofore open information-theoretic order-wise sample-complexity for the well-studied model of threshold group-testing.

–

Perhaps surprisingly, for technical reason our results are somewhat weaker for general “(one-sided) near-noiseless” test functions
f(\cdot)
(i.e., either
f(0)\xrightarrow{d\rightarrow\infty}0
, or
f(d)\xrightarrow{d\rightarrow\infty}1
, or both) than for general noisy test functions. For general near-noiseless test functions
f(\cdot)
we show that
\frac{{H(f)}}{{h(f)}}\in{\cal O}(d^{1+o(1)})
.

–

We also demonstrate a “natural” test-function

whose sample complexity scales “extremally” as
\Theta\left(d^{2}\log n\right)
– our results above exclude the possibility of any monotone test function having optimal sample-complexity higher than this. Note that the optimal sample-complexity scales as

in the case of classical group-testing.

•

Some of our techniques may be of independent interest – in particular:

–

Our achievability requires a delicate saddle-point approximation,

–

Our impossibility proof relies on a novel bound relating the mutual information of pair of random variables with the mean and variance of a specific function, and

–

As a by-product of our proof showing that our sample-complexity upper and lower bounds are close we derive novel structural results about monotone functions.

II
Problem Formulation

A set
\mathcal{N}:=\{1,\dots,n\}
of
n
items contains a subset
\mathcal{D}\subsetneq\mathcal{N}
of
defective
items – elements in
\mathcal{N}\setminus\mathcal{D}
are called
non-defective
. We follow the “combinatorial group testing model”, which assumes that the size of defective set
{\cal D}
is fixed as
d
, and each such subset has the same probability. The identity of
{\cal D}
is unknown
a priori
– the goal of group testing is to correctly identify
{\cal D}
through a minimal series of
group
tests on subsets of items. In “classical” group testing a test outcome is negative if every item in the pool is non-defective, and is positive if at least one item is defective. As such this may be viewed as a
disjunctive
measurement, i.e., viewing each item as a
0
or a
1
depending on whether it is non-defective or defective, each test performs an OR of the items in its pool. A canonical setting in which this measurement model is pertinent is when a small number of individuals in a large population are diseased but only a small number of testing kits are available; in this case samples from different individuals may be “pooled” together in different combinations and the set of test outcomes analyzed jointly to infer
{\cal D}
. In this work we assume that the
number

d=|{{\cal D}}|
of defectives is known
a priori
.
2
2

2

Another branch of the group-testing literature (see for instance
[
16
,
17
,
18
]
) concerns itself with the problem of reliably approximating the number
d
itself with a minimal number of adaptive or non-adaptive tests. In classical group-testing, most algorithms are reasonably robust to small perturbations in the value of
d
, and the task of roughly estimating
d
is an “easier” task (requiring asymptotically fewer tests than the task of estimating the set
{\cal D}
). In this generalized group-testing setting, our algorithms and bounds are sensitive to small perturbations in the value of
d
, and require the exact value of
d
. In Appendix
M
, we present an algorithm for exactly estimating
d
. It turns out to be a “harder” task (requiring asymptotically more tests than the task of estimating the set
{\cal D}
).

Instantiating disjunctive tests which are sensitive to even a single defective in a testing pool may be tricky, for instance due to the impact of dilution on the chemistry used in pooled tests
[
19
]
. Our primary contribution in this work is to consider a very general class of (probabilistic) measurement functions
f(\cdot):\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\rightarrow[0,1]
. The input, say
x
, to the measurement function
f(x)
is the number of defective items
x
in a given pool, and the value of
f(x)
is the probability that the given test results in a positive test outcome.

A slight notational subtlety here – since we will be interested in asymptotic results (when
d
and
n
are “large”), in the interest of generality we allow the function
f(\cdot)
to also depend on the value of
d
, the overall number of defectives in the population of size
n
. Hence our notation
f(\cdot)
actually indexes a set
\{f_{d}(\cdot)\}_{d}
of measurement functions. Since we assume the number
d
to be known in advance (see the discussion above, and Footnote
2
) thus the actual function
f_{d}(\cdot)
in the set
f(\cdot)
is well-specified. Thus for notational convenience we suppress the dependence of
f(\cdot)
on
d
in the remainder of this paper. Hence a statement like
f(0)\xrightarrow{d\rightarrow\infty}0
should be interpreted as meaning that
\lim_{d\rightarrow\infty}f_{d}(0)=0
.

In this work we restrict ourselves to the natural class of measurement,
monotone
measurement functions, i.e.,
x\geq y\Rightarrow f(x)\geq f(y)
. Monotone measurement functions subsume many existing models of group-testing as special cases. For instance, when

f(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}0&\;x=0,\\
1&\;x\geq 1,\end{array}\right.

(1)

this reduces to the problem of classical group testing.
Observe that when

f(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}0&\;x\leq\ell,\\
\frac{x-\ell}{u-\ell}&\;\ell<x<u,\\
1&\;x\geq u,\end{array}\right.

(2)

for some integers
0<\ell<u<d
, this reduces to the “linear gap” stochastic group testing examined by
[
14
]
. To avoid triviality, we further assume that
f(0)<f(d)
. (If
f(0)=f(d)
, no sequence of tests can ever reliably recover the defective set
{\cal D}
.)

It will be helpful to distinguish between two types of test functions
f(\cdot)
.

•

If
0<f(0)<f(d)<1
, we say that
f(\cdot)
is
noisy
. For such
f(\cdot)
, even pools with no defective items have a probability
f(0)
(some positive constant independent of
d
) of resulting in a positive test outcome, and pools with one or more defective items have a probability of at least
1-f(d)
(again, a constant bounded away from
0
, independent of
d
) of resulting in a negative test outcome. The corresponding notion of noisy test outcomes in the classical group-testing literature (see for instance
[
20
]
) often focuses on test functions of the form

f(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}a&\;x=0,\\
b&\;x\geq 1,\end{array}\right.

(3)

for some
0<a<b<1
(with the
symmetric noise setting
, i.e.
b=1-a
, receiving the most attention).

•

In contrast,

–

If either
f(0)\xrightarrow{d\rightarrow\infty}0
or
f(d)\xrightarrow{d\rightarrow\infty}1
we say that
f(\cdot)
is
one-sided near-noiseless
, and

–

If both
f(0)\xrightarrow{d\rightarrow\infty}0
and
f(d)\xrightarrow{d\rightarrow\infty}1
hold we say that
f(\cdot)
is
near-noiseless
.

–

Analogously, if either
f(0)=0
or
f(d)=1
we say that
f(\cdot)
is
one-sided noiseless
, and

–

If both
f(0)=0
and
f(d)=1
hold we say that
f(\cdot)
is
noiseless
.

Note that (one-sided) near-noiselessness is a significantly weaker requirement on
f(\cdot)
than in much of the noiseless group-testing literature, where it is often assumed that
f(0)=0
and
f(1)=1
(the corresponding one-sided noiseless version was studied in
[
21
]
).

Group testing schemes can be adaptive (where each test may be designed based on the outcomes of all preceding tests) or non-adaptive (where all tests must be chosen prior to observing any test outcomes). Here, we focus on non-adaptive group testing.
3
3

3

The adaptive version of group-testing has also been extensively studied – see for instance the survey in
[
2
]
. However, since non-adaptive tests allow for test-parallelization, and also make it easier to design hardware to perform the tests (unlike adaptive test designs, where the composition of (at least some) tests may depend on prior test outcomes), we restrict our attention in this work to designing non-adaptive schemes.

Non-adaptive generalized group-testing test designs are specified by a (possibly randomly chosen) binary matrix
\boldsymbol{M}\in\{0,1\}^{T\times n}
, where
M_{ji}=1
if test
j
includes item
i
and
M_{ji}=0
otherwise. The rows of
\boldsymbol{M}
correspond to tests, and the columns correspond to items. The probability of error of any non-adaptive algorithm (with a specified test matrix
\boldsymbol{M}
) is defined as the probability
that the estimated defective set
\hat{\mathcal{D}}
differs from the true
\mathcal{D}
.
This probability is over the elements comprising
{\cal D}
(which is assumed to be distributed uniformly at random from all
d
-sized subsets of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
), over the randomness in test outcomes (since each test with
x
defectives may result in a positive test outcome with probability
f(x)
and a negative test outcome with probability
1-f(x)
), and randomness if any in the decoding rule.
We require that the probability of error is bounded from above by some
\varepsilon
. That is,
\Pr(\hat{\mathcal{D}}\neq\mathcal{D})\leq\varepsilon
– such test designs will be called
(1-\varepsilon)
-reliable.

. However, as we will elaborate in Section
VI-A
that, the first rule requires fewer tests for
(1-\varepsilon)
-reliable recovery when

(1-\varepsilon)
-reliable recovery when

. Hence we use decoding rule
1
if

, and decoding rule
2
otherwise.

Decoding Rule 1
:
The first decoding rule makes use of the tests that each item participates in. More precisely, denote by
{{m}_{i}}
the number of tests that item
i
participates in, and denote by
{{m}_{i}^{+}}
(respectively
{{m}_{i}^{-}}
) the number of tests with positive (respectively negative) outcome within these
{{m}_{i}}
tests. We then classify
i
as follows:

i=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\text{non-defective}&\text{if}\;\frac{{{m}_{i}^{+}}}{{{m}_{i}}}\leq\frac{{P(-,{q})}+{P(+,{q})}}{2},\\
\text{defective}&\text{if}\;\frac{{{m}_{i}^{+}}}{{{m}_{i}}}>\frac{{P(-,{q})}+{P(+,{q})}}{2}.\end{array}\right.

(16)

Decoding Rule 2
:
In contrast, the second decoding rule makes use of the tests that do not include the item. More precisely, let
{{s}_{i}}
denote the number of tests that item

is excluded from, and let
{{s}_{i}^{+}}
(respectively
{{s}_{i}^{-}}
) denote the number of tests with positive (respectively negative) outcome within these

i

i=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\text{non-defective}&\text{if}\;\frac{{{s}_{i}^{+}}}{{{s}_{i}}}>\frac{{Q(-,{q})}+{Q(+,{q})}}{2},\\
\text{defective}&\text{if}\;\frac{{{s}_{i}^{+}}}{{{s}_{i}}}\leq\frac{{Q(-,{q})}+{Q(+,{q})}}{2}.\end{array}\right.

(17)

IV
Main Results

As described in this Section, we have four main results, corresponding respectively to an an upper bound on the number of tests required for
(1-\varepsilon)
-reliable recovery via the test-design/decoder proposed in Section
III
, an information-theoretic lower bound on the number of tests required by
any
non-adaptive test-design/decoder guaranteeing
(1-\varepsilon)
-reliable recovery, a comparison between our algorithmic upper bound and our information-theoretic lower bound, and an algorithm for estimating the exact number of defectives.

IV-A

Achievability/Upper bound

Before stating our achievability, it is useful to define the “sensitivity parameter”
{H(f)}
of a given monotone test function
f(\cdot)
. This sensitivity parameter, in a certain manner, measures the “fastest rate of change” of
f(\cdot)
, maximized over all intervals
[{L},{U}]\subseteq[0,d]
.

Definition 8

Sensitivity parameter: Given a monotone test function
f(\cdot)
, its sensitivity parameter
{H(f)}
is defined as

\displaystyle{H(f)}:=\min_{0\leq{L}<{U}\leq d}\left(\frac{1}{\min\left\{{U}-{L},\sqrt{{L}+1},\sqrt{d-{U}+1}\right\}}\times\frac{{U}-{L}}{f({U})-f({L})}\right)^{2}.

(18)

Here the
\frac{{U}-{L}}{f({U})-f({L})}
term bounds the inverse slope of the test function
f(\cdot)
in the region
[{L},{U}]
, and the

\min\left\{{U}-{L},\sqrt{{L}+1},\sqrt{d-{U}+1}\right\}

term is an amortization factor that, at a high level, relates to the standard deviation of
f(\cdot)
w.r.t. a certain hypergeometric distribution in that interval.

Further, for any monotone test function
f(\cdot)
, Lemma
2
below (whose proof may be found in Appendix
A
) bounds the sensitivity parameter
{H(f)}
of
f(\cdot)
, and asserts
{H(f)}\in{\cal O}\left(d^{1+o(1)}\right)
.

Lemma 2

For any monotone test function
f(\cdot)
and
d\geq 2
, we have
5
5

5

For
d=1
, we directly have
{H(f)}=\frac{1}{\left(f(d)-f(0)\right)^{2}}
by definition.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\left(f(d)-f(0)\right)^{2}}\leq{H(f)}\leq\frac{16}{\left(f(d)-f(0)\right)^{2}}\left(\log\log d+2\right)^{2}d.

(19)

While the bounds in Lemma
2
hold universally for any monotone
f(\cdot)
, the upper bound in (
19
) may be unduly pessimistic.
For instance, for any
w\in[0,1]
, consider the natural class of test functions for which the slope is
\frac{1}{d^{w}}
. Namely:

Example 1

Let the test function
f(\cdot)
be defined as

f(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{x}{d^{w}}&x\in\left[0,d^{w}\right]\cap\mathbb{Z}^{+},\\
1&\text{otherwise},\end{array}\right.

(20)

For such
f(\cdot)
, one can see that
{H(f)}\in{\cal O}\left(d^{w}\right)
, for instance by choosing
{L}=\left\lfloor\frac{d^{w}}{3}\right\rfloor
and
{U}=\left\lceil\frac{2d^{w}}{3}\right\rceil
.

With the definition of
{H(f)}
and the corresponding bounds at hand, we can now state our main achievability result, including the computation of the test design parameter
{{q}^{*}}
in (
15
).

Theorem 1

The non-adaptive test design and decoding outlined in Section
III
has the following performance:

a)

The probability of error is at most
\varepsilon
;

b)

The number of tests
T
satisfies

\displaystyle T\leq 376017{P_{min}({{q}^{*}})}{H(f)}d\log\left(\frac{2n}{\varepsilon}\right)+1\leq 376017{H(f)}d\log\left(\frac{2n}{\varepsilon}\right)+1;

(21)

c)

The test design parameter
{{q}^{*}}
in (
15
) can be efficiently approximated in
{\cal O}(d^{7}\log^{2}(d))
time;

d)

The computation complexity of decoding is
{\cal O}\left(n{H(f)}d\log\left(\frac{n}{\varepsilon}\right)\right)
.

Proofs of each part of Theorem
1
may be found in consecutive sub-sections in Section
VI
.

Remark 1

Our current method of finding an appropriate
{{q}^{*}}
is via a brute-force method, hence the somewhat high computational complexity of
{\cal O}(d^{7}\log^{2}(d))
(though it is the one-time cost of designing our algorithm parameters, rather than the many-time cost of decoding). We are currently exploring methods to choose
{{q}^{*}}
with lower computational complexity.

Remark 2

Due to Lemma
2
, Theorem
1
guarantees that our scheme requires at most
{\cal O}\left(d^{2+o(1)}\log\left(\frac{n}{\varepsilon}\right)\right)
tests for
(1-\varepsilon)
-reliable recovery. However, as noted in Example

{H(f)}
presented in Lemma

may be loose – for the class of test functions in Example

.

IV-B

Converse/Lower bound

To complement our achievability result in Theorem
1
, we also present an information-theoretic lower bound on the number
of tests required by any non-adaptive group testing algorithm that has a probability of error of at most
\varepsilon
. To this end, it is useful to define the “concentration parameter”
{h(f)}
of a given monotone function
f(\cdot)
. This definition parallels (but is distinct from) the definition of
{H(f)}
in the previous Section
IV-A
) – it may be thought of as a measure of concentration of
f(\cdot)
under hypergeometric sampling.

Note that for a pool size of
{\chi}
, the quantities
{\mu}({\chi})
and
{\sigma^{2}}({\chi})
, defined respectively as

\displaystyle{\mu}({\chi})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum\limits_{{a}=0}^{d}\frac{\binom{d}{{a}}\binom{n-d}{{\chi}-{a}}}{\binom{n}{{\chi}}}f({a}),

(22)

\displaystyle{\sigma^{2}}({\chi})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum\limits_{{a}=0}^{d}\frac{\binom{d}{{a}}\binom{n-d}{{\chi}-{a}}}{\binom{n}{{\chi}}}(f({a})-{\mu}({\chi}))^{2},

correspond respectively to (the hypergeometrically weighted) mean and variance of the measurement function
f(\cdot)
, given that the pool-size is
{\chi}
. That is, conditioned on choosing a random pool of size
{\chi}
, these are the mean and variance of the test positivity probability.

Remark 3

We have
f(0)\leq{\mu}({\chi})\leq f(d)
by the monotonicity of
f(\cdot)
.

Definition 9

For test function
f(\cdot)
and
{\chi}\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}
, we define the concentration parameter
{h(f)}
of
f(\cdot)
as

\displaystyle{h(f)}:=\min_{{\chi}\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}}\frac{{\mu}({\chi})\left(1-{\mu}({\chi})\right)}{{\sigma^{2}}({\chi})},

(23)

We are now in a position to state our main converse/impossibility result (whose proof can be found in Section
VII

) as follows:

Theorem 2

For any non-adaptive group testing algorithm that ensures a reconstruction error of at most
\varepsilon
, the number of tests
T
must satisfy

T\geq\frac{1}{\log e}{h(f)}\left((1-\varepsilon)\log\binom{n}{d}-1\right).

(24)

Remark 4

Note also that for any test function
{h(f)}\geq 1
. This is because
f(a)\leq 1
for all
a
, so using the identity
(f({a})-{\mu}({\chi}))^{2}=f^{2}({a})+{\mu}^{2}({\chi})-2f({a}){\mu}({\chi})
, we have that for all
{\chi}
,

\displaystyle{\sigma^{2}}({\chi})=\sum\limits_{{a}=0}^{d}\frac{\binom{d}{{a}}\binom{n-d}{{\chi}-{a}}}{\binom{n}{{\chi}}}f^{2}({a})-{\mu}^{2}({\chi})\leq\sum\limits_{{a}=0}^{d}\frac{\binom{d}{{a}}\binom{n-d}{{\chi}-{a}}}{\binom{n}{{\chi}}}f({a})-{\mu}^{2}({\chi})={\mu}({\chi})\left(1-{\mu}({\chi})\right).

Hence the lower bound in (
24
) scales as
\Omega\left(\log\binom{n}{d}\right)
, which in turns scales as
\Omega\left(d\log\left(\frac{n}{d}\right)\right)
. However, as noted in Section
IV-C
(see Corollary
1
.
c)
below), the bound
{h(f)}\geq 1
is in general loose – there exist test functions for which
{h(f)}
can be as large as
\Omega(d)
. Hence, due to the
{h(f)}
term in (
24
), this impossibility result may scale as
\Omega\left(d^{2}\log\left(\frac{n}{d}\right)\right)
, which is a strict tightening of the information-theoretic lower bounds
\Omega\left(d\log\left(\frac{n}{d}\right)\right)
extant in the literature (see for instance
[
27
]
).

IV-C

Comparison between upper and lower bounds

Comparing the lower bound in (
24
) with the upper bound in (
21
), we note that their ratio scales order-wise as
\frac{{H(f)}}{{h(f)}}
. Corollary
1
below (whose proof may be found in Appendix
K
) demonstrates that for a variety of test functions in the literature
\frac{{H(f)}}{{h(f)}}\in\Theta\left(1\right)
. Thus, our bounds are order-wise tight for those test functions.

Corollary 1

Consider the sparse regime
d=n^{\theta},0\leq\theta<1
.

a)

For the classical group testing measurement function
f(\cdot)
given in (
1
), both the
{H(f)}
and
{h(f)}
functionals equal
1
, enabling us to recover the well-known fact (see for instance
[
27
]
) that the sample-complexity of classical group-testing is
\Theta(d\log n)

b)

For the threshold test function, i.e., for some
\ell\in\{0,\dots,d-1\}
,

f(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\text{if}\;x\leq{\ell},\\
1&\text{if}\;x>{\ell},\end{array}\right.

(25)

both the upper bound on the number of tests required for
(1-\varepsilon)
-reliable recovery in Theorem
1
and the corresponding lower bound in Theorem
2
scale as
\Theta\left(d\log n\right)
. To the best of our knowledge this is the first order-wise tight characterization of the optimal sample-complexity of threshold group testing.

c)

For the “linear” test function, i.e.,

f(x)=\frac{x}{d},\quad x\in\{0,\dots,d\},

(26)

the upper bound on the number of tests required for
(1-\varepsilon)
-reliable recovery in Theorem
1
matches (order-wise) the lower bound in Theorem
2
, both scaling as
\Theta\left(d^{2}\log n\right)
. Hence, by Lemma
2
, this test function is essentially extremal in its sample complexity.

For general (near-noiseless) monotone test functions
f(\cdot)
, while we are not able to show that the sample-complexities in Theorems
1
and
2
match up to constant factors, we nonetheless show in Theorem
3
below (for which a proof may be found in Section
VIII
) that they match up to a
{\cal O}\left(\frac{{P_{min}({{q}^{*}})}}{{\mu}({\chi}^{*})\left(1-{\mu}({\chi}^{*})\right)}\right)
factor.
Here
{\chi}^{*}
denotes an optimal solution to (
23
), i.e.,

\displaystyle{\chi}^{*}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\argmin_{{\chi}\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}}\frac{{\mu}({\chi})(1-{\mu}({\chi}))}{{\sigma^{2}}({\chi})}

(27)

is the pool-size minimizing
\frac{{\mu}({\chi})\left(1-{\mu}({\chi})\right)}{{\sigma^{2}}({\chi})}
.

Theorem 3

In the sparse regime
d=n^{\theta},0\leq\theta<1
, the number of tests required in Theorem
1
is no more than a
{\cal O}\left(\frac{{P_{min}({{q}^{*}})}}{{\mu}({\chi}^{*})\left(1-{\mu}({\chi}^{*})\right)}\right)
factor larger than the lower bound presented in Theorem
2
. In particular, the number of tests required in Theorem
1
is never more than a
{\cal O}\left(d^{1+o(1)}\right)
factor larger than the information-theoretic lower bound in Theorem
2
.

Using Theorem
3
, we show in Corollary
2
below (whose proof is in Appendix
L
) that for
any
noisy test function our non-adaptive scheme is indeed order-wise optimal.

Corollary 2

In the sparse regime
d=n^{\theta},0\leq\theta<1
, the upper and lower bounds are order-wise tight for all noisy test functions.

Indeed, we present the following conjecture, whose positive resolution would tightly characterize the order-wise sample-complexity for
any
monotone test function.

Conjecture 1

We conjecture that for any monotone test function
f(\cdot)
,
\frac{\min_{{q}\in(0,1)}{{\Gamma}({q})}}{{h(f)}d\log n}\in\Theta(1)
.

IV-D

Estimating the Exact Number of Defectives

As highlighted in Section
III
, our algorithms depend critically on the assumption that the number of defectives
d
is known
a priori
. Moreover, different from the classical group-testing in which most algorithms are robust to small perturbations in the value of
d
, our algorithms require the exact value of
d
. If the value of
d
is not available, which is likely to be the case in practice, it will be useful to have an algorithm for exactly estimating this. In Appendix
M
, we develop such an algorithm, whose performance is summarized in the following result.

Lemma 3

For any fixed
{\varepsilon}\in(0,1)
, there exists an adaptive algorithm that outputs the exact value of
d
with probability at least
1-{\varepsilon}
, using
{\cal O}\left(\left({H(f)}d\right)^{2}\log d\log\left(\frac{\log n}{{\varepsilon}}\right)\right)
tests, where
{H(f)}
is the sensitivity parameter (
18
). Moreover, it uses at most
2\log(2d)
stages of adaptivity.

The idea of the algorithm may be found in Section
V
. The details are given in Appendix
M
.

Comparing Lemma
3
with Theorem
1
, we see the number of tests required by our algorithm for estimating
d
is about a
{\cal O}({H(f)}d)
factor larger than the number of tests required by our algorithm for estimating
{\cal D}
, because we need an exact estimate of
d
. Therefore, as future work we are exploring algorithms which can estimate the set
{\cal D}
even when
d
is only known up to a (small) multiplicative factor.

suitable-designed tests, in which each test must be designed prior to observing any outcomes. Let
\boldsymbol{{Z}}=({Z}_{1},\dots,{Z}_{T})
be a length
T
vector, where
{Z}_{i}
denotes the number of defectives in the
i
-th test.
The test outcomes are represented by a binary vector
\boldsymbol{{Y}}=({Y}_{1},\dots,{Y}_{T})\in\{0,1\}^{T}
, where
{Y}_{i}=1
indicates the outcome of the
i
-th test is positive. We emphasize that
{Z}_{i}
is independent of
({Y}_{1},\dots,{Y}_{i-1},{Y}_{i+1},\dots,{Y}_{T})
. Given the tests and their outcomes, let
\boldsymbol{{\hat{X}}}
be an estimate of
\boldsymbol{{X}}
.

By standard information-theoretic definitions, we have

\displaystyle{H}(\boldsymbol{{X}})

\displaystyle={H}(\boldsymbol{{X}}|\boldsymbol{{Y}})+{I}(\boldsymbol{{X}};\boldsymbol{{Y}})

\displaystyle={H}(\boldsymbol{{X}}|\boldsymbol{{Y}},\boldsymbol{{\hat{X}}})+{I}(\boldsymbol{{X}};\boldsymbol{{Y}})

\displaystyle\leq{H}(\boldsymbol{{X}}|\boldsymbol{{\hat{X}}})+{I}(\boldsymbol{{X}};\boldsymbol{{Y}})

(35)

where the second line follows since
\boldsymbol{{\hat{X}}}
is a function of
\boldsymbol{{Y}}
; the thrid line follows from the fact that conditioning reduces entropy.
Since the defective set
{\cal D}
is uniformly distributed over all length
n
vector of Hamming weight
d
, we have

{H}(\boldsymbol{{X}})=\log\binom{n}{d}.

(36)

By Fano’s inequality,

{H}(\boldsymbol{{X}}|\boldsymbol{{\hat{X}}})\leq 1+\varepsilon\log\binom{n}{d}.

(37)

Let
{Y}^{i-1}:=({Y}_{1},\dots,{Y}_{i-1})
and
{Z}^{i-1}:=({Z}_{1},\dots,{Z}_{i-1})
.
6
6

6

For
i=1
, we follow the convention that
{Y}^{i-1}={Z}^{i-1}=\emptyset
.
Similar to channel coding (see for example
[

, Sec. 7.3]
), it can be easily verified that

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{{X}},{Z}^{i-1},{Y}^{i-1})-{Z}_{i}-{Y}_{i}

which implies

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{{X}},{Y}^{i-1})-{Z}_{i}-{Y}_{i}.

(38)

Following a standard set of inequalities we have

\displaystyle I(\boldsymbol{{X}};\boldsymbol{{Y}})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{T}\left[{H}({Y}_{i}|{Y}^{i-1})-{H}({Y}_{i}|\boldsymbol{{X}},{Y}^{i-1})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum\limits_{i=1}^{T}\left[{H}({Y}_{i})-{H}({Y}_{i}|\boldsymbol{{X}},{Y}^{i-1})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum\limits_{i=1}^{T}\left[{H}({Y}_{i})-{H}({Y}_{i}|\boldsymbol{{X}},{Y}^{i-1},{Z}_{i})\right]

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{T}\left[{H}({Y}_{i})-{H}({Y}_{i}|{Z}_{i})\right]

(39)

where the first line follows from chain rule; the second and third lines follow since conditioning reduces entropy;
the last line follows from (
38
).

Let
{\chi}_{i}
denote the pool-size of the
i
-th test and
\Pr({Z}_{i}={a})
denote the probability that
{Z}_{i}={a}
. We have

\displaystyle\Pr({Z}_{i}={a})

\displaystyle=\frac{\binom{d}{{a}}\binom{n-d}{{\chi}_{i}-{a}}}{\binom{n}{{\chi}_{i}}}.

(40)

For simplicity, we define

\displaystyle{\mu}({\chi}_{i})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum\limits_{{a}=0}^{d}\Pr({Z}_{i}={a})f({a}),

(41)

\displaystyle{\sigma^{2}}({\chi}_{i})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum\limits_{{a}=0}^{d}\Pr({Z}_{i}={a})\left(f({a})-{\mu}({\chi}_{i})\right)^{2},

where
{\mu}({\chi}_{i})
and

denote the mean and variance of
\Pr({Y}_{i}=1|{Z}_{i})
.
It turns out we are able to bound the bracketed term
[\cdot]
in (

Lemma 10

{H}({Y}_{i})-{H}({Y}_{i}|{Z}_{i})\leq\frac{{\sigma^{2}}({\chi}_{i})\log e}{{\mu}({\chi}_{i})(1-{\mu}({\chi}_{i}))}

(42)

Proof:

See Appendix
I
.
∎Now substituting (

) into (

), we see that

\displaystyle\leq\sum\limits_{i=1}^{T}\frac{{\sigma^{2}}({\chi}_{i})\log e}{{\mu}({\chi}_{i})(1-{\mu}({\chi}_{i}))}

\displaystyle\leq T\times\frac{{\sigma^{2}}({\chi}^{*})\log e}{{\mu}({\chi}^{*})(1-{\mu}({\chi}^{*}))},

(43)

where the second line follows by defining

\displaystyle{\chi}^{*}:=\argmax_{{\chi}\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}}\frac{{\sigma^{2}}({\chi})}{{\mu}({\chi})(1-{\mu}({\chi}))}.

(44)

The reason for restricting

is that

.

Finally, combining (

) and (

(45)

This along with the definition of

\displaystyle=\left|{\kappa}-{\eta}\right|,

(53)

\displaystyle\sqrt{{\hat{L}}+1}

\displaystyle=\min\left\{\sqrt{{\kappa}+1},\sqrt{{\eta}+1}\right\},

(54)

\displaystyle\sqrt{d-{\hat{U}}+1}

Since
\min\left\{{U}-{L},\sqrt{{L}+1},\sqrt{d-{U}+1}\right\}\leq{U}-{L}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\left(f(d)-f(0)\right)^{2}}

where the second line follows from the assumption that
f(\cdot)
