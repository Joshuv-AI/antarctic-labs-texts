A Flexible Velocity Boltzmann Scheme for Convection-Diffusion Equations
S.V.Raghurama Rao, K.S.Shrinath, Ankit Ruhi, Veeredhi Vasudeva Rao

Abstract
A framework of finite-velocity model based Boltzmann equation has been developed for convection-diffusion equations. These velocities are kept flexible and adjusted to control numerical diffusion. A flux difference splitting based kinetic scheme is then introduced for solving a wide variety of nonlinear convection-diffusion equations numerically. Based on this framework, a generalized kinetic Lax-Wendroff scheme is also derived, recovering the classical Lax-Wendroff method as one of the choices. Further, a total variation diminishing version of this kinetic flux difference splitting scheme is presented, combining it with the kinetic Lax-Wendroff scheme using a limiter function. The numerical scheme has been extensively tested and the results for benchmark test cases, for 1D and 2D nonlinear convection and convection-diffusion equations, are presented.

Introduction

Conservation equations of fluid and gas dynamics are of importance in several branches of engineering, applied physics and applied mathematics. Typically physical principles representing conservation of mass, momentum, energy and species lead to such conservation equations. The well-known Navier-stokes equations, representing conservation principles, model the nonlinear convection and diffusion processes occuring in fluid flows. The essential features of the Navier-Stokes equations are easily represented by a scalar model, the convection-diffusion equation, with a nonlinear convection term. In this paper, a new finite velocity Boltzmann scheme is presented for simulating nonlinear convection-diffusion equations. The kinetic schemes are some of the alternative approaches to traditional macroscopic description based algorithms for modeling compressible fluid flows, pursued from the 1970s. . In this paper, the focus is on developing kinetic schemes for nonlinear convection equations and convection-diffusion equations. The basic foundation of the number of finite velocities is also different in this work, with the focus being on the minimal number of velocities required to obtain an efficient scheme. The strategy used to obtain second order accuracy is also different. Further, a specific strategy is also introduced to deal with the source terms that arise naturally in some convection systems.

In the current work, first a flexible velocity Boltzmann equation (FVBE) is introduced, with two velocities in 1-D and four velocities in 2-D. This model resembles the discrete velocity Boltzmann system in structure, though the velocities are flexible and not constants. A flux difference splitting numerical scheme is then developed based on this framework. The flexible velocities at the level of designing the numerical algorithm are fixed based on sub-characteristic condition, derived from the Chapman-Enskog expansion (named
Kinetic Flux Difference Splitting
KFDS) or by using the Rankine-Hugoniot(RH) jump conditions (named KFDS+). The use of RH condition to fix the numerical diffusion results in a low diffusive algorithm capable of exact capturing of steady discontinuities. To obtain higher order accuracy, a second order Kinetic Lax-Wendroff type scheme (KLW) has been developed based on the FVBE. Further, a total variation diminishing model of KFDS scheme (TVD-KFDS) has been constructed using limiters. This results in deploying optimal numerical diffusion based on Rankine-Hugoniot condition in resolving the discontinuities and sub-characteristic condition based numerical diffusion in smooth regions. Further, the formulation is extended to systems of hyperbolic PDEs representing the shallow water flows, together with a special way of treating the source terms. Several typical bench-mark problems are solved using the new algorithm to demonstrate its capability in resolving the propagating and diffusing nonlinear waves in numerical simulations.

The new scheme is first derived for pure convection equations in the following sections.

2
Kinetic Schemes for Hyperbolic Conservation Equations

The kinetic or Boltzmann schemes are based on the fact that conservation equations of gas dynamics can be obtained as moments of the classical Boltzmann equation. This strategy can be extended the to scalar conservation laws, with appropriate definition of the equilibrium distribution functions. For example, 1-D Burgers equation, given by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}+\displaystyle\frac{\partial g(u)}{\partial x}=0,\ \textrm{where}\ g(u)=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}u^{2}

(1)

can be written in the moment form

\left\langle\left(\underbrace{\displaystyle\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}+{v}\cdot\displaystyle\frac{\partial f}{\partial{x}}=J(f,f)}_{\rm Boltzmann\ equation}\right)\right\rangle

(2)

where the moments are defined by

\left\langle f\right\rangle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f\ dv

(3)

Here,
f
is the molecular velocity distribution function,
v
is the molecular velocity and
J(f,f)
is the collision term. A simpler model for the collision term,
J(f,f)
, in the Boltzmann equation is given by the popular B-G-K model
[
17
]
, which reduces the otherwise integro-differential equation to a PDE with a simple
(algebraic) source term.

J(f,f)=-\displaystyle\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left[f-f^{eq}\right]

(4)

Here,
\epsilon
is the relaxation time and
f^{eq}
is the distribution representing local thermodynamic equilibrium, given by a simpe Gaussian as

f^{eq}=u\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left(v-\tilde{u}\right)^{2}}

(5)

Utilizing the B-G-K model and operator splitting, the solution of the Boltzmann equation can be split into two steps as

\textrm{\bf Convection Step:}\ \displaystyle\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}+\displaystyle\frac{\partial{h}}{\partial{x}}=0

(6)

\textrm{\bf Collision Step:}\ \displaystyle\frac{df}{dt}=-\displaystyle\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left[f-f^{eq}\right]

(7)

where
{h}={v}f
is the flux, with the Boltzmann equation being written in conservation form.
Choosing an
instantaneous relaxation to equilibrium
(
\epsilon=0
), the collision step becomes a simple relaxation step as
f=f^{eq}
. Thus, a conservation equation can be written in an intriguing form as

\left\langle\left(\underbrace{\displaystyle\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}+\displaystyle\frac{\partial\vec{h}}{\partial\vec{x}}=0}_{\rm convection},\ \underbrace{f=f^{eq}}_{\rm relaxation}\right)\right\rangle

(8)

The essential advantage of using this representation for macroscopic Burgers equation is the linearity of the convection terms in the Boltzmann equation. This makes introducing upwinding easier in a kinetic or Boltzmann scheme, easily leading to an upwind scheme for a conservation equation. While this strategy is explained for Burgers equation here, it is possible to extend the strategy to any conservation equation or a system of conservation laws.

2.1
Strategy for flexible velocity kinetic schemes

Here, we utilize the above formulation and introduce new versions of the equilibrium based on flexible velocities. These discrete distributions are then utilized to introduce novel kinetic schemes for solving various conservation equations. Compared to traditional kinetic schemes, the expressions here are much simpler, as integrations are replaced by summations and Maxwellians are replaced by simpler algebraic expressions. It is worth noting here that the numerical schemes developed in this work begin with the conservation form of the Boltzmann equation. Further, the finite volume framework and the flux splitting form of numerical diffusion ensure the preservation of the conservation form. These frameworks ensure conservation even if we make the velocities functions of conserved variables. Further, the essential numerical diffusion is fixed based on Chapman Engskog type expansion (KFDS) or
by enforcing Rankine-Hugoniot (R-H) conditions
(KFDS+). The former approach (KFDS) ensures boundedness to the maximum eigenvalue and ensures conservation, while the latter (KFDS+) utilises R-H conditions which represent the quintessential conservation of fluxes across discontinuities. In the next section, the new flexible velocity Boltzmann equation is introduced.

3
Flexible Velocity Boltzmann Scheme for 1-D convection equation

3.1
1-D convection equation in moment form

Consider the 1-D nonlinear convection equation as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}+\displaystyle\frac{\partial g(u)}{\partial x}=0

(9)

where
u
is the dependent variable and
g(u)
its nonlinear flux. If we consider the inviscid Burgers equation,
g(u)=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}u^{2}
. The framework which will be constructed in the following sections will be valid for any non-linear flux
g(u)
. We construct here a kinetic framework to derive this nonlinear hyperbolic convection equation as a moment of a Boltzmann equation given by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}+\displaystyle\frac{\partial(vf)}{\partial x}=-\displaystyle\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left[f-f^{eq}\right]

(10)

Here,
f
is the molecular velocity distribution function,
v
is the molecular velocity,
\epsilon
is a small relaxation time and
f^{eq}
is the equilibrium distribution function, which is typically the Maxwellian. The right hand side is the popular BGK relaxation model
[
17
]
in which the distribution function relaxes to its equilibrium within a small relaxation time,
\epsilon
.
The moments to obtain the macroscopic variables are defined by

u=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}fdv\ \textrm{and}\ g(u)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}vfdv

(11)

so that the taking a moment of the Boltzmann equation (
10
) as

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left[\displaystyle\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}+\displaystyle\frac{\partial(vf)}{\partial x}=-\displaystyle\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left\{f-f^{eq}\right\}\right]dv

(12)

yields, using the moment relations (
11
), the original scalar convection equation (
9
). The equilibrium distribution function,
f^{eq}
is typically the Maxwellian in the traditional kinetic theory of gases. To construct
f^{eq}
, let us start with the Gaussian with unit variance as

f^{eq}=u\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left(v-\tilde{u}\right)^{2}}

(13)

Its moment is given by

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f^{eq}dv&=&\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}u\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left(v-\tilde{u}\right)^{2}}dv\\
&=&u\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left(v-\tilde{u}\right)^{2}}dv\end{array}

Let us define a new variable
w
as

w=\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(v-\tilde{u}\right)

(14)

The limits change as follows.

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{c}\textrm{As}\ v\rightarrow-\infty,\ w\rightarrow-\infty\\
\textrm{As}\ v\rightarrow\infty,\ w\rightarrow\infty\\
\textrm{As}\ v\rightarrow 0,\ w\rightarrow-\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\tilde{u}\\
\end{array}

Note also that
dv=dw\sqrt{2}
. Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f^{eq}dv=u\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-w^{2}}dw\sqrt{2}=u\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-w^{2}}dw=u\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sqrt{\pi}=u

thus recovering the first of the moment conditions (
11
). Enforcing the second of the moment conditions (
11
) leads to

\begin{array}[]{rcl}g(u)&=&\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}vf^{eq}dv\\
&=&\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}vu\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left(v-\tilde{u}\right)^{2}}dv\\
&=&u\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(w\sqrt{2}+\tilde{u}\right)e^{-w^{2}}dw\sqrt{2}\\
&=&u\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\left[0+\tilde{u}\sqrt{\pi}\right]\\
&=&u\tilde{u}\end{array}

Therefore, we obtain

\tilde{u}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{u}g(u)

(19)

For the inviscid Burgers equation, we get

\tilde{u}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{u}g(u)=\displaystyle\frac{1}{u}\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}u^{2}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}u

(20)

3.2
Dirac delta based equilibrium distribution and flexible velocities

Let us replace the equilibrium distribution by a combination of Dirac delta functions as

f^{eq}=f^{eq}_{+}\delta(v-\lambda_{+})+f^{eq}_{-}\delta(v-\lambda_{-})

(21)

Let us further assume, for simplicity, that the flexible velocities,
\lambda_{+}
and
\lambda_{-}

are given by

\lambda_{+}=\lambda\ \textrm{and}\ \lambda_{-}=-\lambda

(22)

Thus, we have three unknowns, namely,
f^{eq}_{+}
,
f^{eq}_{-}
and
\lambda
to be fixed for the equilibrium distribution. We therefore first use the two moment relations

u=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f^{eq}dv\ \textrm{and}\ g(u)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}vf^{eq}dv

(23)

together with either the Chapman-Enskog type expansion or the Rankine-Hugoniot jump condition (or both, with each being used in a different part of the domain) as the third relation for fixing the unknowns. The R-H condition is given by

\Delta g(u)=s\Delta u

(24)

Here
s
is the shock speed and we utilize this condition in constructing the cell-interface flux in a finite volume method, with the hope of designing a scheme which can capture discontinuities like shock waves accurately.

Applying the moment conditions (
11
), we obtain the following.

\begin{array}[]{rcl}u&=&\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f^{eq}dv\\
&=&\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left\{f^{eq}_{+}\delta(v-\lambda_{+})+f^{eq}_{-}\delta(v-\lambda_{-})\right\}dv\\
&=&f^{eq}_{+}(1)+f^{eq}_{-}(1)\end{array}

or

f^{eq}_{+}+f^{eq}_{-}=u

(25)

\begin{array}[]{rcl}g(u)&=&\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}vf^{eq}dv\\[8.53581pt]
&=&\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}v\left\{f^{eq}_{+}\delta(v-\lambda_{+})+f^{eq}_{-}\delta(v-\lambda_{-})\right\}dv\\[5.69054pt]
&=&f^{eq}_{+}\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi(v)\delta(v-\lambda_{+})dv+f^{eq}_{-}\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi(v)\delta(v-\lambda_{-})dv\ \textrm{where}\ \phi(v)=v\\
&=&f^{eq}_{+}\lambda_{+}+f^{eq}_{-}\lambda_{-}\end{array}

or

f^{eq}_{+}\lambda_{+}+f^{eq}_{-}\lambda_{-}=g(u)

(26)

Solving the above two equations, we obtain

f^{eq}_{+}=\displaystyle\frac{-\lambda_{-}u+g(u)}{\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{-}}\ \textrm{and}\ f^{eq}_{-}=\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{+}u-g(u)}{\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{-}}

(27)

With our simplification of flexible velocities (
22
), these components of the equilibrium distribution become

f^{eq}_{+}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}u+\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\lambda}g(u)\ \textrm{and}\ f^{eq}_{-}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}u-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\lambda}g(u)

(28)

with
\lambda
to be fixed while applying the finite volume method, using Chapman-Enskog expansion or the Rankine-Hugoniot condition
(
24
).

We can also rewrite the expressions derived above as

u=\sum_{i=1}^{2}f^{eq}_{i}\ \textrm{and}\ g(u)=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\lambda_{i}f^{eq}_{i}

(29)

where

f^{eq}_{1}=f^{eq}_{+},\ \lambda_{1}=\lambda_{+},\ f^{eq}_{2}=f^{eq}_{-}\ \textrm{and}\ \lambda_{2}=\lambda_{-},

(30)

We now define

u=\sum_{i=1}^{2}f_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{2}f^{eq}_{i}\ \textrm{and}\ g(u)=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\lambda_{i}f^{eq}_{i}

(31)

so that the first moment is true for any distribution function. Comparing these moments with the original moments
(
11
) clearly shows that the continuous molecular velocity
v
is replaced by two flexible velocities (
\pm\lambda
) and the molecular velocity distribution function
f
is replaced by two components:
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
.
We thus obtain the framework of
flexible velocity Boltzmann equation
as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f_{i}}{\partial t}+\displaystyle\frac{\partial(\lambda_{i}f_{i})}{\partial x}=-\displaystyle\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left[f_{i}-f^{eq}_{i}\right],\ i=1,2

(32)

with the moment relations (
31
) to be used for recovering the nonlinear convection equation

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}+\displaystyle\frac{\partial g(u)}{\partial x}=0

(33)

The essential advantage of this formulation is the linearity of the convection terms in the flexible velocity Boltzmann equation, which makes the job of developing a numerical method for the nonlinear convection equation simpler.

3.3
Flexible Velocity Boltzmann Equation and Moments

The
Flexible Velocity Boltzmann Equation
(FVBE) derived before can be written in matrix form as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bf f}{\partial t}+\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bf h}{\partial x}=-\displaystyle\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left[\bf f-\bf f^{eq}\right]

(34)

where

\bf f=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}f_{+}\\
f_{-}\end{array}\right],\ \ \bf h=\Lambda\bf f,\ \Lambda=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\lambda_{+}&0\\
0&\lambda_{-}\end{array}\right]\ \textrm{and}\ \bf f^{eq}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}f^{eq}_{+}\\
f^{eq}_{-}\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}u+\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\lambda}g(u)\\[11.38109pt]
\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}u-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\lambda}g(u)\end{array}\right]

(35)

To obtain the moments (which are now based on summations instead of integrals), we introduce

{\bf P}=[1\ 1]

(36)

Therefore, the moments (
31
) are now defined by

u={\bf P}{\bf f^{eq}}\ \textrm{and}\ g(u)={\bf P}\Lambda{\bf f^{eq}}

(37)

Thus, the moment of the FVBE can be written as

{\bf P}\left[\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bf f}{\partial t}+\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Lambda\bf f}{\partial x}=-\displaystyle\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left[\bf f-\bf f^{eq}\right]\right]

(38)

which is equivalent, in the limit
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
, to the original conservation law

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}+\displaystyle\frac{\partial(g(u))}{\partial x}=0

(39)

as

{\bf P}{\bf f}={\bf P}{\bf f^{eq}}=u

(40)

A further simplification can be introduced by using operator splitting, by which the solution of the flexible velocity Boltzmann equation can be split into two steps: a
convection step
and a
relaxation step
. Thus

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bf f}{\partial t}+\displaystyle\frac{\partial\left(\Lambda\bf f\right)}{\partial x}=-\displaystyle\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left[\bf f-\bf f^{eq}\right]

can be written as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bf f}{\partial t}=-\displaystyle\frac{\partial\left(\Lambda\bf f\right)}{\partial x}-\displaystyle\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left[\bf f-\bf f^{eq}\right]

or

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bf f}{\partial t}=O_{1}({\bf f})+O_{2}({\bf f})

where
O_{1}({\bf f})
and
O_{2}({\bf f})
are mathematical operators of
{\bf f}
.
The above equation can be split, using operator splitting, as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bf f}{\partial t}=O_{1}({\bf f})\ \textrm{and}\ \displaystyle\frac{\partial\bf f}{\partial t}=O_{2}({\bf f})

or

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bf f}{\partial t}+\displaystyle\frac{\partial\left(\Lambda\bf f\right)}{\partial x}=0\ \textrm{and}\ \displaystyle\frac{d\bf f}{dt}=-\displaystyle\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left[\bf f-\bf f^{eq}\right]

leading to a
convection step
and a
relaxation step
. The relaxation step can be further simplified into an
instantaneous relaxation step
by assuming that
\epsilon=0
. This leads to instantaneous relaxation of the distribution function to its equilibrium,
{\bf f}
=
{\bf f^{eq}}
. Thus, the solution of the flexible velocity Boltzmann equation can be written as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\bf f}}{\partial t}+\displaystyle\frac{\partial\left(\Lambda\bf f\right)}{\partial x}=0,\ {\bf f}={\bf f^{eq}}

(41)

Now taking moments, we obtain

{\bf P}\left[\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bf f}{\partial t}+\displaystyle\frac{\partial\left(\Lambda\bf f\right)}{\partial x}=0,\ {\bf f}={\bf f^{eq}}\right]

(42)

which is the moment form of the original nonlinear convection equation given by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}+\displaystyle\frac{\partial g(u)}{\partial x}=0

(43)

Note that our strategy of deriving a flexible velocity Boltzmann equation based on approximating the Maxwellian with a combination of Dirac delta functions has led to recovering the diagonal relaxation model of Aregba-Driollet & Natalini
[
10
]
in 1-D, which is also equivalent to the relaxation system of Jin & Xin
[
15
]
. However, our discretization strategy leads to a totally different numerical scheme, which is presented in the following.

3.4
Kinetic Flux Splitting Based on Flexible Velocities

Now consider the flexible velocity Boltzmann formulation given by (
38
) in a finite volume framework.

The update formula can be written as

where

in to two parts, separating the positive and negative velocities.

\displaystyle{\bf h}_{j-\frac{1}{2}}^{n}=[{\Lambda}^{+}{\bf f}_{eq}]_{j-1}+[{\Lambda}^{-}{\bf f}_{eq}]_{j}

Clearly,

where

. In the next sub-section, the coefficient of numerical diffusion, which corresponds to

To recover the macroscopic update formula, let is take moments by multiplying with

as given in (

). Therefore the macroscopic update formula for the Kinetic Flux Difference Splitting (KFDS) scheme thus developed using 2-velocity model based FVBE can be written as

\displaystyle\Delta g(u)_{j+\frac{1}{2}}^{\pm}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}[g(u)_{j+1}-g(u)_{j}]\pm\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}|\lambda|[u_{j+1}-u_{j}]

Note that all the variables of the flexible velocity Boltzmann equation have disappeared in the final scheme, in the spirit of the kinetic or Boltzmann schemes. All the variables in the final scheme are purely macroscopic variables, as
\lambda
, fixing of which is explained in the next sections based on numerical considerations, also takes the values of macroscopic variables. The kinetic framework is used only the conceptual level, exploiting the linearity of the convection terms in the flexible velocity Boltzmann equation, leading to an upwind scheme for the nonlinear convection equation.

3.5
Fixing
\lambda

Two different ways of fixing
\lambda
are proposed here. The first way is based on Chapman-Enskog type asymptotic expansion of the FVBE, which leads to a diffusive scheme. The second way is based on utilizing the Rankine-Hugoniot jump condition, which leads to an accurate shock capturing scheme. By substituting the expressions for the split fluxes into the finite volume update formula, one can easily see that
\lambda
also represents the coefficient of numerical diffusion and therefore the different choices for
\lambda
can be justified to modify the numerical diffusion in the scheme.

3.5.1
Chapman-Enskog type expansion of the flexible velocity Boltzmann equation

One simple way of fixing
\lambda
is to use the Chapman-Enskog type expansion of the flexible velocity Boltzmann equation. This expansion, which is a special form of asymptotic expansion for the flexible velocity Boltzmann equation, is given in the Appendix C. The Chapman-Enskog type expansion shows that the flexible velocity Boltzmann equation leads to the original convection equation augmented by a diffusion term, given by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}+\displaystyle\frac{\partial g(u)}{\partial x}=\epsilon\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left[\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}\left\{\lambda^{2}-\left(a\left(u\right)\right)^{2}\right\}\right]+\mathcal{O}\left(\epsilon^{2}\right)

(64)

where
a(u)
is the wave speed for the original convection equation, given by
a(u)=\displaystyle\frac{\partial g(u)}{\partial u}
. The right hand side in the above equation contains a second derivative of the conserved variable
u
and is multiplied by a small parameter (
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
). Thus, the moment of the flexible velocity Boltzmann equation represents a vanishing diffusion approximation to the original convection equation. For the approximation to be stable, the diffusion term must be non-negative. Therefore, we obtain

\lambda^{2}-\left(a\left(u\right)\right)^{2}\geq 0

(65)

which can approximated to

\lambda=\max\left[a(u)_{j}\right],\ \forall j

(66)

where
j
is the index representing the grid points. The scheme derived using this approach is named as
KFDS
method, as it leads to a kinetic flux difference splitting scheme, based on flexible velocities.

3.5.2
Fixing
\lambda
based on R-H (jump) condition

Let us fix
\lambda
based on enforcing Rankine-Hugoniot jump condition (R-H condition) at the cell-interface. Let us assume that a shock discontinuity is present at the cell-interface
I
, moving with shock speed
s
, as shown in the figure (
2
).

Shock at a cell-interface in FVM

The Rankine-Hugoniot jump condition connecting the left state
L
and the right state
R
is given by

\Delta g(u)=s_{I}\Delta u

(67)

where

\Delta u=u_{R}-u_{L}\ \textrm{and}\ \Delta g(u)=g_{R}(u)-g_{L}(u)

(68)

Splitting the shock speed into a positive part (moving to the right) and a negative part (moving to the left), we can write
(see figure(
2
))

s_{I}=s^{+}_{I}+s^{-}_{I}=\displaystyle\frac{s_{I}+|s_{I}|}{2}+\displaystyle\frac{s_{I}-|s_{I}|}{2}

(69)

Using the above shock speed splitting, we now split the R-H condition into two parts, one to the right of the cell-interface and the other to the left of the cell-interface (see figure
1
). Obviously, out of the three variables (
u
,
g
,
s
) in the R-H equation, as we are modifying
u
and
g
, the third part,
\Delta u
needs to be kept unmodified. Therefore, we obtain

g_{R}(u)-g_{I}(u)=s^{+}_{I}\Delta u\ \textrm{and}\ g_{I}(u)-g_{L}(u)=s^{-}_{I}\Delta u

(70)

Subtracting one from the other, we obtain

g_{R}(u)-g_{I}(u)-g_{I}(u)+g_{L}(u)=\left(s^{+}_{I}-s^{-}_{I}\right)\Delta u

or

g_{I}(u)=\displaystyle\frac{g_{L}(u)+g_{R}(u)}{2}-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\displaystyle\frac{s_{I}+|s_{I}|}{2}-\displaystyle\frac{s_{I}-|s_{I}|}{2}\right)\Delta u

or

g_{I}(u)=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left[g_{L}(u)+g_{R}(u)\right]-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}|s_{I}|\Delta u

(71)

This is the cell-interface flux derived from the R-H condition. The cell-interface flux derived for the flexible velocity based upwind kinetic scheme is

\begin{array}[]{rcl}g(u)_{I}&=&g^{+}(u)_{L}+g^{-}(u)_{R}\\[8.53581pt]
&=&\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left[g(u)_{L}+g(u)_{R}\right]-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\lambda\left[u_{R}-u_{L}\right]\end{array}

Therefore

g(u)_{I}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left[g(u)_{L}+g(u)_{R}\right]-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\lambda\Delta u

(72)

Comparing the cell-interface flux derived from the flexible velocity based upwind kinetic scheme
(
72
) with that derived based on the R-H condition
(
71
), we obtain

\lambda=|s_{I}|

(73)

For the nonlinear convection equation, as
g(u)=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}u^{2}
, we obtain

s=\displaystyle\frac{\Delta g(u)}{\Delta u}=\displaystyle\frac{g(u)_{R}-g(u)_{L}}{u_{R}-u_{L}}=\displaystyle\frac{\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}u_{R}^{2}-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}u_{L}^{2}}{u_{R}-u_{L}}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(u_{L}+u_{R}\right)

Therefore

\lambda=\left|\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(u_{L}+u_{R}\right)\right|

(74)

The method derived thus is named as
KFDS+
scheme, which is an accurate shock capturing variation of
KFDS
scheme.

The interface fluxes

Expanding the distribution by substituting [

{\lambda}_{-}f^{eq}_{-}\end{array}\right]}_{j}^{n}.

{\left[\begin{array}[]{c}f_{+}\\[8.53581pt]

Using expressions in [

{f}_{\alpha,j}^{n+1}={f}_{\alpha,j}^{n}-\displaystyle\frac{|{\lambda}|\Delta t}{\Delta x}\left[{f}^{eq}_{\alpha,j}-{f}^{eq}_{\alpha,(j+\mathop{\mathrm{sgn}}(\frac{3}{2}-\alpha))}\right]^{n},\ \alpha=1,2

with the order of

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{sgn}}(\omega)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}-1\ \textrm{for}\ \omega<1\\

In order to evaluate the stability characteristics of the above system, we perform a von Neumann stability analysis. We introduce the following Fourier transforms.

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}({f}_{j}^{n})={\hat{f}}^{n}_{j}{e}^{ikj\Delta x}

(95)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}({{f}^{eq,n}_{j}})={{\hat{f}}_{j}^{eq,n}}{e}^{ikj\Delta x}

(96)

Also from Chapman-Engkog expansion we have

f={f}^{eq}+{\epsilon{f}^{\prime}}+\cdots

(97)

It is interesting to note in the above equation that

{{f}^{\prime}}\geq 0,\ \ \epsilon\geq 0,\ \ \cdots.

(98)

which implies
f\geq{f}^{eq}
or
\displaystyle\frac{{f}^{eq}}{f}\leq 1
.
Applying Fourier transform to [
90
] and substituting the above relations we get

{\hat{f}}_{\alpha,j}^{n+1}{e}^{ikj\Delta x}={\hat{f}}_{\alpha,j}^{n}{e}^{ikj\Delta x}-\displaystyle\frac{|{\lambda}|\Delta t}{\Delta x}\left[{\hat{f}}_{\alpha,j}^{eq,n}-{\hat{f}}_{\alpha}^{eq,n}{e}^{ik\left(\mathop{\mathrm{sgn}}\left(\frac{3}{2}-\alpha\right)\right)\Delta x}\right]{e}^{ikj\Delta x}.

(99)

Rearranging the terms and substituting
C=\displaystyle\frac{|{\lambda}|\Delta t}{\Delta x}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{{\hat{f}}_{\alpha,j}^{n+1}}{{\hat{f}}_{\alpha,j}^{n}}=1-C\displaystyle\frac{{\hat{f}}_{\alpha,j}^{eq,n}}{{\hat{f}}_{\alpha,j}^{n}}\left[1-{e}^{ik\left(\mathop{\mathrm{sgn}}\left(\frac{3}{2}-\alpha\right)\right)\Delta x}\right]

(100)

The exponential term can be further expanded using Euler’s formula
{e}^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta
. we get

\displaystyle\frac{{\hat{f}}_{\alpha,j}^{n+1}}{{\hat{f}}_{\alpha,j}^{n}}=1-C\displaystyle\frac{{\hat{f}}_{\alpha,j}^{eq,n}}{{\hat{f}}_{\alpha,j}^{n}}\left[1-\left(\cos\left(k\Delta x\right)+\mathop{\mathrm{sgn}}\left(\frac{3}{2}-\alpha\right)i\sin\left(k\Delta x\right)\right)\right]

(101)

Rearranging, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{{\hat{f}}_{\alpha,j}^{n+1}}{{\hat{f}}_{\alpha,j}^{n}}=1+C\displaystyle\frac{{\hat{f}}_{\alpha,j}^{eq,n}}{{\hat{f}}_{\alpha,j}^{n}}\left[\left(\cos\left(k\Delta x\right)+\mathop{\mathrm{sgn}}\left(\frac{3}{2}-\alpha\right)i\sin\left(k\Delta x\right)\right)-1\right].

(102)

or

\displaystyle\frac{{\hat{f}}_{\alpha,j}^{n+1}}{{\hat{f}}_{\alpha,j}^{n}}\leq 1+C\left[\left(\cos\left(k\Delta x\right)+\mathop{\mathrm{sgn}}\left(\frac{3}{2}-\alpha\right)i\sin\left(k\Delta x\right)\right)-1\right].

(103)

Here, we evaluate the RHS part by multiplying it with its complex conjugate
{RHS}^{*}
.

\displaystyle RHS=\left[\left(1+C\cos\left(k\Delta x\right)-C\right)+i\mathop{\mathrm{sgn}}\left(\frac{3}{2}-\alpha\right)C\sin\left(k\Delta x\right)\right]

(104)

\displaystyle{RHS}^{*}=\left[\left(1+C\cos\left(k\Delta x\right)-C\right)+i\mathop{\mathrm{sgn}}\left(\frac{3}{2}-\alpha\right)C\sin\left(k\Delta x\right)\right]

(105)

{|RHS|}^{2}=RHS.{RHS}^{*}

(106)

{|RHS|}^{2}=1+2C\left(\cos\left(k\Delta x\right)-1\right)+{C}^{2}\left({\cos}^{2}\left(k\Delta x\right)+1-2\cos\left(k\Delta x\right)\right)+{C}^{2}{\sin}^{2}\left(k\Delta x\right)

(107)

which simplifies to

{|RHS|}^{2}=1-2C\left(1-C\right)\left(1-\cos\left(k\Delta x\right)\right)

(108)

For the numerical scheme to be stable, we need to find the conditions at which
{|RHS|}^{2}\leq 1
. Therefore the above expression can be written as

2C\left(1-C\right)\left(1-\cos\left(k\Delta x\right)\right)\leq 0.

(109)

We know that
\left[1-\cos\left(k\Delta x\right)\right]\geq 0\ \forall\ k,\Delta x\ \in\mathbb{R}
. Therefore for the scheme to be stable, we get

C\left(C-1\right)\leq 0
, which upon solving yields
0\leq\ C\ \leq 1
. Thus the condition for the scheme to be stable is

0\leq\displaystyle\frac{|\lambda|\Delta t}{\Delta x}\leq 1

(110)

4
Second order accuracy

In this section, the Lax-Wendroff method is first introduced in the flexible velocity Boltzmann framework. Following that, a high resolution TVD scheme in which KFDS method (correspondingly also KFDS+ method) switches over to the
Kinetic Lax-Wendroff Method
in the smooth regions, with the help of a limiter function, is introduced.

4.1
Kinetic Lax-Wendroff (KLW) Method

While the second order accuracy can be obtained by several possibilities, including upwinding on extended stencils, the Lax-Wendroff approach is attractive because of its operation in a compact stencil of three points. Here, the Lax-Wendroff method in the framework of flexible velocity Boltzmann equation is introduced and then moments are taken, a process which yields a
generalized Lax-Wendroff method
.

The flexible velocity Boltzmann framework we start with, to obtain the convection equation, is given by

{\bf P}\left[\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\bf f}}{\partial t}+\displaystyle\frac{\partial\left(\Lambda\bf f\right)}{\partial x}=0,{\bf f}={\bf f}^{eq}\right]\Longrightarrow\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}+\displaystyle\frac{\partial g(u)}{\partial x}=0

(111)

Using Taylor series expansion, we can write

{\bf f}(x,t+\Delta t)={\bf f}(x,t)+\displaystyle\frac{\Delta t}{1!}\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\bf f}(x,t)}{\partial t}+\displaystyle\frac{\Delta t^{2}}{2!}\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}{\bf f}(x,t)}{\partial t^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{3})

Using the Boltzmann equation in the convection step, we can write

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\bf f}}{\partial t}=-\displaystyle\frac{\partial\left(\Lambda\bf f\right)}{\partial x}

and further

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}{\bf f}}{\partial t^{2}}=\Lambda^{2}\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}{\bf f}}{\partial x^{2}}

Thus, we get

{\bf f}^{n+1}_{j}={\bf f}^{n}_{j}-\Delta t\Lambda\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\bf f}^{n}}{\partial x}|_{j}+\displaystyle\frac{\Delta t^{2}}{2}\Lambda^{2}\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\bf f}^{n}}{\partial x^{2}}|_{j}+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{3})

Using central differences, we obtain

{\bf f}^{n+1}_{j}={\bf f}^{n}_{j}-\Delta t\Lambda\left[\displaystyle\frac{{\bf f}^{n}_{j+1}-{\bf f}^{n}_{j-1}}{2\Delta x}\right]+\displaystyle\frac{\Delta t^{2}}{2}\Lambda^{2}\left[\displaystyle\frac{{\bf f}^{n}_{j+1}-2{\bf f}^{n}_{j}+{\bf f}^{n}_{j-1}}{\Delta x^{2}}\right]+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{3},\Delta x^{2}\Delta t)

or

{\bf f}^{n+1}_{j}={\bf f}^{n}_{j}-\displaystyle\frac{\Delta t}{2\Delta x}\Lambda\left[{\bf f}^{n}_{j+1}+{\bf f}^{n}_{j}-{\bf f}^{n}_{j}-{\bf f}^{n}_{j-1}\right]+\displaystyle\frac{\Delta t^{2}}{2\Delta x^{2}}\Lambda^{2}\left[{\bf f}^{n}_{j+1}-2{\bf f}^{n}_{j}+{\bf f}^{n}_{j-1}\right]+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{3},\Delta x^{3})

assuming
\Delta t
and
\Delta x
to be of the same order. With a little algebraic manipulation, we obtain

{\bf f}^{n+1}_{j}={\bf f}^{n}_{j}-\displaystyle\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\left[{\bf h}^{n}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-{\bf h}^{n}_{j-\frac{1}{2}}\right]+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{2},\Delta x^{2})

where

{\bf h}^{n}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left[\Lambda{\bf f}_{j}+\Lambda{\bf f}_{j+1}\right]-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Lambda^{2}\displaystyle\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\left[{\bf f}_{j+1}-{\bf f}_{j}\right]

and

{\bf h}^{n}_{j-\frac{1}{2}}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left[\Lambda{\bf f}_{j-1}+\Lambda{\bf f}_{j}\right]-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Lambda^{2}\displaystyle\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\left[{\bf f}_{j}-{\bf f}_{j-1}\right]

If we now take moments, after using the instantaneous relaxation step as

{\bf f}^{n}_{j}={\bf f}^{eq,n}_{j}
we obtain the finite volume update formula as

{\bf P}\left[{\bf f}^{n+1}_{j}={\bf f}^{n}_{j}-\displaystyle\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\left[{\bf h}^{n}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-{\bf h}^{n}_{j-\frac{1}{2}}\right]\right]

(112)

or

u^{n+1}_{j}=u^{n}_{j}-\displaystyle\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\left[g(u)^{n}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-g(u)^{n}_{j-\frac{1}{2}}\right]

(113)

where

g(u)_{j+\frac{1}{2}}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left[g(u)_{j}+g(u)_{j+1}\right]-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\lambda^{2}\displaystyle\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\left[u_{j+1}-u_{j}\right]

(114)

Here we have used the expressions

{\bf P}{\bf f}=u,\ {\bf P}\Lambda{\bf f^{eq}}=g(u)\ \textrm{and}\ {\bf P}\Lambda^{2}{\bf f^{eq}}=\lambda^{2}u

(115)

as

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\bf P}\Lambda^{2}{\bf f}^{eq}&=&\left[1\ 1\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\left(\lambda\right)^{2}&0\\
0&\left(-\lambda\right)^{2}\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}u+\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\lambda}g(u)\\[14.22636pt]
\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}u-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\lambda}g(u)\end{array}\right]\\[14.22636pt]
&=&\lambda^{2}u\end{array}

It is worth noting that if we take
\lambda=a(u)_{j+\frac{1}{2}}
, where

a(u)_{j+\frac{1}{2}}
is the wave speed of the original convection equation at the cell interface, we obtain the classical Lax-Wendroff method. Since other choices are also possible for
\lambda
, the scheme we obtained, which is the
Kinetic Lax-Wendroff (KLW) method
(
113
,
114
), can be thought of a
generalized Lax-Wendroff method
. As a first attempt, we use the Chapman-Enskog type expansion to fix
\lambda
in the KLW method.

4.2
High resolution TVD KFDS Method

Since KLW method, being second order accurate, produces oscillations near large gradients like shock waves, a Total Variation Diminishing (TVD) Kinetic Lax-Wendroff method, as a high resolution KFDS method, is introduced in this section to obtain a non-oscillatory high resolution flexible velocity Boltzmann scheme. The KLW method is written as DVKS method augmented by additional anti-diffusive terms and these anti-diffusive terms are switched off near large gradients using a limiter function, to obtain a non-oscillatory scheme.

Consider the finite volume update formula for the flexible velocity Boltzmann equation, given by

{\bf f}^{n+1}_{j}={\bf f}^{n}_{j}-\displaystyle\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\left[{\bf h}^{n}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-{\bf h}^{n}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\right]

(116)

with the cell-interface flux for deriving KFDS method being given by

{\bf h}^{KFDS}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left[\Lambda{\bf f}^{eq}_{j}+\Lambda{\bf f}^{eq}_{j+1}\right]-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}|\Lambda|\left[{\bf f}^{eq}_{j+1}-{\bf f}^{eq}_{j}\right]

(117)

Similarly, for deriving the KLW method, cell-inteface flux can be written as

{\bf h}^{KLW}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left[\Lambda{\bf f}^{eq}_{j}+\Lambda{\bf f}^{eq}_{j+1}\right]-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Lambda^{2}\displaystyle\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\left[{\bf f}^{eq}_{j+1}-{\bf f}^{eq}_{j}\right]

(118)

For obtaining a TVD flux, we write the cell-interface flux as

{\bf h}^{TVD-KFDS}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}={\bf h}^{KFDS}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}+\phi\left[{\bf h}^{KLW}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-{\bf h}^{KFDS}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\right]

(119)

where
\phi=\phi(r)
is a limiter function, to be chosen appropriately. Introducing

\Lambda^{\pm}=\displaystyle\frac{\Lambda\pm|\Lambda|}{2}

(120)

we obtain

\Lambda^{+}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\lambda&0\\
0&0\end{array}\right],\ \Lambda^{-}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&0\\
0&-\lambda\end{array}\right]\ \textrm{and}\ |\Lambda|=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\lambda&0\\
0&\lambda\end{array}\right]=\Lambda^{+}-\Lambda^{-}

(121)

We now write

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\bf h}^{TVD-KFDS}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}={\bf h}^{KFDS}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-\phi({\bf r}^{+}_{j})\left[\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Lambda^{2}\displaystyle\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}-\Lambda^{+}\right)\left({\bf f}^{eq}_{j+1}-{\bf f}^{eq}_{j}\right)\right]\\[14.22636pt]
-\phi({\bf r}^{-}_{j+1})\left[\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Lambda^{2}\displaystyle\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}+\Lambda^{-}\right)\left({\bf f}^{eq}_{j+1}-{\bf f}^{eq}_{j}\right)\right]\end{array}

where

{\bf r}^{+}_{j}=\displaystyle\frac{{\bf f}^{eq}_{j}-{\bf f}^{eq}_{j-1}}{{\bf f}^{eq}_{j+1}-{\bf f}^{eq}_{j}}\ \textrm{and}\ {\bf r}^{-}_{j+1}=\displaystyle\frac{{\bf f}^{eq}_{j+2}-{\bf f}^{eq}_{j+1}}{{\bf f}^{eq}_{j+1}-{\bf f}^{eq}_{j}}

(125)

following
[
18
]
.
We choose the limiter function as the
minmod
function and define

\phi({\bf r})=minmod(1,{\bf r})

(126)

where

minmod(a,b)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}a\ \textrm{if}\ |a|<|b|\ \&\ ab>0\\
b\ \textrm{if}\ |b|<|a|\ \&\ ab>0\\
0\ \textrm{if}\ ab\leq 0\end{array}\right.

(127)

Therefore

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\bf h}^{TVD-KFDS}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}&=&{\bf h}^{KFDS}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\\[14.22636pt]
&&-minmod\left(1,\displaystyle\frac{{\bf f}^{eq}_{j}-{\bf f}^{eq}_{j-1}}{{\bf f}^{eq}_{j+1}-{\bf f}^{eq}_{j}}\right)\left[\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Lambda^{2}\displaystyle\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}-\Lambda^{+}\right)\left({\bf f}^{eq}_{j+1}-{\bf f}^{eq}_{j}\right)\right]\\[14.22636pt]
&&-minmod\left(1,\displaystyle\frac{{\bf f}^{eq}_{j+2}-{\bf f}^{eq}_{j+1}}{{\bf f}^{eq}_{j+1}-{\bf f}^{eq}_{j}}\right)\left[\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Lambda^{2}\displaystyle\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}+\Lambda^{-}\right)\left({\bf f}^{eq}_{j+1}-{\bf f}^{eq}_{j}\right)\right]\end{array}

Using the relation

c\ minmod(a,b)=minmod(ca,cb)

(128)

we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\bf h}^{TVD-KFDS}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}&=&{\bf h}^{KFDS}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\\[14.22636pt]
&&-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}minmod\left[\left(\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Lambda^{2}\displaystyle\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}-\Lambda^{+}\right)\left({\bf f}^{eq}_{j+1}-{\bf f}^{eq}_{j}\right),\ \left(\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Lambda^{2}\displaystyle\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}-\Lambda^{+}\right)\left({\bf f}^{eq}_{j}-{\bf f}^{eq}_{j-1}\right)\right]\\[14.22636pt]
&&-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}minmod\left[\left(\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Lambda^{2}\displaystyle\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}+\Lambda^{-}\right)\left({\bf f}^{eq}_{j+1}-{\bf f}^{eq}_{j}\right),\ \left(\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Lambda^{2}\displaystyle\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}+\Lambda^{-}\right)\left({\bf f}^{eq}_{j+2}-{\bf f}^{eq}_{j+1}\right)\right]\end{array}

Now taking moment of the above cell-interface flux and using the relations

{\bf P}\Lambda{\bf f}=g(u),\ {\bf P}\Lambda^{2}{\bf f}=\lambda^{2}u,\ {\bf P}\Lambda^{\pm}{\bf f}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}g(u)\pm\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\lambda u

(133)

we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ll}{g(u)_{j+\frac{1}{2}}}^{TVD-KFDS}&={g(u)_{j+\frac{1}{2}}}^{KFDS}\\[5.69054pt]
&-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}minmod\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\left\{\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\displaystyle\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\lambda^{2}\left(u_{j+1}-u_{j}\right)-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\lambda\left(u_{j+1}-u_{j}\right)-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(g(u)_{j+1}-g(u)_{j}\right)\right\},\\[5.69054pt]
\left\{\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\displaystyle\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\lambda^{2}\left(u_{j}-u_{j-1}\right)-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\lambda\left(u_{j}-u_{j-1}\right)-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(g(u)_{j}-g(u)_{j-1}\right)\right\}\end{array}\right]\\[14.22636pt]
&-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}minmod\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\left\{\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\displaystyle\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\lambda^{2}\left(u_{j+1}-u_{j}\right)-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\lambda\left(u_{j+1}-u_{j}\right)+\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(g(u)_{j+1}-g(u)_{j}\right)\right\},\\[5.69054pt]
\left\{\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\displaystyle\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\lambda^{2}\left(u_{j+2}-u_{j+1}\right)-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\lambda\left(u_{j+2}-u_{j+1}\right)+\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(g(u)_{j+2}-g(u)_{j+1}\right)\right\}\end{array}\right]\end{array}

In a similar way, starting with

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\bf h}^{TVD-KFDS}_{j-\frac{1}{2}}&=&{\bf h}^{KFDS}_{j-\frac{1}{2}}-\phi({\bf r}^{+}_{j-1})\left[\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Lambda^{2}\displaystyle\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}-\Lambda^{+}\right)\left({\bf f}^{eq}_{j}-{\bf f}^{eq}_{j-1}\right)\right]\\[14.22636pt]
&&-\phi({\bf r}^{-}_{j})\left[\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Lambda^{2}\displaystyle\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}+\Lambda^{-}\right)\left({\bf f}^{eq}_{j}-{\bf f}^{eq}_{j-1}\right)\right]\end{array}

where

{\bf r}^{-}_{j}=\displaystyle\frac{{\bf f}^{eq}_{j+1}-{\bf f}^{eq}_{j}}{{\bf f}^{eq}_{j}-{\bf f}^{eq}_{j-1}}\ \textrm{and}\ {\bf r}^{+}_{j-1}=\displaystyle\frac{{\bf f}^{eq}_{j-1}-{\bf f}^{eq}_{j-2}}{{\bf f}^{eq}_{j}-{\bf f}^{eq}_{j-1}}

(145)

we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ll}{g(u)_{j-\frac{1}{2}}}^{TVD-KFDS}&={g(u)_{j-\frac{1}{2}}}^{KFDS}\\[5.69054pt]

The two dimensional shallow water equations can be derived by following the procedures similar to that in one dimension.

6.2
Flexible velocity model for shallow water equations

Introducing two flexible velocities
\lambda^{+}
and
\lambda^{-}
to replace the continuous variation of the molecular velocity
v
and also two corresponding components of
\tilde{f}^{eq}_{+}
and
\tilde{f}^{eq}_{-}
, we write

\tilde{f}^{eq}={\left\{\tilde{f}^{eq}_{+}\delta(v-\lambda^{+})+\tilde{f}^{eq}_{-}\delta(v-\lambda^{-})\right\}}.

(221)

To recover the (vector of) conserved variables we multiply the scalar
\tilde{f}^{eq}
with
\psi
. Thus, the conserved variable vector becomes

U_{i}={\left[\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\psi}_{i}\tilde{f}^{eq}_{+}\delta(v-\lambda^{+})dv+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\psi}_{i}\tilde{f}^{eq}_{+}\delta(v-\lambda^{-})dv\right]}.

(222)

We combine the
\psi\tilde{f}^{eq}
to form
f^{eq}
. Therefore we get the conserved variable vector as

U_{i}={\left[\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f^{eq}_{+}\delta(v-\lambda^{+})dv+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f^{eq}_{+}\delta(v-\lambda^{-})dv\right]}_{i}.

(223)

Let us further assume, for simplicity, that the flexible velocities,
\lambda^{+}
and
\lambda^{-}
for each
i
are given by

\lambda^{+}_{i}=\lambda_{i}\ \textrm{ and }\ \lambda^{-}_{i}=-\lambda_{i}.

(224)

Thus, we have three unknowns, namely,
f^{eq}_{+}
,
f^{eq}_{-}
and
\lambda
for each
i
to be fixed for the equilibrium distribution.

\begin{array}[]{rcl}U_{i}&=&\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left\{f^{eq}_{+}\delta(v-\lambda^{+})+f^{eq}_{-}\delta(v-\lambda^{-})\right\}dv\\
&=&\left\{f^{eq}_{+}(1)+f^{eq}_{-}(1)\right\}_{i}.\end{array}

Thus

\left\{f^{eq}_{+}+f^{eq}_{-}\right\}_{i}=U_{i}.

(225)

\begin{array}[]{rcl}G(U)_{i}&=&\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}v{\psi}_{i}\tilde{f}^{eq}dv\\[8.53581pt]
&=&\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}v\left\{f^{eq}_{+}\delta(v-\lambda^{+})+f^{eq}_{-}\delta(v-\lambda^{-})\right\}_{i}dv\\[5.69054pt]
&=&\left\{f^{eq}_{+}\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}v\delta(v-\lambda^{+})dv+f^{eq}_{-}\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}v\delta(v-\lambda^{-})dv\right\}_{i}\\
&=&\left\{f^{eq}_{+}\lambda^{+}+f^{eq}_{-}\lambda^{-}\right\}_{i}\end{array}

Thus

\left\{f^{eq}_{+}\lambda^{+}+f^{eq}_{-}\lambda^{-}\right\}_{i}=G(U)_{i}

(226)

Solving the above two equations and simplifying of flexible velocities for each
i
using (
224
), we get

{f^{eq}_{+}}_{i}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}U_{i}+\displaystyle\frac{1}{2{\lambda}_{i}}G(U)_{i}\ \textrm{and}\ {f^{eq}_{-}}_{i}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}U_{i}-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2{\lambda}_{i}}G(U)_{i}

(227)

To recover the source terms we substitute the flexible velocities into the source term as explained in Annexure D.

\displaystyle\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\psi}_{i}\mathbb{F}\displaystyle\frac{\partial f}{\partial v}dv=-\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\mathbb{F}\tilde{f}_{eq}\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\psi}_{i}}{\partial v}}dv

(228)

\displaystyle\psi=1:\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\mathbb{F}\tilde{f}_{eq}\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\psi}_{i}}{\partial v}}dv=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\mathbb{F}\left\{\tilde{f}^{eq}_{+}\delta(v-\lambda^{+})+\tilde{f}^{eq}_{-}\delta(v-\lambda^{-})\right\}\displaystyle\frac{\partial(1)}{\partial v}}dv=0

(229)

\displaystyle\psi=v:\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\mathbb{F}\tilde{f}_{eq}\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\psi}_{i}}{\partial v}}dv=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\mathbb{F}\left\{\tilde{f}^{eq}_{+}\delta(v-\lambda^{+})+\tilde{f}^{eq}_{-}\delta(v-\lambda^{-})\right\}\displaystyle\frac{\partial(v)}{\partial v}}dv=\mathbb{F}h

(230)

Thus the moment strategy recovers the source terms as defined in [
213
]. Therefore, the
Flexible Velocity Boltzmann Equation
(FVBE) based on the two flexible velocity model can be written as

\left\{\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bf f}{\partial t}+\displaystyle\frac{\partial\left(\bf{\Lambda}\bf f\right)}{\partial x}+(i-1){\bf F}=-\displaystyle\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left[\bf f-\bf f^{eq}\right]\right\}_{i}\ \textrm{ with}\ \ i=1,2

(231)

where

\bf f_{i}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}f_{+}\\
f_{-}\end{array}\right]_{i},\ \bf F_{i}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbb{F}h\\
0\end{array}\right]_{i}\Lambda_{i}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\lambda^{+}&0\\
0&\lambda^{-}\end{array}\right]_{i}\ \textrm{and}\ \bf{f^{eq}}_{i}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}f^{eq}_{+}\\
f^{eq}_{-}\end{array}\right]_{i}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}U+\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\lambda}G(U)\\[11.38109pt]
\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}U-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\lambda}G(U)\end{array}\right]_{i}.

(232)

6.3
KFDS, KLW and TVD Schemes for Shallow Water Equations

We follow the same methodology used in the scalar framework to obtain the finite volume update formula for the shallow water system of equations. To handle the source terms in the momentum equation, we follow a well-balancing approach.

6.3.1
KFDS

The discrete framework for 1D Shallow water equation with operator split source terms can be obtained as follows.
Let us write a finite volume update formula for the 1D system of shallow water equations with source terms as (
231
)

{\bf P}{\left[\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bf f}{\partial t}+\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Lambda\bf f}{\partial x}+(i-1){\bf F}=0,\ {\bf f}={\bf f^{eq}}\right]}_{i}\ \ i=1,2.

(233)

The numerical scheme is evolved following the KFDS methodology.

{\left[\displaystyle\frac{\left({\bf Pf}\right)^{n+1}_{j}-\left({\bf Pf}\right)^{n}_{j}}{\Delta t}+\displaystyle\frac{\left({\bf P}\Lambda{\bf f}\right)^{n}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-\left({\bf P}\Lambda{\bf f}\right)^{n}_{j-\frac{1}{2}}}{\Delta x}-(i-1){\bf PF}_{j}=0,\ {\bf f}^{n}_{j}={\bf f^{eq,}}^{n}_{j}\right]}_{i}\ \ i=1,2.

To treat the source terms, we introduce well-balancing by evaluating the source term using the average of variables at the interface and substituting for
\mathbb{F}
. Thus we obtain

{\left[\displaystyle\frac{\left({\bf Pf}\right)^{n+1}_{j}-\left({\bf Pf}\right)^{n}_{j}}{\Delta t}+\displaystyle\frac{\left({\bf P}\Lambda{\bf f}\right)^{n}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-\left({\bf P}\Lambda{\bf f}\right)^{n}_{j-\frac{1}{2}}}{\Delta x}-(i-1)g\displaystyle\frac{\left(b_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-b_{j-\frac{1}{2}}\right)}{\Delta x}\displaystyle\frac{\left(h^{n}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}+h^{n}_{j-\frac{1}{2}}\right)}{2}=0,\ {\bf f}^{n}_{j}={\bf f^{eq,}}^{n}_{j}\right]}_{i}\ \ i=1,2.

The Final update formula thus obtained by following finite volume method based upwind flexible velocity Boltzmann scheme is given by

\left[{U}^{n+1}_{j}={{U}}^{n}_{j}-\displaystyle\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\left[{{G(U)}}^{n}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-{{G(U)}}^{n}_{j-\frac{1}{2}}\right]+\displaystyle\frac{(i-1)}{2}\displaystyle\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}(g)({h}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}+h_{j-\frac{1}{2}})({b_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-b_{j-\frac{1}{2}}})\right]_{i}

(234)

where the interface fluxes are given by

\displaystyle\left\{G(U)_{j+\frac{1}{2}}^{n}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}[G(U)_{j+1}^{n}+G(U)_{j}^{n}]-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}[\Delta G(U)^{+,n}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-\Delta G(U)^{-,n}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}]\right\}_{i}

(235)

\displaystyle\left\{G(U)_{j-\frac{1}{2}}^{n}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}[G(U)_{j}^{n}+G(U)_{j-1}^{n}]-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}[\Delta G(U)^{+,n}_{j-\frac{1}{2}}-\Delta G(U)^{-,n}_{j-\frac{1}{2}}]\right\}_{i}

(236)

\displaystyle\left\{\Delta G(U)_{j+\frac{1}{2}}^{\pm}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}[G(U)_{j+1}-G(U)_{j}]\pm\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}|\lambda|[U_{j+1}-U_{j}]\right\}_{i}

(237)

\displaystyle\left\{\Delta G(U)_{j-\frac{1}{2}}^{\pm}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}[G(U)_{j}-G(U)_{j-1}]\pm\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}|\lambda|[U_{j}-U_{j-1}]\right\}_{i}

(238)

6.4
Fixing
\lambda

Consistent with the scalar conservation laws, two different ways of fixing
\lambda
are proposed here. While the first way is based on Chapman-Enskog type asymptotic expansion of the FVBE, the second way is based on utilizing the Rankine-Hugoniot jump condition.

6.4.1
Chapman-Enskog type expansion of the flexible velocity Boltzmann equation

The methodology followed for Chapman-Enskog type expansion of the discrete velocity Boltzmann equation followed in Appendix C can be extended to the FVBE framework of shallow water equations. The resulting expression leads to the original convection equations (plus a source term), augmented by a diffusion term, given by

\left[\displaystyle\frac{\partial U}{\partial t}+\displaystyle\frac{\partial G(U)}{\partial x}+S(U)=\epsilon\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left[\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}\left\{\Lambda^{2}-\left(A\left(U\right)\right)^{2}\right\}\right]+\mathcal{O}\left(\epsilon^{2}\right)\right]

(239)

where
A(U)
is the flux Jacobian, whose eigenvalues form the wave speeds for the original system of convection equations, given by
A(U)=\displaystyle\frac{\partial G(U)}{\partial U}
. For the approximation to be stable, the diffusion term must be non-negative. Therefore, we obtain

\Lambda^{2}-\left(A\left(U\right)\right)^{2}\geq 0\ where\ \Lambda=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\lambda_{1}&0\\
0&\lambda_{2}\end{array}\right]

(240)

which can approximated to

\Lambda=\max\left[A(U)_{j}\right],\ \forall j

(241)

which is the maximum eigenvalue of the system. For the shallow water system of equations, the eigenvalues are given by
(u-a)
and
(u+a)
, where
a=\sqrt{gh}
is the celerity. Therefore the
\lambda
for the scheme is fixed as

\lambda_{i}=max(u_{L}-a_{L},u_{L}+a_{L},u_{R}-a_{R},u_{R}+a_{R}).

(242)

6.5
Fixing
\lambda
based on R-H (jump) condition

Here we fix
\lambda
based on the methodology followed in section [
3.5.2
]. For the shallow water system of equations we have

A(U)=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
{a}^{2}-{u}^{2}&2u\end{array}\right]

(243)

The eigenvalues of A(U) are
(u-a)
and
(u+a)
. Therefore, the wave speed for the scheme is assigned as

\Lambda=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}|u_{L}-a_{L}|&0\\
0&|u_{L}+a_{L}|\end{array}\right]

(244)

6.5.1
KLW

The discretisation of the source term part is inherently second order accurate. Therefore it is sufficient to derive a KLW scheme for the convection part of the system of equations. We obtain the finite volume update formula for the KLW scheme by following procedures as in section
4
.

{\bf P}\left[{\bf f}^{n+1}_{j}={\bf f}^{n}_{j}-\displaystyle\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\left[{\bf h}^{n}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-{\bf h}^{n}_{j-\frac{1}{2}}\right]-\Delta t(i-1){\bf F}_{j}\right]_{i}

(245)

or

\left[U^{n+1}_{j}=U^{n}_{j}-\displaystyle\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\left[G(U)^{n}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-G(U)^{n}_{j-\frac{1}{2}}\right]+\Delta tS(U)\right]_{i}

(246)

where

\left[G(U)_{j+\frac{1}{2}}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left[G(U)_{j}+G(U)_{j+1}\right]-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\lambda^{2}\displaystyle\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\left[U_{j+1}-U_{j}\right]\right]_{i}

(247)

\left[G(U)_{j-\frac{1}{2}}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left[G(U)_{j-1}+G(U)_{j}\right]-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\lambda^{2}\displaystyle\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\left[U_{j}-U_{j-1}\right]\right]_{i}

(248)

Again we rewrite the source term using the well-balancing approach. The final form of [
246
] will become

\left[U^{n+1}_{j}=U^{n}_{j}-\displaystyle\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\left[G(U)^{n}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-G(U)^{n}_{j-\frac{1}{2}}\right]+\displaystyle\frac{(i-1)}{2}\displaystyle\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}(g)({h}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}+h_{j-\frac{1}{2}})({b_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-b_{j-\frac{1}{2}}})\right]_{i}

(249)

where
G(U)_{j+\frac{1}{2}}
&
G(U)_{j-\frac{1}{2}}
are given by [
247
] & [
248
] respectively.

6.5.2
TVD-KFDS

The TVD scheme is derived by following the strategy introduced in [
185
]. The finite volume scheme for the TVD approach thus derived using the approaches defined in earlier section can be written as

\left[U^{n+1}_{j}=U^{n}_{j}-\displaystyle\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\left[G(U)_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-G(U)_{j-\frac{1}{2}}\right]+\displaystyle\frac{(i-1)}{2}\displaystyle\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}(g)({h}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}+h_{j-\frac{1}{2}})({b_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-b_{j-\frac{1}{2}}})\right]_{i}

(250)

where the expressions for
G(U)_{j+\frac{1}{2}}
and
G(U)_{j-\frac{1}{2}}
are given in the TVD form duly regulated by the minmod limiter based on the flux gradient.

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ll}{G_{i}(U)}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}^{TVD-KFDS}&={G_{i}(U)}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}^{KFDS}\\[5.69054pt]
&-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}minmod\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\left\{\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\displaystyle\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\lambda^{2}\left(U_{j+1}-U_{j}\right)-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\lambda\left(U_{j+1}-U_{j}\right)-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(G(U)_{j+1}-G(U)_{j}\right)\right\},\\[5.69054pt]
\left\{\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\displaystyle\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\lambda^{2}\left(U_{j}-U_{j-1}\right)-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\lambda\left(U_{j}-U_{j-1}\right)-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(G(U)_{j}-G(U)_{j-1}\right)\right\}\end{array}\right]_{i}\\[14.22636pt]
&-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}minmod\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\left\{\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\displaystyle\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\lambda^{2}\left(U_{j+1}-U_{j}\right)-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\lambda\left(U_{j+1}-U_{j}\right)+\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(G(U)_{j+1}-G(U)_{j}\right)\right\},\\[5.69054pt]
\left\{\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\displaystyle\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\lambda^{2}\left(U_{j+2}-U_{j+1}\right)-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\lambda\left(U_{j+2}-U_{j+1}\right)+\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(G(U)_{j+2}-G(U)_{j+1}\right)\right\}\end{array}\right]_{i}\end{array}

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ll}{G_{i}(U)}_{j-\frac{1}{2}}^{TVD-KFDS}&={G_{i}(U)}_{j-\frac{1}{2}}^{KFDS}\\[5.69054pt]
&-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}minmod\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\left\{\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\displaystyle\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\lambda^{2}\left(U_{j}-U_{j-1}\right)-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\lambda\left(U_{j}-U_{j-1}\right)-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(G(U)_{j}-G(U)_{j-1}\right)\right\},\\[5.69054pt]
\left\{\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\displaystyle\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\lambda^{2}\left(U_{j-1}-U_{j-2}\right)-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\lambda\left(U_{j-1}-U_{j-2}\right)-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(G(U)_{j-1}-G(U)_{j-2}\right)\right\}\end{array}\right]_{i}\\[14.22636pt]
&-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}minmod\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\left\{\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\displaystyle\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\lambda^{2}\left(U_{j}-U_{j-1}\right)-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\lambda\left(U_{j}-U_{j-1}\right)+\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(G(U)_{j}-G(U)_{j-1}\right)\right\},\\[5.69054pt]
\left\{\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\displaystyle\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\lambda^{2}\left(U_{j+1}-U_{j}\right)-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\lambda\left(U_{j+1}-U_{j}\right)+\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(G(U)_{j+1}-G(U)_{j}\right)\right\}\end{array}\right]_{i}\end{array}

7
KFDS, KLW and TVD Schemes in Two Dimensions

The KFDS, KLW and TVD schemes discussed presented in the previous sections for one dimensional scalar convection-diffusion equation can be extended to multi-dimensions using the finite volume method easily. Across a cell-interface in the finite volume method, the usual mid-point rule based quadrature leads to a locally one-dimensional approximation and hence the procedure presented in the previous sections simply gets extended. In this section, we consider the two dimensional Burgers equation given by

\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial g_{1}(u)}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial g_{2}(u)}{\partial y}=0

(267)

using the standard finite volume method. The terms of the inviscid and viscous fluxes in the above equation have similar definitions as in one dimension.
A four-velocity based FVBE for the above system can be derived as

\frac{\partial\mathbf{f}}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial\mathbf{{h}_{1}}}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial\mathbf{\bf{h}_{2}}}{\partial y}=-\displaystyle\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left[\mathbf{f}-\mathbf{f}^{eq}\right]

(268)

where
\bf{h}_{1}={\Lambda}_{1}f,\bf{h}_{2}={\Lambda}_{2}f
and the flexible velocity matrices are given by

{\Lambda}_{1}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}{-\lambda}&{0}&{0}&{0}\\
{0}&{\lambda}&{0}&{0}\\
{0}&{0}&{\lambda}&{0}\\
{0}&{0}&{0}&{-\lambda}\end{array}\right]\Lambda_{2}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}{-\lambda}&{0}&{0}&{0}\\
{0}&{-\lambda}&{0}&{0}\\
{0}&{0}&{\lambda}&{0}\\
{0}&{0}&{0}&{\lambda}\end{array}\right]

(269)

The equilibria are defined by

{\bf f}^{eq}=\left[\begin{array}[]{l}{f^{eq}_{1}}\\
{f^{eq}_{2}}\\
{f^{eq}_{3}}\\
{f^{eq}_{4}}\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}{\frac{1}{4}U-\frac{1}{4\lambda}G_{1}-\frac{1}{4\lambda}G_{2}}\\
{\frac{1}{4}U+\frac{1}{4\lambda}G_{1}-\frac{1}{4\lambda}G_{2}}\\
{\frac{1}{4}U+\frac{1}{4\lambda}G_{1}+\frac{1}{4\lambda}G_{2}}\\
{\frac{1}{4}U-\frac{1}{4\lambda}G_{1}+\frac{1}{4}\lambda}G_{2}\end{array}\right]

(270)

Let us denote
\mathbf{h}_{x}=\mathbf{\Lambda}_{1}\mathbf{f},\mathbf{h}_{y}=\mathbf{\Lambda}_{2}\mathbf{f}
as the fluxes along the
x-
and
y-
directions.
The cell-interface fluxes across a cell are constructed be normal to the interfaces and can be obtained as

\displaystyle\mathbf{h}_{L}=\left(\mathbf{h}_{x}\cos\theta+\mathbf{h}_{y}\sin\theta\right)_{L}

(271)

\displaystyle\mathbf{h}_{R}=\left(\mathbf{h}_{x}\cos\theta+\mathbf{h}_{y}\sin\theta\right)_{R}

(272)

where the suffix L and R represent the left and the right states of the cell-interface.
A finite volume update formula for the 2D Euler system can be derived as

\mathbf{f}_{j,k}^{n+1}=\mathbf{f}_{j,k}^{n}-\frac{\Delta t}{A_{j,k}}\sum_{I_{c}=1}^{4}\mathbf{h}_{nI_{c}}\Delta s_{I_{c}}

(273)

where
A_{j,k}
is the area of the cell centered at
(j,k)
and
\Delta s_{I_{c}}
is the length of the cell interface
I_{c}
.
Applying the
Kinetic Flux Difference Splitting
across a finite volume cell-interface, we get

\mathbf{h}_{n,I_{c}}=\frac{1}{2}\left[h_{R}^{n}+h_{L}^{n}\right]-\frac{1}{2}\left[\Delta h_{I,n}^{+}-\Delta h_{I,n}^{-}\right]

(274)

where

\Delta h_{I,n}^{\pm}=\left(\Lambda_{1}\cos\theta\right)^{\pm}\Delta\mathbf{f}_{I,n}^{eq}+\left(\Lambda_{2}\sin\theta\right)^{\pm}\Delta\mathbf{f}_{In}^{eq}

(275)

Upon taking moments of the above equations and extending the same techniques that we had applied in one dimensional system in determining the diffusion coefficients, we recover the macroscopic update formula as

\mathbf{U}_{j,k}^{n+1}=\mathbf{U}_{j,k}^{n}-\frac{\Delta t}{A_{j,k}}\sum_{I_{c}=1}^{4}\mathbf{G}_{n,I_{c}}\Delta s_{I_{c}}

(276)

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}_{n,I_{c}}=\frac{1}{2}\left[G(U)_{n,R}^{n}+G(U)_{n,L}^{n}\right]-\frac{1}{2}\left[\Delta G(U)_{I_{c}}^{+}-\Delta G(U)_{I_{c}}^{-}\right]

(277)

\displaystyle\Delta G(U)_{I_{c}}^{\pm}=\left.\frac{1}{2}\left[G(U)_{n,R}^{n}-G(U)_{n,L}^{n}\right]\right]\pm\frac{1}{2}|\lambda|\left[U_{n,R}-U_{n,L}\right]

(278)

Results and Discussion

First order accurate and second order accurate versions of both KFDS and KFDS+ schemes, together with KLW scheme, are tested on the following bench-mark test cases to test their capabilities in resolving the features like shock waves and expansion fans. Test case 1 is to chosen to test the scheme in the framework of a linear convection equation. Test case 2 is selected to study the behavior of the scheme in the framework of a linear convection-diffusion equation. Test cases 3 to 5 have been been formulated for inviscid Burgers equation and test cases 6 to 8 have been formulated for viscous Burgers equation. Test case 9 explores the ability of the numerical schemes in a system of equations such as shallow water equations with a source term. Test case 10 assesses the numerical scheme in two dimensions for linear convection equation whereas test cases 11 and 12 are chosen to check the numerical scheme in a 2D inviscid Burgers equation framework. Test cases 13 and 14 are chosen to evaluate the scheme for two dimensional viscous Burgers equation. Test case 15 is chosen to test the numerical scheme in a two dimensional shallow water system. These test cases will involve Riemann problems, which refer to the solution of nonlinear hyperbolic equations with initial discontinuities. These solutions involve shock wave and expansion wave solutions, the analytical solutions of which, obtained using method of characteristics (see
[
19
]
), are given here. For a non-linear convection equation as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}+\displaystyle\frac{\partial g(u)}{\partial x}=0,\ g(u)=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}u^{2}

(282)

with the initial condition (a discontinuity) given by

u(x,t=0)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}u_{L}\ \textrm{for}\ x<0\\
u_{R}\ \textrm{for}\ x\geq 0\end{array}\right.

(283)

the two possible solutions for this Riemann problem are as follows.

For
u_{L}>u_{R}
: (shock wave)

u(x,t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}u_{L}\ \textrm{for}\ x<st\\
u_{R}\ \textrm{for}\ x\geq st\end{array}\right.

(284)

where

s=\displaystyle\frac{u_{L}+u_{R}}{2}

(285)

is the shock speed.

For
u_{L}<u_{R}
: (expansion wave)

u(x,t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}u_{L}\ \ \ \ \textrm{for}\ x<a(u_{L})t\\[5.69054pt]
a^{-1}\left(\displaystyle\frac{x}{t}\right)\ \ \ \ \ \textrm{for}\ a(u_{L})t\leq x\leq a(u_{R})t\\[5.69054pt]
u_{R}\ \ \ \ \textrm{for}\ x>a(u_{R})t\end{array}\right.

(286)

The results with the first order KFDS and KFDS+ schemes, second order KLW scheme and TVD high resolution KFDS and KFDS+ schemes are presented for each of the following test cases.

Test case 2: Linear Convection-Diffusion Equation

Consider a one-dimensional linear convection equation with a viscous term as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}+\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}=Pe^{-1}\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x^{2}}

(296)

where Pe is the Peclet Number. In this relation, the inverse of Peclet number resembles the viscosity coefficient and therefore demonstrates the effect of Peclet number on the diffusion. The solution is to be evolved to attain steady state condtions for Peclet Numbers 1 and 50. The domain for the test case is
[0,1]
while boundary conditions are
u(0)=0
and
u(1)=1
. The exact solution for this test case is

{u}({x})=\frac{1-e^{xPe}}{1-e^{Pe}}

(297)

The results for this test case are given in figures (
6
) and (
7
). This test case mimics the boundary layer phenomena associated with the viscous flows of the fluids in one dimension. Peclet number is the ratio of the convection terms to the diffusion terms. Higher the Peclet number, greater will be domination of convection in the flow in comparison to diffusion. In this test case, at Peclet Number = 50, we see a large gradient near
x=1
. Each of the schemes captures the steep gradient with moderate accuracy. The accuracy increases significantly with an increase in the number of grid points in the gradient region. At Peclet number =1, the convection and diffusion terms are equally dominant and therefore results in a near linear transition of the flow without any steep gradients. Thus, the schemes demonstrate capability to work in both convection dominant and convection-diffusion dominant cases.

KFDS+

KFDS

\lambda

a(u)_{j}>0

a(u)_{j+1}<0

x_{j+\frac{1}{2}}

KFDS
and

KFDS+

Conclusions

A firm foundation for the flexible velocity Boltzmann equation is established by deriving it from the classical Boltzmann equation, using Dirac delta functions for the equilibrium distribution. Based on the so derived flexible velocity Boltzmann system, upwind schemes are introduced for solving the nonlinear convection equation. The flexible velocity also happens to be the coefficient of numerical diffusion in the finite volume framework and hence the freedom in fixing it is utilized in deriving an accurate shock capturing scheme in steady state, which is otherwise low in numerical diffusion. A generalized kinetic Lax-Wendroff method is introduced by applying the classical strategy to the flexible velocity Boltzmann framework. To get rid of the oscillations near shocks for the higher order accurate versions, total variation diminishing technique is introduced within the discrete kinetic framework, thus obtaining new and efficient kinetic schemes for solving the nonlinear convection-diffusion equations. Discrete Chapman-Enskog distributions are used in the relaxation step for obtaining schemes for the viscous cases. These new schemes are free of any Riemann solvers and enjoy a potential for further applications in convection dominated flows.
