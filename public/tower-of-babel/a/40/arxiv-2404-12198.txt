Mathematical analysis of a model-constrained inverse problem for the reconstruction of early states of prostate cancer growth


Abstract
The availability of cancer measurements over time enables the personalised assessment of tumour growth and therapeutic response dynamics. However, many tumours are treated after diagnosis without collecting longitudinal data, and cancer monitoring protocols may include infrequent measurements. To facilitate the estimation of disease dynamics and better guide ensuing clinical decisions, we investigate an inverse problem enabling the reconstruction of earlier tumour states by using a single spatial tumour dataset and a biomathematical model describing disease dynamics. We focus on prostate cancer, since aggressive cases of this disease are usually treated after diagnosis. We describe tumour dynamics with a phase-field model driven by a generic nutrient ruled by reaction-diffusion dynamics. The model is completed with another reaction-diffusion equation for the local production of prostate-specific antigen, which is a key prostate cancer biomarker. We first improve previous well-posedness results by further showing that the solution operator is continuously Fréchet differentiable. We then analyse the backward inverse problem concerning the reconstruction of earlier tumour states starting from measurements of the model variables at the final time. Since this problem is severely ill-posed, only very weak conditional stability of logarithmic type can be recovered from the terminal data. However, by restricting the unknowns to a compact subset of a finite-dimensional subspace, we can derive an optimal Lipschitz stability estimate. Key words: phase field; non-linear parabolic system; inverse problems; well-posedness; mathematical oncology; prostate cancer. AMS (MOS) Subject Classification: 35K51, 35K58 35R30, 35Q92, 92C50

In this work, we analyse a model-constrained inverse problem aiming to reconstruct earlier tumour configurations from a spatial measurement collected, for instance,
via

We focus on newly detected prostate cancer.
The diagnosis of these tumours relies on a multiparametric magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scan, which is often motivated by increasing values of the blood biomarker prostate-specific antigen (PSA; the most common biomarker in prostate cancer management) and guides the ensuing biopsy procedure to confirm the disease histopathologically

Thus, the accurate identification of the clinical risk of prostate cancer is a critical need of utmost importance to select an adequate clinical management strategy.
Beyond the diagnostic PSA, MRI, and biopsy data, estimating the recent dynamics of prostate cancer growth before diagnosis can further inform the treating physicians about the best course of management for each particular patient

.

Mathematically, the problem we want to address is a backward inverse problem for a nonlinear system of parabolic equations.
Such kind of problems arise any time a diffusion process has to be reversed thereby governing a multitude of applications
[
5
,
34
,
48
,
18
,
2
]
.
To be precise, we call
\mathcal{R}:(\varphi_{0},\sigma_{0},p_{0})\mapsto(\varphi(T),\sigma(T),p(T))
the nonlinear solution operator which associates to any initial data
(\varphi_{0},\sigma_{0},p_{0})
the value of the solution at the final time
(\varphi(T),\sigma(T),p(T))
.
Then, given some measurements
\varphi_{\text{meas}},\sigma_{\text{meas}},p_{\text{meas}}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
, we can formulate our inverse problem as that of finding initial data
\varphi_{0}
,
\sigma_{0}
,
p_{0}
such that the solution
(\varphi,\sigma,p)
to (
1.1
)–(
1.5
) satisfies
\varphi(T)=\varphi_{\text{meas}}
,
\sigma(T)=\sigma_{\text{meas}}
and
p(T)=p_{\text{meas}}
.
The backward inverse problem, even in the linear case, is well-known to be severely ill-posed with very weak conditional logarithmic stability from the data
[
47
,
39
,
33
]
.
Therefore, as a preliminary step, it is of paramount importance to regularise the problem, by establishing physically relevant a priori assumptions on the unknown initial data that lead to better, possibly Lipschitz, dependence of the initial data on the measurements.
In turn, such stability estimates give local convergence of iterative reconstruction methods
[
17
]
.
This strategy has been successfully employed in addressing other non-linear severely ill-posed problems, for example in the context of the conductivity inverse problem, see for example
[
3
,
9
,
6
]
.
The key idea is that, by restricting the unknowns to a compact subset of a finite-dimensional subspace, we can derive Lipschitz dependence, via a quantitative version of the inverse map theorem in Banach spaces
[
7
, Proposition 5]
.
To accomplish this, we need to prove the injectivity and continuity of the inverse map, as well as Fréchet differentiability with continuity of the forward map.
Indeed, even though the system is non-linear, we can adapt logarithmic convexity methods to prove injectivity and a conditional stability estimate for the inverse map for any positive time
t>0
(Proposition
3.1
). Additionally, through a more refined analysis, we are also able to recover a logarithmic stability estimate for the reconstruction of the initial data (Theorem
3.5
).
We just mention that the log-convexity method was already employed for example in
[
28
]
to derive stability estimates for linear parabolic partial differential equations and in
[
38
]
in the case of non-linear parabolic equations.
Carleman estimates for parabolic equations can be also employed to derive uniqueness and stability estimates for the backward inverse problem, see for instance
[
54
,
31
,
32
,
50
]
.
Then, to prove the additional regularity of
\mathcal{R}
required by
[
7
, Proposition 5]
, we show finer continuous dependence estimates on the solutions of the system (
1.1
)–(
1.5
), that allow us to further get that
\mathcal{R}
is
\mathcal{C}^{1}
(Theorem
2.5
).
As a consequence, we can derive the sought quantitative Lipschitz stability estimate (Theorem
3.10
), under the a priori assumption that the initial data lie in a compact subset of a finite-dimensional subspace.
In particular, our explicit computation of the stability constants confirms that, as expected, the inverse problem is severely ill-posed.
Indeed, such constants blow up exponentially with the dimension of the finite-dimensional subspace and doubly exponentially with the final time
T
.
However, such a Lipschitz stability estimate paves the way for the use of iterative locally convergent reconstruction algorithms, such as the Landweber iteration method
[
17
,
36
]
.
Due to the challenging nature of the ill-posed inverse problem, we plan to address an in-depth numerical study in a forthcoming paper, aiming to explore the possibility of an efficient reconstruction to be eventually used in clinical procedures.

The paper is organised as follows.
In Section

and then prove that the solution operator is continuously Fréchet differentiable.
Section

Here we prove our main stability results in Theorem

and Theorem

Section

\sigma,p\in L^{\infty}(Q_{T}),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\sigma(x,t)\geq 0,\,p(x,t)\geq 0\text{ for a.e. }(x,t)\in Q_{T},

with

\lVert\sigma\rVert_{L^{\infty}(Q_{T})}\leq C(\lVert\sigma_{0}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}+1),\hskip 10.00002pt\lVert p\rVert_{L^{\infty}(Q_{T})}\leq C(\lVert p_{0}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}+1).

Furthermore, the solution is continuous with respect to the initial data, that is, taking two solutions
(\varphi_{i},\sigma_{i},p_{i})
corresponding to
(\varphi_{0}^{i},\sigma_{0}^{i},p_{0}^{i})
,
i=1,2
, we have

\begin{split}&\lVert(\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2})(t)\rVert^{2}_{H}+\lVert(\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2})(t)\rVert^{2}_{H}+\lVert(p_{1}-p_{2})(t)\rVert^{2}_{H}\\
&\quad+\lVert\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(0,T;V_{0})}+\lVert\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(0,T;V)}+\lVert p_{1}-p_{2}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(0,T;V)}\\
&\qquad\leq C\left(\lVert\varphi_{0}^{1}-\varphi_{0}^{2}\rVert^{2}_{H}+\lVert\sigma_{0}^{1}-\sigma_{0}^{2}\rVert^{2}_{H}+\lVert p_{0}^{1}-p_{0}^{2}\rVert^{2}_{H}\right),\end{split}

(2.10)

for any
t\in[0,T]
. Finally, if
(\varphi_{0},\sigma_{0},p_{0})\in\mathbb{V}
, the solution enjoys the higher regularity
(\varphi,\sigma,p)\in\mathbb{X}_{0}\times\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{X}
, solves the system (
2.1
)–(
2.5
) almost everywhere and satisfies the estimate

\lVert\varphi\rVert^{2}_{\mathbb{X}_{0}}+\lVert\sigma\rVert^{2}_{\mathbb{X}}+\lVert p\rVert^{2}_{\mathbb{X}}\leq C\left(1+\lVert\varphi_{0}\rVert^{2}_{V_{0}}+\lVert\sigma_{0}\rVert^{2}_{V}+\lVert p_{0}\rVert^{2}_{V}\right).

(2.11)

Remark 2.2
.

Due to our model, hypotheses
A2
and
A3
are very specific on the non-linearities that we consider.
However, from an analytical point of view, one could consider more general expressions of
F
,
\mathbbm{h}
and
m
, as long as the results of Theorem
2.1
still hold.
Hence, the forthcoming analysis can easily be extended to a more general framework.

We additionally prove a stronger continuous dependence estimate, which will be useful when proving the Fréchet-differentiability of the solution operator.

Proposition 2.3
.

Assume hypotheses
A1
–
A3
and let
(\varphi_{0}^{i},\sigma_{0}^{i},p_{0}^{i})\in\mathbb{V}
,
i=1,2
, be two sets of initial data, satisfying also (
2.6
) and (
2.9
). Let
(\varphi_{i},\sigma_{i},p_{i})
,
i=1,2
, be the corresponding strong solutions of (
2.1
)–(
2.5
). Then, there exists a constant
C>0
, depending only on the parameters of the system and on
(\varphi_{0}^{i},\sigma_{0}^{i},p_{0}^{i})
,
i=1,2
, but not on their difference, such that

\begin{split}&\lVert(\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2})(t)\rVert^{2}_{V_{0}}+\lVert(\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2})(t)\rVert^{2}_{V}+\lVert(p_{1}-p_{2})(t)\rVert^{2}_{V}\\
&\quad+\lVert\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(0,T;W_{0})}+\lVert\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(0,T;W)}+\lVert p_{1}-p_{2}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(0,T;W)}\\
&\qquad\leq C\left(\lVert\varphi_{0}^{1}-\varphi_{0}^{2}\rVert^{2}_{V_{0}}+\lVert\sigma_{0}^{1}-\sigma_{0}^{2}\rVert^{2}_{V}+\lVert p_{0}^{1}-p_{0}^{2}\rVert^{2}_{V}\right),\quad\forall\,t\in[0,T].\end{split}

(2.12)

Proof.

Let us define the differences

\varphi:=\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2},\hskip 10.00002pt\sigma:=\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2},\hskip 10.00002ptp:=p_{1}-p_{2}.

Then, up to adding and subtracting some terms, they satisfy the following system of equations:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varphi-\lambda\Delta\varphi+(F^{\prime}(\varphi_{1})-F^{\prime}(\varphi_{2}))

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt-m(\sigma_{1})(\mathbbm{h}^{\prime}(\varphi_{1})-\mathbbm{h}^{\prime}(\varphi_{2}))-(m(\sigma_{1})-m(\sigma_{2}))\mathbbm{h}^{\prime}(\varphi_{2})=0

in
Q_{T}
,

(2.13)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\sigma-\eta\Delta\sigma+\gamma_{h}\sigma+\gamma_{ch}\sigma_{1}(\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2})+\gamma_{ch}(\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2})\varphi_{2}=S_{ch}\varphi\hskip 20.00003pt

in
Q_{T}
,

(2.14)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}p-D\Delta p+\gamma_{p}p=\alpha_{ch}\varphi

in
Q_{T}
,

(2.15)

\displaystyle\varphi=0,\hskip 10.00002pt\partial_{\boldsymbol{n}}\sigma=\partial_{\boldsymbol{n}}p=0

in
\Sigma_{T}
,

(2.16)

\displaystyle\varphi(0)=\varphi_{0}^{1}-\varphi_{0}^{2},\hskip 10.00002pt\sigma(0)=\sigma_{0}^{1}-\sigma_{0}^{2},\hskip 10.00002ptp(0)=p_{0}^{1}-p_{0}^{2}

in
\Omega
.

(2.17)

Note that, being the initial data in
\mathbb{V}
, by Theorem
2.1
the equalities above have to be intended in a strong sense. Therefore, we are allowed to test (
2.13
) by
-\Delta\varphi
, (
2.14
) by
-\Delta\sigma
, (
2.15
) by
-\Delta p
and sum them up, to obtain:

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\lVert\nabla\varphi\rVert^{2}_{H}+\lVert\nabla\sigma\rVert^{2}_{H}+\lVert\nabla p\rVert^{2}_{H}\right)+\lambda\lVert\Delta\varphi\rVert^{2}_{H}+\eta\lVert\Delta\sigma\rVert^{2}_{H}+D\lVert\Delta p\rVert^{2}_{H}\\
&\quad=-\gamma_{h}\lVert\nabla\sigma\rVert^{2}_{H}-\gamma_{p}\lVert\nabla p\rVert^{2}_{H}+(F^{\prime}(\varphi_{1})-F^{\prime}(\varphi_{2}),\Delta\varphi)_{H}\\
&\qquad-(m(\sigma_{1})(\mathbbm{h}^{\prime}(\varphi_{1})-\mathbbm{h}^{\prime}(\varphi_{2})),\Delta\varphi)_{H}-((m(\sigma_{1})-m(\sigma_{2}))\mathbbm{h}^{\prime}(\varphi_{2}),\Delta\varphi)_{H}\\
&\qquad+\gamma_{ch}(\sigma_{1}(\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2}),\Delta\sigma)_{H}+\gamma_{ch}((\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2})\varphi_{2},\Delta\sigma)_{H}\\
&\qquad-S_{ch}(\varphi,\Delta\sigma)_{H}-\alpha_{ch}(\varphi,\Delta p)_{H}.\end{split}

(2.18)

Then, we can easily estimate the terms on the right-hand side by using Cauchy-Schwarz and Young’s inequalities, together with the local Lipschitz continuity of
F^{\prime}
,
\mathbbm{h}^{\prime}
,
m
and the fact that
\varphi_{i},\sigma_{i}\in L^{\infty}(Q_{T})
for
i=1,2
. Indeed, we have that:

\displaystyle(F^{\prime}(\varphi_{1})-F^{\prime}(\varphi_{2}),\Delta\varphi)_{H}-(m(\sigma_{1})(\mathbbm{h}^{\prime}(\varphi_{1})-\mathbbm{h}^{\prime}(\varphi_{2})),\Delta\varphi)_{H}-((m(\sigma_{1})-m(\sigma_{2}))\mathbbm{h}^{\prime}(\varphi_{2}),\Delta\varphi)_{H}

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt+\gamma_{ch}(\sigma_{1}(\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2}),\Delta\sigma)_{H}+\gamma_{ch}((\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2})\varphi_{2},\Delta\sigma)_{H}-S_{ch}(\varphi,\Delta\sigma)_{H}-\alpha_{ch}(\varphi,\Delta p)_{H}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq\frac{\lambda}{2}\lVert\Delta\varphi\rVert^{2}_{H}+\frac{\eta}{2}\lVert\Delta\sigma\rVert^{2}_{H}+\frac{D}{2}\lVert\Delta p\rVert^{2}_{H}+C\lVert\varphi\rVert^{2}_{H}+C\lVert\sigma\rVert^{2}_{H}.

Hence, by also integrating on
(0,t)
, for any
t\in(0,T)
, we get that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\lVert\nabla\varphi(t)\rVert^{2}_{H}+\lVert\nabla\sigma(t)\rVert^{2}_{H}+\lVert\nabla p(t)\rVert^{2}_{H}\right)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\lVert\Delta\varphi\rVert^{2}_{H}\,\mathrm{d}s+\frac{\eta}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\lVert\Delta\sigma\rVert^{2}_{H}\,\mathrm{d}s+\frac{D}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\lVert\Delta p\rVert^{2}_{H}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\lVert\varphi_{0}^{1}-\varphi_{0}^{2}\rVert^{2}_{V_{0}}+\lVert\sigma_{0}^{1}-\sigma_{0}^{2}\rVert^{2}_{V}+\lVert p_{0}^{1}-p_{0}^{2}\rVert^{2}_{V}\right)+C\int_{0}^{T}\lVert\varphi\rVert^{2}_{V_{0}}\,\mathrm{d}s+C\int_{0}^{T}\lVert\sigma\rVert^{2}_{V}\,\mathrm{d}s,

which by Gronwall’s inequality and (
2.10
) implies (
2.12
). Proposition
2.3

is proved.
∎

Given the results of Theorem
2.1
and Proposition
2.3
, we introduce the set of admissible initial data as follows:

\mathcal{I}_{\text{ad}}=\left\{(\varphi_{0},\sigma_{0},p_{0})\in\mathbb{V}\mid 0\leq\varphi_{0}\leq 1,\,0\leq\sigma_{0}\leq\sigma_{\text{max}},\,0\leq p_{0}\leq p_{\text{max}}\right\},

(2.19)

where
\sigma_{\text{max}},p_{\text{max}}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
are given. Note that
\mathcal{I}_{\text{ad}}
is a closed and convex subset of
\mathbb{V}\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)^{3}
.
Next, we want to study in detail the operator
\mathcal{R}:\mathcal{I}_{\text{ad}}\to\mathbb{H}
which associates to any initial data
(\varphi_{0},\sigma_{0},p_{0})\in\mathcal{I}_{\text{ad}}
the corresponding solution
(\varphi(T),\sigma(T),p(T))
to (
2.1
)–(
2.5
), evaluated at the final time, that is

\mathcal{R}:\mathcal{I}_{\text{ad}}\to\mathbb{H},\hskip 10.00002pt\mathcal{R}((\varphi_{0},\sigma_{0},p_{0}))=(\varphi(T),\sigma(T),p(T)).

(2.20)

Note that
\mathcal{R}
is well-defined and Lipschitz-continuous by Theorem
2.1
.
We now want to prove that it is also continuously Fréchet-differentiable. To do this, we introduce the linearised system:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}Y-\lambda\Delta Y+F^{\prime\prime}(\overline{\varphi})Y-m(\overline{\sigma})\mathbbm{h}^{\prime\prime}(\overline{\varphi})Y-m^{\prime}(\overline{\sigma})\mathbbm{h}^{\prime}(\overline{\varphi})Z=0\hskip 20.00003pt

in
Q_{T}
,

(2.21)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}Z-\eta\Delta Z+\gamma_{h}Z+\gamma_{ch}\overline{\sigma}Y+\gamma_{ch}\overline{\varphi}Z-S_{ch}Y=0

in
Q_{T}
,

(2.22)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}P-D\Delta P+\gamma_{p}P=\alpha_{ch}Y

in
Q_{T}
,

(2.23)

\displaystyle Y=0,\hskip 10.00002pt\partial_{\boldsymbol{n}}Z=\partial_{\boldsymbol{n}}P=0

on
\Sigma_{T}
,

(2.24)

\displaystyle Y(0)=h,\hskip 10.00002ptZ(0)=k,\hskip 10.00002ptP(0)=w

in
\Omega
,

(2.25)

where
h,k,w\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. This system is deduced by fixing an initial datum
(\overline{\varphi}_{0},\overline{\sigma}_{0},\overline{p}_{0})
, as well as its corresponding solution
(\overline{\varphi},\overline{\sigma},\overline{p})
, by linearising near it, i.e. by considering

\varphi=\overline{\varphi}+Y,\,\sigma=\overline{\sigma}+Z,\,p=\overline{p}+P,\,\varphi_{0}=\overline{\varphi}_{0}+h,\,\sigma_{0}=\overline{\sigma}_{0}+k,\,p_{0}=\overline{p}_{0}+w,

and by recovering the system solved by
(Y,Z,P)
, while approximating the non-linearities at the first-order of their Taylor expansion. We have the following result about the well-posedness of (
2.21
)–(
2.25
).

Proposition 2.4
.

Assume hypotheses
A1
–
A3
and let
(h,k,w)\in\mathbb{H}
. Let
(\overline{\varphi}_{0},\overline{\sigma}_{0},\overline{p}_{0})\in\mathcal{I}_{\text{ad}}
. Then, (
2.21
)–(
2.25
) admits a unique weak solution
(Y,Z,P)\in\mathbb{Y}_{0}\times\mathbb{Y}\times\mathbb{Y}
, which solves the system in variational formulation and satisfies the estimate:

\lVert Y\rVert^{2}_{\mathbb{Y}_{0}}+\lVert Z\rVert^{2}_{\mathbb{Y}}+\lVert P\rVert^{2}_{\mathbb{Y}}\leq C\left(\lVert h\rVert^{2}_{H}+\lVert k\rVert^{2}_{H}+\lVert w\rVert^{2}_{H}\right).

Moreover, if
(h,k,w)\in\mathbb{V}
, the solution enjoys higher regularity
(Y,Z,P)\in\mathbb{X}_{0}\times\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{X}
, solves the system almost everywhere and satisfies the estimate:

\lVert Y\rVert^{2}_{\mathbb{X}_{0}}+\lVert Z\rVert^{2}_{\mathbb{X}}+\lVert P\rVert^{2}_{\mathbb{X}}\leq C\left(\lVert h\rVert^{2}_{V_{0}}+\lVert k\rVert^{2}_{V}+\lVert w\rVert^{2}_{V}\right).

Proof.

Note that system (
2.21
)–(
2.25
) is composed of three
linear
parabolic equations without sources and with coefficients in
L^{\infty}(Q_{T})
, thanks to Theorem
2.1
. Then, the statements of Proposition
2.4
follow by standard results from the theory of parabolic equations.
∎

The linearised system (
2.21
)–(
2.25
) is an
ansatz
for the explicit expression of the Fréchet-derivative of the operator
\mathcal{R}
. Indeed, we are now in position to show that

\mathrm{D}\mathcal{R}(\overline{\varphi}_{0},\overline{\sigma}_{0},\overline{p}_{0})[(h,k,w)]=(Y(T),Z(T),P(T)).

(2.26)

Let
\mathcal{I}_{R}
be an open subset of
\mathbb{V}\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)^{3}
such that
\mathcal{I}_{\text{ad}}\subseteq\mathcal{I}_{R}
. Note that, in
\mathcal{I}_{R}
, the continuous dependence estimate (
2.12
) holds with
C
depending only on
R
and the fixed data of the system.
Our aim is to show that
\mathcal{R}:\mathcal{I}_{R}\to\mathbb{H}
is continuously Fréchet-differentiable.
Indeed, we can prove:

Theorem 2.5
.

Assume hypothesis
A1
–
A3
. Then
\mathcal{R}:\mathcal{I}_{R}\to\mathbb{H}
is Fréchet-differentiable, i.e. for any
(\overline{\varphi}_{0},\overline{\sigma}_{0},\overline{p}_{0})\in\mathcal{I}_{R}
there exists a unique Fréchet-derivative
\mathrm{D}\mathcal{R}((\overline{\varphi}_{0},\overline{\sigma}_{0},\overline{p}_{0}))\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{V}\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)^{3},\mathbb{H})
such that, as
\lVert(h,k,w)\rVert_{\mathbb{V}}\to 0
,

\frac{\lVert\mathcal{R}((\overline{\varphi}_{0}+h,\overline{\sigma}_{0}+k,\overline{p}_{0}+w))-\mathcal{R}((\overline{\varphi}_{0},\overline{\sigma}_{0},\overline{p}_{0}))-\mathrm{D}\mathcal{R}((\overline{\varphi}_{0},\overline{\sigma}_{0},\overline{p}_{0}))[(h,k,w)]\rVert_{\mathbb{H}}}{\lVert(h,k,w)\rVert_{\mathbb{V}}}\to 0.

(2.27)

Moreover, for any
(h,k,w)\in\mathbb{V}\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)^{3}
, the Fréchet-derivative at
(\overline{\varphi}_{0},\overline{\sigma}_{0},\overline{p}_{0})
in
(h,k,w)
is defined as in (
2.26
), where
(Y(T),Z(T),P(T))
is the solution to the linearised system (
2.21
)–(
2.25
) with initial data
(h,k,w)
, evaluated at the final time.

Additionally, the Fréchet-derivative
\mathrm{D}\mathcal{R}
is Lipschitz-continuous as a function from
\mathcal{I}_{R}
to the space
\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{V}\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)^{3},\mathbb{H})
. More precisely, the following estimate holds:

\lVert\mathrm{D}\mathcal{R}((\overline{\varphi}_{0}^{1},\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{1},\overline{p}_{0}^{1}))-\mathrm{D}\mathcal{R}((\overline{\varphi}_{0}^{2},\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{2},\overline{p}_{0}^{2}))\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{V}\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)^{3},\mathbb{H})}\leq C_{0}\lVert(\overline{\varphi}_{0}^{1},\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{1},\overline{p}_{0}^{1})-(\overline{\varphi}_{0}^{2},\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{2},\overline{p}_{0}^{2})\rVert^{2}_{\mathbb{H}},

(2.28)

for some constant
C_{0}>0
depending only on the parameters of the system.

Proof.

We observe that it is sufficient to prove the result for any small enough perturbation
(h,k,w)
, i.e., we fix
\Lambda>0
and consider only perturbations such that

\lVert(h,k,w)\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)^{3}}\leq\Lambda.

(2.29)

Now, we fix
(\overline{\varphi}_{0},\overline{\sigma}_{0},\overline{p}_{0})
and
(h,k,w)
as above and consider

\displaystyle(\varphi,\sigma,p)\text{ as the solution to \eqref{eq:phi}--\eqref{ic} with data }(\overline{\varphi}_{0}+h,\overline{\sigma}_{0}+k,\overline{p}_{0}+w),

\displaystyle(\overline{\varphi},\overline{\sigma},\overline{p})\text{ as the solution to \eqref{eq:phi}--\eqref{ic} with data }(\overline{\varphi}_{0},\overline{\sigma}_{0},\overline{p}_{0}),

\displaystyle(Y,Z,P)\text{ as the solution to \eqref{eq:philin}--\eqref{iclin} with data }(h,k,w).

For the Fréchet-differentiability, it is enough to show that there exist a constant
C>0
, depending only on the parameters of the system and possibly on
\Lambda
, and an exponent
s>2
such that

\lVert(\varphi(T),\sigma(T),p(T))-(\overline{\varphi}(T),\overline{\sigma}(T),\overline{p}(T))-(Y(T),Z(T),P(T))\rVert^{2}_{\mathbb{H}}\leq C\lVert(h,k,w)\rVert^{s}_{\mathbb{H}}.

To do this, we introduce the additional variables

\displaystyle\psi:=\varphi-\overline{\varphi}-Y\in\mathbb{X}_{0},

\displaystyle\theta:=\sigma-\overline{\sigma}-Z\in\mathbb{X},

\displaystyle\zeta:=p-\overline{p}-P\in\mathbb{X},

which, by Theorem
2.1
and Proposition
2.4
, enjoy the regularities shown above. Then, this is equivalent to showing that

\lVert(\psi(T),\theta(T),\zeta(T))\rVert_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\leq C\lVert(h,k,w)\rVert^{s}_{\mathbb{H}}.

(2.30)

By inserting the equations solved by the variables in the definitions of
\psi
,
\theta
and
\zeta
and exploiting the linearity of the involved differential operators, we infer that
\psi
,
\theta
and
\zeta
satisfy:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\psi-\lambda\Delta\psi+F^{h}=M^{h}

\displaystyle\text{in }Q_{T},

(2.31)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\theta-\eta\Delta\theta+\gamma_{h}\theta=Q^{h}+S_{ch}\psi\hskip 20.00003pt

\displaystyle\text{in }Q_{T},

(2.32)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\zeta-D\Delta\zeta+\gamma_{p}\zeta=\alpha_{ch}\psi

\displaystyle\text{in }Q_{T},

(2.33)

together with boundary and initial conditions:

\displaystyle\psi=0,\hskip 10.00002pt\partial_{\boldsymbol{n}}\theta=\partial_{\boldsymbol{n}}\zeta=0\hskip 20.00003pt

\displaystyle\text{on }\Sigma_{T},

(2.34)

\displaystyle\psi(0)=0,\hskip 10.00002pt\theta(0)=0,\hskip 10.00002pt\zeta(0)=0\hskip 20.00003pt

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega,

(2.35)

where

\displaystyle F^{h}

\displaystyle=F^{\prime}(\varphi)-F^{\prime}(\overline{\varphi})-F^{\prime\prime}(\overline{\varphi})Y,

\displaystyle M^{h}

\displaystyle=m(\sigma)\mathbbm{h}^{\prime}(\varphi)-m(\overline{\sigma})\mathbbm{h}^{\prime}(\overline{\varphi})-m(\overline{\sigma})\mathbbm{h}^{\prime\prime}(\overline{\varphi})Y-m^{\prime}(\overline{\sigma})\mathbbm{h}^{\prime}(\overline{\varphi})Z,

\displaystyle Q^{h}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{ch}\sigma\varphi-\gamma_{ch}\overline{\sigma}\overline{\varphi}-\gamma_{ch}\overline{\sigma}Y-\gamma_{ch}\overline{\varphi}Z.

Before going on, we can rewrite in a better way the terms
F^{h}
,
M^{h}
and
Q^{h}
, by using the following version of Taylor’s theorem with integral remainder for a real function
f\in\mathcal{C}^{2}
at a point
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
:

f(x)=f(x_{0})+f^{\prime}(x_{0})(x-x_{0})+\left(\int_{0}^{1}(1-z)f^{\prime\prime}(x_{0}+z(x-x_{0}))\,\mathrm{d}z\right)(x-x_{0})^{2}.

Indeed, with straightforward calculations, one can see that

\displaystyle F^{h}=F^{\prime\prime}(\overline{\varphi})\psi+R_{1}^{h}(\varphi-\overline{\varphi})^{2},

and also, up to adding and subtracting some additional terms, that

\displaystyle M^{h}

\displaystyle=(m(\sigma)-m(\overline{\sigma}))(\mathbbm{h}^{\prime}(\varphi)-\mathbbm{h}^{\prime}(\overline{\varphi}))+m(\overline{\sigma})[\mathbbm{h}^{\prime\prime}(\overline{\varphi})\psi+R_{2}^{h}(\varphi-\overline{\varphi})^{2}]

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+\mathbbm{h}^{\prime}(\varphi)[m^{\prime}(\overline{\sigma})\theta+R_{3}^{h}(\sigma-\overline{\sigma})^{2}],

\displaystyle Q^{h}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{ch}(\sigma-\overline{\sigma})(\varphi-\overline{\varphi})+\gamma_{ch}\overline{\sigma}\psi+\gamma_{ch}\overline{\varphi}\theta,

where

\displaystyle R_{1}^{h}=\int_{0}^{1}(1-z)F^{\prime\prime\prime}(\overline{\varphi}+z(\varphi-\overline{\varphi}))\,\mathrm{d}z,\hskip 10.00002ptR_{2}^{h}=\int_{0}^{1}(1-z)\mathbbm{h}^{\prime\prime\prime}(\overline{\varphi}+z(\varphi-\overline{\varphi}))\,\mathrm{d}z,

\displaystyle R_{3}^{h}=\int_{0}^{1}(1-z)m^{\prime\prime}(\overline{\sigma}+z(\sigma-\overline{\sigma}))\,\mathrm{d}z.

By using global boundedness of the variables, given by Theorem
2.1
, one can easily see that there exists a constant
C>0
, depending only on the parameters of the system and on
\Lambda
, such that

\lVert R_{1}^{h}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(Q_{T})},\lVert R_{2}^{h}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(Q_{T})},\lVert R_{3}^{h}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(Q_{T})}\leq C.

(2.36)

In order to show (
2.30
), we now proceed with a priori estimates on the system (
2.31
)–(
2.35
). Indeed, we test (
2.31
) by
\psi
, (
2.32
) by
\theta
, (
2.33
) by
\zeta
and sum them up to obtain:

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\lVert\varphi\rVert^{2}_{H}+\lVert\sigma\rVert^{2}_{H}+\lVert p\rVert^{2}_{H}\right)+\lambda\lVert\nabla\varphi\rVert^{2}_{H}+\eta\lVert\nabla\sigma\rVert^{2}_{H}+D\lVert\nabla p\rVert^{2}_{H}+\gamma_{h}\lVert\theta\rVert^{2}_{H}+\gamma_{p}\lVert\zeta\rVert^{2}_{H}\\
&\quad=-(F^{h},\psi)_{H}+(M^{h},\psi)_{H}+(Q^{h},\theta)_{H}+S_{ch}(\psi,\theta)_{H}+\alpha_{ch}(\psi,\zeta)_{H}.\end{split}

(2.37)

The last two terms on the right-hand side of (
2.37
) can easily be treated by means of Cauchy-Schwarz and Young’s inequalities, therefore we focus on estimating the first three. Indeed, by using again Hölder and Young’s inequalities, together with (
2.36
), the global boundedness of
(\overline{\varphi},\overline{\sigma},\overline{p})
given by Theorem
2.1
, the regularity and local Lipschitz continuity of
F
,
m
and
\mathbbm{h}
and the Sobolev embedding
V\hookrightarrow L^{4}(\Omega)
, we infer that

\displaystyle(F^{h},\psi)_{H}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}F^{\prime\prime}(\overline{\varphi})\psi^{2}+R_{1}^{h}(\varphi-\overline{\varphi})^{2}\psi\,\mathrm{d}x\leq C\lVert\psi\rVert^{2}_{H}+C\lVert\varphi-\overline{\varphi}\rVert^{4}_{L^{4}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C\lVert\psi\rVert^{2}_{H}+C\lVert\varphi-\overline{\varphi}\rVert^{4}_{V_{0}},

\displaystyle(M^{h},\psi)_{H}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}(m(\sigma)-m(\overline{\sigma}))(\mathbbm{h}^{\prime}(\varphi)-\mathbbm{h}^{\prime}(\overline{\varphi}))\psi\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}m(\overline{\sigma})[\mathbbm{h}^{\prime\prime}(\overline{\varphi})\psi+R_{2}^{h}(\varphi-\overline{\varphi})^{2}]\psi\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+\int_{\Omega}\mathbbm{h}^{\prime}(\varphi)[m^{\prime}(\overline{\sigma})\theta+R_{3}^{h}(\sigma-\overline{\sigma})^{2}]\psi\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq C\lVert\psi\rVert^{2}_{H}+C\lVert\theta\rVert^{2}_{H}+C\lVert\varphi-\overline{\varphi}\rVert^{4}_{L^{4}(\Omega)}+C\lVert\sigma-\overline{\sigma}\rVert^{4}_{L^{4}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C\lVert\psi\rVert^{2}_{H}+C\lVert\theta\rVert^{2}_{H}+C\lVert\varphi-\overline{\varphi}\rVert^{4}_{V_{0}}+C\lVert\sigma-\overline{\sigma}\rVert^{4}_{V},

\displaystyle(Q^{h},\theta)_{H}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{ch}\int_{\Omega}(\sigma-\overline{\sigma})(\varphi-\overline{\varphi})\theta+\overline{\sigma}\psi\theta+\overline{\varphi}\theta^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq C\lVert\theta\rVert^{2}_{H}+C\lVert\psi\rVert^{2}_{H}+C\lVert\sigma-\overline{\sigma}\rVert^{2}_{L^{4}(\Omega)}\lVert\varphi-\overline{\varphi}\rVert^{2}_{L^{4}(\Omega)}

3
Analysis of the inverse problem

We devote this section to the analysis of the inverse problem of reconstructing the initial data, given a measurement at the final time.
The first step would be to prove a backward uniqueness result for the system (
2.1
)–(
2.5
), namely to show that if
(\varphi_{1},\sigma_{1},p_{1})
and
(\varphi_{1},\sigma_{1},p_{1})
are two solutions such that
(\varphi_{1},\sigma_{1},p_{1})(T)=(\varphi_{2},\sigma_{2},p_{2})(T)
, then
(\varphi_{1},\sigma_{1},p_{1})(t)=(\varphi_{2},\sigma_{2},p_{2})(t)
for any
t\in[0,T]
. In particular, this would imply that the map
\mathcal{R}
is injective i.e. uniqueness for the inverse problem in
\mathcal{I}_{\text{ad}}
.
However, following a logarithmic convexity approach, we prove a conditional stability result which consequently implies uniqueness as a byproduct. Namely, assuming the initial data to belong to
\mathcal{I}_{\text{ad}}
, we prove an Hölder stability estimate for
\lVert(\varphi_{1}(t),\sigma_{1}(t),p_{1}(t))-(\varphi_{2}(t),\sigma_{2}(t),p_{2}(t)\rVert_{\mathbb{H}}
for any
t>0
. To establish this, we exploit the global bounds on the solutions of (
2.1
)–(
2.5
), obtained in Theorem
2.1
. In particular, in the sequel we will denote by
M>0
the minimal constant such that

\lVert(\varphi,\sigma,p)\rVert_{\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,T];\mathbb{H})}\leq M,

(3.1)

uniformly for
(\varphi_{0},\sigma_{0},p_{0})\in\mathcal{I}_{\text{ad}}
.
To prove our conditional stability result, we follow
[
33
, Theorem 3.1.3]
, which is actually a simpler version of the results contained in
[
1
]
. Indeed, we can prove:

Proposition 3.1
.

Assume hypotheses
A1
–
A3
. Let
(\varphi_{1},\sigma_{1},p_{1})
and
(\varphi_{2},\sigma_{2},p_{2})
be two solutions of (
2.1
)–(
2.5
) corresponding to two triples of initial data
(\varphi_{0}^{i},\sigma_{0}^{i},p_{0}^{i})\in\mathcal{I}_{\text{ad}}
for
i=1,2
.

Then, there exists a constant
C_{1}>0
, depending only on the parameters of the system, such that for any
t\in[0,T]
the following estimate holds:

\begin{split}&\lVert(\varphi_{1}(t),\sigma_{1}(t),p_{1}(t))-(\varphi_{2}(t),\sigma_{2}(t),p_{2}(t))\rVert_{\mathbb{H}}\\
&\quad\leq C_{1}M^{1-\lambda(t)}\lVert(\varphi_{1}(T),\sigma_{1}(T),p_{1}(T))-(\varphi_{2}(T),\sigma_{2}(T),p_{2}(T))\rVert_{\mathbb{H}}^{\lambda(t)}.\end{split}

(3.2)

where
\lambda=\lambda(t)\geq 0
can be either

\lambda=\lambda_{1}(t)=\frac{1-e^{-\gamma t}}{1-e^{-\gamma T}}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{or}\hskip 10.00002pt\lambda=\lambda_{2}(t)=\frac{e^{\gamma t}-1}{e^{\gamma T}-1},

(3.3)

for any
t\in[0,T]
, with
\gamma>0
depending only on the fixed parameters of the system.

Proof.

We call
\varphi=\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2}
,
\sigma=\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2}
,
p=p_{1}-p_{2}
and observe that they satisfy the system:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varphi-\lambda\Delta\varphi=f_{\varphi}

in
Q_{T}
,

(3.4)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\sigma-\eta\Delta\sigma=f_{\sigma}

in
Q_{T}
,

(3.5)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}p-D\Delta p=f_{p}

in
Q_{T}
,

(3.6)

\displaystyle\varphi=0,\hskip 10.00002pt\partial_{\boldsymbol{n}}\sigma=\partial_{\boldsymbol{n}}p=0

in
\Sigma_{T}
,

(3.7)

\displaystyle\varphi(0)=\varphi_{0}^{1}-\varphi_{0}^{2},\hskip 10.00002pt\sigma(0)=\sigma_{0}^{1}-\sigma_{0}^{2},\hskip 10.00002ptp(0)=p_{0}^{1}-p_{0}^{2}\hskip 20.00003pt

in
\Omega
,

(3.8)

where

\displaystyle f_{\varphi}:=-(F^{\prime}(\varphi_{1})-F^{\prime}(\varphi_{2}))+m(\sigma_{1})(\mathbbm{h}^{\prime}(\varphi_{1})-\mathbbm{h}^{\prime}(\varphi_{2}))+(m(\sigma_{1})-m(\sigma_{2}))\mathbbm{h}^{\prime}(\varphi_{2}),

\displaystyle f_{\sigma}:=-\gamma_{h}\sigma-\gamma_{ch}\sigma_{1}(\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2})-\gamma_{ch}(\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2})\varphi_{2}+S_{ch}\varphi,

\displaystyle f_{p}:=-\gamma_{p}p+\alpha_{ch}\varphi.

We start by estimating the terms on the right-hand side. Indeed, by using the fact that
\varphi_{i},\sigma_{i}
,
i=1,2
, are uniformly bounded in
L^{\infty}(Q_{T})
and
F,\mathbbm{h}\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
,
m\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and thus locally Lipschitz, we can infer that

\displaystyle\lVert f_{\varphi}\rVert_{H}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert F^{\prime}(\varphi_{1})-F^{\prime}(\varphi_{2})\rVert_{H}+\lVert m(\sigma_{1})\rVert_{\infty}\lVert\mathbbm{h}^{\prime}(\varphi_{1})-\mathbbm{h}^{\prime}(\varphi_{2})\rVert_{H}+\lVert\mathbbm{h}^{\prime}(\varphi_{2})\rVert_{\infty}\lVert m(\sigma_{1})-m(\sigma_{2})\rVert_{H}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\lVert\varphi\rVert_{H}+\lVert\sigma\rVert_{H}\right).

Similarly, we can also deduce that

\displaystyle\lVert f_{\sigma}\rVert_{H}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma_{h}\lVert\sigma\rVert_{H}+\gamma_{ch}\lVert\sigma_{1}\rVert_{\infty}\lVert\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2}\rVert_{H}+\gamma_{ch}\lVert\varphi_{2}\rVert_{\infty}\lVert\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2}\rVert_{H}+S_{ch}\lVert\varphi\rVert_{H}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\lVert\sigma\rVert_{H}+\lVert\varphi\rVert_{H}\right),

and that

\lVert f_{p}\rVert_{H}\leq C\left(\lVert p\rVert_{H}+\lVert\varphi\rVert_{H}\right).

Therefore, in the end, we showed that

\lVert f_{\varphi}\rVert_{H}+\lVert f_{\sigma}\rVert_{H}+\lVert f_{p}\rVert_{H}\leq C\left(\lVert\varphi\rVert_{H}+\lVert\sigma\rVert_{H}+\lVert p\rVert_{H}\right),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for a.e. }t\in[0,T].

(3.9)

To simplify notations, we now introduce a vector-valued formulation by defining:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\uppsi}:=(\varphi,\sigma,p),\hskip 10.00002pt\nabla\boldsymbol{\uppsi}:=(\nabla\varphi,\nabla\sigma,\nabla p),

\displaystyle\Delta\boldsymbol{\uppsi}:=(\Delta\varphi,\Delta\sigma,\Delta p),\hskip 10.00002ptf_{\boldsymbol{\uppsi}}:=(f_{\varphi},f_{\sigma},f_{p}),\hskip 10.00002pt\boldsymbol{D}:=\diag(\lambda,\eta,D).

Then, the previous estimate (
3.9
) now becomes

\lVert f_{\boldsymbol{\uppsi}}\rVert_{\mathbb{H}}\leq C\lVert\boldsymbol{\uppsi}\rVert_{\mathbb{H}}.

(3.10)

Thus, we have shown that the system solved by
(\varphi,\sigma,p)
has the form

\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\uppsi}-\boldsymbol{D}\Delta\boldsymbol{\uppsi}=f_{\boldsymbol{\uppsi}},\hbox{ with $\lVert f_{\boldsymbol{\uppsi}}\rVert_{\mathbb{H}}\leq C\lVert\boldsymbol{\uppsi}\rVert_{\mathbb{H}}$},

where

depends only on the parameters of the system and on
\lVert\varphi_{0}^{i}\rVert_{\infty},\lVert\sigma_{0}^{i}\rVert_{\infty}
,
i=1,2
.
Next, recall that
\boldsymbol{\uppsi}\in\mathbb{X}_{0}\times\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{X}

3.4
) by
\varphi
and

, (
3.5
) by
\sigma
and

, (
3.6

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\lVert\boldsymbol{\uppsi}\rVert^{2}_{\mathbb{H}}+\lVert\boldsymbol{D}\nabla\boldsymbol{\uppsi}\rVert^{2}_{\mathbb{H}}=(f_{\boldsymbol{\uppsi}},\boldsymbol{\uppsi})_{\mathbb{H}},

(3.11)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\lVert\nabla\boldsymbol{\uppsi}\rVert^{2}_{\mathbb{H}}+\lVert\boldsymbol{D}\Delta\boldsymbol{\uppsi}\rVert^{2}_{\mathbb{H}}=(f_{\boldsymbol{\uppsi}},-\Delta\boldsymbol{\uppsi})_{\mathbb{H}}.

(3.12)

Now, we are exactly in the situation to apply
[
33
, Theorem 3.1.3]
, with
\alpha=C
, to obtain the desired estimate. In particular, observe that, by going through the proof of the cited Theorem and especially the one of
[
33
, Lemma 3.1.4]
, the argument can be repeated even in our regularity setting with
\boldsymbol{\uppsi}\in\mathbb{X}_{0}\times\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{X}
. Indeed, the crucial point is the validity of the identities (
3.11
) and (
3.12
). For the sake of completeness, we recall here below the fundamental steps of the proof.

Let

q:=\lVert\boldsymbol{\uppsi}\rVert^{2}_{\mathbb{H}},\hskip 10.00002ptg:=\frac{2(f_{\boldsymbol{\uppsi}},\boldsymbol{\uppsi})_{\mathbb{H}}}{q},\hskip 10.00002ptl:=\log q-\int_{0}^{t}g\,\mathrm{d}s.

Then, the first step is to show, through explicit calculations, that there exists a constant
c\geq 0
such that
l
satisfies the differential inequality

\partial^{2}_{t}l+c\lvert\partial_{t}l\rvert+c\geq 0\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $(0,T)$}.

(3.13)

This can be done exactly as in
[
33
, Lemma 3.1.4]
, by relying on the identities (
3.11
) and (
3.12

A=-\boldsymbol{D}\Delta
has no skew-symmetric part. Then, exactly as in
[
33

, by exploiting maximum, minimum and comparison principles, we can see that
l(t)\leq L(t)
for any

, where

is the solution to the following boundary value problem:

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}^{2}L+c_{\tau}\partial_{t}L+c=0,\\
L(0)=l(0),\quad L(T)=l(T),\end{cases}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{where }c_{\tau}=\begin{cases}c\quad\text{on }(0,\tau),\\
-c\quad\text{on }(\tau,T),\end{cases}

for some
\tau\in(0,T)
, depending on the sign of
\partial_{t}L
. Let us first consider the problem

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}^{2}v+c_{\tau}\partial_{t}v=0,\\
v(0)=l(0),\quad v(T)=l(T).\end{cases}

Observe that, since
\partial_{t}v
satisfies a homogeneous linear differential equation of first order, it does not change its sign, which depends only on the values
v(0)
and
v(T)
. Then, by the maximum principle we can say that
v\leq V
on
[0,T]
, with
V
solution to

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}^{2}V+c\partial_{t}V=0,\\
V(0)=l(0),\quad V(T)=l(T).\end{cases}

Computing directly the solution
V
we find that

v(t)\leq V(t)=l(0)\frac{e^{-\gamma t}-e^{-\gamma T}}{1-e^{-\gamma T}}+l(T)\frac{1-e^{-\gamma t}}{1-e^{-\gamma T}},

for any
t\in[0,T]
, with
\gamma
being either
c
or
-c
, depending on whether
l(0)\leq l(T)
or
l(0)>l(T)
. Then, one can also find an explicit solution
w
of the non-homogeneous equation with zero boundary data and show that

w(t)\leq\frac{2e^{cT}}{c}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for any }t\in(0,T).

We refer to
[
33
, Lemma 3.1.5]
for all these explicit calculations. Hence, we find that

l(t)\leq L(t)=v(t)+w(t)\leq l(0)\frac{e^{-\gamma t}-e^{-\gamma T}}{1-e^{-\gamma T}}+l(T)\frac{1-e^{-\gamma t}}{1-e^{-\gamma T}}+\frac{2e^{cT}}{c},

for any
t\in[0,T]
, with
\gamma=c
or
\gamma=-c
. Therefore, by using the definition of
l
and the fact that
\int_{0}^{t}g\,\mathrm{d}s\leq 2CT
, we deduce that

\log\lVert\boldsymbol{\uppsi}(t)\rVert\leq\log\lVert\boldsymbol{\uppsi}(0)\rVert\frac{e^{-\gamma t}-e^{-\gamma T}}{1-e^{-\gamma T}}+\log\lVert\boldsymbol{\uppsi}(T)\rVert\frac{1-e^{-\gamma t}}{1-e^{-\gamma T}}+\frac{2e^{cT}}{c}+2CT.

Finally, by exponentiating and using
\lVert\boldsymbol{\uppsi}(0)\rVert_{\mathbb{H}}\leq M
, we reach the thesis with a constant
C_{1}>0
depending only on
c
,
C
and
T
.
∎

Remark 3.2

In the proof of Proposition
3.1

\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\uppsi}-\boldsymbol{D}\Delta\boldsymbol{\uppsi}=f_{\boldsymbol{\uppsi}}

Remark 3.3
.

Observe that, from

Corollary 3.4
.

Assume hypotheses
A1
–
A3
. Let

(\varphi_{0}^{1},\sigma_{0}^{1},p_{0}^{1})=(\varphi_{0}^{2},\sigma_{0}^{2},p_{0}^{2})

Proof.

\lVert(\varphi_{1}(t),\sigma_{1}(t),p_{1}(t))-(\varphi_{2}(t),\sigma_{2}(t),p_{2}(t))\rVert_{\mathbb{H}}=0

∎

For future use, we observe that if we define (cf. (
3.1
) for the definition of

\varepsilon:=\frac{\lVert(\varphi_{1}(T),\sigma_{1}(T),p_{1}(T))-(\varphi_{2}(T),\sigma_{2}(T),p_{2}(T))\rVert_{\mathbb{H}}}{M}=\frac{\lVert\boldsymbol{\uppsi}(T)\rVert_{\mathbb{H}}}{M},

for any

\lambda_{1}(t)

\lambda_{2}(t)
.

\lVert(\varphi,\sigma,p)\rVert_{H^{1}(0,T;\mathbb{H})}\leq M_{1},

(3.15)

where

Theorem 3.5
.

Assume hypotheses
A1
–
A3
. Let
(\varphi_{1},\sigma_{1},p_{1})
and
(\varphi_{2},\sigma_{2},p_{2})
be two solutions of (
2.1
)–(
2.5
) corresponding to two triples of initial data
(\varphi_{0}^{i},\sigma_{0}^{i},p_{0}^{i})\in\mathcal{I}_{\text{ad}}
for
i=1,2
.

Assume that for
i=1,2
we also have the following a priori bound:

\lVert(\varphi_{0}^{i},\sigma_{0}^{i},p_{0}^{i})\rVert_{\mathbb{V}}\leq\overline{C},

(3.16)

and let
M>0
,
C_{1}>0
,
M_{1}>0
be as in (
3.1
), (
3.2
), (
3.15
).
Let
\varepsilon
be defined as in (
3.14
)
and assume that

\varepsilon\leq\exp\left\{-\left(\min\left\{1,\frac{4\sqrt{3}MC_{1}^{3/2}}{9M_{1}}\right\}\right)^{-1}\right\}.

Then, there exists a constant
C_{2}>0
such that

\lVert(\varphi_{0}^{1},\sigma_{0}^{1},p_{0}^{1})-(\varphi_{0}^{2},\sigma_{0}^{2},p_{0}^{2})\rVert^{2}_{\mathbb{H}}\leq\frac{C_{2}}{\sqrt{\lvert\log\varepsilon\rvert}},

(3.17)

where we can quantify the constant as

C_{2}=\frac{2M_{1}MC_{1}^{1/2}}{\beta^{1/2}}+\frac{3M_{1}^{2}}{4\beta C_{1}},\hskip 10.00002pt\text{with }\beta=\frac{\gamma}{e^{\gamma T}-1}>0,

and where
\gamma>0
is the constant appearing in (
3.3
).

Proof.

For all this argument, we still use the compact notation with
\boldsymbol{\uppsi}
introduced in the proof of Proposition
3.1
. Note that under our regularity assumptions, by standard results in Lebesgue-Bochner theory, the function
t\to\lVert\boldsymbol{\uppsi}(t)\rVert^{2}_{\mathbb{H}}
is absolutely continuous. Then, we can apply the fundamental theorem of calculus to say that for any
t\in[0,T]

\displaystyle\lVert\boldsymbol{\uppsi}(t)\rVert^{2}_{\mathbb{H}}

\displaystyle=\lVert\boldsymbol{\uppsi}(0)\rVert^{2}_{\mathbb{H}}+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\lVert\boldsymbol{\uppsi}(s)\rVert^{2}_{\mathbb{H}}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\lVert\boldsymbol{\uppsi}(0)\rVert^{2}_{\mathbb{H}}+\int_{0}^{t}2(\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{\uppsi}(s),\boldsymbol{\uppsi}(s))_{\mathbb{H}}\,\mathrm{d}s.

Then, by using Cauchy-Schwarz, the stability inequality (
3.2
) and the bounds (
3.1
) and (
3.15
), we deduce that

\displaystyle\lVert\boldsymbol{\uppsi}(0)\rVert^{2}_{\mathbb{H}}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\boldsymbol{\uppsi}(t)\rVert^{2}_{\mathbb{H}}+2\left(\int_{0}^{t}\lVert\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{\uppsi}(s)\rVert^{2}_{\mathbb{H}}\,\mathrm{d}s\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\lVert\boldsymbol{\uppsi}(s)\rVert^{2}_{\mathbb{H}}\,\mathrm{d}s\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}^{2}M^{2}\varepsilon^{2\lambda(t)}+2M_{1}\left(\int_{0}^{t}M\cdot C_{1}M\varepsilon^{\lambda(s)}\,\mathrm{d}s\right)^{1/2},

where
\lambda
can be either
\lambda_{1}
or
\lambda_{2}
.
Now, by working on the explicit expressions of
\lambda_{i}(t)
,
i=1,2
, one can easily see that
\lambda_{i}(0)=0
,
\lambda_{i}(T)=1
,
\lambda_{i}
is strictly increasing for both
i=1,2
,
\lambda_{1}
is concave and
\lambda_{2}
is convex.
So one has the situation depicted in Figure
3
.
Then, the tangent line at
0
to
\lambda_{2}
, which has the expression

\tilde{\lambda}(t)=\beta t,\hskip 10.00002pt\text{with }\beta=\frac{\gamma}{e^{\gamma T}-1},

is below both
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
. Therefore, since
0<\varepsilon<1
, we can infer that

\varepsilon^{\lambda_{i}(t)}\leq\varepsilon^{\beta t}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for both }i=1,2.

Hence, we can further estimate

\displaystyle\lVert\boldsymbol{\uppsi}(0)\rVert^{2}_{\mathbb{H}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}^{2}M^{2}\varepsilon^{2\beta t}+2M_{1}MC^{1/2}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\varepsilon^{\beta s}\,\mathrm{d}s\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=C_{1}^{2}M^{2}\varepsilon^{2\beta t}+2M_{1}MC^{1/2}\left(\frac{\varepsilon^{\beta t}}{\beta\log\varepsilon}-\frac{1}{\beta\log\varepsilon}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=C_{1}^{2}M^{2}\varepsilon^{2\beta t}+\frac{2M_{1}MC^{1/2}}{\beta^{1/2}\sqrt{\lvert\log\varepsilon\rvert}}(1-\varepsilon^{\beta t})^{1/2}:=g(t).

Now, note that this estimate holds for any
t\in[0,T]
, so we can make it sharper and independent of
t
by minimising the function
g(t)
. Then, we compute its derivative and, recalling that
\log\varepsilon<0
, we obtain that

g^{\prime}(t)=-C_{1}^{2}M^{2}2\beta\lvert\log\varepsilon\rvert\cdot\varepsilon^{2\beta t}+M_{1}MC_{1}^{1/2}\beta^{1/2}\sqrt{\lvert\log\varepsilon\rvert}\frac{\varepsilon^{\beta t}}{(1-\varepsilon^{\beta t})^{1/2}}.

If we call
x=\varepsilon^{\beta t}
,
0<x<1
, we can find the critical points by solving the equation

-C_{1}^{2}M^{2}2\beta\lvert\log\varepsilon\rvert\cdot x^{2}+M_{1}MC_{1}^{1/2}\beta^{1/2}\sqrt{\lvert\log\varepsilon\rvert}\frac{x}{(1-x)^{1/2}}=0,

which, after some manipulations, is equivalent to

x\sqrt{1-x}=\frac{M_{1}}{2\beta^{1/2}C_{1}^{3/2}M\sqrt{\lvert\log\varepsilon\rvert}}.

(3.18)

By analysing the function
f(x)=x\sqrt{1-x}
, we see that its graph is like the one in Figure
4
, with maximum value at
\frac{2\sqrt{3}}{9}
. Then, equation (
3.18
) has at least a solution if

\frac{M_{1}}{2\beta^{1/2}C_{1}^{3/2}M\sqrt{\lvert\log\varepsilon\rvert}}\leq\frac{2\sqrt{3}}{9},

which gives part of the smallness condition in the statement of the Theorem.
Moreover, one can easily see that the solution corresponding to the minimum value of
g(t)
is the smallest one, let us call it
\overline{x}
.
Then, we can see that the increasing branch of the function
f
lies between the two lines
y=x
and
y=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{3}x
, therefore we can estimate the value of
\overline{x}
with the solutions corresponding to the two lines, namely

\frac{M_{1}}{2\beta^{1/2}C_{1}^{3/2}M\sqrt{\lvert\log\varepsilon\rvert}}\leq\overline{x}\leq\frac{\sqrt{3}M_{1}}{2\beta^{1/2}C_{1}^{3/2}M\sqrt{\lvert\log\varepsilon\rvert}}.

(3.19)

Finally, we can use this piece of information to close our estimate:

\displaystyle\lVert\boldsymbol{\uppsi}(0)\rVert^{2}_{\mathbb{H}}

\displaystyle\leq\min_{t\in[0,T]}g(t)=C_{1}^{2}M^{2}\overline{x}^{2}+\frac{2M_{1}MC^{1/2}}{\beta^{1/2}\sqrt{\lvert\log\varepsilon\rvert}}(1-\overline{x})^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}^{2}M^{2}\frac{3M_{1}^{2}}{4\beta C_{1}^{3}M^{2}\lvert\log\varepsilon\rvert}+\frac{2M_{1}MC^{1/2}}{\beta^{1/2}\sqrt{\lvert\log\varepsilon\rvert}}\underbrace{\left(1-\frac{M_{1}}{2\beta^{1/2}C_{1}^{3/2}M\sqrt{\lvert\log\varepsilon\rvert}}\right)^{1/2}}_{\leq 1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lvert\log\varepsilon\rvert}}\left(\frac{2M_{1}MC^{1/2}}{\beta^{1/2}}+\frac{3M_{1}^{2}}{4\beta C_{1}\sqrt{\lvert\log\varepsilon\rvert}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lvert\log\varepsilon\rvert}}\left(\frac{2M_{1}MC^{1/2}}{\beta^{1/2}}+\frac{3M_{1}^{2}}{4\beta C_{1}}\right),

if we also assume that
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lvert\log\varepsilon\rvert}}\leq 1
, which gives the other part of the smallness assumption.
This concludes the proof of Theorem
3.5
.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

Observe that this type of logarithmic estimate can be impractical in applications, as the error actually gets small only if the final data are very close to each other.
Moreover, it can be easily seen that the stability constant
C_{2}
deteriorates exponentially with the final time
T
, making this stability estimate even more unreliable.
Nevertheless, this is expected since we are dealing with a backward problem for a parabolic system, which is known to be severely ill-posed as
T
gets larger.

We now want to improve this stability estimate to one of Lipschitz type, under the additional a priori assumption that the initial data to be reconstructed actually lives in a finite-dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{V}
, for instance, one of the discrete spaces used in numerical approximations.
To do this, we recall the following general result, which was proved in
[
7
, Proposition 5]
.
We note that the same result was successfully used to get Lipschitz stability estimates for other inverse ill-posed problems, like in
[
3
,
6
,
10
]
.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
R
and
r_{0}
be positive numbers. Let
\Lambda
be an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
K
be a subset of
\Lambda
. Assume that

K\subseteq B_{R}(0)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptB_{r_{0}}(p)\subseteq\Lambda,\text{ for any }p\in K.

(3.20)

Let
\mathcal{B}
be a Banach space and
G:\Lambda\to\mathcal{B}
an operator. Assume that
G
satisfies the following conditions:

(i)

G_{\mid K}
is injective,

(ii)

(G_{\mid K})^{-1}:G(K)\to K
is uniformly continuous with modulus of continuity
\omega_{0}(\cdot)
,

(iii)

G
is Fréchet-differentiable with derivative
\mathrm{D}G
,

(iv)

\mathrm{D}G:\Lambda\to\mathcal{L}(\Lambda,\mathcal{B})
is uniformly continuous with modulus of continuity
\omega_{1}(\cdot)
.

(v)

there exists
m_{0}>0
such that

\inf_{x\in K,\lvert y\rvert=1}\lVert\mathrm{D}G(x)[y]\rVert_{\mathcal{B}}\geq m_{0}.

Then, for any
x_{1},x_{2}\in K

\lvert x_{1}-x_{2}\rvert\leq C\lVert G(x_{1})-G(x_{2})\rVert_{\mathcal{B}},

(3.21)

where
C=\max\left\{\frac{2R}{(\omega_{0}^{-1})_{*}(\delta_{1})},\frac{2}{m_{0}}\right\}
,
\delta_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\min\{\delta_{0},r_{0}\}
and
\delta_{0}=(\omega_{1}^{-1})_{*}\left(\frac{m_{0}}{2}\right)
. Here, for a modulus of continuity
\omega
, we denote

(\omega^{-1})_{*}(y):=\inf\{x\in[0,+\infty)\mid\omega(x)\geq y\}.

Before using Lemma
3.7
in our case, we still need to study more in detail the Fréchet-derivative of our operator
\mathcal{R}:\mathbb{V}\to\mathbb{H}
, which associates to any initial data
(\varphi_{0},\sigma_{0},p_{0})
the solution
(\varphi(T),\sigma(T),p(T))
at the final time.
In Theorem
2.5
we showed that
\mathrm{D}\mathcal{R}:\mathbb{V}\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)^{3}\to\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{V}\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)^{3},\mathbb{H})
is such that, for any
(h,k,w)\in\mathbb{V}\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)^{3}
,
\mathrm{D}\mathcal{R}(\varphi_{0},\sigma_{0},p_{0})[h,k,w]=(Y(T),Z(T),P(T))
, which is the solution of the linearised system (
2.21
)–(
2.25
) evaluated at the final time.
Regarding this linearised inverse problem, we can prove a similar logarithmic stability estimate, since it essentially has the same formal structure as (
3.4
)–(
3.8
).

Theorem 3.8
.

Assume hypotheses
A1
–
A3
. Let
(\overline{\varphi},\overline{\sigma},\overline{p})
be a solution of (
2.1
)–(
2.5
) corresponding to a triple of initial data
(\varphi_{0},\sigma_{0},p_{0})\in\mathcal{I}_{\text{ad}}
. Moreover, let
(Y,Z,P)
be a solution of (
2.21
)–(
2.25
) corresponding to
(h,k,w)\in\mathbb{V}
.
Assume also that

\lVert(h,k,w)\rVert_{\mathbb{V}}\leq\overline{Q},

for some
\overline{Q}>0
and call
L=L(\overline{Q})>0
,
L_{1}=L_{1}(\overline{Q})>0
the minimal constants such that by Proposition
2.4

\lVert(Y,Z,P)\rVert_{\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,T];\mathbb{H})}\leq L,\hskip 10.00002pt\lVert(Y,Z;P)\rVert_{H^{1}(0,T;\mathbb{H})}\leq L_{1}.

(3.22)

Then, there exists a constant
Q_{1}>0
, depending only on the parameters of the system, such that we have the following conditional stability estimate for any
t\in[0,T]

\lVert(Y(t),Z(t),P(t))\rVert_{\mathbb{H}}\leq Q_{1}L^{1-\lambda}\lVert(Y(T),Z(T),P(T))\rVert^{\lambda},

where
\lambda
is either
\lambda_{1}(t)
or
\lambda_{2}(t)
.

Moreover, if we additionally assume that

\varepsilon:=\frac{\lVert(Y(T),Z(T),P(T))\rVert_{\mathbb{H}}}{L}\leq\exp\left\{-\left(\min\left\{1,\frac{4\sqrt{3}LQ_{1}^{3/2}}{9L_{1}}\right\}\right)^{-1}\right\},

then there exists a constant
Q_{2}>0
such that

\lVert(h,k,w)\rVert^{2}_{\mathbb{H}}\leq\frac{Q_{2}}{\sqrt{\lvert\log\varepsilon\rvert}},

(3.23)

where we can quantify the constant as

Q_{2}=\frac{2L_{1}LC_{1}^{1/2}}{\beta^{1/2}}+\frac{3L_{1}^{2}}{4\beta Q_{1}}.

(3.24)

Remark 3.9
.

Since the system is linear, it is clear that (
3.23
) gives a stability estimate for the linearised inverse problem of reconstructing the initial data
(h,k,w)
from the final measurements
(Y(T),Z(T),P(T))
; indeed, the difference of two solutions still satisfies the same linear system.

Proof.

Observe that we can rewrite the linearised system (
2.21
)–(
2.25
) in the abstract form:

\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{Y}-\boldsymbol{D}\Delta\boldsymbol{Y}=f_{\boldsymbol{Y}},\hskip 10.00002pt\boldsymbol{Y}(0)=\boldsymbol{h},

where
\boldsymbol{Y}=(Y,Z,P)^{\top}
,
\boldsymbol{h}=(h,k,w)^{\top}
and

f_{\boldsymbol{Y}}=\begin{pmatrix}-F^{\prime\prime}(\overline{\varphi})Y+m(\overline{\sigma})h^{\prime\prime}(\overline{\varphi})Y+m^{\prime}(\overline{\sigma})h^{\prime}(\overline{\varphi})Z\\
-\gamma_{h}Z-\gamma_{ch}\overline{\sigma}Y-\gamma_{ch}\overline{\varphi}Z+S_{ch}Y\\
-\gamma_{p}P+\alpha_{ch}Y\end{pmatrix}.

We recall that the Laplacian operator actually depends on the different boundary conditions.
By using the fact that
\overline{\varphi},\overline{\sigma}\in L^{\infty}(Q_{T})
by Theorem
2.1
, it is easy to see that

\lVert f_{\boldsymbol{Y}}\rVert_{\mathbb{H}}\leq C\lVert\boldsymbol{Y}\rVert_{\mathbb{H}}.

Therefore, we are exactly in the same situation to apply
[
33
, Theorem 3.1.3]
and then follow the same reasoning of the proof of Theorem
3.5
. This concludes the proof.
∎

We are now in the position to apply Lemma
3.7
and establish a Lipschitz stability estimate, when starting from a finite-dimensional subspace.

Theorem 3.10
.

Assume hypotheses
A1
–
A3
. Let
\Lambda
be a finite-dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{V}
and
K\subseteq\Lambda
such that

K=\{\boldsymbol{u}\in\Lambda\mid\lVert\boldsymbol{u}\rVert_{\mathbb{V}}\leq\overline{C}\},

for some constant
\overline{C}>0
to be decided a priori. Assume further that

(\varphi_{0},\sigma_{0},p_{0})\in K\cap\mathcal{I}_{\text{ad}}.

Then, there exists a constant
C_{s}>0
such that for any choice of
(\varphi_{0}^{1},\sigma_{0}^{1},p_{0}^{1})
and
(\varphi_{0}^{2},\sigma_{0}^{2},p_{0}^{2})
in
K\cap\mathcal{I}_{\text{ad}}
the following stability estimate holds

\lVert(\varphi_{0}^{1},\sigma_{0}^{1},p_{0}^{1})-(\varphi_{0}^{2},\sigma_{0}^{2},p_{0}^{2})\rVert_{\mathbb{H}}\leq C_{s}\lVert(\varphi_{1}(T),\sigma_{1}(T),p_{1}(T))-(\varphi_{2}(T),\sigma_{2}(T),p_{2}(T))\rVert_{\mathbb{H}},

(3.25)

where the constant
C_{s}
can be quantified as

C_{s}=\max\left\{\frac{2\overline{C}}{M}e^{\frac{16C_{0}^{2}C_{2}}{m_{0}}},\frac{2}{m_{0}}\right\},\text{ with }m_{0}=\frac{L}{C_{\Lambda}}e^{-Q^{2}_{2}}\text{ and }C_{\Lambda}=\sup_{\boldsymbol{h}\in\Lambda\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\lVert\boldsymbol{h}\rVert_{\mathbb{V}}}{\lVert\boldsymbol{h}\rVert_{\mathbb{H}}},

where
C_{0}
is the Lipschitz constant of the Fréchet-derivative, given by (
2.28
), and
M
,
L
, and
Q_{2}
are defined in (
3.1
), (
3.22
), (
3.24
).

Proof.

We want to apply Lemma
3.7
, so we have to verify its hypotheses.
Indeed, we set
X=\mathbb{V}
and
\Lambda
as above. Being a finite-dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{V}
means that
\Lambda=\mathbb{R}^{n}
in the language of Lemma
3.7
, therefore we can immediately see that
K
satisfies hypothesis (
3.20
).
We consider the operator
\mathcal{R}:\Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{V}\to\mathbb{H}
defined as
\mathcal{R}((\varphi_{0},\sigma_{0},p_{0}))=(\varphi(T),\sigma(T),p(T))
.
Hypothesis
(i)
on the injectivity of
\mathcal{R}_{\mid K}
follows directly from Corollary
3.4
.
Also hypothesis
(ii)
follows immediately from Theorem
3.5
, with modulus of continuity
\omega_{0}(r)=C_{2}/\sqrt{\lvert\log(r/M)\rvert}
.
Then, hypotheses
(iii)
and
(iv)
on Fréchet-differentiability follow from Theorem
2.5
, with modulus of continuity
\omega_{1}(r)=C_{0}r
. Finally, we have to check condition
(v)
.
To do this, notice that for any
(\varphi_{0},\sigma_{0},p_{0})\in K
and any
(h,k,w)\in\Lambda
, we have that
\mathrm{D}\mathcal{R}(\varphi_{0},\sigma_{0},p_{0})[h,k,w]=(Y(T),Z(T),P(T))
, where
(Y,Z,T)
is the solution to (
2.21
)–(
2.25
).
Now, by Theorem
3.8
, we can use (
3.23
) to say that, if
\lVert(h,k,w)\rVert_{\mathbb{V}}\leq\overline{Q}
, it holds that

\lVert(h,k,w)\rVert^{2}_{\mathbb{H}}\leq\frac{Q_{2}}{\sqrt{\left\lvert\log\frac{\lVert(Y(T),Z(T),P(T))\rVert_{\mathbb{H}}}{L}\right\rvert}},

which implies that

\lVert(Y(T),Z(T),P(T))\rVert_{\mathbb{H}}\geq Le^{-\frac{Q_{2}^{2}}{\lVert(h,k,w)\rVert^{4}_{\mathbb{H}}}}.

Hence, we can verify condition
(v)
as

\displaystyle\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\varphi_{0},\sigma_{0},p_{0})\in K\\
(h,k,w)\in\Lambda\setminus\{0\}\end{subarray}}\frac{\lVert(Y(T),Z(T),P(T))\rVert_{\mathbb{H}}}{\lVert(h,k,w)\rVert_{\mathbb{V}}}\geq\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\varphi_{0},\sigma_{0},p_{0})\in K\\
(h,k,w)\in\Lambda\setminus\{0\}\end{subarray}}\frac{\lVert(Y(T),Z(T),P(T))\rVert_{\mathbb{H}}}{C_{\Lambda}\lVert(h,k,w)\rVert_{\mathbb{H}}}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\varphi_{0},\sigma_{0},p_{0})\in K\\
\lVert(h,k,w)\rVert_{\mathbb{H}}=1\end{subarray}}\frac{\lVert(Y(T),Z(T),P(T))\rVert_{\mathbb{H}}}{C_{\Lambda}\lVert(h,k,w)\rVert_{\mathbb{H}}}\geq\frac{L}{C_{\Lambda}}e^{-Q_{2}^{2}},

where we used the linearity of
\mathrm{D}\mathcal{R}
and, since
\Lambda
is finite-dimensional and thus all norms on it are equivalent, we estimated
\lVert(h,k,w)\rVert_{\mathbb{V}}\leq C_{\Lambda}\lVert(h,k,w)\rVert_{\mathbb{H}}
.

Then, we can apply Lemma
3.7
and conclude the proof of Theorem
3.10
.
∎

Remark 3.11
.

We would like to point out that the constant
C_{s}
blows up exponentially as the dimension of
\Lambda
goes to infinity.
Indeed, as one can see from its expression,
C_{s}
has a direct exponential proportionality to the constant
C_{\Lambda}
, which is finite only if
\Lambda
has a finite dimension and blows up as it becomes larger.
Additionally, one can also notice that the dependence of
C_{s}
on the final time is even worse, namely
\log(C_{s})
depends exponentially on
T
.
This means that, even if we have some kind of Lipschitz stability, one has to be very careful when designing numerical algorithms to approximate the solution.

4
Conclusions

In this paper we consider a phase field model for prostate cancer growth previously introduced and studied in
[
13
,
16
]
.
While improving previous well-posedness results, we rigorously analyse an inverse problem aiming to reconstruct an earlier tumour state starting from a single measurement at a later time.
We first derive a conditional logarithmic stability estimate and we subsequently prove that the solution to the inverse problem is unique.
Furthermore, by assuming to know a priori that the solution lies in a finite-dimensional subspace, we are also able to obtain an optimal Lipschitz stability estimate, as in Theorem
3.10
.
A stability estimate of this kind generally opens the possibility of using iterative reconstruction algorithms, such as the Landweber scheme, to approximate numerically the solution to the inverse problem.
Indeed, under the validity of a Lipschitz stability estimate and the Fréchet-differentiability of the forward map (or more generally of the so-called tangential cone condition), it is shown that such iterative methods are locally convergent
[
17
,
27
,
36
,
30
]
.
Therefore, they should provide faithful reconstructions of the earlier tumour state (i.e., the initial conditions of the forward problem), at least if the final time
T
is small.
On the other hand, due to the severe ill-posedness of the non-linear inverse problem, as shown in Theorem
3.10
, the stability constants become doubly exponentially large with
T
.
This means that numerical analysis of this problem for large final times
T
is extremely challenging.
However, in our specific cancer growth application, it is important to be able to efficiently treat even the case of long time horizons, to better understand how the tumour has developed from a small mass to the situation observed in the medical images at diagnosis.
This information on tumour origin and early dynamics holds important value to estimate prognosis and survival, anticipate potential complications derived from disease progression, and contribute to the planning of effective treatments for each individual patient
[
41
,
12
,
19
,
35
,
8
,
4
]
.
For this reason, the objective of a future part of our project is to carry out an in-depth simulation study to validate our analytical findings and propose efficient reconstruction methods.
