Let
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{D})

\mathbb{D}

\mathbb{C}

\mathcal{C}_{\phi}f:=f\circ\phi

\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{D})

\mathbb{D}

[

\mathcal{H}^{2}(\mathbb{D})

\displaystyle||f||^{2}_{\mathcal{H}^{2}(\mathbb{D})}=\sup_{0\leq r<1}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}|f(re^{i\theta})|^{2}d\theta<\infty.

For
\alpha>-1

\mathcal{A}^{2}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{D})
is the set of all functions
f\in\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{D})
for which

\displaystyle||f||^{2}_{\mathcal{A}^{2}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{D})}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}|f(z)|^{2}d{A}_{\alpha}(z)<\infty,

\displaystyle\langle f,g\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{D}}f(w)\overline{g(w)}dA_{\alpha}(w)\;\mbox{for all}\;f,g\in\mathcal{A}^{2}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{D}).

The reproducing kernel
K_{w}(z)

\mathcal{A}^{2}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{D})

\displaystyle K_{w}(z)=\frac{1}{(1-\overline{w}z)^{2+\alpha}}

\langle f(z),K_{w}(z)\rangle=f(w)
for all
f\in\mathcal{A}^{2}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{D})

It is a well-established fact, as shown in

The study of composition operators over Bergman spaces is an important area of research in complex analysis and operator theory. Bergman spaces are a class of function spaces defined on the unit disk in the complex plane, and composition operators are linear operators that map functions from one space to another by composing them with a fixed function. Bergman spaces are fundamental in the study of function theory, particularly in the context of bounded analytic functions. The composition operators over Bergman spaces provide insights into the behavior and properties of these functions when they are composed with specific functions. Understanding the composition operators helps in investigating the structure and properties of the functions in the Bergman space.

The study of composition operators over Bergman spaces is also significant in the realm of operator theory. Composition operators are bounded linear operators that act on function spaces. Analyzing their properties and behavior over Bergman spaces provides insights into their spectral properties, compactness, and other important operator-theoretic properties. The investigation of these properties is crucial for understanding the general theory of operators on function spaces and their applications in various areas of mathematics. The study of composition operators over Bergman spaces has practical applications in engineering and physics. These spaces find applications in signal processing, image analysis, control theory, and quantum mechanics. Understanding the behavior and properties of composition operators over Bergman spaces enables the development of efficient algorithms, signal processing techniques, and mathematical models in various engineering and physical systems.

Let
\phi
be an analytic self-map of
\mathbb{D}
and

. The composition-differentiation operator

D_{n,\phi}f(z):=f^{(n)}(\phi(z))
. Moreover, let

\mathbb{D}

\displaystyle D_{n,\psi,\phi}f(z)=\psi(z)f^{(n)}(\phi(z)),\;f\in\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{D}).

Remark 1.1
.

The following observations are clear.

(i)

If
n=0
, then the operator becomes the weighted composition operator
D_{\psi,\phi}
.

(ii)

In particular, if
\psi(z)\equiv 1
and
n=1
, then the operator
D_{n,\psi,\phi}
becomes the composition operator
C_{\phi}D
.

(iii)

For
\psi(z)=\phi^{\prime}(z)
and

, the operator in fact reduces to the product
DC_{\phi}
, where
C_{\phi}f(z)=D_{0,\phi}f(z)=f(\phi(z))
.

For more details about the operators
C_{\phi}D
and

and their different properties, we refer to the articles
[

56
,
58
]

\mathcal{H}^{2}(\mathbb{D})

In this paper, our objective is to investigate various properties of weighted composition differentiation operators for weighted Bergman spaces, expanding the scope of study beyond existing results. Henceforward, we denote

\displaystyle L_{n,\psi,\phi}:=\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}D_{k,\psi_{k},\phi},\;\mbox{where }\;c_{k}\in\mathbb{C}\;\mbox{for}\;k=1,2,\ldots,n.

Complex symmetric generalized weighted composition-differentiation operator on the Bergman space
\mathcal{A}^{2}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{D})

In this section, we mainly discuss complex symmetry, normality and self-adjointness of the generalized weighted composition-differentiation operator

on
\mathcal{A}^{2}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{D})
.

Suppose
n
is a non-negative integer and
w
belongs to the set
\mathbb{D}
. We define
K^{[n]}_{w}(z)
as the reproducing kernel for point-evaluation of the
n
-th derivative, with

\displaystyle K^{[n]}_{w}(z)=\frac{p_{n}z^{n}}{(1-\bar{w}z)^{n+\alpha+2}},z\in\mathbb{D},

where
p_{n}=(\alpha+2)(\alpha+3)\ldots(\alpha+n+1)
. It can be proven that
\langle f(z),K^{[n]}_{w}(z)\rangle=f^{(n)}(w)
, for all
f\in\mathcal{A}^{2}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{D})
. For any non-negative integer
n
, we define

\displaystyle\gamma_{n}=\sqrt{\frac{\Gamma(n+\alpha+2)}{n!\Gamma(2+\alpha)}}z^{n},z\in\mathbb{D},

where
\Gamma
is the standard Gamma function . Then the set
\{\gamma_{n}:n\geq 0\}
forms an orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{A}^{2}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{D})
(see
[
40
]

An antilinear map
\mathcal{C}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
on a separable Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
is called a conjugation if it satisfies
\langle\mathcal{C}x,\mathcal{C}y\rangle=\langle y,x\rangle
for all
x,y\in\mathcal{H}
, and
\mathcal{C}^{2}=I
. A bounded linear operator
T
on
\mathcal{H}
is said to be complex symmetric if there exists a conjugation
\mathcal{C}
on
\mathcal{H}
such that
T=\mathcal{C}T^{*}\mathcal{C}
.

For
\mu,\eta
on the unit circle
{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|=1}
, the conjugation
\mathcal{C}_{\mu,\eta}
is defined as
\mathcal{C}_{\mu,\eta}f=\mu\overline{f(\overline{\eta z})}
, where
f
belongs to the class of analytic functions. In this paper, we explore the complex symmetric weighted composition-differentiation operator on the weighted Bergman space
\mathcal{A}^{2}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{D})
with respect to the conjugation

The recent work by Garcia and Hammond
[
26
]
focused on the characteristics of a complex symmetric weighted composition operator on weighted Hardy spaces, where they provided explicit formulas for these operators using a particular conjugation technique. From
[
5
]
, we have a lemma that directly addresses the behavior of the adjoint of the weighted composition-differentiation operator
D_{n,\psi,\phi}
with respect to the reproducing kernel of
\mathcal{A}^{2}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{D})
.

Lemma 2.1
.

[
5
]

Let
\phi
be an analytic self-map of
\mathbb{D}
and
\psi\in\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{D})
such that
D_{n,\psi,\phi}
is bounded on
\mathcal{A}^{2}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{D})
. Then for any
w\in\mathbb{D}
,

\displaystyle D^{*}_{n,\psi,\phi}K_{w}(z)=\overline{\psi(w)}K^{[n]}_{\phi(w)}.

In order to make progress with our research, it is imperative to formulate a lemma that broadens the applicability of Lemma
2.1
in a more generalized way. As a result, we present the following lemma, which specifically addresses the action of the adjoint of the generalized composition-differentiation operator
L_{n,\psi,\phi}
on the reproducing kernel of
\mathcal{A}^{2}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{D})
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\phi
be an analytic self-map of
\mathbb{D}
and
\psi_{k}\in\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{D})
(
k=1,2,\ldots,k
) such that
L_{n,\psi,\phi}:=\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}D_{k,\psi,\phi}
(where
c_{k}\in\mathbb{C}
for
k=1,2,\ldots,n
) is bounded on
\mathcal{A}^{2}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{D})
. Then for any
w\in\mathbb{D}
,

\displaystyle L^{*}_{n,\psi,\phi}K_{w}(z)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\overline{c_{k}\psi_{k}(w)}K^{[k]}_{\phi(w)}.

Proof of Lemma
2.2
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{A}^{2}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{D})
. Then a simple computation shows that

\displaystyle\langle f,L^{*}_{n,\psi,\phi}K_{w}\rangle_{\mathcal{A}^{2}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{D})}

\displaystyle=\langle L_{n,\psi,\phi},K_{w}\rangle_{\mathcal{A}^{2}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{D})}

\displaystyle=\bigg\langle\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}D_{k,\psi_{k},\phi},K_{w}\bigg\rangle_{\mathcal{A}^{2}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{D})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\bigg\langle c_{k}\psi_{k}\left(f^{(k)}\circ\phi\right),K_{w}\bigg\rangle_{\mathcal{A}^{2}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{D})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}\psi_{k}(w)f^{(k)}(\phi(w))

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\bigg\langle f,\;\overline{c_{k}\psi_{k}(w)}K^{[k]}_{\phi(w)}\bigg\rangle_{\mathcal{A}^{2}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{D})}

\displaystyle=\bigg\langle f,\;\sum_{k=1}^{n}\overline{c_{k}\psi_{k}(w)}K^{[k]}_{\phi(w)}\bigg\rangle_{\mathcal{A}^{2}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{D})}.

Hence, we see that

\displaystyle\langle f,L^{*}_{n,\psi,\phi}K_{w}\rangle_{\mathcal{A}^{2}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{D})}=\bigg\langle f,\;\sum_{k=1}^{n}\overline{c_{k}\psi_{k}(w)}K^{[k]}_{\phi(w)}\bigg\rangle_{\mathcal{A}^{2}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{D})}\;\mbox{for all}\;f\in\mathcal{A}^{2}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{D})

which leads the following conclusion

\displaystyle L^{*}_{n,\psi,\phi}K_{w}(z)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\overline{c_{k}\psi_{k}(w)}K^{[k]}_{\phi(w)}.

This completes the proof.

\displaystyle L_{n,\psi,\phi}C_{\mu,\eta}K_{w}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{c_{k}p_{k}\mu\psi_{k}(z)(\eta w)^{k}}{(1-\eta w\phi(z))^{k+\alpha+2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{c_{k}p_{k}a_{k}\mu(\eta wz)^{k}}{(1-\eta bz)^{k+\alpha+2}\bigg(1-\eta w(b+\frac{cz}{1-\eta bz})\bigg)^{k+\alpha+2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{c_{k}p_{k}a_{k}\mu(\eta wz)^{k}}{(1-\eta bz-\eta bw+\eta^{2}b^{2}wz-\eta cwz)^{k+\alpha+2}}.

and

(2.8)

\displaystyle C_{\mu,\eta}L^{*}_{n,\psi,\phi}K_{w}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{c_{k}p_{k}\mu\psi_{k}(w)(\eta z)^{k}}{(1-\phi(w)\eta z)^{k+\alpha+2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{c_{k}p_{k}a_{k}\mu(\eta wz)^{k}}{(1-\eta bw)^{k+\alpha+2}(1-\eta z(b+\frac{cw}{1-\eta bw}))^{k+\alpha+2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{c_{k}p_{k}a_{k}\mu(\eta wz)^{k}}{(1-\eta bz-\eta bw+\eta^{2}b^{2}wz-\eta cwz)^{k+\alpha+2}}.

It follows from (
2.7
) and (
2.8
) that

\displaystyle L_{n,\psi,\phi}C_{\mu,\eta}K_{w}(z)=C_{\mu,\eta}L^{*}_{n,\psi,\phi}K_{w}(z)\;\mbox{for all}\;z\in\mathbb{D}.

Since the span of the reproducing kernel functions is dense in
\mathcal{A}^{2}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{D})
, the operator
L_{n,\psi,\phi}
is complex symmetric with conjugation
C_{\mu,\eta}
. This completes the proof.
∎

Next, we proceed to establish a sufficient condition that guarantees the normality of the bounded operator
L_{n,\psi,\phi}
, given the presence of complex symmetry through conjugation
C_{\mu,\eta}
. This result expands upon and strengthens the findings of
[
5
, Theorem 2.3]
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
\phi
be an analytic self-map of
\mathbb{D}
with
\phi(0)=0
and
\psi_{k}\in\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{D})
, where
k=1,2,\ldots,n
, be not identically zero such that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the operator
L_{n,\psi,\phi}
is bounded and complex symmetric with conjugation
C_{\mu,\eta}
. Then
L_{n,\psi,\phi}
is normal.

The consequences of Theorem
2.2
indicate that if
c_{1}=c_{2}=\ldots=c_{n-1}=0
and
c_{n}=1
, then it is evident that
L_{n,\psi,\phi}=D_{n,\psi,\phi}
. Consequently, the following result can be obtained easily from Theorem
2.2
.

Corollary 2.3
.

[
5
, Theorem 2.3]

Let
\phi
be an analytic self-map of
\mathbb{D}
with
\phi(0)=0
and
\psi\in\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{D})
be not identically zero such that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the operator
D_{n,\psi,\phi}
is bounded and complex symmetric with conjugation
C_{\mu,\eta}
. Then
D_{n,\psi,\phi}
is normal.

It is easy to see that the Theorem
2.2
leads us to a straightforward derivation of the subsequent result.

Corollary 2.4
.

Let
\phi
be an analytic self-map of
\mathbb{D}
with
\phi(0)=0
and
\psi\in\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{D})
be not identically zero such that the operator
D_{\psi,\phi}
is bounded on
\mathcal{A}^{2}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{D})
and complex symmetric with conjugation
C_{\mu,\eta}
. Then
D_{\psi,\phi}
is normal.

By the hypothesis of the Corollary
2.4
, we can easily obtain from Corollary
2.2
that
\psi(z)=az
and
\phi(z)=cz
, where
a,c\in\mathbb{C}
. Thus The Corollary
2.4
can be seen as analogous to the sufficient part of
[
49
, Theorem 2.4]
.

Proof of Theorem
2.2
.

Since
L_{n,\psi,\phi}
is
C_{\mu,\eta}
-symmetric on
\mathcal{A}^{2}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{D})
and
\phi(0)=0
, in view of Theorem
2.1
, we have

\displaystyle\psi_{k}(z)=a_{k}z^{k}\;\mbox{for}\;k=1,2,\ldots,n\;\mbox{and}\;\phi(z)=cz,

where
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n},c\in\mathbb{C}
.

Then for any non-negative integer
k
, a simple computation shows that

\displaystyle\lVert L_{n,\psi,\phi}\gamma_{m}\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\lvert\langle L_{n,\psi,\phi}\gamma_{m},\gamma_{j}\rangle\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\bigg\lvert\bigg\langle\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}D_{k,\psi_{k},\phi}\gamma_{m},\gamma_{j}\bigg\rangle\bigg\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\bigg\lvert\bigg\langle\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}\psi_{k}\gamma_{m}^{(k)}(\phi),\gamma_{j}\bigg\rangle\bigg\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\bigg\lvert\bigg\langle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{c_{k}a_{k}m!c^{m-k}}{(m-k)!}\sqrt{\frac{\Gamma(m+\alpha+2)}{m!\Gamma(\alpha+2)}}z^{m},\sqrt{\frac{\Gamma(j+\alpha+2)}{j!\Gamma(\alpha+2)}}z^{j}\bigg\rangle\bigg\rvert^{2}.

Moreover, on the other hand, we have

\displaystyle\lVert L^{*}_{n,\psi,\phi}\gamma_{m}\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\lvert\langle L^{*}_{n,\psi,\phi}\gamma_{m},\gamma_{j}\rangle\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\lvert\langle\gamma_{m},L_{n,\psi,\phi}\gamma_{j}\rangle\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\bigg\lvert\bigg\langle\gamma_{m},\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}D_{k,\psi_{k},\phi}\gamma_{j}\bigg\rangle\bigg\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\bigg\lvert\bigg\langle\gamma_{m},\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}\psi_{k}\gamma_{j}^{(k)}(\phi)\bigg\rangle\bigg\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\bigg\lvert\bigg\langle\sqrt{\frac{\Gamma(m+\alpha+2)}{m!\Gamma(\alpha+2)}}z^{m},\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{c_{k}a_{k}j!c^{j-k}}{(j-k)!}\sqrt{\frac{\Gamma(j+\alpha+2)}{j!\Gamma(\alpha+2)}}z^{j}\bigg\rangle\bigg\rvert^{2}

Since the set
\{\gamma_{n}:n\geq 0\}
forms an orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{A}^{2}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{D})
, thus it follows that for any
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}

\displaystyle\lVert L_{n,\psi,\phi}\gamma_{m}\rVert^{2}=\bigg\lvert\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{c_{k}a_{k}m!c^{m-k}}{(m-k)!}\bigg\rvert^{2}=\lVert L^{*}_{n,\psi,\phi}\gamma_{m}\rVert^{2}.

Consequently, in view of the above estimates, it follows that

\displaystyle\lVert L_{n,\psi,\phi}(f)\rVert=\lVert L^{*}_{n,\psi,\phi}(f)\rVert\;\mbox{for all}\;f\in\mathcal{A}^{2}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{D}),

and
L_{n,\psi,\phi}
is a normal operator. This completes the proof.
∎

It should be emphasized that Theorem
2.2
does not encompass all normal weighted composition-differentiation operators on
\mathcal{A}^{2}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{D})
. It is of significance to highlight that we can precisely determine the normality of an operator
L_{n,\psi,\phi}
when
\psi_{k}
(
k=1,2,\ldots,n
) and
\phi
adopt a specific form that generalizes the symbols presented in the proof of Theorem
2.2
. Despite our efforts, we have not yet been able to establish whether an operator
L_{n,\psi,\phi}
, where
\phi(0)\neq 0
, can exhibit normal behavior for different choices of
\psi_{k}
(
k=1,2,\ldots,n
) and
\phi
.

We know that a bounded linear operator
T
is said to be Hermitian( self-adjoint) if
T^{*}=T
. In recent years, significant research has been focused on investigating the self-adjointness of weighted composition-differentiation operators acting on the reproducing kernel Hilbert space (see
[
25
,
45
,
52
]
and references therein). In
[
24
]
, Fatehi and Hummond provide a complete characterization of self-adjointness of weighted composition-differentiation operator on the Hardy space. In
[
49
]
, Liu
et al.
have discussed the self-adjointness of the weighted composition-differentiation operator on the Bergman space
\mathcal{A}^{2}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{D})
. In the following result, we establish a condition that is both necessary and sufficient for the bounded operator
L_{n,\psi,\phi}
to satisfy Hermitian properties on
\mathcal{A}^{2}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{D})
.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
\phi
be an analytic self-map of
\mathbb{D}
and
\psi_{k}
(where
k=1,2,\ldots,n
) be nonzero analytic functions on the unit disc such that
L_{n,\psi,\phi}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}D_{k,\psi_{k},\phi}
is bounded on
\mathcal{A}^{2}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{D})
and
c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
. Then
L_{n,\psi,\phi}
is Hermitian if, and only if,

\displaystyle\psi_{k}(z)=\frac{a_{k}z^{k}}{(1-\overline{b}z)^{k+\alpha+2}}\;\mbox{for}\;k=1,2,\ldots,n\;\mbox{and}\;\phi(z)=b+\frac{cz}{(1-\overline{b}z)}

for some
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n},c\in\mathbb{R}
and
b\in\mathbb{D}
.

Based on the implications of Theorem
2.3
, it is straightforward to derive the following results.

Corollary 2.5
.

[
5
, Theorem 2.4]

Let
\phi
be an analytic self-map of
\mathbb{D}
and
\psi
be a nonzero analytic functions on the unit disk
\mathbb{D}
such that
D_{n,\psi,\phi}
is bounded on
\mathcal{A}^{2}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{D})
. Then
D_{n,\psi,\phi}
is Hermitian if, and only if,

\displaystyle\psi(z)=\frac{az^{n}}{(1-\overline{b}z)^{n+\alpha+2}}\;\mbox{and}\;\phi(z)=b+\frac{cz}{(1-\overline{b}z)}

for some
a,c\in\mathbb{R}
and
b\in\mathbb{D}
.

Corollary 2.6
.

Let
\phi
be an analytic self-map of
\mathbb{D}
and
\psi
be a nonzero analytic functions on the unit disk
\mathbb{D}
such that
D_{\psi,\phi}
is bounded on
\mathcal{A}^{2}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{D})
. Then
D_{\psi,\phi}
is Hermitian if, and only if,

\displaystyle\psi(z)=\frac{az}{(1-\overline{b}z)^{\alpha+3}}\;\mbox{and}\;\phi(z)=b+\frac{cz}{(1-\overline{b}z)}

for some
a,c\in\mathbb{R}
and
b\in\mathbb{D}
.

It is worth noticing that Corollary
2.6
and
[
49
, Theorem 2.3]
have a similar characterization, making them analogues of each other.

Proof of Theorem
2.3
.

Let
L_{n,\psi,\phi}
be Hermitian. Then we have
L^{*}_{n,\psi,\phi}
=
L_{n,\psi,\phi}
, which is equivalent to

(2.9)

\displaystyle L^{*}_{n,\psi,\phi}K_{w}(z)=L_{n,\psi,\phi}K_{w}(z)\;\mbox{for all}\;z\in\mathbb{D}.

Using Lemma
2.1
, a simple computation shows that

(2.10)

\displaystyle L^{*}_{n,\psi,\phi}K_{w}(z)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\overline{c_{k}\psi_{k}(w)}K^{[k]}_{\phi(w)}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\overline{c_{k}\psi_{k}(w)}\frac{p_{k}z^{k}}{(1-\overline{\phi(w)}z)^{k+\alpha+2}}

and

(2.11)

\displaystyle L_{n,\psi,\phi}K_{w}(z)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}D_{k,\psi_{k},\phi}K_{w}(z)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}\psi_{k}(z)\frac{p_{k}\overline{w}^{k}}{(1-\overline{w}\phi(z))^{k+\alpha+2}}.

Substituting (
2.10
) and (
2.11
) into (
2.9
), we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\overline{c_{k}\psi_{k}(w)}\frac{p_{k}z^{k}}{(1-\overline{\phi(w)}z)^{k+\alpha+2}}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}\psi_{k}(z)\frac{p_{k}\overline{w}^{k}}{(1-\overline{w}\phi(z))^{k+\alpha+2}}.

This implies that

(2.12)

\displaystyle\frac{z^{k}\overline{\psi_{k}(w)}}{(1-\overline{\phi(w)}z)^{k+\alpha+2}}=\frac{\overline{w}^{k}\psi_{k}(z)}{(1-\overline{w}\phi(z))^{k+\alpha+2}}\;\mbox{for all}\;k=1,2,\ldots,n

for all
z,w\in\mathbb{D}
.

Letting
w=0
in the above equality (
2.12
), we obtain
\psi_{k}(0)=0
for all
k=1,2,\ldots,n
. For
\psi_{k}(z)\in\mathcal{A}^{2}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{D})
, if
\psi_{k}(z)=z^{m}g_{k}(z)
, where
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
g_{k}
is analytic with
g_{k}(0)\neq 0
, then we aim to show that
m=k
.

Case I
: If
m>k
, then it follows from (
2.12
) that

(2.13)

\displaystyle\frac{(\overline{w})^{m-k}\overline{g_{k}(w)}}{(1-\overline{\phi(w)}z)^{k+\alpha+2}}=\frac{z^{m-k}g_{k}(z)}{(1-\overline{w}\phi(z))^{k+\alpha+2}}

for all
z,w\in\mathbb{D}
. Setting
w=0
in (
2.13
), we obtain
g_{k}(z)=0
on
\mathbb{D}
, which is a contradiction to the assumption that
g_{k}(0)=0
.

Case II
: If
m<k
, then from (
2.12
), we obtain

(2.14)

\displaystyle\frac{z^{k-m}\overline{g_{k}(w)}}{(1-\overline{\phi(w)}z)^{k+\alpha+2}}=\frac{(\overline{w})^{k-m}g_{k}(z)}{(1-\overline{w}\phi(z))^{k+\alpha+2}}

for all
z,w\in\mathbb{D}
. Putting
w=0
in (
2.14
), we obtain
g_{k}(0)=0
, which is a contradiction. Therefore, we must have
m=k
. Now, (
2.12
) reduces to

(2.15)

\displaystyle\frac{\overline{g_{k}(w)}}{(1-\overline{\phi(w)}z)^{k+\alpha+2}}=\frac{g_{k}(z)}{(1-\overline{w}\phi(z))^{k+\alpha+2}}

for all
z,w\in\mathbb{D}
. Putting
w=0
in (
2.15
), we obtain

\displaystyle g_{k}(z)=\frac{\overline{g_{k}(0)}}{(1-\overline{\phi(0)}z)^{k+\alpha+2}}\;\mbox{for}\;k=1,2,\ldots,n.

Thus, we have

(2.16)

\displaystyle\psi_{k}(z)=\frac{a_{k}z^{k}}{(1-\overline{b}z)^{k+\alpha+2}}\;\mbox{where}\;a_{k}=\overline{g_{k}(0)}\;\mbox{and}\;b=\phi(0)\;\mbox{for}\;k=1,2,\ldots,n.

Substituting (
2.16
) into (
2.12
), we obtain

(2.17)

\displaystyle a_{k}(1-b\overline{w})^{k+\alpha+2}(1-\overline{\phi(w)}z)^{k+\alpha+2}=\overline{a_{k}}(1-\overline{b}z)^{k+\alpha+2}(1-\overline{w}\phi(z))^{k+\alpha+2}

for all
z,w\in\mathbb{D}
. If we set
w=0
in (
2.17
), then it is easy to see that
a_{k}=\overline{a_{k}}
, and hence
a_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
for all
k=1,2,\ldots,n
.

Differentiating both sides of (
2.17
) with respect to
\overline{w}
, we obtain

(2.18)

\displaystyle(1-

\displaystyle\overline{b}z)^{k+\alpha+2}(1-\overline{w}\phi(z))^{k+\alpha+1}\phi(z)=b(1-b\overline{w})^{k+\alpha+1}(1-\overline{\phi(w)}z)^{k+\alpha+2}

\displaystyle\quad+(1-b\overline{w})^{k+\alpha+2}(1-\overline{\phi(w)}z)^{k+\alpha+1}(\overline{z\phi^{\prime}(w)})\;\mbox{for all}\;z\in\mathbb{D}.

Setting
w=0
, we easily obtain from (
2.18
) that

\displaystyle\left(1-\overline{\phi(0)}z\right)^{k+\alpha+2}\phi(z)=\left(1-\overline{\phi(0)}z\right)^{k+\alpha+2}b+\left(1-\overline{\phi(0)}z\right)^{k+\alpha+1}\overline{\phi^{\prime}(0)}z

which implies that

(2.19)

\displaystyle\phi(z)=b+\frac{\phi^{\prime}(0)z}{1-\overline{\phi(0)}z}=b+\frac{cz}{1-\bar{b}z},

where
\overline{\phi^{\prime}(0)}=c
and
\phi(0)=b
.

Differentiating (
2.19
), we obtain
\phi^{\prime}(0)=c
. Therefore, we see that
c=\bar{c}
which implies that
c
is a real number.

Conversely, we assume that

\displaystyle\psi_{k}(z)=\frac{a_{k}z^{k}}{(1-\bar{b}z)^{k+\alpha+2}}\;\mbox{for}\;k=1,2,\ldots,n\;\mbox{and}\;\phi(z)=b+\frac{cz}{1-\bar{b}z},

where
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
.

Then, in view of (
2.10
) and (
2.11
), a straightforward computation gives that

(2.20)

\displaystyle L_{n,\psi,\phi}K_{w}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}\psi_{k}(z)\frac{p_{k}\overline{w}^{k}}{(1-\overline{w}\phi(z))^{k+\alpha+2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}c_{k}\left(\frac{z^{k}}{(1-\bar{b}z)^{k+\alpha+2}}\right)\frac{p_{k}\overline{w}^{k}}{\left(1-\overline{w}\left(b+\frac{cz}{1-\bar{b}z}\right)\right)^{k+\alpha+2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{c_{k}a_{k}p_{k}(z\overline{w})^{k}}{\left(1-\overline{b}z-b\overline{w}+\overline{w}|b|^{2}z-\overline{w}cz\right)^{k+\alpha+2}}

and

(2.21)

\displaystyle L^{*}_{n,\psi,\phi}K_{w}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\overline{c_{k}\psi_{k}(w)}\frac{p_{k}z^{k}}{(1-z\overline{\phi(w)})^{k+\alpha+2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\overline{a_{k}c_{k}}\left(\frac{\overline{w}^{k}}{(1-{b}\overline{w})^{k+\alpha+2}}\right)\frac{p_{k}z^{k}}{\left(1-z\left(\overline{b}+\dfrac{\overline{cw}}{1-\bar{b}\overline{w}}\right)\right)^{k+\alpha+2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\overline{c_{k}a_{k}}p_{k}(z\overline{w})^{k}}{\left(1-\overline{b}z-b\overline{w}+\overline{w}|b|^{2}z-\overline{w}cz\right)^{k+\alpha+2}}.

Since
a_{k}
and
c_{k}
are reals, hence
\overline{a_{k}c_{k}}=a_{k}c_{k}
. Therefore, from (
2.20
) and (
2.21
) we see that
L^{*}_{n,\psi,\phi}K_{w}(z)=L_{n,\psi,\phi}K_{w}(z)
for all
z\in\mathbb{D}
. Since the space of reproducing kernel function is dense in
\mathcal{A}^{2}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{D})
, it follows that
L^{*}_{n,\psi,\phi}=L_{n,\psi,\phi}
which shows that
L_{n,\psi,\phi}
is Hermitian. This completes the proof.
∎

3.
Kernel of generalized composition-differentiation operators on weighted Bergman space
\mathcal{A}^{2}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{D})

The kernel of linear operator T on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
is denoted by
ker(T)
and is defined as
ker(T)=\{x\in\mathcal{H}:T(x)=0\}
. The investigation on finding the kernel of composition operators precisely on the domain of analytic functions holds great importance as it introduces a novel aspect to operator theory. For instance, it follows from
[
44
, Proposition 3.2]
that the kernel of the adjoint of a complex
symmetric weighted composition operator on
{H}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
is
\{0\}
. Moreover, the result
[
37
, Proposition 2.9]
shows that the kernel of the adjoint of a nonzero
J
-symmetric weighted composition–
differentiation operator on Hardy Hilbert space
{H}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
is the whole complex plane
\mathbb{C}
. We obtain the following result exploring that the kernel of the adjoint of a nonzero
C_{\mu,\eta}
-symmetric generalized composition-differentiation operator
L_{n,\psi,\phi}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}D_{k,\psi_{k},\phi}
on the Weighted Bergman Space
\mathcal{A}^{2}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{D})
is
\mathbb{P}_{m-1}(\mathbb{C})
, the set of all polynomials of degree less than or equals to
(m-1)
, where
m=\min\{k:c_{k}\neq 0\}
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\phi
be an analytic self-map of
\mathbb{D}
and
\psi_{k}\in\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{D})
(where
k=1,2,\ldots,n
) be not identically zero such that
L_{n,\psi,\phi}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}D_{k,\psi_{k},\phi}
is bounded on
\mathcal{A}^{2}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{D})
. If
L_{n,\psi,\phi}
is complex symmetric on
\mathcal{A}^{2}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{D})
with conjugation
C_{\mu,\eta}
, then
ker(L_{n,\psi,\phi})=ker(L^{*}_{n,\psi,\phi})=\mathbb{P}_{m-1}(\mathbb{C})
, where
m=min\{k:c_{k}\neq 0\}
.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Suppose
L_{n,\psi,\phi}
is complex symmetric on
\mathcal{A}^{2}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{D})
with conjugation
C_{\mu,\eta}
, it follows from Theorem
3.1
that

\displaystyle\psi_{k}(z)=\frac{a_{k}z^{k}}{(1-\eta bz)^{k+\alpha+2}}\;\mbox{for}\;k=1,2,\ldots,n\;\mbox{and}\;\phi(z)=b+\frac{cz}{1-\eta bz}\;\mbox{for all}\;z\in\mathbb{D}

where
a_{k}={\psi^{(k)}(0)}/{k!}\neq 0
(where
k=1,2,\ldots,n
),
b=\phi(0)
and
c=\phi^{\prime}(0)\neq 0
. Let
f\in\ ker(L_{n,\psi,\phi})
. Then it is easy to see that

\displaystyle L_{n,\psi,\phi}f(z)\equiv 0\;\mbox{on}\;\mathbb{D}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}D_{k,\psi_{k},\phi}f(z)\equiv 0\;\mbox{on}\;\mathbb{D}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\sum_{k=m}^{n}c_{k}\psi_{k}(z)f^{(k)}(\phi(z))\equiv 0\;\mbox{on}\;\mathbb{D}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle f^{(k)}(\phi(z))\equiv 0\;\mbox{on}\;\mathbb{D}\;\mbox{for all}\;k=m,m+1,\ldots,n.

Since
\phi(\mathbb{D})
is open, by Identity Theorem for complex valued functions, we have

\displaystyle f^{(k)}(z)\equiv 0\;\mbox{on}\;\mathbb{D}\;\mbox{for all}\;k=m,m+1,\ldots,n

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle f(z)=a_{0}+a_{1}z+a_{2}z^{2}+\ldots+a_{k-1}z^{k-1}.

for some
a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{k-1}\in\mathbb{C}
. Thus it is clear that

(3.1)

\displaystyle ker(L_{n,\psi,\phi})\subseteq\mathbb{P}_{k-1}(\mathbb{C}).

Let
f\in\mathbb{P}_{m-1}(\mathbb{C})
. Clearly,
f
is a polynomial of degree
\leq(m-1)
. A simple computation shows that

\displaystyle L_{n,\psi,\phi}f(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}D_{k,\psi_{k},\phi}f(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=m}^{n}c_{k}\psi_{k}(z)f^{(k)}(\phi(z))

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=m}^{n}c_{k}\psi_{k}(z)f^{(k)}(z)\;\mbox{on}\;\mathbb{D}

\displaystyle\equiv 0\;\mbox{on}\;\mathbb{D}.

This implies that

(3.2)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{m-1}(\mathbb{C})\subseteq ker(L_{n,\psi,\phi}).

Consequently, from (
3.1
) and (
3.2
), we have
ker(L_{n,\psi,\phi})=\mathbb{P}_{m-1}(\mathbb{C})
. Let
f\in\ ker(L^{*}_{n,\psi,\phi})
. This shows that
L^{*}_{n,\psi,\phi}f\equiv 0
. Moreover,
L_{n,\psi,\phi}
is complex symmetric on
\mathcal{A}^{2}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{D})
with conjugation
C_{\mu,\eta}
we must have
L_{n,\psi,\phi}C_{\mu,\eta}f=C_{\mu,\eta}L^{*}_{n,\psi,\phi}f\equiv 0
. This implies that

\displaystyle C_{\mu,\eta}f\in\ker(L_{n,\psi,\phi})=\mathbb{P}_{m-1}(\mathbb{C})

Clearly,

Spectrum of generalized weighted composition-differential operators on space

In this section, we consider spectral properties of weighted composition-differentiation operators. The study of spectral properties of composition-differentiation operators is focused on understanding the behavior and characteristics of these operators in relation to their eigenvalues and eigenvectors. These operators combine the operations of composition and differentiation on functions, resulting in a new transformed function.

Investigating the spectral properties of composition-differentiation operators is significant for several reasons. Firstly, it provides insights into the structure and behavior of these operators, shedding light on their fundamental properties and how they affect the functions they operate on. This knowledge can be applied in various areas of mathematics and mathematical analysis. Secondly, understanding the spectral properties of composition-differentiation operators enables us to analyze and solve differential equations involving these operators. Differential equations play a crucial role in many scientific and engineering disciplines, and by studying the spectral properties, we can gain a deeper understanding of the solutions to such equations.

Moreover, the study of spectral properties also has connections to harmonic analysis, functional analysis, and operator theory. It allows us to explore the connections between composition-differentiation operators and other important mathematical concepts, leading to further advancements and applications in these fields. Overall, investigating the spectral properties of composition-differentiation operators provides valuable insights into their behavior, facilitates the analysis of differential equations, and establishes connections with other branches of mathematics, contributing to the broader understanding and application of these operators in various mathematical contexts.
