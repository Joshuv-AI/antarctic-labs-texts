Parity anomaly with impurities and the Pauli–Villars subtraction
Ozório Holanda, René Meyer, Dmitri Vassilevich

Abstract
We calculate the anomalous part of the polarization tensor of Dirac fermions in 2 + 1 2+1 dimensions in the presence of impurities described by the scattering rate Γ \Gamma for arbitrary external frequency and momenta. We consider two different versions of the Pauli–Villars subtractions and discuss their physical consequences.



I
Introduction

Due to the parity anomaly
Redlich 1984
;
Niemi and Semenoff 1983
(see also
Deser et al. 1982
), the one-loop effective action for Dirac fermions in
2+1
dimensions contains a parity violating Chern–Simons part. From phenomenological point of view, this means the appearance of a quantum anomalous Hall effect, i.e. of a Hall type conductivity in the absence of an external magnetic field. The momentum dependence of the anomalous part of the polarization tensor was analyzed in
Appelquist et al. 1986
. These papers were followed by a very interesting development in quantum field theory. To learn about general aspects of the Chern–Simons theory the reader may consult
Dunne 1999
.

An intriguing feature of the parity anomaly is that it leads to a Chern–Simons term with the weight
\pm 1/2
(or, equally, to the Hall conductivity being
\pm 1/2
of the Hall quantum). Such a term is not invariant under large gauge transformations which has been a source of confusion for a long period. This apparent contradiction was finally resolved in
Deser et al. 1998
;
Deser et al. 1997
where it was demonstrated that the Ward identities corresponding to large gauge transformations contain nonperturbative contributions capable to restore gauge invariance of the effective action.

Theoretical investigations of quantum anomalies are highly relevant in condensed matter systems, see
Dudal et al. 2022
. For the benefit of the typical high energy physics reader, we here given an overview over the main developments in this direction: Quantum anomalies are tied to relativistic fermions, which arise in topologically protected approximately relativistic band crossings such as topological insulators with 2+1-dimensional Dirac surface states
Kane and Mele 2005
;
Bernevig et al. 2006
;
König et al. 2007
;
Fu et al. 2007
;
Liu et al. 2008a
;
Roy 2009
;
Hsieh et al. 2008
;
Xia et al. 2009
;
Chen et al. 2009
;
Knez et al. 2011
, or Weyl and Dirac
Wan et al. 2011
;
Xu et al. 2011
;
Borisenko et al. 2014
;
Liu et al. 2014a
;
Liu et al. 2014b
;
Neupane et al. 2014
semimetals with 3+1-dimensional Weyl fermions as band crossings. In particular, the parity anomaly directly contributes to the quantum anomalous Hall effect, a Hall effect in the absence of external magnetic fields, in two- dimensional quantum anomalous Hall (QAH) insulators such as (Hg,Mn)Te quantum wells
Liu et al. 2008b
;
Qi et al. 2008
or magnetically doped (Bi,Sb)Te thin films
Yu et al. 2010
;
Chang et al. 2013
. The DC quantum anomalous Hall conductivity has a quantized part that is directly induced by the parity anomaly, i.e. the coefficient of the Chern-Simons term in the low energy effective action. In real-world condensed matter systems such as QAH insulators, the band structure can contain UV relevant terms
Bernevig et al. 2006
which serve as a UV regulator and contribute to the quantized as well as non-quantized parts of the quantum anomalous Hall conductivity
Tutschku et al. 2020a
. They preserve large gauge invariance and break parity, hence contributing as a UV regulator to the parity anomaly
Tutschku et al. 2020a
. From the point of view of the band structure, the quantized part of the anomalous Hall conductivity can be related to the presence of momentum space Berry curvature in the wave functions at the Dirac band touching points
Bernevig 2013
, or equivalently to a quantized winding number in the Brillouin zone
Thouless et al. 1982
. There are other non-quantized,
(T,\mu)
dependent contributions to the anomalous Hall conductivity as well
Tutschku et al. 2020b
. Experimental signatures of the parity anmomaly in 2+1-dimensional Dirac materials have been discussed in e.g.
Böttcher et al. 2019
;
Hu et al. 2022
.

In condensed matter physics, there are several ways to describe impurities depending on the properties of a particular material
Tkachov 2015
. The usual assumption is that impurities provide a randomly distributed non-periodic short-range scattering potential, on which the conduction electrons in a solid scatter in an energy and charge conserving way, but loose momentum on these impurities. For small impurity densities, the momentum-relaxing effect of impurity scattering, together with the details of the potential, can be summarized in a momentum relaxation term on the right hand side of the translation ward identity. An analogous relaxation time approximation is possible for weak breaking of translational symmetry in AdS/CFT models of strongly correlated quantum matter, for a recent review c.f.
Zaanen et al. 2015
;
Hartnoll et al. 2018
. In a particular limit, the AdS/CFT correspondence
Maldacena 1999
;
Gubser et al. 1998
;
Witten 1998
is a duality between strongly interacting quantum systems on the one side, and semiclassical gravity in one additional dimension on the other side (for a review of the correspondence, c.f. e.g.
Ammon and Erdmenger 2015
). In AdS/CFT, breaking of translational symmetry can be implemented in several ways
Horowitz et al. 2012
;
Vegh 2013
;
Davison 2013
;
Blake and Tong 2013
;
Andrade and Withers 2014
;
Goutéraux 2014
;
Donos and Hartnoll 2013
;
Donos and Hartnoll 2013
;
Erdmenger et al. 2015
. For small induced momentum relaxation rate these models are all equivalent to a theory with a massive graviton
Blake et al. 2014
, which is responsible for the relaxation term on the right hand sinde of the translational Ward identity.

Long-range charged impurities or long-range disorder potentials such as the ones generated by the charge puddles in graphene need a different treatment
Tkachov 2015
, by introducing a scattering rate
\Gamma
which enters the propagator of quasiparticles through substitution of the temporal momentum
p_{0}\to p_{0}+\mathrm{i}\Gamma\mbox{sgn}(p_{0})
. This approach to the impurities was used in calculations of the anti-symmetric part of polarization tensor in graphene in the presence of external magnetic field in e.g.
Gusynin and Sharapov 2006
;
Gusynin et al. 2006a
;
Gusynin et al. 2006b
;
Fialkovsky and Vassilevich 2012
. The computations of
Fialkovsky and Vassilevich 2012
are in a very good agreement with the measurements
Crassee et al. 2010
of giant Faraday rotation in graphene. Note that in these calculations an antisymmetric part of the polarization tensor was caused by the presence of an external magnetic field. The anomalous Hall conductivity of graphene cancels between generations of fermions. This explains why the anomalous Hall contributions with impurirites described by scattering rate
\Gamma
were neglected at that time. However, new applications including Hall conductivities of surface states of topological materials put the problem forward again.

The purpose of this work is to close an important gap in the literature by computing the anomalous part of polarization tensor for fermions in
2+1
dimensions in the presence of impurities described by the scattering rate
\Gamma
. We do not relate this computation to any particular material. Our main attention is on the Quantum Field Theory aspects. In particular, we study the Pauli–Villars subtraction scheme and suggest two versions of this scheme leading to qualitatively different dependence of the parity anomaly on
\Gamma
. The first scheme, consists in subtracting the contribution of a regulator field with mass
M
with taking subsequently the limit
|M|\to\infty
. In the second scheme, we also take the limit
\Gamma_{R}\to\infty
, where
\Gamma_{R}
is the impurity parameter for the regulator field, keeping the ratio
\Gamma_{R}/M
fixed. From the Quantum Field Theory perspective, the difference between two schemes is merely a renormalization ambiguity. We perform the computations for arbitrary values of external frequency and momenta which is not common for current literature although this is important for evaluation of the Casimir force, for example.

This paper is organized as follows. In the next section, we compute the unregularaized anomalous polarization tensor. Two possible PV subtraction schemes are analyzed in Section
III
. The results of this work are briefly discussed in Section
IV
.

II
Polarization tensor in presence of impurities

We start with the action

\displaystyle S=\int d^{3}x\bar{\Psi}\not{D}\Psi,

(1)

where
\not{D}=\mathrm{i}\tilde{\gamma}^{\mu}(\partial_{\mu}+ieA_{\mu})-m
. A twiddle over a 3-vector means that that the spatial components are rescaled with the Fermi velocity. In particular,

\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}^{0}=\gamma^{0},\,\,\,\,\tilde{\gamma}^{i}=v_{F}\gamma^{i}

(2)

The Greek letters label the spacetime coordinates,
\mu=0,1,2
, while the Latin letters denote the spatial coordinates,
i,j=1,2
. We use the metric
g^{\mu\nu}=\mbox{diag}(1,-1,-1)
. We fix
\mbox{tr}[\gamma^{\mu}\gamma^{\nu}\gamma^{\alpha}]=2\mathrm{i}\varepsilon^{\mu\nu\alpha}
. Here,
\varepsilon^{\mu\nu\alpha}
is the Levi-Civita tensor, and
\mathrm{i}:=\sqrt{-1}
.

The one-loop effective action due to quantum fermions in the second order of electromagnetic field
A
reads

S_{\mathrm{eff}}=\frac{1}{2}\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}A_{\mu}(-k)\Pi^{\mu\nu}(k)A_{\nu}(k),

(3)

where the polarization tensor is given by

\Pi^{\mu\nu}(k,m)=\mathrm{i}e^{2}\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\mbox{tr}[\tilde{\gamma}^{\mu}G_{f}(p,m)\tilde{\gamma}^{\nu}G_{f}(p+k,m)]

(4)

with the fermion propagator

\displaystyle G_{f}(p,m)=\frac{1}{\not{\tilde{p}}-m}=\frac{(\not{\tilde{p}}+m)}{\tilde{p}^{2}-m^{2}},

(5)

where
\not{\tilde{p}}:=\tilde{\gamma}^{\mu}p_{\mu}
. In the presence of impurities, the temporal momentum has to be replaced as

p_{0}\to\hat{p}_{0}=p_{0}+\mathrm{i}\Gamma\,\mathrm{sgn}\,p_{0}.

(6)

Here
\Gamma
is an impurity scattering rate,
\Gamma>0
.

Note, that the replacement (
6
) done in the propagator alone breaks gauge invariance. Indeed, such propagator can be formally obtained from a Dirac action containing an additional term with
\mathrm{sgn}(-\mathrm{i}\partial_{0})
. To ensure gauge invariance, any derivative has to be accompanied by an electromagnetic potential. Thus, one expects something like
\mathrm{sgn}(-\mathrm{i}(\partial_{0}+eA_{0}))
. Giving a precise meaning to such a term is not easy, but obviously it should modify the couplings of fermions to
A_{0}
and lead to new vertices in the Feynman rules involving
A_{0}
. To avoid complications, we will consider only the diagrams with external legs corresponding to the spatial components
A_{j}
, i.e the
\Pi^{ij}
part of the polarization tensor. This will be enough for our purposes since the full polarization tensor can be recovered through the transversality condition. Thus, we will evaluate

\Pi^{ij}(k)=\mathrm{i}e^{2}\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\mbox{tr}[\tilde{\gamma}^{i}G_{f}(\widehat{p},m)\tilde{\gamma}^{j}G_{f}(\widehat{p+k},m)],

(7)

where
\widehat{p}^{\,\mu}:=(p_{0}+\mathrm{i}\Gamma\,\mbox{sgn}{(p_{0})},\vec{p})
.

To analyze the dependence of polarization tensor on the Fermi velocity one can use the following simple trick
Fialkovsky and Vassilevich 2009
. Let us change the integration variable
p\to\tilde{p}
in (
7
). The Jacobean factor
v_{F}^{-2}
is canceled the factors of
v_{F}
in
\tilde{\gamma}^{i}
adn
\tilde{\gamma}^{j}
, so that one obtains

\Pi^{ij}(v_{F};k)=\Pi^{ij}(v_{F}=1,\tilde{k})

(8)

Thus, from now on we set

v_{F}=1.

(9)

Thus we have

\displaystyle\Pi^{ij}(k,m,\Gamma)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}e^{2}\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{\mbox{tr}[\gamma^{i}(\widehat{\not{p}}+m)\gamma^{j}(\widehat{\not{p}+\not{k}}+m)]}{(\widehat{p}^{2}-m^{2})((\widehat{p+k})^{2}-m^{2})}.

(10)

We are interested in the anomalous part
\Pi_{\mathrm{odd}}
of the polarization tensor which changes the sign under the change of orientation of spacetime. This is the part which is antisymmetric in the indices
i,j
and proportial to
\varepsilon^{ij}\equiv\varepsilon^{ij0}
. After computing the traces over spinor indices and selecting relevant tensor structures we obtain

\displaystyle\Pi_{\mathrm{odd}}^{ij}(k,m,\Gamma)=\varepsilon^{ij}k_{0}\left[\zeta(k,m,\Gamma)+\chi(k,m,\Gamma)\right],

(11)

where

\displaystyle\zeta(k,m,\Gamma)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2me^{2}\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{1}{((p_{0}+\mathrm{i}\Gamma\,\mbox{sgn}{(p_{0})})^{\,2}-\vec{p}^{\,2}-m^{2})}

(12)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\frac{1}{((p_{0}+k_{0}+\mathrm{i}\Gamma\,\mbox{sgn}{(p_{0}+k_{0})})^{2}-(\vec{p}+\vec{k})^{2}-m^{2})}

and

\displaystyle\chi(k,m,\Gamma)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\mathrm{i}me^{2}{\frac{\Gamma}{k_{0}}}\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{\mbox{sgn}{(p_{0}+k_{0})}-\mbox{sgn}{(p_{0})}}{((p_{0}+\mathrm{i}\Gamma\,\mbox{sgn}{(p_{0})})^{\,2}-\vec{p}^{\,2}-m^{2})}

(13)

\displaystyle\times\frac{1}{(p_{0}+k_{0}+\mathrm{i}\Gamma\,\mbox{sgn}{(p_{0}+k_{0})})^{2}-(\vec{p}+\vec{k})^{2}-m^{2}}\,.

We use the Feynman formula

\displaystyle\frac{1}{AB}=\int_{0}^{1}dx{1\over(Ax+(1-x)B)^{2}}.

(14)

Quite remarkably, the expression under integral above has no poles in the whole integration region despite the presence
\Gamma
. We thus have

\displaystyle\zeta(k,m,\Gamma)=-2me^{2}\int_{0}^{1}dx\int\frac{dp_{0}}{2\pi}\int\frac{d^{2}\vec{l}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{1}{(\vec{l}^{2}-M)^{2}}

(15)

and

\displaystyle\chi(k,m,\Gamma)=-2ime^{2}{\Gamma\over k_{0}}\int_{0}^{1}dx\int\frac{dp_{0}}{2\pi}(\mbox{sgn}(p_{0}+k_{0})-\mbox{sgn}(p_{0}))\int\frac{d^{2}\vec{l}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{1}{(\vec{l}^{2}-M)^{2}},

(16)

with
\vec{l}=\vec{p}+\vec{k}x
and
M=(p_{0}+\mathrm{i}\Gamma\,\mbox{sgn}{(p_{0})})^{2}(1-x)+x(p_{0}+k_{0}+\mathrm{i}\Gamma\mbox{sgn}(p_{0}+k_{0}))^{2}-\vec{k}^{2}x(1-x)-m^{2}.

After performing the
\vec{l}
integration we obtain

\displaystyle\zeta(k,m,\Gamma)=\frac{me^{2}}{4\pi^{2}}\,\int_{0}^{1}dx\int dp_{0}\frac{1}{M},

(17)

\displaystyle\chi(k,m,\Gamma)=\frac{\mathrm{i}me^{2}\Gamma}{4\pi^{2}k_{0}}\,\int_{0}^{1}dx\int dp_{0}\frac{\mbox{sgn}{(p_{0}+k_{0})}-\mbox{sgn}(p_{0})}{M}.

(18)

In the presence of
\Gamma
, the
p_{0}
integral cannot be done by computing the residues and requires somewhat more attention. In the expression (
17
), one has to divide the integration region in three intervals depending on the signs of
p_{0}
and
p_{0}+k_{0}
. In (
18
) only one of such intervals contribute. After performing the integrations, one obtains

\displaystyle\zeta(k,m,\Gamma)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}me^{2}\int_{0}^{1}dx\left[\frac{\pi-2\,\mbox{arctan}\left(\frac{k_{0}x+\mathrm{i}\Gamma}{\sqrt{-m^{2}+k^{2}x(1-x)}}\right)}{\sqrt{-m^{2}+k^{2}x(1-x)}}\right.

(19)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left.\frac{2\,\mbox{arctan}\left(\frac{(k_{0}+2\mathrm{i}\Gamma)x-i\Gamma}{\sqrt{-m^{2}+((k_{0}+2\mathrm{i}\Gamma)^{2}-\vec{k}^{2})x(1-x)}}\right)}{\sqrt{-m^{2}+((k_{0}+2\mathrm{i}\Gamma)^{2}-\vec{k}^{2})x(1-x)}}\right]

and

\displaystyle\chi(k,m,\Gamma)=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{4\pi^{2}}me^{2}\frac{\Gamma}{k_{0}}\int_{0}^{1}dx\frac{4\,\mbox{arctan}\left(\frac{(k_{0}+2\mathrm{i}\Gamma)x-\mathrm{i}\Gamma}{\sqrt{-m^{2}+((k_{0}+2\mathrm{i}\Gamma)^{2}-\vec{k}^{2})x(1-x)}}\right)}{\sqrt{-m^{2}+((k_{0}+2\mathrm{i}\Gamma)^{2}-\vec{k}^{2})x(1-x)}}.

(20)

II.1
Limits of the polarization tensor

Before going on with the renormalization, let us consider some limits of unrenormalized polarization tensor. In the
m\to 0
limit with all other parameters staying finite one immediately obtains

\Pi^{ij}_{\mathrm{odd}}(k,0,\Gamma)=0.

(21)

Another important limit is
\Gamma\to 0
,

\Pi_{\mathrm{odd}}^{ij}(k,m,0)=-\frac{\mathrm{i}e^{2}}{4\pi}\varepsilon^{ij}k_{0}\frac{2m}{|k|}\mbox{arctanh}\left(\frac{|k|}{2|m|}\right),

(22)

which reproduces the known result
Appelquist et al. 1986
. Do not confuse
arctanh
in this formula with
\arctan
in Eq. (
19
).

In the limit when both
m
and
\Gamma
become large but their fraction is kept constant, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}{\Pi}_{\mathrm{odd}}^{ij}(k,\lambda m,\lambda\Gamma)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}me^{2}\varepsilon^{ij}k_{0}\left(\frac{-\mathrm{i}\pi+2\mathrm{i}\,\mbox{arctan}\left(\frac{\Gamma}{|m|}\right)}{|m|}\right)

(23)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\mathrm{i}\Gamma me^{2}\varepsilon^{ij}k_{0}\left(\frac{2}{\Gamma^{2}+m^{2}}\right).

(This formula is easier to obtain from (
17
) and (
18
) where the integration over
p_{0}
has not been done yet.)

The same formula (
23
) describes the limit when both
k_{0}
and
|\vec{k}|
are small as compared to
m
and
\Gamma
. This formula also allows to obtain the limits

\lim_{|m|\to\infty}{\Pi}_{\mathrm{odd}}^{ij}(k,m,\Gamma)=-\frac{\mathrm{i}e^{2}}{4\pi}\,\frac{m}{|m|}\varepsilon^{ij}k_{0}

(24)

and

\lim_{\Gamma\to\infty}{\Pi}_{\mathrm{odd}}^{ij}(k,m,\Gamma)=0.

(25)

To recover the dependence of polarization tensor on Fermi velocity it is sufficient to replace
k\to\tilde{k}=(k_{0},v_{F}\vec{k})
in the formulas given above. The full anomalous polarization tensor may be obtained by solving the conservation condition
k_{\mu}\Pi_{\mathrm{odd}}^{\mu\nu}=0
. One obtains
\Pi_{\mathrm{odd}}^{0j}=-(k_{i}/k_{0})\Pi_{\mathrm{odd}}^{ij}
and
\Pi_{\mathrm{odd}}^{00}=0
.

III
Pauli–Villars subtractions

As has been noted already by Redlich
Redlich 1984
, although the parity-odd part of polarization tensor is non-divergent, one has to apply a Pauli–Villars subtraction to get a correct result. The usual prescription consists of subtracting from the non-renormalized polarization tensor a contribution from a fermion field have exactly the same parameters except for the mass
M
and taking the limit
|M|\to\infty
at the end of calculations. We additionally assume that
M
has the same sign as
m
so that to pass from
m
to
M
one does not need to cross the gapless phase. This assumption is not essential. The opposite limit can be analysed along the same lines. We will call this scheme
PV1 (since there also be a PV2). Basically, this prescription boils down to subtracting (
24
) from the polarization tensor.

For further discussion, is it convenient to introduce a quantity
\sigma
,

\displaystyle\Re\left[\frac{\Pi^{ij}_{\mathrm{odd}}}{\mathrm{i}k_{0}}\right]=\varepsilon^{ij}\,\frac{e^{2}}{2\pi}\,\sigma

(26)

which is nothing else than the anti-symmetric (Hall) conductivity measured in the units of Hall quantum
e^{2}/(2\pi)=e^{2}/h
. The imaginary part of conductivity is not affected by the Pauli–Villars subtraction and thus will not be considered here.

We immediately obtain the zero-gap result

\sigma_{\mathrm{PV1}}(k,0,\Gamma)=\frac{1}{2}\,\frac{m}{|m|}\,,

(27)

which comes exclusively from the PV regulator field
1
1

1

The sign on the right hand side is a consequence of our assumption
\mbox{sgn}\,M=\mbox{sgn}\,m
. In general case, the sign factor in (
27
) is given by
\mbox{sgn}\,M
.
, does not depend on
\Gamma
, and represents the classical value of parity anomaly. Also, the limit
\Gamma\to 0
reproduces a known result,

\sigma_{\mathrm{PV1}}(k,m,0)=\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{m}{|m|}-\frac{2m}{|k|}\mbox{arctanh}\left(\frac{|k|}{2|m|}\right)\right]\,.

(28)

We also have

\lim_{\Gamma\to\infty}\sigma_{\mathrm{PV1}}(k,m,\Gamma)=\frac{1}{2}\,\frac{m}{|m|}\,.

(29)

In the scheme PV1, the mass of regulator field goes to infinity while the parameter
\Gamma
which describes impurities remains fixed. Thus, relative to the mass, the impurities become negligible. Since
\Gamma
is a phenomenological parameter, one can also consider other prescriptions for the behavior of the impurity parameter
\Gamma_{R}
for the regulator field. A reasonable choice seems to be to fix the ratio
\Gamma_{R}/M
while taking the limit
\Gamma_{R},\ M\to\infty
. In other words, we take the limit
m\to\infty
keeping all
dimensionless
parameters like
e
and
\Gamma_{R}/M
fixed. We call this scheme PV2. In this scheme, we need to subtract the expression (
23
) from the unrenormalized polarization tensor. Let us discuss physical consequences of the PV2 subtraction.

If
m\neq 0
, there are simple analytic formulas for
k=0
which we present here for the sake of completeness.

\sigma(0,m,\Gamma)=\frac{m}{2\pi}\left(-\pi+\frac{2\arctan(\Gamma/|m|)}{|m|}-\frac{2\Gamma m}{\Gamma^{2}+m^{2}}\right).

(30)

In the PV1 scheme, one has

\sigma_{\mathrm{PV1}}(0,m,\Gamma)=\frac{m}{\pi}\left(\frac{\arctan(\Gamma/|m|)}{|m|}-\frac{\Gamma m}{\Gamma^{2}+m^{2}}\right).

(31)

In the PV2 scheme, the conductivity vanishes identically in this limit,

\sigma_{\mathrm{PV2}}(0,m,\Gamma)=0

which also happens at
\Gamma=0
in any scheme in the absence of a chemical potential. (Note, that the limits
k\to 0
and
m\to 0
do not commute.) Although there are analytic formulas (
30
) and (
31
) we present the plots on Fig.

) for convenience.

In
2+1
dimensions the structure of ultraviolet divergences is quite simple so that just a single regulator field is required. The only essential feature is that one has to take the limit of an infinite mass gap of the regulator field. From this point of view, there are good reasons to expect that both subtraction schemes are internally consistent and remove all ultraviolet divergences. These arguments are however not watertight and we shall pay more attention to the QFT aspects of Pauli–Villars scheme in some future work. Note, that there are examples when modifications of a model impose severe restrictions on the PV subtractions. (See e.g. the paper
Fialkovsky et al. 2019
where the PV scheme in 4D QED with boundaries was analyzed.)

At any rate, the final word in choosing between two subtraction schemes should belong to an experiment.

IV
Discussion

In this paper, we have studied with Quantum Field Theory Methods the anomalous part of polarization tensor of a
2+1
dimensional fermion interacting with impurities described by a scattering rate
\Gamma
for arbitrary external frequency and momenta. We used the PV scheme and argued that there are two natural subtractions. In one of them, which we called PV1, the contribution of a massive regulator field is subtracted in the limit
|M|\to\infty
. This is just the standard PV subtraction known from text books. In the other scheme, we treat
\Gamma/m
as a dimensional parameter similar to the electric charge. With such an interpretation, it is natural to assume that the regulator field sees the same “impurity charge”
\Gamma_{R}/M=\Gamma/m
. This boils down to subtracting a double limit
|M|,\Gamma_{R}\to\infty
while keeping the ratio
\Gamma_{R}/M
fixed. In this scheme, called PV2 throughout this work, the dependence of Hall conductivity
\sigma
on
\Gamma
differs crucially from the predictions of PV1. In PV1, the conductivity is enhanced by the impurities while in PV2 the impuirities tend to diminish
\sigma
. The last word in choosing between PV1 and PV2 should belongs to an experiment. However, further consistency checks are also needed. An important lesson from the papers
Deser et al. 1998
;
Deser et al. 1997
is that large gauge invariance cannot be used to check perturbative calculations since corresponding Ward identities contain important nonperturbative contributions.

There are alternative methods of calculations of the parity anomaly. One of them relies on the
\zeta
function regularization. It allows to evaluate the anomaly for massless
Alvarez-Gaume et al. 1985
and massive
Deser et al. 1998
;
Deser et al. 1997
;
Fosco et al. 1997
fermions, in the presence of an external magnetic field
Beneventano et al. 2009
, and even in the presence of boundaries
Kurkov and Vassilevich 2017
. The results obtained with this method are consistent with the Pauli–Villars scheme. Unfortunately, there is no generalization of the
\zeta
function regularization in the presence of impurities.
For completeness, we like to mention a rather extreme proposal
Del Cima et al. 2009
that the parity anomaly is merely a counterterm which is needed to restore parity.

The Casimir force between surfaces which exhibit a Hall-type conductivity may become repulsive (for an overview of this effect see
Fialkovsky et al. 2018
